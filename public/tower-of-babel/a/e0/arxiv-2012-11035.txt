Abstract
We investigate the contraction coefficients derived from strong data processing inequalities for the 𝖤 γ {\mathsf{E}}_{\gamma} -divergence. By generalizing the celebrated Dobrushin’s coefficient from total variation distance to 𝖤 γ {\mathsf{E}}_{\gamma} -divergence, we derive a closed-form expression for the contraction of 𝖤 γ {\mathsf{E}}_{\gamma} -divergence. This result has fundamental consequences in two privacy settings. First, it implies that local differential privacy can be equivalently expressed in terms of the contraction of 𝖤 γ {\mathsf{E}}_{\gamma} -divergence. This equivalent formula can be used to precisely quantify the impact of local privacy in (Bayesian and minimax) estimation and hypothesis testing problems in terms of the reduction of effective sample size. Second, it leads to a new information-theoretic technique for analyzing privacy guarantees of online algorithms. In this technique, we view such algorithms as a composition of amplitude-constrained Gaussian channels and then relate their contraction coefficients under 𝖤 γ {\mathsf{E}}_{\gamma} -divergence to the overall differential privacy guarantees. As an example, we apply our technique to derive the differential privacy parameters of gradient descent. Moreover, we also show that this framework can be tailored to batch learning algorithms that can be implemented with one pass over the training dataset.

Main Contributions

Contraction coefficient under
{\mathsf{E}}_{\gamma}
-divergence:
We prove that, similar to
\eta_{\mathsf{TV}}

under
{\mathsf{E}}_{\gamma}
-divergence, denoted by

Local differential privacy:
As another application, we use Theorem

Central differential privacy:

As a final application, we apply Theorem
2
(more specifically, Theorem
1
) to investigate the privacy guarantees of online iterative algorithms. In general, an online learning algorithm proceeds as follows: a learner first selects a random point

In Theorem

\nu
are probability measures,

\lVert\mu-\nu\rVert=2\sup_{A\subset{\mathcal{X}}}\big[\mu(A)-\nu(A)\big].

The total variation distance between two distributions
\mu
and
\nu
is

\mathsf{TV}(\mu,\nu):=\frac{1}{2}\lVert\mu-\nu\rVert.

A Markov kernel (or channel)
{\mathsf{K}}:{\mathcal{D}}\to{\mathcal{P}}({\mathcal{W}})
is specified by a collection of distributions
\{{\mathsf{K}}(x)\in{\mathcal{P}}({\mathcal{W}}):x\in{\mathcal{S}}\}
. Given a Markov kernel
{\mathsf{K}}:{\mathcal{D}}\to{\mathcal{P}}({\mathcal{W}})
and
\mu\in{\mathcal{P}}({\mathcal{D}})
, we denote by
\mu{\mathsf{K}}
the push-forward of
\mu
under
{\mathsf{K}}
, i.e., the output distribution of
{\mathsf{K}}
when the input is distributed according to
\mu
, and is given by
\mu{\mathsf{K}}\coloneqq\int\mu(\text{d}x){\mathsf{K}}(x)
. We use
{\mathbb{E}}_{\mu}[\cdot]
to write the expectation with respect to
\mu
and
[n]
for an integer
n\geq 1
to denote
\{1,\dots,n\}
. For
a\in[0,1]
, we define
\bar{a}=1-a
. For a set
\mathcal{D}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we let
\textnormal{dia}(\mathcal{D})
be its diameter, i.e.,

\textnormal{dia}(\mathcal{D})=\sup_{x_{1},x_{2}\in\mathcal{D}}\lVert x_{2}-x_{1}\rVert.

II
Contraction Coefficient of Markov Kernels under
{\mathsf{E}}_{\gamma}
-Divergence

In this section, we establish a closed-form expression for the contraction coefficient of Markov kernels under
{\mathsf{E}}_{\gamma}
-divergence which generalizes Dobrushin’s formula. Relying on this expression, we compute the contraction coefficient of the amplitude-constrained additive Gaussian kernel. Finally, we improve classical estimates for the output
f
-divergence based on our generalization of Dobrushin’s formula.

Similar to Dobrushin’s formula (
5
), the next theorem reduces the computation of the contraction coefficient of a Markov kernel under
{\mathsf{E}}_{\gamma}
-divergence to maximizing the output
{\mathsf{E}}_{\gamma}
-divergence for atomic inputs. In the sequel, we always assume that
\mathcal{D}
and
\mathcal{W}
are subsets of (potentially different) Euclidean spaces. Recall that
\eta_{\gamma}({\mathsf{K}})
denotes the contraction coefficient of kernel
{\mathsf{K}}
under
{\mathsf{E}}_{\gamma}
-divergence.

Theorem 2
.

Let
{\mathsf{K}}:{\mathcal{D}}\to{\mathcal{P}}({\mathcal{W}})
be a Markov kernel. For any
\gamma\geq 1
, we have

\eta_{\gamma}({\mathsf{K}})=\sup_{x_{1},x_{2}\in{\mathcal{D}}}{\mathsf{E}}_{\gamma}({\mathsf{K}}(\cdot|x_{1})\|{\mathsf{K}}(\cdot|x_{2})).

(11)

Furthermore, for any
\gamma<1
, we have

\eta_{\gamma}({\mathsf{K}})=\eta_{1/\gamma}({\mathsf{K}}).

(12)

The proof of this theorem is given in Appendix
-D
. In view of the reciprocity relation (
12
), in the sequel we focus on the case
\gamma\geq 1
.

Liu et al.
[
93
, Proposition 4]
showed that, for
\gamma\geq 1
and any probability measures
\mu
and
\nu
,

{\mathsf{TV}}(\mu,\nu)\leq 1-\frac{1-{\mathsf{E}}_{\gamma}(\mu\|\nu)}{\gamma}.

Applying Theorem
2
to this inequality leads to the following
general relation between the contraction coefficient of a Markov kernel under total variation and the corresponding one under
{\mathsf{E}}_{\gamma}
-divergence.

Lemma 1
.

Let
{\mathsf{K}}:{\mathcal{D}}\to{\mathcal{P}}({\mathcal{W}})
be a Markov kernel. For any
\gamma\geq 1
, we have

\eta_{{\mathsf{TV}}}({\mathsf{K}})\leq 1-\frac{1-\eta_{\gamma}({\mathsf{K}})}{\gamma}.

Recall that, for any convex function
f
satisfying
f(1)=0
and any Markov kernel
{\mathsf{K}}
,

\eta_{f}({\mathsf{K}})\leq\eta_{\mathsf{TV}}({\mathsf{K}}).

(13)

This result, originally proved in
[
16
]
for the discrete case and subsequently extended to the general case in
[
94
]
, and our previous lemma produce the estimate

\eta_{f}({\mathsf{K}})\leq 1-\frac{1-\eta_{\gamma}({\mathsf{K}})}{\gamma},

or, equivalently, for any probability measures
\mu
and
\nu
,

D_{f}(\mu{\mathsf{K}}\|\nu{\mathsf{K}})\leq\left(1-\frac{1-\eta_{\gamma}({\mathsf{K}})}{\gamma}\right)D_{f}(\mu\|\nu).

(14)

In Section
III
we apply the latter bound to quantify the statistical cost of local differential privacy.

Next we extend Lemma
1
to the tensor product of Markov kernels. Given Markov kernels
{\mathsf{K}}_{1},\dots,{\mathsf{K}}_{n}
, we let
{\mathsf{K}}^{\otimes n}
be their
n
-fold tensor product, i.e., the memoryless channel defined by

{\mathsf{K}}^{\otimes n}={\mathsf{K}}_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes{\mathsf{K}}_{n}.

Recall that the contraction coefficient
\eta_{{\mathsf{TV}}}
satisfies (see, e.g.,
[
32
, Corollary 9]
,
[
18
, Lemma 3]
and
[
6
, Eq. (62)]
),

\eta_{\mathsf{TV}}({\mathsf{K}}^{\otimes n})\leq\max_{i\in[n]}\left[1-(1-\eta_{{\mathsf{TV}}}({\mathsf{K}}_{i}))^{n}\right].

The next lemma is an immediate consequence of this inequality and Lemma
1
.

Lemma 2
.

Let
{\mathsf{K}}_{1},\dots,{\mathsf{K}}_{n}
be Markov kernels. For any
\gamma\geq 1
, we have

\eta_{{\mathsf{TV}}}({\mathsf{K}}^{\otimes n})\leq\max_{i\in[n]}\left[1-\left(\frac{1-\eta_{\gamma}({\mathsf{K}}_{i})}{\gamma}\right)^{n}\right].

As before, we can combine the previous lemma with (
13
) to obtain an upper bound for
\eta_{f}({\mathsf{K}}^{\otimes n})
with
f
a convex function satisfying
f(1)=0
.

II-A

Contraction Coefficient of the Amplitude-Constrained Additive Gaussian Kernel

Next we compute the contraction coefficient of the amplitude-constrained additive Gaussian kernel under
{\mathsf{E}}_{\gamma}
-divergence. To this end, we start by recalling the following lemma.

Lemma 3

(
[
95
, Lemma 6]
)
.

For
m\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\sigma>0
, let
\mathcal{N}(m,\sigma^{2}\mathbf{I}_{d})
denote the multivariate Gaussian distribution with mean
m
and covariance matrix
\sigma^{2}\mathbf{I}_{d}
. If
m_{1},m_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\sigma>0
, then

\displaystyle{\mathsf{E}}_{\gamma}(\mathcal{N}(m_{1},\sigma^{2}\mathbf{I}_{d})\|\mathcal{N}(m_{2},\sigma^{2}\mathbf{I}_{d}))={\mathsf{Q}}\left(\frac{\log\gamma}{\beta}-\frac{\beta}{2}\right)-\gamma{\mathsf{Q}}\left(\frac{\log\gamma}{\beta}+\frac{\beta}{2}\right),

with
\displaystyle\beta=\frac{\|m_{2}-m_{1}\|}{\sigma}
and
\displaystyle{\mathsf{Q}}(t)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{t}^{\infty}e^{-u^{2}/2}\text{d}u
.

This lemma motivates the following definition.

Definition 1
.

For
\gamma\geq 1
, we let
\theta_{\gamma}:[0,\infty)\to[0,1]
be the function defined by

\displaystyle\theta_{\gamma}(r)

\displaystyle\coloneqq{\mathsf{E}}_{\gamma}\left(\mathcal{N}(ru,\mathbf{I}_{d})\|\mathcal{N}(0,\mathbf{I}_{d})\right)

\displaystyle={\mathsf{Q}}\left(\frac{\log\gamma}{r}-\frac{r}{2}\right)-\gamma{\mathsf{Q}}\left(\frac{\log\gamma}{r}+\frac{r}{2}\right),

where
u\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is any vector of unit norm.

It is straightforward to verify that
r\mapsto\theta_{\gamma}(r)
is a non-decreasing mapping for every
\gamma\geq 1
. This intuitive property is useful to compute the contraction coefficient of the amplitude-constrained additive Gaussian kernel.

Given
\mathcal{D}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\sigma>0
, the
\mathcal{D}
-constrained additive Gaussian kernel
{\mathsf{K}}:\mathcal{D}\to{\mathcal{P}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
is defined by

{\mathsf{K}}(\cdot|x)=\mathcal{N}(x,\sigma^{2}\mathbf{I}_{d}).

Note that
\mathcal{D}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\lVert x\rVert^{2}\leq dA\}
recovers the usual amplitude-constrained additive Gaussian kernel in (
3
). The following proposition is obtained by combining the previous lemma and our generalization of Dobrushin’s formula in Theorem
2
, as shown in Appendix
-E
. Recall that

\textnormal{dia}(\mathcal{D})=\sup_{x_{1},x_{2}\in\mathcal{D}}\lVert x_{2}-x_{1}\rVert.

Proposition 1
.

Let
\gamma\geq 1
,
\mathcal{D}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and
\sigma>0
. If
{\mathsf{K}}
is the
\mathcal{D}
-constrained additive Gaussian kernel, then

\eta_{\gamma}({\mathsf{K}})=\theta_{\gamma}\left(\frac{\textnormal{dia}(\mathcal{D})}{\sigma}\right).

The contraction coefficient of the additive Gaussian kernel under
{\mathsf{E}}_{\gamma}
-divergence is trivial
1
1

1

Recall that
\eta_{\mathsf{TV}}({\mathsf{K}})=1
for any Gaussian channel
{\mathsf{K}}
without input constraints
[
8
]
and, in addition,
\eta_{\mathsf{TV}}({\mathsf{K}})=1
if and only if
\eta_{f}({\mathsf{K}})=1
for any non-linear function
f

[
16
, Prop. II.4.12]
.
, i.e.,
\eta_{\gamma}({\mathsf{K}})=1
. Hence, Proposition
1
shows that constraints to the channel input must be imposed in order for the contraction coefficient to be non-trivial. Given this proposition, Theorem
1
is a simple consequence of the data processing inequality for
f
-divergences. In Section
IV
we provide differential privacy guarantees for different noisy iterative algorithms based on these results.

Remark 1
.

Note that the
{\mathsf{E}}_{\gamma}
-divergence between Gaussian distributions
{\mathcal{N}}(m_{1},\sigma^{2}\mathbf{I}_{d})
and
{\mathcal{N}}(m_{2},\sigma^{2}\mathbf{I}_{d})
can be concisely expressed as

{\mathsf{E}}_{\gamma}({\mathcal{N}}(m_{1},\sigma^{2}\mathbf{I}_{d})\|{\mathcal{N}}(m_{2},\sigma^{2}\mathbf{I}_{d}))=\theta_{\gamma}\left(\frac{\lVert m_{2}-m_{1}\rVert}{\sigma}\right).

(15)

The mapping
r\mapsto\theta_{\gamma}(r)
is closely related to
the functions
\theta(r)
by Polyanskiy and Wu
[
8
, Sec. 2.2]
,
h(\eta)
by Balle and Wang in
[
95
, Lemma 7]
, and
R_{\alpha}
by Feldman et al.
[
73
, Def. 10]
.

II-B

A General Upper Bound for Output
f
-divergence

The fundamental inequality in (
13
) implies that, for any probability measures
\mu
and
\nu
,

D_{f}(\mu{\mathsf{K}}\|\nu{\mathsf{K}})\leq\eta_{{\mathsf{TV}}}({\mathsf{K}})D_{f}(\mu\|\nu),

(16)

where
f
is a convex function with
f(1)=0
. We end this section by proposing an improvement to this inequality which is very effective in combination with Theorem
2
.

As established in
[
96
, Proposition 3]
, if
f
is twice differentiable and convex, then
D_{f}(\mu\|\nu)
can be expressed in terms of the
{\mathsf{E}}_{\gamma}
-divergence as

\displaystyle D_{f}(\mu\|\nu)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}f^{\prime\prime}(\gamma){\mathsf{E}}_{\gamma}(\mu\|\nu)\text{d}\gamma

\displaystyle=\int_{1}^{\infty}\big[f^{\prime\prime}(\gamma){\mathsf{E}}_{\gamma}(\mu\|\nu)+\gamma^{-3}f^{\prime\prime}(\gamma^{-1}){\mathsf{E}}_{\gamma}(\nu\|\mu)\big]\text{d}\gamma.

(17)

This expression and the definition of the contraction coefficient
\eta_{\gamma}({\mathsf{K}})
directly yield the following corollary.

Corollary 1
.

Let
f:(0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
be a twice differentiable convex function with
f(1)=0
. If
{\mathsf{K}}:{\mathcal{D}}\to{\mathcal{P}}({\mathcal{W}})
is a Markov kernel, then, for any probability measures
\mu,\nu\in{\mathcal{P}}({\mathcal{D}})
,

\displaystyle D_{f}(\mu{\mathsf{K}}\|\nu{\mathsf{K}})

\displaystyle\leq\int_{1}^{\infty}\eta_{\gamma}({\mathsf{K}})\big[f^{\prime\prime}(\gamma){\mathsf{E}}_{\gamma}(\mu\|\nu)+\gamma^{-3}f^{\prime\prime}(\gamma^{-1}){\mathsf{E}}_{\gamma}(\nu\|\mu)\big]\textnormal{d}\gamma.

Observe that, by (
13
),
\eta_{\gamma}(K)\leq\eta_{{\mathsf{TV}}}({\mathsf{K}})
for all
\gamma>0
. Hence, the integral representation (
1
) shows that the bound in the previous corollary improves over (
16
). To illustrate the magnitude of this improvement, we consider the following two examples. Recall that
\chi^{2}
-divergence is the
f
-divergence with
f(t)=(t-1)^{2}
.

Example 1.

Let
X\sim{\mathcal{N}}(0,1)
and
Y\sim{\mathcal{N}}(2,1)
. In addition, let
X_{\mathcal{D}}
and
Y_{\mathcal{D}}
be the projections of
X
and
Y
onto the set

{\mathcal{D}}=\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}:|x|\leq 1/2\right\},

respectively, and denote by
\mu
and
\nu
their corresponding distributions.

Let
{\mathsf{K}}
be the
{\mathcal{D}}
-constrained additive Gaussian kernel. By Proposition
1
,
\eta_{\mathsf{TV}}({\mathsf{K}})=\theta_{1}(1)
and
\eta_{\gamma}({\mathsf{K}})=\theta_{\gamma}(1)
. Thus, (
16
) implies that

\displaystyle\chi^{2}(\mu{\mathsf{K}}\|\nu{\mathsf{K}})

\displaystyle\leq\eta_{\mathsf{TV}}({\mathsf{K}})\chi^{2}(\mu\|\nu)

\displaystyle=2\theta_{1}\left(1\right)\int_{1}^{\infty}{\mathsf{E}}_{\gamma}(\mu\|\nu)+\gamma^{-3}{\mathsf{E}}_{\gamma}(\nu\|\mu)\textnormal{d}\gamma,

where the equality follows from (
1
). By the data processing inequality,

\displaystyle{\mathsf{E}}_{\gamma}(\mu\|\nu)

\displaystyle\leq{\mathsf{E}}_{\gamma}({\mathcal{N}}(0,1)\|{\mathcal{N}}(2,1))=\theta_{\gamma}(2),

\displaystyle{\mathsf{E}}_{\gamma}(\nu\|\mu)

\displaystyle\leq{\mathsf{E}}_{\gamma}({\mathcal{N}}(2,1)\|{\mathcal{N}}(0,1))=\theta_{\gamma}(2).

Therefore, Lemma
3
implies that

\displaystyle\chi^{2}(\mu{\mathsf{K}}\|\nu{\mathsf{K}})

\displaystyle\leq 2\theta_{1}\left(1\right)\int_{1}^{\infty}(1+\gamma^{-3})\theta_{\gamma}\left(2\right)\textnormal{d}\gamma

\displaystyle=0.49.

Note, however, that Corollary
1
implies that

\displaystyle\chi^{2}(\mu{\mathsf{K}}\|\nu{\mathsf{K}})

\displaystyle\leq 2\int_{1}^{\infty}\theta_{\gamma}\left(1\right)\big[{\mathsf{E}}_{\gamma}(\mu\|\nu)+\gamma^{-3}{\mathsf{E}}_{\gamma}(\nu\|\mu)\big]\textnormal{d}\gamma.

As before, the data processing inequality and Lemma
3
lead to

\displaystyle\chi^{2}(\mu{\mathsf{K}}\|\nu{\mathsf{K}})

\displaystyle\leq 2\int_{1}^{\infty}(1+\gamma^{-3})\theta_{\gamma}\left(1\right)\theta_{\gamma}\left(2\right)\textnormal{d}\gamma

\displaystyle=0.26.

In conclusion, the bound for
\chi^{2}(\mu{\mathsf{K}}\|\nu{\mathsf{K}})
obtained from (
16
) is almost twice the corresponding bound obtained from Corollary
1
.

Example 2.

Let
{\mathsf{K}}
be the binary-input binary-output channel with crossover probabilities
a,b\in[0,\frac{1}{2}]
, i.e.,

{\mathsf{K}}=\begin{bmatrix}\bar{a}&a\\
b&\bar{b}\end{bmatrix}.

Theorem
2
implies that, for all
\gamma\geq 1
,

\eta_{\gamma}({\mathsf{K}})=\max\{(\bar{a}-\gamma b)^{+},(\bar{b}-\gamma a)^{+}\},

and, in particular,

\eta_{\mathsf{TV}}({\mathsf{K}})=1-a-b.

Thus, (
16
) implies that, for any binary probability measures
\mu
and
\nu
,

\displaystyle\chi^{2}(\mu{\mathsf{K}}\|\nu{\mathsf{K}})

\displaystyle\leq(1-a-b)\chi^{2}(\mu\|\nu)

\displaystyle=2(1-a-b)\int_{1}^{\infty}\big[{\mathsf{E}}_{\gamma}(\mu\|\nu)+\gamma^{-3}{\mathsf{E}}_{\gamma}(\nu\|\mu)\big]\textnormal{d}\gamma,

where the equality comes from the integral representation (
1
). In particular, for
a=0.1
,
b=0.4
,
\mu={\mathsf{Bernoulli}}(0.1)
, and
\nu={\mathsf{Bernoulli}}(0.4)
, we obtain

\chi^{2}(\mu{\mathsf{K}}\|\nu{\mathsf{K}})\leq 0.19.

On the other hand, Corollary
1
renders

\displaystyle\chi^{2}(\mu{\mathsf{K}}\|\nu{\mathsf{K}})

\displaystyle\leq 2\int_{1}^{\frac{\bar{b}}{a}\vee\frac{\bar{a}}{b}}\left[(\bar{a}-\gamma b)^{+}\vee(\bar{b}-\gamma a)^{+}\right]\big[{\mathsf{E}}_{\gamma}(\mu\|\nu)+\gamma^{-3}{\mathsf{E}}_{\gamma}(\nu\|\mu)\big]\textnormal{d}\gamma.

For our particular choice of
a
,
b
,
\mu
, and
\nu
, we obtain

\chi^{2}(\mu{\mathsf{K}}\|\nu{\mathsf{K}})\leq 0.17.

Hence, as in the previous example, Corollary
1
produces a tighter estimate for
\chi^{2}(\mu{\mathsf{K}}\|\nu{\mathsf{K}})
than (
16
).

III
LDP as the Contraction of
{\mathsf{E}}_{\gamma}
-Divergence

In this section we establish the equivalence between the notions of local differential privacy (LDP) and contraction under
{\mathsf{E}}_{\gamma}
-divergence. We apply this equivalence in a plug-and-play manner to obtain estimates for the cost of privacy in the minimax risk, Bayesian risk, and binary hypothesis testing settings. Motivated by the equivalence between LDP and contraction under
{\mathsf{E}}_{\gamma}
-divergence, we obtain these estimates by bounding the cost of privacy directly in terms of
{\mathsf{E}}_{\gamma}
-divergence.

A privacy mechanism is a (potentially random) mapping from a given set
{\mathcal{D}}
into another set
{\mathcal{W}}
. As a result, it is customary to represent privacy mechanisms as Markov kernels
{\mathsf{K}}:{\mathcal{D}}\to{\mathcal{P}}({\mathcal{W}})
. In this context, LDP can be expressed as follows.

Definition 2

(
[
47
,
40
]
)
.

Let
\varepsilon\geq 0
and
\delta\in[0,1]
. A privacy mechanism
{\mathsf{K}}:{\mathcal{D}}\to{\mathcal{P}}({\mathcal{W}})
is
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-LDP if

\sup_{x_{1},x_{2}\in{\mathcal{D}}}\sup_{A\subset{\mathcal{W}}}\left[{\mathsf{K}}(A|x_{1})-e^{\varepsilon}{\mathsf{K}}(A|x_{2})\right]\leq\delta.

We let
{\mathcal{Q}}_{\varepsilon,\delta}
be the family of all Markov kernels that are
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-LDP. An
(\varepsilon,0)
-LDP mechanism is called
\varepsilon
-LDP.

Observe that, by (
7
), a Markov kernel
{\mathsf{K}}
is
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-LDP if and only if

\sup_{x_{1},x_{2}\in{\mathcal{D}}}{\mathsf{E}}_{\gamma}({\mathsf{K}}(\cdot|x_{1})\|{\mathsf{K}}(\cdot|x_{2}))\leq\delta.

Thus, an immediate application of Theorem
2
shows that
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-LDP is
equivalent
to contraction under
{\mathsf{E}}_{\gamma}
-divergence.

Theorem 3
.

A mechanism
{\mathsf{K}}
is
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-LDP if and only if
\eta_{e^{\varepsilon}}({\mathsf{K}})\leq\delta
, i.e.,

{\mathsf{E}}_{e^{\varepsilon}}(\mu{\mathsf{K}}\|\nu{\mathsf{K}})\leq\delta{\mathsf{E}}_{e^{\varepsilon}}(\mu\|\nu),\quad\forall\mu,\nu.

We note that Duchi et al.
[
20
]
showed that if
{\mathsf{K}}
is
\varepsilon
-LDP then

D_{{\mathsf{KL}}}(\mu{\mathsf{K}}\|\nu{\mathsf{K}})\leq 2(e^{\varepsilon}-1)^{2}{\mathsf{TV}}^{2}(\mu,\nu).

(18)

From this result, they concluded that
\varepsilon
-LDP acts as a contraction on the space of probability measures. Theorem
3
makes this observation precise by showing that
\varepsilon
-LDP is in fact equivalent to contraction under
{\mathsf{E}}_{e^{\varepsilon}}
-divergence. Indeed, according to Theorem
3
, a privacy mechanism
{\mathsf{K}}
is
\varepsilon
-LDP if and only if
{\mathsf{E}}_{e^{\varepsilon}}(\mu{\mathsf{K}}\|\nu{\mathsf{K}})=0
for any distributions
\mu
and
\nu
. An example of a Markov kernel satisfying the latter property is the randomized response mechanism, as described next.

Example 3.

Let
{\mathcal{D}}={\mathcal{W}}=\{0,1\}
and
\varepsilon\geq 0
. The randomized response mechanism
[
97
]
, denoted by
{\mathsf{K}}^{\varepsilon}_{\mathsf{RR}}
, is the mechanism implemented by the binary symmetric channel with crossover probability
\omega_{\varepsilon}\coloneqq(1+e^{\varepsilon})^{-1}
. It is known that
{\mathsf{K}}^{\varepsilon}_{\mathsf{RR}}
is
\varepsilon
-LDP. For the sake of illustration, we verify this fact below using Theorem
3
.

Let
\mu={\mathsf{Bernoulli}}(p)
and
\nu={\mathsf{Bernoulli}}(q)
with
p,q\in[0,1]
. Observe that
\mu{\mathsf{K}}^{\varepsilon}_{\mathsf{RR}}={\mathsf{Bernoulli}}(p*\omega_{\varepsilon})
and
Q{\mathsf{K}}^{\varepsilon}_{\mathsf{RR}}={\mathsf{Bernoulli}}(q*\omega_{\varepsilon})
where

a*b\coloneqq a(1-b)+(1-a)b.

It is straightforward to verify that, for any
p,q
,

\displaystyle p*\omega_{\varepsilon}-e^{\varepsilon}q*\omega_{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle\leq 0,

\displaystyle 1-p*\omega_{\varepsilon}-e^{\varepsilon}(1-q*\omega_{\varepsilon})

\displaystyle\leq 0.

The definition of the
{\mathsf{E}}_{\gamma}
-divergence (
6
) implies that

{\mathsf{E}}_{e^{\varepsilon}}(\mu{\mathsf{K}}^{\varepsilon}_{\mathsf{RR}}\|\nu{\mathsf{K}}^{\varepsilon}_{\mathsf{RR}})=0.

Thus, by Theorem
3
, we conclude that
{\mathsf{K}}^{\varepsilon}_{\mathsf{RR}}
is
\varepsilon
-LDP.

A generalization of this mechanism for
|{\mathcal{D}}|=k\geq 2
has been reported in the literature (see, e.g.,
[
98
,
42
]
). Specifically, the so-called
k
-ary randomized response mechanism
{\mathsf{K}}^{\varepsilon}_{\mathsf{kRR}}:{\mathcal{D}}\to{\mathcal{P}}({\mathcal{D}})
is defined by

{\mathsf{K}}^{\varepsilon}_{\mathsf{kRR}}(z|x)=\begin{cases}\frac{e^{\varepsilon}}{k-1+e^{\varepsilon}}&z=x,\\
\frac{1}{k-1+e^{\varepsilon}}&z\neq x.\end{cases}

Using an argument similar to the one in the paragraph above, the reader can verify that
{\mathsf{K}}^{\varepsilon}_{\mathsf{kRR}}
is
\varepsilon
-LDP.

For each
\varepsilon\geq 0
and
\delta\in[0,1]
, we define

\varphi(\varepsilon,\delta)\coloneqq 1-(1-\delta)e^{-\varepsilon}.

By combining (
14
) and Theorem
3
, we conclude that if
{\mathsf{K}}
is
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-LDP, i.e.,
{\mathsf{K}}\in{\mathcal{Q}}_{\varepsilon,\delta}
, then

D_{f}(\mu{\mathsf{K}}\|\nu{\mathsf{K}})\leq\varphi(\varepsilon,\delta)D_{f}(\mu\|\nu)\qquad\forall\mu,\nu.

(19)

This direct consequence of Theorem
3
reveals a remarkable structural property of LDP mechanisms: any LDP mechanism is contractive under
all

f
-divergences.

In the sequel we focus on two multi-user settings which are common in the literature. Assume there are
n
users, each in possession of a datapoint
X_{i}\in\mathcal{X}
,
i\in[n]
. Furthermore, assume that the users wish to apply a mechanism
{\mathsf{K}}_{i}
that generates a privatized version of
X_{i}
, denoted by
Z_{i}\in\mathcal{Z}_{i}
.

III-
1
Non-interactive setting

The collection of mechanisms
\{{\mathsf{K}}_{i}\}
is said to be
non-interactive
if the distribution of
Z_{i}
is entirely determined by
X_{i}
and independent of
(X_{j},Z_{j})
for
j\neq i
. When all users apply the same mechanism
{\mathsf{K}}
, we can view
Z^{n}\coloneqq(Z_{1},\ldots,Z_{n})
as independent applications of
{\mathsf{K}}
to each
X_{i}
. In this case, the overall mechanism is the
n
-fold tensor power of
{\mathsf{K}}
, denoted by
{\mathsf{K}}^{\otimes n}
.

III-
2
Sequentially interactive setting

The collection of mechanisms
\{{\mathsf{K}}_{i}\}
is said to be
sequentially interactive

[
20
]
if the distribution of
Z_{i}
depends on
X_{i}
, the datapoint in possession of user
i
, and
Z_{1},\ldots,Z_{i-1}
, the output of the
i-1
previous mechanisms. Note that
K_{i}
is a Markov kernel with domain
\mathcal{D}=\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Z}_{1}\times\cdots\times\mathcal{Z}_{i-1}
.
In this case, we denote the overall mechanism by
{\mathsf{K}}^{n}
.

Next, we extend (
19
) for non-interactive mechanisms. For each
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
\varepsilon\geq 0
, and
\delta\in[0,1]
, we define

\varphi_{n}(\varepsilon,\delta)\coloneqq 1-e^{-n\varepsilon}(1-\delta)^{n}.

Fix an
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-LDP mechanism
{\mathsf{K}}
and consider the corresponding non-interactive mechanism
{\mathsf{K}}^{\otimes n}
. By combining Lemma
2
and Theorem
3
, we obtain that

\eta_{f}({\mathsf{K}}^{\otimes n})\leq\varphi_{n}(\varepsilon,\delta),

where
f
is any convex function with
f(1)=0
. From this inequality and (
14
), we conclude that

D_{f}(\mu{\mathsf{K}}^{\otimes n}\|\nu{\mathsf{K}}^{\otimes n})\leq\varphi_{n}(\varepsilon,\delta)D_{f}(\mu\|\nu)\qquad\forall\mu,\nu,

(20)

which generalizes (
19
) as desired. In the rest of this section we rely on the results derived so far to estimate the cost of local differential privacy in some statistical settings.

III-A

Private Minimax Risk

Let
{\mathcal{P}}\subseteq{\mathcal{P}}({\mathcal{X}})
be a family of distributions, let
\vartheta
be a parameter space, and let
\theta:{\mathcal{P}}\to\vartheta
be a function assigning a parameter to each distribution in
\mathcal{P}
. Also, let
X^{n}=(X_{1},\dots,X_{n})
be independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) samples drawn from a distribution
P
with parameter
\theta(P)
. We assume that each user possesses a sample
X_{i}
and applies a sequentially interactive privacy-preserving mechanism
{\mathsf{K}}_{i}
to obtain
Z_{i}
. Given the sequences
\{Z_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
, the goal is to estimate
\theta(P)
through an estimator
\Psi:{\mathcal{Z}}^{n}\to\vartheta
.
The quality of such estimator is assessed by a semi-metric
\ell:\vartheta\times\vartheta\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
and is used to define the private minimax risk

\mathcal{R}_{n}({\mathcal{P}},\ell,\varepsilon,\delta)\coloneqq\inf_{{\mathsf{K}}_{i}\in{\mathcal{Q}}_{\varepsilon,\delta}}\inf_{\Psi}\sup_{P\in{\mathcal{P}}}{\mathbb{E}}[\ell(\Psi(Z^{n}),\theta(P))],

where the first infimum is taken over all
{\mathsf{K}}_{1},\ldots,{\mathsf{K}}_{n}
which are
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-LDP. The quantity
R_{n}({\mathcal{P}},\ell,\varepsilon,\delta)
uniformly characterizes the optimal rate of private statistical estimation over the family
{\mathcal{P}}
using the best possible estimator and privacy-preserving mechanisms in
{\mathcal{Q}}_{\varepsilon,\delta}
. In the absence of privacy constraints (i.e.,
Z^{n}=X^{n}
), we denote the minimax risk by
\mathcal{R}_{n}({\mathcal{P}},\ell)
.

A typical first step in deriving information-theoretic lower bounds for the minimax risk is to reduce the above estimation problem to a testing problem via Le Cam’s, Fano’s, or Assouad’s method
[
99
,
100
,
101
]
. For ease of exposition, in the sequel we focus only on Le Cam’s method, which relies on binary hypothesis testing, although a similar reasoning could be applied to multiple hypothesis testing settings (e.g., Fano’s and Assouad’s methods). The canonical binary hypothesis testing problem is defined as follows: Nature chooses a random variable
V
uniformly at random from
\{0,1\}
, then, conditioned on
V=v
, the samples
X^{n}
are drawn i.i.d. from
P_{v}\in\mathcal{P}
, denoted by
X^{n}\sim P^{\otimes n}_{v}
. It is well-known
[
99
,
100
,
101
]
that if
\ell(\theta(P_{0}),\theta(P_{1}))\geq 2\tau
for some
\tau>0
, then

\mathcal{R}_{n}({\mathcal{P}},\ell)\geq\tau\mathsf{P}_{\mathsf{e}}(V|X^{n}),

where
\mathsf{P}_{\mathsf{e}}(V|X^{n})
denotes the probability of error in guessing
V
given
X^{n}
. In its simplest form, Le Cam’s method relies on the inequality

\mathsf{P}_{\mathsf{e}}(V|X^{n})\geq\frac{1}{2}\left[1-{\mathsf{TV}}(P^{\otimes n}_{0},P^{\otimes n}_{1})\right],

see, e.g.,
[
99
, Lemma 1]
or
[
101
, Theorem 2.2]
, which yields the following lower bound for the minimax risk

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{n}({\mathcal{P}},\ell)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\tau}{2}\left[1-{\mathsf{TV}}(P^{\otimes n}_{0},P^{\otimes n}_{1})\right]

(21)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\tau}{2}\left[1-\sqrt{\frac{n}{2}D_{\mathsf{KL}}(P_{0}\|P_{1})}\right],

(22)

where the second inequality follows from Pinsker’s inequality
2
2

2

Observe that Pinsker’s inequality is ineffective when
D_{\mathsf{KL}}(P_{0}\|P_{1})
is sufficiently large. In that case, Pinsker’s inequality could be replaced by the Bretagnolle-Huber inequality
[
102
]

{\mathsf{TV}}(P_{0},P_{1})\leq\sqrt{1-e^{-D_{{\mathsf{KL}}}(P_{0}\|P_{1})}},

or Vajda’s inequality
[
103
]

\log\left(\frac{1+{\mathsf{TV}}(P_{0},P_{1})}{1-{\mathsf{TV}}(P_{0},P_{1})}\right)-\frac{2{\mathsf{TV}}(P_{0},P_{1})}{1+{\mathsf{TV}}(P_{0},P_{1})}\leq D_{{\mathsf{KL}}}(P_{0}\|P_{1}).

and the chain rule for KL-divergence.

In the presence of privacy, the estimator
\Psi
depends on
Z^{n}
which is generated upon
X^{n}
by a sequentially interactive mechanism
{\mathsf{K}}^{n}
. To derive the private counterpart of (
21
), we need to replace
P^{\otimes n}_{v}
, the marginal distribution of
X^{n}
conditioned on
V=v
, with
P^{\otimes n}_{v}{\mathsf{K}}^{n}
, the marginal distribution of
Z^{n}
conditioned on
V=v
. A lower bound for
\mathcal{R}_{n}({\mathcal{P}},\ell,\varepsilon,\delta)
is therefore obtained by deriving an upper bound for

{\mathsf{TV}}(P_{0}^{\otimes n}{\mathsf{K}}^{n},P_{1}^{\otimes n}{\mathsf{K}}^{n})
for all
{\mathsf{K}}^{n}\subset{\mathcal{Q}}_{\varepsilon,\delta}
. This strategy is implemented in the following lemma, whose proof could be found in Appendix
-F
.

Lemma 4
.

If
P_{0},P_{1}\in{\mathcal{P}}
satisfy
\ell(\theta(P_{0}),\theta(P_{1}))\geq 2\tau
for some
\tau>0
, then

\mathcal{R}_{n}({\mathcal{P}},\ell,\varepsilon,\delta)\geq\frac{\tau}{2}\left[1-\sqrt{\frac{\varphi(\varepsilon,\delta)n}{2}D_{\mathsf{KL}}(P_{0}\|P_{1})}\right],

where
\varphi(\varepsilon,\delta)\coloneqq 1-(1-\delta)e^{-\varepsilon}
.

By comparing the previous lemma with the original non-private Le Cam’s method (
22
), we observe that the effect of
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-LDP is to reduce the effective sample size from
n
to
(1-(1-\delta)e^{-\varepsilon})n
. Setting
\delta=0
and
\varepsilon<0.224
, this result strengthens Duchi et al.
[
20
, Corollary 2]
, where the effective sample size was shown to be
4\varepsilon^{2}n
for sufficiently small
\varepsilon
.

Example 4. (1-dimensional mean estimation)

For some
k>1
, we assume that
{\mathcal{P}}={\mathcal{P}}_{k}
is given by

{\mathcal{P}}_{k}\coloneqq\left\{P\in{\mathcal{P}}({\mathcal{X}}):\penalty\ |{\mathbb{E}}_{P}[X]|\leq 1,{\mathbb{E}}_{P}[|X|^{k}]\leq 1\right\}.

Consider the problem of estimating
\theta(P)={\mathbb{E}}_{P}[X]
when
\ell=\ell_{2}^{2}
, i.e., the loss is given by the the squared
\ell_{2}
metric. This problem was first studied by Duchi et al. in
[
20
, Prop. 1]
where it was shown that
\mathcal{R}_{n}({\mathcal{P}}_{k},\ell_{2}^{2},\varepsilon,0)\geq(\varepsilon^{2}n)^{-(k-1)/k}
for
\varepsilon\leq 1
. Applying our framework to this example, we obtain a similar lower bound that holds for all
\varepsilon\geq 0
and
\delta\in[0,1]
, stated below.

Corollary 2
.

For the
1
-dimensional mean estimation problem, for all
k>1
,
\varepsilon\geq 0
, and
\delta\in(0,1)
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{n}({\mathcal{P}}_{k},\ell_{2}^{2},\varepsilon,\delta)

\displaystyle\gtrsim\min\Big\{1,\left[\varphi^{2}(\varepsilon,\delta)n\right]^{-\frac{k-1}{k}}\Big\}.

(23)

It is worth instantiating this corollary, whose proof can be found in Appendix
-G
, for some special values of
k
. Consider first the usual finite variance setting, i.e.,
k=2
. In the non-private case, it is known that the sample mean has mean-squared error that scales as
n^{-1}
. According to Corollary
2
, this rate worsens to
(\varphi(\varepsilon,\delta)\sqrt{n})^{-1}
in the presence of an
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-LDP requirement. Now, consider the limiting setting in which
k\to\infty
. Observe that the moment condition
{\mathbb{E}}_{p}[|X|^{k}]\leq 1
implies the boundedness of
X
. In this case, the non-private minimax risk scales as
n^{-1}
, while Corollary
2
implies that its LDP counterpart scales as
(\varphi^{2}(\varepsilon,\delta)n)^{-1}
.

We end this section with an alternative to Lemma
4
in the non-interactive setting. Its proof, which can be found in Appendix
-H
, relies on Lemma
2
and leads to tighter bounds for certain values of
n
,
\varepsilon
, and
\delta
.

Lemma 5
.

If
P_{0},P_{1}\in{\mathcal{P}}
satisfy
\ell(\theta(P_{0}),\theta(P_{1}))\geq 2\tau
for some
\tau>0
, then

\mathcal{R}_{n}({\mathcal{P}},\ell,\varepsilon,\delta)\geq\frac{\tau}{2}\left[1-\sqrt{\frac{\varphi^{2}_{n}(\varepsilon,\delta)n}{2}D_{\mathsf{KL}}(P_{0}\|P_{1})}\right],

where
\varphi_{n}(\varepsilon,\delta)\coloneqq 1-e^{-n\varepsilon}(1-\delta)^{n}
.

III-B

Private Bayesian Risk

In the minimax setting, the worst-case parameter is considered which usually leads to over-pessimistic bounds. In practice, the parameter that incurs a worst-case risk may appear with very small probability. To capture this prior knowledge, it is reasonable to assume that the true parameter is sampled from an underlying prior distribution. In this case, we are interested in the
Bayesian risk
of the problem as described next. Let
\vartheta
be a parameter space endowed with a prior distribution
P_{\Theta}
and let
{\mathcal{P}}=\{P_{X|\Theta}(\cdot|\theta):\theta\in\vartheta\}
be a collection of parametric probability distributions over
{\mathcal{X}}
. Given an i.i.d. sequence
X^{n}
drawn from
P_{X|\Theta}
, the goal is to estimate
\Theta
from a privatized sequence
Z^{n}
via an estimator
\Psi:{\mathcal{Z}}^{n}\to\vartheta
. Throughout this section, we focus on the non-interactive setting. In this case, the private Bayesian risk is defined as

R_{n}^{\mathsf{Bayes}}(P_{\Theta},\ell,\varepsilon,\delta)\coloneqq\inf_{{\mathsf{K}}\in{\mathcal{Q}}_{\varepsilon,\delta}}\inf_{\Psi}{\mathbb{E}}[\ell(\Theta,\Psi(Z^{n}))],

(24)

where the expectation is taken with respect to the randomness of both
\Theta\sim P_{\Theta}
and
Z^{n}
. It is evident that
R_{n}^{\mathsf{Bayes}}(P_{\Theta},\ell,\varepsilon,\delta)
must depend on the prior
P_{\Theta}
. One way to quantify this dependence is through the so-called
small ball probability
of
\Theta
with respect
\ell
defined as

{\mathcal{L}}(\zeta)\coloneqq\sup_{\theta\in\vartheta}\Pr(\ell(\Theta,\theta)\leq\zeta).

Xu and Raginsky
[
18
]
showed that the non-private Bayesian risk (
Z^{n}=X^{n}
), denoted by
R_{n}^{\mathsf{Bayes}}(P_{\Theta},\ell)
, is lower bounded as

R_{n}^{\mathsf{Bayes}}(P_{\Theta},\ell)\geq\sup_{\zeta>0}\zeta\left[1-\frac{I(\Theta;X^{n})+\log 2}{\log(1/{\mathcal{L}}(\zeta))}\right].

(25)

By replacing
I(\Theta;X^{n})
with
I(\Theta;Z^{n})
in the previous inequality, an application of (
20
) directly leads to the following lower bound for
R_{n}^{\mathsf{Bayes}}(P_{\Theta},\ell,\varepsilon,\delta)
.

Corollary 3
.

In the non-interactive setting, the private Bayesian risk
R_{n}^{\mathsf{Bayes}}(P_{\Theta},\ell,\varepsilon,\delta)
is bounded below by

\sup_{\zeta>0}\zeta\left[1-\frac{\varphi_{n}(\varepsilon,\delta)I(\Theta;X^{n})+\log 2}{\log(1/{\mathcal{L}}(\zeta))}\right],

(26)

where
\varphi_{n}(\varepsilon,\delta)\coloneqq 1-e^{-n\varepsilon}(1-\delta)^{n}
.

The following theorem, whose proof can be found in Appendix
-I
, provides a lower bound for the private Bayesian risk that directly involves
{\mathsf{E}}_{\gamma}
-divergence, leading to a tighter bound than (
26
). For
\gamma\geq 0
and any pair of random variables
(A,B)\sim P_{AB}
with marginals
P_{A}
and
P_{B}
, we define their
E_{\gamma}
-information as

I_{\gamma}(A;B)\coloneqq{\mathsf{E}}_{\gamma}(P_{AB}\|P_{A}P_{B}).

Theorem 4
.

In the non-interactive setting, the private Bayesian risk
R_{n}^{\mathsf{Bayes}}(P_{\Theta},\ell,\varepsilon,\delta)
is bounded below by

\sup_{\zeta>0}\zeta\left[1-\varphi_{n}(\varepsilon,\delta)I_{e^{\varepsilon}}(\Theta;X^{n})-e^{\varepsilon}{\mathcal{L}}(\zeta)\right].

Furthermore, for
n=1
we have that

R_{1}^{\mathsf{Bayes}}(P_{\Theta},\ell,\varepsilon,\delta)\geq\sup_{\zeta>0}\zeta\left[1-\delta I_{e^{\varepsilon}}(\Theta;X)-e^{\varepsilon}{\mathcal{L}}(\zeta)\right].

We compare the bound in Theorem
4
with those of Corollary
3
in the next example.

Example 5.

Suppose
\Theta
is uniformly distributed on
[0,1]
,
P_{X|\Theta=\theta}={\mathsf{Bernoulli}}(\theta)
, and
\ell(\theta,\theta^{\prime})=|\theta-\theta^{\prime}|
. Observe that in this case
{\mathcal{L}}(\zeta)\leq\min\{2\zeta,1\}
. For ease of notation, let
\gamma=e^{\varepsilon}
. Note that

I_{\gamma}(\Theta;X^{n})=\int_{0}^{1}{\mathsf{E}}_{\gamma}(P_{X^{n}|\Theta=\theta}\|P_{X^{n}})\text{d}\theta.

A routine calculation shows that, for any
\theta\in[0,1]
,

\displaystyle P_{X^{n}|\Theta=\theta}(x^{n})

\displaystyle=\theta^{s(x^{n})}(1-\theta)^{n-s(x^{n})},

\displaystyle P_{X^{n}}(x^{n})

\displaystyle=\frac{s(x^{n})!(n-s(x^{n}))!}{(n+1)!},

where
s(x^{n})
is the number of 1’s in
x^{n}
. Given these marginal and conditional distribution, one can obtain that

I_{\gamma}(\Theta;X^{n})=\sum_{s=0}^{n}\int_{0}^{1}\left[\frac{n!\theta^{s}(1-\theta)^{n-s}}{s!(n-s)!}-\frac{\gamma}{n+1}\right]_{+}\text{d}\theta.

Plugging this equation into Theorem
4
, we arrive at a maximization problem that can be numerically solved. Similarly, we compute

I(\Theta;X^{n})=\int_{0}^{1}D_{\mathsf{KL}}(P_{X^{n}|\Theta=\theta}\|P_{X^{n}})\text{d}\theta,

plug it into Corollary
26
, and numerically solve the resulting optimization problem. In Figure
1
, we compare these two lower bounds for
\delta=10^{-4}
and
n=20
. Observe the significant advantage of Theorem
4
against Corollary
26
for small
\varepsilon
.

.

.

Although we are mainly interested in the private Bayesian risk, it is possible to obtain from the proof of Theorem

that the non-private Bayesian risk
R_{n}^{\mathsf{Bayes}}(P_{\Theta},\ell)
is bounded below by

\sup_{\gamma\geq 0}\sup_{\zeta>0}\zeta\left[1-I_{\gamma}(\Theta;X^{n})-\gamma{\mathcal{L}}(\zeta)-(1-\gamma)_{+}\right].

(27)

III-C

Private Binary Hypothesis Testing

We now turn our attention to the well-known problem of binary hypothesis testing under local differential privacy constraints.
Assume that we observe
n
i.i.d. samples
X^{n}
drawn from a distribution
Q\in{\mathcal{P}}({\mathcal{X}})
. We assume further that each
X_{i}
is mapped to
Z_{i}
via a privacy-preserving mechanism
{\mathsf{K}}\in{\mathcal{Q}}_{\varepsilon,\delta}
, i.e., we assume a non-interactive setting with
{\mathsf{K}}_{i}={\mathsf{K}}
. Given
Z^{n}
, the goal is to distinguish between the null hypothesis
H_{0}:Q=P_{0}
and the alternative hypothesis
H_{1}:Q=P_{1}
. Let
T
be a binary statistic generated from a randomized decision rule
P_{T|Z^{n}}:{\mathcal{Z}}^{n}\to\mathcal{P}(\{0,1\})
, where
T=1
indicates that
H_{0}
is rejected. The type I and type II error probabilities corresponding to this statistic are given by
\Pr(T=1|H_{0})
and
\Pr(T=0|H_{1})
, respectively. To capture the optimal tradeoff between type I and type II error probabilities, it is customary to define

\beta^{\varepsilon,\delta}_{n}(\alpha)\coloneqq\inf_{{\mathsf{K}}\in{\mathcal{Q}}_{\varepsilon,\delta}}\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}P_{T|Z^{n}}:\\
\Pr(T=1|H_{0})\leq\alpha\end{subarray}}\Pr(T=0|H_{1}),

The following corollary, whose proof relies on (
19
) and can be found in Appendix
-J
, provides an asymptotic lower bound for
\beta_{n}^{\varepsilon,\delta}(\alpha)
.

Corollary 4
.

For any
\varepsilon\geq 0
and
\delta\in[0,1]
, we have that

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log\beta_{n}^{\varepsilon,\delta}(\alpha)\geq-\varphi(\varepsilon,\delta)D_{\mathsf{KL}}(P_{0}\|P_{1}).

(28)

Kairouz et al.
[
42
, Sec. 3]
proved a similar result which is optimal for sufficiently small (albeit unspecified)
\varepsilon
and
\delta=0
. Recall that the Chernoff-Stein lemma
[
104
, Thm. 11.8.3]
establishes that
D_{\mathsf{KL}}(P_{0}\|P_{1})
is the asymptotic exponential decay rate of
\beta_{n}(\alpha)
in the absence of privacy constraints. Thus, the above corollary exhibits, once again, a reduction of the effective sample size from
n
to
\varphi(\varepsilon,\delta)n
in the presence of an
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-LDP constraint.

We end this section with a remark. In the spirit of Theorem
4
, it is possible to investigate
\alpha\mapsto\beta^{\varepsilon,\delta}_{n}(\alpha)
directly in terms of
{\mathsf{E}}_{\gamma}
-divergence, for instance, by considering the dual representation of
{\mathsf{E}}_{\gamma}
-divergence. This method can potentially lead to a tighter lower bound than Corollary
4
and is left as future work. The details of this approach for the non-private case can be found in the pioneering work of
[
13
]
.

IV
Differentially Private Online Learning

In this section, we use Theorem
2
to develop a framework for
quantifying the DP guarantees of general online learning algorithms.
Before delving into the technical results, we first describe the framework of online learning studied in this section and give the definition of central differential privacy.

IV-A

Online Learning Algorithms

Let
{\mathcal{W}}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
denote a parameter space, e.g., the coefficients of a linear regression model, and let
\Pi_{\mathcal{W}}(\cdot)
denote the projection operator onto
{\mathcal{W}}
. We describe typical
online learning
algorithms in the next definition.

Definition 3

(Online Algorithm)
.

An online learning algorithm
{\mathcal{M}}
proceeds as follows. A learner initiates the algorithm by taking a random point
W_{1}
from
{\mathcal{W}}
. Once
W_{1}
is chosen, a convex cost function
{\mathsf{f}}_{1}:{\mathcal{W}}\to\mathbb{R}
is revealed, implying that the cost associated with
W_{1}
is
{\mathsf{f}}_{1}(W_{1})
. Upon observing
{\mathsf{f}}_{t}
and
W_{t}
, the learner at time
t+1
chooses
W_{t+1}
according to an update rule
\Psi_{{\mathsf{f}}_{t}}:\mathcal{W}\to\mathcal{W}
, specifically,

W_{t+1}=\Pi_{{\mathcal{W}}}\left(\Psi_{{\mathsf{f}}_{t}}(W_{t})\right).

After
n
iterations, the algorithm outputs
W_{n+1}
. We say that
{\mathcal{M}}
is randomized if, for
\{{\mathsf{f}}_{t}\}
and
W_{t}
fixed ,
W_{t+1}
is a random variable on
{\mathcal{W}}
.

Letting
{\mathsf{F}}
be the collection of all possible convex cost functions, an online learning algorithm can thus be viewed as the mapping

{\mathcal{M}}:{\mathsf{F}}^{n}\to{\mathcal{W}},

given by

{\mathcal{M}}(\{{\mathsf{f}}_{1},\dots,{\mathsf{f}}_{n}\})=W_{n+1}.

For brevity, we denote
\{\mathsf{f}_{1},\ldots,\mathsf{f}_{n}\}
by
\{{\mathsf{f}}_{t}\}
. It is worth noting that in this setting we assume that the collection of cost functions
\{{\mathsf{f}}_{1},\dots,{\mathsf{f}}_{n}\}
is fixed before the algorithm is started—often referred to as the
oblivious setting
in the literature
[
49
, Section 5.5]
. We also assume that
\Psi_{{\mathsf{f}}_{t}}
, the update function at time
t
, only depends on
{\mathsf{f}}_{t}
and not on the previous cost functions
{\mathsf{f}}_{1},\dots,{\mathsf{f}}_{t-1}
.

The goal of the learner is to minimize the
regret
, i.e., the difference between the cumulative cost of the algorithm’s choices and that of the best fixed (offline) solution in hindsight. Specifically, the regret of a randomized algorithm
{\mathcal{M}}
after
n
iterations is defined as

{\mathbb{E}}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{n}\mathsf{f}_{t}(W_{t})\right]-\min_{w\in{\mathcal{W}}}\sum_{t=1}^{n}\mathsf{f}_{t}(w),

(29)

where the expectation is taken over the algorithm’s randomness.
Typically, for online learning algorithms, a
sublinear
regret is sought, as it implies that asymptotically an online algorithm performs almost as well as the optimal solution in hindsight.

In many practical scenarios, the cost functions
\{{\mathsf{f}}_{t}\}
might leak private information about the learner. For example, in offline setting (cf. Section
IV-B
)
{\mathsf{f}}_{t}(w)=\ell(w,x_{t})
where
x_{t}
is a ground truth observation (e.g., stock price) at time
t
and
\ell
is loss function. If the loss function is linear, then
{\mathsf{f}}_{t}(w)
reveals significant information about the observation
x_{t}
. Other examples of privacy leakage in online algorithms are listed in
[
105
,
106
]
. As such, we focus on privacy attacks aimed to infer information about the cost function
{\mathsf{f}}_{i}
for some
i\in[n]
upon observing the output of the algorithm, i.e.,
{\mathcal{M}}(\{{\mathsf{f}}_{t}\})=W_{n+1}
. Thus, our goal is to design online algorithms such that
W_{n+1}
does not reveal significant information about any single cost function in
\{{\mathsf{f}}_{t}\}
. The following definition formalizes this goal. We say that two collection of cost functions
\{{\mathsf{f}}_{t}\}
and
\{{\mathsf{f}}^{\prime}_{t}\}
are
neighboring
at index
i\in[n]
, if
{\mathsf{f}}_{t}={\mathsf{f}}^{\prime}_{t}
for all
t\in[n]\backslash{i}
and
{\mathsf{f}}_{i}\neq{\mathsf{f}}^{\prime}_{i}
. We denote this by
\{{\mathsf{f}}_{t}\}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle i}}{{\sim}}\{{\mathsf{f}}^{\prime}_{t}\}
.

Definition 4

(
[
73
]
)
.

Given
\varepsilon\geq 0
and
\delta\in[0,1]
, a randomized online algorithm
{\mathcal{M}}
is said to be
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-DP at index
i\in[n]
, if

\sup_{\{{\mathsf{f}}_{t}\}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle i}}{{\sim}}\{{\mathsf{f}}^{\prime}_{t}\}}{\mathsf{E}}_{e^{\varepsilon}}(\mu_{n+1}\|\mu^{\prime}_{n+1})\leq\delta,

where
\mu_{n+1}
and
\mu^{\prime}_{n+1}
are the distributions of
{\mathcal{M}}(\{{\mathsf{f}}_{t}\})
and
{\mathcal{M}}(\{{\mathsf{f}}^{\prime}_{t}\})
, respectively. We say that
{\mathcal{M}}
is
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-DP if it is
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-DP at index
i
for all
i\in[n]
.

Notice that in light of the definition of
{\mathsf{E}}_{\gamma}
-divergence in (
7
), we can equivalently say
{\mathcal{M}}
is
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-DP if

\Pr({\mathcal{M}}(\{{\mathsf{f}}_{t}\})\in A)-e^{\varepsilon}\Pr({\mathcal{M}}(\{{\mathsf{f}}^{\prime}_{t}\})\in A)\leq\delta,

for any
A\subset{\mathcal{W}}
and any pair of neighboring collections of cost functions
\{{\mathsf{f}}_{t}\}
and
\{{\mathsf{f}}^{\prime}_{t}\}
. While this is a more widely used definition in DP literature, we will use its equivalent form, given in Definition
4
, to emphasize the connection between
{\mathsf{E}}_{\gamma}
-divergence and DP.

Remark 3
.

The above definition of DP is specific to online learning. Here, a pair of neighboring collections of cost functions is considered rather than the "neighboring datasets" used in offline learning scenarios. Definition
4
is also the standard notion of DP for online learning algorithm considered in
[
28
,
27
,
29
,
26
,
107
]
. Nevertheless, Definition
4
differs from previous works on differentially private online algorithms in that privacy is ensured with respect to only the last parameter
W_{n+1}
rather than the entire set of parameters
\{W_{1},\dots,W_{n+1}\}
.

IV-B

Empirical Risk Minimization

Here, we consider an offline learning setting, namely empirical risk minimization (ERM) which is a fundamental problem in machine learning. Given a dataset

A simple algorithm for approximating the solution of this problem can be implemented with one pass of stochastic gradient descent (SGD) over the data set. This procedure starts with a random point
