On constrained matchings, stable under random preferences
Boris Pittel

Abstract
. Colloquially, there are two groups, n n men and n n women, each man (woman) ranking women (men) as potential marriage partners. A complete matching is called stable if no unmatched pair prefer each other to their partners in the matching. If some pairs are not admissible, then such a matching may not exist, but a properly defined partial stable matching exists always, and all such matchings involve the same, equi-numerous, groups of men and women. Earlier we proved that, for the complete, random, preference lists, with high probability (whp) the total number of complete stable matchings is, roughly, of order n 1 / 2 n^{1/2} , at least. Here we consider the case that the preference lists are still complete, but a generic pair (man,woman) is admissible with probability p p , independently of all other n 2 − 1 n^{2}-1 pairs. It is shown that the expected number of complete stable matchings tends to 0 0 if, roughly, p < log 2 ⁡ n n p<\tfrac{\log^{2}n}{n} and to infinity if p > log 2 ⁡ n n p>\tfrac{\log^{2}n}{n} . We show that whp: (a) there exists a complete stable matching if p > ( 9 / 4 ) ​ log 2 ⁡ n n p>(9/4)\tfrac{\log^{2}n}{n} , (b) the number of unmatched men and women is bounded if p > log 2 ⁡ n n p>\tfrac{\log^{2}n}{n} , and (c) this number grows as a fractional power of n n for p < log 2 ⁡ n n p<\tfrac{\log^{2}n}{n} .

1.
Introduction and results

The classical model of a stable matching problem is sometimes formulated in colloquial terms of marriages. An instance of such a problem involves
n
men and
n
women, with every member ranking all, or some of
the members of opposite sex as potential marriage partners. A complete matching of two sets, if any exists, is called stable
if no unmatched pair (man, woman) prefer each other to their partners under matching. In a classic paper
[
5
]
,
Gale and Shapley proved that when all ranking lists are complete, of length
n
that is, at least one stable matching always exists. They proved this remarkable result by analyzing an equally remarkable algorithm: men propose to women in simultaneous rounds, and each woman temporarily accepts the best proposal, by selecting her favorite man among the current proponents and her current suitor if she has one already, while each of the rejected men proposes, in the next round, to a favorite woman among women whom he hasn’t proposed to yet. The algorithm terminates once each woman holds a proposal; the resulting matching is stable. And of course the roles can be reversed: women propose and men choose. It is well known (Gusfield and Irving
[
6
]
) that the “men propose/women choose” algorithm delivers a men-optimal stable matching: at every other stable matching each wife is not higher on her husband’s preference list than in the terminal matching for this algorithm. And likewise, the “women propose/men choose” algorithm delivers a women-optimal stable matching.

A decade later, McVitie and Wilson
[
9
]
suggested a sequential algorithm: men propose and women choose like in Gale-Shapley process, but the proposals are made in sequence, one proposal at a step. This fundamemtal algorithm determines the same, men-optimal, stable matching, as the Gale-Shapley algorithm does. Soon after Wilson
[
14
]
proved that the average running time, i.e. the
total number of proposals by men is bounded by
nH_{n}
,
H_{n}=\sum_{j\in[n]}\tfrac{1}{j}
, if it is assumed that all
2n
preference lists are uniformly random, independent permutations of the opposite sex members. The ingenious proof was based on observation that the total number of proposals is
stochastically dominated
by the duration of a classic
coupon collecting
process: at each step a ball is put uniformly at random at one of
n
distinguishable boxes, until each box is non-empty. Knuth
[
7
]
undertook a systematic study of the stable matchings, showing in particular that the expected running time of the McVitie-Wilson
algorithm is actually asymptotic to
nH_{n}\sim n\log n
. Consequently, the average rank of the wife from the men-optimal matching is asymptotic to
\log n
.

The book
[
7
]
turned out to be highly influential, thanks to a list of thought-provoking, open problems. One of them was to estimate the expected value of
S_{n}
, the total number of stable matchings for the random instance of the matching problem. Using an inclusion-exclusion formula followed-up by an ingenious interpretation of each alternating term as a value of a multi-dimensional integral, Knuth found that

(1.1)

\mathbb{E}[S_{n}]=n!P_{n},\quad P_{n}=\int\limits_{\mathbb{x},\mathbb{y}\in[0,1]^{n}}\prod_{1\leq i\neq j\leq n}(1-x_{i}y_{j})\,d\mathbb{x}\,d\mathbb{y}.

The roots of this remarkable formula are clear:
n!
is the total number of bijections between the set of men and the set of women, so
P_{n}
has to be, and it is, the probability that a generic bijection is a stable matching under the independently uniform preference lists.

We used the equation (
1.1
) in
[
10
]
to prove that

, as
n\to\infty
. Twenty years later
Lennon and the author
[
8

proved an analogous, more complex, formula for

, and it was used to show that

\mathbb{E}[S_{n}^{2}]\sim(e^{-2}+(2e^{3})^{-2})(n\log n)^{2}
, implying that
S_{n}
exceeds

with probability

>\bigl(1+(2e)^{-1}\bigr)^{-1}
. We also proved
[
11

that whp

is of order
\sqrt{n\log^{-1}n}
, at least.

There is an alternative proof of (

and

,
for example.

When some of the preference lists are incomplete, so that some pairs

Recently Stoyan Dimitrov and Adam Hammett
[
2

posed a problem of stable matchings with incomplete random preference lists. Supposing that that the preference list of each man (woman) consists of
k

k

uniformly random and independent, what is the probability that a complete stable matching exists? What is the threshold value of
k(n)
for likely existence of a complete stable matching? Stoyan and Adam’s question
jolted the author’s memory: it was proved in
[
11

that in the case of unrestricted preference lists the maximum rank
of a wife, when men propose and women select, is likely to be of order
\log^{2}n
.
However, contrary to a casual remark in
[

]

is of order
\log^{2}n
.

Unlike the above model, in this paper we continue to stay with the case of complete preference lists, though this time the prospective pairs must each pass an “admissibility” test. (We leave it to the reader to come up with their own colloquial interpretation of such a test. Romeo and Juliet story, as a poetic inspiration?) For a chance of sharp analysis, we need to preserve homogeneity of the model and mutual independence of admissibility tests for individual pairs. To this end, we make the simplest assumption, namely that the results of the tests for various pairs form a sequence of independent trials with success (admissibility) probability equal to a given
p\in(0,1)
. What is the threshold value
p=p(n)
for likely existence of a complete stable matching?

The main result in this paper is that
p(n)
is exactly of order
\tfrac{\log^{2}n}{n}
. For the critical
p(n)
, the likely length of a shortest preference list is of order
\tfrac{\log^{2}n}{\log\log n}
, at least, which makes it plausible that the critical
k(n)
for Stoyan and Adam’s question is also of order
\tfrac{\log^{2}n}{\log\log n}
, at least.

To put this research into perspective, a classic
result of Erdős and Rényi
[
3

is that the critical value

for likely existence of a perfect matching in the random bipartite graph on

\tfrac{\log n}{n}
.

(s,v_{1},\dots,v_{\ell-1})
is
s^{\ell-1}
, implying that

(1.9)

f_{\ell}(s,v_{1},\dots,v_{\ell-1})=s^{\ell-1}\,\mathbb{I}\Bigl(\sum_{j<\ell}v_{j}\leq 1\text{ and }\max_{j\leq\ell}v_{j}\leq\tfrac{1}{s}\Bigr),

where
v_{\ell}:=1-\sum_{j<\ell}v_{j}
. Now,
(\ell-1)!\cdot\mathbb{I}\Bigl(\sum_{j<\ell}v_{j}\leq 1\Bigr)
is the joint density of
L_{1},\dots,L_{\ell-1}
, the lengths of the first
(\ell-1)
consecutive intervals obtained by selecting
(\ell-1)
points
independently and uniformly at random in
[0,1]
. So, integrating (
1.9
) over
v_{1},\dots,v_{\ell-1}
, we obtain:
the density of
S_{\ell}
is given by

(1.10)

f_{\ell}(s)=\tfrac{s^{\ell-1}}{(\ell-1)!}\cdot\mathbb{I}\bigl(\max_{j\in[\ell]}L_{j}\leq\tfrac{1}{s}\bigr),

cf. Feller
[
4
]
. Dropping the constraint
\max_{j\leq\ell}v_{j}\leq\tfrac{1}{s}
in (

), we obtain a crucial
inequality

(1.11)

f_{\ell}(s,v_{1},\dots,v_{\ell-1})\leq\tfrac{s^{\ell-1}}{(\ell-1)!}\cdot g_{\ell}(v_{1},\dots,v_{\ell-1}),

where
g_{\ell}(v_{1},\dots,v_{\ell-1})
is the density of
L_{1},\dots,L_{\ell-1}
.

To use this connection to
L_{1},\dots,L_{\ell}
, we will need asymptotic properties of
\L_{\ell}^{+}=\max_{j\in[\ell]}L_{j}
and
U_{\ell}:=\sum_{j\in[\ell]}L_{j}^{2}
:

Lemma 1.2
.

As
\ell\to\infty
, in probability
\tfrac{L_{\ell}^{+}}{\ell^{-1}\log\ell}\to 1
, and
\ell\,U_{\ell}\to 2
. More sharply, for every
\rho>0
, we have

\mathbb{P}\bigl(L_{\ell}^{+}\leq\ell^{-1}(\log\tfrac{\ell}{\log\ell}-\rho)\bigr)=O(\ell^{-d}),\quad\forall\,d\in(0,e^{\rho}-1).,

and for
\omega(\ell)\to\infty
however slowly

\mathbb{P}\bigl(L_{\ell}^{+}\leq\ell^{-1}\log(\ell\omega(\ell))\bigr)=(1+o(1))\exp\bigl(-\tfrac{1+o(1)}{\omega(\ell)}\bigr)\to 1,

while, for every
\delta<\tfrac{1}{3}
,

\mathbb{P}\biggl(\big|\tfrac{\ell\,U_{\ell}}{2}-1\big|\geq\ell^{-\delta}\biggr)=O\bigl(e^{-\Theta(\ell^{1/3-\delta})}\bigr).

Note.
The proof of Lemma
1.2
, is based on the following fact:
\{L_{j}:j\in[\ell]\}
has the same distribution as
\bigl\{W_{j}/\sum_{i\in[\ell]}W_{i}:j\in[\ell]\bigr\}
, where
W_{1},\dots,W_{\ell}
are i.i.d. exponentials, with the common parameter
1
, say. The large deviation estimate for
\ell\,U_{\ell}
was proved in Pittel
[
12
]
.

1.3.1.
Proof of Theorem
1.1
, part
(i)
.

Since
\mathbb{E}[S_{n}]
is increasing with
p
, it suffices to consider
p=p(n)=\tfrac{\log^{2}n}{n}\bigl(1-\alpha\tfrac{\log\log n}{\log n}\bigr)
,
\alpha>2
. By (
1.3
),
\mathbb{E}[S_{n}]=n!P_{n},
where

P_{n}=p^{n}\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\int\limits_{\mathbb{x},\mathbb{y}\in[0,1]^{n}}\prod_{1\leq i\neq j\leq n}(1-px_{i}y_{j})\,d\mathbb{x}\,d\mathbb{y}.

So, using
1-\xi\leq\exp(-\xi-\xi^{2}/2)
,
\xi\in(0,1)
, and denoting
s_{j}=\sum_{i\neq j}x_{i}
,
t_{j}=\sum_{i\neq j}x_{i}^{2}
, we have

(1.12)

P_{n}\leq p^{n}\int\limits_{\mathbb{x}\in[0,1]^{n}}\biggl(\prod_{j\in[n]}\int_{0}^{1}\exp\bigl(-pys_{j}-\tfrac{p^{2}y^{2}t_{j}}{2}\bigr)\,dy\biggr)d\mathbb{x}.

Consider
\int_{1}
, the contribution to the RHS of (
1.12
) from
\{\mathbb{x}\in[0,1]^{n}:s\leq s(n)\}
,
s:=\sum_{i\in[n]}x_{i}
,
s(n):=\tfrac{\log n-\beta\log\log n}{p}
,
\beta\in(1,\alpha)
. Dropping
\exp\bigl(-\tfrac{p^{2}y^{2}t_{j}}{2}\bigr)
, and integrating over
y\in[0,1]
, we have

\int_{1}\leq p^{n}\int\limits_{\mathbb{x}:s\leq s(n)}\prod_{j\in[n]}F(s_{j}p)\,d\mathbb{x},\quad F(z):=\tfrac{1-e^{-z}}{z}.

Now,

(\log F(z))^{\prime}=\tfrac{1}{e^{z}-1}-\tfrac{1}{z}\left\{\begin{aligned} &\to-\tfrac{1}{2},&&z\downarrow 0,\\
&\sim-\tfrac{1}{z},&&z\uparrow\infty,\end{aligned}\right.

so that, for
A:=\sup_{z>0}(z+1)\bigl(\tfrac{1}{z}-\tfrac{1}{e^{z}-1}\bigr)
, we have

(1.13)

\Big|(\log F(z))^{\prime}\Bigr|\leq\tfrac{A}{z+1},

Consequently

\log F(s_{j}p)=\log F(sp)-\int\limits_{s-x_{j}}^{s}(\log F(yp))^{\prime}_{y}\,dy\\
\leq\log F(sp)+\tfrac{Apx_{j}}{(s-x_{j})p+1}\leq\log F(sp)+\tfrac{Ax_{j}}{s},

whence

\prod_{j\in[n]}F(s_{j}p)\leq e^{A}F^{n}(sp).

Then, by (
1.10
),

\int_{1}\leq e^{A}p^{n}\int_{0}^{s(n)}\biggl(\tfrac{1-e^{-sp}}{sp}\biggr)^{n}\tfrac{s^{n-1}}{(n-1)!}\,ds=\tfrac{e^{A}}{(n-1)!}\int_{0}^{ps(n)}\exp(\Phi_{n}(\eta))\,d\eta,\\

\Phi_{n}(\eta):=n\log(1-e^{-\eta})-\log\eta
. Here, as
\tfrac{e^{\eta}-1}{\eta}
increases,

\displaystyle\Phi^{\prime}_{n}(\eta)

\displaystyle=\tfrac{n}{e^{\eta}-1}-\tfrac{1}{\eta}=\tfrac{n}{e^{\eta}-1}\bigl(1-\tfrac{e^{\eta}-1}{n\eta}\bigr)

\displaystyle\geq\tfrac{n}{e^{\eta}-1}\bigl(1-\tfrac{n\log^{-\beta}n-1}{n(\log n-\beta\log\log n)}\bigr)=\tfrac{n}{e^{\eta}-1}(1-O(\log^{-\beta-1}n))>0,

if
n
sufficiently large, and, as
\tfrac{e^{\eta/2}-e^{-\eta/2}}{\eta}
increases,

\displaystyle\Phi_{n}^{\prime\prime}(\eta)

\displaystyle=-\tfrac{n}{(e^{\eta/2}-e^{-\eta/2})^{2}}+\tfrac{1}{\eta^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq-\tfrac{n}{(e^{\eta/2}-e^{-\eta/2})^{2}}\bigl(1-\tfrac{\bigl(e^{(\log n-\beta\log\log n)/2}-e^{-(\log n-\beta\log\log n)/2)}\bigr)^{2}}{n(\log n-\beta\log\log n)^{2}}\bigr)

\displaystyle=-\tfrac{n}{(e^{\eta/2}-e^{-\eta/2})^{2}}(1-O(\log^{-2-\beta}n))<0,

if
n
is sufficiently large. So, that
\Phi^{\prime}_{n}(\eta)>0
and
\Phi_{n}^{\prime\prime}(\eta)<0
for
\eta\in[0,1]
, and
n
sufficiently large, i.e.
\Phi_{n}(\eta)
is concave and decreases on
[0,ps(n)]
. Therefore, for
\eta\in[0,ps(n)]
, we have

\Phi_{n}(\eta)\leq\Phi_{n}(1)+\Phi_{n}^{\prime}(1)(\eta-1).

Consequently,

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{ps(n)}\exp(\Phi_{n}(\eta))\,d\eta

\displaystyle\leq\tfrac{e^{A}}{(n-1)!}\exp[\Phi_{n}(ps(n))]\int_{0}^{1}\exp\bigl(\Phi_{n}^{\prime}(ps(n))(\eta-1)\bigr)\,d\eta

\displaystyle\leq\tfrac{e^{A}}{(n-1)!}\tfrac{\exp[\Phi_{n}(ps(n))]}{\Phi_{n}^{\prime}(ps(n))}.

So,
n!\int_{1}
, the contribution of
s\leq s(n)
to
\mathbb{E}[S_{n}](=n!P_{n})
, is of order

n\tfrac{(1-e^{-ps(n)})^{n}}{p(s(n))}\cdot\tfrac{e^{ps(n)}-1}{n}\leq\exp\bigl[-ne^{-ps(n)}+ps(n)\bigr]\\
\leq\exp\bigl(-ne^{-(\log n-\beta\log\log n)}+\log n\bigr)=\exp\bigl(-\log^{\beta}n+\log n\bigr)\to 0,

since
\beta>1
.

Turn to the contribution to the RHS of (
1.12
) from
\mathbb{x}
with
s=\sum_{i\in[n]}x_{i}\geq s(n)
. The
j
-th integral on the RHS of (
1.12
) is bounded by

\displaystyle\tfrac{1}{s_{j}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\exp\bigl(-pz-\tfrac{p^{2}t_{j}z^{2}}{2s^{2}}\bigr)\,dz

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{s_{j}}\biggl[\tfrac{1}{p}-\tfrac{pt_{j}}{s^{2}}\int_{0}^{\infty}z\exp\bigl(-pz-\tfrac{p^{2}t_{j}z^{2}}{2s^{2}}\bigr)\,dz\biggr]

\displaystyle\leq\tfrac{1}{s_{j}}\biggl[\tfrac{1}{p}-\tfrac{pt_{j}}{s^{2}}\int_{0}^{\infty}z\exp\bigl(-pz-\tfrac{p^{2}tz^{2}}{2s^{2}}\bigr)\,dz\biggr].

So,

p^{n}\biggl(\prod_{j\in[n]}\int_{0}^{1}\exp\bigl(-pys_{j}-\tfrac{p^{2}y^{2}t_{j}}{2}\bigr)\,dy\biggr)\\
\leq\prod_{j=1}^{n}\tfrac{1}{s_{j}}\cdot\prod_{j\in[n]}\biggl[1-\tfrac{p^{2}t_{j}}{s^{2}}\int_{0}^{\infty}z\exp\bigl(-pz-\tfrac{p^{2}tz^{2}}{2s^{2}}\bigr)\,dz\biggr]\\
\leq s^{-n}\exp\biggl(-\tfrac{p^{2}}{s^{2}}\Bigl(\sum_{j\in[n]}t_{j}\Bigr)\int_{0}^{\infty}z\exp\bigl(-pz-\tfrac{p^{2}tz^{2}}{2s^{2}}\bigr)\,dz\biggr)\prod_{j\in[n]}\bigl(1-\tfrac{x_{j}}{s}\bigr)^{-1}.

Here

\displaystyle\sum_{j\in n]}t_{j}

\displaystyle=(n-1)t=(n-1)\sum_{j\in[n]}x_{j}^{2},

\displaystyle\prod_{j\in[n]}\bigl(1-\tfrac{x_{j}}{s}\bigr)^{-1}

\displaystyle=\exp\biggl(\sum_{j\in[n]}\bigl(\tfrac{x_{j}}{s}+O(\tfrac{x_{j}^{2}}{s^{2}})\bigr)\biggr)=\exp\bigl(1+O(p)\bigr).

Therefore

p^{n}\biggl(\prod_{j\in[n]}\int_{0}^{1}\exp\bigl(-pys_{j}-\tfrac{p^{2}y^{2}t_{j}}{2}\bigr)\,dy\biggr)\\
\leq s^{-n}\exp\bigl(1+O(p)\bigr)\exp\biggl(\!-\tfrac{p^{2}}{s^{2}}(n-1)t\int_{0}^{\infty}z\exp\bigl(-pz-\tfrac{p^{2}tz^{2}}{2s^{2}}\bigr)\,dz\!\biggr)\\
=O\biggl[s^{-n}\exp\biggl(\!-\tfrac{(n-1)t}{s^{2}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\eta\exp\bigl(-\eta-\tfrac{t\eta^{2}}{2s^{2}}\bigr)\,d\eta\!\biggr)\biggr].

By convexity of the exponential function, and
\int_{\eta\geq 0}\eta e^{-\eta}\,d\eta=1
, the last integral is at least

\exp\biggl(-\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\eta}\tfrac{t\eta^{3}}{2s^{2}}\,d\eta\biggr)=\exp\bigl(-\tfrac{6t}{2s^{2}}\bigr).\\

Therefore, uniformly for
s=\sum_{j\in[n]}x_{j}\geq s(n)
,

p^{n}\biggl(\prod_{j\in[n]}\int_{0}^{1}\exp\bigl(-pys_{j}-\tfrac{p^{2}y^{2}t_{j}}{2}\bigr)\,dy\biggr)=O\Bigl(s^{-n}\exp\bigl(-\tfrac{(n-1)t}{s^{2}}\exp\bigl(-\tfrac{6t}{2s^{2}}\bigr)\bigr)\Bigr).

Importantly, the last bound is the product of two functions, first dependent only on
s=\sum_{i\in[n]}x_{i}
, and second dependent only on
t/s^{2}=\sum_{i\in[n]}\tfrac{x_{i}^{2}}{s^{2}}
. So, by (
1.11
), the contribution
of
\{\mathbb{x}\in[0,1]^{n}:s\in[s(n),n]\}
to the RHS of (
1.12
) is at most of order

\tfrac{1}{(n-1)!}\int\limits_{s=s(n)}^{n}\!\!\!\!\tfrac{1}{s}\,\mathbb{E}\biggl[\mathbb{I}\bigl(L_{n}^{+}\leq\tfrac{1}{s}\bigr)\exp\Bigl(-(n-1)U_{n}\cdot e^{-3U_{n}}\Bigr)\biggr]\,ds\\
\leq\tfrac{\log n}{(n-1)!}\mathbb{P}\bigl(L_{n}^{+}\leq\tfrac{1}{s(n)}\bigr)=\tfrac{\log n}{(n-1)!}\,\mathbb{P}\bigl(L_{n}^{+}\leq\tfrac{p}{\log n-\beta\log\log n}\bigr).

where
L_{n}^{+}=\max_{i\in[n]}L_{i}
and
U_{n}=\sum_{i\in[n]}L_{i}^{2}
. Here

\tfrac{p}{\log n-\beta\log\log n}=\tfrac{\log n}{n}\cdot\tfrac{\log n-\alpha\log\log n}{\log n-\beta\log\log n}\\
=n^{-1}\Bigl(\log n+(\beta-\alpha)\log\log n+O\bigl(\tfrac{(\log\log n)^{2}}{\log n}\bigr)\Bigr)\\
=n^{-1}\Bigl(\log\tfrac{n}{\log n}+(\beta-\alpha+1+o(1))\log\log n\Bigr)

Since
\alpha>2
, we can pick
\beta\in(1,\alpha-1)
, making
\beta-\alpha+1<0
. Therefore, by Lemma
1.2
,

\mathbb{P}\bigl(L_{n}^{+}\leq\tfrac{p}{\log n-\beta\log\log n}\bigr)=O(n^{-d}),\quad\forall\,d>0.

So, the contribution to the RHS of (
1.12
) from
\mathbb{x}
with
s=\sum_{i\in[n]}x_{i}\geq s(n)
is of order

n!\cdot\tfrac{\log n}{(n-1)!}n^{-d}
, i. e. super-polynomially small.

We conclude that
\mathbb{E}[S_{n}]
is super-polynomially small as well. The proof of Theorem
1.1

(i)

is complete.

Proof of Theorem

This time we need to sharply bound

Let

, and

\displaystyle\tfrac{n}{\log(n\omega_{1}(n))}\leq s\leq\tfrac{n}{\log(n\omega_{2}(n))},

\mathbb{x}\in D
,

, we have

\tfrac{\log\omega_{1}(n)}{\log n}\to 0
. For this

Consequently

\prod_{j\in[n]}\biggl(\int_{0}^{1}\prod_{i\neq j}(1-px_{i}y_{j})\,dy_{j}\biggr)\geq e^{-4}\bigl(\tfrac{1-e^{-ps}}{ps}\bigr)^{n},

implying that

(1.16)

\hat{P}_{n}\geq e^{-4}\int_{\mathbb{x}\in D}\bigl(\tfrac{1-e^{-ps}}{s}\bigr)^{n}\,d\mathbb{x}.

i. e. the integrand is a function of

only. It remains to bound this integral from below. Let us switch to the new variables:
u=\sum_{j\in[n]}x_{j}(=s)
,
v_{j}=x_{j}/s
,
\,j\in[n-1]

and

\displaystyle\tfrac{n}{\log(n\omega_{1}(n))}\leq u\leq\tfrac{n}{\log(n\omega_{2}(n))},

is the range of
\mathbb{v}

\hat{P}_{n}\geq\tfrac{1}{(n-1)!}\biggl(\int_{\tfrac{n}{\log(n\omega_{1}(n))}}^{\tfrac{n}{\log(n\omega_{2}(n))}}\tfrac{(1-e^{-pu})^{n}}{u}\,du\biggr)\mathbb{P}(\mathbb{L}\in D^{*}),\\

where

see Lemma
1.2
.

, and

Hence

(ii)
is complete.

1.4.
Likely size of a stable partial matching.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
\mathcal{L}_{n}
denote the size of the stable partial matching delivered by the
p
-proposal algorithm,
that is the cardinality of the set of men and of the set of women matched in every stable partial matching. If
p=p(n)\gg 1/n
, then for
\gamma>2

\mathbb{P}\bigl(\mathcal{L}_{n}\geq n-\tfrac{\gamma\log(np)}{p}\bigr)\geq 1-n^{-(1+o(1))(\gamma^{2}-2\gamma)\log(np)}.\\

Thus for
\gamma>2
, with probability super-polynomially close to
1
, all but at most
\tfrac{\gamma\log(np)}{p}
men and
\tfrac{c\log(np)}{p}
women, i. e. of order
o(n)
each, are matched.

Proof.

By (
1.7
),

(1.19)

\mathbb{P}\bigl(\mathcal{L}_{n}\leq n-\tfrac{\gamma\log(np)}{p}\bigr)\leq\!\!\!\sum_{\ell\leq n-\tfrac{\gamma\log(np)}{p}}\!\!\!(1-p)^{(n-\ell)^{2}}\binom{n}{\ell}^{2}\mathbb{E}[S_{\ell}].

Here
\mathbb{E}[S_{\ell}]
is the expected number of stable matchings for
\ell
men and
\ell
women,
and

p=1
,
which is of order
O(\ell\log\ell)=O(n\log n)
. Furthermore,

(1-p)^{j^{2}}\binom{n}{j}^{2}\leq e^{f(j)},\quad f(x):=-x^{2}p+2x\log\tfrac{en}{x}.

So,

f\bigl(\tfrac{\gamma\log(np)}{p}\bigr)=-\tfrac{\gamma^{2}\log^{2}(np)}{p}+\tfrac{2\gamma\log(np)}{p}\log\tfrac{en}{\tfrac{\gamma\log(np)}{p}}\leq-\tfrac{\gamma^{2}-2\gamma}{p}\log^{2}(np).

Next,
f(x)
is concave, and

f^{\prime}\bigl(\tfrac{\gamma\log(np)}{p}\bigr)=-\tfrac{2p\gamma\log(np)}{p}+2\log\tfrac{np}{\gamma\log(np)}\leq-2(\gamma-1)\log(np)\to-\infty.\\

Therefore, the RHS of (
1.19
) is of order

(n\log n)\exp\bigl(-\tfrac{\gamma^{2}-2\gamma}{p}\log^{2}(np)\bigr).

Here, since
\tfrac{\log x}{x}
decreases for
x\geq e
,

\tfrac{\log^{2}(np)}{p}=n\log(np)\cdot\tfrac{\log(np)}{np}\geq\log n\cdot\log(np).

Therefore

(n\log n)\exp\bigl(-\tfrac{\gamma^{2}-2\gamma}{p}\log^{2}(np)\bigr)\leq(\log n)\exp\bigl(\log n-(\gamma^{2}-2\gamma)\log n\cdot\log(np)\bigr)\\
=(\log n)\cdot n^{-(\gamma^{2}-2\gamma)\log(np)+1}.

∎

Theorem 1.4
.

If
p\geq c\tfrac{\log^{2}n}{n}
,
c>1+\tfrac{1}{2\Delta}
, then

\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{L}_{n}\leq n-\Delta)\to 0
. In words, if
p>c\tfrac{\log^{2}n}{n}
,

, then the number of unmatched men and women is bounded in probability. In particular, for

, then whp
\mathcal{L}_{n}=n
.

Proof.

By Theorem
1.3
and (
1.7
),

(1.20)

\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{L}_{n}\leq n-\Delta)\leq n^{-\Theta(\log(np))}+\sum_{\ell=n-cp^{-1}\log(np)}^{n-\Delta}p^{\ell}\binom{n}{\ell}^{2}\ell!\,I_{n,\ell}.

Here, denoting
s_{j}=\sum_{i\in[\ell]\setminus\{j\}}x_{j}
,
s=\sum_{i\in[\ell]}x_{i}
, and using

, we bound

\leq\int\limits_{\mathbb{x},\,\mathbb{y}\in[0,1]^{\ell}}\exp\biggl(-p(n-\ell)s-\sum_{j\in[\ell]}y_{j}(ps_{j}+p(n-\ell))\biggr)\,d\mathbb{x}\,d\mathbb{y}\\
=\int\limits_{\mathbb{x}\in[0,1]^{\ell}}e^{-p(n-\ell)s}\,\prod_{j\in[\ell]}\tfrac{1-e^{-ps_{j}-p(n-\ell)}}{ps_{j}+p(n-\ell)}\,\,d\mathbb{x}\\
\leq e^{A}\int\limits_{s\in[0,\ell]}e^{-p(n-\ell)s}\biggl(\tfrac{1-e^{-ps-p(n-\ell)}}{ps+p(n-\ell)}\biggr)^{\ell}\cdot\tfrac{s^{\ell-1}}{(\ell-1)!}\,ds\\
\leq\tfrac{e^{A}}{p^{\ell}(\ell-1)!}\int\limits_{0}^{p\ell}e^{-(n-\ell)u}\biggl(\tfrac{u(1-e^{-u-p(n-\ell)})}{u+p(n-\ell)}\biggr)^{\ell-1}\,du\\
\leq\tfrac{e^{A}}{p^{\ell}(\ell-1)!}\int\limits_{0}^{p\ell}\exp\Bigl[-(n-\ell)\Bigl(u+\tfrac{p(\ell-1)}{u+p(n-\ell)}\Bigr)\Bigr]\,du.

. We bound the bottom integral using Laplace method. Observe that, denoting by

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}^{\prime}_{u}

\displaystyle=-(n-\ell)\Bigl(1-\tfrac{p(\ell-1)}{(u+p(n-\ell))^{2}}\Bigr),

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}^{\prime\prime}_{u}

\displaystyle=-(n-\ell)\tfrac{2p(\ell-1)}{(u+p(n-\ell))^{3}},

implying concavity of

attains its maximum at

and that

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}(\bar{u})

\displaystyle=-(n-\ell)\Bigl(2\sqrt{p(\ell-1)}-p(n-\ell)\Bigr)=-2(n-\ell)\sqrt{np}(1+o(1)),

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}^{\prime\prime}(\bar{u})

\displaystyle=-\tfrac{2(n-\ell)}{\sqrt{p(\ell-1)}},

since

. So, approximating the bottom integrand in (

=O\Bigl[n\exp\bigl[2(n-\ell)\bigl(\log n-(1+o(1))c^{1/2}\log n\bigr)\bigr]\Bigr],\\

if
p\geq c\tfrac{\log^{2}n}{n}
. So, for

=O\Bigl(\cdot\exp\bigl[(2\Delta+1-(1+o(1)))2\Delta c^{1/2}\log n\bigr]\Bigr)\to 0,

if

Theorem 1.5

1.5.
Asymptotic ranks of stable partners.

Let
\mathcal{Q}_{n}^{(-)}
,
\mathcal{Q}_{n}^{(+)}

,
\mathcal{R}_{n}^{(+)}

Theorem 1.6

In a sharp contrast, for unconstrained matchings we proved
[

. By
(
1.29
) and (
1.30
), we have

\displaystyle\psi(k/\ell)

\displaystyle\leq\exp\Bigl[-(n-\ell)\bigl(\tfrac{k}{\ell}+\tfrac{\ell^{2}p}{k}\bigr)\bigl(1+O(\tfrac{\log(np)}{\sqrt{np}})\bigr)\Bigr],

\displaystyle(\log\psi)^{\prime}(k/\ell)

\displaystyle=(n-\ell)\Bigl(\tfrac{p\ell^{3}}{k^{2}}-1+O\bigl(\tfrac{\log(np)}{\sqrt{np}}\bigr)\Bigr),

and this time the factor
\frac{p\ell^{3}}{k^{2}}-1
is
negative and bounded away from
0
, since
\ell\sim n
and
k^{2}\geq(1+\varepsilon)^{2}pn^{3}
. It follows that
\int_{2}^{+}
is of order

\exp\Bigl[-(n-\ell)\bigl(\tfrac{k}{\ell}+\tfrac{\ell^{2}p}{k}\bigr)\bigl(1+O(\tfrac{\log(np)}{\sqrt{np}})\bigr)\Bigr]\\
+\mathbb{P}\bigl(\max_{j}L_{j}\geq\tfrac{\ell}{k}\bigr)\exp\Bigl[(n-\ell)\tfrac{k}{\ell}\bigl(1-\tfrac{p\ell^{3}}{k^{2}}+O\bigl(\tfrac{\log(np)}{\sqrt{np}}\bigr)\bigr)\\
-(n-\ell)\bigl(\tfrac{k}{\ell}+\tfrac{p\ell^{2}}{k}\bigr)\bigl(1+O\bigl(\tfrac{\log(np)}{\sqrt{np}}\bigr)\bigr)\Bigr],

uniformly for
\ell_{n}\leq\ell<n
. The second exponent equals
-2(n-\ell)\tfrac{p\ell^{2}}{k}\bigl(1+O(\tfrac{\log(np)}{\sqrt{np}})\bigr)
.
Since
\tfrac{p\ell^{2}}{k}\leq\tfrac{k}{\ell}
, we obtain that
\int_{2}^{+}
is of order
\exp\Bigl[-2(n-\ell)\tfrac{p\ell^{2}}{k}\bigl(1+O(\tfrac{\log(np)}{\sqrt{np}})\bigr)\Bigr]
.

So, the contribution of
\mathbb{x}
with
u=ps\geq k/\ell
to the
\ell
-term in
(
1.31
) is of order

n\bigl(\tfrac{en}{n-\ell}\bigr)^{n-\ell}\exp\Bigl(-(n-\ell)\bigl(\tfrac{k}{\ell}+\tfrac{p\ell^{2}}{k}\bigr)\Bigr).

Hence, the overall contribution of those
\mathbb{x}
’ to the RHS in (
1.31
) that comes from
\ell\leq n-\Delta_{n}
is sub-exponentially small.

We conclude that so is the total sum over
\ell\in[\ell_{n},n-\Delta_{n}-1)]
on the RHS of (
1.31
), implying sub-exponentiality of
\mathbb{P}\bigl(\mathcal{Q}_{n}^{(+)}\geq k\bigr)
.

The proof of Theorem
1.6
is complete.
∎

Finally,

Theorem 1.7
.

Suppose that
p\geq c\tfrac{\log^{2}n}{n}
,
\bigl(c>\tfrac{9}{4}\bigr)
. Let
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
. Whp

\mathcal{Q}_{n}^{(-)}\geq(1-\varepsilon)n\log n
.

Proof.

Using (
1.4
), analogously to (
1.23
), we have: for
k:=\lfloor(1-\varepsilon)n\log n\rfloor
,

(1.34)

\mathbb{P}\bigl(\mathcal{Q}_{n}^{(-)}\leq k\bigr)\leq\mathbb{P}\bigl(\mathcal{L}_{n}<n)\\
+p^{n}n!\int\limits_{\mathbb{x},\mathbb{y}\in[0,1]^{n}}\sum_{m=n}^{k}\bigl[\xi^{k-n}\bigr]\prod_{1\leq i\neq j\leq n}\bigl[1-px_{i}(1-\xi+\xi y_{j})\bigr]\,d\mathbb{x}d\mathbb{y},\\

Here
\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\bigl(\mathcal{L}_{n}<n)=0
, and following closely the steps of the proof of Theorem
1.6
.
we obtain that
p^{n}n!\times
the double integral is at most of order

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad n\int_{0}^{pn}\exp\bigl[\inf_{\xi\leq 1}H(\xi,u)\bigr]\,du,

\displaystyle H(\xi,u):=-k\log\xi-(n-1)(1-\xi)u+n\log(1-e^{-\xi u})-\log u.

(Similarly to those steps, it is important that we consider the case
\xi\leq 1
.)
Again analogously to that proof, we are content to select
\xi=\tfrac{k/n}{u}
for
u>\tfrac{k}{n}
, and
\xi\equiv 1
for
u\leq\tfrac{k}{n}
. Then, due to log-concavity of the resulting integrand, and
k:=\lfloor(1-\varepsilon)n\log n\rfloor
, the contribution of
u\leq k/n
is

n\int_{0}^{k/n}\exp\bigl[H(\xi,u)\bigr]\,du=n\int_{0}^{k/n}\tfrac{(1-e^{-u})^{n}}{u}\,du\\
\leq n\tfrac{(1-e^{-k/n})^{n}}{k/n}\cdot\bigl(\tfrac{n}{e^{k/n}-1}-\tfrac{n}{k}\bigr)^{-1}=\exp\bigl(-\Theta(n^{\varepsilon})\bigr).

Furthermore,

n\int_{k/n}^{pn}\exp\bigl[H(\xi,u)\bigr]\,du\leq n\bigl(\tfrac{n}{k}\bigr)^{k}\cdot e^{k}\bigl(1-e^{-k/n}\bigr)^{n}\int_{0}^{\infty}\eta^{k}e^{-(n-1)\eta}\,d\eta\\
\leq n\bigl(\tfrac{n}{k}\bigr)^{k}e^{k}\exp\bigl(-ne^{-k/n}\bigr)\tfrac{k!}{(n-1)^{k}}=\exp\bigl(-\Theta(n^{\varepsilon})\bigr).

(ii)
For
k:=\big\lceil(1+\varepsilon)\tfrac{n^{2}}{\log n}\big\rceil
, analogously to the part
(ii)
of Theorem
1.6
,

(1.35)

\mathbb{P}\bigl(\mathcal{Q}_{n}^{(+)}\geq k\bigr)\leq\mathbb{P}\bigl(\mathcal{L}_{n}<n)\\
+p^{n}n!\int\limits_{\mathbb{x},\mathbb{y}\in[0,1]^{n}}\xi(\mathbb{x})^{n-k}\prod_{1\leq i\neq j\leq n}\bigl[1-px_{i}(1-\xi+\xi y_{j})\bigr]\,d\mathbb{x}d\mathbb{y},\\

where
\xi=\xi(\mathbb{x}):=\tfrac{k/n}{ps}>1
since
pn<\tfrac{k}{n}
.

\max_{j\leq n}\tfrac{x_{j}}{s}\leq\tfrac{n}{k}
, and
\xi(\mathbb{x})\equiv 1
otherwise. Since
pn\leq\tfrac{n}{\log n}\leq\tfrac{k}{n}

This time

n\int_{0}^{k/n}\exp\bigl[H(\xi,u)\bigr]\,du\\
\leq n\mathbb{P}\Bigl(\max_{i\in[n]}L_{i}\leq\tfrac{n}{k}\Bigr)\cdot\bigl(\tfrac{n}{k}\bigr)^{k}e^{k}\bigl(1-e^{-k/n}\bigr)^{n}\int_{0}^{\infty}\eta^{k}e^{-(n-1)\eta}\,d\eta\\
\leq n\mathbb{P}\Bigl(\max_{i\in[n]}L_{i}\leq\tfrac{n}{k}\Bigr)\cdot\bigl(\tfrac{n}{k}\bigr)^{k}e^{k}\tfrac{k!}{(n-1)^{k}}\leq n^{3}\mathbb{P}\Bigl(\max_{i\in[n]}L_{i}\leq\tfrac{n}{k}\Bigr)\\
\leq n^{3}\mathbb{P}\Bigl(\max_{i\in[n]}L_{i}\leq\tfrac{\log n}{(1+\varepsilon)n}\Bigr)\leq n^{3}\exp\Bigl(-n^{\tfrac{\varepsilon}{1+\varepsilon}}\Bigr)\to 0.

∎
