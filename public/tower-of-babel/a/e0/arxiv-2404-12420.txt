Band theory for heterostructures with interface superlattices
Bernhard Putzer, Lucas V. Pupim, Mathias S. Scheurer

Abstract
Motivated by recent experiments demonstrating the creation of atomically sharp interfaces between hexagonal sapphire and cubic SrTiO 3 with finite twist, we here develop and study a general electronic band theory for this novel class of moiré heterostructures. We take into account the three-dimensional nature of the two crystals, allow for arbitrary combinations of Bravais lattices, finite twist angles, and different locations in momentum space of the low-energy electronic bands of the constituent materials. We analyze the general condition for a well-defined crystalline limit in the interface electron system and classify the associated “crystalline reference points”. We discuss this in detail for the example of the two-dimensional lattice planes being square and triangular lattices on the two sides of the interface; this reveals non-trivial reference points at finite twist angle and lattice mismatch, leading to a novel form of magic angles, which we refer to as “geometric magic angles”. We further show that band structures of mixed dimensionality naturally emerge, where quasi-one- and two-dimensional pockets coexist. Explicit computations for different bulk Bloch Hamiltonians yield a collection of interesting features, such as isolated bands localized at interfaces of non-topological insulators, Dirac cones, van Hove singularities, a non-trivial evolution of the band structures with Zeeman-field, and topological interface bands. Our work illustrates the potential of these heterostructures and is anticipated to provide the foundation for moiré interface design and for the analysis of correlated physics in these systems.

In this work, we will develop a general theoretical framework for these novel types of moiré interfaces and demonstrate, using explicit illustrative examples, the unique potential of this setup.
More specifically, we will derive and study the general conditions under which the electron liquid in the vicinity of the interface can be approximated as crystalline. To this end, we will derive the associated continuum models taking into account the 3D bulk nature of the materials forming the heterostructure. As illustrated schematically in Fig.

For a given distribution of the low-energy electronic degrees of freedom in the respective bulk materials, there are in general multiple continuum reference points, which we therefore characterize by their

We identify and discuss all 11, symmetry-inequivalent leading (i.e., of smallest momentum) continuum reference points for the case of one material being cubic (or tetragonal) and the other being hexagonal—the situation relevant for sapphire and SrTiO

We further illustrate the physics using explicit model calculations for an interface between a tetragonal and hexagonal lattice. We consider the following combinations of band structures: quadratic bulk bands in both systems, of either particle or hole type, an interface between a topological insulator and bulk materials with a quadratic band, and two topological insulators. Among other interesting band features, such as van Hove singularities and topologically non-trivial bands, we analyze under which conditions isolated, well-localized interface bands appear.

&\qquad a^{\dagger}_{\ell,\boldsymbol{R},Z,\alpha}\hat{h}_{\alpha,\alpha^{\prime}}^{\ell}(\boldsymbol{R}-\boldsymbol{R}^{\prime},Z,Z^{\prime})a^{\phantom{\dagger}}_{\ell,\boldsymbol{R}^{\prime},Z^{\prime},\alpha^{\prime}},\end{split}

Crystalline reference points

IV
Explicit model calculations

In the following, we apply the general framework to concrete models. We build on the geometric arguments of Sec.
III
and investigate the quasi-1D electronic bands arising from the moiré modulations at the interfaces of 2D as well as 3D materials with square and triangular lattice symmetries. At the end, we will discuss how the presence of non-trivial crystalline reference points leads to a novel type of “magic angle” with flat bands.

V
Conclusion and Outlook

In this work, we studied the electronic spectrum at an interface between two different materials, with in general different crystal structures and Bravais lattices, as shown in Fig.
1
. For a given set of high-symmetry momentum points
\boldsymbol{P}_{j,\ell}
around which the low-energy degrees of freedom in the Brillouin are localized in each material
\ell=1,2
, we studied the sets of lattice parameters
p^{*}
, i.e., the relative orientation and length of the primitive vectors of the lattices, for which the electronic spectrum can be approximated as being crystalline. Such a “crystalline reference point”
p^{*}
is determined by the existence of reciprocal lattice vectors
\boldsymbol{G}_{j}
such that
\delta b\rightarrow 0
in Eq. (
6
), when the lattice parameters approach
p^{*}
. Each crystalline reference point is characterized by a momentum
Q
, defined as the smallest possible value of Eq. (
7
); the larger Q, the weaker the associated moiré-reconstruction of the bands. The, in this sense, leading set of continuum reference points are summarized in Table
1
for each combination of high symmetry points
\boldsymbol{P}_{j,\ell}
for one material exhibiting a square and the other a triangular lattice in the respective lattice planes forming the interface. We see that, as consequence of the two lattices being different, some of the continuum reference points are not at zero twist (and lattice mismatch). We further observe that a variety of different types of band reconstruction is possible, which includes (i) a single quasi-1D channel with time-reversal and
C_{2z}
symmetry (
\Gamma
-
\Gamma
and
M
-
\Gamma
), (ii) two 1D channels, related by time-reversal/
C_{2z}
symmetry (
\Gamma
-
K
and
M
-
K
), and (iii) mixed dimensionality (all other combinations) in the sense that a subset of the pockets exhibit a significant moiré modulation leading to quasi-1D physics (with one or two channels) while the complement is effectively unreconstructed and, thus, 2D.

While the generalization to other pairs of Bravais lattices is straightforward, for our explicit band structure calculations, we focused on square and triangular lattices meeting at the interface. We considered (a) quadratic bulk bands in both systems, (b) an interface between a topological insulator and bulk materials with a quadratic band, and (c) two topological insulators, uncovering a variety of interesting features. For instance, in case (a), which is also directly relevant to the experiment in Ref.
60
, we observe that the presence or absence of a well-defined interface band, i.e., a band that is both energetically separated (at least in part of the Brillouin zone) from the other bulk bands and localized in the vicinity of the interface, strongly depends on the nature of bulk bands: if the bands on either side are both particle-like or both hole-like, there is a regime where the moiré reconstruction leads to a well-defined interface band, even when the materials have sizable hopping processes
t_{z}
between the lattice planes; if one band is particle- and the other hole-like, the surface bands will quickly hybridize and be absorbed by the bulk states when
t_{z}
is turned on. To mention a second example, we show that twisting two different TIs, one with square and one with a triangular Bravais lattice in the relevant lattice planes, gives rise to a quasi-1D surface theory with Dirac cones (even when both materials, considered separately, have surface Dirac cones at the
\Gamma
point) located at the time-reversal invariant points
(0,0)
and
(\pi,0)
; these are gapped out by a Zeeman field that can induce multiple topological phase transitions.

Naturally, there are many open questions concerning the physics of moiré interfaces. First, using the formalism of this work, there is a plethora of different combinations of lattices, high-symmetry points, and types of bands in the two materials to explore. Second, an important extension of the current work would be to include lattice relaxation effects in the vicinity of the interface, which result from the mutual influence of the two incompatible lattices, and study its impact on the electronic properties. Finally, there is a large set of questions associated with the impact of electronic correlations in these heterostructures. For instance, the tunable quasi-1D nature of some of the bands
[
66
,
44
]
as well as the presence and tunability of van Hove singularities
[
67
,
68
]
very naturally comes with enhanced correlation effects, likely leading to competing superconducting and particle-hole instabilities, such as charge- or spin-density waves. In that regard, the systems with mixed dimensionality are particularly interesting since the mutual impact of quasi-1D and 2D channels has the potential to induce rich physics.

Appendix A
Detailed derivation of momentum-space model

Since the momentum-space model, described briefly in Sec.
II.1
, is a central to our analysis, we here present a detailed derivation.

A.1
Two layers of one-band models

Although we are ultimately interested in twisting 3D bulk crystals with in general multiple relevant bands, we start for pedagogical reasons by considering two quasi-2D materials with only one active band each but allowing for arbitrary and possibly different Bravais lattices in each layer. Once the basic notation is established in this minimal setup, the generalization will be straightforward.

More specifically, the Hamiltonian we start from has two components
\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{H}_{1}+\mathcal{H}_{2}
, where

\mathcal{H}_{1}=\sum_{\ell=1,2}\int\mathrm{d}^{2}\boldsymbol{r}\,c^{\dagger}_{\ell}(\boldsymbol{r})\left[-\frac{1}{2m_{\ell}}\Delta+\mu_{\ell}+V_{\ell}(\mathcal{R}_{-\theta_{\ell}}\boldsymbol{r})\right]c^{\phantom{\dagger}}_{\ell}(\boldsymbol{r}).

(10)

Here,
c^{\dagger}_{\ell}(\boldsymbol{r})
is the electronic creation operator at position
\boldsymbol{r}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
and in layer
\ell
,
\mathcal{R}_{\theta}
is a 2D rotation matrix by angle
\theta
,
\theta_{\ell}
the twist angle, and
V_{\ell}(\boldsymbol{r})
is the (untwisted) lattice potential in layer
\ell
. The latter obeys

V_{\ell}(\boldsymbol{r})=V_{\ell}(\boldsymbol{r}+\boldsymbol{R}),\qquad\boldsymbol{R}\in\text{BL}_{\ell},

(11)

defining the Bravais lattice in the untwisted layer
\ell
. We here allow for general
\text{BL}_{\ell}
which may or may not differ in the two layers. In Eq. (
10
), we neglected spin-orbit coupling, which is why we do not need to include an explicit spin index.
The second term in the Hamiltonian,

\mathcal{H}_{2}=\frac{1}{A}\int\mathrm{d}^{2}\boldsymbol{r}\int\mathrm{d}^{2}\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}\,t(|\boldsymbol{r}-\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}|)c^{\dagger}_{1}(\boldsymbol{r})c^{\phantom{\dagger}}_{2}(\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime})+\text{H.c.},

(12)

describes the interlayer tunneling, where
A
is the area of the layers.

We start from the Bloch states
\psi_{\boldsymbol{k},\ell}(\boldsymbol{r})
of the band of interest of the uncoupled and untwisted layers, obeying

\left[-\frac{1}{2m_{\ell}}\Delta+\mu_{\ell}+V_{\ell}(\boldsymbol{r})\right]\psi_{\boldsymbol{k},\ell}(\boldsymbol{r})=\epsilon_{\boldsymbol{k},\ell}\psi_{\boldsymbol{k},\ell}(\boldsymbol{r}),\quad\boldsymbol{k}\in\text{BZ}_{\ell},

(13)

where
\text{BZ}_{\ell}
is the first Brillouin zone of the Bravais lattice
\text{BL}_{\ell}
(both untwisted). With these states at hand, it is straightforward to project
\mathcal{H}_{1}
onto this band and diagonalize it; we set (with
N_{\ell}
denoting the number of unit cells in layer
\ell
)

c_{\ell}(\boldsymbol{r})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N_{\ell}}}\sum_{\boldsymbol{k}\in\text{BZ}_{\ell}}\psi_{\boldsymbol{k},\ell}(R_{-\theta_{\ell}}\boldsymbol{r})\bar{c}_{\ell,\boldsymbol{k}}

(14)

such that, under band projection,

\mathcal{H}_{1}\,\rightarrow\,\mathcal{H}^{P}_{1}=\sum_{\ell=1,2}\sum_{\boldsymbol{k}\in\text{BZ}_{\ell}}\epsilon_{\boldsymbol{k},\ell}\bar{c}^{\dagger}_{\ell,\boldsymbol{k}}\bar{c}^{\phantom{\dagger}}_{\ell,\boldsymbol{k}}.

(15)

While this result looks very compact, the current choice of basis makes the physics less transparent: as is apparent from Eq. (
14
), the two different fermion species are defined in different bases that are rotated relative to each other. This will make the tunneling terms, to be considered below, look less clear since momentum conservation will become obscured (e.g.,
\bar{c}^{\dagger}_{1,\boldsymbol{k}}\bar{c}^{\phantom{\dagger}}_{2,\boldsymbol{k}}
does
not
conserve momentum). To this end, we choose to work in the “lab frame”. To motivate that basis and for future reference, we introduce Wannier functions
w_{\ell}(\boldsymbol{r}-\boldsymbol{R})
in the usual way,

\psi_{\ell,\boldsymbol{k}}(\boldsymbol{r})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N_{\ell}}}\sum_{\boldsymbol{R}\in\text{BL}_{\ell}}w_{\ell}(\boldsymbol{r}-\boldsymbol{R})e^{i\boldsymbol{k}\boldsymbol{R}}\,\,\Leftrightarrow\,\,w_{\ell}(\boldsymbol{r}-\boldsymbol{R})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N_{\ell}}}\sum_{\boldsymbol{k}\in\text{BZ}_{\ell}}\psi_{\ell,\boldsymbol{k}}(\boldsymbol{r})e^{-i\boldsymbol{k}\boldsymbol{R}}.

(16)

The associated Wannier functions after rotating the two layers are now just given by

w_{\ell}^{\theta_{\ell}}(\boldsymbol{r}-\boldsymbol{R}):=w_{\ell}(\mathcal{R}_{-\theta_{\ell}}(\boldsymbol{r}-\boldsymbol{R})),

(17)

which we can now use to define Bloch states for both layers in the same “lab frame” of reference,

\psi^{\theta_{\ell}}_{\ell,\boldsymbol{k}}(\boldsymbol{r})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N_{\ell}}}\sum_{\boldsymbol{R}\in\text{BL}_{\ell,\theta_{\ell}}}w^{\theta_{\ell}}_{\ell}(\boldsymbol{r}-\boldsymbol{R})e^{i\boldsymbol{k}\boldsymbol{R}},

(18)

where
\text{BL}_{\ell,\theta_{\ell}}:=\{\mathcal{R}_{\theta_{\ell}}\boldsymbol{R}\,|\boldsymbol{R}\in\text{BL}_{\ell}\}
is the Bravais lattice
\text{BL}_{\ell}
rotated by
\theta_{\ell}
. To relate
\psi_{\ell,\boldsymbol{k}}(\boldsymbol{r})
and
\psi^{\theta_{\ell}}_{\ell,\boldsymbol{k}}(\boldsymbol{r})
, consider

\displaystyle\psi_{\ell,\boldsymbol{k}}(R_{-\theta_{\ell}}\boldsymbol{r})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N_{\ell}}}\sum_{\boldsymbol{R}\in\text{BL}_{\ell}}w_{\ell}(R_{-\theta_{\ell}}\boldsymbol{r}-\boldsymbol{R})e^{i\boldsymbol{k}\boldsymbol{R}}

(19)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N_{\ell}}}\sum_{\boldsymbol{R}^{\prime}\in\text{BL}_{\ell,\theta_{\ell}}}w_{\ell}(R_{-\theta_{\ell}}(\boldsymbol{r}-\boldsymbol{R}^{\prime}))e^{i\boldsymbol{k}(R_{-\theta_{\ell}}\boldsymbol{R}^{\prime})}

(20)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N_{\ell}}}\sum_{\boldsymbol{R}^{\prime}\in\text{BL}_{\ell,\theta_{\ell}}}w^{\theta_{\ell}}_{\ell}(\boldsymbol{r}-\boldsymbol{R}^{\prime})e^{i(R_{\theta_{\ell}}\boldsymbol{k})\boldsymbol{R}^{\prime}}=\psi^{\theta_{\ell}}_{\ell,R_{\theta_{\ell}}\boldsymbol{k}}(\boldsymbol{r}).

(21)

This allows us to rewrite Eq. (
14
) as

c_{\ell}(\boldsymbol{r})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N_{\ell}}}\sum_{\boldsymbol{k}\in\text{BZ}_{\ell}}\psi_{\boldsymbol{k},\ell}(R_{-\theta_{\ell}}\boldsymbol{r})\bar{c}_{\ell,\boldsymbol{k}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N_{\ell}}}\sum_{\boldsymbol{k}\in\text{BZ}_{\ell}}\psi^{\theta_{\ell}}_{R_{\theta_{\ell}}\boldsymbol{k},\ell}(\boldsymbol{r})\bar{c}_{\ell,\boldsymbol{k}}\equiv\frac{1}{\sqrt{N_{\ell}}}\sum_{\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime}\in\text{BZ}_{\ell,\theta_{\ell}}}\psi^{\theta_{\ell}}_{\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime},\ell}(\boldsymbol{r})c_{\ell,\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime}},

(22)

where we introduced the new operators

c_{\ell,\boldsymbol{k}}=\bar{c}_{\ell,R_{-\theta_{\ell}}\boldsymbol{k}}

(23)

and the rotated Brillouin zone
\text{BZ}_{\ell,\theta_{\ell}}:=\{\mathcal{R}_{\theta_{\ell}}\boldsymbol{k}\,|\boldsymbol{k}\in\text{BZ}_{\ell}\}
, i.e., the Brillouin zone associated with the rotated Bravais lattice
\text{BL}_{\ell,\theta_{\ell}}
.

Using these operators, the projected intralayer Hamiltonian becomes

\mathcal{H}^{P}_{1}=\sum_{\ell=1,2}\sum_{\boldsymbol{k}\in\text{BZ}_{\ell,\theta_{\ell}}}\epsilon_{R_{-\theta_{\ell}}\boldsymbol{k},\ell}c^{\dagger}_{\ell,\boldsymbol{k}}c^{\phantom{\dagger}}_{\ell,\boldsymbol{k}}.

(24)

This form makes sense in that, in the lab frame, the dispersion should rotate with the lattice. We will next see that also the tunneling part of the Hamiltonian is more intuitive in this bases.

To project
\mathcal{H}_{2}
in Eq. (
12
) onto the band of interest, we write

c_{\ell}(\boldsymbol{r})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N_{\ell}}}\sum_{\boldsymbol{k}\in\text{BZ}_{\ell,\theta_{\ell}}}\psi^{\theta_{\ell}}_{\boldsymbol{k},\ell}(\boldsymbol{r})c_{\ell,\boldsymbol{k}}=\frac{1}{N_{\ell}}\sum_{\boldsymbol{k}\in\text{BZ}_{\ell,\theta_{\ell}}}c_{\ell,\boldsymbol{k}}\sum_{\boldsymbol{R}\in\text{BL}_{\ell,\theta_{\ell}}}w^{\theta_{\ell}}_{\ell}(\boldsymbol{r}-\boldsymbol{R})e^{i\boldsymbol{k}\boldsymbol{R}}

(25)

which we insert into
\mathcal{H}_{2}
, yielding

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathcal{H}_{2}\,\rightarrow\,\mathcal{H}^{P}_{2}&=\frac{1}{N_{1}N_{2}}\sum_{\boldsymbol{k}_{1}\in\text{BZ}_{1,\theta_{1}}}\sum_{\boldsymbol{k}_{2}\in\text{BZ}_{2,\theta_{2}}}c^{\dagger}_{1,\boldsymbol{k}_{1}}c^{\phantom{\dagger}}_{2,\boldsymbol{k}_{2}}\sum_{\boldsymbol{R}_{1}\in\text{BL}_{1,\theta_{1}}}\sum_{\boldsymbol{R}_{2}\in\text{BL}_{2,\theta_{2}}}e^{i(\boldsymbol{k}_{2}\boldsymbol{R}_{2}-\boldsymbol{k}_{1}\boldsymbol{R}_{1})}\\

(26)

We now define

\tilde{t}(\boldsymbol{R}_{1}-\boldsymbol{R}_{2}):=\frac{1}{A}\int\mathrm{d}^{2}\boldsymbol{r}\int\mathrm{d}^{2}\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}\,(w^{\theta_{1}}_{1}(\boldsymbol{r}-\boldsymbol{R}_{1}))^{*}t(|\boldsymbol{r}-\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}|)w^{\theta_{2}}_{2}(\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}-\boldsymbol{R}_{2})=:\sum_{\boldsymbol{q}}T_{\boldsymbol{q}}e^{i\boldsymbol{q}(\boldsymbol{R}_{1}-\boldsymbol{R}_{2})},

(27)

leading to

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathcal{H}^{P}_{2}&=\frac{1}{N_{1}N_{2}}\sum_{\boldsymbol{k}_{1}\in\text{BZ}_{1,\theta_{1}}}\sum_{\boldsymbol{k}_{2}\in\text{BZ}_{2,\theta_{2}}}c^{\dagger}_{1,\boldsymbol{k}_{1}}c^{\phantom{\dagger}}_{2,\boldsymbol{k}_{2}}\sum_{\boldsymbol{R}_{1}\in\text{BL}_{1,\theta_{1}}}\sum_{\boldsymbol{R}_{2}\in\text{BL}_{2,\theta_{2}}}\sum_{\boldsymbol{q}}T_{\boldsymbol{q}}e^{i((\boldsymbol{k}_{2}-\boldsymbol{q})\boldsymbol{R}_{2}-(\boldsymbol{k}_{1}-\boldsymbol{q})\boldsymbol{R}_{1})}+\text{H.c.}.\end{split}

(28)

We next use that

\frac{1}{N_{\ell}}\sum_{\boldsymbol{R}\in\text{BL}_{\ell,\theta_{\ell}}}e^{i\boldsymbol{k}\boldsymbol{R}}=\sum_{\boldsymbol{G}\in\text{RL}_{\ell,\theta_{\ell}}}\delta_{\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{G}}=:\delta_{\boldsymbol{k}\in\text{RL}_{\ell,\theta_{\ell}}},

(29)

where
\text{RL}_{\ell,\theta_{\ell}}
is the reciprocal lattice of
\text{BL}_{\ell,\theta_{\ell}}

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}^{P}_{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\boldsymbol{k}_{\ell}\in\text{BZ}_{\ell,\theta_{\ell}}}c^{\dagger}_{1,\boldsymbol{k}_{1}}c^{\phantom{\dagger}}_{2,\boldsymbol{k}_{2}}\sum_{\boldsymbol{q}}T_{\boldsymbol{q}}\delta_{\boldsymbol{k}_{1}-\boldsymbol{q}\in\text{RL}_{1,\theta_{1}}}\delta_{\boldsymbol{k}_{2}-\boldsymbol{q}\in\text{RL}_{2,\theta_{2}}}+\text{H.c.}

(30a)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\boldsymbol{k}_{\ell}\in\text{BZ}_{\ell,\theta_{\ell}}}\sum_{\boldsymbol{G}_{\ell}\in\text{RL}_{\ell,\theta_{\ell}}}c^{\dagger}_{1,\boldsymbol{k}_{1}}c^{\phantom{\dagger}}_{2,\boldsymbol{k}_{2}}\delta_{\boldsymbol{k}_{1}+\boldsymbol{G}_{1},\boldsymbol{k}_{2}+\boldsymbol{G}_{2}}T_{\boldsymbol{k}_{1}+\boldsymbol{G}_{1}}+\text{H.c.}.

(30b)

The total momentum-space Hamiltonian, projected to the band of interested, is then given by

A.2
Generalization to multiple bands and bulk materials

As anticipated above, we now generalize this form to multiple active bands and taking into account that the materials between which an interface is formed are not quasi 2D but instead 3D bulk materials. We start by considering each material

Let us assume that only a subset of

/
\downarrow
(pseudospin-

/
\downarrow

(32)

which are now localized around the rotated centers

where

[cf. also Eq. (

) for the same transformation of the Bloch states]. We denote the electronic creation operators for these basis states by
a^{\dagger}_{\ell,\boldsymbol{R},Z,\alpha}
which allows us to state the Hamiltonian of the heterostructure involving both materials and the interface: as before, the Hamiltonian consists of two parts,
\mathcal{H}_{\text{bulk}}+\mathcal{H}_{\text{inter}}
, with

\mathcal{H}_{\text{bulk}}=\sum_{\ell=1,2}\sum_{\boldsymbol{R},\boldsymbol{R}^{\prime}\in\text{BL}_{\ell}}\sum_{Z,Z^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Z}_{\ell}}\sum_{\alpha,\alpha^{\prime}=1}^{N_{b}^{\ell}}a^{\dagger}_{\ell,\boldsymbol{R},Z,\alpha}\hat{h}_{\alpha,\alpha^{\prime}}^{\ell}(\boldsymbol{R}-\boldsymbol{R}^{\prime},Z,Z^{\prime})a^{\phantom{\dagger}}_{\ell,\boldsymbol{R}^{\prime},Z^{\prime},\alpha^{\prime}},

(33)

where
\hat{h}^{\ell}
contains all the tight-binding matrix elements and
\mathcal{Z}_{\ell}
is the set of lattice planes of material
\ell
; for concreteness, we will choose the interface between the topmost layer (
Z=0
) of
\ell=1
and the bottom layer (
Z=d_{2}
) of
\ell=2
. We then have
\mathcal{Z}_{1}=\{0,-d_{1},-2d_{1},\dots\}
and
\mathcal{Z}_{2}=\{d_{2},2d_{2},3d_{2},\dots\}
. The coupling between the two materials across the interface is captured by

\mathcal{H}_{\text{inter}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N_{1}N_{2}}}\sum_{\boldsymbol{R}_{\ell}\in\text{BL}_{\ell}}\sum_{\alpha_{\ell}=1}^{N_{b}^{\ell}}a^{\dagger}_{1,\boldsymbol{R}_{1},Z=0,\alpha_{1}}\bar{t}_{\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}}\hskip-3.00003pt\left(\mathcal{R}_{\theta_{1}}(\boldsymbol{R}_{1}+\boldsymbol{d}_{\alpha_{1}})-\mathcal{R}_{\theta_{2}}(\boldsymbol{R}_{2}+\boldsymbol{d}_{\alpha_{2}}),z_{\alpha_{1}}-(d+z_{\alpha_{2}})\right)a^{\phantom{\dagger}}_{2,\boldsymbol{R}_{2},Z=d_{2},\alpha_{2}}+\text{H.c.},

(34)

where we made the natural assumption that only the degrees of freedom on the layers closest to the interface couple significantly and that the coupling strength is a function of the distance between the respective Wannier centers and of
\alpha_{1,2}
(the type of Wannier state). To keep the notation compact, we write

t_{\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}}(\mathcal{R}_{\theta_{1}}\boldsymbol{R}_{1}-\mathcal{R}_{\theta_{2}}\boldsymbol{R}_{2})\equiv\bar{t}_{\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}}\hskip-1.00006pt(\mathcal{R}_{\theta_{1}}(\boldsymbol{R}_{1}+\boldsymbol{d}_{\alpha_{1}})-\mathcal{R}_{\theta_{2}}(\boldsymbol{R}_{2}+\boldsymbol{d}_{\alpha_{2}}),z_{\alpha_{1}}-(d+z_{\alpha_{2}}))

(35)

in the following.

We continue by rewriting
\mathcal{H}_{\text{bulk}}
via introduction of partially Fourier-transformed operators,

a_{\ell,\boldsymbol{R},Z,\alpha}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N_{\ell}}}\sum_{\boldsymbol{k}\in\text{BZ}_{\ell}}e^{i\boldsymbol{k}\boldsymbol{R}}\bar{c}_{\ell,\boldsymbol{k},Z,\alpha},

(36)

such that Eq. (
33

\mathcal{H}_{\text{bulk}}=\sum_{\ell=1,2}\sum_{\boldsymbol{k}\in\text{BZ}_{\ell}}\sum_{Z,Z^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Z}_{\ell}}\sum_{\alpha,\alpha^{\prime}=1}^{N_{b}^{\ell}}\bar{c}^{\dagger}_{\ell,\boldsymbol{k},Z,\alpha}h_{\alpha,\alpha^{\prime}}^{\ell}(\boldsymbol{k},Z,Z^{\prime})\bar{c}^{\phantom{\dagger}}_{\ell,\boldsymbol{k},Z^{\prime},\alpha^{\prime}},

(37)

where
h_{\alpha,\alpha^{\prime}}^{\ell}(\boldsymbol{k},Z,Z^{\prime})=\sum_{\boldsymbol{R}\in\text{BL}_{\ell}}e^{i\boldsymbol{k}\boldsymbol{R}}\hat{h}_{\alpha,\alpha^{\prime}}^{\ell}(\boldsymbol{R},Z,Z^{\prime})
. Equation (
37
) is the analogue of Eq. (
15
); similar to our analysis above [cf. Eq. (
23
)], we will transform to the ‘lab frame’,

c_{\ell,\boldsymbol{k},Z,\alpha}=\bar{c}_{\ell,\mathcal{R}_{-\theta_{\ell}}\boldsymbol{k},Z,\alpha},

(38)

and arrive at

\mathcal{H}_{\text{bulk}}=\sum_{\ell=1,2}\sum_{\boldsymbol{k}\in\text{BZ}_{\ell,\theta_{\ell}}}\sum_{Z,Z^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Z}_{\ell}}\sum_{\alpha,\alpha^{\prime}=1}^{N_{b}^{\ell}}c^{\dagger}_{\ell,\boldsymbol{k},Z,\alpha}h_{\alpha,\alpha^{\prime}}^{\ell}(\mathcal{R}_{-\theta_{\ell}}\boldsymbol{k},Z,Z^{\prime})c^{\phantom{\dagger}}_{\ell,\boldsymbol{k},Z^{\prime},\alpha^{\prime}}

(39)

which is the form used in the main text. We insert Eq. (
36
) also into the interlayer tunneling
\mathcal{H}_{\text{inter}}

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{\text{inter}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N_{1}N_{2}}}\sum_{\boldsymbol{R}_{\ell}\in\text{BL}_{\ell}}\sum_{\alpha_{\ell}=1}^{N_{b}^{\ell}}a^{\dagger}_{1,\boldsymbol{R}_{1},Z=0,\alpha_{1}}t_{\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}}(\mathcal{R}_{\theta_{1}}\boldsymbol{R}_{1}-\mathcal{R}_{\theta_{2}}\boldsymbol{R}_{2})a^{\phantom{\dagger}}_{2,\boldsymbol{R}_{2},Z=d_{2},\alpha_{2}}+\text{H.c.},

(40)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N_{1}N_{2}}\sum_{\boldsymbol{k}_{\ell}\in\text{BZ}_{\ell,\theta_{\ell}}}\sum_{\boldsymbol{R}_{\ell}\in\text{BL}_{\ell,\theta_{\ell}}}\sum_{\alpha_{\ell}=1}^{N_{b}^{\ell}}e^{i(\boldsymbol{k}_{2}\boldsymbol{R}_{2}-\boldsymbol{k}_{1}\boldsymbol{R}_{1})}\bar{c}^{\dagger}_{1,\mathcal{R}_{-\theta_{1}}\boldsymbol{k}_{1},Z=0,\alpha_{1}}t_{\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}}(\boldsymbol{R}_{1}-\boldsymbol{R}_{2})\bar{c}^{\phantom{\dagger}}_{2,\mathcal{R}_{-\theta_{2}}\boldsymbol{k}_{2},Z=d_{2},\alpha_{2}}+\text{H.c.}.

(41)

38

t_{\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}}(\boldsymbol{R})=\sum_{\boldsymbol{q}}(T_{\boldsymbol{q}})_{\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}}e^{i\boldsymbol{q}\boldsymbol{R}}

29

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{\text{inter}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\boldsymbol{k}_{\ell}\in\text{BZ}_{\ell,\theta_{\ell}}}\sum_{\boldsymbol{q}}\sum_{\alpha_{\ell}=1}^{N_{b}^{\ell}}\delta_{\boldsymbol{k}_{1}-\boldsymbol{q}\in\text{RL}_{1,\theta_{1}}}\delta_{\boldsymbol{k}_{2}-\boldsymbol{q}\in\text{RL}_{2,\theta_{2}}}c^{\dagger}_{1,\boldsymbol{k}_{1},Z=0,\alpha_{1}}(T_{\boldsymbol{q}})_{\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}}c^{\phantom{\dagger}}_{2,\boldsymbol{k}_{2},Z=d_{2},\alpha_{2}}+\text{H.c.}

(42a)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\boldsymbol{k}_{\ell}\in\text{BZ}_{\ell,\theta_{\ell}}}\sum_{\boldsymbol{G}_{\ell}\in\text{RL}_{\ell,\theta_{\ell}}}\sum_{\alpha_{\ell}=1}^{N_{b}^{\ell}}\delta_{\boldsymbol{k}_{1}+\boldsymbol{G}_{1},\boldsymbol{k}_{2}+\boldsymbol{G}_{2}}c^{\dagger}_{1,\boldsymbol{k}_{1},Z=0,\alpha_{1}}(T_{\boldsymbol{k}_{1}+\boldsymbol{G}_{1}})_{\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}}c^{\phantom{\dagger}}_{2,\boldsymbol{k}_{2},Z=d_{2},\alpha_{2}}+\text{H.c.},

(42b)

which is the analogue of Eq. (
30
) and the form of the momentum-space interface Hamiltonian used in the main text.

A.3
Projection to surface states of topological insulators

To formally connect this Hamiltonian to the discussion of the topological insulator surface states, let us denote the eigenstates of
h_{\alpha,\alpha^{\prime}}^{\ell}(\mathcal{R}_{-\theta_{\ell}}\boldsymbol{k},Z,Z^{\prime})
by
\phi_{\boldsymbol{k},n}
, i.e.,

\sum_{\alpha^{\prime},Z^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Z}_{\ell}}h_{\alpha,\alpha^{\prime}}^{\ell}(\mathcal{R}_{-\theta_{\ell}}\boldsymbol{k},Z,Z^{\prime})(\phi_{\boldsymbol{k},n})_{Z^{\prime},\alpha^{\prime}}=\epsilon_{\boldsymbol{k},n}(\phi_{\boldsymbol{k},n})_{Z,\alpha}.

(43)

If there is a finite bulk gap but in-gap surface states, associated with indices
n\in\mathcal{N}_{\ell}
and around momenta
\boldsymbol{k}\in P_{\ell}
in Eq. (
43
), we can project at low energies to these modes. Instead of using
\phi_{\boldsymbol{k},n}
directly, we perform this projection in a more general basis spanned by the unitarily related states

\widetilde{\phi}_{\boldsymbol{k},\sigma}=\sum_{n\in\mathcal{N}_{\ell}}U_{\sigma,n}(\boldsymbol{k})\phi_{\boldsymbol{k},n},\qquad\sigma=1,2\dots,|\mathcal{N}_{\ell}|,\quad\boldsymbol{k}\in P_{\ell},

(44)

where
U(\boldsymbol{k})
is a unitary
|\mathcal{N}_{\ell}|\times|\mathcal{N}_{\ell}|
matrix. We will only assume that
U(\boldsymbol{k})
is chosen such that
\widetilde{\phi}_{\boldsymbol{k},\sigma}
are smooth in
P_{\ell}
. For instance, for the single Dirac cone surface states analyzed in the main text, we have
|\mathcal{N}_{\ell}|=2
and
\{\widetilde{\phi}_{\boldsymbol{k},\sigma}|\sigma=\uparrow,\downarrow\}
are the wave functions transforming the same way as the spin quantum number (relating the “pseudospin” quantum number to the microscopic degrees of freedom).

To project the Hamiltonian, we substitute

\displaystyle c_{\ell,\boldsymbol{k},Z,\alpha}\,\longrightarrow\,\begin{cases}\sum_{\sigma=1}^{|\mathcal{N}_{\ell}|}\left(\widetilde{\phi}_{\boldsymbol{k},\sigma}\right)_{Z,\alpha}d_{\ell,\boldsymbol{k},\sigma},\quad&\boldsymbol{k}\in P_{\ell},\\
0,\qquad&\boldsymbol{k}\notin P_{\ell},\end{cases}

(45)

for the
\ell
(both or only one of them) with in-gap surface states. In Eq. (
45
),
d_{\ell,\boldsymbol{k},\sigma}
are the electronic operators associated with these surface modes. For instance, if both materials are topological insulators, the projection,
\mathcal{H}_{\text{bulk}}+\mathcal{H}_{\text{inter}}\rightarrow\mathcal{H}^{\text{SS}}_{\text{bulk}}+\mathcal{H}^{\text{SS}}_{\text{inter}}
, leads to an effectively 2D theory with

\mathcal{H}^{\text{SP}}_{\text{bulk}}=\sum_{\ell=1,2}\sum_{\boldsymbol{k}\in P_{\ell}}\sum_{\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}=1}^{|\mathcal{N}_{\ell}|}d^{\dagger}_{\ell,\boldsymbol{k},\sigma}h_{\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}}^{\text{SS},\ell}(\boldsymbol{k})d^{\phantom{\dagger}}_{\ell,\boldsymbol{k},\sigma^{\prime}},

(46)

where we introduced
h_{\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}}^{\text{SS},\ell}(\boldsymbol{k}):=\sum_{Z,Z^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Z}_{\ell}}\sum_{\alpha,\alpha^{\prime}}\left(\widetilde{\phi}^{*}_{\boldsymbol{k},\sigma}\right)_{Z,\alpha}h_{\alpha,\alpha^{\prime}}^{\ell}(\boldsymbol{k},Z,Z^{\prime})\left(\widetilde{\phi}_{\boldsymbol{k},\sigma^{\prime}}\right)_{Z^{\prime},\alpha^{\prime}}
. Furthermore, the interlayer coupling in Eq. (
42
) becomes

\mathcal{H}^{\text{SS}}_{\text{inter}}=\sum_{\boldsymbol{k}_{\ell}\in P_{\ell}}\sum_{\boldsymbol{q}}\sum_{\sigma_{\ell}=1}^{|\mathcal{N}_{\ell}|}\delta_{\boldsymbol{k}_{1}-\boldsymbol{q}\in\text{RL}_{1,\theta_{1}}}\delta_{\boldsymbol{k}_{2}-\boldsymbol{q}\in\text{RL}_{2,\theta_{2}}}d^{\dagger}_{1,\boldsymbol{k}_{1},\sigma_{1}}(T^{\text{SS}}_{\boldsymbol{q}})_{\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2}}d^{\phantom{\dagger}}_{2,\boldsymbol{k}_{2},\sigma_{2}}+\text{H.c.}.

(47)

Similarly, we here defined the projected tunneling Hamiltonian

(T^{\text{SS}}_{\boldsymbol{q}})_{\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2}}:=\sum_{\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}}\left(\widetilde{\phi}^{*}_{\boldsymbol{k}_{1},\sigma_{1}}\right)_{Z=0,\alpha_{1}}(T_{\boldsymbol{q}})_{\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}}\left(\widetilde{\phi}_{\boldsymbol{k}_{2},\sigma_{2}}\right)_{Z=d_{2},\alpha_{2}}.

(48)

Appendix B
Additional subleading crystalline reference points for triangular and square lattices

To find the crystalline reference points and their momentum
Q
, we consider a finite set of points from the square and triangular reciprocal lattices. For any such finite set, one will miss continuum reference points since the required reciprocal lattice vectors are not included. However, for the purpose of determining the leading (and next-to-leading etc.) continuum reference points, it is sufficient to consider a finite set only, as we explain next.

From the definition of
Q
,

Q(\boldsymbol{G}_{\ell},j_{\ell}):=\text{max}_{\ell=1,2}|\boldsymbol{P}_{j_{\ell},\ell}+\boldsymbol{G}_{\ell}|,

(49)

we can see that
Q
increases for large values of
\boldsymbol{G}_{1,2}
. As such, once we have identified the leading continuum reference point using a sufficiently large number of shells, further increasing the number of shells cannot yield an additional continuum reference point with smaller momentum.

To exemplify this reasoning, we show the reference points for the
(X_{1},\Gamma)
case when we consider 8 shells of lattice points in Fig.

. We can see that the number of reference points grows immensely in comparison to what we observe in Fig.

\delta b
with the lowest
Q
value. In Fig.
9
, we show the masked plot. Hence we only see the regions of
\delta b
corresponding to the lowest
Q
. It is no surprise that we find the exact same leading order reference points from the main text.
