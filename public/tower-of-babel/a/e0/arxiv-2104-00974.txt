A test of cosmic distance duality relation using SPT-SZ galaxy clusters, Type Ia supernovae, and cosmic chronometers
Kamal Bora, Shantanu Desai

Abstract
We carry out a test of the cosmic distance duality relation using a sample of 52 SPT-SZ clusters, along with X-ray measurements from XMM-Newton. To carry out this test, we need an estimate of the luminosity distance ( D L D_{L} ) at the redshift of the cluster. For this purpose, we use three independent methods: directly using D L D_{L} from the closest Type Ia Supernovae from the Union 2.1 sample, non-parametric reconstruction of D L D_{L} using the same Union 2.1 sample, and finally using H ⁡ ( z ) H(z) measurements from cosmic chronometers and reconstructing D L D_{L} using Gaussian Process regression. We use four different functions to characterize the deviations from CDDR. All our results for these ( 4 × 3 4\times 3 ) analyses are consistent with CDDR to within 1 σ \sigma .



I
Introduction

One of the most fundamental tenet in Cosmology is the cosmic distance duality relation (CDDR, hereafter) between the angular diameter distance (
D_{A}
) and the luminosity distance (
D_{L}
), which is sometimes also known as the Etherington relation
Etherington 1933
;
Ellis 2007
. This relation is given by:

\eta(z)\equiv\frac{D_{L}}{D_{A}(1+z)^{2}}=1

(1)

The CDDR involves three assumptions
Bassett and Kunz 2004
;
Qin et al. 2021
: spacetime is endowed with a metric theory of gravity; photons propagate along null geodesics; and the total number of photons is conserved. The CDDR relation is one of the edifice of the standard
\Lambda
CDM model
Ratra and Vogeley 2008
. Although, the
\Lambda
CDM is very successful in describing the large scale structure from CMB and other probes
(
Planck Collaboration
et al. 2020
)
, a number of tensions have crept up in recent years, such as the Hubble constant tension between low redshift and high redshift probes
Di Valentino et al. 2021
;
Verde et al. 2019
;
Bethapudi and Desai 2017
,
\sigma_{8}
tension
Benisty 2021
, Lithium-7 problem in Big-Bang Nucleosynthesis
Fields et al. 2020
, core-cusp and missing satellites problem
Bullock and Boylan-Kolchin 2017
;
Weinberg et al. 2015
, failure to detect cold dark matter in the laboratory
Merritt 2017
, failure to explain regularities in cosmic structure at galactic scales such as the radial acceleration relation and Baryonic Tully-Fisher relation
McGaugh et al. 2016
;
Pradyumna et al. 2021
.
Therefore, a large number of works have tried to test the CDDR in a model-independent way using a variety of astrophysical probes
Uzan et al. 2004
;
de Bernardis et al. 2006
;
Khedekar and Chakraborti 2011
;
Li et al. 2011
;
Nair et al. 2011
;
Meng et al. 2012
;
Ellis et al. 2013
;
Liao et al. 2016
;
Lv and Xia 2016
;
Li and Lin 2018
;
Lin et al. 2018
;
Yang et al. 2019
;
Ruan et al. 2018
;
Lyu et al. 2020
;
Qin et al. 2021
;
Arjona 2020
. Any violation of CDDR would result in a value for
\eta(z)
in Eq.
1
, different from one. In such cases,
D_{L}
can be expressed in terms of
D_{A}
and
\eta(z)
, which is sometimes known as the deformed CDDR relation
Holanda et al. 2019
.

In this work, we shall use galaxy cluster observations observed in the microwave, via Sunyaev-Zeldovich effect (SZ, hereafter) and X-rays to test CDDR. Galaxy clusters are the most massive virialized collapsed objects in the universe and are wonderful laboratories for a whole range of topics in Cosmology, galaxy evolution, and fundamental Physics
Allen et al. 2011
;
Kravtsov and Borgani 2012
;
Vikhlinin et al. 2014
;
Desai 2018
.
The first test of CDDR using galaxy clusters was done by
Uzan et al. 2004
. They used the X-ray and SZ observations from
Reese et al. 2002
to test the validity of the CDDR, and obtained a value for
\eta
consistent with one at
2\sigma
. Later,
de Bernardis et al. 2006
did a similar test using the angular diameter distances to 38 galaxy clusters from X-ray observations with Chandra and SZ observations with the OVRO and BIMA interferometric arrays. Their results were consistent with no violation of CDDR at
1\sigma
.
The first model-independent cosmological test of CDDR was carried out in
Holanda et al. 2010
, by using joint SZ and X-ray surface brightness measurements, along with Type Ia supernovae (SNe) from the Constitution data
Hicken et al. 2009
. Here, they used two different sample of galaxy clusters from
LaRoque et al. 2006
and
Ettori et al. 2009
, corresponding to elliptical and spherical geometry, respectively.
For their analyses, two different parametric forms for
\eta(z)
were tested. Their results for clusters with elliptical geometry were consistent with CDDR to within
2\sigma
. For the sample with spherical geometry, discrepancy with CDDR at about
3\sigma
was reported.
Hence, they concluded that the elliptical geometry of galaxy clusters is compatible with the validity of CDDR but not the spherical geometry.

Gonçalves
et al. 2012
used X-ray
f_{gas}
data, along with SNe Ia from the Union 2 compilation
Amanullah et al. 2010
. Here also, the same two samples of galaxy clusters
LaRoque et al. 2006
;
Ettori et al. 2009
were used. This work found that one of the samples
LaRoque et al. 2006
is consistent with CDDR, whereas the other sample
Ettori et al. 2009
shows a 3.5
\sigma
deviation from CDDR.
Later,
Holanda et al. 2012
reported no violation of CDDR using a combination of SZ and X-ray gas mass fraction data
LaRoque et al. 2006
.

Liang et al. 2013
tested CDDR using a sample of 38 angular diameter distances from galaxy clusters assuming the spherical model
Bonamente et al. 2006
, along with SNe Ia data from the Union 2 compilation
(
Amanullah et al. 2010
)
.
They found no evidence for the violation of CDDR at
1-2\sigma
, depending on the parameterization used.

Yang et al. 2013
carried out a model-independent test of CDDR using the angular diameter distances from two different cluster samples
Bonamente et al. 2006
;
De Filippis et al. 2005
corresponding to spherical and elliptical geometry, respectively, in conjunction with the Union 2 SNe Ia sample
Amanullah et al. 2010
. However, instead of using the SNe Ia
D_{L}
from the distance modulus, they used the original SNe data for the rest-frame peak magnitudes and other parameters describing the influence of intrinsic color and reddening by dust. They obtained no violation of CDDR at
1\sigma
for
both the elliptical and spherical cluster samples, but concluded that the spherical model
Bonamente et al. 2006
can better describe the intrinsic geometry of clusters as compared to the elliptical one
De Filippis et al. 2005
, if the CDDR relation is valid.

Santos-da-Costa
et al. 2015
used two different methods to test CDDR using 38 angular diameter distances obtained from
Bonamente et al. 2006
(which was also used in earlier tests of CDDR
Yang et al. 2013
), along with
f_{gas}
measurements
LaRoque et al. 2006
spanning the redshift range
0.14<z<0.89
. This was then compared with an independent estimate of the angular diameter distance, reconstructed using
H(z)
data from cosmic chronometers and BAO. In this work, the departure from CDDR was reconstructed in a non-parametric manner. Both the methods were consistent with the validity of CDDR at
1\sigma
.
Holanda and Pereira 2016
studied the temporal variation of the fine structure constant, modified distance duality relation, and the modified evolution law of the cosmic microwave background radiation by using the angular diameter distances to 29 galaxy clusters
Bonamente et al. 2006
, Union 2.1 Type Ia SNe compilation
Suzuki et al. 2012
, and 38
T_{CMB}
measurements
Luzzi et al. 2009
;
Hurier et al. 2014
. All of these variants/modifications of the Standard Model arise naturally from a class of modified gravity theories, which break the Einstein Equivalence Principle. Their results are consistent with no violation of these laws at
1\sigma
. (See also
Holanda
et al. 2017a
;
Holanda
et al. 2017b
for similar follow-up studies).

Holanda et al. 2019
(H19, hereafter) have also done a study of the validity of CDDR relation using the
Y_{SZ}-Y_{X}
scaling relation for 61 Planck ESZ clusters jointly with deep XMM-Newton archive observations in the redshift range
0.044\leqslant z\leqslant 0.444

Planck Collaboration
et al. 2011
, along with Type Ia SNe from the Pantheon compilation
Scolnic et al. 2018
. They reported no violation of CDDR at
1.5\sigma
. Most recently,
da Silva et al. 2020
did a Bayesian model comparison assuming CDDR violation for different forms of
\eta(z)
using two sets of data namely SNe Ia
Scolnic et al. 2018
+ angular diameter distances from galaxy clusters
De Filippis et al. 2005
and SNe Ia
Scolnic et al. 2018
+ gas mass fraction data
Mantz et al. 2014
. Their results showed agreement with CDDR at
2\sigma
for SNe Ia and the angular diameter distances from galaxy clusters sample, and at
1\sigma
for SNe Ia along with the gas mass fraction measurements.

In this work, we shall use SZ selected clusters from the South Pole telescope in conjunction with X-ray followup observations from the XMM-Newton telescope to test for a violation of CDDR.
This paper is structured as follows. In Sec.
II
, we briefly explain the basic theory behind the SZ and X-ray observables used to test CDDR. In Sec.
III
, we describe our cluster sample. Sec.
IV
discusses the calculation of
D_{L}
using three independent methods. Details of our analysis and results can be found in Sec.
V
. We conclude in Sec.
VI
.

II
methodology

The Inverse Compton scattering between the CMB photons and the hot electrons present in the intra-cluster medium causes a spectral distortion of the CMB black body spectrum
Sunyaev and Zeldovich 1972
;
Birkinshaw 1999
;
Carlstrom et al. 2002
;
Mroczkowski et al. 2019
. This spectral distortion is known as the SZ effect. It is nearly independent of redshift, and hence can detect galaxy clusters upto very high redshifts.
The distortion is characterized by a parameter, called Compton-
y
parameter, which is given by
Carlstrom et al. 2002
;
Birkinshaw 1999
:

y=\frac{{\sigma_{T}}k_{B}}{m_{e}c^{2}}\int n_{e}Tdl,

(2)

where,
T
is the electron temperature,
m_{e}
is mass of the electron,
c
is the speed of light,
n_{e}
is the electron number density,
k_{B}
is the Boltzmann constant, and
\sigma_{T}
is the Thompson scattering cross section which is given by,

\sigma_{T}=\frac{8\pi}{3}\left(\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{m_{e}c^{2}}\right)^{2}=\frac{8\pi}{3}\left(\frac{\hbar^{2}\alpha^{2}}{m_{e}^{2}c^{2}}\right)

(3)

If we model the variation in
\alpha
as
\alpha(z)\equiv\alpha_{0}\phi(z)
, where
\alpha_{0}
is the present value of
\alpha
, the fractional variation in
\alpha
can be written as,

\frac{\Delta\alpha}{\alpha_{0}}=\phi(z)-1

(4)

We assume that the intra-cluster medium is an ideal gas, with its equation of state given by
P=n_{e}k_{B}T
, where
P
refers the pressure of the intra-cluster gas. The relation between the integrated Compton parameter over the solid angle (
Y_{SZ}
) and the angular diameter distance to the cluster (
D_{A}
) is given by
Bora and Desai 2021a
:

Y_{SZ}D_{A}^{2}\propto\phi(z)^{2}

(5)

The hot intra-cluster gas emits X-rays mainly through thermal bremsstrahlung emission
Sarazin 1986
;
Allen et al. 2011
. If
M_{g}(R)
is the gas mass within radius
R
and
T_{X}
is the X-ray temperature, then the average thermal energy of the cluster gas is given by
Kravtsov et al. 2006
,

Y_{X}=M_{g}(R)T_{X}

(6)

An exact expression for the gas mass
M_{g}(<R)
within a radius
R
can be obtained by assuming that the number density obeys the spherical-
\beta
model
Sarazin 1986
and X-ray emission via thermal bremsstrahlung, and is given by
Sasaki 1996

M_{g}(<R)=m_{H}\left(\frac{3\pi\hbar m_{e}c^{2}}{2(1+X)e^{6}}\right)^{1/2}\left(\frac{3m_{e}c^{2}}{2\pi k_{B}T_{e}}\right)^{1/4}\frac{1}{[\overline{g_{B}}(T_{e})]^{1/2}}{r_{c}}^{3/2}\left[\frac{I_{M}(R/r_{c},\beta)}{I_{L}^{1/2}(R/r_{c},\beta)}\right][L_{X}(<R)]^{1/2}\;,

(7)

We then absorb the terms involving
e
,
c
, and
\hbar
into
\alpha
, and rewrite this expression in terms of
\alpha\equiv\alpha_{0}\phi(z)
(from Eq.
4
) as follows:

M_{g}(<R)=\alpha_{0}\phi(z)^{-3/2}m_{H}\left(\frac{3\pi m_{e}}{2(1+X)\hbar^{2}}\right)^{1/2}\left(\frac{3m_{e}}{2\pi k_{B}T_{e}}\right)^{1/4}\frac{1}{[\overline{g_{B}}(T_{e})]^{1/2}}{r_{c}}^{3/2}\left[\frac{I_{M}(R/r_{c},\beta)}{I_{L}^{1/2}(R/r_{c},\beta)}\right][L_{X}(<R)]^{1/2}\;,

(8)

In Eq.
8
,
L_{X}(<R)
is the total X-ray luminosity;
X
is the mass fraction of hydrogen,
r_{c}
indicates the core radius;
T_{e}
is the gas temperature,
\overline{g_{B}}(T_{e})
the
Gaunt factor; and
I_{M}
,

I_{L}
are defined as follows
Sasaki 1996
:

I_{M}(R/r_{c},\beta)\equiv\int_{0}^{R/r_{c}}(1+x^{2})^{-3\beta/2}x^{2}dx\;,

I_{L}(R/r_{c},\beta)\equiv\int_{0}^{R/r_{c}}(1+x^{2})^{-3\beta}x^{2}dx\;.

L_{X}
,
r_{c}
, and
R
are not direct observables, but depend on the underlying cosmological model and are given as
Sasaki 1996
:

\displaystyle L_{X}(<R)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 4\pi D_{L}^{2}f_{X}(<\theta),

(9)

\displaystyle r_{c}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\theta_{c}D_{A},

(10)

\displaystyle R

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\theta D_{A},

(11)

where
f_{X}(<\theta)
is the total bolometric flux within the outer angular radius
\theta
, and
\theta_{c}
is the core angular radius.
Therefore, from Eq.
8
, Eq.
9
, and Eq.
10

we find that
M_{g}(R)
scales with
\phi(z)
,
D_{L}
, and
D_{A}
according to:

M_{g}(<\theta)\propto\phi(z)^{-3/2}D_{L}D_{A}^{3/2},

(12)

D_{L}
and
D_{A}
are connected via CDDR (cf. Eq.
1
). If we parameterize a violation of CDDR using the deformed CDDR
D_{L}\equiv\eta(z)(1+z)^{2}D_{A}
, then
M_{g}
and consequently,
Y_{X}
scales with
\alpha
and
\eta(z)
as
Colaço
et al. 2019
;
Bora and Desai 2021a
,

Y_{X}\propto M_{g}\propto\phi(z)^{-3/2}\eta(z)

(13)

For a wide class of modified theories of gravity
Hees et al. 2014
;
Minazzoli and Hees 2014
;
Gonçalves
et al. 2020
, Einstein’s equivalence principle breaks down due to the coupling of the scalar field with the electromagnetic sector. In these theories,
\alpha(z)
(and thereby
\phi(z)
) and
\eta(z)
are intertwined and related according to
\phi(z)=\left(\eta(z)\right)^{2}
.

Y_{SZ}
and
Y_{X}
can then be rewritten in terms of
\eta(z)
as:

Y_{SZ}D_{A}^{2}\propto\eta(z)^{4}

(14)

and

Y_{X}\propto\eta(z)^{-2}

(15)

Y_{SZ}
and
Y_{X}
are two different proxies for the thermal energy of the cluster
Rozo et al. 2012
, so the ratio
Y_{SZ}D_{A}^{2}/C_{XSZ}Y_{X}
is expected be constant with redshift, since both these quantities scale with redshift and mass in exactly the same way. Simulations show that this ratio is constant with
5-15\%
scatter
Planelles et al. 2017
;
Biffi et al. 2014
;
Fabjan et al. 2011
;
Kay et al. 2012
;
Stanek et al. 2010
. The ratio would be exactly one for clusters with isothermal or universal temperature profiles
Loken et al. 2002
;
Galli 2013
;
Colaço
et al. 2019
.
From Eq.
14
and
15
, for any violations of CDDR, this ratio scales with
\eta(z)
as:

\frac{Y_{SZ}D_{A}^{2}}{Y_{X}C_{XSZ}}=C\eta(z)^{6}

(16)

where
C_{XSZ}\approx 1.416\times 10^{-19}\left(\frac{Mpc^{2}}{M_{\odot}keV}\right)
and
C
is an arbitrary constant, which contains all the cluster astrophysics and is equal to one for an isothermal profile
Colaço
et al. 2019
.

From Eq.
6
and
12
, one can see that
Y_{X}
scales with
D_{A}
according
Y_{X}\propto D_{A}^{5/2}
, if we assume a flat
\Lambda
CDM cosmology and the validity of CDDR. Following H19, we multiply
Y_{X}
by
D_{A}^{5/2}/(D_{A}^{ref})^{5/2}
, where
D_{A}^{ref}
is the angular diameter distance in the fiducial reference
\Lambda
CDM model, corresponding to
\Omega_{M}=0.3
and
\Omega_{\Lambda}=0.7
.
This eliminates the dependence of
M_{g}(R)
, with respect to the fiducial cosmology.
Hence we get,

\frac{Y_{SZ}D_{A}^{2}(D_{A}^{ref})^{5/2}}{Y_{X}C_{XSZ}D_{A}^{5/2}}=C\eta(z)^{6}

(17)

Since we want to carry out a test of CDDR in a model-agnostic fashion, we recast this equation in terms of
D_{L}
by parameterizing any violation of CDDR using
\eta(z)
defined in Eq.
1
.
So Eq.
17
can be written as,

\frac{Y_{SZ}(D_{A}^{ref})^{5/2}(1+z)}{Y_{X}C_{XSZ}D_{L}^{1/2}}=C\eta(z)^{11/2}

(18)

III
Cluster Sample

For our analysis, we use
Y_{SZ}
and
Y_{X}
for 58 SPT-SZ selected galaxy clusters from
Bulbul et al. 2019
. The SZ data have been taken from the South Pole Telescope (hereafter SPT) which is a 10 m millimeter wave telescope located at the South Pole
Carlstrom et al. 2011
. One of the main goals of the SPT is to find galaxy clusters using their SZ signatures upto very high redshifts. SPT has imaged the sky at three different frequencies viz. 95 GHz, 150 GHz, and 220 GHz
Carlstrom et al. 2011
, and carried out a 2500 square degree survey between 2007 to 2011. A total of 516 galaxy clusters have been detected in this survey, with a mass threshold of
3\times 10^{14}M_{\odot}
upto a redshift of 1.8
Bleem et al. 2015
;
Bocquet et al. 2019
. Many dedicated optical surveys, e.g. DES
Saro et al. 2015
, BCS
Desai et al. 2012
as well as targeted observations
Song et al. 2012
have followed-up the SPT cluster candidates in order to determine confirm these as true clusters and determine their redshifts.
Here, we have also used the X-ray observations (by XMM-Newton) for 58 of these clusters, in the redshift range 0.2
\leq

z

\leq
1.5
Bulbul et al. 2019
. The
Y_{X}
parameter from XMM-Newton measurements has been measured at
R_{500}
.
The
Y_{SZ}
measurements provided by SPT have been obtained by averaging over a cylindrical volume. These need to be transformed to a spherical volume with radius equal to
R_{500}
, since
Y_{X}
measurements are provided at
R_{500}
. The details of the conversion procedure are discussed in the Appendix.

IV
Calculation of luminosity distance
D_{L}

We use three different methods to estimate the the luminosity distance,
D_{L}
for each cluster. We now describe each of these methods.

IV.1
Method 1: Using a redshift cut

We use Type Ia SNe sample from the Union 2.1 compilation
Suzuki et al. 2012
, which consists of 580 Type Ia supernovae data spanning the redshift range
0.015\leqslant z\leqslant 1.414
(with median redshift of
z\approx
0.294) in order to estimate
D_{L}
for the SPT-SZ sample by choosing the closest SNe corresponding to every cluster. Similar to H19, we use the following cut on the supernova redshift (
z_{SNe}
) for a cluster with redshift
z
:
|z-z_{SNe}|\leq 0.005
. For most galaxy clusters, there are multiple supernovae satisfying the above cut. To determine the
D_{L}
and its error at the cluster location in such cases, we first need to evaluate the weighted average of the distance modulus of Type Ia SNe (
\bar{\mu}(z)
) and its associated error.

To reconstruct
D_{L}
from the distance modulus
\mu
, we start from the following equation
Huterer and Shafer 2018
:

\mu=m-M=5\log_{10}\left(\frac{d_{L}}{10(pc)}\right)

(19)

The Type Ia SNe surveys cannot directly measure
\mu
, as they only measure the relative distances. The distance modulus for the Union 2.1 catalog
1
1

1

This catalog has been downloaded from
http://supernova.lbl.gov/Union/figures/SCPUnion2.1_mu_vs_z.txt
, has obtained by assuming
M=-19.31
and
h=0.7
, where
h\equiv H_{0}/100
. Therefore, we can re-write
\mu
as a function of
h
as follows:

\mu=m-M=m-\left[-19.31+5\log_{10}\left(\frac{h}{0.7}\right)\right]

(20)

Currently, there are discrepancies between the different measurements of
H_{0}
, with a fierce ongoing debate on whether this is a harbinger of new Physics or only systematics
Di Valentino et al. 2021
. The tension between the model-independent low redshift based probes and those estimated using early time probes with the
\Lambda
CDM model ranges between 4-6
\sigma
. (See
Di Valentino et al. 2021
;
Verde et al. 2019
;
Bethapudi and Desai 2017
for extensive reviews of these different measurements).
\textcolor
blackSince the
\mu
obtained from Type Ia SNe measurements has an explicit dependence on
h
, we consider it as a nuisance parameter and marginalize over

h
, while testing for deviations from CDDR.
This is similar to the analysis done in
Wu et al. 2015
.
For this purpose, we use a Gaussian prior on
h
, using the latest
H_{0}
measurement from the SH0ES team of
H_{0}=73.2\pm 1.3
km/sec/Mpc
Riess et al. 2021
. The reason is that the SH0ES measurement is agnostic to details of the underlying
\Lambda
CDM model, and is based on directly measuring the distance-redshift relation by constructing a distance ladder.

One then invert Eq.
19

to obtain
D_{L}
as a function of
h
using the
\mu
provided for the Union 2.1 catalog

D_{L}(z)\text{(Mpc)}=\left(\frac{0.7}{h}\right)10^{(\mu-25)/5}

(21)

In case of multiple matches for a given redshift, we use the weighted average
\bar{\mu}(z)
in the above equation.
\textcolor
blackWe then plug Eq.
21
in Eq.
18
.

For six clusters, we could not find any supernovae counterparts with the aforementioned redshift cut, and these clusters were therefore culled from our sample. Thereafter, we are left with a sample of 52 clusters for our analysis.

IV.2
Method 2: Non-parametric reconstruction using Type Ia SNe

We now adopt another method to find
D_{L}
for every cluster, by using the Gaussian Processes Regression (GPR). A Gaussian process is the generalization of a Gaussian distribution. It is characterized by a mean and a covariance function (usually called the kernel function)
Seikel et al. 2012
. More details about GPR can be found in our previous works
Singirikonda and Desai 2020
;
Bora and Desai 2021b
, and references therein. For this work, we select the squared exponential (RBF) covariance function, which is given by:

K(x,\tilde{x})=\sigma_{f}^{2}\exp{\left[\frac{-(x-\tilde{x})^{2}}{2l^{2}}\right]},

(22)

It depends on two hyperparameters:
\sigma_{f}
and
l
. The length parameter
l
controls the smoothness of the kernel function. To reconstruct
D_{L}
at the cluster redshift, we used the
scikit-learn
module in python
Pedregosa et al. 2011
. Fig
1
shows the reconstructed luminosity distance as a function of
z
using GPR. Using this non-parametric reconstruction, one can estimate
D_{L}
for every SPT-SZ cluster. In order to be consistent with Method 1, we omit the same six clusters, for which
D_{L}
could not be estimated using Method 1.
\textcolor
blackSimilar to Method 1 ,
D_{L}
reconstructed using GPR from the Union 2.1 sample also needs to be rescaled as a function of
h
, while testing for CDDR violation, in order to marginalize over
h
.

Suzuki et al. 2012
. The black line indicates the best GP fit to the data along with
1\sigma
and
2\sigma
error bands shown by the two different shades of blue color.

h=0.7

IV.3
Method 3: Reconstruction using cosmic chronometers

Here, we used 31
H(z)
measurements from
Li et al. 2021
(same as that used in
Singirikonda and Desai 2020
;
Bora and Desai 2021b
) spanning the redshift range
0.07\leqslant z\leqslant 1.965
. These

measurements are obtained from cosmic chronometers, which provide a model agnostic estimate of the expansion history at any redshift
z

Jimenez and Loeb 2002
.
Using these

for any redshift. Furthermore, the reconstructed

which can be written as,

D_{L}(z)=c(1+z)\int_{0}^{z}\frac{dz^{{}^{\prime}}}{H(z^{\prime})}

(23)

where
H(z^{\prime})
is the reconstructed Hubble data. Fig
2
shows the reconstructed

data from chronometers as a function of

along with their

and
2\sigma

. The black line indicates the best GP fit to data along with

In order to constrain
\eta(z)
(as used in Eq.
18
), we use the following parametric forms
Hees et al. 2014
. These were also used in H19 and other previous works testing for a violation of CDDR.

\displaystyle\eta(z)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1+\eta_{0}z

(24)

\displaystyle\eta(z)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1+\eta_{0}[z/(1+z)]

(25)

\displaystyle\eta(z)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1+\eta_{0}\ln(1+z)

(26)

\displaystyle\eta(z)

\displaystyle(1+z)^{\eta_{0}}

\eta_{0}

We now proceed to obtaining constraints on
\eta(z)
for each of the parametrizations. The likelihood function
\mathcal{L}
used in our analysis is given by:

-2\ln\mathcal{L}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{(\eta_{obs}(z_{i})-C\eta(z)^{11/2})^{2}}{\sigma_{i}^{2}}+\sum_{i=1}^{N}\ln 2\pi{\sigma_{i}^{2}},

(28)

where
\eta_{obs}
is obtained from Eq.
18
and
\sigma_{i}
denotes the total error calculated as follows:

\sigma_{i}^{2}=\sigma_{\eta}^{2}+\sigma_{int}^{2}

(29)

where,
\sigma_{\eta}^{2}

\eta_{obs}
which is calculated by propagating the errors in
Y_{SZ}
,
Y_{X}
,
D_{L}(z)
, and
D_{A}^{ref}

\textcolor
blackWhen we estimate

h

We have also included an intrinsic scatter term (
\sigma_{int}

Foreman-Mackey
et al. 2013
to estimate the model parameters i.e.
\eta_{0}
and

VI
Conclusions

In this work, we carried out a model-independent test of CDDR, along the same lines as H19. For this purpose, we used 52 SPT-SZ clusters in the redshift range 0.2
\leq

z

\leq
1.5 in conjunction with X-ray measurements from XMM-Newton
Bulbul et al. 2019
. To get independent distance measurements at the cluster redshifts, we used
D_{L}
from Union 2.1 Type Ia SNe sample
Suzuki et al. 2012
and
H(z)
measurements from cosmic chronometers
Li et al. 2021
. The cluster observables used to test CDDR include the SZ Compto-ionization parameter (
Y_{SZ}
), along with its X-ray counterpart (
Y_{X}
), both of which are independent thermal energy proxies, having different scalings with
\eta(z)
(cf. Eq.
14
and
15
). The
Y_{SZ}
to
Y_{X}
ratio is then used to test the CDDR, which is given by Eq.
18
. One input needed for this analysis is the luminosity distance at the cluster redshift, for which we have used three independent estimators. These are summarized in Sect
IV
and tabulated in Table
1
.

The dimensionless ratio of
Y_{SZ}
to
Y_{X}
(after suitable scalings) is shown in Fig
4
as a function of
z
for all the three methods used for estimating
D_{L}
.

To test the validity of CDDR, we chose different parametric forms, defined in Eqs.
24
- Eq.
27
, where the parameter
\eta_{0}
characterizes any departure from CDDR.
Table
1
shows all the possible used cases studied in this work.
Furthermore, to estimate the parameters in Eqs.
24
- Eq.
27
, we maximize the likelihood function given by Eq
28
.
\textcolor
blackTwo of the three methods obtain the
D_{L}
value from the Union 2.1 catalog, which assumed
h=0.7
. While using these
D_{L}
values, we marginalized over
h
, using a Gaussian prior over
h
from the latest SH0ES measurement
Riess et al. 2021
, in order to obtain marginalized constraints on the CDDR violating parameters.

Our results for all these analyses can be found in Table
2
along with a comparison with previous results.
A graphical summary of all our results for CDDR (for all the cases studied herein) can be found in Fig
8
.
\textcolor
blackFor Cases I, V, and IX, which test the parametric form in Eq.
24
using Methods 1, 2, and 3, respectively the joint 2-D credible intervals for
\eta_{0}
,
C
(a parameter in the
Y_{SZ}
to
Y_{X}
ratio) and
h
(if needed) can be found in Fig
5
- Fig
7
. Our results from all these analyses are consistent with CDDR within
1\sigma
.
Therefore, we conclude that there is no evidence for a violation of CDDR.

APPENDIX

The
Y_{SZ}
measurements provided by SPT have been obtained by averaging over a cylindrical volume within an aperture radius of
0.75R^{\prime}

Bleem et al. 2015
. Since, the
Y_{SZ}
parameter defined in Eq.
14
needs to be averaged over a spherical volume, we need to convert the cylindrically averaged
Y_{SZ}
(provided by SPT) to a spherically averaged
Y_{SZ}
at
R_{500}
, for which we have used the following conversion method
Arnaud et al. 2010
;
Melin et al. 2011
:

\displaystyle Y_{cyl}(R_{1})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle Y_{sph}(R_{b})-\frac{\sigma_{T}}{m_{e}c^{2}}\int_{R_{1}}^{R_{b}}4\pi P(r)\sqrt{r^{2}-R_{1}^{2}}rdr

(30)

\displaystyle Y_{sph}(R_{2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\sigma_{T}}{m_{e}c^{2}}\int_{0}^{R_{2}}4\pi P(r)r^{2}dr

(31)

where the subscripts
cyl
and
sph
denote the
Y_{SZ}
parameter, measured in cylindrical and spherical volumes, respectively.
R_{1}
indicates the cylindrical aperture in which
Y_{cyl}
is measured and
R_{b}
denotes the radial extent of the cluster. We assume
R_{b}
=
10R_{500}

Arnaud et al. 2010
,
R_{1}=0.75^{\prime}R_{500}
corresponding to the SPT measurement, and
R_{2}=R_{500}
, since
Y_{X}
measurements are provided at
R_{500}
. Note that in
Liu et al. 2015
, the SPT collaboration assumed
R_{b}=5R_{500}
, in order to convert
Y_{SZ}(cyl)
to
Y_{SZ}(sph)
, for a sample of low-mass clusters and groups.
However, the difference in the estimation of
Y_{SZ}(sph)
with these two values of
R_{b}
is negligible.
In Eq.
31
,
P(r)
refers to the pressure profile.
To do the conversion, we have used the Universal Pressure Profile (UPP) for
P(r)

Arnaud et al. 2010
, similar to our previous work
Bora and Desai 2021a
.

\frac{P(r)}{P_{500}}=\frac{P_{0}}{x^{\gamma}(1+x^{\alpha})^{(\beta-\gamma)/\alpha}}

(32)

where
x=r/R_{500}
, and
P_{500}
is given by
Nagai et al. 2007

P_{500}=1.65\times 10^{-3}E(z)^{8/3}\left[\frac{M_{500}}{3\times 10^{14}h_{70}^{-1}M_{\odot}}\right]^{2/3}h^{2}_{70}keVcm^{-3}

(33)

As a cross-check we also did the conversion using the Battaglia et al (BPP) pressure profile
Battaglia et al. 2010
, which is given by

P/P_{500}=A[1+(x/x_{c})^{\alpha}]^{-\gamma/\alpha}

(34)

where
x=r/R_{500}
; and the values of the constants
A
,
\alpha
, and
\gamma
are provided in
Battaglia et al. 2010
. A histogram of the difference in
Y_{SZ}(sph)
between the two pressure profiles is shown in Fig.
9
. Since the difference between the two estimates is negligible, we do only report results using the UPP profile in this manuscript.

Therefore, from Eq.
30
and Eq.
31
, we can estimate the ratio of
Y_{sph}(R_{500})
to
Y_{cyl}(0.75^{\prime}D_{A})
. By using this ratio one can estimate
Y_{sph}(R_{500})
for every SPT-SZ cluster.

Figure 9:
Histogram of the fractional deviation in
Y_{sph}(R_{500})
between the UPP and BPP pressure profile for the SPT-SZ clusters analyzed in this work. As we can see, the fractional deviation is less than 1%.
