Non-linear characterization of Jordan ∗ * -isomorphisms via maps on positive cones of C ∗ C^{*} -algebras
Osamu Hatori, Shiho Oi

Abstract
. We study maps between positive definite or positive semidefinite cones of unital C ∗ C^{*} -algebras. We describe surjective maps that preserve (1) the norm of the quotient or multiplication of elements; (2) the spectrum of the quotient or multiplication of elements; (3) the spectral seminorm of the quotient or multiplication of elements. These maps relate to the Jordan ∗ * -isomorphisms between the specified C ∗ C^{*} -algebras. While a surjection between positive definite cones that preserves the norm of the quotient of elements may not be extended to a linear map between the underlying C ∗ C^{*} -algebras, the other types of surjections can be extended to a Jordan ∗ * -isomorphism or a Jordan ∗ * -isomorphism followed by the implementation by a positive invertible element. We also study conditions for the centrality of positive invertible elements. We generalize ”the corollary” regarding surjections between positive semidefinite cones of unital C ∗ C^{*} -algebras. Applying it, we provide positive solutions to the problem posed by Molnár for general unital C ∗ C^{*} -algebras.

We will start by clarifying the notation and introducing the necessary definitions and properties that we will use throughout the paper.
In this paper, we use

to denote unital

-algebras. We always write the unit by

We denote

The positive semidefinite cone is

The spectrum of

It is worth noting that

A Jordan

-isomorphism is a complex linear bijective map between two unital
C^{*}
-algebras that preserves the Jordan product (

It preserves squaring,

-isomorphism may not preserve multiplication, it is known to preserve the triple product: that is,
J(aba)=J(a)J(b)J(a)
, for
a,b

It is worth noting that a Jordan
*
-isomorphism preserves the norm. Kadison’s theorem is a celebrated result stating that if a complex linear unital bijection preserves the norm, it is a Jordan
*
-isomorphism
[
14
, Theorem 7]

A complex linear unital bijection is a Jordan
*
-isomorphism if it preserves the order in both directions, as stated in
[
15
, Corollary 5]

Molnár presented the following striking result in
[
21
]

\phi\colon A_{1+}^{-1}\to A_{2+}^{-1}

We say that

\phi(a)\leq\phi(b)

We say that

It is worth noting that if

\phi(x)=\phi(x^{\prime})

\phi(x)\leq\phi(x^{\prime})

In section

2.
Maps that preserve the spectrum or norm of the quotients of elements in positive definite cones

We aim to prove that the map
x\mapsto(ax^{2}a)^{\frac{1}{2}}a^{-1}
on
A_{+}^{-1}
preserves the norm in Proposition
2.1
, for any
a\in A_{+}^{-1}
. We can extend this map to a linear map on
A
if and only if
a
is central in
A
(Proposition
2.2
). Using this approach, we prove the main result of this section, Theorem
2.3
. We observe that a bijection between positive definite cones, which preserves the norm of the quotient of any two elements, is related to a Jordan
*
-isomorphism between the whole algebras. Nevertheless, this bijection generally cannot be extended to a linear map between entire algebras.

In the first draft of the paper, we apply the Gelfand-Naimark theorem to prove the following proposition. However, Lajos Molnár pointed out that direct proof is possible. This adjustment not only streamlines the proof but also enhances the clarity and accessibility of our paper.

Proposition 2.1
.

For any trio
a,x,y\in A_{+}^{-1}
we have

\|(ax^{2}a)^{\frac{1}{2}}a^{-1}\|=\|x\|

and

\|(ax^{2}a)^{\frac{1}{2}}(ay^{2}a)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\|=\|xy^{-1}\|.

Proof.

We have

\|(ax^{2}a)^{\frac{1}{2}}a^{-1}\|^{2}=\|((ax^{2}a)^{\frac{1}{2}}a^{-1})^{*}((ax^{2}a)^{\frac{1}{2}}a^{-1})\|\\
=\|a^{-1}(ax^{2}a)a^{-1}\|=\|x^{2}\|=\|x\|^{2}.

Thus the first equation holds. For the second equation, we have

\|(ax^{2}a)^{\frac{1}{2}}(ay^{2}a)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\|^{2}=\|((ax^{2}a)^{\frac{1}{2}}(ay^{2}a)^{-\frac{1}{2}})^{*}((ax^{2}a)^{\frac{1}{2}}(ay^{2}a)^{-\frac{1}{2}})\|\\
=\|(ay^{2}a)^{-\frac{1}{2}}ax^{2}a(ay^{2}a)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\|=\|xa(ay^{2}a)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\|^{2}\\
=\|(xa(ay^{2}a)^{-\frac{1}{2}})(xa(ay^{2}a)^{-\frac{1}{2}})^{*}\|\\
=\|xa(ay^{2}a)^{-1}ax\|=\|xy^{-2}x\|=\|xy^{-1}\|^{2}.

∎

To prove the main result of this section, Theorem
2.3
, we will utilize the following proposition.

Proposition 2.2
.

Let
a\in A_{+}^{-1}
.
Suppose that
\phi\colon A_{+}^{-1}\to A_{+}^{-1}
is defined as
\phi(x)=(ax^{2}a)^{\frac{1}{2}}
,
x\in A_{+}^{-1}
.
Then
\phi
is additive if and only if
a
is a central element in
A
. In this case,

\phi(x)=ax
,
x\in A_{+}^{-1}
.

Proof.

If
a
is a central element, then by an elementary calculation, we have
\phi(x)=ax
for every
x\in A_{+}^{-1}
, so
\phi
is additive.

Suppose that
\phi(x)=(ax^{2}a)^{\frac{1}{2}}
,
x\in A_{+}^{-1}
is an additive map. Then

(a(x^{2}+xy+yx+y^{2})a)^{\frac{1}{2}}=(a(x+y)^{2}a)^{\frac{1}{2}}=(ax^{2}a)^{\frac{1}{2}}+(ay^{2}a)^{\frac{1}{2}}

for every
x,y\in A_{+}^{-1}
. To simplify the equation, we can square both sides, resulting in:

axya+ayxa=(ax^{2}a)^{\frac{1}{2}}(ay^{2}a)^{\frac{1}{2}}+(ay^{2}a)^{\frac{1}{2}}(ax^{2}a)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

Substituting
x=a^{-1}
we obtain

ya+ay=2(ay^{2}a)^{\frac{1}{2}},\quad y\in A_{+}^{-1}.

(1)

Since
\phi
is additive, the inverse map
\phi^{-1}(x)=(a^{-1}x^{2}a^{-1})^{\frac{1}{2}}
,
x\in A_{+}^{-1}
is also additve. In a similar way as (
1
) we obtain

ya^{-1}+a^{-1}y=2(a^{-1}y^{2}a^{-1})^{\frac{1}{2}},\quad y\in A_{+}^{-1}.

(2)

Replacing
y
by
aya
in (
2
), we have

ay+ya=2(ya^{2}y)^{\frac{1}{2}},\quad y\in A_{+}^{-1}.

(3)

By compairing (
1
) and (
3
) we have

(ay)(ay)^{*}=ay^{2}a=ya^{2}y=(ay)^{*}(ay),\quad y\in A_{+}^{-1}.

Thus
ay
is normal for every
y\in A_{+}^{-1}
. On the other hand, the equation

\sigma(ay)=\sigma(y^{\frac{1}{2}}ay^{\frac{1}{2}})\subset(0,\infty)

implies that the spectrum of
ay
consists of positive real numbers.
It may be well known that a normal element, whose spectrum is confined to real numbers, is self-adjoint.
This can be demonstrated by examining the closed subalgebra generated by the corresponding normal element, constituting a commutative
C^{*}
-algebra. As the spectra in a
C^{*}
-algebra and in a closed
*
-subalgebra are identical, and in a commutative
C^{*}
-algebra, an element whose spectrum is confined to the real numbers is self-adjoint, it follows that the element
ay
is self-adjoint.
This implies that
a
and
y
commute for every
y\in A_{+}^{-1}
, which further implies that
a
is a central element in
A
. Finally, a simple calculation shows that
\phi(x)=ax
for
x\in A_{+}^{-1}
.
∎

Theorem 2.3
.

Let
\phi\colon A_{1+}^{-1}\to A_{2+}^{-1}
be a surjection. Then, the following are equivalent:

(i)

\|\phi(x)\phi(y)^{-1}\|=\|xy^{-1}\|
,
x,y\in A_{1+}^{-1}
,

(ii)

there exists a Jordan
*
-isomorphism
J\colon A_{1}\to A_{2}
such that

\phi(x)=(\phi(e)J(x)^{2}\phi(e))^{\frac{1}{2}},\quad x\in A_{1+}^{-1}.

When
\phi
satisfies the condition
(i)
, or equivalently
(ii)
, the map
\phi
is additive if and only if
\phi(e)
is a central element in
A_{2}
. In this case,

\phi(x)=\phi(e)J(x),\quad x\in A_{1+}^{-1}.

Proof.

We prove that (i) implies (ii).
Define
\psi\colon A_{1+}^{-1}\to A_{2+}^{-1}
by
\psi(x)=\phi(x^{\frac{1}{2}})^{2}
,
x\in A_{1+}^{-1}
. Then
\psi
is a surjection. By calculation, we have

\|y^{-\frac{1}{2}}x^{\frac{1}{2}}\|=\|(x^{\frac{1}{2}}y^{-\frac{1}{2}})^{*}\|=\|x^{\frac{1}{2}}y^{-\frac{1}{2}}\|=\|\phi(x^{\frac{1}{2}})\phi(y^{\frac{1}{2}})^{-1}\|\\
=\|\psi(x)^{\frac{1}{2}}\psi(y)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\|=\|\psi(y)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\psi(x)^{\frac{1}{2}}\|.

Then we have

\|y^{-\frac{1}{2}}xy^{-\frac{1}{2}}\|=\|(y^{-\frac{1}{2}}x^{\frac{1}{2}})(y^{-\frac{1}{2}}x^{\frac{1}{2}})^{*}\|=\|y^{-\frac{1}{2}}x^{\frac{1}{2}}\|^{2}\\
=\|\psi(y)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\psi(x)^{\frac{1}{2}}\|^{2}=\|\psi(y)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\psi(x)\psi(y)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\|.

(4)

Similarly, we have

\|x^{-\frac{1}{2}}yx^{-\frac{1}{2}}\|=\|\psi(x)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\psi(y)\psi(x)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\|.

(5)

Since
y^{-\frac{1}{2}}xy^{-\frac{1}{2}}
is positive, we have

\|y^{-\frac{1}{2}}xy^{-\frac{1}{2}}\|=\sup\{t\colon t\in\sigma(y^{-\frac{1}{2}}xy^{-\frac{1}{2}})\}\\
=\inf\{t\colon y^{-\frac{1}{2}}xy^{-\frac{1}{2}}\leq te\}=\inf\{t\colon x\leq ty\}.

(6)

In the same way, we have

\|x^{-\frac{1}{2}}yx^{-\frac{1}{2}}\|=\inf\{s\colon y\leq sx\}.

(7)

Recall that the Thompson metric
d_{T}(\cdot,\cdot)
on
A_{+}^{-1}
is

d_{T}(x,y)=\log\max\{\inf\{t\colon x\leq ty\},\inf\{s\colon y\leq sx\}\},\quad x,y\in A_{+}^{-1}.

It follows by (
4
), (
5
), (
6
) and (
7
) that for every
x,y\in A_{1+}^{-1}

d_{T}(x,y)=\log\max\{\|x^{-\frac{1}{2}}yx^{-\frac{1}{2}}\|,\|y^{-\frac{1}{2}}xy^{-\frac{1}{2}}\|\}\\
=\log\max\{\|\psi(x)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\psi(y)\psi(x)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\|,\|\psi(y)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\psi(x)\psi(y)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\|\}\\
=d_{T}(\psi(x),\psi(y)).

We conclude that
\psi\colon A_{1+}^{-1}\to A_{2+}^{-1}
is a surjective Thompson isometry. By
[
10
, Theorem 9]
there exists a Jordan
*
-isomorphism
J\colon A_{1}\to A_{2}
and a central projection
P\in A_{2}
such that

\psi(x)=\psi(e)^{\frac{1}{2}}(PJ(x)+(e-P)J(x^{-1}))\psi(e)^{\frac{1}{2}},\quad x\in A_{1+}^{-1}.

(8)

Substituting
x=\frac{1}{2}e
and
y=e
in (
4
), we obtain by (
8
) that

\frac{1}{2}=\left\|e^{-\frac{1}{2}}\frac{1}{2}ee^{-\frac{1}{2}}\right\|=\left\|\psi(e)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\psi\left(\frac{1}{2}e\right)\psi(e)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\right\|\\
=\left\|PJ\left(\frac{1}{2}e\right)+(e-P)J\left(\left(\frac{1}{2}e\right)^{-1}\right)\right\|=\left\|\frac{1}{2}P+2(e-P)\right\|,

as
P
is a central projection, we have

=\max\{\|\frac{1}{2}P\|,\|2(e-P)\|\}.

It follows that
e-P=0
, so
\psi=\psi(e)^{\frac{1}{2}}J\psi(e)^{\frac{1}{2}}
. We conclude that

\phi(x)=\psi(x^{2})^{\frac{1}{2}}=(\phi(e)J(x)^{2}\phi(e))^{\frac{1}{2}},\quad x\in A_{1+}^{-1}.

Suppose that (ii) holds. We prove (i). Applying Proposition
2.1
we have

\|\phi(x)\phi(y)^{-1}\|=\|(\phi(e)J(x)^{2}\phi(e))^{\frac{1}{2}}(\phi(e)J(y)^{2}\phi(e))^{-\frac{1}{2}}\|\\
=\|J(x)J(y)^{-1}\|,\quad x,y\in A_{1+}^{-1}.

(9)

On the other hand, as a Jordan
*
-isomorphism preserves squaring, the triple product, the inverse, and the norm, we have

\|J(x)J(y)^{-1}\|^{2}=\|(J(x)J(y)^{-1})^{*}J(x)J(y)^{-1}\|\\
=\|J(y)^{-1}J(x^{2})J(y)^{-1}\|=\|J(y^{-1}x^{2}y^{-1})\|\\
=\|y^{-1}x^{2}y^{-1}\|=\|xy^{-1}\|^{2},\quad x,y\in A_{1+}^{-1}.

Then by (
9
) we conclude

\|\phi(x)\phi(y)^{-1}\|=\|xy^{-1}\|

holds for every
x,y\in A_{1+}^{-1}
.

Suppose that
\phi(x)=(\phi(e)J(x)^{2}\phi(e))^{\frac{1}{2}}
,
x\in A_{1+}^{-1}
and
\phi
is additive. This implies that the mapping
y\mapsto(\phi(e)y^{2}\phi(e))^{\frac{1}{2}}
is additive. Therefore, by Proposition
2.2
, we conclude that
\phi(e)
is a central element in
A_{2}
. We infer by a simple calculation that
\phi(x)=\phi(e)J(x)
for every
x\in A_{1+}^{-1}
.
∎

Note that if
\|\phi(x)\phi(y)^{-1}\|=\|xy^{-1}\|
holds for every
x,y\in A_{1+}^{-1}
and
\phi(e)
is not a central element in
A_{2}
, then
\phi
is not extended to even an additive map from

into
A_{2}
.

The structure of a surjection that preserves the spectrum or the spectrum seminorm of the quotient is relatively simple. It can be extended to a Jordan
*
-isomorphism followed by the implementation of an element in
A_{2+}^{-1}
.
To elaborate, the following holds true.

Theorem 2.4
.

Let
\phi\colon A_{1+}^{-1}\to A_{2+}^{-1}
be a surjection. Then, the following are equivalent:

(i)

There exists a Jordan
*
-isomorphism
J\colon A_{1}\to A_{2}
such that

\phi(x)=\phi(e)^{\frac{1}{2}}J(x)\phi(e)^{\frac{1}{2}},\quad x\in A_{1+}^{-1},

(ii)

\|\phi(x)\phi(y)^{-1}\|_{S}=\|xy^{-1}\|_{S},\quad x,y\in A_{1+}^{-1}
,

(iii)

\sigma(\phi(x)\phi(y)^{-1})=\sigma(xy^{-1}),\quad x,y\in A_{1+}^{-1}
.

Proof.

By the definition of the spectrum norm, it is evident that (iii) implies (ii).

We prove that (ii) implies (i). For every
x,y\in A_{1+}^{-1}
,

\|xy^{-1}\|_{S}=\sup\{t\colon t\in\sigma(xy^{-1})\}=\sup\{t\colon t\in\sigma(y^{-\frac{1}{2}}xy^{-\frac{1}{2}})\}\\
=\inf\{t\colon y^{-\frac{1}{2}}xy^{-\frac{1}{2}}\leq te\}=\inf\{t\colon x\leq ty\}.

In the same way,
\|yx^{-1}\|_{S}=\inf\{t\colon y\leq tx\}
,

\|\phi(x)\phi(y)^{-1}\|_{S}=\inf\{t\colon\phi(x)\leq t\phi(y)\}
, and

\|\phi(y)\phi(x)^{-1}\|_{S}=\inf\{t\colon\phi(y)\leq t\phi(x)\}
.
Due to the definition of the Thompson metric, we obtain

d_{T}(x,y)=\log\max\{\|xy^{-1}\|_{S},\|yx^{-1}\|_{S}\}\\
=\log\max\{\|\phi(x)\phi(y)^{-1}\|_{S},\|\phi(y)\phi(x)^{-1}\|_{S}\}=d_{T}(\phi(x),\phi(y))

for every
x,y\in A_{1+}^{-1}
;
\phi
is a (surjective) Thompson isometry.
Hence, according to
[
10
, Theorem 9]
there exist a Jordan
*
-isomorphism
J
and a central projection
P\in A_{2}
such that

\phi(x)=\phi(e)^{\frac{1}{2}}(PJ(x)+(e-P)J(x^{-1}))\phi(e)^{\frac{1}{2}},\quad x\in A_{1+}^{-1}.

Letting
x=\frac{1}{2}e
, we have

\phi\left(\frac{1}{2}e\right)=\phi(e)^{\frac{1}{2}}\left(\frac{1}{2}P+2(e-P)\right)\phi(e)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

So

\frac{1}{2}=\left\|\frac{1}{2}ee^{-1}\right\|_{S}=\left\|\phi(e)^{\frac{1}{2}}\left(\frac{1}{2}P+2(e-P)\right)\phi(e)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\right\|_{S}\\
=\left\|\frac{1}{2}P+2(e-P)\right\|_{S}.

It follows that
e-P=0
. We conclude that
\phi=\phi(e)^{\frac{1}{2}}J\phi(e)^{\frac{1}{2}}
.

Suppose that (i) holds. Then for every
x,y\in A_{1+}^{-1}
, we infer
\phi(x)\phi(y)^{-1}=\phi(e)^{\frac{1}{2}}J(x)J(y^{-1})\phi(e)^{-\frac{1}{2}}
, so

\sigma(\phi(x)\phi(y)^{-1})=\sigma(\phi(e)^{\frac{1}{2}}J(x)J(y^{-1})\phi(e)^{-\frac{1}{2}})\\
=\sigma(J(x)J(y^{-1}))=\sigma(J(x^{\frac{1}{2}})J(y^{-1})J(x^{\frac{1}{2}}))\\
=\sigma(J(x^{\frac{1}{2}}y^{-1}x^{\frac{1}{2}}))=\sigma(x^{\frac{1}{2}}y^{-1}x^{\frac{1}{2}})=\sigma(xy^{-1}).

Thus, (iii) holds.
∎

Lemma 3.1
.

Let

.
Then, the following are equivalent:

(i)

(iii)

\|xax\|=\|xa^{\prime}x\|

Proof.

If

by
[

\|xa^{2}x\|=\|ax\|^{2}=\|a^{\prime}x\|^{2}=\|xa^{\prime 2}x\|,\quad x\in A_{+}^{-1}.

∎

Lemma 3.2
.

Let

Proof.

Suppose that

(resp.
\sigma(a^{\prime}y)=\sigma(y^{\frac{1}{2}}a^{\prime}y^{\frac{1}{2}})

\|ay\|_{S}=\sup\{t\colon t\in\sigma(ay)\}=\sup\{t\colon t\in\sigma(y^{\frac{1}{2}}ay^{\frac{1}{2}})\}\\
\leq\sup\{t\colon t\in\sigma(y^{\frac{1}{2}}a^{\prime}y^{\frac{1}{2}})\}=\sup\{t\colon t\in\sigma(a^{\prime}y)\}=\|a^{\prime}y\|_{S}

for every

Conversely, suppose that

Since

\sigma(a^{\prime}y^{2})=\sigma(ya^{\prime}y)

∎

\phi=b^{\frac{1}{2}}Jb^{\frac{1}{2}}
.
As
b\#x=b^{\frac{1}{2}}(b^{-\frac{1}{2}}xb^{-\frac{1}{2}})^{\frac{1}{2}}b^{\frac{1}{2}}
we have

J(x)=(b^{-\frac{1}{2}}xb^{-\frac{1}{2}})^{\frac{1}{2}}b(b^{-\frac{1}{2}}xb^{-\frac{1}{2}})^{\frac{1}{2}},\quad x\in A_{+}^{-1}.

As a Jordan
*
-isomorphism preserves the triple product, we have

J(b^{\frac{1}{2}})J(x)J(b^{\frac{1}{2}})=J(b^{\frac{1}{2}}xb^{\frac{1}{2}})=x^{\frac{1}{2}}bx^{\frac{1}{2}},\quad x\in A_{+}^{-1}.

(16)

On the other hand, as we have

b^{\frac{1}{2}}J(b)b^{\frac{1}{2}}=\phi(b)=(b\#b)^{2}=b^{2},

we obtain
J(b)=b
, hence
J(b^{\frac{1}{2}})=b^{\frac{1}{2}}
.
By (
16
)
we obtain

b^{\frac{1}{2}}J(x)b^{\frac{1}{2}}=x^{\frac{1}{2}}bx^{\frac{1}{2}}

(17)

for every
x\in A_{+}^{-1}
. Substituting
x^{-1}
instead of
x
we have

b^{\frac{1}{2}}J(x)^{-1}b^{\frac{1}{2}}=b^{\frac{1}{2}}J(x^{-1})b^{\frac{1}{2}}=x^{-\frac{1}{2}}bx^{-\frac{1}{2}}.

(18)

Hence

b^{-\frac{1}{2}}J(x)b^{-\frac{1}{2}}=x^{\frac{1}{2}}b^{-1}x^{\frac{1}{2}}.

(19)

By (
17
) and (
19
) we have

b^{-\frac{1}{2}}x^{\frac{1}{2}}bx^{\frac{1}{2}}b^{-\frac{1}{2}}=b^{\frac{1}{2}}x^{\frac{1}{2}}b^{-1}x^{\frac{1}{2}}b^{\frac{1}{2}},

so

(b^{\frac{1}{2}}x^{\frac{1}{2}}b^{-\frac{1}{2}})^{*}(b^{\frac{1}{2}}x^{\frac{1}{2}}b^{-\frac{1}{2}})=(b^{\frac{1}{2}}x^{\frac{1}{2}}b^{-\frac{1}{2}})(b^{\frac{1}{2}}x^{\frac{1}{2}}b^{-\frac{1}{2}})^{*}.

This means that
b^{\frac{1}{2}}x^{\frac{1}{2}}b^{-\frac{1}{2}}
is normal.
Alongside the equation

\sigma(b^{\frac{1}{2}}x^{\frac{1}{2}}b^{-\frac{1}{2}})=\sigma(x^{\frac{1}{2}})\subset(0,\infty),

we observe that
b^{\frac{1}{2}}x^{\frac{1}{2}}b^{-\frac{1}{2}}
is self-adjoint,
similar to the manner highlighted in the proof of Proposition
2.2
.
Hence, we have

b^{\frac{1}{2}}x^{\frac{1}{2}}b^{-\frac{1}{2}}=(b^{\frac{1}{2}}x^{\frac{1}{2}}b^{-\frac{1}{2}})^{*}=b^{-\frac{1}{2}}x^{\frac{1}{2}}b^{\frac{1}{2}}.

It follows that
bx^{\frac{1}{2}}=x^{\frac{1}{2}}b
holds for every
x
. Then
b
is a central element in
A
.
∎

We present a proof of a localized version of Ogasawara’s theorem
[
26
]
concerning commutativity. This result is a specific instance of a theorem of Nagy
[
25
, Theorem 1]
and Virosztek
[
32
, Theorem 1]
applied to the squaring functions, addressing the local monotonicity of a strictly convex increasing function.
Our presentation provides a concise and straightforward direct proof, despite the application of two profound results: the Russo and Dye theorem
[
29
, Corollary 1]
and Kadison’s generalized Schwarz lemma
[
15
, Theorem 1]
.

Proposition 4.3
.

Let
a\in A_{+}^{-1}
. Then
a
is central if and only if
a^{2}\leq x^{2}
holds for every
x\in A_{+}^{-1}
with
a\leq x
.

Proof.

If
a
is central, it is apparent that
a^{2}\leq x^{2}
holds for every
x\in A_{+}^{-1}
with
a\leq x
.

We prove the converse. Suppose that
a\in A_{+}^{-1}
which satisfies that
a^{2}\leq x^{2}
holds for every
x\in A_{+}^{-1}
with
a\leq x
.
We first prove
0\leq ab+ba
for every
b\in A
with
0\leq b
. For any
0<t\leq 1
we have
a\leq(1-t)a+t(a+b)
. By the hypothesis, we have
a^{2}\leq((1-t)a+t(a+b))^{2}
. By calculation, we obtain that

(2-t)a^{2}\leq(1-t)(2a^{2}+ab+ba)+t(a+b)^{2}.

Letting
t\to 0
, we have

0\leq ab+ba.

Define a map
T\colon A\to A
as
T(y)=\frac{1}{2}(a^{\frac{1}{2}}ya^{-\frac{1}{2}}+a^{-\frac{1}{2}}ya^{\frac{1}{2}})
,
y\in A
.
Then
T
is positive since
T(b)=\frac{1}{2}a^{-\frac{1}{2}}(ab+ba)a^{-\frac{1}{2}}\geq 0
for any
b\geq 0
. We also have
T(e)=e
.
By a theorem of Russo and Dye
[
29
, Corollary 1]
, we see that
\|T\|=1
.
Kadison’s generalized Schwarz lemma
[
15
, Theorem 1]
asserts that
T(x^{2})\geq T(x)^{2}
for each self-adjoint element
x
. After calculating
T(x^{2})
and
T(x)^{2}
, we can observe that

a^{\frac{1}{2}}x^{2}a^{-\frac{1}{2}}+a^{-\frac{1}{2}}x^{2}a^{\frac{1}{2}}\geq a^{\frac{1}{2}}xa^{-1}xa^{\frac{1}{2}}+a^{-\frac{1}{2}}xaxa^{-\frac{1}{2}}.

By multiplying
a^{\frac{1}{2}}
from the left and right-hand sides of each term of both sides of the inequality, we infer that

ax^{2}+x^{2}a\geq axa^{-1}xa+xax.

Then

ax^{2}-xax\geq axa^{-1}xa-x^{2}a=(ax-xa)a^{-1}xa.

Thus we obtain

(ax-xa)a^{-1}(ax-xa)\geq 0.

(20)

On the other hand, as
(ax-xa)^{*}=-(ax-xa)
, we obtain

0\leq((ax-xa)a^{-\frac{1}{2}})((ax-xa)a^{-\frac{1}{2}})^{*}=-(ax-xa)a^{-1}(ax-xa).

(21)

Combining (
20
) and (
21
), we infer that

\|(ax-xa)a^{-\frac{1}{2}}\|^{2}=\|(ax-xa)a^{-1}(ax-xa)\|=0.

It follows that
ax=xa
. As
x
is an arbitrary self-adjoint element, we conclude that
a
is a central element.
∎

Proposition 4.4
.

Let
a\in A_{+}^{-1}
. The following are equivalent:

(i)

a
is a central element in
A
,

(ii)

\|axa^{-1}\|=\|x\|
for every element
x\in A_{+}^{-1}
,

(iii)

\|a^{2}x\|=\|axa\|
for every element
x\in A_{+}^{-1}
,

(iv)

\|ax\|=\|ax\|_{S}
for every element
x\in A_{+}^{-1}
,

(v)

\|a^{2}x\|=\|a^{2}x\|_{S}
for every element
x\in A_{+}^{-1}
.

Proof.

First, we prove that (i) and (iv) are equivalent.
Suppose that (i) holds;
a
is a central element. Then, so is
a^{\frac{1}{2}}
since
a^{\frac{1}{2}}
is approximated by polynomials of
a
. Hence
ax=a^{\frac{1}{2}}xa^{\frac{1}{2}}
, so
\|ax\|=\|a^{\frac{1}{2}}xa^{\frac{1}{2}}\|
. As
a^{\frac{1}{2}}xa^{\frac{1}{2}}
is positive, so self-adjoint, we have
\|a^{\frac{1}{2}}xa^{\frac{1}{2}}\|=\|a^{\frac{1}{2}}xa^{\frac{1}{2}}\|_{S}
. As
\sigma(a^{\frac{1}{2}}xa^{\frac{1}{2}})=\sigma(ax)
, we have
\|a^{\frac{1}{2}}xa^{\frac{1}{2}}\|_{S}=\|ax\|_{S}
. Thus, (iv) holds.

Suppose that
a
is not a central element. We prove (iv) does not hold. As the above, we see that
a^{2}
is not a central element. Applying Proposition
4.3
, there exists
x_{0}\in A_{+}^{-1}
such that
a\leq x_{0}^{-1}
and
a^{2}\not\leq x_{0}^{-2}
. Put

t_{0}=\inf\{t\colon a^{2}\leq tx_{0}^{-2}\},

and

s_{0}=\inf\{s\colon a\leq sx_{0}^{-1}\}.

Then
t_{0}>1
as
a^{2}\not\leq x_{0}^{-2}
. Since
a\leq x_{0}^{-1}
we have
s_{0}\leq 1
. We compute

t_{0}=\inf\{t\colon x_{0}a^{2}x_{0}\leq te\}=\sup\{t\colon t\in\sigma(x_{0}a^{2}x_{0})\}=\|x_{0}a^{2}x_{0}\|,

where the last equality holds since
x_{0}a^{2}x_{0}
is positive, hence self-adjoint. Since
x_{0}a^{2}x_{0}=(ax_{0})^{*}(ax_{0})
we have

\|x_{0}a^{2}x_{0}\|=\|ax_{0}\|^{2}.

On the other hand, we compute

s_{0}=\inf\{s\colon a\leq sx_{0}^{-1}\}=\inf\{s\colon x_{0}^{\frac{1}{2}}ax_{0}^{\frac{1}{2}}\leq se\}\\
=\sup\{s\colon s\in\sigma(x_{0}^{\frac{1}{2}}ax_{0}^{\frac{1}{2}})\}=\sup\{s\colon s\in\sigma(ax_{0})\}=\|ax_{0}\|_{S}.

Since
t_{0}>1\geq s_{0}^{2}
, we can conclude that
\|ax_{0}\|>\|ax_{0}\|_{S}
, so (iv) does not hold.

In a similar way as above, we see that (i) and (v) are equivalent.

By a calculation, we have

\|axa\|=\sup\{t\colon t\in\sigma(axa)\}=\sup\{t\colon t\in\sigma(a^{2}x)\}=\|a^{2}x\|_{S}.

Hence (iii) and (v) are equivalent.

Suppose that (ii) holds. By substituting
axa
in place of
x
in equation (ii), we have
\|a^{2}x\|=\|axa\|
. Thus (iii) holds.

Suppose that (iii) holds. Substituting
a^{-1}xa^{-1}
in place of
x
in equation (iii), we have

\|axa^{-1}\|=\|x\|
. Thus (ii) holds.
∎

5.
Maps between positive semidefinite cones

In this section, we exhibit results on maps between positive semidefinite cones of unital
C^{*}
-algebras.
Theorem
1.1
, ”the corollary” of Molnár, can be slightly generalized for the case of positive semidefinite cones of unital
C^{*}
-algebras as Theorem
5.1
. It is in the same vein as ”the corollary”.
Notably,
the Thompson metric is not well defined on the positive semidefinite cones. However, a representation theorem is possible for positively homogeneous order isomorphisms between positive semidefinite cones. As an application of Theorem
5.1
, we provide a solution (Theorem
5.5
) to the problem posed by Molnár
[
20
, p.194]
.

Theorem 5.1
.

Suppose that
\phi\colon A_{1+}\to A_{2+}
is a positively homogeneous order isomorphism, that is,

(1)

\phi
is a bijection,

(2)

\phi(tx)=t\phi(x)
,
\quad x\in A_{1+},\,\,t\geq 0
,

(3)

x\leq x^{\prime}
if and only if
\phi(x)\leq\phi(x^{\prime})
.

Then there exists a Jordan
*
-isomorphism
J\colon A_{1}\to A_{2}
such that
\phi=\phi(e)^{\frac{1}{2}}J\phi(e)^{\frac{1}{2}}
on
A_{1+}
.

Proof.

We first prove that
\phi(A_{1+}^{-1})=A_{2+}^{-1}
. Suppose that
a\in A_{1+}^{-1}
. Let
b=\phi^{-1}(e)
, where
e
is the unit in
A_{2}
. As
a
is invertible,
a^{-\frac{1}{2}}ba^{-\frac{1}{2}}\geq 0
,
so there exists a positive real number
t_{b}>0
such that
b\leq t_{b}a
. As
\phi
preserves the order and is positively homogeneous, we infer that

e=\phi(b)\leq\phi(t_{b}a)=t_{b}\phi(a).

Hence
\phi(a)
is invertible, so
\phi(a)\in A_{2+}^{-1}
. Conversely, we infer in a similar way that
if
c\in A_{2+}^{-1}
, then
\phi^{-1}(c)\in A_{1+}^{-1}
.
Thus we have
\phi(A_{1+}^{-1})=A_{2+}^{-1}
.

According to ”the corollary”, there exists a Jordan
*
-isomorphism
J\colon A_{1}\to A_{2}
such that
\phi=\phi(e)^{\frac{1}{2}}J\phi(e)^{\frac{1}{2}}
on
A_{1+}^{-1}
. We prove that the equality also holds on
A_{1+}
. Let
a\in A_{1+}
be arbitrary. For any
t>0
,
a+te\in A_{1+}^{-1}
. Then

\phi(a)\leq\phi(a+te)=\phi(e)^{\frac{1}{2}}J(a+te)\phi(e)^{\frac{1}{2}}=\phi(e)^{\frac{1}{2}}J(a)\phi(e)^{\frac{1}{2}}+t\phi(e)

as
\phi
preserves the order. Letting
t\to 0
we have

\phi(a)\leq\phi(e)^{\frac{1}{2}}J(a)\phi(e)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

As
\phi
is a surjection, there exists
a^{\prime}\in A_{1+}
such that
\phi(a^{\prime})=\phi(e)^{\frac{1}{2}}J(a)\phi(e)^{\frac{1}{2}}
.
Hence we have
\phi(a)\leq\phi(a^{\prime})
, so
a\leq a^{\prime}
as
\phi
is an order isomorphism. Next, for any
t>0
we have

\phi(a^{\prime})=\phi(e)^{\frac{1}{2}}J(a)\phi(e)^{\frac{1}{2}}\leq\phi(e)^{\frac{1}{2}}J(a+te)\phi(e)^{\frac{1}{2}}=\phi(a+te),

as
J
preserves the order and
a+te\in A_{1+}^{-1}
.
Thus, we have
a^{\prime}\leq a+te
for every
t>0
. Letting
t\to 0
, we have
a^{\prime}\leq a
. It follows that
a=a^{\prime}
and
\phi(a)=\phi(a^{\prime})=\phi(e)^{\frac{1}{2}}J(a)\phi(e)^{\frac{1}{2}}
.
As
a\in A_{1+}
is arbitrary, we conclude that
\phi=\phi(e)^{\frac{1}{2}}J\phi(e)^{\frac{1}{2}}
on
A_{1+}
.
∎

The proposition below is about a characterization of the inequality
a\leq b
for
a,b
in a positive semidefinite cone of a unital
C^{*}
-algebra.
It is a generalization of a part of

[
4
, Lemma 13]
which applies to positive definite cones, as well as a part of
[
20
, Lemma 2.8]
, which applies to positive semidefinite cones of von Neumann algebras. To prove it, we apply
[
4
, Lemma 13]
.

Proposition 5.2
.

Let
a,b\in A_{+}
. Suppose that
\|xax\|\leq\|xbx\|
holds for every
x\in A_{+}^{-1}
. Then
a\leq b
. Suppose conversely that
a\leq b
. Then
\|xax\|\leq\|xbx\|
holds for all
x\in A_{+}
.
In particular,
a\leq b
if and only if
\|xax\|\leq\|xbx\|
for every
x\in A_{+}
.

Proof.

Suppose that
\|xax\|\leq\|xbx\|
for every
x\in A_{+}^{-1}
. Let
x\in A_{+}^{-1}
and
0<t<1
be arbitrary. We have

\|x(a+te)x\|\leq\|xax\|+\|x(te)x\|\leq\|xbx\|+\|x(te)x\|\\
\leq\|x(b+te)x\|+2\|x(te)x\|.

(22)

As
0<te\leq\sqrt{t}b+te
, we have by Lemma 13 in
[
4
]
that

\|x(te)x\|\leq\|x(\sqrt{t}b+te)x\|=\sqrt{t}\|x(b+\sqrt{t}e)x\|.

As
0<t<1
we have
0<b+te\leq b+\sqrt{t}e
, so by
[
4
, Lemma 13]
,

\|x(b+te)x\|\leq\|x(b+\sqrt{t}e)x\|.

We obtain that

\|x(b+te)x\|+2\|x(te)x\|\leq(1+2\sqrt{t})\|x(b+\sqrt{t}e)x\|.

Then by (
22
) we have

\|x(a+te)x\|\leq(1+2\sqrt{t})\|x(b+\sqrt{t}e)x\|=\|x(1+2\sqrt{t})(b+\sqrt{t}e)x\|.

As
x\in A_{+}^{-1}
is arbitrary, by
[
4
, Lemma 13]
we have

a+te\leq(1+2\sqrt{t})(b+\sqrt{t}e).

Letting
t\to 0
, we obtain
a\leq b
.

Conversely, suppose that
a\leq b
. Then for every
x\in A_{+}
,
0\leq xax\leq xbx
. Hence

\|xax\|_{S}=\sup\{t\colon t\in\sigma(xax)\}\leq\sup\{t\colon t\in\sigma(xbx)\}=\|xbx\|_{S}.

As
xax
and
xbx
are self-adjoint, the norm and the spectral seminorm coincide with each other, hence we obtain

\|xax\|\leq\|xbx\|
for all
x\in A_{+}
.

∎

Corollary 5.3
.

Let
a,b\in A_{+}
. Then,
a=b
if and only if

\|xax\|=\|xbx\|
for every
x\in A_{+}
with
x\leq e
.

Proof.

Suppose that
\|xax\|=\|xbx\|
for every
x\in A_{+}
with
x\leq e
. Then for every positive real number
t
, we have
\|(tx)a(tx)\|=\|(tx)b(tx)\|
. Thus we have
\|yay\|=\|yby\|
for every
y\in A_{+}
. By Proposition
5.2
we infer that
a=b
.

The converse statement is trivial.
∎

The following proposition is a version of Lemma

.

Proposition 5.4
.

Suppose that
\phi
and
\psi
are surjections from
A_{1+}
onto
A_{2+}
such that

\|yxy\|=\|\phi(y)\psi(x)\phi(y)\|,\quad x,y\in A_{1+}.

(23)

and

-isomorphism

, we have

\|yxy\|\leq\|yx^{\prime}y\|

The following theorem provides a solution to the problem posed by Molnár
[
20
, p.194]

Theorem 5.5
.

Suppose that
\phi\colon A_{1+}\to A_{2+}
is a surjection. The following are equivalent:

(i)

There exists a Jordan
*
-isomorphism
J\colon A_{1}\to A_{2}
such that
\phi=J
on
A_{1+}
;

(ii)

\|\phi(x)\phi(y)\|=\|xy\|
,
\quad x,y\in A_{1+}
;

(iii)

\|\phi(x)\phi(y)\|_{S}=\|xy\|_{S}
,
\quad x,y\in A_{1+}
,

(iv)

\sigma(\phi(x)\phi(y))=\sigma(xy)
,
\quad x,y\in A_{1+}
,

(v)

For a positive real number
p
,

\|(\phi(x)^{\frac{p}{2}}\phi(y)^{p}\phi(x)^{\frac{p}{2}})^{\frac{1}{p}}\|=\|(x^{\frac{p}{2}}y^{p}x^{\frac{p}{2}})^{\frac{1}{p}}\|,\quad x,y\in A_{1+}.

Suppose that (ii) holds. We prove (i). As
(ab)^{*}ab=ba^{2}b
for every
a,b\in A_{1+}
, we infer by the equation (ii) that

\|yxy\|=\|x^{\frac{1}{2}}y\|^{2}=\|\phi(x^{\frac{1}{2}})\phi(y)\|^{2}=\|\phi(y)\phi(x^{\frac{1}{2}})^{2}\phi(y)\|,\quad x,y\in A_{1+}

(24)

Defining
\psi\colon A_{1+}\to A_{2+}
by
\psi(x)=\phi(x^{\frac{1}{2}})^{2}
,
x\in A_{1+}
,
\psi
is a surjection, and by (
24
) we obtain

\|yxy\|=\|\phi(y)\psi(x)\phi(y)\|,\quad x,y\in A_{1+}.

(25)

We prove
\psi(e)=e
. Letting
x=y
in the equation of (ii), we have
\|y^{2}\|=\|\phi(y)^{2}\|
.
Thus we have

\|\phi(y)\phi(e)^{2}\phi(y)\|=\|\phi(e)\phi(y)\|^{2}\\
=\|ey\|^{2}=\|y^{2}\|=\|\phi(y)^{2}\|=\|\phi(y)e\phi(y)\|.

By Corollary
5.3
, we have
\phi(e)^{2}=e
, so
\phi(e)=e
, hence
\psi(e)=e
.
Applying Proposition
5.4
, there exists a Jordan
*
-isomorphism
J\colon A_{1}\to A_{2}
such that
\psi=J
on
A_{1+}
. For any
x\in A_{1+}
,
\phi(x)=\psi(x^{2})^{\frac{1}{2}}=J(x^{2})^{\frac{1}{2}}=J(x)
. Thus
\phi=J
on
A_{1+}
.

We prove (v) implies (i). Suppose first we consider
p=1
. In this case, the equation of (iii) is

\|\phi(x)^{\frac{1}{2}}\phi(y)\phi(x)^{\frac{1}{2}}\|=\|x^{\frac{1}{2}}yx^{\frac{1}{2}}\|,\quad x,y\in A_{1+}.

(26)

We prove
\phi(e)=e
. Inserting
x=y
in (
26
), we obtain
\|\phi(x)^{2}\|=\|x^{2}\|
. Hence
\|\phi(x)\|=\|x\|
for every
x\in A_{1+}
.
Then by (
26
) we have

\|\phi(x)^{\frac{1}{2}}\phi(e)\phi(x)^{\frac{1}{2}}\|=\|x^{\frac{1}{2}}ex^{\frac{1}{2}}\|\\
=\|x\|=\|\phi(x)\|=\|\phi(x)^{\frac{1}{2}}e\phi(x)^{\frac{1}{2}}\|,\quad x\in A_{1+}.

As
\phi
is a surjection, we see that
\{\phi(x)^{\frac{1}{2}}\colon x\in A_{1+}\}=A_{2+}
. Then Corollary
5.3
ensures that
\phi(e)=e
.
Define
\varphi\colon A_{1+}\to A_{2+}
by

\varphi(x)=\phi(x^{2})^{\frac{1}{2}}
,
x\in A_{1+}
. Then by (
26
) we obtain

\|\varphi(x)\phi(y)\varphi(x)\|=\|xyx\|,\quad x,y\in A_{1+}.

Then by Proposition
5.4
we observe that (i) holds.

We consider a general positive
p
.
Define

The set

is known as the effect algebra.
This structure holds significant importance in the quantum theory of measurements, as it establishes the foundation for unsharp measurements within quantum mechanics.
The following result generalizes Corollary 2.9 in

.
