On large 3 / 2 3/2 -stable maps
Emmanuel Kammerer

Abstract
We discuss asymptotics of large Boltzmann random planar maps such that every vertex of degree k k has weight of order k − 2 k^{-2} . Infinite maps of that kind were studied by Budd, Curien and Marzouk. These maps correspond to the dual of the discrete α \alpha -stable maps studied by Le Gall and Miermont for α = 3 / 2 \alpha=3/2 and to the gaskets of critical O ⁡ ( 2 ) O(2) -decorated random planar maps. We compute the asymptotics of the graph and first passage percolation distances between two uniform vertices, which are respectively equivalent in probability to ( log ⁡ ℓ ) 2 / π 2 (\log\ell)^{2}/\pi^{2} and 2 ​ ( log ⁡ ℓ ) / ( π 2 ​ p 𝐪 ) 2(\log\ell)/(\pi^{2}p_{\bf q}) when the perimeter of the map ℓ \ell goes to ∞ \infty , where p 𝐪 p_{\bf q} is a constant depending on the model. We also show that the diameter is of the same order as those distances for both metrics and establish the absence of scaling limits in the sense of Gromov-Prokhorov or Gromov-Hausdorff for lack of tightness. To study the peeling exploration of these maps, we prove local limit and scaling limit theorems for a class of random walks with heavy tails conditioned to remain positive until they die at − ℓ -\ell towards processes that we call stable Lévy processes conditioned to stay positive until they jump and die at − 1 -1 .

1
Introduction

1.1
Boltzmann
3/2
-stable planar maps

Random planar maps have been studied very actively, motivated partly by their connections with two dimensional Liouville quantum gravity. In this work, we study the geometry of large random
3/2
-stable Boltzmann planar maps. A planar map
\mathfrak{m}
is a connected planar graph embedded in the sphere seen up to orientation-preserving homeomorphism and equipped with a distinguished oriented edge
\vec{e}_{r}
called the root edge. The face
f_{r}
adjacent to the right of the root edge is called the root face. The degree of
f_{r}
is also called the perimeter of
\mathfrak{m}
. We also require the map
\mathfrak{m}
to be bipartite, i.e. that all its faces have even degree, inasmuch as the enumeration of bipartite maps is particularly simple. We write
\mathcal{M}
for the set of finite bipartite rooted planar maps.

Random planar maps can be picked uniformly at random among maps of a given size. A more general way to pick a random map
\mathfrak{m}\in\mathcal{M}
is to assign Boltzmann weights to them, following
[
22
]
. Let
{\bf q}=(q_{k})_{k\geq 1}
be a non-zero sequence of non-negative numbers called the sequence of weights. The weight of a map
\mathfrak{m}\in\mathcal{M}
is defined as

w_{\bf q}(\mathfrak{m})=\prod_{f\in\mathrm{Faces}(\mathfrak{m})\setminus\left\{f_{r}\right\}}q_{\mathrm{deg}(f)/2}.

For all
\ell\geq 1
, let
\mathcal{M}^{(\ell)}\subset\mathcal{M}
be the set of all (finite) maps with perimeter
2\ell
. We introduce the partition function

W^{(\ell)}=\sum_{\mathfrak{m}\in\mathcal{M}^{(\ell)}}w_{\bf q}(\mathfrak{m}).

When
W^{(1)}<\infty
, we say that
\bf q
is admissible.
In what follows, we also require the weight sequence to be critical of type
a\in(3/2,5/2]
, which is equivalent by Proposition 5.10 of
[
13
]
to having

W^{(\ell)}\mathop{\sim}\limits_{\ell\to\infty}\frac{p_{\bf q}}{2}c_{\bf q}^{\ell+1}\ell^{-a},

(1.1)

for some constant
p_{\bf q}>0
. The cases
a\in(3/2,5/2)
are called non-generic. Such sequences
\bf q
do exist, see Lemma 6.1 of
[
10
]
for explicit examples.
If
\bf q
is admissible, we write
\mathbb{P}^{(\ell)}
the probability measure on
\mathcal{M}^{(\ell)}
associated with the weight sequence
{\bf q}
, called the Boltzmann measure, defined by

\mathbb{P}^{(\ell)}(\mathfrak{m})=\frac{w_{\bf q}(\mathfrak{m})}{W^{(\ell)}}.

We also write
\mathbb{E}^{(\ell)}
for the associated mathematical expectation. A random map
\mathfrak{M}^{(\ell)}
following the law
\mathbb{P}^{(\ell)}
is called a
\bf q
-Boltzmann random map of perimeter
2\ell
. The
\bf q
-Boltzmann random maps for
\bf q
of type
a
are also called
(a-1/2)
-stable maps. The object of study in this work is not the map
\mathfrak{M}^{(\ell)}
in itself but rather its dual map
\mathfrak{M}^{(\ell,\dagger)}
obtained by exchanging the roles of the faces and of the vertices.

For
a=2
, the local limit, the infinite
3/2
-stable map, was studied in
[
11
]
by Budd, Curien and Marzouk. In particular, they established the scaling limit of the perimeter associated with the exploration of the infinite map, and studied its basic metric properties and their results will be especially helpful in Section
4
.

The peeling exploration of the map makes appear a random walk
S
on
\mathbb{Z}
with step distribution
\nu
which will play a crucial part in this work. It is related to
\bf q
by

\forall k\geq 0,\enskip\nu(k)=q_{k+1}c_{\bf q}^{k}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\nu(-k-1)=2W^{(k)}c_{\bf q}^{-k-1}.

(1.2)

When
\bf q
is critical of type
a=2
, we know in particular by Proposition 5.10 of
[
13
]
that

\nu(-k)\sim p_{\bf q}k^{-2}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\nu([k,\infty))\sim p_{\bf q}k^{-1}\enskip\text{ as }k\longrightarrow\infty,

(1.3)

so that
\nu
is in the domain of attraction of the symmetric Cauchy process (with no drift).

1.2
Main results

Throughout this work, unless explicitly stated otherwise,
\bf q
will be a critical non-generic weight sequence of type
a=2
and, for all
\ell\geq 1
,
\mathfrak{M}^{(\ell)}
will be a
\bf q
-Boltzmann random map of perimeter
2\ell
.

Before stating the main results, let us introduce the distance functions on a map
\mathfrak{m}
. For every faces
f_{1}
,
f_{2}
of
\mathfrak{m}
, we denote by
d_{\mathrm{gr}}^{\dagger}(f_{1},f_{2})
the graph distance between
f_{1}
and
f_{2}
in the dual map
\mathfrak{m}^{\dagger}
. Another distance, easier to study in our context, is the first-passage percolation distance
d_{\mathrm{fpp}}^{\dagger}
defined as follows: to each edge of
\mathfrak{m}^{\dagger}
we assign an independent random variable following the exponential law of parameter
1
(that we will write
\mathcal{E}(1)
), interpreted as its random length. For every faces
f_{1}
,
f_{2}
of
\mathfrak{m}
, we then denote by
d_{\mathrm{fpp}}^{\dagger}(f_{1},f_{2})
the distance between
f_{1}
and
f_{2}
in the dual map
\mathfrak{m}^{\dagger}
whose edges are equipped with those random lengths.

The first theorem describes the distance from the root face to a uniform random face (we also prove that the same results hold for a uniform random edge or a uniform random vertex).

Theorem 1.1
.

For all
\ell\geq 1
, let
F^{(\ell)}
be a random uniform face in
\mathfrak{M}^{(\ell)}
(conditionally on
\mathfrak{M}^{(\ell)}
) and recall that
f_{r}
is the root face. Then

\frac{d_{\mathrm{fpp}}^{\dagger}(f_{r},F^{(\ell)})}{\log\ell}\mathop{\longrightarrow}\limits_{\ell\to\infty}^{(\mathbb{P})}\frac{1}{\pi^{2}p_{\bf q}}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\frac{d_{\mathrm{gr}}^{\dagger}(f_{r},F^{(\ell)})}{(\log\ell)^{2}}\mathop{\longrightarrow}\limits_{\ell\to\infty}^{(\mathbb{P})}\frac{1}{2\pi^{2}}.

The scales for the distances in
\log\ell
and
(\log\ell)^{2}
are specific to the
3/2
-stable case on account of its criticality and of the singular behavior of Cauchy processes. Deeply related behaviors have already been observed for the volume growth of balls in the infinite map in Theorems A and B of
[
11
]
.

We then prove that the geodesics from the root to two uniform random faces “split very quickly”, so that we get the asymptotic of the distance between two uniform random faces, see Corollary
5.1
.
This corollary implies that if
(F_{i}^{(\ell)})_{i\geq 1}
are i.i.d. uniform random faces of
\mathfrak{M}^{(\ell)}
for all
\ell\geq 1
, then, for the product topology,

\left(\frac{d_{\mathrm{fpp}}^{\dagger}(F_{i},F_{j})}{\log\ell}\right)_{i,j\geq 1}\mathop{\longrightarrow}\limits_{\ell\to\infty}^{(\mathbb{P})}\left(\frac{2}{\pi^{2}p_{\bf q}}\right)_{i,j\geq 1}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\left(\frac{d_{\mathrm{gr}}^{\dagger}(F_{i},F_{j})}{(\log\ell)^{2}}\right)_{i,j\geq 1}\mathop{\longrightarrow}\limits_{\ell\to\infty}^{(\mathbb{P})}\left(\frac{1}{\pi^{2}}\right)_{i,j\geq 1}.

(1.4)

So as to study the diameter in Section
6
, we control the distances uniformly on all the faces. Aside from the first and second moment methods, a convenient tool is a martingale associated with the perimeter process, hence enabling us to show that the diameter is of the same order as the distance between two faces picked uniformly at random.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
\mathrm{Diam}_{\mathrm{gr}}^{\dagger}(\mathfrak{M}^{(\ell)})
(resp.
\mathrm{Diam}_{\mathrm{fpp}}^{\dagger}(\mathfrak{M}^{(\ell)})
) be the diameter of
\mathfrak{M}^{(\ell,\dagger)}
for the graph distance (resp. fpp distance). Let
\delta>0
. There exists a constant
K({\bf q})
depending on
\bf q
such that, with probability
1-o(1)
when
\ell\to\infty
,

\left(\frac{3}{4(1-q_{1}^{2})}\vee\frac{2}{\pi^{2}p_{\bf q}}\right)-\delta\leq\frac{\mathrm{Diam}_{\mathrm{fpp}}^{\dagger}(\mathfrak{M}^{(\ell)})}{\log\ell}\leq K({\bf q})\qquad\text{and}\qquad\frac{1}{\pi^{2}}-\delta\leq\frac{\mathrm{Diam}_{\mathrm{gr}}^{\dagger}(\mathfrak{M}^{(\ell)})}{(\log\ell)^{2}}\leq 18+\delta.

These bounds on the diameter indicate in particular the scale at which one should look for studying Gromov-Hausdorff convergence. Actually, (
1.4
) shows that at the macroscopic scale,
\mathfrak{M}^{(\ell),\dagger}
looks like a star with many branches when
\ell\to\infty
, see the simulation in Figure
1
. As a consequence, the sequence
\left(\mathfrak{M}^{(\ell),\dagger}\right)_{\ell\geq 1}
equipped with the rescaled distances is not tight, neither for the Gromov-Hausdorff topology, nor for the Gromov-Prokhorov topology. Indeed, on the one hand, the limit of any subsequence can not be compact due to (
1.4
), while on the other hand any limiting random separable metric measured space
(X,d,\mu)
of some subsequence for Gromov-Prokhorov would have an empty support since two uniform points of law
\mu
would be at constant distance by (
1.4
), which contradicts the separability (see e.g. Remark 4.5 of
[
17
]
for details on this reasoning).

More precisely, for all
\ell\geq 1
, let
\mu_{\ell}
be the uniform measure on the faces of
\mathfrak{M}^{(\ell)}
. We have thus shown the following corollary.

Corollary 1.3
.

The sequences
(\mathfrak{M}^{(\ell,\dagger)},\frac{1}{(\log\ell)^{2}}d_{\mathrm{gr}}^{\dagger},\mu_{\ell})_{\ell\geq 1}
and
(\mathfrak{M}^{(\ell,\dagger)},\frac{1}{\log\ell}d_{\mathrm{fpp}}^{\dagger},\mu_{\ell})_{\ell\geq 1}
are not tight for the Gromov-Prokhorov topology. Besides, the sequences

(\mathfrak{M}^{(\ell,\dagger)},\frac{1}{(\log\ell)^{2}}d_{\mathrm{gr}}^{\dagger})_{\ell\geq 1}
and

(\mathfrak{M}^{(\ell,\dagger)},\frac{1}{\log\ell}d_{\mathrm{fpp}}^{\dagger})_{\ell\geq 1}

are not tight for the Gromov-Hausdorff topology. In particular, there is no Gromov-Hausdorff scaling limit of any subsequence of
(\mathfrak{M}^{(\ell,\dagger)},d_{\mathrm{gr}}^{\dagger})_{\ell\geq 1}
or
(\mathfrak{M}^{(\ell,\dagger)},d_{\mathrm{fpp}}^{\dagger})_{\ell\geq 1}

Still, following
[
16
]
and
[
18
]
, the convergence
(\ref{eq \'{e}toile})
can also be interpreted as the convergence in probability of
(\mathfrak{M}^{(\ell,\dagger)},\frac{1}{(\log\ell)^{2}}d_{\mathrm{gr}}^{\dagger},\mu_{\ell})_{\ell\geq 1}
and
(\mathfrak{M}^{(\ell,\dagger)},\frac{1}{\log\ell}d_{\mathrm{fpp}}^{\dagger},\mu_{\ell})_{\ell\geq 1}
towards some long dendrons defined in Example 7.2 of
[
18
]
.

1.3
Conditioned random walks

The main technique to study the metric properties of
\mathfrak{M}^{(\ell,\dagger)}
is the filled-in peeling exploration. It was first introduced in
[
27
]
and
[
1
]
for triangulations. A general version is defined in
[
8
]
and is presented in Section
3
. See
[
7
]
and
[
13
]
for a more detailed introduction. The peeling process consists in a step-by-step Markovian exploration of the map starting from the root face according some peeling algorithm. At each step, the peeling process reveals the face behind the peeled edge chosen by the peeling algorithm. Various choices of peeling algorithms enable to understand different properties of the map. Our use of the peeling algorithm is similar to that of
[
10
]
for the study of infinite
(a-1/2)
-stable Boltzmann maps for
\bf q
critical non-generic of type
a\in(3/2,5/2)\setminus\left\{2\right\}
and
[
11
]
for the study of infinite
3/2
-stable Boltzmann maps. A difference is that most of the time we will work with the peeling algorithm on the map which has been rerooted (see Subsection
3.4
).

In our case, the evolution of the perimeter of the explored part has the same law as the
\nu
-random walk conditioned to stay positive until it jumps and dies at
-\ell
. This is, to the best of our knowledge, a new type of conditioning which we study in details in Section
2
.
In that section we introduce a key coupling (Lemma
2.5
) between this conditioned random walk and the
\nu
-random walk conditioned to stay positive forever
, using that such conditionings can be performed via Doob
h
-transforms. This coupling enables us in particular to use some results of
[
11
]
.
This lemma states that for all
n\geq 1
, there exists a coupling between the two conditioned random walks such that if the second one has not been killed yet at time
n
, then the two conditioned random walks coincide.

The rest of Section
2
can be seen as an asymptotic study of this conditioned
\nu
-random walk.
We first prove a local limit theorem for the lifetime and the last positive value of the walk. Relying on this local limit, we next establish in Theorem
2.8
the scaling limit of that conditioned random walk dying at
-\ell
towards a process that we call an
(a-1)
-stable Lévy process conditioned to die at
-1
. We hope that those results could be fruitful in other contexts.

-stable map, where the case

.
The

For the infinite Boltzmann

2.1
The

The criticality condition (

For all

h^{\downarrow}_{p}(\ell)=h^{\downarrow}(\ell)h^{\downarrow}(p)\frac{\ell}{\ell+p}\qquad\text{and}\qquad h_{0}^{\downarrow}(\ell)=h^{\downarrow}(\ell)=2^{-2\ell}\binom{2\ell}{\ell},

Let

is a probability measure on

such that

(I)

The functions

and

are harmonic for

.

(II)

We have
\nu(-k)\sim p_{\bf q}/k^{a}
as

These two conditions are equivalent to the criticality of type

Moreover, jointly with the above convergence, we have the convergence of the lifetimes:

where

and

.

One may alternatively prove the first convergence of the above proposition by applying Theorem 2 of

]
.

2.3
Coupling random walks conditioned to stay positive with random walks conditioned to die at
-\ell

Let us denote by
\tau_{-\ell}
the first hitting time of
-\ell
by
(P_{\ell}(k))_{k\geq 0}
the random walk conditioned to remain positive until it dies at
-\ell
under
\mathbb{P}^{(1)}_{\ell}

Lemma 2.5
.

There exists a coupling of the random walks
P_{\ell}
under
\mathbb{P}^{(1)}_{\ell}
and
P_{\infty}
under
\mathbb{P}^{(1)}_{\infty}
up to time
n
written
\mathbb{P}^{(1),n}_{\ell,\infty}
such that:
under
\mathbb{P}^{(1),n}_{\ell,\infty}
, for all
s_{1},\ldots,s_{n}\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
, conditionally on

(P_{\ell}(k))_{1\leq k\leq n}=(s_{k})_{1\leq k\leq n}
holds with probability
\frac{1+\ell}{s_{n}+\ell}
and
\tau_{-\ell}\leq n
with probability
1-\frac{1+\ell}{s_{n}+\ell}
(see Figure

).

Figure 2:
Simulation of the conditioned random walks
P_{\infty}
and
P_{\ell}
under the coupling
\mathbb{P}^{(1),n}_{\ell,\infty}
in the case
\tau_{-\ell}>n
for

\ell=2000
(after time

Proof.

The key ingredient of the proof is the relation
h^{\downarrow}_{\ell}(k)=h^{\downarrow}(\ell)h^{\uparrow}(k)/(2(\ell+k))
for
k,\ell\geq 1
coming from the exact expression of the harmonic functions. From the definition of
P_{\ell}
under
\mathbb{P}^{(1)}_{\ell}
as a
h^{\downarrow}_{\ell}
-Doob transform of a
\nu
-random walk
S
, we note that if
s_{1},\ldots,s_{n}\in\mathbb{N}^{*}

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}^{(1)}_{\ell}((P_{\ell}(k))_{1\leq k\leq n}=(s_{k})_{1\leq k\leq n})

\displaystyle=\frac{h^{\downarrow}_{\ell}(s_{n})}{h^{\downarrow}_{\ell}(1)}\mathbb{P}_{1}((S_{k})_{1\leq k\leq n}=(s_{k})_{1\leq k\leq n})

\displaystyle=\frac{h^{\uparrow}(s_{n})}{h^{\uparrow}(1)}\frac{1+\ell}{s_{n}+\ell}\mathbb{P}_{1}((S_{k})_{1\leq k\leq n}=(s_{k})_{1\leq k\leq n})

\displaystyle=\frac{1+\ell}{s_{n}+\ell}\mathbb{P}^{(1)}_{\infty}((P_{\infty}(k))_{1\leq k\leq n}=(s_{k})_{1\leq k\leq n})

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}^{(1)}_{\infty}((P_{\infty}(k))_{1\leq k\leq n}=(s_{k})_{1\leq k\leq n}).

One can thus define such a coupling.
∎

One can see from the proof that the above lemma only relies on condition (

\tau_{-\ell}/\ell^{(a-1)}
when
\bf q
has type

. Recall that
\Upsilon_{a}^{\uparrow}
is the stable Lévy process

Proposition 2.6
.

We have the convergence in law
{\tau_{-\ell}}/{\ell^{a-1}}\mathop{\longrightarrow}\limits_{\ell\to\infty}^{\text{d}}\tilde{\tau}/p_{\bf q}

where
\tilde{\tau}
is a random variable such that for all

\mathbb{P}(\tilde{\tau}\geq u)=\mathbb{E}({1/(1+\Upsilon_{a}^{\uparrow}(u))}).

Proof.

By Lemma
2.5
and Theorem
2.1
, for every

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}^{(1)}_{\ell}(\tau_{-\ell}>u\ell^{a-1})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}^{(1)}_{\infty}\frac{1+\ell}{P_{\infty}({\lfloor u\ell^{a-1}\rfloor})+\ell}\mathop{\longrightarrow}\limits_{\ell\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\frac{1}{1+\Upsilon_{a}^{\uparrow}(p_{\bf q}u)},

and the claim follows.
∎

2.4
Local limit results for the random walk conditioned to die at
-\ell

The result of Proposition

can be refined to obtain a joint local limit theorem for the lifetime

together with the last positive value of the conditioned random walk

Proposition 2.7
.

For all
\varepsilon>0
the convergence below holds uniformly in

\ell^{a}\mathbb{P}^{(1)}_{\ell}(\tau_{-\ell}=\lfloor\ell^{a-1}s\rfloor\text{ and }P_{\ell}(\tau_{-\ell}-1)=\lfloor\ell u\rfloor)\mathop{\longrightarrow}\limits_{\ell\to\infty}\frac{\pi p_{\bf q}^{1-1/(a-1)}}{s^{1/(a-1)}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{u}(1+u)^{a}}f_{a}^{\uparrow}\left(\frac{u}{(p_{\bf q}s)^{1/(a-1)}}\right),

and the right-hand side is a probability density.

Proof.

Let
\varepsilon>0
. Let us write
n=\lfloor\ell^{a-1}s\rfloor
and
x=\lfloor\ell u\rfloor
. Then,

\frac{1}{2}\frac{h^{\downarrow}(\ell)}{\ell+1}\mathbb{P}^{(1)}_{\ell}(\tau_{-\ell}=\lfloor\ell^{a-1}s\rfloor\text{ and }P_{\ell}(\tau_{-\ell}-1)=\lfloor\ell u\rfloor)=\mathbb{P}_{1}(S_{1},\ldots,S_{n-1}\geq 1\text{ and }S_{n-1}=x)\nu(-\ell-x).

But by Lemma
2.3
and since
\nu(-k)\sim p_{\bf q}k^{-a}
as

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{1}(S_{1},\ldots,S_{n-1}\geq 1\text{ and }S_{n-1}=x)\nu(-\ell-x)

\displaystyle\mathop{\sim}\limits_{\ell\to\infty}\frac{\sqrt{\pi}p_{\bf q}^{1-1/(a-1)}}{2s^{1/(a-1)}\ell^{a+3/2}}f_{a}^{\uparrow}\left(\frac{u}{(p_{\bf q}s)^{1/(a-1)}}\right)\frac{1}{\sqrt{u}(1+u)^{a}}.

This proves the desired convergence using that
h^{\downarrow}(\ell)\sim 1/\sqrt{\pi\ell}
as
\ell\to\infty
. Let us check that the right term indeed corresponds to a probability density, in other words that

\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\pi p_{\bf q}^{1-1/(a-1)}}{s^{1/(a-1)}}\int_{0}^{\infty}f_{a}^{\uparrow}\left(\frac{u}{(p_{\bf q}s)^{1/(a-1)}}\right)\frac{1}{\sqrt{u}(1+u)^{a}}duds=1

By the change of variable
w=\frac{u}{(p_{\bf q}s)^{1/(a-1)}}
at fixed
u
,

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\pi p_{\bf q}^{1-1/(a-1)}}{s^{1/(a-1)}}\int_{0}^{\infty}f_{a}^{\uparrow}\left(\frac{u}{(p_{\bf q}s)^{1/(a-1)}}\right)\frac{1}{\sqrt{u}(1+u)^{a}}duds

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\pi u^{a-2}(a-1)}{\sqrt{u}(1+u)^{a}}w^{1-a}f_{a}^{\uparrow}(v)dwdu

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\pi u^{a-2}(a-1)}{\sqrt{u}(1+u)^{a}}du\mathbb{E}\frac{1}{\Upsilon^{\uparrow}_{a}(1)^{a-1}}.

Yet, by the change of variable
t=1/(1+u)
, by using the link between the Beta function and the Gamma function and using that

, one can see that

\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\pi u^{a-2}(a-1)}{\sqrt{u}(1+u)^{a}}du=\frac{(a-1)\pi^{3/2}\Gamma(a-3/2)}{2\Gamma(a)}.

Finally, by exactly the same reasoning as in the end of Remark 3 in
[
11

, one can compute

\mathbb{E}\frac{1}{\Upsilon^{\uparrow}_{a}(1)^{a-1}}=\frac{2\Gamma(a)}{(a-1)\pi^{3/2}\Gamma(a-3/2)},

which is precisely the inverse of the above integral. Indeed, since
\Upsilon_{a}^{\uparrow}
is a positive self-similar Markov process, let us denote by
\xi^{\uparrow}
the Lévy process arising in the Lamperti representation of
\Upsilon^{\uparrow}_{a}
. Then by
[
4
]
, we know that
\mathbb{E}1/\Upsilon^{\uparrow}_{a}(1)^{a-1}=1/((a-1)\mathbb{E}\xi^{\uparrow}_{1})
. But the Laplace exponent of
(\Gamma(a)/\pi)\xi^{\uparrow}
is given by
\kappa_{a-1}(\omega_{+}+q)
where
\omega_{+}=a+1/2
and
\kappa_{a-1}
is defined by Equation (19) of
[
2
]
. One could have made the computations using the characteristic exponent of
\xi^{\uparrow}
identified in Proposition 2 of
[
20
]
.
∎

From Proposition
2.7
one can get the following local estimates for

and

\mathbb{P}^{(1)}_{\ell}(\tau_{-\ell}=\lfloor\ell^{a-1}s\rfloor)\geq(1+o(1))\frac{\pi p_{\bf q}^{1-1/(a-1)}}{\ell^{a-1}s^{1/(a-1)}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\sqrt{u}(1+u)^{a}}f_{a}^{\uparrow}\left({u\over(p_{\bf q}s)^{1/(a-1)}}\right)du.

2.5
Scaling limit of the random walk conditioned to die at
-\ell

The convergence in distribution of
(\tau_{-\ell}/\ell^{a-1},P_{\ell}(\tau_{-\ell}-1)/\ell)
implied by Proposition
2.7
leads us to the following scaling limit: the rescaled random walk conditioned to die at
-\ell
, which is written
({P_{\ell}(\ell^{a-1}t)/\ell})_{t\geq 0}
under
\mathbb{P}^{(1)}_{\ell}
converges in distribution towards
(\Upsilon^{\downarrow}_{-1,a}(p_{\bf q}t))_{t\geq 0}
where
\Upsilon^{\downarrow}_{-1,a}
is the
(a-1)
-stable Lévy process conditioned to stay positive until it dies at
-1
. This process is defined as follows. Let
(\tilde{\tau},Z)
be a random variable of law

g(s,z)dsdz\coloneqq\frac{\pi}{s^{1/(a-1)}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{z}(1+z)^{a}}f_{a}^{\uparrow}\left(\frac{z}{s^{1/(a-1)}}\right)dsdz.

We define
\Upsilon^{\downarrow}_{-1,a}
so that, conditionally on
(\tilde{\tau},Z)
, the path
(\Upsilon^{\downarrow}_{-1,a}(t))_{0\leq t<\tilde{\tau}}
is a stable meander associated with
\Upsilon_{a}
of length
\tilde{\tau}
conditioned on
\Upsilon^{\downarrow}_{-1,a}(\tilde{\tau}-)=Z
, and for all
t\geq\tilde{\tau}
, we set
\Upsilon^{\downarrow}_{-1,a}(t)=-1
.

Formally, the law of the process
\Upsilon^{\downarrow}_{-1,a}
can be characterized with the law of the meander of length one
\widetilde{\Upsilon}_{a}
. To express it, we introduce the density of
\tilde{\tau}
that we write
f
.
For all
s>0
, we define the process
\widetilde{\Upsilon}_{a}^{s}
using
\widetilde{\Upsilon}_{a}
by setting for all
t\geq 0
,

\widetilde{\Upsilon}^{s}_{a}(t)\coloneqq\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}s^{1/(a-1)}\widetilde{\Upsilon}_{a}\left(t/s\right)&\text{ if }t<s,\\
-1&\text{ otherwise.}\end{array}\right.

Notice that
(s^{1/(a-1)}\widetilde{\Upsilon}_{a}(t/s))_{t\geq 0}
has the same law as the meander of length
s
evaluated at time
t
because
\Upsilon_{a}
is
(a-1)
-stable.
We then define the law of
\Upsilon^{\downarrow}_{-1,a}
by the relation

\mathbb{E}\,F\left(\left(\Upsilon^{\downarrow}_{-1,a}(t)\right)_{t\geq 0}\right)=\int_{0}^{\infty}f(s)\mathbb{E}\left(\frac{g(s,s^{1/(a-1)}\widetilde{\Upsilon}_{a}(1))}{f(s)\tilde{f}_{a}\left(\widetilde{\Upsilon}_{a}(1)\right)}F\left(\widetilde{\Upsilon}_{a}^{s}\right)\right)ds

for all bounded measurable function
F:\mathbb{D}(\mathbb{R}_{+},\mathbb{R})\to\mathbb{R}
, where
\mathbb{D}(\mathbb{R}_{+},\mathbb{R})
is the space of càdlàg functions from
\mathbb{R}_{+}
to
\mathbb{R}
equipped with the
J_{1}
Skorokhod topology and
\tilde{f}_{a}
is the density of
\widetilde{\Upsilon}_{a}(1)
.
This is indeed a characterization of the law of
\widetilde{\Upsilon}^{\downarrow}_{-1,a}
as the meander of length
s>0
has the same law as
(s^{1/(a-1)}\widetilde{\Upsilon}_{a}(t/s))_{t\geq 0}
.

Theorem 2.8
.

With the convention that
P_{\ell}(k)=-\ell
for
k\geq\tau_{-\ell}
, we have the convergence in distribution with respect to the Skorokhod topology:

\text{Under }\mathbb{P}^{(1)}_{\ell},\qquad\left(\frac{P_{\ell}(\ell^{a-1}t)}{\ell}\right)_{t\geq 0}\mathop{\longrightarrow}\limits_{\ell\to\infty}^{(\mathrm{d})}(\Upsilon^{\downarrow}_{-1,a}(p_{\bf q}t))_{t\geq 0}.

The above theorem provides a first reason to see
\Upsilon^{\downarrow}_{-1,a}
as the Lévy process
\Upsilon_{a}
conditioned to stay positive until it jumps and dies at
-1
.

Proof.

The main ideas of the proof are the scaling limit result on random walks conditioned to stay positive up to time
n
of
[
14
]
, which was recalled in Theorem
2.2
, together with absolute continuity relations. Let
F:\mathbb{D}(\mathbb{R}_{+},\mathbb{R})\to\mathbb{R}
be a bounded continuous real function. For conciseness, we set for all
\ell\geq 1
, for all
s,u\geq 0
,

g_{\ell}(s,u)=\ell^{a}\mathbb{P}_{\ell}^{(1)}\left(\tau_{-\ell}=\lfloor\ell^{a-1}s\rfloor\text{ and }P_{\ell}(\lfloor\ell^{a-1}s\rfloor-1)=\lfloor\ell u\rfloor\right),\ f_{\ell}(s)=\ell^{a-1}\mathbb{P}_{\ell}^{(1)}\left(\tau_{-\ell}=\lfloor\ell^{a-1}s\rfloor\right)

and

\tilde{f}_{\ell,s}(u)=\ell\mathbb{P}_{1}\left(\left.S_{\lfloor\ell^{a-1}s\rfloor-1}=\lfloor\ell u\rfloor\right|S_{1},\ldots,S_{\lfloor\ell^{a-1}s\rfloor-1}\geq 1\right),

where we recall that
(S_{i})_{i\geq 0}
is a
\nu
-random walk. We also write for all
s\geq 0,\ell\geq 1,t\geq 0
,

S^{s,\ell}(t)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}{S_{\lfloor\ell^{a-1}t\rfloor}}/{\ell}&\mbox{if }t<s,\\
-1&\mbox{otherwise.}\end{array}\right.

Then, by the expression of the law of
P_{\ell}
under
\mathbb{P}^{(1)}_{\ell}
, we get

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}^{(1)}_{\ell}

\displaystyle F\left(\left(\frac{P_{\ell}(\ell^{a-1}t)}{\ell}\right)_{t\geq 0}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k,n\geq 1}\mathbb{P}_{\ell}^{(1)}\left(\tau_{-\ell}=k,P_{\ell}(k-1)=n\right)\mathbb{E}_{1}\left(\left.F\left(S^{k/\ell^{a-1},\ell}\right)\right|S_{1},\ldots,S_{k-1}\geq 1,\ S_{k-1}=n\right)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}ds\sum_{n\geq 1}\frac{1}{\ell}g_{\ell}(s,\frac{n}{\ell})\mathbb{E}_{1}\left(\left.F\left(S^{s,\ell}\right)\right|S_{1},\ldots,S_{\lfloor\ell^{a-1}s\rfloor-1}\geq 1,\ S_{\lfloor\ell^{a-1}s\rfloor-1}=n\right)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}dsf_{\ell}(s)\mathbb{E}_{1}\left(\left.\frac{g_{\ell}(s,S^{s,\ell}(s-1/\ell^{a-1}))}{f_{\ell}(s)\tilde{f}_{\ell,s}(S^{s,\ell}(s-1/\ell^{a-1}))}F\left(S^{s,\ell}\right)\right|S_{1},\ldots,S_{\lfloor\ell^{a-1}s\rfloor-1}\geq 1\right).

The factor inside the expectation can be computed as follows: for all
k,n\geq 1
,

\displaystyle\frac{g_{\ell}\left({k}/{\ell^{a-1}},{n}/{\ell}\right)}{f_{\ell}(k/\ell^{a-1})\tilde{f}_{\ell,k/\ell^{a-1}}(n/\ell)}

\displaystyle=\frac{\frac{2(\ell+1)}{h^{\downarrow}(\ell)}\mathbb{P}_{1}(S_{1},\ldots,S_{k-1}\geq 1\text{ and }S_{k-1}=n)\nu(-\ell-n)}{\mathbb{P}^{(1)}_{\ell}(\tau_{-\ell}=k)\mathbb{P}_{1}(S_{k-1}=n|S_{1},\ldots,S_{k-1}\geq 1)}

\displaystyle=\frac{2(\ell+1)\nu(-\ell-n)\mathbb{P}_{1}(S_{1},\ldots,S_{k-1}\geq 1)}{h^{\downarrow}(\ell)\mathbb{P}_{\ell}^{(1)}(\tau_{-\ell}=k)}

\displaystyle=\frac{\nu(-\ell-n)\mathbb{P}_{1}(S_{1},\ldots,S_{k-1}\geq 1)}{\mathbb{P}_{1}(S_{1},\ldots,S_{k-1}\geq 1\text{ and }S_{k}=-\ell)}

\displaystyle=\left({\sum_{m\geq 1}\frac{\nu(-\ell-m)}{\nu(-\ell-n)}\mathbb{P}_{1}(S_{k-1}=m|S_{1},\ldots,S_{k-1}\geq 1)}\right)^{-1}

\displaystyle=\left({\mathbb{E}_{1}\left(\left.\frac{\nu(-\ell-S_{k-1})}{\nu(-\ell-n)}\right|S_{1},\ldots,S_{k-1}\geq 1\right)}\right)^{-1}.

Using Theorem
2.2
, is easy to see that if
s,u>0
, then

\mathbb{E}_{1}\left(\left.\frac{\nu(-\ell-S_{\lfloor\ell^{a-1}s\rfloor-1})}{\nu(-\ell-\lfloor\ell u\rfloor)}\right|S_{1},\ldots,S_{\lfloor\ell^{a-1}s\rfloor-1}\geq 1\right)\mathop{\longrightarrow}\limits_{\ell\to\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty}\left(\frac{1+u}{1+v}\right)^{a}\tilde{f}_{a}\left(\frac{v}{(p_{\bf q}s)^{1/(a-1)}}\right)dv,

where
\tilde{f}_{a}
is the density of
\widetilde{\Upsilon}_{a}(1)
. Indeed, by (
II
),

\sup_{n,m\geq 1}\left|\frac{\nu(-\ell-m)}{\nu(-\ell-n)}-\left(\frac{\ell+n}{\ell+m}\right)^{a}\right|\mathop{\longrightarrow}\limits_{\ell\to\infty}0.

Furthermore, since there exists a constant
C_{1}>0
such that for all
x\geq 0
,
f_{a}^{\uparrow}(x)=C_{1}x^{1/2}\tilde{f}_{a}(x)
, we have

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\left(\frac{1+u}{1+v}\right)^{a}\tilde{f}_{a}\left(\frac{v}{(p_{\bf q}s)^{1/(a-1)}}\right)dv

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{C_{1}}({p_{\bf q}s})^{1/(2(a-1))}(1+u)^{a}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\sqrt{v}(1+v)^{a}}f_{a}^{\uparrow}\left(\frac{v}{(p_{\bf q}s)^{1/(a-1)}}\right)dv

\displaystyle=\frac{({p_{\bf q}s})^{1/(2(a-1))}(1+u)^{a}}{C_{1}}\frac{(p_{\bf q}s)^{1/(a-1)}}{\pi}f(p_{\bf q}s)

\displaystyle=\frac{f(p_{\bf q}s)\tilde{f}_{a}\left(\frac{u}{(p_{\bf q}s)^{1/(a-1)}}\right)}{g(p_{\bf q}s,u)}.

Thus, again using Theorem
2.2
, we obtain that for all
s>0
, conditionally on
S_{1},\ldots,S_{\lfloor\ell^{a-1}s\rfloor}\geq 1
, the random variable

{g_{\ell}(s,S^{s,\ell}(s-1/\ell^{a-1}))}/({f_{\ell}(s)\tilde{f}_{\ell,s}(S^{s,\ell}(s-1/\ell^{a-1}))})

converges in law towards

\frac{1}{\int_{0}^{\infty}\left(\frac{1+(p_{\bf q}s)^{1/(a-1)}\widetilde{\Upsilon}_{a}(1)}{1+v}\right)^{a}\tilde{f}_{a}\left(\frac{v}{(p_{\bf q}s)^{1/(a-1)}}\right)dv}=\frac{g(p_{\bf q}s,(p_{\bf q}s)^{1/(a-1)}\widetilde{\Upsilon}_{a}(1))}{f(p_{\bf q}s)\tilde{f}_{a}\left(\widetilde{\Upsilon}_{a}(1)\right)}.

Besides, if
s>0
, one can upper bound uniformly in
n,\ell\geq 1

\displaystyle\frac{g_{\ell}\left(s,{n}/{\ell}\right)}{f_{\ell}(s)\tilde{f}_{\ell,s}(n/\ell)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sum_{m\geq 1}\frac{\nu(-\ell-m)}{\nu(-\ell-n)}\mathbb{P}_{1}(S_{\lfloor\ell^{a-1}s\rfloor-1}=m|S_{1},\ldots,S_{\lfloor\ell^{a-1}s\rfloor-1}\geq 1)}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C}{\mathbb{P}_{1}(\left.S_{\lfloor\ell^{a-1}s\rfloor-1}\in[0,\ell]\right|S_{1},\ldots,S_{\lfloor\ell^{a-1}s\rfloor-1}\geq 1)}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C^{\prime}}{\mathbb{P}((p_{\bf q}s)^{1/(a-1)}\widetilde{\Upsilon}_{a}(1)\in[0,1])}\text{ by Theorem \ref{cvm\'{e}andre}},

where
C,C^{\prime}
are constants that only depend on
\nu
.
Therefore, by dominated convergence and using once again Theorem
2.2
, for all
s>0
,

\displaystyle G_{\ell}(s)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\mathbb{E}_{1}\left(\left.\frac{g_{\ell}(s,S^{s,\ell}(s-1/\ell^{a-1}))}{f_{\ell}(s)\tilde{f}_{\ell,s}(S^{s,\ell}(s-1/\ell^{a-1}))}F\left(S^{s,\ell}\right)\right|S_{1},\ldots,S_{\lfloor\ell^{a-1}s\rfloor-1}\geq 1\right)

\displaystyle\mathop{\longrightarrow}\limits_{\ell\to\infty}G(s)\coloneqq\mathbb{E}\left(\frac{g(p_{\bf q}s,(p_{\bf q}s)^{1/(a-1)}\widetilde{\Upsilon}_{a}(1))}{f(p_{\bf q}s)\tilde{f}_{a}\left(\widetilde{\Upsilon}_{a}(1)\right)}F\left((\widetilde{\Upsilon}_{a}^{p_{\bf q}s}(p_{\bf q}t))_{t\geq 0}\right)\right).

Furthermore, from Proposition
2.7
, and using Scheffé’s lemma,
f_{\ell}
converges in
\mathrm{L}^{1}(\mathbb{R})
towards
p_{\bf q}f(p_{\bf q}\cdot)
. Therefore, again by dominated convergence, we obtain that for all
A>0
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}^{(1)}_{\ell}\left(F\left(\left(\frac{P_{\ell}(\ell^{a-1}t)}{\ell}\right)_{t\geq 0}\right){\bf 1}_{\frac{\tau_{-\ell}}{\ell^{a-1}}<A}\right)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{A}f_{\ell}(s)G_{\ell}(s)ds

\displaystyle\mathop{\longrightarrow}\limits_{\ell\to\infty}\int_{0}^{A}p_{\bf q}f(p_{\bf q}s)G(s)ds

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left(F\left(\left(\Upsilon^{\downarrow}_{-1,a}(p_{\bf q}t)\right)_{t\geq 0}\right){\bf 1}_{\tilde{\tau}<p_{\bf q}A}\right),

where the last equality stems from the definition of
\Upsilon^{\downarrow}_{-1,a}
and from the change of variables
s^{\prime}=p_{\bf q}s
. But since
{\tau_{-\ell}}/{\ell^{a-1}}
converges in law, and since
F
is bounded, we get that

\sup_{\ell\geq 1}\mathbb{E}^{(1)}_{\ell}\left(F\left(\left(\frac{P_{\ell}(\ell^{a-1}t)}{\ell}\right)_{t\geq 0}\right){\bf 1}_{\frac{\tau_{-\ell}}{\ell^{a-1}}\geq A}\right)\mathop{\longrightarrow}\limits_{A\to\infty}0,

hence the desired result.
∎

\Upsilon_{a}^{\uparrow}
using Lemma

and Theorem

:

Corollary 2.9
.

Let
\Upsilon^{\downarrow}_{-1,a}
as in Theorem

. Let
\tilde{\tau}
be the time at which
\Upsilon^{\downarrow}_{-1,a}
dies at

\mathbb{E}\,{\bf 1}_{\tilde{\tau}>t}F\left(\left(\Upsilon^{\downarrow}_{-1,a}(s)\right)_{0\leq s\leq t}\right)=\mathbb{E}\,\frac{1}{1+\Upsilon_{a}^{\uparrow}(t)}F\left(\left(\Upsilon_{a}^{\uparrow}(s)\right)_{0\leq s\leq t}\right).

One can also define for all
\lambda>0
, the Lévy process conditioned to stay positive until it jumps and dies at

\Upsilon^{\downarrow}_{-\lambda,a}
. It satisfies the scaling limit:

\text{Under }\mathbb{P}^{(1)}_{\lfloor\lambda\ell\rfloor},\ \left(\frac{P_{\lfloor\lambda\ell\rfloor}(\ell^{a-1}t)}{\ell}\right)_{t\geq 0}\mathop{\longrightarrow}\limits_{\ell\to\infty}^{(\mathrm{d})}\left(\Upsilon^{\downarrow}_{-\lambda,a}(p_{\bf q}t)\right)_{t\geq 0}.

Let
\tilde{\tau}_{-\lambda,a}
be the time at which
\Upsilon^{\downarrow}_{-\lambda,a}
jumps at
-\lambda
. From the coupling
\mathbb{P}^{(1),n}_{\lfloor\lambda\ell\rfloor,\infty}
, we get that conditionally on
\tilde{\tau}_{-\lambda,a}>t
, the process
(\Upsilon^{\downarrow}_{-\lambda,a}(s))_{0\leq s\leq t}
has the same law as
(\Upsilon_{a}^{\uparrow}(s))_{0\leq s\leq t}
. Furthermore, from the scaling limit it is easy to see that
\tilde{\tau}_{-\lambda,a}
has the same law as
\lambda^{a-1}\tilde{\tau}
. It is thus possible to show that

\Upsilon^{\downarrow}_{-\lambda,a}\mathop{\longrightarrow}\limits_{\lambda\to\infty}^{(\mathrm{d})}\Upsilon_{a}^{\uparrow}

in
\mathbb{D}(\mathbb{R}_{+},\mathbb{R})
equipped with the Skorokhod
J_{1}
-topology. This convergence echoes the local convergence of
P_{\ell}
under
\mathbb{P}^{(1)}_{\ell}
towards
P_{\infty}
under
\mathbb{P}^{(1)}_{\infty}
when
\ell\to\infty
.

3
The peeling exploration “starting from a uniform random edge”

In this section, we recall the filled-in (edge) peeling exploration of a map with a distinguished face called the target and how this exploration enables to recover the distance from the root face to that target face. In Subsection
3.4
, we show that for our purpose, we can start the exploration “from a uniform random edge” which will become the root while the former root face of degree
2\ell
will become the target face.

3.1
Filled-in exploration of a map with a target

The edge-peeling process was introduced by Budd in
[
8
]
and the results presented here can be found in more details in Chapters III and IV of
[
13
]
or in
[
7
]
.

A map with a target face
\mathfrak{m}_{\square}=(\mathfrak{m},\square)
is a (finite rooted bipartite planar) map
\mathfrak{m}
given with a distinguished face
\square
different from the root face
f_{r}
. For all
\ell,p\geq 1
, we denote by
\mathcal{M}_{p}^{(\ell)}
the set of finite maps of perimeter
2\ell
with a target face of perimeter
2p
. We set for all
\mathfrak{m}_{\square}\in\mathcal{M}_{p}^{(\ell)}
:

w_{\bf q}(\mathfrak{m}_{\square})=\prod_{f\in\mathrm{Faces}(\mathfrak{m})\setminus\left\{f_{r},\square\right\}}q_{\deg(f)/2}.

We then define also the partition function
W_{p}^{(\ell)}=\sum_{\mathfrak{m}_{\square}\in\mathcal{M}_{p}^{(\ell)}}w_{\bf q}(\mathfrak{m}_{\square})
.
Analogously, a map with a target vertex
\mathfrak{m}_{\bullet}=(\mathfrak{m},\bullet)
is a (finite rooted bipartite planar) map
\mathfrak{m}
with a distinguished vertex
\bullet
. We denote by
\mathcal{M}_{0}^{(\ell)}
the set of finite maps of perimeter
2\ell
with a target vertex. We set
w_{\bf q}(\mathfrak{m}_{\bullet})=w_{\bf q}(\mathfrak{m})
for all
\mathfrak{m}_{\bullet}\in\mathcal{M}_{0}^{(\ell)}
, and
W_{0}^{(\ell)}=\sum_{\mathfrak{m}_{\bullet}\in\mathcal{M}_{0}^{(\ell)}}w_{\bf q}(\mathfrak{m}_{\bullet})
. The fact that
\bf q
is admissible implies that
W_{p}^{(\ell)}<\infty
for all
\ell\geq 1,p\geq 0
. Moreover, we know that (see e.g. Equation (3.9) in
[
13
]
) for all
p,\ell\geq 1
,

W_{p}^{(\ell)}=\frac{1}{2}h^{\downarrow}_{p}(\ell)c_{\bf q}^{\ell+p}\qquad\text{and}\qquad W_{0}^{(\ell)}=h^{\downarrow}(\ell)c_{\bf q}^{\ell}.

(3.1)

We write
\mathbb{P}^{(\ell)}_{p}
the associated Boltzmann probability measure on
\mathcal{M}_{p}^{(\ell)}
for all
p\geq 0
.

Let
\ell\geq 1
. By Theorem 2 of
[
7
]
, one can define a random infinite planar map
\mathfrak{M}_{\infty}^{(\ell)}
as the local limit in distribution as
p\to\infty
of random maps of law
\mathbb{P}^{(\ell)}_{p}
. The map
\mathfrak{M}_{\infty}^{(\ell)}
is almost surely locally finite and one-ended, and is called a
\bf q
-Boltzmann infinite planar random map of the plane. Its law is denoted by
\mathbb{P}_{\infty}^{(\ell)}
.

A (filled-in) peeling algorithm
\mathcal{A}
is a function which associates to a map
\overline{\mathfrak{e}}
with a distinguished simple face (different from the root face) an edge on the boundary
\partial\overline{\mathfrak{e}}
of this distinguished face. Let
\mathfrak{m}_{*}
be a map with a target face
\square
(“
*=\square
”), a target vertex
\bullet
(“
*=\bullet
”) or an infinite one-ended bipartite map (“
*=\infty
”). A filled-in peeling exploration of
\mathfrak{m}_{*}
with algorithm
\mathcal{A}
is an increasing sequence
(\overline{\mathfrak{e}}_{n})_{n\geq 0}
of sub-maps of
\mathfrak{m}
which contain the root face and which are maps with a distinguished simple face called a hole, where by sub-map we mean that gluing a well chosen map with perimeter corresponding with that of the hole of
\overline{\mathfrak{e}}_{n}
will give back the map
\mathfrak{m}_{*}
. This sequence of sub-maps is constructed inductively using the algorithm
\mathcal{A}
:
\overline{\mathfrak{e}}
is the map with only two faces which are the root face and a hole of the same perimeter and then for each
n\geq 0
, the sub-map
\overline{\mathfrak{e}}_{n+1}
is obtained from
\overline{\mathfrak{e}}_{n}
by peeling the edge
\mathcal{A}(\overline{\mathfrak{e}}_{n})
in
\mathfrak{m}_{*}
(see Figure
3
). When peeling an edge, there are three cases:

(i)

The face in
\mathfrak{m}_{*}
on the other side of
\mathcal{A}(\overline{\mathfrak{e}}_{n})
is not a face of
\overline{\mathfrak{e}}_{n}
and is not the target face
\square
when
*=\square
; then
\overline{\mathfrak{e}}_{n+1}
is obtained by gluing that face to
\overline{\mathfrak{e}}_{n}
onto the edge
\mathcal{A}(\overline{\mathfrak{e}}_{n})
; we denote this case by
C_{k}
where
k
is the half-degree of the discovered face.

(ii)

The face in
\mathfrak{m}_{*}
on the other side of
\mathcal{A}(\overline{\mathfrak{e}}_{n})
is the target face
\square
(when
*=\square
); in that case, denoted by
C_{p}^{\mathrm{stop}}
where
p
is the half-degree of the target face, we set
\overline{\mathfrak{e}}_{n+1}=\mathfrak{m}_{\square}
and the exploration stops.

(iii)

Or the other side of
\mathcal{A}(\overline{\mathfrak{e}}_{n})
corresponds to a face already discovered in
\overline{\mathfrak{e}}_{n}
which must be on the boundary of the hole. In this case
\overline{\mathfrak{e}}_{n+1}
is obtained by identifying the two edges in the hole of
\overline{\mathfrak{e}}_{n}
. This creates at most two holes. If
*\in\{\square,\bullet\}
, we fill-in the hole which does not contain the target. If
*=\infty
, we fill-in the hole containing a finite part of
\mathfrak{m}_{\infty}
. This case is denoted by
G_{k,*}
or
G_{*,k}
where
2k
is the degree of the hole which is filled-in, depending whether this hole is created on the left or on the right of the peeled edge. If
*=\bullet
and
k=|\partial\overline{\mathfrak{e}}_{n}|/2-1
, then the exploration stops.

Figure 3:
A filled-in exploration of a map with a target face
\mathfrak{m}_{\square}\in\mathcal{M}^{5}_{1}
. The peeled edge is in red, the root face is in white, the target face is in black and the hole containing the target is in grey. At the first step, an event
C_{4}
happens, then an event
G_{1,*}
occurs and finally the event
C_{1}^{\mathrm{stop}}
ends the exploration.

The peeling algorithm
\mathcal{A}
may be arbitrary, even random, but we require it to be Markovian with respect to the exploration in the sense that, conditionally on the explored region
\overline{\mathfrak{e}}_{n}
, the conditional law of
\mathcal{A}(\overline{\mathfrak{e}}_{n})
only depends on
\overline{\mathfrak{e}}_{n}
. For instance, the peeled edge may be chosen uniformly at random on the boundary of the hole as in Subsection
3.2
or in such a way that the dual distance from the peeled edge to the root is non-decreasing along the exploration as in Subsection
3.3
. During the peeling exploration, one records the evolution of half of the number of edges on the boundary of the hole, which is denoted by
(P_{p}(n))_{n\geq 0}
if
*\in\{\bullet,\square\}
and
(P_{\infty}(n))_{n\geq 0}
if
*=\infty
and called the perimeter process associated with the exploration. One can express the law of the peeling process (and the law of the perimeter process) under
\mathbb{P}^{(\ell)}_{p}
or under
\mathbb{P}^{(\ell)}_{\infty}
using Doob
h
-transforms of the random walk
S=(S_{n})_{n\geq 0}
of step distribution
\nu
.

Now we recall the law of the filled-in peeling exploration
(\overline{\mathfrak{e}}_{n})_{n\geq 0}
under
\mathbb{P}^{(\ell)}_{p}
or under
\mathbb{P}^{(\ell)}_{\infty}
, as described by Proposition 4.7 from
[
13
]
in the case
*=\square,\bullet
and
[
8
]
in the case
*=\infty
(see also Proposition 7.4 in
[
13
]
).

Proposition 3.1
.

(
[
13
]
,
[
8
]
)
Let
\ell,p\geq 1
, let
\mathcal{A}
be a peeling algorithm and
(\overline{\mathfrak{e}}_{n})_{n\geq 0}
be the peeling process under
\mathbb{P}_{0}^{(\ell)},\mathbb{P}^{(\ell)}_{p}
or
\mathbb{P}^{(\ell)}_{\infty}
. Then
(\overline{\mathfrak{e}}_{n})_{n\geq 0}
is a Markov chain with the following transition probabilities: conditionally on
\overline{\mathfrak{e}}_{n}
, and
P(n)=m\geq 1
(assuming that the exploration has not stopped yet), the events
C_{k},C_{p}^{\mathrm{stop}}
(for
*=\square
)
,G_{k,*}
and
G_{*,k}
occur respectively with probabilities

\nu(k-1)\frac{h(m+k-1)}{h(m)},\ \nu(-m-p)\frac{h(-p)}{h(m)},\ \frac{1}{2}\nu(-k-1)\frac{h(m-k-1)}{h(m)}\text{ and }\frac{1}{2}\nu(-k-1)\frac{h(m-k-1)}{h(m)},

where
h=h^{\downarrow}_{p}
for
*=\square
,
h=h^{\downarrow}
for
*=\bullet
and
h=h^{\uparrow}
for
*=\infty
.
Moreover, conditionally on each event, the holes filled-in during the exploration are independent finite
\bf q
-Boltzmann maps (without target) of corresponding perimeters.

As a consequence, for all
p\geq 0
, the perimeter process under
\mathbb{P}_{p}^{(\ell)}
(resp. under
\mathbb{P}_{\infty}^{(\ell)}
) indeed corresponds to the Doob
h^{\downarrow}_{p}
-transform killed when it reaches
-p
(resp. Doob
h^{\uparrow}
-transform) of the walk
S
started from
\ell
, if we set by convention that the perimeter drops to
-p
when the exploration stops.

3.2
Uniform peeling and first-passage percolation distance

It is possible to use the peeling process to study the first-passage percolation distance on a map with a target
\mathfrak{m}_{*}
, or an infinite map of the plane. A very convenient algorithm is the uniform peeling algorithm
\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{uniform}}
which selects an edge on the boundary of the hole uniformly at random at every step. We refer to
[
13
]
13.1 or
[
10
]
2.4 for details.

This exploration can be related to the fpp distance on
\mathfrak{m}_{*}
in the following way: for all
t\geq 0
, let
\overline{\mathrm{Ball}}^{\mathrm{fpp}}_{t}(\mathfrak{m}_{*})
be the sub-map of
\mathfrak{m}_{*}
obtained by keeping the connected subset of dual edges whose endpoints are at fpp distance at most
t
from the root face, then by gluing the corresponding faces according to those dual edges and finally by filling in the holes that do not contain the target (the finite holes in the case
*=\infty
). Then the process
(\overline{\mathrm{Ball}}^{\mathrm{fpp}}_{t}(\mathfrak{m}_{*}))_{t\geq 0}
admits jumps
0=T_{0}<T_{1}<\cdots
. One can check (see Proposition 2.3 in
[
10
]
) that for all
n\geq 0
, the sub-map

\overline{\mathrm{Ball}}^{\mathrm{fpp}}_{T_{n+1}}(\mathfrak{m})
can be obtained from
\overline{\mathrm{Ball}}^{\mathrm{fpp}}_{T_{n}}(\mathfrak{m}_{*})
by the peeling of a uniform random edge on the boundary of the hole. Furthermore, conditionally on
(\overline{\mathrm{Ball}}^{\mathrm{fpp}}_{T_{n}}(\mathfrak{m}_{*}))_{n\geq 0}
, the random variables
T_{n+1}-T_{n}
are independent and follow exponential laws of parameter
|\partial\overline{\mathrm{Ball}}^{\mathrm{fpp}}_{T_{n}}(\mathfrak{m}_{*})|
where
\partial\overline{\mathrm{Ball}}^{\mathrm{fpp}}_{T_{n}}(\mathfrak{m}_{*})
is the set of edges on the boundary of
\overline{\mathrm{Ball}}^{\mathrm{fpp}}_{T_{n}}(\mathfrak{m}_{*})
.

From the above results, by coupling the uniform exploration with the exponential variables that define the fpp distance, the fpp distance from the root face to the submap
\mathfrak{u}
filling the hole of boundary
\partial\overline{\mathfrak{e}}_{n}
can be written as

d_{\mathrm{fpp}}^{\dagger}(f_{r},\mathfrak{u})=T_{n+1}=\sum_{i=0}^{n}\frac{\mathcal{E}_{i}}{|\partial\overline{\mathrm{Ball}}^{\mathrm{fpp}}_{T_{i}}(\mathfrak{m}_{*})|}=\sum_{i=0}^{n}\frac{\mathcal{E}_{i}}{2P(i)},

(3.2)

where
P(i)=|\partial\overline{\mathrm{Ball}}^{\mathrm{fpp}}_{T_{i}}(\mathfrak{m})|/2
is the perimeter process associated with the exploration and the
\mathcal{E}_{i}
for
i\geq 0
are i.i.d. exponential random variables of parameter
1
which are independent from the exploration
(\overline{\mathfrak{e}}_{n})_{n\geq 0}
, and a fortiori from
(P(n))_{n\geq 0}
. In particular, if
\mathfrak{m}_{\square}\in\mathcal{M}^{(\ell)}_{p}
for
\ell,p\geq 1
and
n
is the time at which the target face
\square
is discovered, i.e. the event
C^{\mathrm{target}}_{p}
happens at time
n
, or equivalently
\overline{\mathfrak{e}}_{n}=\mathfrak{m}_{\square}
, then
d_{\mathrm{fpp}}^{\dagger}(f_{r},\square)=T_{n}
.

3.3
Peeling by layers and dual graph distance

In the same way, a particular peeling algorithm
\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{layers}}
called the peeling by layers algorithm is suited to the study of the dual graph distance on a map with a target
\mathfrak{m}_{*}
(or an infinite map of the plane). Recall that
d^{\dagger}_{\mathrm{gr}}
denotes the dual graph distance on a map
\mathfrak{m}
. If
f
is a face of
\mathfrak{m}
the distance to the root face
d^{\dagger}_{\mathrm{gr}}(f_{r},f)
is also called the height of
f
. We can also define the height of an edge
e
as the minimum of the heights of the faces on both sides of
e
.

The peeling algorithm
\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{layers}}
, described in
[
10
]
2.3, see also
[
13
]
p.185, is defined only on (finite) sub-maps
\overline{\mathfrak{e}}
of
\mathfrak{m}_{*}
with a hole (containing the target when
*=\square
) which satisfy the following hypothesis:

(
H
)

There exists an integer
h\geq 0
such that all the edges on the boundary of the hole
\partial\overline{\mathfrak{e}}
are at height
h
or
h+1
, and such that the set of edges of
\partial\overline{\mathfrak{e}}
at heigth
h
forms a (non empty) segment.

If
\overline{\mathfrak{e}}
satisfies (
H
), we then set
\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{layers}}(\overline{\mathfrak{e}})
to be the unique edge of
\partial\overline{\mathfrak{e}}
at height
h
such that the edge immediately on its left is at height
h+1
. If all the edges of
\partial\overline{\mathfrak{e}}
are at height
h
the edge
\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{layers}}(\overline{\mathfrak{e}})
is then chosen deterministically.

One can check that on the events
C_{k}
,
G_{*,k}
and
G_{k,*}
the sub-map obtained after peeling
\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{layers}}(\overline{\mathfrak{e}})
satisfies (
H
) again. Moreover,
\overline{\mathfrak{e}}_{0}
satisfies (
H
) as well. Thus the peeling exploration with algorithm
\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{layers}}
is well defined.

For every
n\geq 0
, let
H(n)
be the minimum of the heights of the edges of
\partial\overline{\mathfrak{e}}_{n}
. Then
H(0)=0
and for all
n\geq 0
, we have
\Delta H(n)\coloneqq H(n+1)-H(n)\in\{0,1\}
. One has
\Delta H(n)=1
when all the edges of
\partial\overline{\mathfrak{e}}_{n+1}
are at height
H(n)+1
. The process
H(n)
is called the height process associated with the peeling by layers exploration. The integer
H(n)+1
can also be seen as the height of the hole in
\overline{\mathfrak{e}}_{n}
. In particular, if
\mathfrak{m}_{\square}\in\mathcal{M}^{(\ell)}_{p}
for some
\ell,p\geq 1
and
n
is the first time such that
\overline{\mathfrak{e}}_{n}=\mathfrak{m}_{\square}
, then
d_{\mathrm{gr}}^{\dagger}(f_{r},\square)=H(n-1)+1
.

3.4
Exchanging the root and the target

In order to study the distance from the root face to a uniform random face of
\mathfrak{M}^{(\ell)}
, we will first look at the distance between the root face and a uniform random edge
E
in
\mathfrak{M}^{(\ell)}
. We will then come back to a uniform random face using a biasing argument in Subsection
4.3
. This distance is defined as the minimum of the distances from the root face to a face next to this edge.
By unzipping the uniform random edge and thus creating a distinguished face of degree
2
, one can see that the map obtained from
\mathfrak{M}^{(\ell)}
belongs to
\mathcal{M}_{1}^{(\ell)}
. We denote this map by
\widetilde{\mathfrak{M}}_{1}^{(\ell)}
.

Remark 3.2
.

This unzipping operation does not modify the distances so much.
More precisely, the graph distance
d^{\dagger}_{\mathrm{gr}}(f_{r},\square)
in
\widetilde{\mathfrak{M}}_{1}^{(\ell)}
equals
d^{\dagger}_{\mathrm{gr}}(f_{r},E)+1
in
\mathfrak{M}^{(\ell)}
while
d^{\dagger}_{\mathrm{fpp}}(f_{r},\square)
in
\widetilde{\mathfrak{M}}_{1}^{(\ell)}
is lower bounded by
d^{\dagger}_{\mathrm{fpp}}(f_{r},E)
and is upper bounded by
d^{\dagger}_{\mathrm{fpp}}(f_{r},E)+\mathcal{E}
in
\mathfrak{M}^{(\ell)}
where
\mathcal{E}
is an independent exponential variable of parameter
1
.

Besides, one can notice that exchanging the roles of the root face and of the target face by choosing a root edge on the former target face does not modify the distance between the root face and the target face.
All the operations we did are illustrated by Figure
4
. This swap between the root face and the target face will be useful insofar as in the next subsection we will introduce a coupling of
\mathbb{P}_{\ell}^{(1)}
with
\mathbb{P}^{(1)}_{\infty}
that will enable us to use the results of
[
11
]
. Let
\widetilde{\mathfrak{M}}_{\ell}^{(1)}
be the map obtained from
\widetilde{\mathfrak{M}}_{1}^{(\ell)}
by exchanging the root face and the target face and choosing the root edge uniformly at random around the new root face.

So as to transfer our results under
\mathbb{P}^{(1)}_{\ell}
to the map
\mathfrak{M}^{(\ell)}
equipped with a random uniform edge
E
, we state the following lemma.

Lemma 3.3
.

If
(\varphi_{\ell})_{\ell\geq 1}
is a sequence of functions from
\mathcal{M}^{(1)}_{\ell}
to
\mathbb{R}_{+}
uniformly bounded by a constant
C>0

such that

\mathbb{E}^{(1)}_{\ell}\varphi_{\ell}(\mathfrak{m}_{\square})\mathop{\longrightarrow}\limits_{\ell\to\infty}0,

then

\mathbb{E}\varphi_{\ell}\left(\widetilde{\mathfrak{M}}_{\ell}^{(1)}\right)\mathop{\longrightarrow}\limits_{\ell\to\infty}0.

In particular, if
(A_{\ell})_{\ell\geq 1}
is a sequence of subsets of
\mathcal{M}^{(1)}_{\ell}

such that

\mathbb{P}^{(1)}_{\ell}(A_{\ell})\to 0
as
\ell\to\infty
,
then

\mathbb{P}(\widetilde{\mathfrak{M}}_{\ell}^{(1)}\in A_{\ell})\to 0
.

Proof.

Observe first that the law of
\widetilde{\mathfrak{M}}_{1}^{(\ell)}
can be characterized using
\mathbb{P}^{(\ell)}_{1}
. Indeed, if
\mathfrak{m}_{\square}\in\mathcal{M}_{1}^{(\ell)}
is obtained by unzipping an edge of a map
\mathfrak{m}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{M}^{(\ell)}
, then

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\widetilde{\mathfrak{M}}_{1}^{(\ell)}=\mathfrak{m}_{\square})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\#\mathrm{Edges}(\mathfrak{m}^{\prime})}\mathbb{P}(\mathfrak{M}^{(\ell)}=\mathfrak{m}^{\prime})=\frac{1}{\#\mathrm{Edges}(\mathfrak{m}^{\prime})}\frac{w_{\bf q}(\mathfrak{m}^{\prime})}{W^{(\ell)}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\#\mathrm{Edges}(\mathfrak{m}_{\square})-1}\frac{W^{(\ell)}_{1}}{W^{(\ell)}}\frac{w_{\bf q}(\mathfrak{m}_{\square})}{W^{(\ell)}_{1}}=\frac{W^{(\ell)}_{1}}{W^{(\ell)}}\frac{1}{\#\mathrm{Edges}(\mathfrak{m}_{\square})-1}\mathbb{P}^{(\ell)}_{1}(\mathfrak{m}_{\square}).

Another well known and useful consequence of this unzipping operation is that

\mathbb{E}\,\#\mathrm{Edges}(\mathfrak{M}^{(\ell)})=\frac{W_{1}^{(\ell)}}{W^{(\ell)}}\mathop{\sim}\limits_{\ell\to\infty}\frac{\frac{1}{2}h^{\downarrow}_{1}(\ell)c_{\bf q}^{\ell+1}}{\frac{p_{\bf q}}{2}c_{\bf q}^{\ell+1}\ell^{-2}}\mathop{\sim}\limits_{\ell\to\infty}b_{\bf q}\ell^{3/2}\text{ where }b_{\bf q}=\frac{1}{2p_{\bf q}\sqrt{\pi}},

(3.3)

applying successively (
1.1
), (
3.1
), (
2.1
) and the fact that
h^{\downarrow}(\ell)\sim 1/\sqrt{\pi\ell}
.

Next, the law of
\widetilde{\mathfrak{M}}_{\ell}^{(1)}
can be expressed as follows. Let
\mathfrak{m}_{\square}\in\mathcal{M}^{(1)}_{\ell}
which is obtained by re-rooting a map
\mathfrak{m}^{\prime}_{\square}\in\mathcal{M}^{(\ell)}_{1}
. Then one can check that

\mathbb{P}\left(\widetilde{\mathfrak{M}}_{\ell}^{(1)}=\mathfrak{m}_{\square}\right)=\frac{2\ell}{2}\mathbb{P}\left(\widetilde{\mathfrak{M}}_{1}^{(\ell)}=\mathfrak{m}^{\prime}_{\square}\right)=\frac{2\ell}{2}\frac{W^{(\ell)}_{1}}{W^{(\ell)}}\frac{\mathbb{P}^{(\ell)}_{1}(\mathfrak{m}^{\prime}_{\square})}{\#\mathrm{Edges}(\mathfrak{m}^{\prime}_{\square})-1}=\frac{W^{(\ell)}_{1}}{W^{(\ell)}}\frac{\mathbb{P}^{(1)}_{\ell}(\mathfrak{m}_{\square})}{\#\mathrm{Edges}(\mathfrak{m}_{\square})-1}.

(3.4)

We deduce that

\mathbb{E}\varphi_{\ell}\left(\widetilde{\mathfrak{M}}_{\ell}^{(1)}\right)=\sum_{\mathfrak{m}_{\square}\in\mathcal{M}^{(1)}_{\ell}}\frac{W^{(\ell)}_{1}}{W^{(\ell)}}\frac{\mathbb{P}^{(1)}_{\ell}(\mathfrak{m}_{\square})}{\#\mathrm{Edges}(\mathfrak{m}_{\square})-1}\varphi_{\ell}(\mathfrak{m}_{\square})=\frac{W^{(\ell)}_{1}}{W^{(\ell)}}\mathbb{E}^{(1)}_{\ell}\left(\frac{\varphi_{\ell}(\mathfrak{m}_{\square})}{\#\mathrm{Edges}(\mathfrak{m}_{\square})-1}\right).

(3.5)

Let
\varepsilon>0
. We next distinguish whether the number of edges is larger than
\varepsilon\ell^{3/2}+1
or not. On the one hand, using (
3.3
)
, one can see that

\frac{W^{(\ell)}_{1}}{W^{(\ell)}}\mathbb{E}^{(1)}_{\ell}\left(\frac{\varphi_{\ell}(\mathfrak{m}_{\square})}{\#\mathrm{Edges}(\mathfrak{m}_{\square})-1}{\bf 1}_{\#\mathrm{Edges}(\mathfrak{m}_{\square})\geq\varepsilon\ell^{3/2}+1}\right)\leq\frac{W^{(\ell)}_{1}}{\varepsilon\ell^{3/2}W^{(\ell)}}\mathbb{E}^{(1)}_{\ell}\varphi_{\ell}(\mathfrak{m}_{\square})\mathop{\longrightarrow}\limits_{\ell\to\infty}0.

On the other hand,

\displaystyle\frac{W^{(\ell)}_{1}}{W^{(\ell)}}\mathbb{E}^{(1)}_{\ell}\left(\frac{\varphi_{\ell}(\mathfrak{m}_{\square}){\bf 1}_{\#\mathrm{Edges}(\mathfrak{m}_{\square})\leq\varepsilon\ell^{3/2}}}{\#\mathrm{Edges}(\mathfrak{m}_{\square})-1}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{W^{(\ell)}_{1}}{W^{(\ell)}}\mathbb{E}^{(1)}_{\ell}\left(C\frac{{\bf 1}_{\#\mathrm{Edges}(\mathfrak{m}_{\square})\leq\varepsilon\ell^{3/2}}}{\#\mathrm{Edges}(\mathfrak{m}_{\square})-1}\right)

\displaystyle=C\mathbb{P}\left(\#\mathrm{Edges}\left(\widetilde{\mathfrak{M}}_{\ell}^{(1)}\right)\leq\varepsilon\ell^{3/2}\right)\text{\enskip by (\ref{eq preuve lemme racine}) }

\displaystyle=C\mathbb{P}(\#\mathrm{Edges}(\mathfrak{M}^{(\ell)})\leq\varepsilon\ell^{3/2}-1).

Furthermore,

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\limsup_{\ell\to\infty}\mathbb{P}^{(\ell)}(\#\mathrm{Edges}(\mathfrak{m})\leq\varepsilon\ell^{3/2}-1)=0,

since

converges in distribution towards a positive random variable as

, see e.g. Proposition 10.4 in
[

]
.

4
Scaling limit of the distance to a target

This section and the rest of this paper are devoted to the study of the dual distances on the
3/2
-stable map
\mathfrak{M}^{(\ell)}
of perimeter
2\ell
. We thus henceforth assume that
\bf q
is non-generic critical of type
a=2
. We will drop the subscript
2
in
\Upsilon_{2}
,
\Upsilon^{\uparrow}_{2}
,
\Upsilon^{\downarrow}_{2}
and
\Upsilon^{\downarrow}_{-1,2}
.

As announced before, we first study the distance from the root face to a uniform random edge of
\mathfrak{M}^{(\ell)}
and then transpose the result to the distance to a uniform random face and to a uniform random vertex. From Subsection
3.4
, it suffices to study the distance from the root face
f_{r}
to the target face
\square
under
\mathbb{P}^{(1)}_{\ell}
. Thus, we may use the filled-in exploration of
\mathbb{P}^{(1)}_{\ell}
presented in Section
3
with in particular the peeling algorithms
\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{uniform}}
and
\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{layers}}
.

4.1
The first-passage percolation distance to a uniform random edge in
\mathfrak{M}^{(\ell)}

From Subsection
3.2
, in particular (
3.2
), we know that the fpp distance from the root face
f_{r}
to the target face
\square
under
\mathbb{P}_{\ell}^{(1)}
is

d_{\text{fpp}}^{\dagger}(f_{r},\square)=\sum_{k=0}^{\tau_{-\ell}-1}\frac{\mathcal{E}_{k}}{2P_{\ell}(k)},

(4.1)

where the
\mathcal{E}_{k}
’s are i.i.d. exponential random variables of parameter one independent of the perimeter process.
The properties of the perimeter process proved in Section
2
enable us to state the following proposition, taking us closer to the first convergence of Theorem
1.1
.

Proposition 4.1
.

Under
\mathbb{P}_{\ell}^{(1)}
,

\frac{d_{\mathrm{fpp}}^{\dagger}(f_{r},\square)}{\log\ell}\mathop{\longrightarrow}\limits_{\ell\to\infty}^{(\mathbb{P})}\frac{1}{\pi^{2}p_{\bf q}}.

As a consequence, if
E^{(\ell)}
is a uniform edge in
\mathfrak{M}^{(\ell)}
, then

\frac{d_{\mathrm{fpp}}^{\dagger}(f_{r},E^{(\ell)})}{\log\ell}\mathop{\longrightarrow}\limits_{\ell\to\infty}^{(\mathbb{P})}\frac{1}{\pi^{2}p_{\bf q}}.

Proof.

For the first convergence, it suffices to prove that

\frac{1}{\log\ell}\sum_{k=0}^{\tau_{-\ell}-1}\frac{1}{2P_{\ell}(k)}\mathop{\longrightarrow}\limits_{\ell\to\infty}^{(\mathbb{P})}\frac{1}{\pi^{2}p_{\bf q}}.

(4.2)

Indeed, assuming (
4.2
), like in the beginning of the proof of Proposition 3 of
[
11
]
, we can reason conditionally on
P_{\ell}
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left(\left.\left(\frac{1}{\log\ell}\sum_{k=0}^{\tau_{-\ell}-1}\frac{\mathcal{E}_{k}-1}{2P_{\ell}(k)}\right)^{2}\right|P_{\ell}\right)=\frac{1}{(\log\ell)^{2}}\sum_{k=0}^{\tau_{-\ell}-1}\frac{1}{4P_{\ell}(k)^{2}}\leq\frac{1}{(\log\ell)^{2}}\sum_{k=0}^{\tau_{-\ell}-1}\frac{1}{4P_{\ell}(k)}\mathop{\longrightarrow}\limits_{\ell\to\infty}^{(\mathbb{P})}0,

where the convergence comes from (
4.2
).
In order to prove (
4.2
), one can first see that by Theorem
2.8
, for all
\varepsilon>0
,

\sum_{k=\lfloor\varepsilon\ell\rfloor}^{\tau_{-\ell}-1}\frac{1}{2P_{\ell}(k)}\mathop{\longrightarrow}\limits_{\ell\to\infty}^{(\mathrm{d})}\int_{\varepsilon}^{\tilde{\tau}}\frac{1}{2\Upsilon^{\downarrow}_{-1}(p_{\bf q}t)}dt.

(4.3)

Besides, under the coupling
\mathbb{P}^{(1),\lfloor\varepsilon\ell\rfloor}_{\ell,\infty}
of Lemma
2.5
, on the event
\tau_{-\ell}>\lfloor\varepsilon\ell\rfloor
(which holds with probability greater than
1-o(1)
uniformly in
\ell
as
\varepsilon\to 0
), for all
k\leq\lfloor\varepsilon\ell\rfloor
we have
P_{\ell}(k)=P_{\infty}(k)
. Thus, on the event
\tau_{-\ell}>\lfloor\varepsilon\ell\rfloor
, we have

{1\over\log\ell}\sum_{k=0}^{\lfloor\varepsilon\ell\rfloor}{1\over 2P_{\ell}(k)}={1\over\log\ell}\sum_{k=0}^{\lfloor\varepsilon\ell\rfloor}{1\over 2P_{\infty}(k)}\mathop{\longrightarrow}\limits_{\ell\to\infty}^{(\mathbb{P})}{1\over\pi^{2}p_{\bf q}},

by Equation (15) of
[
11
]
. Consequently, combining this convergence with (
4.3
), we can conclude that (
4.2
) holds, which ends the proof of the first convergence.

The first convergence of the statement is immediately transferred to the map
\mathfrak{M}^{(\ell)}
equipped with a uniform edge by Remark
3.2
and by Lemma
3.3
, using the function
\varphi_{\ell}
defined by

\forall\mathfrak{m}_{\square}\in\mathcal{M}^{(1)}_{\ell},\qquad\varphi_{\ell}(\mathfrak{m}_{\square})=\mathbb{E}\left(\left|\frac{d_{\mathrm{fpp}}^{\dagger}(f_{r},\square)}{\log\ell}-\frac{1}{\pi^{2}p_{\bf q}}\right|\wedge 1\right),

where the expectation applies to the exponential weights defining the fpp distance.
∎

4.2
The dual graph distance to a uniform random edge in
\mathfrak{M}^{(\ell)}

The height process along the filled-in exploration using the peeling by layers algorithm of a map under
\mathbb{P}_{\ell}^{(1)}
is denoted by
H_{\ell}
. We recall that
H_{\ell}(n)
is the smallest height of an edge in
\partial\overline{\mathfrak{e}}_{n}
. From Subsection
3.3
, we know that the dual graph distance from the root face
f_{r}
to the target face
\square
under
\mathbb{P}_{\ell}^{(1)}
is

d_{\mathrm{gr}}^{\dagger}(f_{r},\square)=H_{\ell}(\tau_{-\ell}-1)+1
.

Now we can state and prove the main result of this section, which gives the asymptotic behavior of the dual graph distance from the root face to the target face under
\mathbb{P}^{(1)}_{\ell}
, and brings us closer to the second convergence of Theorem
1.1
by the arguments of Subsection
3.4
.

Proposition 4.2
.

Under
\mathbb{P}^{(1)}_{\ell}
,

{d_{\mathrm{gr}}^{\dagger}(f_{r},\square)\over(\log\ell)^{2}}\mathop{\longrightarrow}\limits_{\ell\to\infty}^{(\mathbb{P})}{1\over 2\pi^{2}}.

As a consequence, if
E^{(\ell)}
is a uniform random edge of
\mathfrak{M}^{(\ell)}
, then, in
\mathfrak{M}^{(\ell)}
,

{d_{\mathrm{gr}}^{\dagger}(f_{r},E^{(\ell)})\over(\log\ell)^{2}}\mathop{\longrightarrow}\limits_{\ell\to\infty}^{(\mathbb{P})}{1\over 2\pi^{2}}.

Proof.

First of all, from Proposition
3.1
, it is easy to see that for all
n\geq 1
, for any increasing sequence of maps with holes
\mathfrak{u}_{0}\subset\mathfrak{u}_{1}\subset\cdots\subset\mathfrak{u}_{n-1}
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\ell}^{(1)}

\displaystyle(\mathfrak{e}_{0}=\mathfrak{u}_{0},\ldots,\mathfrak{e}_{n-1}=\mathfrak{u}_{n-1}|\tau_{-\ell}=n\text{ and }P_{\ell}({n-1})=|\partial\mathfrak{u}_{n-1}|/2)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}_{\infty}^{(1)}(\mathfrak{e}_{0}=\mathfrak{u}_{0},\ldots,\mathfrak{e}_{n-1}=\mathfrak{u}_{n-1}|P_{\infty}(n-1)=|\partial\mathfrak{u}_{n-1}|/2).

As a result, if
F:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is a continuous bounded function,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}^{(1)}_{\ell}F\left(\frac{H_{\ell}(\tau_{-\ell}-1)}{(\log\ell)^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n,k\geq 1}\mathbb{E}_{\infty}^{(1)}\left(\left.F\left(\frac{H_{\infty}(\tau_{-\ell}-1)}{(\log\ell)^{2}}\right)\right|P_{\infty}(n-1)=k\right)\mathbb{P}_{\ell}^{(1)}\left(\tau_{-\ell}=n,P_{\ell}(n-1)=k\right)

We take
F:x\mapsto|x-1/(2\pi^{2})|\wedge 1
. Let
\varepsilon>0
. Let
\delta>0
be small enough so that for all
\ell\geq 1
, under
\mathbb{P}^{(1)}_{\ell}
, we have
\tau_{-\ell}/\ell\in[\delta,1/\delta]
and
P_{\ell}(\tau_{-\ell}-1)/\ell\in[\delta,1/\delta]
with probability at least
1-\varepsilon
. Such a
\delta
exists thanks to Proposition
2.7
.
Then, by Proposition
2.7
and Lemma
2.3
, uniformly on
s,u\in[\delta,1/\delta]
as
\ell\to\infty
,

\displaystyle\frac{\mathbb{P}^{(1)}_{\ell}(\tau_{-\ell}=\lfloor\ell s\rfloor\text{ and }P_{\ell}(\lfloor\ell s\rfloor-1)=\lfloor\ell u\rfloor)}{\mathbb{P}_{\infty}^{(1)}(P_{\infty}(\lfloor\ell s\rfloor-1)=\lfloor\ell u\rfloor)}

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathbb{P}^{(1)}_{\ell}(\tau_{-\ell}=\lfloor\ell s\rfloor\text{ and }P_{\ell}(\lfloor\ell s\rfloor-1)=\lfloor\ell u\rfloor)}{(h^{\uparrow}(\lfloor\ell u\rfloor)/h^{\uparrow}(1))\mathbb{P}_{1}(S_{1},\ldots,S_{\lfloor\ell s\rfloor-1}\geq 1\text{ and }S_{\lfloor\ell s\rfloor-1}=\lfloor\ell u\rfloor)}

\displaystyle\sim\frac{\pi p_{\bf q}}{\ell\sqrt{u}(1+u)^{2}},

where we used that
h^{\uparrow}(\ell)\sim 2\sqrt{\ell/\pi}
. In particular, the above ratio is uniformly bounded in
\ell\geq 1
and
u,s\in[\delta,1/\delta]
by a constant
C(\delta)
times
1/\ell
.
Thus

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}^{(1)}_{\ell}F\left(\frac{H_{\ell}(\tau_{-\ell}-1)}{(\log\ell)^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\varepsilon+C(\delta)\ell^{-1}\int_{\delta}^{1/\delta}ds\int_{\delta}^{1/\delta}du\ell^{2}\mathbb{E}^{(1)}_{\infty}\left(F\left(\frac{H_{\infty}(\lfloor\ell s\rfloor-1)}{(\log\ell)^{2}}\right){\bf 1}_{P_{\infty}(\lfloor\ell s\rfloor-1)=\lfloor\ell u\rfloor}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\varepsilon+C(\delta)\int_{\delta}^{1/\delta}ds\mathbb{E}^{(1)}_{\infty}\left(F\left(\frac{H_{\infty}(\lfloor\ell s\rfloor-1)}{(\log\ell)^{2}}\right)\right).

The last integral tends to
0
as
\ell\to+\infty
because of Proposition 4 from
[
11
]
, stating that under
\mathbb{P}_{\infty}^{(1)}
,

{H_{\infty}(\ell)\over(\log\ell)^{2}}\mathop{\longrightarrow}\limits_{\ell\to\infty}^{(\mathbb{P})}{1\over 2\pi^{2}}.

This entails the first convergence.

The second convergence is a straightforward consequence of the first one due to Remark
3.2
and to Lemma
3.3
, using the function
\varphi_{\ell}
defined by

\forall\mathfrak{m}_{\square}\in\mathcal{M}^{(1)}_{\ell},\qquad\varphi_{\ell}(\mathfrak{m}_{\square})=\left|\frac{d_{\mathrm{gr}}^{\dagger}(f_{r},\square)}{(\log\ell)^{2}}-\frac{1}{2\pi^{2}}\right|\wedge 1.

This concludes the proof.
∎

4.3
Replacing the uniform random edge by a vertex or a face

Let us explain how the results of the preceding subsections also give us the distance from the root face to a uniform random vertex or to a uniform random face, hence proving Theorem
1.1
at the end of this subsection. This subsection relies on classical arguments involving the peeling exploration and bijections with trees which may be skipped by the reader.

We first focus on the case of a uniform random vertex. Notice that
|\mathrm{Vertices}(\mathfrak{M}^{(\ell)})|=\sum_{\vec{e}}{1\over\deg v(\vec{e})}
, where the sum is over all the oriented edges
\vec{e}
of
\mathfrak{M}^{(\ell)}
.
To get a uniform random vertex on
\mathfrak{M}^{(\ell)}
, conditionally on
\mathfrak{M}^{(\ell)}
, we pick a random oriented edge
\vec{E}
of
\mathfrak{M}^{(\ell)}
of law

\forall\vec{e}\in\mathrm{Edges}(\mathfrak{M}^{(\ell)}),\ \ \mathbb{P}\left(\left.\vec{E}=\vec{e}\ \right|\mathfrak{M}^{(\ell)}\right)\coloneqq{1\over\deg v(\vec{e})}{1\over|\mathrm{Vertices}(\mathfrak{M}^{(\ell)})|},

(4.4)

where
v(\vec{e})
is the vertex from which starts
\vec{e}
. Then for all vertex
v
of
\mathfrak{M}^{(\ell)}

\mathbb{P}\left(\left.v(\vec{E})=v\right|\mathfrak{M}^{(\ell)}\right)={1\over|\mathrm{Vertices}(\mathfrak{M}^{(\ell)})|},

(4.5)

i.e.
V\coloneqq v(\vec{E})
is a random vertex taken uniformly on
\mathfrak{M}^{(\ell)}
.

Let us control the degree of
the root vertex
v_{r}
(from which starts the root edge) under
\mathbb{P}^{(1)}_{\ell}
. The lemma below is an extension of Lemma 15.6 from
[
13
]
.

Lemma 4.3
.

There exists
c\in(0,1)
such that for all
\ell,j\geq 1
,

\mathbb{P}^{(1)}_{\ell}(\deg v_{r}\geq j)\leq c^{j-2}.

Proof.

As in
[
13
]
we use the peeling exploration “peeling around the root vertex”, defined as follows: as long as the root vertex belongs to the boundary of the hole
\partial\overline{\mathfrak{e}}_{n}
, we peel the edge on
\partial\overline{\mathfrak{e}}_{n}
adjacent on the left of
v_{r}
until it becomes an interior vertex. More precisely, after an event of type
G
, we fill-in the hole which does not have
v_{r}
on its boundary and we continue the exploration. Thus, the hole may not contain the target after some time. We write
G_{k_{1},k_{2}}
the event of an identification of two edges on the boundary of a hole of perimeter
2p=2k_{1}+2k_{2}+2
which does not contain the target, giving rise to a hole of perimeter
2k_{1}
on the left of the peeled edge (which will be filled-in) and a hole of perimeter
2k_{2}
on the right. This event has the transition probability
W^{(k_{1})}W^{(k_{2})}/W^{(p)}
. The exploration ends when
v_{r}
becomes an inner vertex, i.e. after an event of type
G_{*,0}
or after an event of type
G_{k,0}
for some
k\geq 0
.

At one step of this peeling exploration, let
2p
be the perimeter of the hole whose boundary contains
v_{r}
. We distinguish the two possible cases:

•

Either
v_{r}
is in the boundary of the hole containing the target face. In that case, the exploration stops if and only if the event
G_{*,0}
occurs. And this happens with probability
\frac{1}{2}\nu(-1)\frac{h^{\downarrow}_{\ell}(p-1)}{h^{\downarrow}_{\ell}(p)}
by Proposition
3.1
(and necessarily
p\geq 2
so that the hole which contains the target has positive boundary length).

•

Either
v_{r}
is in the boundary of a hole which does not contain the target face. In this case, the exploration stops when the event
G_{p-1,0}
occurs. This event happens with probability
W^{(p-1)}W^{(0)}/W^{(p)}
.

But one can see by (
1.1
), (
2.1
) and since
h^{\downarrow}(\ell)\sim 1/\sqrt{\pi\ell}
when
\ell\to\infty
that

\frac{W^{(0)}W^{(0)}}{W^{(1)}}\wedge\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}p\geq 2\\
\ell\geq 1\end{subarray}}\left(\frac{1}{2}\nu(-1)\frac{h^{\downarrow}_{\ell}(p-1)}{h^{\downarrow}_{\ell}(p)}\wedge\frac{W^{(p-1)}W^{(0)}}{W^{(p)}}\right)>0.

Besides, in the first case, when
p=1
we automatically have
p\geq 1
at the next step by an event of type
C_{k}
, and with positive probability we will have
p\geq 2
at the next step. Moreover, at each step of the exploration,
\deg(v_{r})
only increases by zero or one, hence the statement of the lemma.
∎

We now recall a lemma which gathers some well known consequences of the Bouttier-Di Francesco-Guitter bijection (from
[
6
]
) and Janson & Stefánsson’s trick (from
[
19
]
Section 3), together with the Łukasiewicz path.

Lemma 4.4
.

(
[
6
]
,
[
19
]
)
The measure
\mu
on
\mathbb{Z}_{\geq-1}
defined by

\mu(-1)=\frac{4}{c_{\bf q}}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\mu(k)=q_{k+1}\binom{2k+1}{k+1}(c_{\bf q}/4)^{k}\text{ for all }k\geq 0.

is a probability measure.
Moreover, to a map
\mathfrak{m}_{\bullet}
under
\mathbb{P}^{(\ell)}_{0}
we can associate a random walk
(Y_{n})_{n\geq 0}
of step distribution
\mu
starting at zero and stopped when it attains
-\ell
such that if we denote by
\tau_{-\ell}(Y)
the time at which
Y
reaches
-\ell
, then

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\#\left\{n\in[\![0,\tau_{-\ell}(Y)-1]\!];\ Y_{n+1}-Y_{n}=-1\right\}+1=\#\mathrm{Vertices}(\mathfrak{m}_{\bullet})\\
\#\left\{n\in[\![0,\tau_{-\ell}(Y)-1]\!];\ Y_{n+1}-Y_{n}\neq-1\right\}+1=\#\mathrm{Faces}(\mathfrak{m}_{\bullet}).\end{array}\right.

As a consequence
\tau_{-\ell}(Y)=\#\mathrm{Edges}(\mathfrak{m}_{\bullet})
.

Using the previous lemmas, we are able to control the degree of a random uniform vertex in the map
\mathfrak{M}^{(\ell)}
. The degree of a uniform random vertex of the map
\mathfrak{M}^{(\ell)}
is stochastically dominated by a random variable whose law does not depend on
\ell
:

Lemma 4.5
.

There exists
C>0
and
c\in(0,1)
such that for all
k,\ell\geq 1
, if
V
is a uniform random vertex of
\mathfrak{M}^{(\ell)}
, then

\mathbb{P}(\deg(V)=k)\leq Cc^{k}.

Proof.

Let
\ell\geq 1
and
\varepsilon>0
. Let
K>0
whose value will be chosen later.
For
k\geq 1
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\deg(V)=k)=

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\deg(V)=k\text{ and }K|\mathrm{Vertices}(\mathfrak{M}^{(\ell)})|\geq|\mathrm{Edges}(\mathfrak{M}^{(\ell)})|)

(4.6)

\displaystyle+\mathbb{P}(\deg(V)=k\text{ and }K|\mathrm{Vertices}(\mathfrak{M}^{(\ell)})|<|\mathrm{Edges}(\mathfrak{M}^{(\ell)})|)

(4.7)

For the first term (
4.6
), if we denote by
\vec{E}^{\prime}
a uniform oriented edge of
\mathfrak{M}^{(\ell)}
, one can see that for all
k\geq 1
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}

\displaystyle\left(\deg(V)=k\text{ and }K|\mathrm{Vertices}(\mathfrak{M}^{(\ell)})|\geq|\mathrm{Edges}(\mathfrak{M}^{(\ell)})|\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left({\bf 1}_{K|\mathrm{Vertices}(\mathfrak{M}^{(\ell)})|\geq|\mathrm{Edges}(\mathfrak{M}^{(\ell)})|}\sum_{\vec{e}}{1\over\deg v(\vec{e})}{1\over|\mathrm{Vertices}(\mathfrak{M}^{(\ell)})|}{\bf 1}_{\deg(v(\vec{e}))=k}\right)\text{ by (\ref{eq sommet uniforme})}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}^{(\ell)}\left({\bf 1}_{K|\mathrm{Vertices}(\mathfrak{m})|\geq|\mathrm{Edges}(\mathfrak{m})|}{1\over k|\mathrm{Vertices}(\mathfrak{m})|}\#\{\vec{e};\ \deg v(\vec{e})=k\}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}^{(\ell)}\left({\bf 1}_{K|\mathrm{Vertices}(\mathfrak{m})|\geq|\mathrm{Edges}(\mathfrak{m})|}{2|\mathrm{Edges}(\mathfrak{m})|\over k|\mathrm{Vertices}(\mathfrak{m})|}\mathbb{P}(\deg(v(\vec{E}^{\prime}))=k|\mathfrak{M}^{(\ell)}=\mathfrak{m})\right)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}^{(\ell)}\left(2K\mathbb{P}(\deg(v(\vec{E}^{\prime}))=k|\mathfrak{M}^{(\ell)}=\mathfrak{m})\right)

\displaystyle=2K\mathbb{P}_{\ell}^{(1)}(\deg(v_{r})=k)

\displaystyle\leq 2Kc^{k-2}\text{ by Lemma \ref{degr\'{e} ar\^{e}te},}

with
C>0
and
c\in(0,1)
independent of
\ell
. We now focus on the second term (
4.7
).

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}

\displaystyle\left(\deg(V)=k\text{ and }K|\mathrm{Vertices}(\mathfrak{M}^{(\ell)})|<|\mathrm{Edges}(\mathfrak{M}^{(\ell)})|\right)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\left(|\mathrm{Edges}(\mathfrak{M}^{(\ell)})|\geq k\text{ and }K|\mathrm{Vertices}(\mathfrak{M}^{(\ell)})|<|\mathrm{Edges}(\mathfrak{M}^{(\ell)})|\right).

Let
Y
be the
\mu
-random walk stopped when it attains
-\ell
associated with
\mathfrak{m}_{\bullet}
under
\mathbb{P}_{0}^{(\ell)}
by Lemma
4.4
. We also know that
\tau_{-\ell}(Y)=|\mathrm{Edges}(\mathfrak{m})|
and
D\coloneqq\#\{i\in[\![0,\tau_{-\ell}(Y)-1]\!];\ Y_{i+1}-Y_{i}=-1\}+1=|\mathrm{Vertices}(\mathfrak{m})|
.
Thus, by biasing by the number of vertices, we obtain that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}

\displaystyle\left(|\mathrm{Edges}(\mathfrak{M}^{(\ell)})|\geq k\text{ and }K|\mathrm{Vertices}(\mathfrak{M}^{(\ell)})|<|\mathrm{Edges}(\mathfrak{M}^{(\ell)})|\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{W^{(\ell)}}\sum_{\mathfrak{m}\in\mathcal{M}^{(\ell)}}w_{\bf q}(\mathfrak{m}){\bf 1}_{|\mathrm{Edges}(\mathfrak{m})|\geq k\text{ and }K|\mathrm{Vertices}(\mathfrak{m})|<|\mathrm{Edges}(\mathfrak{m})|}

\displaystyle=\frac{W^{(\ell)}_{0}}{W^{(\ell)}}\mathbb{E}^{(\ell)}_{0}\left(\frac{1}{|\mathrm{Vertices}(\mathfrak{m_{\bullet}})|}{\bf 1}_{|\mathrm{Edges}(\mathfrak{m}_{\bullet})|\geq k\text{ and }K|\mathrm{Vertices}(\mathfrak{m}_{\bullet})|<|\mathrm{Edges}(\mathfrak{m}_{\bullet})|}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{W^{(\ell)}_{0}}{W^{(\ell)}}\mathbb{E}\left(\frac{1}{D}{\bf 1}_{KD\leq\tau_{-\ell}(Y)\text{ and }\tau_{-\ell}(Y)\geq k}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{W^{(\ell)}_{0}}{W^{(\ell)}}\mathbb{E}\left(\frac{1}{D}\mathbb{P}\left(\left.KD\leq\tau_{-\ell}(Y)\text{ and }\tau_{-\ell}(Y)\geq k\right|D\right)\right).

Let us upper bound the conditional probability inside the expectation. One can note that the random walk
Y
can be built by first sampling the non-zero steps and then inserting between these steps a geometric number (starting at zero, of parameter
1-\mu(0)
) of zero steps (when
\mu(0)=0
, we do not insert any such zero step). Moreover the number of positive steps before
\tau_{-\ell}(Y)
is smaller than
D
. Thus one can write

\tau_{-\ell}(Y)\leq 2D+\sum_{i=1}^{2D}\mathcal{G}_{i},

where the
\mathcal{G}_{i}
are geometric i.i.d. random variables of parameter
1-\mu(0)
, where by convention
\mathcal{G}_{i}=0
when
\mu(0)=0
. Let
\beta=(1-\mu(0))/(2(3-\mu(0)))
so that
1-2\beta=4\beta/(1-\mu(0))
. We will distinguish whether
D\geq\beta k
or not. On the one hand, if
D\leq\beta
, then

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\left.KD\leq\tau_{-\ell}(Y)\text{ and }\tau_{-\ell}(Y)\geq k\right|D\right){\bf 1}_{D\leq\beta k}

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\left(\left.\tau_{-\ell}(Y)\geq k\right|D\right){\bf 1}_{D\leq\beta k}

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\left(\left.2D+\sum_{i=1}^{2D}\mathcal{G}_{i}\geq k\right|D\right){\bf 1}_{D\leq\beta k}

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{2\beta k}\mathcal{G}_{i}\geq(1-2\beta)k\right)

\displaystyle\leq C^{\prime}(c^{\prime})^{k},

for
C^{\prime}>0
,
c^{\prime}\in(0,1)
which do not depend on
\ell
, where the last inequality comes from the large deviations for sums of i.i.d. geometric variables.
On the other hand, when
D\geq\beta k
, by choosing
K\geq 2+4/(1-\mu(0))
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\left.KD\leq\tau_{-\ell}(Y)\text{ and }\tau_{-\ell}(Y)\geq k\right|D\right){\bf 1}_{D\geq\beta k}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left(\left.KD\leq\tau_{-\ell}(Y)\right|D\right){\bf 1}_{D\geq\beta k}

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\left(\left.(K-2)D\leq\sum_{i=1}^{2D}\mathcal{G}_{i}\right|D\right){\bf 1}_{D\geq\beta k}

\displaystyle\leq C^{\prime}(c^{\prime})^{D}{\bf 1}_{D\geq\beta k}\leq C^{\prime}(c^{\prime})^{\beta k},

by the same large deviations. This ends the proof since

\mathbb{E}({1}/{D})=\mathbb{E}^{(\ell)}_{0}({1}/{|\mathrm{Vertices}(\mathfrak{m})|})={W^{(\ell)}}/{W^{(\ell)}_{0}}

and thus

\frac{W^{(\ell)}_{0}}{W^{(\ell)}}\mathbb{E}\left(\frac{1}{D}\mathbb{P}\left(\left.KD\leq\tau_{-\ell}(Y)\text{ and }\tau_{-\ell}(Y)\geq k\right|D\right)\right)\leq C^{\prime}(c^{\prime})^{\beta k},

hence the upper bound of the statement.
∎

If
v
is a vertex of
\mathfrak{m}
, and if
d^{\dagger}
is a distance on
\mathfrak{m}^{\dagger}
, we define
d^{\dagger}(f_{r},v)
as the smallest distance from
f_{r}
to a face
f
next to
v
.

Proposition 4.6
.

If for all
\ell\geq 1
,
V^{(\ell)}
is a random uniform vertex of
\mathfrak{M}^{(\ell)}
, then

\frac{d_{\mathrm{gr}}^{\dagger}(f_{r},V^{(\ell)})}{(\log\ell)^{2}}\mathop{\longrightarrow}\limits_{\ell\to\infty}^{(\mathbb{P})}\frac{1}{2\pi^{2}}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\frac{d_{\mathrm{fpp}}^{\dagger}(f_{r},V^{(\ell)})}{\log\ell}\mathop{\longrightarrow}\limits_{\ell\to\infty}^{(\mathbb{P})}\frac{1}{\pi^{2}p_{\bf q}}.

Proof.

We do the proof for the dual graph distance (the same ideas work for the fpp distance using Proposition
4.1
). First of all, we can work with
d_{\mathrm{gr}}^{\dagger}(f_{r},\vec{E})
with

6
Diameter of
\mathfrak{M}^{(\ell,\dagger)}

The main purpose of this section is to give bounds on the diameter of
\mathfrak{M}^{(\ell,\dagger)}
for the graph distance and for the fpp distance, proving Theorem
1.2
. The upper bounds are achieved using a first moment method together with large deviations on the height of a uniform random edge, obtained using supermartingales. Two of these supermartingales (of Lemmas
6.2
and
6.9
) are related to the martingale
(M_{n}^{(\ell)})_{n\geq 0}
defined using the perimeter process
P_{\ell}
under
\mathbb{P}^{(1)}_{\ell}
as follows:

\forall n\geq 0,\qquad M_{n}^{(\ell)}=\frac{1}{h^{\downarrow}_{\ell}(P_{\ell}(n))}\prod_{j=0}^{n-1}\left(\frac{1}{\nu([-P_{\ell}(j)+1,\infty))+\nu(-P_{\ell}(j)-\ell)}\vee{\bf 1}_{P_{\ell}(j)=-\ell}\right).

(6.1)

It is indeed a martingale since for all
n\geq 0
, if
P_{\ell}(n)\geq 1
, then

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}^{(1)}_{\ell}\left(\left.M_{n+1}^{(\ell)}\right|\mathcal{F}_{n}\right)=

\displaystyle\frac{h^{\downarrow}_{\ell}(-\ell)}{h^{\downarrow}_{\ell}(P_{\ell}(n))}\nu(-P_{\ell}(n)-\ell)\frac{1}{h^{\downarrow}_{\ell}(-\ell)}\prod_{j=0}^{n}\frac{1}{\nu([-P_{\ell}(j)+1,\infty))+\nu(-P_{\ell}(j)-\ell)}

\displaystyle+\sum_{k\geq 1-P_{\ell}(n)}\frac{h^{\downarrow}_{\ell}(P_{\ell}(n)+k)}{h^{\downarrow}_{\ell}(P_{\ell}(n))}\nu(k)\frac{1}{h^{\downarrow}_{\ell}(P_{\ell}(n)+k)}\prod_{j=0}^{n}\frac{1}{\nu([-P_{\ell}(j)+1,\infty))+\nu(-P_{\ell}(j)-\ell)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle M^{(\ell)}_{n},

while the indicator
{\bf 1}_{P_{\ell}(j)=-\ell}
enables to keep a martingale after
P_{\ell}
is absorbed at
-\ell
. This martingale is particularly well suited to the study of the distances on
3/2
-stable maps owing to the asymptotic behavior (
1.3
) of the step distribution
\nu
: intuitively, since
\nu((-\infty,-P_{\ell}(n)))\approx p_{\bf q}/P_{\ell}(n)
, the above product can be approximated by the exponential of
\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}1/P_{\ell}(j)
(times an appropriate constant) and can thus be related to the fpp distance (
4.1
) via a concentration inequality.

One notable fact is that our bounds show that the diameter under the fpp distance heavily depends on the tails of
\nu
, but also on the non-asymptotic behavior of
\nu
. Hence, even though the fpp diameter and the distance between uniform points are of the same order, the ratio does not converge to a universal constant. On the contrary, in the case of the dual graph distance, our upper bound for the diameter is universal, echoing the universal scaling limit of the height of a random uniform face in Theorem
1.1
.

6.1
Upper bound of the diameter for the fpp distance

In this subsection, we upper bound the diameter of
\mathfrak{M}^{(\ell,\dagger)}
for the first-passage percolation distance. Let

Lemma 6.2

Let

If

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\ell}^{(1)}\left(\left.M_{n+1}^{\lambda,\ell}\right|\mathcal{F}_{n}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k>-P_{\ell}(n)\\
\text{ or }k=-P_{\ell}(n)-\ell\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{h^{\downarrow}_{\ell}(P_{\ell}(n)+k)}\exp\left({\lambda\sum_{j=0}^{n}\frac{1}{P_{\ell}(j)}{\bf 1}_{P_{\ell}(j)>0}}\right)\frac{h^{\downarrow}_{\ell}(P_{\ell}(n)+k)}{h^{\downarrow}_{\ell}(P_{\ell}(n))}\nu(k)

\displaystyle=M_{n}^{\lambda,\ell}e^{\lambda\frac{1}{P_{\ell}(n)}{\bf 1}_{P_{\ell}(n)>0}}\left(\left(\sum_{k>-P_{\ell}(n)}\nu(k)\right)+\nu(-P_{\ell}(n)-\ell)\right)

The above supermartingales lead to a large deviation inequality for the distance from the root to a uniform random edge.

6.2

A lower bound for the diameter is already provided by Corollary

Proposition 6.4

For all
\delta>0

\frac{\mathrm{diam}_{\mathrm{fpp}}^{\dagger}(\mathfrak{M}^{(\ell)})}{\log\ell}\geq\frac{3}{4(1-q_{1}^{2})}-\delta.

6.3
Upper bound of the diameter for the graph distance

We next upper bound the diameter of
\mathfrak{M}^{(\ell,\dagger)}
for the graph distance. The proposition below implies the second upper bound of Theorem

. Unlike the case of the fpp distance, the upper bound for the graph distance is universal (i.e. it does not depend on

Proposition 6.5
.

Let
\mathrm{Diam}_{\mathrm{gr}}^{\dagger}(\mathfrak{M}^{(\ell)})
be the diameter of
\mathfrak{M}^{(\ell,\dagger)}
for the graph distance. Then for all

when

To prove this result, we will need to use the height process

Let us first recall the definition of the interpolated height process from

where by convention, we set

Lemma 6.6
.

Let

. If

where
