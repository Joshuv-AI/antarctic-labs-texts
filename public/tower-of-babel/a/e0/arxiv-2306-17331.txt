Computation of random time-shift distributions for stochastic population models
Dylan Morris, John Maclean, Andrew J. Black

Abstract
Even in large systems, the effect of noise arising from when populations are initially small can persist to be measurable on the macroscale. A deterministic approximation to a stochastic model will fail to capture this effect, but it can be accurately approximated by including an additional random time-shift to the initial conditions. We present a efficient numerical method to compute this time-shift distribution for a large class of stochastic models. The method relies on differentiation of certain functional equations, which we show can be effectively automated by deriving rules for different types of model rates that arise commonly when mass-action mixing is assumed. Explicit computation of the time-shift distribution can be used to build a practical tool for the efficient generation of macroscopic trajectories of stochastic population models, without the need for costly stochastic simulations. Full code is provided to implement this and we demonstrate our method on an epidemic model and a model of within-host viral dynamics.

Introduction

The choice of a stochastic or deterministic model for problems in mathematical biology is not always simple. Deterministic models are in general much cheaper to solve, and often the most appropriate if the scale of the system is large, but stochastic effects can still be important on the macroscale
(
Black et al. 2009
;
Butler and
Goldenfeld 2011
;
Rogers et al. 2012
;
Black and McKane 2012
)
.
Examples that motivate this work are populations that initially start from small numbers (cells, virons, invading species, infected individuals, etc.).
Due to the random nature of the events, these populations initially go through a period of noisy dynamics (and possible extinction), before entering an exponential growth phase
(
Black et al. 2014
)
.
The effect of this early time noise is not averaged out in the large system but instead persists on the macroscale
(
Baker et al. 2018
)
.
For populations that grow to a maximum before declining, such as seen in a susceptible-infected-recovered (SIR) model, this noise manifests as variability in the time for the population to peak
(
Nitschke et al. 2022
;
Curran-Sebastian et al. 2023
)
.
A deterministic approximation to the full stochastic process can accurately capture the large scale dynamics (the early growth rate and shape of the curves at the peak), but fails to capture the variability in the time to peak.

Recent work has shown that the effect of this type of stochasticity on macroscopic population dynamics can be captured by a single univariate random variable representing a time shift applied to the initial conditions of a deterministic approximation to the full stochastic system
(
Barbour et al. 2015
;
Baker et al. 2018
)
.
Although
Barbour et al. 2015
presents the analytical theory for these time shifts distributions and when they are valid, they do not present a method to actually calculate the distributions for general models. In this paper we fill this gap by showing how to numerically compute the time shift distributions for a broad class of continuous- and discrete-time Markov chain models. Our work therefore greatly expands the applicability of these random time-shift ideas for applied modelling.

The key to calculating the time-shift distribution comes from a branching process (BP) approximation to the full model that is valid at early times.
The BP approximation can accurately quantify the variability in the population numbers due to the initial stochasticity before the exponential growth phase is entered.
In the long time limit, a suitably re-scaled version of this process tends to a limiting distribution, often denoted
W
in the literature
(
Athreya and Ney 1972
;
Mode 1971
;
Harris 1964
)
.
Matching the solution of the BP with a linearised version of a deterministic approximation to the same system shows that the time-shift distribution and
W
are related via a simple transformation
(
Barbour et al. 2015
)
.
Although analytic calculation of
W
for a few simple models is possible
(
Harris 1948
;
Harris 1951
;
Kimmel and Axelrod 2015
)
, in general this appears to be a hard problem, especially for multivariate models.
Hence we instead develop an efficient numerical scheme to evaluate
W
.
This is based on a Taylor series expansion of the Laplace-Stieltjes transform (LST) of the distribution, the terms of which can be calculated via differentiation of an implicit functional equation.
Similar to the operation of automatic differentiation routines
(
Bartholomew-Biggs et al. 2000
)
, we show that this procedure can be encoded as a series of rules for a given model structure. These insights lead to an efficient algorithm and we provide a numerical package
1
1

1

add link to github repo
to automate this computation for a given model.
We also present another approximate, but even faster, method that employs moment matching, whereby the analytical moments of a surrogate distribution are matched to those of
W
.

Time-shift distributions are useful in themselves for quantifying the role of early stochasticity in population models, but also suggest an elegant and fast method for the generation of macroscopic solutions that also capture this effect.
This simulation method requires a single solution of the deterministic approximation (typically found by solving a set of coupled ordinary differential equations) that is then replicated many times, and each replicate then shifted in time by a sample from the univariate time-shift distribution.
This simulation approach can be considered as a type of hybrid simulation method
(
Rebuli et al. 2017
;
Kreger et al. 2021
)
, but with a greatly reduced computational cost as only one deterministic trajectory needs to be simulated.
The reduced computational cost is particularly useful for applications such as Bayesian inference
(
Wilkinson 2019
;
Kreger et al. 2021
)
, where the use of exact
(
Gillespie 1977
)
, or even approximate
(
Gillespie 2001
)
, stochastic simulation methods can become computationally expensive for large systems.
Moreover, the time-shift distribution can also be used as an importance sampling distribution for even more efficient sequential inference algorithms
(
Kroese et al. 2011
;
Black 2018
)
.

In the next section we present an example to illustrate the basic idea of approximating the macroscopic stochastic dynamics of a model with a deterministic model subject to random initial conditions.
We then present our general method for computing
W
, and hence the time shift distribution, in Section
3
.

The distribution of this time-shift can be found by equating of two early time approximations of the model: a branching process approximation and a linearised deterministic approximation for the mean. Our major contribution in the manuscript is to provide a method to compute this distribution for a general class of CTMC models, described in
Section

3
.

2.1
Early time approximations to the SIR model

The analysis begins by considering the early-time approximation of the SIR model when
N
is large and
S(0)\approx N
, meaning susceptible depletion can be ignored, and the rate of infection is approximately linear, i.e.
\beta mn/(N-1)\approx\beta n
.
Hence, as the rate of recovery is already linear, the number of infected individuals can be approximated with a continuous-time branching process
(
Dorman et al. 2004
)
.
Continuous-time branching processes are defined in more detail in
Section

3.1
.
The number of individuals infected at time
t
is then given by the random variable
I_{b}(t)
.

We next consider two approximations to the branching process dynamics. For the first, define

W(t)=e^{-\lambda t}I_{b}(t)\;,

(2)

a rescaled branching process with initial condition
W(0)=I_{0}
. The parameter
\lambda
is the so-called Malthusian parameter or early growth rate
(
Dorman et al. 2004
)
, and for the SIR model is simply
\lambda=\beta-\gamma

(
Allen 2017
)
.
The process
W(t)
is well studied in the literature: it is a martingale and converges to a random variable
W
almost surely
(
Barbour et al. 2015
;
Athreya and Ney 1972
, e.g)
. Let us pause to interpret the limiting behaviour when the time is long enough such that
W(t)\approx W
. In this limit, the number of infected
I_{b}(t)\approx e^{\lambda t}W
only depends on
t
in the exponent, and hence the dynamics have entered the exponential growth phase.
Substituting this approximation in
Eq.

2
and rearranging, we have,

I_{b}(t)\approx\exp{\left(\lambda\left(t+\lambda^{-1}\log{W}\right)\right)}.

(3)

In this equation,
W
has been placed in the exponent so that we can in future interpret it as a shift to the time
t
.

A second approximation to the full BP is to just consider the mean number of infected,
I_{d}(t)=\mathds{E}[I_{b}(t)]=I_{0}e^{\lambda t}

(
Athreya and Ney 1972
)
.
This can be expressed similarly to
Eq.

3

I_{d}(t)=\exp{\left(\lambda\left(t+\lambda^{-1}\log{\mathds{E}[W]}\right)\right)}

(4)

where
\mathds{E}[W]=I_{0}
since
W(t)
forms a martingale
(
Barbour et al. 2015
;
Athreya and Ney 1972
)
.
The two approximations for the dynamics look similar, and clearly capture the observed exponential growth in this model, but are different. The approximation for
I_{b}(t)
is stochastic as
W
is a random variable,
I_{d}(t)
is deterministic.

2.2
Random time-shifts

As alluded to above, the form of
Eqs.

3
and
4
are deliberate and suggest an elegant way to understand the effect of the early time stochasticity on the longer time mean dynamics.
Equating the two solutions we see that the process
I_{b}(t)
can be approximated by
I_{d}(t+\tau)
, where

\tau=\lambda^{-1}(\log W-\log\mathds{E}[W]).

(5)

The two solutions are identical up to a random time shift of the initial conditions of the deterministic mean solution.
The main panel of
Fig.

2
illustrates this concept. The green lines show full stochastic realisations and the orange dashed lines indicate the corresponding shifted mean solutions.

The above analysis shows that the times-shift is a simple transformation of the random variable
W
.
As the early time approximation of the SIR model is a one-dimensional, linear, birth-death process, the variable
W
can be derived analytically and hence so can the time shift distribution. We cover the main points here; a full derivation is given in
Appendix

A
. The distribution of
W
conditional on the event of non-extinction is exponentially distributed with rate
1-\gamma/\beta

(
Harris 1951
)
.
Making the transformation (
Eq.

5
), we can easily obtain the probability distribution function for the time-shift
\tau
,

g_{\tau^{\star}}(t)=\lambda\left(1-\frac{\gamma}{\beta}\right)e^{\lambda t-\left(1-\frac{\gamma}{\beta}\right)e^{\lambda t}},\quad t\in\mathbb{R}.

(6)

2.3
Macroscopic dynamics

The above discussion shows how the stochastic early time dynamics can be approximated by a deterministic solution for the mean subject to random initial conditions. As we saw in the discussion of the model dynamics, this noise persists on the macroscale as well.
Theorem 1.1 of
Barbour et al. 2015
, shows how the above time-shift analysis can be extended to the full non-linear deterministic approximation for the density process given in
Eq.

1
.
This states that given a CTMC with state vector
\boldsymbol{X}(t)
, that is well approximated by a branching process near the initial condition and has a deterministic approximation,
\boldsymbol{\zeta}(t)
, then the process
\boldsymbol{\zeta}(t+\tau)
with
\tau
given by
Eq.

5
also approximates
\boldsymbol{X}(t)
.
In our example here,
\boldsymbol{\zeta}(t)=N\boldsymbol{x}(t)
, where
\boldsymbol{x}(t)=(s(t),i(t))
.

Thus the time shift distribution has predictive power on the macroscale as well. For example,
Fig.

1
.B shows that we can predict the timing of peak infections as follows.
Obtain a deterministic prediction for the timing of the peak number of infections,
t_{p}
, by either solving
Eq.

1
numerically or employing an analytic approximation
(
Turkyilmazoglu 2021
)
.
We can then determine the distribution on the peak timing from simply adding
t_{p}+\tau
.

3
Methods

This section details the theoretical foundations for estimating the distribution of
W
and subsequently the distribution of the random time-shifts.
We assume a branching process model is being used directly or has been derived from a CTMC model as was done for the SIR example in
Section

2
.

If a CTMC is the starting point, then the requirements for this analysis to be applicable are as follows:
The system has a natural scaling parameter
K
(e.g. the total population or carrying capacity), such that one may consider how the dynamics scale in the limit
K\rightarrow\infty
. Typically this parameter is referred to as the system size and one talks about the dynamics in the deterministic limit
(
Black and McKane 2012
)
.
For this limit to actually exist, it is required that the rates of events can be written in a density dependent form
(
Kurtz 1970
;
Kurtz 1976
;
Barbour 1980
)
.
This means that in the limit that
K\rightarrow\infty
, the density follows a set of ODEs (for example see
Eq.

1
for the SIR model, and
Appendix

E
for the innate response model). Finally, we require that the rates of the CTMC are approximately linear near the initial condition so a BP approximation to the CTMC can be constructed
(
Barbour et al. 2015
;
Allen and Lahodny 2012
;
Allen 2017
)
.
Many population models are naturally formulated such that all these conditions are simultaneously satisfied and hence the methodology is suitable
(
Barbour 1980
;
Schuster 2016
;
Wilkinson 2019
)
.

The three conditions above guarantee that we can approximate the early-time dynamics by a branching process, which can be
matched
with a deterministic approximation in the same manner as in
Sections

2.1
and
2.2
. This matching can be done since the linearised (about the unstable equilibria) deterministic system produces an equivalent system of differential equations to the mean of the branching process. The resulting time shift distribution is guaranteed by
Barbour et al. 2015
to accurately characterise the
difference
between the true stochastic process and the limiting (large
K
) deterministic system.

The rest of this section is as follows: In
Section

3.1
we detail the branching process theory required for defining
W
in the multivariate, continuous-time, case.

Section

3.2
details our method for computing the the LST of
W
using a moment expansion and a suitably formulated imbedded process.

Section

3.3
shows how the conditional moments required for the calculation of the LST can be found using a recursive approach.
Section

3.4
shows how the method can be applied to discrete time models and
Section

3.5
details a simpler, but approximate, approach for estimating the distribution of
W
by fitting a generalised gamma distribution to its first five moments.

3.1
Branching process theory

A branching process models the evolution of particles, or agents, that each live for a particular lifetime at which point they die and reproduce according to predefined rules
(
Athreya and Ney 1972
;
Dorman et al. 2004
;
Kimmel and Axelrod 2015
)
.
The particular property that makes a branching process amenable to analysis is that, once created, each individual is assumed to evolve independently of all others.
We only consider Markovian branching processes where the lifetime for each type of individual is assumed be exponentially distributed, which we herein refer to as a continuous-time multi-type branching process (CT-MBP)
(
Dorman et al. 2004
)
.

Formulated mathematically, a CT-MBP
\{\boldsymbol{Z}(t),t\geq 0\}
is defined on a state space
\mathcal{S}\subseteq\mathbb{N}_{0}^{m}
where
Z_{i}(t)
is the number of individuals of type
i=1,\dots,m
, at time
t
.
Each type lives for an exponentially distributed amount of time with mean
1/a_{i}
, and upon death they create a number of offspring with
p_{i}(\boldsymbol{k})
the probability of individual
i
having
\boldsymbol{k}=(k_{1},\dots,k_{m})
offspring of each type.
This information is conveniently summarised in the progeny generating functions

f_{i}(\boldsymbol{s})=\sum_{\boldsymbol{k}}p_{i}(\boldsymbol{k})\prod_{j=1}^{m}s_{j}^{k_{j}},\quad\boldsymbol{s}\in[0,1]^{m}.

(7)

A CT-MBP is therefore fully specified by the rates
a_{i}
and the probabilities
p_{i}(\boldsymbol{k})
.

The dynamics of this model can be largely analysed through a matrix
\Omega
with elements
(
Athreya and Ney 1972
;
Dorman et al. 2004
)

\Omega_{ij}=a_{i}\left(\mathinner{\mathinner{\dfrac{\partial{}f_{i}(\boldsymbol{s})}{\partial{s_{j}}}}\Biggr\rvert}_{\boldsymbol{s}=1}-\delta_{ij}\right),

where
\delta_{ij}
is the Kronecker delta.
In particular, defining
\boldsymbol{\zeta}(t)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\mathds{E}[\boldsymbol{Z}(t)]
, we can characterise the mean behaviour of the branching process, which can be considered a deterministic approximation since
\boldsymbol{\zeta}(t)
satisfies the following system of ordinary differential equations
(
Athreya and Ney 1972
)

\mathinner{\dfrac{\dif{}}{\dif{t}}}\boldsymbol{\zeta}(t)=\Omega\boldsymbol{\zeta}(t).

(8)

The solution of
Eq.

8
is given by the matrix exponential (assuming an initial condition
\boldsymbol{\zeta}(0)=\boldsymbol{z}_{0}
)

\boldsymbol{\zeta}(t)=e^{\Omega t}\boldsymbol{z}_{0}.

(9)

By Theorem 2.7 of
Seneta 1981
and the Perron-Frobenius theorem
(
Athreya and Ney 1972
;
Barbour et al. 2015
)
, in the limit as
t\to\infty
then
e^{\Omega^{T}t}\approx e^{\lambda t}\boldsymbol{v}\boldsymbol{u}^{T}
where
\boldsymbol{u}
and
\boldsymbol{v}^{T}
are the right and left eigenvectors of
\Omega
, corresponding to the dominant eigenvalue
\lambda
, normalised such that
\boldsymbol{u}\cdot\boldsymbol{1}=1
and
\boldsymbol{u}\cdot\boldsymbol{v}=1

(
Athreya and Ney 1972
;
Dorman et al. 2004
;
Harris 1964
)
.
In the remainder of this section we restrict ourselves to the
super-critical
regime which occurs when
\lambda>0
.
Hence, an approximate solution to
Eq.

9
valid at long times (but not so long that the BP approximation has broken down) is given by

\boldsymbol{\zeta}(t)\approx e^{\lambda t}\boldsymbol{v}\boldsymbol{u}^{T}\boldsymbol{z}_{0}.

(10)

From these quantities a rescaled branching process can also be defined,

\boldsymbol{W}(t)=e^{-\lambda t}\boldsymbol{Z}(t),

(11)

which is a non-negative Martingale with
\lim_{t\to\infty}\boldsymbol{W}(t)=W\boldsymbol{v}

almost surely
(
Athreya and Ney 1972
, Chapter V.7, Theorem 2)
.

This martingale is the main tool to understand how the mean dynamics differs from the the stochastic realisations.
For a long enough time such that
\boldsymbol{W}(t)
has converged, we can make the substitution
W\boldsymbol{v}
for
\boldsymbol{W}(t)
in
Eq.

11
and rearrange to get
\boldsymbol{Z}(t)\approx e^{\lambda t}W\boldsymbol{v}
which can be expressed equivalently as
(
Barbour et al. 2015
)

\boldsymbol{Z}(t)\approx\exp{\left(\lambda\left(t+\lambda^{-1}\log W\right)\right)}\boldsymbol{v}.

The deterministic approximation for the mean
Eq.

9
can be written as
\boldsymbol{\zeta}(t)\approx e^{\lambda t}\mathds{E}[W]\boldsymbol{v}
where
\mathds{E}[W]=\boldsymbol{z}_{0}^{T}\boldsymbol{u}
and can be similarly expressed as

\boldsymbol{\zeta}(t)\approx\exp{\left(\lambda\left(t+\lambda^{-1}\log\mathds{E}[W]\right)\right)}\boldsymbol{v}.

The two processes are identical up to the random time delay of
(
Barbour et al. 2015
)

\tau=\lambda^{-1}(\log W-\log\mathds{E}[W]).

(12)

The time-shift is a simple transformation of
W
and thus the main part of our method is concerned with computing
W
itself.

We decompose the process
\boldsymbol{W}(t)
into multiple pieces, as our later branching process calculations require that the initial condition is a single individual of type
i
.
Hence we define
\boldsymbol{W}_{i}(t)
as the random variable
\boldsymbol{W}(t)
conditional on starting from a single individual of type
i
.
Then
Athreya and Ney 1972
show that

\lim_{t\to\infty}\boldsymbol{W}_{i}(t)=W_{i}\boldsymbol{v}.

almost surely and
\mathds{E}[W_{i}]=u_{i}
.
We will compute the
W_{i}
directly in our method, but then we can also compute
W
for an arbitrary initial condition,
\boldsymbol{Z}(0)=\boldsymbol{z}_{0}
, as follows.
By the independence of the agents in the branching process,
\boldsymbol{Z}(t)
can be expressed in terms of independent sub-processes
\boldsymbol{Z}_{i}^{(j)}
that are each started from a single individual of type
i
for each of the
j=1,\dots,z_{0,i}
initial individuals,

\boldsymbol{Z}(t)=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sum_{j=1}^{z_{0,i}}\boldsymbol{Z}_{i}^{(j)}(t).

(13)

Multiplying
Eq.

13
by
e^{-\lambda t}
and taking the limit as
t\to\infty
results in

W=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sum_{j=1}^{z_{0,i}}W_{i}^{(j)}

(14)

which follows from Chapter V.7, Theorem 2 of
Athreya and Ney 1972
and that the left eigenvector
\boldsymbol{v}
is constant.

In order to compute the distribution of
W
we work with the Laplace-Stieltjes transforms of the
W_{i}
defined as

\phi_{i}(\theta)=\mathds{E}[e^{-\theta W_{i}}],\quad\theta\in\mathbb{C},

(15)

and we define the vector
\boldsymbol{\varphi}(\theta)=(\phi_{1}(\theta),\dots,\phi_{m}(\theta))
.
Since the random variables
W_{i}^{(j)}
are independent and identically distributed copies of the
W_{i}
, the LST of
W
is simply

\phi(\theta)=\prod_{i=1}^{m}\phi_{i}(\theta)^{z_{0,i}}.

(16)

3.2
Computation of the LST of
W_{i}

To compute the distribution of
W
we will derive approximations for
\phi_{i}
, then
Eq.

16
can be inverted using standard methods.

Since
\lambda>0
, a Taylor series expansion of the term
e^{-\theta W_{i}}
about
0
in
Eq.

15
yields an approximation to the LST in terms of the first
n
moments of
W_{i}

\hat{\phi}_{i}(\theta)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\frac{(-\theta)^{k}}{k!}\mathds{E}[W_{i}^{k}].

(17)

The calculation of the moments,
\mathds{E}[W_{i}^{k}]
, can be done by recursively solving sets of linear equations and is discussed in
Section

3.3
.
Simply evaluating
Eq.

17
at
\theta\in\mathbb{C}
will result in a poor approximation as
\theta
becomes further away from
0
due to the error term in the Taylor series
(
Hubbard and Hubbard 1999
)
.
By Lagrange’s remainder theorem, and defining
\gamma=\max_{i}\{\mathds{E}[W_{i}^{n+1}],i=1,\dots,m\}
,
\theta
in the region

\mathcal{A}_{\epsilon}=\left\{\theta\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathinner{\!\left\lvert\theta\right\rvert}\leq\left(\frac{(n+1)!\epsilon}{\gamma}\right)^{1/(n+1)}\right\}

(18)

ensures that the error term is no more than some pre-defined tolerance
\epsilon
(see
Appendix

B
for derivation).
The denominator ensures that we simultaneously satisfy the error tolerance for all the LSTs.
This provides a bound on the size of the open neighbourhood about
0
where the approximation has its error controlled to arbitrary levels of precision.

In order to extend the region where the approximation
Eq.

17
is accurate from
\mathcal{A}_{\epsilon}
to all of
\mathbb{C}
, we consider the construction of an imbedded discrete-time branching process (DT-MBP).
Before we give the full details of the approach we provide insight into why this approach was the one taken.
The construction of the imbedded DT-MBP means we can formulate another rescaled process (similar to
Eq.

11
) which has the same limit,
W\boldsymbol{v}
, as the original CT-MBP
(
Athreya and Ney 1972
;
Doob 1940
)
.
With a discrete-time BP the LSTs of the
W_{i}
can be shown to satisfy a simple functional equation that relates the progeny generating function (of the imbedded process) and the LSTs.
This functional equation provides a method for
shrinking
(we provide detail for what this means shortly) the value of
\theta
in the cases where
\theta\notin\mathcal{A}_{\epsilon}
.
From the functional equation we can derive a simple recursive algorithm for evaluating the LSTs at
\theta\in\mathbb{C}
.

The detailed realisation of this approach begins by constructing the imbedded DT-MBP of
\boldsymbol{Z}(t)
which is defined, for some
h>0
, as
\boldsymbol{Z}^{\prime}(n)=\boldsymbol{Z}(hn)
with
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}

(
Athreya and Ney 1972
)
.
The progeny generating functions for the imbedded process can be calculated from the generating functions of the continuous time process.
Defining

F_{i}(\boldsymbol{s},t)=\mathds{E}\left[\prod_{j=1}^{m}s_{j}^{Z_{j}(t)}\,\big|\,\boldsymbol{Z}(0)=\boldsymbol{e}_{i}\right],\quad i=1,\dots,m

where
\boldsymbol{e}_{i}
is a standard basis vector in
\mathbb{R}^{M}
, then
F_{i}(\boldsymbol{s},t)
satisfies the system of differential equations
(
Allen 2015
;
Athreya and Ney 1972
)
,

\mathinner{\dfrac{\partial{}F_{i}(\boldsymbol{s},t)}{\partial{t}}}=a_{i}\left(f_{i}(F_{i}(\boldsymbol{s},t))-F_{i}(\boldsymbol{s},t)\right),\quad i=1,\dots,m,

(19)

with initial conditions
F_{i}(\boldsymbol{s},0)=s_{i}
.
Then the progeny generating function of an individual of type
i
in the imbedded process is then simply
\tilde{f}_{i}(\boldsymbol{s})=F_{i}(\boldsymbol{s},h)
.

Once more we condition on the process starting with a single individual of type
i
and define a rescaled version of the imbedded chain

\boldsymbol{W}_{i}^{\prime}(n)=e^{-\lambda hn}\boldsymbol{Z}_{i}^{\prime}(n),\quad n\in\mathbb{N}_{0},\quad i=1,\dots,m.

Crucially both the imbedded process,
\boldsymbol{W}_{i}^{\prime}(n)
, and original continuous time process,
\boldsymbol{W}_{i}(t)
, converge to the same limit,
W_{i}\boldsymbol{v}
for all
i=1,\dots,m

(
Doob 1940
;
Athreya and Ney 1972
)
.
Furthermore, we have the following functional equation for

\boldsymbol{\varphi}(\theta)

(
Athreya and Ney 1972
;
Harris 1964
;
Mode 1971
)
,

\boldsymbol{\varphi}(\theta)=\boldsymbol{\tilde{f}}\left(\boldsymbol{\varphi}\left(\theta e^{-\lambda h}\right)\right).

(20)

This provides a way of accurately evaluating the approximation
\boldsymbol{\hat{\varphi}}(\theta)
for all
\theta\in\mathbb{C}
. If
\theta\in\mathcal{A}_{\epsilon}
then we simply evaluate
Eq.

17
.
If
\theta\notin\mathcal{A}_{\epsilon}
then we can recursively evaluate
Eq.

20

\kappa
times until
\theta e^{-\lambda h\kappa}\in\mathcal{A}_{\epsilon}
.
This recursive calculation is equivalent to calculating

\boldsymbol{\varphi}(\theta)=\underbrace{\boldsymbol{\tilde{f}}\circ\boldsymbol{\tilde{f}}\circ\dots\circ\boldsymbol{\tilde{f}}}_{\kappa\textrm{ times}}\left(\boldsymbol{\varphi}\left(\theta e^{-\lambda\kappa h}\right)\right).

The value of
\kappa
can be chosen ahead of time for a particular value of
L
where
L
is the bound given in
Eq.

18
as

\kappa\geq-\frac{1}{\lambda h}\log{\left(\frac{L}{\mathinner{\!\left\lvert\theta\right\rvert}}\right)}.

(21)

The two hyperparameters are the number of moments used in the LST expansions,

With an approximation of the LST in hand, we can use numerical inversion techniques to obtain the distribution of

.
Let

The distribution of

of size
q^{\star}
corresponding to the probability of ultimate extinction
(
Harris 1964
;
Athreya and Ney 1972
)
.
This probability can be calculated through analysis of the unscaled branching process as
\boldsymbol{W}(t)\to\boldsymbol{0}\iff\boldsymbol{Z}(t)\to\boldsymbol{0}
(since
e^{-\lambda t}>0,\,\forall t\geq 0
).
Let
q_{i}

\boldsymbol{q}=(q_{1},\dots,q_{m})

The vector

(

;

)

(22)

By the independence assumption of the particles in the branching process the probability of ultimate extinction for a given initial conditions is simply

q^{\star}=\prod_{i=1}^{m}q_{i}^{z_{0,i}},

where

is the initial number of individuals of type
i
.
Note that the point mass in the distribution of
W
at
W=0
manifests as an infinite time-shift (i.e. the BP hitting the absorbing state
\boldsymbol{0}
).

Instead of working with the LST of
G_{W}(w)
directly, typically inversion methods target the complementary CDF (CCDF)
G_{W}^{c}(w)=1-G(w)
as this offers a more numerically stable target
(

)
.
Working with the CCDF handles some of the numerical inaccuracies arising from large arguments as a result of the approximation
Eq.

17
.
Inversion of

gives an approximation of the CCDF of

(
Abate et al. 2000
)
.
In this work we use the concentrated matrix exponential method with

(
Horváth et al. 2020
)
.
Numerical inversion methods are only valid for

(

;
Horváth et al. 2020
)
but this is solved through addition of the point mass at

post-inversion if required.
Our interest lies in the non-extinction case and this can be easily derived by defining a random variable
W^{\star}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=W\,|\,W>0
.
In this case we can remove the point mass at

and we can express the probability density function (PDF) of

as

g_{W^{\star}}(w)=\frac{1}{1-q^{\star}}\mathinner{\dfrac{\dif{}G_{W}(w)}{\dif{w}}},\quad w>0.

(23)

We will refer to this method as the probability-estimation (PE) method.

3.3
Calculating moments

In this section we outline the calculation of the moments
\mathds{E}[W_{i}^{k}]
that are required in the Taylor expansion of the LST (
Eq.

17
).

The moment generating function (MGF) of
W_{i}
is defined as

\xi_{i}(\theta)=\mathds{E}[e^{\theta W_{i}}],\quad\theta\in\mathbb{R},

(24)

and
\boldsymbol{\Xi}(\theta)=(\xi_{1}(\theta),\dots,\xi_{m}(\theta))
.
The
i
th MGF then satisfies the following functional equation
(
Athreya and Ney 1972
;
Mode 1971
)

\xi_{i}(\theta)=\int_{0}^{\infty}f_{i}\left(\boldsymbol{\Xi}(\theta e^{-\lambda y})\right)a_{i}e^{-a_{i}y}\dif y,

(25)

where
f_{i}(\boldsymbol{s})
is the progeny generating function and
a_{i}
is the rate parameter of the exponential lifetime distribution for individuals of type
i
respectively.

We note that throughout this section, unless otherwise specified, we use the notation
\xi_{i}^{(\ell)}(x)
to denote the
\ell
th derivative of
\xi_{i}(\theta)
with respect to
\theta
evaluated at
x
.
We also note that quantities of the form
\boldsymbol{c}^{(\ell)}
without implicit arguments (as opposed to functions
\xi_{i}^{(\ell)}(\theta)
) indicate a constant vector.

The
k
th derivative of the
i
th MGF yields the
k
th conditional moment of
W_{i}
and hence all the conditional moments can be obtained by differentiating
Eq.

25

n
times and evaluating the result at
\theta=0

(
Bellman and Harris 1952
)
,

\xi_{i}^{(n)}(0)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathinner{\mathinner{\dfrac{\partial{{}^{n}}}{\partial{\theta^{n}}}}f_{i}\left(\boldsymbol{\Xi}(\theta e^{-\lambda y})\right)\Biggr\rvert}_{\theta=0}a_{i}e^{-a_{i}y}\dif y.

(26)

The process of repeated differentiation of the progeny generating functions is complicated in general, however frequently occurring rules can be captured and encoded in an approach similar to automatic differentiation
(
Baydin et al. 2018
)
.
This enables the calculations to be automated and examples of this for common use cases (linear and quadratic progeny generating functions) will be provided here.
Using the equations arising from this process, the conditional moments can be solved for recursively.

3.3.1
The left-hand side

The first step of automation is to consider the left-hand side of
Eq.

26
which involves only the simple conditional generating function
\xi_{i}(\theta)
and is easily differentiated.
Differentiation of just the left-hand side corresponds to the simple factor

\boldsymbol{c}_{0}^{(n)}\boldsymbol{\Xi}^{(n)}(0)^{T},

(27)

where the vector
\boldsymbol{c}^{(n)}_{0}
has elements

c_{0,\ell}^{(n)}=\begin{cases}1,&\textrm{ for }\ell=i\\
0,&\textrm{ otherwise.}\end{cases}

3.3.2
Linear progeny generating function

The first common case is a linear progeny generating function.
For types
i
and
j
(including the case
i=j
) this has the form

f_{i}(\boldsymbol{s})=\frac{\eta}{a_{i}}+\frac{\alpha}{a_{i}}s_{j}.

(28)

The parameters
\eta
and
\alpha
are positive, real constants and
a_{i}=\eta+\alpha
.
Ignoring the scalar factor
\alpha/a_{i}
the interior component of the integral in
Eq.

26
is

\mathinner{\mathinner{\dfrac{\partial{{}^{n}}}{\partial{\theta^{n}}}}\xi_{j}(\theta e^{-\lambda t})\Biggr\rvert}_{\theta=0}=e^{-n\lambda t}\xi_{j}^{(n)}(0),

and substitution of
Eq.

27
and this into
Eq.

26
gives

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{c}_{0}^{(n)}\boldsymbol{\Xi}^{(n)}(0)^{T}

\displaystyle=\xi_{j}^{(n)}(0)\int_{0}^{\infty}\alpha e^{-(n\lambda+a_{i})t}\dif t

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha}{n\lambda+a_{i}}\xi_{j}^{(n)}(0).

This can be expressed as the linear equation

(\boldsymbol{c}_{0}^{(n)}+\boldsymbol{c}_{1}^{(n)})\boldsymbol{\Xi}^{(n)}(0)^{T}=d_{1}^{(n)}

where
d_{1}^{(n)}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=0
and
\boldsymbol{c}_{1}^{(n)}
is a vector with elements

c_{1,\ell}^{(n)}=\begin{cases}-\dfrac{\alpha}{n\lambda+a_{i}},&\textrm{ if }\ell=j\\
0,&\textrm{ otherwise}.\end{cases}

3.3.3
Quadratic progeny generating function

The last common case we considered was for quadratic progeny generating functions.
Assume for types
i
,
j
and
k
(including the case
i=j=k
) that

f_{i}(\boldsymbol{s})=\frac{\eta}{a_{i}}+\frac{\alpha}{a_{i}}s_{j}s_{k}.

(29)

Once again, the parameters
\eta
and
\alpha
are positive, real constants and
a_{i}=\eta+\alpha
.
The interior component of the integral in
Eq.

26
is

\mathinner{\mathinner{\dfrac{\partial{{}^{n}}}{\partial{\theta^{n}}}}\xi_{j}(\theta e^{-\lambda t})\xi_{k}(\theta e^{-\lambda t})\Biggr\rvert}_{\theta=0}=e^{-n\lambda t}\sum_{\ell=0}^{n}\binom{n}{\ell}\xi_{j}^{(\ell)}(0)\xi_{k}^{(n-\ell)}(0)

Substituting this into
Eq.

26
we obtain

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{c}_{0}^{(n)}\boldsymbol{\Xi}^{(n)}(0)^{T}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell=0}^{n}\binom{n}{\ell}\xi_{j}^{(\ell)}(0)\xi_{k}^{(n-\ell)}(0)\int_{0}^{\infty}\alpha e^{-(n\lambda+a_{i})t}\dif t

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha}{n\lambda+a_{i}}\sum_{\ell=0}^{n}\binom{n}{\ell}\xi_{j}^{(\ell)}(0)\xi_{k}^{(n-\ell)}(0).

This can be expressed as the linear equation

(\boldsymbol{c}_{0}^{(n)}+\boldsymbol{c}_{2}^{(n)})\boldsymbol{\Xi}^{(n)}(0)^{T}=d_{2}^{(n)},

where
\boldsymbol{c}_{2}^{(n)}
is a vector with elements

c_{2,\ell}^{(n)}=\begin{cases}-\dfrac{\alpha}{n\lambda+a_{i}},&\textrm{ if }\ell=j,k\\
0,&\textrm{ otherwise}.\end{cases}

and the constant,
d_{2}^{(n)}
, is given by

d_{2}^{(n)}=\sum_{\ell=1}^{n-1}\binom{n}{\ell}\xi_{j}^{(\ell)}(0)\xi_{k}^{(n-\ell)}(0).

3.3.4
Compositions of progeny generating functions

The rules can be applied to generating functions that are linear combinations of the various types by noting that their progeny generating function can be expressed as

f_{i}(\boldsymbol{s})=f_{i,1}(\boldsymbol{s})+f_{i,2}(\boldsymbol{s}).

The functional equations are then given by

\displaystyle\xi_{i}(\theta)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\left[f_{i,1}(\boldsymbol{\Xi}(\theta e^{-\lambda t}))+f_{i,2}(\boldsymbol{\Xi}(\theta e^{-\lambda t}))\right]a_{i}e^{-a_{i}t}\dif t

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}f_{i,1}(\boldsymbol{\Xi}(\theta e^{-\lambda t}))a_{i}e^{-a_{i}t}\dif t+\int_{0}^{\infty}f_{i,2}(\boldsymbol{\Xi}(\theta e^{-\lambda t}))a_{i}e^{-a_{i}t}\dif t.

As such, we can apply the rules for particular simple progeny generating functions to each integrand separately.
Care only needs to be taken as the left-hand side of the equation
\xi_{i}(\theta)
now only appears once.
This means that we need only account for the
\boldsymbol{c}_{0}^{(n)}
term from the LHS a single time.

3.3.5
Solving for the moments

Using the equations derived for the moments, we can formulate a matrix
C^{(n)}
and a constant vector
\boldsymbol{d}^{(n)}
that constitute a linear system that can be solved to get the
n
th moments.
The matrix
C^{(n)}
can be constructed row-wise, where each row corresponds to a linear system derived for a particular progeny generating function obtained by solving
Eq.

26
for
i=1,\dots,m
.
The elements of the constant vector,
\boldsymbol{d}^{(n)}
, correspond to all values appearing though the differentiation (of
Eq.

26
) which may depend on the previous
n-1
moments or the parameters of the model.
The
n
th conditional moments are determined by solving the recursive linear systems

C^{(n)}\boldsymbol{\Xi}^{(n)}(0)=\boldsymbol{d}^{(n)},\quad\boldsymbol{\Xi}^{(1)}(0)=\boldsymbol{u}.

3.4
Application to discrete-time models

The method developed so far has been for continuous-time processes, but discrete-time processes can also be handled with some simplifications to the method that we briefly outline in this section.
A discrete-time multi-type branching process (DT-MBP)
\boldsymbol{Z}(t)
is specified similarly to the CT-MBP but with
t\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
.
The progeny generating functions take the same form as
Eq.

7
but we now consider individuals having (non-random) unit lifetimes.
We study the behaviour of the system through the mean offspring matrix
\mathcal{M}
with elements

\mathcal{M}_{ij}=\mathinner{\mathinner{\dfrac{\partial{}f_{i}(\boldsymbol{s})}{\partial{s_{j}}}}\Biggr\rvert}_{\boldsymbol{s}=1}\quad i,j=1,\dots,n.

The dominant eigenvalue of this matrix is given by
\rho
and with it we define the analogous form of
Eq.

11
,
\boldsymbol{W}(t)=\boldsymbol{Z}(t)\rho^{-t}

(
Athreya and Ney 1972
)
.
Note that
\rho^{-t}=e^{-t\log\rho}
and letting
\lambda=\log\rho
we have
\boldsymbol{W}(t)=\boldsymbol{Z}(t)e^{-\lambda t}
as in the continuous-time case (
Eq.

11
).
The rescaled process
\boldsymbol{W}(t)
approaches the limit
W\boldsymbol{v}
where
\boldsymbol{u}
and
\boldsymbol{v}
now correspond to the right and left (normalised such that
\boldsymbol{u}\cdot\boldsymbol{1}=1
and
\boldsymbol{u}\cdot\boldsymbol{v}=1
) eigenvectors of
M
.
All the remaining constructions from the previous sections apply, but with the simplification that the conditional LSTs of
W
directly satisfy
(
Athreya and Ney 1972
;
Mode 1971
)

\boldsymbol{\varphi}(\theta)=\boldsymbol{f}\left(\boldsymbol{\varphi}\left(\theta\rho^{-1}\right)\right).

(30)

Algorithm

1
can be used to compute the LSTs by setting
\boldsymbol{\tilde{f}}(\boldsymbol{s})=\boldsymbol{f}(\boldsymbol{s})
,
\lambda=\log\rho
and
h=1
.
Note that the process of deriving the moments given a model specification is simplified dramatically in the case of a discrete-time model as we can directly differentiate
Eq.

30
.

3.5
Moment matching

We also consider a second approach to estimating the distribution of
W
which draws on the distributional properties of
W
to propose a suitable surrogate distribution.
The distribution of
W
is strictly non-negative and absolutely continuous, conditional on the event of non-extinction
(
Athreya and Ney 1972
;
Mode 1971
)
.
Furthermore, we assume that the distribution of the sample paths of the branching processes at time
t
are unimodal and likely heavy-tailed away from
0
, which implies similar characteristics for
\boldsymbol{W}(t)
(and hence
W
).
This suggests that suitable candidate distributions would likely be from the exponential family of distributions and as such we consider fitting a generalised gamma distribution to the
W_{i}
conditional on non-extinction,
W_{i}^{\star}=W_{i}\,|\,W_{i}>0
, by using a moment matching method (M3).
This distribution has the Weibull, exponential and gamma distributions as special cases and in testing appeared to be well fitting.
The generalised gamma distribution is denoted
\textrm{GG}(\beta,\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2})
and has PDF

g_{W}(w)=\frac{\alpha_{2}}{\beta^{\alpha_{1}}\Gamma(\alpha_{1}/\alpha_{2})}w^{\alpha_{1}-1}\exp{\left(-\left(\frac{w}{\beta}\right)^{\alpha_{2}}\right)}.

To determine the parameters of the distribution we use the first five estimated moments of
W_{i}^{\star}
.
The moments of
W_{i}^{\star}
can be calculated from the moments of
W_{i}
by renormalising them using the extinction probability
q_{i}
, hence

\mathds{E}[W_{i}^{\star k}]=(1-q_{i})^{-1}\mathds{E}[W_{i}^{k}].

(31)

We define the loss function as the sum of squares

L(\beta,\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2})=\sum_{k=1}^{5}\frac{\left(\mathds{E}[W_{i}^{{\star}k}]-M_{k}(\beta,\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2})\right)^{2}}{\eta_{k}}

(32)

where

M_{k}(\beta,\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2})=\beta^{k}\frac{\Gamma((\alpha_{1}+k)/\alpha_{2})}{\Gamma(\alpha_{1}/\alpha_{2})}

is the
k
th moment of a
\textrm{GG}(\beta,\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2})
distribution.
The constants
\eta_{k}
are automatically selected based on the magnitude of the moments to standardise the difference between the target moment and moments of the
\textrm{GG}(\beta,\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2})
distribution.
The loss function
Eq.

32
can be minimised (numerically) to estimate the parameters.
Samples can be drawn from the distribution using the inverse CDF method.

To generalise this approach to arbitrary initial conditions, we can estimate the surrogate distribution by calculating the best fitting distribution under each of the
m
initial conditions.
Denote these distributions
D_{1},\dots,D_{m}
and then using the independence of the subprocesses, we can sample realisations of
W
by drawing realisations of
W_{i}^{(j)}
and applying
Eq.

14
.
This process avoids the need to estimate the parameters of the distribution of
W
and samples can be drawn efficiently.
To sample a realisation of
W
we first run a Bernoulli trial to determine whether or not a particular subprocess has faded out and then if it hasn’t we sample
W_{i}^{\star(j)}
.
The full procedure for sampling a single realisation of
W
(not conditioned on non-extinction) is provided in
Appendix

C
.

4
Results

In
Section

3
we provided the details of two approaches for estimating the distribution of
W
and hence the time-shift itself.
The first method, which we refer to as the PE method, relies on approximating the LST of
W
and inverting it (detailed in
Section

3.1
) and the second, called the M3 method, relies on matching the first five moments (detailed
Section

3.5
). This section focuses on how these methods perform on two models of increasing complexity.

In
Section

4.1
we look at applying the methods to an SEIR model, which is a simple extension of the SIR example.

Section

4.1.1
explores the effect of the hyper-parameters (the number of moments in the expansion and the step size for the imbedded process

) on the accuracy of the resulting distributions as well as the computation time.
Following this, in
Section

4.1.2
we assess the effect of the total population size, on the time-shift distributions which provides some insight to when this method is suitable.
Finally, in
Section

4.1.3
we explore more complex initial conditions and how this influences the shape and location of the resulting time-shift distributions.
In
Section

4.2
we explore a more complicated model which demonstrates how the method can be used to approximate the macroscopic dynamics of a more complex 6-state CTMC model.

4.2
Innate response model

Our second example is a within-host viral kinetics model that characterises a respiratory infection such as COVID-19 or influenza.
This demonstrates our method on a model with higher complexity and shows the simplicity offered in simulating macroscopic dynamics using the time-shift approach.
This model is a (slightly adjusted) stochastic version of that used in
Ke et al. 2021
, which is an extension to the so-called target-cell-limited model which incorporates an innate immune response
(
Baccam et al. 2006
)
.
Our adjustment is that we also account for two mechanisms of infection, through virions and cell-to-cell interactions to further increase the complexity of the model
(
Odaka and Inoue 2021
)
.

This model tracks
6
population sizes; target (or susceptible) cells (
U
), cells in an eclipse phase (
E
), infectious cells (
I
), number of viral particles (
V
), number of interferons (
A
) and the number of cells refractory to infection (
R
).
Target (or susceptible cells) (
U
) can become infected when bound to by a virus particle at rate
\beta_{1}
or infected by infectious cells (

) through the viral synapse structure at rate
\beta_{2}

(
Odaka and Inoue 2021
)
.
Infected cells transition through an eclipse phase (
E
) at rate
\sigma

I
) and being removed at rate
\gamma

During the eclipse phase we account for the death of infected but not yet infectious cells at rate
\eta

Over a cell’s infectious period, new virus particles (

c_{V}

Furthermore, we consider the effect of an innate immune response whereby an infectious cell produces interferons (

5
Discussion

We have presented a framework to estimate the distribution of the time-shift between sample paths of a broad class of Markov chain models and the corresponding deterministic solution. The recent work of
Barbour et al. 2015
establishes a crucial theoretical result, that the initial dynamics of the Markov process affects the long-time dynamics in a manner that is largely captured by a random variable
W
and the time shift is a simple transformation of this. Our contribution is a numerical framework to approximate the distribution of
W
for the class of continuous-time Markov chains identified at the start of
Section

3
. We introduced the PE method (
Section

3.2
), an accurate approximation dependent on several hyper-parameters and built on the Laplace-Stieltjes transform of
W
, and the M3 method (
Section

3.5
), a fast approximation matching the first five moments of
W
to a generalised gamma distribution.

The PE and M3 methods are both flexible and can be applied to multi-type branching process models in discrete-time and continuous-time.
They allow one to generate solution curves that preserve the macroscopic behaviours of the sample paths from the stochastic model.
Sample paths obtained in this way can be used for rapid and accurate simulation studies or inference on medium to long time scales.

The theory of branching processes is well established (see
Harris 1948
;
Harris 1964
;
Mode 1971
;
Athreya and Ney 1972
).
The random variable
W
was constructed and studied in the literature as early as 1948
(
Harris 1948
;
Harris 1951
)
.
However, these bodies of work focus on models with very particular structure that aids analytic tractability; for example the linear birth-death process we derive as an early time approximation to the SIR model in
Section

2
is studied in
Harris 1951
. Another tractable example is the linear fractional branching process featured in
Kimmel and Axelrod 2015
.
To our knowledge, prior to this manuscript there were no tools developed specifically to compute
W
in the more general multivariate case and our methodology bridges this gap.

We explored three models of varying complexity, including a 6-dimensional innate response model in
Section

4.2
, a SEIR model in
Section

4.1
, and a SIR model in
Section

2
.
The numerical results show that both PE and M3 methods reproduce the empirical distribution accurately across a broad range of model conditions, and how the parameters of the PE method can easily be chosen.
Furthermore, the innate response model demonstrates an effective
macroscale reduction
performed by our method
(
Givon et al. 2004
;
Roberts 2015
)
.
In this model, the branching process is 3-dimensional, and the computation of
W
from this low-dimensional space allows us to accurately reproduce the macroscale dynamics of the full 6-dimensional system (shown in
Fig.

7
).
This macroscale reduction is a known consequence of Theorem 1.1 of
Barbour et al. 2015
, and the contribution of our manuscript is to provide a practical route to compute it.

All of the models we considered shared relatively simple branching dynamics with linear and quadratic progeny generating functions.
This may seem restrictive, but most biological or physical processes will have dynamics of this form, especially if the common assumption of mass action mixing is made.
The only requirement common to both the PE and M3 methods is that we can compute the moments of
W
.
It should be straightforward to extend the rules derived in
Section

3.3
for the calculation of the moments to more complex dynamics.
An example exhibiting such dynamics arises in the modelling of the accumulation
of HIV-1 mutations where progeny generating functions arise that are polynomial of large (positive) integer order
(
Shiri and Welte 2011
)
.
Other examples are where the offspring distribution has a parametric form, such as the negative binomial often used in epidemic models that feature super-spreading
(
Garske and Rhodes 2008
)
.
We have provided flexible open source code to automate these computations, which can be easily extended to handle new cases when they are investigated.

Our work can theoretically be extended to branching processes with non-Markovian lifetimes of individuals;
these models are referred to as age-dependent branching processes
(
Athreya and Ney 1972
;
Harris 1964
;
Mode 1971
)
.
Age-dependent processes have the same definition for
\boldsymbol{W}(t)
(e.g.
Eq.

11
) with equivalent limiting behaviours and can be studied in much the same ways as we did in
Section

3.1
.
The main source of difficulty in working with age-dependent processes arises from differentiating the functional equations required for computing the conditional moments of
W
.
As opposed to the Markovian case, the integrals that need to be computed may be much more challenging to solve and may even require numerical integration to calculate.
This would slow the computation, as exact results like those presented in
Section

3.3
may not arise. Current work is investigating which cases are amenable to analysis.

One of the primary advantages of our methodology is the fast generation of macroscopic sample paths that also capture the early time stochasticity.
Simulation of stochastic models in either an exact
(
Gillespie 1977
)
or approximate framework
(
Gillespie 2001
)
is often a computational bottleneck
(
Gillespie 2001
;
Black 2018
;
Kreger et al. 2021
)
.
This computational expense is mostly a result of the requirement to generate random numbers to calculate which event occurs next and the number of events scales linearly with the size of the system
Gillespie 1977
.
Approximate stochastic simulation methods alleviate this demand somewhat by approximating the number of events over a time-step, usually referred to as tau-leaping
(
Gillespie 2001
)
.
The secondary class of methods—which our methodology can loosely be classified under—are hybrid simulation methods.
These methods combine deterministic and stochastic simulation methods to produce more efficient simulation routines
(
Kreger et al. 2021
;
Rebuli et al. 2017
)
.
Often these methods are formulated with a regime switching during the period where the populations experience exponential growth, whereby we switch from a stochastic simulation over to a deterministic solution.
These methods then suffer from the computational complexity required for the random number generation during the stochastic simulation regime as well as the need to solve the deterministic model with an arbitrary initial condition for each path generated
(
Rebuli et al. 2017
;
Kreger et al. 2021
)
.
Our approach generates sample paths using only a
single
solve of the deterministic model and then drawing from the univariate time-shift distribution.
Then each sample path is obtained by simply replicating the solution and shifting it by the random variate.
This is much less computationally demanding and as such is readily applicable inference methods that rely on forward simulations of the model such as particle filters.

As detailed in
Section

4.1.1
, estimating PDF values using the PE method is fast (on the order or
10^{-2}
s) and using the method we can easily draw realisations of
W
through standard sampling approaches (i.e. rejection sampling).
The M3 method provides further performance for sampling by approximating the distribution analytically.
Fitting an analytical distribution means we can directly sample realisations using the inverse CDF method (taking around
10^{-6}
s).
When accuracy is paramount, the PE method offers a framework for computing the LST to arbitrary precision through increasing the number of moments and controlling the tolerance for the moment expansion in the neighbourhood of 0.
In situations where performance is the priority then the M3 method provides a much faster route to a reliable approximation of the distribution of
W
(as seen for the examples in
Section

4
).
However, as it is an approximating the distribution of
W
, it may be the case that some characteristics of the distribution are not captured (i.e. very long tails).
From our testing, matching to the first five moments yields a highly accurate approximation to the distribution.
By providing two methods for computing the distribution of
W
, we give a framework which can be used to assess how reliably the M3 method is working for a given problem if needed.

Appendix A
Time-shift distribution for the SIR example

For the SIR model, the BP approximation
Z(t)
is a linear birth-death process and from
Harris 1951
the LST for
W
is given by

\phi(\theta)=\frac{\gamma}{\beta}+\frac{\beta-\gamma}{\beta}\cdot\frac{1}{1+\frac{\beta\theta}{\beta-\gamma}}.

For the SIR model, the probability of extinction is given by
q=\gamma/\beta

(
Allen 2017
)
and hence
1-q=(\beta-\gamma)/\beta
.
Rewriting the LST in terms of
q
gives

\phi(\theta)=q+(1-q)\cdot\frac{1}{1+\frac{\theta}{1-q}}.

(34)

The LST can be inverted by applying the linearity property of inverse LSTs which allows us to invert the two terms separately.
The inverse transform of
q
is simply
q\delta(w)
where
\delta(w)
is the Dirac delta function.
This term represents the point mass at
0
of size
q
mentioned in
Section

3
.
The inverse transform of the second term is obtained by applying the property
\mathscr{L}^{-1}\left\{aF(\theta)\right\}(w)=a\mathscr{L}^{-1}\left\{F(\theta)\right\}(w)
and noting that

\mathscr{L}^{-1}\left\{\frac{1}{1+\frac{\theta}{1-q}}\right\}(w)=(1-q)e^{-(1-q)w}.

Together this yields the inverse transform

\mathscr{L}^{-1}\left\{\phi(\theta)\right\}(w)=q\delta(w)+(1-q)^{2}e^{-(1-q)w}.

We can see that the inverse transform is the sum of a point mass at
0
of size
q
and another term which corresponds to the
w>0
case.
Conditioning on the event of non-extinction involves removing the point mass term at 0 and renormalising the remaining function such that it integrates to
1
as it is now a probability density function.
Let
W^{\star}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=W|W>0
as in
Section

3
, then the density is given by

g_{W^{\star}}(w)=(1-q)e^{-(1-q)w},\quad w>0

(35)

which is precisely the density of an
\textrm{Exp}(1-q)
random variable and hence
W^{\star}\sim\textrm{Exp}(1-q)
.

The distribution of the time-shift can easily be derived by starting with the time-shift
\tau=\lambda^{-1}(\log W-\log\mathds{E}[W])
.
Since
\mathds{E}[W]=1
when
z_{0}=1
, we have
\tau=\lambda^{-1}\log W
which is equivalent to
W=e^{\lambda\tau}
.
Furthermore, as the transform is monotonically increasing we can obtain the density function for
\tau^{\star}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\tau|\tau|W>0
(where the conditioning ensures the process doesn’t fadeout) by noting that
G_{\tau^{\star}}(t)=\textrm{Pr}(\tau\leq t|W>0)=\textrm{Pr}(W\leq e^{\lambda t}|W>0)
and hence using the CDF method and differentiating we obtain

g_{\tau^{\star}}(t)=\lambda(1-q)e^{\lambda t-(1-q)e^{\lambda t}},\quad t\in\mathbb{R}.

(36)

Appendix B
Error calculations for LST approximation

Using a Taylor series expansion on the generating function

\displaystyle\phi(\theta)

\displaystyle=\mathds{E}[e^{-\theta W}]

\displaystyle=\mathds{E}\left[\sum_{k=0}^{n}\frac{(-\theta W)^{k}}{k!}+R_{n}(\theta,s)\right]

\displaystyle=\hat{\phi}(\theta)+\mathds{E}\left[R_{n}(\theta,s)\right]

where
\hat{\phi}
is from
Eq.

17
and
R_{n}(\theta,s)
is the Lagrange remainder term with
s\in[0,\theta W)
. The remainder term is given by

R_{n}(\theta,s)=\frac{(-1)^{n+1}e^{-s}(-\theta W)^{n+1}}{(n+1)!}=\frac{e^{-s}(\theta W)^{n+1}}{(n+1)!}.

Our interest lies in bounding the error term,

\displaystyle\left|\mathds{E}\left[R_{n}(\theta,s)\right]\right|

\displaystyle\leq\mathds{E}\left[\mathinner{\!\left\lvert R_{n}(\theta,s)\right\rvert}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathds{E}\left[\frac{\mathinner{\!\left\lvert e^{-s}\right\rvert}\mathinner{\!\left\lvert\theta^{n+1}\right\rvert}\mathinner{\!\left\lvert W^{n+1}\right\rvert}}{(n+1)!}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathinner{\!\left\lvert e^{-s}\right\rvert}\mathinner{\!\left\lvert\theta^{n+1}\right\rvert}}{(n+1)!}\mathds{E}[|W^{n+1}|]

We can simplify this by noting that
W\geq 0
by definition and that
\mathinner{\!\left\lvert e^{-s}\right\rvert}\leq 1
, hence

\displaystyle\left|\mathds{E}\left[R_{n}(\theta,s)\right]\right|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\mathinner{\!\left\lvert\theta^{n+1}\right\rvert}}{(n+1)!}\mathds{E}[W^{n+1}].

Define the left-hand side of this equation as
E_{n}(\theta)
. We can then determine the maximal size of the neighbourhood about 0 by solving
E_{n}(\theta)<\epsilon
for some target tolerance
\epsilon
. For

\mathinner{\!\left\lvert\theta\right\rvert}\leq\left(\frac{(n+1)!\epsilon}{\mathds{E}[W^{n+1}]}\right)^{1/(n+1)}.

the error term is no more than
\epsilon
.

Appendix E
Deriving the deterministic approximation to the innate response model

Let
\boldsymbol{x}(t)=K^{-1}\boldsymbol{X}(t)
denote the density process and note that we use lower-case letters to denote the densities (i.e.
u=K^{-1}U
).
Then reading down Column 2 of
Table

4
and provided
\boldsymbol{x}+\boldsymbol{l}\in\mathcal{S}
where
\mathcal{S}
is the state-space and
\boldsymbol{l}
represents the jumps of the process, then we can express the (positive) transition rates in terms of the following functions

\displaystyle y(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}+\boldsymbol{l})=\begin{cases}K\beta_{1}uv,&\textrm{if }\boldsymbol{l}=(-1,0,1,0,1,0)\\
K\beta_{2}ui,&\textrm{if }\boldsymbol{l}=(-1,0,1,0,0,0)\\
K\varrho ua,&\textrm{if }\boldsymbol{l}=(-1,1,0,0,0,0)\\
K\delta r,&\textrm{if }\boldsymbol{l}=(1,-1,0,0,0,0)\\
K\sigma e,&\textrm{if }\boldsymbol{l}=(0,0,-1,1,0,0)\\
K\eta e,&\textrm{if }\boldsymbol{l}=(0,0,-1,0,0,0)\\
K\gamma i,&\textrm{if }\boldsymbol{l}=(0,0,0,-1,0,0)\\
Kp_{V}i,&\textrm{if }\boldsymbol{l}=(0,0,0,0,1,0)\\
Kc_{V}v,&\textrm{if }\boldsymbol{l}=(0,0,0,0,-1,0)\\
Kp_{a}i,&\textrm{if }\boldsymbol{l}=(0,0,0,0,0,1)\\
Kc_{a}a,&\textrm{if }\boldsymbol{l}=(0,0,0,0,0,-1).\end{cases}

(37)

Since the rates can all be expressed in this form (referred to as “density dependent”), by Theorem 3.1 of
Kurtz 1970
, as
K\to\infty
the process
\boldsymbol{x}(t)
converges to
\bar{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}(t)
(the deterministic trajectory) uniformly in probability over finite time intervals provided
\bar{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}(0)=K^{-1}\boldsymbol{X}(0)
.
The system of ODEs governing the deterministic approximation given by
Eq.

33
is then obtained by summing across the non-zero values of an element of the jump vectors and the corresponding values of
K^{-1}y(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}+\boldsymbol{l})
.
As an example, consider the first ODE
\mathrm{d}u/\mathrm{d}t
.
The first 4 rates have non-zero first elements of
\boldsymbol{l}
and summing across these gives

\mathinner{\dfrac{\dif{}u}{\dif{t}}}=-\beta_{1}uv-\beta_{2}ui-\varrho ua+\delta r

which is exactly the first differential equation in
Eq.

33
.
The rest of the differential equations in
Eq.

33
are obtained in the same way and then the deterministic trajectory is simply given by the unique solution to
Eq.

33
given a known initial condition
\bar{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}(0)
.
