Estimating Average Treatment Effects in Regression Discontinuity Designs with Covariates under Minimal Assumptions
Patrick Kramer, Alexander Kreiß

Abstract
We study regression discontinuity designs with the use of additional covariates for estimation of the average treatment effect. We provide a detailed proof of asymptotic normality of the covariate-adjusted estimator under minimal assumptions, which may serve as an accessible text to the mathematics behind regression discontinuity with covariates. In addition, this proof carries at least three benefits. First of all, it allows to draw straightforward consequences concerning the impact of the covariates on the bias and variance of the estimator. In fact, we can provide conditions under which the influence of the covariates on the bias vanishes. Moreover, we show that the variance in the covariate-adjusted case is never worse than in the case of the baseline estimator under a very general invertibility condition. Finally, our approach does not require the existence of potential outcomes, allowing for a sensitivity analysis in case confounding cannot be ruled out, e.g., by a manipulated forcing variable.



keywords
Regression discontinuity design, covariates, average treatment effect estimation, local polynomial regression

1
Introduction

Regression discontinuity design (RDD) is regularly used in economics, political science and other social sciences like biomedical science
(
Calonico et al 2019
)
. The aim of RDD is to estimate causal effects of a treatment on some outcome in settings where the treatment is applied depending on some running variable (also called forcing variable or score value). Therefore, given data needs to contain a score value for each unit which decides whether the treatment is applied to that unit or not. In fact, if the score value lies above a certain cutoff point, the treatment is applied, otherwise it is not. Then, the effect of the treatment on the outcome can be estimated by comparing units that are close to the cutoff, but are on different sides of it. This concept, including its theoretical properties and applications, has already been studied by a variety of researchers.

Cattaneo et al 2019
and
Cattaneo et al 2023
provide a two-part guide for the analysis and interpretation of regression discontinuity designs. Also the work of
Lee and Lemieux 2010
can be seen as a guide for empirical researchers on RDDs, discussing basic theory, different methods of estimation and presenting examples to the reader.
Imbens and Lemieux 2008
summarize practical and theoretical issues that one encounters when implementing RD methods.
Cattaneo et al 2017
offer an empirical comparison of different inference approaches, such as parametric and non-parametric local polynomial methods, as well as local randomization methods.
Hahn et al 2001
present non-parametric estimation of the average treatment effect under minimal assumptions, as well as an interpretation of the Wald estimator as an RD estimator. Focusing on local linear regression,
Imbens and Kalyanaraman 2011
provide an asymptotically optimal bandwidth choice under squared error loss. In
Armstrong and Kolesár 2018
, confidence intervals for linear functionals of regression functions, e.g. the regression discontinuity parameter, are constructed, carrying optimal finite-sample properties and sharp efficiency bounds under normal errors with known variance.
Calonico et al 2014
address the issue of biased data-driven confidence intervals, presenting a more robust confidence interval estimator for average treatment effects in different RDD settings.

In practice, there is often more data than only the score value and the outcome. For this reason, many researchers want to incorporate additional data into the analysis of the causal effect. Indeed, this can be done by including additional covariates linearly in a local linear regression around the cutoff as suggested by
Calonico et al 2019
. In this paper, they provide a formal study of the impact of covariate adjustments on estimation and inference in RD designs. In order to account for misspecification in finite samples
(
Calonico et al 2014
)
, they established a bias-corrected version of the covariate-adjusted estimator. In addition to that, smoothness assumptions that are necessary to obtain a consistent estimator that delivers reliable results are given. Under this imposed regularity conditions, they showed that the bias-corrected covariate-adjusted estimator is asymptotically normally distributed.

In this paper we continue the study of asymptotic normality of the regression discontinuity estimator using covariates. More precisely, we simplify the proof strategy of
Kreiß and Rothe 2021
and use that in typical regression discontinuity designs using locally linear estimation, one estimates

\tau_{0}:=\argmin_{\tau}\min_{a,b,c\in\mathbb{R},\gamma\in\mathbb{R}^{p}}\mathbb{E}\left(\left(Y_{i}-a-\tau T_{i}-bX_{i}-cT_{i}X_{i}-\gamma^{T}Z_{i}\right)^{2}\right),

where
X_{i}
denotes the running variable,
T_{i}:=\mathbbm{1}(X_{i}\geq c)
denotes the treatment indicator (where
T_{i}=1
means that unit
i
was treated, hence we study sharp RD with treatment threshold
c
), and
Z_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
is an observed, additional vector of covariates (in contrast to
Kreiß and Rothe 2021
we let
p
be fixed). Taking this view-point allows us to reprove the asymptotic normality result of
Calonico et al 2019
in a situation without potential outcomes, i.e., we do not require
Y_{i}=T_{i}Y_{i}(1)+(1-T_{i})Y_{i}(0)
for some potential outcomes
Y_{i}(0),Y_{i}(1)
, and under no assumptions on the bandwidth
h
other than the standard assumptions of
h\to 0
and
nh\to\infty
. The main contribution of this paper is therefore to provide a proof of asymptotic normality in regression discontinuity designs with covariates under minimal assumptions. Such a result is useful for at least three reasons: First, the variance is obtained in a form that allows us to conclude that it is less or equal to the variance of the baseline estimator under a very general invertibility condition, which is a slightly stronger result than the one stated in
Calonico et al 2019
. Second, the representation of the bias allows to state straightforward adjustments and assumptions under which the leading term of the bias is independent of the covariates, dispelling the concern that the bias might increase when incorporating covariates. Finally, we are able to conduct a sensitivity analysis in case confounding cannot be ruled out.

Furthermore, this paper can serve as an accessible introduction to studying regression discontinuity under the influence of covariates. Since we tackle the situation of a fixed number of covariates, the idea of proving asymptotic normality is not diluted by more complicated arguments. In fact, our provided proof is fairly comparable to
Hahn et al 2001
in terms of its structure as well as the required assumptions, and can be seen as a canonical extension to the situation with incorporated covariates.

This paper contributes to the wide area of literature considering regression discontinuity designs using covariates. In addition to
Calonico et al 2019
and
Kreiß and Rothe 2021
, this includes the following research:
Frölich and Huber 2019
propose a fully non-parametric kernel method to account for observed covariates in RDDs.
Noack et al 2021
develop a novel class of estimators, opting for a more efficient estimation than linear adjustment estimators. Finally,
Arai et al 2021
study the case of potentially high-dimensional covariates, proposing estimation and inference methods with covariate selection that use a local approach with kernel weights in combination with
\ell_{1}
-penalization.

The remainder of this paper is structured as follows. Section
2
introduces basic terminology and the setup. Section
3
states the main theorem on asymptotic normality of the covariate-adjusted estimator and provides a proof. Moreover, consequences concerning the representation of the bias and variance, as well as a sensitivity analysis, are discussed. Finally, Section
4
entails all statements that were used in the proof of the main theorem. This particularly includes four propositions, each elaborated in its own sub-chapter. All additionally necessary supporting statements are provided in the supplementary appendix of this paper.

2
Setup and Notation

The data is given by an independent sample
\{(Y_{i},X_{i},Z_{i}),i=1,...,n\}
whereby
n
is the population size,
Y_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
is the outcome variable,
X_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
the running variable with its probability density function
f_{X}
and
Z_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
is a vector of pre-treatment covariates. The running variable
X_{i}
decides whether a unit receives a treatment or not. In fact, we consider so called sharp RDDs and define the treatment variable
T_{i}
by
T_{i}:=\mathds{1}(X_{i}\geq c)
, which means that a treatment is applied if and only if the running variable exceeds a certain cutoff
c
. Without loss of generality and for the sake of simplicity we want this cutoff to be zero throughout this paper, that is
c=0
and
T_{i}=\mathds{1}(X_{i}\geq 0)
.
Moreover, with potential outcomes we mean the random variables
Y_{i}(0)
and
Y_{i}(1)
, which are the outcome in case a unit receives a treatment or not, respectively. In case of their existence (which we do not require), we have that
Y_{i}=Y_{i}(T_{i})
, which is the real outcome depending on the value of the treatment variable.

The target of our estimation is
\tau_{Y}=\lim_{x\searrow 0}\mathbb{E}(Y_{i}\mid X_{i}=x)-\lim_{x\nearrow 0}\mathbb{E}(Y_{i}\mid X_{i}=x)
, in other words, the height of the jump of
\mu_{Y}(x):=\mathbb{E}(Y_{i}\mid X_{i}=x)
at
x=0
. On the one hand, we note that under the classical assumption
Y_{i}=T_{i}Y_{i}(1)+(1-T_{i})Y_{i}(0)
with
\mathbb{E}(Y_{i}(t)\mid X_{i}=x)
being continuous at
x=0
for
t\in\{0,1\}
,
\tau_{Y}=\mathbb{E}(Y_{i}(1)\mid X_{i}=0)-\mathbb{E}(Y_{i}(0)\mid X_{i}=0)
can be interpreted as an average treatment effect at the cut-off. On the other hand, however, manipulation of the running variable can lead to discontinuities in
\mathbb{E}(Y_{i}(1)\mid X_{i}=x)
as pointed out by
Gerard et al 2020
. We do neither insist on continuous conditional expectations, nor consider a specific confounding mechanism, and instead allow for general outcomes
Y_{i}
.

For arbitrary random variables
A
and
B
, we want to define
\mu_{A}(x):=\mathbb{E}(A\mid X_{i}=x)
,
\sigma^{2}_{AB}(x):=\mu_{AB^{\top}}(x)-\mu_{A}(x)\mu_{B}(x)^{\top}
and
\sigma^{2}_{A}(x):=\sigma^{2}_{AA}(x)
. Moreover, given a function
f
defined on the real line for which the left- and right-sided limit in zero exists, we set
f_{+}=\lim_{x\searrow 0}f(x)
and
f_{-}=\lim_{x\nearrow 0}f(x)
. Then we can also represent the average treatment effect, the object of interest, by
\tau_{Y}=\mu_{Y+}-\mu_{Y-}
. To estimate
\tau
, we will use a local linear adjustment estimator: Let
K
denote a kernel function,
K_{h}(x):=\frac{1}{h}K\left(\frac{x}{h}\right)
,
h>0
the bandwidth and
V_{i}:=(1,T_{i},X_{i}/h,T_{i}X_{i}/h)^{\top}
. For the sake of notational simplicity we will just write
V_{i}
instead of
V_{i}(h)
and omit its dependency of
h
. However, it should be noted that this dependency exists and also needs to be considered when looking at the convergence of expressions containing
V_{i}
. The estimator is then given by

\hat{\tau}_{h}=e_{2}^{\top}\big(\hat{\theta}_{n},\hat{\gamma}_{n}\big),

where
e_{2}:=\begin{pmatrix}0&1&0\dots&0\end{pmatrix}^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{4+p}
and

\big(\hat{\theta}_{n},\hat{\gamma}_{n}\big)=\argmin_{(\theta,\gamma)\in\mathbb{R}^{p+4}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}K_{h}(X_{i})\left(Y_{i}-V_{i}^{\top}\theta-Z_{i}^{\top}\gamma\right)^{2}.

If we use the term
kernel
in the remainder of this paper, we refer to a function which is defined as follows.

Definition 1

(Kernel function)
.

A kernel is a function
K:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
which

•

is non-negative and integrable,

•

integrates to one, i.e.,
\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}K(x)\,dx=1
, and

•

is symmetric, i.e.,
K(-x)=K(x)
.

In order to proof the asymptotic normality later on, we will have to deal with the regression error
r_{i}(h)=Y_{i}-V_{i}^{\top}\theta_{0}(h)-Z_{i}^{\top}\gamma_{0}(h)
, whereby
\theta_{0}(h)
and
\gamma_{0}(h)
are the population regression coefficients defined by

\big(\theta_{0}(h),\gamma_{0}(h)\big)=\argmin_{(\theta,\gamma)\in\mathbb{R}^{p+4}}\mathbb{E}\left(K_{h}(X_{i})\left(Y_{i}-V_{i}^{\top}\theta-Z_{i}^{\top}\gamma\right)^{2}\right).

We can also define the average treatment effect on random variables other than the outcome as follows:

Definition 2

(Average treatment effect)
.

Let
m\in\mathbb{N}
and
A\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
be a random variable such that the left- and right-sided limit of
\mu_{A}
in zero exist. Then we define the average treatment effect of
T_{i}
on
A
as

\tau_{A}=\mu_{A+}-\mu_{A-}.

In the following, we give some more definitions that are relevant for the subsequent content of this paper.

Definition 3
.

Let
L:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
be an arbitrary integrable function. Then we define

L_{-}^{(\alpha)}=\int_{-\infty}^{0}L(u)u^{\alpha}\,du,\quad L_{+}^{(\alpha)}=\int_{0}^{\infty}L(u)u^{\alpha}\,du\quad\text{and}\quad L^{(\alpha)}=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}L(u)u^{\alpha}\,du.

Definition 4
.

Let
K
be a kernel function with
K^{(2)}<\infty
. Then we define the matrix

\kappa(K):=\begin{pmatrix}K^{(0)}&K_{+}^{(0)}&K^{(1)}&K_{+}^{(1)}\\
K_{+}^{(0)}&K_{+}^{(0)}&K_{+}^{(1)}&K_{+}^{(1)}\\
K^{(1)}&K_{+}^{(1)}&K^{(2)}&K_{+}^{(2)}\\
K_{+}^{(1)}&K_{+}^{(1)}&K_{+}^{(2)}&K_{+}^{(2)}\end{pmatrix}.

The following definition will be used to state the regularity assumptions in the assertions of this paper more compactly and concisely.

Definition 5
.

We call a function
f:\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}\to\mathbb{R}

k
-times one-sided differentiable at 0 when
f
can be extended continuously to zero from the left and from the right, and all derivatives of
f
up to order
k
exist on
(-\infty,0)
and
(0,\infty)
and can be extended continuously to zero from the left and from the right. Then we denote the value of the continuous extension of
f
to zero from the left by
f_{-}
and from the right by
f_{+}
. In an analogous manner, we use that notation for the extension of the derivatives, such as
f^{\prime}_{-}
or
f^{\prime}_{+}
.

3
Asymptotic Normality of the Estimator

This section aims to prove the main theorem of this paper, stating that the covariate-adjusted estimator of the average treatment effect is asymptotically normally distributed. As several conditions are required such that this holds, the next subsection will briefly cover all necessary assumptions. After that, the final theorem will be stated and proved, followed by its implications regarding the comparison of the bias and variance to the case of estimation without covariates, as well as a sensitivity analysis in case of a confounded forcing variable.

3.1
Assumptions

In order to state the asymptotic normality of the covariate-adjusted estimator of the average treatment effect, we need to formulate several assumptions that all need to be satisfied.

Assumption 1

(Convergence of bandwidth)
.

Let the bandwidth
h=h_{n}
be a sequence
(h_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
with
h_{n}>0
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
such that
h\to 0
and
nh\to\infty
for
n\to\infty
.

Assumption
1
states that the bandwidth
h
converges to zero with increasing number of observations, however, slow enough such that
nh
still goes to infinity. This is a standard assumption when dealing with kernel regressions.

Assumption 2

(Kernel function)
.

Let
K:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
be a kernel function (cf. Definition
1
) with
K^{(4)},\left(K^{2}\right)^{(2)}<\infty
which is supported on
[-1,1]
.

These conditions are standard assumptions on the kernel. The finiteness of
K^{(4)},\left(K^{2}\right)^{(2)}<\infty
indeed can also be replaced by additionally requiring continuity. In either case, typical kernels, such as triangular and Epanechnikov kernels, are covered under this assumption. Kernels with unbounded support, like the Gaussian kernel, could be used by enhancing the theorem’s proof with some slightly more complex arguments.

Assumption 3

(Differentiability)
.

Let
\mu_{Z}
be continuous. Further, let the conditional expectations
\mu_{Z}
and
\mu_{Y}
be three times one-sided differentiable at zero (cf. Definition
5
). Also, let
\mu_{ZZ^{\top}}
and
\mu_{ZY}
be one time one-sided differentiable in zero.

This differentiability assumption is used to perform Taylor expansions up to order two and obtain remainder terms of order
O(h^{3})
.

In order to state the next assumptions, the following two definitions regarding an adjusted version of the covariates and a covariate-adjusted outcome are necessary.

Definition 6
.

Define the matrix
M_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{4\times p}
as

M_{n}:=\begin{pmatrix}\mu_{Z}(0)&\mathbf{0}&h{\mu^{\prime}_{Z}}_{-}&h\left({\mu^{\prime}_{Z}}_{+}-{\mu^{\prime}_{Z}}_{-}\right)\end{pmatrix}^{\top}.

Definition 7

(Adjusted variables)
.

Define

\tilde{Z_{i}}:=Z_{i}-M_{n}^{\top}V_{i}\quad\text{ and }\quad\tilde{Y_{i}}:=Y_{i}-Z_{i}^{\top}\tilde{\gamma}

with
\tilde{\gamma}:=\left({\sigma_{Z}^{2}}_{-}+{\sigma_{Z}^{2}}_{+}\right)^{-1}\left(\sigma^{2}_{ZY-}+\sigma^{2}_{ZY+}\right)

The existence of the inverse in the definition of
\tilde{\gamma}
is assumed in the next assumption.

Assumption 4

(Invertibility)
.

Let
\mathbb{E}\left(K_{h}(X_{i})\tilde{Z_{i}}\tilde{Z}_{i}^{\top}\right)
and
{\sigma_{Z}^{2}}_{-}+{\sigma_{Z}^{2}}_{+}
be invertible and

\left\lVert\mathbb{E}\left(K_{h}(X_{i})\tilde{Z_{i}}\tilde{Z}_{i}^{\top}\right)^{-1}\right\rVert_{2}=O(1).

In fact, we assume invertibility of a local version of the design matrix which is typical for regression.

Assumption 5

(Density)
.

Let the probability density function
f_{X}
of
X_{i}
be three times continuously differentiable in a neighborhood around zero with
f_{X}(0)>0
.

This assumption is particularly relevant in order to have coinciding left and right limits of all derivatives up to order three at zero. Also it allows to find upper bounds for all those derivatives on a compact interval. Indeed we will often be in the case of having a compact interval since integration mostly involves the kernel function which has a compact support by assumption.

Assumption 6

(Uniform boundedness)
.

Let the following statements be true for all
k,l\in\{1,...,p\}

\displaystyle\sup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\sup_{x\in[-h,h]}\,\mathbb{E}\left(\left(\tilde{Z}_{i}^{(k)}r_{i}(h)\right)^{2}\;\,\vrule\;X_{i}=x\right)<\infty,

\displaystyle\sup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\sup_{x\in[-h,h]}\,\mathbb{E}\left(\left(\tilde{Z}_{i}^{(k)}\tilde{Z}_{i}^{(l)}\right)^{2}\;\,\vrule\;X_{i}=x\right)<\infty

and let
\delta>0
such that

\sup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\sup_{x\in[-h,h]}\,\mathbb{E}\left(|r_{i}(h)|^{2+\delta}\mid X_{i}=x\right)<\infty.

These uniform boundedness assumptions are needed to find upper bounds of the means irrespective of the value of the running variable or the number of observations. In particular, it allows us to conclude integrability over
x\in[-h,h]
later on.
Please note that if we consider the simpler case when the outcome variable has the form
Y_{i}=V_{i}^{\top}\overline{\theta}+Z_{i}^{\top}\overline{\gamma}+\varepsilon_{i}
for some fixed
h
with, e.g., zero-mean, normal
\varepsilon_{i}
independent of
X_{i}
,
Z_{i}
, then the third statement of this assumption is satisfied due to
r_{i}(h)=\varepsilon_{i}
. Moreover, if we additionally assume boundedness of the covariates, also the first two statements of the assumption are immediately true.

Assumption 7

(Equi-continuity)
.

Let
\sigma_{l},\sigma_{r}>0
be finite numbers such that

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\sup_{\lambda\in[0,1]}\left|\mathbb{E}\left(r_{i}(h)^{2}\mid X_{i}=\lambda h\right)-\sigma_{r}^{2}\right|=0,

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\sup_{\lambda\in[0,1]}\left|\mathbb{E}\left(r_{i}(h)^{2}\mid X_{i}=-\lambda h\right)-\sigma_{l}^{2}\right|=0.

The existence of those limits will be used to verify the convergence condition of Lyapunov’s Central Limit Theorem, when proving the convergence to the standard normal distribution. Again, this assumption is satisfied if we examine an outcome of the form
Y_{i}=V_{i}^{\top}\overline{\theta}+Z_{i}^{\top}\overline{\gamma}+\varepsilon_{i}
as described previously.

3.2
Main Theorem

The representation of the bias and the variance that are used in the theorem include two coefficients which are dependent on the kernel function. After defining those, we will finally state the theorem.

Definition 8
.

Define

\mathcal{C_{B}}:=\frac{2\left(K_{+}^{(2)}\right)^{2}-2K_{+}^{(1)}K_{+}^{(3)}}{K_{+}^{(2)}-2\left(K_{+}^{(1)}\right)^{2}}

and

\mathcal{C_{S}}:=\frac{\left(K^{2}\right)_{+}^{(0)}\left(K_{+}^{(2)}\right)^{2}+\left(K^{2}\right)^{(2)}_{+}\left(K_{+}^{(1)}\right)^{2}-2\left(K^{2}\right)^{(1)}_{+}K_{+}^{(2)}K_{+}^{(1)}}{\left[\left(K_{+}^{(1)}\right)^{2}-\frac{1}{2}K_{+}^{(2)}\right]^{2}}.

Theorem 1
.

Suppose that the assumptions of Section
3.1
hold. There are sequences
\mathcal{B}_{n}
and
\mathcal{S}_{n}
with

\begin{split}\mathcal{B}_{n}=\frac{\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{B}}}{2}\left({\mu^{\prime\prime}_{\tilde{Y}}}_{+}-{\mu^{\prime\prime}_{\tilde{Y}}}_{-}\right)+o(1),\quad\mathcal{S}_{n}^{2}=\frac{\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{S}}}{f_{X}(0)}\left({\sigma_{\tilde{Y}}^{2}}_{+}+{\sigma_{\tilde{Y}}^{2}}_{-}\right)+o(1)\end{split}

(1)

such that the following statement is true:

\begin{split}\frac{\sqrt{nh}(\hat{\tau}_{h}-\tau_{Y}-h^{2}\mathcal{B}_{n})}{\mathcal{S}_{n}}\xrightarrow{d}\mathcal{N}(0,1).\end{split}

The proof of the theorem consists of two main steps. The first one is to deduce a representation of the bias by using the result of Chapter
4.1
. In the second main step, we show the asymptotic normality in the covariate-adjusted case and derive a representation of the variance, which is done in Chapter
4.3
. In addition, just some extra effort is required in order to exactly obtain the representation of the bias and variance as claimed above, which is covered by the calculations in Chapters
4.2
and
4.4
.

Proof 3.2

(Proof of Theorem
1
)
.

We start by showing that it is enough to consider the case when
\mu_{Z}
is continuously differentiable. Using the differentiablity and continuity statements about
\mu_{Z}
in Assumption
3
, it can be verified that
\mu_{\tilde{Z}}
is continuous and three times one-sided differentiable at zero. Also, we can calculate that
\mu_{\tilde{Z}}(0)=0
and
\mu^{\prime}_{\tilde{Z}+}=\mu^{\prime}_{\tilde{Z}-}=0
. In particular,
\mu^{\prime}_{\tilde{Z}}
can be continuously extended in zero. Similarly we argue that
\mu_{\tilde{Z}\tilde{Z}^{\top}}
and
\mu_{\tilde{Z}Y}
are one time one-sided differentiable at zero.

By defining an estimator which is based on
V_{i}
and
\tilde{Z_{i}}
, namely

\displaystyle\left(\check{\theta}_{n}(h),\check{\gamma}_{n}(h)\right)=\argmin_{(\theta,\gamma)\in\mathbb{R}^{p+4}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}K_{h}(X_{i})\left(Y_{i}-V_{i}^{\top}\theta-\tilde{Z_{i}}^{\top}\gamma\right)^{2},

we can compare it to our normal estimator based on
V_{i}
and
Z_{i}
:

\displaystyle\big(\hat{\theta}_{n}(h),\hat{\gamma}_{n}(h)\big)

\displaystyle=\argmin_{(\theta,\gamma)\in\mathbb{R}^{p+4}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}K_{h}(X_{i})\left(Y_{i}-V_{i}^{\top}\theta-Z_{i}^{\top}\gamma\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=\argmin_{(\theta,\gamma)\in\mathbb{R}^{p+4}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}K_{h}(X_{i})\left(Y_{i}-V_{i}^{\top}(\theta+M_{n}\gamma)-\tilde{Z_{i}}^{\top}\gamma\right)^{2}.

In case of uniqueness of those minimizing parameters, they obviously have to be identical and we can conclude:

\check{\gamma}_{n}(h)=\hat{\gamma}_{n}(h)\text{\hskip 10.22217ptand\hskip 10.22217pt}\check{\theta}_{n}(h)=\hat{\theta}_{n}(h)+M_{n}\hat{\gamma}_{n}(h).

If multiple minimizing parameters exist, we just state that there are parameters such that the above identification holds.
In an analogous way, we also have

\left(\check{\theta}_{0}(h),\check{\gamma}_{0}(h)\right):=\argmin_{(\theta,\gamma)\in\mathbb{R}^{p+4}}\mathbb{E}\left(K_{h}(X_{i})\left(Y_{i}-V_{i}^{\top}\theta-\tilde{Z_{i}}^{\top}\gamma\right)^{2}\right)

and

\left(\theta_{0}(h),\gamma_{0}(h)\right)=\argmin_{(\theta,\gamma)\in\mathbb{R}^{p+4}}\mathbb{E}\left(K_{h}(X_{i})\left(Y_{i}-V_{i}^{\top}(\theta+M_{n}\gamma)-\tilde{Z_{i}}^{\top}\gamma\right)^{2}\right).

By providing the same argument as above, we obtain

\check{\gamma}_{0}(h)=\gamma_{0}(h)\text{\hskip 10.22217ptand\hskip 10.22217pt}\check{\theta}_{0}(h)=\theta_{0}(h)+M_{n}\gamma_{0}(h).

As we are only interested in the effect of the treatment variable, we proceed by calculating the second entry of those parameters:

\check{\theta}_{n}^{(2)}(h)=\hat{\theta}_{n}^{(2)}(h)+(M_{n})_{2}\hat{\gamma}_{n}(h)=\hat{\theta}_{n}^{(2)}(h),

whereby the second equality follows from the fact that the second row of
M_{n}
is zero by definition. We can now substitute the estimators in the expression which we want to examine asymptotically:

\sqrt{\frac{nh}{\mathcal{S}_{n}^{2}}}\left(\hat{\theta}_{n}^{(2)}(h)-\check{\theta}_{0}^{(2)}(h)\right)=\sqrt{\frac{nh}{\mathcal{S}_{n}^{2}}}\left(\check{\theta}_{n}^{(2)}(h)-\check{\theta}_{0}^{(2)}(h)\right).

(2)

We want to apply Proposition
4.4
with
Z_{i}=\tilde{Z_{i}}
now. Indeed, all necessary conditions are satisfied due to Assumption
1
to
5
and the above regularity considerations on
\mu_{\tilde{Z}}
. Indeed, Proposition
4.4
gives us
\check{\theta}_{0}^{(2)}=\tau_{Y}+h^{2}\check{\mathcal{B}}_{n}
whereby

\check{\mathcal{B}}_{n}=\frac{\mathcal{C_{B}}}{2}\left({\mu^{\prime\prime}_{Y}}_{+}-{\mu^{\prime\prime}_{Y}}_{-}-\left(\mu^{\prime\prime}_{\tilde{Z}+}-\mu^{\prime\prime}_{\tilde{Z}-}\right)^{\top}\check{\beta}_{n}\right)+o(1)

with

\check{\beta}_{n}=\mathbb{E}\left(K_{h}(X_{i})\tilde{Z_{i}}\tilde{Z}_{i}^{\top}\right)^{-1}\mathbb{E}\left(K_{h}(X_{i})\tilde{Z_{i}}Y_{i}\right).

By Proposition
4.6
, which converts the representation of the bias, we can conclude
\check{\mathcal{B}}_{n}=\mathcal{B}_{n}
, leading to

\check{\theta}_{0}^{(2)}=\tau_{Y}+h^{2}\mathcal{B}_{n}.

Also, we know that
\hat{\theta}_{n}^{(2)}=\hat{\tau}_{h}
by definition. Inserting these expressions in (
2
) shows that it is enough to examine the asymptotic behavior of

\sqrt{\frac{nh}{\mathcal{S}_{n}^{2}}}\left(\check{\theta}_{n}^{(2)}(h)-\check{\theta}_{0}^{(2)}(h)\right)

We have

\begin{split}\check{r}_{i}(h)&=Y_{i}-V_{i}^{\top}\check{\theta}_{0}(h)-\tilde{Z_{i}}^{\top}\check{\gamma}_{0}(h)\\
&=Y_{i}-V_{i}^{\top}\big(\theta_{0}(h)+M_{n}\gamma_{0}(h)\big)-\left(Z_{i}-M_{n}^{\top}V_{i}\right)^{\top}\gamma_{0}(h)\\
&=Y_{i}-V_{i}^{\top}\theta_{0}(h)-Z_{i}\gamma_{0}(h)\\
&=r_{i}(h).\end{split}

(3)

As a consequence, we can replace
r_{i}(h)
by
\check{r}_{i}(h)
in Assumption

and
7
and they still hold. Thus, Proposition
4.9
can be applied with
Z_{i}=\tilde{Z_{i}}
since its assumptions are satisfied by using Assumption

to
7
from Section

. This gives us that

\sqrt{\frac{nh}{\check{\mathcal{S}_{n}^{2}}}}\left(\check{\theta}_{n}^{(2)}(h)-\check{\theta}_{0}^{(2)}(h)\right)\xrightarrow{d}\mathcal{N}(0,1)

whereby

\check{\mathcal{S}_{n}^{2}}=\frac{1}{h}\mathbb{E}\left(K\left(\frac{X_{i}}{h}\right)^{2}(w^{\top}V_{i})^{2}\check{r}_{i}(h)^{2}\right).

From Proposition
4.11
, which converts the representation of the variance, we know that
\check{\mathcal{S}_{n}^{2}}=\mathcal{S}_{n}^{2}
, directly leading to

\frac{\sqrt{nh}(\hat{\tau}_{h}-\tau_{Y}-h^{2}\mathcal{B}_{n})}{\mathcal{S}_{n}}\xrightarrow{d}\mathcal{N}(0,1)

by the above considerations. This proves the assertion of the theorem.

3.3
Consequences

In the following, we consider the three main implications that our approach of proving the above theorem has. In particular, we discuss whether additional covariates actually enhance our estimator and its MSE. Therefore, similarly to
Calonico et al 2019
and
Kreiß and Rothe 2021
, we want to study the bias and variance in comparison to those of the baseline estimator without covariates. Moreover, we conduct a sensitivity analysis in case of a potentially manipulated forcing variable.

3.3.1
Discussion of Variance

We want to show that the variance of the covariate-adjusted estimator is less or equal, but does not exceed the variance of the baseline estimator under the condition that the left- and right-sided limits of the covariance matrix of
Z_{i}
are invertible, i.e. we need the additional assumption that

\lim_{x\searrow 0}\left(\mathrm{Cov}\left(Z_{i}^{(k)},Z_{i}^{(l)}\mid X_{i}=x\right)\right)_{k,l\in\{1,...,p\}}\text{ and }\lim_{x\nearrow 0}\left(\mathrm{Cov}\left(Z_{i}^{(k)},Z_{i}^{(l)}\mid X_{i}=x\right)\right)_{k,l\in\{1,...,p\}}

are invertible. With this condition being satisfied, the idea of the proof is to show that
\tilde{\gamma}
minimizes the function

\gamma\mapsto\lim_{x\searrow 0}\mathrm{Var}\left(Y_{i}-Z_{i}^{\top}\gamma\mid X_{i}=x\right)+\lim_{x\nearrow 0}\mathrm{Var}\left(Y_{i}-Z_{i}^{\top}\gamma\mid X_{i}=x\right)

leading to

{\sigma_{\tilde{Y}}^{2}}_{+}+{\sigma_{\tilde{Y}}^{2}}_{-}\leq\sigma^{2}_{Y+}+\sigma^{2}_{Y-}

(4)

whereby the expression on the right-hand side is the leading term of the baseline estimator’s variance.

This particularly shows that the variance does not worsen in comparison to the case without covariates, and is a stronger statement than the one deduced by
Calonico et al 2019
. In fact, they state that in their setting, (
4
) is just satisfied under the condition
\tilde{\gamma}=\gamma_{Y+}=\gamma_{Y-}
, whereby
\gamma_{Y+}=\left(\sigma_{Z+}^{2}\right)^{-1}\sigma_{ZY+}^{2}
and
\gamma_{Y-}=\left(\sigma_{Z-}^{2}\right)^{-1}\sigma_{ZY-}^{2}
. Yet, there are examples of RDDs without this condition being satisfied. For instance, we can assume

•

\mu_{Z+}=\mu_{Z-}
,
\mu_{ZZ^{\top}+}=\mu_{ZZ^{\top}-}
,

•

p,q
polynomials with
p(0)\neq q(0)
,
0\neq w\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
and

•

Y_{i}=T_{i}(p(X_{i})+Z_{i}^{\top}2w)+(1-T_{i})(q(X_{i})+Z_{i}^{\top}w)
.

Then
\tilde{\gamma}=\frac{3}{2}w\neq 2w=\gamma_{Y+}
, which can be verified by calculating
\sigma_{Z+}^{2}=\sigma_{Z-}^{2}
,
\sigma_{ZY+}^{2}=2\sigma_{Z+}^{2}w
and
\sigma_{ZY-}^{2}=\sigma_{Z+}^{2}w
. Despite this condition not being satisfied,
\mathrm{Var}(\tilde{Y}+)+\mathrm{Var}(\tilde{Y}-)\leq\mathrm{Var}(Y+)+\mathrm{Var}(Y-)
still holds true, which is a consequence of
\sigma_{\tilde{Y}+}^{2}+\sigma_{\tilde{Y}-}^{2}=\sigma_{Y+}^{2}+\sigma_{Y-}^{2}-\frac{9}{2}w^{\top}\sigma_{Z+}^{2}w
and
\sigma_{Z+}^{2}
being positive semidefinite.

Next, we show that
\tilde{\gamma}
minimizes the variance under the above invertibility assumption, nonetheless irrespective of the condition
\tilde{\gamma}=\gamma_{Y+}=\gamma_{Y-}
.

Proof 3.3

(Proof (that
\tilde{\gamma}
minimizes the function).)
.

We have

\displaystyle\nabla_{\gamma}\left(\lim_{x\searrow 0}\mathrm{Var}\left(Y_{i}-Z_{i}^{\top}\gamma\mid X_{i}=x\right)+\lim_{x\nearrow 0}\mathrm{Var}\left(Y_{i}-Z_{i}^{\top}\gamma\mid X_{i}=x\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\;

\displaystyle 2\left(\left(\sigma^{2}_{Z-}+\sigma^{2}_{Z+}\right)\gamma-\left(\sigma^{2}_{ZY-}+\sigma^{2}_{ZY+}\right)\right).

This gradient vanishes when inserting
\tilde{\gamma}
. Now, it remains to show that the Hessian matrix is positive definite. In fact, we obtain

\begin{split}&\mathrm{H}_{\gamma\mapsto\left(\lim_{x\searrow 0}\mathrm{Var}\left(Y_{i}-Z_{i}^{\top}\gamma\mid X_{i}=x\right)+\lim_{x\nearrow 0}\mathrm{Var}\left(Y_{i}-Z_{i}^{\top}\gamma\mid X_{i}=x\right)\right)}\\
\equiv\;&2\lim_{x\searrow 0}\left(\mathrm{Cov}\left(Z_{i}^{(k)},Z_{i}^{(l)}\mid X_{i}=x\right)\right)_{k,l\in\{1,...,p\}}\\
&+2\lim_{x\nearrow 0}\left(\mathrm{Cov}\left(Z_{i}^{(k)},Z_{i}^{(l)}\mid X_{i}=x\right)\right)_{k,l\in\{1,...,p\}}.\end{split}

(5)

We consider the first summand, and obtain for
y\in\mathbb{R}^{p}\setminus\{0\}
that

\displaystyle y^{\top}\lim_{x\searrow 0}\left(\mathrm{Cov}\left(Z_{i}^{(k)},Z_{i}^{(l)}\mid X_{i}=x\right)\right)_{k,l\in\{1,...,p\}}y

\displaystyle=\;

\displaystyle\lim_{x\searrow 0}\mathbb{E}\left(\left(\sum_{k=1}^{p}y_{k}\left(Z_{i}^{(k)}-\mathbb{E}\left(Z_{i}^{(k)}\mid X_{i}=x\right)\right)\right)^{2}\;\,\vrule\;X_{i}=x\right)\overset{(*)}{\geq}0.

Hence, the matrix
\lim_{x\searrow 0}\left(\mathrm{Cov}\left(Z_{i}^{(k)},Z_{i}^{(l)}\mid X_{i}=x\right)\right)_{k,l\in\{1,...,p\}}
is positive semidefinite. Consequently, we can apply the Cholesky decomposition to get

\lim_{x\searrow 0}\left(\mathrm{Cov}\left(Z_{i}^{(k)},Z_{i}^{(l)}\mid X_{i}=x\right)\right)_{k,l\in\{1,...,p\}}=L^{\top}DL

for some orthogonal lower triangular matrix
L\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}
and a diagonal matrix
D\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}
having the real eigenvalues on its diagonal. Note that those eigenvalues are non-negative since the matrix is positive semidefinite and unequal to zero since we assumed the limit of the covariance matrix to be invertible. For this reason,
D
as well as
D^{\frac{1}{2}}
are invertible, whereby
D^{\frac{1}{2}}
is the diagonal matrix obtained by taking the square root of the diagonal entries of
D
. Therefore,
D^{\frac{1}{2}}L
is invertible, which means that

y^{\top}L^{\top}DLy=y^{\top}L^{\top}D^{\frac{1}{2}}D^{\frac{1}{2}}Ly=(D^{\frac{1}{2}}Ly)^{\top}D^{\frac{1}{2}}Ly=\left\lVert D^{\frac{1}{2}}Ly\right\rVert_{2}^{2}=0\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad y=0.

Therefore, even a strict inequality holds in
(*)
, which proves that the matrix is positive definite. In an analogous way, we can prove that also the second summand in (
5
) is positive definite, leading to the whole Hessian being positive definite. Finally, we can conclude that
\tilde{\gamma}
is a minimum.

3.3.2

The first term of the bias representation can be written as

{\mu^{\prime\prime}_{\tilde{Y}}}_{+}-{\mu^{\prime\prime}_{\tilde{Y}}}_{-}=\left({\mu^{\prime\prime}_{Y}}_{+}-{\mu^{\prime\prime}_{Y}}_{-}\right)-\left(\mu^{\prime\prime}_{Z+}-\mu^{\prime\prime}_{Z-}\right)^{\top}\tilde{\gamma}.

3.3.3
Sensitivity Analysis

In this section we show how Theorem
1
can be applied in case
\tau_{Y}
does not admit a causal interpretation. Examples for confounding through manipulation of the forcing variable
X_{i}
are given in, e.g.,
Gerard et al 2020
. In that paper, the manipulation is reflected as a potential discontinuity of the density of the forcing variable by dividing the units in two different categories: so called
always-assigned
units, which always have a realization of the forcing variable greater or equal the cutoff (i.e. we have a one-sided manipulation), and
potentially-assigned
units, which behave according to the standard RD framework. Let us therefore suppose that there are potential outcomes
Y_{i}(t)
with continuous
\mathbb{E}(Y_{i}(t)\mid X_{i}=x)
for
t\in\{0,1\}
but that we observe

Y_{i}=T_{i}Y_{i}(1)+(1-T_{i})Y_{i}(0)+\varepsilon_{i},

where we suppose that
\Delta:=\lim_{x\searrow 0}\mathbb{E}(\varepsilon\mid X_{i}=x)-\lim_{x\nearrow 0}\mathbb{E}(\varepsilon_{i}\mid X_{i}=x)\neq 0
. Denote furthermore
\tau_{0}:=\mathbb{E}(Y_{i}(1)\mid X_{i}=0)-\mathbb{E}(Y_{i}(0)\mid X_{i}=0)
. In this case Theorem
1
still applies with
\tau_{Y}=\tau_{0}+\Delta
. We call
\tau_{0}
the effect of interest and
\Delta
the level of confounding. Suppose our interest lies in understanding if
\tau_{0}>\overline{\tau}
for some given
\overline{\tau}>0
. While it is not possible to test this hypothesis directly, Theorem
1
still allows to conduct a sensitivity analysis as in
Jin et al 2023
. Intuitively speaking, the idea is to test for statements of the type, either
\tau_{0}>\overline{\tau}
or the confounding level is larger than
\hat{\delta}_{n}
. If the confounding level
\hat{\delta}_{n}
is unplausibly high, one would have to accept that the effect
\tau_{0}
is larger than
\overline{\tau}
. More formally, consider for
\delta>0
the hypotheses

H_{0}(\delta):\quad\tau_{0}\leq\overline{\tau}\textrm{ and }|\Delta|\leq\delta.

These hypotheses formalize the previously mentioned intuition: we reject
H_{0}(\delta)
at level
\alpha\in(0,1)
either if the true effect is larger than
\overline{\tau}
or if the level of confounding is larger than
\delta
. Our interest is finding the largest
\delta
for which
H_{0}(\delta)
would be rejected. A natural strategy is to test all hypotheses
H_{0}(\delta)
and let
\mathcal{R}:=\{\delta>0:\,H_{0}(\delta)\textrm{ rejected}\}
. Then,
\hat{\delta}_{n}:=\sup\mathcal{R}
denotes the largest hypothesis which would be rejected on confidence level
\alpha
.
Jin et al 2023
argue that this strategy does not run into multiple testing issues. We explain their argument in our setting.

First, we specify confidence intervals for
\tau_{0}
under the assumption that the level of confounding is smaller than
\delta>0
. Let
\hat{\mathcal{S}_{n}}
be an estimator for
\mathcal{S}_{n}
such that
\hat{\mathcal{S}_{n}}/\mathcal{S}_{n}\overset{P}{\to}1
and suppose undersmoothing, i.e.,
nh^{5}\to 0
. In this case we obtain from Theorem
1
that

\frac{\sqrt{nh}}{\hat{\mathcal{S}_{n}}}\left(\hat{\tau}_{h}-\tau_{0}-\Delta\right)\overset{d}{\to}\mathcal{N}(0,1).

As an alternative to undersmoothing, one could certainly handle the bias differently as, e.g., in
Calonico et al 2019
or
Armstrong and Kolesár 2018
, but we would like to present an approach that is compatible with Theorem
1
. Let
q_{1-\alpha}
denote the
(1-\alpha)
-quantile of the standard normal distribution. Then, we obtain under the assumption
|\Delta|\leq\delta
that

\displaystyle\limsup_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(\tau_{0}\geq\hat{\tau}_{h}-\delta-\frac{\hat{\mathcal{S}_{n}}}{\sqrt{nh}}q_{1-\alpha}\right)\geq\limsup_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(\tau_{0}\geq\hat{\tau}_{h}-\Delta-\frac{\hat{\mathcal{S}_{n}}}{\sqrt{nh}}q_{1-\alpha}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\limsup_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(\frac{\sqrt{nh}}{\hat{\mathcal{S}_{n}}}\left(\hat{\tau}_{h}-\tau_{0}-\Delta\right)\leq q_{1-\alpha}\right)=1-\alpha.

Therefore,
C_{n}(\delta):=(\hat{\tau}_{h}-\delta-q_{1-\alpha}\hat{\mathcal{S}_{n}}/\sqrt{nh},\infty)
is a confidence interval for
\tau_{0}
of level
1-\alpha
under the assumption
|\Delta|\leq\delta
. This directly yields a way of testing each hypothesis
H_{0}(\delta)
at level
\alpha
: reject
H_{0}(\delta)
if
\overline{\tau}\notin C_{n}(\delta)
. Using this test, we obtain,

\mathcal{R}:=\left(0,\hat{\tau}_{h}-\overline{\tau}-q_{1-\alpha}\frac{\hat{\mathcal{S}_{n}}}{\sqrt{nh}}\right].

Let furthermore
\mathcal{H}_{0}
be the set of all true hypotheses, i.e., if
\tau_{0}>\overline{\tau}
, then
\mathcal{H}_{0}=\emptyset
and in case
\tau_{0}\leq\overline{\tau}
, we have
\mathcal{H}_{0}=[|\Delta|,\infty)
. We can show (as in
Jin et al 2023
) that in case of no true effect, i.e.
\tau_{0}\leq\overline{\tau}
, we reject no
H_{0}(\delta)
with an unreasonably large
\delta
. In fact, the probability of rejecting a true hypothesis is bounded from above by
\alpha
: if
\tau_{0}\leq\overline{\tau}
, we get

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{R}\cap\mathcal{H}_{0}\neq\emptyset)=

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\hat{\tau}_{h}-\overline{\tau}-q_{1-\alpha}\frac{\hat{\mathcal{S}_{n}}}{\sqrt{nh}}\geq|\Delta|\right)\leq\mathbb{P}\left(\hat{\tau}_{h}-\tau_{0}-q_{1-\alpha}\frac{\hat{\mathcal{S}_{n}}}{\sqrt{nh}}\geq\Delta\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\frac{\sqrt{nh}}{\hat{\mathcal{S}_{n}}}\left(\hat{\tau}_{h}-\tau_{0}-\Delta\right)\geq q_{1-\alpha}\right)\to\alpha.

Hence, we conclude that, if there is no large effect
\tau_{0}>\overline{\tau}
, we reject only with probability
\alpha
a hypothesis which is in fact true. Therefore we may say that if there is no effect larger than
\overline{\tau}
, then we have to accept a level of confounding

|\Delta|\geq\hat{\delta}_{n}:=\hat{\tau}_{h}-\overline{\tau}-q_{1-\alpha}\frac{\hat{\mathcal{S}_{n}}}{\sqrt{nh}}.

(23)

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{h}\mathbb{E}\left(K\left(\frac{X_{i}}{h}\right)^{2}\nu\left(\frac{X_{i}}{h}\right)^{2}r_{i}(h)^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\;

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{h}\mathbb{E}\left(K\left(\frac{X_{i}}{h}\right)^{2}\nu\left(\frac{X_{i}}{h}\right)^{2}\mathbb{E}(r_{i}(h)^{2}\mid X_{i})\right)

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}K(y)^{2}\nu(y)^{2}\mathbb{E}(r_{i}(h)^{2}\mid X_{i}=yh)f_{X}(yh)\,dy

\displaystyle=\;

\displaystyle\int_{-1}^{0}K(y)^{2}\nu(y)^{2}\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{E}(r_{i}(h)^{2}\mid X_{i}=yh)f_{X}(yh)\,dy

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{1}K(y)^{2}\nu(y)^{2}\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{E}(r_{i}(h)^{2}\mid X_{i}=yh)f_{X}(yh)\,dy

whereby we used the Theorem of Dominated Convergence to move the limit inside the integral. For both
1\geq y>0
and
-1\leq y<0
,

exists directly due to the assumptions stated in (
13

The upper bound in (

since

\displaystyle\frac{1}{h}\mathbb{E}\left(K\left(\frac{X_{i}}{h}\right)^{2+\delta}\left|\nu\left(\frac{X_{i}}{h}\right)\right|^{2+\delta}\left|r_{i}(h)\right|^{2+\delta}\right)

\displaystyle=\;

\displaystyle\int_{-1}^{1}K(y)^{2+\delta}|\nu(y)|^{2+\delta}\mathbb{E}(|r_{i}(h)|^{2+\delta}\mid X_{i}=yh)f_{X}(yh)\,dy.

\displaystyle=\;

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{n^{-\frac{2+\delta}{2}}h^{-\frac{2+\delta}{2}}\mathbb{E}\left(K\left(\frac{X_{i}}{h}\right)^{2+\delta}\left|\nu\left(\frac{X_{i}}{h}\right)\right|^{2+\delta}\left|r_{i}(h)\right|^{2+\delta}\right)}{\mathcal{S}_{n}^{2+\delta}}

\displaystyle=\;

which is condition (

\displaystyle\frac{1}{M^{2}}\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}\left((K_{h}(X_{i})A_{i}-\mathbb{E}(K_{h}(X_{i})A_{i}))^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\;

\displaystyle\frac{1}{M^{2}}\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{E}\left((K_{h}(X_{i})A_{i})^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\;

\displaystyle\frac{1}{M^{2}}\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{E}\left(K_{h}(X_{i})^{2}\mathbb{E}\left(A_{i}^{2}\mid X_{i}\right)\right)

\displaystyle\leq\;

\displaystyle\sup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\sup_{x\in[-h,h]}\mathbb{E}\left(A_{i}^{2}\mid X_{i}=x\right)\frac{1}{M^{2}}\frac{1}{nh}\int_{-1}^{1}K\left(y\right)^{2}f_{X}(yh)\,dy=O\left(\frac{1}{nhM^{2}}\right),

whereby
\int_{-1}^{1}K\left(y\right)^{2}f_{X}(yh)\,dy=O(1)
due to
(K^{2})^{(0)}<\infty
,
h\to 0
and the continuity of
f_{X}
in a neighborhood around zero.
Overall, this leads to

P\left(|S_{n}-\mathbb{E}(S_{n})|>M\right)\leq\frac{C}{nhM^{2}}

for some
C>0
. Given an arbitrary
\varepsilon>0
, choose
M
large enough such that
\frac{C}{M^{2}}<\varepsilon
. Then,

P(\sqrt{nh}|S_{n}-\mathbb{E}(S_{n})|>M)=P\left(|S_{n}-\mathbb{E}(S_{n})|>\frac{M}{\sqrt{nh}}\right)\leq\frac{C}{M^{2}}<\varepsilon,

which means that
S_{n}-\mathbb{E}(S_{n})=O_{P}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{nh}}\right)
.

Lemma 6.27
.

Let
K
be a kernel which is compactly supported on
[-1,1]
and satisfies
(K^{2})^{(0)}<\infty
. Let
f_{X}
be continuous in a neighborhood around zero. Furthermore, let
h\to 0,nh\to\infty
as
n\to\infty
and

\sup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\sup_{x\in[-h,h]}\mathbb{E}(r_{i}(h)^{2}\mid X_{i}=x)<\infty.

(40)

Then,

\frac{1}{n}\mathbf{V}^{\top}\mathbf{K}_{h}\mathbf{r}(h)=O_{P}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{nh}}\right).

Proof 6.28
.

We want to prove this row-wise. Let
a\in\{1,...,4\}
, then

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left(\frac{1}{h}K\left(\frac{X_{i}}{h}\right)^{2}V_{i,a}^{2}r_{i}(h)^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left(\mathbb{E}\left(\frac{1}{h}K\left(\frac{X_{i}}{h}\right)^{2}V_{i,a}^{2}r_{i}(h)^{2}\;\,\vrule\;X_{i}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left(\frac{1}{h}K\left(\frac{X_{i}}{h}\right)^{2}V_{i,a}^{2}\mathbb{E}(r_{i}(h)^{2}\mid X_{i})\right)=O(1)

due to assumption (
40
) and our assumptions imposed on
f_{X}
and
K
. Using Markov’s inequality, we obtain for every
\varepsilon>0
that

\displaystyle P\left(\left|\frac{1}{n}\mathbf{V}^{\top}_{\cdot a}\mathbf{K}_{h}\mathbf{r}(h)\right|>M\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{M^{2}}\mathbb{E}\left(\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{h}K\left(\frac{X_{i}}{h}\right)V_{i,a}r_{i}(h)\right)^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{nhM^{2}}\mathbb{E}\left(\frac{1}{h}K\left(\frac{X_{i}}{h}\right)^{2}V_{i,a}^{2}r_{i}(h)^{2}\right)=O\left(\frac{1}{nhM^{2}}\right),

leading to

\displaystyle P\left(\left|\frac{1}{n}\mathbf{V}^{\top}_{\cdot a}\mathbf{K}_{h}\mathbf{r}(h)\right|>M\right)\leq\frac{C}{nhM^{2}}

for some constant
C>0
. Now we can conclude with the same argument as in the proof of Lemma
6.25
.

Lemma 6.29
.

Let all assumptions of Lemma
5.23
hold. Also, let
K
be a kernel which is compactly supported on
[-1,1]
and satisfies
K^{(4)},(K^{2})^{(0)}<\infty
. Additionally, define

M:=I-\mathbf{K}_{h}^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathbf{Z}(\mathbf{Z}^{\top}\mathbf{K}_{h}\mathbf{Z})^{-1}\mathbf{Z}^{\top}\mathbf{K}_{h}^{\frac{1}{2}}

and suppose that for all
k,l\in\{1,...,p\}

\begin{split}&\sup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\sup_{x\in[-h,h]}\,\mathbb{E}\left(\left(Z_{i}^{(k)}r_{i}(h)\right)^{2}\;\,\vrule\;X_{i}=x\right)<\infty,\\
&\sup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\sup_{x\in[-h,h]}\,\mathbb{E}\left(\left(Z_{i}^{(k)}Z_{i}^{(l)}\right)^{2}\;\,\vrule\;X_{i}=x\right)<\infty.\end{split}

(41)

Then

\frac{1}{n}\mathbf{V}^{\top}\mathbf{K}_{h}^{\frac{1}{2}}M\mathbf{K}_{h}^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathbf{V}=f_{X}(0)\kappa(K)+o_{P}(1)

and

\frac{1}{n}\mathbf{V}^{\top}\mathbf{K}_{h}^{\frac{1}{2}}M\mathbf{K}_{h}^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathbf{r}(h)=\frac{1}{n}\mathbf{V}^{\top}\mathbf{K}_{h}\mathbf{r}(h)+o_{P}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{nh}}\right).

Proof 6.30
.

The idea of the proof is to use Lemma
6.25
multiple times. Indeed, we can do this by calculating

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\mathbf{V}^{\top}\mathbf{K}_{h}^{\frac{1}{2}}M\mathbf{K}_{h}^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathbf{V}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\mathbf{V}^{\top}\mathbf{K}_{h}^{\frac{1}{2}}\left(I-\mathbf{K}_{h}^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathbf{Z}(\mathbf{Z}^{\top}\mathbf{K}_{h}\mathbf{Z})^{-1}\mathbf{Z}^{\top}\mathbf{K}_{h}^{\frac{1}{2}}\right)\mathbf{K}_{h}^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathbf{V}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\mathbf{V}^{\top}\mathbf{K}_{h}\mathbf{V}-\frac{1}{n}\mathbf{V}^{\top}\mathbf{K}_{h}\mathbf{Z}(\mathbf{Z}^{\top}\mathbf{K}_{h}\mathbf{Z})^{-1}\mathbf{Z}^{\top}\mathbf{K}_{h}\mathbf{V}

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\frac{1}{n}\mathbf{V}^{\top}\mathbf{K}_{h}\mathbf{V}}_{(1)}-\underbrace{\left(\frac{1}{n}\mathbf{V}^{\top}\mathbf{K}_{h}\mathbf{Z}\right)\left(\frac{1}{n}\mathbf{Z}^{\top}\mathbf{K}_{h}\mathbf{Z}\right)^{-1}\left(\frac{1}{n}\mathbf{Z}^{\top}\mathbf{K}_{h}\mathbf{V}\right)}_{(2)}.

Concerning term
(2)
, we can now estimate each of the three factors individually. For the first factor we obtain

\frac{1}{n}\mathbf{V}^{\top}\mathbf{K}_{h}\mathbf{Z}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}K_{h}(X_{i})V_{i}Z_{i}^{\top},

leading to the fact that we can consider this expression entry-wise and, respectively, set
A_{i}
of Lemma
6.25
to every entry of
V_{i}Z_{i}^{\top}
. The necessary assumption (
39
) for that is implied by (
41
) as we have for
m\in\{1,...,4\}
,
k\in\{1,...,p\}
and
u(x):=(1,\mathds{1}(x\geq 0),x/h,x\mathds{1}(x\geq 0)/h)^{\top}
that

\displaystyle\sup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\sup_{x\in[-h,h]}\,\mathbb{E}\left(\left(V_{i}^{(m)}Z_{i}^{(k)}\right)^{2}\;\,\vrule\;X_{i}=x\right)

\displaystyle=\;

\displaystyle\sup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\sup_{x\in[-h,h]}\,u^{(m)}(x)^{2}\mathbb{E}\left(\left(Z_{i}^{(k)}\right)^{2}\;\,\vrule\;X_{i}=x\right)

\displaystyle\leq\;

\displaystyle\sup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\sup_{x\in[-h,h]}\,\mathbb{E}\left(\left(Z_{i}^{(k)}\right)^{2}\;\,\vrule\;X_{i}=x\right)<\infty.

As result, we obtain

\frac{1}{n}\mathbf{V}^{\top}\mathbf{K}_{h}\mathbf{Z}=\mathbb{E}(K_{h}(X_{i})V_{i}Z_{i}^{\top})+O_{P}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{nh}}\right).

(42)

In an analogous way, the third factor results in

\frac{1}{n}\mathbf{Z}^{\top}\mathbf{K}_{h}\mathbf{V}=\mathbb{E}(K_{h}(X_{i})Z_{i}V_{i}^{\top})+O_{P}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{nh}}\right).

(43)

For the second factor we have

\begin{split}\left(\frac{1}{n}\mathbf{Z}^{\top}\mathbf{K}_{h}\mathbf{Z}\right)^{-1}&=\left(\mathbb{E}(K_{h}(X_{i})Z_{i}Z_{i}^{\top})+O_{P}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{nh}}\right)\right)^{-1}\\
&\overset{(*)}{=}\mathbb{E}(K_{h}(X_{i})Z_{i}Z_{i}^{\top})^{-1}+O_{P}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{nh}}\right),\end{split}

(44)

whereby
(*)
can be concluded using assumption (
36
) and
\sqrt{nh}\to\infty
.

Overall, taking the equations (
42
), (
43
) and (
44
) together,
(2)
evaluates as follows:

\displaystyle\left(\frac{1}{n}\mathbf{V}^{\top}\mathbf{K}_{h}\mathbf{Z}\right)\left(\frac{1}{n}\mathbf{Z}^{\top}\mathbf{K}_{h}\mathbf{Z}\right)^{-1}\left(\frac{1}{n}\mathbf{Z}^{\top}\mathbf{K}_{h}\mathbf{V}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\;\underbrace{\mathbb{E}(K_{h}(X_{i})V_{i}Z_{i}^{\top})\mathbb{E}(K_{h}(X_{i})Z_{i}Z_{i}^{\top})^{-1}\mathbb{E}(K_{h}(X_{i})Z_{i}V_{i}^{\top})}_{(\triangle)}+o_{P}(1).

Note that we used the fact that all the means are of order
O(1)
because
\mathbb{E}(K_{h}(X_{i})Z_{i}Z_{i}^{\top})^{-1}=O(1)
by assumption and
\mathbb{E}(K_{h}(X_{i})Z_{i}V_{i}^{\top})^{\top}=\mathbb{E}(K_{h}(X_{i})V_{i}Z_{i}^{\top})=O(1)
by applying Lemma
5.15
as in (
7
). As we now use equation (
37
) taken from Lemma
5.23
, which states that expression
(\triangle)
is of order
O(h^{4})
, we obtain

\left(\frac{1}{n}\mathbf{V}^{\top}\mathbf{K}_{h}\mathbf{Z}\right)\left(\frac{1}{n}\mathbf{Z}^{\top}\mathbf{K}_{h}\mathbf{Z}\right)^{-1}\left(\frac{1}{n}\mathbf{Z}^{\top}\mathbf{K}_{h}\mathbf{V}\right)=O(h^{4})+o_{P}(1)=o_{P}(1).

Now we work on term
(1)
. Also here, we apply Lemma
6.25
component-wise. It is clear that the necessary assumption (
39
) is satisfied. Afterwards, we apply Lemma
5.19
, leading to

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\mathbf{V}^{\top}\mathbf{K}_{h}\mathbf{V}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}(K_{h}(X_{i})V_{i}V_{i}^{\top})+O_{P}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{nh}}\right)

\displaystyle=f_{X}(0)\kappa(K)+o_{P}(1)+O_{P}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{nh}}\right)

\displaystyle=f_{X}(0)\kappa(K)+o_{P}(1).

Overall, the calculations for term
(1)
and
(2)
result in

\frac{1}{n}\mathbf{V}^{\top}\mathbf{K}_{h}^{\frac{1}{2}}M\mathbf{K}_{h}^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathbf{V}=f_{X}(0)\kappa(K)+o_{P}(1).

Now we have to show the second assertion of the lemma. We start in the same way by splitting up the expression:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\mathbf{V}^{\top}\mathbf{K}_{h}^{\frac{1}{2}}M\mathbf{K}_{h}^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathbf{r}(h)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\mathbf{V}^{\top}\mathbf{K}_{h}^{\frac{1}{2}}\left(I-\mathbf{K}_{h}^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathbf{Z}(\mathbf{Z}^{\top}\mathbf{K}_{h}\mathbf{Z})^{-1}\mathbf{Z}^{\top}\mathbf{K}_{h}^{\frac{1}{2}}\right)\mathbf{K}_{h}^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathbf{r}(h)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\mathbf{V}^{\top}\mathbf{K}_{h}\mathbf{r}(h)-\frac{1}{n}\mathbf{V}^{\top}\mathbf{K}_{h}\mathbf{Z}(\mathbf{Z}^{\top}\mathbf{K}_{h}\mathbf{Z})^{-1}\mathbf{Z}^{\top}\mathbf{K}_{h}\mathbf{r}(h)

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\frac{1}{n}\mathbf{V}^{\top}\mathbf{K}_{h}\mathbf{r}(h)}_{(1)}-\underbrace{\left(\frac{1}{n}\mathbf{V}^{\top}\mathbf{K}_{h}\mathbf{Z}\right)\left(\frac{1}{n}\mathbf{Z}^{\top}\mathbf{K}_{h}\mathbf{Z}\right)^{-1}\left(\frac{1}{n}\mathbf{Z}^{\top}\mathbf{K}_{h}\mathbf{r}(h)\right)}_{(2)}.

We do not have to examine expression
(1)
since it appears in the expression of the assertion. We just have to show that term
(2)
is of order
o_{P}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{nh}}\right)
.
We know that:

•

\frac{1}{n}\mathbf{V}^{\top}\mathbf{K}_{h}\mathbf{Z}=\mathbb{E}(K_{h}(X_{i})V_{i}Z_{i}^{\top})+O_{P}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{nh}}\right)=o(1)+O_{P}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{nh}}\right)=o_{P}(1)
whereby the first equation follows from Lemma
6.25
and the second by
\mathbb{E}(K_{h}(X_{i})V_{i}Z_{i}^{\top})=o(1)
, which for example can be seen in the calculation of (
7
) or by applying Lemma
5.15
.

•

\left(\frac{1}{n}\mathbf{Z}^{\top}\mathbf{K}_{h}\mathbf{Z}\right)^{-1}=\mathbb{E}(K_{h}(X_{i})Z_{i}Z_{i}^{\top})^{-1}+O_{P}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{nh}}\right)=O_{P}(1)
whereby we used (
44
) and assumption (
36
).

•

\frac{1}{n}\mathbf{Z}^{\top}\mathbf{K}_{h}\mathbf{r}(h)=\mathbb{E}(K_{h}(X_{i})Z_{i}r_{i}(h))+O_{P}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{nh}}\right)=O_{P}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{nh}}\right)
by applying Lemma
6.25
with
A_{i}
set respectively to the components of
Z_{i}r_{i}(h)
and the fact that

\mathbb{E}(K_{h}(X_{i})Z_{i}r_{i}(h))=0

due to the definition of the residual with
\theta_{0}(h)
and
\gamma_{0}(h)
being defined as minimizing arguments.

Overall, this shows that
(2)
is of order
o_{P}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{nh}}\right)
, leading to the second assertion of the lemma being proved.
