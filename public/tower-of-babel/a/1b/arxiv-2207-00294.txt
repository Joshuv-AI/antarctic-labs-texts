The augmented Lagrangian method as a framework for stabilised methods in computational mechanics
Erik Burman, Peter Hansbo, Mats G. Larson

Abstract
In this paper we will review recent advances in the application of the augmented Lagrange multiplier method as a general approach for generating multiplier–free stabilised methods. We first show how the method generates Galerkin/Least Squares type schemes for equality constraints and then how it can be extended to develop new stabilised methods for inequality constraints. Application to several different problems in computational mechanics is given.

Introduction

The Augmented Lagrangian Method (ALM) has a long history in optimisation. In its standard form it can be seen as
augmenting standard Lagrange multiplier methods with a penalty term, penalising the constraint equations. It was introduced in order to combine the advantages of the penalty method and the multiplier method in the context on constrained optimisation independently by Hestenes and Powell in
[
72
,
89
]
. It was then extended to the case of optimization with inequality constraints by Rockafellar in
[
90
,
91
]
.
Soon afterwards the potential of ALM for the numerical approximation of partial differential equations (pde) and computational mechanics was explored in Glowinski and Morocco
[
57
]
and by Fortin in
[
51
]
. For overviews of the early results on augmented Lagrangian methods for approximation of pde we refer to the monographs by Glowinski and coworkers
[
52
,
55
]
.

In computational mechanics, Lagrangian methods have the drawback of having to fulfil an
inf–sup
condition to ensure
stability of the discrete scheme such that the balance between the discretisation of the primal variable and the multiplier variable must be
chosen carefully. Adding a penalty term does not change this situation, and in computational mechanics ALM has therefore
been used mostly in an iterative approach (improving the conditioning of the discrete system)
[
52
,
55
,
94
,
104
,
82
,
105
,
102
]
, or as a way of strengthening control of the constraints in cases where the
discretisation is under-constrained. It was also shown to improve convergence in some cases by making the penalty parameter mesh dependent in
[
14
]
. Recently similar ideas have been applied in the context of preconditioning solution methods for discretisations of incompressible flows
[
50
,
86
]
. The ideas of extending the ALM to variational inequalities of
[
90
,
91
]
were introduced in the context of contact mechanics by Alart and Curnier in
[
1
]
.

An early approach to weak boundary conditions for finite element methods was introduced orignally by Nitsche in
[
85
]
, using a method that is related to ALM, but without any multiplier. Indeed here the multiplier has been replaced by its physical representation, the normal boundary flux. Only recently
this possibility of substituting the multiplier by its physical interpretation in the discrete augmented Lagrangian formulation has been explored in its generality. This approach gives rise to
schemes that are formally equivalent to stabilised Lagrange multiplier methods, where the stabilisation is of Galerkin/Least Squares (GLS) type
[
97
]
.

There is, however a crucial difference between the ALM and GLS stabilisation method, and that is the treatment of variational inequalities.
The classical GLS formulation for variational inequalities of Barbosa and Hughes
[
5
]
is very close to standard multiplier schemes, whereas
the ALM supplies an alternative way to define the stabilisation mechanism which transforms the
variational inequalities to nonlinear
equalities
to which iterative schemes can be readily applied.

Particular focus will be given to variational inequalities that are rewritten as nonlinear equalities in the ALM framework. Here we prove existence and best approximation estimates

2
The finite dimensional setting

We begin by recalling the ALM for finite dimensional optimisation problems and by giving an informal introduction to some key ideas to be used in the following.
Below we will frequently use the notation
a\lesssim b
for
a\leq Cb
.

2.1
Optimisation with equality constraints

We consider the quadratic optimisation problem:

\displaystyle\min_{{\boldsymbol{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}f({\boldsymbol{x}})\quad\text{subject to}\quad g_{i}({\boldsymbol{x}})=0,\quad i=1,\dots,m

(2.1)

This problem can be solved by the Lagrange multiplier method, seeking stationary points to the function

\mathcal{L}({\boldsymbol{x}},\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{m})=f({\boldsymbol{x}})+\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}g_{i}({\boldsymbol{x}})

(2.2)

solving the system of equations

\displaystyle\nabla f-\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}\nabla g_{i}={}

\displaystyle{\bf 0}

(2.3)

\displaystyle g_{i}={}

\displaystyle 0,\;i=1,\ldots,m

(2.4)

It can also be solved approximately by the penalty method, seeking the minimum to the function

\mathcal{L}_{\gamma}({\boldsymbol{x}})=f({\boldsymbol{x}})+\frac{\gamma}{2}\sum_{i}g_{i}({\boldsymbol{x}})^{2}

(2.5)

where
\gamma\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
is a given (large) penalty parameter.
We note that the penalty method has a strong regularising effect on the problem in the sense that if
some of the side conditions are (close to being) linear combinations of each other, this does not matter; indeed even if
g_{j}({\boldsymbol{x}})=g_{1}({\boldsymbol{x}})
for all
j
we simply solve

\mathcal{L}_{\gamma}({\boldsymbol{x}})=f({\boldsymbol{x}})+m\frac{\gamma}{2}g_{1}({\boldsymbol{x}})^{2}

(2.6)

which is a well posed problem. This is not the case in the multiplier method, where the system (
2.3
)–(
2.4
) would then be ill posed.
The key point is that the side conditions do not come into play explicitly in the penalty method.

On the other hand, in general the minimiser of (
2.5
) coincides with that of (
2.1
) only in the limit as
\gamma\rightarrow\infty
.

The ALM is a combination of the penalty method and the multiplier method: seek the stationary point to

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\gamma}({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{\lambda}})

\displaystyle=f({\boldsymbol{x}})-\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}g_{i}({\boldsymbol{x}})+\frac{\gamma}{2}\sum_{i}g_{i}({\boldsymbol{x}})^{2}

(2.7)

This problem has the same stationary point as (
2.2
) and the same stability problem in case of linearly independent side conditions.
We note, however, that the multiplier can be eliminated by first solving (
2.3
), which we symbolically denote by

\lambda_{i}=\frac{df}{dg_{i}}({\boldsymbol{x}})

(2.8)

(the multipliers can be interpreted as the change in objective with respect to change in the corresponding side condition), and seek the minimum to the reduced Lagrangian

\mathcal{L}_{A}({\boldsymbol{x}})=f({\boldsymbol{x}})+\sum_{i}\frac{\gamma}{2}g^{2}_{i}({\boldsymbol{x}})-\frac{df}{dg_{i}}({\boldsymbol{x}})g_{i}({\boldsymbol{x}})

(2.9)

Like in the penalty method, the side conditions are then no longer explicit; however, in case of linear dependence we still have an ill posed problem
in solving (
2.3
) and we cannot obtain the representation (
2.8
). But say that we had an alternative way of computing the multiplier so that symbolically we had

\lambda_{i}^{*}({\boldsymbol{x}})\approx\frac{df}{dg_{i}}({\boldsymbol{x}}),\quad\lambda_{i}^{*}({\boldsymbol{x}})\;\text{computable}

(2.10)

Then we could consider the problem of minimising

\mathcal{L}_{A}^{*}({\boldsymbol{x}})=f({\boldsymbol{x}})+\sum_{i}\left(\frac{\gamma}{2}g_{i}({\boldsymbol{x}})^{2}-\lambda_{i}^{*}({\boldsymbol{x}})g_{i}({\boldsymbol{x}})\right)

(2.11)

The accuracy of this method would then depend on the accuracy of the approximation (
2.10
) and the stability of the formulation. A typical situation is that there
is a constant such that

\sum_{i}|\lambda_{i}^{*}({\boldsymbol{x}})|^{2}\leq Cf({\boldsymbol{x}})

(2.12)

which gives

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{A}^{*}({\boldsymbol{x}})

\displaystyle=f({\boldsymbol{x}})+\sum_{i}\left(\frac{\gamma}{2}g_{i}({\boldsymbol{x}})^{2}-\lambda_{i}^{*}({\boldsymbol{x}})g_{i}({\boldsymbol{x}})\right)

(2.13)

\displaystyle=f({\boldsymbol{x}})+\sum_{i}\left(\frac{\gamma}{2}g_{i}({\boldsymbol{x}})^{2}-\delta|\lambda_{i}^{*}({\boldsymbol{x}})|^{2}-\frac{1}{4\delta}g_{i}^{2}({\boldsymbol{x}})\right)

(2.14)

\displaystyle\geq f({\boldsymbol{x}})-\delta\sum_{i}|\lambda_{i}^{*}({\boldsymbol{x}})|^{2}+\sum_{i}\left(\frac{\gamma}{2}-\frac{1}{4\delta}\right)g_{i}^{2}({\boldsymbol{x}})

(2.15)

\displaystyle\geq(1-\delta C)f({\boldsymbol{x}})+\sum_{i}\left(\frac{\gamma}{2}-\frac{1}{4\delta}\right)g_{i}^{2}({\boldsymbol{x}})

(2.16)

\displaystyle\gtrsim f({\boldsymbol{x}})+\sum_{i}g_{i}^{2}({\boldsymbol{x}})

(2.17)

where we obtained the last estimate by taking
\delta
sufficiently small and
\gamma
sufficiently large. We conclude that the minimization problem for
\mathcal{L}_{A}^{*}({\boldsymbol{x}})
is well posed if
\gamma>\gamma_{C}
. This is the basic idea
that underlies the application of the ALM as a stabilisation method, in cases where the multiplier can be eliminated.

2.2
Optimisation with inequality constraints

We consider next a quadratic optimisation problems of the type:

\displaystyle\min_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}f({\boldsymbol{x}})\quad\text{subject to}\quad g_{i}({\boldsymbol{x}})\leq 0,\quad i=1,\dots,m

(2.18)

The augmented Lagrangian for this problem proposed by Rockafellar
[
90
, Equation (7)]
(here with
\gamma=2r
, and with the multiplier chosen negative) takes the form for
\gamma\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{A}({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{\lambda}})=

\displaystyle f({\boldsymbol{x}})+\frac{1}{2\gamma}\sum_{i}\left([\gamma g_{i}({\boldsymbol{x}})-\lambda_{i}]_{+}^{2}-\lambda_{i}^{2}\right)

(2.19)

where
[x]_{+}=\max(x,0)
.

Observe that another equivalent reformulation is given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{A}({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{\lambda}})=

\displaystyle f({\boldsymbol{x}})-\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}g_{i}({\boldsymbol{x}})+\frac{\gamma}{2}\sum_{i}g_{i}({\boldsymbol{x}})^{2}-\frac{1}{2\gamma}\sum_{i}[\gamma g_{i}({\boldsymbol{x}})-\lambda_{i}]_{-}^{2}

(2.20)

where
[x]_{-}=\min(x,0)
.
This is easily seen by using that
x=[x]_{+}+[x]_{-}
and hence

[x]_{+}^{2}=([x]_{+}+[x]_{-})^{2}-[x]_{-}^{2}-\underbrace{2[x]_{+}[x]_{-}}_{=0}=x^{2}-[x]_{-}^{2}

Applying this in (
2.19
) with
x=\gamma g_{i}({\boldsymbol{x}})-\lambda_{i}
leads to (
2.20
).
In (
2.20
) we recognise the augmented Lagrangian for the equality constraint (
2.7
) in the first three terms and the last term is the non-linear switch that introduces the inequality constraint.

To see that (
2.19
) is indeed the natural formulation we introduce slack variables
z_{i}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
and rewrite (
2.18
)
in the form

\displaystyle\min_{({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{z}})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}_{+}}f({\boldsymbol{x}})\quad\text{subject to}\quad g_{i}({\boldsymbol{x}})+z_{i}=0,\quad i=1,\dots,m

(2.21)

with corresponding augmented Lagrangian

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{A}({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{z}},{\boldsymbol{\lambda}})=f({\boldsymbol{x}})-\sum_{i}\left\{(g_{i}({\boldsymbol{x}})+z_{i})\lambda_{i}+\frac{\gamma}{2}(g_{i}({\boldsymbol{x}})+z_{i})^{2}\right\}

(2.22)

for which we seek stationary points, minimizing in
({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{z}})
.
Here we may now perform the optimization over
{\boldsymbol{z}}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{m}
explicitly by noting that for each
{\boldsymbol{x}}
and
{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}
we obtain a sum of quadratic polynomials in
z_{i}
of
the form

\displaystyle-(g_{i}({\boldsymbol{x}})+z_{i})\lambda_{i}+\frac{\gamma}{2}(g_{i}({\boldsymbol{x}})+z_{i})^{2}=\frac{1}{2\gamma}\Big((\gamma(g_{i}({\boldsymbol{x}})+z_{i})-\lambda_{i})^{2}-\lambda_{i}^{2}\Big)

(2.23)

and therefore the minimum is attained at
\gamma z_{i}=-(\gamma g_{i}-\lambda_{i})
and taking the constraint
z_{i}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
into account we find that
\gamma z_{i}=[-(\gamma g_{i}({\boldsymbol{x}})-\lambda_{i})]_{+}
. Inserting this expression for
\gamma z_{i}
into (
2.23
) and using the identity
a+[-a]_{+}=[a]_{+}
we arrive at

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{A}({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{z}},{\boldsymbol{\lambda}})=f({\boldsymbol{x}})+\frac{1}{2\gamma}\sum_{i}([\gamma g_{i}({\boldsymbol{x}})-\lambda_{i}]_{+}^{2}-\lambda_{i}^{2})

(2.24)

Alternatively we may seek stationary points to the standard Lagrangian

\mathcal{L}({\boldsymbol{x}},\lambda)=f({\boldsymbol{x}})-\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}g_{i}({\boldsymbol{x}})

(2.25)

under the Karush–Kuhn–Tucker (KKT) conditions

\displaystyle g_{i}\leq{}

\displaystyle 0,\;i=1,\ldots,m

(2.26)

\displaystyle\lambda_{i}\leq{}

\displaystyle 0,\;i=1,\ldots,m

(2.27)

\displaystyle\lambda_{i}g_{i}={}

\displaystyle 0,\;i=1,\ldots,m

(2.28)

Noting that the KKT conditions (
2.26
)–(
2.28
) are equivalent to
the single statement

\lambda_{i}=-[\gamma g_{i}-\lambda_{i}]_{+}

(2.29)

where
\gamma\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
is an arbitrary positive number.
We may then rewrite the Lagrangian in the form

\displaystyle f({\boldsymbol{x}})-\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}g_{i}({\boldsymbol{x}})

\displaystyle=f({\boldsymbol{x}})-\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}\Big(g_{i}({\boldsymbol{x}})-\frac{1}{\gamma}\lambda_{i}\Big)-\frac{1}{\gamma}\lambda_{i}^{2}

(2.30)

\displaystyle=f({\boldsymbol{x}})+\frac{1}{\gamma}\sum_{i}\underbrace{[\gamma g_{i}({\boldsymbol{x}})-\lambda_{i}]_{+}(\gamma g_{i}({\boldsymbol{x}})+\lambda_{i})}_{[\gamma g_{i}({\boldsymbol{x}})-\lambda_{i}]_{+}^{2}}-\frac{1}{\gamma}\lambda_{i}^{2}

(2.31)

where we used (
2.29
) and the fact that
[a]_{+}a=[a]_{+}^{2}
. The substitutions
\lambda_{i}\mapsto\lambda_{i}/2
and
\gamma\mapsto 2\gamma
manufactures the Lagrangian (
2.24
).

Writing the optimality system of (
2.24
) results in the system of equations

\displaystyle\nabla f+\sum_{i}[\gamma g_{i}-\lambda_{i}]_{+}\nabla g_{i}={}

\displaystyle{\bf 0}

(2.32)

\displaystyle[\gamma g_{i}-\lambda_{i}]_{+}={}

\displaystyle-\lambda_{i},\;i=1,\ldots,m

(2.33)

which is a nonlinear equality problem which explicitly includes the KKT conditions.

Again, if we can use (
2.10
) we may instead seek the minima to

\mathcal{L}_{A}^{*}({\boldsymbol{x}}):=f({\boldsymbol{x}})+\frac{1}{2\gamma}\sum_{i}[\gamma g_{i}({\boldsymbol{x}})-\lambda_{i}^{*}({\boldsymbol{x}})]_{+}^{2}-(\lambda_{i}^{*}({\boldsymbol{x}}))^{2}

(2.34)

3
Iterative solution using the augmented Lagrangian

The augmented Lagrangian is possibly most well known as the basis for an iterative algorithm for constrained optimization problems. The stationary points of the functional (
2.7
) can be approximated using the following classical algorithm attributed to Usawa, with the application to augmented Lagrangian methods developed in the works of Glowinski and co-workers
[
3
,
56
,
52
,
55
]
. Following
[
52
]
we consider the situation where the model problem is to minimize

J({\boldsymbol{x}}):=\frac{1}{2}{\boldsymbol{x}}^{T}{\boldsymbol{A}}{\boldsymbol{x}}-{\boldsymbol{b}}^{T}{\boldsymbol{x}}

over
{\boldsymbol{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
under the constraint
{\boldsymbol{B}}{\boldsymbol{x}}={\boldsymbol{c}}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
. Here
{\boldsymbol{A}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
is symmetric positive definite,
{\boldsymbol{b}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
{\boldsymbol{B}}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
. The augmented Lagrangian (
2.7
) then takes the form,

\mathcal{L}_{A}({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}):=\frac{1}{2}{\boldsymbol{x}}^{T}{\boldsymbol{A}}{\boldsymbol{x}}-{\boldsymbol{b}}^{T}{\boldsymbol{x}}+{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}^{T}({\boldsymbol{B}}{\boldsymbol{x}}-{\boldsymbol{c}})+\frac{\gamma}{2}|{\boldsymbol{B}}\bf x-{\boldsymbol{c}}|^{2}

(3.1)

(Uzawa’s algorithm)

1.

Let
{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}^{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}

2.

Find
{\boldsymbol{x}}^{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that
\mathcal{L}_{A}({\boldsymbol{x}}^{n},{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}^{n})\leq\mathcal{L}_{A}({\boldsymbol{y}}^{n},{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}^{n})
for all
{\boldsymbol{y}}^{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}

3.

Update the multiplier:
{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}^{n+1}={\boldsymbol{\lambda}}^{n}+\rho_{n}({\boldsymbol{B}}{\boldsymbol{x}}^{n}-{\boldsymbol{c}}),\quad\rho_{n}>0
.

We note that step 2 is equivalent to solving the linear system, find
{\boldsymbol{x}}^{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that

({\boldsymbol{A}}+\gamma{\boldsymbol{B}}^{T}{\boldsymbol{B}}){\boldsymbol{x}}^{n}=-{\boldsymbol{B}}^{T}{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}^{n}+{\boldsymbol{b}}+\gamma{\boldsymbol{B}}^{T}{\boldsymbol{c}}

The iterates
{\boldsymbol{x}}^{n},{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}^{n}
of the iterative method converges to the saddle point of (
3.1
) provided the steplength
\rho_{n}
satisfies

0<\alpha_{0}\leq\rho_{n}\leq\alpha_{1}<2\left(\gamma+\frac{1}{\beta^{2}}\right)

where
\beta^{2}
is the largest eigenvalue of the matrix
{\boldsymbol{A}}^{-1}{\boldsymbol{B}}^{T}{\boldsymbol{B}}
defined by

\beta^{2}=\max_{{\boldsymbol{v}}\neq 0}\frac{|{\boldsymbol{B}}{\boldsymbol{v}}|^{2}}{{\boldsymbol{v}}^{T}{\boldsymbol{A}}{\boldsymbol{v}}}

For a proof of the convergence result we refer to
[
56
, Chapter 2, Section 4]
or
[
52
, Chapter 1, Section 2]
.

4
Augmented Lagrangian methods and Galerkin/Least squares

We now turn to the case where the Lagrangian is a functional taking values in some Sobolev space and the numerical method is obtained by finding the stationary points in a finite dimensional approximation space. Typically we are interested in the discretisation of a problem where some energy is minimised under a constraint. To illustrate this we consider the case with equality constraints. Let
V
and
H
denote two Hilbert spaces, with dual spaces
V^{\prime}
and
H^{\prime}
, respectively. Let
F:V\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
denote a strictly convex
C^{2}
-functional and
B:V\rightarrow H
a
linear operator. We are interested in minimising
F
under a
constraint defined by
B
. Given the data
f\in V^{\prime}
and
g\in H
We consider the optimization problem

u=\mbox{arginf}_{v\in V}F(v)-\left<f,v\right>_{V^{\prime},V}\mbox{ such that }Bu=g.

(4.1)

The Lagrangian takes the form

\mathcal{L}(v,\mu):=F(v)-\left<f,v\right>_{V^{\prime},V}-\left<\mu,Bv-g\right>_{H^{\prime},H}.

(4.2)

This problem can be shown to have unique solution under suitable hypothesis on the spaces
V
and
H
and the operators
F
,
B
,
f
and
g
(see for instance
[
83
, Chapter 1, Section 2.1, Theorems 2.1 and 2.2]
). Augmenting the Lagrangian has no effect on the continuous level, but formally an augmented version of (
4.2
), in the spirit of (
2.7
) can be written

\mathcal{L}_{\text{A}}(v,\mu):=F(v)-\left<f,v\right>_{V^{\prime},V}-\left<\mu,Bv-g\right>_{H^{\prime},H}+\frac{\gamma}{2}\|Bv-g\|_{H}^{2}.

(4.3)

The discrete version of the ALM based on (
4.3
), would then be obtained by restricting
\mathcal{L}_{\text{A}}
to finite dimensional spaces. As we saw in the previous section the ALM on the discrete level combines the control of the constraint given by the Lagrange multiplier and of the penalty. It also gives us an iterative procedure to find the minimiser. When using the ALM in the context of pde problems the ALM also gives enhanced control of the side condition in the sense of a GaLS method, or a variational multiscale method. To see this we assume that
H=H^{\prime}=L^{2}
and that
H^{\prime}_{h}\subset H^{\prime}
,
V_{h}\subset V
are some finite dimensional approximation spaces. Here
h
denotes the characteristic lengthscale (or mesh parameter) of the discrete space. We let
\pi_{H}:H\mapsto H^{\prime}_{h}
denote the
L^{2}
-orthogonal projection onto
H^{\prime}_{h}
. Since

\left<\mu_{h},Bv-g\right>_{L^{2}}=\left<\mu_{h},\pi_{H}(Bv-g)\right>_{L^{2}}

we see that the Lagrange multiplier only gives control of the projection of
Bv-g
on the finite dimensional subspace
H_{h}^{\prime}
. This may be insufficient for the stability of the method, in particular since
H_{h}^{\prime}
may need to be chosen small compared to
V_{h}
for stability reasons, i.e. to satisfy the inf-sup stability condition that we will discuss below. A classical example is the stability of the incompressibility constraint (in which case
B
is the divergence operator) of the Brinkman problem when the viscosity becomes negligible. Adding the term
\|Bv-g\|_{H}^{2}
enhances the stability,
by adding control of
(I-\pi_{H})(Bv-g)
compared to the pure Lagrange multiplier method. This also shows that a sufficient stabilization can be achieved by augmenting with
\|(I-\pi_{H})(Bv-g)\|_{H}^{2}
. This we recognise as a stabilization of the orthogonal subscales, which is a member of the family of variational multiscale methods. Of course in the associated Euler-Lagrange equations these terms take the form of GLS stabilizations of some residual quantities. Indeed a number of ideas from the field of stabilized methods can be made to bear to the ALM, but we will not explore this further herein. Instead we will show in the examples below how the design of finite element methods using the ALM allows us to recover some well known GLS methods from computational mechanics.

We can discern two different situations for the continuous problem (
4.3
):

A.

The multiplier has enough regularity to define a scalar product with the side condition.

B.

The multiplier has only regularity enough to support a duality pairing with the side condition.

In the first case we can use an analogue to the reformulation (
2.19
) which is convenient for the treatment of inequality conditions, and formulate the problem on the continuous level; in the second case this is not formally correct. Indeed if the multiplier does not have sufficient regularity the augmented continuous formulation does not lead to a well-defined problem, unless the augmentation is taken in the continuous
H
-norm, which may be inconvenient from computational standpoint. In this case the reformulation (
2.19
) is not available. We emphasize that this is not a problem
in the discrete setting since we can use norm equivalence of discrete spaces to obtain an ALM that has the right asymptotic scaling. However in order to carry out a rigorous numerical analysis of the resulting finite element method the assumption of additional regularity of the exact solution must be justifiable. This is often, but not always the case. In that sense ALM methods in the situation B can be seen as a non-conforming method.

For the discrete as well as the continuous problem we have two further cases:

C.

The multiplier has a physical interpretation in terms of the primal variable.

D.

The multiplier cannot be interpreted (or be easily interpreted) in terms of the primal variable.

For the discrete case, we also have the problem of finding suitable approximations to fulfil a discrete
inf–sup
condition.
In case C we can use a trick analogous to that of (
2.10
), which gives a class of problems where the
multiplier has been eliminated beforehand; alternatively, the multiplier can be retained and stabilised by the addition of a GLS term, in the spirit of
[
4
,
5
]
. These approaches
give stability without balancing the discretisation of the multiplier space and the space for the primal variable. In case D the multiplier has to be retained, but the inequality case can still be handled in the same way as above and stabilisation is still possible, for instance using interior penalty stabilization where the stabilization acts on the multiplier alone
[
22
,
25
]
.

4.1
Abstract framework

Since the
rationale of the method is from numerical approximation we will only
consider formulations that work in the finite dimensional setting,
then A and B above are treated similarly. However it is only in case A
that the discussion holds also for the continuous case.
The resulting numerical methods can be shown to be optimally converging for sufficiently smooth exact solutions, but the problem of convergence is not established for exact solutions that has no additional regularity.
The question of how to design methods that are valid formulations also for the original pde problem is subtle and requires the design of sophisticated stabilization operators, for an interesting work in this direction we refer to
[
12
]
. Below we let
\left<\cdot,\cdot\right>
denote the
L^{2}
scalar product over the the domain of definition of functions in
H
and we denote the associated norm
\|v\|:=\left<v,v\right>^{\frac{1}{2}}
.

We are interested in minimising
F
under a
constraint defined by
B
, either as an equality or an inequality
constraint. We will now introduce some sufficient conditions for the abstract analysis below to hold. We will then in the examples show that the assumptions are verified.

1.

We assume that the operator
B
is bounded and surjective from

V
to
H
, so that for every
\zeta\in H
there exists
\xi\in V

such that
B\xi=\zeta
and
\|\xi\|_{V}\leq C\|\zeta\|_{H}
. It follows that there exists
\alpha>0
such that for every

\mu\in H^{\prime}
there holds

\alpha\|\mu\|_{H^{\prime}}\leq\sup_{v\in V}\frac{\left<Bv,\mu\right>_{H,H^{\prime}}}{\|v\|_{V}}

(4.4)

2.

We also assume that
V_{h}
and
H_{h}^{\prime}
are chosen in such a way that this property carries over to the finite dimensional setting, in the sense that a so called Fortin interpolant exists, for all
v\in V
such that
Bv\in H
, there exists
\exists i_{F}v\in V_{h}
such that for all
q_{h}\in H_{h}^{\prime}
,

\left<B(v-i_{F}v),q_{h}\right>=0,\quad\|i_{F}v\|_{V}+\|Bi_{F}v\|_{H_{h}}\lesssim\|v\|_{V}+\|Bv\|_{H_{h}}

(4.5)

Note that for
v\in V_{h}
there holds
i_{F}v=v
.

3.

We assume that the surjectivity also holds for the discrete spaces on the following form: for all
\mu_{h}\in H_{h}^{\prime}
there exists
v_{h}\in V_{h}
such that for all
q_{h}\in H_{h}^{\prime}
,

\left<\mu_{h}-Bv_{h},q_{h}\right>=0,\quad\|v_{h}\|_{V}+\|Bv_{h}\|_{H_{h}}\lesssim\|\mu_{h}\|_{H_{h}}

(4.6)

Discrete surjectivity is a consequence of the discrete inf-sup condition which typically is equivalent with the existence of the Fortin interpolant
[
45
, Lemma 26.9]
. We state both (
4.5
) and (
4.6
) separately here for future reference and to highlight the difference of the norms required in the right hand side. If we are in a non-conforming situation it is not immediately clear that equivalence holds. Note however that if the spaces are such that
\|Bv-\pi_{H_{h}}Bv\|_{H_{h}}\leq\|v\|_{V}
then (
4.6
) implies (
4.5
).

The form of the stabilities in (
4.5
) and (
4.6
) appear a bit ad hoc here, but as we shall see below this is the natural stability to require for the analysis. Here the
norm
\|\cdot\|_{H_{h}}
is an
h
-weighted
L^{2}
-norm and will be discussed below.

4.2
Equality constraints

We wish to solve the optimization problem (
4.1
) and recall the formal
augmented Lagrangian similar to (
2.7
) given by

\mathcal{L}_{\text{A}}(v,\mu):=F(v)-\left<f,v\right>_{V^{\prime},V}-\left<\mu,Bv-g\right>_{H^{\prime},H}+\frac{\gamma}{2}\|Bv-g\|_{H}^{2}

(4.7)

For later use with inequality constraints, we would now like to use the analogy to (
2.19
).
However, this is not possible unless
H^{\prime}=H:=L_{2}
, where
L_{2}
denotes the space of square integrable functions over the pertinent domain, which is case A above. In this particular case,
completing the square,
-2ab+b^{2}=(a-b)^{2}-a^{2}
, results in the following equivalent formulation

\mathcal{L}_{\text{A}}(v,\mu):=F(v)+\left<f,v\right>_{V^{\prime},V}+\frac{\gamma}{2}\|Bv-g-\frac{1}{2\gamma}\mu\|^{2}-\frac{1}{2\gamma}\|\mu\|^{2}

(4.8)

analogous to (
2.19
). We let the semi-linear form
a:V\times V\rightarrow\mathbb{R}

be defined by the Gateaux derivative of
F(v)
,

a(u;v):=\left<\frac{\partial F}{\partial u}(u),v\right>_{V^{\prime},V}

(4.9)

and we assume that the form
a
satisfies the positivity, monotonicity and continuity conditions

\displaystyle a(v;v)\geq\alpha\|v\|_{V}^{2},\qquad\alpha>0

(4.10)

\displaystyle a(w_{1};w_{1}-w_{2})-a(w_{2};w_{1}-w_{2})\geq\alpha\|w_{1}-w_{2}\|_{V}^{2}

(4.11)

\displaystyle|a(w_{1};v)-a(w_{2};v)|\leq C\|w_{1}-w_{2}\|_{V}\|v\|_{V}

(4.12)

The optimality system obtained by differentiating (
4.8
) then reads:
find
(u,\lambda)\in V\times H^{\prime}
such that

\displaystyle a(u;v)-\left<\lambda,Bv\right>_{H^{\prime},H}-\left<\mu,Bu\right>_{H^{\prime},H}+{\gamma}\left<Bu,Bv\right>_{H}=\left<f,v_{h}\right>_{V^{\prime},V}+\left<g,\mu+{\gamma}Bv\right>_{H}

(4.13)

for all
(v,\mu)\in V\times H^{\prime}
. Here we simply replace
V
and
H^{\prime}
by
V_{h}
and
H_{h}^{\prime}
to obtain the discrete method.

We also want to handle case B. Then typically
Bv\in H:=H^{r}
where
H^{r}
denotes a (potentially fractional) Hilbert space with
r>0
,
and consequently
\mu\in H^{\prime}:=H^{-r}
, the dual to
H^{r}
. Since
H^{-r}\not\subset H^{r}
the formulation (
4.8
) no longer makes sense. Instead in the spirit of discretize first then optimize we move to the discrete counterpart of (
4.1
) and introduce
discrete spaces
V_{h}\subset V
and
H^{\prime}_{h}\subset H^{\prime}
. The finite element method then amounts to seek stationary points in
V_{h}
and
H^{\prime}_{h}
to the augmented Lagrangian (
4.7
).
On the finite dimensional finite element spaces we can approximate the continuous norms
\|\cdot\|_{H}
and
\|\cdot\|_{H^{\prime}}
by discrete counterparts

\|Bv\|_{H}^{2}\approx\|Bv\|_{H_{h}}^{2}:=\|h^{-r}Bv\|_{L_{2}}^{2}

(4.14)

and

\|\mu\|_{H^{\prime}}^{2}\approx\|\mu\|_{H^{\prime}_{h}}^{2}:=\|h^{r}\mu\|_{L_{2}}^{2}

(4.15)

where
h
is the local meshsize (assumed constant in the following for simplicity) and
r\geq 0
depends
on the space
H
; loosely speaking
r
corresponds to the number of derivatives present in the norm

\|\cdot\|_{H}
. It is also immediate by the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality that the following discrete duality property holds

\left<v,\mu\right>_{H_{h},H_{h}^{\prime}}:=\left<v,\mu\right>\leq\|v\|_{H_{h}}\|\mu\|_{H^{\prime}_{h}}.

This is done for two reasons

1.

To obtain a well conditioned method, we wish to have the same condition number emanating from the penalty term as from the form
a(\cdot,\cdot)
.

2.

The analysis of the resulting methods requires that the discrete norms can be bounded in terms of the form
a(\cdot,\cdot)
which is only possible if they scale the same way.

Now we can use the arbitrariness of
\gamma
to set

\gamma=\gamma_{0}/h^{2r}

(4.16)

where
\gamma_{0}
is a problem– and discretization–dependent constant. Proceeding as above we find that on discrete spaces

\mathcal{L}^{h}_{\text{A}}(v,\mu):=F(v)+\left<f,v\right>_{V^{\prime},V}+\frac{\gamma_{0}}{2h^{2r}}\|Bv-g-\frac{h^{2r}}{2\gamma_{0}}\mu\|^{2}-\frac{h^{2r}}{2\gamma_{0}}\|\mu\|^{2}

and the discrete optimality system reads: find
(u_{h},\lambda_{h})\in V_{h}\times H^{\prime}_{h}

such that

a(u_{h};v)-\left<\lambda_{h},Bv\right>-\left<\mu,Bu_{h}\right>+\frac{\gamma_{0}}{h^{2r}}\left<Bu_{h},Bv\right>=\left<f,v_{h}\right>_{V^{\prime},V}+\left<g,\mu+\frac{\gamma_{0}}{h^{2r}}Bv\right>

(4.17)

for all
(v,\mu)\in V_{h}\times H^{\prime}_{h}
, where
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
denotes the standard
L_{2}
scalar product.
Introducing the global form

A[(w,\eta);(v,\mu)]:=a(w;v)-\left<\eta,Bv\right>-\left<\mu,Bw\right>+\frac{\gamma_{0}}{h^{2r}}\left<Bw,Bv\right>,

we can cast the optimality system on the compact form: find
(u_{h},\lambda_{h})\in V_{h}\times H^{\prime}_{h}

such that

A[(u_{h},\lambda_{h});(v,\mu)]=\left<f,v\right>_{V^{\prime},V}+\left<g,\mu+\frac{\gamma_{0}}{h^{2r}}Bv\right>.

(4.18)

for all
(v,\mu)\in V_{h}\times H^{\prime}_{h}
.

It follows by inspection that any solution to (
4.1
) that is sufficiently smooth, i.e.
(u,\lambda)\in V\times H^{\prime}\cap L^{2}
is a solution to (
4.17
) and hence the formulation is consistent. Indeed the stationary point of (
4.2
) is given by the solution to

a(u;v)-\left<\lambda,Bv\right>_{H^{\prime},H}=\left<f,v\right>_{V^{\prime},V},\quad\forall v\in V

and

\left<Bu,\mu\right>_{H,H^{\prime}}=\left<g,\mu\right>_{H,H^{\prime}}.

If the solution is sufficiently regular these equalities hold with
H
and
H^{\prime}
replaced by the
L^{2}
norm and we see that in that case the exact solution satisfies the finite element formulation,

\underbrace{a(u;v)-\left<\lambda,Bv\right>}_{=\left<f,v\right>_{V^{\prime},V}}-\underbrace{\left<\mu,Bu\right>+\frac{\gamma_{0}}{h^{2r}}\left<Bu,Bv\right>}_{=\left<g,\mu+\frac{\gamma_{0}}{h^{2r}}Bv\right>}=\left<f,v_{h}\right>_{V^{\prime},V}+\left<g,\mu+\frac{\gamma_{0}}{h^{2r}}Bv\right>.

We do not give a full analysis of the linear problem herein, but focus on the nonlinear case in the next section. The analysis immediately also applies to the linear case.

4.3
Inequality constraints

For the subsequent analysis, we will consider the discrete case and
hence we use the space
V_{h}
for the primal variable and
H_{h}^{\prime}
for
the dual variable. For simplicity we do not use the subscript
h
on
all variables below. We wish to solve the continuous optimization problem

u=\mbox{arginf}_{v\in V}F(v)-\left<f,v\right>_{V^{\prime},V}\mbox{ such that }Bu\leq 0

(4.19)

Where the inequality constraint must be interpreted in the sense of
distributions on
H
and we will denote the continuous multiplier
appearing in the constrained optimization
\lambda\in H^{\prime}
.
The weak formulation characterizing the solution to the continuous problem is as follows. Find
(u,\lambda)\in V\times K
(where

) such that

a(u;v)-\left<\lambda,Bv\right>_{H^{\prime},H}=\left<f,v\right>_{V^{\prime},V},\quad\forall v\in V

\left<Bu,\lambda-\mu\right>_{H,H^{\prime}}\leq 0,\quad\forall\mu\in K

It follows by choosing
\lambda-\mu>0
in (

) that
Bu\leq 0
. By taking
\mu=0
it follows that
\left<Bu,\lambda\right>_{H,H^{\prime}}\leq 0
and since both
Bu
and
\lambda
are negative it follows that
\lambda Bu=0
.

We have arrived at the following Kuhn–Tucker conditions on the multiplier and side condition:

Bu\leq 0,\quad\lambda\leq 0,\quad\lambda Bu=0.

(4.22)

We now use the analogue to (
2.29
), to show that
(
4.22
) formally is equivalent to

\lambda=-{\gamma}\,[Bu-\gamma^{-1}\,\lambda]_{+}

(4.23)

To derive the finite element formulation we also proceed
formally following the discussion of section
2.1
applied to the problem (
4.19
) with the min taken over the finite dimensional space
V_{h}
and write the
augmented Lagrangian, for
\gamma\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
,
(v,\mu)\in V_{h}\times H_{h}^{\prime}

\mathcal{L}_{\text{A}}(v,\mu):=F(v)-\left<f,v\right>_{V^{\prime},V}+\frac{\gamma}{2}\|[Bv-\mu/\gamma]_{+}\|^{2}-\frac{1}{2\gamma}\|\mu\|^{2}

(4.24)

we note that if
\gamma
is chosen as in (
4.16
) we may use (
4.14
) and (
4.15
) to write

\mathcal{L}_{\text{A}}(v,\mu):=F(v)-\left<f,v\right>_{V^{\prime},V}+\frac{\gamma_{0}}{2}\|[Bv-\mu/\gamma]_{+}\|_{H_{h}}^{2}-\frac{1}{2\gamma_{0}}\|\mu\|_{H_{h}^{\prime}}^{2}

(4.25)

The finite element optimality system reads: find
(u_{h},\lambda_{h})\in V_{h}\times H_{h}^{\prime}

such that

A[(u_{h},\lambda_{h});(v,\mu)]=\left<f,v\right>_{V^{\prime},V}

(4.26)

for all
(v,\mu)\in V_{h}\times H_{h}^{\prime}
,
where

A[(w,\eta);(v,\mu)]:=a(w;v)+\left<\gamma[Bw-\zeta/\gamma]_{+},Bv-\mu/\gamma\right>-\left<\gamma^{-1}\zeta,\mu\right>.

(4.27)

Note that in general (
H\neq H^{\prime}
) and it is not possible to prove well-posedness of (
4.26
) in continuous spaces. Nevertheless also in this case a sufficiently smooth solution of the original continuous problem will also be solution to the
formulation (
4.26
), showing that the formulation remains
consistent.

First we note that for smooth solutions
\lambda\in K
and (
4.21
) are equivalent to (
4.23
). Then evaluating (
4.26
) at a sufficiently smooth exact solution
(u,\lambda)
we see that for all
(v,\mu)\in V_{h}\times H_{h}^{\prime}

A[(u,\lambda);(v,\mu)]:=a(u;v)+\underbrace{\left<\gamma[Bu-\lambda/\gamma]_{+},Bv-\mu/\gamma\right>}_{=-\left<\lambda,Bv-\mu/\gamma\right>\,\mbox{by \eqref{eq:lambda0}}}-\left<\gamma^{-1}\lambda,\mu\right>=a(u;v)-\left<\lambda,Bv\right>_{H^{\prime},H}

(4.28)

and hence by (
4.20
) the formulation (
4.26
) is consistent for exact solutions
(u,\lambda)\in V\times H^{\prime}\cap L^{2}
.

To see the effect of the nonlinear formulation for active and non-active constraints, first assume
[Bw-\zeta/\gamma]_{+}>0
in (
4.26
). The constraint is active and we see that the equation becomes

a(w;v)-\left<\mu,Bw\right>-\left<\eta,Bv\right>+\left<\gamma Bw,Bv\right>=0

which we recognise as the augmented Lagrangian form from (
4.17
) imposing the
equality constraint
Bw=0
. If on the other hand

[Bw-\eta/\gamma]_{+}=0
then the constraint is not
active and the equation (
4.26
) takes the form

a(w;v)-\left<\gamma^{-1}\eta,\mu\right>=0

and we see that
Bw
is free and
\eta=0
is imposed. As expected the formulation expresses the conditions of (
4.22
) and acts as a nonlinear switch between imposing either
Bu=0
and
\lambda=0
.

Using the parameter
\gamma
introduced in
(
4.16
) and the h-weighted norms introduced in
(
4.14
) and (
4.15
) together with the
inequality
|[a]_{+}-[b]_{+}|\leq|a-b|

[

]
we see that
the following continuity holds

\left<\gamma([Bw_{1}-\eta_{1}/\gamma]_{+}-[Bw_{2}-\eta_{2}/\gamma]_{+},Bv+\mu/\gamma\right>\\
\lesssim(\|B(w_{1}-w_{2})\|_{H_{h}}+\|\eta_{1}-\eta_{2}\|_{H_{h}^{\prime}})(\|Bv\|_{H_{h}}+\|\mu\|_{H_{h}^{\prime}})

(4.29)

Together with (
4.12
) this shows that the form

is continuous.
If
H\equiv L^{2}
, the formulation (
4.26
) and (
4.29
) makes sense on the
continuous level. Observe that unless

the norms are

dependent and hence the bound degenerates for decreasing

4.3.1
Stability, existence and uniqueness of solutions

We will now show that thanks to the properties (

Proposition 1

\displaystyle\left<\gamma([Bw_{1}-\eta_{1}/\gamma]_{+}-[Bw_{2}-\eta_{2}/\gamma]_{+},Be+\zeta\gamma\right>+\left<\gamma^{-1}(\eta_{1}-\eta_{2}),\zeta\right>

\displaystyle\qquad=\left<\gamma([Bw_{1}-\eta_{1}/\gamma]_{+}-[Bw_{2}-\eta_{2}/\gamma]_{+},Be-\zeta/\gamma\right>

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+2\left<\gamma([Bw_{1}-\eta_{1}/\gamma]_{+}-[Bw_{2}-\eta_{2}/\gamma]_{+},\zeta/\gamma\right>+\gamma_{0}^{-1}\|\zeta\|^{2}_{H_{h}^{\prime}}

\displaystyle\qquad\geq\gamma_{0}\|[Bw_{1}-\eta_{1}/\gamma]_{+}-[Bw_{2}-\eta_{2}/\gamma]_{+}+\gamma^{-1}\zeta\|_{H_{h}}^{2}.

(4.39)

It follows from (
4.11
) and (
4.39
) that

\boxed{E_{C}[(w_{1},\eta_{1}),(w_{2},\eta_{2})]^{2}+\alpha\|e\|_{V}^{2}\leq A[(w_{1},\eta_{1}),(e,-\zeta)]-A[(w_{2},\eta_{2}),(e,-\zeta)]}

(4.40)

where
E_{C}
is the error in the approximation of the contact zone defined by

E_{C}[(w_{1},\eta_{1}),(w_{2},\eta_{2})]:=\gamma_{0}^{\frac{1}{2}}\|[Bw_{1}+\eta_{1}/\gamma]_{+}-[Bw_{2}+\eta_{2}/\gamma]_{+}+\gamma^{-1}\zeta\|_{H_{h}}

If we assume that both
\{w_{1},\eta_{1}\}
and
\{w_{2},\eta_{2}\}
are
solutions to (
4.26
) it follows that the right hand side of (
4.40
)
is zero and

E_{C}[(w_{1},\eta_{1}),(w_{2},\eta_{2})]^{2}+\alpha\|e\|_{V}^{2}=0.

It follows that

and the primal solution is unique. To see that also
the multiplier is unique once again choose
\xi(\eta)
such that
B\xi(\eta)=-\eta/\gamma
, in the sense that

\left<B\xi(\zeta),q_{h}\right>=-\left<\zeta/\gamma,q_{h}\right>
, for all
q_{h}\in H_{h}^{\prime}
,
and test with
v=\xi
and
\mu=0
, and use
arguments similar as those leading to (

) to see that

\displaystyle 0=

\displaystyle A[(w_{1},\eta_{1}),(\xi(\zeta),0)]-A[(w_{2},\eta_{1}),(\xi(\zeta),0)]

\displaystyle\qquad\geq-C^{2}\gamma_{0}^{-1}\underbrace{\|e\|_{V}^{2}}_{I_{1}}+\frac{1}{2}\gamma_{0}^{-1}\|\zeta\|^{2}_{H_{h}^{\prime}}-\gamma_{0}C^{2}\underbrace{E_{C}[(w_{1},\eta_{1}),(w_{2},\eta_{2})]^{2}}_{I_{2}}

(4.41)

We have already shown in (
4.40
) that
I_{1}=I_{2}=0
if both
\{w_{1},\eta_{1}\}

and
\{w_{2},\eta_{2}\}
are solutions, hence we conclude that

which finishes the discussion of (discrete) well-posedness.

4.3.2
Best approximation results

In this section we will derive a best approximation result for the solution of (
4.26
).
Due to the nonconforming character of the ALM we need to assume that the multiplier is in
H^{\prime}\cap L^{2}
. By specifying the approximation properties of our finite element spaces optimal a priori error estimates can be deduced.

Proposition 2

Assume that (
4.4
)-(
4.6
) and (
4.10
)-(
4.12
) hold. Let
(u,\lambda)\in V\times(H^{\prime}\cap L^{2})
be the solution to (
4.19
)-(
4.22

(u_{h},\lambda_{h})\in V_{h}\times H_{h}^{\prime}
be the solution of (
4.26

\Phi[(u,\lambda),(u_{h},\lambda_{h})]:=E_{C}[(u,\lambda),(u_{h},\lambda_{h})]+\|u-u_{h}\|_{V}+\gamma_{0}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\|\lambda-\lambda_{h}\|_{H_{h}^{\prime}}
then there holds

\boxed{\Phi[(u,\lambda),(u_{h},\lambda_{h})]\lesssim\inf_{(v_{h},\mu_{h})\in V_{h}\times H_{h}^{\prime}}(\|u-v_{h}\|_{V}+\gamma_{0}^{\frac{1}{2}}\|B(u-v_{h})\|_{H_{h}}+\gamma_{0}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\|\lambda-\mu_{h}\|_{H_{h}^{\prime}})}

(4.42)

Proof.

Since (
4.40
) holds for all
w_{1},w_{2}\in V
and
\zeta\in H^{\prime}\cap L^{2}
, if the exact solution
u,\lambda\in V\times H^{\prime}\cap L^{2}
we may apply it with
w_{1}=u
,
\eta_{1}=\lambda
and
w_{2}=u_{h}
,
\eta_{2}=\lambda_{h}
to obtain, with
e=u-u_{h}
and
\zeta=\lambda-\lambda_{h}

E_{C}[(u,\lambda),(u_{h},\lambda_{h})]^{2}+\alpha\|e\|_{V}^{2}\leq A[(u,\lambda),(e,-\zeta)]-A[(u_{h},\lambda_{h}),(e,-\zeta)]

(4.43)

Using the consistency of the method we have

A[(u,\lambda),(e,-\zeta)]-A[(u_{h},\lambda_{h}),(e,-\zeta)]=A[(u,\lambda),(u-i_{F}u,\pi_{H}\lambda-\lambda)]-A[(u_{h},\lambda_{h}),(u-i_{F}u,\pi_{H}\lambda-\lambda)]

By the continuity of
a
we have

a(u;u-i_{F}u)-a(u_{h},u-i_{F}u)\leq C\|e\|_{V}\|u-i_{F}u\|_{V}

For the nonlinearity imposing the constraint we notice that by the
L^{2}
-orthogonality of
\pi_{H}
,

\gamma^{-1}\left<\zeta,\lambda-\pi_{H}\lambda\right>=\gamma_{0}^{-1}\|\lambda-\pi_{H}\lambda\|_{H_{h}^{\prime}}^{2}

and using in addition the properties of

we have
\left<\pi_{H}\zeta,B(u-i_{F}u)+(\lambda-\pi_{H}\lambda)/\gamma\right>=0
and hence using that
\pi_{H}\zeta=\zeta+\pi_{H}\zeta-\zeta=\zeta-(\lambda-\pi_{H}\lambda)

\left<\gamma([Bu+\lambda/\gamma]_{+}-[Bu_{h}+\lambda_{h}/\gamma]_{+}),B(u-i_{F}u)+(\lambda-\pi_{H}\lambda)/\gamma\right>\\
=\left<\gamma([Bu+\lambda/\gamma]_{+}-[Bu_{h}+\lambda_{h}/\gamma]_{+})+\zeta,B(u-i_{F}u)+(\lambda-\pi_{H}\lambda)/\gamma\right>\\
-\left<\lambda-\pi_{H}\lambda,B(u-i_{F}u)+(\lambda-\pi_{H}\lambda)/\gamma\right>

Collecting the above inequalities we obtain using the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality and the arithmetic-geometric inequality in each right hand side,

A[(u,\lambda),(e,-\zeta)]-A[(u_{h},\lambda_{h}),(e,-\zeta)]\leq\frac{1}{2}(E_{C}[(u,\lambda),(u_{h},\lambda_{h})]^{2}+\alpha\|e\|_{V}^{2})\\

It follows that the following error bound holds,

E_{C}[(u,\lambda),(u_{h},\lambda_{h})]^{2}+\alpha\|e\|_{V}^{2}\lesssim\alpha\|u-i_{F}u\|_{V}^{2}+\gamma_{0}\|B(u-i_{F}u)\|^{2}_{H_{h}}+\gamma_{0}^{-1}\|\lambda-\pi_{H}\lambda\|_{H_{h}^{\prime}}^{2}

By adding and subtracting
v_{h}
, applying the triangle inequality followed by the stability of the Fortin operator (right inequality of (
4.5
)) there holds

\|u-i_{F}u\|_{V}+\gamma_{0}^{\frac{1}{2}}\|B(u-i_{F}u)\|_{H_{h}}\lesssim\|u-v_{h}\|_{V}+\gamma_{0}^{\frac{1}{2}}\|B(u-v_{h})\|_{H_{h}}

and we conclude using also the definition of the

-projection
\pi_{H}
, that

\boxed{E_{C}[(u,\lambda),(u_{h},\lambda_{h})]+\|e\|_{V}\lesssim\inf_{(v_{h},\mu_{h})\in V_{h}\times H_{h}^{\prime}}(\|u-v_{h}\|_{V}+\gamma_{0}^{\frac{1}{2}}\|B(u-v_{h})\|_{H_{h}}+\gamma_{0}^{-1}\|\lambda-\mu_{h}\|_{H_{h}^{\prime}})}

(4.45)

Turning to the error in the multiplier we have using (
4.6
)

\gamma_{0}^{-1}\|\pi_{H}\zeta\|_{H_{h}^{\prime}}^{2}=\left<\pi_{H}\zeta,B\xi(\gamma^{-1}\pi_{H}\zeta)\right>

where
\xi(\gamma^{-1}\pi_{H}\zeta)
is defined by (
4.6
) with
z_{h}=\gamma^{-1}\pi_{H}\zeta

using the equation we see that

\gamma_{0}^{-1}\|\pi_{H}\zeta\|_{H_{h}^{\prime}}^{2}=\left<\pi_{H}\lambda-\lambda,B\xi(\pi_{H}\zeta)\right>+\left<\gamma([Bu+\lambda/\gamma]_{+}-[Bu_{h}+\lambda`-h/\gamma]_{+})+\zeta,B\xi(\pi_{H}\zeta)\right>\\
+a(u;\xi(\pi_{H}\zeta))-a(u_{h};\xi(\pi_{H}\zeta))

Applying the bound (
4.12
) to the last two terms of the right hand side and the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality to the others and applying the stability of (

) we see that

a(u;\xi(\pi_{H}\eta))-a(u_{h};\xi(\pi_{H}\eta))\leq C_{4.12}\|e\|_{V}\|\xi(\pi_{H}\eta)\|_{V}\leq C_{4.12}\|e\|_{V}\gamma_{0}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\|\pi_{H}\zeta\|_{H_{h}^{\prime}},

\left<\pi_{H}\lambda-\lambda,B\xi(\pi_{H}\zeta)\right>\leq\gamma_{0}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\|\pi_{H}\lambda-\lambda\|_{H_{h}^{\prime}}\gamma_{0}^{\frac{1}{2}}\|B\xi(\pi_{H}\zeta)\|_{H_{h}}\leq\gamma_{0}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\|\pi_{H}\lambda-\lambda\|_{H_{h}^{\prime}}\gamma_{0}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\|\pi_{H}\zeta\|_{H_{h}^{\prime}}

and

\left<\gamma([Bu+\lambda/\gamma]_{+}-[Bu_{h}+\lambda_{h}/\gamma]_{+})+\eta,B\xi(\pi_{H}\eta)\right>\leq E_{C}[(u,\lambda),(u_{h},\lambda_{h})]\gamma_{0}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\|\pi_{H}\zeta\|_{H_{h}^{\prime}}.

Collecting terms and dividing through by

\gamma_{0}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\|\pi_{H}\zeta\|_{H_{h}^{\prime}}\lesssim E_{C}[(u,\lambda),(u_{h},\lambda_{h})]+\|e\|_{V}+\gamma_{0}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\|\pi_{H}\lambda-\lambda\|_{H_{h}^{\prime}}

We conclude by applying (

to obtain,

\boxed{\gamma_{0}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\|\zeta\|_{H_{h}^{\prime}}\lesssim\inf_{(v_{h},\mu_{h})\in V_{h}\times H_{h}^{\prime}}(\|u-v_{h}\|_{V}+\gamma_{0}^{\frac{1}{2}}\|B(u-v_{h})\|_{H_{h}})+\gamma_{0}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\|\lambda-\mu_{h}\|_{H_{h}^{\prime}})}

(4.46)

The claim now follows by combining (
4.42
) and (
4.45
).

We observe that the natural norm for

here would be

4.3.3
Remark on stabilized methods

If the discrete spaces
V_{h}
,
H_{h}^{\prime}
do not satisfy the infsup condition (
4.5
), one can introduce a stabilization operator
s(\cdot,\cdot)
which is designed to control the unstable modes.
If a stable pair
V_{h}
,
\tilde{H}_{h}^{\prime}
, where
\tilde{H}_{h}^{\prime}
has the same approximation properties as
H_{h}^{\prime}
up to a constant factor, is known, i.e. (
4.5
) and (
4.6
) are satisfied for these spaces, then a convenient way of choosing
s
is by using the following design criteria

1.

Control of unstable modes:

\gamma_{0}^{-1/2}\|\mu-\tilde{\pi}_{H}\mu\|_{H_{h}^{\prime}}\lesssim s(\mu,\mu)^{\frac{1}{2}},\quad\forall\mu_{h}\in H_{h}^{\prime}+L^{2}

(4.47)

where
\tilde{\pi}_{H}
denotes the
L^{2}
projection on
\tilde{H}_{h}
.

2.

Weak consistency:

s(\mu-\tilde{\pi}_{H}\mu,\mu-\tilde{\pi}_{H}\mu)\sim\gamma_{0}^{-1}\|\mu-\tilde{\pi}_{H}\mu\|_{H_{h}^{\prime}}^{2},\quad\forall\mu\in L^{2}

(4.48)

Here the
\sim
notation means that the two quantities have the same asymptotics in
h
for smooth enough
\mu
.

The simplest choice of
s
is

s(\eta,\mu)=\gamma^{-1}\left<(\pi_{H}-\tilde{\pi}_{H})\eta,\mu\right>

The optimality system of the finite
element formulation then reads: find
(u_{h},\lambda_{h})\in V_{h}\times H_{h}^{\prime}

such that

A[(u_{h},\lambda_{h});(v,\mu)]-s(\lambda_{h},\mu)=\left<f,v\right>_{V^{\prime},V}

(4.49)

for all
(v,\mu)\in V_{h}\times H_{h}^{\prime}
, with
A
defined in (
4.28
).

It is then possible to use the monotonicity, the inf-sup stability
(
4.5
) together with (
4.47
) and (
4.48
) to obtain bounds similar to (
4.42
) for the error of the stabilized Galerkin
approximation. We only sketch the arguments. The only modification of the stability is that the stabilization operator appears in the left hand side. If
e=u-u_{h}
and
\zeta=\lambda-\lambda_{h}
then

E_{C}[(u,\lambda),(u_{h},\lambda_{h})]^{2}+\alpha\|e\|_{V}^{2}+s(\zeta,\zeta)\leq A[(u,\lambda),(e,-\zeta)]-A[(u_{h},\lambda_{h}),(e,-\zeta)]-s(\zeta,-\zeta)

(4.50)

The key observation to obtain optimal approximation is to use Galerkin orthogonality using
u_{h}-i_{F}u
and
\lambda_{h}-\tilde{\pi}_{H}\lambda
and then apply a modified continuity estimate. Indeed by the assumptions we have

\left<\tilde{\pi}_{H}\zeta,B(u-i_{F}u)\right>=0
and hence we can modify the continuity (
4.44
) the following way,

\left<\gamma([Bu+\lambda/\gamma]_{+}-[Bu_{h}+\lambda_{h}/\gamma]_{+}),B(u-i_{F}u)+(\lambda-\tilde{\pi}_{H}\lambda)/\gamma\right>\\
=\left<\gamma([Bu+\lambda/\gamma]_{+}-[Bu_{h}+\lambda_{h}/\gamma]_{+})+\tilde{\pi}_{H}\zeta,B(u-i_{F}u)+(\lambda-\tilde{\pi}_{H}\lambda)/\gamma\right>\\
=\left<\gamma([Bu+\lambda/\gamma]_{+}-[Bu_{h}+\lambda_{h}/\gamma]_{+})+\zeta,B(u-i_{F}u)+(\lambda-\tilde{\pi}_{H}\lambda)/\gamma\right>\\
-\left<\zeta-\tilde{\pi}_{H}\zeta,B(u-i_{F}u)+(\lambda-\tilde{\pi}_{H}\lambda)/\gamma\right>

where we used that
\left<\tilde{\pi}_{H}\zeta,B(u-i_{F}u)+(\lambda-\tilde{\pi}_{H}\lambda)/\gamma\right>=0
.
In this expression all but the last term can be bounded in the same fashion as before. For the last term we apply the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality and then (
4.47
) to see that

\left<\zeta-\tilde{\pi}_{H}\zeta,B(u-i_{F}u)+(\lambda-\tilde{\pi}_{H}\lambda)/\gamma\right>\leq s(\zeta,\zeta)^{\frac{1}{2}}(\gamma_{0}^{\frac{1}{2}}\|B(u-i_{F}u)\|_{H_{h}}+\gamma_{0}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\|\lambda-\tilde{\pi}_{H}\lambda\|_{H_{h}^{\prime}})

where now the right hand side is controlled by stability and approximation respectively. This leads to an error estimate for
u-u_{h}
. The error in the multiplier can also be estimated using that

\|\zeta\|_{H^{\prime}_{h}}\leq\|\tilde{\pi}_{h}(\zeta-\zeta_{h})\|_{H^{\prime}_{h}}+s(\zeta,\zeta)^{\frac{1}{2}}

and noting that the first term of the right hand side can be controlled as in the infsup stable case and the second is bounded by (
4.47
).

4.4
Eliminating the multiplier

Now we assume that the multiplier can be expressed in the primal
variable through a linear operator
T
on the continuous level,
i.e.
\lambda=Tu
, such that for
v_{h}\in V_{h}
, the following inequality that typically is of inverse type, holds

\|Tu_{h}\|_{H_{h}^{\prime}}^{2}\leq C_{\text{I}}\|u_{h}\|^{2}_{V}.

(4.51)

where
C_{\text{I}}
is a constant that may depend on the mesh geometry, but not on the mesh size. We may then write the Nitsche type form of the equation
(
4.26
):
find
u_{h}\in V_{h}

such that

A[(u_{h},Tu_{h});(v,Tv)]=\left<f,v\right>_{V^{\prime},V}

(4.52)

for all

,
where
A_{h}
was defined in (
4.28
). This formulation,
where the multiplier is eliminated is identified as a nonlinear

For this GLS formulation existence and uniqueness is ensured without any
inf-sup condition
[
32
, Theorem 3.3]
. Stability is obtained thanks to the continuity of
the
T

4.51
).

We now revisit the analysis of the previous section and show that the same results hold for the case when the multiplier has been eliminated.

4.4.1
Continuity and stability

We only need to verify (
4.29
) for the method (
4.52

w_{1},w_{2},v\in V_{h}

\left<\gamma([Bw_{1}-Tw_{1}/\gamma]_{+}-[Bw_{2}-Tw_{2}/\gamma]_{+},Bv+Tv/\gamma\right>\\
\lesssim(\|B(w_{1}-w_{2})\|_{H_{h}}+\|T(w_{1}-w_{2})\|_{H_{h}^{\prime}})(\|Bv\|_{H_{h}}+\|Tv\|_{H_{h}^{\prime}})\\
\leq C(\|B(w_{1}-w_{2})\|_{H_{h}}+\|w_{1}-w_{2}\|_{V})(\|Bv\|_{H_{h}}+\|v\|_{V})

(4.53)

where we used (
4.51
) for the second inequality. To prove the a priori estimate that together with the continuity allows for the fixed point analysis we test with
v=u_{h}
in (
4.52
) to obtain using (
4.10
)

\alpha\|u_{h}\|_{V}^{2}+\|[Bu_{h}-Tu_{h}/\gamma]_{+}\|^{2}_{H_{h}}-\gamma_{0}^{-1}\|Tu_{h}\|_{H_{h}^{\prime}}^{2}\leq A[(u_{h},Tu_{h});(u_{h},Tu_{h})]

Applying (
4.51

\boxed{(\alpha-C_{I}/\gamma_{0})\|u_{h}\|_{V}^{2}+\gamma_{0}\|[Bu_{h}-Tu_{h}/\gamma]_{+}\|^{2}_{H_{h}}\leq A[(u_{h},Tu_{h});(u_{h},Tu_{h})]}

We conclude that the stability holds for
\gamma_{0}>C_{I}/\alpha
. Hence under this condition there exists a discrete solution to (
4.52
)

4.4.2
Uniqueness and best approximation estimates

Uniqueness and best approximation follows using similar arguments, we only detail the best approximation case.
We assume that the exact solution
u
to (
4.19
) is sufficiently smooth that

A_{h}[(u,Tu);(v,Tv)]=\left<f,v\right>_{V^{\prime},V},\forall v\in V_{h}

(4.54)

Then we may write,
e=u-u_{h}
and using the monotonicity of
a
(
4.11
) and of
[\cdot]_{+}
we see that, using the notation
E_{C}(u,u_{h}):=\gamma_{0}\|[Bu-Tu\gamma]_{+}-[Bu_{h}-Tu_{h}/\gamma]_{+}\|_{H_{h}}^{2}
,

A_{h}[(u,Tu);(e,Te)]-A_{h}[(u_{h},Tu_{h});(e,Te)]\\
\geq\alpha\|e\|_{V}^{2}+E_{C}(u,u_{h})-\gamma_{0}^{-1}\|Te\|_{H_{h}^{\prime}}^{2}

For the last term of the right hand side observe that

\|Te\|_{H_{h}^{\prime}}\leq\|T(u-v_{h})\|_{H_{h}^{\prime}}+\|T(u_{h}-v_{h})\|_{H_{h}^{\prime}}\\
\leq\|T(u-v_{h})\|_{H_{h}^{\prime}}+C_{I}^{1/2}\|u_{h}-v_{h}\|_{V}\\
\leq\|T(u-v_{h})\|_{H_{h}^{\prime}}+C_{I}^{1/2}(\|e\|_{V}+\|u-v_{h}\|_{V})

Hence

A_{h}[(u,Tu);(e,Te)]-A_{h}[(u_{h},Tu_{h});(e,Te)]\\
\geq(\alpha-3C_{I}/\gamma_{0})\|e\|_{V}^{2}+E_{C}(u,u_{h})-3\|T(u-v_{h})\|_{H_{h}^{\prime}}^{2}-3C_{I}\|u-v_{h}\|_{V}^{2}

(4.55)

Fix
\gamma_{0}=6C_{I}/\alpha
so that
\alpha-3C_{I}/\gamma_{0}=\alpha/2
.
Considering the left hand side we have using (
4.54
), for all
v_{h}\in V_{h}

A_{h}[(u,Tu);(e,Te)]-A_{h}[(u_{h},Tu_{h});(e,Te)]\\
=A_{h}[(u,Tu);(u-v_{h},T(u-v_{h}))]-A_{h}[(u_{h},Tu_{h});(u-v_{h},T(u-v_{h}))]

To conclude we use the continuity (
4.12
) and the arithmetic-geometric inequality,

a(u;u-v_{h})-a(u_{h};u-v_{h})\leq C\|e\|_{V}\|u-v_{h}\|_{V}\leq\frac{\alpha}{4}\|e\|_{V}^{2}+C^{2}\|u-v_{h}\|_{V}^{2}

together with the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality and the arithmetic-geometric inequality,

\left<\gamma([Bu-Tu\gamma]_{+}-[Bu_{h}-Tu_{h}/\gamma]_{+},B(u-v_{h})+T(u-v_{h})/\gamma\right>\\
\leq\frac{1}{2}E_{C}(u,u_{h})+\gamma_{0}\|B(u-v_{h})\|_{H_{h}}^{2}+\gamma_{0}^{-1}\|T(u-v_{h})\|_{H_{h}^{\prime}}^{2}.

Applying these inequalities in (
4.55
) we see that for all
v_{h}\in V_{h}

\alpha\|e\|_{V}^{2}+E_{C}(u,u_{h})\\
\lesssim\|u-v_{h}\|_{V}^{2}+\|B(u-v_{h})\|_{H_{h}}^{2}+\|T(u-v_{h})\|_{H_{h}^{\prime}}^{2}

Taking square roots of both sides and the infimum over
v_{h}\in V_{h}
in the right hand side we conclude

\boxed{E_{C}(u,u_{h})+\|e\|_{V}\lesssim\inf_{v_{h}\in V_{h}}(\|u-v_{h}\|_{V}+\|B(u-v_{h})\|_{H_{h}}+\|T(u-v_{h})\|_{H_{h}^{\prime}})}

(4.56)

4.52
). Observe that no condition needs to be imposed on the finite element space in this case. Instead stability is ensured by the inverse inequality (
4.51
) that bounds the
H_{h}^{\prime}
-norm of the multiplier expressed in the primal variable by the
V
-norm of the primal variable. By equivalence of norms on finite dimensional spaces this bound is always true. The key to optimality of the estimate is the proper
h
-scaling of the discrete norms given in (
4.14
) and (
4.15
).

5.1
The Stokes problem with cavitation

Consider a domain
\Omega
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
,
n=2
or
n=3
with boundary

\partial\Omega
that is composed of the two subsets
\Gamma_{D}
and

\Gamma_{N}
such that
\partial\Omega=\bar{\Gamma}_{D}\cup\bar{\Gamma}_{N}
. We consider a lubricant with viscosity
\mu
. The Stokes
equation can then be written

-\mu\Delta\boldsymbol{u}+\nabla p={\boldsymbol{f}}\;\text{and}\;\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{u}=0\quad\text{in}\;\Omega,

(5.1)

with
\boldsymbol{u}=0
on
\Gamma_{D}
and

(-p\boldsymbol{I}+\mu\nabla\boldsymbol{u})\cdot\boldsymbol{n}=\boldsymbol{0}
on
\Gamma_{N}
. Here,

\boldsymbol{u}
is the velocity of the lubricant,
p
is the pressure, and
{\boldsymbol{f}}
is a force term. The lubricant cannot support subatmospheric
pressure, so an additional condition is
p\geq 0
in
\Omega
. In order to
incorporate this condition into the model, it can be written as a
variational inequality as follows. Let

a(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v}):=\int_{\Omega}\mu\nabla\boldsymbol{u}:\nabla\boldsymbol{v}\,{\rm d}\Omega,\quad L(\boldsymbol{v}):=\int_{\Omega}\boldsymbol{f}\cdot\boldsymbol{v}\,{\rm d}\Omega

and

K=\{p\in L_{2}(\Omega):\quad p\geq 0\}

Seek
\boldsymbol{u}\in[H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)]^{n}
and
p\in K
such that

a(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v})-\int_{\Omega}p\,\nabla\cdot{\boldsymbol{v}}\,d\Omega=L(\boldsymbol{v}),

(5.2)

for all
{\boldsymbol{v}}\in[H^{1}(\Omega)]^{n}
, and

-\int_{\Omega}\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{u}\,(q-p)\,d\Omega\leq 0,\quad\forall q\in K

(5.3)

To rewrite this problem as a variational equality, we use the Kuhn-Tucker conditions

p\geq 0,\quad\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{u}\geq 0,\quad p\,\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{u}=0

(5.4)

and
again replace conditions (
5.4
) by the equivalent statement

p=\gamma_{0}[\gamma_{0}^{-1}p-\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{u}]_{+}

(5.5)

with
\gamma_{0}
a positive number. We note here that we can identify the abstract spaces
H
and
H^{\prime}
with
L_{2}(\Omega)

and that here the pressure cannot easily be interpreted as coming from a linear operator on the velocity, so we are in cases A and D from Section
4
; the pressure has to be retained but
r=0
in the discrete norms.

Defining function spaces

V=\{\boldsymbol{v}\in[H^{1}(\Omega)]^{n}:\;\boldsymbol{v}=\boldsymbol{0}\;\text{on $\Gamma^{\text{D}}$}\},\quad Q=L_{2}(\Omega)

(5.6)

and seeking
(\boldsymbol{u},p)\in V\times Q

we seek stationary points to the functional

\mathcal{L}_{\text{A}}(\boldsymbol{u},p):=\frac{1}{2}a(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{u})-L(\boldsymbol{u})+\int_{\Omega}\frac{\gamma_{0}}{2}\left[\gamma_{0}^{-1}p-\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{u}\right]_{+}^{2}d\Omega-\int_{\Omega}\frac{1}{2\gamma_{0}}p^{2}d\Omega

(5.7)

analogously to (
4.24
).

For the discrete problem, we will use the inf–sup stable Taylor-Hood approximation which utilises the finite element space

\vec{V}^{h}=\{\boldsymbol{v}:\boldsymbol{v}\in\left[C^{0}(\Omega)\right]^{d},\;\boldsymbol{v}|_{K}\in[P^{2}(K)]^{d},\ \forall K\in{\mathcal{T}}^{h},\;\text{$\boldsymbol{v}=\boldsymbol{0}$ on $\Gamma^{\text{D}}$}\}

for the velocity, where
P^{2}(K)
denotes the space of piecewise quadratic polynomials on
K
,
and the space

Q_{h}
of piecewise linears for the pressure:

Q^{h}=\{p\in C^{0}(\Omega):\;p|_{K}\in P^{1}(K),\,\forall K\in{\mathcal{T}}^{h}\}.

(5.8)

The finite element method based on (
5.7
) is to find
(\boldsymbol{u}^{h},p^{h})\in\vec{V}^{h}\times Q^{h}
such that

a(\boldsymbol{u}^{h},\boldsymbol{v})-\int_{\Omega}{\gamma_{0}}[\gamma_{0}^{-1}p^{h}-\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{u}^{h}]_{+}\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{v}d\Omega=(\boldsymbol{f},\boldsymbol{v})\quad\forall\boldsymbol{v}\in\vec{V}^{h},

(5.9)

and

\int_{\Omega}\left({\gamma_{0}}[\gamma_{0}^{-1}p^{h}-\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{u}^{h}]_{+}-p^{h}\right)qd\Omega=0,\quad\forall q\in Q^{h}.

(5.10)

5.1.1
Satisfaction of assumptions for the abstract analysis

For the present problem we have
V=[H^{1}(\Omega)]^{n}
,
H=H_{h}=H_{h}^{\prime}=L^{2}(\Omega)
. The constraint operator
B
is the divergence operator.
It if well known that the Taylor-Hood element admits a Fortin interpolant satisfying

\|\nabla\pi_{F}\boldsymbol{v}\|_{\Omega}\lesssim\|\nabla\boldsymbol{v}\|_{\Omega},\forall\boldsymbol{v}\in[H^{1}(\Omega)]^{d}.

Since
\|\nabla\pi_{F}\boldsymbol{v}\|_{\Omega}\lesssim\|\nabla\pi_{F}\boldsymbol{v}\|_{\Omega}
the relation (
4.5
) holds. This means that for all
\mu_{h}\in Q_{h}
there exists
\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\in\vec{V}^{h}
such that
(\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{v}_{h},q_{h})_{\Omega}=(\mu_{h},q_{h})_{\Omega}
and
\|\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\|_{V}\lesssim\|\mu_{h}\|_{\Omega}
. Hence (
4.6
) is also satisfied.

Since
a(\cdot,\cdot)
is a linear operator in this case we see that (
4.10
)-(
4.12
) are satisfied using standard arguments.
Hence the assumptions of section
4
are satisfied in this case and hence
we conclude that the best approximation estimate
(
4.42
) holds.

5.2
Weak imposition of Dirichlet boundary conditions

5.2.1
Model problem

Let us first consider the Poisson model problem: find
u:\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
such
that

-\Delta u=f~\text{in}~\Omega,\quad u=g~\text{on}~\Gamma:=\partial\Omega

(5.11)

where
\Omega
is a bounded domain in two or three space dimensions, with outward
pointing normal
\boldsymbol{n}
, and
f
and
g
are given functions. For simplicity,
we shall assume that
\Omega
is polyhedral (polygonal).
A classical way of prescribing
u=g
on the boundary is to pose the problem (
5.11
) as a minimisation problem with side conditions and seek stationary points to the functional

\mathcal{L}(v,\mu):=\frac{1}{2}a(v,v)-\left<\mu,v-g\right>_{H^{1/2}(\Gamma),H^{-1/2}(\Gamma)}-(f,v)_{\Omega}

(5.12)

where

(f,v)_{\Omega}:=\int_{\Omega}fv\,d\Omega,\quad a(u,v):=\int_{\Omega}\nabla u\cdot\nabla v\,d\Omega

(5.13)

and
\left<\mu,v-g\right>_{H^{1/2}(\Gamma),H^{-1/2}(\Gamma)}
is interpreted as a duality pairing on
H^{-1/2}(\Gamma)\times H^{1/2}(\Gamma)
. We are thus in case B of Sec.
4
, and the method proposed will only make sense on discrete spaces.

The stationary points to (
5.12
) are given by finding
(u,\lambda)\in H^{1}(\Omega)\times H^{-1/2}(\Gamma)
such that

a(u,v)-\left<\lambda,v\right>_{H^{1/2}(\Gamma),H^{-1/2}(\Gamma)}=(f,v)\quad\forall v\in H^{1}(\Omega)

(5.14)

\left<\mu,u\right>_{H^{1/2}(\Gamma),H^{-1/2}(\Gamma)}=\left<\mu,g\right>_{H^{1/2}(\Gamma),H^{-1/2}(\Gamma)}\quad\forall\mu\in H^{-1/2}(\Gamma)

(5.15)

As mentioned above, the discretisation of this problem requires
balancing of the discrete spaces for the multiplier
\lambda
and the primal solution
u
in order for the method to be stable.

5.2.2
The augmented Lagrangian method for boundary conditions

The Lagrangian in (
5.12
) is augmented by a penalty term scaled by a parameter
\gamma\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
so that we seek stationary points to

\mathcal{L}_{A}(v,\mu):=\frac{1}{2}a(v,v)-\left<\mu,v-g\right>_{H^{1/2}(\Gamma),H^{-1/2}(\Gamma)}+\frac{\gamma}{2}\|(v-g)\|^{2}_{H^{1/2}(\Gamma)}-(f,v)_{\Omega}

(5.16)

We note that the continuous norms imply
r=1/2
in the discrete norms.
To find the stationary points we seek
(u,\lambda)
such that

\displaystyle a(u,v)-\left<\lambda,v\right>_{H^{1/2}(\Gamma),H^{-1/2}(\Gamma)}+\gamma(u-g,v)_{H^{1/2}(\Gamma)}+\left<u,\mu\right>_{H^{1/2}(\Gamma),H^{-1/2}(\Gamma)}={}

\displaystyle(f,v)_{\Omega}

\displaystyle+\gamma\langle g,v\rangle_{H^{1/2}(\Gamma)}

\displaystyle+\left<g,\mu\right>_{H^{1/2}(\Gamma),H^{-1/2}(\Gamma)}

To determine the Lagrange multiplier
\lambda
we set
\mu=0
, and
integrate by parts which gives

\displaystyle(-\Delta v+f,v)_{\Omega}+\langle\nabla_{n}u-\lambda,v\rangle_{H^{1/2}(\Gamma),H^{-1/2}(\Gamma)}=0

(5.17)

For the exact solution the first term vanish and we conclude that
\lambda=\nabla_{n}v
.

We now wish to find a stable discrete counterpart to this optimisation problem. To this end, let
\mathcal{T}_{h}

be a family of quasi–uniform partitions, with
mesh parameter
h
, of
\Omega
into shape
regular triangles or tetrahedra
T
and the discrete space

V_{h}:=\{v_{h}\in H^{1}(\Omega):v_{h}|_{T}\in\mathbb{P}_{k}(T),\,\forall T\in{\mathcal{T}_{h}}\},\quad\mbox{ for }k\geq 1

(5.18)

and some discrete space
Q_{h}
(not explicitly defined) for the approximation of the
Lagrange multiplier.

We first follow the idea of (
4.14
) and replace the
H^{1/2}
–norm by the discrete counterpart
h^{-1/2}\|\cdot\|_{L_{2}(\Gamma)}
, which by an inverse estimate dominates the
H^{1/2}(\Gamma)
norm,

\|v\|^{2}_{H^{1/2}(\Gamma)}\lesssim h^{-1}\|v\|^{2}_{L_{2}(\Gamma)}\quad\text{$v\in V_{h}$}

(5.19)

and introduce the problem of finding the stationary point in
V_{h}\times Q_{h}
of the discrete Lagrangian

\mathcal{L}_{A}^{h}(v,\mu):=\frac{1}{2}a(v,v)-({\mu},{v-g})_{\Gamma}+\frac{\gamma_{0}}{2h}\|v-g\|^{2}_{L_{2}(\Gamma)}-(f,v)_{\Omega}

(5.20)

Recalling next that formally the Lagrange multiplier in (
5.14
) is given by
\mu=\nabla_{n}v
, which provides a direct way of computing the Lagrange multiplier from the primal solution, we obtain

\mathcal{L}_{A}^{h}(v):=\frac{1}{2}a(v,v)-(\nabla_{n}{v},{v-g})_{\Gamma}+\frac{\gamma_{0}}{2h}\|v-g\|^{2}_{L_{2}(\Gamma)}-(f,v)_{\Omega}

(5.21)

This is our stabilised ALM, the minimiser to which solves the problem of finding
u_{h}\in V_{h}
such that

a(u_{h},v)-({\nabla_{n}u_{h}},{v})_{\Gamma}-({\nabla_{n}v},{u_{h}})_{\Gamma}+\gamma_{0}h^{-1}({u_{h}},{v})_{\Gamma}=l(v)\quad\forall v\in V_{h}

(5.22)

where

l(v):=(f,v)+(\gamma_{0}h^{-1}{v}-{\nabla_{n}v},{g})_{\Gamma}

(5.23)

We identify the classical method of Nitsche
[
85
]
, stable if
\gamma_{0}
is chosen so that
\gamma_{0}>\gamma_{C}
, where
\gamma_{C}
is the
constant in the inverse inequality

h\|\nabla_{n}v\|_{L_{2}(\Gamma)}^{2}\leq\gamma_{C}\|\nabla v\|_{L_{2}(\Omega)}^{2}

(5.24)

Remark 1

As shown by Stenberg
[
97
]
(and discussed in Sec.
4.4
), Nitsche’s method can be viewed as a particular instance of the GLS stabilisation method of Barbosa–Hughes
[
4
]
; in this sense the ALM is a variant of GLS, with the multiplier eliminated.

Remark 2

We note that the ALM leads to the symmetric form of Nitsche’s method. The corresponding unsymmetric forms, as discussed, e.g., in
[
36
]
, are derived using different arguments.

5.3
Inequality boundary conditions

An important feature of the augmented Lagrangian approach is that it can be extended
to the case of inequality constraints, as first shown by Chouly and Hild in the context of elastic contact
[
32
]
. We consider the problem: find
u:\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
such that

-\Delta u=f~\text{in}~\Omega,\quad u-g\leq 0~\text{on}~\Gamma

(5.25)

We have the following Kuhn–Tucker conditions on the multiplier and side condition:

u-g\leq 0,\quad\lambda\leq 0,\quad\lambda(u-g)=0.

(5.26)

We now use the analogue to (
2.29
), that
(
5.26
) is equivalent to

\lambda=-{\gamma}\,[u-g-\gamma^{-1}\,\lambda]_{+}

(5.27)

first used in this context by Alart and Curnier
[
1
]
.
Now we can take another route to the augmented Lagrangian method. Taking the discrete counterpart to the standard multiplier equilibrium equation (
5.14
) we find

(f,v)=a(u_{h},v)-({\lambda_{h}},{v})_{\Gamma}=a(u_{h},v)-({\lambda_{h}},{v-\gamma^{-1}\mu})_{\Gamma}-({\gamma^{-1}\lambda_{h}},{\mu})_{\Gamma}

(5.28)

for all
v\in V_{h}
and
\mu\in Q_{h}
arbitrary.
Using now (
5.27
) we find

(f,v)=a(u_{h},v)+({{\gamma}\,[u_{h}-g-\gamma^{-1}\,\lambda_{h}]_{+}},{v-\gamma^{-1}\mu})_{\Gamma}-({\gamma^{-1}\lambda_{h}},{\mu})_{\Gamma}\quad\forall(v,\mu)\in V_{h}\times Q_{h}.

(5.29)

This is the optimality system for the Lagrangian

\mathcal{L}_{A}^{h}(v,\mu):=\frac{1}{2}a(v,v)+\frac{1}{2}\|\gamma^{1/2}[v-g-\gamma^{-1}\mu]_{+}\|_{L_{2}(\Gamma)}^{2}-\|\gamma^{-1/2}\mu\|^{2}_{L_{2}(\Gamma)}-(f,v)_{\Omega}

(5.30)

cf.
[
1
]
.

Approximating
\lambda_{h}\approx\partial_{n}u_{h}
and setting
\mu=\partial_{n}v
, we seek
u_{h}\in V_{h}
such that

a(u_{h},v)+({{\gamma}\,[u_{h}-g-\gamma^{-1}\,\partial_{n}u_{h}]_{+}},{v-\gamma^{-1}\partial_{n}v})_{\Gamma}-({\gamma^{-1}\partial_{n}u_{h}},{\partial_{n}v})_{\Gamma}=(f,v)_{\Omega}\quad\forall\in V_{h}

(5.31)

The solution to this problem is the minimiser of the nonlinear augmented Lagrangian

\mathcal{L}_{A}^{h}(v):=\frac{1}{2}a(v,v)+\frac{1}{2}\|\gamma^{1/2}[v_{h}-g-\gamma^{-1}\partial_{n}v]_{+}\|_{L_{2}(\Gamma)}^{2}-\|\gamma^{-1/2}\partial_{n}v\|^{2}_{L_{2}(\Gamma)}-(f,v_{h})_{\Omega}

(5.32)

Again, we choose
\gamma=\gamma_{0}/h
.
Variants and several extensions of (
5.31
) can be found in
[
23
]
. We remark here that (
5.31
) coincides with (
5.22
) in case of contact and gives a penalty on
\partial_{n}u=0
on
\Gamma
in case of no contact. This penalty does not destroy the coercivity of the problem if (
5.19
) is satisfied.

Remark 3

In the GLS stabilisation for variational inequalities proposed by Barbosa and Hughes
[
5
]
, no penalty is added to the Lagrangian;
the multiplier is not eliminated, and their approach is a stabilised Lagrange multiplier method which requires the solution of
an inequality problem. It is also possible to retain the multiplier in the ALM and add GLS stabilisation to the augmented Lagrangian.
This approach, which also leads to a nonlinear equality problem, was explored in
[
67
]
.

5.3.1
Satisfaction of assumptions for the abstract analysis

In this case
V=H^{1}(\Omega)
and
H=H^{\frac{1}{2}}(\partial\Omega)
,
H^{\prime}=H^{-\frac{1}{2}}(\partial\Omega)
. However since the solution to (
5.25
) is known to have the additional regularity
u\in H^{\frac{3}{2}+\epsilon}(\Omega)
,
\epsilon>0
it follows that
\partial_{n}u\in L^{2}(\Omega)
and the discrete norms
H_{h}
and
H_{h}^{\prime}
defined by (
4.14
) and (
4.15
) are well defined on the exact solution.
While (
5.24
) then is enough to make the formulation (
5.31
) satisfy the assumptions necessary for the analysis of section
4.4
, the formulation (
5.30
) still requires the satisfaction of (
4.5
) and (
4.6
). For a charaterisation of spaces satisfying these conditions (in the
h
-weighted
L^{2}
-norm) we refer to
[
87
]
. An example of a construction is two space dimension is to take element wise constant approximation for
Q_{h}
and let
V_{h}
consist of piecewise quadratic continuous approximation, or piecewise affine approximation enriched with a quadratic bubble added to elements adjacent to the boundary on each boundary face. The Fortin interpolant can then be constructed by first defining the nodal degrees of freedom using any
H^{1}
-stable interpolant and then fixing the degree of freedom associated to the bubble on each boundary faces so that (
4.5
) and (
4.6
) are satisfied. Indeed here they are equivalent. The same construction may be used for the forthcoming sections.

5.4
A model for elastic contact

5.4.1
Treatment of Robin boundary conditions

To show the versatility of the ALM
we shall consider the equations of linear elasticity in contact with a springy substrate. We start with the linear case of a Robin boundary condition: Find the displacement
{\boldsymbol{u}}=\left[u_{i}\right]_{i=1}^{n}
and the symmetric stress tensor
{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}=\left[\sigma_{ij}\right]_{i,j=1}^{n}
such that

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}={}

\displaystyle\frac{\nu E}{(1+\nu)(1-2\nu)}~\text{tr}\,{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}({\boldsymbol{u}})\,{\boldsymbol{I}}+\frac{E}{(1+\nu)}{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}({\boldsymbol{u}})\quad\text{in}\quad\Omega,

(5.33)

\displaystyle-\nabla\cdot{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}={}

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{f}}\quad\text{in}\quad\Omega,

(5.34)

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{S}}{\boldsymbol{u}}={}

\displaystyle-{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}\cdot{\boldsymbol{n}}\quad\text{on}\quad\partial\Omega_{\text{S}},

(5.35)

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}\cdot{\boldsymbol{n}}={}

\displaystyle{\bf 0}\quad\text{on}\quad\partial\Omega\setminus\partial\Omega_{\text{S}}.

(5.36)

Here
\Omega
is a closed subset of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
n=2
or
n=3
,

E
is Young’s modulus and
\nu
is Poisson’s ratio.

{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}\left({\boldsymbol{u}}\right)=\left[\varepsilon_{ij}({\boldsymbol{u}})\right]_{i,j=1}^{n}

is the strain tensor with components

\varepsilon_{ij}({\boldsymbol{u}})=\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\partial u_{i}}{\partial x_{j}}+\frac{\partial u_{j}}{\partial x_{i}}\right),

and trace

\text{tr}\,{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}({\boldsymbol{u}})=\sum_{i}\varepsilon_{ii}({\boldsymbol{u}})=\nabla\cdot{\boldsymbol{u}}.

Furthermore,

\nabla\cdot{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}=\left[\sum_{j=1}^{n}\partial\sigma_{ij}/\partial x_{j}\right]_{i=1}^{n}
,

{\boldsymbol{I}}=\left[\delta_{ij}\right]_{i,j=1}^{n}
with
\delta_{ij}=1

if
i=j
and
\delta_{ij}=0
if
i\neq j
, and
{\boldsymbol{f}}
is a
given load. Finally, we assume that the boundary stiffness
{\boldsymbol{S}}
is of the form

{\boldsymbol{S}}=\alpha^{-1}{\boldsymbol{n}}\otimes{\boldsymbol{n}}+\beta^{-1}{\boldsymbol{P}},\quad{\boldsymbol{P}}:=({\boldsymbol{I}}-{\boldsymbol{n}}\otimes{\boldsymbol{n}})

where
\alpha
and
\beta
are flexibility parameters in the normal and tangential direction, respectively.
The solution to (
5.33
)–(
5.36
) minimises the functional

\mathcal{L}_{S}({\boldsymbol{u}}):=\frac{1}{2}a({\boldsymbol{u}},{\boldsymbol{u}})-({\boldsymbol{f}},{\boldsymbol{u}})_{\Omega}+\langle{\boldsymbol{S}}{\boldsymbol{u}},{\boldsymbol{u}}\rangle_{\partial\Omega_{\text{S}}}

(5.37)

where

a({\boldsymbol{u}},{\boldsymbol{v}}):=({\boldsymbol{\sigma}}({\boldsymbol{u}}),{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}({\boldsymbol{v}}))_{\Omega}=\int_{\Omega}{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}({\boldsymbol{u}}):{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}({\boldsymbol{v}})\,d\Omega

which is the usual foundation for a discrete method. However, to obtain a robust method for the case of
\alpha\rightarrow 0
or
\beta\rightarrow 0
, we can introduce a new variable
{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}\in[L_{2}(\partial\Omega_{\text{S}})]^{n}
and seek stationary points to

\mathcal{L}({\boldsymbol{u}},{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}):=\frac{1}{2}a({\boldsymbol{u}},{\boldsymbol{u}})-({\boldsymbol{f}},{\boldsymbol{u}})_{\Omega}-\frac{1}{2}\langle{\boldsymbol{K}}{\boldsymbol{\lambda}},{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}\rangle_{\partial\Omega_{\text{S}}}-\langle{\boldsymbol{\lambda}},{\boldsymbol{u}}\rangle_{\partial\Omega_{\text{S}}}

(5.38)

where
{\boldsymbol{K}}:={\boldsymbol{S}}^{-1}
is a flexibility matrix which simply tends to the zero matrix if
\alpha,\beta\rightarrow 0
, and the Robin condition becomes a Dirichlet condition.
The stationary point to (
5.38
) fulfils the variational equations of finding
({\boldsymbol{u}},{\boldsymbol{\lambda}})\in[H^{1}(\Omega)]^{n}\times[L_{2}(\partial\Omega_{\text{S}})]^{n}
such that

\displaystyle a({\boldsymbol{u}},{\boldsymbol{v}})-\langle{\boldsymbol{\lambda}},{\boldsymbol{v}}\rangle_{\partial\Omega_{\text{S}}}={}

\displaystyle({\boldsymbol{f}},{\boldsymbol{v}})_{\Omega}\quad\forall{\boldsymbol{v}}\in[H^{1}(\Omega)]^{n}

(5.39)

\displaystyle\langle{\boldsymbol{K}}{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}+{\boldsymbol{u}},{\boldsymbol{\mu}}\rangle_{\partial\Omega_{\text{S}}}={}

\displaystyle{0}\quad\forall{\boldsymbol{\mu}}\in[L_{2}(\partial\Omega_{\text{S}})]^{n}

(5.40)

and we note that, formally,

{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}={\boldsymbol{\sigma}}({\boldsymbol{u}})\cdot{\boldsymbol{n}}

(5.41)

In the discrete case, we can now formulate an ALM by adding a penalty term and replacing
{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}
using (
5.41
), looking for the minimiser of

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{A}^{h}({\boldsymbol{u}}):={}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}a({\boldsymbol{u}},{\boldsymbol{u}})-({\boldsymbol{f}},{\boldsymbol{u}})_{\Omega}-\langle{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}({\boldsymbol{u}})\cdot{\boldsymbol{n}},{\boldsymbol{u}}\rangle_{\partial\Omega_{\text{S}}}

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\langle{\boldsymbol{K}}{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}({\boldsymbol{u}})\cdot{\boldsymbol{n}},{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}({\boldsymbol{u}})\cdot{\boldsymbol{n}}\rangle_{\partial\Omega_{\text{S}}}+\frac{1}{2}\langle{\boldsymbol{S}}_{h}({\boldsymbol{K}}{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}({\boldsymbol{u}})\cdot{\boldsymbol{n}}+{\boldsymbol{u}}),{\boldsymbol{K}}{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}({\boldsymbol{u}})\cdot{\boldsymbol{n}}+{\boldsymbol{u}}\rangle_{\partial\Omega_{\text{S}}}

(5.42)

where
{\boldsymbol{S}}_{h}
is a discrete stiffness matrix, to be chosen. The minimiser to (
5.42
) satisfies the variational equation of finding
{\boldsymbol{u}}_{h}\in[V_{h}]^{n}
such that

a_{{\boldsymbol{S}}_{h}}({\boldsymbol{u}}_{h},{\boldsymbol{v}})=({\boldsymbol{f}},{\boldsymbol{v}})_{\Omega}\quad\forall{\boldsymbol{v}}\in V:=[V_{h}]^{n}

(5.43)

where

\displaystyle a_{{\boldsymbol{S}}_{h}}({\boldsymbol{u}},{\boldsymbol{v}}):={}

\displaystyle a({\boldsymbol{u}},{\boldsymbol{v}})-\langle{\boldsymbol{u}}+{\boldsymbol{K}}{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}({\boldsymbol{u}})\cdot{\boldsymbol{n}},{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}({\boldsymbol{v}})\cdot{\boldsymbol{n}}\rangle_{\partial\Omega_{\text{S}}}-\langle{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}({\boldsymbol{u}})\cdot{\boldsymbol{n}},{\boldsymbol{v}}+{\boldsymbol{K}}{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}({\boldsymbol{v}})\cdot{\boldsymbol{n}}\rangle_{\partial\Omega_{\text{S}}}

(5.44)

\displaystyle+\langle{\boldsymbol{K}}{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}({\boldsymbol{u}})\cdot{\boldsymbol{n}},{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}({\boldsymbol{v}})\cdot{\boldsymbol{n}}\rangle_{\partial\Omega_{\text{S}}}+\langle{\boldsymbol{S}}_{h}({\boldsymbol{u}}+{\boldsymbol{K}}{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}({\boldsymbol{u}})\cdot{\boldsymbol{n}}),{\boldsymbol{v}}+{\boldsymbol{K}}{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}({\boldsymbol{v}})\cdot{\boldsymbol{n}}\rangle_{\partial\Omega_{\text{S}}}

(5.45)

which is related to the Nitsche method for interfaces in
[
64
,
65
]
, and a variant of the method of Juntunen and Stenberg
[
78
]
for Poisson’s problem with Robin boundary conditions.
With the particular choice

{\boldsymbol{S}}_{h}=\left((h/\gamma_{0}){\boldsymbol{I}}+{\boldsymbol{K}}\right)^{-1}

(5.46)

we regain the standard Nitsche method for the Dirichet problem if
{\boldsymbol{K}}
is the zero matrix, and if
{\boldsymbol{K}}
is nonzero we approach the minimiser of (
5.37
) as
h\rightarrow 0
.
Thus the method is robust also in the limit of zero flexibility.

5.4.2
One–sided conditions in contact

We now wish to activate the Robin boundary only if
{\boldsymbol{u}}\cdot{\boldsymbol{n}}-g>0
, corresponding to contact with a springy foundation at a distance
g
from the elastic body. Since this condition is only on the normal part of the displacement, we consider the case of slip, i.e., we choose

{\boldsymbol{K}}=\alpha{\boldsymbol{n}}\otimes{\boldsymbol{n}}

Setting
\sigma_{n}:={\boldsymbol{n}}\cdot{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}\cdot{\boldsymbol{n}}
and
u_{n}={\boldsymbol{u}}\cdot{\boldsymbol{n}}
, the linear case is then to find stationary points to (
5.38
) simplified as

\mathcal{L}({\boldsymbol{u}},\lambda_{n}):=\frac{1}{2}a({\boldsymbol{u}},{\boldsymbol{u}})-({\boldsymbol{f}},{\boldsymbol{u}})_{\Omega}-\frac{1}{2}\langle\alpha\lambda_{n},\lambda_{n}\rangle_{\partial\Omega_{\text{S}}}-\langle\lambda_{n},u_{n}-g\rangle_{\partial\Omega_{\text{S}}}

(5.47)

where formally
\lambda_{n}=\sigma_{n}({\boldsymbol{u}})
. In the case of contact we now have the KKT condition

\displaystyle u_{n}-g+\alpha\lambda_{n}\leq{}

\displaystyle 0

(5.48)

\displaystyle\lambda_{n}\leq{}

\displaystyle 0

(5.49)

\displaystyle\lambda_{n}\left(u_{n}-g+\alpha\lambda_{n}\right)={}

\displaystyle 0

(5.50)

which we can formally rewrite as

\lambda_{n}=-\gamma[(u_{n}-g+\alpha\lambda_{n})-\gamma^{-1}\lambda_{n}]_{+}

(5.51)

Proceeding as in (
5.28
), the equilibrium equation resulting from (
5.47
) is

({\boldsymbol{f}},{\boldsymbol{v}})_{\Omega}=a({\boldsymbol{u}},{\boldsymbol{v}})-\langle\lambda_{n},v_{n}\rangle_{\partial\Omega_{\text{S}}}

(5.52)

and seeing as

\displaystyle-\langle\lambda_{n},v_{n}\rangle_{\partial\Omega_{\text{S}}}={}

\displaystyle-\langle\lambda_{n},v_{n}+\alpha\mu_{n}\rangle_{\partial\Omega_{\text{S}}}+\langle\alpha\lambda_{n},\mu_{n}\rangle_{\partial\Omega_{\text{S}}}

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle-\langle\lambda_{n},v_{n}+(\alpha-\gamma^{-1})\mu_{n}\rangle_{\partial\Omega_{\text{S}}}+\langle(\alpha-\gamma^{-1})\lambda_{n},\mu_{n}\rangle_{\partial\Omega_{\text{S}}}

(5.53)

with
\mu_{n}
arbitrary, we find that the discrete augmented Lagrangian can be written

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{A}^{h}({\boldsymbol{u}},\lambda_{n}):={}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}a({\boldsymbol{u}},{\boldsymbol{u}})-({\boldsymbol{f}},{\boldsymbol{u}})_{\Omega}+\frac{1}{2}\|\gamma^{1/2}[(u_{n}-g+(\alpha-\gamma^{-1})\lambda_{n})]_{+}\|^{2}_{\partial\Omega_{\text{S}}}

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}\langle(\alpha-\gamma^{-1})\lambda_{n},\lambda_{n}\rangle_{\partial\Omega_{\text{S}}}

(5.54)

and with
\lambda_{n}\approx\sigma_{n}({\boldsymbol{u}})
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{A}^{h}({\boldsymbol{u}}):={}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}a({\boldsymbol{u}},{\boldsymbol{u}})-({\boldsymbol{f}},{\boldsymbol{u}})_{\Omega}+\frac{1}{2}\|\gamma^{1/2}[u_{n}-g+(\alpha-\gamma^{-1})\sigma_{n}({\boldsymbol{u}})]_{+}\|^{2}_{\partial\Omega_{\text{S}}}

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}\langle(\alpha-\gamma^{-1})\sigma_{n}({\boldsymbol{u}}),\sigma_{n}({\boldsymbol{u}})\rangle_{\partial\Omega_{\text{S}}}

(5.55)

the minimiser of which is
{\boldsymbol{u}}\in V
satisfying

\displaystyle({\boldsymbol{f}},{\boldsymbol{v}})_{\Omega}={}

\displaystyle a({\boldsymbol{u}},{\boldsymbol{v}})+\langle\gamma[u_{n}-g+(\alpha-\gamma^{-1})\sigma_{n}({\boldsymbol{u}})]_{+},v_{n}+(\alpha-\gamma^{-1})\sigma_{n}({\boldsymbol{v}})\rangle

\displaystyle+\langle(\alpha-\gamma^{-1})\sigma_{n}({\boldsymbol{u}}),\sigma_{n}({\boldsymbol{v}})\rangle_{\partial\Omega_{\text{S}}}\quad\forall{\boldsymbol{v}}\in V

(5.56)

which coincides with (
5.43
) in contact, and gives an additional penalty on the condition
\sigma_{n}({\boldsymbol{u}})=0
if there is no contact.
Choosing now

\gamma=(h/\gamma_{0}+\alpha)^{-1}\;\Rightarrow\;\alpha-\gamma^{-1}=-\frac{h}{\gamma_{0}}

(5.57)

we obtain
the same penalty on the normal stress as in
[
32
]
, which does not destroy the positive definite nature of the problem if we take

\gamma_{0}>\gamma_{C}
where
\gamma_{C}
is the (stiffness dependent) constant in the inverse inequality

\|h^{1/2}{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}({\boldsymbol{v}})\cdot{\boldsymbol{n}}\|_{\partial\Omega_{\text{S}}}^{2}\leq\gamma_{C}a({\boldsymbol{v}},{\boldsymbol{v}})\,\quad\forall{\boldsymbol{v}}\in V

(5.58)

5.5
Stabilising the Kirchhoff plate model

5.5.1
Approximation with independent rotations and displacement

In the Kirchhoff plate model, posed on a domain

\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
with boundary
\partial\Omega
, we seek an out–of–plane
(scalar) displacement
u
to which we associate the strain (curvature) tensor

{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}(\nabla u):=\frac{1}{2}\left(\nabla\otimes(\nabla u)+(\nabla u)\otimes\nabla\right)=\nabla\otimes\nabla u=\nabla^{2}u

(5.59)

and the plate stress (moment) tensor

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{P}(\nabla u)

\displaystyle:=\text{D}\left({\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}(\nabla u)+\nu(1-{\nu})^{-1}\nabla\cdot\nabla u\,{\boldsymbol{I}}\right)

(5.60)

\displaystyle=\text{D}\left(\nabla^{2}u+\nu(1-\nu)^{-1}\Delta u{\boldsymbol{I}}\right)

(5.61)

where

\text{D}=\frac{Et^{3}}{12(1+\nu)}

(5.62)

where
t
denotes the
plate thickness.

The Kirchhoff clamped problem then takes the form: given the out–of–plane
(scaled) load
t^{3}f
, find the displacement
u
such that

\displaystyle\nabla\cdot\left(\nabla\cdot{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{P}(\nabla u)\right)=t^{3}f

in
\Omega

(5.63)

\displaystyle u=0

on
\partial\Omega

(5.64)

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{n}}\cdot\nabla u=0

on
\partial\Omega

(5.65)

The corresponding variational problem takes the form:
Find the displacement
u\in H^{2}_{0}(\Omega)
such that

a_{P}(\nabla u,\nabla v)=(f,v)_{\Omega}\qquad\forall v\in H^{2}_{0}(\Omega

(5.66)

where

\displaystyle a_{P}(\nabla v,\nabla w)

\displaystyle:=(t^{-3}{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{P}(\nabla v),{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}(\nabla w))_{\Omega}

(5.67)

From a computational point of view (
5.66
) is cumbersome since it requires
C^{1}
–conforming elements or
carefully constructed nonconforming approximations. It is therefore common to use instead the Mindlin–Reissner model
which
is described by the following partial differential equations:

\begin{split}-t^{-3}\nabla\cdot{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{P}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})-\kappa\,t^{-2}\left(\nabla u-{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\right)&=0,\quad\text{in }\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{2},\\
-\kappa\,t^{-2}\,\nabla\cdot\left(\nabla u-{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\right)&=f,\quad\text{in }\Omega,\end{split}

(5.68)

where

{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
is the
rotation of the median surface and

\kappa
is a shear correction factor.
We note that this relaxes the continuity requirement on
u
and that, as
t\rightarrow 0
, tends to the Kirchhoff model.
However, the requirement on the approximation to allow
|\nabla u-{\boldsymbol{\theta}}|\rightarrow 0
is difficult to realise
in the discrete setting and if this condition cannot be met, shear locking occurs, destroying the approximation properties of the discrete model.
The ALM can offer an alternative approach in which we enforce the requirement
\nabla u={\boldsymbol{\theta}}
by a Lagrange multiplier.
To this end we consider the Lagrangian

\mathcal{L}(u,{\boldsymbol{\theta}},{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}):=\frac{1}{2}a_{P}({\boldsymbol{\theta}},{\boldsymbol{\theta}})+({\boldsymbol{\lambda}},\nabla u-{\boldsymbol{\theta}})_{\Omega}-(f,u)_{\Omega}

(5.69)

The Euler stationary points of (
5.69
) satisfy the weak system

\displaystyle a_{P}({\boldsymbol{\theta}},{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}})+({\boldsymbol{\lambda}},\nabla v-{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}})_{\Omega}={}

\displaystyle(f,v)_{\Omega}\quad\forall(v,{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}})\in H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)\times[H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)]^{2},

(5.70)

\displaystyle(\nabla u-{\boldsymbol{\theta}},{\boldsymbol{\mu}})_{\Omega}={}

\displaystyle 0\quad\forall{\boldsymbol{\mu}}\in[L_{2}(\Omega)]^{2},

(5.71)

corresponding to the strong form

\displaystyle-\nabla\cdot{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{P}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})

\displaystyle=t^{3}{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}\quad\text{in }\Omega

(5.72)

\displaystyle-\nabla\cdot{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}

\displaystyle=f\quad\text{in }\Omega

(5.73)

\displaystyle\nabla u-{\boldsymbol{\theta}}

\displaystyle=0\quad\text{in }\Omega

(5.74)

We now wish to stabilise (
5.69
) using the ALM. To this end, we
use (
5.72
) to eliminate
{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}
and add a penalty term on the side condition to obtain the augmented discrete functional

\mathcal{L}_{A}^{h}(u_{h},{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{h}):=\frac{1}{2}a_{P}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{h},{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{h})-(t^{-3}\nabla\cdot{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{P}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{h}),\nabla u_{h}-{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{h})_{h}+\frac{\gamma}{2}\|\nabla u_{h}-{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{h}\|^{2}_{\Omega}-(f,u_{h})_{\Omega}

(5.75)

where
u_{h}\in V^{h}_{1}
and
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{h}\in[V^{h}_{2}]^{2}
for some discrete spaces
V^{h}_{1}
and
V^{h}_{2}
. Here we use the notation

({\boldsymbol{u}},{\boldsymbol{v}})_{h}:=\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}\int_{T}{\boldsymbol{u}}\cdot{\boldsymbol{v}}\,dxdy

(5.76)

The Euler equations corresponding to the augmented system are

A_{h}(({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{h},u_{h}),({\boldsymbol{\vartheta}},v))=(f,v)\

(5.77)

for all
(v,{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}})\in V^{h}_{1}\times[V^{h}_{2}]^{2}
, where

A_{h}(({\boldsymbol{\theta}},u),({\boldsymbol{\vartheta}},v)):=a_{P}({\boldsymbol{\theta}},{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}})-(t^{-3}\nabla\cdot{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{P}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}),\nabla v-{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}})_{h}-(\nabla u-{\boldsymbol{\theta}},t^{-3}\nabla\cdot{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{P}({\boldsymbol{\vartheta}}))_{h}+\gamma(\nabla u-{\boldsymbol{\theta}},\nabla v-{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}})

(5.78)

Now, if
V_{2}^{h}
is the space of piecewise linears, the terms
(\cdot,\cdot)_{h}
vanish
and, seeing as
\theta\in H^{1}(\Omega)
and thus
\lambda\in H^{-1}(\Omega)
, we choose
r=1
in (
4.15
) and
\gamma=\gamma_{0}/h^{2}
to obtain a scheme proposed by Pitkäranta
[
88
]
; for higher order polynomial approximations we recover
a GLS stabilisation method due to Stenberg
[
96
,
6
]
.

5.5.2
The plate obstacle problem

We next consider applying the model from the previous Section to a regularised plate obstacle problem. The continuous model is

\displaystyle-\nabla\cdot{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{P}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})

\displaystyle=t^{3}{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}\quad\text{in }\Omega

(5.79)

\displaystyle-\nabla\cdot{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}+p

\displaystyle=f\quad\text{in }\Omega

(5.80)

\displaystyle\nabla u-{\boldsymbol{\theta}}

\displaystyle=0\quad\text{in }\Omega

(5.81)

\displaystyle p\geq 0,\;u-g+\beta p\geq 0,\;p(u-g+\beta p)

\displaystyle=0\quad\text{in }\Omega

(5.82)

Here,
\beta
is a given compliance which regularises the problem, in the limit case of
\beta=0

(rigid obstacle) we instead have the KKT conditions
p\geq 0
,
u-g\geq 0
, and
p(u-g)=0
. Note that the regularity in the limit case is insufficient for the analysis above.
Indeed it is well known that
u\not\in H^{4}(\Omega)
, which is insufficient for the multiplier to be in
L_{2}
.
It is however known that for
\beta>0
,
u\in H^{4}(\Omega)
if the interior angles of the domain are smaller than
126^{\circ}
(see
[
13
]
). Therefore the analysis is valid for all
\beta>0
,
since we have

p\in Q=\left\{\begin{array}[]{>{\displaystyle}l}L_{2}(\Omega)\quad\text{if $\beta>0$}\\
H^{-2}(\Omega)\quad\text{if $\beta=0$}\end{array}\right.

(5.83)

We see that, again, formally
{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}=-t^{-3}\nabla\cdot{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{P}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})
and that
p=f-t^{-3}\nabla\cdot\left(\nabla\cdot{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{P}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})\right)
. Following the
strategy from Sec.
5.4.2
we write

p=\epsilon[(u_{n}-g+\beta p)-\epsilon^{-1}p]_{+}

(5.84)

We need to also stabilise the rotations, and
to this end we consider the discrete Lagrangian

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{A}^{h}({\boldsymbol{\vartheta}},v):=

\displaystyle{}\frac{1}{2}A_{h}(({\boldsymbol{\vartheta}},v),({\boldsymbol{\vartheta}},v))+\frac{1}{2}\|\epsilon^{1/2}[u-g-(\epsilon^{-1}-\beta)(f-t^{-3}\nabla\cdot\left(\nabla\cdot{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{P}({\boldsymbol{\vartheta}}))\right)]_{+}\|_{h}^{2}

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\|(\epsilon^{-1}-\beta)^{1/2}(f-t^{-3}\nabla\cdot(\nabla\cdot{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{P}({\boldsymbol{\vartheta}})))\|_{h}^{2}-(f,v)_{\Omega}

(5.85)

where, considering the limit case
p\in H^{-2}(\Omega)
, we choose
r=2
and thus

\epsilon=(h^{4}/\gamma_{1}+\beta)^{-1}

(5.86)

with
\gamma_{1}
a sufficiently large constant. A similar approach has been suggested by Gustafsson et al.
[
61
,
62
]
in the context of
C^{1}
approximations of the clamped Kirchhoff plate with GLS stabilisation, without specific reference to augmented Lagrangian methods.
