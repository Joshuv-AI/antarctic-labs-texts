On the rigidity of Ricci shrinkers
Yu Li, and, Wenjia Zhang

Abstract
In this paper, we establish the rigidity of the generalized cylinder N n × ℝ m − n N^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{m-n} , or a quotient thereof, in the space of Ricci shrinkers equipped with the pointed-Gromov-Hausdorff topology. Here, N N is a stable Einstein manifold that has an obstruction of order 3 3 . The proof is based on a quantitative characterization of the rigidity of compact Ricci shrinkers, a rigidity inequality of mixed orders on generalized cylinders, and the method of contraction and extension. As an application, we prove the uniqueness of the tangent flow for general compact Ricci flows under the assumption that one tangent flow is a generalized cylinder.



1
Introduction

A Ricci shrinker
(M,g,f)
is a complete Riemannian manifold
(M,g)
together with a smooth function
f:M\to\mathbb{R}
such that

\mathrm{Rc}+\nabla^{2}f=\frac{1}{2}g,

(1.1)

where the potential function
f
is normalized such that

\displaystyle R+|\nabla f|^{2}

\displaystyle=f.

(1.2)

As the critical metrics of Perelman’s
\boldsymbol{\mu}
-functional, Ricci shrinkers play a crucial role in analyzing the singularity formation of the Ricci flow. Enders-Müller-Topping
[
24
]
prove that for a Ricci flow with a type-I curvature bound, any proper blow-up sequence converges smoothly to a non-trivial Ricci shrinker. Furthermore, Bamler
[
4
]
has shown that the finite-time singularities of general compact Ricci flows are modeled on Ricci shrinkers that contain a singular set, by using the theory of
\mathbb{F}
-convergence developed in
[
2
,
3
,
4
]
.

In dimension
2
and
3
, all Ricci shrinkers are completely classified (cf.
[
29
]
[
47
]
[
48
]
[
8
]
, etc). We know that
\mathbb{R}^{2},S^{2},\mathbb{R}^{3},S^{3},S^{2}\times\mathbb{R}
and their quotients form the complete list. However, in higher dimensions, the classification is far from complete and may even be impossible. We apply the strategy from
[
41
]
to consider all Ricci shrinkers of the same dimension
n
as a moduli space
\mathcal{M}_{n}
equipped with the pointed-Gromov-Hausdorff distance. Here, one can always assign a minimum point of the potential function
f
as the base point. In
[
41
]
and
[
32
]
, the weak-compactness theory is established for the moduli subspace
\mathcal{M}_{n}(A)
, which consists of all Ricci shrinkers with
\boldsymbol{\mu}\geq-A
. More precisely, any sequence of Ricci shrinkers in
\mathcal{M}_{n}(A)
subconverges to a Ricci shrinker conifold, and the convergence is smooth away from the singularities. In particular, if the limiting Ricci shrinker conifold is smooth, the convergence is improved to be pointed-Cheeger-Gromov.

The question of rigidity in geometry pertains to whether a particular geometric object is uniquely determined within its moduli space, i.e., whether it admits any non-trivial deformations. In the context of Ricci shrinkers, the rigidity problem concerns whether a given Ricci shrinker is isolated in the moduli space
\mathcal{M}_{n}
. The analogous question was studied by Koiso for compact Einstein manifolds
[
33
,
34
,
35
]
. Later, the deformation theory for compact Ricci shrinkers was established by Podestà-Spiro
[
52
]
and Kröncke
[
38
]
. In
[
38
]
, Kröncke proved that a compact Ricci shrinker
(N^{n},\bar{g},\bar{f})
is rigid if and only if any symmetric
2
-tensor
h
in the infinitesimal solitonic deformation (ISD) space (see Definition
2.5
) is not integrable up to some order (see Definition
2.14
). Conversely, if
(N^{n},\bar{g},\bar{f})
is not rigid, then there exists a smooth family of Ricci shrinker metrics
g(t)
starting from
\bar{g}
.

For example, the standard sphere
S^{n}
and its quotients are rigid since
\textrm{ISD}=0
. Kröncke
[
38
]
proved that
\mathbb{CP}^{2m}
with the standard metric is also rigid by showing that any
h\in\mathrm{ISD}
is not integrable up to order
2
. More recently, the rigidity of
S^{2}\times S^{2}
and
\mathbb{CP}^{2m-1}
was established by Sun-Zhu
[
57
]
and Li-Zhang
[
45
]
, respectively, by showing that any
h\in\mathrm{ISD}
is not integrable up to order
3
.

The first main result of this paper is the following new characterization of the rigidity of a compact Ricci shrinker.

Theorem 1.1
.

A compact Ricci shrinker
(N^{n},\bar{g},\bar{f})
is rigid if and only if it satisfies the rigidity inequality of some order
k
, i.e., there exist positive constants
\epsilon
and
C
such that the following property holds:

For any
g\in C^{2,\alpha}(S^{2}(N))
with
\|g-\bar{g}\|_{C^{2,\alpha}}<\epsilon
, there exists a
C^{3,\alpha}
self-diffeomorphism
\varphi
of
N
such that
\|\varphi-\mathrm{Id}\|_{C^{3,\alpha}}\leq C\|\delta_{\bar{f}}(g-\bar{g})\|_{C^{1,\alpha}}
and

\displaystyle\|\varphi^{*}g-\bar{g}\|^{k}_{C^{2,\alpha}}\leq C\|\Phi(g)\|_{C^{\alpha}}.

(1.3)

In Theorem
1.1
,
\alpha\in(0,1)
is a fixed constant, and the symbol
\Phi
represents the Ricci shrinker operator (as defined in Definition
2.1
). The self-diffeomorphism
\varphi
is chosen such that the gauge is fixed in such a way that
\delta_{\bar{f}}(\varphi^{*}g-\bar{g})=0
(cf. Definition
2.2
). It should be noted that the “if” part of Theorem
1.1
can be straightforwardly deduced from (
1.3
). To investigate rigidity, two closely related concepts are introduced: Definition
2.14
and Definition
2.20
, which are equivalent to the rigidity inequality of order
k
(see Figure
2
). Definition
2.14
states that a symmetric
2
-tensor
h_{1}\in\mathrm{ISD}
is strongly integrable up to order
k
if it is integrable up to order
k
with solutions
\{h_{1},h_{2},\cdots,h_{k}\}
such that each
h_{i}\in\ker(\delta_{\bar{f}})\cap\mathrm{ISD}^{\perp}
. The additional requirement is motivated by Theorem
2.4
and has the advantage that each
h_{i}
, if it exists, must be unique. Even if
h_{1}
may not be strongly integrable up to some order, it still induces a unique approximate sequence
\{h_{i}\}
by modifying the variational equations of
\Phi
, so that each
h_{i}\in\ker(\delta_{\bar{f}})\cap\mathrm{ISD}^{\perp}
(see Definition
2.17
). Therefore, Definition
2.20
gives a quantitative description that no
h_{1}\in\mathrm{ISD}
is strongly integrable up to order
k
. Finally, the “only if” part of Theorem
1.1
is proved by showing that any
h\in\mathrm{ISD}
is not strongly integrable up to some fixed order
k
that is independent of
h
.

The question of rigidity is considerably more complex for non-compact Ricci shrinkers. The primary challenge arises because the concept of closeness under the pointed-Gromov-Hausdorff or pointed-Cheeger-Gromov metric is not global. By definition, a non-compact Ricci shrinker
(\bar{M}^{m},\bar{g},\bar{f})
is said to be rigid if any Ricci shrinker
(M^{m},g,f)
that is sufficiently close to
(\bar{M}^{m},\bar{g},\bar{f})
on a sufficiently large compact set must be isometric to it. This means that uniqueness radiates out from a compact set, a principle known as the shrinker principle, which was originally discovered in the context of mean curvature flow
[
16
]
[
20
]
and subsequently applied to Ricci flow by Colding-Minicozzi
[
21
]
.

The Gaussian soliton
(\mathbb{R}^{m},g_{E})
is the first non-compact example of a rigid Ricci shrinker. This rigidity has been independently proved by Yokota
[
59
,
60
]
and Li-Wang
[
42
]
by using an entropy-gap argument. Recently, Li-Wang
[
43
]
has also shown that the standard round cylinder
S^{n-1}\times\mathbb{R}
is rigid. Independently, Colding-Minicozzi
[
21
]
have proved that any standard
S^{n-k}\times\mathbb{R}^{k}
is also rigid.

In this paper, we focus on the rigidity of the generalized cylinder
(\bar{M}^{m},\bar{g},\bar{f})=(N^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{m-n},g_{N}\times g_{E},|z|^{2}/4+n/2)
, where
(N,g_{N})
is an
H
-stable Einstein manifold (cf. Definition
2.11
) that has an obstruction of order
3
, and
z
is the coordinate of
\mathbb{R}^{m-n}
. It is worth noting that
(\bar{M}^{m},\bar{g},\bar{f})
cannot be rigid if
(N,g_{N})
is not. Roughly speaking, the requirement on
(N,g_{N})
ensures that any potential deformation under a suitable gauge can only come from either the infinitesimal solitonic deformation on
(N,g_{N})
or the tensor
ug_{N}
, where
u
is a quadratic Hermite polynomial on
\mathbb{R}^{m-n}
. The main result of this paper is to confirm the rigidity of generalized cylinders of this type and their quotients.

Theorem 1.2

(Main Theorem)
.

Suppose
(N^{n},g_{N})
is an
H
-stable Einstein manifold with
\mathrm{Rc}(g_{N})=g_{N}/2
that has an obstruction of order
3
, and let
(\bar{M}^{m},\bar{g},\bar{f})
denote either the generalized cylinder
(N^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{m-n},g_{N}\times g_{E},|z|^{2}/4+n/2)
or its quotient. Then any
m
-dimensional Ricci shrinker
(M,g,f,p)
that is close to
(\bar{M},\bar{g},\bar{f},\bar{p})
in the pointed-Gromov-Hausdorff sense must be isometric to
(\bar{M},\bar{g})
.

There are several key ingredients in the proof of Theorem
1.2
. For illustration, we set
(\bar{M}^{m},\bar{g},\bar{f})=(N^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{m-n},g_{N}\times g_{E},|z|^{2}/4+n/2)
.

(I) Rigidity inequality on generalized cylinders.

Similar to the rigidity of compact Ricci shrinkers, a rigidity inequality as (
1.3
) needs to be derived. However, there are some differences in the process. Firstly, in the definition of the Ricci shrinker operator
\Phi
(cf. Definition
2.1
), the potential function
f
for compact Ricci shrinkers arises naturally as the unique minimizer of
\boldsymbol{\mu}(g,1)
, while in the non-compact case such minimizer may not exist. Therefore, in the non-compact case, the deformation on
(\bar{M}^{m},\bar{g},\bar{f})
involves the pair
(g,f)
which is nearby
(\bar{g},\bar{f})
.

Secondly, to derive the rigidity inequality, one needs to obtain the corresponding inequality for the stability operator
\mathcal{L}
(see Theorem
3.19
). Hence, it is crucial to derive the necessary elliptic estimates on the non-compact Ricci shrinkers with respect to the Sobolev spaces.

Thirdly, the order of the rigidity inequality in the compact case indicates the order up to which a potential deformation tensor is strongly integrable. In the case of a generalized cylinder, the corresponding
\mathrm{ISD}
consists of
\mathrm{ISD}_{N}
and a particular tensor
ug_{N}
(see Proposition
4.2
). By our assumption on
(N,g_{N})
, any
h_{1}\in\mathrm{ISD}_{N}
naturally satisfies a rigidity inequality of order
3
. An important observation is that the tensor
ug_{N}
also satisfies a rigidity inequality of order
2
. This fact is essential in deriving the rigidity inequality for
S^{n-k}\times\mathbb{R}^{k}
in
[
21
]
, where
\mathrm{ISD}_{N}=0
if
N=S^{n-k}
. In our case, we need to show that the deformations
h_{1}
and
ug_{N}
do not interfere with each other in the higher-order estimates. In other words, these two types of rigidity inequalities are compatible. Eventually, we obtain a rigidity inequality of mixed orders (see Theorem
4.10
).

(II) The method of contraction and extension.

Unlike in the compact case, the rigidity inequality alone is insufficient to establish rigidity in the non-compact case. One reason for this is that the deformation
\{g,f\}
from
\{\bar{g},\bar{f}\}
is global, which implies that the diffeomorphism type of the underlying manifold remains unchanged. Another reason is that, unlike in the compact case, we cannot always choose a nice gauge such that
\delta_{\bar{f}}(g-\bar{g})=0
.

To overcome these challenges, we employ the method of contraction and extension, which is similar to the approach taken in
[
16
]
and
[
21
]
. Roughly speaking, if two regions on two Ricci shrinkers are close, then we can shrink both regions to obtain a better estimate. Conversely, we can extend both regions to obtain a slightly worse estimate. By adjusting the range of contraction and extension, we can iterate this process and conclude that the two Ricci shrinkers are identical.

We explain the method of contraction and extension in detail.

(IIa) The contraction process.

To obtain a better estimate for the pair
\{h,\chi\}=\{g-\bar{g},f-\bar{f}\}
, one can shrink the given regions and apply the rigidity inequality to an appropriate cutoff of the pair
\{h,\chi\}
after the action of a diffeomorphism. While it is not always possible to choose a diffeomorphism
\varphi
such that
\delta_{\bar{f}}(\varphi^{*}g-\bar{g})=0
, the first-order approximation can be used to solve
2\mathcal{P}w=\delta_{\bar{f}}h
(see Definition
2.2
) for a
1
-form
w
, and the time-one diffeomorphism
\varphi_{w}
generated by
w
can be considered. The key point is that we are able to obtain a pointwise estimate of
w
(see Theorem
3.13
), in addition to the
W^{2,2}
estimate (see Proposition
3.10
), which is derived from the growth estimate of eigentensor-like tensors established by Colding-Minicozzi (cf.
[
21
, Section 3]
). After performing a diffeomorphism, shrinking the region slightly and applying the rigidity inequality yields a better estimate for
\{h,\chi\}
. While some error may develop during this process, mainly from the neck part where the pair is cut off, the very small weight
e^{-\bar{f}}
may render such error negligible.

(IIb) The extension process.

The extension process described here differs slightly from the one in
[
21
]
. To extend the corresponding regions, we first use the pseudolocality theorem for Ricci shrinkers (see
[
42
, Theorem 24]
) to ensure that a slightly larger region
\Omega_{1}\supset\Omega
in
M
has bounded curvature. Since the two smaller regions
\Omega\subset M
and
\Omega^{\prime}\subset\bar{M}
are close enough, we can guarantee that two larger regions
\Omega_{1}\subset M
and
\Omega_{1}^{\prime}\subset\bar{M}
are diffeomorphic and that their geometries on the necks
\Omega_{1}\setminus\Omega
and
\Omega_{1}^{\prime}\setminus\Omega^{\prime}
are sufficiently close. We can then glue
(\Omega_{1},g,f)
and
(\bar{M},\bar{g},\bar{f})
at the neck to form a complete Riemannian manifold
(\bar{M},g^{\prime},f^{\prime})
that is almost a Ricci shrinker.

To obtain estimates for
{h,\chi}
on the larger region, we consider the first
m-n
non-constant eigenfunctions
{u_{i}}
with respect to
\Delta_{f^{\prime}}
. Based on our assumptions, it can be shown that their corresponding eigenvalues are almost equal to
1/2
, which implies that all
{u_{i}}
are almost splitting maps. In order to obtain estimates for
u_{i}
and
\nabla^{2}u_{i}
on this larger region, we need to use a growth estimate similar to the one in
[
21
]
on
(\bar{M},g^{\prime},f^{\prime})
, as explained in Appendix
A
. By using
{u_{i}}
, we can extend the almost-splitting property near the base point outwards, resulting in only slightly weakened estimates.

The contraction and extension processes described above have the advantage of being local. Indeed, the localization in the contraction process is achieved by using a cutoff function, while in the extension process, it is ensured by using the gluing argument, as long as the pseudolocality can be applied. As a result, these processes can be naturally generalized to prove the rigidity of the quotient of a generalized cylinder, provided that the corresponding conditions are appropriately modified (see Theorem
5.5
).

In
[
38
]
and
[
45
]
, the authors prove that
(\mathbb{CP}^{n},g_{FS})
is rigid by showing that it has an obstruction of order
3
. On the other hand, it is well-known that
(\mathbb{CP}^{n},g_{FS})
is
H
-stable. As a direct corollary of Theorem
1.2
, we obtain the following result. More examples of
(N,g_{N})
satisfying the condition of Theorem
1.2
can be found in
[
10
, Table 1, Table 2]
.

Corollary 1.3
.

The Ricci shrinker
(\mathbb{CP}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{m-2n},g_{FS}\times g_{E},|z|^{2}/4+n)
is rigid.

Munteanu-Wang
[
46
, Corollary 4]
have proven that any Ricci shrinker with nonnegative curvature operator must be locally symmetric. Specifically in dimension
4
,
\mathbb{R}^{4},S^{4},\mathbb{CP}^{2},S^{2}\times S^{2},S^{2}\times\mathbb{R}^{2},S^{3}\times\mathbb{R}
and their quotients are all possibilitites. By combining Theorem
1.2
with the results from
[
59
]
[
60
]
[
42
]
[
38
]
[
57
]
[
43
]
and
[
21
]
, we can deduce the following:

Corollary 1.4
.

Any four-dimensional Ricci shrinker with nonnegative curvature operator is rigid.

Another application of Theorem
1.2
and related techniques is the proof of the uniqueness of the tangent flow for a compact Ricci flow, provided that one tangent flow is
(\bar{M},\bar{g},\bar{f})
. In the case of the mean curvature flow, the uniqueness of the tangent flow at each cylindrical singular point is proven in
[
18
]
[
19
]
. Moreover, under the type-I assumption on curvature, the corresponding uniqueness of tangent flow in the setting of the Ricci flow is also established; see
[
21
, Theorem 9.21]
. In fact, the proof of
[
21
, Theorem 9.21]
can be extended to any rigid Ricci shrinkers, since the convergence is always smooth under the type-I assumption. In general, we consider the tangent flows at a particular conjugate heat flow
(\mu_{t})
with controlled variance, which can be regarded as a “point” at the singular time (cf. Definition
6.1
).

Theorem 1.5

(Uniqueness of the tangent flow)
.

Let
(M^{m},g(t))_{t\in[-T,0)}
be a compact Ricci flow solution with singular time
0
, and let
(\mu_{t})_{t\in[-T,0)}
be a conjugate heat flow satisfying
\emph{\textrm{Var}}_{t}(\mu_{t})\leq H_{m}|t|
for any
t\in[-T,0)
, where
H_{m}:=(m-1)\pi^{2}/2+4
. If one tangent flow at
(\mu_{t})
is
(\bar{M},\bar{g},\bar{f})
, which satisfies the assumption of Theorem
1.2
, then any tangent flow at
(\mu_{t})
is.

The proof of Theorem
1.5
builds on the concepts and techniques of the theory of
\mathbb{F}
-convergence developed by Bamler
[
2
,
3
,
4
]
. Essentially, we aim to demonstrate that a sequence of metric solitons, which are not
(\bar{M},\bar{g},\bar{f})
, cannot
\mathbb{F}
-converge to
(\bar{M},\bar{g},\bar{f})
. Using a continuity argument, we prove that such a sequence, if it exists, must converge to
(\bar{M},\bar{g},\bar{f})
in the pointed-Cheeger-Gromov sense. However, while the convergence is smooth, any metric soliton in the sequence may have singularities far away from the base point, making it impossible to apply Theorem
1.2
directly. To overcome this, we use the method of contraction and extension once again since all processes can be made local, and the pseudolocality theorem is valid in this situation. Indeed, all metric solitons as tangent flows come from a compact Ricci flow. Therefore, we prove the uniqueness of the tangent flow and show that the convergence is in the smooth sense. Notice that the uniqueness of the tangent flow at infinity for the ancient solutions to the Ricci flow can be similarly obtained; see Theorem
6.5
.

This paper is organized as follows. Section
2
focuses on the rigidity of compact Ricci shrinkers. We prove in this section that a compact Ricci shrinker is not strongly integrable up to order
k
if and only if it has an obstruction of order
k
if and only if it satisfies the rigidity inequality of order
k
. Moreover, we prove Theorem
1.1
as a consequence of the equivalence between these concepts. In section
3
, we develop necessary elliptic estimates for general non-compact Ricci shrinkers with respect to the Sobolev spaces. These elliptic estimates are crucial in deriving the rigidity inequality. In section
4
, we prove the rigidity inequality of mixed orders for generalized cylinders. In section 5, we prove our main result, Theorem
1.2
, by carefully applying the method of contraction and extension. In section 6, we introduce the background of
\mathbb{F}
-convergence and tangent flows, and then prove Theorem
1.5
. In the Appendix, we slightly generalize the growth estimate of Colding-Minicozzi to general smooth metric measure spaces, which plays an essential role in the extension process.

2
Rigidity of the compact Ricci shrinkers

For any compact Riemannian manifold
(N^{n},g)
, we recall that Perelman’s celebrated entropy
\boldsymbol{\mu}(g,1)

[
50
]
is defined as

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mu}(g,1):=\inf_{\rho}\left((4\pi)^{-\frac{n}{2}}\int_{N}\left(|\nabla\rho|^{2}+R+\rho-n\right)e^{-\rho}\,dV_{g}\right),

(2.1)

where the infimum is taken for all smooth functions
\rho
such that

\displaystyle(4\pi)^{-\frac{n}{2}}\int_{N}e^{-\rho}\,dV_{g}=1.

Clearly, any minimizer
\rho
of (
2.1
) satisfying the following Euler-Lagrange equation:

\displaystyle 2\Delta\rho-|\nabla\rho|^{2}+R+\rho-n=\boldsymbol{\mu}(g,1).

(2.2)

Let
(N^{n},g,f)
be a compact Ricci shrinker with normalization (
1.2
), which can be regarded as a critical point of
\boldsymbol{\mu}
for nearby variations. We define
C^{\infty}(S^{2}(N))
as the space of smooth symmetric
2
-tensors on
N
. Additionally, we fix a constant
\alpha\in(0,1)
and define
C^{k,\alpha}(S^{2}(N))
as the completion of
C^{\infty}(S^{2}(N))
with the
C^{k,\alpha}
-norm for
k\geq 2
. We are interested in metrics that are in a small neighborhood of
g
in
C^{k,\alpha}(S^{2}(N))
, equipped with the
C^{k,\alpha}
-topology.

It is proved by Sun-Wang
[
56
, Lemma 2.2]
(see also
[
36
, Lemma 4.1]
) that there exits a small neighborhood
\mathcal{U}
of
g
in
C^{2,\alpha}(S^{2}(N))
such that for any
\tilde{g}\in\mathcal{U}
, the minimizer
\rho_{\tilde{g}}
of
\boldsymbol{\mu}(\tilde{g},1)
is unique and depends analytically on
\tilde{g}
. In this case, we define

\displaystyle f_{\tilde{g}}:=\rho_{\tilde{g}}-\boldsymbol{\mu}(\tilde{g},1).

(2.3)

In particular, it follows from
[
56
, Lemma 3.4]
(see also
[
42
, Proposition 3]
) that
f=f_{g}
. It is immediate from (
2.2
) that

\displaystyle 2\Delta f_{\tilde{g}}-|\nabla f_{\tilde{g}}|^{2}+R(\tilde{g})+f_{\tilde{g}}-n=0,

(2.4)

where the underlying metric is
\tilde{g}
.

We define the following Ricci shrinker operator on the neighborhood
\mathcal{U}
of
g
.

Definition 2.1
.

For any
\tilde{g}\in\mathcal{U}
, we define

\displaystyle\Phi(\tilde{g}):=\frac{1}{2}\tilde{g}-\mathrm{Rc}(\tilde{g})-\nabla_{\tilde{g}}^{2}f_{\tilde{g}},

(2.5)

where
f_{\tilde{g}}
is given by (
2.3
). In particular,
\Phi(\tilde{g})=0
if and only if
(N,\tilde{g},f_{\tilde{g}})
is a Ricci shrinker with normalization (
1.2
).

Before we continue the discussion, we recall some definitions and notations for later use.

Definition 2.2
.

On
(N^{n},g,f)
, we have the following definitions.

1.

The
L^{2}
-norm for
(r,s)
-tensor fields, denoted by
T^{r,s}N
, is defined with respect to the volume form
dV_{f}:=e^{-f}dV_{g}
, i.e., for any
T\in L^{2}(T^{r,s}N)
,
\|T\|_{L^{2}}=\int|T|^{2}\,dV_{f}
. The Sobolev spaces
W^{k,2}(T^{r,s}N)
are defined with respect to
dV_{f}
as well.

2.

The weighted divergence
\delta_{f}:C^{\infty}(T^{r,s}N)\rightarrow C^{\infty}(T^{r,s-1}N)
is defined as

\displaystyle(\delta_{f}T)^{i_{1},\cdots,i_{r}}_{j_{1},\dots,j_{s-1}}:=\nabla_{i}T^{i_{1},\cdots,i_{r}}_{i,j_{1},\dots,j_{s-1}}-T^{i_{1},\cdots,i_{r}}_{i,j_{1},\dots,j_{s-1}}f_{i}

for any
T\in C^{\infty}(T^{r,s}N)
. We denote by
\delta_{f}^{*}
the adjoint operator of
\delta_{f}
with respect to the
L^{2}
-norm.

3.

The weighted Laplacian operator
\Delta_{f}
is defined as

\Delta_{f}=\Delta-\langle\nabla\cdot,\nabla f\rangle
on
C^{\infty}(T^{r,s}N)
. We set
\mathcal{L}
to be the stability operator
\Delta_{f}+2\mathrm{Rm}
on
C^{\infty}(S^{2}(N))
, that is, for any
h\in C^{\infty}(S^{2}(N))
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(h)_{ij}:=\Delta_{f}h_{ij}+2R_{ikjl}h_{kl}.

4.

The second-order elliptic operator
\mathcal{P}
is defined as

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}w=\delta_{f}\delta_{f}^{*}w

for any
1
-form
w\in C^{\infty}(T^{*}N)
. It is clear by the definition that the kernel of
\mathcal{P}
, denoted by
\mathcal{K}_{\mathcal{P}}
, consists of all Killing forms (i.e., the dual forms of Killing fields) on
N
.

Next, we prove the following orthogonal decomposition; see also
[
52
, Proposition 2.2]
.

Proposition 2.3
.

For any
k\geq 1
,

\displaystyle C^{k,\alpha}(S^{2}(N))=\ker\left(\delta_{f}|_{C^{k,\alpha}(S^{2}(N))}\right)\oplus\delta_{f}^{*}\left(C^{k+1,\alpha}(T^{*}N)\right).

(2.6)

In particular, we have

\displaystyle C^{\infty}(S^{2}(N))=\ker(\delta_{f})\oplus\mathrm{Im}(\delta_{f}^{*}),

(2.7)

where
\ker(\delta_{f}):=\ker\left(\delta_{f}|_{C^{\infty}(S^{2}(N))}\right)
and
\mathrm{Im}(\delta_{f}^{*}):=\delta_{f}^{*}\left(C^{\infty}(T^{*}N)\right)
. Furthermore, the decompositions are orthogonal with respect to
L^{2}
.

Proof.

For any
h\in C^{k,\alpha}(S^{2}(N))
, we solve the following equation for
w\in C^{k+1,\alpha}(T^{*}N)

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}w=\delta_{f}h.

(2.8)

Notice that by the standard theory for the elliptic operator (cf.
[
7
, Theorem 27]
) that such a
w
exists since
\delta_{f}h
is orthogonal to all Killing forms. Therefore, it follows from (
2.8
) that
h-\delta_{f}^{*}w\in\ker\left(\delta_{f}|_{C^{k,\alpha}(S^{2}(N))}\right)
. Clearly, the decompositions (
2.6
) and (
2.7
) are orthogonal, and the proof is complete.
∎

The next theorem shows that for any small perturbation
h
lies in
\ker(\delta_{f})
up to the modification of a diffeomorphism. This can be regarded as an infinitesimal version of the Ebin-Palais slice theorem
[
23
]
; see also
[
54
, Theorem 3.6]
. For simplicity, we denote by
\varphi_{w}
the time-one diffeomorphism generated by the dual vector field of a
1
-form
w
.

Theorem 2.4
.

For any
k\geq 1
, there exist positive constants
\epsilon
and
C
satisfying the following property.

If
\|h\|_{C^{k,\alpha}}<\epsilon
, then there exists a
1
-form
w\in C^{k+1,\alpha}(T^{*}N)
such that
\|w\|_{C^{k+1,\alpha}}\leq C\|\delta_{f}h\|_{C^{k-1,\alpha}}
and

\displaystyle\delta_{f}(\varphi_{w}^{*}(g+h)-g)=0.

Moreover,
\varphi_{w}
depends analytically on
g
.

Proof.

Let
\{w_{1},\cdots,w_{k}\}
denote a basis for
\mathcal{K}_{\mathcal{P}}
.
Consider the map

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}=(\mathcal{N}_{1},\mathcal{N}_{2}):C^{k+1,\alpha}(T^{*}N)\times\mathbb{R}^{k}\times C^{k,\alpha}(S^{2}(N))\rightarrow C^{k-1,\alpha}(T^{*}N)\times\mathbb{R}^{k}

defined by for any
(w,x,h)\in C^{k+1,\alpha}(T^{*}N)\times\mathbb{R}^{k}\times C^{k,\alpha}(S^{2}(N))
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}_{1}(w,x,h):=

\displaystyle\delta_{f}(\varphi^{*}_{w}(g+h)-g)+\sum_{i}x_{i}w_{i},

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}_{2}(w,x,h):=

\displaystyle\left(\langle w,w_{1}\rangle_{L^{2}},\cdots,\langle w,w_{k}\rangle_{L^{2}}\right),

where
x=\left(x_{1},\cdots,x_{k}\right)
.

Differentiating
\mathcal{N}(w,x,0)
at
(0,0)
with respect to
(w,x)
, we obtain from the definition that

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}_{1}^{\prime}(w,x,0)=

\displaystyle-2\mathcal{P}w+\sum_{i}x_{i}w_{i},

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}_{2}^{\prime}(w,x,0)=

\displaystyle\left(\langle w,w_{1}\rangle_{L^{2}},\cdots,\langle w,w_{k}\rangle_{L^{2}}\right).

We claim that
\mathcal{N}^{\prime}(w,x,0)
is an isomorphism. By the theory of the elliptic operator, it is clear that
\mathcal{N}^{\prime}
is surjective. In addition, if
\mathcal{N}^{\prime}(w,x,0)=0
, then
-2\mathcal{P}w+\sum_{i}x_{i}w_{i}=0
and consequently
\mathcal{P}w=\sum_{i}x_{i}w_{i}=0
since
\mathrm{Im}(\mathcal{P})
is orthogonal to
\mathcal{K}_{\mathcal{P}}
. In other words,
w
is a Killing form, and all
x_{i}=0
. From
\mathcal{N}_{2}^{\prime}(w,x,0)=0
, we also conclude that
w=0
. Therefore,
\mathcal{N}^{\prime}(w,x,0)
is also injective.

Applying the implicit function theorem for Banach spaces (see, e.g.,
[
40
, Theorem 5.9, P19]
), for any
h\in C^{k,\alpha}(S^{2}(N))
small enough, there exists a unique pair
(w,x)
nearby
(0,0)
such that
\mathcal{N}(w,x,h)=0
.
From
\mathcal{N}_{1}(w,x,h)=0
, we obtain

\displaystyle\delta_{f}(\varphi^{*}_{w}(g+h)-g)+\sum_{i}x_{i}w_{i}=0.

(2.9)

Since the two summands in (
2.9
) are orthogonal in
L^{2}
, we conclude that
\delta_{f}(\varphi^{*}_{w}(g+h)-g)=x=0
. Moreover, it follows from
\mathcal{N}_{2}(w,x,h)=0
that
w
is perpendicular to all Killing forms.

In sum, there exists a
C^{1}
map
w=w(h)
from
C^{k,\alpha}(S^{2}(N))
to
C^{k+1,\alpha}(S^{2}(N))
for any small
h\in C^{k,\alpha}(S^{2}(N))
such that
\delta_{f}(\varphi^{*}_{w}(g+h)-g)=0
. On the other hand, it follows from the proof of Proposition
2.3
that there exists
w_{1}\in C^{k+1,\alpha}(S^{2}(N))
such that

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}w_{1}=\delta_{f}h\quad\text{and}\quad\|w_{1}\|_{C^{k+1,\alpha}}\leq C\|\delta_{f}h\|_{C^{k-1,\alpha}}.

(2.10)

Therefore, it follows from the definition of
w
and (
2.10
) that

\displaystyle\|w(h)\|_{C^{k+1,\alpha}}\leq\|w(h-\delta_{f}^{*}w_{1})\|_{C^{k+1,\alpha}}+\|w(\delta_{f}^{*}w_{1})\|_{C^{k+1,\alpha}}\leq C\|\delta_{f}^{*}w_{1}\|_{C^{k,\alpha}}\leq C\|\delta_{f}h\|_{C^{k-1,\alpha}},

where we have used
w(h-\delta_{f}^{*}w_{1})=w(0)=0
. Now, the last conclusion follows from the implicit function theorem in the real analytic category (cf.
[
25
, Theorem 3.12]
).
∎

Next, we recall the following definition.

Definition 2.5
.

The infinitesimal solitonic deformation space with respect to
(N^{n},g,f)
is defined as

\displaystyle\mathrm{ISD}:=\{h\in C^{\infty}(S^{2}(N))\mid h\in\ker(\Phi^{\prime})\cap\ker(\delta_{f})\},

where
\Phi^{\prime}
is the first variation of
\Phi
in (
2.5
) at

Notice that
\Phi^{\prime}
can be explicitly calculated by the following lemma, whose proof is similar to
[
45
, Lemma 2.5]
; see also
[
12
, Theorem 1.1]
.

Lemma 2.6
.

For any
h\in C^{2,\alpha}(S^{2}(N))
, we have

\displaystyle\Phi^{\prime}(h)=\frac{1}{2}\mathcal{L}(h)+\delta_{f}^{*}\delta_{f}h+\nabla^{2}(\mathrm{Tr}_{g}(h)/2-f^{\prime}),

where

is determined by

\displaystyle(2\Delta_{f}+1)(\mathrm{Tr}_{g}(h)/2-f^{\prime})=\delta_{f}^{2}h.

(2.11)

In particular, if

, then

and we have

\displaystyle\Phi^{\prime}(h)=\frac{1}{2}\mathcal{L}(h).

Moreover,
\Phi^{\prime}
is a self-adjoint operator on
C^{2,\alpha}(S^{2}(N))
and preserves the decomposition (
2.6
).

Combining Lemma
2.6
and Definition
2.5
, we immediately conclude that ISD is a finite-dimensional linear space.

Lemma 2.7
.

We have the following orthogonal decomposition

\displaystyle\ker(\Phi^{\prime})=\mathrm{ISD}\oplus\mathrm{Im}(\delta_{f}^{*}).

Proof.

Since
\Phi^{\prime}
preserves the decomposition

, we only need to prove that
\mathrm{Im}(\delta_{f}^{*})\subset\ker(\Phi^{\prime})
, which follows from the fact that
\Phi(\varphi^{*}g)=0
for any self-diffeomorphism
\varphi
of

.
∎

We next prove that

Lemma 2.8

For any

\displaystyle\delta_{f}(\mathcal{L}h)=(\Delta_{f}+1/2)\delta_{f}h\quad\text{and}\quad\mathcal{L}(\delta_{f}^{*}w)=\delta_{f}^{*}(\Delta_{f}w+w/2).

preserves the decomposition (

\ker(\delta_{f})
. Notice that by direct computation,

and
\mathcal{L}(\mathrm{Rc})-\mathrm{Rc}=0
(cf.
[

, Lemma 2.1]
and
[

, Lemma 3.2]

-1\in\mathrm{spec}(\mathcal{L}|_{\ker(\delta_{f})})
for any compact Ricci shrinker. Next, we recall the following definition of a linearly stable Ricci shrinker (cf.
[

]
[

]

[

]
.

Definition 2.9
.

A compact Ricci shrinker

is called

is the only negative eigenvalue of

on

.

A compact Ricci shrinker is linearly stable if and only if the second derivative of Perelman’s
\boldsymbol{\nu}
-functional is nonpositive (cf.
[

]
[
12
]
). For instance, the standard

and

are linearly stable Ricci shrinkers (cf.
[

]
). We remark that even though the standard

is linearly stable, it is not the local maximizer of

(cf.
[

]
). Unstable examples include all compact Kähler -Ricci shrinkers with Hodge number

(cf.
[

]

, Table 1, Table 2]
. On the other hand, if

is strictly linearly stable, then it is dynamically stable in the sense that any normalized Ricci flow starting from a metric in a small neighborhood of

converges to

up to a diffeomorphism

.

Notice that any linearly stable compact Ricci shrinker must be irreducible, which follows from the next Lemma.

Lemma 2.10
.

Let
(N,g,f)=(N_{1}\times N_{2},g_{1}\times g_{2},f_{1}+f_{2})
is the product of two compact Ricci shrinkers. Then
(N,g,f)
is linearly unstable.

Proof.

Since
\mathrm{Rc}(g_{1})
and
\mathrm{Rc}(g_{2})
are two linearly independent eigentensors of

corresponding to

,

is linearly unstable by definition.
∎

In the special case of Einstein manifolds, we have the following definition of the
H
-stability (cf.
[
34
]
).

Definition 2.11
.

A compact Einstein manifold
(N^{n},g)
is called
H
-stable if the eigenvalues of
\mathcal{L}
on the TT (traceless-transverse) space is nonnegative.

For a compact Einstein manifold
(N^{n},g)
,
\mathcal{L}|_{TT}\leq 0
if and only if the second derivative of the renormalized Einstein-Hilbert functional is nonpositive for all volume-preserving deformations modulo the conformal variations (cf.
[
34
]
). The following characterization of the
H
-stability is well-known; see also
[
10
, Lemma 3.5]
.

Lemma 2.12
.

A compact Einstein manifold
(N^{n},g)
with
\mathrm{Rc}(g)=g/2
is linearly stable if and only if either it is
H
-stable such that all nonzero eigenvalues of
\Delta
on functions are no smaller than
1
, or isometric to the standard
S^{n}
.

Proof.

We first assume
(N^{n},g)
is linearly stable. Then it is clear by definition that the
\mathcal{L}|_{TT}\leq 0
. If
\Delta u+\mu u=0
for some constant
\mu\in(0,1)
, it follows from direct computation that
h:=(\mu-1/2)ug+\nabla^{2}u
satisfies

\displaystyle\delta h=0\quad\text{and}\quad\mathcal{L}h+(\mu-1)h=0.

Therefore, it follows from Definition
2.9
that
h=0
and hence
(\mu-1/2)ug+\nabla^{2}u=0
. By taking the trace, we obtain

\displaystyle\Delta u+n(\mu-1/2)u=0.

Consequently, either
u=0
and in this case
\mu
is not an eigenvalue, or
\mu=\frac{n}{2(n-1)}
and hence by Obtata’s theorem
[
49
]
,
(N,g)
is isometric to the standard
S^{n}
.

Conversely, if
(N,g)
is isometric to the standard
S^{n}
, it is obviously linearly stable. Now, we assume

\displaystyle\delta h=0\quad\text{and}\quad\mathcal{L}h+(\mu-1)h=0.

(2.12)

By taking the trace (
2.12
), we obtain
\Delta H+\mu H=0
, where
H:=\mathrm{Tr}_{g}(h)
. If
\mu=0
, then
H
is a constant and hence
h^{\prime}:=h-Hg/n
satisfies

\displaystyle\delta h^{\prime}=\mathrm{Tr}_{g}(h^{\prime})=0\quad\text{and}\quad\mathcal{L}h^{\prime}-h^{\prime}=0.

Therefore, we conclude that
h^{\prime}=0
and hence
h
is the multiple of
g
. If
\mu>0
, then
\mu\geq 1
by our assumption, and thus it follows from (
2.12
) that the corresponding eigenvalue of
h
is nonnegative.

In sum, the proof is complete.
∎

(N^{n},\bar{g},\bar{f})
, and the underlying metric is
\bar{g}
unless otherwise stated. By the deformation theory of compact Ricci shrinkers established by Podestà-Spiro

starting from
\bar{g}

of

is smooth by the elliptic regularity. In particular, all derivatives of

at

\ker(\delta_{\bar{f}})

If we take derivatives in succession,

\displaystyle\Phi^{\prime}(h^{k})+\sum_{l=2}^{k}\sum_{1\leq k_{1}\leq\cdots\leq k_{l},k_{1}+\cdots+k_{l}=k}C(k,l,k_{1},\cdots,k_{l})\Phi^{(l)}(h^{k_{1}},\cdots,h^{k_{l}})=0,

are constants depending only on

.
For later applications, we define the following operators.

Definition 2.13
.

We define

and for

\displaystyle M_{k}(h_{1},\cdots,h_{k-1}):=\sum_{l=2}^{k}\sum_{1\leq k_{1}\leq\cdots\leq k_{l},k_{1}+\cdots+k_{l}=k}C(k,l,k_{1},\cdots,k_{l})\Phi^{(l)}(h_{k_{1}},\cdots,h_{k_{l}}),

For instance,
M_{2}(h_{1})=\Phi^{(2)}(h_{1},h_{1})

\displaystyle\Phi^{\prime}(h^{k})+M_{k}(h^{1},\cdots,h^{k-1})=0.

Conversely, we have the following definition (cf.

Definition 2.14
.

For any

\displaystyle\Phi^{\prime}(h_{l})+M_{l}(h_{1},\cdots,h_{l-1})=0

(N^{n},\bar{g},\bar{f})

Notice that if

\displaystyle g=g(s,t):=\varphi_{t}^{*}\left(\bar{g}+\sum_{i=1}^{k}\frac{s^{i}}{i!}h_{i}\right)\quad\text{and}\quad\mu(s,t)=\boldsymbol{\mu}(g(s,t),1).

\displaystyle(2\Delta_{\bar{f}}+1)f^{k}+L_{k}(h^{1},\cdots,h^{k},f^{1},\cdots,f^{k-1})=0,

where
L_{k}
is an operator. For instance, if
k=1
, then it follows from (
2.11
) that

\displaystyle L_{1}(h^{1})=\delta_{\bar{f}}^{2}h^{1}-(\Delta_{\bar{f}}+1/2)\mathrm{Tr}_{\bar{g}}(h^{1}).

In particular,
f^{1}=\mathrm{Tr}_{\bar{g}}(h^{1})/2
if
h^{1}\in\ker(\delta_{\bar{f}})
. Next, we define

Definition 2.29
.

Given
h_{1}\in\mathrm{ISD}
, we say the sequence of functions
\{f_{i}\}
is
induced
by
h_{1}
if
f_{1}=\mathrm{Tr}_{\bar{g}}(h_{1})/2
and for any
l\geq 2
,

\displaystyle(2\Delta_{\bar{f}}+1)f_{l}+L_{l}(h_{1},\cdots,h_{l},f_{1},\cdots,f_{l-1})=0,

(2.52)

where
\{h_{i}\}
is the sequence of
2
-tensors induced by
h_{1}
.

Since
1/2
is not an eigenvalue of
\Delta_{\bar{f}}
, one can solve
f_{2},f_{3},\cdots
recursively from (
2.52
), and the solutions are unique. Therefore, it follows from (
2.51
) that for any constant
\lambda
,
\{\lambda^{i}f_{i}\}
is induced by
\lambda h_{1}
. From the homogeneity, the same argument as before indicates that all possible
f_{i}
lies in a finite-dimensional linear space as
h_{1}
varies in
\mathrm{ISD}
. Moreover, the following result can be proved similarly to Proposition
2.19
.

Proposition 2.30
.

Suppose
h_{1}\in\mathrm{ISD}
and
\{f_{i}\}
is the sequence of functions induced by
h_{1}
. Then for any
i\geq 1
,

\displaystyle\|f_{i}\|_{C^{2,\alpha}}\leq C\|h_{1}\|^{i}_{C^{\alpha}}.

Given
h_{1}\in\mathrm{ISD}
with induced sequences
\{h_{i}\}
and
\{f_{i}\}
, if we define

\displaystyle g(t):=\bar{g}+\sum_{l=1}^{k}\frac{t^{l}}{l!}h_{l}\quad\text{and}\quad f(t):=f_{g(t)}.

Then it is clear from the uniqueness of the solution
f_{l}
in (
2.52
) that

\displaystyle d_{t^{i}}^{i}|_{t=0}f(t)=f_{i}

(2.53)

for any
1\leq i\leq k
. In other words,

can be approximated by
\bar{f}+\sum_{l=1}^{k}\dfrac{t^{l}}{l!}f_{l}
.

3
Elliptic estimates on non-compact Ricci shrinkers

In this section, we consider an
m
-dimensional non-compact Ricci shrinker
(M^{m},g,f)
with normalization (
1.2
). The notations and operators in Section 2 (cf. Definition
2.2
) can be defined similarly on
(M,g,f)
.

If
(M,g)
has bounded curvature, then
|\nabla^{k}\mathrm{Rm}|
is also bounded by Shi’s local estimates
[
53
]
. Moreover, it follows from the no-local-collapsing theorem (see
[
47
, Theorem 4.2]

[
42
, Theorem 23]
) that each unit ball of
M
has a uniformly positive volume lower bound. In particular,
(M,g)
has bounded geometry.

First, we recall the following well-known estimate of
f
.

Lemma 3.1

(
[
11
]
[
31
]
)
.

There exists a point
p\in M
where
f
attains its infimum and
f
satisfies the quadratic growth estimate

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4}\left(d(x,p)-5m\right)^{2}_{+}\leq f(x)\leq\frac{1}{4}\left(d(x,p)+\sqrt{2m}\right)^{2}

for all
x\in M
, where
a_{+}:=\max\{0,a\}
.

Lemma
3.1
indicates
f
grows like
d^{2}(p,\cdot)/4
. For this reason, we define
b:=2\sqrt{f}
, which can be regarded as a replacement for the distance function. Moreover, we have the following volume estimate.

Lemma 3.2

(
[
11
]
[
31
]
)
.

There exists a constant
C=C(m)>0
such that for any
r>0
,

\displaystyle|B(p,r)|\leq Cr^{m}.

In particular, Lemma
3.1
and Lemma
3.2
imply that any tensor
T
with polynomial growth belongs to
L^{2}
.

To deal with the integration by parts on non-compact Ricci shrinkers, we recall that there exists a family of cutoff functions constructed in
[
42
, Section 3]
. Specifically, we define

\displaystyle\phi^{r}:=\eta\left(\frac{f}{r}\right),

where
\eta
is a fixed smooth function on
\mathbb{R}
such that
\eta=1
on
(-\infty,1]
and
\eta=0
on
[2,\infty)
. In particular,
\phi^{r}
is identical
1
if
f\leq r
and supported on
f\leq 2r
. Moreover, we have

\displaystyle|\nabla\phi^{r}|^{2}\leq Cr^{-1}\quad\text{and}\quad|\Delta_{f}\phi^{r}|\leq C

(3.1)

for a constant
C=C(m)
. Here, the identity
\Delta_{f}f=m/2-f
is crucial.

If we further assume
(M,g)
has bounded curvature, it follows from the bound of
|\nabla^{i}\mathrm{Rm}|
and the Ricci shrinker equation (
1.1
) that for any
i\geq 2
,

\displaystyle|\nabla^{i}\phi^{r}|\leq C_{i}r^{-i}.

(3.2)

We first derive the following formula for integration by parts for
(r,s)
-tensor fields, denoted by
T^{r,s}M
.

Lemma 3.3

(Integration by parts)
.

Let
S,T\in T^{r,s}M
such that
S,T,\Delta_{f}S,\Delta_{f}T\in L^{2}
. Then
|\nabla S|,|\nabla T|\in L^{2}
and

\displaystyle\int\langle\Delta_{f}S,T\rangle\,dV_{f}=\int\langle S,\Delta_{f}T\rangle\,dV_{f}=-\int\langle\nabla S,\nabla T\rangle\,dV_{f}.

(3.3)

Proof.

We first prove that
|\nabla T|\in L^{2}
.
Applying the integration by parts, we obtain

\displaystyle 2\int(\phi^{r})^{2}(\langle\Delta_{f}T,T\rangle+|\nabla T|^{2})\,dV_{f}=\int(\phi^{r})^{2}\Delta_{f}|T|^{2}\,dV_{f}=-2\int\langle\nabla\phi^{r},\nabla|T|^{2}\rangle\phi^{r}\,dV_{f}.

(3.4)

By using Cauchy–Schwarz inequality, it follows from (
3.4
) that

\displaystyle\int(\phi^{r})^{2}(\langle\Delta_{f}T,T\rangle+|\nabla T|^{2})\,dV_{f}\leq\frac{1}{2}\int(\phi^{r})^{2}|\nabla T|^{2}\,dV_{f}+2\int|\nabla\phi^{r}|^{2}|T|^{2}\,dV_{f}

and hence

\displaystyle\int(\phi^{r})^{2}|\nabla T|^{2}\,dV_{f}\leq-2\int(\phi^{r})^{2}\langle\Delta_{f}T,T\rangle\,dV_{f}+4\int|\nabla\phi^{r}|^{2}|T|^{2}\,dV_{f}.

(3.5)

Since
T,\Delta_{f}T\in L^{2}
, it is clear from (
3.5
) by taking
r\to\infty
that
|\nabla T|\in L^{2}
.

Applying the integration by parts again, we have

\displaystyle 2\int\phi^{r}(\langle\Delta_{f}T,T\rangle+|\nabla T|^{2})\,dV_{f}=\int\phi^{r}\Delta_{f}|T|^{2}\,dV_{f}=\int|T|^{2}\Delta_{f}\phi^{r}\,dV_{f}.

(3.6)

By using (
3.1
), we immediately conclude from (
3.6
) that

\displaystyle\int|\nabla T|^{2}+\langle\Delta_{f}T,T\rangle\,dV_{f}=0.

(3.7)

Now, the general identity (
3.3
) follows by polarizing (
3.7
).
∎

Remark 3.4
.

It is a standard fact that the space of smooth tensors with compact support is dense in
W^{1,2}
. Indeed, it follows from (
3.1
) that any
T\in W^{1,2}
can be approximated by
\phi^{r}T
. Moreover,
\phi^{r}T
can be further approximated by smooth tensors with compact support through a standard convolution argument. Futhermore, if we assume
|\mathrm{Rm}|
is bounded, then the space of smooth tensors with compact support is dense in
W^{k,2}
for any
k\geq 2
by using (
3.2
).

Applying (
3.3
), we immediately obtain

Corollary 3.5
.

For any
\epsilon>0
and tensor field
T\in L^{2}
with
\Delta_{f}T\in L^{2}
,

\displaystyle\|T\|^{2}_{W^{1,2}}\leq\epsilon\|\Delta_{f}T\|^{2}_{L^{2}}+\left(1+\frac{1}{4\epsilon}\right)\|T\|_{L^{2}}^{2}.

(3.8)

Next, we have the following elementary estimate (cf.
[
21
, Lemma 1.50]
).

Lemma 3.6
.

For any
k\geq 0
, there exists a constant
C_{k}=C_{k}(m)
such that for any tensor
T\in W^{k,2}(T^{r,s}M)
,

\displaystyle\int f^{k}|T|^{2}\,dV_{f}\leq C_{k}\|T\|_{W^{k,2}}^{2}.

(3.9)

Proof.

The case
k=0
is obvious. Suppose (
3.9
) holds for
k-1
.

We compute from
\Delta_{f}f=m/2-f
that

\displaystyle\Delta_{f}f^{k}=kf^{k-1}\Delta_{f}f+k(k-1)f^{k-2}|\nabla f|^{2}=\frac{km}{2}f^{k-1}-kf^{k}+k(k-1)f^{k-2}|\nabla f|^{2}.

(3.10)

Using integration by parts, we obtain

\displaystyle\int(\Delta_{f}f^{k})|T|^{2}\,dV_{f}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\int\langle\nabla f^{k},\nabla|T|^{2}\rangle\,dV_{f}\geq-2k\int f^{k-1}|\nabla f||T||\nabla T|\,dV_{f}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle-2k\int f^{k-\frac{1}{2}}|T||\nabla T|\,dV_{f}\geq-\frac{k}{2}\int f^{k}|T|^{2}\,dV_{f}-2k\int f^{k-1}|\nabla T|^{2}\,dV_{f},

(3.11)

where we have used
|\nabla f|^{2}\leq f
.

Combining (
3.10
) and (
3.11
), we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\int f^{k}|T|^{2}\,dV_{f}\leq

\displaystyle\frac{m}{2}\int f^{k-1}|T|^{2}\,dV_{f}+(k-1)\int f^{k-2}|\nabla f|^{2}|T|^{2}\,dV_{f}+2\int f^{k-1}|\nabla T|^{2}\,dV_{f}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle(m/2+k-1)\int f^{k-1}|T|^{2}\,dV_{f}+2\int f^{k-1}|\nabla T|^{2}\,dV_{f}\leq C\|T\|_{W^{k,2}}^{2},

where we have used the inductive assumption for
T
and
\nabla T
.
∎

Next, we prove the following elliptic estimate for
\Delta_{f}
on tensors.

Proposition 3.7
.

Suppose
(M,g)
has bounded curvature. For any
k\geq 2
, there exists a constant
C
so that if
T\in L^{2}(T^{r,s}M)
and
\Delta_{f}T\in W^{k-2,2}(T^{r,s}M)
, then
T\in W^{k,2}(T^{r,s}M)
and

\displaystyle\|T\|_{W^{k,2}}\leq C\left(\|\Delta_{f}T\|_{W^{k-2,2}}+\|T\|_{L^{2}}\right).

(3.12)

Proof.

From Remark
3.4
, we only need to prove (
3.12
) for smooth
T
with compact support.

For any
k\geq 1
, it is clear by direct computation that

\displaystyle\Delta\nabla^{k}T=

\displaystyle\nabla^{k}\Delta T+\sum_{i=0}^{k}\nabla^{i}\mathrm{Rm}*\nabla^{k-i}T,

(3.13)

\displaystyle f_{i}\nabla_{i}\nabla^{k}T=

\displaystyle f_{i}\nabla^{k}\nabla_{i}T+\nabla f*\left(\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}\nabla^{i}\mathrm{Rm}*\nabla^{k-1-i}T\right),

(3.14)

where
A*B
denotes a linear combination of contractions of the tensor product of
A
and
B
.

In addition, we compute

\displaystyle\nabla^{k}(f_{i}\nabla_{i}T)=

\displaystyle f_{i}\nabla^{k}\nabla_{i}T+\sum_{i=2}^{k+1}\nabla^{i}f*\nabla^{k+2-i}T=f_{i}\nabla^{k}\nabla_{i}T+1*\nabla^{k}T+\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}\nabla^{i}\mathrm{Rm}*\nabla^{k-i}T,

(3.15)

where we have used
\nabla^{2}f=g/2-\mathrm{Rc}
. Combining (
3.13
), (
3.14
) and (
3.15
), we obtain

\displaystyle\Delta_{f}\nabla^{k}T=\nabla^{k}\Delta_{f}T+1*\nabla^{k}T+\sum_{i=0}^{k}\nabla^{i}\mathrm{Rm}*\nabla^{k-i}T+\nabla f*\left(\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}\nabla^{i}\mathrm{Rm}*\nabla^{k-1-i}T\right).

(3.16)

Based on (
3.16
) and the integration by parts, we have

\displaystyle\int|\nabla(\nabla^{k}T)|^{2}\,dV_{f}=-\int\langle\Delta_{f}\nabla^{k}T,\nabla^{k}T\rangle\,dV_{f}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle-\int\langle\nabla^{k}(\Delta_{f}T),\nabla^{k}T\rangle\,dV_{f}+C\int|\nabla^{k}T|^{2}+\sum_{i=0}^{k}|\nabla^{i}\mathrm{Rm}||\nabla^{k-i}T||\nabla^{k}T|+\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}|\nabla f||\nabla^{i}\mathrm{Rm}||\nabla^{k-1-i}T||\nabla^{k}T|\,dV_{f}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle-\int\langle\nabla^{k}(\Delta_{f}T),\nabla^{k}T\rangle\,dV_{f}+C\int\sum_{i=0}^{k}|\nabla^{i}T|^{2}+\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}f|\nabla^{i}T|^{2}\,dV_{f}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle-\int\langle\nabla^{k}(\Delta_{f}T),\nabla^{k}T\rangle\,dV_{f}+C\|T\|_{W^{k,2}}^{2},

(3.17)

where we have used Lemma
3.6
for the last inequality. Similarly, we compute

\displaystyle-\int\langle\nabla^{k}(\Delta_{f}T),\nabla^{k}T\rangle\,dV_{f}=\int\langle\nabla^{k-1}(\Delta_{f}T),\Delta_{f}\nabla^{k-1}T\rangle\,dV_{f}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int|\nabla^{k-1}(\Delta_{f}T)|^{2}\,dV_{f}+C\int|\nabla^{k-1}(\Delta_{f}T)|\left(\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}|\nabla^{i}T|+\sum_{i=0}^{k-2}|\nabla f||\nabla^{i}T|\right)\,dV_{f}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C\int|\nabla^{k-1}(\Delta_{f}T)|^{2}\,dV_{f}+C\|T\|_{W^{k-1,2}}^{2}\leq C\left(\|\Delta_{f}T\|_{W^{k-1,2}}^{2}+\|T\|_{W^{k-1,2}}^{2}\right).

(3.18)

Combining (
3.17
) and (
3.18
), we conclude that

\displaystyle\int|\nabla^{k+1}T|^{2}\,dV_{f}\leq C\left(\|\Delta_{f}T\|_{W^{k-1,2}}^{2}+\|T\|_{W^{k,2}}^{2}\right).

(3.19)

Therefore, the conclusion follows from (
3.8
), (
3.19
) and an inductive argument.
∎

We investigate the spectral properties of
\Delta_{f}
acting on
T^{r,s}M
.

Proposition 3.8
.

\Delta_{f}
has discrete eigenvalues
0=\mu_{0}\leq\mu_{1}\leq\cdots\to+\infty
with finite-dimensional eigenspaces
E_{\mu_{i}}\subset W^{1,2}(T^{r,s}M)
. Moreover, there exists a constant
C
such that for any
T\in W^{1,2}(T^{r,s}M)
, we have

\displaystyle\|T-\pi_{E_{0}}(T)\|_{W^{1,2}}\leq C\|\Delta_{f}T\|_{L^{2}}.

(3.20)

If we assume
(M,g)
has bounded curvature, then for any
T\in W^{k,2}(T^{r,s}M)
with
k\geq 2
, we have

\displaystyle\|T-\pi_{E_{0}}(T)\|_{W^{k,2}}\leq C\|\Delta_{f}T\|_{W^{k-2,2}}.

(3.21)

Proof.

The discreteness of the spectrum follows from Corollary
3.5
and the Rellich compactness from
W^{1,2}(T^{r,s}M)
to
L^{2}(T^{r,s}M)
. Notice that the Rellich compactness for tensor fields can be proved similarly as in
[
15
, Appendix]
. We sketch the proof for readers’ convenience. Suppose
T_{i}
is a sequence of tensor fields with uniformly bounded
W^{1,2}
. By taking a subsequence, we may assume
T_{i}\to T
weakly in
W^{1,2}
and
T_{i}\to T
a.e. In particular,
T\in W^{1,2}
by the weak convergence. By using the log-Sobolev inequality on Ricci shrinkers (cf.
[
42
, Theorem 11,12]
), we obtain

\displaystyle\int|T_{i}-T|^{2}\log|T_{i}-T|^{2}\,dV_{f}-\left(\int|T_{i}-T|^{2}\,dV_{f}\right)\log\left(\int|T_{i}-T|^{2}\,dV_{f}\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 4\int|\nabla|T_{i}-T||^{2}\,dV_{f}\leq 4\int|\nabla(T_{i}-T)|^{2}\,dV_{f},

where we have used Kato’s inequality for the last inequality. Clearly, this implies that

\displaystyle\int|T_{i}-T|^{2}\log|T_{i}-T|^{2}\,dV_{f}\leq C

for a uniform constant
C
. Then
|T_{i}-T|
is uniformly integrable and hence
T_{i}\to T
in
L^{2}
(cf.
[
15
, Lemma 1]
).

The estimate
\|T-\pi_{E_{0}}(T)\|_{L^{2}}\leq C\|\Delta_{f}T\|_{L^{2}}
follows from a standard argument by using the decomposition of
T
into the sum of eigentensors. In general, (
3.20
) and (
3.21
) follow from Corollary
3.5
and Proposition
3.7
, respectively.
∎

As a corollary of Proposition
3.8
, we have the following characterization of the Sobolev spaces, which is well-known for compact manifolds (cf.
[
58
, Chapter 5]
).

Corollary 3.9
.

Suppose
(M,g)
has bounded curvature. Let
\{U_{i}\}
be an orthonormal basis of
L^{2}(T^{r,s}M)
such that
\Delta_{f}U_{i}+\mu_{i}U_{i}=0
. For any
T\in L^{2}(T^{r,s}M)
, we set
x_{i}:=\langle T,U_{i}\rangle_{L^{2}}
. Then for any
k\geq 0
,
T\in W^{k,2}
if and only if

\displaystyle\|T\|^{2}_{D^{k}}:=\sum_{i}\mu_{i}^{k}x_{i}^{2}<\infty.

Moreover, the norms
\|\cdot\|_{D^{k}}
and
\|\cdot\|_{W^{k,2}}
are comparable.

Proof.

The case
k=0
is trivially true. We next prove the case
k=1
.

First, we assume
T\in W^{1,2}
, then by Remark
3.4
that there exists a sequence of smooth tensors
T_{j}
with compact support such that
T_{j}\to T
in
W^{1,2}
. We set the coefficients of
T_{j}
with respect to
U_{i}
to be
x_{j,i}
. Then we have

\displaystyle\sum_{i}\mu_{i}x_{j,i}^{2}=-\int\langle\Delta_{f}T_{j},T_{j}\rangle\,dV_{f}=\int|\nabla T_{j}|^{2}\,dV_{f}

for a constant
C
independent of
j
. Since
x_{j,i}\to x_{i}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\|T\|^{2}_{D^{1}}=\sum_{i}\mu_{i}x_{i}^{2}\leq\int|\nabla T|^{2}\,dV_{f}\leq\|T\|_{W^{1,2}}^{2}.

Conversely, suppose

\displaystyle\|T\|^{2}_{D^{1}}=\sum_{i}\mu_{i}x_{i}^{2}<\infty.

We set
T_{j}=\sum_{i\leq j}x_{i}U_{i}
, and it is clear that
T_{j}\to T
in
L^{2}
. Again by Lemma
3.3
, we obtain

\displaystyle\int|\nabla T_{j}|^{2}\,dV_{f}=-\int\langle\Delta_{f}T_{j},T_{j}\rangle\,dV_{f}=\sum_{i\leq j}\mu_{i}x_{i}^{2}\leq\|T\|^{2}_{D^{1}}.

In other words,
\|T_{j}\|_{W^{1,2}}
is uniformly bounded. By taking a subsequence, we may assume
T_{j}\to T
weakly in
W^{1,2}
. From this, it is immediate that

\displaystyle\int|\nabla T|^{2}\,dV_{f}\leq\liminf_{j}\int|\nabla T_{j}|^{2}\,dV_{f}\leq\|T\|^{2}_{D^{1}}.

In addition, since
\mu_{i}\to\infty
, it is clear that

\displaystyle\int|T|^{2}\,dV_{f}=\sum x_{i}^{2}\leq\|T\|^{2}_{D^{1}}.

Therefore,
\|T\|^{2}_{D^{1}}
is comparable to
\|T\|_{W^{1,2}}
.

In general, suppose the conclusion holds for any integer
<k
.

If
\|T\|_{D^{k}}^{2}<\infty
, it follows from Proposition
3.7
that

\displaystyle\|T\|_{W^{k,2}}^{2}\leq C\left(\|\Delta_{f}T\|_{W^{k-2,2}}^{2}+\|T\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\right)\leq C\left(\|\Delta_{f}T\|_{D^{k-2}}^{2}+\|T\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\right).

(3.22)

where we have used our inductive assumption. Since
\Delta_{f}T=-\sum_{i}\mu_{i}x_{i}U_{i}
, we have

\displaystyle\|\Delta_{f}T\|_{D^{k-2}}^{2}=\sum\mu_{i}^{k-2}(\mu_{i}x_{i})^{2}=\sum\mu_{i}^{k}x_{i}^{2}=\|T\|_{D^{k}}^{2}.

Therefore, it follows from (
3.22
) that
\|T\|_{W^{k,2}}\leq C\|T\|_{D^{k}}^{2}
.

Conversely, if
T\in W^{k,2}
, we divide into two cases.

Case 1
:
k=2l
is even.

In this case, it is clear that

\displaystyle\|T\|_{D^{k}}^{2}=\|\Delta^{l}_{f}T\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\leq C\|T\|_{W^{2l,2}}^{2}.

Case 2
:
k=2l+1
is odd.

In this case, we have

\displaystyle\|T\|_{D^{k}}^{2}=\|\Delta^{l}_{f}T\|_{D^{1}}^{2}\leq C\|\Delta^{l}_{f}T\|_{W^{1,2}}^{2}\leq C\|T\|_{W^{2l+1,2}}^{2}.

In sum, the conclusion holds for
k
, and the proof is complete by induction.
∎

For the rest of the section, we assume
|\mathrm{Rm}|
is bounded and focus on operators acting on symmetric
2
-tensors or
1
-forms.

We first consider the operator
\mathcal{P}=\delta_{f}\delta_{f}^{*}
on
1
-forms on
M
. Notice that
\mathcal{P}
is a self-adjoint elliptic operator (cf.
[
21
, Lemma 2.1]
). In addition, it follows from
[
21
, Lemma 4.2]
that
\mathcal{P}
has a discrete spectrum such that each eigenspace is finitely dimensional and generated by eigenvectors of
\Delta_{f}
. In particular, if we denote the kernel space of
\mathcal{P}
in
L^{2}
by
\mathcal{K}_{\mathcal{P}}
, then
\mathcal{K}_{\mathcal{P}}
is finitely dimensional and each element lies in
W^{k,2}\cap C^{\infty}(T^{*}M)
for any
k
. In particular, integration by parts for
\mathcal{P}
indicates that each element in
\mathcal{K}_{\mathcal{P}}
is indeed a Killing form in the usual sense.

Next, we prove the following existence result, which is similar to
[
21
, Theorem 4.15]
.

Proposition 3.10
.

For any
k\geq 1
, there exists a constant
C
so that for any
h\in W^{k,2}(S^{2}(M))
, there exists a
1
-form
w\in W^{k+1,2}(T^{*}M)
with
\mathcal{P}w=\delta_{f}h
that is
L^{2}
-orthogonal to
\mathcal{K}_{\mathcal{P}}
and satisfies

\displaystyle\|w\|_{W^{k+1,2}}\leq C\|\delta_{f}h\|_{W^{k-1,2}}.

Proof.

It follows from
[
21
, Lemma 4.20]
that there exists an orthonormal basis
\{U_{i}\}_{i\geq 0}
of
L^{2}(T^{*}M)
such that
\mathcal{P}U_{i}=\lambda_{i}U_{i}
for
0=\lambda_{0}<\lambda_{1}\leq\lambda_{2}\leq\cdots\to\infty
. Moreover,
\Delta_{f}U_{i}+\tau_{i}U_{i}=0
.

We set
\delta_{f}h=\sum_{i\geq 0}x_{i}U_{i}
, where
x_{i}=\langle\delta_{f}h,U_{i}\rangle_{L^{2}}
. Then it follows from our assumption and Corollary
3.9
that

\displaystyle\|\delta_{f}h\|^{2}_{D^{k-1}}=\sum_{i\geq 0}\tau_{i}^{k-1}x_{i}^{2}<\infty.

We define

In this appendix, we slightly generalize the growth control of eigentensors on Ricci shrinkers as established by Colding and Minicozzi

Let

Notice that for Ricci shrinkers, both

Substituting this into (

), we find:

\displaystyle(\log U(r))^{\prime}\geq

Remark A.2
.

As an application, Proposition
A.1
can be applied to a small perturbation of a Ricci shrinker with uniformly bounded Ricci curvature. Specifically, let
(M^{n},\bar{g},\bar{f})
be a Ricci shrinker with normalization (
1.2
) and bounded Ricci curvature.

Consider a smooth symmetric
(0,2)
-tensor
h
and a smooth function
\psi
on
M
satisfying

\displaystyle\|h\|_{C^{1}}+\|\psi\|_{C^{2}}\leq\theta,

where
\theta
is a small constant. For the perturbed metric
g:=\bar{g}+h
and function
f:=\bar{f}+\psi
, it is straightforward to estimate:

\displaystyle|n/2-\Delta f|\leq C\theta b+C\quad\text{and}\quad|f-|\nabla f|^{2}|\leq C\theta b^{2}+C,

where
b:=2\sqrt{f}
, and
C
is a constant depending on the dimension
n
and the Ricci curvature bound of
\bar{g}
. Therefore, Proposition
A.1
is applicable to
(M,g,f)
.
