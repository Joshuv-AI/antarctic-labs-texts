Construction of genuinely multipartite entangled subspaces and the associated bounds on entanglement measures for mixed states Preprint: APS/123-QED
K. V. Antipin

Abstract
Genuine entanglement is the strongest form of multipartite entanglement. Genuinely entangled pure states contain entanglement in every bipartition and as such can be regarded as a valuable resource in the protocols of quantum information processing. A recent direction of research is the construction of genuinely entangled subspaces — the class of subspaces consisting entirely of genuinely multipartite entangled pure states. In this paper we present several methods of construction of such subspaces including those of maximal possible dimension. The approach is based on the correspondence between bipartite entangled subspaces and quantum channels of a certain type. The examples include maximal subspaces for systems of three qubits, four qubits, three qutrits. We also provide lower bounds on two entanglement measures for mixed states, the concurrence and the convex-roof extended negativity, which are directly connected with the projection on genuinely entangled subspaces.



I
Introduction

Quantum entanglement is an incredibly rich phenomenon relevant to a wide range of fields from condensed matter physics to quantum information science to particle physics. In quantum information theory entanglement is regarded as a resource for the tasks of quantum communication and computation
Wilde 2013
;
Preskill 2015
.

The structure of multipartite entanglement is far more complex than that in bipartite systems. There are various inequivalent entanglement classes
Dür et al. 2000
;
Verstraete et al. 2002
. There are also such peculiar properties as monogamy relations
Terhal 2004
;
Coffman et al. 2000
;
Koashi and Winter 2004
exhibited by some correlations of particles.

Genuine multipartite entanglement (GME)
Dür et al. 1999
is an extreme form of the described multipartite phenomenon. Pure GME states are entangled with respect to any bipartition of a compound system, the property that has recently found applications in the protocols of quantum information processing (see, e. g.,
Yeo and Chua 2006
;
Muralidharan and Panigrahi 2008
;
Yamasaki et al. 2018
).

An interesting direction of research is construction and characterization of genuinely entangled subspaces (GESs) — subspaces consisting entirely of genuinely multipartite entangled states. To our knowledge, this concept was first introduced in Ref.
Demianowicz and Augusiak 2018
, where some approaches to construction of GESs were also proposed. GESs can be interesting in connection with generating mixed GME states — any state with support on a GES is genuinely entangled. It is believed that GESs will be useful in quantum error correction
Huber and Grassl 2020
and quantum cryptography
Shenoy and Srikanth 2019
.

The research into the new methods of constructing GESs was continued in Refs.
Demianowicz and Augusiak 2019
;
Wang et al. 2019
;
Agrawal et al. 2019
;
Demianowicz and Augusiak 2020
. In Refs.
Demianowicz and Augusiak 2019
;
Wang et al. 2019
;
Agrawal et al. 2019
the construction was based mainly on the unextendible product bases (UPB) method
Bennett et al. 1999
;
DiVincenzo et al. 2003
and its variations. The approach in Ref.
Demianowicz and Augusiak 2020
was aimed at construction of GESs of maximal possible dimension and relied on the characterization of some bipartite entangled subspaces.

The motivation of the present paper is to develop an alternative approach to the construction of GES which is not based on UPB. At the center of our considerations is the correspondence between quantum channels and subspaces of a tensor product Hilbert space (see Section
II
). In many cases such technique allows us to obtain families of GESs with simple parameterization, including those of maximal possible dimension. Another aspect is detecting entanglement of mixed states and estimating entanglement measures for them. It is known that there is an entanglement witness associated with the projection on an entangled bipartite subspace
Sarbicki 2008
. The extension of this witness to the GME case was obtained in Ref
Demianowicz and Augusiak 2018
. In connection with the (bipartite) witness also lower bounds on some entanglement measures for bipartite mixed states can be derived
Antipin 2020
. We aim to extend these bounds to the GME case and use the examples of GESs we construct to illustrate their application. It was shown that computation of the convex-roof entanglement measures is NP-complete
Huang 2014
, so, in genneral, efficient algorithms or even closed mathematical expressions for them are impossible unless
\mathrm{P}=\mathrm{NP}
. In this regard such bounds play an important role in the theory of entanglement.

The paper is organized as follows. In Section
II
we provide the necessary definitions and theoretical background. The methods of constructing GESs and their application to concrete three- and four-partite systems are presented in Section
III
. In Section
IV
we provide estimation of entanglement of some constructed subspaces and talk about lower bounds on entanglement measures for mixed states connected with overlap with a given GES. We conclude in Section
V
and discuss possible developments of the present line of research.

II
Preliminaries

II.1
Genuine multipartite entanglement

Consider
n
-partite quantum states in the finite dimensional tensor product Hilbert space
H_{1}\otimes\ldots\otimes H_{n}
. A pure
n
-partite state
\ket{\psi}
is called
fully separable
if it can be written as a tensor product of states for every subsystem, i. e.

\ket{\psi}=\ket{\phi}_{1}\otimes\ldots\otimes\ket{\phi}_{n}.

(1)

States that are not fully separable are said to be
entangled
.

A pure
n
-partite state
\ket{\psi}
is called
biseparable
if it can be written as a tensor product

\ket{\psi}=\ket{\psi}_{A}\otimes\ket{\psi}_{\bar{A}}

(2)

with respect to some bipartition
A|\bar{A}
where
A
denotes a particular subset of subsystems and
\bar{A}
denotes its complement. A multipartite pure state is called
genuinely multipartite entanged
if it is not biseparable with respect to any bipartition.

There is generalization of these concepts to mixed states. A mixed multipartite state is called
biseparable
if it can be decomposed into a convex sum of biseparable pure states (note that different terms of the sum can be biseparable with respect to different bipartitions). Otherwise it is called
genuinely multipartite entanged
.

A subspace of a multipartite Hilbert space consisting entirely of entangled pure states is called
completely entangled
(CES). The examples of CESs are known
Parthasarathy 2004
;
Bhat 2006
;
Cubitt et al. 2008
. The natural generalization of this notion is
genuinely multipartite entangled subspaces
(GESs) – those composed entirely of genuinely entangled pure states.

II.2
Entanglement measures of states and subspaces

For the purpose of quantifying entanglement many entanglement measures were introduced, initially for bipartite systems
Plenio and Virmani 2007
;
Horodecki et al. 2009
;
Gühne and Tóth 2009
. Among them – the concurrence
Bennett et al. 1996
;
Wootters 1998
;
Badziag et al. 2002
, the negativity
Vidal and Werner 2002
;
Lee et al. 2003
and the geometric measure of entanglement
Barnum and Linden 2001
;
Wei and Goldbart 2003
.

The
concurrence
of a pure bipartite state
\psi
is defined by

C(\psi)=\sqrt{2\left(1-\Tr{\rho_A^2}\right)},

(3)

where
\rho=\outerproduct{\psi}{\psi}
and
\rho_{A}=\mathrm{Tr}_{B}\{\rho\}
.

For mixed states
\rho
the concurrence is given by the convex roof construction, the minimum average concurrence taken over all ensemble decompositions of
\rho
:

C(\rho)=\min_{\{(p_{j},\,\psi_{j})\}}\,\sum_{j}\,p_{j}\,C(\psi_{j}).

(4)

The
negativity
of
\rho
is defined as

N(\rho)=\frac{1}{2}(\norm{\rho^{T_B}}_{1}-1),

(5)

where
\rho^{T_{B}}
is the partial transpose of
\rho
with respect to party
B
, and
\norm{A}_{1}=\Tr{\sqrt{A^{\dagger}A}}
is the trace norm of
A
.

From the definition of
N
it is seen that entanglement of states with a positive partial transpose (PPT states) is
not detected
by this measure.

The
convex-roof extended negativity
(CREN) is given by

N^{\mathrm{CREN}}(\rho)=\min_{\{(p_{j},\,\psi_{j})\}}\,\sum_{j}\,p_{j}\,N(\psi_{j}).

(6)

The two measures defined via convex roof are able to detect
all
entangled states.

Given the Schmidt decomposition
\ket{\psi}=\sum_{i}\,\sqrt{\lambda_{i}}\ket{i}\otimes\ket{i}
of a bipartite pure state the geometric measure of entanglement is defined by

G(\psi)=1-\max_{i}\{\lambda_{i}\}.

(7)

This measure is also extended to mixed states by the convex roof construction.

Bipartite entanglement measures can be generalized to measures of genuine multipartite entanglement
Dai et al. 2020
. Given a bipartite entanglement measure
E
, the corresponding GME measure for a pure multipartite state
\ket{\psi}
is defined as

E_{GME}(\psi)=\min_{A}E_{A}(\psi),

(8)

where the minimum is taken over all possible bipartitions
A|\bar{A}
of a multipartite system and
E_{A}
denotes the bipartite entanglement measure with respect to bipartition
A|\bar{A}
. The measure
E_{GME}
is extended to mixed states by the convex roof construction

E_{GME}(\rho)=\min_{\{(p_{j},\,\psi_{j})\}}\,\sum_{j}\,p_{j}\,E_{GME}(\psi_{j}),

(9)

where, as usual, the minimization runs over all possible ensemble decompositions
\rho=\sum_{j}p_{j}\outerproduct{\psi_j}{\psi_j}
.

There are several ways of quantifying entanglement of a subspace. In the present paper we will use the approach suggested in Refs.
Gour and Wallach 2007
,
Demianowicz and Augusiak 2019
: the entanglement of a subspace
W
measured by
E
is defined by

E(W)=\min_{\ket{\psi}\in W}E(\psi).

(10)

In place of
E
here we will use the geometric measure of genuine entanglement,
G_{GME}
, which can be calculated via eqs. (
7
) and (
8
).

II.3
Quantum channels and entangled subspaces

Let
H
be a finite dimensional Hilbert space, and
\mathcal{B}(H)
– a collection of linear operators on
H
. Given two finite dimensional Hilbert spaces
H_{A}
and
H_{B}
, a
quantum channel
is a linear, completely positive and trace-preserving map between
\mathcal{B}(H_{A})
and
\mathcal{B}(H_{B})

Wilde 2013
. By definition, a quantum channel maps density operators to density operators.

Action of a quantum channel
\mathrm{\Phi}\colon\,\mathcal{B}(H_{A})\rightarrow\mathcal{B}(H_{B})
on an operator
C\in\mathcal{B}(H_{A})
, by Choi’s theorem
Choi 1975
, can be represented as

\mathrm{\Phi}(C)=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\,K_{i}CK_{i}^{\dagger},

(11)

where
N\leqslant\mathrm{dim}(H_{A})\,\mathrm{dim}(H_{B})
, and the operators
K_{i}\colon\,H_{A}\rightarrow H_{B}
satisfy the trace-preserving property

\sum_{i=1}^{N}\,K_{i}^{\dagger}K_{i}=I,

(12)

where
I
– the identity operator, which in this case acts on
H_{A}
. Representation (
11
) is often referred to as the Kraus decomposition of a quantum channel with Kraus operators
\{K_{i}\}
.

There is a one-to-one correspondence between channels and linear subspaces of composite Hilbert spaces
Aubrun and Szarek 2017
. Given a set of three Hilbert spaces
(H_{A},\,H_{B},\,H_{C})
, a subspace
W
of the composite space
H_{B}\otimes H_{C}
, and an isometry
V\,\colon\,H_{A}\rightarrow H_{B}\otimes H_{C}
whose range is
W
, the corresponding quantum channel
\mathrm{\Phi}\colon\,\mathcal{B}(H_{A})\rightarrow\mathcal{B}(H_{B})
can be defined by

\mathrm{\Phi}(\rho)=\mathrm{Tr}_{H_{C}}(V\rho V^{\dagger}).

(13)

Conversely, due to Stinespring’s dilation theorem
Stinespring 1955
, any quantum channel
\mathrm{\Phi}\colon\,\mathcal{B}(H_{A})\rightarrow\mathcal{B}(H_{B})
can be represented by eq. (
13
) for some subspace
W\subset H_{B}\otimes H_{C}
.

Given some Kraus representation (
11
) of a quantum channel
\mathrm{\Phi}\colon\,\mathcal{B}(H_{A})\rightarrow\mathcal{B}(H_{B})
, the isometry
V\,\colon\,H_{A}\rightarrow H_{B}\otimes H_{C}
can be defined by the action on a vector state
\ket{\psi}\in H_{A}
in the following way:

V\ket{\psi}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\,K_{i}\ket{\psi}\otimes\ket{i},

(14)

where
\{\ket{i}\}
– an orthonormal basis in
H_{C}
, and
\mathrm{dim}(H_{C})=N
. Choosing some orthonormal basis
\{{\ket{\mu}}\}
in
H_{A}
, via eq. (
14
) we obtain an orthonormal system of vectors
\{V\ket{\mu}\}
spanning the subspace
W\subset H_{B}\otimes H_{C}
.

Fig.
1
provides the diagrammatic representation of eq. (
13
). We adopt tensor diagram notation for quantum processes from Ref.
Coecke and Kissinger 2017
. Refs.
Wood et al. 2015
,
Biamonte 2019
are also excellent introductions into diagrammatic reasoning in quantum information theory.

Figure 1:
A representation of quantum channel
\mathrm{\Phi}
acting on a pure state
\ket{\phi}\in H_{A}
: at first, isometry
V
takes the state to
H_{B}\otimes H_{C}
, then subsystem
C
is traced out. The result is some density operator acting on
H_{B}
.

The entanglement of the subspace
W\subset H_{B}\otimes H_{C}
depends on the output characteristics of the corresponding quantum channel such as the maximal output norm
Amosov et al. 2000
. Let
\mathcal{D}(H)
denote the set of density operators in
\mathcal{B}(H)
.
The maximal output norm
of a channel
\mathrm{\Phi}\colon\,\mathcal{B}(H_{A})\rightarrow\mathcal{B}(H_{B})
is defined by

\nu_{p}(\mathrm{\Phi})=\sup_{\rho\in\mathcal{D}(H_{A})}\norm{\mathrm\Phi(\rho)}_{p},\,\,p>1,

(15)

where
\norm{\rho}_{p}=(\mathrm{Tr}(\absolutevalue{\rho}^{p}))^{1/p}
is the standard
p
-norm. Since
p
-norm is convex, it will take its maximum on the extremal states, and the supremum in eq. (
15
) can be taken over pure input states. We will mostly use the Frobenius norm (
p=2
) and refer to

(\nu_{2}(\mathrm{\Phi}))^{2}
as the
output purity
of a channel
\mathrm{\Phi}
.

Obviously, a subspace
W\subset H_{B}\otimes H_{C}
is completely entangled if and only if the output purity of the corresponding quantum channel is strictly less than one. In this case the isometry
V
will transform the vector states of
H_{A}
into bipartite entangled states of the subspace
W\subset H_{B}\otimes H_{C}
, and there will not be separable states in
W
. Therefore, we can construct bipartite completely entangled subspaces finding examples of quantum channels with output purity less than one along with the corresponding isometries. Choosing some orthonormal basis
\{\ket{i}\}
in
H_{A}
, we obtain the orthonormal basis
\{V\ket{i}\}
of the entangled subspace
W
via the isometry
V
. As we will see, the most interesting cases, examples of entangled subspaces of maximal dimension, can be obtained by constructing dimension changing channels
\mathrm{\Phi}\colon\,\mathcal{B}(H_{A})\rightarrow\mathcal{B}(H_{B})
with
\mathrm{dim}(H_{A})\neq\mathrm{dim}(H_{B})
. Furthermore, such channels can be used in construction of genuinely entangled subspaces.

Figure 2:
An isometry
V
acting on subsystem
B
of a pure bipartite entangled state
\ket{\psi_{AB}}
. The resulting pure state is tripartite, with subsystems
A
,
C
,
D
.

III
Construction of entangled subspaces

In this section we describe several methods of constructing completely and genuinely entangled subspaces with the use of quantum channels. We begin with the construction of genuinely entangled subspaces from entangled subspaces of a bipartite Hilbert space.

III.1
GES from bipartite CES

This method was inspired by tensor diagrams. Given a pure bipartite entangled state
\ket{\psi}_{AB}
, we can act on one of its two parties,
B
, with an isometry
V\,\colon\,H_{B}\rightarrow H_{C}\otimes H_{D}
corresponding to some quantum channel
\mathrm{\Phi}\colon\,\mathcal{B}(H_{B})\rightarrow\mathcal{B}(H_{C})
, as it is illustrated on Fig.
2
. When will the resulting state be entangled for any bipartite cut? It is convenient to consider the initial state
\ket{\psi}_{AB}
in the Schmidt form:

\ket{\psi}_{AB}=\sum_{i}\,\sqrt{p_{i}}\,\ket{i}_{A}\otimes\ket{i'}_{B},

(16)

where there are at least two nonzero Schmidt coefficients
\sqrt{p_{i}}
.
The resulting state

\displaystyle\ket{\chi}_{ACD}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(I\otimes V)\ket{\psi}_{AB}

(17)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}\,\sqrt{p_{i}}\,\ket{i}_{A}\otimes V\ket{i'}_{B}

is surely entangled across bipartition
A|CD
: tracing out, for example, subsystem
CD
, we obtain a mixed state on
A
:

\mathrm{Tr}_{CD}\left(\outerproduct{\chi}{\chi}_{ACD}\right)=\sum_{i}\,p_{i}\outerproduct{i}{i}_{A}.

(18)

Now consider bipartition
C|AD
. Tracing out subsystem
A
, then
D
, we obtain:

\displaystyle\mathrm{Tr}_{AD}\left(\outerproduct{\chi}{\chi}_{ACD}\right)

(19)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}\,p_{i}\mathrm{Tr}_{D}(V\outerproduct{i'}{i'}_{B}V^{\dagger})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}\,p_{i}\mathrm{\Phi}(\outerproduct{i'}{i'}_{B}),

where the second equality is due to eq. (
13
). For an arbitrary channel
\mathrm{\Phi}
this state may be pure – for example, we can choose a channel mapping all states to some pure state
\outerproduct{\phi}{\phi}_{C}
. In order to guarantee mixedness, we can choose a channel with
output purity less than one
– in this case each term in eq. (
19
) will be a mixed state, and the resulting state will be a convex combination of such mixed density operators. Entanglement across bipartition
D|AC
is analyzed similarly. Therefore, we’ve established that genuinely entangled tripartite states can be obtained from entangled bipartite ones by applying to one of the parties the isometry corresponding to a quantum channel with output purity less than 1.

Now, applying such an isometry to each state in a completely entangled subspace of a bipartite Hilbert space, we obtain a genuinely entangled subspace of a tripartite Hilbert space, and, since isometry preserves inner products, the orthonormal system of vectors spanning the latter is obtained by action of
V
on the orthonormal system spanning the former.

As an illustration of this approach we construct a four-dimensional genuinely entangled
3\otimes 3\otimes 3
subspace from a completely entangled
3\otimes 3
subspace of the same dimension. Here the bipartite
3\otimes 3
subspace is taken to be spanned by the following orthonormal system of vectors:

\ket{\psi_1}=\sqrt{\lambda_{1}}\ket{0}\otimes\ket{0}+\sqrt{1-\lambda_{1}}\ket{2}\otimes\ket{2},

(20a)

\ket{\psi_2}=\sqrt{\lambda_{2}}\ket{1}\otimes\ket{0}+\sqrt{1-\lambda_{2}}\ket{0}\otimes\ket{1},

(20b)

\ket{\psi_3}=\sqrt{\lambda_{3}}\ket{2}\otimes\ket{0}+\sqrt{1-\lambda_{3}}\ket{1}\otimes\ket{2},

(20c)

\ket{\psi_4}=\sqrt{\lambda_{4}}\ket{2}\otimes\ket{1}+\sqrt{1-\lambda_{4}}\ket{0}\otimes\ket{2},

(20d)

where
0<\lambda_{i}<1,\>i=1,\,2,\,3,\,4
.

In fact, eq. (
20
) determines a family of completely entangled subspaces parameterized by
\{\lambda_{i}\}
. They can be constructed with the use of specific dimension changing quantum channels (see Appendix
A
).

Now, given a concrete
3\otimes 3
entangled subspace, we choose an isometry to act with on subsystem
B
, the second qutrit. As was shown above, the range of the isometry should be a completely entangled subspace, or, equivalently, the corresponding quantum channel should have output purity less than one. Such a role can play, for example, the isometry
V\,\colon\,H_{B}\rightarrow H_{C}\otimes H_{D}
which maps orthonormal basis in
H_{B}
to vectors spanning the antisymmetric subspace of
H_{C}\otimes H_{D}
:

V\ket{0}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\ket{0}\otimes\ket{1}-\ket{1}\otimes\ket{0}),

(21a)

V\ket{1}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\ket{0}\otimes\ket{2}-\ket{2}\otimes\ket{0}),

(21b)

V\ket{2}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\ket{1}\otimes\ket{2}-\ket{2}\otimes\ket{1}).

(21c)

Acting with
(I\otimes V)
on the basis (
20a
-
20d
), we obtain the orthonormal system of vectors spanning a four-dimensional genuinely entangled subspace:

\displaystyle\ket{\phi_1}=\sqrt{\frac{\lambda_{1}}{2}}(\ket{0}\otimes\ket{0}\otimes\ket{1}-\ket{0}\otimes\ket{1}\otimes\ket{0})

\displaystyle+\sqrt{\frac{1-\lambda_{1}}{2}}(\ket{2}\otimes\ket{1}\otimes\ket{2}-\ket{2}\otimes\ket{2}\otimes\ket{1}),

(22a)

\displaystyle\ket{\phi_2}=\sqrt{\frac{\lambda_{2}}{2}}(\ket{1}\otimes\ket{0}\otimes\ket{1}-\ket{1}\otimes\ket{1}\otimes\ket{0})

\displaystyle+\sqrt{\frac{1-\lambda_{2}}{2}}(\ket{0}\otimes\ket{0}\otimes\ket{2}-\ket{0}\otimes\ket{2}\otimes\ket{0}),

(22b)

\displaystyle\ket{\phi_3}=\sqrt{\frac{\lambda_{3}}{2}}(\ket{2}\otimes\ket{0}\otimes\ket{1}-\ket{2}\otimes\ket{1}\otimes\ket{0})

\displaystyle+\sqrt{\frac{1-\lambda_{3}}{2}}(\ket{1}\otimes\ket{1}\otimes\ket{2}-\ket{1}\otimes\ket{2}\otimes\ket{1}),

(22c)

\displaystyle\ket{\phi_4}=\sqrt{\frac{\lambda_{4}}{2}}(\ket{2}\otimes\ket{0}\otimes\ket{2}-\ket{2}\otimes\ket{2}\otimes\ket{0})

\displaystyle+\sqrt{\frac{1-\lambda_{4}}{2}}(\ket{0}\otimes\ket{1}\otimes\ket{2}-\ket{0}\otimes\ket{2}\otimes\ket{1}).

(22d)

Another example will be given in Section
IV
in the context of estimating entanglement of the constructed subspaces.

The disadvantage of the described approach is that we cannot construct genuinely entangled subspaces of maximal dimension – the resulting subspace has the same dimension as the initial bipartite one. In general, according to Parthasarathy’s result
Parthasarathy 2004
, the maximal dimension of completely entangled subspaces of a
d_{1}\otimes d_{2}
Hilbert space is

d_{1}d_{2}-(d_{1}+d_{2})+1,

(23)

and so this is the upper bound for the dimension of genuinely entangled
d_{1}\otimes d^{\prime}_{2}\otimes d^{\prime}_{3}
subspaces generated with this approach (
d^{\prime}_{2}
,
d^{\prime}_{3}
– output dimensions of the isometry used for the construction).

III.2
Constructing GES of maximal dimension

Our next approach aims at constructing genuinely entangled subspaces of maximal possible dimension.
Given a pure state
\ket{\psi}_{ABC}
on a tripartite
d\otimes d\otimes d
Hilbert space, we consider its bipartition
AB|C
where subsystems
A
and
B
are gathered into one subsystem
AB
described by a Hilbert space of dimension
d^{2}
(see Fig.
3
).

Figure 3:
Gathering subsystems of a tripartite pure state
\ket{\psi}_{ABC}
into bipartition
AB|C
. The dimensions of the corresponding Hilbert spaces are shown.

Figure 4:
Steps of constructing a genuinely entangled subspace: the isometry
V
maps a Hilbert space
H_{E}
to a completely entangled subspace of
H_{A}\otimes H_{B}
, then subsystem
A
is factorized into
A_{1}
and
A_{2}
.

According to Ref.
Parthasarathy 2004
, the maximal dimension of subspaces, which are completely entangled over bipartition
AB|C
, is
d^{2}\times d-(d^{2}+d)+1
. The same being said about the other two bipartitions,
BC|A
and
AC|B
, this number is the maximal dimension of genuinely entangled
d\otimes d\otimes d
subspaces.

Our strategy is first to construct a maximal dimensional completely entangled subspace of a tensor product Hilbert space
H_{A}\otimes H_{B}
with
\mathrm{dim}(H_{A})=d^{2}
and
\mathrm{dim}(H_{B})=d
, then
factorize
the first subsystem
A
into two
d
-dimensional subsystems
A_{1}
and
A_{2}
, i. e., perform an operation somewhat inverse to the one shown on Fig.
3
. There are many ways to do such a factorization, and for our purpose it should be chosen in such a way that the resulting tripartite states are entangled across bipartitions
A_{1}|A_{2}B
and
A_{2}|A_{1}B
(the entanglement across
A_{1}A_{2}|B
is preserved under such an operation).

According to the above plan, we first construct an isometry
V\colon\,H_{E}\rightarrow H_{A}\otimes H_{B}
, where
H_{E}
– a Hilbert space of dimension
d^{2}\times d-(d^{2}+d)+1
. If this isometry corresponds, via Stinespring’s dilation, to a quantum channel with output purity less than one, then its range will be a completely entangled subspace of
H_{A}\otimes H_{B}
with maximal dimension. Next, we apply a proper factorization of
H_{A}
into
H_{A_{1}}\otimes H_{A_{2}}
and obtain a genuinely entangled tripartite subspace of maximal possible dimension,
d^{2}\times d-(d^{2}+d)+1
(see also Fig.
4
).

In order to find the desired isometry, we analyze the expression for the output purity of a quantum channel
\mathrm{\Phi}\colon\,\mathcal{B}(H_{E})\rightarrow\mathcal{B}(H_{A})
in terms of its Kraus operators. As it was mentioned above, it will suffice to consider the action of
\mathrm{\Phi}
only on pure states, and for an arbitrary pure state
\ket{\psi}\in H_{E}
we have:

\displaystyle\mathrm{Tr}(\mathrm{\Phi}(\outerproduct{\psi}{\psi})^{2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i,\,j}\mathrm{Tr}\left(K_{i}\outerproduct{\psi}{\psi}K_{i}^{\dagger}K_{j}\outerproduct{\psi}{\psi}K_{j}^{\dagger}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i,\,j}\mathrm{Tr}\left(\outerproduct{\psi}{\psi}K_{i}^{\dagger}K_{j}\outerproduct{\psi}{\psi}K_{j}^{\dagger}K_{i}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i,\,j}\absolutevalue{\bra{\psi}K_i^{\dagger}K_j\ket{\psi}}^{2}

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle\sum_{i,\,j}\bra{\psi}K_{i}^{\dagger}K_{i}\ket{\psi}\bra{\psi}K_{j}^{\dagger}K_{j}\ket{\psi}

(24)

\displaystyle=1,

where the Kraus decomposition (
11
) was used; the inequality follows from the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality; the last equality follows from eq. (
12
) averaged over
\ket{\psi}
and squared.

It is known that the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality for two vectors holds strictly, without equality if and only if they are linearly independent. Therefore, to guarantee that the trace in Eq. (
24
) is strictly less than one, we need to search for Kraus operators
\{K_{i}\}
satisfying the following conditions:

1.

The eigenvalues of
K_{i}^{\dagger}K_{i}
, for all
i
, are strictly less than one (otherwise, the trace in Eq. (
24
) will evaluate to
1
on the corresponding eigenvector)):

\norm{K_i^{\dagger}K_i}<1.

(25)

2.

For
any

\ket{\psi}\in H_{E}
there is at least one pair
\left(K_{i},\,K_{j}\right)
of distinct Kraus operators such that the vectors
K_{i}\ket{\psi}
and
K_{j}\ket{\psi}
are not proportional to each other.

Having found the proper Kraus operators

via eq. (

V

We illustrate the approach with several examples.

III.2.1
GES in three-qubit systems

Now we consider a system of
3

2\otimes 2\otimes 2

4\otimes 2

\mathrm{dim}(H_{E})=3

There are two Kraus operators in this particular case, and, in search for

III.2.3
GES in three-qutrit systems

Now we construct an example of GES of maximal dimension in a
3\otimes 3\otimes 3
Hilbert space. The bound on the dimension in this case is the same as the bound on the dimension of a
9\otimes 3
CES:
9\times 3-(9+3)+1=16
. Consequently, we need to construct an isometry
V\colon\,H_{E}\rightarrow H_{A}\otimes H_{B}
, with
\mathrm{dim}(H_{E})=16
,
\mathrm{dim}(H_{A})=9
,
\mathrm{dim}(H_{B})=3
, and with a completely entangled range.

Our first step is to construct the Kraus operators
K_{i}\colon\,H_{E}\rightarrow H_{A},\,i=1,\,2,\,3
, which in this case are represented by matrices with dimensions
9\times 16
. As in the previous examples, we first consider matrices corresponding to
K_{i}^{\dagger}K_{i}
, which should satisfy eqs. (
12
) and (
25
). Any attempt to make them all be purely diagonal immediately fails: we can write out the diagonals of
K_{1}^{\dagger}K_{1}
,
K_{2}^{\dagger}K_{2}
,
K_{3}^{\dagger}K_{3}
as columns of a matrix and, to satisfy eqs. (
12
) and (
25
), in each row of this matrix there must be at least two nonzero values adding up to
1
. Therefore, the total number of these values in all rows must be not less than
16\times 2=32
. On the other hand, being the eigenvalues of
K_{i}^{\dagger}K_{i}
, these numbers are the squares of singular values of
K_{i}
. There can be at most
9
nonzero singular values for each
K_{i}
because it is represented by a
9\times 16
matrix. In total, for all three
K_{i}
there can be at most
3\times 9=27
nonzero singular values, so we have a contradiction which shows that
K_{i}^{\dagger}K_{i}
cannot be purely diagonal.

To come around the described problem, we introduce a block-diagonal form for
K_{i}^{\dagger}K_{i}
. For each matrix representing
K_{i}^{\dagger}K_{i}
we consider the area consisting of three diagonals: the main diagonal and the two adjacent ones below and above. This area is divided into two parts down the diagonals: the fist part entirely consists of
2\times 2
blocks and the second one is purely diagonal – it has nonzero values only on the main diagonal. The
2\times 2
blocks placed on the same positions in
K_{1}^{\dagger}K_{1}
,
K_{2}^{\dagger}K_{2}
,
K_{3}^{\dagger}K_{3}
should add up to a
2\times 2
identity matrix to satisfy eq. (
12
). To satisfy also eq. (
25
), they should have eigenvalues strictly less than
1
. As we’ve shown above, for each
K_{i}^{\dagger}K_{i}
we only have
9
eigenvalues at our disposal to cover the diagonals, so we need to spare them. This can be achieved by choosing all
2\times 2
blocks to have only
1
nonzero eigenvalue. In this case each such block will cover two positions on the diagonal, but it will take only one eigenvalue. Having placed
5
such blocks down the diagonals in each
K_{i}^{\dagger}K_{i}
, we will have
9-5=4
eigenvalues left at our disposal. We can then put these eigenvalues down the main diagonal, thus covering the remaining positions. If we put the described three-diagonal areas of
K_{1}^{\dagger}K_{1}
,
K_{2}^{\dagger}K_{2}
,
K_{3}^{\dagger}K_{3}
in front of each other in the form of columns, the picture will look like this:

\begin{matrix}K_{1}^{\dagger}K_{1}\colon&&&&K_{2}^{\dagger}K_{2}\colon&&&&K_{3}^{\dagger}K_{3}\colon\\
\\
P_{1}^{(1)}&&&&P_{1}^{(2)}&&&&P_{1}^{(3)}\\
P_{2}^{(1)}&&&&P_{2}^{(2)}&&&&P_{2}^{(3)}\\
P_{3}^{(1)}&&&&P_{3}^{(2)}&&&&P_{3}^{(3)}\\
P_{4}^{(1)}&&&&P_{4}^{(2)}&&&&P_{4}^{(3)}\\

\lambda_{2}^{(1)}&&&&\lambda_{2}^{(2)}&&&&0\\
\lambda_{3}^{(1)}&&&&0&&&&\lambda_{3}^{(3)}\\
0&&&&\lambda_{3}^{(2)}&&&&\lambda_{4}^{(3)}\\
\lambda_{4}^{(1)}&&&&\lambda_{4}^{(2)}&&&&0\par\end{matrix},

(41)

IV
Entanglement of GES and the associated bounds on entanglement measures

IV.1
Controlling genuine entanglement of constructed subspaces

Let us return to the first approach to constructing GES described in Subsection
III.1
. Such subspaces are obtained from CES of a bipartite Hilbert space by the action of some isometry on one party. The entanglement of GES can be estimated if the characteristics of both the initial CES and the channel corresponding to the isometry are known. Across one bipartition it is determined by the entanglement of the initial CES (see eq. (
18
) for bipartition
A|CD
), across the other two – by the output norm of the quantum channel (see eq. (
19
) for bipartition
C|AD
). The maximal output norm of the channel will be an upper bound on the norms of the reduced density operators of the states in GES across these two bipartitions because the supremum in eq. (
15
) is achieved on pure states.

As an illustration, we take the initial CES to be the antisymmetric subspace
W_{-}
of a
3\otimes 3
Hilbert space, spanned by the three vectors

\displaystyle\ket{\psi}_{ij}=(\ket{i}\otimes\ket{j}-\ket{j}\otimes\ket{i})/\sqrt{2}

,

(52)

\displaystyle i,\,j=0,\ldots,2,\quad i<j.

It is known
Vidal et al. 2002
,
Antipin 2020
that the maximal first Schmidt coefficient over all states in this subspace is given by

\sup_{\psi_{AB}\in W_{-}}\norm{\mathrm{Tr}_B\dyad{\psi_{AB}}}^{1/2}=1/\sqrt{2}.

(53)

Consider the Holevo-Werner channel
Werner and Holevo 2002
acting on
d\times d
density matrices as

\displaystyle\mathrm{\Phi}(\rho)=\frac{1}{d-1}\left(I-\rho^{T}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i<j}K_{ij}\rho K_{ij}^{\dagger},

(54)

where

\displaystyle K_{ij}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{d-1}}\left(|i\rangle\langle j|-|j\rangle\langle i|\right),

\displaystyle i,\,j=0,\ldots,d-1,\quad i<j,

(55)

and
\rho^{T}
denotes the matrix transpose with respect to the computational basis.

The maximal output norm of the Holevo-Werner channel can be easily obtained from the first representation in eq. (
IV.1
) and is given by

\nu_{p}(\mathrm{\Phi})=(d-1)^{-(1-1/p)}

(56)

In our case,
d=3
, the channel can be represented by the
3
Kraus operators given by eq. (
IV.1
). The associated isometry
V
, obtained from eq. (
14
), acts on the computational basis as

\displaystyle V\ket{0}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left[-\ket{1}\otimes\ket{0}-\ket{2}\otimes\ket{1}\right],

\displaystyle V\ket{1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left[\ket{0}\otimes\ket{0}-\ket{2}\otimes\ket{2}\right],

\displaystyle V\ket{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left[\ket{0}\otimes\ket{1}+\ket{1}\otimes\ket{2}\right].

(57)

Acting with this isometry on the second qutrit of the vectors in eq. (

3\otimes 3\otimes 3
Hilbert space:

\displaystyle\ket{\phi_1}=\frac{1}{2}(\ket{0}\otimes\ket{0}\otimes\ket{0}-\ket{0}\otimes\ket{2}\otimes\ket{2}

\displaystyle+\ket{1}\otimes\ket{1}\otimes\ket{0}+\ket{1}\otimes\ket{2}\otimes\ket{1}),

(58a)

\displaystyle\ket{\phi_2}=\frac{1}{2}(\ket{0}\otimes\ket{0}\otimes\ket{1}+\ket{0}\otimes\ket{1}\otimes\ket{2}

\displaystyle+\ket{2}\otimes\ket{1}\otimes\ket{0}+\ket{2}\otimes\ket{2}\otimes\ket{1}),

(58b)

\displaystyle\ket{\phi_3}=\frac{1}{2}(\ket{1}\otimes\ket{0}\otimes\ket{1}+\ket{1}\otimes\ket{1}\otimes\ket{2}

\displaystyle-\ket{2}\otimes\ket{0}\otimes\ket{0}+\ket{2}\otimes\ket{2}\otimes\ket{2}).

(58c)

Let
S
denote this subspace. Consider the geometric measure

of genuine entanglement of

. The three qutrits are referred to in the same way as on Figure
2

A
,
C
and
D

A|CD
is determined by that of the initial antisymmetric bipartite subspace given by eq. (
53

C|AD
and

will be upper bounded by the output norm
\nu_{\infty}(\mathrm{\Phi})^{1/2}
of the Holevo-Werner channel, which, by eq. (

G_{GME}(S)\geqslant 1/2.

IV.2
Lower bounds on GME concurrence and negativity of mixed states

Obtained in Ref.
Antipin 2020
lower bounds on the concurrence and the convex-roof extended negativity of an arbitrary bipartite mixed state
\rho
are determined by its overlap with some completely entangled subspace
W
of a
d\times d
Hilbert space. For the concurrence the bound reads

C(\rho)\,\geqslant\,\max\left(\sqrt{\frac{2}{d(d-1)}}\,\frac{\Tr{\rho\,\Pi_W}-\bar{\lambda}^{\scriptscriptstyle W}_{1}}{\bar{\lambda}^{\scriptscriptstyle W}_{1}},\,0\right),

(60)

where
\Pi_{W}
– an orthogonal projector onto
W
,
\bar{\lambda}^{\scriptscriptstyle W}_{1}
– the supremum of the largest Schmidt coefficient squared taken over all vector states in the subspace
W
.
The bound on the negativity is given by

N^{\mathrm{CREN}}(\rho)\,\geqslant\,\max\left(\frac{\Tr{\rho\,\Pi_W}-\bar{\lambda}^{\scriptscriptstyle W}_{1}}{2\bar{\lambda}^{\scriptscriptstyle W}_{1}},\,0\right).

(61)

The underlying separability criterion was derived earlier in Ref.
Sarbicki 2008
with the use of the theory of entanglement witnesses. (see also Ref.
Demianowicz and Augusiak 2018
for the generalization to the GME case).

With the use of eqs. (
7
)-(
9
) these inequalities can be straightforwardly extended to the GME case:

\displaystyle C_{GME}(\rho)\,

\displaystyle\geqslant\,\sqrt{\frac{2}{d(d-1)}}\frac{1}{1-G_{GME}(W)}

\displaystyle\times

\displaystyle\max(\Tr{\rho\,\Pi_W}+G_{GME}(W)-1,\,0),

(62)

\displaystyle N_{GME}(\rho)\,

\displaystyle\geqslant\,\frac{1}{2(1-G_{GME}(W))}

\displaystyle\times

\displaystyle\max(\Tr{\rho\,\Pi_W}+G_{GME}(W)-1,\,0),

(63)

where now
W
is a genuinely entangled subspace. Therefore, the two GME measures can be estimated for a mixed state
\rho
if the value
G_{GME}(W)
or some good lower bound on it can be determined.

As an example, with the GES
S
from eq. (
58
), for a mixed state
\rho
the lower bound on the negativity is given by

N_{GME}(\rho)\,\geqslant\,\max(\Tr{\rho\,\Pi_S}-1/2,\,0),

(64)

where eqs. (
59
) and (
63
) were used.

Eqs. (
62
) and (
63
) (or the underlying separability criterion) are also convenient for estimating the robustness of genuine multipartite entanglement under mixing with noise. Consider a state
\rho
with support in some GES
W
being mixed with noise described by a density operator
N
. After mixing the state is given by the convex sum

(1-p)\rho+pN,\quad p\in[0;1].

(65)

By eqs. (
62
) and (
63
), this state will still be genuinely entangled if the lower bound on the entanglement measures is positive, i. e.,

\mathrm{Tr}\{(1-p)\rho\Pi_{W}+pN\Pi_{W}\}+G_{GME}(W)>1.

(66)

Taking into account that
\mathrm{Tr}\{\rho\Pi_{W}\}=1
(
\rho
has support in
W
), we obtain the upper bound

p<\frac{G_{GME}(W)}{1-\mathrm{Tr}\{N\Pi_{W}\}}

(67)

saying that under such proportions of the noise
N
the state in eq. (
65
) is guaranteed to be genuinely entangled.

Let
H
denote the Hilbert space of the described states. If only the white noise is considered,

N=\frac{1}{\mathrm{dim}(H)}I,

(68)

then the bound on
p
reads

p<\frac{G_{GME}(W)}{1-\mathrm{dim}(W)/\mathrm{dim}(H)}.

(69)

As an example, for the subspace
S
given by eq. (
58
) the robustness of entanglement under the white noise can be estimated with the use of eq. (
59
). It is given by
p<9/16=0.5625
.

Following Ref
Antipin 2020
, it is also interesting to analyze the case when the expression for
N
is unknown and only the spectrum of the noise is given (
robustness from spectrum
). The immediate generalization of eq. (55) in Ref.
Antipin 2020
to the GME case is given by

p<G_{GME}(W)/\norm{N}_{\scriptstyle{(\mathrm{codim}(W))}},

(70)

where
\mathrm{codim}(W)=\mathrm{dim}(H)-\mathrm{dim}(W)
and
\norm{N}_{(k)}
is the sum of
k
largest eigenvalues of
N
(
the
k
-th Ky Fan norm

Bhatia 1997
;
Horn and Johnson 2013
).

Eq. (
70
) may be useful in analysis of quantum states under the influence of random noise where some results on eigenvalues of random matrices can be applied.

V
Discussions

Several methods of constructing GESs have been presented. In the first one tripartite GESs are obtained from bipartite CESs by applying an isometry corresponding to a quantum channel with the maximal output norm strictly less than
1
. On the one hand, the dimensions of the obtained subspaces are not high and bounded by those of the initial CESs. On the other hand, the method is pretty simple and allows us to control the measures of entanglement of the constructed subspaces on condition that we have control over the CESs and the output characteristics of the channel being used. Highly entangled GESs can be generated in this manner. Quite possibly, other characteristics, such as distillability of entanglement across every bipartition, can be controlled in a similar way. In principle, the whole procedure can be applied to obtain
n+1
-partite GESs from
n
-partite ones. It is also interesting to ask whether this method can be realized in experimental setting: the isometry, for example, can be extended in some way to a unitary which may correspond to some physical operation. These questions will be addressed in the forthcoming work.

The second method is based on the direct construction of certain quantum channels with the subsequent factorization of some compound systems into smaller ones. The method can generate GESs of maximal possible dimension but it gets computationally hard with the increase of the number of parties and the dimensionalities of their systems. We were able to come up with families of maximal GESs for three-qubit systems and, to some extent, for four-qubit and three-qutrit systems where we can only conjecture that we are dealing with GESs in a wide range of the values of the parameters (we checked that they are GESs for various sets of the parameters).

One may benefit from these parameterizations in the analysis of various properties of entangled states and subspaces. As an example, starting from a maximal GES for a system of
3
qubits given by eqs. (
32
), one can easily find another maximal GES orthogonal to the first one:

\displaystyle\ket{\psi_1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sqrt{1-\lambda_{1}}\ket{0}\otimes\ket{0}\otimes\ket{0}

\displaystyle-\sqrt{\lambda_{1}}\ket{1}\otimes\ket{1}\otimes\ket{1},

\displaystyle\ket{\psi_2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sqrt{\frac{1-\lambda_{2}}{2}}\left(\ket{0}\otimes\ket{1}+\ket{1}\otimes\ket{0}\right)\otimes\ket{0}

\displaystyle-\sqrt{\lambda_{2}}\ket{0}\otimes\ket{0}\otimes\ket{1},

\displaystyle\ket{\psi_3}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sqrt{\frac{1-\lambda_{3}}{2}}\left(\ket{0}\otimes\ket{1}-\ket{1}\otimes\ket{0}\right)\otimes\ket{0}

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle\sqrt{\frac{\lambda_{3}}{2}}\left(\ket{0}\otimes\ket{1}+\ket{1}\otimes\ket{0}\right)\otimes\ket{1}.

These two GESs span in total
6
dimensions out of 8 in a tripartite Hilbert space (but note that their direct sum can’t be genuinely entangled). Their orthogonal complement, a
2
-dimensional subspace, contains separable vectors, for example,
\ket{1}\otimes\ket{1}\otimes\ket{0}
. Given an arbitrary mixed state of
3
qubits, one can optimize over the parameters
\lambda_{i}
and find the GES of one of the two types having maximal overlap with the state. If the state is genuinely entangled, it can be expected that the overlap will be significant. With the geometric measure of the optimal GES obtained, for example, numerically, one could then use eqs. (
62
), (
63
) to detect genuine entanglement of the state or to get estimates for its entanglement measures. The same can be said in relation to the four-qubit subspaces determined by eqs. (
35
), (
III.2.2
), (
III.2.2
), in which case the two orthogonal GESs will span together
14
dimensions out of
16
possible. The numerical analysis of the effectiveness of such an approach will be conducted elsewhere.

It seems that there is some trade-off between the geometric measure of entanglement of a GES and its dimension. It would be interesting to find examples of GESs optimal with respect to the robustness of entanglement of their states under mixing with the white noise, an estimate for which is given in eq. (
69
).
