Elusive phase transition in the replica limit of monitored systems
Guido Giachetti, Andrea De Luca

Abstract
We study an exactly solvable model of monitored dynamics in a system of N N spin 1 / 2 1/2 particles with pairwise all-to-all noisy interactions, where each spin is constantly perturbed by weak measurements of the spin component in a random direction. We make use of the replica trick to account for the Born’s rule weighting of the measurement outcomes in the study of purification and other observables, with an exact description in the large- N N limit. We find that the nature of the phase transition strongly depends on the number n n of replicas used in the calculation, with the appearance of non-perturbative logarithmic corrections that destroy the disentangled/purifying phase in the relevant n → 1 n\to 1 replica limit. Specifically, we observe that the purification time of a mixed state in the weak measurement phase is always exponentially long in the system size for arbitrary strong measurement rates.

A major difficulty in studying monitored systems is the inherently nonlinear effect induced by Born’s rule: the probability of obtaining a certain outcome depends on the quantum state itself. One way to cope with this nonlinearity is the replica trick, a celebrated technique in the statistical physics of disordered systems

n
identical copies of the system with unbiased measurements and finally takes the
n\to 1
limit by analytic continuation so as to restore the correct probability
Vasseur et al. 2019
. Attempts to construct a quantitative theory of MIPTs have mainly been based on this approach and the introduction of large-N models, flanked by Landau-Ginzburg-style field theories

Nahum et al. 2021a

Lopez-Piqueres et al. 2020

Kamenev and Levchenko 2009

Fava et al. 2023

Fava et al. 2023

n\to 1

In this letter, we introduce an exactly solvable model of monitored dynamics, composed by a system of
N
spin-
1/2

n

n\to 1

This implies that the purification time of a mixed state is always exponentially long in the system size. Beyond purification and entanglement, we validate our theory by looking at the full statistics of single-site observables.

For the calculation of the purity

Conclusions. —

We proposed the exact solution of an interacting many-body quantum system subject to continuous isotropic monitoring. Despite the mean-field nature of the solution, we found that the model does not undergo a MIPT: starting from the fully mixed state, the system purifies in a time exponentially long in system size. In terms of the replica formalism, this means that the Landau-Ginzbourg action which describes the system is always in a replica-broken phase. We remark that this is a consequence of the analytic continuation to
n\rightarrow 1
as, for larger
n
, the system indeed exhibits a spontaneous breaking of the permutational symmetry between replicas corresponding to different Keldysh path in correspondence of a finite
\gamma_{c}
.
A natural question is to understand the origin of the logarithmic perturbative corrections appearing in the
n\to 1
and whether the existence of an area-law / purifying phase could be restored with anisotropic measurements. It is also possible that a
N
-dependent scaling for
\gamma
could lead to a non-conventional phase transition, similar to what happens in many-body localisation
Morningstar et al. 2020
and integrability breaking
Bulchandani et al. 2022
.

Appendix A
Derivation of the weak measurement equations

A.1
Repeated interaction with the ancilla

Here, we describe briefly how the stochastic Schrödinger equation (SSE) for the weak measurement dynamics can be derived.
To simplify the notation, we will consider a generic system evolving under the Hamiltonian
H
and where a single observable
\hat{O}
is continuously monitored. First of all, we discretize time considering a finite small interval
\Delta t
.
The basic idea is that in a time step
t\in[\tau\Delta t,(\tau+1)\Delta t]
, the system
\mathcal{S}
is coupled to a (new) ancilla
\mathcal{A}
. For simplicity, the ancilla is supposed to be a spin
1/2
initial set in the state

\ket{\mathcal{A}}=\frac{\ket{+}+\ket{-}}{\sqrt{2}}

(SA.1)

At time
t=\tau\Delta t
, the state of the system + ancilla is thus in the factorized state

\ket{\Psi_{\tau}}=\ket{\psi_{\tau}}\otimes\ket{\mathcal{A}}

(SA.2)

The time evolution up to the next time step
\tau+1
is performed in two steps:

1.

the system and the ancilla evolve unitarily for a time
\Delta t
and get entangled because of the coupling between them;

2.

the
z
-component of the ancilla spin is measured projectively;

Let’s analyse the two steps. Under the unitary evolution
U
, one arrives at

\ket{\Psi_{\tau}}^{\prime}=U\ket{\Psi_{\tau}}=(K_{+}\ket{\psi_{\tau}})\otimes\ket{+}+(K_{-}\ket{\psi_{\tau}})\otimes\ket{-}\;,\qquad K_{\pm}=\braket{\pm|U|\mathcal{A}}

(SA.3)

where we introduced the operators
K_{\pm}
acting on the Hilbert space of the system
\mathcal{S}
. Unitarity of
U
and normalization of
\ket{\mathcal{A}}
imply the only constraint

\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{S}}=\braket{A|U^{\dagger}U|A}=K_{+}^{\dagger}K_{+}+K_{-}^{\dagger}K_{-}

(SA.4)

where
\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{S}}
is the identity operator on the system Hilbert space. This shows that
K_{\pm}
are Kraus operators.

Measuring the spin
\sigma_{z}
of the ancilla, one can obtain two possible outcomes
a_{\tau}=\pm 1
.
Correspondingly, the state of the system takes the form

\ket{\psi_{\tau+1}}=\frac{K_{a_{\tau}}\ket{\psi_{\tau}}}{\sqrt{\braket{\psi_{\tau}|K_{a_{\tau}}^{\dagger}K_{a_{\tau}}\ket{\psi_{\tau}}}}}

(SA.5)

A.2
Scaling limit

We now focus on the scaling limit
\Delta t\to 0
. To do so, we need an explicit form of the operators
K_{\pm}
. For simplicity, we focus on the situation when only one operator
O
is being monitored, the generalisation being straightforward.
Let us first consider the explicit form of the system and ancilla Hamiltonian
\mathcal{S}+\mathcal{A}
. We take

U=e^{-\mathrm{i}\Delta tH_{\mathcal{S}+\mathcal{A}}}\;,\quad H_{\mathcal{S}+\mathcal{A}}=H_{0}+\lambda O\sigma_{y}\;.

(SA.6)

Choosing
\sigma_{y}
as the operator on the ancilla is the simplest way to achieve monitoring.
The scaling limit is achieved taking
\lambda\to\infty
and
\Delta t\to 0
but in such a way that
\gamma=\lambda^{2}\Delta t
is kept constant where the rate
\gamma
parameterises the strength of the measurements. With this choice, expanding to the order
O(\Delta t)
, we have

U=e^{-\mathrm{i}(\Delta tH_{0}+\sqrt{\gamma\Delta t}O\sigma_{y})}\sim\mathbf{1}-\mathrm{i}\Delta tH_{0}-\mathrm{i}\sqrt{\gamma\Delta t}O\sigma_{y}-\frac{1}{2}\gamma\Delta tO^{2}+O(\Delta t^{3/2})

(SA.7)

From the definitions of
K_{\pm}
, we thus obtain

K_{\pm}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(\mathbf{1}-\mathrm{i}\Delta tH_{0}\mp\sqrt{\gamma\Delta t}O-\frac{1}{2}\gamma\Delta tO^{2}\right)+O(\Delta t^{3/2})

(SA.8)

In order to compute the norm, we expand

K_{a}^{\dagger}K_{a}=\frac{1}{2}-a\sqrt{\gamma\Delta t}O+O(\Delta t^{3/2})

(SA.9)

Therefore, we have

\ket{\psi_{\tau+1}}=\ket{\psi_{\tau}}-\mathrm{i}H_{0}\Delta t\ket{\psi_{\tau}}-a_{\tau}\sqrt{\gamma\Delta t}(O-\langle O\rangle)\ket{\psi_{\tau}}+\frac{3}{2}\gamma\Delta t\langle O\rangle^{2}\ket{\psi_{\tau}}-\gamma\Delta t\langle O\rangle O\ket{\psi_{\tau}}-\frac{1}{2}\gamma\Delta tO^{2}\ket{\psi_{\tau}}+O(\Delta t^{3/2})

(SA.10)

where we have introduced the notation
\langle O\rangle=\braket{\Psi_{t}|O|\Psi_{t}}
.
Finally, to get the continuous time limit as a stochastic differential equation, we observe that the measurement outcome
a_{\tau}
is a random variable that satisfies

\overline{a_{\tau}}=-2\sqrt{\gamma\Delta t}\langle O\rangle\;,\quad\overline{a_{\tau}^{2}}=1\;.

(SA.11)

Therefore, defining
M_{\tau}=\sqrt{\Delta t}\sum_{\tau^{\prime}\leq\tau}a_{\tau^{\prime}}
, we find that the variable
Y_{n}
converges in the limit
\Delta t\to 0
to a stochastic process solving

dM=-2\sqrt{\gamma}\langle O\rangle dt+dM

(SA.12)

being

a standard Wiener process (i.e.
\overline{dY}=0
and
\overline{dY^{2}}=dt
).
Eq. (
SA.12
) is easily generalized to Eq. (
1
) in the main text.
Replacing
a_{n}\to dM/\sqrt{\Delta t}
and using (
SA.12
) in the limit
\Delta t\to 0
, we recover

d\ket{\Psi_{t}}=-\mathrm{i}\hat{H}_{0}dt\ket{\Psi_{t}}+\left(\sqrt{\gamma}[\hat{O}-\langle\hat{O}\rangle_{t}]dY-\frac{\gamma}{2}[\hat{O}-\langle\hat{O}\rangle_{t}]^{2}dt\right)\ket{\Psi_{t}}

(SA.13)

This equation can be easily generalised to the case where one simultaneously monitors several observables
O_{1},\ldots

introducing independent Wiener processes for each operator undergoing monitoring

d\ket{\Psi_{t}}=-\mathrm{i}\hat{H}dt\ket{\Psi_{t}}+\sum_{i}\left(\sqrt{\gamma}[\hat{O}_{i}-\langle\hat{O}_{i}\rangle]dY_{i}-\frac{\gamma}{2}[\hat{O}_{i}-\langle\hat{O}_{i}\rangle_{t}]^{2}dt\right)\ket{\Psi_{t}}

(SA.14)

From this one can derive the evolution for the density matrix
\rho=\ket{\Psi_{t}}\bra{\Psi_{t}}

d\rho=-\mathrm{i}dt[\hat{H},\varrho]-\gamma\sum_{i}\left[\frac{1}{2}\{\hat{O}_{i}^{2},\varrho\}-O_{i}\varrho O_{i}\right]+\sqrt{\gamma}\sum_{i}dY_{i}\{\hat{O}-\langle\hat{O}\rangle_{t},\varrho\}

(SA.15)

A.3
Replica trick

The previous derivation allows computing the average over the outcomes of the measurements of functions on the state
\ket{\Psi_{t}}
. Typical examples are moments of observables:

\mathbb{E}(\langle A\rangle^{k})=\int\mathcal{D}YP_{W}(Y)\braket{\Psi_{t}|A|\Psi_{t}}^{k}

(SA.16)

where
\mathbb{E}(\ldots)
indicates an averaging over the measurements outcomes,
and
P_{W}(Y)
is the measure over the Wiener processes introduced in Eq. (
SA.12

SA.16

\ket{\tilde{\Psi}_{\textbf{a},t}}=K_{a_{N}}\ldots K_{a_{2}}K_{a_{1}}\ket{\Psi_{0}}=\mathcal{K}_{\textbf{a}}\ket{\Psi_{0}}\;,\qquad\mathcal{K}_{\textbf{a}}=K_{a_{N}}\ldots K_{a_{2}}K_{a_{1}}

(SA.17)

where the

. For clarity, we wrote explicitly the dependence on the sequence of measurements as a superscript. Then, we have

\mathbb{E}(\langle A\rangle^{k})=\sum_{\textbf{a}}P(\textbf{a})\left(\frac{\braket{\tilde{\Psi}_{\textbf{a},t}|A|\tilde{\Psi}_{\textbf{a},t}}}{\braket{\tilde{\Psi}_{\textbf{a},t}|\tilde{\Psi}_{\textbf{a},t}}}\right)^{k}=\frac{1}{2^{N}}\sum_{\textbf{a}}\braket{\Psi_{0}|\mathcal{K}^{{\dagger}}_{\textbf{a}}\mathcal{K}_{\textbf{a}}|\Psi_{0}}^{1-k}\braket{\Psi_{0}|\mathcal{K}^{{\dagger}}_{\textbf{a}}A\mathcal{K}_{\textbf{a}}|\Psi_{0}}^{k}

(SA.18)

where we used that

K_{a}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\exp[-i\Delta tH-a\sqrt{\gamma\Delta t}O-\gamma\Delta tO^{2}]+O(\Delta t^{3/2})

(SA.19)

Observe that, because of Ito’s calculus, the last term in the exponent must be inserted to obtain the proper expansion up to the order

. We can now take the limit

(SA.20)

Note that in the continuous limit the non-unitary evolution operator

satisfy

\mathcal{K}_{t+dt,M}=e^{-\mathrm{i}d\hat{H}}\mathcal{K}_{t,M}\;,\qquad d\hat{H}=\hat{H}_{0}dt-\mathrm{i}\sqrt{\gamma}dM\hat{O}-\mathrm{i}\gamma\hat{O}^{2}dt

(SA.21)

so that

plays the role of a non-hermitian stochastic Hamiltonian. Its generalisation to many monitored operators leads to Eq. (
6
) in the main text.

Finally, in order to avoid the presence of a negative exponent

in the averaging procedure in Eq. (
SA.20
),
we can make use of the replica trick thus replacing

\rho^{(n)}=\int\mathcal{D}M\underbrace{\tilde{\rho}_{t,M}\otimes\ldots\otimes\tilde{\rho}_{t,M}}_{n}\equiv\mathbb{E}_{G}[\rho_{t,M}^{\otimes n}]\;,\qquad\tilde{\rho}_{t,M}=\mathcal{K}_{t,M}\ket{\Psi_{0}}\bra{\Psi_{0}}\mathcal{K}^{\dagger}_{t,M}\;.

(SA.22)

where we introduce the Gaussian expectation
\mathbb{E}_{G}[\ldots]
where the signal

is treated as a unbiased Wiener process. In this way, we can write via the replica trick

\mathbb{E}(\langle A\rangle^{k})=\lim_{n\to 1}\operatorname{Tr}[\rho^{(n)}\underbrace{A\otimes\ldots\otimes A}_{\text{$k$ times}}]=\lim_{n\to 1}\operatorname{Tr}[\rho^{(n)}A^{\otimes k}]\equiv\mathbb{E}_{G}[\tilde{\rho}_{M}^{\otimes n}A^{\otimes k}]

. It follows that the saddle point solution corresponds to the minimization of the effective action

\mathcal{I}[X^{+\pm}_{ab}]=\frac{1}{2T}\int dt\sum_{a,b}\left((X^{+-}_{a,b})^{2}-(X^{++}_{a,b})^{2}\right)-\frac{1}{T}\ln\operatorname{tr}\Omega\varrho(T)

(SB.41)

where now

\begin{split}\frac{d}{dt}\varrho\equiv\mathcal{L}^{(n)}(\varrho)=\sum_{ab}\left[(\gamma+X_{ab}^{+-}(t))S^{\alpha}_{a}\varrho S^{\alpha}_{b}+\frac{1}{2}(\gamma-X_{a,b}^{++}(t))\{\mathbf{S}_{a}\cdot\mathbf{S}_{b},\varrho\}\right]-2\gamma ns(s+1)\varrho.\end{split}

(SB.42)

For
s=1/2
, we recover the results of Eq. (
7
) and Eq. (
8
) of the main text.

Finally, we notice that, as to to determine
X_{ab}^{+-}(t)
we have to minimize Eq. (

X^{++}_{aa}=\braket{\mathbf{S}^{2}}=s(s+1)\ .

(SB.43)

C.1
Derivation of Eq. (
10
) of the main text

For large time

, the effective action will be dominate by the constant bulk value of the

so that we can write

\mathcal{I}(X^{+\pm}_{ab})=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{a,b}\left((X^{+-}_{a,b})^{2}-(X^{++}_{a,b})^{2}\right)-\lambda_{n}(X^{+\pm}_{a,b})

(SC.44)

where the

\lambda_{n}(X^{+\pm}_{a,b})
is the maximum eigenvalue of
\mathcal{L}^{(n)}
.
Note that using the symmetry of the action, for any permutation
\mathbb{P}
, one can generate different minima by letting
\mathbb{P}
act on the
+
replicas (equivalently one could act on the
-
ones)

\begin{split}&X^{+-}_{ab}\left[P\right]\equiv\bar{X}^{+-}_{P(a)b},\\
&X^{++}_{ab}\left[P\right]\equiv\bar{X}^{++}_{P(a)P(b)}\\
&X^{--}_{ab}[P]\equiv\bar{X}^{--}_{a,b}.\end{split}

(SC.45)

In the unbroken phase, there will be a unique solution so that
all
X^{+\pm}_{ab}
for all

n!
solutions. As discussed in the main text, we can assume for the

the ansatz

\begin{split}\bar{X}^{+-}_{a,b}&=r+X\delta_{ab}\\
\bar{X}^{++}_{a,b}&=q(1-\delta_{a,b})+s(s+1)\delta_{a,b},\end{split}

(SC.46)

where the scalar variable
X
plays the role of the order parameter.
Given that
\varrho_{\rm max}^{\mathbb{1}}
is the maximum eigenvector of
\mathcal{L}^{(n)}
in the identity sector, we can obtain the steady states in the other sectors using

\varrho_{\rm max}^{\mathbb{P}}\to R[\mathbb{P}^{-1}]\varrho_{\rm max}^{\mathbb{1}}

(SC.47)

where
R[\mathbb{P}^{-1}]
is the representation of the permutation
\mathbb{P}
in the space of replicas.

In terms of this Eq. (
SB.42
) becomes

\frac{d}{dt}\tilde{\varrho}=(\gamma+r)S^{\alpha}_{\rm tot}\tilde{\varrho}S^{\alpha}_{\rm tot}+\frac{1}{2}(\gamma-q)\{\mathbf{S}^{2}_{\rm top},\tilde{\varrho}\}+X\sum_{a}S^{\alpha}_{a}\tilde{\varrho}S^{\alpha}_{a}+\left(q-2\gamma-s(s+1)\right)ns(s+1)\tilde{\varrho}

(SC.48)

where
\mathbf{S}_{\rm tot}=\sum_{a}\mathbf{S}_{a}
. In terms of this parametrization, the effective action
\mathcal{I}
becomes, up to additive constants,

\mathcal{I}(q,r,X)=\left[\frac{1}{2}n(n-1)(r^{2}-q^{2})+\frac{n}{2}(r+X)^{2}-\lambda_{n}(X,q,r)\right]\ .

(SC.49)

We can now look for the maximum eigenvector by looking at the replica-symmetric subspace. In turn, this means writing
\tilde{\varrho}
as linear combination of the projectors
\Pi_{\ell}
onto the
\mathbf{S}^{2}_{\rm tot}=\ell(\ell+1)
subspace, with
\ell=n/2-\left\lfloor{n/2}\right\rfloor,\dots,n/2
. The
\Pi_{\ell}
are normalized as such that
\Pi_{\ell}\Pi_{\ell^{\prime}}=0
for
\ell\neq\ell^{\prime}
and
\operatorname{tr}\Pi_{\ell}=1
. Let us note that
S^{\alpha}_{\rm tot}\Pi_{\ell}=\Pi_{\ell}S^{\alpha}_{\rm tot}
and
\mathbf{S}^{2}_{\rm tot}\Pi_{\ell}=\ell(\ell+1)\Pi_{\ell}
. We thus have that the diagonalization of the operator
\mathcal{L}^{(n)}

\left\lceil{n/2}\right\rceil

\begin{split}(\mathcal{L}^{(n)})_{\ell,\ell^{\prime}}&=\operatorname{tr}\left(\Pi_{\ell^{\prime}}\mathcal{L}^{(n)}(\Pi_{\ell})\right)\\
&=(2\gamma+r-q)\ell(\ell+1)\delta_{\ell,\ell^{\prime}}+X\sum_{a,\alpha}\operatorname{tr}\Pi_{\ell}S_{a}^{\alpha}\Pi_{\ell^{\prime}}S^{\alpha}_{a}+\frac{3}{4}n\left(q-2\gamma-\frac{3}{4}\right)\delta_{\ell,\ell^{\prime}}.\end{split}

(SC.50)

As a consequence, we have that

\lambda_{n}=(2\gamma+r-q)\Lambda_{n}(x)+s(s+1)n\left(q-2\gamma-s(s+1)\right)

(SC.51)

where

x=\frac{X}{2\gamma+r-q}

(SC.52)

and
\Lambda_{n}(x)
is the maximum eigenvalue of the matrix

L_{\ell,\ell^{\prime}}=\ell(\ell+1)\delta_{\ell,\ell^{\prime}}+x\sum_{a,\alpha}\operatorname{tr}\Pi_{\ell}S_{a}^{\alpha}\Pi_{\ell^{\prime}}S^{\alpha}_{a}\ .

(SC.53)

The effective action
\mathcal{I}

\mathcal{I}=\frac{1}{2}n(n-1)(r^{2}-q^{2})+\frac{n}{2}\left(r+(2\gamma+r-q)x\right)^{2}-(2\gamma+r-q)\Lambda_{n}(x)-s(s+1)nq\ .

(SC.54)

is now quadratic in
q
and

\partial_{q}\mathcal{I}=0
,
\partial_{r}\mathcal{I}=0

(SC.55)

which, for

C.4
The
n\rightarrow 1
replica limit

As we do not have the generic analytic form of
\Lambda_{n}(x)
, it is not straightforward to take the
n\rightarrow 1
limit. As
\Lambda_{n}(0)=n(n+2)/4
, a perturbative expansion around
x=0
could be carried out; unfortunately, the higher order (
\geq 3
) coefficients of these expansions turn out to be non-analytic functions of
n
. On the other hand, we can notice that the matrix
B=\partial_{x}L
introduced in Eq. (
SC.59
) can be expressed in terms of
SU(2)
ladder operators, so that its spectrum
\{b_{p}\}
is given by

b_{p}=\frac{3}{4}n-2p

(SC.75)

with
p=0,\cdots,\left\lceil{n/2}\right\rceil
. Therefore,
\Lambda_{n}(x)\sim 3n/4x
for
x\gg 1
, and it is possible to carry out a perturbative expansion around
x=\infty
obtaining

\Lambda_{n}(x)=\frac{3}{4}n(1+x)+n(n-1)\sum^{\infty}_{k=1}P_{k}(n)x^{-k}

(SC.76)

where
P_{k}(n)
are increasingly complicated degree
k-1
polynomials in
n
. In the vicinity of
n=1
we get

\Lambda_{n}(x)=\frac{3}{4}n(1+x)+(n-1)\left(\frac{3}{16}\frac{1}{x}-\frac{3}{16}\frac{1}{x^{2}}+\frac{63}{256}\frac{1}{x^{3}}-\frac{27}{64}\frac{1}{x^{4}}+\frac{1899}{2048}\frac{1}{x^{5}}-\frac{81}{32}\frac{1}{x^{6}}+\cdots\right)+O(n-1)^{2}.

(SC.77)

Even if it is not straightforward to determine the analytic dependency on
n
of the generic term of this asymptotic expansion, one can recognize in the square bracket term the expansion of

\frac{K_{0}(x)}{K_{1}(x)}=1-\frac{1}{2x}+\frac{3}{8}\frac{1}{x^{2}}-\frac{3}{8}\frac{1}{x^{3}}+\frac{63}{128}\frac{1}{x^{4}}-\frac{27}{32}\frac{1}{x^{5}}+\frac{1899}{1024}\frac{1}{x^{6}}-\frac{81}{16}\frac{1}{x^{7}}+\cdots\

(SC.78)

where
K_{p}(x)
denotes the order-

modified Bessel function of the second kind (see
Martin and Kearney 2010
for an analysis of this series). We have the following

\Lambda_{n}(x)=\frac{3}{4}(1+x)+(n-1)\left(1+\frac{x}{4}+\frac{x}{2}\frac{K_{0}(x)}{K_{1}(x)}\right)+O(n-1)^{2}.

(SC.79)

Finally, we find

\mathcal{I}(x)=\frac{2(n-1)}{1+2x}\left[\left(\gamma+\frac{1}{4}\right)(1+x)-\frac{x}{4}\frac{K_{0}(x)}{K_{1}(x)}\right]^{2}+O(n-1)^{2}.

(SC.80)

As expected the action vanishes for
n=1
, so we inspect the behavior for
n\to 1
. As it happens often in replica limits of disordered systems, the nature of minima/maxima in the Landau-Ginzburg action changes for
n\to 1^{+}
and
n\to 1^{-}
. It is clear that the physical limit for us corresponds to
n\rightarrow 1^{+}
, regardless of the value of
\gamma
, as for
n<1
, the action is not bounded from below. For
n\to 1^{+}
, the action always has a local maximum for
x=0
(
\mathcal{I}(x)\sim\mathcal{I}(0)+x^{2}\ln x
) and a minimum for finite
x
(see Fig.
S2
, right panel). So, as stated in the main text, the system is always in the broken phase, that is,

for any
\gamma
. Small and large

expansions of Eq. (
SC.80
) give the asymptotic regimes

\displaystyle x^{\ast}\sim\frac{3}{8\gamma}\to\infty\;,\qquad\gamma\to 0

(SC.81)

\displaystyle x^{\ast}\sim 2e^{-2\gamma-\gamma_{\rm EM}-1}\;,\qquad\gamma\to\infty

(SC.82)

where
\gamma_{\rm EM}
is the Euler-Mascheroni constant.
