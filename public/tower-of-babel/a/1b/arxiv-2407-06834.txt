Efficient nonlocal linear image denoising: Bilevel optimization with Nonequispaced Fast Fourier Transform and matrix-free preconditioning Thanks: Submitted to the editors DATE.
Andrés Miniguano-Trujillo, John W. Pearson, Benjamin D. Goddard

Abstract
We present a new approach for nonlocal image denoising, based around the application of an unnormalized extended Gaussian ANOVA kernel within a bilevel optimization algorithm. A critical bottleneck when solving such problems for finely–resolved images is the solution of huge–scale, dense linear systems arising from the minimization of an energy term. We tackle this using a Krylov subspace approach, with a Nonequispaced Fast Fourier Transform utilized to approximate matrix–vector products in a matrix–free manner. We accelerate the algorithm using a novel change of basis approach to account for the (known) smallest eigenvalue–eigenvector pair of the matrices involved, coupled with a simple but frequently very effective diagonal preconditioning approach. We present a number of theoretical results concerning the eigenvalues and predicted convergence behavior, and a range of numerical experiments which validate our solvers and use them to tackle parameter learning problems. These demonstrate that very large problems may be effectively and rapidly denoised with very low storage requirements on a computer.



keywords

Parameter identification; ANOVA kernel; Nonequispaced Fast Fourier Transform; fast matrix–vector multiplication; nonlocal image denoising

Introduction

Image denoising is the process of reducing or removing unwanted noise in an image, and forms a crucial step for image processing and image analysis
[
79
,
19
,

]
. This paper focuses on fast, effective, and storage–efficient numerical methods for the resolution of such problems.

Variational methods are a powerful approach to study and formalize denoising models from a functional perspective
[
80
,
79
]
. In particular, the minimization of the total variation seminorm
[
77
]
and the success of the Chambolle–Pock algorithm
[

have inspired an extensive catalog of improved methods and algorithms. In this work, we consider nonlocal image denoising, which was inspired by image filter methods incorporating information from generalized neighborhoods of a pixel to reconstruct it. This way, the amount of redundancy of data in a digital image becomes a crucial tool for guiding the reconstruction
[
56
,
44
]
. In particular, the nonlocal means filter
[
12
]
and its many extensions
[
14
,
22
]
have been effective in removing noise, while preserving textures and avoiding common pitfalls of local models. Crucially, its extension to a variational setting in
[

]
led to a range of new developments, including a rich analytical theory
[

,
25
]
, by making use of tools from nonlocal calculus
[
44
]
.

Two caveats are encountered when employing nonlocal tools for denoising: parameter identification and computational cost. The former is a classical question of denoising methods, due to the parametric nature of models that weight regularization against fidelity and underlying information. Bilevel optimization has proven an effective tool to determine high–quality reconstructions from trained parameters; see
[
25
]
for a review. The latter is instead inherent to nonlocal terms. The use of such terms yields dense discretizations of operators, the computation of which can be computationally cumbersome on its own, and thus limits the usage of these tools for large–scale or more complex applications.

The parameter calibration of the nonlocal means model was studied in
[
36
]
. There, the nonlocal kernel was approximated using a large–neighborhood localized approach that was suitable for small– and medium–sized images and which resulted in a multi–banded semi–sparse matrix. This sparsification technique is just another approach to improve the performance of the reconstruction originally proposed in
[
12
]
. For instance,
[

]
used a semilocal approach to reduce the amount of comparisons between patches; see also
[

,

,

.

Nonlocal operators in variational imaging arise as continuous extensions of kernel–based methods that encode an underlying space of features. Reproducible kernel Hilbert spaces (RKHSs) have gained significant attention due to their key connection with Gaussian processes
[
38
,
75
,
93
]
, their flexibility in representing complex function spaces
[

,
67
]
, and their effectiveness in various inverse problem settings
[
60
,
5
,
92
]
. A notable example is the ANOVA kernel, which is designed to decompose interactions among different feature components, allowing for a structured representation of multi–dimensional dependencies
[

,
48
,
6

. The kernel is often presented as

\gamma^{t}_{\textsf{ANOVA}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\coloneqq\sum_{1\leq i_{1}<i_{2}<\ldots<i_{t}\leq d}\,\prod_{j=1}^{t}\gamma_{i_{j}}(x_{i_{j}},y_{i_{j}}),

of the additive kernel which allow us to rely on fewer features

.

Letting all underlying kernels
\gamma_{i_{j}}
in

\gamma(x,y)=e^{-\sigma^{-2}|x-y|^{2}}

\sigma>0

\gamma^{d}_{\textsf{ANOVA}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})=\exp\mathinner{\left\{-\sigma^{-2}\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\right\rVert}^{2}\right\}}.

In other words, the

, §3]

98
]
. Summing all the

. In

\gamma_{\textsf{e--ANOVA}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})=\frac{1}{\mathsf{L}}\sum_{\ell=1}^{\mathsf{L}}\exp\mathinner{\left\{{-}\sigma^{-2}\big\|\mathsf{W}_{\ell}[\mathbf{x}]-\mathsf{W}_{\ell}[\mathbf{y}]\big\|_{2}^{2}\right\}}\eqqcolon\frac{1}{\mathsf{L}}\sum_{\ell=1}^{\mathsf{L}}\gamma_{\ell}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}).

Here, the sub–kernels

The kernel in

This work introduces an efficient computational framework, suitable for large images and for the incorporation of a large number of features without the need for localization or sparsity enforcement. The method is based on the fast summation approach and feature splitting for applying the
unnormalized extended Gaussian ANOVA kernel

eq.

2
. The summation algorithm is in turn based on the Nonequispaced Fast Fourier Transform (NFFT)
[
59
,
72
,
73
]
, which we deploy for image denoising problems in this work. This algorithm yields an operator that is used to solve the denoising problem for a given parameter configuration. In our setting, this leads to a linear system where the nonlocal kernel is used to assemble an abstract unnormalized graph Laplacian shifted by a constant scaling of the identity. The abstract linear operator setting is suitable for matrix–free solution schemes, including suitable Krylov subspace iterative methods that require a fixed (moderate) number of matrix–vector multiplications.

Our proposed framework extends the applicability of
[
36
]
to large–scale imaging tasks. Specifically, a broader class of kernels is introduced to incorporate full covariate information without enforcing sparsity, thus capturing broad nonlocal interactions. This step avoids the introduction of approximation errors from localization while gaining computational efficiency through fast summation techniques. Moreover, in contrast to
[
36
]
, where the numerical challenges posed by ill–conditioning are not addressed, this work analyzes and integrates dedicated preconditioning strategies. Additionally, we provide a rigorous analysis of discrete nonlocal operators, enabling the use of conjugate gradient (CG) as an efficient linear solver.
Thus, our combined contributions provide an efficient and portable procedure for solving discretized nonlocal systems arising in similar imaging applications. The structure of the linear systems involved may also arise in applications outside imaging, for instance feature training for neural networks
[
49
]
.

The solution of linear systems governed by graph Laplacians using such methods is greatly aided by effective preconditioning. A number of approaches have been developed and refined when the full matrix is available, see for instance
[
85
,
68
,
42
]
, however besides diagonal–based preconditioners
[
90
,
74
]
there is limited work on methods for matrix–free systems. The sparse approximate inverse approach for
M
–matrices is an exception, where
(\mathsf{I}+S)
–type preconditioners have proven a computationally cheap alternative
[
83
,
91
,
51
,
103
]
; see also
[
78
]
. Notwithstanding, in practice, many of these reduce to the classical Jacobi diagonal preconditioner for the graph Laplacian. This work extends the Jacobi preconditioner, and its quadratic variant applied in
[
36
]
, using a matrix–free approach that would otherwise result in dense preconditioners under a change of basis that singles out the smallest eigenvalue of the system. Motivated by deflation, exact operator formulae are derived for the change of basis, whose applicability extends to Hermitian finite dimensional operators. The transformation displays linear computational complexity and can be used efficiently to project the action of any preconditioner under the new basis for a given subblock of the linear system.

The key contributions of this article are summarized below:

•

We embed an NFFT routine into bilevel optimization for image denoising for the first time.

•

We devise a novel strategy based on a change of basis and preconditioning, to accelerate the matrix–free solution of the resulting linear systems with Krylov subspace methods.

•

We present new analytic results concerning spectral properties of graph Laplacians and shifted equations governed by them. We also present results on the effectiveness of the preconditioners, specifically by localizing the eigenvalues of the preconditioned system.

•

We complement our analysis with numerical tests to demonstrate the effectiveness of our NFFT and deflation by change of basis strategies, with near–constant iteration counts exhibited.

•

We also train a denoising parameters from a dataset of images featuring patterns, textures, and sudden intensity jumps, showcasing the effectiveness of the kernel to obtain reconstructions under the presence of such structures.

•

We provide open–source code for the above tests, available at
edin.ac/3zh86hT
.

2
Preliminaries on variational denoising and control

Our work is motivated by the practical computation of a particular class of nonlocal noise filters. Image denoising is the process of reducing or removing unwanted noise in an image. It is used either to enhance the visual appeal of images or as a preliminary step for image analysis and feature extraction. Noise typically corrupts an observed image due to instrument manipulation or medium artifacts. There are many models for noise
[
79
, §2.3]
, where Gaussian additive noise is the most suitable for digital images
[
61
, §1]
. The model states that a clean discretized image
\mathbf{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is distorted by a Gaussian signal
\mathbf{h}\in\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}_{n},\hat{\sigma}^{2}\mathsf{I}_{n})
, yielding
\mathbf{f}=\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{h}
as a registered image. Finding
\mathbf{u}
from this relation is a difficult task, due to the random nature of
\mathbf{h}
. As a result, the main task of denoising is to identify and remove the noise while preserving the most important information and structures. Thus, there are several approaches to analyzing and tackling this problem from the perspectives of stochastic processes, calculus of variations, partial differential equations, and wavelet theory
[
61
,
13
]
. We refer the reader to
[
7
,
80
,
79
,
19
]
for comprehensive introductions and reviews on imaging and denoising methods.

2.1
Variational denoising

Variational imaging casts an image as a signal
u
defined on a continuous medium. In this context, a registered noisy sample
\mathbf{f}
is just a quantization of a noisy signal
f
. Here, denoising methods use image priors and minimize an energy function to calculate the denoised image:

u\in\argmin_{v\in V}\mathcal{E}(v),

(3)

where
V
is a suitable function space. The motivation for
eq.

3
is the
maximum a posteriori
(MAP) probability estimate. From a Bayesian perspective, the MAP estimate of
v
can be written as

u=\argmax_{v\in V}\,\log\mathrm{P}(f|v)+\log\mathrm{P}(v),

where the first term
\mathrm{P}(f|v)
is a likelihood function of
v
, and the second term
\mathrm{P}(v)
represents the image prior. For additive white Gaussian noise, the energy
\mathcal{E}
is often selected as

u=\argmin_{v\in V}\,\mu R(v)+\frac{\lambda}{2}\|v-f\|^{2}_{L^{2}},

where
R
denotes a regularization term weighted by
\mu>0
, and
\frac{1}{2}\|v-f\|^{2}_{L^{2}}
is a data fidelity weight denoting the distance between the reconstructed and noisy samples weighted by
\lambda>0
. We refer the reader to
[
71
]
for a comprehensive review on the design of priors inspired by the underlying statistical properties of the noise model. In this work, we will focus on a prior that exploits the variational structure of the problem. This perspective naturally connects to the use of differential operators and their nonlocal counterparts, which we now discuss.

The variational standpoint allows us to properly define local differential operators and energies (e.g., Total Variation, Laplacians, Mean Curvature, and so on) and extend them to their nonlocal counterparts
[
56
,
44
]
.
The reasoning behind local operators is that they are good for smoothing out regions of the image, as showcased in the Rudin–Osher–Fatemi model of Total Variation Minimization (ROF)
[
77
]
. ROF allows for
u
to be piece–wise discontinuous, thus an effective choice for edge preservation. However, the ROF model and many of its variants can fail to preserve the fine structure; i.e., details and textures of an image. Due to the regularity assumptions of local operators, fine structures are smoothed out because they behave, under the action of the operator, as noise.
Nonlocal imaging is a valuable alternative that aims to preserve most of the fine details (e.g., contours, texture, high-contrast edges, flat areas) by considering lower regularity assumptions on
u
and the large amount of redundant information in textured images
[
28
,
27
]
.

2.2
Preliminaries on nonlocal calculus

We follow the approach of
[
43
]
. Let
\Omega
be a bounded domain in
\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}}
and
u\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
be a real function. The notion of a derivative can be generalized to a nonlocal framework by introducing the
nonlocal derivative
:

\partial_{\mathbf{y}}u(\mathbf{x})\coloneqq\frac{u(\mathbf{y})-u(\mathbf{x})}{\delta(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{x})},

(4)

where
\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\in\Omega
and
\delta(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{x})\in(0,\infty]
is a positive and symmetric quantity. Let us define the weight function
\gamma\coloneqq\delta^{-2}
so that
\gamma(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\in[0,\infty)
and
\gamma(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})=\gamma(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{x})
.
Then
eq.

4
can be written as

\partial_{\mathbf{y}}u(\mathbf{x})\coloneqq[u(\mathbf{y})-u(\mathbf{x})]\sqrt{\gamma(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})}.

Moreover, we define the
nonlocal gradient

\nabla_{\gamma}u(\mathbf{x})\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\Omega\to\Omega\times\Omega
as the family of nonlocal derivatives

\nabla_{\gamma}u(\mathbf{x})=\{\partial_{\mathbf{y}}u(\mathbf{x})\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\,\mathbf{y}\in\Omega\}.

Alternatively, we have the pointwise representation
\nabla_{\gamma}u\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\Omega\times\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
given by

\nabla_{\gamma}u(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})=[u(\mathbf{y})-u(\mathbf{x})]\newline
\sqrt{\gamma(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})}.

Let us also define the
nonlocal divergence
for vector functions
v\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\Omega\times\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
given by

(\dive_{\gamma}v)(\mathbf{x})\coloneqq\int\limits_{\Omega}[v(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})-v(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{x})]\sqrt{\gamma(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})}\dif y.

We define the
nonlocal diffusion
of

as the operator
\Delta_{\gamma}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
such that

\displaystyle\Delta_{\gamma}u(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle\coloneqq\frac{1}{2}\dive_{\gamma}\big(\nabla_{\gamma}u(\mathbf{x})\big)=\dfrac{1}{2}\int\limits_{\Omega}[\partial_{\mathbf{y}}u(\mathbf{x})-\partial_{\mathbf{x}}u(\mathbf{y})]\sqrt{\gamma(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})}\dif\mathbf{y}=\int\limits_{\Omega}[u(\mathbf{y})-u(\mathbf{x})]\gamma(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\dif\mathbf{y}.

Notice that, for the previous operators to be well defined, we require that at least
\gamma\in L^{\infty}(\Omega,\Omega)
and

Many properties from classical calculus extend to this nonlocal extension of the derivative. In particular, we have that: (a) the adjoint of the nonlocal gradient is the negative of the nonlocal divergence (i.e.,
\nabla_{\gamma}^{*}=-\dive_{\gamma}
), (b) a zero flux property (divergence theorem:

). See
[
66
, §12]
for detailed proofs.

2.3
Nonlocal denoising

Originally inspired by convolution filters, nonlocal methods predict the value of a pixel based on generalized neighborhoods of the same image that represent or approximate the local behavior of the pixel. In this class of methods, the nonlocal means (NLM) filter introduced by
[

has proven to be successful, yielding several variants

]
as the BM3D filter

We will use the notational shorthand

]
.

Inspired by the classical ROF model, and based on the maximum a posteriori probability estimate criterion
[
19
, §4.5.4]
, the nonlocal denoising problem can be posed as the minimization of an energy consisting of the combination of a regularizing term and a fidelity term
[
43
]
:

\argmin_{u\in V}\mathcal{E}(u;\lambda)\coloneqq\mu R(u)+\frac{\lambda}{2}\|u-f\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)},

(6)

where
V
is a suitable closed subspace of
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
, and
\mu,\lambda
are positive. The quotient
\nicefrac{{\lambda}}{{\mu}}
balances the fidelity term against the nonlocal regularizer. In principle, the regularizer weight can be omitted, yet it plays an essential rôle for numerical computations as the values of
R
can be large. Furthermore, the value of the fidelity weight determines how much of the noisy sample
f
is preserved when minimizing
\mathcal{E}
with respect to
u
. Thus, finding an optimal value of
\lambda
in
eq.

6
is crucial if we want to implement nonlocal denoising inside the scope of large–scale image recovery algorithms.

2.4
Parameter learning via bilevel optimization

Bilevel optimization has played an important rôle in image recovery and parameter calibration
[
30
,
17
,
31
,
29
]
. In the context of nonlocal denoising, this was first showcased in
[
36
]
for optimizing the fidelity weight
\lambda
and kernel selection.
If a clean and noise–less version of
f
, labeled
u_{\textsf{c}}
, is available, then an optimal fidelity weight can be trained by solving

\displaystyle\hskip 20.00003pt\min_{u\in V,\lambda\in\Lambda}J(u;\lambda)\coloneqq\frac{1}{2}\|u_{\textsf{c}}-u\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}

(7a)

subject to

\displaystyle u=\argmin_{v\in V}\mathcal{E}(v;\lambda)\in\{v\in V\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\,{-}\mu\mathcal{L}v+\lambda v=\lambda f\},

(7b)

\displaystyle\lambda\in\Lambda\coloneqq[\Lambda_{\min},\Lambda_{\max}].

(7c)

The linear equation in
eq.

7b
is just the Euler–Lagrange equation of
eq.

6
, where we have used
eq.

5
. A solution
u=\argmin\limits_{v\in V}\mathcal{E}(v;\lambda)
is the best reconstruction of the image for a given regularization level
\lambda
and a given regularization kernel embedded through
\mathcal{L}
. The choice of
\lambda
is then influenced by comparing
u
with the ground truth
u_{\textsf{c}}
.

It turns out that if
0<\Lambda_{\min}\leq\Lambda_{\max}
, then the solution set of the lower–level problem
eq.

7b
contains a single point. Furthermore, depending on the choice of
V
and
\gamma
, we can even extend
\Lambda
to include the zero fidelity case, which we do not cover here. For simplicity, let us consider
V=L^{2}(\Omega)
and kernels with support fully contained in
\Omega
, thus we also fix
\Lambda_{\min}>0
.

Theorem 2
.

For every
\lambda\in\Lambda
, there exists a unique solution to
eq.

7b
. Moreover, if
f
is bounded, it holds that
\min\limits_{\mathbf{x}\in\Omega}f(\mathbf{x})\leq u\leq\max\limits_{\mathbf{x}\in\Omega}f(\mathbf{x})
.

The result follows from a minimizing sequence argument and the maximum principle for the elliptic operator
-\mathcal{L}

[
66
, §12]
.
From here, we can replace
eq.

7b
with the constraint of the Euler–Lagrange equation, thus revealing that
eq.

7
is nothing else than a nonlocal optimal control problem. Using a minimizing sequence along the Bolzano–Weierstrass theorem for
\Lambda
, we obtain the following result:

Theorem 3
.

The bilevel optimization problem
eq.

7
admits a solution
(\lambda,u)\in\Lambda\times V
satisfying the optimality system:

\left\{\begin{aligned} -\mu\mathcal{L}u+\lambda u&=\lambda f&&\quad\emph{in}\Omega,\\
-\mu\mathcal{L}p+\lambda p&=u-u_{\mathsf{c}}&&\quad\emph{in}\Omega,\\
\proj_{\Lambda}\big(\lambda+c\langle u-f,p\rangle\big)&=\lambda&&\quad\forall c>0.\end{aligned}\right.

(8)

Moreover, if the kernel
\gamma=\gamma_{\theta}
is parameterized by a vector
\theta
in a convex and compact set
\Theta
, then it can be proven
[
36
]
that the extended problem

\min_{u\in V,\lambda\in\Lambda,\theta\in\Theta}J(u;\lambda,\theta)\coloneqq\frac{1}{2}\|u_{\textsf{c}}-u\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\hskip 20.00003pt\text{subject to}\hskip 20.00003ptu=\argmin_{v\in V}\mathcal{E}_{\theta}(v;\lambda).

(9)

also admits a solution which satisfies
eq.

8
with the additional constraint

\proj_{\Theta}\mathinner{\left(\theta-c\tfrac{\partial{}}{\partial{\theta}}\langle p,\mathcal{L}_{\theta}u\rangle\right)}=\theta
for all
c>0
.
Moreover, we have the gradient

\nabla_{\lambda,\theta}\,J(u;\lambda,\theta)=\begin{bmatrix}\langle f-u,p\rangle&\mu\dfrac{\partial{}}{\partial{\theta}}\langle p,\mathcal{L}_{\theta}u\rangle\end{bmatrix}^{\top}\hskip-3.00003pt.

For example, if
\theta
is a scalar inducing a kernel
\gamma_{\theta}
, as in the case of the shape parameter in the extended Gaussian ANOVA kernel
eq.

2
, then

\mu\dfrac{\partial{}}{\partial{\theta}}\langle p,-\mathcal{L}_{\theta}u\rangle=\mu\int\limits_{\Omega}p(\mathbf{x})\mathinner{\Biggl[u(\mathbf{x})\int\limits_{\Omega}\mathinner{\tfrac{\partial{}\gamma_{\theta}}{\partial{\theta}}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\dif\mathbf{y}-\int\limits_{\Omega}u(\mathbf{y})\mathinner{\tfrac{\partial{}\gamma_{\theta}}{\partial{\theta}}}\dif\mathbf{y}\Biggr]}\dif\mathbf{x}.

Remark 4
.

Note that our discussion has focused on single–channel images. Notwithstanding, this is not a limitation, as the framework can be readily adapted to multichannel signals through a mixing technique. For further details on such approaches, see
[
43
,
15
,
13
]
. Additionally, the review by
[

]
provides additional insights on colour mixtures.

2.5
The choice of weights

The rôle of
\gamma
, also known as the
nonlocal kernel
, is key to determining the behavior of the regularizing term. This function often incorporates information from the image domain and the features that are relevant in the image (e.g., textures, periodicity) and thus guides the optimization process.

Figure 1
:
Representation of a neighborhood of features for a point
\mathbf{x}\in\Omega
.

Inspired by neighborhood filters, one can define the kernel weights based on
affinity
functions. The basic affinity structure is of similarity between image features present already in the noisy sample
f\in L^{2}(\Omega)
. To every point
\mathbf{x}
, we can assign a
neighborhood of features

\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{x})
which, in a broad sense, can consist of a finite set of
windows
that can have different sizes and shapes to better adapt to the image; see
fig.

1
. Formally, we can write
\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{x})=\{\mathsf{W}_{\ell}(\mathbf{x})\subseteq\Omega\}_{\ell\in\sf{L}}
for some
\mathsf{L}\in\mathbb{N}
. Here, each index
\ell
can represent a region of interest in the image domain.
The map
\mathcal{N}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\Omega\to(2^{\Omega})^{\mathsf{L}}
does not necessarily need to define windows that contain every pixel as we can take advantage of the high degree of redundancy of any natural image. This is an extension of the framework of a neighborhood system, which has shown that as long as representative values are assigned to each pixel, then all pixels in the neighborhood can be used to predict the value of such a pixel
[
37
,
14
]
. This idea has inspired the design of patch–based priors; see for instance
[
71
,
3
,
105
]
. Since our particular prior is given in the form of the linear operator
-\mathcal{L}
, we focus next on describing several kernel–based filters.

A neighborhood of features allows us to define the following composite similarity kernel:

\gamma(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})=\begin{cases}\sum\limits_{\ell=1}^{\mathsf{L}}\alpha_{\ell}\exp\Big\{{-}\sigma^{-1}\mathrm{dist}\big(\mathsf{W}_{\ell}[f](\mathbf{x}),\mathsf{W}_{\ell}[f](\mathbf{y})\big)\Big\}^{2}&\text{if }(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\in\mathrm{supp}(\gamma),\\
0&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

(10)

where
\{\alpha_{\ell}\}_{\ell\in\llbracket 1,\mathsf{L}\rrbracket}
is a family of positive weights, the
shape
or
filtering
parameter
\sigma>0
controls how much information from the distance between
\mathsf{W}_{\ell}[f](\mathbf{x})
and
\mathsf{W}_{\ell}[f](\mathbf{y})
is retained, and
\mathsf{W}_{\ell}[f](\mathbf{x})=\{w[f](\mathbf{z})\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\,\mathbf{z}\in\mathsf{W}_{\ell}(\mathbf{x})\}
with
w
some vector field that may or not depend on
\ell
for
\ell\in\llbracket 1,\mathsf{L}\rrbracket
. The formalism
eq.

10
extends other classical kernels like the Sigma or Yaroslavsky & Lee kernel (
\mathsf{L}=1
,
\mathsf{W}_{1}(\mathbf{x})=\{\mathbf{x}\}
,
\alpha_{1}=1
,
w[f]=f
, and
\mathrm{dist}=|\cdot|
)
[
62
,
102
]
, the SUSAN kernel (
\mathsf{L}=1
,
\mathsf{W}_{1}(\mathbf{x})=\{\mathbf{x}\}
,
\alpha_{1}=1
,
w[f](\mathbf{x})=\begin{bmatrix}f(\mathbf{x})&(\nicefrac{{\sigma}}{{\rho}})\mathbf{x}\end{bmatrix}
, and
\mathrm{dist}=\|\cdot\|_{2}
)
[
84
]
, and the NLM kernel (
\mathsf{L}=1
,
\mathsf{W}_{1}(\mathbf{x})=B(\mathbf{x};\rho)
,
\alpha_{1}=1
,
w[f]=f
, and
\mathrm{dist}=\|\cdot\|_{L^{2}_{G_{a}}(\mathbb{R})}
)
[
11
,
12
]
. In this work, we are interested in the unnormalized extended Gaussian ANOVA kernel
[
69
]
which obtains a composite similarity from windows of fixed size. To be precise, fix
\mathsf{L}\geq 1
and for each
\ell\in\llbracket 1,\mathsf{L}\rrbracket
define
\alpha_{\ell}=\mathsf{L}^{-1}
, take
\mathsf{W}_{\ell}(\mathbf{x})
as a discrete subset of
\Omega
of size
d_{2,\ell}
, and define
w[f]=f
. As a result, for any
(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\in\Omega\times\Omega
, we have

\gamma(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})=\frac{1}{\mathsf{L}}\sum_{\ell=1}^{\mathsf{L}}\exp\Big\{{-}\sigma^{-2}\big\|\mathsf{W}_{\ell}[f](\mathbf{x})-\mathsf{W}_{\ell}[f](\mathbf{y})\big\|_{2}^{2}\Big\}.

(11)

In
[
69
]
,
eq.

11
was introduced to overcome the computational complexity of computing and storing neighborhood kernels over high–dimensional sets of features. This task can be achieved efficiently for windows of size
d_{2,\ell}\leq 3
using the NFFT, which we introduce in
section

3
. The core principle lies in transferring the cost of computing the distance between large–sized windows with local information to instead compute additional terms in the composite kernel with relevant information.

2.6
Discretization

Since we are interested in finding a solution that can display discontinuities, following the approach in
[
36
]
, we use a piece–wise constant finite element basis to approximate
u
in
L^{2}(\Omega)
. Specifically, consider the partition of the image domain
\Omega
in the natural pixel grid of
n_{1}\times n_{2}
single–pixel squares. For each square we associate a unit step function, the indicator of the pixel. Let us denote the induced finite element method (FEM) functional space by
V^{h}
. Here, collect
\mathbf{u}^{h}
and
\mathbf{f}^{h}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
as the discretizations of
u
and
f
in
V^{h}
, where
n\coloneqq n_{1}n_{2}
is the computational dimension. Let
\{\mathbf{x}_{i}^{h}\}_{i\in\llbracket 1,n\rrbracket}\subset\Omega
denote the elements (pixels) in the image domain. Moreover, denote
\gamma^{h}_{i,j}
as the FEM approximation at the pixels
\mathbf{x}_{i}^{h}
and
\mathbf{x}_{j}^{h}
, which we collect in the matrix
\Gamma^{h}=(\gamma_{i,j}^{h})_{(i,j)}
. Then the nonlocal product
-\mathcal{L}u
is discretized as

\big[\pi_{V^{h}}(-\mathcal{L}u)\big]_{i}=u_{i}\sum\limits_{j=1}^{n}\gamma^{h}_{i,j}-\sum\limits_{j=1}^{n}\gamma^{h}_{i,j}u_{j}

for each
i\in\llbracket 1,n\rrbracket
. Let us denote
\boldsymbol{\eta}^{h}\coloneqq\Gamma^{h}\mathbf{1}_{n}
, then the nonlocal transformation can be represented as
L^{h}\coloneqq\diag(\boldsymbol{\eta}^{h})-\Gamma^{h}
(notice the sign change). Here,
L^{h}
is nothing else than a graph Laplacian associated to a graph with weights given by
\Gamma^{h}
. Then, the lower–level optimality condition
eq.

7b
can be written as

A^{h}\mathbf{u}^{h}=\big(\lambda\mathsf{I}_{n}+\mu(\diag(\boldsymbol{\eta}^{h})-\Gamma^{h})\big)\mathbf{u}^{h}=\lambda\mathbf{f}^{h},

(12)

which we will refer to as the
nonlocal system
. Due to the nature of a graph Laplacian, the system is ill–conditioned for small values of
\lambda
. We will develop a method for effectively preconditioning
eq.

12
in
section

4
.

The FEM discretization of
\gamma
yields the discrete unnormalized extended Gaussian ANOVA kernel

\gamma^{h}_{i,j}=\frac{1}{\mathsf{L}}\sum_{\ell=1}^{\mathsf{L}}\exp\Big\{{-}\sigma^{-2}\big\|(\mathsf{W}_{\ell}[\mathbf{f}^{h}])_{i}-(\mathsf{W}_{\ell}[\mathbf{f}^{h}])_{j}\big\|_{2}^{2}\Big\}.

(13)

Often we will refer to
\Gamma^{h}
just as the ANOVA or the nonlocal kernel and each summand in
eq.

13
as a
subkernel
. At this point, notice that if
\gamma^{h}_{i,i}\neq 0
, then
(L^{h})_{i,i}=\sum\limits_{j=1}^{n}\gamma^{h}_{i,j}-\gamma_{i,i}=\sum\limits_{j\neq i}\gamma_{i,j}^{h}
, thus the diagonal of
\Gamma^{h}
does not contribute to the reconstruction process. As a result, from now on, we will assume that the diagonal of
\Gamma^{h}
only contains zero entries. Moreover, we will drop the super–index
h
keeping in mind that all the bold or capitalized quantities are discrete approximations in
V^{h}
. We delay the construction of the similarity windows
\mathsf{W}_{\ell}
to
section

5
.

3
NFFT–based Fast Gauss Transform

In this section, we provide a brief overview of the Nonequispaced Fast Fourier Transform and the fast summation method for the Gauss Transform. The NFFT is an extension of the classical FFT algorithm
[
34
,
32
,
86
,
57
]
to evaluate the trigonometric polynomial

f(\mathbf{x})\coloneqq\sum_{\mathbf{k}\in\mathbf{I}_{\mathbf{M}}}\widehat{f}_{\mathbf{k}}e^{-2\pi i\,\mathbf{k}^{\top}\mathbf{x}}

(14)

at the nonequispaced (i.e., arbitrary, often irregularly distributed) nodes
\{\mathbf{x}_{j}\}_{j\in\llbracket 0,N-1\rrbracket}\subset\mathbb{T}^{d}
from a finite number of Fourier coefficients
\{\widehat{f}_{\mathbf{k}}\}_{\mathbf{k}\in I_{M}}\subset\mathbb{C}
, where
\mathbf{M}\in 2\mathbb{N}^{d}
and
\mathbf{I_{M}}\coloneqq\prod\limits_{s=1}^{d}\{-\nicefrac{{m_{s}}}{{2}},\ldots,\nicefrac{{m_{s}}}{{2}}-1\}
. The Torus is defined as
\mathbb{T}\coloneqq\mathbb{R}\pmod{\mathbb{Z}}\simeq[-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}},\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}})
. The adjoint NFFT instead interchanges the rôles of the Fourier coefficients and node evaluation in
eq.

14
with a sign change in the argument of each exponential term.

The NFFT algorithm makes use of an oversampled FFT and the convolution theorem to yield an approximation of
eq.

14
with arithmetic complexity
\mathcal{O}(|\mathbf{I}_{\mathbf{M}}|\log|\mathbf{I}_{\mathbf{M}}|+N)

[
58
]
. Notice that the computation time of the NFFT increases considerably with
d
due to the exponential relationship
\min\mathbf{M}^{d}\leq|\mathbf{I}_{\mathbf{M}}|
. Moreover, the NFFT algorithm deliberately incorporates a systematic error into its calculations to maintain its advantageous computational efficiency. This extra error is manageable and, if required, can be minimized to the level of machine precision
[
53
]
. For more information on the algorithm and the controlling parameters, we refer to
[
53
,
52
,
57
]
.

The discrete Gauss Transform computes sums of the form

g(\mathbf{x})\coloneqq\sum_{j=0}^{N_{y}-1}\alpha_{j}e^{-\hat{\sigma}\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}_{j}\|^{2}_{2}},

(15)

for a given shape parameter
\hat{\sigma}\in\mathbb{C}
with
\Re(\hat{\sigma})>0
and the sets of coefficients
\{\alpha_{j}\}_{j\in\llbracket 1,N_{y}\rrbracket}
, source nodes
\{\mathbf{y}_{j}\}_{j\in\llbracket 1,N_{y}\rrbracket}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and target nodes
\{\mathbf{x}_{j}\}_{j\in\llbracket 1,N_{x}\rrbracket}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
. In
[
59
]
, the Fast Gauss Transform (FGT) was presented as a fast summation method to approximate
eq.

15
regardless of the distribution of the source and target nodes. The method is based on the application of an adjoint NFFT to
\alpha
, followed by a product with Fourier coefficients related to a truncation of a periodic approximation of the shape function
e^{-(\nicefrac{{r}}{{\hat{\sigma}}})^{2}}
, and a forward NFFT
[
72
,
73
]
. The complexity of the method is
\mathcal{O}(N_{x}+N_{y}+N_{d}\log N_{d})
, where
N_{d}
is the expansion degree of the Fourier coefficients in the NFFT. The value of
N_{d}
is only related to the expected accuracy of the method and is independent of
N_{x}
and
N_{y}
; thus, the FGT behaves as
\mathcal{O}(N_{x}+N_{y})

[
57
, §4]
. Notwithstanding, the associated cost of the constant in this complexity computation suffers from the curse of dimensionality. As a result, the FGT is an efficient algorithm for small–sized feature size
d
.

Recalling the ANOVA kernel
eq.

13
, the FGT can be used to efficiently approximate the action of
\Gamma
on a vector
\mathbf{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
by successive application of
\mathsf{L}
FGTs. Due to the curse of dimensionality, the authors in
[
69
]
recommend the use of
\mathsf{L}
windows of size less than four, where each window samples the feature dimension, thus overcoming the computational limitation of the expansion degree. As a result, the computational complexity of evaluating
\Gamma\mathbf{u}
is
\mathcal{O}(\mathsf{L}n)
. Error estimates on the approximation are provided in
[
73
,
59
,
57
]
, where parameter studies are performed.

4
Preconditioning under a new basis

Let us motivate this section by considering an ill–conditioned system of linear equations, to be solved by an iterative method, including Krylov subspace solvers such as the Conjugate Gradient (CG)
[
50
]
or Minimum Residual (MINRES)
[
70
]
methods. We emphasize that our approach may be embedded within any such solver which requires a fixed (low) number of matrix–vector applications. It is well-known that even if the condition number is large, these methods can perform well if the eigenvalues are clustered in few and small intervals
[
54
,
46
]
. The smaller and further away the clusters are from the origin and each other, the better
[
100
,
2
]
. Often, if we wish to obtain clustered eigenvalues, we require the use of a
preconditioner
. Here, we are interested in the case where a matrix has an eigenvalue too close to the origin, such that this eigenvalue prevents an ‘ideal’ clustering of the eigenvalues of the preconditioned system because (a) it drags down the remainder of the eigenvalues, or (b) it results in the smallest eigenvalue being lower for the preconditioned system than the original system. This occurs when preconditioning the nonlocal system
eq.

12
. In what follows, we propose a methodology to resolve this issue by isolating the smallest eigenvalue of a matrix without modifying the rest of the spectrum. We introduce and derive explicit transformations which can be easily implemented within any iterative framework, and show their computational complexity to be linear in time, hence extending their applicability for large and dense linear systems.

4.1
Change of basis

Let
(\lambda,\mathbf{v})
be an eigenpair of a Hermitian (or real symmetric) matrix
A\in\mathcal{M}_{n}(\mathbb{F})
1
1

1

Most of the following results can be generalized to the non–Hermitian case without loss of generality. However, we focus on the Hermitian case for the relationship between the spectrum of
A
and its condition number.
. By definition, we know that
\dim\mathbf{v}^{\perp}=n-1
; as a result, we can build a matrix
Q=(\mathbf{q}_{i})_{i\in\llbracket 1,n\rrbracket}
such that
\mathbf{q}_{1}=\mathbf{v}
and
\{\mathbf{q}_{i}\}_{i\in\llbracket 2,n\rrbracket}
is a basis in
\mathbf{v}^{\perp}
. Due to linear independence, the matrix
Q
is invertible. As a result, we can conjugate
A
by the change of basis given by
Q
and define
\mathsf{A}_{Q}\coloneqq Q^{-1}A\,Q
.

The matrix
\mathsf{A}_{Q}
has a particular structure that is useful to solve ill–conditioned systems. To see this, let us start by finding its first column through analyzing the product
\mathsf{A}_{Q}\mathbf{e}_{1}
. We know that
Q\mathbf{e}_{1}=\mathbf{v}
, and since this is an eigenvector of
A
, we have that
AQ\mathbf{e}_{1}=\lambda\mathbf{v}
. Now, since
\mathbf{v}=\mathbf{q}_{1}
, it holds that
Q^{-1}\mathbf{v}=\mathbf{e}_{1}
, thence
\mathsf{A}_{Q}\mathbf{e}_{1}=\lambda\mathbf{e}_{1}
. As a result, we can assert that
Q
is a change of basis that maps the eigenvector
\mathbf{v}
to
\mathbf{e}_{1}
. Notice that the first row of
\mathsf{A}_{Q}
is determined by the first row of
Q^{-1}
, labeled
\mathbf{r}_{1}
, in the following fashion:

\displaystyle(\mathsf{A}_{Q})_{1,1\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}n}=\mathbf{r}_{1}AQ

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}\langle\lambda\mathbf{v},\mathbf{r}_{1}^{\dagger}\rangle&\langle A\mathbf{q}_{2},\mathbf{r}_{1}^{\dagger}\rangle&\cdots&\langle A\mathbf{v}_{n},\mathbf{r}_{1}^{\dagger}\rangle\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\lambda&\langle A\mathbf{q}_{2},\mathbf{r}_{1}^{\dagger}\rangle&\cdots&\langle A\mathbf{v}_{n},\mathbf{r}_{1}^{\dagger}\rangle\end{bmatrix}.

(16)

Let us define
\mathbf{b}_{1}\coloneqq\big(\langle A\mathbf{q}_{i},\mathbf{r}_{1}^{\dagger}\rangle\big)_{i\in\llbracket 2,n\rrbracket}
and
B_{2}\coloneq(\mathsf{A}_{Q})_{2\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}n,2\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}n}
.
We can thus write
\mathsf{A}_{Q}
as the block matrix

\mathsf{A}_{Q}=\left[\begin{array}[]{@{} c ;{2pt/2pt} c c c c c c c}\lambda&\mathbf{b}_1\\[1.00006pt]
\cline{1-2}\cr[2pt/2pt]\\[-8.99994pt]
\mathbf{0}_{n-1}&B_2\end{array}\right].

(17)

Observe that since
\mathbf{e}_{1}
is an eigenvector of
\mathsf{A}_{Q}
, then
\Sigma(B_{2})=\Sigma(A)\setminus\{\lambda\}
when
\lambda
is simple. As a result, the block structure of
eq.

17
is useful when we try to solve systems of equations of the form
A\mathbf{u}=\mathbf{f}
that suffer from ill–conditioning (either by having a very small minimum eigenvalue or a very large spectral radius). To see this, notice that we can select
\lambda
accordingly and solve the equivalent system
\mathsf{A}_{Q}\mathbf{x}=Q^{-1}\mathbf{f}=\mathbf{g}
. We can easily recover a solution in the original basis by the transformation
\mathbf{u}=Q\mathbf{x}
. By construction, if we let
\mathbf{u}=(u_{1}\,\,\mathbf{u}_{2\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}n}^{\top})^{\top}
and
\mathbf{g}=(g_{1}\,\,\mathbf{g}_{2\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}n}^{\top})^{\top}
, then
\mathbf{u}_{2\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}n}=B_{2}^{-1}\mathbf{g}_{2\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}n}
but also
u_{1}=\lambda^{-1}[g_{1}-\mathbf{b}_{1}\mathbf{u}_{2\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}n}]
. The system
B_{2}\mathbf{u}_{2\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}n}=\mathbf{g}_{2\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}n}
can be solved using either a direct or iterative method.

It turns out that since
A
is Hermitian, then
\mathbf{b}_{1}
is always a vector of zeros. To see this, recall that
\langle\mathbf{v},\mathbf{r}_{1}^{\dagger}\rangle=1
, and the orthogonality of
\mathbf{v}^{\perp}
implies that we can only have
\mathbf{r}_{1}=\|\mathbf{v}\|^{-1}\mathbf{v}^{\dagger}
.
Now, for any index
i\in\llbracket 2,n\rrbracket
, it holds that

\langle A\mathbf{q}_{i},\mathbf{r}_{1}^{\dagger}\rangle=\langle\mathbf{q}_{i},A\mathbf{r}_{1}^{\dagger}\rangle=\frac{1}{\|\mathbf{v}\|}\langle\mathbf{q}_{i},A\mathbf{v}\rangle=\frac{\lambda}{\|\mathbf{v}\|}\langle\mathbf{q}_{i},\mathbf{v}\rangle=0.

A particular case of interest is when
Q
is unitary or orthogonal whenever
\mathbb{F}=\mathbb{R}
. Under these assumptions, we obtain that
\mathsf{A}_{Q}
and
B_{2}
are then Hermitian (symmetric).
The unitary case is a special case of
deflation
and is used for solving eigenvalue problems
[
45
, Lemma 7.1.2]
. In particular, if we find a matrix
Q
that is Hermitian (symmetric), we can induce a deflation under an orthogonalization process which simplifies due to the additional structure.
Moreover, the condition number of
B_{2}
is just

\kappa(B_{2})=\begin{cases}\left|\frac{\rho(A)}{a(A)}\right|&\text{if }\lambda=\min\big|\Sigma(A)\big|,\\[5.0pt]

4.1.2
Unitary representation

After determining
Q
and its inverse, it may be pertinent to establish a unitary (orthogonal) representation of this change of basis. Assuming the same hypotheses as those that led to
section

4.1.1
, we can orthonormalize the columns in
Q
to yield a unitary transformation, denoted as
U
.
This can be done exactly using the Gram–Schmidt process and the fact that
\mathbf{v}\perp Q_{\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}},2\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}n}
. To see this, let us build the family
\{\mathbf{w}_{i}\}_{i\in\llbracket 1,n\rrbracket}
with
\mathbf{w}_{1}=\mathbf{v}
, and let
\mathcal{I}_{\mathbf{v}}=\{i\in\llbracket 2,n\rrbracket\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\,\imath_{v_{i}=0}\}
; i.e.,
\mathcal{I}_{\mathbf{v}}
contains the indices of
\mathbf{v}
corresponding to zero entries. We know that in such cases
\mathbf{q}_{i}=-v_{1}\mathbf{e}_{i}
according to
eq.

18
, hence we define
\mathbf{w}_{i}=-v_{1}\mathbf{e}_{i}
for all
i\in\mathcal{I}_{\mathbf{v}}
and notice that in such cases
\mathbf{v}\perp\mathbf{w}_{i}
and
\|\mathbf{w}_{i}\|_{2}=|v_{1}|
.
Observe that
\mathbf{w}_{2}=\mathbf{q}_{2}
as well.
Now, for any other index
i\in\llbracket 3,n\rrbracket\setminus\mathcal{I}_{\mathbf{v}}
, we have that

\displaystyle\mathbf{w}_{i}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{q}_{i}-\hskip 10.00002pt\mathclap{\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j\in\llbracket 2,i-1\rrbracket\\
j\notin\mathcal{I}_{\mathbf{v}}\end{subarray}}}\hskip 10.00002pt\proj_{\mathbf{w}_{j}}(\mathbf{q}_{i})=\overline{v}_{i}\mathbf{e}_{1}-v_{1}\mathbf{e}_{i}-\,\,\,\mathclap{\sum_{j=2}^{i-1}}\hskip 10.00002pt\frac{\langle\mathbf{q}_{i},\mathbf{w}_{j}\rangle}{\langle\mathbf{w}_{j},\mathbf{w}_{j}\rangle}\mathbf{w}_{j}

\displaystyle=-v_{1}\mathbf{e}_{i}+\overline{v}_{i}\Bigg[\mathbf{e}_{1}-\sum_{j=2}^{i-1}\|\mathbf{w}_{j}\|^{-2}\overline{w}_{j,1}\mathbf{w}_{j}\Bigg].

From here we can conclude that the matrix
U=(\mathbf{w}_{i}/\|\mathbf{w}_{i}\|_{2})_{i\in\llbracket 1,n\rrbracket}
can be written as

U=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{v}&T_{\mathbf{v}}\end{bmatrix}\diag\big(\|\mathbf{v}\|_{2},\|\mathbf{q}\|_{2},\|\mathbf{w}_{3}\|_{2},\ldots,\|\mathbf{w}_{n}\|_{2}\big)^{-1},

where

is an upper triangular matrix with constant first lower diagonal equal to
-v_{1}
.

4.2
Application to graph Laplacians

In the previous section we constructed an exact change of basis that allowed us to express any Hermitian matrix as a block matrix based on a known eigenpair. In what follows, we will apply our methodology to analyze scalings of graph Laplacians and derive some spectral properties of these operators.

4.3
Applications for preconditioning graph Laplacians

In this section we propose two families of preconditioners for solving the system

These preconditioners will be extended to a second family of dense preconditioners. Examples will showcase the effects of each family on preconditioning the system

53
. We note that, alongside research on solvers for graph Laplacians (see e.g.,
[

,
68
,
42
,
36
]
), recent work on interior point methods for support vector machines, involving the second author
[

]
, addressed preconditioning matrices of the form

(including analogous diagonal entries), summed with diagonal matrices from logarithmic barrier terms with entries that may be very large or small. However, under our current framework in this work, the replacement of the diagonal with

4.3.1
Diagonal preconditioners

As
A
is a positive definite operator, the Jacobi preconditioner
\mathsf{P}_{\text{a}}=\diag(A)
is an out–of–the–box suitable choice. By our choice of
\Gamma
, we have that

\mathsf{P}_{\text{a}}=\lambda\mathsf{I}_{n}+\mu\diag(\boldsymbol{\eta})-\mu\diag(\Gamma)=\lambda\mathsf{I}_{n}+\mu\diag(\boldsymbol{\eta}).

We have the following result for the numerical range of the preconditioned system
\mathsf{P}_{\text{a}}^{-1}A
:

Lemma 10
.

The eigenvalues of the preconditioned system satisfy

\frac{\lambda}{\lambda+\mu\max\boldsymbol{\eta}}\leq\Sigma(\mathsf{P}_{\text{a}}^{-1}A)\leq\min\left\{2,\frac{n}{n-1}\frac{\rho(L)}{a(L)}\right\}.

(66)

Proof.

Let us start by noticing that
\mathsf{P}_{\text{a}}^{-1}A
can be written as the difference between an identity matrix and a stochastic matrix. Hence by
[
47
, Theorem 2.7]
, we have that
\Sigma(\mathsf{P}_{\text{a}}^{-1}A)\subset[0,2]
.

Now let us analyse the generalized Rayleigh quotient of
\mathsf{P}_{\text{a}}^{-1}A
. Here, let
\mathbf{x}\neq\mathbf{0}_{n}
and

\Rayleigh(A,\mathsf{P}_{\text{a}},\mathbf{x})=\frac{\langle\mathbf{x},A\mathbf{x}\rangle}{\langle\mathbf{x},\mathsf{P}_{\text{a}}\mathbf{x}\rangle}=\frac{\lambda\langle\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}\rangle+\mu\langle\mathbf{x},\boldsymbol{\eta}\circ\mathbf{x}\rangle-\mu\langle\mathbf{x},\Gamma\mathbf{x}\rangle}{\lambda\langle\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}\rangle+\mu\langle\mathbf{x},\boldsymbol{\eta}\circ\mathbf{x}\rangle}.

(67)

Now consider the inequality
\frac{a+b}{a+c}\leq\max\{1,\nicefrac{{b}}{{c}}\}
, which holds for all
a,b\geq 0
and
c>0
. Hence

\Rayleigh(A,\mathsf{P}_{\text{a}},\mathbf{x})\leq\max\left\{\frac{\langle\mathbf{x},\boldsymbol{\eta}\circ\mathbf{x}\rangle-\langle\mathbf{x},\Gamma\mathbf{x}\rangle}{\langle\mathbf{x},\boldsymbol{\eta}\circ\mathbf{x}\rangle},1\right\}.

Notice that
\langle\mathbf{x},\boldsymbol{\eta}\circ\mathbf{x}-\Gamma\mathbf{x}\rangle\leq\rho(L)\|\mathbf{x}\|^{2}
and
\langle\mathbf{x},\boldsymbol{\eta}\circ\mathbf{x}\rangle\geq\min\boldsymbol{\eta}\|\mathbf{x}\|^{2}
. Thus, we can further bound the above by

\Rayleigh(A,\mathsf{P}_{\text{a}},\mathbf{x})\leq\max\left\{\frac{\rho(L)}{\min\boldsymbol{\eta}},1\right\}.

From
lemma

8
, we may conclude that

\Rayleigh(A,\mathsf{P}_{\text{a}},\mathbf{x})\leq\min\left\{2,\frac{n}{n-1}\frac{\rho(L)}{a(L)}\right\}.

For the lower bound, by the positive definiteness of
L
, we have that

\Rayleigh(A,\mathsf{P}_{\text{a}},\mathbf{x})\geq\frac{\lambda\langle\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}\rangle}{\lambda\langle\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}\rangle+\mu\langle\mathbf{x},\boldsymbol{\eta}\circ\mathbf{x}\rangle}\geq\min_{i\in\llbracket 1,n\rrbracket}\frac{\lambda}{\lambda+\mu\eta_{i}}=\frac{\lambda}{\lambda+\mu\max\boldsymbol{\eta}}.

Remark 11
.

From
eq.

67
we also see, by the positivity of
\boldsymbol{\eta}
, that

\Rayleigh(A,\mathsf{P}_{\text{a}},\mathbf{x})\leq 1+\frac{\mu}{\lambda}\frac{\langle\mathbf{x},L\mathbf{x}\rangle}{\langle\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}\rangle}\leq 1+\frac{2\mu}{\lambda}\max\boldsymbol{\eta}.

This is nothing else than the upper bound from the condition number of
A
as in
eq.

54
. Also, notice that if
\lambda\gg 2\mu\max\boldsymbol{\eta}
, then the spectral radius of the preconditioned system is very close to
1
.
On the other hand, the lower bound on
eq.

66
tells us that the smallest eigenvalue of
\mathsf{P}_{\text{a}}^{-1}A
can be even smaller than the smallest eigenvalue of
A
. In particular, this becomes critical in the case
\lambda\ll\mu\max\boldsymbol{\eta}
, as often
\boldsymbol{\eta}\geq\mathbf{1}_{n}
, for which we can suspect that the smallest eigenvalue can be closer to zero with preconditioning, hence damping the performance of Krylov subspace solvers.

Now let us consider the preconditioner proposed in
[
36
]
for the nonlocal means kernel:

\mathsf{P}_{\text{b}}=\diag\bigg(\Big[\sum_{j}a_{i,j}^{2}\Big]^{1/2}\bigg)_{i\in\llbracket 1,n\rrbracket}.

Taking into account the definitions of
A
,
\Gamma
, and
\boldsymbol{\eta}
, we have that
a_{i,i}^{2}=(\mu\boldsymbol{\eta}+\lambda)_{i}^{2}
, while
a_{i,j}^{2}=\mu^{2}\gamma_{i,j}^{2}
for
i\neq j
(here, we employ the convention of overwriting
\gamma_{i,i}=0
).
Summing up either by rows or columns, we obtain the following:

(\mathsf{P}_{\text{b}})_{i,i}^{2}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}a_{i,j}^{2}=\mu^{2}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\gamma_{i,j}^{2}+(\mu\eta_{i}+\lambda)^{2}=\mu^{2}[(\Gamma\circ\Gamma)\mathbf{1}_{n}]_{i}+(\mathsf{P}_{\text{a}})_{i,i}^{2},

(68)

or alternatively

\mathsf{P}_{\text{b}}=\big[\mu^{2}\diag(\Gamma^{\circ 2}\,\mathbf{1}_{n})+(\mathsf{P}_{\text{a}})^{2}\big]^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\hskip-3.00003pt.

Lemma 12
.

The operators
\mathsf{P}_{\text{a}}
and
\mathsf{P}_{\text{b}}
are spectrally equivalent; specifically,
\mathsf{P}_{\text{a}}\preccurlyeq\mathsf{P}_{\text{b}}\preccurlyeq\sqrt{2}\mathsf{P}_{\text{a}}
.

Proof.

Due to the
\ell_{1}
–
\ell_{2}
norm equivalence, we have that

\Big(\sum_{j=1}^{n}\gamma_{i,j}^{2}\Big)^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}=\|\gamma_{i,(\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}})}\|_{2}\leq\|\gamma_{i,(\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}})}\|_{1}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\gamma_{i,j}=\eta_{i}.

As a result, we have that
\sum_{j=1}^{n}\gamma_{i,j}^{2}\leq\eta_{i}^{2}
, and by basic manipulation

\displaystyle(\mathsf{P}_{\text{b}})_{i,i}^{2}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}a_{i,j}^{2}\leq\mu^{2}\eta_{i}^{2}+(\mathsf{P}_{\text{a}})_{i,i}^{2}\leq 2(\mathsf{P}_{\text{a}})_{i,i}^{2}.

Taking the square root of both sides, we obtain
(\mathsf{P}_{\text{b}})_{i,i}\leq\sqrt{2}(\mathsf{P}_{\text{a}})_{i,i}.

Further, by the non–negativity of all the terms involved in
eq.

68
, we also have that
(\mathsf{P}_{\text{a}})_{i,i}\leq(\mathsf{P}_{\text{b}})_{i,i}
.

Lemma 13
.

The eigenvalues of the preconditioned matrix
\mathsf{P}_{\text{b}}^{-1}A
satisfy

2^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\lambda_{\min}(\mathsf{P}_{\text{a}}^{-1}A)\leq\Sigma(\mathsf{P}_{\text{b}}^{-1}A)\leq\lambda_{\max}(\mathsf{P}_{\text{a}}^{-1}A).

Proof.

The result follows directly from
lemma

12
. To see this, consider the Rayleigh quotient

\Rayleigh(A,\mathsf{P}_{\text{b}},\mathbf{x})=\frac{\langle\mathbf{x},A\mathbf{x}\rangle}{\langle\mathbf{x},\mathsf{P}_{\text{a}}\mathbf{x}\rangle}\frac{\langle\mathbf{x},\mathsf{P}_{\text{a}}\mathbf{x}\rangle}{\langle\mathbf{x},\mathsf{P}_{\text{b}}\mathbf{x}\rangle}.

The second quotient is bounded in
[2^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}},1]
; hence,

2^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\Rayleigh(A,\mathsf{P}_{\text{a}},\mathbf{x})\leq\Rayleigh(A,\mathsf{P}_{\text{b}},\mathbf{x})\leq\Rayleigh(A,\mathsf{P}_{\text{a}},\mathbf{x})

for all
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

Remark 14
(Computing
\Gamma^{\circ 2}
).

Note that if computing
\Gamma
is computationally inefficient, this will also be the case with
\Gamma^{\circ 2}
. However, we are interested in particular cases of
\Gamma
: (i) if the entries of
\Gamma
are moderated by a variance parameter
\sigma
as in
\gamma_{i,j}=\exp(-\sigma^{-2}r_{i,j})
, then
\gamma_{i,j}^{2}=\exp(-(\sigma/\sqrt{2})^{-2}r_{i,j})
, so computing
\Gamma^{\circ 2}\,\mathbf{1}_{n}
is as expensive as computing
\Gamma\mathbf{1}_{n}
with a different
\sigma
; (ii) if
\Gamma
is a composite operator of the form
\Gamma=\frac{1}{\mathsf{L}}\sum\limits_{\ell=1}^{\mathsf{L}}\Gamma_{\ell}
and
\Gamma_{\ell}
is as in case (i), then by Pascal’s simplex we obtain the expansion

\displaystyle\gamma_{i,j}^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\mathsf{L}^{2}}\sum_{\ell=1}^{\mathsf{L}}(\gamma_{\ell})_{i,j}^{2}+\frac{2}{\mathsf{L}^{2}}\sum_{\ell<\kappa}(\gamma_{\ell})_{i,j}(\gamma_{\kappa})_{i,j}

(69)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\mathsf{L}^{2}}\sum_{\ell=1}^{\mathsf{L}}\exp\big({-}(\sigma/\sqrt{2})^{-2}(r_{\ell})_{i,j}\big)+\frac{2}{\mathsf{L}^{2}}\sum_{\ell<\kappa}\exp\big({-}\sigma^{-2}(r_{\ell}+r_{\kappa})_{i,j}\big).

Out of the
\frac{1}{2}\mathsf{L}(\mathsf{L}+1)
terms, the
\frac{1}{2}\mathsf{L}(\mathsf{L}-1)
pairwise products can exceed the computational limitations of the NFFT. Hence, assembling
\mathsf{P}_{\text{b}}
would require more computational time than assembling
A
itself, making it an unpractical preconditioner.

As a result, let us redefine the preconditioner
\mathsf{P}_{\text{b}}
using just the first
\mathsf{L}
terms in
eq.

69
:

\mathsf{P}_{\text{b'}}=\bigg[\big(\nicefrac{{\mu}}{{\mathsf{L}}}\big)^{2}\sum_{\ell=1}^{\mathsf{L}}\diag(\Gamma_{\ell}^{\circ 2}\,\mathbf{1}_{n})+(\mathsf{P}_{\text{a}})^{2}\bigg]^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\hskip-3.00003pt.

(70)

Hence, we obtain that

(\mathsf{P}_{\text{a}})_{i,i}^{2}\leq(\mathsf{P}_{\text{b'}})_{i,i}^{2}\leq(\mathsf{P}_{\text{b}})_{i,i}^{2}\leq 2(\mathsf{P}_{\text{a}})_{i,i}^{2}.

Furthermore, the spectral results in
lemma

13
also apply to
\mathsf{P}_{\text{b'}}
. We will use
eq.

70
whenever we precondition systems based on the ANOVA kernel.

4.3.2
Dense preconditioners

As proven in
lemma

10
and extended by
lemma

13
, the preconditioners
\mathsf{P}_{\text{a}}
and
\mathsf{P}_{\text{b}}
introduced in the previous section can result in an eigenvalue that is smaller than
\lambda
when applied to
A
. We aim to mitigate this effect by applying the change of basis explored in
section

4.2
.

Let
X\in\{Q,U\}
be any of the change of basis matrices in
eqs.

55
and
4.2
. Recall that the matrix
\mathsf{A}_{X}
that has the form

\mathsf{A}_{X}=\left[\begin{array}[]{@{} c ;{2pt/2pt} c c c c c c c}\lambda&\mathbf{0}_{n-1}^\top\\[1.00006pt]
\cline{1-2}\cr[2pt/2pt]\\[-8.99994pt]
\mathbf{0}_{n-1}&\ast\end{array}\right]=X^{-1}A\,X.

(71)

In this sense, as discussed in
section

4.1
, we can solve the system
\mathsf{A}_{X}\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{g}
by splitting it so that
x_{1}=g_{1}/\lambda
and
(\mathsf{A}_{X})_{2\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}n,2\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}n}\mathbf{x}_{2\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}n}=\mathbf{g}_{2\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}n}
. For solving the latter system, we would benefit from the action of the preconditioner
\mathsf{P}_{\text{}}\in\{\mathsf{P}_{\text{a}},\mathsf{P}_{\text{b}}\}
. However, we need to express the preconditioner under the new basis; i.e., we introduce
\mathsf{D}_{X}\coloneqq X^{-1}\mathsf{P}_{\text{}}X
. As a result
\mathsf{D}_{X}^{-1}\mathsf{A}_{X}=X^{-1}\mathsf{P}_{\text{}}^{-1}A\,X
has the same range of eigenvalues as the preconditioned system in the original basis. Moreover, notice that
\mathsf{D}_{X}^{-1}\mathsf{A}_{X}
is dense as
\mathsf{D}_{X}
is dense, otherwise we would not be able to decouple the preconditioned system as for
eq.

71
.

Our interest turns instead towards the projection of the preconditioner on the second diagonal sub–block of
\mathsf{A}_{X}
. At this point, let us define the projection operator
\pi_{2}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathcal{M}_{n}(\mathbb{F})\to\mathcal{M}_{n-1}(\mathbb{F})
such that
\pi_{2}(A)=A_{2\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}n,2\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}n}
.
Now, consider the equivalent system

\underbrace{\left[\begin{array}[]{@{} c ;{2pt/2pt} c c c c c c c}\hbox{\multirowsetup$\,\mathbf{0}_{n}$}&\mathbf{0}_{n-1}^\top\\[1.00006pt]
\cline{2-2}\cr[2pt/2pt]\\[-8.99994pt]
&\pi_2 ( \mathsf{A}_{X} )\end{array}\right]}_{\eqqcolon\mathsf{S}_{X}}\left[\begin{array}[]{@{} c }0\\[1.00006pt]
\cline{1-1}\cr[2pt/2pt]\\[-8.99994pt]
\mathbf{x}_{2\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}n}\end{array}\right]=\left[\begin{array}[]{@{} c }0\\[1.00006pt]
\cline{1-1}\cr[2pt/2pt]\\[-8.99994pt]
\mathbf{g}_{2\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}n}\end{array}\right].

When we apply
\mathsf{D}_{X}^{-1}
to
\mathsf{S}_{X}
, we obtain a system of the form

\left[\begin{array}[]{@{} c ;{2pt/2pt} c c c c c c c}\hbox{\multirowsetup$\,\mathbf{0}_{n}$}&\begin{smallmatrix}\ast&\ast&\ast&\ast&\ast&\ast\end{smallmatrix\\[1.00006pt]
\cline{2-2}\cr[2pt/2pt]\\[-8.99994pt]
&\pi_2 ( \mathsf{D}_{X} ^{-1}) \pi_2( \mathsf{A}_{X} )\end{array}\right]\left[\begin{array}[]{@{} c }0\\[1.00006pt]
\cline{1-1}\cr[2pt/2pt]\\[-8.99994pt]
\mathbf{x}_{2\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}n}\end{array}\right]=\left[\begin{array}[]{@{} c }\ast\\[1.00006pt]
\cline{1-1}\cr[2pt/2pt]\\[-8.99994pt]
\pi_{2}(\mathsf{D}_{X}^{-1})\mathbf{g}_{2\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}n}\end{array}\right].}

(72)

However, it is not difficult to also see that
\pi_{2}(\mathsf{D}_{X}^{-1}\mathsf{A}_{X})=\pi_{2}(\mathsf{D}_{X}^{-1})\pi_{2}(\mathsf{A}_{X})
(this is a consequence of the block structure). Hence we define a preconditioner for
\mathsf{A}_{X}
by its inverse form:

\mathcal{P}_{X,\mathsf{P}_{\text{}}}^{-1}\coloneqq\left[\begin{array}[]{@{} c ;{2pt/2pt} c c c c c c c}1&\mathbf{0}_{n-1}^\top\\[1.00006pt]
\cline{1-2}\cr[2pt/2pt]\\[-8.99994pt]
\mathbf{0}_{n-1}&\pi_2( \mathsf{D}_{X} ^{-1} )\end{array}\right].

As
\mathcal{P}_{X,\mathsf{P}_{\text{}}}^{-1}
is a block matrix, its action on
\mathsf{A}_{X}
retains the block structure, and hence we can decouple
\mathcal{P}_{X,\mathsf{P}_{\text{}}}^{-1}\mathsf{A}_{X}
and focus just on the sub–block
\pi_{2}(\mathsf{D}_{X}^{-1}\mathsf{A}_{X})
.

The spectral properties of
\pi_{2}(\mathsf{D}_{X}^{-1}\mathsf{A}_{X})
can be studied from the higher–dimensional system
eq.

72
as its lower diagonal block contains all of the relevant eigen–information.

Lemma 15
.

Let
\mathsf{P}_{\text{}}
be any of the diagonal preconditioners introduced in
section

4.3.1
and
\mathsf{D}_{X}=X^{-1}\mathsf{P}_{\text{}}X
. Further, let
X\in\{Q,U\}
be any of the change of basis matrices in
eqs.

55
and
4.2
. Then
\pi_{2}(\mathsf{D}_{X}^{-1}\mathsf{A}_{X})
has a strictly positive smallest eigenvalue, and the following holds:

a(\mathsf{P}_{\text{}}^{-1}A)-\frac{\lambda}{\lambda+\mu\min\boldsymbol{\eta}}\leq\Sigma\big(\pi_{2}(\mathsf{D}_{X}^{-1}\mathsf{A}_{X})\big)\leq\rho(\mathsf{P}_{\text{}}^{-1}A).

Moreover, the upper bounds of
lemma

10
are satisfied.

Proof.

Start by noticing that
\mathsf{D}_{X}^{-1}\mathsf{S}_{X}
is equivalent to
\mathsf{D}_{X}^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\mathsf{S}_{X}\mathsf{D}_{X}^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}
.
Moreover, we have that
\mathsf{S}_{X}
is nothing more than a perturbation of
\mathsf{A}_{X}
:

\mathsf{S}_{X}=\mathsf{A}_{X}-\diag(\lambda,\mathbf{0}_{n-1})=X^{-1}\Big(A-\frac{\lambda}{n}\mathbf{1}_{n,n}\Big)X\eqqcolon X^{-1}TX.

The last equality comes from the fact that conjugation by
X
diagonalizes the matrix
(\nicefrac{{\lambda}}{{n}})\mathbf{1}_{n,n}
by construction of
Q
and
U
for the graph Laplacian.

At this point, notice that
T
is a deflation of
A
. To see this, observe that

T\mathbf{1}_{n}=\lambda\mathbf{1}_{n}-\lambda(\nicefrac{{n}}{{n}})\mathbf{1}_{n}=0\mathbf{1}_{n}
.
Further, for any other eigenpair of
A
,
(\beta,\mathbf{v})
, we have that
\mathbf{1}_{n}\perp\mathbf{v}
and thus

T\mathbf{v}=\beta\mathbf{v}
.
As a result, the rank–one perturbation of
A
by
(\nicefrac{{\lambda}}{{n}})\mathbf{1}_{n,n}
does not modify the eigenvalues of
A
that are different than
\lambda
:
\Sigma(\mathsf{S}_{X})=\{0\}\cup\Sigma(A)\setminus\{\lambda\}
.

From the fact that

\mathsf{D}_{X}^{-1}\mathsf{S}_{X}=X^{-1}\mathsf{P}_{\text{}}^{-1}TX
,
we can describe the spectrum of the preconditioned operator
\pi_{2}(\mathsf{D}_{X}^{-1}\mathsf{A}_{X})
by a sub–range of the Rayleigh quotient

\Rayleigh(T,\mathsf{P}_{\text{}},\mathbf{y}).

By the form of
eq.

72
, the smallest value this quotient can take is
0
, this value is attained by selecting
\mathbf{y}=\mathbf{1}_{n}
. We also have that

\displaystyle\Rayleigh(T,\mathsf{P}_{\text{}},\mathbf{y})

\displaystyle=\frac{\langle\mathbf{y},T\mathbf{y}\rangle}{\langle\mathbf{y},\mathsf{P}_{\text{}}\mathbf{y}\rangle}=\frac{\langle\mathbf{y},A\mathbf{y}\rangle}{\langle\mathbf{y},\mathsf{P}_{\text{}}\mathbf{y}\rangle}-\frac{\lambda}{n}\frac{\langle\mathbf{y},\mathbf{1}_{n,n}\mathbf{y}\rangle}{\langle\mathbf{y},\mathsf{P}_{\text{}}\mathbf{y}\rangle}\leq\frac{\langle\mathbf{y},A\mathbf{y}\rangle}{\langle\mathbf{y},\mathsf{P}_{\text{}}\mathbf{y}\rangle},

which is bounded by the spectral radius of
\mathsf{P}_{\text{}}^{-1}A
. Further, taking into account
lemma

12
, we get

\displaystyle\Rayleigh(T,\mathsf{P}_{\text{}},\mathbf{y})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\langle\mathbf{y},A\mathbf{y}\rangle}{\langle\mathbf{y},\mathsf{P}_{\text{}}\mathbf{y}\rangle}=\frac{\langle\mathbf{y},A\mathbf{y}\rangle}{\langle\mathbf{y},\mathsf{P}_{\text{a}}\mathbf{y}\rangle}\frac{\langle\mathbf{y},\mathsf{P}_{\text{a}}\mathbf{y}\rangle}{\langle\mathbf{y},\mathsf{P}_{\text{}}\mathbf{y}\rangle}\leq\frac{\langle\mathbf{y},A\mathbf{y}\rangle}{\langle\mathbf{y},\mathsf{P}_{\text{a}}\mathbf{y}\rangle}.

As a result, the upper bound
eq.

66
holds.

Now, the smallest eigenvalue of
\pi_{2}(\mathsf{D}_{X}^{-1}\mathsf{A}_{X})
is just the algebraic connectivity of
\mathsf{D}_{X}^{-1}\mathsf{S}_{X}
. Here, notice that

a(\mathsf{P}_{\text{}}^{-1}T)=a(\mathsf{P}_{\text{}}^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}T\mathsf{P}_{\text{}}^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}})=a\mathinner{\left(\mathsf{P}_{\text{}}^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}A\mathsf{P}_{\text{}}^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}-\frac{\lambda}{n}\mathsf{P}_{\text{}}^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\mathbf{1}_{n,n}\mathsf{P}_{\text{}}^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\right)},

and
a(\mathsf{P}_{\text{}}^{-1}A)=a(\mathsf{P}_{\text{}}^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}A\mathsf{P}_{\text{}}^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}})
. Now, by Weyl’s inequality
[
45
, Corollary 8.1.6]
, we have that

a(\mathsf{P}_{\text{}}^{-1}A)\leq a(\mathsf{P}_{\text{}}^{-1}T)+\frac{\lambda}{n}\rho\mathinner{\left(\mathsf{P}_{\text{}}^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\mathbf{1}_{n,n}\mathsf{P}_{\text{}}^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\right)}.

Thus, we obtain

a(\mathsf{P}_{\text{}}^{-1}T)\geq a(\mathsf{P}_{\text{}}^{-1}A)-\frac{\lambda}{n}\rho\mathinner{\left(\mathsf{P}_{\text{}}^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\mathbf{1}_{n,n}\mathsf{P}_{\text{}}^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\right)}=a(\mathsf{P}_{\text{}}^{-1}A)-\frac{\lambda}{n}\rho\mathinner{\left(\mathsf{P}_{\text{}}^{-1}\mathbf{1}_{n,n}\right)}.

At this point, let us define
\mathbf{u}\coloneqq\diag(\mathsf{P}_{\text{a}}^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}})
, and observe that from
lemma

12
:

\max_{\mathbf{y}}\frac{\langle\mathbf{y},\mathbf{1}_{n,n}\mathbf{y}\rangle}{\langle\mathbf{y},\mathsf{P}_{\text{a}}\mathbf{y}\rangle}\frac{\langle\mathbf{y},\mathsf{P}_{\text{a}}\mathbf{y}\rangle}{\langle\mathbf{y},\mathsf{P}_{\text{}}\mathbf{y}\rangle}\leq\max_{\mathbf{x}}\frac{\langle\mathbf{x},\mathsf{P}_{\text{a}}^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\mathbf{1}_{n,n}\mathsf{P}_{\text{a}}^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\mathbf{x}\rangle}{\langle\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}\rangle}=\max_{\mathbf{x}}\frac{\langle\mathbf{x},\mathbf{u}\mathbf{u}^{\top}\mathbf{x}\rangle}{\langle\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}\rangle}\leq\|\mathbf{u}\|_{2}^{2}.

Here
\mathbf{u}=(\lambda+\mu\boldsymbol{\eta})^{\circ-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}
and by the
\ell_{2}
–
\ell_{\infty}
norm equivalence, we have that

\|\mathbf{u}\|_{2}^{2}\leq n\|\mathbf{u}\|_{\infty}^{2}=n(\lambda+\mu\min\boldsymbol{\eta})^{-1}
. Going back to the algebraic connectivity of
\mathsf{P}_{\text{}}^{-1}T
, we obtain

a(\mathsf{P}_{\text{}}^{-1}T)\geq a(\mathsf{P}_{\text{}}^{-1}A)-\frac{\lambda}{\lambda+\mu\min\boldsymbol{\eta}}.

(73)

Remark 16
.

Notice that if
\lambda\ll\mu\min\boldsymbol{\eta}
, then the last fraction in
eq.

73
is negligible. Something similar happens as well when
n\gg 1
, as the term
(\nicefrac{{\lambda}}{{n}}\mathbf{1}_{n,n})
could stop being significant in floating point arithmetic.

4.3.3
Comparison of preconditioners

At this point, let us observe how the preconditioners analyzed in
sections

4.3.1
and
4.3.2
transform the eigenvalues of a particular instance of the ANOVA kernel. For this, we assembled a kernel using the image in
fig.

5(a)
, and computed the eigenvalues of the exact unnormalized extended Gaussian ANOVA kernel based on a scaled sample with
n=3\,630
and
\mathsf{L}=41
.

Effects of
\sigma
.

To build any given kernel, we need to select a real value of
\sigma>0
. This parameter plays a critical rôle in shaping the behavior of each kernel. It determines the smoothness and spread of the function
s(\sigma;r)\coloneqq\exp\{-(\nicefrac{{r}}{{\sigma}})^{2}\}
, which in turn influences the eigenvalues of the kernel. Observe that
s(\sigma;r)
is bounded with values in
[0,1]
. Moreover, for any nontrivial
r
it holds that

\lim_{\sigma\to\infty}s(\sigma;r)=\lim_{y\downarrow 0}e^{-y}=e^{0}=1\hskip 20.00003pt\text{and}\hskip 20.00003pt\lim_{\sigma\to 0}s(\sigma;r)=\lim_{Y\uparrow\infty}e^{-Y}=0.

As
\sigma
increases,
s
approaches
1
, indicating a smoother, broader interaction between features. Conversely, as
\sigma
approaches
0
,
s
trends towards
0
, reflecting sharper, more localized interactions. In the trivial case
r=0
, the value of
\sigma
does not have an effect on the interaction.

Since the off–diagonal entries of
\Gamma
are given by terms of the form
\frac{1}{{\mathsf{L}}}\sum\limits_{\ell=1}^{\mathsf{L}}s(\sigma;(r_{\ell})_{i,j})
for all
i\neq j
, we can conclude that
\sigma
is a parameter that interpolates between one of the matrices in the set
\{\mathbf{0}_{n,n},\mathbf{E}\}
and the matrix of ones
\mathbf{1}_{n,n}
; i.e., the map
\sigma\mapsto A
is an interpolator in the set of graph Laplacians. Here, the symmetric matrix
\mathbf{E}
has nonnegative entries in the range
[\mathsf{L}^{-1},1]
which only occur in the entries where at least one
(\gamma_{\ell})_{i,j}
is equal to one. From the upper end of the interpolation, the uniform bound
\rho(A)\leq\lambda+\mu n
holds for any
\sigma>0
. Let us label the resulting limit operators as
A_{\sigma\to\infty}\coloneqq\lambda\mathsf{I}_{n}+\mu(n\mathsf{I}_{n}-\mathbf{1}_{n,n})
,
A_{1,\sigma\to 0}\coloneqq\lambda\mathsf{I}_{n}
and
A_{2,\sigma\to 0}\coloneqq\lambda\mathsf{I}_{n}+\mu(\diag(\mathbf{E}\mathbf{1}_{n})-\mathbf{E})
. Notice that for the last limit case, it is possible for the matrix
\mathbf{E}
to be associated with a disconnected graph. In fact, any
\Gamma_{\ell}
within such a regime will either weight a complete graph or a disconnected graph; thus,
\mathbf{E}
represents a convex combination of weights associated with disconnected and complete graphs.

The smoothness of
s
suggests that the eigenvalues of
A
and the preconditioned operators will also vary smoothly with respect to
\sigma\in[10,1.4\times 10^{3}]
. Effectively, this is showcased in
fig.

. Here, four panels depict how different values of
\sigma
result in different spectra and condition numbers for
A
and its preconditioned variants. The values of
\sigma
were chosen such that the gap between
\rho(A)
and

was larger than 1. The eigenvalue curves were subsampled to improve the visualization. Furthermore, the eigenvalue maps of
\mathsf{P}_{\text{b}}^{-1}A
and
\pi_{2}(\mathsf{D}^{-1}_{b,X}\mathsf{A}_{X})
were not included as they behave in a very similar way as
\mathsf{P}_{\text{a}}^{-1}A
and

, presenting almost identical curves and ranges of values. All eigenvalues were computed to machine precision.

5
Numerical experiments

In this section we test and showcase the effectiveness of using NFFT for fast summations when solving systems of the form
eq.

53
and the performance of the preconditioners studied in the previous section.

The unnormalized extended Gaussian ANOVA kernel
\Gamma
has the form of a composite nonlocal filter, hence it is natural to test it in the context of image denoising. To assemble
\Gamma
and each subkernel
\Gamma_{\ell}
, we will consider a similar approach to
[
36
]
. Given a clean image
\mathbf{u}_{\textsf{c}}
with
n
pixels, we generate some additive noise
\mathbf{h}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}_{n},s^{2}\mathsf{I}_{n})
to obtain a noisy variant
\mathbf{f}\coloneqq\mathbf{u}_{\textsf{c}}+\mathbf{h}
. Then, a set of patches based on
\mathbf{f}
is formed by tracing closed square regions of radius
\rho
for each pixel. We pad with zeros as needed in the areas of pixels that are below a distance
\rho
to the border. This results in a set of features of size
n\times(2\rho+1)^{2}
.

Due to the limitations of the NFFT regarding the size of the feature space, we process the features into windows of size
n\times\hat{d}
, where
\hat{d}\in\{1,2,3\}
. For the generation of such windows of features, we follow the approach of
[
69
,
99
]
which relies on the Mutual Information Score (MIS) to rank and identify the most relevant features before separating them into
\mathsf{L}=\lceil(2\rho+1)^{2}/3\rceil
windows. Then we assemble
\Gamma
as an operator that weights the action of each subkernel
\Gamma_{\ell}
for
\ell\in\llbracket 1,\mathsf{L}\rrbracket
. If the available memory allows it, we also store
\Gamma
in its matrix form for comparison purposes.

The feature engineering step is crucial for reducing the computational complexity of kernel evaluation for large–scale instances while ensuring that the most informative components are retained. While the use of small–sized features allows us to use the NFFT, more windows lead to more kernels what leads to more large–scale kernel evaluations for solving dense linear systems; as a result, we ought not incorporate all possible feature interactions. Specifically, by computing MIS, we can take the noisy pixel values in
\mathbf{f}
as a target that acts as a proxy for the underlying clean image, and these are contrasted against the patches. Thus we obtain features that are statistically predictive of the observed data with a low computational cost. However, the MIS is a univariate measure that does not examine the impact of a combination of several features on predicting the target. In order to study the computational tradeoff of feature combination and selection to capture linear and nonlinear relationships,
[
98
]
provides a thorough study on alternative approaches for feature engineering. Moreover, the study of the patch distribution
[
71
]
could potentially be used in future work to develop alternative purpose–designed windows targeted for denoising and other imaging tasks.

A nonlocal image denoising task approximates
\mathbf{u}_{\textsf{c}}
with the solution of
(\lambda\mathsf{I}_{n}+\mu L)\mathbf{u}=\lambda\mathbf{f}
. The value of the regularizer
\lambda
determines how much of the information in
\mathbf{f}
is retained. The value of
\mu
works as a scaling parameter that weights the action of the ANOVA kernel and, in principle, can be omitted. However, in practice this parameter is useful to control the range of intensity values of the product
\mu L\mathbf{u}
and prevent cancellation errors. Notice that, if we fix
\mu
, then a different choice for
\lambda
will result in a different approximation of
\mathbf{u}_{\textsf{c}}
.

This section is structured as follows. In
section

5.1
, two image datasets are presented, which we use to set up and run our experiments.
Section

5.2
showcases the computational gain of using the NFFT for fast matrix–vector products. It is quickly seen that, as the dimension of the problem grows, explicitly computing and storing each subkernel becomes a computational burden that the NFFT easily overcomes. This is again exemplified in
section

5.3
, where the two versions of the operator are employed to solve a denoising problem using direct and iterative methods. In
section

5.4
the different preconditioning techniques developed in
section

4.3
are tested when using preconditioned CG to solve a denoising task. Finally,
section

5.5
solves a parameter learning problem for recovering a dataset of images using the preconditioned CG iteration and the NFFT fast summation method.

All the experiments were performed on a MacBook Pro 2020 M1 with 16 GB RAM. The code was written in Python 3.9 and relies mainly on
NumPy
1.23,
SciPy
1.11.1, and
FastAdj
0.2,

library
[

. The NFFT does not require the use of costly hardware or access to HPC systems, which facilitates its broad and accessible application.

5.4
Comparison of preconditioned iterative methods

For this set of experiments, we test the performance of CG for different preconditioner choices in combination with the NFFT fast summation scheme. The considered setup is as follows: for each problem size
n
, we solved the system
A\mathbf{u}=\lambda\mathbf{f}
using CG for different values of
\lambda
in decreasing order. In principle, as
n
increased and
\lambda
became smaller, we could expect each test to become more ill–conditioned than the last. To precondition CG, we considered two bases to work with, namely the original basis where
A
is defined and the conjugated basis under the unitary transformation
U
; i.e., where
\mathsf{A}_{U}
is decomposed into a block diagonal system. For the first basis, we considered the unpreconditioned case represented by the preconditioner
\mathsf{M}=\mathsf{I}_{n}
, the two diagonal preconditioners from
section

4.3.1
; i.e.,
\mathsf{M}\in\{\mathsf{P}_{\text{a}},\mathsf{P}_{\text{b}}\}
. For comparison with a different class of preconditioning strategies, specifically low–rank methods, we also tested a recently–developed strategy built from the inverse of the Nyström–based randomly pivoted Cholesky approximation
\widehat{B}\approx B

[
20
]
, which we denote
\mathsf{P}_{\text{Ch}}
. The approximation
\widehat{B}
allows us to use the Sherman–Morrison–Woodbury formula
[
45
, §2.1.4]
to approximate the inverse of
A
through
\mathsf{P}_{\text{Ch}}
; see
[
21
,
88
,
39
,
1
,
101
]
for examples using this technique. For the basis induced by
U
, we considered similar preconditioners as in the previous case; however, first we decoupled the system
\mathsf{A}_{U}\mathbf{x}=\lambda U^{\top}\mathbf{f}
into its diagonal subblocks and then applied CG to the largest subblock. The unpreconditioned method is labeled, for notational convenience, by
\mathsf{I}_{U}
, and the two dense preconditioners developed in
section

4.3.2
are labeled
\mathcal{P}_{U,\mathsf{a}}
and
\mathcal{P}_{U,\mathsf{b}}
, where the second subindex indicates which diagonal preconditioner was transformed with
U
. To sum up, the preconditioners used under the change of basis induced by
U
are given by
\mathsf{M}\in\{\mathsf{I}_{U},\mathcal{P}_{U,\mathsf{a}},\mathcal{P}_{U,\mathsf{b}}\}
. Notice that to find a solution in the original basis, the approximate solution
\mathbf{x}_{2\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}n}
has to be concatenated with the solution of the one–dimensional block
x_{1}
, and then
\mathbf{u}=U\mathbf{x}
transforms the solution back to the original basis.

\mathsf{P}_{\text{Ch}}

Although tests were conducted for additional values of
\lambda
, the presented results capture a clear trend that summarizes the behavior of CG and the preconditioners as
\lambda
becomes smaller and

grows.

Let us begin by analysing the rows of
table

3
. In terms of the dimension, it seems that, in a similar way as in
table

2
, the problem becomes more ill–conditioned as
n
grows, hence the number of iterations for all methods grows for any
\lambda
. We note that for small instances
(n<2\,000)
, all methods were able to run under the maximum number of iterations except for the very ill–conditioned case
\lambda=10^{-9}
, where only the preconditioners under the change of basis induced by
U
were able to produce a solution. Let us focus on the cases
\lambda\geq 10^{-6}
. Here we notice that as
n
grows, unpreconditioned CG in the original basis is the first method to fail in finding a solution. The behavior is closely followed by the unpreconditioned case in the basis induced by
U
and then by the two diagonal preconditioners. Now let us consider the performance of the preconditioners by carrying out a column–wise comparison. First, notice that the two diagonal preconditioners and their dense counterparts always took the same number of iterations. This behavior was expected from the equivalence results of
lemmas

12
and
15
, suggesting that the Jacobi preconditioner, and its dense variant, is a preconditioner–of–choice due to its simplicity in contrast to the
\ell_{2}
choice
eq.

70
. Now focus on the range
\lambda\leq 10^{-3}
. Here we observe that the number of CG iterations in the original basis always exceeds the number of iterations for the basis induced by
U
. In particular, we can see that the case
\mathsf{M}=\mathsf{I}_{U}
already acts as a preconditioned method as the decoupling removes the ill–conditioned diagonal block given by
\lambda
, while the second diagonal block is better conditioned and controlled only by the spectral radius and the algebraic connectivity of
B
shifted by
\lambda
. It is interesting to notice that the number of preconditioned iterations does not change for the decoupled system as
\lambda
becomes smaller. This behavior is explained by the quality of the approximation
\nicefrac{{[\rho(B)+\lambda]}}{{[a(B)+\lambda]}}\approx\nicefrac{{\rho(B)}}{{a(B)}}
. In other words, if
\lambda
is small enough in terms of the order of magnitude of
a(B)
, then the decoupled method will have roughly the same condition number regardless of how small
\lambda
can be. The opposite behavior occurs when
\lambda
lies close to
a(B)
or has a larger value. This can be seen for
n<20\,000
and
\lambda=1
, where we had that
a(B)<\lambda
. Here, the number of iterations of the diagonal preconditioners and their dense variants was identical, and the unpreconditioned case under the change of basis induced by
U
no longer affected the eigenvalues, yielding the only case where CG with
\mathsf{M}=\mathsf{I}_{U}
was outperformed by CG with
\mathsf{M}=\mathsf{I}_{n}
. However, notice that as
n
grew, it held that
\lambda\lesssim a(B)
, and the problem became more ill–conditioned, then once more the dense preconditioners were able to take over and outperform the diagonal preconditioners.

\rho(B)
, as the system will essentially behave like a scaling of the identity.

\mathsf{P}_{\text{Ch}}
due to its poor performance. For
\lambda=10^{-9}
and
\lambda=10^{-6}
,
\mathsf{P}_{\text{Ch}}
failed to produce an error–acceptable solution in all cases. For
\lambda=10^{-3}
, CG consistently required more iterations under this choice of preconditioner than the unpreconditioned strategy
\mathsf{M}=\mathsf{I}_{n}
, and similarly for
\lambda=1

\mathsf{P}_{\text{Ch}}
is prohibitive: its usage in CG succeeded in only 13 out of 56 attempts. In fact, even the preconditioners with the richest sampled data, accumulating a total count of 150 matrix–vector evaluations, were not able to compete against the other preconditioners. The time for building
\mathsf{P}_{\text{Ch}}
ranged from around
7

3

\mathsf{P}_{\text{Ch}}
is not an optimal choice of preconditioner for this class of problems: we believe this is due to the full–rank and relatively well–conditioned nature of this problem structure better captured with diagonal information than a low–rank approximation.

5.5
Parameter learning application

As a proof of concept, here we are interested in the solving the inverse problem of determining
\lambda
from a set of clean and noisy images
\big\{(\mathbf{u}_{t,\textsf{c}},\mathbf{f}_{t})\big\}_{t\in\llbracket 1,\mathsf{T}\rrbracket}
. Following the approach of
[
36
]
, we aim to solve the bilevel problem, which we state in fully discretized form, given by

\displaystyle\hskip 20.00003pt\min J(\mathbf{u};\lambda)\coloneqq\frac{1}{\mathsf{T}}\sum\limits_{t=1}^{\mathsf{T}}\|\mathbf{u}_{t,\textsf{c}}-\mathbf{u}_{t}\|_{2}^{2}

(74a)

subject to

\displaystyle\mathbf{u}_{t}=\argmin_{\mathbf{v}}\frac{\mu}{2}\langle\mathbf{v},L_{t}\mathbf{v}\rangle+\frac{\lambda}{2}\|\mathbf{v}-\mathbf{f}_{t}\|_{2}^{2}\hskip 20.00003pt\forall t\in\llbracket 1,\mathsf{T}\rrbracket,

(74b)

\displaystyle\lambda\in\Lambda\coloneqq[\Lambda_{\min},\Lambda_{\max}].

(74c)

Here, for any index
t\in\llbracket 1,\mathsf{T}\rrbracket
, the Laplacian operator
L_{t}
is associated with the ANOVA kernel
\Gamma_{t}
built using features of the noisy variant
\mathbf{f}_{t}
, which in turn was constructed using additive noise with high variance. The bounds of
\Lambda
are positive to always ensure that any feasible
\mathbf{u}_{t}
resembles
\mathbf{f}_{t}
. For more details on the analysis of problems like
eq.

74
, we refer the reader to
[
36
]
. In particular, we know that the problem has a unique solution for any
\lambda\in\Lambda
, and that we can optimize the alternative reduced function
j(\lambda)\coloneqq J\big(\mathbf{u}(\lambda);\lambda\big)
. Moreover, the solution of the lower–level problem is completely characterized by the family of systems
(\lambda\mathsf{I}_{n}+\mu L_{t})\mathbf{u}_{t}=\lambda\mathbf{f}_{t}
for all
t\in\llbracket 1,\mathsf{T}\rrbracket
.

Given a solution of
eq.

74
, which we label
\lambda_{\mathsf{T}}
, we validate the parameter by solving the lower–level problem
eq.

74b
adapted to a new set of noisy samples from the validation set
\big\{(\mathbf{u}_{v,\textsf{c}},\mathbf{f}_{v})\big\}_{v\in\llbracket 1,\mathsf{V}\rrbracket}
. To quantify the effectiveness of a reconstruction, we use the structural similarity index (SSIM), which measures the similarity of the recovered image against its corresponding clean version.

The training set is composed of clean images corresponding to their noisy variants in
fig.

7(a)
. Similarly, the clean images corresponding to their noisy variants in
fig.

8(a)
form the validation set. Overall, the datasets contained
\mathsf{T}=15
and
\mathsf{V}=11
images of different sizes. The problem sizes range from
6\,794
to
10\,560
for the training set and from
7\,566
to
11\,160
for the validation set, respectively.

To solve the bilevel problem
eq.

74
, we used the Brent method which is developed to minimize a scalar function of a scalar variable efficiently
[
8
, §5]
. Moreover, we parallelized the subkernel evaluation to speed up the overall kernel fast summation. By distributing the work of each subkernel when reconstructing a particular image, we obtained an efficient way to evaluate the composite objective functional. We fixed
\mathsf{L}=41
,
\sigma=40
,
\mu=10^{-2}
, and
\Lambda=[10^{-9},10]
. The value of the upper bound on
\lambda
was selected to test the preconditioner for the ill–conditioned regime. By the nature of the training data, we would already need 335
GB
in memory just to store all the kernels used for the evaluation of the lower–level systems. For solving the lower–level systems, we used preconditioned CG limited to 25 iterations and a relative tolerance of
10^{-10}
. In particular, we employed the dense preconditioner
\mathcal{P}_{U,\mathsf{a}}
tailored to each image. Notice that the cost of assembling this preconditioner is absorbed by the computation of
\boldsymbol{\eta}
.

The optimization method was able to find a solution
\lambda_{\mathsf{T}}\approx 7.48
in 26 iterations with a tolerance of
10^{-10}
. The average number of CG iterations per step was
4
, highlighting the advantage of the our preconditioning scheme as the evaluation of the lower level problem was almost automatic. The results of the training are depicted in
fig.

7(b)
, where the average SSIM increased from 0.5221 to 0.5740. In general, we can observe that the method was able to smoothen the images and remove noise. However, in some cases the noise removal was not aggressive. This can be explained either by the choice of parameters and the clear limitations of using a one–parameter–fits–all approach. Notwithstanding, the method was able to find a solution in less than ten minutes that could later be postprocessed to remove the remaining artifacts. The method did not struggle with recovering textures and patterns and only slightly lowered the contrast of the output images. The validation test, depicted in
fig.

8(b)
, also showed an increase of the average SSIM from 0.5214 to 0.5576 while also featuring the preservation of some noise. Overall, this exercise suggests that the method can be used as the backbone of more specialized imaging tasks by allowing the computation and efficient solution of otherwise dense and ill–conditioned systems.

At this point, it is reasonable to question whether the reconstruction can be improved by jointly optimizing the regularization parameter
\lambda
and the shape parameter
\sigma
in
eq.

74
. By defining
\theta\coloneqq\sigma^{-2}
, we adapt the bilevel training setup from
eq.

9
to guarantee the existence of solutions for the bivariate case. We employed L–BFGS–B
[
16
,
104
]
with a two–point finite difference estimation of the Jacobian to avoid the additional computational cost of evaluating and storing the derivative kernel. The optimization was performed with fixed parameters:
\mathsf{L}=41
,
\mu=10^{-2}
,
\Lambda=[10^{-9},255]
, and
\Theta=[10^{-10},15]
. We also set a maximum of 50 iterations, a function discrepancy tolerance of
10^{-15}
, and a limited memory size of
5
. Under this setup, the algorithm converged to a minimizing pair
(\lambda_{\mathsf{T}},\theta_{\mathsf{T}})\approx(15.87,2.86\times 10^{-4})
in nine iterations, taking approximately
38
minutes. Once more, the average number of CG iterations was
4
. Notice that
\theta_{\mathsf{T}}^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\approx 59
, which does not differ much from the selection of
\sigma
in the previous experiment. The objective function achieved a reduction of
1.84\%
, while the mean SSIM increased to
0.5758
, although we note that this is not the quantity that the problem formulation is minimizing. For the validation set, the reduction was
4.15\%
, though the average SSIM remained unchanged compared to the one–dimensional problem in
eq.

74
. Overall, while the joint optimization process led to marginal improvements in reconstruction quality, it quadrupled the training time. This suggests that the additional computational effort may not be justified by the gain in the reconstruction performance, depending on the problem being examined, but it highlights the feasibility of our approach within such settings.

6
Conclusions

We have introduced a new NFFT–based framework for tackling bilevel optimization problems arising from image denoising applications. We utilized an ANOVA kernel, which may be readily applied in a matrix–free way within Krylov subspace solvers. To accelerate the solution algorithm for linear systems, we employed diagonal approximations coupled with a bespoke change of basis routine. Theoretical and numerical results underlined the potency of our methodology on a wide range of denoising and parameter learning models. Future work will involve applying similar frameworks to other classes of imaging problems, and investigating alternative preconditioners for problems where the eigenvalue distributions suggest alternative strategies to diagonal preconditioning.
