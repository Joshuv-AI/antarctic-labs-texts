On the Optimization and Generalization of Multi-head Attention
Puneesh Deora puneeshdeora@ece.ubc.ca, Rouzbeh Ghaderi rghaderi@ece.ubc.ca, Hossein Taheri hossein@ucsb.edu, Christos Thrampoulidis cthrampo@ece.ubc.ca

Abstract
The training and generalization dynamics of the Transformer’s core mechanism, namely the Attention mechanism, remain under-explored. Besides, existing analyses primarily focus on single-head attention. Inspired by the demonstrated benefits of overparameterization when training fully-connected networks, we investigate the potential optimization and generalization advantages of using multiple attention heads. Towards this goal, we derive convergence and generalization guarantees for gradient-descent training of a single-layer multi-head self-attention model, under a suitable realizability condition on the data. We then establish primitive conditions on the initialization that ensure realizability holds. Finally, we demonstrate that these conditions are satisfied for a simple tokenized-mixture model. We expect the analysis can be extended to various data-model and architecture variations.

Introduction

Transformers have emerged as a promising paradigm in deep learning, primarily attributable to their distinctive self-attention mechanism.
Motivated by the model’s state-of-the-art performance in natural language processing
(
Devlin et al. 2019
;
Brown et al. 2020
;
Raffel et al. 2020
)
and computer vision
(
Dosovitskiy et al. 2021
;
Radford et al. 2021
;
Touvron et al. 2021
)
,
the theoretical study of the attention mechanism has seen a notable surge in interest recently.
Numerous studies have already explored the expressivity of Attention, e.g.
(
Baldi & Vershynin 2022
;
Dong et al. 2021
;
Yun et al. 2020a
;
Yun et al. 2020b
;
Sanford et al. 2023
;
Bietti et al. 2023
)
, and initial findings regarding memory capacity have been very recently studied in
(
Baldi & Vershynin 2022
;
Dong et al. 2021
;
Yun et al. 2020a
;
Yun et al. 2020b
;
Mahdavi et al. 2023
)
. In an attempt to comprehend optimization aspects of training attention models,
Sahiner et al. 2022
;
Ergen et al. 2022
have investigated convex-relaxations, while
Tarzanagh et al. 2023a
investigates the model’s implicit bias.
Additionally,
Edelman et al. 2021
have presented capacity and Rademacher complexity-based generalization bounds for Self-Attention.
However, the exploration of the
finite-time
optimization and generalization dynamics of gradient-descent (GD) for training attention models largely remains an open question.

Recent contributions in this direction, which serve as motivation for our work, include the studies by
Jelassi et al. 2022
;
Li et al. 2023a
;
Oymak et al. 2023
. These works concentrate on single-layer attention models with a
single attention head
. Furthermore, despite necessary simplifying assumptions made for the data, the analyses are rather intricate and appear highly specialized on the individual attention and data model. These direct and highly specialized analyses present certain challenges. First, it remains uncertain whether they can be encompassed within a broader framework that can potentially be extended to more complex attention architectures and diverse data models. Second, they appear disconnected from existing frameworks that have been flourishing in recent years for conventional fully-connected and convolutional neural networks e.g.,
(
Jacot et al. 2018
;
Ji & Telgarsky 2020
;
Richards & Rabbat 2021
;
Liu et al. 2020
;
Taheri & Thrampoulidis 2023
)
. Consequently, it is also unclear how the introduction of attention alters the analysis landscape.

In this work, we study the optimization and generalization properties of multi-head attention mechanism trained by gradient methods.
Our approach
specifically leverages the use of
multiple attention heads
. Despite the operational differences between attention heads in an attention model and hidden nodes in an MLP, we demonstrate, from an analysis perspective, that this parallelism enables the exploitation of frameworks developed for the latter to study the former.
Particularly for the generalization analysis, we leverage recent advancements in the application of the algorithmic-stability framework to overparameterized MLPs
(
Richards & Kuzborskij 2021
;
Taheri & Thrampoulidis 2023
)
.

Contributions.

We study training and generalization of gradient descent optimization for a multi-head attention (MHA) layer with
H
heads in a binary classification setting. For this setting, detailed in Section
2
, we analyze training with logistic loss both the attention weights (parameterizing the softmax logits), as well as, the linear decoder that turns output tokens to label prediction.

In Section
3
, we characterize key properties of the empirical loss
\widehat{L}
, specifically establishing that it is self-bounded and satisfies a key self-bounded weak-convexity property, i.e.
\lambda_{\min}(\nabla^{2}\widehat{L}(\bm{\theta}))\gtrsim-\frac{\kappa}{\sqrt{H}}\widehat{L}(\bm{\theta})
for a parameter
\kappa
that depends only mildly on the parameter vector
\bm{\theta}
. Establishing these properties (and also quantifying
\kappa
) involves carefully computing and bounding the gradient and Hessian of the MHA layer, calculations that can be useful beyond the context of our paper.

In Sections
4.1
-
4.2
, we present our training and generalization bounds in their most general form. The bounds are given in terms of the empirical loss
\widehat{L}(\bm{\theta})
and the distance
\|\bm{\theta}-\bm{\theta}_{0}\|
to initialization
\bm{\theta}_{0}
of an appropriately chosen target vector
\bm{\theta}
. The distance to initialization also controls the minimum number of heads
H\gtrsim\|\bm{\theta}-\bm{\theta}_{0}\|^{6}
required for the bounds to hold. The choice of an appropriate parameter
\bm{\theta}
that makes the bounds tight is generically specific to the data setting and the chosen initialization. To guide such a choice, in Section
4.3
, we formalize primitive and straightforward-to-check conditions on the initialization
\bm{\theta}_{0}
that ensure it is possible to find an appropriate
\bm{\theta}
. In short, provided the model output at initialization is logarithmic on the train-set size
n
and the data are separable with respect to the neural-tangent kernel (NTK) features of the MHA model with constant margin
\gamma
, then Corollary
2
shows that with step-size
\eta=\widetilde{O}(1)
and
\Theta(n)
gradient descent steps, the train loss and generalization gap is bounded by
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(1/n)
provided only a polylogarithmic number of heads
H=\Omega(\log^{6}(n))
. We remark that the aforementioned NTK separability assumption, although related to, differs from the standard NTK analysis. Besides, while this assumption is sufficient to apply our general bounds, it is not a necessary condition.

In Section
5
, we investigate a tokenized-mixture data model with label-(ir)relevant tokens. We show that after one randomized gradient step from zero initialization, the NTK features of the MHA model separate the data with margin
\gamma_{\star}
. Thus, applying our general analysis from Section
4.1
, we establish training and generalization bounds as described above, for a logarithmic number of heads. Towards assessing the optimality of these bounds, we demonstrate that MHA is expressive enough to achieve margin
\gamma_{\text{attn}}
that is superior to
\gamma_{\star}
. The mechanism to reach
\gamma_{\text{attn}}
involves selecting key-query weights of sufficiently large norm, which saturates the softmax nonlinearity by suppressing label-irrelevant tokens. We identify the large-norm requirement as a potential bottleneck in selecting those weights as target parameters in our theory framework and discuss open questions regarding extending the analytical framework into this specific regime.

The remaining parts are organised as follows. Proof sketches of our main training/generalization bounds are given in Section
6
. The paper concludes in Section
7
with remarks on our findings’ implications and open questions. Detailed proofs are in the appendix, where we also present synthetic numerical experiments.

Related work.
We give a brief overview of the most relevant works on understanding optimization/generalization of self-Attention or its variants. Please see Section
H
for more detailed exposition.
Oymak et al. 2023
diverges from traditional self-Attention by focusing on a variant called prompt-Attention, aiming to gain understanding of prompt-tuning.
Jelassi et al. 2022
shed light on how ViTs learn spatially localized patterns using gradient-based methods.
Li et al. 2023a
provides sample complexity bounds for achieving zero generalization error on training three-layer ViTs for classification tasks for a similar tokenized mixture data model as ours. Contemporaneous work
Tian et al. 2023
presents SGD-dynamics of single-layer attention for next-token prediction by re-parameterizing the original problem in terms of the softmax and classification logit matrices, while
Tarzanagh et al. 2023b
;
Tarzanagh et al. 2023a
study the implicit bias of training the softmax weights
\bm{W}
with a fixed decoder
\bm{U}
. All these works focus on a single attention head; instead, we leverage the use of multiple heads to establish connections to the literature on GD training of overparameterized MLPs. Conceptually,
Hron et al. 2020
drew similar connections, linking multi-head attention to a Gaussian process in the limit as the number of heads approaches infinity.
In contrast, we study the more practical regime of finite heads
and obtain
finite-time
optimization and generalization bounds.

Among the extensive studies on training/generalization of overparameterized MLPs, our work closely aligns with
Nitanda et al. 2019
;
Ji & Telgarsky 2020
;
Cao & Gu 2019
;
Chen et al. 2020
;
Telgarsky 2022
;
Taheri & Thrampoulidis 2023
focusing on classification with logistic loss. Conceptually, our findings extend this research to attention models. The use of algorithmic-stability tools towards order-optimal generalization bounds for overparameterized MLPs has been exploited recently by
Richards & Kuzborskij 2021
;
Richards & Rabbat 2021
;
Taheri & Thrampoulidis 2023
;
Lei et al. 2022
.
To adapt these tools to the MHA layer, we critically utilize the smoothness of the softmax function and derive bounds on the growth of the model’s gradient/Hessian, which establish a self-bounded weak convexity property for the empirical risk (see Corollary
1
). Our approach also involves training both the classifier and attention weights, necessitating several technical adjustments detailed in Section
6
and Appendix
B.1
.

Multi-head Self-attention.
Our focus is on the multi-head attention (MHA) model with
H
heads:

\sum_{h\in[H]}\text{ATTN}({\bm{X}};{\bm{W}_{Q}}_{h},{\bm{W}_{K}}_{h},{\bm{W}_{V}}_{h}){\bm{W}_{O}}_{h},

for output matrices
{\bm{W}_{O}}_{h}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{v}\times d}
.
Absorbing
{\bm{W}_{V}}_{h}{\bm{W}_{O}}_{h}
into a projection layer (similar to the single-head attention) and parameterizing
\bm{W}_{h}:={\bm{W}_{Q}}_{h}{\bm{W}_{K}}_{h}^{\top}
we arrive at the following MHA model:

\displaystyle\widetilde{\Phi}({\bm{X}};\widetilde{\bm{W}},\widetilde{\bm{U}}):=\frac{1}{\sqrt{H}}\operatorname{\sum}_{h\in[H]}\Phi({\bm{X}};\bm{W}_{h},\bm{U}_{h})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{H}}\operatorname{\sum}_{h\in[H]}\langle\bm{U}_{h},\bm{\varphi}({\bm{X}}\bm{W}_{h}{\bm{X}}^{\top}){\bm{X}}\rangle\,,

(2)

parameterized by
\widetilde{\bm{W}}:=\operatorname{concat}\big(\{\bm{W}_{h}\}_{h\in[H]}\big)
and
\widetilde{\bm{U}}:=\operatorname{concat}\big(\{\bm{U}_{h}\}_{h\in[H]}\big).

The
1/\sqrt{H}
scaling is analogous to the normalization in MLP literature
e.g.
(
Du et al. 2019
;
Ji & Telgarsky 2021
;
Richards & Kuzborskij 2021
)
, ensuring the model variance is of constant order when
\bm{U}_{h}
is initialized

. Note that these relaxations sacrifice some generality since it is common practice to set

, thus imposing low-rank restrictions on matrices
{\bm{W}_{Q}}_{h}{\bm{W}_{K}}_{h}^{\top}
,
{\bm{W}_{V}}_{h}{\bm{W}_{O}}_{h}
. We defer a treatment of these to future work.

Throughout, we will use

\bm{\theta}_{h}:=\operatorname{concat}(\bm{U}_{h},\bm{W}_{h})\in\mathbb{R}^{dT+d^{2}}\,,

to denote the trainable parameters of the
h
-attention head and
\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}:=\operatorname{concat}(\{\bm{\theta}_{h}\}_{h\in[H]})\in\mathbb{R}^{H(dT+d^{2})}\,
for the trainable parameters of the overall model. More generally,
we use the convention of applying “
\,\widetilde{\,\,\cdot\,\,}\,
” notation for quantities relating to the multi-head model. Finally, with some slight abuse of notation, we define:
\lVert\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}\rVert_{2,\infty}:=\max_{h\in[H]}\|\bm{\theta}_{h}\|.

Training.

Given training set
({\bm{X}}_{i},y_{i})_{i\in[n]}
, with
n

IID
samples, we minimize logistic-loss based empirical risk

\widehat{L}(\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}):=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i\in[n]}\ell(y_{i}\widetilde{\Phi}({\bm{X}}_{i};\widetilde{\bm{\theta}})):=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i\in[n]}\log(1+e^{-y_{i}\widetilde{\Phi}({\bm{X}}_{i};\widetilde{\bm{\theta}})})\,.

Our analysis extends to any convex, smooth, Lipschitz and self-bounded loss.
2
2

2

A function
\ell:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is self-bounded if
\exists

C>0
such that
|\ell^{\prime}(t)|\leq C\ell(t).
The empirical risk is minimized as an approximation of the
test loss
defined as

L(\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}):=\mathbb{E}_{({\bm{X}},y)}[\ell(y\widetilde{\Phi}({\bm{X}};\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}))]\,.

We consider standard gradient-descent (GD) applied to empirical risk
\widehat{L}
. Formally, initialized at
\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}^{(0)}
and equipped with step-size
\eta>0
, at each iteration
k\geq 0
, GD performs the following update:

\displaystyle\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}^{(k+1)}=\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}^{(k)}-\eta\nabla\widehat{L}(\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}^{(k)})\,.

Gradient and Hessian bounds of soft-max attention

This section establishes bounds on the gradient and Hessian of the logistic empirical risk
\widehat{L}(\cdot)
evaluated on the multi-head attention model.
To do this, we first derive bounds on the Euclidean norm and spectral-norm for the gradient and Hessian of the self-attention model. In order to simplify notations, we state here the bounds for the single-head model (see App.
A.1
for multi-head model):

\Phi({\bm{X}};\bm{\theta}):=\Phi({\bm{X}};\bm{W},\bm{U})=\langle\bm{U},\bm{\varphi}({\bm{X}}\bm{W}{\bm{X}}^{\top}){\bm{X}}\rangle\,.

Lemma 1

(Gradient/Hessian formulas)
.

For all
\bm{a}\in\mathbb{R}^{T}
,
\bm{b},\bm{c}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
the model’s gradients satisfy:

\bullet

\begin{aligned} \nabla_{\bm{U}}\Phi({\bm{X}};\bm{\theta})=\bm{\varphi}({\bm{X}}\bm{W}{\bm{X}}^{\top}){\bm{X}}\,,\end{aligned}\quad
and
\qquad\begin{aligned} \nabla_{\bm{W}}\Phi({\bm{X}};\bm{\theta})=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\bm{x}_{t}\bm{u}_{t}^{\top}{\bm{X}}^{\top}{\bm{\varphi}}^{\prime}({\bm{X}}\bm{W}^{\top}\bm{x}_{t}){\bm{X}}\,.\end{aligned}

\bullet

\begin{aligned} \nabla_{\bm{W}}\langle\bm{a},\nabla_{\bm{U}}\Phi({\bm{X}};\bm{\theta})\,\bm{b}\rangle=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\bm{x}_{t}a_{t}\bm{b}^{\top}{\bm{X}}^{\top}{\bm{\varphi}}^{\prime}({\bm{X}}\bm{W}^{\top}\bm{x}_{t}){\bm{X}}\,,\end{aligned}\quad
and

\begin{aligned} \nabla_{\bm{W}}\langle\bm{c},\nabla_{\bm{W}}\Phi({\bm{X}};\bm{\theta})\,\bm{b}\rangle&=\sum_{t=1}^{T}(\bm{c}^{\top}\bm{x}_{t})\,\bm{x}_{t}\mathbf{d}^{\top}\,{\bm{\varphi}}^{\prime}({\bm{X}}\bm{W}^{\top}\bm{x}_{t}){\bm{X}}\,\\

These calculations imply the following useful bounds.

Proposition 1

(Model Gradient/Hessian bounds)
.

The Euclidean norm of the gradient and the spectral norm of the Hessian of the single-head Attention model (
1
) are bounded as follows:

\bullet

\begin{aligned} \|\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\Phi({\bm{X}};\bm{\theta})\|\leq 2\,\lVert{\bm{X}}\rVert^{2}_{2,\infty}\,\sum_{t=1}^{T}\,\lVert{\bm{X}}\bm{u}_{t}\rVert_{\infty}+\sqrt{T}\,\lVert{\bm{X}}\rVert_{2,\infty}\,.\end{aligned}

\bullet

\begin{aligned} \|\nabla^{2}_{\bm{\theta}}\Phi({\bm{X}};\bm{\theta})\|\leq 6\,d^{2}\,\lVert{\bm{X}}\rVert_{2,\infty}\,\lVert{\bm{X}}\rVert_{1,\infty}^{3}\,\sum_{t=1}^{T}\,\lVert{\bm{X}}\bm{u}_{t}\rVert_{\infty}+2\,d\,\sqrt{T\,d}\,\lVert{\bm{X}}\rVert_{2,\infty}\,\lVert{\bm{X}}\rVert_{1,\infty}^{2}\,.\end{aligned}

Under Assumption

4
Main results

In this section, we present our training and generalization bounds for multi-head attention.

4.1
Training bounds

We state our main result on train loss convergence in the following theorem.
See App.
B
for exact constants and the detailed proofs.

Theorem 1

(Training loss)
.

Fix iteration horizon
K\geq 1
and any
\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}\in\mathbb{R}^{H(dT+d^{2})}
and
H
satisfying

\displaystyle{\sqrt{H}}\gtrsim d^{2}\,T^{1/2}R^{5}\lVert\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}\rVert_{2,\infty}\lVert\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}-\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}^{(0)}\rVert^{3}.

(3)

Fix step-size

\eta\leq 1\,\wedge\,1/\rho(\widetilde{\bm{\theta}})\,\wedge\,\frac{\|\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}-\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}^{(0)}\|^{2}}{K\widehat{L}(\widetilde{\bm{\theta}})}\,\wedge\,\frac{\|\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}-\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}^{(0)}\|^{2}}{\widehat{L}(\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}^{(0)})},
with

\rho(\widetilde{\bm{\theta}})\gtrsim d^{9/2}\,T^{3/2}\,R^{13}\,\lVert\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}\rVert_{2,\infty}^{2}\,\lVert\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}-\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}^{(0)}\rVert^{2}.

Then, the following bounds hold for the training loss and the weights’ norm at iteration
K
of GD:

\displaystyle\widehat{L}(\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}^{(K)})\leq\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\widehat{L}(\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}_{k})\leq 2\widehat{L}(\widetilde{\bm{\theta}})+\frac{5\lVert\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}-\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}^{(0)}\rVert^{2}}{4\eta K},

(4)

\displaystyle\|\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}^{(K)}-\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}^{(0)}\|\leq 4\|\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}-\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}^{(0)}\|.

Yielding a concrete train loss bound requires an appropriate set of target parameters
\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}
in the sense of minimizing the bound in (
4
). Hence,
\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}
should simultaneously attain small loss (
\widehat{L}(\widetilde{\bm{\theta}})
) and distance to initialization (
\|\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}-\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}^{(0)}\|
). This desiderata is formalized in Assumption
2
below. The distance to initialization, as well as
\|\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}\|_{2,\infty}
, determine how many heads are required for our bounds to hold. Also, in view of the bound in (
4
), it is reasonable that an appropriate choice for
\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}
attains
\widehat{L}(\widetilde{\bm{\theta}})
of same order as
\|\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}-\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}^{(0)}\|^{2}/K.

Hence, the theorem’s restriction on the step-size is governed by the inverse local-smoothness of the loss:
\eta\lesssim 1/\rho(\widetilde{\bm{\theta}})
.

4.2
Generalization bounds

Next we bound the expected generalization gap. Expectations are with respect to (w.r.t) randomness of the train set. See App.
C
for the detailed proof, which is based on algorithmic-stability.

Theorem 2

(Generalization loss)
.

Fix any
K\geq 1
, any
\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}
and
H
satisfying (
3
), and any
step-size
\eta
satisfying the conditions of Thm.
1
.
Then the expected generalization gap at iteration
K
satisfies,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[L(\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}^{(K)})-\widehat{L}(\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}^{(K)})\big]\leq\frac{4}{n}\,\mathbb{E}\big[2\,K\,\widehat{L}(\widetilde{\bm{\theta}})+\frac{9\|\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}-\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}^{(0)}\|^{2}}{4\eta}\big]\,.

(5)

The condition on the number of heads is same up to constants to the corresponding condition in Theorem
1
. Also, the generalization-gap bound translates to test-loss bound by combining with Thm.
1
. Finally, similar to Thm.
1
, we can get concrete bounds under the realizability assumption; see Cor.
4
in App.
C.2
.
For the generalization analysis, we require that the realizability assumption holds almost surely over all training sets sampled from the data distribution.

The bounds on optimization and generalization are up to constants same as analogous bounds for logistic regression
(
Soudry et al. 2018
;
Ji & Telgarsky 2018
;
Shamir 2021
)
. Yet, for these bounds to be valid, we require sufficiently large number of heads as well as the existence of an appropriate set of target parameters
\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}
, as stated in the conditions of theorem.
Namely, these conditions are related to the realizability condition, which guarantees small training error near initialization. The next assumption formalizes these conditions.

Assumption 2

(Realizability)
.

There exist non-increasing functions
g\;:\;\mathbb{R}_{+}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
and
g_{0}\;:\;\mathbb{R}_{+}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
such that
\forall\epsilon>0
, there exists model parameters
\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}_{(\epsilon)}\in\mathbb{R}^{H(dT+d^{2})}
for which: (i) the empirical loss over
n
data samples satisfies
\widehat{L}(\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}_{(\varepsilon)})\leq\varepsilon
, (ii)

\|\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}_{(\varepsilon)}-\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}^{(0)}\|\leq g_{0}(\varepsilon)
, and, (iii)
\lVert\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}_{(\varepsilon)}\rVert_{2,\infty}\leq g(\varepsilon).

With this assumption, we can specialize the result of Thms. above to specific data settings; see Cor.
3
and
4
in App.
B.5
and
C.2
. In the next section we will further show how the realizability assumption is satisfied.

4.3
Primitive conditions for checking realizability

Here, we introduce a set of more primitive and straightforward-to-check conditions on the data and initialization that ensure the realizability Assumption
2
holds.

Definition 1

(Good initialization)
.

We say
\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}^{(0)}=\operatorname{concat}(\bm{\theta}_{1}^{(0)},\ldots,\bm{\theta}_{H}^{(0)})
is a
good
initialization with respect to training data
({\bm{X}}_{i},y_{i})_{i\in[n]}
provided the following three properties hold.

P1.
Parameter
L_{2,\infty}
-bound:
There exists parameter
B_{2}\geq 1
such that
\forall h\in[H]
it holds
\|\bm{\theta}^{(0)}_{h}\|_{2}\leq B_{2}\,.

P2.
Model bound:
There exists parameter
B_{\Phi}\geq 1
such that
\forall i\in[n]
it holds
\left\lvert\widetilde{\Phi}({\bm{X}}_{i};\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}^{(0)})\right\rvert\leq B_{\Phi}
.

P3.
NTK separability:
There exists
\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}_{\star}\in\mathbb{R}^{H(dT+d^{2})}
and
\gamma>0
such that
\|\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}_{\star}\|=\sqrt{2}
and
\forall i\in[n]
, it holds

y_{i}\left\langle\nabla\widetilde{\Phi}\left({\bm{X}}_{i};\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}^{(0)}\right),\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}_{\star}\right\rangle\geq\gamma.

Prop.
7
in the appendix shows that starting from a
good
initialization we can always find
\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}_{(\epsilon)}
satisfying the realizability Assumption
2
provided large enough number of heads. Thus, given
good
initialization, we can immediately apply Theorems
1
and
2
to get the following concrete bounds.

Corollary 2

(General bounds under
good
initialization)
.

Suppose
good
initialization
\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}^{(0)}
and let

\displaystyle\sqrt{H}\gtrsim d^{2}\,T^{1/2}\,R^{5}\,B_{2}^{2}\,\big(g_{0}(1/K)\big)^{3},\qquad\text{where}~~g_{0}(\frac{1}{K})=\frac{2B_{\Phi}+\log(K)}{\gamma}\,.

Further fix step-size
\eta\leq 1\,\wedge\,1/\rho(K)\,\wedge\,\frac{4B_{\Phi}^{2}}{\gamma^{2}\log(1+e^{B_{\Phi}})}
with

\rho(K)\gtrsim d^{9/2}\,T^{3/2}\,R^{13}\,g_{0}(\frac{1}{K})^{4}.

Then, it holds that

\displaystyle\widehat{L}(\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}^{(K)})\leq\frac{2}{K}+\frac{5\left(2B_{\Phi}+\log(K)\right)^{2}}{4\gamma^{2}\,\eta\,K},\quad\text{and}\quad\mathbb{E}\big[L(\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}^{(K)})-\widehat{L}(\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}^{(K)})\big]\leq\frac{17\left(2B_{\Phi}+\log(K)\right)^{2}}{\gamma^{2}\,\eta\,n}\,.

Consider training loss after
K
GD steps: Assuming
B_{\Phi}=\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}_{K}(1)
and
\gamma=\mathcal{O}_{K}(1)
, then choosing
\eta=\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}_{K}({1})
, the corollary guarantees train loss is
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}_{K}(\frac{1}{K})
provided polylogarithmic number of heads
H=\Omega(\log^{6}(K))
. Moreover, after
K\approx n
GD steps the expected test loss is
\mathcal{O}(1/n)
.

Remark 1
.

The last two conditions (P2 and P3) for
good
initialization are similar to the conditions needed in
(
Taheri & Thrampoulidis 2023
;
Ji & Telgarsky 2020
;
Nitanda et al. 2019
)
for analysis of two-layer MLPs. Compared to
(
Ji & Telgarsky 2020
;
Nitanda et al. 2019
)
which assume random Gaussian initialization
\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}^{(0)}
, and similar to
(
Taheri & Thrampoulidis 2023
)

the NTK separability assumption (P3) can potentially accommodate deterministic
\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}^{(0)}
.
Condition (P1) appears because we allow training both layers of the model. Specifically the
L_{2,\infty}
norm originates from the Hessian bounds in Corollary
1
.

5
Application to tokenized-mixture model

We now demonstrate through an example how our results apply to specific data models.

Data model: An example.

Consider
M+2
distinct patterns
\{\bm{\mu}_{+},\bm{\mu}_{-},\bm{\nu}_{1},\bm{\nu}_{2},...,\bm{\nu}_{M}\}
, where discriminative patterns
\bm{\mu}_{\pm}
correspond to labels
y=\pm 1
.
The tokens are split into (i) a label-relevant set (
\mathcal{R}
) and (ii) a label-irrelevant set (
\mathcal{R}^{c}:=[T]\backslash\mathcal{R}
).
Conditioned on the label and
\mathcal{R}
, the tokens
\bm{x}_{t},t\in[T]
are
IID
as follows

\displaystyle\bm{x}_{t}|y=\begin{cases}\bm{\mu}_{y}&\text{,\,$t\in\mathcal{R}$}\\
\bm{\nu}_{j_{t}}+\bm{z}_{t}&\text{,\,$j_{t}\sim$ Unif$(1,...,M)$ and $t\in\mathcal{R}^{c}$},\\
\end{cases}

(DM1)

where
\bm{z}_{t}
are noise vectors. Let
\mathcal{D}
denote the joint distribution induced by the described
(\mathbf{X},y)
pairs.

Assumption 3
.

The labels are equi-probable and we further assume the following:

\bullet

Orthogonal, equal-energy means:
All patterns are orthogonal to each other, i.e.
\bm{\mu}_{+}\perp\bm{\mu}_{-}\perp\bm{\nu}_{\ell}\perp\bm{\nu}_{\ell^{\prime}},\,\,\forall\,\ell,\ell^{\prime}\in[M].

Also, for all
y\in\{\pm 1\},\ell\in[M]
that
\|\bm{\mu}_{y}\|=\|\bm{\nu}_{\ell}\|=S
, where
S
denotes the
signal strength
.

\bullet

Sparsity level:
The number of label-relevant tokens is
|\mathcal{R}|=\zeta T
; for sparsity level
\zeta\in(0,1)
.

\bullet

Noise distribution:
The noise tokens
\bm{z}_{t}
are sampled from a distribution
\mathcal{D}_{z}
, such that
it holds almost surely for
\bm{z}_{t}\sim\mathcal{D}_{z}
that

\left\lvert\langle\bm{z}_{t},\bm{\mu}_{y}\rangle\right\rvert\leq Z_{\mu},\,\,y\in\{\pm 1\}

and

\left\lvert\langle\bm{z}_{t},\bm{\nu}_{\ell}\rangle\right\rvert\leq Z_{\nu}/M,\,\,\forall\,\ell\in[M]\,.

Moreover,

\|\bm{z}_{t}\|\leq Z\,.

Overall, Assumption
1
is satisfied with
R=\sqrt{S^{2}+Z^{2}+2Z_{\nu}/M}
.

The above assumptions can be relaxed, but without contributing new insights. We have chosen to present a model that is representative and transparent in its analysis.

5.1
Finding a
good
initialization

To apply the general Corollary
2
to the specific data model
DM1
, it suffices to find
good
initialization.
While we cannot directly show that
\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}^{(0)}=\mathbf{0}
is
good
, we can show this is the case for first step of gradient descent
\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}^{(1)}
. Thus, we consider training in two phases as follows.

First phase: One step of GD as initialization.

We use
n_{1}
training samples to update the model parameters by running one-step of gradient descent starting from zero initialization. Specifically,

\displaystyle(\bm{U}_{h}^{(1)},\bm{W}_{h}^{(1)})=\bm{\theta}_{h}^{(1)}=\bm{\theta}_{h}^{(0)}-\alpha_{h}\sqrt{H}\cdot\nabla_{\bm{\theta}_{h}}\widehat{L}_{n_{1}}(\bm{\theta}_{h}^{(0)}),\,\,\text{where}\quad\bm{\theta}^{(0)}_{h}=\mathbf{0}\,\,\forall\,\,h\in[H].

Here,
\alpha_{h}
denotes the step-size for head
h\in[H]
and the scaling by
\sqrt{H}
guarantees the update of each head is
\mathcal{O}\left(1\right)
. The lemma below shows that at the end of this phase, we have

\lVert\bm{U}_{h}^{(1)}-\frac{\zeta\alpha_{h}}{2}\bm{1}_{T}\bm{u}_{\star}\top\rVert_{F}=\mathcal{O}\left(1/\sqrt{n_{1}}\right)

where
\bm{u}_{\star}
is the oracle classifier
\bm{u}_{\star}=\bm{\mu}_{+}-\bm{\mu}_{-}
. On the other hand, the attention weight-matrix does
not
get updated; the interesting aspect of the training lies in the second phase, which involves updating
\bm{W}
.

Lemma 2

(First phase)
.

After the first-gradient step as described above, we have
\bm{U}_{h}^{(1)}=\alpha_{h}\,\mathds{1}_{T}\,\big(\frac{\zeta}{2}\bm{u}_{\star}^{\top}+{\bm{p}}^{\top}\big)
and
\bm{W}_{h}^{(1)}=\mathbf{0}
.
where with probability at least
1-\delta\in(0,1)
over the randomness of labels there exists positive universal constant
C>0
such that

\displaystyle\|{\bm{p}}\|\leq C\,\left(2S+Z\right)\,\big(\sqrt{\frac{d}{n_{1}}}+\sqrt{\frac{\log(1/\delta)}{n_{1}}}\big)=:P\,.

(6)

Second phase: GD with constant step size.

During the second phase,
K
gradient steps are performed on
n
new samples (distinct from those used in the first phase). Concretely,

\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}^{(k+1)}=\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}^{(k)}-\eta\cdot\nabla_{\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}}\widehat{L}_{n}(\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}^{(k)}),\quad k=1,\ldots,K\,,

with
\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}^{(1)}=\operatorname{concat}\big(\{\bm{\theta}_{h}^{(1)}\}_{h\in[H]}\big)
the step obtained by the first-phase update and
\eta
the step-size of the second phase.
In order to analyze the second phase, during which both
\widetilde{\bm{W}}
and
\widetilde{\bm{U}}
get updated, we employ the general results of Section
4
. To do so, we show that
\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}^{(1)}
serves as
good
initialization as per Definition
1
.

Proposition 2
.

Consider the first-phase iterate
\{\bm{\theta}_{h}^{(1)}\}_{h\in[H]}
and condition on the event
\|{\bm{p}}\|\leq P
(depending only on the data randomness in the first phase) of Lemma
2
. Suppose the step-size of the first phase is chosen
IID

\alpha_{h}\sim\operatorname{Unif}(\pm 1),\,h\in[H]
. Then, the initialization
\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}^{(1)}=\operatorname{concat}\big(\bm{\theta}_{1}^{(1)},\ldots,\bm{\theta}_{H}^{(1)}\big)
is
good
with respect to data sampled from
DM1
and satisfying Assumption
3
. Specifically, the three desired properties hold as follows.

\bullet
Almost surely,
P1
holds with
B_{2}=\sqrt{T}(S+P)\,.

\bullet
With probability
1-\delta\in(0,1)
,
P2
holds with
B_{\Phi}=TR(S+P)\sqrt{2\log(n/\delta)}\,.

\bullet

Suppose

\sqrt{H}\gtrsim\frac{R^{4}T(S+P)}{\gamma_{\star}}\cdot\sqrt{2\log(n/\delta)}\,.

Then, with probability
1-\delta\in(0,1)
,

P3
holds with
\gamma=\gamma_{\star}/2
where

\displaystyle\gamma_{\star}:=\frac{T(1-\zeta)\zeta\big(\zeta S^{4}-7\bar{Z}S^{2}-12\bar{Z}^{2}-16\frac{\bar{Z}^{3}}{S^{2}}\big)}{4\sqrt{2(M+1)}}-PT^{5/2}(S+Z)^{3}+\frac{S\,\sqrt{T}\Big(\zeta-2(1-\zeta)\frac{Z_{\mu}}{S^{2}}\Big)}{\sqrt{2}}\,,

(7)

and
\bar{Z}:=Z_{\mu}\,\vee\,Z_{\nu}
. The randomness is with respect to the sampling of
\alpha_{h},\,h\in[H]
.

The parameter
\gamma_{\star}
in (
7
) represents the NTK margin of the model at initialization
\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}^{(1)}
. By Corollary
2
, larger margin translates to better train/generalization bounds and smaller requirements on the number of heads. For a concrete example, suppose
T\,\vee\,M=\mathcal{O}\left(1\right)
and
Z\,\vee\,\bar{Z}=\mathcal{O}\left(S\right)
. Then, provided first-phase sample size
n_{1}\gtrsim S^{2}d
so that
P=\mathcal{O}\left(1\right)
, it holds
\gamma_{\star}=\gamma_{\text{lin}}+\Omega(\zeta^{2}(1-\zeta)S^{4})
, where
\gamma_{\text{lin}}=\Omega({\zeta S})
is the margin of a linear model for the same dataset (see App.
F
). Overall, applying Cor.
2
for
K=n
and a polylogarithmic
\operatorname{polylog}(n)
number of heads leads to
{\tilde{\mathcal{O}}}\left(\frac{1}{\eta\gamma_{\star}^{2}n}\right)
train loss and expected generalization gap.

5.2
Proof sketch of
P3
: NTK separability

It is instructive to see how
P3
follows as it sheds light on the choice of an appropriate target parameter
\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}
as per Thms.
1
and
2
. We choose

\bm{W}_{\star}=\bm{\mu}_{+}\bm{\mu}_{+}^{\top}+\bm{\mu}_{-}\bm{\mu}_{-}^{\top}+\sum_{\ell\in[M]}\bm{\nu}_{\ell}(\bm{\mu}_{+}+\bm{\mu}_{-})^{\top}\,\quad\text{and}\quad\bm{U}_{\star}=\mathds{1}_{T}\bm{u}_{\star}^{\top}=\mathds{1}_{T}(\bm{\mu}_{+}-\bm{\mu}_{-})^{\top}\,,

and normalize parameters such that

\bm{\theta}_{\star}:=(\overline{\bm{U}}_{\star}=\frac{1}{\lVert\bm{U}_{\star}\rVert_{F}}\bm{U}_{\star}\,,\,\,\texttt{sign}(\alpha)\overline{\bm{W}}_{\star}=\texttt{sign}(\alpha)\frac{1}{\lVert\bm{W}_{\star}\rVert_{F}}\bm{W}_{\star})\,
. It is easy to see that
\bm{U}_{\star}
is the optimal classifier for the label-relevant tokens. To gain intuition on the choice of
\bm{W}_{\star}
, note that
\bm{W}_{\star}=\bm{W}_{K,\star}\bm{W}_{Q,\star}^{\top}
, with key-query matrices chosen as
\bm{W}_{K,\star}=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{\mu}_{+}&\bm{\mu}_{-}&\bm{\nu}_{1}&\cdots&\bm{\nu}_{M}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times(M+2)}
and
\bm{W}_{Q,\star}=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{\mu}_{+}&\bm{\mu}_{-}&\bm{\mu}_{+}+\bm{\mu}_{-}&\cdots&\bm{\mu}_{+}+\bm{\mu}_{-}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times(M+2)}
. With these choices, the relevance scores (aka softmax logits) of relevant tokens turn out to be strictly larger compared to the irrelevant tokens. Concretely, we show in App.
D.2.3
that the
t
-th row
\bm{r}_{t}({\bm{X}};\bm{W}_{\star})={\bm{X}}\bm{W}_{\star}^{\top}\bm{x}_{t}
of the softmax-logit matrix satisfies the following:

\displaystyle\forall t:[\bm{r}_{t}]_{t^{\prime}}=\begin{cases}\mathcal{O}\left(S^{4}\right)&\text{,}\,t^{\prime}\in\mathcal{R}\,,\\
\mathcal{O}\left(S^{2}\right)&\text{,}\,t^{\prime}\in\mathcal{R}^{c}\,.\\
\end{cases}

(8)

Thus, under this parameter choice, softmax can attend to label-relevant tokens and supresses noisy irrelevant tokens. In turn, this increases the signal-to-noise ratio for classification using
\bm{U}_{\star}
.

We now show how to compute
\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\theta}^{(1)}}\,y\langle\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\Phi({\bm{X}};\bm{\theta}^{(1)}),\bm{\theta}_{\star}\rangle
for a single head. Recall
\bm{\theta}_{\star}
consists of
\overline{\bm{U}}_{\star}
,
\overline{\bm{W}}_{\star}
. First, since
\bm{W}^{{(1)}}=\textbf{0}
, using Assumption
3
, a simple calculation shows

y\,\langle\nabla_{\bm{U}}\Phi({\bm{X}};\bm{\theta}^{(1)}),\overline{\bm{U}}_{\star}\rangle\geq\frac{S\,\sqrt{T}}{\sqrt{2}}\,\Big(\zeta-2(1-\zeta)\frac{Z_{\mu}}{S^{2}}\Big).

Second, to compute
\mathbb{E}_{\alpha\sim\operatorname{Unif}(\pm 1)}y\langle\nabla_{\bm{W}}\Phi\left({\bm{X}};\bm{\theta}^{(1)}\right),\texttt{sign}(\alpha)\overline{\bm{W}}_{\star}\rangle
it follows from
Lemma
1
that

\displaystyle\nabla_{\bm{W}}\Phi({\bm{X}};\bm{\theta}^{(1)})=\frac{\alpha\zeta}{2}\sum_{t\in[T]}\bm{x}_{t}\bm{u}_{\star}^{\top}{\bm{X}}^{\top}{\bm{\varphi}}^{\prime}(\textbf{0}){\bm{X}}+\alpha\sum_{t\in[T]}\bm{x}_{t}{\bm{p}}^{\top}{\bm{X}}^{\top}{\bm{\varphi}}^{\prime}(\textbf{0}){\bm{X}}\,.

Note the first term is dominant here since the second term can be controlled by making
\|{\bm{p}}\|_{2}
small as per Lemma
2
. Thus, ignoring here the second term (see Appendix
D.2.3
for full calculation)
y\langle\nabla_{\bm{W}}\Phi({\bm{X}};\bm{\theta}^{(1)}),\bm{W}_{\star}\rangle
is governed by the following term:
\frac{\alpha\zeta}{2}\sum_{t\in[T]}y\,\bm{u}_{\star}^{\top}{\bm{X}}^{\top}{\bm{\varphi}}^{\prime}(\textbf{0}){\bm{X}}\bm{W}_{\star}^{\top}\bm{x}_{t}=\frac{\alpha\zeta}{2}\sum_{t\in[T]}y\,\bm{u}_{\star}^{\top}{\bm{X}}^{\top}{\bm{\varphi}}^{\prime}(\textbf{0})\,\bm{r}_{t}\,.

Note that
{\bm{\varphi}}^{\prime}(\textbf{0})=\bm{I}_{T}-\frac{1}{T}\mathds{1}_{T}\mathds{1}_{T}^{\top}
. To simplify the exposition here, let us focus on the identity component and leave treatment of the the rank-one term to the detailed proof. The corresponding term then becomes

\displaystyle\frac{\alpha\zeta}{2}\sum_{t\in[T]}\sum_{t^{\prime}\in[T]}\underbrace{\left(y\,\bm{u}_{\star}^{\top}\bm{x}_{t^{\prime}}\right)}_{\text{class. logits}}\cdot\underbrace{\left([\bm{r}_{t}]_{t^{\prime}}\right)}_{\text{softmax logits}},

which involves for each output token
t
, the sum of products over all tokens
t^{\prime}\in[T]
of softmax logits (i.e. relevant scores
[\bm{r}_{t}]_{t^{\prime}}
) and corresponding classification logits (i.e.
y\,\bm{u}_{\star}^{\top}\bm{x}_{t^{\prime}}
). Note that by choice of
\bm{u}_{\star}
and
\bm{W}_{\star}
, both the classification and softmax logits are large from label-relevant tokens, while being small for noise tokens. Intuitively, this allows for a positive margin
\gamma_{\star}
as stated in Proposition
2
. We defer the detailed calculations to Appendix
D.2.3
.

In the appendix, we also detail how to yield the computation for the MHA, which builds on the calculations for the single-head attention model above. In short,
we simply choose multi-head parameter
\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}_{\star}
as
\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}_{\star}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{H}}\operatorname{concat}\left(\bm{\theta}_{\star}\big(\bm{\theta}_{1}^{(1)}\big),\ldots,\bm{\theta}_{\star}\big(\bm{\theta}_{H}^{(1)}\big)\right)\,.

This guarantees that
\|\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}_{\star}\|=\sqrt{2}
and maintains the multi-head NTK margin be at least
\gamma_{\star}
in expectation. To complete the proof, it remains to get a high-probability version of this bound. To do this, notice that
\bm{\theta}_{h}^{(1)}
are
IID
, hence we can apply Hoeffding’s inequality, which finally gives the desired bound on the NTK margin provided sufficient number of heads
H
, which controls the degree of concentration when applying Hoeffding’s inequality. See Lemmas
14
and
15
for details.

5.3
Is the NTK margin optimal?

Below, we discuss the optimality of the NTK margin
\gamma_{\star}
. First, define set of parameters
\bm{\theta}_{\text{opt}}:=(\bm{U}_{\text{opt}},\bm{W}_{\text{opt}})
:

\displaystyle\bm{U}_{\text{opt}}:=\frac{1}{S\sqrt{2T}}\,\bm{U}_{\star}\,\qquad\text{and}\qquad\bm{W}_{\text{opt}}:=\frac{1}{S^{2}\sqrt{2(M+1)}}\bm{W}_{\star}\,,

(9)

normalized so that
\|\bm{\theta}_{\text{opt}}\|_{F}=\sqrt{2}
. Recall here the definitions of
\bm{U}_{\star},\bm{W}_{\star}
in the section above. As we already explained above, this choice of parameters guarantees that relevant tokens are assigned larger relevance and classification scores compared to irrelevant ones. Specifically about
\bm{W}_{\star}
, we saw in Eq. (
8
) that it ensures a gap of
\mathcal{O}(S^{2})
between relevance scores of label-relevant and label-irrelevant tokens. Thanks to this gap, it is possible for softmax to fully attend to the label-relevant tokens by saturating the softmax. To do this, it suffices to scale-up
\bm{W}_{\star}
by an amount
\propto 1/S^{2}
. This is formalized in the proposition below.

Proposition 3

(Attention expressivity for tokenized-mixture model)
.

Consider single-head attention model. Suppose the noise level is such that
Z_{\mu}=Z_{\nu}\leq S^{2}/8
. For any
\epsilon>0
, consider
\Gamma_{\epsilon}
satisfying

\Gamma_{\epsilon}\geq\frac{8\sqrt{2(M+1)}}{3S^{2}}\log\left(\frac{\zeta^{-1}-1}{\epsilon}\right)\,.

Then, the attention scores corresponding to weights
\Gamma_{\epsilon}\cdot\bm{W}_{\text{opt}}
satisfy

\displaystyle\forall t\in[T]\,:\,0\leq 1-\sum_{t^{\prime}\in\mathcal{R}}\bm{\varphi}_{t^{\prime}}(\bm{x}_{t}^{\top}\,\Gamma_{\epsilon}\bm{W}_{\text{opt}}\,{\bm{X}}^{T})=\sum_{t^{\prime}\in\mathcal{R}^{c}}\bm{\varphi}_{t^{\prime}}(\bm{x}_{t}^{\top}\,\Gamma_{\epsilon}\bm{W}_{\text{opt}}\,{\bm{X}}^{T})\leq\epsilon\,.

(10)

Thus, almost surely over data
({\bm{X}},y)
generated from data model
DM1
the margin of single-head attention with parameters
(\bm{U}_{\text{opt}},\Gamma_{\epsilon}\cdot\bm{W}_{\text{opt}})
satisfies

\displaystyle y\Phi({\bm{X}};\bm{U}_{\text{opt}},\Gamma_{\epsilon}\cdot\bm{W}_{\text{opt}})\geq\gamma_{\text{attn}}:=\gamma_{\text{attn}}(\epsilon):=\frac{\sqrt{T}}{\sqrt{2}S}\left(S^{2}(1-\epsilon)-2\epsilon Z_{\mu}\right)\,.

(11)

From Eq. (
10
), note that as
\epsilon\rightarrow 0
and
\Gamma_{\epsilon}\rightarrow\infty
, the softmax map saturates, i.e. it approaches a hard-max map that attends only to the label-relevant tokens (
\mathcal{R}
) and suppress the rest (
\mathcal{R}^{c}
). As a consequence of this, Eq. (
11
) shows that the achieved margin approaches
\gamma_{\text{attn}}:=S\sqrt{T}/\sqrt{2}
. Note this is independent of the sparsity level
\zeta
. In particular,
\gamma_{\text{attn}}\geq\gamma_{\star}\geq\gamma_{\text{lin}}
and the gap increases with decreasing sparsity. See appendix for experiments and discussion regarding the margin achieved by GD for data model
DM1
.

Following Proposition
3
, a natural question arises: Is it possible to choose “good” parameters
\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}=(\widetilde{\bm{U}},\widetilde{\bm{W}})
based on the set of optimal parameters
\bm{\theta}_{\text{opt}}
? This would then yield train-loss and expected generalization-gap bounds
{\tilde{\mathcal{O}}}\left(1/({\eta\gamma_{\text{attn}}^{2}n})\right)
after
\Theta(n)
steps of GD starting at
\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}^{(0)}=\mathbf{0}
. To investigate this question, define the following parameters for each head, aligning with the aforementioned “good” directions of Proposition
3
:

\displaystyle\bm{U}_{h}:=\frac{\log(n)}{\gamma_{\text{attn}}}\frac{1}{H^{1/2}}\,\bm{U}_{\text{opt}},\hskip 5.69054pt\bm{W}_{h}:=\frac{C}{H^{p}}\,\bm{W}_{\text{opt}}\,,

for some
C>0,\,p>0,\,\text{and}\,\forall h\in[H]
. To yield the margin
\gamma_{\text{attn}}
of (
10
), we need that each
\bm{W}_{h}
has norm at least
\Gamma_{\epsilon}\propto 1/S^{2}
. Thus, we need

\lVert\bm{W}_{h}\rVert\gtrsim\frac{1}{S^{2}}\implies S^{2}\gtrsim\frac{1}{C}\cdot H^{p}\,.

Now, in order to apply Thms.
1
and
2
, the requirement on the number of heads
H
in terms of distance of
\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}
to
\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}^{(0)}=\mathbf{0}
yields the following condition:

\displaystyle H^{1/2}\gtrsim S^{5}\lVert\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}\rVert^{3}\,.

(12)

Note that
\lVert\widetilde{\bm{U}}\rVert=\frac{\log(n)}{\gamma_{\text{attn}}}\approx\frac{\log(n)}{S},\,\lVert\widetilde{\bm{W}}\rVert=C\cdot H^{1/2-p}
. Hence, in computing
\lVert\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}\rVert
, we distinguish
two cases.

First, assume that
\lVert\widetilde{\bm{W}}\rVert\geq\lVert\widetilde{\bm{U}}\rVert
which implies that
S\gtrsim\frac{\log(n)}{C}\cdot H^{p-1/2}
and

\lVert\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}\rVert\gtrsim\lVert\widetilde{\bm{W}}\rVert\,\vee\,\lVert\widetilde{\bm{U}}\rVert=C\cdot H^{1/2-p}
.
Since

\displaystyle S\gtrsim\frac{1}{C^{1/2}}\cdot H^{p/2}\,\vee\,\frac{\log(n)}{C}\cdot H^{p-1/2}\,,

by using Eq. (
12
), we get the following conditions on
H
:

\displaystyle H^{1/2}\gtrsim S^{5}\cdot C^{3}\cdot H^{3/2-3p}\gtrsim C^{1/2}\cdot H^{3/2-p/2}\,\vee\,\frac{\log^{5}(n)}{C^{2}}\cdot H^{2p-1}\implies H^{p-2}\gtrsim C\,\,\,\,\text{and}\,\,\,\,H^{p-3/4}\lesssim\frac{C}{\log^{5/2}(n)}\,.

Combining these two gives

C\lesssim\frac{C}{\log^{5/2}(n)}\implies\log(n)\lesssim 1\,,

a contradiction since
n>1
. Thus, there are no possible choices for
p
and
C
that satisfy both conditions. The case
\lVert\widetilde{\bm{W}}\rVert\leq\lVert\widetilde{\bm{U}}\rVert
can be treated similarly leading to the same conclusion; thus, is omitted for brevity.

Intuitively, this contradiction arises because of the large
\lVert\bm{W}_{h}\rVert
requirement to achieve margin
\gamma_{\text{attn}}
. Finally, one can ask if it is possible to resolve the contradiction by changing the scaling of normalization with respect to
H
in the MHA model Eq. (
2
), from
1/H^{1/2}
to
1/H^{c}
for
c>0
. It can be shown via the same argument that no such value of
c
exists for which
\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}
constructed above satisfies the overparameterization requirement
H^{c}\gtrsim S^{5}\|\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}\|^{3}
. We thus conclude that the construction of weights in Proposition
3
does not yield a target parameter that simultaneously achieves low empirical loss and allows choosing
H
large enough as per (
3
). This triggers interesting questions for future research: Does GD converge to weights attaining margin
\gamma_{\text{attn}}
as in Proposition
3
? If so, under what conditions on initialization? See also the remarks in Section
7
.

\widetilde{\cdot}
in

and
\widetilde{\Phi}({\bm{X}}_{i};\widetilde{\bm{\theta}})
as everything refers to the full model. Moreover,
\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}^{(K)}

\bm{\theta}_{K}

6.1
Training analysis

The proof begins by showing step-wise descent for any iteration
k\geq 0
of GD (see Lemma
7
), where step-size at each iteration
\eta_{k}\leq\frac{1}{\rho_{k}}
depends on the objective’s local smoothness parameters
\rho_{k}=\beta_{2}(\bm{\theta}_{k})\,\vee\,\beta_{2}(\bm{\theta}_{k+1})
:

\displaystyle\widehat{L}(\bm{\theta}_{k+1})\leq\widehat{L}(\bm{\theta}_{k})-\frac{\eta_{k}}{2}\left\|\nabla\widehat{L}\left(\bm{\theta}_{k}\right)\right\|^{2}\,.

(13)

Now, using Taylor’s theorem we can link
\widehat{L}(\bm{\theta}_{k})
to
\widehat{L}(\bm{\theta})
for any
\bm{\theta}
as follows:

\displaystyle\widehat{L}(\bm{\theta})\geq\widehat{L}(\bm{\theta}_{k})+\langle\nabla\widehat{L}(\bm{\theta}_{k}),\bm{\theta}-\bm{\theta}_{k}\rangle+\frac{1}{2}\min_{\bm{\theta}_{k_{\alpha}}}\lambda_{\min}\left(\nabla^{2}\widehat{L}(\bm{\theta}_{k_{\alpha}})\right)\lVert\bm{\theta}-\bm{\theta}_{k}\rVert^{2},

(14)

where
\bm{\theta}_{k_{\alpha}}:=\alpha\bm{\theta}_{k}+(1-\alpha)\bm{\theta},\,\,\alpha\in[0,1]
. We can plug this into (
13
) to relate the loss at iterates
\bm{\theta}_{k}
and
\bm{\theta}_{k+1}
.
To continue, we need to lower bound
\min_{\bm{\theta}_{k_{\alpha}}}\lambda_{\min}\left(\nabla^{2}\widehat{L}(\bm{\theta}_{k_{\alpha}})\right)
. For this, we use the following property of the loss objective from Corollary
1
:

\forall\bm{\theta}\,:\,\,\,\lambda_{\min}\left(\nabla^{2}\widehat{L}(\bm{\theta})\right)\geq-\kappa(\bm{\theta})\cdot\widehat{L}(\bm{\theta}),
where
\kappa(\bm{\theta}):=\frac{\beta_{3}(\bm{\theta})}{\sqrt{H}}.

Note from the definition of
\beta_{3}(\cdot)
that

\forall\bm{\theta}_{1},\bm{\theta}_{2}\,:\,\,\,\max_{\bm{\theta}\in[\bm{\theta}_{1},\bm{\theta}_{2}]}\beta_{3}(\bm{\theta})=\beta_{3}(\bm{\theta}_{1})\,\vee\,\beta_{3}(\bm{\theta}_{2})\,.

Thus, the above property of the loss implies the following
local self-bounded weak convexity
property on the line
[\bm{\theta}_{1},\bm{\theta}_{2}]
for arbitrary points
\bm{\theta}_{1},\bm{\theta}_{2}
:

\displaystyle\forall\bm{\theta}_{1},\bm{\theta}_{2}\,:\,\,\,\min_{\bm{\theta}\in[\bm{\theta}_{1},\bm{\theta}_{2}]}\lambda_{\min}\left(\nabla^{2}\widehat{L}(\bm{\theta})\right)\geq-\frac{\beta_{3}(\bm{\theta}_{1})\,\vee\,\beta_{3}(\bm{\theta}_{2})}{\sqrt{H}}\cdot\max_{\bm{\theta}\in[\bm{\theta}_{1},\bm{\theta}_{2}]}\widehat{L}(\bm{\theta})\,.

(15)

Therefore, using Eq. (
15
) in Eq. (
14
), we can get:

\displaystyle\widehat{L}(\bm{\theta})\geq\widehat{L}(\bm{\theta}_{k})+\langle\nabla\widehat{L}(\bm{\theta}_{k}),\bm{\theta}-\bm{\theta}_{k}\rangle-\frac{1}{2}\frac{\beta_{3}(\bm{\theta}_{1})\,\vee\,\beta_{3}(\bm{\theta}_{2})}{\sqrt{H}}\cdot\max_{\alpha\in[0,1]}\widehat{L}(\bm{\theta}_{k_{\alpha}})\,\lVert\bm{\theta}-\bm{\theta}_{k}\rVert^{2}\,.

(16)

To apply the Descent Lemma in (
13
), we need to fix a step-size such that satisfies the condition of the Lemma at each iteration
\eta\leq\eta_{k}
for all
k<K
. Then, combining with Eq. (
16
) and applying standard telescope summation, we arrive at the following:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\widehat{L}(\bm{\theta}_{k})\leq\widehat{L}(\bm{\theta})+\frac{\lVert\bm{\theta}-\bm{\theta}_{0}\rVert^{2}}{2\eta K}+\frac{1}{2K}\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}\frac{\beta_{3}(\bm{\theta})\,\vee\,\beta_{3}(\bm{\theta}_{k})}{\sqrt{H}}\cdot\max_{\alpha\in[0,1]}\widehat{L}(\bm{\theta}_{k_{\alpha}})\lVert\bm{\theta}-\bm{\theta}_{k}\rVert^{2}\,.

(17)

Next, we use the following generalized local quasi-convexity (GLQC) of the loss function.

Proposition 4

(GLQC property: Slight variation of Prop. 8 of
Taheri & Thrampoulidis 2023
)
.

Let
\bm{\theta}_{1}
and
\bm{\theta}_{2}
be two points that are sufficiently close to each other, such that

\displaystyle{2\,\left(\beta_{3}(\bm{\theta}_{1})\,\vee\,\beta_{3}(\bm{\theta}_{2})\right)}\,\|\bm{\theta}_{1}-\bm{\theta}_{2}\|^{2}\leq\sqrt{H}\,.

(18)

Then,

\max_{\bm{\theta}\in[\bm{\theta}_{1},\bm{\theta}_{2}]}\widehat{L}(\bm{\theta})\leq\frac{4}{3}\,\left(\widehat{L}(\bm{\theta}_{1})\,\vee\,\widehat{L}(\bm{\theta}_{2})\right).

Using Proposition
4
in Eq. (
17
) and assuming sufficiently large heads
H
such that
\sqrt{H}\geq{2\,\left(\beta_{3}(\bm{\theta})\,\vee\,\beta_{3}(\bm{\theta}_{k})\right)}\,\|\bm{\theta}-\bm{\theta}_{k}\|^{2}
, we can get the advertised regret bound in (
4
).

In order to remove the dependence of
H
on iteration
k
, by an induction argument we can show bounded iterates-norm i.e.
\|\bm{\theta}_{k}-\bm{\theta}\|\leq 3\|\bm{\theta}-\bm{\theta}_{0}\|
(see Lemma
10
). Using this and the definition of
\beta_{3}(\cdot)
we can control
\beta_{3}(\bm{\theta})\,\vee\,\beta_{3}(\bm{\theta}_{k})
as
(\beta_{3}(\bm{\theta})\,\vee\,\beta_{3}(\bm{\theta}_{k}))\lesssim\lVert\bm{\theta}-\bm{\theta}_{0}\rVert+\lVert\bm{\theta}\rVert_{2,\infty}
to get the desired requirement of heads
{\sqrt{H}}\gtrsim\lVert\bm{\theta}\rVert_{2,\infty}\lVert\bm{\theta}-\bm{\theta}_{0}\rVert^{3}
stated in Eq. (
3
).

The remaining piece to guarantee descent at each step is establishing a
\rho(\bm{\theta})
such that
\rho_{k}\leq\rho(\bm{\theta})
for all
k<K
. To do this, we recall that
\rho_{k}=\beta_{2}(\bm{\theta}_{k})\,\vee\,\beta_{2}(\bm{\theta}_{k+1})
. By definition of
\beta_{2}(\cdot)
in Corollary
1
, we can control
\beta_{2}(\bm{\theta}_{k})\,\vee\,\beta_{2}(\bm{\theta}_{k+1})
with controlling
\lVert\bm{\theta}_{k}\rVert_{2,\infty}\,\vee\,\lVert\bm{\theta}_{k+1}\rVert_{2,\infty}
as
\left(\lVert\bm{\theta}_{k}\rVert_{2,\infty}\,\vee\,\lVert\bm{\theta}_{k+1}\rVert_{2,\infty}\right)\lesssim\lVert\bm{\theta}-\bm{\theta}_{k}\rVert+\lVert\bm{\theta}\rVert_{2,\infty}+1
. Using iterates-norm bound and setting

\rho(\bm{\theta})=\Big(\frac{2\,d\,\sqrt{T\,d}\,R^{3}}{\sqrt{H}}+\frac{T\,R^{2}}{4}\Big)\,\alpha(\bm{\theta})^{2}
with

\alpha(\bm{\theta}):=3\,d\,\sqrt{d}\,R^{2}\,\left[3\,\sqrt{T}\,R^{3}\left(3\,\lVert\bm{\theta}-\bm{\theta}_{0}\rVert+\lVert\bm{\theta}\rVert_{2,\infty}\right)+2\,\sqrt{T}\,R\right]
,
satisfies the desired condition for the Descent Lemma completing the proof.

6.2
Generalization analysis

In order to bound the expected generalization gap, we leverage the algorithmic stability framework. To begin, consider the leave-one-out (loo) training loss
\widehat{L}^{\neg i}(\bm{\theta}):=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j\neq i}\ell_{j}(\bm{\theta})
for
i\in[n]
, where
\ell_{j}(\bm{\theta}):=\ell(y_{j}\Phi({\bm{X}}_{j};\bm{\theta}))
denotes the
j
-th sample loss. With these, define the loo model updates of GD on the loo loss for
\eta>0
:

\bm{\theta}_{k+1}^{\neg i}:=\bm{\theta}_{k}^{\neg i}-\eta\nabla\widehat{L}^{\neg i}(\bm{\theta}_{k}^{\neg i}),~k\geq 0,\qquad\bm{\theta}_{0}^{\neg i}=\bm{\theta}_{0}\,.

The following lemma relates expected generalization loss to average model stability for any
G
-Lipschitz loss.

Lemma 3

(
Lei & Ying 2020
, Thm. 2)
.

For
G
-Lipschitz loss and for all iterates
K
, it holds that
\mathbb{E}\Big[L(\bm{\theta}_{K})-\widehat{L}(\bm{\theta}_{K})\Big]\leq 2G\cdot\mathbb{E}\Big[\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|\bm{\theta}_{K}-\bm{\theta}_{K}^{\neg i}\|\Big]\,.

To bound the average model-stability on the r.h.s of the lemma’s inequality, we use GD expansiveness. Specifically applying
(
Taheri & Thrampoulidis 2023
, Lemma B.1.)
to our setting, gives
\forall\bm{\theta},\,\bm{\theta}^{\prime}
:

\displaystyle\lVert(\bm{\theta}-\eta\nabla\widehat{L}(\bm{\theta}))-(\bm{\theta}^{\prime}-\eta\nabla\widehat{L}(\bm{\theta}^{\prime}))\rVert\leq\max_{\alpha\in[0,1]}\Big\{\Big(1+\frac{\eta\beta_{3}(\bm{\theta}_{\alpha})}{\sqrt{H}}\widehat{L}(\bm{\theta}_{\alpha})\Big)\,\,\vee\,\,\eta\beta_{2}(\bm{\theta}_{\alpha})\Big\}\lVert\bm{\theta}-\bm{\theta}^{\prime}\rVert\,,

(19)

where,
\bm{\theta}_{\alpha}=\alpha\bm{\theta}+(1-\alpha)\bm{\theta}^{\prime}
,
\alpha\in[0,1]
. Using this and gradient self-boundedness from Corollary
1
, we get:

\displaystyle\left\|\bm{\theta}_{k+1}-\bm{\theta}_{k+1}^{\neg i}\right\|\leq\max_{\alpha\in[0,1]}\Big\{\Big(1+\frac{\eta\beta_{3}(\bm{\theta}_{k_{\alpha}}^{\neg i})}{\sqrt{H}}\widehat{L}^{\neg i}(\bm{\theta}_{k_{\alpha}}^{\neg i})\Big)\,\,\vee\,\,\eta\beta_{2}(\bm{\theta}_{k_{\alpha}}^{\neg i})\Big\}\cdot\left\|\bm{\theta}_{k}-\bm{\theta}_{k}^{\neg i}\right\|+\frac{\eta\beta_{1}(\bm{\theta}_{k})}{n}\ell_{i}\left(\bm{\theta}_{k}\right),

(20)

where
\bm{\theta}_{k_{\alpha}}^{\neg i}:=\alpha\bm{\theta}_{k}+(1-\alpha)\bm{\theta}_{k}^{\neg i}
for
\alpha\in[0,1]
.
Further using the bounded iterates-norm property from the training analysis, we can control
\beta_{2}(\bm{\theta}_{k_{\alpha}}^{\neg i})\leq\tilde{\beta}_{2}(\bm{\theta})
and
\beta_{3}(\bm{\theta}_{k_{\alpha}}^{\neg i})\leq\tilde{\beta}_{3}(\bm{\theta})
making them independent of
k
(See Lemma
11
for the definitions of
\tilde{\beta}_{2}(\cdot),\tilde{\beta}_{3}(\cdot)
). In order to invoke the Descent Lemma, we set the step-size same as in the training analysis. Thus, (
20
) becomes:

\displaystyle\left\|\bm{\theta}_{k+1}-\bm{\theta}_{k+1}^{\neg i}\right\|\leq\Big((1+\frac{\eta\tilde{\beta}_{3}(\bm{\theta})}{\sqrt{H}})\max_{\alpha\in[0,1]}\widehat{L}^{\neg i}(\bm{\theta}_{k_{\alpha}}^{\neg i})\Big)\left\|\bm{\theta}_{k}-\bm{\theta}_{k}^{\neg i}\right\|+\frac{\eta\beta_{1}(\bm{\theta}_{k})}{n}\ell_{i}\left(\bm{\theta}_{k}\right)\,.

(21)

As in the training analysis, we can control the loo empirical loss
\widehat{L}^{\neg i}
for any point on the line
[\bm{\theta}_{k},\bm{\theta}_{k}^{\neg i}]
of two sufficiently close points satisfying
\sqrt{H}\geq 2\,\left(\beta_{3}(\bm{\theta}_{k})\,\vee\,\beta_{3}(\bm{\theta}_{k}^{\neg i})\right)\,\|\bm{\theta}_{k}-\bm{\theta}_{k}^{\neg i}\|^{2}
. Using Prop.
4
, Eq. (
21
) becomes

\displaystyle\left\|\bm{\theta}_{k+1}-\bm{\theta}_{k+1}^{\neg i}\right\|\leq(1+\alpha_{k,i})\left\|\bm{\theta}_{k}-\bm{\theta}_{k}^{\neg i}\right\|+\frac{\eta\tilde{\beta}_{1}(\bm{\theta})}{n}\ell_{i}\left(\bm{\theta}_{k}\right),

(22)

where
\alpha_{k,i}:=\frac{4\eta\tilde{\beta}_{3}(\bm{\theta})}{3\sqrt{H}}\left(\widehat{L}^{\neg i}(\bm{\theta}_{k})+\widehat{L}^{\neg i}(\bm{\theta}_{k}^{\neg i})\right)
and
\beta_{1}(\bm{\theta}_{k})\leq\tilde{\beta}_{1}(\bm{\theta})
similar to
\beta_{2}(\cdot),\beta_{3}(\cdot)
using bounded iterates-norm. Unrolling the iterates in (
22
), summing over
i\in[n]
and using training regret bounds, we have the following average model stability bound for any iterate
K
:

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left\|\bm{\theta}_{K}-\bm{\theta}_{K}^{\neg i}\right\|\leq\frac{2\eta\tilde{\beta}_{1}(\bm{\theta})}{n}\left(2\,K\widehat{L}(\bm{\theta})+\frac{9\|\bm{\theta}-\bm{\theta}_{0}\|^{2}}{4\eta}\right)\,,

Combining this with an application of Lemma
3
for our objective, which is
G\leq\tilde{\beta}_{1}(\bm{\theta})
-Lipschitz from Corollary
1
, and using
\eta\leq\frac{1}{\rho(\bm{\theta})}\leq\frac{1}{(\tilde{\beta}_{1}(\bm{\theta}))^{2}}
, we get the desired generalization gap stated in Thm.
2
.

7
Concluding remarks

We studied convergence and generalization of GD for training a multi-head attention layer in a classification task.
Our training and generalization bounds hold under an appropriate realizability condition asking for the existence of an a target model
\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}
achieving good train loss while being sufficiently close to initialization. In particular, from the condition on the number of heads
H
in (
3
), we need
\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}
at most

{\tilde{\mathcal{O}}}\left(d^{-2/3}T^{-1/6}R^{-5/3}H^{1/6}\right)

far from initialization (provided
\lVert\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}\rVert_{2,\infty}=\mathcal{O}\left(1\right)
). In Sec.
4.3
we showed that such a model exists if the initialization is chosen appropriately. Specifically it suffices that
\lVert\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}^{(0)}\rVert_{2,\infty}=\mathcal{O}\left(1\right)
, the model output at initialization is
{\tilde{\mathcal{O}}}\left(1\right)
-bounded and that the data are linearly separable with margin
\gamma
with respect to the NTK features of the model at initialization. Then,

\mathcal{O}\left(d^{4}TR^{10}\operatorname{polylog}(n)/\gamma^{6}\right)

number of heads guarantee that
\Theta(n)
GD steps result in train and test loss bounds
{\tilde{\mathcal{O}}}\left(1/({\eta\gamma^{2}n})\right)
. In Sec.
5
we applied our results to a tokenized-mixture model. We showed that after one randomized gradient step from
\mathbf{0}
, the model satisfies the above conditions for
good
intialization. For this initialization, we computed the NTK margin
\gamma_{\star}
which in turn governs the guaranteed rate of convergence and generalization based on our general bounds. This opens several interesting questions for future work.

First, does random initialization of attention weights satisfy NTK separability, and if so, what is the corresponding margin? Second, are there other initialization strategies that guarantee the realizability conditions are satisfied? Here, note that our conditions for
good
initialization are only shown to be sufficient for realizability leaving room for improvements.
Third, how suboptimal is the best NTK margin (among other potential natural initializations) compared to the model’s global margin
\arg\max_{\|\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}\|=\sqrt{2}}\min_{i\in[n]}y_{i}\widetilde{\Phi}({\bm{X}}_{i};\widetilde{\bm{\theta}})
?
In Proposition
3

we showed for the data model
DM1
that there exists single-head attention model
\bm{\theta}_{\text{opt}}=(\bm{U}_{\text{opt}},\bm{W}_{\text{opt}})
with
\|\bm{\theta}_{\text{opt}}\|=\sqrt{2}
such that
y\Phi({\bm{X}};\bm{U}_{\text{opt}},\Gamma_{\epsilon}\cdot\bm{W}_{\text{opt}})=\frac{S\sqrt{T}}{\sqrt{2}}\left((1-\epsilon)-2\epsilon Z_{\mu}/S\right)
for all
\Gamma_{\epsilon}\gtrsim\frac{\log((\zeta^{-1}-1)/\epsilon)}{S}
and any
\epsilon\in(0,1)
(see App.
E
). In particular, as
\epsilon\rightarrow 0
and
\Gamma_{\epsilon}\rightarrow\infty
(for which the softmax map gets saturated and attends to tokens with highest relevance score) the achieved margin approaches
\gamma_{\text{attn}}:=S\sqrt{T}/\sqrt{2}
, which is independent of the sparsity level
\zeta
. In particular,
\gamma_{\text{attn}}\geq\gamma_{\star}\geq\gamma_{\text{lin}}
and the gap increases with decreasing sparsity. Is it possible to establish finite-time convergence bounds to models with margin
\approx\gamma_{\text{attn}}
under appropriate initialization? How is the answer affected by the fact that the optimal attention weights in this case are diverging in norm (
\Gamma_{\epsilon}\rightarrow\infty
)? Using our approach, we argued in Sec.
5.3
that the key challenge is the saturation of norm of
\bm{W}_{\text{opt}}
(
\Gamma_{\epsilon}
), which does not allow the appropriate realizability condition to hold (at least for
\mathbf{0}
initialization). Finally, it is interesting to consider other data models for which multiple heads are necessary to interpolate the data.

\displaystyle\leq 6\,d^{2}\,R^{4}\,\sum_{t=1}^{T}\max_{\tau\in[T]}\bm{x}_{\tau}^{\top}\bm{u}_{t}+2\,d\,\sqrt{T\,d}\,R^{3}

\displaystyle\leq 6\,d^{2}\,R^{4}\,\sum_{t=1}^{T}\max_{\tau\in[T]}\lVert\bm{x}_{\tau}\rVert\lVert\bm{u}_{t}\rVert+2\,d\,\sqrt{T\,d}\,R^{3}

\displaystyle\leq 6\,\sqrt{T}\,d^{2}\,R^{5}\,\lVert\bm{U}\rVert_{F}+2\,d\,\sqrt{T\,d}\,R^{3}\,,

(60)

where in the second and the last inequalities we used Cauchy-Schwartz inequality.

∎

We now derive bounds for the multi-head attention model.

.

Recall the multihead model in Eq. (

\displaystyle\widetilde{\Phi}({\bm{X}};\widetilde{\bm{\theta}})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{H}}\sum_{h=1}^{H}\langle\bm{U}_{h},\bm{\varphi}({\bm{X}}\bm{W}_{h}{\bm{X}}^{\top}){\bm{X}}\rangle\,.

(61)

The following are true under Assumption

Proof.

From Equation (

\displaystyle\lVert\nabla_{\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}}\widetilde{\Phi}({\bm{X}};\widetilde{\bm{\theta}})\rVert^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{H}\sum_{h=1}^{H}\Big(2\,\sqrt{T}\,R^{3}\,\lVert\bm{U}_{h}\rVert_{F}+\sqrt{T}\,R\Big)^{2}

Denote
{\bm{v}}^{\top}=\begin{bNiceMatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{H(Td+d^{2})}

\displaystyle\langle{\bm{v}},\nabla_{\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}}^{2}\widetilde{\Phi}({\bm{X}};\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}){\bm{v}}\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{H}}\,\Big(6\,\sqrt{T}\,d^{2}\,R^{5}\,\max_{h\in[H]}\lVert\bm{U}_{h}\rVert_{F}+2\,d\,\sqrt{T\,d}\,R^{3}\Big)\,.

Proof of Corollary

: Objective’s Gradient/Hessian

Corollary
1
follows immediately by the result of Lemma

below and using a more relaxed bound
\max_{h\in[H]}\lVert\bm{U}_{h}\rVert_{F}\leq\lVert\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}\rVert_{2,\infty}
.

Lemma 6

(Tight version of Corollary
1
)
.

Let Assumption
1
hold and we use logistic loss function. Then, the following are true for the loss gradient and Hessian:

1.

\begin{aligned} \lVert\nabla\widehat{L}(\widetilde{\bm{\theta}})\rVert\leq\sqrt{T}\,R\,\left(2\,R^{2}\,\max_{h\in[H]}\lVert\bm{U}_{h}\rVert_{F}+1\right)\widehat{L}(\widetilde{\bm{\theta}})\,\end{aligned}
.

2.

\begin{aligned} \lVert\nabla^{2}\widehat{L}(\widetilde{\bm{\theta}})\rVert&\leq\frac{2\,d\,\sqrt{T\,d}\,R^{3}}{\sqrt{H}}\,\left(3\,\sqrt{d}\,R^{2}\,\max_{h\in[H]}\lVert\bm{U}_{h}\rVert_{F}+1\right)+\frac{T\,R^{2}}{4}\,\left(2\,R^{2}\,\max_{h\in[H]}\lVert\bm{U}_{h}\rVert_{F}+1\right)^{2}\,.\end{aligned}

3.

\begin{aligned} \lambda_{\text{min}}(\nabla^{2}\widehat{L}(\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}))\geq-\frac{2\,d\,\sqrt{T\,d}\,R^{3}}{\sqrt{H}}\,\left(3\,\sqrt{d}\,R^{2}\,\max_{h\in[H]}\lVert\bm{U}_{h}\rVert_{F}+1\right)\,\widehat{L}(\widetilde{\bm{\theta}})\,.\end{aligned}

Proof.

The loss gradient is derived as follows,

\displaystyle\nabla\widehat{L}(\widetilde{\bm{\theta}})=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\ell^{\prime}(y_{i}\,\widetilde{\Phi}({\bm{X}}_{i};\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}))\,y_{i}\,\nabla_{\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}}\widetilde{\Phi}({\bm{X}}_{i};\widetilde{\bm{\theta}})\,.

Recalling that
y_{i}\in\{\pm 1\}
, we can write

\displaystyle\lVert\nabla\widehat{L}(\widetilde{\bm{\theta}})\rVert

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\lVert\sum_{i=1}^{n}\ell^{\prime}(y_{i}\,\widetilde{\Phi}({\bm{X}}_{i};\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}))\,y_{i}\,\nabla_{\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}}\widetilde{\Phi}({\bm{X}}_{i};\widetilde{\bm{\theta}})\rVert\leq\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left\lvert\ell^{\prime}(y_{i}\,\widetilde{\Phi}({\bm{X}}_{i};\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}))\right\rvert\,\lVert\nabla_{\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}}\widetilde{\Phi}({\bm{X}}_{i};\widetilde{\bm{\theta}})\rVert\,.

Thus, using Lemma
5
to bound the norm of the model’s gradient:

\displaystyle\lVert\nabla\widehat{L}(\widetilde{\bm{\theta}})\rVert\leq\sqrt{T}\,R\,\left(2\,R^{2}\,\max_{h\in[H]}\lVert\bm{U}_{h}\rVert_{F}+1\right)\widehat{L}(\widetilde{\bm{\theta}})\,.

(64)

For the Hessian of loss, note that

\displaystyle\nabla^{2}\widehat{L}(\widetilde{\bm{\theta}})=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\ell^{\prime\prime}(y_{i}\,\widetilde{\Phi}({\bm{X}}_{i};\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}))\,\nabla_{\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}}\widetilde{\Phi}({\bm{X}}_{i};\widetilde{\bm{\theta}})\,\nabla_{\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}}\widetilde{\Phi}({\bm{X}}_{i};\widetilde{\bm{\theta}})^{\top}+\ell^{\prime}(y_{i}\,\widetilde{\Phi}({\bm{X}}_{i};\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}))\,y_{i}\,\nabla_{\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}}^{2}\widetilde{\Phi}({\bm{X}}_{i};\widetilde{\bm{\theta}})\,.

(65)

It follows that

\displaystyle\lVert\nabla^{2}\widehat{L}(\widetilde{\bm{\theta}})\rVert

\displaystyle=\lVert\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\ell^{\prime\prime}(y_{i}\,\widetilde{\Phi}({\bm{X}}_{i};\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}))\,\nabla_{\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}}\widetilde{\Phi}({\bm{X}}_{i};\widetilde{\bm{\theta}})\,\nabla_{\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}}\widetilde{\Phi}({\bm{X}}_{i};\widetilde{\bm{\theta}})^{\top}+\ell^{\prime}(y_{i}\,\Phi({\bm{X}}_{i};\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}))\,y_{i}\,\nabla_{\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}}^{2}\widetilde{\Phi}({\bm{X}}_{i};\widetilde{\bm{\theta}})\rVert

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left\lvert\ell^{\prime}(y_{i}\,\widetilde{\Phi}({\bm{X}}_{i};\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}))\right\rvert\,\lVert\nabla_{\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}}^{2}\widetilde{\Phi}({\bm{X}}_{i};\widetilde{\bm{\theta}})\rVert+\left\lvert\ell^{\prime\prime}(y_{i}\,\widetilde{\Phi}({\bm{X}}_{i};\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}))\right\rvert\,\lVert\nabla_{\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}}\widetilde{\Phi}({\bm{X}}_{i};\widetilde{\bm{\theta}})\rVert^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2\,d\,\sqrt{T\,d}\,R^{3}}{\sqrt{H}}\,\left(3\,\sqrt{d}\,R^{2}\,\max_{h\in[H]}\lVert\bm{U}_{h}\rVert_{F}+1\right)+\frac{T\,R^{2}}{4}\,\left(2\,R^{2}\,\max_{h\in[H]}\lVert\bm{U}_{h}\rVert_{F}+1\right)^{2}\,.

(66)

To lower-bound the minimum eigenvalue of the Hessian of loss, note that
\ell(\cdot)
is convex and thus
\ell^{\prime\prime}(\cdot)\geq 0
. Therefore, the first term in (
65
) is positive semi-definite and the second term can be lower-bounded as follows,

\displaystyle\lambda_{\text{min}}(\nabla^{2}\widehat{L}(\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}))

\displaystyle\geq-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lVert\ell^{\prime}(y_{i}\,\widetilde{\Phi}({\bm{X}}_{i};\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}))\,y_{i}\,\nabla_{\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}}^{2}\widetilde{\Phi}({\bm{X}}_{i};\widetilde{\bm{\theta}})\rVert

\displaystyle\geq-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left\lvert\ell^{\prime}(y_{i}\,\widetilde{\Phi}({\bm{X}}_{i};\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}))\right\rvert\,\lVert\nabla_{\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}}^{2}\widetilde{\Phi}({\bm{X}}_{i};\widetilde{\bm{\theta}})\rVert

\displaystyle\geq-\frac{2\,d\,\sqrt{T\,d}\,R^{3}}{\sqrt{H}}\,\left(3\,\sqrt{d}\,R^{2}\,\max_{h\in[H]}\lVert\bm{U}_{h}\rVert_{F}+1\right)\,\widehat{L}(\widetilde{\bm{\theta}})\,.

∎

B.1
Preliminaries

Throughout this section we drop the
\widetilde{\cdot}
in
\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}
and
\widetilde{\Phi}({\bm{X}}_{i};\widetilde{\bm{\theta}})
as everything refers to the full model. Moreover,
\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}^{(K)}
and
\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}^{(0)}
are denoted by
\bm{\theta}_{K}
and
\bm{\theta}_{0}
.

The proof of both the training and generalization analysis follows the high-level steps outlined in
Taheri & Thrampoulidis 2023
. However, our analysis focuses on the self-attention model, which differs from the two-layer perceptron studied in the referenced work. Notably, we train both the attention weights and the classifier head, whereas
Taheri & Thrampoulidis 2023
assumes fixed outer layer weights. This introduces a new challenge as the smoothness and curvature of the objective function at point
\bm{\theta}
become dependent on
\bm{\theta}
itself (see Corollary
1
). Consequently, we make careful adjustments in the proof to account for this challenge.

Our analysis critically uses the following property of the loss objective from Corollary
1
:

\forall\bm{\theta}\,:\,\,\,\lambda_{\min}\left(\nabla^{2}\widehat{L}(\bm{\theta})\right)\geq-\kappa(\bm{\theta})\cdot\widehat{L}(\bm{\theta})
,
\kappa(\bm{\theta}):=\frac{\beta_{3}(\bm{\theta})}{\sqrt{H}}\,.

Note from the definition of
\beta_{3}(\cdot)
that

\forall\bm{\theta}_{1},\bm{\theta}_{2}\,:\,\,\,\max_{\bm{\theta}\in[\bm{\theta}_{1},\bm{\theta}_{2}]}\beta_{3}(\bm{\theta})=\beta_{3}(\bm{\theta}_{1})\,\vee\,\beta_{3}(\bm{\theta}_{2})\,.

Thus, the above property of the loss implies the following
local self-bounded weak convexity
property on the line
[\bm{\theta}_{1},\bm{\theta}_{2}]
:

\displaystyle\forall\bm{\theta}_{1},\bm{\theta}_{2}\,:\,\,\,\min_{\bm{\theta}\in[\bm{\theta}_{1},\bm{\theta}_{2}]}\lambda_{\min}\left(\nabla^{2}\widehat{L}(\bm{\theta})\right)\geq-\frac{\beta_{3}(\bm{\theta}_{1})\,\vee\,\beta_{3}(\bm{\theta}_{2})}{\sqrt{H}}\cdot\widehat{L}(\bm{\theta}).

(67)

In turn, Equation (
67
) can be used to prove that the loss satisfies a generalized local quasi-convexity (GLQC) property as formalized in the proposition below. The proposition is only a slight modification of
(
Taheri & Thrampoulidis 2023
, Prop. 8)
. While, a direct application of their result is not possible since the self-bounded weak convexity property in (
67
) holds only locally on the line
[\bm{\theta}_{1},\bm{\theta}_{2}]
, an inspection of their proof shows that this is sufficient.

B.2
Proof of Proposition
4

First, we restate the proposition below for the reader’s convenience.

Proposition 6

(GLQC property)
.

Let
\bm{\theta}_{1},\bm{\theta}_{2}
be points sufficiently close to each other, such that

{2\,\left(\beta_{3}(\bm{\theta}_{1})\,\vee\,\beta_{3}(\bm{\theta}_{2})\right)}\,\|\bm{\theta}_{1}-\bm{\theta}_{2}\|^{2}\leq\sqrt{H}\,.

Then, the following generalized local quasi-convexity (GLQC) property holds:

\displaystyle\max_{\bm{\theta}\in[\bm{\theta}_{1},\bm{\theta}_{2}]}\widehat{L}(\bm{\theta})\leq\frac{4}{3}\,\left(\widehat{L}(\bm{\theta}_{1})\,\vee\,\widehat{L}(\bm{\theta}_{2})\right).

Proof.

Although the loss
\widehat{L}
is not uniformly self-bounded weakly convex as assumed in
(
Taheri & Thrampoulidis 2023
, Prop. 8)
, an inspection of their proof shows that for every
\bm{\theta}_{1},\bm{\theta}_{2}
it suffices that the loss is locally self-bounded weakly convex. With this observation, we can apply their proposition with
\kappa\leftarrow\kappa(\bm{\theta}_{1})\,\vee\,\kappa(\bm{\theta}_{2})
, which gives the desired.
∎

B.3

The proof of Theorem

consists of several intermediate lemma, which we state and prove in this section.

Lemma 7

(Descent Lemma)
.

Let Assumption
1
hold. Then, for any iteration
k\geq 0
we have step-wise descent:

\displaystyle\widehat{L}(\bm{\theta}_{k+1})\leq\widehat{L}(\bm{\theta}_{k})-\frac{\eta}{2}\left\|\nabla\widehat{L}\left(\bm{\theta}_{k}\right)\right\|^{2},

(68)

provided
\eta\leq\frac{1}{\rho_{k}}
where
\rho_{k}
is the objective’s local smoothness parameter defined as below,

\displaystyle\rho_{k}:=\beta_{2}(\bm{\theta}_{k})\,\vee\,\beta_{2}(\bm{\theta}_{k+1}).

Proof.

By Taylor’s expansion, there exists a
\bm{\theta}^{\prime}\in\left[\bm{\theta}_{k},\bm{\theta}_{k+1}\right]
such that,

\displaystyle\hat{L}\left(\bm{\theta}_{k+1}\right)

\displaystyle=\widehat{L}\left(\bm{\theta}_{k}\right)+\left\langle\nabla\widehat{L}\left(\bm{\theta}_{k}\right),\bm{\theta}_{k+1}-\bm{\theta}_{k}\right\rangle+\frac{1}{2}\left\langle\bm{\theta}_{k+1}-\bm{\theta}_{k},\nabla^{2}\widehat{L}(\bm{\theta}^{\prime})\left(\bm{\theta}_{k+1}-\bm{\theta}_{k}\right)\right\rangle

\displaystyle\leq\widehat{L}\left(\bm{\theta}_{k}\right)+\left\langle\nabla\widehat{L}\left(\bm{\theta}_{k}\right),\bm{\theta}_{k+1}-\bm{\theta}_{k}\right\rangle+\frac{1}{2}\max_{\bm{\theta}^{\prime}\in\left[\bm{\theta}_{k},\bm{\theta}_{k+1}\right]}\left\|\nabla^{2}\widehat{L}(\bm{\theta}^{\prime})\right\|\cdot\left\|\bm{\theta}_{k+1}-\bm{\theta}_{k}\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\widehat{L}\left(\bm{\theta}_{k}\right)-\eta\left\|\nabla\widehat{L}\left(\bm{\theta}_{k}\right)\right\|^{2}+\frac{\eta^{2}\rho_{k}}{2}\cdot\left\|\nabla\widehat{L}\left(\bm{\theta}_{k}\right)\right\|^{2},

where the last step follows from Corollary
1
, and using
\max_{\bm{\theta}^{\prime}\in\left[\bm{\theta}_{k},\bm{\theta}_{k+1}\right]}\beta_{2}(\bm{\theta}^{\prime})=\beta_{2}(\bm{\theta})\,\vee\,\beta_{2}(\bm{\theta}_{k})=\rho_{k}
. For
\eta\leq\frac{1}{\rho_{k}}
, we conclude the claim.
∎

Lemma 8
.

Assume
\eta>0
such that step-wise descent (
68
) holds for all
k\in[K-1]
.
Then, for any
\bm{\theta}
, the following holds:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\widehat{L}(\bm{\theta}_{k})\leq\widehat{L}(\bm{\theta})+\frac{\lVert\bm{\theta}-\bm{\theta}_{0}\rVert^{2}}{2\eta K}+\frac{1}{2K}\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}\tau_{k}\lVert\bm{\theta}-\bm{\theta}_{k}\rVert^{2}\,,

(69)

where
\tau_{k}:=\frac{1}{\sqrt{H}}\left(\beta_{3}(\bm{\theta})\,\vee\,\beta_{3}(\bm{\theta}_{k})\right)\max_{\alpha\in[0,1]}\,\widehat{L}(\bm{\theta}_{{k_{\alpha}}})
.

Proof.

By Taylor’s theorem, for
\bm{\theta}_{k_{\alpha}}:=\alpha\bm{\theta}_{k}+(1-\alpha)\bm{\theta},\,\,\alpha\in[0,1]
we know that:

\displaystyle\widehat{L}(\bm{\theta})\geq\widehat{L}(\bm{\theta}_{k})+\langle\nabla\widehat{L}(\bm{\theta}_{k}),\bm{\theta}-\bm{\theta}_{k}\rangle+\frac{1}{2}\lambda_{\min}\left(\nabla^{2}\widehat{L}(\bm{\theta}_{k_{\alpha}})\right)\lVert\bm{\theta}-\bm{\theta}_{k}\rVert^{2}.

Using (
68
), we get:

\displaystyle\widehat{L}(\bm{\theta}_{k+1})

\displaystyle\leq\widehat{L}(\bm{\theta})+\langle\nabla\widehat{L}(\bm{\theta}_{k}),\bm{\theta}_{k}-\bm{\theta}\rangle-\frac{1}{2}\lambda_{\min}\left(\nabla^{2}\widehat{L}(\bm{\theta}_{k_{\alpha}})\right)\lVert\bm{\theta}-\bm{\theta}_{k}\rVert^{2}-\frac{\eta}{2}\lVert\nabla\widehat{L}(\bm{\theta}_{k})\rVert^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\widehat{L}(\bm{\theta})+\frac{\lVert\bm{\theta}-\bm{\theta}_{k}\rVert^{2}}{2\eta}-\frac{\lVert\bm{\theta}-\bm{\theta}_{k+1}\rVert^{2}}{2\eta}+\frac{1}{2}\tau_{k}\lVert\bm{\theta}-\bm{\theta}_{k}\rVert^{2},

(70)

where the last step follows by completion of squares using
\bm{\theta}_{k+1}-\bm{\theta}_{k}=-\eta\nabla\widehat{L}(\bm{\theta}_{k})
, and also uses Corollary
1
with
\tau_{k}:=\max_{\alpha\in[0,1]}\tau_{k_{\alpha}}
, where
\tau_{k_{\alpha}}=\frac{\beta_{3}(\bm{\theta}_{k_{\alpha}})}{\sqrt{H}}\widehat{L}(\bm{\theta}_{k_{\alpha}})
. Telescoping in (
70

k=0,...,K-1
, we get the desired.

∎

Next, we use the generalized local quasi-convexity property (Proposition
4

Lemma 9
.

Suppose the assumptions of Lemma
8
hold. Moreover, assume for all

(71)

Proof.

We have

\min_{\bm{\theta}^{\prime}\in[\bm{\theta},\bm{\theta}_{k}]}\lambda_{\text{min}}(\nabla^{2}\widehat{L}(\bm{\theta}^{\prime}))\geq-\frac{\beta_{3}(\bm{\theta})\,\vee\,\beta_{3}(\bm{\theta}_{k})}{\sqrt{H}}\max_{\bm{\theta}^{\prime}\in[\bm{\theta},\bm{\theta}_{k}]}\widehat{L}(\bm{\theta}^{\prime})

1

Thus, using Proposition
4
, we can control
\max_{\alpha\in[0,1]}\widehat{L}(\bm{\theta}_{k_{\alpha}})

Specifically, by assumption we have for all

that

\sqrt{H}\geq 2\left(\beta_{3}(\bm{\theta})\,\vee\,\beta_{3}(\bm{\theta}_{k})\right)\,\|\bm{\theta}-\bm{\theta}_{k}\|^{2}=2\max_{\alpha\in[0,1]}\beta_{3}(\bm{\theta}_{k_{\alpha}})\,\|\bm{\theta}-\bm{\theta}_{k}\|^{2}\,.

\max_{\alpha\in[0,1]}\widehat{L}(\bm{\theta}_{k_{\alpha}})\leq\frac{4}{3}\,\max\{\widehat{L}(\bm{\theta}_{k}),\widehat{L}(\bm{\theta})\}\leq\frac{4}{3}\widehat{L}(\bm{\theta}_{k})+\frac{4}{3}\widehat{L}(\bm{\theta})\,.

\displaystyle\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\widehat{L}(\bm{\theta}_{k})

\displaystyle\leq\widehat{L}(\bm{\theta})+\frac{\lVert\bm{\theta}-\bm{\theta}_{0}\rVert^{2}}{2\eta K}+\frac{2}{3K}\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}\frac{1}{\sqrt{H}}\left(\beta_{3}(\bm{\theta})\,\vee\,\beta_{3}(\bm{\theta}_{k})\right)(\widehat{L}(\bm{\theta}_{k})+\widehat{L}(\bm{\theta}))\lVert\bm{\theta}-\bm{\theta}_{k}\rVert^{2}

(72)

where we use the condition on

Lemma 10

(Iterates-norm bound)
.

Suppose the descent property (
68

\displaystyle\|\bm{\theta}-\bm{\theta}_{0}\|^{2}\geq\max\{\eta K\widehat{L}(\bm{\theta}),\eta\widehat{L}(\bm{\theta}_{0})\}\,.

\displaystyle{\sqrt{H}}\geq 36\,d\,\sqrt{T\,d}\,R^{3}\,\left(3\,\sqrt{d}\,R^{2}\,\bigg(3\lVert\bm{\theta}-\bm{\theta}_{0}\rVert+\lVert\bm{\theta}\rVert_{2,\infty}\bigg)+1\right)\,\|\bm{\theta}-\bm{\theta}_{0}\|^{2}\,,

Proof.

Denote

Start by recalling from Eq. (

\displaystyle A_{k+1}^{2}\leq A_{k}^{2}+2\eta\widehat{L}(\bm{\theta})-2\eta\widehat{L}(\bm{\theta}_{k+1})+\eta\,(\max_{\alpha\in[0,1]}\tau_{k_{\alpha}})\,A_{k}^{2}\,,

\displaystyle A_{j+1}^{2}

Theorem 3

(Restatement of Thm.

Fix any

Proof.

Define

\displaystyle\rho_{k}:=\beta_{2}(\bm{\theta}_{k})\,\vee\,\beta_{2}(\bm{\theta}_{k+1})\,.

\rho_{k}

Corollary

Corollary 4

(Generalization loss under realizability)
.

Let boundedness Assumption

hold. Also let realizability assumption
2
holds almost surely over the data distribution. Fix

. Assume any

\displaystyle\sqrt{H}\geq 256\,d\,\sqrt{T\,d}\,R^{3}\,\left(3\,\sqrt{d}\,R^{2}\,\Big(3g_{0}(\frac{1}{K})+g(\frac{1}{K})\Big)+1\right)\,g_{0}(\frac{1}{K})^{2}\,.

Then the expected generalization gap at iteration

of GD satisfies,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Big[L(\bm{\theta}_{K})-\widehat{L}(\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}_{K})\Big]\leq\frac{17\,g_{0}(\frac{1}{K})^{2}}{\eta\,n}\,.

Proof.

According to Assumption

, for any sufficiently small

, there exists a

where in the second inequality we used the fact that

The proposition below shows that starting from a

Proposition 7

Suppose

\bm{\theta}_{0}

For the latter, we used triangle inequality and the rough bound

\displaystyle t\in\mathcal{R}^{c}:[\bm{r}_{t}]_{t^{\prime}}=\begin{cases}S^{4}+S^{2}(\bm{\mu}_{y}^{\top}\bm{z}_{t})+S^{2}(\sum_{\ell}\bm{\nu}_{\ell}^{\top}\bm{z}_{t})&\text{,}\,t^{\prime}\in\mathcal{R}\\
(\bm{\mu}_{+}^{\top}\bm{z}_{t})(\bm{\mu}_{+}^{\top}\bm{z}_{t^{\prime}})+(\bm{\mu}_{-}^{\top}\bm{z}_{t})(\bm{\mu}_{-}^{\top}\bm{z}_{t^{\prime}})&\text{,}\,t^{\prime}\in\mathcal{R}^{c}\\
+\sum_{\ell}(\bm{\nu}_{\ell}^{\top}\bm{z}_{t})(\bm{\mu}_{+}^{\top}\bm{z}_{t^{\prime}})+\sum_{\ell}(\bm{\nu}_{\ell}^{\top}\bm{z}_{t})(\bm{\mu}_{-}^{\top}\bm{z}_{t^{\prime}})+S^{2}(\bm{\mu}_{+}+\bm{\mu}_{-})^{\top}\bm{z}_{t^{\prime}}\\
\end{cases}

Therefore, using the noise bound assumptions, we have

\displaystyle t\in\mathcal{R}^{c}:[\bm{r}_{t}]_{t^{\prime}}\begin{cases}\geq S^{4}-S^{2}(Z_{\mu}+Z_{\nu})&\text{,}\,t^{\prime}\in\mathcal{R}\\
\leq 2Z_{\mu}^{2}+2Z_{\mu}Z_{\nu}+2S^{2}Z_{\mu}&\text{,}\,t^{\prime}\in\mathcal{R}^{c}\,.\end{cases}

(128)

Combining the above two cases, specifically Equations (

) and (

), we find that for all

the relevance-score vectors

\displaystyle t\in[T]:[\bm{r}_{t}]_{t^{\prime}}\begin{cases}\geq S^{4}-S^{2}(Z_{\mu}+Z_{\nu}):=A&\text{,}\,t^{\prime}\in\mathcal{R}\\
\leq 2(Z_{\mu}^{2}+Z_{\mu}Z_{\nu}+S^{2}Z_{\mu}):=B&\text{,}\,t^{\prime}\in\mathcal{R}^{c}\,,\end{cases}

(129)

where we defined the parameters
A
and
B

Z_{\mu}=Z_{\nu}\leq\frac{S^{2}}{8}\implies A\geq\frac{3}{4}S^{4}>\frac{1.25}{4}S^{4}\geq 2B\,.

Thus, the conditions of Lemma

L=|\mathcal{R}|=\zeta T

Applying the lemma we can immediately conclude that for

\Gamma_{*}\geq\frac{8}{3S^{4}}\log\left(\frac{\zeta^{-1}-1}{\epsilon}\right)\geq\frac{2}{A}\log\left(\frac{\zeta^{-1}-1}{\epsilon}\right)\,.

it holds:

\displaystyle\forall t\in[T]\,:\,0\leq 1-\sum_{t^{\prime}\in\mathcal{R}}\left[\bm{\varphi}(\Gamma_{*}\bm{r}_{t})\right]_{t^{\prime}}=\sum_{t^{\prime}\in\mathcal{R}^{c}}\left[\bm{\varphi}(\Gamma_{*}\bm{r}_{t})\right]_{t^{\prime}}\leq\epsilon\,.

(130)

Now, recall that

\bm{W}_{\star}=\widetilde{\Gamma}\bm{W}\qquad\text{for}\quad\widetilde{\Gamma}=\Gamma/(S^{2}\sqrt{2(M+1)})\,.

Thus, it holds for all
t\in[T]

\bm{\varphi}(\bm{x}_{t}^{\top}\bm{W}_{\star}{\bm{X}}^{T})=\bm{\varphi}(\widetilde{\Gamma}\bm{r}_{t})\,.

Combining this with (
130

\Gamma

\displaystyle\forall t\in[T]\,:\,0\leq 1-\sum_{t^{\prime}\in\mathcal{R}}\left[\bm{\varphi}(\bm{x}_{t}^{\top}\bm{W}_{\star}{\bm{X}}^{T})\right]_{t^{\prime}}=\sum_{t^{\prime}\in\mathcal{R}^{c}}\left[\bm{\varphi}(\bm{x}_{t}^{\top}\bm{W}_{\star}{\bm{X}}^{T})\right]_{t^{\prime}}\leq\epsilon\,.

(131)

In the rest of the proof, we use (
131
) to lower-bound the margin:

\displaystyle y\Phi({\bm{X}};\bm{\theta}_{\star})

\displaystyle=y\langle\bm{U}_{\star},\text{ATTN}({\bm{X}};\bm{W}_{\star})\rangle=\frac{y}{S\sqrt{2T}}\sum_{t\in[T]}\sum_{t^{\prime}\in[T]}\left[\phi(\bm{x}_{t}^{\top}\bm{W}_{\star}{\bm{X}}^{T})\right]_{t^{\prime}}(\bm{\mu}_{+}-\bm{\mu}_{-})^{\top}\bm{x}_{t^{\prime}}=:\frac{y}{S\sqrt{2T}}\sum_{t\in[T]}\psi_{t}\,,

where we defined
\psi_{t},t\in[T]
for convenience.
For any
t\in[T]
, we have

\displaystyle\psi_{t}

\displaystyle=\sum_{t^{\prime}\in\mathcal{R}}\left[\phi(\bm{x}_{t}^{\top}\bm{W}_{\star}{\bm{X}}^{T})\right]_{t^{\prime}}yS^{2}+\sum_{t^{\prime}\in\mathcal{R}^{c}}\left[\phi(\bm{x}_{t}^{\top}\bm{W}_{\star}{\bm{X}}^{T})\right]_{t^{\prime}}(\bm{\mu}_{+}-\bm{\mu}_{-})^{\top}\bm{z}_{t^{\prime}}

\displaystyle\geq yS^{2}\sum_{t^{\prime}\in\mathcal{R}}\left[\phi(\bm{x}_{t}^{\top}\bm{W}_{\star}{\bm{X}}^{T})\right]_{t^{\prime}}-2Z_{\mu}\sum_{t^{\prime}\in\mathcal{R}^{c}}\left[\phi(\bm{x}_{t}^{\top}\bm{W}_{\star}{\bm{X}}^{T})\right]_{t^{\prime}}

\displaystyle\geq yS^{2}(1-\epsilon)-2\epsilon Z_{\mu}\,.

Putting the last two displays together and using

Appendix H
Related work

This section elaborates on the paragraph on related work in Section
1
.

Transformers and Self-attention.

The landscape of NLP and machine translation was profoundly reshaped by the advent of Transformers, as pioneered by
Vaswani et al. 2017
building upon earlier investigations into self-attention as explored in the works of
Cheng et al. 2016
;
Lin et al. 2017
;
Parikh et al. 2016
. More recent developments include the transformative success of large language models like, LLaMA
(
Touvron et al. 2023
)
, ChatGPT
(
OpenAI 2022
)
, and GPT4
(
OpenAI 2023
)
. Despite this, the learning dynamics of the self-attention mechanism remain largely unknown. Some recent works have focused on understanding the expressive power
(
Baldi & Vershynin 2022
;
Dong et al. 2021
;
Yun et al. 2020a
;
Yun et al. 2020b
;
Sanford et al. 2023
;
Bietti et al. 2023
)
and memory capacity of the attention mechanism
(
Baldi & Vershynin 2022
;
Dong et al. 2021
;
Yun et al. 2020a
;
Yun et al. 2020b
;
Mahdavi et al. 2023
)
. Other aspects which are explored include obtaining convex reformulations for the training problem
(
Sahiner et al. 2022
;
Ergen et al. 2022
)
, studying sparse function representations in self-attention mechanism
(
Edelman et al. 2021
;
Likhosherstov et al. 2021
)
and investigating the inductive bias of masked self-attention models. Additionally, a sub-area gaining increasing popularity is theoretical investigation of in-context learning, e.g.
(
von Oswald et al. 2022
;
Zhang et al. 2023
;
Akyürek et al. 2023
;
Li et al. 2023b
)
.

Here, we discuss works that aim to understand the optimization and generalization dynamics of self-Attention or its variants.
Oymak et al. 2023
diverges from traditional self-Attention by focusing on a variant called prompt-Attention, aiming to gain understanding of prompt-tuning.
Lu et al. 2021
show that for a bag of words model, an attention model optimized with gradient flow for a topic classification task discovers the “topic” word as training proceeds. However, they don’t provide finite-time optimization-generalization rates.

Jelassi et al. 2022
shed light on how ViTs learn spatially localized patterns, even though this spatial structure is no longer explicitly represented after the image is split into patches. Specifically, they show that for binary classification using gradient-based methods from random initialization, transformers implicitly prefer the solution that learns the spatial structure of the dataset.
Li et al. 2023a
provided theoretical results on training three-layer ViTs for classification tasks for a similar data model as ours (tokenized-mixture data). They provide sample complexity for achieving zero generalization error, and also examined the degree of sparsity in attention maps when trained using SGD. Contemporaneous works include
(
Tian et al. 2023
;
Tarzanagh et al. 2023a
)
: The former presents SGD-dynamics of single-layer transformer for the task of next-token prediction by re-parameterizing the original problem in terms of the softmax and classification logit matrices, and analyzing their training dynamics instead. The latter studies the implicit bias of training the softmax weights
\bm{W}
with a fixed decoder
\bm{U}
via a regularization path analysis. All these works focus on a single attention head. Instead, we leverage the use of multiple heads to establish connections to the literature on GD training of overparameterized neural networks. Conceptually similar connections have also been studied by
Hron et al. 2020
who connect multi-head attention to a limiting Gaussian process when the number of heads increase to infinity. In contrast, we study performance in the more practical regime of finite number of heads and obtain
and obtain
finite-time
optimization and generalization bounds.

Overparameterized MLPs.

There has been an abundance of literature that discusses NN training convergence and generalization dynamics via an NTK type approach, e.g.
(
Allen-Zhu et al. 2019
;
Oymak & Soltanolkotabi 2020
;
Arora et al. 2019
;
Nguyen & Mondelli 2020
;
Banerjee et al. 2022
;
Nguyen et al. 2021
;
Zhu et al. 2023
)
. However, most of these works focus on GD dynamics on regression problems using square loss and relate the training convergence and generalization to the minimum eigenvalue of the Hessian of the NTK. On the other hand, relatively fewer works focus on classification with logistic loss under an NTK separable data assumption, and
(
Nitanda et al. 2019
;
Ji & Telgarsky 2020
;
Cao & Gu 2019
;
Chen et al. 2020
;
Telgarsky 2013
;
Taheri & Thrampoulidis 2023
)
are most relevant works that share overlapping ideas with our work. We refer the reader to these for a more thorough overview of the NTK-regime analysis for NNs.

Other than these,
Richards & Kuzborskij 2021
;
Richards & Rabbat 2021
;
Taheri & Thrampoulidis 2023
;
Lei et al. 2022
use algorithmic-stability based tools to understand the training dynamics and generalization of GD in shallow NNs.
Lei et al. 2022
;
Richards & Kuzborskij 2021
establish generalization bounds with polynomial width
\tilde{\Omega}(\text{poly}(n))
requirement while minimizing square loss. Here, we make use of the tools developed by
Taheri & Thrampoulidis 2023
who work with self-bounded Lipschitz loss functions, like logistic loss, similar to our analysis. The algorithmic stability arguments in the analysis of the above referenced papers are rooted on a technique to bound generalization gap by directly relating it to train loss based on the notion of average model stability introduced by
Lei & Ying 2020
. This technique has also been leveraged by
Schliserman & Koren 2022
to study linear logistic regression and
Nikolakakis et al. 2022
who establish generalization-risk bounds for Lipschitz function optimization with bounded optimal set.
