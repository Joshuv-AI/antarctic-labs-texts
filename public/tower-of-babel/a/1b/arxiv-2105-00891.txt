Hölder regularity of the nonlinear stochastic time-fractional slow and fast diffusion equations on ℝ d \mathbb{R}^{d}
Le Chen, Guannan Hu

Abstract




Abstract:
In this paper, we use
local fraction derivative
to show the Hölder
continuity of the solution to the following nonlinear time-fractional slow and fast diffusion
equation:

\displaystyle\left(\partial^{\beta}+\frac{\nu}{2}(-\Delta)^{\alpha/2}\right)u(t,x)=I_{t}^{\gamma}\left[\sigma\left(u(t,x)\right)\dot{W}(t,x)\right],\hskip 8.50012ptt>0,\>x\in\mathbb{R}^{d},

where
\dot{W}
is the space-time white noise,
\alpha\in(0,2]
,
\beta\in(0,2)
,
\gamma\geq 0
and

\nu>0
, under the condition that
2(\beta+\gamma)-1-d\beta/\alpha>0
. The case when

\beta+\gamma\leq 1
has been obtained in
[
8
]
. In this paper, we have removed this extra
condition, which in particular includes all cases for
\beta\in(0,2)
.

MSC 2010 subject classifications:
Primary 60H15. Secondary 60G60, 35R60.

Keywords:
Fractional Taylor theorem, local fractional derivative, nonlinear stochastic time-fractional slow and fast
diffusion equations, Hölder continuity,
Mittag-Leffler
function,
Fox-H
function.

Introduction

In this article, we study the Hölder continuity of the solution of the following nonlinear
stochastic time-fractional slow and fast diffusion equations:

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\left(\displaystyle\partial^{\beta}+\frac{\nu}{2}(-\Delta)^{\alpha/2}\right)u(t,x)=I_{t}^{\gamma}\left[\sigma\left(u(t,x)\right)\dot{W}(t,x)\right],&t>0,\>x\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\\[1.99997pt]
u(0,\cdot)=\mu&\text{if $\beta\in\>(0,1]$,}\\[1.99997pt]
\displaystyle u(0,\cdot)=\mu_{0},\quad\frac{\partial}{\partial t}u(0,\cdot)=\mu_{1}&\text{if $\beta\in\>(1,2)\>$,}\end{cases}

(1.1)

where
\nu>0
is the diffusion parameter, the function
\sigma:\mathbb{R}\mapsto\mathbb{R}
is globally Lipschitz
continuous, and
\dot{W}
denotes the space-time white noise. In this equation, there are three
fractional operators:
(-\Delta)^{\alpha/2}
,
\alpha\in(0,2]
, refers to the standard fractional
Laplacian in the spatial variable;
\partial^{\beta}
denotes the
Caputo fractional
derivative

\displaystyle\partial^{\beta}f(t):=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\frac{1}{\Gamma(m-\beta)}\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{d}\tau\>\frac{f^{(m)}(\tau)}{(t-\tau)^{\beta+1-m}}&\text{if $m-1<\beta<m$\;,}\\[10.00002pt]
\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}^{m}}{\mathrm{d}t^{m}}f(t)&\text{if $\beta=m$}\;,\end{cases}

(1.2)

and
I_{s+}^{\gamma}
refers to the (left-sided)
Riemann-Liouville fractional integral
of order
\gamma>0
:

\displaystyle\left(I_{s+}^{\gamma}f\right)(t):=\frac{1}{\Gamma(\gamma)}\int_{s}^{t}(t-r)^{\gamma-1}f(r)\mathrm{d}r,\quad\text{for $t>s$},

and the term
I_{t}^{\gamma}\left[\sigma(u(t,x))\dot{W}(t,x)\right]
in (
1.1
) refers to the more cumbersome notation

\displaystyle\left(I_{0+}^{\gamma}\left[\sigma\left(u(\cdot,x)\right)\dot{W}(\cdot,x)\right]\right)(t).

The existence and uniqueness of a random field solution has been established in
[
8
]

under the
Dalang’s condition
:
d<\Theta
, which is equivalent to

\displaystyle\rho>0\quad\text{and}\quad d<2\alpha,

(1.3)

where the constants
\rho
and
\Theta
are defined as follows:

\displaystyle\rho

\displaystyle:=2(\beta+\gamma)-1-d\beta/\alpha\qquad\text{and}\qquad\Theta:=2\alpha+\frac{\alpha}{\beta}\min(2\gamma-1,0).

(1.4)

The stochastic partial differential equations (SPDE’s) with time-fractional differential operators
have received many attentions recently, which have been studied in one way or another using various
tools from stochastic analysis; see
[
2
,
7
,
8
,
9
,
14
,
21
,
22
]
. This current paper follows the same setup as
[
8
]
, where the
existence/uniqueness of the solution to (
1.1
), moment Lyapunov exponent, sample-path
Hölder regularity, etc., have been studied. In particular, the sample-path regularity was obtained
under the following additional condition:

\displaystyle\beta+\gamma\leq 1,

(1.5)

which in particular excludes the case when
\beta\in(1,2)
. The question is whether one can get rid
of this additional requirement.

By carefully examining the corresponding proof in
[
8
]
, one finds that the completely
monotonic property
[
25
]
of the
two-parameter Mittag-Leffler
function has
been applied in a crucial way. This complete monotonic property, applied to
E_{\beta,\beta+\gamma}(-x)
,
x\geq 0
, says that

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} x\in[0,\infty)\mapsto E_{\beta,\beta+\gamma}(-x)\>\>\text{is complete monotonic}\quad&\Longleftrightarrow\quad 0<\beta\leq\min(\beta+\gamma,1)\\
&\Longleftrightarrow\quad 0<\beta<1\>\>\text{and}\>\>\gamma\geq 0,\end{aligned}

(1.6)

which in particular restricts the results in
[
8
]

only to the cases when
\beta\in(0,1]
. Moreover, the proof in
[
8
]
requires an even
stronger condition, namely, (
1.5
), than those on the far right side of (
1.6
). It is
well known that when
\beta\in(1,2)
, the Mittag-Leffler function has finite (odd) number of
negative real zeros (see, e.g., Section 18.1 of
[
12
]
), or the function of
interest in (
1.6
) is in general oscillatory when
\beta\in(1,2)
, which, together with the
fact that one needs to bound the Mittag-Leffler functions of the form in (
1.6
) from below in
the proof of
[
2
]
, poses a significant challenge to extend the method used in

[
8
]
to cover the cases without the extra restriction (
1.5
). Instead, inspired
by the
local fractional derivatives
(LFD), we manage to obtain the desired regularity results
without condition (
1.5
), which covers the corresponding results obtained in
[
8
]

as a special case.

\displaystyle\left|\>\derivative{^n}{t^n}\>\mathfrak{E}(t,\beta+\gamma,r,\xi)\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\mathfrak{E}(t,\beta+\gamma-n,r,\xi)\right|

\displaystyle\leq C\frac{1}{1+2^{-1}\nu(t-r)^{\beta}|\xi|^{\alpha}}\times\frac{1}{1+2^{-1}\nu(s-r)^{\beta}|\xi|^{\alpha}}

\displaystyle\leq C\left(1+2^{-1}\nu(s-r)^{\beta}|\xi|^{\alpha}\right)^{-2},

where we have applied the asymptotic property (
2.10
). Notice that the upper bound
does not depend on
t
and is
\mathrm{d}\xi
integrable:

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left(1+2^{-1}\nu(s-r)^{\beta}|\xi|^{\alpha}\right)^{-2}\mathrm{d}\xi=C(s-r)^{-\frac{d\beta}{\alpha}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{u^{d-1}}{(1+u^{\alpha})^{2}}\mathrm{d}u,

which is finite because
d<2\alpha
. Therefore, one can apply Fubini’s theorem to see that

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathrm{d}\xi\>\>\derivative{^n}{t^n}\>\mathfrak{E}(t,\beta+\gamma,r,\xi)=\derivative{^n}{t^n}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathrm{d}\xi\>\mathfrak{E}(t,\beta+\gamma,r,\xi),\quad\text{for all $t\in[s,T]$.}

(3.3)

Moreover, notice that, by the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality for the
\mathrm{d}\xi
-integral,

\displaystyle\left|\derivative{^n}{t^n}\>\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathrm{d}\xi\>\mathfrak{E}(t,\beta+\gamma,r,\xi)\right|

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathrm{d}\xi\left|\mathfrak{E}(t,\beta+\gamma-n,r,\xi)\right|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d}}(t-r)^{\beta+\gamma-n-1}(s-r)^{\beta+\gamma-1}

\displaystyle\quad\times\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathrm{d}\xi\>\left|E_{\beta,\beta+\gamma-n}\left(-2^{-1}\nu(t-r)^{\beta}|\xi|^{\alpha}\right)\right|\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\quad\times\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathrm{d}\xi\>\left|E_{\beta,\beta+\gamma}\left(-2^{-1}\nu(s-r)^{\beta}|\xi|^{\alpha}\right)\right|\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle=C_{*}(t-r)^{\beta+\gamma-n-1-\frac{d\beta}{2\alpha}}(s-r)^{\beta+\gamma-1-\frac{d\beta}{2\alpha}},

where the last equality is obtained through change of variables followed by applications of
(
2.9
), and
C_{*}:=\sqrt{C_{\gamma-n}C_{\gamma}}
. We claim that

\displaystyle\beta+\gamma-n-1-\frac{d\beta}{2\alpha}\leq 0.

(3.4)

Otherwise, we will have
\beta+\gamma-1-\frac{d\beta}{2\alpha}>0
as well, which implies that

\displaystyle 0<\left(\beta+\gamma-1-\frac{d\beta}{2\alpha}\right)+\left(\beta+\gamma-n-1-\frac{d\beta}{2\alpha}\right)=\rho-n-1,

which contradicts (
3.1
). Thanks to (
3.4
), we can replace
t
by
s
to see
that

\displaystyle\left|\derivative{^n}{t^n}\>\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathrm{d}\xi\>\mathfrak{E}(t,\beta+\gamma,r,\xi)\right|\leq C_{*}(s-r)^{2(\beta+\gamma)-n-2-\frac{d\beta}{\alpha}}=C_{*}(s-r)^{\rho-n-1},

with the right-hand side both independent of
t
and integrable with respect to
\mathrm{d}r
:

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{s}C_{*}(s-r)^{\rho-n-1}\mathrm{d}r=\frac{C_{*}}{\rho-n}s^{\rho-n},

which is finite because
\rho>n
.
Therefore, one can apply Fubini’s theorem again to see that, for all
t\in[s,T]
,

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{s}\mathrm{d}r\>\derivative{^n}{t^n}\>\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathrm{d}\xi\>\mathfrak{E}(t,\beta+\gamma,r,\xi)=\derivative{^n}{t^n}\>\int_{0}^{s}\mathrm{d}r\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathrm{d}\xi\>\mathfrak{E}(t,\beta+\gamma,r,\xi).

(3.5)

A combination of (
3.3
) and (
3.5
) proves (
3.2
). Moreover,
the bounds obtained from the above two Cases I and II prove the following useful bound

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{s}\mathrm{d}r\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathrm{d}\xi\>\left|\>\derivative{^n}{t^n}\>\mathfrak{E}(t,\beta+\gamma,r,\xi)\right|\leq\frac{C_{*}}{\rho-n}s^{\rho-n}<\infty.

(3.6)

Another direct consequence of (
3.2
) is that
t\mapsto h^{(n)}_{s}(t)
is a continuous
function on
[s,T]
.

Now we are ready to calculate
h_{s}^{\prime}(t)|_{t=s}
and
h_{s}^{\prime\prime}(t)|_{t=s}
by passing the derivatives all
the way inside the double integrals. In case of
\rho>1
, we apply Lemma
2.3
to (
3.2
)
for
n=1
to see that

\displaystyle h_{s}^{\prime}(t)\Big|_{t=s}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathrm{d}\xi\int^{s}_{0}\mathrm{d}r\>

\displaystyle\mathfrak{E}(t,\beta+\gamma-1,r,\xi).

Thanks to Lemma
2.3
, one can integrate
\mathrm{d}r
to see that

\displaystyle h_{s}^{\prime}(t)\big|_{t=s}=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{1}{2}\left[s^{\beta+\gamma-1}E_{\beta,\beta+\gamma}\left(-2^{-1}\nu(s)^{\beta}|\xi|^{\alpha}\right)\right]^{2}\mathrm{d}\xi=\frac{C_{\gamma}}{2}s^{2(\beta+\gamma-1)-\frac{d\beta}{\alpha}}=\frac{C_{\gamma}}{2}s^{\rho-1}.

If
\rho>2
, apply Lemma
2.3
to (
3.2
) for
n=2
and integrate
h^{\prime\prime}(t)
by parts, we have

\displaystyle h_{s}^{\prime\prime}(t)\Big|_{t=s}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathrm{d}\xi\>\mathfrak{E}\left(s,\beta+\gamma-1,r,\xi\right)\Big|^{r=0}_{r=s}

\displaystyle\quad-\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathrm{d}\xi\>\left[(s-r)^{\beta+\gamma-2}E_{\beta,\beta+\gamma-1}\left(-2^{-1}\nu(s-r)^{\beta}|\xi|^{\alpha}\right)\right]^{2}\Bigg|^{r=0}_{r=s}

\displaystyle=C_{\gamma,\gamma-1}s^{2(\beta+\gamma)-3-d\beta/\alpha}-C_{\gamma-1}\frac{1}{\rho-2}s^{2(\beta+\gamma)-3-d\beta/\alpha}

\displaystyle=\left[C_{\gamma,\gamma-1}-\frac{C_{\gamma-1}}{\rho-2}\right]s^{\rho-2},

where both constants
C_{\gamma-1}
and
C_{\gamma,\gamma-1}
are defined in (
2.9
).
∎

4
Hölder continuity of
t\mapsto h_{s}^{(n)}(t)
via local fractional derivative

In this section, we will calculate the
local fractional derivative
(LFD) of
h_{s}(t)
at

t=s
, and then prove the following Proposition
4.1
about the Hölder continuity of

h_{s}(t)
at
t=s
:

Proposition 4.1
.

For all
t\in[s,\infty)
, for
n
and
q
defined in (
3.1
), it holds that

\displaystyle h_{s}(t)-\sum_{k=0}^{n}\frac{h_{s}^{(k)}(s)}{k!}(t-s)^{k}=o\left[(t-s)^{q}\right].

Proof.

The proof of this proposition is an application of the Fractional Taylor theorem (see Lemma

4.5
) to
h_{s}(t)
with all conditions verified in Lemma
4.8
below.
∎

In the following, we first give a brief overview of about LFD in Section
4.1
below. We
refer the interested reader to
[
27
,
16
,
19
]
for more details about LFD.

4.1
Local fractional derivative

Recall that for
-\infty<s<T<\infty
and
q\geq 0
, the (left-sided)
Riemann-Liouville fractional integral
of
order
q
of
f\in L^{1}([s,T];\mathbb{R})
is defined by (see, e.g.,
[
24
, (2.17), p.33]
)

\displaystyle\left(I_{s+}^{q}f\right)(t):=\frac{1}{\Gamma(q)}\int_{s}^{t}f(y)(t-y)^{q-1}\mathrm{d}y,\quad t\in(s,T),

(4.1)

and the corresponding (left-sided) fractional derivative of order
q
is defined formally by (see, e.g.,

[
24
, (2.22), p.35]
)

\displaystyle\left(D_{s+}^{q}f\right)(t):=\left(\derivative{ }{t}\right)^{n+1}\left(I_{s+}^{n+1-q}f\right)(t)={1\over\Gamma(n+1-q)}\left(\derivative{ }{t}\right)^{n+1}\int_{s}^{t}f(y)(t-y)^{n-q}\mathrm{d}y.

(4.2)

When
q=n
, the above definition gives
f^{(n)}(t)
(if
f^{(n)}(t)
exists) on
(s,T)
.

Definition 4.2
.

For arbitrary
q>0
, the (left-sided)
local fractional derivative

(LFD) of
f(t)
of order
q
at
t=s
is defined as, provided the limit exits,

\displaystyle I\!\!D^{q}f(s):=\lim_{t\rightarrow s+}\left(D_{s+}^{q}\left[f(\cdot)-\sum_{k=0}^{n}\frac{f^{(k)}(s)}{k!}(\cdot-s)^{k}\right]\right)(t),\quad 0<n<q\leq n+1.

(4.3)

Clearly, when
q\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
,
I\!\!D^{q}f(s)=f^{(q)}(s)
. Kolwankar and Gangal

[
19
,
20
]
introduced the above LFD of order
q\in(0,1)
to investigate the regularity
of non-differentiable functions. Recall that the
(left-sided) Caputo fractional derivative
is defined as (see, e.g.,
[
18
, (2.4.1), p.91]
)

\displaystyle\left(\partial_{s+}^{q}f\right)(t):=\left(D_{s+}^{q}\left[f(\cdot)-\sum_{k=0}^{n}\frac{f^{(k)}(s)}{k!}(\cdot-s)^{k}\right]\right)(t),\qquad n=\left\lceil q\right\rceil-1,

(4.4)

whereas the notation
\partial^{\beta}
in (
1.2
) is a short-hand notation for
\partial_{0+}^{\beta}
.

Comparing (
4.3
) and (
4.4
), one
sees that the LFD is nothing but the limit of Caputo fractional derivative, namely,
I\!\!D^{q}f(s)=\lim_{t\rightarrow s}\left(\partial_{s+}^{q}f\right)(t)
. When
f^{(n)}(t)
is absolutely continuous on
[s,T]
,
both the Riemann-Liouville derivative and the Caputo fractional derivative are well defined

a.e.
on
[s,T]
with the Caputo derivative having a shorter form after
\left\lceil q\right\rceil
times
of integration-by-parts (see
[
24
, Theorem 2.2, p.39]
or
[
18
, Theorem 2.1,
p.92]
):

\displaystyle\left(\partial_{s+}^{q}f\right)(t)=\frac{1}{\Gamma(n+1-q)}\int_{s}^{t}\>\frac{f^{(n+1)}(\tau)}{(t-\tau)^{q-n}}\mathrm{d}\tau,\qquad n=\left\lceil q\right\rceil-1.

(4.5)

In particular, when
s=0
, this reduces to the form (
1.2
).

However, in this paper, for
0\leq n<q<n+1
, the function
h_{s}^{(n)}(t)
is in general not absolutely
continuous over
[s,T]
, or equivalently,
h_{s}^{(n+1)}(t)
may not be well defined. When computing

\left(\partial_{s+}^{q}h_{s}\right)(t)
, one cannot blindly apply the expression (
4.5
).
We don’t presume that
\left(\partial_{s+}^{q}h_{s}\right)(t)
is well defined until it’s shown in
Lemma
4.8
. We will need the following composition property of Caputo derivative without
assuming
f^{(n)}(t)
to be absolutely continuous, which shows that the LFD of
f(t)
of order

q\in\mathbb{R}_{+}\setminus\mathbb{Z}_{+}
is also the
(q-n)
-th order LFD of
f^{(n)}(t)
, where
n=\left\lfloor q\right\rfloor
.

Lemma 4.3
.

Suppose
0<n<q<n+1
and
T>0
. Let
f^{(n-1)}(t)
be absolutely
continuous on
[s,T]
. If either
\left(\partial_{s+}^{q}f\right)(t)
or

\left(\partial_{s+}^{q-n}f^{(n)}\right)(t)
exists on
(s,T)
, then

\left(\partial_{s+}^{q}f\right)(t)=\left(\partial_{s+}^{q-n}f^{(n)}\right)(t)
on
(s,T).

Proof.

Fix an arbitrary
t\in(s,T)
. Starting from the definition of the Caputo derivative
(
4.4
), via integration-by-parts, we see that

\displaystyle\left(\partial_{s+}^{q}f\right)(t)=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\Gamma(n+1-q)}\left(\derivative{ t}\right)^{n+1}\left\{-\left.\frac{(t-y)^{n+1-q}}{n+1-q}\left[f(y)-\sum_{k=0}^{n}\frac{f^{(k)}(s)}{k!}(y-s)^{k}\right]\right|_{y=s}^{t}\right.

\displaystyle\quad\left.+\int_{s}^{t}\frac{(t-y)^{n+1-q}}{n+1-q}\derivative{ y}\left[f(y)-\sum_{k=0}^{n}\frac{f^{(k)}(s)}{k!}(y-s)^{k}\right]\mathrm{d}y\right\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\Gamma(n+1-q)}\left(\derivative{ t}\right)^{n+1}\int_{s}^{t}\frac{(t-y)^{n+1-q}}{n+1-q}\left[f^{\prime}(y)-\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{f^{(k)}(s)}{(k-1)!}(y-s)^{k-1}\right]\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\Gamma(n+1-q)}\left(\derivative{ t}\right)^{n}\int_{s}^{t}(t-y)^{n-q}\left[f^{\prime}(y)-\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{f^{(k)}(s)}{(k-1)!}(y-s)^{k-1}\right]\mathrm{d}y.

Repeat the above integration-by-parts
n
times to see that

\displaystyle\left(\partial_{s+}^{q}f\right)(t)=\frac{1}{\Gamma(n+1-q)}\derivative{ t}\int_{s}^{t}(t-y)^{n-q}\left[f^{(n)}(y)-f^{(n)}(s)\right]\mathrm{d}y,

(4.6)

which is nothing but what we need to prove, namely,
\left(\partial_{s+}^{q}f\right)(t)=\left(\partial_{s+}^{q-n}f^{(n)}\right)(t)
.
∎

Remark 4.4
.

If
f^{(n)}
is absolutely continuous on

[s,T]
as is assumed in Theorem 2.1 of
[
18
]
, one can apply one more time this
integration-by-parts to arrive at (
4.5
). Here, we will postpone the determination of
the well-posedness of
\left(\partial_{s+}^{q}f^{(n)}\right)(t)
latter.

The following
Fractional Taylor theorem
is crucial for the estimation of
h_{s}(t)
.

Lemma 4.5

(Fractional Taylor theorem)
.

Let
f
be a real-valued function. Assume that for some
q>0
and
s\in\mathbb{R}
, the following two
conditions hold:

1.

f^{(\left\lceil q\right\rceil-1)}(t)
be right-continuous at
s
, namely,
\lim_{t\to s_{+}}f^{(\left\lceil q\right\rceil-1)}(t)=f^{(\left\lceil q\right\rceil-1)}(s)
;

2.

I\!\!D^{q}f(s)
exists;

then

\displaystyle f(t)-\sum_{k=0}^{\left\lceil q\right\rceil-1}\frac{f^{(k)}(s)}{k!}(t-s)^{k}=\frac{I\!\!D^{q}f(s)}{\Gamma(q+1)}(t-s)^{q}+o\left[(t-s)^{q}\right],\quad\text{as $t\downarrow s$.}

(4.7)

Remark 4.6
.

This lemma is a rephrasing of
[
1
, Theorems 4 and 5]
, which has been both tailored
for our application and generalized from
q\in(0,1)
to all
q>0
. To the best of our
knowledge, Lemma
4.5
also appeared, with stronger or equivalent conditions, in the
following references: In the case of
n=0
,
[
19
]
proved it under a stronger condition
that
\left(\partial_{s+}^{q}f\right)(t)
is absolutely continuous; Proposition 2 of

[
10
]
in fact needs that
(I_{s+}^{1-q}f)(t)
is absolutely continuous. Note that both
Proposition 3.1 of
[
15
]
and Theorem 1.12 of
[
27
]
give different types of
Fractional Taylor theorem under more stringent conditions on
f(t)
. For completeness and also
for the readers’ convenience, we reproduce below the proof of this lemma with slight more
details.

Proof of Lemma
4.5
.

When
q\geq 1
is an integer, (
4.7
) reduces to the classical Taylor’s expansion. In the
following, we will assume that
q\not\in\mathbb{Z}
.

Let us first consider the case
0<q<1
. Since
f(t)
is right continuous at
t=s
, there exists
some
T>s
such
f\in C([s,T])\subseteq L^{1}([s,T])
. Therefore, for all
t\in[s,T]
,

\displaystyle f(t)-f(s)=

\displaystyle\derivative{t}\left(I_{s+}^{1}[f(\cdot)-f(s)]\right)(t)=\derivative{t}\left(I_{s+}^{q}\circ I_{s+}^{1-q}[f(\cdot)-f(s)]\right)(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\derivative{t}\frac{1}{\Gamma(q)}\int_{s}^{t}\left(I_{s+}^{1-q}[f(\cdot)-f(s)]\right)(y)\>(t-y)^{q-1}\mathrm{d}y,

(4.8)

where the first and the second equalities are due to the fundamental theorem of calculus and
Lemma 2.3 on p. 73 of
[
18
]
, respectively, both applied to continuous functions.
Now in order to apply the integration-by-parts formula to the above
\mathrm{d}y
-integral, we need to
show that

the function
y\mapsto\left(I_{s+}^{1-q}\left[f(\cdot)-f(s)\right]\right)(y)
is absolutely continuous on
[s,T]
.

(4.9)

Indeed, by (
4.4
),

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}y}\left(I_{s+}^{1-q}\left[f(\cdot)-f(s)\right]\right)(y)=(\partial_{s+}^{q}f)(y),

where the right-hand side is well defined, at least at a small right neighborhood of
s
, thanks
to the condition that
I\!\!D^{q}f(s)
exists. Hence, by moving
T
towards
s
whenever necessary,
we see that
y\mapsto\left(I_{s+}^{1-q}\left[f(\cdot)-f(s)\right]\right)(y)
is is
differential
everywhere
on
[s,T]
with its derivative equal to
\left(D_{s+}^{q}\left[f(\cdot)-f(s)\right]\right)(y)
or, equivalently,
\left(\partial_{s+}^{q}f\right)(y)
(see
(
4.4
)). Again, the existence of
I\!\!D^{q}f(s)
guarantees that

\displaystyle(\partial_{s+}^{q}f)(\cdot)\in L^{\infty}([s,T])\cap L^{1}([s,T]).

(4.10)

This proves (
4.9
). Hence, we can apply the integration-by-parts to see that for all

y\in[s,T]
, the right-hand side of (
4.8
) is equal to

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\derivative{t}\frac{1}{\Gamma(q)}\bigg[\>-\frac{1}{q}(t-y)^{q}\times\left(I_{s+}^{1-q}[f(\cdot)-f(s)]\right)(y)\Big|_{y=s}^{y=t}+\int_{s}^{t}\left(\partial_{s+}^{q}f\right)(y)\times\frac{1}{q}(t-y)^{q}\mathrm{d}y\>\bigg]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\derivative{t}\frac{1}{\Gamma(q)}\int_{s}^{t}\left(\partial_{s+}^{q}f\right)(y)\times\frac{1}{q}(t-y)^{q}\mathrm{d}y,

where the first term in the bracket is equal to zero because
f\in C([s,T])\subseteq L^{1}([s,T])
. Thanks to the boundedness of the integrand (see (
4.10
)), one can switch the
\mathrm{d}t
derivative and the
\mathrm{d}y

integral to see that

\displaystyle f(t)-f(s)=\left(I_{s+}^{q}\circ D_{s+}^{q}\left[f(\cdot)-f(s)\right]\right)(t),\quad\text{for all $t\in[s,T]$},

(4.11)

where the fractional integral is well defined thanks to (
4.10
). Finally, the definition
of LFD in (
4.3
) implies that

\displaystyle\left(I_{s+}^{q}\circ D_{s+}^{q}[f(\cdot)-f(s)]\right)(t)=\left(I_{s+}^{q}[I\!\!D^{q}f(\cdot)+o(1)]\right)(t)=\frac{I\!\!D^{q}f(s)}{\Gamma(q+1)}(t-s)^{q}+o\left[(t-s)^{q}\right].

(4.12)

Combining (
4.11
) and (
4.12
) proves (
4.7
) for the case when
q\in(0,1)
.

It remains to prove the case when
q>1
and
q\not\in\mathbb{Z}
. Notice that by Lemma

4.3
, the existence of
I\!\!D^{q}f(s)
is equivalent to the existence of

I\!\!D^{q-\left\lfloor q\right\rfloor}f^{(\left\lfloor q\right\rfloor)}(s)
. Now we can apply the case of
0<q<1
to

f^{(\left\lfloor q\right\rfloor)}(t)
to see that

\displaystyle f^{(\left\lfloor q\right\rfloor)}(t)-f^{(\left\lfloor q\right\rfloor)}(s)=

\displaystyle\frac{I\!\!D^{q-\left\lfloor q\right\rfloor}f^{(\left\lfloor q\right\rfloor)}(s)}{\Gamma(q-\left\lfloor q\right\rfloor+1)}(t-s)^{q-\left\lfloor q\right\rfloor}+o\left((t-s)^{q-\left\lfloor q\right\rfloor}\right).

(4.13)

Finally, the case is proved by applying the
I_{s+}^{\left\lfloor q\right\rfloor}
on both sides of (
4.13
).
This completes the proof of Lemma
4.5
.
∎

4.2
Local fractional derivative of
h_{s}^{(n)}(t)

By Lemma
4.5
and Lemma
4.3
, to prove Proposition
4.1
we only need
to show
I\!\!D^{q-n}h_{s}^{(n)}(t)=0
for
0\leq n<q<n-1
. We will apply Fubini’s Theorem to show
the interchangeability of local fractional operator
I\!\!D^{q}
and the integral operator

\int^{s}_{0}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}
in
h_{s}^{(n)}(t)
in the following two lemmas.

Lemma 4.7
.

Assuming (
3.1
), for all
T\in(s,\infty)
, it holds that

\displaystyle t\mapsto\left(I_{s+}^{n+1-q}\left[h_{s}^{(n)}(\cdot)-h_{s}^{(n)}(s)\right]\right)(t)\in C([s,T])

(4.14)

and for all
t\in[s,T]
,

\left(I_{s+}^{n+1-q}[h_{s}^{(n)}(\cdot)-h_{s}^{(n)}(s)]\right)(t)\\
=\int^{s}_{0}\mathrm{d}r\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathrm{d}\xi\left(I_{s+}^{n+1-q}\left[\mathfrak{E}(\cdot,\beta+\gamma-n,r,\xi)-\mathfrak{E}(s,\beta+\gamma-n,r,\xi)\right]\right)(t).

(4.15)

Proof.

The statement of (
4.14
) is due to the fact that
h_{s}^{(n)}(t)\in C([s,T])
; see
Lemma
3.1
. As for (
4.15
), there are two fractional integrals. The
second one is trivially true since since
h_{s}^{(n)}(s)
does not depend on
t
. For the first
fractional integral, we can switch the order of integrations because, by (
3.6
),

\displaystyle\left(I_{s+}^{n+1-q}\int^{s}_{0}\mathrm{d}r\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathrm{d}\xi\left|\mathfrak{E}(\cdot,\beta+\gamma-n,r,\xi)\right|\right)(t)<\left(I_{s+}^{n+1-q}\>C_{T}\right)(t)<C_{T}^{\prime}<\infty.

This proves the lemma.
∎

Lemma 4.8
.

Assuming (
3.1
), for all
T\in(s,\infty)
, it holds that
\left(\partial_{s+}^{q-n}h_{s}^{(n)}\right)(t)\in C([s,T])
and

\displaystyle\left(\partial_{s+}^{q-n}h_{s}^{(n)}\right)(t)=\int^{s}_{0}\mathrm{d}r\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathrm{d}\xi\;\;\left(\partial_{s+}^{q-n}\mathfrak{E}(\cdot,\beta+\gamma-n,r,\xi)\right)(t)\quad\text{for all $t\in[s,T]$.}

(4.16)

Consequently,

\displaystyle I\!\!D^{q}h_{s}(s)=I\!\!D^{q-n}h_{s}^{(n)}(s)=0,\qquad\text{for all $s>0$.}

(4.17)

Proof.

We will prove this lemma in the following three steps:

Step 1.
We will first verify the condition of Fubini’s theorem is satisfied,
so that (
4.16
) is valid
a.e.
on
[s,T]
. By the definition of the Caputo
derivative (
4.4
) and Lemma
4.7
,

\displaystyle\left(\partial_{s+}^{q-n}h_{s}^{(n)}\right)(t)

Noticing that

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}

\displaystyle\left(I_{s+}^{n+1-q}\left[\mathfrak{E}(\cdot,\beta+\gamma-n,r,\xi)-\mathfrak{E}(s,\beta+\gamma-n,r,\xi)\right]\right)(t)=\left(\partial_{s+}^{q-n}\mathfrak{E}(\cdot,\beta+\gamma-n,r,\xi)\right)(t),

. Indeed, notice that, by (

\displaystyle\left(\partial_{s+}^{q-n}\mathfrak{E}(\cdot,\beta+\gamma-n,r,\xi)\right)(t)=\frac{1}{\Gamma(n+1-q)}\int_{s}^{t}(t-y)^{n-q}\mathfrak{E}(y,\beta+\gamma-n-1,r,\xi)\mathrm{d}y,

In light of Theorem 4 of

\displaystyle J:=\int^{s}_{0}\mathrm{d}r\;\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathrm{d}\xi\;\int_{s}^{t}\mathrm{d}y\>(t-y)^{n-q}\left|\mathfrak{E}(y,\beta+\gamma-n-1,r,\xi)\right|<\infty.

\displaystyle J\leq

\displaystyle C_{*}^{\prime}\int^{s}_{0}\mathrm{d}r\int^{t}_{s}\mathrm{d}y\>(y-r)^{\frac{\rho-3}{2}-n}(t-y)^{n-q}(s-r)^{\frac{\rho-1}{2}},

\displaystyle J_{22}\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n+1-q}\int_{2s-t}^{s}\mathrm{d}r\>\left(\frac{s-r}{2}\right)^{\frac{\rho-1}{2}-q}(s-r)^{\frac{\rho-1}{2}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n+1-q}\int_{0}^{s}\mathrm{d}r\>\left(\frac{s-r}{2}\right)^{\frac{\rho-1}{2}-q}(s-r)^{\frac{\rho-1}{2}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n+1-q}2^{q-\frac{\rho-1}{2}}\frac{1}{\rho-q}s^{\rho-q}.

Finally, for

\displaystyle J_{21}=

\displaystyle\int^{s}_{2s-t}\mathrm{d}r\int_{s}^{\frac{t+r}{2}}\mathrm{d}y\;\;(y-r)^{\frac{\rho-3}{2}-n}(t-y)^{n-q}(s-r)^{\frac{\rho-1}{2}}

\displaystyle\int^{s}_{2s-t}\mathrm{d}r\left(\frac{t-r}{2}\right)^{n-q}(s-r)^{\frac{\rho-1}{2}}\int_{s}^{\frac{t+r}{2}}(y-r)^{\frac{\rho-3}{2}-n}\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle\frac{1}{{n-\frac{\rho-1}{2}}}\int^{s}_{2s-t}\mathrm{d}r\left(\frac{t-r}{2}\right)^{n-q}(s-r)^{\frac{\rho-1}{2}}\left[(s-r)^{\frac{\rho-1}{2}-n}-\left(\frac{t-r}{2}\right)^{\frac{\rho-1}{2}-n}\right].

where we have used the fact that

\displaystyle J_{21}\leq C^{\dagger}\int^{s}_{0}(s-r)^{\rho-1-q}\mathrm{d}r=\frac{C^{\dagger}}{\rho-q}s^{\rho-q},\quad\text{with $C^{\dagger}:=\frac{2}{2n-\rho+1}\left[2^{q-n}-2^{q-\frac{\rho-1}{2}}\right].$}

Combining all these terms proves

Step 2.
Now we will show

\displaystyle\lim_{t\rightarrow t_{0}}\left(\partial_{s+}^{q-n}h_{s}^{(n)}\right)(t)=\left(\partial_{s+}^{q-n}h_{s}^{(n)}\right)(t_{0})\qquad\text{for all $t_{0}\in[s,T]$},

\displaystyle\left(\partial_{s+}^{q-n}h_{s}^{(n)}\right)(t)=C\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mathrm{d}r\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mathrm{d}y\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathrm{d}\xi

Proof of proposition
2.2
.

Denote the left-hand side of (
2.8
) by
I
and then

\displaystyle I=C_{\gamma}\int_{0}^{s}\mathrm{d}r\left[(t-r)^{2(\beta+\gamma-1)-d\beta/\alpha}+(s-r)^{2(\beta+\gamma-1)-d\beta/\alpha}\right]-2h_{s}(t).

When
\rho\leq 1
, by Proposition
4.1
, we have
h_{s}(t)=h_{s}(s)+o\left[(t-s)^{q}\right]
and
so there is a constant
K
, such that
|h_{s}(t)-h_{s}(s)|<K(t-s)^{q}
. Therefore,

\displaystyle I

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C_{\gamma}}{\rho}\left[t^{\rho}-(t-s)^{\rho}+s^{\rho}\right]-2\left[h_{s}(s)-K(t-s)^{q}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{C_{\gamma}}{\rho}\left[t^{\rho}-(t-s)^{\rho}+s^{\rho}-2s^{\rho}\right]+2K(t-s)^{q}

\displaystyle=O\left[(t-s)^{\rho}\right]+2K(t-s)^{q},

with
2K(t-s)^{q}
being the dominating term.

Similarly, when
1<\rho\leq 2
, by Proposition
4.1
and Lemma
3.1
,

\displaystyle I\leq

\displaystyle\frac{C_{\gamma}}{\rho}\left[t^{\rho}-(t-s)^{\rho}+s^{\rho}\right]-2\left[h_{s}(s)+\frac{1}{2}C_{\gamma}s^{\rho-1}(t-s)-K(t-s)^{q}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle C_{\gamma}\left[\frac{1}{\rho}\left(t^{\rho}-s^{\rho}\right)-s^{\rho-1}(t-s)\right]-\frac{C_{\gamma}}{\rho}(t-s)^{\rho}+2*K(t-s)^{q}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{C_{\gamma}(\rho-1)s^{\rho-2}}{2}(t-s)^{2}+O\left[(t-s)^{3}\right]+O\left[(t-s)^{\rho}\right]+2K(t-s)^{q},

with
2K(t-s)^{q}
being the dominating term.

Finally, when
\rho>2
,

\displaystyle I\leq

\displaystyle\frac{C_{\gamma}}{\rho}\left[t^{\rho}-(t-s)^{\rho}+s^{\rho}\right]-2\left[h_{s}(s)+\frac{1}{2}C_{\gamma}s^{\rho-1}(t-s)+\frac{1}{2}h_{s}^{\prime\prime}(s)(t-s)^{2}-K(t-s)^{q}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle C_{\gamma}\left[\frac{1}{\rho}\left(t^{\rho}-s^{\rho}\right)-s^{\rho-1}(t-s)\right]-\frac{C_{\gamma}}{\rho}(t-s)^{\rho}-h_{s}^{\prime\prime}(s)(t-s)^{2}+2K(t-s)^{q}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{C_{\gamma}(\rho-1)s^{\rho-2}}{2}(t-s)^{2}+O\left[(t-s)^{3}\right]+O\left[(t-s)^{\rho}\right]-h_{s}^{\prime\prime}(s)(t-s)^{2}+2K(t-s)^{q}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\frac{C_{\gamma}(\rho-1)}{2}+\frac{C_{\gamma-1}}{\rho-2}-C_{\gamma,\gamma-1}\right)s^{\rho-2}(t-s)^{2}+O\left[(t-s)^{3}\right]+O\left[(t-s)^{\rho}\right]+2K(t-s)^{q},

where the second order term is the dominating term.
This completes the proof of Proposition
2.2
.
