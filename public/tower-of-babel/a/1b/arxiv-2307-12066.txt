Simple loop conjecture for discrete representations in PSL ⁡ ( 2 , ℝ ) {\mathrm{PSL}(2,\mathbb{R})}
Gianluca Faraco, Subhojoy Gupta

Abstract
. We show that the Simple Loop Conjecture holds for any representation ρ : π 1 ​ ( S ) ⟶ PSL ⁡ ( 2 , ℝ ) \rho\colon\pi_{1}(S)\longrightarrow{\mathrm{PSL}(2,\mathbb{R})} that is discrete but not faithful. That is, we show the existence of a simple closed curve in the kernel of such a representation.



2010 Mathematics Subject Classification
57M50

1.
Introduction

Let
S
be a closed orientable surface of genus
g\geq 2
. Let
\rho\colon\pi_{1}(S)\longrightarrow{\mathrm{PSL}(2,\mathbb{R})}
be a representation; recall that
\rho
is said to be
discrete
if the image of
\rho
is a discrete subgroup of
{\mathrm{PSL}(2,\mathbb{R})}
and
faithful
if
\rho
is injective. In this short note we shall prove:

Main Theorem
.

Let
\rho\colon\pi_{1}(S)\longrightarrow{\mathrm{PSL}(2,\mathbb{R})}
be a discrete representation which is not faithful. Then
\textnormal{ker}(\,\rho\,)
contains a simple closed curve.

Originally, the so-called Simple Loop Conjecture stated that if a map
f\colon S\longrightarrow\Sigma
between closed surfaces induces a non-injective map of fundamental groups
f_{*}\colon\pi_{1}(S)\to\pi_{1}(\Sigma)
, then there exists a simple closed curve in
\textnormal{ker}(f_{*})
. In
[
Gab85
]
, Gabai provided a positive answer to this conjecture, see Theorem
3.2
.

Shortly thereafter, in
[
Has87
]
Hass proved that the Simple Loop Conjecture holds for maps from a closed surface
S
to a Seifert-fibered
3
-manifold
M
. A longstanding question has been whether result still holds if the target is replaced by any orientable
3
-manifold; although this is known for graph-manifolds, see
[
RW98
]
, the case of a
hyperbolic

3
-manifold is still open. Recently, counterexamples to the Simple loop conjecture in dimension higher than
3
has been found in
[
Far23
]
. At the level of fundamental groups, the open question for hyperbolic
3
-manifolds could be stated as follows:

Question 1.1
.

Let
\rho\colon\pi_{1}(S)\longrightarrow{\mathrm{PSL}(2,\mathbb{C})}
be a discrete co-compact representation which is non-injective. Then does
\textnormal{ker}(\,\rho\,)
always contain a simple closed curve?

If the assumption of discreteness is dropped, then it is known that the Simple Loop Conjecture fails – counterexamples were first provided by Cooper and Manning in
[
CM15
]
, and this answered a question of Minsky in
[
Min00
, Question 5.3]
. More counterexamples were described in the papers of Louder
[
Lou14
]
and Calegari
[
Cal13
]
. For representations into
{\mathrm{PSL}(2,\mathbb{R})}
, which is the focus of this article, explicit counterexamples were constructed by Mann in
[
Man14
]
; our theorem implies that these would necessarily be indiscrete.

Another motivation for understanding the case of surface group representations into
{\mathrm{PSL}(2,\mathbb{R})}
is the following question of Bowditch, stated here for a closed surface
S
.

Question 1.2
.

[
Bow98
, Question C]

Suppose
\rho\colon\pi_{1}(S)\longrightarrow{\mathrm{PSL}(2,\mathbb{R})}
is a non-elementary representation that is not both discrete and faithful. Then does there always exist a simple closed curve whose image is a non-hyperbolic element of
{\mathrm{PSL}(2,\mathbb{R})}
?

Bowditch’s question has been answered in the affirmative for surfaces of genus two by Marché and Wolff in
[
MW16
]
but it is currently open for higher genus surfaces. Our result provides an affirmative answer in the special case of discrete but non-faithful representations.

Structure of the paper

The paper is organised as follows. In Section §
2
we recall basic facts about surface group representations into
{\mathrm{PSL}(2,\mathbb{R})}
for the reader’s convenience, in order to make the exposition self-contained. Sections §
3
, §
4
and §
5
are devoted to prove our main result in the following cases respectively: purely hyperbolic representations, representations with cocompact image having torsion elements, which we call
crystallographic
and, finally, all remaining discrete representations, with necessarily non-cocompact image. Our proof relies on Gabai’s result that we mentioned above and results from Edmonds’ work concerning maps between surfaces in
[
Edm79
]
.

2.
Fuchsian groups and surface group representations

In this section we mainly recall basic and well-known facts about Fuchsian groups, i.e. discrete subgroups of
{\mathrm{PSL}(2,\mathbb{R})}
. Good references for a full treatment of this topic are
[
BP92
]
,
[
Gol88
]
and
[
Kat92
]
. We shall then introduce surface group representations into
{\mathrm{PSL}(2,\mathbb{R})}
which are discrete, and prove our main result in the case that the discrete represetation is elementary.

2.1.
The Lie group
{\mathrm{PSL}(2,\mathbb{R})}

The group
{\mathrm{PSL}(2,\mathbb{R})}
is defined as the quotient of the Lie group
{\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{R})}
with its center
\{\pm I\}
and it identifies with the group of orientation-preserving isometries of
\mathbb{H}
, the upper half-plane model of the hyperbolic plane, in which the elements acts by Möbius transformations

(2.1)

{\mathrm{PSL}(2,\mathbb{R})}\times\mathbb{H}\longrightarrow\mathbb{H},\quad\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}a&b\\
c&d\\
\end{array}\right)\cdot z\longmapsto\dfrac{az+b}{cz+d}

In
{\mathrm{PSL}(2,\mathbb{R})}
, we mainly distinguish four types of elements according to the nature of the solutions of the equation

(2.2)

g(z)=\dfrac{az+b}{cz+d}=z.

1.

The Identity
: Here the equation (
2.2
) reduces to the tautological identity
z=z
and, of course, every point is fixed.

2.

Elliptics
: If the equation (
2.2
) has two complex (but not real) solutions, then these are conjugated because the coefficients of (
2.2
) are real. Only one of these solutions lies in
\mathbb{H}
and
g
acts as a rotation around it.

3.

Parabolics
: In the case the equation (
2.2
) has only one real solution of multiplicity two. In this case,
g
has no fixed points in
\mathbb{H}
but it has only one fixed point on the boundary at infinity
\partial_{\infty}\mathbb{H}\cong\mathbb{R}\mathrm{\mathrm{P}}^{1}
. One can think of the action of
g
as a rotation with a fixed point in
\partial_{\infty}\mathbb{H}
and the orbits are given by the horocircles based at that fixed point.

4.

Hyperbolics
: In the case the equation (
2.2
) has two real solutions, the map
g
has no fixed point in
\mathbb{H}
and have two fixed points on the boundary at infinity. There is a unique geodesic line (the axis of
g
) with these as its endpoints, that is invariant under the action of
g
.

As the equation (
2.2
) defines a polynomial of degree two with real coefficients, the list above is exhaustive and no other cases occur.

2.2.
Fuchsian groups

We now recall a few special classes of subgroups of
{\mathrm{PSL}(2,\mathbb{R})}
along with their properties which will be relevant for us in the sequel.

Definition 2.1
.

A
Fuchsian group
is a discrete subgroup
\Gamma
of
{\mathrm{PSL}(2,\mathbb{R})}
; so a group of orientation-preserving isometries acting properly discontinuously on
\mathbb{H}
.

Definition 2.2
.

A subgroup
\Gamma
of
{\mathrm{PSL}(2,\mathbb{R})}
is said to be
elementary
if there is a finite
\Gamma-
orbit in
\mathbb{H}\,\cup\,\mathbb{R}\mathrm{\mathrm{P}}^{1}
; otherwise
\Gamma
is non-elementary.

A discrete group may very well be elementary and hence it is worthwhile to have a proper description of them. In order to find all such groups we first consider the case when
\Gamma
fixes a single point, say
p\in\mathbb{H}
. Recall that the fixed points of elliptic elements are not in
\mathbb{R}\mathrm{\mathrm{P}}^{1}
whereas the fixed points of parabolic and hyperbolic transformations of
\Gamma
are in
\mathbb{R}\mathrm{\mathrm{P}}^{1}
. It is possible to show that an elementary
\Gamma
cannot contain parabolic and hyperbolic elements at the same time because they would necessarily have a common fixed point, in which case it cannot be discrete – see for instance
[
Bea83
, Theorem 5.1.2]
. We then deduce that, in the case there is a single global fixed point,
\Gamma
can only contain elements of one type. Further considerations lead to the following result; for a proof the reader is invited to consult
[
Kat92
, Theorem 2.4.3]
.

Proposition 2.3
.

Any elementary Fuchsian group is either cyclic or it is conjugate to a subgroup generated by
g(z)=\lambda z
, with
\lambda>1
, and
h(z)=-\frac{1}{z}
.

In the sequel we shall make use of Proposition
2.3
in order to show that
Main Theorem
follows easily for elementary representations,
i.e.
discrete representations into
{\mathrm{PSL}(2,\mathbb{R})}
whose image is an elementary subgroup, see Definition
2.6
. Henceforth, in Sections §
3
, §
4
and §
5
we shall have the blanket assumption that the image of the representations are non-elementary. The following proposition provides a useful characterisation of discrete subgroups of
{\mathrm{PSL}(2,\mathbb{R})}
that are non-elementary . For a list of equivalent characterisations see
[
Bea83
, Section §8.4]
.

Proposition 2.4
.

A non-elementary subgroup
\Gamma
of
{\mathrm{PSL}(2,\mathbb{R})}
is discrete if and only if each elliptic element (if any) of
\Gamma
has finite order.

Proof.

We provide here an outline of the proof for the reader’s convenience; for details we refer to
[
Bea83
, Section §8.4]
. One implication is trivial: if
\Gamma
is discrete, any convergent sequence of elements of
\Gamma
is eventually constant. However, if
\Gamma
contains an elliptic of infinite order then we may find a sequence of transformations converging to the identity which is not eventually constant, reaching a contradiction. The opposite implication relies on Selberg’s Lemma: For any pair of elements, say
f,g\in\Gamma
, consider the group
\langle f,\,g\rangle
contains a finite index subgroup, say
\Gamma_{o}
without torsion elements;
i.e.
without elliptics, see
[
Sel60
]
. It can be shown that any non-elementary subgroup of
{\mathrm{PSL}(2,\mathbb{R})}
with no elliptics is necessarily discrete (see
[
Bea83
, Theorem 8.3.1]
). Hence
\Gamma_{o}
is discrete and hence, being of finite index in
\langle f,\,g\rangle
, the latter group is also discrete. Finally, one can show that a non-elementary group
\Gamma
is discrete if and only if
\langle f,\,g\rangle
for any pair
f,g\in\Gamma
, (see
[
Bea83
, Theorem 5.4.2]
), using the so-called Jørgensen inequality (see
[
Bea83
, Theorem 5.4.1]
). This completes the proof.
∎

Let
\Gamma
be a Fuchsian group. Being
\Gamma
discrete, its action on the hyperbolic plane is properly discontinuous; such an action admits a fundamental domain. Moreover, note that in this paper, we deal only with finitely generated Fuchsian groups, since the fundamental group
\pi_{1}(S)
is a finitely generated. For such a Fuchsian group
\Gamma
its Dirichlet domain
\mathcal{D}(\Gamma)
is a fundamental domain that enjoys nice properties like a local finiteness which guarantees that
\mathbb{H}/\Gamma\cong\mathcal{D}(\Gamma)/\Gamma
, see
[
Bea83
, Section §9]
. A Fuchsian group
\Gamma
is said to be
cocompact
if the identification space
\mathbb{H}/\Gamma
is compact. Of course, if the Dirichlet domain
\mathcal{D}(\Gamma)
is compact, then the identification space is compact and
\Gamma
does not contain any parabolic elements (see
[
Kat92
, Theorem 4.2.1]
). On the other hand, if
\mathcal{D}(\Gamma)
is not compact then neither is
\mathbb{H}/\Gamma
. Moreover, if
\mathcal{D}(\Gamma)
has finite hyperbolic area but it is not compact, then
\Gamma
contains parabolic elements (see
[
Kat92
, Theorem 4.2.2]
).

2.3.
Surface group representations

Let
S
be a closed genus
g
surface of hyperbolic type,
i.e.

2-2g<0
, and let
\pi_{1}(S)
denote its fundamental group. A
surface group representation
, or often in this paper, simply a
representation
, is a homomorphism
\rho:\pi_{1}(S)\longrightarrow{\mathrm{PSL}(2,\mathbb{R})}
.

For a surface
S
, let
\mathcal{R}_{g}=\textnormal{Hom}\big(\,\pi_{1}(S),\,{\mathrm{PSL}(2,\mathbb{R})}\,\big)
denote the representation space. Each representation is uniquely determined by the images of
2g
generators of
\pi_{1}(S)
, which satisfy the relation given by the products of commutators. The geometry and topology of this space of representations has been studied extensively in last decades. In his work
[
Gol88
]
, Goldman showed that the representation space exhibits
4g-3
connected components which are distinguish by the
Euler number
; a topological invariant
\mathcal{E}(\rho)
naturally attached to a representation
\rho
. In brief, the Euler number measures the failure for the existence of a global section of the
\mathbb{R}\mathrm{\mathrm{P}}^{1}-
bundle over
S
with holonomy
\rho
, see
[
Gol88
]
and
[
Mil58
]
. The Euler number has to obey the so-called Milnor-Wood inequality;
i.e.

|\,\mathcal{E}(\rho)\,|\leq 2g-2
. The
maximal components
of
\mathcal{R}_{g}
are those connected components of representations that satisfy
\mathcal{E}(\rho)=\pm 2g-2
. These are two diffeomorphic copies of the Teichmüller space of
S
. We now introduce a class of representations which is of particular relevance for us.

Definition 2.5

(Discrete representation)
.

A representation
\rho\colon\pi_{1}(S)\longrightarrow{\mathrm{PSL}(2,\mathbb{R})}
is said to be
discrete
if the subgroup
\text{Im}(\,\rho\,)=\Gamma
of
{\mathrm{PSL}(2,\mathbb{R})}
is a Fuchsian group. Furthermore, a discrete representation
\rho
is said to be
Fuchsian
if, in addition, it is a faithful representation.

Maximal components of the representation space
\mathcal{R}_{g}
comprises all Fuchsian representations, which are in addition faithful representations. On the other hand, in each
non
-maximal connected component, discrete representations are somewhat rare as they form a closed subset which is a non-empty and nowhere dense, see
[
FW07
, Proposition 1.3]
.

Recalling Definition
2.2
, we can also define:

Definition 2.6

(Elementary representation)
.

A representation
\rho\colon\pi_{1}(S)\longrightarrow{\mathrm{PSL}(2,\mathbb{R})}
is said to be
elementary
if the subgroup
\text{Im}(\,\rho\,)=\Gamma
is an elementary subgroup of
{\mathrm{PSL}(2,\mathbb{R})}
.

In the next subsection we focus on this type of representations and, from Section §
3
all the representations will be supposed non-elementary.

2.4.
Main Theorem
for elementary representations

In the present subsection we aim to prove the Simple Loop Conjecture for elementary representations, see Proposition
2.8
below. We shall make use of the following terminology.

Definition 2.7

(Handle, handle-generators)
.

On surface
S
of genus
g\geq 2
, a
handle
is an embedded subsurface
\Sigma
that is homeomorphic to a punctured tours, and a
handle-generator
is a simple closed curve;
i.e.
one of the generators of
\text{H}_{1}(\Sigma,\mathbb{Z})
. A
pair of handle-generators
for a handle will refer to a pair of simple closed curves
\{\alpha,\beta\}
that generate
\text{H}_{1}(\Sigma,\mathbb{Z})
; in particular,
\alpha
and
\beta
intersect once.

Note that, according to the definition just given, for a surface
S
of hyperbolic type its fundamental group is generated by
g
suitable pairs of handle generators
\{\alpha_{1},\beta_{1},\dots,\alpha_{g},\beta_{g}\}
. We define such a set of generators as
system of handle generators
.

Proposition 2.8
.

Let
S
be a surface of genus
g\geq 2
and let
\rho\colon\pi_{1}(S)\longrightarrow{\mathrm{PSL}(2,\mathbb{R})}
be an elementary representation. Then
\textnormal{ker}(\rho)
contains a simple closed curve.

Before moving on to the proof of Proposition
2.8
, a few preliminaries are in order. Let
\rho
be an elementary representation and suppose, up to conjugation, that

(2.3)

\Gamma=\textnormal{Im}(\,\rho\,)=\,\langle\,g,\,h\,\rangle;

where
g,\,h\in{\mathrm{PSL}(2,\mathbb{R})}
are as in Proposition
2.3
. We may observe that
\Gamma
is a subgroup of the larger group of transformations defined as

(2.4)

\left\{\,z\mapsto\,\lambda\,z^{\varepsilon}\,|\,\lambda\in\mathbb{R}^{*}\,\,\textnormal{ and }\,\,\varepsilon=\pm 1\,\right\}.

Since
\Gamma
is generated by
g(z)=\lambda\,z
and
h(z)=-z^{-1}
, there is a handle generator
\alpha_{i}
such that
\rho(\alpha_{i})(z)=\lambda\,z
and there is another generator
\beta_{j}
such that
\rho(\beta_{j})(z)=-z^{-1}
. In principle,
\alpha_{i}
and
\beta_{j}
may belong to different handles. Observe that the map
\varepsilon:\Gamma\longrightarrow\{\pm 1\}\cong{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}
is a homomorphism. We now invoke
[
Edm82
, Theorem 1.2]
to claim the following

Proposition 2.9
.

The action of
\textnormal{Aut}^{+}\left(\pi_{1}(S)\right)
on the set
\textnormal{Epi}(\pi_{1}(S),\,{\mathbb{Z}}_{n})
is transitive.

As a consequence, we claim the existence of a basis
\{\alpha_{1},\beta_{1},\dots,\alpha_{g},\beta_{g}\}
of handle generators such that

(2.5)

\varepsilon(\,\rho(\alpha_{1})\,)=-1\,\quad\,\text{ and }\,\quad\,\varepsilon(\,\rho(\delta)\,)=1

for all
\delta\in\{\beta_{1},\dots,\alpha_{g},\beta_{g}\}
.

Proof of Proposition
2.8
.

Let
\rho
be an elementary representation and let
\Gamma
be its image. By definition it is an elementary group. According to Proposition
2.3
, the group
\Gamma
is either cyclic or conjugated to a group of the form
\left\langle\,g,\,h\,\right\rangle
with
g(z)=\lambda z
and
h(z)=-1/z
. In the first case, the group
\Gamma
is abelian and the commutator
\gamma=[\alpha,\,\beta]
of any pair of handle generators lies in the kernel of
\rho
. Since
\gamma
is a simple closed curve we are done. Therefore assume to be in the second case. According to the discussion above, there is a system of handle generators
\{\alpha_{1},\beta_{1},\dots,\alpha_{g},\beta_{g}\}
that satisfies the reduced form above, see (
2.5
). For all
i\geq 2
it follows that
\rho\big(\,[\alpha_{i},\,\beta_{i}]\,\big)=I
. Since the commutator of a pair of handle generators is a simple closed separating curve we get the desired result.
∎

It remains to deal with the case of non-elementary and discrete representations in
{\mathrm{PSL}(2,\mathbb{R})}
.

3.
Purely hyperbolic cocompact representations

In the present section we prove
Main Theorem
for a particular class of discrete representations, in which case it reduces to Gabai’s theorem on the Simple Loop Conjecture.

Definition 3.1
.

A Fuchsian group
\Gamma
is said to be
purely hyperbolic
if it contains only hyperbolic elements and the identity. A discrete representation
\rho\colon\pi_{1}(S)\longrightarrow{\mathrm{PSL}(2,\mathbb{R})}
is said to be
purely hyperbolic
if its image is a purely hyperbolic Fuchsian group.

This class of representations clearly includes Fuchsian representations,
i.e.
faithful and discrete representations in
{\mathrm{PSL}(2,\mathbb{R})}
. However, for the
Main Theorem
we need to focus on non-faithful representations, and also by virtue of the discussion in §2.4, only on those that are
non-elementary
. In addition, in this section we shall only consider the case when the image of the purely hyperbolic representation
\rho
is cocompact.

Let
\rho\colon\pi_{1}(S)\longrightarrow{\mathrm{PSL}(2,\mathbb{R})}
be a non-elementary and purely hyperbolic cocompact representation. Let
\Gamma
be the image of
\rho
. Since
\Gamma
is purely hyperbolic, its action on
\mathbb{H}
is free and properly discontinuous and the identification space
\mathbb{H}/\Gamma\cong\mathcal{D}(\Gamma)\cong\Sigma
is a closed surface of hyperbolic type equipped with a complete hyperbolic structure with Fuchsian holonomy representation
\sigma\colon\pi_{1}(\Sigma)\longrightarrow{\mathrm{PSL}(2,\mathbb{R})}
. Since
\sigma
is Fuchsian, it is by definition an injective representation and hence
\pi_{1}(\Sigma)
identifies with
\Gamma
. Therefore, there is a well-defined epimorphism
f_{*}\colon\pi_{1}(S)\longrightarrow\pi_{1}(\Sigma)
such that
\rho=\sigma\circ f_{*}
. Since surfaces are
K(\pi_{1},\,1)
spaces, the map
f
is induced by a map
f\colon S\longrightarrow\Sigma
which is unique up to homotopy. If
\rho
is faithful, hence a Fuchsian representation, then no simple closed curve is mapped to the identity because
\textnormal{ker}(\rho)
is trivial. Note that, in this case we would have
\pi_{1}(S)\cong\Gamma\cong\pi_{1}(\Sigma)
; in particular
S\cong\Sigma
. However since
\rho
is a non-faithful representation, we can invoke the Theorem of Gabai mentioned in §
1
, see
[
Gab85
]
:

Theorem 3.2
.

If
f\colon S\to\Sigma
is a map between closed connected surfaces such that
f_{*}\colon\pi_{1}(S)\to\pi_{1}(\Sigma)
is not injective, then there exists an essential simple closed curve
\gamma\subset S
such that
f(\gamma)
is homotopically trivial.

In other words, if
f_{*}
is not injective, then there exists an essential (non-contractible) simple closed curve
\gamma
in
\textnormal{ker}(f_{*})
. Since the representation
\sigma
is Fuchsian (in particular, injective) it follows that
f_{*}\colon\pi_{1}(S)\longrightarrow\pi_{1}(\Sigma)
is
not
injective. There is an essential simple closed curve
\gamma\in\pi_{1}(S)
such that
f_{*}(\gamma)=1_{\Sigma}
; as a consequence

(3.1)

\rho(\gamma)=\sigma\circ f_{*}(\gamma)=1_{S}

and we have found a simple closed curve in
\textnormal{ker}(\rho)
. Therefore the
Main Theorem
holds for purely hyperbolic cocompact representations.

4.
Crystallographic representations

In the present section we still consider cocompact Fuchsian subgroups
\Gamma
of
{\mathrm{PSL}(2,\mathbb{R})}
and we drop the condition on
\Gamma
to be purely hyperbolic. In other words, we now allow a Fuchsian group to have elliptic transformations. Recall that according to Proposition
2.4
every such elliptic transformation has finite order.

Definition 4.1
.

A group
\Gamma<{\mathrm{PSL}(2,\mathbb{R})}
is said to be
crystallographic
if it a cocompact Fuchsian group; that is
\Gamma
acts properly discontinuously on
\mathbb{H}
and the identification space
\mathbb{H}/\Gamma
is compact. A discrete representation
\rho\colon\pi_{1}(S)\longrightarrow{\mathrm{PSL}(2,\mathbb{R})}
is said to be
crystallographic
if its image is a crystallographic group.

A crystallographic group in the above sense has a presentation of the form

(4.1)

\Gamma(g,\,m_{1},\dots,m_{k})=\left\langle\,a_{1},b_{1},\dots,a_{g},b_{g},c_{1},\dots,c_{k}\,|\,c_{i}^{m_{i}}=1,\,[a_{1},b_{1}]\cdots[a_{g},b_{g}]c_{1}\cdots c_{k}=1\right\rangle

where
g\geq 0
and
k\geq 3
if
g=0
, such that

(4.2)

2-2g-\sum_{i=1}^{k}\left(1-\frac{1}{m_{i}}\right)<0.

For instance, all triangle groups are crystallographic groups, see
[
JW16
]
for a nice exposition of the topic. In this section, we prove our
Main Theorem
for crystallographic representations. We begin with the following

Remark 4.2
.

For a crystallographic group
\Gamma
, the quotient space
\Sigma=\mathbb{H}/\Gamma
carries an orbifold hyperbolic structure on
S
, with cone points
\{p_{1},\dots,p_{k}\}
of angles
2\pi/m_{i}
. Its holonomy representation is a faithful representation
\chi\colon\pi_{1}^{\textnormal{orb}}(\Sigma)\longrightarrow{\mathrm{PSL}(2,\mathbb{R})}
, where the domain, known as
orbifold fundamental group
, is defined as:

(4.3)

\pi_{1}^{\textnormal{orb}}(\Sigma)\cong\left\langle\,\alpha_{1},\beta_{1},\dots,\alpha_{g},\beta_{g},\gamma_{1},\dots,\gamma_{k}\,|\,\gamma_{i}^{m_{i}}=1,\,[\alpha_{1},\beta_{1}]\cdots[\alpha_{g},\beta_{g}]\gamma_{1}\cdots\gamma_{k}=1\right\rangle

with
\{\alpha_{1},\beta_{1},\dots,\alpha_{g},\beta_{g}\}
be a set of handle generators and
\gamma_{i}
is a simple loop around
p_{i}
for
i=1,\dots,k
. We denote by
\pi_{\textnormal{orb}}\colon\pi_{1}^{\textnormal{orb}}(\Sigma)\longrightarrow\pi_{1}(\Sigma)
the natural forgetful map on the fundamental group of the underlying topological surface.

Let
S
be a closed oriented surface of genus
g\geq 2
and let
\rho\colon\pi_{1}(S)\to{\mathrm{PSL}(2,\mathbb{R})}
be a representation whose image
\Gamma
is a co-compact discrete subgroup of
{\mathrm{PSL}(2,\mathbb{R})}
with torsion. Let
\Sigma={\mathbb{H}}/\Gamma
be the corresponding hyperbolic orbifold. There is a continuous
\rho
-equivariant map
\widetilde{f}\colon\widetilde{S}\longrightarrow{\mathbb{H}}
that descends to a continuous map
f\colon S\to\Sigma
. Here
f_{*}\colon\pi_{1}(S)\longrightarrow\pi_{1}(\Sigma)
does not coincide with
\rho
because
\Gamma=\pi_{1}^{\textnormal{orb}}(\Sigma)
, and in fact
f_{\ast}=\pi_{\text{orb}}\circ\rho
. Gabai’s Theorem ensures the existence of a simple closed curve, say
\gamma
, such that
f(\gamma)
is null-homotopic in the target surface
\Sigma
. In order to show that
\gamma\in\text{ker}(\rho)
we need to show that the (possibly immersed) disk
D
that
f(\gamma)
bounds does not include any cone point of the orbifold
\Sigma
.

Remark 4.3
.

Let
\gamma
be a simple closed curve provided by Gabai’s Theorem. Since
f(\gamma)
is homotopic to a point,
f(\gamma)
is the boundary of an immersed disk
D
(we shall describe this as “
f(\gamma)
encloses
D
"). If
D
is disjoint from the set of cone-points then
f(\gamma)
is trivial in
\Gamma=\pi_{1}^{\text{orb}}(\Sigma)
. In particular, in this case
\gamma\in\text{ker}(\rho)
and we are done. On the other hand, if
f(\gamma)
encloses a cone-point we cannot directly infer that
\gamma\in\text{ker}(\rho)
.

In the light of Remark
4.3
above, we proceed as follows. Let
p
be any cone point, let
D
be a closed disc centered at
p
and consider the preimage
f^{-1}(D)=U_{1}\,\cup\,\cdots\,\cup\,U_{k}
. In principle, some components may have positive genus. Up to perturbing
f
a little bit with a homotopy, we may assume two different connected components have disjoint boundaries. Let
U
be any connected component of
f^{-1}(D)
and consider the restriction
f_{|U}\colon U\longrightarrow D
. According to
[
Edm79
, pag. 116]
we give the following

Definition 4.4
.

f_{|U}
is an
allowable map
if
f_{|\partial U}\colon\partial U\longrightarrow\partial D
is a covering.

The mapping
f
defined above need not to be an allowable map when restricted to any preimage of
D
. We show how to modify
f
in such a way the restriction is allowable in the sense of Definition
4.4
above. More precisely we prove the following

Lemma 4.5
.

We may assume
f_{|U}
is an allowable map for any connected component
U
of
f^{-1}(D)
.

Proof.

Let
U
be any connected component of
f^{-1}(D)
and let
\partial U
be its boundary. Let
A
be a closed collar containing
\partial U
in its interior and denote by
\delta_{l}
and
\delta_{r}
the left and right boundary of
A
, where left and right clearly depends on the orientation of
\gamma
– here we do not need to rely on any particular orientation so lets fix one of them. Let
A_{l}
denote the annulus having
\delta_{l}
and
\partial U
as the boundary components. In the same fashion let
A_{r}
denote the annulus having
\delta_{r}
and
\partial U
as the boundary components. Clearly
\delta_{l}\cong\mathbb{S}^{1}
and we can regard
f_{|\,\delta_{l}}
as an element in
\textnormal{Map}(\mathbb{S}^{1},\,\mathbb{S}^{1})
with non-zero degree
\textnormal{deg}(f_{|\,\delta_{l}})=n
. Therefore
f_{|\,\delta_{l}}\simeq g
where
g\colon\mathbb{S}^{1}\to\mathbb{S}^{1}
is the covering map
z\longmapsto z^{n}
. There is a homotopy

(4.4)

\mathcal{H}_{l}\colon[-1,\,0]\,\times\,\mathbb{S}^{1}\longrightarrow\mathbb{S}^{1}

such that
\mathcal{H}_{l}\,(-1,\,p)=f_{|\,\delta_{l}}(p)
and
\mathcal{H}_{l}\,(0,\,p)=g(p)=p^{n}
. On the other hand, we can also regard
f_{|\,\delta_{r}}
as an element in
\textnormal{Map}(\mathbb{S}^{1},\,\mathbb{S}^{1})
of the same degree
n
and hence
f_{|\,\delta_{r}}\simeq g
. In particular there is a homotopy

(4.5)

\mathcal{H}_{r}\colon[0,\,1]\,\times\,\mathbb{S}^{1}\longrightarrow\mathbb{S}^{1}

such that
\mathcal{H}_{r}\,(0,\,p)=g(p)=p^{n}
and
\mathcal{H}_{r}\,(1,\,p)=f_{|\,\delta_{r}}(p)
. By identifying
A
with
[-1,\,1]\times\mathbb{S}^{1}
, these two homotopies can be glued to obtain a continuous function
\mathcal{H}\colon A\longrightarrow f(A)\subset\Sigma
. Since
f\equiv\mathcal{H}
on the set
\{\delta_{l},\,\delta_{r}\}=\partial A
by construction, we can replace
f_{|A}
with the function
\mathcal{H}
. With a small abuse of notation let us still denote the new function with
f
and observe it lifts to a
\rho-
equivariant mapping
\widetilde{S}\longrightarrow\mathbb{H}
. By construction we have that
\partial U=\{\,0\,\}\times\mathbb{S}^{1}\subset A
and
\mathcal{H}(0,\,-\,)=g
; therefore
f_{|\partial U}\colon\partial U\longrightarrow\partial D
is a covering map and hence
f_{|U}
is allowable.
∎

We now invoke the following result, see
[
Edm79
, Theorem 3.1]
.

Theorem 4.6
.

Let
h\colon M\longrightarrow N
be an allowable map of nonzero degree between connected, compact, oriented surfaces. Then there is a pinch map
\pi\colon M\longrightarrow Q
and a branched covering
\beta\colon Q\longrightarrow N
such that
h\simeq\beta\circ\pi

rel

\partial M
.

According
[
Edm79
]
, recall that a mapping
f\colon S\to\Sigma
is called
pinch map
if there is a compact and connected sub-manifold, say
S^{\prime}\subset S
, with boundary consisting of a single simple closed curve in the interior of
S
, such that
\Sigma\cong S\,/\,S^{\prime}
, the quotient of
S
with
S^{\prime}
identified to a point, such that
f
is the quotient map. Note that this is equivalent to say that
\deg(\,f\,)=1
and there are two simple closed non-contractible curves, say
\alpha
and
\beta
, meeting transversally at a single point such that
f(\alpha)
and
f(\beta)
are both contractible in
\Sigma
.

We apply this latter result to our mapping
f_{|U}\colon U\longrightarrow D
to claim that, up to homotopy, we may assume it as a composition of a pinching map with a branched covering. We shall now explain how the case when
U
has positive genus is easier to handle.

Remark 4.7
.

First, recall that
winding number
of a closed curve in a disk around a given point is an integer representing the total number of times that curve travels counterclockwise around the point itself. In our case, if
p
is a cone point of magnitude
\frac{2\pi}{m}
, then a simple closed curve
\delta\subset U\subset S
belongs to
\textnormal{ker}(\rho)
if the winding number of its image via
f_{|U}
is an integral multiple of
m
.

Assume
U
has positive genus and let
\alpha
be a handle generator. Then
f_{|U}(\alpha)
is a loop in
D
and it is necessarily null-homotopic because
D
is a disk. Recall that
p
is the chosen cone-point. If the winding number
w\left(f_{|U}(\alpha),\,p\right)=k\,m
with
k\in\mathbb{Z}
, then
f_{|U}(\alpha)
is trivial in
\Gamma
and hence
\alpha\in\text{ker}(\rho)
. In the case the winding number of
f_{|U}(\alpha)
is not a multiple of
m
we consider a handle generator
\beta
such that
i(\alpha,\,\beta)=1
; where
i(\,\cdot,\,\cdot\,)
denotes as usual the intersection number. Once again, since
D
is a disk,
f_{|U}(\beta)
is a null-homotopic loop. If the winding number
w\left(f_{|U}(\beta),\,p\right)=k\,m
, then
f_{|U}(\beta)
is trivial in
\Gamma
and hence
\beta\in\text{ker}(\rho)
. Even if this is not the case, it always follows that
\delta=[\alpha,\,\beta]
is a closed separating curve in
U\subset S
and
\delta\in\text{ker}(\rho)
because the winding number of
f_{|U}(\delta)
around
p
is zero.

Henceforth, assume
U
has genus zero and
f_{|U}\colon U\longrightarrow D
is a branched covering of degree
\deg(f_{|U})\leq\deg(f)
where the equality holds if
U=f^{-1}(D)
. Note that we are assuming that
f_{|U}
does not admit a pinch map as a factor, as otherwise we will be done. If
p
is a regular value then it has exactly
\deg(f_{|U})
preimages and they all are regular points. Otherwise, if
p
is a branched value we perturb
f_{|U}
a little by changing it by a homotopy if necessary. So we may always assume
p
to be a regular value for
f_{|U}
.

Note that what we have discussed so far applies to any connected component
U
of
f^{-1}(D)
. Two possible situations may happen. Either:

1.

There is a cone point
p\in\Sigma
and a closed disk
D
containing
p
(and no other cone points) such that at least one connected component of
f^{-1}(D)
has positive genus, or

2.

for every cone point
p
and closed disk
D
around
p
all the connected components of
f^{-1}(D)
have genus
0
(together with some boundary components).

In the first case we have already proved the existence of some simple closed curve
\gamma\in\textnormal{ker}(\rho)
. Therefore let us assume to be in the second case. Let
p_{1},\dots,p_{\ell}
be the cone points of
\Sigma
and let
D_{i}
denote a small closed disk around
p_{i}
that contains no cone points other than
p_{i}
. We apply Lemma
4.5
to all boundary components of
f^{-1}(D_{i})
for all
i=1,\dots,\ell
so that we may assume the restriction of
f
to each connected component of the cut surface

(4.6)

S\setminus\bigcup_{i=1}^{\ell}f^{-1}(\partial D_{i})

is an allowable map. We next apply Edmonds’ Theorem
4.6
to the restriction of
f
to every connected component of the cut surface. Note that, in principle, the surface

(4.7)

S^{\prime}=S\setminus\bigcup_{i=1}^{\ell}f^{-1}(D_{i}^{\circ})

may very well be disconnected. The restriction of
f
to any connected component, say
U
, of
S^{\prime}
is a map
f|_{U}:U\to\Sigma\setminus(D_{1}\cup D_{2}\cup\cdots\cup D_{\ell})
. If it admits a pinch then we can find a simple closed curve in
\textnormal{ker}(\rho)
because the target surface has all the disks
D_{i}
removed, so the image of the curve is null-homotopic in the
\ell
-holed surface, and consequently also in
\Sigma
. If the restriction of
f
to every connected component does not admit a pinching map, that restriction is a branched covering for each component. Moreover, by the allowability of these restrictions, each map also restricts to a standard covering map on each boundary circle. Gluing these maps back along the boundary components, we can thus assume that the entire map
f\colon S\longrightarrow\Sigma
is a branched covering. The assumption that
f
is a branched covering will hold in the remaining part of the argument.

Now let
\gamma\subset S
be a simple closed curve provided by Gabai’s Theorem,
i.e.

f(\gamma)
is null-homotopic in
\Sigma
. In fact, Gabai’s proof shows that we could choose
f
, in its the homotopy class, such that
f(\gamma)
it bounds a disk, say
D
, in
\Sigma
. See also the modification of
\gamma
sketched in page 9. Let
S_{o}
be a subsurface bounded by some components of
f^{-1}\big(\partial D\big)
such that the restriction
f_{|S_{o}}\colon S_{o}\longrightarrow D
is a branched covering and
\chi(S_{o})\leq 0
. This exists since the entire map
f
is a branched covering, and at least one component of
f^{-1}(D)
is not a disk otherwise
\gamma
would be not essential. Riemann-Hurwitz formula implies that

(4.8)

\chi(S_{o})=\deg(\,f\,)\cdot\chi(D)-\beta

where
\beta
is a measure of the "total branching". Since
\chi(S_{o})\leq 0
and
\chi(D)=1
, we obtain that
\beta\geq\deg(\,f\,)
. Note that if
D
contains no orbifold points (i.e. cone-points), then for any simple closed curve
\alpha
in
S_{o}
, the image
f(\alpha)
is trivial in the orbifold fundamental group, and we are done.

Suppose now that
D
contains
k\geq 1
orbifold points. By construction no branch point of
f
coincides with a cone-point in
\Sigma
. Remove small disks around each orbifold point from
D
, to get a
k
-holed disk
D_{o}
which has Euler characteristic
\chi(D_{o})=1-k
. Since the map
f_{|S_{o}}
is a
\deg(\,f\,)
-fold covering map away from the branch-points, each such disk has exactly
\deg(\,f\,)
preimages in
S_{o}
. Removing them also, we obtain a surface-with-boundary
S_{o}^{\prime}
having Euler characteristic
\chi(S_{o})-k\deg(\,f\,)
, and a branched covering
f_{|_{S_{o}^{\prime}}}\colon S_{o}^{\prime}\to D_{o}
. We now apply Riemann-Hurwitz’s Theorem to this branched cover and we get
\chi(S_{o}^{\prime})=(1-k)\,\deg(\,f\,)-\beta
. On the other hand, since
\beta\geq\deg(\,f\,)
, we conclude that
\chi(S_{o}^{\prime})\leq-k\deg(\,f\,)
. Hence
\chi(S^{\prime})\leq\big(\chi(D_{o})-1\big)\deg(\,f\,)
. In this case we need to invoke the following result of Edmonds, see
[
Edm79
, Theorem 4.5]

Theorem 4.8
.

Let
f\colon S\longrightarrow\Sigma
be an allowable map between connected, compact, orientable surfaces. Suppose in addition that
\chi(S)\leq|\deg(\,f\,)|\,\Big(\,\chi(\Sigma)-1\,\Big)
. Then there is a non-contractible simple closed curve
\gamma\subset S
such that
f(\gamma)
is null-homotopic.

Note from the preceding discussion that the hypotheses of the above theorem are satisfied. So, by applying that Theorem, there is a simple closed curve
\alpha
on
S_{o}^{\prime}
whose image under
f
is null-homotopic in
D_{o}
. Since the target surface has all the orbifold points removed, such a null-homotopy avoids them, and
f(\alpha)
is null-homotopic in the orbifold fundamental group. Therefore
Main Theorem
holds for crystallographic representations.

5.
Non-cocompact representations

It remains to handle the case of discrete representations
\rho\colon\pi_{1}(S)\longrightarrow{\mathrm{PSL}(2,\mathbb{R})}
with non-cocompact image
\text{Im}(\rho)=\Gamma
;
i.e.

\Gamma
is discrete but
\mathbb{H}/\Gamma
is not compact. We shall refer to such representations as
non-cocompact
representations.

Let
\rho\colon\pi_{1}(S)\longrightarrow{\mathrm{PSL}(2,\mathbb{R})}
be a non-cocompact representation and let
\mathcal{D}(\Gamma)
be the Dirichlet domain of
\Gamma
. Note that
\pi_{1}(S)
is a finitely generated group and hence so is the image
\Gamma
. Since
\Gamma
is a Fuchsian group but non-cocompact,
\mathcal{D}(\Gamma)
is not compact and
\mathbb{H}/\Gamma\cong\Sigma_{g,n}
, where
\Sigma_{g,\,n}
is a finite type surface of genus
g
and
n\geq 1
punctures of hyperbolic type;
i.e.

2-2g-n<0
.

Assume first that
\Gamma
is torsion-free. There exists a
\rho-
equivariant map
\widetilde{f}\colon\widetilde{S}\longrightarrow\mathbb{H}
which induces a map
f\colon S\longrightarrow\Sigma_{g,n}
. In this case
\pi_{1}(\Sigma_{g,n})\cong\Gamma
, and
f_{\ast}:\pi_{1}(S)\to\Gamma
is surjective and coincides with
\rho
. Since
\Sigma_{g,n}
is a punctured surface, it is homotopically equivalent to a bouquet
T
of
2g+n-1
circles,
i.e.
a wedge of
2g+n-1
curves at some point
t_{o}\in T
. In other words, for a point
s_{o}\in\Sigma
there is a retraction
r\colon\Sigma_{g,n}\longrightarrow T
mapping
s_{o}
to
t_{o}
satisfying
r\circ j=\text{id}_{T}
where
j\colon T\longrightarrow\Sigma_{g,n}
is the inclusion map. Since
j\circ r\simeq\text{id}_{\Sigma_{g,\,n}}
it follows from functoriality properties that
j_{*}\colon\pi_{1}(T)\longrightarrow\pi_{1}(\Sigma_{g,n})
and
r_{*}\colon\pi_{1}(\Sigma_{g,n})\longrightarrow\pi_{1}(T)
are inverses of each other and hence
\pi_{1}(T)\cong\pi_{1}(\Sigma_{g,n})\cong F_{2g+n-1}
, which is the free group with
2g+n-1
generators.

Consider the map
r\circ f\colon S\longrightarrow T
. We invoke the following technical result from
[
Jac69
, Lemma 3.2]
.

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
S
be a closed surface. Suppose
g
is a map from
S
into
T
, a wedge at
t_{o}
of
k
simple closed curves
T_{1},\dots,T_{k}
and
g(s_{o})=t_{o}
. If
g_{*}
is an epimorphism, then there is a PL map
h\colon S\to T
so that:

1.

h\simeq g

rel

\{s_{o}\}
;

2.

for each
i
,
1\leq i\leq k
, there is a point
t_{i}\in T_{i}-\{t_{o}\}
so that
h^{-1}(t_{i})
is a single simple closed curve
\gamma_{i}
in
S
and
g
is transverse with respect to
\{t_{1},\dots,t_{k}\}
; and

3.

S-\{\gamma_{i}\}_{1\leq i\leq k}
is connected.

Here
transverse
refers to a technical condition which shall not be relevant for us, see
[
Jac69
, pag. 367]
. A straightforward consequence of this lemma is that
r\circ f
maps a simple closed curve
\gamma
to a contractible (in fact a constant) loop in
T
and hence
\gamma\in\text{ker}(r_{*}\circ f_{*})
. On the other hand, being
r_{*}
an isomorphism, it follows that
\gamma\in\text{ker}(f_{*})
. Since
f_{*}=\rho
in this case, the desired result follows.

Let us finally assume
\Gamma
has torsion elements, that is elliptic transformations. We can argue exactly as above but, in this case,
f_{\ast}=\pi_{\textnormal{orb}}\circ\rho
and hence
f_{*}
no longer coincides with
\rho
; recall that
\pi_{\textnormal{orb}}
is the projection from
\Gamma
to the fundamental group of the surface
\mathbb{H}/\Gamma
that kills the torsion elements. So from the existence of a simple closed curve
\gamma\in\text{ker}(f_{*})
we cannot immediately infer the existence of some simple closed curve in
\textnormal{ker}(\,\rho\,)
.

We proceed as follows. The following argument is an adaption of that used in Section §
4
; therefore we just summarise it in brief and highlight the main differences. Recall that, since
\Gamma
has both torsion and parabolics elements, the quotient space
\Sigma=\mathbb{H}/\Gamma
is a non-compact space and has cone-points as well as cusps or funnels. Even in this case, for a cone point, say
p
, we consider a closed disk
D
centered at
p
and let
f^{-1}(D)=U_{1}\,\cup\,\cdots\,\cup\,U_{k}
. If any one of the preimages
U_{i}
of
D
has positive genus then we can easily infer the existence of a simple closed essential curve in
\textnormal{ker}(\rho)
as already argued in Section §
4
. Let
\{p_{1},\dots,p_{\ell}\}
be the cone points of
\Sigma
and let us assume that for every
p_{i}
and closed disk
D_{i}
around
p_{i}
, all the connected components of
f^{-1}(D_{i})
have genus
0
. Let
D_{\infty}
be an closed set containing all the punctures corresponding to cusps and funnels whose interior is homeomorphic to an
n
-punctured disk for some
n\geq 1
. Once again we apply Lemma
4.5
to all the boundary components of
f^{-1}(D_{i})
for
i=1,\dots,\ell,\infty
. Thus we can also assume that the restriction of
f
to any connected component of

(5.1)

S\setminus\left(\,\bigcup_{i=1}^{\ell}f^{-1}(\partial D_{i})\cup f^{-1}(\partial D_{\infty})\,\right)

is an allowable map. Next we apply Edmonds’ Theorem
4.6
to each connected component of
f^{-1}(D_{i})
as well as to every connected component of the cut surface

(5.2)

S^{\prime}=S\setminus\left(\,\bigcup_{i=1}^{\ell}f^{-1}(D_{i}^{\circ})\cup f^{-1}(D^{\circ}_{\infty})\,\right).

Note that these cut surfaces differ from those defined in (
4.6
) and (
4.7
) since we also remove the pre-images of
D_{\infty}
. In the case the restriction of
f
to any connected component, say
U
, of
S^{\prime}
admits a pinch then we are done. Even in this case, since
f(U)
avoids every disk, we can find a simple closed curve, say
\gamma\in\textnormal{ker}(\rho)
– recall that, in order to find an essential curve
\gamma\in\textnormal{ker}(\rho)
it is sufficient to show that
f(\gamma)
does not enclose any cone point. As before, if the restriction of
f
does not have a pinching map as a factor for any connected component of
S^{\prime}
, then we can assume that the restriction of
f
to
S\setminus f^{-1}(D_{\infty})
is a branched covering onto its image. Let
\gamma
be a simple closed curve provided by Jaco’s Lemma above, see Lemma
5.1
. Note that we can choose the neighborhood
D_{\infty}
above so that the contractible loop
f(\gamma)
is not contained in
D_{\infty}
. Moreover, by changing
\gamma
by a homotopy, still keeping it a simple curve, we can ensure that
f(\gamma)
bounds an embedded disk
D
. We sketch this modification briefly: away from the branch-points of
f
, the homotopy is simply the lift of the homotopy of the contractible arc
f(\gamma)
to an embedded curve, which exists by an innermost-disk argument. If such an innermost-disk, say
E
, contains a branch-point, the homotopy is chosen to collapse the boundary of
E
to that branch-point, and the corresponding lift in the domain surface will be an arc that passes through a ramification point. If
E
contains multiple branch-points, then we first modify the image curve by a homotopy of the boundary arc so that
E
is replaced by a collection of disks, each containing a single branch-point, thus reducing to the case already handled.

Now let
D\subset\Sigma\setminus D_{\infty}
be the disk bounded
f(\gamma)
. If
D
does not contain any cone point in its interior then
\gamma\in\textnormal{ker}(\rho)
. If
D
contains cone points, then we proceed as in Section §
4
: applying Theorem
4.8
we find a simple closed curve
\gamma
in
f^{-1}(D)
so that its image
f(\gamma)
is null-homotopic in
D\setminus\{\,\text{cone points in }\,D\}
. This concludes the proof of the non-compact case and indeed the proof of our
Main Theorem
.
