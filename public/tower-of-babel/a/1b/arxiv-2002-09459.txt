Hidden invariance of last passage percolation and directed polymers
Duncan Dauvergne

Abstract
Last passage percolation and directed polymer models on ℤ 2 \mathds{Z}^{2} are invariant under translation and certain reflections. When these models have an integrable structure coming from either the RSK correspondence or the geometric RSK correspondence (e.g. geometric last passage percolation or the log-gamma polymer), we show that these basic invariances can be combined with a decoupling property to yield a rich new set of symmetries. Among other results, we prove shift and rearrangement invariance statements for last passage times, geodesic locations, disjointness probabilities, polymer partition functions, and quenched polymer measures. We also use our framework to find ‘scrambled’ versions of the classical RSK correspondence, and to find an RSK correspondence for moon polyominoes. The results extend to limiting models, including the KPZ equation and the Airy sheet.

Introduction

Consider an array of random variables
M=\{M_{x}:x\in\mathds{Z}^{2}\}
. For an up-right path
\pi
in
\mathds{Z}^{2}
(henceforth simply a path), the weight of
\pi
in the environment
M
is given by

M(\pi)=\sum_{x\in\pi}M_{x}.

For two points
x,y\in\mathds{Z}^{2}
, we say that
x\nearrow y
if
x_{1}\leq y_{1},x_{2}\leq y_{2}
. Let

\mathds{Z}^{4}_{\uparrow}=\{u=(u^{-};u^{+})\in\mathds{Z}^{2}\times\mathds{Z}^{2}:u^{-}\nearrow u^{+}\}.

For any
u\in\mathds{Z}^{4}_{\uparrow}
, we say that
\pi
is a
u
-path if
\pi
goes from
u^{-}
to
u^{+}
. Define the last passage value

Z_{M}(u)=\max_{\pi}M(\pi),

where the
\max
is taken over all
u
-paths
\pi
. For
\mathbf{u}=(u_{1},\dots,u_{k})\in(\mathds{Z}^{4}_{\uparrow})^{k}
, we also define the multi-point last passage value

Z_{M}(\mathbf{u})=\max_{\pi}\sum_{i=1}^{k}M(\pi_{i}),

where the max is taken over all
k
-tuples of disjoint paths
\pi=(\pi_{1},\dots,\pi_{k})
, where each
\pi_{i}
is a
u_{i}
-path. Note that this is only defined for vectors

for which a set of disjoint paths exists. Letting
\mathcal{E}
denote this set of vectors, we can think of
Z_{M}
as a function from
\mathcal{E}
to
\mathds{R}
.

Last passage percolation is one of the most tractable models in the KPZ (Kardar-Parisi-Zhang) universality class for random growth. Over the past twenty-five years, the KPZ universality class has been the focus of intense and fruitful research. For a gentle introduction to this area, see
[
47
]
. Surveys and reviews focussing on more recent developments include
[
15
,

,
8
,
6
,

.

Any i.i.d. last passage percolation model is invariant under translation and certain reflections:

(I)

For
c\in\mathds{Z}^{2}
, define
(T_{c}M)_{x}=M_{x+c}
. Then
Z_{M}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}Z_{T_{c}M}
.

(II)

Define
(R_{1}M)_{(x,y)}=M_{(y,x)}
. Then
Z_{M}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}Z_{R_{1}M}
.

(III)

Define
(R_{2}M)_{(x,y)}=M_{(-x,-y)}
. Then
Z_{M}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}Z_{R_{2}M}
.

For general weights, we shouldn’t necessarily expect that there are distributional symmetries beyond the ones listed above.

On the other hand, for a related integrable model known as Brownian last passage percolation, Borodin, Gorin, and Wheeler
[
7
]
recently proved a new and remarkable shift invariance. Translated to the context of last passage percolation on
\mathds{Z}^{2}
, this would read as follows.

(IV)

Let
u_{1},\dots,u_{k},v\in\mathds{Z}^{4}_{\uparrow}
, let
c\in\mathds{Z}^{2}
, and set
v^{\prime}=v+(c,c)
. Suppose that for any
i
, every
u_{i}
-path intersects every
v
-path and every
v^{\prime}
-path. Then

(Z_{M}(u_{1}),\dots,Z_{M}(u_{k}),Z_{M}(v))\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}(Z_{M}(u_{1}),\dots,Z_{M}(u_{k}),Z_{M}(v^{\prime})).

See Figure
1
for an example of this invariance in the most basic
k=1
case.

This potential invariance is much more nontrivial than (I)-(III) above, and its proof for Brownian last passage percolation in
[
7
]
is very indirect. The proof proceeds by interpreting Brownian last passage percolation as a certain degeneration of the inhomogeneous coloured stochastic six vertex model, and then proving the analogue of (IV) for that model. Moreover, the proof in the context of the inhomogeneous coloured stochastic six vertex model is itself difficult, relying on the Yang-Baxter integrability of that model, a delicate induction argument, and Lagrange interpolation. The full power and complexity of the coloured stochastic six vertex model is necessary in their proof; the same proof would fail for any degenerations. Since it is not known whether the coloured stochastic six vertex model degenerates to any of the lattice last passage percolation models discussed above, it is not clear how to apply their proof and result in this setting.

This paper is devoted to understanding invariances such as (IV) in the context of lattice last passage percolation. We will also address directed polymer models. Unsurprisingly, (IV) does not hold for general weight distributions (see Example
7.1
). This suggests that we should restrict our focus to integrable models, and that integrability should play a fundamental role.

Last passage percolation is integrable when the weight distribution is either exponential or geometric. When this is the case, the classical Robinson-Schensted-Knuth correspondence (RSK) connects certain processes of multi-point last passage values with pairs of random Young tableaux via Greene’s theorem
[
26
]
. This allows for explicit formulas for last passage value probabilities, and yields the following remarkable independence structure for these models.

For a point
u=(u^{-};u^{+})=(u^{-}_{1},u^{-}_{2};u^{+}_{1},u^{+}_{2})\in\mathds{Z}^{4}_{\uparrow}
, define the vector

\displaystyle u^{k}

\displaystyle=\big((u^{-};u^{+}-(k-1,0)),(u^{-}+(1,0);u^{+}-(k-2,0)),\dots,(u^{-}+(k-1,0);u^{+})\big),

and let

D(u)=\{u^{k}:k\in\{1,\dots,\min(u^{+}_{1}-u^{-}_{1}+1,u^{+}_{2}-u^{-}_{2}+1)\}\}.

We think of
Z_{M}|_{D(u)}
as the collection of all multi-point last passage values going ‘diagonally’ between the lower left and top right corners of
u
(here the notation
Z_{M}|_{X}
is the restriction of the function
Z_{M}
to the set
X
). For any sets
V=\{v_{1},\dots,v_{n}\}\subset\mathds{Z}^{4}_{\uparrow}
where each
v_{i}=(u^{-};u^{+}_{1},v^{+}_{i,2})
for some
v^{+}_{i,2}\in[u^{-}_{2},u^{+}_{2}]
and
W=\{w_{1},\dots,w_{k}\}
where each
w_{i}=(u^{-};w^{+}_{i,1},u^{+}_{2})
for some
w^{+}_{i,1}\in[u^{-}_{1},u^{+}_{1}]
, the last passage values
Z_{M}|_{V}
and
Z_{M}|_{W}
are conditionally independent given
Z_{M}|_{D(u)}
. In words, this says that the collection of last passage values going from
u^{-}
to the right edge of
u
and the collection of last passage values going from from
u^{-}
to the top edge of
u
are conditionally independent given the collection of multi-point last passage values that cross
u
diagonally.

In the case when the weights are geometric, this amounts to the fact that when the RSK correspondence is applied to an
m\times n
matrix of independent geometric variables
M_{i,j}
on
\{0,1,\dots\}
with parameter
\alpha_{i}\beta_{j}
for vectors
\alpha\in\mathds{R}^{m},\beta\in\mathds{R}^{n}
, then the joint law of the two output Young tableaux is proportional to a product of Schur functions

s_{\lambda}(\alpha)s_{\lambda}(\beta).

(1)

See
[
49
]
for general background about the combinatorics of RSK and Schur functions,
[
6
]
for an introduction to the application of RSK and Schur functions in probability theory, or Section 3 of
[
38
]
for a direct derivation of the above fact.
Here
\lambda
is the shared shape of the Young tableaux; it corresponds to our
Z_{M}|_{D(u)}
. The conditional independence comes from the product structure.

This conditional independence extends to sets of vectors
V,W
where each
\mathbf{v}=(v_{1},\dots,v_{k})\in V
has entries
v_{i}
of the form
(u^{-}_{1},v^{-}_{i,2};u^{+}_{1},v^{+}_{i,2})
with
[v^{-}_{i,2},v^{+}_{i,2}]\subset[u^{-}_{2},u^{+}_{2}]
and each
\mathbf{w}\in W
has entries
w_{i}
of the form
(w^{-}_{i,1},u^{-}_{2};w^{+}_{i,1},u^{+}_{2})
with
[w^{-}_{i,1},w^{+}_{i,1}]\subset[u^{-}_{1},u^{+}_{1}]
. That is, the collections of multi-point last passage values going from left to right across
u
and from bottom to top across
u
are conditionally independent given the collection of multi-point last passage values that cross
u
diagonally.

In the context of Brownian last passage percolation, this is a consequence of a recent result of the author, Ortmann, and Virág
[
22
]
regarding preservation of certain last passage values under RSK. It turns out that highly similar results had appeared independently in two much older papers. Biane, Bougerol, and O’Connell
[
3
]
proved this in the case of equal start and end points (see their Lemma 4.8) and Noumi and Yamada
[
37
]
studied closely related phenomena in the discrete and positive-temperature settings.
Using the ideas from
[
37
]
, we provide another proof of this preservation result in Theorem
3.3
.

This extended conditional independence implies the existence of certain Markov chains of multi-point last passage values, giving the following strategy for proving that
(X,Y)
and
(X,Z)
are equal in distribution, where
X,Y,
and
Z
are vectors of (potentially multi-point) last passage values.

1.

Find Markov chains
(X_{0},\dots,X_{n})
and
(Y_{0},\dots,Y_{n})
with
(X_{0},X_{n})=(X,Y)
and
(Y_{0},Y_{n})=(X,Z)
(by using the conditional independence property coming from RSK and the independence of different entries in
M
).

2.

Check that the Markov chains have the same transition probabilities (e.g. by using the basic invariances (I)-(III)).

We give an example of how this strategy yields the symmetry in Figure
1
. The proof we present here is essentially complete, up to showing the conditional indepedendence statements that will follow from RSK (see Theorem
3.3
for details).

Example 1.1
.

Let
M
be an environment of exponential or geometric random variables. Let
u,v,w\in\mathds{Z}^{4}_{\uparrow}
be such that

•

w=v+(c,0;c,0)
for some
c\in\mathbb{N}
,

•

u^{-}_{2}=v^{-}_{2}
and
u^{+}_{2}=v^{+}_{2}
,

•

[v_{1}^{-},v_{1}^{+}]\cup[w_{1}^{-},w_{1}^{+}]\subset[u^{-}_{1},u^{+}_{1}]
.

Then
(Z_{M}(u),Z_{M}(v))\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}(Z_{M}(u);Z_{M}(w))
.

Proof sketch.

Let
(v^{-};w^{+})=[v^{-}_{1},w^{+}_{1}]\times[v^{-}_{2},w^{+}_{2}]=[v^{-}_{1},w^{+}_{1}]\times[u^{-}_{2},u^{+}_{2}]
denote the box whose southwest and northeast corners are
v^{-}
and
w^{+}
, respectively.
The last passage value
Z_{M}(u)
is a function of the random variables
\{M_{x}:x\notin(v^{-};w^{+})\}
and the last passage values going from left-to-right across
(v^{-};w^{+})
:

Z_{M}|_{X},\qquad\text{ where }\qquad X=\{(v_{1}^{-},x;w^{+}_{1},y):x\leq y\in[u^{-}_{2},u^{+}_{2}]\}.

Moreover, since
Z_{M}(v),Z_{M}(w)
are both last passage values going from bottom-to-top across
(v^{-};w^{+})
, by the independence properties coming from RSK,
Z_{M}|_{X}
and
(Z_{M}(v),Z_{M}(w))
are conditionally independent given
Z_{M}|_{D(v^{-};w^{+})}
. Therefore

(Z_{M}(u),Z_{M}|_{D(v^{-};w^{+})},Z_{M}(v))\qquad\;\text{and}\;\qquad(Z_{M}(u),Z_{M}|_{D(v^{-};w^{+})},Z_{M}(w))

are Markov chains.
To check that these Markov chains have the same transition probabilities, we just need to show that

(Z_{M}|_{D(v^{-};w^{+})},Z_{M}(v))\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}(Z_{M}|_{D(v^{-};w^{+})},Z_{M}(w)).

(2)

To see this, observe that under the
180
-degree rotation of the plane that switches the two corners of
(v^{-};w^{+})
, that
(v,D(v^{-};w^{+}))\mapsto(w,D(v^{-};w^{+}))
. Last passage percolation in any i.i.d. environment is invariant under this rotation (it is a product of the reflections
R_{1}
and
R_{2}
and a translation), yielding (
2
).
∎

A slight extension of the above argument quickly gives the symmetry (IV). Moreover, exploring these ideas further yields a whole selection of interesting invariance statements for geometric and exponential last passage percolation.

This proof framework also works in the context of inhomogeneous geometric and exponential last passage percolation, and for the inhomogeneous log-gamma polymer. For the log-gamma polymer, the geometric RSK correspondence (see
[
32
,
37
]
) replaces RSK as the key combinatorial input. The analogue of the preservation result from
[
22
]
in the geometric RSK context is a recent result of Corwin
[
16
]
. Our proof of preservation also works for geometric RSK.

The invariance statements we prove pass through to the large collection of models that arise as limits of geometric and exponential last passage percolation and the log-gamma polymer: taseps from different initial conditions coupled with the same noise; Brownian last passage percolation; Poisson last passage percolation in the plane and the polynuclear growth model; Poisson last passage percolation across lines; the directed landscape, the Airy sheet, and the KPZ fixed point; the O’Connell-Yor Brownian polymer; and the continuum directed random polymer, the KPZ equation, and the multiplicative stochastic heat equation.
Nonetheless, there are still many integrable models where our results do not apply (see Section
6
for some discussion about such models).

The invariance statements for geometric last passage percolation also give rise to new RSK-like bijections and yield other combinatorial consequences.

f:\bigcup U_{i}\to\bigcup T_{c_{i}}U_{i}
be a function satisfying the conditions of Theorem

, and let
\alpha,\beta
be biinfinite sequences indexing an environment of exponential or geometric random variables

. Then there exist rearrangements
\alpha^{\prime},\beta^{\prime}
of
\alpha,\beta
such that

Z_{M_{\alpha,\beta}}|_{\bigcup U_{i}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}Z_{M_{\alpha^{\prime},\beta^{\prime}}}|_{T_{c_{i}}U_{i}}\circ f.

The precise restriction on the types of allowable rearrangements
\alpha^{\prime},\beta^{\prime}
in Theorem
1.5
is a generalization of the following well-known fact. For a box

and two environments of independent geometric or exponential random variables indexed by
(\alpha,\beta)
and

, we have

Z_{M_{\alpha,\beta}}(u)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}Z_{M_{\alpha^{\prime},\beta^{\prime}}}(u)

if and only if

(\alpha^{\prime}_{u^{-}_{1}},\dots,\alpha^{\prime}_{u^{+}_{1}})=\sigma(\alpha_{u^{-}_{1}},\dots,\alpha_{u^{+}_{1}}),\quad\;\text{and}\;\quad(\beta^{\prime}_{u^{-}_{2}},\dots,\beta^{\prime}_{u^{+}_{2}})=\tau(\beta_{u^{-}_{2}},\dots,\beta_{u^{+}_{2}})

for some permutations

and
\tau
. In the case of geometric last passage percolation, this is again a consequence of (
1
), and the fact that Schur functions are symmetric.
For
(\alpha^{\prime},\beta^{\prime})
to be an allowable rearrangement in Theorem
1.5
we require this sort of condition to hold for many boxes. More precisely, we require the following.

•

For each
i
, let
I(i)^{-}_{1}
be the smallest interval
containing
\{u^{-}_{1}:u\in U_{i}\}
. Similarly define
I(i)^{-}_{2},I(i)^{+}_{1},
and
I(i)^{+}_{2}
. Then for any
i
, and any
a\in I(i)^{-}_{1},b\in I(i)^{+}_{1},c\in I(i)^{-}_{2},d\in I(i)^{+}_{2}
, there exist permutations
\sigma
and
\tau
such that

(\alpha^{\prime}_{a+c_{i,1}},\dots,\alpha^{\prime}_{b+c_{i,1}})=\sigma(\alpha_{a},\dots,\alpha_{b}),\quad\;\text{and}\;\quad(\beta^{\prime}_{c+c_{i,2}},\dots,\beta^{\prime}_{d+c_{i,2}})=\tau(\beta_{c},\dots,\beta_{d}).

Finally, in both the inhomogeneous and homogeneous cases the last passage invariance statements extend naturally to certain collections of multi-point last passage values. See Section
4
for precise statements.

The invariance statements from Section

Here we give a few sample results to illustrate this (note that stronger statements are easily possible, if desired). Most of these results follow straightforwardly from Theorem

1.4
Some combinatorial consequences

We can use Theorem
4.2
to obtain new versions of the RSK bijection and other interesting combinatorial consequences. To state these consequences, we first recall some definitions.

A
partition
is a weakly decreasing sequence
\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\dots\lambda_{k})
of positive integers. The size of the partition is
|\lambda|=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\lambda_{i}
. We will also identify (possibly finite) weakly decreasing sequences of the form
(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{k},0,0,\dots)
with the partition
\lambda
. To any partition
\lambda
, the
Young diagram
associated to
\lambda
is the set of squares
\{(i,j)\in\mathds{Z}^{2}:1\leq i\leq\lambda_{j}\}
, see Figure
7
. By associating a set to every partition, we have a naturally defined notion of containment,
\lambda\subset\mu
, and we can define the difference
\lambda\setminus\mu
as a subset of
\mathds{Z}^{2}
. For two partitions
\mu\subset\lambda
, we say that
\lambda\setminus\mu
is a
horizontal strip
if for all
i
, it contains at most one square in the column
\{i\}\times\mathds{Z}
.

A
semistandard Young tableau
of shape
\lambda
is a sequence of partitions
(\emptyset=\lambda_{0},\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{k}=\lambda)
where for all
i
,
\lambda_{i-1}\subset\lambda_{i}
and
\lambda_{i}\setminus\lambda_{i-1}
is a horizontal strip. Equivalently, a semistandard Young tableau of shape
\lambda
is a filling of the corresponding Young diagram with positive integers such that the entries are strictly increasing along columns and weakly increasing along rows.

For a point
u\in\mathds{Z}^{4}_{\uparrow}
and
k\leq\min(u^{+}_{1}-u^{-}_{1}+1,u^{+}_{2}-u^{-}_{2}+1)
define

Z^{\Delta k}_{M}(u)=Z_{M}(u^{k})-Z_{M}(u^{k-1}),

where
Z_{M}(u^{0}):=0
. The quantities
Z^{\Delta k}_{M}(u)
are nonincreasing in
k
when
M
has nonnegative entries. In particular,

Z^{\Delta}_{M}(u):=(Z^{\Delta 1}_{M}(u),Z^{\Delta 2}_{M}(u),\dots)

is a partition whenever
M
has nonnegative integer entries. By Greene’s theorem
[
26
]
, the RSK bijection relates matrices
M
with nonnegative entries to pairs of semistandard Young tableaux by recording certain partition sequences of the form
Z^{\Delta}_{M}(u)
. Our framework yields other bijections of this form.

Let
\mathcal{X}_{n,m}
be the set of
n\times m
matrices
M
with nonnegative integer entries. We think of a matrix
M\in\mathcal{X}_{n,m}
as a weight environment on the box
\{1,\dots,n\}\times\{1,\dots,m\}
so that we can study last passage percolation using the weights given by
M
. Let
\Omega_{n,\lambda}
be the space of semi-standard Young tableaux of length
n
and shape
\lambda
:
\emptyset=\lambda_{0}\subset\lambda_{1}\subset\dots\subset\lambda_{n}=\lambda
. Now consider sequences of intervals

I=I_{1}\subset\dots\subset I_{n}=\{1,\dots,n\}\quad\;\text{and}\;\quad J=J_{1}\subset\dots\subset J_{n}=\{1,\dots,m\}

where
|J_{i}|=i,|I_{i}|=i
. Let
\bar{I}_{i}=I_{i}\times[1,m],\bar{J}_{i}=[1,n]\times J_{i}\in\mathds{Z}^{4}_{\uparrow}
.
Define maps
\Phi_{I},\Psi_{J}
mapping
\mathcal{X}_{n,m}
to the space of semistandard Young tableaux by

\displaystyle\Phi_{I}(M)

\displaystyle=(\emptyset,Z^{\Delta}_{M}(\bar{I}_{1}),Z^{\Delta}_{M}(\bar{I}_{2}),\dots,Z^{\Delta}_{M}(\bar{I}_{n}))\quad\;\text{and}\;

\displaystyle\quad\Psi_{J}(M)

\displaystyle=(\emptyset,Z^{\Delta}_{M}(\bar{J}_{1}),Z^{\Delta}_{M}(\bar{J}_{2}),\dots,Z^{\Delta}_{M}(\bar{J}_{n})).

Theorem 1.8

(The scrambled RSK bijection)
.

Fix
n,m\in\mathbb{N}
. Then for any sequences
I,J
as above, the map

M\mapsto(\Phi_{I}(M),\Psi_{J}(M))

is a bijection from
\mathcal{X}_{n,m}\to\bigcup_{\lambda}\Omega_{n,\lambda}\times\Omega_{m,\lambda}
, where the union is over all partitions
\lambda
. Moreover, for all
i\in[1,n],j\in[1,m]
, we have

\sum_{\ell=1}^{m}M_{i,\ell}=|\Phi_{I}(M)_{\sigma(i)}\setminus\Phi_{I}(M)_{\sigma(i)-1}|\quad\;\text{and}\;\quad\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}M_{\ell,j}=|\Psi_{J}(M)_{\tau(j)}\setminus\Psi_{J}(M)_{\tau(j)-1}|,

where
\sigma(i),\tau(j)
are the smallest indices such that
i\in I_{\sigma(i)},j\in J_{\tau(j)}
.

The case when
I_{i}=[1,i]
and
J_{i}=[1,j]
in Theorem
1.8
is the usual RSK bijection. The functions
\Phi_{I}(M)
and
\Psi_{J}(M)
are the two Young tableaux, and
Z_{M}^{\Delta}(\{1,\dots,n\}\times\{1,\dots,m\})
is their common shape.
If we set
I_{i}=[n-i+1,n]
or
J_{i}=[m-i+1,m]
, then the resulting maps
\Phi_{I}
and
\Phi_{J}
are compositions of the usual RSK bijection with a Schützenberger involution. Setting
I_{i}=[1,i]
and
J_{i}=[m-i+1,m]
yields the Burge (or Hillman-Grassl) correspondence
[
12
]
(see Krattenthaler
[
33
]
for the connection between this correspondence and increasing subsequences).
If we set
n=m
and restrict to the set of permutation matrices, then we recover scrambled versions of the Robinson-Schensted bijection between permutations and pairs of standard Young tableaux.

The scrambled RSK bijections have previously been described in different language (and with entirely orthogonal proofs) by Garver, Patrias, and Thomas
[
24
]
. In that paper, the authors developed bijections between sets of quiver representations and reverse plane partitions. Section 6 of
[
24
]
shows how these bijections for particular type
A
Dynkin quivers yield the RSK and Burge correspondences. More generally, there is an exact correspondence between scrambled RSK bijections on an
n\times m
box and these bijections for Dynkin quivers of type
A_{n+m-1}
with miniscule vertex
n
. Very roughly, the direction of each arrow in the Dynkin quiver encodes whether each of the intervals
I_{i},J_{i}
should grow to the left or to the right.

The classical RSK bijection has a generalization from rectangular arrays of nonnegative integers to fillings of Young diagrams with nonnegative integers, see
[
33
]
. Our framework allows us to generalize the RSK bijection to an even larger class of shapes, known as moon polyominoes.
A

is a finite subset

•

(Convexity) For every

and let

. Then for all
i\in\mathds{Z}
, the sets
S^{i}
and
S_{i}
are (possibly empty) intervals.

•

(Intersection-free) For all
i,j\in\mathds{Z}
, either
S^{i}\subset S^{j}
or
S^{j}\subset S^{i}
. Similarly, either
S_{i}\subset S_{j}
or
S_{j}\subset S_{i}
.

1.5
Directed polymers

The framework used to prove invariance results of last passage percolation also works in the positive temperature setting of directed polymers, where the operations
(\max,+)
are replaced by
(+,\times)
.

Let
M=\{M_{x}:x\in\mathds{Z}^{2}\}
be an array of nonnegative random variables, and for
u\in\mathds{Z}^{4}_{\uparrow}
, define the
polymer partition function

Z_{M}(u)=\sum_{\pi}\prod_{x\in\pi}M_{x},

where the sum is over all
u
-paths
\pi
. Just as in the last passage case, we can also define multi-point partition functions. When
M
consists of i.i.d inverse-gamma random variables, then this is the log-gamma polymer first introduced by Seppäläinen
[
51
]
. Throughout the paper, we use the same notation for last passage values and polymer partition functions as the two cases will be treated together. All our main theorems hold for the log-gamma polymer.

Theorem 1.10
.

Theorems
1.2
,
1.3
,
1.4
, and Corollary
1.6
hold when

is an environment of i.i.d. inverse-gamma random variables and

is the polymer partition function. Theorem
1.5

is an environment of independent random variables where each

has inverse-gamma distribution with parameter

and

for

Next, we write down an analogue of Corollary
1.7
in the polymer case.
The analogue of the last passage geodesic in the directed polymer setting is the
quenched polymer measure

, this is a random probability measure on
u

Q_{M}(u)(\pi)=\frac{1}{Z_{M}(u)}\prod_{v\in\pi}M_{v}.

For this corollary, for
S\subset\mathds{Z}^{2}
we let

denote the pushforward of the measure

under the projection

Corollary 1.11

(Quenched measure invariance)
.

Let
M
be an environment of i.i.d. inverse-gamma random variables, and let

\displaystyle\big[((P_{u\setminus w})_{*}Q_{M}(u):u\in U),((P_{T_{c}v\setminus T_{c}x})_{*}Q_{M}(v):v\in V)\big].

The correspondences in Section
1.4
also have analogues in the polymer setting which we do not explore in this paper. Just as with the geometric RSK correspondence, we expect that these correspondences are not only bijective, but also volume-preserving in log-log variables (see
[
41
]
). For the geometric analogue of the Burge correspondence, this volume-preservation property was recently shown by Bisi, O’Connell, and Zygouras
[
4
]
. Bisi et al.
[
4
]
also obtained a distributional identity between the partition function of the symmetric and persymmetric log-gamma polymer which is closely related to some of the invariance statements we study here. (Note that our invariance statements do not imply this identity since they do not apply to symmetric or persymmetric polymer environments).

1.6
Limiting models

There are many models that fall out through bijections or limits of geometric and exponential last passage percolation and the log-gamma polymer.

For example, exponential last passage percolation can be coupled to multiple taseps evolving with the same noise in a standard way. Brownian last passage percolation, planar Poisson last passage percolation and Poisson lines last passage percolation are all limits of geometric last passage percolation via straightforward limiting procedures (see
[
21
]
, Section 6 for descriptions).

The Airy sheet, the directed landscape, and the KPZ fixed point are (conjecturally universal) scaling limits. The Airy sheet and the directed landscape have been proven to arise as scaling limits of Brownian last passage percolation
[
22
]
and the KPZ fixed point has been proven to arise as the scaling limit of tasep
[
35
]
.

The O’Connell-Yor Brownian polymer (see
[
40
]
) arises as the limit of the log-gamma polymer via the same procedure that takes geometric last passage percolation to Brownian last passage percolation (see
[
7
]
, Section 7.3 for a description). The continuum directed random polymer/multiplicative stochastic heat equation/KPZ equation arises in a certain limit of the log-gamma polymer. See
[
2
,
1
]
for background and convergence of single-point partition functions and
[
42
,
18
]
for a description and convergence of certain multi-point partition functions.

Our results also apply to all of these models. Appropriate statements can be found by examining how certain invariances are transformed under limiting procedures or certain mappings.

We only include two of the more striking results here for illustrative purposes. For these corollaries,
\mathcal{S}:\mathds{R}^{2}\to\mathds{R}

as

. See Section
5.3
for a more precise setup. For this next result, for sets
S,T\subset\mathds{R}^{2}

if

for all

We also define translations

1.7
Related work

Shortly after the first version of this paper was posted, Galashin
[
23
]
found a new flip symmetry of the coloured stochastic six vertex model. This flip symmetry leads to a large number of other symmetries for the stochastic six vertex model and its degenerations. In the context of models that are degenerations or limits of both the stochastic six vertex model and the log-gamma polymer (e.g. the KPZ equation), this flip symmetry can be used to give alternate proofs of some of our results. For example, the aformentioned Conjecture 1.6 in
[
7
]
can be deduced from both Theorem 1.9 in
[
23
]
and our Theorem
1.2
. Galashin’s flip symmetry can be viewed as a special, but fundamental, case of Theorem
1.2
.

Interestingly, our proofs and the proofs in
[
23
]
are quite different. Galashin’s methods are mostly algebraic, relying on a new connection between the stochastic six vertex model and the Hecke algebra of the symmetric group.

2.2
Decoupled models

We now state the main theorem we will use regarding integrable last passage and polymers models.

Theorem 2.2
.

Let
M,Z_{M}
be one of the following three environments (with associated polymer model):

(i)

Fix biinfinite sequences
\alpha,\beta
of positive numbers with
\alpha_{i}\beta_{j}<1
for all
i,j
. Let
M
be an array of independent geometric random variables where

\mathbb{P}(M_{(i,j)}=n)=(1-\alpha_{i}\beta_{j})(\alpha_{i}\beta_{j})^{n},

for
n=0,1,\dots,
and define
Z_{M}
with the
(\max,+)
-algebra.

(ii)

Fix biinfinite sequences
\alpha,\beta
of real numbers with
\alpha_{i}+\beta_{j}>0
for all
i,j
. Let
M
be an array of independent exponential random variables with
\mathbb{E}M_{(i,j)}=(\alpha_{i}+\beta_{j})^{-1}
,
and define
Z_{M}
with the
(\max,+)
-algebra.

(iii)

Fix two biinfinite sequences
\alpha,\beta
of real numbers with
\alpha_{i}+\beta_{j}>0
for all
i,j
. Let
M
be an array of independent inverse-gamma random variables with parameter
\alpha_{i}+\beta_{j}
.

Then for every box
u\in\mathds{Z}^{4}_{\uparrow}
, the vectors
Z_{M}|_{H(u)}
and
Z_{M}|_{V(u)}
are conditionally independent given
Z_{M}|_{D(u)}
.

Theorem
2.2
is well-known. It can be interpreting as saying that the three models have a particular ‘decoupling’ property.
In the first two cases, it follows from the connection between these models and the RSK bijection. The key fact coming from RSK is that it is a bijection from matrices onto all pairs of Young tableaux with a shared shape; the fact that the tableaux only need to have a shared shape yields the decoupling property. The result in these cases was first observed by Johansson (see
[
29
]
and
[
27
]
). See also Section 3 in O’Connell
[
38
]
for a derivation of these properties that more closely uses the language of our paper. In the third case, the geometric RSK correspondence plays the role that RSK plays in the first two cases. Here the result was shown by Corwin, O’Connell, Seppäläinen, and Zygouras (see Corollary 3.3. in
[
41
]
and also equations (1.2) and (1.3) in
[
19
]
).

In the context of Theorem
2.2
, if we permute elements of the sequences
\alpha,\beta
, then certain last passage statistics are known to be preserved. This is summarized by the following theorem. For biinfinite sequences
\alpha,\beta
and
u\in\mathds{Z}^{4}_{\uparrow}
, define the vectors

\alpha_{u}=(\alpha_{i}:(i,j)\in u\text{ for some }j)\quad\;\text{and}\;\quad\hat{\beta}_{u}=(\beta_{j}:(i,j)\in u\text{ for some }i).

Theorem 2.3
.

Let
M_{\alpha,\beta},Z_{M_{\alpha,\beta}},M_{\alpha^{\prime},\beta^{\prime}},Z_{M^{\prime}_{\alpha^{\prime},\beta^{\prime}}}
be two polymer models of the same type (i), (ii), or (iii) in Theorem
2.2
, indexed by different sequences
(\alpha,\beta)
and
(\alpha^{\prime},\beta^{\prime})
. Then for any box
u
, we have

Z_{M_{\alpha,\beta}}|_{H(u)}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}Z_{M_{\alpha^{\prime},\beta^{\prime}}}|_{H(u)}

whenever
\hat{\beta}_{u}=\hat{\beta}_{u}^{\prime}
and
\alpha_{u}
is a permutation of
\alpha_{u}^{\prime}
.
That is, the joint distribution of all last passage statistics from
u^{-}
to the
right
side of
u
remains unchanged under permutations of the column parameters
\alpha_{i}
. Similarly, we have

Z_{M_{\alpha,\beta}}|_{V(u)}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}Z_{M_{\alpha^{\prime},\beta^{\prime}}}|_{V(u)}

whenever
\alpha_{u}=\alpha_{u}^{\prime}
and
\hat{\beta}_{u}
is a permutation of
\hat{\beta}_{u}^{\prime}
.
That is, the joint distribution of all last passage statistics from
u^{-}
to the
top
side of
u
remains unchanged under permutations of the row parameters
\beta_{i}
.

Theorem
2.3
follows from symmetries of the RSK and geometric RSK correspondences. Again, it is well known and was observed in the first two cases in
[
29
]
and in the third case in
[
19
]
. In the case of geometric last passage percolation, this follows from (
1
) above and the fact that Schur functions are symmetric in their variables. The exponential last passage percolation statement follows by taking the standard geometric-to-exponential limit. For the case of the log-gamma polymer, this follows from the formula (1.2) in
[
19

and the fact that the

-Whittaker functions

used in that formula are symmetric in permutations of the index vector
\theta
(e.g. see Section 2 of
[
41
]
for the definition of these functions and their basic properties).

In both cases, the fact that underlies Theorem
2.3
is that the law of

is given by a product of functions
s_{\cdot}(\alpha)s_{\cdot}(\beta)
whose values depend only on the order of the vectors
\alpha
and
\beta
. In the case of geometric last passage percolation, these are normalized Schur symmetric functions, e.g. see equation (1.1) in
[
19
]
, and in the case of the log-gamma polymer these are essentially Whittaker functions, see equation (1.2) in
[
19
]
.

\bar{H}(u)
follows by symmetry.

By using the observations of Propositions
3.1
and
3.2
, we can extend the decoupling property from Theorem
2.2
to more general partition functions. To state these extensions, we introduce the following notation.
Let

\sqcup S:=\bigcup_{s\in S}s

denote the union (as a subset of
\mathds{Z}^{2}
) of all boxes in
S
. We use the
\sqcup
notation for the middle union above to distinguish the operation from the usual union of sets. Finally, for any function
F:\mathds{Z}^{4}_{\uparrow}\to\mathds{Z}^{4}_{\uparrow}
(e.g.
D,\hat{D},H,V,\bar{H},\bar{V})
, let

F(S)=\bigcup_{s\in S}F(s).

Definition 3.4
.

Let
E,F,G\subset\mathds{Z}^{4}_{\uparrow}
. Then
(E,F,G)
is a
Markov triple
if there are partitions
E=E_{1}\cup E_{2},F=F_{1}\cup F_{2},G=G_{1}\cup G_{2}
such that the following conditions hold:

(i)

\sqcup E\cap\sqcup G\subset\sqcup F
.

(ii)

For
i\neq j
,
E_{i}\times F_{j},F_{i}\times G_{j},G_{j}\times E_{i}\subset\mathcal{N}
and
f_{1}\cap f_{2}=\emptyset
for any distinct boxes
f_{1},f_{2}\in\mathcal{F}
.

(iii)

E_{1}\times F_{1}
and
F_{1}\times G_{1}
are contained in
\mathcal{H}\cup\mathcal{N}
. Similarly,
E_{2}\times G_{2}\cup G_{2}\times F_{2}\subset\mathcal{V}\cup\mathcal{N}
.

Finally, for
U\subset\mathds{Z}^{4}_{\uparrow}
, define the extended endpoint set

\mathcal{E}_{D}(U)=\{\mathbf{u}\in\mathcal{E}:\mathbf{u}=(u_{1}^{k_{1}},\dots,u_{m}^{k_{m}})\in\mathcal{E},\text{ where }u_{i}\in U,k_{i},m\in\mathbb{N}\}.

The set
\mathcal{E}_{D}(U)
contains all endpoints consisting of tuples of points in the diagonal set
D(U)
.

Proposition 3.5
.

Let
(E,F,G)
be a Markov triple and let
M,Z_{M}
be as in (i), (ii), or (iii) of Theorem
2.2
, i.e.
Z_{M}
is either last passage percolation with geometric or exponential weights or the log-gamma polymer.

Then
Z_{M}|_{\mathcal{E}_{D}(E)}
and
Z_{M}|_{\mathcal{E}_{D}(G)}
are conditionally independent given
Z_{M}|_{\mathcal{E}_{D}(F)}
. In other words,
(Z_{M}|_{\mathcal{E}_{D}(E)},Z_{M}|_{\mathcal{E}_{D}(F)},Z_{M}|_{\mathcal{E}_{D}(G)})
is a Markov chain.

Note that since any pair of distinct boxes in
F
are disjoint, that
Z_{M}|_{\mathcal{E}_{D}(F)}
and
Z_{M}|_{D(F)}
are functions of each other, so we can equivalently show
(Z_{M}|_{\mathcal{E}_{D}(E)},Z_{M}|_{D(F)},Z_{M}|_{\mathcal{E}_{D}(G)})
is also a Markov chain.

Proof.

Define

E_{1,H}=\bigcup_{e\in E_{1}}\{u\subset e:(u,e)\in\mathcal{H}\},\quad\;\text{and}\;\quad E_{2,V}=\bigcup_{e\in E_{2}}\{u\subset e:(u,e)\in\mathcal{V}\}.

Observe that whenever

\mathbf{u}=(u_{1}^{k_{1}},\dots,u_{m}^{k_{m}})\in\mathcal{E}_{D}(E_{1}\cup E_{2}),

then for every
i
, either
u^{k_{i}}_{i}\in\mathcal{E}(E_{2,V})
or
\hat{u}^{k_{i}}_{i}\in\mathcal{E}(E_{1,H})
. Therefore by the correspondence (
6
),
Z_{M}|_{\mathcal{E}_{D}(E)}
is a function of
Z_{M}|_{\mathcal{E}(E_{1,H}\cup E_{2,V})}
.
Moreover, by Definition
3.4
(ii, iii) and the construction of
E_{1,H},E_{2,V}
, we have

(E_{1,H}\cup E_{2,V})\times F_{1}\subset\mathcal{H}\cup\mathcal{N},\quad(E_{1,H}\cup E_{2,V})\times F_{2}\subset\mathcal{V}\cup\mathcal{N},\qquad\sqcup(E_{1,H}\cup E_{2,V})=\sqcup E.

Therefore by Proposition
3.1
,
Z_{M}|_{\mathcal{E}(E_{1,H}\cup E_{2,V})}
is a measurable function of

\{M_{x}:x\in\sqcup E\setminus\sqcup F\},\quad Z_{M}|_{\bar{H}(F_{1})},\quad\;\text{and}\;\qquad Z_{M}|_{\bar{V}(F_{2})}.

Hence so is
Z_{M}|_{\mathcal{E}_{D}(E)}
. By Proposition
3.2
, this implies that
Z_{M}|_{\mathcal{E}_{D}(E)}
is measurable function of
\{M_{x}:x\in\sqcup E\setminus\sqcup F\},Z_{M}|_{H(F_{1})}
, and
Z_{M}|_{V(F_{2})}
.
Similarly,
Z_{M}|_{\mathcal{E}_{D}(G)}
is measurable function of

\{M_{x}:x\in\sqcup G\setminus\sqcup F\},Z_{M}|_{V(F_{1})}
, and
Z_{M}|_{H(F_{2})}
. Therefore since
M
has independent entries, and
\sqcup E\cap\sqcup G\subset\sqcup F
, to prove the proposition we just need to show that

(Z_{M}|_{H(F_{1})},Z_{M}|_{V(F_{2})})\quad\;\text{and}\;\quad(Z_{M}|_{V(F_{1})},Z_{M}|_{H(F_{2})})

are conditionally independent given
Z_{M}|_{D(F)}
. This follows immediately from the decoupling property of
M
and the fact that any pair of distinct boxes in
F
are disjoint.
∎

By iterating Proposition
3.5
, we can get longer Markov chains. We note one such construction here that will be particularly useful to us.

Corollary 3.6
.

Let
(E,F,G)
and
(E,F^{\prime},G)
be two Markov triples that satisfy Definition
3.4
for partitions
E=E_{1}\cup E_{2},F=F_{1}\cup F_{2},F^{\prime}=F_{1}^{\prime}\cup F_{2}^{\prime},G=G_{1}\cup G_{2}
. Suppose that

•

F_{1}\times F_{1}^{\prime}\subset\mathcal{H}\cup\mathcal{N}
and
F_{2}\times F_{2}^{\prime}\subset\mathcal{V}\cup\mathcal{N}
,
and
F_{i}\times F_{j}^{\prime}\subset\mathcal{N}
for
i\neq j
,

•

\sqcup F\cap\sqcup G\subset\sqcup F^{\prime}
and
\sqcup E\cap\sqcup F^{\prime}\subset\sqcup F
.

Then
(E\cup F,F^{\prime},G)
and
(E,F,F^{\prime}\cup G)
are Markov triples and

(Z_{M}|_{\mathcal{E}_{D}(E)},Z_{M}|_{\mathcal{E}_{D}(F)},Z_{M}|_{\mathcal{E}_{D}(F^{\prime})},Z_{M}|_{\mathcal{E}_{D}(G)})

(13)

is a Markov chain.

Proof.

It is straightforward to check the conditions of Definition
3.4
for the triples
(E\cup F,F^{\prime},G)
and
(E,F,F^{\prime}\cup G)
. The fact that (
13
) is a Markov chain then follows from two applications of Proposition
3.5
.
∎

We will refer to a quadruple
(E,F,F^{\prime},G)
satisfying the conditions of Corollary
3.6
as a
Markov quadruple
.

4
Hidden invariance

In this section, we use the decoupling statements of Section
3
to find new invariances of the models in Theorem
2.2
.

4.1
Homogeneous models

We start with the homogeneous case. Throughout this subsection we assume that
Z_{M}
is either the log-gamma polymer with i.i.d. weights, or else geometric or exponential last passage percolation with i.i.d. weights
(e.g. the cases when all
\alpha_{i},\beta_{i}
are equal in Theorem
2.2
).

Definition 4.1
.

Let
\mathcal{F}
be the set of bijections between subsets of
\mathds{Z}^{4}_{\uparrow}
defined by the following four rules:

(i)

(Contains translations) For any
c\in\mathds{Z}^{2}
, the translation
T_{c}:\mathds{Z}^{4}_{\uparrow}\to\mathds{Z}^{4}_{\uparrow}\in\mathcal{F}
.

(ii)

(Contains reflections) The reflections
R_{1},R_{2}:\mathds{Z}^{4}_{\uparrow}\to\mathds{Z}^{4}_{\uparrow}
are in
\mathcal{F}
.

(iii)

(Closed under restriction, composition, inverses, and finite exhaustion) If
f:S\to T,g:T\to Q
are elements of
\mathcal{F}
, then
g\circ f:S\to Q
is in
\mathcal{F}
. Also, if
f:S\to T\in\mathcal{F}
, then
f^{-1}:T\to S\in\mathcal{F}
and
f|_{Q}:Q\to f(Q)
is in
\mathcal{F}
for any
Q\subset S
. Finally, if
f:S\to T
is a bijection between infinite subsets of
\mathds{Z}^{4}_{\uparrow}
, then
f\in\mathcal{F}
if
f|_{Q}\in\mathcal{F}
for any finite subset
Q\subset S
.

(iv)

(Factoring) Suppose that
(U,V,W),(U^{\prime},V^{\prime},W^{\prime})
are any Markov triples, and that
f:U\cup V\cup W\to U^{\prime}\cup V^{\prime}\cup W^{\prime}
is a function with
f(U)=U^{\prime},f(V)=V^{\prime},f(W)=W^{\prime}
. Suppose also that
f|_{U\cup V},f|_{V\cup W}\in\mathcal{F}
. Then
f\in\mathcal{F}
.

To state our main invariance theorem, we will extend a function
f:S\to T\in\mathcal{F}
to a function
\bar{f}:\mathcal{E}_{D}(S)\to\mathcal{E}_{D}(T)
by letting

f(u_{1}^{k_{1}},\dots,u_{\ell}^{k_{\ell}})=(f(u_{1})^{k_{1}},\dots,f(u_{\ell})^{k_{\ell}})

for any
u_{1},\dots,u_{\ell}\in S
. As part of the proof of the next theorem, we will check that any such
\bar{f}
is a well-defined bijection.

Theorem 4.2
.

Let
M
be an i.i.d. model from Theorem
2.2
. Let
f:S\to T\in\mathcal{F}
, and let
\bar{f}
be its extension to a function from
\mathcal{E}_{D}(S)
to
\mathcal{E}_{D}(T)
. Then

Z_{M}|_{\mathcal{E}_{D}(S)}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}Z_{M}|_{\mathcal{E}_{D}(T)}\circ\bar{f}.

To prove Theorem
4.2
, we first state and prove a lemma about functions in
\mathcal{F}
.

Lemma 4.3
.

Any
f\in\mathcal{F}
preserves
\mathcal{N}
and
\mathcal{H}\cup\mathcal{V}
. That is,
(u,v)\in\mathcal{N}
if and only if
(f(u),f(v))\in\mathcal{N}
, and
(u,v)\in\mathcal{H}\cup\mathcal{V}
if and only if
(f(u),f(v))\in\mathcal{H}\cup\mathcal{V}
.

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}\subset\mathcal{F}
be the set of functions that preserve
\mathcal{N}
and
\mathcal{H}\cup\mathcal{V}
. It is easy to see that
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}
contains translations, reflections, and is closed under restriction, composition, inverses, and finite exhaustion. It remains to show that
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}
is closed under factoring. Suppose that
f:U\cup V\cup W\to U^{\prime}\cup V^{\prime}\cup W^{\prime}
is such that

f(U)=U^{\prime},\quad f(V)=V^{\prime},\quad f(W)=W^{\prime},\quad f|_{U\cup V},f|_{V\cup W}\in\mathcal{F}^{\prime}.

The map
f
preserves
\mathcal{N}
and
\mathcal{H}\cup\mathcal{V}
for all pairs in
(U\cup V)^{2}
or in
(V\cup W)^{2}
by the corresponding properties of the restrictions
f|_{U\cup V}
and
f|_{V\cup W}
. Now suppose that
(u,w)\in U\times W
. We first check that

\text{ if }(f(u),f(w))\notin\mathcal{N},\text{ then }(u,w)\notin\mathcal{N}.

(14)

For this, observe that if
(U,V,W)
is a Markov triple, then:

(I)

If
(u,w)\in(U\times W)\cap\mathcal{H}
, there exists
v\in V
such that
(u,v),(v,w)\in\mathcal{H}
. Similarly, if
(u,w)\in(U\times W)\cap\mathcal{V}
, there exists
v\in V
such that
(u,v),(v,w)\in\mathcal{V}
.

(II)

If
(u,v,w)\in U\times V\times W
are such that
(u,v),(v,w)
cross, then either
(u,v),(v,w)
and
(u,w)
are all in
\mathcal{H}
or they are all in
\mathcal{V}
.

Now if
(f(u),f(w))\notin\mathcal{N}
then since
(f(u),f(w))\in U^{\prime}\times W^{\prime}
, by (ii) and (iii) of Definition
3.4
,
(f(u),f(w))\in\mathcal{H}\cup\mathcal{V}
. Therefore by (I) above there exists
v\in V^{\prime}
such that
(f(u),v),(v,f(w))\in\mathcal{H}\cup\mathcal{V}
. Using that
f|_{U\cup V},f|_{V\cup W}
preserve
\mathcal{H}\cup\mathcal{V}
, we have that

(u,f^{-1}(v)),(f^{-1}(v),w)\in\mathcal{H}\cup\mathcal{V}.

Key observation (II) then implies that
(u,w)
crosses, proving (
14
). The converse of (
14
) follows from the same argument applied to
f^{-1}
. Finally, since
f
is a bijection and
U\times W,U^{\prime}\times W^{\prime}\subset\mathcal{N}\cup\mathcal{H}\cup\mathcal{V}
, we have that
(u,w)\in\mathcal{H}\cup\mathcal{V}
if and only if
(f(u),f(w))\in\mathcal{H}\cup\mathcal{V}
for
(u,w)\in U\times W
. This completes the proof.
∎

Proof of Theorem
4.2
.

Let
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}
be the set of bijections
f\in\mathcal{F}
for which
\bar{f}
is a well-defined bijection. We first check that
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}=\mathcal{F}
by showing that
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}
is closed under (i-iv) of Definition
4.1
. The only nontrivial thing to check is that
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}
is closed under factoring. Let
f:S\to T
with
S=U\cup V\cup W
and
T=U^{\prime}\cup V^{\prime}\cup W^{\prime}
be such that
f(U)=U^{\prime},f(V)=V^{\prime},f(W)=W^{\prime}
, and
f|_{U\cup V},f|_{V\cup W}\in\mathcal{F}^{\prime}
.

Suppose that
(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v})\in\mathcal{E}_{D}(S)
where
\mathbf{u}=(u_{1}^{k_{1}},\dots,u_{\ell}^{k_{\ell}})\in\mathcal{E}_{D}(U)
and
\mathbf{v}=(v_{1}^{m_{1}},\dots,v_{n}^{m_{n}})\in\mathcal{E}_{D}(V\cup W)
. By Definition
3.4
,
U\times(V\cup W)\subset\mathcal{N}\cup\mathcal{H}\cup\mathcal{V}
. In particular, this means that the set of disjoint paths
\mathcal{D}((\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}))
is empty unless
\{u_{1},\dots,u_{\ell}\}\times\{v_{1},\dots,v_{n}\}\subset\mathcal{N}.
By Lemma
4.3
, this in turn implies that

\{f(u_{1}),\dots,f(u_{\ell})\}\times\{f(v_{1}),\dots,f(v_{n})\}\subset\mathcal{N}.

(15)

Moreover,
f(\mathbf{u})\in\mathcal{E}_{D}(U^{\prime})
and
f(\mathbf{v})\in\mathcal{E}_{D}(V^{\prime}\cup W^{\prime})
since
\bar{f}
is well-defined on the restrictions of
f
, and hence
(f(\mathbf{u}),f(\mathbf{v}))\in\mathcal{E}_{D}(T)
by (
15
). The bijectivity of
\bar{f}
then follows from the bijectivity of
f
.

Now let
\mathcal{G}
be the set of bijections
f
for which the theorem holds. The set
\mathcal{G}
contains translations by the homogeneity of
M
. It contains reflections again by the homogeneity of
M
along with the general identity (
7
) between
u^{k}
and
\hat{u}^{k}
. It is clear that
\mathcal{G}
is closed under restriction, composition, inverses, and finite exhaustion. To check that
\mathcal{G}
is closed under factoring, suppose
f
is as in Definition
4.1
(iv) with restrictions
f|_{U\cup V},f|_{V\cup W}\in\mathcal{G}
. Note that
U\times V,U\times W,V\times W\subset\mathcal{N}\cup\mathcal{H}\cup\mathcal{V}
as
(U,V,W)
is a Markov triple. Therefore the restriction
Z_{M}|_{\mathcal{E}_{D}(U\cup V\cup W)}
is a function of
Z_{M}|_{\mathcal{E}_{D}(U)},Z_{M}|_{\mathcal{E}_{D}(V)},
and
Z_{M}|_{\mathcal{E}_{D}(W)}
(and similarly for
U^{\prime},V^{\prime},W^{\prime}
). The joint distribution of
Z_{M}|_{\mathcal{E}_{D}(U)},Z_{M}|_{\mathcal{E}_{D}(V)},
and
Z_{M}|_{\mathcal{E}_{D}(W)}
is preserved by
f
by Proposition
3.5
.
∎

As written, Theorem
4.2
is rather abstract and doesn’t give a great deal of insight into the nature of the functions in
\mathcal{F}
. We spend the rest of this subsection exploring what types of functions
\mathcal{F}
contains. We don’t attempt to give a complete classification, but rather hope to give a good account of the kinds of functions
\mathcal{F}
does and does not contain. Beyond those in Lemma
4.3
, we first note a few more restrictions on the types of functions in
\mathcal{F}
.

Lemma 4.4
.

Suppose that
f:S\to T\in\mathcal{F}
. Then

(i)

For all
s\in S
,
f(s)
can be obtained from
s
by translation and the reflections
R_{1}
and
R_{2}
.

(ii)

If
u,v\in S
and
(u,v)\notin\mathcal{H}\cup\mathcal{V}\cup\mathcal{N}
, then the restriction
f|_{\{u,v\}}
is a product of reflections and a translation. In other words, the pair
(u,v)
is congruent to the pair
(f(u),f(v))
.

(iii)

Let
(s_{1},\dots,s_{k})
be a
k
-tuple of distinct points in
S
with
(s_{i},s_{i+1})\in\mathcal{H}\cup\mathcal{V}
for all
i
. Let
(a_{1},\dots,a_{k-1})\in\{\mathcal{H},\mathcal{V}\}^{k-1}
be the sequence where
(s_{i},s_{i+1})\in a_{i}
for all
i
, and
(b_{1},\dots,b_{k-1})
be the corresponding sequence defined from
(f(s_{1}),\dots,f(s_{k}))
. Then either
a_{i}=b_{i}
for all
i
, or else
a_{i}\neq b_{i}
for all
i
.

When
|S|\leq 2
, it turns out that any function satisfying the conditions of Lemmas
4.3
and
4.4
is in
\mathcal{F}
. When
|S|\geq 3
, this is no longer the case. See Example
7.2
for a map
f:S\to T
satisfying the conclusions of Lemmas
4.3
and
4.4
with
|S|=|T|=3
, but yet
Z_{M}|_{S}\neq Z_{M}|_{T}\circ f
for any bijection
f
when
M
is geometric or exponential last passage percolation.

Proof.

Each of the three properties above holds when
f
is a translation or a reflection, and each of these properties is closed under restriction, composition, inverses, and finite exhaustion. We just need to check that each of the properties is closed under factoring for functions in
\mathcal{F}
. To this end, let
f:U\cup V\cup W\to U^{\prime}\cup V^{\prime}\cup W^{\prime}
be a function in
\mathcal{F}
constructed as in Definition
4.1
(iv), and suppose that the restrictions
f|_{U\cup V},f|_{V\cup W}
satisfy the conclusions of the lemma. Now, if

(u,v)\in(U\cup V\cup W)^{2}\setminus\mathcal{H}\cup\mathcal{V}\cup\mathcal{N},

then since
(U,V,W)
is a Markov triple, either
(u,v)\in U^{2}
or else
(u,v)\in W^{2}
. In particular, this implies that
f
inherits property (ii) from
f|_{U\cup V}
and
f|_{V\cup W}
. The function
f
also has property (i) since property (ii) implies property (i).
It is enough to check property (iii) for chains
(s_{1},s_{2},s_{3})
of length three.

Case 1:

s_{1},s_{3}\in V\cup W,s_{2}\in U\cup V
.   If
s_{2}\in V
, then the assertion follows from property (iii) of the restriction
f|_{V\cup W}
. If not, then the pair
((s_{1},s_{2}),(s_{2},s_{3}))
is either in
\mathcal{H}\times\mathcal{V}
or
\mathcal{V}\times\mathcal{H}
; the other cases are ruled out by Definition
3.4
(ii),(iii). The same assertion holds for
((f(s_{1}),f(s_{2})),(f(s_{1}),f(s_{2}))
since
f(U)=U^{\prime},f(V)=V^{\prime}
and
f(W)=W^{\prime}
. Hence the assertion holds in this case. This argument also covers the case when the roles of
W
and
U
are switched.

Case 2:

s_{1},s_{2}\in V\cup W,s_{3}\in U
with
(s_{2},s_{3})\in\mathcal{H}
.   All remaining cases are symmetric versions of this case. By observation (I) from Lemma
4.3
, we can find
v\in V
such that
(s_{2},v),(v,s_{3})\in\mathcal{H}
. Now, assertion (iii) holds for the triple
(s_{1},s_{2},v)
since all points lie in
V\cup W
. It holds for
(v,s_{2},s_{3})
by case 1 (applied to the case where the roles of
W
and
U
are switched). Therefore assertion (iii) also holds for
(s_{1},s_{2},s_{3})
.
∎

We now give examples of the kind of functions that lie in
\mathcal{F}
. We build up starting with two nearly trivial examples.

Lemma 4.5
.

1.

(Basic reflection) Let
u\in\mathds{Z}^{4}_{\uparrow}
be such that
w=u+(0,k;0,0),w^{\prime}=u+(0,0,0,-k)\in\mathds{Z}^{4}_{\uparrow}
for some
k\in\mathbb{N}
. Let
f(u)=u
and
f(w)=w^{\prime}
. Then
f:\{u,w\}\to\{u,w^{\prime}\}\in\mathcal{F}
.

2.

(Disjoint movements) Let
\{U_{i}:i\in I\},\{U_{i}^{\prime}:i\in I\}
be two collections of subsets of
\mathds{Z}^{4}_{\uparrow}
indexed by a (finite or countable) index set
I
. Suppose that
U_{i}\times U_{j},U_{i}^{\prime}\times U_{j}^{\prime}\subset\mathcal{N}
for any
i\neq j
. Suppose also that for all
i
, there exists a function that
f_{i}:U_{i}\to U_{i}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{F}
. Let
f:\bigcup U_{i}\to\bigcup U_{i}^{\prime}
be the function whose restriction to
U_{i}
is
f_{i}
. Then
f\in\mathcal{F}
.

Proof.

The map in (1) is a composition of a translation and two reflections. We prove (2) in the case when
|I|
is finite by a straightforward induction. The
|I|=1
case is evident. Now assume that the statement holds for collections of
j
sets whenever
j\leq k-1
, and consider
U_{1},\dots,U_{k},U^{\prime}_{1},\dots,U_{k}^{\prime}
satisfying the assumptions of (2) and the function
f
above. Then
U=(U_{1},\dots,U_{k-1}),V=\emptyset,W=U_{k}
and
U^{\prime}=(U^{\prime}_{1},\dots,U^{\prime}_{k-1}),V^{\prime}=\emptyset,W^{\prime}=U^{\prime}_{k}
are two Markov triples and
f|_{U},f|_{W}\in\mathcal{F}
by the inductive hypothesis. Hence
f\in\mathcal{F}
. Extension to infinite index sets
I
follows since
\mathcal{F}
is closed under finite exhaustion.
∎

The next lemma already illustrates the kinds of nontrivial functions contained in
\mathcal{F}
.

Lemma 4.6
.

(Basic transposition)
Let
u\in\mathds{Z}^{4}_{\uparrow}
, and let
v,w,v^{\prime},w^{\prime}\subset u
with
\{u\}\times\{v,w,v^{\prime},w^{\prime}\}\subset\mathcal{H}
and
(v,w),(v^{\prime},w^{\prime})\in\mathcal{N}
. Suppose also that
v^{\prime}
is a translate of
v
and
w^{\prime}
is a translate of
w
. Define
f:\{u,v,w\}\to\{u,v^{\prime},w^{\prime}\}
by
f(u)=u,f(v)=v^{\prime},f(w)=w^{\prime}
. Then
f\in\mathcal{F}
.

Note that Lemma
4.6
also holds with
w
and
w^{\prime}
omitted since
\mathcal{F}
is closed under restrictions.

Proof.

Without loss of generality, we may assume that
v^{-}_{1}<w^{-}_{1}
.

Case 1:

v^{\prime}=T_{(k,0)}v
for some

and
v^{\prime-}_{1}<w^{\prime-}_{1}
. Let
b_{v}
be the smallest box containing both
v,v^{\prime}
. In this case, both

(U=\{u\},V=\{b_{v}\},W=\{v,w\})\quad\;\text{and}\;\quad(U,V,W^{\prime}=\{v^{\prime},w^{\prime}\})

are Markov triples. Now extend the map
f
so that
f(b_{v})=b_{v}
. Then
f|_{U\cup V}\in\mathcal{F}
since it is simply the identity. Also
f|_{V\cup W}\in\mathcal{F}
as it is of the form of Lemma

(2). To see this, observe that

and that

satisfies Lemma

(i). Hence

.

Case 2:

{v^{\prime}}^{-}_{1}<{w^{\prime}}^{-}_{1}
. If

, then

by a symmetric version of Case 1. Any other map with

can be obtained by composing functions where

and functions where
w=w^{\prime}
.

Case 3:

{v^{\prime}}^{-}_{1}>{w^{\prime}}^{-}_{1}

is a composition of a translation and two reflections with a function of the form of Case 2.

We will show that richer sets of functions lie in
\mathcal{F}
by using Corollary

. Reinterpreted in terms of the set of functions
\mathcal{F}
, that corollary says the following.

Corollary 4.7
.

Let
(U,V,V^{\prime},W)
and
(\bar{U},\bar{V},\bar{V}^{\prime},\bar{W})
be two Markov quadruples (i.e. they satisfy the assumptions of Corollary

). Let

be a bijection mapping

, and suppose that
f|_{U\cup V},f|_{V\cup V^{\prime}},f|_{V^{\prime}\cup W}\in\mathcal{F}
. Then
f\in\mathcal{F}
.

Corollary
4.7
follows immediately from property (iv) of
\mathcal{F}
and Corollary
3.6
. Our strategy for applying it will be as follows. Consider a map
f:U\cup W\to\bar{U}\cup\bar{W}
mapping
U\mapsto\bar{U},W\mapsto\bar{W}
. Suppose that we can find connecting sets
V,V^{\prime}
and
\bar{V},\bar{V}^{\prime}
such that
(U,V,V^{\prime},W)
and
(\bar{U},\bar{V},\bar{V}^{\prime},\bar{W})
are Markov quadruples, and that
f
can be extended to map from
V
to
\bar{V}
and
V^{\prime}
to
\bar{V}^{\prime}
. Then
f\in\mathcal{F}
if
f|_{U\cup V},f|_{V\cup V^{\prime}},f|_{V^{\prime}\cup W}\in\mathcal{F}
. We will check that each of these restrictions lie in
\mathcal{F}
via Lemma
4.5
and Lemma
4.6
.
To enact this strategy, we first make a few straightforward observations about the structure of crossing boxes.

Lemma 4.8
.

(i)

Suppose that
u,w,u^{\prime},w^{\prime}\in\mathds{Z}^{4}_{\uparrow}
are such that
(u,w),(u,w^{\prime}),(u^{\prime},w)\in\mathcal{H}
and
(u,u^{\prime}),(w,w^{\prime})\notin\mathcal{N}
. Then
(u^{\prime},w^{\prime})\notin\mathcal{N}
.

(ii)

Suppose that
U\times W\subset\mathcal{H}
. Let
v
be the smallest box containing
\sqcup W
. Then
U\times\{v\}\subset\mathcal{H}
. Also, if
U\times W\subset\mathcal{N}
and
\sqcup W=v\in\mathds{Z}^{4}_{\uparrow}
, then
U\times\{v\}\subset\mathcal{N}
.

(iii)

If
(u,v)\in\mathcal{H}
and
(v,w)\in\mathcal{H}
, then
(u,w)\in\mathcal{H}
.

All parts of Lemma
4.8
follow immediately from definition. We now prove a technical lemma that will aid in the construction of the connecting sets
V,V^{\prime}
. For this lemma and throughout this section, we introduce a graph on
\mathds{Z}^{4}_{\uparrow}
(with induced graphs on its subsets) whereby a pair
u,v\in\mathds{Z}^{4}_{\uparrow}
are connected by an edge whenever
(u,v)\notin\mathcal{N}
. For the remainder of the paper, the notion of a connected subset of
\mathds{Z}^{4}_{\uparrow}
is with respect to this graph structure.

Lemma 4.9
.

Let
U,W\subset\mathds{Z}^{4}_{\uparrow}
. Suppose that we can partition
U=U_{1}\cup U_{2}
and
W=W_{1}\cup W_{2}
such that
U_{1}\times W_{1},W_{2}\times U_{2}\subset\mathcal{H}\cup\mathcal{N}
and
U_{1}\times W_{2},U_{2}\times W_{1}\subset\mathcal{N}
. Then there exists a set
V\subset\mathds{Z}^{4}_{\uparrow}
such that
(U,V,W)
is a Markov triple. The set
V
can be constructed as follow.
For every
w\in W
, let

G(w)=\{u\in U:(u,w)\text{ cross}\}\qquad\;\text{and}\;\qquad\bar{G}(u)=\{w\in W:(u,w)\text{ cross}\}

For
(u,w)\in U\times W\cap(\mathcal{V}\cup\mathcal{H})
, let
G(w)_{u}
be the connected component of
G(w)
containing
u
, and let
\bar{G}(u)_{w}
be the connected component of
\bar{G}(u)
containing
w
. Then

\sqcup G(w)_{u}\cap\sqcup\bar{G}(u)_{w}

is a box for every
(u,w)\in U\times W\cap(\mathcal{V}\cup\mathcal{H})
. Letting
v(u,w)\in\mathds{Z}^{4}_{\uparrow}
be this box, we can take

V=\{v(u,w):(u,w)\in U\times W\cap(\mathcal{V}\cup\mathcal{H})\}.

Proof.

Fix a pair
(u,w)\in U\times W\cap(\mathcal{V}\cup\mathcal{H})
. We first show that
\sqcup G(w)_{u}\cap\sqcup\bar{G}(u)_{w}
is a box. Without loss of generality, we may assume
(u,w)\in\mathcal{H}
.
First observe that
G(w)_{u}\subset U_{1},G(u)_{w}\subset W_{1}
. Secondly, by appealing to Lemma
4.8
(i), for any
u^{\prime}\in G(w)_{u},w^{\prime}\in\bar{G}(u)_{w}
that are connected by edges to
u,w
, respectively,
(u^{\prime},w^{\prime})\notin\mathcal{N}
, and so since
u^{\prime}\in U_{1},w^{\prime}\in W_{1}
, we must have
(u^{\prime},w^{\prime})\in\mathcal{H}
. Iterating this along the edges of
G(w)_{u},\bar{G}(u)_{w}
gives that
G(w)_{u}\times\bar{G}(u)_{w}\subset\mathcal{H}
.
Now define

I_{w,u}=\bigcup_{w^{\prime}\in\bar{G}(u)_{w}}[w^{\prime-}_{1},w^{\prime+}_{1}]\qquad\;\text{and}\;\qquad J_{w,u}=\bigcup_{u^{\prime}\in G(w)_{u}}[u_{2}^{\prime-},u_{2}^{\prime+}]

The connectivity of
\bar{G}(u)_{w}
and
G(w)_{u}
implies that
I_{w,u}
and
J_{w,u}
are intervals. Moreover,
u^{\prime}\cap w^{\prime}=[w^{\prime-}_{1},w^{\prime+}_{1}]\times[u_{2}^{\prime-},u_{2}^{\prime+}]
for all
u^{\prime},w^{\prime}\in G(w)_{u}\times\bar{G}(u)_{w}
since
G(w)_{u}\times\bar{G}(u)_{w}\subset\mathcal{H}
. Therefore
\sqcup\bar{G}(u)_{w}\cap\sqcup G(w)_{u}=I_{w,u}\times J_{w,u}
, and hence is a box.

Now, with
V
as in the statement of the lemma, we check that
(U,V,W)
is a Markov triple. Definition
3.4
(i) follows since
u\cap w\subset v(u,w)
for all
u,w
. Next, we show that

for any boxes
v_{1}\neq v_{2}\in V
, we have
v_{1}\cap v_{2}=\emptyset
.

(16)

For this, we just check that
v(u,w)=v(\bar{u},\bar{w})
whenever
v(u,w)\cap v(\bar{u},\bar{w})\neq\emptyset
. First observe that since
G(w)_{u}\times\bar{G}(u)_{w}\subset\mathcal{H}
for all
(u,w)\in\mathcal{H}
and
G(w)_{u}\times\bar{G}(u)_{w}\subset\mathcal{V}
for all
(u,w)\in\mathcal{V}
that

G(w)_{u}=G(w^{\prime})_{u^{\prime}}\quad\text{ for any }\quad w^{\prime}\in G(w)_{u},u^{\prime}\in\bar{G}(u)_{w}.

(17)

Next, if
v(u,w)\cap v(u^{\prime},w^{\prime})\neq\emptyset
, then for some
w^{\prime}\in G(w)_{u},u^{\prime}\in\bar{G}(u)_{w},\bar{w}^{\prime}\in G(\bar{w})_{u},\bar{u}^{\prime}\in\bar{G}(\bar{u})_{w}
we have

\bar{u}^{\prime}\cap u^{\prime}\cap w^{\prime}\cap\bar{w}^{\prime}\neq\emptyset.

In particular, (
17
) then implies that

G(w)_{u}=G(w^{\prime})_{u^{\prime}}=G(\bar{w}^{\prime})_{\bar{u}^{\prime}}=G(\bar{w})_{\bar{u}}.

(18)

Similarly,
\bar{G}(u)_{w}=\bar{G}(\bar{u})_{\bar{w}}
. Combining this with (
18
) proves (
16
).
This argument also implies that we can partition
G=G_{1}\cup G_{2}
, where
g(u,w)\in G_{1}
if and only if
(u,w)\in\mathcal{H}
and
g(u,w)\in G_{2}
if and only if
(u,w)\in\mathcal{V}
and
u\neq w
.

This partition, along with
U=U_{1}\cup U_{2},W=W_{1}\cup W_{2}
, satisfies conditions (ii) and (iii) of Definition
3.4
. For example, suppose that
u\in U_{1}
and
g(u^{\prime},w^{\prime})\in G_{1}
and
(u,g(u^{\prime},w^{\prime}))\notin\mathcal{N}
. Then
u\cap w\cap g(u^{\prime},w^{\prime})\neq\emptyset
for some
w\in G(u^{\prime})_{w^{\prime}}
, so
g(u,w)\cap g(u^{\prime},w^{\prime})\neq\emptyset
and hence
g(u,w)=g(u^{\prime},w^{\prime})
. Therefore
(u,g(u,w))\in\mathcal{H}
. Checking the other parts of Definition
3.4
(ii), (iii) is similar
∎

Proposition 4.10

(Column Transposition)

be the set constructed from

and

as in that lemma. We can write

and

are disjoint. Moreover, by the connectedness of

and
W_{b}

for all

v(u,w)
for all

.

from

.

.

(Slides)

Proof.

Proof of 1.
(Towers):   We proceed inductively starting with

by a common amount, we may assume that

4.2
Inhomogeneous models

In this section, we only work with environments and models that fit into the framework of Theorem

for nonconstant sequences

for such environments. Throughout this section, we assume that different environments

The type of statements that we can obtain will be of the form

Z_{M_{\alpha,\beta}}|_{\mathcal{E}_{D}(S)}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}Z_{M_{\phi_{f}(\alpha,\beta)}}|_{\mathcal{E}_{D}(T)}\circ\bar{f},

(20)

where

, and

is another pair of biinfinite sequences obtained in an

. Also, it is easy to construct examples of

and biinifinite sequences
\alpha,\beta
for which no

satisfying (
20

exists and exactly what the restrictions on it are.
Rather we will just illustrate the sort of statements that can be proven by showing the tower case of Proposition

and the corresponding statement in Theorem

We start with a few simple lemmas. The first is an analogue of Lemma

introduced prior to Theorem

Lemma 4.14
.

Let

and suppose
w=u+(1,0;0,0),w^{\prime}=u+(0,0,-1,0)\in\mathds{Z}^{4}_{\uparrow}
. Let

be biinfinite sequences such that

is a permutation of
\alpha^{\prime}_{u}

is a permutation of

Z_{M_{\alpha,\beta}}|_{\mathcal{E}_{D}(u,w)}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}Z_{M_{\alpha^{\prime},\beta}}|_{\mathcal{E}_{D}(u,w^{\prime})}\circ\bar{f}.

Proof.

The map

is composed of two reflections and a translation. Applying these reflections and translations to

to give

gives that

Z_{M_{\alpha,\beta}}|_{\mathcal{E}_{D}(u,w)}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}Z_{M_{\bar{\alpha},\bar{\beta}}}|_{\mathcal{E}_{D}(u,w^{\prime})}\circ\bar{f}.

(21)

On the other hand, by Theorem

,

only depends on the ordering of
\beta_{u}
. In particular, in (

for

without affecting the distributional equality. Next, observe that by Propositions
3.1
and

, that

, these two objects are determined by only the sets of numbers in

for

).
∎

Lemma 4.15

First, we introduce last passage percolation on

\{F_{i}:\mathds{R}\to\mathds{R}:i\in\mathds{Z}\}

dF([a,b]\times\{i\})=F_{i}(b)-F_{i}(a).

\mathcal{Q}_{\uparrow}=\{u=(x,n;y,m)\in(\mathds{R}\times\mathds{Z})^{2}:x\leq y,n\leq m\}.

Definition 5.5

(Brownian last passage percolation)
.

Let

be a sequence of independent two-sided Brownian motions. For

. This is defined as long as

is small enough so that disjoint paths exist.

Brownian last passage percolation arises as a distributional limit of geometric last passage percolation on long thin boxes. In particular, Brownian last passage percolation inherits all invariance statements in Theorem
4.2
from geometric last passage percolation. We state a few select statments here.

.

6
Concluding remarks

We end with a few questions that arise from this work.

Classification of invariances

A natural problem raised by this paper is the following.

Problem 6.1
.

Let
Z_{M}
be the last passage function of either i.i.d. exponential or geometric last passage percolation, or the partition function of the i.i.d. log-gamma polymer. Find the set
\mathcal{G}
of all bijections
g:S\to T
between subsets of
\mathds{Z}^{4}_{\uparrow}
such that

Z_{M}|_{S}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}Z_{M}|_{T}\circ g.

When
|S|=|T|=1
, it is relatively straightforward to see that the only functions
g
that work are translations and reflections. When
|S|=|T|=2
, a case-by-case analysis based on comparing probabilities of tail events reveals that the only invariances are those in
\mathcal{F}
. The situation gets more complicated for sets with more than
2
elements, where enumerating cases quickly becomes unwieldy. In particular, it is unclear to me whether or not
\mathcal{G}=\mathcal{F}
.

Other models and other symmetries

It would be interesting to see if a version of our techniques can work for generalizations of the models studied in this paper, or for other related models. The key tools needed to carry the proofs through in a more general setting would seem to be an analogue of the RSK correspondence that allows for decoupling, together with a version of Theorem
3.3
. Generalizations of the geometric RSK correspondence do exist further up the hierarchy of Macdonald processes (see
[
9
,
36
,
43
,
11
]
). However, these generalizations involve randomization and are no longer bijective. Moreover, even some basic translation and reflection symmetries become highly nontrivial results in the context of vertex models (see the discussion on page 6 in
[
7
]
and the relevant references
[
10
,
5
]
).

Two models where the situation is more promising and the key tools are available are the Seppäläinen-Johansson model
[
50
,
28
]
of last passage percolation (where dual RSK, rather than RSK, is the relevant correspondence), and the strict-weak polymer model (where a dual version of geometric RSK is used, see
[
39
,
20
]
).

There are also other geometric symmetries of exponential last passage percolation based on the Burke property of this model
[
14
,
44
]
. The most striking of these is perhaps an equality in law between the infinite geodesic tree in a given direction and its dual
[

Combinatorics

The bijections found in Section

arose incidentally from the main theorems in the paper and we have not attempted to study them here. It would be interesting explore these bijections further and to see if the methods used to obtain them can be extended to other settings.

For example, are there nice local descriptions of these bijections, analogous to the local descriptions of the usual RSK bijection? More generally, what features of the usual RSK bijection extend to these new bijections? How are the output tableaux coming from different scrambled RSK bijections related to each other? Here we provide a few basic examples mentioned in the previous sections regarding the limitations of invariance. The first shows how certain invariance statements might not hold for nonintegrable models.

Example 7.1
.

Let
M
be an i.i.d. environment of random variables such that

\mathbb{P}(M_{i,j}=1)=1-\epsilon,\quad\mathbb{P}(M_{i,j}=0)=\epsilon

for some
\epsilon>0
. Then, unlike in the case of decoupled polymer models, for small enough
\epsilon>0
the joint distribution of the last passage values

Z_{M}(1,1;2,3)\quad\;\text{and}\;\quad Z_{M}(1,1;2,1)

is different from the joint distribution of the last passage values

Z_{M}(1,1;2,3)\quad\;\text{and}\;\quad Z_{M}(1,2;2,2).

Proof.

Observe that

\mathbb{P}(Z_{M}(1,1;2,3)=4\;|\;Z_{M}(1,1;2,1)=1)\leq 1/2,

(29)

since conditionally on
Z_{M}(1,1;2,1)=1
, the random variable
M(1,1)
is equal to
0
with probability
1/2
. On the other hand,

\mathbb{P}(Z_{M}(1,1;2,3)=4\;|\;Z_{M}(1,2;2,2)=1)=(1-\epsilon)^{3},

(30)

since conditionally on
Z_{M}(1,2;2,2)=1
, exactly one of
M(1,2)
and
M(2,2)
is
1
, and there is one path from
(1,1)
to
(2,3)
that picks up this
1
, along with three random variables that are independent of
Z_{M}(1,2;2,2)
. When
\epsilon<1-2^{1/3}
, (
29
) and (
30
) imply that the two joint distributions are different.
∎

A similar construction to Example
7.1
is possible in the polymer setting.

f:S\to T
satisfying the conditions of Lemma
4.4
but
Z_{M}|_{S}\neq Z_{M}|_{T}\circ f
when
M
is geometric or exponential last passage percolation.

Example 7.2
.

Let
u=(1,1;2,3),v=(2,2;2,2),w=(1,3;2,3),
and
w^{\prime}=(1,1;2,1)
and let
S=\{u,v,w\},T=\{u,v,w^{\prime}\}
. Define
f:S\to T
by
f(u)=u,f(v)=v,
and
f(w)=w^{\prime}
. Then
f
satisfies (i)-(iv) of Lemma
4.4
, but
Z_{M}|_{S}\neq Z_{M}|_{T}\circ f
when
M
consists of independent exponential or geometric random variables and
Z_{M}
is last passage percolation.

Proof.

First, for any point
x\in\mathds{Z}^{2}
,
M_{x}
either has support
\{0,1,2,\dots\}
(for geometric last passage percolation) or support
(0,\infty)
(for exponential last passage percolation). Therefore the events
(Z_{M}(v)\geq 1,Z_{M}(w)\geq 1)
and
(Z_{M}(v)\geq 1,Z_{M}(w^{\prime})\geq 1)
have positive probability.

Now, since there is a
u
-path
\pi
containing
w^{\prime}
and
v
, we have that

\mathbb{P}(Z_{M}(u)\geq 2\;|\;Z_{M}(v)\geq 1,Z_{M}(w^{\prime})\geq 1)=1.

On the other hand,

\mathbb{P}(Z_{M}(u)\geq 2\;|\;Z_{M}(v)\geq 1,Z_{M}(w)\geq 1)<1,

since there is no
u
-path
\pi
containing
v
and
w
, and there is a positive probability that
M_{x}\in[0,\epsilon)
for all
x\in u\setminus(v\cup w)
, for any
\epsilon>0
.
∎
