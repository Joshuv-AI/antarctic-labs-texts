Multivariate generalized Pareto distributions along extreme directions
Anas Mourahib, Anna Kiriliouk, Johan Segers

Abstract
When modeling a vector of risk variables, extreme scenarios are often of special interest. The peaks-over-thresholds method hinges on the notion that, asymptotically, the excesses over a vector of high thresholds follow a multivariate generalized Pareto distribution. However, existing literature has primarily concentrated on the setting when all risk variables are always large simultaneously. In reality, this assumption is often not met, especially in high dimensions. In response to this limitation, we study scenarios where distinct groups of risk variables may exhibit joint extremes while others do not. These discernible groups are derived from the angular measure inherent in the corresponding max-stable distribution, whence the term extreme direction . We explore such extreme directions within the framework of multivariate generalized Pareto distributions, with a focus on their probability density functions in relation to an appropriate dominating measure. Furthermore, we provide a stochastic construction that allows any prespecified set of risk groups to constitute the distribution’s extreme directions. This construction takes the form of a smoothed max-linear model and accommodates the full spectrum of conceivable max-stable dependence structures. Additionally, we introduce a generic simulation algorithm tailored for multivariate generalized Pareto distributions, offering specific implementations for extensions of the logistic and Hüsler–Reiss families capable of carrying arbitrary extreme directions.



1
Introduction

The statistical description of extremes is a long-standing subject of research, with the ultimate goal of extrapolating beyond the range of the data and estimating probabilities of events more extreme than those observed in the past. The foundations of univariate extreme value theory were established many decades ago through the derivation of the asymptotic distributions of suitably normalized block maxima and
excesses over high thresholds.
Modeling multivariate extreme events is a bigger challenge because, contrary to the univariate setting, the class of multivariate extreme-value distributions is infinite-dimensional. Over the last thirty years, numerous parametric extreme-value models have been proposed with the goal of capturing the tail dependence structure of a random
d
-dimensional vector
\boldsymbol{X}=(X_{1},\ldots,X_{d})
; see, e.g.,
Beirlant et al. 2004
for an early overview or
Cooley et al. 2010
,
Ballani and Schlather 2011
and
Opitz 2013
for more recent examples. However, many such models are limited to the setting where all components of
\boldsymbol{X}
can become large simultaneously. While this may be sufficient when the dimension
d
of
\boldsymbol{X}
is low, more often than not, one encounters the setting where some variables may be large while at the same time, the others are of smaller order.
In finance, for example, characterizing groups of stocks within a portfolio that have the potential of suffering major losses simultaneously is of great importance. Managing flood risk requires identifying and modeling groups of gauging stations where extreme water discharges may occur simultaneously.

Groups of variables that may become large without the others being so—in an asymptotic sense to be made precise below—will be called
extreme directions
. More specifically, if
J\subseteq\{1,\ldots,d\}
is such that the components
(X_{j},j\in J)
can be large simultaneously while the other components
(X_{j},j\in\{1,\ldots,d\}\setminus J)
are small, then
J
defines an extreme direction. The terminology is in line with a characterization of the support of the angular measure of the multivariate extreme-value distribution to which
\boldsymbol{X}
is attracted. In the literature, such groups are also referred to as extreme clusters
[
6
]
, extreme features
[
27
]
, or concomitant extremes
[
20
]
.
Many parametric extreme-value families are limited to the single extreme direction
J=\{1,\ldots,d\}
. The overarching goal of this paper is to explore the case when other extreme directions than
\{1,\ldots,d\}
exist, and this in terms of several well-known objects in extreme-value theory, and multivariate generalized Pareto (mgp) distributions in particular.

In the literature, tail dependence is described mathematically through various concepts, such as the exponent measure, the angular measure, and the stable tail dependence function, among others. When the focus is on modeling multivariate threshold exceedances, the mgp distribution is a natural candidate
[
34
]
. In the univariate case, peaks-over-threshold modeling with the generalized Pareto distribution is well-established
[
10
]
, while in the multivariate set-up, statistical modeling is quite recent
[
35
,
29
,
24
,
16
,
23
]
.

Asymptotically, the class of mgp distributions represents proper multivariate probability distributions on a region where at least one variable is large. Moreover, parametric mgp models can be constructed quite easily via the
generators
proposed in
Kiriliouk et al. 2018
.

Current theory for mgp distributions does not cover extreme directions other than
J=\{1,\ldots,d\}
, a gap we aim to fill. From their generators, we derive expressions for mgp densities with respect to an appropriate dominating measure.

Furthermore, we propose a stochastic construction based on smoothed max-linear models, wherein the strong dependence within factors inherent in max-linear models is substituted with a more lenient dependency framework. Our construction is designed to accommodate all mgp models, regardless of their extreme directions.

Learning extreme directions from data has become an active area of research.

Goix et al. 2016
,
Goix et al. 2017
focus on the identification of extreme directions, providing a sparse representation of the support of the angular measure.

Chiapino and Sabourin 2016
and
Chiapino et al. 2019
study an alternative algorithm with better performance when the number of extreme directions is high.
Jalalzai and Leluc 2021
introduce an approach based on empirical risk minimization.
Chiapino et al. 2020
propose a Dirichlet mixture model for the angular measure of a heavy-tailed distribution whose support has been identified and apply it to anomaly detection.
Simpson et al. 2020
exploit the concept of hidden regular variation to identify extreme directions. Finally,
Meyer and Wintenberger 2021
,
Meyer and Wintenberger 2023
frame extreme directions within what they call sparse regular variation.

The study of extreme directions is related to that of clustering and dimension reduction methods for multivariate extremes.
For example,
Chautru 2015
and
Janßen and Wan 2020
identify extreme directions via spherical
k
-means clustering of the angles
\boldsymbol{X}/\|\boldsymbol{X}\|
provided
\|\boldsymbol{X}\|
is large.
Fomichov and Ivanovs 2023
provide theoretical results in favor of this approach and extend it to a spherical
k
-principal components algorithm. Clustering angles is also considered in
Medina et al. 2024
, based on a random
k
-nearest neighbors graph. Finally,
Cooley and Thibaud 2019
and
Drees and Sabourin 2021
propose to perform principal component analysis of the angles
\boldsymbol{X}/\|\boldsymbol{X}\|
. These and other methods are surveyed in
Engelke and Ivanovs 2021
.

Our contribution differs from those above from several perspectives. First, our focus is on extreme directions in the context of multivariate threshold exceedances. Second, our aim is not to learn extreme directions, but rather to introduce parametric mgp models capable of generating extreme directions other than
\left\{{1,\ldots,d}\right\}
. For multivariate extreme-value distributions, the max-linear factor model
[
15
]
and the asymmetric logistic model
[
41
]
already allow for this possibility. In the recent literature, only
Simpson et al. 2020
and
Chiapino et al. 2020
propose mixture models for multivariate extreme-value distributions. The power of our model construction resides in the fact that it is capable of representing mgp distributions with general extreme directions. Moreover, for specific parametric families, our model is straightforward to simulate from.

The paper is organized as follows. Section
2
recalls essential background on multivariate extreme-value theory. Section
3
defines and characterizes extreme directions in terms of the angular measure, the exponent measure, and the mgp distribution. We also compute the density of an mgp distribution with arbitrary extreme directions in terms of its generators, extending results in
Kiriliouk et al. 2018
. A model construction tool based on a mixture of mgp generators is presented in Section
4
. Apart from enjoying a convenient interpretation as a smoothed max-linear model, the construction is able to reproduce any mgp distribution. For the sake of illustration, we work out two examples based on the logistic and the Hüsler–Reiss families.
Moreover, the construction underlies a simulation algorithm for mgp distributions with non-standard extreme directions (Section
5
). As a by-product, we revisit known simulation algorithms in
Dombry et al. 2016
and
Engelke and Hitz 2020
that can be used to simulate mgp random vectors. Section
6
brings the paper to a close by outlining several avenues for future research. All proofs are deferred to the appendices.

2
Background

Notation.

Let
d
be a positive integer and write
D=\left\{{1,\ldots,d}\right\}
. Throughout, bold symbols will refer to multivariate quantities. Let
\boldsymbol{0}
and
\boldsymbol{1}
denote vectors of zeroes and ones, respectively, of a dimension clear from the context. A
d
-dimensional vector
\boldsymbol{x}
will be written as
\boldsymbol{x}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d})
and for a non-empty set
J\subseteq\left\{{1,\ldots,d}\right\}
, write
\boldsymbol{x}_{J}=(x_{j})_{j\in J}
. Mathematical operations on vectors such as addition, multiplication and comparison are considered component-wise apart from
\boldsymbol{a}\nleqslant\boldsymbol{b}
which denotes
a_{j}>b_{j}
for at least one
j\in D
.

We also fix notation for some sets that will be used throughout the paper. For two vectors
\boldsymbol{a},\;\boldsymbol{b}
, the set
[\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{b}]
will denote the Cartesian product
\prod_{j=1}^{d}[a_{j},b_{j}]
and so forth for
(\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{b}),\;[\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{b}),\;(\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{b}]
. Write
\mathbb{E}=[0,\infty)^{d}\setminus\left\{{\boldsymbol{0}}\right\}
. Let
\mathcal{B}(F)
denote the Borel sets over a topological space
F
. For a general norm
\|\,\cdot\,\|
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, let
\mathbb{S}=\left\{{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{E}:\;\|\boldsymbol{x}\|=1}\right\}
denote the intersection of the unit sphere with the positive orthant. For a non-empty set
J\subseteq D
, let

\mathbb{A}_{J}:=\left\{{\boldsymbol{x}\in[-\infty,\infty)^{d}:\;x_{j}>-\infty\text{ iff }j\in J}\right\},

(2.1)

and let
\pi_{J}:\mathbb{A}_{J}\to\mathbb{R}^{J}
be the projection
\boldsymbol{x}\mapsto\boldsymbol{x}_{J}
and with
\mathbb{R}^{J}
the set of real vectors
(x_{j})_{j\in J}
indexed by
J
. Clearly,
\mathbb{R}^{J}
is isomorphic to
\mathbb{R}^{|J|}
, with
\lvert J\rvert
the number of elements of
J
, but it will be convenient to keep track of the index set explicitly, not only its cardinality.

The arrow
\rightsquigarrow
denotes convergence in distribution (weak convergence). If
\mu
is a measure, then a Borel set
B
is
\mu
-continuous if
\mu(\partial B)=0
, with
\partial B
the topological boundary of
B
. The distribution or law of a random vector
\boldsymbol{X}
is denoted by
\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{X})
. Finally, the Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{R}^{m}
is denoted by
\lambda_{m}
.

2.1
Multivariate extreme value distributions

Consider a random vector
\boldsymbol{\xi}
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with joint cumulative distribution function (cdf)
F
and margins
F_{1},\ldots,F_{d}
. We say that
F
is in the domain of attraction of a multivariate extreme-value (mev) distribution
G
and we write
F\in\operatorname{DA}(G)
if there exist sequences
\boldsymbol{a}_{n}\in(0,\infty)^{d}
and
\boldsymbol{b}_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that

\lim_{n\to\infty}F^{n}(\boldsymbol{a}_{n}\boldsymbol{x}+\boldsymbol{b}_{n})=G(\boldsymbol{x}),

for every point
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
where
G
is continuous. Equivalently, if
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{\xi}_{n}
are i.i.d. random vectors with common cdf
F
, then

\dfrac{\max_{i=1,\ldots,n}\boldsymbol{\xi}_{i}-\boldsymbol{b}_{n}}{\boldsymbol{a}_{n}}\rightsquigarrow G,\qquad n\to\infty.

(2.2)

The margins of an mev distribution are univariate extreme-value distributions themselves. Thus, for
j\in D
, the marginal distribution
G_{j}
belongs to a three-parameter family and is

G_{j}(x_{j})=\begin{cases}\exp\left[{-\left\{{1+\gamma_{j}\left({x_{j}-\mu_{j}}\right)/\alpha_{j}}\right\}^{-1/\gamma_{j}}}\right],&\text{if }\gamma_{j}\neq 0,\\
\exp\left[{-\exp\left\{{-\left({x_{j}-\mu_{j}}\right)/\alpha_{j}}\right\}}\right],&\text{if }\gamma_{j}=0,\end{cases}

for some shape parameter
\gamma_{j}\in\mathbb{R}
, location parameter
\mu_{j}\in\mathbb{R}
, and scale parameter
\alpha_{j}>0
; further,
x_{j}
needs to be such that
1+\gamma_{j}(x_{j}-\mu_{j})/\alpha_{j}>0
.

For
d\geqslant 2
, the family of
d
-dimensional mev distributions no longer constitutes a finite-dimensional parametric family, in contrast to the univariate case. This is due to the nature of the dependence structure between the components of
G
, or equivalently, the extremal dependence between the components of
\boldsymbol{\xi}
.

Many functions and measures that describe this dependence are discussed in the literature
[see, e.g.,
2
, Chapter 8]
.
In this paper, three such objects will appear on the forefront: the exponent measure, the angular measure
[
11
]
and the stable tail dependence function
[
13
]
.

To concentrate on the dependence structure of
G
, it is convenient to standardize its margins. The choice of common scale is a matter of convention and convenience. In this section, we will opt for the unit-Fréchet distribution, i.e.,
\alpha_{j}=\gamma_{j}=\mu_{j}=1
for all
j\in D
, in line with the theory as originally exposed in
[
11
]
. In Section
2.2
, however, we will choose the Gumbel distribution instead (
\alpha_{j}=1
and
\gamma_{j}=\mu_{j}=0
for all
j\in D
), in order to link up with the additive model formulation for the mgp in
Rootzén et al. 2018b
. Switching between different marginal scales is an easy and sometimes illuminating exercise.

An mev distribution
G
with general margins admits the representation

G(\boldsymbol{x})=G^{*}\left({-1/\ln G_{1}(x_{1}),\ldots,-1/\ln G_{d}(x_{d})}\right),

where
G^{*}
is an mev distribution with unit-Fréchet margins. For
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{i}
as in (
2.2
), if
\boldsymbol{X}_{i}=\left({X_{i1},\ldots,X_{id}}\right)
is defined by

X_{ij}=-1/\ln F_{j}\left({\xi_{ij}}\right),\qquad i\in\mathbb{N},\;j\in D,

(2.3)

then we have

\max_{i=1,\ldots,n}\boldsymbol{X}_{i}/n\rightsquigarrow G^{*}
as
n\to\infty
.

The
exponent measure
of
G^{*}
is the unique Borel measure
\mu
concentrated on
\mathbb{E}
such that

G^{*}(\boldsymbol{x})=\exp\left\{{-\mu\left({\mathbb{E}\setminus\left[\boldsymbol{0},\boldsymbol{x}\right]}\right)}\right\},\qquad\boldsymbol{x}\in[\boldsymbol{0},\boldsymbol{\infty}].

(2.4)

The measure
\mu
is finite on Borel sets whose topological closure does not contain the origin, i.e., is bounded away from the origin. Moreover,
\mu\left({\mathbb{E}\setminus\left[\boldsymbol{0},\boldsymbol{x}\right]}\right)=\infty
as soon as
x_{j}=0
for some
j\in D
.
The exponent measure
\mu

is
(-1)
-homogeneous
in the sense that
\mu(c\,\cdot\,)=c^{-1}\mu(\,\cdot\,)
for all
c>0
. Moreover, for any
\mu
-continuous Borel set
B\subset\mathbb{E}
bounded away from the origin, we have

\lim_{n\to\infty}n\operatorname{\mathsf{P}}(\boldsymbol{X}\in nB)=\mu(B).

(2.5)

Another way to describe the dependence structure of
G
is via the
stable tail dependence function (stdf)
,

\ell(\boldsymbol{x})=\mu\left({\mathbb{E}\setminus[\boldsymbol{0},\boldsymbol{1}/\boldsymbol{x}]}\right)=-\ln G^{*}(\boldsymbol{1}/\boldsymbol{x}),\qquad\boldsymbol{x}\in[\boldsymbol{0},\boldsymbol{\infty}).

(2.6)

The margins are constrained by
\ell(0,\ldots,0,x_{j},0,\ldots,0)=x_{j}
for
x_{j}\geqslant 0
and
j\in D
. Moreover,
\ell
is homogeneous in the sense that for
c\geqslant 0
and
\boldsymbol{x}\in[\boldsymbol{0},\boldsymbol{\infty})
, we have
\ell(c\boldsymbol{x})=c\ell(\boldsymbol{x})
.

The
angular measure

\phi
of
G^{*}
with respect to a general norm
\|\,\cdot\,\|
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
is

\phi(B)=\mu\left({\left\{{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{E}:\;\|\boldsymbol{x}\|>1,\;\boldsymbol{x}/\|\boldsymbol{x}\|\in B}\right\}}\right),\qquad B\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{S}).

(2.7)

Equations (
2.5
) and (
2.7
) yield

\lim_{n\to\infty}n\operatorname{\mathsf{P}}(\|\boldsymbol{X}\|>n,\;\boldsymbol{X}/\|\boldsymbol{X}\|\in B)=\phi(B),

for any
\phi
-continuous Borel set
B\subseteq S
.

2.2
Multivariate generalized Pareto distributions

The peaks-over-thresholds method is grounded in the fact that asymptotically, vectors of component-wise maxima follow an mev distribution if and only if exceedances over a high threshold vector follow an mgp distribution. Recall
\boldsymbol{\xi}
and
G
in (
2.2
) and suppose
G(\boldsymbol{0})>0
. Convergence (
2.2
) is satisfied if and only if there exists a distribution
H
such that

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}\left(\dfrac{\boldsymbol{\xi}-\boldsymbol{b}_{n}}{\boldsymbol{a}_{n}}\vee\boldsymbol{\zeta}\;\middle|\;\boldsymbol{\xi}\nleqslant\boldsymbol{b}_{n}\right)\rightsquigarrow H,\qquad n\to\infty,

where
\boldsymbol{\zeta}
is the vector of lower endpoints of
G_{1},\ldots,G_{d}
and
\vee
denotes the component-wise maximum.

The conditioning event
\boldsymbol{\xi}\nleqslant\boldsymbol{b}_{n}
is to be read as
\exists j\in D:\xi_{j}>b_{nj}
, that is, at least one variable within
\boldsymbol{\xi}
exceeds a high threshold.

We write
H=\operatorname{GP}(G)
. As in
Rootzén et al. 2018b
, we consider the standardized case where
G
has Gumbel margins, i.e.,
\gamma_{j}=\mu_{j}=0
and
\alpha_{j}=1
for all
j\in D
. The distribution of an mgp random vector
\boldsymbol{Y}\sim H
is then determined by the stdf
\ell
.

Recall the exponent measure
\mu
of
G^{*}
. By
Rootzén and Tajvidi 2006
, the law of
\mathrm{e}^{\boldsymbol{Y}}
can be expressed in terms of
\mu
on the collection of sets
\mathbb{A}=\left\{{[\boldsymbol{0},\boldsymbol{y}],\;\boldsymbol{y}\in\left[\boldsymbol{0},\boldsymbol{\infty}\right)}\right\}
, via

\operatorname{\mathsf{P}}[\mathrm{e}^{\boldsymbol{Y}}\leqslant\boldsymbol{y}]=\dfrac{\mu\left({\left[\boldsymbol{0},\boldsymbol{y}\right]\cap\left\{{\boldsymbol{x}\geqslant\boldsymbol{0}:\;\boldsymbol{x}\nleqslant\boldsymbol{1}}\right\}}\right)}{\mu\left({\left\{{\boldsymbol{x}\geqslant\boldsymbol{0}:\;\boldsymbol{x}\nleqslant\boldsymbol{1}}\right\}}\right)}.

Since a probability measure on
\left[\boldsymbol{0},\boldsymbol{\infty}\right)
is determined by its values on
\mathbb{A}
, we get

\operatorname{\mathsf{P}}\left[{\mathrm{e}^{\boldsymbol{Y}}\in B}\right]=\frac{\mu\left({B\cap\left\{{\boldsymbol{x}\geqslant\boldsymbol{0}:\;\boldsymbol{x}\nleqslant\boldsymbol{1}}\right\}}\right)}{\mu\left({\left\{{\boldsymbol{x}\geqslant\boldsymbol{0}:\;\boldsymbol{x}\nleqslant\boldsymbol{1}}\right\}}\right)},\qquad B\in\mathcal{B}\left({\left[\boldsymbol{0},\boldsymbol{\infty}\right)}\right).

(2.8)

In words, the law of
\mathrm{e}^{\boldsymbol{Y}}
is equal to the restriction of
\mu
to
\left\{\boldsymbol{x}\geqslant\boldsymbol{0}:\;\boldsymbol{x}\nleqslant\boldsymbol{1}\right\}
, normalized to a probability measure.
Conversely, since
\mu\left({\left\{{\boldsymbol{x}\geqslant\boldsymbol{0}:\;x_{j}>1}\right\}}\right)=-\ln G^{*}_{j}(1)=1
by (
2.6
), we obtain the normalization constant via
\operatorname{\mathsf{P}}\left[{Y_{j}>0}\right]=1/\mu\left({\left\{{\boldsymbol{x}\geqslant\boldsymbol{0}:\;\boldsymbol{x}\nleqslant\boldsymbol{1}}\right\}}\right)
for all
j\in D
, so that
\mu
can be reconstructed from
\boldsymbol{Y}
by

\mu\left({B\cap\left\{{\boldsymbol{x}\geqslant\boldsymbol{0}:\;\boldsymbol{x}\nleqslant\boldsymbol{1}}\right\}}\right)=\operatorname{\mathsf{P}}\left[{\mathrm{e}^{\boldsymbol{Y}}\in B}\right]/\operatorname{\mathsf{P}}\left[{Y_{j}>0}\right],\qquad B\in\mathcal{B}\left({\left[\boldsymbol{0},\boldsymbol{\infty}\right)}\right).

(2.9)

Rootzén et al. 2018b
propose three stochastic representations for
H
. In view of their importance to the construction device and the simulation algorithm to follow, we review these representations in some detail.

Spectral random vector
\boldsymbol{S}
.

Let
\boldsymbol{S}
be a random vector in
[-\infty,0]^{d}
such that
\operatorname{\mathsf{P}}[\max(\boldsymbol{S})=0]=1
,
\operatorname{\mathsf{P}}[S_{j}>-\infty]>0
for all
j\in D
and
\operatorname{\mathsf{E}}[\mathrm{e}^{S_{1}}]=\ldots=\operatorname{\mathsf{E}}[\mathrm{e}^{S_{d}}]
. Then
\boldsymbol{S}
is said to be the
spectral random vector

[
36
, Theorem 6]
associated to the mgp
H
if

\ell(\boldsymbol{y})=\operatorname{\mathsf{E}}[\max\left({\boldsymbol{y}\mathrm{e}^{\boldsymbol{S}}}\right)]/\operatorname{\mathsf{E}}[\mathrm{e}^{S_{1}}],\qquad\boldsymbol{y}\in\left[\boldsymbol{0},\boldsymbol{\infty}\right).

(2.10)

We write
H=\operatorname{GP}_{\boldsymbol{S}}(\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{S}))
, while
Y\sim H
admits the representation

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{Y}=E+\boldsymbol{S},

(2.11)

with
E=\max(\boldsymbol{Y})
a unit exponential random variable independent of the spectral random vector
\boldsymbol{S}=\boldsymbol{Y}-\max(\boldsymbol{Y})
; see
Rootzén et al. 2018b
.

The distribution of the random vector
\boldsymbol{S}
can thus be obtained directly from the mgp
H
.
Conversely, any random vector
\boldsymbol{S}
with the above properties generates an mgp
H
.
Still, the constraint
\max(\boldsymbol{S})=0
almost surely is not satisfied by common families of distributions, which raises the question how to construct appropriate
\boldsymbol{S}
. The next representation resolves this issue, thereby facilitating the construction of an mgp.

Generator
\boldsymbol{T}
.

Let
\boldsymbol{T}
be a random vector in
\left[-\infty,\infty\right)^{d}
such that
\operatorname{\mathsf{P}}[\max(\boldsymbol{T})>-\infty]=1
,
\operatorname{\mathsf{P}}[T_{j}>-\infty]>0
for all
j\in D
and
\operatorname{\mathsf{E}}[\mathrm{e}^{T_{1}-\max(\boldsymbol{T})}]=\ldots=\operatorname{\mathsf{E}}[\mathrm{e}^{T_{d}-\max(\boldsymbol{T})}]
. The random vector
\boldsymbol{T}
is called a
\boldsymbol{T}
-generator
of
H

[
36
, Proposition 8]
, if we have the equality in distribution

\boldsymbol{S}\overset{\mathsf{d}}{=}\boldsymbol{T}-\max(\boldsymbol{T}),

(2.12)

where
\boldsymbol{S}
is the spectral random vector associated to
H
. We will write
H=\operatorname{GP}_{\boldsymbol{T}}(\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{T}))
. Equations (
2.11
) and (
2.12
) yield that if
\boldsymbol{T}
is a
\boldsymbol{T}
-generator of
H
and if
\boldsymbol{Y}\sim H
, then

\boldsymbol{Y}\overset{\mathsf{d}}{=}\boldsymbol{T}-\max(\boldsymbol{T})+E.

(2.13)

Any random vector
\boldsymbol{T}
with the above properties yields an mgp random vector
\boldsymbol{Y}
via (
2.13
). In comparison to
\boldsymbol{S}
, it has one constraint less to satisfy; the construction in (
2.12
) guarantees that
\max(\boldsymbol{S})
vanishes almost surely. On the other hand, the
\boldsymbol{T}
-generator of an mgp distribution
H
is not unique: adding the same random variable to all components of
\boldsymbol{T}
does not change the distribution of
\boldsymbol{T}-\max(\boldsymbol{T})
and produces the same mgp
H
.

A drawback of both representations
\boldsymbol{S}
and
\boldsymbol{T}
is that they are not stable with respect to marginalization. If
\boldsymbol{Y}\sim H
is a
d
-dimensional mgp random vector and if
J\subset D
is non-empty, the conditional distribution of
\boldsymbol{Y}_{J}
given
\max\boldsymbol{Y}_{J}>0
is a
|J|
-dimensional mgp, say
H_{|J}
(which is different from
H_{J}
, the unconditional distribution of
\boldsymbol{Y}_{J}
, which is in general not a mgp). However, if
\boldsymbol{S}
is the spectral random vector of
H
, then
\boldsymbol{S}_{J}
is
not
the spectral random vector of
H_{|J}
. The following representation resolves this issue, at the price of a change of measure.

Generator
\boldsymbol{U}
.

Let
\boldsymbol{U}
be a random vector on
[-\infty,\infty)^{d}
such that
\operatorname{\mathsf{E}}[\mathrm{e}^{U_{1}}]=\ldots=\operatorname{\mathsf{E}}[\mathrm{e}^{U_{d}}]
and
0<\operatorname{\mathsf{E}}[\mathrm{e}^{U_{1}}]<\infty
. The random vector
\boldsymbol{U}
is said to be a
\boldsymbol{U}
-generator
of
H

[
36
, Proposition 9]
if

\ell(\boldsymbol{y})=\operatorname{\mathsf{E}}\left[{\max\left({\boldsymbol{y}\mathrm{e}^{\boldsymbol{U}}}\right)}\right]/\operatorname{\mathsf{E}}\left[{\mathrm{e}^{U_{1}}}\right],\qquad\boldsymbol{y}\in\left[\boldsymbol{0},\boldsymbol{\infty}\right).

(2.14)

We write
H=\operatorname{GP}_{\boldsymbol{U}}(\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{U}))
. If
\boldsymbol{U}
is a generator of
H
and if
c\in\mathbb{R}
, then
\boldsymbol{U}+c
is also a generator of
H
. This means that without loss of generality, we can and will assume that

\operatorname{\mathsf{E}}\left[{\mathrm{e}^{U_{j}}}\right]=1,\qquad j\in D.

(2.15)

This constraint still does not make
\boldsymbol{U}
identifiable from
H
: if the random variable
V
satisfies
\operatorname{\mathsf{E}}\left[{\mathrm{e}^{V}}\right]=1
and is independent of
\boldsymbol{U}
, then the random vector
(U_{1}+V,\ldots,U_{d}+V)
yields another
\boldsymbol{U}
-generator for the same mgp
H
.

If
\boldsymbol{U}
is a generator of
\boldsymbol{Y}\sim H
, then, for non-empty
J\subset D
, the random vector
\boldsymbol{U}_{J}
is a generator of
\boldsymbol{Y}_{J}\mid\max(\boldsymbol{Y}_{J})>0

(Max-linear model)

Remark 3.8
.

The tail dependence coefficient

in (

\chi_{J}=\mu\left(\left\{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{E}:\;\boldsymbol{x}_{J}\in(1,\infty)^{J}\right\}\right)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\bar{J}\subseteq D\\

3.2
Density of an mgp distribution with multiple extreme directions

We provide density expressions for potential use in statistical inference on mgp models.
As in Section
2.2
, let
\boldsymbol{Y}
be an mgp random vector with distribution
H=\operatorname{GP}(G)
where
G
is an mev distribution with Gumbel margins. Further, let
\boldsymbol{T}
and
\boldsymbol{U}
be its generators as defined in Section
2
. With respect to a dominating measure
\upsilon
, defined as a sum of Lebesgue measures
\lambda_{m}
of various dimensions

, we express the density of
\boldsymbol{Y}
in terms of the densities of
\boldsymbol{U}
and
\boldsymbol{T}
. This will be helpful since the latter densities are known for several parametric families, for instance, the logistic and the Hüsler–Reiss model
[
29
, Sections 7.1 and 7.2]
. Furthermore, in view of (
2.9
), the density of the exponent measure
\mu
on
\left\{{\boldsymbol{x}\geqslant\boldsymbol{0}:\boldsymbol{x}\nleqslant\boldsymbol{1}}\right\}
is proportional to the mgp density, after appropriate marginal scaling; by homogeneity, this determines the exponent measure density everywhere.

Let
\lambda_{k}
be the
k
-dimensional Lebesgue measure and let
\upsilon=\upsilon_{d}
be the measure on
[-\infty,\infty)^{d}
defined by

\upsilon(\,\cdot\,):=\sum_{J:\emptyset\neq J\subseteq D}\lambda_{\lvert J\rvert}(\pi_{J}(\,\cdot\,\cap\mathbb{A}_{J})),

(3.10)

where we recall the set
\mathbb{A}_{J}
and the projection
\pi_{J}
defined in and right after Eq. (
2.1
). For Borel sets
B
in
[-\infty,\infty)^{d}
, we have thus

\upsilon(B)=\sum_{J:\emptyset\neq J\subseteq D}\lambda_{|J|}\left({\left\{{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{|J|}:\;\text{$\exists\,\boldsymbol{y}\in B$ such that $y_{j}=x_{j}$ if $j\in J$ and $y_{j}=-\infty$ if $j\not\in J$}}\right\}}\right).

Theorem 3.9
.

If the generator
\boldsymbol{U}
of
\boldsymbol{Y}
has density
f_{\boldsymbol{U}}
with respect to
\upsilon
, then the mgp random vector
\boldsymbol{Y}
has density
h_{\boldsymbol{Y}}
with respect to
\upsilon
equal to

h_{\boldsymbol{Y}}(\boldsymbol{y})=\operatorname{\mathbbm{1}}\left\{{\max(\boldsymbol{y})>0}\right\}\frac{1}{\operatorname{\mathsf{E}}\left[{\mathrm{e}^{\max(\boldsymbol{U})}}\right]}\int_{r\in\mathbb{R}}f_{\boldsymbol{U}}\left({r+\boldsymbol{y}}\right)e^{r}\,\mathrm{d}r.

Theorem 3.10
.

If the generator
\boldsymbol{T}
has density
f_{\boldsymbol{T}}
with respect to
\upsilon
, then the mgp random vector
\boldsymbol{Y}
has density
h_{\boldsymbol{Y}}
with respect to
\upsilon
equal to

h_{\boldsymbol{Y}}(\boldsymbol{y})=\operatorname{\mathbbm{1}}\left\{{\max(\boldsymbol{y})>0}\right\}\mathrm{e}^{-\max(\boldsymbol{y})}\int_{r\in\mathbb{R}}f_{\boldsymbol{T}}(r+\boldsymbol{y})\,\mathrm{d}r.

The proofs of Theorems
3.9
and
3.10
are identical to the ones of Theorems 12 and 13 in
Rootzén et al. 2018b
. In the next section, we will propose a model for the
\boldsymbol{U}
-generator that leads to mgp distributions with general extreme directions and whose density can be computed by means of Theorem
3.9
.

4
A model with extreme directions

We will provide a stochastic construction that allows any collection
\mathcal{R}
of non-empty subsets of
D=\left\{{1,\ldots,d}\right\}
with the property
\bigcup\left\{{J:J\in\mathcal{R}}\right\}=D
to be the extreme directions (Definition
3.1
) of a random vector. We do so by considering a smoothed version of the max-linear model that we will call mixture model (Section
4.1
). We show that this model determines an mev distribution, of which we compute the stdf, the extreme directions, and the
\boldsymbol{U}
-generator of the associated mgp distribution. Importantly, the mixture model covers all mev distributions and thus also all mgp distributions. Density expressions will be provided too. Next, in Section
4.2
, we work out two parametric examples for the mixture model based on the logistic and the Hüsler–Reiss dependence structures.

4.1

Definition 4.1
.

Let

and positive column sums
\sum_{j=1}^{d}a_{jk}>0
for all

. The

\boldsymbol{M}=(M_{1},\ldots,M_{d})=\left(\max_{k\in R}\{a_{1k}Z_{1}^{(k)}\},\ldots,\max_{k\in R}\{a_{dk}Z_{d}^{(k)}\}\right),

will be called the

with coefficient matrix

.

Remark 4.2

In (

matter for the value of the maximum. Therefore, only the subvectors

is then that its only extreme direction is

Remark 4.3

In case

is max-linear with unit-Fréchet margins

For

The following theorem was proved in

Theorem 4.4

6
Conclusion

The extreme directions of a random vector refer to those groups of variables that can be large simultaneously while those outside the group are not. As such scenarios are likely to occur in high dimensions or in spatial contexts, we have studied mgp models featuring arbitrary collections of extreme directions. Furthermore, we have provided a general stochastic representation of mev distributions with arbitrary extreme directions and we have computed their corresponding mgp distributions. The construction involved a mixture specification extending the max-linear model by including multivariate factors. The density of such an mgp distribution—and thus of the exponent measure of the mev distribution—with respect to an appropriate dominating measure has been expressed in terms of those of its generators. In addition, we have provided a simulation algorithm for drawing samples from such mgp distributions. The formulas have been made explicit for two parametric models, extending the classical logistic and Hüsler–Reiss mev and mgp distributions to families with arbitrary extreme directions.

Our work raises intriguing questions for further research. The foremost among these is the challenge of statistical inference for mgp mixture models based on multivariate threshold excesses. This multifaceted problem encompasses parameter estimation, model selection, and the incorporation of sparsity constraints.

Additionally, our research opens doors to the application of mgp mixture models in the realm of graphical models for extremes. Whereas the foundations laid by
Engelke and Hitz 2020
involve a single extreme direction, the extension offered in
Engelke et al. 2022
incorporates general exponent measures, enabling a more comprehensive understanding of extreme events in diverse contexts.

\displaystyle\tilde{\mu}\left({[\boldsymbol{0},\boldsymbol{x}]^{c}}\right)=\sum_{k\in R}\mu_{J_{k}}^{(k)}\left({\left[{0,\left({x_{j}/a_{jk}}\right)_{j\in J_{k}}}\right]^{c}}\right)=\sum_{k\in R}\mu_{J_{k}}^{(k)}\left({\left({1/a_{jk}}\right)_{j\in J_{k}}\,\cdot\,\left[{0,\boldsymbol{x}_{J_{k}}}\right]^{c}}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\in R}\mu\left({\pi_{J_{k}}^{-1}\left({\left({a_{jk}}\right)_{j\in J_{k}}\,\cdot\,\left({1/a_{jk}}\right)_{j\in J_{k}}\,\cdot\,\left[{0,\boldsymbol{x}_{J_{k}}}\right]^{c}}\right)}\right)=\sum_{k\in R}\mu\left({\pi_{J_{k}}^{-1}\left({[\boldsymbol{0},\boldsymbol{x}_{J_{k}}]^{c}}\right)}\right)=\mu\left({[\boldsymbol{0},\boldsymbol{x}]^{c}}\right).

The last equality follows from

since the sets
\mathbb{E}_{J_{k}}
are all disjoint and
\mu
has no mass outside

.
Hence
G
and

are both mev distributions with unit-Fréchet margins and the same exponent measure. Thus

.
∎

Lemma C.1
.

Let
J
be a positive integer. If
\boldsymbol{P}
is a random vector on
\mathbb{R}^{J}
with Lebesgue density
f_{\boldsymbol{P}}
such that
p_{j}:=\operatorname{\mathsf{E}}\left[{\exp(P_{j})}\right]<\infty
for all
j\in\{1,\ldots,J\}
, then the random vector
\boldsymbol{T}
with distribution
\operatorname{\mathsf{P}}\left[{\boldsymbol{T}\in\,\cdot\,}\right]=\operatorname{\mathsf{E}}\left[{\mathrm{e}^{\max\boldsymbol{P}}\operatorname{\mathbbm{1}}\left\{{\boldsymbol{P}\in\,\cdot\,}\right\}}\right]/\operatorname{\mathsf{E}}\left[{\mathrm{e}^{\max\boldsymbol{P}}}\right]
has Lebesgue density

f_{\boldsymbol{T}}(\boldsymbol{t})=c\,g(\boldsymbol{t})\sum_{j=1}^{J}n_{j}\,q_{j}(\boldsymbol{t}),\qquad\boldsymbol{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{J},

with
c=\sum_{j=1}^{J}p_{j}/\operatorname{\mathsf{E}}\left[{\mathrm{e}^{\max\boldsymbol{P}}}\right]
,
g(\boldsymbol{t})=\mathrm{e}^{\max\boldsymbol{t}}/\sum_{j=1}^{J}\mathrm{e}^{t_{j}}
, mixture weights
n_{j}=p_{j}/\sum_{i=1}^{J}p_{i}
and probability densities
q_{j}(\boldsymbol{t})=\mathrm{e}^{t_{j}}f_{\boldsymbol{P}}(\boldsymbol{t})/p_{j}
.

Proof of Lemma
C.1
.

The density of
\boldsymbol{T}
evaluated in
\boldsymbol{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{J}
is

\displaystyle f_{\boldsymbol{T}}(\boldsymbol{t})=\frac{\mathrm{e}^{\max\boldsymbol{t}}}{\operatorname{\mathsf{E}}\left[{\mathrm{e}^{\max\boldsymbol{P}}}\right]}f_{\boldsymbol{P}}(\boldsymbol{t})=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{J}p_{i}}{\operatorname{\mathsf{E}}\left[{\mathrm{e}^{\max\boldsymbol{P}}}\right]}\frac{\mathrm{e}^{\max\boldsymbol{t}}}{\sum_{j=1}^{J}\mathrm{e}^{t_{j}}}\sum_{j=1}^{J}\frac{p_{j}}{\sum_{i=1}^{J}p_{i}}\frac{\mathrm{e}^{t_{j}}}{p_{j}}f_{\boldsymbol{P}}(\boldsymbol{t}),

which is the expression stated in the lemma.
