Interacting Contour Stochastic Gradient Langevin Dynamics
Wei Deng, Siqi Liang, Botao Hao, Guang Lin, Faming Liang, Purdue University Morgan Stanley DeepMind fmliang@purdue.edu; weideng056@gmail.com

Abstract
We propose an interacting contour stochastic gradient Langevin dynamics (ICSGLD) sampler, an embarrassingly parallel multiple-chain contour stochastic gradient Langevin dynamics (CSGLD) sampler with efficient interactions . We show that ICSGLD can be theoretically more efficient than a single-chain CSGLD with an equivalent computational budget. We also present a novel random-field function, which facilitates the estimation of self-adapting parameters in big data and obtains free mode explorations. Empirically, we compare the proposed algorithm with popular benchmark methods for posterior sampling. The numerical results show a great potential of ICSGLD for large-scale uncertainty estimation tasks.



1
Introduction

Stochastic gradient Langevin dynamics (SGLD)
(
Welling & Teh 2011
)
has achieved great successes in simulations of high-dimensional systems for big data problems. It, however, yields only a fast mixing rate when the energy landscape is simple, e.g., local energy wells are shallow and not well separated. To improve its convergence for the problems with complex energy landscapes, various strategies have been proposed, such as momentum augmentation
(
Chen et al. 2014
;
Ding et al. 2014
)
, Hessian approximation
(
Ahn et al. 2012
;
Li et al. 2016
)
, high-order numerical schemes
(
Chen et al. 2015
;
Li et al. 2019b
)
, and cyclical learning rates

(
Izmailov et al. 2018
;
Maddox et al. 2019
;
Zhang et al. 2020b
)
. In spite of their asymptotic properties in Bayesian inference
(
Vollmer et al. 2016
)
and non-convex optimization
(
Zhang et al. 2017
)
, it is still difficult to achieve compelling empirical results for pathologically complex deep neural networks (DNNs).

To simulate from distributions with complex energy landscapes, e.g., those with a multitude of modes well separated by high energy barriers,
an emerging trend is to run multiple chains, where interactions between different chains can potentially accelerate the convergence of the simulation. For example,
Song et al. 2014
and
Futami et al. 2020
showed theoretical advantages of appropriate interactions in ensemble/population simulations. Other multiple chain methods include particle-based nonlinear Markov (Vlasov) processes
(
Liu & Wang 2016
;
Zhang et al. 2020a
)
and replica exchange methods (also known as parallel tempering)
(
Deng et al. 2021a
)
. However, the particle-based methods result in an expensive kernel matrix computation given a large number of particles
(
Liu & Wang 2016
)
; similarly, naïvely extending replica exchange methods to population chains leads to a long waiting time to swap between non-neighboring chains
(
Syed et al. 2021
)
. Therefore, how to conduct interactions between different chains, while maintaining the scalability of the algorithm, is the key to the success of the parallel stochastic gradient MCMC algorithms.

In this paper, we propose an interacting contour stochastic gradient Langevin dynamics (ICSGLD) sampler, a pleasingly parallel extension of contour stochastic gradient Langevin dynamics (CSGLD)
(
Deng et al. 2020b
)
with
efficient interactions
.
The proposed algorithm requires minimal communication cost in that each chain shares with others the marginal energy likelihood estimate only.
As a result, the interacting mechanism improves the convergence of the simulation, while the minimal communication mode between different chains enables the proposed algorithm to be naturally adapted to distributed computing with little overhead.
For the single-chain CSGLD algorithm, despite its theoretical advantages as shown in
Deng et al. 2020b
, estimation of the marginal energy likelihood remains challenging for big data problems with a wide energy range, jeopardizing the empirical performance of the class of importance sampling methods
(
Gordon et al. 1993
;
Doucet et al. 2001
;
Wang & Landau 2001
;
Liang et al. 2007
;
Andrieu et al. 2010
;
Deng et al. 2020b
)
in big data applications. To resolve this issue, we resort to a novel interacting random-field function based on multiple chains for an ideal variance reduction and a more robust estimation. As such, we can greatly facilitate the estimation of the marginal energy likelihood so as to accelerate the simulations of notoriously complex distributions. To summarize, the algorithm has three main contributions:

•

We propose a scalable interacting importance sampling method for big data problems with the minimal communication cost. A novel random-field function is derived to tackle the incompatibility issue of the class of importance sampling methods in big data problems.

•

Theoretically, we study the local stability of a non-linear mean-field system and justify regularity properties of the solution of Poisson’s equation. We also prove the asymptotic normality for the stochastic approximation process in mini-batch settings and show that ICSGLD is asymptotically more efficient than the single-chain CSGLD with an equivalent computational budget.

•

Our proposed algorithm achieves appealing mode explorations using a fixed learning rate on the MNIST dataset and obtains remarkable performance in large-scale uncertainty estimation tasks.

Preliminaries

2.1
Stochastic gradient Langevin dynamics

A standard sampling algorithm for big data problems is SGLD
(
Welling & Teh 2011
)
, which is a numerical scheme of a stochastic differential equation in mini-batch settings:

{\bf x}_{k+1}={\bf x}_{k}-\epsilon_{k}\frac{N}{n}\nabla_{{\bf x}}\widetilde{U}({\bf x}_{k})+\sqrt{2\tau\epsilon_{k}}{\bm{w}}_{k},

(1)

where
{\bf x}_{k}\in{\mathcal{X}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
\epsilon_{k}
is the learning rate at iteration
k
,
N
denotes the number of total data points,
\tau
is the temperature, and
{\bm{w}}_{k}
is a standard Gaussian vector of dimension
d
. In particular,
\frac{N}{n}\nabla_{{\bf x}}\widetilde{U}({\bf x})
is an unbiased stochastic gradient estimator based on a mini-batch data
\mathcal{B}
of size
n
and
\frac{N}{n}\widetilde{U}({\bf x})
is the unbiased energy estimator for the exact energy function
U({\bf x})
. Under mild conditions on
U
,
{\bf x}_{k+1}
is known to converge weakly to a unique invariant distribution
\pi({\bf x})\propto e^{-\frac{U({\bf x})}{\tau}}
as
\epsilon_{k}\rightarrow 0
.

2.2
Contour stochastic gradient Langevin dynamics

Despite its theoretical guarantees, SGLD can converge exponentially slow when
U({\bf x})
is non-convex and exhibits high energy barriers. To remedy this issue, CSGLD
(
Deng et al. 2020b
)
exploits the flat histogram idea and proposes to simulate from a flattened density with much lower energy barriers

\varpi_{\Psi_{{\bm{\theta}}}}({\bf x})\propto{\pi({\bf x})}/{\Psi^{\zeta}_{{\bm{\theta}}}(U({\bf x}))},

(2)

where
\zeta
is a hyperparameter,
\Psi_{{\bm{\theta}}}(u)=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\bigg(\theta(i-1)e^{(\log\theta(i)-\log\theta(i-1))\frac{u-u_{i-1}}{\Delta u}}\bigg)1_{u_{i-1}<u\leq u_{i}}.

In particular,
\{u_{i}\}_{i=0}^{m}
determines the partition
\{{\mathcal{X}}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{m}
of
{\mathcal{X}}
such that
{\mathcal{X}}_{i}=\{{\bf x}:u_{i-1}<U({\bf x})\leq u_{i}\}
, where
-\infty=u_{0}<u_{1}<\cdots<u_{m-1}<u_{m}=\infty
. For practical purposes, we assume
u_{i+1}-u_{i}=\Delta u
for
i=1,\cdots,m-2
. In addition,
{\bm{\theta}}=(\theta(1),\theta(2),\ldots,\theta(m))

is the self-adapting parameter in the space
{\bm{\Theta}}=\bigg\{\left(\theta(1),\cdots,\theta(m)\right)\big|0<\theta(1),\cdots,\theta(m)<1\&\ \sum_{i=1}^{m}\theta(i)=1\bigg\}
.

Ideally, setting
\zeta=1
and
\theta(i)=\theta_{\infty}(i)
, where
\theta_{\infty}(i)=\int_{{\mathcal{X}}_{i}}\pi({\bf x})d{\bf x}
for
i\in\{1,2,\cdots,m\}
, enables CSGLD to achieve a “random walk” in the space of energy and to penalize the over-visited partition
(
Wang & Landau 2001
;
Liang et al. 2007
;
Fort et al. 2011
;
Fort et al. 2015
)
. However, the optimal values of
{\bm{\theta}}_{\infty}
is unknown
a priori
. To tackle this issue, CSGLD proposes the following procedure to adaptively estimate
{\bm{\theta}}
via stochastic approximation (SA)
(
Robbins & Monro 1951
;
Benveniste et al. 1990
)
:

(1)

Sample
{\bf x}_{k+1}={\bf x}_{k}+\epsilon_{k}\frac{N}{n}\nabla_{{\bf x}}\widetilde{U}_{\Psi_{{\bm{\theta}}_{k}}}({\bf x}_{k})+\sqrt{2\tau\epsilon_{k}}{\bm{w}}_{k}
,

(2)

Optimize
\bm{\theta}_{k+1}=\bm{\theta}_{k}+\omega_{k+1}\mathbb{\widetilde{H}}(\bm{\theta}_{k},{\bf x}_{k+1}),

where
\nabla_{{\bf x}}\widetilde{U}_{\Psi_{{\bm{\theta}}}}(\cdot)
is a stochastic gradient function of
\varpi_{\Psi_{{\bm{\theta}}}}(\cdot)
to be detailed in Algorithm
1
.
\mathbb{\widetilde{H}}({\bm{\theta}},{\bf x}):=\left(\mathbb{\widetilde{H}}_{1}({\bm{\theta}},{\bf x}),\cdots,\mathbb{\widetilde{H}}_{m}({\bm{\theta}},{\bf x})\right)
is random-field function where each entry follows

\mathbb{\widetilde{H}}_{i}({\bm{\theta}},{\bf x})={\theta}^{\zeta}(J_{\widetilde{U}}({\bf x}))\left(1_{i=J_{\widetilde{U}}({\bf x})}-{\theta}(i)\right),\text{where }J_{\widetilde{U}}({\bf x})=\sum_{i=1}^{m}i1_{u_{i-1}<\frac{N}{n}\widetilde{U}({\bf x})\leq u_{i}}.

(3)

Theoretically, CSGLD converges to a sampling-optimization equilibrium in the sense that
{\bm{\theta}}_{k}
approaches to a fixed point
{\bm{\theta}}_{\infty}
and the samples are drawn from the flattened density
\varpi_{\Psi_{{\bm{\theta}}_{\infty}}}({\bf x})
. Notably, the mean-field system is
globally stable
with a unique stable equilibrium point in a small neighborhood of
{\bm{\theta}}_{\infty}
. Moreover, such an appealing property holds even when
U({\bf x})
is non-convex.

3
Interacting contour stochastic gradient Langevin dynamics

The major goal of interacting CSGLD (ICSGLD) is to improve the efficiency of CSGLD. In particular, the self-adapting parameter
{\bm{\theta}}
is crucial for ensuring the sampler to escape from the local traps and traverse the whole energy landscape, and how to reduce the variability of
{\bm{\theta}}_{k}
’s is the key to the success of such a dynamic importance sampling algorithm. To this end, we propose an efficient variance reduction scheme via interacting parallel systems to improve the accuracy of
{\bm{\theta}}_{k}
’s.

3.1
Interactions in parallelism

Now we first consider a naïve parallel sampling scheme with
P
chains as follows

{\bf x}_{k+1}^{\bigotimes P}={\bf x}_{k}^{\bigotimes P}+\epsilon_{k}\frac{N}{n}\nabla_{{\bf x}}\widetilde{\bm{U}}_{\Psi_{{\bm{\theta}}_{k}}}({\bf x}_{k}^{\bigotimes P})+\sqrt{2\tau\epsilon_{k}}{\bm{w}}_{k}^{\bigotimes P},

where
{\bf x}^{\bigotimes P}=({\bf x}^{(1)},{\bf x}^{(2)},\cdots,{\bf x}^{(P)})^{\top}
,
{\bm{w}}_{k}^{\bigotimes P}
denotes
P
independent standard Gaussian vectors, and
\widetilde{\bm{U}}_{\Psi_{{\bm{\theta}}}}({\bf x}^{\bigotimes P})=(\widetilde{U}_{\Psi_{{\bm{\theta}}}}({\bf x}^{(1)}),\widetilde{U}_{\Psi_{{\bm{\theta}}}}({\bf x}^{(2)}),\cdots,\widetilde{U}_{\Psi_{{\bm{\theta}}}}({\bf x}^{(P)}))^{\top}
.

Stochastic approximation aims to find the solution
{\bm{\theta}}
of the mean-field system
h({\bm{\theta}})
such that

\begin{split}h({\bm{\theta}})&=\int_{{\mathcal{X}}}\widetilde{H}(\bm{\theta},\bm{{\bf x}})\varpi_{{\bm{\theta}}}(d{\bf x})=0,\end{split}

where
\varpi_{{\bm{\theta}}}
is the invariant measure simulated via SGLD that approximates
\varpi_{\Psi_{{\bm{\theta}}}}
in (
2
) and
\widetilde{H}(\bm{\theta},\bm{{\bf x}})
is the novel random-field function to be defined later in (
8
). Since
h({\bm{\theta}})
is observable only up to large random perturbations (in the form of
\widetilde{H}(\bm{\theta},\bm{{\bf x}})
), the optimization of
{\bm{\theta}}
based on isolated random-field functions may not be efficient enough. However, due to the
conditional independence
of
{\bf x}^{(1)},{\bf x}^{(2)},\cdots,{\bf x}^{(P)}
in parallel sampling, it is very natural to consider a Monte Carlo average

\begin{split}h({\bm{\theta}})&=\frac{1}{P}\sum_{p=1}^{P}\int_{{\mathcal{X}}}\widetilde{H}(\bm{\theta},\bm{{\bf x}}^{(p)})\varpi_{{\bm{\theta}}}(d{\bf x}^{(p)})=0.\end{split}

(6)

Namely, we are considering the following stochastic approximation scheme

\bm{\theta}_{k+1}=\bm{\theta}_{k}+\omega_{k+1}\widetilde{\bm{H}}(\bm{\theta}_{k},{\bf x}_{k+1}^{\bigotimes P}),

(7)

where
\widetilde{\bm{H}}(\bm{\theta}_{k},{\bf x}_{k+1}^{\bigotimes P})
is an interacting random-field function
\widetilde{\bm{H}}(\bm{\theta}_{k},{\bf x}_{k+1}^{\bigotimes P})=\frac{1}{P}\sum_{p=1}^{P}\widetilde{H}(\bm{\theta}_{k},{\bf x}_{k+1}^{(p)})
.
Note that the Monte Carlo average is very effective to reduce the variance of the interacting random-field function
\widetilde{\bm{H}}(\bm{\theta},\bm{{\bf x}}^{\bigotimes P})
based on the conditionally independent random field functions. Moreover, each chain shares with others only a very short message during each iteration. Therefore, the interacting parallel system is well suited for distributed computing, where the
implementations and communication costs
are further detailed in section
B.1.2
in the supplementary material. By contrast, each chain of the non-interacting parallel CSGLD algorithm deals with the parameter
{\bm{\theta}}
and a large-variance random-field function
\widetilde{H}(\bm{\theta},{\bf x})
individually, leading to coarse estimates in the end.

Formally, for the population/ensemble interaction scheme (
7
), we define a novel random-field function
\widetilde{H}({\bm{\theta}},{\bf x})=(\widetilde{H}_{1}({\bm{\theta}},{\bf x}),\widetilde{H}_{2}({\bm{\theta}},{\bf x}),\cdots,\widetilde{H}_{m}({\bm{\theta}},{\bf x}))
, where each component satisfies

\widetilde{H}_{i}({\bm{\theta}},{\bf x})={\theta}(J_{\widetilde{U}}({\bf x}))\left(1_{i=J_{\widetilde{U}}({\bf x})}-{\theta}(i)\right).

(8)

As shown in Lemma
1
, the corresponding mean-field function proposes to converge to a different fixed point
{\bm{\theta}}_{\star}
, s.t.

\theta_{\star}(i)\propto\left(\int_{{\mathcal{X}}_{i}}e^{-\frac{U({\bf x})}{\tau}}d{\bf x}\right)^{\frac{1}{\zeta}}\propto{\bm{\theta}}_{\infty}^{\frac{1}{\zeta}}(i).

(9)

A large data set often renders the task of estimating
{\bm{\theta}}_{\infty}
numerically challenging. By contrast, we resort to a different solution by estimating
{\bm{\theta}}_{\star}
instead based on a large value of
\zeta
. The proposed algorithm is summarized in Algorithm
1
. For more study on the scalablity of the new scheme, we leave the discussion in section
B.1.3
.

3.2
Related works

Replica exchange SGLD
(
Deng et al. 2020a
;
Deng et al. 2021a
)
has successfully extended the traditional replica exchange
(
Swendsen & Wang 1986
;
Geyer 1991
;
Earl & Deem 2005
)
to big data problems. However, it works with two chains only and has a low swapping rate. As shown in Figure
1
(a), a naïve extension of multi-chain replica exchange SGLD yields low communication efficiency. Despite some recipe in the literature
(
Katzgraber et al. 2008
;
Bittner et al. 2008
;
Syed et al. 2021
)
, how to conduct multi-chain replica exchange with low-frequency swaps is still an open question.

A comparison of communication costs between replica exchange (RE) and ICSGLD. We see RE takes many iterations to swap with all the other chains; by contrast, ICSGLD possesses a pleasingly parallel mechanism where the only cost comes from sharing a light message.

Stein variational gradient descent (SVGD)
(
Liu & Wang 2016
)
is a popular approximate inference method to drive a set of particles for posterior approximation. In particular, repulsive forces are proposed to prevent particles to collapse together into neighboring regions, which resembles our strategy of penalizing over-visited partition. However, SVGD tends to underestimate the uncertainty given a limited number of particles. Moreover, the quadratic cost in kernel matrix computation further raises the scalability concerns as more particles are proposed.

Admittedly, ICSGLD is not the first interacting importance sampling algorithm. For example,
a population stochastic approximation Monte Carlo (pop-SAMC) algorithm has been proposed in
Song et al. 2014
, and
an interacting particle Markov chain Monte Carlo (IPMCMC) algorithm has been proposed in
Rainforth et al. 2016
.
A key difference between our algorithm and others is that our algorithm is mainly devised for big data problems. The IPMCMC and pop-SAMC are gradient-free samplers, which are hard to be adapted to high-dimensional big data problems.

Other parallel SGLD methods
(
Ahn et al. 2014
;
Chen et al. 2016
)
aim to reduce the computational cost of gradient estimations in distributed computing, which, however, does not consider interactions for accelerating the convergence.
Li et al. 2019a
proposed asynchronous protocols to reduce communication costs when the master aggregates model parameters from all workers. Instead, we don’t communicate the parameter
{\bf x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
but only share
{\bm{\theta}}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
and the indices, where
m\ll d
.

Our work also highly resembles the well-known Federated Averaging (FedAvg) algorithm
(
Li et al. 2020
;
Deng et al. 2021b
)
, except that the stochastic gradient
\widetilde{U}({\bf x})
is replaced with the random field function
\widetilde{H}({\bm{\theta}},{\bf x})
and we only share the low-dimensional latent vector
{\bm{\theta}}
. Since privacy concerns and communication cost are not major bottlenecks of our problem, we leave the study of taking the Monte Carlo average in Eq.(
6
) every
K>1
iterations for future works.

4
Convergence properties

To study theoretical properties of ICSGLD, we first show a local stability property that is well-suited to big data problems, and then we present the asymptotic normality for the stochastic approximation process in
mini-batch settings
, which eventually yields the desired result that ICSGLD is asymptotically more efficient than a single-chain CSGLD with an equivalent computational cost.

4.1
Local stability for non-linear mean-field systems in big data

The first obstacle for the theoretical study is to approximate the components of
{\bm{\theta}}_{\infty}
corresponding to the high energy region.
To get around this issue, the random field function
\widetilde{H}({\bm{\theta}},{\bf x})
in (
8
) is adopted to estimate a different target
{\bm{\theta}}_{\star}\propto{\bm{\theta}}_{\infty}^{\frac{1}{\zeta}}
. As detailed in Lemma
3
in the supplementary material, the mean-field equation is now formulated as follows

h_{i}({\bm{\theta}})\propto\theta_{\star}^{\zeta}(i)-{\left(\theta(i)C_{{\bm{\theta}}}\right)^{\zeta}}+{\text{perturbations}},

(10)

where
C_{{\bm{\theta}}}=\bigg(\frac{\widetilde{Z}_{\zeta,{\bm{\theta}}}}{\widetilde{Z}_{\zeta,{\bm{\theta}}_{\star}}^{\zeta}}\bigg)^{\frac{1}{\zeta}}
and
\widetilde{Z}_{\zeta,{\bm{\theta}}}=\sum_{k=1}^{m}\frac{\int_{{\mathcal{X}}_{k}}\pi({\bf x})d{\bf x}}{\theta^{\zeta-1}(k)}
. We see that (
10
) may not be linearly stable as in
Deng et al. 2020b
. Although the solution of the mean-field system
h({\bm{\theta}})=0
is still unique, there may exist unstable invariant subspaces, leading us to consider the local properties. For a proper initialization of
{\bm{\theta}}
, which can be achieved by pre-training the model long enough time through SGLD, the mean value theorem implies a linear property in a local region

h_{i}({\bm{\theta}})\propto\theta_{\star}(i)-\theta(i)+{\text{perturbations}}.

Combining the perturbation theory
(
Vanden-Eijnden 2001
)
, we present the following stability result:

Lemma 1
(Local stability, informal version of Lemma
3
)

Assume Assumptions A1-A4 (given in the supplementary material) hold. For any properly initialized
{\bm{\theta}}
, we have
\langle h({\bm{\theta}}),{\bm{\theta}}-\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{\star}\rangle\leq-\phi\|{\bm{\theta}}-\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{\star}\|^{2}
, where
\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{\star}={\bm{\theta}}_{\star}+\mathcal{O}\left(\sup_{{\bf x}}\mathrm{Var}(\xi_{n}({\bf x}))+\epsilon+\frac{1}{m}\right)
,
{\bm{\theta}}_{\star}\propto{\bm{\theta}}_{\infty}^{\frac{1}{\zeta}}
,

\phi>0
,
\epsilon
denotes a learning rate, and
\xi_{n}({\bf x})
denotes the noise in the stochastic energy estimator of batch size
n
and
\mathrm{Var}(\cdot)
denotes the variance.

By justifying the drift conditions of the adaptive transition kernel and relevant smoothness properties, we can prove the existence and regularity properties of the solution of the Poisson’s equation in Lemma
6
in the supplementary material. In what follows, we can control the fluctuations in stochastic approximation and eventually yields the
L^{2}
convergence.

Lemma 2
(
L^{2}
convergence rate, informal version of Lemma
7
)

Given standard Assumptions A1-A5.
{\bm{\theta}}_{k}
converges to
\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{\star}
,
where
\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{\star}={\bm{\theta}}_{\star}+\mathcal{O}\left(\sup_{{\bf x}}\mathrm{Var}(\xi_{n}({\bf x}))+\epsilon+\frac{1}{m}\right)
, such that

\mathbb{E}\left[\|\bm{\theta}_{k}-\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{\star}\|^{2}\right]=\mathcal{O}\left(\omega_{k}\right).

The result differs from Theorem 1 of
Deng et al. 2020b
in that the biased fixed point
\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{\star}
instead of
{\bm{\theta}}_{\star}
is treated as the equilibrium of the continuous system, which provides us a user-friendly proof. Similar techniques have been adopted by
Durmus & Éric Moulines 2017
;
Xu et al. 2018
. Although the global stability
(
Deng et al. 2020b
)
may be sacrificed when
\zeta\neq 1
based on Eq.(
8
),
{\bm{\theta}}_{\star}\propto{\bm{\theta}}_{\infty}^{\frac{1}{\zeta}}
is much easier to estimate numerically for any
i
that yields
0<{\bm{\theta}}_{\infty}(i)\ll 1
based on a large
\zeta>1
.

4.2
Asymptotic normality

To study the asymptotic behavior of
\omega_{k}^{-\frac{1}{2}}({\bm{\theta}}_{k}-\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{\star})
, where
\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{\star}
is the equilibrium point s.t.
\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{\star}={\bm{\theta}}_{\star}+\mathcal{O}\left(\mathrm{Var}(\xi_{n}({\bf x}))+\epsilon+\frac{1}{m}\right)
, we consider a fixed step size
\omega
in the SA step for ease of explanation. Let
\bar{\bm{\theta}}_{t}
denote the solution of the mean-field system in continuous time (
\bar{\bm{\theta}}_{0}={\bm{\theta}}_{0}
), and rewrite the single-chain SA step (
7
) as follows

\begin{split}{\bm{\theta}}_{k+1}-\bar{\bm{\theta}}_{(k+1)\omega}&={\bm{\theta}}_{k}-\bar{\bm{\theta}}_{k\omega}+\omega\left(H({\bm{\theta}}_{k},{\bf x}_{k+1})-H(\bar{\bm{\theta}}_{k\omega},{\bf x}_{k+1})\right)\\
&\quad+\omega\left(H(\bar{\bm{\theta}}_{k\omega},{\bf x}_{k+1})-h(\bar{\bm{\theta}}_{k\omega})\right)-\left(\bar{\bm{\theta}}_{(k+1)\omega}-\bar{\bm{\theta}}_{k\omega}-\omega h(\bar{\bm{\theta}}_{k\omega})\right).\end{split}

Further, we set
\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}_{k\omega}:=\omega^{-\frac{1}{2}}({\bm{\theta}}_{k}-\bar{\bm{\theta}}_{k\omega})
. Then the stochastic approximation differs from the mean field system in that

\begin{split}\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}_{(k+1)\omega}&=\underbrace{\omega^{\frac{1}{2}}\sum_{i=0}^{k}\left(H({\bm{\theta}}_{i},{\bf x}_{i+1})-H(\bar{\bm{\theta}}_{i\omega},{\bf x}_{i+1})\right)}_{\text{I: perturbations}}+\omega^{\frac{1}{2}}\sum_{i=0}^{k}\underbrace{\left(H(\bar{\bm{\theta}}_{i\omega},{\bf x}_{i+1})-h(\bar{\bm{\theta}}_{i\omega})\right)}_{\text{II: martingale}\ \mathcal{M}_{i}}-\omega^{\frac{1}{2}}\cdot\text{remainder}\\
&\approx\omega^{\frac{1}{2}}\sum_{i=0}^{k}h_{{\bm{\theta}}}({\bm{\theta}}_{i\omega})\underbrace{({\bm{\theta}}_{i}-\bar{\bm{\theta}}_{i\omega})}_{\approx\omega^{\frac{1}{2}}\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}_{i\omega}}+\omega^{\frac{1}{2}}\sum_{i=0}^{k}\mathcal{M}_{i}\approx\int_{0}^{(k+1)\omega}h_{{\bm{\theta}}}(\bar{\bm{\theta}}_{s})\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}_{s}ds+\int_{0}^{(k+1)\omega}{\bm{R}}^{\frac{1}{2}}(\bar{\bm{\theta}}_{s})d{\bm{W}}_{s},\end{split}

where
h_{{\bm{\theta}}}({\bm{\theta}}):=\frac{d}{d{\bm{\theta}}}h({\bm{\theta}})
is a matrix,
{\bm{W}}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
is a standard Brownian motion, the last term follows from a certain central limit theorem
(
Benveniste et al. 1990
)
and
{\bm{R}}
denotes the covariance matrix of the random-field function s.t.
{\bm{R}}({\bm{\theta}}):=\sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty}{\mbox{Cov}}_{{\bm{\theta}}}(H({\bm{\theta}},{\bf x}_{k}),H({\bm{\theta}},{\bf x}_{0}))
.

We expect the weak convergence of
{\bm{U}}_{k}
to the stationary distribution of a diffusion

d{\bm{U}}_{t}=h_{{\bm{\theta}}}({\bm{\theta}}_{t}){\bm{U}}_{t}dt+{\bm{R}}^{1/2}({\bm{\theta}}_{t})d{\bm{W}}_{t},

(11)

where
{\bm{U}}_{t}=\omega_{t}^{-1/2}({\bm{\theta}}_{t}-\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{\star})
. Given that
{\bm{\theta}}_{t}
converges to
\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{\star}
sufficiently fast and the local linearity of
h_{{\bm{\theta}}}
, the diffusion (
11
) resembles the Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process and yields the following solution

{\bm{U}}_{t}\approx e^{-th_{{\bm{\theta}}}(\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{\star})}{\bm{U}}_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}e^{-(t-s)h_{{\bm{\theta}}}(\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{\star})}\circ{\bm{R}}(\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{\star})d{\bm{W}}_{s}.

Then we have the following theorem, whose formal proof is given in section
C.3
.

Theorem 1
(Asymptotic Normality)

Assume Assumptions A1-A5 (given in the supplementary material) hold. We have the following weak convergence

\begin{split}\omega_{k}^{-1/2}({\bm{\theta}}_{k}-\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{\star})\Rightarrow\mathcal{N}(0,{\bm{\Sigma}}),\text{ where }{\bm{\Sigma}}=\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{th_{{\bm{\theta}}_{\star}}}\circ{\bm{R}}\circ e^{th^{\top}_{{\bm{\theta}}_{\star}}}dt,h_{{\bm{\theta}}_{\star}}=h_{{\bm{\theta}}}(\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{\star}).\end{split}

4.3
Interacting parallel chains are more efficient

For clarity, we first denote an estimate of
{\bm{\theta}}
based on ICSGLD with
P
interacting parallel chains by
{\bm{\theta}}_{k}^{P}
and denote the estimate based on a single-long-chain CSGLD by
{\bm{\theta}}_{kP}
.

Note that Theorem
1
holds for any step size
\omega_{k}=\mathcal{O}(k^{-\alpha})
, where
\alpha\in(0.5,1]
. If we simply run a single-chain CSGLD algorithm with
P
times of iterations, by Theorem
1
,

\begin{split}\omega_{kP}^{-1/2}({\bm{\theta}}_{kP}-\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{\star})\Rightarrow\mathcal{N}(0,{\bm{\Sigma}}).\end{split}

As to ICSGLD, since the covariance
{\bm{\Sigma}}
relies on
{\bm{R}}
, which depends on the covariance of the martingale
\{\mathcal{M}_{i}\}_{i\geq 1}
, the conditional independence of
{\bf x}^{(1)},{\bf x}^{(2)},\cdots,{\bf x}^{(P)}
naturally results in an efficient variance reduction such that

Corollary 1
(Asymptotic Normality for ICSGLD)

Assume the same assumptions. For ICSGLD with
P
interacting chains, we have the following weak convergence

\begin{split}\omega_{k}^{-1/2}({\bm{\theta}}_{k}^{P}-\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{\star})\Rightarrow\mathcal{N}(0,{\bm{\Sigma}}/P).\end{split}

That is, under a similar computational budget, we have
\frac{\|\mathrm{Var}({\bm{\theta}}_{kP}-\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{\star})\|_{\text{F}}}{\|\mathrm{Var}({\bm{\theta}}_{k}^{P}-\widehat{{\bm{\theta}}}_{*})\|_{\text{F}}}=\frac{w_{kP}}{w_{k}/P}\approx P^{1-\alpha}
.

Corollary 2
(Efficiency)

Given a decreasing step size
\omega_{k}=\mathcal{O}(k^{-\alpha})
, where
0.5<\alpha<1
,
ICSGLD is asymptotically more efficient than the single-chain CSGLD with an equivalent training cost.

In practice, slowly decreasing step sizes are often preferred in stochastic algorithms for a better non-asymptotic performance
(
Benveniste et al. 1990
)
.

6
Conclusion

We have proposed the ICSGLD as an efficient algorithm for sampling from distributions with a complex energy landscape, and shown theoretically that ICSGLD is indeed more efficient than the single-chain CSGLD for a slowly decreasing step size. To our best knowledge, this is the first interacting importance sampling algorithm that adapts to big data problems without scalability concerns. ICSGLD has been compared with numerous state-of-the-art baselines for various tasks, whose remarkable results indicate its promising future in big data applications.

We summarize the supplementary material as follows: Section
A
provides the preliminary knowledge for stochastic approximation; Section
B
shows a local stability condition that adapts to high losses; Section
C
proves the main asymptotic normality for the stochastic approximation process, which naturally yields the conclusion that interacting contour stochastic gradient Langevin dynamics (ICSGLD) is more efficient than the analogous single chain based on slowly decreasing step sizes; Section
D
details the experimental settings.

Appendix A
Preliminaries

A.1
Stochastic approximation

Given a random-field function
\widetilde{H}(\bm{{\bm{\theta}}},\bm{{\bf x}})
, the stochastic approximation algorithm
(
Benveniste et al. 1990
)
proposes to solve the mean-field equation
h({\bm{\theta}})=0
in the analysis of adaptive algorithms

\begin{split}h({\bm{\theta}})&=\int_{{\mathcal{X}}}\widetilde{H}(\bm{\theta},\bm{{\bf x}})\varpi_{\bm{\theta}}(d\bm{{\bf x}})=0,\end{split}

where
{\bf x}\in{\mathcal{X}}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
{\bm{\theta}}\in{\bm{\Theta}}\subset\mathbb{R}^{m}
,
\varpi_{{\bm{\theta}}}({\bf x})
is a distribution that depends on the self-adapting parameter
{\bm{\theta}}
. Given the transition kernel
\Pi_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{x},A)
for any Borel subset
A\subset{\mathcal{X}}
, the algorithm can be written as follows

(1)

Simulate
{\bf x}_{k+1}\sim\Pi_{\bm{\theta_{k}}}({\bf x}_{k},\cdot)
, which yields the invariant distribution
\varpi_{\bm{\theta_{k}}}(\cdot)
,

(2)

Optimize
\bm{\theta}_{k+1}=\bm{\theta}_{k}+\omega_{k+1}\widetilde{H}(\bm{\theta}_{k},{\bf x}_{k+1}).

Compared with the standard Robbins–Monro algorithm
(
Robbins & Monro 1951
)
, the algorithm proposes to simulate
{\bf x}
from a transition kernel
\Pi_{\bm{\theta}}(\cdot,\cdot)
instead of the distribution
\varpi_{\bm{\theta}}(\cdot)
directly. In other words, ,
\widetilde{H}({\bm{\theta}}_{k},{\bf x}_{k+1})-h({\bm{\theta}}_{k})
is not a Martingale but rather a Markov state-dependent noise.

A.2
Poisson’s equation

In the stochastic approximation algorithm, the sequence of
\{({\bf x}_{k},{\bm{\theta}}_{k})\}_{k=1}^{\infty}
on the product space
{\mathcal{X}}\times{\bm{\Theta}}
is generated, which is an inhomogeneous Markov chain and requires the tool of the Poisson’s equation to study the convergence

\mu_{{\bm{\theta}}}(\bm{x})-\mathrm{\Pi}_{\bm{\theta}}\mu_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{x})=\widetilde{H}(\bm{\theta},\bm{x})-h(\bm{\theta}),

where
\mu_{{\bm{\theta}}}(\cdot)
is a function on
{\mathcal{X}}
. The solution
\mu_{{\bm{\theta}}}(\bm{x})
to the Poisson’s equation exists and is formulated in the form

\mu_{{\bm{\theta}}}({\bf x}):=\sum_{k\geq 0}\Pi_{{\bm{\theta}}}^{k}(\widetilde{H}({\bm{\theta}},{\bf x})-h({\bm{\theta}})),

when the above series converges. To ensure such a convergence,
Benveniste et al. 1990
made the following regularity conditions on the solution
\mu_{{\bm{\theta}}}(\cdot)
of the Poisson’s equation:

There exist a Lyapunov function
V:{\mathcal{X}}\to[1,\infty)
and a positive constant
C>0
such that
\forall\bm{\theta},\bm{\theta}^{\prime}\in\bm{{\bm{\Theta}}}
, we have

\begin{split}\|\mathrm{\Pi}_{\bm{\theta}}\mu_{{\bm{\theta}}}({\bf x})\|&\leq CV({\bf x}),\quad\|\mathrm{\Pi}_{\bm{\theta}}\mu_{\bm{\theta}}({\bf x})-\mathrm{\Pi}_{\bm{\theta^{\prime}}}\mu_{\bm{\theta^{\prime}}}({\bf x})\|\leq C\|\bm{\theta}-\bm{\theta}^{\prime}\|V({\bf x}),\quad\mathbb{E}[V({\bf x})]\leq\infty,\\
\end{split}

(12)

where a common choice for the Lyapunov function is to set
V({\bf x})=1+\|{\bf x}\|^{2}

(
Teh et al. 2016
;
Vollmer et al. 2016
)
.

A.3
Gaussian diffusions

Consider a stochastic linear differential equation

d{\bm{U}}_{t}=h_{{\bm{\theta}}}({\bm{\theta}}_{t}){\bm{U}}_{t}dt+{\bm{R}}^{1/2}({\bm{\theta}}_{t})d{\bm{W}}_{t},

(13)

where
{\bm{U}}
is a
m
-dimensional random vector,
h_{{\bm{\theta}}}:=\frac{d}{d{\bm{\theta}}}h({\bm{\theta}})
,
{\bm{R}}({\bm{\theta}}):=\sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty}{\mbox{Cov}}_{{\bm{\theta}}}(H({\bm{\theta}},{\bf x}_{k}),H({\bm{\theta}},{\bf x}_{0}))
is a positive definite matrix that depends on
{\bm{\theta}}(\cdot)
,
{\bm{W}}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
is a standard Brownian motion. Given a large enough
t
such that
{\bm{\theta}}_{t}
converges to a fixed point
\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{\star}
sufficiently fast, we may write the diffusion associated with Eq.(
13
) as follow

{\bm{U}}_{t}\approx e^{-th_{{\bm{\theta}}}(\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{\star})}{\bm{U}}_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}e^{-(t-s)h_{{\bm{\theta}}}(\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{\star})}\circ{\bm{R}}(\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{\star})d{\bm{W}}_{s},

(14)

Suppose that the matrix
h_{{\bm{\theta}}}(\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{\star})
is negative definite, then
{\bm{U}}_{t}
converges in distribution to a Gaussian variable

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}[{\bm{U}}_{t}]&=e^{-th_{{\bm{\theta}}}(\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{\star})}{\bm{U}}_{0}\\
{\mbox{Var}}({\bm{U}}_{t})&=\int_{0}^{t}e^{th_{{\bm{\theta}}}(\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{\star})}\circ{\bm{R}}\circ e^{th_{{\bm{\theta}}}(\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{\star})}du.\end{split}

The main goal of this supplementary file is to study the Gaussian approximation of the process
\omega_{k}^{-1/2}({\bm{\theta}}_{k}-\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{\star})
to the solution Eq.(
14
) for a proper step size
\omega_{k}
. Thereafter, the advantage of interacting mechanisms can be naturally derived.

Appendix B
Stability and convergence analysis

As required by the algorithm, we update
P
contour stochastic gradient Langevin dynamics (CSGLD) simultaneously. For the notations, we denote the particle of the p-th chain at iteration
k
by
{\bf x}_{k}^{(p)}\in{\mathcal{X}}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
and the joint state of the
P
parallel particles at iteration
k
by
{\bf x}_{k}^{\bigotimes P}:=\left({\bf x}_{k}^{(1)},{\bf x}_{k}^{(2)},\cdots,{\bf x}_{k}^{(P)}\right)^{\top}\in{\mathcal{X}}^{\bigotimes P}\subset\mathbb{R}^{dP}
. We also denote the learning rate and step size at iteration
k
by
\epsilon_{k}
and
\omega_{k}
, respectively. We denote by
\mathcal{N}({0,\bm{I}_{dP}})
a standard
dP
-dimensional Gaussian vector and denote by
\zeta
a positive hyperparameter.

B.1
ICSGLD algorithm

First, we introduce the interacting contour stochastic gradient Langevin dynamics (ICSGLD) with
P
parallel chains:

(1)

Simulate
{\bf x}_{k+1}^{\bigotimes P}={\bf x}_{k}^{\bigotimes P}-\epsilon_{k}\nabla_{{\bf x}}\widetilde{\bm{L}}({\bf x}_{k}^{\bigotimes P},{\bm{\theta}}_{k})+\mathcal{N}({0,2\epsilon_{k}\tau\bm{I}_{dP}}),\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ (\text{S}_{1})

(2)

Optimize
\bm{\theta}_{k+1}=\bm{\theta}_{k}+\omega_{k+1}\widetilde{\bm{H}}(\bm{\theta}_{k},{\bf x}_{k+1}^{\bigotimes P}),\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ (\text{S}_{2})

where
\nabla_{{\bf x}}\widetilde{\bm{L}}({\bf x}^{\bigotimes P},{\bm{\theta}}):=\left(\nabla_{{\bf x}}\widetilde{L}({\bf x}^{(1)},{\bm{\theta}}),\nabla_{{\bf x}}\widetilde{L}({\bf x}^{(2)},{\bm{\theta}}),\cdots,\nabla_{{\bf x}}\widetilde{L}({\bf x}^{(P)},{\bm{\theta}})\right)^{\top}
,
\nabla_{{\bf x}}\widetilde{L}({\bf x},{\bm{\theta}})
is the
stochastic adaptive gradient given by

\nabla_{{\bf x}}\widetilde{L}({\bf x},{\bm{\theta}})=\frac{N}{n}\underbrace{\left[1+\frac{\zeta\tau}{\Delta u}\left(\log\theta({J}_{\widetilde{U}}({\bf x}))-\log\theta((J_{\widetilde{U}}({\bf x})-1)\vee 1)\right)\right]}_{\text{gradient multiplier}}\nabla_{{\bf x}}\widetilde{U}({\bf x}).

(15)

In particular, the interacting random-field function is written as

\widetilde{\bm{H}}(\bm{\theta}_{k},{\bf x}_{k+1}^{\bigotimes P})=\frac{1}{P}\sum_{p=1}^{P}\widetilde{H}({\bm{\theta}}_{k},{\bf x}_{k+1}^{(p)}),

(16)

where each random-field function
\widetilde{H}({\bm{\theta}},{\bf x})=(\widetilde{H}_{1}({\bm{\theta}},{\bf x}),\ldots,\widetilde{H}_{m}({\bm{\theta}},{\bf x}))
follows

\widetilde{H}_{i}({\bm{\theta}},{\bf x})={\theta}(J_{\widetilde{U}}({\bf x}))\left(1_{i=J_{\widetilde{U}}({\bf x})}-{\theta}(i)\right),\quad i=1,2,\ldots,m.

(17)

Here
J_{\widetilde{U}}({\bf x})
denotes the index
i\in\{1,2,3,\cdots,m\}
such that
u_{i-1}<\frac{N}{n}\widetilde{U}({\bf x})\leq u_{i}
for a set of energy partitions
\{u_{i}\}_{i=0}^{m}
and
\widetilde{U}({\bf x})=\sum_{i\in B}U_{i}({\bf x})
where
U_{i}
denotes the negative log of a posterior based on a single data point
i
and
B
denotes a mini-batch of data of size
n
. Note that the stochastic energy estimator
\widetilde{U}({\bf x})
results in a biased estimation for the partition index
J_{\widetilde{U}}({\bf x})
due to a non-linear transformation. To avoid such a bias asymptotically with respect to the learning rate
\epsilon_{k}
, we may consider a variance-reduced energy estimator
\widetilde{U}_{\text{VR}}({\bf x})
following
Deng et al. 2021a

\frac{N}{n}\widetilde{U}_{\text{VR}}({\bf x})=\frac{N}{n}\sum_{i\in B_{k}}\left(U_{i}({\bf x})-U_{i}\left({\bf x}_{q\lfloor\frac{k}{q}\rfloor}\right)\right)+\sum_{i=1}^{N}U_{i}\left({\bf x}_{q\lfloor\frac{k}{q}\rfloor}\right),

(18)

where the control variate
{\bf x}_{q\lfloor\frac{k}{q}\rfloor}
is updated every
q
iterations.

Compared with the naïve parallelism of CSGLD, a key feature of the ICSGLD algorithm lies in the joint estimation of the interacting random-field function
\widetilde{\bm{H}}(\bm{\theta},{\bf x}^{\bigotimes P})
in Eq.(
16
) for the same mean-field function
h({\bm{\theta}})
.

B.1.1
Discussions on the hyperparameters

The most important hyperparameter is
\zeta
. A fine-tuned
\zeta
usually leads to a small or even slightly negative learning rate in low energy regions to avoid local-trap problems. Theoretically,
\zeta
affects the
L^{2}
convergence rate hidden in the big-O notation in Lemma
3
.

The other hyperparameters can be easily tuned. For example, the ResNet models yields the full loss ranging from 10,000 to 60,000 after warm-ups, we thus partition the sample space according to the energy into 200 subregions equally without tuning; since the optimization of SA is nearly convex, tuning
\{\omega_{k}\}
is much easier than tuning
\{\epsilon_{k}\}
for non-convex learning.

B.1.2
Discussions on distributed computing and communication cost

In shared-memory settings, the implementation is trivial and the details are omitted.

In distributed-memory settings:
{\bm{\theta}}_{k+1}
is updated by the central node as follows:

•

The
p
-th worker conducts the sampling step
(\text{S}_{1})
and sends the indices
J_{\widetilde{U}({\bf x}_{k+1}^{(p)})}
’s to the central node;

•

The central node aggregates the indices from all worker and updates
{\bm{\theta}}_{k}
based on
(\text{S}_{2})
;

•

The central node sends
{\bm{\theta}}_{k+1}
back to each worker.

We emphasize that we don’t communicate the model parameters
{\bf x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, but rather share the self-adapting parameter
{\bm{\theta}}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
, where
m\ll d
. For example, WRN-16-8 has 11 M parameters (40 MB), while
{\bm{\theta}}
can be set to dimension
200
of size 4 KB; hence, the communication cost is not a big issue. Moreover, the theoretical advantage still holds if the communication frequency is slightly reduced.

B.1.3
Scalability to big data

Recall that the adaptive sampler follows that

{{\bf x}_{k+1}={\bf x}_{k}-\epsilon_{k+1}\frac{N}{n}\underbrace{\left[1+\zeta\tau\frac{\log{\theta}_{k}(J_{\widetilde{U}}({\bf x}_{k}))-\log{\theta}_{k}((J_{\widetilde{U}}({\bf x}_{k})-1)\vee 1)}{\Delta u}\right]}_{\text{gradient multiplier}}\nabla_{{\bf x}}\widetilde{U}({\bf x}_{k})+\sqrt{2\tau\epsilon_{k+1}}w_{k+1}},

The key to the success of (I)CSGLD is to generate sufficiently strong bouncy moves (
negative
gradient multiplier) to escape local traps. To this end,
\zeta
can be tuned to generate proper bouncy moves.

Take the CIFAR100 experiments for example:

•

the self-adjusting mechanism fails if the gradient multiplier uniformly “equals” to 1 and a too small value of
\zeta=1
could lead to this issue;

•

the self-adjusting mechanism works only if we choose a large enough
\zeta
such as 3e6 to generate (desired) negative gradient multiplier in over-visited regions.

However, when we set
\zeta=
3e6, the original stochastic approximation (SA) update proposed in
(
Deng et al. 2020b
)
follows that

{\theta}_{k+1}(i)={\theta}_{k}(i)+\omega_{k+1}\underbrace{{\theta}_{k}^{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\zeta}}(J_{\widetilde{U}}({\bf x}_{k+1}))}_{\textbf{essentially 0 for }\zeta\gg 1}\left(1_{i=J_{\widetilde{U}}({\bf x}_{k+1})}-{\theta}_{k}(i)\right).

Since
\theta(i)<1
for any
i\in\{1,\cdots,m\}
,
\theta(i)^{\zeta}
is essentially 0 for such a large
\zeta
, which means that
the original SA fails to optimize when
\zeta
is large
. Therefore, the limited choices of
\zeta
inevitably limits the scalability to big data problems. Our newly proposed SA scheme

{\theta}_{k+1}(i)={\theta}_{k}(i)+\omega_{k+1}\underbrace{{\theta}_{k}(J_{\widetilde{U}}({\bf x}_{k+1}))}_{\text{independent of }\zeta}\left(1_{i=J_{\widetilde{U}}({\bf x}_{k+1})}-{\theta}_{k}(i)\right)

is more independent of
\zeta
and proposes to converge to a much smoother equilibrium
\theta_{\infty}^{1/\zeta}
instead of
\theta_{\infty}
, where
\theta_{\infty}(i)=\int_{\chi_{i}}\pi(x)dx\propto\int_{\chi_{i}}e^{-\frac{U(x)}{\tau}}dx
is the energy PDF. As such, despite the linear stability is sacrificed, the resulting algorithm is more scalable. For example, estimating
e^{-10,000\times\frac{1}{\zeta}}
is numerically much easier than
e^{-10,000}
for a large
\zeta
such as
10,000
, where
10,000
can be induced by the high losses in training deep neural networks in big data.

B.2
Assumptions

A long-standing problem for stochastic approximation is the difficulty in establishing the stability property and a practical remedy for this problem is to study
{\bm{\Theta}}
on a fixed compact set.

Assumption A1
(Compactness)

The space
{\bm{\Theta}}
is compact and for any
i\in\{1,2,\ldots,m\}
we have
\inf_{{\bm{\Theta}}}\theta(i)>0
. In addition, there exists a positive constant
Q>0
that satisfies
\forall{\bm{\theta}}\in{\bm{\Theta}}
and
{\bf x}\in{\mathcal{X}}
,

\|{\bm{\theta}}\|\leq Q,\quad\|\widetilde{H}({\bm{\theta}},{\bf x})\|\leq Q.

(19)

For weaker assumptions, we refer readers to Theorem 3.2
(
Fort et al. 2015
)
, where a recurrence property can be proved for the Metropolis-based Wang-Landau algorithm, which eventually established that the estimates return to a desired compact set often enough.

Next, we lay out the smoothness assumption, which is standard in the convergence analysis of SGLD, see e.g.
Mattingly et al. 2010
,
Raginsky et al. 2017
and
Xu et al. 2018
.

Assumption A2
(Smoothness)

U(\bm{x})
is
M
-smooth when there exists a positive constant
M
that satisfies
\forall{\bf x},{\bf x}^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{X}}
,

\begin{split}\|\nabla_{{\bf x}}U({\bf x})-\nabla_{{\bf x}}U(\bm{{\bf x}}^{\prime})\|&\leq M\|{\bf x}-{\bf x}^{\prime}\|.\\
\end{split}

(20)

In addition, we assume the dissipativity condition to ensure that the geometric ergodicity of the dynamical system holds. This assumption is also crucial for verifying the solution properties of the solution of Poisson’s equation. Similar assumptions have been made in
Mattingly et al. 2010
;
Raginsky et al. 2017
and
Xu et al. 2018
.

Assumption A3
(Dissipativity)

There exist constants
\tilde{m}>0
and
\tilde{b}\geq 0
that satisfies
\forall{\bf x}\in{\mathcal{X}}
and any
{\bm{\theta}}\in{\bm{\Theta}}
,

\langle\nabla_{{\bf x}}L({\bf x},{\bm{\theta}}),{\bf x}\rangle\leq\tilde{b}-\tilde{m}\|{\bf x}\|^{2}.

(21)

To further establish a bounded second moment on
{\bf x}\in{\mathcal{X}}
with respect to a proper Lyapunov function
V({\bf x})
, we impose the following conditions
on the gradient noise:

Assumption A4
(Gradient noise)

The stochastic gradient based on mini-batch settings is an unbiased estimator such that

\mathbb{E}[\nabla_{{\bf x}}\widetilde{U}({\bf x}_{k})-\nabla_{{\bf x}}U({\bf x}_{k})]=0;

furthermore, for some positive constants
M
and
B
, we have

\mathbb{E}[\|\nabla_{{\bf x}}\widetilde{U}({\bf x}_{k})-\nabla_{{\bf x}}U({\bf x}_{k})\|^{2}]\leq M^{2}\|{\bf x}\|^{2}+B^{2},

where
\mathbb{E}[\cdot]
acts on the distribution of the noise in the stochastic gradient
\nabla_{{\bf x}}\widetilde{U}({\bf x}_{k})
.

(22)

where

Remark 1

The newly proposed random-field function Eq.(
17
) may sacrifice the global stability by including an approximately linear mean-field system Eq.(
29
) instead of a linear stable system (see formula (15) in
Deng et al. 2020b
). The advantage, however, is that such a mechanism facilitates the estimation of
{\bm{\theta}}_{\star}
. We emphasize that the original energy probability in each partition
\big\{\int_{{\mathcal{X}}_{k}}\pi({\bf x})d{\bf x}\big\}_{k=1}^{m}

(
Deng et al. 2020b
)
may be very difficult to estimate for big data problems. By contrast, the estimation of

Lemma 4

The stochastic energy estimator
\widetilde{U}({\bf x})
leads to a controllable bias in the random-field function.

|\mathbb{E}[\widetilde{H}_{i}({\bm{\theta}},{\bf x})]-H_{i}({\bm{\theta}},{\bf x})|=\mathcal{O}\left(\mathrm{Var}(\xi_{n}({\bf x}))\right),

where the expectation

is taken with respect to the random noise in the stochastic energy estimator of

Proof

Denote the noise in the stochastic energy estimator by

, such that
\widetilde{U}(\cdot)=U(\cdot)+\xi(\cdot)
. Recall that
\widetilde{H}_{i}({\bm{\theta}},{\bf x})={\theta}(J_{\widetilde{U}}({\bf x}))\left(1_{i=J_{\widetilde{U}}({\bf x})}-{\theta}(i)\right)

satisfies
u_{J_{\widetilde{U}}({\bf x})-1}<\frac{N}{n}\widetilde{U}({\bf x})\leq u_{J_{\widetilde{U}}({\bf x})}

to
(0,1)

maps

to
(0,1)
. In what follows, the bias of random-field function is upper bounded as follows

\begin{split}|\mathbb{E}[\widetilde{H}_{i}({\bm{\theta}},{\bf x})]-H_{i}({\bm{\theta}},{\bf x})|&=\left|\int\Phi(U({\bf x})+\xi({\bf x}))-\Phi(U({\bf x}))d\mu(\xi({\bf x}))\right|\\
&=\left|\int\xi({\bf x})\Phi^{\prime}(U({\bf x}))+\frac{\xi({\bf x})^{2}}{2}\Phi^{\prime\prime}(u)d\mu(\xi({\bf x}))\right|\\
&=\mathcal{O}\left(\mathrm{Var}(\xi_{n}({\bf x}))\right),\\
\end{split}

where the second equality follows from Taylor expansion for some energy

is clearly bounded due to the definition of
{\bm{\theta}}

\mathbb{E}[\bm{e}_{k+1}|\mathcal{F}_{k}]=\bm{0},\ \ \ \ \ \sup_{k\geq 0}\mathbb{E}[\|\bm{e}_{k+1}\|^{\widetilde{\alpha}}|\mathcal{F}_{k}]<\infty.

[formula]

(II) By the definition of
h({\bm{\theta}}_{k})
in Eq.(
27
), we can easily check that
h({\bm{\theta}}_{k})
is Lipschitz continuous in a neighborhood of
\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{\star}
. Combining Eq.(
32
), we have
\|h({\bm{\theta}}_{k})-h(\ddot{\bm{\theta}}_{k})\|=\mathcal{O}(\|{\bm{\theta}}_{k}-\ddot{\bm{\theta}}_{k}\|)=\mathcal{O}(\|\omega_{k+1}\Pi_{{{\bm{\theta}}_{k}}}\mu_{{\bm{\theta}}_{k}}({\bf x}_{k})\|)=\mathcal{O}(\omega_{k+1})
. Then
\mathbb{E}[\|\bm{\nu}_{k+1}\|]\leq C\|{\bm{\theta}}_{k}-\ddot{\bm{\theta}}_{k}\|+\mathcal{O}(\omega_{k+2})=\mathcal{O}(\omega_{k+1})
by the step size condition Eq.(
33
). In what follows, we can verify

\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{\|\bm{\tilde{\nu}}_{k+1}\|^{2}}{\omega_{k}}\right]\leq 2\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{\|\bm{\nu}_{k+1}\|^{2}}{\omega_{k}}\right]+2\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{\|h({\bm{\theta}}_{k})-h(\ddot{\bm{\theta}}_{k})\|^{2}}{\omega_{k}}\right]=\mathcal{O}(\omega_{k})\rightarrow 0.

[formula]

(III) For the martingale difference noise
\bm{e}_{k+1}=\mu_{{\bm{\theta}}_{k}}({\bf x}_{k+1})-\Pi_{{{\bm{\theta}}_{k}}}\mu_{{\bm{\theta}}_{k}}({\bf x}_{k})
with mean 0, we have

\mathbb{E}[\bm{e}_{k+1}\bm{e}_{k+1}^{\top}|\mathcal{F}_{k}]=\mathbb{E}[\mu_{{\bm{\theta}}_{k}}({\bf x}_{k+1})\mu_{{\bm{\theta}}_{k}}({\bf x}_{k+1})^{\top}|\mathcal{F}_{k}]-\Pi_{{{\bm{\theta}}_{k}}}\mu_{{\bm{\theta}}_{k}}({\bf x}_{k})\Pi_{{{\bm{\theta}}_{k}}}\mu_{{\bm{\theta}}_{k}}({\bf x}_{k})^{\top}.

We denote
\mathbb{E}[\bm{e}_{k+1}\bm{e}_{k+1}^{\top}|\mathcal{F}_{k}]
by a function
f({\bf x}_{k})
. Applying Lemma
9
, we have

\mathbb{E}[\bm{e}_{k+1}\bm{e}_{k+1}^{\top}|\mathcal{F}_{k}]=\mathbb{E}[f({\bf x}_{k})]\rightarrow\int f({\bf x})\varpi_{\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{\star}}d{\bf x}=\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\mathbb{E}[\bm{e}_{k+1}\bm{e}_{k+1}^{\top}|\mathcal{F}_{k}]:={\bm{R}},

[formula]

where
{\bm{R}}:={\bm{R}}(\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{\star})
and
{\bm{R}}({\bm{\theta}})
is also equivalent to
\sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty}{\mbox{Cov}}_{{\bm{\theta}}}(H({\bm{\theta}},{\bf x}_{k}),H({\bm{\theta}},{\bf x}_{0}))
.

Having the conditions C1, C2 and C3 verified, we apply Lemma
8
and have the following weak convergence for
\ddot{\bm{\theta}}_{k}

\begin{split}\omega_{k}^{-1/2}(\ddot{\bm{\theta}}_{k}-\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{\star})\Rightarrow\mathcal{N}(0,{\bm{\Sigma}}),\end{split}

where
{\bm{\Sigma}}=\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{th_{{\bm{\theta}}_{\star}}}\circ{\bm{R}}\circ e^{th^{\top}_{{\bm{\theta}}_{\star}}}dt
and
h_{{\bm{\theta}}_{\star}}=h_{{\bm{\theta}}}(\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{\star})+\widehat{\xi}{\bm{I}}
,
\widehat{\xi}=\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\omega_{k}^{0.5}-\omega_{k+1}^{0.5}}{\omega_{k}^{1.5}}
.

Considering the definition that
\ddot{\bm{\theta}}_{k}={\bm{\theta}}_{k}+\omega_{k+1}\Pi_{{{\bm{\theta}}_{k}}}\mu_{{\bm{\theta}}_{k}}({\bf x}_{k})
and
\mathbb{E}[\|\Pi_{{{\bm{\theta}}_{k}}}\mu_{{\bm{\theta}}_{k}}({\bf x}_{k})\|]
is uniformly bounded by Eq.(
32
), we have

\omega_{k}^{1/2}\Pi_{{{\bm{\theta}}_{k}}}\mu_{{\bm{\theta}}_{k}}({\bf x}_{k})\rightarrow 0\text{\ \ \ \ \ in\ probability.}

By Slutsky’s theorem, we eventually have the desired result

\omega_{k}^{-1/2}({\bm{\theta}}_{k}-\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{\star})\Rightarrow\mathcal{N}(0,{\bm{\Sigma}}).

where the step size
\omega_{k}
decays with an order
\alpha\in(0.5,1]
such that
\omega_{k}=\mathcal{O}(k^{-\alpha})
.

D.3
Deep contextual bandits on mushroom tasks

For the UCI Mushroom data set, each mushroom is either edible or poisonous. Eating an edible mushroom yields a reward of 5, but eating a poisonous mushroom has a 50% chance to result in a reward of -35 and a reward of 5 otherwise. Eating nothing results in 0 reward. All the agents use the same architecture. In particular, we fit a two-layer neural network with 100 neurons each and ReLU activation functions. The input of the network is a feature vector with dimension 22 (context) and there are 2 outputs, representing the predicted reward for eating or not eating a mushroom. The mean squared loss is adopted for training the models. We initialize 1024 data points and keep a data buffer of size 4096 as the training proceeds. The size of the mini-batch data is set to 512.
