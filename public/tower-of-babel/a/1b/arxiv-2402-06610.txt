Equi-affine minimal-degree moving frames for polynomial curves
Hoon Hong, Irina A. Kogan

Abstract
We develop a theory and an algorithm for constructing minimal-degree polynomial moving frames for polynomial curves in an affine space. The algorithm is equivariant under volume-preserving affine transformations of the ambient space and the parameter shifts. We show that any matrix-completion algorithm can be turned into an equivariant moving frame algorithm via an equivariantization procedure that we develop. We prove that if a matrix-completion algorithm is of minimal degree then so is the resulting equivariant moving frame algorithm. We propose a novel minimal-degree matrix-completion algorithm, complementing the existing body of literature on this topic.



Keywords:
polynomial moving frames, polynomial curves, equivariance, affine transformations, parameter shifts, matrix completion, Bézout vectors,
\mu
-bases.

Problem statement and definitions

Throughout the paper,
\mathbb{K}
denotes a field of characteristic zero and
\overline{\mathbb{K}}
its algebraic closure, while
\mathbb{K}[t]
denotes the ring of polynomials over a field
\mathbb{K}

\mathbb{K}[t]^{n}
denotes the set of polynomial vectors of length

\mathbb{K}[t]^{n\times l}
denotes the set of

In examples, its is natural to assume
\mathbb{K}=\mathbb{Q}

\mathbb{K}=\mathbb{R}

Our goal is to design an algorithm whose input is a polynomial curve in an
n
-dimensional affine space and whose output is an
n
-tuple of linearly independent polynomial vectors, such that the first vector is tangent to the input curve, and the parallelepiped spanned by all
n
vectors has constant volume equal to one. The output can be visualized as
a frame
of basis vectors in
n
-dimensional affine space
moving
along the curve. In addition, we impose two desirable geometric and algebraic properties on our algorithm: degree minimality of the output and equivariance of the algorithm under affine transformations of the ambient space and parameterization-shifts. A rigorous statement of the problem is given at the end of this section and is based on the following definitions.

Definition 1
(degree)

The
degree
of a polynomial vector
{\mathbf{v}}=\begin{bmatrix}{\mathbf{v}}_{1}\\
\vdots\\
{\mathbf{v}}_{n}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{K}[t]^{n}
is defined by

\deg{\mathbf{v}}=\max_{i=1}^{n}\ \deg{\mathbf{v}}_{i}.

The
degree
of a polynomial matrix
{\mathbf{W}}\in\mathbb{K}\left[t\right]^{n\times\ell}

is defined by

\deg{\mathbf{W}}=\sum_{j=1}^{\ell}\deg\mathbf{W}_{*j}\;\;\;\;\;(\text{where $\mathbf{W}_{*j}$ denotes the $j$-th column vector of $\mathbf{W}$})

The set of polynomial vectors of length
n
and degree
d
is denoted by
\mathbb{K}[t]^{n}_{d}
.

Definition 2
(generic polynomial curves)

A column vector
\mathfrak{c}\in\mathbb{K}\left[t\right]^{n}
is called a

generic polynomial curve
if

1.

For every
t\in\overline{\mathbb{K}}\ \
we have
\mathfrak{c}^{\prime}\left(t\right)\neq 0
.

2.

The curve does not lie in a proper affine subspace of
\mathbb{K}^{n}
.

3.

\deg\mathfrak{c}>n
.

The set of all generic polynomial curves is denoted by
\mathcal{C}
.

Remark 3

The three conditions in the definition are motivated by the following considerations.

1.

The requirement that the tangent vector is non-zero is the standard genericity condition in differential geometry. If
\mathbb{K}
is not algebraically closed, our requirement is stronger and turns out to be necessary and sufficient for a moving frame to exist along the curve.

2.

If a curve lies in a proper affine subspace of
\mathbb{K}^{n}
, then after an appropriate affine transformation it can be represented by a polynomial vector in a lower dimensional linear space. The first and the second conditions combined are necessary and sufficient for a curve to be included in the domain a moving frame algorithm, which is equivariant under the ambient space affine transformations, however, it is not sufficient for a curve to be included in the domain of a parameter-shift and ambient space equivariant moving frame algorithm (see Definition
14
)

3.

For the second condition to hold it is necessary that
\deg\mathfrak{c}\geq n
. The sharp inequality in the third condition, in combination with the above two conditions, turns out to be necessary and sufficient for a curve to be included in the domain of a fully equivariant moving frame algorithm.

When
d>n
, the set of generic curves of degree
d
is Zariski open and dense in
\mathbb{K}[t]^{n}_{d}
.

Before giving the definition of an equi-affine moving frame map, we review the standard definitions of a group action, as well as definitions of invariant and equivariant maps.

Definition 7
(action)

An
action
of a group
{G}
on a set
\mathcal{Z}
is a map
\alpha\colon{G}\times\mathcal{Z}\to\mathcal{Z}
satisfying the following two properties:

i.

Associativity:

\alpha({g}_{1}\star{g}_{2},{z})=\alpha({g}_{1},\alpha({g}_{2},{z}))
,
\forall{g}_{1},{g}_{2}\in{G}
and
\forall{z}\in\mathcal{Z}
.

ii.

Action of the identity element:

\alpha(e,{z})={z}
,
\forall{z}\in\mathcal{Z}
.

We say that an action
\alpha
is
free
if for all
{z}\in\mathcal{Z}
and
{g}\in{G}
:

\alpha({g},{z})={z}\qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad{g}=e.

Remark 8

1.

In the above definition,
\star
denotes the group operation. The abbreviation
\alpha({g},{z})={g}\cdot{z}
will be used when the map
\alpha
is clear from the context.

2.

To avoid cluttering of parentheses, we
will sometimes use

to denote either of the two quantities:
g_{1}\cdot(g_{2}\cdot{z})
and
(g_{1}\star g_{2})\cdot{z}
, whose equality is due to the associativity of the action.

3.

If two groups

and

and their actions commute, then there is a well defined action of the direct product

Conversely, an action of a direct product

and

Theorem 47
(minimal-degree matrix-completion)

Let
{\mathbf{v}}\in\mathbb{K}[t]^{n}
be a polynomial vector
such that
\gcd({{\mathbf{v}}})=1
. Assume
{\mathbf{b}}
is a normalized Bézout vector of

{\mathbf{v}}
and
\left\{{\mathbf{u}}_{1},\dots,{\mathbf{u}}_{n-1}\right\}
is a normalized
\mu
-basis of

{\mathbf{b}}
,
then:

1.

The matrix
\mathbf{M}=\begin{bmatrix}{\mathbf{v}}&{\mathbf{u}}_{1}&\dots&{\mathbf{u}}_{n-1}\end{bmatrix}
is a matrix-completion of
{\mathbf{v}}
.

2.

\deg\mathbf{M}=\deg{\mathbf{v}}+\deg{\mathbf{b}}
.

3.

If
{\mathbf{b}}
is a minimal-degree Bézout vector, then
\mathbf{M}
is a minimal-degree matrix-completion of
{\mathbf{v}}
.

Proof.
Using the definitions of a Bézout vector and a
\mu
-basis, we have:

1.

|\mathbf{M}|=\left|\begin{matrix}{\mathbf{v}}&{\mathbf{u}}_{1}&\dots&{\mathbf{u}}_{n-1}\end{matrix}\right|=\big<{\mathbf{v}},{\mathbf{u}}_{1}\wedge\dots\wedge{\mathbf{u}}_{n-1}\big>=\big<{\mathbf{v}},{\mathbf{b}}\big>=1
.

2.

\deg\mathbf{M}=\deg{\mathbf{v}}+\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\deg({\mathbf{u}}_{i})=\deg{\mathbf{v}}+\deg{\mathbf{b}}
.

3.

Assume
\tilde{\mathbf{M}}=\begin{bmatrix}{\mathbf{v}}&{\mathbf{w}}_{1}&\cdots&{\mathbf{w}}_{n-1}\end{bmatrix}
is any matrix-completion of
{\mathbf{v}}
. Let
{\mathbf{a}}={\mathbf{w}}_{1}\wedge\dots\wedge{\mathbf{w}}_{n-1}
. Since
|\tilde{\mathbf{M}}|=\big<{\mathbf{v}},{\mathbf{a}}\big>=1
, then
{\mathbf{a}}
is a Bézout vector of
{\mathbf{v}}
. The definition of the outer product implies that
\deg{\mathbf{a}}\leq\deg{\mathbf{w}}_{1}+\dots+\deg{\mathbf{w}}_{n-1}
and so

\deg\tilde{\mathbf{M}}=\deg{\mathbf{v}}+\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\deg({\mathbf{w}}_{i})\geq\deg{\mathbf{v}}+\deg{\mathbf{a}}\geq\deg{\mathbf{v}}+\deg{\mathbf{b}},

where the last inequality is due to degree-minimality of
{\mathbf{b}}
. From Part 2 it follows that
\mathbf{M}
is of minimal degree.

4.2
Minimality degree preservation

In this section, we show that minimal-degree property of
any
matrix-completion map is preserved by the equivariantization process described in Theorem
35
.
We will need a few preliminary results.

Lemma 53

For
{\mathbf{v}}\in\mathbb{K}[t]^{n}
and
(L,s)\in SL_{n}(\mathbb{K})\times\mathbb{K}
,

\deg{\mathbf{v}}=\deg\left[(L,s)\cdot{\mathbf{v}}\right],

where, as we defined before,
(L,s)\cdot{\mathbf{v}}(t)=L{\mathbf{v}}(t+s)
.

Proof.
Observe that
\deg{\mathbf{v}}(t)=\deg{\mathbf{v}}(t+s)
because a shift of the parameter does not change the degree of a polynomial. Let

{\mathbf{v}}=\begin{bmatrix}{\mathbf{v}}_{1}\\
\vdots\\
{\mathbf{v}}_{n}\end{bmatrix},

and

\deg{\mathbf{v}}=\max_{i=1\dots n}\deg{\mathbf{v}}_{i}=d,\;\;\;\;\text{and}\;\;\;\;{\rm coeff}({\mathbf{v}}_{i},d)=c_{id}.

Since
L{\mathbf{v}}={\mathbf{v}}_{1}L_{*1}+\dots+{\mathbf{v}}_{n}L_{*n}
, we have
\deg(L{\mathbf{v}})\leq\deg{\mathbf{v}}=d
, and
\deg L{\mathbf{v}}<d
if and only if

c_{1d}L_{*1}+\dots+c_{nd}L_{*n}=0.

(39)

Since at least one of the coefficients
c_{id}
is non-zero, (
39
) implies linear dependence of the columns of
L
, which contradicts our assumption that
L\in SL_{n}(\mathbb{K})
and so
|L|=1
. Thus
\deg L{\mathbf{v}}=\deg{\mathbf{v}}
.

Corollary 54

For
\mathbf{W}(t)\in\mathbb{K}[t]^{n\times l}
and
(L,s)\in SL_{n}(\mathbb{K})\times\mathbb{K}
,

\deg\mathbf{W}=\deg\left[(L,s)\cdot\mathbf{W}\right],

where
(L,s)\cdot\mathbf{W}(t)=L\mathbf{W}(t+s)
.

Proof.
First, note that the transformation induced by
(L,s)
is column-wise:

\big[(L,s)\cdot\mathbf{W}\big]_{*i}=(L,s)\cdot\mathbf{W}_{*i},\qquad i=1,\dots l.

Then, from Lemma
53
,
\deg\big[(L,s)\cdot\mathbf{W}\big]_{*i}=\deg\mathbf{W}_{*i}
.
Since the degree of a polynomial matrix is defined as the sum of the degrees of its columns, the conclusion follows.

Lemma 55

For
{\mathbf{v}}\in\mathbb{K}[t]^{n}
such that
\gcd({\mathbf{v}})=1
and
(L,s)\in SL_{n}(\mathbb{K})\times\mathbb{K}
, let

\bar{\mathbf{v}}=(L,s)\cdot{\mathbf{v}}
, and let
{\mathbf{b}}
and
\bar{\mathbf{b}}
be minimal degree Bézout vectors of
{\mathbf{v}}
and
\bar{\mathbf{v}}
, respectively.
Then
\gcd(\bar{\mathbf{v}})=1
and

\deg{\mathbf{b}}=\deg\bar{\mathbf{b}}.

Proof.
Since
\big<{\mathbf{b}}(t),{\mathbf{v}}(t)\big>=1
,
we have

\big<L^{-T}{\mathbf{b}}(t+s),L{\mathbf{v}}(t+s)\big>=\big<\tilde{\mathbf{b}},\bar{\mathbf{v}}\big>=1,

where

\tilde{\mathbf{b}}=(L^{-T},s)\cdot{\mathbf{b}}=L^{-T}{\mathbf{b}}(t+s).

Therefore,
\gcd(\bar{\mathbf{v}})=1
and
\tilde{\mathbf{b}}
is a Bézout vector of
\bar{\mathbf{v}}
. From Lemma
53
,
\deg\tilde{b}=\deg b
.

Assume
\tilde{\mathbf{b}}
is not a
minimal-degree
Bézout vector of
\bar{\mathbf{v}}
, i.e.
\deg\tilde{\mathbf{b}}>\deg\bar{\mathbf{b}}
. Applying the above argument to
\bar{\mathbf{b}}
, we see that
\hat{\mathbf{b}}=(L^{T},-s)\cdot\bar{\mathbf{b}}
is a Bézout vector of
{\mathbf{v}}
such that
\deg\hat{\mathbf{b}}=\deg\bar{\mathbf{b}}
. From the previous paragraph we have, however, that
\deg\bar{\mathbf{b}}<\deg\tilde{\mathbf{b}}=\deg{\mathbf{b}}
. Thus
\deg\hat{\mathbf{b}}<\deg{\mathbf{b}}
, which contradicts the assumption that
{\mathbf{b}}
is of minimal degree. We are forced to conclude that
\tilde{\mathbf{b}}
is a minimal degree Bézout vector of
\bar{\mathbf{v}}
and so

\deg\bar{\mathbf{b}}=\deg\tilde{\mathbf{b}}=\deg{\mathbf{b}}
.

Proposition 56

Let
\mathcal{M}\colon\mathcal{V}\to\mathbb{K}[t]^{n\times n}
be a minimal degree matrix-completion map. For any
(L,s)\in SL_{n}(\mathbb{K})\times\mathbb{K}
the map
\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}\colon\mathcal{V}\rightarrow\mathbb{K}[t]^{n\times n}

defined by

\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}({\mathbf{v}})=(L,s)\cdot\mathcal{M}\left((L,s)^{-1}\cdot{\mathbf{v}}\right)

(40)

is a minimal degree matrix-completion map.

Proof.

1.

By Lemma
21
,
\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}
is a matrix-completion map.

2.

By Corollary
54
,
\deg\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}({\mathbf{v}})=\deg\mathcal{M}\left((L,s)^{-1}\cdot{\mathbf{v}}\right)
.

3.

By Theorem
47

\deg\mathcal{M}\left((L,s)^{-1}\cdot{\mathbf{v}}\right)=\deg\bar{\mathbf{v}}+\deg\bar{\mathbf{b}}
, where
\bar{v}=(L,s)^{-1}\cdot{\mathbf{v}}
and
\bar{\mathbf{b}}
is a minimal degree Bézout vector of
\bar{\mathbf{v}}
.

4.

By Lemmas
53
and
55
,
\deg\bar{\mathbf{v}}=\deg{\mathbf{v}}
and
\deg\bar{\mathbf{b}}=\deg{\mathbf{b}}
, where
{\mathbf{b}}
is a minimal-degree Bézout vector of
{\mathbf{v}}
.

Combining the above facts, we conclude that
\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}
is a matrix completion map, such that, for every
{\mathbf{v}}\in\mathcal{V}
,
\deg\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}({\mathbf{v}})=\deg{\mathbf{v}}+\deg{\mathbf{b}}
, where
{\mathbf{b}}
is a minimal degree Bézout vector of
{\mathbf{v}}
. Corollary
49
, implies that
\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}
is a minimal degree matrix-completion map.

We immediately have the following result:

Theorem 57
(equivariant minimal-degree matrix-completion map)

Let

•

\mathcal{M}\colon\mathcal{V}\to\mathbb{K}[t]^{n\times n}
be a minimal-degree matrix-completion map,

•

\rho\colon\mathcal{V}\to SL_{n}(\mathbb{K})\times\mathbb{K}
be any equivariant section (for instance, the map defined by (
27
) in Theorem
36
).

Then the map
\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}\colon\mathcal{V}\rightarrow\mathbb{K}[t]^{n\times n}

defined by

\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}({\mathbf{v}})=\rho({\mathbf{v}})\cdot\mathcal{M}\left(\rho({\mathbf{v}})^{-1}\cdot{\mathbf{v}}\right)

(41)

is an equivariant minimal-degree matrix-completion map.

Proof.

The fact that the map
\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}\colon\mathcal{V}\rightarrow\mathbb{K}[t]^{n\times n}
is an equivariant
matrix completion map
follows from Theorem
35
. To show that it is of
minimal degree
map we can use the same argument as in Proposition
56
with
L
replaced by
L({\mathbf{v}})
and
s
replaced with
s({\mathbf{v}})
, where
\rho({\mathbf{v}})=\left(L({\mathbf{v}}),s({\mathbf{v}})\right)
.

Remark 58

\mathcal{M}

\deg\mathcal{M}({\mathbf{v}})=\deg\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}({\mathbf{v}})\text{ for all }{\mathbf{v}}\in\mathcal{V}.

This appendix provides a theoretical background for the minimal-degree Bézout vector and

-basis algorithms that we used in our implementation. It draws the material from
a published paper
[

]
, an unpublished manuscript
[
29
]
and a Ph.D. Thesis
[

.
Theorems

and

show that, given a vector

such that
\gcd({\mathbf{v}})=1
, one can deduce a minimal-degree Bézout vector of

6.1
Sylvester-type matrix
A
and its properties

For a nonzero polynomial vector

{\mathbf{v}}=\underset{V}{\underbrace{\left[\begin{array}[c]{ccc}v_{10}&\dots&v_{1d}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots\\
v_{n0}&\dots&v_{nd}\end{array}\right]}}\left[\begin{array}[c]{c}t^{0}\\
\vdots\\
t^{d}\end{array}\right]\in\mathbb{K}[t]^{n}_{d},

(44)

we correspond a
\mathbb{K}^{(2d+1)\times n(d+1)}
matrix

A=\left[\begin{array}[c]{cccccccccc}v_{10}&\cdots&v_{n0}&&&&&&&\\
\vdots&\cdots&\vdots&v_{10}&\cdots&v_{n0}&&&&\\
\vdots&\cdots&\vdots&\vdots&\cdots&\vdots&\ddots&&&\\
v_{1d}&\cdots&v_{nd}&\vdots&\cdots&\vdots&\ddots&v_{10}&\cdots&v_{n0}\\
&&&v_{1d}&\cdots&v_{nd}&\ddots&\vdots&\cdots&\vdots\\
&&&&&&\ddots&\vdots&\cdots&\vdots\\
&&&&&&&v_{1d}&\cdots&v_{nd}\end{array}\right]

(45)

with the blank spaces filled by zeros. In other words, matrix
A
is obtained
by taking
d+1
copies of a
(d+1)\times n
matrix

. The blocks are repeated horizontally from left to
right, and each block is shifted down by one relative to the previous one.

Definition 73

A column of any matrix
N
is called
pivotal
if it is either the first
column and is nonzero or it is linearly independent of all previous columns.
The rest of the columns of
N
are called
non-pivotal
. The index of a
pivotal (non-pivotal) column is called a
pivotal (non-pivotal) index
.

From this definition, it follows that every non-pivotal column can be written
as a linear combination of the preceding
pivotal columns
.
We denote the set of pivotal indices of

as

and the set of its
non-pivotal indices as

[

show how the specific structure of the matrix

Lemma 74
(periodicity)

If
j\in q
then
j+kn\in q
for
0\leq k\leq\left\lfloor\frac{n(d+1)-j}{n}\right\rfloor
. Moreover,

A_{*j}=\sum_{r<j}\alpha_{r}\,A_{*r}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad A_{*j+kn}=\sum_{r<j}\alpha_{r}\,A_{*r+kn},

(46)

where
A_{*j}
denotes the
j
-th column of
A
.

Definition 75

Let
q
be the set of non-pivotal indices. Let
q/(n)
denote
the set of equivalence classes of
q\text{ modulo }n
. Then the set
\tilde{q}=\{\min\varrho\,|\varrho\in q/(n)\}
will be called the set of

non-pivotal indices
. The remaining indices in

will be called

.

6.2
Isomorphism between
\mathbb{K}[t]_{\delta}^{\eta}
and
\mathbb{K}^{\eta(\delta+1)}

Our second ingredient is an explicit isomorphism between the vector space

\mathbb{K}[t]_{\delta}^{\eta}
of polynomial vectors of length
\eta
and degree
\delta
and the vector space
\mathbb{K}^{\eta(\delta+1)}
of constant vectors of length
\eta(\delta+1)
.

For fixed
\eta
and
\delta
, we define the
sharp map

\sharp^{\eta}_{\delta}\colon\mathbb{K}[t]_{\delta}^{\eta}\to\mathbb{K}^{\eta(\delta+1)}
by sending a polynomial vector

{\mathbf{h}}=\underset{H}{\underbrace{\left[\begin{array}[c]{ccc}h_{10}&\dots&h_{1\delta}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots\\
h_{\eta 0}&\dots&h_{\eta\delta}\end{array}\right]}}\left[\begin{array}[c]{c}t^{0}\\
\vdots\\
t^{\delta}\end{array}\right]\in\mathbb{K}[t]^{\eta}_{\delta},

(47)

to a constant vector

{\mathbf{h}}^{\sharp^{\eta}_{\delta}}=\left[\begin{array}[c]{c}H_{*0}\\
\vdots\\
H_{*d}\end{array}\right]\in\mathbb{K}^{\eta(\delta+1)}

(48)

obtained by stacking the
\delta+1
columns of the coefficient matrix
H
.

For fixed
\eta
and
\delta
, we define the
flat map

\flat^{\eta}_{\delta}\colon\mathbb{K}^{\eta(\delta+1)}\to\mathbb{K}[t]^{\eta}_{\delta}

by

h\to h^{\flat^{\eta}_{\delta}}=S^{\eta}_{\delta}\,h

(49)

where

S^{\eta}_{\delta}=\left[\begin{array}[c]{ccc}I_{\eta}&tI_{\eta}&\cdots t^{\delta}I_{\eta}\end{array}\right]\in\mathbb{K}[t]^{\eta\times\eta(\delta+1)}

with
I_{\eta}
denoting the
\eta\times\eta
identity matrix.

It is easy to check that the
flat and sharp maps are linear and they are the inverse of each other
.
For the sake of
notational simplicity, we will often write
\sharp
,
\flat
and
S
instead
of
\sharp^{\eta}_{\delta}
,
\flat^{\eta}_{\delta}
and
S^{\eta}_{\delta}
when the values of
\eta

and
\delta
are clear from the context.

6.3
The minimal Bézout vector theorem

In this section, we construct a minimal-degree Bézout vector of
{\mathbf{v}}
by finding an appropriate solution to the linear equation

A\,b=e_{1},\text{ where }e_{1}=[1,0,\,\dots,\,0]^{T}\in\mathbb{K}^{2d+1}.

(51)

The following lemma establishes a one-to-one correspondence between the set

\operatorname{Bez}_{d}({\mathbf{v}})
of Bézout vectors of
{\mathbf{v}}
of degree
at most

d
and the set
of solutions to (
51
).

Lemma 82

Let
{\mathbf{v}}\in\mathbb{K}[t]_{d}^{n}
be a nonzero vector
such that
\gcd({\mathbf{v}})=1
. Then
{\mathbf{b}}\in\mathbb{K}[t]^{n}_{d}

belongs to
\operatorname{Bez}_{d}({\mathbf{v}})
if and only if
{\mathbf{b}}^{\sharp}
is a solution of (
51
). Also
b\in\mathbb{K}^{n(d+1)}

is a solution of (
51
) if and only if
b^{\flat}
belongs to

\operatorname{Bez}_{d}({\mathbf{v}})
.

Proof.
Follows immediately from (
50
) and the observation that
e_{1}^{\flat^{1}_{2d}}=1
.

Now, the goal is to construct a solution
b
of (
51
), such that

b^{\flat}
is a Bézout vector of
{\mathbf{v}}
of minimal degree. To accomplish this,
we recall that, when
\gcd({\mathbf{v}})=1
, Proposition
78
asserts
that
\mathrm{rank}(A)=2d+1
. Therefore,
A
has exactly
2d+1
pivotal
indices, which we can list in the increasing order
p=\{p_{1},\dots,p_{2d+1}\}
. The corresponding columns of matrix
A
form a basis of
\mathbb{K}^{2d+1}
and, therefore,
e_{1}\in\mathbb{K}^{2d+1}
can be
expressed as a unique linear combination of the pivotal columns:

e_{1}=\sum_{j=1}^{2d+1}\alpha_{j}A_{*p_{j}}.

(52)

Define vector
b\in\mathbb{K}^{n(d+1)}
by setting its
p_{j}
-th element to be

\alpha_{j}
and all other elements to be 0. We prove that
{\mathbf{b}}=b^{\flat}
is a Bézout vector of
{\mathbf{v}}
of the minimal degree.

Theorem 83
(Minimal-Degree Bézout Vector)

Let
{\mathbf{v}}\in\mathbb{K}[t]^{n}_{d}
be a polynomial vector with
\gcd({\mathbf{v}})=1
, and let
A
be the
corresponding matrix defined by (
45
). Let
p=\{p_{1},\dots,p_{2d+1}\}
be the pivotal indices of
A
, and let
\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{2d+1}\in\mathbb{K}
be defined by the unique expression
(
52
) of the vector
e_{1}\in\mathbb{K}^{2d+1}
as a linear
combination of the pivotal columns of
A
. Define vector
b\in\mathbb{K}^{2d+1}
by setting its
p_{j}
-th element to be
\alpha_{j}
for
j=1,\dots,2d+1
and all other elements to be 0, and let
\mathbf{b}=b^{\flat}
. Then

1.

{\mathbf{b}}\in\operatorname{Bez}_{d}({\mathbf{v}})

2.

\deg({\mathbf{b}})=\displaystyle{\min_{{\mathbf{b}}^{\prime}\in\operatorname{Bez}({\mathbf{v}})}}\deg(\mathbf{{\mathbf{b}}}^{\prime}).

Proof.

1.

From (
52
), it follows immediately that
Ab=e_{1}
.
Therefore, by Lemma
82
, we have that
{\mathbf{b}}=b^{\flat}\in\operatorname{Bez}_{d}({\mathbf{v}})
.

2.

To show that
\mathbf{b}
is of minimal degree, we rewrite
(
52
) as

e_{1}=\sum_{j=1}^{k}\alpha_{j}A_{*p_{j}},

(53)

where
k
is the largest integer between 1 and
2d+1
, such that
\alpha_{k}\neq 0
. Then the last nonzero entry of
b
appears in
p_{k}
-th position
and, therefore,

\deg(\mathbf{b})=\deg(b^{\flat})=\big\lceil{p_{k}/n}\big\rceil-1.

(54)

Assume that
\mathbf{b}^{\prime}\in\operatorname{Bez}({\mathbf{v}})
is such
that
\deg({\mathbf{b}}^{\prime})<\deg({\mathbf{b}})
. Then
\mathbf{b}^{\prime}\in\operatorname{Bez}_{d}({\mathbf{v}})
and, therefore,
A\,b^{\prime}=e_{1}
,
for
b^{\prime}=\mathbf{b}^{\prime\sharp}=[b^{\prime}_{1},\dots,b^{\prime}_{n(d+1)}]\in\mathbb{K}^{n(d+1)}
. Then

e_{1}=\sum_{j=1}^{n(d+1)}\,b^{\prime}_{j}A_{*j}=\sum_{j=1}^{r}b^{\prime}_{j}A_{*j},

(55)

where
r
is the largest integer between 1 and
n(d+1)
, such that
b^{\prime}_{r}\neq 0
. Then

\deg({\mathbf{b}}^{\prime})=\big\lceil{r/n}\big\rceil-1

(56)

and since we assumed that
\deg({\mathbf{b}}^{\prime})<\deg({\mathbf{b}})
, we
conclude from (
54
) and (
56
) that
r<p_{k}
.

On the other hand, since all non-pivotal columns are linear combinations of
the preceding pivotal columns, we can rewrite (
55
) as

e_{1}=\sum_{j\in\{1,\dots,2d\,|\,p_{j}\leq r<p_{k}\}}\alpha^{\prime}_{j}A_{*p_{j}}=\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}\alpha^{\prime}_{j}A_{*p_{j}}.

(57)

By the uniqueness of the representation of
e_{1}
as a linear combination of the
A_{\ast p_{j}}
, the coefficients in the expansions (
53
) and
(
57
) must be equal, but
\alpha_{k}\neq 0
in (
53
). Contradiction.

The algorithm presented in
[
29
,
31
]
exploits the fact that
the coefficients
\alpha
’s in (
53
), needed to construct the minimal-degree
Bézout vector of
{\mathbf{v}}
prescribed by Theorem
83
, can be read off the reduced row-echelon form
[\hat{A}|\hat{b}]
of the augmented matrix
[A|e_{1}]
.

Theorem 84
(
\mu
-Basis)

Let

column

columns:

A_{*\tilde{q}_{i}}=\sum_{\{r\in p\,|\,r<\tilde{q}_{i}\}}\alpha_{ir}A_{*r},

(58)

for some
\alpha_{ir}\in\mathbb{K}
. Define vector
u_{i}\in\mathbb{K}^{n(d+1)}

\tilde{q}_{i}
-th element to be
1

-\alpha_{ir}

\mathbf{u}_{1}=u_{1}^{\flat},\quad\dots\quad,\mathbf{u}_{n-1}=u_{n-1}^{\flat}

is a degree-ordered
\mu
-basis of
{\mathbf{v}}
.

Proof.
The fact that
\mathbf{u}_{1}=u_{1}^{\flat},\quad\dots\quad,\mathbf{u}_{n-1}=u_{n-1}^{\flat}

\mu
-basis of
{\mathbf{v}}

[

]
. By construction, the last nonzero entry of vector

Since the indices in
