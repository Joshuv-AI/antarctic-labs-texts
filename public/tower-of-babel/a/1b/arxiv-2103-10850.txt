Quantum and classical ergotropy from relative entropies
Akira Sone, Sebastian Deffner

Abstract
The quantum ergotropy quantifies the maximal amount of work that can be extracted from a quantum state without changing its entropy. Given that the ergotropy can be expressed as the difference of quantum and classical relative entropies of the quantum state with respect to the thermal state, we define the classical ergotropy, which quantifies how much work can be extracted from distributions that are inhomogeneous on the energy surfaces. A unified approach to treat both quantum as well as classical scenarios is provided by geometric quantum mechanics, for which we define the geometric relative entropy. The analysis is concluded with an application of the conceptual insight to conditional thermal states, and the correspondingly tightened maximum work theorem.



I
Introduction

According to its definition, the adjective
ergotropic
refers to the physiological mechanisms of a nervous system to favor an organism’s capacity to expend energy
Gellhorn 1970
. Generalizing this notion to physical systems,
quantum ergotropy
was then coined to denote the maximal amount of work that can be extracted by isentropic transformations
Allahverdyan et al. 2004
. In particular, the quantum ergotropy quantifies the amount of energy that is stored in
active
quantum states, and which can be extracted by making the state
passive

Pusz and Woronowicz 1978
;
Koukoulekidis et al. 2021
;
Górecki and Pusz 1980
;
Daniëls 1981
. In simple terms, a passive state is diagonal in the energy basis, and its eigenstates are ordered in descending magnitude of its eigenvalues. Gibbs states are then called
completely passive

Pusz and Woronowicz 1978
.

The quantum ergotropy plays a prominent role in quantum thermodynamics
Deffner and Campbell 2019
. In particular, when assessing the
thermodynamic value
of genuine quantum properties
Goold et al. 2016
;
Perarnau-Llobet et al. 2017
;
Levy and Lostaglio 2020
;
Santos et al. 2019
, such as squeezed and nonequilibrium reservoirs
Niedenzu et al. 2018
;
Cherubim et al. 2019
, coherence
Francica et al. 2020
;
Çakmak 2020
, or quantum correlations
Francica et al. 2017
;
Touil et al. 2021
, it has proven powerful. However, if the quantum system is not in contact with a heat reservoir, computing the quantum ergotropy is far from trivial. This is due to the fact that the ergotropy is determined by a maximum over all unitaries that can act upon the system
Allahverdyan et al. 2004
. Note that not all passive states can be reached by unitary operations, in particular, including the completely passive state.

In this paper, given that the quantum ergotropy can be written as the difference of quantum and classical relative entropies (the Kullback–Leibler divergence of the eigenvalue distributions), we define a
classical ergotropy
, which quantifies the maximal amount of work that can be extracted from inhomogeneities on the energy surfaces, which have been shown to be analogous to quantum coherences
Smith 2019
;
Smith et al.
.

In a second part of the analysis, we turn to a unified framework, namely geometric quantum mechanics. Exploiting this approach
Anza and Crutchfield 2020a
;
Anza and Crutchfield 2020b
;
Anza and Crutchfield 2020c
, we define the
geometric relative entropy
. With this, it becomes particularly transparent to characterize the one-time measurement approach to quantum work
Deffner et al. 2016
;
Beyer et al. 2020
;
Sone et al. 2020
;
Sone and Deffner 2021
;
Allahverdyan and Nieuwenhuizen 2005
. In this paradigm, work is determined by first measuring the energy of the system, and then letting it evolve under time-dependent dynamics. In contrast to the two-time measurement approach
Kurchan 2001
;
Tasaki 2000
;
Talkner et al. 2007
;
Huber et al. 2008
;
Campisi et al. 2011
;
Deffner and Lutz 2011
;
Kafri and Deffner 2012
;
Mazzola et al. 2013
;
Dorner et al. 2013
;
Roncaglia et al. 2014
;
Batalhão et al. 2014
;
An et al. 2015
;
Deffner and Saxena 2015a
;
Deffner and Saxena 2015b
;
Talkner and Hänggi 2016
;
Gardas et al. 2016
;
Bartolotta and Deffner 2018
;
Gardas and Deffner 2018
;
Touil and Deffner 2021
, no projective measurement is taken at the end of the process. Hence, the work probability distribution is entirely determined by the statistics
conditioned
on the initial energy. Here, we identify the distinct contributions to the thermodynamic cost of projective measurements by separating out the coherent and incoherent ergotropies, and the population mismatch in the conditional statistics.

Hence, by expressing the quantum ergotropy as a difference of relative entropies, we are able to (i) generalize the notion to classical scenarios, and to (ii) elucidate the thermodynamics of projective measurements. This analysis further cements ergotropy as one of the salient pillars of quantum thermodynamics.

The paper is organized as follow. In Section
II
, we review quantum ergotropy in terms of relative entropies and its relation to the quantum coherence in Section
III
. Then, we introduce the formulation of classical ergotropy in Section
IV
, and discuss the geometric quantum mechanics approach to ergotropies in Section
V
. Finally, we discuss the physical meaning of the conditional thermal states in the second law of thermodynamics based on its ergotropy in Section
VI
before our conclusions in Section
VII
.

II
Quantum Ergotropy

We begin by deriving a simple expression for the quantum ergotropy, which does not explicitly depend on the optimization over unitary maps. To this end, consider a quantum system with Hamiltonian
H
and quantum state
\rho
. Then, the ergotropy is defined as
Allahverdyan et al. 2004

\mathcal{E}\left(\rho\right)\equiv\mathrm{tr}\left\{\rho\,H\right\}-\min_{U\in\mathcal{U}}\left[\mathrm{tr}\left\{U\rho U^{\dagger}\,H\right\}\right]\,,

(1)

where
\mathcal{U}
is the unitary group.

Our goal is now to express Eq. (
1
) as a difference of relative entropies. To this end, we write the quantum state
\rho
in its “ordered” eigenbasis

\rho=\sumint_{i}p_{i}\left|p_{i}\right\rangle\left\langle p_{i}\right|\quad\text{with}\quad p_{i}\geq p_{i+1}\,.

(2)

Let
\sigma
be a second quantum state, which we write

\sigma=\sumint_{i}s_{i}\left|s_{i}\right\rangle\left\langle s_{i}\right|\quad\text{with}\quad s_{i}\geq s_{i+1}\,.

(3)

In principle,
\rho
and
\sigma
can be vastly different quantum states. To better compare
\rho
and
\sigma
, it is then interesting to identify the unitary operation that takes
\rho
as close as possible to
\sigma
. Hence, considering the quantum relative entropy

S(U\rho U^{\dagger}||\sigma)\equiv\mathrm{tr}\left\{\rho\ln{\left(\rho\right)}\right\}-\mathrm{tr}\left\{U\rho U^{\dagger}\ln{\left(\sigma\right)}\right\}\,,

(4)

it is known that the minimization of the quantum relative entropy over all the unitary operations is the classical relative entropy
Nielsen and Chuang 2011
(see Appendix
A
for the proof)

\min_{U\in\mathcal{U}}\left[S(U\rho U^{\dagger}||\sigma)\right]=\sumint_{i}p_{i}\ln{\left(\frac{p_{i}}{s_{i}}\right)}\equiv D\left(\rho||\sigma\right)\,.

(5)

To this end, we choose
\sigma
as the Gibbs state

\rho^{\mathrm{eq}}\equiv\frac{\exp{\left(-\beta H\right)}}{Z}\quad\text{with}\quad Z\equiv\mathrm{tr}\left\{\exp{\left(-\beta H\right)}\right\}\,.

(6)

For the sake of simplicity, we further assume that the eigenenergies are ordered in ascending magnitude,
E_{i}\leq E_{i+1}
. As an alternative expression, the quantum ergotropy can be expressed as the difference of relative entropies
Francica et al. 2020
;
Łobejko 2021
(see Appendix
B
for the proof)

\beta\,\mathcal{E}(\rho)=S(\rho||\rho^{\mathrm{eq}})-D(\rho||\rho^{\mathrm{eq}})\,.

(7)

Note that the quantum ergotropy does not depend on the specific value of the temperature, but
rather Eq. (
7
) holds for any
\beta
.
In conclusion, the quantum ergotropy is written as the difference of the quantum and classical relative entropies of the quantum state
\rho
with respect to
\rho^{\mathrm{eq}}
. Note that Eq. (
7
) is entirely determined by
\rho
and
\rho^{\mathrm{eq}}
, and independent of any optimization.

III
Ergotropy from Quantum Coherence

It was recently recognized
Francica et al. 2020
;
Çakmak 2020
;
Touil et al. 2021
that the quantum ergotropy (
1
) can be separated into two fundamentally different contributions

\mathcal{E}(\rho)=\mathcal{E}_{i}(\rho)+\mathcal{E}_{c}(\rho)\,.

(8)

The
incoherent ergotropy

\mathcal{E}_{i}(\rho)
denotes the maximal work that can be extracted from
\rho
without changing its coherence, which is defined as
Francica et al. 2020

\mathcal{E}_{i}(\rho)\equiv\mathrm{tr}\left\{(\rho-\tau)H\right\}\,.

(9)

Here, we call
\tau
the coherence-invariant state of
\rho
, which is defined as
Francica et al. 2020

\mathrm{tr}\left\{\tau H\right\}=\min_{U\in\mathcal{U}^{(i)}}\mathrm{tr}\left\{U\rho U^{\dagger}H\right\}\,,

(10)

where
\mathcal{U}^{(i)}
is the set of unitary operations without changing the coherence of
\rho
. Refer to Ref.
Francica et al. 2020
for more details about
\mathcal{U}^{(i)}
.

The
coherent ergotropy

\mathcal{E}_{c}(\rho)
is the work that is exclusively stored in the coherences. This can be quantified by the relative entropy of coherence
Baumgratz et al. 2014

\mathcal{C}(\rho)=\mathcal{H}\left(\mathcal{L}(\rho)\right)-\mathcal{H}(\rho)\,,

(11)

where
\mathcal{H}(\rho)\equiv-\mathrm{tr}\left\{\rho\ln{\left(\rho\right)}\right\}
is the von Neumann entropy of
\rho
, and
\mathcal{L}
is the purely dephasing map, i.e., the map that removes all coherences but leaves the diagonal elements in the energy basis invariant. From the expression of the coherent ergotropy derived in Ref.
Francica et al. 2020
and Eq. (
5
), the coherent ergotropy can be rewritten in terms of classical relative entropy

\beta\,\mathcal{E}_{c}(\rho)=\mathcal{C}(\rho)+S(\mathcal{L}(\tau)||\rho^{\mathrm{eq}})-D(\rho||\rho^{\mathrm{eq}})\,.

(12)

Hence, we conclude that there are three distinct contributions to the coherent ergotropy. Namely, work can be extracted not only from the coherences directly, but also from the population mismatch between the completely decohered state and the corresponding thermal state. However, the total extractable work is lowered by the fact that generally
\rho
is not diagonal in energy; hence, the classical relative entropy is different from the quantum relative entropy of the completely decohered state.

IV
Classical Ergotropy from Inhomogeneity

Remarkably, the above discussion of the quantum treatment can be generalized to purely classical scenarios. It was recently recognized that distributions that are inhomogeneous on the energy surfaces can be considered the classical equivalent of quantum states with coherences
Smith 2019
;
Smith et al.
. Therefore, we proceed by defining the
classical ergotropy
, which quantifies the maximal work that can be extracted from inhomogeneous distributions under Hamiltonian dynamics, i.e., under the classical equivalent of unitary maps.

We start with the classical distribution,
p(\Gamma)
, which measures how likely it is to find a system at a point in phase space
\Gamma
. Now consider a situation in which
\Gamma
is sampled microcanonically from an (initial) energy surface
A
; we then let
p_{A}(\Gamma)
evolve under Liouville’s equation. We are interested in assessing how close to equilibrium the system is driven. To this end, consider the joint distribution of finding
\Gamma^{\prime}
on energy surface
B
, given that
\Gamma
was sampled from energy surface
A

p_{B|A}(\Gamma,\Gamma^{\prime})=p(\Gamma^{\prime}|\Gamma)\,p_{A}(\Gamma)\,,

(13)

where
p(\Gamma^{\prime}|\Gamma)
is the classical transition probability distribution, which satisfies

\int d\Gamma\,p(\Gamma^{\prime}|\Gamma)=\int d\Gamma^{\prime}\,p(\Gamma^{\prime}|\Gamma)=1\,,

(14)

which follows from Liouville’s theorem and normalization. In the following, we formulate the classical ergotropy by focusing on the joint distribution
p_{B|A}(\Gamma,\Gamma^{\prime})
in general classical systems.

In complete analogy to the quantum case, we now consider the relative entropy of
p_{B|A}(\Gamma,\Gamma^{\prime})
with respect to the thermal distribution on energy surface
B

p_{B}^{\mathrm{eq}}(\Gamma^{\prime})=\frac{\exp{\left(-\beta E_{B}(\Gamma^{\prime})\right)}}{Z}\quad\text{with}\quad Z\equiv\int d\Gamma^{\prime}\exp{\left(-\beta E_{B}(\Gamma^{\prime})\right)}\,.

(15)

We can write

D(p_{B|A}||p_{B}^{\mathrm{eq}})=\int d\Gamma\int d\Gamma^{\prime}\,p_{B|A}(\Gamma,\Gamma^{\prime})\ln{\left(\frac{p_{B|A}(\Gamma,\Gamma^{\prime})}{p^{\mathrm{eq}}_{B}(\Gamma^{\prime})}\right)}\,.

(16)

Eq. (
16
) is a divergence-like quantity, which becomes non-negative only for the thermodynamic scenario (See Appendix
C
).
Note that the normalization of the transition probabilities (
14
) is essential to guarantee that the classical distributions,
p_{B|A}
and
p_{B}^{\mathrm{eq}}
, have the same support.

As before, we then seek a “transformed” joint distribution
\mathscr{Q}_{B|A}
for which the relative entropy
D\left(\mathscr{Q}_{B|A}||p_{B}^{\text{eq}}\right)
becomes minimal. This
\mathscr{Q}_{B|A}
can be written as

\mathscr{Q}_{B|A}(\Gamma^{\prime\prime},\Gamma)\equiv\int d\Gamma^{\prime}\,q(\Gamma^{\prime\prime}|\Gamma^{\prime})p(\Gamma^{\prime}|\Gamma)p_{A}(\Gamma)\,,

(17)

and we need to minimize
D\left(\mathscr{Q}_{B|A}||p_{B}^{\text{eq}}\right)
as a function of the transition probability distribution
q(\Gamma^{\prime\prime}|\Gamma^{\prime})
. We start by recognizing that the convolution of two transition probability distributions is also a transition probability distribution

\xi(\Gamma^{\prime\prime}|\Gamma)\equiv\int d\Gamma^{\prime}\,q(\Gamma^{\prime\prime}|\Gamma^{\prime})p(\Gamma^{\prime}|\Gamma)\,.

(18)

Then, we have
\mathscr{Q}_{B|A}(\Gamma^{\prime\prime},\Gamma)=\xi(\Gamma^{\prime\prime}|\Gamma)\,p_{A}(\Gamma)
, thus we need to minimize
D\left(\mathscr{Q}_{B|A}||p_{B}^{\text{eq}}\right)
as a function
\xi
. In a complete analogy to the quantum case, we can choose
\xi(\Gamma^{\prime}|\Gamma)=\delta(\Gamma^{\prime}-\Gamma)
and obtain the following result (see Appendix
D
for the proof)

\min_{\xi}\left[D\left(\mathscr{Q}_{B|A}||p_{B}^{\text{eq}}\right)\right]=D\left(p_{A}||p_{B}^{\text{eq}}\right)\,.

(19)

Accordingly, we define the classical ergotropy as

\beta\,\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{class}}(p_{B|A})\equiv D\left(p_{B|A}||p_{B}^{\text{eq}}\right)-D\left(p_{A}||p_{B}^{\text{eq}}\right)\,,

(20)

which quantifies the maximal amount of work that can be extracted from the joint distribution
p_{B|A}
under Liouvillian maps. Remarkably, both the quantum (
7
) as well as the classical (
20
) ergotropy comprise the classical relative entropy with respect to a thermal state.

Eq. (
20
) can also be re-written to resemble more closely the established expression of the quantum ergotropy (
1
). We have

\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{class}}(p_{B|A})=\int d\Gamma^{\prime}\,\varphi_{B}(\Gamma^{\prime})E_{B}(\Gamma^{\prime})

(21)

where we introduced

\varphi_{B}(\Gamma^{\prime})=\int d\Gamma\,p_{B|A}(\Gamma^{\prime},\Gamma)-p_{A}(\Gamma^{\prime})\,.

(22)

In this form, it becomes apparent that the classical ergotropy quantifies the maximal amount of work stored in the inhomogeneities. Notice that
\varphi_{B}
is not an explicit function of the Hamiltonian of the system, which was shown to be a classical equivalent of the quantum coherences
Smith 2019
;
Smith et al.
. This is the analogy of how the quantum ergotropy quantifies the maximal work extractable from quantum coherences.

V
Ergotropy in Geometric Quantum Mechanics

Thus far, we have seen that in quantum as well as in classical systems, work can be extracted by “reshaping” the states in phase space without changing their entropy. Remarkably, in either case, the ergotropy is given by a difference of relative entropies (see Eqs. (
7
) and (
20
)). The natural question arises as to whether the quantum-to-classical limit can be taken systematically, or rather the seemingly independent results can be derived within a unifying framework.

Only very recently, Anza and Crutchfield
Anza and Crutchfield 2020a
;
Anza and Crutchfield 2020b
;
Anza and Crutchfield 2020c
recognized that for such thermodynamic considerations, so-called geometric quantum mechanics
Ashtekar and Schilling 1999
;
Bengtsson and Zyczkowski 2017
;
Cariñena et al. 2007
are a uniquely suited paradigm. In standard quantum theory, a quantum state is described by a density operator
\rho
, which can be expanded in many different decompositions of pure states. However, an often overlooked consequence is that, thus, the probabilistic interpretation of quantum states is not unique. To remedy this issue,
geometric quantum states

Ashtekar and Schilling 1999
;
Bengtsson and Zyczkowski 2017
;
Cariñena et al. 2007
were introduced, which are probability distributions on the manifold spanned by the quantum states. In this sense, classical and quantum mechanics only differ in the geometric properties of the underlying manifold.

We proceed by briefly outlining the main notions of geometric quantum mechanics, which is well developed (cf. Refs.
Ashtekar and Schilling 1999
;
Bengtsson and Zyczkowski 2017
;
Cariñena et al. 2007
;
Anza and Crutchfield 2020a
;
Anza and Crutchfield 2020b
;
Anza and Crutchfield 2020c
for a more complete exposition). In the geometric approach, a pure quantum state
\left|\psi\right\rangle
is described as a point in a complex projective space
\mathcal{V}_{d}\equiv\mathbb{C}P^{d-1}

Bengtsson and Zyczkowski 2017
, where
d
is the dimension of the Hilbert space. Note that
d
can also be infinite
Ashtekar and Schilling 1999
. Here,
\bm{z}
is the set of complex homogeneous coordinates in
\mathcal{V}_{d}
, and
\bm{z^{*}}
is the complex conjugate.

Hence, any pure state
\left|\psi\right\rangle
can be written as

\left|\psi(\bm{z})\right\rangle=\sum_{\alpha=0}^{d-1}z_{\alpha}\left|e_{\alpha}\right\rangle\,,

(23)

where
\{e_{\alpha}\}_{\alpha=0}^{d-1}
is an arbitrary basis. The geometry of the manifold is determined by the Fubini–Study metric
Bengtsson and Zyczkowski 2017

ds^{2}=2\sum_{\alpha,\gamma}g_{\alpha\gamma^{*}}dz_{\alpha}dz^{*}_{\gamma}\equiv\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\alpha,\gamma}\partial_{z_{\alpha}}\partial_{z^{*}_{\gamma}}\ln{\left(\bm{z}\cdot\bm{z^{*}}\right)}dz_{\alpha}dz^{*}_{\gamma}\,,

(24)

where we define
g_{\alpha\gamma^{*}}\equiv\frac{1}{4}\partial_{z_{\alpha}}\partial_{z_{\gamma}}\ln(\bm{z}\cdot\bm{z}^{*})
and which allows to define a unique, unitarily invariant volume element,
dV\equiv\sqrt{\det(g)}\,d\bm{z}d\bm{z^{*}}
.

It is easy to recognize that pure states are represented as generalized delta functions on the projective space. In particular, for
\left|\psi_{0}\right\rangle\equiv\left|\psi(\bm{z}_{0})\right\rangle
, the corresponding geometric quantum state becomes

\mathcal{P}(\bm{z})=\widetilde{\delta}(\bm{z}-\bm{z}_{0})\equiv\frac{\delta(\bm{z}-\bm{z}_{0})}{\sqrt{\det(g)}}\,,

(25)

where we introduce the coordinate-covariant Dirac delta. Any (mixed) quantum state can then be written as

\rho=\int_{\mathcal{V}_{d}}dV\,\mathcal{P}(\bm{z})\,\left|\psi(\bm{z})\right\rangle\left\langle\psi(\bm{z})\right|\,,

(26)

where the geometric quantum states are given by

\mathcal{P}(\bm{z})=\sum_{j=1}^{d}p_{j}\,\widetilde{\delta}\left(\bm{z}-\bm{z}_{j}^{p}\right)\,,

(27)

and
p_{j}
is again the eigenvalues of
\rho
and
\bm{z}_{j}^{p}\equiv\bm{z}(\left|p_{j}\right\rangle)
.

We are now equipped to return to the expressions for the quantum and classical ergotropies, Eqs. (
7
) and (
20
), respectively. We immediately recognize that to proceed, we have to consider a generalization of the relative entropy to geometric quantum states. In complete analogy to the classical case, we need to guarantee that the geometric quantum states have the same support
Anza and Crutchfield
. Hence, we introduce a geometric quantum generalization of the conditional distribution to include a generalized transition probability distribution. To this end, consider

\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}(\bm{z})\equiv\sum_{j=1}^{d}p_{j}\,\widetilde{\delta}\left(\bm{z}-\bm{z}_{j}^{s}\right)\,,

(28)

where now
\bm{z}_{j}^{s}\equiv\bm{z}(\left|s_{j}\right\rangle)
, and
\left|s_{j}\right\rangle
is an eigenstate of a density operator
\sigma
. The density operator,
\widetilde{\rho}
, corresponding to
\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}(\bm{z})
reads

\widetilde{\rho}=\widetilde{U}\,\rho\,\widetilde{U}^{\dagger}=\sum_{j}p_{j}\,\left|s_{j}\right\rangle\left\langle s_{j}\right|\,,

(29)

where
\widetilde{U}
is the “optimal” unitary maps.

The
geometric relative entropy
is then defined as

\mathcal{D}\left(\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}||\mathcal{S}\right)\equiv\int_{\mathcal{V}_{d}}dV\,\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}(\bm{z})\ln{\left(\frac{\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}(\bm{z})}{\mathcal{S}(\bm{z})}\right)}\,,

(30)

where
\mathcal{S}
is the geometric quantum state corresponding to
\sigma
(same as before). Moreover, we have by construction
\mathcal{D}\left(\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}||\mathcal{S}\right)=S\left(\widetilde{\rho}||\sigma\right)=D\left(\rho||\sigma\right)
, and we conclude that the geometric relative entropy is identical in value to the classical relative entropy (
5
). Therefore, we can write the quantum ergotropy (
7
) as

\beta\,\mathcal{E}(\rho)=S(\rho||\rho^{\mathrm{eq}})-\mathcal{D}\left(\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}||\mathcal{P}^{\mathrm{eq}}\right)\,,

(31)

where
\mathcal{P}^{\mathrm{eq}}
is the geometric quantum state corresponding to
\rho^{\mathrm{eq}}
. In other words, the quantum ergotropy is the difference of the relative entropies of the density operator and the geometric quantum state with respect to
\rho^{\mathrm{eq}}
.

Remarkably, also the classical case can be fully treated within the geometric approach. To this end, note that for any classical distribution, we can construct the corresponding geometric quantum state. Therefore, it now becomes a fair comparison to consider the difference of quantum and classical ergotropy,
\Delta\mathcal{E}\equiv\mathcal{E}(\rho)-\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{class}}(\rho)
. It is not far-fetched to realize that
\Delta\mathcal{E}
is the genuinely quantum contribution to the extractable work. A more careful analysis of this contribution may be related to quantum correlations (see also Ref.
Touil et al. 2021
), yet a thorough analysis is beyond the scope of the present discussion. Rather, the remainder of this analysis is dedicated to an application of the gained insight to quantum work relations.

VI
Ergotropy from Conditional Thermal States

To this end, imagine a closed system that is driven by the variation of some external control parameter. We denote the initial Hamiltonian by
H_{A}
and the final Hamiltonian by
H_{B}
, and the average work is simply given by
\left\langle W\right\rangle=\left\langle H_{B}\right\rangle-\left\langle H_{A}\right\rangle
. The maximum work theorem predicts that
\left\langle W\right\rangle
is always larger than the work performed for quasistastic
driving
Deffner and Campbell 2019
. If the system was initially prepared in a thermal state, the quasistatic work is nothing but the difference in Helmholtz free energy
\Delta F

Deffner and Lutz 2010
. The difference of total work and free energy difference is called
irreversible work
, and we have
\left\langle W_{\mathrm{irr}}\right\rangle=\left\langle W\right\rangle-\Delta F\geq 0

Deffner and Lutz 2010
. Only rather recently, it was recognized that a sharper inequality can be derived, for both
quantum
Deffner et al. 2016
as well as classical
Sone and Deffner 2021
systems if the quantum work statistics are conditioned on the initial state. Note that this corresponds to the one-time measurement approach, where only one projective measurement is taken at the beginning of the process.

In particular, the following was shown
Deffner et al. 2016
;
Sone and Deffner 2021

\beta\left\langle W_{\text{irr}}\right\rangle\geq S\left(\varrho_{B}||\rho_{B}^{\text{eq}}\right)\,,

(32)

where
\rho_{B}^{\text{eq}}=\exp{\left(-\beta H_{B}\right)}/Z_{B}
, and
\varrho_{B}
is called the conditional thermal state
Sone and Deffner 2021
. It reads
Deffner et al. 2016

\varrho_{B}\equiv\sum_{j}\frac{\exp{\left(-\beta\,h_{B}(j_{A})\right)}}{\mathcal{Z}(B|A)}\,U_{\tau}\left|j_{A}\right\rangle\left\langle j_{A}\right|U_{\tau}^{\dagger}\,,

(33)

where
\left|j_{A}\right\rangle
is an eigenstate of the initial Hamiltonian
H_{A}
. Further,
U_{\tau}
is the unitary evolution operator corresponding to driving the system from
H_{A}
to
H_{B}
, and

h_{B}(j_{A})\equiv\left\langle j_{A}\right|U_{\tau}^{\dagger}H_{B}U_{\tau}\left|j_{A}\right\rangle\,.

(34)

Finally,
\mathcal{Z}(B|A)
is the conditional partition function of
\varrho_{B}
. Since the discovery of
Eq. (
32
)
, the significance of the conditional thermal state has been somewhat obscure. In Ref.
Deffner et al. 2016
;
Sone et al. 2020
, the lower bound in Eq. (
32
) was understood as some contribution to the usable work that would have been destroyed by a second projective measurement. Yet, a transparent interpretation is lacking.

Remarkably, it is not hard to see that
\varrho_{B}
is a representation of the
geometric canonical ensemble
as proposed by Anza and Crutchfield
Anza and Crutchfield 2020a
;
Anza and Crutchfield 2020c
. The geometric canonical ensemble is defined as the geometric state that maximizes the corresponding Shannon entropy under the usual boundary conditions
Jaynes 1957
. Specifically, we have
Anza and Crutchfield 2020a
;
Anza and Crutchfield 2020c

\mathfrak{P}(\bm{z})\equiv\frac{\exp{\left(-\beta\,h(\bm{z})\right)}}{\mathcal{Z}}\,,

(35)

where
h(\bm{z})\equiv\left\langle\psi(\bm{z})\right|H\left|\psi(\bm{z})\right\rangle
and the geometric partition function is

\mathcal{Z}\equiv\int_{\mathcal{V}_{d}}dV\exp{\left(-\beta\,h(\bm{z})\right)}\,.

(36)

Thus, to maintain the consistency of the presentation, we continue to employ the geometric formulation of quantum states.
Now, consider the geometric representation of
\varrho_{B}

\varrho_{B}=\int_{\mathcal{V}_{d}}dV\mathfrak{P}_{B}\,(\bm{z})\,\left|\psi(\bm{z})\right\rangle\left\langle\psi(\bm{z})\right|\

(37)

and we have

\mathfrak{P}_{B}(\bm{z})=\sum_{j}\frac{\exp{\left(-\beta\,h_{B}(\bm{z})\right)}}{\mathcal{Z}(B|A)}\,\widetilde{\delta}\left(\bm{z}-\bm{z}_{j}\right)\,,

(38)

where, as before,
h_{B}(\bm{z})\equiv\left\langle\psi(\bm{z})\right|H_{B}\left|\psi(\bm{z})\right\rangle
and the covariant Dirac delta is evaluated at
\left|\psi(\bm{z}_{j})\right\rangle\equiv U_{\tau}\left|j_{A}\right\rangle
. Comparing Eqs. (
35
) and (
38

The natural question arises as to whether any work can be extracted from the geometric ensemble. To this end, consider the corresponding ergotropy (

\beta\,\mathcal{E}(\varrho_{B})=S\left(\varrho_{B}||\rho_{B}^{\text{eq}}\right)-\mathcal{D}\left(\widetilde{\mathfrak{P}}_{B}||\mathfrak{P}_{B}^{\text{eq}}\right)\,,

\left|j_{B}\right\rangle

H_{B}

In conclusion, realizing that the conditional thermal state (

\varrho_{B}

\rho_{B}^{\text{eq}}

From the fact that the classical and quantum ergotropy share the same geometric relative entropy, we emphasize that thermodynamics based on geometric quantum mechanics is a unified approach to the quantum-to-classical limit.

VII
Conclusions

In conclusion, motivated by the desire to express the maximally extractable work in a form independent of the optimization over unitary operations, we have obtained several results. Given that the quantum ergotropy can be expressed as the difference of the quantum and classical relative entropies, we identified three distinct contributions to the coherent ergotropy, of which the relative entropy of coherence and the population mismatch between thermal state and fully decohered state are the most important. This insight was extended to classical systems, in which inhomogeneities in the energy distribution play the role of quantum coherences. To quantify how much work can be extracted from classical states, we introduced the classical ergotropy, and we postulated that the genuine quantum contribution to the ergotropy is given by the difference of the quantum and classical expressions. Our analysis provides a consistent approach to maximum work extraction in both quantum and
classical systems. In particular, we have not only shown that classical inhomogeneities play the role of “classical coherence”, but also that work can be extracted that is quantified by the classical ergotropy. This was solidified by exploiting the geometric approach to quantum mechanics, in which quantum and classical states can be treated in a unified framework. As an application, we demonstrated that the recently introduced notion of “conditional thermal state” actually belongs to the family of geometric canonical ensembles and that, hence, the corresponding sharpened maximum work theorem becomes easy to interpret. This demonstrates that understanding quantum as well as classical ergotropies is an essential pillar of modern thermodynamics with a myriad of potential applications. Finally, these results demonstrate that the geometric approach can be regarded as a methodology of unifying the quantum and classical approaches to the second law of thermodynamics.

In this section, let us explain why the divergence-like quantity introduced in

i

Consider the relative entropy of the probability distribution

p_{B|A}(\Gamma^{\prime},\Gamma)=p(\Gamma^{\prime}|\Gamma)p_{A}(\Gamma)

(52)

with respect to a certain probability distribution
r_{B}(\Gamma^{\prime})
. Let us write

p_{B}(\Gamma^{\prime})\equiv\int d\Gamma p_{B|A}(\Gamma^{\prime},\Gamma)=\int d\Gamma p(\Gamma^{\prime}|\Gamma)p_{A}(\Gamma)\,.

(53)

Then, we have

\begin{split}D\left(p_{B|A}||r_{B}\right)&=\int d\Gamma^{\prime}d\Gamma p_{B|A}(\Gamma^{\prime},\Gamma)\ln{\left(\frac{p_{B|A}(\Gamma^{\prime},\Gamma)}{r_{B}(\Gamma^{\prime})}\right)}\\
&=\int d\Gamma^{\prime}d\Gamma p_{B|A}(\Gamma^{\prime},\Gamma)\ln{\left(p_{B|A}(\Gamma^{\prime},\Gamma)\right)}-\int d\Gamma^{\prime}p_{B}(\Gamma^{\prime})\ln{\left(r_{B}(\Gamma^{\prime})\right)}\,.\end{split}

(54)

Because of the vanishing conditional entropy due to the Liouville’s equation,

\mathcal{H}(B|A)\equiv-\int d\Gamma^{\prime}d\Gamma p_{B|A}(\Gamma^{\prime},\Gamma)\ln{\left(p(\Gamma^{\prime}|\Gamma)\right)}=0\,,

(55)

and the normalization of the conditional probability distribution in Eq. (

\int d\Gamma^{\prime}d\Gamma p_{B|A}(\Gamma^{\prime},\Gamma)\ln{\left(p_{B|A}(\Gamma^{\prime},\Gamma)\right)}=\int d\Gamma p_{A}(\Gamma)\ln{\left(p_{A}(\Gamma)\right)}\,.

(56)

Therefore, we have

D\left(p_{B|A}||r_{B}\right)=\int d\Gamma p_{A}(\Gamma)\ln{\left(p_{A}(\Gamma)\right)}-\int d\Gamma^{\prime}p_{B}(\Gamma^{\prime})\ln{\left(r_{B}(\Gamma^{\prime})\right)}\,.

(57)

(58)

Hence, we have

D\left(p_{B|A}||r_{B}\right)\geq\int d\Gamma^{\prime}~p_{B}(\Gamma^{\prime})\ln{\left(\frac{p_{B}(\Gamma^{\prime})}{r_{B}(\Gamma^{\prime})}\right)}=D(p_{B}||r_{B})\geq 0\,.

(59)

Therefore, the divergence-like quantity in Eq. (

D\left(p_{B|A}||r_{B}\right)\geq 0\,.
