Noise propagation in hybrid tensor networks
Hiroyuki Harada, Yasunari Suzuki, Bo Yang, Yuuki Tokunaga, Suguru Endo

Abstract
The hybrid tensor network (HTN) method is a general framework allowing for the construction of an effective wavefunction with the combination of classical tensors and quantum tensors, i.e., amplitudes of quantum states. In particular, hybrid tree tensor networks (HTTNs) are very useful for simulating larger systems beyond the available size of the quantum hardware. However, while the realistic quantum states in NISQ hardware are highly likely to be noisy, this framework is formulated for pure states. In this work, as well as discussing the relevant methods, i.e., Deep VQE and entanglement forging under the framework of HTTNs, we investigate the noisy HTN states by introducing the expansion operator for providing the description of the expansion of the size of simulated quantum systems and the noise propagation. This framework enables the general tree HTN states to be explicitly represented and their physicality to be discussed. We also show that the expectation value of a measured observable exponentially vanishes with the number of contracted quantum tensors. Our work will lead to providing the noise-resilient construction of HTN states.



I
introduction

Noisy intermediate-scale quantum (NISQ) computers are expected to have many applications ranging from quantum computational chemistry
McArdle et al. 2020
;
Cao et al. 2019
to machine learning
Romero et al. 2017
;
Mitarai et al. 2018
and even quantum sensing
Kaubruegger et al. 2019
;
Koczor et al. 2020
. However, despite the potential significant advantages of NISQ devices
Cerezo et al. 2021
;
Endo et al. 2021
;
Tilly et al. 2022
, many obstacles need to be overcome for practical applications.
One of the significant problems is the scalability of quantum computers: the largest number of integrated qubits is restricted to the order of
10^{2}

Arute et al. 2019
;
Kim et al. 2023
, and we need technical leaps to scale quantum computers beyond.

Considering the current situation of scalability of NISQ computers, a couple of methods are being developed to effectively enlarge the simulated quantum systems, e.g., entangling forging
Eddins et al. 2022
;
Huembeli et al. 2022
;
Castellanos et al. 2023
, Deep variational quantum eigensolver (DeepVQE)
Fujii et al. 2022
;
Mizuta et al. 2021
;
Erhart et al. 2022
, and hybrid tensor networks (HTN)
Yuan et al. 2021
;
Kanno et al. 2021
;
Kanno et al. 2023
. Additional operations on quantum computers and classical post-processing of measurement outcomes allow us to virtually emulate the effect of quantum entanglement between subsystems when we compute expectation values of observables. Since many NISQ algorithms rely on the measurement of observables, for example, the variational quantum eigensolver (VQE)
Peruzzo et al. 2014
;
McArdle et al. 2020
;
Cao et al. 2019
, optimizes the energy of molecules, i.e., the expectation values of the Hamiltonian, these techniques are quite useful in NISQ applications.

In particular, the HTN framework is a quite general framework that comprehensively discusses the hybridization of quantum tensors and classical tensors
Yuan et al. 2021
. While classical tensors are the same as conventional tensors, quantum tensors refer to amplitudes of quantum states, which have two types of indices: classical index and quantum index. Suppose an example of quantum tensor
\ket{\psi^{i}}=\sum_{i_{1},i_{2},...,i_{k}}\psi_{i_{1},i_{2},...i_{k}}^{i}\ket{i_{1},i_{2},...i_{k}}
, where
\ket{i_{k}}
(
~i_{k}\in\{0,1\}~\forall k
) indicates qubit states; the upper index
i
indicate the classical tensors contracted in a similar vein to a conventional classical tensor with lower indices being quantum indices, which is contracted through quantum measurements because it is relevant to qubits. It has also been pointed out that hybrid tree tensor networks (HTTNs) are significantly useful for simulating larger quantum systems.

While the above frameworks for simulating larger quantum systems are appealing for small-scale NISQ devices, another massive problem in NISQ devices is inevitable noise effects due to unwanted system interactions with the environment
Endo et al. 2021
;
Cerezo et al. 2021
. We remark that those methods for expanding the simulated quantum systems have been analyzed only for pure states under the assumption that noise effects are negligible
Yuan et al. 2021
;
Fujii et al. 2022
;
Eddins et al. 2022
.
However, such a strong assumption does not reflect the reality of the current situation in which NISQ devices suffer from a significant amount of computation errors.

In the present paper, we try to bridge the gap between the theoretical framework and the actual experiments. To do so, we first show that Deep VQE and entangling forging can be discussed with the language of HTN, especially HTTNs. Next, we introduce the expansion operator to consistently describe the propagation of noise and expansion of the simulated quantum system in HTN states.
Note that we detail the form of the expansion operator depending on the type of tensor contractions. This argument allows us to represent the explicit form of general multiple-layer tree HTN states and discuss their physicality, i.e., whether the noisy HTN states
\tilde{\rho}_{\rm HT}
satisfies the positivity
\tilde{\rho}_{\rm HT}\geq 0
. In addition, we reveal that the computation results from noisy tree tensor states become exponentially small with the number of contracted quantum tensors, which indicates that adjustments of the number of quantum tensors and classical tensors are required in the realistic experimental setup.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. In Sec.
II
, we review the framework of HTN, highlighting the importance of tree tensor networks and classifying the contraction rules of quantum tensors. In Sec.
III
, we reformulate the Deep VQE and entanglement-forging methods under the framework of HTTNs. In Sec.
IV
, we study the explicit form of the expansion operator in accordance with the contraction rules and discuss the physicality of the HTN states. In Sec.
V
, we discuss the exponential decay of the observable with the number of quantum tensors. Finally, we conclude this paper with discussions and conclusions.

II
Hybrid Tensor Networks

Classical tensor network (TN) methods
White 1992
;
White 1993
;
Schollwöck 2011
;
Shi et al. 2006
;
Vidal 2007
try to efficiently describe quantum states of interest with a much smaller subset of the entire Hilbert space on the basis of physical observation. For example, the matrix product state (MPS)
Schollwöck 2011
ansatz represents the state as

\ket{\psi_{\rm MPS}}=\sum_{j_{1},j_{2},...,j_{M}}\mathrm{Tr}[A_{1}^{j_{1}}A_{2}^{j_{2}}...A_{M}^{j_{M}}]\ket{j_{1},j_{2},...,j_{M}},

(1)

where
j_{k}
takes
0
or
1
in the case of qubit systems, and
A_{1}
and
A_{M}
are rank-2 tensors with
A_{k}
being rank-3 tensors. This ansatz construction allows us to compress the number of parameters representing the state to
O(M\kappa^{2})
from
O(2^{M})
for the bond dimension
\kappa
. While MPS representations may capture weakly and locally entangled quantum systems, they probably fail to simulate strongly entangled quantum dynamics.

While an efficient ansatz representation is necessary for classical simulations of quantum systems, quantum computers allow for an efficient generation of rank-
n
tensors, which motivated the introduction of quantum tensors
Yuan et al. 2021
. Quantum tensors are generally represented as
\psi_{i_{1},i_{2},...,i_{N}}^{j_{1},j_{2},...,j_{M}}
, where the lower indices
(i_{1},i_{2},...,i_{N})
are quantum indices and the upper indices
(j_{1},j_{2},...,j_{M})
are classical indices. Quantum tensors are generated from quantum devices; then, the corresponding quantum state is:

\ket{\psi^{j_{1},j_{2},...,j_{M}}}=\sum_{i_{1},i_{2},...,i_{N}}\psi_{i_{1},i_{2},...,i_{N}}^{j_{1},j_{2},...,j_{M}}\ket{i_{1},i_{2},...,i_{N}}.

(2)

The quantum indices are necessarily related to the “physical” quantum devices. For
i_{k}\in\{0,1\}~\forall k
,
\ket{i_{1},i_{2},...,i_{N}}
is a computational basis state in qubit-based quantum computers. Tensor contractions of quantum indices are performed through the measurement of quantum states.

As a simple example of the HTN formalism, let us consider a tensor
\tilde{\psi}_{i_{1},i_{2},...,i_{N}}=\sum_{j}\alpha^{j}\psi^{j}_{i_{1},i_{2},...,i_{N}}
for a rank-1 classical tensor
\alpha^{j}
. This hybrid tensor corresponds to a quantum state
\ket{\tilde{\psi}}=\sum_{j}\alpha^{j}\ket{\psi^{j}}
with
\ket{\psi^{j}}=\sum_{i_{1},i_{2},...,i_{N}}\psi^{j}_{i_{1},i_{2},...,i_{N}}\ket{i_{1},i_{2},...,i_{N}}
. A linear combination of quantum states with a classical coefficient is a generalization of quantum subspace expansion, which can strengthen the representability of quantum states
McClean et al. 2017
;
Takeshita et al. 2020
;
Yoshioka et al. 2022a
and accordingly works as a quantum error mitigation method
McClean et al. 2020
;
Yoshioka et al. 2022b
. To compute an expectation value of an observable
O
for the state
\ket{\tilde{\psi}}
, we have
\braket{O}_{\tilde{\psi}}=\sum_{ij}\alpha^{i*}\alpha^{j}M^{i,j}
, where
M^{i,j}=\bra{\psi^{i}}O\ket{\psi^{j}}
is evaluated on a quantum computer. For example, when quantum tensors can be represented as
\ket{\psi^{j}}=P_{j}\ket{\psi_{0}}
with
P_{j}
being a Pauli operator, we obtain
M^{i,j}=\bra{\psi_{0}}P_{i}OP_{j}\ket{\psi_{0}}
. When we linearly decompose the observable as
O=\sum_{k}f_{k}P_{k}
,
M^{i,j}
can be evaluated only from measurements of Pauli operators. As another example, when quantum tensors are described as
\ket{\psi^{(j)}}=U^{j}\ket{\psi_{0}}
for a unitary operator
U^{(j)}
, we obtain
M^{i,j}=\bra{\psi_{0}}U^{(i){\dagger}}OU^{(j)}\ket{\psi_{0}}=\sum_{k}f_{k}\bra{\psi_{0}}U^{(i){\dagger}}P_{k}U^{(j)}\ket{\psi_{0}}
. Note that each term
\bra{\psi_{0}}U^{(i){\dagger}}P_{k}U^{(j)}\ket{\psi_{0}}
can be computed with a Hadamard test circuit.

Here, we review hybrid tree tensor networks (HTTNs) as an important class of HTNs. The
2
-layer tree TN state consisting of quantum tensors can be expressed by

\ket{\psi_{\rm HT}}=\frac{1}{C}\sum_{i_{1},...,i_{N}}\psi_{i_{1},...,i_{N}}\ket{\psi^{i_{1}}_{1}}\otimes...\otimes\ket{\psi^{i_{N}}_{N}}.

(3)

Let
\ket{\psi^{i_{k}}_{k}}(k=1,...,N)
be a
K
-qubit quantum tensor,
\psi_{i_{1},...,i_{N}}=\braket{i_{1},...,i_{2}|\psi}
be the probability ampulitude of a
N
-qubit system, and
C
be a normalization constant,
\ket{\psi_{\rm HT}}
is an
NK
-qubit system. The expectation values of observables for
\ket{\psi_{\rm HT}}
can be computed only with a
\mathrm{max}\{N,K\}
-qubit system. Denote an
NK
-qubit observable as
O=\bigotimes_{k=1}^{N}O_{k}
for a
K
-qubit observable
O_{k}
. Then, we obtain the expectation value including the normalization constant as follows:

\displaystyle\braket{O}_{\psi_{\rm HT}}

\displaystyle={\frac{1}{C^{2}}}\sum_{\vec{i},\vec{i}^{\prime}}\psi_{\vec{i}}^{*}\psi_{\vec{i}^{\prime}}\prod_{k=1}^{N}M_{k}^{i_{k},i_{k}^{\prime}}

(4)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{C^{2}}\bra{\psi}\bigotimes_{k=1}^{N}M_{k}\ket{\psi}

and

\displaystyle C^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\vec{i},\vec{i}^{\prime}}\psi_{\vec{i}}^{*}\psi_{\vec{i}^{\prime}}\prod_{k=1}^{N}S_{k}^{i_{k},i_{k}^{\prime}}

(5)

\displaystyle=\bra{\psi}\bigotimes_{k=1}^{N}S_{k}\ket{\psi},

where
\vec{i}=(i_{1},i_{2},...i_{N})
,
\vec{i}^{\prime}=(i_{1}^{\prime},i_{2}^{\prime},...i_{N}^{\prime})
,
M_{k}^{i_{k},i_{k}^{\prime}}=\bra{\psi_{k}^{i_{k}}}O_{k}\ket{\psi_{k}^{i_{k}^{\prime}}}
and
S_{k}^{i_{k},i_{k}^{\prime}}=\braket{\psi_{k}^{i_{k}}|\psi_{k}^{i_{k}^{\prime}}}
. Therefore,
\braket{O}_{\psi_{\rm HT}}
can be evaluated as follows. First, we calculate
\{M_{k}\}_{k=1}^{N}
(
\{S_{k}\}_{k=1}^{N}
) via contractions of local quantum tensors
\{\ket{\psi^{i_{k}}}_{k}\}_{k}
. Then, because
\{M_{k}\}_{k}
(
\{S_{k}\}_{k}
) are Hermitian operators, we have the spectral decomposition
M_{k}=U_{k}^{\dagger}\Lambda_{k}U_{k}
(
S_{k}=V_{k}^{\dagger}\Lambda^{\prime}_{k}V_{k}
) for a unitary operator
U_{k}
(
V_{k}
) and a diagonal operator
\Lambda_{k}
(
\Lambda^{\prime}_{k}
). Thus, by applying
\otimes_{k=1}^{N}U_{k}
immediately before measurement and measuring on the
Z
basis and assigning the elements of
\Lambda_{k}
(
\Lambda^{\prime}_{k}
) to the measurement outcomes, we can measure the expectation value of
\bigotimes_{k=1}^{N}M_{k}
(
\bigotimes_{k=1}^{N}S_{k}
) for the state
\ket{\psi}
. A graphical diagram for the procedure of contractions is shown in Fig.
1
. For the contraction rule for the transition amplitudes
\bra{\psi^{\prime}_{\rm HT}}O\ket{\psi_{\rm HT}}
for different hybrid tensors
\ket{\psi_{\rm HT}}
and
\ket{\psi^{\prime}_{\rm HT}}
, refer to
Kanno et al. 2021
. While we considered the HTN state comprising only quantum tensors above, note that we can also consider the HTN model where quantum tensors and classical tensors are used interchangeably
Yuan et al. 2021
.

The efficiency of calculating each
M_{k}(S_{k})
is highly dependent on the way of preparing a set of quantum states
\{\ket{\psi_{k}^{i_{k}}}\}_{i_{k}}
. To see the detailed procedures for computing
M_{k}(S_{k})
, we consider the more general scenario where one contracts the
K
-qubit local tensor
\psi_{k}^{\vec{i}_{k}}
, which is connected to the index
\vec{i}_{k}\in\{0,1\}^{\tau}
of its parent tensor, and the local observable
O_{k}
to obtain
M_{k}^{\vec{i}_{k},\vec{i}^{\prime}_{k}}=\braket{\psi_{k}^{\vec{i}_{k}}|O_{k}|\psi_{k}^{\vec{i}^{\prime}_{k}}}
. When
\psi^{\vec{i}_{k}}
is a classical tensor (i.e., the set of states
\{\ket{\psi_{k}^{\vec{i}_{k}}}\}_{\vec{i}_{k}}
is prepared by classical computers), we calculate each element of
M_{k}(S_{k})
on classical computers. For example, if we use the MPS representation to express the local tensor as
\ket{\psi_{k}^{\vec{i}_{k}}}=\sum_{j_{1},j_{2},...,j_{K}}\mathrm{Tr}[A_{1}^{\vec{i}_{k},j_{1}}A_{2}^{j_{2}}...A_{K}^{j_{K}}]\ket{j_{1},j_{2},...,j_{K}}
, we obtain matrix elements
M_{k}^{\vec{i}_{k},\vec{i}^{\prime}_{k}}=\braket{\psi_{k}^{\vec{i}_{k}}|O_{k}|\psi_{k}^{\vec{i}^{\prime}_{k}}}
by a classical contraction of the MPS state. Meanwhile, when
\psi^{\vec{i}_{k}}
is a quantum tensor (i.e., the set of states
\{\ket{\psi_{k}^{\vec{i}_{k}}}\}_{\vec{i}_{k}}
is prepared by quantum computers), we calculate
M_{k}(S_{k})
by performing measurements on a quantum computer. Since the specific calculation varies depending on the definition of
\{\ket{\psi_{k}^{\vec{i}_{k}}}\}_{\vec{i}_{k}}
, we explain the computation procedures to obtain
M_{k}(S_{k})
for the following four cases, where the index
\vec{i}_{k}
is associated with

(i) initial states

\ket{\psi_{k}^{\vec{i}_{k}}}=U_{k}\ket{\vec{i}_{k}}\ket{\vec{0}}

(ii) projections

\ket{\psi_{k}^{\vec{i}_{k}}}=\braket{\vec{i}_{k}|\psi_{k}}
,

(iii) Pauli operators

\ket{\psi_{k}^{\vec{i}_{k}}}=P_{k}^{\vec{i}_{k}}\ket{\psi_{k}}
,

(iv) unitary gates

\ket{\psi_{k}^{\vec{i}_{k}}}=U_{k}^{\vec{i}_{k}}\ket{\vec{0}}
.

Note that although certain types can be represented in the framework of another type from a mathematical perspective (e.g., type (i) and (iii) can be regarded as special examples of type (iv)), we distinguish each type for the practical reason that different characterizations of the state
\ket{\psi^{\vec{i}_{k}}_{k}}
by
\vec{i}_{k}
require the different quantum circuits and the procedure to calculate the
M_{k}(S_{k})
.

Let us consider

-qubit quantum state composed of
N

n_{\rm tot}=\sum_{s=1}^{N}n_{s}
. Note that we are focusing on the following situation: the whole system can be divided into the
N
clusters with strong internal interactions but weak interaction between them. Then, the Hamiltonian can be written as

H=\sum_{s=1}^{N}H_{s}+\sum_{t,u=1}^{N}V_{tu},\quad V_{tu}=\sum_{\alpha}c_{tu}^{\alpha}W^{\alpha}_{t}\otimes W^{\alpha}_{u}

(12)

where
H_{s}
corresponds to the
s
-th subsystem’s Hamiltonian and
V_{tu}
to the inter-subsystem Hamiltonian acting on the
s
-th and
t
-th subsystems. Here, the interaction term
V_{tu}
is decomposed into the linear sum of the tensor products of
W^{\alpha}_{t}
and
W^{\alpha}_{u}
acting on the
t
-th and the
u
-th subsystems with the coefficients
c_{tu}^{\alpha}
. The Deep VQE algorithm is roughly comprised of four steps, each of which is described as follows.

The first step of this algorithm is to find the ground state of each subsystem’s Hamiltonian
H_{s}
by the conventional VQE approach, minimizing the expectation value of
H_{s}
by optimizing the parameters
\vec{\theta}^{(s)}
in each quantum state
\ket{\psi_{s}}=U^{(s)}(\vec{\theta}^{(s)})\ket{0^{n_{s}}}
. Hereafter, let
\ket{\psi_{s}^{\mathrm{G}}}=U^{(s)}(\vec{\theta}^{(s)\star})\ket{0^{n_{s}}}
be the approximated ground state, where
\vec{\theta}^{(s)\star}
is an optimized parameter set of the subsystem
s
.

In the second step, we generate the
K_{s}
-dimensional orthonormal local basis
\{\ket{\widehat{\psi}_{s}^{m}}\}_{m=0}^{K_{s}-1}
on the basis of the ground state
\ket{\psi_{s}^{\rm G}}
. First, we construct non-orthonormal local basis
\{\ket{{\psi}_{s}^{m}}\}_{m=0}^{K_{s}-1}
as

\ket{\psi_{s}^{m}}=D_{s}^{m}\ket{\psi_{s}^{\mathrm{G}}},

(13)

where
D_{s}^{m}
denotes the
2^{n_{s}}\times 2^{n_{s}}
local excitation operator on the
s
-th subsystem, and
D_{s}^{0}
is an identity operator.
Fujii et al. 2022
pointed out that when the number of dimensions
K_{s}
is fixed, one of the proper choices of
D_{s}^{0}
is assigning the local excitation
D_{s}^{m}
to
W_{s}^{m}
at the boundary
1
1

1

The appropriate choice of a basis set is highly important because the selection determine the fundamental limits of accuracy that an ansatz can perform and the number of qubits
\kappa_{s}
required for the implementation
Erhart et al. 2022
. Accordingly, several methods to prepare the basis sets have recently been proposed
Mizuta et al. 2021
;
Erhart et al. 2022
.
. In this review part, following the original research
Fujii et al. 2022
, we choose
D_{s}^{m}
as
W_{s}^{m}
. Then, we define the orthonormal local basis
\{\ket{\widehat{\psi}_{s}^{m}}\}_{m=0}^{K_{s}-1}
using the Gram-Schmidt procedure for
\{\ket{{\psi}_{s}^{m}}\}_{m=0}^{K_{s}-1}
:

\ket{\widehat{\psi}_{s}^{m}}=\frac{\ket{{\psi}_{s}^{m}}-\sum_{l<m}\braket{\widehat{\psi}_{s}^{l}|{\psi}_{s}^{m}}\ket{\widehat{\psi}_{s}^{l}}}{\|\ket{{\psi}_{s}^{m}}-\sum_{l<m}\braket{\widehat{\psi}_{s}^{l}|{\psi}_{s}^{m}}\ket{\widehat{\psi}_{s}^{l}}\|}.

(14)

From Eq. (
14
), the matrix
P_{s}
, which transforms the basis
\{\ket{{\psi}_{s}^{m}}\}_{m=0}^{K_{s}-1}
to the orthonormalized basis
\{\ket{\widehat{\psi}_{s}^{m}}\}_{m=0}^{K_{s}-1}
, can be determined as

\ket{\widehat{\psi}_{s}^{m}}=\sum_{m^{\prime}=0}^{K_{s}-1}P^{mm^{\prime}}_{s}\ket{\psi_{s}^{m^{\prime}}}.

(15)

Each matrix element
P^{mm^{\prime}}_{s}
can be calculated, by exploiting a set of inner products of local basis
\{\braket{\psi_{s}^{l}|\psi_{s}^{m}}\}_{l,m}
. Here, each inner product
\braket{\psi_{s}^{l}|\psi_{s}^{m}}
can be calculated with direct measurement because it can be expressed as

\braket{\psi_{s}^{\mathrm{G}}|{W_{s}^{l}}^{\dagger}W_{s}^{m}|\psi_{s}^{\mathrm{G}}}

(16)

and the operator
{W_{s}^{l}}^{\dagger}W_{s}^{m}
can be decomposed into a linear combination of Hermitian operators. The matrix
P_{s}
formulated above is needed to normalize the effective Hamiltonian in the next step.

In the third step, we construct the normalized effective Hamiltonian on the basis of the orthonormal basis
\{\ket{\widehat{\psi}_{s}^{m}}\}_{m=0}^{K_{s}-1}
. The normalized effective Hamiltonian
H_{s}^{\mathrm{eff}}
for the
s
-th subsystem is defined as

(\widehat{H}_{s}^{\mathrm{eff}})^{lm}:=\braket{\widehat{\psi}^{l}_{s}|H_{s}|\widehat{\psi}^{m}_{s}}.

(17)

Note that terms that can be directly measured on quantum computers are un-normalized ones
(H_{s}^{\mathrm{eff}})^{lm}:=\braket{\psi^{l}_{s}|H_{s}|\psi^{m}_{s}}
. Thus, we first evaluate

(H_{s}^{\mathrm{eff}})^{lm}=\braket{\psi_{s}^{\mathrm{G}}|{W_{s}^{l}}^{\dagger}H_{s}W_{s}^{m}|\psi_{s}^{\mathrm{G}}},

(18)

by decomposing the operator
{W_{s}^{l}}^{\dagger}H_{s}W_{s}^{m}
into a linear combination Hermitian operator and performing the direct measurement. Then, we transform
(H_{s}^{\rm eff})^{lm}
into the normalized effective Hamiltonian
(\widehat{H}_{s}^{\mathrm{eff}})^{lm}
, by applying the matrix
P_{s}
to
(H_{s}^{\mathrm{eff}})^{lm}
as

\begin{split}(\widehat{H}_{s}^{\mathrm{eff}})^{lm}&=\sum_{l^{\prime}=0}^{K_{s}-1}\sum_{m^{\prime}=0}^{K_{s}-1}{P^{ll^{\prime}}_{s}}^{\ast}\braket{\psi^{l^{\prime}}_{s}|H_{s}|\psi^{m^{\prime}}_{s}}P^{mm^{\prime}}_{s}\\
&=\left({P_{s}}^{\ast}H_{s}^{\mathrm{eff}}{P_{s}}^{\rm T}\right)^{lm},\end{split}

(19)

where
\ast
and
\mathrm{T}
denote the complex conjugate and transposition of matrices. Similarly, the normalized effective interaction Hamiltonian
\widehat{V}_{tu}^{\mathrm{eff}}
is defined as

(\widehat{V}_{tu}^{\mathrm{eff}})^{abcd}:=\bra{\widehat{\psi}^{a}_{t}}\bra{\widehat{\psi}^{b}_{u}}V_{tu}\ket{\widehat{\psi}^{c}_{t}}\ket{\widehat{\psi}^{d}_{u}}.

(20)

Since
V_{tu}
can be decomposed into the tensor products of the local Hamiltonian acting on the
t
-th and
u
-th subsystems as Eq. (
12
), un-normalized effective interaction Hamiltonian
(V^{\mathrm{eff}}_{tu})^{abcd}:=\bra{\psi^{a}_{t}}\bra{\psi^{b}_{u}}V_{tu}\ket{\psi^{c}_{t}}\ket{\psi^{d}_{u}}
can be derived from integrating the expectation value of each subsystem. Then, applying the matrices
P_{t}
and
P_{u}
to
(V^{\mathrm{eff}}_{tu})_{abcd}
, we obtain the normalized effective interaction Hamiltonian as

(\widehat{V}_{tu}^{\mathrm{eff}})^{abcd}=\sum_{\alpha}c_{tu}^{\alpha}(\widehat{W}_{t}^{\alpha^{\mathrm{eff}}})^{ac}(\widehat{W}_{u}^{\alpha^{\mathrm{eff}}})^{bd}.

(21)

The normalized effective Hamiltonian
(\widehat{W}_{t}^{\alpha^{\mathrm{eff}}})^{ac}
and
(\widehat{W}_{u}^{\alpha^{\mathrm{eff}}})^{bd}
can be calculated in the same way as Eq. (
19
). After calculating
(\widehat{H}_{s}^{\mathrm{eff}})^{lm}
and
(\widehat{V}_{tu}^{\mathrm{eff}})^{abcd}
, we can convert the original Hamiltonian
H
into the normalized effective one as

\widehat{H}^{\mathrm{eff}}=\sum_{s=1}^{N}\widehat{H}_{s}^{\mathrm{eff}}+\sum_{t,u=1}^{N}\widehat{V}_{tu}^{\mathrm{eff}}.

(22)

In the fourth step, we estimate the ground state for the effective Hamiltonian
\widehat{H}^{\mathrm{eff}}
by the typical VQE with the
\kappa
-qubit parameterized quantum circuit
V(\vec{\,\eta})
in which
\kappa:=\sum_{s=1}^{N}\kappa_{s}
with
\kappa_{s}:=\lceil\log_{2}K_{s}\rceil
. The expectation value of the effective Hamiltonian
\widehat{H}^{\mathrm{eff}}
for the approximated ground state
V(\vec{\,\eta}^{\star})\ket{0^{\kappa}}
approximates the ground state energy
E_{\rm min}
for the original Hamiltonian
H
:

E_{\rm min}\simeq\braket{0^{\kappa}|V(\vec{\,\eta}^{\star})^{\dagger}\widehat{H}^{\mathrm{eff}}V(\vec{\,\eta}^{\star})|0^{\kappa}}.

(23)

One of the notable points of this technique is that it only takes
\max(n_{s},\kappa)
qubits to simulate a
n_{\rm tot}
-qubit quantum system. Furthermore, by repeating the series of steps, we can simulate even larger-scale systems.

Having viewed a series of procedures of the Deep VQE following the description in
Fujii et al. 2022

\ket{\psi_{\rm DV}}=\sum_{\vec{i}_{1},...,\vec{i}_{N}}\phi_{\vec{i}_{1},...,\vec{i}_{N}}\ket{\widehat{\psi}_{1}^{\vec{i}_{1}}}\otimes...\otimes\ket{\widehat{\psi}_{N}^{\vec{i}_{N}}},

(24)

where

is the
\kappa
-qubit quantum tensor representing the correlations between subsystems with the quantum indices

(

is the
s

24

(25)

where

are the

\ket{\psi_{\rm DV}}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\vec{i}_{1},...,\vec{i}_{N}\\

(26)

III.2
Entanglement forging

In this subsection, we review
entanglement forging
proposed by
Eddins et al. 2022
, which allows the size of the simulatable quantum system on an available quantum processor to be doubled with the help of classical processing.

Let us consider the state of a bipartite
2N
-qubit system
\ket{\psi_{\rm EF}}
, and decompose it into
N
-qubit subsystems using Schmidt decomposition, i.e.,

\ket{\psi_{\rm EF}}=(V_{1}\otimes V_{2})\sum_{x=0}^{2^{N}-1}\lambda_{x}\ket{b_{x}}\otimes\ket{b_{x}},

(29)

where
V_{1}
and
V_{2}
are the
N
-qubit unitary operators acting on the 1st and 2nd subsystems respectively,
\lambda_{x}
are the Schmidt coefficients, and
\ket{b_{x}}\in\{\ket{0},\ket{1}\}^{\otimes N}
are the computational basis corresponding to indices
x
. Note that this hybrid ansatz structure could vary depending on how one truncates the Schmidt decomposition
\lambda_{x}
, similar to the MPS ansatz construction. Importantly, by choosing only the leading
k(<2^{n})
bitstrings
\ket{b_{x}}
that is sufficient for capturing the physical property of a target system, rather than considering all possible bitstrings, we can mitigate sampling overheads and the technical difficulties to execute a number of the different structure of quantum circuits in the real quantum devices.

Figure 4:
The HTN representations of entanglement forging. (a) The graphical representation of a
2N
-qubit bipartite quantum system
\ket{\psi_{\rm EF}}

U

V_{1}

V_{2}
. (b) Applying Schmidt decomposition to the
2N

U\ket{0}^{\otimes 2N}

U_{1}

\{\ket{b_{x}}_{1}\}_{x}

\{\ket{b_{x}}_{2}\}_{x}

). (c) The graphical diagram of the calculation of the expectation value of the observable
O=O_{1}\otimes O_{2}
for the state
\ket{\psi_{\rm EF}}
. Sampling
M_{1}^{\vec{i},\vec{i^{\prime}}}M_{2}^{\vec{i},\vec{i^{\prime}}}
(green area) in proportion to the Schmidt coefficients
\lambda^{\vec{i}*}\lambda^{\vec{i^{\prime}}}
, we can efficiently contract the whole system.

Here, our purpose is to calculate the expectation value of the
2N
-qubit observable
O=O_{1}\otimes O_{2}
for the state
\ket{\psi_{\rm EF}}
, that is,

\begin{split}\braket{O}_{\psi_{\rm EF}}&=\sum_{x=0}^{2^{N}-1}\lambda_{x}^{2}\braket{b_{x}|\widehat{O}_{1}|b_{x}}\braket{b_{x}|\widehat{O}_{2}|b_{x}}+\sum_{x=0}^{2^{N}-1}\sum_{y<x}\Bigl(\lambda_{x}\lambda_{y}\\
&\quad\times\sum_{p\in{\mathbb{Z}_{4}}}(-1)^{p}\braket{\phi^{p}_{b_{x}b_{y}}|\widehat{O}_{1}|\phi^{p}_{b_{x}b_{y}}}\braket{\phi^{p}_{b_{x}b_{y}}|\widehat{O}_{2}|\phi^{p}_{b_{x}b_{y}}}\Bigr),\end{split}

(30)

where
\widehat{O}_{1}=V_{1}^{\dagger}O_{1}V_{1}
,
\widehat{O}_{2}=V_{2}^{\dagger}O_{2}V_{2}
, and
\ket{\phi^{p}_{b_{x}b_{y}}}=(\ket{b_{x}}+i^{p}\ket{b_{y}})/\sqrt{2}
with
p\in\{0,1,2,3\}=\mathbb{Z}_{4}
. Note that Eq. (
30
) indicates that the expectation value
\braket{O}_{\psi_{\rm EF}}
can be estimated by linearly combing the calculation results in each subsystem, all of which can be evaluated on only
N
-qubit quantum processors.

In the actual implementation, one may estimate
\braket{O}_{\psi_{\rm EF}}
using the weighted sampling strategy. To adopt this approach, it is convenient to rewrite Eq. (
30
) as follows:

\braket{O}_{\psi_{\rm EF}}=\|\mu\|_{1}\sum_{a}p_{\mu_{a}}\mathrm{sgn}(\mu_{a})\cdot\mathrm{Tr}(O_{1}\rho_{a,1})\mathrm{Tr}(O_{2}\rho_{a,2}),

(31)

where
\mu_{a}
are real coefficients,
\|\mu\|_{1}=\sum_{a}|\mu_{a}|
is a 1-norm of the coefficients
\mu_{a}
,
p_{a}=|\mu_{a}|/\|\mu\|_{1}
are probabilities, and
\rho_{a,s}
are the
N
-qubit states on the
s
-th subsystem, represented as
V_{s}\ket{b_{x}}
or
V_{s}\ket{\phi_{b_{x}b_{y}}^{p}}
. The basic procedure of this sampling strategy is as follows. For each shot, we randomly prepare the states
\rho_{a,s}
on each subsystem with probability
p_{a}
, measure each qubit related to the observable
O_{s}
(
s=1,2
), and store the product of measurement outcomes of the 1st, and 2nd subsystems and
\mathrm{sgn}(\mu_{a})
. By repeating the above process for
N
shots and taking the total sample averages with the scaling factor
\|\mu\|_{1}
, we can obtain the expectation values
\braket{O}_{\psi_{\rm EF}}
. For further details, refer to (SM.5) in
Eddins et al. 2022
.

Here, note that not all states with the doubled system size can be efficiently estimated with this method because the total required number of samples is
O(\|\vec{\lambda}\|_{1}^{4}/\epsilon^{2})
with
\epsilon
being the desired accuracy and
\|\vec{\lambda}\|_{1}
depends on the entanglement structure between the
N
-qubit subsystems.
The worst sampling overhead happens when the entanglement between the 1st and 2nd subsystems is maximal (i.e.,
\ket{\psi_{\rm EF}}
is maximally entangled state), and in this case
\|\vec{\lambda}\|_{1}
is
\sqrt{2^{N}}
. As the entanglement between the clusters becomes weaker,
\|\vec{\lambda}\|_{1}

When employing the

Having seen the formulation in the original research

IV
Noise analysis in hybrid tensor networks

In the previous section, we reviewed recently proposed algorithms that effectively enlarge the size of simulatable quantum systems, such as Deep VQE
Fujii et al. 2022
and entanglement forging
Eddins et al. 2022
, and they can be discussed within the framework of tree HTNs
Yuan et al. 2021
. One notable characteristic of HTNs lies in its step-by-step construction of new observables, which requires not only the classical post-processing but also quantum computations, e.g., the contraction of the effective Hamiltonian in Deep VQE
Fujii et al. 2022
and the relevant algorithms
Mizuta et al. 2021
;
Erhart et al. 2022
need the measurements on quantum computers. In applying these techniques to practical applications, one of the most critical issues is that the presence of physical noise in the quantum computations affects the overall outputs. Here, we take the effect of noise into account and introduce the expansion operator to explicitly represent the effective noisy tree HTN state
\tilde{\rho}_{\rm HT}
corresponding to the computation result for all the types of state preparation (i)-(iv) and even the case where these contractions are interchangeably used. We also discuss noise propagation for the Deep VQE and entangle forge methods under this framework. As a natural consequence of this argument, we can show that as long as type (i)-(iii) state preparation is used, the physicality of the HTN state
\tilde{\rho}_{\rm HT}
, i.e.,
\mathrm{Tr}[\tilde{\rho}_{\rm HT}H]\geq E_{\rm min}
holds for the minimum energy
E_{\rm min}
.

\tilde{M}_{k}
and
\tilde{S}_{k}
can be represented as
\tilde{M}_{k}^{\vec{i}_{k},\vec{i}^{\prime}_{k}}=r_{k}^{\vec{i}_{k},\vec{i}^{\prime}_{k}}M_{k}^{\vec{i}_{k},\vec{i}^{\prime}_{k}}
and
\tilde{S}_{k}^{\vec{i}_{k},\vec{i}^{\prime}_{k}}=s_{k}^{\vec{i}_{k},\vec{i}^{\prime}_{k}}S_{k}^{\vec{i}_{k},\vec{i}^{\prime}_{k}}
where

r_{k}^{\vec{i}_{k},\vec{i}^{\prime}_{k}}=\begin{cases}1-p_{\,\vec{i}_{k}},&\vec{i}_{k}=\vec{i}^{\prime}_{k},\\
(1-q_{\,\vec{i}_{k}})(1-q_{\,\vec{i}^{\prime}_{k}}),&\vec{i}_{k}\neq\vec{i}^{\prime}_{k},\end{cases}

(57)

and

s_{k}^{\vec{i}_{k},\vec{i}^{\prime}_{k}}=\begin{cases}1,&\vec{i}_{k}=\vec{i}^{\prime}_{k},\\
(1-q_{\,\vec{i}_{k}})(1-q_{\,\vec{i}^{\prime}_{k}}),&\vec{i}_{k}\neq\vec{i}^{\prime}_{k}.\end{cases}

(58)

Here, let us define the following functions
\Gamma_{k}(\bullet)
and
\Lambda_{k}(\bullet)
working on the arbitrary matrix with the same size as the
M_{k}^{(4)}
and
S_{k}^{(4)}
, i.e.

\Gamma_{k}\left(\sum_{\vec{i}_{k},\vec{i}^{\prime}_{k}}e^{\vec{i}_{k},\vec{i}^{\prime}_{k}}\ket{\vec{i}_{k}}\bra{\vec{i}^{\prime}_{k}}\right):=\sum_{\vec{i}_{k},\vec{i}^{\prime}_{k}}r_{k}^{\vec{i}_{k},\vec{i}^{\prime}_{k}}e^{\vec{i}_{k},\vec{i}^{\prime}_{k}}\ket{\vec{i}_{k}}\bra{\vec{i}^{\prime}_{k}},

(59)

and

\Lambda_{k}\left(\sum_{\vec{i}_{k},\vec{i}^{\prime}_{k}}e^{\vec{i}_{k},\vec{i}^{\prime}_{k}}\ket{\vec{i}_{k}}\bra{\vec{i}^{\prime}_{k}}\right):=\sum_{\vec{i}_{k},\vec{i}^{\prime}_{k}}s_{k}^{\vec{i}_{k},\vec{i}^{\prime}_{k}}e^{\vec{i}_{k},\vec{i}^{\prime}_{k}}\ket{\vec{i}_{k}}\bra{\vec{i}^{\prime}_{k}},

(60)

where
e^{\vec{i}_{k},\vec{i}^{\prime}_{k}}
are arbitrary complex numbers for all
\vec{i}_{k}
and
\vec{i}^{\prime}_{k}
. Note that the functions
\Gamma_{k}
and
\Lambda_{k}
transform the operator
M_{k}^{(4)}
and
S_{k}^{(4)}
into noisy ones
\tilde{M}_{k}^{(4)}=\Gamma_{k}(M_{k}^{(4)})
and
\tilde{S}_{k}^{(4)}=\Lambda_{k}(S_{k}^{(4)})
, respectively. Introducing
\Gamma_{k}
and
\Lambda_{k}~(k=1,...,N)
into Eq. (
38
), we have

\begin{split}\braket{\tilde{O}}_{\rho_{\rm HT}^{(4)}}&=\frac{\mathrm{Tr}[\,\bigotimes_{k=1}^{N}\Gamma_{k}(M_{k}^{(4)})~\rho~]}{\,\mathrm{Tr}[\,\bigotimes_{k=1}^{N}\Lambda_{k}(S_{k}^{(4)})~\rho~]}\\
&=\mathrm{Tr}\left[O\cdot\frac{(\bigotimes_{k=1}^{N}A_{k}^{(4)})\bigotimes_{k=1}^{N}\Gamma_{k}(\rho)(\bigotimes_{k=1}^{N}A_{k}^{(4)})^{\dagger}}{(\bigotimes_{k=1}^{N}A_{k}^{(4)})\bigotimes_{k=1}^{N}\Lambda_{k}(\rho)(\bigotimes_{k=1}^{N}A_{k}^{(4)})^{\dagger}}\right].\end{split}

(61)

Regarding the component separated from the observable
O
as effective density operator
\tilde{\rho}_{\rm HT}^{(4)}
, we obtain

\tilde{\rho}_{\rm HT}^{(4)}=\frac{(\bigotimes_{k=1}^{N}A_{k}^{(4)})\bigotimes_{k=1}^{N}\Gamma_{k}(\rho)(\bigotimes_{k=1}^{N}A_{k}^{(4)})^{\dagger}}{(\bigotimes_{k=1}^{N}A_{k}^{(4)})\bigotimes_{k=1}^{N}\Lambda_{k}(\rho)(\bigotimes_{k=1}^{N}A_{k}^{(4)})^{\dagger}}.

(62)

Since
\otimes_{k=1}^{N}\Gamma_{k}(\rho)
is not always a positive-semidefinite operator in general, we arrive at the conclusion that
\tilde{\rho}_{\rm HT}
could have negative eigenvalues.

IV.6
Classical case

Let us consider the case where
\xi_{k}=\mathrm{c}
for all
k
. Given that errors in classical computations are sufficiently small and negligible, the contracted operators
\tilde{M}_{k}^{(\mathrm{c})}
and
\tilde{S}_{k}^{(\mathrm{c})}
can be represented as
\tilde{M}^{(\mathrm{c})}_{k}=M^{(\mathrm{c})}_{k}={A_{k}^{(\mathrm{c})}}^{\dagger}O_{k}A^{(\mathrm{c})}_{k}
and
\tilde{S}^{(\mathrm{c})}_{k}=S^{(\mathrm{c})}_{k}={A_{k}^{(\mathrm{c})}}^{\dagger}A^{(\mathrm{c})}_{k}
. Thus, the following equations clearly hold:

\begin{split}\mathrm{Tr}[\,\tilde{M}_{k}^{(\mathrm{c})}\bullet\,]&=\mathrm{Tr}[\,O_{k}\,\tilde{\mathcal{A}}^{(c)}_{k}(\bullet)\,],\\
\mathrm{Tr}[\,\tilde{S}_{k}^{(\mathrm{c})}\bullet\,]&=\mathrm{Tr}[\,\tilde{\mathcal{A}}^{(\mathrm{c})}_{k}(\bullet)\,],\end{split}

(63)

where we defined
\tilde{\mathcal{A}}^{(\mathrm{c})}_{k}(\bullet)=A^{(\mathrm{c})}_{k}\bullet{A_{k}^{(\mathrm{c})}}^{\dagger}
. Hence, we can easily obtain

\tilde{\rho}_{\rm HT}^{(\mathrm{c})}=\frac{(\bigotimes_{k=1}^{N}A_{k}^{(\mathrm{c})})\,\rho\,(\bigotimes_{k=1}^{N}A_{k}^{(\mathrm{c})})^{\dagger}}{\mathrm{Tr}[(\bigotimes_{k=1}^{N}A_{k}^{(\mathrm{c})})\,\rho\,(\bigotimes_{k=1}^{N}A_{k}^{(\mathrm{c})})^{\dagger}]}.

(64)

Since
\rho
is a density operator,
\tilde{\rho}_{\rm HT}^{(\mathrm{c})}
is obviously a density operator.

IV.7
Quantum-classical mixed-type HTTN state preparations with multiple layers

In the preceding discussions, we have investigated a noisy effective HTTN state

V
Analysis of noise propagation for specific noise models

Here, we further delve into the expansion operator by specifying the noise model to the global depolarizing model for each quantum tensor. Note that global depolarizing noise has been shown to be a good approximation for error mitigation
Tsubouchi et al. 2022
;
Qin et al. 2023
;
Urbanek et al. 2021
;
Vovrosh et al. 2021
. We show that the expectation value of observables exponentially vanishes with the number of quantum tensors.

Here, we assume that the global depolarizing channel acts just after the unitary channel in Fig.
2
. In this case, the expansion operators
\tilde{\mathcal{A}}^{(\xi_{k})}_{k}(\xi_{k}=1,2,3)
for a noisy quantum tensor can be represented as:

\tilde{\mathcal{A}}^{(\xi_{k})}_{k}(\bullet)=(1-\varepsilon_{k})\mathcal{A}^{(\xi_{k})}_{k}(\bullet)+\varepsilon_{k}\mathcal{K}^{(\xi_{k})}_{k}(\bullet),

(84)

where
\varepsilon_{k}
are noise rates,
\mathcal{A}^{(\xi_{k})}_{k}
is the noiseless expansion operator, and

\mathcal{K}^{(\xi_{k})}_{k}(\bullet)=\begin{cases}\frac{I^{\otimes K_{k}}}{2^{K_{k}}},&\xi_{k}=1,\\
\mathrm{Tr}(\bullet)\frac{I^{\otimes K_{k}}}{2^{K_{k}}},&\xi_{k}=2,\\
\mathcal{P}_{k}^{(3)\dagger}\bigl\{(\bullet)\otimes\frac{I^{\otimes K_{k}}}{2^{K_{k}}}\bigr\}\mathcal{P}_{k}^{(3)},&\xi_{k}=3.\end{cases}

(85)

By assuming that the HTN state consists only of noisy quantum tensors and that
\varepsilon_{k}=\varepsilon
for simplicity, we obtain

\displaystyle\bigcirc_{k=2}^{N_{\mathrm{tot}}}\tilde{\mathcal{A}}^{(\xi_{k})}_{k}(\rho)

\displaystyle=(1-\varepsilon)^{N_{\mathrm{tot}}-1}\bigcirc_{k=2}^{N_{\mathrm{tot}}}\mathcal{A}^{(\xi_{k})}_{k}(\rho)

(86)

\displaystyle+\sum_{m=1}^{N_{\mathrm{tot}}-1}p(N_{\mathrm{tot}}-1,m,\varepsilon)\mathcal{B}_{m},

where
p(N_{\mathrm{tot}}-1,m,\varepsilon)=\binom{N_{\mathrm{tot}}-1}{m}(1-\varepsilon)^{N_{\mathrm{tot}}-m-1}\varepsilon^{m}
is the binomial distribution, and
\mathcal{B}_{m}
is the process in which the error
\mathcal{K}_{k}^{(\xi_{k})}
occurs
m
times. Here,
\bigcirc_{k=1}^{N}\mathcal{E}_{k}=\mathcal{E}_{N}\circ\mathcal{E}_{N-1}\circ...\circ\mathcal{E}_{1}
for a quantum process
\mathcal{E}_{k}~(k=1,2,...,N)
. The process
\mathcal{B}_{m}
renders the HTN states highly mixed, which makes expectation values of non-local Pauli observables negligible. Then we can regard
\bigcirc_{k=2}^{N_{\mathrm{tot}}}\tilde{\mathcal{A}}^{(\xi_{k})}_{k}(\rho)\sim(1-\varepsilon)^{N_{\mathrm{tot}}-1}\bigcirc_{k=2}^{N_{\mathrm{tot}}}\mathcal{A}^{(\xi_{k})}_{k}(\rho)
.

Considering the
L
-layer TTNs consisting of noisy
N
-rank quantum tensors, since the total number of the contracted quantum tensor is
N_{\rm tot}=1+N+N^{2}+...+N^{L-1}=\frac{1-N^{L}}{1-N}
, we have

\braket{O}_{\rm noisy}\sim(1-\varepsilon)^{\frac{1-N^{L}}{1-N}}\braket{O}_{\rm EF}.

(87)

We numerically verify Eq. (
87
) by simulating a
L
-layer TTN comprising of noisy rank-
N
tensors
\rho_{\vec{i},\vec{i}^{\prime}}
for
\vec{i}=(i_{1},i_{2},...,i_{N}),~i_{k}\in\{0,1\}
for
O=Z^{\otimes N^{L}}
with the type (i) state preparation. Each tensor is generated by a two-layer hardware-efficient ansatz shown in Fig.
7
, accompanied by the global depolarizing noise. For efficiently simulating noisy TTNs, once we obtain a contracted matrix
\tilde{M}
in some layer, we use
\tilde{M}^{\otimes N}
for the contraction in the next layer.

We then show the ratio between the noise-free and the noisy value
r_{L}(\varepsilon)=\braket{O}_{\rm noisy}/\braket{O}_{\rm EF}
for
L=4,5,6
with
N=10
in Fig.
8
. In all the cases, we can see

\tilde{r}_{L}(\varepsilon)=(1-\varepsilon)^{\frac{1-N^{L}}{1-N}}

(88)

exactly coincides with the numerical results, which indicates Eq. (
87
) is a very good approximation.

When we can characterize the value of
\varepsilon
, division of
\braket{O}_{\rm noisy}
by
\tilde{r}_{L}(\varepsilon)
yields:

\braket{O}_{\rm QEM}=\braket{O}_{\rm noisy}/\tilde{r}_{L}(\varepsilon),

(89)

which effectively works as quantum error mitigation
Temme et al. 2017
;
Endo et al. 2018
;
Endo et al. 2021
;
Cai et al. 2022

\mathrm{Var}[\braket{O}_{\rm QEM}]=\tilde{r}_{L}(\varepsilon)^{2}\mathrm{Var}[\braket{O}_{\rm noisy}],

(90)

times more samples to achieve the same accuracy as the error-free case.

These discussions indicate that, although the simulatable scale of the quantum systems can be significantly expanded, we need to have exponential resources with the number of contracted quantum tensors. To avoid this problem, we must take the following strategies: (1). adjusting the number of classical and quantum tensors in accordance with the magnitude of the error; or (2). suppressing the error rate itself with error-suppression techniques such as quantum error correction
Devitt et al. 2013
;
Lidar and Brun 2013
and dynamical decoupling
Viola et al. 1999

VI
Conclusions and Discussions

In this paper, we first reviewed the hybrid tensor network (HTN) framework and discussed the related methods, i.e., the Deep VQE and entanglement forging under the language of HTN. Then, whereas these methods are formulated for pure states, the quantum states should be described with the density matrices due to the effect of physical noise in actual experiments. Therefore, we introduced the density matrix representation for HTNs by introducing the expansion operator and discussed the HTN states’ physicality by considering each contraction method case. We found that as long as we use type (i)-(iii) state preparation, the HTN state is physical, while type (iv) may induce unphysical states. We also revealed that the exponential decay of the computation results occurs with the number of contracted quantum tensors in the tree network.

While we mainly analyzed the expectation values, some algorithms use transition amplitudes in the HTN framework, e.g., quantum-inspired Monte-Carlo simulations
Kanno et al. 2023
; therefore, we cannot directly apply our arguments to this case, although the exponential decay of the measured values with the number of quantum tensors is likely to be observed.
