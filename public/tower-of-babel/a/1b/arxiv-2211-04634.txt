Learning Optimal Graph Filters for Clustering of Attributed Graphs
Meiby Ortiz-Bouza, Selin Aviyente

Abstract
Many real-world systems can be represented as graphs where the different entities in the system are presented by nodes and their interactions by edges. An important task in studying large datasets with graphical structure is graph clustering. While there has been a lot of work on graph clustering using the connectivity between the nodes, many real-world networks also have node attributes. Clustering attributed graphs requires joint modeling of graph structure and node attributes. Recent work has focused on combining these two complementary sources of information through graph convolutional networks and graph filtering. However, these methods are mostly limited to lowpass filtering and do not explicitly learn the filter parameters for the clustering task. In this paper, we introduce a graph signal processing based approach, where we learn the parameters of Finite Impulse Response (FIR) and Autoregressive Moving Average (ARMA) graph filters optimized for clustering. The proposed approach is formulated as a two-step iterative optimization problem, focusing on learning interpretable graph filters that are optimal for the given data and that maximize the separation between different clusters. The proposed approach is evaluated on attributed networks and compared to the state-of-the-art methods.

Introduction

Many real-world systems with relational data such as social interactions, citation and co-author relationships, and biological systems are represented as networks

In most real-world networks, both the node attributes and graph connectivity are available.
For example, known properties of proteins,

the set of edges between the

. On
the other hand, community detection algorithms find

, ignoring node attributes. Employing just one of these two sources of information can result in the algorithm failing to account for important structures in the data. For instance, while it would be hard to determine the community membership of a sparsely connected node by solely relying on the network’s connectivity, attributes may help reveal the community affiliation. Conversely, the network may suggest that two nodes belong to the same community, even if one lacks attribute information. Hence, it is crucial to take both information sources into account and view network communities as clusters of closely linked nodes that also share common attributes.

I-B

Contributions

In this paper, we address the shortcomings of the existing methods by proposing a graph filtering based method, GraFiCA, for community detection in attributed networks. A cost function quantifying the separability of the filtered attributes is proposed and a general framework for learning the parameters of both FIR and ARMA filters is introduced. The proposed approach is formulated as a two-step alternating minimization problem, where the first step learns the optimal graph partitioning for the given node attributes while the second step learns the optimal graph filter parameters.

The main contributions of the proposed framework are as follows:

•

The proposed framework is the first that addresses the problem of parametric graph filter design in the form of both FIR and IIR filters for the purpose of attributed graph clustering. While there has been prior work in graph filter design for denoising or graph signal recovery
[
42
]
, GraFiCA is the first unsupervised approach that learns the parameters of the filters for the purpose of clustering.

•

The filters learned by the proposed approach are not limited to low-pass filters as the structure of the filter is determined directly by the data. The filters take into account the useful information in middle and high frequency bands, i.e., higher-order neighborhoods, providing interpretability to the learned filters.

•

GraFiCA uses a cost function that quantifies the discriminability between different classes unlike GCN-based approaches where the loss function is usually the reconstruction error. Thus, the filter coefficients are updated at each step of the algorithm to ensure that the smoothed node attributes are representative of the node assignments.

II
Preliminaries

II-A

Graphs

Let
\mathcal{G}=(V,E,\mathbf{A})
be a graph where
V
is the node set with
|V|=N
,
E
is the edge set and
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}
is the adjacency matrix. The graph Laplacian is given by
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{L}}=\mathbf{D-A}
, where
\mathbf{D}
is the diagonal degree matrix defined as
D_{ii}=\sum_{j}A_{ij}
.
The normalized Laplacian matrix
\mathbf{L}_{n}
is defined as
\mathbf{L}_{n}=\mathbf{D}^{-1/2}(\mathbf{D-A})\mathbf{D}^{-1/2}=\mathbf{I}_{N}-\mathbf{D}^{-1/2}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{D}^{-1/2}=\mathbf{I}_{N}-\mathbf{A}_{n}
, where
\mathbf{I}_{N}
is the identity matrix of size
N
and
\mathbf{A}_{n}
is the normalized adjacency matrix. The spectrum of
\mathbf{L}_{n}
is composed of the diagonal matrix of the eigenvalues,
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}=\text{diag}(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{N})
with
\lambda_{1}\leq\lambda_{2}\leq\ldots\leq\lambda_{N}
, and the eigenvector matrix
\mathbf{U}=[u_{1}|u_{2}|\ldots|u_{N}]
such that
\mathbf{L}_{n}=\mathbf{U}\mathbf{\Lambda}\mathbf{U}^{\top}

[
43
]
.

II-B

Spectral Clustering

Given data with
N
samples,
(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{N})
, the intuition of clustering is to partition the data points based on their similarities. Let
s_{ij}=s(x_{i},x_{j})
be the pairwise similarity between two data objects quantified by some similarity function which is symmetric and nonnegative. Given the similarity graph with an adjacency matrix
\mathbf{W}
, the problem of data clustering can be restated into graph partitioning such that the edges between different groups have a very low weight and the edges within a group have a high weight. For finding
K
clusters, minimizing the cut of this graph consists of finding a partition
\{\mathcal{C}_{1},\mathcal{C}_{2},\ldots,\mathcal{C}_{K}\}
which minimizes:

\text{Cut}(\mathcal{C}_{1},\mathcal{C}_{2},\ldots,\mathcal{C}_{K})=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\text{links}(\mathcal{C}_{k},V\setminus\mathcal{C}_{k}),

(1)

where
\text{links}(\mathcal{C}_{k},V\setminus\mathcal{C}_{k})=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{C}_{k},j\notin\mathcal{C}_{k}}W_{ij}
. In practice, minimizing the cut results in clusters that are unbalanced. In order to address this issue, two different variations of the cut definition, RatioCut (RCut)
[
44
]
and NormalizedCut (Ncut)
[
45
]
, have been proposed:

\begin{split}\text{RCut}(\mathcal{C}_{1},\mathcal{C}_{2},\ldots,\mathcal{C}_{K})=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\frac{\text{links}(\mathcal{C}_{k},V\setminus\mathcal{C}_{k})}{|\mathcal{C}_{k}|},\\
\text{NCut}(\mathcal{C}_{1},\mathcal{C}_{2},\ldots,\mathcal{C}_{K})=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\frac{\text{links}(\mathcal{C}_{k},V\setminus\mathcal{C}_{k})}{\text{vol}(\mathcal{C}_{k})},\end{split}

(2)

where
|\mathcal{C}_{k}|
is the number of nodes in
\mathcal{C}_{k}
and
\text{vol}(\mathcal{C}_{k})
is the total degree of all nodes in
\mathcal{C}_{k}
.

Similar to minimizing the cut metrics, one can determine the partition by maximizing the corresponding association metrics
[
46
]
. For example, the normalized association is defined as:

\text{NAssoc}(\mathcal{C}_{1},\mathcal{C}_{2},\ldots,\mathcal{C}_{K})=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\frac{\text{links}(\mathcal{C}_{k},\mathcal{C}_{k})}{\text{vol}(\mathcal{C}_{k})}.

(3)

Minimizing or maximizing these cost functions are NP hard. Spectral clustering is a way to solve relaxed versions of these problems
[
47
]
.
Introducing a cluster indicator vector for cluster
k
, i.e.
z_{k_{i}}=1
when node
i
belongs to cluster
k
, we can set the matrix
\mathbf{Z}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times K}
as the matrix whose columns correspond to the
K
indicator vectors. The normalized association can then be rewritten as
\sum_{k=1}^{K}\frac{\text{links}(\mathcal{C}_{k},\mathcal{C}_{k})}{\text{vol}(\mathcal{C}_{k})}=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\frac{z_{k}\mathbf{W}z_{k}^{\top}}{z_{k}\mathbf{D}z_{k}^{\top}}=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\tilde{z_{k}}\mathbf{W}\tilde{z_{k}}^{\top}
with
\tilde{z_{k}}=z_{k}/(z_{k}^{\top}\mathbf{D}z_{k})^{1/2}
.
This can be rewritten as the following optimization problem

\underset{\mathbf{\bar{Z}},\mathbf{\bar{Z}}^{\top}\mathbf{\bar{Z}}=\mathbf{I}}{\maximize}\hskip 7.22743pt\text{tr}(\mathbf{\bar{Z}}^{\top}\mathbf{D}^{-1/2}\mathbf{W}\mathbf{D}^{-1/2}\mathbf{\bar{Z}}),

(4)

where
\mathbf{\bar{Z}}=\mathbf{D}^{1/2}\mathbf{\tilde{Z}}
.
This is the standard form of a trace maximization problem,
and the Rayleigh-Ritz theorem states that the solution
is given by choosing
\mathbf{\bar{Z}}
as the matrix which contains the
K
eigenvectors corresponding to the largest eigenvalues of
\mathbf{D}^{-1/2}\mathbf{W}\mathbf{D}^{-1/2}
as columns.

Similar to clustering data points, one of the most fundamental tasks in analyzing large-scale networks is community detection
[
6
]
. Community detection is equivalent to graph partitioning and aims to uncover groups of nodes with higher connectivity amongst themselves compared to the rest of the network.
Given a graph with adjacency matrix
\mathbf{A}
, which represents the graph connectivity information, one can find the communities using spectral clustering by either minimizing the NCut or maximizing the NAssoc of the graph yielding

\underset{\mathbf{\bar{Z}},\mathbf{\bar{Z}}^{\top}\mathbf{\bar{Z}}=\mathbf{I}}{\maximize}\hskip 7.22743pt\text{tr}(\mathbf{\bar{Z}}^{\top}\mathbf{D}^{-1/2}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{D}^{-1/2}\mathbf{\bar{Z}}).

(5)

II-C

Graph Filtering

In graph signal processing, two fundamental filter types, Finite Impulse Response (FIR) and Autoregressive Moving Average (ARMA)
[
35
]
graph filters, are considered
[
24
]
. FIR polynomial graph filter is described as the linear operator

\mathcal{H}(\mathbf{L})=\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}h_{t}\mathbf{L}^{t}=\mathbf{U}(\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}h_{t}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}^{t})\mathbf{U}^{\top},

where
T
is the filter order and
h_{t}
’s are the coefficients. On the other hand, an ARMA filter is defined as

\mathcal{H}(\mathbf{L})=\mathbf{U}\frac{\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}a_{t}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}^{t}}{\mathbf{I}+\sum_{q=1}^{Q-1}b_{q}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}^{q}}\mathbf{U}^{\top},

where
(T,Q)
are the filter orders and
a_{t}
’s and
b_{q}
’s are the filter coefficients. Note that the FIR polynomial filter is a special case of ARMA filter for
Q=1
.

Signals defined on the nodes of an attributed graph can be represented as a matrix
\mathbf{F}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times P}
, where
P
is the number of attributes for each node. The filtered graph signal
\mathbf{\tilde{F}}
is obtained as
\mathbf{\tilde{F}}=\mathcal{H}(\mathbf{L})\mathbf{F}=\mathbf{U}\mathcal{H}(\boldsymbol{\Lambda})\mathbf{U}^{\top}\mathbf{F}
, where
\mathcal{H}(\boldsymbol{\Lambda})=\text{diag}(\mathcal{H}(\lambda_{1}),\ldots,\mathcal{H}(\lambda_{N}))
is the frequency response of the graph filter.

III
Graph Filtering for Clustering Attributed Graphs (GraFiCA)

In this paper, we propose to learn the optimal graph filter such that the within-cluster association of the filtered attributes is minimized while the between-class distance of the filtered attributes is maximized. Using an alternating minimization approach, in the first step, given the node attributes, we find the best cluster assignment to minimize the clustering cost function. In the second step, the graph partition is fixed and the cost function is optimized with respect to the filter coefficients. In this manner, the resulting graph filters are optimized for the clustering task (see Fig.
1
for an overview of the method). This results in an attributed graph clustering method that takes into account both the topology and the node attributes.

In this section, we will first introduce the general problem formulation and the corresponding optimization problem. We will then present solutions for the optimal filter design for two different filter types; FIR and ARMA.

III-A

Problem Formulation

Fig. 1
:
Framework of the proposed method

Given a graph
\mathcal{G}
with normalized adjacency matrix
\mathbf{A}_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}
and graph signal (attributes)
\mathbf{F}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times P}
, the goal is to find the best partition, i.e.,
K
non-overlapping clusters,
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{C}}=\{\mathcal{C}_{1},\mathcal{C}_{2},\ldots,\mathcal{C}_{K}\}
, and the optimal graph filter
\mathcal{H}(\boldsymbol{\Lambda};\mathbf{\beta})
with parameters
\mathbf{\beta}
.
We quantify the quality of the clustering based on the filtered graph attributes,
\mathbf{\tilde{F}}=\mathbf{U}\mathcal{H}(\boldsymbol{\Lambda})\mathbf{U}^{\top}\mathbf{F}
, as follows:

\begin{split}\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{C}},&\mathcal{H}(\boldsymbol{\Lambda};\mathbf{\beta}))=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\frac{1}{\text{vol}(\mathcal{C}_{k})}\sum_{i,j\in\mathcal{C}_{k}}||\tilde{F}_{i\cdot}-\tilde{F}_{j\cdot}||^{2}\\
&-\gamma\sum_{k=1}^{K}\frac{1}{\text{vol}(\mathcal{C}_{k})}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\in\mathcal{C}_{k}\\
j\notin\mathcal{C}_{k}\end{subarray}}||\tilde{F}_{i\cdot}-\tilde{F}_{j\cdot}||^{2},\end{split}

(6)

where the first and second terms quantify the similarity of the filtered node attributes within clusters and between clusters, respectively. Thus, we want to minimize the dissimilarity within clusters, i.e., association, while maximizing the separation between clusters, i.e., cut. Defining the dissimilarity matrix based on
\mathbf{\tilde{F}}
as
\tilde{W}_{ij}=||\tilde{F}_{i\cdot}-\tilde{F}_{j\cdot}||^{2}
, (
6
) can be rewritten as

\begin{split}\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{C}},&\mathcal{H}(\boldsymbol{\Lambda};\mathbf{\beta}))=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\frac{1}{\text{vol}(\mathcal{C}_{k})}\sum_{i,j\in\mathcal{C}_{k}}\tilde{W}_{ij}\\
&-\gamma\sum_{k=1}^{K}\frac{1}{\text{vol}(\mathcal{C}_{k})}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\in\mathcal{C}_{k}\\
j\notin\mathcal{C}_{k}\end{subarray}}\tilde{W}_{ij}.\end{split}

(7)

Our goal is to minimize this cost function in terms of both the graph partition,
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{C}}
and the graph filter parameters
\beta
. The corresponding optimization problem can be formulated as

\begin{split}\underset{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{C}},\beta}{\minimize}\hskip 2.84544pt\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{C}},\mathcal{H}(\boldsymbol{\Lambda};\mathbf{\beta}))+\alpha\mathcal{R}(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{C}}),\end{split}

(8)

where the regularization term
\mathcal{R}(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{C}})
will be specified to put additional constraints on the partition such that the connectivity information is also taken into account. We propose a two-step alternating minimization approach to solve this problem, where at each iteration
l
we first learn the optimal graph partitioning for the given graph signal while the second step learns the optimal graph filter parameters:

\begin{split}&\boldsymbol{\mathcal{C}}^{(l+1)}:=\underset{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{C}}}{\argmin}\hskip 2.84544pt\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{C}},\mathcal{H}(\boldsymbol{\Lambda};\mathbf{\beta}^{(l)}))+\alpha\mathcal{R}(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{C}}),\\
&\beta^{(l+1)}:=\underset{\beta}{\argmin}\hskip 2.84544pt\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{C}}^{(l+1)},\mathcal{H}(\boldsymbol{\Lambda};\mathbf{\beta})).\end{split}

(9)

III-B

\boldsymbol{\mathcal{C}}
update: Clustering

For the clustering task, given the filtered attributes,
\mathbf{\tilde{F}}
, we aim to find the graph partition,
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{C}}
. Fixing
\mathcal{H}(\boldsymbol{\Lambda};\mathbf{\beta})
, the cost function in (
7
) is closely related to graph based data clustering described in Section
II-B
. Following the definitions of the normalized cut and association in Eqs. (
2
) and (
3
), respectively, we can rewrite (
7
) as

\begin{split}\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{C}},\mathcal{H}(\boldsymbol{\Lambda};\mathbf{\beta}))=\sum_{k=1}^{K}&\frac{\text{links}(\mathcal{C}_{k},\mathcal{C}_{k})}{\text{vol}(\mathcal{C}_{k})}-\gamma\sum_{k=1}^{K}\frac{\text{links}(\mathcal{C}_{k},V\setminus\mathcal{C}_{k})}{\text{vol}(\mathcal{C}_{k})}.\\
\end{split}

(10)

Since it can be shown that
\text{links}(\mathcal{C}_{k},V\setminus\mathcal{C}_{k})=\text{vol}(\mathcal{C}_{k})-\text{links}(\mathcal{C}_{k},\mathcal{C}_{k})
, the normalized cut is equivalent to the normalized association
[
46
]
. Therefore, we can rewrite (
10
) in terms of the normalized association as

\begin{split}\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{C}},\mathcal{H}(\boldsymbol{\Lambda};\mathbf{\beta}))=(1+\gamma)\sum_{k=1}^{K}&\frac{\text{links}(\mathcal{C}_{k},\mathcal{C}_{k})}{\text{vol}(\mathcal{C}_{k})}+K.\end{split}

(11)

As mentioned in Section
II-B
, the problem of minimizing/maximizing normalized association can be rewritten as a trace optimization problem as in Eq. (
4
). Therefore, minimizing the cost function in Eq. (
11
) is equivalent to minimizing the following

\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{\bar{Z}},\mathcal{H}(\boldsymbol{\Lambda};\mathbf{\beta}))=\text{tr}(\mathbf{\bar{Z}}^{\top}\mathbf{D}^{-1/2}\mathbf{\tilde{W}}\mathbf{D}^{-1/2}\mathbf{\bar{Z}}),

(12)

subject to
\mathbf{\bar{Z}}^{\top}\mathbf{\bar{Z}}=\mathbf{I}
.

Regularization
\mathcal{R}
:
In order to incorporate the connectivity information into the clustering problem, we propose to use the regularization term
\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{\bar{Z}})=-\text{tr}(\mathbf{\bar{Z}}^{\top}\mathbf{D}^{-1/2}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{D}^{-1/2}\mathbf{\bar{Z}})
, such that minimizing our regularization term is equivalent to maximizing NAssoc in Eq. (
5
). Thus,
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{C}}
update in Eq. (
9
) can be equivalently expressed by the following
\mathbf{\bar{Z}}
update equation:

\begin{split}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\bar{Z}}}^{(l+1)}:=\underset{\mathbf{\bar{Z}},\mathbf{\bar{Z}}^{\top}\mathbf{\bar{Z}}=\mathbf{I}}{\argmin}&\hskip 2.84544pt\text{tr}(\mathbf{\bar{Z}}^{\top}\mathbf{D}^{-1/2}\mathbf{\tilde{W}}\mathbf{D}^{-1/2}\mathbf{\bar{Z}})\\
&-\alpha\text{tr}(\mathbf{\bar{Z}}^{\top}\mathbf{D}^{-1/2}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{D}^{-1/2}\mathbf{\bar{Z}}),\\
:=\underset{\mathbf{\bar{Z}},\mathbf{\bar{Z}}^{\top}\mathbf{\bar{Z}}=\mathbf{I}}{\argmin}&\hskip 2.84544pt\text{tr}(\mathbf{\bar{Z}}^{\top}(\mathbf{\tilde{W}}_{n}-\alpha\mathbf{A}_{n})\mathbf{\bar{Z}}).\end{split}

(13)

The optimal solution to this problem is the set of eigenvectors corresponding to the
K
smallest eigenvalues of
\mathbf{\tilde{W}}_{n}-\alpha\mathbf{A}_{n}
. The graph partition,
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{C}}^{(l+1)}
, is then updated by applying k-means to the rows of
\mathbf{\bar{Z}}
.

III-C

\beta
Update: Optimal Filter Design

Once we have the cluster assignments, we want to determine the coefficients of the optimal filter
\mathcal{H}(\mathbf{\Lambda};\beta)
. In this paper, we present the derivations for both FIR and IIR filter types, with
\beta=\mathbf{h}
for FIR and
\beta=\{\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}\}
for the ARMA filter.

III-C
1
FIR Filter

In this section, we present the optimization problem for learning the parameters of the optimal polynomial filter with a given filter order
T
,
\mathcal{H}(\mathbf{\Lambda})=\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}h_{t}\mathbf{\Lambda}^{t}
, for the clustering task.

Following the definitions in
[
33
]
, we can define the
t
-th shifted input signal,
\mathbf{S}^{(t)}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times P}
, as
\mathbf{S}^{(t)}:=\mathbf{U}\Lambda^{t}\mathbf{U}^{\top}\mathbf{F}
and
\mathbf{S}_{(i)}
can then be defined as a
T\times P
matrix corresponding to the
i
-th node where each row corresponds to the
t
-th shifted input signal at that node with
[\mathbf{S}_{(i)}]_{t}:=[\mathbf{S}^{(t)}]_{(i)}
.
With
\mathbf{\tilde{F}}
denoting the output of a graph filter for the input signal
\mathbf{F}
, it follows that

\mathbf{\tilde{F}}=\mathbf{U}\left(\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}h_{t}\Lambda^{t}\right)\mathbf{U}^{\top}\mathbf{F}=\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}h_{t}\mathbf{S}^{(t)}.

(14)

Hence, the filtered graph signal corresponding to the
i
-th node can be computed as
\mathbf{\tilde{F}}_{i}=\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}h_{t}[\mathbf{S}^{(t)}]_{i}=\mathbf{h}^{\top}\mathbf{S}_{(i)}
, with
\mathbf{h}=[h_{0},h_{1},\cdots,h_{T-1}]
. The cost function in (
6
) can then be rewritten in terms of the filter coefficient vector,
\mathbf{h}
, as follows

\begin{split}\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{C}}&,\mathbf{h})=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\frac{1}{\text{vol}(\mathcal{C}_{k})}\sum_{i,j\in\mathcal{C}_{k}}||\mathbf{h}^{\top}\mathbf{S}_{(i)}-\mathbf{h}^{\top}\mathbf{S}_{(j)}||^{2}\\
&-\gamma\sum_{k=1}^{K}\frac{1}{\text{vol}(\mathcal{C}_{k})}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\in\mathcal{C}_{k}\\
j\notin\mathcal{C}_{k}\end{subarray}}||\mathbf{h}^{\top}\mathbf{S}_{(i)}-\mathbf{h}^{\top}\mathbf{S}_{(j)}||^{2},\\
=\sum_{k=1}^{K}&\frac{1}{\text{vol}(\mathcal{C}_{k})}\sum_{i,j\in\mathcal{C}_{k}}\mathbf{h}^{\top}(\mathbf{S}_{(i)}-\mathbf{S}_{(j)})(\mathbf{S}_{(i)}-\mathbf{S}_{(j)})^{\top}\mathbf{h}\\
-\gamma\sum_{k=1}^{K}&\frac{1}{\text{vol}(\mathcal{C}_{k})}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\in\mathcal{C}_{k}\\
j\notin\mathcal{C}_{k}\end{subarray}}\mathbf{h}^{\top}(\mathbf{S}_{(i)}-\mathbf{S}_{(j)})(\mathbf{S}_{(i)}-\mathbf{S}_{(j)})^{\top}\mathbf{h}.\end{split}

(15)

Eq. (
15
) can be rewritten as follows:

\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{C}},\mathbf{h})=(\mathbf{h}^{\top}(\mathbf{B}_{T}-\gamma\mathbf{C}_{T})\mathbf{h}),

(16)

where
\mathbf{B}_{T}
and
\mathbf{C}_{T}
are
T\times T
matrices defined as

\mathbf{B}=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\frac{1}{\text{vol}(\mathcal{C}_{k})}\sum_{i,j\in\mathcal{C}_{k}}(\mathbf{S}_{(i)}-\mathbf{S}_{(j)})(\mathbf{S}_{(i)}-\mathbf{S}_{(j)})^{\top},

(17)

\mathbf{C}=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\frac{1}{\text{vol}(\mathcal{C}_{k})}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\in\mathcal{C}_{k}\\
j\notin\mathcal{C}_{k}\end{subarray}}(\mathbf{S}_{(i)}-\mathbf{S}_{(j)})(\mathbf{S}_{(i)}-\mathbf{S}_{(j)})^{\top}.

(18)

Our optimization problem becomes

\underset{\mathbf{h}}{\minimize}\hskip 5.69046pt(\mathbf{h}^{\top}(\mathbf{B}_{T}-\gamma\mathbf{C}_{T})\mathbf{h}).

(19)

The solution to the optimization problem is the eigenvector of
\mathbf{B}_{T}-\gamma\mathbf{C}_{T}
corresponding to the smallest eigenvalue. Once
\mathbf{h}
is obtained, the filtered signal can be updated as
\mathbf{\tilde{F}}=\mathbf{U}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}h_{t}\mathbf{\Lambda}^{t}\mathbf{U}^{\top}\mathbf{F}
.

III-C
2
ARMA Filter

In this section, we present the optimization problem for learning the parameters of an ARMA filter with the following graph frequency
response

\mathcal{H}(\boldsymbol{\Lambda})=\frac{\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}a_{t}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}^{t}}{\mathbf{I}+\sum_{q=1}^{Q-1}b_{q}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}^{q}},

(20)

where
\mathbf{a}=[a_{0},a_{1},\dots,a_{T-1}]
and
\mathbf{b}=[b_{1},b_{2},\dots,b_{Q-1}]
are the filter coefficients, and
(T,Q)
is the pair of filter orders.

In order to find the filter parameters
\mathbf{a}
and
\mathbf{b}
we introduce an auxiliary polynomial
\Gamma_{M}(\lambda)=\sum_{m=0}^{M-1}c_{m}\lambda^{m}
.
\Gamma_{M}(\lambda)
can be viewed as the reciprocal polynomial of
(1+\sum_{q=1}^{Q-1}b_{q}\lambda^{q})
. In general, the reciprocal polynomial has a larger order than the denominator polynomial, i.e.,
M\geq Q

[
48
]
. Letting
\sum_{m=0}^{M-1}c_{m}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}^{m}=\frac{1}{\mathbf{I}+\sum_{q=1}^{Q-1}b_{q}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}^{q}}
, Eq. (
20
) becomes

\mathcal{H}(\boldsymbol{\Lambda})=\sum_{m=0}^{M-1}c_{m}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}^{m}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}a_{t}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}^{t}.

(21)

To solve for the filter parameters, we introduce a constraint to assure that
(\sum_{m=0}^{M-1}c_{m}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}^{m})(\mathbf{I}+\sum_{q=1}^{Q-1}b_{q}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}^{q})=\mathbf{I}
.
We can rewrite the polynomials as
\sum_{m=0}^{M-1}c_{m}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}^{m}=\text{diag}(\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{M}\mathbf{c})
and
\sum_{q=1}^{Q-1}b_{q}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}^{q}=\text{diag}(\boldsymbol{\bar{\Psi}}_{Q}\mathbf{b})
, where
\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{M}
and
\boldsymbol{\bar{\Psi}}_{Q}
are defined as

\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{M}=\begin{bmatrix}1&\lambda_{1}&\lambda_{1}^{2}&\cdots&\lambda_{1}^{M-1}\\
1&\lambda_{2}&\lambda_{2}^{2}&\cdots&\lambda_{2}^{M-1}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
1&\lambda_{N}&\lambda_{N}^{2}&\cdots&\lambda_{N}^{M-1}\\
\end{bmatrix},\\

\boldsymbol{\bar{\Psi}}_{Q}=\begin{bmatrix}\lambda_{1}&\lambda_{1}^{2}&\cdots&\lambda_{1}^{Q-1}\\
\lambda_{2}&\lambda_{2}^{2}&\cdots&\lambda_{2}^{Q-1}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\lambda_{N}&\lambda_{N}^{2}&\cdots&\lambda_{N}^{Q-1}\\
\end{bmatrix}.

Using these definitions we can then rewrite
(\sum_{m=0}^{M-1}c_{m}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}^{m})(\mathbf{I}+\sum_{q=1}^{Q-1}b_{q}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}^{q})=\mathbf{I}
as
(\text{diag}(\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{M}\mathbf{c}))(\mathbf{I}+\text{diag}(\boldsymbol{\bar{\Psi}}_{Q}\mathbf{b}))=\mathbf{I}
and the optimization problem becomes

\begin{split}&\underset{\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b},\mathbf{c}}{\minimize}\hskip 7.22743pt\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{C}},\mathcal{H}(\boldsymbol{\Lambda};\mathbf{[a,c]}))\\
&+||\text{diag}(\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{M}\mathbf{c})[\mathbf{I}+\text{diag}(\boldsymbol{\bar{\Psi}}_{Q}\mathbf{b})]-\mathbf{I}||_{F}^{2}.\end{split}

(22)

In order to find the parameters
\mathbf{a}
,
\mathbf{b}
and
\mathbf{c}
, we propose an alternating minimization approach where in order to learn each of the variables we fix the other two. Thus, the
\beta
update in (
9
) becomes

\begin{split}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{a}}^{(l+1)}:=&\underset{\mathbf{a}}{\argmin}\hskip 2.84544pt\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{C}},\mathcal{H}(\boldsymbol{\Lambda};[\mathbf{a},\mathbf{c}^{(l)}])),\\
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{b}}^{(l+1)}:=&\underset{\mathbf{b}}{\argmin}\hskip 2.84544pt||\text{diag}(\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{M}\mathbf{c}^{(l)})[\mathbf{I}+\text{diag}(\boldsymbol{\bar{\Psi}}_{Q}\mathbf{b})]-\mathbf{I}||_{F}^{2},\\
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{c}}^{(l+1)}:=&\underset{\mathbf{c}}{\argmin}\hskip 2.84544pt\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{C}},\mathcal{H}(\boldsymbol{\Lambda};[\mathbf{a}^{(l+1)},\mathbf{c}]))\\
+&||\text{diag}(\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{M}\mathbf{c})[\mathbf{I}+\text{diag}(\boldsymbol{\bar{\Psi}}_{Q}\mathbf{b}^{(l+1)})]-\mathbf{I}||_{F}^{2}.\\
\end{split}

(23)

Update a:
In order to update
\mathbf{a}
, we define the
t
-th shifted input signal as
\mathbf{S}^{(t)}:=\mathbf{U}(\sum_{m=0}^{M-1}c_{m}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}^{m})\boldsymbol{\Lambda}^{t}\mathbf{U}^{\top}\mathbf{F}
and
\mathbf{S}_{(i)}
as a
T\times P
matrix corresponding to the
i
-th node where each row corresponds to the
t
-th shifted input signal at that node,
[\mathbf{S}^{(t)}]_{(i)}
. Therefore,
\mathbf{\tilde{F}}_{i}=\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}a_{t}[\mathbf{S}^{(t)}]_{i}=\mathbf{a}^{\top}\mathbf{S}_{(i)}
.

The cost function in (
6
) can be rewritten in terms of the filter coefficients
\mathbf{a}
and the newly defined
\mathbf{S}_{(i)}
as follows

\begin{split}\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{C}},&\mathbf{a},\mathbf{c})=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\frac{1}{\text{vol}(\mathcal{C}_{k})}\sum_{i,j\in\mathcal{C}_{k}}||\mathbf{a}^{\top}\mathbf{S}_{(i)}-\mathbf{a}^{\top}\mathbf{S}_{(j)}||^{2}\\
&-\gamma\sum_{k=1}^{K}\frac{1}{\text{vol}(\mathcal{C}_{k})}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\in\mathcal{C}_{k}\\
j\notin\mathcal{C}_{k}\end{subarray}}||\mathbf{a}^{\top}\mathbf{S}_{(i)}-\mathbf{a}^{\top}\mathbf{S}_{(j)}||^{2},\\
=\sum_{k=1}^{K}&\frac{1}{\text{vol}(\mathcal{C}_{k})}\sum_{i,j\in\mathcal{C}_{k}}\mathbf{a}^{\top}(\mathbf{S}_{(i)}-\mathbf{S}_{(j)})(\mathbf{S}_{(i)}-\mathbf{S}_{(j)})^{\top}\mathbf{a}\\
-\gamma\sum_{k=1}^{K}&\frac{1}{\text{vol}(\mathcal{C}_{k})}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\in\mathcal{C}_{k}\\
j\notin\mathcal{C}_{k}\end{subarray}}\mathbf{a}^{\top}(\mathbf{S}_{(i)}-\mathbf{S}_{(j)})(\mathbf{S}_{(i)}-\mathbf{S}_{(j)})^{\top}\mathbf{a}.\end{split}

(24)

Eq. (
24
) can be rewritten as follows:

\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{C}},\mathbf{a},\mathbf{c})=(\mathbf{a}^{\top}(\mathbf{B}_{T}-\gamma\mathbf{C}_{T})\mathbf{a}),

(25)

where
\mathbf{B}_{T}
and
\mathbf{C}_{T}
are
T\times T
matrices defined as in Eqs. (
17
) and (
18
) using the newly defined
\mathbf{S}_{(i)}
.
And, our optimization problem becomes

\underset{\mathbf{a}}{\minimize}\hskip 5.69046pt(\mathbf{a}^{\top}(\mathbf{B}_{T}-\gamma\mathbf{C}_{T})\mathbf{a}).

(26)

whose solution is the eigenvector of
\mathbf{B}_{T}-\gamma\mathbf{C}_{T}
corresponding to the smallest eigenvalue.

Update b:
Vectorizing each term in (
23
) for the
\mathbf{b}
update, we have the following equivalent optimization problem

\underset{\mathbf{b}}{\minimize}\hskip 5.69046pt||\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{M}\mathbf{c}+\text{diag}(\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{M}\mathbf{c})\boldsymbol{\bar{\Psi}}_{Q}\mathbf{b}-\mathbf{1}_{N}||^{2},

(27)

where
\mathbf{1}_{N}\in\mathbf{R}^{N}
is an all ones vector. To find
\mathbf{b}=[b_{1},b_{2},\cdots,b_{Q-1}]
, the following objective function
\mathcal{L}_{1}(\mathbf{b})=||\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{M}\mathbf{c}+\text{diag}(\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{M}\mathbf{c})\boldsymbol{\bar{\Psi}}_{Q}\mathbf{b}-\mathbf{1}_{N}||^{2}

is minimized with respect to
\mathbf{b}
by setting

\begin{split}\nabla_{b}\mathcal{L}_{1}=2\boldsymbol{\bar{\Psi}}_{Q}^{\top}\text{diag}&(\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{M}\mathbf{c})[\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{M}\mathbf{c}+\text{diag}(\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{M}\mathbf{c})\boldsymbol{\bar{\Psi}}_{Q}\mathbf{b}]\\
&-2\boldsymbol{\bar{\Psi}}_{Q}^{\top}\text{diag}(\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{M}\mathbf{c})\mathbf{1}_{N}=0.\end{split}

After some algebraic manipulations,
\mathbf{b}
can be updated as

\begin{split}\mathbf{b}=\mathbf{Y}_{1}^{-1}\mathbf{v}_{1},\end{split}

(28)

where
\mathbf{Y}_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{Q-1\times Q-1}
and
\mathbf{v}_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{Q-1}
are defined as
\mathbf{Y}_{1}=(\boldsymbol{\bar{\Psi}}_{Q}^{\top}\text{diag}(\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{M}\mathbf{c})\text{diag}(\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{M}\mathbf{c})\boldsymbol{\bar{\Psi}}_{Q})
and
\mathbf{v}_{1}=\boldsymbol{\bar{\Psi}}_{Q}^{\top}\text{diag}(\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{M}\mathbf{c})[\mathbf{1}_{N}-\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{M}\mathbf{c}]
, respectively.
\mathbf{Y}_{1}
is full rank and thus
\mathbf{Y}_{1}^{-1}
exists.

Update c:
We can define the
m
-th shifted input signal as
\mathbf{S}^{(m)}:=\mathbf{U}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}^{m}(\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}a_{t}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}^{t})\mathbf{U}^{\top}\mathbf{F}
and
\mathbf{\tilde{F}}_{i}=\sum_{m=0}^{M-1}c_{m}[\mathbf{S}^{(m)}]_{i}=\mathbf{c}^{\top}\mathbf{S}_{(i)}
, with
\mathbf{S}_{(i)}
being a
M\times P
matrix corresponding to the
i
-th node where each row corresponds to the
m
-th shifted input signal at that node,
[\mathbf{S}^{(m)}]_{(i)}
.

The
\mathbf{c}
update in (
23
) can be rewritten as

\begin{split}\underset{\mathbf{c}}{\minimize}\hskip 2.84544pt&\mathbf{c}^{\top}(\mathbf{B}_{M}-\gamma\mathbf{C}_{M})\mathbf{c}\\
&+||\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{M}\mathbf{c}+\text{diag}(\boldsymbol{\bar{\Psi}}_{Q}\mathbf{b})\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{M}\mathbf{c}-\mathbf{1}_{N}||^{2}.\end{split}

(29)

To find
\mathbf{c}=[c_{0},c_{1},\cdots,c_{M-1}]
, the following objective function
\mathcal{L}_{2}(\mathbf{c})=\mathbf{c}^{\top}(\mathbf{B}_{M}-\gamma\mathbf{C}_{M})\mathbf{c}+||\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{M}\mathbf{c}+\text{diag}(\boldsymbol{\bar{\Psi}}_{Q}\mathbf{b})\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{M}\mathbf{c}-\mathbf{1}_{N}||^{2}

is minimized with respect to
\mathbf{c}
by setting

\begin{split}\nabla_{c}\mathcal{L}_{2}&=2(\mathbf{B}_{M}-\gamma\mathbf{C}_{M})\mathbf{c}\\
&+2\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{M}^{\top}(\mathbf{I}+\text{diag}(\boldsymbol{\bar{\Psi}}_{Q}\mathbf{b}))^{\top}(\mathbf{I}+\text{diag}(\boldsymbol{\bar{\Psi}}_{Q}\mathbf{b}))\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{M}\mathbf{c}\\
&-2\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{M}^{\top}(\mathbf{I}+\text{diag}(\boldsymbol{\bar{\Psi}}_{Q}\mathbf{b})^{\top})\mathbf{1}_{N}=0.\end{split}

After some algebraic manipulations,
\mathbf{c}
can be updated as

\begin{split}\mathbf{c}=\mathbf{Y}_{2}^{-1}\mathbf{v}_{2},\end{split}

(30)

where
\mathbf{Y}_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times M}
and
\mathbf{v}_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{M}
are defined as
\mathbf{Y}_{2}=(\mathbf{B}_{M}-\gamma\mathbf{C}_{M})+\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{M}^{\top}(\mathbf{I}+\text{diag}(\boldsymbol{\bar{\Psi}}_{Q}\mathbf{b}))^{\top}(\mathbf{I}+\text{diag}(\boldsymbol{\bar{\Psi}}_{Q}\mathbf{b}))\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{M}
and
\mathbf{v}_{2}=\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{M}^{\top}(\mathbf{I}+\text{diag}(\boldsymbol{\bar{\Psi}}_{Q}\mathbf{b})^{\top})\mathbf{1}_{N}
, respectively.
\mathbf{Y}_{2}
is full rank and thus
\mathbf{Y}_{2}^{-1}
exists.

\mathbf{a}

\mathbf{b}

The computational complexity of the algorithm is mostly due to the eigendecompositions and inverse operations at each iteration. There is only one full eigendecomposition at the beginning of the algorithm for the normalized Laplacian of the graph. Eigendecompositions of a
N\times N

\mathcal{O}(N^{3})
. However, it is important to note that this is the worst-case scenario, and there exist fast algorithms that will reduce this to
\mathcal{O}(N^{e})
,
2<e<2.376

[
49
]

[
50

\mathcal{O}(N^{2})

K

V-C

Interpretation of the Learned Filters

Fig.
2(f)
-
2(o)
show the frequency responses of the optimal FIR and ARMA filters for each dataset. For most datasets, the optimal filters have both a low-pass and high-pass region, thus extracting both smooth and non-smooth features from the node attributes. This is in contrast to GAE-based methods that are limited to first-order low-pass filtering and AGC, which is a higher-order low-pass filter. While the clustering performance of FIR and ARMA filters are similar to each other, the filter shapes obtained by ARMA are smoother as they fit IIR filters to approximate the same frequency response. It is also interesting to note that the optimal filter for Sinanet is an all-pass filter, as the original node attributes carry most of the class information. In this case, filtering the attributes may not improve the accuracy of clustering as seen in the second row of Fig.
3
where the attributes before and after filtering are very similar. This also explains why methods like k-means and CGFKM which only rely on the attributes perform well in clustering this dataset. On the contrary, for Cora, the clusters become better separated after filtering, as seen in the first row of Fig.
3
. This is in line with the poor clustering performance of methods that only use node attributes. These results indicate that our method adapts to the characteristics of the data and yields interpretable filters.

Except for the filters for Sinanet, which are all-pass, for most of the other datasets, the filters are mostly low-pass with content in the middle and high frequencies. The fact that our filters do not entirely suppress high-frequency components suggests a balance between preserving the cohesiveness of clusters and highlighting crucial differences across various regions of the graph.

In order to better understand and interpret the frequency responses of the learned filters, we examine the Spectral Energy Distribution (SED) of the graph signals with respect to the eigenvalues of the graph Laplacian.
For a graph signal
\mathbf{F}
and its graph Fourier transform
\hat{\mathbf{F}}=\mathbf{U}^{\top}\mathbf{F}

\lambda_{i}

\hat{F}_{ip}^{2}/\sum_{i=1}^{N}(\hat{F}_{ip})^{2}
across the
p

Fig.

-

show the spectral energy distribution of the graph signals for each dataset. A concentration of energy in the lower end of the spectrum suggests that the graph signal is smooth and varies slowly across the graph. This can also indicate strong connectivity within certain graph regions or the presence of well-defined communities. Significant energy in the higher frequencies indicates that the graph signal has high variability or rapid changes across edges. This might also suggest that the graph contains regions of sparse connectivity and other regions with higher density.

As we can see in Fig.

Both datasets represent papers in different machine learning areas, where it is fair to assume that the papers could be easily related to more than just one of those areas and therefore, the clusters are not very well defined. Due to this distribution of SED, the optimal filters for Cora and Citeseer have significant power in the middle frequencies. On the other hand, in Sinanet, the main areas of the 10 different forums range from parenting, history and arts, politics, to science and technology, and the clusters are well separated, hence there is a significant peak at the low frequencies as seen in Fig.

VI
Conclusions

As the amount of large-scale network data with node attributes increases, it is important to develop efficient and interpretable graph clustering methods that identify the node labels. In this paper, we introduced an optimal parametric graph filter learning method for attributed graph clustering. The proposed method makes some key contributions to the field. First, the parameters of polynomial graph filters are learned with respect to a loss function that quantifies both the association within and the separation between clusters. Thus, the filter parameters are optimized for the clustering task.
Second, the proposed method does not constrain the filters to be lowpass. Results indicate that the learned filters take into account the useful information in middle and high-frequency bands and the structure of the filter is determined directly by the data. Third, the method is formulated for both FIR and IIR filters, providing similar performance across datasets. While FIR filters are computationally less expensive, IIR filters provide a smoother frequency response. Finally, the learned filters are evaluated with respect to spectral energy distribution of the attributed graphs providing interpretability to the proposed design procedure.
Future work will consider learning a combination of filters and extension to nonlinear filters such as graph wavelets.
