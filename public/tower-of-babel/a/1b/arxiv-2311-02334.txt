Abstract
In this paper, we study the semiclassical behavior of Bloch electrons in the presence of Weyl nodes, which are singular points in the band structure of certain materials. We carry out asymptotic analysis and present a rigorous derivation of the semiclassical asymptotic expansion of the current of Bloch electrons with the presence of Weyl nodes. The analysis shows that the current contains two parts, one independent of the Weyl nodes and the other a contribution from the singular points. This work provides a theoretical foundation towards a rigorous justification of recent scientific discoveries in Weyl semimetals. The main innovation of this paper is a new strategy to deal with the singular points with quantitative estimates, which may have broader applications in multiscale models with singularities.

1
Introduction

In this paper, we consider the semiclassical asymptotic behavior of Bloch
electrons in an external electric field as a perturbation. The motion of Bloch
electrons is one of the fundamental problems in solid-state physics, which is
concerned with the quantum dynamics of electrons in a periodic crystal
lattice, and is found to have connections with many intriguing physical phenomena, such as the Hall effect

[
20
,
5
,
28
,
29
]
.
It is worth emphasizing that the Berry phase, first introduced by

[
3
]
, plays an important role in Bloch dynamics. (see
e.g.

[
6
,
8
,
16
,
40
]
).

There are two basic scenarios in the Bloch theory: the adiabatic and the
non-adiabatic regimes. In the adiabatic regime, the energy bands are isolated
from one another and the electrons effectively remain in the same energy bands
as they move through the crystal.
It has been thoroughly investigated in the mathematical
literature (see e.g.

[
34
,
33
,
35
,
21
]
).
In contrast, in the non-adiabatic regime, the crossings between energy bands
occur and transitions between different energy levels become significant. In
spite of many scientific applications due to band crossing phenomenon, the
mathematical understanding of such models are comparatively less complete.

Weyl equation was first proposed to describe massless fermions with a definite chirality
[
39
]
. When degeneracies occur in electronic band structures, the dispersion in the vicinity of these 3D band touching points is
generically linear and resembles the Weyl equation
[
19
]
, and thus these touching points are referred as
Weyl nodes
. Weyl nodes lead to exotic surface states in the form of Fermi arcs, which manifests the topological aspects of Weyl fermions
[
36
,
1
]
. Weyl nodes can occur in certain types of materials, such as topological
insulators and semimetals
[
37
,
2
]
,
where the band structure exhibits a non-trivial topology.

The Weyl nodes have been studied in the mathematical literature from the aspect of spectrum theory. For example, in

[
13
,
12
,
14
]
,
the authors illustrated the properties of Dirac points, as the 2D analogy of
Weyl nodes, in honeycomb structures and the existence of topologically
protected edge states. For Weyl nodes in 3D, the mathematical study is still sparse, while there are also some recent progress. For example, in
[
17
]
, the authors proved the universality of the quadratic response of the quasi-particle flow in some 3D lattice models for Weyl semimetals.

In this paper, we take the usual “independent electron” assumption and
neglect the interaction between electrons. Then the dynamic of Bloch electrons
is described by the following semiclassical Schrödinger equation with a
periodic lattice potential and a slow-varying scalar potential,

\mathrm{i}\varepsilon\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\psi^{\varepsilon}=(-\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{2}\Delta+V(\frac{x}{\varepsilon})-\varepsilon E(t)\cdot x)\psi^{\varepsilon},\quad x\in\mathbb{R}^{3},\quad t>0.

(1.1)

Here,
\psi^{\varepsilon}
is the wave function and
V
is the lattice potential, which
will be formally defined in Section
2
, and may result in Weyl nodes in
the band structure.
\varepsilon\ll 1
is referred to as the semiclassical parameter, which indicates the typical oscillation scale of the wave function as well as the size of the periodic lattice. When
\varepsilon\to 0
, the semiclassical analysis produces an asymptotic solution that captures the classical behavior of Bloch electrons. Many works are
devoted to this direction of research based on
the WKB ansatz
[
7
,
25
]
or the Wigner transform

[
11
,
26
,
18
,
32
]
, which are tailored to treat the periodic lattice potential.

The optical properties of a material can be described by its response to
external electromagnetic fields. In this work, we assume that the Bloch dynamics
are subject to a small external electric field
\varepsilon E(t)
, which
results in an
O(\varepsilon)
perturbation,
\varepsilon E(t)\cdot x
,
to the potential field. Here, we suppose that the electric field is
homogenuous in space and periodic in time. Such electronic systems, subjected to periodic driving forces, are
also known as Floquet-driven models. Floquet-driven models have been
used to study a wide range of phenomena in physics and chemistry, like the
laser systems
[
10
]
, the photoelectric
effect
[
41
]
and the Stark
effect
[
30
]
.

We aim to carry out asymptotic analysis for this model due to two
aspects of motivations. Mathematically, there are a lot of works on Bloch
electrons in spatially homogenuous electric field, such as

[
38
,
23
,
24
]
. However, to
the best of our knowledge, none of the existing of works is able to explicitly
quantify the effect of the Weyl nodes in Bloch dynamics. Besides, in
semiclassical regime, the small parameter
\varepsilon
makes the wave
function highly oscillatory in time and space. Therefore,
rigorous asymptotic analysis for such a multiscale model with
singularities, i.e. the Weyl nodes, is even more challenging.

This work is also partially inspired by some recent works in the physics literature
[
9
]
, which shows that in Weyl semimetals,
the injection contribution to the CPGE (circular photogalvanic effect) trace is
effectively quantized in terms of the fundamental constants with no
material-dependent parameters. However, the mathematical explanation of this
phenomenon is still absent. Our analysis of the semiclassical Bloch model with
Weyl nodes is developed to serve as a theoretical foundation towards a rigorous
justification of the results.

The presence of Weyl nodes and the semiclassical regime endow the model
(
1.1
) with a multi-scale nature, which we will
elaborate later in Section
4
. It calls for novel expansion treatments
and convergence estimates in developing a complete asymptotic analysis result.
The main innovation of this paper is that we develop a new strategy to deal
with the the singular points with quantitative estimates. More precisely, we
first use the Bloch transform to convert the semiclassical Schrödinger
equation into an equivalent dynamical system for the density of the Bloch
functions (
2.24
), which agrees with the result in the physics
literature
[
31
]
. Then we divide the Brillouin zone
into three parts: the regular part, the singular part and the intermediate part. We deal
with each part with different strategies and finally combine them together to
derive an asymptotic expansion with error estimates..

The main result of this paper is to give a derivation of the semiclassical
asymptotic behavior of the current of Bloch electrons with the presence of Weyl nodes. In fact, we can also apply the asymptotic theory to a large family
of physical observables. Our final result can be briefly summarized as follows. In the presence of the
O(\varepsilon)
external electric field, the current
J(t)
has the following asymptotic expansion,

J(t)=J_{1}(t)+J_{2}(t)+o(\varepsilon^{3}).

(1.2)

It contains two
parts
J_{1}
and
J_{2}
.
J_{1}
depends on the whole Brillouin zone, which is
independent of the presence of Weyl nodes. When Weyl nodes are present, the third order term of
J_{1}
becomes singular. As a result,
J_{2}
, the contribution from Weyl nodes, appears. Because of the singular nature of Weyl nodes, the leading
order contribution of
J_{2}
is
O(\varepsilon^{3}\log\varepsilon)
instead of
O(\varepsilon^{3})
. In some special cases, the zeroth to second order terms of
J_{1}
may vanish, and then
J_{2}
becomes the leading order term. The precise statement of the results is presented in Section
4
.

In
[
9
]
, the
authors focused on the second part and derived a quantized value of the system.
Therefore, the final results justified the linear approximation used in

[
9
]
from a mathematical aspect. We also note that the influence of Weyl nodes appears only in high order terms. There are several works on the high order expansions of semiclassical Schrodinger equation with periodic potentials, such as
[
25
,
15
]
. Our result is a generalization of these works in the case when Weyl nodes are present.

This paper is organized as following: In Section
2
, we transform the
original equation into the density of Bloch functions representation (
2.24
), which facilitates further analysis. In Section
3
, we derive
some results about Weyl nodes with a similar argument to

[
12
]
. In Section
4
, we present our main
result and lay out the proof strategy. In Section
6
, we finish the proof
of the main result.

2
Preliminaries

Recall the Schrödinger equation with a
periodic lattice potential and a spatial uniform electric field

\mathrm{i}\varepsilon\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\psi=\bigl(-\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{2}\Delta+V(\frac{x}{\varepsilon})-\varepsilon E(t)\cdot x\bigr)\psi,\quad x\in\mathbb{R}^{3},\quad t>0,

(2.1)

with initial data given by

\psi(t=0,x)=\psi^{in}(x).

(2.2)

For simplicity, we suppress all dependence on
\varepsilon
in the notation. In this paper, we only study
initial data with some special structures, which we will explain in more details in
Section
2.2
(see (
2.18
)).
V(z)
is the periodic lattice potential, and
-\varepsilon E(t)\cdot x
is the external electric potential.

2.1
Bloch decomposition

As we mentioned before,
\varepsilon
indicates the typical size of the lattice. It is natural to use a change of variable
z=x/\varepsilon
. Then the Hamiltonian operator becomes

-\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{2}\Delta_{x}+V(\frac{x}{\varepsilon})-\varepsilon x\cdot E(t)=-\frac{1}{2}\Delta_{z}+V(z)-\varepsilon^{2}z\cdot E(t)

(2.3)

In the absence of the external potential, the Hamiltonian is given by

H_{\operatorname{per}}=-\frac{1}{2}\Delta_{z}+V(z).

(2.4)

It is translational invariant with respect to the lattice
\mathbb{L}
. As a
result, the spectrum of the Hamiltonian can be understood by the Bloch-Floquet
theory. In particular, we have the periodic Bloch wave functions
\Psi^{0}_{n}(z,p)
, given as the eigenvectors of

H^{0}(p)\Psi_{n}^{0}(z,p):=\left(\frac{1}{2}(-\mathrm{i}\nabla_{z}+p)^{2}+V(z)\right)\Psi_{n}^{0}(z,p)=\lambda_{n}(p)\Psi^{0}_{n}(z,p)

(2.5)

on
\Gamma
with periodic boundary conditions. Here
\Gamma
is the

unit cell
of lattice
\mathbb{L}
and
p\in\Gamma^{\ast}
is the
crystal momentum, where
\Gamma^{\ast}
denotes the
first Brillouin
zone
(unit cell of the reciprocal lattice). For each fixed
p\in\Gamma^{\ast}
, the Bloch Hamiltonian
H^{0}(p)
is a self-adjoint operator
with compact resolvent, the spectrum of which is given by

\sigma(H^{0}(p))=\{\lambda_{n}(p)\mid n\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}\}\subset\mathbb{R},

where the eigenvalues
\lambda_{n}(p)
(counting multiplicity) are increasingly
ordered
\lambda_{1}(p)\leq\cdots\leq\lambda_{n}(p)\leq\lambda_{n+1}(p)\leq\cdots
. The spectrum of
H^{0}
is exactly the same as that of
H_{per}
. It is shown by Nenciu
[
27
]
that for any

n\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
there exists a closed set
A_{n}\subset\Gamma^{\ast}
of
measure zero such that

\lambda_{n-1}(p)<\lambda_{n}(p)<\lambda_{n+1}(p),\quad p\in\Gamma^{\ast}\setminus A_{n},

(2.6)

and moreover
\lambda_{n}(p)
and
\Psi^{0}_{n}(\cdot,p)
are analytic on
p\in\Gamma^{\ast}\setminus A_{n}
.

Given
f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, we recall the
Bloch transform
,
which is an isometry from
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
to
L^{2}(\Gamma\times\Gamma^{\ast})

\mathfrak{B}f(z,p)=\frac{1}{\lvert\Gamma^{\ast}\rvert^{1/2}}\sum_{X\in\mathbb{L}}f(z+X)e^{-\mathrm{i}p\cdot(z+X)},

(2.7)

where
\lvert\Gamma\rvert
denotes the volume of the unit cell of the
lattice
\mathbb{L}
. The inverse transform is given by

f(z)=\frac{1}{\lvert\Gamma^{\ast}\rvert^{1/2}}\int_{\Gamma^{\ast}}e^{\mathrm{i}p\cdot z}\mathfrak{B}f(z,p)\hskip 1.69998pt\mathrm{d}p.

(2.8)

With the external potential
\varepsilon E(t)\cdot x
, our analysis needs perturbation of the Bloch
waves. For this, let us recall the perturbation theory in the context of Bloch
wave functions. Let a family of Hamiltonian
H^{\varepsilon}
denote the Bloch transform of the original Hamiltonian. We consider the asymptotic expansion for
H^{\varepsilon}(t)
as

H^{\varepsilon}(t)=H^{0}(t)+\varepsilon H^{1}(t)+\varepsilon^{2}H^{2}(t)+\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{3}).

(2.9)

where the zeroth order term is the Bloch Hamiltonian
H^{0}=H^{0}(p)=\tfrac{1}{2}(-\mathrm{i}\nabla_{z}+p)^{2}+V(z)
, which is time-independent. The slow-varying potential
\varepsilon E(t)\cdot x=\varepsilon^{2}E(t)\cdot z
corresponds a second order
correction to the unperturbed Hamiltonian.
More specifically, we note that using Bloch transformation, we have

zf(z)=\int_{\Gamma^{\ast}}ze^{\mathrm{i}p\cdot z}\mathfrak{B}f(z,p)\hskip 1.69998pt\mathrm{d}p=\int_{\Gamma^{\ast}}-\mathrm{i}\nabla_{p}e^{\mathrm{i}p\cdot z}\mathfrak{B}f(z,p)\hskip 1.69998pt\mathrm{d}p=\int_{\Gamma^{\ast}}\mathrm{i}e^{\mathrm{i}p\cdot z}\nabla_{p}\mathfrak{B}f(z,p)\hskip 1.69998pt\mathrm{d}p.

Hence, we arrived at the second order correction to the Hamiltonian, which is independent of
x

H^{2}(t)=-\mathrm{i}E(t)\cdot\nabla_{p}.

(2.10)

2.2
Berry connection

Let us now apply Bloch transform to (
2.1
). For a given wave function
\psi(t,x)\in L^{3}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
, we use an
L^{2}
-invariant rescaling,

\psi_{r}(t,z)=\varepsilon^{3/2}\psi(t,\varepsilon z),

(2.11)

and

\psi^{in}_{r}(t,z)=\varepsilon^{3/2}\psi^{in}(t,\varepsilon z).

It is easy to see that
\|\psi_{r}(t,z)\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}=\|\psi(t,x)\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}
. Recall that the Bloch transform defined by (
2.7
) is

\mathfrak{B}\psi_{r}(t,z,p)=\frac{1}{\lvert\Gamma^{\ast}\rvert^{1/2}}\sum_{X\in\mathbb{L}}\psi_{r}(t,z+X)e^{-\mathrm{i}p\cdot(z+X)}.

(2.12)

Apply the rescaling (
2.11
) and the Bloch transform to (
2.1
) and get

\displaystyle\mathrm{i}\varepsilon\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\mathfrak{B}\psi_{r}(t,z,p)=H^{\varepsilon}\mathfrak{B}\psi_{r}(t,z,p).

(2.13)

With the basis
\{\Psi^{0}_{n}\}_{n=0}^{+\infty}

\mathfrak{B}\psi_{r}
and
\mathfrak{B}\psi_{r}^{in}

\displaystyle\mathfrak{B}\psi_{r}(t,z,p)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{m}\hat{\psi}_{m}^{\varepsilon}(t,p)\Psi^{0}_{m}(z,p),

(2.14)

\displaystyle\mathfrak{B}\psi_{r}^{in}(z,p)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{m}\hat{\psi}_{m}^{in}(p)\Psi^{0}_{m}(z,p).

(2.15)

Here,
\hat{\psi}_{m}^{\varepsilon}
and
\hat{\psi}_{m}^{in}(p)

\displaystyle\hat{\psi}_{m}^{\varepsilon}(t,p)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle\mathfrak{B}\psi_{r}(t,z,p),\Psi^{0}_{m}(z,p)\rangle,

\displaystyle\hat{\psi}_{m}^{in}(p)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle\mathfrak{B}\psi_{r}^{in}(z,p),\Psi^{0}_{m}(z,p)\rangle.

and the inner product
\langle f,g\rangle
is given by

\langle f,g\rangle=\int_{\Gamma}\overline{f}(z)g(z)dz.

We label the variables of integral as the subscript of the right bracket and the region
of integral is the domain of the function.

Since
\mathfrak{B}
is an isometry, thanks to Parseval’s Theorem, it follows that

\sum_{i=0}^{+\infty}\|\hat{\psi}_{m}^{in}(p)\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma^{\ast})}=1.

For any initial data
\psi^{in}
, we can regard the wave function as a
superposition of its components on different energy bands. Since
(
2.1

Therefore, without loss of generality, we can assume that

\forall m\geqslant 1,\hat{\psi}_{m}^{in}(p)=0,

(2.16)

and

\|\hat{\psi}_{0}^{in}(p)\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma^{\ast})}=1.

(2.17)

This means we focus on the component of the 0-th energy band.

Therefore, we will assume the initial condition
\psi^{in}
in (
2.2
) is given by

\psi^{in}(x)=\varepsilon^{-3/2}\mathfrak{B}^{-1}(\hat{\psi}_{0}^{in}(p)\Psi^{0}_{0}(x/\varepsilon,p)).

(2.18)

After
\hat{\psi}_{0}^{in}
is chosen,
\psi^{in}
is determined. Here, we
assign
\hat{\psi}_{0}^{in}
as a smooth periodic function on
\Gamma^{\ast}
.
This is our ansatz for the initial data
\psi^{in}
.

Plugging the expansion (
2.14
) into (
2.13
) yields

\sum_{m}\mathrm{i}\varepsilon\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\hat{\psi}_{m}^{\varepsilon}(t,p)\Psi^{0}_{m}(z,p)=\sum_{m}H^{\varepsilon}(\hat{\psi}_{m}^{\varepsilon}(t,p)\Psi^{0}_{m}(z,p)).

(2.19)

Recall the expansion of
H^{\varepsilon}
given by

H^{\varepsilon}(t)=H^{0}(p)+\varepsilon^{2}H^{2}(t),\quad H^{0}(p)=\tfrac{1}{2}(-\mathrm{i}\nabla_{z}+p)^{2}+V(z),\quad H^{2}(t)=-\mathrm{i}E(t)\cdot\nabla_{p},

(2.20)

and that
\Psi^{0}_{m}(t,z,p)
is an eigenvector of
H^{0}(p)
. Using the expansion of
H^{\varepsilon}
leads to

\displaystyle\mathrm{i}\varepsilon\sum_{m}\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\hat{\psi}_{m}^{\varepsilon}(t,p)\Psi^{0}_{m}(z,p)=\sum_{m}\lambda_{m}(p)\hat{\psi}_{m}^{\varepsilon}(t,p)\Psi^{0}_{m}(z,p)

\displaystyle-\mathrm{i}\varepsilon^{2}E(t)\cdot\sum_{m}(\nabla_{p}\hat{\psi}_{m}^{\varepsilon}(t,p)\Psi^{0}_{m}(z,p)+\hat{\psi}_{m}^{\varepsilon}(t,p)\nabla_{p}\Psi^{0}_{m}(z,p)).

Take the inner product with
\Psi^{\varepsilon}_{n}
and obtain

\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\hat{\psi}_{n}^{\varepsilon}(t,p)+\frac{\mathrm{i}}{\varepsilon}\lambda_{n}(p)\hat{\psi}_{n}^{\varepsilon}(t,p)=-\varepsilon E(t)\cdot\nabla_{p}\hat{\psi}_{n}^{\varepsilon}(t,p)-\mathrm{i}\varepsilon E(t)\cdot\sum_{m}A_{nm}\hat{\psi}_{m}^{\varepsilon}(t,p).

(2.21)

Here, the vector
A_{nm}
, usually referred to as Berry connection, is defined
by

A_{nm}=\langle\Psi^{0}_{n},\mathrm{i}\nabla_{p}\Psi^{0}_{m}\rangle.

(2.22)

Since

\mathrm{i}\nabla_{p}\langle\Psi^{0}_{n},\Psi^{0}_{m}\rangle=\langle\Psi^{0}_{n},\mathrm{i}\nabla_{p}\Psi^{0}_{m}\rangle-\langle\mathrm{i}\nabla_{p}\Psi^{0}_{n},\Psi^{0}_{m}\rangle=0,

we know that

A_{mn}=\bar{A}_{nm}.

2.3
Density Matrix of Bloch Functions

We define the density matrix of Bloch functions as

c_{mn}=\hat{\psi}_{n}^{\ast}\hat{\psi}_{m}.

(2.23)

Here,
\hat{\psi}^{\ast}
means the conjugate of
\hat{\psi}
. Then we have

\frac{\partial}{\partial t}c_{mn}+\frac{\mathrm{i}}{\varepsilon}\lambda_{mn}(p)c_{mn}=-\varepsilon E(t)\cdot\nabla_{p}c_{mn}-\mathrm{i}\varepsilon E(t)\cdot\sum_{p}(A_{mp}c_{pn}-c_{mp}A_{pn}).

(2.24)

Here,
\lambda_{mn}
is given by

\lambda_{mn}=\lambda_{m}-\lambda_{n}.

(2.25)

The initial data is given by

c_{mn}^{in}(p)=(\hat{\psi}^{in}_{n}(p))^{\ast}\hat{\psi}_{m}^{in}(p)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}|\hat{\psi}^{in}_{0}(p)|^{2},&m=n=0,\\
0,&\operatorname{otherwise}.\end{array}\right.

(2.26)

When

, the second term of the right side of (

) contains
two terms

.

From (

), we can define the following characteristic line,

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\dot{p}(t)=\varepsilon E(t),\\
p(0)=p_{0},\end{array}\right.

(2.27)

and let
P_{t}(p_{0})
denote its solution. It is obvious that

P_{t}(p_{0})=p_{0}+\varepsilon\int_{0}^{t}E(s)ds.

(2.28)

For a function

, let
\breve{f}
denote

\breve{f}(t,p)=f(t,P_{t}(p)),

(2.29)

Applying the method of characteristic line,

\breve{c}_{mn}
satisfies the following equation

\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\breve{c}_{mn}+\frac{\mathrm{i}}{\varepsilon}\breve{\lambda}_{mn}(p)\breve{c}_{mn}=-\mathrm{i}\varepsilon E(t)\cdot\sum_{p}(\breve{A}_{mp}\breve{c}_{pn}-\breve{c}_{mp}\breve{A}_{pn}).

(2.30)

with initial data still given by

\breve{c}_{mn}^{in}(p)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}|\hat{\psi}^{in}_{0}(p)|^{2},&m=n=0,\\
0,&\operatorname{otherwise}.\end{array}\right.

2.4
Observables

Our goal is to find an asymptotic expansion of electric current
J
when
\varepsilon\rightarrow 0
. In this subsection, we start with deriving an expression of
J
with
c_{mn}
.

Here, we focus on some observables given by

O(t)=\int_{R^{d}}\psi_{r}(t,z)^{\ast}\mathcal{O}\psi_{r}(t,z)dz,

with
\mathcal{O}
denoting some self-adjoint operators. Here, recall that

\psi_{r}
is the rescaled wave function defined by (

). We will express
O
using
c_{mn}

defined by (

Since the Bloch transformation is an isometry, we have

\displaystyle O(t)=\langle\psi_{r},\mathcal{O}\psi_{r}\rangle=\langle\mathfrak{B}\psi_{r},\mathfrak{B}(\mathcal{O}\psi_{r})\rangle_{z,p}.

Let

\hat{\mathcal{O}}=\mathfrak{B}\circ\mathcal{O}\circ\mathfrak{B}^{-1}.

Then it follows that

\displaystyle O(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle\mathfrak{B}\psi_{r},\hat{\mathcal{O}}\mathfrak{B}\psi_{r}\rangle_{z,p}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{m,n}\langle\hat{\psi}^{\varepsilon}_{m}\Psi^{0}_{m},\hat{\mathcal{O}}(\hat{\psi}^{\varepsilon}_{n}\Psi^{0}_{n})\rangle_{z,p}.

If we further assume that

\hat{\mathcal{O}}(\hat{\psi}^{\varepsilon}_{n}\Psi^{0}_{n})=\hat{\psi}^{\varepsilon}_{n}\hat{\mathcal{O}}\Psi^{0}_{n},

then we get

Remark 1

Two common examples are

and

\mathcal{O}f(z)=-\mathrm{i}\nabla f(z)

for the second case later). They both

Hereafter, we will concentrate on the case where

. In
this case,

is just the current
J
defined by

J(t)=-\mathrm{i}\varepsilon\int_{R^{d}}\psi(t,x)^{\ast}\nabla_{x}\psi(t,x)dx.

(2.33)

In fact, we only need to notice that after rescaling,

can be rewritten as

J(t)=-\mathrm{i}\int_{R^{d}}\psi_{r}(t,z)^{\ast}\nabla_{z}\psi_{r}(t,z)dx.

(2.34)

It is easy to see that

\displaystyle\nabla_{z}(\mathfrak{B}\psi_{r})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\lvert\Gamma^{\ast}\rvert^{1/2}}\sum_{Y\in\mathbb{L}}\nabla_{z}(\psi_{r}(z+Y)e^{-\mathrm{i}p\cdot(z+Y)})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\lvert\Gamma^{\ast}\rvert^{1/2}}\sum_{Y\in\mathbb{L}}\nabla_{z}\psi_{r}(z+Y)e^{-\mathrm{i}p\cdot(z+Y)}-\frac{1}{\lvert\Gamma^{\ast}\rvert^{1/2}}\sum_{Y\in\mathbb{L}}\mathrm{i}p\psi_{r}(z+Y)e^{-\mathrm{i}p\cdot(z+Y)}

\displaystyle=\mathfrak{B}(\nabla_{z}\psi_{r})-\mathrm{i}p\mathfrak{B}\psi_{r}.

Therefore, we obtain

\hat{\mathcal{O}}=(-\mathrm{i}\nabla_{z}+p).

Then
J(t)=(J_{1}(t),J_{2}(t),J_{3}(t))
is given by

J_{k}(t)=\int_{\Gamma^{\ast}}\sum_{m,n}c_{nm}D^{k}_{mn}dp,\quad k=1,2,3.

3
Weyl nodes and effective Hamiltonian

The major difficulty for the asymptotic expansion of

defined by
(

) is the degeneracy of energy spectrum. Specifically, the degeneracy of energy gap leads to the singularity of Berry connections, which requires a multiscale analysis of the system. Therefore, in this part, we
will concentrate on crossing points of two bands. We will show that with some

an approximation of eigenvalues and eigenvectors when

still get a global expansion for the current

the contribution from the local structures of degenerated points and the
contribution from the regular region.

5

The proof of the main result involves several key steps. In this section, we proceed with calculating the asymptotic expansion of

Instead of

As mentioned before, the degeneracy of energy gap makes the problem difficult.
By Duhamel’s formula, we can rewrite (

and §

7
Conclusion

In this paper, we have developed an asymptotic analysis theory for the semiclassical model of Bloch electrons in the presence of Weyl nodes. The main result is a rigorous derivation of the asymptotic expansion of the current, which explicitly demonstrates the contribution from the singular points. The technical innovation lies in dealing with the multi-scale nature and singularities through a novel strategy of dividing the Brillouin zone into different regions and treating each appropriately. The quantitative estimates obtained justify the approximations used in the physics literature
[
9
]
to explain experimental observations.

This work opens up several directions for future research. One is to consider more complex models incorporating electron interactions or disorder. Another is to extend the analysis to other physical observables beyond the current. It would also be interesting to apply the expansion techniques developed here to other multiscale problems with singular features. Overall, this paper provides a mathematical foundation for studying topological effects in quantum dynamics, which will lead to many fruitful avenues for future exploration.

\displaystyle\varepsilon\sum_{j}c_{00}^{in}(p_{j}^{0})\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}\backslash B_{W_{j}}(0,r_{1})}\mathrm{i}\exp\left(\mathrm{i}\int_{0}^{t}|W_{j}\tilde{P}_{s}(p)|ds\right)\frac{E(0)\cdot D_{j,10}(p)\check{D}^{k}_{j,01}(t,p)}{|W_{j}p|^{2}}dp

\displaystyle+

E.4
The analysis of
L_{0}

Therefore, we can let

L_{0}=L_{r}+L_{s},

with

\displaystyle L_{r}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\int_{P_{t}^{-1}\left(\Gamma_{r}\bigcup\Gamma_{m}\right)}c_{00}^{in}(p)\operatorname{Re}\left(\frac{E^{\prime}(t)\cdot D_{01}(p)D^{k}_{10}(p)}{\lambda_{10}(p)^{3}}\right)dp

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle 2\sum_{j}\int_{B_{W_{j}}(p_{j}^{0},r_{2})\backslash P_{t}^{-1}(\Gamma_{s})}c_{00}^{in}(p_{j}^{0})\operatorname{Re}\left(\frac{E^{\prime}(t)\cdot D_{j,01}(p-p_{j}^{0})D^{k}_{j,10}(p-p_{j}^{0})}{|W_{j}(p-p_{j}^{0})|^{3}}\right)dp,

and

L_{s}=2\sum_{j}\int_{B_{W_{j}}(p_{j}^{0},r_{2})\backslash P_{t}^{-1}(\Gamma_{s})}c_{00}^{in}(p_{j}^{0})\operatorname{Re}\left(\frac{E^{\prime}(t)\cdot D_{j,01}(p-p_{j}^{0})D^{k}_{j,10}(p-p_{j}^{0})}{|W_{j}(p-p_{j}^{0})|^{3}}\right)dp.

Recall that around the Weyl node
p_{j}^{0}
, we have

\displaystyle D_{01}(p_{j}^{0}+\delta p)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle D_{j,01}(\delta p)+O(|\delta p|),

\displaystyle\lambda_{10}(p_{j}^{0}+\delta p)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle|W_{j}\delta p|+O(|\delta p|),

\displaystyle c_{00}^{in}(p_{j}^{0}+\delta p)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle c_{00}^{in}(p_{j}^{0})+O(|\delta p|).

Here
\delta p
is a small perturbation. If follows that

\frac{D_{01}(p_{j}^{0}+\delta p)D^{k}_{10}(p_{j}^{0}+\delta p)}{\lambda_{10}(p_{j}^{0}+\delta p)^{3}}=\frac{D_{j,01}(\delta p)D^{k}_{j,10}(\delta p)}{|W_{j}\delta p|^{3}}+O(|\delta p|^{-2}),

with the remainder integrable.
L_{r}
is not singular and can be rewritten as

\displaystyle L_{r}=

\displaystyle 2\operatorname{Re}E^{\prime}(t)\cdot\int_{P_{t}^{-1}\left(\Gamma_{r}\bigcup\Gamma_{m}\right)}\left(c_{00}^{in}(p)\frac{D_{01}(p)D^{k}_{10}(p)}{\lambda_{10}(p)^{3}}\right.

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle\sum_{j}I_{B_{W_{j}}(p_{j}^{0},r_{2})\backslash P_{t}^{-1}(\Gamma_{s})}(p)c_{00}^{in}(p_{j}^{0})\left.\frac{D_{j,01}(p-p_{j}^{0})D^{k}_{j,10}(p-p_{j}^{0})}{|W_{j}(p-p_{j}^{0})|^{3}}\right)dp

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\operatorname{Re}E^{\prime}(t)\cdot\int_{\Gamma^{\ast}}\left(c_{00}^{in}(p)\frac{D_{01}(p)D^{k}_{10}(p)}{\lambda_{10}(p)^{3}}\right.

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle\left.\sum_{j}c_{00}^{in}(p_{j}^{0})I_{B_{W_{j}}(p_{j}^{0},r_{2})}(p)\frac{D_{j,01}(p-p_{j}^{0})D^{k}_{j,10}(p-p_{j}^{0})}{|W_{j}(p-p_{j}^{0})|^{3}}\right)dp+o(1).

For

\displaystyle L_{s}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\operatorname{Re}\sum_{j}c_{00}^{in}(p_{j}^{0})\int_{B_{W_{j}}(p_{j}^{0},r_{2})}I_{|W_{j}(P_{t}^{-1}(p)-p_{j}^{0})|>\varepsilon r_{1}}\frac{2E^{\prime}(t)\cdot D_{j,01}(p-p_{j}^{0})D^{k}_{j,10}(p-p_{j}^{0})}{|W_{j}(p-p_{j}^{0})|^{3}}dp

\displaystyle 2\operatorname{Re}\sum_{j}c_{00}^{in}(p_{j}^{0})\int_{P_{t}(B_{W_{j}}(p_{j}^{0},r_{2}))\backslash B_{W_{j}}(p_{j}^{0},\varepsilon r_{1})}\frac{2E^{\prime}(t)\cdot\breve{D}_{j,01}(t,p-p_{j}^{0})\breve{D}_{j,10}^{k}(t,p-p_{j}^{0})}{|P_{t}(p)-p_{j}^{0}|^{3}}dp
