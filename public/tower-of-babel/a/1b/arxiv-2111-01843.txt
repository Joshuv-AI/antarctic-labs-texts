Visibility Properties of Spiral Sets
Faustin Adiceam and Ioannis Tsokanos

Abstract
. A spiral in ℝ d + 1 {\mathbb{R}}^{d+1} is defined as a set of the form { n d + 1 ⋅ 𝒖 n } n ≥ 1 , \left\{\sqrt[d+1]{n}\cdot\bm{u}_{n}\right\}_{n\geq 1}, where ( 𝒖 n ) n ≥ 1 \left(\bm{u}_{n}\right)_{n\geq 1} is a spherical sequence. Such point sets have been extensively studied, in particular in the planar case d = 1 d=1 , as they then serve as natural models describing phyllotactic structures (i.e. structures representing configurations of leaves on a plant stem). Recent progress in this theory provides a fine analysis of the distribution of spirals (e.g., their covering and packing radii). Here, various concepts of visiblity from discrete geometry are employed to characterise density properties of such point sets. More precisely, necessary an sufficient conditions are established for a spiral to be (1) an orchard (a “homogeneous” density property defined by Pòlya), (2) a uniform orchard (a concept introduced in this work), (3) a set with no visible point (implying that the point set is dense enough in a suitable sense) and (4) a dense forest (a quantitative and uniform refinement of the previous concept).

1.
Introduction

Define a spiral in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d+1}
as a set of points of the form

(1.1)

\mathfrak{S}_{d}\left(\bm{U}\right)\;=\;\left\{\sqrt[d+1]{n}\cdot\bm{u}_{n}\right\}_{n\geq 1},

where
\bm{U}=\left(\bm{u}_{n}\right)_{n\geq 1}
is a sequence in the
d
–dimensional unit sphere
\mathbb{S}^{d}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d+1}
. Such discrete point sets appear naturally in the modelisation of phyllotactic models (i.e. in the modelisation of configurations of leaves on a plant stem) and have long been studied in this respect. Recently, Akiyama
[
7
]
initiated a systematic study of their distribution properties. In the planar case
d=1
, he thus established that the spiral obtained from the circle sequence
\bm{U}=\left(\exp\left(2i\pi n\theta\right)\right)_{n\geq 1}
is
Delone
if and ony if
\theta
is a badly approximable number; that is, if and only if

(1.2)

\inf_{q\geq 1,r\in{\mathbb{Z}}}q\left|q\theta-r\right|>0.

Recall that a set is Delone in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d+1}
if it is both uniformly discrete (the infimum of the distances between two distinct points in the set is positive) and relatively dense (the supremum of the distances from any point in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d+1}
to the set is finite). Marklof
[
12
]
then generalised this result by providing a necessary and sufficient condition for a given planar spiral to be Delone.

The resulting question of determining whether this theory can be extended to higher dimensions was later settled by the authors : they indeed established in
[
5
]
a necessary and sufficient condition for a spiral set to be Delone in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d+1}
and described an explicit construction proving that such point sets do exists in any dimension. In particular, given a sequence
\left(\bm{u}_{n}\right)_{n\geq 1}
in
\mathbb{S}^{d}
and an increasing function
f~:{\mathbb{R}}_{+}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
, it is noticed in
[
5
, §1]
that a necessary condition for a point set of the form
\left\{f(n)\cdot\bm{u}_{n}\right\}_{n\geq 1}
to be uniformy discrete and relatively dense is that
0<\liminf_{n\rightarrow\infty}f(n)/\sqrt[d+1]{n}
and
\limsup_{n\rightarrow\infty}f(n)/\sqrt[d+1]{n}<\infty
, respectively. This motivates the choice of the factor
\sqrt[d+1]{n}
in the definition of a spiral set in (
1.1
).

The goal of this paper is to study distribution properties of spiral sets from a complementary standpoint; namely, from that of so–called visibility problems in discrete geometry which quantify in suitable senses the density of a point set. More precisely, the goal is to establish necessary and sufficient conditions for a spiral set to become arbitrarily close to line segments, provided they are long enough. This can be formalised in several distinct ways.

A first possible formalisation is motivated by Pólya’s classical orchard’s problem stated in
[
13
, Problem 239]
: “how thick must the trunks of the trees in a regularly spaced circular orchard grow if they are to block completely the view from the center?”. Assume that the observer stands at the origin, that the centres of the trees are located at the nonzero points of the lattice
{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}
and that they have Euclidean norms at most
Q>0
. Pólya (
ibid.
), based on a method due to Speiser, and then Honsberger
[
11
]
, based on Mikowski’s Convex Body Theorem, showed that it is enough for the trees to have radius
1/Q
to block any ray emerging from the origin. Slightly sharper estimates are also obtained by Allen
[
8
]
, and some generalisations studied in
[
4
, §4]
.

The following definition introduces the concept of an orchard in full generality. Before stating it, recall that a set
\mathcal{B}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d+1}
has
finite density
if

\limsup_{T\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\#\left(B_{2}\left(\bm{0},T\right)\cap\mathcal{B}\right)}{T^{d+1}}\;<\;\infty,

where
\#
denotes the cardinality function and where, given
\bm{x}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d+1}
and
T>0
,
B_{2}\left(\bm{x},T\right)
stands for the Euclidean ball centered at
\bm{x}
with radius
T>0
. In visibility problems, it is natural to work under the assumption that point sets have finite density for the visibility conditions under consideration not to be trivially met. It is easy to see that spiral sets defined in (
1.1
) have finite density.

Definition 1.1

(Orchard)
.

A subset
\mathcal{O}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d+1}\backslash\left\{\bm{0}\right\}
is an
orchard
if it has finite density and if there exists a function
V~:\epsilon\in(0,1)\rightarrow V(\epsilon)\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
, increasing as
\epsilon\rightarrow 0^{+}
, such that the following holds : for every
\epsilon>0
and every direction
\bm{v}\in\mathbb{S}^{d}
, there exists a point
\bm{o}\in\mathcal{O}
and a real number
0<t<V(\epsilon)
such that
\left\|\bm{o}-t\bm{v}\right\|_{2}\leq\epsilon
.

As is not hard to see, a visibility function
V
in an orchard in dimension
(d+1)
has to satisfy the bound

(1.3)

V(\epsilon)\geq c\cdot\epsilon^{-d}

for some constant
c>0
. This will be justified in detail in Section
2
.

In Definition
1.1
, a given point set is required to become arbitrary close to long enough line segments which have the origin as one of their end points. Removing this assumption while keeping the constraint that the line segments must be supported on directions passing through the origin leads one to the concept of a
uniform orchard
, which is introduced in this paper.

Definition 1.2

(Uniform Orchard)
.

A subset
\mathcal{O}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d+1}\backslash\left\{\bm{0}\right\}
is a
uniform orchard
if it has finite density and if there exists a function
V~:\epsilon\in(0,1)\rightarrow V(\epsilon)\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
, increasing as
\epsilon\rightarrow 0^{+}
, such that the following holds : for every
\epsilon>0
, every
t_{0}\in{\mathbb{R}}
and every direction
\bm{v}\in\mathbb{S}^{d}
, there exists a point
\bm{o}\in\mathcal{O}
and a real number
t_{0}<t<t_{0}+V(\epsilon)
such that
\left\|\bm{o}-t\bm{v}\right\|_{2}\leq\epsilon
.

Clearly, a uniform orchard is also an orchard (just take
t_{0}=0
in the above definition). The converse, however, does not hold and, despite the apparent similarity in their definitions, these two concepts should be thought of as being rather different in nature : indeed, Section
2
provides examples of an orchard and of a uniform orchard which have drastically different visibility properties (in a sense made precise therein).

Removing the assumption in Definitions
1.1
and
1.2
that the line segments should be supported on directions passing through the origin leads one to further concepts of visibility which have previously appeared in the literature — see, e.g.,
[
10
]
. In order to state them, define the
ray emanating from a point
\bm{x}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d+1}
in direction
\bm{v}\in\mathbb{S}^{d}
as the half–line

(1.4)

L(\bm{x},\bm{v})\;=\;\left\{\bm{x}+t\bm{v}\right\}_{t\geq 0}.

Also, given two subsets
\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d+1}
, set

\textrm{dist}_{2}(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})\;=\;\inf_{\bm{a}\in\mathcal{A},\bm{b}\in\mathcal{B}}\left\|\bm{a}-\bm{b}\right\|_{2}.

Definition 1.3

(Visible Points)
.

Let
\mathfrak{Y}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d+1}
be non–empty. Fix a direction
\bm{v}\in\mathbb{S}^{d}
.

The set of
visible points of
\mathfrak{Y}
in direction
\bm{v}
is defined as

\textrm{Vis}\left(\mathfrak{Y},\bm{v}\right)\;=\;\left\{\bm{x}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d+1}\>:\>\textrm{dist}_{2}\left(L(\bm{x},\bm{v}),\,\mathfrak{Y}\backslash\left\{\bm{x}\right\}\right)>0\right\}.

The set of
visible points of
\mathfrak{Y}
is defined as

\textrm{Vis}\left(\mathfrak{Y}\right)\;=\;\left\{\bm{x}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d+1}\>:\>\exists\bm{v}\in\mathbb{S}^{d},\;\bm{x}\in\textrm{Vis}\left(\mathfrak{Y},\bm{v}\right)\right\}.

The set of
hidden points of
\mathfrak{Y}
is the complement
{\mathbb{R}}^{d+1}\backslash\textrm{Vis}\left(\mathfrak{Y}\right)
.

Making the concept of
\epsilon
–hidden points more quantitative leads one to the concept of a
dense forest
.

Definition 1.4

(Dense Forest)
.

A subset
\mathcal{F}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d+1}
is an
dense forest
if it has finite density and if there exists a function
V~:\epsilon\in(0,1)\rightarrow V(\epsilon)\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
, increasing as
\epsilon\rightarrow 0^{+}
, such that the following holds : for every reals
\epsilon>0
and
t_{0}\in{\mathbb{R}}
, every vector
\bm{x}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d+1}
and every direction
\bm{v}\in\mathbb{S}^{d}
, there exists a point
\bm{f}\in\mathcal{F}
and a real number
t_{0}<t<t_{0}+V(\epsilon)
such that
\left\|\bm{x}+t\bm{v}-\bm{f}\right\|_{2}\leq\epsilon
.

Clearly, a dense forest is both a set whose hidden point set is the full space and a uniform orchard (and therefore also an orchard). The latter implies in particular that the lower bound (
1.3
) still holds for the visibility function in a dense forest.

The concept of a dense forest emerged in
[
9
]
in a question of rectifiability of curves and was later substantially developed in relation with the Danzer Problem, e.g. in
[
1
,
3
,
6
,
14
,
15
]
. The Danzer Problem asks for the existence of a set with finite density intersecting any convex body of volume one. Relaxing the assumption on the volume of the convex bodies gives rise to the construction of dense forests in this context : as detailed in
[
2
]
, a set solution to the Danzer problem in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d+1}
is a dense forest with visibility
V(\epsilon)=C\cdot\epsilon^{-d}
for some
C>0
(which is, up to the multiplicative constant, optimal in view of (
1.3
)), and the converse holds true in the planar case
d=1
.

Some of the visibility properties of the spiral
\mathfrak{S}_{d}\left(\bm{U}\right)
in (
1.1
) will be expressed in terms of a covering property of the spherical sequence
\bm{U}
introduced in the following definition. To this end, the natural geodesic metric on
\mathbb{S}^{d}
is denoted by
\textrm{dist}_{\mathbb{S}^{d}}\left(\,\cdot\,,\,\cdot\,\right)
.

Definition 1.5

(Uniform Covering Parameter of a Spherical Sequence)
.

Let
\bm{U}=\left(\bm{u}_{n}\right)_{n\geq 1}
be an infinite sequence in
\mathbb{S}^{d}
. Given a constant
C>0
, define its
uniform covering parameter at scale
C>0
as the quantity

R_{d}(\bm{U},C)\;=\;\sup_{\bm{v}\in\mathbb{S}^{d}}\;\;\;\sup_{m\geq 0}\;\;\;\sup_{N\geq 1}\;\;\;\sqrt[d]{N}\cdot\left(\inf_{1\leq n\leq C\cdot N}\textrm{dist}_{\mathbb{S}^{d}}\left(\bm{u}_{m+n},\bm{v}\right)\right).

In the case that the sequence
\bm{U}
is finite, say
\bm{U}=\left(\bm{u}_{n}\right)_{1\leq n\leq N}
for some integer
N\geq 1
, set

R_{d}(\bm{U})\;=\;\sup_{\bm{v}\in\mathbb{S}^{d}}\;\min_{1\leq n\leq N}\;\textrm{dist}_{\mathbb{S}^{d}}\left(\bm{u}_{n},\bm{v}\right).

The main result of this paper is then the following :

Theorem 1.6
.

Let
\mathfrak{S}_{d}\left(\bm{U}\right)
be a spiral point set in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d+1}
defined from a spherical sequence
\bm{U}=\left(\bm{u}_{n}\right)_{n\geq 1}
as in (
1.1
). Let
V~:\epsilon\in(0,1)\rightarrow V(\epsilon)\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
be a function increasing as
\epsilon\rightarrow 0^{+}
. Given constants
c_{\bm{U}}\geq 1
and
\kappa_{\bm{U}}>0
depending only on
\bm{U}
, define the auxiliary function

(1.5)

W~:\epsilon\mapsto c_{\bm{U}}\cdot V(\kappa_{\bm{U}}\cdot\epsilon).

Given a real
x\geq 0
and an integer
h\geq 1
, denote by
A_{\bm{U}}\left(h,x\right)
the finite set

(1.6)

A_{\bm{U}}\left(h,x\right)\;=\;\left\{\bm{u}_{h^{d+1}+i}\;:\;1\,\leq\,i\,\leq\,x\right\}\;\subset\;\mathbb{S}^{d}.

(1)

(Spirals and orchards)
The spiral
\mathfrak{S}_{d}\left(\bm{U}\right)
forms an orchard with visibility
W
for some constant
c_{\bm{U}}\geq 1
and
\kappa_{\bm{U}}>0
if and only if the following holds for some constants
\kappa,K>0
: for all
\epsilon>0
small enough and all
\bm{v}\in\mathbb{S}^{d}
, there exists an integer

such that

and
\textrm{dist}_{\mathbb{S}^{d}}\left(\bm{u}_{n},\bm{v}\right)\leq\kappa\cdot\epsilon/\sqrt[d+1]{n}
.

(2)

(Spirals and uniform orchards)
The following two claims are equivalent :

(a)

There exist constants
c_{\bm{U}}\geq 1
and
\kappa_{\bm{U}}>0
such that the spiral
\mathfrak{S}_{d}\left(\bm{U}\right)
is a uniform orchard with visibility function
W
.

(b)

There exist constants

\sup_{\epsilon\in(0,1)}\;\sup_{h\geq W\left(\epsilon\right)}\;h\cdot\epsilon^{-1}\cdot R_{d}\left(A_{\bm{U}}\left(h,\,K\cdot h^{d}\cdot W\left(\epsilon\right)\right)\right)\;<\;\infty,

is defined as in (

).

Furthermore :

(i)

whenever

.

(ii)

a sufficient condition for the set
\mathfrak{S}_{d}\left(\bm{U}\right)

\lim_{x\rightarrow\infty}\left(\sup_{h>x}\,h\cdot R_{d}\left(A_{\bm{U}}\left(h,h^{d}x\right)\right)\right)\;=\;0

should hold. In this case, there exist constants
c_{\bm{U}}\geq 1
and
\kappa_{\bm{U}}>0
such that it admits the function
W
defined in (
1.5
) as a visibility function obtained from the map

(1.7)

V~:\epsilon\mapsto\sup\left\{x\geq 0\;:\;\sup_{h>x}\,h\cdot R_{d}\left(A_{\bm{U}}\left(h,h^{d}x\right)\right)\geq\epsilon\right\}.

(3)

(Visible points in spirals)
The following two claims hold :

(i)

The spiral
\mathfrak{S}_{d}\left(\bm{U}\right)
has an empty set of visible points if and only if the following holds for some constant
c>0
: for any
\epsilon>0
, any
\lambda\geq 0
and any choice of orthogonal directions
\bm{v},\bm{w}\in\mathbb{S}^{d}

(1.8)

\left|\sqrt[d+1]{n}-\sqrt{\lambda^{2}+t^{2}}\right|\leq\epsilon\qquad\textrm{and}\qquad\textrm{dist}_{\mathbb{S}^{d}}\left(\bm{u}_{n},\frac{\lambda\bm{v}+t\bm{w}}{\sqrt{\lambda^{2}+t^{2}}}\right)\leq\frac{c\cdot\epsilon}{\sqrt{\lambda^{2}+t^{2}}}.\\

are satisfied.

(ii)

A spiral set which is a uniform orchard has an empty set of visible points (in other words, all points in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d+1}
are hidden). The converse, however, does not hold.

(4)

(Spirals and dense forests)
The spiral
\mathfrak{S}_{d}\left(\bm{U}\right)
is a dense forest with visibility function
W
for some constants
c_{\bm{U}}\geq 1
and
\kappa_{\bm{U}}>0

such that for any
\epsilon>0
,

and any choice of orthogonal directions

2.
Proof of the Main Theorem

The claim on the lower bound (
1.3
) for a visibility function in an orchard is first established. The proof rests on the following relations, which are readily verified : given reals
\rho,r>0
and unit vectors
\bm{u},\bm{v}\in\mathbb{S}^{d}
,

(2.1)

\textrm{dist}_{\mathbb{S}^{d}}\left(\bm{u},\bm{v}\right)\;\asymp\;\left\|\bm{u}-\bm{v}\right\|_{2}\qquad\textrm{and }\qquad\left\|r\bm{u}-\rho\bm{v}\right\|_{2}\;\asymp\;\left|r-\rho\right|+\sqrt{r\rho}\left\|\bm{u}-\bm{v}\right\|_{2}

(working in the plane spanned by
\bm{u}
and
\bm{v}
, the second relation is easily deduced from the usual formula for the Euclidean distance between two points expressed in polar coordinates.)

Proof of the lower bound (
1.3
).

Let
\mathcal{O}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d+1}\backslash\left\{\bm{0}\right\}
be an orchard with visibility function
V
. Enumerate the points in
\mathcal{O}
in a set
\left\{\bm{x}_{k}=\rho_{k}\bm{u}_{k}\right\}_{k\geq 1}
in such a way that
\left\|\bm{x}_{k}\right\|_{2}=\rho_{k}\leq\left\|\bm{x}_{k+1}\right\|_{2}=\rho_{k+1}
for all
k\geq 1
(in particular,
\bm{u}_{k}\in\mathbb{S}^{d}
for all
k\geq 1
). Since
\mathcal{O}
has finite density,

(2.2)

\rho_{k}\gg\sqrt[d+1]{k}.

By assumption, given a direction
\bm{v}\in\mathbb{S}^{d}
, there exists a real number

(2.3)

\rho\in\left(0,V\left(\epsilon\right)\right)

and an index
k\geq 1
such that
\epsilon\;\geq\;\left\|\rho\bm{v}-\rho_{k}\bm{u}_{k}\right\|_{2}
. From (
2.1
), this implies that
\rho=\rho_{k}+\kappa\cdot\epsilon
for some
\kappa\in{\mathbb{R}}
. If
\epsilon\ll\min_{j\geq 1}\rho_{j}
(which may be assumed without loss of generality), then

(2.4)

\rho_{k}\asymp\rho.

Consequently, again from (
2.1
),
\textrm{dist}_{\mathbb{S}^{d}}\left(\bm{u}_{k},\bm{v}\right)\;\asymp\;\left\|\bm{u}_{k}-\bm{v}\right\|_{2}\ll\epsilon/\rho_{k}.

As this holds for any direction
\bm{v}\in\mathbb{S}^{d}
, it follows that the successive spherical caps centered at
\bm{u}_{k}
with radii
C\cdot\epsilon/\rho_{k}
, where
1\leq k\ll V(\epsilon)^{d+1}
and where
C>0
is a constant independent of
k
, cover the sphere
\mathbb{S}^{d}
. Here, the upper bound for the index
k
is obtained by combining relations (
2.2
), (
2.3
) and(
2.4
).

Denoting by
A_{d}
the surface area of
\mathbb{S}^{d}
, one thus deduces that, for some constant
C>0
,

A_{d}\;\ll\;\sum_{k=1}^{C\cdot V\left(\epsilon\right)^{d+1}}\frac{\epsilon^{d}}{\rho_{k}^{d}}\;\ll\;\sum_{k=1}^{V\left(\epsilon\right)^{d+1}}\frac{\epsilon^{d}}{k^{d/(d+1)}}\;\asymp\;\epsilon^{d}\cdot V\left(\epsilon\right).

The claim follows.
∎

The direct implication in Point 3(ii) in Theorem
1.6
is now proved seperately from the other points. This is because it holds in a generality greater than that of spiral point sets as detailed in the following proposition.

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
\mathcal{O}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d+1}\backslash\left\{\bm{0}\right\}
be a uniform orchard. Then, its set of visible points is empty.

Proof.

Let
V
denote a visibility function for the uniform orchard
\mathcal{O}
. Fix a vector
\bm{x}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d+1}
, a direction
\bm{v}\in\mathbb{S}^{d}
and a real
\epsilon>0
. The goal is to show that
\textrm{dist}_{2}\left(L(\bm{x},\bm{v}),\mathcal{O}\right)\leq\epsilon
, where
L(\bm{x},\bm{v})
is the ray emanating from
\bm{x}
in direction
\bm{v}
as defined in (
1.4
).

Let
\bm{v}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{S}^{d}
be a direction such that
L(\bm{x},\bm{v})\cap L(\bm{0},\bm{v}^{\prime})\neq\emptyset
and

(2.5)

\left\|\bm{v}-\bm{v}^{\prime}\right\|_{2}=\epsilon\cdot\left(4\pi\cdot V\left(\epsilon/2\right)\right)^{-1}.

Set
\left\{\bm{w}\right\}=L(\bm{x},\bm{v})\cap L(\bm{0},\bm{v}^{\prime})
and let
t_{0}\geq 0
be such that
\bm{w}=t_{0}\bm{v}^{\prime}
. Since
\mathcal{O}
is a uniform orchard, there exists

(2.6)

0\leq t\leq V\left(\epsilon/2\right)

and
\bm{y}\in\mathcal{O}
such that

(2.7)

\left\|(t_{0}+t)\cdot\bm{v}^{\prime}-\bm{y}\right\|_{2}\;=\;\left\|\bm{w}+t\cdot\bm{v}^{\prime}-\bm{y}\right\|_{2}\;\leq\;\frac{\epsilon}{2}.

Consequently,

\displaystyle\textrm{dist}_{2}\left(L(\bm{x},\bm{v}),\mathcal{O}\right)\;

\displaystyle\leq\;\left\|\left(\bm{w}+t\cdot\bm{v}\right)-\bm{y}\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\;\left\|\bm{w}+t\cdot\bm{v}^{\prime}-\bm{y}\right\|_{2}+t\cdot\left\|\bm{v}-\bm{v}^{\prime}\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle\underset{\eqref{dotprod},\eqref{boundt}\&\eqref{distmeas}}{\leq}\;\epsilon.

This completes the proof.
∎

Proposition
2.1
justifies the claim made in the introduction, namely that the concepts of an orchard on the one hand and that of a uniform orchard on the other are rather different in nature when considering their visibility properties. This can already be seen in the case of spiral sets, which can be both orchards and have a non–empty set of visible points (compare with the statement of Proposition
2.1
). As established in the following statement, an example of such spiral in the plane is obtained by considering the point set

\Lambda=\left\{\sqrt{n}\cdot\exp\left(2i\pi\cdot p/k\right)\right\}_{n\geq 1}.

Here, given an integer
n\geq 1
, the integers
k
and
p
are defined by the unique decomposition of
n
as

(2.8)

n=\frac{k(k+1)}{2}+p\qquad\textrm{with}\qquad k\geq 1\qquad\textrm{and}\qquad 0\leq p\leq k.

Proposition 2.2
.

The spiral
\Lambda
is an orchard with visibility
V(\epsilon)\ll\epsilon^{-1}
but has a non–empty set of visible points.

The spiral
\Lambda
is depicted in Figure
2
below. In the same way as for the standard lattice in Pólya’s original orchard problem, the points close to the origin play a preponderant role in blocking rays emanating from the origin. By constrast, horizontal half–lines not passing through the origin but close to it determine visible points.

Proof.

Let
\theta\in\left[0,1\right]
and
N\geq 3
. From Dirichlet’s Theorem in Diophantine approximation, there exists a rational
p/k\in\left[0,1\right]
with
1\leq k\leq N
such that
\left|\theta-p/k\right|\leq 1/(kN)
. Therefore,
\left|\exp\left(2i\pi\theta\right)-\exp\left(2i\pi(p/k)\right)\right|\leq 2\pi/(kN)
. Setting
n=k(k+1)/2+p
, one thus gets that

\displaystyle\left|\sqrt{n}\cdot\exp\left(2i\pi\theta\right)-\sqrt{n}\cdot\exp\left(2i\pi\frac{p}{k}\right)\right|\;\leq\;\sqrt{\left(\frac{k(k+1)}{2}+p\right)}\cdot\frac{2\pi}{kN}\;\leq\;\frac{4\pi}{N}\cdot

The visibility claim then follows upon noticing that
\sqrt{n}\leq N
when
N\geq 3
.

The claim that the set of visible points is non–empty is implied by the existence of a vacant strip of the form
0<y<\delta
for some
\delta>0
: this is saying that the set of points
(x,y)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
such that their second coordinate satisfies this contraint does not contain any point of the spiral. To see this, note that the distance between a point
\sqrt{n}\cdot\exp\left(2i\pi\frac{p}{k}\right)
in the spiral set (identified with the planar point
\sqrt{n}\left(\cos\left(2\pi p/k\right),\,\sin\left(2\pi p/k\right)\right)
) and the axis
\left\{y=0\right\}
is
\sqrt{n}\left|\sin\left(2\pi p/k\right)\right|
. This quantity is non–zero if and only if
p\not\in\left\{0,k\right\}
, in which case

\sqrt{n}\left|\sin\left(2\pi p/k\right)\right|\;\geq\;\frac{\sqrt{n}}{k}\;\underset{\eqref{defn}}{\gg}\;1.

This concludes the proof.

∎
