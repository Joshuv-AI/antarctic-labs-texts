Two-phase flows through porous media described by a Cahn–Hilliard–Brinkman model with dynamic boundary conditions
Pierluigi Colli, Patrik Knopf, Giulio Schimperna, Andrea Signori

Abstract




Abstract

We investigate a new diffuse-interface model that describes creeping two-phase flows (i.e., flows exhibiting a low Reynolds number), especially flows that permeate a porous medium. The system of equations consists of a Brinkman equation for the volume averaged velocity field as well as a convective Cahn–Hilliard equation with dynamic boundary conditions for the phase-field, which describes the location of the two fluids within the domain. The dynamic boundary conditions are incorporated to model the interaction of the fluids with the wall of the container more precisely. In particular, they allow for a dynamic evolution of the contact angle between the interface separating the fluids and the boundary, and also for a convection-induced motion of the corresponding contact line. For our model, we first prove the existence of global-in-time weak solutions in the case where regular potentials are used in the Cahn–Hilliard subsystem. In this case, we can further show the uniqueness of the weak solution under suitable additional assumptions. Moreover, we further prove the existence of weak solutions in the case of singular potentials. Therefore, we regularize such singular potentials by a Yosida approximation, such that the results for regular potentials can be applied, and eventually pass to the limit in this approximation scheme.

Keywords:
two-phase flows, porous media, Cahn–Hilliard equation, Brinkman equation, dynamic boundary conditions, bulk-surface interaction.

1  Introduction

The mathematical study of two-phase flows is an important topic in many areas of applied science such as engineering, chemistry and biology. To predict the motion of two fluids, it is crucial to understand how the interface between these fluids evolves.
To provide a mathematical description of this interface, two fundamental methods have been developed: the
sharp-interface approach
and the
diffuse-interface approach
.
In the former, the interface is represented as a hypersurface evolving in the surrounding domain. The occurring quantities (e.g., the velocity fields) are then described by a free boundary problem.
In the latter, the fluids are represented by a
phase-field
function which is expected to attain values close to
1
in the region where the first fluid is present, and close to
-1
in the region where the second fluid is located. However, unlike in sharp-interface models, this phase-field does not jump between the values
1
and
-1
but exhibits a continuous transition between these values in a thin tubular neighborhood around the boundary between the fluids. This tubular neighborhood is referred to as the
diffuse interface
and its thickness is proportional to a small parameter
\epsilon>0
.
For a comparison of sharp-interface methods and diffuse-interface methods, we refer to
[
28
,
4
,
35
,
55
]
.
We point out that, even though the sharp-interface and the diffuse-interface approach are conceptually different, they can, in general, be related by the
sharp-interface limit
in which a parameter related to the thickness of the diffuse interface is sent to zero.

In the context of diffuse-interface models, such models in which the phase-field is described by a Cahn–Hilliard type equation have become particularly popular. One of the most widely used models for describing the motion of two viscous, incompressible fluids with matched (constant) densities is the
Model H
.
It was first proposed in
[
45
]
and was later rigorously derived in
[
43
]
. The PDE system consists of an incompressible Navier–Stokes equation coupled with a convective Cahn–Hilliard equation.
In terms of mathematical analysis, the Model H was investigated quite extensively, see, e.g., in
[
1
,
10
,
30
,
41
]
.
Further generalizations of this model can be found in
[
51
,
11
,
27
,
57
,
58
,
35
,
44
,
29
]
.

One drawback of the Model H is that it can merely be used to describe the situation in which the fluids have the same individual density. To overcome this issue, a thermodynamically consistent diffuse-interface model for incompressible two-phase flows with possibly
unmatched
densities was derived in the seminal work
[
6
]
. This model is usually referred to as the
AGG model
.
Concerning mathematical analysis of this model, we refer the reader to

[
2
,
3
,
7
,
39
,
38
,
5
]
.
The connection between the AGG model and the two-phase Navier–Stokes free boundary problem is explained in
[
6
,
4
]
.

Even though the AGG model and the Model H subject to the classical boundary conditions (i.e., a
no-slip
boundary condition for the velocity field and homogeneous Neumann boundary conditions for the convective Cahn–Hilliard equation) are well suited to describe the motion of the fluids in the the interior of the considered domain, they still inherit some limitations from the underlying (convective) Cahn–Hilliard system with homogeneous Neumann boundary conditions.
The main limitations are:

(L1)

The homogeneous Neumann condition on the phase-field enforces that the diffuse interface always intersects the boundary at a perfect ninety degree contact angle. This will not be fulfilled in many applications. In general, the contact angle might even change dynamically over the course of time.

(L2)

The no-slip boundary condition on the velocity field makes the model not very suitable to describe general moving contact line phenomena. As the trace of the velocity field at the boundary is fixed to be identically zero, any motion of the contact line of the diffuse interface can be caused only by diffusive but not by convective effects.

(L3)

The mass of the fluids in the bulk is conserved. Therefore, a transfer of material between the bulk and the boundary (caused, e.g., by absorption processes or chemical reactions) cannot be described.

A more detailed discussion can be found in
[
40
]
.

To overcome the aforementioned restrictions (L1) and (L2), a class of dynamic boundary conditions was derived in
[
56
]
. It involves an Allen–Cahn type dynamic boundary condition for the phase-field coupled to a generalized Navier-slip boundary condition for the velocity field. The Model H subject to this boundary condition was analyzed in
[
31
]
whereas the AGG model subject to this boundary condition was investigated in
[
32
]
.

Recently, a thermodynamically consistent generalization of the AGG model subject to another class of dynamic boundary conditions was derived in
[
40
]
. Here, the boundary condition consists of a convective surface Cahn–Hilliard equation and a generalized Navier-slip boundary condition. Compared to the models studied in
[
56
,
32
,
31
]
, the Navier–Stokes–Cahn–Hilliard system introduced in
[
40
]
provides more regularity for the boundary quantities and therefore, the uniqueness of weak solutions can be established in two space dimensions. Moreover, due to the fourth-order dynamic boundary condition of Cahn–Hilliard type, the model in
[
40
]
is not only capable of overcoming the limitations (L1) and (L2) but also (L3).

In the present paper, we particularly want to consider the situation of
creeping flows
meaning that the Reynolds number

\displaystyle\mathrm{Re}=\frac{uL}{\nu}

associated with the fluids is very small (
\mathrm{Re}\ll 1
). This occurs if the flow speed
u
and/or the characteristic length
L
of the flow are small compared to the kinematic viscosity
\nu
. In this situation, it is not necessary to describe the time evolution of the velocity field by the full Navier–Stokes equation. Since advective inertial forces are small compared to viscous forces, the material derivative can be neglected. This leads to the
Stokes equation
. If a creeping flow through a porous medium is to be considered, an additional term accounting for the permeability needs to be included. The velocity field is then determined by the
Brinkman equation
.

Therefore, in this paper, we study the following
Cahn–Hilliard–Brinkman

The vector-valued function

The functions

\psi:\Sigma\to{\mathbb{R}}
and

represent the phase-field and the chemical potential on the boundary, respectively. Furthermore, the parameters
\epsilon
and

are positive real numbers which are related to the thickness of the diffuse interface in the bulk and on the surface, respectively. Therefore, these constants are usually chosen to be quite small. However, as their values do not have any impact on the mathematical analysis, we will simply fix

\varphi
and

are directly related by the coupling condition (

is a given nonnegative constant.

From a physical point of view, the
kinematic viscosity

\nu(\varphi)
and the
permeability coefficient

\lambda(\varphi)
in the Brinkman/Stokes equation (
1.1a
) can be expressed as

\displaystyle\nu(\varphi)=\frac{\eta(\varphi)}{\varrho}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{and}\hskip 10.00002pt\lambda(\varphi)=\frac{\eta(\varphi)}{\varkappa},

where
\eta(\varphi)>0
denotes the

, and the constants

of the porous medium, respectively.
If both
\nu(\varphi)
and
\lambda(\varphi)
are positive, (
1.1a
) is the

which describes the flow through a porous medium.
However, if the porosity
\varkappa
is large compared to the viscosity
\eta(\varphi)
, the function
\lambda(\varphi)
is very small and can be neglected.
In this case we enter the
Stokes regime
, where no porous media is considered (or the effects of the porous medium are at least negligible). In the formal limit
\varkappa\to\infty
or
\lambda(\varphi)\to 0
, (
1.1a
) degenerates to the

. In our analysis, we will be able to handle the Brinkman case (
\nu(\varphi)>0

\lambda(\varphi)>0
) and the Stokes case (
\nu(\varphi)>0
and
\lambda(\varphi)\equiv 0
) simultaneously. On the other hand, if
\lambda(\varphi)
remains positive and the porosity
\varrho
is large compared to the dynamic viscosity
\eta(\varphi)
such that
\nu(\varphi)
can be neglected, (
1.1a
) degenerates to
Darcy’s law
. However, we are not able to handle this case in terms of mathematical analysis as due to the absence of spatial derivatives of the velocity field in (
1.1a
), we would not obtain enough regularity to define the trace of
{\boldsymbol{v}}
on the boundary in a reasonable manner.

The functions
F^{\prime}
and
G^{\prime}
are the derivatives of double-well potentials

and
G
, respectively.
Especially in applications related to materials science, a physically relevant choice for

and/or

is the

, which is also referred to as the

. It is given by

\displaystyle W_{\mathrm{log}}(s)=\frac{\Theta}{2}\big[(1+s)\,\ln(1+s)+(1-s)\,\ln(1-s)\big]+\frac{\Theta_{c}}{2}(1-s^{2}),

for all

is the absolute temperature of the mixture, and

is a critical temperature such that phase separation will occur in case

. The logarithmic potential is classified as a singular potential since its derivative

diverges to

when its argument approaches
\pm 1
. It is often approximated by a
polynomial double-well potential

\displaystyle W_{\mathrm{pol}}(s)=\frac{\alpha}{4}(s^{2}-1)^{2}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{for all $s\in(-1,1)$},

(1.3)

where
\alpha>0
is a suitable constant.
Another very commonly used singular potential is the
double-obstacle potential
, which is given by

\displaystyle W_{\mathrm{obst}}(s)=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{2}(1-s^{2})&\text{if $|s|\leq 1$,}\\
+\infty&\text{else}.\end{cases}

(1.4)

In the case
K=0
, the convective bulk-surface Cahn–Hilliard subsystem (

In contrast to the model introduced in
[

, the phase-fields
\varphi
and

for an Allen–Cahn type dynamic boundary condition, and later in
[

for a Cahn–Hilliard type dynamic boundary condition. In particular, it was rigorously shown in

, the Dirichlet type coupling condition for the phase-fields already fixes one degree of freedom and is therefore a bad match for the no-mass-flux boundary condition
\eqref{eq:8}_{1}

) with

, such problems do not arise. Hence, our strategy is to first prove the existence of weak solutions for

An important property of the system (

) (for any

) is its thermodynamic consistency with respect to the free energy functional

\displaystyle E_{K}(\varphi,\psi)

\displaystyle:=\int_{\Omega}\Big(\frac{\epsilon}{2}|\nabla\varphi|^{2}+\frac{1}{\epsilon}F(\varphi)\Big)+\int_{\Gamma}\Big(\frac{\epsilon_{\Gamma}}{2}|\nabla_{\Gamma}\psi|^{2}+\frac{1}{\epsilon_{\Gamma}}G(\psi)\Big)

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt+\frac{\sigma(K)}{2}\int_{\Gamma}(\psi-\varphi)^{2},

This means that sufficiently regular solutions of (

\displaystyle\frac{1}{|\Omega|}\int_{\Omega}\varphi(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|\Omega|}\int_{\Omega}\varphi_{0}=:m_{0}

The structure of the paper is as follows. In Section
2
we collect some notation, assumptions, preliminaries and important tools as well as the main results of our work. In the case of regular potentials, the Cahn–Hilliard–Brinkman system is analyzed in Section
3
. There, we first establish the existence of a weak solution in the case
K>0
, where phase-fields are coupled by a Robin type boundary condition. By sending

, we then obtain the existence of a weak solution also in the case
K=0
, where the phase-fields are coupled by a trace relation. Afterwards, we prove uniqueness in all cases

under suitable additional assumptions. Eventually, in Section

, we show the existence of a weak solution to the Cahn–Hilliard–Brinkman system with singular potentials. This is done based on the results established in Section
3
by approximating the singular potentials by means of Yosida regularizations and finally passing to the limit.

2.2  General assumptions

(A1)

2.3  Preliminaries

Throughout the paper, we will frequently use the following bulk-surface Poincaré inequality:

Lemma 2.1
.

There exists a constant
C_{P}^{\prime}
depending only on
\Omega
such that

\|u\|_{H}\leq C_{P}^{\prime}\left(\|\nabla u\|_{{\bf H}}+\|u\|_{{H}_{\Gamma}}\right)\hskip 10.00002pt\text{for all $u\in V$.}

(2.3)

A proof of this inequality can be found, for instance, in
[
61
, Chapter II, Section 1.4]
.

We further recall the following result on interpolation between Sobolev spaces:

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
U\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
with
m\in\mathbb{N}
be a bounded Lipschitz domain, and suppose that
\theta\in(0,1)
and
r,s_{0},s_{1}\in{\mathbb{R}}
satisfy

r=(1-\theta)s_{0}+\theta s_{1}.

We further assume that
U
is of class
C^{\ell}
with an integer
\ell\geq\max\{s_{0},s_{1}\}
.
Then, there exist positive constants
C_{U}
and
C_{\partial U}
depending only on
U
,
r
,
s_{0}
,
s_{1}
and
\theta
such that the following interpolation inequalities hold:

\displaystyle\|f\|_{H^{r}(U)}

\displaystyle\leq C_{U}\|f\|_{H^{s_{0}}(U)}^{1-\theta}\|f\|_{H^{s_{1}}(U)}^{\theta},

(2.4)

\displaystyle\|f\|_{H^{r}(\partial U)}

\displaystyle\leq C_{\partial U}\|f\|_{H^{s_{0}}(\partial U)}^{1-\theta}\|f\|_{H^{s_{1}}(\partial U)}^{\theta}.

(2.5)

Inequality (
2.4
) follows from an interpolation result shown in
[
62
, Sec 4.3.1, Theorem 1 and Remark 1]
, whereas (
2.5
) follows from an interpolation result presented in
[
63
, Sec 7.4.5, Remark 2]
.

(R1)

2.4.2 Definition of weak solutions for regular potentials

Definition 2.3

Let

be arbitrary.
Suppose that
(A1)
–
(A3)
and

are fulfilled and let

A quintuplet

\displaystyle\in L^{2}(0,T;{\bf V}_{\sigma,{\bf n}}),\hskip 10.00002pt{\boldsymbol{v}}|_{\Gamma}\in L^{2}(0,T;{\bf H}_{\Gamma}),

(ii)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &2\int_{\Omega}\nu(\varphi)\mathbf{D}{\boldsymbol{v}}:\mathbf{D}{\boldsymbol{w}}+\int_{\Omega}\lambda(\varphi){\boldsymbol{v}}\cdot{\boldsymbol{w}}+\int_{\Gamma}\gamma(\psi){\boldsymbol{v}}\cdot{\boldsymbol{w}}\\

holds a.e. in

\displaystyle E_{K}\big(\varphi(t),\psi(t)\big)+2\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}\nu(\varphi)|\mathbf{D}{\boldsymbol{v}}|^{2}+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}\lambda(\varphi)|{\boldsymbol{v}}|^{2}+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Gamma}\gamma(\psi)|{\boldsymbol{v}}|^{2}

\displaystyle\hskip 20.00003pt+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}M_{\Omega}(\varphi)|\nabla\mu|^{2}+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Gamma}M_{\Gamma}(\psi)|\nabla_{\Gamma}\theta|^{2}

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\leq E_{K}(\varphi_{0},\psi_{0})

holds for all

.

2.5  The Cahn–Hilliard–Brinkman system with singular potentials

We now consider the system (
1.1
) for a general class of singular potentials. For those, we manage to establish just existence of weak solutions due to the lower regularity at disposal. Recall that
\epsilon=\epsilon_{\Gamma}=1
as mentioned above.

2.5.1 Assumptions for singular potentials

For the potentials
F
and
G
, we now make the following assumptions.

(S1)

The potentials
F
and
G
can be decomposed as
F=\widehat{\beta}+\widehat{\pi}
and
G={\widehat{\beta}}_{\Gamma}+{\widehat{\pi}}_{\Gamma}
.

Here,
\widehat{\beta},{\widehat{\beta}}_{\Gamma}:{\mathbb{R}}\to[0,\infty]
are lower semicontinuous and convex functions with
\widehat{\beta}(0)=0
and

{\widehat{\beta}}_{\Gamma}(0)=0
.
For brevity, we define

\displaystyle\beta:=\partial\widehat{\beta}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{and}\hskip 10.00002pt\beta_{\Gamma}:=\partial{\widehat{\beta}_{\Gamma}}.

Moreover, we suppose that
\widehat{\pi},{\widehat{\pi}}_{\Gamma}\in C^{1}({\mathbb{R}})
with Lipschitz continuous derivatives
\pi:=\widehat{\pi}^{\prime}
and
\pi_{\Gamma}:={\widehat{\pi}}_{\Gamma}^{\prime}
.

We point out that
\beta
and
{\beta_{\Gamma}}
are maximal monotone graphs in
{\mathbb{R}}\times{\mathbb{R}}
whose effective domains are denoted by
D(\beta)
and
D(\beta_{\Gamma})
, respectively.
In particular, as
0
is a minimum point of both
\widehat{\beta}
and
{\widehat{\beta}}_{\Gamma}
, it turns out that
0\in\beta(0)
and
0\in{\beta_{\Gamma}}(0)
.
Finally, we denote by
\beta^{\circ}
the
minimal section
of the graph
\beta
, which is defined as

\displaystyle\beta^{\circ}(r)\coloneqq\Big\{r^{\ast}\in D(\beta):\left|r^{\ast}\right|=\min_{s\in\beta(r)}\left|s\right|\Big\}\hskip 20.00003pt\text{for all $r\in D(\beta)$}

(see, e.g.,
[
12
]
).
The same definition applies to
\beta^{\circ}_{\Gamma}
for
{\beta_{\Gamma}}
.

(S2)

We also assume the growth condition

\displaystyle\lim_{r\to+\infty}\frac{\widehat{\beta}(r)}{|r|^{2}}=+\infty\,.

(2.25)

Moreover, we demand
D(\beta_{\Gamma})\subseteq D(\beta)
, and postulate that the boundary graph dominates the bulk graph in the following sense:

\displaystyle\exists\,\kappa_{1},\kappa_{2}>0:\hskip 10.00002pt|\beta^{\circ}(r)|\leq\kappa_{1}|\beta_{\Gamma}^{\circ}(r)|+\kappa_{2}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{for every $r\in D(\beta_{\Gamma})$.}

(2.26)

Here,
\beta^{\circ}
and
\beta_{\Gamma}^{\circ}
are the minimal sections introduced in
(S1)
.

Note that all the examples of potentials given in (
1.2
)–(
1.4
) fulfill the assumptions
(S1)
and
(S2)
, provided that the boundary potential dominates the one in the bulk as demanded in (
2.26
).
In particular, the only scenario where a singular and a regular potential may coexist is the case in which the boundary potential is the singular one.
This assumption has first been made in
[
13
]
and was used afterwards in several contributions in the literature, see, e.g.,
[
15
,
21
,
24
,
23
,
25
,
22
,
17
,
18
,
19
,
14
]
. However, in some other works such as
[
36
,
37
]
different compatibility conditions were assumed.

3.1  Existence of weak solutions in the case

Proof of Theorem

.

We intend to construct a weak solution to system (
1.1
) by discretizing the weak formulation (
2.13
) by means of a Faedo–Galerkin scheme. In this proof, the letter

will denote generic positive constants that may depend on
K
,
\Omega
,
T
, the initial data and the constants introduced in
(A1)
–
(A3)
, and may change their value from line to line.
Recall that, as
K
is assumed to be positive here, we have
\sigma(K)=\frac{1}{K}
.

3.1.1 Construction of local-in-time approximate solutions

It is well known that the Poisson–Neumann eigenvalue problem

\displaystyle-\Delta u=\lambda_{\Omega}u\;\;\text{in $\Omega$},\hskip 10.00002pt\partial_{\bf n}u=0\;\;\text{on $\Gamma$}

(3.1)

possesses countably many eigenvalues and a corresponding sequence
\{u_{i}\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\subset V
of
H
-normalized eigenfunctions which form an orthonormal basis of
H
and an orthogonal Schauder basis of
V
. Similarly, invoking the spectral theorem for compact self-adjoint operators, it follows that the eigenvalue problem

\displaystyle-\Delta_{\Gamma}v=\lambda_{\Gamma}v\;\;\text{on $\Gamma$}

(3.2)

for the Laplace–Beltrami operator
possesses countably many eigenvalues and a corresponding sequence
\{v_{i}\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\subset V_{\Gamma}
of
H_{\Gamma}
-normalized eigenfunctions which form an orthonormal basis of
H_{\Gamma}
and an orthogonal Schauder basis of
V_{\Gamma}
.
For any
k\in\mathbb{N}
, we now define the finite-dimensional subspaces

\displaystyle V_{k}

\displaystyle:={\rm span}\{u_{i}:1\leq i\leq k\}\subset V,

\displaystyle V_{\Gamma,k}

\displaystyle:={\rm span}\{v_{j}:1\leq j\leq k\}\subset V_{\Gamma},

\displaystyle{\cal V}_{k}

\displaystyle:={\rm span}\{(u_{i},v_{j}):1\leq i,j\leq k\}\subset{\cal V}.

We point out that, due to the above considerations, the inclusions

\displaystyle\bigcup_{k\in\mathbb{N}}V_{k}\subseteq V,\hskip 20.00003pt\bigcup_{k\in\mathbb{N}}V_{\Gamma,k}\subseteq V_{\Gamma},\hskip 20.00003pt\bigcup_{k\in\mathbb{N}}{\cal V}_{k}\subseteq{\cal V}

are dense.
To construct a sequence of approximate solutions, we now make the ansatz

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \varphi_{k}(x,t)&=\sum_{i=1}^{k}a_{i}^{k}(t)u_{i}(x),\quad&&\psi_{k}(x,t)=\sum_{i=1}^{k}b_{i}^{k}(t)v_{i}(x),\\
\mu_{k}(x,t)&=\sum_{i=1}^{k}c_{i}^{k}(t)u_{i}(x),\quad&&\theta_{k}(x,t)=\sum_{i=1}^{k}d_{i}^{k}(t)v_{i}(x)\end{aligned}

(3.3)

for every
k\in\mathbb{N}
, where the coefficients
\mathbf{a}^{k}:=(a_{1}^{k},...,a_{k}^{k})^{\top}
,
\mathbf{b}^{k}:=(b_{1}^{k},...,b_{k}^{k})^{\top}
,
\mathbf{c}^{k}:=(c_{1}^{k},...,c_{k}^{k})^{\top}
,
\mathbf{d}^{k}:=(d_{1}^{k},...,d_{k}^{k})^{\top}
are still to be determined.

Let now
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
and
t\in[0,T]
be arbitrary. We consider the bilinear form

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{k,t}:{\bf V}_{\sigma,{\bf n}}\times{\bf V}_{\sigma,{\bf n}}\to{\mathbb{R}},

\displaystyle({\boldsymbol{v}},{\boldsymbol{w}})\mapsto 2\int_{\Omega}\nu\big(\varphi_{k}(t)\big)\,\mathbf{D}{\boldsymbol{v}}:\mathbf{D}{\boldsymbol{w}}+\int_{\Omega}\lambda\big(\varphi_{k}(t)\big)\,{\boldsymbol{v}}\cdot{\boldsymbol{w}}+\int_{\Gamma}\gamma\big(\psi_{k}(t)\big)\,{\boldsymbol{v}}\cdot{\boldsymbol{w}},

which is related to the weak formulation of the Brinkman equation with Navier-slip boundary condition.
It is obvious that
\mathcal{B}_{k,t}
is symmetric, and, in view of
(A3)
, it is easy to see that
\mathcal{B}_{k,t}
is continuous.
We further recall that every
{\boldsymbol{v}}\in{\bf V}_{\sigma,{\bf n}}
satisfies
\mathop{\rm div}\nolimits({\boldsymbol{v}})=0
a.e. in
\Omega
, and
{\boldsymbol{v}}\cdot{\bf n}=0
a.e. on
\Gamma
. By means of
(A3)
, integration by parts and the Poincaré inequality presented in Lemma
2.1
, we deduce

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{k,t}({\boldsymbol{v}},{\boldsymbol{v}})

\displaystyle\geq 2\nu_{1}\int_{\Omega}\,\mathbf{D}{\boldsymbol{v}}:\mathbf{D}{\boldsymbol{v}}+\gamma_{1}\int_{\Gamma}|{\boldsymbol{v}}|^{2}

\displaystyle=\nu_{1}\int_{\Omega}|\nabla{\boldsymbol{v}}|^{2}+\gamma_{1}\int_{\Gamma}|{\boldsymbol{v}}|^{2}\geq C\|{\boldsymbol{v}}\|_{{\bf{V}}}^{2}

for all
{\boldsymbol{v}}\in{\bf V}_{\sigma,{\bf n}}
. This means that the bilinear form
\mathcal{B}_{k,t}
is coercive in
{\bf V}_{\sigma,{\bf n}}
. Hence, the Lax–Milgram lemma implies that there exists a unique function
{\boldsymbol{v}}_{k}(t)\in{\bf V}_{\sigma,{\bf n}}
solving

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{k,t}\big({\boldsymbol{v}}_{k}(t),{\boldsymbol{w}}\big)=-\int_{\Omega}\varphi_{k}(t)\nabla\mu_{k}(t)\cdot{\boldsymbol{w}}-\int_{\Gamma}\psi_{k}(t)\nabla_{\Gamma}\theta_{k}(t)\cdot{\boldsymbol{w}}

for all
{\boldsymbol{w}}\in{\bf V}_{\sigma,{\bf n}}
. As

was arbitrary, this defines a function
{\boldsymbol{v}}_{k}:[0,T]\to{\bf V}_{\sigma,{\bf n}}
. We point out that by construction,
{\boldsymbol{v}}_{k}
depends continuously on the coefficients
\mathbf{a}^{k}
,
\mathbf{b}^{k}
,
\mathbf{c}^{k}
and
\mathbf{d}^{k}
.

We now want to adjust the coefficient vectors
\mathbf{a}^{k}
,
\mathbf{b}^{k}
,
\mathbf{c}^{k}
and
\mathbf{d}^{k}
such that the discretized weak formulation

\displaystyle 2\int_{\Omega}\nu(\varphi_{k})\mathbf{D}{\boldsymbol{v}}_{k}:\mathbf{D}{\boldsymbol{w}}+\int_{\Omega}\lambda(\varphi_{k}){\boldsymbol{v}}_{k}\cdot{\boldsymbol{w}}+\int_{\Gamma}\gamma(\psi_{k}){\boldsymbol{v}}_{k}\cdot{\boldsymbol{w}}

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt=-\int_{\Omega}\varphi_{k}\nabla\mu_{k}\cdot{\boldsymbol{w}}-\int_{\Gamma}\psi_{k}\nabla_{\Gamma}\theta_{k}\cdot{\boldsymbol{w}},

(3.4a)

\displaystyle\mathopen{\langle}\partial_{t}\varphi_{k},\zeta\mathclose{\rangle}_{V}-\int_{\Omega}\varphi_{k}{\boldsymbol{v}}_{k}\cdot\nabla\zeta+\int_{\Omega}M_{\Omega}(\varphi_{k})\nabla\mu_{k}\cdot\nabla\zeta=0,

(3.4b)

\displaystyle\mathopen{\langle}\partial_{t}\psi_{k},{\zeta_{\Gamma}}\mathclose{\rangle}_{{V}_{\Gamma}}-\int_{\Gamma}\psi_{k}{\boldsymbol{v}}_{k}\cdot\nabla_{\Gamma}{\zeta_{\Gamma}}+\int_{\Gamma}M_{\Gamma}(\psi_{k})\nabla_{\Gamma}\theta_{k}\cdot\nabla_{\Gamma}{\zeta_{\Gamma}}=0,

(3.4c)

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\mu_{k}\eta+\int_{\Gamma}\theta_{k}{\eta_{\Gamma}}=\int_{\Omega}\nabla\varphi_{k}\cdot\nabla\eta+\int_{\Omega}F^{\prime}(\varphi_{k})\eta+\int_{\Gamma}\nabla_{\Gamma}\psi_{k}\cdot\nabla_{\Gamma}{\eta_{\Gamma}}

\displaystyle\hskip 86.11084pt+\int_{\Gamma}G^{\prime}(\psi_{k}){\eta_{\Gamma}}+\frac{1}{K}\int_{\Gamma}(\psi_{k}-\varphi_{k})({\eta_{\Gamma}}-\eta)

(3.4d)

for all test functions
{\boldsymbol{w}}\in{\bf V}_{\sigma,{\bf n}}
,
\zeta\in V_{k}
,
{\zeta_{\Gamma}}\in V_{\Gamma,k}
and
(\eta,{\eta_{\Gamma}})\in{\cal V}_{k}
, and the initial conditions

\displaystyle\varphi_{k}(0)=\varphi_{0,k}:=\mathbb{P}_{V_{k}}(\varphi_{0})\hskip 10.00002pt\text{and}\hskip 10.00002pt\psi_{k}(0)=\psi_{0,k}:=\mathbb{P}_{V_{\Gamma,k}}(\psi_{0})

(3.5)

are fulfilled. With the symbol

we denote the

whereas

Choosing

in (

\displaystyle[\mathbf{a}^{k}]_{i}(0)=a_{i}^{k}(0)=\left(\varphi_{0},u_{i}\right)_{H}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{and}\hskip 10.00002pt[\mathbf{b}^{k}]_{i}(0)=b_{i}^{k}(0)=\left(\psi_{0},v_{i}\right)_{H_{\Gamma}}

In particular, since the functions

\displaystyle\left\|(\mu_{k},\theta_{k})\right\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T_{k};{\cal V}^{*})}\leq C.

(3.16)

We further have

\displaystyle\left\|(\mu_{k},\theta_{k})\right\|^{2}_{\cal H}

\displaystyle=\mathopen{\langle}(\mu_{k},\theta_{k}),(\mu_{k},\theta_{k})\mathclose{\rangle}_{{\cal V}}

\displaystyle\leq C\left\|(\mu_{k},\theta_{k})\right\|_{{{\cal V}^{*}}}\big(\left\|(\mu_{k},\theta_{k})\right\|_{\cal H}+\left\|(\nabla\mu_{k},\nabla_{\Gamma}\theta_{k})\right\|_{{\bf H}\times{\bf H}_{\Gamma}}\big)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\left\|(\mu_{k},\theta_{k})\right\|^{2}_{\cal H}+C\left\|(\mu_{k},\theta_{k})\right\|_{{{\cal V}^{*}}}\left\|(\nabla\mu_{k},\nabla_{\Gamma}\theta_{k})\right\|_{{\bf H}\times{\bf H}_{\Gamma}}+C\left\|(\mu_{k},\theta_{k})\right\|_{{{\cal V}^{*}}}^{2}.

Hence, squaring and integrating this estimate with respect to time, we use (
3.1.2
) and (
3.16
) to conclude

\displaystyle\left\|(\mu_{k},\theta_{k})\right\|_{L^{4}(0,T_{k};{\cal H})}\leq C.

(3.17)

In particular, we thus have

\displaystyle\left\|\mu_{k}\right\|_{L^{2}(0,T_{k};V)}+\left\|\theta_{k}\right\|_{L^{2}(0,T_{k};{V}_{\Gamma})}\leq C.

(3.18)

Overall estimate.
Combining (
3.1.2
)–(
3.11
), (
3.13
), (
3.15
), (
3.17
) and (
3.18
), we obtain the overall uniform estimate

\displaystyle\left\|{\boldsymbol{v}}_{k}\right\|_{L^{2}(0,T_{k};{\bf V}_{\sigma,{\bf n}})\cap L^{2}(0,T_{k};\mathbf{H}_{\Gamma})}+\left\|\varphi_{k}\right\|_{H^{1}(0,T_{k};{V^{*}})\cap L^{\infty}(0,T_{k};V)}+\left\|\psi_{k}\right\|_{H^{1}(0,T_{k};{{V}_{\Gamma}^{*}})\cap L^{\infty}(0,T_{k};{V}_{\Gamma})}

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt+\left\|\mu_{k}\right\|_{L^{4}(0,T_{k};H)\cap L^{2}(0,T_{k};V)}+\left\|\theta_{k}\right\|_{L^{4}(0,T_{k};{H}_{\Gamma})\cap L^{2}(0,T_{k};{V}_{\Gamma})}\leq C.

(3.19)

3.1.3 Extension of the approximate solution onto the whole time interval
[0,T]

In Step 1, we constructed the coefficients

(\mathbf{a}^{k},\mathbf{b}^{k})^{\top}
as a solution of a nonlinear system of ODEs existing on its right-maximal time interval
[0,T_{k}^{*})\cap[0,T]
. We now assume that
T_{k}^{*}\leq T
.
By the definition of the approximate solutions given in (
3.3
) and the uniform bound (
3.1.2
), we infer that for any
T_{k}\in[0,T_{k}^{*})
, all
t\in[0,T_{k}]
, and all
i\in\{1,...,k\}
,

\displaystyle|a_{i}^{k}(t)|+|b_{i}^{k}(t)|

\displaystyle=\big|\left(\varphi_{k}(t),u_{i}\right)_{H}\big|+\big|\left(\psi_{k}(t),v_{i}\right)_{{H}_{\Gamma}}\big|

\displaystyle\leq\|\varphi_{k}\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T_{k};H)}+\|\psi_{k}\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T_{k};{H}_{\Gamma})}\leq C.

This means that the solution
(\mathbf{a}^{k},\mathbf{b}^{k})^{\top}
is bounded on the time interval
[0,T_{k}^{*})
by a constant that is independent of
T_{k}
and
k
.
Hence, according to classical ODE theory, the solution can thus be extended beyond the time
T_{k}^{*}
. However, as the solution was assumed to be right-maximal, this is a contradiction.
We thus have
T_{k}^{*}>T
, which directly implies
[0,T_{k}^{*})\cap[0,T]=[0,T]
. This means that the solution
(\mathbf{a}^{k},\mathbf{b}^{k})^{\top}
of the ODE system actually exists on the whole time interval
[0,T]
.
As the coefficients
\mathbf{c}^{k}
and
\mathbf{d}^{k}
can be reconstructed from
\mathbf{a}^{k}
and
\mathbf{b}^{k}
by the corresponding system of algebraic equations, they also exist on the whole time interval
[0,T]
. Recalling (
3.3
) and the construction of
{\boldsymbol{v}}_{k}
this directly entails that the approximate solution
({\boldsymbol{v}}_{k},\varphi_{k},\mu_{k},\psi_{k},\theta_{k})
actually exists in
[0,T]
.
Hence, choosing
T_{k}=T
in (

), we eventually conclude

\displaystyle\left\|{\boldsymbol{v}}_{k}\right\|_{L^{2}(0,T;{\bf V}_{\sigma,{\bf n}})\cap L^{2}(0,T;\mathbf{H}_{\Gamma})}+\left\|\varphi_{k}\right\|_{H^{1}(0,T;V^{*})\cap L^{\infty}(0,T;V)}+\left\|\psi_{k}\right\|_{H^{1}(0,T;{V}_{\Gamma}^{*})\cap L^{\infty}(0,T;{V}_{\Gamma})}

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt+\left\|\mu_{k}\right\|_{L^{4}(0,T;H)\cap L^{2}(0,T;V)}+\left\|\theta_{k}\right\|_{L^{4}(0,T;{H}_{\Gamma})\cap L^{2}(0,T;{V}_{\Gamma})}\leq C.

(3.20)

3.1.5 Additional regularity for the phase-fields

To prove additional regularity for the phase-fields, we assume
\Omega
to be of class
C^{\ell}
with
\ell\in\{2,3\}
, and we return to the Faedo–Galerkin scheme.
First of all, applying regularity theory for Poisson’s equation with an inhomogeneous
Neumann boundary condition (see, e.g.,
[
52
, Theorem 4.18]
or
[
59
, s. 5, Proposition 7.7]
), we infer that the eigenfunctions
u_{i}
exhibit the regularity
u_{i}\in H^{\ell}(\Omega)
for all
i\in{\mathbb{N}}
. Moreover, applying regularity theory for elliptic equations on submanifolds (see, e.g.,
[
59
, s. 5, Theorem 1.3]
), we conclude that the eigenfunctions
v_{i}
have the regularity
v_{i}\in H^{\ell}(\Gamma)
for all
i\in{\mathbb{N}}
. This directly entails

\displaystyle\varphi_{k}(t)\in V_{k}\cap H^{\ell}(\Omega)\hskip 10.00002pt\text{and}\hskip 10.00002pt\psi_{k}(t)\in V_{\Gamma,k}\cap H^{\ell}(\Gamma)

for all
t\in[0,T]
.

Let now
t\in[0,T]
be arbitrary.
We infer from (
3.4
) that there exists a null set
\mathcal{N}\subset[0,T]
such that

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\nabla\varphi_{k}(t)\cdot\nabla\eta+\int_{\Gamma}\nabla_{\Gamma}\psi_{k}(t)\cdot\nabla_{\Gamma}{\eta_{\Gamma}}+\frac{1}{K}\int_{\Gamma}\big(\psi_{k}(t)-\varphi_{k}(t)\big)({\eta_{\Gamma}}-\eta)

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt=\int_{\Omega}\Big(\mu_{k}(t)-\mathbb{P}_{V_{k}}\big[F^{\prime}\big(\varphi_{k}(t)\big)\big]\Big)\,\eta+\int_{\Gamma}\Big(\theta_{k}(t)-\mathbb{P}_{V_{\Gamma,k}}\big[G^{\prime}\big(\psi_{k}(t)\big)\big]\Big)\,{\eta_{\Gamma}}

(3.38)

for all test functions
(\eta,{\eta_{\Gamma}})\in{\cal V}
and all
t\in[0,T]\setminus\mathcal{N}
.

Let now
t\in[0,T]\setminus\mathcal{N}
be arbitrary.
Then, (
3.1.5
) entails that the pair
\big(\varphi_{k}(t),\psi_{k}(t)\big)
is a weak solution of the bulk-surface elliptic problem

\displaystyle-\Delta\varphi_{k}(t)

\displaystyle=f_{k}(t)

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\text{in $\Omega$},

(3.39a)

\displaystyle-\Delta_{\Gamma}\psi_{k}(t)+\partial_{\bf n}\varphi_{k}(t)

\displaystyle=g_{k}(t)

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\text{on $\Gamma$},

(3.39b)

\displaystyle K\partial_{\bf n}\varphi_{k}(t)

\displaystyle=\psi_{k}(t)-\varphi_{k}(t)

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\text{on $\Gamma$},

(3.39c)

where

\displaystyle f_{k}(t)

\displaystyle:=\mu_{k}(t)-\mathbb{P}_{V_{k}}\big[F^{\prime}\big(\varphi_{k}(t)\big)\big]

\displaystyle\in V_{k}\subset V,

\displaystyle g_{k}(t)

\displaystyle:=\theta_{k}(t)-\mathbb{P}_{V_{\Gamma,k}}\big[G^{\prime}\big(\psi_{k}(t)\big)\big]

\displaystyle\in V_{\Gamma,k}\subset{V}_{\Gamma}.

In the following, without loss of generality, we assume that the growth conditions from
(R1)
hold with
p\in[5,6)
.
Most part of the computations to follow resemble those performed in
[
40
]
. Therefore, we just highlight the key steps for the reader’s convenience.
Using the uniform estimate (
3.1.3
), the Gagliardo–Nirenberg inequality and Young’s inequality, we derive the estimate

\displaystyle\big\|F^{\prime}\big(\varphi_{k}(t)\big)\big\|_{H}

\displaystyle\leq C+C\|\varphi_{k}(t)\|_{L^{2(p-1)}(\Omega)}^{p-1}

\displaystyle\leq C+C\|\varphi_{k}(t)\|_{L^{6}(\Omega)}^{\frac{p+2}{2}}\|\varphi_{k}(t)\|_{H^{2}(\Omega)}^{\frac{p-4}{2}}

\displaystyle\leq C\varepsilon^{-1}+\varepsilon\|\varphi_{k}(t)\|_{H^{2}(\Omega)},

(3.40)

for any

(R1)
and (
3.1.3
), we have

\displaystyle\big\|G^{\prime}\big(\psi_{k}(t)\big)\big\|_{{H}_{\Gamma}}\leq C+C\|\psi_{k}(t)\|_{L^{2(q-1)}(\Gamma)}^{q-1}\leq C.

(3.41)

We first consider the case
\ell=2
. Applying regularity theory for elliptic problems with bulk-surface coupling (see
[
48
, Theorem 3.3]
), we deduce

\big(\varphi_{k}(t),\psi_{k}(t)\big)\in H^{2}(\Omega)\times H^{2}(\Gamma)
with

\displaystyle\left\|\varphi_{k}(t)\right\|_{H^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\left\|\psi_{k}(t)\right\|_{H^{2}(\Gamma)}^{2}\leq C\left\|f_{k}(t)\right\|_{H}^{2}+C\left\|g_{k}(t)\right\|_{{H}_{\Gamma}}^{2}

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\leq C\left\|\mu_{k}(t)\right\|_{H}^{2}+C\left\|\theta_{k}(t)\right\|_{{H}_{\Gamma}}^{2}+C(1+\varepsilon^{-2})+C\varepsilon^{2}\|\varphi_{k}(t)\|_{H^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}.

After choosing
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
sufficiently small, we absorb the term
C\varepsilon^{2}\|\varphi_{k}(t)\|_{H^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}
by the left-hand side. Squaring and integrating the resulting inequality with respect to
t
from
0
to
T
, we use (
3.1.3
) to arrive at the uniform estimate

\displaystyle\left\|\varphi_{k}\right\|_{L^{4}(0,T;H^{2}(\Omega))}+\left\|\psi_{k}\right\|_{L^{4}(0,T;H^{2}(\Gamma))}\leq C.

(3.42)

Consequently, we have, as

,

\displaystyle\varphi_{k}

\displaystyle\to\varphi\hskip 10.00002pt\text{weakly in $L^{4}(0,T;H^{2}(\Omega))$},

\displaystyle\psi_{k}

\displaystyle\to\psi\hskip 10.00002pt\text{weakly in $L^{4}(0,T;H^{2}(\Gamma))$},

after another subsequence extraction. This proves the regularity assertion (
2.15a
). As a direct consequence, it follows via integration by parts and the fundamental lemma of calculus that the equations (
1.1d
), (

) and (

) are now even satisfied in the strong sense (i.e., a.e. in

and on
\Sigma
, respectively).

.
Employing the uniform estimate (

), the Gagliardo–Nirenberg inequality, Agmon’s inequality and Young’s inequality, we derive the estimate

\displaystyle\big\|F^{\prime\prime}\big(\varphi_{k}(t)\big)\nabla\varphi_{k}(t)\big\|_{{\bf H}}

\displaystyle\leq C\|\nabla\varphi_{k}(t)\|_{{\bf H}}+C\||\varphi_{k}(t)|^{p-2}\nabla\varphi_{k}(t)\|_{{\bf H}}

\displaystyle\leq C+C\|\varphi_{k}(t)\|_{L^{2(p-2)}(\Omega)}^{p-2}\|\nabla\varphi_{k}(t)\|_{{\bf{L}}^{\infty}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\leq C+C\|\varphi_{k}(t)\|_{L^{6}(\Omega)}^{\frac{p+1}{2}}\|\varphi_{k}(t)\|_{H^{2}(\Omega)}^{\frac{p-5}{2}+\frac{1}{2}}\|\varphi_{k}(t)\|_{H^{3}(\Omega)}^{\frac{1}{2}}

for any

\displaystyle\big\|F^{\prime}\big(\varphi_{k}(t)\big)\big\|_{V}\leq C\varepsilon^{-1}+\varepsilon\|\varphi_{k}(t)\|_{H^{3}(\Omega)}.

4  Analysis of the Cahn–Hilliard–Brinkman system with singular potentials

We are now dealing with the proof of the existence of weak solutions for singular potentials.
Our strategy is to approximate the convex parts of the singular potentials
F
and
G
satisfying
(S1)
and
(S2)
by means of a Yosida regularization. In this way, the approximate potentials are regular and exhibit quadratic growth and we can thus use Theorem
2.4
and Theorem
2.5
to obtain suitable approximate solutions. We then derive uniform estimates with respect to the approximation parameter, and eventually pass to the limit.
In the forthcoming analysis, the splitting
F^{\prime}=\beta+\pi
and
G^{\prime}={\beta_{\Gamma}}+\pi_{\Gamma}
from
(S1)
will be adopted.

4.1  Yosida regularization

As mentioned, we rely on a Yosida regularization to smooth the singular parts of the potentials
F
and
G
. For any
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
, we approximate the maximal monotone graphs
\beta
and
{\beta_{\Gamma}}
by

\displaystyle{\beta_{\varepsilon}}(r)

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\Big(r-\big(I+\varepsilon\beta\big)^{-1}(r)\Big),\hskip 10.00002pt\beta_{\Gamma,\varepsilon}(r):=\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\Big(r-\big(I+\varepsilon{\beta_{\Gamma}}\big)^{-1}(r)\Big),\hskip 10.00002ptr\in{\mathbb{R}}.

Then, the condition in (
2.26
) implies that

\bigl|\beta_{\varepsilon}(r)\bigr|\leq\kappa_{1}\bigl|\beta_{\Gamma,\varepsilon}(r)\bigr|+\kappa_{2}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{for all $r\in\mathbb{R}$ and all $\varepsilon\in(0,1)$}

(4.1)

(see, e.g.,
[
15
, Appendix]
), where
\kappa_{1}
and
\kappa_{2}
are the constants introduced in (
2.26
).
Next, we define

G_{\varepsilon}:=\widehat{\beta}_{\Gamma,\varepsilon}+\widehat{\pi}_{\Gamma},

where

\displaystyle\widehat{\beta_{\varepsilon}}(r):=\int_{0}^{r}{\beta_{\varepsilon}}(s)\,{\rm ds},\hskip 10.00002pt\widehat{\beta}_{\Gamma,\varepsilon}(r):=\int_{0}^{r}\beta_{\Gamma,\varepsilon}(s)\,{\rm ds},\hskip 10.00002ptr\in{\mathbb{R}}.

It is well-known that for every
r\in{\mathbb{R}}
,

\displaystyle 0\leq\widehat{\beta_{\varepsilon}}(r)\leq\widehat{\beta}(r)

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\forall\varepsilon\in(0,1),\hskip 10.00002pt

\displaystyle\widehat{\beta_{\varepsilon}}(r)

\displaystyle\nearrow\widehat{\beta}(r)

monotonically as
\varepsilon\to 0,

(4.2a)

\displaystyle|{\beta_{\varepsilon}}(r)|\leq|\beta^{\circ}(r)|

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\forall\varepsilon\in(0,1),\hskip 10.00002pt

\displaystyle{\beta_{\varepsilon}}(r)

\displaystyle\to\beta^{\circ}(r)

as
\varepsilon\to 0
.

(4.2b)

Analogous properties hold for
\beta_{\Gamma,\varepsilon}
.
Moreover, owing to the growth condition (
2.25
),
\widehat{\beta_{\varepsilon}}
fulfills the following growth condition:

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\hbox{For every $M>0$ there exist $C_{M}>0$ and $\varepsilon_{M}\in(0,1)$ such that}\\
&\widehat{\beta_{\varepsilon}}(r)\geq M\,r^{2}-C_{M}\quad\hbox{for every $r\in{\mathbb{R}}$ and every $\varepsilon\in(0,\varepsilon_{M})$}\,.\end{aligned}

(4.3)

This property is checked in detail in the paper
[
20
, beginning of Section 3]
. Obviously, as a consequence, a similar condition holds for

since (

) entails that

\displaystyle\widehat{\beta_{\varepsilon}}(r)\leq\kappa_{1}\widehat{\beta}_{\Gamma,\varepsilon}(r)+\kappa_{2}|r|\hskip 10.00002pt\text{for every $r\in{\mathbb{R}},\ \varepsilon\in(0,1)$},

thanks to

and

Due to their construction by the Yosida approximation,

and

) along with the (at most) quadratic growth of

\displaystyle\hskip 20.00003pt+2\int_{0}^{t}\!\!\int_{\Omega}\nu(\varphi_{\varepsilon})|\mathbf{D}{\boldsymbol{v}}_{\varepsilon}|^{2}+\int_{0}^{t}\!\!\int_{\Omega}\lambda(\varphi_{\varepsilon})|{\boldsymbol{v}}_{\varepsilon}|^{2}+\int_{0}^{t}\!\!\int_{\Gamma}\gamma(\psi_{\varepsilon})|{\boldsymbol{v}}_{\varepsilon}|^{2}

\displaystyle\hskip 20.00003pt+\int_{0}^{t}\!\!\int_{\Omega}M_{\Omega}(\varphi_{\varepsilon})|\nabla\mu_{\varepsilon}|^{2}+\int_{0}^{t}\!\!\int_{\Gamma}M_{\Gamma}(\psi_{\varepsilon})|\nabla_{\Gamma}\theta_{\varepsilon}|^{2}

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\leq\frac{1}{2}\left\|\nabla\varphi_{0}\right\|^{2}_{{{\bf H}}}+\int_{\Omega}F_{\varepsilon}(\varphi_{0})+\frac{1}{2}\left\|\nabla_{\Gamma}\psi_{0}\right\|^{2}_{\mathbf{H}_{\Gamma}}+\int_{\Gamma}G_{\varepsilon}(\psi_{0})+\frac{\sigma(K)}{2}\left\|\psi_{0}-\varphi_{0}\right\|^{2}_{{H}_{\Gamma}}

(4.6)

for all
t\in[0,T]
. Now, observe that

\frac{1}{2}\left\|\nabla\varphi_{0}\right\|^{2}_{{\bf H}}+\int_{\Omega}F_{\varepsilon}(\varphi_{0})+\frac{1}{2}\left\|\nabla_{\Gamma}\psi_{0}\right\|^{2}_{\mathbf{H}_{\Gamma}}+\int_{\Gamma}G_{\varepsilon}(\psi_{0})+\frac{\sigma(K)}{2}\left\|\psi_{0}-\varphi_{0}\right\|^{2}_{{H}_{\Gamma}}\leq C

(4.7)

since
(\varphi_{0},\psi_{0})\in{\cal V}_{K}
satisfies (

) and (
4.2a
) holds.
Hence, in view of
(A2)
and
(A3)
and thanks to (
4.5
) and the Poincaré inequality, it is not difficult to infer that

\displaystyle\left\|\varphi_{\varepsilon}\right\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;V)}+\|F_{\varepsilon}(\varphi_{\varepsilon})\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{1}(\Omega))}+\left\|\psi_{\varepsilon}\right\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;{V}_{\Gamma})}+\|G_{\varepsilon}(\psi_{\varepsilon})\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{1}(\Gamma))}

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt+\left\|{\boldsymbol{v}}_{\varepsilon}\right\|_{L^{2}(0,T;{\bf V})\cap L^{2}(0,T;{\bf H}_{\Gamma})}+\left\|\nabla\mu_{\varepsilon}\right\|_{L^{2}(0,T;{\bf{H}})}+\left\|\nabla_{\Gamma}\theta_{\varepsilon}\right\|_{L^{2}(0,T;{\bf{H}}_{\Gamma})}\leq C.

(4.8)

Second estimate.
We proceed as in the derivation of (
3.13
) and (
3.15
) in the proof of Theorem
2.4
. Indeed, let us
take an arbitrary test function
\zeta\in L^{2}(0,T;V)
in (
2.13b
), then integrate over time and use Hölder’s inequality to obtain that

\displaystyle\Big|\int_{0}^{T}\mathopen{\langle}\partial_{t}\varphi_{\varepsilon},\zeta\mathclose{\rangle}_{V}\Big|

\displaystyle\leq C\int_{0}^{T}(\left\|\varphi_{\varepsilon}\right\|_{L^{4}(\Omega)}\left\|{\boldsymbol{v}}_{\varepsilon}\right\|_{{\bf{L}}^{4}(\Omega)}+M_{2}\left\|\nabla\mu_{\varepsilon}\right\|_{{\bf H}})\left\|\nabla\zeta\right\|_{{\bf H}}

\displaystyle\leq C\left(\left\|\varphi_{\varepsilon}\right\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;V)}\left\|{\boldsymbol{v}}_{\varepsilon}\right\|_{L^{2}(0,T;{\bf V})}+\left\|\nabla\mu_{\varepsilon}\right\|_{L^{2}(0,T;{\bf H})}\right)\left\|\zeta\right\|_{L^{2}(0,T;V)}

\displaystyle\leq C\left\|\zeta\right\|_{L^{2}(0,T;V)}.

Taking the supremum over all
\zeta\in L^{2}(0,T;V)
with
\left\|\zeta\right\|_{L^{2}(0,T;V)}\leq 1
, we infer

\displaystyle\left\|\partial_{t}\varphi_{\varepsilon}\right\|_{L^{2}(0,T;{V^{*}})}\leq C.

(4.9)

The same argument, acting on equation (
2.13c
), leads us to infer as well that

\displaystyle\left\|\partial_{t}\psi_{\varepsilon}\right\|_{L^{2}(0,T;V^{*}_{\Gamma})}\leq C.

(4.10)

Third estimate.
To handle the cases
K>0
and
K=0
simultaneously, we introduce the following notation:

\displaystyle\hskip 20.00003pt\alpha(K):=\begin{cases}0&\text{if $K>0$},\\
1&\text{if $K=0$}.\end{cases}

(4.11)

We now test (
2.13d
) by the pair

\displaystyle(\eta,{\eta_{\Gamma}})=\begin{cases}(\varphi_{\varepsilon}-m_{0},\psi_{\varepsilon}-m_{0})&\text{if $K=0$},\\
(\varphi_{\varepsilon}-m_{0},\psi_{\varepsilon}-m_{\Gamma 0})&\text{if $K>0$},\end{cases}

which clearly belongs to
{\cal V}_{K}
.
After some rearrangements, as well as adding and subtracting the constant
m_{\Gamma 0}
multiple times in the case
K=0
, we deduce

\displaystyle\left\|\nabla\varphi_{\varepsilon}\right\|^{2}_{{\bf H}}+\int_{\Omega}\beta_{\varepsilon}(\varphi_{\varepsilon})(\varphi_{\varepsilon}-m_{0})+\left\|\nabla_{\Gamma}\psi_{\varepsilon}\right\|^{2}_{{\bf H}_{\Gamma}}+\int_{\Gamma}\beta_{\Gamma,\varepsilon}(\psi_{\varepsilon})(\psi_{\varepsilon}-m_{\Gamma 0})

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt=\int_{\Omega}(\mu_{\varepsilon}-\mathopen{\langle}\mu_{\varepsilon}\mathclose{\rangle}_{\Omega})(\varphi_{\varepsilon}-m_{0})+\int_{\Gamma}(\theta_{\varepsilon}-\mathopen{\langle}\theta_{\varepsilon}\mathclose{\rangle}_{\Gamma})(\psi_{\varepsilon}-m_{\Gamma 0})

\displaystyle\hskip 20.00003pt+\sigma(K)\int_{\Gamma}(\psi_{\varepsilon}-\varphi_{\varepsilon})\big(\varphi_{\varepsilon}-\psi_{\varepsilon}-(m_{0}-m_{\Gamma 0})\big)

\displaystyle\hskip 20.00003pt-\int_{\Omega}\pi(\varphi_{\varepsilon})(\varphi_{\varepsilon}-m_{0})-\int_{\Gamma}\pi_{\Gamma}(\psi_{\varepsilon})(\psi_{\varepsilon}-m_{\Gamma 0})

\displaystyle\hskip 20.00003pt{}+\alpha(K)\int_{\Gamma}(G^{\prime}_{\varepsilon}(\psi_{\varepsilon})-\theta_{\varepsilon})(m_{0}-m_{\Gamma 0}).

(4.12)

Note that the subtracted mean values
\mathopen{\langle}\mu_{\varepsilon}\mathclose{\rangle}_{\Omega}
and
\mathopen{\langle}\theta_{\varepsilon}\mathclose{\rangle}_{\Gamma}
in the first two summands on the right-hand side of (
4.2
) do not change the values of these integrals since, due to (
4.5
), we have
\mathopen{\langle}\varphi_{\varepsilon}-m_{0}\mathclose{\rangle}_{\Omega}=0
and

\mathopen{\langle}\psi_{\varepsilon}-m_{\Gamma 0}\mathclose{\rangle}_{\Gamma}=0
.

To deal with the terms on the left-hand side of (
4.2
), we recall that due to assumption (
2.27
),
m_{0}
and
m_{\Gamma 0}
lie in the interior of the domains
D(\beta)
and
D(\beta_{\Gamma})
, respectively.
We can thus exploit a useful property (see, e.g.,
[
54
, Appendix, Prop. A.1]
and/or the detailed proof given in
[
36
, p. 908]
), namely there exist positive constants
c_{1},c_{2}
and a nonnegative constant
c_{3}
such that

\displaystyle c_{1}\left\|\beta_{\varepsilon}(\varphi_{\varepsilon})\right\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}+c_{2}\left\|\beta_{\Gamma,\varepsilon}(\psi_{\varepsilon})\right\|_{L^{1}(\Gamma)}-c_{3}

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\leq\int_{\Omega}\beta_{\varepsilon}(\varphi_{\varepsilon})(\varphi_{\varepsilon}-m_{0})+\int_{\Gamma}\beta_{\Gamma,\varepsilon}(\psi_{\varepsilon})(\psi_{\varepsilon}-m_{\Gamma 0}).

(4.13)

For the integrals in the second line of (
4.2
), we employ Poincaré’s inequality in the bulk and on the surface to obtain that

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}(\mu_{\varepsilon}-\mathopen{\langle}\mu_{\varepsilon}\mathclose{\rangle}_{\Omega})(\varphi_{\varepsilon}-m_{0})+\int_{\Gamma}(\theta_{\varepsilon}-\mathopen{\langle}\theta_{\varepsilon}\mathclose{\rangle}_{\Gamma})(\psi_{\varepsilon}-m_{\Gamma 0})

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\leq C\Bigl(\left\|\nabla\mu_{\varepsilon}\right\|_{{\bf H}}\left\|\nabla\varphi_{\varepsilon}\right\|_{{\bf H}}+\left\|\nabla_{\Gamma}\theta_{\varepsilon}\right\|_{{\bf H}_{\Gamma}}\left\|\nabla_{\Gamma}\psi_{\varepsilon}\right\|_{{\bf H}_{\Gamma}}\Bigr).

(4.14)

Moreover, integrals in the third and the fourth line of (
4.2
) can be bounded by virtue of estimate (
4.2
) as well as the Lipschitz continuity of
\pi
and
\pi_{\Gamma}
, so that

\displaystyle\sigma(K)\int_{\Gamma}(\psi_{\varepsilon}-\varphi_{\varepsilon})\big(\varphi_{\varepsilon}-\psi_{\varepsilon}-(m_{0}-m_{\Gamma 0})\big)

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt-\int_{\Omega}\pi(\varphi_{\varepsilon})(\varphi_{\varepsilon}-m_{0})-\int_{\Gamma}\pi_{\Gamma}(\psi_{\varepsilon})(\psi_{\varepsilon}-m_{\Gamma 0})\leq C\bigl(\left\|\varphi_{\varepsilon}\right\|^{2}_{H}+\left\|\psi_{\varepsilon}\right\|^{2}_{{H}_{\Gamma}}+1\bigr).

It remains to estimate the integral in the last line of (
4.2
), which is only present in the case
K=0
. Recall that if
K=0
, we assumed
\Omega
to be of class
C^{2}
. Hence,
we know from Corollary
4.1
that
(\varphi_{\varepsilon},\psi_{\varepsilon})\in L^{2}\big(0,T;H^{2}(\Omega)\times H^{2}(\Gamma)\big)
, and that the equations

\displaystyle\mu_{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle=-\Delta\varphi_{\varepsilon}+F^{\prime}_{\varepsilon}(\varphi_{\varepsilon})

\displaystyle\text{a.e. in $Q$},

(4.15)

\displaystyle\theta_{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle=-\Delta_{\Gamma}\psi_{\varepsilon}-G^{\prime}_{\varepsilon}(\psi_{\varepsilon})+\partial_{\bf n}\varphi_{\varepsilon}

a.e. on
\Sigma

(4.16)

hold in the strong sense.
Then, with the help of (
4.16
) and a simple integration by parts, it is not difficult to conclude that

\displaystyle\alpha(K)\int_{\Gamma}(G^{\prime}_{\varepsilon}(\psi_{\varepsilon})-\theta_{\varepsilon})(m_{0}-m_{\Gamma 0})

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt{}=-\alpha(K)\int_{\Gamma}\partial_{\bf n}\varphi_{\varepsilon}(m_{0}-m_{\Gamma 0})\leq C\,\alpha(K)\left\|\partial_{\bf n}\varphi_{\varepsilon}\right\|_{{H}_{\Gamma}}.

(4.17)

In the following, we write
\Phi_{\varepsilon}
to denote generic nonnegative functions

\displaystyle t\mapsto\Phi_{\varepsilon}(t)\in L^{2}(0,T)\hskip 20.00003pt\text{with}\hskip 20.00003pt\left\|\Phi_{\varepsilon}\right\|_{L^{2}(0,T)}\leq C\hskip 10.00002pt\text{for all $\varepsilon>0$}

(4.18)

i.e., the
L^{2}
-norm is bounded uniformly in
\varepsilon
. Here, “generic” means that the explicit definition of the function
\Phi_{\varepsilon}
may vary throughout this proof.

All in all, collecting the inequlities (
4.2
)–(
4.2
) and (
4.17
), we conclude that

\displaystyle\left\|\beta_{\varepsilon}(\varphi_{\varepsilon}(t))\right\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}+\left\|\beta_{\Gamma,\varepsilon}(\psi_{\varepsilon}(t))\right\|_{L^{1}(\Gamma)}\leq\Phi_{\varepsilon}(t)+C\,\alpha(K)\left\|\partial_{\bf n}\varphi_{\varepsilon}(t)\right\|_{{H}_{\Gamma}}

(4.19)

for almost all
t\in(0,T)
.
Having shown (
4.19
), now we aim to prove additional
L^{2}
-bounds for the terms
\beta_{\varepsilon}(\varphi_{\varepsilon})
and

\beta_{\Gamma,\varepsilon}(\psi_{\varepsilon})
. For that, we take advantage of the growth condition (
4.1
), which follows from (
2.26
) in
(S2)
. However, the related analysis has to be performed differently for the cases
K>0
and
K=0
.

Further estimate in the case
K>0
.
As
\alpha(K)=0
in this case, (
4.19
) yields

\displaystyle\left\|\beta_{\varepsilon}(\varphi_{\varepsilon})\right\|_{L^{2}(0,T;L^{1}(\Omega))}+\left\|\beta_{\Gamma,\varepsilon}(\psi_{\varepsilon})\right\|_{L^{2}(0,T;L^{1}(\Gamma))}\leq C.

(4.20)

Of course, thanks to (
4.2
) we also have

\left\|F^{\prime}_{\varepsilon}(\varphi_{\varepsilon})\right\|_{L^{2}(0,T;L^{1}(\Omega))}+\left\|G^{\prime}_{\varepsilon}(\psi_{\varepsilon})\right\|_{L^{2}(0,T;L^{1}(\Gamma))}\leq C,

since
\pi
and
\pi_{\Gamma}
are Lipschitz continuous.
Consequently, by testing (
2.13d
) first by
(1,0)
and then by
(0,1)
, one easily realizes that

\displaystyle\left\|\mathopen{\langle}\mu_{\varepsilon}\mathclose{\rangle}_{\Omega}\right\|_{L^{2}(0,T)}+\left\|\mathopen{\langle}\theta_{\varepsilon}\mathclose{\rangle}_{\Gamma}\right\|_{L^{2}(0,T)}\leq C,

(4.21)

whence, using (
4.2
) and Poincaré’s inequality once again, we infer that

\displaystyle\left\|\mu_{\varepsilon}\right\|_{L^{2}(0,T;V)}+\left\|\theta_{\varepsilon}\right\|_{L^{2}(0,T;{V}_{\Gamma})}\leq C.

(4.22)

Next, recalling that
\sigma(K)=\frac{1}{K}
, we test (
2.13d
) by
(0,\beta_{\Gamma,\varepsilon}(\psi_{\varepsilon}))
obtaining

\displaystyle\left\|\beta_{\Gamma,\varepsilon}(\psi_{\varepsilon})\right\|^{2}_{{H}_{\Gamma}}+\int_{\Gamma}\beta^{\prime}_{\Gamma,\varepsilon}(\psi_{\varepsilon})|\nabla_{\Gamma}\psi_{\varepsilon}|^{2}

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt=\int_{\Gamma}(\theta_{\varepsilon}-\pi_{\Gamma}(\psi_{\varepsilon}))\beta_{\Gamma,\varepsilon}(\psi_{\varepsilon})-\frac{1}{K}\int_{\Gamma}(\psi_{\varepsilon}-\varphi_{\varepsilon})\beta_{\Gamma,\varepsilon}(\psi_{\varepsilon}).

Observe now that the second term on the left-hand side is nonnegative due to the monotonicity of
\beta_{\Gamma,\varepsilon}
.
For the terms on the right-hand side, we use Hölder’s inequality, Young’s inequality and the trace theorem to infer that

\displaystyle\int_{\Gamma}(\theta_{\varepsilon}-\pi_{\Gamma}(\psi_{\varepsilon}))\beta_{\Gamma,\varepsilon}(\psi_{\varepsilon})-\frac{1}{K}\int_{\Gamma}(\psi_{\varepsilon}-\varphi_{\varepsilon})\beta_{\Gamma,\varepsilon}(\psi_{\varepsilon}).

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\leq\frac{1}{2}\left\|\beta_{\Gamma,\varepsilon}(\psi_{\varepsilon})\right\|^{2}_{{H}_{\Gamma}}+C\bigl(\left\|\theta_{\varepsilon}\right\|^{2}_{{H}_{\Gamma}}+\left\|\psi_{\varepsilon}\right\|^{2}_{{H}_{\Gamma}}+\left\|\varphi_{\varepsilon}\right\|^{2}_{V}+1\bigr).

Hence, rearranging the terms and integrating over time we conclude that

\displaystyle\left\|\beta_{\Gamma,\varepsilon}(\psi_{\varepsilon})\right\|_{L^{2}(0,T;{H}_{\Gamma})}\leq C.

(4.23)

Next, proceeding similarly, we test (
2.13d
) by
(\beta_{\varepsilon}(\varphi_{\varepsilon}),0)
. This leads us to

\displaystyle\left\|\beta_{\varepsilon}(\varphi_{\varepsilon})\right\|^{2}_{H}+\int_{\Omega}\beta^{\prime}_{\varepsilon}(\varphi_{\varepsilon})|\nabla\varphi_{\varepsilon}|^{2}=\int_{\Omega}(\mu_{\varepsilon}-\pi(\varphi_{\varepsilon}))\beta_{\varepsilon}(\varphi_{\varepsilon})+\frac{1}{K}\int_{\Gamma}(\psi_{\varepsilon}-\varphi_{\varepsilon})\beta_{\varepsilon}(\varphi_{\varepsilon}).

Again, the second term on the left-hand side is nonnegative owing to
(S1)
, whereas the first term on the right can be easily controlled by Young’s inequality as

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}(\mu_{\varepsilon}-\pi(\varphi_{\varepsilon}))\beta_{\varepsilon}(\varphi_{\varepsilon})\leq\frac{1}{2}\left\|\beta_{\varepsilon}(\varphi)\right\|^{2}_{H}+C\bigl(\left\|\mu_{\varepsilon}\right\|^{2}_{H}+\left\|\varphi_{\varepsilon}\right\|^{2}_{H}+1\bigr).

Besides, we handle the last term by combining the monotonicity of
\beta_{\varepsilon}
with the property in (
4.1
). Namely, it holds that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{K}\int_{\Gamma}(\psi_{\varepsilon}-\varphi_{\varepsilon})\beta_{\varepsilon}(\varphi_{\varepsilon})

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt=-\frac{1}{K}\int_{\Gamma}(\varphi_{\varepsilon}-\psi_{\varepsilon})(\beta_{\varepsilon}(\varphi_{\varepsilon})-\beta_{\varepsilon}(\psi_{\varepsilon}))+\frac{1}{K}\int_{\Gamma}(\psi_{\varepsilon}-\varphi_{\varepsilon})\beta_{\varepsilon}(\psi_{\varepsilon})

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\leq\frac{1}{K}\int_{\Gamma}|\psi_{\varepsilon}-\varphi_{\varepsilon}|\,|\beta_{\varepsilon}(\psi_{\varepsilon})|

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\leq\frac{\kappa_{1}}{K}\int_{\Gamma}(|\psi_{\varepsilon}|+|\varphi_{\varepsilon}|)|\beta_{\Gamma,\varepsilon}(\psi_{\varepsilon})|+\frac{\kappa_{2}}{K}\int_{\Gamma}(|\psi_{\varepsilon}|+|\varphi_{\varepsilon}|)

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\leq\left\|\beta_{\Gamma,\varepsilon}(\psi_{\varepsilon})\right\|^{2}_{{H}_{\Gamma}}+C\bigl(\left\|\psi_{\varepsilon}\right\|^{2}_{{H}_{\Gamma}}+\left\|\varphi_{\varepsilon}\right\|^{2}_{V}+1\bigr).

Hence, with the help of (
4.23
), this shows the corresponding estimate

\displaystyle\left\|\beta_{\varepsilon}(\varphi_{\varepsilon})\right\|_{L^{2}(0,T;H)}\leq C.

(4.24)

Further estimate in the case
K=0
.
Recall that, as
K=0
, it now holds that
\sigma(K)=0
,
\alpha(K)=1
, and
\varphi_{\varepsilon}|_{\Gamma}=\psi_{\varepsilon}
a.e. on
\Sigma
, along with (
4.15
) and (
4.16
). Here, in our argumentation, we follow in parts the procedure devised in
[

]
.

Multiplying (
4.15
) by
1/|\Omega|
and integrating over
\Omega
, we find that

\displaystyle|\mathopen{\langle}\mu_{\varepsilon}\mathclose{\rangle}_{\Omega}|\leq C\left\|\partial_{\bf n}\varphi_{\varepsilon}\right\|_{{H}_{\Gamma}}+\frac{1}{|\Omega|}\bigl(\left\|\beta_{\varepsilon}(\varphi_{\varepsilon})\right\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}+\left\|\pi(\varphi_{\varepsilon})\right\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}\bigr).

(4.25)

Similarly, multiplying (
4.16
) by
1/|\Gamma|
and integrating over
\Gamma
, we infer that

\displaystyle|\mathopen{\langle}\theta_{\varepsilon}\mathclose{\rangle}_{\Gamma}|\leq C\left\|\partial_{\bf n}\varphi_{\varepsilon}\right\|_{{H}_{\Gamma}}+\frac{1}{|\Gamma|}\bigl(\left\|\beta_{\Gamma,\varepsilon}(\psi_{\varepsilon})\right\|_{L^{1}(\Gamma)}+\left\|\pi_{\Gamma}(\psi_{\varepsilon})\right\|_{L^{1}(\Gamma)}\bigr).

(4.26)

Then, combining (
4.25
) and (
4.26
), on account of the estimates (
4.2
) and (
4.19
)
along with the Lipschitz continuity of
\pi
and
\pi_{\Gamma}
, we deduce that

\displaystyle|\mathopen{\langle}\mu_{\varepsilon}\mathclose{\rangle}_{\Omega}|+|\mathopen{\langle}\theta_{\varepsilon}\mathclose{\rangle}_{\Gamma}|\leq C\bigl(\Phi_{\varepsilon}+\left\|\partial_{\bf n}\varphi_{\varepsilon}\right\|_{{H}_{\Gamma}}\bigr).

(4.27)

Combining (
4.2
) and (
4.7
), we obtain the estimate

\displaystyle\left\|\nabla\mu_{\varepsilon}\right\|_{{{\bf{H}}}}+\left\|\nabla_{\Gamma}\theta_{\varepsilon}\right\|_{{{\bf{H}}_{\Gamma}}}\leq\Phi_{\varepsilon}.

Hence, with the help of Poincaré’s inequality, we arrive at

\displaystyle\left\|\mu_{\varepsilon}(t)\right\|_{V}+\left\|\theta_{\varepsilon}(t)\right\|_{V_{\Gamma}}\leq C\bigl(\Phi_{\varepsilon}(t)+\left\|\partial_{\bf n}\varphi_{\varepsilon}(t)\right\|_{{H}_{\Gamma}}\bigr)

(4.28)

for almost all
t\in(0,T)
.
Now, we multiply (
4.15
) by
\beta_{\varepsilon}(\varphi_{\varepsilon})
and integrate by parts. This yields

\displaystyle\left\|\beta_{\varepsilon}(\varphi_{\varepsilon})\right\|^{2}_{H}+\int_{\Omega}\beta^{\prime}_{\varepsilon}(\varphi_{\varepsilon})|\nabla\varphi_{\varepsilon}|^{2}

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt=\int_{\Omega}(\mu_{\varepsilon}-\pi(\varphi_{\varepsilon}))\beta_{\varepsilon}(\varphi_{\varepsilon})+\int_{\Gamma}\partial_{\bf n}\varphi_{\varepsilon}\beta_{\varepsilon}(\varphi_{\varepsilon}).

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\leq\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}|\mu_{\varepsilon}-\pi(\varphi_{\varepsilon}))|^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\left\|\beta_{\varepsilon}(\varphi_{\varepsilon})\right\|^{2}_{H}+\int_{\Gamma}\partial_{\bf n}\varphi_{\varepsilon}\beta_{\varepsilon}(\varphi_{\varepsilon}).

(4.29)

Similarly, multiplying (
4.16
) by
-\beta_{\Gamma,\varepsilon}(\psi_{\varepsilon})
, it is straightforward to deduce that

\displaystyle\left\|\beta_{\Gamma,\varepsilon}(\psi_{\varepsilon})\right\|^{2}_{{H}_{\Gamma}}+\int_{\Gamma}\beta^{\prime}_{\Gamma,\varepsilon}(\psi_{\varepsilon})|\nabla_{\Gamma}\psi_{\varepsilon}|^{2}

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt=\int_{\Gamma}(\theta_{\varepsilon}-\pi_{\Gamma}(\psi_{\varepsilon}))\beta_{\Gamma,\varepsilon}(\psi_{\varepsilon})-\int_{\Gamma}\partial_{\bf n}\varphi_{\varepsilon}\beta_{\Gamma,\varepsilon}(\psi_{\varepsilon})

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\leq\int_{\Omega}|\theta_{\varepsilon}-\pi_{\Gamma}(\psi_{\varepsilon})|^{2}+\frac{1}{4}\left\|\beta_{\Gamma,\varepsilon}(\psi_{\varepsilon})\right\|^{2}_{{H}_{\Gamma}}-\int_{\Gamma}\partial_{\bf n}\varphi_{\varepsilon}\beta_{\Gamma,\varepsilon}(\psi_{\varepsilon}).

(4.30)

Recalling (
4.1
), we observe that

\displaystyle\biggl|\int_{\Gamma}\partial_{\bf n}\varphi_{\varepsilon}\beta_{\varepsilon}(\varphi_{\varepsilon})-\int_{\Gamma}\partial_{\bf n}\varphi_{\varepsilon}\beta_{\Gamma,\varepsilon}(\psi_{\varepsilon})\biggr|

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt{}\leq\left\|\partial_{\bf n}\varphi_{\varepsilon}\right\|_{{H}_{\Gamma}}\left\|(\kappa_{1}+1)|\beta_{\Gamma,\varepsilon}(\psi_{\varepsilon})|+\kappa_{2}\right\|_{{H}_{\Gamma}}

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt{}\leq\frac{1}{4}\left\|\beta_{\Gamma,\varepsilon}(\psi_{\varepsilon})\right\|^{2}_{{H}_{\Gamma}}+C\bigl(\left\|\partial_{\bf n}\varphi_{\varepsilon}\right\|_{{H}_{\Gamma}}^{2}+1\bigr).

Hence, adding (
4.2
) and (
4.2
), and using (
4.2
) as well as (

\displaystyle\left\|\beta_{\varepsilon}(\varphi_{\varepsilon}(t))\right\|_{H}+\left\|\beta_{\Gamma,\varepsilon}(\psi_{\varepsilon}(t))\right\|_{{H}_{\Gamma}}\leq C\bigl(\Phi_{\varepsilon}(t)+\left\|\partial_{\bf n}\varphi_{\varepsilon}(t)\right\|_{{H}_{\Gamma}}\bigr)

(4.31)

for almost all
t\in(0,T)
.
Now, recalling again (
4.15
) and (
4.16
), we observe that
\varphi_{\varepsilon}
solves the following bulk-surface elliptic problem:

\displaystyle-\Delta\varphi_{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle=\mu_{\varepsilon}-\beta_{\varepsilon}(\varphi_{\varepsilon})-\pi(\varphi_{\varepsilon})

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\text{in $\Omega$},

\displaystyle-\Delta_{\Gamma}\psi_{\varepsilon}+\partial_{\bf n}\varphi_{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle=\theta_{\varepsilon}-\beta_{\Gamma,\varepsilon}(\psi_{\varepsilon})-\pi_{\Gamma}(\psi_{\varepsilon})

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\text{on $\Gamma$},

\displaystyle\varphi_{\varepsilon}|_{\Gamma}

\displaystyle=\psi_{\varepsilon}

on
\Gamma

a.e. in
(0,T)
.
Due to (

), (

), (

) and the Lipschitz continuity of
\pi
and
\pi_{\Gamma}
, it is clear that the right-hand sides in the above system belong to

and

, respectively.
Hence, applying regularity theory for elliptic problems with bulk-surface coupling (see
[

, Theorem 3.3]
), we deduce that the estimate

\displaystyle\left\|\varphi_{\varepsilon}\right\|_{H^{2}(\Omega)}+\left\|\psi_{\varepsilon}\right\|_{H^{2}(\Gamma)}

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt{}\leq C\Bigl(\left\|\mu_{\varepsilon}-\beta_{\varepsilon}(\varphi_{\varepsilon})-\pi(\varphi_{\varepsilon})\right\|_{H}+\left\|\theta_{\varepsilon}-\beta_{\Gamma,\varepsilon}(\psi_{\varepsilon})+\psi_{\varepsilon}-\pi_{\gamma}(\psi_{\varepsilon})\right\|_{{H}_{\Gamma}}\Bigl)

(0,T)
.
Now, in view of (

), (
4.28
), (

) we can completely control the above right-hand side and infer that

\displaystyle\left\|\varphi_{\varepsilon}(t)\right\|_{H^{2}(\Omega)}+\left\|\psi_{\varepsilon}(t)\right\|_{H^{2}(\Gamma)}\leq C\bigl(\Phi_{\varepsilon}(t)+\left\|\partial_{\bf n}\varphi_{\varepsilon}(t)\right\|_{{H}_{\Gamma}}\bigr)

for almost all
t\in(0,T)
. On this basis, at this point we can use the standard trace theorem for the normal derivative concluding that, for some fixed

there is a positive constant

such that

\left\|\partial_{\bf n}\varphi_{\varepsilon}(t)\right\|_{{H}_{\Gamma}}\leq C_{s}\left\|\varphi_{\varepsilon}(t)\right\|_{H^{s}(\Omega)}

for almost all

. Hence, as

with compact embeddings, we infer from (

\displaystyle\left\|\varphi_{\varepsilon}(t)\right\|_{H^{2}(\Omega)}+\left\|\psi_{\varepsilon}(t)\right\|_{H^{2}(\Gamma)}+\left\|\partial_{\bf n}\varphi_{\varepsilon}(t)\right\|_{{H}_{\Gamma}}

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\leq C\bigl(\Phi_{\varepsilon}(t)+C_{s}\left\|\varphi_{\varepsilon}(t)\right\|_{H^{s}(\Omega)}\bigr)+C_{s}\left\|\varphi_{\varepsilon}(t)\right\|_{H^{s}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\leq\delta\left\|\varphi_{\varepsilon}(t)\right\|_{H^{2}(\Omega)}+C\,\Phi_{\varepsilon}(t)+C\delta^{-1}\left\|\varphi_{\varepsilon}(t)\right\|_{V},

(4.33)

for all
t\in(0,T)
and any
\delta\in(0,1)
.
Eventually, from (
4.2
) (
4.2
), it follows that

\displaystyle\left\|\varphi_{\varepsilon}\right\|_{L^{2}(0,T;H^{2}(\Omega))}+\left\|\psi_{\varepsilon}\right\|_{L^{2}(0,T;H^{2}(\Gamma))}+\left\|\partial_{\bf n}\varphi_{\varepsilon}\right\|_{L^{2}(0,T;{H}_{\Gamma})}\leq C

(4.34)

and consequently, recalling (
4.28
) and (
4.31
), we also have

\displaystyle\left\|\mu_{\varepsilon}\right\|_{L^{2}(0,T;V)}+\left\|\theta_{\varepsilon}\right\|_{L^{2}(0,T;V_{\Gamma})}+\left\|\beta_{\varepsilon}(\varphi_{\varepsilon}(t))\right\|_{L^{2}(0,T;H)}+\left\|\beta_{\Gamma,\varepsilon}(\psi_{\varepsilon})\right\|_{L^{2}(0,T;{H}_{\Gamma})}\leq C.

(4.35)

4.3  Passage to the limit and conclusion of the proof

The final step consists in passing to the limit as
\varepsilon
is sent to zero.
As the line of argument resembles the one presented in Section
3.1.4
, we proceed rather quickly just pointing out the main points and differences.

Owing to the above uniform estimates and to standard compactness results, we obtain that
there exist a subsequence of
\varepsilon
and a seventuple of limits

({\boldsymbol{v}}^{*},\varphi^{*},\xi^{*},\mu^{*},\psi^{*},\xi_{\Gamma}^{*},\theta^{*})

such that, as
\varepsilon\to 0
,

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{v}}_{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle\to{\boldsymbol{v}}^{*}\hskip 10.00002pt

weakly in
L^{2}(0,T;{\bf V}_{\sigma,{\bf n}})
,

strongly in
C^{0}([0,T];H^{s}(\Omega))
, and
a.e. in

Appendix: Some calculus for bulk-surface function spaces

Proposition A.1
.

Let
T>0
and
K\geq 0
be arbitrary.

(a)

Let
(u,v)\in L^{2}(0,T;{\cal V}_{K})
and suppose that the weak time derivative satisfies
(\partial_{t}u,\partial_{t}v)\in L^{2}(0,T;{\cal V}_{K}^{*})
. Then, the continuity property
(u,v)\in C^{0}([0,T];{\cal H})
holds, the mapping

t\mapsto\left\|(u,v)(t)\right\|_{{\cal H}}^{2}=\left\|u(t)\right\|_{H}^{2}+\left\|v(t)\right\|_{{H}_{\Gamma}}^{2}

is absolutely continuous in
[0,T]
, and the chain rule formula

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\Big[\left\|u(t)\right\|_{H}^{2}+\left\|v(t)\right\|_{{H}_{\Gamma}}^{2}\Big]=2\big<(\partial_{t}u,\partial_{t}v)(t),(u,v)(t)\big>_{{\cal V}_{K}}

(A.1)

holds for almost all
t\in[0,T]
.

(b)

Let
(u,v)\in L^{2}\big(0,T;H^{3}(\Omega)\times H^{3}(\Gamma)\big)

with
K\partial_{n}u=v-u
a.e. on
\Sigma
,
and suppose that their weak time derivative satisfies
(\partial_{t}u,\partial_{t}v)\in L^{2}(0,T;{{\cal V}}^{*})
.
Then, the continuity property
(u,v)\in C^{0}([0,T];{\cal V}_{K})
holds, the mapping

t\mapsto\left\|\nabla u(t)\right\|_{\bf{H}}^{2}+\left\|\nabla_{\Gamma}v(t)\right\|_{\mathbf{H}_{\Gamma}}^{2}+\sigma(K)\left\|v(t)-u(t)\right\|^{2}_{{H}_{\Gamma}}

is absolutely continuous in
[0,T]
, and the chain rule formula

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\Big[\left\|\nabla u(t)\right\|_{\bf{H}}^{2}+\left\|\nabla_{\Gamma}v(t)\right\|_{\mathbf{H}_{\Gamma}}^{2}+\sigma(K)\left\|v(t)-u(t)\right\|^{2}_{{H}_{\Gamma}}\Big]

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt=2\big<(\partial_{t}u,\partial_{t}v)(t),(-\Delta u,-\Delta_{\Gamma}v+\partial_{\bf n}u)(t)\big>_{{\cal V}}

(A.2)

holds for almost all
t\in[0,T]
.

Proof.

Proof of
(a)
.
Since
{\cal V}_{K}
and
{\cal H}
are separable Hilbert spaces with compact embedding
{\cal V}_{K}{\hookrightarrow}{\cal H}
and continuous embedding

{\cal H}{\hookrightarrow}{\cal V}^{*}_{K}
,
the assertion follows directly from the Lions–Magenes lemma (see, e.g.,
[
60
, Chapter III, Lemma 1.2]
).

Proof of
(b)
.

We first fix
u
and
v
as arbitrary representatives of their respective equivalence class.
Recall that due to
(a)
, we have
u\in C^{0}([0,T];H)
and
v\in C^{0}([0,T];{H}_{\Gamma})
.
We can thus extend the functions
u
and
v
onto
[-T,0]
by defining

u(t)
and
v(t)
by reflection for all
t<0
.

Let
\rho\in C^{\infty}_{c}({\mathbb{R}})
be a nonnegative function with
\mathop{\rm supp}\nolimits\rho\subset(0,1)
and
\left\|\rho\right\|_{L^{1}({\mathbb{R}})}=1
. For any
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
, we set

\displaystyle\rho_{k}(s):=k\rho(ks)\hskip 10.00002pt\text{for all $s\in{\mathbb{R}}$}.

For any Banach space
X
and any function
f\in L^{2}(-1,T;X)
, we define

\displaystyle f_{k}(t):=(\rho_{k}*f)(t)=\int_{t-\frac{1}{k}}^{t}\rho_{k}(t-s)\,f(s)\,{\mathrm{d}s}

for all
t\in[0,T]
and all
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
. By this construction, we have
f_{k}\in C^{\infty}([0,T];X)
with
f_{k}\to f
strongly in
L^{2}(0,T;X)
as
k\to\infty
.

For any
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
, we now choose
X=H^{3}(\Omega)
to define
u_{k}
and
X=H^{3}(\Gamma)
to define
v_{k}
as described above.
By this construction, it holds
\partial_{t}u_{k}=(\partial_{t}u)_{k}
and
\partial_{t}\nabla u_{k}=\nabla\partial_{t}u_{k}
a.e. in
Q
as well as
\partial_{t}v_{k}=(\partial_{t}v)_{k}
and
\partial_{t}\nabla_{\Gamma}v_{k}=\nabla_{\Gamma}\partial_{t}v_{k}
a.e. on
\Sigma
for all
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
. Moreover, as
k\to\infty
, we have

\displaystyle u_{k}

\displaystyle\to u

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\text{strongly in $L^{2}(0,T;H^{3}(\Omega))$},

(A.3)

\displaystyle v_{k}

\displaystyle\to v

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\text{strongly in $L^{2}(0,T;H^{3}(\Gamma))$},

(A.4)

\displaystyle(u_{k},v_{k})

\displaystyle\to(u,v)

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\text{strongly in $L^{2}(0,T;{\cal V}_{K})$},

(A.5)

\displaystyle(\partial_{t}u_{k},\partial_{t}v_{k})

\displaystyle\to(\partial_{t}u,\partial_{t}v)

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\text{strongly in $L^{2}(0,T;{\cal V}^{*})$}.

(A.6)

In the following, the letter
C
will denote generic positive constants that are independent of
k
and may change their value from line to line.
Now, for any
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
, we derive the identity

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\Big[\left\|\nabla u_{k}\right\|_{{\bf{H}}}^{2}+\left\|\nabla_{\Gamma}v_{k}\right\|_{\mathbf{H}_{\Gamma}}^{2}+\sigma(K)\left\|v_{k}-u_{k}\right\|^{2}_{{H}_{\Gamma}}\Big]

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt=2\big<(\partial_{t}u_{k},\partial_{t}v_{k}),(-\Delta u_{k},-\Delta_{\Gamma}v_{k}+\partial_{\bf n}u_{k})\big>_{{{\cal V}}}

(A.7)

in
[0,T]
by differentiating under the integral sign, applying integration by parts, and employing the relation

\displaystyle\sigma(K)(v_{k}-u_{k})=\begin{cases}0&\text{if $K=0$},\\
\partial_{\bf n}u_{k}&\text{if $K>0$},\end{cases}\hskip 20.00003pt\hbox{a.e.\ on~$\Sigma$}.

Let now
j,k\in{\mathbb{N}}
be arbitrary. Proceeding as above, we calculate

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\Big[\left\|\nabla u_{j}-\nabla u_{k}\right\|_{{\bf{H}}}^{2}+\left\|\nabla_{\Gamma}v_{j}-\nabla_{\Gamma}v_{k}\right\|_{\mathbf{H}_{\Gamma}}^{2}+\sigma(K)\left\|(v_{j}-v_{k})-(u_{j}-u_{k})\right\|^{2}_{{H}_{\Gamma}}\Big]

(A.8)

in

.
Here, we have used the embedding

\left\|\partial_{\bf n}(u_{j}-u_{k})\right\|_{{V}_{\Gamma}}\leq\left\|u_{j}-u_{k}\right\|_{H^{2}(\Gamma)}\leq C\left\|u_{j}-u_{k}\right\|_{H^{3}(\Omega)}.

Let now
s,t\in[0,T]
be arbitrary with
s\leq t

This yields

\displaystyle\left\|(\nabla u_{j}-\nabla u_{k})(t)\right\|_{{\bf{H}}}^{2}+\left\|(\nabla_{\Gamma}v_{j}-\nabla_{\Gamma}v_{k})(t)\right\|_{\mathbf{H}_{\Gamma}}^{2}

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt+\sigma(K)\left\|(v_{j}(t)-v_{k}(t))-(u_{j}(t)-u_{k}(t))\right\|^{2}_{{H}_{\Gamma}}

\displaystyle\leq\left\|(\nabla u_{j}-\nabla u_{k})(s)\right\|_{{\bf{H}}}^{2}+\left\|(\nabla_{\Gamma}v_{j}-\nabla_{\Gamma}v_{k})(s)\right\|_{\mathbf{H}_{\Gamma}}^{2}

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt+\sigma(K)\left\|(v_{j}(s)-v_{k}(s))-(u_{j}(s)-u_{k}(s))\right\|^{2}_{{H}_{\Gamma}}

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt+C\int_{s}^{t}\left\|\big(\partial_{t}(u_{j}-u_{k}),\partial_{t}(v_{j}-v_{k})\big)\right\|_{{{\cal V}^{*}}}^{2}+\left\|u_{j}-u_{k}\right\|_{H^{3}(\Omega)}^{2}+\left\|v_{j}-v_{k}\right\|_{H^{3}(\Gamma)}^{2}.

Since

strongly in

strongly in
H^{3}(\Omega)\times H^{3}(\Gamma)
.
Recalling the convergences (
A.3
)–(
A.6
), we thus infer that the right-hand side in (
Proof.
) tends to zero as

. Consequently,
(\nabla u_{k})_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}
is a Cauchy sequence in

is a Cauchy sequence in
C^{0}([0,T];\mathbf{H}_{\Gamma})
. We thus conclude

\displaystyle\nabla u_{k}

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\text{strongly in $C^{0}([0,T];{\bf{H}})$},

(A.10)

\displaystyle\nabla_{\Gamma}v_{k}

as

Let now

A.7

s
to
t

\displaystyle\left\|\nabla u_{k}(t)\right\|_{{\bf{H}}}^{2}+\left\|\nabla_{\Gamma}v_{k}(t)\right\|_{\mathbf{H}_{\Gamma}}^{2}+\sigma(K)\left\|v_{k}(t)-u_{k}(t)\right\|^{2}_{{H}_{\Gamma}}

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt=\left\|\nabla u_{k}(s)\right\|_{{\bf{H}}}^{2}+\left\|\nabla_{\Gamma}v_{k}(s)\right\|_{\mathbf{H}_{\Gamma}}^{2}+\sigma(K)\left\|v_{k}(s)-u_{k}(s)\right\|^{2}_{{H}_{\Gamma}}
