Improved annealing for sampling from multimodal distributions via landscape modification
Michael C.H. Choi and Jing Zhang

Abstract
. Given a target distribution μ ∝ e − ℋ \mu\propto e^{-\mathcal{H}} to sample from with Hamiltonian ℋ \mathcal{H} on a finite state space, in this paper we propose and analyze new Metropolis-Hastings sampling algorithms that target an alternative distribution μ 1 , α , c f ∝ e − ℋ 1 , α , c f \mu^{f}_{1,\alpha,c}\propto e^{-\mathcal{H}^{f}_{1,\alpha,c}} , where ℋ 1 , α , c f \mathcal{H}_{1,\alpha,c}^{f} is a landscape-modified Hamiltonian which we introduce explicitly. The advantage of the Metropolis dynamics which targets μ 1 , α , c f \mu_{1,\alpha,c}^{f} is that it enjoys reduced critical height described by the threshold parameter c c , function f f , and a penalty parameter α ⩾ 0 \alpha\geqslant 0 that controls the state-dependent effect. First, we identify a convergence-bias tradeoff phenomenon: a larger value of α \alpha gives faster convergence yet a larger bias from μ \mu . To mitigate this bias, in the case of fixed α \alpha we propose a self-normalized estimator that corrects for the bias and prove asymptotic convergence results and Chernoff-type bound of the proposed estimator. Next, we consider the case of annealing the penalty parameter α \alpha so that the state-dependent effect diminishes to zero in the long run. We prove strong ergodicity and bounds on the total variation mixing time of the resulting non-homogeneous chain subject to appropriate assumptions on the decay of α \alpha . Finally, we illustrate the proposed algorithms by comparing their mixing times with the original Metropolis dynamics on a variety of statistical physics models including the ferromagnetic Ising model on the hypercube or the complete graph and the q q -state Potts model on the two-dimensional torus. In these cases, the mixing times of the classical Glauber dynamics are at least exponential in the system size as the critical height grows at least linearly with the size, while the proposed annealing algorithm, with appropriate choice of f f , c c and annealing schedule on α \alpha , mixes rapidly with at most polynomial dependence on the size. The crux of the proof harnesses on the important observation that the reduced critical height can be bounded independently of the size that consequently gives rise to rapid mixing. We emphasize that the techniques developed in this paper are not only limited to Metropolis-Hastings or Glauber dynamics, and are broadly applicable to other sampling algorithms. AMS 2010 subject classifications : 60J27, 60J28, 82B20, 82B80 Keywords : Metropolis-Hastings; simulated annealing; Glauber dynamics; landscape modification; Potts model; spectral gap; self-normalization; Chernoff bound



1.
Introduction

Sampling lies at the heart of modern computational physics and Bayesian statistics. Often we would like to generate samples efficiently from a given target distribution
\mu\propto e^{-\mathcal{H}}
with Hamiltonian
\mathcal{H}
on a finite state space
\mathcal{X}
, without knowing the normalization constant or the partition function of
\mu
. In this setting, the classical Metropolis-Hastings (MH) algorithm offers a way to construct Markov chains that converge to
\mu
in the long run: a proposal chain is utilized to generate a possible move, followed by either accepting or rejecting the proposal move based on a ratio of the target
\mu
. The resulting MH chain is reversible with respect to
\mu
, and various results have been developed concerning its long-time convergence. We refer readers to
Roberts and Rosenthal 2004
;
Brooks et al. 2011
;
Diaconis 2009
for further pointers in the literature on the MH algorithm.

While the MH chain is known to mix quickly on unimodal target
Johndrow and Smith 2018
, in reality, the target distribution is often multimodal and high-dimensional, and as much the MH chain can possibly get stuck at a local mode that consequently leads to torpid mixing. For instance, in Section
6
we investigate the ferromagnetic Ising model on the
n
-dimensional hypercube. The total variation mixing time of the classical MH algorithm in this model is at least exponential in the number of vertices. It is, therefore, crucial to develop acceleration techniques to tackle the challenges arising from sampling multimodal
\mu
and to possibly improve the convergence of Markov chain Monte Carlo algorithms. A wide variety of tools and algorithms have been suggested to overcome this bottleneck, for example, the equi-energy sampler
Kou et al. 2006
;
Andrieu et al. 2008
;
Ebbers and Löwe 2020
, simulated tempering or replica exchange methods
Bhatnagar and Randall 2016
;
Madras and Zheng 2003
;
Woodard et al. 2009
, and more recently non-reversible Markovian Monte Carlo algorithms
Bierkens 2016
;
Hwang et al. 1993
;
Hwang et al. 2005
, to name but a few.

The core of this paper centers around a technique that we call
landscape modification
. The high-level idea is to run algorithms on an alternative landscape with reduced critical height that hopefully accelerate the convergence of the Markov chain. The starting point of this work originates from
Fang et al. 1997
, in which the authors therein analyze a variant of the overdamped Langevin diffusion with state-dependent diffusion coefficient for simulated annealing. In
Choi 2020
, it is realized that the state-dependent effect can be effectively cast to kinetic simulated annealing by simply altering the target Hamiltonian function to optimize, and in
Choi 2021a
the same idea is applied in the discrete setting.

While the focus of the previous papers
Choi 2020
;
Choi 2021a
are on global optimization, in this paper we propose and analyze new MH sampling algorithms that target an alternative distribution
\mu^{f}_{1,\alpha,c}\propto e^{-\mathcal{H}^{f}_{1,\alpha,c}}
, where
\mathcal{H}_{1,\alpha,c}^{f}
is a landscape-modified Hamiltonian which we shall define explicitly in Section
2
. One significant benefit of the Metropolis dynamics which targets
\mu_{1,\alpha,c}^{f}
is that it enjoys reduced critical height which depends on the threshold parameter
c
, function
f
and a penalty parameter
\alpha\geqslant 0
that controls the state-dependent effect.

We summarize the major contributions of this paper as follows:

•

Propose and analyze a self-normalized estimator in the case of fixed penalty
:
To mitigate the bias of sampling from
\mu^{f}_{1,\alpha,c}
instead of
\mu
, we propose a self-normalized estimator that correct for the bias and prove both asymptotic and non-asymptotic convergence results of the proposed estimator.

•

Propose and analyze an annealing algorithm on the penalty parameter
:
The second algorithm that we propose involves annealing the penalty parameter
\alpha
. We prove strong ergodicity and bounds on the total variation mixing time of the resulting non-homogeneous Markov chain subject to appropriate assumptions on the decay of
\alpha
.

•

Rapid mixing of the proposed annealing algorithm on the ferromagnetic Ising model on complete graph or hypercube and the
q
-state Potts model on the two-dimensional torus
:
We illustrate the benefits of utilizing landscape modification in the ferromagnetic Ising model on the hypercube or the complete graph and the
q
-state Potts model on the two-dimensional torus. In these cases, the mixing times of the classical Glauber dynamics are at least exponential in the system size as the critical height grows at least linearly with the number of vertices, while the proposed annealing algorithm, with appropriate choice of
f
,
c
and annealing schedule on
\alpha
, mixes rapidly with at most polynomial dependence on the system size. The crux of the proof relies on the observation that the reduced critical height can be bounded independently of the size that consequently leads to rapid mixing.

We stress that the techniques developed in this paper are not only limited to MH, and are in fact broadly applicable to other sampling algorithms by simply altering the target distribution from
\mu
to
\mu^{f}_{1,\alpha,c}
, followed by either annealing the penalty parameter or to correct the bias via self-normalization.

The rest of this paper is organized as follows. In Section
1.1
, we fix some commonly-used notations throughout the paper. In Section
2
, we first recall the classical MH and the notion of critical or communication height, followed by defining the landscape-modified Hamiltonian
\mathcal{H}_{1,\alpha_{t},c}^{f}
and the MH chain on the modified landscape. We conclude the Section by calculating explicitly
\mathcal{H}_{1,\alpha_{t},c}^{f}
when
f
is a linear, quadratic or exponential function. We then proceed to bound the spectral gap with reduced critical height of the proposed chain in Section
3
. In Section
4
, we consider the case of constant penalty and introduce a self-normalized estimator. We prove related limit theorems as well as non-asymptotic Chernoff bounds of the estimator. In Section
5
, we investigate the situation with time-dependent penalty and offer bounds on the total variation mixing time. Finally, we illustrate the model-independent results obtained in previous Sections in the ferromagnetic Ising model on hypercube or complete graph and the
q
-state Potts model on the two-dimensional torus in Section
6
.

1.1.
Notations

Throughout this paper, we adopt the following notations. For
x,y\in\mathbb{R}
, we write
x_{+}=\max\{x,0\}
to denote the non-negative part of
x
, and
x\wedge y=\min\{x,y\}
. We also write
\mathds{1}_{A}
to be the indicator function of the set
A
.

In order to compare the efficiency of algorithms, we now introduce a few common asymptotic notations. For two functions
g_{1},g_{2}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
, we say that
g_{1}=\mathcal{O}(g_{2})
if there exists a constant
C>0
such that for sufficiently large
x
, we have
|g_{1}(x)|\leqslant Cg_{2}(x)
. On the other hand, we use
\Omega
to denote the tight lower bound, that is, we say that
g_{1}=\Omega(g_{2})
if there exists a constant
C>0
such that for sufficiently large
x
, we have
|g_{1}(x)|\geqslant Cg_{2}(x)
. The
\ell^{\infty}
-norm of a bounded
g
is defined as
\|g\|_{\infty}=\sup_{x}\{|g(x)|\}
.

We say that a sequence of random variables
(X_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
converges almost surely to the random variable
X
, denoted as

X_{n}\rightarrow^{a.s.}X
, if

\mathbb{P}\left(\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}X_{n}=X\right)=1
. Meanwhile, we say that
(X_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
converges in distribution to
X
, shown as

X_{n}\rightarrow^{d}X
, if
\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}(X_{n}\leqslant x)=\mathbb{P}(X\leqslant x)
for any
x\in\mathbb{R}
. Throughout the paper, we denote
\mathbb{P}_{x}
to be the probability measure when the Markov chain is initialized at
x
, and
\mathbb{E}_{\mu}(X)
denotes the expectation of
X
with respect to
\mu
.
In Section
5
, we introduce the time-dependent penalty parameter
(\alpha_{t})_{t\geqslant 0}
and write
\alpha^{\prime}_{t}=d\alpha_{t}/dt
as the derivative of
\alpha_{t}
with respect to
t
.

2.
Preliminaries

Let
\mathcal{H}:\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be a given Hamiltonian function on a finite state space
\mathcal{X}
. The primary objective in this paper is to sample from the target distribution
\mu\propto e^{-\mathcal{H}}
. Among various Markov chain Monte Carlo algorithms to sample from
\mu
, we shall be interested in the classical MH chain and its variants in the continuous-time setting. The MH algorithm offers a way to construct Markov chain that is reversible with respect to the target
\mu
, and now we recall its definition:

Definition 2.1

(Classical MH with target distribution
\mu
)
.

Given a target distribution
\mu
on
\mathcal{X}
and a proposal continuous-time Markov chain
X^{0}=(X^{0}(t))_{t\geqslant 0}
with symmetric generator
Q
, the MH chain has generator given by
M^{0}=M^{0}(Q,\mu)=(M^{0}(x,y))_{x,y\in\mathcal{X}}
, where

M^{0}(x,y)=M^{0}(Q,\mu)(x,y):=\begin{cases}\min\left\{Q(x,y),\dfrac{\mu(y)}{\mu(x)}Q(y,x)\right\}=Q(x,y)e^{-(\mathcal{H}(y)-\mathcal{H}(x))_{+}},&\mbox{if }x\neq y,\\
-\sum_{y:x\neq y}M^{0}(x,y),&\mbox{if }x=y.\end{cases}

We shall also denote the transition semigroup to be
((P^{0})^{t})_{t\geqslant 0}
.

We remark that the superscript of
0
that appears in Definition
2.1
will be explained in Section
2.1
below: it turns out that in the special case when
f=0
, the modified MH dynamics that we propose reduces to the classical MH.

We equip the Hilbert space
\ell^{2}(\mu)
with the usual inner product weighted by the distribution
\mu
: for
g_{1},g_{2}\in\ell^{2}(\mu)
,

\langle g_{1},g_{2}\rangle_{\mu}:=\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}g_{1}(x)g_{2}(x)\mu(x),~~~~\|g_{1}\|_{\mu}=\langle g_{1},g_{1}\rangle_{\mu},~~\text{ and }~~\mu(g_{1})=\langle g_{1},1\rangle_{\mu}.

For any infinitesimal generator
M
of an ergodic continuous-time Markov chain
X
with stationary distribution
\mu
, for instance
M^{0}
, we let
\{0=\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},...,\lambda_{|\mathcal{X}|}\}
be the eigenvalues, counted with their algebraic multiplicities and arranged in non-descending order, of
-M
. In particular,
\lambda_{2}(-M)
is the spectral gap of
X
, that is,

(2.1)

\displaystyle\lambda_{2}(-M):=\inf_{g\in\ell^{2}(\mu):\mu(g)=0}\dfrac{\langle-Mg,g\rangle_{\mu}}{\langle g,g\rangle_{\mu}}.

The spectral gap controls the rate at which a Markov chain converges to its stationary distribution.
In this paper, we shall be interested in bounding the total variation mixing time. To this end, let us recall that for any probability measure
\nu_{1},\nu_{2}
with support on
\mathcal{X}
, the
total variation distance
between
\nu_{1}
and
\nu_{2}
is

||\nu_{1}-\nu_{2}||_{TV}:=\sup_{A\subset\mathcal{X}}|\nu_{1}(A)-\nu_{2}(A)|=\dfrac{1}{2}\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}|\nu_{1}(x)-\nu_{2}(x)|.

The total variation mixing time of
X^{0}
is then defined as:

(2.2)

t_{mix}^{0}(\varepsilon):=\inf\bigg\{t\geqslant 0;~\max_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\left\|\left(P^{0}\right)^{t}(x,\cdot)-\mu\right\|_{TV}\leqslant\varepsilon\bigg\}.

To bound the spectral gap, we recall the concept of critical height or communication height as in the metastability literature
Holley and Stroock 1987
;
Bovier and den Hollander 2015
. For a given irreducible proposal chain with generator
Q
, a path from
x
to
y
is any sequence of points starting from
x_{0}=x,x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n}=y
such that
Q(x_{i-1},x_{i})>0
for
i=1,2,\ldots,n
. For any
x\neq y
, such path exists as
Q
is irreducible. We write
\chi^{x,y}
to be the set of paths from
x
to
y
, and elements of
\chi^{x,y}
are denoted by
\omega=(\omega_{i})_{i=0}^{n}
. The highest value of the original Hamiltonian function
\mathcal{H}
along a path
\omega\in\chi^{x,y}
, known as the elevation, is defined to be

\mathrm{Elev}(\omega):=\max_{\omega_{i}\in\omega}\{\mathcal{H}(\omega_{i})\},

and the lowest possible highest elevation along path(s) from
x
to
y
is

H(x,y):=\min_{\omega\in\chi^{x,y}}\{\mathrm{Elev}(\omega)\}.

For
X^{0}
, the associated
critical height
is defined to be

(2.3)

\displaystyle m^{0}

\displaystyle:=\max_{x,y\in\mathcal{X}}\{H(x,y)-\mathcal{H}(x)-\mathcal{H}(y)+\min_{x}\mathcal{H}(x)\}.

2.1.
Metropolis-Hastings with landscape-modified Hamiltonian

Instead of directly targeting
\mathcal{H}
in the Gibbs distribution
\mu(x)\propto e^{-\mathcal{H}(x)}
in MH, we shall consider sampling from an alternative Gibbs distribution

\displaystyle\mu_{1,\alpha,c}^{f}(x)=\dfrac{e^{-\mathcal{H}_{1,\alpha,c}^{f}(x)}}{Z_{1,\alpha,c}^{f}}\propto e^{-\mathcal{H}_{1,\alpha,c}^{f}(x)}

in the MH chain, where

(2.4)

\displaystyle Z_{1,\alpha,c}^{f}:=\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}e^{-\mathcal{H}_{1,\alpha,c}^{f}(x)}

is the normalization constant. The modified or transformed Hamiltonian function
\mathcal{H}_{1,\alpha,c}^{f}
depends on three parameters: the function
f
, the threshold parameter
c
and the penalty parameter
\alpha
. These parameters are assumed to be chosen in the following manner:

Assumption 2.1
.

(1)

The function
f:\mathbb{R}^{+}\to\mathbb{R}^{+}
is differentiable, non-negative and non-decreasing. Furthermore,
f
satisfies

f(0)=0.

(2)

The threshold parameter
c
is chosen so that
\mathcal{H}_{\max}\geqslant c\geqslant\mathcal{H}_{\min}
, where
\mathcal{H}_{\max}:=\max_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\mathcal{H}(x)
and
\mathcal{H}_{\min}:=\min_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\mathcal{H}(x)
are respectively the global maximum and minimum value of
\mathcal{H}
.

(3)

The penalty parameter
\alpha
is non-negative, i.e.
\alpha\geqslant 0
.

In the rest of this paper, we suppose that Assumption
2.1
holds. We now state the definition of
\mathcal{H}_{1,\alpha,c}^{f}
:

Definition 2.2

(Landscape-modified Hamiltonian
\mathcal{H}_{\epsilon,\alpha,c}^{f}
)
.

Suppose Assumption
2.1
holds. At temperature
\epsilon>0
, the landscape-modified function
\mathcal{H}_{\epsilon,\alpha,c}^{f}
is

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}^{f}_{\epsilon,\alpha,c}(x)

\displaystyle:=\int_{\mathcal{H}_{\min}}^{\mathcal{H}(x)}\dfrac{1}{\alpha f((u-c)_{+})+\epsilon}\,du.

In particular, if we take
\epsilon=1
, then

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{1,\alpha,c}^{f}(x)=\int_{\mathcal{H}_{\min}}^{\mathcal{H}(x)}\dfrac{1}{\alpha f((u-c)_{+})+1}\,du=\begin{cases}\mathcal{H}(x)-\mathcal{H}_{\min},\quad&\text{if}\,x\in\{\mathcal{H}(x)<c\},\\
c-\mathcal{H}_{\min}+\int_{c}^{\mathcal{H}(x)}\dfrac{1}{\alpha f(u-c)+1}\,du,\quad&\text{if}\,x\in\{\mathcal{H}(x)\geqslant c\}.\\
\end{cases}

If we further specialize into
f=0
or
\alpha=0
, then we retrieve the original target distribution as
\mu=\mu^{0}_{1,\alpha,c}=\mu^{f}_{1,0,c}
.

We shall see in Section
3
that one important benefit of running MH on the modified landscape
\mathcal{H}_{1,\alpha,c}^{f}
is that its associated critical height is less than or equal to the original critical height
m^{0}
.

To understand
\mathcal{H}_{1,\alpha,c}^{f}
intuitively, we recall that this transformation is first introduced in
Choi 2021a
for simulated annealing in the discrete setting for global optimization, and is inspired by the overdamped Langevin diffusion with state-dependent diffusion coefficient in
Fang et al. 1997
. The parameter
c
can be interpreted as a threshold parameter: on
\{\mathcal{H}(x)<c\}
,
\mathcal{H}_{1,\alpha,c}^{f}
shares the same scale as the original
\mathcal{H}
; while on
\{\mathcal{H}(x)\geqslant c\}
, the state-dependent effect is activated since the integral

\int_{c}^{\mathcal{H}(x)}\dfrac{1}{\alpha f(u-c)+1}\,du\leqslant\mathcal{H}(x)-c.

Thus, the function
f
can be understood as determining the magnitude of the state-dependent effect: if we choose a larger
f
, the above integral on the left hand side and hence
\mathcal{H}_{1,\alpha,c}^{f}
are smaller. On the other hand, this effect is also controlled and penalized by
\alpha
. A larger
\alpha
gives smaller
\mathcal{H}_{1,\alpha,c}^{f}
yet a larger bias between
\mu_{1,\alpha,c}^{f}
and
\mu
. The name penalty parameter is largely motivated by the work in
Karagulyan and Dalalyan 2020
, where the authors therein consider a penalized Langevin diffusion in which the negative of the gradient of the potential function is perturbed with a penalty that vanishes as time increases.

We now define formally the MH chain that targets
\mu_{1,\alpha,c}^{f}
:

Definition 2.3

(MH with target distribution
\mu_{1,\alpha,c}^{f}
)
.

Suppose Assumption
2.1
holds. Given the target distribution
\mu_{1,\alpha,c}^{f}
on
\mathcal{X}
and a proposal continuous-time Markov chain
X_{1,\alpha,c}^{f}=(X_{1,\alpha,c}^{f}(t))_{t\geqslant 0}
with symmetric generator
Q
, the MH chain has generator given by
M_{1,\alpha,c}^{f}=M_{1,\alpha,c}^{f}(Q,\mu)=(M_{1,\alpha,c}^{f}(x,y))_{x,y\in\mathcal{X}}
, where

M_{1,\alpha,c}^{f}(x,y):=\begin{cases}\min\left\{Q(x,y),\dfrac{\mu_{1,\alpha,c}^{f}(y)}{\mu_{1,\alpha,c}^{f}(x)}Q(y,x)\right\}=Q(x,y)e^{-(\mathcal{H}_{1,\alpha,c}^{f}(y)-\mathcal{H}_{1,\alpha,c}^{f}(x))_{+}},&\mbox{if }x\neq y,\\
-\sum_{y:x\neq y}M_{1,\alpha,c}^{f}(x,y),&\mbox{if }x=y.\end{cases}

We shall also denote the transition semigroup to be
((P_{1,\alpha,c}^{f})^{t})_{t\geqslant 0}
, and its spectral gap to be

\lambda_{2}(-M_{1,\alpha,c}^{f}):=\inf_{g\in\ell^{2}(\mu_{1,\alpha,c}^{f}):\mu_{1,\alpha,c}^{f}(g)=0}\dfrac{\langle-M_{1,\alpha,c}^{f}g,g\rangle_{\mu_{1,\alpha,c}^{f}}}{\langle g,g\rangle_{\mu_{1,\alpha,c}^{f}}}.

Note that if
f=0
or
\alpha=0
, the above dynamics reduce to the classical MH with target distribution
\mu
since

M^{0}=M^{0}_{1,\alpha,c}=M^{f}_{1,0,c}.

Remark 2.1
.

In view of Definition
2.3
, the knowledge of
\mathcal{H}_{\min}
is not required in simulating
X_{1,\alpha,c}^{f}
since the difference, for
x,y\in\mathcal{X}
,

\mathcal{H}_{1,\alpha,c}^{f}(y)-\mathcal{H}_{1,\alpha,c}^{f}(x)=\int_{\mathcal{H}(x)}^{\mathcal{H}(y)}\dfrac{1}{\alpha f((u-c)_{+})+1}\,du

does not depend on
\mathcal{H}_{\min}
.

In the following subsections, we compute explicitly the landscape-modified Hamiltonian
\mathcal{H}_{1,\alpha,c}^{f}
with linear, quadratic or exponential
f
.

2.2.
Landscape-modified Hamiltonian
\mathcal{H}_{1,\alpha,c}^{f}
with
f(x)=x

In this subsection, we compute explicitly
\mathcal{H}_{1,\alpha,c}^{f}
when we specialize into
f(x)=x
, which gives

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{1,\alpha,c}^{f}(x)=\begin{cases}\mathcal{H}(x)-\mathcal{H}_{\min},\quad&\text{if}\,x\in\{\mathcal{H}(x)<c\},\\
c-\mathcal{H}_{\min}+\dfrac{1}{\alpha}\ln(1+\alpha(\mathcal{H}(x)-c)),\quad&\text{if}\,x\in\{\mathcal{H}(x)\geqslant c\}.\\
\end{cases}

Using the fact that

\lim_{\alpha\to 0}\dfrac{1}{\alpha}\ln(1+\alpha(\mathcal{H}(x)-c))=\mathcal{H}(x)-c,

we thus obtain, for
x\in\mathcal{X}
,

\lim_{\alpha\to 0}\mathcal{H}_{1,\alpha,c}^{f}(x)=\mathcal{H}(x)-\mathcal{H}_{\min}.

In other words, we retrieve the original target distribution
\mu\propto{e^{-\mathcal{H}}}\propto e^{-(\mathcal{H}-\mathcal{H}_{\min})}
when we take
\alpha\to 0
, as expected.

2.3.
Landscape-modified Hamiltonian
\mathcal{H}_{1,\alpha,c}^{f}
with
f(x)=x^{2}

In this subsection, we write down explicitly
\mathcal{H}_{1,\alpha,c}^{f}
when we specialize into
f(x)=x^{2}
, which yields

(2.5)

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{1,\alpha,c}^{f}(x)=\begin{cases}\mathcal{H}(x)-\mathcal{H}_{\min},\quad&\text{if}\,x\in\{\mathcal{H}(x)<c\},\\
c-\mathcal{H}_{\min}+\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha}}\arctan(\sqrt{\alpha}(\mathcal{H}(x)-c)),\quad&\text{if}\,x\in\{\mathcal{H}(x)\geqslant c\}.\\
\end{cases}

As a result, we observe that

\max_{x}\mathcal{H}_{1,\alpha,c}^{f}(x)\leqslant c-\mathcal{H}_{\min}+\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha}}\dfrac{\pi}{2}.

Consequently, if we take
c=\mathcal{H}_{\min}+\delta
, where
\delta>0
, the maximum of
\mathcal{H}_{1,\alpha,c}^{f}
can be bounded above independently of
\mathcal{H}_{\max}
or
\mathcal{H}_{\min}
. This important observation will be utilized in Section
6
on a few statistical physics models to illustrate rapid mixing of the proposed annealing algorithm in Section
5
.

2.4.
Landscape-modified Hamiltonian
\mathcal{H}_{1,\alpha,c}^{f}
with
f(x)=e^{x}-1

In this subsection, we calculate explicitly
\mathcal{H}_{1,\alpha,c}^{f}
when we specialize into
f(x)=e^{x}-1
, which yields

(2.6)

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{1,\alpha,c}^{f}(x)=\begin{cases}\mathcal{H}(x)-\mathcal{H}_{\min},\quad&\text{if}\,x\in\{\mathcal{H}(x)<c\},\\
c-\mathcal{H}_{\min}+\dfrac{\log\left(\alpha\left(e^{\mathcal{H}(x)}-1\right)+1\right)-\log\left(\alpha\left(e^{c}-1\right)+1\right)-(\mathcal{H}(x)-c)}{\alpha-1},\quad&\text{if}\,x\in\{\mathcal{H}(x)\geqslant c\}.\\
\end{cases}

Also, we know that

\lim_{\alpha\to 0}\dfrac{\log(\alpha(e^{\mathcal{H}(x)}-1)+1)}{\alpha-1}=0,

thus

\lim_{\alpha\to 0}\dfrac{\log\left(\alpha\left(e^{\mathcal{H}(x)}-1\right)+1\right)-\log\left(\alpha\left(e^{c}-1\right)+1\right)-(\mathcal{H}(x)-c)}{\alpha-1}=H(x)-c,

which implies that, for
x\in\mathcal{X}
,

\lim_{\alpha\to 0}\mathcal{H}_{1,\alpha,c}^{f}(x)=\mathcal{H}(x)-\mathcal{H}_{\min}.

In other words, we recover the original target distribution
\mu\propto{e^{-\mathcal{H}}}\propto e^{-(\mathcal{H}-\mathcal{H}_{\min})}
when we take
\alpha\to 0
, as expected.

3.
Spectral gap bounds in terms of the reduced critical height

In this Section, we bound the spectral gap of
X^{f}_{1,\alpha,c}
, the MH dynamics with target distribution
\pi^{f}_{1,\alpha,c}
as introduced in Definition
2.3
, in terms of its associated critical height. It turns out that this critical height is reduced and is less than or equal to the original critical height
m^{0}
, which demonstrates one advantage of running MH algorithms on the modified landscape described by
\mathcal{H}^{f}_{1,\alpha,c}
.

Recall that we write
\chi^{x,y}
to be the set of paths from
x
to
y
as specified by the proposal generator. The highest value of the landscape-modified Hamiltonian function
\mathcal{H}^{f}_{1,\alpha,c}
along a path
\omega\in\chi^{x,y}
is defined to be

\mathrm{Elev}^{f}_{1,\alpha,c}(\omega):=\max_{\omega_{i}\in\omega}\{\mathcal{H}^{f}_{1,\alpha,c}(\omega_{i})\},

and the lowest possible highest elevation along path(s) from
x
to
y
is

H^{f}_{1,\alpha,c}(x,y):=\min_{\omega\in\chi^{x,y}}\{\mathrm{Elev}^{f}_{1,\alpha,c}(\omega)\}.

With the above notations in mind, we define the concept of reduced critical height:

Definition 3.1

(Reduced critical height)
.

Given the same proposal chain with generator
Q
of both
X^{0}
and
X^{f}_{1,\alpha,c}
, Then the critical height of
X^{f}_{1,\alpha,c}
is defined to be

m_{1,\alpha,c}^{f}:=\max_{x,y\in\mathcal{X}}\{H^{f}_{1,\alpha,c}(x,y)-\mathcal{H}^{f}_{1,\alpha,c}(x)-\mathcal{H}^{f}_{1,\alpha,c}(y)\}.

Remark 3.1
.

Suppose
(x^{*},y^{*})
attains the critical height
m^{f}_{1,\alpha,c}
of
X^{f}_{1,\alpha,c}
in the sense that it satisfies
m^{f}_{1,\alpha,c}=\mathcal{H}^{f}_{1,\alpha,c}(x^{*})-\mathcal{H}^{f}_{1,\alpha,c}(y^{*})
.
Since
\alpha\geqslant 0
and
f\geqslant 0
we thus have

\displaystyle m_{1,\alpha,c}^{f}=\int_{\mathcal{H}\left(y^{*}\right)}^{\mathcal{H}\left(x^{*}\right)}\frac{1}{\alpha f\left((u-c)_{+}\right)+1}\,du\leqslant\mathcal{H}(x^{*})-\mathcal{H}(y^{*})\leqslant m^{0}.

Thus, the reduced critical height
m_{1,\alpha,c}^{f}
is bounded above by the original critical height
m^{0}
. This illustrates one advantage of using the chain
X^{f}_{1,\alpha,c}
.

Using
(
Holley and Stroock 1988
, Lemma
2.7
)
with the Hamiltonian function therein be replaced by
\mathcal{H}^{f}_{1,\alpha,c}
and at temperature
1
, we can readily bound the spectral gap
\lambda_{2}(-M^{f}_{1,\alpha,c})
in terms of
m^{f}_{1,\alpha,c}
:

Lemma 3.1
.

\tilde{C}e^{-m_{1,\alpha,c}^{f}}\geqslant\lambda_{2}(-M_{1,\alpha_{t},c}^{f})\geqslant Ce^{-m_{1,\alpha,c}^{f}}\geqslant Ce^{-m^{0}},

where
C
and
\tilde{C}
only depend on
\mathcal{X}
and

m_{1,\alpha,c}^{f}
is the reduced critical height as introduced in Definition
3.1
.

Consequently, thanks to Lemma
3.1
we obtain

Corollary 3.1
.

Suppose
\alpha=(\alpha_{t})_{t\geqslant 0}
is time-dependent. We then have

(3.1)

\lambda_{2}(-M_{1,\alpha_{t},c}^{f})\geqslant Ce^{-m^{0}}

where
Ce^{-m^{0}}
is independent of
t
, which implies that

\int_{0}^{\infty}\lambda_{2}(-M_{1,\alpha_{t},c}^{f})dt=\infty.

We remark that Corollary
3.1
is useful in establishing strong ergodicity in Section
5
when we consider the time-dependent penalty.

4.
Constant penalty
\alpha_{t}=\alpha
for all
t\geqslant 0

In this Section, we focus in the constant penalty case, i.e. the penalty parameter takes on a fixed value with
\alpha_{t}=\alpha>0
for all
t\geqslant 0
. In this setting, we first identify a phenomenon that we call the (total-variation) convergence-bias tradeoff: using triangle inequality, we note that, for any
x\in\mathcal{X}
,

(4.1)

\displaystyle\left\|\left(P_{1,\alpha,c}^{f}\right)^{t}(x,\cdot)-\mu\right\|_{TV}\leqslant\underbrace{\left\|\left(P_{1,\alpha,c}^{f}\right)^{t}(x,\cdot)-\mu_{1,\alpha,c}^{f}\right\|_{TV}}_{\text{convergence}}+\underbrace{\|\mu_{1,\alpha,c}^{f}-\mu\|_{TV}}_{\text{bias}}.

On one hand, we would like to choose
\alpha
to be large so that the reduced critical height
m^{f}_{1,\alpha,c}
is small, leading to fast convergence of
\left(P_{1,\alpha,c}^{f}\right)^{t}(x,\cdot)
towards
\mu_{1,\alpha,c}^{f}
. On the other hand, we would like to choose
\alpha
to be small so that the bias between
\mu_{1,\alpha,c}^{f}
and
\mu
is small when measured in terms of the total variation distance. We quantify the above statements into the following theorem:

Theorem 4.1
.

Suppose that Assumption
2.1
holds.

(1)

(Large
\alpha
leads to fast convergence): For
x\in\mathcal{X}
,

\displaystyle\dfrac{1}{2}e^{-\lambda_{2}(-M_{1,\alpha,c}^{f})t}\leqslant\left\|\left(P_{1,\alpha,c}^{f}\right)^{t}(x,\cdot)-\mu_{1,\alpha,c}^{f}\right\|_{TV}\leqslant\dfrac{e^{\mathcal{H}_{\max}-\mathcal{H}_{\min}}|\mathcal{X}|}{2}e^{-\lambda_{2}(-M_{1,\alpha,c}^{f})t}.

(2)

(Small
\alpha
gives small total variation bias):

\|\mu_{1,\alpha,c}^{f}-\mu\|_{TV}=\mathcal{O}(\alpha).

The proof of Theorem
4.1
is given in Section
4.1
.

To mitigate the bias induced by running the Markov chain
X^{f}_{1,\alpha,c}
to approximately sample from
\mu_{1,\alpha,c}^{f}
instead of
\mu
, in this Section, we consider a self-normalized estimator to correct the bias. Define
the weight function
to be

w(x)=w^{f}_{1,\alpha,c}(x):=\dfrac{e^{-(\mathcal{H}(x)-\mathcal{H}_{\min})}}{e^{-\mathcal{H}_{1,\alpha,c}^{f}(x)}},

and for any
g\in\ell^{2}(\mu)
,
the self-normalized estimator
by

\dfrac{\int_{0}^{t}w(X_{1,\alpha,c}^{f}(s))g(X_{1,\alpha,c}^{f}(s))\,ds}{\int_{0}^{t}w(X_{1,\alpha,c}^{f}(s))\,ds}.

We remark that the weight function
w(x)
can be computed readily since

\displaystyle w(x)

\displaystyle=\dfrac{e^{-(\mathcal{H}(x)-\mathcal{H}_{\min})}}{e^{-\mathcal{H}_{1,\alpha,c}^{f}(x)}}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{e^{-(\mathcal{H}(x)-\mathcal{H}_{\min})}}{\exp\bigg\{-(c\wedge\mathcal{H}(x)-\mathcal{H}_{\min})-\int_{c\wedge\mathcal{H}(x)}^{\mathcal{H}(x)}\dfrac{1}{\alpha f((u-c)_{+})+1}\,du\bigg\}}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{e^{-\mathcal{H}(x)}}{\exp\bigg\{-c\wedge\mathcal{H}(x)-\int_{c\wedge\mathcal{H}(x)}^{\mathcal{H}(x)}\dfrac{1}{\alpha f((u-c)_{+})+1}\,du\bigg\}}

does not depend on
\mathcal{H}_{\min}
. In the special case where
f=0
or
\alpha=0
, we note that
w=1
. Recall that
Z_{1,\alpha,c}^{f}
is the normalization constant of
\mu_{1,\alpha,c}^{f}
(
2.4
), and we adopt the notation

Z^{0}:=Z^{0}_{1,\alpha,c}=\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}e^{-(\mathcal{H}(x)-\mathcal{H}_{\min})}

to be the normalization constant when
f=0
. We then have the following asymptotic convergence results for the proposed self-normalized estimator:

Theorem 4.2
.

For
g\in\ell^{2}(\mu)
, as
t\to\infty
we have

(1)

(Strong consistency of the self-normalized estimator)

\dfrac{\int_{0}^{t}w(X_{1,\alpha,c}^{f}(s))g(X_{1,\alpha,c}^{f}(s))\,ds}{\int_{0}^{t}w(X_{1,\alpha,c}^{f}(s))\,ds}\rightarrow^{a.s.}\mu(g).

(2)

(Central limit theorem of the self-normalized estimator)

\sqrt{t}\left(\dfrac{\int_{0}^{t}w(X_{1,\alpha,c}^{f}(s))g(X_{1,\alpha,c}^{f}(s))\,ds}{\int_{0}^{t}w(X_{1,\alpha,c}^{f}(s))\,ds}-\mu(g)\right)\rightarrow^{d}\dfrac{Z_{1,\alpha,c}^{f}}{Z^{0}}N(0,\mathrm{Var}_{\mu_{1,\alpha,c}^{f}}(wg)).

where the limiting variance is defined to be

\mathrm{Var}_{\mu_{1,\alpha,c}^{f}}(wg):=\lim_{t\to\infty}\mathbb{E}_{\mu}\left(\dfrac{\int_{0}^{t}w(X_{1,\alpha,c}^{f}(s))g(X_{1,\alpha,c}^{f}(s))\,ds}{\int_{0}^{t}w(X_{1,\alpha,c}^{f}(s))\,ds}-\mu(g)\right)^{2}.

In particular, in the special case when
f=0
or
\alpha=0
so that
w=1
, the above results reduce to the classical Markov chain law of large number and central limit theorem for the MH chain respectively.

The proof of Theorem
4.2
is delayed to Section
4.2
.

Our next result concerns the finite-time non-asymptotic deviation of the self-normalized estimator from the target mean
\mu(g)
. To this end, for any
\eta,\varepsilon>0
,
g\in\ell^{2}(\mu)
, we define the first time such that the deviation probability is less than or equal to
\varepsilon
to be:

(4.2)

\displaystyle t_{conc}(\eta,\varepsilon):=\inf\bigg\{t\geqslant 0;~\sup_{g\in\ell^{2}(\mu);~\left\lVert g\right\rVert_{\infty}\leqslant a}\sup_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\mathbb{P}_{x}\left(\dfrac{\int_{0}^{t}w(X_{1,\alpha,c}^{f}(s))g(X_{1,\alpha,c}^{f}(s))\,ds}{\int_{0}^{t}w(X_{1,\alpha,c}^{f}(s))\,ds}-\mu(g)\geqslant\eta\right)\leqslant\varepsilon\bigg\}.

Theorem 4.3

(Chernoff’s bound of the self-normalized estimator)
.

Given
\eta,\varepsilon>0
,
g\in\ell^{2}(\mu)
such that
\left\lVert g\right\rVert_{\infty}\leqslant a
, if we have

t\geqslant\dfrac{Z_{1,\alpha,c}^{f}}{Z^{0}}\dfrac{32a^{2}(1+\eta)^{2}}{\lambda_{2}(-M_{1,\alpha,c}^{f})\eta^{2}}\ln\left(\dfrac{1}{\varepsilon\min_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\mu_{1,\alpha,c}^{f}(x)}\right),

then for any
x\in\mathcal{X}
,

\mathbb{P}_{x}\left(\dfrac{\int_{0}^{t}w(X_{1,\alpha,c}^{f}(s))g(X_{1,\alpha,c}^{f}(s))\,ds}{\int_{0}^{t}w(X_{1,\alpha,c}^{f}(s))\,ds}-\mu(g)\geqslant\eta\right)\leqslant\varepsilon.

In particular,

\displaystyle t_{conc}(\eta,\varepsilon)=\mathcal{O}\left(|\mathcal{X}|\dfrac{a^{2}(1+\eta)^{2}}{\lambda_{2}(-M_{1,\alpha,c}^{f})\eta^{2}}\ln\left(\dfrac{|\mathcal{X}|\exp\{\max_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\mathcal{H}_{1,\alpha,c}^{f}(x)\}}{\varepsilon}\right)\right).

The proof of Theorem
4.3
can be found in Section
4.3
, which utilizes the results in
Lezaud 2001
.

4.1.
Proof of Theorem
4.1

First, we prove item (
1
). For the lower bound, let
\phi
be an eigenfunction of
-M_{1,\alpha,c}^{f}
with eigenvalue
\lambda\neq 0
. For
x\in\mathcal{X}
, we have

\displaystyle e^{-\lambda t}|\phi(x)|=\left|\left(P_{1,\alpha,c}^{f}\right)^{t}\phi(x)\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\sum_{y\in\mathcal{X}}\left[\left(P_{1,\alpha,c}^{f}\right)^{t}(x,y)\phi(y)-\mu_{1,\alpha,c}^{f}(y)\phi(y)\right]\right|

\displaystyle\leqslant\|\phi\|_{\infty}2\left\|\left(P_{1,\alpha,c}^{f}\right)^{t}(x,\cdot)-\mu_{1,\alpha,c}^{f}\right\|_{TV}.

Taking
x

|\phi(x)|=\|\phi\|_{\infty}
and
\lambda=\lambda_{2}(-M_{1,\alpha,c}^{f})
yield the desired result since

e^{-\lambda_{2}(-M_{1,\alpha,c}^{f})t}\leqslant 2\left\|\left(P_{1,\alpha,c}^{f}\right)^{t}(x,\cdot)-\mu_{1,\alpha,c}^{f}\right\|_{TV}.

As for the upper bound, it follows from
(
Levin et al. 2009

(20.21)

that

\displaystyle 2\left\|\left(P_{1,\alpha,c}^{f}\right)^{t}(x,\cdot)-\mu_{1,\alpha,c}^{f}\right\|_{TV}

\displaystyle\leqslant e^{-\lambda_{2}(-M_{1,\alpha,c}^{f})t}\sum_{y\in\mathcal{X}}\frac{\mu_{1,\alpha,c}^{f}(y)}{\sqrt{\mu_{1,\alpha,c}^{f}(y)\mu_{1,\alpha,c}^{f}(x)}}

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{e^{-\lambda_{2}(-M_{1,\alpha,c}^{f})t}}{\min_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\mu_{1,\alpha,c}^{f}(x)}

\displaystyle=\frac{Z_{1,\alpha,c}^{f}}{e^{-\max_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\mathcal{H}_{1,\alpha,c}^{f}(x)}}e^{-\lambda_{2}(-M_{1,\alpha,c}^{f})t}

\displaystyle\leqslant e^{\mathcal{H}_{\max}-\mathcal{H}_{\min}}Z_{1,\alpha,c}^{f}e^{-\lambda_{2}(-M_{1,\alpha,c}^{f})t}

\displaystyle\leqslant e^{\mathcal{H}_{\max}-\mathcal{H}_{\min}}|\mathcal{X}|e^{-\lambda_{2}(-M_{1,\alpha,c}^{f})t}.

Next, we prove item (
2
). First, we compute the difference of
Z_{1,\alpha,c}^{f}
and
Z^{0}

\displaystyle Z_{1,\alpha,c}^{f}-Z^{0}

\displaystyle=\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}e^{-\mathcal{H}_{1,\alpha,c}^{f}(x)}-e^{-(\mathcal{H}(x)-\mathcal{H}_{\min})}

\displaystyle=\sum_{x:\mathcal{H}(x)>c}e^{-\mathcal{H}_{1,\alpha,c}^{f}(x)}-e^{-(\mathcal{H}(x)-\mathcal{H}_{\min})}

\displaystyle\leqslant\sum_{x:\mathcal{H}(x)>c}\left|e^{-\mathcal{H}_{1,\alpha,c}^{f}(x)}-e^{-(\mathcal{H}(x)-\mathcal{H}_{\min})}\right|

\displaystyle\leqslant\sum_{x:\mathcal{H}(x)>c}\left|{\mathcal{H}_{1,\alpha,c}^{f}(x)}-{(\mathcal{H}(x)-\mathcal{H}_{\min})}\right|

\displaystyle=\sum_{x:\mathcal{H}(x)>c}\left|\int_{\mathcal{H}_{\min}}^{\mathcal{H}(x)}\left(\frac{1}{\alpha f\left((u-c)_{+}\right)+1}-1\right)du\right|

\displaystyle\leqslant\sum_{x:\mathcal{H}(x)>c}\int_{\mathcal{H}_{\min}}^{\mathcal{H}(x)}{\alpha f\left((u-c)_{+}\right)}du\leqslant\alpha{\xi}

where
\xi:=|{x:\mathcal{H}(x)>c}|({\mathcal{H}_{\max}-\mathcal{H}_{\min}}){f((\mathcal{H}_{\max}-c)_{+})}
. Using the bound above, we calculate the total variation distance between
\mu_{1,\alpha,c}^{f}
and
\mu

\displaystyle\|\mu_{1,\alpha,c}^{f}-\mu\|_{TV}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}|\mu_{1,\alpha,c}^{f}(x)-\mu(x)|

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\left|\frac{e^{-\mathcal{H}_{1,\alpha,c}^{f}(x)}}{Z_{1,\alpha,c}^{f}}-\frac{e^{-(\mathcal{H}(x)-\mathcal{H}_{\min})}}{Z^{0}}\right|

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\left|\frac{e^{-\mathcal{H}_{1,\alpha,c}^{f}(x)}}{Z_{1,\alpha,c}^{f}}-\frac{e^{-(\mathcal{H}(x)-\mathcal{H}_{\min})}}{Z_{1,\alpha,c}^{f}}+\frac{e^{-(\mathcal{H}(x)-\mathcal{H}_{\min})}}{Z_{1,\alpha,c}^{f}}-\frac{e^{-(\mathcal{H}(x)-\mathcal{H}_{\min})}}{Z^{0}}\right|

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{1}{2}\frac{Z_{1,\alpha,c}^{f}-Z^{0}}{Z_{1,\alpha,c}^{f}}+\frac{1}{2}\frac{Z_{1,\alpha,c}^{f}-Z^{0}}{Z_{1,\alpha,c}^{f}}\leqslant\dfrac{\alpha\xi}{|\mathcal{X}|}e^{\max_{x}\mathcal{H}_{1,\alpha,c}^{f}(x)}\leqslant\dfrac{\alpha\xi}{|\mathcal{X}|}e^{\mathcal{H}_{\max}-\mathcal{H}_{\min}}=\mathcal{O}(\alpha).

First, we prove item (

Next, we prove item (

Using the Markov chain central limit theorem, we have

\sqrt{t}\left(\frac{1}{t}\int_{0}^{t}w(X_{1,\alpha,c}^{f}(s))g(X_{1,\alpha,c}^{f}(s))\,ds-\mu_{1,\alpha,c}^{f}(wg)\right)\rightarrow^{d}N(0,\mathrm{Var}_{\mu_{1,\alpha,c}^{f}}(wg)).

Finally, using

gives the desired result since

\sqrt{t}\left(\dfrac{\int_{0}^{t}w(X_{1,\alpha,c}^{f}(s))g(X_{1,\alpha,c}^{f}(s))\,ds}{\int_{0}^{t}w(X_{1,\alpha,c}^{f}(s))\,ds}-\mu(g)\right)\rightarrow^{d}\dfrac{Z_{1,\alpha,c}^{f}}{Z^{0}}N(0,\mathrm{Var}_{\mu_{1,\alpha,c}^{f}}(wg)).

5.
Time-dependent penalty
(\alpha_{t})_{t\geqslant 0}

In Section
4
, we focus on the constant penalty parameter case: we run a homogeneous Markov chain
X_{1,\alpha,c}^{f}=(X_{1,\alpha,c}^{f}(t))_{t\geqslant 0}
at a fixed
\alpha
, and correct the bias by proposing a self-normalized estimator. In this Section, instead of fixing
\alpha
, we anneal the penalty parameter by sending
\alpha_{t}\to 0
as the time
t\to\infty
. This results in running a
non-homogeneous
Markov chain
X_{1,\bm{\alpha},c}^{f}=(X_{1,\bm{\alpha},c}^{f}(t))_{t\geqslant 0}
to approximately sample from
\mu
, where
\bm{\alpha}=(\alpha_{t})_{t\geqslant 0}
. Unlike simulated annealing where the temperature parameter is annealed, we instead anneal the penalty parameter so that the state-dependent effect diminishes to zero in the long run, which allows for approximate sampling from the target distribution
\mu
.

In the following, we shall denote, for
x\in\mathcal{X}
and
A\subseteq\mathcal{X}
,

(P_{1,\bm{\alpha},c}^{f})^{t}(x,A):=e^{\int_{0}^{t}M^{f}_{1,\alpha_{s},c}\,ds}(x,A)=\mathbb{P}_{x}(X_{1,\bm{\alpha},c}^{f}(t)\in A).

Our first main result states that the non-homogeneous chain
X_{1,\bm{\alpha},c}^{f}
is weakly ergodic as long as
\alpha^{\prime}_{t}\to 0
. In other words, if the change of
\alpha_{t}
approaches
0
as
t\to\infty
, the non-homogeneous chain tracks closely its instantaneous stationary distribution
\mu_{1,\alpha_{t},c}^{f}
:

Theorem 5.1

(Weak ergodicity of
X_{1,\bm{\alpha},c}^{f}
)
.

If
\alpha^{\prime}_{t}\to 0
as
t\to\infty
, then for any
x\in\mathcal{X}
,

(5.1)

\left\|\left(P_{1,\bm{\alpha},c}^{f}\right)^{t}(x,\cdot)-\mu_{1,\alpha_{t},c}^{f}\right\|_{TV}\to 0\text{ as }t\to\infty.

The proof of Theorem
5.1
is given in Section
5.1
.

If we impose the additional assumption that
\alpha_{t}\downarrow 0
, then the non-homogeneous chain
X_{1,\bm{\alpha},c}^{f}
is strongly ergodic and converges to the desired target distribution
\mu
in total variation distance:

Theorem 5.2

(Strong ergodicity of
X_{1,\bm{\alpha},c}^{f}
)
.

If
\alpha_{t}\downarrow 0,~\alpha^{\prime}_{t}\to 0
as
t\to\infty
, then for any
x\in\mathcal{X}
,

\left\|\left(P_{1,\bm{\alpha},c}^{f}\right)^{t}(x,\cdot)-\mu\right\|_{TV}\to 0\text{ as }t\to\infty.

Unlike simulated annealing where logarithmic cooling schedule of the temperature is commonly employed to ensure strong ergodicity
Choi 2021b
;
Miclo 1992
;
Monmarché 2018
, in this context the penalty parameter can decay to zero at a faster rate than logarithmic cooling while maintaining strong ergodicity. For instance, we can adopt the exponential annealing schedule by taking
\alpha_{t}=e^{-t}
. With this choice, we note that the conditions in Theorem

are satisfied.

The proof of Theorem
5.2
is deferred to Section
5.2
.

In our next result, we quantify the convergence rate of the total variation mixing by specializing into the case where
f(x)=x^{2}
. The primary reason for considering quadratic

is that the reduced critical height can be bounded independently of the system size, leading to rapid mixing in the examples to be presented in Section

.

We would be interested in estimating
the total variation mixing time

of the non-homogeneous chain
X_{1,\bm{\alpha},c}^{f}
, defined to be for

,

(5.2)

\displaystyle t_{mix}^{f}(\varepsilon):=\inf\bigg\{t\geqslant 0;~\max_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\left\|\left(P_{1,\bm{\alpha},c}^{f}\right)^{t}(x,\cdot)-\mu\right\|_{TV}\leqslant\varepsilon\bigg\}.

Note that the dependence on
\bm{\alpha}
and

Theorem 5.3

(Total variation mixing time with

.

Suppose that

, where we recall

For

.
For any

\left\|\left(P_{1,\bm{\alpha},c}^{f}\right)^{t}(x,\cdot)-\mu\right\|_{TV}={\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{O}(|\mathcal{X}|e^{\mathcal{M}}e^{-\mathcal{M}\beta_{t}}).}

In particular,

\displaystyle t_{mix}^{f}(\varepsilon)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}\left(\dfrac{1}{p}\left(\exp\bigg\{\dfrac{3\pi(\ln|\mathcal{X}|+\mathcal{M}+\log(1/\varepsilon))}{2\mathcal{M}}\bigg\}-\exp\{3\pi/2\}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}\left(\dfrac{(\ln|\mathcal{X}|)\vee\left(\mathcal{H}_{\max}-\mathcal{H}_{\min}\right)}{K}\left(\dfrac{1}{\varepsilon}\right)^{\frac{3\pi}{2\left((\ln|\mathcal{X}|)\vee\left(\mathcal{H}_{\max}-\mathcal{H}_{\min}\right)\right)}}\right).

5.1.
Proof of Theorem

In view of

, it suffices to find

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\lambda_{2}(-M_{1,\alpha_{t},c}^{f})dt=+\infty.

). We proceed to show (
5.4

We observe that

G_{1,\alpha_{t},c}^{f}(x)=\int_{\mathcal{H}_{\min}}^{\mathcal{H}(x)}\frac{\alpha^{\prime}_{t}f((u-c)_{+})}{(\alpha_{t}f\left((u-c)_{+}\right)+1)^{2}}du\geqslant\alpha_{t}^{\prime}f(\mathcal{H}_{\max}-c)(\mathcal{H}_{\max}-\mathcal{H}_{\min})

and

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\mu_{1,\alpha_{t},c}^{f}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{Z_{1,\alpha_{t},c}^{f}}\frac{d}{dt}\left(e^{-\mathcal{H}_{1,\alpha_{t},c}^{f}(x)}\right)+e^{-\mathcal{H}_{1,\alpha_{t},c}^{f}(x)}\frac{d}{dt}\left(\frac{1}{Z_{1,\alpha_{t},c}^{f}}\right)

\displaystyle=G_{1,\alpha_{t},c}^{f}(x)\cdot\mu_{1,\alpha_{t},c}^{f}(x)-\mu_{1,\alpha_{t},c}^{f}(x)\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\mu_{1,\alpha_{t},c}^{f}(x)\cdot G_{1,\alpha_{t},c}^{f}(x),

which implies

\displaystyle\left|\frac{d}{dt}\mu_{1,\alpha_{t},c}^{f}(x)\right|

\displaystyle\leqslant\mu_{1,\alpha_{t},c}^{f}(x)\left(\max_{x}G_{1,\alpha_{t},c}^{f}(x)-\min_{x}G_{1,\alpha_{t},c}^{f}(x)\right)

Proof of Theorem

We follow the same roadmap as the proof of
(

. First, using the Cauchy-Schwartz inequality, we have

\displaystyle\left\|\left(P_{1,\bm{\alpha},c}^{f}\right)^{t}(x,\cdot)-\mu\right\|_{TV}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{y\in\mathcal{X}}\left|\left(P_{1,\bm{\alpha},c}^{f}\right)^{t}(x,y)-\mu(y)\right|

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{1}{2}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\sqrt{\sum_{y\in\mathcal{X}}\left(\frac{\left(P_{1,\bm{\alpha},c}^{f}\right)^{t}(x,y)-\mu(y)}{\mu_{1,\alpha_{t},c}^{f}(y)}\right)^{2}\mu_{1,\alpha_{t},c}^{f}(y)}=:\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{u_{t}(x)}}

Next, we calculate the time derivative of

\displaystyle\frac{du_{t}(x)}{dt}

\displaystyle=\frac{d}{dt}\left(\sum_{y\in\mathcal{X}}\left(g_{t}(x,y)-\frac{\mu(y)}{\mu_{1,\alpha_{t},c}^{f}(y)}\right)^{2}\mu_{1,\alpha_{t},c}^{f}(y)\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{y\in\mathcal{X}}2\left(g_{t}(x,y)-\frac{\mu(y)}{\mu_{1,\alpha_{t},c}^{f}(y)}\right)\left(\mu_{1,\alpha_{t},c}^{f}(y)\frac{dg_{t}(x,y)}{dt}+\frac{\mu(y)}{\mu_{1,\alpha_{t},c}^{f}(y)}\frac{d\mu_{1,\alpha_{t},c}^{f}(y)}{dt}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad+\sum_{y\in\mathcal{X}}\left(g_{t}(x,y)-\frac{\mu(y)}{\mu_{1,\alpha_{t},c}^{f}(y)}\right)^{2}\frac{d\mu_{1,\alpha_{t},c}^{f}(y)}{dt}

\displaystyle\qquad+\sum_{y\in\mathcal{X}}2\left(g_{t}(x,y)-\frac{\mu(y)}{\mu_{1,\alpha_{t},c}^{f}(y)}\right)\frac{\mu(y)}{\mu_{1,\alpha_{t},c}^{f}(y)}\cdot\frac{d\mu_{1,\alpha_{t},c}^{f}(y)}{dt}+\sum_{y\in\mathcal{X}}\left(g_{t}(x,y)-\frac{\mu(y)}{\mu_{1,\alpha_{t},c}^{f}(y)}\right)^{2}\frac{d\mu_{1,\alpha_{t},c}^{f}(y)}{dt}

(5.7)

\displaystyle=-2\langle-M_{1,\alpha_{t},c}^{f}g_{t},g_{t}\rangle_{\mu_{1,\alpha_{t},c}^{f}}-\sum_{y\in\mathcal{X}}\left(g_{t}(x,y)-\frac{\mu(y)}{\mu_{1,\alpha_{t},c}^{f}(y)}\right)^{2}\frac{d\mu_{1,\alpha_{t},c}^{f}(y)}{dt},

where the third equality follows from

In the special case where

with respect to

\displaystyle\frac{d}{d\beta}\mu_{1,\alpha,c}^{f}(y)=\mu_{1,\alpha,c}^{f}(y)\left(-\frac{d\mathcal{H}_{1,\alpha,c}^{f}(y)}{d\beta}\right)-\mu_{1,\alpha,c}^{f}(y)\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\mu_{1,\alpha,c}^{f}(x)\left(-\frac{d\mathcal{H}_{1,\alpha,c}^{f}(x)}{d\beta}\right)

with

\dfrac{\mathcal{H}(y)-c}{2(\mathcal{H}(y)-c)^{2}+2\beta}-\dfrac{\text{arctan}\left(\sqrt{\frac{1}{\beta}}\left(\mathcal{H}(y)-c\right)\right)}{2\sqrt{\beta}},&H(y)>c.\end{array}\right.

6.1.

n

In this Section, we specialize into the case of the Ising model on a finite connected graph
G=(V,E)
. Let
\mathcal{X}=\{-1,1\}^{V}
be the set of possible configurations
\sigma=\{\sigma(v);~v\in V\}

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}(\sigma)=-\dfrac{J}{2}\sum_{(v,w)\in E}\sigma(v)\sigma(w)-\dfrac{h}{2}\sum_{v\in V}\sigma(v),

For the Glauber dynamics, we consider a simple random walk proposal with transition matrix

\displaystyle P^{SRW}(\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2})

\displaystyle:=\dfrac{1}{n}\mathds{1}_{\{\textrm{there exists }i\textrm{ such that }\sigma_{1}(i)=-\sigma_{2}(i)\textrm{ and }\sigma_{1}(j)=\sigma_{2}(j)\textrm{ for all }j\neq i\}}.

. In this Section, we shall specialize into the two-dimensional torus
G=\left(\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}\right)^{2}
. Each configuration
\sigma\in\mathcal{X}=S^{V}
assigns a spin value
\sigma(v)\in S
to each vertex
v\in V
, and the Hamiltonian is given by

(6.2)

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}(\sigma):=-J\sum_{(v,w)\in E}\mathds{1}_{\{\sigma(v)=\sigma(w)\}},\quad\sigma\in\mathcal{X},

where
J=\mathcal{O}(1)>0
is the coupling constant. In subsequent analysis, we shall be interested in monochromatic configurations. Denote
\mathbf{m}
to be the spin configuration in which
\sigma(v)=m
for all
v\in V
, where
m\in S
. Unlike the previous two examples of ferromagnetic Ising model where
\mathbf{+1}
is the only ground-state configuration, we note that the
q
monochromatic configurations are the ground-state configurations in the
q
-state Potts model.

For the Glauber dynamics, we consider a simple random walk proposal with transition matrix
P^{Potts}=(P^{Potts}(\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2}))_{\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2}\in\mathcal{X}}
given by

\displaystyle P^{Potts}(\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2})

\displaystyle:=\dfrac{1}{q|V|}\mathds{1}_{\{|v\in V;~\sigma_{1}(v)\neq\sigma_{2}(v)|=1\}}.

The stationary distribution of
P^{Potts}
is the uniform distribution on
\mathcal{X}
. We now state the main result of this Section:

Theorem 6.3
.

Suppose
G=\left(\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}\right)^{2}=(V,E)
, consider the Glauber dynamics on
\mathcal{X}=\{1,2,\ldots,q\}^{V}
with proposal generator
P^{Potts}-I
, target distribution
\mu_{1,\alpha,c}^{f}
with Hamiltonian given in (
6.2
),
f(x)=x^{2}
and
c=\mathcal{H}(\mathbf{1})+\delta
. Given
\varepsilon>0
, we have

(1)

[Rapid mixing: mixing time is at most polynomial in
n
in the modified Glauber dynamics with time-dependent penalty] Assume the logarithmic annealing schedule
\alpha_{t}=\frac{3\pi}{2}\dfrac{1}{\ln(e^{\frac{3\pi}{2}}+pt)}
, where
p
is chosen as in Theorem
5.3
, then

t_{mix}^{f}(\varepsilon)=\mathcal{O}\left(n^{4}e^{3\delta}\left(\dfrac{1}{\varepsilon}\right)^{\frac{3\pi}{2n^{2}\ln q}}\right).

(2)

[Torpid mixing: mixing time is at least exponential in
n
in the original Glauber dynamics]
Assume in addition that
2\leqslant q\leqslant 4
. For constant
\mathbb{M}=\mathbb{M}(q)
that does not depend on
n
,

t_{mix}^{0}(\varepsilon)=\Omega\left(e^{\mathbb{M}n}\ln\left(\dfrac{1}{2\varepsilon}\right)\right).

Remark 6.3
.

For
m\in\{1,\ldots,q\}
, we can alternatively take
c=\mathcal{H}(\textbf{m})+\delta=\mathcal{H}(\textbf{1})+\delta
, where we recall that there are
q
ground-state configurations for the
q
-state Potts model.

6.3.1.
Proof of Theorem
6.3

We first state a spectral gap lower bound that generalizes Lemma
6.1
:

Lemma 6.3
.

Given
G=\left(\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}\right)^{2}=(V,E)
, consider the Glauber dynamics on
\mathcal{X}=\{1,2,\ldots,q\}^{V}
with proposal generator
P^{Potts}-I
and target distribution
\mu_{1,\alpha,c}^{f}
, then

\displaystyle\lambda_{2}(-M_{1,\alpha,c}^{f})\geqslant\dfrac{q}{(q-1)|V|}e^{-3\max_{\sigma}\mathcal{H}_{1,\alpha,c}^{f}(\sigma)}.

In particular, if we take
f(x)=x^{2}
and
c=\mathcal{H}(\mathbf{1})+\delta
, where
\delta>0
, we have

\displaystyle\lambda_{2}(-M_{1,\alpha,c}^{f})\geqslant\dfrac{q}{(q-1)|V|}e^{-3\delta-\frac{3}{\sqrt{\alpha}}\frac{\pi}{2}}.

[
Proof of Lemma
6.3
.
]
To prove the desired result, we again invoke Lemma
6.2
: the desired result follows if we take
L_{1}=M_{1,\alpha,c}^{f}
,
L_{2}=P^{Potts}-I
and
A=\exp\bigg\{-\max_{\sigma}\mathcal{H}_{1,\alpha,c}^{f}(\sigma)\bigg\}
therein,
and recall that in
(

, Corollary
12.13
)
and
(
Aldous and Fill 2002
, Chapter
5
Example
9
)
the spectral gap of

can be computed to be
\lambda_{2}(-(P^{Potts}-I))=\frac{q}{(q-1)|V|}

In the special case when
f(x)=x^{2}
and
c=\mathcal{H}(\mathbf{1})+\delta

2.3
and
\sup_{x}\arctan(x)=\pi/2
.

We proceed to give the proof of Theorem
6.3
.

[
Proof of Theorem
6.3
.
]
First, we prove item (

), which simply follows from Theorem
5.3

\mathcal{H}_{\max}-\mathcal{H}_{\min}=\mathcal{O}(n^{2}),\quad K=\dfrac{q}{(q-1)4n^{2}}e^{-3\delta}.
