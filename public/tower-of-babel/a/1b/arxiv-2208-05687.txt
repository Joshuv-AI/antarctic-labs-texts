Abstract
. This paper is to look for bi-Frobenius algebra structures on quantum complete intersections over field k k . We find a class of comultiplications, such that if − 1 ∈ k \sqrt{-1}\in k , then a quantum complete intersection becomes a bi-Frobenius algebra with comultiplication of this form if and only if all the parameters q i ​ j = ± 1 q_{ij}=\pm 1 . Also, it is proved that if − 1 ∈ k \sqrt{-1}\in k then a quantum exterior algebra in two variables admits a bi-Frobenius algebra structure if and only if the parameter q = ± 1 q=\pm 1 . While if − 1 ∉ k \sqrt{-1}\notin k , then the exterior algebra with two variables admits no bi-Frobenius algebra structures. We prove that the quantum complete intersections admit a bialgebra structure if and only if it admits a Hopf algebra structure, if and only if it is commutative, the characteristic of k k is a prime p p , and every a i a_{i} a power of p p . This also provides a large class of examples of bi-Frobenius algebras which are not bialgebras (and hence not Hopf algebras). In commutative case, other two comultiplications on complete intersection rings are given, such that they admit non-isomorphic bi-Frobenius algebra structures. Keywords: Bi-Frobenius algebras, coalgebras, bialgebras, Hopf algebras, quantum complete intersections, quantum exterior algebras

Introduction

Frobenius algebras are of particular interest and have wide applications (see e.g.
[
31
,
17
]
),
their module categories are Frobenius categories, and the corresponding stable categories
are triangulated (
[
16
]
).
A bi-Frobenius algebra
A
, introduced by Y. Doi and M. Takeuchi
[
14
]
, is both a Frobenius algebra and a Frobenius coalgebra,
together with a linear map
S:A\longrightarrow A
, called the antipode, which is an algebra anti-homomorphism
and also a coalgebra anti-homomorphism.

Finite-dimensional Hopf algebras are bi-Frobenius algebras, but the converse is not true.
A bi-Frobenius algebra is a Hopf algebra if and only if it is a bialgebra (
[
15
]
). The theory of bi-Frobenius algebras is developed in
[
11
,
12
,
15
]
.
Some results in Hopf algebras have been generalized to bi-Frobenius algebras, e.g.
Radford’s
S^{4}
-formula (
[
24
]
), see
[
14
]
.

One of problems in this theory is to find
bi-Frobenius algebras which are not Hopf algebras. In
[
14
]
a coalgebra structure on
k[X]/\langle X^{n}\rangle
is constructed such that it is a bi-Frobenius algebra but not a Hopf algebra.
Group-like algebras are introduced in
[
12
]
such that they become cocommutative bi-Frobenius algebras.
This class of bi-Frobenius algebras are further studied in
[
15
]
,
[
13
]
, and
[
29
]
.
In particular, using group-like algebras, a class of bi-Frobenius algebras which are not Hopf algebras
are found in
[
15
]
, where the antipode
S
is still the inverse of
{\rm Id}
in the convolution algebra; also
the complexified stable Green algebra is a group-like algebra (
[
29
]
).
Using quiver approach,
some bi-Frobenius algebras which are not Hopf algebras are constructed in
[
30
]
and
[
28
]
.

Quantum complete intersections originate from Yu. I. Manin’s quantum planes (
[
20
]
),
reach their present form by L. L. Avramov, V. N. Gasharov and I. V. Peeva (
[
3
]
),
and are closely related to braided Hopf algebras via quantum linear spaces by N. Andruskiewitsch and H.-J. Schneider (
[
1
,
2
]
).
They reveal many exotic homological and representation properties of algebras
(see e.g.,
[
18
,
25
,
9
,
5
,
7
,
8
,
6
,
21
,
32
,
26
]
).

Quantum complete intersections are Frobenius algebras. Thus, it is natural
to look for bi-Frobenius algebra structures on them. In this paper, we construct a class of comultiplications on an arbitrary quantum complete intersection
A({\bf q},a_{1},\cdots,a_{n})
over field
k
; and prove that
if
\sqrt{-1}\in k
, then it becomes a bi-Frobenius algebra, with any comultiplication of this form, if and only if all the parameters
q_{ij}=\pm 1
. See Theorem
3.2
.
Also, it is proved that if
\sqrt{-1}\in k
, then
the quantum exterior algebra in two variables admits a bi-Frobenius algebra structure (no restrictions on the form of comultiplication) if and only if
q=\pm 1
(see Theorem
5.1
).
While if
\sqrt{-1}\notin k
, then the exterior algebra with two variables admits no bi-Frobenius algebra structures (see Proposition

We stress that the comultiplication we used in Theorem
3.2
is different from
the one in Example 1.9 of
[
2

Using Kummer’s theorem in number theory, we prove that the quantum complete intersections over filed

is
a prime

In commutative case, we find other two comultiplications on complete intersection rings, such that they admit non-isomorphic bi-Frobenius algebra structures. See Theorems

2.
Preliminary

2.1.
Frobenius algebras and Frobenius coalgebras.

Let
A
be a finite-dimensional algebra over field
k
. Then the
k
-dual
A^{*}=\mathrm{Hom}_{k}(A,\ k)
has a canonical
A
-bimodule structure. Denote by

a\rightharpoonup f
the left action of
a\in A
on
f\in A^{*}
, i.e.,
(a\rightharpoonup f)(x)=f(xa),\ \forall\ x\in A
. Similarly, denote by
f\leftharpoonup a
the right action of
a
on
f
, i.e.,
(f\leftharpoonup a)(x)=f(ax)
.

Recall that
A
is
a Frobenius algebra
provided that there is an isomorphism
A\cong A^{*}
of left
A
-modules, or equivalently, there is an isomorphism
A\cong A^{*}
of right
A
-modules; and that

A
is
a symmetric algebra
, provided that there is an isomorphism
A\cong A^{*}
of
A
-bimodules.

By an elementary fact in linear algebra, a Frobenius algebra can also be characterized as a pair
(A,\ \phi)
, where
\phi\in A^{*}
, such that
A^{*}=A\rightharpoonup\phi
(or equivalently,
A^{*}=\phi\leftharpoonup A
).
Such a
\phi
is called
a Frobenius homomorphism
of
A
.

Let
(C,\ \Delta,\ \varepsilon)
be a finite-dimensional coalgebra over
k
. All the tensor products are over
k
. We use the Heyneman-Sweedler notation
\Delta(c)=\sum c_{1}\otimes c_{2}
.
Then
C^{*}:=\mathrm{Hom}_{k}(C,\ k)
is an algebra and
C
has a canonical
C^{*}
-bimodule structure.
Denote by
f\rightharpoonup c
the left action of
f\in C^{*}
on
c\in C
, i.e.,
f\rightharpoonup c=\sum c_{1}f(c_{2})
. Similarly,
c\leftharpoonup f=\sum f(c_{1})c_{2}
.

A finite-dimensional coalgebra
C
is a Frobenius coalgebra if there is an element
t\in C
such that
C=t\leftharpoonup C^{*}
,
or equivalently,
C=C^{*}\rightharpoonup t
. Usually we denote a Frobenius coalgebra by a pair
(C,\ t)
.

It is clear that
(A,\ \phi)
is a Frobenius algebra if and only if
(A^{*},\ \phi)
is a Frobenius coalgebra.

For a coalgebra structure with comultiplication
\Delta
on an algebra
A
, an element
x\in A
is
primitive
, if
\Delta(x)=1\otimes x+x\otimes 1
, where
1
is the identity.
All the primitive elements form a linear space, denoted by
P(A)
.

2.2.
Bi-Frobenius algebras.

Definition 2.1
.

(
[
14
]
)
Let
A
be a finite-dimensional algebra and coalgebra over a field
k
with
t\in A
and
\phi\in A^{*}
. Let
S:A\longrightarrow A
be the
k
-linear map
defined by
S(a)=\sum\phi(t_{1}a)t_{2}.
The quadruple
(A,\phi,t,S)
, or simply,
A
is a bi-Frobenius algebra, if the following conditions are satisfied
:

(i)
The counit
\varepsilon:A\longrightarrow k
is an algebra homomorphism, and the identity
1_{A}
is a group-like element
;

(ii)

(A,\ \phi)
is a Frobenius algebra, and
(A,\ t)
is a Frobenius coalgebra
;

(iii)

S
is an algebra anti-homomorphism, and
S
is a coalgebra anti-homomorphism
(
i.e.,
\varepsilon\circ S=\varepsilon
, and
\Delta(S(a))=\sum S(a_{2})\otimes S(a_{1}),\ \forall\ a\in A)
.

In this case,
S
is called the antipode of bi-Frobenius algebra
(A,\phi,t,S)
.

Let
(A,\ \phi,\ t,\ S)
and
(A^{\prime},\ \phi^{\prime},\ t^{\prime},\ S^{\prime})
be bi-Frobenius algebras. A linear map
F:A\longrightarrow A^{\prime}
is
a homomorphism of bi-Frobenius algebra
(
[
11
]
), if
F
is an algebra homomorphism and a coalgebra homomorphism, such that
F\circ S=S^{\prime}\circ F
.

An algebra
A
is
augmented
, if there is an algebra homomorphism
\varepsilon:A\longrightarrow k
.
For an augmented algebra
(A,\varepsilon)
, an element
t\in A
is
a right
(respectively,
left
)
integral
of
A
if
tx=t\varepsilon(x)
(respectively,
xt=\varepsilon(x)t
), for all
x\in A
.
If the left integral space coincides with the right integral space, then
A
is called
unimodular
.

Dually, a coalgebra
C
is
coaugmented
, if it has a group-like element
h\in C
. For a coaugmented coalgebra
(C,\ h)
,
an element
\phi\in C^{*}
is
a right
(respectively,
left
)
cointegral
of

C
if
x\leftharpoonup\phi=\phi(x)h
(respectively,
\phi\rightharpoonup x=h\phi(x)
), for all
x\in C
.
If the left cointegral space coincides with the right cointegral space, then
C
is called
counimodular
.

Lemma 2.2
.

(
[
14
,
15
]
)

(1)
If
A
is an augmented Frobenius algebra, then the space of right integrals is one-dimensional.

(2)
If
C
is a Frobenius coalgebra with a group-like element, then the space of right cointegrals is one-dimensional.

(3)
If
(A,\ \phi,\ t,\ S)
is a bi-Frobenius algebra, then
t
is a right integral and
\phi
is a right cointegral, and thus
the space of right integral is
kt
, the space of right cointegral is
k\phi
.

(4)
If
(A,\ \phi,\ t,S)
is a bi-Frobenius algebra, then
(A,\ c\phi,\ \frac{1}{c}t,S)
is also a bi-Frobenius algebra for any nonzero element
c\in k
.

We need the following observation in
[
11
]
.

Lemma 2.3
.

(
[
11
]
)
Let A be a finite-dimensional algebra and coalgebra satisfying the condition
(i)
in
Definition 2.1
.
If there are
\phi\in A^{*}
and
t\in A
such that the
k
-linear map

S:A\longrightarrow A,\ \ a\mapsto\sum\phi(t_{1}a)t_{2}

is an algebra anti-isomorphism and a coalgebra anti-isomorphism, then

2.3.
Quantum complete intersections.

Let
k
be a field,
a_{1},\cdots,a_{n}
integers with all
a_{i}\geq 2
and
n\geq 2
, and
{\bf q}=(q_{ij})
an
n\times n
matrix
over
k
with
q_{ii}=-1
and
q_{ij}q_{ji}=1
for
1\leq i,j\leq n
.
A quantum complete intersection

A({\bf q},a_{1},\cdots,a_{n})
is the following
k
-algebra

k\langle x_{1},\cdots,x_{n}\rangle/\langle x_{i}^{a_{i}},\ x_{i}x_{j}+q_{ij}x_{j}x_{i},\ 1\leq i,\ j\leq n\rangle.

When all
a_{i}=2
, it is called
a quantum exterior algebra
.
It is well-known that a quantum complete intersection is a local Frobenius algebra and it is symmetric if and only if
\prod\limits_{1\leq i\leq n}(-q_{ij})^{a_{i}-1}=1
for all
j
(see P. A. Bergh
[
4
, Lemma 3.1]
).

Denote by
\mathbb{N}_{0}
the set of non-negative integers. Put

V=\{\mathbf{v}=(v_{1},\cdots,v_{n})\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{n}\ |\ v_{i}\leq a_{i}-1,\ 1\leq i\leq n\}.

For
\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{n}
, write
x_{\mathbf{v}}=x_{1}^{v_{1}}\cdots x_{n}^{v_{n}}
. Then
\mathcal{B}=\{x_{\mathbf{v}}\ |\ \mathbf{v}\in V\}
is a basis of
A({\bf q},a_{1},\cdots,a_{n})
. The dual basis of
A^{*}
is
\{x_{\bf v}^{*}\ |\ {\bf v}\in V\}
, where

x_{\bf v}^{*}(x_{\bf u})=\delta_{{\bf v},{\bf u}},\ \forall\ {\bf v},{\bf u}\in V
.

The degree of
x_{\mathbf{v}}\in\mathcal{B}
is defined as
|x_{\mathbf{v}}|=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}v_{i}
,
and the degree of
x_{\mathbf{u}}\otimes x_{\mathbf{v}}\in\mathcal{B}\otimes\mathcal{B}
is defined as
|x_{\mathbf{u}}|+|x_{\mathbf{v}}|
.

Put
{\bf a}=(a_{1},\cdots,a_{n}),\ \ {\bf 0}=(0,\cdots,0),\ \ {\bf 1}=(1,\cdots,1)
. For
1\leq i\leq n
, let
{\bf e_{i}}
be the vector in
\mathbb{N}_{0}^{n}
with the
i
-th component
1
and other components
0
.
Endow a partial order on
\mathbb{N}_{0}^{n}
by
\mathbf{u}\leq\mathbf{v}
if and only if
u_{i}\leq v_{i}
for all
1\leq i\leq n
. Then
\mathbf{v}\leq\mathbf{a-1}
for all
\mathbf{v}\in V.

For
\mathbf{u,v}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{n}
, put
{\bf q}^{\langle\mathbf{u}|\mathbf{v}\rangle}=\prod\limits_{1\leq i<j\leq n}(-\frac{1}{q_{ij}})^{u_{j}v_{i}}
. Then

x_{\mathbf{u}}x_{\mathbf{v}}={\bf q}^{\langle\mathbf{u}|\mathbf{v}\rangle}x_{\mathbf{u+v}}.

See S. Oppermann
[
23
, Section 2]
.

To look for bi-Frobenius algebra structures on
A=A({\bf q},\ a_{1},\cdots,a_{n})
, it is helpful to know all the
Frobenius homomorphisms of
A
. By the proof of Lemma 3.1 in
[
4
]
, it is known that
x_{\mathbf{a-1}}^{*}
is a Frobenius homomorphism. Thus, if
\phi\in A^{*}
is a Frobenius homomorphism, then
\phi=x_{\mathbf{a-1}}^{*}\leftharpoonup r
, where
r
is an invertible element of
A
. See M. Lorenz
[
19
, 1.2.5]
. Since
A
is a finite-dimensional local algebra,
r=c^{\prime}_{\mathbf{a-1}}1+\sum\limits_{\mathbf{v}\neq\mathbf{0}}c^{\prime}_{\mathbf{a-1-v}}x_{\mathbf{v}}\in A
with each
c^{\prime}_{\mathbf{a-1-v}}\in k
is invertible if and only if
c^{\prime}_{\mathbf{a-1}}\neq 0
. It follows that a Frobenius homomorphism
\phi
is of the form

\phi=x_{\mathbf{a-1}}^{*}\leftharpoonup r=x_{\mathbf{a-1}}^{*}\leftharpoonup(c^{\prime}_{\mathbf{a-1}}1+\sum\limits_{\mathbf{v}\neq\mathbf{0}}c^{\prime}_{\mathbf{a-1-v}}x_{\mathbf{v}})=\sum\limits_{\mathbf{v}\in V}c_{\mathbf{v}}\ x_{\mathbf{v}}^{*}

with
c_{\mathbf{a-1}}=c^{\prime}_{\mathbf{a-1}}\neq 0
. This proves (1) below.

Lemma 2.4
.

(1)
All the Frobenius homomorphisms of
A=A({\bf q},a_{1},\cdots,a_{n})
are

\phi=\sum_{\mathbf{v}\in V}c_{\mathbf{v}}x_{\mathbf{v}}^{*}\ \ \mbox{where}\ c_{\mathbf{v}}\in k\ \mbox{and}\ c_{\mathbf{a-1}}\neq 0.

(2)
Put
t=x_{\mathbf{a-1}}
and
\phi=(x_{{\bf a-1}})^{*}
.
Then
(A,\ \phi)
is a unimodular, augmented Frobenius algebra, with the space of integrals
kt.

Proof.

(2) By (1),
\phi
is a Frobenius homomorphism.
Let
\varepsilon:A\longrightarrow k
be the algebra homomorphism given by
\varepsilon(x_{\mathbf{v}})=\delta_{\mathbf{v,0}}
. Then
t\ x_{\bf v}=x_{\bf a-1}\ x_{\bf v}=\delta_{\bf v,0}\ t=t\ \varepsilon(x_{\bf v})
for

.
Thus

is a right integral of augmented Frobenius algebra
(A,\ \phi)
.
Similarly,
t
is a left integral.
By Lemma
2.2
(1), the space of right (respectively, left) integrals is one-dimensional, thus

is unimodular, and the space of integrals
kt.

∎

3.
Bi-Frobenius algebra structures on quantum complete intersections

We will give a class of comultiplications on an arbitrary quantum complete intersection

A=A({\bf q},a_{1},\cdots,a_{n})=k\langle x_{1},\cdots,x_{n}\rangle/\langle x_{i}^{a_{i}},\ x_{i}x_{j}+q_{ij}x_{j}x_{i},\ 1\leq i,\ j\leq n\rangle

and then prove that if
\sqrt{-1}\in k
, then
A
becomes a bi-Frobenius algebra,
with comultiplication of the given form, if and only if
q_{ij}=\pm 1

1\leq i,j\leq n.

3.1.

Put
g_{\mathbf{0,a-1}}=1=g_{\mathbf{a-1,0}},

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\varepsilon(x_{\mathbf{v}})=\delta_{\mathbf{v,0}};\\

By

3.3.

With
\Delta

Lemma 3.6
.

Let
\Delta
and
\varepsilon
be given by
(3.1)
, and
S:A\longrightarrow A
the
k
-linear map given by
(3.2)
.
Then
S
is an algebra anti-homomorphism of
A
if and only if the following conditions are satisfied
:

[formula]

q_{i,j}^{2}=1,\ \ \forall\ 1\leq i,j\leq n

[formula]

g_{\mathbf{a-1-(u+v),u+v}}=g_{\mathbf{a-1-u,u}}\ g_{\mathbf{a-1-v,v}},\ \ \forall\ {\bf u},\ {\bf v}\in V,\ \ {\bf u}+{\bf v}\leq{\bf a-1}.

Proof.

Since the actions of
S
on elements in the basis
\mathcal{B}=\{x_{\bf v}\ |\ {\bf v}\in V\}
are just scalar multiplications,
to show
S
is an anti-homomorphism of algebra
A
, it is enough to show
S(x_{\bf u}x_{\bf v})=S(x_{\bf v})S(x_{\bf u})
for
{\bf u},{\bf v}\in V,\ {\bf u}+{\bf v}\leq{\bf a-1}
.
By (3.2) one has

\displaystyle S(x_{\mathbf{u}}x_{\mathbf{v}})

\displaystyle=S({\bf q}^{\langle\mathbf{u|v}\rangle}x_{\bf u+v})={\bf q}^{\langle\mathbf{u|v}\rangle}S(x_{\bf u+v})

\displaystyle={\bf q}^{\langle\mathbf{u|v}\rangle}{\bf q}^{\langle\mathbf{a-1-(u+v)|u+v}\rangle}g_{\mathbf{a-1-(u+v),u+v}}\ x_{\mathbf{u+v}}

\displaystyle S(x_{\mathbf{u}})

\displaystyle={\bf q}^{\langle\mathbf{a-1-u|u}\rangle}g_{\mathbf{a-1-u,u}}\ x_{\mathbf{u}}

\displaystyle S(x_{\mathbf{v}})

\displaystyle={\bf q}^{\langle\mathbf{a-1-v|v}\rangle}g_{\mathbf{a-1-v,v}}\ x_{\mathbf{v}}

\displaystyle S(x_{\mathbf{v}})S(x_{\mathbf{u}})

\displaystyle={\bf q}^{\langle\mathbf{a-1-u|u}\rangle}{\bf q}^{\langle\mathbf{a-1-v|v}\rangle}\ g_{\mathbf{a-1-u,u}}\ g_{\mathbf{a-1-v,v}}\ x_{\mathbf{v}}x_{\mathbf{u}}

\displaystyle={\bf q}^{\langle\mathbf{v|u}\rangle}{\bf q}^{\langle\mathbf{a-1-u|u}\rangle}{\bf q}^{\langle\mathbf{a-1-v|v}\rangle}\ g_{\mathbf{a-1-u,u}}\ g_{\mathbf{a-1-v,v}}\ x_{\mathbf{u+v}}.

Since

\displaystyle{\bf q}^{\langle\mathbf{u|v}\rangle}{\bf q}^{\langle\mathbf{a-1-(u+v)|u+v}\rangle}

\displaystyle=\prod_{1\leq i\leq j\leq n}(-\frac{1}{q_{ij}})^{u_{j}v_{i}+(a_{j}-1-u_{j}-v_{j})(u_{i}+v_{i})}

\displaystyle=\prod_{1\leq i\leq j\leq n}(-\frac{1}{q_{ij}})^{(a_{j}-1-u_{j})u_{i}+(a_{j}-1-v_{j})v_{i}-u_{i}v_{j}}

\displaystyle={\bf q}^{\langle\mathbf{a-1-u|u}\rangle}{\bf q}^{\langle\mathbf{a-1-v|v}\rangle}{\bf q}^{-\langle\mathbf{v|u}\rangle}

where
{\bf q}^{-\langle\mathbf{v|u}\rangle}=\frac{1}{{\bf q}^{\langle\mathbf{v|u}\rangle}}
, it follows that
S(x_{\bf u}x_{\bf v})=S(x_{\bf v})S(x_{\bf u})
for
\forall\ {\bf u},\ {\bf v}\in V,\ \ {\bf u}+{\bf v}\leq{\bf a-1}
if and only if

[formula]

g_{\mathbf{a-1-(u+v),u+v}}=({\bf q}^{\langle\mathbf{v|u}\rangle})^{2}\ g_{\mathbf{a-1-u,u}}\ g_{\mathbf{a-1-v,v}},\ \ \forall\ {\bf u},\ {\bf v}\in V,\ \ {\bf u}+{\bf v}\leq{\bf a-1}.

Assume that (3.5) holds. Exchanging the position of
{\bf u}
and
{\bf v}
, one gets

({\bf q}^{\langle\mathbf{u|v}\rangle})^{2}=({\bf q}^{\langle\mathbf{v|u}\rangle})^{2},\ \ \ \ \mbox{i.e.,}\ \ \ \ \prod_{1\leq i\leq j\leq n}q_{ij}^{-2u_{j}v_{i}}=\prod_{1\leq i\leq j\leq n}q_{ij}^{-2v_{j}u_{i}}.

Taking
\mathbf{v=e_{i},\ u=e_{j}}
with
1\leq i<j\leq n
, one gets
q_{i,j}^{2}=1
for all
1\leq i,j\leq n
. Then

({\bf q}^{\langle\mathbf{u|v}\rangle})^{2}=\prod\limits_{1\leq i\leq j\leq n}q_{ij}^{-2u_{j}v_{i}}=1
,
and by (3.6) one gets

g_{\mathbf{a-1-(u+v),u+v}}=g_{\mathbf{a-1-u,u}}\ g_{\mathbf{a-1-v,v}},\ \ \forall\ {\bf u},\ {\bf v}\in V,\ \ {\bf u}+{\bf v}\leq{\bf a-1}.

That is, the conditions (3.3) and (3.4) are satisfied.

Conversely, assume that the conditions (3.3) and (3.4) are satisfied.
Since
q_{i,j}^{2}=1
for all
1\leq i,j\leq n
,

({\bf q}^{\langle\mathbf{v|u}\rangle})^{2}=\prod\limits_{1\leq i\leq j\leq n}q_{ij}^{-2u_{i}v_{j}}=1
, and then
(3.5) holds, i.e.,
S
is an anti-homomorphism of algebra.

In conclusion,
S
is an anti-homomorphism of algebra if and only if the conditions (3.3) and (3.4) are satisfied. ∎

4.
When a quantum complete intersection admits a Hopf algebra structure?

The following main result of this section shows that only in very special
cases quantum complete intersections can admit Hopf algebra structures.

Theorem 4.1
.

For the quantum complete intersection
A=A({\bf q},a_{1},\cdots,a_{n})
over field
k
, the following are equivalent.

(1)

A
admits a Hopf algebra structure
;

(2)

A
admits a bialgebra structure
;

(3)

A
is commutative, and each
a_{i}
is a positive power of
p
, where
p={\rm char}k
is a prime.

4.1.
Kummer’s theorem

To prove Theorem
4.1
, we need a consequence of Kummer’s theorem.

Let
p
be a prime. Denote by
\nu_{p}(x)
the
p
-adic valuation of a nonzero integer
x
, i.e.,
the largest non-negative integer
s
such that
p^{s}
divides
x
.
For
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, write
n
as the expansion in base
p
:

n=n_{0}+n_{1}p+\cdots+n_{r}p^{r}

where
0\leq n_{0},\cdots,n_{r}\leq p-1.

For
n,m\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
with
n\geq m
, write
n,\ m
and
t=n-m
as the expansion in base
p
, with the digits
n_{j}
,
m_{j}
and
t_{j}
as above, respectively.
Let
\epsilon_{j}=1
if there is a carry in the
j
-th digit when adding
m
and
t
in base
p
, and let
\epsilon_{j}=0
otherwise.
Then Kummer’s theorem claims that the
p
-adic valuation of binomial coefficient
\binom{n}{m}
is

\nu_{p}(\binom{n}{m})=\sum_{j\geq 0}\epsilon_{j}.

See e.g.
[
22
, Theorem 2.6.7]
.

Lemma 4.2
.

(E. Kummer)
Let
n
be a positive integer and
p
a prime.
Assume that
p\mid n
with
\nu_{p}(n)=r
. Then
p\nmid\binom{n}{p^{r}}.

Proof.

For convenience write
m=p^{r}
. Then the expansion of
m
in base
p
is just

m=p^{r}
, i.e.,

m_{j}=\delta_{j,r}=\begin{cases}0,&j\neq r;\\
1,&j=r.\end{cases}

To know
\nu_{p}(\binom{n}{p^{r}})
, let
t=n-m=p^{r}(\frac{n}{m}-1)
. Then the expansion of
t
in base
p
looks like

t=t_{r}p^{r}+t_{r+1}p^{r+1}+\cdots+n_{r+s}p^{r+s}.

It is clear that
t_{r}\neq p-1
. Otherwise,
n=m+t
is divided by
p^{r+1}
, contradicts
\nu_{p}(n)=r
.

Thus, when adding
m
and
t
in base
p
, there is no carry in
r
-th digit, and hence there are no carries in any digit.
By Kummer’s theorem,
\nu_{p}(\binom{n}{p^{r}})=0,
i.e.,
p\nmid\binom{n}{p^{r}}.
∎

4.2.
A main lemma

Lemma 4.3
.

If there is an
a_{i}
such that it is not a power of
char

k
, then the quantum complete intersection
A({\bf q},a_{1},\cdots,a_{n})
admits no bialgebra structures.

In particular, if
{\rm char}\ k=0
, then any quantum complete intersection
admits no bialgebra structures.

Proof.

Without loss of generality, one may assume that
a_{1}
is not a power of char
k
. Assume otherwise that
A=A({\bf q},a_{1},\cdots,a_{n})
has a
bialgebra structure, say, with comultiplication
\Delta
and counit
\varepsilon
. Thus

\Delta
is an algebra homomorphism.
Write

\Delta(x_{1})=\sum_{\mathbf{u,v}\in V}{a_{\mathbf{u,v}}}x_{\mathbf{u}}\otimes x_{\mathbf{v}}\ \ \mbox{with}\ \ a_{\mathbf{u,v}}\in k.

Since
A
is a finite-dimensional local algebra, the kernel of the algebra homomorphism
\varepsilon:A\longrightarrow k
is the unique maximal ideal of
A
. Thus
\varepsilon(x_{\mathbf{u}})=\delta_{\mathbf{u},\mathbf{0}}
, where
\delta
is the Kronecker symbol. By
(\varepsilon\otimes{\rm Id})\Delta\cong{\rm Id}\cong({\rm Id}\otimes\varepsilon)\Delta
, one has

\sum_{\mathbf{v}\in V}a_{\mathbf{0,v}}x_{\mathbf{v}}=x_{1}=\sum_{\mathbf{u}\in V}a_{\mathbf{u,0}}x_{\mathbf{u}}.

Thus
a_{\mathbf{0,v}}=\delta_{\mathbf{v,e_{1}}}
and
a_{\mathbf{u,0}}=\delta_{\mathbf{u,e_{1}}}
, and hence

\Delta(x_{1})=1\otimes x_{1}+x_{1}\otimes 1+\sum_{\mathbf{u,v}\in V/\{\mathbf{0}\}}{a_{\mathbf{u,v}}}x_{\mathbf{u}}\otimes x_{\mathbf{v}}.

Write

\displaystyle\Delta(x_{1})^{a_{1}}

\displaystyle=(1\otimes x_{1}+x_{1}\otimes 1+\sum\limits_{\mathbf{u,v}\in V/\{\mathbf{0}\}}{a_{\mathbf{u,v}}}x_{\mathbf{u}}\otimes x_{\mathbf{v}})^{a_{1}}

\displaystyle=(1\otimes x_{1}+x_{1}\otimes 1)^{a_{1}}+\Sigma_{1}

\displaystyle=\sum_{1\leq i\leq a_{1}-1}\binom{a_{1}}{i}x_{1}^{i}\otimes x_{1}^{a_{1}-i}+\Sigma_{1}

and write
\Sigma_{1}
as a
k
-combination of elements in
\mathcal{B}\otimes\mathcal{B}
.
Notice that the degree of each term in
x_{1}^{i}\otimes x_{1}^{a_{1}-i}\ (1\leq i\leq a_{1}-1)
is
a_{1}
;
and the degree of each term in
\Sigma_{1}
is bigger than or equal to

a_{1}-i+2i=a_{1}+i

with
1\leq i\leq a_{1}
. Thus the degree of each term in
\Sigma_{1}
is bigger than
a_{1}
.
Since
\Delta
is an algebra homomorphism,
(\Delta x_{1})^{a_{1}}=\Delta(x_{1}^{a_{1}})=0
. It follows that

\binom{a_{1}}{i}=0,\ 1\leq i\leq a_{1}-1
. This is absurd if char
k=0
.

So, suppose that
{\rm char}\ k=p>0
and
a_{1}
is not a power of
p
. If
p\nmid a_{1}
, then
\binom{a_{1}}{1}=a_{1}\neq 0
in
k
, a contradiction.
If the largest positive integer
t
such that
p^{t}\mid a_{1}
is
s
, then by Lemma
4.2
,

p\nmid\binom{a_{1}}{p^{s}}
, and hence
\binom{a_{1}}{p^{s}}\neq 0
in
k
, again a contradiction. This completes the proof. ∎

4.3.
Proof of Theorem
4.1
.

The implication
(1)\Longrightarrow(2)
is clear.

(2)\Longrightarrow(3)
: Suppose that
A
admits a bialgebra structure for some comultiplication
\Delta
and counit
\varepsilon
. By Lemma
4.3
,
{\rm char}k=p
should be a prime, and each
a_{i}
is a positive power of
p
.

As in the proof of Lemma
4.3
, for any
1\leq i\leq n
one has

\Delta(x_{i})=1\otimes x_{i}+x_{i}\otimes 1+\sum_{\mathbf{u,v}\in V/\{\mathbf{0}\}}{a_{\mathbf{u,v}}}x_{\mathbf{u}}\otimes x_{\mathbf{v}}

where
a_{\bf u,v}\in k
. Then for any
1\leq i,j\leq n
, one has

\displaystyle\Delta(x_{i}x_{j})

\displaystyle=\Delta(x_{i})\Delta(x_{j})=1\otimes x_{i}x_{j}+x_{i}\otimes x_{j}+x_{j}\otimes x_{i}+x_{i}x_{j}\otimes 1+\Sigma

with some
\Sigma\in A\otimes A
. On the other hand, one has

\displaystyle\Delta(x_{i}x_{j})

\displaystyle=\Delta(-q_{ij}x_{j}x_{i})=-q_{ij}\Delta(x_{j})\Delta(x_{i})

\displaystyle=-q_{ij}(1\otimes x_{j}x_{i}+x_{i}\otimes x_{j}+x_{j}\otimes x_{i}+x_{j}x_{i}\otimes 1+\Sigma^{\prime})

\displaystyle=1\otimes x_{i}x_{j}-q_{ij}x_{i}\otimes x_{j}-q_{ij}x_{j}\otimes x_{i}+x_{i}x_{j}\otimes 1-q_{ij}\Sigma^{\prime}

with some
\Sigma^{\prime}\in A\otimes A
. Thus

\displaystyle 1\otimes x_{i}x_{j}+x_{i}\otimes x_{j}+x_{j}\otimes x_{i}+x_{i}x_{j}\otimes 1+\Sigma

\displaystyle=1\otimes x_{i}x_{j}-q_{ij}x_{i}\otimes x_{j}-q_{ij}x_{j}\otimes x_{i}+x_{i}x_{j}\otimes 1-q_{ij}\Sigma^{\prime}.

Since
\mathcal{B}\otimes\mathcal{B}
is a set of basis of
A\otimes A

and the degree of each term in
\Sigma
and
\Sigma^{\prime}
is bigger than or equal to
3
(see Subsection 2.3), one gets

q_{ij}=-1,\ \ \ \forall\ 1\leq i,j\leq n

i.e.,
A
is commutative.

(3)\Longrightarrow(1)
: Assume that
A
is commutative, and that
a_{i}=p^{r_{i}}
for some positive integer
r_{i}
for all
1\leq i\leq n
, where
p={\rm char}k
. Note that

\mathcal{B}=\{x_{\mathbf{v}}=x_{1}^{v_{1}}\cdots x_{n}^{v_{n}}\ |\ \mathbf{v}\in V\}
is a basis of
A
, where
V=\{\mathbf{v}=(v_{1},\cdots,v_{n})\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{n}\ |\ v_{i}\leq a_{i}-1,\ 1\leq i\leq n\}.
Define
\Delta:A\longrightarrow A\otimes A
,
\varepsilon:A\longrightarrow k
and
S:A\longrightarrow A
to be the
k
-linear maps as follows:

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\varepsilon(x_{\mathbf{v}})=\delta_{\mathbf{v,0}};\\
\Delta(x_{\mathbf{v}})=\prod\limits_{1\leq i\leq n}\sum\limits_{0\leq t\leq v_{i}}\binom{v_{i}}{t}x_{i}^{t}\otimes x_{i}^{v_{i}-t};\\
S(x_{\bf v})=(-1)^{|{\bf v}|}x_{\bf v},\\
\end{array}\right.

It is clear that
\varepsilon
is an algebra homomorphism. We claim that
\Delta
is an algebra homomorphism.

In fact, by definition
\Delta(x_{i})=1\otimes x_{i}+x_{i}\otimes 1
for
1\leq i\leq n
and
\Delta(x_{\bf v})=\prod\limits_{1\leq i\leq n}(\Delta(x_{i}))^{v_{i}}
for
{\bf v}\in V
. Thus for
{\bf u,v}\in V
, if
{\bf u+v}\in V
, then

\Delta(x_{\bf u+v})=\prod\limits_{1\leq i\leq n}\Delta(x_{i})^{u_{i}+v_{i}}=\prod\limits_{1\leq i\leq n}\Delta(x_{i})^{u_{i}}\Delta(x_{i})^{v_{i}}=\Delta(x_{\bf u})\Delta(x_{\bf v}).

If
{\bf u+v}\notin V
, then
x_{\bf u}x_{\bf v}=0
, i.e., there is an integer
l
with
1\leq l\leq n
such that
u_{l}+v_{l}\geq a_{l}
.
Since char
k=p
is a prime and
a_{l}=p^{r_{l}}
, it follows that

\Delta(x_{l})^{a_{l}}=(1\otimes x_{l}+x_{l}\otimes 1)^{p^{r_{l}}}=1\otimes x_{l}^{p^{r_{l}}}+x_{l}^{p^{r_{l}}}\otimes 1=1\otimes x_{l}^{a_{l}}+x_{l}^{a_{l}}\otimes 1=0

where we have used the rule
(a+b)^{p^{r}}=a^{p^{r}}+b^{p^{r}}.

Hence
\Delta(x_{l})^{u_{l}+v_{l}}=0
, since
u_{l}+v_{l}\geq a_{l}
.
Thus

\displaystyle\Delta(x_{\bf u}x_{\bf v})=0

\displaystyle=\Delta(x_{l})^{u_{l}+v_{l}}\prod\limits_{1\leq i\leq n,\ i\neq l}\Delta(x_{i})^{u_{i}+v_{i}}

\displaystyle=\prod\limits_{1\leq i\leq n}\Delta(x_{i})^{u_{i}}\Delta(x_{i})^{v_{i}}=\Delta(x_{\bf u})\Delta(x_{\bf v}).

This proves the claim.

Since
\Delta
is an algebra homomorphism, it follows that
\Delta\otimes{\rm Id}
and
{\rm Id}\otimes\Delta
are also algebra homomorphisms. One has

\displaystyle(\Delta\otimes{\rm Id})\Delta(x_{\bf v})

\displaystyle=\Delta\otimes{\rm Id}(\prod\limits_{1\leq i\leq n}\Delta(x_{i})^{v_{i}})

\displaystyle=\prod\limits_{1\leq i\leq n}((\Delta\otimes{\rm Id})\Delta(x_{i}))^{v_{i}}

\displaystyle=\prod\limits_{1\leq i\leq n}(1\otimes 1\otimes x_{i}+1\otimes x_{i}\otimes 1+x_{i}\otimes 1\otimes 1)^{v_{i}}

\displaystyle=\prod\limits_{1\leq i\leq n}(({\rm Id}\otimes\Delta)\Delta(x_{i}))^{v_{i}}

\displaystyle=({\rm Id}\otimes\Delta)\Delta(x_{\bf v}),\ \text{ for }{\bf v}\in V.

This shows the coassociativity of

. It is clear that the counitary property

is satisfied. Thus
(A,\Delta,\varepsilon)
is a coalgebra.

Finally, one can show that

is an antipode. For any
\mathbf{v}\in V
,

\displaystyle S*{\rm Id}(x_{\bf v})

\displaystyle=m\circ(S\otimes{\rm Id})\Delta(x_{\bf v})

\displaystyle=m\circ(S\otimes{\rm Id})\prod_{1\leq i\leq n}\sum_{0\leq t\leq v_{i}}\binom{v_{i}}{t}x_{i}^{t}\otimes x_{i}^{v_{i}-t}

\displaystyle=\prod_{1\leq i\leq n}(\sum_{0\leq t\leq v_{i}}(-1)^{t}\binom{v_{i}}{t})x_{i}^{v_{i}}

\displaystyle=\varepsilon(x_{\bf v})1={\rm Id}*S(x_{\bf v}).

Thus

is a Hopf algebra.
\square

Corollary 4.4
.

A quantum exterior algebra admits a bialgebra structure if and only if
{\rm char}\ k=2
. In this case, it is a Hopf algebra.

for

5.
Bi-Frobenius algebra structures on quantum exterior algebras in two variables

In the special case of quantum exterior algebras in two variables, Theorem

has a stronger form.
Namely, let

The main result of this section is as follows.

Theorem 5.1
.

If

admits a bi-Frobenius algebra structure, then

Conversely, if
\sqrt{-1}\in k
and

then

admits a bi-Frobenius algebra structure.

Moreover,

admits a Hopf algebra structure if and only if
{\rm char}\ k=2
.

5.1.
The condition on
q

Denote a basis of
A_{q}
by

b_{0}=1,\ \ b_{1}=x_{1},\ \ b_{2}=x_{2},\ \ b_{3}=x_{1}x_{2}.

Consider the dual basis of

, where
b_{i}^{*}(b_{j})=\delta_{ij},\ \forall\ 0\leq i,j\leq 3
. By Lemma
2.4
one has

.

All the Frobenius homomorphisms of

are
\phi=c_{0}b_{0}^{*}+c_{1}b_{1}^{*}+c_{2}b_{2}^{*}+c_{3}b_{3}^{*}
, where

.

The following lemma is not a consequence of Theorem
3.2
, since the coalgebra structure in Theorem

is special, as given in (3.1); but in the following lemma there are no restrictions on coalgebra structures.

Lemma 5.3
.

If
A_{q}
admits a bi-Frobenius algebra structure, then

Proof.

Suppose that

and counit

.

c_{11}=\frac{1}{q^{2}}c_{11}c_{21}^{2}-\frac{1}{q}c_{11}c_{12}c_{21}-\frac{1}{q}c_{11}c_{21}^{2}+c_{22}c_{11}^{2},

[formula]

c_{12}=\frac{1}{q^{2}}c_{11}c_{21}c_{22}-\frac{1}{q}c_{11}c_{12}c_{22}-\frac{1}{q}c_{12}c_{21}^{2}+c_{22}c_{11}c_{12},

[formula]

c_{21}=\frac{1}{q^{2}}c_{11}c_{21}c_{22}-\frac{1}{q}c_{21}c_{12}^{2}-\frac{1}{q}c_{11}c_{21}c_{22}+c_{11}c_{12}c_{22}.

By
S(x_{1})^{2}=0
one has
(c_{21}c_{22})(1-q)=0
. By
S(x_{2})^{2}=0
one has
c_{11}c_{12}(1-q)=0
. By
S(x_{1}x_{2})=S(x_{2})S(x_{1})
one has
c_{12}c_{21}-qc_{11}c_{22}=q^{2}.

All together one has the following:

(i)
c_{11}c_{22}\neq c_{12}c_{21};

(ii)
(c_{21}c_{22})(1-q)=0;

(iii)
c_{11}c_{12}(1-q)=0;

(iv)
c_{12}c_{21}-qc_{11}c_{22}=q^{2}.

Now, assume that
q\neq 1
. By (ii) and (iii),
c_{21}c_{22}=0=c_{11}c_{12}.
By (5.2) and (5.3) one has

[formula]

c_{11}=\frac{1}{q^{2}}c_{11}c_{21}^{2}-\frac{1}{q}c_{11}c_{21}^{2}+c_{22}c_{11}^{2}

[formula]

c_{12}=-\frac{1}{q}c_{12}c_{21}^{2}

By
c_{11}c_{22}\neq c_{12}c_{21}
and
c_{21}c_{22}=0=c_{11}c_{12}
, there are two situations:

(1)

c_{11}=0=c_{22}
and
c_{12}\neq 0\neq c_{21};

(2)

c_{12}=0=c_{21}
and
c_{11}\neq 0\neq c_{22}.

If (1) happens, then by (iv) one has
c_{12}c_{21}=q^{2}
. By
(5.5)
and (5.6) one gets
c_{21}^{2}=-q=c_{12}^{2}
. Thus
q^{4}=q^{2}
, and hence
q=-1
.

If (2) happens, then by (iv) one has
c_{11}c_{22}=-q
. By
(5.5)
one gets
c_{11}c_{22}=1
. Thus
q=-1
.

In conclusion, if
A_{q}
has a bi-Frobenius algebra structure, then either
q=1
, or
q=-1
. That is, if
q^{2}\neq 1
, then

A_{q}
can not be a bi-Frobenius algebra.∎

6.
Non-isomorphic bi-Frobenius algebra structures on a commutative algebra

We give two coalgebra structures on a complete intersection ring, such that it admits non-isomorphic bi-Frobenius algebra structures.

6.1.
Coalgebra structure I

Taking
{\bf q=-1}
(i.e.,
q_{ij}=-1
for
1\leq i,j\leq n
) in quantum complete intersections, one gets complete intersection ring

A=A({\bf-1},a_{1},\cdots,a_{n})=k\langle x_{1},\cdots,x_{n}\rangle/\langle x_{i}^{a_{i}},\ x_{i}x_{j}-x_{j}x_{i},\ \ 1\leq i,\ j\leq n\rangle.

Thus
\mathcal{B}=\{x_{\mathbf{v}}=x_{1}^{v_{1}}\cdots x_{n}^{v_{n}}\ |\ \mathbf{v}\in V\}
is a basis of
A
,
where
V=\{\mathbf{v}=(v_{1},\cdots,v_{n})\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{n}\ |\ v_{i}\leq a_{i}-1,\ 1\leq i\leq n\}.
Inspired by
the construction of path coalgebra of quivers (see D. Simson
[
27
]
; also C. Cibils and M. Rosso
[
10
]
), we consider the
k
-linear maps
\varepsilon:A\longrightarrow k
and
\Delta:A\longrightarrow A\otimes A
given by

[formula]

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\varepsilon(x_{\mathbf{v}})=\delta_{\mathbf{v,0}},\ \ \forall\ \mathbf{v}\in V;\\
\\
\Delta(x_{\mathbf{v}})=\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{v_{1}+v_{2}=v},\\
\mathbf{v_{1},v_{2}}\in V\end{subarray}}x_{\mathbf{v_{1}}}\otimes x_{\mathbf{v_{2}}},\ \ \forall\ \mathbf{v}\in V.\end{array}\right.

Theorem 6.1
.

With counit
\varepsilon
and comultiplication
\Delta
as in
(6.1)
, the complete intersection ring
A=A({\bf-1},a_{1},\cdots,a_{n})
forms a bi-Frobenius algebra

(A,\ (x_{\mathbf{a-1}})^{*},\ x_{\mathbf{a-1}},\ {\rm Id})
.

Moreover, in general this bi-Frobenius algebra is not isomorphic
(
as a bi-Frobenius algebra
)
to the one in
Corollary
3.9
, with coalgebra structure given by
(3.1)
.

Proof.

It is straightforward to verify that
(A,\ \Delta,\ \varepsilon)
is a coalgebra, the identity
1
is a group-like element, and that
\varepsilon
is an algebra map.
Put
t=x_{\bf a-1}
. Since
A
is commutative and cocommutative,
{\rm Id}
is an algebra anti-homomorphism and a coalgebra anti-homomorphism. To prove
(A,\ (x_{\mathbf{a-1}})^{*},\ x_{\mathbf{a-1}},\ {\rm Id})
is a bi-Frobenius algebra,
by Lemma
2.3
,
it remains to check

\sum(x_{\bf a-1})^{*}(t_{1}x_{\bf v})t_{2}=x_{\bf v},\ \ \forall\ {\bf v}\in V.

In fact, by
\Delta(t)=\Delta(x_{\bf a-1})=\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{v_{1}+v_{2}=a-1},\\
\mathbf{v_{1},v_{2}}\in V\end{subarray}}x_{\mathbf{v_{1}}}\otimes x_{\mathbf{v_{2}}}
,
one has

\displaystyle\sum(x_{\bf a-1})^{*}(t_{1}x_{\bf v})t_{2}=(x_{\bf a-1})^{*}(x_{\bf a-1-v}\ x_{\bf v})\ x_{\bf v}=x_{\bf v},\ \forall\ {\bf v}\in V.

This proves
(A,\ (x_{\mathbf{a-1}})^{*},\ x_{\mathbf{a-1}},\ {\rm Id})
is a bi-Frobenius algebra.
For convenience, denote this bi-Frobenius algebra by
B_{1}
.

When
\sqrt{-1}\in k
, to show that
B_{1}
is not isomorphic to the bi-Frobenius algebra
B_{2}=(A,\ (x_{{\bf a-1}})^{*},\ x_{{\bf a-1}},\ S)
in Theorem
3.2
, we consider the spaces
P(B_{1})
and
P(B_{2})
, of the primitive elements of
B_{1}
and
B_{2}
, respectively.

By Lemma
3.4
, dim
P(B_{2})=(\prod\limits_{1\leq i\leq n}a_{i})-2.

On the other hand, each
x_{i}
is a primitive element of
B_{1}
.
We claim that
{\rm dim}\ P(B_{1})=n
. Otherwise, there exists a primitive element

x=\sum\limits_{{\bf v}\in W}c_{\bf v}\ x_{\bf v}
, where
W
is a non empty subset of
V
such that
c_{\bf v}\neq 0
and
{\bf v\neq e_{i}}
for
1\leq i\leq n
, for all
{\bf v}\in W
. From the proof below, one can see that, without loss of generality, one may assume that
{\bf 0}\notin W
. From the right hand side of the equality

\sum\limits_{{\bf v}\in W}c_{\bf v}\ \Delta(x_{\bf v})=\Delta(x)=1\otimes x+x\otimes 1=\sum\limits_{{\bf v}\in W}c_{\bf v}\ (1\otimes x_{\bf v})+\sum\limits_{{\bf v}\in W}c_{\bf v}\ (x_{\bf v}\otimes 1)

and since
{\bf 0}\notin W
, one sees that
\Delta(x)
is a
k
-linear combination of
2|W|
elements in the basis
\mathcal{B}\otimes\mathcal{B}
of
A\otimes A
, with each coefficient nonzero, where
|W|
is the number of elements in
W
. While by (6.1), each
\Delta(x_{\bf v})
in the left hand side is a
k
-linear combination of
t
elements in the basis
\mathcal{B}\otimes\mathcal{B}
, where
t>2
.
For
{\bf u\neq v}\in V
, there are no the same summands in
\Delta(x_{\bf u})
and
\Delta(x_{\bf v})
, since
x_{\bf u_{1}}\otimes x_{\bf u_{2}}=x_{\bf v_{1}}\otimes x_{\bf v_{2}}
implies
{\bf u=u_{1}+u_{2}=v_{1}+v_{2}=v}
. In this way we see that
\Delta(x)
is a
k
-linear combination of
s
elements in the basis
\mathcal{B}\otimes\mathcal{B}
, with each coefficient nonzero, where
s>2|W|
. A contradiction! This proves dim
P(B_{1})=n
.

Thus, one has
{\rm dim}\ P(B_{2})=(\prod\limits_{1\leq i\leq n}a_{i})-2>n={\rm dim}\ P(B_{1})
, except the case
n=2=a_{1}=a_{2}
. So
B_{1}\ncong B_{2}
as coalgebras, except
n=2=a_{1}=a_{2}
. This completes the proof. ∎

6.2.
Coalgebra structure II

In Subsection 6.1, taking

, namely

Consider the
k
-linear maps
\varepsilon:A\longrightarrow k
and
\Delta:A\longrightarrow A\otimes A
given by

[formula]

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\varepsilon(x_{\mathbf{v}})=\delta_{\mathbf{0,v}},\ \ \forall\ \mathbf{v}\in V;\\
\\
\Delta(x_{\mathbf{v}})=\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{v_{1},v_{2}\leq v}\\
\mathbf{v\leq v_{1}+v_{2}}\end{subarray}}(-1)^{|\mathbf{v_{1}+v_{2}-v}|}\ x_{\mathbf{v_{1}}}\otimes x_{\mathbf{v_{2}}},\ \ \forall\ \mathbf{v}\in V.\end{array}\right.

Theorem 6.3
.

With counit
\varepsilon
and comultiplication
\Delta
given by
(6.2)
,

(A,\ \phi,\ t=x_{1}\cdots x_{n},\ {\rm Id})
is a bi-Frobenius algebra, where
\phi=\sum\limits_{\mathbf{v}\in V}(x_{\mathbf{v}})^{*}
.

Moreover, this bi-Frobenius algebra has no primitive elements, and hence it can not be isomorphic to the bi-Frobenius algebras given in
Corollary
3.9
and in
Theorem
6.1
.

Proof.

One directly verify that
(A,\ \Delta,\ \varepsilon)
is a coalgebra:

\displaystyle(\Delta\otimes{\rm Id})\Delta(x_{\mathbf{v}})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{v_{1},v_{2}\leq v}\\
\mathbf{v\leq v_{1}+v_{2}}\end{subarray}}(-1)^{|\mathbf{v_{1}+v_{2}-v}|}\ \Delta(x_{\mathbf{v_{1}}})\otimes x_{\mathbf{v_{2}}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{v_{1},v_{2},v_{3}\leq v}\\
\mathbf{v\leq v_{1}+v_{2}+v_{3}}\end{subarray}}(-1)^{|\mathbf{v_{1}+v_{2}+v_{3}-v}|}\ x_{\mathbf{v_{1}}}\otimes x_{\mathbf{v_{2}}}\otimes x_{\mathbf{v_{3}}}

\displaystyle=({\rm Id}\otimes\Delta)\Delta(x_{\mathbf{v}}),\ \ \forall\ \mathbf{v}\in V

and

\displaystyle(\varepsilon\otimes{\rm Id})\Delta(x_{\mathbf{v}})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathbf{v\leq v_{2}\leq v}}(-1)^{|\mathbf{v_{2}-v}|}\ 1\otimes x_{\mathbf{v_{2}}}=1\otimes x_{\mathbf{v}}=x_{\mathbf{v}}

\displaystyle=x_{\mathbf{v}}\otimes 1=\sum_{\mathbf{v\leq v_{1}\leq v}}(-1)^{|\mathbf{v_{1}-v}|}\ x_{\mathbf{v_{1}}}\otimes 1=({\rm Id}\otimes\varepsilon)\Delta(x_{\mathbf{v}}),\ \ \forall\ \mathbf{v}\in V.

Put
t=x_{1}\cdots x_{n}
. Since
A
is commutative and cocommutative,
{\rm Id}
is an algebra anti-homomorphism and a coalgebra anti-homomorphism.
By Lemma
2.3
, it remains to prove

\sum\phi(t_{1}x_{\bf u})t_{2}=x_{\bf u},\ \ \forall\ {\bf u}\in V.

In fact, by

\Delta(t)=\Delta(x_{1}\cdots x_{n})=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{v_{1},v_{2}}\leq(1,\cdots,1)\\
(1,\cdots,1)\leq\mathbf{v_{1}+v_{2}}\end{subarray}}(-1)^{|\mathbf{v_{1}+v_{2}-1}|}\ x_{\mathbf{v_{1}}}\otimes x_{\mathbf{v_{2}}}

one has

\displaystyle\sum\phi(t_{1}x_{\bf u})t_{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{v_{1},v_{2}}\leq(1,\cdots,1)\\
(1,\cdots,1)\leq{\bf v_{1}+v_{2}}\end{subarray}}(-1)^{\bf|{v_{1}}+{v_{2}}-1|}\ \phi(x_{\mathbf{v_{1}}}\ x_{\mathbf{u}})\ x_{\mathbf{v_{2}}},\ \forall\ \mathbf{u}\in V.

If
v_{1i}>1-u_{i}
for some
i
(here
v_{1i}
means the
i
th component of
{\bf v_{1}}
), then
v_{1i}+u_{i}\geq 2
, and then
x_{\mathbf{v_{1}}}\ x_{\mathbf{u}}=x_{\mathbf{v_{1}+u}}=0
. Thus, we only need to consider those
\mathbf{v_{1}}
with

\mathbf{v_{1}}\leq\mathbf{1-u}
, and hence
{\bf v_{2}\geq 1-v_{1}\geq u}
. Fixing
{\bf v_{2}}
, then the coefficient of
x_{\mathbf{v_{2}}}
in the sum above is (note that
\phi=\sum\limits_{\mathbf{v}\in V}(x_{\mathbf{v}})^{*}
)

\displaystyle\sum_{\mathbf{1-v_{2}\leq v_{1}\leq 1-u}}(-1)^{|{\bf v_{1}+v_{2}-1}|}\ \phi(x_{\mathbf{v_{1}+u}})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathbf{1-v_{2}\leq v_{1}\leq 1-u}}(-1)^{\bf|{v_{1}}+{v_{2}}-1|}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathbf{0\leq v_{1}+v_{2}-1\leq v_{2}-u}}(-1)^{\bf|{v_{1}}+{v_{2}}-1|}.

If
\mathbf{v_{2}=u}
, then the vector
{\bf v_{1}}
in the sum is unique, so the coefficient of
x_{\mathbf{v_{2}}}
in the sum is

\sum\limits_{\mathbf{0\leq v_{1}+v_{2}-1\leq v_{2}-u}}(-1)^{|{\bf v_{1}+v_{2}}-1|}=(-1)^{|{\bf 0}|}=1.

Assume that
{\bf v_{2}}\neq\mathbf{u}
. Set
m=|{\bf v_{2}-u}|
. Note that
{\bf v_{2}-u}
is
a vector with
m
components being
1
and
n-m
components being
0
. We count the number of vectors

{\bf v_{1}}
in the sum via
|{\bf v_{1}+v_{2}-1}|
as follows.

When
|{\bf v_{1}+v_{2}-1}|=0
, then the choice of
{\bf v_{1}}
is unique,
i.e.,
{\bf v_{1}=1-v_{2}}
. That is, the number of
{\bf v_{1}}
is
\binom{m}{0}=1
.

When
|{\bf v_{1}+v_{2}-1}|=1
, since
\mathbf{v_{1}+v_{2}-1\leq v_{2}-u}
and
{\bf v_{2}-u}
has

m
components being
1
, it follows that the number of the choices of
{\bf v_{1}}
such that
|{\bf v_{1}+v_{2}-1}|=1
is just
\binom{m}{1}
;

In general, when
|{\bf v_{1}+v_{2}-1}|=i
with
1\leq i\leq n
, then the number of the choices of
{\bf v_{1}}
such that
|{\bf v_{1}+v_{2}-1}|=i
is just
\binom{m}{i}
.

All together, if
{\bf v_{2}}\neq\mathbf{u}
, then we see that the coefficient of
x_{\mathbf{v_{2}}}
in the sum above is

\sum_{\mathbf{0\leq v_{1}+v_{2}-1\leq v_{2}-u}}(-1)^{\bf|{v_{1}}+{v_{2}}-1|}=\sum_{i=0}^{m}\binom{m}{i}(-1)^{i}=0.

In this way we see that

\displaystyle\sum\phi(t_{1}x_{\bf u})t_{2}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{v_{1},v_{2}}\leq(1,\cdots,1)\\
(1,\cdots,1)\leq{\bf v_{1}+v_{2}}\end{subarray}}(-1)^{\bf|{v_{1}}+{v_{2}}-1|}\ \phi(x_{\mathbf{v_{1}}}\ x_{\mathbf{u}})\ x_{\mathbf{v_{2}}}=x_{\bf u},\ \ \forall\ {\bf u}\in V.

This proves that
(A,\ \phi,\ x_{\mathbf{1}}=x_{1}\cdots x_{n},\ {\rm Id})
is a bi-Frobenius algebra.

Using the similar idea as in the proof of Theorem
6.1
, one claim that
this bi-Frobenius algebra has no primitive elements. For completeness, we include a proof.

In fact, assume otherwise that
x=\sum\limits_{{\bf v}\in W}c_{\bf v}\ x_{\bf v}
is a primitive element, where
W
is a non empty subset of
V
such that
c_{\bf v}\neq 0
for all
{\bf v}\in W
.

First, assume that
{\bf 0}\notin W
. From the right hand side of the equality

\Delta(x)=1\otimes x+x\otimes 1=\sum\limits_{{\bf v}\in W}c_{\bf v}\ (1\otimes x_{\bf v})+\sum\limits_{{\bf v}\in W}c_{\bf v}\ (x_{\bf v}\otimes 1)

and since
{\bf 0}\notin W
, one sees that
\Delta(x)
is a
k
-linear combination of
2|W|
elements in the basis
\mathcal{B}\otimes\mathcal{B}
of
A\otimes A
, with each coefficient nonzero. On the other hand,

\Delta(x)=\sum\limits_{{\bf v}\in W}c_{\bf v}\ \Delta(x_{\bf v})

and by (6.2) one has

\Delta(x_{\mathbf{v}})=\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{v_{1},v_{2}\leq v}\\
\mathbf{v\leq v_{1}+v_{2}}\end{subarray}}(-1)^{|\mathbf{v_{1}+v_{2}-v}|}\ x_{\mathbf{v_{1}}}\otimes x_{\mathbf{v_{2}}}.

Since
{\bf 0}\notin W
and
\Delta(x_{\bf v})
at least includes the terms:

x_{\bf v}\otimes x_{\bf v},\ \ x_{\bf v-e_{i}}\otimes x_{\bf v},\ \ x_{\bf v}\otimes x_{\bf v-e_{i}}

for some
{\bf e_{i}}
, it follows that
\Delta(x_{\bf v})
is a
k
-linear combination of
t

\mathcal{B}\otimes\mathcal{B}
, where

.

For
{\bf u\neq v}\in V
, there are no the same terms in

and
\Delta(x_{\bf v})
. Actually, if
x_{\bf u_{1}}\otimes x_{\bf u_{2}}=x_{\bf v_{1}}\otimes x_{\bf v_{2}}
, then

{\bf u_{1}}={\bf v_{1}},\ \ {\bf u_{2}}={\bf v_{2}},\ \ {\bf u_{1},u_{2}\leq u\leq u_{1}+u_{2}},\ \ {\bf v_{1},v_{2}\leq v\leq v_{1}+v_{2}}.

Since
a_{i}=2

, one has

u_{i}=\begin{cases}0&\text{if}\ \ {u_{1}}_{i}={u_{2}}_{i}=0;\\
1&\text{if \ \ otherwise}\end{cases}

and

It follows that
{\bf u=v}.
In this way we see that
\Delta(x)
is a
k
-linear combination of
s
elements in the basis
\mathcal{B}\otimes\mathcal{B}
, with each coefficient nonzero, where

. A contradiction!

Next, assume that
{\bf 0}\in W
. Since

is not a primitive element,

. By the similar argument as above, one sees that

\sum\limits_{{\bf v}\in W}c_{\bf v}\ \Delta(x_{\bf v})=\Delta(x)=\sum\limits_{{\bf v}\in W}c_{\bf v}\ (1\otimes x_{\bf v})+\sum\limits_{{\bf v}\in W}c_{\bf v}\ (x_{\bf v}\otimes 1).

The left hand side is a

-linear combination of
s
elements in the basis
\mathcal{B}\otimes\mathcal{B}
, with each coefficient nonzero, where

; while
the right hand side is a
k
-linear combination of

elements in the basis
\mathcal{B}\otimes\mathcal{B}
, with each coefficient nonzero. A contradiction!

This completes the proof. ∎
