The handlebody group is a virtual duality group
Dan Petersen, Richard D. Wade

Abstract
. We show that the mapping class group of a handlebody is a virtual duality group, in the sense of Bieri and Eckmann. In positive genus we give a description of the dualising module of any torsion-free, finite-index subgroup of the handlebody mapping class group as the homology of the complex of non-simple disc systems .



1.
Introduction

Let
\Gamma
be a discrete group. Suppose that there exists a closed
d
-manifold
X
which is a
K(\Gamma,1)
. Then by Poincaré duality on
X
one has isomorphisms

H^{k}(\Gamma,A)\cong H_{d-k}(\Gamma,A\otimes D)

for all
k\in\mathbb{Z}
and all
\Gamma
-modules
A
, where
D=\mathbb{Z}
and the
\Gamma
-action on
D
corresponds to the orientation local system on
X
. The isomorphisms are natural, being implemented by capping with the fundamental class in
H_{d}(\Gamma,D)
. A group
\Gamma
is a
Poincaré duality group
of
cohomological dimension

d
if it admits such a
dualising module

D
and fundamental class in
H_{d}(\Gamma,D)
inducing isomorphisms as above.

It is natural to relax the condition that
D=\mathbb{Z}
as an abelian group. Allowing the dualising module
D
to be arbitrary leads to the more general notion of a
duality group
, as introduced by Bieri–Eckmann
[
2
]
. Many groups of interest in topology and geometric group theory are known to be duality groups, at least virtually: in particular, arithmetic groups, mapping class groups of compact surfaces, and (outer) automorphism groups of free groups are all virtual duality groups
[
3
,
12
,
1
]
.

McCullough
[
19
]
showed that the mapping class group
\mathrm{Mod}(M)
of a Haken three-manifold
M
is also a virtual duality group. Away from the Haken case, let
V
be a solid handlebody of genus
g
, with
b
marked discs and
p
marked points on its boundary. In the same paper, McCullough showed that
\mathrm{Mod}(V)
virtually has a finite classifying space. However, he could only show that
\mathrm{Mod}(V)
is a virtual duality group when
g=2
, using very special properties of the genus two handlebody group. Our first main theorem shows that the mapping class group of any handlebody is indeed a virtual duality group.

Theorem A
.

For
2g-2+b+p>0
, the handlebody group
\mathrm{Mod}(V)
is a virtual duality
group of dimension
d(g,b,p)
, where
d(g,0,0)=4g-5
;
d(g,b,p)=4g+2b+p-4
when
g>0
and
b+p>0
; and
d(0,b,p)=2b+p-3
.

Remark 1.1
.

Let
S
be the surface with
p
punctures and
b
boundary components obtained by removing the marked points and the interiors of the marked discs from
\partial V
. Then
\mathrm{Mod}(V)
can be identified with the subgroup of
\mathrm{Mod}(S)
consisting of diffeomorphisms of
S
that extend to
V
. Hirose
[
15
]
showed that
\mathrm{Mod}(V)
has the same virtual cohomological dimension as
\mathrm{Mod}(S)
. The fact that the virtual cohomological dimension of
\mathrm{Mod}(S)
is
d(g,b,p)
is due to Harer
[
12
]
.

One reason to care about a group being a virtual duality group is that it provides a powerful tool for studying its cohomology in high degrees, close to the virtual cohomological dimension; this is in a sense complementary to the phenomenon of homological stability. However, this is only feasible if one knows a meaningful description of the dualising module. We refer the reader to the survey of Brück
[
7
]
for more about this circle of ideas and many pointers to the literature.

In the case of arithmetic groups, the dualising module is called the
Steinberg module
. It is given by the unique nontrivial reduced homology group of the
Tits building
associated with the arithmetic group
[
3
]
. The analogue of the Steinberg module for the mapping class group was also determined in Harer’s work
[
12
]
: the dualising module of
\mathrm{Mod}(S)
is isomorphic to the unique nontrivial reduced homology group of the
complex of curves
(as a
\mathrm{Mod}(S)
-module).

The second main theorem of this paper describes the analogue of the Steinberg module for the handlebody group, i.e. the dualising module of
\mathrm{Mod}(V)
. We do this only when
g>0
; indeed, the map
\mathrm{Mod}(V)\to\mathrm{Mod}(S)
is an isomorphism when
g=0
, and in this case we already have a description of the dualising module from Harer’s work.

Definition 1.2
.

By an
essential disc
in
V
we shall always mean an isotopy class of embedded disc, with boundary in the interior of
S
, whose boundary does not bound an annulus or a (punctured) disc in
S
. By a
disc system
we mean a set of distinct disjointly embeddable essential discs. A disc system is
simple
if it cuts
V
into pieces of genus zero, and is
non-simple
otherwise.

The
disc complex

\mathbf{D}=\mathbf{D}(V)
has vertex set equal to the essential discs in
V
. A set of discs spans a simplex in
\mathbf{D}
if and only if they form a disc system in
V
. We let
\mathbf{NS}=\mathbf{NS}(V)
denote the
complex of non-simple disc systems
in
V
, which is the subcomplex
\mathbf{NS}\subset\mathbf{D}
whose
k
-simplices are given by non-simple systems of
k+1
essential discs in the handlebody
V
.

Theorem B
.

Let
2g-2+b+p>0
and
g>0
. Then
\mathbf{NS}(V)
is homology equivalent to a wedge of spheres of dimension
\nu(g,b,p)
, where
\nu(g,0,0)=2g-3
and
\nu(g,b,p)=2g-4+b+p
if
b+p>0
.

Remark 1.3
.

If
g>2
, then
\mathbf{NS}
is also simply connected, hence homotopy equivalent to a wedge of spheres. See
. Although we believe that
\mathbf{NS}
is always homotopic to a wedge of spheres, we were unable to show this in general.

Theorem C
.

Keep notation as in
Theorem

B
.
The dualising module of any torsion-free finite index subgroup of
\mathrm{Mod}(V)
is
\widetilde{H}_{\nu(g,b,p)}(\mathbf{NS}(V),\mathbb{Z})
.

Remark 1.4
.

The preceding theorem is correct also in the exceptional case
g=1
,
b+p=1
, if one uses the convention that the reduced homology of the empty space is
\mathbb{Z}
in degree
-1
.

Remark 1.5
.

The appearance of the simplicial complex
\mathbf{NS}
is striking, especially in relation to Culler–Vogtmann’s
Outer space

[
9
]
. Let us expand on this connection, assuming for simplicity that
V
has genus
g\geq 2
and
b=p=0
. A disc in
V
gives rise to a sphere in the double
M
of
V
, which is the connected sum of
g
copies of
S^{1}\times S^{2}
, and
\pi_{1}(V)\cong\pi_{1}(M)\cong F_{g}
. The action of
\mathrm{Mod}(V)
on
\pi_{1}(V)
induces a surjective homomorphism
\mathrm{Mod}(V)\to\mathrm{Out}(F_{g})
.

Doubling discs induces a natural map
f:\mathbf{D}\to\mathbf{Sph}
to the
sphere complex
of
M
, which is equivariant under the map
\mathrm{Mod}(V)\to\mathrm{Out}(F_{g})
, and also surjective. In the same manner as above, one defines a sphere system to be
simple
if it cuts the doubled handlebody into simply connected pieces. We denote the subcomplex of non-simple sphere systems by
\partial\mathbf{CV}\subset\mathbf{Sph}
, so that
f^{-1}(\partial\mathbf{CV})=\mathbf{NS}
. The complement
\mathbf{Sph}\setminus\partial\mathbf{CV}
is precisely Outer space. Thus
\mathbf{NS}
is a close analogue of the simplicial boundary of Outer space.

Theorem
B
gives the homotopy type of
\mathbf{NS}
and
Theorem

C
that its homology is the dualising module of
\mathrm{Mod}(V)
. We do not know the homotopy type of
\partial\mathbf{CV}
, nor do we know its relationship with the dualising module of
\mathrm{Out}(F_{g})
(this is explored further in
[
22
]
). Brück and Gupta
[
6
, Theorem 2]
have shown that
\partial\mathbf{CV}
is homotopy equivalent to the realization of the poset of
free factor systems
of rank
g
, and that it is at least
(g-2)
-connected. Vogtmann
[
21
]
has shown that a reduced version
\partial\mathbf{CV}^{r}
is homotopy equivalent to the
boundary of Jewel space
. By
[
8
]
, this reduced boundary
\partial\mathbf{CV}^{r}
is also the boundary of the bordification of reduced Outer space used in Bestvina and Feighn’s proof that
\mathrm{Out}(F_{g})
is a virtual duality group. Beyond
\partial\mathbf{CV}
and
\partial\mathbf{CV}^{r}
, a third analogue of the Tits building in this context is the free factor complex, which in general is known not to present the dualising module
[
14
]
.

We remark that the distinction between reduced and unreduced Outer space is often blurred in the literature. When considering their simplicial boundary, it is important to distinguish between them:
\partial\mathbf{CV}\not\simeq\partial\mathbf{CV}^{r}
.

1.1.
An outline of the proofs

To ease the outline, assume
V
satisfies
g\geq 2
and
b=p=0
. A group
G
is a virtual duality group if it satisfies the following three conditions (Corollary
2.8
below):

(1)

G
is virtually torsion-free.

(2)

G
acts properly and cocompactly on a contractible
orientable
d
-dimensional topological manifold-with-boundary
M
.

(3)

\partial M
is homology equivalent to a wedge of spheres of constant dimension.

If (1)—(3) hold, then
\widetilde{H}_{\ast}(\partial M,\mathbb{Z})
is the dualising module of
G
. We apply this to
G=\mathrm{Mod}(V)
. The fact that
\mathrm{Mod}(V)
is virtually torsion-free follows from the fact that it is a subgroup of
\mathrm{Mod}(S)
, which is virtually torsion-free
[
10
, Theorem 6.9]
.

Recall that a
meridian
on
S=\partial V
is a curve bounding a disc in
V
. Discs are determined up to isotopy by their meridians. We pick constants
\varepsilon<\varepsilon^{\prime}<\log(3+\sqrt{8})
and take
M
to be the subspace of the Teichmüller space of
S
consisting of marked surfaces
\sigma
where each simple closed curve on
\sigma
has length at least
\varepsilon
, and the set of curves of length at most
\varepsilon^{\prime}
on
\sigma
form a
simple system of meridians
(the requirement
\varepsilon^{\prime}<\log(3+\sqrt{8})
ensures that the set of curves of length at most
\varepsilon^{\prime}
are disjoint). The space
M
is a truncated cover of the orbifold classifying space of
\mathrm{Mod}(V)
constructed by Hainaut and the first author in
[
11
]
. The work in
[
11
]
implies that
M
is a contractible manifold with boundary (Theorem
3.1
), and the truncation ensures the action is cocompact.

We can write
\partial M=N_{1}\cup N_{2}
, where
N_{1}
parametrises hyperbolic surfaces with a closed geodesic of length
\varepsilon
, and
N_{2}
parametrises hyperbolic surfaces with the property that the closed geodesics of length
strictly less
than
\varepsilon^{\prime}
form a
non-simple
system of meridians. We show that:

(1)

N_{1}
is contractible,

(2)

N_{2}
is contractible,

(3)

N_{1}\cap N_{2}
is homotopy equivalent to
\mathbf{NS}
.

Consequently,
\partial M
is homotopy equivalent to the suspension
\Sigma\mathbf{NS}
. The results (i)—(iii) are established by considerations similar to those of Borel–Serre
[
3
]
and Harer
[
12
]
: we cover
\partial M
by closed contractible pieces, and deduce that
\partial M
is homotopy equivalent to the nerve of the covering.
Concretely, we obtain a poset — we denote it
\partial\mathsf{RGB}
— such that
|\partial\mathsf{RGB}|\simeq\partial M
, and such that
N_{1}
,
N_{2}
, and
N_{1}\cap N_{2}
are homotopic to realizations of subposets of
\partial\mathsf{RGB}
. These subposets are analyzed using poset arguments in the spirit of Quillen
[
20
]
to show (i)—(iii).

After the above, Theorems
A
,
B
, and
C
all follow after showing that
\mathbf{NS}
is homology equivalent to a wedge sum of copies of
S^{2g-3}
. This is achieved via two steps:

•

\mathbf{NS}
is homologically
(2g-4)
-connected.

•

\mathbf{NS}
is homology equivalent to a
(2g-3)
-dimensional CW complex.

The first bullet point is easy: we already know that the virtual cohomological dimension of
\mathrm{Mod}(V)
is
4g-5
, so the compactly supported cohomology
H^{\ast}_{c}(M,\mathbb{Z})
of
M
vanishes for
\ast>4g-5
. As
M
is
(6g-6)
-dimensional, Lefschetz duality implies that
\partial M\simeq\Sigma\mathbf{NS}
is
(2g-3)
-acyclic.

The second bullet point is carried out by an adaptation of the strategy used in the work of Ivanov
[
17
]
to prove that the curve complex is homotopy equivalent to a CW complex of dimension at most
2g-2
.

2.
Background on (virtual) duality groups

In this section we recall some well-known facts about duality groups, for future reference and for the reader’s convenience. The notion of a duality group was introduced by Bieri and Eckmann
[
2
]
, and nearly all that follows can be found in their original article.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
G
be a group. We say that
G
is a
duality group
of
cohomological dimension

n
, with
dualising module

C
, if there exists a class
e\in H_{n}(G,C)
such that cap-product with
e
defines a natural isomorphism of functors

H^{k}(G,-)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\cong}}{{\longrightarrow}}H_{n-k}(G,C\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}-)

for all integers
k
.

Remark 2.2
.

If
C=\mathbb{Z}
(as an abelian group, possibly with nontrivial
G
-action), then one arrives at the special case of a
Poincaré duality group
. A source of geometric examples of Poincaré duality groups of dimension
n
are the fundamental groups of aspherical closed manifolds of dimension
n
, in which case
C
is the rank one local system given by the orientation sheaf. Allowing
C
to have larger rank leads to a significantly richer theory.

Duality for a group of type
\mathit{FP}
can be detected by cohomology with coefficients in the group ring.

Theorem 2.3
.

The following two properties are equivalent for a group
G
.

(1)

G
is a duality group of dimension
n
.

(2)

G
is of type
\mathit{FP}
,
H^{*}(G,\mathbb{Z}[G])
is torsion-free, and
H^{k}(G,\mathbb{Z}[G])=0
unless
k=n
.

When this holds,
H^{n}(G,\mathbb{Z}[G])
is the dualising module of
G
.

Proof.

All parts of the implication (1)
\Rightarrow
(2) is proven in
[
2
, Section 1]
, except for
G
necessarily being of type
\mathit{FP}
, which is due to Brown
[
4
]
. The implication (2)
\Rightarrow
(1) is
[
2
, Theorem 4.5]
.
∎

Theorem

2.3
implies in particular that the dualising module and the dimension are uniquely determined by the group
G
.

Duality groups have finite homological dimension and are in particular torsion-free. The theory is however still useful for groups which are merely virtually torsion-free; namely, one is led to the following definition:

Definition 2.4
.

A group
G
is a
virtual duality group
if it admits a finite index subgroup which is a duality group.

Proposition 2.5
.

Let
G
be a torsion-free group. Then
G
is a duality group if and only if any one of its finite-index subgroups is a duality group. The dualising module of each finite-index subgroup is then canonically isomorphic.

Proof.

[
2
, Theorem 3.2]
and
[
2
, Theorem 3.3]
.
∎

Proposition

2.5
thus says that if
some
finite index subgroup of a group
G
is a duality group, then
all
torsion-free finite index subgroups of
G
are duality groups, and they all have the same cohomological dimension and dualising module. In particular, the dimension and dualising module of a virtual duality group are well-defined.

Proposition 2.6
.

Let
1\to K\to G\to H\to 1
be a short exact sequence of groups. If
K
is a duality group of dimension
k
and
H
is a (virtual) duality group of dimension
l
, then
G
is a (virtual) duality group of dimension
k+l
, and the dualising module for
G
is the tensor product of the dualising modules for
K
and
H
.

Proof.

For actual duality groups this is
[
2
, Theorem 3.5]
. The virtual case follows by choosing a finite-index duality subgroup
H^{\prime}\leq H
, taking
G^{\prime}
to be the preimage of
H^{\prime}
in
G
, and considering
1\to K\to G^{\prime}\to H^{\prime}\to 1
.
∎

Proposition 2.7
.

Let
G
be a group acting freely, properly and cocompactly on a contractible orientable
d
-dimensional topological manifold-with-boundary
M
. Then there are isomorphisms

H^{k}(G,\mathbb{Z}[G])\cong\widetilde{H}_{d-1-k}(\partial M,\mathbb{Z})

for all
k
.

Proof.

[
2
, §6.4]
.
∎

Corollary 2.8
.

Let
G
be a (virtually) torsion-free group acting properly and cocompactly on a contractible orientable
d
-dimensional topological manifold-with-boundary
M
. Then
G
is a (virtual) duality group if and only if
\partial M
is homology equivalent to a wedge of spheres of equal dimension
q
. When this holds, the dualising module is
\widetilde{H}_{q}(\partial M,\mathbb{Z})
, and the (virtual) cohomological dimension is
d-q-1
.

Proof.

This is
[
2
, Theorem 6.2]
. If
G
is torsion-free then
G
acts freely on
M
, and
G
is of type
\mathit{FP}
since it admits a classifying space
M/G
of the homotopy type of a finite CW complex. The result is then immediate from
Theorem

2.3
and
Proposition

2.7
. If
G
is only virtually torsion-free then we merely replace
G
by a finite-index torsion-free subgroup.
∎

Corollary

2.8
generalises the fact that fundamental groups of closed aspherical manifolds are Poincaré duality groups with trivial dualising module (noting that the reduced homology of the empty space is
\mathbb{Z}
in degree
-1
). The geometric criterion of
Corollary

2.8
leads to large classes of examples of (virtual) duality groups. In particular:

(1)

Arithmetic groups are virtual duality groups. One chooses for
M
the Borel–Serre bordification
[
3
]
of the associated symmetric space. The fact that the boundary has the homotopy type of a wedge of spheres is the Solomon–Tits theorem.

(2)

The mapping class group of an orientable surface is a virtual duality group
[
12
,
17
]
. One chooses for
M
the
thick part of Teichmüller space
(which will be discussed further shortly); one could alternatively use Harvey’s bordification of Teichmüller space. The fact that the boundary has the homotopy type of a wedge of spheres is the heart of Harer’s proof.

(3)

Fundamental groups of knot complements are duality groups (see Example 1 in Section VIII.9 of
[
5
]
: this also contains some helpful, more general, discussion). One chooses for
M
the universal cover of the complement of a tubular neighborhood of a knot in
S^{3}
. The fact that knot groups are duality groups can also be seen more directly.

Our proof that the handlebody mapping class group is a virtual duality group will likewise be an application of the above criterion.

3.
A combinatorial model of a classifying space for the handlebody group

3.1.
Teichmüller space and thick Teichmüller space

This material is covered in Chapter 10 of
[
10
]
. To expand on the notation given in the introduction, throughout
V
will denote a handlebody of genus
g
, with
b
marked discs on its boundary, and
p
marked points on its boundary, all of which are disjoint. We let
S
denote the complement of the interiors of the marked discs and points in
\partial V
. Hence
S
is an oriented surface of genus
g
with
b
boundary components, and
p
punctures. A simple closed curve on
S
is
essential
if it does not bound a disc in
S
, and
nonperipheral
if it does not bound a punctured disc or annulus in
S
.

We let
\mathrm{Mod}(S)
denote the mapping class group of self-diffeomorphisms of
S
fixing the punctures, and a collar neighborhood of the boundary, pointwise. We let
\mathrm{Mod}(V)
denote the
handlebody group
, i.e. the mapping class group of self-diffeomorphisms of
V
fixing the marked boundary discs and marked points.

Let
\mathcal{T}(S)
be the
Teichmüller space
of the surface
S
. This is the space of marked, complete, hyperbolic metrics on
S
, with totally geodesic boundary. It is diffeomorphic to an open ball of dimension
6g-6+3b+2p
. If
b=0
, then the group
\mathrm{Mod}(S)
acts properly on
\mathcal{T}(S)
, and the quotient of
\mathcal{T}(S)
by
\mathrm{Mod}(S)
is Riemann’s moduli space
\mathcal{M}_{g,p}
of compact Riemann surfaces of genus
g
with
p
marked points.

If
\mathbf{b}\in\mathbb{R}^{b}_{>0}
is a vector, then we denote by
\mathcal{T}(S;\mathbf{b})
the subspace of
\mathcal{T}(S)
where the hyperbolic lengths of the boundary components are exactly given by
\mathbf{b}
.

The group
\mathrm{Mod}(S)
does not act properly on
\mathcal{T}(S)
when
b>0
, since a Dehn twist around a boundary component acts trivially. To remedy this, we let
\mathcal{T}^{\mathrm{tw}}(S)
denote
\mathcal{T}(S)\times\mathbb{R}^{b}
, where we have informally speaking introduced an additional Fenchel–Nielsen twist parameter along each boundary component. More intrinsically, if
\mathcal{T}(S)
is the universal cover of the moduli space of hyperbolic surfaces of type
(g,b,p)
, then
\mathcal{T}^{\mathrm{tw}}(S)
is the universal cover of the moduli space which parametrises in addition the datum of a marked point on each boundary component. We define
\mathcal{T}^{\mathrm{tw}}(S;\mathbf{b})
similarly.

Fix a positive real number
\varepsilon
with
\varepsilon<\log(3+\sqrt{8})
. We write
\mathcal{T}^{\mathrm{tw}}_{\varepsilon}(S;\mathbf{b})
for the
\varepsilon
-thick Teichmüller space
, by which we mean the locus inside
\mathcal{T}^{\mathrm{tw}}(S;\mathbf{b})
parametrising hyperbolic metrics with the property that all essential, nonperipheral, closed geodesics on
S
have length at least
\varepsilon
. The space
\mathcal{T}^{\mathrm{tw}}(S;\mathbf{b})
admits a
\mathrm{Mod}(S)
-equivariant deformation retraction down to
\mathcal{T}^{\mathrm{tw}}_{\varepsilon}(S;\mathbf{b})

[
18
]
. The space
\mathcal{T}^{\mathrm{tw}}_{\varepsilon}(S;\mathbf{b})
is naturally a smooth manifold-with-corners of dimension
6g-6+3b+2p
, and
\mathrm{Mod}(S)
acts on it properly and cocompactly.

3.2.
Handlebody Teichmüller space

We now describe a classifying space for the handlebody group in terms of Teichmüller theory. The following construction was introduced by Hainaut and the first named author
[
11
]
. For background on handlebody mapping class groups, see
[
13
]
.

Fix a second real number
\varepsilon^{\prime}
with
\varepsilon<\varepsilon^{\prime}<\log(3+\sqrt{8})
, and an arbitrary length vector
\mathbf{b}\in\mathbb{R}^{b}_{>0}
. Let
\mathcal{Y}(V)\subset\mathcal{T}^{\mathrm{tw}}_{\varepsilon}(S;\mathbf{b})
denote the closed subspace of the
\varepsilon
-thick Teichmüller space parametrising hyperbolic metrics on
S
with the following property:

all essential, nonperipheral, closed geodesics on
S
of length at most
\varepsilon^{\prime}
bound discs in
V
, and the collection of these discs form a simple disc system in
V
.

Theorem 3.1

(Hainaut–Petersen)
.

The space
\mathcal{Y}(V)
is a contractible smooth oriented manifold-with-corners of dimension
6g-6+3b+2p
, and the group
\mathrm{Mod}(V)
acts properly and cocompactly on
\mathcal{Y}(V).

Theorem

3.1
is not stated explicitly in
[
11
]
, so let us comment on this. The fact that
\mathcal{Y}(V)
is a manifold-with-corners is an easy local verification in Fenchel–Nielsen coordinates (taking coordinates defined by a pants decomposition containing all short geodesics), once one knows the following
Lemma

3.2
. We refer to
[
11
, Section 2]
for more details.

Lemma 3.2
.

There is an open cover of
\,\mathcal{T}(S)
such that on each open set, there are only a finite number of simple closed curves on
S
for which the associated length function takes values
\leq\varepsilon^{\prime}
, and they are disjoint.

Proof.

This follows from the Collar Lemma and Wolpert’s Lemma. See the discussion following
[
11
, Theorem 2.3]
.
∎

What remains of
Theorem

3.1
is contractibility of
\mathcal{Y}(V)

The RGB poset

Definition 3.3

(The posets

•

Theorem 3.7

The map

Continuity of

Definition 3.8

Proposition 3.9
.

Let

(1)

The stratification of

admits a basis of open sets each of which meets only finitely many closures of strata.

(2)

f^{-1}(\mathsf{P}_{\leq p})
is contractible for all

.

(3)

Each inclusion

f^{-1}(\mathsf{P}_{<p})\hookrightarrow f^{-1}(\mathsf{P}_{\leq p})

is a cofibration.

Then

is weakly homotopy equivalent to the geometric realization

.

Proof.

Consider the diagram (i.e. functor)

given by

. Condition (1) implies that the colimit of this diagram is

. Indeed, this means precisely that

, which is the case for any locally finite closed covering of a topological space. Condition (2) tells us that every subspace

is contractible, so that the homotopy colimit of this diagram is

. Therefore it is enough to show that the colimit and homotopy colimit are weakly homotopy equivalent in this case.

Since every element of
\mathsf{P}
has finite height, the height function

endows

with the structure of a

in the sense of
[

, §5.1]
. This implies that the projective model structure on

exists, and has the property that

is cofibrant if and only if, for each

, the canonical map

is a cofibration

Theorem 3.10
.

\,\mathcal{Y}(V)

The proof is the same in both cases.
Local finiteness of the stratification is clear from

. Let us describe the closures of strata. Take

, and suppose that

cuts

into genus zero handlebodies

, each with some marked discs and points on its boundary. If

acts on the cartesian product

by shifting the twist parameters along each disc in

corresponds to two distinct marked boundary discs among the handlebodies

(resp.

(3)

To prove that $\mathbf{NS}(V)$ is $1$-connected, one only needs to study bad simplices of dimension $\leq 2$. If one wanted to push the argument further, then in genus $2$ one would need to consider both bad $2$-cells and bad edges, and in genus $1$ one would moreover need to consider bad vertices. Bad $2$-cells could be dealt with in the same manner in any genus, but bad edges and vertices seem like they would moreover require connectivity or simple connectivity of complexes of separating curves on a partitioned genus zero surface (cf.\ \cite[cite]{[\@@bibref{}{looijenga}{}{}]}). \end{rem}\par\par\par\bibstyle{alpha}\lx@bibliography{database}\par\par\par\par\par\par\par\@add@PDF@RDFa@triples\par\end{document}}}}}
