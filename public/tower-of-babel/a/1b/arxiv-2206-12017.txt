YITP-22-66 Splitting-merging transitions in tensor-vectors systems in exact large- N N limits
Naoki Sasakura

Abstract
Matrix models have phase transitions in which distributions of variables change topologically like the Gross-Witten-Wadia transition. In a recent study, similar splitting-merging behavior of distributions of dynamical variables was observed in a tensor-vectors system by numerical simulations. In this paper, we study the system exactly in some large- N N limits, in which the distributions are discrete sets of configurations rather than continuous. We find cascades of first-order phase transitions for fixed tensors, and first- and second-order phase transitions for random tensors, being characterized by breaking patterns of replica symmetries. The system is of interest across three different subjects at least: The splitting dynamics plays essential roles in emergence of classical spacetimes in a tensor model of quantum gravity; The splitting dynamics automatically detects the rank of a tensor in the tensor rank decomposition in data analysis; The system provides a variant of the spherical p p -spin model for spin glasses with a new non-trivial parameter. We discuss some implications of the results from these perspectives. The results are compared with some numerical simulations to check the large- N N convergence and the assumptions made in the analysis.

\displaystyle Z_{N,R}(\beta,C)=\int d\psi d\tilde{\psi}d\lambda d\tilde{Q}\,e^{-S_{\psi\tilde{Q}}(\beta,C)+i\sum_{i,j=1}^{R}\lambda_{ij}(\tilde{Q}_{ij}-\tilde{\psi}^{i}\cdot\tilde{\psi}^{j})},

C
, rather than for some particular values as studied in Section
3
.
Following the successes in the study of spin glasses, the main strategy we take is to apply the replica trick,

\displaystyle\bar{\beta}F=-\left.\frac{\partial}{\partial T}\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{NR}\log\left\langle Z_{N,R}(\beta,C)^{T}\right\rangle_{C}\right|_{T=0},

(23)

where
\bar{\beta}=\beta/N
,

\langle\cdot\rangle_{C}
denotes the average over the random distribution of
C
,
and
T
is the replica number. As in (
6
), we restrict the range of non-zero values of
C
as

C_{abc}\ (a,b,c\leq n)
, and assume them to be distributed by the normal distribution:

\displaystyle\langle{\cal O}\rangle_{C}=A\int\prod_{a\leq b\leq c=1}^{n}dC_{abc}\,{\cal O}\,e^{-\alpha C_{abc}C_{abc}},

(24)

where
\alpha
is a positive number, and
A
is a normalization factor for
\langle 1\rangle_{C}=1
.

Let us first rewrite the power
T
in (
23
) by introducing
T
replicas of
\phi_{a}^{i}
:

\displaystyle\left\langle Z_{N,R}(\beta,C)^{T}\right\rangle_{C}=A\int dC\,e^{-\alpha C^{2}}\,Z_{N,R}(\beta,C)^{T}=A\int dC\int_{I^{T}}\prod_{t=0}^{T-1}\prod_{a,i=1}^{N,R}d\phi^{it}_{a}\,e^{-S_{T}},

(25)

where

\displaystyle\begin{split}S_{T}=\alpha\,C_{abc}C_{abc}+\beta\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\left(C_{abc}-\sum_{i=1}^{R}\phi_{a}^{it}\phi_{b}^{it}\phi_{c}^{it}\right)^{2},\end{split}

(26)

and we have introduced an additional upper index
t\ (t=0,1,\ldots,T-1)
for
\phi
, which starts from zero
for later convenience.
With the same spirit as (
7
), we divide
\phi
into two parts,

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\psi_{a}^{it}=\phi_{a}^{it}\ (a=1,2,\ldots,n),\\
&\tilde{\psi}_{a}^{it}=\phi_{a}^{it}\ (a=n+1,n+2,\ldots,N).\end{split}

(27)

After a straightforward computation, we obtain

\displaystyle\begin{split}S_{T}&=(\alpha+\beta T)\left(C_{abc}-\frac{\beta}{\alpha+\beta T}\sum_{i,t}\psi_{a}^{it}\psi_{b}^{it}\psi_{b}^{it}\right)^{2}-\frac{\beta^{2}}{\alpha+\beta T}\sum_{i,i^{\prime},t,t^{\prime}}\left(\psi^{it}\cdot\psi^{i^{\prime}t^{\prime}}\right)^{3}\\
&+\beta\sum_{i,i^{\prime},t}\left(\psi^{it}\cdot\psi^{i^{\prime}t}\right)^{3}+3\beta\sum_{i,i^{\prime},t}\left(\psi^{it}\cdot\psi^{i^{\prime}t}\right)^{2}\left(\tilde{\psi}^{it}\cdot\tilde{\psi}^{i^{\prime}t}\right)\\
&+3\beta\sum_{i,i^{\prime},t}\left(\psi^{it}\cdot\psi^{i^{\prime}t}\right)\left(\tilde{\psi}^{it}\cdot\tilde{\psi}^{i^{\prime}t}\right)^{2}+\beta\sum_{i,i^{\prime},t}\left(\tilde{\psi}^{it}\cdot\tilde{\psi}^{i^{\prime}t}\right)^{3},\end{split}

(28)

where the ranges of sums have been omitted for brevity, since they are obvious.

The first term of (
28
) can be integrated over
C
, and this generates

\displaystyle\frac{\#C}{2}\log\left(\frac{\alpha+\beta T}{\alpha}\right)

(29)

as an additional term to the exponent, where
\#C=n(n+1)(n+2)/6
, namely, the number of independent elements of
C
.
To compute the other terms, let us introduce the overlaps,

\displaystyle\begin{split}&Q_{iti^{\prime}t^{\prime}}=\psi^{it}\cdot\psi^{i^{\prime}t^{\prime}},\\
&\tilde{Q}_{iti^{\prime}t^{\prime}}=\tilde{\psi}^{it}\cdot\tilde{\psi}^{i^{\prime}t^{\prime}},\end{split}

(30)

as in (
9
). The same procedure as before generates similar logarithmic terms as in (
12
),

\displaystyle-\frac{n}{2}\log\det Q-\frac{N-n}{2}\log\det\tilde{Q},

(31)

where
Q
and
\tilde{Q}
are regarded as
RT\times RT
matrices in the determinants.
By assembling the above expressions, we obtain

\displaystyle\begin{split}S_{T}^{\rm eff}(\beta)=&\frac{\#C}{2}\log\left(1+\frac{\beta}{\alpha}T\right)-\frac{\beta^{2}}{\alpha+\beta T}\sum_{i,i^{\prime},t,t^{\prime}}\left(Q^{iti^{\prime}t^{\prime}}\right)^{3}+\beta\sum_{i,i^{\prime},t}\left(Q^{iti^{\prime}t}\right)^{3}\\
&+3\beta\sum_{i,i^{\prime},t}\left(Q^{iti^{\prime}t}\right)^{2}\left(\tilde{Q}^{iti^{\prime}t}\right)+3\beta\sum_{i,i^{\prime},t}\left(Q^{iti^{\prime}t}\right)\left(\tilde{Q}^{iti^{\prime}t}\right)^{2}+\beta\sum_{i,i^{\prime},t}\left(\tilde{Q}^{iti^{\prime}t}\right)^{3}\\
&-\frac{n}{2}\log\det Q-\frac{N-n}{2}\log\det\tilde{Q}.\end{split}

(32)

For later use, it is more convenient to perform a rescaling of variables in (
32
):

Q=(N/\beta)^{1/3}\bar{Q},\ \tilde{Q}=(N/\beta)^{1/3}\bar{\tilde{Q}}
. Then we obtain

\displaystyle\begin{split}S_{T}^{\rm eff}(\beta)=&\frac{\#C}{2}\log\left(1+\frac{\beta}{\alpha}T\right)+\frac{NRT}{6}\log\beta-\frac{N\beta}{\alpha+\beta T}\sum_{i,i^{\prime},t,t^{\prime}}\left(\bar{Q}^{iti^{\prime}t^{\prime}}\right)^{3}+N\sum_{i,i^{\prime},t}\left(\bar{Q}^{iti^{\prime}t}\right)^{3}\\
&+3N\sum_{i,i^{\prime},t}\left(\bar{Q}^{iti^{\prime}t}\right)^{2}\left(\bar{\tilde{Q}}^{iti^{\prime}t}\right)+3N\sum_{i,i^{\prime},t}\left(\bar{Q}^{iti^{\prime}t}\right)\left(\bar{\tilde{Q}}^{iti^{\prime}t}\right)^{2}+N\sum_{i,i^{\prime},t}\left(\bar{\tilde{Q}}^{iti^{\prime}t}\right)^{3}\\
&-\frac{n}{2}\log\det\bar{Q}-\frac{N-n}{2}\log\det\bar{\tilde{Q}},\end{split}

(33)

where we have ignored an unimportant constant shift.

Further computations depend on assumptions made on replica symmetry breaking. Below
we consider only two possibilities, replica symmetric (RS) and one-step replica symmetry breaking (1RSB).
A reason to consider only these possibilities is that 1RSB is known to be exact
[
21
]
in the spherical

p
-spin model. In fact numerical simulations, which we will show in Section
5
,
seem to support this assumption at least in the large-
N
limit
of this paper.

4.1
R=1
with random
C

Since
R=1
, we can simply write
\bar{Q}^{tt^{\prime}}
in place of
\bar{Q}^{iti^{\prime}t^{\prime}}
, neglecting the real replica index.
Under the assumption of 1RSB
[
21
,
22
]
, the overlap
\bar{Q}
is assumed to have the form,

\displaystyle\bar{Q}^{tt^{\prime}}=\delta_{\lfloor t/M\rfloor,\lfloor t^{\prime}/M\rfloor}I_{t\bmod M,t^{\prime}\bmod M}(q_{0},q_{1})+q_{2}(1-\delta_{\lfloor t/M\rfloor,\lfloor t^{\prime}/M\rfloor}),

(34)

where
q_{i}
are new variables,
\lfloor\cdot\rfloor
is the floor function,
a\bmod b
denotes
a
modulo
b
,
and
I
is an
M\times M
matrix with components,

\displaystyle I_{tt^{\prime}}(q_{0},q_{1})=q_{0}\,\delta_{tt^{\prime}}+q_{1}(1-\delta_{tt^{\prime}}).

(35)

Then it is straightforward to obtain

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t,t^{\prime}}(\bar{Q}^{tt^{\prime}})^{3}=(T-M)q_{2}^{3}+(M-1)q_{1}^{3}+q_{0}^{3},\\
&\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t}(\bar{Q}^{tt})^{3}=q_{0}^{3},\\
&\log\det\bar{Q}=\log\left(q_{0}+(M-1)q_{1}+(T-M)q_{2}\right)+\left(\frac{T}{M}-1\right)\log\left(q_{0}-q_{1}+M(q_{1}-q_{2})\right)\\
&\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ +\left(T-\frac{T}{M}\right)\log(q_{0}-q_{1}).\end{split}

(36)

These expressions are also assumed for
\bar{\tilde{Q}}
,
with replacements
q_{i}\rightarrow\tilde{q}_{i},\ M\rightarrow\tilde{M}
.

By putting (
36
) and the corresponding expressions of
\bar{\tilde{Q}}
to (
33
),
and applying the replica trick formula in (
23
), one obtains

\displaystyle\begin{split}\bar{\beta}&F^{1RSB}=\frac{\#C}{2N}\frac{\beta}{\alpha}+\frac{1}{6}\log\beta-\frac{\beta}{\alpha}\left(-Mq_{2}^{3}+(M-1)q_{1}^{3}+q_{0}^{3}\right)+q_{0}^{3}+3q_{0}^{2}\tilde{q}_{0}+3q_{0}\tilde{q}_{0}^{2}+\tilde{q}_{0}^{3}\\
&-\frac{\bar{n}}{2}\left(\left(1-\frac{1}{M}\right)\log(q_{0}-q_{1})+\frac{1}{M}\log(q_{0}-q_{1}+M(q_{1}-q_{2}))+\frac{q_{2}}{q_{0}+(M-1)q_{1}-Mq_{2}}\right)\\
&-\frac{1-\bar{n}}{2}\left(\left(1-\frac{1}{\tilde{M}}\right)\log(\tilde{q}_{0}-\tilde{q}_{1})+\frac{1}{\tilde{M}}\log(\tilde{q}_{0}-\tilde{q}_{1}+\tilde{M}(\tilde{q}_{1}-\tilde{q}_{2}))+\frac{\tilde{q}_{2}}{\tilde{q}_{0}+(\tilde{M}-1)\tilde{q}_{1}-\tilde{M}\tilde{q}_{2}}\right),\end{split}

(37)

where we have introduced
\bar{n}=n/N
.

Here let us comment on the ranges of the parameters which appear in the above expressions
(See
[
22
]
for more details).
From the physical point of view, the overlaps of configurations in the same state will not be less than
those in different states. Therefore

\displaystyle q_{0}\geq q_{1}\geq q_{2},\ \tilde{q}_{0}\geq\tilde{q}_{1}\geq\tilde{q}_{2}.

(38)

In addition, the computations of

\langle\langle\psi_{a_{1}}\cdots\psi_{a_{k}}\rangle\langle\psi_{a_{1}}\cdots\psi_{a_{k}}\rangle\rangle_{C}

under the 1RSB assumption (and the same for
\tilde{\psi}
)
leads to a probability distribution of the overlap,

\displaystyle\langle P(q)\rangle_{C}=\frac{M-1}{T-1}\delta(q-q_{1})+\frac{T-M}{T-1}\delta(q-q_{2}),

(39)

and a similar one for
\tilde{q}_{i}
.
Then the positivity of this probability distribution in the
T\rightarrow 0
limit requires

\displaystyle 0\leq M\leq 1,\ 0\leq\tilde{M}\leq 1.

(40)

One can check that (
38
) and (
40
) assures the arguments of the logarithms and
the denominators in (
37
) do not become less than zero.

For simplicity of discussions, in the rest of this paper, we assume
q_{2},\tilde{q}_{2}=0
, since this is known to be
true for the
p
-spin spherical model
[
21
]
. The consistency of our analysis of this section and
the numerical results of Section
5
will support this simplification.

When
\beta
is small enough, namely, in high temperatures, the system will be in RS phase.
The RS expression of the free energy can be obtained by putting
q_{1}=\tilde{q}_{1}=0
(and
q_{2}=\tilde{q}_{2}=0
)
to the 1RSB expression (
37
):

\displaystyle\begin{split}\bar{\beta}F^{RS}=&\frac{\#C}{2N}\frac{\beta}{\alpha}+\frac{1}{6}\log\beta+\left(1-\frac{\beta}{\alpha}\right)q_{0}^{3}+3q_{0}^{2}\tilde{q}_{0}+3q_{0}\tilde{q}_{0}^{2}+\tilde{q}_{0}^{3}-\frac{\bar{n}}{2}\log q_{0}-\frac{1-\bar{n}}{2}\log\tilde{q}_{0}.\end{split}

(41)

Note that
M,\tilde{M}
have disappeared from the expression, since they have no roles
for
q_{1}=\tilde{q}_{1}=q_{2}=\tilde{q}_{2}=0
in (
34
).

The free energy of the system is obtained by searching for the stationary points of these expressions
(
37
) and (
41
) in terms of the variables,
q_{i},\tilde{q}_{i},M,\tilde{M}
.

In this subsection, let us perform concrete analysis

and

\displaystyle\begin{split}\bar{\beta}F^{1RSB}_{R=1\,\bar{n}=1}=&\frac{\#C}{2N}\frac{\beta}{\alpha}+\frac{1}{6}\log\beta-\frac{\beta}{\alpha}\left((M-1)q_{1}^{3}+q_{0}^{3}\right)+q_{0}^{3}\\

,

\displaystyle\bar{\beta}F^{RS}_{R=1\,\bar{n}=1}=\frac{\#C}{2N}\frac{\beta}{\alpha}+\frac{1}{6}\log\beta+\frac{1}{6}+\frac{1}{6}\log 6+\frac{1}{6}\log\left(1-\frac{\beta}{\alpha}\right).

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\bar{\beta}F^{1RSB}_{R=1\,\bar{n}=1}}{\partial q_{0}}=\frac{\partial\bar{\beta}F^{1RSB}_{R=1\,\bar{n}=1}}{\partial q_{1}}=\frac{\partial\bar{\beta}F^{1RSB}_{R=1\,\bar{n}=1}}{\partial M}=0.

Then the last equation can be numerically solved to obtain

In this case, we have
\bar{\tilde{Q}}

6
Summary and discussions

In matrix models, there are phase transitions in which distributions of dynamical variables change topologically,
like in Gross-Witten-Wadia transition
[
11
,
12
]

and in the transitions among the large-
N
limit multi-cut solutions
[
13
]
.
In a recent study
[
10
]
,
similar splitting-merging behavior of dynamical variables
was observed in a tensor-vectors system by numerical simulations, but
the results were not convincing enough to characterize it and conclude whether this is a phase transition
or just a crossover.
In this paper, we have studied the system exactly in some large-
N
limits,
and have found cascades of first-order phase transitions for fixed tensors, and a second- or first- order phase transition
for random tensors, applying the replica trick for the random cases.
These phases can be distinguished by breaking patterns of real replica symmetries
for fixed tensors, and those of replica symmetries for random tensors, respectively.
We have also performed some numerical simulations to compare with the exact results:
We have found consistent results, which support the assumptions made in the derivation of the exact results;
We have also found rather slow convergence of numerical data toward sharp transitions of the exact results,
which implies the necessity of our cautious attitude toward numerical simulations of our system in future study.

As will be mentioned in the final paragraph, the large-
N
limit of this paper is different from that in the tensor model.
However, the results of this paper suggest the following interesting possibilities.
The presence of the sharp phase transitions implies that what we called the quantum and classical phases in
the previous study
[
10
]
of the tensor model wave function could actually be different phases.
This means that classical spacetimes could emerge through phase transitions in the tensor model.
A new finding of this paper is that the transition rather consists of multiple first-order phase transitions.
Therefore the classical phase, where there emerge classical spacetimes,
could not be just a single phase but rather a collection of phases.
This aspect can explicitly be seen in the
SO(2)
-invariant example given in Section
3.3
:
We see that the number of discrete points forming
S^{1}
increases one by one as the system undergoes
the phase transitions, as in Figure
5
. It would be straightforward to check similar matters in more
general cases
S^{n}
with
SO(n+1)
symmetry
[
10
]
,
and it would also be more interesting to consider general spacetimes like black holes in future study,
using the general procedure of constructing tensors
corresponding to geometries developed in
[
30
,
10
]
.

Here we would like to stress that the phase transitions in the previous paragraph are all first-order
and should be linked solely to the emergence of classical spacetimes, which should not be mixed with
emergence of continuum physics in them. The latter is a different thing which could be checked by
analyzing fluctuation modes around the emergent classical spacetimes.

Another potential application of our system is to the tensor rank decomposition
[
14
,
15
,
16
,
17
]

in data analysis.
This is an important technique to extract information from tensors of real-life data in data analysis,
but an efficient procedure is yet to be established because of its hardness
[
19
]
.
An interesting insight from our model is that the problems should be common across
the tensor rank decomposition and the spin glasses, since our model can be seen bilaterally as
a cost function of the former and a generalization of the spherical
p
-spin model for the latter.
In this sense, this paper can be seen as a single instance of using a technique developed
in spin glass theory for a problem in data analysis (See also
[
18
]
).
Though restricted to the large-
N
limit with finite
R
in this paper,
the confirmation of the splitting phenomenon (
4
) as a result of phase transitions seems meaningful
for the tensor rank decomposition, since the splitting gives a tensor rank decomposition with
an automatically determined rank for an arbitrarily given tensor.
Though this phenomenon cannot be used immediately for the actual process of the tensor rank
decomposition due to its inefficiency,
studying our model and the phenomenon more deeply could lead to new useful procedures in the future.

As argued in Section
1
, our system (
1
) with random
C
can be considered to be
a variant of the spherical
p
-spin model with a new non-trivial parameter
R
.
In this paper’s limit of large-
N
with finite
R
, however, the RS/1RSB analyses have reduced to
the same as
R=1

in Sections

standard one, we have found large fluctuations of the overlaps between

This fact would suggest that, for our system to be properly treated in mean field theory, we would need to
consider many numbers of

open new possibilities.

In fact the large-

If it were continuous, the dynamics would be more interesting, like topological transitions

This disadvantage could be dealt with by simultaneously taking the large

Appendix Appendix A
Rank of
SO(2)
invariant
C

A fact used in Section
3.3
is that the rank of
SO(2)
invariant
C
of
n=3
is given by four.
This is a special case of the following general formula:

\displaystyle\hbox{Rank}(C^{SO(2)})=\frac{3n-1}{2}.

(54)

This statement is equivalent to that
r=(3n-1)/2
is the minimum number
for the following summation expression of the integral to hold:

\displaystyle\begin{split}\int_{0}^{2\pi}d\theta f_{a}f_{b}f_{c}=\sum_{j=1}^{r}A_{a}^{j}A_{b}^{j}A_{c}^{j},\end{split}

(55)

where

\displaystyle\{f_{a}|a=1,2,\ldots,2\Lambda+1\}=\{\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}},\cos\theta,\sin\theta,\cos 2\theta,\cdots,\sin(\Lambda\theta)\},

(56)

with
n=2\Lambda+1
.

To prove (
55
) let us recall what is called quadrature
[
34
]
.
Quadrature is an approximation to an integral in terms of a summation, which however gives exact values if the integrand is
contained in a particular set of functions.
In the present case, we want a quadrature,

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{2\pi}d\theta\ g(\theta)=\sum_{j=1}^{r}w_{j}\,g(\theta_{j}),

(57)

where
g(\theta)
is contained in a certain set of periodic functions of period
2\pi
,
\theta_{j}
are quadrature nodes,
and
w_{j}
are quadrature weights. Let us consider the set of
g(\theta)
to be given by

\displaystyle\{1,\cos\theta,\sin\theta,\cos 2\theta,\ldots,\sin M\theta\}.

(58)

Then it is straightforward to show that
the nodes can be taken as

\displaystyle\theta_{j}=\frac{2\pi j}{M+1},\ (j=0,1,\ldots,M),

(59)

with weights
w_{j}=2\pi/(M+1)
, because

\displaystyle\frac{2\pi}{M+1}\sum_{j=0}^{M}e^{i\frac{2\pi kj}{M+1}}=\delta_{k,0}=\int_{0}^{2\pi}d\theta e^{ik\theta},

(60)

for integer
0\leq k\leq M
.
Here the number of nodes,
r=M+1
, is the least, because
M+1
independent conditions must be satisfied.

In our present case,
g
is given by a product of three functions in (
56
). Therefore
M=3\Lambda
, and
hence
r=M+1=(3n-1)/2
, which indeed agrees with (
54
).
By applying (
57
) to (
55
), we obtain

\displaystyle A_{a}^{j}=\left(\frac{2\pi}{3\Lambda+1}\right)^{\frac{1}{3}}f_{a}(\theta_{j}).

(61)

Appendix Appendix B
Details of the derivation of (
49
)

In this appendix, we show some details of the derivation of the free energy (
49
).

From the expression (
48
), we find

\displaystyle\begin{split}\sum_{ii^{\prime}tt^{\prime}}(\bar{Q}^{iti^{\prime}t^{\prime}})^{3}&=R\sum_{tt^{\prime}}(\bar{Q}^{1t1t^{\prime}})^{3}+R(R-1)\sum_{tt^{\prime}}(\bar{Q}^{1t2t^{\prime}})^{3}\\
&=RT(q_{0}^{3}+(M-1)q_{1}^{3})+R(R-1)T((q_{0}^{\prime})^{3}+(M-1)(q_{1}^{\prime})^{3}).\end{split}

(62)

We also obtain

\displaystyle\begin{split}\sum_{ii^{\prime}t}(\bar{Q}^{iti^{\prime}t})^{3}&=R\sum_{t}(\bar{Q}^{1t1t})^{3}+R(R-1)\sum_{t}(\bar{Q}^{1t2t})^{3}\\
&=RTq_{0}^{3}+R(R-1)T(q_{0}^{\prime})^{3}.\end{split}

(63)

Computation of
\det(\bar{Q})
is also straightforward but a little involved. First we note that
\bar{Q}
has the form,

\displaystyle\bar{Q}^{iti^{\prime}t^{\prime}}=A_{tt^{\prime}}\delta_{ii^{\prime}}+A^{\prime}_{tt^{\prime}}(1-\delta_{ii^{\prime}}),

(64)

where

\displaystyle A_{tt^{\prime}}=\delta_{\lfloor t/M\rfloor,\lfloor t^{\prime}/M\rfloor}I_{t\bmod M,t^{\prime}\bmod M}(q_{0},q_{1}),

(65)

and similarly for
A^{\prime}
with
q_{0}^{\prime}
and
q_{1}^{\prime}
. Concerning the indices
i,i^{\prime}
,

\bar{Q}
in (
64
) has the “eigenvalues”,
A+(R-1)A^{\prime}

and
A-A^{\prime}
with degeneracies
1
and
R-1
, respectively. On the other hand,
A
has the eigenvalues,
q_{0}+(M-1)q_{1}

and
q_{0}-q_{1}
with degeneracies
T/M
and
(M-1)T/M
, respectively. Combining these, we obtain the following
list of eigenvalues of
\bar{Q}
:

\displaystyle\begin{split}&q_{0}+(R-1)q_{0}^{\prime}+(M-1)(q_{1}+(R-1)q_{1}^{\prime}),\ \ \hbox{deg}=T/M,\\
&q_{0}+(R-1)q_{0}^{\prime}-(q_{1}+(R-1)q_{1}^{\prime}),\ \ \hbox{deg}=(M-1)T/M,\\
&q_{0}-q_{0}^{\prime}+(M-1)(q_{1}-q_{1}^{\prime}),\ \ \hbox{deg}=(R-1)T/M,\\
&q_{0}-q_{0}^{\prime}-(q_{1}-q_{1}^{\prime}),\ \ \hbox{deg}=(M-1)(R-1)T/M.\end{split}

(66)

This determines the terms coming from

.
