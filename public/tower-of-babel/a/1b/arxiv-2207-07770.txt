Abstract
. We prove that several Feigin-Odesskii Poisson brackets associated with normal elliptic curves in ℙ n {\mathbb{P}}^{n} are compatible if and only if they are contained in a scroll or in a Veronese surface in ℙ 5 {\mathbb{P}}^{5} (with an exception of one case when n = 3 n=3 ). In the case n = 3 n=3 we determine the quartic corresponding to the Schouten bracket of two (non-compatible) Poisson brackets associated with normal elliptic curves E 1 E_{1} and E 2 E_{2} .



1.
Introduction

We work over an algebraically closed field
\mathbf{k}
of characteristic
0
.

This work is a contribution to the study of a remarkable class of Poisson brackets on projective
spaces associated with elliptic curves, that were introduced by Feigin and Odesskii (see
[
3
]
).
Namely, for every normal elliptic curve
E\subset{\mathbb{P}}^{n}
, there is an associated Poisson bracket
\Pi_{E}

defined up to rescaling (we refer to these as FO brackets; sometimes they are denoted as
q_{n+1,1}(E)
).
Setting
L={\mathcal{O}}(1)|_{E}
one can identify
{\mathbb{P}}^{n}

with the projective space of extensions of
L
by
{\mathcal{O}}
. Then the bracket
\Pi_{E}
can be defined and studied in
terms of geometry of such extensions on
E
.

Recall that two Poisson structures
\Pi_{1},\Pi_{2}
on the same space
X
are called compatible if every linear combination
\lambda_{1}\Pi_{1}+\lambda_{2}\Pi_{2}
is still a Poisson
structure. This is equivalent to the identity
[\Pi_{1},\Pi_{2}]=0
, where we use the Schouten bracket of bivectors. More generally, one can consider larger linear subspaces of Poisson bivectors.
In the work
[
7
]
Odesskii and Wolf gave a construction of
9
-dimensional subspaces of Poisson structures on
{\mathbb{P}}^{n}
whose general member is a bracket
\Pi_{E}
for some
E\subset{\mathbb{P}}^{n}
.
In
[
5
]
this construction was interpreted geometrically in terms of families of anticanonical divisors on Hirzebruch surfaces, and extended to give new examples of compatible
Poisson structures. In the present work we show that these constructions are the only way to produce compatible FO brackets of type
q_{n+1,1}(E)
, with one exception occurring for
n=3
.

First, let us consider the case of FO brackets associated with normal elliptic curves in
{\mathbb{P}}^{3}
.

Theorem A
.

For a collection of normal elliptic curves
(E_{i})_{i\in I}
in
{\mathbb{P}}^{3}={\mathbb{P}}V
, the brackets
(\Pi_{E_{i}})
are compatible if and only if either all
E_{i}
lie on one quadric surface (possibly singular),
or there exists a
3
-dimensional subspace
W\subset S^{2}V^{*}
such that every
E_{i}
is the intersection of two quadrics in
W
.

Theorem A has a natural generalization to FO brackets in higher-dimensional projective spaces.
Let us fix
n\geq 4
. With every normal elliptic curve
E
in
{\mathbb{P}}^{n}
we can associate a
1
-dimensional family
{\mathcal{S}}_{E}
of rational normal scrolls
S(r,r)\subset{\mathbb{P}}^{n}
if
n=2r+1
,
with several exceptional members of type
S(r-1,r+1)
(resp.,
S(r-1,r)
if
n=2r
), which are parametrized by points of
\operatorname{Pic}_{2}(E)
, see Sec.
2.2
for details.
In the case of
n=5
, each normal elliptic curve
E
in
{\mathbb{P}}^{5}
is contained in four Veronese surfaces, corresponding to choices of a square root of the line bundle
{\mathcal{O}}(1)|_{E}
of degree
6
.

Theorem B
.

For a collection of normal elliptic curves
(E_{i})_{i\in I}
in

{\mathbb{P}}^{n}
, where
n\geq 4
, the brackets
(\Pi_{E_{i}})

are compatible if and only if

•

either the corresponding families
({\mathcal{S}}_{E_{i}})
have an element in common,

•

or
n=5
and all
E_{i}
are contained in a Veronese surface
{\mathbb{P}}^{2}\subset{\mathbb{P}}^{5}
.

The family of elliptic curves contained in a scroll is precisely the family of anticanonical divisors producing compatible Poisson brackets of Odesskii-Wolf
(see
[
5
, Sec. 5.3]
).
The fact that elliptic curves contained in a Veronese surface in
{\mathbb{P}}^{5}
give rise to compatible Poisson brackets was observed in
[
5
, Ex. 4.6]
.

Let us call an
FO-subspace
any linear subspace of compatible Poisson brackets on
{\mathbb{P}}^{n}
, whose generic point is of the form
\Pi_{E}
for some normal elliptic curve
E
.
Odesskii-Wolf conjectured that their examples of
9
compatible Poisson brackets on
{\mathbb{P}}^{n}
give maximal linear subspaces of Poisson brackets.
We prove that this is true if
n
is even, while for odd
n
we prove maximality of these subspaces among FO-subspaces.

Corollary C
.

(a) Given a rational normal scroll
S=S(s,r)
in
{\mathbb{P}}^{n}
, with
0<s\leq r\leq s+2
, consider the
9
-dimensional FO-subspace
V_{S}
of Poisson brackets on
{\mathbb{P}}^{n}
coming from anticanonical
divisors on
S
. Then
V_{S}
is a maximal subspace of Poisson brackets provided
n
is even (resp., a maximal FO-subspace if
n
is odd).

(b) The maximal dimension of a linear subspace of Poisson brackets on
{\mathbb{P}}^{n}
, containing some FO bracket
\Pi_{E}
, is
9
provided
n
is even and
n\geq 4
.
For odd
n
, the maximal dimension of an FO-subspace of Poisson brackets on
{\mathbb{P}}^{n}

is
9
, for
n\geq 3
,
n\neq 5
, and is
10
for
n=5
.

The construction of compatible Poisson brackets in
[
5
]
also produces some families of compatible FO brackets of type
q_{n,k}(E)
(these are associated with
higher rank stable bundles on elliptic curves). It would be interesting to find criteria for compatibility of brackets of type
q_{n,k}(E)
similar to our Theorems A and B.

Let us return to FO brackets on
{\mathbb{P}}^{3}
.
In the case when
\Pi_{E_{1}}
and
\Pi_{E_{2}}
are not compatible, the Schouten bracket
[\Pi_{E_{1}},\Pi_{E_{2}}]

is a section of
{\bigwedge}^{3}T_{{\mathbb{P}}^{3}}\simeq{\mathcal{O}}_{{\mathbb{P}}^{3}}(4)
, so it gives a quartic hypersurface in
{\mathbb{P}}^{3}
. We will compute this hypersurface in terms of defining pairs of quadrics for
E_{1}
and
E_{2}
.
Let
{\mathbb{P}}^{3}={\mathbb{P}}V
, where
V
is a
4
-dimensional vector space. Consider a natural linear map

\Phi:{\bigwedge}^{4}(S^{2}V^{*})\to\det(V^{*})\otimes S^{4}(V^{*})

that associates with
Q_{1}\wedge Q_{2}\wedge Q_{3}\wedge Q_{4}
the polynomial map of degree
4
with values in
\det(V^{*})
,

v\mapsto(\partial_{v}Q_{1})\wedge(\partial_{v}Q_{2})\wedge(\partial_{v}Q_{3})\wedge(\partial_{v}Q_{4}).

On the other hand, the Poisson bivector associated with
Q_{1}=Q_{2}=0
canonically is an element of
\det(V^{*})\otimes H^{0}({\mathbb{P}}V,{\bigwedge}^{2}T_{{\mathbb{P}}V})
,
hence the Schouten bracket of two such bivectors is a section of

(\det V^{*})^{\otimes 2}\otimes{\bigwedge}^{3}T_{{\mathbb{P}}^{3}}\simeq(\det V^{*})^{\otimes 2}\otimes\det(V)\otimes{\mathcal{O}}_{{\mathbb{P}}V}(4)\simeq\det(V^{*})\otimes{\mathcal{O}}_{{\mathbb{P}}V}(4).

Theorem D
.

One has an equality in
\det(V^{*})\otimes S^{4}(V^{*})
,

[\Pi_{Q_{1}=Q_{2}=0},\Pi_{Q_{3}=Q_{4}=0}]=4\cdot\Phi(Q_{1}\wedge Q_{2}\wedge Q_{3}\wedge Q_{4}).

The quartic surface
X_{E_{1},E_{2}}\subset{\mathbb{P}}^{3}
given by
[\Pi_{E_{1}},\Pi_{E_{2}}]=0
can be characterized geometrically as follows.
First, with a normal elliptic curve
E\subset{\mathbb{P}}^{3}
and a point
p\in{\mathbb{P}}^{3}
, which is not the vertex of one of the four singular quadrics through
E
, we can associate a line
L_{E}(p)\subset{\mathbb{P}}^{3}
called the
polar
line of
p
with respect to
E
: if
E
is given by
Q_{1}=Q_{2}=0
, then
L_{E}(p)
is the intersection of polar planes to
p
with respect to
Q_{1}=0
and
Q_{2}=0
(it does not
depend on a choice of
Q_{1},Q_{2}
). Now
X_{E_{1},E_{2}}
is the closure of the set of points
p
such that the polars
L_{E_{1}}(p)
and
L_{E_{2}}(p)
have a nontrivial
intersection (this quartic also contains vertices of singular quadrics through
E_{1}
and
E_{2}
).
Another definition of the polar line to a point
p
with respect to
E
(for a generic
p
) is the following. Take two secant lines to
E
,
L_{1}=\overline{p_{1}q_{1}}
and
L_{2}=\overline{p_{2}q_{2}}
, passing through
p
(they coincide with two lines through
p
on the unique quadric
Q
passing through
E
and
p
). On each of these two lines
L_{i}
, choose a point
r_{i}
, such that

(p,p_{i},q_{i},r_{i})
is a harmonic quadruple (have double ratio
-1
). Then the polar line
L_{E}(p)
is the line passing through
r_{1}
and
r_{2}
.

2.
Some facts about FO brackets

2.1.
Conormal Lie algebras

Recall that for any point
x
of a smooth Poisson variety
(X,\Pi)
, there is a natural Lie algebra structure on the space

{\mathfrak{g}}_{\Pi,x}:=\operatorname{ker}(\Pi_{x}:T_{x}^{*}X\to T_{x}X)\subset T_{x}^{*}X.

Namely, if we lift
a,b\in{\mathfrak{g}}_{x}\subset{\mathfrak{m}}_{x}/{\mathfrak{m}}_{x}^{2}
to some local functions
\widetilde{a},\widetilde{b}\in{\mathfrak{m}}_{x}
, then

\{\widetilde{a},\widetilde{b}\}\in{\mathfrak{m}}_{x}
and

[a,b]:=\{\widetilde{a},\widetilde{b}\}\operatorname{mod}{\mathfrak{m}}_{x}^{2}.

Now let
\Pi=\Pi_{E}
denote the FO bracket on
{\mathbb{P}}^{n}={\mathbb{P}}\operatorname{Ext}^{1}(L,{\mathcal{O}})
, associated with a normal elliptic curve
E\subset{\mathbb{P}}^{n}
(so
L={\mathcal{O}}(1)|_{E}
).
It is known (see
[
5
, Prop. 2.3]
) that the rank of
\Pi_{x}
is equal to
n+1-\dim\operatorname{End}(V)
, where
V
is the extension of
L
by
{\mathcal{O}}
corresponding to
x
.
The next result realizes this equality by an isomorphism of natural Lie algebras.

Proposition 2.1.1
.

For
x\in\operatorname{Ext}^{1}(L,{\mathcal{O}})\setminus\{0\}
, which is the class of an extension

0\to{\mathcal{O}}\rTo{i}V\rTo{p}L\to 0,

(2.1)

the conormal Lie algebra
{\mathfrak{g}}_{\Pi,x}
for the FO-bracket
\Pi=\Pi_{E}
is naturally isomorphic to the Lie algebra
\operatorname{End}(V)/\langle\operatorname{id}\rangle
.

Proof.

Recall (see
[
5
, Lem. 2.1]
) that for
x\in\operatorname{Ext}^{1}(L,{\mathcal{O}})\setminus\{0\}
and
s_{1},s_{2}\in\langle x\rangle^{\perp}\subset H^{0}(L)
, the bivector
\Pi_{x}
is given by

s_{1}\wedge s_{2}\mapsto\langle MP(s_{1},x,s_{2}),x\rangle,

where
MP(s_{1},x,s_{2})\in H^{0}(L)/\langle s_{1},s_{2}\rangle
is the Massey product.
Note that we can identify
\langle x\rangle^{\perp}\subset H^{0}(L)
with the kernel of the connecting homomorphism
H^{0}(L)\to H^{1}({\mathcal{O}})
associated with (
2.1
),
hence,
\langle x\rangle^{\perp}
consists of sections liftable to
H^{0}(V)
.
By definition,
MP(s_{1},x,s_{2})
is the
\langle s_{1},s_{2}\rangle
-coset represented by
\widetilde{s}_{1}(\widetilde{s}_{2})
, where

\widetilde{s}_{1}:V\to L
is a map extending
s_{1}:{\mathcal{O}}\to L
and
\widetilde{s}_{2}\in H^{0}(V)
is a lifting of
s_{2}
.

Let us consider the map

\operatorname{End}(V)/\langle\operatorname{id}\rangle\to H^{0}(L):A\mapsto p\circ A\circ i.

We claim that this map is injective. Indeed, if
p\circ A\circ i=0
for
A\in\operatorname{End}(V)
, then
A\circ i=c\cdot i
for some constant
c
. Hence,
(A-c\operatorname{id})\circ i=0
, so
A-c\operatorname{id}
factors through
a map
L\to V
. But any such map is zero since the extension (
2.1
) does not split, so
A\in\langle\operatorname{id}\rangle
, which proves our claim.

Now suppose
s_{1}=p\circ A\circ i
, for some
A\in\operatorname{End}(V)
. Then we can take
\widetilde{s}_{1}=p\circ A
. Hence,
\widetilde{s}_{1}(\widetilde{s}_{2})=p(A(\widetilde{s}_{2}))
which lies in
\langle x\rangle^{\perp}

since it is liftable to
H^{0}(V)
. Thus, we have an inclusion

\operatorname{End}(V)/\langle\operatorname{id}\rangle\hookrightarrow\operatorname{ker}(\Pi_{x})\subset\langle x\rangle^{\perp}.

Since the dimensions are equal, in fact, we get an identification of vector spaces

\operatorname{End}(V)/\langle\operatorname{id}\rangle\rTo{\sim}{\mathfrak{g}}_{\Pi,x}=\operatorname{ker}(\Pi_{x}).

It remains to check compatibility of the brackets. Let us fix a pair of endomorphisms
A,A^{\prime}\in\operatorname{End}(V)
, and let
s=p\circ A\circ i
and
s^{\prime}=p\circ A^{\prime}\circ i
be the corresponding elements of

\operatorname{ker}(\Pi_{x})
. Let us also fix a vector
v\in\operatorname{Ext}^{1}(L,{\mathcal{O}})\setminus\langle x\rangle
, representing a nontrivial tangent direction to
\langle x\rangle
in the projective space.
Let us consider the corresponding line
x(t)=x+tv
in
\operatorname{Ext}^{1}(L,{\mathcal{O}})
, and the induced line
\langle x(t)\rangle
in the projective space.
In order to compute
\langle[s,s^{\prime}],v\rangle
we have to extend
s
and
s^{\prime}
to sections
s(t)
,
s^{\prime}(t)
of the cotangent bundle along the line
\langle x(t)\rangle
. Then by definition, we have

\langle[s,s^{\prime}],v\rangle=\frac{d}{dt}\langle\Pi,s(t)\wedge s^{\prime}(t)\rangle|_{t=0}.

Furthermore, it is enough to work over dual numbers, where
t^{2}=0
.
Thus, we can pick any elements
u,u^{\prime}\in H^{0}(L)
, such that

\langle x,u\rangle+\langle v,s\rangle=0,\ \langle x,u^{\prime}\rangle+\langle v,s^{\prime}\rangle=0,

and set
s(t)=s+tu
,
s^{\prime}(t)=s^{\prime}+tu^{\prime}
. Then

\langle x(t),s(t)\rangle=\langle x+tv,s+tu\rangle=0,\ \ \langle x(t),s^{\prime}(t)\rangle=0,

so
s(t)
and
s^{\prime}(t)
are in sections of the cotangent bundle. Now we have to calculate the derivative at
0
(i.e., the coefficient of
t
)of

\langle\Pi,s(t)\wedge s^{\prime}(t)\rangle=\langle MP(s+tu,x+tv,s^{\prime}+tu^{\prime}),x+tv\rangle,

To calculate this we use Cech representatives. We cover
E
with two affine opens
U_{1}
and
U_{2}
, set
U_{12}=U_{1}\cap U_{2}
, and realize
x
and
v
by
Cech
1
-cocycles
x\in L^{-1}(U_{12})
,
v\in L^{-1}(U_{12})
. The extension
V=V_{x}
of
L
by
{\mathcal{O}}
associated with
x
is equipped with splitting
\sigma_{a}:L\to V
over each
U_{a}
,

a=1,2
, such that over
U_{12}
we have

(\sigma_{2}-\sigma_{1})(\phi)=i(x\cdot\phi),

where
\phi\in L(U_{12})
.

If we have a section
s\in\langle x\rangle^{\perp}\subset H^{0}(L)
, then there exist
f_{a}\in{\mathcal{O}}(U_{a})
,
a=1,2
, such that

x\cdot s=f_{1}-f_{2}.

(2.2)

Then we can extend
s
to a section
\alpha=\alpha(s,f_{\bullet}):{\mathcal{O}}\to V
and to a morphism
\beta=\beta(s,f_{\bullet}):V\to L
such that

p(\alpha)=s=\beta\circ i
, and

\alpha|_{U_{a}}=i(f_{a})+\sigma_{a}(s),\ \ \beta|_{U_{a}}(\sigma_{a}(\phi))=-f_{a}\cdot\phi.

It is easy to see that an operator
A\in\operatorname{End}(V)
with
\operatorname{tr}(A)=0
, is determined by the section
s=p\circ A\circ i
, together with
functions
f_{a}\in{\mathcal{O}}(U_{a})
and sections
A_{a}\in L^{-1}(U_{a})
,
a=1,2
, satisfying (
2.2
) and

A_{2}-A_{1}=(f_{1}+f_{2})\cdot x.

(2.3)

Namely, the operator
A:V\to V
is given over
U_{i}
by

Ai(1)=f_{a}+\sigma_{a}(s),\ \ A\sigma_{a}(\phi)=i(A_{a}\cdot\phi)-f_{a}\cdot\sigma_{a}(\phi),

for
\phi\in L(U_{a})
.

Next, we need to consider the extension
V_{x+tv}
of
L
by
{\mathcal{O}}
corresponding to
x+tv
. We denote by
\sigma_{a}(t):L\to V_{x+tv}
the splittings over
U_{a}
,

a=1,2
, such that

(\sigma_{2}(t)-\sigma_{1}(t))(\phi)=i((x+tv)\cdot\phi).

We start with two traceless operators
A,A^{\prime}\in\operatorname{End}_{0}(V)
and consider the corresponding sections
s,s^{\prime}\in\langle x\rangle^{\perp}\subset H^{0}(L)
. Thus, we can assume that we have
the corresponding functions
f_{a},f^{\prime}_{a}\in{\mathcal{O}}(U_{a})
and sections
A_{a},A^{\prime}_{a}\in L^{-1}(U_{a})
satisfying (
2.2
), (
2.3
) and the similar relations
involving
s^{\prime}
,
f^{\prime}_{a}
and
A^{\prime}_{a}
.
We also choose functions
g_{a},g^{\prime}_{a}\in{\mathcal{O}}(U_{a})
such that

v\cdot s+x\cdot u=g_{1}-g_{2},\ \ v\cdot s^{\prime}+x\cdot u^{\prime}=g^{\prime}_{1}-g^{\prime}_{2}.

(2.4)

Then we have the following deformed version of (
2.2
) over
U_{12}
:

(x+tv)\cdot(s+tu)=(f_{1}+g_{1}t)-(f_{2}+g_{2}t).

By definition, the Massey product
MP(s+tu,x+tv,s^{\prime}+tu^{\prime})
is the class of the composition
\beta\circ\alpha^{\prime}
, where
\alpha^{\prime}:{\mathcal{O}}\to V_{x+tv}
is the global section projecting to
s^{\prime}+tu^{\prime}
,
while
b:V_{x+tv}\to L
is the morphism extending
s+tu
. As we have seen before, we can define
\alpha^{\prime}
and
\beta
so that over
U_{a}
we have

a^{\prime}=i(f^{\prime}_{a}+tg^{\prime}_{a})+\sigma_{a}(s^{\prime}+tu^{\prime}),

\beta(i(f)+\sigma_{a}(\phi))=f(s+tu)-(f_{a}+tg_{a})\phi.

Hence,

\displaystyle MP=MP(s+tu,x+tv,s^{\prime}+tu^{\prime})=(s+tu)(f^{\prime}_{a}+tg^{\prime}_{a})-(s^{\prime}+tu^{\prime})(f_{a}+tg_{a})=

\displaystyle sf^{\prime}_{a}-s^{\prime}f_{a}+t(sg^{\prime}_{a}+uf^{\prime}_{a}-s^{\prime}g_{a}-u^{\prime}f_{a}),

and therefore, considering the coefficient of
t
in
\langle MP,x+tv\rangle
we get

[s,s^{\prime}]=v\cdot(sf^{\prime}_{a}-s^{\prime}f_{a})+x\cdot(sg^{\prime}_{a}+uf^{\prime}_{a}-s^{\prime}g_{a}-u^{\prime}f_{a})\operatorname{mod}\operatorname{im}(\delta),

where
a=1
or
2
, and we view the right-hand side as a Cech cohomology class
H^{1}({\mathcal{O}})\simeq\mathbf{k}

(the answers for
a=1
and
a=2
differ by a cocycle).
Therefore, we have

2[s,s^{\prime}]=v\cdot[s(f^{\prime}_{1}+f^{\prime}_{2})-s^{\prime}(f_{1}+f_{2})]+C\operatorname{mod}\operatorname{im}(\delta),

where

C=x\cdot[s(g^{\prime}_{1}+g^{\prime}_{2})+u(f^{\prime}_{1}+f^{\prime}_{2})-s^{\prime}(g_{1}+g_{2})-u^{\prime}(f_{1}+f_{2})]\operatorname{mod}\operatorname{im}(\delta).

Note that
u
and
u^{\prime}
are global sections, while
x(f_{1}+f_{2})
and
x(f^{\prime}_{1}+f^{\prime}_{2})
are coboundaries due to the relation (
2.3
).
Hence,

C=xs(g^{\prime}_{1}+g^{\prime}_{2})-xs^{\prime}(g_{1}+g_{2})=(f_{1}-f_{2})(g^{\prime}_{1}+g^{\prime}_{2})-(f^{\prime}_{1}-f^{\prime}_{2})(g_{1}+g_{2})\equiv f_{1}g^{\prime}_{2}-f_{2}g^{\prime}_{1}-f^{\prime}_{1}g_{2}+f^{\prime}_{2}g_{1}\operatorname{mod}\operatorname{im}(\delta),

where we removed the coboundary terms
f_{1}g^{\prime}_{1}
, etc.

On the other hand, multiplying the first of the relations (
2.4
) with
f^{\prime}_{1}+f^{\prime}_{2}
, we get

f^{\prime}_{2}g_{1}-f^{\prime}_{1}g_{2}\equiv(f^{\prime}_{1}+f^{\prime}_{2})(g_{1}-g_{2})=(f^{\prime}_{1}+f^{\prime}_{2})vs+(f^{\prime}_{1}+f^{\prime}_{2})xu\equiv(f^{\prime}_{1}+f^{\prime}_{2})vs\operatorname{mod}\operatorname{im}(\delta).

Similarly,

f_{2}g^{\prime}_{1}-f_{1}g^{\prime}_{2}\equiv(f_{1}+f_{2})vs^{\prime}\operatorname{mod}\operatorname{im}(\delta).

Hence, we obtain

C\equiv v\cdot[s(f^{\prime}_{1}+f^{\prime}_{2})-s^{\prime}(f_{1}+f_{2})],

which gives

\langle[s,s^{\prime}],v\rangle=v\cdot[s(f^{\prime}_{1}+f^{\prime}_{2})-s^{\prime}(f_{1}+f_{2})]\operatorname{mod}\operatorname{im}(\delta).

Finally, using (
2.2
), we get

(f_{1}-f_{2})\cdot s^{\prime}=x\cdot s\cdot s^{\prime}=(f^{\prime}_{1}-f^{\prime}_{2})\cdot s,

or equivalently,

sf^{\prime}_{1}-s^{\prime}f_{1}=sf^{\prime}_{2}-s^{\prime}f_{2}.

Hence, we can rewrite the above formula as

\langle[s,s^{\prime}],v\rangle=2v\cdot(sf^{\prime}_{1}-s^{\prime}f_{1}).

It remains to compare this with
p(AA^{\prime}-A^{\prime}A)i(1)
. We can compute this over
U_{1}
:

p(AA^{\prime}-A^{\prime}A)i(1)=pA[i(f^{\prime}_{1})+\sigma_{1}(s^{\prime})]-pA^{\prime}[i(f_{1})+\sigma_{1}(s)]=2(f^{\prime}_{1}s-f_{1}s^{\prime}).

Comparing this with the formula above, we get

\langle p(AA^{\prime}-A^{\prime}A)i(1),v\rangle=\langle[s,s^{\prime}],v\rangle

which proves our assertion.

Remark 2.1.2
.

Proposition
2.1.1
is a part of a broader picture, involving the notion of a symplectic groupoid (see
[
13
]
). Given
a symplectic groupoid
M
with the space of objects
X
(in the category of smooth schemes), then
X
has a natural Poisson structure. In this situation
one can also consider the algebraic stack
{\mathcal{X}}
associated with the groupoid
M
. Then the Lie algebra of automorphisms in
M
of an object
x\in X

(or equivalently, of the corresponding point of
{\mathcal{X}}
) is naturally identified with the coisotropic Lie algebra at
x\in X
of the Poisson structure on
X
(see
[
6
, ch. 2, Lem. 1.2]
).

In the case of the Feigin–Odesskii Poisson structure on
X={\mathbb{P}}\operatorname{Ext}^{1}(L,{\mathcal{O}})
, the symplectic groupoid
M
can be constructed as follows. We have a
natural map
f:X\to\operatorname{Bun}_{L}
, where
\operatorname{Bun}_{L}
is the stack of rank
2
bundles on
E
with
the determinant
L
, and
f
associates the bundle
V
with an extension
(
2.1
). Then we define
M
to be the
2
-fibered product

M:=X\times_{\operatorname{Bun}_{L}}X.

The first part of the proof of Prop.
2.1.1
shows that the map
f

induces a surjection on tangent spaces, hence,
M
is a smooth algebraic space. From the fibered product structure we get a groupoid structure on
M
.
The corresponding stack
{\mathcal{X}}
is equipped with a fully faithful map

{\mathcal{X}}\to\operatorname{Bun}_{L}
(see
[
12
, Lem. 93.16.1]
, so automorphisms of points are the same.
One can show that in fact
M
has a structure of a symplectic groupoid that induces the Feigin–Odesskii Poisson structure on

(the details will appear elsewhere).
This gives a more conceptual proof of the identification of Lie algebras in Prop.
2.1.1
.

It is well known that the FO-bracket
\Pi_{E}
vanishes at any
p\in E
. Hence, in this case we get a Lie algebra structure on
T_{p}^{*}{\mathbb{P}}^{n}
.

Corollary 2.1.3
.

For
p\in E\subset{\mathbb{P}}^{n}
, the conormal Lie algebra
{\mathfrak{g}}=T_{p}^{*}{\mathbb{P}}^{n}
admits a basis
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n-1},y
, such that

\langle x_{1},\ldots,x_{n-1}\rangle=(T_{x}E)^{\perp}
is an abelian subalgebra, and
[y,x_{i}]=x_{i}
.

Proof.

In this case
V={\mathcal{O}}(p)\oplus L(-p)
and the algebra
\operatorname{End}(V)/\langle\operatorname{id}\rangle
has an abelian subalgebra
A
corresponding to endomorphisms of
the form
V\to{\mathcal{O}}(p)\to L(-p)\to V
. The
1
-dimensional complement to
A
is spanned by the idempotent
e
corresponding to the summand
L(-p)
, and for any
a\in A

we have
ea=a
,
ae=0
, so
[e,a]=a
.
∎

Using this description of the conormal Lie algebras, we get the following constraints on Poisson brackets compatible with
\Pi_{E}
.

Proposition 2.1.4
.

Let
E\subset{\mathbb{P}}^{n}
be a normal elliptic curve, and let
\Pi
be a Poisson bracket on
{\mathbb{P}}^{n}
. Assume that
[\Pi_{E},\Pi]=0
.
Then for every
p\in E
, one has
\Pi|_{p}=v_{1}\wedge v_{2}
, where
v_{1}\in T_{p}E
.

Proof.

We will calculate
[\Pi_{E},\Pi]|_{p}
for
p\in E
, and then equate it to zero. We have
\Pi_{E}|_{p}=0
, and
the linear part of
\Pi_{E}
is

\Pi_{E}^{lin}:=\sum_{i}x_{i}\partial_{x_{i}}\wedge\partial_{y},

where
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n-1},y
is a basis of
T_{p}{\mathbb{P}}^{n}
chosen as in Corollary
2.1.3
.
The bracket
[\Pi_{E},\Pi]|_{p}=[\Pi_{E}^{lin},\Pi]|_{p}
depends only on
\Pi|_{p}
. We have

[\Pi_{E}^{lin},\partial_{x_{i}}\wedge\partial_{y}]=0,

[\Pi_{E}^{lin},\partial_{x_{i}}\wedge\partial_{x_{j}}]=\pm 2\partial_{x_{i}}\wedge\partial_{x_{j}}\wedge\partial_{y}.

Hence,
[\Pi_{E},\Pi]|_{p}=0
if and only if
\Pi|_{p}=v\wedge\partial_{y}
, for some
v\in T_{p}{\mathbb{P}}^{n}
.
Since
\langle\partial_{y}\rangle=T_{p}E
, our assertion follows.
∎

Assume

We refer to

Lemma 2.2.2

∎

Fix a normal elliptic curve

Proposition 2.3.1
.

(i) Assume
n=4
or
n\geq 6
. Then for

, one has
\operatorname{rk}(\Pi_{E})_{x}\leq 2
if and only if

.

(ii) Assume
n=5
. Then for for
x\in{\mathbb{P}}^{n}
, one has
\operatorname{rk}(\Pi_{E})_{x}\leq 2
if and only if
x\in\operatorname{Sec}^{2}(E)
or
x\in P_{M}\subset{\mathbb{P}}^{5}
,
for some
M\in\operatorname{Pic}_{3}(E)
such that

.

Proof.

(i) Let
\Pi=\Pi_{E}
. By
[
5
, Prop. 2.3]
, we have
\operatorname{rk}\Pi_{x}\leq 2
if and only if
\dim\operatorname{End}(V)\geq\deg(L)-2
, where
\deg(L)=n+1
.
Assume first that
n\geq 6
. Then we get
\dim\operatorname{End}(V)\geq 5
, so
V
cannot be semistable. Hence,
V
is isomorphic to a direct sum of line bundles:

V=L_{1}\oplus L_{2}
, where
\deg(L_{1})<\deg(L_{2})
. Furthermore, we have

\dim\operatorname{End}(V)=2+\deg(L_{2})-\deg(L_{1})\geq\deg(L)-2=\deg(L_{1})+\deg(L_{2})-2,

so
\deg(L_{1})\leq 2
. The composed map
{\mathcal{O}}\to V\to L_{1}
cannot be zero, so it vanishes on an effective divisor
D
of degree
\leq 2
. Since
V
is a nontrivial extension,
we have
\deg(D)\geq 1
. If
\deg(D)=1
then our extension splits under
{\mathcal{O}}\to{\mathcal{O}}(p)
for some point, so
x
lies on the elliptic curve
E\subset{\mathbb{P}}^{n}
. If
\deg(D)=2
then our
extension splits under
{\mathcal{O}}\to{\mathcal{O}}(D)
, so it lies on the chord of
E
in
{\mathbb{P}}^{n}
corresponding to
D
.

Conversely, if
x\in\operatorname{Sec}^{2}(E)
, then there exists an effective degree
2
divisor
D
on
E
such that the extension associated with
x

splits under
{\mathcal{O}}\to{\mathcal{O}}(D)
. If
x\in E
then the extension splits already under
{\mathcal{O}}\to{\mathcal{O}}(q)
for some

. Then

,

, which means that
\Pi_{x}=0
. Otherwise, if
x\in\operatorname{Sec}^{2}(E)\setminus E
, then we get an exact sequence

0\to L(-D)\to V\to{\mathcal{O}}(D)\to 0.

Since

, we have
\deg(L(-D))=3>2=\deg({\mathcal{O}}(D))
, so

. Hence,
\deg\operatorname{End}(V)=\deg(L)-2
and
\operatorname{rk}\Pi_{x}=2
.

Assume now that
n=4
. Then the extensions
V
corresponding points where
\operatorname{rk}\Pi_{x}\leq 2
satisfy
\dim\operatorname{End}(V)\geq 3
. Since
\deg(V)=5
, this means that

is unstable,

where

is either
(1,4)
or
(2,3)
. We finish the proof in the same way as before.

(ii) Now the condition on

(which has degree

\dim\operatorname{End}(V)\geq 4
. This means that either
V
is unstable, or

, for some line bundle

of degree

such that

. In the former case, we use the same argument as in part (i) to show that the correspond point lies on
\operatorname{Sec}^{2}(C)
. We claim that extensions
with

correspond to points of

, for the Veronese surface

. Indeed, an extension of the form

corresponds to a base point free pencil

The line in

corresponding to our extension is given by the hyperplane

, obtained as the image of the map
H^{0}(M)^{2}\to H^{0}(L)
induced by

It remains to observe that

is exactly the space of quadrics on

under the Veronese embedding

∎

3.1.
Proof of Theorem A

Consider first the case of two brackets. Assume
[\Pi_{E_{1}},\Pi_{E_{2}}]=0
. By Proposition
2.1.4
, at every point

p\in E_{1}\setminus(E_{2}\cup\Sigma_{E_{2}})
(where
\Sigma_{E_{2}}
is the set of four vertices of singular quadrics through
E_{2}
), one has
(\Pi_{E_{2}})_{p}=v_{1}\wedge v_{2}
, where
v_{1}\in T_{p}E_{1}
.
Note also that the subspace
\langle v_{1},v_{2}\rangle\subset T_{p}{\mathbb{P}}^{3}
is exactly the image of
(\Pi_{E_{2}})_{p}:T_{p}^{*}{\mathbb{P}}^{3}\to T_{p}{\mathbb{P}}^{3}
, which is contained
in
T_{p}Q
where
Q
is the unique quadric passing through
p
and
E_{2}
(see Lemma
2.3.3
(ii)).
Hence, we get an inclusion
T_{p}E_{1}\subset T_{p}Q
.
It follows that the restriction to
E_{1}\setminus E_{2}
of the map

{\mathbb{P}}^{3}\setminus E_{2}\to{\mathbb{P}}^{1}
given by quadrics through
E_{2}
, has zero tangent map at every point. Hence, this map is constant,
which means that
E_{1}
is contained in a single quadric through
E_{2}
.

For the case of a general collection
(E_{i})
, let
(L_{i}=L(E_{i}))
denote the corresponding collection of projective lines in the projective space of all quadrics (where

L(E_{i})
is the pencil of quadrics passing through
E_{i}
). Then
(\Pi_{E_{i}})
are compatible if and only if every pair of lines
L_{i}
and
L_{j}
has nontrivial intersection.
It is well known that this happens if and only if either all lines pass through one point, or all are contained in a projective plane.

Conversely, the fact that the FO brackets associated with anticanonical divisors on a quadric in
{\mathbb{P}}^{3}
are compatible follows from the results of
[
5
]

(see
[
5
, Ex. 4.8]
).
∎

3.2.
Proof of Theorem B

Consider first the case when we have two normal elliptic curves

such that
[\Pi_{E_{1}},\Pi_{E_{2}}]=0
. By Proposition
2.1.4
, at every point

, one has

, where

. In particular,
\operatorname{rk}(\Pi_{E_{2}})_{x}\leq 2
, so by Proposition

3.3.
Proof of Theorem D

Let
{\mathbb{P}}^{3}={\mathbb{P}}V
, where
V
is a
4
-dimensional vector space. Since normal elliptic curves in
{\mathbb{P}}^{3}
are intersections of pairs of quadrics,
we can view the construction of the FO Poisson bracket as associating with a pair of generic quadratic forms
Q_{1},Q_{2}\in S^{2}V^{*}
, a Poisson bivector

\Pi_{Q_{1}=Q_{2}=0}\in H^{0}({\mathbb{P}}V,{\bigwedge}^{2}T)
defined up to rescaling.
More precisely, the choice of quadrics
Q_{1},Q_{2}
and of a volume form on
V
gives a nonvanishing differential on the elliptic curve
E=(Q_{1}=0)\cap(Q_{2}=0)
,
which is used in the construction of
\Pi(Q_{1},Q_{2})
. We claim that in fact there is a linear
\operatorname{GL}(V)
-equivariant map

\Psi:{\bigwedge}^{2}(S^{2}V^{*})\to\det(V^{*})\otimes H^{0}({\mathbb{P}}V,{\bigwedge}^{2}T)

such that for
Q_{1}
and
Q_{2}
defining an elliptic curve,
\Pi(Q_{1},Q_{2})
coincides with
\Psi(Q_{1}\wedge Q_{2})
up to a factor.
Indeed, it is well known that the FO brackets associated with the elliptic curve
Q_{1}=Q_{2}=0
are induced by the quadratic Poisson bracket on
S(V^{*})
given by

\{\ell_{1},\ell_{2}\}=d\ell_{1}\wedge d\ell_{2}\wedge dQ_{1}\wedge dQ_{2}/\operatorname{vol}_{V}^{*},

where
\operatorname{vol}_{V}^{*}
is a generator of
\det(V^{*})
(see e.g.,
[
11
, Sec. 2.1]
). This gives the required linear map
\Psi
which should be viewed as a normalized version of the bivectors
\Pi_{Q_{1}=Q_{2}=0}
.

Hence, taking the Schouten bracket of two FO brackets corresponds to a linear
\operatorname{GL}(V)
-invariant map

\Psi^{(2)}:({\bigwedge}^{2}(S^{2}V^{*}))^{\otimes 2}\to\det(V^{*})^{2}\otimes H^{0}({\mathbb{P}}V,{\bigwedge}^{3}T)\simeq\det(V^{*})\otimes S^{4}(V^{*}).

We claim that this map factors through the multiplication map in the exterior algebra of
S^{2}V^{*}
,

\mu:({\bigwedge}^{2}(S^{2}V^{*}))^{\otimes 2}\to{\bigwedge}^{4}(S^{2}V^{*}).

The proof will be based on the fact that by Theorem A,
\Psi^{(2)}((Q_{1}\wedge Q_{2})\otimes(Q_{3}\wedge Q_{4}))=0
whenever the planes
\langle Q_{1},Q_{2}\rangle
and
\langle Q_{3},Q_{4}\rangle
in
S^{2}(V^{*})
have
nonzero intersection (since the corresponding FO brackets are compatible). Set
W:=S^{2}(V^{*})
.
Namely, we can interpret the dual map
(\Psi^{(2)})^{\vee}
as a section of the vector bundle
\det(V^{*})\otimes S^{4}(V^{*})\otimes{\mathcal{O}}(1,1)
on the square of the Grassmannian variety

G(2,W)^{2}
, which vanishes on the subvariety
Z\subset G(2,W)^{2}
consisting of pairs of planes
P_{1},P_{2}\subset W
with
P_{1}\cap P_{2}\neq 0
.
It is well known that the subspace

H^{0}(G(2,W)^{2},{\mathcal{I}}_{Z}(1,1))\subset H^{0}(G(2,W)^{2},{\mathcal{O}}(1,1))

is precisely the image of the map

\mu^{\vee}:{\bigwedge}^{4}(W)^{*}\to({\bigwedge}^{2}(W)^{*})^{\otimes 2}

dual to the multiplication. Hence,
(\Psi^{(2)})^{\vee}
belongs to the subspace
\det(V^{*})\otimes S^{4}(V^{*})\otimes\operatorname{im}(\mu^{\vee})
, or equivalently,
\Psi^{(2)}
factors through
\mu
.

Thus, we get
\Psi^{(2)}=\Phi^{\prime}\circ\mu
for some nonzero
\operatorname{GL}(V)
-invariant map

\Phi^{\prime}:{\bigwedge}^{4}(S^{2}V^{*})\to\det(V^{*})\otimes S^{4}(V^{*}).

But
\det(V^{*})\otimes S^{4}(V^{*})
is an irreducible
\operatorname{GL}(V)
-representation occurring with multiplicity one in
{\bigwedge}^{4}(S^{2}V^{*})
, Hence,
\Phi^{\prime}=c\Phi
for some constant
c
.

Finally, to find the constant
c
, we calculate both sides for

Q_{1}=x_{1}^{2},\ Q_{2}=x_{1}x_{2},\ Q_{3}=x_{1}x_{3},\ Q_{4}=x_{1}x_{4}.

We use coordinates
y_{i}=x_{i}/x_{1}
,
i=2,3,4
on the affine part of
{\mathbb{P}}^{3}
.
The Poisson structure associated with
(Q_{1},Q_{2})
has the only nonzero bracket between the coordinates

\{y_{3},y_{4}\}_{1}=2

(where we trivialize
\det(V^{*})
using
\operatorname{vol}=x_{1}\wedge x_{2}\wedge x_{3}\wedge x_{4}
), so

\Pi_{1}=2\partial_{y_{3}}\wedge\partial_{y_{4}}.

The Poisson structure associated with
(Q_{3},Q_{4})
has

\{y_{2},y_{3}\}=2y_{3},\ \{y_{2},y_{4}\}=2y_{4},\ \{y_{3},y_{4}\}=0,

so

\Pi_{2}=2y_{3}\cdot\partial_{y_{2}}\wedge\partial_{y_{3}}+2y_{4}\cdot\partial_{y_{2}}\wedge\partial_{y_{4}}.

Therefore,

[\Pi_{1},\Pi_{2}]=8\partial_{y_{2}}\wedge\partial_{y_{3}}\wedge\partial_{y_{4}},

which corresponds to the quartic
8x_{1}^{4}
.
On the other hand the quartic polynomial associated with
Q_{1}\wedge Q_{2}\wedge Q_{3}\wedge Q_{4}
sends
e_{1}
to

2\cdot\operatorname{vol}
. Hence,
c=4
.
∎
