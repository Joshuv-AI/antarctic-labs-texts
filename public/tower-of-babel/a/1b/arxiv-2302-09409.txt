LOCUS – LOcalization with Channel Uncertainty and Sporadic Energy
Subrata Biswas, Mohammad Nur Hossain Khan, Alex Colwell, Jack Adiletta, Bashima Islam

Abstract
. Accurate sound source localization (SSL) requires consistent multichannel data for reliable degree of arrival (DoA) estimation. However, intermittently powered batteryless systems often suffer from incomplete sensor data due to the stochastic nature of energy harvesting. Existing methods struggle with missing channels, leading to significant performance degradation. In this paper, we propose LOCUS , a novel deep learning-based system designed to recover corrupted features for SSL in batteryless systems. LOCUS addresses missing data by leveraging information entropy estimation and conditional interpolation, combining three modules: (1) Information-Weighted Focus (InFo), which identifies and quantifies corrupted data elements, (2) Latent Feature Synthesizer (LaFS), which synthesizes missing features, and (3) Guided Replacement (GRep), which intelligently replaces missing elements while preserving valid data. We demonstrate significant performance improvements using two datasets: DCASE and LargeSet, where LOCUS achieves up to 36.91% lower DoA error compared to existing methods. Real-world evaluations across three environments with intermittent power sources show a 25.87-59.46% improvement in performance when channels are stochastically missing. Additionally, we release a 50-hour multichannel dataset to support further research in SSL.



1.
Introduction

Accurate degree of arrival (DoA) estimation relies on extracting temporal and spatial differences from multichannel data captured by multiple microphones. Both signal processing
(
Schmidt 1986
;
DiBiase 2000
;
Roy and Kailath 1989
)
and deep learning
(
Xiao et al. 2015
;
Yalta et al. 2017
;
Chakrabarty and Habets 2017
)
approaches use this multichannel data to localize sound sources effectively. In real-world scenarios, where complex multipath profiles and room impulse responses arise from signal reflections and refractions, deep learning methods excel by leveraging the richness of multichannel data to capture intricate patterns that traditional signal processing techniques may miss.

However, intermittently powered batteryless systems face significant challenges in maintaining full-channel data integrity due to the stochastic nature of energy harvesting. These systems, reliant on sporadic power availability, often struggle to consistently capture data from all channels, which is crucial for precise DoA estimation. Intermittent systems typically can only process short-lived events, lasting only a few hundred milliseconds, and are primarily limited to tasks such as classification
(
Majid et al. 2020a
;
Islam and Nirjon 2020
;
Monjur et al. 2023
)
. This constraint arises from the core design of intermittent systems, which rely on a fixed-size capacitor to store sufficient harvested energy for operation. During the system’s active phase, it consumes this stored energy to perform designated tasks, after which it enters a sleep phase when the energy is depleted. By selecting different capacitor sizes, the duration of the active and sleep phases can be adjusted.
However, intermittent systems inevitably encounter one or more sleep phases when insufficient energy is harvested, resulting in incomplete or missing sensor data
(
Monjur et al. 2023
)
. This lack of sensor data significantly degrades the system’s performance, particularly for longer events that last on the order of tens of seconds. As a result, these systems are not typically used for sound source localization (SSL), where complete multichannel data is essential.

Despite these challenges, batteryless systems hold great potential in applications such as environmental monitoring and wildlife tracking, where low-power, autonomous operation is essential. Even though these systems experience frequent power interruptions, they can still provide valuable data during operational periods.

Working with features instead of raw time-domain data is particularly important in batteryless systems. Extracted features like generalized cross-correlation (GCC) and spectral power encapsulate the most relevant information for sound source localization, reducing noise and data dimensionality. This not only improves computational efficiency but also minimizes energy consumption, which is critical in resource-constrained systems. Moreover, feature extraction can be implemented using low-power analog circuits in embedded systems, further reducing the energy footprint.

Existing solutions for addressing missing or corrupted data in sound source localization typically rely on statistical imputation techniques such as mean imputation
(
Allison 2001
)
, hot deck imputation
(
Little and Rubin 2019
)
, and multiple imputations
(
Rubin 1996
)
. While these methods can fill data gaps, they have notable limitations. Mean imputation oversimplifies by averaging values, hot deck imputation can introduce inaccuracies if data is poorly matched, and multiple imputations are computationally intensive and struggle with long missing data sequences
(
Hasan et al. 2021
)
. Recent approaches using mutual information and conditional entropy
(
Vergara and Estévez 2014
)
help identify corrupted data but fall short in recovering the information necessary for DNN-based sound source localization. Additionally, recent work on imputation-based data recovery integrated into DNN pipelines for batteryless systems
(
Monjur et al. 2023
)
is designed for single-channel input, making it inadequate for the multi-channel sound source localization needed in more complex systems.

The gap in current sound source localization systems lies in the inability to effectively recover missing channel data caused by power interruptions in intermittently powered systems. Without advanced methods to intelligently infer or reconstruct this information, localization accuracy suffers significantly. Frequent data loss, especially in batteryless systems relying on stochastic energy harvesting, presents a major challenge for precise localization.

This paper bridges the gap between faulty multi-channel microphone arrays and deep learning-based batteryless sound source localization (SSL) by proposing
LOCUS
.
LOCUS
combines information entropy estimation and conditional interpolation to identify corrupted portions in the feature set and estimate the true feature values. The system consists of three novel modules: (1) information-weighted focus (InFo), which estimates the information entropy for each element of the calculated features to understand the impact of corrupted data; (2) latent feature synthesizer (LaFS), which estimates missing information by leveraging the relationships derived from information entropy; and (3) guided replacement (GRep), which uses the information entropy from InFo to replace corrupted elements with interpolated features from LaFS, leaving unaffected elements intact. These reconstructed features are then fed into the DNN for sound source localization.

The main contributions of this work are as follows:

•

We propose
LOCUS
, a three-step information recovery system that leverages information entropy estimation and conditional interpolation to recover corrupted features, mitigating performance loss in deep learning-based sound source localization due to missing or faulty input channels.

•

We provide a mathematical formulation of the problem and establish a connection with information theory principles.

•

Beyond evaluation with multiple datasets, we conduct real-world evaluations in three different environments, where missing channels occur due to power intermittence from energy harvesting systems (solar and radio frequency). This demonstrates the practical applicability and robustness of
LOCUS
in real-world scenarios.

•

We release a new 6-channel acoustic dataset (
LargeSet
) comprising 50 hours of audio recorded in 10 different environments. This dataset supports further research and development in sound source localization.

Our evaluation shows significant performance gains, with
LOCUS
achieving 21.27% and 17.14% less average DoA error than
SoundSeive
(
Monjur et al. 2023
)
on the DCASE
(
Politis et al. 2021
)
and LargeSet datasets, respectively. In real-world evaluations,
LOCUS
improves performance by 25.87-59.46% in scenarios with stochastically missing information. Additionally,
LOCUS
reduces the 90th percentile localization error by 25.52% in wireless source localization tasks using the WiFi channel state information (CSI) dataset
(
Ayyalasomayajula et al. 2020
)
.

Managing Missing Data in Batteryless Microphone Arrays

Quantifying Missing Data (MDP)
.
In a multi-channel microphone array, data from a sound source can be corrupted by missing or intermittent data from one or more channels, while others may remain unaffected. To standardize the measurement of missing data, we define the
Missing Data Percentage (MDP)
, denoted as
p\%
. The MDP quantifies the proportion of the total audio that is corrupted.
The MDP is calculated using the following formula:

MDP=\frac{t_{1}+t_{2}+\cdots+t_{n}}{T}\times 100

where
t_{1},t_{2},\ldots,t_{n}
represent the segments with missing data, and
T
is the total audio duration.

For example, Figure
1
illustrates an instance with a 75% MDP, where up to two channels are missing at any given time. In time segments
t_{1}
and
t_{2}
, which account for 75% of the total duration, one or more channels are missing data. In contrast, segment
t_{3}
has no missing data across any channels. As a result, the MDP in this example is 75%.

Missing Channels in Intermittent Systems
.
Intermittent power systems, such as those powered by RF energy harvesters, frequently experience unpredictable power fluctuations that can lead to significant data loss in multi-channel microphone arrays. These fluctuations are caused by external factors, such as uncontrolled human traffic between the RF transmitter and receiver, which dynamically affects the system’s ability to maintain consistent power. As a result, some microphones intermittently fail to collect data, leading to incomplete or corrupted information across the array.

Figure
2
illustrates energy traces from an RF-harvested system over a 120-second period, showing how fluctuations in available energy result in a 56.67% MDP. This scenario demonstrates how missing data affects nearly 60% of the total audio, with varying numbers of channels missing at any given time.

In Section
7
, we evaluate the impact of stochastic power variations on sound source localization. The results highlight the need for robust techniques like
LOCUS
to handle missing data in real-world applications, where power instability is common, and traditional methods often fail.

Impact of Missing Channels on SSL
.
There is extensive research on sound source localization (SSL) with multichannel microphones, both in simulated and real-world environments
(
Evers et al. 2020

Challenges in Replacing Corrupted Channels
.
Given the high correlation between closely spaced microphones, one might assume that excluding a faulty channel is straightforward. However, experiments show that this assumption is not effective in practice.

We conducted an experiment with two scenarios with a 75% MDP. First, we replaced missing data in faulty channels with information from the most correlated channel (
REP_{corr}
). In the second scenario, we excluded faulty channels altogether (
EXC
) and retrained the DNN to work only with the remaining active channels.

Figure
5
shows that both methods increased DoA error, by
46.34%
and
63.02%
. These findings demonstrate that neither detection nor retraining with fewer channels is sufficient to handle the dynamic nature of intermittent power systems, where missing data varies unpredictably. Traditional approaches fail to manage this challenge effectively in real-world applications. A more comprehensive, information entropy-based approach is needed to address missing data effectively, without requiring prior knowledge of corrupted microphones or relying on time-domain recovery models.

3.
Problem Formulation and Mathematical Foundation

This section formulates the problem and provides the mathematical foundation of the proposed
LOCUS
.

First, we revisit the definitions of
Full Rank and Low Rank Matrices
. A full rank matrix
F
has all its rows and columns linearly independent. In the context of feature extraction for sound source localization, this means that all channels provide complete and accurate information, ensuring no data loss. Conversely, a low-rank matrix
\tilde{F}
has rows or columns that are linearly dependent, indicating missing or corrupted information in one or more channels. This loss or corruption of data reduces the effectiveness of the sound source localization algorithm.

Consider
X^{n\times T}
as the raw time-domain data representing the input multichannel audio from a microphone array.
X^{n\times T}
is a set of
n
random variables ,
\mathcal{X}=\{x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n}\}
, where
n
is the number of input channels and
T
is the time duration of each channel. A feature extraction function,
G
, maps the time-domain data into the feature domain,
F^{n\times D}=G(X)
, where
D
is the length of each feature.

When all information from all channels is present,
F
is considered a full rank matrix. However, when one or more input channels have missing or corrupted elements,
X^{n\times T}
changes to
\tilde{X}^{n^{\prime}\times T}
, where
n^{\prime}
is less than
n
. As a result, the feature extraction function generates
\tilde{F}^{n^{\prime}\times D}=G(\tilde{X})
. In this paper, we focus on recovering
\hat{F}
from
\tilde{F}
, where
\hat{F}\approx F
.

To recover the missing information, we use interpolation based on the existing information in
\tilde{F}
. However, during interpolation, the existing information in the interpolated feature map,
\bar{\bar{F}}
, may get polluted, introducing additional errors. To eliminate this residual error, we use the less-affected input information as feedback to the estimated feature.

Since it is unknown which portions of
\tilde{F}
contain available information, we first identify the information content of
\tilde{F}
. Given that
\tilde{F}
is a function of
\tilde{X}
, the information entropy at the
(j,k)^{\text{th}}
element of
\tilde{F}
is formulated as:

(1)

I_{j,k}(\tilde{F})=\sigma(-\tilde{F}_{j,k}\log\tilde{F}_{j,k})

Here, the sigmoid function
\sigma
in Equation
1
normalizes the entropy value between 0 and 1.
I_{j,k}(\tilde{F})
is a real number between 0 and 1, where 1 represents the presence of all information in the
(j,k)^{\text{th}}
element of
\tilde{F}
, while 0 indicates a complete absence of information in that element. Any value between 0 and 1 implies fractional missing information. For a full-rank feature map
F
, every element of
I(F)
is 1.

The recovered feature set,
\hat{F}
, can be written as:

(2)

\hat{F}=\bar{\bar{F}}+I(\tilde{F})\otimes(\bar{\bar{F}}-\tilde{F})

Here,
\otimes
represents element-wise multiplication, and
I(\tilde{F})\otimes(\bar{\bar{F}}-\tilde{F})
represents the feedback from the available information of
\tilde{F}
. We can rewrite Equation
2
as:

(3)

\hat{F}=I(\tilde{F})\otimes\tilde{F}+(1-I(\tilde{F}))\otimes\bar{\bar{F}}

The first term represents the information content of
\tilde{F}
written in terms of
\tilde{F}
and the element-wise information entropy
I(\tilde{F})
. The second term represents the interpolated missing information extracted from
\bar{\bar{F}}
. This paper aims to determine the estimated full-rank feature matrix,
\hat{F}
, from a given low-rank feature matrix
\tilde{F}
.

LOCUS
: Entropy-Guided Feature Restoration Framework

Our proposed
LOCUS
recovers the perturbed information of the low-rank feature matrix,
\tilde{F}
(Equation
3
), and estimates the full-rank feature matrix,
\hat{F}\approx F
. Figure
6
shows the architecture of
LOCUS
, which sits between the feature extraction module and the DNN-based sound source localization (SSL) task.

LOCUS
has three main components: (1) Early Attention (InFo), which estimates the element-wise information entropy,
I(\tilde{F})
, indicating the amount of available information in
\tilde{F}
;
(2) Deep Conditional Interpolator (LaFS), which interpolates the feature set,
\bar{\bar{F}}
, from the available low-rank feature matrix,
\tilde{F}
; and
(3) Guided Replacement (GRep), which combines the available information from the original feature set with the interpolated feature values guided by element-wise information entropy.
Equation
3
governs this combining process. The resultant estimated full-rank feature set,
\hat{F}
, is then fed to the downstream SSL task for inference.

Information-Weighted Focus (InFo)

The purpose of
In

Fo
cus (
InFo
) module is to estimate the information entropy,
I(\tilde{F})
, for each element of the low-rank feature set
\tilde{F}
. The InFo module estimates the channel and spatial information entropy
(
Park et al. 2018
)
by giving higher attention to elements with more available information. The input to InFo is the low-rank feature set
\tilde{F}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\tilde{X}}}
, where
d_{\tilde{X}}
is the dimension of
\tilde{F}
. The left-hand side of Figure

The first component of InFo is the
channel information branch
,
f_{\gamma}(\tilde{F})
, parameterized with
\gamma
. This branch performs average pooling followed by a multi-layer perceptron to extract the channel information entropy. Average pooling aggregates information across all indices, enabling the model to learn more robust features while accurately depicting the overall strength of a feature. The multi-layer perceptron consists of three fully connected layers with batch normalization and rectified linear unit (ReLU) activation after the first two layers. This branch outputs a vector
I_{c}=f_{\gamma}(\tilde{F})
that measures global information for each channel.

The second component of InFo is the
point-wise information branch
,
f_{\lambda}(\tilde{F})
, parameterized with
\lambda
. The input
\tilde{F}
first passes through a convolution layer with a kernel size of
(1\times 1)
and a channel reduction factor of
r
. It then goes through
n
dilated convolution layers with a kernel size of
(3\times 3)
, dilation value
d
, batch normalization, and ReLU activation. In our architecture,
n=3
and
d=2
. The dilated convolution increases the receptive field, enabling us to leverage contextual information. Finally, this contextual information passes through another convolutional layer with a kernel size of
(1\times 1)
and outputs
I_{s}=f_{\lambda}(\tilde{F})
, which estimates the point-wise information of the input feature.

Next, we broadcast
I_{s}
to
\mathbb{R}^{d_{\tilde{X}}}
to match the dimension of
\tilde{F}
and perform element-wise addition on
I_{c}
and
I_{s}
to estimate the information entropy. Finally, the
sigmoid
function,
\sigma
, maps the results between 0 and 1, where a value closer to 1 indicates higher information availability and vice versa. Therefore, the information entropy is defined as:

(4)

I(\tilde{F})=\sigma(I_{c}+I_{s})

4.2.
Latent Feature Synthesizer (LaFS)

The
La
tent
F
eature
S
ynthesizer (
LaFS
) estimates a full-rank feature set,
\bar{\bar{F}}
, from the available low-rank feature set
\tilde{F}
by exploiting relations among the available channels. The right-hand side of Figure
6
shows the architecture of LaFS, which takes
\tilde{F}
as input and provides the interpolated feature
\bar{\bar{F}}
as output. We choose an auto-encoder for estimating
\bar{\bar{F}}
as it can learn compressed intermediate representations. Auto-encoders can efficiently interpolate missing information by semantically mixing characteristics from data
(
Berthelot et al. 2018
)
and are used in latent variable generation
(
Dumoulin et al. 2016
;
Ha and Eck 2017
)
.

LaFS contains two paths: (1) contraction and (2) expansion. The contraction path has
5
downsampling blocks, each consisting of a convolution layer (kernel size =
5\times 3
), ReLU activation, and batch normalization. The output of the contraction path is the latent intermediate representation
F_{con}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{c}}
, where
d_{c}
represents the intermediate feature dimension.

The expansion path passes the input
F_{con}
through 5 consecutive upsampling blocks, each consisting of transposed convolution with batch normalization and leaky-ReLU activation. The final convolution uses sigmoid activation without batch normalization. After each upsampling block, we halve the number of output feature filters, setting the last layer’s output filter number to match the shape of the input feature map. The expansion path returns the interpolated feature matrix,
\bar{\bar{F}}=f_{\phi}(\tilde{F})
, where
\bar{\bar{F}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{z}}
.

The
G
uided
Rep
lacement (
GRep
) module recovers the full-rank feature
\hat{F}
using Equation
3
. Figure
7a
depicts this information retrieval process. First, we perform element-wise multiplication between the information entropy
I(\tilde{F})
and the low-rank feature set
\tilde{F}
to retrieve the available information in the low-rank feature set
\hat{F}_{\tilde{X}}
.

Next, we find the missing information matrix
\hat{F}_{Z|\tilde{X}}
from the interpolated feature
\bar{\bar{F}}
. Here,
Z
represents the missing data in
\tilde{S}
. As shown in Figure

, we take the complement of the element-wise information matrix
I(\tilde{F})
and multiply it element-wise with
\bar{\bar{F}}
to find the missing information, disregarding regions (dotted darker green) where errors might have been introduced during interpolation.

Finally, GRep utilizes the available information matrix
\hat{F}_{\tilde{X}}
and the missing information matrix
\hat{F}_{Z|\tilde{X}}
to estimate the full-rank feature
\hat{F}
from Equation

5.2.
Data Preparation

Feature Extraction.

In our approach to sound source localization, we utilize two key acoustic features: Mel-Frequency Cepstral Coefficients (MFCC) and Generalized Cross Correlation (GCC). We extract these features from the raw audio data using a 1024-point Fast Fourier Transform (FFT) with a 40 ms window length and a 20 ms hop length at sampling rates of 24 kHz and 44.1 kHz. MFCC, calculated from 64 Mel bands, captures magnitude-based audio information, while GCC represents the relative time difference and phase between audio channels, crucial for determining the spatial origin of sound. We chose these features because MFCC provides important spectral details for distinguishing sound sources, while GCC captures spatial timing differences, enabling accurate localization. After truncating the GCC sequences to match the length of the Mel bins, we combine the two features into a feature matrix,
\tilde{F}
, which serves as input to deep learning pipeline for robust sound source localization. Integrating MFCC and GCC features enhances the network’s ability to accurately localize sound sources in complex acoustic environments.

Missing Channel Perturbation

To prepare data with missing microphone channels, we randomly select
m\leq\frac{n}{2}
channels from a total of
n
microphones, where
m
represents the number of malfunctioning or missing channels. For each Missing Data Percentage (MDP), we replace
p\%
of the original audio with Gaussian noise, characterized by a mean of 0 and a standard deviation of 1. This noise simulates signal distortion or degraded microphone output. In addition, we simulate channel failures by randomly dropping entire channels for certain time frames (100% MDP), effectively mimicking intermittent outages where all microphones temporarily stop recording.

These perturbation techniques, including the introduction of Gaussian noise and random channel drops, ensure the model can handle real-world scenarios where microphone channels may fail or capture distorted signals.

5.3.
Implementation Details

Training Pipeline of
LOCUS
.

We train
LOCUS
jointly with the DNN-based sound source localization (SSL) task. The loss function for the SSL task, denoted as
\mathcal{L}_{DT}
, backpropagates through the SSL network and the channel and point-wise information branches (
f_{\gamma}
and
f_{\lambda}
) of InFo. This ensures that the SSL task benefits from the enriched features provided by these branches.

However, the LaFS branch (
f_{\phi}
) has a separate training objective which is to estimate the input features. The loss function for DCI,
\mathcal{L}_{DCI}
, is defined as the squared
L_{2}
distance between the original feature matrix
F
and the reconstructed feature matrix
\bar{\bar{F}}
:

(5)

\mathcal{L}_{DCI}=||F-\bar{\bar{F}}||^{2}

This loss function ensures that the reconstructed features closely match the original features, promoting consistency and robustness in the data representation.

To optimize the training process, we use two different optimizers: one for training InFo and the SSL task with
\mathcal{L}_{DT}
, and another for training the LaFS branch with
\mathcal{L}_{DCI}
. This dual-optimizer approach allows us to fine-tune each component of the system independently, ensuring that both the SSL task and the LaFS branch achieve optimal performance.

Sound Source Localization Pipeline.

We implement two DNN-based SLL solutions to estimate the Direction of Arrival (DoA) of sound sources using multi-channel audio – SELDNet
(
Shimada et al. 2021
;
Adavanne et al. 2018
)
and SALSA
(
Nguyen et al. 2022
)
.
SELDNet and SALSA both use MFCC as described in Section
5.2
.

SELDNet
is a Convolutional Recurrent Neural Network (CRNN)
(
Cao et al. 2019
)
, combining CNNs for spatial feature extraction and RNNs for temporal dependencies. A fully connected (FC) layer estimates source locations in Cartesian coordinates, providing precise DoA estimates.

SALSA
is an advanced SSL algorithm featuring a CRNN model with a CNN backbone based on the PANN ResNet22
(
Kong et al. 2020
)
architecture for audio tagging. It includes a two-layer Bidirectional GRU and fully connected output layers. The algorithm adapts to various input features by adjusting the number of input channels in the first convolutional layer. During inference, sound classes with probabilities above the detection threshold are considered active, and their corresponding DOAs are selected.

5.4.
Evaluation Baseline

We rigorously evaluate
LOCUS
by benchmarking it against four established imputation methods and one deep interpolation technique. The selected baselines encompass a spectrum of strategies, from straightforward imputation techniques to sophisticated neural network models, ensuring a thorough and robust comparison. This comprehensive evaluation framework allows us to precisely assess the effectiveness and adaptability of
LOCUS
in handling missing data scenarios, highlighting its strengths and potential advantages over existing solutions.

Imputation Methods.

We benchmark
LOCUS
against three popular signal processing and two state-of-the-art deep learning imputation methods, one of which is specifically designed for acoustic signals. Each method is selected for its unique approach and effectiveness in handling missing data. Additionally, we evaluate each method on both pre-trained and retrained SSL networks.

\bullet

Mean Imputation (
Mean
).

Missing channel information is replaced with the mean of the available channels’ information
(
Allison 2001
)
. This method is chosen for its simplicity and widespread use as a baseline in missing data problems.

\bullet

Hot Deck Imputation (
HotDeck
).

This method fills in missing channel information using data from the most correlated channels
(
Little and Rubin 2019
)
. This method leverages the inherent similarity between channels to estimate missing data, offering a more informed and context-aware alternative to mean imputation.

\bullet

Probabilistic Imputation (
Prob
).

Missing channel information is estimated using probabilistic methods
(
Fekade et al. 2018
)
. By incorporating probabilistic models, it can better handle the complexity and randomness inherent in real-world datasets, making it a robust choice for imputation.

\bullet

Deep Interpolation (
AutoEnc
).

We leverage the interpolation ability of autoencoders (AutoEnc) to interpolate the complex missing information and then use the interpolated feature as the input for SSL network. We compare against this method to evaluate the effectiveness of deep learning-based interpolation in handling missing data, ensuring that our model’s performance is benchmarked against advanced techniques that utilize neural networks for data reconstruction.

\bullet

SoundSeive
.

We apply a state-of-the-art hierarchical interpolation method for acoustic signals, as proposed by SoundSeive
(
Monjur et al. 2023
)
. Unlike previous time-domain imputation methods, this approach leverages deep learning techniques in the Short-Time Fourier Transform (STFT) domain. By working in the frequency domain,
SoundSeive
effectively captures and reconstructs complex spectral patterns, leading to a more accurate and robust imputation of missing data. We validate this method to benchmark
LOCUS
against cutting-edge deep learning approaches, ensuring our model’s performance is compared to the best techniques in both time and frequency domains.

Evaluation Networks.

We evaluate each imputation method on two types of networks – (1) pretrained and (2) retrained.

\bullet

Pre-trained (
PreTrain
)
. The SSL network (e.g., SELDNet) is trained without any missing channel information (
MDP=0\%
) and then tested on data with
p=\{
10, 25, 50, 75, 100}% missing timeframes. This baseline helps us understand the performance degradation due to missing channels when the model is not explicitly trained to handle such scenarios.

\bullet

Retrained (
ReTrain
).

The SSL network is retrained on corrupt data (
Corrupt
) or imputed data (
mean
,
HotDeck
,
SoundSieve
, and
AutoEnc
) with missing channels for
p
% of the time (e.g., MDP=
75
%). Retraining with corrupt data evaluates the model’s performance when it is specifically trained to handle missing data, providing a more robust comparison. It demonstrates whether training the model with data that includes missing channels is sufficient. On the other hand, retraining with imputed data Missing data is imputed data evaluates the effectiveness of these strategies in conjunction with retraining.

5.5.
Evaluation Metrics

We evaluate the performance of our sound event localization on the
Degree of Arrival Estimation Error (
E_{DoA}
)
of the entire dataset.

E_{DoA}
is a suitable metric for sound source localization because it directly measures the angular difference between the estimated and true locations of sound sources. This angular error provides a clear indication of how accurately the localization system can determine the direction of incoming sounds, which is crucial for applications that rely on precise spatial audio information.

Ablation Study

This section evaluates the contribution of each component of
LOCUS
using the DCASE dataset and SELDNet SSL network.

Effect of InFo.

To understand the impact of InFo, we evaluate
LOCUS
without LaFS, leaving only InFo and the SSL network. Figure
15
shows that this configuration reduces the
E_{DoA}
by
22.85\pm 10.57\%
compared to retrained network with corrupt data. Adding InFo enhances performance by focusing attention on the elements of the feature matrix (
\tilde{F}
) that contain more information. However, without LaFS to estimate the values of the missing elements in the full-rank version,
F
, this setup performs
14.05\pm 8.92\%
worse than the full
LOCUS
implementation.

Effect of LaFS.

Next, we investigate the effect of LaFS alone by removing InFo from
LOCUS
. This examination reveals the ability of an interpolator to recover complex missing information and the extent of additional errors introduced during interpolation. Figure
15
shows that LaFS alone reduces
E_{DoA}
by
16.22\pm 8.87\%
compared to retrained network with corrupt data. However, while LaFS estimates the full-rank features (
\bar{\bar{F}}
), it unintentionally introduces errors in elements that are less affected by missing data. The inclusion of InFo helps mitigate this by providing information entropy, which allows the replacement of these polluted components with their original elements, thereby reducing the overall error.

Effect of GRep.

GRep in
LOCUS
combines the information entropy,
I(\tilde{F})
from InFo with the interpolated full-rank feature
\bar{\bar{F}}
from LaFS. As previously discussed, InFo estimates the available information matrix,
\hat{F}
from
{\tilde{X}}
, while LaFS interpolates to estimate the full-rank feature,
\bar{\bar{F}}
. For feature elements in
\hat{F}_{\tilde{X}}
significantly impacted by missing channels, GRep replaces them with interpolated elements from
\bar{\bar{F}}
. Conversely, for elements less affected by missing channels, GRep preserves the original features from
\hat{F}_{\tilde{X}}
using InFo. This method ensures a better estimation of
\hat{F}
, enabling
LOCUS
to outperform both InFo and LaFS alone by
16.22\pm 8.87\%
and
22.85\pm 10.57\%
, respectively, as shown in Figure
15
. These observations confirm that retraining the baseline model alone is insufficient and that using InFo or LaFS independently is inadequate. Thus, all three components of
LOCUS
are crucial for recovering corrupted information.

8.
Related Work

Intermittent System
.

Initial research in intermittent computing emphasized maintaining data consistency and integrity under unreliable power conditions. Systems such as Mementos
(
Ransford et al. 2011
)
introduced checkpointing strategies to periodically store system states, thus mitigating data and computation loss due to power interruptions. Although checkpointing reduces data loss, it does not fully address the issue of missing data caused by power interruptions between checkpoint intervals. Recent systems, such as Greentooth
(
Babatunde et al. 2024
)
, propose resilient architectures for energy-harvesting devices to better withstand power outages. Other recent works, including FreeML
(
Farina et al. 2024
)
and SONIC
(
Gobieski et al. 2018
)
, explore efficient inference of deep neural network (DNN) models on intermittent systems. In addition,
(
Majid et al. 2020b
)
demonstrate audio sensing capabilities on intermittently-powered devices; however, their system only captures audio samples of short events ( 283 ms), which remains insufficient for many real-world applications requiring extended and continuous data collection.

Missing Information Retrieval
.
To recover missing information from sensors, statistical methods
(
Jerez et al. 2010
)
like mean imputation
(
Allison 2001
)
, hot deck
(
Little and Rubin 2019
)
, and multiple imputations
(
Rubin 1996
)
are often used. However, these methods focus on single-channel data and fail for long missing data sequences
(
Hasan et al. 2021
)
.

In the deep learning world, missing information retrieval from a single sensor has been studied by the Natural Language Processing (NLP) and Computer Vision (CV) Community. While works in the NLP domain focus on predicting missing words and syllables in texts
(
Katwe et al. 2020
)
, while CV works focus on restoring old images
(
Zhang et al. 2017
)
, removing obstacles
(
Christiansen et al. 2016
)
, or eliminating raindrops
(
Peng et al. 2020
)
. Some other works focus on learning the input distribution
(
Alemi et al. 2016
)
to denoise the noisy inputs but can not handle the missing information. The most related work to acoustic missing information recovery is SoundSieve
(
Monjur et al. 2023
)
, which recovers missing segments from single-channel audio that occurred due to energy intermittence for a sound event classification task. However, this method does not consider multiple channels and takes phase information into account for imputation. Few works on speech enhancements have looked into recovering corrupted multi-channel data
(
Taherian et al. 2022
)
. However, unlike source localization, speech enhancement only partially depends on hard-to-recover spatial information and thus can not be directly used for localization tasks. Though some recent works focus on recovering missing modalities in multimodal sensing
(
Ma et al. 2022
;
Ma et al. 2021
)
, no work focuses on interrelated sensors of a single modality.

Sound Source Localization
.
Classic sound source localization (SSL) methods, e.g., MUSIC
(
Gupta and Kar 2015
)
, independent component analysis
(
Noohi et al. 2013
)
, and sparse models
(
Yang et al. 2018
)
, perform poorly for under-determined scenarios. Though DNN has shown promising results for SSL
(
Ferguson et al. 2018
;
Yiwere and Rhee 2017
;
Hirvonen 2015
)
, none of the existing works focus on missing or corrupted information.

Attention Mechanism
.
Attention learns to focus on essential information and ignore the rest selectively
(
Colombini et al. 2014
)
. For multi-channel input, attention explores the channel characteristics and estimates the channel state information
(
Gao et al. 2021
)
. Though commonly used later in a network, early attention improves performance with substantial margins
(
Hajavi and Etemad 2020
)
motivating our InFo component.

Discussions and Limitations

This section describes the design choices and limitations and future directions of
LOCUS
.

Figure 18.
Effect of retraining the downstream task.

Impact of Joint Training
.

LOCUS
requires joint training of InFo, LaFS, and the sound source localization network to effectively recover missing information. To assess the need for retraining, we tested a setup where InFo and LaFS were added to a pre-trained, frozen SELDNet and compared it to
LOCUS
with a jointly trained localization network. Figure
18
shows that the pre-trained network increases
E_{DoA}
by
14.3-103.08\%
compared to no missing channels, while
LOCUS
reduces
E_{DoA}
by
1.9-16.62\%
. This is because InFo alters the feature distribution, requiring the localization network to adapt during training, making retraining essential. Future work will explore domain generalization to potentially eliminate the need for retraining and make
LOCUS
a plug-and-play solution for missing information recovery.

Generative Models for Information Retrieval.
.
Generative models like VAEs and GANs have shown promise in recovering information, but VAEs often struggle with reconstruction accuracy and diversity due to their simplified assumptions, making them inadequate for complex, discrete data. GANs, while powerful, require large datasets and are sensitive to hyper-parameters, making them difficult to train in dynamic environments. While these models could replace the auto-encoder in LaFS, we find that a simpler, easier-to-train auto-encoder performs comparably with less overhead, making it the better choice for multi-channel information retrieval in
LOCUS
.

LOCUS
for Multi-Sensor Systems
.
Multi-modal systems involve data from different sources, such as video, audio, and text, which may or may not be interrelated. In contrast, multi-sensor data, like a 12-lead ECG, collects interrelated data from multiple channels. This paper demonstrates
LOCUS
’s effectiveness in a multi-sensor setup and suggests its potential expansion into multi-modal domains. For example, in video conferencing,
LOCUS
could retrieve corrupted audio data from video and text to enhance overall quality.

Microphone Geometry Dependency
.

LOCUS
operates on top of existing sound source localization methods and, like them, relies on the microphone-array geometry through time difference of arrival (TDoA) and triangulation. As a result, it performs poorly when used with a different geometry, requiring retraining each time the geometry changes. For example, the network trained in section
6.2
cannot be applied directly in section
7
. Our future work plans to explore domain generalization techniques to address this dependency.

Large Real-World Localization Dataset
.
In the absence of large public datasets for sound source localization, we created and released a 50-hour simulated dataset. While useful for training DNNs, simulated data may lack the diversity and complexity of real-world conditions due to inherent assumptions. Future work will focus on developing a comprehensive real-world sound source localization dataset.

Image Recovery Limitations for Acoustic Features
.
While deep learning methods for image pixel recovery are effective
(
Zhang et al. 2017
;
Chen et al. 2022
)
, they may not work for frequency domain features like GCC and MFCC. Unlike images, where neighboring pixels often share similar values, these acoustic features lack such similarity, making image recovery methods unsuitable for restoring missing acoustic channel information.

10.
Conclusion

In this paper, we develop an information retrieval-based sound source localization system that can hold its performance in the presence of missing or corrupted data. We leverage the interdependence among different random variables among the channels to recover missing information. Meanwhile, classical algorithms to handle missing data points are insufficient due to their limitations, e.g., the requirement of prior knowledge and performance degradation on high and complex data distributions.
LOCUS
overcomes these challenges by proposing InFo, responsible to quantify
how
much and
where
the corruptions are and LaFS to
interpolate
them. Our evaluation shows that our proposed
LOCUS
can achieve up to
36.91\%
less degree of arrival error than just retraining the localization model using data with missing channels on DCASE dataset. Our real-world evaluation shows
25.87-59.46\%
performance gain in stochastically missing information.
