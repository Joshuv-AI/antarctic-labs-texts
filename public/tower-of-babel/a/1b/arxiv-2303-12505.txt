Collective vs. individual behaviour for sums of i.i.d. random variables: appearance of the one-big-jump phenomenon
Quentin Berger , Matthias Birkner and Linglong Yuan

Abstract
This article studies large and local large deviations for sums of i.i.d. real-valued random variables in the domain of attraction of an α \alpha -stable law, α ∈ ( 0 , 2 ] \alpha\in(0,2] , with emphasis on the case α = 2 \alpha=2 . There are two different scenarios: either the deviation is realised via a collective behaviour with all summands contributing to the deviation (a Gaussian scenario), or a single summand is atypically large and contributes to the deviation (a one-big-jump scenario). Such results are known when α ∈ ( 0 , 2 ) \alpha\in(0,2) (large deviations always follow a one big-jump scenario) or when the random variables admit a moment of order 2 + δ 2+\delta for some δ > 0 \delta>0 . We extend these results, including in particular the case where the right tail is regularly varying with index − 2 -2 (treating cases with infinite variance in the domain of attraction of the normal law). We identify the threshold for the transition between the Gaussian and the one-big-jump regimes; it is slightly larger when considering local large deviations compared to integral large deviations. Additionally, we complement our results by describing the behaviour of the sum and of the largest summand conditionally on a (local) large deviation, for any α ∈ ( 0 , 2 ] \alpha\in(0,2] , both in the Gaussian and in the one-big-jump regimes. As an application, we show how our results can be used in the study of condensation phenomenon in the zero-range process at the critical density, extending the range of parameters previously considered in the literature.

Large deviation, Local large deviation, Extended & intermediate regular variation, Phase transition, One-big-jump phenomenon, Condensation,

Introduction

Let
\xi
be a real-valued
random variable and let
\xi_{1},\xi_{2},\ldots
be independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) copies drawn from the distribution of
\xi
. We denote, for
x\in\mathbb{R}
,

F(x):=\mathbb{P}(\xi\leq x),\qquad\overline{F}(x):=1-F(x)\,.

The general theme of the present paper is to study the interplay between

S_{n}:=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\xi_{i}\quad\text{and}\quad M_{n}:=\max\{\xi_{1},\xi_{2},\ldots,\xi_{n}\},

when one of them is atypically large.

We assume that
\xi
is in the domain of attraction of an
\alpha
-stable law, with
\alpha\in(0,2]
,
i.e.
that there exist sequences
(a_{n})_{n\geq 1},(b_{n})_{n\geq 1}
such that
(S_{n})_{n\geq 1}

satisfies

\frac{S_{n}-b_{n}}{a_{n}}\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{d}\mathcal{S}_{\alpha},

(1.1)

where
\mathcal{S}_{\alpha}
is an
\alpha
-stable random variable and
\xrightarrow{d}
denotes convergence in distribution.
From Feller
[
Fel08
, Ch. XVII.5, Thm. 2]
, a necessary and sufficient condition is the following:

•

If
\alpha\in(0,2)
: there is a slowly varying function
L(\cdot)
such that

\overline{F}(x)\sim pL(x)x^{-\alpha}\,,\quad F(-x)\sim qL(x)x^{-\alpha}\quad\text{ as }x\to\infty\,,

(1.2)

with
p,q\geq 0
,
p+q=1
; if
p=0
, we interpret (
1.2
) as
\overline{F}(x)=o(L(x)x^{-\alpha})
and similarly if
q=0
.
Note that in this case we have that
\overline{F}(x)+F(-x)\sim L(x)x^{-\alpha}
as
x\to\infty
.

•

If
\alpha=2
:
\xi
has a finite expectation, that we denote by
\mu=\mathbb{E}[\xi]
, and the truncated variance

\sigma^{2}(x):=\mathbb{E}\big[(\xi-\mu)^{2}{\text{\Large$\mathfrak{1}$}}_{\{|\xi-\mu|\leq x\}}\big]\,,\quad x\geq 0

(1.3)

is slowly varying as
x\to\infty
. Note that this contains the case of a finite second moment,
i.e.

\lim_{x\to\infty}\sigma^{2}(x)=\mathbb{E}[(\xi-\mu)^{2}]=:\sigma^{2}
.

In the case
\alpha=2
, let us recall the fact that (see
[
Fel08
, Ch. IX.8, Eq. (8.5)]
)

\overline{F}(x)+F(-x)=o(x^{-2}\sigma^{2}(x)),\quad\text{ as }x\to\infty.

(1.4)

The normalising sequence
(a_{n})_{n\geq 1}
verifies, as
n\to\infty

L(a_{n})a_{n}^{-\alpha}\sim n^{-1}\ \ \text{ if }\alpha\in(0,2);\qquad\sigma^{2}(a_{n})a_{n}^{-2}\sim n^{-1}\ \ \text{ if }\alpha=2\,,

(1.5)

and the centering sequence
(b_{n})_{n\geq 1}
is given by

b_{n}=0\ \text{ if }\alpha\in(0,1);\quad b_{n}=n\mathbb{E}\left[\xi{\text{\Large$\mathfrak{1}$}}_{\{|\xi|\leq a_{n}\}}\right]\ \text{ if }\alpha=1;\quad b_{n}=n\mathbb{E}[\xi]\ \text{ if }\alpha\in(1,2]\,.

(1.6)

Note that we can also replace (
1.5
) by

L(a_{n})a_{n}^{-\alpha}\sim an^{-1},\sigma^{2}(a_{n})a_{n}^{-2}\sim an^{-1}
in the different cases for any
a>0
. The value of
a
only changes the law of
\mathcal{S}_{\alpha}
by a dilation factor.

The one-big-jump phenomenon

The “one-big-jump phenomenon” asserts that the large deviation event of having
S_{n}
unusually large
may be realised essentially thanks to exactly one of the variables
\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{n}
being very large.
This is captured in the large deviation behaviour of the probability

\mathbb{P}(S_{n}-b_{n}\geq x_{n})\sim\mathbb{P}(S_{n}-b_{n}\geq x_{n},M_{n}\geq x_{n})\sim\mathbb{P}(M_{n}\geq x_{n})\quad\text{as }n\to\infty\,,

(1.7)

where
x_{n}
is a sequence going to infinity. We also refer to Theorem
2.10
and Corollary
3.2
below for a more detailed interpretation of (
1.7
).
Finding conditions on the distribution of
\xi
and on the sequence
(x_{n})_{n\geq 1}
for (
1.7
) to hold has a long story, in particular within the class of subexponential distributions

The one-big-jump phenomenon occurs in a large deviation regime,

, which is equivalent to
\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{x_{n}}{a_{n}}=\infty

Example 1.1
.

As a first example, consider a centered random variable
\xi
, i.e.
\mu=\mathbb{E}[\xi]=0
, with finite variance
\sigma^{2}=\operatorname{Var}(\xi)

\overline{F}(x):=\mathbb{P}(\xi>x)\sim L(x)x^{-\beta}\quad\text{as }x\to\infty\,,

and some slowly varying function

In this case, Nagaev

\mathbb{E}[|\xi|^{2+\delta}]<\infty

, then for any sequence
(x_{n})_{n\geq 1}
such that

, we have

\mathbb{P}(S_{n}\geq x_{n})=(1+o(1))\overline{\Phi}(\tfrac{x_{n}}{\sigma\sqrt{n}})+(1+o(1))n\overline{F}(x_{n}),\quad n\to\infty

(1.9)

where

is the tail probability function of the standard normal distribution.
Let us set

\gamma_{n}:=\frac{x_{n}^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}n}-\Big(\frac{\beta}{2}-1\Big)\log n-\frac{1}{2}(\beta-1)\log\log n+\log L(\sqrt{n\log n})\,,

so that in particular
x_{n}^{2}\sim\sigma^{2}(\beta-2)n\log n
if
\gamma_{n}=o(\log n)
(which is the most interesting case).
Then, using the asymptotics
\overline{\Phi}(t)\sim\frac{1}{t\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-t^{2}/2}
as
t\to\infty
, a straightforward (but tedious) calculation gives that if
\lim_{n\to\infty}\gamma_{n}=\gamma_{\infty}\in[-\infty,\infty]
, we obtain

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\mathbb{P}(S_{n}\geq x_{n})}{n\overline{F}(x_{n})}=1+c_{\beta,\sigma}e^{-\gamma_{\infty}},\quad\text{ with }c_{\beta,\sigma}=\frac{\sigma^{\beta}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}(\beta-2)^{(\beta-1)/2}\,.

In particular, the one-big-jump phenomenon (
1.7
) holds if and only if
\lim_{n\to\infty}\gamma_{n}=\infty
. We will discuss how the condition that
\mathbb{E}[|\xi|^{2+\delta}]<\infty
for some
\delta>0
(which in this example, in view of (
1.8
), is a condition on the left tail) and also the regular variation condition (
1.8
) for the right tail can be weakened, see
[
Roz90
, Thm. 6]
or Theorem
2.1
below.

First set of results: Large deviations and conditional laws

We now collect a few results on large deviation probabilities for
S_{n}
. Our main assumption, aside from the fact that
\xi
is in the domain of attraction of a normal law, will be that
\overline{F}
is intermediate regularly varying,
i.e.
unless otherwise specified we assume that
\overline{F}
satisfies (
2.11
).
We will point out explicitly when we need the stronger condition (
2.10
) of extended regular variation.
In all the following, we assume that
\alpha=2
and we let
(a_{n})_{n\geq 1}
be a normalising sequence as in (
1.5
).

2.2.1
Large deviations

Let us consider a function
r:(0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
that satisfies the asymptotic relation
\frac{r(t)}{\sigma^{2}(r(t))}\sim t
as
t\to\infty
.
Such a function exists because
\sigma^{2}(x)
is slowly varying at infinity and thus we have
\lim_{t\to\infty}r(t)=\infty
.

Then for any positive sequence
(x_{n})_{n\geq 1}
we define

r_{n}:=r\Big(\frac{n}{x_{n}}\Big).

(2.12)

In fact, we will mostly focus on the case where
\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{x_{n}}{n}=0
, in which case
r_{n}
verifies

\lim_{n\to\infty}r_{n}=\infty\,;\qquad\frac{r_{n}}{\sigma^{2}(r_{n})}\sim\frac{n}{x_{n}}\,.

(2.13)

In order to better explain the estimates of
\mathbb{P}(S_{n}-b_{n}\geq x_{n})
, we make the following decomposition:

\mathbb{P}(S_{n}-b_{n}\geq x_{n})=\mathbb{P}(S_{n}-b_{n}\geq x_{n},M_{n}\leq r_{n})+\mathbb{P}(S_{n}-b_{n}\geq x_{n},M_{n}>r_{n}).

(2.14)

Analysis of the first term on the r.h.s. of (
2.14
).

Let us introduce, for
u\geq 0
, the tilted (and truncated) distribution
\mathbb{P}_{u}=\mathbb{P}_{u}^{(r_{n})}
defined by

\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}_{u}}{\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}}(x)=\frac{1}{M(u)}e^{u(x-\mu)}{\text{\Large$\mathfrak{1}$}}_{(-\infty,r_{n}]}(x)\quad\text{ with }M(u)=M_{r_{n}}(u):=\mathbb{E}\left[e^{u(\xi-\mu)}{\text{\Large$\mathfrak{1}$}}_{\{\xi\leq r_{n}\}}\right]\,.

(2.15)

Define also, for
u\geq 0
,

m(u)=\frac{M^{\prime}(u)}{M(u)}=\mathbb{E}_{u}[\xi-\mu]

(2.16)

and its inverse (
u\mapsto m(u)
is increasing since
m^{\prime}(u)=\mathbb{V}\mathrm{ar}_{u}(\xi)>0
for any
u\geq 0
)

\lambda(t):=m^{-1}(t)

(2.17)

for
t\geq\mathbb{E}_{0}[\xi-\mu]
(note that
\mathbb{E}_{0}[\xi-\mu]<0
), so that
\mathbb{E}_{\lambda(t)}[\xi]=\mu+t
.
Finally, let

H(t)=H_{r_{n}}(t):=-\log M(\lambda(t))+t\lambda(t)\,,

(2.18)

which is the relative entropy of
\mathbb{P}_{\lambda(t)}
w.r.t. to
\mathbb{P}
. Note that we allow a small abuse of notation here since
\mathbb{P}_{u}
will later refer both to the law of a single variable and to the law of
n
i.i.d. copies of that variable.
With this notation we can state the following result.

Proposition 2.4
.

Let
\alpha=2
and
(a_{n})_{n\geq 1}
be a normalising sequence as in (
1.5
). Let
(x_{n})_{n\geq 1}
be a sequence such that
x_{n}\geq a_{n}
and
\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{x_{n}}{n}=0
, and let
r_{n}
be as in (
2.12
). Then

\mathbb{P}(S_{n}-b_{n}\geq x_{n},M_{n}\leq r_{n})\sim\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\frac{a_{n}}{x_{n}}\sqrt{\frac{\sigma^{2}(r_{n})}{\sigma^{2}(a_{n})}}\,e^{-nH(\frac{x_{n}}{n})}\quad\text{ as }n\to\infty\,.

(2.19)

Remark 2.5
.

Let us stress that if
a_{n}\leq x_{n}\leq Ca_{n}\sqrt{|\log q(a_{n})|}
for some constant
C>0
, then assuming that
\overline{F}
is intermediate regularly varying (see (
2.11
)) we get that
\sigma^{2}(r_{n})\sim\sigma^{2}(a_{n})
, see Remark
A.9
in the Appendix.
Note also that (
2.19
) falls into the scope of the central limit theorem when
x_{n}=O(a_{n})
.

We also provide the following lemma that estimates the relative entropy
nH(\frac{x_{n}}{n})
.

Lemma 2.6
.

Under the assumptions of Proposition
2.4

we have

nH\left(\frac{x_{n}}{n}\right)=(1+o(1))\frac{1}{2}\frac{x_{n}^{2}}{n\sigma^{2}(r_{n})}=(1+o(1))\frac{1}{2}\frac{x_{n}^{2}}{a_{n}^{2}}\frac{\sigma^{2}(a_{n})}{\sigma^{2}(r_{n})}.

Remark 2.7
.

Note that if
\frac{x_{n}}{a_{n}}=O(1)
, then
nH(\frac{x_{n}}{n})=(1+o(1))\frac{x_{n}^{2}}{2a_{n}^{2}}=\frac{x_{n}^{2}}{2a_{n}^{2}}+o(1)
(also using that
\sigma^{2}(r_{n})\sim\sigma^{2}(a_{n})
, see Remark
2.5
).
Rozovskii’s theorem actually tells that, if
\overline{F}
is extended regularly varying, see (
2.10
), then condition (
2.4
) is a criterion to ensure that
nH(\frac{x_{n}}{n})=\frac{x_{n}^{2}}{2a_{n}^{2}}+o(1)
uniformly for
a_{n}\leq x_{n}\leq Ca_{n}\sqrt{|\log q(a_{n})|}
; the r.h.s. of (
2.19
) is then asymptotically equivalent to
\overline{\Phi}(\frac{x_{n}}{a_{n}})
. Note that the threshold
a_{n}\sqrt{|\log q(a_{n})|}
appears when comparing
\overline{\Phi}(\frac{x_{n}}{a_{n}})
with
n\overline{F}(x_{n})
: if
x_{n}\geq Ca_{n}\sqrt{|\log q(a_{n})|}
with
C>\sqrt{2}
then (
2.19
) becomes negligible compared to
n\overline{F}(x_{n})
, see Remark
A.6
in the Appendix.

Analysis of the second term on the r.h.s. of (
2.14
).

For the remaining term in (
2.14
), we have the following result.

Proposition 2.8
.

Let
\alpha=2
and
(a_{n})_{n\geq 1}
be a normalising sequence as in (
1.5
).
Assume that
\overline{F}
is intermediate regularly varying, see (
2.11
).
If
\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{x_{n}}{a_{n}}=\infty
and
\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{x_{n}}{n}=0
, then letting
r_{n}
be as in (
2.12
), we have

\mathbb{P}(S_{n}-b_{n}\geq x_{n},M_{n}>r_{n})\sim\mathbb{P}(S_{n}-b_{n}\geq x_{n},M_{n}\geq x_{n})\,,

or equivalently

\mathbb{P}(S_{n}-b_{n}\geq x_{n})=\mathbb{P}(S_{n}-b_{n}\geq x_{n},M_{n}\leq r_{n})+(1+o(1))n\overline{F}(x_{n})\,.

(2.20)

If
\liminf_{n\to\infty}\frac{x_{n}}{n}>0
, then

\mathbb{P}(S_{n}-b_{n}\geq x_{n})\sim\mathbb{P}(S_{n}-b_{n}\geq x_{n},M_{n}\geq x_{n})\sim n\overline{F}(x_{n})
.

The statement with
\liminf_{n\to\infty}\frac{x_{n}}{n}>0
is standard, see e.g.
[
DDS08
]
. We include it in the proposition for the sake of completeness.

Remark 2.9
.

Denisov, Dieker and Shneer
[
DDS08
]
give conditions for the
appearance of the one-big-jump phenomenon in the general context of
subexponential distributions. Since we work here under much more restricted assumptions, we are able to
give sharper conditions for the minimal size of
x_{n}
to be in
the one-big-jump regime for specific examples:

[
DDS08
]
consider in Section 8.2 centered
\xi
’s with
\mathbb{E}[\xi^{2}]=1
and
certain assumptions on
\overline{F}
.
If we assume, as in
[
DDS08
, Thm. 8.1]
, that
\overline{F}
is in fact intermediate regularly varying and also that (
2.7
) holds (which is in this case equivalent to
(
2.4
)), we can combine Proposition
2.8
, Proposition
2.4
and
Remark
2.7
(see also Remark
A.6
in the Appendix) to see that

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\sup_{y\geq ca_{n}\sqrt{|\log q(a_{n})|}}\Big|\frac{\mathbb{P}(S_{n}>y)}{n\overline{F}(y)}-1\Big|\;\begin{cases}=0,&\text{if }c>\sqrt{2},\\
>0,&\text{if }0\leq c<\sqrt{2}.\end{cases}

Using the nomenclature from
[
DDS08
]
, we see that in this case if we set

x_{n}=ca_{n}\sqrt{|\log q(a_{n})|}
with
c>\sqrt{2}
and define
r_{n}
via (
2.13
), then

(r_{n})
is a “truncation sequence” and

(x_{n})
is a “
(r_{n})
-small-steps sequence”
(we can use
\sqrt{n}
as the “natural-scale sequence” and any sequence
(I_{n})
with
\sqrt{n}/I_{n}\to 0

as “insensitivity sequence”).

If in this situation we impose that
\overline{F}
is in fact regularly varying, we can read off the
precise behaviour from Example
2.2
: A given sequence
(x_{n})
is a
small-steps sequence in the sense of
[
DDS08
]
if and only if the sequence
(\gamma_{n})
defined in
(
2.9
) diverges. An analogous situation occurs in the local case, see Example
2.17
.

2.2.2
Conditional laws on large deviation events

Using Propositions
2.4
and
2.8
(and their proofs), we can make the one-big-jump phenomenon more precise:
The intuition behind (
1.7
) is of course that only one random variable absorbs the large deviation, without effectively changing the distribution of the
n-1
others. The following results make the fact that the remaining random variables are “left untouched” explicit by showing that their distribution is close to that of
n-1
i.i.d. random variables with the same law as
\xi
.

For
y=(y_{1},\dots,y_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we define
R(y)\in\mathbb{R}^{n-1}
, the vector obtained by removing the
i
-th coordinate from
y
, where
i
is the index for which the maximum
\max_{1\leq j\leq n}|y_{j}|
is attained

i.e.

|y_{i}|=\max_{1\leq j\leq n}|y_{j}|=:M_{n}(y)
. In case of a tie for the maximum, we take away the variable with the index
\min\{i:y_{i}=M_{n}(y)\}
. In fact, other choices are possible and will lead to the same limit behaviour as in Theorem
2.10
and in Proposition
4.1
below. In the following, we write
\mathscr{L}(X)
to denote the law of a random variable (or a vector of random variables)
X
.

Theorem 2.10

(One-big-jump phenomenon)
.

Let
\alpha=2
and
(a_{n})_{n\geq 1}
be a normalising sequence as in (
1.5
).
Assume that
\overline{F}
is intermediate regularly varying, see (
2.11
). If
\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{x_{n}}{a_{n}}=\infty
holds, and then letting
r_{n}
be as in (
2.12
), we have

\lim_{n\to\infty}d_{\mathrm{TV}}\Big(\mathscr{L}\big(R(\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{n})\,\big|\,S_{n}-b_{n}\geq x_{n},M_{n}>r_{n}\big),\,\big(\mathscr{L}(\xi)\big)^{\otimes(n-1)}\Big)=0,

(2.21)

\lim_{n\to\infty}d_{\mathrm{TV}}\Big(\mathscr{L}\big(R(\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{n})\,\big|\,S_{n}-b_{n}\geq x_{n},M_{n}\leq r_{n}\big),\,\big(\mathscr{L}(\xi)\big)^{\otimes(n-1)}\Big)=1,

(2.22)

where
d_{\mathrm{TV}}
denotes the total variation distance.

As a consequence, if in addition
(x_{n})
satisfies
\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{n\overline{F}(x_{n})}{\mathbb{P}(S_{n}\geq x_{n})}=s\in[0,1]
,
then we have

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}d_{\mathrm{TV}}\Big(\mathscr{L}\big(R(\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{n})\,\big|\,S_{n}-b_{n}\geq x_{n}\big),\,\big(\mathscr{L}(\xi)\big)^{\otimes(n-1)}\Big)=1-s\,.

(2.23)

If we assume further that
\overline{F}
is extended regularly varying (see (
2.10
)) and that (
2.4
) holds, then
s=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{n\overline{F}(x_{n})}{n\overline{F}(x_{n})+\overline{\Phi}(\frac{x_{n}}{a_{n}})}
.

For the value of
s
in the context of Example
2.2
, recalling the definition (
2.9
) of
\gamma_{n}
, we have
s=\frac{1}{1+c_{\beta}e^{-\gamma_{\infty}}}\in[0,1]
, with
\gamma_{\infty}=\lim_{n\to\infty}\gamma_{n}\in[-\infty,\infty]
and
c_{\beta}
as in Example
2.2
.

Remark 2.11
.

If
\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{x_{n}}{a_{n}}\in[-\infty,\infty)
, then it is natural to expect that

\lim_{n\to\infty}d_{\mathrm{TV}}\Big(\mathscr{L}\big(R(\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{n})\,\big|\,S_{n}-b_{n}\geq x_{n}\big),\,\big(\mathscr{L}(\xi)\big)^{\otimes(n-1)}\Big)>0\,,

since conditioning on a typical fluctuation will affect all the summands.
This is analogous to the situation of Corollary
3.2
below in the case
\alpha\in(0,2)
, but we do not go into further detail here.

In the course of the proofs of Propositions
2.4
and
2.8
, we also obtain information on the overshoot
S_{n}-b_{n}-x_{n}
, conditioned on the large deviation event
\{S_{n}-b_{n}\geq x_{n}\}
.

Corollary 2.12
.

Let
\alpha=2
and
(a_{n})_{n\geq 1}
be a normalising sequence as in (
1.5
), and assume that
\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{x_{n}}{a_{n}}=\infty
. Then we have

1.

If
\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{x_{n}}{n}=0
, then conditionally on having no big-jump (
M_{n}\leq r_{n}
),

\mathscr{L}\Big(\frac{S_{n}-b_{n}-x_{n}}{\textstyle\sqrt{x_{n}/r_{n}}}\,\Big|\,S_{n}-b_{n}\geq x_{n},M_{n}\leq r_{n}\Big)\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{w}\mathrm{Exp}(1)\,

(2.24)

where
\xrightarrow{w}
denotes weak convergence;

2.

If
\overline{F}
is intermediate regularly varying, see (
2.11
), then conditionally on having a big-jump (
M_{n}>r_{n}
), for any sequence
x^{\prime}_{n}\geq x_{n}
we have, as
n\to\infty
,

\mathbb{P}\big(S_{n}-b_{n}\geq x_{n}^{\prime}\mid S_{n}-b_{n}\geq x_{n},M_{n}>r_{n}\big)\sim\frac{\overline{F}(x_{n}^{\prime})}{\overline{F}(x_{n})}.

(2.25)

In particular, if
\overline{F}
varies regularly with index
-\beta
for some
\beta\geq 2
, the last part gives that, for
y\in[1,\infty)

\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\Big(\frac{S_{n}-b_{n}}{x_{n}}\geq y\,\Big|\,S_{n}-b_{n}\geq x_{n},M_{n}>r_{n}\Big)=y^{-\beta}\,,

(2.26)

i.e.
conditioned on
\{S_{n}-b_{n}\geq x_{n},M_{n}>r_{n}\}
, the overshoot behaves asymptotically like
x_{n}
times a Pareto random variable.
In particular,
in the regime where
s=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{n\overline{F}(x_{n})}{\mathbb{P}(S_{n}-b_{n}\geq x_{n})}\in(0,1)
, the conditional law of the overshoot will be a non-trivial mixture of a scaled exponential and of a (differently) scaled Pareto distribution.

2.3
Second set of results: Local large deviations and conditional laws

In this section, we study local versions of the large deviations, namely we obtain estimates on the probabilities of the type
\mathbb{P}(S_{n}-b_{n}\in[x_{n},x_{n}+\Delta))
in the regime
\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{x_{n}}{a_{n}}=\infty
.
To simplify the statements, we will assume that
\xi
is integer valued and that
(S_{n})_{n\geq 1}
is aperiodic (the latter is true if
\overline{F}
is intermediate regularly varying). The case that
\xi
has support in
a+h\mathbb{Z}
for some
h>0
and
a\in\mathbb{R}
is completely analogous, just notationally a little more cumbersome.

To obtain sharp results, we need some local condition on the distribution of
\xi
. Our main assumption is that

\mathbb{P}(\xi=x)
is intermediate regularly varying at infinity (which implies that
\overline{F}(x)
is also intermediate regularly varying), see (
2.11
), and that
\mathbb{P}(\xi=x)
is “almost monotone”, in the sense that

\limsup_{x\to\infty}\Big(\frac{\sup_{y\geq x}\mathbb{P}(\xi=y)}{\mathbb{P}(\xi=x)}\Big)<+\infty\,.

(2.27)

Note that (
2.27
) is not implied by the intermediate regular variation of
\mathbb{P}(\xi=x)
(but would be guaranteed if
\mathbb{P}(\xi=x)
were regularly varying).

Example 2.13
.

An important class of examples that we consider is when

\mathbb{P}(\xi=x)
is regularly varying at infinity:
there exists a slowly varying function
L(\cdot)
and some
\beta\geq 2
such that

\mathbb{P}(\xi=x)\sim\beta L(x)x^{-(1+\beta)}\qquad\text{ as }x\to\infty\,.

(2.28)

In particular, we have that
\overline{F}(x)\sim L(x)x^{-\beta}
. Doney
[
Don01
]
proves that if in addition to (
2.28
) we have
\mathbb{E}[|\xi|^{2+\delta}]<\infty
for some
\delta>0
(implying
\beta>2
), then analogously to Nagaev’s result (
1.9
) for the integral case, we have

\mathbb{P}(S_{n}-\lfloor b_{n}\rfloor=x_{n})=(1+o(1))\frac{1}{\sigma\sqrt{n}}g\left(\frac{x_{n}}{\sigma\sqrt{n}}\right)+(1+o(1))n\mathbb{P}(\xi=x_{n})\,,

(2.29)

where
g(t)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-\frac{t^{2}}{2}}
is the standard normal density and
\sigma^{2}=\mathrm{Var}(\xi)
.
Let us mention that
[
Mog08
, Thm. 2.2]
gives the same result, assuming
\beta>2
in (
2.28
) and with the moment condition
\mathbb{E}[\xi^{2}{\text{\Large$\mathfrak{1}$}}_{\{|\xi|>x\}}]=o(1/\log x)
as
x\to\infty
, analogously
to (
2.7
).

Example 2.14
.

Continuing the discussion in Example
2.13
, assume that (
2.28
) holds with
\beta>2
and that
\mathbb{E}[\xi^{2}{\text{\Large$\mathfrak{1}$}}_{\{|\xi|>x\}}]=o(1/\log x)
as
x\to\infty
.
Then since (
2.29
) holds, it is a standard calculation to obtain that letting

\tilde{\gamma}_{n}:=\frac{x_{n}^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}n}-\Big(\frac{\beta}{2}-1\Big)\log n-\frac{1}{2}(\beta+1)\log\log n+\log L(\sqrt{n\log n})

with
\sigma^{2}=\mathrm{Var}(\xi)
,
then if
\lim_{n\to\infty}\tilde{\gamma}_{n}=\tilde{\gamma}_{\infty}\in[-\infty,\infty]
, we obtain

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\mathbb{P}(S_{n}-\lfloor b_{n}\rfloor=x_{n})}{n\mathbb{P}(\xi=x_{n})}=1+\tilde{c}_{\beta,\sigma}e^{-\tilde{\gamma}_{\infty}},\quad\text{ with }\tilde{c}_{\beta,\sigma}=\frac{\sigma^{\beta}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\beta^{-1}(\beta-2)^{(\beta+1)/2}\,.

Hence the one-big-jump phenomenon occurs if and only if
\lim_{n\to\infty}\tilde{\gamma}_{n}=\infty
.
Let us stress that, compared with Example
1.1
, the definition of
\tilde{\gamma}_{n}
differs from that of
\gamma_{n}
in the constant in front of
\log\log n
.
In particular, we might have
\gamma_{n}\to\infty
while
\tilde{\gamma}_{n}\to-\infty
. In other words, there is a regime where the one-big-jump phenomenon occurs for the large deviations but not for the local large deviations. ∎

Our goal is to extend the asymptotics (
2.29
) of
[
Don01
,
Mog08
]
to include the non-normal domain
of attraction to the normal law:
in particular, in (
2.28
) we include the case
\beta=2
.

2.3.1
Local large deviations

As for the (integral) large deviation case, we split the local large deviation probability according to the threshold
r_{n}
defined in (
2.12
):

\mathbb{P}(S_{n}-\lfloor b_{n}\rfloor=x_{n})=\mathbb{P}(S_{n}-\lfloor b_{n}\rfloor=x_{n},M_{n}\leq r_{n})+\mathbb{P}(S_{n}-\lfloor b_{n}\rfloor=x_{n},M_{n}>r_{n})\,.

We analyse the two terms through two separate results respectively.

Proposition 2.15
.

Let
\alpha=2
and let
(a_{n})_{n\geq 1}
be a normalising sequence as in (
1.5
); assume also that
(S_{n})_{n\geq 0}
is aperiodic.
If
(x_{n})_{n\geq 1}
is a sequence such that
x_{n}\geq a_{n}
and
\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{x_{n}}{n}=0
,
then letting
r_{n}
be as in (
2.12
), we have as
n\to\infty

\mathbb{P}(S_{n}-\lfloor b_{n}\rfloor=x_{n},M_{n}\leq r_{n})\sim\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi n\sigma^{2}(r_{n})}}e^{-nH(\frac{x_{n}}{n})}\,.

(2.30)

Proposition 2.16
.

Let
\alpha=2
, let
(a_{n})_{n\geq 1}
be a normalising sequence as in (
1.5
). Assume that
\mathbb{P}(\xi=x)
is intermediate regularly varying (see (
2.11
)) and that (
2.27
) holds.
If
\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{x_{n}}{a_{n}}=\infty
and
\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{x_{n}}{n}=0
,
then letting
r_{n}
be as in (
2.12
), we have for any
\varepsilon>0

\mathbb{P}(S_{n}-\lfloor b_{n}\rfloor=x_{n},M_{n}>r_{n})\sim\mathbb{P}\left(S_{n}-\lfloor b_{n}\rfloor=x_{n},|M_{n}-x_{n}|\leq\varepsilon x_{n}\right)\,,

(2.31)

and also

\mathbb{P}(S_{n}-\lfloor b_{n}\rfloor=x_{n})=\mathbb{P}(S_{n}-\lfloor b_{n}\rfloor=x_{n},M_{n}\leq r_{n})+(1+o(1))n\mathbb{P}(\xi=x_{n})\,.

If
\liminf_{n\to\infty}\frac{x_{n}}{n}>0
, then we have

\mathbb{P}(S_{n}-\lfloor b_{n}\rfloor=x_{n})\sim\mathbb{P}\left(S_{n}-\lfloor b_{n}\rfloor=x_{n},|M_{n}-x_{n}|\leq\varepsilon x_{n}\right)\sim n\mathbb{P}(\xi=x_{n})\,.

The statement with
\liminf_{n\to\infty}\frac{x_{n}}{n}>0
is standard, see e.g.
[
AL09
, Prop. 1]
. We
include it in our proposition for the sake of completeness.

Let us mention that Remark
2.7
also applies here in the local case:
If additionally to the assumptions in Proposition
2.16
condition (
2.4
) holds, then thanks to Lemma
2.6
we have
nH\big(\frac{x_{n}}{n}\big)=\frac{x_{n}^{2}}{2a_{n}^{2}}+o(1)
uniformly for
a_{n}\leq x_{n}\leq Ca_{n}\sqrt{|\log q(a_{n})|}
.
Therefore, if
\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{x_{n}}{a_{n}}=\infty
and
x_{n}\leq Ca_{n}\sqrt{|\log q(a_{n})|}
, thanks to Propositions
2.15
and
2.16
we obtain

\mathbb{P}(S_{n}-\lfloor b_{n}\rfloor=x_{n})=(1+o(1))\frac{1}{a_{n}}g\left(\frac{x_{n}}{a_{n}}\right)+(1+o(1))n\mathbb{P}(\xi=x_{n})\,,

(2.32)

where we recall
g(\cdot)
is the standard normal density (and we have used that
\sqrt{n\sigma^{2}(r_{n})}\sim\sqrt{n\sigma^{2}(a_{n})}\sim a_{n}
in (
2.30
), see Remark
2.5
).

Example 2.17
.

Let
\xi
be an integer-valued random variable that satisfies (
2.28
). We also assume that Rozovskii’s condition (
2.4
) holds, see Example
A.10
for a simple sufficient condition.
Note that compared to Example
2.14
, we include the case
\beta=2
.
If we set

\tilde{\gamma}_{n}=\frac{x_{n}^{2}}{2a_{n}^{2}}-|\log q(a_{n})|-\frac{1}{2}(\beta+1)\log|\log q(a_{n})|-\log\Big(\frac{L(a_{n})}{L(a_{n}\sqrt{|\log q(a_{n})|})}\Big)\,,

(2.33)

then if
\lim_{n\to\infty}\tilde{\gamma}_{n}=\tilde{\gamma}_{\infty}\in[-\infty,\infty]
, thanks to (
2.32
) we obtain that

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\mathbb{P}(S_{n}-\lfloor b_{n}\rfloor=x_{n})}{n\mathbb{P}(\xi=x_{n})}=1+\tilde{c}_{\beta}e^{-\tilde{\gamma}_{\infty}},\quad\text{ with }\tilde{c}_{\beta}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\,\beta^{-1}2^{(\beta+1)/2}\,.

Hence the one-big-jump phenomenon occurs if and only if
\lim_{n\to\infty}\tilde{\gamma}_{n}=\infty
.
We stress again that, compared with Example
2.2
, the definition (
2.33
) of
\tilde{\gamma}_{n}
differs from that (
2.9
) of
\gamma_{n}
in the prefactor of
\log|\log q(a_{n})|
; in particular we might have
\gamma_{n}\to\infty
while
\tilde{\gamma}_{n}\to-\infty
.
∎

Remark 2.18
.

Let us mention that even without the local assumption that
\mathbb{P}(\xi=x)
is (intermediate) regularly varying, one can still obtain useful estimates on the local large deviation probabilities.
For instance, assuming that
\alpha=2
and letting
(a_{n})_{n\geq 1}
be a normalising sequence as in (
1.5
),

[
MT22
, Thm. 1.1]
gives the following bound (with optimal decay rate), in the case where the left and right tails
F(-x),\overline{F}(x)
are regularly varying: uniformly for
x_{n}\geq a_{n}

\mathbb{P}(S_{n}-\lfloor b_{n}\rfloor=x_{n})\leq\frac{C}{a_{n}}\times\frac{n\sigma^{2}(x_{n})}{x_{n}^{2}}\,.

(2.34)

If one has that
\overline{F}(x)\leq L(x)x^{-\beta}
for some
\beta\geq 2
and some slowly varying function
L(\cdot)
, then
[
Ber19a
, Thm. 2.1]
gives that there are positive constants

such that uniformly in

,

\mathbb{P}(S_{n}-\lfloor b_{n}\rfloor=x_{n})\leq\frac{C_{1}}{a_{n}}\Big(e^{-c_{1}x_{n}^{2}/a_{n}^{2}}+nL(x_{n})x_{n}^{-\beta}\Big).

This is obviously not as sharp as (
2.29
) (in particular the constant

in the exponential is not optimal) but it is sharper than (

The above display requires very little assumption and can be useful in several situations.

3
The case of the domain of attraction of an
\alpha
-stable law with
\alpha\in(0,2)

The one-big-jump phenomenon is well understood in the case where
\xi
is in the domain of attraction of an
\alpha
-stable law with
\alpha\in(0,2)
:
in a nutshell, one has a “big-jump” whenever
\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{x_{n}}{a_{n}}=\infty
, see
[
CH89
,
Nag79
]
for the case
\alpha\in(0,1)\cup(1,2)
and
[
Ber19
]
for the case
\alpha=1
.
In order to put our results in perspective we recall here
some results for large and local large deviations and give their consequences in terms of conditional laws.

3.1
Large deviations and conditional laws

Assume that
\alpha\in(0,2)
and recall the definitions (
1.5
)-(
1.6
) of the normalising and centering sequences
(a_{n})_{n\geq 1}
,
(b_{n})_{n\geq 1}
.
Then Theorem 2.1 in
[
Ber19
]
collects large deviation results in that setting.

Theorem 3.1

(Thm. 2.1 in
[
Ber19
]
)
.

Assume that
\alpha\in(0,2)
, let
(a_{n})_{n\geq 1}
,
(b_{n})_{n\geq 1}
be the sequences in (
1.1
), and recall (
1.2
).
If
\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{x_{n}}{a_{n}}=\infty
, then we have, as
n\to\infty
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(S_{n}-b_{n}\geq x_{n})

\displaystyle\sim npL(x_{n})x_{n}^{-\alpha}\,,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(S_{n}-b_{n}\leq-x_{n})

\displaystyle\sim nqL(x_{n})x_{n}^{-\alpha}\,.

If
p=0
or
q=0
, one interprets the corresponding term on the r.h.s as
o(nL(x)x^{-\alpha}).

As a corollary, we obtain a result on the law of
\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{n}
conditionally on the large deviation event
\{S_{n}-b_{n}\geq x_{n}\}
.

Corollary 3.2

(One-big-jump phenomenon)
.

Assume
\alpha\in(0,2)
, let
(a_{n})_{n\geq 1}
,
(b_{n})_{n\geq 1}
be the sequences in (
1.1
) and suppose that
p>0
in (
1.2
).
If
\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{x_{n}}{a_{n}}=\infty
, then as
n\to\infty

\mathbb{P}(S_{n}-b_{n}\geq x_{n})\sim\mathbb{P}(S_{n}-b_{n}\geq x_{n},M_{n}\geq x_{n})\sim\mathbb{P}(M_{n}\geq x_{n}).

(3.1)

Moreover the condition
\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{x_{n}}{a_{n}}=\infty
is necessary and sufficient for

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}d_{\mathrm{TV}}\Big(\mathscr{L}\big(R(\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{n})\,\big|\,S_{n}-b_{n}\geq x_{n}\big),\,\big(\mathscr{L}(\xi)\big)^{\otimes(n-1)}\Big)=0.

(3.2)

In particular, if
\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{x_{n}}{a_{n}}=\infty
, we have

\mathscr{L}\left(\frac{S_{n}-b_{n}-M_{n}}{a_{n}}\,\Big|\,S_{n}-b_{n}\geq x_{n}\right)\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{w}\mathscr{L}(\mathcal{S}_{\alpha}),

(3.3)

where
\xrightarrow{w}
denotes weak convergence and
\mathcal{S}_{\alpha}
is the
\alpha
-stable random variable appearing in (
1.1
).

Remark 3.3
.

When
p=0
, Theorem
3.1
only gives that
\mathbb{P}(S_{n}-b_{n}\geq x_{n})=o(nL(x_{n})x_{n}^{-\alpha})
. We mention
[
DDS08
, Thms. 9.2 and 9.3]
and also
[
Bor03
]
for sufficient conditions to have a one-big-jump phenomenon in this case, but we are not aware of a general necessary and sufficient condition.

3.2
Local large deviations and conditional laws

To simplify the statements, we assume that
\xi
is integer valued. We are interested in estimating the local large deviation probabilities
\mathbb{P}(S_{n}-\lfloor b_{n}\rfloor=x_{n})
as
n\to\infty
, if
(x_{n})_{n\geq 1}
is a sequence of integers such that
\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{x_{n}}{a_{n}}=\infty
.
For this type of results, we need an extra condition on the local tail behaviour of the distribution of
\xi
:
in addition to (
1.2
), we assume that there is some
\alpha\in(0,2)
and some slowly varying function
L(\cdot)
such that

\mathbb{P}(\xi=x)\sim p\alpha L(x)x^{-(1+\alpha)},\qquad\text{ as }x\to\infty\,,

(3.4)

with
p\geq 0
; note that this implies the first half of (
1.2
).
If
p=0
, one interprets it as
o(nL(x)x^{-(\alpha+1)}).

We now recall the result obtained in
[
Ber19
]
(see also
[
Don97
]
for the case
\alpha\in(0,1)
).

Theorem 3.4

(Thm. 2.4 in
[
Ber19
]
)
.

Assume that (
1.2
) holds for some
\alpha\in(0,2)
and that additionally one has (
3.4
); let
(a_{n})_{n\geq 1}
,
(b_{n})_{n\geq 1}
be the sequences in (
1.1
).
Then, if
\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{x_{n}}{a_{n}}=\infty
, we have

\mathbb{P}(S_{n}-\lfloor b_{n}\rfloor=x_{n})\sim np\alpha L(x_{n})x_{n}^{-(1+\alpha)}\,.

(3.5)

Corollary 3.5

(Local one-big-jump phenomenon)
.

Assume
\alpha\in(0,2)
, let
(a_{n})_{n\geq 1}
,
(b_{n})_{n\geq 1}
be the sequences in (
1.1
) and assume that (
3.4
) holds for some
p>0
.
Then the condition
\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{x_{n}}{a_{n}}=\infty
is necessary and sufficient for

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}d_{\mathrm{TV}}\Big(\mathscr{L}\big(R(\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{n})\,\big|\,S_{n}-\lfloor b_{n}\rfloor=x_{n}\big),\,\big(\mathscr{L}(\xi)\big)^{\otimes(n-1)}\Big)=0.

(3.6)

In particular, under
\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{x_{n}}{a_{n}}=\infty

\mathscr{L}\left(\frac{x_{n}-M_{n}-b_{n}}{a_{n}}\,\Big|\,S_{n}-\lfloor b_{n}\rfloor=x_{n}\right)\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{w}\mathscr{L}(\mathcal{S}_{\alpha}).

(3.7)

Remark 3.6
.

Without assuming the local tail condition (
3.4
), one is still able to find an estimate on the local large deviation probability, see
[
CD19
, Thm. 1.1]
and
[
MT22

(or
[
Ber19

which includes the case

\alpha\in(0,2)
and
(a_{n})_{n\geq 1}
,

C_{0}
such that uniformly for sequences

\mathbb{P}(S_{n}-\lfloor b_{n}\rfloor=x_{n})\leq\frac{C_{0}}{a_{n}}\,n\mathbb{P}(|\xi|\geq x_{n})\,.

4
Further comments, applications and open questions

The behaviour of summands in a sum of i.i.d. random variables is of course a ubiquitous and classical theme in probability theory: the
phenomenon of one big-jump being responsible for atypically large values of the sum features in many applications. Let us mention here
random walks and renewal processes (see, e.g.
[
Ber19a
,
Ber19
]
and
the references there), population models in regimes with
asymptotically infinite offspring variance (where reproduction
proceeds in two steps, with each of
N
individuals first generating a
random number
\xi_{i}
of juveniles, from which then
N
are sampled to
form the next generation, as e.g. in
[
Sch03
,
BLS18
,
BY23
]
), the
zero-range process on finite graphs subject to certain homogeneity conditions (since then the stationary law of
the occupation numbers is i.i.d. conditioned on the sum, one big-jump
corresponds to condensation, see Section
4.2
below), critical Galton-Watson trees with
heavy-tailed offspring distribution (see e.g.
[
Kor15
]
and

[
KR19
]
, where conditioned on being large, a node with macroscopic
degree may emerge) and their applications in the study of random
planar maps (e.g.
[
KR20
]
), ruin problems in insurance
mathematics (e.g.
[
BBS15
]
).
Note also that for i.i.d. sums with exponential tails, if in the so-called borderline case the appropriately tilted laws are sufficiently heavy-tailed, even richer behaviour than one big jump may appear when conditioning on reaching an atypically large value, see
[
FP11
]
for instance.

4.2
Application to the zero-range process

As an example of application, we discuss in detail how Corollary
2.22
can be applied to the zero-range process.
We provide a short introduction to the zero-range process, using the terminology in
[
AGL13
]
(up to minor differences) and rephrasing certain results in our notation.
Our goal is to state (part of) Theorem 2.1 in
[
AGL13
]
and explain how our Corollary
2.22
extends it.

Consider a finite set
\Lambda_{L}
of
L
sites. Each site can host any number of (indistinguishable) particles.
Informally, the zero-range process is a continuous-time Markov chain with the following dynamics:
a site
x\in\Lambda_{L}
loses a particle at rate
g(\eta_{x})
, where
\eta_{x}
is the number of particles at site
x
and
g:\mathbb{N}_{0}\mapsto[0,\infty)
is a function such that
g(k)=0
if and only if
k=0
.
That particle jumps to site
y\in\Lambda_{L}
with probability
p(x,y)
, where
p(\cdot,\cdot)
is a transition kernel on
\Lambda_{L}\times\Lambda_{L}
(which is assumed to induce an irreducible Markov chain on
\Lambda_{L}
, with a spatially homogeneous invariant measure).
To proceed, we need some more notation as follows:

•

A particle configuration is denoted
\eta=(\eta_{x}:x\in\Lambda_{L})
;

•

for a configuration
\eta
with
\eta_{x}>0
, define the configuration
\eta^{x,y}=(\eta_{z}^{x,y})_{z\in\Lambda_{L}}
by
\eta_{x}^{x,y}=\eta_{x}-1
,
\eta_{y}^{x,y}=\eta_{y}+1
and
\eta_{z}^{x,y}=\eta_{z}
for all
z\neq x,y
.

Then, the zero-range process is described by the following generator on the space of configurations:

\mathcal{L}f(\eta)=\sum_{x,y\in\Lambda_{L}}g(\eta_{x})p(x,y)\left(f(\eta^{x,y})-f(\eta)\right)\,,

which corresponds to the description given above.
Note that the total number of particles is preserved, and we denote it by
N
.

Setting
w(n):=\prod_{k=1}^{n}\frac{1}{g(k)}
, it is known that there is a family of invariant measures, that are product measures indexed by a parameter
\varphi\geq 0
(called
fugacity
), that are defined by

\nu_{\varphi}(\eta)=\frac{1}{z(\varphi)^{\Lambda_{L}}}\prod_{x\in\Lambda_{L}}w(\eta_{x})\varphi^{\eta_{x}}\,,\quad\text{ with }z(\varphi)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}w(n)\varphi^{n}\,,

as soon as
\varphi
is such that the normalising constant verifies
z(\varphi)<\infty
.
Then, one can easily observe that since the number of particles
S_{L}(\eta):=\sum_{x\in\Lambda_{L}}\eta_{x}
is invariant under the microscopic dynamics, the invariant measure on the set of configurations
\{\eta:S_{L}(\eta)=N\}
is given by

\mu_{N,L}(\cdot)=\nu_{\varphi}\big(\cdot\,\big|\,S_{L}(\eta)=N\big)\,.

(4.4)

The measure
\mu_{N,L}
is in fact independent of the choice of
\varphi
.
Then, a natural question is whether the convergence of the measures
\mu_{N,L}
holds in the so-called thermodynamic limit,
i.e.
taking
L,N\to\infty
with
\frac{N}{L}\to\rho
for some
\rho>0
.

Since
[
Eva00
]
, a lot of interest has been put on the case where the jump rate
g(n)
decays with the number of particles.
A natural assumption is that

g(k)=1+\frac{b}{k}+\frac{\varepsilon_{k}}{k}

for some
b>1
and some vanishing sequence
(\varepsilon_{k})_{k\geq 0}
.
Note that this implies that
4
4

4

Let us mention that there is a notational inaccuracy in
[
AGL13
]
: the authors in fact assume that
w(n)\sim cn^{-b}
for some constant
c>0
, but for this, one would need the additional condition
\sum_{k\geq 1}\frac{\varepsilon_{k}}{k}<\infty
.

w(n)=\prod_{k=1}^{n}\frac{1}{g(k)}\sim L(n)n^{-b}\quad\text{as }n\to\infty\,,

(4.5)

for some slowly varying function
L(n)\sim c^{\prime}\exp(\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{\varepsilon_{k}}{k})
.
In fact, in a large part of the literature the choice
g(k)=1+\frac{b}{k}
or
g(k)=(k/(k-1))^{b}
is made, so that
w(n)
is explicit with
w(n)\sim cn^{-b}
, see
[
Eva00
,
AL09
,
Xu20
]
.
Let us stress that when
\varphi=1
we have

\nu_{1}(\eta_{x}=n)=\frac{w(n)}{\sum_{n\geq 1}w(n)}\sim cL(n)n^{-b}\,,

which therefore corresponds to the assumption (
2.28
) with
b=\beta+1
(note also that
\eta_{x}
is a non-negative random variable).
We can then define the critical density

\rho_{c}:=\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{1}}[\eta_{x}]\in(0,\infty]
.
Then, if
L,N\to\infty
with

4.3
Going further: scaling of the (recentered) maximum

Similarly to what is done for the overshoot in Corollary
2.12
, we could try to obtain the correct scale of the maximum.
We have in mind the following results, analogous to those in
[
AGL13
, Thm. 2.1]
(in the setting of Theorem
4.2
), which identify the scaling limit of the condensate in the zero-range process (we present them here with a integral and a local conditioning and to a wider range of distributions).

Conjecture 4.4
.

Let
\alpha=2
, let
(a_{n})_{n\geq 1}
be a normalising sequence as in (
1.5
), and assume that
\overline{F}
is intermediate regularly varying (see (
2.11
)).
Let
(x_{n})_{n\geq 1}
be a sequence such that
\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{x_{n}}{a_{n}}=\infty
and let
r_{n}
be defined as in (
2.12
).

•

If
\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{n\overline{F}(x_{n})}{\mathbb{P}(S_{n}-b_{n}\geq x_{n})}<1
, we have

\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}(M_{n}\leq t_{n}\,|\,S_{n}-b_{n}\geq x_{n},M_{n}\leq r_{n})=e^{-t}\,,

(4.8)

for any sequence
(t_{n})_{n\geq 0}
such that
\lim_{n\to\infty}n\overline{F}(t_{n})=t\in[0,\infty]
.

•

If
\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{n\overline{F}(x_{n})}{\mathbb{P}(S_{n}-b_{n}\geq x_{n})}>0
, we have

\mathscr{L}\left(\frac{S_{n}-b_{n}-M_{n}}{a_{n}}\,\Big|\,S_{n}-b_{n}\geq x_{n},M_{n}>r_{n}\right)\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{w}N(0,1).

(4.9)

Similar results hold if we replace
\{S_{n}-b_{n}\geq x_{n}\}
in the conditioning by
\{S_{n}-\lfloor b_{n}\rfloor=x_{n}\}
, assuming
\mathbb{P}(\xi=x)
is intermediate regularly varying and (
2.27
), and replacing the condition on
\frac{n\overline{F}(x_{n})}{\mathbb{P}(S_{n}-b_{n}\geq x_{n})}
by a condition on
\frac{n\mathbb{P}(\xi=x_{n})}{\mathbb{P}(S_{n}-\lfloor b_{n}\rfloor=x_{n})}
.

We stress that the conjecture is actually only about the statement in (
4.8
), since (
4.9
) is a direct consequence of Theorem
2.10
; we have kept the statement inside the conjecture since it gives a complete picture of the phenomenon.
We have stated (
4.8
) as a conjecture since it is not a simple consequence of results proved in this paper, but the main idea of the proof would be to use the conditional probability formula and a similar approach as for Proposition
2.4
.

The statements in Conjecture
4.4
can roughly be understood as follows:

•

Conditioning on having a large deviation but no big-jump (
M_{n}\leq r_{n}
), the maximum behaves exactly as the maximum of i.i.d. random variables with law
\mathbb{P}

i.e.
without conditioning.
Note that (
4.8
) is a unified way of considering convergence to extreme value distributions: if
\overline{F}(x)\sim e^{-x}
(resp.
\overline{F}(x)\sim x^{-\beta}
), then
t_{n}=\log n-\log t+o(1)
(resp.
t_{n}\sim t^{-1/\beta}n^{1/\beta}
) so one recovers the Gumbel (resp. Fréchet) distribution by a simple change of variable
u=-\log t
(resp.
u=t^{-1/\beta}
).

•

Conditioning on having a big jump (
M_{n}>r_{n}
), the fluctuations of the maximum around its typical value
S_{n}-b_{n}(\geq x_{n})
is Gaussian, on a scale
a_{n}
.

In particular, Conjecture
4.4
sheds light on the law of the maximum (the condensate) in a regime where
\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{n\overline{F}(x_{n})}{\mathbb{P}(S_{n}-b_{n}\geq x_{n})}=s\in(0,1)
: by using Proposition
2.8
, we have the decomposition

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(M_{n}\leq t_{n}\mid S_{n}-b_{n}\geq x_{n})

\displaystyle=(1+o(1))s\mathbb{P}(M_{n}\leq t_{n}\mid S_{n}-b_{n}\geq x_{n},M_{n}>r_{n})

\displaystyle\qquad+(1+o(1))(1-s)\mathbb{P}(M_{n}\leq t_{n}\mid S_{n}-b_{n}\geq x_{n},M_{n}\leq r_{n})\,,

so that the conditional law of the maximum will be a non-trivial mixture of a scaled exponential and a (differently) scaled and translated Gaussian.

4.4
Multiple big-jumps

An interesting direction of research would be to generalise the results obtained in the present paper to a setting where the large deviation is obtained not by a single big jump but by multiple ones.
This occurs for instance if the random variables are subject to a cutoff (or a dampening) which may depend on the number of variables in the sum, as considered in
[
KM22
]
.
The idea is to consider large deviation probabilities of the type

\mathbb{P}(S_{n}-b_{n}\geq x_{n},M_{n}\leq c_{n})\,,

where
c_{n}
is a given cutoff. Alternatively, one may consider a triangular array of variables
(\xi^{(n)}_{i})_{1\leq i\leq n}
, with a heavy-tailed law which is truncated (or dampened) at a threshold
c_{n}
.
Then, if
\xi
has a heavy tail and
x_{n}
grows sufficiently fast, one expects a “fewest-big-jumps” principle to replace the “one-big-jump” principle:
the large deviation should be realised mostly thanks to
k_{n}=\lceil x_{n}/c_{n}\rceil
random variables being close to the cutoff
c_{n}
. This is what is investigated in
[
KM22
]
; let us also mention
[
CD19
, Thm. 1.1]
where a general upper bound for the local large deviation is given, with this underlying philosophy.

Several questions have been answered in
[
KM22
]
, making the fewest-big-jumps principle precise, but many problems remain open.
For instance, can we obtain a result analogous to Propositions
2.8
and
2.16
if one adds a dampening to the law of
\xi
?
More precisely, if one assumes that
\mathbb{P}(\xi=x)\sim\beta x^{-(1+\beta)}
for some
\beta\geq 2
, one could conjecture that, for some reasonable choices of the cutoff
(c_{n})_{n\geq 1}
(e.g.
c_{n}=\gamma n
for some
\gamma\in(0,1)
), one has roughly

\begin{split}\mathbb{P}(S_{n}-b_{n}\geq x_{n},M_{n}\leq c_{n})&\approx\mathbb{P}(S_{n}-b_{n}\geq x_{n},M_{n}\leq r_{n})+\binom{n}{k_{n}}\mathbb{P}(\xi=c_{n})^{k_{n}}\\
&\approx\overline{\Phi}\Big(\frac{x_{n}}{a_{n}}\Big)+\binom{n}{k_{n}}\mathbb{P}(\xi=c_{n})^{k_{n}}\,,\end{split}

where
k_{n}=\lceil x_{n}/c_{n}\rceil
.
A natural question would then be to understand the transition between a collective (Gaussian) and a few-individuals (multiple big-jumps) behaviour, analogously to Examples
1.1
and
2.14
. The same question can be considered in the local large deviation setting.

Let us conclude by mentioning that in the context of the zero-range processes, one could consider a model with a saturation threshold
c_{L}
, meaning that a site cannot host (much) more than
c_{L}
particles.
This corresponds to taking the function
g
in Section
4.2
as being
L
-dependent and dampening the effective weight
w(n)
at the threshold
c_{L}
; for instance taking
g(n)=\infty
for
n>c_{L}
, one gets that
w(n)=0
for all
n\geq c_{L}
.
Proving the “fewest big-jumps” principle,
i.e.
obtaining sharp asymptotics of the type
\mathbb{P}(S_{L}=N,M_{L}\leq c_{L})\sim\binom{L}{k_{L}}\mathbb{P}(\xi=c_{L})^{k_{L}}
with
k_{L}=\lceil L/c_{L}\rceil
, could then possibly be translated into a statement that the zero-range process possesses
k_{L}
condensates of size close to
c_{L}
.

5
Proofs of the large deviations and conditional laws results

Before we start the proof of our results, let us introduce some notation.
We use the same letter
c,c_{0},c_{1},c_{2}
etc, to denote constants at various places, but they may refer to different values.
For positive functions
f,g
we write
f(y)\asymp g(y)
if there exist
c>0,C>0
such that
cg(y)\leq f(y)\leq Cg(y)
for
y\geq 1
.

Also, to simplify the statements, we will assume that
\mu=\mathbb{E}[\xi]=0
.
Recall the definition (
1.3
) of
\sigma^{2}(x)=\mathbb{E}[\xi^{2}{\text{\Large$\mathfrak{1}$}}_{|\xi|\leq x}]
; as we assume in this section that
\xi
is in the domain of attraction of the normal law,
\sigma^{2}(x)
is slowly varying at
\infty
.

5.1
Some preliminary estimates

Let us now collect some estimates that will be useful in the rest of the paper: bounds on
\mathbb{P}(|\xi|>x)
and truncated moments that involve
\sigma^{2}(x)
;
a Fuk–Nagaev type inequality for
\mathbb{P}(S_{n}\geq x,M_{n}\leq y)
in the case
\alpha=2
.

5.1.1
Estimating the tail with the truncated second moment

For
x>0
, let us set

\overline{q}(x):=\frac{x^{2}}{\sigma^{2}(x)}\mathbb{P}(|\xi|>x)\,,

and
q^{*}(x):=\sup_{y\geq x}\overline{q}(y)
and
\tilde{q}(x):=\frac{1}{x}\int_{0}^{x}\overline{q}(t)\mathrm{d}t
.

Claim 5.1
.

If
x\mapsto\sigma^{2}(x)
is slowly varying, then we have
\lim_{x\to\infty}\overline{q}(x)=0
;
a direct consequence is that
\lim_{x\to\infty}q^{*}(x)=\lim_{x\to\infty}\tilde{q}(x)=0
.
Also, there is a constant
c>0
such that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[|\xi|{\text{\Large$\mathfrak{1}$}}_{\{|\xi|>x\}}]\leq c\,q^{*}(x)\sigma^{2}(x)x^{-1}\,,

(5.1)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[|\xi|^{3}{\text{\Large$\mathfrak{1}$}}_{\{|\xi|\leq x\}}]\leq c\,\tilde{q}(x)\sigma^{2}(x)x\,.

(5.2)

Proof.

For the first part of the statement, the fact that
\lim_{x\to\infty}\overline{q}(x)=0
is recalled in (
1.4
), see e.g.
[
Fel08
, Ch. IX.8, Eq. (8.5)]
.

To prove (
5.1
), we write

\mathbb{E}[|\xi|{\text{\Large$\mathfrak{1}$}}_{\{|\xi|>x\}}]=x\mathbb{P}(|\xi|>x)+\int_{x}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}(|\xi|>t)\mathrm{d}t\leq\frac{\overline{q}(x)\sigma^{2}(x)}{x}+q^{*}(x)\int_{x}^{\infty}\sigma^{2}(t)t^{-2}\mathrm{d}t\,,

where we have used that
\mathbb{P}(|\xi|>t)=\overline{q}(t)\sigma^{2}(t)t^{-2}
and the definition of
q^{*}
.
Then, using the properties of regularly varying functions, the integral is asymptotically equivalent to
\sigma^{2}(x)x^{-1}
, which gives the desired bound.

To prove (
5.2
), write

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}[|\xi|^{3}{\text{\Large$\mathfrak{1}$}}_{\{|\xi|\leq x\}}]&=3\int_{0}^{\infty}t^{2}\mathbb{P}(|\xi|{\text{\Large$\mathfrak{1}$}}_{\{|\xi|\leq x\}}>t)\mathrm{d}t\leq 3\int_{0}^{x}t^{2}\mathbb{P}(|\xi|>t)\mathrm{d}t\leq 3\sigma^{2}(x)\int_{0}^{x}\overline{q}(t)\mathrm{d}t\,,\end{split}

where we used the fact that
t\mapsto\sigma^{2}(t)
is non-decreasing. With the definition of
\tilde{q}(x)
, this gives the desired conclusion.
∎

5.1.2
A Fuk–Nagaev inequality

Lemma 5.2
.

Assume that
\mu=0
and that
x\mapsto\sigma^{2}(x)
is slowly varying. Then there is some
r_{0}>0
such that for any
x,y\geq r_{0}

\mathbb{P}(S_{n}\geq x,M_{n}\leq y)\leq e^{x/y}\Big(1+\frac{xy}{n\sigma^{2}(y)}\Big)^{-x/y}\,.

Proof.

We use
[
Nag79
, Theorem 1.2]
with
t=2
, to get the following:

\mathbb{P}(S_{n}\geq x,M_{n}\leq y)\leq e^{\frac{x}{y}}\Big(1+\frac{xy}{n\sigma^{2}(y)}\Big)^{-\frac{x}{y}+\frac{n\mu(y)}{y}-\frac{n\sigma^{2}(y)}{y^{2}}}\,,

with
\mu(y)=\mathbb{E}[\xi{\text{\Large$\mathfrak{1}$}}_{|\xi|\leq y}]
.
Since
\mathbb{E}[\xi]=0
, we have
\mu(y)=-\mathbb{E}[\xi{\text{\Large$\mathfrak{1}$}}_{|\xi|>y}]\leq\mathbb{E}[|\xi|{\text{\Large$\mathfrak{1}$}}_{|\xi|>y}]
.
Therefore, thanks to Claim
5.1
above, we have that

\mathbb{E}[|\xi|{\text{\Large$\mathfrak{1}$}}_{|\xi|>y}]=o(\sigma^{2}(y)/y)
: if
y
is large enough we have
y\mu(y)\leq\sigma^{2}(y)
, so
\frac{n\mu(y)}{y}-\frac{n\sigma^{2}(y)}{y^{2}}\leq 0
, which concludes the proof.
∎

5.2
Proof of Proposition
2.4

We present here a proof in a slightly more general setup: we will replace the constraint
M_{n}\leq r_{n}
by
M_{n}\leq ar_{n}
, for some fixed
a>0
. We stated Proposition
2.4
only for
a=1
but we also need the result in the proof of Proposition
2.8
with
a=4
, see (
5.20
) below.
Let us start with estimates in the case of a generic
r
, which will later on be replaced by
r=ar_{n}
.

5.2.1
Estimates on the tilted measure

Let us redefine
\mathbb{P}_{u}
from (
2.15
) with a general
r
instead of
r=r_{n}
(we keep the same notation for simplicity).
For any
u\geq 0
and
r>0

\frac{\text{\rm\,d}\mathbb{P}_{u}}{\text{\rm\,d}\mathbb{P}}(x)=\frac{\text{\rm\,d}\mathbb{P}_{u}^{(r)}}{\text{\rm\,d}\mathbb{P}}(x)=\frac{1}{M(u)}e^{ux}{\text{\Large$\mathfrak{1}$}}_{(-\infty,r]}(x)\,,\quad\text{with }M(u)=M_{r}(u):=\mathbb{E}[e^{u\xi}{\text{\Large$\mathfrak{1}$}}_{\{\xi\leq r\}}]\,.

(5.3)

Claim 5.3
.

Let
0<c<C
be fixed constants. Assume
\mu=0
.
If
x\mapsto\sigma^{2}(x)
is slowly varying at infinity, then as
r\to\infty
, uniformly for
\frac{c}{r}\leq u\leq\frac{C}{r}
, we have

\displaystyle M(u)-1=o(r^{-1}\sigma^{2}(r))\,;

(i)

\displaystyle M^{\prime}(u)=(1+o(1))u\,\sigma^{2}(r)\,;

(ii)

\displaystyle M^{\prime\prime}(u)=(1+o(1))\,\sigma^{2}(r)\,.

(iii)

We also have that
M^{\prime\prime}(u)\geq(1+o(1))\sigma^{2}(r)
for any
0\leq u\leq\frac{C}{r}
.
Finally, setting
\tilde{q}^{*}(x):=q^{*}(x)+\tilde{q}(x)
, we have

\mathbb{E}\big[|\xi|^{3}e^{u\xi}{\text{\Large$\mathfrak{1}$}}_{(-\infty,r]}(\xi)\big]\leq c_{0}\tilde{q}^{*}(r)r\sigma^{2}(r)\,.

(iv)

Proof.

Define, for
k\geq 0
,

m_{k}(r)=\mathbb{E}[\xi^{k}{\text{\Large$\mathfrak{1}$}}_{[-r,r]}(\xi)]
; in particular we have
\sigma^{2}(r)=m_{2}(r).

Item (i).

We have

M(u)=\mathbb{E}[e^{u\xi}{\text{\Large$\mathfrak{1}$}}_{\{|\xi|\leq r\}}]+\mathbb{E}[e^{u\xi}{\text{\Large$\mathfrak{1}$}}_{\{\xi<-r\}}]\,.

(5.4)

We can estimate the first term as follows: using that
|u\xi|\leq C
for any
|\xi|\leq r
, expanding the exponential, we get

\mathbb{E}[e^{u\xi}{\text{\Large$\mathfrak{1}$}}_{\{|\xi|\leq r\}}]=m_{0}(r)+u\,m_{1}(r)+O(u^{2}m_{2}(r))\,.

Using that
u\leq C/r
, we get that the last term is bounded by a constant times
r^{-2}\sigma^{2}(r)=o(r^{-1}\sigma^{2}(r))
.
Now, thanks to Claim
5.1
, we obtain the following estimates (using that
\mu=0
to get that
m_{1}(r)=-\mathbb{E}[\xi{\text{\Large$\mathfrak{1}$}}_{\{|\xi|>r\}}]
):

\begin{split}m_{0}(r)&=1-\mathbb{P}(|\xi|>r)=1-o(r^{-2}\sigma^{2}(r))\,,\\
|m_{1}(r)|&\leq\mathbb{E}[|\xi|{\text{\Large$\mathfrak{1}$}}_{\{|\xi|>r\}}]=o(r^{-1}\sigma^{2}(r))\,.\\
\end{split}

Therefore, we obtain
\mathbb{E}[e^{u\xi}{\text{\Large$\mathfrak{1}$}}_{\{|\xi|\leq r\}}]=o(r^{-1}\sigma^{2}(r)).

For the second term in (
5.4
), recalling Claim
5.1
, we have

\begin{split}0\leq\mathbb{E}[e^{u\xi}{\text{\Large$\mathfrak{1}$}}_{\{\xi<-r\}}]&\leq\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}e^{-c2^{k}}\mathbb{P}(\xi\in[-2^{k+1}r,-2^{k}r))\\
&\leq\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}e^{-c2^{k}}\mathbb{P}(|\xi|>2^{k}r))=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}e^{-c2^{k}}\frac{\bar{q}(2^{k}r)\sigma^{2}(2^{k}r)}{2^{2k}r^{2}})\\
&\leq r^{-2}q^{*}(r)\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}2^{2k}e^{-c2^{k}}\sigma^{2}(2^{k}r)\leq Cr^{-2}q^{*}(r)\sigma^{2}(r)\,,\end{split}

where we used Potter’s bound (see
[
BGT89
, §1.5.4]
) for the last inequality, to get that
\sigma^{2}(2^{k}r)\leq 2^{k}\sigma^{2}(r)
. All together, we obtain (i).

Item (ii).

Let us now turn to
M^{\prime}(u)
.
Again, we write
M^{\prime}(u)
as

M^{\prime}(u)=\mathbb{E}[e^{u\xi}\xi{\text{\Large$\mathfrak{1}$}}_{\{|\xi|\leq r\}}]+\mathbb{E}[e^{u\xi}\xi{\text{\Large$\mathfrak{1}$}}_{\{\xi<-r\}}]\,.

For the first term, expanding the exponential, we get

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{x_{n}}{a_{n}}=\infty
, we get that the lower bound is asymptotically equivalent to
n\overline{F}((1+\varepsilon)x_{n})
.
Then, thanks to the intermediate regular variation of
\overline{F}
, we get

\lim_{\varepsilon\downarrow 0}\liminf_{x\to\infty}\frac{\overline{F}((1+\varepsilon)x)}{\overline{F}(x)}=1
,
which concludes the proof.
∎

5.3.2
Completion of the proof of Proposition
2.8

Recall that we assume that
\overline{F}
is intermediate regularly varying and also that
\mu=0
. We will focus on the case where
\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{x_{n}}{a_{n}}=\infty
and
\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{x_{n}}{n}=0
; recall also that (
2.13
) holds.
(We briefly discuss at the end of the proof for the case where
\liminf_{n\to\infty}\frac{x_{n}}{n}>0
, which can also be found e.g. in
[
DDS08
]
.)
Using Lemma
5.5
, we simply need to show that

\mathbb{P}(S_{n}\geq x_{n},M_{n}>r_{n})\sim\mathbb{P}(S_{n}\geq x_{n},M_{n}>(1-\varepsilon)x_{n}),

(5.17)

for any
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
.

We fix
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
and write the probability as
Q_{1}+Q_{2}+Q_{3}+Q_{4}
, with

\begin{split}&Q_{1}=\mathbb{P}(S_{n}\geq x_{n},M_{n}>(1-\varepsilon)x_{n})\,,\quad Q_{2}=\mathbb{P}(S_{n}\geq x_{n},\varepsilon x_{n}<M_{n}\leq(1-\varepsilon)x_{n})\,,\\
&Q_{3}=\mathbb{P}(S_{n}\geq x_{n},4r_{n}<M_{n}\leq\varepsilon x_{n})\,,\quad Q_{4}=\mathbb{P}(S_{n}\geq x_{n},r_{n}<M_{n}\leq 4r_{n})\,.\end{split}

By Lemma
5.5
,
Q_{1}\sim n\overline{F}(x_{n})
. We show next that all other terms are negligible compared to
n\overline{F}(x_{n}).

The term
Q_{2}
can be estimated as follows: we have that

Q_{2}\leq n\mathbb{P}\big(\varepsilon x_{n}<\xi\leq(1-\varepsilon)x_{n}\big)\mathbb{P}\big(S_{n-1}\geq\varepsilon x_{n}\big)\,.

Using Potter’s bound (
A.2
) (see
[
Cli94
, Thm. 2.3]
), we get that
\mathbb{P}(\varepsilon x_{n}<\xi\leq(1-\varepsilon)x_{n})\leq\overline{F}(\varepsilon x_{n})\leq c^{\prime}\kappa(1/\varepsilon)\overline{F}(x_{n})
.
Also, since
\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{x_{n}}{a_{n}}=\infty
holds, by the convergence in distribution of (
1.1
) (recall
b_{n}=0
since
\mu=0
) we get that
\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}(S_{n-1}\geq\varepsilon x_{n})=0
for any fixed
\varepsilon>0
.
All together, this shows that
\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{Q_{2}}{n\overline{F}(x_{n})}=0
.

Note that we have

\frac{r_{n}}{a_{n}}\frac{\sigma^{2}(a_{n})}{\sigma^{2}(r_{n})}\sim\frac{a_{n}}{x_{n}},\quad\text{ and }\quad\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{r_{n}}{a_{n}}=0\quad\text{ if}\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{x_{n}}{a_{n}}=\infty.

(5.18)

The first result is due to (
2.13
) and the definition of
a_{n}
, see (
1.5
). The second result relies on the first one and uses that
\sigma^{2}(\cdot)
is slowly varying.
Then, since
\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{x_{n}}{a_{n}}=\infty
we have
\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{x_{n}}{r_{n}}=\infty
. We can now bound
Q_{3}
as follows (omitting the floor function
\lfloor\cdot\rfloor
to lighten notation):

\begin{split}Q_{3}&=\sum_{k=\log_{2}(\frac{1}{\varepsilon})}^{\log_{2}(\frac{x_{n}}{r_{n}})-1}\mathbb{P}\left(S_{n}\geq x_{n},M_{n}\in(2^{-k-1}x_{n},2^{-k}x_{n}]\right)\\
&\leq\sum_{k=\log_{2}(\frac{1}{\varepsilon})}^{\log_{2}(\frac{x_{n}}{r_{n}})-1}n\overline{F}(2^{-k-1}x_{n})\mathbb{P}\big(S_{n-1}\geq\tfrac{1}{2}x_{n},M_{n-1}\leq 2^{-k}x_{n}\big)\,.\end{split}

Then, using Potter’s bound (
A.2
), we have that
n\overline{F}(2^{-k-1}x_{n})\leq c_{0}\kappa(2^{k+1})n\overline{F}(x_{n})
uniformly in
k\geq 1
for some
c_{0}>0
, for
n
large enough.
Using the Fuk–Nagaev inequality of Lemma
5.2
, we get that

\begin{split}\frac{Q_{3}}{n\overline{F}(x_{n})}\leq c_{0}\sum_{k=\log_{2}(\frac{1}{\varepsilon})}^{\log_{2}(\frac{x_{n}}{r_{n}})-1}\kappa(2^{k+1})e^{2^{k}}\Big(1+\frac{2^{-k}x_{n}^{2}}{n\sigma^{2}(2^{-k}x_{n})}\Big)^{-2^{k}}\leq c_{0}\sum_{k=\log_{2}(\frac{1}{\varepsilon})}^{\log_{2}(\frac{x_{n}}{r_{n}})-1}\kappa(2^{k+1})e^{2^{k}}\Big(\frac{11}{4}\Big)^{-2^{k}}\,,\end{split}

where we have used that
\frac{x_{n}y}{n\sigma^{2}(y)}\geq 7/4
uniformly for
y\geq 2r_{n}
(provided that
n
is large enough).
Indeed, we have that
\frac{x_{n}y}{n\sigma^{2}(y)}\geq(1+o(1))2\frac{x_{n}r_{n}}{n\sigma^{2}(r_{n})}
uniformly for
y\geq r_{n}
, and the lower bound goes to
2
as
n\to\infty
.
We therefore have shown that

\frac{Q_{3}}{n\overline{F}(x_{n})}\leq c_{0}\sum_{k=\log_{2}(\frac{1}{\varepsilon})}^{\infty}\kappa(2^{k+1})(11/4e)^{-2^{k}}\xrightarrow{}0,\quad\text{as }\varepsilon\downarrow 0.

(5.19)

The convergence is due to the definition of
\kappa(\cdot)
and the fact that
11>4e.

It remains to treat
Q_{4}
.
We apply again Potter’s bound (
A.2
) to obtain that

Q_{4}\leq n\overline{F}(r_{n})\mathbb{P}\big(S_{n-1}\geq\tfrac{1}{2}x_{n},M_{n-1}\leq 4r_{n}\big)\leq cn\overline{F}(x_{n})\kappa\Big(\frac{x_{n}}{r_{n}}\Big)\mathbb{P}\big(S_{n-1}\geq\tfrac{1}{2}x_{n},M_{n-1}\leq 4r_{n}\big)\,.

For the last probability, the Fuk–Nagaev inequality of Lemma
5.2
is not enough, because of the first factor
e^{x_{n}/r_{n}}
.
However, thanks to Proposition
2.4
(one can replace
n
by
n-1
,
x_{n}
by
\frac{1}{2}x_{n}
and
r_{n}
by
4r_{n}
, that changes only some constants, see in particular (
5.7
)), we have

\mathbb{P}\big(S_{n-1}\geq x_{n}/2,M_{n-1}\leq 4r_{n}\big)\leq e^{-nH(\frac{x_{n}}{2n})}\leq e^{-c\frac{x_{n}^{2}}{n\sigma^{2}(r_{n})}}\leq e^{-c\frac{x_{n}}{r_{n}}}\,,

(5.20)

see (
5.7
) and Lemma
2.6
; we also have used that
n\sigma^{2}(r_{n})\sim r_{n}x_{n}
.
Hence, we get that

\frac{Q_{4}}{n\overline{F}(x_{n})}\leq c\kappa\Big(\frac{x_{n}}{r_{n}}\Big)e^{-cx_{n}/r_{n}}\,,

which goes to
0
since
x_{n}/r_{n}\to\infty
as noticed above.

All together, this concludes the proof of Proposition
2.8
in the case where
\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{x_{n}}{n}=0
and
\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{x_{n}}{a_{n}}=\infty
.
In the case where
\liminf_{n\to\infty}\frac{x_{n}}{n}>0
, we get that
r_{n}=O(1)
. Then, by exactly the same proof we can control the terms
Q_{1},Q_{2},Q_{3}
. It then simply remains to show that for any fixed
R>0
,
\hat{Q}_{4}:=\mathbb{P}(S_{n}\geq x_{n},M_{n}\leq R)
is negligible compared to
Q_{1}\sim n\overline{F}(x_{n})
.
But this is simply a large deviation estimate for bounded random variables (with negative mean): we get that
\hat{Q}_{4}\leq e^{-cx_{n}}
(note that
\hat{Q}_{4}=0
if
x_{n}\geq Rn
), which is indeed negligible compared to
n\overline{F}(x_{n})
since
\overline{F}
decays slower than any exponential function.
∎

6
Proofs of the local versions of the theorems

6.1
Proof of Proposition
2.15

Recall
x_{n}\geq a_{n}
with
\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{x_{n}}{n}=0
. For simplicity, we also assume
\mu=0.
Analogous to (
5.7
), we have that

\mathbb{P}(S_{n}=x_{n},M_{n}\leq ar_{n})=e^{-nH(\frac{x_{n}}{n})}\mathbb{P}_{\lambda}\big(S_{n}=x_{n}\big)

with
\lambda=\lambda(\frac{x_{n}}{n})
.
Then, we can apply a local limit theorem to estimate
\mathbb{P}_{\lambda}\big(S_{n}=x_{n}\big)
; in particular, since the distribution
\mathbb{P}_{\lambda}
depends on
n
and
x_{n}
, we need explicit estimates on the rate of convergence.
Several results exist in this direction, such as
[
Muk92
, Thm. 5]
or
[
GW17
,
SN21
]
, but none of these appear to give a sufficient bound for our purpose.
Instead, we start from
[
Don97
, Lem. 3]
: setting
\sigma_{\lambda}^{2}:=\mathrm{Var}_{\lambda}(\xi)
and
\nu_{\lambda}:=\mathbb{E}_{\lambda}[|\xi-\frac{x_{n}}{n}|^{3}]
, it gives that

\Big|\sqrt{n}\sigma_{\lambda}\mathbb{P}_{\lambda}(S_{n}=x_{n})-\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\Big|\leq C\frac{\nu_{\lambda}}{\sqrt{n}\sigma_{\lambda}^{3}}+2\sqrt{n\sigma_{\lambda}}\int_{\ell}^{\pi}e^{-n(1-|\varphi_{\lambda}(t)|)}\mathrm{d}t\,,

(6.1)

with
\ell:=\frac{\sigma_{\lambda}^{2}}{4\nu_{\lambda}}

and
\varphi_{\lambda}(t)=\mathbb{E}_{\lambda}(e^{it\xi})
the characteristic function of
\xi
under
\mathbb{P}_{\lambda}
.
Since we have that
\sigma^{2}_{\lambda}\sim\sigma^{2}(r_{n})
, see the first two results in (
5.8
), we only have to show that both terms on the r.h.s. of (
6.1
) go to zero.

For the first term, we have from (
5.8
) that

\frac{\nu_{\lambda}}{\sqrt{n}\sigma_{\lambda}^{3}}\leq C\frac{\tilde{q}^{*}(r_{n})r_{n}}{\sqrt{n\sigma^{2}(r_{n})}}\,,

which goes to
0
as proved for the last term in (
5.9
).

For the remaining term, notice that thanks to (
5.8
) we have that
\ell=\frac{\sigma_{\lambda}^{2}}{4\nu_{\lambda}}\geq\ell_{n}:=\frac{c}{\tilde{q}^{*}(r_{n})r_{n}}
and
\sigma^{2}_{\lambda}\sim\sigma^{2}(r_{n})
.
We fix
\varepsilon>0
(small enough so that the last inequality in (
6.3
) below holds uniformly on
[0,\varepsilon]
) and we need to show that both of the following terms go to
0
:

I_{1}=\sqrt{n\sigma^{2}(r_{n})}\int_{\ell_{n}}^{\varepsilon}e^{-n(1-|\varphi_{\lambda}(t)|)}\mathrm{d}t\,,\qquad I_{2}=\sqrt{n\sigma^{2}(r_{n})}\int_{\varepsilon}^{\pi}e^{-n(1-|\varphi_{\lambda}(t)|)}\mathrm{d}t\,.

Let us now estimate
1-|\varphi_{\lambda}(t)|
.
Since
\xi
has maximum span
1
(equivalent to
(S_{n})
being aperiodic; see the definition of maximum span in
[
GK54
]
), we can choose some
K>0
such that the law of
\xi
restricted to
\{-K,\ldots,K\}
has maximum span
1
.
Then, it is standard to get that for any
0<\varepsilon<1
, there is a constant
c_{0}>0
(that depends on
K,\varepsilon
) such that for any
t\in[\varepsilon,\pi]

\left|\mathbb{E}\big[e^{it\xi}\,\big|\,|\xi|\leq K\big]\right|\leq 1-c_{0}\,,

see
e.g.

[
GK54
, §14, Cor. 2 to Thm. 5]
.
Now, turning back to
\varphi_{\lambda}
, we have that, for
n
large enough so that
K\leq r_{n}
,

\begin{split}|\varphi_{\lambda}(t)|=\left|\mathbb{E}_{\lambda}\big[e^{it\xi}{\text{\Large$\mathfrak{1}$}}_{\{|\xi|\leq K\}}\big]\right|+\left|\mathbb{E}_{\lambda}\big[e^{it\xi}{\text{\Large$\mathfrak{1}$}}_{\{|\xi|>K\}}\big]\right|\leq\left|\mathbb{E}_{\lambda}\big[e^{it\xi}{\text{\Large$\mathfrak{1}$}}_{\{|\xi|\leq K\}}\big]\right|+\mathbb{P}_{\lambda}(|\xi|>K)\,.\end{split}

Now, recalling that
\lambda\to 0
, we can apply Taylor expansion to
e^{\lambda\xi}
on the event
\{|\xi|\leq K\}
: for
n
large enough, we obtain

\begin{split}M(\lambda)\left|\mathbb{E}_{\lambda}\big[e^{it\xi}{\text{\Large$\mathfrak{1}$}}_{\{|\xi|\leq K\}}\big]\right|&=\left|\mathbb{E}\big[e^{it\xi+\lambda\xi}{\text{\Large$\mathfrak{1}$}}_{\{|\xi|\leq K\}}\big]\right|\\
&\leq\mathbb{P}(|\xi|\leq K)\left(\left|\mathbb{E}_{\lambda}\big[e^{it\xi}\,\,\big|\,\,|\xi|\leq K\big]\right|+2\lambda K\right)\leq(1-\tfrac{c_{0}}{2})\mathbb{P}(|\xi|\leq K)\,.\end{split}

Now, since
\lambda\to 0
, we also easily get that

M(\lambda)\mathbb{P}(|\xi|\leq K)=(1+o(1))\mathbb{P}_{\lambda}(|\xi|\leq K)
, so we obtain that for
n
large enough

|\varphi_{\lambda}(t)|\leq(1-\tfrac{c_{0}}{3})\mathbb{P}_{\lambda}(|\xi|\leq K)+\mathbb{P}_{\lambda}(|\xi|>K)=1-\tfrac{c_{0}}{3}\mathbb{P}_{\lambda}(|\xi|\leq K)\leq 1-c^{\prime}\,,

where we also have used for the last inequality that
\mathbb{P}_{\lambda}(|\xi|\leq K)=(1+o(1))\mathbb{P}(|\xi|\leq K)
, since
\lambda\to 0
,
M(\lambda)\to 1
.
All together, we get that there is a constant
c^{\prime}=c^{\prime}(\varepsilon)>0
such that

1-|\varphi_{\lambda}(t)|\geq c^{\prime}
for any
t\in[\varepsilon,\pi]
.
Therefore, we obtain that

I_{2}\leq\sqrt{n\sigma^{2}(r_{n})}\pi e^{-c^{\prime}n}\to 0\,.

To deal with
I_{1}
, we use the idea of
[
GK54
, §50, Lemma]
: we prove that, for all
t\in[\frac{1}{r_{n}},\varepsilon]
,

1-|\varphi_{\lambda}(t)|\geq ct^{2}\sigma^{2}(1/t)\,.

(6.2)

One could have used Lemma 4 in
[
Muk92
]
, together with Lemmas 1-2 in
[
Muk92
]
to get that
1-|\varphi_{\lambda}(t)|\geq ct^{2}
uniformly for
t\in[0,\varepsilon]
; this is however not enough for our purpose.

Let us introduce
\mathbb{P}_{\lambda}^{*}
, the distribution of the symmetrised version of
\mathbb{P}_{\lambda}
,
i.e.
the law of
\xi^{*}=\xi-\xi^{\prime}
, where
\xi,\xi^{\prime}
are two independent copies with law
\mathbb{P}_{\lambda}
.
Then,
|\varphi_{\lambda}(t)|^{2}=\varphi_{\lambda}^{*}(t)=\mathbb{E}^{*}_{\lambda}[e^{it\xi^{*}}]
, so we only need to show that
1-\varphi_{\lambda}^{*}(t)\geq ct^{2}\sigma^{2}(1/t)
.
We have that

1-\varphi_{\lambda}^{*}(t)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}(1-\cos(tx))\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}_{\lambda}^{*}(x)\geq\int_{-1/t}^{1/t}(1-\cos(tx))\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}_{\lambda}^{*}(x)\,,

using that
1-\cos(tx)
is always non-negative.
Now, expanding the cosine (using
|tx|\leq 1
), we get

1-\varphi^{*}(t)\geq ct^{2}\int_{-1/t}^{1/t}x^{2}\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}_{\lambda}^{*}(x)\,.

Now, let
C>0
be fixed such that
c^{\prime}=\mathbb{P}(|\xi|\leq C)>0
; we also have
\mathbb{P}_{\lambda}(|\xi|\leq C)\geq\frac{c^{\prime}}{2}
for
n
large enough, since
\lambda\to 0
.
Then, for
t
small enough so that
1/t\geq 4C
,

\int_{-1/t}^{1/t}x^{2}\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}_{\lambda}^{*}(x)\geq\mathbb{E}_{\lambda}^{\otimes 2}\left[(\xi-\xi^{\prime})^{2}{\text{\Large$\mathfrak{1}$}}_{\{|\xi-\xi^{\prime}|\leq\frac{1}{t}\}}{\text{\Large$\mathfrak{1}$}}_{\{|\xi^{\prime}|\leq C,|\xi|\geq 2C\}}\right]\geq\frac{c^{\prime}}{8}\mathbb{E}_{\lambda}\left[\xi^{2}{\text{\Large$\mathfrak{1}$}}_{\{2C\leq|\xi|\leq\frac{1}{t}-C\}}\right]\,.

Then, since
1/t\leq r_{n}
and
\lambda\leq c/r_{n}
(recall Corollary
5.4
), we get that

\mathbb{E}_{\lambda}\left[\xi^{2}{\text{\Large$\mathfrak{1}$}}_{\{2C\leq|\xi|\leq\frac{1}{t}-C\}}\right]\geq M(\lambda)^{-1}e^{-\lambda r_{n}}\mathbb{E}\left[\xi^{2}{\text{\Large$\mathfrak{1}$}}_{\{2C\leq|\xi|\leq\frac{1}{t}-C\}}\right]\geq c^{\prime}\sigma^{2}(1/t)\,,

using also that
M(\lambda)\to 1
as
\lambda\to 0
. This concludes the proof of (
6.2
).

With (
6.2
) at hand, we get that

I_{1}\leq\sqrt{n\sigma^{2}(r_{n})}\int_{\ell_{n}}^{\varepsilon}e^{-cnt^{2}\sigma^{2}(1/t)}\mathrm{d}t\,.

Now, for
t\in(0,1]
, let
g(t)=t\bar{\sigma}^{2}(1/t)
, with
\bar{\sigma}^{2}(y)=2\int_{0}^{y}u\mathbb{P}(|\xi|>u)\mathrm{d}u
which verifies
\bar{\sigma}(y)\sim\sigma^{2}(y)
as
y\to\infty
(see (
A.5
)).
Then,
g
is differentiable and one can easily check that
g^{\prime}(t)\geq 0
, at least for
t
small enough (
e.g.
using that
\mathbb{P}(|\xi|>1/t)=o(t^{2}\sigma^{2}(1/t^{2}))
, see (
1.4
)).
We also let
h(t)=\int_{0}^{t}g(u)\mathrm{d}u
, which verifies
h(t)\sim t^{2}\sigma^{2}(1/t)
as
t\to 0
.
Then, by an integration by parts, we get that

\int_{\ell_{n}}^{\varepsilon}e^{-cnh(t)}\mathrm{d}t\leq\frac{1}{cng(\ell_{n})}e^{-cnh(\ell_{n})}-\int_{\ell_{n}}^{\varepsilon}\frac{g^{\prime}(t)}{cng(t)^{2}}e^{-cnh(t)}\mathrm{d}t\leq\frac{1}{cng(\ell_{n})}e^{-cnh(\ell_{n})}\,,

(6.3)

where the last inequality holds if
\varepsilon
has been fixed small enough so that
g^{\prime}(t)\geq 0
on
[0,\varepsilon]
.
Now, using that

g(\ell_{n})=\ell_{n}\bar{\sigma}^{2}(r_{n})
and
h(\ell_{n})\geq(1+o(1))\ell_{n}^{2}\sigma^{2}(r_{n})
,

I_{1}\leq\sqrt{n\sigma^{2}(r_{n})}\int_{\ell_{n}}^{\varepsilon}e^{-cnh(t)}\mathrm{d}t\leq\frac{1}{\ell_{n}\sqrt{n\sigma^{2}(r_{n})}}e^{-cnh(\ell_{n})}\leq\frac{c^{\prime}\tilde{q}^{*}(r_{n})r_{n}}{\sqrt{n\sigma^{2}(r_{n})}}e^{-\frac{c^{\prime\prime}n\sigma^{2}(r_{n})}{\tilde{q}^{*}(r_{n})^{2}r_{n}^{2}}}\,,

where we have used that
\ell_{n}=\frac{c}{\tilde{q}^{*}(r_{n})r_{n}}\geq\frac{1}{r_{n}}

for the last inequality.

Since
r_{n}/\sqrt{n\sigma^{2}(r_{n})}
remains bounded (recall (
5.10
) and the sentence below), we get that

I_{1}\leq c_{1}\tilde{q}^{*}(r_{n})e^{-c_{2}/\tilde{q}^{*}(r_{n})^{2}}
, which proves that
I_{1}
goes to
0
as
n\to\infty
.

Going back to (
6.1
), we have obtained that
\mathbb{P}_{\lambda}(S_{n}=x_{n})\sim\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi n\sigma^{2}(r_{n})}}
, which finally gives that

\mathbb{P}(S_{n}=x_{n},M_{n}\leq ar_{n})\sim\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi n\sigma^{2}(r_{n})}}e^{-nH(\frac{x_{n}}{n})}\,.\vskip-18.0pt

∎

6.2
Proof of Proposition
2.16

Recall that we assume that
\mathbb{P}(\xi=x)
is intermediate regularly varying and (
2.27
) holds, and
\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{x_{n}}{a_{n}}=\infty,\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{x_{n}}{n}=0
. For simplicity, we also assume
\mu=0.

As in the proof of Proposition
2.8
, we focus on the case where
\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{x_{n}}{n}=0
.
(We briefly discuss at the end of the proof the case
\liminf_{n\to\infty}\frac{x_{n}}{n}>0
, which can be found e.g. in
[
AL09
]
).
We decompose the probability as before, in the following terms:

\begin{split}&Q_{0}=\mathbb{P}(S_{n}=x_{n},M_{n}>(1+\varepsilon)x_{n})\,,\quad Q_{1}=\mathbb{P}(S_{n}=x_{n},(1-\varepsilon)x_{n}\leq M_{n}\leq(1+\varepsilon)x_{n})\,,\\
&Q_{2}=\mathbb{P}(S_{n}=x_{n},\varepsilon x_{n}<M_{n}<(1-\varepsilon)x_{n})\\
&Q_{3}=\mathbb{P}(S_{n}=x_{n},4r_{n}<M_{n}\leq\varepsilon x_{n})\,,\quad Q_{4}=\mathbb{P}(S_{n}=x_{n},r_{n}<M_{n}\leq 4r_{n})\,.\end{split}

For the term
Q_{2}
, we have, for some constant
c=c(\varepsilon)\in(0,\infty)
,

\begin{split}Q_{2}&\leq\sum_{y\in(\varepsilon x_{n},(1-\varepsilon)x_{n})}n\mathbb{P}(\xi=y)\mathbb{P}(S_{n-1}=x_{n}-y,M_{n-1}\leq y)\\
&\leq cn\mathbb{P}(\xi=x_{n})\sum_{y\in(\varepsilon x_{n},(1-\varepsilon)x_{n}]}\mathbb{P}(S_{n-1}=x_{n}-y)\leq cn\mathbb{P}(\xi=x_{n})\mathbb{P}(S_{n-1}\geq\varepsilon x_{n})\end{split}

where we have used Potter’s bound (
A.2
)
for the second line.
Then, since
\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{x_{n}}{a_{n}}=\infty
, we have
\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}(S_{n-1}\geq\varepsilon x_{n})=0
, so
\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{Q_{2}}{n\mathbb{P}(\xi=x_{n})}=0
.

For the term
Q_{3}
, we have (omitting integer parts to lighten notation)

\begin{split}Q_{3}&=\sum_{k=\log_{2}(\frac{1}{\varepsilon})}^{\log_{2}(\frac{x_{n}}{r_{n}})-1}\mathbb{P}\left(S_{n}=x_{n},M_{n}\in(2^{-k-1}x_{n},2^{-k}x_{n}]\right)\\
&\leq\sum_{k=\log_{2}(\frac{1}{\varepsilon})}^{\log_{2}(\frac{x_{n}}{r_{n}})-1}\sum_{y\in(2^{-k-1}x_{n},2^{-k}x_{n}]}n\mathbb{P}(\xi=y)\mathbb{P}\big(S_{n-1}=x_{n}-y,M_{n-1}\leq 2^{k}x_{n}\big)\\
&\leq cn\mathbb{P}(\xi=x_{n})\sum_{k=\log_{2}(\frac{1}{\varepsilon})}^{\log_{2}(\frac{x_{n}}{r_{n}})-1}2^{ck}\mathbb{P}\big(S_{n-1}\geq\tfrac{1}{2}x_{n},M_{n-1}\leq 2^{-k}x_{n}\big)\,,\end{split}

where we have used (
A.3
) and summed over
y\in(2^{-k-1}x_{n},2^{-k}x_{n}]
.
We conclude as in the proof of Proposition
2.8
that

\frac{Q_{3}}{n\mathbb{P}(\xi=x_{n})}\leq c^{\prime}\varepsilon^{3}(11/4e)^{-1/\varepsilon}
, see (
5.19
).

For the term
Q_{4}
, we also get thanks again to (
A.3
)

\begin{split}Q_{4}&\leq\sum_{y\in(r_{n},4r_{n}]}n\mathbb{P}(\xi=y)\mathbb{P}\big(S_{n-1}=x_{n}-y,M_{n-1}\leq 4r_{n}\big)\\
&\leq cn\mathbb{P}(\xi=x_{n})\Big(\frac{x_{n}}{r_{n}}\Big)^{c}\mathbb{P}\big(S_{n-1}\geq\tfrac{1}{2}x_{n},M_{n-1}\leq 4r_{n}\big)\,,\end{split}

and the conclusion follows exactly as in the proof for the term
Q_{4}
of Proposition
2.8
.

For the term
Q_{0}
, by sub-additivity and using the “almost monotonicity” (
2.27
), we obtain

Q_{0}\leq n\sum_{y>(1+\varepsilon)x_{n}}\mathbb{P}(\xi=y)\mathbb{P}(S_{n-1}=x_{n}-y)\leq c\mathbb{P}(\xi=x_{n})\mathbb{P}(S_{n-1}\leq-\varepsilon x_{n})\,,

which is negligible compared to
\mathbb{P}(\xi=x_{n})
, since
\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{x_{n}}{a_{n}}=\infty
.

It remains only to deal with
Q_{1}
; we follow Doney’s approach
[
Don01
]
. We prove a lower and an upper bound separately.
We have

\displaystyle Q_{1}

\displaystyle\geq n\mathbb{P}(S_{n}=x_{n},\exists\,i\text{ s.t. }\xi_{i}\in((1-\varepsilon)x_{n},(1+\varepsilon)x_{n}),\forall\,j\neq i,\xi_{j}\leq(1-\varepsilon)x_{n})

\displaystyle\geq n\sum_{y=(1-\varepsilon)x_{n}}^{(1+\varepsilon)x_{n}}\mathbb{P}(\xi=y)\mathbb{P}(S_{n-1}=x_{n}-y,M_{n-1}\leq(1-\varepsilon)x_{n})

\displaystyle\geq n\;\min_{|y-x_{n}|\leq\varepsilon x_{n}}\mathbb{P}(\xi=y)\;\,\mathbb{P}\big(|S_{n-1}|\leq\varepsilon x_{n},M_{n-1}\leq(1-\varepsilon)x_{n}\big).

Since
\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{x_{n}}{a_{n}}=\infty
, the last probability in the last line goes to
1
.
Using also that
\mathbb{P}(\xi=x)
is intermediate regularly varying, recall (
2.11
), we have that

\lim_{\varepsilon\downarrow 0}\liminf_{n\to\infty}\min_{|y-x_{n}|\leq\varepsilon x_{n}}\frac{\mathbb{P}(\xi=y)}{\mathbb{P}(\xi=x_{n})}=1\,.

We therefore end up with

\lim_{\varepsilon\downarrow 0}\liminf_{n\to\infty}\frac{Q_{1}}{n\mathbb{P}(\xi=x_{n})}\geq 1.

In the other direction, we have

Q_{1}\leq n\sum_{y=(1-\varepsilon)x_{n}}^{(1+\varepsilon)x_{n}}\mathbb{P}(\xi=y)\mathbb{P}(S_{n-1}=x_{n}-y)\leq n\max_{|y-x_{n}|\leq\varepsilon x_{n}}\mathbb{P}(\xi=y).

Using again the intermediate regular variation of
\mathbb{P}(\xi=x)
, we therefore end up with

\lim_{\varepsilon\downarrow 0}\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{Q_{1}}{n\mathbb{P}(\xi=x_{n})}\leq 1.

In the case
\liminf_{n\to\infty}\frac{x_{n}}{n}>0
, we can deal with all the terms
Q_{0},Q_{1},Q_{2},Q_{3}
exactly as above.
Recalling that in this case we have
r_{n}=O(1)
, its then only remains to show that for any
R>0
the term
\hat{Q}_{4}:=\mathbb{P}(S_{n}=x_{n},M_{n}\leq R)
is negligible compared to
n\mathbb{P}(\xi=x_{n})
.
But one simply bounds
\hat{Q}_{4}:=\mathbb{P}(S_{n}=x_{n},M_{n}\leq R)\leq\mathbb{P}(S_{n}\geq x_{n},M_{n}\leq R)\leq e^{-cx_{n}}
, by a standard large deviation bound (already mentioned in the integral case); this is negligible compared to
n\mathbb{P}(\xi=x_{n})
.
∎

6.3
Proof of Theorem
2.19

Recall that we assume
\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{x_{n}}{a_{n}}=\infty
and that
\mathbb{P}(\xi=x)
is intermediate regularly varying. For simplicity of notation, we also assume that
\mu=0
, so
b_{n}=0
.

We start with the proof of the first convergence, following the approach in
[
AL09
]
.
Let
0<\varepsilon<1
. Using (
2.31
), we know that

\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\big(S_{n}=x_{n},|M_{n}-x_{n}|\leq\varepsilon x_{n}\,\big|\,S_{n}=x_{n},M_{n}>r_{n}\big)=1\,.

For simplicity of notation, let

\mathcal{M}_{1}=\big(\mathscr{L}(\xi)\big)^{\otimes(n-1)},\quad\mathcal{M}_{2}=\mathscr{L}\big(R(\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{n})\,\big|\,S_{n}=x_{n},|M_{n}-x_{n}|\leq\varepsilon x_{n}\big)\,,

so we only need to show that

\lim_{n\to\infty}d_{TV}(\mathcal{M}_{1},\mathcal{M}_{2})=0
.

Let
A
be any measurable set in
\mathbb{Z}^{n-1}
.
Let
0<\varepsilon<1
. We define the event

B_{n}=\{S_{n}-b_{n}=x_{n}\}\cap\{M_{n-1}<(1-\varepsilon)x_{n}\}\cap\{|S_{n-1}-b_{n}|<\varepsilon x_{n}\}\,,

which is a subset of the event
\{M_{n-1}<(1-\varepsilon)x_{n}<\xi_{n}=M_{n}<(1+\varepsilon)x_{n}\}
.
Then

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}

\displaystyle(S_{n}=x_{n},\,|M_{n}-x_{n}|\leq\varepsilon x_{n})\mathcal{M}_{2}(A)=\mathbb{P}(R(\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{n})\in A,\,S_{n}=x_{n},\,|M_{n}-x_{n}|\leq\varepsilon x_{n})

\displaystyle\geq n\mathbb{P}\Big((\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{n-1})\in A,\,B_{n},\,|\xi_{n}-x_{n}|<\varepsilon x_{n}\Big)

\displaystyle\geq n\min_{|t-x_{n}|<\varepsilon x_{n}}\mathbb{P}(\xi=t)\mathcal{M}_{1}\Big((\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{n-1})\in A,\,M_{n-1}<(1-\varepsilon)x_{n},\,|S_{n-1}|<\varepsilon x_{n}\Big).

Using the Potter’s bound (
A.2
)
for
\mathbb{P}(\xi=x)
, we obtain that

\min_{|t-x_{n}|<\varepsilon x_{n}}\mathbb{P}(\xi=t)\geq c(\varepsilon)\mathbb{P}(\xi=x_{n})

for
n
large enough,
with
\lim_{\varepsilon\downarrow 0}c(\varepsilon)=1
.
We also have

\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathcal{M}_{1}(M_{n-1}<(1-\varepsilon)x_{n})=1,\quad\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathcal{M}_{1}(|S_{n-1}|<\varepsilon x_{n})=1\,,

using that
\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{x_{n}}{a_{n}}=\infty
for the last limit.
Thus we get that

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{2}(A)\geq\frac{n\mathbb{P}(\xi=x_{n})}{\mathbb{P}(S_{n}-b_{n}=x_{n},|M_{n}-x_{n}|\leq\varepsilon x_{n})}\mathcal{M}_{1}(A)(1+o(1)),

where the error term
o(1)
is uniformly small in all sets
A\subset\mathbb{Z}^{n-1}
.
Using Proposition
2.16
, we obtain that

\mathcal{M}_{2}(A)\geq(1+o(1))\mathcal{M}_{1}(A)
as
n\to\infty
.
Since this holds uniformly for any set
A
, we conclude that

\lim_{n\to\infty}d_{\mathrm{TV}}(\mathcal{M}_{1},\mathcal{M}_{2})=0
.

For the second convergence, letting
\tilde{\mathcal{M}}_{2}=\mathscr{L}\big(R(\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{n})\,\big|\,S_{n}=x_{n},M_{n}\leq r_{n}\big)
, we also need to prove that

.
But this is clear: if one writes

and consider the event

, then we naturally have that
\tilde{\mathcal{M}}_{2}(A_{n})=1
, but
\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathcal{M}(A_{n})=0
, since
x_{n}^{\prime}:=x_{n}-r_{n}
verifies
\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{x_{n}^{\prime}}{a_{n}}=\infty
.

For the last result, (
2.35
) follows directly from Proposition
2.16
.

7.1
A regular conditional distribution

We give here a concrete construction of

\mathscr{L}\big(R(\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{n})\,\big|\,M_{n}=x\big),\quad x\in\mathbb{R}

(7.1)

as follows:

•

If
\mathbb{P}(x-\delta\leq\xi\leq x+\delta)=0
for some
\delta>0,

i.e.

x
is not in the support, we can basically assume any conditional distribution. But for simplicity, let

\mathscr{L}\big(R(\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{n})\,\big|\,M_{n}=x\big)=\mathscr{L}\big(\xi\big)^{\otimes(n-1)}.

(7.2)

•

If
\mathbb{P}(\xi=x)>0,
then we use the usual conditional probability formula to define (
7.1
).

•

If
\mathbb{P}(\xi=x)=0
and
\mathbb{P}(x-\delta\leq\xi\leq x+\delta)>0
for any
\delta>0
, it turns out that

\lim_{\delta\to 0}d_{\mathrm{TV}}\left(\mathscr{L}\big(R(\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{n})\,\big|\,x-\delta\leq M_{n}\leq x+\delta\big),\mathscr{L}\big(\xi\,\big|\,\xi\leq x\big)^{\otimes(n-1)}\right)=0.

(7.3)

This is proved in Proposition
7.1
below. Then we can define

\mathscr{L}\big(R(\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{n})\,\big|\,M_{n}=x\big)=\mathscr{L}\big(\xi\,\big|\,\xi\leq x\big)^{\otimes(n-1)}.

(7.4)

Using standard measure-theoretic approaches, the construction indeed gives a version of regular conditional distribution.
It remains to prove the following proposition.

Proposition 7.1
.

Let
\xi
be any real-valued random variable. Assume that
x\in\mathbb{R}
is such that

\mathbb{P}(\xi=x)=0
and
\mathbb{P}(x-\delta\leq\xi\leq x+\delta)>0
for any
\delta>0
, then (
7.3
) holds.

Proof.

Fix
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
x\in\mathbb{R}
satisfying the assumptions of Proposition
7.1
.
First of all, by assumption, it is straightforward to see that since
F
is continuous at

\mathbb{P}\big(M_{n}\in[x-\delta,x+\delta]\big)\sim n\mathbb{P}\big(\xi\in[x-\delta,x+\delta]\big)F(x)^{n-1}=o(1),\quad\text{ as }\delta\downarrow 0.

(7.5)

For
A\subset\mathscr{B}(\mathbb{R}^{n-1})
, let us bound

\displaystyle\Big|\mathbb{P}\big(R(\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{n})

\displaystyle\in A\,\big|\,M_{n}\in[x-\delta,x+\delta]\big)-\mathscr{L}\big(\xi\,\big|\,\xi\leq x\big)^{\otimes(n-1)}(A)\Big|

\displaystyle=\bigg|\frac{\mathbb{P}\big(R(\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{n})\in A,M_{n}\in[x-\delta,x+\delta]\big)}{\mathbb{P}(M_{n}\in[x-\delta,x+\delta])}-\mathscr{L}\big(\xi\,\big|\,\xi\leq x\big)^{\otimes(n-1)}(A)\bigg|

The first term is

The second term

\!\!\!\!\bigg|\frac{\mathbb{P}\big(R(\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{n})\in A\cap(-\infty,x-\delta)^{n-1},M_{n}\in[x-\delta,x+\delta]\big)}{\mathbb{P}(M_{n}\in[x-\delta,x+\delta])}-\mathscr{L}\big(\xi\,\big|\,\xi<x-\delta\big)^{\otimes(n-1)}(A)\bigg|\\

\tilde{P}_{2}
is

\!\!\!\bigg|\frac{\mathbb{P}\big(R(\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{n})\in A\cap(-\infty,x_{n})^{n-1},M_{n}=x_{n}\big)}{\mathbb{P}(M_{n}=x_{n})}-\mathbb{P}\big((\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{n-1})\in A\cap(-\infty,x_{n})^{n-1}\big)\bigg|\\
=\left|\frac{n\mathbb{P}(\xi=x_{n})}{\mathbb{P}(M_{n}=x_{n})}-1\right|\mathbb{P}\big((\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{n-1})\in A\cap(-\infty,x_{n})^{n-1}\big)\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}0\,,

using again (
7.8
).
The last term is
\tilde{P}_{3}:=\mathbb{P}(M_{n-1}\geq x_{n})
, which goes to
0
, thanks to (
7.6
).
This concludes the proof of (
4.3
).
∎

Appendix A
Discussions on Rozovskii’s theorem

In this section, we make some comments on Rozovskii’s result, in several directions:

•

We discuss the condition under which Theorem
2.1
is true: Rozovskii states its result in terms of the function
q(\cdot)
, recall the definition (
2.1
); we show in Section
A.1
that it is equivalent to (
2.2
), which is in turn equivalent to
\overline{F}
being extended regularly varying.

•

We discuss how one may find equivalent (or sufficient) conditions to have (
2.4
): our goal is to obtain a more tractable condition in order to get the large deviation asymptotics (
2.3
); this is the purpose of Proposition
A.2
below.

•

We provide some examples of distributions for which Rozovskii’s result can be applied, some for which it cannot be applied.

Again, for simplicity of notation, we assume that
\mu=\mathbb{E}[\xi]=0
in this appendix.

A.1
About the condition (
2.2
) and extended/intermediate regular variation

In
[
Roz90
, Thm. 6]
, Rozovskii assumes that the function
q
defined in (
2.1
) by
q(x)=x^{2}\overline{F}(x)/\sigma^{2}(x)

verifies that there exists some
c>0
such that
x^{c}q(x)
is asymptotically equivalent to a non-decreasing
function.
We now state some equivalent statements and in particular we show that it is equivalent to
\overline{F}
being extended regularly varying, see (
2.10
).

Claim A.1
.

Assume that
x\mapsto\sigma^{2}(x)
is slowly varying at
\infty
. The following statements are equivalent:

(i)

\exists

c>0
such that
x^{c}q(x)
is equivalent to a non-decreasing function;

(ii)

\exists

c>0
such that
x^{c}\overline{F}(x)
is equivalent to a non-decreasing function, i.e. (
2.2
);

(iii)

there is a constant
c>0
and a function
y\mapsto\delta_{y}
such that
\lim_{y\to\infty}\delta_{y}=0
, for which one has

\overline{F}(x)\leq\overline{F}(y)\leq(1+\delta_{y})\Big(\frac{x}{y}\Big)^{c}\,\overline{F}(x)\,\qquad\forall x\geq y\,;

(A.1)

(iv)

The function
\overline{F}
is extended regularly varying at infinity (see (
2.10
)).

Proof.

(i)\Rightarrow(ii)
.
If
x^{c}q(x)
is asymptotically equivalent to a non-decreasing function
v(x)
,
then

x^{c+2}\overline{F}(x)=x^{c}\sigma^{2}(x)q(x)=(1+o(1))\sigma^{2}(x)v(x),

with both
\sigma^{2}(x)
and
v(x)
non-decreasing. Thus condition (
2.2
) follows.

(ii)\Rightarrow(i)
.
If
x^{c}\overline{F}(x)
is asymptotically equivalent to a non-decreasing function
v(x)
(
i.e.
assuming (
2.2
)), then
x^{c}q(x)=(1+o(1))v(x)x^{2}/\sigma^{2}(x)
.
Note that
x^{2}/\sigma^{2}(x)
is asymptotically equivalent to
x^{2}/\overline{\sigma}^{2}(x)
(recall that
\overline{\sigma}(x):=\mathbb{E}[(|\xi|\wedge x)^{2}]
) which is differentiable with derivative
\frac{2x}{\overline{\sigma}^{2}(x)}(1-\frac{x^{2}\mathbb{P}(|\xi|>x)}{\overline{\sigma}^{2}(x)})\geq 0
for
x
large. Hence
x^{c}q(x)
is asymptotic to a non-decreasing function.

(ii)\Rightarrow(iii)
. If
x^{c}\overline{F}(x)
is asymptotically equivalent to an increasing function
v(x)
, we can write
x^{c}\overline{F}(x)=(1+\varepsilon_{x})v(x)
, with
\lim_{x\to\infty}\varepsilon_{x}=0
. Hence, for
y\leq x
, using that
v
is increasing, we have

\frac{y^{c}\overline{F}(y)}{x^{c}\overline{F}(x)}=\frac{1+\varepsilon_{y}}{1+\varepsilon_{x}}\frac{v(y)}{v(x)}\leq\frac{1+|\varepsilon_{y}|}{1-\inf_{x\geq y}|\varepsilon_{x}|}\to 1,\,\text{ as }y\to\infty,

which proves the upper bound in (
A.1
) (the lower bound is trivial).

(iii)\Rightarrow(ii)
.
Let
v(y)=y^{c}\overline{F}(y)
and
\tilde{v}(y)=\inf_{x\geq y}v(x)
.
Then, clearly,
\tilde{v}
is non-decreasing and by (
A.1
) we have that

(1+\delta_{y})^{-1}v(y)\leq\tilde{v}(y)\leq v(y)
, so
v(y)
and
\tilde{v}(y)
are asymptotically equivalent. Hence
(ii)
is verified.

(iii)\Rightarrow(iv)
is obvious from the definition of extended regular variation; note that (
A.1
) gives the upper and lower Matuszewska indices are
c
and
1
respectively.

(iv)\Rightarrow(iii)
follows by Potter’s bound for extended regularly varying functions, see
[
Cli94
, Thm. 2.3]
: for any
\delta>0
, there is some
x_{0}>0
such that for any
\lambda<1
and
x\geq x_{0}

1\leq\frac{\overline{F}(\lambda x)}{\overline{F}(x)}\leq(1+\delta)\lambda^{-c-1}
,
where
c
is the upper Matuszewska index;
the lower bound simply follows from the fact that
\overline{F}
is non-increasing.
∎

Claim A.2
.

The fact that function
\overline{F}
is intermediate regularly varying (see (
2.11
))
is equivalent to the following:
there exists some non-decreasing function
\kappa:[1,\infty)\to[1,\infty)
such that

\overline{F}(x)\leq\overline{F}(y)\leq(1+\delta_{y})\kappa\Big(\frac{x}{y}\Big)\,\overline{F}(x)\qquad\forall x\geq y\,,

(A.2)

with
\kappa
satisfying
\lim_{s\to 1+}\kappa(s)=1
and
\lim_{s\to\infty}\frac{1}{s}\log\kappa(s)=0
.
Additionally, there is a constant
c>0
such that

\kappa(s)\leq cs^{c},\text{ for all }s\geq 1.

(A.3)

Proof.

Note that (
A.2
) readily implies intermediate regular variation by verifying the definition.
For the reverse implication, we proceed exactly as for Claim
A.1
: using Potter’s bound
[
Cli94
, Thm. 2.3]
for intermediate regularly varying functions, we obtain that for all
\delta>0
there is some
x_{0}>0
such that for any
\lambda>1
and
x\geq x_{0}
,

1\leq\frac{\overline{F}(\lambda x)}{\overline{F}(x)}\leq(1+\delta)\kappa(\lambda)^{-1}
for some
\kappa(\lambda)
with
\lim_{\lambda\to 1}\kappa(\lambda)=1
.

The fact that
\kappa
grows at most polynomially follows directly from the representation theorem for intermediate regularly varying functions, see
[
Cli94
, Cor. 3.2I]
.
∎

A.2
About the condition (
2.4
) in Theorem
2.1

We now find equivalent conditions to the condition (
2.4
) in Theorem
2.1
.
Recall that we defined

\overline{\sigma}^{2}(x)=\mathbb{E}[(|\xi|\wedge x)^{2}]=2\int_{0}^{x}t\mathbb{P}(|\xi|>t)dt\,,

and let
(a_{n})_{n\geq 1}
be a sequence defined by the relation (

\overline{\sigma}^{2}(x)=\sigma^{2}(x)+x^{2}\mathbb{P}(|\xi|>x),

(A.4)

we have

This shows that

defined by (

i.e.

Proposition A.3
.

Let

, let

A.2.1
Preliminary observations

Let us first give some consequences of the intermediate regular variation of
\overline{F}
, which we recall is equivalent to (
A.2
).

Claim A.4
.

Assume that
x\mapsto\sigma^{2}(x)
is slowly varying at infinity and that
\overline{F}
is intermediate regularly varying. Let
x\geq y>0.
Then for
y
large enough

\left(\frac{y}{x}\right)^{2}\leq\frac{q(y)}{q(x)}\leq(1+\delta_{y})\kappa\Big(\frac{x}{y}\Big)\frac{y}{x},

(A.7)

where
\delta_{y}
and
\kappa(\cdot)
are the same as in (
A.2
).

Proof.

Note that by (
A.2
), we have

1\leq\frac{\overline{F}(y)}{\overline{F}(x)}\leq(1+\delta_{y})\kappa(\frac{x}{y})
for all
x\geq y>0
. Then we can use the definition (
2.1
) of
q
to obtain

\frac{\sigma^{2}(x)}{\sigma^{2}(y)}\left(\frac{y}{x}\right)^{2}\leq\frac{q(y)}{q(x)}\leq(1+\delta_{y})\kappa\left(\frac{x}{y}\right)\frac{\sigma^{2}(x)}{\sigma^{2}(y)}\left(\frac{y}{x}\right)^{2}.

Note that
\sigma^{2}(x)\geq\sigma^{2}(y)
for
x\geq y>0
using monotonicity, and
\frac{\sigma^{2}(x)}{\sigma^{2}(y)}\leq\frac{x}{y}
for

large enough using Potter’s bound for slowly varying function. Then we obtain (
A.7
).
∎

Lemma A.5
.

Assume that
x\mapsto\sigma^{2}(x)
is slowly varying at infinity and that
\overline{F}
is intermediate regularly varying. Then uniformly for
\frac{x}{C|\log q(x)|}\leq y\leq x
,
we have

|\log q(x)|\sim|\log q(y)|\qquad\text{as }x\to\infty.

In particular, recalling that
\omega_{n}:=\frac{a_{n}}{\sqrt{|\log q(a_{n})|}}
, we have
|\log q(\omega_{n})|\sim|\log q(a_{n})|
as
n\to\infty
.

Similarly, the above display also holds uniformly for
x\leq y\leq Cx|\log q(x)|
.

Proof.

We will prove only the first part as the second part follows similar lines of reasoning. Recall that
\lim_{x\to\infty}q(x)=0
, see (
1.4
).
Let us assume that
x
is large enough so that
\log q(x)<0
and let us set
h:=x/y
, which verifies
1\leq h\leq C|\log q(x)|
.
Using the second inequality in (
A.7
), we have

\frac{q(y)}{q(x)}\leq(1+\delta_{y})\frac{\kappa(h)}{h}\leq 2\frac{\kappa(h)}{h},\quad\text{ for }x\text{ large enough}.

Then

\log q(x)\geq\log q(y)-\log 2\kappa(h)+\log h
, so that

|\log q(x)|\leq|\log q(y)|+\log 2\kappa(h)\leq|\log q(y)|+\log 2\kappa(C|\log q(x)|)\,,

using that
\kappa
is non-decreasing.
On the other hand, the first inequality in (
A.7
) shows that
\frac{q(y)}{q(x)}\geq h^{-2}
,
which implies

|\log q(x)|\geq|\log q(y)|-2\log h\geq|\log q(y)|-2\log\kappa(C|\log q(x)|).

Since
\lim_{x\to\infty}q(x)=0
and recalling that
\lim_{s\to\infty}\frac{1}{s}\log\kappa(s)=0
, see (
A.2
), this concludes the proof.
∎

Remark A.6
.

Thanks to Lemma

, if
x_{n}\sim ca_{n}\sqrt{|\log q(a_{n})|}

Hence, using also that

On the other hand, recalling the definition (

In conclusion, a sufficient condition to have that

A.3
A few examples

First, let us give a generic example where Rozovskii’s condition is verified.

Example A.10
.

Assume that
\overline{F}(x)\sim L(x)x^{-\beta}
as
x\to\infty
, for some slowly varying function
L(\cdot)
and some
\beta\geq 2
.
Assume also that the left tail is “dominated” by the right tail, in the sense that
F(-x)\sim c\overline{F}(x)
for some
c\geq 0
; with
F(-x)=o(\overline{F}(x))
if
c=0
.
This is for instance the case if
\xi=X-\mathbb{E}[X]
for some non-negative random variable
X
.

If
\beta>2
, then we have
\mathbb{E}[|\xi|^{2+\delta}]<\infty
for some
\delta>0
, so Nagaev’s condition is verified.
We will therefore focus on the case
\beta=2
: Nagaev’s condition is not verified, but we will see that Rozovskii’s condition (
2.4
) is verified for
a_{n}
defined by
a_{n}^{2}=n\overline{\sigma}^{2}(a_{n})
.

If
\beta=2
, then we have
\overline{\sigma}^{2}(x)=2\int_{0}^{x}t\mathbb{P}(|\xi|>t)dt
, with
\mathbb{P}(|\xi|>t)\sim(1+c)L(t)t^{-2}
as
t\to\infty
. Denote
\ell(t)=2t^{2}\mathbb{P}(|\xi|>t)
, so that

\overline{\sigma}(x)^{2}=\int_{0}^{x}\ell(t)t^{-1}\text{\rm\,d}t

(A.16)

and note that
\ell(x)\sim 2(1+c)L(x)
as
x\to\infty
.
Then we have that
q(x)\sim c^{\prime}\ell(x)/\sigma^{2}(x)
, with
\ell(x)/\sigma^{2}(x)
which is known to verify
\lim_{x\to\infty}\ell(x)/\sigma^{2}(x)=0
, see
[
BGT89
, Prop. 1.5.9.a]
.

Then, thanks to Proposition
A.3
, we simply need to verify that

\sigma^{2}(x\sqrt{|\log q(x)|})-\sigma^{2}(x)=o\left(\frac{\sigma^{2}(x)}{|\log q(x)|}\right)\,.

(A.17)

Since
\overline{\sigma}^{2}(x)-\sigma^{2}(x)=x^{2}\mathbb{P}(|\xi|>x)=\ell(x)

and since
\ell(x)\sim 2(1+c)\sigma^{2}(x)q(x)=o\left(\frac{\sigma^{2}(x)}{|\log q(x)|}\right)
, it suffices to prove the above display with
\sigma^{2}(\cdot)
replaced by
\overline{\sigma}^{2}(\cdot)
on the l.h.s. of (
A.17
), thanks to Lemma
A.5
.
We can use Claim
A.11
below to get that

\begin{split}0\leq\overline{\sigma}^{2}(x\sqrt{|\log q(x)|})-\overline{\sigma}^{2}(x)&\leq\ell(x)\Big(\tfrac{1}{2}\log|\log q(x)|+\varepsilon_{x}\sqrt{|\log q(x)|}\Big)\\
&\leq C\sigma^{2}(x)q(x)\sqrt{|\log q(x)|}=o\left(\frac{\sigma^{2}(x)}{|\log q(x)|}\right)\,,\end{split}

which concludes the proof.∎

Claim A.11
.

Let
\ell
be a non-negative slowly varying function.
Then there exists
\varepsilon_{x}
that verifies
\lim_{x\to\infty}\varepsilon_{x}=0
such that for any
z\geq x

\int_{x}^{z}\frac{\ell(t)}{t}\text{\rm\,d}t\leq\ell(x)\Big(\log\Big(\frac{z}{x}\Big)+\varepsilon_{x}\,\Big(\frac{z}{x}-1\Big)\Big)\,,\qquad\text{ as }x\to\infty\,.

Proof.

Setting
g(y):=\ell(y)y^{-1}
, we get by a change of variable that

\int_{x}^{z}\ell(t)t^{-1}\text{\rm\,d}t=\ell(x)\int_{1}^{z/x}\frac{g(ux)}{g(x)}\text{\rm\,d}u\,.

Now, using that
g
is regularly varying of index
-1
, we have that uniformly for
u\geq 1
,
g(ux)/g(x)=u^{-1}+\varepsilon_{x}
with
\lim_{x\to\infty}\varepsilon_{x}=0
(this is the uniform convergence theorem, see
[
BGT89
]
).
We end up with

0\leq\int_{x}^{z}\ell(t)t^{-1}\text{\rm\,d}t\leq\ell(x)\Big(\log\Big(\frac{z}{x}\Big)+\varepsilon_{x}\,\Big(\frac{z}{x}-1\Big)\Big)\,,

which concludes the proof.
∎

We now present an example where the left tail is heavier than the right tail: we give some conditions for (
2.4
) to hold; this will also stress the importance of the choice of the normalising sequence
(a_{n})_{n\geq 1}
.

Example A.12
.

Assume that
\xi
is centered and that
\sigma^{2}=\mathrm{Var}(\xi)<\infty
. We will focus on a case where Nagaev’s condition
\mathbb{E}[|\xi|^{2+\delta}]<\infty
for some
\delta>0
is not satisfied.
We consider a case where
|\log q(x)|\asymp\log x
as
x\to\infty
; this is ensured for instance if the right tail verifies
x^{-\beta_{+}}\leq\overline{F}(x)\leq x^{-\beta_{-}}
for some
\beta_{+}>\beta_{-}>2
(for
x
large).

Our goal is to discuss the conditions imposed by (
2.4
) on the left tail of the distribution. We assume that
F(-x)\sim\ell(x)x^{-2}
for some slowly varying function
\ell(\cdot)
; think about taking
\ell(x)=(\log x)^{-a}
for some
a>1
(so that
\sigma^{2}<\infty
).
We now compare two possible choices for the normalising sequence
(a_{n})_{n\geq 1}
.

If one defines
a_{n}=\sigma\sqrt{n}
, then recalling (
2.7
), (
A.5
) and (
A.16
), (
2.4
) is equivalent to

\int_{x}^{\infty}\frac{\ell(t)}{t}\text{\rm\,d}t\sim\mathbb{E}[\xi^{2}{\text{\Large$\mathfrak{1}$}}_{\{|\xi|>x\}}]=o\left(\frac{1}{\log x}\right)\,,

(recall also that
|\log q(x)|\asymp\log x
).
Since
\ell(x)=(\log x)^{-a}
for some
a>1
, this amounts to having
a>2
for this particular choice of
a_{n}
.

If on the other hand one defines
a_{n}
by
a_{n}^{2}=n\overline{\sigma}(a_{n})
, then Proposition
A.3
tells that (
2.4
) is equivalent to

\mathbb{E}[\xi^{2}{\text{\Large$\mathfrak{1}$}}_{\{x<|\xi|<x\sqrt{|\log q(x)|}\}}]\,\sim\,\int_{x}^{x\sqrt{|\log q(x)}|}\frac{\ell(t)}{t}\text{\rm\,d}t=o\left(\frac{1}{\log x}\right)

By Claim
A.11
, we have that the integral is
o(\ell(x)\sqrt{\log x})
(using that
|\log q(x)|\asymp\log x
), so it is enough to have
\ell(x)\leq(\log x)^{-3/2}
(or
a>3/2
).
If
\ell(x)
is non-increasing, then the integral is bounded by a constant times
\ell(x)\log\log x
, so a sufficient condition becomes
\ell(x)=o(\frac{1}{\log x\log\log x})
; this is verified if
\ell(x)=(\log x)^{-a}
with any
a>1
.∎

Example A.13
.

Let us consider the case where
\xi
is centered and where the left and right tails verify
\overline{F}(x)\sim L(x)x^{-2}
and
F(-x)\sim\ell(x)x^{-2}
, with the left tail being heavier, that is
L(x)=o(\ell(x))
as
x\to\infty
.
In this case,
q(x)
is slowly varying and the interplay between the slowly varying functions
L,\ell,\sigma^{2}
is subtle.
For simplicity, let us consider the case where
\ell(x)\sim(\log x)^{-a}
for some
a\in\mathbb{R}
.
We will find conditions on the right tail for (
2.4
) to be verified.

If
\sigma^{2}<\infty
(i.e.
a>1
), let us set for now
a_{n}=\sigma\sqrt{n}
.
We have that

\mathbb{E}[\xi^{2}{\text{\Large$\mathfrak{1}$}}_{\{|\xi|>x\}}]\sim\int_{x}^{\infty}\frac{\ell(t)}{t}\text{\rm\,d}t\sim(\log x)^{1-a}\,,

so (
2.7
) is equivalent to
|\log q(x)|=o((\log x)^{a-1})
.
If
a\geq 2
, the fact that
q
is slowly varying ensures that
|\log q(x)|=o(\log x)
, thus (
2.7
) holds. If
a\in(1,2)
, we have to check the right tail: for instance, if
L(x)\asymp e^{(\log x)^{b}}
for some
b\in(0,1)
, then
|\log q(x)|\asymp(\log x)^{b}
and the condition (
2.7
) is verified provided that
a-1>b
.

If we define
a_{n}
by the relation
a_{n}^{2}=n\overline{\sigma}^{2}(a_{n})
, we need to consider

\mathbb{E}\big[\xi^{2}{\text{\Large$\mathfrak{1}$}}_{\{x<|\xi|<x\sqrt{|\log q(x)|}\}}\big]\sim\int_{x}^{x\sqrt{|\log q(x)|}}\frac{\ell(t)}{t}\text{\rm\,d}t\sim\frac{1}{2}(\log x)^{-a}\log|\log q(x)|\,.

Therefore, (
2.6
) is equivalent to

|\log q(x)|\log(|\log q(x)|)=o\left(\sigma^{2}(x)(\log x)^{a}\right)
.
If
a>1
, this is automatic, since we have
|\log q(x)|=o(\log x)
as noticed above.
If
a=1
, this is also verified thanks to the same observation, using also that
\sigma^{2}(x)\sim\log\log x
.
If
a<1
, then
\sigma^{2}(x)\asymp(\log x)^{1-a}
, so the condition becomes
|\log q(x)|\log|\log q(x)|=o(\log x)
;
this is verified for instance if in the right tail we have
L(x)\asymp e^{(\log x)^{b}}
for some
b\in(0,1)
, but is not verified if one takes for instance
L(x)\asymp\exp(\log x/\log\log x)
.∎

We now provide an example of a distribution in the domain of attraction of the normal law where one cannot find any normalising sequence
(a_{n})_{n\geq 1}
that verifies Rozovskii’s condition (
2.4
).

Example A.14
.

The example is constructed to make the left tail too heavy compared to the right tail. Take some function
q(x)
and suppose that
\sigma^{2}(x)=\exp\big(\log x/\log^{(3)}q(x)\big)
, for
x
large enough with
\log^{(3)}t=\log\log\log\frac{1}{t}
. Let
(a_{n})_{n\geq 1}
be any normalising sequence, i.e. verifying (
1.5
).

Since
\omega_{n}=a_{n}/\sqrt{|\log q(a_{n})|}
, we have that

\frac{\log\omega_{n}}{\log^{(3)}q(\omega_{n})}=\frac{\log a_{n}}{\log^{(3)}q(\omega_{n})}-\frac{\frac{1}{2}\log|\log q(a_{n})|}{\log^{(3)}q(\omega_{n})}.

So if we assume that
\log^{(3)}q(\omega_{n})-\log^{(3)}q(a_{n})=o(\log^{(3)}q(a_{n})/\log a_{n})
(take for instance
q(y)=y^{-1}
or
q(y)=1/\log y
), we get that

\frac{\log\omega_{n}}{\log^{(3)}q(\omega_{n})}=\frac{\log a_{n}}{\log^{(3)}q(a_{n})}-\frac{1}{2}\frac{\log|\log q(a_{n})|}{\log^{(3)}q(a_{n})}+o(1)\,.

Hence

\sigma^{2}(\omega_{n})\sim e^{-\frac{1}{2}\frac{\log|\log q(a_{n})|}{\log^{(3)}q(a_{n})}}\sigma^{2}(a_{n})\,

and

\left|\frac{n}{w_{n}^{2}}\sigma^{2}(\omega_{n})-\frac{a_{n}^{2}}{\omega_{n}^{2}}\right|=\frac{a_{n}^{2}}{\omega_{n}^{2}}\left|\frac{n\sigma^{2}(\omega_{n})}{a_{n}^{2}}-1\right|\,,

with
\frac{a_{n}^{2}}{\omega_{n}^{2}}\to\infty
and

\frac{n\sigma^{2}(\omega_{n})}{a_{n}^{2}}\sim e^{-\frac{1}{2}\frac{\log|\log q(a_{n})|}{\log^{(3)}q(a_{n})}}\frac{n\sigma^{2}(a_{n})}{a_{n}^{2}}\to 0.

This shows that

\left|\frac{n}{w_{n}^{2}}\sigma^{2}(\omega_{n})-\frac{a_{n}^{2}}{\omega_{n}^{2}}\right|\to\infty
, which tells that (
2.4
) does not hold.∎

Appendix B
One-big-jump phenomenon when
\alpha\in(0,2)

In this section we prove Corollaries
3.2
and
3.5
.
We begin with the following lemma.

Lemma B.1
.

For any
\xi
that is non-degenerate (i.e. whose law is not concentrated on a single point), we have that

d_{\mathrm{TV}}\Big(\mathscr{L}\big(R(\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{n})\big),\,\big(\mathscr{L}(\xi)\big)^{\otimes(n-1)}\Big)\text{ does not converge to }0\text{ as }n\to\infty.

(B.1)

Proof.

Let us denote

(\eta_{1},\eta_{2},\ldots,\eta_{n-1})=R(\xi_{1},\xi_{2},\ldots,\xi_{n})

and
N_{n}
be the second largest variable in
\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{n}
, so
N_{n}=\max\{\eta_{1},\ldots,\eta_{n-1}\}
.
Define
T:=\sup\{t:\mathbb{P}(\xi>t)>0\}
and let us distinguish two cases.

If
\mathbb{P}(\xi=T)>0
it is easy to see that

\mathbb{P}(\eta_{1}=T)=\mathbb{P}(\xi_{1}=\xi_{2}=T)=\mathbb{P}(\xi=T)^{2}<\mathbb{P}(\xi_{1}=T).

Thus (
B.1
) holds in this case.

If
\mathbb{P}(\xi=T)=0,
let
x
converge to
T
from the left and put
n=n(x)=\lfloor\frac{1}{\mathbb{P}(\xi>x)}\rfloor.
Then

\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}(M_{n-1}\leq x)=\lim_{n\to\infty}(1-\mathbb{P}(\xi>x))^{n-1}=e^{-1},

and

\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}(N_{n}\leq x)=\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}(M_{n}\leq x)+\lim_{n\to\infty}n\mathbb{P}(\xi>x)\mathbb{P}(M_{n-1}\leq x)=2e^{-1}.

Therefore

\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}(N_{n}\leq x)=e^{-1}\neq 2e^{-1}=\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}(M_{n-1}\leq x).

Hence (
B.1
) holds in this case as well.
∎

B.1
Proof of Corollary
3.2

The statement (
3.1
) is already contained in the proof of
[
Ber19
, Theorem 2.1]
, so we focus on the proof of (
3.2
).

The fact that
\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{x_{n}}{a_{n}}=\infty
is sufficient to obtain (
3.2
)
can be seen by using (
3.1
) and the statement (
4.2
) in Proposition
4.1
. We will prove here the necessity.

Let
(x_{n})_{n\geq 1}
be such that

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{x_{n}}{a_{n}}=l\in[-\infty,\infty)\,.

(B.2)

If
l=-\infty,
then

\lim_{n\to\infty}d_{\mathrm{TV}}\Big(\mathscr{L}\big(R(\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{n})\,\big|\,S_{n}-b_{n}\geq x_{n}\big),\,\mathscr{L}\big(R(\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{n})\big)\Big)=0,

(B.3)

since
\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}(S_{n}-b_{n}\geq x_{n})=1
. By Lemma
B.1
, (
3.2
) does not hold. Next we assume
l\in(-\infty,\infty)
and we prove that (
3.2
) does not hold.
We split the proof according to several cases: recall our assumption (
1.2
), which ensures the convergence of
S_{n}
(properly centered and normalised) to an
\alpha
-stable random variable
\mathcal{S}_{\alpha}
, recall (
1.1
).

(i) If
\alpha\in(0,1)
,
p=1
, and
l\in(-\infty,0]
.
The limiting
\alpha
-stable random variable
\mathcal{S}_{\alpha}
is then supported on
[0,\infty)
(see
[
Fel08
, XIII.7 Thm. 2]
and the following remark). Then we have that

\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}(S_{n}-b_{n}>x_{n})=1
and we deduce (
B.3
). By the same argument as in the case

, we conclude that (
3.2
) does not hold.

(ii) In the rest, we treat all the following cases in the same way (so we will not distinguish them in the following analysis):

\begin{cases}\alpha\in(0,1),\ p=1,\ l\in(0,\infty),\\[2.0pt]
\alpha\in(0,1),\ p\in(0,1),\ l\in(-\infty,\infty),\\[2.0pt]
\alpha\in[1,2),\ p\in(0,1],\ l\in(-\infty,\infty).\end{cases}

(B.4)

Let
\Lambda
be the Lévy measure associated to the
\alpha
-stable random variable
\mathcal{S}_{\alpha}
, given by
\Lambda(\mathrm{d}x)=\frac{2-\alpha}{\alpha}|x|^{-\alpha-1}(p{\text{\Large$\mathfrak{1}$}}_{\{x>0\}}+q{\text{\Large$\mathfrak{1}$}}_{\{x<0\}})\mathrm{d}x

the characteristic function of
\mathcal{S}_{\alpha}
is given by:

\varphi(t):=\mathbb{E}[e^{it\mathcal{S}_{\alpha}}]=\exp\left(\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}(e^{itx}-1-it\sin(x))d\Lambda(x)\right),\quad t\in\mathbb{R}\,.

(B.5)

Let
Y=(Y_{u})_{u\geq 0}
be a Lévy process with Lévy measure
\Lambda
with
Y_{0}=0
, and denote by
\zeta_{1},\zeta_{2}
the largest and second largest jump sizes of the Lévy process
Y
in the interval
[0,1]
. Let
N_{n}
be the second largest value of
\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{n}
. Then by (
B.5
) and the stable functional central limit theorem
[
Whi02
, Thm. 4.5.3]
, we have for
y\in\mathbb{R}

\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\big(a_{n}^{-1}N_{n}<y\,\big|\,S_{n}-b_{n}\geq x_{n}\big)=\mathbb{P}\big(\zeta_{2}<y\,\big|\,Y_{1}\geq l\big)

(B.6)

and

\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\big(a_{n}^{-1}M_{n}<y\big)=\mathbb{P}\big(\zeta_{1}<y\big)=\exp(-\Lambda_{y}),

(B.7)

where
\Lambda_{y}=\Lambda([y,\infty)).

To prove that (
3.2
) does not hold, it suffices to show that there exists some
y>0
such that

\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}(a_{n}^{-1}N_{n}<y\,|\,S_{n}-b_{n}\geq x_{n})\neq\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}(a_{n}^{-1}M_{n}<y)
,
or in other words that there is some
y>0
such that

\mathbb{P}(\zeta_{2}<y\,|\,Y_{1}\geq l)\neq\mathbb{P}(\zeta_{1}<y)=\exp(-\Lambda_{y}).

(B.8)

Considering jumps that occur on
[0,1]
, let
Y_{1}^{(1)}
be the sum of all jump sizes that are not smaller than
y
;
let also
Y_{1}^{(2)}=Y_{1}-Y_{1}^{(1)}
. Then
Y_{1}^{(1)}
and
Y_{1}^{(2)}
are independent, with respective characteristic functions

\mathbb{E}\Big[e^{itY_{1}^{(1)}}\Big]=\exp\left(\int_{y}^{\infty}(e^{itx}-1)d\Lambda(x)\right),\quad t\in\mathbb{R}

and

\mathbb{E}\Big[e^{itY_{1}^{(2)}}\Big]=\exp\left(\int_{y}^{\infty}-it\sin(x)d\Lambda(x)+\int_{-\infty}^{y}(e^{itx}-1-it\sin(x))d\Lambda(x)\right),\quad t\in\mathbb{R}.

Let
V
be a random variable with distribution
\Lambda{\text{\Large$\mathfrak{1}$}}_{[y,\infty)}/\Lambda_{y}
which is independent of
Y_{1}^{(2)}
. Note that if
\zeta_{1}<y
then
Y_{1}^{(1)}=0
: using this fact, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\zeta_{2}<y\,|\,Y_{1}\geq l)

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathbb{P}(\zeta_{1}<y,Y_{1}^{(2)}\geq l)}{\mathbb{P}(Y_{1}\geq l)}+\frac{\mathbb{P}(\zeta_{2}<y\leq\zeta_{1},\zeta_{1}+Y_{1}^{(2)}\geq l)}{\mathbb{P}(Y_{1}\geq l)}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}(K=0)\frac{\mathbb{P}(Y_{1}^{(2)}\geq l)}{\mathbb{P}(Y_{1}\geq l)}+\mathbb{P}(K=1)\frac{\mathbb{P}(V+Y_{1}^{(2)}\geq l)}{\mathbb{P}(Y_{1}\geq l)},

where
K
denotes the number of jumps with size not smaller than
y
. Note that
K
follows the Poisson distribution
\mathrm{Poi}(\Lambda_{y})
with parameter
\Lambda_{y}
and is independent of
Y_{1}^{(2)}
.
Then we have

\begin{split}\mathbb{P}(\zeta_{1}<y)&-\mathbb{P}(\zeta_{2}<y\,|\,Y_{1}\geq l)\\
&=\mathbb{P}(K=0)-\mathbb{P}(K=0)\frac{\mathbb{P}(Y_{1}^{(2)}\geq l)}{\mathbb{P}(Y_{1}\geq l)}-\mathbb{P}(K=1)\frac{\mathbb{P}(V+Y_{1}^{(2)}\geq l)}{\mathbb{P}(Y_{1}\geq l)}\\
&=\frac{\exp(-\Lambda_{y})}{\mathbb{P}(Y_{1}\geq l)}\left(\mathbb{P}(Y_{1}^{(2)}<l,\,Y_{1}^{(1)}+Y_{1}^{(2)}\geq l)-\Lambda_{y}\mathbb{P}(V+Y_{1}^{(2)}\geq l)\right)\\
&\leq\frac{\exp(-\Lambda_{y})}{\mathbb{P}(Y_{1}\geq l)}\left(\mathbb{P}(K>0)\mathbb{P}(Y_{1}^{(2)}<l)-\Lambda_{y}\mathbb{P}(Y_{1}^{(2)}\geq l-y)\right),\end{split}

(B.9)

where we have used the fact that
V\geq y
. Note that, as
y\to\infty
, we have that

Y_{1}^{(2)}
converges in distribution to
Y_{1}

and
\mathbb{P}(Y_{1}^{(2)}\geq l-y)
goes to
1
.
Since
\Lambda_{y}\to 0
, as
y\to\infty
, we use the above display to obtain that, as

,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(K>0)\mathbb{P}(Y_{1}^{(2)}<l)-\Lambda_{y}\mathbb{P}(Y_{1}^{(2)}\geq l-y)

\displaystyle=\big(1-e^{-\Lambda_{y}}\big)\mathbb{P}(Y_{1}^{(2)}<l)-\Lambda_{y}\mathbb{P}(Y_{1}^{(2)}\geq l-y)

\displaystyle=-\Lambda_{y}\mathbb{P}(Y_{1}\geq l)+o(\Lambda_{y})\,.

Therefore we have that

\mathbb{P}(K>0)\mathbb{P}(Y_{1}^{(2)}<l)-\Lambda_{y}\mathbb{P}(Y_{1}^{(2)}\geq l-y)<0

for
y
large enough.
Going back to (
B.9
), we end up with

\mathbb{P}(\zeta_{1}<y)-\mathbb{P}(\zeta_{2}<y\,|\,Y_{1}\geq l)<0

for
y
large enough, which proves (
B.8
).
This completes the proof for (

). ∎
