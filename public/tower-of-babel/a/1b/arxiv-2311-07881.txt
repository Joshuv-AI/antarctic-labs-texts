Accurate estimates of dynamical statistics using memory
Chatipat Lorpaiboon, Spencer C. Guo, John Strahan, Jonathan Weare, Aaron R. Dinner

Abstract
Many chemical reactions and molecular processes occur on timescales that are significantly longer than those accessible by direct simulation. One successful approach to estimating dynamical statistics for such processes is to use many short time series observations of the system to construct a Markov state model (MSM), which approximates the dynamics of the system as memoryless transitions between a set of discrete states. The dynamical Galerkin approximation (DGA) generalizes MSMs for the problem of calculating dynamical statistics, such as committors and mean first passage times, by replacing the set of discrete states with a projection onto a basis. Because the projected dynamics are generally not memoryless, the Markov approximation can result in significant systematic error. Inspired by quasi-Markov state models, which employ the generalized master equation to encode memory resulting from the projection, we reformulate DGA to account for memory and analyze its performance on two systems: a two-dimensional triple well and helix-to-helix transitions of the AIB 9 peptide. We demonstrate that our method is robust to the choice of basis and can decrease the time series length required to obtain accurate kinetics by an order of magnitude.



I
Introduction

Models that seek to treat complex systems with high fidelity typically have many variables (e.g., the positions and velocities of all the atoms in a molecular system). However, often the dynamics of interest of such models can be largely captured by a relatively small number of variables that are functions of the original ones (collective variables). Projecting the dynamics to these collective variables can facilitate analysis, by both improving convergence of statistics and simplifying interpretation. However, the coupling of the collective variables to orthogonal ones generally introduces a form of memory in which the projected dynamics depend not only on the states of the collective variables but their histories.

When the dynamics have a separation of time scales, it may be possible to find collective variables that minimize the memory, so that it can be neglected. This is the idea behind Markov State Models (MSMs), in which the dynamics are treated as hops between discrete states, and large numbers of states are often used in the hope of minimizing the time for equilibration within states.
However, not all dynamics have a clear separation of time scales, and, even if they do, finding collective variables that minimize the memory can be challenging
Guttenberg et al. 2013
.

Thus various strategies have been introduced to account for memory. One is to consider explicit histories of the collective variables, as in delay embedding
Liebert and Schuster 1989
;
Das and Giannakis 2019
;
Kamb et al. 2020
;
Thiede et al. 2019
;
Strahan et al. 2021
. However, this increases the effective number of variables, mitigating the advantages of projecting described above. An alternative is to account for the memory through the Mori–Zwanzig formalism
Mori 1965
;
Zwanzig 1983
. In this approach, the projected dynamics are represented through a generalized Langevin/Master equation (GLE/GME) that involves a time integral over the collective variable histories and a kernel. This approach is physically well-motivated and can in principle yield highly accurate dynamics. In practice, it can be hard to determine the memory kernel.

Traditionally, researchers chose the memory kernel based on physical intuition
Berkowitz et al. 1981
;
Berkowitz, Morgan, and McCammon 1983
, and many studies still assume a specific functional form for the kernel (most often exponential) when estimating it from data. A few studies allow for more flexibility by introducing an expansion in basis functions
Perico et al. 1993
;
Kostov and Freed 1997
;
Kostov and Freed 1999
;
Chorin, Hald, and Kupferman 2002
or alternative fitting forms
Lei, Baker, and Li 2016
;
Grogan et al. 2020
for the kernel or its Laplace/Fourier transforms
Ayaz et al. 2021
, and these show that it does not have a simple universal functional form. However, solving for the coefficients of the expansion can be numerically ill-posed
Lange and Grubmüller 2006
, and data-driven parameterization of GLEs/GMEs remains an active area of research
Lee, Ahn, and Darve 2019
;
Lin et al. 2021
;
Vroylandt et al. 2022
;
Aristoff, Johnson, and Perez 2023
;
Lin et al. 2023
.

Recently, approaching the problem from the perspective of improving the accuracy of MSMs with relatively small numbers of states
Cao et al. 2020
, Huang, Markland, Montoya-Castillo and co-workers obtained the memory kernel at successive discrete time points by directly inverting a GME for the state-to-state transition matrix
Cao et al. 2020
;
Cao et al. 2023
;
Dominic III et al. 2023
. This enabled them to significantly shorten the lag times needed to construct the transition matrix, which reduced the amount of simulation needed to converge statistics and increased the time resolution of the model; the small number of states facilitates interpretation.

Like many MSM studies, the studies described immediately above focus on computing implied time scales (via time correlation functions rather than directly from the eigenvalues of the transition matrix). However, MSMs can be used to solve for statistics that provide more direct insight into the mechanisms of specified events, such as the committor
Noé et al. 2009
, and we recently introduced a closely related framework that solves operator equations for such statistics, that we term dynamical Galerkin approximation (DGA)
Thiede et al. 2019
;
Strahan et al. 2021
. The goal of this paper is to show how memory can improve the accuracy of such statistics.

To this end, we incorporate memory into DGA by interpreting it as an iterative algorithm and applying a discrete-time Mori–Zwanzig formalism.
We present formulas for computing the aforementioned statistics, and numerically demonstrate on a two-dimensional triple well and on the AIB
9
peptide that memory indeed improves to accuracy of these statistics.

II
Theory

In this section, we briefly review DGA as previously presented
Thiede et al. 2019
;
Strahan et al. 2021
. We then recast it as an iteration to show that its solutions can be viewed as projections; the iteration and resulting projection operators lead naturally to a discrete-time GME and, in turn, an improved estimator. This estimator can be used without iteration.

II.1
Form of the problem

Let
X_{t}
denote a time-homogeneous ergodic Markov process at time
t
.
Our goal is to compute a statistic
u
of this process that satisfies an equation of the form

\dot{\mathcal{A}}^{0}u=-\dot{b}^{0},

(1)

where

is the rate of change in

We can interpret (

\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{u_{t}}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{t}}=\dot{\mathcal{A}}^{0}u_{t}+\dot{b}^{0}.

(2)

Integrating (
2

u_{t}=u_{t-\tau}+(\mathcal{A}^{\tau}u_{t-\tau}+b^{\tau}),

(3)

where
\mathcal{A}^{\tau}=\int_{0}^{\tau}\mathcal{C}^{\tau^{\prime}}\dot{\mathcal{A}}^{0}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{\tau^{\prime}}=\mathcal{C}^{\tau}-\mathcal{I}
,

,
\mathcal{C}^{\tau}=e^{\dot{\mathcal{A}}^{0}\tau}
, and
\mathcal{I}
is the identity operator.
Eq. (
3
), as we discuss further in Section

, corresponds to an iteration in

that is consistent with the dynamics in (

\mathcal{A}^{\tau}u=-b^{\tau}.

(4)

For example, the mean time to first enter the set

, where

(\mathcal{S}_{B}^{\tau}-\mathcal{I})m=-\int_{0}^{\tau}\mathcal{S}_{B}^{t}\mathbf{1}_{B^{\mathsf{c}}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{t},

(5)

subject to the boundary condition

for
x\in B
. Here,

\mathcal{S}_{B}^{\tau}f(x)=\E[f(X_{\tau\wedge T_{B}})\mid X_{0}=x]

(6)

is the stopped transition operator,

\int_{0}^{\tau}\mathcal{S}_{B}^{t}\mathbf{1}_{B^{\mathsf{c}}}(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{t}=\E[\tau\wedge T_{B}\mid X_{0}=x],

(7)

\tau\wedge T_{B}=\min\{\tau,T_{B}\}
, and
\mathbf{1}_{B^{\mathsf{c}}}(x)
is an indicator function that equals one for

and zero for

. We define other statistics of interest in Section
III
.

II.2
Dynamical Galerkin approximation (DGA)

DGA solves (
4
) by expanding
u
in a basis and estimating the resulting matrix elements by averages over random samples.
We represent the basis by a vector
\phi
of functions that satisfy homogeneous boundary conditions.
Then we approximate
u
by

\hat{u}=\hat{u}_{0}+\phi^{\mathsf{T}}v,

(8)

where
\hat{u}_{0}
is a “guess function” for
u
that satisfies the boundary conditions,
v
is a vector of coefficients, and we use the symbol
\hat{\ }
to denote functions that can be represented by (
8
).
To solve for the coefficients, we substitute (
8
) into (
4
), multiply from the left by
\phi
, and integrate over an arbitrary distribution of samples of initial conditions
\mu
. This yields

G^{\tau}v=-h^{\tau},

(9)

where

\displaystyle G^{\tau}

\displaystyle=\langle\phi,\mathcal{A}^{\tau}\phi^{\mathsf{T}}\rangle,

(10)

\displaystyle h^{\tau}

\displaystyle=\langle\phi,\mathcal{A}^{\tau}\hat{u}_{0}+b^{\tau}\rangle,

(11)

and
\langle f,g\rangle=\int f(x)g(x)\mu(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{x}
.
The matrix
G^{\tau}
and vector
h^{\tau}
are estimated from averages over trajectories of length
\tau
; explicit expressions are given in Sec.
III
.
Given these estimates, (
9
) can be solved directly for
v
.

II.3
Iterative solution

The form in (
3
) and our recent use of it in the context of inexact numerical linear algebra
Strahan et al. 2023a
suggests that one can view DGA as the fixed point of the
inexact
iteration

\displaystyle u^{\tau}_{t}

\displaystyle=\hat{u}^{\tau}_{t-\tau}+(\mathcal{A}^{\tau}\hat{u}^{\tau}_{t-\tau}+b^{\tau}),

(12a)

\displaystyle\hat{u}^{\tau}_{t}

\displaystyle=\hat{u}^{\tau}_{t-\tau}+\mathcal{P}(u^{\tau}_{t}-\hat{u}^{\tau}_{t-\tau}),

(12b)

where
\mathcal{P}
is an operator that projects functions onto linear combinations of the basis functions:

\mathcal{P}f=\phi^{\mathsf{T}}(K^{0})^{-1}\langle\phi,f\rangle,

(13)

and

K^{t}=\langle\phi,\mathcal{C}^{t}\phi^{\mathsf{T}}\rangle.

(14)

We use the superscript
\tau
on
u^{\tau}_{t}
to distinguish that it comes from the inexact iteration (
12
) involving the projected function
\hat{u}^{\tau}_{t}
(and thus a layer of approximation) rather than the exact iteration (
3
).
Fixed points are indicated without subscripts:
u^{\tau}=\lim_{t\to\infty}u^{\tau}_{t}
and
\hat{u}^{\tau}=\lim_{t\to\infty}\hat{u}^{\tau}_{t}
.

We now identify the approximation associated with DGA by comparing the inexact iteration (
12
) with the
exact
iteration

\displaystyle u_{t}

\displaystyle=u_{t-\tau}+(\mathcal{A}^{\tau}u_{t-\tau}+b^{\tau}),

(15a)

\displaystyle\hat{u}_{t}

\displaystyle=\hat{u}_{t-\tau}+\mathcal{P}(u_{t}-\hat{u}_{t-\tau}).

(15b)

Let us define the complementary projection

\mathcal{Q}=\mathcal{I}-\mathcal{P}.

(16)

We note that
\mathcal{Q}(\hat{u}_{t}-\hat{u}_{t^{\prime}})=0
for any
t
and
t^{\prime}
since
\mathcal{Q}\phi=0
and
\hat{u}_{t}-\hat{u}_{t^{\prime}}=\phi^{\mathsf{T}}v
for some
v
(we work with differences because we do not assume
\mathcal{Q}\hat{u}_{0}=0
).
By substituting
\mathcal{P}=\mathcal{I}-\mathcal{Q}
into (
15b
) and replacing
t
with
t-\tau
, we find that
u_{t-\tau}=\hat{u}_{t-\tau}+\mathcal{Q}(u_{t-\tau}-\hat{u}_{t-2\tau})
; in turn, by substituting this expression into (
15a
), we can express (
15
) as

\displaystyle u_{t}

\displaystyle=\hat{u}_{t-\tau}+(\mathcal{A}^{\tau}\hat{u}_{t-\tau}+b^{\tau})+\mathcal{C}^{\tau}\mathcal{Q}(u_{t-\tau}-\hat{u}_{t-2\tau}),

(17a)

\displaystyle\hat{u}_{t}

\displaystyle=\hat{u}_{t-\tau}+\mathcal{P}(u_{t}-\hat{u}_{t-\tau}).

(17b)

The fixed point
\hat{u}=\lim_{t\to\infty}\hat{u}_{t}
satisfies
\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{A}^{\tau}\hat{u}+b^{\tau})+\mathcal{P}\mathcal{C}^{\tau}\mathcal{Q}(u-\hat{u})=0
.
Comparing (
12a
) and (
15a
) shows that they both use the previous estimate (
\hat{u}^{\tau}_{t-\tau}
or
u^{\tau}_{t-\tau}
, respectively) to construct an improved estimate
u^{\tau}_{t}
.
Because
\hat{u}^{\tau}_{t-\tau}
can be represented using the basis, we only need length
\tau
trajectories to approximate
u^{\tau}_{t}
.
Hence, DGA makes the approximation
\mathcal{P}\mathcal{C}^{\tau}\mathcal{Q}(u-\hat{u})\approx 0
, which avoids the need for trajectories longer than
\tau
.
In contrast, the exact iteration requires infinite-length trajectories since the last term of (
17a
) prevents
\hat{u}_{t}
from depending on
\hat{u}_{t-\tau}
alone.

To understand the need for memory, we observe that (
15a
) specifies Markovian dynamics for
u_{t}
: the value of
u_{t}
at current iteration
t
is fully determined by the value of
u_{t-\tau}
at the last iteration
t-\tau
. DGA amounts to making the approximation that the dynamics of the
projected
function
\hat{u}_{t}
observed at discrete time intervals
\tau
is Markovian.
This perspective suggests that we can derive an improved estimator by mitigating the Markov approximation.
We do this by using projected statistics at multiple times (i.e., using memory), rather than a single time,
as we now describe.

II.4
Dynamical Galerkin approximation with memory

Our starting point is (
17a
) with
\tau
replaced by
\sigma
, where
\tau/\sigma
is a positive integer:

u_{t}=\hat{u}_{t-\sigma}+(\mathcal{A}^{\sigma}\hat{u}_{t-\sigma}+b^{\sigma})+\mathcal{C}^{\sigma}\mathcal{Q}(\underline{u_{t-\sigma}}-\hat{u}_{t-2\sigma}).

(18)

The underlined term is not representable by the basis and prevents solving for
\hat{u}
by fixed point iteration with length
\tau
trajectories.
By repeatedly substituting (
18
), we obtain

\displaystyle u_{t}

\displaystyle=\hat{u}_{t-\sigma}

\displaystyle+\sum_{n=1}^{2}(\mathcal{C}^{\sigma}\mathcal{Q})^{n-1}(\mathcal{A}^{\sigma}\hat{u}_{t-n\sigma}+b^{\sigma})

\displaystyle+(\mathcal{C}^{\sigma}\mathcal{Q})^{2}(\underline{u_{t-2\sigma}}-\hat{u}_{t-3\sigma})

\displaystyle=\hat{u}_{t-\sigma}

\displaystyle+\sum_{n=1}^{\tau/\sigma}(\mathcal{C}^{\sigma}\mathcal{Q})^{n-1}(\mathcal{A}^{\sigma}\hat{u}_{t-n\sigma}+b^{\sigma})

\displaystyle+(\mathcal{C}^{\sigma}\mathcal{Q})^{\tau/\sigma}(\underline{u_{t-\tau}}-\hat{u}_{t-\tau-\sigma}),

(19)

where the underline indicates the location of the next substitution.
We now have an alternative iteration to (

\displaystyle u_{t}

\displaystyle=\hat{u}_{t-\sigma}

\displaystyle+\sum_{n=1}^{\tau/\sigma}(\mathcal{C}^{\sigma}\mathcal{Q})^{n-1}(\mathcal{A}^{\sigma}\hat{u}_{t-n\sigma}+b^{\sigma})

\displaystyle+(\mathcal{C}^{\sigma}\mathcal{Q})^{\tau/\sigma}(u_{t-\tau}-\hat{u}_{t-\tau-\sigma}),

(20a)

\displaystyle\hat{u}_{t}

\displaystyle=\hat{u}_{t-\sigma}

\displaystyle+\mathcal{P}(u_{t}-\hat{u}_{t-\sigma}).

(20b)

Equation (

It describes

at iteration

at previous iterations

The approximation used in DGA is the first term of (

\tau

The remaining terms correct for the Markov approximation by introducing memory.

Omitting the last term of (

\displaystyle u^{\sigma,\tau}_{t}

\displaystyle=\hat{u}^{\sigma,\tau}_{t-\sigma}+\sum_{n=1}^{\tau/\sigma}(\mathcal{C}^{\sigma}\mathcal{Q})^{n-1}(\mathcal{A}^{\sigma}\hat{u}^{\sigma,\tau}_{t-n\sigma}+b^{\sigma}),

(21a)

\displaystyle\hat{u}^{\sigma,\tau}_{t}

\displaystyle=\hat{u}^{\sigma,\tau}_{t-\sigma}+\mathcal{P}(u^{\sigma,\tau}_{t}-\hat{u}^{\sigma,\tau}_{t-\sigma}),

(21b)

with a fixed point that solves

\sum_{n=1}^{\tau/\sigma}\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{C}^{\sigma}\mathcal{Q})^{n-1}(\mathcal{A}^{\sigma}\hat{u}^{\sigma,\tau}+b^{\sigma})=0.

(22)

Comparing (

\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{C}^{\sigma}\mathcal{Q})^{\tau/\sigma}(u-\hat{u})\approx 0

When the basis captures much of the slower dynamics of the system, we expect the DGA with memory approximation
\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{C}^{\sigma}\mathcal{Q})^{\tau/\sigma}(u-\hat{u})\approx 0

as

\displaystyle\langle f,w^{\sigma,\tau}\rangle=\E\biggl[

\displaystyle f(X_{\tau})\hat{w}^{\sigma,\tau}(X_{0})

(33)

\displaystyle-\sum_{n=1}^{\tau/\sigma}f(X_{\tau-n\sigma})\delta w^{\sigma,\tau}_{n}(X_{0})\biggm|X_{0}\sim\mu\biggr].

III.2
Mean first passage time

The mean first passage time (MFPT)

m(x)=\E[T_{B}\mid X_{0}=x]

(34)

is the expected time until a trajectory starting at
x
first enters
B
. As noted above, it satisfies an equation of the form (
4
):

(\mathcal{S}_{B}^{t}-\mathcal{I})m=-\int_{0}^{t}\mathcal{S}_{B}^{t^{\prime}}\mathbf{1}_{B^{\mathsf{c}}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{t^{\prime}},

(35)

subject to the boundary condition
m(x)=0
for
x\in B
.
Physically, this equation says that each application of
\mathcal{S}_{B}^{t}
reduces the mean remaining time to exit by
\int_{0}^{t}\mathcal{S}_{B}^{t^{\prime}}\mathbf{1}_{B^{\mathsf{c}}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{t^{\prime}}=\E[t\wedge T_{B}\mid X_{0}=x]
.
The corresponding matrices are

\displaystyle K^{t}

\displaystyle=\langle\phi,\mathcal{S}_{B}^{t}\phi^{\mathsf{T}}\rangle

\displaystyle=\E[\phi(X_{0})\phi^{\mathsf{T}}(X_{t\wedge T_{B}})\mid X_{0}\sim\mu],

(36a)

\displaystyle G^{t}

\displaystyle=\langle\phi,(\mathcal{S}_{B}^{t}-\mathcal{I})\phi^{\mathsf{T}}\rangle

\displaystyle=\E[\phi(X_{0})(\phi^{\mathsf{T}}(X_{t\wedge T_{B}})-\phi^{\mathsf{T}}(X_{0}))\mid X_{0}\sim\mu],

(36b)

\displaystyle h^{t}

\displaystyle=\biggl\langle\phi,(\mathcal{S}_{B}^{t}-\mathcal{I})\hat{m}_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}\mathcal{S}_{B}^{t^{\prime}}\mathbf{1}_{B^{\mathsf{c}}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{t^{\prime}}\biggr\rangle

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} {}=\E[\phi(X_{0})(&\hat{m}_{0}(X_{t\wedge T_{B}})-\hat{m}_{0}(X_{0})\\
&+(t\wedge T_{B}))\mid X_{0}\sim\mu],\end{aligned}

(36c)

and
m
can be approximated from its projection using

\displaystyle m^{\sigma,\tau}(x)=\E\biggl[

\displaystyle\hat{m}^{\sigma,\tau}(X_{\tau\wedge T_{B}})+(\tau\wedge T_{B})

(37)

\displaystyle-\sum_{n=1}^{\tau/\sigma}\delta m^{\sigma,\tau}_{n}(X_{(\tau-n\sigma)\wedge T_{B}})\biggm|X_{0}=x\biggr].

III.3
Forward committor

The forward committor

q(x)=\E[\mathbf{1}_{B}(X_{T_{A\cup B}})\mid X_{0}=x]

(38)

is the probability that a trajectory starting at
x
will enter
B
before
A
.
The forward committor satisfies

(\mathcal{S}_{A\cup B}^{t}-\mathcal{I})q=0

(39)

with boundary condition
q(x)=\mathbf{1}_{B}(x)
for
x\in A\cup B
.
The corresponding matrices are

\displaystyle K^{t}

\displaystyle=\langle\phi,\mathcal{S}_{A\cup B}^{t}\phi^{\mathsf{T}}\rangle

\displaystyle=\E[\phi(X_{0})\phi(X_{t\wedge T_{A\cup B}})\mid X_{0}\sim\mu],

(40a)

\displaystyle G^{t}

\displaystyle=\langle\phi,(\mathcal{S}_{A\cup B}^{t}-\mathcal{I})\phi^{\mathsf{T}}\rangle

\displaystyle=\E[\phi(X_{0})(\phi(X_{t\wedge T_{A\cup B}})-\phi(X_{0}))\mid X_{0}\sim\mu],

(40b)

\displaystyle h^{t}

\displaystyle=\langle\phi,(\mathcal{S}_{A\cup B}^{t}-\mathcal{I})\hat{q}_{0}\rangle

\displaystyle=\E[\phi(X_{0})(\hat{q}_{0}(X_{t\wedge T_{A\cup B}})-\hat{q}_{0}(X_{0}))\mid X_{0}\sim\mu],

(40c)

and
q
can be approximated as

\displaystyle q^{\sigma,\tau}(x)=\E\biggl[

\displaystyle\hat{q}^{\sigma,\tau}(X_{\tau\wedge T_{A\cup B}})

(41)

\displaystyle-\sum_{n=1}^{\tau/\sigma}\delta q^{\sigma,\tau}_{n}(X_{(\tau-n\sigma)\wedge T_{A\cup B}})\biggm|X_{0}=x\biggr].

III.4
Backward committor

The backward committor is the probability that a trajectory ending at
x
exited
A
after
B
,

\tilde{q}(x)=\E[\mathbf{1}_{A}(X_{-\tilde{T}_{A\cup B}})\mid X_{0}=x],

(42)

where
\tilde{T}_{A\cup B}=\min\{t\geq 0\mid X_{-t}\in A\cup B\}
.
The backward committor satisfies

(\mathcal{S}_{A\cup B}^{-t}-\mathcal{I})\tilde{q}=0

(43)

with boundary condition
\tilde{q}(x)=\mathbf{1}_{A}(x)
for
x\in A\cup B
.

\mathcal{S}_{A\cup B}^{-t}f(x)=\E[f(X_{-(t\wedge\tilde{T}_{A\cup B})})\mid X_{0}=x]

(44)

is the stopped transition operator for the time-reversed process.
We take conditional expectations backward-in-time with respect to the
time-reversed process
: given an infinite, statistically stationary, trajectory
X
, we look backward in time from each time
t
where
X_{t}=x
.
Mathematically,

\displaystyle\E[f(X_{-(t\wedge\tilde{T}_{A\cup B})})\mid X_{0}=x]

\displaystyle\quad{}=\int\E[f(X_{-(t\wedge\tilde{T}_{A\cup B})})\mid X_{0}=x,X_{-\tau}=x^{\prime}]\pi(x^{\prime})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{x^{\prime}},

(45)

which follows from the definition of conditional probability and
\pi(x)=\Pr[X_{-\tau}=x]
. Because
\pi(x)=w(x)\mu(x)=\Pr[X_{0}=x]=\Pr[X_{-\tau}=x]
,

\displaystyle\langle g,\mathcal{S}_{A\cup B}^{-t}f\rangle

\displaystyle\quad{}=\int\E[g(X_{0})f(X_{-(t\wedge\tilde{T}_{A\cup B})})\mid X_{0}=x]\mu(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{x}

\displaystyle\quad{}=\E\biggl[g(X_{0})f(X_{-(t\wedge\tilde{T}_{A\cup B})})\frac{w(X_{-\tau})}{w(X_{0})}\biggm|X_{-\tau}\sim\mu\biggr],

(46)

as we show explicitly in Ref.
Strahan et al. 2021
(with the zero of time shifted).
The corresponding matrices are

\displaystyle K^{t}

\displaystyle=\langle\phi,\mathcal{S}_{A\cup B}^{-t}\phi^{\mathsf{T}}\rangle

\displaystyle=\E\biggl[\phi(X_{0})\phi(X_{-(t\wedge\tilde{T}_{A\cup B})})\frac{w(X_{-\tau})}{w(X_{0})}\biggm|X_{-\tau}\sim\mu\biggr],

(47a)

\displaystyle G^{t}

\displaystyle=\langle\phi,(\mathcal{S}_{A\cup B}^{-t}-\mathcal{I})\phi^{\mathsf{T}}\rangle

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} {}=\E\biggl[&\phi(X_{0})(\phi(X_{-(t\wedge\tilde{T}_{A\cup B})})-\phi(X_{0}))\\
&\times\frac{w(X_{-\tau})}{w(X_{0})}\biggm|X_{-\tau}\sim\mu\biggr],\end{aligned}

(47b)

\displaystyle h^{t}

\displaystyle=\langle\phi,(\mathcal{S}_{A\cup B}^{-t}-\mathcal{I})\hat{\tilde{q}}_{0}\rangle

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} {}=\E\biggl[&\phi(X_{0})(\hat{\tilde{q}}_{0}(X_{-(t\wedge\tilde{T}_{A\cup B})})-\hat{\tilde{q}}_{0}(X_{0}))\\
&\times\frac{w(X_{-\tau})}{w(X_{0})}\biggm|X_{-\tau}\sim\mu\biggr].\end{aligned}

(47c)

We estimate
\tilde{q}
as

\displaystyle\tilde{q}^{\sigma,\tau}(x)=\E\biggl[

\displaystyle\hat{\tilde{q}}^{\sigma,\tau}(X_{-(\tau\wedge\tilde{T}_{A\cup B})})

(48)

\displaystyle-\sum_{n=1}^{\tau/\sigma}\delta\tilde{q}^{\sigma,\tau}_{n}(X_{-((\tau-n\sigma)\wedge\tilde{T}_{A\cup B})})\biggm|X_{0}=x\biggr].

In this section, we illustrate DGA with memory on a simple model system.
The system is a drift-diffusion process obeying the Fokker–Planck equation

\partial_{t}p_{t}(x)=\nabla\cdot(p_{t}(x)\nabla V(x))+\beta^{-1}\nabla^{2}p_{t}(x)

(49)

on the two-dimensional potential

\displaystyle V(x)={}

\displaystyle 3e^{-\chi_{1}^{2}-(\chi_{2}-1/3)^{2}}-3e^{-\chi_{1}^{2}-(\chi_{2}-5/3)^{2}}

(50)

\displaystyle-5e^{-(\chi_{1}-1)^{2}-\chi_{2}^{2}}-5e^{-(\chi_{1}+1)^{2}-\chi_{2}^{2}}

\displaystyle+0.2\chi_{1}^{4}+0.2(\chi_{2}-1/3)^{4},

\beta=2
.
We define the reactant state
A
and product state
B
as circles with radii
0.25
centered at
(\pm 1.05,-0.05)

We discretize the system on a
80\times 80
grid of equally-spaced points
(with spacing

) in the range
[-2,2]\times[-1.5,2.5]
.
The dynamics of this discretized system follow the master equation
Thiede et al. 2019
;
Strahan et al. 2023a

\partial_{t}p_{t}(x)=\sum_{x^{\prime}}p_{t}(x^{\prime})\mathcal{L}(x^{\prime},x),

(51)

where the elements of the matrix
\mathcal{L}
are

\mathcal{L}(x,x^{\prime})=\frac{2\beta^{-1}}{h^{2}}\frac{1}{1+e^{-\beta(V(x)-V(x^{\prime}))}}

for

\mathcal{L}(x,x)=-\sum_{x^{\prime}\neq x}\mathcal{L}(x,x^{\prime})
, and

otherwise.
We use

to calculate finite-time operators such as

and
\mathcal{S}_{B}^{t}

For example, we compute (

\langle\phi,(\mathcal{T}^{t})^{\dagger}\phi^{\mathsf{T}}\rangle=\sum_{x,x^{\prime}}\mu(x)\phi(x)(\mathcal{T}^{t})^{\dagger}(x,x^{\prime})\phi^{\mathsf{T}}(x^{\prime}),

.

31

35
), (

), and (

(for

(for

V
Conclusions

In this paper we incorporate memory to improve the accuracy of dynamical statistics estimated by dynamical Galerkin approximation. Our work builds on numerical approaches for solving the orthogonal dynamics equation of the Mori–Zwanzig formalism
Darve, Solomon, and Kia 2009
and quasi-MSMs
Cao et al. 2020
;
Cao et al. 2023
;
Dominic III et al. 2023
and shows that the basic concept behind these approaches can be generalized and used to compute much more than relaxation time scales. A key feature of quasi-MSMs and the present approach is that the memory corrections are obtained by directly inverting a GME, so that it is not necessary to assume a particular functional form for the memory. Quasi-MSMs and DGA with memory also contrast with traditional applications of the Mori–Zwanzig formalism in that the models are high-dimensional. As a result, the memory decays rapidly, and we find that even a few correction terms are sufficient to achieve high accuracy (with most of the correction coming from the first term beyond the Markov model). In our numerical experiments, the memory correction decreases the data requirement by an order of magnitude.

While DGA with memory can decrease the sensitivity of estimates to the choice of basis, a choice must still be made. To address this issue, we recently introduced an inexact subspace iteration to learn a basis represented by neural networks
Strahan et al. 2023a
. In Ref.
Strahan et al. 2023a
, we apply the memory correction after we learn the basis, but one could apply it after each subspace iteration to accelerate convergence.
Even when the representation of a dynamical statistic does not have the form in (
8
) (e.g., Ref.
Strahan et al. 2023b
, or the Richardson iterate in Ref.
Strahan et al. 2023a
), one could define a nonlinear projection operator and in turn compute a memory correction to compensate for deficiencies of the input features. A separate strategy that can be applied to improve the numerical performance further is to integrate over lag times
Lorpaiboon et al. 2020
;
Cao et al. 2023
.
It would be interesting to compare the Mori–Zwanzig approach to memory taken here to alternative means of treating memory, such as delay embedding
Liebert and Schuster 1989
;
Das and Giannakis 2019
;
Kamb et al. 2020
;
Thiede et al. 2019
;
Strahan et al. 2021
, with regard to data requirements and robustness to hyperparameter choices for high-dimensional models like those considered here.

Computing finite-time operators using the infinitesimal generator

In this section, we express the finite-time operators (e.g.,
\mathcal{T}^{t}
and
\mathcal{S}_{B}^{t}
) in terms of the infinitesimal generator

\mathcal{L}=\lim_{\epsilon\to 0^{+}}\frac{\mathcal{T}^{\epsilon}-\mathcal{I}}{\epsilon}.

(55)

For a continuous-time Markov jump process on a finite discrete state space,
\mathcal{L}
is a matrix (e.g., (
52
)) and functions on the grid are vectors, so

\mathcal{L}f(x)=\sum_{x^{\prime}}\mathcal{L}(x,x^{\prime})f(x^{\prime}),

(56)

and we can evaluate expressions in terms of
\mathcal{L}
directly.

B.1
Transition operator

The transition operator can be expressed as

\mathcal{T}^{t}=(\mathcal{T}^{\epsilon})^{t/\epsilon}=\lim_{\epsilon\to 0^{+}}(\mathcal{I}+\epsilon\mathcal{L})^{t/\epsilon}=e^{t\mathcal{L}}.

(57)

Similarly,

(\mathcal{T}^{t})^{\dagger}(x,x^{\prime})=e^{t\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}}(x,x^{\prime}),

(58)

and

\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}(x,x^{\prime})=\mu(x^{\prime})\mathcal{L}(x^{\prime},x)/\mu(x)

(59)

from the definition of the adjoint.

B.2

Here, we express the stopped transition operator in terms of the infinitesimal generator. To this end, we observe that

(60)

(the product above is 1 only if
