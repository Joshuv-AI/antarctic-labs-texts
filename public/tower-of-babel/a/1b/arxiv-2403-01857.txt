Reward Model Learning vs. Direct Policy Optimization: A Comparative Analysis of Learning from Human Preferences
Andi Nika, Debmalya Mandal, Parameswaran Kamalaruban, Georgios Tzannetos, Goran Radanović, Adish Singla

Abstract
In this paper, we take a step towards a deeper understanding of learning from human preferences by systematically comparing the paradigm of reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) with the recently proposed paradigm of direct preference optimization (DPO). We focus our attention on the class of loglinear policy parametrization and linear reward functions. In order to compare the two paradigms, we first derive minimax statistical bounds on the suboptimality gap induced by both RLHF and DPO, assuming access to an oracle that exactly solves the optimization problems. We provide a detailed discussion on the relative comparison between the two paradigms, simultaneously taking into account the sample size, policy and reward class dimensions, and the regularization temperature. Moreover, we extend our analysis to the approximate optimization setting and derive exponentially decaying convergence rates for both RLHF and DPO. Next, we analyze the setting where the ground-truth reward is not realizable and find that, while RLHF incurs a constant additional error, DPO retains its asymptotically decaying gap by just tuning the temperature accordingly. Finally, we extend our comparison to the Markov decision process setting, where we generalize our results with exact optimization. To the best of our knowledge, we are the first to provide such a comparative analysis for RLHF and DPO.

One of the most effective ways to learn from preferences is through reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF), which involves a two-step process of reward learning and regularized policy optimization. The attractiveness of this paradigm lies in its ability to model the reward function based solely on preference data. As an alternative to RLHF,
Rafailov et al. (2023)
have recently proposed direct preference optimization (DPO), an RL-free paradigm to learning from preferences. DPO circumvents the reward modeling phase and directly optimizes the policy parameters based on the preference data. In certain LLM instances, DPO seems to be empirically superior to RLHF, due to its simple optimization framework.

That said, a statistical analysis of the differences between these paradigms is lacking. The sample complexity of RLHF in various settings has already been studied
(
Zhu et al., 2023
;
Zhan et al., 2023
;
Xiong et al., 2023
)
, and there have been some initial attempts at theoretically understanding DPO and its variants
(
Azar et al., 2023
)
. However, it is unclear when one of the paradigms is better and when these two paradigms are statistically comparable. Motivated by this observation, we initiate a thorough discussion of the theoretical comparison between RLHF and DPO. Specifically, the purpose of this paper is to address the following research questions:

What are the statistical guarantees of RLHF relative to those of DPO? What conditions benefit one as opposed to the other?

As DPO does not learn a reward model, but directly optimizes over the policy space, a dependence on the policy dimensionality
d_{P}
is expected. On the other hand, RLHF’s performance evidently implies some dependence on reward dimensionality
d_{R}
due to its reward learning phase. Does this imply a discrepancy in the statistical bounds of these paradigms when the reward and policy dimensions are different? Moreover, what can be said about the dependency of the bounds on the sample size
n
or the regularization temperature
\beta
?

We address these questions in the following setting: finite spaces, Bradley-Terry preference model, linear rewards and loglinear policies. We first study the exact optimization setting and derive bounds on both RLHF and DPO. Then, we proceed to derive fast convergence rates for a modified version of the policy gradient for RLHF, and gradient descent for DPO. Next, we discuss some implications of our bounds when the reward function is not fully realizable. We close our paper by extending our comparative analysis to deterministic Markov decision processes. Our contributions are summarized below; see Table
1
for explicit bounds.

•

First, we derive minimax bounds on the suboptimality gap induced by RLHF and DPO in the exact optimization setting by leveraging smoothness and strong convexity properties. We show that when the optimal regularized policy is loglinear and the reward function is linear, RLHF is
\widetilde{\Theta}(\sqrt{d_{R}/n})
-close to its objective, while DPO is
\widetilde{\Theta}(d_{P}/(\beta n))
-close. These results emphasize the comparison of the two paradigms in terms of the reward and policy dimensions when setting
\beta={\Theta}(\sqrt{d_{P}/n})
for DPO.

•

Furthermore, we study the convergence rates of a version of the natural policy gradient for RLHF and gradient descent for DPO. Motivated by recent fast convergence results for entropy regularized RL with tabular softmax policies, we derive
O\left(e^{-t}/\beta\right)
convergence rates in
t
iterations for a version of the natural policy gradient for RLHF. Moreover, for gradient descent we are able to show
O\left((1/\beta)(1-\beta/n)^{t}\right)
convergence rates by using the fact that the DPO loss function satisfies the
PL condition

(
Karimi et al., 2016
)
. These results replicate the implications from the exact optimization setting on the difference in terms of reward and policy dimensions.

•

We also consider the case where the ground-truth reward function is not realizable and its best linear fit is
\epsilon_{\textnormal{app}}
-close to it. We show that, while RLHF incurs an additional constant term on the suboptimality gap, DPO’s dependence on the additional term can be controlled by setting the regularization temperature accordingly.

•

Finally, we extend our comparison to deterministic Markov decision processes by proposing a new formulation of the DPO objective for this setting and then generalizing our results. The main motivation for this extension is that, arguably, the difference in reward and policy dimensions in this setting is higher.

Related Work

Learning from pairwise comparisons.

In the context of RL, the problem of learning from pairwise comparisons has been studied thoroughly in the bandit setting, where the problem is known as the
dueling bandit
problem
(
Yue et al., 2009
;
Faury et al., 2020
;
Ailon et al., 2014
;
Gajane et al., 2015
;
Komiyama et al., 2015
;
Zoghi et al., 2014
;
Saha & Gopalan, 2019
;
Saha & Krishnamurthy, 2022
)
. For the case of dueling RL for linear MDPs,
Saha et al. (2023)
propose an algorithm that satisfies tight regret guarantees, while
Chen et al. (2022)
extend this formulation to the MDPs with general function approximation. Finally,
Chatterji et al. (2021)
consider a more general setting in which the trajectory-based feedback is generated from a generalized linear model, and they propose variants of optimistic algorithms for online RL.

In this paper, we consider the offline setting, where
Zhu et al. (2023)
and
Zhan et al. (2023)
have already provided statistical bounds on pessimistic RLHF for direct value maximization. Our focus, however, is on the regularized value maximization problem. While pessimism mitigates poor coverage for the setting considered by
Zhu et al. (2023)
and
Zhan et al. (2023)
, for regularized RLHF and, as a consequence, for DPO, this issue remains and is captured by the coverage coefficients which we define with respect to both reward and policy features. The aim of this paper, however, is not to mitigate these issues, but to thoroughly analyse the statistical guarantees of the existing prominent paradigms of learning from human preferences and derive insightful results that shed light on their relative performances.

Direct preference optimization.

In recent works, the RL-free fine-tuning paradigm of direct preference optimization (DPO) has gained popularity
(
Rafailov et al., 2023
;
An et al., 2023
;
Azar et al., 2023
;
Wang et al., 2023
)
. Its original formulation was proposed for the contextual bandit setting.
Hejna et al. (2023)
propose an extension of DPO to MDPs under the assumption that the preferences depend on the advantage function of the optimal policy. While we also provide an extension of the DPO formulation for MDPs in Section
7
, our primary focus is on a comparative analysis between the two paradigms in the contextual bandit setting.

Offline RL.

In recent years, there has been a significant surge in interest towards offline RL, with an extensive literature both in the empirical front
(
Jaques et al., 2019
;
Laroche et al., 2019
;
Fujimoto et al., 2019
;
Kumar et al., 2020
;
Agarwal et al., 2020
;
Kidambi et al., 2020
)
and the theoretical one
(
Jin et al., 2021
;
Xie et al., 2021
;
Rashidinejad et al., 2021
;
Uehara & Sun, 2021
;
Zanette et al., 2021
)
. While the focus of this line of work is on the traditional reward-based offline RL, our problem is derived from a combination of reward-learning from pairwise feedback and KL-regularized offline RL based on it.

Preliminaries

Let

Definition 3.1

(
Linear reward function class
)
.

Let

be a

-dimensional feature mapping with

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}=\Big\{r_{\omega}\in[0,1]^{XY}:r_{\omega}(x,y)=\omega^{\top}\phi(x,y),

\displaystyle\forall(x,y)\in\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}\;\text{where}\;\omega\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{R}}\;\text{and}\;\left\lVert\omega\right\rVert_{2}\leq F\Big\}~.

Given

, a policy

is a distribution over actions. Throughout the paper, we will consider the loglinear class of policies, defined as follows.

Definition 3.2

(
Loglinear policy class
)
.

Let
\psi
be a
d_{P}
-dimensional feature mapping with
\max_{x,y}\left\lVert\psi(x,y)\right\rVert_{2}\leq 1

. We consider the following class of loglinear policies:

\displaystyle\Pi

\displaystyle=\Big\{\pi_{\theta}:\pi_{\theta}(y|x)=\frac{\exp(\theta^{\top}\psi(x,y))}{\sum_{y^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Y}}\exp(\theta^{\top}\psi(x,y^{\prime}))},

\displaystyle\forall(x,y)\in\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}\;\text{where}\;\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{P}}\;\text{and}\;\left\lVert\theta\right\rVert_{2}\leq B\Big\}~.

Given policy
\pi
, the value function of
\pi
with respect to reward function

and context distribution

3.2
Offline Learning from Human Preferences

Let
\mu
be a reference policy fixed throughout the paper, and
\beta>0
be a regularization parameter. Let us define the KL-regularized objective as

\mathcal{V}^{\pi}_{r}(\rho)=V^{\pi}_{r}(\rho)-\beta D_{\textnormal{KL}}\left(\pi||\mu\right),

where
D_{\textnormal{KL}}\left(\pi||\mu\right)=\sum_{x}\rho(x)\sum_{y}\pi(y|x)\log\frac{\pi(y|x)}{\mu(y|x)}
.

We assume access to the dataset
\mathcal{D}_{n}=\{(x_{i},y^{w}_{i},y^{l}_{i})\}^{n}_{i=1}
, where
y^{w}_{i}
denotes the preferred action over
y^{l}_{i}
. In this paper, we will assume that the distribution of human preferences follows the Bradley-Terry (BT) model
(
Bradley & Terry, 1952
)
, which we formally state below.

Definition 3.3

(
Bradley-Terry preference model
)
.

There exists a latent reward function
r^{*}
and a probability law

such that, for every tuple
(x,y^{w},y^{l})
, we have

\displaystyle P^{*}(y^{w}\succ y^{l}|x)=\frac{\exp\left(r^{*}(x,y^{w})\right)}{\exp\left(r^{*}(x,y^{w})\right)+\exp\left(r^{*}(x,y^{l})\right)}~,

where
y^{w}\succ y^{l}
denotes
y^{w}
being preferred over
y^{l}
.

The latent reward function
r^{*}
will be fixed throughout the paper as the ground-truth reward function.

3.3
Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback

We consider the reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) paradigm as formulated in

Ziegler et al., 2019
)

and a fixed reference policy

For the reward learning phase, RLHF estimates

by applying maximum likelihood estimation (MLE) to the dataset

\displaystyle\min_{r}\mathcal{L}^{r}_{\textnormal{RLHF}}

\displaystyle({\mathcal{D}_{n}}):=-\mathbb{E}_{(x,y^{w},y^{l})\sim\mathcal{D}_{n}}

\displaystyle\quad\Big[\log\Big(\sigma\big(r(x^{w},y^{w})-r(x^{l},y^{l})\big)\Big)\Big]~,

(P1.1)

where
\sigma(z)=1/(1+\exp(-z))
denotes the sigmoid function.
Let
\widehat{r}
denote the solution of Problem (

The final phase of RLHF consists of maximizing the KL-regularized objective with respect to
\widehat{r}
by solving

\displaystyle\max_{\pi}\mathcal{V}^{\pi}_{\widehat{r}}(\mathcal{D}_{n}):=\mathbb{E}_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\sim\mathcal{D}_{n}\\
y\sim\pi(\cdot|x)\end{subarray}}\left[\widehat{r}(x,y)-\beta\log\frac{\pi(y|x)}{\mu(y|x)}\right]~.

(P1.2)

3.4
Direct Preference Optimization

Recently, alternative paradigms to RLHF have been studied. In particular,
Rafailov et al. (2023)
introduced direct preference optimization (DPO), a new fine-tuning paradigm that directly optimizes the policy parameters instead of going through the reward modeling phase. Their key observation is that the latent reward can be expressed in terms of its optimal policy and the reference policy. This yields a loss function that is directly defined in terms of the preference data.

Formally,
Rafailov et al. (2023)
show that there exists a policy
\pi
that maximizes the KL-regularized objective, for which we have

\displaystyle r^{*}(x,y)=\beta\log\frac{\pi(y|x)}{\mu(y|x)}+\beta\log Z(x)

(1)

for every
(x,y)
, where
Z(x)
denotes the partition function
\sum_{y}\mu(y|x)\exp(r^{*}(x,y)/\beta)
. A new objective is then derived, which directly depends on the policy. Given preference dataset
\mathcal{D}_{n}
, this objective leads to the following optimization problem:

\displaystyle\min_{\pi}\mathcal{L}^{\pi}_{\textnormal{DPO}}({\mathcal{D}_{n}}):=-\mathbb{E}_{(x,y^{w},y^{l})\sim\mathcal{D}_{n}}

\displaystyle\left[\log\left(\sigma\Big(\beta\log\frac{\pi(y^{w}|x)}{\mu(y^{w}|x))}-\beta\log\frac{\pi(y^{l}|x)}{\mu(y^{l}|x))}\Big)\right)\right]~.

(P2)

As it turns out, this elegant approach yields practical benefits. However, it is unclear whether these benefits can be theoretically justified. In this paper, we will provide a comparative analysis of both RLHF and DPO in different settings. Next, we define a unified metric of performance to compare these paradigms.

3.5
Performance Metric

Given a reward function
r
, let
\pi^{\textnormal{opt}}_{r}\in\arg\max_{\pi}V^{\pi}_{r}(\rho)
denote an optimal policy for
V^{\pi}_{r}(\rho)
and
V^{\textnormal{opt}}_{r}(\rho)
the optimal value. For a given policy
\pi
, we define the suboptimality gap of
\pi
as

\displaystyle G(\pi)=V^{\textnormal{opt}}_{r^{*}}(\rho)-V^{\pi}_{r^{*}}(\rho)~.

G(\pi)
captures how well a policy is performing w.r.t. the ground-truth reward function
r^{*}
. In this paper, we will use the suboptimality gap
G
as our unified measure of performance when comparing the two paradigms.

We note that RLHF and DPO are designed to minimize regularlized objectives
\mathcal{V}
instead of optimizing the value function
V
(see Sections
3.3
and
3.4
). In order to rigorously analyze the differences between RLHF and DPO, we need to establish some additional notation. Let
\pi^{*}_{r}\in\arg\max_{\pi}\mathcal{V}^{\pi}_{r}(\rho)
denote a regularized optimal policy with respect to
r
and
\mathcal{V}^{*}_{r}(\rho)
denote the optimal regularized value. Analogous to
G
, we define the regularized suboptimality gap of
\pi
as

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}(\pi)=\mathcal{V}^{*}_{r^{*}}(\rho)-\mathcal{V}^{\pi}_{r^{*}}(\rho)=

\displaystyle(V^{\pi^{*}_{r^{*}}}_{r^{*}}(\rho)-V^{\pi}_{r^{*}}(\rho))-\beta(D_{KL}(\pi^{*}_{r^{*}}||\mu)-D_{KL}(\pi||\mu))~.

As RLHF and DPO are designed to minimize
\mathcal{G}(\pi)
, both will incur an additional term on their bounds when comparing their bounds w.r.t.
G(\pi)
. For this reason, we will formally define this discrepancy term as
D(\pi)=G(\pi)-\mathcal{G}(\pi)
and discuss it further in Section
4
. Next, we proceed to provide a comparative analysis for these paradigms, starting with the exact optimization setting when the ground-truth reward is realizable. We note that any
\log
terms are omitted for clarity of presentation. All proofs and related discussions can be found in the Appendix.

r^{*}(x,y)=\beta\log(\pi_{\theta^{*}}(y|x)/\mu(y|x))+\beta\log Z(x)

With these observations in place, we are now ready to state the minimax bounds on the suboptimality for DPO.

Theorem 4.2
.

Let
\delta>0
and
\beta>0
. Assume that
r^{*}\in\mathcal{F}
,
\mu\in\Pi
, and that the condition of Lemma
4.1
is satisfied. Let
n\geq O\left(tr(\Sigma_{{\mathcal{D}_{n},P}}^{\dagger})/(\beta B^{2})\right)
. Then, with probability at least
1-\delta
, the suboptimality gap of DPO is

\displaystyle G\left(\pi_{\widetilde{\theta}}\right)

\displaystyle=D\left(\pi_{\widetilde{\theta}}\right)+{\Theta}\left(\frac{\Lambda_{P}(d_{P}+1)}{\beta n}+\beta\lambda\Lambda_{P}B^{2}\right)~.

Proof sketch.
We start by splitting the suboptimality gap and focus on the
\mathcal{G}(\pi_{\widetilde{\theta}})
term. Here, we utilize the expression for the ground-truth reward as
r^{*}(x,y)=\beta\log\pi_{\theta^{*}}(y|x)-\beta\log\mu(y|x)+Z_{\widetilde{\theta}}(x)
, where
Z_{\widetilde{\theta}}(x)
denotes the partition function with respect to
\pi_{\widetilde{\theta}}
. Then, using the fact that the policies are loglinear we further expand and reduce the whole gap in terms of the log differences. Next, we utilize the smoothness and Lipschitzness of the log-sum-exp function to finally obtain the upper bounds. For the lower bound, we construct an example where the policy feature matrix
\Psi
is full rank, and show that the log-sum-exp function becomes strongly convex. This finally leads to the stated bounds.∎

Before discussing the implications of our results, let us say a few words on the regularization gap
D(\pi)
for RLHF and DPO. Let
\pi_{\theta^{*}}
denote an optimal loglinear regularized policy. A characterization of
D(\pi)
is given as follows.

Lemma 4.2
.

For any
\theta
, we have that
\beta D_{\textnormal{KL}}\left(\pi_{\theta^{*}}||\mu\right)-\beta D_{\textnormal{KL}}\left(\pi_{\theta}||\mu\right)\leq D(\pi_{\theta})
and
D(\pi_{\theta})\leq\beta D_{\textnormal{KL}}\left(\pi^{\textnormal{opt}}_{r^{*}}||\mu\right)-\beta D_{\textnormal{KL}}\left(\pi_{\theta}||\mu\right)
.

Furthermore, for DPO, this quantity can be upper bounded in terms of
\pi^{\textnormal{opt}}_{r^{*}}
and
\pi_{\theta^{*}}
as follows.

Lemma 4.3
.

Given
\delta>0
, with probability at least
1-\delta
, we have
D(\pi_{\widetilde{\theta}})\leq\beta D_{\textnormal{KL}}\left(\pi^{\textnormal{opt}}_{r^{*}}||\mu\right)-\beta D_{\textnormal{KL}}\left(\pi_{\theta^{*}}||\mu\right)+\widetilde{O}\left(d_{P}/n^{3/2}\right)
.

In general, it is known that the KL divergence may not be upper-bounded. However, assuming that optimal policy
\pi^{\textnormal{opt}}_{r^{*}}
, optimal regularized policy
\pi_{\theta^{*}}
, and sampling policy
\mu
are not far away from each other, then these
D_{\textnormal{KL}}(\cdot)
quantities would not be too large.

4.2
Comparative Analysis

In this section, we will provide some insights into the implications of our theoretical results. For the purpose of this section, we will focus our attention on the problem-dependent parameters and ignore the quantity
D(\pi)
.

The role of dimensionality.
Note that RLHF has
\widetilde{\Theta}(\sqrt{d_{R}})
dependence on the reward dimension, while DPO has
\Theta(d_{P})
dependence on the policy dimension. When
d_{R}=d_{P}
and the sample size is small, RLHF seems to statistically outperform DPO. Any setting where
d_{R}\ll d_{P}
makes this difference more apparent. In Section
7
, we will discuss an extension of our analysis to a setting where the reward dimension can be much smaller than the policy dimension in practice.

The role of sample size.
Next, we take into consideration the sample size. Note that DPO’s bounds depend on
n
being large enough (cf. Theorem
4.2
). Assume everything else constant and
d=d_{R}=d_{P}
. If the
D(\pi)
terms are similar for both paradigms, then, for large sample sizes such that
n\gg d
, DPO seems to outperform RLHF asymptotically. Whenever
n<d
(which is usually the case for large language models), RLHF has a smaller suboptimality gap.

The role of
\beta
.
Finally, we discuss the role of the temperature
\beta
on the bounds. First, note that RLHF can effectively set
\beta=0
to annihilate the effect that
D(\pi)
has on its bounds. On the other hand, DPO cannot set
\beta
to
0
due to a disproportional dependence of its bounds on it. Thus, the optimal choice of
\beta
for DPO is
\beta=\Theta(\sqrt{d_{P}/n})
, yielding
\Theta(\sqrt{d_{P}/n})
bounds and matching the order of
n
in the bounds of RLHF. For such a value of
\beta
, the same implications hold – the main difference between both settings is in terms of the differences between the reward and policy parameter dimensions.

5
Realizable Rewards: Approximate Optimization

In this section, we shift our focus to the approximate setting, where access to oracles is not given. Here, both paradigms have to approximately solve their estimation problems based on the given data. Similar to the previous section, we assume throughout this section that the ground-truth reward function
r^{*}
is linear and realizable in
\mathcal{F}
, and that there exists a loglinear regularized policy
\pi^{*}_{r^{*}}\in\Pi
. Moreover, we assume that, for every data tuple
(x,y^{w},y^{l})\in\mathcal{D}_{n}
, we have
\phi(x,y^{w})\neq\phi(x,y^{l})
and
\psi(x,y^{w})\neq\psi(x,y^{l})
.

5.1
Theoretical Results

Let us start with the reward learning phase. Recall the definition of the loss
\mathcal{L}^{\omega}_{\textnormal{RLHF}}(\mathcal{D}_{n})
for MLE, as defined in Section
4
. Let
\omega_{0}
be initialized randomly, and let

\displaystyle\omega_{t+1}=\underset{\omega:\left\lVert\omega\right\rVert_{2}\leq F}{\proj}\left(\omega_{t}-\eta\nabla_{\omega}\mathcal{L}^{\omega}_{\textnormal{RLHF}}(\mathcal{D}_{n})\right)~,

(4)

for any iterate
t\geq 0
, where
\eta
denotes the learning rate. Let
\omega^{*}_{\mathcal{D}_{n}}\in\arg\max\mathcal{L}^{\omega}_{\textnormal{RLHF}}(\mathcal{D}_{n})
.
The first result of this section provides fast convergence rates of gradient descent for the reward learning phase of RLHF.

Theorem 5.1
.

For every
t\geq 0
, the gradient descent procedure (
4
) with learning rate
\eta=1/\exp(2F)
satisfies

\displaystyle\left\lVert\omega_{t}-\omega^{*}_{\mathcal{D}_{n}}\right\rVert^{2}_{\Sigma_{\mathcal{D}_{n},R}}\leq O\left(1-\frac{1}{n}\right)^{t}~.

Proof sketch.
We begin by showing Lipschitzness and smoothness of
\mathcal{L}^{\omega}_{\textnormal{RLHF}}(\mathcal{D}_{n})
with respect to
\omega
. Then, we show that the PL condition
(
Karimi et al., 2016
)
is enough to guarantee fast convergence of projected gradient descent by showing that such a condition implies that
\mathcal{L}^{\omega}_{\textnormal{RLHF}}(\mathcal{D}_{n})
also satisfies the
proximal
PL condition
(
Karimi et al., 2016
)
when its domain is restricted to a ball. The result follows by applying the convexity of
\mathcal{L}^{\omega}_{\textnormal{RLHF}}(\mathcal{D}_{n})
.∎

Next, we discuss the policy optimization phase of RLHF. Let
r_{\widehat{\omega}}
denote the reward estimated from the previous phase. Initialize
\theta_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{P}}
with
\left\lVert\theta_{0}\right\rVert_{2}\leq B
. For any
t\geq 0
, let

\displaystyle\theta_{t+1}=\theta_{t}+\eta^{\prime}\left(\Psi_{n}\Psi^{\top}_{n}\right)^{\dagger}\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{V}^{\pi_{\theta}}_{r_{\widehat{\omega}}}(\mathcal{D}_{n})~,

(5)

where
\eta^{\prime}>0
is the learning rate and
\Psi_{n}=[\psi(x,y)]_{x\in\mathcal{D}_{n},y\in\mathcal{Y}}
denotes the sample feature matrix.
2
2

2

We only need
\left\lVert\theta_{0}\right\rVert_{2}\leq B
for RLHF, since its bounds do not depend on
\left\lVert\theta_{t}\right\rVert
, for
t\geq 1
. Thus, we do not need projection.
Then, the following result holds.

Theorem 5.2
.

Let
\delta>0
. Assume that
\Psi_{n}
has full column rank. Then, with probability at least
1-\delta
, for every
t\geq 1
, update rule (
5
) with learning rate
\eta^{\prime}\leq n/\beta
satisfies

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}^{*}_{r_{\widehat{\omega}}}\left(\mathcal{D}_{n}\right)-\mathcal{V}^{\pi_{\theta_{t}}}_{r_{\widehat{\omega}}}\left(\mathcal{D}_{n}\right)\leq O\left(\frac{1}{\beta}\exp\left(-(t-1)\right)\right)~.

Proof sketch.
After deriving a naive gradient update rule in matrix notation, we examine the conditions needed to obtain fast convergence rates for our setting. As a consequence, we design our gradient update which resembles natural policy gradient for loglinear policies – the matrix
(\Psi_{n}\Psi_{n}^{\top})^{\dagger}
captures the gradient information for this case. Then, we use similar techniques to those in
(
Mei et al., 2020
)
and use the fact that
\Psi_{n}
is full rank, to finally obtain the desired bounds. ∎

Remark 5.1
.

It is important to emphasize that the assumption on
\Psi_{n}
is not restrictive. Indeed, given a preference dataset, it is always possible to construct alternative feature representations that satisfy the assumption with respect to the given data (e.g. different LLMs use different encoding methods while being fine-tuned using the same data).

Remark 5.2
.

Before going to our next result, it is important to clarify the double usage of
\mathcal{D}_{n}
for both the reward learning and policy optimization phases. Our theoretical guarantees are based on the data being independently generated in these phases. Thus, the standard approach is to split the data into two batches for both purposes. Note that both batches would still be
O(n)
in size and the dependence of the results on
n
would not change. We use the same
\mathcal{D}_{n}
for both phases for simplicity of presentation.

Next, we provide convergence results of gradient descent for DPO with loglinear policies. Let
\theta_{0}
be initialized randomly, and let

\displaystyle\theta_{t+1}=\underset{\theta:\left\lVert\theta\right\rVert\leq B}{\proj}\left(\theta_{t}-\eta^{\prime\prime}\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{L}^{\theta}_{\textnormal{DPO}}(\mathcal{D}_{n})\right)~,

(6)

for any iterate
t\geq 0
, where
\eta^{\prime\prime}
denotes the learning rate. Let
\theta^{*}_{\mathcal{D}_{n}}\in\arg\max\mathcal{L}^{\theta}_{\textnormal{DPO}}(\mathcal{D}_{n})
. Then, we have the following result.

Theorem 5.3
.

For every
t\geq 0
, the gradient descent procedure (
6
) with learning rate
\eta^{\prime\prime}=O\left(1/\beta^{2}\right)
satisfies

\displaystyle\left\lVert\theta_{t}-\theta^{*}_{\mathcal{D}_{n}}\right\rVert^{2}_{\Sigma_{\mathcal{D}_{n},P}}

\displaystyle\leq O\left(\frac{1}{\beta}\left(1-\frac{\beta}{n}\right)^{t}\right)~.

We have omitted the dependence on the absolute constants that are irrelevant to our discussion – see Appendix
F
for a detailed expression of the hidden constants.

5.2
Comparative Analysis

The regularized suboptimality gap for RLHF is

\mathcal{G}(\pi_{\widehat{\theta}})\leq\Theta\left(\sqrt{\frac{d_{R}}{n}}\right)+O\left(\left(1-\frac{1}{n}\right)^{t}+\frac{\exp(-t)}{\beta}\right)

and the regularized suboptimality gap for DPO is

\mathcal{G}\left(\pi_{\widetilde{\theta}}\right)\leq\Theta\left(\frac{d_{P}}{\beta n}\right)+O\left(\frac{1}{\beta}\left(1-\frac{\beta}{n}\right)^{t}\right)~.

Both of these paradigms satisfy exponential convergence rates, thus, the main implications of the discussion of Section
4.2
hold in this setting as well if
\beta
is to be set as constant.
If
\beta
is to be tuned for DPO, it cannot be made arbitrarily small or large as observed in Section
4.2
– DPO’s overall bounds disproportionately depend on the parameter
\beta
. Even so, setting
\beta
to its optimal value of
\Theta(1/\sqrt{n})
for the exact optimization setting would not affect the convergence rate of gradient descent for DPO.

6
Non-realizable Rewards: Exact Optimization

In this section, we consider the case when the ground-truth reward function
r^{*}
does not belong to the linear class
\mathcal{F}
(see Definition
3.1
). We again assume that there exists an optimal
\pi^{*}_{r^{*}}
regularized policy that belongs to the loglinear class
\Pi
(see Definition
3.2
) for some
\theta^{*}
. We will capture the mismatch between the reward function and its best linear approximation in
\mathcal{F}
by the following condition.

Assumption 6.1

(
Non-realizability of the ground-truth reward
)
.

There exists
r_{\omega^{*}}\in\mathcal{F}
with parameter
\omega^{*}
such that
\left\lVert r^{*}-r_{\omega^{*}}\right\rVert_{\infty}\leq\epsilon_{\textnormal{app}}
,
where
\epsilon_{\textnormal{app}}>0
denotes the mismatch coefficient.

As we will see, the mismatch coefficient will appear linearly in the RLHF bounds on the gap as an additional constant that cannot be improved by increasing the data size.

6.1
Theoretical Results

We begin with the RLHF result, which can be derived from Theorem
4.1
.

Theorem 6.1
.

Let
\delta>0
. Suppose that Assumption
6.1
holds. Then, with probability at least
1-\delta
, we have
G\left(\pi_{\widehat{\theta}}\right)\leq D\left(\pi_{\widehat{\theta}}\right)+\widetilde{\Theta}\left({\Lambda_{R}}\sqrt{d_{R}/n}\right)+2\epsilon_{\textnormal{app}}
.

We can directly obtain a similar dependence on
\epsilon_{\textnormal{app}}
for DPO. In addition to that, we can also obtain alternative bounds that can be controlled by
\beta
as follows.

Theorem 6.2
.

Let
\delta>0
. Suppose that Assumption
6.1
and the condition of Lemma
4.1
hold. Then, with probability at least
1-\delta
, we have
G\left(\pi_{\widetilde{\theta}}\right)\leq D\left(\pi_{\widetilde{\theta}}\right)+\Theta\left(\Lambda_{P}d_{P}/(\beta n)\right)+\min\{2\epsilon_{\textnormal{app}},O\left(\beta D_{\textnormal{KL}}\left(\pi_{\theta^{*}}||\pi^{*}\right)\right)\}
.

6.2
Comparative Analysis

The key observation to be made in this section is the discrepancy of the bounds in terms of the reward mismatch coefficient. RLHF does not use MLE for the policy parameter estimation, but first learns a reward model. Thus, it cannot bypass the error coming from the reward unrealizability. For DPO, note that, if we set
\beta=O(1/\sqrt{n})
, its bounds improves asymptotically with
n
, assuming that
D_{\textnormal{KL}}(\pi_{\theta^{*}}||\pi^{*}_{r^{*}})
is bounded (recall that
\pi^{*}_{r^{*}}
denotes the optimal regularized policy and
\pi_{\theta^{*}}
denotes its best loglinear fit). This setting benefits DPO as it is designed to bypass the reward function and directly optimize over the policy space.

7
Realizable Rewards: Exact Optimization – An Extension to Deterministic MDPs

Up to this point, our discussion was concentrated on the contextual bandit setting, which has been used in the DPO literature for the KL-regularized problem
(
Rafailov et al., 2023
)
. Now, we focus on a generalization of our comparative analysis to Markov decision processes (MDPs), where contexts are related to each other through transition dynamics.

As mentioned previously, the discrepancy between reward and policy dimensions plays a crucial role in the relative performances of RLHF and DPO. While these dimensions could arguably be similar (or have a small gap) for the contextual bandit setting, that is not necessarily the case in general when extending to MDPs, where the reward dimension can be smaller than the policy dimension. For this section, we assume that the ground-truth reward function
r^{*}
is linear and realizable in
\mathcal{F}
.

7.1
Preliminaries for Deterministic MDPs

For an MDP,
\mathcal{X}
is the set of states and
\mathcal{Y}
the set of actions. In particular, we consider deterministic MDPs, with a transition function
T:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}\rightarrow\mathcal{X}
that provides the next state, given the current state-action.

The value function in infinite-horizon MDPs is given as
V^{\pi}_{r}(x)=\mathbb{E}[\sum_{t\geq 0}\gamma^{t}r(x_{t},y_{t})|\rho,\pi]
, where
\rho
denotes the initial state distribution. Given policy
\pi
, the occupancy measure of
\pi
is given by
d^{\pi}_{\rho}(x,y)=(1-\gamma)\sum_{t\geq 0}\gamma^{t}\mathbb{P}\left(x_{t}=x,y_{t}=y|\rho,\pi\right)
. We will consider the class of loglinear occupancy measures, as defined next.

Definition 7.1

(
Loglinear occupancy measures class
)
.

Let
\psi^{\prime}(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{M}}
denote the feature vector of the pair
(x,y)
with
\max_{x,y}\left\lVert\psi^{\prime}(x,y)\right\rVert_{2}\leq 1
, and
B^{\prime}>0
. We consider the following class of loglinear occupancy measures:

\displaystyle\Pi^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\Big\{d^{\theta}_{\rho}:d^{\theta}_{\rho}(x,y)=\frac{\exp(\theta^{\top}\psi^{\prime}(x,y))}{\sum_{x^{\prime},y^{\prime}}\exp(\theta^{\top}\psi^{\prime}(x^{\prime},y^{\prime}))},

\displaystyle\forall(x,y)\in\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}\;\text{where}\;\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{M}}\;\text{and}\;\left\lVert\theta\right\rVert_{2}\leq B^{\prime}\Big\}~.

In this section, we use the
\mathcal{V}^{d_{\rho}}_{r}(\rho)
notation, instead of
\mathcal{V}^{\pi}_{r}(\rho)
. Similarly, we use
G(d_{\rho})
to denote the gap in terms of occupancy measure
d_{\rho}
, and
D(d_{\rho})
for the difference of the gaps. For a complete discussion, see Appendix
H
.

In this setting, we are given a dataset
\mathcal{D}_{n}=\{(x_{0,i},\tau^{w}_{i},\tau^{l}_{i})\}^{n}_{i=1}
, where
x_{0,i}
denotes the initial state of the
i
th sample and
\tau^{w}_{i}
denotes the preferred trajectory
(x_{0,i},y^{w}_{0,i},x^{w}_{1,i},\ldots)
over
\tau^{l}_{i}
. Analogous to Section
3
, we define the Bradley-Terry preference model for two trajectories
\tau^{w}
and
\tau^{l}
as
P^{*}(\tau^{w}\succ\tau^{l}|x_{0})=\sigma(R^{*}(\tau^{w})-R^{*}(\tau^{l}))
,
where
R^{*}(\tau)=\sum_{t\geq 0}\gamma^{t}r^{*}(x_{t},y_{t})
is the discounted return, and
\tau^{w}\succ\tau^{l}
denotes
\tau^{w}
being preferred over
\tau^{l}
.

7.2
RLHF and DPO for MDPs

Similar to the contextual bandit setting, the objective for the reward learning phase of RLHF in MDPs with linear rewards can be written as follows:

\displaystyle\min_{\omega}\mathcal{L}^{\omega}_{\textnormal{RLHF}}(\mathcal{D}_{n}):=-\mathbb{E}_{(x_{0},\tau^{w},\tau^{l})\sim\mathcal{D}_{n}}

(P3.1)

\displaystyle\left[\log\sigma\left(\omega^{\top}\left(\sum_{t\geq 0}\gamma^{t}\left(\phi(x^{w}_{t},y^{w}_{t})-\phi(x^{l}_{t},y^{l}_{t})\right)\right)\right)\right]~.

Once we have the estimated reward function
r_{\widehat{\omega}}
, the objective is to solve the KL-regularized problem. Following previous literature on KL-regularized RL
(
Nachum et al., 2019
;
Lee et al., 2021
)
, we formulate the objective in this setting as

\displaystyle\max_{\pi}V^{\pi}_{r_{\widehat{\omega}}}(\rho)-\beta D_{\textnormal{KL}}\left(d^{\pi}_{\rho}||d^{\mu}_{\rho}\right)~.

(P3.2)

We will assume throughout this section that we are given access to oracles that exactly solve Problem (
P3.1
) and (
P3.2
).

Remark 7.2
.

Note that the objective in Problem (
P3.2
) depends on
\rho
, while the objective in Problem (
P1.2
) depends on
\mathcal{D}_{n}
. This is due to considering occupancy measures instead of policies. We keep our current formulation for ease of presentation and leave its extension to a sample objective formulation for future work.

For the purposes of our comparative analysis, we also need an extension of DPO to MDPs, based on the preference model of Section
7.1
. The key difficulty of extending DPO to the MDP setting is that the gradient has a non-linear dependence on the policy. To bypass this issue, we leverage the fact that transitions are deterministic to simplify cumulative differences of the optimal Lagrange multipliers for Problem (
P3.2
), and obtain the following loss function for DPO:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\textnormal{DPO}}^{d_{\rho}}(\mathcal{D}_{n})=-\mathbb{E}_{(x_{0},\tau^{w},\tau^{l})\sim\mathcal{D}_{n}}\Bigg[

\displaystyle\log\sigma\left(\beta\sum^{\infty}_{t=0}\gamma^{t}\left(\log\frac{d_{\rho}(x^{w}_{t},y^{w}_{t})}{d^{\mu}_{\rho}(x^{w}_{t},y^{w}_{t})}-\log\frac{d_{\rho}(x^{l}_{t},y^{l}_{t})}{d^{\mu}_{\rho}(x^{l}_{t},y^{l}_{t})}\right)\right)\Bigg]~.

All derivations are in Appendix
H
.
Next, we generalize the bounds from Section
4
for the above formulations.

7.3
Theoretical Results

Analogous to the previous sections, let us define

\displaystyle\Sigma^{\prime}_{\mathcal{D}_{n},R}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{(x_{0},\tau^{w},\tau^{l})\in\mathcal{D}_{n}}\overline{\phi}^{\prime}(x_{0},\tau^{w},\tau^{l})\overline{\phi}^{\prime}(x_{0},\tau^{w},\tau^{l})^{\top}~,

where
\overline{\phi}^{\prime}(x_{0},\tau^{w},\tau^{l})=\sum_{t\geq 0}\gamma^{t}(\phi(x_{t},y^{w}_{t})-\phi(x_{t},y^{l}_{t}))
. For
\lambda>0
, define
\Lambda^{\prime}_{R}=\left\lVert(\Sigma^{\prime}_{\mathcal{D}_{n},R}+\lambda I)^{-1/2}\right\rVert_{2}
. Similarly, let the sample covariance matrix with respect to occupancy measure features be defined as

\displaystyle\Sigma^{\prime}_{\mathcal{D}_{n},M}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{(x_{0},\tau^{w},\tau^{l})\in\mathcal{D}_{n}}\overline{\psi}^{\prime}(x_{0},\tau^{w},\tau^{l})\overline{\psi}^{\prime}(x_{0},\tau^{w},\tau^{l})^{\top}~,

where
\overline{\psi}^{\prime}(x_{0},\tau^{w},\tau^{l})=\sum_{t\geq 0}\gamma^{t}((\psi^{\prime}(x_{t},y^{w}_{t})-\psi^{\prime}(x_{t},y^{l}_{t}))
. Let
\Lambda^{\prime}_{M}=\left\lVert(\Sigma^{\prime}_{\mathcal{D}_{n},M}+\lambda I)^{-1/2}\right\rVert_{2}
.
For RLHF with exact optimization, it is straightforward to extend our previous bounds as follows.

Theorem 7.1
.

Let
\delta>0
. Assume that the policy learning phase yields
{\widehat{\theta}}\in\arg\max_{\theta}\mathcal{V}^{d^{\theta}_{\rho}}_{r_{\widehat{\omega}}}(\rho)
, where
r_{\widehat{\omega}}
is the estimated reward. Then, with probability at least
1-\delta
, the suboptimality gap incurred by RLHF is
G(d^{\widehat{\theta}}_{\rho})=D(d^{\widehat{\theta}}_{\rho})+\widetilde{\Theta}(\Lambda^{\prime}_{R}\sqrt{d_{R}/n})~.

Now that we have a formulation for DPO, we can also extend the previous bounds for the MDP setting. Similar to Lemma
4.1
, we now state an analogous result for this setting that guarantees the expression of the ground-truth reward in terms of optimal loglinear occupancy measures. Recall that
\Phi
and
\Psi
denote the reward and policy feature matrices, respectively. Let
\pi^{*}_{r^{*}}\in\arg\max_{\pi}\mathcal{V}^{\pi}_{r^{*}}(\rho)
. Define
\Phi_{\pi^{*}_{r^{*}}}
to be the
d_{R}\times XY
-dimensional matrix with columns defined as
\gamma\mathbb{E}[\sum_{t}\gamma^{t}\phi(x_{t},y_{t})|x_{0}=x,\pi^{*}_{r^{*}}]-\mathbb{E}[\sum_{t}\gamma^{t}\phi(x_{t},y_{t})|x_{0}=x,y_{0}=y,\pi^{*}_{r^{*}}]
. We have the following result.

Lemma 7.3
.

Assume that
r^{*}\in\mathcal{F}
,
d^{\mu}_{\rho}\in\Pi^{\prime}
and
d^{\pi^{*}_{r^{*}}}_{\rho}\in\Pi^{\prime}
, for some optimal
d^{\pi^{*}_{r^{*}}}_{\rho}
. Furthermore, assume that the column space of
\Phi+\Phi_{\pi^{*}_{r^{*}}}
is contained in the column space of
\Psi
. Then, for finite MDPs with deterministic transitions, there exists
\theta^{*}
such that
d^{\theta^{*}}_{\rho}\in\arg\max_{d}\mathcal{V}^{d}_{r^{*}}(\rho)
and
d^{\theta^{*}}_{\rho}(x,y)\propto d^{\mu}_{\rho}(x,y)\exp(A^{\pi^{*}_{r^{*}}}_{r^{*}}(x,y)/\beta)
, where
A^{\pi^{*}_{r^{*}}}_{r^{*}}(x,y)=Q^{\pi^{*}_{r^{*}}}_{r^{*}}(x,y)-V^{\pi^{*}_{r^{*}}}_{r^{*}}(x)
.

Now we are ready to state the DPO result for this section.

Theorem 7.2
.

Let
\delta>0
. Let
d^{\widetilde{\theta}}_{\rho}
denote the occupancy measure returned by DPO and assume that
d^{\pi^{*}}_{\rho}\in\Pi^{\prime}
, for some
d^{\pi^{*}}_{\rho}\in\arg\max_{d_{\rho}}\mathcal{V}^{d_{\rho}}_{r^{*}}(\rho)
, and that the condition of Lemma
7.3
is satisfied. Then, for any
n\geq O\left(tr((\Sigma^{\prime}_{{\mathcal{D}_{n},M}})^{\dagger})/(\beta(B^{\prime})^{2})\right)
, with probability at least
1-\delta
, we have
G(d^{\widetilde{\theta}}_{\rho})=D(d^{\widetilde{\theta}}_{\rho})+\Theta(\Lambda^{\prime}_{M}(d_{M}+1)/(\beta n)+\beta\Lambda_{M}\lambda(B^{\prime})^{2})~.

Proof sketch.
We start by expressing the optimal discounted reward in terms of an optimal occupancy measure, which is also loginear, by using Lemma
7.3
. Then, we equivalently express the value function in terms of occupancy measures. This allows us to cancel out some terms and express the whole gap in terms of the
D_{\textnormal{KL}}(d^{\widetilde{\theta}}_{\rho}||d^{\theta^{*}}_{\rho})
. Finally, similar to the proof of Theorem
4.2
, using loglinearity and properties of the log-sum-exp function, we obtain the desired bounds.∎

7.4
Comparative Analysis

The main implication of the above results is that the observations made in Section
4
extend to deterministic MDPs, using our proposed formulation of RLHF and DPO. For the optimal value of
\beta
for DPO as discussed in Section
4.2
, the RLHF and DPO bounds become directly comparable in terms of the dimension differences for deterministic MDPs. In MDPs with simple reward models (e.g., low-dimensional linear reward models), typically there is still a necessity for high-dimensional policy parameters to represent the value function effectively. This suggests that the complexity of the policy class exceeds that of the reward class and that RLHF outperforms DPO in such instances.

8
Concluding Discussion

In this paper, we provided a comparative analysis between reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) and direct preference optimization (DPO). We performed a thorough analysis under different settings, where we derived sample complexity bounds for both paradigms and drew conclusions on their statistical comparison, based on sample size, regularization temperature, and the dimensionality of their respective parametrizations. We believe these results will initiate a larger discussion on the differences between these two paradigms.

There are many interesting future directions to pursue. The first natural extension of this work is to relax the assumptions made on policy and reward classes and provide a comparative analysis of RLHF and DPO on more realistic settings (eg. general function approximation). Next, a systematic large-scale empirical investigation that would validate the theoretical insights of this paper would be of great importance. Finally, as our current extension of DPO for MDPs is limited to deterministic MDPs using a loglinear occupancy measure regularization, it would be interesting to see whether DPO can be extended to more general formulations.

Impact Statement

This paper focuses on the theoretical aspects of machine learning, providing a comparative analysis of different paradigms of learning from human preferences. We do not foresee any direct negative outcomes from the findings of this paper. On the contrary, we believe that our results might initiate a larger discussion on the statistical properties of learning from human preferences.

\displaystyle=D\left(d^{\widetilde{\theta}}_{\rho}\right)+\mathbb{E}_{(x,y)\sim d^{\widetilde{\theta}}_{\rho}}\left[\beta\log d^{\widetilde{\theta}}_{\rho}(x,y)-\beta\log d^{\theta^{*}}_{\rho}(x,y)\right]

\displaystyle=D\left(d^{\widetilde{\theta}}_{\rho}\right)+\mathbb{E}_{(x,y)\sim d^{\widetilde{\theta}}_{\rho}}\Bigg[\beta\left\langle\psi^{\prime}(x,y),\widetilde{\theta}-\theta^{*}\right\rangle+\beta\log\frac{\sum_{x^{\prime},y^{\prime}}\exp(\psi^{\prime}(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})^{\top}\theta^{*})}{\sum_{x^{\prime},y^{\prime}}\exp(\psi^{\prime}(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})^{\top}\widetilde{\theta})}\Bigg]

(10)

\displaystyle=D\left(d^{\widetilde{\theta}}_{\rho}\right)+\beta\mathbb{E}_{(x,y)\sim d^{\widetilde{\theta}}_{\rho}}\left[\left\langle\psi^{\prime}(x,y),\widetilde{\theta}-\theta^{*}\right\rangle+A(\theta^{*})-A(\widetilde{\theta})\right]~,

where we have denoted by

A(\theta)=\log\sum_{x^{\prime},y^{\prime}}\exp(\psi^{\prime}(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})^{\top}\theta)

the log-sum-exp function. Above, in Equation (
8
) we have applied Equation (
7
); Equation (
9
) uses the fact that, for any policy
\pi
and function

, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\rho,y_{t}\sim\pi(\cdot|x_{t})}\left[\sum_{t\geq 0}\gamma^{t}f(x_{t},y_{t})\right]=\mathbb{E}_{(x,y)\sim d^{\pi}}\left[f(x,y)\right]~.

Finally, for Equation (
10
) we have used loglinearity. Now, given
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{P}}
, note that

\displaystyle\nabla_{\theta}A(\theta)

\displaystyle=\frac{\sum_{x,y}\exp(\psi^{\prime}(x,y)^{\top}\theta)\cdot\psi^{\prime}(x,y)}{\sum_{x^{\prime},y^{\prime}}\exp(\psi^{\prime}(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})^{\top}\theta)}=\sum_{x,y}d^{\theta}_{\rho}(x,y)\psi^{\prime}(x,y)~.

On the other hand, the Hessian of
A(\theta)
is

\displaystyle\nabla^{2}_{\theta}A(\theta)

\displaystyle=\sum_{x,y}\nabla_{\theta}d^{\theta}_{\rho}(x,y)\psi^{\prime}(x,y)

\displaystyle=\sum_{x,y}d^{\theta}_{\rho}(x,y)\left(\psi^{\prime}(x,y)-\mathbb{E}_{(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\sim d^{\theta}_{\rho}}[\psi^{\prime}(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})]\right)\psi^{\prime}(x,y)^{\top}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{(x,y)\sim d^{\theta}_{\rho}}\left[\psi^{\prime}(x,y)\psi^{\prime}(x,y)^{\top}\right]-\mathbb{E}_{(x,y)\sim d^{\theta}_{\rho}}[\psi^{\prime}(x,y)]\mathbb{E}_{(x,y)\sim d_{\theta}}[\psi^{\prime}(x,y)]^{\top}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{(x,y)\sim d^{\theta}_{\rho}}\left[\left(\psi^{\prime}(x,y)-\mathbb{E}_{\theta}\left[\psi^{\prime}(x,y)\right]\right)\left(\psi^{\prime}(x,y)-\mathbb{E}_{\theta}\left[\psi^{\prime}(x,y)\right]\right)^{\top}\right]~.

By assumption on the feature mapping, we have that

\displaystyle\left\lVert\nabla^{2}_{\theta}A(\theta)\right\rVert_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\max_{x,y}\left\lVert\left(\psi^{\prime}(x,y)-\mathbb{E}_{\theta}\left[\psi^{\prime}(x,y)\right]\right)\left(\psi^{\prime}(x,y)-\mathbb{E}_{\theta}\left[\psi^{\prime}(x,y)\right]\right)^{\top}\right\rVert_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\max_{x,y}\left\lVert\psi^{\prime}(x,y)-\mathbb{E}_{\theta}[\psi^{\prime}(x,y)]\right\rVert_{2}

\displaystyle\leq 2\max_{x,y}\left\lVert\psi^{\prime}(x,y)\right\rVert_{2}=2~.

Therefore, the function
A(\theta)
is
2
-smooth in
\theta
, which implies that

\displaystyle A(\theta^{*})-A(\widetilde{\theta})

\displaystyle\leq\left\langle\nabla_{\theta}A(\widetilde{\theta}),\theta^{*}-\widetilde{\theta}\right\rangle+\left\lVert\theta^{*}-\widetilde{\theta}\right\rVert^{2}_{2}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{(x,y)\sim d^{\widetilde{\theta}}_{\rho}}\left[\left\langle\psi^{\prime}(x,y),\theta^{*}-\widetilde{\theta}\right\rangle\right]+\left\lVert\theta^{*}-\widetilde{\theta}\right\rVert^{2}_{2}~.

Substituting to the suboptimality gap equalities, we obtain

\displaystyle G\left(d^{\widetilde{\theta}}_{\rho}\right)

\displaystyle\leq D\left(d^{\widetilde{\theta}}_{\rho}\right)+\left\lVert\theta^{*}-\widetilde{\theta}\right\rVert^{2}_{2}

\displaystyle\leq D\left(d^{\widetilde{\theta}}_{\rho}\right)+\beta\left\lVert(\Sigma^{\prime}_{\mathcal{D}_{n},M}+\lambda I)^{-1}\right\rVert_{2}\left\lVert\theta^{*}-\widetilde{\theta}\right\rVert^{2}_{\Sigma^{\prime}_{\mathcal{D}_{n},M}+\lambda I}

\displaystyle\leq D\left(d^{\widetilde{\theta}}_{\rho}\right)+\frac{\Lambda_{M}U^{\prime}d_{M}}{S_{M}\beta n}+4\beta\Lambda_{M}\lambda(B^{\prime})^{2}~,

where the last inequality follows from Theorem
J.7
and the assumption on exact optimization.

For the lower bound, let
\psi^{\prime}
be sampled from a
0
-mean bounded distribution. Note that, for any non-zero vector in
\mathbb{R}^{d_{M}}
, we have

\displaystyle z^{\top}\nabla^{2}_{\theta}A(\theta)z

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{(x,y)\sim d^{\theta}_{\rho}}\left[z^{\top}\psi^{\prime}(x,y)\psi^{\prime}(x,y)^{\top}z\right]

\displaystyle\geq\min_{\theta,x,y}d^{\theta}_{\rho}(x,y)\sum_{x,y}(\psi^{\prime}(x,y)^{\top}z)^{2}

\displaystyle\geq C_{3}\sum_{x,y}(\psi^{\prime}(x,y)^{\top}z)^{2}~,

for a positive
C_{3}
, since
d^{\theta}_{\rho}
is in the loglinear class, for every
\theta
. Now, note that, if
z
can be expressed as a linear combination of
\{\psi^{\prime}(x,y)\}_{x,y}
, the summation cannot be zero for non-zero
z
. Thus, if
\{\psi^{\prime}(x,y)\}_{x,y}
spans
\mathbb{R}^{d_{M}}
, that is, the feature matrix is full rank, then there exists an absolute positive constant
\kappa
, such that we have

\displaystyle\left\lVert\nabla^{2}_{\theta}A(\theta)\right\rVert_{2}\geq\kappa>0~.

Thus, the function
A(\theta)
is
\kappa
-strongly convex. This, in turn, implies that

\displaystyle A(\theta^{*})-A(\widetilde{\theta})

\displaystyle\geq\left\langle\nabla_{\theta}A(\widetilde{\theta}),\theta^{*}-\widetilde{\theta}\right\rangle+\frac{\kappa}{2}\left\lVert\widetilde{\theta}-\theta^{*}\right\rVert^{2}_{2}

\displaystyle\geq\left\langle\nabla_{\theta}A(\widetilde{\theta}),\theta^{*}-\widetilde{\theta}\right\rangle+\frac{\kappa}{2}\left\lVert\widetilde{\theta}-\theta^{*}\right\rVert^{2}_{\Sigma^{\prime}_{\mathcal{D}_{n},M}}

\displaystyle=-\mathbb{E}_{(x,y)\sim d^{\widetilde{\theta}}_{\rho}}\left[\left\langle\psi^{\prime}(x,y),\widetilde{\theta}-\theta^{*}\right\rangle\right]+\frac{\kappa}{2}\left\lVert\widetilde{\theta}-\theta^{*}\right\rVert^{2}_{\Sigma^{\prime}_{\mathcal{D}_{n},M}}~,

using a similar argument as in the proof of Theorem
4.2
. Thus, substituting in the original gap expression, we obtain

\displaystyle G\left(d^{\widetilde{\theta}}_{\rho}\right)

\displaystyle\geq D\left(d^{\widetilde{\theta}}_{\rho}\right)+\frac{\beta\kappa}{2}\left\lVert\widetilde{\theta}-\theta^{*}\right\rVert^{2}_{2}

\displaystyle\geq D\left(d^{\widetilde{\theta}}_{\rho}\right)+\Omega\left(\frac{d_{M}+1}{\beta n}\right)~,

for any
n\geq O\left(tr((\Sigma^{\prime}_{\mathcal{D}})^{\dagger})/(S_{M}(B^{\prime})^{2})\right)
, by Theorem
J.7
.
∎

Appendix D
Convergence of Gradient Descent for RLHF Reward Learning (Section
5
)

In this section, we will prove convergence bounds for projected gradient descent for the RLHF reward learning phase. Recall that, the projected gradient update rule for the reward learning phase is given as

\displaystyle\omega_{t+1}=\underset{\omega:\left\lVert\omega\right\rVert_{2}\leq F}{\proj}\left(\omega_{t}-\eta\nabla_{\omega}\mathcal{L}^{\omega}_{\textnormal{RLHF}}(\mathcal{D}_{n})\right)~.

First, we show Lipschitzness and smoothness of the loss function.

Lemma D.1
.

The RLHF reward learning objective
\mathcal{L}^{\omega}_{\textnormal{RLHF}}(\mathcal{D}_{n})
is
2\exp(2F)
-Lipschitz and
2\exp(2F)
-smooth.

Proof.

Note that the gradient of
\mathcal{L}^{\omega}_{\textnormal{RLHF}}(\mathcal{D}_{n})
satisfies

\displaystyle\left\lVert\nabla_{\omega}\mathcal{L}^{\omega}_{\textnormal{RLHF}}(\mathcal{D}_{n})\right\rVert_{2}

\displaystyle=\left\lVert\frac{1}{n}\sum_{(x,y^{w},y^{l})\sim\mathcal{D}_{n}}\nabla_{\omega}\log\left(1+\exp\left(\omega^{\top}\left(\phi(x,y^{w})-\phi(x,y^{l})\right)\right)\right)\right\rVert_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{n}\sum_{(x,y^{w},y^{l})\sim\mathcal{D}_{n}}\frac{\exp\left(\omega^{\top}\left(\phi(x,y^{w})-\phi(x,y^{l})\right)\right)}{1+\exp\left(\omega^{\top}\left(\phi(x,y^{w})-\phi(x,y^{l})\right)\right)}\left\lVert\left(\phi(x,y^{w})-\phi(x,y^{l})\right)\right\rVert_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\exp(2F)\left\lVert\phi(x,y^{w})-\phi(x,y^{l})\right\rVert_{2}

\displaystyle\leq 2\exp(2F)~.

Moreover, the Hessian of
\mathcal{L}^{\omega}_{\textnormal{RLHF}}(\mathcal{D}_{n})
satisfies

\displaystyle\left\lVert\nabla^{2}_{\omega}\mathcal{L}^{\omega}_{\textnormal{RLHF}}(\mathcal{D}_{n})\right\rVert_{2}=\left\lVert\frac{1}{n}\sum_{(x,y^{w},y^{l})\sim\mathcal{D}_{n}}\nabla_{\omega}\frac{\exp\left(\omega^{\top}\left(\phi(x,y^{w})-\phi(x,y^{l})\right)\right)}{1+\exp\left(\omega^{\top}\left(\phi(x,y^{w})-\phi(x,y^{l})\right)\right)}\left(\phi(x,y^{w})-\phi(x,y^{l})\right)\right\rVert_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{n}\sum_{(x,y^{w},y^{l})\sim\mathcal{D}_{n}}\frac{\exp\left(\omega^{\top}\left(\phi(x,y^{w})-\phi(x,y^{l})\right)\right)}{\left(1+\exp\left(\omega^{\top}\left(\phi(x,y^{w})-\phi(x,y^{l})\right)\right)\right)^{2}}\left\lVert\left(\phi(x,y^{w})-\phi(x,y^{l})\right)\left(\phi(x,y^{w})-\phi(x,y^{l})\right)^{\top}\right\rVert_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\exp(2F)\left\lVert\left(\phi(x,y^{w})-\phi(x,y^{l})\right)\right\rVert_{2}

\displaystyle\leq 2\exp(2F)~.

The result follows.
∎

Next, we show that
\mathcal{L}^{\omega}_{\textnormal{RLHF}}(\mathcal{D}_{n})
satisfies the PL condition
(
Karimi et al., 2016
)
defined below.

Definition D.2
.

A function
\mathcal{L}
is said to satisfy the PL condition with coefficient
C_{PL}>0
if, for every
\omega
in the domain of
\mathcal{L}
, we have

\displaystyle\left\lVert\nabla_{\omega}\mathcal{L}(\omega)\right\rVert^{2}_{2}\geq C_{PL}\left(\mathcal{L}(\omega)-\mathcal{L}^{*}\right)~,

where
\mathcal{L}^{*}
is the minimum value of
\mathcal{L}
in its domain.

Lemma D.3
.

Let
L_{2}=2\exp(2F)
and

\displaystyle C_{PL}=\frac{\exp(-2F)\xi(1+\exp(-2F))}{n(1+\exp(2F)^{2}}~,

where

\displaystyle 0<\xi=\min_{(x,y^{w},y^{l})\sim\mathcal{D}_{n}}\left\lVert\phi(x,y^{w})-\phi(x,y^{l})\right\rVert^{2}_{2}~.

Then, we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert\nabla_{\omega}\mathcal{L}^{\omega}_{\textnormal{RLHF}}(\mathcal{D}_{n})\right\rVert^{2}\geq C_{PL}\left(\mathcal{L}^{\omega}_{\textnormal{RLHF}}(\mathcal{D}_{n})-\mathcal{L}^{*}_{\textnormal{RLHF}}(\mathcal{D}_{n})\right)~.

Proof.

Due to the assumption on the features and parameter vectors, we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert\nabla_{\omega}\mathcal{L}^{\omega}_{\textnormal{RLHF}}(\mathcal{D}_{n})\right\rVert^{2}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2n^{2}}\sum_{(x,y^{w},y^{l})\sim\mathcal{D}_{n}}\frac{\exp(-2F)}{1+\exp(2F)}\min_{(x,y^{w},y^{l})\sim\mathcal{D}_{n}}\left\lVert\phi(x,y^{w})-\phi(x,y^{l})\right\rVert^{2}_{2}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\exp(-2F)\xi}{n(1+\exp(2F))}~.

On the other hand, note that, for some
\omega^{*}
such that
\left\lVert\omega^{*}\right\rVert_{2}\leq F
, we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{\omega}_{\textnormal{RLHF}}(\mathcal{D}_{n})-\mathcal{L}^{*}_{\textnormal{RLHF}}(\mathcal{D}_{n})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{(x,y^{w},y^{l})\sim\mathcal{D}_{n}}\Big[\log\left(1+\exp\left(\omega^{\top}\left(\phi(x,y^{w})-\phi(x,y^{l})\right)\right)\right)

\displaystyle\quad\quad-\log\left(1+\exp\left((\omega^{*})^{\top}\left(\phi(x,y^{w})-\phi(x,y^{l})\right)\right)\right)\Big]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{(x,y^{w},y^{l})\sim\mathcal{D}_{n}}\log\frac{1+\exp\left(\omega^{\top}\left(\phi(x,y^{w})-\phi(x,y^{l})\right)\right)}{1+\exp\left((\omega^{*})^{\top}\left(\phi(x,y^{w})-\phi(x,y^{l})\right)\right)}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{n}\sum_{(x,y^{w},y^{l})\sim\mathcal{D}_{n}}\left(\frac{1+\exp\left(\omega^{\top}\left(\phi(x,y^{w})-\phi(x,y^{l})\right)\right)}{1+\exp\left((\omega^{*})^{\top}\left(\phi(x,y^{w})-\phi(x,y^{l})\right)\right)}-1\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{n}\sum_{(x,y^{w},y^{l})\sim\mathcal{D}_{n}}\frac{1+\exp(2F)}{1+\exp(-2F)}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1+\exp(2F)}{1+\exp(-2F)}~,

where the third inequality follows from
\log x\leq x-1
, for
x>0
.
Solving for
C_{PL}
the equation

\displaystyle C_{PL}\frac{1+\exp(2F)}{1+\exp(-2F)}=\frac{\exp(-2F)\xi}{n(1+\exp(2F)}~,

we obtain

\displaystyle C_{PL}=\frac{\exp(-2F)\xi(1+\exp(-2F))}{n(1+\exp(2F)^{2}}~.

∎

Now, we are ready to state the convergence result for gradient descent.
See
5.1

Proof.

The projected gradient descent rule is equivalent to the proximal gradient update
(
Karimi et al., 2016
)
, given as

\displaystyle\omega_{t+1}=\arg\min_{\omega}\left(\left\langle\nabla_{\omega}\mathcal{L}^{\omega_{t}}_{\textnormal{RLHF}}(\mathcal{D}_{n}),\omega-\omega_{t}\right\rangle+\frac{L_{2}}{2}\left\lVert\omega-\omega_{t}\right\rVert^{2}_{2}+g(\omega)-g(\omega_{t})\right)~,

where
g(\omega)=0
, if
\left\lVert\omega\right\rVert_{2}\leq F
and
\infty
otherwise. To see that, note that we can equivalently write the above as

\displaystyle\omega_{t+1}

\displaystyle=\arg\min_{\omega}\left\lVert\omega-\left(\omega_{t}-\frac{1}{L_{2}}\nabla_{\omega}\mathcal{L}^{\omega_{t}}_{\textnormal{RLHF}}(\mathcal{D}_{n})\right)\right\rVert_{2},\;\textnormal{s.t.}\;\left\lVert\omega\right\rVert_{2}\leq F

\displaystyle=\proj_{\omega:\left\lVert\omega\right\rVert_{2}\leq F}\left(\omega_{t}-\frac{1}{L_{2}}\nabla_{\omega}\mathcal{L}^{\omega_{t}}_{\textnormal{RLHF}}(\mathcal{D}_{n})\right)~.

Theorem 5 of
(
Karimi et al., 2016
)
gives us linear rates of convergence for projected gradient descent under the
proximal PL
condition. This condition is shown in Appendix G of
(
Karimi et al., 2016
)
to be equivalent to the following condition.

Definition D.4
.

A function
F
is said to satisfy the Kurdyka-Lojasiewicz condition with exponent
1/2
if there exists
C>0
such that

\displaystyle\min_{s\in\partial F(\omega)}\left\lVert s\right\rVert^{2}_{2}\geq C(F(\omega)-F_{*})~,

where
\partial F(\omega)
is the Frechet subdifferential of
F
at
\omega
and
F_{*}
denotes the minimum value of
F
.

Note that, in our case we have
F(\omega)=\mathcal{L}^{\omega}_{\textnormal{RLHF}}(\mathcal{D}_{n})-g(\omega)
and the Frechet subdifferential of this function in the domain
\{\omega:\left\lVert\omega\right\rVert_{2}\leq F\}
only contains
\nabla_{\omega}\mathcal{L}^{\omega}_{\textnormal{RLHF}}(\mathcal{D}_{n})
. Thus, the above condition is equivalent to the PL condition. As a consequence Theorem 5 of
(
Karimi et al., 2016
)
implies that

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{\omega_{t}}_{\textnormal{RLHF}}(\mathcal{D}_{n})-\mathcal{L}^{*}_{\textnormal{RLHF}}(\mathcal{D}_{n})\leq\left(1-\frac{C_{PL}}{L_{2}}\right)^{t}\left(\mathcal{L}^{\omega_{0}}_{\textnormal{RLHF}}(\mathcal{D}_{n})-\mathcal{L}^{*}_{\textnormal{RLHF}}(\mathcal{D}_{n})\right)~.

Now, recalling the Hessian of our loss, note that for any non-zero vector
v\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{P}}
, we have

\displaystyle v^{\top}\nabla^{2}_{\omega}\mathcal{L}^{\omega}_{\textnormal{RLHF}}(\mathcal{D}_{n})v

\displaystyle=v^{\top}\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{(x,y^{w},y^{l})\sim\mathcal{D}_{n}}\frac{\exp\left(\omega^{\top}\left(\phi(x,y^{w})-\phi(x,y^{l})\right)\right)}{(1+\exp\left(\omega^{\top}\left(\phi(x,y^{w})-\phi(x,y^{l})\right)\right))^{2}}\left(\phi(x,y^{w})-\phi(x,y^{l})\right)\left(\phi(x,y^{w})-\phi(x,y^{l})\right)^{\top}\right)v

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\exp(-F)}{(1+\exp(2F))^{2}}\left\lVert v\right\rVert^{2}_{\Sigma_{\mathcal{D}_{n},R}}~.

Thus,
\mathcal{L}^{\omega}_{\textnormal{RLHF}}(\mathcal{D}_{n})
is
\frac{\exp(-F)}{(1+\exp(2F))^{2}}
-strongly convex with respect to the semi-norm
\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{\Sigma_{\mathcal{D}_{n},R}}
around
\omega^{*}
. Therefore, for any
\omega
we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{\omega}_{\textnormal{RLHF}}(\mathcal{D}_{n})-\mathcal{L}^{*}_{\textnormal{RLHF}}(\mathcal{D}_{n})

\displaystyle\geq\left\langle\nabla_{\omega}\mathcal{L}^{*}_{\textnormal{RLHF}}(\mathcal{D}_{n}),\omega-\omega^{*}\right\rangle+\frac{\exp(-2F)}{(1+\exp(2F))^{2}}\left\lVert\omega-\omega^{*}\right\rVert_{\Sigma_{\mathcal{D}_{n},R}}^{2}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\exp(-2F)}{(1+\exp(2F))^{2}}\left\lVert\omega-\omega^{*}\right\rVert_{\Sigma_{\mathcal{D}_{n},R}}^{2}~.

Putting everything together, we obtain

\displaystyle\left\lVert\omega-\omega^{*}\right\rVert_{\Sigma_{\mathcal{D}_{n},R}}^{2}\leq O\left(\left(1-\frac{1}{n}\right)^{t}\right)~.

∎

Appendix E
Convergence of Natural Policy Gradient for RLHF (Section
5
)

In this section, we prove fast convergence rates for the a version of the natural policy gradient algorithm with loglinear policy class.
We begin by deriving the gradient of the KL-regularized objective. Throughout, let us fix reward function
r
and dataset
{\mathcal{D}_{n}}
.

Lemma E.1
.

Given reward
r
and policy
\pi_{\theta}
, the gradient of
\mathcal{V}^{\pi_{\theta}}_{r}({\mathcal{D}_{n}})
, for the softmax policy class can be written as

\displaystyle\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{V}^{\pi_{\theta}}_{r}({\mathcal{D}_{n}})=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{x\in{\mathcal{D}_{n}}}\sum_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}\pi_{\theta}(y|x)\left(r(x,y)-\beta\log\left(\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y|x)}{\mu(y|x)}\right)\right)\overline{\psi}_{\theta}(x,y)~,

where

\displaystyle\overline{\psi}_{\theta}(x,y)=\psi(x,y)-\sum_{y^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Y}}\pi_{\theta}(y^{\prime}|x)\psi(x,y^{\prime})~.

Proof.

First, note that, for softmax policies with linear action preferences, we have, for any given
(x,y)
,

\displaystyle\nabla_{\theta}\pi_{\theta}(y|x)=\nabla_{\theta}\frac{\exp(\theta^{\top}\psi(x,y))}{\sum_{y^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Y}}\exp(\theta^{\top}\psi(x,y^{\prime}))}

\displaystyle=\frac{\exp(\theta^{\top}\psi(x,y))\sum_{y^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Y}}\exp(\theta^{\top}\psi(x,y^{\prime}))}{(\sum_{y^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Y}}\exp(\theta^{\top}\psi(x,y^{\prime})))^{2}}\psi(x,y)-\frac{\exp(\theta^{\top}\psi(x,y))\sum_{y^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Y}}\exp(\theta^{\top}\psi(x,y^{\prime}))\psi(x,y^{\prime})}{(\sum_{y^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Y}}\exp(\theta^{\top}\psi(x,y^{\prime})))^{2}}\psi(x,y)

\displaystyle=\pi_{\theta}(y|x)\left(\psi(x,y)-\mathbb{E}_{y^{\prime}\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot|x)}[\psi(x,y^{\prime})]\right)~.

On the other hand, for the regularizer, we have

\displaystyle\nabla_{\theta}\log\left(\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y|x)}{\mu(y|x)}\right)

\displaystyle=\nabla_{\theta}\left(\theta^{\top}\psi(x,y)-\log\overline{Z}_{\theta}(x)-\log\mu(y|x)\right)

\displaystyle=\psi(x,y)-\frac{1}{\overline{Z}_{\theta}(x)}\sum_{y^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Y}}\exp(\theta^{\top}\psi(x,y))\psi(x,y)

\displaystyle=\psi(x,y)-\mathbb{E}_{y^{\prime}\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot|x)}[\psi(x,y^{\prime})]~,

where

\displaystyle\overline{Z}_{\theta}(x)=\sum_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}\exp(\theta^{\top}\psi(x,y))~.

Using the definition of
\mathcal{V}^{\pi_{\theta}}_{r}({\mathcal{D}_{n}})
and the above derivations, we have

\displaystyle\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{V}^{\pi_{\theta}}_{r}({\mathcal{D}_{n}})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{x\in{\mathcal{D}_{n}}}\nabla_{\theta}\left(\sum_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}\pi_{\theta}(y|x)\left(r(x,y)-\beta\log\left(\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y|x)}{\mu(y|x)}\right)\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{x\in{\mathcal{D}_{n}}}\sum_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}\pi_{\theta}(y|x)\left(r(x,y)-\beta\log\left(\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y|x)}{\mu(y|x)}\right)\right)\left(\psi(x,y)-\sum_{y^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Y}}\pi_{\theta}(y^{\prime}|x)\psi(x,y^{\prime})\right)

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{x\in{\mathcal{D}_{n}}}\sum_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}\pi_{\theta}(y|x)\left(\psi(x,y)-\sum_{y^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Y}}\pi_{\theta}(y^{\prime},x)\psi(x,y^{\prime})\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{x\in{\mathcal{D}_{n}}}\sum_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}\pi_{\theta}(y|x)\left(r(x,y)-\beta\log\left(\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y|x)}{\mu(y|x)}\right)\right)\left(\psi(x,y)-\sum_{y^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Y}}\pi_{\theta}(y^{\prime}|x)\psi(x,y^{\prime})\right)

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{x\in{\mathcal{D}_{n}}}\sum_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}\pi_{\theta}(y|x)\psi(x,y)+\frac{1}{n}\sum_{x\in{\mathcal{D}_{n}}}\sum_{y^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Y}}\pi_{\theta}(y^{\prime}|x)\psi(x,y^{\prime})\sum_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}\pi_{\theta}(y|x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{x\in{\mathcal{D}_{n}}}\sum_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}\pi_{\theta}(y|x)\left(r(x,y)-\beta\log\left(\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y|x)}{\mu(y|x)}\right)\right)\left(\psi(x,y)-\sum_{y^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Y}}\pi_{\theta}(y^{\prime}|x)\psi(x,y^{\prime})\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{x\in{\mathcal{D}_{n}}}\sum_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}\pi_{\theta}(y|x)\left(r(x,y)-\beta\log\left(\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y|x)}{\mu(y|x)}\right)\right)\overline{\psi}_{\theta}(x,y)~.

∎

Now we consider the following gradient update rule. Let
\Psi_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{P}\times nY}
denote the feature matrix corresponding to
{\mathcal{D}_{n}}
with columns
\psi(x,y)
, for every
(x,y)\in{\mathcal{D}_{n}}\times\mathcal{Y}
. We will assume that
\Psi_{n}
is full column rank. For every
t\geq 0
, let

\displaystyle\theta_{t+1}=\theta_{t}+\eta^{\prime}\left(\Psi_{n}\Psi_{n}^{\top}\right)^{\dagger}\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{V}^{\pi_{\theta}}_{r}({\mathcal{D}_{n}})~.

(11)

First, we will expand this gradient expression in the following lemma.

Lemma E.2
.

For every
t\geq 0
, the gradient update can be written as

\displaystyle\theta_{t+1}=\theta_{t}+\frac{\eta^{\prime}}{n}\left(\Psi_{n}\Psi_{n}^{\top}\right)^{\dagger}\Psi_{n}H(\pi_{\theta_{t}})\alpha_{t}~,

where
\mathbb{R}^{nY\times nY}\ni H(\pi)=diag(\pi)-M(\pi)
, for any policy
\pi
, with
M(\pi)
being a blog-diagonal matrix composed of
n
blocks
\pi(x)\pi(x)^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{Y\times Y}
, with
\pi(x)=[\pi(y|x)]_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}
, for every
x\in{\mathcal{D}_{n}}
, and

\displaystyle\alpha_{t}=\beta\Psi_{n}^{\top}\theta_{t}-r-\beta\log\mu-\frac{\left(\beta\Psi_{n}^{\top}\theta_{t}-r-\beta\log\mu\right)^{\top}\mathbf{1}}{Y}\cdot\mathbf{1}~.

Here,
r=[r(x,y)]_{(x,y)\in{\mathcal{D}_{n}}\times\mathcal{Y}}
denotes the reward vector, and
\log\mu=[\log\mu(y|x)]_{(x,y)\in{\mathcal{D}_{n}}\times\mathcal{Y}}
denotes the vector of log values for te reference policy.

Proof.

Using the gradient update and Lemma
E.1
, we have

\displaystyle\theta_{t+1}

\displaystyle=\theta_{t}+\eta^{\prime}\left(\Psi_{n}\Psi_{n}^{\top}\right)^{\dagger}\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{V}^{\pi_{\theta}}_{r}({\mathcal{D}_{n}})

\displaystyle=\theta_{t}+\eta^{\prime}\left(\Psi_{n}\Psi_{n}^{\top}\right)^{\dagger}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{x\in{\mathcal{D}_{n}}}\sum_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}\pi_{\theta_{t}}(y|x)\left(r(x,y)-\beta\log\left(\frac{\pi_{\theta_{t}}(y|x)}{\mu(y|x)}\right)\right)\overline{\psi}_{\theta_{t}}(x,y)

\displaystyle=\theta_{t}+\frac{\eta^{\prime}}{n}\left(\Psi_{n}\Psi_{n}^{\top}\right)^{\dagger}\sum_{x\in{\mathcal{D}_{n}}}\sum_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}\pi_{\theta_{t}}(y|x)\Big(r(x,y)-\beta\theta_{t}^{\top}\psi(x,y)+\beta\log\overline{Z}_{\theta_{t}}(x)+\beta\log\mu(y|x)\Big)\overline{\psi}_{\theta_{t}}(x,y)

\displaystyle=\theta_{t}+\frac{\eta^{\prime}}{n}\left(\Psi_{n}\Psi_{n}^{\top}\right)^{\dagger}\sum_{x\in{\mathcal{D}_{n}}}\sum_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}\pi_{\theta_{t}}(y|x)\Big(r(x,y)-\beta\theta_{t}^{\top}\psi(x,y)+\beta\log\mu(y|x)\Big)\overline{\psi}_{\theta_{t}}(x,y)

\displaystyle=\theta_{t}+\frac{\eta^{\prime}}{n}\left(\Psi_{n}\Psi_{n}^{\top}\right)^{\dagger}\Psi_{n}H(\pi_{\theta_{t}})\left(r-\beta\Psi_{n}^{\top}\theta_{t}+\beta\log\mu\right)

\displaystyle=\theta_{t}-\frac{\eta^{\prime}}{n}\left(\Psi_{n}\Psi_{n}^{\top}\right)^{\dagger}\Psi_{n}H(\pi_{\theta_{t}})\left(\beta\Psi_{n}^{\top}\theta_{t}-r-\beta\log\mu-\frac{\left(\beta\Psi_{n}^{\top}\theta_{t}-r-\beta\log\mu\right)^{\top}\mathbf{1}}{Y}\cdot\mathbf{1}\right)~,

where the fourth equality follows from the observation that

\displaystyle\beta\sum_{y}\pi_{\theta}(y|x)\log\overline{Z}_{\theta}(x)\overline{\psi}_{\theta}(x,y)=\beta\log\overline{Z}_{\theta}(x)\sum_{y}\pi_{\theta}(x,y)\left(\psi(x,y)-\mathbb{E}_{y^{\prime}\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot|x)}[\psi(x,y^{\prime})]\right)=0~,

while the last equality follows from the fact that
H(\pi_{\theta})c\mathbf{1}=\mathbf{0}
, for any constant
c
.
∎

Now we will express
\alpha_{t+1}
in a different way using the above derivation.

Lemma E.3
.

For every
t\geq 0
, we have

\displaystyle\alpha_{t+1}=\left(I-(\eta^{\prime}\beta/n)H(\pi_{\theta_{t}})\right)\alpha_{t}

Proof.

Observe that

\displaystyle\alpha_{t+1}=\beta\Psi_{n}^{\top}\theta_{t+1}-r-\beta\log\mu-\frac{\left(\beta\Psi_{n}^{\top}\theta_{t+1}-r-\beta\log\mu\right)^{\top}\mathbf{1}}{Y}\cdot\mathbf{1}

\displaystyle=\beta\Psi_{n}^{\top}\theta_{t}-r-\beta\log\mu-\frac{\left(\beta\Psi_{n}^{\top}\theta_{t}-r-\beta\log\mu\right)^{\top}\mathbf{1}}{Y}\cdot\mathbf{1}+\beta\Psi_{n}^{\top}(\theta_{t+1}-\theta_{t})-\frac{\beta(\Psi_{n}^{\top}(\theta_{t}-\theta_{t+1}))^{\top}\mathbf{1}}{Y}\cdot\mathbf{1}

\displaystyle=\beta\Psi_{n}^{\top}\theta_{t}-r-\beta\log\mu-\frac{\left(\beta\Psi_{n}^{\top}\theta_{t}-r-\beta\log\mu\right)^{\top}\mathbf{1}}{Y}\cdot\mathbf{1}-\frac{\beta(\Psi_{n}^{\top}(\theta_{t}-\theta_{t+1}))^{\top}\mathbf{1}}{Y}\cdot\mathbf{1}

\displaystyle\quad\quad-\beta\Psi_{n}^{\top}\left(\frac{\eta^{\prime}}{n}\left(\Psi_{n}\Psi_{n}^{\top}\right)^{\dagger}\Psi_{n}H(\pi_{\theta_{t}})\left(\beta\Psi_{n}^{\top}\theta_{t}-r-\beta\log\mu-\frac{\left(\beta\Psi_{n}^{\top}\theta_{t}-r-\beta\log\mu\right)^{\top}\mathbf{1}}{Y}\cdot\mathbf{1}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\left(I-(\beta\eta^{\prime}/n)\Psi_{n}^{\top}\left(\Psi_{n}\Psi_{n}^{\top}\right)^{\dagger}\Psi_{n}H(\pi_{\theta_{t}})\right)\left(\beta\Psi_{n}^{\top}\theta_{t}-r-\beta\log\mu-\frac{\left(\beta\Psi_{n}^{\top}\theta_{t}-r-\beta\log\mu\right)^{\top}\mathbf{1}}{Y}\cdot\mathbf{1}\right)

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad-\frac{\beta(\Psi_{n}^{\top}(\theta_{t}-\theta_{t+1}))^{\top}\mathbf{1}}{Y}\cdot\mathbf{1}

\displaystyle=\left(I-(\beta\eta^{\prime}/n)H(\pi_{\theta_{t}})\right)\left(\beta\Psi_{n}^{\top}\theta_{t}-r-\beta\log\mu-\frac{\left(\beta\Psi_{n}^{\top}\theta_{t}-r-\beta\log\mu\right)^{\top}\mathbf{1}}{Y}\cdot\mathbf{1}\right)

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad-\frac{\beta(\Psi_{n}^{\top}(\theta_{t}-\theta_{t+1}))^{\top}\mathbf{1}}{Y}\cdot\mathbf{1}~,

where the third equality uses Lemma
E.2
and the last equality follows from the fact that

\displaystyle\Psi_{n}^{\top}\left(\Psi_{n}\Psi_{n}^{\top}\right)^{\dagger}\Psi_{n}=\Psi_{n}^{\top}(\Psi_{n}^{\dagger})^{\top}\Psi_{n}^{\dagger}\Psi_{n}=(\Psi_{n}^{\dagger}\Psi_{n})^{\top}\Psi_{n}^{\dagger}\Psi_{n}=I~,

since we assume
\Psi_{n}
to be full column rank. For the last term in the derivation above, we have

\displaystyle\frac{\beta(\Psi_{n}^{\top}(\theta_{t}-\theta_{t+1}))^{\top}\mathbf{1}}{Y}\cdot\mathbf{1}

\displaystyle=\frac{\beta\eta^{\prime}}{nY}\left(\Psi_{n}^{\top}\left(\Psi_{n}\Psi_{n}^{\top}\right)^{\dagger}\Psi_{n}H(\pi_{\theta_{t}})\left(\beta\Psi_{n}^{\top}\theta_{t}-r-\beta\log\mu-\frac{\left(\beta\Psi_{n}^{\top}\theta_{t}-r-\beta\log\mu\right)^{\top}\mathbf{1}}{Y}\cdot\mathbf{1}\right)\right)^{\top}\mathbf{1}\cdot\mathbf{1}

\displaystyle=\frac{\beta\eta^{\prime}}{nY}\left(H(\pi_{\theta_{t}})\left(\beta\Psi_{n}^{\top}\theta_{t}-r-\beta\log\mu-\frac{\left(\beta\Psi_{n}^{\top}\theta_{t}-r-\beta\log\mu\right)^{\top}\mathbf{1}}{Y}\cdot\mathbf{1}\right)\right)^{\top}\mathbf{1}\cdot\mathbf{1}

\displaystyle=\frac{\beta\eta^{\prime}}{nY}\left(\beta\Psi_{n}^{\top}\theta_{t}-r-\beta\log\mu-\frac{\left(\beta\Psi_{n}^{\top}\theta_{t}-r-\beta\log\mu\right)^{\top}\mathbf{1}}{Y}\cdot\mathbf{1}\right)^{\top}H(\pi_{\theta_{t}})^{\top}\mathbf{1}\cdot\mathbf{1}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{0}~,

where we have used the fact that
H(\pi_{\theta})^{\top}\mathbf{1}=\mathbf{0}
. The result follows.
∎

Next, we will decompose the matrix
H(\pi_{\theta})
into simpler pieces and explore its structure.

Lemma E.4
.

The eigenvalues of
H(\pi_{\theta})
satisfy the following. The lowest eigenvalue is
\lambda_{1}=0
with multiplicity
n
with corresponding eigenvectors
e_{i}
, for each
i\in[n]
, where
e_{i}
are the vectors of ones in indices
Y(i-1)
to
Yi
and zeros everywhere else. Furthermore, we have that
\min_{x\in{\mathcal{D}_{n}},y\in\mathcal{Y}}\pi(y|x)\leq\lambda_{2}
and
\lambda_{\max}\leq\max_{x\in{\mathcal{D}_{n}},y\in\mathcal{Y}}\pi(y|x)
.

Proof.

Again, given
x\in{\mathcal{D}_{n}}
, let us denote by
\pi(x)
the vector
[\pi(y|x)]_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}
. Note that, for any policy
\pi
, we can write
H(\pi)
as

\displaystyle H(\pi)

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}diag(\pi(x_{1}))&0&\ldots&0\\
0&diag(\pi(x_{2}))&\ldots&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&\ldots&0&diag(\pi(x_{n}))\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle\quad\quad-\begin{bmatrix}\pi(x_{1})\pi(x_{1})^{\top}&0&\ldots&0\\
0&\pi(x_{2})\pi(x_{2})^{\top}&\ldots&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&\ldots&0&\pi(x_{n})\pi(x_{n})^{\top}\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}H(\pi(x_{1}))&0&\ldots&0\\
0&H(\pi(x_{2}))&\ldots&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&\ldots&0&H(\pi(x_{n}))\end{bmatrix}

Now, given
x\in{\mathcal{D}_{n}}
, Lemma 22 of
(
Mei et al., 2020
)
states that the spectrum of
H(\pi(x))
satisfies
\lambda_{1}=0
with corresponding eigenvector
\mathbf{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{Y}
, and

\displaystyle\pi(y_{i-1}|x)\leq\lambda_{i}\leq\pi(y_{i}|x)~,

for each
2\leq i\leq Y
, where
\lambda_{1}\leq\ldots\leq\lambda_{Y}
and
\pi(y_{1}|x)\leq\ldots\leq\pi(y_{Y}|x)
. Furthermore, it is known that the spectrum of a block diagonal matrix is composed of the eigenvalues of each block, counting multiplicities. Thus, we have that
0
is the lowest eigenvalue of
H(\pi)
occurring with multiplicity
n
. The rest follows.
∎

Lemma E.5
.

Let
v\in\mathbb{R}^{nY}
be any given vector. Then, we have that

\displaystyle\left\lVert\left(I-H(\pi)\right)\left(v-\frac{v^{\top}\mathbf{1}}{Y}\mathbf{1}\right)\right\rVert_{2}\leq\left(1-\min_{x\in{\mathcal{D}_{n}},y\in\mathcal{Y}}\pi(y|x)\right)\left\lVert v-\frac{v^{\top}\mathbf{1}}{Y}\mathbf{1}\right\rVert_{2}

Proof.

First, for every
i\in[n]
, let
e_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{nY}
denote a vector with entries
1
at the indices
Y(i-1)
to
Yi
and
0
everywhere else. Note that

\displaystyle\sum_{i\leq n}e_{i}=\mathbf{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{nY}~.

Next, let
v(j)
denote an
nY
-dimensional vector with entries
v_{k}
for each
Y(j-1)\leq k\leq Yj
.
Since
H(\pi)
is diagonalizable, as a symmetric matrix, any vector can be represented as a linear combination of its eigenvectors. Since
H(\pi)
is symmetric, this representation is unique. Now, by Lemma
E.4
, note that

\displaystyle v=\sum_{j\leq nY}a_{j}u_{j}=\sum_{j\leq n}a_{j}e_{j}+\sum^{nY}_{k=n+1}a_{k}u_{k}=\sum_{j\leq n}\frac{v(j)^{\top}\mathbf{1}_{Y}}{Y}e_{j}+\sum^{nY}_{k=n+1}a_{k}u_{k}=\frac{v^{\top}\mathbf{1}}{Y}\mathbf{1}+\sum^{nY}_{k=n+1}a_{k}u_{k}~,

where
(u_{i,j})_{i\leq n,j\leq Y}
is the eigenvector basis, with the first
n
eigenvectors being
e_{i}
, for
i\leq n
. Thus, we have that

\displaystyle v^{\prime}=v-\frac{v^{\top}\mathbf{1}}{Y}\mathbf{1}=\sum^{nY}_{k=n+1}a_{k}u_{k}~,

with
a_{n+1}>0
, and that

\displaystyle\left\lVert v^{\prime}\right\rVert_{2}=\sum^{nY}_{j=n+1}a_{j}^{2}~.

From the above, we obtain

\displaystyle\left(I-H(\pi)\right)v^{\prime}=\sum^{nY}_{j=n+1}a_{j}(1-\lambda_{j})u_{j}~,

and thus, by Lemma
E.4
,

\displaystyle\left\lVert\left(I-H(\pi)\right)v^{\prime}\right\rVert_{2}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\sum^{nY}_{j=n+1}a^{2}_{j}(1-\lambda_{j})^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{(1-\lambda_{n+1})\left(\sum^{nY}_{j=n+1}a^{2}_{j}\right)}

\displaystyle=(1-\lambda_{n+1})\left\lVert v^{\prime}\right\rVert_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\left(1-\min_{x\in{\mathcal{D}_{n}},y\in\mathcal{Y}}\pi(y|x)\right)\left\lVert v^{\prime}\right\rVert_{2}~.

∎

Lemma E.6
.

Suppose
\eta^{\prime}
and
\beta
are such that
\eta^{\prime}\beta/n\leq 1
. For the loglinear policy class, for every
t\geq 1
,

\displaystyle\left\lVert\alpha_{t}\right\rVert_{2}\leq\frac{2\left(\beta B+1\right)\sqrt{Y}}{\exp\left(\eta^{\prime}\beta\sum^{t-1}_{s=1}\min_{x\in{\mathcal{D}_{n}},y\in\mathcal{Y}}\pi_{\theta}(y|x)\right)}~.

Proof.

By Lemma
E.3
and Lemma
E.5
, for all
t\geq 1
,

\displaystyle\left\lVert\left(I-(\eta^{\prime}\beta/n)H(\pi_{\theta_{t+1}})\right)\alpha_{t+1}\right\rVert_{2}\leq\left(1-(\eta^{\prime}\beta/n)\min_{x\in{\mathcal{D}_{n}},y\in\mathcal{Y}}\pi_{\theta_{t}}(y|x)\right)\left\lVert\alpha_{t}\right\rVert_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\exp\left((\eta^{\prime}\beta/n)\min_{x\in{\mathcal{D}_{n}},y\in\mathcal{Y}}\pi_{\theta_{t}}(y|x)\right)}\left\lVert\alpha_{t}\right\rVert_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\exp\left((\eta^{\prime}\beta/n)\min_{x\in{\mathcal{D}_{n}},y\in\mathcal{Y}}\pi_{\theta_{t}}(y|x)\right)}\left(1-\min_{x\in{\mathcal{D}_{n}},y\in\mathcal{Y}}\pi_{\theta_{t-1}}(y|x)\right)\left\lVert\alpha_{t-1}\right\rVert_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\exp\left((\eta^{\prime}\beta/n)\sum^{t}_{s=t-1}\min_{x\in{\mathcal{D}_{n}},y\in\mathcal{Y}}\pi_{\theta_{s}}(y|x)\right)}\left\lVert\alpha_{t-1}\right\rVert_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\exp\left((\eta^{\prime}\beta/n)\sum^{t}_{s=1}\min_{x\in{\mathcal{D}_{n}},y\in\mathcal{Y}}\pi_{\theta_{s}}(y|x)\right)}\left\lVert\alpha_{1}\right\rVert_{2}~.

For the first iteration, observe that

\displaystyle\left\lVert\alpha_{1}\right\rVert_{2}

\displaystyle=\left\lVert\beta\Psi_{n}^{\top}\theta_{0}-r-\beta\log\mu-\frac{\left(\beta\Psi_{n}^{\top}\theta_{0}-r-\beta\log\mu\right)^{\top}\mathbf{1}}{Y}\mathbf{1}\right\rVert_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert\beta\Psi_{n}^{\top}\theta_{0}-r-\beta\log\mu\right\rVert_{2}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{Y}}\left\lVert\beta\Psi_{n}^{\top}\theta_{0}-r-\beta\log\mu\right\rVert_{2}\left\lVert\mathbf{1}\right\rVert_{2}

\displaystyle=2\left\lVert\beta\Psi_{n}^{\top}\theta_{0}-r-\beta\log\mu\right\rVert_{2}

\displaystyle\leq 2\left(\beta\left\lVert\Psi_{n}^{\top}\theta_{0}\right\rVert_{\infty}+1\right)\sqrt{Y}

\displaystyle\leq 2\left(\beta B+1\right)\sqrt{Y}~,

where the second inequality follows from the triangle inequality, the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality and the fact that rewards lie in the unit ball, while the last follows from the fact that the features lie in a unit subspace of
\mathbb{R}^{d_{P}}
, while
\left\lVert\theta_{0}\right\rVert_{\infty}\leq B
. The result follows.
∎

Lemma E.7
.

There exists a constant
C=C(\beta,Y,B)>0
, such that, for all
t\geq 1
, we have
\min_{x\in{\mathcal{D}_{n}},y\in\mathcal{Y}}\pi_{\theta_{t}}(y|x)\geq C
.

Proof.

First, by Lemma
E.5
, note that, for any
t\geq 1
,

\displaystyle\left\lVert\alpha_{t+1}\right\rVert_{2}\leq\left(1-(\eta^{\prime}\beta/n)\min_{x\in{\mathcal{D}_{n}},y\in\mathcal{Y}}\pi_{\theta_{t}}(y|x)\right)\left\lVert\alpha_{t}\right\rVert_{2}\leq\left\lVert\alpha_{t}\right\rVert_{2}\leq\ldots\leq\left\lVert\alpha_{1}\right\rVert_{2}\leq 2\left(\beta B+1\right)\sqrt{Y}~,

where the second inequality follows from the fact that policies are probability distributions, and the last inequality follows from Lemma
E.6
. Next, observe that, for any
(x,y)\in{\mathcal{D}_{n}}\times\mathcal{Y}
,

\displaystyle\Bigg|\psi(x,y)^{\top}\theta_{t}-\frac{1}{\beta}r(x,y)

\displaystyle-\log\mu(y|x)-\frac{\left(\Psi_{n}^{\top}\theta_{t}-r/\beta-\log\mu\right)^{\top}\mathbf{1}}{Y}\Bigg|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\beta}\left|\beta\psi(x,y)^{\top}\theta_{t}-r(x,y)-\beta\log\mu(y|x)-\frac{\beta\left(\Psi_{n}^{\top}\theta_{t}-r-\beta\log\mu\right)^{\top}\mathbf{1}}{Y}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\beta}\left\lVert\beta\Psi_{n}^{\top}\theta_{t}-r-\beta\log\mu-\frac{\left(\beta\Psi_{n}^{\top}\theta_{t}-r-\beta\log\mu\right)^{\top}\mathbf{1}}{Y}\mathbf{1}\right\rVert_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\beta}\left\lVert\alpha_{t}\right\rVert_{2}

\displaystyle\leq 2(B+1/\beta)\sqrt{Y}~.

Now, define
(x_{1},y_{1})=\arg\min_{x\in{\mathcal{D}_{n}},y\in\mathcal{Y}}\psi(x,y)^{\top}\theta_{t}
and
(x_{2},y_{2})=\arg\max_{x\in{\mathcal{D}_{n}},y\in\mathcal{Y}}\psi(x,y)^{\top}\theta_{t}
. By the above, we have

\displaystyle\Psi_{n}(x_{1},y_{1})^{\top}\theta_{t}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{\beta}r(x_{1},y_{1})+\log\mu(y_{1}|x_{1})+\frac{\left(\Psi_{n}^{\top}\theta_{t}-r/\beta-\log\mu\right)^{\top}\mathbf{1}}{Y}-2(B+1/\beta)\sqrt{Y}~,

\displaystyle-\Psi_{n}(x_{2},y_{2})^{\top}\theta_{t}

\displaystyle\geq-\frac{1}{\beta}r(x_{2},y_{2})-\log\mu(y_{2}|x_{2})-\frac{\left(\Psi_{n}^{\top}\theta_{t}-r/\beta-\log\mu\right)^{\top}\mathbf{1}}{Y}-2(B+1/\beta)\sqrt{Y}~,

which imply

\displaystyle\min_{x\in{\mathcal{D}_{n}},y\in\mathcal{Y}}\pi_{\theta_{t}}(y|x)

\displaystyle\geq\min_{x\in{\mathcal{D}_{n}},y\in\mathcal{Y}}\frac{\exp(\psi(x,y)^{\top}\theta_{t})}{\sum_{y^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Y}}\exp(\psi(x,y)\top\theta_{t})}\geq\frac{1}{Y}\exp\left(\left(\Psi_{n}(x_{1},y_{1})-\Psi_{n}(x_{2},y_{2})\right)^{\top}\theta_{t}\right)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{Y}\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}\left(r(x_{1},y_{1})-r(x_{2},y_{2})\right)+\log\frac{\mu(y_{1}|x_{1})}{\mu(y_{2}|x_{2})}-4\left(B+1/\beta\right)\sqrt{Y}\right)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{Y}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{\beta}-4\left(B+1/\beta\right)\sqrt{Y}\right)=C~.

∎

Let us denote by
softmax(\Psi_{n}^{\top}v)
the policy
\exp(\psi(x,y)^{\top}v)/\sum_{y^{\prime}}\exp(\psi(x,y^{\prime})^{\top}v)
, for any parameter vector
v
and pair
(x,y)\in\mathcal{D}_{n}\times\mathcal{Y}
. Now, we are ready to prove the main result of this section.

Theorem E.8
.

Let
\pi_{\theta_{t}}=\text{softmax}(\Psi^{\top}_{n}\theta_{t})
. Using update rule (
11
) with
\eta^{\prime}\leq n/\beta
, for all
t\geq 1
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}^{\pi_{\theta^{*}}}_{r}(\mathcal{D}_{n})-\mathcal{V}^{\pi_{\theta_{t}}}_{r}(\mathcal{D}_{n})\leq\frac{2\sqrt{Y}(B+1/\beta)}{\exp((\beta\eta^{\prime}/n)\cdot C\cdot(t-1))}~,

where

\displaystyle C=\frac{1}{Y}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{\beta}-4\left(B+1/\beta\right)\sqrt{Y}\right)~.

Proof.

Observe that, since
\pi_{\theta^{*}}\propto\mu(y|x)\exp(r(x,y)/\beta)
, we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}^{\pi_{\theta^{*}}}_{r}(\mathcal{D}_{n})-\mathcal{V}^{\pi_{\theta_{t}}}_{r}(\mathcal{D}_{n})=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{x\in{\mathcal{D}_{n}}}\sum_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}\left(\pi_{\theta^{*}_{n}}(y|x)r(x,y)-\beta D_{\textnormal{KL}}(\pi_{\theta^{*}}||\mu)-\pi_{\theta_{t}}(y|x)r(x,y)+\beta D_{\textnormal{KL}}(\pi_{\theta_{t}}||\mu)\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{n}\sum_{x\in\mathcal{D}_{n}}\left\lVert\pi_{\theta^{*}}(\cdot|x)-\pi_{\theta_{t}}(\cdot|x)\right\rVert_{1}

\displaystyle\quad\quad+\frac{1}{n}\sum_{x\in\mathcal{D}_{n}}-\beta D_{\textnormal{KL}}(\pi_{\theta^{*}}||\pi_{\theta^{*}})+\pi_{\theta^{*}}(y|x)r(x,y)+\beta D_{\textnormal{KL}}(\pi_{\theta_{t}}||\pi_{\theta^{*}})-\pi_{\theta_{t}}(y|x)r(x,y)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2}{n}\sum_{x\in\mathcal{D}_{n}}\left\lVert\pi_{\theta^{*}}(\cdot|x)-\pi_{\theta_{t}}(\cdot|x)\right\rVert_{1}+\beta D_{\textnormal{KL}}(\pi_{\theta_{t}}||\pi_{\theta^{*}})

\displaystyle\leq(2Y+\beta)D_{\textnormal{KL}}(\pi_{\theta_{t}}||\pi_{\theta^{*}})

\displaystyle\leq(2Y+\beta)\left\lVert\Psi_{n}^{\top}\theta^{*}-\Psi_{n}\theta_{t}+\frac{(\Psi_{n}^{\top}(\theta_{t}-\theta^{*}))^{\top}\mathbf{1}}{Y}\mathbf{1}\right\rVert^{2}_{\infty}~,

where the third inequality uses Pinsker’s inequality and the last one follows from Lemma
J.8
.
Now, note that the optimal softmax policy parameter
\theta^{*}
satisfies, for each
(x,y)\in\mathcal{D}_{n}
,

\displaystyle\psi(x,y)^{\top}\theta^{*}=\frac{1}{\beta}\left(r(x,y)+\log\mu(y|x)\right)~,

by setting the gradient at
(x,y)
to
0
. Its existence is guaranteed by the assumption that
r^{*}\in\mathcal{F}
and Lemma
J.1
. Thus, we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}^{\pi_{\theta^{*}}}_{r}(\mathcal{D}_{n})-\mathcal{V}^{\pi_{\theta_{t}}}_{r}(\mathcal{D}_{n})

\displaystyle\leq(2Y+\beta)\left\lVert\Psi_{n}^{\top}\theta^{*}-\Psi_{n}\theta_{t}+\frac{(\Psi_{n}^{\top}(\theta_{t}-\theta^{*}))^{\top}\mathbf{1}}{Y}\mathbf{1}\right\rVert^{2}_{\infty}

\displaystyle=(2Y+\beta)\left\lVert\frac{1}{\beta}\left(r+\log\mu\right)-\Psi_{n}^{\top}\theta_{t}+\frac{(\beta\Psi^{\top}\theta_{t}-r-\beta\log\mu)^{\top}\mathbf{1}}{\beta Y}\mathbf{1}\right\rVert^{2}_{\infty}

\displaystyle=\frac{(2Y+\beta)}{\beta}\left\lVert\beta\Psi_{n}^{\top}\theta_{t}-r-\beta\log\mu-\frac{(\beta\Psi^{\top}\theta_{t}-r-\beta\log\mu)^{\top}\mathbf{1}}{Y}\mathbf{1}\right\rVert^{2}_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2}{\beta}\frac{(2Y+\beta)2\sqrt{Y}(\beta B+1)}{\exp((\eta^{\prime}\beta/n)C(t-1))}~,

where the last inequality follows from Lemma
E.6
and Lemma
E.7
.
∎

Appendix F
Convergence of Gradient Descent for DPO (Section
5
)

In this section, we will prove convergence bounds for the projected gradient descent procedure for DPO. Recall that the projected gradient descent is defined as

\displaystyle\theta_{t+1}=\underset{\theta:\left\lVert\theta\right\rVert_{2}\leq B}{\proj}\left(\theta_{t}-\eta^{\prime\prime}\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{L}^{\theta}_{\textnormal{DPO}}(\mathcal{D}_{n})\right)~,

We begin by showing that the DPO objective satisfies the PL condition
(
Karimi et al., 2016
)
stated in Definition
D.2
.
We will show that the DPO objective satisfies this condition for the loglinear parametrization. First, we need to show that such an objective has Lipschitz gradients, which holds under the assumption that the parameter vectors
\theta
have a length of no more than
B
.

Lemma F.1
.

The DPO objective
\mathcal{L}^{\theta}_{\textnormal{DPO}}(\mathcal{D}_{n})
is Lipschitz continuous with parameter
L^{\prime}_{1}=\beta\exp(2\beta(B+J))
and has Lipschitz gradients with parameter
L^{\prime}_{2}=\beta^{2}\exp\left(2\beta(B+J)\right)
, where

J=\max_{(x,y^{w},y^{l})\in{\mathcal{D}_{n}}}\beta\left|\log\frac{\mu(y^{w}|x)}{\mu(y^{l}|x)}\right|~.

Proof.

Note that, in order to show
L
-Lipschitzness, it suffices to prove that the Hessian of
\mathcal{L}^{\theta}_{\textnormal{DPO}}(\mathcal{D}_{n})
has bounded eigenvalues. Let us first compute the Hessian. Before doing that, we first simplify the gradient expression, when instantiated for the softmax parametrization. First, given parameter vector
\theta
, corresponding to
\pi_{\theta}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{\theta}_{\textnormal{DPO}}(\mathcal{D}_{n})=-\mathbb{E}_{(y^{w},y^{l},x)\sim{\mathcal{D}_{n}}}\left[\log\sigma\left(\beta\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y^{w}|x)}{\mu(y^{w}|x)}-\beta\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y^{l}|x)}{\mu(y^{l}|x)}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=-\mathbb{E}_{(y^{w},y^{l},x)\sim{\mathcal{D}_{n}}}\left[\log\sigma\left(\beta\log\frac{\exp(\theta^{\top}\psi(x,y^{w}))}{\sum_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}\exp(\theta^{\top}\psi(x,y))}-\beta\log\frac{\exp(\theta^{\top}\psi(x,y^{l}))}{\sum_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}\exp(\theta^{\top}\psi(x,y))}-\beta\log\frac{\mu(y^{w}|x)}{\mu(y^{l}|x)}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=-\mathbb{E}_{(y^{w},y^{l},x)\sim{\mathcal{D}_{n}}}\left[\log\sigma\left(\beta\theta^{\top}(\psi(x,y^{w})-\psi(x,y^{l}))-\beta\log\frac{\mu(y^{w}|x)}{\mu(y^{l}|x)}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{(y^{w},y^{l},x)\sim{\mathcal{D}_{n}}}\left[\log\left(1+\exp\left(\beta\theta^{\top}\left(\psi(x,y^{w})-\psi(x,y^{l})\right)-J(x,y^{w},y^{l})\right)\right)\right]~,

where we let

J(x,y^{w},y^{l})=\beta\log\frac{\mu(y^{w}|x)}{\mu(y^{l}|x)}~.

Based on the above, we have

\displaystyle\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{L}^{\theta}_{\textnormal{DPO}}(\mathcal{D}_{n})=\nabla_{\theta}\mathbb{E}_{(y^{w},y^{l},x)\sim{\mathcal{D}_{n}}}\left[\log\left(1+\exp\left(\beta\theta^{\top}\left(\psi(x,y^{w})-\psi(x,y^{l})\right)-J(x,y^{w},y^{l})\right)\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{(x,y^{w},y^{l})\in{\mathcal{D}_{n}}}\frac{\beta\exp\left(\beta\theta^{\top}(\psi(x,y^{w})-\psi(x,y^{l}))-J(x,y^{w},y^{l})\right)}{\left(1+\exp\left(\beta\theta^{\top}(\psi(x,y^{w})-\psi(x,y^{l}))-J(x,y^{w},y^{l})\right)\right)}\left(\psi(x,y^{w})-\psi(x,y^{l})\right)~,

and

\displaystyle\nabla^{2}_{\theta}\mathcal{L}^{\theta}_{\textnormal{DPO}}(\mathcal{D}_{n})=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{(x,y^{w},y^{l})\in{\mathcal{D}_{n}}}

\displaystyle\quad\quad\nabla_{\theta}\frac{\beta\exp\left(\beta\theta^{\top}(\psi(x,y^{w})-\psi(x,y^{l}))-J(x,y^{w},y^{l})\right)}{\left(1+\exp\left(\beta\theta^{\top}(\psi(x,y^{w})-\psi(x,y^{l}))-J(x,y^{w},y^{l})\right)\right)}\left(\psi(x,y^{w})-\psi(x,y^{l})\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{(x,y^{w},y^{l})\in{\mathcal{D}_{n}}}

\displaystyle\frac{\beta^{2}\exp\left(\beta\theta^{\top}(\psi(x,y^{w})-\psi(x,y^{l}))-J(x,y^{w},y^{l})\right)}{\left(1+\exp\left(\beta\theta^{\top}(\psi(x,y^{w})-\psi(x,y^{l}))-J(x,y^{w},y^{l})\right)\right)^{2}}\left(\psi(x,y^{w})-\psi(x,y^{l})\right)\left(\psi(x,y^{w})-\psi(x,y^{l})\right)^{\top}~.

Now, define

\displaystyle E(\theta,x,y)=\exp\left(\beta\theta^{\top}(\psi(x,y^{w})-\psi(x,y^{l}))-J(x,y^{w},y^{l})\right)~.

Note that we have

\displaystyle\left\lVert\nabla\mathcal{L}^{\theta}_{\textnormal{DPO}}(\mathcal{D}_{n})\right\rVert_{2}\leq\beta\exp(2\beta(B+J))\left\lVert\psi(x,y^{w})-\psi(x,y^{l})\right\rVert_{2}\leq\beta\exp(2\beta(B+J))~,

and

\displaystyle\nabla^{2}_{\theta}\mathcal{L}^{\theta}_{\textnormal{DPO}}(\mathcal{D}_{n})

\displaystyle=\beta^{2}\sum_{(x,y^{w},y^{l})\in{\mathcal{D}_{n}}}\frac{E(\theta,x,y)}{n\left(1+E(\theta,x,y)\right)^{2}}\psi(x)\psi(x)^{\top}

\displaystyle\preceq\frac{\beta^{2}\exp\left(2\beta(B+J)\right)}{n}\sum_{(x,y^{w},y^{l})\in{\mathcal{D}_{n}}}\psi(x)\psi(x)^{\top}

\displaystyle\preceq\beta^{2}\exp\left(2\beta(B+J)\right)I_{d}~,

where the last inequality follows from the fact that the feature norms are bounded by
1
, and thus the maximum eigenvalue of the sample covariance matrix is no more than
1
.
∎

Next, we show that the DPO objective satisfies the PL condition under some mild assumption on the data.

Lemma F.2
.

Assume that, for each triple
(x,y^{w},y^{l})\in{\mathcal{D}_{n}}
, we have that
\psi(x,y^{w})\neq\psi(x,y^{l})
.
Then, if we let

\displaystyle C^{\prime}_{PL}=\frac{\beta\exp(-2\beta(B+J))^{3}\left(1+\exp(-2\beta(B+J))\right)}{n\left(1+\exp(2\beta(B+J))\right)^{2}}\min_{(x,y^{w},y^{l})\in{\mathcal{D}_{n}}}\left\lVert\psi(x,y^{w})-\psi(x,y^{l})\right\rVert^{2}~,

we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert\nabla\mathcal{L}^{\theta}_{\textnormal{DPO}}(\mathcal{D}_{n})\right\rVert^{2}\geq C^{\prime}_{PL}\left(\mathcal{L}^{\theta}_{\textnormal{DPO}}(\mathcal{D}_{n})-\mathcal{L}^{*}_{\textnormal{DPO}}(\mathcal{D}_{n})\right)

where
\mathcal{L}^{*}_{\textnormal{DPO}}(\mathcal{D}_{n})=\min_{\theta}\mathcal{L}^{\theta}_{\textnormal{DPO}}(\mathcal{D}_{n})
denotes the optimal loss value.

Proof.

Using the notation
E(\theta,x,y)=\exp\left(\beta\theta^{\top}(\psi(x,y^{w})-\psi(x,y^{l}))-E(x)\right)
, and noting that every quantity in the expression below is non-negative, we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{L}^{\theta}_{\textnormal{DPO}}(\mathcal{D}_{n})\right\rVert^{2}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\beta^{2}}{2n^{2}}\sum_{(x,y)\in{\mathcal{D}_{n}}}\frac{E(\theta,x,y)^{2}}{\left(1+E(\theta,x,y)\right)^{2}}\left\lVert\psi(x,y^{w})-\psi(x,y^{l})\right\rVert^{2}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\beta^{2}\exp(-2\beta(B+J))^{2}}{n\left(1+\exp(2\beta(B+J))\right)^{2}}\min_{(x,y)\in{\mathcal{D}_{n}}}\left\lVert\psi(x,y^{w})-\psi(x,y^{l})\right\rVert^{2}

since
-J\leq E(x)\leq J
, and thus,
\exp(-2\beta(B+J))\leq E(\theta,x,y)\leq\exp(2\beta(B+J))
. On the other hand, observe that

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{\theta}_{\textnormal{DPO}}(\mathcal{D}_{n})-\mathcal{L}^{*}_{\textnormal{DPO}}(\mathcal{D}_{n})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{(x,y)\sim{\mathcal{D}_{n}}}\Big[\log\left(1+E\left(\theta,x,y\right)\right)-\log\left(1+E(\theta^{*},x,y)\right)\Big]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum^{n}_{i=1}\log\frac{1+E(\theta,x_{i},y_{i})}{1+E(\theta^{*},x_{i},y_{i})}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{n}\sum^{n}_{i=1}\left(\frac{1+E(\theta,x_{i},y_{i})}{1+E(\theta^{*},x_{i},y_{i})}-1\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum^{n}_{i=1}\frac{E(\theta,x_{i},y_{i})-E(\theta^{*},x_{i},y_{i})}{1+E(\theta^{*},x_{i},y_{i})}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{n}\sum^{n}_{i=1}\frac{E(\theta,x_{i},y_{i})}{1+E(\theta^{*},x_{i},y_{i})}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\exp(2\beta(B+J))}{1+\exp(-2\beta(B+J))}~.

Now, the assumption on the data implies that, there exists
\xi
such that

\displaystyle 0<\xi^{\prime}=\min_{(x,y^{w},y^{l})\in{\mathcal{D}_{n}}}\left\lVert\psi(x,y^{w})-\psi(x,y^{l})\right\rVert^{2}~.

Using
\xi^{\prime}
and solving the equation

\displaystyle C^{\prime}_{PL}\cdot\frac{\exp(2\beta(B+J))}{1+\exp(-2\beta(B+J))}=\frac{\beta\exp\left(-2\beta(B+J)\right)^{2}}{n\left(1+\exp\left(2\beta(B+J)\right)\right)^{2}}\xi^{\prime}

for

, we obtain

\displaystyle C^{\prime}_{PL}=\frac{\beta\exp(-2\beta(B+J))^{3}\left(1+\exp(-2\beta(B+J))\right)}{n\left(1+\exp(2\beta(B+J))\right)^{2}}\xi^{\prime}~.

(12)

∎

Appendix H
The DPO Extension to MDPs (Section
7
)

First, we start with MDP setting preliminaries.

H.1
Deterministic Markov Decision Processes

An infinite-horizon discounted deterministic Markov decision process (MDP) is a mathematical object
\mathcal{M}=\left(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y},T,r^{*},\gamma,\rho\right)
, where
\mathcal{X}
denotes the state space,
\mathcal{Y}
denotes the action space, both of which are assumed to be finite with cardinalities
X
and
Y
, respectively.
T:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}\rightarrow\mathcal{X}
denotes the deterministic transition function, where
T(x,y)
denotes the next state after taking action
y
in state
x
. The reward function is denoted by
r^{*}:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}\rightarrow[0,1]
. Finally,
\gamma\in[0,1)
denotes the discount factor, while
\rho\in\Delta(\mathcal{X})
denotes the initial state distribution.

Policies
\pi
are mappings from states to distributions over actions, that is,
\pi:\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\Delta(\mathcal{Y})
.
Given policy
\pi
, the state occupancy measure of state
x
with respect to initial state
x_{0}
is given as

\displaystyle d^{\pi}_{x_{0}}(x)=(1-\gamma)\sum_{t\geq 0}\gamma^{t}\mathbb{P}\left(x_{t}=x|x_{0},\pi\right)~,

while the state-action occupancy measure is given as
d^{\pi}_{x_{0}}(x,y)=d^{\pi}_{x_{0}}(x)\pi(y|x)
. We also write
d^{\pi}_{\rho}(x,y)=\mathbb{E}_{x_{0}\sim\rho}[d_{x_{0}}(x,y)]
.
Furthermore, given policy
\pi
and an arbitrary reward function
r
, the value function of policy
\pi
with respect to reward
r
is defined as

\displaystyle V^{\pi}_{r}(x)=\mathbb{E}\left[\sum^{\infty}_{t=0}\gamma^{t}r(x_{t},y_{t})\Big|x_{0}=x,\pi\right]~,

and the action-value function is defined as

\displaystyle Q^{\pi}_{r}(x,y)=\mathbb{E}\left[\sum^{\infty}_{t=0}\gamma^{t}r(x_{t},y_{t})\Big|x_{0}=x,y_{0}=y,\pi\right]~,

for every state-action pair
(x,y)
. We denote by
V^{\pi}_{r}(\rho)=\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\rho}[V^{\pi}_{r}(x)]
the expected value function over the initial distribution.

H.2
DPO for MDPs

A direct extension of DPO to the MDP setting is not straightforward. To understand this, it is enough to see that the optimal policy-to-reward mapping in this case is not linear. Fix a reward function
r
. The gradient of the KL-regularized objective with respect to
r
is given as

\displaystyle\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{V}^{\theta}_{r}(\rho)=\frac{1}{1-\gamma}\sum_{x}d^{\pi_{\theta}}_{\rho}(x)\sum_{y}\pi_{\theta}(y|x)\left(r(x,y)+\gamma\mathcal{V}^{\pi_{\theta}}(T(x,y))-\beta\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y|x)}{\mu(y|x)}\right)\overline{\psi}_{\theta}(x,y)~,

where
\overline{\psi}_{\theta}(x,y)=\psi(x,y)-\mathbb{E}_{y^{\prime}\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot|x)}[\psi(x,y^{\prime})]
. See Appendix
I
for derivations. What complicates things is the occupancy measure
d^{\pi}_{\rho}
, which is non-linearly dependent on policy
\pi
, and the gradient of the occupancy measure. To allow for the change of variables to carry through in this case, we utilize the dual formulation of Problem (
P3.2
):

\displaystyle\max_{d_{\rho}}\;\;

\displaystyle\sum_{x,y}d_{\rho}(x,y)r(x,y)-\beta\sum_{x,y}d_{\rho}(x,y)\log\frac{d_{\rho}(x,y)}{d^{\mu}_{\rho}(x,y)}

(P3.2’)

s.t.

\displaystyle\sum_{y}d_{\rho}(x,y)=(1-\gamma)\rho(x)+\gamma\sum_{x^{\prime},y^{\prime}}\mathds{1}\left(x=T\left(x^{\prime},y^{\prime}\right)\right)d_{\rho}(x^{\prime},y^{\prime}),\forall x\in\mathcal{X}~,

where we have used that

\displaystyle V^{\pi}_{r}(\rho)=\sum_{x,y}d^{\pi}_{\rho}(x,y)r(x,y)~,

and also taken the KL-divergence of the occupancy measures, instead of the actual policies.
This is a convex program and thus any stationary points are optimal. The Lagrangian of the above problem can be written as

\displaystyle L(d_{\rho},\alpha)

\displaystyle=\sum_{x,y}d_{\rho}(x,y)\left(r^{*}(x,y)-\beta\log\frac{d_{\rho}(x,y)}{d^{\mu}_{\rho}(x,y)}\right)

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{x}\alpha(x)\left(\sum_{y}d_{\rho}(x,y)-(1-\gamma)\rho(x)-\gamma\sum_{x^{\prime},y^{\prime}}\mathds{1}(x=T(x^{\prime},y^{\prime}))d_{\rho}(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\right)

\displaystyle=-\beta\sum_{x,y}d_{\rho}(x,y)\log\frac{d_{\rho}(x,y)}{d^{\mu}_{\rho}(x,y)}-(1-\gamma)\sum_{x}\rho(x)\alpha(x)

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{x,y}d_{\rho}(x,y)\left(\underbrace{r^{*}(x,y)-\gamma\sum_{x^{\prime}}\mathds{1}(x=T(x^{\prime},y^{\prime}))\alpha(x^{\prime})+\alpha(x)}_{e_{\alpha}(x,y)}\right)~,

Then, given
(x,y)
, the gradient of the Lagrangian with respect to
d_{\rho}(x,y)
is

\displaystyle\nabla_{d_{\rho}(x,y)}L(d_{\rho},\alpha)=-\beta\left(\log\frac{d_{\rho}(x,y)}{d^{\mu}_{\rho}(x,y)}-\mathbf{1}\right)+e_{\alpha}(x,y)~,

which, when set to zero, yields

\displaystyle d_{\rho}(x,y)=d^{\mu}_{\rho}(x,y)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}e_{\alpha}(x,y)\right)\exp(-1)~.

Primal feasibility implies that
\sum_{x,y}d_{\rho}(x,y)=1
, thus, our choice of
\alpha
should satisfy such condition. Letting
Z=\exp(1)
, and
\alpha^{*}
be the optimal Lagrange multiplier, we have

\displaystyle d^{*}_{\rho}(x,y)=\frac{1}{Z}d^{\mu}_{\rho}(x,y)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}e_{\alpha^{*}}(x,y)\right)~.

(13)

Writing the expression for the reward function, we get

\displaystyle r^{*}(x,y)=\beta\log\frac{d^{*}_{\rho}(x,y)}{d^{\mu}_{\rho}(x,y)}+\beta+\gamma\sum_{x^{\prime}}\mathds{1}(x=T(x^{\prime},y^{\prime}))\alpha^{*}(x^{\prime})-\alpha^{*}(x)~.

Now, observe that, given a trajectory
\tau=(x_{0},y_{0},x_{1},\ldots)
, we can write the discounted return using the above expression and obtain

\displaystyle\sum^{\infty}_{t=0}\gamma^{t}r^{*}(x_{t},y_{t})

\displaystyle=\sum^{\infty}_{t=0}\gamma^{t}\left(\beta\log\frac{d^{*}_{\rho}(x_{t},y_{t})}{d^{\mu}_{\rho}(x_{t},y_{t})}+\beta+\gamma\sum_{x}\mathds{1}(x=T(x^{\prime},y^{\prime}))\alpha^{*}(x)-\alpha^{*}(x_{t})\right)

\displaystyle=\sum^{\infty}_{t=0}\gamma^{t}\left(\beta\log\frac{d^{*}_{\rho}(x_{t},y_{t})}{d^{\mu}_{\rho}(x_{t},y_{t})}+\beta+\gamma\alpha^{*}(x_{t+1})-\alpha^{*}(x_{t})\right)

(14)

\displaystyle=\sum^{\infty}_{t=0}\gamma^{t}\left(\beta\log\frac{d^{*}_{\rho}(x_{t},y_{t})}{d^{\mu}_{\rho}(x_{t},y_{t})}+\beta-\alpha^{*}(x_{0})\right)~,

(15)

where for Equation (
14
) we have used the fact that the transitions are deterministic, and for Equation (
15
) note that the terms
\alpha^{*}(x)
cancel each other out.

Now, let us get back to the BT preference model for MDPs. Given a dataset
\mathcal{D}_{n}
of pairs of trajectories, each pair of which starts from the same initial state, we can express the MLE loss directly in terms of the occupancy measures using the above derivation as follows:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\textnormal{DPO}}(d_{\rho})=-\mathbb{E}_{(\tau^{w},\tau^{l})\sim\mathcal{D}_{n}}\left[\log\sigma\left(\sum^{\infty}_{t=0}\gamma^{t}\beta\log\frac{d_{\rho}(x^{w}_{t},y^{w}_{t})}{d^{\mu}_{\rho}(x^{w}_{t},y^{w}_{t})}-\sum^{\infty}_{t=0}\gamma^{t}\beta\log\frac{d_{\rho}(x^{l}_{t},y^{l}_{t})}{d^{\mu}_{\rho}(x^{l}_{t},y^{l}_{t})}\right)\right]~,

where we have used the fact that the terms
\beta
and
\alpha^{*}(x_{0})
cancel out.

Now, note that the minimizer to the above loss may not satisfy the Bellman flow constraints of Problem (
P3.2’
). Thus, we need to restrict the domain of the problem to the following set

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}=\bigg\{d\in\Delta(\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}):\sum_{y}d(x,y)=(1-\gamma)\rho(x)+\gamma\sum_{x^{\prime},y^{\prime}}\mathds{1}\left(x=T(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\right)d(x^{\prime},y^{\prime}),\forall x\in\mathcal{X}\bigg\}

H.3
DPO for MDPs with Loglinear Occupancy Measures

Similar to the contextual bandit setting, we want to write the DPO loss such that it resembles logistic regression. For loglinear occupancy measures, as defined in Definition
7.1
, with parameter set restricted to

\displaystyle\Theta^{\prime}:=\{\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{M}}:d^{\pi_{\theta}}_{\rho}\in\mathcal{B}\}~,

we can write the loss so that it resembles logistic regression.
Note that the domain of
\theta
is restricted only to those parameters which imply that
d^{\pi_{\theta}}_{\rho}
is an occupancy measure with respect to the underlying MDP.
For this case, the DPO loss becomes

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{D}_{n}}(\theta)=-\mathbb{E}_{(\tau^{w},\tau^{l})\sim\mathcal{D}_{n}}\Bigg[\log\sigma\left(\beta\theta^{\top}\left(\sum^{\infty}_{t=0}\gamma^{t}\left(\psi(x^{w}_{t},y^{w}_{t})-\psi(x^{l}_{t},y^{l}_{t})\right)\right)+K(\tau^{w},\tau^{l})\right)\Bigg]

where

\displaystyle K(\tau^{w},\tau^{l})=\sum^{\infty}_{t=0}\gamma^{t}\log\frac{d^{\mu}_{\rho}(x^{l}_{t},y^{l}_{t})}{d^{\mu}_{\rho}(x^{w}_{t},y^{w}_{t})}~.

Given the learned occupancy measure
d^{\pi_{\theta}}_{\rho}
, one can finally compute an optimal policy, for each state-action pair, as

\displaystyle\pi_{\theta}(y|x)=\frac{d^{\pi_{\theta}}_{\rho}(x,y)}{\sum_{y}d^{\pi_{\theta}}_{\rho}(x,y)}~.

Note that, in general, the quantity
K(\tau^{w},\tau^{l})
is not easy to compute as it requires access to the occupancy measure with respect to
\mu
. However, in practice,
K(\tau^{w},\tau^{l})
can be treated as a hyperparameter of the problem and tuned accordingly.

Appendix I
Gradient Expression for KL-regularized Objective in MDPs

In this section, we derive the gradient for the loglinear policy class. We rewrite the problem below for convenience.

\displaystyle\max_{\theta}\;

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\rho}\left[\sum^{\infty}_{t=0}\gamma^{t}\left(r(x_{t},y_{t})-\beta D_{\textnormal{KL}}\left(\pi_{\theta}(\cdot|x_{t})||\mu(\cdot|x_{t})\right)\right)\Big|y_{t}\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot|x_{t})\right]

Lemma I.1
.

Let

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}^{\pi_{\theta}}_{r}(x)=\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\rho,y_{t}\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot|x_{t})}\left[\sum_{t\geq 0}\gamma^{t}\left(r(x_{t},y_{t})-\beta\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{t}|x_{t})}{\mu(y_{t}|x_{t})}\right)\right]

and

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}^{\pi_{\theta}}_{r}(x,y)=r(x,y)+\gamma\mathcal{V}^{\pi_{\theta}}_{r}(T(x,y))~.

The gradient expression for
\mathcal{V}^{\pi_{\theta}}(\rho)
is given by

\displaystyle\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{V}^{\theta}_{r}(\rho)=\frac{1}{1-\gamma}\sum_{x}d^{\pi_{\theta}}_{\rho}(x)\sum_{y}\pi_{\theta}(y|x)\left(\mathcal{Q}^{\pi_{\theta}}_{r}(x,y)-\beta\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y|x)}{\mu(y|x)}\right)\overline{\psi}_{\theta}(x,y)~.

Proof.

Note that we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}^{\pi_{\theta}}_{r}(\rho)=\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\rho}\left[\sum_{y}\pi_{\theta}(y|x)\left(\mathcal{Q}^{\pi_{\theta}}_{r}(x,y)-\beta\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y|x)}{\mu(y|x)}\right)\right]~.

Thus, we can write

\displaystyle\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{V}^{\pi_{\theta}}_{r}(\rho)

\displaystyle=\sum_{x,y}\rho(x)\Bigg(\nabla_{\theta}\pi_{\theta}(y|x)\left(\mathcal{Q}^{\pi_{\theta}}_{r}(x,y)-\beta\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y|x)}{\mu(y|x)}\right)

\displaystyle\quad\quad+\pi_{\theta}(y|x)\left(\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{Q}^{\pi_{\theta}}_{r}(x,y)-\frac{\mu(y|x)}{\pi_{\theta}(y|x)}\nabla_{\theta}\pi_{\theta}(y|x)\right)\Bigg)

\displaystyle=\sum_{x,y}\rho(x)\left(\pi_{\theta}(y|x)\left(\mathcal{Q}^{\pi_{\theta}}_{r}(x,y)-\beta\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y|x)}{\mu(y|x)}-1\right)\overline{\psi}_{\theta}(x,y)+\pi_{\theta}(y|x)\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{Q}^{\pi_{\theta}}_{r}(x,y)\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{x,y}\rho(x)\left(\pi_{\theta}(y|x)\left(\mathcal{Q}^{\pi_{\theta}}_{r}(x,y)-\beta\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y|x)}{\mu(y|x)}\right)\overline{\psi}_{\theta}(x,y)\right)

\displaystyle\quad\quad+\gamma\sum_{x,y}\rho(x)\pi_{\theta}(y|x)\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{V}^{\pi_{\theta}}_{r}(T(x,y))

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{1-\gamma}\sum_{x}d^{\pi_{\theta}}_{\rho}(x)\sum_{y}\pi_{\theta}(y|x)\left(\mathcal{Q}^{\pi_{\theta}}_{r}(x,y)-\beta\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y|x)}{\mu(y|x)}\right)\overline{\psi}_{\theta}(x,y)~,

where the second equality follows from the derivation of the gradient of loglinear policies (see the proof of Lemma
E.1
, while the third equality follows from the fact that
\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot|x)}[\overline{\psi}_{\theta}(x,y)]=0
, for each
x\in\mathcal{X}
.
∎

Lemma J.3
.

For any
\theta
, we have

\displaystyle\beta D_{\textnormal{KL}}\left(\pi_{\theta^{*}}||\mu\right)-\beta D_{KL}\left(\pi_{\theta}||\mu\right)\leq D(\pi_{\theta})\leq\beta D_{\textnormal{KL}}\left(\pi^{\textnormal{opt}}_{r^{*}}||\mu\right)-\beta D_{KL}\left(\pi_{\theta}||\mu\right)~,

Next, we will control the quantity
D(\pi_{\theta})
for the DPO setting.

Lemma J.4
.

With probability at least
1-\delta
, we have

\displaystyle D(\pi_{\widetilde{\theta}})

\displaystyle\leq\beta D_{\textnormal{KL}}(\pi^{\textnormal{opt}}_{r^{*}},\pi_{\theta^{*}})+\frac{\Lambda_{P}Ud_{P}}{S_{P}n}\sqrt{\frac{\log(4/\delta)}{2n}}

\displaystyle=\beta\left(D_{\textnormal{KL}}\left(\pi^{\textnormal{opt}}_{r^{*}}||\mu\right)-D_{\textnormal{KL}}\left(\pi_{\theta^{*}}||\mu\right)\right)+O\left(\frac{d_{P}}{n^{3/2}}\right)~.

Proof.

Recall that, for any
\theta
, we have defined

\displaystyle D_{\textnormal{KL}}\left(\pi_{\theta}||\mu\right)=\sum_{x}\rho(x)\sum_{x,y}\pi_{\theta}(y|x)\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y|x)}{\mu(y|x)}~.

First, note that

\displaystyle D_{\textnormal{KL}}\left(\pi_{\theta^{*}}||\mu\right)

\displaystyle-D_{\textnormal{KL}}\left(\pi_{\theta}||\mu\right)=\Bigg(D_{\textnormal{KL}}\left(\pi_{\theta^{*}}||\mu\right)-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{x\in\mathcal{D}}D_{\textnormal{KL}}\left(\pi_{\theta^{*}_{\mathcal{D}_{n}}}||\mu\right)\Bigg)+\Bigg(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{x\in\mathcal{D}}D_{\textnormal{KL}}\left(\pi_{\theta^{*}_{\mathcal{D}_{n}}}||\mu\right)

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{x\in\mathcal{D}}D_{\textnormal{KL}}\left(\pi_{\widetilde{\theta}}||\mu\right)\Bigg)+\Bigg(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{x\in\mathcal{D}}D_{\textnormal{KL}}\left(\pi_{\widetilde{\theta}}||\mu\right)-D_{\textnormal{KL}}\left(\pi_{\theta}||\mu\right)\Bigg)

\displaystyle=\Bigg(D_{\textnormal{KL}}\left(\pi_{\theta^{*}}||\mu\right)-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{x\in\mathcal{D}}D_{\textnormal{KL}}\left(\pi_{\theta^{*}_{\mathcal{D}_{n}}}||\mu\right)\Bigg)+\Bigg(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{x\in\mathcal{D}}D_{\textnormal{KL}}\left(\pi_{\widetilde{\theta}}||\mu\right)-D_{\textnormal{KL}}\left(\pi_{\theta}||\mu\right)\Bigg)~,

where the second equality follows from the exact optimization assumption. Note that the two summands above are deviations from means. If we can show that each individual quantity is bounded, then we can apply Hoeffding bounds.
To that end, first, note that

\displaystyle D_{\textnormal{KL}}\left(\pi_{\theta^{*}}||\mu\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{x}\rho(x)\sum_{y}\pi_{\theta^{*}}(y|x)\log\frac{\pi_{\theta^{*}}(y|x)}{\mu(y|x)}=\sum_{x}\rho(x)\sum_{y}\pi_{\theta^{*}}(y|x)\log\frac{\mu(y|x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r^{*}(x,y)\right)}{\mu(y|x)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\beta}\sum_{x}\rho(x)\sum_{y}\pi_{\theta^{*}}(y|x)r^{*}(x,y)\leq\frac{1}{\beta}~,

since the reward cannot be more than
1
. Similarly, for every
x\in\mathcal{D}
, we have

\displaystyle D_{\textnormal{KL}}\left(\pi_{\widetilde{\theta}}||\mu\right)

\displaystyle=D_{\textnormal{KL}}\left(\pi_{\theta^{*}_{\mathcal{D}_{n}}}||\mu\right)=\sum_{x}\rho(x)\sum_{y}\pi_{\theta^{*}_{\mathcal{D}_{n}}}(y|x)\log\frac{\mu(y|x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r^{*}(x,y)\right)}{\mu(y|x)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\beta}\sum_{x}\rho(x)\sum_{y}\pi_{\theta^{*}}(y|x)r^{*}(x,y)\leq\frac{1}{\beta}~.

For other contexts
x\not\in\mathcal{D}
, such a relation does not hold. Thus, we take another approach. We show that, for such points, the KL divergence between the learned policy and the sampling policy cannot be too far away from that between the optimal policy and the sampling policy.

\displaystyle\left|D_{\textnormal{KL}}\left(\pi_{\theta^{*}}||\mu\right)-D_{\textnormal{KL}}\left(\pi_{\theta^{*}_{\mathcal{D}_{n}}}||\mu\right)\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\sum_{x}\rho(x)\sum_{y}\pi_{\theta^{*}}(y|x)\log\frac{\pi_{\theta^{*}}(y|x)}{\mu(y|x)}-\sum_{x,y}\pi_{\theta^{*}_{\mathcal{D}_{n}}}(y|x)\log\frac{\pi_{\theta^{*}_{\mathcal{D}_{n}}}(y|x)}{\mu(y|x)}\right|

\displaystyle=\Bigg|\sum_{x}\rho(x)\sum_{y}\pi_{\theta^{*}}(y|x)\left(\log\frac{\pi_{\theta^{*}}(y|x)}{\pi_{\theta^{*}}(y|x)}+\log\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r^{*}(x,y)\right)\right)

\displaystyle\quad-\sum_{x,y}\pi_{\theta^{*}_{\mathcal{D}_{n}}}(y|x)\left(\log\frac{\pi_{\theta^{*}_{\mathcal{D}_{n}}}(y|x)}{\pi_{\theta^{*}}(y|x)}+\log\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r^{*}(x,y)\right)\right)\Bigg|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\beta}\left|\left(V^{\pi_{\theta^{*}}}_{r^{*}}(\rho)-V^{\pi_{\theta^{*}_{\mathcal{D}_{n}}}}(\rho)\right)-D_{\textnormal{KL}}\left(\pi_{\theta^{*}}||\pi_{\theta^{*}_{\mathcal{D}_{n}}}\right)\right|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\beta}+D_{\textnormal{KL}}\left(\pi_{\theta^{*}}||\pi_{\theta^{*}_{\mathcal{D}_{n}}}\right)~.

Now, for the last term of the right-hand side, we have

\displaystyle D_{\textnormal{KL}}\left(\pi_{\theta^{*}}||\pi_{\theta^{*}_{\mathcal{D}_{n}}}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{x}\rho(x)\sum_{y}\pi_{\theta^{*}}(y|x)\left(\log\pi_{\theta^{*}}(y|x)-\log\pi_{\theta^{*}_{\mathcal{D}_{n}}}(y|x)\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{x}\rho(x)\sum_{y}\pi_{\theta^{*}}(y|x)\left(\left\langle\psi(x,y),\theta^{*}-\theta^{*}_{\mathcal{D}_{n}}\right\rangle+\log\frac{\sum_{x^{\prime},y^{\prime}}\exp\left(\psi(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})^{\top}\theta^{*}_{\mathcal{D}_{n}}\right)}{\sum_{x^{\prime},y^{\prime}}\exp\left(\psi(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})^{\top}\theta^{*}\right)}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\Lambda_{P}Ud_{P}}{S_{P}\beta n}~,

where the last inequality follows from the same arguments as in the proof of Theorem
4.2
.
Going back to the original expression, note that, for any given
x\in\mathcal{X}
, we have

\displaystyle 0\leq D_{\textnormal{KL}}\left(\pi_{\theta^{*}}||\mu\right)\leq\frac{1}{\beta}~,\;\;\;\text{and}\;\;\;0\leq D_{\textnormal{KL}}\left(\pi_{\theta^{*}_{\mathcal{D}_{n}}}||\mu\right)\leq\frac{1}{\beta}+\frac{\Lambda_{P}Ud_{P}}{S_{P}\beta n}~.

Thus, by Hoeffding’s inequality, for any
\delta\geq 0
, with probability at least
1-\delta
, we have

\displaystyle\left|D_{\textnormal{KL}}\left(\pi_{\theta^{*}}||\mu\right)-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{x\in\mathcal{D}}D_{\textnormal{KL}}\left(\pi_{\theta^{*}_{\mathcal{D}_{n}}}||\mu\right)\right|\leq\frac{1}{\beta}\sqrt{\frac{\log(4/\delta)}{2n}}~,

and

\displaystyle\left|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{x\in\mathcal{D}}D_{\textnormal{KL}}\left(\pi_{\widetilde{\theta}}||\mu\right)-D_{\textnormal{KL}}\left(\pi_{\theta}||\mu\right)\right|\leq\left(\frac{1}{\beta}+\frac{\Lambda_{P}Ud_{P}}{S_{P}\beta n}\right)\sqrt{\frac{\log(4/\delta)}{2n}}~,

which implies that

\displaystyle-\left(\frac{2}{\beta}+\frac{\Lambda_{P}Ud_{P}}{S_{P}\beta n}\right)\sqrt{\frac{\log(4/\delta)}{2n}}\leq D_{\textnormal{KL}}\left(\pi_{\theta^{*}}||\mu\right)

\displaystyle-D_{\textnormal{KL}}\left(\pi_{\theta}||\mu\right)\leq\left(\frac{2}{\beta}+\frac{\Lambda_{P}Ud_{P}}{S_{P}\beta n}\right)\sqrt{\frac{\log(4/\delta)}{2n}}~.

On the other hand, note that

\displaystyle D_{\textnormal{KL}}\left(\pi^{\textnormal{opt}}_{r^{*}}||\mu\right)-D_{\textnormal{KL}}\left(\pi_{\widetilde{\theta}}||\mu\right)

\displaystyle=\left(D_{\textnormal{KL}}\left(\pi^{\textnormal{opt}}_{r^{*}}||\mu\right)-D_{\textnormal{KL}}\left(\pi_{\theta^{*}}||\mu\right)\right)+\left(D_{\textnormal{KL}}\left(\pi_{\theta^{*}}||\mu\right)-D_{\textnormal{KL}}\left(\pi_{\widetilde{\theta}}||\mu\right)\right)

\displaystyle\leq\left(D_{\textnormal{KL}}\left(\pi^{\textnormal{opt}}_{r^{*}}||\mu\right)-D_{\textnormal{KL}}\left(\pi_{\theta^{*}}||\mu\right)\right)+\left(\frac{2}{\beta}+\frac{\Lambda_{P}Ud_{P}}{S_{P}\beta n}\right)\sqrt{\frac{\log(4/\delta)}{2n}}~.

Next, we prove some useful properties of the log-exp-sum function.

Lemma J.5
.

The function defined as

\displaystyle A(\theta)=\sum_{x}\rho(x)\log\sum_{x,y}\exp\left(\theta^{\top}\psi(x,y)\right)~.

is
1
-Lipschitz and
2
-smooth. Moreover, if the features are sampled from a
0
-mean distribution and span
R^{d_{P}}
, then there exists
\kappa>0
, such that
A(\theta)
is
\kappa
-strongly convex.

Proof.

Let
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{P}}
. Note that

\displaystyle\nabla_{\theta}A(\theta)

\displaystyle=\sum_{x}\rho(x)\frac{\sum_{y}\exp(\psi(x,y)^{\top}\theta)}{\sum_{y^{\prime}}\exp(\psi(x,y^{\prime})^{\top}\theta)}\psi(x,y^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\sum_{x}\rho(x)\sum_{y}\pi_{\theta}(y|x)\psi(x,y)

\displaystyle\leq\max_{x,y}\left\lVert\psi(x,y)\right\rVert_{2}

\displaystyle\leq 1~.

On the other hand, the Hessian of
A(\theta)
is

\displaystyle\nabla^{2}_{\theta}A(\theta)

\displaystyle=\sum_{x}\rho(x)\sum_{y}\nabla_{\theta}\pi_{\theta}(y|x)\psi(x,y)

\displaystyle=\sum_{x}\rho(x)\sum_{y}\pi_{\theta}(y|x)\left(\psi(x,y)-\mathbb{E}_{y^{\prime}\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot|x)}[\psi(x,y^{\prime})]\right)\psi(x,y)^{\top}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\rho,y\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot|x)}\left[\psi(x,y)\psi(x,y)^{\top}\right]-\mathbb{E}_{x\sim,\rho,y\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot|x)}[\psi(x,y)]\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\rho,y\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot|x)}[\psi(x,y)]^{\top}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\rho,y\sim\pi_{\theta}(y|x)}\left[\left(\psi(x,y)-\mathbb{E}_{\theta}\left[\psi(x,y)\right]\right)\left(\psi(x,y)-\mathbb{E}_{\theta}\left[\psi(x,y)\right]\right)^{\top}\right]~.

By assumption on the feature mapping, we have that

\displaystyle\left\lVert\nabla^{2}_{\theta}A(\theta)\right\rVert_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\max_{x,y}\left\lVert\left(\psi(x,y)-\mathbb{E}_{\theta}\left[\psi(x,y)\right]\right)\left(\psi(x,y)-\mathbb{E}_{\theta}\left[\psi(x,y)\right]\right)^{\top}\right\rVert_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\max_{x,y}\left\lVert\psi(x,y)-\mathbb{E}_{\theta}[\psi(x,y)]\right\rVert_{2}

\displaystyle\leq 2\max_{x,y}\left\lVert\psi(x,y)\right\rVert_{2}=2~.

Therefore, the function
A(\theta)
is
2
-smooth in
\theta
.
For strong convexity, let
\psi
be sampled from a
0
-mean bounded distribution. Note that, for any non-zero vector in
\mathbb{R}^{d_{P}}
, we have

\displaystyle z^{\top}\nabla^{2}_{\theta}A(\theta)z

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\rho,y\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot|x)}\left[z^{\top}\psi(x,y)\psi(x,y)^{\top}z\right]

\displaystyle\geq\min_{\theta,x,y}\pi_{\theta}(\cdot|x)\sum_{x,y}(\psi(x,y)^{\top}z)^{2}

\displaystyle\geq C_{3}\sum_{x,y}(\psi(x,y)^{\top}z)^{2}~,

for a positive
C_{3}
, since
\pi_{\theta}
is in the loglinear class, for every
\theta
, and using Lemma
E.7
. Now, note that, if
z
can be expressed as a linear combination of
\{\psi(x,y)\}_{x,y}
, the summation cannot be zero for non-zero
z
. Thus, if
\{\psi(x,y)\}_{x,y}
spans
\mathbb{R}^{d_{P}}
, that is, the feature matrix is full rank, then there exists an absolute positive constant
\kappa
, such that we have

\displaystyle\left\lVert\nabla^{2}_{\theta}A(\theta)\right\rVert_{2}\geq\kappa>0~.

Thus, the function
A(\theta)
is
\kappa

∎

Lemma J.6
.

In general, the norms of the gradient and Hessian for the loss of tabular DPO are unbounded from above.

Proof.

Observe that, given policy
\pi
and
(x,y^{w})\in\mathcal{D}
, we have

\displaystyle\nabla_{\pi(y^{w}|x)}\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{D}}(\pi)=\frac{\beta}{n}\left(1-\sigma\left(\beta\log\frac{\pi(y^{w}|x)}{\mu(y^{w}|x)}-\beta\log\frac{\pi(y^{l}|x)}{\mu(y^{l}|x)}\right)\right)\frac{1}{\pi(y^{w}|x)}~.

On the other hand, for the second derivative with respect to
\pi(y^{w}|x)
, we have the following. First, let

\displaystyle f(\pi(y^{w}|x))=\beta\log\frac{\pi(y^{w}|x)}{\mu(y^{w}|x)}-\beta\log\frac{\pi(y^{l}|x)}{\mu(y^{l}|x)}~.

We have that
\nabla_{\pi(y^{w}|x)}f(\pi(y^{w}|x))=\beta/\pi(y^{w}|x)
. Now, observe that

\displaystyle\nabla^{2}_{\pi(y^{w}|x)}\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{D}}(\pi)=\frac{\beta}{n}\nabla_{\pi(y^{w}|x)}\frac{\exp(f(\pi(y^{w}|x)))}{\pi(y^{w}|x)(1+\exp(f(\pi(y^{w}|x))))}

\displaystyle=\frac{\beta}{n}\left(\frac{\frac{\beta}{\pi(y^{w}|x)}\exp\left(f(\pi(y^{w}|x))\right)\pi(y^{w}|x)(1+\exp(f(\pi(y^{w}|x))))}{\left(\pi(y^{w}|x)(1+\exp(f(\pi(y^{w}|x))))\right)^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle\quad\quad-\frac{\beta}{n}\left(\frac{\exp\left(f(\pi(y^{w}|x))\right)\left((1+\exp(f(\pi(y^{w}|x))))+\pi(y^{w}|x)\frac{\beta}{\pi(y^{w}|x)}\exp\left(f(\pi(y^{w}|x))\right)\right)}{\left(\pi(y^{w}|x)(1+\exp(f(\pi(y^{w}|x))))\right)^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\beta\left((\beta-1)\exp(f(\pi(y^{w}|x)))(1+\exp(f(\pi(y^{w}|x))))-\beta\exp(f(\pi(y^{w}|x)))^{2}\right)}{n\left(\pi(y^{w}|x)(1+\exp(f(\pi(y^{w}|x))))\right)^{2}}~.

The above numerator is not always non-negative, as solving for
\exp(f(\pi(y^{w}|x)))
will show. Moreover, neither the norm of the gradient nor the operator norm of the Hessian can be upper-bounded in general, due to the presence of
\pi(y^{w}|x)
in the denominator.
∎
