Anomalous singularity of the solution of the vector Dyson equation in the critical case This work was done under the summer internship program of IST Austria.
Oleksii Kolupaiev

Abstract




Abstract

We consider the solution of the vector Dyson equation
-1/m=z+Sm
in the case when
S
has a block staircase structure with
(n-1)
different critical zero blocks below the strictly positive anti-diagonal and all elements right above the anti-diagonal are strictly positive. We prove that the components of
m
behave as fractional powers of
z
in the neighbourhood of zero and show that the self-consistent density of states
\rho(E)
behaves as
|E|^{-\frac{n-1}{n+1}}
as
E
tends to zero, where
n^{2}
is a number of blocks. Both constant block and non-constant block cases are considered. In the non-constant case uniform estimates for the components of
m
are obtained.

AMS Subject Classification (2010):
60B20.

Keywords:
Vector Dyson equation; Generalized Wigner matrices.

1
Set-up and main results.

Consider a generalized Wigner matrix
H
, i.e. a hermitian random matrix
H=\left(h_{i,j}\right)_{i,j=1}^{n}
such that
\mathbb{E}H=0
,
h_{i,j}
are independent up to a hermitian symmetry, but not necessarily identically distributed.
One way to describe the distribution of eigenvalues of
H
is to calculate the trace of the resolvent
G=\left(H-z\right)^{-1}
and to apply the inverse Stieltjes transform to it (e.g. see [1]). The diagonal entries of
G
can be typically well approximated by the components of the solution of the vector Dyson equation (VDE)

-\frac{1}{m}=z+Sm,

(1.1)

these results are called entrywise (local) laws. In (
1.1
)
S=\left(\mathbb{E}\left|h_{i,j}\right|^{2}\right)_{i,j=1}^{n}
is a real-symmetric matrix with non-negative entries;
z\in\mathbb{H}
(we denote complex upper half-plane by
\mathbb{H}
);
m
is a vector-function,
m:\mathbb{H}\rightarrow\mathbb{H}^{n}
. Here
-1/m
is understood in entry-wise sense, by
z
we denote a vector from
\mathbb{H}^{n}
whose components are equal to
z
. From [1, Theorem 6.1.4] it is known that (
1.1
) has a unique solution. The self-consistent density of states is defined in [1]:

\rho(E)=\lim_{\eta\searrow 0}{\frac{1}{\pi n}\sum\limits_{k=1}^{n}\operatorname{Im}{m_{k}\left(E+i\eta\right)}}.

(1.2)

We impose the following assumption on the matrix
S
:
S
does not have any rectangular zero blocks with a perimeter at least
2(n+1)
. It is known that in the case when
S
does not meet this assumption,
\|m(z)\|
behaves as
\frac{1}{|z|}
near zero and a delta-measure appears in
\rho(E)
at zero. This reflects the fact that the corresponding random matrix
H
does not have full rank. On the other hand, if
S
has only zero blocks with a perimeter strictly less than
2n
(the situation extensively investigated in [4]), then the solution of (
1.1
) is bounded. We will consider the case, when
S
has a staircase structure with
(n-1)
different critical zero block (i.e. with a perimeter exactly
2(n+1)
) below the strictly positive anti-diagonal. We also assume strict positivity of the elements right above the anti-diagonal, namely

Assumption 1.1
.

For all
i,j\in\left\{1,\ldots,n\right\}
we have

\begin{cases}s_{i,j}>0,\text{if}\quad i+j=n\\
s_{i,j}>0,\text{if}\quad i+j=n+1\\
s_{i,j}=0,\text{if}\quad i+j\geq n+2\\
\end{cases}

We also adhere to the following conventions:

Convention 1
.

The variable
z
always lies in the complex upper half-plane. Particularly, if we consider
z\rightarrow 0
it means that
z
tends to zero from the upper half-plane.

Convention 2
.

c,C
are positive constants, which can change their value from line to line for simplicity of notation.

Definition 1.2
.

Let
\left\{z_{l}\right\}_{l=1}^{\infty}\subset\mathbb{H}
be a fixed sequence converging to zero,
f,g:\mathbb{H}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
. We will say that
f
is equivalent to
g
with respect to the sequence
\left\{z_{l}\right\}_{l=1}^{\infty}
if there exist constants
c,C
such that
c<\frac{|f(z_{l})|}{|g(z_{l})|}<C
for all
l\in\mathbb{N}
.
Equivalence of functions
f
and
g
will be denoted by
f\sim g
.

Our main results are as follows:

Theorem 1.3
.

Under Assumption
1.1
there exist positive constants
\left\{c_{k}\right\}_{k=1}^{n}
such that for all
k\in\left\{1,\ldots,n\right\}
we have

\lim_{z\to 0}{\left(m_{k}(z)z^{-\left(1-\frac{2k}{n+1}\right)}\right)}=c_{k}e^{i\frac{\pi k}{n+1}},

(1.3)

where the branch of
z^{-\left(1-\frac{2k}{n+1}\right)}
is chosen in the complex upper half-plane in such a way that
z^{-\left(1-\frac{2k}{n+1}\right)}
is positive for positive
z
.

Corollary 1.4
.

Under assumptions and with notations of Theorem
1.3
we have that the following limit exists and it is positive

\lim_{E\to 0}{\rho(E)|E|^{-\frac{n-1}{n+1}}}>0.

(1.4)

A weaker version of Corollary
1.4
was already obtained in [3]: the matrix
S
was considered under the stronger assumption
s_{i,j}>0
for all
i+j\leq n+1
and only the equivalence
\rho(E)\sim|E|^{-\frac{n-1}{n+1}}
, as
E
tends to zero, was established without proving the limit.

The equation (
1.1
) typically arises when
H
is a random matrix consisting of
n\times n
blocks of size
N\times N
each. We think of
n
to be fixed and
N\to\infty
. If the variances inside each block are constant
s_{i,j}
,
i,j\in\left\{1,\ldots,n\right\}
, then
G_{l,l}(z)\approx m_{k}(z)
if
l
belongs to the
k
-th block and
m_{k}
is the
k
-th component of the solution (
1.1
). One may also consider the case of non-constant blocks in the matrix of variances
S
, where the corresponding VDE has
nN
possible different components. Nevertheless, the following result similar to Theorem
1.3
still holds:

Theorem 1.5
.

(Non-constant block case.)

Let
\mathcal{S}
be a real-symmetric
nN\times nN
matrix with non-negative entries. Assume that
\mathcal{S}
has a following block structure:

\mathcal{S}=\left(\mathcal{S}^{j,k}\right)_{j,k=1}^{n},

(1.5)

where
\mathcal{S}^{j,k}
are
N\times N
matrices for
j,k\in\left\{1,\ldots,n\right\}
and

1.

All entries of
\mathcal{S}^{j,k}
are either positive or all entries are zero.

2.

If all entries of
\mathcal{S}^{j,k}
are positive then
\frac{\mathfrak{c}}{N}<\mathcal{S}^{j,k}_{\nu,\tau}<\frac{\mathfrak{C}}{N}
for all
\nu,\tau\in\left\{1,\ldots,N\right\}
, where
\mathfrak{c}
and
\mathfrak{C}
are positive constants which do not depend on
N
.

3.

\mathcal{S}^{j,k}=0
if
j+k>n+1
.

4.

All entries of
\mathcal{S}^{j,k}
are positive if
j+k\in\left\{n,n+1\right\}
.

Then there exist positive constants
\mathscr{C}_{1}
,
\mathscr{C}_{2}
which do not depend on
N
and a neighbourhood
U
of zero in
\mathbb{H}
such that for all
k\in\left\{1,\ldots,nN\right\}
and
z\in U
it holds that

\mathscr{C}_{1}<|m_{k}(z)|\cdot|z|^{-\left(1-\frac{2}{n+1}\left\lceil\frac{k}{N}\right\rceil\right)}<\mathscr{C}_{2}.

(1.6)

Moreover,

\operatorname{arg}{\left(m_{k}(z)z^{-\left(1-\frac{2}{n+1}\left\lceil\frac{k}{N}\right\rceil\right)}\right)}\to\frac{\pi}{n+1}\left\lceil\frac{k}{N}\right\rceil,\quad\text{as}\quad z\to 0,z\in\mathbb{H},

(1.7)

where the branch of
z^{-\left(1-\frac{2}{n+1}\left\lceil\frac{k}{N}\right\rceil\right)}
is chosen in the complex upper half-plane in such a way that
z^{-\left(1-\frac{2}{n+1}\left\lceil\frac{k}{N}\right\rceil\right)}
is positive for positive
z
.

Note that the arguments of the solution still converge within each block (
1.7
), but their moduli typically do not converge. However we have uniform bounds on their asymptotics (
1.6
).

In Sections 2 to 6 we prove Theorem
1.3
and in Section 7 prove Theorem
1.5
generalizing arguments from the previous sections to the non-constant block case.

We prove Theorem
1.3
in two steps: (
1.3
) is verified separately for absolute value and for argument of the function
m_{k}(z)z^{-\left(1-\frac{2k}{n+1}\right)}
.

During the preparation of this article D.Renfrew informed us on an independent ongoing work [5] studying similar questions for the same model. In [5] the classification of singularities of the solution at zero is given for any constant block matrix S in contrast to our Assumption 1.1 but the non constant block case (Theorem 1.5) was not considered in [5]. Note that the method presented in [5] is completely different from the method described in the current paper.

2
Relation between the behaviour of the solution of (
1.1
) and the structure of
S
.

We fix a sequence
\left\{z_{l}\right\}_{l=1}^{\infty}\subset\mathbb{H}
such that

\lim_{l\to\infty}z_{l}=0.

(2.1)

In Sections 2 to 4 we will temporarily need the following technical assumption, which will be removed in Section 5:

Assumption 2.1
.

\lim_{l\to\infty}{\left(z_{l}\cdot m_{j}(z_{l})\right)}=0,\quad\forall j\in\left\{1,\ldots,n\right\}.

(2.2)

In Subsection
2.1
we establish a block staircase structure of the matrix
S
assuming that components of the solution of (
1.1
) are ordered in the sense of their asymptotic size near 0. First we formulate this ordering as an assumption and later in Subsection
2.2
we show that it follows from Assumption
1.1
.

Assumption 2.2
.

There exists a subsequence
\left\{z_{l_{q}}\right\}_{q=1}^{\infty}
of the sequence
\left\{z_{l}\right\}_{l=1}^{\infty}
such that for every
k\in\left\{1,\ldots,n-1\right\}
either
m_{k}\sim m_{k+1}
with respect to the sequence
\left\{z_{l_{q}}\right\}_{q=1}^{\infty}
or

\lim_{q\to\infty}{\frac{m_{k}(z_{l_{q}})}{m_{k+1}(z_{l_{q}})}}=0.

(2.3)

2.1
Block staircase structure of the matrix
S
.

In this subsection we work under Assumptions
2.1
and
2.2
. For simplicity of notation we denote
w_{q}:=z_{l_{q}}
,
q\in\mathbb{N}
.

Let us split the vector
m
into
p
blocks
\left(m^{(1)},m^{(2)},\ldots,m^{(p)}\right)
of sizes
d_{1},d_{2},\ldots,d_{p}
,
m^{(a)}\in\mathbb{C}^{d_{a}}
for
a\in\left\{1,\ldots,p\right\}
, in such a way that two components of
m
belong to the same block if and only if they are equivalent with respect to the sequence
\left\{w_{q}\right\}_{q=1}^{\infty}
, i.e. for
j,k\in\left\{1,\ldots,n\right\}

m_{j}\sim m_{k}\quad\text{WRT}\quad\left\{w_{q}\right\}_{q=1}^{\infty}\Leftrightarrow

\Leftrightarrow\exists a\in\left\{1,\ldots,p\right\};\exists\nu,\tau\in\left\{1,\ldots,d_{a}\right\}:\quad j=\sum\limits_{b=1}^{a-1}d_{b}+\nu,\quad k=\sum\limits_{b=1}^{a-1}d_{b}+\tau.

(2.4)

In short, all components within each block are comparable along
\left\{w_{l}\right\}_{l=1}^{\infty}
, and

\|m^{(a)}\|\ll\|m^{(b)}\|\quad\text{iff}\quad a<b

(2.5)

by Assumption
2.2
.

Now a corresponding partition of the matrix
S
into a block matrices is naturally obtained:
S=\left(\mathfrak{S}_{a,b}\right)_{a,b=1}^{p}
, where
\mathfrak{S}_{a,b}
is a rectangular matrix of the size
d_{a}\times d_{b}
. As
S
is symmetric,
\mathfrak{S}_{a,b}^{T}=\mathfrak{S}_{b,a}
,
a,b=1,\ldots,p
. In the notation above the initial VDE (
1.1
) takes the form:

-\frac{1}{m^{(a)}}=z+\sum\limits_{b=1}^{p}\mathfrak{S}_{a,b}m^{(b)},\quad a=1,\ldots,p,

(2.6)

or, equivalently,

-1=zm^{(a)}+\sum\limits_{b=1}^{p}m^{(a)}\mathfrak{S}_{a,b}m^{(b)},\quad a=1,\ldots,p.

(2.7)

We will prove that

Proposition 2.3
.

Under Assumption
2.2
the matrix
S=\left(\mathfrak{S}_{a,b}\right)_{a,b=1}^{p}
has a block staircase structure in the following sense:

1.

\mathfrak{S}_{a,p-a+1}
is a non-zero matrix for
a\in\left\{1,\ldots,p\right\}
,

2.

\mathfrak{S}_{a,b}=0
for all
a,b\in\left\{1,\ldots,p\right\}
such that
a+b>p+1
.

Additionally under Assumption
2.1
we also have that
\mathfrak{S}_{a,p-a+1}
is a square matrix for
a\in\left\{1,\ldots,p\right\}
.

Define
r_{a}
to be the index of the rightmost not identically zero block in the
a
-th row of blocks, i.e.:

r_{a}:=\max\left\{b\mid\mathfrak{S}_{a,b}\neq 0\right\},\quad a=1,\ldots,p.

(2.8)

Lemma 2.4
.

Under Assumption
2.2
for every
a,b\in\left\{1,\ldots,p\right\}
and
\nu\in\left\{1,\ldots,d_{a}\right\}
,
\tau\in\left\{1,\ldots,d_{b}\right\}
it holds that:

1.

m^{(a)}_{\nu}(w_{q})m^{(b)}_{\tau}(w_{q})\to 0
as
q\to\infty
if
b<r_{a}
,

2.

m^{(a)}_{\nu}m^{(b)}_{\tau}\sim 1
WRT
\left\{w_{q}\right\}_{q=1}^{\infty}
if
b=r_{a}
.

In order to prove Lemma
2.4
we will need the notion of saturated self-energy operator from [1], that is a linear operator defined by the matrix

F=|m|S|m|=\left(|m_{k}|s_{k,j}|m_{j}|\right)_{k,j=1}^{n}.

(2.9)

Further we will denote for short
\frac{m}{|m|}:=\left\{\frac{m_{j}}{|m_{j}|}\right\}_{j=1}^{n}
.

From [1, Proposition 7.2.9] it is known that

\|F\|_{L^{2}\rightarrow L^{2}}<1,

(2.10)

where
L^{2}=\left(\mathbb{C}^{n},\|.\|\right)
, for
v=\left(v_{1},\ldots,v_{n}\right)\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
:

\|v\|=\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum\limits_{j=1}^{n}|v_{j}|^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

From (
2.10
) it follows that for all
j,k\in\left\{1,\ldots,n\right\}
and
z\in\mathbb{H}

|m_{k}(z)|s_{k,j}|m_{j}(z)|<1.

(2.11)

We will also need the following estimates for the solution of (
1.1
).

Proposition 2.5
.

For all
k=1,\ldots,n
and
z\in\mathbb{H}
,
|z|<1
we have:

c|z|<|m_{k}(z)|<\frac{C}{|z|}.

(2.12)

Proof of Proposition
2.5
:
From [1, Proposition 7.2.9] it is known that

\|m(z)\|_{2}\leq\frac{2}{|z|},\quad\forall z\in\mathbb{H}.

(2.13)

Therefore, the upper estimate in (
2.12
) holds. On the other hand,

-\frac{1}{m(z)}=z+Sm(z)\Rightarrow\left\|\frac{1}{m(z)}\right\|_{2}=\|z+Sm(z)\|_{2}\leq\|z\|_{2}+\|S\|\|m(z)\|_{2}\leq|z|+\frac{2\|S\|}{|z|}\leq\frac{C}{|z|}

for
|z|<1
. So, the lower estimate in (
2.12
) also holds.

\square

Proof of Lemma
2.4
:
By the definition of
r_{a}
there exist
\nu_{0}\in\left\{1,\ldots,d_{a}\right\}
and
\tau_{0}\in\left\{1,\ldots,d_{r_{a}}\right\}
such that
\left(\mathfrak{S}_{a,r_{a}}\right)_{\nu_{0},\tau_{0}}>0
.

(1)
For
b<r_{a}
and
\nu\in\left\{1,\ldots,d_{a}\right\}
,
\tau\in\left\{1,\ldots,d_{b}\right\}
we have:

m^{(a)}_{\nu}(w_{q})m^{(b)}_{\tau}(w_{q})=o\left(m^{(a)}_{\nu_{0}}(w_{q})m^{(r_{a})}_{\tau_{0}}(w_{q})\right)=o(1),\quad\text{as}\quad q\to\infty.

(2.14)

In (
2.14
) the first equality follows from (
2.5
) and the second from (
2.11
).

(2)
By the definition of the block structure of the matrix
S
we have that for any
\nu\in\left\{1,\ldots,d_{a}\right\}
and
\tau\in\left\{1,\ldots,d_{r_{a}}\right\}
it holds that

m^{(a)}_{\nu}m^{(r_{a})}_{\tau}\sim m^{(a)}_{\nu_{0}}m^{(r_{a})}_{\tau_{0}}\quad\text{WRT}\quad\left\{w_{q}\right\}_{q=1}^{\infty},

So, it is sufficient to check that

m^{(a)}_{\nu_{0}}m^{(r_{a})}_{\tau_{0}}\sim 1\quad\text{WRT}\quad\left\{w_{q}\right\}_{q=1}^{\infty}

(2.15)

From (
2.11
) we have an upper estimate:

\left|m^{(a)}_{\nu_{0}}(w_{q})\right|\cdot\left|m^{(r_{a})}_{\tau_{0}}(w_{q})\right|<\frac{1}{\left(\mathfrak{S}_{a,r_{a}}\right)_{\nu_{0},\tau_{0}}}<C,\quad\forall l\in\mathbb{N}

(2.16)

Suppose that the lower estimate does not hold. Therefore, there exists a subsequence
\left\{w_{q_{u}}\right\}_{u=1}^{\infty}
such that

\lim_{u\to\infty}\left|m^{(a)}_{\nu_{0}}(w_{q_{u}})\right|\cdot\left|m^{(r_{a})}_{\tau_{0}}(w_{q_{u}})\right|=0

(2.17)

Hence, for all
\nu\in\left\{1,\ldots,d_{a}\right\},\tau\in\left\{1,\ldots,d_{r_{a}}\right\}
it holds that

\lim_{u\to\infty}\left|m^{(a)}_{\nu}(w_{q_{u}})\right|\cdot\left|m^{(r_{a})}_{\tau}(w_{q_{u}})\right|=0

(2.18)

Note that for every
\nu\in\left\{1,\ldots,d_{a}\right\}
we have

\left|zm^{(a)}_{\nu}\right|=\left|m^{(r_{a})}_{1}m^{(a)}_{\nu}\right|\cdot\left|\frac{z}{m^{(r_{a})}_{1}}\right|<C\left|m^{(a)}_{\nu}m^{(r_{a})}_{1}\right|,

(2.19)

here we used the lower estimate from Proposition
2.5
. Hence,

\lim_{u\to\infty}{w_{q_{u}}m^{(a)}_{\nu}(w_{q_{u}})}=0

(2.20)

Therefore, from (
2.18
), (
2.20
) and the first part of Lemma
2.4
it follows that for given
a
all summands in the RHS of (
2.7
) tend to zero along the sequence
\left\{w_{q_{u}}\right\}_{u=1}^{\infty}
, while the LHS of (
2.7
) is a non-zero constant, which is a contradiction. So, the second part of Lemma
2.4
is also proved.

\square

Proof of Lemma

2.2

\square

Proof of Proposition
3.1
:

. We will find
\left\{\varphi_{k}\right\}_{k=1}^{n+1}
explicitly from (
6.13
):

\pi-2\varphi_{0}=\varphi_{n+1}-\varphi_{0}=(\varphi_{n+1}-\varphi_{n})+(\varphi_{n}-\varphi_{n-1})+\ldots+(\varphi_{1}-\varphi_{0})=\\
=(n+1)(\varphi_{1}-\varphi_{0})+2\pi\sum\limits_{k=1}^{n}t_{k}.

So for
k\in\left\{1,\ldots,n\right\}
we have

\varphi_{k}=(\varphi_{k}-\varphi_{k-1})+(\varphi_{k-1}-\varphi_{k-2}+\ldots+(\varphi_{1}-\varphi_{0})+\varphi_{0}=\varphi_{0}+k(\varphi_{1}-\varphi_{0})+\sum\limits_{j=1}^{k}t_{j}=\\
=\left(1-\frac{2k}{n+1}\right)\varphi_{0}+\left(\frac{\pi k}{n+1}-\frac{2\pi k}{n+1}\sum\limits_{j=1}^{n}t_{j}+\sum\limits_{j=1}^{k}t_{k}\right).

Hence from (
6.12
) it follows that for
k\in\left\{1,\ldots,n\right\}

\psi_{k}=\frac{\pi k}{n+1}-\frac{2\pi k}{n+1}\sum\limits_{j=1}^{n}t_{j}+\sum\limits_{j=1}^{k}t_{j}.

But each of
t_{k}
takes a finite number of values, so the set
\Lambda_{0}
is also finite. Together with Lemma
6.2
and (
6.9
) this finishes the proof of Theorem
6.1
.

\square

Proof of Theorem
1.3
:
(
1.3
) directly follows from (
5.1
) and (
6.1
).

\square
