Generalization Bounds for Noisy Iterative Algorithms Using Properties of Additive Noise Channels
Hao Wang hao_wang@g.harvard.edu, Rui Gao rui.gao@mccombs.utexas.edu, Flavio P. Calmon flavio@seas.harvard.edu

Abstract
Machine learning models trained by different optimization algorithms under different data distributions can exhibit distinct generalization behaviors. In this paper, we analyze the generalization of models trained by noisy iterative algorithms. We derive distribution-dependent generalization bounds by connecting noisy iterative algorithms to additive noise channels found in communication and information theory. Our generalization bounds shed light on several applications, including differentially private stochastic gradient descent (DP-SGD), federated learning, and stochastic gradient Langevin dynamics (SGLD). We demonstrate our bounds through numerical experiments, showing that they can help understand recent empirical observations of the generalization phenomena of neural networks.



Hao Wang, Rui Gao, Flavio P. Calmon

Introduction

Many learning algorithms aim to solve the following (possibly non-convex) optimization problem:

\displaystyle\min_{\bm{w}\in\mathcal{W}}~L_{\mu}(\bm{w}),\quad\text{where }L_{\mu}(\bm{w})\triangleq\mathbb{E}\left[\ell(\bm{w},\textnormal{Z})\right]=\int_{\mathcal{Z}}\ell(\bm{w},\bm{z})\textrm{d}\mu(\bm{z}),

(1)

where
\bm{w}\in\mathcal{W}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
is the model parameter (e.g., weights of a neural network) to optimize;
\mu
is the underlying data distribution that generates
Z
; and
\ell:\mathcal{W}\times\mathcal{Z}\to\mathbb{R}^{+}
is the loss function (e.g., 0-1 loss). In the context of supervised learning,
Z
is often composed by a feature vector
X
and its corresponding label
Y
. Since the data distribution
\mu
is unknown,
L_{\mu}(\bm{w})
cannot be computed directly. In practice, a data set
\textnormal{S}\triangleq(\textnormal{Z}_{1},\cdots,\textnormal{Z}_{n})
containing
n
i.i.d. points
\textnormal{Z}_{i}\sim\mu
is used to minimize an empirical risk:

\displaystyle\min_{\bm{w}\in\mathcal{W}}~L_{\textnormal{S}}(\bm{w}),\quad\text{where }L_{\textnormal{S}}(\bm{w})\triangleq\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\ell(\bm{w},\textnormal{Z}_{i}).

(2)

We consider the following (projected) noisy iterative algorithm for solving the empirical risk optimization in (
2
). The parameter
\bm{w}
is initialized with a random point
\textnormal{W}_{0}\in\mathcal{W}
and updated using the following rule:

\displaystyle\textnormal{W}_{t}=\mathsf{Proj}_{\mathcal{W}}\left(\textnormal{W}_{t-1}-\eta_{t}\cdot g(\textnormal{W}_{t-1},\{\textnormal{Z}_{i}\}_{i\in\mathcal{B}_{t}})+m_{t}\cdot\textnormal{N}_{t}\right),

(3)

where
\eta_{t}
is the learning rate;
\textnormal{N}_{t}
is an additive noise drawn independently from a distribution
P_{\textnormal{N}}
;
m_{t}
is the magnitude of the noise;
\mathcal{B}_{t}\subseteq[n]
contains the indices of the data points used at the current iteration and
b_{t}\triangleq|\mathcal{B}_{t}|
;
g
is the direction for updating the parameter (e.g., gradient of the loss function); and

\displaystyle g(\textnormal{W}_{t-1},\{\textnormal{Z}_{i}\}_{i\in\mathcal{B}_{t}})\triangleq\frac{1}{b_{t}}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{B}_{t}}g(\textnormal{W}_{t-1},\textnormal{Z}_{i}).

(4)

At the end of each iteration, the parameter is projected onto the domain
\mathcal{W}
, i.e.,
\mathsf{Proj}_{\mathcal{W}}(\bm{w})\triangleq\argmin_{\bm{w}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{W}}\|\bm{w}^{\prime}-\bm{w}\|
. The recursion in (
3
) is run
T
iterations and the final output is a random variable
\textnormal{W}_{T}
.

The goal of this paper is to provide an upper bound for the
expected generalization gap
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[L_{\mu}(\textnormal{W}_{T})-L_{\textnormal{S}}(\textnormal{W}_{T})\right],

(5)

where the expectation is taken over the randomness of the training data set
S
and of the noisy iterative algorithm.

Noisy iterative algorithms are used in different practical settings due to their many attractive properties
(
Li et al. 2016
;
Zhang et al. 2017b
;
Raginsky et al. 2017
;
Xu et al. 2018
, see e.g.,)
.
For example, differentially private SGD (DP-SGD) algorithm
(
Song et al. 2013
;
Abadi et al. 2016
, see e.g.,)
, one kind of noisy iterative algorithm, is often used to train machine learning models while protecting user privacy
(
Dwork et al. 2006
)
. Recently, it has been implemented in open-source libraries, including Opacus
(
Facebook AI 2020
)
and TensorFlow Privacy
(
Radebaugh and Erlingsson 2019
)
.
The additive noise in iterative algorithms may also mitigate overfitting for deep neural networks (DNNs)
(
Neelakantan et al. 2015
)
.
From a theoretical perspective, noisy iterative algorithms can escape local minima
(
Kleinberg et al. 2018
)
or saddle points
(
Ge et al. 2015
)
and generalize well
(
Pensia et al. 2018
)
.

We derive generalization bounds for noisy iterative algorithms. Although these bounds may be vacuous, when calculated numerically, they maintain a high correlation with the generalization gap. As a result, they shed light on many empirical observations of neural networks that are not explained by uniform notions of hypothesis class complexity
(
Vapnik and Chervonenkis 1971
;
Valiant 1984
)
.
For example, a neural network trained using true labels exhibits better generalization ability than a network trained using corrupted labels even when the network architecture is fixed and perfect training accuracy is achieved
(
Zhang et al. 2017a
)
. Distribution-independent bounds is unable to capture this phenomenon because they are invariant to both true data and corrupted data.

In contrast, our bounds capture this empirical observation, exhibiting a lower value on networks trained on true labels compared to ones trained on corrupted labels (Figure

Another example is that a wider network often has a more favourable generalization capability
(

. This may seem counter-intuitive at first glance since one may expect that wider networks have a higher VC-dimension and, consequently, would have a higher generalize gap. Our bounds capture this behaviour and are decreasing with respect to the neural network width (Figure

).

We present three generalization bounds for the noisy iterative algorithms (Section
4
). These bounds rely on different kinds of
f
-divergence but are proved in a uniform manner by exploring properties of additive noise channels (Section
3
). Among them, the KL-divergence bound can deal with sampling with replacement; the total variation bound is often the tightest one; and the
\chi^{2}
-divergence bound requires the mildest assumption. We apply our results to applications, including DP-SGD, federated learning, and SGLD (Section
5
). Under these applications, our generalization bounds can be significantly simplified and estimated from the training data. Finally, we demonstrate our bounds through numerical experiments (Section
6
), showing that they can predict the behavior of the true generalization gap.

Our generalization bounds incorporate a time-decaying factor. This decay factor tightens the bounds by enabling the impact of early iterations to reduce with time. Our analysis is motivated by a line of recent works
(
Feldman et al. 2018
;
Balle et al. 2019
;
Asoodeh et al. 2020
)
which observed that data points used in the early iterations enjoy stronger differential privacy guarantees than those occurring late. Accordingly, we prove that if a data point is used at an early iteration, its contribution to our generalization bounds is decreasing with time due to the cumulative effect of the additive noise.

The proof techniques of this paper are based on fundamental tools from information theory. We first use an information-theoretic framework, proposed by
Russo and Zou 2016
and
Xu and Raginsky 2017
and further tightened by
Bu et al. 2020

5

-information

I_{f}(\textnormal{W}_{T};\textnormal{Z}_{i})
between the algorithmic output

. However, estimating this

1.1
Related Works

There are significant recent works which adopt the information-theoretic framework
(
Xu and Raginsky 2017
)
for analyzing the generalization capability of noisy iterative algorithms. Among them,
Pensia et al. 2018
initially derived a generalization bound in Corollary 1 and their bound was extended in Proposition 3 of
Bu et al. 2020
for the SGLD algorithm. Although the framework in
Pensia et al. 2018
can be applied to a broad class of noisy iterative algorithms, their bound in Corollary 1 and Proposition 3 in
Bu et al. 2020
rely on the Lipschitz constant of the loss function, which makes them independent of the data distribution. Distribution-independent bounds can be potentially loose since the Lipschitz constant may be large and may not capture some empirical observations (e.g., label corruption
(
Zhang et al. 2017a
)
). Specifically, this Lipschitz constant only relies on the architecture of the network instead of the weight matrices or the data distribution so it is the same for a network trained from corrupted data and a network trained from true data.

To obtain a distribution-dependent bound,
Negrea et al. 2019
improved the analysis in
Pensia et al. 2018
by replacing the Lipschitz constant with a gradient prediction residual when analyzing the SGLD algorithm.
Their follow-up work
(
Haghifam et al. 2020
)
investigated the Langevin dynamics algorithm (i.e., full batch SGLD), which was later extended by
Rodríguez-Gálvez et al. 2021
to SGLD, and observed a time-decaying phenomenon in their experiments. Specifically,
(
Haghifam et al. 2020
)
incorporated a quantity, namely the squared error probability of the hypothesis test, into their bound in Theorem 4.2 and this quantity decays with the number of iterations. This seems to suggest that earlier iterations have a larger impact on their generalization bound. In contrast, our decay factor indicates that the impact of earlier iterations is reducing with the total number of iterations. Furthermore, the bound in their Theorem 4.2 requires a bounded loss function while our
\chi^{2}
-based generalization bound only needs the variance of the loss function to be bounded.
More broadly,
Neu et al. 2021
investigated the generalization properties of SGD. However, the generalization bound in their Proposition 3 suffers from a weaker order
O(1/\sqrt{n})
when the analysis is applied to the SGLD algorithm.

In addition to the works discussed above, there is a line of papers on deriving SGLD generalization bounds
(
Mou et al. 2018
;
Li et al. 2020
)
through other proof techniques.
Among them,
Mou et al. 2018
introduced two generalization bounds. The first one
(
Mou et al. 2018
, Theorem 1 of)
, a stability-based bound, achieves
O(1/n)
rate in terms of the sample size
n
but relies on the Lipschitz constant of the loss function which makes it distribution-independent.
The second one
(
Mou et al. 2018
, Theorem 2 of)
, a PAC-Bayes bound, replaces the Lipschitz constant by an expected-squared gradient norm but suffers from a slower rate
O(1/\sqrt{n})
. In contrast, our SGLD bound in Proposition
15
has order
O(1/n)
and tightens the expected-squared gradient norm by the variance of gradients. The PAC-Bayes bound in
Mou et al. 2018
also incorporates an explicit time-decaying factor. However, their analysis seems to heavily rely on the Gaussian noise. In contrast, our generalization bounds include a decay factor for a broad class of noisy iterative algorithms.
A follow-up work by
Li et al. 2020
combined the algorithmic stability approach with PAC-Bayesian theory and presented a bound which achieves order
O(1/n)
. However, their bound requires the scale of the learning rate to be upper bounded by the inverse Lipschitz constant of the loss function, which could result in negligible learning rates in practice. In contrast, we do not need any assumptions on the learning rate.

A standard approach
(
He et al. 2021
, see e.g.,)
of deriving a generalization bound for the DP-SGD algorithm follows two steps: (i) prove that DP-SGD satisfies the
(\epsilon,\delta)
-DP guarantees
(
Song et al. 2013
;
Wu et al. 2017
;
Feldman et al. 2018
;
Balle et al. 2019
;
Asoodeh et al. 2020
)
; (ii) derive/apply a generalization bound that holds for
any

(\epsilon,\delta)
-DP algorithm
(
Dwork et al. 2015
;
Bassily et al. 2021
;
Jung et al. 2021
)
. However, generalization bounds obtained from this procedure are distribution-independent since DP is robust with respect to the data distribution. In contrast, our bounds in Section
5.1
are distribution-dependent. We extend our analysis and derive a generalization bound in the setting of federated learning in Section
5.2
. A previous work by
Yagli et al. 2020
also proved a generalization bound for federated learning in their Theorem 3 but their bound involves a mutual information which could be hard to estimate from data.

The conference version
(
Wang et al. 2021
)
of this work investigates the generalization of the SGLD and DP-SGD algorithms. In this paper, we study a broader class of noisy iterative algorithms, including SGLD and DP-SGD as two examples. This extension requires a significant improvement of the proof techniques presented in
Wang et al. 2021
. Specifically, we introduce a unified framework for deriving generalization bounds through
f
-divergence while the analysis in the prior work
(
Wang et al. 2021
)
is tailored to the KL-divergence. In the context of the DP-SGD algorithm, we prove that the total variation distance leads to a tighter generalization bound and the
\chi^{2}
-divergence leads to a bound requiring milder assumptions compared with the results in
Wang et al. 2021
. Finally, we derive a new generalization bound in the context of federated learning as an application of our framework.

(6)

\displaystyle\mathsf{mmae}(\textnormal{X})

\displaystyle\triangleq\inf_{\bm{a}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathbb{E}\left[\|\textnormal{X}-\bm{a}\|_{1}\right].

(8)

In order to measure the difference between two probability distributions, we recall Csiszár’s

(

(10)

The

-information between

\displaystyle I_{f}(\textnormal{X};\textnormal{Y})\triangleq\textnormal{D}_{f}(P_{\textnormal{X},\textnormal{Y}}\|P_{\textnormal{X}}\otimes P_{\textnormal{Y}})=\mathbb{E}\left[\textnormal{D}_{f}(P_{\textnormal{Y}|\textnormal{X}}\|P_{\textnormal{Y}})\right],

(11)

where

)
.

2.2
Information-theoretic Generalization Bounds

A recent work by
Xu and Raginsky 2017
provided a framework for analyzing algorithmic generalization. Specifically, they considered a learning algorithm as a channel (i.e., conditional probability distribution) that takes a training set
S
as input and outputs a parameter
W
. Furthermore, they derived an upper bound for the expected generalization gap using the mutual information
I(\textnormal{W};\textnormal{S})
. This bound was later tightened by
Bu et al. 2020
using an individual sample mutual information. Next, we recall the generalization bound in
Bu et al. 2020
. By adapting their proof and leveraging variational representations of
f
-divergence
(
Nguyen et al. 2010
)
, we present another two generalization bounds based on different kinds of
f
-information. Note that these two bounds can also be obtained from Corollary 1 in

and applying Jensen’s inequality to Eq. (15) in the same paper, respectively.

.

Consider a learning algorithm which takes a data set
\textnormal{S}=(\textnormal{Z}_{1},\cdots,\textnormal{Z}_{n})

•

(

\displaystyle\left|\mathbb{E}\left[L_{\mu}(\textnormal{W})-L_{\textnormal{S}}(\textnormal{W})\right]\right|\leq\frac{\sqrt{2}\sigma}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sqrt{I(\textnormal{W};\textnormal{Z}_{i})},

(12)

where

is the mutual information (i.e.,

•

\displaystyle\left|\mathbb{E}\left[L_{\mu}(\textnormal{W})-L_{\textnormal{S}}(\textnormal{W})\right]\right|\leq\frac{A}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\textnormal{T}}(\textnormal{W};\textnormal{Z}_{i}),

(13)

where

\displaystyle\left|\mathbb{E}\left[L_{\mu}(\textnormal{W})-L_{\textnormal{S}}(\textnormal{W})\right]\right|\leq\frac{\sqrt{\mathsf{Var}\left(\ell(\textnormal{W};\textnormal{Z})\right)}}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sqrt{\chi^{2}(\textnormal{W};\textnormal{Z}_{i})},

2.3
Strong Data Processing Inequalities

In order to characterize the time-decaying phenomenon, we use an information-theoretic tool: strong data processing inequalities
(
Dobrushin 1956
;
Cohen et al. 1998
)
.
We start with recalling the data processing inequality.

Lemma 3
.

If a Markov chain
\textnormal{U}\to\textnormal{X}\to\textnormal{Y}
holds, then

\displaystyle I_{f}(\textnormal{U};\textnormal{Y})\leq I_{f}(\textnormal{U};\textnormal{X}).

(15)

The data processing inequality states that no post-processing of
X
can increase the information about
U
. Under certain conditions, the data processing inequality can be sharpened, which leads to a strong data processing inequality, often cast in terms of a contraction coefficient. Next, we recall the contraction coefficients of
f
-divergences and show their connection with strong data processing inequalities.

For a given transition probability kernel
P_{\textnormal{Y}|\textnormal{X}}:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{Y})
where
\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{Y})
is the set of all distributions on
\mathcal{Y}
, let
P_{\textnormal{Y}|\textnormal{X}}\circ P
be the distribution on
\mathcal{Y}
induced by the push-forward of the distribution
P
(i.e., the distribution of
Y
when the distribution of
X
is
P
). The contraction coefficient of
P_{\textnormal{Y}|\textnormal{X}}
for
\textnormal{D}_{f}
is defined as

\displaystyle\eta_{f}(P_{\textnormal{Y}|\textnormal{X}})\triangleq\sup_{P,Q:P\neq Q}\frac{\textnormal{D}_{f}(P_{\textnormal{Y}|\textnormal{X}}\circ P\|P_{\textnormal{Y}|\textnormal{X}}\circ Q)}{\textnormal{D}_{f}(P\|Q)}\in[0,1].

In particular, when the total variation distance is used, the corresponding contraction coefficient
\eta_{\scalebox{.6}{{T}V}}(P_{\textnormal{Y}|\textnormal{X}})
is known as the Dobrushin’s coefficient
(
Dobrushin 1956
)
, which owns an equivalent expression:

\displaystyle\eta_{\scalebox{.6}{{T}V}}(P_{\textnormal{Y}|\textnormal{X}})=\sup_{\bm{x},\bm{x}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}}\textnormal{D}_{\scalebox{.6}{{T}V}}(P_{\textnormal{Y}|\textnormal{X}=\bm{x}}\|P_{\textnormal{Y}|\textnormal{X}=\bm{x}^{\prime}}).

(16)

Note that the Dobrushin’s coefficient upper bounds all other contraction coefficients
(
Cohen et al. 1998
)
:

\displaystyle\eta_{f}(P_{\textnormal{Y}|\textnormal{X}})\leq\eta_{\scalebox{.6}{{T}V}}(P_{\textnormal{Y}|\textnormal{X}}).

Furthermore, for any Markov chain
\textnormal{U}\to\textnormal{X}\to\textnormal{Y}
, the contraction coefficients satisfy
(see Theorem 5.2 in
Raginsky 2016
, for a proof)

\displaystyle I_{f}(\textnormal{U};\textnormal{Y})\leq\eta_{f}(P_{\textnormal{Y}|\textnormal{X}})\cdot I_{f}(\textnormal{U};\textnormal{X}).

(17)

When
\eta_{f}(P_{\textnormal{Y}|\textnormal{X}})<1
, the strict inequality
I_{f}(\textnormal{U};\textnormal{Y})<I_{f}(\textnormal{U};\textnormal{X})
improves the data processing inequality and, hence, is referred to as a strong data processing inequality. We refer the reader to
Polyanskiy and Wu 2016
and
Raginsky 2016
for a more comprehensive review on strong data processing inequalities and
Calmon et al. 2017
for non-linear strong data processing inequalities in Gaussian channels.

3
Properties of Additive Noise Channels

Additive noise channels have a long history in information theory. Here we show two important properties of additive noise channels which will be used for deriving the generalization bounds in the next section. The first property (Lemma
4
) leads to a decay factor into our bounds. The second property (Lemma
6
) produces computable generalization bounds.

Consider a single use of an additive noise channel. Let
(\textnormal{X},\textnormal{Y})
be a pair of random variables related by
\textnormal{Y}=\textnormal{X}+m\textnormal{N}
where
\textnormal{X}\in\mathcal{X}
;
m>0
is a constant; and
N
represents an independent noise. In other words, the conditional distribution of
Y
given
X
can be characterized by
P_{\textnormal{Y}|\textnormal{X}=\bm{x}}=P_{\bm{x}+m\textnormal{N}}
.
If
\mathcal{X}
is a compact set, the contraction coefficients often have a non-trivial upper bound, leading to a strong data processing inequality. This is formalized in the following lemma whose proof follows directly from the definition of the Dobrushin’s coefficient in (
16
) and the fact that the Dobrushin’s coefficient is a universal upper bound of all the contraction coefficients. Note that the function
\delta(A,m)
in (
18
) upper bounds the Dobrushin’s coefficient of the additive noise channel and has a closed-form expression for many noise distributions (see Table
1
for examples).

Lemma 4
.

Let
N
be a random variable which is independent of
(\textnormal{U},\textnormal{X})
. For a given norm
\|\cdot\|
on a compact set
\mathcal{X}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
m,A>0
, we define

\displaystyle\delta(A,m)

\displaystyle\triangleq\sup_{\|\bm{x}-\bm{x}^{\prime}\|\leq A}\textnormal{D}_{\scalebox{.6}{{T}V}}\left(P_{\bm{x}+m\textnormal{N}}\|P_{\bm{x}^{\prime}+m\textnormal{N}}\right).

(18)

Then the Markov chain
\textnormal{U}\to\textnormal{X}\to\textnormal{X}+m\textnormal{N}
holds and

\displaystyle I_{f}(\textnormal{U};\textnormal{X}+m\textnormal{N})\leq\delta(\mathsf{diam}(\mathcal{X}),m)\cdot I_{f}(\textnormal{U};\textnormal{X}),

(19)

where
\mathsf{diam}(\mathcal{X})\triangleq\sup_{\bm{x},\bm{x}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}}\|\bm{x}-\bm{x}^{\prime}\|
is the diameter of
\mathcal{X}
.

Remark 5
.

In the next section, we use Lemma
4
to incorporate a time-decaying factor into our generalization bounds. The function
\delta(A,m)
in (
18

(
Feldman et al. 2018
;
Asoodeh et al. 2020
)
. For example,
Feldman et al. 2018
proved that passing two probability distributions through a noisy iterative algorithm would shrink their Rényi divergence, leading to amplification for Rényi differential privacy.

Asoodeh et al. 2020
reformulated the definition of differential privacy by using the

divergence, which is a certain

-divergence. They characterized the contraction coefficient of

In this paper, we introduce a general framework for deriving generalization bounds through
f
-divergences. We leverage Dobrushin’s coefficient since it upper bounds all other contraction coefficients for
f
-divergences.

4
Generalization Bounds for Noisy Iterative Algorithms

In this section, we present our main result—generalization bounds for noisy iterative algorithms.
First, by leveraging strong data processing inequalities, we prove that the amount of information about the data points used in early iterations decays with time. Accordingly, our generalization bounds incorporate a time-decaying factor which enables the impact of early iterations on our bounds to reduce with time. Second, by using properties of additive noise channels developed in the last section, we further upper bound the
f
-information by a quantity which is often easier to estimate. The above two aspects correspond to Lemma
8
and
9
which are the basis of our main result in Theorem
10
.

Before diving into the analysis, we first discuss assumptions made in this paper.

Assumption 1

(Sampling w/o Replacement)
.

The mini-batch indices
(\mathcal{B}_{1},\cdots,\mathcal{B}_{T})
in (
3
) are specified before the algorithm is run and data are drawn without replacement.

If the mini-batches are selected when the algorithm is run, one can analyze the expected generalization gap by first conditioning on
\mathcal{B}\triangleq(\mathcal{B}_{1},\cdots,\mathcal{B}_{T})
and then taking an expectation over the randomness of
\mathcal{B}
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[L_{\mu}(\textnormal{W}_{T})-L_{\textnormal{S}}(\textnormal{W}_{T})\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbb{E}\left[L_{\mu}(\textnormal{W}_{T})-L_{\textnormal{S}}(\textnormal{W}_{T})\mid\mathcal{B}\right]\right].

Our analysis can be extended to the case where data are drawn with replacement (see Proposition
15
) by using the chain rule for mutual information.

Assumption 2

(Bounded Gradient & Compact Domain)
.

The parameter domain
\mathcal{W}
is compact and
\|g(\bm{w},\bm{z})\|\leq K
for all
\bm{w},\bm{z}
. We denote the diameter of
\mathcal{W}
by
D\triangleq\sup_{\bm{w},\bm{w}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{W}}\|\bm{w}-\bm{w}^{\prime}\|
.

Our generalization bounds rely on the second assumption mildly. In fact, this assumption only affects the time-decaying factor in our bounds which is always upper bounded by
1
. If we remove this assumption, our bounds still hold though the decay factor disappears.

Now we are in a position to derive generalization bounds under the above assumptions. As a consequence of strong data processing inequalities, the following lemma indicates that the information of a data point
\textnormal{Z}_{i}
contained in the algorithmic output
\textnormal{W}_{T}
will reduce with time
T
.

Lemma 8
.

Under Assumption
1
(Sampling w/o Replacement) and
2
(Bounded Gradient & Compact Domain), if a data point
\textnormal{Z}_{i}
is used in the
t
-th iteration, then

\displaystyle I_{f}(\textnormal{W}_{T};\textnormal{Z}_{i})\leq I_{f}(\textnormal{W}_{t};\textnormal{Z}_{i})\cdot\prod_{t^{\prime}=t+1}^{T}\delta(D+2\eta_{t^{\prime}}K,m_{t^{\prime}}),

(24)

where the function
\delta(\cdot,\cdot)
is defined in (
18
).

Proof.

For the
t
-th iteration, we rewrite the recursion in (
3
) as

\displaystyle\textnormal{U}_{t}=\textnormal{W}_{t-1}-\eta_{t}\cdot g(\textnormal{W}_{t-1},\{\textnormal{Z}_{i}\}_{i\in\mathcal{B}_{t}})

(25a)

\displaystyle\textnormal{V}_{t}=\textnormal{U}_{t}+m_{t}\cdot\textnormal{N}_{t}

(25b)

\displaystyle\textnormal{W}_{t}=\mathsf{Proj}_{\mathcal{W}}\left(\textnormal{V}_{t}\right).

(25c)

Let
\textnormal{Z}_{i}
be a data point used at the
t
-th iteration. Under Assumption
1
(Sampling w/o Replacement), the following Markov chain holds:

\displaystyle\textnormal{Z}_{i}\to\textnormal{U}_{t}\to\textnormal{V}_{t}\to\textnormal{W}_{t}\to\cdots\to\textnormal{W}_{T-1}\to\textnormal{U}_{T}\to\textnormal{V}_{T}\to\textnormal{W}_{T}.

(26)

Let
\mathcal{U}_{T}
be the range of
\textnormal{U}_{T}
. By Assumption
2
(Bounded Gradient & Compact Domain) and the triangle inequality,

\displaystyle\mathsf{diam}(\mathcal{U}_{T})\leq\mathsf{diam}(\mathcal{W})+2\eta_{T}K=D+2\eta_{T}K.

Now we leverage the strong data processing inequality in Lemma
4
and obtain

\displaystyle I_{f}(\textnormal{W}_{T};\textnormal{Z}_{i})

\displaystyle\leq I_{f}(\textnormal{V}_{T};\textnormal{Z}_{i})

\displaystyle\leq\delta(D+2\eta_{T}K,m_{T})\cdot I_{f}(\textnormal{U}_{T};\textnormal{Z}_{i})

\displaystyle\leq\delta(D+2\eta_{T}K,m_{T})\cdot I_{f}(\textnormal{W}_{T-1};\textnormal{Z}_{i}),

where the first and last steps are due to the data processing inequality (Lemma
3
). Applying this procedure recursively leads to the desired conclusion.
∎

For many types of noise (e.g., Gaussian or Laplace noise), the function

is
strictly
smaller than

(see Table

). In this case, the information about the data points used in early iterations is reducing via the multiplicative factor in (

as

if the magnitude of the additive noise in (
3

Lemma

explains how our generalization bounds in Theorem

incorporate a time-decaying factor. However, it still involves an

Under Assumption

\displaystyle I_{f}(\textnormal{W}_{t};\textnormal{Z}_{i})\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\mathsf{C}_{f}\left(g(\textnormal{W}_{t-1},\textnormal{Z}),g(\textnormal{W}_{t-1},\bar{\textnormal{Z}});\frac{m_{t}b_{t}}{\eta_{t}}\right)\right],

.

Suppose that Assumption

(35)

\displaystyle\frac{\sigma}{n}\sum_{t=1}^{T}b_{t}\sqrt{\mathbb{E}\left[\mathsf{C}_{\chi^{2}}\left(g(\textnormal{W}_{t-1},\textnormal{Z}),g(\textnormal{W}_{t-1},\bar{\textnormal{Z}});\frac{m_{t}b_{t}}{\eta_{t}}\right)\right]\prod_{t^{\prime}=t+1}^{T}\delta(D+2\eta_{t^{\prime}}K,m_{t^{\prime}})},

(36)

where

Our generalization bounds involve the information (e.g., step size
\eta_{t}
, magnitude of noise
m_{t}
, and batch size
b_{t}
) at all iterations. Moreover, our bounds depend on the data distribution
\mu
through the expectation terms. Under Assumption
1
(Sampling w/o Replacement), if a data point
\textnormal{Z}_{i}
is used in the
t
-th iteration, it is independent of
\textnormal{W}_{t-1}
(i.e.,
P_{\textnormal{W}_{t-1},\textnormal{Z}_{i}}=P_{\textnormal{W}_{t-1}}\otimes\mu
). This is why the expectations are taken over
(\textnormal{W}_{t-1},\textnormal{Z},\bar{\textnormal{Z}})\sim P_{\textnormal{W}_{t-1}}\otimes\mu\otimes\mu
. Finally, since the function
\delta
is often strictly smaller than
1
(see Table
1
for some examples), the multiplicative factor
\prod_{t^{\prime}=t+1}^{T}\delta(D+2\eta_{t^{\prime}}K,m_{t^{\prime}})
enables the impact of early iterations on our bounds to reduce with time
T
.

The generalization bounds in Theorem

may seem contrived at first glance as they rely on the functions
\delta
and
\mathsf{C}_{f}
defined in (

) and (

). However, in the next section, we will show that these bounds can be significantly simplified when we apply them to real applications. Furthermore, we will also compare the advantage of each bound under these applications.

We demonstrate the generalization bounds in Theorem

through several applications in this section.

5.1
Differentially Private Stochastic Gradient Descent (DP-SGD)

Differentially private stochastic gradient descent (DP-SGD) is a variant of SGD where noise is added to a stochastic gradient estimator in order to ensure privacy of each individual record. We recall an implementation of (projected) DP-SGD
(
Feldman et al. 2018
, see e.g., Algorithm 1 in)
. At each iteration, the parameter of the empirical risk is updated using the following rule:

\displaystyle\textnormal{W}_{t}=\mathsf{Proj}_{\mathcal{W}}\left(\textnormal{W}_{t-1}-\eta\left(g(\textnormal{W}_{t-1},\{\textnormal{Z}_{i}\}_{i\in\mathcal{B}_{t}})+\textnormal{N}_{t}\right)\right),

(37)

where
\textnormal{N}_{t}
is an additive noise drawn independently from a distribution
P_{\textnormal{N}}
;
\mathcal{B}_{t}
contains the indices of the data points used at the current iteration and
b_{t}\triangleq|\mathcal{B}_{t}|
; the function
g
indicates a direction for updating the parameter. The recursion in (
37
) is run for
T
iterations and we assume that data are drawn without replacement. At the end of each iteration, the parameter is projected onto a compact domain
\mathcal{W}
. We denote the diameter of
\mathcal{W}
by
D
. The output from the DP-SGD algorithm is the last iterate
\textnormal{W}_{T}
. Finally, we assume that

\displaystyle\sup_{\bm{w}\in\mathcal{W},\bm{z}\in\mathcal{Z}}\|g(\bm{w},\bm{z})\|\leq K.

(38)

This assumption can be satisfied by gradient clipping and is crucial for guaranteeing differential privacy as it controls the sensitivity of each update.

The differential privacy guarantees of the DP-SGD algorithm have been extensively studied in the literature
(
Song et al. 2013
;
Wu et al. 2017
;
Feldman et al. 2018
;
Balle et al. 2019
;
Asoodeh et al. 2020
, see e.g.,)
. Here we consider a different angle: the generalization of DP-SGD. We derive generalization bounds for the DP-SGD algorithm under Laplace and Gaussian mechanisms by using our Theorem
10
.

Proposition 11

(Laplace mechanism)
.

Suppose that the additive noise
\textnormal{N}_{t}
in (
37
) follows a standard multivariate Laplace distribution. Let
\mathcal{W}
be equipped with the 1-norm and
q\triangleq 1-\exp\left(-(D+2\eta K)/\eta\right)\in(0,1)
.

•

If the loss
\ell(\bm{w},\textnormal{Z})
is
\sigma
-sub-Gaussian under
\textnormal{Z}\sim\mu
for all
\bm{w}\in\mathcal{W}
, then

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[L_{\mu}(\textnormal{W}_{T})-L_{\textnormal{S}}(\textnormal{W}_{T})\right]\leq\frac{2\sigma}{n}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\sqrt{b_{t}\cdot\mathsf{mmae}\left(g(\textnormal{W}_{t-1},\textnormal{Z})\right)\cdot q^{T-t}}.

(39)

•

If the loss function is upper bounded by

5.3
Stochastic Gradient Langevin Dynamics (SGLD)

We analyze the generalization gap of the stochastic gradient Langevin dynamics (SGLD) algorithm
(
Gelfand and Mitter 1991
;
Welling and Teh 2011
)
. We start by recalling a standard framework of SGLD. The data set
S
is first divided into
m
disjoint mini-batches:

\displaystyle\textnormal{S}=\bigcup_{j=1}^{m}\textnormal{S}_{j},\quad\text{where }|\textnormal{S}_{j}|=b\text{ and }\textnormal{S}_{j}\cap\textnormal{S}_{k}=\emptyset\text{ for }j\neq k.

We initialize the parameter of the empirical risk with a random point
\textnormal{W}_{0}\in\mathcal{W}
and update using the following rule:

\displaystyle\textnormal{W}_{t}=\textnormal{W}_{t-1}-\eta_{t}\nabla_{\bm{w}}\hat{\ell}(\textnormal{W}_{t-1},\textnormal{S}_{B_{t}})+\sqrt{\frac{2\eta_{t}}{\beta_{t}}}\textnormal{N}_{t},

(45)

where
\eta_{t}
is the learning rate;
\beta_{t}
is the inverse temperature;
\textnormal{N}_{t}
is drawn independently from a standard Gaussian distribution;
B_{t}\in[m]
is the mini-batch index;
\hat{\ell}
is a surrogate loss (e.g., hinge loss); and

\displaystyle\nabla_{\bm{w}}\hat{\ell}(\textnormal{W}_{t-1},\textnormal{S}_{B_{t}})\triangleq\frac{1}{b}\sum_{\textnormal{Z}\in\textnormal{S}_{B_{t}}}\nabla_{\bm{w}}\hat{\ell}(\textnormal{W}_{t-1},\textnormal{Z}).

(46)

We study a general setting where the output from SGLD can be any function of the parameters across all iterations (i.e.,
\textnormal{W}=f(\textnormal{W}_{1},\cdots,\textnormal{W}_{T})
), including the setting considered before where
\textnormal{W}=\textnormal{W}_{T}
. For example, the output can be an average of all iterates (i.e., Polyak averaging)
\textnormal{W}=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t}\textnormal{W}_{t}
or the parameter which achieves the smallest value of the loss function
\textnormal{W}=\argmin_{\textnormal{W}_{t}}L_{\mu}(\textnormal{W}_{t})
.

Alas, Theorem
10
cannot be applied directly to the SGLD algorithm because the Markov chain in (
26
) does not hold any more when data are drawn with replacement. In order to circumvent this issue, we develop a different proof technique by using the chain rule for mutual information.

Our bound incorporates the gradient variance which measures a particular kind of “flatness” of the loss landscape. We note that a recent work
(
Jiang et al. 2020
)
has observed empirically that the variance of gradients is predictive of and highly correlated with the generalization gap of neural networks. Here we evidence this connection from a theoretical viewpoint by incorporating the gradient variance into the generalization bound.

Unfortunately, our generalization bound does not incorporate a decay factor anymore.

4

We note that the analysis in
Mou et al. 2018
requires
\textnormal{W}=\textnormal{W}_{T}
. Hence, in the setting we consider (i.e.,
W
is a function of
\textnormal{W}_{1},\cdots,\textnormal{W}_{T}
), it is unclear if it is possible to include a decay factor in the bound.
To understand why it happens, let us imagine an extreme scenario in which the SGLD algorithm outputs all the iterates (i.e.,
\textnormal{W}=(\textnormal{W}_{1},\cdots,\textnormal{W}_{T})
). For a data point
\textnormal{Z}_{i}
used at the
t
-th iteration, the data processing inequality implies that

\displaystyle I(\textnormal{W}_{1},\cdots,\textnormal{W}_{T};\textnormal{Z}_{i})\geq I(\textnormal{W}_{t};\textnormal{Z}_{i}).

Hence, it is impossible to have
I(\textnormal{W}_{1},\cdots,\textnormal{W}_{T};\textnormal{Z}_{i})\to 0
as

unless
I(\textnormal{W}_{t};\textnormal{Z}_{i})=0
.

Many existing SGLD generalization bounds
(
Mou et al. 2018
;
Li et al. 2020
;
Pensia et al. 2018
;
Negrea et al. 2019

are expressed as a sum of errors associated with each training iteration. In order to compare with these results, we present an analogous bound in the following corollary. This bound is obtained by combining a key lemma for proving Proposition

\displaystyle\frac{\sqrt{2}\sigma}{2}\min\left\{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\sqrt{\beta_{t}\eta_{t}\cdot\mathsf{Var}\left(\nabla_{\bm{w}}\hat{\ell}(\textnormal{W}_{t-1},\textnormal{Z}^{\dagger}_{t})\right)},\sqrt{\frac{1}{bn}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\beta_{t}\eta_{t}\cdot\mathsf{Var}\left(\nabla_{\bm{w}}\hat{\ell}(\textnormal{W}_{t-1},\textnormal{Z}^{\dagger}_{t})\right)}\right\},

Our bound is distribution-dependent through the variance of gradients in contrast with Corollary 1 of
Pensia et al. 2018
, Proposition 3 of
Bu et al. 2020
, and Theorem 1 of
Mou et al. 2018
, which rely on the Lipschitz constant:
\sup_{\bm{w},\bm{z}}\|\nabla_{\bm{w}}\hat{\ell}(\bm{w},\bm{z})\|_{2}
. These bounds fail to explain some generalization phenomena of DNNs, such as label corruption
(
Zhang et al. 2017a
)
, because the Lipschitz constant takes a supremum over all possible weight matrices
\bm{w}
and data points
\bm{z}
. In other words, this Lipschitz constant only relies on the architecture of the network instead of the weight matrices or data distribution. Hence, it is the same for a network trained from corrupted data and a network trained from true data. We remark that the Lipschitz constant used by
Pensia et al. 2018
;
Bu et al. 2020
;
Mou et al. 2018
is different from the Lipschitz constant of the function corresponding to a network w.r.t. the input variable. The latter one has been used in the literature
(
Bartlett et al. 2017
, see e.g.,)
for deriving generalization bounds and, to some degree, can capture generalization phenomena, such as label corruption.

The order of our generalization bound in Corollary
16
is
\min\Big(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\sqrt{\beta\eta_{t}},\sqrt{\frac{\beta}{bn}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\eta_{t}}\Big)
. It is tighter than Theorem 2 of
Mou et al. 2018
whose order is
\sqrt{\frac{\beta}{n}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\eta_{t}}
. Our bound is applicable regardless of the choice of learning rate while the bound in
Li et al. 2020
requires the scale of the learning rate to be upper bounded by the reciprocal of the Lipschitz constant. Our Corollary
16
has the same order with
Negrea et al. 2019
but we incorporate an additional decay factor when applying our bounds to the DP-SGD algorithm (see Proposition
12
) and numerical experiments suggest that our bound is more favourably correlated with the true generalization gap (see Table
2
).

7
Conclusion

In this paper, we investigate the generalization of noisy iterative algorithms and derive three generalization bounds based on different
f
-divergence.
We establish a unified framework and leverage fundamental tools from information theory (e.g., strong data processing inequalities and properties of additive noise channels) for proving these bounds.
We demonstrate our generalization bounds through applications, including DP-SGD, FL, and SGLD, in which our bounds own a simple form and can be estimated from data. Numerical experiments suggest that our bounds can predict the behavior of the true generalization gap.

B.1
Proof of Lemma
6

Lemma
6
follows from a slight tweak of the proof of Theorem 4 in
Polyanskiy and Wu 2016
.

Proof.

First, we choose a coupling
P_{\textnormal{X},\textnormal{X}^{\prime}}
, which has marginals
P_{\textnormal{X}}
and
P_{\textnormal{X}^{\prime}}
. The probability distribution of
\textnormal{X}+m\textnormal{N}
can be written as the convolution of
P_{\textnormal{X}}
and
P_{m\textnormal{N}}
. Specifically, for any measurable set
\mathcal{A}\subseteq\mathcal{X}
,

\displaystyle P_{\textnormal{X}+m\textnormal{N}}(\mathcal{A})=\int_{\bm{y}\in\mathcal{A}}\int_{\bm{x}\in\mathcal{X}}\textrm{d}P_{m\textnormal{N}}(\bm{y}-\bm{x})\textrm{d}P_{\textnormal{X}}(\bm{x})=\int_{\bm{y}\in\mathcal{A}}\int_{\bm{x}\in\mathcal{X}}\int_{\bm{x}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}}\textrm{d}P_{\bm{x}+m\textnormal{N}}(\bm{y})\textrm{d}P_{\textnormal{X},\textnormal{X}^{\prime}}(\bm{x},\bm{x}^{\prime}).

Similarly, we have

\displaystyle P_{\textnormal{X}^{\prime}+m\textnormal{N}}(\mathcal{A})

\displaystyle=\int_{\bm{y}\in\mathcal{A}}\int_{\bm{x}\in\mathcal{X}}\int_{\bm{x}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}}\textrm{d}P_{\bm{x}^{\prime}+m\textnormal{N}}(\bm{y})\textrm{d}P_{\textnormal{X},\textnormal{X}^{\prime}}(\bm{x},\bm{x}^{\prime}).

Since the mapping
(P,Q)\to\textnormal{D}_{f}(P\|Q)
is convex
(see Theorem 6.1 in
Polyanskiy and Wu 2019
, for a proof)
, Jensen’s inequality yields

\displaystyle\textnormal{D}_{f}\left(P_{\textnormal{X}+m\textnormal{N}}\|P_{\textnormal{X}^{\prime}+m\textnormal{N}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\bm{x}\in\mathcal{X}}\int_{\bm{x}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}}\textnormal{D}_{f}(P_{\bm{x}+m\textnormal{N}}\|P_{\bm{x}^{\prime}+m\textnormal{N}})\textrm{d}P_{\textnormal{X},\textnormal{X}^{\prime}}(\bm{x},\bm{x}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\mathsf{C}_{f}(\textnormal{X},\textnormal{X}^{\prime};m)\right],

(49)

where the last step follows from the definition. The left-hand side of (
49
) only relies on the marginal distributions of
X
and
\textnormal{X}^{\prime}
, so taking the infimum on both sides of (
49
) over all couplings
P_{\textnormal{X},\textnormal{X}^{\prime}}
leads to the desired conclusion.
∎

B.2
A Useful Property

We derive a useful property of
\mathsf{C}_{f}(\bm{x},\bm{y};m)
which will be used in our proofs.

Lemma 17
.

For any
\bm{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
a>0
, the function
\mathsf{C}_{f}(\bm{x},\bm{x}^{\prime};m)
in (
20
) satisfies

\displaystyle\mathsf{C}_{f}(a\bm{x}+\bm{z},a\bm{x}^{\prime}+\bm{z};m)=\mathsf{C}_{f}\left(\bm{x},\bm{x}^{\prime};\frac{m}{a}\right),\quad\mathsf{C}_{f}(\bm{x},\bm{x}^{\prime};m)=\mathsf{C}_{f}(-\bm{x}^{\prime},-\bm{x};m).

Proof.

For simplicity, we assume that
N
is a continuous random variable in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with probability density function (PDF)
p(\bm{w})
. Then the PDFs of
a\bm{x}+\bm{z}+m\textnormal{N}
and
a\bm{x}^{\prime}+\bm{z}+m\textnormal{N}
are

\displaystyle\frac{1}{m^{d}}\cdot p\left(\frac{\bm{w}-a\bm{x}-\bm{z}}{m}\right)\quad\text{and}\quad\frac{1}{m^{d}}\cdot p\left(\frac{\bm{w}-a\bm{x}^{\prime}-\bm{z}}{m}\right).

By definition,

\displaystyle\mathsf{C}_{f}(a\bm{x}+\bm{z},a\bm{x}^{\prime}+\bm{z};m)

\displaystyle=\textnormal{D}_{f}(P_{a\bm{x}+\bm{z}+m\textnormal{N}}\|P_{a\bm{x}^{\prime}+\bm{z}+m\textnormal{N}})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{m^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}p\left(\frac{\bm{w}-a\bm{x}^{\prime}-\bm{z}}{m}\right)f\left(\frac{p\left(\frac{\bm{w}-a\bm{x}-\bm{z}}{m}\right)}{p\left(\frac{\bm{w}-a\bm{x}^{\prime}-\bm{z}}{m}\right)}\right)\textrm{d}\bm{w}.

(50)

Let
\bm{v}=(\bm{w}-\bm{z})/a
. Then (
50
) is equal to

\displaystyle\frac{a^{d}}{m^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}p\left(\frac{\bm{v}-\bm{x}^{\prime}}{m/a}\right)f\left(\frac{p\left(\frac{\bm{v}-\bm{x}}{m/a}\right)}{p\left(\frac{\bm{v}-\bm{x}^{\prime}}{m/a}\right)}\right)\textrm{d}\bm{v}=\mathsf{C}_{f}\left(\bm{x},\bm{x}^{\prime};\frac{m}{a}\right).

Therefore,
\mathsf{C}_{f}(a\bm{x}+\bm{z},a\bm{x}^{\prime}+\bm{z};m)=\mathsf{C}_{f}\left(\bm{x},\bm{x}^{\prime};\frac{m}{a}\right)
. By choosing
a=1
and
\bm{z}=-\bm{x}-\bm{x}^{\prime}
, we have
\mathsf{C}_{f}(-\bm{x}^{\prime},-\bm{x};m)=\mathsf{C}(\bm{x},\bm{x}^{\prime};m)
.
∎

We first derive closed-form expressions (or upper bounds) for the function
\delta(A,m)
. The closed-form expression of
\delta(A,m)
for uniform distribution can be naturally obtained from its definition so we skip the proof. The closed-form expressions for standard multivariate Gaussian distribution and standard univariate Laplace distribution can be found at
Polyanskiy and Wu 2016
and
Asoodeh et al. 2020

In what follows, we provide an upper bound for
\delta(A,m)
when
N
follows a standard multivariate Laplace distribution.

Proof.

For a given positive number
A
and a random variable
N
which follows a standard multivariate Laplace distribution, consider the following optimization problem:

\displaystyle\sup_{\|\bm{v}\|_{1}\leq A}\textnormal{D}_{\scalebox{.6}{{T}V}}\left(P_{\textnormal{N}}\|P_{\bm{v}+\textnormal{N}}\right)=\sup_{\|\bm{v}\|_{1}\leq A}\mathbb{E}\left[\left(1-\frac{\exp(-\|\textnormal{N}-\bm{v}\|_{1})}{\exp(-\|\textnormal{N}\|_{1})}\right)\mathbb{I}_{\|\textnormal{N}-\bm{v}\|_{1}\geq\|\textnormal{N}\|_{1}}\right].

By exchanging the supremum and the expectation, we have

\displaystyle\sup_{\|\bm{v}\|_{1}\leq A}\textnormal{D}_{\scalebox{.6}{{T}V}}\left(P_{\textnormal{N}}\|P_{\bm{v}+\textnormal{N}}\right)\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{\|\bm{v}\|_{1}\leq A}\left\{\left(1-\frac{\exp(-\|\textnormal{N}-\bm{v}\|_{1})}{\exp(-\|\textnormal{N}\|_{1})}\right)\mathbb{I}_{\|\textnormal{N}-\bm{v}\|_{1}\geq\|\textnormal{N}\|_{1}}\right\}\right].

(51)

Note that

\displaystyle\sup_{\|\bm{v}\|_{1}\leq A}\left\{\left(1-\frac{\exp(-\|\textnormal{N}-\bm{v}\|_{1})}{\exp(-\|\textnormal{N}\|_{1})}\right)\mathbb{I}_{\|\textnormal{N}-\bm{v}\|_{1}\geq\|\textnormal{N}\|_{1}}\right\}

\displaystyle=1-\exp\left(-\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}\|\bm{v}\|_{1}\leq A\end{subarray}}\left\{\|\textnormal{N}-\bm{v}\|_{1}-\|\textnormal{N}\|_{1}\right\}\right)=1-\exp(-A).

Substituting this equality into (
51
) gives

\displaystyle\sup_{\|\bm{x}-\bm{x}^{\prime}\|_{1}\leq A}\textnormal{D}_{\scalebox{.6}{{T}V}}\left(P_{\bm{x}+\textnormal{N}}\|P_{\bm{x}^{\prime}+\textnormal{N}}\right)=\sup_{\|\bm{v}\|_{1}\leq A}\textnormal{D}_{\scalebox{.6}{{T}V}}\left(P_{\textnormal{N}}\|P_{\bm{v}+\textnormal{N}}\right)\leq 1-\exp(-A),

which leads to
\delta(A,1)\leq 1-\exp(-A)
. Finally, we have

\displaystyle\delta(A,m)=\delta\left(\frac{A}{m},1\right)\leq 1-\exp\left(-\frac{A}{m}\right).

∎

Now we consider the function
\mathsf{C}_{f}(\bm{x},\bm{x}^{\prime};m)
.

Proof.

By Lemma
17
, we have

\displaystyle\mathsf{C}_{\scalebox{.6}{{K}L}}(\bm{x},\bm{x}^{\prime};m)=\mathsf{C}_{\scalebox{.6}{{K}L}}\left(0,\frac{\bm{x}^{\prime}-\bm{x}}{m};1\right).

We denote
(\bm{x}^{\prime}-\bm{x})/m
by
\bm{v}
. Since all the coordinates of
\textnormal{N}=(\textnormal{N}_{1}\cdots,\textnormal{N}_{d})
are mutually independent,
P_{\textnormal{N}}=P_{\textnormal{N}_{1}}\cdots P_{\textnormal{N}_{d}}
and
P_{\bm{v}+\textnormal{N}}=P_{v_{1}+\textnormal{N}_{1}}\cdots P_{v_{d}+\textnormal{N}_{d}}
. By the chain rule of KL-divergence, we have

\displaystyle\mathsf{C}_{\scalebox{.6}{{K}L}}(\bm{x},\bm{x}^{\prime};m)=\textnormal{D}_{\scalebox{.6}{{K}L}}(P_{\textnormal{N}}\|P_{\bm{v}+\textnormal{N}})=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\textnormal{D}_{\scalebox{.6}{{K}L}}(P_{\textnormal{N}_{i}}\|P_{v_{i}+\textnormal{N}_{i}}).

(52)

Hence, we only need to calculate
\textnormal{D}_{\scalebox{.6}{{K}L}}(P_{\textnormal{N}}\|P_{v+\textnormal{N}})
for a constant

and a random variable
\textnormal{N}\in\mathbb{R}

(1) If
N
follows a standard Gaussian distribution, then

\displaystyle\textnormal{D}_{\scalebox{.6}{{K}L}}(P_{\textnormal{N}}\|P_{v+\textnormal{N}})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\log\frac{\exp(-\textnormal{N}^{2}/2)}{\exp(-(\textnormal{N}-v)^{2}/2)}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}\left[(\textnormal{N}-v)^{2}-\textnormal{N}^{2}\right]=\frac{v^{2}}{2}.

Substituting this equality into (

\displaystyle\mathsf{C}_{\scalebox{.6}{{K}L}}(\bm{x},\bm{x}^{\prime};m)=\frac{\|\bm{v}\|_{2}^{2}}{2}=\frac{\|\bm{x}-\bm{x}^{\prime}\|_{2}^{2}}{2m^{2}},

where the last step is due to the definition of
\bm{v}
.

(2) If
N
follows a standard Laplace distribution, then

\displaystyle\textnormal{D}_{\scalebox{.6}{{K}L}}(P_{\textnormal{N}}\|P_{v+\textnormal{N}})=\mathbb{E}\left[\log\frac{\exp(-|\textnormal{N}|)}{\exp(-|\textnormal{N}-v|)}\right]=|v|+\exp(-|v|)-1.

Substituting this equality into (
52
) gives

\displaystyle\mathsf{C}_{\scalebox{.6}{{K}L}}(\bm{x},\bm{x}^{\prime};m)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{d}|v_{i}|+\exp(-|v_{i}|)-1=\frac{\|\bm{x}-\bm{x}^{\prime}\|_{1}}{m}+\sum_{i=1}^{d}\left(\exp\left(-\frac{|x_{i}-x^{\prime}_{i}|}{m}\right)-1\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\|\bm{x}-\bm{x}^{\prime}\|_{1}}{m}.

Similarly, by Lemma
17

\displaystyle\mathsf{C}_{\chi^{2}}(\bm{x},\bm{x}^{\prime};m)=\mathsf{C}_{\chi^{2}}\left(0,\frac{\bm{x}^{\prime}-\bm{x}}{m};1\right).

We denote
(\bm{x}^{\prime}-\bm{x})/m
by
\bm{v}

\displaystyle\mathsf{C}_{\chi^{2}}(\bm{x},\bm{x}^{\prime};m)=\textnormal{D}_{\chi^{2}}(P_{\textnormal{N}}\|P_{\bm{v}+\textnormal{N}})=\prod_{i=1}^{d}\left(1+\textnormal{D}_{\chi^{2}}(P_{\textnormal{N}_{i}}\|P_{v_{i}+\textnormal{N}_{i}})\right)-1.

(1) If
N

\displaystyle\textnormal{D}_{\chi^{2}}(P_{\textnormal{N}}\|P_{v+\textnormal{N}})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{\exp(-\textnormal{N}^{2}/2)}{\exp(-(\textnormal{N}-v)^{2}/2)}\right]-1

\displaystyle=\exp(v^{2}/2)\mathbb{E}\left[\exp(-vN)\right]-1=\exp(v^{2})-1.

\displaystyle\mathsf{C}_{\chi^{2}}(\bm{x},\bm{x}^{\prime};m)=\exp(\|\bm{v}\|_{2}^{2})-1=\exp\left(\frac{\|\bm{x}-\bm{x}^{\prime}\|_{2}^{2}}{m^{2}}\right)-1.

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{3}\exp(|v|)+\frac{1}{3}\exp(-2|v|)-1.

Substituting this equality into (

\displaystyle\mathsf{C}_{\chi^{2}}(\bm{x},\bm{x}^{\prime};m)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(\frac{4\|g(\textnormal{W}_{t-1},\textnormal{Z})-\bm{e}\|_{2}^{2}}{b_{t}^{2}}\right)\right].

(68)

By choosing the constant vector
\bm{e}=\mathbb{E}\left[g(\textnormal{W}_{t-1},\textnormal{Z})\right]
and combining (
67
) with (
68
), we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\mathsf{C}_{\chi^{2}}\left(g(\textnormal{W}_{t-1},\textnormal{Z}),g(\textnormal{W}_{t-1},\bar{\textnormal{Z}});b_{t}\right)\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(\frac{4\|g(\textnormal{W}_{t-1},\textnormal{Z})-\bm{e}\|_{2}^{2}}{b_{t}^{2}}\right)\right]-1.

(69)

Since for any
x\geq 0
and
b\geq 1
,

\displaystyle\exp\left(\frac{x}{b}\right)-1\leq\frac{\exp(x)-1}{b},

the inequality in (
69
) can be further upper bounded as

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\mathsf{C}_{\chi^{2}}\left(g(\textnormal{W}_{t-1},\textnormal{Z}),g(\textnormal{W}_{t-1},\bar{\textnormal{Z}});b_{t}\right)\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{b_{t}^{2}}\left(\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(4\|g(\textnormal{W}_{t-1},\textnormal{Z})-\bm{e}\|_{2}^{2}\right)\right]-1\right).

(70)

Substituting (
59
), (
70
) into (
58
) leads to the generalization bound in (
44
).

∎

for the Laplace mechanism.

Proof.

Within the

as follows. The parameter is initialized by
\textnormal{W}_{t,0}^{l}=\textnormal{W}_{t-1}
and for
j\in[M]
,

\displaystyle\textnormal{U}_{t,j}^{l}=\textnormal{W}_{t,j-1}^{l}-\eta\cdot g\left(\textnormal{W}_{t,j-1}^{l},\{\textnormal{Z}_{i}^{l}\}_{i\in[b]}\right)

(71a)

\displaystyle\textnormal{V}_{t,j}^{l}=\textnormal{U}_{t,j}^{l}+\eta\cdot\textnormal{N}_{t,j}^{l}

(71b)

\displaystyle\textnormal{W}_{t,j}^{l}=\mathsf{Proj}_{\mathcal{W}}\left(\textnormal{V}_{t,j}^{l}\right)

(71c)

where
\{\textnormal{Z}_{i}^{l}\}_{i\in[b]}
are drawn independently from the data distribution
\mu_{l}
and
\textnormal{N}_{t,j}^{l}\sim N(0,\mathbf{I}_{d})
. If a data point
\textnormal{Z}_{i}^{k}
is used at the
t
-th global update,
j
-th local update, by the client
k\in\mathcal{S}
, then the following Markov chain holds:

\displaystyle\underbrace{\textnormal{Z}_{i}^{k}\to\{\textnormal{U}_{t,j}^{l}\}_{l\in\mathcal{S}_{t}}\to\{\textnormal{V}_{t,j}^{l}\}_{l\in\mathcal{S}_{t}}\to\{\textnormal{W}_{t,j}^{l}\}_{l\in\mathcal{S}_{t}}\to\cdots\to\{\textnormal{W}_{t,M}^{l}\}_{l\in\mathcal{S}_{t}}}_{\text{local}}\underbrace{\to\textnormal{W}_{t}}_{\text{global}}\to\cdots\to\textnormal{W}_{T}

Let
\mathcal{U}_{t,j}^{l}
be the range of
\textnormal{U}_{t,j}^{l}
. Note that
\mathsf{diam}\left(\mathcal{U}_{t,j}^{l}\right)\leq\mathsf{diam}(\mathcal{W})+2\eta K=D+2\eta K

, then

\displaystyle\mathsf{diam}\left(\prod_{l\in\mathcal{S}_{t}}\mathcal{U}_{t,j}^{l}\right)\leq\sqrt{\sum_{l\in\mathcal{S}_{t}}\mathsf{diam}\left(\mathcal{U}_{t,j}^{l}\right)^{2}}\leq\sqrt{C}(D+2\eta K).

Following a similar analysis in the proof of Lemma
8
, we have

\displaystyle{\textnormal{T}}(\textnormal{W}_{T};\textnormal{Z}_{i}^{k})

\displaystyle\leq q^{M(T-t)}\cdot{\textnormal{T}}(\textnormal{W}_{t};\textnormal{Z}_{i}^{k})

\displaystyle\leq q^{(M-j)+M(T-t)}\cdot{\textnormal{T}}(\{\textnormal{W}_{t,j}^{l}\}_{l\in\mathcal{S}_{t}};\textnormal{Z}_{i}^{k}),

(72)

where the constant

\displaystyle q\triangleq 1-2\bar{\Phi}\left(\frac{\sqrt{C}(D+2\eta K)}{2\eta}\right).

\displaystyle{\textnormal{T}}(\{\textnormal{W}_{t,j}^{l}\}_{l\in\mathcal{S}_{t}};\textnormal{Z}_{i}^{k})\leq\frac{1}{b}\mathbb{E}\left[\|g(\textnormal{W}_{t,j-1}^{k},\textnormal{Z}^{k})-\bm{e}\|_{2}\right]

where

\textnormal{Z}_{i}^{k}
is only used by the client
k\in\mathcal{S}_{t}
, the right-hand side of (

) does not involve

. Combining (

), (

) with the

D.3
Proof of Proposition
15

We first present the following lemma whose proof follows by using the technique in Section II. E of
Guo et al. 2005
.

Lemma 19
.

Let
X
be a random variable which is independent of
\textnormal{N}\sim N(0,\mathbf{I}_{d})
. Then for any
m>0
and deterministic function
f

\displaystyle I(f(\textnormal{X})+m\textnormal{N};\textnormal{X})\leq\frac{1}{2m^{2}}\mathsf{Var}\left(f(\textnormal{X})\right).

(74)

More generally, if
Z
is another random variable which is independent of
N
, then for any fixed
\bm{z}

\displaystyle I(f(\textnormal{X})+m\textnormal{N};\textnormal{X}\mid\textnormal{Z}=\bm{z})\leq\frac{1}{2m^{2}}\mathsf{Var}\left(f(\textnormal{X})\mid\textnormal{Z}=\bm{z}\right).

(75)

Proof.

By the property of mutual information
(
Polyanskiy and Wu 2019
, see Theorem 2.3 in)
,

\displaystyle I(f(\textnormal{X})+m\textnormal{N};\textnormal{X})=I\left(\frac{f(\textnormal{X})-\bm{e}}{m}+\textnormal{N};\textnormal{X}\right)

(76)

where
\bm{e}\triangleq\mathbb{E}\left[f(\textnormal{X})\right]
. We denote

\displaystyle g(\bm{x})\triangleq\frac{f(\bm{x})-\bm{e}}{m}.

(77)

The golden formula
(see Theorem 3.3 in
Polyanskiy and Wu 2019
, for a proof)
yields

\displaystyle I\left(g(\textnormal{X})+\textnormal{N};\textnormal{X}\right)

\displaystyle=\textnormal{D}_{\scalebox{.6}{{K}L}}\left(P_{g(\textnormal{X})+\textnormal{N}|\textnormal{X}}\|P_{\textnormal{N}}|P_{\textnormal{X}}\right)-\textnormal{D}_{\scalebox{.6}{{K}L}}\left(P_{g(\textnormal{X})+\textnormal{N}}\|P_{\textnormal{N}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\textnormal{D}_{\scalebox{.6}{{K}L}}\left(P_{g(\textnormal{X})+\textnormal{N}|\textnormal{X}}\|P_{\textnormal{N}}|P_{\textnormal{X}}\right).

(78)

Furthermore, since
X
and
N
are independent, we have

\displaystyle\textnormal{D}_{\scalebox{.6}{{K}L}}\left(P_{g(\textnormal{X})+\textnormal{N}|\textnormal{X}=\bm{x}}\|P_{\textnormal{N}}\right)=\textnormal{D}_{\scalebox{.6}{{K}L}}\left(P_{g(\bm{x})+\textnormal{N}}\|P_{\textnormal{N}}\right)=\frac{\|g(\bm{x})\|_{2}^{2}}{2},

where the last step is due to the closed-form expression of the KL-divergence between two Gaussian distributions. Finally, by the definition of conditional divergence, we have

\displaystyle\textnormal{D}_{\scalebox{.6}{{K}L}}\left(P_{g(\textnormal{X})+\textnormal{N}|\textnormal{X}}\|P_{\textnormal{N}}|P_{\textnormal{X}}\right)=\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}\left[\|g(\textnormal{X})\|_{2}^{2}\right]=\frac{1}{2m^{2}}\mathsf{Var}\left(f(\textnormal{X})\right),

(79)

where the last step is due to the definition of
g
in (
77
). Combining (
76
–
79
) leads to the desired conclusion. Finally, it is straightforward to obtain (
75
) by conditioning on
\textnormal{Z}=\bm{z}
and repeating our above derivations.
∎

Next, we present the second lemma which will be used for proving Proposition
15
.

Lemma 20
.

If the loss function
\ell(\bm{w},\textnormal{Z})
is
\sigma
-sub-Gaussian under
\textnormal{Z}\sim\mu
for all
\bm{w}\in\mathcal{W}
, the expected generalization gap of the SGLD algorithm can be upper bounded by

\displaystyle\frac{\sqrt{2}\sigma}{2n}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\sqrt{\sum_{t\in\mathcal{T}_{j}}\beta_{t}\eta_{t}\cdot\mathsf{Var}\left(\nabla_{\bm{w}}\hat{\ell}(\textnormal{W}_{t-1},\bar{\textnormal{Z}}_{j})\right)},

where the set
\mathcal{T}_{j}
contains the indices of iterations in which the mini-batch
\textnormal{S}_{j}
is used and the variance is over the randomness of
(\textnormal{W}_{t-1},\bar{\textnormal{Z}}_{j})\sim P_{\textnormal{W}_{t-1},\bar{\textnormal{Z}}_{j}}
with
\bar{\textnormal{Z}}_{j}
being any data point in the mini-batch
\textnormal{S}_{j}
.

Proof.

We denote
\textnormal{Z}^{(k)}\triangleq(\textnormal{Z}_{1},\cdots,\textnormal{Z}_{k})
for
k\in[n]
and
\textnormal{W}^{(t)}\triangleq(\textnormal{W}_{1},\cdots,\textnormal{W}_{t})
for
t\in[T]
. For simplicity, in what follows we only provide an upper bound for
I(\textnormal{W};\textnormal{Z}_{n})
. Since
W
is a function of
\textnormal{W}^{(T)}=(\textnormal{W}_{1},\cdots,\textnormal{W}_{T})
, the data processing inequality yields

\displaystyle I(\textnormal{W};\textnormal{Z}_{n})\leq I(\textnormal{W}^{(T)};\textnormal{Z}_{n})\leq I(\textnormal{W}^{(T)},\textnormal{Z}^{(n-1)};\textnormal{Z}_{n}).

(80)

By the chain rule,

\displaystyle I(\textnormal{W}^{(T)},\textnormal{Z}^{(n-1)};\textnormal{Z}_{n})=I(\textnormal{W}_{T};\textnormal{Z}_{n}\mid\textnormal{W}^{(T-1)},\textnormal{Z}^{(n-1)})+I(\textnormal{W}^{(T-1)},\textnormal{Z}^{(n-1)};\textnormal{Z}_{n}).

(81)

Let
\bm{w}=(\bm{w}_{1},\cdots,\bm{w}_{T-1})
and
\bm{z}=(\bm{z}_{1},\cdots,\bm{z}_{n-1})
be any two vectors. If
\textnormal{Z}_{n}
is not used at the
T
-th iteration, without loss of generality we assume that the data points
\textnormal{Z}_{1},\cdots,\textnormal{Z}_{b}
are used in this iteration. Then

\displaystyle I(\textnormal{W}_{T};\textnormal{Z}_{n}\mid\textnormal{W}^{(T-1)}=\bm{w},\textnormal{Z}^{(n-1)}=\bm{z})

\displaystyle=I\left(\bm{w}_{T-1}-\frac{\eta_{T}}{b}\sum_{i=1}^{b}\nabla_{\bm{w}}\hat{\ell}(\bm{w}_{T-1},\bm{z}_{i})+\sqrt{\frac{2\eta_{T}}{\beta_{T}}}\textnormal{N}_{T};\textnormal{Z}_{n}\mid\textnormal{W}^{(T-1)}=\bm{w},\textnormal{Z}^{(n-1)}=\bm{z}\right)

\displaystyle=I\left(\textnormal{N}_{T};\textnormal{Z}_{n}\mid\textnormal{W}^{(T-1)}=\bm{w},\textnormal{Z}^{(n-1)}=\bm{z}\right)

\displaystyle=0.

(82)

On the other hand, if
\textnormal{Z}_{n}
is used at the
T
-th iteration, without loss of generality we assume that the other
b-1
data points which are also used in this iteration are
\textnormal{Z}_{1},\cdots,\textnormal{Z}_{b-1}
. Then

\displaystyle I(\textnormal{W}_{T};\textnormal{Z}_{n}\mid\textnormal{W}^{(T-1)}=\bm{w},\textnormal{Z}^{(n-1)}=\bm{z})

\displaystyle=I\left(\bm{w}_{T-1}-\frac{\eta_{T}}{b}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{b-1}\nabla_{\bm{w}}\hat{\ell}(\bm{w}_{T-1},\bm{z}_{i})+\nabla_{\bm{w}}\hat{\ell}(\bm{w}_{T-1},\textnormal{Z}_{n})\right)+\sqrt{\frac{2\eta_{T}}{\beta_{T}}}\textnormal{N}_{T};\textnormal{Z}_{n}\mid\textnormal{W}^{(T-1)}=\bm{w},\textnormal{Z}^{(n-1)}=\bm{z}\right)

\displaystyle=I\left(-\frac{\eta_{T}}{b}\nabla_{\bm{w}}\hat{\ell}(\bm{w}_{T-1},\textnormal{Z}_{n})+\sqrt{\frac{2\eta_{T}}{\beta_{T}}}\textnormal{N}_{T};\textnormal{Z}_{n}\mid\textnormal{W}^{(T-1)}=\bm{w},\textnormal{Z}^{(n-1)}=\bm{z}\right).

(83)

By Lemma
19
, we have

\displaystyle I\left(-\frac{\eta_{T}}{b}\nabla_{\bm{w}}\hat{\ell}(\bm{w}_{T-1},\textnormal{Z}_{n})+\sqrt{\frac{2\eta_{T}}{\beta_{T}}}\textnormal{N}_{T};\textnormal{Z}_{n}\mid\textnormal{W}^{(T-1)}=\bm{w},\textnormal{Z}^{(n-1)}=\bm{z}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\beta_{T}\eta_{T}}{4b^{2}}\mathsf{Var}\left(\nabla_{\bm{w}}\hat{\ell}(\bm{w}_{T-1},\textnormal{Z}_{n})\mid\textnormal{W}^{(T-1)}=\bm{w},\textnormal{Z}^{(n-1)}=\bm{z}\right).

(84)

Substituting (
84
) into (
83
) gives

\displaystyle I(\textnormal{W}_{T};\textnormal{Z}_{n}\mid\textnormal{W}^{(T-1)}=\bm{w},\textnormal{Z}^{(n-1)}=\bm{z})\leq\frac{\beta_{T}\eta_{T}}{4b^{2}}\mathsf{Var}\left(\nabla_{\bm{w}}\hat{\ell}(\bm{w}_{T-1},\textnormal{Z}_{n})\mid\textnormal{W}^{(T-1)}=\bm{w},\textnormal{Z}^{(n-1)}=\bm{z}\right).

Taking expectation w.r.t.
(\textnormal{W}^{(T-1)},\textnormal{Z}^{(n-1)})
on both sides of the above inequality and using the law of total variance lead to

\displaystyle I(\textnormal{W}_{T};\textnormal{Z}_{n}\mid\textnormal{W}^{(T-1)},\textnormal{Z}^{(n-1)})\leq\frac{\beta_{T}\eta_{T}}{4b^{2}}\mathsf{Var}\left(\nabla_{\bm{w}}\hat{\ell}(\textnormal{W}_{T-1},\textnormal{Z}_{n})\right).

(85)

To summarize, (
82
) and (
85
) can be rewritten as

\displaystyle I(\textnormal{W}_{T};\textnormal{Z}_{n}\mid\textnormal{W}^{(T-1)},\textnormal{Z}^{(n-1)})

(86)

\displaystyle\leq\begin{cases}\frac{\beta_{T}\eta_{T}}{4b^{2}}\mathsf{Var}\left(\nabla_{\bm{w}}\hat{\ell}(\textnormal{W}_{T-1},\textnormal{Z}_{n})\right)\quad&\text{if }\textnormal{Z}_{n}\text{ is used at the }T\text{-th iteration},\\
0&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

Assume that the data point
\textnormal{Z}_{n}
belongs to the
j
-th mini-batch
\textnormal{S}_{j}
. Now substituting (
86
) into (
81
) and doing this procedure recursively lead to

\displaystyle I(\textnormal{W}^{(T)},\textnormal{Z}^{(n-1)};\textnormal{Z}_{n})\leq\sum_{t\in\mathcal{T}_{j}}\frac{\beta_{t}\eta_{t}}{4b^{2}}\mathsf{Var}\left(\nabla_{\bm{w}}\hat{\ell}(\textnormal{W}_{t-1},\textnormal{Z}_{n})\right),

where the set
\mathcal{T}_{j}
contains the indices of iterations in which the mini-batch
\textnormal{S}_{j}
is used. Hence, this upper bound along with (
80
) naturally gives

\displaystyle I(\textnormal{W};\textnormal{Z}_{n})\leq\sum_{t\in\mathcal{T}_{j}}\frac{\beta_{t}\eta_{t}}{4b^{2}}\mathsf{Var}\left(\nabla_{\bm{w}}\hat{\ell}(\textnormal{W}_{t-1},\textnormal{Z}_{n})\right).

(87)

By symmetry, for any data point in
\textnormal{S}_{j}
besides
\textnormal{Z}_{n}
, the mutual information between
W
and this data point can be upper bound by the right-hand side of (
87
) as well. Finally, recall that Lemma
1
provides an upper bound for the expected generalization gap:

\displaystyle\frac{\sqrt{2}\sigma}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sqrt{I(\textnormal{W}_{T};\textnormal{Z}_{i})}=\frac{\sqrt{2}\sigma}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\sum_{\textnormal{Z}\in\textnormal{S}_{j}}\sqrt{I(\textnormal{W}_{T};\textnormal{Z})}.

(88)

By substituting (
87
) into the above expression, we know the expected generalization gap can be further upper bounded by

\displaystyle\frac{\sqrt{2}\sigma}{2n}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\sqrt{\sum_{t\in\mathcal{T}_{j}}\beta_{t}\eta_{t}\cdot\mathsf{Var}\left(\nabla_{\bm{w}}\hat{\ell}(\textnormal{W}_{t-1},\bar{\textnormal{Z}}_{j})\right)},

where
\bar{\textnormal{Z}}_{j}
is any data point in the mini-batch
\textnormal{S}_{j}
.
∎

Finally, we are in a position to prove Proposition
15
.

Proof.

Consider a new loss function and the gradient of a new surrogate loss:

\displaystyle\ell(\bm{w},\textnormal{S}_{j})\triangleq\frac{1}{b}\sum_{\textnormal{Z}\in\textnormal{S}_{j}}\ell(\bm{w},\textnormal{Z}),\quad\nabla_{\bm{w}}\hat{\ell}(\bm{w},\textnormal{S}_{j})\triangleq\frac{1}{b}\sum_{\textnormal{Z}\in\textnormal{S}_{j}}\nabla_{\bm{w}}\hat{\ell}(\bm{w},\textnormal{Z}).

Then
\ell(\bm{w},\textnormal{S}_{j})
is
\sigma/\sqrt{b}
-sub-Gaussian under
\textnormal{S}_{j}\sim\mu^{\otimes b}
for all
\bm{w}\in\mathcal{W}
. We view each mini-batch
\textnormal{S}_{j}
as a data point and view
\ell(\bm{w},\textnormal{S}_{j})
as a new loss function. By using Lemma
20
, we obtain:

\displaystyle\left|\mathbb{E}\left[L_{\mu}(\textnormal{W})-L_{\textnormal{S}}(\textnormal{W})\right]\right|\leq\frac{\sqrt{2}\sigma}{2m\sqrt{b}}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\sqrt{\sum_{t\in\mathcal{T}_{j}}\beta_{t}\eta_{t}\cdot\mathsf{Var}\left(\nabla_{\bm{w}}\hat{\ell}(\textnormal{W}_{t-1},\textnormal{S}_{j})\right)}.

(89)

Since the data set contains
n
data points and is divided into
m
disjoint mini-batches with size
b
, we have
n=mb
. Substituting this into (
89
) leads to the desired conclusion.
∎

D.4
Proof of Corollary
16

Proof.

The Minkowski inequality implies that for any non-negative
x_{i}
, the inequality
\sqrt{\sum_{i}x_{i}}\leq\sum_{i}\sqrt{x_{i}}
holds. Therefore, we can further upper bound the generalization bound in Lemma
20
by

\displaystyle\frac{\sqrt{2}\sigma}{2n}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\sum_{t\in\mathcal{T}_{j}}\sqrt{\beta_{t}\eta_{t}\cdot\mathsf{Var}\left(\nabla_{\bm{w}}\hat{\ell}(\textnormal{W}_{t-1},\bar{\textnormal{Z}}_{j})\right)}=\frac{\sqrt{2}\sigma}{2n}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\sqrt{\beta_{t}\eta_{t}\cdot\mathsf{Var}\left(\nabla_{\bm{w}}\hat{\ell}(\textnormal{W}_{t-1},\textnormal{Z}^{\dagger}_{t})\right)}.

Alternatively, by Jensen’s inequality and
n=mb
, we can further upper bound the generalization bound in Lemma
20
by

\displaystyle\frac{\sqrt{2}\sigma}{2}\sqrt{\frac{1}{bn}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\beta_{t}\eta_{t}\cdot\mathsf{Var}\left(\nabla_{\bm{w}}\hat{\ell}(\textnormal{W}_{t-1},\textnormal{Z}^{\dagger}_{t})\right)}.

∎
