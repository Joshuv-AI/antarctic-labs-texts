Matrix Orthogonal Polynomials: A Riemann–Hilbert approach in memory of José Carlos Petronilho
Amílcar Branquinho, Ana Foulquié-Moreno, Assil Fradi, Manuel Mañas

Abstract
. In this work we show how to get advantage from the Riemann–Hilbert analysis in order to obtain information about the matrix orthogonal polynomials and functions of second kind associated with a weight matrix. We deduce properties for the recurrence relation coefficients from differential properties of the weight matrix. We take the matrix polynomials of Hermite, Laguerre and Jacobi type as a case study.

The main purpose of this paper is to show how the Riemann–Hilbert analysis could help in order to derive algebraic and analytic properties for the sequences of matrix orthogonal polynomials associated with a weight matrix in the class of Hermite, Laguerre or Jacobi, presented in a sequel of works

Also, several examples have been considered of matrix-valued orthogonal polynomials, most of them associated with second order differential equations (cf.

The study of equations for the recursion coefficients for orthogonal polynomials constitutes a subject of current interest. The question of how some properties of the weight, for example to satisfy a Pearson type equation,
translates to the recursion coefficients has been treated in several places, for a review see

Here we will see that these properties can be derived directly from a generalized matrix Pearson differential equation.

Now, we present the structure of the work:
In Section
1
we state the basic facts of the theory of matrix orthogonal polynomials that will be used in the text.
We begin with the definition of regular weight matrix and the three term recurrence relation for the matrix orthogonal polynomials. Next we reinterpret the Berezanskii matrix orthogonal polynomials in terms of scalar orthogonal polynomials.
We also make a reinterpretation of the matrix orthogonality in terms of the Gauss–Borel factorization of the moment matrix, and at the end of the section we state a general Riemann–Hilbert problem associated with the sequences of monic matrix orthogonal polynomials.
In Section

we present the general weights that we have considered in the previous works on matrix orthogonal polynomials. We will see that all the properties of these systems come from the analytical properties of a fundamental matrix associated with the matrix functions

From these first order differential equations we derive a second order ones for the

There, we will construct second order differential operators that have sequence of Berezanskii matrix polynomials (respectively, of second kind matrix functions) as eigenfunctions.
We end this work, with Section

1.2.2.
Gauss–Borel factorization

Let us give a nice interpretation of this biorthogonality.
First of all, we can see that the Stieltjes–Markov like transformation of the weight matrix
\omega
is a generating function of the moments of
\omega
. In fact,

\displaystyle S_{\omega}(z)=\int\frac{\omega(t)}{t-z}\frac{\operatorname{d}t}{2\pi\operatorname{i}}=\frac{1}{2\pi\operatorname{i}}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{\int t^{n}\omega(t)\,\operatorname{d}t}{z^{n+1}}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{\omega_{n}}{z^{n+1}},

for
|z|>r\coloneqq\max\big\{|t|,t\in\gamma\big\}
.
Hence,
S_{\omega}
is an analytic function on compact sets of
\mdmathbb C\setminus\big\{z\in\mdmathbb C:|z|<r\big\}
.

We write down the biorthogonality conditions (
1.15
) that we have just derived in Theorem
1.1
,

\displaystyle\int_{C}P_{n}^{\mathsf{L}}(z)S_{\omega}(z)P_{m}^{\mathsf{R}}(z)\,\frac{\operatorname{d}z}{2\pi\operatorname{i}}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\int_{C}P_{n}^{\mathsf{L}}(z)\frac{\omega_{n}}{z^{n+1}}P_{m}^{\mathsf{R}}(z)\,\frac{\operatorname{d}z}{2\pi\operatorname{i}}

and by the Cauchy integral formula we get

(1.16)

\displaystyle C_{n}^{-1}\delta_{n,m}=\int_{C}P_{n}^{\mathsf{L}}(z)S_{\omega}(z)P_{m}^{\mathsf{R}}(z)\,\frac{\operatorname{d}z}{2\pi\operatorname{i}}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{\Big(P_{n}^{\mathsf{L}}(z)\omega_{k}P_{m}^{\mathsf{R}}(z)\Big)^{(k)}\Big|_{z\to 0}}{k!}.

Now, from the Leibniz rule for the derivatives, we know that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{k!}\big(P_{n}^{\mathsf{L}}(z)\omega_{k}P_{m}^{\mathsf{R}}(z)\big)^{(k)}\Big|_{z\to 0}=\sum_{j=0}^{k}\frac{\big(P_{n}^{\mathsf{L}}\big)^{(j)}(0)}{j!}\omega_{k}\frac{\big(P_{m}^{\mathsf{R}}\big)^{(k-j)}(0)}{(k-j)!}.

With this identity we can reinterpret (
1.16
) in matrix notation

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}^{\mathsf{L}}\,\boldsymbol{\mathsf{U}}\,\mathcal{P}^{\mathsf{R}}=\operatorname{diag}\big\{C_{0}^{-1},C_{1}^{-1},\ldots\big\}

where

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathsf{U}}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}\omega_{0}&\cdots&\omega_{n}&\cdots\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\omega_{n}&\cdots&\omega_{2n}&\cdots\\
\vdots&&\vdots&\ddots\end{bmatrix}

moment matrix

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}^{\mathsf{L}}

\displaystyle=\left.\begin{bmatrix}P_{0}^{\mathsf{L}}&\\[2.84544pt]
P_{1}^{\mathsf{L}}&\big(P_{1}^{\mathsf{L}}\big)^{\prime}&\\[2.84544pt]
P_{2}^{\mathsf{L}}&\big(P_{2}^{\mathsf{L}}\big)^{\prime}&\frac{1}{2!}\big(P_{2}^{\mathsf{L}}\big)^{\prime\prime}\\[2.84544pt]
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots\\[2.84544pt]
P_{n}^{\mathsf{L}}&\big(P_{n}^{\mathsf{L}}\big)^{\prime}&\frac{1}{2!}\big(P_{2}^{\mathsf{L}}\big)^{\prime\prime}&\cdots&&\frac{1}{n!}\big(P_{n}^{\mathsf{L}}\big)^{(n)}\\[2.84544pt]
\vdots&&&&&\ddots\end{bmatrix}\right|_{z\to 0}

P_{n}^{\mathsf{L}}

Taylor coefficients

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}^{\mathsf{R}}

\displaystyle=\left.\begin{bmatrix}P_{0}^{\mathsf{R}}&\\[2.84544pt]
P_{1}^{\mathsf{R}}&\big(P_{1}^{\mathsf{R}}\big)^{\prime}&\\[2.84544pt]
P_{2}^{\mathsf{R}}&\big(P_{2}^{\mathsf{R}}\big)^{\prime}&\frac{1}{2!}\big(P_{2}^{\mathsf{R}}\big)^{\prime\prime}\\[2.84544pt]
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots\\[2.84544pt]
P_{n}^{\mathsf{R}}&\big(P_{n}^{\mathsf{R}}\big)^{\prime}&\frac{1}{2!}\big(P_{2}^{\mathsf{R}}\big)^{\prime\prime}&\cdots&&\frac{1}{n!}\big(P_{n}^{\mathsf{R}}\big)^{(n)}\\[2.84544pt]
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&&&\ddots\end{bmatrix}\right|_{z\to 0}^{\top}

P_{n}^{\mathsf{R}}
Taylor coefficients

As a conclusion:
We get the Gauss–Borel factorization of the moment matrix

(1.17)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathsf{U}}=\big(\mathcal{P}^{\mathsf{L}}\big)^{-1}\,\operatorname{diag}\big\{C_{0}^{-1},C_{1}^{-1},\ldots\big\}\,\big(\mathcal{P}^{\mathsf{R}}\big)^{-1}.

We can see from (
1.17
) that the orthogonality relays on the Gauss–Borel factorization of the moment matrix.

Remember that the necessary and sufficient conditions in order to assure that this representation takes place are exactly the ones we assume at the beginning in order to define the sequence of monic polynomials (left and right ones), i.e. (
1.1
) takes place.

1.3.
Riemann–Hilbert problem

It can be seen that

\displaystyle{P}_{n}^{\mathsf{L}}(z)\,Q_{0}^{\mathsf{L}}(z)=\int\big({P}^{\mathsf{L}}_{n}(z)-{P}^{\mathsf{L}}_{n}(x)\big)\,\frac{\omega(x)}{z-x}\,\frac{\operatorname{d}x}{2\pi\operatorname{i}}+\int{P}^{\mathsf{L}}_{n}(x)\,\frac{\omega(x)}{z-x}\,\frac{\operatorname{d}x}{2\pi\operatorname{i}}

and so,
{P}_{n-1}^{\mathsf{L},(1)}
, defined by

(1.18)

\displaystyle{P}^{\mathsf{L}}_{n}(z)\,Q^{\mathsf{L}}_{0}(z)-Q^{\mathsf{L}}_{n}(z)={P}_{n-1}^{\mathsf{L},(1)}(z)

is for each
n\in\mdmathbb Z_{+}\coloneqq\big\{1,2,\ldots\big\}
, a polynomial of degree at most
\displaystyle n-1
, called the
first kind associated polynomial
with respect to
\displaystyle\big\{{P}_{n}^{\mathsf{L}}\,\big\}_{n\in\mdmathbb N}
and
\displaystyle\omega
.
In fact,

\displaystyle{P}_{n-1}^{\mathsf{L},(1)}(z)\coloneqq\int\big({P}^{\mathsf{L}}_{n}(z)-{P}^{\mathsf{L}}_{n}(x)\big)\,\frac{\omega(x)}{z-x}\,\frac{\operatorname{d}x}{2\pi\operatorname{i}},

\displaystyle n\in\mdmathbb Z_{+}.

We can matricially summarize the identities (
1.5
), (
1.13
), as

\displaystyle\left[\begin{matrix}{P}^{\mathsf{L}}_{n+1}(z)&Q^{\mathsf{L}}_{n+1}(z)\\[2.84544pt]
C_{n}\,{P}_{n}^{\mathsf{L}}(z)&C_{n}\,Q_{n}^{\mathsf{L}}(z)\end{matrix}\right]=\left[\begin{matrix}z\operatorname{I}-\beta^{\mathsf{L}}_{n}&-C_{n}^{-1}\\[2.84544pt]
C_{n}&\boldsymbol{0}\end{matrix}\right]\,\left[\begin{matrix}{P}_{n}^{\mathsf{L}}(z)&Q_{n}^{\mathsf{L}}(z)\\[2.84544pt]
C_{n-1}\,{P}_{n-1}^{\mathsf{L}}(z)&C_{n-1}\,Q_{n-1}^{\mathsf{L}}(z)\end{matrix}\right]\,;

and by (
1.18
)
we also have that

\displaystyle\left[\begin{matrix}{P}_{n}^{\mathsf{L},(1)}(z)\\[2.84544pt]
C_{n}\,{P}_{n-1}^{\mathsf{L},(1)}(z)\end{matrix}\right]=\left[\begin{matrix}z\operatorname{I}-\beta^{\mathsf{L}}_{n}&-C_{n}^{-1}\\[2.84544pt]
C_{n}&\boldsymbol{0}\end{matrix}\right]\,\left[\begin{matrix}{P}_{n-1}^{\mathsf{L},(1)}(z)\\[2.84544pt]
C_{n-1}\,{P}_{n-2}^{\mathsf{L},(1)}(z)\end{matrix}\right].

Taking

\displaystyle Y_{n}^{\mathsf{L}}(z)\coloneqq\begin{bmatrix}{P}_{n}^{\mathsf{L}}(z)&Q_{n}^{\mathsf{L}}(z)\\[2.84544pt]
C_{n-1}\,{P}_{n-1}^{\mathsf{L}}(z)&C_{n-1}\,Q_{n-1}^{\mathsf{L}}(z)\end{bmatrix}

and

\displaystyle T_{n}^{\mathsf{L}}\coloneqq\begin{bmatrix}z\operatorname{I}-\beta^{\mathsf{L}}_{n}&-C_{n}^{-1}\\[2.84544pt]
C_{n}&\boldsymbol{0}\end{bmatrix},

for all
n\in\mdmathbb N
, we get

(1.19)

\displaystyle\det\,Y_{n}^{\mathsf{L}}(z)=\det\,Y_{0}^{\mathsf{L}}(z)=1

as

\displaystyle\det\,T_{n}^{\mathsf{L}}=1,

\displaystyle n\in\mdmathbb N.

In the same way, we get from (
1.6
) and (
1.14
) that

\displaystyle Y_{n+1}^{\mathsf{R}}(z)=Y_{n}^{\mathsf{R}}(z)\,T_{n}^{\mathsf{R}}(z),

\displaystyle n\in\mdmathbb N,

where

\displaystyle Y_{n}^{\mathsf{R}}(z)\coloneqq\begin{bmatrix}P^{\mathsf{R}}_{n}(z)&-P^{\mathsf{R}}_{n-1}(z)C_{n}\\[2.84544pt]
{Q}^{\mathsf{R}}_{n}(z)&-{Q}^{\mathsf{R}}_{n-1}(z)C_{n}\end{bmatrix}

and

\displaystyle T_{n}^{\mathsf{R}}(z)\coloneqq\begin{bmatrix}z\operatorname{I}-\beta_{n}^{\mathsf{R}}&-C_{n}\\[2.84544pt]
C_{n}^{-1}&\boldsymbol{0}\end{bmatrix}

where
\beta_{n}^{\mathsf{R}}
is defined in (
1.7
).
Here we also have

(1.20)

\displaystyle\det\,Y_{n}^{\mathsf{R}}(z)=\det\,Y_{0}^{\mathsf{R}}(z)=1

as

\displaystyle\det\,T_{n}^{\mathsf{R}}=1,

\displaystyle n\in\mdmathbb N.

As a conclusion:
We can establish that the matrix function
\displaystyle Y_{n}^{\mathsf{L}}
(respectively
\displaystyle Y_{n}^{\mathsf{R}}
) is, for each
n\in\mdmathbb N
, the unique solution of the Riemann–Hilbert problem; which consists in determining a
2N\times 2N
matrix complex function

\displaystyle G_{n}^{\mathsf{L}}
(respectively
\displaystyle G_{n}^{\mathsf{R}}
) such that:

(RH1):

\displaystyle G_{n}^{\mathsf{L}}
(respectively
\displaystyle G_{n}^{\mathsf{R}}
) is analytic in
\displaystyle\mdmathbb C\setminus\mdmathbb\gamma
;

(RH2):

has the following asymptotic behavior near infinity,

\displaystyle G_{n}^{\mathsf{L}}(z)

\displaystyle=\big(\operatorname{I}+\operatorname{O}(z^{-1})\big)\begin{bmatrix}\operatorname{I}\,z^{n}&\boldsymbol{0}\\
\boldsymbol{0}&\operatorname{I}\,z^{-n}\end{bmatrix}

(respectively,

\displaystyle G_{n}^{\mathsf{R}}(z)

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}\operatorname{I}z^{n}&\boldsymbol{0}\\
\boldsymbol{0}&\operatorname{I}z^{-n}\end{bmatrix}\big(\operatorname{I}+\operatorname{O}(z^{-1})\big)\text{);}

(RH3):

satisfies the jump condition

\displaystyle\big(G_{n}^{\mathsf{L}}(z)\big)_{+}=\big(G_{n}^{\mathsf{L}}(z)\big)_{-}\,\left[\begin{matrix}\operatorname{I}&\omega(x)\\
\boldsymbol{0}&\operatorname{I}\end{matrix}\right]
(respectively,

\displaystyle\big(G^{\mathsf{R}}_{n}(z)\big)_{+}=\begin{bmatrix}\operatorname{I}&\boldsymbol{0}\\
\omega(x)&\operatorname{I}\end{bmatrix}\big(G^{\mathsf{R}}_{n}(z)\big)_{-}
),
\displaystyle x\in\gamma
.

Now, we will see that these two Riemann–Hilbert problems are related by similarity.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
Y_{n}^{\mathsf{L}}
and
Y_{n}^{\mathsf{R}}
be, for each
n\in\mdmathbb N
, the unique solutions of the Riemann–Hilbert problems just defined; then

(1.21)

\displaystyle\Big(Y_{n}^{\mathsf{L}}(z)\Big)^{-1}=\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{0}&\operatorname{I}\\
-\operatorname{I}&\boldsymbol{0}\end{bmatrix}Y_{n}^{\mathsf{R}}(z)\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{0}&-\operatorname{I}\\
\operatorname{I}&\boldsymbol{0}\end{bmatrix},

\displaystyle n\in\mdmathbb N.

This is an easy consequence of the Christoffel–Darboux identities, valid for all
n\in\mdmathbb N
,

\displaystyle(z-t)\sum_{k=0}^{n}P_{k}^{\mathsf{R}}(t)C_{k}P_{k}^{\mathsf{L}}(z)

\displaystyle=P_{n}^{\mathsf{R}}(t)C_{n}P_{n+1}^{\mathsf{L}}(z)-P_{n+1}^{\mathsf{R}}(t)C_{n}P_{n}^{\mathsf{L}}(z),

\displaystyle(z-t)\sum_{k=0}^{n}Q_{k}^{\mathsf{R}}(t)C_{k}Q_{k}^{\mathsf{L}}(z)

\displaystyle=Q_{n}^{\mathsf{R}}(t)C_{n}Q_{n+1}^{\mathsf{L}}(z)-Q_{n+1}^{\mathsf{R}}(t)C_{n}Q_{n}^{\mathsf{L}}(z)

\displaystyle\hskip 115.23373pt+S_{\omega}(z)-S_{\omega}(t),

\displaystyle(z-t)\sum_{k=0}^{n}Q_{k}^{\mathsf{R}}(t)C_{k}P_{k}^{\mathsf{L}}(z)

\displaystyle=Q_{n}^{\mathsf{R}}(t)C_{n}P_{n+1}^{\mathsf{L}}(z)-Q_{n+1}^{\mathsf{R}}(t)C_{n}P_{n}^{\mathsf{L}}(z)+\operatorname{I}

\displaystyle(z-t)\sum_{k=0}^{n}P_{k}^{\mathsf{R}}(t)C_{k}Q_{k}^{\mathsf{L}}(z)

\displaystyle=P_{n}^{\mathsf{R}}(t)C_{n}Q_{n+1}^{\mathsf{L}}(z)-P_{n+1}^{\mathsf{R}}(t)C_{n}Q_{n}^{\mathsf{L}}(z)-\operatorname{I}.

Taking
z=t
we arrive to the identities

\displaystyle P_{n}^{\mathsf{R}}(t)C_{n}P_{n+1}^{\mathsf{L}}(z)-P_{n+1}^{\mathsf{R}}(t)C_{n}P_{n}^{\mathsf{L}}(z)

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{0},

\displaystyle Q_{n}^{\mathsf{R}}(t)C_{n}Q_{n+1}^{\mathsf{L}}(z),-Q_{n+1}^{\mathsf{R}}(t)C_{n}Q_{n}^{\mathsf{L}}(z)

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{0},

\displaystyle Q_{n+1}^{\mathsf{R}}(t)C_{n}P_{n}^{\mathsf{L}}(z)-Q_{n}^{\mathsf{R}}(t)C_{n}P_{n+1}^{\mathsf{L}}(z)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{I},

\displaystyle P_{n}^{\mathsf{R}}(t)C_{n}Q_{n+1}^{\mathsf{L}}(z)-P_{n+1}^{\mathsf{R}}(t)C_{n}Q_{n}^{\mathsf{L}}(z)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{I}.

All of this becomes

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}-Q_{n-1}^{\mathsf{R}}(z)C_{n-1}&-Q_{n}^{\mathsf{R}}(z)\\
P_{n-1}^{\mathsf{R}}(z)C_{n-1}&P_{n}^{\mathsf{R}}(z)\end{bmatrix}Y_{n}^{\mathsf{L}}(z)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{I},

\displaystyle n\in\mdmathbb N,

and as

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}-Q_{n-1}^{\mathsf{R}}(z)C_{n-1}&-Q_{n}^{\mathsf{R}}(z)\\
P_{n-1}^{\mathsf{R}}(z)C_{n-1}&P_{n}^{\mathsf{R}}(z)\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{0}&\operatorname{I}\\
-\operatorname{I}&\boldsymbol{0}\end{bmatrix}Y_{n}^{\mathsf{R}}(z)\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{0}&-\operatorname{I}\\
\operatorname{I}&\boldsymbol{0}\end{bmatrix},

\displaystyle n\in\mdmathbb N,

we get the desired result.

2.
Fundamental matrices from Riemann–Hilbert problems

Here we consider weight matrices,
\omega
, satisfying a matrix Pearson type equation

\displaystyle\phi(z)\omega^{\prime}(z)=h^{\mathsf{L}}(z)\omega(z)+\omega(z)h^{\mathsf{R}}(z),

where
\phi
is a scalar polynomial of degree at most
2
, and
h^{\mathsf{L}}
,
h^{\mathsf{R}}

are entire matrix functions.
If we take a weight function
\omega^{\mathsf{L}}
such that

(2.22)

\displaystyle\phi(z)\big(\omega^{\mathsf{L}}\big)^{\prime}(z)=h^{\mathsf{L}}(z)\omega^{\mathsf{L}}(z),

then there exists a matrix function
\omega^{\mathsf{R}}(z)
such that
\omega(z)=\omega^{\mathsf{L}}(z)\omega^{\mathsf{R}}(z)
with

(2.23)

\displaystyle\phi(z)\big(\omega^{\mathsf{R}}\big)^{\prime}(z)=\omega^{\mathsf{R}}(z)h^{\mathsf{R}}(z).

The reciprocal is also true.

For each factorization
\omega=\omega^{\mathsf{L}}\omega^{\mathsf{R}}
, we introduce the
constant jump fundamental matrices
which will be instrumental in what follows

(2.24)

\displaystyle Z_{n}^{\mathsf{L}}(z)

\displaystyle\coloneqq Y^{\mathsf{L}}_{n}(z)\begin{bmatrix}\omega^{\mathsf{L}}(z)&\boldsymbol{0}\\
\boldsymbol{0}&\big(\omega^{\mathsf{R}}(z)\big)^{-1}\end{bmatrix},

(2.25)

\displaystyle{Z}^{\mathsf{R}}_{n}(z)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\begin{bmatrix}\omega^{\mathsf{R}}(z)&\boldsymbol{0}\\
\boldsymbol{0}&\big(\omega^{\mathsf{L}}(z)\big)^{-1}\end{bmatrix}{Y}^{\mathsf{R}}_{n}(z),

\displaystyle n\in\mdmathbb N.

Taking inverse on (
2.24
) and applying (
1.21
) we see that

Z_{n}^{\mathsf{R}}
given in (
2.25
) admits the representation

(2.26)

\displaystyle Z_{n}^{\mathsf{R}}(z)

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{0}&-\operatorname{I}\\
\operatorname{I}&\boldsymbol{0}\end{bmatrix}\big(Z_{n}^{\mathsf{L}}(z)\big)^{-1}\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{0}&\operatorname{I}\\
-\operatorname{I}&\boldsymbol{0}\end{bmatrix},

\displaystyle n\in\mdmathbb N.

In parallel to the matrices
Z^{\mathsf{L}}_{n}(z)
and
Z^{\mathsf{R}}_{n}(z)
, we introduce what we call
structure matrices
given in terms of the
left
, respectively
right
, logarithmic derivatives by,

(2.27)

\displaystyle M^{\mathsf{L}}_{n}(z)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\big(Z^{\mathsf{L}}_{n}\big)^{\prime}(z)\big(Z^{\mathsf{L}}_{n}(z)\big)^{-1},

\displaystyle{M}^{\mathsf{R}}_{n}(z)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\big({Z}^{\mathsf{R}}_{n}(z)\big)^{-1}\big(Z^{\mathsf{R}}_{n}\big)^{\prime}(z).

It is not difficult to see that

(2.28)

\displaystyle{M}^{\mathsf{R}}_{n}(z)

\displaystyle=-\left[\begin{matrix}\boldsymbol{0}&-\operatorname{I}\\
\operatorname{I}&\boldsymbol{0}\end{matrix}\right]M_{n}^{\mathsf{L}}(z)\left[\begin{matrix}\boldsymbol{0}&\operatorname{I}\\
-\operatorname{I}&\boldsymbol{0}\end{matrix}\right],

\displaystyle n\in\mdmathbb N,

as well as, the following properties hold (cf.
[
4
]
):

(1)

The transfer matrices satisfy

\displaystyle T^{\mathsf{L}}_{n}(z)Z_{n}^{\mathsf{L}}(z)

\displaystyle=Z^{\mathsf{L}}_{n+1}(z),

\displaystyle{Z}^{\mathsf{R}}_{n}(z){T}^{\mathsf{R}}_{n}(z)

\displaystyle={Z}^{\mathsf{R}}_{n+1}(z),

\displaystyle n\in\mdmathbb N.

(2)

The zero curvature formulas holds,

\displaystyle\left[\begin{matrix}\operatorname{I}&\boldsymbol{0}\\
\boldsymbol{0}&\boldsymbol{0}\end{matrix}\right]

\displaystyle=M^{\mathsf{L}}_{n+1}(z)T^{\mathsf{L}}_{n}(z)-T^{\mathsf{L}}_{n}(z)M^{\mathsf{L}}_{n}(z),

\displaystyle n\in\mdmathbb N,

\displaystyle\left[\begin{matrix}\operatorname{I}&\boldsymbol{0}\\
\boldsymbol{0}&\boldsymbol{0}\end{matrix}\right]

\displaystyle=T^{\mathsf{R}}_{n}(z)\,M^{\mathsf{R}}_{n+1}(z)-M^{\mathsf{R}}_{n}(z)T^{\mathsf{R}}_{n}(z),

\displaystyle n\in\mdmathbb N.

We see from (
1.21
), (
2.26
) and (
2.28
) that we only need to consider the left side of the objects
Y_{n}
,
Z_{n}
and
M_{n}
.

2.1.
Hermite case

In this case we consider
\phi(z)=1
.
We underline that for a given regular weight matrix
\omega(z)
we will have many possible factorization
\omega(z)=\omega^{\mathsf{L}}(z)\omega^{\mathsf{R}}(z)
. Indeed, if we define an equivalence relation
\big(\omega^{\mathsf{L}},\omega^{\mathsf{R}}\big)\sim\big(\widetilde{\omega}^{\mathsf{L}},\widetilde{\omega}^{\mathsf{R}}\big)
if, and only if,
\omega^{\mathsf{L}}\omega^{\mathsf{R}}=\widetilde{\omega}^{\mathsf{L}}\widetilde{\omega}^{\mathsf{R}}
, then each weight matrix
\omega
can be though as a class of equivalence, and can be described by the orbit

\displaystyle\big\{\big(\omega^{\mathsf{L}}\varphi,\varphi^{-1}\omega^{\mathsf{R}}\big),\ \varphi\ \text{ is a nonsingular matrix of entire functions}\big\}.

The constant jump fundamental matrices
Z^{\mathsf{L}}_{n}(z)
and
Z^{\mathsf{R}}_{n}(z)
are, for each
n\in\mdmathbb N
, characterized by the following properties:

i)

They are holomorphic on
\mdmathbb C\setminus\gamma
.

ii)

We have the following asymptotic behaviors

\displaystyle Z^{\mathsf{L}}_{n}(z)

\displaystyle=\big(\operatorname{I}+\operatorname{O}(z^{-1})\big)\begin{bmatrix}z^{n}\omega^{\mathsf{L}}(z)&\boldsymbol{0}\\
\boldsymbol{0}&z^{-n}(\omega^{\mathsf{R}}(z))^{-1}\end{bmatrix},

\displaystyle Z^{\mathsf{R}}_{n}(z)

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}z^{n}\omega^{\mathsf{R}}(z)&\boldsymbol{0}\\
\boldsymbol{0}&(\omega^{\mathsf{L}}(z))^{-1}z^{-n}\end{bmatrix}\big(\operatorname{I}+\operatorname{O}(z^{-1})\big),

for
z\to\infty
.

iii)

They present the following
constant jump condition
on
\gamma

\displaystyle\big(Z^{\mathsf{L}}_{n}(z)\big)_{+}

\displaystyle=\big(Z^{\mathsf{L}}_{n}(z)\big)_{-}\begin{bmatrix}\operatorname{I}&\operatorname{I}\\
\boldsymbol{0}&\operatorname{I}\end{bmatrix},

\displaystyle\big({Z}^{\mathsf{R}}_{n}(z)\big)_{+}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}\operatorname{I}&\boldsymbol{0}\\
\operatorname{I}&\operatorname{I}\end{bmatrix}\big({Z}^{\mathsf{R}}_{n}(z)\big)_{-},

for all
z\in\gamma
in the support on the weight matrix.

In
[
4
]
we have proved that the structure matrices
M^{\mathsf{L}}_{n}(z)
and
M^{\mathsf{R}}_{n}(z)
, just defined, cf.(
2.27
), are, for each
n\in\mdmathbb N
, matrices of entire functions in the complex plane.

2.2.
Laguerre case

Here we consider,
\phi(z)=z
, and
\omega
a regular Laguerre type weight matrix, i.e.

\omega=\left[\begin{smallmatrix}\omega^{(1,1)}&\cdots&\omega^{(1,N)}\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\omega^{(N,1)}&\cdots&\omega^{(N,N)}\end{smallmatrix}\right]
,
with

\displaystyle\omega^{(j,k)}(z)=\sum_{m\in I_{j,k}}A_{m}(z)z^{\alpha_{m}}\log^{p_{m}}z,

\displaystyle z\in(0,+\infty),

where

I_{j,k}
denotes a finite set of indexes,
\operatorname{Re}\left(\alpha_{m}\right)>-1
,
p_{m}\in\mdmathbb{N}
and
A_{m}(z)
is Hölder continuous and bounded.

In
[
10
]
different examples of Laguerre weights for the matrix orthogonal polynomials on the real line are studied.

In order to state a Riemann–Hilbert problem for the Laguerre type weights we must add to the one presented in Section
1.3
, i.e. to (RH1)–(RH3), the condition

(RH4):

Y^{\mathsf{L}}_{n}(z)=\left[\begin{matrix}\operatorname{O}(1)&s^{\mathsf{L}}_{1}(z)\\[2.84544pt]
\operatorname{O}(1)&s^{\mathsf{L}}_{2}(z)\end{matrix}\right]

and
Y^{\mathsf{R}}_{n}(z)=\left[\begin{matrix}\operatorname{O}(1)&\operatorname{O}(1)\\[2.84544pt]
s^{\mathsf{R}}_{1}(z)&s^{\mathsf{R}}_{2}(z)\end{matrix}\right]
,
as
z\to 0
,
with

\displaystyle\lim_{z\to 0}zs^{\mathsf{L}}_{j}(z)=\boldsymbol{0}
,

\displaystyle\lim_{z\to 0}zs^{\mathsf{R}}_{j}(z)=\boldsymbol{0}
,

j=1,2
and the
\operatorname{O}
conditions are understood entrywise.

The solutions of (
2.22
) and (
2.23
) are of type

\displaystyle\omega^{\mathsf{L}}(z)=H^{\mathsf{L}}(z)z^{A^{\mathsf{L}}}\omega_{0}^{\mathsf{L}},

\displaystyle\omega^{\mathsf{R}}(z)=\omega_{0}^{\mathsf{R}}z^{A^{\mathsf{R}}}H^{\mathsf{R}}(z)

where

H^{\mathsf{L}}
,
H^{\mathsf{R}}
are entire and nonsingular matrix function such that
H^{\mathsf{L}}(0)=H^{\mathsf{R}}(0)=\operatorname{I}
, and
\omega_{0}^{\mathsf{L}}
,
\omega_{0}^{\mathsf{R}}
are a constant nonsingular matrix.

The constant jump fundamental matrices, introduced in (
2.24
) and (
2.25
), i.e.
Z^{\mathsf{L}}_{n}
and
Z^{\mathsf{R}}_{n}
satisfy, for each
n\in\mdmathbb N
, the following properties:

i)

They are holomorphic on
\mdmathbb{C}\setminus\gamma
.

ii)

Present the following constant jump condition on
\gamma

\displaystyle\big(Z^{\mathsf{L}}_{n}(z)\big)_{+}

\displaystyle=\big(Z^{\mathsf{L}}_{n}(z)\big)_{-}\left[\begin{matrix}(\omega_{0}^{\mathsf{L}})^{-1}e^{-2\pi\operatorname{i}A^{\mathsf{L}}}\omega_{0}^{\mathsf{L}}&(\omega_{0}^{\mathsf{L}})^{-1}e^{-2\pi\operatorname{i}A^{\mathsf{L}}}\omega_{0}^{\mathsf{L}}\\[2.84544pt]
\boldsymbol{0}&\omega_{0}^{\mathsf{R}}e^{2\pi\operatorname{i}A^{\mathsf{R}}}(\omega_{0}^{\mathsf{R}})^{-1}\end{matrix}\right],

\displaystyle\big(Z^{\mathsf{R}}_{n}(z)\big)_{+}

\displaystyle=\left[\begin{matrix}\omega_{0}^{\mathsf{R}}e^{-2\pi\operatorname{i}A^{\mathsf{R}}}(\omega_{0}^{\mathsf{R}})^{-1}&\boldsymbol{0}\\[2.84544pt]
\omega_{0}^{\mathsf{R}}e^{-2\pi\operatorname{i}A^{\mathsf{R}}}(\omega_{0}^{\mathsf{R}})^{-1}&(\omega_{0}^{\mathsf{L}})^{-1}e^{2\pi\operatorname{i}A^{\mathsf{L}}}\omega_{0}^{\mathsf{L}}\end{matrix}\right]\big(Z^{\mathsf{R}}_{n}(z)\big)_{-},

for all
z\in\gamma
,
where
A^{\mathsf{L}}=h^{\mathsf{L}}(0)
,
A^{\mathsf{R}}=h^{\mathsf{R}}(0)
.

In
[
