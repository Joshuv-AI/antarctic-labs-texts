Asymmetric compressive learning guarantees with applications to quantized sketches
Vincent Schellekens 1, Laurent Jacques 1

Abstract
The compressive learning framework reduces the computational cost of training on large-scale datasets. In a sketching phase, the data is first compressed to a lightweight sketch vector, obtained by mapping the data samples through a well-chosen feature map, and averaging those contributions. In a learning phase, the desired model parameters are then extracted from this sketch by solving an optimization problem, which also involves a feature map. When the feature map is identical during the sketching and learning phases, formal statistical guarantees (excess risk bounds) have been proven. However, the desirable properties of the feature map are different during sketching and learning ( e.g. , quantized outputs, and differentiability, respectively). We thus study the relaxation where this map is allowed to be different for each phase. First, we prove that the existing guarantees carry over to this asymmetric scheme, up to a controlled error term, provided some Limited Projected Distortion (LPD) property holds. We then instantiate this framework to the setting of quantized sketches, by proving that the LPD indeed holds for binary sketch contributions. Finally, we further validate the approach with numerical simulations, including a large-scale application in audio event classification.



I
Introduction

The availability of large-scale datasets has risen tremendously over the past few years, which empowered machine learning solutions in a staggering amount of application areas. However, training from ever larger quantities of data requires ever more computational resources (
e.g.
, memory storage and processing time). For instance, datasets that cannot fit on the memory of standard workstations are increasingly common. In order to keep up with the accelerating rate at which data is produced, the design of novel resource-efficient learning paradigms has thus grown into an important research area
[
1
,
2
,
3
]
.

Compressive (statistical) learning
(CL) was proposed to learn from datasets
\mathcal{X}=\{\bm{x}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\}_{i=1}^{n}
of massive scale (in particular, with a large number of examples
n
, typically at least several millions) while keeping computational resources (
i.e.
, memory, training time) under control
[
4
,
5
]
. To do so, CL first compresses the data as a single
m
-dimensional (possibly complex-valued) vector
\bm{z}_{\Phi,\mathcal{X}}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Phi(\bm{x}_{i})
, called
sketch
, by simple averaging of a
feature map

\Phi:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}^{m}
. Crucially, computing the sketch requires only one single pass over
\mathcal{X}
, which is moreover easy to distribute and parallelize thanks to the independent contributions of each
\bm{x}_{i}

[
4
]
. This ensures that the sketch can always be constructed in an efficient manner, even when the dataset size grows drastically.

The choice of the feature map
\Phi
determines the range of machine learning tasks that one is able to solve from the sketch
\bm{z}_{\Phi,\mathcal{X}}
(note that
\Phi
should be nonlinear, otherwise the sketch cannot capture anything beside the data mean).
For example, it is possible to solve k-means
[
6
]
or Gaussian mixture modeling
[
7
]
within this framework, when
\Phi
is chosen to be random Fourier features (RFF)
[
8
]
. These features are defined as the complex exponential of random projections of the data,
i.e.
,

\textstyle\Phi_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{RFF}}(\bm{x})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}\exp(\mathrm{i}\mkern 1.0mu(\bm{\Omega}^{\top}\bm{x}+\bm{\xi})),

(1)

where the exponential is applied component-wise, and
\bm{\Omega}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times m}
has randomly drawn columns
\bm{\omega}_{j}\sim_{i.i.d.}\Lambda
for some probability distribution
\Lambda
(usually Gaussian).

Remark:
In our definition of the RFF, we also added a random
dither

\bm{\xi}
, which has uniform entries
\xi_{j}\sim_{i.i.d.}\mathcal{U}([0,2\pi))
. Although not strictly necessary at this point, this dithering will be useful when we consider quantization. It is also mandatory if the imaginary exponential in (
2
) is replaced by a sine or a cosine, as initially formulated in
[
8
]
.

After this “sketching phase”, the “learning phase” of CL extracts the desired machine learning model parameters
\bm{\theta}\in\Theta
from the sketch. This is achieved by solving an optimization problem of the form

\textstyle\min_{\bm{\theta}\in\Theta}\mathcal{C}_{\Phi}(\bm{\theta};\bm{z}_{\Phi,\mathcal{X}}).

Since the cost
\mathcal{C}_{\Phi}
involves only the sketch of size
m\ll nd
(
i.e.
, much smaller than the volume of
\mathcal{X}
), this procedure is typically much more efficient from a computational point of view than the classical approach of learning directly from the entire dataset
\mathcal{X}
, especially for large
n
.

Intuitively, given a map
\Phi
(
e.g.
,
\Phi_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{RFF}}
), the cost
\mathcal{C}_{\Phi}(\bm{\theta};\bm{z})
captures the mismatch between the vector
\bm{z}
and another sketch, obtained using
\Phi
, associated with the candidate model
\bm{\theta}
. Postponing the technical details for the moment, to solve k-means for instance (where one seeks a set of
K
centroids
\bm{\theta}=\{\bm{c}_{k}\}_{k=1}^{K}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
that best cluster the data), this cost is given by
\mathcal{C}_{\Phi}(\bm{\theta};\bm{z})=\|\bm{z}-\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k}\Phi(\bm{c}_{k})\|_{2}
,
i.e.
, the Euclidean distance between the sketch of the data and the “sketch of the centroids”
[
6
]
.

Numerical experiments demonstrated the power of CL, sometimes solving k-means clustering
[
6
]
and Gaussian mixture modeling (GMM)
[
7
]
with training time and memory consumption reduced by several orders of magnitude compared to classical approaches. Moreover, formal
statistical learning guarantees
(bounds on the so-called excess risk) were derived
[
4
]
: the risk is controlled whenever a form of Lower Restricted Isometry Property (LRIP) holds, which translates the compatibility between the sketch map
\Phi
and the target learning task. Proving this LRIP is however quite technical;
e.g.
, see
[
9
]
for the LRIP between
\Phi_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{RFF}}
and k-means and GMM.
This makes the design of a compressive learning schemes a bit rigid, as any deviation from the beaten path breaks the strong, but tediously constructed, theoretical guarantees. The ambition of this paper is to somehow relax this constraint on a specific aspect: allowing the designer to tweak the sketching phase (
e.g.
, to further improve its computational efficiency) without having to re-prove the LRIP-based guarantees from scratch.

More precisely, we study the scenario where the feature map for the sketching phase (now noted
\Psi
) is allowed to differ from the one used for the learning phase (noted
\Phi
),
i.e.
, we consider the
asymmetric
CL (ACL) strategy modeled by
\min_{\bm{\theta}}\mathcal{C}_{\Phi}(\bm{\theta};\bm{z}_{\Psi,\mathcal{X}})
, with
\Psi\neq\Phi
. This scheme is interesting from a practical point of view because both phases, occurring in different contexts, may require very different properties from their respective feature maps.

Fig. 1:
Our Asymmetric Compressive Learning (ACL) scheme: a dataset
\mathcal{X}
of
n
examples
\bm{x}_{i}
(sampled
i.i.d.
from
\mathcal{P}_{0}
) is first compressed as a lightweight vector—the
sketch
—by averaging data features
\Psi(\bm{x}_{i})
. This operation can be performed in parallel by a sensor network, which benefits greatly from hardware-friendliness and quantization. A model
\widehat{\bm{\theta}}^{\prime}
is then learned from the sketch
\bm{z}_{\Psi,\mathcal{X}}
by solving a CL optimization procedure that uses a different, differentiable map
\Phi\neq\Psi
. Our goal is to prove statistical learning guarantees (
w.r.t.

\mathcal{P}_{0}
) for the model
\widehat{\bm{\theta}}^{\prime}
.

This fact is best explained by a concrete example, illustrated Fig.
1
: consider a sensor network, where each node collects a few data samples
\bm{x}_{i}
, and sends their contributions
\Psi(\bm{x}_{i})
to a centralized server which aggregates them to construct the sketch
\bm{z}_{\Psi,\mathcal{X}}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Psi(\bm{x}_{i})
. For efficient transmission of those numerous messages,
quantization
of the contributions
\Psi(\bm{x}_{i})
is critical. Moreover, to ensure low power consumption of the sensor nodes, a compact
hardware implementation
of
\Psi
is highly desirable.

The learning phase however, being performed locally and in software, does not benefit as much from these aspects. Instead, the cost
\mathcal{C}_{\Phi}(\bm{\theta};\bm{z}_{\Psi,\mathcal{X}})
must have
meaningful minimizers
(
i.e.
, as ensured by statistical guarantees), and must be efficient to optimize;
e.g.
, gradient-based algorithms
[
7
,
6
]
require
differentiability
of
\Phi
. All these objectives do not necessarily align, and some are even incompatible (for example, differentiability cannot be reconciled with the discontinuity induced by quantization), hence the interest of allowing different sketching and learning maps
\Psi\neq\Phi
.

In previous work
[
10
]
, we replaced the RFF sketch contributions
\Phi_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{RFF}}(\bm{x}_{i})\in\mathbb{C}^{m}
by quantized ones
\Psi_{q}(\bm{x}_{i})\in\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}\{\pm 1\pm\mathrm{i}\mkern 1.0mu\}^{m}
, obtained by taking the sign of usual RFF:

\textstyle\Psi_{q}(\bm{x}):=\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}\,{\rm sign}\left(\exp(\mathrm{i}\mkern 1.0mu(\bm{\Omega}^{\top}\bm{x}+\bm{\xi}))\right),

(2)

where the sign of a complex number
z\in\mathbb{C}
is applied component-wise to its real and imaginary part,
i.e.
,
{\rm sign}(z)={\rm sign}(\Re(z))+\mathrm{i}\mkern 1.0mu\,{\rm sign}(\Im(z))\in\{\pm 1\pm\mathrm{i}\mkern 1.0mu\}
.
The embedding
\Psi_{q}
is also known as (the complex extension of) the one-bit universal embedding studied in
[
11
,
12
]
. We showed numerically on the k-means task that the performance degraded only slightly, and provided an intuitive justification by showing
\mathcal{C}_{\Phi_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{RFF}}}(\bm{\theta};\bm{z}_{\Psi_{q},\mathcal{X}})\simeq\mathcal{C}_{\Phi_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{RFF}}}(\bm{\theta};\bm{z}_{\Phi_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{RFF}},\mathcal{X}})
, for a fixed dataset
\mathcal{X}
and parameter vector
\bm{\theta}
. In fact, this approximation of the “un-quantized” costs is enabled by the dithering
\bm{\xi}
—a crucial ingredient of our scheme—which picks out (on average) the fundamental frequency of
q(t)={\rm sign}(e^{\mathrm{i}\mkern 1.0mut})
. However, this fact did not
guarantee
anything regarding the excess risk achieved by ACL. This is remedied by the present paper; its main goal is to provide formal guarantees for the ACL scheme, by building upon the existing LRIP. This ensures that our result can be applied to other and future sketch constructions, provided these satisfy the LRIP.

Remark:
As will become clearer below, our results allow to replace, in the sensor implementing
\Psi
, the complex exponential
\exp(\mathrm{i}\mkern 1.0mut)
by a
completely generic
periodic map
f(t)
(of which
q(t)={\rm sign}(\exp(\mathrm{i}\mkern 1.0mut))
defined above is a particular case). Remark that our results thus apply in particular to the case where, while the sensor can ensure the periodicity of the embedded map
f
, the precise shape of
f
cannot be fully controlled, such that can’t really rely the sensor to accurately implement any
specific
map—
e.g.
, due to imperfections and non-linearities.

Contributions:

After reviewing compressive learning background, we formalize the ACL problem in Sec.
II
.

We first prove a general statistical guarantee for ACL in Sec.
III
; to do so, we introduce a Limited Projected Distortion (LPD) property (capturing, roughly speaking, the similarity between
\Phi
and
\Psi
), which we combine with the existing LRIP (which holds for
\Phi
). This result is “general” in the sense that it makes no assumption on the task to solve or the maps
\Phi
and
\Psi
(beyond the LRIP and LPD).

We then work towards concrete applications of this generic result in Sec.
IV
, dedicated to proving that the LPD actually holds for specific choices of
\Phi
and
\Psi
. To achieve this, we first introduce—at the cost of an additional (but mild) assumption—a sufficient condition for the LPD, called “signal-level LPD” (sLPD). Next, we prove the sLPD on one particular combination of feature maps, where the “learning phase feature map” are random Fourier features
\Phi=\Phi_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{RFF}}
, and the “sketching phase feature map ” are
random periodic features

\Psi=\Psi_{f}
,
i.e.
, a modification of the RFF where the complex exponential is replaced by a generic periodic function
f
.
This allows to obtain at last, as a corollary of all the results above, formal statistical learning guarantees for the ACL scheme for the pair
(\Phi_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{RFF}},\Psi_{q})
considered in
[
10
]
, which ensures the theoretical soundness of that approach. To demonstrate the broader applicability of our results, we also apply them to the pair
(\Phi_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{RFF}},\Psi_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{mod}})
, where
\Psi_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{mod}}
represents the—hardware-friendly—modulo measurement map studied (among others) in
[
13
]
.

Moving on, we provide in Sec.
V
further extensive empirical validations for the ACL strategy, confirming that, as our theory supports, it can be extended to other tasks (such as GMM) as well as other feature maps (such as
\Psi_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{mod}}
the modulo measurements map). We also highlight the practical advantage of our quantized compressive learning strategy on a large-scale audio classification task. Finally, we conclude in Sec.
VI
.

II-A

Statistical Learning

In the
statistical learning
(SL) framework
[
16
,
17
]
, one assumes that the signals of interest are generated by a
data distribution

\mathcal{P}_{0}\in\mathcal{M}
.
The goal is then to fit some machine learning model, parametrized by a vector
\bm{\theta}\in\Theta
, to that distribution. More precisely,
one seeks the model parameters
\bm{\theta}^{*}
that minimize the
risk
objective
\mathcal{R}(\bm{\theta};\mathcal{P}_{0}):=\mathop{{}\mathbb{E}}_{\bm{x}\sim\mathcal{P}_{0}}\ell(\bm{x},\bm{\theta})
,
i.e.
, the expectation of a loss
\ell:\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\Theta\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
with respect to the data distribution:

\bm{\theta}^{*}\in\arg\min_{\bm{\theta}\in\Theta}\mathcal{R}(\bm{\theta};\mathcal{P}_{0})=\arg\min_{\bm{\theta}\in\Theta}\>\textstyle\mathop{{}\mathbb{E}}_{\bm{x}\sim\mathcal{P}_{0}}\ell(\bm{x},\bm{\theta}).

(5)

In practice, the true data distribution
\mathcal{P}_{0}
is unknown, but a dataset
\mathcal{X}=\{\bm{x}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
of
n
samples
\bm{x}_{i}\sim_{i.i.d.}\mathcal{P}_{0}
is available. The “ideal” risk minimization (
5
) is thus replaced by
empirical risk minimization
(ERM), which uses the empirical distribution
\widehat{\mathcal{P}}_{\mathcal{X}}:=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{\bm{x}_{i}}\in\mathcal{M}
instead of the true data distribution:

\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}\in\arg\min_{\bm{\theta}\in\Theta}\mathcal{R}(\bm{\theta};\widehat{\mathcal{P}}_{\mathcal{X}})=\arg\min_{\bm{\theta}\in\Theta}\textstyle\sum_{\bm{x}_{i}\in\mathcal{X}}\ell(\bm{x}_{i},\bm{\theta}).

(6)

A regularization term can also be added to (
6
),
e.g.
, to avoid overfitting
\mathcal{X}
.

Many common machine learning task can be cast into the SL framework. In classification for instance,
\ell(\bm{x};\bm{\theta})\in\{0,1\}
is the 0-1 loss function, equal to
1
(resp.
0
) whenever the decision function associated with
\bm{\theta}
classifies
\bm{x}
correctly (resp. incorrectly). In this work we focus on two unsupervised tasks: k-means and Gaussian mixture modeling.

As summarized in Table
II
,
k-means clustering
seeks
K
centroids
\bm{c}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
which minimize the sum of squared errors (SSE) over the dataset (the “error” is the distance between each sample
\bm{x}_{i}
and the centroid closest to it):

\mathrm{SSE}(\bm{\theta};\mathcal{X}):=\textstyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\min_{1\leqslant k\leqslant K}\|\bm{x}_{i}-\bm{c}_{k}\|_{2}^{2}

(7)

On the other hand,
Gaussian mixture modeling
(GMM) seeks a weighted mixture of
K
Gaussians
\mathcal{N}(\bm{\mu}_{k},\bm{\Gamma}_{k})
(
i.e.
, weights
w_{k}\geqslant 0
that sum to one, centers
\bm{\mu}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and positive definite covariance matrices
\bm{\Gamma}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
) that
maximizes
the log-likelihood (LL) of the dataset
\mathcal{X}
:

\mathrm{LL}(\bm{\theta};\mathcal{X}):=\textstyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log\big(\sum_{k=1}^{K}w_{k}p_{\mathcal{N}}(\bm{x}_{i};\bm{\mu}_{k},\bm{\Gamma}_{k})\big),

(8)

where
p_{\mathcal{N}}(\bm{x};\bm{\mu},\bm{\Gamma})
is the probability density function of the Gaussian distribution
\mathcal{N}(\bm{\mu},\bm{\Gamma})
evaluated at
\bm{x}
.

The central goal of SL is to control the
excess risk

\mathcal{R}(\widetilde{\bm{\theta}};\mathcal{P}_{0})-\mathcal{R}(\bm{\theta}^{*};\mathcal{P}_{0})
(also known as generalization error for prediction tasks), in the form of
statistical guarantees
: for some
\delta\in(0,1)
and
\eta>0
, the ERM solution
\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}
satisfies

\mathbb{P}[\mathcal{R}(\widetilde{\bm{\theta}};\mathcal{P}_{0})-\mathcal{R}(\bm{\theta}^{*};\mathcal{P}_{0})\leqslant\eta]\geqslant 1-\delta.

(9)

In words, this guarantee ensures that, with probability larger than
1-\delta
over the sampling of
\mathcal{X}
, the estimate of the ERM is not worse than the optimal solution
\bm{\theta}^{*}
(on the true data distribution
\mathcal{P}_{0}
) by a margin smaller than
\eta
; this is also called Probably Approximately Correct (PAC) learning
[
17
]
.

II-B

Compressive Learning

The computational resources (such as memory and time) required to solve (
6

n
(consider for example that merely evaluating of the ERM cost at any one solution
\bm{\theta}
already requires a full pass over the
n
learning examples). Compressive learning (CL)
[
4
]
avoids this issue, since the
m

m\ll nd
(in fact,
m
does not dependent on
n

More precisely, CL theory
[
4
]
actually introduces a general
sketch operator

\mathcal{A}_{\Phi}

\mathcal{M}

\mathcal{P}\in\mathcal{M}
by computing
m

\Phi

Given a feature map

\textstyle\mathcal{A}_{\Phi}(\mathcal{P}):=\mathop{{}\mathbb{E}}_{\bm{x}\sim\mathcal{P}}\Phi(\bm{x})=\int\Phi(\bm{x})\mathrm{d}\mathcal{P}(\bm{x})\in\mathbb{C}^{m}.

(10)

In particular, the
sketch of a dataset

\mathcal{X}=\{\bm{x}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}

, is actually the sketch of its empirical distribution,

\textstyle\bm{z}_{\Phi,\mathcal{X}}:=\mathcal{A}_{\Phi}(\widehat{\mathcal{P}}_{\mathcal{X}})=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Phi(\bm{x}_{i})\in\mathbb{C}^{m}.

(11)

II-C

Asymmetric CL with distorted or quantized sketches

To summarize, the typical CL scenario consists of two phases: the sketching phase where the dataset
\mathcal{X}
is harshly compressed to a sketch vector
\bm{z}_{\Phi,\mathcal{X}}
(by averaging some feature map
\Phi
over the data samples), followed by the learning phase, where the desired machine learning model (parametrized by
\bm{\theta}
) is then extracted by solving
\widehat{\bm{\theta}}\in\arg\min_{\bm{\theta}\in\Theta}\mathcal{C}_{\Phi}(\bm{\theta};\bm{z}_{\Phi,\mathcal{X}})
,
i.e.
, the sketch matching principle described in (
13
). The success of this scheme can thus be guaranteed by establishing the LRIP of the sketch operator
\mathcal{A}_{\Phi}
associated to the “reference” feature map
\Phi:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}^{m}
(
e.g.
, the random Fourier features,
\Phi_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{RFF}}
), which must hold over the relevant task’s model set
\mathcal{G}
.

In this work, we question the possibility to extend this scheme by allowing the sketching phase to use a different—or “distorted”—feature map
\Psi\neq\Phi
(
e.g.
, the binarized RFF,
\Psi_{q}
from (
2
)). This amounts to studying this
asymmetric compressive learning
scenario (ACL for short): given a
reference feature map

\Phi
and a different
distorted feature map

\Psi
, and having observed the “distorted sketch”
\bm{z}_{\Psi,\mathcal{X}}=\mathcal{A}_{\Psi}(\widehat{\mathcal{P}}_{\mathcal{X}})=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Psi(\bm{x}_{i})
, we select the parameters
\widehat{\bm{\theta}}^{\prime}
that solve the “asymmetric sketch matching” problem,
i.e.
,

\widehat{\bm{\theta}}^{\prime}\in\arg\min_{\bm{\theta}\in\Theta}\mathcal{C}_{\Phi}(\bm{\theta};\bm{z}_{\Psi,\mathcal{X}})=\|\bm{z}_{\Psi,\mathcal{X}}-\mathcal{A}_{\Phi}(\mathcal{P}_{\bm{\theta}})\|_{2}.

(17)

To be perfectly clear, the “asymmetry” here refers to the fact that
only
the sketching map is distorted, since we still use the reference map
\Phi
to learn from
\bm{z}_{\Psi,\mathcal{X}}
, as precised by the subscript in the cost
\mathcal{C}_{\Phi}
(the only difference with the symmetric sketch matching from (
13
) is the sketch map).

This (perhaps surprising) strategy is inspired by a well-known equivalent in classical signal estimation: provided a signal follows a low-complexity model (such as a sparse or a low-rank description), one can treat its nonlinearly distorted measurements as noisy linear observations,
e.g.
, ignoring quantization, with provable reconstruction guarantees if the involved nonlinearity respects a few mild conditions
[
21
]
. Our work adopts a similar approach to get risk control guarantees for (
17
), of the same form as Thm.
1
. This enables to quantify when (and under which conditions on
\Psi
) the asymmetric sketch matching scheme (
17
) succeeds.

\Phi
is the random Fourier features map
\Phi_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{RFF}}

1

III
A generic guarantee for ACL

Our goal is thus to prove guarantees in the spirit of Thm.
1
for the ACL scheme. To derive unifying guarantees for each combination of model set
\mathcal{G}
(task), reference map
\Phi
and distortion
\Psi
, we build upon the existing LRIP (characterizing the compatibility between
\mathcal{G}
and
\Phi
), which we combine with another property, the
Limited Projected Distortion
(LPD) property (extending its definition from
[
26
]
). The LPD better characterizes the closeness between the distorted and reference sketches,
\bm{z}_{\Psi,\mathcal{X}}
and
\bm{z}_{\Phi,\mathcal{X}}
, than the too restrictive Euclidean distance
\|\bm{z}_{\Psi,\mathcal{X}}-\bm{z}_{\Phi,\mathcal{X}}\|_{2}
: for example, with the quantized RFF sketch
[
10
]
, this distance does not vanish as
m
grows to infinity.

The LPD relies on an additional assumption characterizing over which datasets
\mathcal{X}
it should hold.

Assumption 1
.

There exists a set
\widehat{\mathcal{G}}\subseteq\mathcal{M}
, coined
empirical set
, such that, for any considered dataset
\mathcal{X}
, the empirical distribution
\widehat{\mathcal{P}}_{\mathcal{X}}
belongs to
\widehat{\mathcal{G}}
.

Note that Assumption
1
is quite permissive. We allow in particular to pick
\widehat{\mathcal{G}}=\mathcal{M}
, which means it holds trivially. Moreover, even when
\widehat{\mathcal{G}}\subsetneq\mathcal{M}
, it is quite mild; it is for example fulfilled under the natural assumption that the data samples
\bm{x}_{i}
belong to a bounded domain
\Sigma
(more on this later). That being said, we can now formalize the LPD.

Definition 4

(LPD)
.

Given the empirical set
\widehat{\mathcal{G}}\subseteq\mathcal{M}
, an error
\epsilon>0
, a reference sketch operator
\mathcal{A}_{\Phi}
, and a (task-dependent) model set
\mathcal{G}\subset\mathcal{M}
, we say that the distorted sketch operator
\mathcal{A}_{\Psi}
satisfies the LPD of error
\epsilon
on
\widehat{\mathcal{G}}
with respect to
\mathcal{A}_{\Phi}
and
\mathcal{G}
, or shortly
\mathrm{LPD}(\epsilon;\widehat{\mathcal{G}},\mathcal{G},\mathcal{A}_{\Phi})
, if

\textstyle\forall\mathcal{P}\in\widehat{\mathcal{G}},\mathcal{Q}\in\mathcal{G},\quad|\langle\mathcal{A}_{\Psi}(\mathcal{P})-\mathcal{A}_{\Phi}(\mathcal{P}),\mathcal{A}_{\Phi}(\mathcal{Q})\rangle|\leqslant\epsilon.

(22)

In words, the LPD thus ensures that, for any considered dataset
\mathcal{X}
, the difference between its distorted sketch
\bm{z}_{\Psi,\mathcal{X}}
and its reference sketch
\bm{z}_{\Phi,\mathcal{X}}
is sufficiently small when projected on any possible “reference sketch” of the model set,
i.e.
, projected on any
\mathcal{A}_{\Phi}(\mathcal{P}_{\bm{\theta}})
for all parameters
\bm{\theta}\in\Theta
. As made clear below,
\{\mathcal{A}_{\Phi}(\mathcal{P}_{\bm{\theta}}):\mathcal{P}_{\bm{\theta}}\in\mathcal{G}\}
contains actually the “directions” that matter for solving (
17
).

Our first main result (Prop.
1
) states that if the reference sketching operator
\mathcal{A}_{\Phi}
satisfies the LRIP, and the distorted sketching operator
\mathcal{A}_{\Psi}
the LPD, then the excess risk of the ACL solution (
17
) can be controlled. To prove this, we adapt the proof of Thm
1
found in
[
4
,
27
]
, and we leverage the LPD to show that distorting the sketch does not modify the cost function too much—as first shown in Lemma
1
.

Lemma 1
.

Assume
\mathcal{A}_{\Psi}
satisfies the
\mathrm{LPD}(\epsilon;\widehat{\mathcal{G}},\mathcal{G},\mathcal{A}_{\Phi})
. For any
\widehat{\mathcal{P}}_{\mathcal{X}}\in\widehat{\mathcal{G}}
, the asymmetric sketch matching solution
\widehat{\bm{\theta}}^{\prime}
to (
17
) is sub-optimal with respect to the symmetric matching solution
\widehat{\bm{\theta}}
to (
13
) by at most

\mathcal{C}_{\Phi}(\widehat{\bm{\theta}}^{\prime};\bm{z}_{\Phi,\mathcal{X}})-\mathcal{C}_{\Phi}(\widehat{\bm{\theta}};\bm{z}_{\Phi,\mathcal{X}})\leqslant 2\sqrt{\epsilon}.

(23)

Proof.

For conciseness, we drop the
\mathcal{X}
subscript and denote
\bm{a}:=\mathcal{A}_{\Phi}(\mathcal{P}_{\widehat{\bm{\theta}}})
,
\bm{a}^{\prime}:=\mathcal{A}_{\Phi}(\mathcal{P}_{\widehat{\bm{\theta}^{\prime}}})
. The LPD implies both

\displaystyle\textstyle\|\bm{z}_{\Psi}-\bm{a}\|_{2}^{2}-\|\bm{z}_{\Phi}-\bm{a}\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\textstyle\leqslant 2\epsilon+\|\bm{z}_{\Psi}\|_{2}^{2}-\|\bm{z}_{\Phi}\|_{2}^{2},

\displaystyle\textstyle\|\bm{z}_{\Phi}-\bm{a}^{\prime}\|_{2}^{2}-\|\bm{z}_{\Psi}-\bm{a}^{\prime}\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\textstyle\leqslant 2\epsilon+\|\bm{z}_{\Phi}\|_{2}^{2}-\|\bm{z}_{\Psi}\|_{2}^{2}.

By optimality of (
17
),
\|\bm{z}_{\Psi}-\bm{a}^{\prime}\|_{2}^{2}-\|\bm{z}_{\Psi}-\bm{a}\|_{2}^{2}\leqslant 0
, and adding the three inequalities together gives
\|\bm{z}_{\Phi}-\bm{a}^{\prime}\|_{2}^{2}\leqslant\|\bm{z}_{\Phi}-\bm{a}\|_{2}^{2}+4\epsilon
; a square root completes the proof.
∎

Proposition 1

(Asymmetric sketch matching risk control)
.

Assume
\mathcal{A}_{\Phi}
satisfies the
\mathrm{LRIP}(\gamma;\mathcal{G})
and
\mathcal{A}_{\Psi}
the
\mathrm{LPD}(\epsilon;\widehat{\mathcal{G}},\mathcal{G},\mathcal{A}_{\Phi})
. The solution
\widehat{\bm{\theta}}^{\prime}
to the asymmetric problem (
17
) satisfies
\mathcal{R}(\widehat{\bm{\theta}}^{\prime};\mathcal{P}_{0})-\mathcal{R}(\bm{\theta}^{*};\mathcal{P}_{0})\leqslant\eta^{\prime}
, where

\eta^{\prime}=2D(\mathcal{P}_{0},\mathcal{G})+4\gamma\|\mathcal{A}_{\Phi}(\mathcal{P}_{0})-\mathcal{A}_{\Phi}(\widehat{\mathcal{P}}_{\mathcal{X}})\|_{2}+4\gamma\sqrt{\epsilon},

(24)

with
D(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{G})
as in Thm.
1
.

Proof.

We use the same notations as in the previous proof. For some arbitrary
\mathcal{Q}\in\mathcal{G}
, since
\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal{R}}
is a seminorm
[
4
]
,

\|\mathcal{P}_{\widehat{\bm{\theta}}^{\prime}}-\mathcal{P}_{0}\|_{\mathcal{R}}\leqslant\|\mathcal{P}_{\widehat{\bm{\theta}}^{\prime}}-\mathcal{Q}\|_{\mathcal{R}}+\|\mathcal{Q}-\mathcal{P}_{0}\|_{\mathcal{R}}.

Since
\mathcal{P}_{\widehat{\bm{\theta}}^{\prime}},\mathcal{Q}\in\mathcal{G}
, the LRIP and the triangle inequality give

\begin{split}\|\mathcal{P}_{\widehat{\bm{\theta}}^{\prime}}-\mathcal{Q}\|_{\mathcal{R}}&\leqslant\gamma\|\mathcal{A}_{\Phi}(\mathcal{P}_{\widehat{\bm{\theta}}^{\prime}})-\mathcal{A}_{\Phi}(\mathcal{Q})\|_{2}\\
&\leqslant\gamma\|\bm{a}^{\prime}-\bm{z}_{\Phi}\|_{2}+\gamma\|\bm{z}_{\Phi}-\mathcal{A}_{\Phi}(\mathcal{Q})\|_{2}.\end{split}

Using Lemma
1
then the optimality of (
13
), we get

\begin{split}\textstyle\frac{\|\mathcal{P}_{\widehat{\bm{\theta}}^{\prime}}-\mathcal{Q}\|_{\mathcal{R}}}{\gamma}&\leqslant 2\sqrt{\epsilon}+\|\bm{a}-\bm{z}_{\Phi}\|_{2}+\|\bm{z}_{\Phi}-\mathcal{A}_{\Phi}(\mathcal{Q})\|_{2}\\
&\leqslant 2\sqrt{\epsilon}+2\|\bm{z}_{\Phi}-\mathcal{A}_{\Phi}(\mathcal{Q})\|_{2}.\end{split}

We develop the second term with the triangle inequality,

\|\bm{z}_{\Phi}-\mathcal{A}_{\Phi}(\mathcal{Q})\|_{2}\leqslant\|\bm{z}_{\Phi}-\mathcal{A}_{\Phi}(\mathcal{P}_{0})\|_{2}+\|\mathcal{A}_{\Phi}(\mathcal{P}_{0})-\mathcal{A}_{\Phi}(\mathcal{Q})\|_{2}.

Gathering the results, and taking the infimum with respect to
\mathcal{Q}\in\mathcal{G}
, we obtain that
\|\mathcal{P}_{\widehat{\bm{\theta}}^{\prime}}-\mathcal{P}_{0}\|_{\mathcal{R}}\leqslant\frac{\eta^{\prime}}{2}
, with

\textstyle\frac{\eta^{\prime}}{2}=D(\mathcal{P}_{0},\mathcal{G})+2\gamma\|\bm{z}_{\Phi}-\mathcal{A}_{\Phi}(\mathcal{P}_{0})\|_{2}+2\gamma\sqrt{\epsilon}.

Finally, we combine this with the risk metric definition (
14
), and since the map
\bm{\theta}\mapsto\mathcal{P}_{\bm{\theta}}
satisfies the risk consistency property (
12
), we get

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}(\widehat{\bm{\theta}}^{\prime};\mathcal{P}_{0})-\mathcal{R}(\bm{\theta}^{*};\mathcal{P}_{0})

\displaystyle=\;\mathcal{R}(\widehat{\bm{\theta}}^{\prime};\mathcal{P}_{0})-\mathcal{R}(\widehat{\bm{\theta}}^{\prime};\mathcal{P}_{\widehat{\bm{\theta}}^{\prime}})+\mathcal{R}(\widehat{\bm{\theta}}^{\prime};\mathcal{P}_{\widehat{\bm{\theta}}^{\prime}})-\mathcal{R}(\bm{\theta}^{*};\mathcal{P}_{\widehat{\bm{\theta}}^{\prime}})

\displaystyle\qquad+\mathcal{R}(\bm{\theta}^{*};\mathcal{P}_{\widehat{\bm{\theta}}^{\prime}})-\mathcal{R}(\bm{\theta}^{*};\mathcal{P}_{0})

\displaystyle\leqslant\textstyle\frac{\eta^{\prime}}{2}+0+\frac{\eta^{\prime}}{2}=\eta^{\prime}.

∎

For comparison, the excess risk guarantee of Prop.
1
for the asymmetric sketch matching solution (
17
) is thus exactly the same as the guarantee in Thm.
1
for the symmetric solution (
13
), up to the additive term
4\sqrt{\epsilon}
in the excess risk bound (
i.e.
,
\eta^{\prime}=\eta+4\sqrt{\epsilon}
), which expresses the “mismatch” between
\Psi
and
\Phi
. Whenever the LPD holds with a reasonably small error
\epsilon
, we can thus expect that the asymmetric scheme (learning from the distorted sketch
\bm{z}_{\Psi,\mathcal{X}}
) will perform almost as well as the symmetric one (learning from the reference sketch
\bm{z}_{\Phi,\mathcal{X}}
).

Of course, it remains to show that the LPD actually holds in practice to complete this guarantee, which we tackle next.

IV
Proving the LPD for quantized CL

In this section, we first provide one possible strategy to prove the LPD. Under an additional assumption (which we first introduce and show to be met in practice for k-means and GMM), it is sufficient to prove a somewhat simpler “signal-level” version of the LPD instead (which we call signal-LPD, or
sLPD
for short). We then apply this strategy to the specific case where
\Phi=\Phi_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{RFF}}
are the random Fourier features and
\Psi=\Psi_{f}
are generic random periodic features (
18
),
i.e.
, the same RFF but where
t\mapsto\exp(\mathrm{i}\mkern 1.0mut)
is replaced by a generic periodic function
f(t)
. This finally allows us to obtain, as a particular case, statistical learning guarantees for the quantized CL scheme introduced in
[
10
]
,
i.e.
, where the distorted map is the binarized RFF
\Psi_{q}
(
2
). To demonstrate the generic nature of our result, we also apply it to the modulo features
\Psi_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{mod}}
.

IV-A

Assumption on the data domain

In practical machine learning applications, the data vectors
\bm{x}_{i}
do not take
any
possible value in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
—one can usually assume
a priori
that they belong to a compact set
\Sigma\subset\mathbb{R}
. We extend this assumption to the
probability distributions
involved in the compressive learning problem (
17
),
i.e.
, to the empirical distributions
\widehat{\mathcal{P}}_{\mathcal{X}}\in\widehat{\mathcal{G}}
(see Assumption
1
) and the parametric distributions
\mathcal{P}_{\bm{\theta}}\in\mathcal{G}
.

Assumption 2
.

For a compact set
\Sigma\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the model set
\mathcal{G}
and the empirical set
\widehat{\mathcal{G}}
are subsets of
\mathcal{M}_{\Sigma,\zeta}\subset\mathcal{M}
, the set of probability measures that are
mostly
supported on
\Sigma
,
i.e.
, for some
0\leqslant\zeta<1
,
\mathcal{G},\widehat{\mathcal{G}}\subset\mathcal{M}_{\Sigma,\zeta}
with

\textstyle\mathcal{M}_{\Sigma,\zeta}:=\{\mathcal{P}\in\mathcal{M}:\mathcal{P}(\Sigma)=\int_{\Sigma}\mathrm{d}\mathcal{P}\geqslant 1-\zeta\}.

(25)

In particular, for
\zeta=0
all
\mathcal{P}\in\mathcal{M}_{\Sigma,0}
are “almost surely” supported on
\Sigma
,
i.e.
,
{\rm supp}\,(\mathcal{P})\subset\Sigma
.

Assumption
2
holds in practice. Consider the common case where there are known lower and upper bounds
\bm{l},\bm{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
for the values that the learning samples
\bm{x}_{i}
can take (
e.g.
, due to physical constraints). This means that all the learning examples lie in a “box”
\Sigma_{\bm{l},\bm{u}}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
:

\bm{x}_{i}\in\Sigma_{\bm{l},\bm{u}}:=\{\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\bm{l}\leqslant\bm{x}\leqslant\bm{u}\}.

Since all the examples of any considered dataset
\mathcal{X}
necessarily lie in that box, this directly implies that all the related empirical distributions
\widehat{\mathcal{P}}_{\mathcal{X}}\in\widehat{\mathcal{G}}
satisfy
\widehat{\mathcal{P}}_{\mathcal{X}}\in\mathcal{M}_{\Sigma_{\bm{l},\bm{u}},0}
.

The inclusion of the model set
\mathcal{G}
can be reached from additional constraints on
\Theta
, imposed during the optimization procedure. In the simplest case, the
k-means
task (Table
II
, left), the optimal centroids obviously lie inside the data-enclosing box, hence it makes sense (as done in
[
6
]
) to restrict the problem to
\bm{c}_{k}\in\Sigma_{\bm{l},\bm{u}}
. These constraints can be encoded in
\Theta
, which in turn imply that
\mathcal{G}\subset\mathcal{M}_{\Sigma_{\bm{l},\bm{u}},0}
. If the data-enclosing box
\Sigma_{\bm{l},\bm{u}}
is known, Assumption
2
thus holds for k-means, with
\Sigma=\Sigma_{\bm{l},\bm{u}}
and
\zeta=0
.

For the
Gaussian mixture modeling
task (Table
II
, right), one can similarly enforce that the Gaussian centers lie in the box
\bm{\mu}_{k}\in\Sigma_{\bm{l},\bm{u}}
. Moreover, given that the data lie in a bounded domain, it is also reasonable to bound the variance of the Gaussian modes (the typical spread of a Gaussian mode should not be much larger than the box). This can be done by bounding the eigenvalues of the covariance matrices
\bm{\Gamma}_{k}
,
i.e.
,
\lambda_{\max}(\bm{\Gamma}_{k})\leqslant S
for a bound
S>0
to be set according to the size of the box. Assuming
diagonal covariances, as commonly done CL for GMM
[
7
]
, the following lemma shows that
\mathcal{G}\subset\mathcal{M}_{\Sigma^{(\rho)},\zeta}
for some slightly extended set
\Sigma^{(\rho)}\supset\Sigma_{\bm{l},\bm{u}}
and small
\zeta>0
.

Lemma 2

(Assumption
2
for GMM with box contraints)
.

Let
\mathcal{G}
be the model set of a GMM task with diagonal covariances
\bm{\Gamma}_{k}
and the box constraints
\bm{\mu}_{k}\in\Sigma_{\bm{l},\bm{u}}
and
\lambda_{\max}(\bm{\Gamma}_{k})\leqslant S
. Given
\rho>d
, we define the bounds of an “extended” box
\widetilde{\bm{l}}=\bm{l}-\rho S\bm{1}
and
\widetilde{\bm{u}}=\bm{u}+\rho S\bm{1}
, with
\bm{1}=(1,1,...,1)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Then, for
\Sigma^{(\rho)}=\Sigma_{\widetilde{\bm{l}},\widetilde{\bm{u}}}
, we have

\textstyle\mathcal{G}\subset\mathcal{M}_{\Sigma^{(\rho)},\zeta},\quad\text{with}\ \zeta\lesssim e^{-\rho^{2}}.

Proof.

From (
25
), we can set
\zeta=\sup\{\int_{\Sigma^{(\rho)c}}\mathrm{d}\mathcal{P}:\mathcal{P}\in\mathcal{G}\}
to ensure
\mathcal{G}\subset\mathcal{M}_{\Sigma^{(\rho)},\zeta}
,
i.e.
, the maximal failure probability is reached when the largest amount of the GMMs probability mass lies outside
\Sigma^{(\rho)}
. For diagonal covariances, this is reached if we have both
\bm{\Gamma}_{k}=S\bm{I}_{d}
,
i.e.
, the Gaussian is maximally spread in each dimension, and, by symmetry, each Gaussian mode is located at a corner of the box
\Sigma_{\bm{l},\bm{u}}
,
e.g.
, with
\bm{\mu}_{k}=\bm{u}
for all
k
. Denoting by
\mathcal{P}^{*}\subset\mathcal{G}
this GMM configuration, we easily show that, for
\Sigma^{(\rho)}=\Sigma_{\widetilde{\bm{l}},\widetilde{\bm{u}}}
,
i.e.
,
\Sigma^{(\rho)}=\Sigma_{\bm{l},\bm{u}}+\rho S\mathbb{B}_{\infty}^{d}
,
\int_{\Sigma^{(\rho)}}\mathrm{d}\mathcal{P}^{*}
is bounded by
\phi^{d}(\rho)
, with
\phi
the cumulative density function of a one-dimensional standard normal random variable. Using well-known Gaussian tail bounds
[
28
]
, we get
\zeta=1-\phi^{d}(\rho)\leqslant d\cdot\phi(\rho)\leqslant\frac{d}{\rho\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-\rho^{2}/2}
, which decays exponentially fast in
\rho
; in particular
\zeta\lesssim e^{-\rho^{2}}
when
\rho>d
.
∎

To wrap up, Assumption
2
thus seems fairly reasonable for the GMM task as well. There is however still the issue of which value of
\rho
one should pick in Lemma
2
; we’ll come back to this nontrivial question at the end of this section.

IV-B

Reducing the LPD to the signal-level LPD

Under Assumption
2
, to have the LPD, it is sufficient to prove a simpler “signal-LPD” (sLPD) over
\Sigma
, defined as

\forall\bm{x},\bm{y}\in\Sigma,\>|\langle\Psi(\bm{x})-\Phi(\bm{x}),\Phi(\bm{y})\rangle|\leqslant\epsilon_{s}.

(26)

This is formalized by the following lemma.

Lemma 3

(sLPD implies LPD)
.

For a compact set
\Sigma
and
0\leqslant\zeta<1
, assume
\mathcal{G},\widehat{\mathcal{G}}\subset\mathcal{M}_{\Sigma,\zeta}
and the sLPD (
26
) holds on
\Sigma
with error
\epsilon_{s}
. Moreover, assume that the feature maps are bounded,
\sup_{\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\|\Phi(\bm{x})\|_{2}\leqslant C_{\Phi}
(similarly for
\Psi
).
Then,
\mathcal{A}_{\Psi}
has the
\mathrm{LPD}(\epsilon;\widehat{\mathcal{G}},\mathcal{G},\mathcal{A}_{\Phi})
with error
\epsilon=\epsilon_{s}+c\zeta
, where
c\leqslant 2C_{\Phi}(C_{\Phi}+C_{\Psi})
.

Proof.

Define the “difference kernel”

\widetilde{\kappa}(\bm{x},\bm{y}):=\langle\Psi(\bm{x})-\Phi(\bm{x}),\Phi(\bm{y})\rangle,

which is bounded by
|\widetilde{\kappa}(\bm{x},\bm{y})|\leqslant C_{\Phi}(C_{\Phi}+C_{\Psi})=:C_{\widetilde{\kappa}}
using Cauchy-Schwarz. Given any
\mathcal{P},\mathcal{Q}\in\mathcal{M}_{\Sigma,\zeta}
, we have

\displaystyle\textstyle|\langle\mathcal{A}_{\Psi}(\mathcal{P})-\mathcal{A}_{\Phi}(\mathcal{P}),\mathcal{A}_{\Phi}(\mathcal{Q})\rangle|=\big|\mathop{{}\mathbb{E}}_{\bm{x}\sim\mathcal{P}}\mathop{{}\mathbb{E}}_{\bm{y}\sim\mathcal{Q}}\widetilde{\kappa}(\bm{x},\bm{y})\big|

\displaystyle\textstyle\leqslant\mathop{{}\mathbb{E}}_{\bm{x}\sim\mathcal{P}}\mathop{{}\mathbb{E}}_{\bm{y}\sim\mathcal{Q}}\big|\widetilde{\kappa}(\bm{x},\bm{y})\big|

\displaystyle=\textstyle I(\Sigma,\Sigma)+I(\Sigma^{c},\Sigma)+I(\Sigma,\Sigma^{c})+I(\Sigma^{c},\Sigma^{c})

\displaystyle\leqslant\epsilon_{s}(1-\zeta)^{2}+C_{\widetilde{\kappa}}(2\zeta(1-\zeta)+\zeta^{2})\leqslant\epsilon_{s}+2C_{\widetilde{\kappa}}\zeta,

with
I(U,V)=\int_{U}\int_{V}\left|\widetilde{\kappa}(\bm{x},\bm{y})\right|\>\mathrm{d}\mathcal{P}(\bm{x})\mathrm{d}\mathcal{Q}(\bm{y})
,
U,V\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
∎

Combining with Prop.
1
, under Assumption
2
, the sLPD (
26
) with error
\epsilon_{s}
thus implies the excess risk is controlled, with an additive increase given by
4\sqrt{\epsilon_{s}+c\zeta}
compared to Thm.
1
.

IV-C

Proving the signal-LPD for random periodic features

The tools developed up to now were purposefully as general as possible. We now focus on proving the LPD for the case where the reference feature map are random Fourier features (
1
)
\Phi_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{RFF}}
, and the distorted feature map are random periodic features (
18
)
\Psi_{f}
; as a reminder, these are defined by replacing
\exp(\mathrm{i}\mkern 1.0mut)
in the usual RFF by a generic
2\pi
-periodic function
f(t)
,
i.e.
,
\textstyle\Psi_{f}(\bm{x}):=\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}f(\bm{\Omega}^{\top}\bm{x}+\bm{\xi})
. Quantized RFF (
2
)
\Psi_{q}
are a particular case of RPF, further explored in the next subsection.

To satisfy the sLPD, the RPF feature map must be scaled by
F_{1}
, the first Fourier Series (FS) coefficient of
f

[
10
,
15
]
. We thus consider the
renormalized features

\overline{\Psi}_{f}:=\frac{1}{F_{1}}\Psi_{f}
during the ACL optimization procedure. This renormalization is not restrictive, it can for example be performed after the sketch has been computed, since
\bm{z}_{\overline{\Psi}_{f},\mathcal{X}}=\frac{1}{F_{1}}\bm{z}_{\Psi_{f},\mathcal{X}}
.

We can then prove our second main result: the LPD (
22
) holds—with high probability on the draw of
\bm{\Omega}
and
\bm{\xi}
—for the (normalized) RPF sketch operator. The proof is based on Lemma
3
to reduce the LPD to the sLPD, and the latter is shown using a result of our previous work
[
15
, Cor.1]
.

Proposition 2

(LPD for normalized RPF)
.

Let us consider a compact set
\Sigma
with Kolmogorov
\nu
-entropy
\mathcal{H}_{\nu}(\Sigma)<\infty
, and

•

the random Fourier features
\Phi_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{RFF}}
defined in (
1
) associated with the distribution
\Lambda
(which generates the
m
columns of
\bm{\Omega}
), with
smoothness constant

\textstyle C_{\Lambda}:=\max_{\bm{a}\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\|\bm{a}\|_{2}=1}\;\mathop{{}\mathbb{E}}_{\bm{\omega}\sim\Lambda}|\bm{\omega}^{\top}\bm{a}|<\infty,

(27)

•

the related RPF
\overline{\Psi}_{f}:=\frac{1}{F_{1}}\Psi_{f}
, with
\Psi_{f}
defined in (
18
), and
f
a periodic, mean Lipschitz smooth function with constant
L^{\mu}_{f}<\infty
(see Def.
1
), FS coefficients
\{F_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
, and
C_{f}:=(1+\|f\|_{\infty}/|F_{1}|)<\infty
.

Given some
0\leqslant\zeta<1
and
\epsilon_{0}>0
, assume that:

(i)
\mathcal{G},\widehat{\mathcal{G}}\subset\mathcal{M}_{\Sigma,\zeta}
(Assumption
2
);

(ii) the sketch dimension
m
satisfies

m\geqslant 128\cdot\epsilon_{0}^{-2}\cdot\mathcal{H}_{\epsilon_{0}/c_{f}}(\Sigma)

with constant
c_{f}:=4C_{\Lambda}(4+L^{\mu}_{f}/|F_{1}|)
.

Then, with probability exceeding
1-3\exp(-m\epsilon_{0}^{2}/64)
on the draw of
\bm{\Omega}
and
\bm{\xi}
, the normalized RPF sketch operator
\mathcal{A}_{\overline{\Psi}_{f}}
has the LPD over
\widehat{\mathcal{G}}
with respect to
\mathcal{A}_{\Phi_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{RFF}}}
, with error

\epsilon=C_{f}\big(\epsilon_{0}+2\zeta\big).

Proof.

Let us define the periodic function
\widehat{f}(t)=\frac{1}{D}\big(\frac{1}{F_{1}}f(t)-\exp(\mathrm{i}\mkern 1.0mut)\big)
, with
D:=\max(1,\|\frac{1}{F_{1}}f(\cdot)-\exp(\mathrm{i}\mkern 1.0mu\cdot)\|_{\infty})
. Since
\|\exp(\mathrm{i}\mkern 1.0mu\cdot)\|_{\infty}\leqslant 1
and
\|\widehat{f}\|_{\infty}\leqslant 1
, Corollary 1 from
[
15
]
ensures that, for any
\epsilon_{0}>0
, if
m\geqslant 128\cdot\epsilon_{0}^{-2}\cdot\mathcal{H}_{\epsilon_{0}/c}(\Sigma)
, then, with probability at least
1-3\exp(-m\epsilon_{0}^{2}/64)
, we have for all
\bm{x},\bm{y}\in\Sigma

\textstyle\frac{1}{m}\big|\langle\widehat{f}(\bm{\Omega}^{\top}\bm{x}+\bm{\xi}),\exp(\mathrm{i}\mkern 1.0mu(\bm{\Omega}^{\top}\bm{y}+\bm{\xi}))\rangle\big|\leqslant\epsilon_{0},

(28)

where the constant
c
in the metric entropy radius is

c=4C_{\Lambda}(L^{\mu}_{\widehat{f}}+L^{\mu}_{\exp(\mathrm{i}\mkern 1.0mu\cdot)}+2\min(L^{\mu}_{\widehat{f}},L^{\mu}_{\exp(\mathrm{i}\mkern 1.0mu\cdot)})).

By definition of
\widehat{f}
,
\Phi_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{RFF}}
, and
\overline{\Psi}_{f}
, the bound (
28
) is equivalent to the sLPD property (
26
) with error
\epsilon_{s}=D\epsilon_{0}
.

The mean Lipschitz constant of the relevant functions reads
L^{\mu}_{\exp(\mathrm{i}\mkern 1.0mu\cdot)}\leqslant 1
and
L^{\mu}_{\widehat{f}}\leqslant D^{-1}(\frac{1}{|F_{1}|}L^{\mu}_{f}+L^{\mu}_{\exp(\mathrm{i}\mkern 1.0mu\cdot)})
, and we apply the simplification
\min(L^{\mu}_{\widehat{f}},L^{\mu}_{\exp(\mathrm{i}\mkern 1.0mu\cdot)})\leqslant L^{\mu}_{\exp(\mathrm{i}\mkern 1.0mu\cdot)}
. Since
D\geqslant 1
, we thus get that
c
is upper bounded by

\textstyle 4C_{\Lambda}\big(3+D^{-1}(1+\frac{1}{|F_{1}|}L^{\mu}_{f})\big)\leqslant c_{f}:=4C_{\Lambda}(4+\frac{1}{|F_{1}|}L^{\mu}_{f}).

We can now turn the sLPD (
28
) into the LPD (with a proper rescaling of the error) by applying Lemma
3
. We note that
C_{\Phi_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{RFF}}}\leqslant\|\exp(\mathrm{i}\mkern 1.0mu\cdot)\|_{\infty}=1
and
C_{\overline{\Psi}_{f}}\leqslant\|f/F_{1}\|_{\infty}=\|f\|_{\infty}/|F_{1}|
, which implies that
2C_{\Phi_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{RFF}}}(C_{\Phi_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{RFF}}}+C_{\overline{\Psi}_{f}})\leqslant 2(1+\|f\|_{\infty}/|F_{1}|)=2C_{f}
. Finally, since
D\leqslant(1+\|f\|_{\infty}/F_{1})=C_{f}
, Lemma
3
shows that the desired LPD holds with error
C_{f}(\epsilon_{0}+2\zeta)
.

∎

IV-D

ACL guarantees for quantized or modulo contributions

To formulate our final guarantees and use Lemma
2
, we need this bound on the Kolmogorov entropy of
\Sigma_{\widetilde{\bm{l}},\widetilde{\bm{u}}}\supset\Sigma_{\bm{l},\bm{u}}
.

Lemma 4
.

In the notations of Lemma
2
, we have

\textstyle\mathcal{H}_{\nu}(\Sigma_{\widetilde{\bm{l}},\widetilde{\bm{u}}})\leqslant d\log\big(1+\frac{\sqrt{d}(2\rho S+\|\bm{u}-\bm{l}\|_{\infty})}{\nu}\big).

(29)

Proof.

Since
\Sigma_{\widetilde{\bm{l}},\widetilde{\bm{u}}}\subset\bm{c}+(\rho S+r)\mathbb{B}_{\infty}^{d}
, with
2\bm{c}=\bm{l}+\bm{u}
and
2r=\|\bm{u}-\bm{l}\|_{\infty}
, the entropy of
\Sigma_{\widetilde{\bm{l}},\widetilde{\bm{u}}}
is bounded by the one of
\mathcal{B}:=(\rho S+r)\mathbb{B}_{\infty}^{d}
. From Lemma 4.10 in
[
29
]
, given
\nu^{\prime}>0
, there exists a
\nu^{\prime}
-covering
\mathcal{S}
of
\mathcal{B}
in the
\ell_{\infty}
-metric—
i.e.
, for all
\bm{x}\in\mathcal{B}
, there is one
\bm{q}\in\mathcal{S}
such that
\|\bm{x}-\bm{q}\|_{\infty}\leqslant\nu^{\prime}
—where
|\mathcal{S}|\leqslant(1+\frac{2(\rho S+r)}{\nu^{\prime}})^{d}
. Since
\|\bm{x}-\bm{q}\|_{\infty}\geqslant\|\bm{x}-\bm{q}\|_{2}/\sqrt{d}
,
\mathcal{S}
is also a (
\ell_{2}
) covering of
\mathcal{B}
with radius
\nu^{\prime}\sqrt{d}
. Taking
\nu^{\prime}=\nu/\sqrt{d}
shows that the Kolmogorov
\nu
entropy of
\mathcal{B}
, and thus that of
\Sigma_{\widetilde{\bm{l}},\widetilde{\bm{u}}}
, is bounded as in (
29
).
∎

We can finally combine all our results together to obtain statistical learning guarantees (excess risk bounds) for the ACL problem with RPF sketches (and in particular, for quantized or modulo RFF), when solving the tasks of k-means and GMM specifically, under the assumption that the data is constrained in the box
\Sigma_{\bm{l},\bm{u}}
.

Corollary 1
.

In the notations of Prop.
2
, consider
\widehat{\bm{\theta}}^{\prime}
the solution to the ACL problem (
17
), where the sketch is obtained by the normalized random periodic features
\overline{\Psi}_{f}
, and the reference sketch map is
\Phi_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{RFF}}
.

We assume that,
(i)
all the data samples lie in a box
\Sigma_{\bm{l},\bm{u}}
, and the known upper and lower bounds
\bm{l}
and
\bm{u}
are used to restrict the optimization procedure (for GMM, a constant
S
is also used to upper bound the variance of the modes), and
(ii)
, the LRIP holds for
\Phi_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{RFF}}
on the chosen task with constant
\gamma
.

Then, given
\epsilon_{0}>0
, we have the following guarantees with probability at least
1-3\exp(-m\epsilon_{0}^{2}/64)
:

•

[for
k-means
] If the sketch size is at least

\textstyle m\geqslant 128\cdot\epsilon_{0}^{-2}\cdot d\log\big(1+\frac{c_{f}\sqrt{d}\|\bm{u}-\bm{l}\|_{\infty}}{\epsilon_{0}}\big),

then, for
C_{\rm km}:=4\gamma\sqrt{C_{f}}
, the excess risk is bounded by

\textstyle\mathcal{R}(\widehat{\bm{\theta}}^{\prime};\mathcal{P}_{0})-\mathcal{R}(\bm{\theta}^{*};\mathcal{P}_{0})\leqslant\eta+C_{\rm km}\sqrt{\epsilon_{0}},

with
\eta
the excess risk in symmetric CL (
16
).

•

[for
GMM
] For some
\rho>d
, if the sketch size is at least,

\begin{split}\textstyle m\geqslant 128\cdot\epsilon_{0}^{-2}\cdot d\log\big(1+\frac{c_{f}\sqrt{d}(2\rho S+\|\bm{u}-\bm{l}\|_{\infty})}{\epsilon_{0}}\big),\end{split}

then the excess risk is bounded by

\mathcal{R}(\widehat{\bm{\theta}}^{\prime};\mathcal{P}_{0})-\mathcal{R}(\bm{\theta}^{*};\mathcal{P}_{0})\leqslant\eta+C_{\rm km}\sqrt{\epsilon_{0}}+C_{\rm gmm}\cdot e^{-\rho^{2}/4},

with
C_{\rm gmm}:=4\gamma\big(\frac{2C_{f}^{2}}{\pi}\big)^{\frac{1}{4}}
, and
\eta
and
C_{\rm km}
as above.

Proof.

The proof consists in applying Prop.
1
and Prop.
2
, combined with Lem.
2
and Lem.
4
, the entropy of
\Sigma_{\bm{l},\bm{u}}
being found by setting
\rho=0
in (
29
).
∎

A few remarks can be made about this corollary. First, we rely on the fact that
\Phi_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{RFF}}
satisfies the LRIP; actually, ensuring that this holds (with high probability on the draw of
\bm{\Omega}
) imposes additional constraints on
m
. They depend on the considered task and the complexity of the related model set
\mathcal{G}
; for example, for a GMM with
K
modes in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we should have
m=\Omega(Kd)
, up to some additional factors and restrictions on
\mathcal{G}
(see
[
5
, Sec. 5.5]
and
[
9
]
). Second, the choice of the parameter
\rho
necessitates solving a trade-off: increasing
\rho
decreases the excess risk bound (excess risk proportional to
e^{-\rho^{2}/4}
), but at the cost of logarithmically increasing the required sketch size
m
.

Finally, Cor.
1
allows us to determine which between quantized or modulo RPF requires more measurements. Indeed, we compute in App.
A
that
C_{q}=1+\frac{\pi}{2\sqrt{2}}<C_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{mod}}=1+\frac{\sqrt{5}\pi}{4}
and
c_{q}=24C_{\Lambda}<c_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{mod}}=(24+2\sqrt{2})C_{\Lambda}
. Therefore, both for k-means and GMM, the sample complexities and the excess risk bounds of Cor.
1
shows that, while requiring a higher number of measurements, ACL with modulo features gets a higher bound on the excess risk compared to that of a quantized sketch. Thus, the sketch size must be further increased (to allow a smaller
\epsilon_{0}
) when using modulo sketches in order to meet quantized sketch performances. We observe this effect experimentally in the following section.

Experiments

We further validate the ACL approach through three practical numerical experiments. Going beyond the preliminary results in

VI
Conclusion

As a first main contribution, we defined the asymmetric compressive learning (ACL) scheme and formally established excess risk bounds for it. This was achieved by introducing a specific LPD property—telling us “how far” a distorted feature map is from an undistorted one—that combines with the classical LRIP of compressive learning to explain the ACL performances. Our second key contribution was to apply this result (
i.e.
, proving the LPD property) to the specific case of quantized (and modulo) sketch contributions. We then further validated those results with numerical simulations.

However, our contribution focused on deriving an excess risk bound without particular care for its tightness. In particular, using Lemma
3
to prove the LPD requires to have a sketch size
m
scaling with the complexity (Kolmogorov entropy) of a signal set
\Sigma
, which is not a required ingredient in previous (symmetric) CL guarantees
[
4
]
; this could be suboptimal if
\Sigma
is large (in the Kolmogorov entropy sense); ideally, our results should depend on the complexity of the model set
\mathcal{G}
rather than the signal space
\Sigma
.

Moreover, just as the existing CL guarantees, the excess risk bound is not readily exploitable in practice. For instance, the LRIP constant and the Kolmogorov entropy are hard to pin down accurately, and involve solving non-trivial trade-offs to be interpreted properly (
e.g.
, between the sketch size
m
, the probability of failures, the error contributions
\epsilon
). As a last caveat, let us recall that the current compressive learning algorithms used in practice are heuristics that do not have convergence guarantees. Future work is thus needed to bridge the gap between the theoretical guarantees and empirical performances of compressive learning—symmetric or not.
