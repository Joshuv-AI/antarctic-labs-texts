The Extremal GDoF Gain of Optimal versus Binary Power Control in K K User Interference Networks Is Θ ⁡ ( K ) \Theta(\sqrt{K})
Yao-Chia Chan, Pouya Pezeshkpour, Chunhua Geng, Syed A. Jafar

Abstract
Using ideas from Generalized Degrees of Freedom (GDoF) analyses and extremal network theory, this work studies the extremal gain of optimal power control over binary (on/off) power control, especially in large interference networks, in search of new theoretical insights. Whereas numerical studies have already established that in most practical settings binary power control is close to optimal, the extremal analysis shows not only that there exist settings where the gain from optimal power control can be quite significant, but also bounds the extremal values of such gains from a GDoF perspective. As its main contribution, this work explicitly characterizes the extremal GDoF gain of optimal over binary power control as Θ ⁡ ( K ) \Theta\left(\sqrt{K}\right) for all K K . In particular, the extremal gain is bounded between ⌊ K ⌋ \lfloor\sqrt{K}\rfloor and 2.5 ​ K 2.5\sqrt{K} for every K K . For K = 2 , 3 , 4 , 5 , 6 K=2,3,4,5,6 users, the precise extremal gain is found to be 1 , 3 / 2 , 2 , 9 / 4 1,3/2,2,9/4 and 41 / 16 41/16 , respectively. Networks shown to achieve the extremal gain may be interpreted as multi-tier heterogeneous networks. It is worthwhile to note that because of their focus on asymptotic analysis, the sharp characterizations of extremal gains are valuable primarily from a theoretical perspective, and not as contradictions to the conventional wisdom that binary power control is generally close to optimal in practical, non-asymptotic settings.



I
Introduction

The design of communication systems involves many optimization problems. The inherent combinatorial complexity of these optimizations often makes direct analysis infeasible, and motivates complementary approaches from theoretical and practical perspectives. On one hand, useful practical insights may be obtained from extensive numerical simulations. On the other hand, theoretical understanding may be advanced through extremal analyses, focused on identifying key cornerstones, e.g., asymptotic limits that are theoretically insightful. For instance, information theory often relies on extremal analysis (e.g., bounding the performance of the best code among all codes) under asymptotic (unbounded code length, unbounded complexity) relaxations to obtain theoretically insightful answers (channel capacity) that are complemented by numerical simulations to yield efficient communication designs.

As the focus shifts from channels to networks, the combinatorial challenge is further compounded. Additional tools have emerged to facilitate extremal/asymptotic theoretical analysis of wireless networks. Among these tools are the high SNR perspective taken by Generalized Degrees of Freedom (GDoF) studies
[
1
]
, and more recently the idea of extremal network theory introduced in
[
2
]
, i.e., the study of the extremal network settings that maximize the gain of one coding scheme over another. Extremal network theory and practical simulations complement each other — the latter focuses on what is likely to happen in settings of current practical interest, while the former tries to understand what is the best/worst scenario possible. Extremal network theory is particularly valuable for large networks that are beyond the reach of numerical simulations. For example,
[
2
]
studies the extremal GDoF gain from joint coding (transmitter cooperation) over separate coding in a
K

1.5

2-1/K

, respectively. Remarkably,
[

K
user interference channel, extremal GDoF analysis has been applied by Joudeh and Caire in
[
4
]
to downlink cellular networks, modeled as interfering broadcast channels, to characterize the gains of cooperation among base stations. Building on these advances, in this work we apply the GDoF and extremal network theory framework to an aspect that is essential to wireless network design – power control

Power control in conjunction with treating interference as noise (TIN) is one of the most widely prevalent ideas in wireless networks with multiple interfering links, because of its relative simplicity and robustness compared with other sophisticated schemes, such as rate-splitting

This is the challenge that motivates the extremal network theory perspective introduced in

Specifically we explore the extremal gain, defined by the supremum of the ratio of the sum GDoF corresponding to optimal and binary power control, across all possible network topologies. Our main result is that the extremal gain of optimal over binary power control is
\Theta(\sqrt{K})
. We also explicitly identify a class of network topologies that allow this extremal gain asymptotically at high SNR. Remarkably, these topologies may be interpreted as multi-tier heterogeneous networks. Thus, the extremal analysis adds surprising theoretical insights to the picture presented by numerical simulations — whereas the numerical studies shows that binary power control is close to optimal for most commonly studied network topologies, the theoretical analysis reveals exceptional network topologies where binary power control can be significantly suboptimal, and presents a tight (orderwise) bound on this suboptimality penalty. , respectively.

II-A

Optimal and Binary Power Control

The main goal of this work is to compare Optimal Power Control (OPC) against Binary Power Control (BPC). Let us define these two power control schemes under the GDoF framework.
With OPC, the power exponents

On the other hand, Binary Power Control (BPC) allows each

\displaystyle R_{\Sigma,o}(\boldsymbol{\alpha},P)

\displaystyle=\max_{\boldsymbol{r}\in\Omega^{K}}R_{\Sigma}(\boldsymbol{\alpha},P,\boldsymbol{r}),

\displaystyle R_{\Sigma,b}(\boldsymbol{\alpha},P)

\displaystyle=\max_{\boldsymbol{r}\in\Omega_{b}^{K}}R_{\Sigma}(\boldsymbol{\alpha},P,\boldsymbol{r}),

\displaystyle D_{\Sigma,o}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})

\displaystyle=\max_{\boldsymbol{r}\in\Omega^{K}}D_{\Sigma}(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{r}),

\displaystyle D_{\Sigma,b}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})

\displaystyle=\max_{\boldsymbol{r}\in\Omega_{b}^{K}}D_{\Sigma}(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{r}).

Key Lemmas

Define a subset of all possible topologies

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{K}^{+}

\displaystyle\triangleq\left\{\boldsymbol{\alpha}:~\left(\exists\boldsymbol{r}\in\Omega^{K}\text{ s.t. }D_{\Sigma,o}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})=\sum_{i=1}^{K}d_{i}(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{r})\right)\Rightarrow\left(\min_{i\in[K]}d_{i}(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{r})>0\right)\right\},

can be achieved only with GDoF tuples

or
(0.5,1)

On the other hand, the topology
\boldsymbol{\alpha}=[1,0.5;0.5,1]
does not lie in
\mathcal{A}_{2}^{+}

, we can add a
(K+1)^{th}
user who neither causes nor suffers interference from any of the original
K
users
(\alpha_{i,K+1}=\alpha_{K+1,i}=0,\forall i\in[K])
, and has a desired channel strength
\alpha_{K+1,K+1}=\epsilon>0
, to obtain a
K+1
user topology,
\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}_{K+1}^{+}
. Since this
(K+1)^{th}
user must contribute
\epsilon>0
to the sum-GDoF optimal solution with either OPC or BPC,
D_{\Sigma,o}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{\prime})=D_{\Sigma,o}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})+\epsilon
, and
D_{\Sigma,b}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{\prime})=D_{\Sigma,b}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})+\epsilon
. Since
\epsilon
can be chosen to be arbitrarily small, the RHS of (
17
) cannot be smaller than the LHS.
∎

The next lemma bounds the extremal GDoF gain from above by two upper bounds in two complementary topology subsets.

Lemma 2
.

For
K\geq 2
,

\displaystyle\mu_{K}\leq\max\left\{\mu_{K-1},\sup_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}\in\mathcal{A}_{K}^{+}}\frac{D_{\Sigma,o}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})}{D_{\Sigma,b}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})}\right\}.

(18)

Proof.

If
\mu_{K}=\sup_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}\in\mathcal{A}_{K}^{+}}\frac{D_{\Sigma,o}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})}{D_{\Sigma,b}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})}
then (
18
) holds. So let us assume the alternative, i.e.,

\mu_{K}=\sup_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}\in\mathcal{A}_{K}\setminus\mathcal{A}_{K}^{+}}\frac{D_{\Sigma,o}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})}{D_{\Sigma,b}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})}.

Consider any
K
user topology
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{K}\in\mathcal{A}_{K}\setminus\mathcal{A}_{K}^{+}
. By definition, the sum-GDoF value
D_{\Sigma,o}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{K})
must be achieved with
d_{i}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{K},\boldsymbol{r})=0
for some
i\in[K]
. Removing this user
i
, we obtain a
K-1
user topology
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{K-1}
. Since User
i
contributed nothing to the sum-GDoF under OPC, removing this user cannot hurt, i.e.,
D_{\Sigma,o}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{K-1})=D_{\Sigma,o}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{K})
. On the other hand, removing this user cannot help increase the sum-GDoF with BPC, because switching off this user was already an option in the original
K
user topology under BPC. Therefore,
D_{\Sigma,b}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{K-1})\leq D_{\Sigma,b}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{K})
, and we have,

\displaystyle\frac{D_{\Sigma,o}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{K})}{D_{\Sigma,b}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{K})}\leq\frac{D_{\Sigma,o}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{K-1})}{D_{\Sigma,b}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{K-1})}\leq\mu_{K-1}.

(19)

Since this holds for all topologies
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{K}\in\mathcal{A}_{K}\setminus\mathcal{A}_{K}^{+}
, we have the bound
\mu_{K}\leq\mu_{K-1}
, which concludes the proof.
∎

Lemma 3
.

For any topology
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
and any power allocation vector
\boldsymbol{r}=(r_{1},r_{2},\cdots,r_{K})\in\Omega^{K}
, with
r_{\max}\triangleq\max_{k\in[K]}r_{k}
,

\displaystyle d_{k}(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{r})

\displaystyle=d_{k}(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}),

\displaystyle\forall k\in[K],

(20)

where the new power allocation vector
\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}=(r^{\prime}_{1},r^{\prime}_{2},\cdots,r^{\prime}_{K})\in\Omega^{K}
and

\displaystyle r^{\prime}_{k}

\displaystyle=r_{k}-r_{\max},

\displaystyle\forall k\in[K].

(21)

Proof.

The transmit power levels (
r_{k}
,
k\in[K]
) of all users are elevated (e.g., as illustrated in Figure

-r_{\max}
), until at least one user hits its maximum transmit power level
(0)
. The elevated power allocation variables

are still valid
(\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}\in\Omega^{K})
because their values are not more than

) in its desired signal as well as all the interfering signals. The difference of power levels between desired signals and interference is therefore unaffected. As a result, the GDoF achieved by TIN are unchanged, i.e., (

) holds.

.
The following lemma serves as a key building block for bounding such a gain.

Lemma 4
.

Consider any network topology
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}=[\tilde{\alpha}_{ij}]_{i\in[K],j\in[K]}\in\mathcal{A}_{K}^{+}
, and any power allocation vector
\tilde{\boldsymbol{r}}=(\tilde{r}_{1},\tilde{r}_{2},\cdots,\tilde{r}_{K})\in\Omega^{K}

that achieves
D_{\Sigma,o}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\alpha}})
, i.e.,

\displaystyle D_{\Sigma,o}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\alpha}})

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{K}d_{k}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\alpha}},\tilde{\boldsymbol{r}}).

(22)

For any
\mathcal{S}=\{\beta_{1},\beta_{2},\cdots,\beta_{L}\}\subseteq[K]
satisfying
\tilde{r}_{\beta_{1}}\geq\tilde{r}_{\beta_{2}}\geq\cdots\geq\tilde{r}_{\beta_{L}}
, we have

\displaystyle D_{\Sigma,b}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\alpha}})

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{i=1}^{L-1}\left(d_{\beta_{i}}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\alpha}},\tilde{\boldsymbol{r}})+\tilde{r}_{\beta_{L}}-\tilde{r}_{\beta_{i}}\right)+\left(d_{\beta_{L}}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\alpha}},\tilde{\boldsymbol{r}})+\tilde{r}_{\beta_{L-1}}-\tilde{r}_{\beta_{L}}\right),

\displaystyle\text{if }L>1,

(23)

\displaystyle D_{\Sigma,b}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\alpha}})

\displaystyle\geq d_{\beta_{1}}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\alpha}},\tilde{\boldsymbol{r}})-\tilde{r}_{\beta_{1}},

\displaystyle\text{if }L=1.

(24)

Remark 1
.

For brevity, we will refer to the bounds (
23
), (
24
) as
B_{[\beta_{1},\beta_{2},\cdots,\beta_{L}]}
.

Proof.

First we note that, since
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\in\mathcal{A}_{K}^{+}
, by (
6
)

\displaystyle{d}_{i}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\alpha}},\tilde{\boldsymbol{r}})=\tilde{\alpha}_{ii}+\tilde{r}_{i}-\max_{k\in[K],k\neq i}(\tilde{\alpha}_{ik}+\tilde{r}_{k})^{+}>0

(25)

for all
i\in[K]
.
For any
\mathcal{S}\subseteq[K]
, define
\boldsymbol{r}(\mathcal{S})=(r_{1}(\mathcal{S}),r_{2}(\mathcal{S}),\cdots,r_{K}(\mathcal{S}))\in\Omega^{K}_{b}
as the power allocation that sets
r_{i}(\mathcal{S})=0
if
i\in\mathcal{S}
and
r_{i}(\mathcal{S})=-\infty
if
i\notin\mathcal{S}
. In other words, the power allocation
\boldsymbol{r}(\mathcal{S})
amounts to switching on all transmitters in
\mathcal{S}
at full power, and switching off all transmitters not in
\mathcal{S}
.
We note that

\displaystyle D_{\Sigma,b}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\alpha}})=\max_{\mathcal{S}\subseteq[K]}D_{\Sigma}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\alpha}},\boldsymbol{r}(\mathcal{S}))\geq D_{\Sigma}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\alpha}},\boldsymbol{r}(\mathcal{S}))

(26)

for all
\mathcal{S}\subseteq[K].

If
L=1
, i.e.,
\mathcal{S}=\{\beta_{1}\}\subseteq[K]
, then the bound (
24
) is obtained from (
26
) as follows:

\displaystyle D_{\Sigma,b}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\alpha}})

\displaystyle\geq D_{\Sigma}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\alpha}},\boldsymbol{r}(\mathcal{S}))=\tilde{\alpha}_{\beta_{1}\beta_{1}}

(27)

\displaystyle\geq\left(\tilde{\alpha}_{\beta_{1}\beta_{1}}+\tilde{r}_{\beta_{1}}-\max_{k\in[K],k\neq\beta_{1}}(\tilde{\alpha}_{\beta_{1}k}+\tilde{r}_{k})^{+}\right)-\tilde{r}_{\beta_{1}}

(28)

\displaystyle=d_{\beta_{1}}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\alpha}},\tilde{\boldsymbol{r}})-\tilde{r}_{\beta_{1}}.

(29)

Next we consider
L>1
, and prove the bound (
23
). We will create a new topology
\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{\prime}
based on the given topology
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}
, such that the sum GDoF under OPC remains the same, i.e.,
D_{\Sigma,o}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\alpha}})=D_{\Sigma,o}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{\prime})
, while the sum GDoF under BPC cannot be better but may be possibly reduced, i.e.,
D_{\Sigma,b}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\alpha}})\geq D_{\Sigma,b}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{\prime})
. The main idea is that given a power allocation
\tilde{\boldsymbol{r}}
, since the GDoF achieved by each user are limited only by the strongest interference signal seen by its receiver, if the cross-channel strengths of the remaining interferers to that receiver are increased so that every interfering signal is as strong as the original strongest interference signal, this does not hurt the GDoF achieved under OPC, but it can potentially reduce the GDoF achieved with BPC.

For any given subset of users
\mathcal{S}=\{\beta_{1},\beta_{2},\cdots,\beta_{L}\}
and the given topology
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}
, we define the new topology
\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{\prime}=[\alpha_{ij}^{\prime}]_{i\in[K],j\in[K]}
, such that for all
i,j\in[K]
,

\displaystyle\alpha_{ij}^{\prime}\triangleq\begin{cases}\tilde{\alpha}_{ij},&\text{if }(i=j)\lor(i\notin\mathcal{S})\lor(j\notin\mathcal{S}),\\
\max_{k\in\mathcal{S},k\neq i}(\tilde{\alpha}_{ik}+\tilde{r}_{k})^{+}-\tilde{r}_{j},&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

(30)

Note that
\alpha_{ij}^{\prime}\geq\tilde{\alpha}_{ij}
, since for
i,j\in\mathcal{S}
and
i\neq j
, we have
\alpha_{ij}^{\prime}\geq(\tilde{\alpha}_{ij}+\tilde{r}_{j})^{+}-\tilde{r}_{j}\geq\tilde{\alpha}_{ij}+\tilde{r}_{j}-\tilde{r}_{j}=\tilde{\alpha}_{ij}
. Also note that relative to
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}
the new topology
\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{\prime}
differs in only those cross-links that both emerge and terminate at users in
\mathcal{S}
. For such cross links, their strengths are lifted up to the extent that, with the power allocations
\tilde{\boldsymbol{r}}
, their interference levels at each receiver reach the maximal interference level at that receiver due to all undesired transmitters in
\mathcal{S}
in the original topology
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}
.
Figure
29
illustrates an example of how the new topology
\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{\prime}
is created from
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}
, where we assume
\tilde{r}_{1}\geq\tilde{r}_{2}\geq\cdots\geq\tilde{r}_{K}
, choose
\mathcal{S}=[K]
and focus on Receiver 1. We firstly identify the maximal interference level in the original topology in Figure
29
(a) as
\max_{k\in\mathcal{S},k\neq 1}(\tilde{\alpha}_{1k}+\tilde{r}_{k})^{+}=\tilde{\alpha}_{12}+\tilde{r}_{2}
. Then in the new topology, we define
\alpha_{11}^{\prime}=\tilde{\alpha}_{11}
, and
\alpha_{1j}^{\prime}=\tilde{\alpha}_{12}+\tilde{r}_{2}-\tilde{r}_{j}
for
j=2,3,\cdots,K
. In the new topology
\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{\prime}
with the power allocations
\tilde{\boldsymbol{r}}
, the interference caused at Receiver
1
by all undesired transmitters is thus at the same level, as shown in Figure
29
(b).
In the new topology
\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{\prime}
, the sum GDoF
D_{\Sigma,o}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{\prime})\geq D_{\Sigma,o}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\alpha}})
because the GDoF achieved by the original power allocation
\tilde{\boldsymbol{r}}
remain unchanged in the new topology. On the other hand, because increasing the cross-channel strengths cannot help a TIN scheme under OPC, this implies that
D_{\Sigma,o}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{\prime})=D_{\Sigma,o}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\alpha}})
. For the same reason, i.e., because increasing cross-channel strengths cannot help a TIN scheme under BPC, we have
D_{\Sigma,b}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\alpha}})\geq D_{\Sigma,b}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{\prime})
.

Next, in the new topology
\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{\prime}
let us apply the binary power allocation vector
\boldsymbol{r}(\mathcal{S})
, i.e., switch on the transmitters in
\mathcal{S}
at full power and keep the remaining transmitters switched off. Obviously,
D_{\Sigma,b}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{\prime})\geq D_{\Sigma}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{r}(\mathcal{S}))
.

Next let us show that for
i\in\mathcal{S}
, we can further bound
d_{i}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{r}(\mathcal{S}))
from below as

\displaystyle d_{i}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{r}(\mathcal{S}))

\displaystyle\geq{d}_{i}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\alpha}},\tilde{\boldsymbol{r}})-\tilde{r}_{i}+\min_{j\in\mathcal{S},j\neq i}\tilde{r}_{j}.

(31)

This is because, by the definition of
d_{i}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{r}(\mathcal{S}))
, we have for
i\in\mathcal{S}
,

\displaystyle d_{i}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{r}(\mathcal{S}))

\displaystyle=\left(\tilde{\alpha}_{ii}-\max_{j\in\mathcal{S},j\neq i}\alpha_{ij}^{\prime}\right)^{+}

(32)

\displaystyle\geq\tilde{\alpha}_{ii}-\max_{j\in\mathcal{S},j\neq i}\alpha_{ij}^{\prime}

(33)

\displaystyle=\tilde{\alpha}_{ii}-\max_{j\in\mathcal{S},j\neq i}\left\{\max_{k\in\mathcal{S},k\neq i}(\tilde{\alpha}_{ik}+\tilde{r}_{k})^{+}-\tilde{r}_{j}\right\}

(34)

\displaystyle=\tilde{\alpha}_{ii}-\max_{k\in\mathcal{S},k\neq i}(\tilde{\alpha}_{ik}+\tilde{r}_{k})^{+}+\min_{j\in\mathcal{S},j\neq i}\tilde{r}_{j}

(35)

\displaystyle=\left((\tilde{\alpha}_{ii}+\tilde{r}_{i})-\max_{k\in\mathcal{S},k\neq i}(\tilde{\alpha}_{ik}+\tilde{r}_{k})^{+}\right)-\tilde{r}_{i}+\min_{j\in\mathcal{S},j\neq i}\tilde{r}_{j}

(36)

\displaystyle\geq\left((\tilde{\alpha}_{ii}+\tilde{r}_{i})-\max_{k\in[K],k\neq i}(\tilde{\alpha}_{ik}+\tilde{r}_{k})^{+}\right)-\tilde{r}_{i}+\min_{j\in\mathcal{S},j\neq i}\tilde{r}_{j}

(37)

\displaystyle={d}_{i}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\alpha}},\tilde{\boldsymbol{r}})-\tilde{r}_{i}+\min_{j\in\mathcal{S},j\neq i}\tilde{r}_{j}.

(38)

Equality (
34
) follows from the definition of
\alpha_{ij}^{\prime}
in (
30
). In (
34
) we note that the inner maximum is a constant with respect to the outer one; thus we have (
35
).
Step (
37
) holds because we expand the scope of values over which the first maximum is taken, from
\mathcal{S}
to
[K]
.
Finally, (
38
) follows due to (
25
).

Summing up (
31
) for all
i\in\mathcal{S}
and applying the assumption
\tilde{r}_{\beta_{1}}\geq\tilde{r}_{\beta_{2}}\geq\cdots\geq\tilde{r}_{\beta_{L}}
, we obtain

\displaystyle D_{\Sigma}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{r}(\mathcal{S}))\geq\sum_{i=1}^{L-1}\left(\tilde{d}_{\beta_{i}}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\alpha}},\tilde{\boldsymbol{r}})-\tilde{r}_{\beta_{i}}+\tilde{r}_{\beta_{L}}\right)+\left(\tilde{d}_{\beta_{L}}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\alpha}},\tilde{\boldsymbol{r}})-\tilde{r}_{\beta_{L}}+\tilde{r}_{\beta_{L-1}}\right).

(39)

By putting (
39
) together with
D_{\Sigma,b}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\alpha}})\geq D_{\Sigma,b}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{\prime})\geq D_{\Sigma}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{r}(\mathcal{S}))
, we arrive at the bound in (
23
).
∎

The next lemma is essential to this work as it bounds the sum-GDoF gains as a function of
K
.

Lemma 5
.

Let
m\in\mathbb{N}
. When
K\geq m^{2}
, there exists a topology
\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{*}\in\mathcal{A}_{K}
such that

\displaystyle\frac{D_{\Sigma,o}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{*})}{D_{\Sigma,b}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{*})}

\displaystyle\geq m.

(40)

On the other hand, when
K=m^{2}
, we have

\displaystyle\sup_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}\in\mathcal{A}_{K}^{+}}\frac{D_{\Sigma,o}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})}{D_{\Sigma,b}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{3}{2}m-\frac{1}{2}.

(41)

Proof.

First we show the lower bound (
40
) with a topology
\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{*}\in\mathcal{A}_{m^{2}}
depicted in Figure
27
.
We divide the
m^{2}
users into
m
groups, each of which consists of
m
users and is denoted by
\mathcal{G}_{i}
,
i\in\{1,2,...,m\}
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{i}=\left\{(i-1)m+1,(i-1)m+2,...,im\right\}.

(42)

In each group
\mathcal{G}_{i}
, all direct links have strength equal to
i
.
For cross links, each transmitter in the group
\mathcal{G}_{i}
connects to each receiver in
\mathcal{G}_{j}
with strength
i-1
, where
j\leq i
and
i,j\in\{1,2,...,m\}
.
All the other cross links are disconnected, i.e., transmitters in the
i^{th}
group do not interfere with receivers in groups
i+1,i+2,\cdots,m
.
As an example, when
m=2
, we get the
4
-user interference channel in Figure
26
(b), where
\mathcal{G}_{1}=\{1,2\}
and
\mathcal{G}_{2}=\{3,4\}
.

For the topology
\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{*}
, each user achieves one GDoF when the power allocation vector
\boldsymbol{r}^{*}=\left(r_{1}^{*},r_{2}^{*},\cdots,r_{K}^{*}\right)
is set as

\displaystyle r_{k}^{*}

\displaystyle=1-j,

\displaystyle\forall k\in\mathcal{G}_{j},j\in[m].

(43)

Thus the sum GDoF
D_{\Sigma}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{*},\boldsymbol{r}^{*})=m^{2}
, implying that
D_{\Sigma,o}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{*})\geq m^{2}
.
Now consider binary power control, where we either activate a user by switching his transmitter on at full power, or deactivate the user by switching his transmitter off.
In the topology
\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{*}
, activating any one of the transmitters in group
\mathcal{G}_{i}
at full power overwhelms all the receivers in
\mathcal{G}_{j}
, for all
j<i
; i.e., all users in
\bigcup_{j\in[K],j<i}\mathcal{G}_{j}
get zero GDoF whether or not they are activated. This means that only the users in the group with the largest index will survive. So it suffices to consider one single group at a time and decide how many users in that group may be activated to maximize the sum GDoF. Activating one user in group
i
yields sum-GDoF value of
i
, and activating
k
users,
1<k\leq m
, in group
i
yields a sum-GDoF value of
k
. The optimal value is therefore
D_{\Sigma,b}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{*})=m
, which is achieved by activating all
m
users in one single group only, or by activating any one user in group
\mathcal{G}_{m}
.
Therefore, we establish the lower bound
\frac{D_{\Sigma,o}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{*})}{D_{\Sigma,b}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{*})}\geq m
for
K=m^{2}
.
For
K\geq m^{2}
, the same lower bound still holds, because we can achieve the same bound with a topology
\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{*}\in\mathcal{A}_{K}
, where we copy the one depicted in Figure
27
to the first
m^{2}
users, and remove all links associated with the rest
K-m^{2}
users. Here we conclude the proof of (
40
).

Next, we show the upper bound (
41
) for any
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\in\mathcal{A}_{K}^{+}
when
K=m^{2}
. Let
\tilde{\boldsymbol{r}}
be a power allocation vector associated with
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}
as in Lemma
4
.
A key non-trivial step of the proof is the idea of partitioning the sum-GDoF in a particular way and applying bounds obtained from Lemma
4
to each partition. In the following we first present the bounds, and then the explicit sum-GDoF partition.
Without loss of generality, let us assume that

\displaystyle\tilde{r}_{1}\geq\tilde{r}_{2}\geq\cdots\geq\tilde{r}_{K},

(44)

which carries no loss of generality because it only amounts to a labeling of users
in this order.

First consider the bounds
B_{[i,i+1,...i+m-1]}
. With the assumption (
44
),

\displaystyle B_{[i,i+1,...i+m-1]}\implies D_{\Sigma,b}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\alpha}})\geq\sum_{j=i}^{i+m-1}d_{j}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\alpha}},\tilde{\boldsymbol{r}})-\sum_{j=i}^{i+m-3}\tilde{r}_{j}+(m-2)\tilde{r}_{i+m-1},

(45)

where
i\in[m^{2}-m+1]
. Adding the bounds
\sum_{i=1}^{m^{2}-m+1}B_{[i,i+1,...i+m-1]}
we obtain

\displaystyle(m^{2}-m+1)D_{\Sigma,b}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\alpha}})

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{j=1}^{m-1}jd_{j}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\alpha}},\tilde{\boldsymbol{r}})-\sum_{j=1}^{m-2}j\tilde{r}_{j}-(m-2)\tilde{r}_{m-1}+\sum_{j=m}^{m^{2}-m+1}md_{j}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\alpha}},\tilde{\boldsymbol{r}})

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{j=1}^{m-1}(m-j)d_{m^{2}-m+1+j}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\alpha}},\tilde{\boldsymbol{r}})+\sum_{j=1}^{m-2}j\tilde{r}_{m^{2}-m+1+j}+(m-2)\tilde{r}_{m^{2}}.

(46)

Then we consider the bounds
B_{[1,2,...,i]}
, which are obtained from Lemma
4
and the assumption (
44
):

\displaystyle B_{[1]}

\displaystyle\implies D_{\Sigma,b}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\alpha}})\geq d_{1}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\alpha}},\tilde{\boldsymbol{r}})-\tilde{r}_{1},

(47)

\displaystyle B_{[1,2,...,i]}

\displaystyle\implies D_{\Sigma,b}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\alpha}})\geq\sum_{j=1}^{i}d_{j}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\alpha}},\tilde{\boldsymbol{r}})-\sum_{j=1}^{i-2}\tilde{r}_{j}+(i-2)\tilde{r}_{i},

(48)

where
i=2,3,\cdots,m-1
. Adding
B_{[1]}+\sum_{i=2}^{m-1}B_{[1,2,...,i]}
, we obtain,

\displaystyle(m-1)D_{\Sigma,b}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\alpha}})

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{j=1}^{m-1}(m-j)d_{j}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\alpha}},\tilde{\boldsymbol{r}})-\tilde{r}_{1}-\sum_{j=1}^{m-3}(m-2-j)\tilde{r}_{j}+\sum_{j=3}^{m-1}(j-2)\tilde{r}_{j}

(49)

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{j=1}^{m-1}(m-j)d_{j}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\alpha}},\tilde{\boldsymbol{r}})+\sum_{j=3}^{m-1}(j-2)\tilde{r}_{j},

(50)

where (
50
) holds because
\tilde{r}_{j}\leq 0
for
j\in[m^{2}]
.

Next we consider the bounds
B_{[i+m^{2}-m+1]}

VII
Conclusion

Using ideas from GDoF analyses and extremal network theory, we studied the extremal gain of optimal power control over binary power control especially in large interference networks, in search of theoretical counterpoints to well established insights from numerical studies. Whereas numerical studies have established that in most practical settings binary power control is close to optimal
[
54
,
55
,
56
,
57
,
58
]
, our extremal analysis shows not only that there exist settings where the gain from optimal power control can be quite significant, but also bounds the extremal values of such gains from a GDoF perspective. As our main contribution, we explicitly characterize the extremal GDoF gain of optimal over binary power control as
\Theta\left(\sqrt{K}\right)
for all
K
. For
K=2,3,4,5,6
users, the precise extremal gain is found to be
1,3/2,2,9/4
and
41/16
, respectively. Networks shown to achieve the extremal gain may be interpreted as multi-tier heterogeneous networks.

It is worthwhile to note that the findings of this work do not contradict conventional wisdom that binary power control is generally close to optimum. Indeed numerical experiments suggest that such extremal gains are unlikely to be encountered in practice. Even in the heterogeneous multi-tier topologies that emerge as extremal networks, numerical experiments suggest that the extremal gains are manifested only at very high SNRs. Figure
30
highlights this sobering insight with a simple numerical simulation for the network topology depicted in Figure
27
with
K=9
users. This topology, labeled
\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{9}
, achieves sum-GDoF gain
\sqrt{K}=3
according to the lower bound (
40
) in Lemma
5
. In Figure
30
we illustrate the sum rate achieved by two power control policies. The black dashed curve, labeled
R_{\Sigma,b}
, is the sum rate achieved by binary power control; i.e.
R_{\Sigma,b}=R_{\Sigma,b}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{9},P)
. On the other hand, the red solid curve, labeled with
R_{\Sigma}\eqref{eq:KKpwr}
, is the sum rate achieved by the power allocation vector specified in (
43
). The power allocation (
43
) is motivated by the high SNR setting and indeed it reaches
D_{\Sigma,o}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{9})
. Such a power allocation does not necessarily achieve
R_{\Sigma,o}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{9},P)
for finite
P
. This is reflected by the observation that
R_{\Sigma}
is less than
R_{\Sigma,b}
at very low SNRs (
P\leq 5
dB). Nevertheless, here we still use
R_{\Sigma}\eqref{eq:KKpwr}
as an approximation of
R_{\Sigma,o}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{9},P)
because they are close at high SNRs. The performance gap of the two sum rates is quantified by the sum-rate gain
R_{\Sigma}\eqref{eq:KKpwr}/R_{\Sigma,b}
(blue dot-dashed curve). The sum-rate gain grows as
P
increases, and eventually approaches
3

P
goes to infinity, which is guaranteed by (
40
). However, the sum-rate gain is relatively modest at moderate SNRs, e.g., it is below 2 when
P\leq 20
dB. Thus, while the extremal gain is valuable as a sharp theoretical limit, and the
\Theta(\sqrt{K})
scaling is remarkable, it offers only a complementary perspective from asymptotic analysis, and not a contradiction to the conventional wisdom that binary power control is generally close to optimal in practical settings.
