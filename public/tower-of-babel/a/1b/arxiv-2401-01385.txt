General Berndt-Type Integrals and Series Associated with Jacobi Elliptic Functions
Ce Xu and Jianqiang Zhao[1mm] a. School of Mathematics and Statistics, Anhui Normal University,Wuhu 241002, P.R. Chinab. Department of Mathematics, The Bishop’s School, La Jolla, CA 92037, USA[5mm] Dedicated to Professor Bruce C. Berndt on the occasion of his 85th birthday

Abstract




Abstract.
In this paper, we prove two structural theorems on the general Berndt-type integrals with the denominator having arbitrary positive degrees by contour integrations involving hyperbolic and trigonometric functions, and hyperbolic sums associated with Jacobi elliptic functions. We first establish explicit relations between these integrals and four classes of hyperbolic sums. Then, using our previous results on hyperbolic series and applying the matrix method from linear algebra, we compute explicitly several general hyperbolic sums and their higher derivatives. These enable us to express two families of general Berndt-type integrals as polynomials in
\Gamma^{4}(1/4)
and
\pi^{-1}
with rational coefficients, where
\Gamma
is the Euler gamma function. At the end of the paper, we provide some conjectures of general Berndt-type integrals.

Keywords
: Berndt-type integral,
q
-series, hyperbolic and trigonometric functions, contour integration, Jacobi elliptic functions, Fourier series expansions.

05A30, 32A27, 42A16, 33E05, 11B68.

Introduction

For nonnegative integer
a

Berndt-type integrals of order
b

\displaystyle\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\displaystyle\frac{x^{a}dx}{(\cos x\pm\cosh x)^{b}},

(1.1)

where
a\geq 0
and
b\geq 1
if the denominator has “
+

a\geq 2b
otherwise.

The study of Berndt-type integrals has a long history, first as a problem submitted by Ramanujan
[
15
, pp. 325-326]

over a century ago to the
Indian Journal of Pure and Applied Mathematics
:

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\displaystyle\frac{\sin(nx)}{x(\cos x+\cosh x)}dx=\displaystyle\frac{\pi}{4}\quad\text{(for any odd integer $n$)}.

Wilkinson
[
22
]
provided a proof four years later.
At the International Conference on Orthogonal Polynomials and
q
-Series, which was held in Orlando in May 2015 in celebration of the 70th birthday of Professor Mourad Ismail, Dennis Stanton gave a plenary talk titled “A small slice of Mourad’s work”. One of the topics in that talk was about “the mystery integral of Mourad Ismail”
[
13
]
:

\displaystyle\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\displaystyle\frac{dx}{\cos(K\sqrt{x})+\cosh(K^{\prime}\sqrt{x})}=2,

(1.2)

where
K\equiv K(x)
denotes the complete elliptic integral of the first kind, see (
2.3
). This curious integral (
1.2
) first appeared in
[
20
]
by Ismail and Valent. Berndt
[
7
]
provided a direct evaluation of (
1.2
) and other similar integrals of this type by using residue computations, the Fourier series expansions, and the Maclaurin series expansions of Jacobi elliptic functions. In particular, he proved that some Berndt-type integrals of order one can be evaluated by special values of the Gamma function. For example,

\displaystyle\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\displaystyle\frac{x^{3}}{\cos x-\cosh x}=

\displaystyle\,-\displaystyle\frac{\Gamma^{8}(1/4)}{256\pi^{2}},\quad

\displaystyle\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\displaystyle\frac{x^{7}}{\cos x-\cosh x}=

\displaystyle\,\displaystyle\frac{9\Gamma^{16}(1/4)}{2^{13}\pi^{4}},

\displaystyle\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\displaystyle\frac{x^{5}}{\cos x+\cosh x}=

\displaystyle\,\displaystyle\frac{3\pi^{3}\Gamma^{6}(1/4)}{256\Gamma^{6}(3/4)},\quad

\displaystyle\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\displaystyle\frac{x^{9}}{\cos x+\cosh x}=

\displaystyle\,\displaystyle\frac{3^{3}\cdot 7\pi^{5}\Gamma^{10}(1/4)}{2^{12}\Gamma^{10}(3/4)}.

It turns out that Berndt’s results and methods can be generalized and organized further. In our previous paper
[
26
]
, by extending an argument as used in the proof of the main theorem of
[
7
]
, we proved that the following explicit evaluations of two Berndt-type integrals of order two (cf.
[

\displaystyle\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\displaystyle\frac{x^{4p+1}}{(\cos x-\cosh x)^{2}}dx\in\mathbb{Q}\displaystyle\frac{\Gamma^{8p}(1/4)}{\pi^{2p}}+\mathbb{Q}\displaystyle\frac{\Gamma^{8p+8}(1/4)}{\pi^{2p+4}}\quad(p\in\mathbb{N}),

\displaystyle\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\displaystyle\frac{x^{4p+1}}{(\cos x+\cosh x)^{2}}dx\in\mathbb{Q}\displaystyle\frac{\Gamma^{8p}(1/4)}{\pi^{2p}}+\mathbb{Q}\displaystyle\frac{\Gamma^{8p+8}(1/4)}{\pi^{2p+4}}\quad(p\in\mathbb{N}).

Similarly, we also proved the following explicit evaluations of two Berndt-type integrals of order one (
\mathbb{N}_{0}:=\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\displaystyle\frac{x^{4p-1}}{\cos x-\cosh x}dx\in\mathbb{Q}\displaystyle\frac{\Gamma^{8p}(1/4)}{\pi^{2p}}\quad(p\in\mathbb{N}),

\displaystyle\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\displaystyle\frac{x^{4p+1}}{\cos x+\cosh x}dx\in\mathbb{Q}\displaystyle\frac{\Gamma^{8p+4}(1/4)}{\pi^{2p+1}}\quad(p\in\mathbb{N}_{0}).

Further, we proved the following explicit evaluations of Berndt-type integrals of order three (see

Notation and Some Preliminary Results

The Gaussian or ordinary hypergeometric function
{{}_{2}}F_{1}(a,b;c;x)
is defined for
|x|<1
by the power series

{{}_{2}}F_{1}(a,b;c;x)=\displaystyle\sum\limits_{n=0}^{\infty}\displaystyle\frac{(a)_{n}(b)_{n}}{(c)_{n}}\displaystyle\frac{x^{n}}{n!}\quad(a,b,c\in\mathbb{C}).

Here
(a)_{n}
denotes the
ascending Pochhammer symbol
defined by

(a)_{n}:=\displaystyle\frac{\Gamma(a+n)}{\Gamma(a)}=a(a+1)\cdots(a+n-1)\quad{\rm and}\quad(a)_{0}:=1,

where
a\in\mathbb{C}
is any complex number and
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
is a nonnegative integer. Here
\Gamma(s)
denotes
the Gamma function. When
\Re(s)>0

\Gamma(s):=\displaystyle\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}{{e^{-t}}{t^{s-1}}dt}.

The complete elliptic integral of the first kind is defined by (Whittaker and Watson
[
21
]
)

\displaystyle K:=K(x):=K(k^{2}):=\displaystyle\int\limits_{0}^{\pi/2}\displaystyle\frac{d\varphi}{\sqrt{1-k^{2}\sin^{2}\varphi}}=\displaystyle\frac{\pi}{2}{{}_{2}}F_{1}\left(\displaystyle\frac{1}{2},\displaystyle\frac{1}{2};1;k^{2}\right).

(2.3)

Here
x=k^{2}
and
k\ (0<k<1)
is the modulus of
K
. The complementary modulus
k^{\prime}
is defined by
k^{\prime}=\sqrt{1-k^{2}}
. Furthermore,

K^{\prime}:=K(k^{\prime 2})=\displaystyle\int\limits_{0}^{\pi/2}\displaystyle\frac{d\varphi}{\sqrt{1-k^{\prime 2}\sin^{2}\varphi}}=\displaystyle\frac{\pi}{2}{{}_{2}}F_{1}\left(\displaystyle\frac{1}{2},\displaystyle\frac{1}{2};1;1-k^{2}\right).

Similarly, the complete elliptic integral of the second kind is denoted by (Whittaker and Watson
[
21
]
)

E:=E(x):=E(k^{2}):=\displaystyle\int\limits_{0}^{\pi/2}\sqrt{1-k^{2}\sin^{2}\varphi}d\varphi=\displaystyle\frac{\pi}{2}{{}_{2}}F_{1}\left(-\displaystyle\frac{1}{2},\displaystyle\frac{1}{2};1;k^{2}\right).

In order to better state our main results, we shall henceforth adopt the notations of Ramanujan (see Berndt’s book
[
6
]
). Let

\displaystyle x:=k^{2},\quad y:=y(x):=\pi\displaystyle\frac{K^{\prime}}{K},\quad q:=q(x):=e^{-y},

\displaystyle z:=z(x):=\displaystyle\frac{2}{\pi}K,\quad z^{\prime}:=\displaystyle\frac{dz}{dx},\quad z":=\displaystyle\frac{d^{2}z}{dx^{2}},\quad z^{(n)}:=\displaystyle\frac{d^{n}z}{dx^{n}}.

(2.4)

Using the identity
(a)_{n+1}=a(a+1)_{n}
, it is easily shown that

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dx}{{}_{2}}F_{1}(a,b;c;x)=\displaystyle\frac{ab}{c}{{}_{2}}F_{1}(a+1,b+1;c+1;x)

and more generally,

\displaystyle\frac{d^{n}}{dx^{n}}{{}_{2}}F_{1}(a,b;c;x)=\displaystyle\frac{(a)_{n}(b)_{n}}{(c)_{n}}{{}_{2}}F_{1}(a+n,b+n;c+n;x)\quad(n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}).

Then,

\displaystyle\frac{d^{n}z}{dx^{n}}=\displaystyle\frac{\left(1/2\right)^{2}_{n}}{n!}{{}_{2}}F_{1}\left(\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}+n,\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}+n;1+n;x\right).

Applying the identity (see Andrews-Askey-Roy’s book
[
1
, Chapter 3, Section 3.5, Theorem 3.5.4(i)]
)

{{}_{2}}F_{1}\left(a,b;\displaystyle\frac{a+b+1}{2};\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\right)=\displaystyle\frac{\Gamma\left(\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\right)\Gamma\left(\displaystyle\frac{a+b+1}{2}\right)}{\Gamma\left(\displaystyle\frac{a+1}{2}\right)\Gamma\left(\displaystyle\frac{b+1}{2}\right)},

letting
a=b=1/2+n
and noting the fact that
\Gamma(1/2)=\sqrt{\pi}
, we can find by an elementary calculation that

\displaystyle\left.\displaystyle\frac{d^{n}z}{dx^{n}}\right|_{x=1/2}=\displaystyle\frac{(1/2)^{2}_{n}\sqrt{\pi}}{\Gamma^{2}\left(\displaystyle\frac{n}{2}+\displaystyle\frac{3}{4}\right)}.

(2.5)

Clearly, for any
m\in\mathbb{N}
, we have

\displaystyle\left.\displaystyle\frac{d^{2m}z}{dx^{2m}}\right|_{x=1/2}\in\mathbb{Q}\displaystyle\frac{\Gamma^{2}(1/4)}{\pi^{3/2}}\quad\text{and}\quad\left.\displaystyle\frac{d^{2m-1}z}{dx^{2m-1}}\right|_{x=1/2}\in\mathbb{Q}\displaystyle\frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{\Gamma^{2}(1/4)}.

In particular, taking

in (
2
) and applying (

), we obtain that

\displaystyle y\Big(\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\Big)=\pi,\quad z\Big(\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\Big)=\displaystyle\frac{\Gamma^{2}(1/4)}{2\pi^{3/2}},\quad z^{\prime}\Big(\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\Big)=\displaystyle\frac{4\sqrt{\pi}}{\Gamma^{2}(1/4)},

\displaystyle z^{\prime\prime}\Big(\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\Big)=\displaystyle\frac{\Gamma^{2}(1/4)}{2\pi^{3/2}},\quad z^{(3)}\Big(\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\Big)=\displaystyle\frac{36\sqrt{\pi}}{\Gamma^{2}(1/4)},

Lemma 4.3

Lemma 5.1

Let

\displaystyle\,\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}&\,z^{n+k}\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i_{0},\dots,i_{k}\geq 0\\

\displaystyle\,\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}&\,z^{n+k}\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i_{0},\dots,i_{k}\geq 0\\

\end{array}\right.
