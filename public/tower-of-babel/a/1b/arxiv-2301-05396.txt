[2]Automorphisms of the canonical double cover of a toroidal grid
Dave Witte Morris

Abstract
. The Cartesian product of two cycles ( C n [2] □ C m C_{n}\mathbin{\raise 0.7pt\hbox{\smaller[2]$\square$}}C_{m} ) has a natural embedding on the torus, such that each face of the embedding is a 4 4 -cycle. The toroidal grid Qd ⁡ ( m , n , r ) \Qd(m,n,r) is a generalization of this in which there is a shift by r r when traversing the meridian of length m m . In 2008, Steve Wilson found two interesting infinite families of (nonbipartite) toroidal grids that are unstable. (By definition, this means that the canonical bipartite double cover of the grid has more than twice as many automorphisms as the grid has.) It is easy to see that bipartite grids are also unstable, because the canonical double cover is disconnected. Furthermore, there are degenerate cases in which there exist two different vertices that have the same neighbours. This paper proves Wilson’s conjecture that Qd ⁡ ( m , n , r ) \Qd(m,n,r) is stable for all other values of the parameters. In addition, we prove an analogous conjecture of Wilson for the triangular grids Tr ⁡ ( m , n , r ) \Tr(m,n,r) that are obtained by adding a diagonal to each face of Qd ⁡ ( m , n , r ) \Qd(m,n,r) (with all of the added diagonals parallel to each other).

1.1 Definition

The

(A.2)

(A.3)

(A.4)

1.3 Remark

\Qd(m,n,r)

(In the special case where

There are some trivial reasons for a graph to be unstable
[

, p. 360]

(1)

Every disconnected graph is unstable.

(2)

Every bipartite graph with a nontrivial automorphism is unstable.

(3)

If two different vertices of a graph have the same neighbours, then the graph is unstable. (These are called “twin vertices”
[

]

This motivates the following definition:

An unstable graph is

2A.
Abelian Cayley graphs

2.1 Definition

(cf.
[
4
, p. 34]
)
.

Let
S
be a subset of an additive abelian group
G
, such that
S=-S
and
0\notin S
. The corresponding
abelian Cayley graph

\Cay(G;S)
is the graph whose vertices are the elements of
G
, and with an edge joining the vertices
g
and
h
if and only if
g=h+s
for some
s\in S
.

2.2 Remark
.

The adjective “abelian” in “abelian Cayley graph” is to emphasize the assumption that
G
is abelian, so we will sometimes omit it when it is not relevant.
(The usual definition of
\Cay(G;S)
does not require
G
to be abelian, but we have no need for the nonabelian case in this paper.)

The following simple (and well known) observation notes that the toroidal grids
\Qd(m,n,r)
and
\Tr(m,n,r)
are isomorphic to abelian Cayley graphs. The minus sign in
\Tr(m,n,-r)
is because the definition of
\Tr(m,n,-r)
would naturally identify it with the Cayley graph having
a-b
as the third generator, but, for our purposes, it is more convenient to use
a+b
.

2.3 Lemma
.

Given
m,n,r\in\mathbb{Z}

(
with
m,n\geq 2
)
, let

G=\langle\,a,b\mid ma=rb,\ nb=0,\ a+b=b+a\,\rangle,

so
G
is an abelian group of order
mn
. Then

\Qd(m,n,r)\cong\Cay(G;\pm a,\pm b)

and

\Tr(m,n,-r)\cong\Cay\bigl(G;\pm a,\pm b,\pm(a+b)\bigr).

2.4 Corollary
.

\Qd(m,n,r)\cong\Qd(m,n,-r)
and
\Tr(m,n,r)\cong\Tr(m,n,m-r)
.

Proof.

(
\Qd
) We have
ma=-r(-b)
and
n(-b)=0
, so using
-b
in the place of
b
yields a representation of
\Cay(G;\pm a,\pm b)
as
\Qd(m,n,-r)
.

(
\Tr
) Let
c=-(a+b)
, so
\{\pm a,\pm b,\pm(a+b)\}=\{\pm c,\pm b,\pm(c+b)\}
. Then

mc=-ma-mb=-rb-mb=-(r+m)b,

so using
c
in the place of
a
yields a representation of
\Cay(G;\pm a,\pm b,\pm(a+b))
as
\Tr(m,n,r+m)
. Therefore
\Tr(m,n,-r)\cong\Tr(m,n,r+m)
.
∎

2.5 Remark
.

By replacing
a
with
-a
, the proof of
2.4
shows that
\Qd(m,n,r)\cong\Qd(m,n,-r)
. However, the same trick does not work for
\Tr(m,n,r)
: if
a
is replaced with
-a
, then the equation
a+b+c=0
forces
b
and
c
to also be replaced with their negatives. Since
m(-a)=-ma=-rb=r(-b)
, this does not result in any change in the parameter
r
.

2C.
Criteria for stability or instability

2.12 Lemma

(cf.
[
3
, Lem. 2.4]
)
.

A connected, abelian Cayley graph
X=\Cay(G;S)
is unstable if and only if there exists
\alpha\in\Aut BX
, such that
\alpha(0,0)=(0,0)
, but
\alpha(0,1)\neq(0,1)

2.13 Remark
.

It is easy to see (and well known) that an abelian Cayley graph
\Cay(G;S)
has twin vertices if and only if
S+\mathsf{z}=S
, for some nonzero
\mathsf{z}\in G
. In other words,
S
is a union of cosets of
\langle\mathsf{z}\rangle
. By passing to a subgroup of
\langle\mathsf{z}\rangle
, there is no harm in assuming that
|\mathsf{z}|
is prime.

The following result was stated only for circulant graphs in
[
7
, Prop. 3.7]
(which is a slight generalization of
[
17
, Thm. C.4]
), but the same proof applies more generally. (In fact, the proof even applies without the assumption that
\mathsf{z}
has order
2
, if
S+\mathsf{z}=-(S+\mathsf{z})
is symmetric. And there is no need for
G
to be abelian.)

2.14 Lemma

(
[
7
, Prop. 3.7]
, cf.
[
17
, Thm. C.4]
)
.

An abelian Cayley graph
\Cay(G;S)
is unstable if
\Cay(G;S)\cong\Cay(G;S+\mathsf{z})
, for some element
\mathsf{z}
of order

2.15 Lemma

(Wilson
[

, §2.2]
)

A graph

is unstable if it has an automorphism

4.2 Lemma
.

Let
\{a,b,c\}
be a generating set of a finite abelian group
G
, such that

a+b+c=0

and  the sets
\{\pm a\}
,
\{\pm b\}
,
\{\pm c\}
are distinct.

The Cayley graph
X=\Cay(G;\pm a,\pm b,\pm c)
has twin vertices
(
or, equivalently, is trivially unstable
)
if and only if
(
perhaps after permuting
a
,
b
, and
c
)
either:

(1)

|a|=8
and
b=2a
,
or

(2)

|a|=|b|=3
.

In each case, the Cayley graph is listed in
4.1
.

Proof.

(
\Leftarrow
) Up to a group isomorphism,
X
is either
\Cay(\mathbb{Z}_{8};S_{8})
or
\Cay(\mathbb{Z}_{3}\times\mathbb{Z}_{3};S_{3})
, where

S_{8}=\{\pm 1,\pm 2,\pm 3\}

and
S_{3}=\{\pm(1,0),\pm(0,1),\pm(1,1)\}
.

We have
S_{8}=S_{8}+4
and
S_{3}=S_{3}+(-1,1)
, so both Cayley graphs have twin vertices, and are therefore trivially unstable.

Since
2
is an element of order
4
in
\mathbb{Z}_{8}
, the first Cayley graph is listed in
4.1
(
1
). The second is listed in
4.1
(
5
).

(
\Rightarrow
)
Let
S=\{\pm a,\pm b,\pm c\}
. Since
X
has twin vertices, we see from
2.13
that
S
is a union of cosets of some subgroup
\langle\mathsf{z}\rangle
of prime order. Since
|S|\leq 6
, we must have
|z|\in\{2,3,5\}
.

Case 1 .
Assume
|\mathsf{z}|=2
.

Then
-s+\mathsf{z}=-(s+\mathsf{z})
for all
s\in S
, so the permutation
x\mapsto x+\mathsf{z}
induces a well-defined action on
\bigl\{\{\pm a\},\{\pm b\},\{\pm c\}\bigr\}
. Since the permutation has order
2
, this implies
s+\mathsf{z}=-s
for some
s\in S
. Assume without loss of generality that
s=a
, so
|a|=4
and
\mathsf{z}=2a
.

If
b+\mathsf{z}=-b
, then we also have
c+\mathsf{z}=-c
, so

0=-(a+b+c)=(a+\mathsf{z})+(b+\mathsf{z})+(c+\mathsf{z})=(a+b+c)+\mathsf{z}=0+\mathsf{z}=\mathsf{z},

which contradicts the fact that
|\mathsf{z}|=2
.

Therefore, we must have
b+\mathsf{z}=\pm c
, so
a+b\pm(b+\mathsf{z})=0
.

•

For the minus sign, we have
0=a+b-(b+\mathsf{z})=a+\mathsf{z}
. This contradicts the fact that
|a|=4\neq|\mathsf{z}|
.

•

For the plus sign, we have
0=a+2b+\mathsf{z}=-a+2b
, so
a=2b
. Since
|a|=4
, this implies
|b|=8
, so
X
is the Cayley graph in (
1
).

Case 2 .
Assume
|\mathsf{z}|=3
.

This implies
|S|=6
, so no element of
S
has order
2
. Therefore, if
C
is any coset of
\langle\mathsf{z}\rangle
, then

(since
|C|
is odd). Since the cosets form a partition, we conclude that

. Hence, we may assume that

contains
a
,
b
, and
\pm c
. Then

0=a+(a+\mathsf{z})\pm(a+2\mathsf{z}),

so either
3a=0
or

. However,

, since

. Therefore
3a=0
, which means
|a|=3
. Since
\mathsf{z}
also has order
3
,
X
is the Cayley graph in (
2
).

Case 3 .
Assume
|\mathsf{z}|=5
.

Then
|S|=5
, so some element

of

has order 2. Since

, this implies

(\widetilde{s_{1}}^{m_{1}},\widetilde{s_{2}}^{m_{2}},\ldots,\widetilde{s_{\ell}}^{m_{\ell}})
denotes the sequence consisting of

m_{1}
copies of
\widetilde{s_{1}}
,
followed by
m_{2}
copies of
\widetilde{s_{2}}
,
followed by …,
followed by
m_{\ell}
copies of
\widetilde{s_{\ell}}
.

Proof of
4.1
.

(
\Leftarrow
) See
4.3
.

(
\Rightarrow
)
Let
S=\{\pm a,\pm b,\pm c\}
, so
X=\Cay(G;S)
.
Assuming that
X
is unstable, we will show that (at least) one of the listed conditions is satisfied.
Note that, since the equation
a+b+c=0
is completely symmetric, we are free to permute the elements of
\{a,b,c\}
arbitrarily.

By
2.12
, we may let
\alpha
be an automorphism of
BX
that fixes
(0,0)
, but does not fix
(0,1)
. Since
G\times\{0\}
and
G\times\{1\}
are the bipartition sets of
BX
, we know that each of these sets is
\alpha
-invariant.

Case 1 .

Assume
|G|
is odd.

By
2.7
, we know that
X
is trivially unstable.
Therefore,
4.2
applies.

Case 2 .

Assume
G=\langle s\rangle
for some
s\in S
.

See
4.4
.

Case 3 .

Assume
|c|=2
.

We may assume
Section

4
does not apply; therefore
c\notin\langle a\rangle
. If we let
n=|a|
, then

X\cong\Cay\bigl(\mathbb{Z}_{n}\times\mathbb{Z}_{2};\pm(1,0),\pm(1,1),(0,1)\bigr).

•

If
n\geq 8
, then
2.16
tells us that
X
is stable.

•

If
n\leq 7
, and
n\neq 4
, then
2.22
tells us that
X
is stable.

•

If
n=4
, then
|a|=4
and
|G|=8
, so condition (
1
) is satisfied.

Assumption C
.

Henceforth, we assume
|s|\geq 3
for all
s\in S

(
so
X
has valency
6
)
, and
|G|
is even.

Case 4 .

Assume
|s|=4
, for some
s\in S
.

We may assume
|G|=4k
, where
k
is odd, for otherwise condition (
1
) is satisfied. Then
G
is cyclic (since
|a|=4
and
G/\langle a\rangle
is generated by
b
). We may also assume that
X
is nontrivially unstable, for otherwise
4.2
applies. Then
X
must be listed in
2.11
. More precisely, since
|G|
is not divisible by
8
, it must be listed in
2.11
(
2
),
2.11
(
3
), or
2.11
(
6
). We will look at each of these possibilities separately (similarly to how these cases were considered in the proof of
4.4
).

2.11
(
2
)
:
Since the generator
\pm a
is odd, but the other two are even, the equation
a+b+c=0
cannot be satisfied.

2.11
(
3
)
:
Since
S
must contain the element
k
of order
4
, but
a\equiv 0\ (\mathrm{mod}\penalty\ 4)
, we must have

a+k=k
or
a+k=-k
or
a-k=k
or
a-k=-k
,

so
a\in\{0,2k\}
. However, we know
a\neq 0
. And
a\neq 2k
, because
2k\not\equiv 0\ (\mathrm{mod}\penalty\ 4)
(or because we are assuming that no element of
S
has order
2
).

2.11
(
6
)
:
Since
m
is odd, we know that
b
and
mb+2k
have the same parity. Therefore, the equation
a+b+c=0
implies that
a
is even. So
a\neq\pm k
. Then
\{\pm b,\pm mb+2k\}
contains
k
, and is therefore contained in
\langle k\rangle
. This is impossible, because
\langle k\rangle
does not contain 4 distinct nonzero elements.

Case 5 .

Assume
\alpha
is a group automorphism.

Then

•

\alpha(g,0)=\bigl(\varphi(g),0\bigr)
, for some automorphism
\varphi
of
G
,
and

•

\alpha(0,1)=(\mathsf{z},1)
, for some element
\mathsf{z}
of order
2
.

Then, since
\alpha\bigl(S\times\{1\}\bigr)=S\times\{1\}
, we have
\varphi(S)=S+\mathsf{z}
, so
\varphi
is an isomorphism from
\Cay(G;S)
to
\Cay(G;S+\mathsf{z})
.

We have
\varphi(s)\in\pm\{a,b,c\}+\mathsf{z}
for all
s\in\{a,b,c\}
. At least two elements of
S
must use the same sign, which implies there exists
\epsilon\in\{0,1\}
, such that
\varphi(S)
contains at least two elements of
\{\epsilon a+\mathsf{z},\epsilon b+\mathsf{z},\epsilon c+\mathsf{z}\}
. We may assume
\epsilon=1
(by composing with the automorphism
x\mapsto-x
if necessary). Then, by permuting
\{a,b,c\}
, we may assume that
a+\mathsf{z}
and
b+\mathsf{z}
are in
\varphi(\{a,b,c\})
. Therefore

0=\varphi(a+b+c)=\varphi(a)+\varphi(b)+\varphi(c)=(a+\mathsf{z})+(b+\mathsf{z})\pm(c+\mathsf{z})=(a+b)\pm c+\mathsf{z}=-c\pm c+\mathsf{z}.

Since
\mathsf{z}\neq 0
, we conclude that
2c=\mathsf{z}
, so
c
has order
4
. Therefore,
Section

4
applies.

Case 6 .

Assume
2s\neq 2t
, for all
s,t\in S
, such that
s\neq t
.

We may assume
\alpha
is not a group automorphism, for otherwise
Section

4
applies.
Therefore,
2.17
implies there exist
s,t,u,v\in S
such that
s+t=u+v\neq 0
and
\{s,t\}\neq\{u,v\}
. From the assumption of this
Section
, we see that this implies either
3a=\pm c
or
2a=\pm b\pm c
(perhaps after permuting
a
,
b
, and
c
).

•

If
3a=\pm c
, then
c=\pm 3a\in\langle a\rangle
, so
G=\langle a\rangle
. Therefore
Section

4
applies.

•

If
2a=\pm b-c=\pm b+(a+b)
, then
a=\pm b+b\in\langle b\rangle
, so
G=\langle b\rangle
. Therefore
Section

4
applies.

So we may assume

2a=\pm b+c=\pm b-(a+b)\in\{-a,-a-2b\},

so
3a\in\{0,-2b\}
.

•

If
3a=-2b
, then
a=-2a-2b=-2(a+b)=2c\in\langle c\rangle
, so
G=\langle c\rangle
. Therefore
Section

4
applies.

Hence, we may assume

\text{$3a=0$, so $|a|=3$}.

We may also assume
a\notin\langle b\rangle
, for otherwise
Section

4
applies. So
\langle a\rangle\cap\langle b\rangle=\{0\}
. This implies

, so, letting

, we have

X\cong\Cay\bigl(\mathbb{Z}_{3}\times\mathbb{Z}_{n};\pm(1,0),\pm(0,1),\pm(1,1)\bigr).

Note that:

•

(\widetilde{a}^{3})
and
(\widetilde{a}^{-3})
are paths of length
3
in
BX
from
(0,0)
to
\widetilde{0}=(0,1)
,

•

(\widetilde{b}^{3})
and
(\widetilde{c}^{-3})
are paths of length
3
in
BX
from
(0,0)
to

,
and

•

(\widetilde{b}^{-3})
and
(\widetilde{c}^{3})
are paths of length

in
BX
from
(0,0)
to
3\widetilde{c}
.

Also note that if

,

, and

\widetilde{0}
,

, and

We may assume

from

to

is

•

\binom{6}{3}

,
and

•

the paths
(\widetilde{b}^{6})

.

. For example, paths from
\widetilde{0}
to

.

Case 7 .

The remaining case.

Since
Section

4
does not apply, we have
2s=2t
, for some
s,t\in S
, such that
s\neq t
.
Since
Section

4
does not apply, we know

. Therefore, we may assume

, so

4

We may assume

and

4

and

is even and, up to a group isomorphism,

,

, and
c=-(2,1)
.

We may assume

does not satisfy condition (
2

\text{$n/2$ is odd}.

Also, since
Section

4
(and
Section

4
) does not apply, we have

. In addition,
since
Section

4
does not apply, we know

, so

. Therefore (since

is not odd), we have

Note that, for every

implies that

\alpha(v+k\widetilde{c})=\alpha(v)\pm k\widetilde{c}

.

Since

is
\alpha
-invariant, for all

Let
Y

BX
that is obtained by removing all edges of the form

. By (
4.6
), we know that

\alpha
-invariant, so

Now,
\widetilde{(0,0)}
and
\widetilde{(0,1)}
are the only vertices that are at distance

\widetilde{c}
and

-invariant.

Since

.

\widetilde{(0,1)}=\frac{n}{2}\widetilde{(2,1)}=\frac{n}{2}\,\widetilde{c}.
