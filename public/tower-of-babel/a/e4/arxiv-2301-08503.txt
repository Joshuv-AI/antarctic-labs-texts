A Systolic Inequality for the Filling Area Conjecture
Chaitanya Ambi

Abstract
We prove an upper bound on the systolic ratio of an orientable isometric filling of the circle equipped with a Riemannian metric. The bound depends only on the genus of isometric filling. We also apply the bound to the class of orientable isometric filling with a certain lower bound on the systole. We deduce that the Filling Area Conjecture holds true for this class when the genus is sufficiently large.

1
Introduction.

The
filling volume
of a compact, orientable
n
-dimensional manifold
N
with a piecewise smooth Riemannian metric
\mathcal{H}
is an important metric invariant discovered by Gromov in
[
2
]
. It is defined for
n\geq 2
as

\displaystyle FillVol(N,\mathcal{H}):=\inf_{(M,\mathcal{G})}\big\{vol_{n+1}(M,\mathcal{G}):N=\partial M\text{ and }(dist_{\mathcal{G}})|_{\partial M}=dist_{\mathcal{H}}\big\},

(1)

where
dist_{\mathcal{G}}
and
dist_{\mathcal{H}}
denote the distances induced by
\mathcal{G}
and
\mathcal{H}
, respectively.
Whenever

(dist_{\mathcal{G}})|_{\partial M}=dist_{\mathcal{H}},

we say that
M
fills
N

isometrically
. We refer the reader to
[
4
]
for more details.

For
n=1
, Gromov’s Filling Area Conjecture (see
[
2
]
) may be stated as follows.

Conjecture 1
.

For a smooth Riemannian metric
\mathcal{H}
on the circle
\mathbb{S}^{1}
, we have

FillVol(\mathbb{S}^{1},\mathcal{H})=\dfrac{vol_{1}(\mathbb{S}^{1},\mathcal{H})^{2}}{2\pi}.

If one assumes that a minimal filling is diffeomorphic to a hemisphere, then Conj. (
1
) can be deduced from Pu’s systolic inequality (see
[
4
]
, Eq. (5.2.2), p. 40). Note the upper bound

\displaystyle FillVol(\mathbb{S}^{1},\mathcal{H})\leq\dfrac{vol_{1}(\mathbb{S}^{1},\mathcal{H})^{2}}{2\pi},

(2)

obtained by considering the isometric filling by the hemisphere of constant curvature. In
[
1
]
, it is proven that Conj. (
1
) holds true for all genus one fillings. (See also
[
3
]
for the Finslerian case.)

We shall denote by
\Sigma_{g}
a compact surface of genus
g\geq 1
throughout this article. By virtue of the upper bound in Eq. (
2
), we may restrict our attention to following the class
\mathcal{I}_{g}(\mathbb{S}^{1},\mathcal{H})
of piecewise smooth, orientable and
isometric
filling
(M,\mathcal{G})
of
(\mathbb{S}^{1},\mathcal{H})
of fixed genus
g\geq 1
(defined so that
\chi(M)=1-2g
).

\mathcal{I}_{g}(\mathbb{S}^{1},\mathcal{H}):=\bigg\{(M,\mathcal{G}):M\text{ has genus }g\text{ and }vol_{2}(M,\mathcal{G})<\dfrac{vol_{1}(\mathbb{S}^{1},\mathcal{H})^{2}}{2\pi}\bigg\}.

Thus, to prove Conj. (
1
) for orientable isometric fillings is tantamount to showing that the class
\mathcal{I}_{g}(\mathbb{S}^{1},\mathcal{H})
is empty for each
g\geq 1
.

Consider a compact surface (with or without boundary) carrying a Riemannian metric
(\Sigma,\mathcal{G})
. Whenever
\Sigma
is not simply connected, we define a
systole
to be a closed curve with the shortest possible length which is not null-homotopic. We denote its length by
sys\pi_{1}(\Sigma,\mathcal{G})
. The
systolic ratio
of
(\Sigma,\mathcal{G})
(denoted by
SR(\Sigma,\mathcal{G})
) is defined as follows.

SR(\Sigma,\mathcal{G}):=\dfrac{(sys\pi_{1}(\Sigma,\mathcal{G}))^{2}}{vol_{2}(\Sigma,\mathcal{G})}.

When
\partial\Sigma=\emptyset
, we further denote the supremum of
SR(\Sigma,\mathcal{G})
over all metrics
\mathcal{G}
by
SR(\Sigma)
. For estimates of the systolic ratio of surfaces without boundary, we refer the reader to
[
4
]
.

As mentioned before, Pu’s systolic inequality yields the bound given by Eq. (
2
); see
[
2
]
. This suggests intimate connections between systolic and filling inequalitites. Indeed, we prove the following.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
\mathcal{H}
be a smooth Riemannian metric on
\mathbb{S}^{1}
. Consider an orientable and piecewise smooth isometric filling
(M,\mathcal{G})
of
(\mathbb{S}^{1},\mathcal{H})
. Assume that
\pi_{1}(M)
is non-trivial and put
g:=(1-\chi(M))/2
. Then the systolic ratio of
(M,\mathcal{G})
satisfies

SR(M,\mathcal{G})\leq SR(\Sigma_{g+1})/2.

(3)

The above result applies, in particular, to every possible minimal filling of non-zero genus. Furthermore, Thm. (
1.1
) allows us to infer the following.

Corollary 1.1
.

Let the notation and assumptions be as in Theorem
1.1
. There exists an integer
g_{0}\geq 1
(independently of
(\mathbb{S}^{1},\mathcal{H})
) such that whenever
g\geq g_{0}
and the systole of
(M,\mathcal{G})
satisfies

sys\pi_{1}(M,\mathcal{G})>\big(\dfrac{\log g}{2\pi\sqrt{g}}\big)\cdot vol_{1}(\mathbb{S}^{1},\mathcal{H}),

(4)

we have
(M,\mathcal{G})\not\in\mathcal{I}_{g}(\mathbb{S}^{1},\mathcal{H})
.

Thus, every orientable isometric filling of the circle with sufficiently large genus and systole exceeding the right hand side of Eq. (
4
) satisfies Conj.
1
.

Remark 1.2
.

There exist isometric fillings with arbitrarily large genus whose area is marginally greater than the filling area. Consider, for instance, an isometric filling placed on the top of a small cylinder with totally geodesic boundary
\mathbb{S}^{1}
. If we attach
g\geq 1
small handles to the hemisphere away from the boundary, the area of the resulting surface is close to the initial area, whereas the genus has increased by
g
.

Thus, it is the exact value of filling volume of a manifold which is important.

Remark 1.3
.

Interestingly, we may also consider Def. (
1
) in relation with cobordism. Set

(N,\mathcal{H})=(N_{1},\mathcal{H}_{1})\amalg(N_{2},\mathcal{H}_{2}),

(5)

the disjoint union two
n
-dimensional compact, orientable and connected Riemannian manifolds which are cobordant. One may consider the infimal volume of an
(n+1)
-dimensional Riemannian manifold filling both these manifolds isometrically. Note that if
N_{2}=\emptyset
, one recovers Def. (
1
) for
(N_{1},\mathcal{H}_{1})
.

Proof of Theorem
1.1
.

Proof.

For a orientable and piecewise smooth isometric filling
(M,\mathcal{G})
of
(\mathbb{S}^{1},\mathcal{H})
(with
g\geq 1
), we have
\partial M=\mathbb{S}^{1}
and
(dist_{\mathcal{G}})|_{\partial M}=dist_{\mathcal{H}}
. This implies, in particular, that
\partial M
is a smooth geodesic in
M
with

vol_{1}(\partial M,\mathcal{G}|_{\partial M})=vol_{1}(\mathbb{S}^{1},\mathcal{H}).

Note that
\partial M
is not null-homotopic because
\pi_{1}(M)
is non-trivial. Also, there exists a systole in
M
whose length satisfies
0<sys\pi_{1}(M,\mathcal{G})\leq vol_{1}(\mathbb{S}^{1},\mathcal{H})
.

We construct a closed surface
(\Sigma_{g+1},\mathcal{G}_{+})
from
(M,\mathcal{G})
as follows. Let
\partial M
carry the orientation compatible with the one on
M
. Choose any two antipodal points
p
and
p^{\prime}
on
\partial M
. Let
q
be a point on
\partial M
located at a distance
sys\pi_{1}(M,\mathcal{G})
from
p
. The point antipodal to
q
will be denoted by
q^{\prime}
. As the geodesic arcs
pq
and
p^{\prime}q^{\prime}
both have length equal to
sys\pi_{1}(M,\mathcal{G})
, we may glue these together. Finally, we glue
pq^{\prime}
and
qp^{\prime}
together. The piecewise smooth metric determined by
\mathcal{G}
on the resulting closed and orientable surface (which is homeomorphic to
\Sigma_{g+1}
) will be denoted by
\mathcal{G}_{+}
.

The closed curve in
(\Sigma_{g+1},\mathcal{G}_{+})
determined by
pp^{\prime}
with length
sys\pi_{1}(M,\mathcal{G})
must be a geodesic. (This holds true because
(M,\mathcal{G})
was an isometric filling to begin with.) Moreover, since
\pi_{1}(M)
is non-trivial, this closed geodesic must be a
systole
in
(\Sigma_{g+1},\mathcal{G}_{+})
. It is also clear that

vol_{2}(\Sigma_{g+1},\mathcal{G}_{+})=vol_{2}(M,\mathcal{G}).

Hence, we have

SR(\Sigma_{g+1},\mathcal{G}_{+})=SR(M,\mathcal{G}).

(6)

Next, we construct a closed and
non-orientable
surface
(\Sigma,\mathcal{G}_{-})
from
(M,\mathcal{G})
by gluing
pq
with
q^{\prime}p^{\prime}
and
pq^{\prime}
with
p^{\prime}q
(in that order). Observe that the orientable double cover of
(\Sigma,\mathcal{G}_{-})
has genus
(g+1)
. Moreover, it carries the unique piecewise smooth metric
\tilde{\mathcal{G}}_{-}
which makes the map

(\Sigma_{g+1},\tilde{\mathcal{G}}_{-})\twoheadrightarrow(M,\mathcal{G})

a Riemannian covering. Arguing exactly as in the case of
(\Sigma_{g+1},\mathcal{G}_{+})
, we get

SR(\Sigma,\mathcal{G}_{-})=SR(M,\mathcal{G}).

(7)

But now,

sys\pi_{1}(\Sigma_{g+1},\tilde{\mathcal{G}}_{-})\geq 2sys\pi_{1}(\Sigma,\mathcal{G}_{-}).

By Eq. (
7
), we get

\displaystyle SR(\Sigma_{g+1},\tilde{\mathcal{G}}_{-})\geq\dfrac{4(sys\pi_{1}(\Sigma,\mathcal{G}_{-}))^{2}}{2vol_{2}(\Sigma,\mathcal{G}_{-})},

\displaystyle=2SR(M,\mathcal{G}).

In view of Eq. (
6
), we see that

SR(\Sigma_{g+1},\tilde{\mathcal{G}}_{-})\geq 2SR(\Sigma_{g+1},\mathcal{G}_{+}).

Considering the definition of
SR(\Sigma_{g+1})
together with Eq. (
6
), we obtain

SR(\Sigma_{g+1})\geq 2SR(M,\mathcal{G}).

This proves Eq. (
3
) as required.
∎

Proof of Corollary
1.1
.

We maintain the notation as in Section
1
.

Proof.

Assume the contrary that there are isometric fillings in the class
\mathcal{I}_{g}(\mathbb{S}^{1},\mathcal{H})
for arbitrarily large
g\geq 1
which satisfy Eq. (
4
). For such an integer
g
, let
(M,\mathcal{G})\in\mathcal{I}_{g}(\mathbb{S}^{1},\mathcal{H})
be any piecewise smooth, orientable and isometric filling of the circle
(\mathbb{S}^{1},\mathcal{H})
. Thus,

vol_{2}(M,\mathcal{G})<(vol_{1}(\mathbb{S}^{1},\mathcal{H}))^{2}/2\pi.

We claim that
\partial M
is
not
a systole in
(M,\mathcal{G})
. Indeed, if it were so, then
sys\pi_{1}(M,\mathcal{G})=vol_{1}(\mathbb{S}^{1},\mathcal{H})
. Hence, the preceeding equation would force that

\displaystyle SR(M,\mathcal{G})>\dfrac{(vol_{1}(\mathbb{S}^{1},\mathcal{H}))^{2}}{(vol_{1}(\mathbb{S}^{1},\mathcal{H}))^{2}/2\pi},

\displaystyle=2\pi.

Combined with Thm.
1.1
, we would get

2\pi<SR(M,\mathcal{G})\leq SR(\Sigma_{g+1})/2.

This stark contradiction with the basic estimate
SR(\Sigma_{g+1})\leq 4/3
proves the claim (see
[
4
]
, Eq. (11.2.1)).

Next, we glue along
\mathbb{S}^{1}\approx\partial M
the (totally geodesic) boundary of hemisphere of constant sectional curvature equal to
(2\pi/vol_{1}(\mathbb{S}^{1},\mathcal{H}))^{2}
. We denote the piecewise smooth metric on the resulting closed, orientable surface (which is homeomorphic to
\Sigma_{g}
) by
\mathcal{G}_{0}
. Note that

vol_{2}(\Sigma_{g},\mathcal{G}_{0})=vol_{2}(M,\mathcal{G})+(vol_{1}(\mathbb{S}^{1},\mathcal{H}))^{2}/2\pi.

Hence,

2\cdot vol_{2}(M,\mathcal{G})\leq vol_{2}(\Sigma_{g},\mathcal{G}_{0})\leq(vol_{1}(\mathbb{S}^{1},\mathcal{H}))^{2}/\pi.

(8)

As
\partial M
is not a systole in
M
, we have

sys\pi_{1}(\Sigma_{g},\mathcal{G}_{0})=sys\pi_{1}(M,\mathcal{G}).

If we square the above equation and use Eq. (
8
), we obtain

\dfrac{\pi\cdot(sys\pi_{1}(M,\mathcal{G}))^{2}}{vol_{1}(\mathbb{S}^{1},\mathcal{H})^{2}}\leq SR(\Sigma_{g},\mathcal{G}_{0})\leq\dfrac{SR(M,\mathcal{G})}{2}\leq\dfrac{SR(\Sigma_{g+1})}{4}.

Here, we have used Thm.
1.1
for the rightmost inequality. In particular,

\bigg(\dfrac{sys\pi_{1}(M,\mathcal{G})}{vol_{1}(\mathbb{S}^{1},\mathcal{H})}\bigg)^{2}\leq\dfrac{SR(\Sigma_{g+1})}{4\pi}.

In view of the lower bound given by Eq. (
4
), this implies

\frac{1}{\pi}<\dfrac{g\cdot SR(\Sigma_{g+1})}{\log^{2}(g)}.

(9)

But now, we also have (see
[
4
]
, Thm. 11.3.1, Eq. (11.3.1), p. 88)

\limsup_{g\rightarrow\infty}\dfrac{(g+1)\cdot SR(\Sigma_{g+1})}{\log^{2}(g+1)}\leq\frac{1}{\pi}.

(10)

In view of the above Eq. (
10
), we see that Eq. (
9
) cannot hold for arbitrarily large value of
g
. This contradiction shows that there must be an integer
g_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
such that for
g\geq g_{0}
, Cor. (
1.1
) holds true.
∎
