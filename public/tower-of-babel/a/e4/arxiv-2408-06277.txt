Multi-marginal Schrödinger Bridges with Iterative Reference Refinement
Yunyi Shen, Renato Berlinghieri, Tamara Broderick

Abstract
Practitioners frequently aim to infer an unobserved population trajectory using sample snapshots at multiple time points. For instance, in single-cell sequencing, scientists would like to learn how gene expression evolves over time. But sequencing any cell destroys that cell. So we cannot access any cell’s full trajectory, but we can access snapshot samples from many cells. Stochastic differential equations are commonly used to analyze systems with full individual-trajectory access; since here we have only sample snapshots, these methods are inapplicable. The deep learning community has recently explored using Schrödinger bridges (SBs) and their extensions to estimate these dynamics. However, these methods either (1) interpolate between just two time points or (2) require a single fixed reference dynamic within the SB, which is often just set to be Brownian motion. But learning piecewise from adjacent time points can fail to capture long-term dependencies. And practitioners are typically able to specify a model class for the reference dynamic but not the exact values of the parameters within it. So we propose a new method that (1) learns the unobserved trajectories from sample snapshots across multiple time points and (2) requires specification only of a class of reference dynamics, not a single fixed one. In particular, we suggest an iterative projection method inspired by Schrödinger bridges; we alternate between learning a piecewise SB on the unobserved trajectories and using the learned SB to refine our best guess for the dynamics within the reference class. We demonstrate the advantages of our method via a well-known simulated parametric model from ecology, simulated and real data from systems biology, and real motion-capture data.



1
Introduction

Practitioners are often interested in the possible paths taken by a population of particles moving from one location to another in a given space. For example, biologists want to understand how gene expression, as measured by mRNA levels, changes when normal cells transform into cancer cells. This knowledge can help in developing methods to reverse this transformation. We can model the dynamics of mRNA concentration in each cell using a stochastic differential equation (SDE). However, we cannot measure mRNA concentration continuously, but only at several snapshots. Moreover, since measuring mRNA concentration requires destroying the cell, we cannot track the trajectory of any individual cell. Consequently, we only have marginal samples of the mRNA concentration at few snapshots. Despite this limitation, scientists aim to infer the distribution of possible trajectories that cells might follow during the transition from normal to cancerous states and predict their potential future paths.

While there is extensive theory on SDEs from trajectories densely sampled in time, these methods are not applicable to the kind of data in the mRNA example. In contrast, recent works in the deep learning community have demonstrated the potential of using Schrödinger bridges (SBs) to inferring possible trajectories connecting two time snapshots
(
Wang et al., 2023
;
Vargas et al., 2021
;
De Bortoli et al., 2021
)
. This approach addresses the problem by learning a pair of forward-backward SDEs that can transport particles between two time points (forward or backward, respectively). Once these SDEs are learnt, we can numerically solve them to sample from the underlying population of trajectories. However, simple SBs do not account for situations where we have multiple time snapshots. Extensions to handle multiple time snapshots have been explored by
Chen et al., 2019
and
Lavenant et al., 2021
. These extensions were shown to be equivalent to having a series of Schrödinger Bridges (SBs) between adjacent time steps. As a result, the learned dynamics may fail to capture long-term dependencies, seasonal patterns, and cyclic behaviors.

Another interesting advancement in multimarginal SBs was recently proposed by
Chen et al., 2024
. In this work, the authors modified the dynamics used in SBs (where one models the location of particles with SDEs) to Langevin dynamics, where instead the SDE governs particles’ velocity. This change allowed the dynamics to (1) pass through multiple snapshots and (2) generate smooth trajectories. By numerically solving this Langevin dynamic, we can sample trajectories that are smooth and differentiable. However, this method, like the other SB approaches mentioned above
(
Wang et al., 2023
;
Vargas et al., 2021
;
De Bortoli et al., 2021
)
, determines the distribution over possible trajectories by minimizing the relative entropy between the process and a pre-defined reference measure defined by an SDE. Ideally, these reference dynamics should be close to the underlying true dynamics particles might follow. Although scientists might have prior knowledge of how their system works
(
Pratapa et al., 2020
, e.g., the parametric form of the underlying dynamical model,)
, it is very rare that they have access to all the parameters. Consequently, practitioners often default to using Brownian motion as the reference dynamic. In fact, in the original work by
Schrödinger, 1932

(see also
Léonard, 2013
, for a historical review)
, the reference dynamic used was indeed Brownian motion, which was suitable for modeling particles moving under thermal fluctuations in a closed system. In this context, solving the SB problem is equivalent to solving an entropy-regularized optimal transport problem
(
Cuturi, 2013
)
. However, this approach may not be ideal for many open systems with energy intake. For instance, biological systems are such open systems that intake energy and do not necessarily adhere to the same entropy laws as gas molecules under thermal fluctuations. As Schrödinger himself recognized in
Schrödinger, 1946
, Chapter 6, with the famous remark ”What organisms feed upon is
negative
entropy.”

Due to the limitations of existing methods, we propose a new approach that (1) infers the distribution of trajectories from sample snapshots taken at multiple time points, and (2) relaxes the requirement for SBs to have a single fixed reference dynamic. Our algorithm (Algorithm
1
) provides pairs of forward-backward SDEs for each pair of time points, which can be used to obtain samples from the underlying population of trajectories. Our method focuses on identifying the dynamic model within a specified parametric reference class (e.g., adhering to inherent biases such as physical constraints) that is closest to all the observed marginals as the reference.

Our method iterates between two optimization steps with the goal of refining both our interpolation and our reference in the known reference class: (1) Learn a piecewise SB and sample the unobserved trajectories spanning the entire time horizon (over which we have observations) guided by our current best guess of the underlying dynamics. Essentially, this phase uses our initial model to fill in the gaps between observed data points, sketching a continuous trajectory that aligns with the known snapshots. (2) Use the learnt SB to refine our best guess (in the reference class) for the underlying dynamics. This step does not strictly require the model to match every observed data point perfectly. Instead, it ensures that the refined model is the best at representing the marginal samples within the pre-specified reference class. This balance allows us to maintain a degree of flexibility, avoiding the trap of overfitting to specific data points while still adhering to our fundamental understanding of the system. We show that by this iterating process, we always reduce the Kullback–Leibler divergence between (1) our inferred trajectory distribution and (2) the current best reference, and under further assumptions this procedure would converge.

We illustrate our proposal using two simulated dynamic systems: (1) the Lotka-Volterra model
(
Goel et al., 1971
)
, to demonstrate our method’s effectiveness in a toy setting, and (2) a simulated repressilator system
(
Nakajima et al., 2005
)
, which shows the applicability of our iterative procedure in a simple biological context. We then apply our method to real embryoid body cell differentiation data
(
Moon et al., 2019b
)
, human embryonic stem cell data
(
Chu et al., 2016
)
, and the CMU Graphics Lab Motion Capture Database
(
CMU Graphics Lab, 2024
)
to showcase its practical utility with real-world data.

Setup

In this section, we introduce our problem and the main challenges we aim to address. Following the biology example introduced above, suppose we are interested in finding a way to sample the trajectories of mRNA concentration in a population of cells. At time step

n_{i}

t_{i}

In total, we have

We assume the time steps are unique and increasing, i.e.,

For each particle

A1

•

A2

Moreover, note that the SDE in

The ideal goal of this work is to use the samples

{\bm{b}}_{\text{gt}}(\cdot,\cdot)

3
Our method

In this section, we present our method and provide guarantees regarding its behavior. Recall that our ultimate goal is to infer the underlying distribution of trajectories,
p_{{\bm{b}}_{\text{gt}}}
, that interpolate the observed marginals, based on the samples
\bm{Y}_{t_{i}}^{n_{i}}
, for
n_{i}=1,\dots,N_{i}
and
i=1,\dots,I
.

Schrödinger Bridges and multi-marginal generalization.

When we have some prior knowledge about the system’s underlying dynamics, Schrödinger Bridges
(
Wang et al., 2023
;
Vargas et al., 2021
;
Lavenant et al., 2021
, SBs,)
can be used to address this trajectory distribution inference problem. The goal of SBs is to find a distribution over trajectories that interpolate between two marginal distributions, ensuring that the learned distribution is as close as possible — in a Kullback–Leibler divergence (
D_{\mathrm{KL}}
) sense — to that prior knowledge (the “reference”, often set as Brownian motion).

From a mathematical point of view, as observed in
Lavenant et al., 2021
, it is appealing to formalize this problem in terms of KL divergence between probability distributions over trajectories, as this allows the application of tools from convex optimization and information theory. However, in practical algorithms, this learned distribution is usually represented using a pair of forward-backward SDEs, with drifts
{\bm{b}}^{\text{forward}}
and
{\bm{b}}^{\text{backward}}

(
Wang et al., 2023
;
Vargas et al., 2021
;
Lavenant et al., 2021
;
Chen et al., 2019
)
. These drifts can then be used to obtain sample trajectories by numerically solving the associated forward and backward SDEs. We will follow this approach in our subsequent algorithms.

First of all, observe that the straightforward multi-marginal generalization of SB to multiple time steps
(
Chen et al., 2019
)
, a building block of our method, can be written as a constrained optimization problem:

\displaystyle\argmin_{q\in\mathcal{D}}D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q||p_{{\bm{b}}}),~~~~\mathcal{D}=\left\{q:\tilde{q}_{0}=\pi_{0},\dots,\tilde{q}_{t_{I}}=\pi_{t_{I}}\right\}

(2)

where
p_{{\bm{b}}}
is the density (relative to a Wiener measure) of the probability distribution associated with the reference SDE with drift
{\bm{b}}
, and
\mathcal{D}
is the family of densities over trajectories, such that the associated probability distributions at time points
0,\ldots,t_{I}
are
\tilde{q}_{0},\ldots,\tilde{q}_{t_{I}}
. These distributions at each time point match the given marginal distributions
\pi_{0},\ldots,\pi_{t_{I}}
, respectively
2
2

2

Note that, in the SB literature, this KL divergence is often defined in terms of probability distributions. But since implicitly to compute the KL divergence one uses the densities ratios rather than the distributions, here we define it directly in terms of the underlying densities.
.

Issues with multi-marginal Schrödinger Bridges.

Even though
eq.

2
alone appears to leverage information from all time snapshots jointly,
Lavenant et al., 2021
;
Chen et al., 2019
showed that — under very mild conditions — this SB problem is equivalent to a collection of
I-1
separate SB problems, each between two adjacent time steps. That is, this method is essentially performing piecewise interpolation between each pair of consecutive time points and does not share information across different time intervals. Therefore, in practice, the solution to this problem is a collection of
I-1
pairs of forward and backward drifts
\{{\bm{b}}^{\text{forward}}_{i},{\bm{b}}^{\text{backward}}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{I-1}
. The estimate of the true forward drift is then a piecewise function, whose value is the same as
{\bm{b}}^{\text{forward}}_{i}
in interval
i
, from
t_{i-1}
to
t_{i}
. And, similarly, the estimated backward drift is a piecewise function with
{\bm{b}}^{\text{backward}}_{i}
in interval
i
, from
t_{i-1}
to
t_{i}
.

Another limitation of this naive approach is that it requires the practitioners to specify one single reference SDE via a reference drift
{\bm{b}}
, and this can be challenging. Indeed, even if scientists knew a parametric form for the underlying dynamical system
(e.g.,
Pratapa et al., 2020
, for mRNA dynamics)
, very rarely they have access to all of the parameters.

Our solution.

To overcome these issues, we propose to iterate the following steps: for each of the
I-1
time intervals, we solve an SB problem to learn a pair of forward-backward drifts
{\bm{b}}^{\text{forward}}_{i},{\bm{b}}^{\text{backward}}_{i}
that we can use to generate samples from the underlying distribution over trajectories. Then, we use these samples to refine our reference SDE. In this refining step, we find the best density,
p_{{\bm{b}}}
, of a probability distribution associated to an SDE with drift
{\bm{b}}
in some parametric model family
\mathcal{F}
by optimizing over the second argument of the KL divergence. In practice, optimizing over the second argument of KL divergence can be achieved by (1) sampling from the obtained distribution of trajectories by using
{\bm{b}}^{\text{forward}}_{i},{\bm{b}}^{\text{backward}}_{i}
for each of the
I-1
time intervals, and (2) learning the best drift via maximum likelihood using these sampled trajectories. Note that since we only have access to samples from the marginals
\pi_{0},\dots,\pi_{t_{I}}
, our marginal constraints can be seen as empirical distributions, and we denote them as
\hat{\pi}_{0},\dots,\hat{\pi}_{t_{I}}
. Concretely, we can write our proposed estimator of trajectory distribution as

\hat{q}=\argmin_{q\in\mathcal{D}}\min_{p_{\bm{b}}\in\mathcal{F}}D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q||p_{{\bm{b}}}),~~~~\mathcal{D}=\left\{q:\tilde{q}_{0}=\hat{\pi}_{0},\dots,\tilde{q}_{t_{I}}=\hat{\pi}_{t_{I}}\right\}

(3)

where
\mathcal{D}
is — as above — the family of densities of probability distributions over trajectories associated to SDEs satisfying the constraints of the marginal distribution, and
\mathcal{F}
is another family of densities of probability distributions associated to SDEs with parametric drift.

Here,
\hat{q}
represents the ultimate goal of our iterative procedure: the optimal density corresponding to a distribution over trajectories that both passes through the observed marginals and minimizes the KL divergence with respect to our model family
\mathcal{F}
. In practice, our algorithm doesn’t yield a closed-form expression for this density, but rather a method to sample from it. Specifically, it generates
I-1
pairs of forward and backward SDEs, characterized by their respective drifts
\{{\bm{b}}^{\text{forward}}_{i},{\bm{b}}^{\text{backward}}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{I-1}
, which can be used to draw samples from
\hat{q}
. This approach provides an estimator for the distribution of trajectories, represented as pairs of forward-backward drifts. This iterative solution offers greater flexibility by allowing the reference to adapt to the data, emphasizing general trends over specific observed values, and aligning more closely with practitioners’ understanding of the underlying dynamics.

Solving our minimization problem.

We can solve this minimization problem by iteratively minimizing the Kullback-Leibler divergence with respect to one argument while keeping the other constant. When keeping
p_{{\bm{b}}}
constant, we are finding the optimal
q\in\mathcal{D}
which satisfies the marginal constraints and is as close as possible to
p_{{\bm{b}}}
. When keeping
q
constant, we are finding the optimal density associated to drift
{\bm{b}}
, which is in
\mathcal{F}
and is as close as possible to
q
. This approach consistently reduces the KL divergence between the two densities at each step. To intuitively understand why this works, consider one iteration of the process: by fixing one density and adjusting the other to minimize the KL divergence, we ensure that each step moves us closer to the optimal solution. Under assumptions specified in
1
, this gradual reduction in distance guarantees convergence to a density associated to the distribution that best fit the observed data within the specified model family.

\displaystyle=\argmin_{q\in\mathcal{D}}\,D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q\|p_{{\bm{b}}}^{(k)})

\displaystyle=\argmin_{p_{\bm{b}}\in\mathcal{F}}\,D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q^{(k+1)}\|p_{{\bm{b}}})

D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q^{(k+1)}\|p_{{\bm{b}}}^{(k+1)})\leq D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q^{(k)}\|p_{{\bm{b}}}^{(k)})

Solving for the minimum in
eq.

4
.

This step is a standard Schrödinger bridge minimization problem, where the reference dynamic is
p_{{\bm{b}}}^{(k)}
, the density associated to the probability distribution associated to SDE with current best reference drift,
\hat{\bm{b}}^{(k)}

Caluya and Halder, 2022

In this step, we refine the reference dynamic exploiting the samples we obtained from

\displaystyle\argmin_{p_{\bm{b}}\in\mathcal{F}}D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q^{(k+1)}||p_{{\bm{b}}})

\displaystyle=\argmin_{p_{\bm{b}}\in\mathcal{F}}\mathbb{E}_{q^{(k+1)}}\log(q^{(k+1)}/p_{{\bm{b}}})

(6)

\displaystyle=\argmin_{p_{\bm{b}}\in\mathcal{F}}\left[\mathbb{E}_{q^{(k+1)}}\log q^{(k+1)}-\mathbb{E}_{q^{(k+1)}}\log p_{{\bm{b}}}\right]

\displaystyle=\argmax_{p_{\bm{b}}\in\mathcal{F}}\mathbb{E}_{q^{(k+1)}}\log p_{{\bm{b}}}

That is, the minimization problem in the second projection step is equivalent to a maximum likelihood estimation (MLE) problem, where observations are from
q^{(k+1)}
and we are interested in estimating the best

in the family
\mathcal{F}

{\bm{b}}

q^{(k+1)}

q^{(k+1)}

\log p_{b}

\Delta t

4
Experiments

In this section, we evaluate our model using data from two simulated biological examples and three real-world datasets. Our results demonstrate that our method not only accurately reconstructs the trajectories but also consistently outperforms the baseline models in terms of performance metrics.

Experimental setup.

For our method, we use a different parametric reference family for each experiment (to reflect the knowledge we have about the system). We always start with a Brownian motion as the initial reference, so that the first iteration is comparable to a SB step with a standard Brownian reference measure. We iterate through our algorithm 10 times to refine the model: we find empirically that this number of iterations balances efficiency and accuracy, with little improvement beyond this point for all the experiments. To train our model, we use data from even steps (starting 0) as training and odd steps as holdout validation data. Across the experiments, we set the known volatility
\gamma
to 0.1, as suggested in
Vargas et al., 2021
. Since these algorithms are stochastic, each experiment was repeated 10 times with different random seeds.

Metrics of success.

For each experiment, we evaluate the performance of our model on the validation data both visually and via a summary of error. For the summary, we calculate the Earth Mover’s Distance (EMD) between the distribution of the inferred trajectory at each validation time point and the corresponding holdout validation data. The EMD, as described by
Rubner et al., 1998
, measures the distance between two probability distributions by computing the minimum cost required to transform one distribution into the other. This robust metric allows us to quantify the similarity between the inferred and actual trajectories. This is a valid metric for our experiments, since if the inferred and actual trajectories are from the same distribution we expect it to converge to 0 (as the amount of trajectories goes to infinity). See
Fournier and Guillin, 2015
for more details on this property. We evaluate the mean and standard deviation of EMD in each experiment for performance and variation across the 10 different seeds. We report these results in tables
1
,
2
,
3
,
4
. In each table, we highlight in green the method(s) performing the best. In particular, we always highlight the method having best mean EMD across seeds, and every other method whose mean minus one standard deviation is lower or equal than the mean of the best method. In
appendix

C
we also include results considering the Maximum Mean Discrepancy (MMD). In general, we see that the results using MMD and EMD are aligned.

Baselines.

We compare our approach to:

1.

A vanilla multimarginal SB (
Vanilla-SB
), where an SDE is learnt separately for each pair of adjacent time steps solving a SB problem with Brownian motion reference. We provide details on the implementation of this baseline in
section

A.4

2.

The Deep Momentum multimarginal Schrödinger bridge (
DM-SB
) by
(
Chen et al., 2024
)
. The main idea behind DPSB is that to get a smooth trajectory for the locations, instead of directly inferring locations using a multimarginal SB, one can focus on learning the trajectory of the underlying velocity (the derivative of the location with respect to time) with a SB. By doing this, one can be guaranteed to get smooth trajectories for the locations. This proposal focused on getting smooth trajectories rather than refining the reference (which is a Brownian motion in their case). In principle, our method could be combined with this to generate smooth trajectories while iteratively refining the reference. This is an interesting future direction to explore. As a baseline to be compared to, however, we take the exact proposal by
Chen et al., 2024
without combining it with our method or using alternative reference dynamics. For this baseline, we use the code provided by the authors of the paper at
https://github.com/TianrongChen/DMSB

(
Chen, 2021
)
. We provide more details in
section

A.5
.

Computational Cost.

In this section we briefly discuss the cost of training each model we are considering. The simplest model is the Vanilla-SB. With our implementation, we can train the model and obtain trajectories from the underlying trajectory distribution in approximately 1 to 2 minutes. In terms of computation, our method is basically
K
times more expensive than a Vanilla-SB. Indeed, we have to solve an SB problem
K
times, and the
MLEFit
step is a simple least squares step, which can be completed much faster than the SB estimation step. Therefore we can obtain trajectories from the underlying trajectory distribution in approximately 10 to 20 minutes. The DM-SB paper, instead, requires learning trajectory of the underlying velocity, and this is more expensive. With the implementation suggested in
https://github.com/TianrongChen/DMSB
, training the model takes approximately 1 to 2 hours. Consequently, our method offers a substantial speed advantage compared to DM-SB, providing a faster solution without compromising on the quality of the estimated trajectories, as we will see in the next subsections.

5
Discussion

We introduced an iterative approach for learning trajectories using only marginal samples, without access to one single reference dynamics’ parameters. We show that our method provides superior performance in experiments with biological significance. Nonetheless, challenges remain. Marginal samples may not always provide sufficient information for accurate system identification (see Appendix
D
for a more detailed discussion on identifiability). Moreover, our approach presumes observations are free from observational noise (that is, noise not coming from the SDE but from other sources that do not influence the trajectory, e.g. measurement errors). This is often a restrictive assumption in practice. To overcome this limitation, our method could be adapted to include observational noise, following a strategy employed in
Wang et al., 2023
. We might also combine our method with the momentum-based method by
Chen et al., 2024
to obtain smooth trajectories. Finally, we would like to extend our method to make forecasts, i.e., predict the distribution of latent trajectories at future time points. We believe that our method can be extended to address these challenges, and we hope to explore these directions in future work.

Appendix A
Implementation details

In this section we discuss the implementation details of our method.

A.1
First projection at one consecutive pair

In this step, we are interested in learning
I-1
pairs of forward and backward drifts,
\{{\bm{b}}^{\text{forward}}_{i},{\bm{b}}^{\text{backward}}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{I-1}
, that are ideally one the time inversion of the other. As explained in the main text, any SB algorithm currently in the literature could be used for this step. Some potential alternatives are
Vargas et al., 2021
,
Wang et al., 2021
and
Caluya and Halder, 2022
.

In our implementation, we decided to use the work in
Vargas et al., 2021
. In particular, we use Algorithm 2 in the paper
4
4

4

The code implementation available at
https://github.com/franciscovargas/GPSinkhorn
. We use the available code as it is and stick to the authors’ choice for most parameters. The only difference comes from the fact that in
Vargas et al., 2021
, the authors have only access to two time steps,
t_{0}
and
t_{1}
, such that
t_{1}-t_{0}=1
. The authors in the paper pick discretization step
\Delta t=0.01
, and therefore the total number of steps in their algorithm is
N=1/\Delta t=100
. In our method, we also pick
\Delta t=0.01
, but we have multiple time intervals, not necessarily all of length 1. Therefore we have a different amount of discretized steps,
N_{i}=[(t_{i+1}-t_{i})/\Delta t]
, for each time interval
i
.

Note that in
Vargas et al., 2021
the authors suggested 5 iterations are enough for this algorithm to converge
(
Vargas et al., 2021
, see also Fig.4 of)
, but we decided to run their algorithm for 10 iterations, which is the default in the available code
(
Vargas, 2021
)
.

A.2
Sampling routine

In this sampling step, we have access to
I-1
pairs of forward and backward drifts, denoted as
\{{\bm{b}}^{\text{forward}}_{i},{\bm{b}}^{\text{backward}}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{I-1}
, and we are interested in obtaining samples from the underlying trajectories distribution. More precisely, we are interested in obtaining one trajectory
\bm{X}_{t}^{(i,n_{i})}
,
{0<t\leq t_{I}}
, for each observed sample
\bm{Y}_{t_{i}}^{n_{i}}
. We can do this by numerically solving the SDEs associated to these drifts using a standard Euler-Maruyama approach
(
Särkkä and Solin, 2019
, Chapter 3.4)
with sampling rate
\Delta t
.

More precisely, we can obtain
\bm{X}_{t}^{(i,n_{i})}
for
{t_{i}<t\leq t_{I}}
, by starting from
\bm{Y}_{t_{i}}^{n_{i}}
and simulating forward, with discrete step
\Delta t
, the SDEs with drifts
\{{\bm{b}}^{\text{forward}}_{i},{\bm{b}}^{\text{forward}}_{i+1},\ldots,{\bm{b}}^{\text{forward}}_{I}\}
. We start at
t_{i}
and use
{\bm{b}}^{\text{forward}}_{i}
to simulate forward
\bm{Y}_{t_{i}}^{n_{i}}
to obtain the trajectory at discrete times
t_{i}+\Delta t,t_{i}+2\Delta t,\ldots
. We do this for
L_{i}
steps, where
L_{i}
is the smallest integer such that
t_{i}+L_{i}\Delta t\geq t_{i+1}
. That is, we use
{\bm{b}}^{\text{forward}}_{i}
as long as we are diffusing over the time interval
[t_{i},t_{i+1}]
. As soon as our discrete solving routine gives us an observation beyond time
t_{i+1}
, we stop using
{\bm{b}}^{\text{forward}}_{i}
and start using
{\bm{b}}^{\text{forward}}_{i+1}
. In particular, we start using
{\bm{b}}^{\text{forward}}_{i+1}
from
\bm{X}_{t_{i}+L_{i}\Delta t}^{(i,n_{i})}
, and simulate this forward for
L_{i+1}
steps, until
t_{i}+(L_{i}+L_{i+1})\Delta t\geq t_{i+2}
. And we proceed in this way until we have simulated all the way forward until
t_{I}
. With this routine, it might happen that in the very last step we obtain
\bm{X}_{t_{i}+\sum_{j=i}^{I-1}L_{j}\Delta t}^{(i,n_{i})}
with
t_{i}+\sum_{j=i}^{I-1}L_{j}\Delta t\geq t_{I}
. Since we are assuming A1 and A2, and the trajectories are continuous, we do not expect the trajectory to vary a lot in the time past
t_{I}
, so we just consider the trajectory up to
\bm{X}_{t_{i}+\sum_{j=i}^{I-1}L_{j}\Delta t}^{(i,n_{i})}
.

In a very similar manner, we obtain
\bm{X}_{t}^{(i,n_{i})}
for
0\leq t\leq t_{i}
, by starting from
\bm{Y}_{t_{i}}^{n_{i}}
and simulating backward, with discrete step
\Delta t
, the SDEs with drifts
\{{\bm{b}}^{\text{backward}}_{i},{\bm{b}}^{\text{backward}}_{i-1},\ldots,{\bm{b}}^{\text{backward}}_{1}\}
. We can then obtain a full trajectory for
\bm{Y}_{t_{i}}^{n_{i}}
by concatenating the backward and forward simulated trajectories. We denote this by
\bm{X}_{t}^{(i,n_{i})}
. These trajectories represent samples from the distribution over trajectories at step
k+1
,
q^{(k+1)}
, and can be used in the next step of our algorithm. In practice, we obtain a time discretized approximation of
\bm{X}_{t}^{(i,n_{i})}
with a total of
\sum_{i}L_{i}
steps (and
\sum_{i}L_{i}+1
end points). We denote this discretized trajectory as
\bm{X}_{\ell\Delta t}^{(i,n_{i})}
for
\ell=0,\dots,\sum_{i}L_{i}
. This solution will slightly overshoot the first or last step, we do not anticipate this to cause a large problem since our drift is at most linear growth, and the difference in less than one step size will be small since sample trajectories must be continuous.

A.3
Second projection

Here we describe the subroutine we denoted as MLEfit in Algorithm
1
.

We first observe that the inverse projection
\argmin_{p_{{\bm{b}}}}D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q||p_{{\bm{b}}})
is equivalent to maximizing the cross entropy between
p_{{\bm{b}}}
and
q

\displaystyle\argmin_{p_{{\bm{b}}}}D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q||p_{{\bm{b}}})

\displaystyle=\argmin_{p_{{\bm{b}}}}\mathbb{E}_{q}\log\frac{q}{p_{{\bm{b}}}}=\argmin_{p_{{\bm{b}}}}\left[\mathbb{E}_{q}\log q-\mathbb{E}_{q}\log p_{{\bm{b}}}\right]=\argmax_{p_{{\bm{b}}}}\mathbb{E}_{q}\log p_{{\bm{b}}}

That is, the second projection can be viewed as the expected log-likelihood when true data is from
q
and our model is
p_{{\bm{b}}}
. We do not have access to the full distribution
q
, but only to the time discretized samples obtained from the previous sampling routine,
\bm{X}_{\ell\Delta t}^{(i,n_{i})}
for
\ell=0,\dots,\sum_{i}L_{i}
. Therefore, we can approximate the expectation with a sample mean.

To do so, observe that the likelihood of these time discretized samples under model
p_{{\bm{b}}}
can be calculated by an autoregressive process, since under SDE model
p_{b}
with drift
b
, these
\bm{X}_{\ell\Delta t}^{(i,n_{i})}
form a Markov chain with transition kernel being Gaussian, i.e.,
\bm{X}_{(\ell+1)\Delta t}^{(i,n_{i})}|\bm{X}_{\ell\Delta t}^{(i,n_{i})}\sim{\mathcal{N}}(\bm{X}_{\ell\Delta t}^{(i,n_{i})}+{\bm{b}}(\bm{X}_{\ell\Delta t}^{(i,n_{i})},\ell\Delta t),\gamma\Delta t)
. That is, each point in the trajectory is dependent only on the immediate previous point, with the next value being a normally distributed variable centered around the previous value adjusted by the drift term

{\bm{b}}
, with variance
\gamma\Delta t
.

Therefore, we can approximate
\mathbb{E}_{q}\log p_{{\bm{b}}}
by the sample mean

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{q}\log p_{{\bm{b}}}\approx L({\bm{b}}):=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sum_{i=1}^{I}N_{i}}\frac{1}{\sum_{i}L_{i}}\sum_{i=1}^{I}\sum_{n_{i}=1}^{N_{i}}\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}-\frac{1}{2\gamma\Delta t}||(\bm{X}_{(\ell+1)\Delta t}^{(i,n_{i})}-\bm{X}_{\ell\Delta t}^{(i,n_{i})})-{\bm{b}}(\bm{X}_{\ell\Delta t}^{(i,n_{i})},\ell\Delta t)||_{2}^{2}

(7)

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\log(\gamma\Delta t)

And so to find the new optimal reference drift
\hat{b}
it is enough to solve the following minimization problem:

\displaystyle\hat{{\bm{b}}}=\argmin_{\bm{b}}\frac{1}{\sum_{i=1}^{I}N_{i}}\frac{1}{\sum_{i}L_{i}}\sum_{i=1}^{I}\sum_{n_{i}=1}^{N_{i}}\sum_{\ell=0}^{L-1}\frac{1}{2\gamma\Delta t}||(\bm{X}_{(\ell+1)\Delta t}^{(i,n_{i})}-\bm{X}_{\ell\Delta t}^{(i,n_{i})})-{\bm{b}}(\bm{X}_{\ell\Delta t}^{(i,n_{i})},\ell\Delta t)||_{2}^{2}

We can solve this minimization problem by any standard nonlinear least squares routine. In our implementation, for each experiment we parametrize the reference family of drifts as a neural network (more precisely, a
nn.Module
in PyTorch), and train this neural network to predict the finite differences
\bm{X}_{(\ell+1)\Delta t}^{(i,n_{i})}-\bm{X}_{\ell\Delta t}^{(i,n_{i})}
using an MSE loss and gradient descent. We provide more details for the reference family choice (and corresponding neural architecture) for each experiment in
section

C.1.2
(Lotka-Volterra),
section

C.2.2
(repressilator),
section

C.3.2
(single cell) and
section

C.4.2
(motion capture). During training, the learning rate is set to 0.01 and we consider 50 epochs. These hyperparameter values were determined by a grid search on combinations of learning rates
\{0.05,0.01,0.005,0.001\}
and epochs of
\{20,50\}

B.1
Existence of densities and convexity

In our work, we assume that all the SDEs share the same diffusion term
\sqrt{\gamma}dW_{t}
, with fixed
\gamma
. When we consider these SDEs over a finite time horizon, it is well-known that the probability distribution over trajectories admits a density with respect to the Wiener measure defined by a Brownian motion with volatility
\gamma
. This is because of Girsanov’s theorem (see, e.g., Theorem 8.6.3 in
Oksendal, 2013
). This theorem guarantees that the probability distribution over trajectories induced by the solution to an SDE with a drift and a Brownian motion component can be transformed into a probability distribution over trajectories induced by just the Brownian motion (i.e., the Wiener measure) through a change of measure. Note that Girsanov’s theorem holds true in this setting since assumptions A1 and A2 imply Novikov’s condition, equation (8.6.7) in Theorem 8.6.3 in
Oksendal, 2013
.

Having established the existence of densities, we can now define convexity for the sets
\mathcal{F}
and
\mathcal{D}
in terms of convexity of the densities in the sets. In the rest of our work, we say that
\mathcal{F}
and
\mathcal{D}
are convex if for all densities associated to SDEs
p,p^{\prime}\in\mathcal{F}
and
q,q^{\prime}\in\mathcal{D}
and all
\omega\in[0,1]
we have
(1-\omega)\tilde{p}+\omega\tilde{p}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{F}
and
(1-\omega)\tilde{q}+\omega\tilde{q}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{D}
.

B.2
Continuous setting adaptation of Theorem 5.3,
Csiszár and Shields, 2004

Now that we have clearly defined what we mean when we talk about convex families, we can state and prove
B.1
.

Theorem B.1

(Continuous setting adaptation of Theorem 5.3,
Csiszár and Shields, 2004
)
.

Let
D(q,p)
be a divergence between densities
q
and
p
from convex families of (probability) densities
\mathcal{D}
and
\mathcal{F}
respectively. Define
p^{*}(q):=\arg\min_{p\in\mathcal{F}}D(q,p)
and
q^{*}(p):=\arg\min_{q\in\mathcal{D}}D(q,p)
. Consider the iterative scheme given by
q^{(k)}=q^{*}(p^{(k-1)})
and
p^{(k)}=p^{*}(q^{(k)})
. Assume the existence of a non-negative function
\delta(q,p)
such that the following conditions are satisfied:

1. Three-Points Property:
For all
q\in\mathcal{D}
and
p\in\mathcal{F}
,

\delta(q,q^{*}(p))+D(q^{*}(p),p)\leq D(q,p).

(8)

2. Four-Points Property:
For all
q^{\prime}\in\mathcal{D}
and
p^{\prime}\in\mathcal{F}
, if
\min_{p\in\mathcal{F}}D(q,p)<\infty
, then

D(q^{\prime},p^{\prime})+\delta(q^{\prime},q)\geq D(q^{\prime},p^{*}(q)).

(9)

3. Boundedness Property:
For all
p\in\mathcal{F}
, if
\min_{q\in\mathcal{D}}D(q,p)<\infty
, then

\delta(q^{*}(p),q^{(1)})<\infty.

(10)

Then, if
\min_{q\in\mathcal{D},p\in\mathcal{F}}D(q,p)<\infty
, the iteration converges to the infimum of
D(q,p)
over
\mathcal{D}
and
\mathcal{F}
:

\lim_{n\to\infty}D(q_{n},p_{n})=\inf_{q\in\mathcal{D},p\in\mathcal{F}}D(q,p).

Proof.

First, from the Three-Points property we have that for all
q\in\mathcal{D}
:

\delta(q,q^{(k+1)})+D(q^{(k+1)},p^{(k)})\leq D(q,p^{(k)}),

where
q^{(k+1)}=q^{*}(p^{(k)})
. Then, using the Four-Points property, we get:

D(q,p^{(k)})\leq D(q,p)+\delta(q,q^{(k)}).

for all
q\in\mathcal{D}
and
p\in\mathcal{F}
because
p^{(k)}=p^{*}(q^{(k)})
. Combining these results yields:

\delta(q,q^{(k+1)})\leq D(q,p)-D(q^{(k+1)},p^{(k)})+\delta(q,q^{(k)}).

This inequality shows the change in the discrepancy measure
\delta
across iterations. By the definition of the iterative process, the sequence of divergences decreases monotonically:

D(q^{(1)},p^{(0)})\geq D(q^{(1)},p^{(1)})\geq D(q^{(2)},p^{(1)})\geq\ldots

Assume for contradiction that
\lim_{k\to\infty}D(q^{(k)},p^{(k)})\neq\inf_{q,p}D(q,p)
. Suppose there exists
p
and
\epsilon>0
such that:

D(q^{(k+1)},p^{(n)})>D(q^{*}(p),p)+\epsilon,\quad\text{for all }k.

Applying the earlier derived inequality to
q=q^{*}(p)
, we find that for all
k
:

\delta(q^{*}(p),q^{(k+1)})\leq\delta(q^{*}(p),q^{(k)})-\epsilon.

Since
\delta(q^{*}(p),q^{(1)})<\infty
, by assumption the Boundedness property, this recursion results in:

\delta(q^{*}(p),q^{(k+1)})<0\text{ for some }k,

contradicting the non-negativity of
\delta
. Therefore, our initial assumption must be false, and we conclude:

\lim_{k\to\infty}D(q^{(k)},p^{(k)})=\inf_{q,p}D(q,p).

∎

B.3
Continuous setting adaptation of Theorem 3.1,
Csiszár and Shields, 2004

In order to apply this theorem to prove
1

eqs.

8
,
9
and
10
) are true when
D(q,p)=D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q,p)
,
\delta(q,p)=D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q,p)\geq 0
, and
p,q
are densities.

To this end, we first state and prove
B.1
, which is a continuous setting adaptation of Theorem 3.1 of
Csiszár and Shields, 2004
. This establishes that — under one additional assumption on densities — the three point property,
eq.

8
, is satisfied for
\delta(q,p)=D(q,p)=D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q||p)
.

Lemma B.1

(Continuous setting adaptation of Theorem 3.1 of
Csiszár and Shields, 2004
)
.

Let
\mathcal{D}
and
\mathcal{F}
be two convex sets of densities with respect to a base measure
\mu
. For any
q,q^{\prime}\in\mathcal{D}
and
p\in\mathcal{F}
, assume
\mathbb{E}_{q}|\log(q^{\prime}/p)|<\infty
. Define
q^{*}(p):=\argmin_{q\in\mathcal{D}}D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q||p)
. Then, for any
q\in\mathcal{D}
and
p\in\mathcal{F}
:

D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q||p)\geq D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q||q^{*})+D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q^{*}||p)

Proof.

Let
q_{\omega}=(1-\omega)q^{*}+\omega q\in\mathcal{D}
for
\omega\in[0,1]
. By the mean value theorem,
\exists\tilde{\omega}\in(0,1)
such that:

0\leq\frac{1}{\omega}[D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q_{\omega}||p)-D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q^{*}||p)]=\frac{d}{d\omega}D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q_{\omega}||p)|_{\omega=\tilde{\omega}}

The lower bound is 0 as
q^{*}
minimizes KL divergence. We now examine the derivative:

\frac{d}{d\omega}D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q_{\omega}||p)=\frac{d}{d\omega}\int q_{\omega}\log\frac{q_{\omega}}{p}\text{d}\mu

q_{\omega}\log\frac{q_{\omega}}{p}
is integrable by our initial assumption. To move the derivative inside the integral, we need to show the partial derivative is integrable. The partial derivative is:

\frac{\partial}{\partial\omega}q_{\omega}\log\frac{q_{\omega}}{p}=(q-q^{*})(\log\frac{q_{\omega}}{p}+1)

We can bound this expression:

|(q-q^{*})(\log\frac{q_{\omega}}{p}+1)|\leq(q+q^{*})|\log\frac{q_{\omega}}{p}+1|

Now we show this bound is integrable:

\int(q+q^{*})|\log\frac{q_{\omega}}{p}+1|\text{d}\mu\leq\mathbb{E}_{q}|\log\frac{q_{\omega}}{p}|+\mathbb{E}_{q^{*}}|\log\frac{q_{\omega}}{p}|+2<\infty

This integrability allows us to apply the Dominated Convergence Theorem (DCT):

\frac{d}{d\omega}D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q_{\omega}||p)=\int(q-q^{*})\log\frac{q_{\omega}}{p}\text{d}\mu

We can now examine the limit as
\omega
approaches 0. Again applying DCT:

\lim_{\omega\to 0}\frac{d}{d\omega}D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q_{\omega}||p)=\int(q-q^{*})\log\frac{q^{*}}{p}\text{d}\mu\geq 0

The inequality holds because
q^{*}
minimizes KL divergence. Finally, we expand this expression:

\int(q-q^{*})\log\frac{q^{*}}{p}\text{d}\mu=D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q||p)-D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q||q^{*})-D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q^{*}||p)\geq 0

This final step proves the stated inequality, completing our proof.
∎

B.4
Continuous setting adaptation of Theorem 3.4,
Csiszár and Shields, 2004

Next we show
B.2
, which is a continuous setting version of Theorem 3.4 of
Csiszár and Shields, 2004
. This establishes that — under the same additional assumption on densities that we have for
B.1
— the four-point property,
eq.

9
, is satisfied for
\delta(q,p)=D(q,p)=D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q||p)
.

Lemma B.2

(modified version of theorem 3.4 of
Csiszár and Shields, 2004
)
.

Let
\mathcal{D}
and
\mathcal{F}
be two convex sets of densities with respect to some base measure
\mu
. Assume for all
q\in\mathcal{D}
and
p,p^{\prime}\in\mathcal{F}
, we have
\mathbb{E}_{q}[p/p^{\prime}]<\infty
. Then,
p^{*}\in\mathcal{F}
is the minimizer of
D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q||p)
over
p\in\mathcal{F}
if and only if for all
q^{\prime}\in\mathcal{D}
and
p^{\prime}\in\mathcal{F}
:

D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q^{\prime}||p^{\prime})+D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q^{\prime}||q)\geq D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q^{\prime}||p^{*})

Proof.

”If” part
: Take
q^{\prime}=q
. The inequality directly implies
p^{*}
is the minimizer.

”Only if” part:
We claim that

\int q(1-\frac{p^{\prime}}{p^{*}})\text{d}\mu\geq 0

.
If this holds, then
1-\int\frac{qp^{\prime}}{p^{*}}\text{d}\mu\geq 0
, and

\int q^{\prime}(1-\frac{qp^{\prime}}{q^{\prime}p^{*}})\text{d}\mu\geq 0

Using the inequality
\log(1/x)\geq 1-x
, we have:

D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q^{\prime}||p^{\prime})+D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q^{\prime}||q)-D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q^{\prime}||p^{*})=\int q^{\prime}\log\frac{q^{\prime}p^{*}}{qp^{\prime}}\text{d}\mu\geq\int q^{\prime}(1-\frac{qp^{\prime}}{q^{\prime}p^{*}})\text{d}\mu\geq 0

This proves the main result. Thus, it suffices to prove
\int q(1-\frac{p^{\prime}}{p^{*}})\text{d}\mu\geq 0
.

To prove this claim, set
p_{\omega}=(1-\omega)p^{*}+\omega p^{\prime}\in\mathcal{F}
for
\omega\in[0,1]
. Since
p^{*}
is a minimizer, by the mean value theorem,
\exists\tilde{\omega}\in(0,1)
such that:

0\leq\frac{1}{\omega}(D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q||p_{\omega})-D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q||p^{*}))=\frac{d}{d\omega}D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q||p_{\omega})|_{\omega=\tilde{\omega}}

To move the derivative inside the integral, we check if it’s absolutely integrable:

\frac{d}{d\omega}q\log\frac{q}{(1-\omega)p^{*}+\omega p^{\prime}}=q\frac{p^{*}-p^{\prime}}{(1-\omega)p^{*}+\omega p^{\prime}}

We show it has finite integral:

\int|q\frac{p^{*}-p^{\prime}}{(1-\omega)p^{*}+\omega p^{\prime}}|\text{d}\mu\leq\mathbb{E}_{q}\frac{p^{*}}{(1-\omega)p^{*}+\omega p^{\prime}}+\mathbb{E}_{q}\frac{p^{\prime}}{(1-\omega)p^{*}+\omega p^{\prime}}<\infty

This is finite by our assumption, as
(1-\omega)p^{*}+\omega p^{\prime}\in\mathcal{F}
.

Taking the limit as
\omega\to 0
:

0\leq\lim_{\omega\to 0}\frac{d}{d\omega}D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q||p_{\omega})=\lim_{\omega\to 0}\int q\frac{p^{*}-p^{\prime}}{(1-\omega)p^{*}+\omega p^{\prime}}\text{d}\mu

By the Dominated Convergence Theorem, we can move the limit inside the integral:

0\leq\int q\lim_{\omega\to 0}\frac{p^{*}-p^{\prime}}{(1-\omega)p^{*}+\omega p^{\prime}}\text{d}\mu=\int q(1-\frac{p^{\prime}}{p^{*}})\text{d}\mu

This completes the proof of our claim and thus the lemma.
∎

B.5
Expectation of density ratios

Next, we state and prove
B.3
. Informally, this lemma says that — in our specific SDE setting — the assumption on densities needed in
B.1
and
B.2
is satisfied. The Boundedness Property,
eq.

10
, in
B.1
is also a direct consequence of this lemma.

Lemma B.3

(Expectation of density ratio)
.

Consider three SDEs that share the same diffusion term
\sqrt{\gamma}\text{d}\bm{W}_{t}
and have different drift terms
{\bm{b}}_{p}(\cdot,\cdot)
,
{\bm{b}}_{q}(\cdot,\cdot)
, and
{\bm{b}}_{q^{\prime}}(\cdot,\cdot)
. Let
p
,
q
, and
q^{\prime}
be the corresponding densities over the Wiener measure.

The SDEs are defined as follows:

\displaystyle\text{For density }p:

\displaystyle\text{d}\bm{X}_{t}={\bm{b}}_{p}(\bm{X}_{t},t)\text{d}t+\sqrt{\gamma}\text{d}\bm{W}_{t},

\displaystyle\text{For density }q:

\displaystyle\text{d}\bm{X}_{t}={\bm{b}}_{q}(\bm{X}_{t},t)\text{d}t+\sqrt{\gamma}\text{d}\bm{W}_{t},

\displaystyle\text{For density }q^{\prime}:

\displaystyle\text{d}\bm{X}_{t}={\bm{b}}_{q^{\prime}}(\bm{X}_{t},t)\text{d}t+\sqrt{\gamma}\text{d}\bm{W}_{t}.

Assume that the time horizon is finite, i.e.,
t\in[0,T]
, and that all drift terms are bounded in the
L_{\infty}
norm. Specifically, there exists a constant
C
such that

\|{\bm{b}}_{i}(x,t)\|_{\infty}<C<\infty\quad\text{for }i\in\{p,q,q^{\prime}\}\text{ and for all }x,t.

Under these conditions, the density ratio between the processes is bounded. In particular, the following expectations are finite:

1.

The expected log density ratio:
\mathbb{E}_{q}\left[\left|\log\frac{q^{\prime}}{p}\right|\right]<\infty
. (Assumption in
B.1
)

2.

The expected density ratio:
\mathbb{E}_{p}\left[\frac{q}{q^{\prime}}\right]<\infty
. (Assumption in
B.2
)

3.

The KL divergence between q and p:
\mathbb{E}_{q}\left[\frac{q}{p}\right]<\infty
(Boundedness Property,
eq.

10
)

Proof.

We first show that the expected density ratio is finite. To this end, note first that since the diffusion terms
\sqrt{\gamma}\text{d}\bm{W}_{t}
are shared among the SDEs, the three SDEs define distributions over trajectories that admit densities over a Wiener measure. By direct application of Girsanov’s theorem
(
Kailath, 1971
, see e.g.,)
, the density ratio between the two measures on a sample path
\bm{X}_{t}
is given by:

\frac{q}{q^{\prime}}=\exp\left[\frac{1}{\gamma}\int_{0}^{T}({\bm{b}}_{q}(\bm{X}_{t},t)-{\bm{b}}_{q^{\prime}}(\bm{X}_{t},t))^{\top}\text{d}\bm{W}_{t}-\frac{1}{2\gamma}\int_{0}^{T}\|{\bm{b}}_{q}(\bm{X}_{t},t)-{\bm{b}}_{q^{\prime}}(\bm{X}_{t},t)\|_{2}^{2}\text{d}t\right].

Next, we consider the expectation of this density ratio when trajectories are from another distribution over trajectories defined by the SDE with density
p
. This expectation is:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{p}\left[\frac{q}{q^{\prime}}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{p}\left[\exp\left(\frac{1}{\gamma}\int_{0}^{T}({\bm{b}}_{q}(\bm{X}_{t},t)-{\bm{b}}_{q^{\prime}}(\bm{X}_{t},t))^{\top}\text{d}\bm{W}_{t}-\frac{1}{2\gamma}\int_{0}^{T}\|{\bm{b}}_{q}(\bm{X}_{t},t)-{\bm{b}}_{q^{\prime}}(\bm{X}_{t},t)\|_{2}^{2}\text{d}t\right)\right].

Using the assumption that the drift terms are bounded, i.e.,
\|{\bm{b}}_{i}(x,t)\|_{\infty}<C<\infty
for
i=p,q,q^{\prime}
, we get:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{p}\left[\frac{q}{q^{\prime}}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}_{p}\left[\exp\left(\frac{1}{\gamma}\int_{0}^{T}2C\bm{1}^{\top}\text{d}\bm{W}_{t}+\frac{1}{2\gamma}\int_{0}^{T}4dC^{2}\text{d}t\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\exp\left(\frac{2dC^{2}T}{\gamma}\right)\mathbb{E}_{p}\left[\exp\left(\frac{2C}{\gamma}\int_{0}^{T}\bm{1}^{\top}\text{d}\bm{W}_{t}\right)\right].

The last factor,
\exp\left(\frac{2C}{\gamma}\int_{0}^{T}\bm{1}^{\top}\text{d}\bm{W}_{t}\right)
, is a log-normal random variable, which has a finite expectation. Therefore:

\mathbb{E}_{p}\left[\frac{q}{q^{\prime}}\right]<\infty.

For the first statement regarding the expected log density ratio, we proceed in a similar manner. We need to show:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{q}\left[\left|\log\frac{q^{\prime}}{p}\right|\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{q}\left[\left|\int_{0}^{T}({\bm{b}}_{q^{\prime}}(\bm{X}_{t},t)-{\bm{b}}_{p}(\bm{X}_{t},t))^{\top}\text{d}\bm{W}_{t}-\frac{1}{2\gamma}\int_{0}^{T}\|{\bm{b}}_{q^{\prime}}(\bm{X}_{t},t)-{\bm{b}}_{p}(\bm{X}_{t},t)\|_{2}^{2}\text{d}t\right|\right]<\infty

Using the boundedness of the drift terms, we get:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{q}\left[\left|\log\frac{q^{\prime}}{p}\right|\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}_{q}\left[\left|\int_{0}^{T}\left|{\bm{b}}_{q^{\prime}}(\bm{X}_{t},t)-{\bm{b}}_{p}(\bm{X}_{t},t)\right|^{\top}\text{d}\bm{W}_{t}\right|+\frac{1}{2\gamma}\int_{0}^{T}4dC^{2}\text{d}t\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}_{q}\left[\left|\int_{0}^{T}2C\bm{1}^{\top}\text{d}\bm{W}_{t}\right|\right]+\frac{2dC^{2}T}{\gamma}.

The first term is finite since it is a Gaussian random variable, and the second term is finite by assumption that
C<\infty
. Thus:

\mathbb{E}_{q}\left[\left|\log\frac{q^{\prime}}{p}\right|\right]<\infty.

Finally, considering the case
q=q^{\prime}
, the KL divergence between
q
and
p
is finite under our assumption:

\mathbb{E}_{q}\left[\log\frac{q}{p}\right]\leq\mathbb{E}_{q}\left[\left|\log\frac{q}{p}\right|\right]<\infty.

This completes the proof.
∎

Proof of

B.1
in the specific case where
\delta(q,p)=D(q,p)=D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q||p)
, to restate and prove

.

See

Proof.

We show that this result follows directly from
B.1
by verifying all necessary conditions. Let
D(q,p)=D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q,p)
and
\delta(q,p)=D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q,p)\geq 0
, where
p
and
q
are densities.

To apply
B.1

B.1
hold true:

1.

Three-point property (

8
):
This property is established using
B.1
. The conditions for

are satisfied due to our assumption of bounded drift and

.

2.

Four-point property (

9
):
This property is established using
B.2
. The conditions for
B.2
are satisfied due to our assumption of bounded drift and

This assumption is directly established by

Given that all three required properties are satisfied, we can apply

Appendix D
Identifiability concerns

In settings like ours, marginal samples may not provide sufficient information to discern the underlying system. One case this might happen is when the system is at equilibrium to start with, e.g., our initial distribution is the invariant measure of the system (if it exists). However, this is not the only case and could happen even the system is not at equilibrium or even without invariant measures. For the former, consider a scenario where the drift is the sum of a rotationally symmetric gradient field and a constant-curl rotation, with the initial sample distribution also being rotationally symmetric. In this case, the marginal distribution would maintain rotational symmetry at each subsequent time step, irrespective of the constant-curl rotation’s angular velocity. This means that merely by observing marginal samples, we might fail to detect the rotation that controls angular velocity thus cannot have them in our inferred trajectories.

One particular case of a rotational invariant vector field is a simple vector field with constant curl with isotropic Gaussian initial distribution. Formally in the settings of
eq.

1
, consider a simple drift parameterized by
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
,
{\bm{b}}(\bm{x}):=[\alpha x_{2},-\alpha x_{1}]^{\top}
, a simple vector field with constant curl. When
\alpha=0
the dynamics is driven just by the Brownian motion. Suppose
\pi_{0}
is an isotropic normal,
\pi_{0}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\beta I_{2})
for some
\beta
(and
I_{2}
denoting the 2D identity matrix). For a given constant volatility
\gamma
, when
\alpha=0
, the distribution of the particles at any time step is always isotropic normal.

Indeed, if we consider the general form of the Fokker-Planck equation, this can be written as

\displaystyle\frac{\partial p(\bm{x},t)}{\partial t}

\displaystyle=-\nabla\cdot({\bm{b}}(\bm{x})p(\bm{x},t))+\gamma\nabla^{2}p(\bm{x},t)

\displaystyle=-p(\bm{x},t)(\nabla\cdot{\bm{b}}(\bm{x}))-\nabla p(\bm{x},t)\cdot{\bm{b}}(\bm{x})+\gamma\nabla^{2}p(\bm{x},t)

\displaystyle=-\nabla p(\bm{x},t)\cdot{\bm{b}}(\bm{x})+\gamma\nabla^{2}p(\bm{x},t)

If we adapt this to isotropic Gaussians, we have
\nabla p(\bm{x},t)\cdot{\bm{b}}(\bm{x})=0
, and therefore we only end up with the diffusion term for the Brownian motion, meaning that the distribution of the particles at any time step will always remain isotropic Gaussian with increasing variance. Schroödinger bridges between these Gaussians are in fact having a unique closed-form
(
Bunne et al., 2023
)
that cannot account for all possible rotations.

In general, the identifiability of the drift and trajectories is an interesting question for future research.
Lavenant et al., 2021
showed with marginals sampled arbitrarily dense in time and the underlying vector field is gradient the trajectories can be identified. However, this is one sufficient condition and requires samples dense in time. We believe that the identifiability literature of partial differential equations (especially Fokker-Planck equations) should be useful in this regard.
