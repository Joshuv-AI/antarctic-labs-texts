Low complexity convergence rate bounds for the synchronous gossip subclass of push-sum algorithms
Balázs Gerencsér, Miklós Kornyik

Abstract
We develop easily accessible quantities for bounding the almost sure exponential convergence rate of push-sum algorithms. We analyze the scenario of i.i.d. synchronous gossip, every agent communicating towards its single target at every step. Multiple bounding expressions are developed depending on the generality of the setup, all functions of the spectrum of the network. While the most general bound awaits further improvement, with more symmetries, close bounds can be established, as demonstrated by numerical simulations.

Average consensus algorithms have been around for a while

,

Often some symmetry is imposed on the communication, in terms of the matrix describing the linear update of the vector of values to be either doubly stochastic, or even symmetric. , later also

and joined by variants such as

[

.
The goal of these algorithms is the same,
but now using only local, directed communication and without requiring message passing to happen synchronously or consistently across the network. Given the simple objective of the algorithm, it also serves as a building block for more complex tasks, e.g., the spectral analysis of the network
[
12
]
or distributed optimization algorithms
[
13
]
.

With other real-life communication challenges taken into account, the concept has been extended in multiple ways to handle such aspects, including packet loss

]
, delay

or even the presence of malicious agents

In the meantime, there is work to better understand the effect of such communication deficiencies for the reference algorithms. The error of the consensus value compared to the true average for the push-sum algorithm has been analyzed in case of packet loss

An essential question in the analysis for usability and efficiency is understanding the asymptotics of the processes, their convergence and the rate at which it happens.
In the cases above, the convergence of the push-sum algorithm (or variants) has been confirmed. Additionally, for the original push-sum scheme, an exponential convergence has been proven
[
11
]
. However, at that time the focus was not yet on approximating the true rate.
An important step ahead was made in

More recently, the

]
.
As a follow-up, it was possible to combine the inspiration of
[

N

Our goal is to provide even simpler convergence rate estimates. For this purpose, we focus our attention to the natural setup, where a weighted network determines the communication scheme driving the consensus process. In particular, we assume synchronized gossip message passing, i.e. every node sending a single packet to a single (random) recipient at each time slot. Convergence of this scheme has been known since the formation of the push-sum concept
[
11
]
, ensuring that distributed average computation takes place.

The bounds provided can be computed directly once the standard spectral description of the network is available. We are to formulate multiple variants, both to provide general, but more conservative estimates, and also sharper ones for a more restricted setting with stronger symmetries.

Main results

Let us introduce the push-sum algorithm along with other concepts that will be used.
Given is a finite graph
G=(V,E)
with the vertex set
V=[N]:=\{1,2,\ldots,N\}
, having degree sequence
d_{1},\ldots,d_{N}
. There is an initial vector of values
x(0)\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
at the vertices to be averaged. The process is also using an auxiliary vector initialized at
w(0)=\mathbf{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
.

At each time step, a linear row-stochastic update - representing local communication - is performed to both vectors as

\displaystyle x(t)^{\top}

\displaystyle=x(t-1)^{\top}K(t),

\displaystyle w(t)^{\top}

\displaystyle=w(t-1)^{\top}K(t).

The average
\bar{x}:=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i}x_{i}(0)
is then locally estimated by
x_{i}(t)/w_{i}(t)
.
There is a wide generality of how
(K(t))_{t\geq 0}
can be chosen. In the current paper, we focus on the scenario of i.i.d.
K(t)
, when at each step, every vertex sends a single message to a randomly chosen neighbor with a constant proportion and all these choices independent from one another. Formally,
K(t)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}K=(1-q)I+q\sum_{i}e_{i}e_{\beta_{i}}^{T}
with some fixed
q\in[0,1]
and independent
\beta_{i}
. By setting
p_{ij}:=\mathbb{P}(\beta_{i}=j)
, we obtain an overall transition probability matrix
P
which by construction has to be compatible with the adjacency matrix of
G
.

For convenience, we introduce the notation
P_{q}=(1-q)I+qP
. It is easy to check that
\mathbb{E}K=P_{q}
. In case
P
has only real eigenvalues let
\lambda_{i}
denote its
i^{th}
largest eigenvalue and let
\lambda_{q,i}=(1-q)+q\lambda_{i}
denote that of
P_{q}
. Following our setup let us state our main results. Theorem
1
targets scenarios in more general settings, while Theorem
2
is designed for more symmetric cases.

Theorem 1
.

Let us consider a push-sum algorithm with message probability matrix
P
. Then

\limsup\frac{1}{t}\max_{i}\log\left|\frac{x_{i}(t)}{w_{i}(t)}-\bar{x}\right|\leq\frac{1}{2}\log\rho\bigg((I-J)(P_{q}^{\top}P_{q}+q^{2}(\Gamma-P^{\top}P))(I-J)\bigg)\quad a.s.

(1)

where
\Gamma
is a diagonal matrix with
\gamma_{ii}=\sum_{j}p_{ji}
and
\rho(\cdot)
denotes the spectral radius.
Furthermore, if
P
is symmetric, then

\limsup\frac{1}{t}\max_{i}\log\left|\frac{x_{i}(t)}{w_{i}(t)}-\bar{x}\right|\leq\frac{1}{2}\log((1-q)^{2}+2q(1-q)\lambda_{2}+q^{2})\quad a.s.

(2)

Remark 1
.

In case each vertex chooses a recipient uniformly among its neighbors, the bounding quantity in Theorem
1
will depend only on the graph structure, furthermore the diagonal matrix
\Gamma
takes the form
\Gamma_{ii}=\sum\limits_{j:(j,i)\in E}d_{j}^{-1}
with
d_{j}
denoting the degree of vertex
j
.

Proof.

It is easy to check that in this case
\mathbb{E}K=P_{q}
with
P=D^{-1}A
, where
D
denotes the diagonal matrix consisting of the degrees of the underlying graph’s vertices, while
A
denotes the graph adjacency matrix.

∎

A better bound can be obtained for cases with stronger symmetries. A message probability matrix is said to be transitive if for any pair
(i,j)
there exists a permutation matrix
\Pi
with
\Pi_{ij}=1
such that
\Pi P\Pi^{-1}=P
.

Theorem 2
.

Suppose that the message probability matrix
P
is symmetric and transitive. Then

\limsup\frac{1}{t}\max_{i}\log\left|\frac{x_{i}(t)}{w_{i}(t)}-\bar{x}\right|\leq\frac{1}{2}\log\xi_{1},

(3)

with
\xi_{1}
being the largest root of the polynomial

f(\xi)=\prod_{i>1}(\xi-\lambda_{q,i}^{2})-\frac{q^{2}}{N}\sum_{i>1}(1-\lambda_{i}^{2})\prod_{i\neq j>1}(\xi-\lambda_{q,i}^{2}).

Remark 2
.

If
G
is a transitive graph and each vertex chooses a recipient uniformly among its neighbors, then the corresponding message probability matrix satisfies the assumption of Theorem
2
.

A special case has been analyzed in

[
10
]
, where the underlying topology was given by the complete graph with
q=1/2
. For this topology and general
q
Theorem
2
immediately gives

\displaystyle f(\xi)=(\xi-(1-q)^{2})^{N-2}\big(\xi-(1-q)^{2}-q^{2}(1-N^{-1})\big).

from which the convergence rate bound
(1-q)^{2}+q^{2}(1-N^{-1})
can be easily obtained.

Tools

Let us first introduce a framework and corresponding tools in a general setting. The alignment to the assumptions of the theorems will be carried out later.

First we remark that the elements
w_{i}(t)
are all positive because the diagonal elements of the nonnegative
K(t)
are strictly positive.
Using the notations
H(t)=K(1)K(2)\cdots K(t)
,
J=\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^{\top}/N

easy calculation shows

\displaystyle x(t)^{\top}-\bar{x}w(t)^{\top}

\displaystyle=x_{0}^{\top}H(t)-\bar{x}w(t)^{\top}

\displaystyle=x_{0}^{\top}(JH(t)+(I-J)H(t))-\bar{x}w(t)^{\top}

\displaystyle=x_{0}^{\top}(I-J)H(t)

meaning that

\displaystyle\max_{i}\left|\frac{x_{i}(t)}{w_{i}(t)}-\bar{x}\right|

\displaystyle\leq C\{\min_{i}(w_{i}(t))\}^{-1}||x_{0}||_{2}||(I-J)H(t)||_{2}

\displaystyle\leq C\{\min_{i}(w_{i}(t))\}^{-1}||x_{0}||_{2}||(I-J)H(t)||_{F}.

(4)

from some constant
C>0
. We are interested in the almost sure convergence rate of the quantity on the left of (
4
). As we will see the dominant term will be
||(I-J)H(t)||_{F}
. To get a handle on this factor let us analyze
the expectation of
||(I-J)H(t)||_{F}^{2}
.

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}||(I-J)H(t)||_{F}^{2}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\ \mathrm{Tr}\{(I-J)H(t)H(t)^{\top}(I-J)\}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Tr}\{(I-J)\mathbb{E}[H(t)H(t)^{\top}](I-J)\}.

According to the definition of
H(t)
we can write

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[H(t)H(t)^{\top}]=\mathbb{E}\big[\mathbb{E}[K(1)\tilde{H}(t)\tilde{H}(t)^{\top}K(1)^{\top}\big|\tilde{H}(t)]\big]=\mathbb{E}[\mathbb{E}\big.[K(1)XK(1)^{\top}]\big|_{X=\tilde{H}(t)\tilde{H}(t)^{\top}}],

(5)

where
\tilde{H}(t)=K(2)K(3)\cdots K(t)
, thus by the i.i.d. nature of the updates
\tilde{H}(t)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}H(t-1)
.
This motivates the following definition of the linear operator
\Phi:\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}\to\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}
acting on matrices:

\Phi(X):=\mathbb{E}[KXK^{\top}],

which we will need to understand for further developing (
5
).
For satisfactory notation, before we progress let us introduce the linear operator
\Psi:\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}\to\mathbb{R}^{N}

(\Psi(X))_{i}=x_{ii}

and its pseudo-inverse
\Psi^{-}:\mathbb{R}^{N}\to\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}

(\Psi^{-}(v))_{ij}=\begin{cases}v_{i}&\mbox{ if }i=j,\\
0&\mbox{ otherwise.}\end{cases}

Following the pattern of (
5
) we can prove the following

Lemma 1
.

For any matrix
X
, we have

\displaystyle\Phi(X)=P_{q}XP_{q}^{\top}+q^{2}\big\{\Psi^{-}[P\ \Psi(X)]-\Psi^{-}\Psi(PXP^{\top})\big\}

reminding that
P_{q}=(1-q)I+qP
.

Proof.

Let
L:=\sum_{i}e_{i}e_{\beta_{i}}^{\top}

then

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[KXK^{\top}]

\displaystyle=(1-q)^{2}X+q(1-q)\mathbb{E}[XL^{\top}]+q(1-q)\mathbb{E}[LX]+q^{2}\mathbb{E}[LXL^{\top}]

\displaystyle=(1-q)^{2}X+q(1-q)XP^{\top}+q(1-q)PX+q^{2}\mathbb{E}[LXL^{\top}]

\displaystyle=P_{q}XP_{q}^{\top}-q^{2}PXP^{\top}+q^{2}\mathbb{E}[LXL^{\top}].

Next we will compute the term
\mathbb{E}[LXL^{\top}]
as

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[LXL^{\top}]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,i^{\prime}}\mathbb{E}[e_{i}e_{\beta_{i}}^{\top}Xe_{\beta_{i^{\prime}}}e_{i^{\prime}}^{\top}]=\sum_{i\neq i^{\prime}}\mathbb{E}x_{\beta_{i},\beta_{i^{\prime}}}e_{i}e_{i^{\prime}}^{\top}+\sum_{i}\mathbb{E}x_{\beta_{i},\beta_{i}}e_{i}e_{i}^{\top}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\neq i^{\prime}\\
j,j^{\prime}\end{subarray}}p_{ij}p_{i^{\prime}j^{\prime}}x_{jj^{\prime}}e_{i}e_{i^{\prime}}^{\top}+\sum_{i}p_{ij}x_{jj}e_{i}e_{i}^{\top}

\displaystyle=PXP^{\top}-\sum_{i,j,j^{\prime}}p_{ij}p_{ij^{\prime}}x_{jj^{\prime}}e_{i}e_{i}^{\top}+\sum_{i,j}p_{ij}x_{jj}e_{i}e_{i}^{\top}

\displaystyle=PXP^{\top}-\Psi^{-}\Psi(PXP^{\top})+\Psi^{-}(P\ \Psi(X)).

Thus putting together the two parts gives

\Phi(X)=P_{q}XP_{q}^{\top}+q^{2}\{\Psi^{-}(P\Psi(X))-\Psi^{-}\Psi(PXP^{\top})\}

and this concludes the proof.
∎

In order to obtain a bound on
\mathrm{Tr}\hskip 2.27621pt\{(I-J)\mathbb{E}[H(t)H(t)^{\top}](I-J)\}
it is enough to understand
\Phi
, since

\mathrm{Tr}\hskip 2.27621pt\{(I-J)\mathbb{E}[H(t)H(t)^{\top}](I-J)\}=\mathrm{Tr}\hskip 2.27621pt\{(I-J)\Phi^{t}(I)(I-J)\},

\Phi^{t}(I)

\underbrace{\Phi\circ\Phi\circ\cdots\circ\Phi}_{t\ times}(I)

Proposition 1
.

The map
\Phi
has the following fundamental properties:

(P1)

\Phi
is linear,

(P2)

\Phi(X^{\top})=\Phi(X)^{\top}
,

(P3)

for any skew-symmetric matrix
X

\Phi(X)=P_{q}XP_{q}^{\top},

(P4)

if
X\geq 0
then
\Phi(X)\geq 0
, i.e.
\Phi
keeps the positive semi-definite property,

(P5)

if
x_{kl}\geq 0

\forall(k,l)
, then
\Phi(X)_{kl}\geq 0

\forall(k,l)
,

(P6)

J
is an eigenmatrix of
\Phi
with eigenvalue
1
, i.e.
\Phi(J)=J
,

(P7)

for
X\geq 0
, and
P=P^{\top}

we have
\mathrm{Tr}\hskip 2.27621pt\Phi(X)\leq\mathrm{Tr}\hskip 2.27621ptX.

For the adjoint map
\Phi^{*}
the following observations can be added:

(P*1)

\Phi^{*}(Y)=P_{q}^{\top}YP_{q}+q^{2}\{\Psi^{-}[P^{\top}\Psi(Y)]-P^{\top}(\Psi^{-}\Psi Y)P\}
,

(P*2)

if
X\geq 0

\Phi^{*}(X)\geq 0
, i.e.
\Phi^{*}
also keeps the positive semi-definite property,

(P*3)

if
x_{kl}\geq 0

, then
\Phi^{*}(X)\geq 0

\forall(k,l)
,

(P*4)

if

\Phi^{*}(X)\mathbf{1}=0
.

Proof.

The first three properties follow directly from the definition of
\Phi
, hence their proofs are left to the respected reader.

Property (P4) can be proved as follows. Let
X\geq 0
and let
w
be an arbitrary vector, then

\displaystyle w^{\top}\Phi(X)w

\displaystyle=w^{\top}\mathbb{E}[KXK^{\top}]w=\mathbb{E}[w^{\top}KXK^{\top}w]\geq 0

(P5) is analogous to (P4), namely

\displaystyle\Phi(X)_{kl}=(\mathbb{E}[KXK^{\top}])_{kl}\geq 0.

Property (P6) is the result of a short series of calculations.

\displaystyle\Phi(J)

\displaystyle=PJP^{\top}+q^{2}(\Psi^{-}P\Psi J-\Psi^{-}\Psi(PJP^{\top}))

\displaystyle=J+q^{2}(1/N\cdot I-1/N\cdot I)=J,

due to the facts
PJ=JP^{\top}=J
and
\Psi J=\mathbf{1}/N
.

Before proving (P7) let us note that due to
X\geq 0
and the linearity of
\Phi
it is enough to prove this property for
X=xx^{\top}
. Using the definition of
K=(1-q)I+q^{2}\sum_{i}e_{i}e_{\beta_{i}}^{\top}
we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{Tr}\hskip 2.27621pt\Phi(xx^{\top})

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Tr}\hskip 2.27621pt\mathbb{E}[Kxx^{\top}K^{\top}\textasciibreve]=

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Tr}\hskip 2.27621pt\mathbb{E}\{(1-q)^{2}xx^{\top}+q(1-q)(Lxx^{\top}+xx^{\top}L^{\top})+q^{2}Lxx^{\top}L^{\top}\}

\displaystyle=(1-q)^{2}||x||_{2}^{2}+2q(1-q)x^{\top}Px+q^{2}\mathbb{E}||Lx||_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=(1-q)^{2}||x||_{2}^{2}+2q(1-q)x^{\top}Px+q^{2}\sum_{i,j}p_{ij}x_{j}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq||x||^{2}_{2}

where in the last step we used the facts
P=P^{\top}
,
P\mathbf{1}=\mathbf{1}
and
x^{\top}Px\leq\lambda_{1}(P)||x||_{2}^{2}=||x||_{2}^{2}
.
Now we proceed with proving the properties of the adjoint.

The proof of (P*1) is based on the following series of calculations:
due to the equivalences

\displaystyle\mathrm{Tr}\hskip 2.27621pt(\Psi^{-}(P\ \Psi X))Y^{\top}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,k}p_{ik}x_{kk}y_{ii}=\sum_{k}x_{kk}\sum_{i}p_{ik}y_{ii}=\mathrm{Tr}\hskip 2.27621pt\{X\Psi^{-}(P^{\top}Y^{\top})\},

\displaystyle\mathrm{Tr}\hskip 2.27621pt\Psi^{-}\Psi(PXP^{\top})Y^{\top}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,k,l}p_{ik}x_{kl}p_{il}y_{ii}=\sum_{k,l}x_{kl}\sum_{i}p_{ik}y_{ii}p_{il}=\mathrm{Tr}\hskip 2.27621pt\{XP^{\top}\Psi(Y^{\top})P\}

we have

\displaystyle\langle\Phi(X),Y\rangle

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Tr}\hskip 2.27621pt\Phi(X)Y^{\top}=\mathrm{Tr}\hskip 2.27621ptP_{q}XP_{q}^{\top}Y^{\top}+q^{2}\mathrm{Tr}\hskip 2.27621pt\bigg\{[\Psi^{-}(P\Psi(X))]Y^{\top}-[\Psi^{-}\Psi(PXP^{\top})]Y^{\top}\bigg\}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Tr}\hskip 2.27621pt(XP_{q}^{\top}Y^{\top}P_{q})+q^{2}\mathrm{Tr}\hskip 2.27621pt\{X\Psi^{-}(P^{\top}\Psi Y^{\top})-XP^{\top}\Psi(Y^{\top})P\}

\displaystyle=\langle X,\Phi^{*}(Y)\rangle.

Properties (P*2) and (P*3) can be confirmed analogously to (P4), (P5).

(P*4) is a result of the short derivation

\displaystyle\Phi^{*}(X)\mathbf{1}

\displaystyle=P_{q}^{\top}XP_{q}\mathbf{1}+q^{2}(\Psi^{-}P\Psi X\mathbf{1}-P^{\top}(\Psi^{-}\Psi X)P\mathbf{1})

\displaystyle=0+q^{2}(\Psi^{-}(P^{\top}\Psi(X))\mathbf{1}-P^{\top}(\Psi^{-}\Psi X)\mathbf{1})

since
P_{q}\mathbf{1}=P\mathbf{1}=\mathbf{1}
and we assumed

\displaystyle(\Psi^{-}(P^{\top}\Psi(X))\mathbf{1})_{i}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j}p_{ji}x_{jj}

\displaystyle(P^{\top}(\Psi^{-}\Psi X)\mathbf{1})_{i}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j}p_{ji}x_{jj},

so
\Phi^{*}(X)\mathbf{1}=0
.
This concludes the proof.

Proofs

The next proposition is the final step before proving Theorem
1
.

Proposition 2
.

Let
P
be a row stochastic matrix. Then

\mathrm{Tr}\hskip 2.27621pt(\Phi^{*})^{t}(I-J)\leq N\rho\big((I-J)B_{q}(I-J)\big)^{t}

where
B_{q}=P_{q}^{\top}P_{q}+q^{2}(\Gamma-P^{\top}P)
is a positive definite matrix, and
D
is a diagonal matrix with
d_{ii}=\sum_{j}p_{ji}
on its diagonal.

Proof.

Due to its properties
\Phi^{*}:\mathcal{X}_{0}\to\mathcal{X}_{0}=\{X\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}:X=X^{\top},XJ=0\},
and

X\geq 0\implies\Phi^{*}(X)\geq 0
, we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{Tr}\hskip 2.27621pt(\Phi^{*})^{t}(I-J)

\displaystyle\leq N\rho((\Phi^{*})^{t}(I-J))=N\max\{v^{\top}(\Phi^{*})^{t}(I-J)v:\|v\|=1,v\perp\mathbf{1}\}

\displaystyle=N\|(\Phi^{*})^{t}(I-J)\|_{2}\leq N\|(\Phi^{*})^{t}\|_{\mathcal{X}_{0}\to\mathcal{X}_{0}}\|(I-J)\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq N\|\Phi^{*}\|^{t}_{\mathcal{X}_{0}\to\mathcal{X}_{0}}

where

\|\Phi^{*}\|_{\mathcal{X}_{0}\to\mathcal{X}_{0}}=\max\{\|\Phi^{*}(X)\|_{2}:\|X\|_{2}\leq 1,X\in\mathcal{X}_{0}\}.

It is not hard to show that
\arg\max\|\Phi^{*}(X)\|_{2}\geq 0
, since let
X=\arg\max\|\Phi^{*}(X)\|_{2}
and let us consider its decomposition
X=X^{*}-X^{-}
with
X^{+},X^{-}\geq 0
. Then

v^{\top}\Phi^{*}(X)v=v^{\top}\Phi^{*}(X^{+})v-v^{\top}\Phi^{*}(X^{-})v\leq v^{\top}\Phi^{*}(X^{+})v+v^{\top}\Phi^{*}(X^{-})v,

and this would lead to a contradiction if
X^{-}
was not
0
. This implies

\|\Phi^{*}\|_{\mathcal{X}_{0}\to\mathcal{X}_{0}}=\max\{v^{\top}\Phi^{*}(X)v:X\in\mathcal{X}_{0},X\geq 0,\|X\|_{2}\leq 1,\|v\|\leq 1,v\perp\mathbf{1}\}.

meaning that it is enough to bound
v^{\top}\Phi^{*}(X)v
from above. Let
v\perp\mathbf{1}
and
X\in\mathcal{X}_{0}
with
\|X\|_{2}\leq 1
, then

\displaystyle v^{\top}\Phi^{*}(X)v

\displaystyle=(P_{q}v)^{\top}XP_{q}v+q^{2}(\sum_{i,j}p_{ij}x_{ii}v_{j}^{2}-\sum_{i}(Pv)_{i}^{2}x_{ii}).

Due to the conditions imposed on
X
, we have
x_{ii}\in[0,1]
, furthermore
\sum_{j}p_{ij}-(Pv)_{i}^{2}\geq 0
for any
i
, thus

\displaystyle v^{\top}\Phi^{*}(X)v

\displaystyle\leq\|P_{q}v\|^{2}+q^{2}(\sum_{i,j}p_{ij}v_{j}^{2}-\sum_{i}(Pv)_{i}^{2})

\displaystyle=v^{\top}\big(P_{q}^{\top}P_{q}+q^{2}(\Gamma-P^{\top}P)\big)v\leq\rho\bigg((I-J)(P^{\top}_{q}P_{q}+q^{2}(\Gamma-P^{\top}P)(I-J)\bigg)

where
\Gamma
is a diagonal matrix with diagonal elements
\gamma_{ii}=\sum_{j}p_{ji}
. Note that for symmetric
P
we have
\Gamma=I
and so the upper bound above becomes
(1-q)^{2}+2q(1-q)\lambda_{2}+q^{2}.

∎

With all the tools at our hands we can prove Theorem
1
.

Proof of Theorem
1
.

According to Lemma 10 in
[
4
]
whose assumptions are clearly satisfied we have

\limsup_{t}\frac{1}{t}\log\frac{1}{\min_{i}w_{i}(t)}\leq 0

thus considering the quantity in (
4
) we can infer that

\limsup_{t}\frac{1}{t}\log\big(\{\min_{i}w_{i}(t)\}^{-1}||x_{0}||\cdot||(I-J)H(t)||_{F}\big)\leq\limsup_{t}\frac{1}{t}\log||(I-J)H(t)||_{F}.

The matrix
(I-J)H(t)
can we written as a product of the i.i.d. random matrices
(I-J)K(t)
, therefore due to the Fürstenberg-Kesten theorem it follows that

\displaystyle\limsup_{t}\frac{1}{t}\log||(I-J)H(t)||_{F}

\displaystyle=\lim_{t}\frac{1}{t}\log||(I-J)H(t)||_{F}=\lim_{t}\frac{1}{t}\mathbb{E}\log||(I-J)H(t)||_{F}

\displaystyle=\lim_{t}\frac{1}{2t}\mathbb{E}\log\|(I-J)H(t)\|_{F}^{2}.

Using Jensen’s inequality yields

\mathbb{E}\log||(I-J)H(t)||^{2}_{F}\leq\log\mathbb{E}||(I-J)H(t)||_{F}^{2}

thus

\limsup_{t}\frac{1}{t}\log\|(I-J)H(t)\|_{F}\leq\lim_{t}\frac{1}{2t}\log\mathbb{E}\|(I-J)H(t)\|_{F}^{2}.

By taking the expectation we obtain

\mathbb{E}||(I-J)H(t)||_{F}^{2}=\mathrm{Tr}\hskip 2.27621pt(\Phi^{*})^{t}(I-J).

Combining the series of calculations above with Proposition
2
we arrive at

\limsup_{t}\frac{1}{2t}\log\mathrm{Tr}\hskip 2.27621pt(\Phi^{*})^{t}(I-J)\leq\frac{1}{2}\begin{cases}\rho\bigg((I-J)(P_{q}^{\top}P_{q}+q^{2}(\Gamma-P^{\top}P))(I-J)\bigg)&\mbox{in general,}\\
(1-q)^{2}+2q(1-q)\lambda_{2}+q^{2}&\mbox{ if }P=P^{\top}.\end{cases}

∎

Now we will turn to the case when the underlying graph is transitive. In this scenario it is possible to give stronger bounds, but in order to do this we need to reformulate the problem. Rearranging our main quantity of interest as

\mathbb{E}||(I-J)H(t)||_{F}^{2}=\mathrm{Tr}\hskip 2.27621pt\mathbb{E}[H(t)^{\top}(I-J)H(t)],

therefore

\mathbb{E}||(I-J)H(t)||_{F}^{2}=\mathrm{Tr}\hskip 2.27621pt\{(\Phi^{*})^{t}(I-J)\}.

Let us define
X_{t}=(\Phi^{*})^{t}(I-J).
The following two lemmas will help us in our progress.

Lemma 2
.

Assume that
P
is a kernel of the transitive Markov chain, implying that it is symmetric and any diagonal element
p^{(k)}_{ii}
of
P^{k}
depends solely on
k
and not on
i
. Then we have

1.

X_{t}\in\mathcal{P}_{t}:=\mathrm{Span}\{P^{k},J\ ;\ 0\leq k\leq 2t\}
, hence the diagonal of
X_{t}
is also constant,

2.

X_{t+1}=P_{q}X_{t}P_{q}+q^{2}r_{t}(I-P^{2})
,
where
r_{t}
denotes the common diagonal element of
X_{t}
.

Proof.

We prove by induction. For
t=0

X_{0}=I-J
, which trivially is a polynomial of
P
and
J
.
For the induction step
t\to t+1
assume that
X_{t}\in\mathcal{P}_{t}
, then

\displaystyle X_{t+1}

\displaystyle=\Phi^{*}(X_{t})=P_{q}X_{t}P_{q}+q^{2}\{(\Psi^{-}P\Psi X_{t})-P(\Psi^{-}\Psi X_{t})P\}

since
X_{t}
is a polynomial of
P
and
J
it is also transitive. Noting
\Psi X_{t}=r_{t}\mathbf{1}
and
\Psi^{-}\Psi X_{t}=r_{t}I
, where
r_{t}
denotes the common diagonal element of
X_{t}
, we can derive the recursion

\displaystyle X_{t+1}=P_{q}X_{t}P_{q}+q^{2}r_{t}(I-P^{2})

(7)

showing that
X_{t+1}\in\mathcal{P}_{t+1}
.
∎

Proposition 3
.

If
P
is symmetric and transitive then
X_{t}
and
P
possess the same eigenvectors. If
v
is an eigenvector to
P
and
X_{t}
corresponding to the eigenvalue
\lambda
and
\mu_{t}
respectively then the following recursion holds for
\mu_{t}
:

\mu_{t+1}=\lambda_{q}^{2}\mu_{t}+q^{2}r_{t}(1-\lambda^{2}).

(8)

Recall that
r_{t}
is the common diagonal element of
X_{t}
.
Furthermore the largest eigenvalue of
X_{t}
is asymptotically bounded by the second largest root
\xi_{2}
of the polynomial

p(x)=\prod_{i}(x-\lambda_{q,i}^{2})\bigg(1+\frac{q^{2}}{N}\sum_{j>1}\frac{1-\lambda_{j}^{2}}{x-\lambda_{q,j}^{2}}\bigg)

in the following sense:

\limsup_{t}\frac{1}{t}\log\max_{i}\mu_{t,i}\leq\log\xi_{2}.

Proof.

Using
r_{t}=\frac{1}{N}\mathrm{Tr}\hskip 2.27621ptX_{t}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i}\mu_{t,i}
we can write the recursion described in (
8
) as

\boldsymbol{y}_{t+1}=\bigg(D\mathbf{+}\frac{q^{2}}{N}\mathbf{b}\mathbf{1}^{\top}\bigg)\boldsymbol{y}_{t}

(9)

with the vectors
(\boldsymbol{y}_{t})_{i}=\mu_{t,i}
,
\mathbf{b}_{i}=1-\lambda_{i}^{2}
, and
D=\mathrm{diag}\hskip 2.84526pt(\lambda_{q,1}^{2},\ldots,\lambda_{q,N}^{2})
, where
\lambda_{q,i}
denotes the
i^{th}
largest eigenvalue of
P_{q}
and
\mu_{t,i}
denotes the eigenvalue of
X_{t}
corresponding to
\lambda_{q,i}
. By correspondence we mean in the sense of defined by the recursion (
8
), in which case
\mu_{t,1}=0
, since
\lambda_{q,1}=1
and
X_{t}\mathbf{1}=0
for any
t
.
Let
(\lambda,v)
denote an eigen-pair of
P
. According to the previous lemma
X_{t}
is a polynomial of
P
and
J
, hence
v
is an eigenvector of
X_{t}
, furthermore, due to the symmetry of
P
, we have
JP=PJ=J
. Recursion (
7
) then yields

\displaystyle X_{t+1}v

\displaystyle=P_{q}X_{t}P_{q}v+q^{2}r_{t}(I-P^{2})v

\displaystyle=\lambda_{q}^{2}\mu_{t}v+q^{2}r_{t}(1-\lambda^{2})v

and we have

\mu_{t+1}=\lambda_{q}^{2}\mu_{t}+q^{2}r_{t}(1-\lambda^{2}),

proving the first part.

Before continuing with the proof of the second part, let us note that
e_{1}
is a left-eigenvector of the matrix
D+q^{2}\mathbf{b}\mathbf{1}^{\top}/N
corresponding to the eigenvalue 1, meaning that the right-eigenvectors corresponding to a different eigenvalue are orthogonal to
e_{1}
. Now let us choose the following vectors as basis:
f_{1}=e_{1}
,
f_{i}=e_{i}-e_{1},i>1
, then for
i>1
we have
f_{i}\perp\mathbf{1}
.
Writing equation (
9
) in basis
\{f_{i}\}
yields

\displaystyle Df_{1}

\displaystyle=De_{1}=\lambda_{q,1}^{2}e_{1},

\displaystyle Df_{i}

\displaystyle=De_{i}-De_{1}=\lambda_{q,i}^{2}e_{i}-\lambda_{q,1}^{2}e_{1}=

\displaystyle=\lambda_{q,1}^{2}f_{i}+(\lambda_{q,i}^{2}-\lambda_{q,1}^{2})f_{1},\quad i>1,

\displaystyle\mathbf{b}\mathbf{1}^{\top}f_{1}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{b}=b_{1}f_{1}+\sum_{i>1}b_{i}(f_{i}+f_{1}),

\displaystyle\mathbf{b}\mathbf{1}^{\top}f_{i}

\displaystyle=0\quad i>1,

thus rewriting the matrix
D+q^{2}\mathbf{b}\mathbf{1}^{\top}/N
in this new basis gives us

\begin{bmatrix}(q^{2}/N)\sum_{i}b_{i}+\lambda_{q,1}^{2}&-(\lambda_{q,1}^{2}-\lambda_{q,2}^{2})&-(\lambda_{q,1}^{2}-\lambda_{q,3}^{2})&\ldots&-(\lambda_{q,1}^{2}-\lambda_{q,N}^{2})\\
(q^{2}/N)b_{2}&\lambda_{q,2}^{2}&0&\ldots&0\\
(q^{2}/N)b_{3}&0&\lambda_{q,3}^{2}&\ldots&0\\
\vdots&&&\ddots&\\
(q^{2}/N)b_{N}&0&\ldots&0&\lambda_{q,N}^{2}\end{bmatrix}

(10)

The characteristic polynomial of the matrix in (
10
) can be computed via expanding along the first column, leading to

\displaystyle p(x)

\displaystyle=\bigg(x-\frac{q^{2}}{N}\sum_{i}b_{i}-\lambda_{q,1}^{2}\bigg)\prod_{i>1}(x-\lambda_{q,i}^{2})+\sum_{i>1}\frac{q^{2}}{N}b_{i}(\lambda_{q,1}^{2}-\lambda_{q,i}^{2})\prod_{1<j\neq i}(x-\lambda_{q,j}^{2})

\displaystyle=\prod_{i>1}(x-\lambda_{q,i}^{2})\bigg\{x-\lambda_{q,1}^{2}-\frac{q^{2}}{N}\sum_{i}(1-\lambda_{i}^{2})+\frac{q^{2}}{N}\sum_{j>1}(1-\lambda_{j}^{2})\frac{(1-\lambda_{q,j}^{2})}{x-\lambda_{q,j}^{2}}\bigg\}.

Exploiting the fact
\lambda_{1}=\lambda_{q,1}=1
we obtain

\displaystyle p(x)

\displaystyle=\prod_{i>1}(x-\lambda_{q,i}^{2})\bigg\{x-1+\frac{q^{2}}{N}\sum_{j>1}\bigg[(1-\lambda_{j}^{2})\bigg(\frac{1-\lambda_{q,j}^{2}}{x-\lambda_{q,j}^{2}}-1\bigg)\bigg]\bigg\}

\displaystyle=\prod_{i>1}(x-\lambda_{q,i}^{2})\bigg\{x-1+\frac{q^{2}}{N}\sum_{j>1}(1-\lambda_{j}^{2})\frac{1-x}{x-\lambda_{q,j}^{2}}\bigg\}

\displaystyle=\prod_{i}(x-\lambda_{q,i}^{2})\bigg(1-\frac{q^{2}}{N}\sum_{j>1}\frac{1-\lambda_{j}^{2}}{x-\lambda_{q,j}^{2}}\bigg).

We note here that due the initialization
\mu_{0}=(0,1,\ldots,1)
and the fact that
e_{1}
is a left-eigenvector of the recursion, we have for each subsequent vector
\mu_{t}\perp e_{1}
. This means that we are only interested in the second largest root
\eta_{2}
of the characteristic polynomial to obtain the asymptotic growth rate of
\mu_{t}
, i.e.

||\mu_{t}||_{\infty}\leq C\eta_{2}^{t}\ ||\mu_{0}||_{\infty}

for some
C>0
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
2
.

The first part of the proof, up until the point where we have to bound the quantity
\mathbb{E}||(I-J)H(t)||_{F}^{2}
from above, is analogous to the proof of Theorem
1
, hence it will be omitted here. Before proceeding with the actual proof we note that

||(I-J)H(t)||_{F}^{2}=\mathrm{Tr}\hskip 2.27621pt(I-J)H(t)H(t)^{\top}(I-J)=\mathrm{Tr}\hskip 2.27621ptH(t)^{\top}(I-J)H(t),

whence

\mathbb{E}||(I-J)H(t)||_{F}^{2}=\mathrm{Tr}\hskip 2.27621pt\{(\Phi^{*})^{t}(I-J)\}=\sum_{j}\mu_{t,j}\leq N\max_{j}\mu_{t,j},

where
\mu_{t,j}
denotes the eigenvalue of
X_{t}=(\Phi^{*})^{t}(I-J)
corresponding to the
j^{th}
largest eigenvalue
\lambda_{j}
of
P
. Due to
X_{0}=I-J
and
\lambda_{1}=1
with
v_{1}=c\mathbf{1}
, we have
\mu_{0,1}=0
and due to the recursion (
8
),
\mu_{t,1}=0
for any
t>0
. In Proposition
3
it was shown that

||\mu_{t}||_{\infty}\leq C\eta_{2}^{t}||\mu_{0}||=C\eta_{2}^{t},

where
\eta_{2}
denoted the second largest root of the polynomial

\displaystyle p(x)=\prod_{i}(x-\lambda_{q,i}^{2})-\frac{q^{2}}{N}\sum_{i>1}(1-\lambda_{i}^{2})\prod_{i\neq j>1}(x-\lambda_{q,i}^{2}).

Exploiting the fact that
1
is a root of
p
we have

\displaystyle p_{1}(x):=p(x)/(x-1)=\prod_{i>1}(x-\lambda_{q,i}^{2})-\frac{q^{2}}{N}\sum_{i>1}(1-\lambda_{i}^{2})\prod_{i\neq j>1}(x-\lambda_{q,i}^{2})

thus by denoting
\xi_{1}
the largest root of
p_{1}(x)
we can write
||\mu_{t}||_{\infty}\leq C\xi_{1}^{t}
. Altogether

\limsup_{t}\frac{1}{2t}\log\mathbb{E}||(I-J)H(t)||_{F}^{2}\leq\frac{1}{2}\log\xi_{1},

and this concludes the proof.
∎

Summary and future plans

In this work we have presented bounds of various accuracy depending on the level of symmetry of the underlying topology.
Along the way proving our main results we have developed a framework as described in Section
3
relying on matrix operators
\Phi,\Phi^{*}
that we hope can be useful when analyzing similar dynamics.

The computational cost of these bounds is orders of magnitude less than that of the simulations or computation of
\eta
from
[
4
]
confirming their usefulness in assessing the efficiency of various push-sum algorithms.

Concerning our future plans, we observed in some of our numerical experiments that the expression in (
3
) was also a valid bound for regular graphs. This lead us to state the following

Conjecture 1
.

The bound given in
2
remains valid in the simple symmetric case, e.g. for regular graphs without transitivity.
