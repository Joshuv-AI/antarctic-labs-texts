The Assouad dimension of Kakeya sets in ℝ 3 \mathbb{R}^{3}
Hong Wang, Joshua Zahl

Abstract
This paper studies the structure of Kakeya sets in ℝ 3 \mathbb{R}^{3} . We show that for every Kakeya set K ⊂ ℝ 3 K\subset\mathbb{R}^{3} , there exist well-separated scales 0 < δ < ρ ≤ 1 0<\delta<\rho\leq 1 so that the δ \delta neighborhood of K K is almost as large as the ρ \rho neighborhood of K K . As a consequence, every Kakeya set in ℝ 3 \mathbb{R}^{3} has Assouad dimension 3 and every Ahlfors-David regular Kakeya set in ℝ 3 \mathbb{R}^{3} has Hausdorff dimension 3. We also show that every Kakeya set in ℝ 3 \mathbb{R}^{3} that has “stably equal” Hausdorff and packing dimension (this is a new notion, which is introduced to avoid certain obvious obstructions) must have Hausdorff dimension 3. The above results follow from certain multi-scale structure theorems for arrangements of tubes and rectangular prisms in three dimensions, and a generalization of the sticky Kakeya theorem previously proved by the authors.

1.1
Main ideas, and a sketch of the proof

In
[
16
]
, the authors proved that every sticky Kakeya set in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
has Hausdorff and Minkowski dimension 3. In brief, a
Sticky Kakeya Set
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a Kakeya set that, when discretized at scale
\delta
, gives rise to a collection of
\delta
-tubes with the following two properties:

(a)

The tubes point in
\delta
-separated directions.

(b)

For each
\delta\leq\rho\leq 1
, the tubes can be covered by a set of
\rho
-tubes, with few (i.e. about 1)
\rho
-tubes pointing in each direction.

Sticky Kakeya sets have two useful properties. First, for
\delta\leq\rho\leq 1
, the
\rho
-neighborhood of a sticky Kakeya set at scale
\delta
is a sticky Kakeya set at scale
\rho
. In other words, if we thicken a sticky Kakeya set, we obtain a sticky Kakeya set at a coarser scale. Second, for
\delta\leq\rho\leq 1
, the
\rho
-neighborhood of a typical tube in our sticky Kakeya set will contain about
(\rho/\delta)^{1-n}

\delta
-tubes; if we rescale this
\rho
-tube to be comparable to the unit ball, then we obtain a sticky Kakeya set at scale
\delta/\rho
. In other words, if we zoom-in and rescale a sticky Kakeya set, we obtain a sticky Kakeya set at a coarser scale.

The Sticky Kakeya Theorem (or more precisely, a generalization that we will prove in Section
6
) will play an important role in the proof of Theorem
1.5
. In Section
4
we show that every set of
\delta
-tubes satisfying the hypotheses of Theorem
1.5
must either satisfy a suitable analogue of stickiness (and hence we can apply our generalization of the Sticky Kakeya Theorem and obtain the conclusion of Theorem
1.5
), or else at least one of the following three things must happen:

(i)

After thickening the tubes at a suitable scale, we obtain a richer collection of tubes, i.e. a collection of tubes that has similar structural properties, but larger cardinality (relative to the new scale).

(ii)

After zooming-in and rescaling at a suitably chosen location and scale, we obtain a richer collection of tubes.

(iii)

After possibly coarsening and zooming-in, the tubes arrange themselves into “planks” (like planks of wood, these sets are long (i.e. length 1) in one direction, much shorter in a second direction, and shorter still in the final direction).

In Section
4
we show that if Item (iii) occurs, then the corresponding collection of tubes satisfies the conclusion of Theorem
1.5
. This observation is the main new geometric input in the proof of Theorem
1.5
. In brief, the argument is as follows. Suppose each plank has dimensions roughly
s\times t\times 1
, with
\delta\leq s<\!\!<t\leq 1
. This means that each plank has an associated plane, which is the span of the two vectors pointing in the longest and second-longest directions. First we consider the case in which a typical pair of intersecting planks have corresponding planes that make angle
\theta>\!\!>s/t
. If this occurs, then the union of the
s
-neighborhood of these planks will contain a ball of radius
r=\theta t.
This is much larger than
s
, and hence (
1.1
) holds. Second, we consider the case in which a typical pair of intersecting planks have corresponding planes that make angle
\leq s/t
. This forces the planks to cluster into bigger planks dimensions
s^{\prime}\times t^{\prime}\times 1
, where
s^{\prime}\ll t^{\prime}\leq 1
are substantially larger than
s
and
t
, respectively. We can then repeat this argument at a larger scale. After finitely many iterations of this argument, we must find ourselves in the First Case. This concludes the argument.

Summarizing the arguments above, if
\mathbb{T}
is a counter-example to Theorem
1.5
, then there exists either a thickening or a zooming-in and rescaling that produces a richer collection of tubes. However, Theorem
1.5
is agnostic to location and scale, and this means that if
\mathbb{T}
is a counter-example to Theorem
1.5
, then any thickening or zooming-in and rescaling of
\mathbb{T}
remains a counter-example. Thus to prove Theorem
1.5
, we show that if a counter-example to Theorem
1.5
exists, then there must exist a (nearly) maximally rich counter-example. But this is impossible, since some thickening or zooming-in and rescaling of this (supposedly) maximally rich counter-example is an even richer counter-example.

In Section
2.2
we precisely define the relevant generalization of stickiness, and in Section
3
we define what it means for
\mathbb{T}
to be a maximally rich counter-example to Theorem
1.5
. In Section
4
we carry out the bulk of the geometric arguments described above. In Section
5
we show that a (hypothetical) counterexample to Theorem
1.5
must satisfy the hypotheses of (a generalization of) the sticky Kakeya Theorem. In Sections
6
and
7
we prove the necessary generalization of the Sticky Kakeya Theorem (this is Theorem
5.2
), thereby proving Theorem
1.5
. Finally, in Section
8
we show how Theorem
1.5
implies Theorems
1.2
and
1.4
.

Wolff axioms and their generalizations

In this section we will define several anti-clustering conditions that we can impose on our collection of tubes. In
[
17
]

\delta

Definition 2.4
.

Let
\mathbb{T}
be a set of
\delta
-tubes in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. We say
\mathbb{T}
satisfies the
Wolff axioms
if:

•

Every
\rho

\mathbb{R}^{n}
can contain at most
C(\rho/\delta)^{n-1}
tubes from
\mathbb{T}
.

•

Every rectangular prism in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
of dimensions

can contain at most
C(\rho/\delta)
tubes from
\mathbb{T}
.

Remarks

1. In the above definition, the constant
C
is generally chosen to be a large number depending only on the ambient dimension
n
.

2. Every set of
\delta
-tubes pointing in

.

3. Every set of
\delta
-tubes contained in

.

4

In this section we will carefully execute the arguments described in Section

4.1
Arrangements of rectangular prisms

We begin the process described at the end of the previous section. Let
\mathcal{R}
be a set of planks, i.e. rectangular prisms of dimensions roughly

\delta\leq s\leq t\leq 1

(i.e. the union of the other planks that intersect

.

\delta\in(0,\delta_{0}]
. Let
\delta\leq s\leq t\leq 1
with
s\leq\delta^{\varepsilon}t
. Let
\mathcal{R}
be a multi-set of
s\times t\times 1
rectangular prisms contained in
B(0,1)
, and suppose that
\mathcal{R}
satisfies the Convex Wolff Axioms with error
\delta^{-\eta}
. Let
\{Y(R),R\in\mathcal{R}\}
be a
\delta^{\eta}
-dense shading.

Then at least one of the following two things must happen:

(A)

\bigcup_{\mathcal{R}}Y(R)
has discretized Assouad dimension at least
3-\varepsilon
at scale
s
, with scale separation
\delta^{-\eta}
. This means that there exists
r\in[\delta^{-\eta}s,1]
and a
r
-ball
B
so that

\Big|B\cap\bigcup_{R\in\mathcal{R}}Y(R)\Big|\geq(s/r)^{\varepsilon}|B|.

(4.1)

(B)

There are scales
s^{\prime}\in[\delta^{-\varepsilon^{2}/4}s,\delta^{\varepsilon/2}]
and
t^{\prime}=\min\big(1,t\frac{s^{\prime}}{s}\big)
, and a multi-set
\mathcal{R}^{\prime}
of
s^{\prime}\times t^{\prime}\times 1
prisms that satisfies the Convex Wolff Axioms with error
\delta^{-\tau}
, so that the shading

Y^{\prime}(R^{\prime})=R^{\prime}\cap N_{s^{\prime}}\Big(\bigcup_{\begin{subarray}{c}R\in\mathcal{R}\\
R\subset 2R^{\prime}\end{subarray}}Y(R)\Big)

(4.2)

is
\delta^{\tau}
-dense.

Remark 4.3
.

Note that as
\varepsilon\searrow 0
, Conclusion (A) becomes stronger, while conclusion (B) becomes weaker (indeed, if
\varepsilon=0
then Conclusion (B) vacuously holds with
s^{\prime}=s
and
t^{\prime}=t
). As
\tau\searrow 0
, Conclusion (A) is unchanged, while Conclusion (B) becomes stronger.

Proof.

Step 1: Robust broadness.

After replacing
\mathcal{R}
by a subset of cardinality
\gtrsim\delta^{\eta}(\#\mathcal{R})
if necessary, we may suppose that
|Y(R)|\geq\frac{1}{2}\delta^{\eta}|R|
for each
R\in\mathcal{R}
. Let
Y_{1}(R)\subset Y(R)
be a regular (in the sense of Definition
2.2
) refinement of
Y
, with
|Y_{1}(R)|\geq\frac{1}{2}|Y(R)|
for each
R\in\mathcal{R}
.

Next, we perform a “two-ends” style robust broadness reduction. Let
\varepsilon_{1}>0
be a small quantity to be chosen below. For each
x\in\bigcup_{R\in\mathcal{R}}Y_{1}(R)
, let
u_{x}\in[t,1]
and let
T_{u_{x}}
be a
u_{x}
-tube that comes within a multiplicative factor of 2 of maximizing the quantity

u_{x}^{-\varepsilon_{1}}\#\{R\in\mathcal{R}\colon x\in Y_{1}(R),\ R\subset T_{u_{x}}\}.

Since there exists a 1-tube that contains a
\gtrsim 1
fraction of
\mathcal{R}
, and since
u_{x}\geq\delta
, we have

\#\{R\in\mathcal{R}\colon x\in Y_{1}(R),\ R\subset T_{u_{x}}\}\gtrsim\delta^{\varepsilon_{1}}\#\{R\in\mathcal{R}\colon x\in Y_{1}(R)\}.

Define the shading

Y_{2}(R)=\{x\in Y_{1}(R)\colon R\subset T_{u_{x}}\}.

Then
\sum_{R\in\mathcal{R}}|Y_{2}(R)|\gtrsim\delta^{\varepsilon_{1}}\sum_{R\in\mathcal{R}}|Y_{1}(R)|
. For each
x\in\bigcup_{R\in\mathcal{R}}Y_{2}(R)
, each
t\leq r\leq u_{x}
, and each
r
-tube
T_{r}
, we have

\#\{R\in\mathcal{R}\colon x\in Y_{2}(R),\ R\subset T_{r}\}\leq 2(r/u_{x})^{\varepsilon_{1}}\#\{R\in\mathcal{R}\colon x\in Y_{2}(R)\}.

(4.3)

By dyadic pigeonholing, we can select a multiplicity
\mu
and a number
t\leq u\leq 1
so that if we define

Y_{3}(R)=Y_{2}(R)\cap\Big\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{3}\colon\sum_{R\in\mathcal{R}}\chi_{Y_{2}(R)}(x)\in[\mu,2\mu),\ u_{x}\in[u/2,u)\Big\},

then provided we choose
\delta_{0}
sufficiently small and
\eta<\varepsilon_{1}/2
, we have
\sum_{R\in\mathcal{R}}|Y_{3}(R)|\geq\delta^{2\varepsilon_{1}}\sum_{R\in\mathcal{R}}|R|
.

We first consider the case where
\mu\leq\delta^{-\varepsilon^{2}+2\varepsilon_{1}}(st\#\mathcal{R})
. Then

\Big|\bigcup_{R\in\mathcal{R}}Y(R)\Big|\geq\mu^{-1}\sum_{R\in\mathcal{R}}|Y_{3}(R)|\geq\delta^{\varepsilon^{2}-2\varepsilon_{1}}\delta^{2\varepsilon_{1}}\frac{\sum_{R\in\mathcal{R}}|R|}{st(\#\mathcal{R})}\geq\delta^{\varepsilon^{2}}\geq s^{\varepsilon},

where the final inequality used the hypothesis that
s\leq\delta^{\varepsilon}t
and hence
s\leq\delta^{\varepsilon}
. Thus Conclusion (A) holds with
r=1
and
B
the unit ball, and we are done.

Our choice of
\varepsilon_{1}
will satisfy
\varepsilon_{1}\leq\varepsilon^{3}/6
, and thus for the remainder of the proof we may suppose that

\mu>\delta^{-(2/3)\varepsilon^{2}}(st\#\mathcal{R}).

(4.4)

Step 2: Localization.

Let
\mathbb{T}_{u}
be a set of essentially distinct
u
tubes with the property that for every
u
-tube
T_{u}
, there is at least one, and at most
O(1),
tubes from
\mathbb{T}_{u}
whose 2-fold dilate contains
T_{u}
.
For each
x\in\bigcup_{R\in\mathcal{R}}Y_{3}(R)
, select a tube
T_{u}(x)\in\mathbb{T}_{u}
with the property that
T_{u_{x}}\subset 2T_{u}
. Define the shading
Y(T_{u})=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{3}\colon T_{u}(x)=T_{u}\}
. We have
\bigcup_{R\in\mathcal{R}}Y_{3}(R)=\bigsqcup_{T_{u}\in\mathbb{T}_{u}}Y(T_{u})
. Note that technically,
Y(T_{u})
is a subset of
2T_{u}
, rather than a subset of
T_{u}
. Observe that if
R\in\mathcal{R}
with
x\in Y_{3}(R)
, then
R\subset T_{u_{x}}\subset 2T_{u}(x)
.

Since the tubes in
\mathbb{T}_{u}
are essentially distinct, for each
T_{u}\in\mathbb{T}_{u}
there are
O(1)
tubes
T_{u}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{T}_{u}
with the property that
2T_{u}\cap 2T_{u}^{\prime}
contains a unit line segment — indeed, if
2T_{u}\cap 2T_{u}^{\prime}
contains a unit line segment, then
T_{u}
and
T_{u}^{\prime}
must make angle
\lesssim u
. Thus by pigeonholing, we can replace
\mathbb{T}_{u}
by a
O(1)
refinement
\mathbb{T}_{u}^{\prime}
with the following properties:

•

For each pair of distinct tubes
T_{u},T_{u}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{T}_{u}^{\prime}
,
2T_{u}\cap 2T_{u}^{\prime}
does not contain a unit line segment. In particular, each
R\in\mathcal{R}
is contained in at most one dilated tube
2T_{u}
.

•

\Big|\bigsqcup_{T_{u}\in\mathbb{T}_{u}^{\prime}}Y(T_{u})\Big|\gtrsim\Big|\bigsqcup_{T_{u}\in\mathbb{T}_{u}}Y(T_{u})\Big|=\Big|\bigcup_{R\in\mathcal{R}}Y_{3}(R)\Big|.

For each
R\in\mathcal{R}
, define
Y_{4}(R)=Y_{3}(R)\cap Y(T_{u})
, where
T_{u}
is the unique tube from
\mathbb{T}_{u}^{\prime}
for which
R\subset 2T_{u}
. If no such tube exists, then define
Y_{4}(R)=\emptyset
. We have

\sum_{R\in\mathcal{R}}|Y_{4}(R)|\geq\mu\sum_{T_{u}\in\mathbb{T}_{u}^{\prime}}|Y(T_{u})|\gtrsim\mu\sum_{T_{u}\in\mathbb{T}_{u}}|Y(T_{u})|\geq\frac{1}{2}\sum_{R\in\mathcal{R}}|Y_{3}(R)|\geq\delta^{2\varepsilon_{1}}\sum_{R\in\mathcal{R}}|R|.

Finally, let
\mathbb{T}_{u}^{\prime\prime}\subset\mathbb{T}_{u}^{\prime}
consist of those
T_{u}\in\mathbb{T}_{u}^{\prime}
for which

\sum_{R\in\mathcal{R}[2T_{u}]}|Y_{4}(R)|\geq c\delta^{2\varepsilon_{1}}\sum_{R\in\mathcal{R}[2T_{u}]}|R|.

(4.5)

We will choose the constant
c\sim 1
sufficiently small so that

\sum_{T_{u}\in\mathbb{T}_{u}^{\prime\prime}}\sum_{R\in\mathcal{R}[2T_{u}]}|Y_{4}(R)|\gtrsim\sum_{T_{u}\in\mathbb{T}_{u}^{\prime}}\sum_{R\in\mathcal{R}[2T_{u}]}|Y_{4}(R)|.

In summary, the situation is as follows: For each
T_{u}\in\mathbb{T}_{u}^{\prime\prime}
, we have

\sum_{R\in\mathcal{R}[2T_{u}]}|Y_{4}(R)|\gtrsim\delta^{2\varepsilon_{1}}\sum_{R\in\mathcal{R}[2T_{u}]}|R|,

(4.6)

and for each
x\in\bigcup_{R\in\mathcal{R}[2T_{u}]}Y_{4}(R),
we have

\{R\in\mathcal{R}[2T_{u}]\colon x\in Y_{4}(R)\}=\{R\in\mathcal{R}\colon x\in Y_{3}(R)\}=\{R\in\mathcal{R}\colon x\in Y_{2}(R)\}.

(4.7)

The cardinality of the above set is between
\mu
and
2\mu
.

For each
T_{u}\in\mathbb{T}_{u}^{\prime\prime}
and each
R\in\mathcal{R}[2T_{u}]
, the unit rescaling of
R
relative to
2T_{u}
is comparable to a

prism. Define
\tilde{s}=s/u
and
\tilde{t}=t/u
. Abusing notation slightly, we will call this set a
\tilde{s}\times\tilde{t}\times 1
prism, and we will denote the (multi) set of these prisms by
\tilde{\mathcal{R}}^{T_{u}}
. Let
\tilde{Y}_{1}
(resp.
\tilde{Y}_{4}
) be the images of the shadings
Y_{1}
(resp.
Y_{4}
) under the unit rescaling described above. Thus for each
T_{u}\in\mathbb{T}_{u}
, we have that
(\tilde{\mathcal{R}}^{T_{u}},\tilde{Y}_{1})
is a set of
\tilde{s}\times\tilde{t}\times 1
prisms and a shading on these prisms, and similarly for
(\tilde{\mathcal{R}}^{T_{u}},\tilde{Y}_{4})
. By (
4.7
) and our definition of
Y_{3}
, for each
T_{u}\in\mathbb{T}_{u}
and each
x\in\bigcup_{\tilde{R}\in\tilde{\mathcal{R}}}\tilde{Y}_{4}(\tilde{R})
, we have

\mu\leq\#\{\tilde{R}\in\tilde{\mathcal{R}}^{T_{u}}\colon x\in\tilde{Y}_{4}(\tilde{R})\}<2\mu.

(4.8)

For each
\tilde{R}\in\tilde{\mathcal{R}}^{T_{u}}
, let
v(\tilde{R})
be the direction of a tube of minimal diameter that contains
\tilde{R}
. Thus
v(R)
is the “direction” of the tube
\tilde{R}
. We have that
v(R)
is meaningfully defined up to accuracy
\tilde{t}
, in the sense that if
\tilde{R}
and
\tilde{R}^{\prime}
are comparable, then
\angle(v(\tilde{R}),\ v(\tilde{R}^{\prime}))=O(\tilde{t})
.

As a consequence of (
4.3
), we have that for each
x\in\bigcup_{\tilde{R}\in\tilde{\mathcal{R}}^{T_{u}}}\tilde{Y}_{4}(\tilde{R})
, each
\tilde{t}\leq r\leq 1
, and each unit vector

, we have

\#\{\tilde{R}\in\tilde{\mathcal{R}}^{T_{u}}\colon x\in\tilde{Y}_{4}(\tilde{R}),\ \angle(v(\tilde{R}),\ v)\leq r\}\lesssim r^{\varepsilon_{1}}\mu.

(4.9)

Step 3: Dealing with transverse intersection.
Fix a
u
-tube
T_{u}\in\mathbb{T}_{u}^{\prime\prime}
, and define
\tilde{\mathcal{R}}=\tilde{\mathcal{R}}^{T_{u}}
. Each
\tilde{R}\in\tilde{\mathcal{R}}
is comparable to a rectangular prism of dimensions
\tilde{s}\times\tilde{t}\times 1
, i.e.
\tilde{R}
is comparable to the intersection of the following three sets: the
\tilde{s}
neighbourhood of a plane
\Pi(\tilde{R})
; the
\tilde{t}
neighbourhood of a tube
T(\tilde{R})
; and the unit ball. Let
n(\tilde{R})
be the unit normal vector of
\Pi(\tilde{R})
; this is meaningfully defined up to accuracy
\tilde{s}/\tilde{t}=s/t
, in the sense that if
\tilde{R}
and
\tilde{R}^{\prime}
are comparable, then
\angle(n(\tilde{R}),\ n(\tilde{R}^{\prime}))=O(\tilde{s}/\tilde{t})
(here
\angle(\cdot,\cdot)
refers to unsigned angle, since we do not distinguish between the vectors
n(\tilde{R})
and
-n(\tilde{R})
).

For each
x\in\bigcup_{\tilde{R}\in\tilde{\mathcal{R}}}\tilde{Y}_{4}(R)
, define the “plane spread”

\operatorname{P-spread}(x)=\max\Big\{\angle\big(n(\tilde{R}),n(\tilde{R}^{\prime})\big)\Big\},

where the maximum is taken over all pairs
\tilde{R},\tilde{R}^{\prime}
for which
x\in\tilde{Y}_{4}(R)
and
x\in\tilde{Y}_{4}(R)
. Since
n(\tilde{R})
and
n(\tilde{R}^{\prime})
are defined up to uncertainty
O(\tilde{s}/\tilde{t})=O(s/t)
,
\operatorname{P-spread}(x)
is meaningfully defined if it is substantially larger than
s/t
. If
x\not\in\bigcup_{\tilde{R}\in\tilde{\mathcal{R}}}\tilde{Y}_{4}(\tilde{R})
, then define
\operatorname{P-spread}(x)=0
.

Suppose there exists a prism
\tilde{R}_{0}\in\tilde{\mathcal{R}}
and a number
\theta\geq\delta^{-7\varepsilon_{1}/\varepsilon}(\tilde{s}/\tilde{t}\phantom{.})
so that

\big|\{x\in\tilde{R}_{0}\colon\theta\leq\operatorname{P-spread}(x)<2\theta\}\big|\geq\frac{c}{100}|\log\delta|^{-2}\delta^{2\varepsilon_{1}}|\tilde{R}_{0}|,

(4.10)

where
c\sim 1
is the constant from (
4.5
).

Since
\tilde{R}_{0}
is a
\tilde{s}\times\tilde{t}\times 1
prism, for each
x_{0}\in\tilde{R}_{0}
we have
|\tilde{R}_{0}\cap B(x_{0},\tilde{t})|\sim\tilde{s}\tilde{t}\phantom{.}^{2}
. By pigeonholing, we can select a point
x_{0}\in\tilde{R}_{0}
so that

\big|\{x\in\tilde{R}_{0}\cap B(x_{0},\tilde{t})\colon\theta\leq\operatorname{P-spread}(x)<2\theta\}\big|\gtrapprox\delta^{2\varepsilon_{1}}\tilde{s}\tilde{t}\phantom{.}^{2}.

(4.11)

After a harmless translation and rotation, we may suppose that
\Pi(\tilde{R}_{0})
is the
yz
-plane, i.e.
\operatorname{span}\{e_{2},e_{3}\}
, and that
\tilde{R}_{0}\cap B(x_{0},\tilde{t})
is contained in the “square”
S=[-\tilde{s},\tilde{s}]\times[-\tilde{t},\tilde{t}\phantom{.}]\times[-\tilde{t},\tilde{t}\phantom{.}]
(note as well that
|S|\sim|\tilde{R}_{0}\cap B(x_{0},\tilde{t})|
). Let
S^{{\dagger}}
denote the thickened square
[-10\tilde{t}\theta,10\tilde{t}\theta]\times[-10\tilde{t},10\tilde{t}\phantom{.}]\times[-10\tilde{t},10\tilde{t}\phantom{.}]
, i.e.
S^{{\dagger}}
is comparable to the
\tilde{t}\theta

S
.

Denote the set on the LHS of (
4.11
) by
E
; then for each
x\in E
there is a prism
\tilde{R}_{x}\in\mathcal{R}
with
x\in\tilde{Y}_{4}(\tilde{R})
and
\angle(n(\tilde{R}_{x}),e_{1})\in[\theta,2\theta)
. This implies that
\tilde{R}_{x}\cap B(x,\tilde{t})\subset\tilde{R}_{x}\cap B(x_{0},10\tilde{t})\subset S^{{\dagger}}
. Recall that the shading
\tilde{Y}_{1}(\tilde{R}_{x})
is regular, and thus we have

|\tilde{Y}_{1}(\tilde{R}_{x})\cap B(x_{0},2\tilde{t})|\gtrapprox|\tilde{Y}_{1}(\tilde{R}_{x})|\frac{|\tilde{R}_{x}\cap B(x_{0},2\tilde{t})|}{|\tilde{R}_{x}|}\gtrsim\delta^{2\varepsilon_{1}}\tilde{s}\tilde{t}\phantom{.}^{2}.

(4.12)

Define the linear map
\tilde{A}(x,y,z)=\Big(\frac{x}{10\tilde{t}\theta},\ \frac{y}{10\tilde{t}},\ \frac{z}{10\tilde{t}}\Big)
, so
A(S^{{\dagger}})=[-1,1]^{3}
. Define

\hat{S}=A(S)=[-\frac{\tilde{s}}{10\tilde{t}\theta},\frac{\tilde{s}}{10\tilde{t}\theta}]\times[-1/10,1/10]\times[-1/10,1/10],

and let
\hat{E}=A(E)\subset\hat{S}
. For each
x\in E
, let
\hat{R}_{x}=A(\tilde{R}_{x}\cap S^{\dagger})
; this is comparable to a rectangular prism of dimensions roughly
\frac{\tilde{s}}{\tilde{t}\theta}\times 1\times 1
, and this prism makes angle
\sim 1
with the
yz
-plane, i.e. there is a constant
c_{0}\sim 1
(independent of the choice of
x\in E
) so that the prism has a normal direction
n(\hat{R}_{x})
(defined up to uncertainty
O(\frac{\tilde{s}}{\tilde{t}\theta})
) with
\angle(n(\hat{R}_{x}),e_{1})\geq c_{0}
. By (
4.12
) we have

|\hat{Y}_{2}(\hat{R}_{x})|\gtrapprox\delta^{2\varepsilon_{1}}\frac{\tilde{s}}{\tilde{t}\theta}.

(4.13)

By pigeonholing, we can find a unit vector
e\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
and a set
\hat{E}^{\prime}\subset\hat{E}
with
|\hat{E}^{\prime}|\sim|\hat{E}|
, so that
\angle\big(e,n(\hat{R}_{x})\big)\leq c_{0}/2
for each
x\in\hat{E}^{\prime}
. In particular this means that
\angle(e,e_{1})\geq c_{0}/2\gtrsim 1
, and hence the projection of
e
to the
yz
-plane has magnitude
\sim 1
; denote the image of
e
under this projection by
e^{\prime}
.

(
4.11
) says that
|E|\gtrapprox\delta^{2\varepsilon_{1}}\tilde{s}\tilde{t}\phantom{.}^{2}
and hence
|\hat{E}^{\prime}|\gtrsim|\hat{E}|\gtrapprox\delta^{\eta}\frac{s}{t\theta}
. On the other hand,
\hat{E}^{\prime}\subset\hat{S}
, and the latter set has volume
\sim\frac{\tilde{s}}{\tilde{t}\theta}
. Since
\hat{S}=[-\frac{\tilde{s}}{10\tilde{t}\theta},\frac{\tilde{s}}{10\tilde{t}\theta}]\times[-1/10,1/10]\times[-1/10,1/10]
and
e^{\prime}
is contained in the

\ell
pointing in direction
e^{\prime}
that satisfies
|\ell\cap\hat{E}^{\prime}|\gtrapprox\delta^{2\varepsilon_{1}}
.

Select a
\frac{\tilde{s}}{\tilde{t}\theta}
-separated set of points

(here

) from

. Note that
\hat{R}_{x_{i}}\cap\ell
is a line segment of length between
\frac{\tilde{s}}{\tilde{t}\theta}
and

, and hence if
i,j
is a pair of indices with

, then

. Since the line
\ell
points in direction
e^{\prime}
, and

, we conclude that
\operatorname{dist}(x_{i},\hat{R}_{x_{j}})\gtrsim\frac{\tilde{s}}{\tilde{t}\theta}|i-j|
, so in particular
\hat{R}_{x_{i}}
contains a point that has distance
\gtrsim\frac{\tilde{s}}{\tilde{t}\theta}|i-j|
from
\hat{R}_{x_{j}}
. Thus either
\hat{R}_{x_{i}}
and
\hat{R}_{x_{j}}
do not intersect, or if they do intersect then their normal vectors satisfy
\angle\big(n(\hat{R}_{x_{i}}),\ n(\hat{R}_{x_{j}})\big)\gtrsim\frac{\tilde{s}}{\tilde{t}\theta}|i-j|.
In either case, we have

|\hat{R}_{x_{i}}\cap\hat{R}_{x_{j}}|\lesssim\frac{(\frac{\tilde{s}}{\tilde{t}\theta})^{2}}{\frac{\tilde{s}}{\tilde{t}\theta}(1+|i-j|)}=\frac{\tilde{s}}{\tilde{t}\theta(1+|i-j|)}.

(4.14)

The above inequality vacuously holds if
i,j
are a pair of indices with
|x_{i}-x_{j}|<100\frac{\tilde{s}}{\tilde{t}\theta}
, since in this case
|i-j|\sim 1
and we can use the trivial bound
|\hat{R}_{x_{i}}\cap\hat{R}_{x_{j}}|\leq|\hat{R}_{x_{i}}|
.

We conclude that

\Big\|\sum_{i=1}^{N}\chi_{\hat{Y}_{2}(\hat{R}_{i})}\Big\|_{2}^{2}\leq\sum_{i,j=1}^{N}|\hat{R}_{i}\cap\hat{R}_{j}|\lesssim N\frac{\tilde{s}}{\tilde{t}\theta}\big|\log\big(\frac{\tilde{s}}{\tilde{t}\theta}\big)\big|,

(4.15)

and hence

\Big|\bigcup_{i=1}^{N}\hat{Y}_{2}(\hat{R}_{i})\Big|\geq\frac{\Big(\sum_{i}|\hat{Y}_{2}(\hat{R}_{i})|\Big)^{2}}{\Big\|\sum_{i}\chi_{\hat{Y}_{2}(\hat{R}_{i})}\Big\|_{2}^{2}}\gtrapprox\frac{\Big(N\delta^{2\varepsilon_{1}}\frac{\tilde{s}}{\tilde{t}\theta}\Big)^{2}}{N\frac{\tilde{s}}{\tilde{t}\theta}\big|\log\big(\frac{\tilde{s}}{\tilde{t}\theta}\big)\big|}\gtrapprox\delta^{4\varepsilon_{1}}N\frac{\tilde{s}}{\tilde{t}\theta}\gtrapprox\delta^{6\varepsilon_{1}},

where the second inequality used (
4.13
) and (
4.15
). Undoing the scaling transformation
A
, we conclude that

\Big|S^{\dagger}\cap\bigcup_{\tilde{R}\in\tilde{\mathcal{R}}}\tilde{Y}_{1}(\tilde{R})\Big|\gtrapprox\delta^{6\varepsilon_{1}}|S^{\dagger}|.

Recall that
S^{\dagger}
is a rectangular prism of dimensions roughly
\tilde{t}\theta\times\tilde{t}\times\tilde{t}
.

Next, undo the unit rescaling with respect to

; recall that this unit rescaling anisotropically dilated two directions by a multiplicative factor of roughly

, and left the third direction unchanged. Thus the preimage of

under this rescaling is comparable to a rectangular prisms whose smallest dimension is at least
\gtrsim\tilde{t}\theta u=t\theta\geq\delta^{-7\varepsilon_{1}/\varepsilon}s
.

By pigeonholing, we can find a ball
B
of radius

contained in the preimage of

, so that

\Big|B\cap\bigcup_{R\in\mathcal{R}}Y(R)\Big|\gtrapprox\delta^{6\varepsilon_{1}}|B|\geq\delta^{-\varepsilon_{1}}(s/r)^{\varepsilon}|B|.

(4.16)

If
\delta_{0}
is selected sufficiently small, then the LHS of (

) is at least
(s/r)^{\varepsilon}|B|
, and thus Conclusion (A) holds.

Step 4: Ensuring that
u
is large.

At this point we may assume that for every
T_{u}\in\mathbb{T}_{u}^{\prime\prime}
, every prism
\tilde{R}_{0}\in\tilde{\mathcal{R}}^{T_{u}}
, and every
\theta\geq\delta^{-7\varepsilon_{1}/\varepsilon}(\tilde{s}/\tilde{t})
, we have that (

) fails.

For each
T_{u}\in\mathbb{T}_{u}^{\prime\prime}
and each
\tilde{R}\in\tilde{\mathcal{R}}^{T_{u}}
, define

\tilde{Y}_{5}(\tilde{R})=\{x\in\tilde{Y}_{4}(R)\colon\operatorname{P-spread}(x)\leq\delta^{-7\varepsilon_{1}/\varepsilon}(\tilde{s}/\tilde{t}).

For each
R\in\bigcup_{T_{u}\in\mathbb{T}_{u}^{\prime\prime}}\mathcal{R}[2T_{u}]
, define
Y_{5}(R)\subset Y_{4}(R)
to be the preimage of

Lemma 4.4
.

Let
0<s\leq 1/2
and
K\geq 1
. Let
\mathcal{R}
be a multi-set of
s\times 1\times 1
rectangular prisms, and suppose that
\mathcal{R}
satisfies the Convex Wolff Axioms with error
K

\{Y(R),R\in\mathcal{R}\}
be a
K^{-1}
-dense shading.
Then

\Big|\bigcup_{R\in\mathcal{R}}Y(R)\Big|\gtrsim K^{-3}(\log 1/s)^{-1}.

(4.24)

Proof.

Let
E=\bigcup_{R\in\mathcal{R}}Y(R)
. Since the shading
Y
is
K^{-1}
dense, by Cauchy-Schwartz we have

K^{-1}s(\#\mathcal{R})\leq\int\chi_{E}\cdot\sum_{R\in\mathcal{R}}\chi_{Y(R)}\leq\int\chi_{E}\cdot\sum_{R\in\mathcal{R}}\chi_{R}\leq|E|^{1/2}\Big\|\sum_{R\in\mathcal{R}}\chi_{R}\Big\|_{2}.

(4.25)

In the argument that follows, we will obtain an upper bound for
\big\|\sum_{\mathcal{R}}\chi_{R}\big\|_{2}
.

For each prism
R\in\mathcal{R}
, there is an associated normal vector
v(R)\in S^{2}
, which is well-defined up to uncertainty
O(s)
. For
R,R^{\prime}\in\mathcal{R}
, define
\angle(R,R^{\prime})
to be the (unsigned) angle between
v(R)
and
v(R^{\prime})
.

Fix
R\in\mathcal{R}
. Observe that if
R\cap R^{\prime}
is non-empty and
\angle(R,R^{\prime})=\theta\sim 2^{j}s
, then
R^{\prime}
is contained in the

rectangular prism concentric with

\mathcal{R}
obeys the Convex Wolff Axioms, at most
K(2^{j}s)(\#\mathcal{R})
slabs

can have this property.

Let

be the smallest integer with

. Using the above observation, we compute

\begin{split}\Big\|\sum_{R\in\mathcal{R}}\chi_{R}\Big\|_{2}^{2}&=\sum_{R^{\prime}\in\mathcal{R}}|R\cap R^{\prime}|=\sum_{j=0}^{j_{1}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}R^{\prime}\colon R\cap R^{\prime}\neq\emptyset,\\
\angle(R,R^{\prime})\sim 2^{j}s\end{subarray}}|R\cap R^{\prime}|\\
&\lesssim\sum_{j=0}^{j_{1}}\Big(K(2^{j}s\#\mathcal{R})\Big)\Big(\frac{s^{2}}{2^{j}s}\Big)\lesssim Ks^{2}(\#\mathcal{R})\log(1/s).\end{split}

(4.26)

Chaining (
4.25
) and (
4.26

4.2
Arrangements of tubes that cluster into rectangular prisms

In Section
4.1
, we showed that unions of planks have discretized Assouad dimension close to 3. In this section we will use these results to show that if an arrangement of
\delta
-tubes clusters into planks, then the union of these tubes must have discretized Assouad dimension close to 3. This is made precise in Lemmas
4.7
and

below.

As a first step, the following lemma says that if
\mathbb{T}
is a set of
\delta
tubes of cardinality
>\!\!>\rho/\delta

\delta\times\rho\times 1
prism, then these tubes must cluster into
\delta\times\omega\times 1

\delta<\!\!<\omega\leq\rho

\delta\times\omega\times 1

to have discretized Assouad dimension close to 3.

Lemma 4.6

For all
\varepsilon>0
, there exists
\eta,\delta_{0}>0
so that the following holds for all
\delta\in(0,\delta_{0}]
. Let

and let

. Let
(\mathbb{T},Y)_{\delta}
be a set of essentially distinct
\delta
-tubes, with
\#\mathbb{T}\geq K\rho/\delta
, and

a uniformly
\delta^{\eta}
-dense shading. Let
R_{0}
be a
10\delta\times\rho\times 1
rectangle, and suppose each
T\in\mathbb{T}
is contained in
R_{0}
.

Then there exists a number
K\delta\lesssim\omega\leq\rho
and a set
\mathcal{R}
of essentially distinct
10\delta\times\omega\times 1
prisms, so that

\#\Big(\bigcup_{R\in\mathcal{R}}\{T\in\mathbb{T}\colon T\subset R\}\Big)\gtrapprox\#\mathbb{T},

(4.29)

and for each prism

\Big|\bigcup_{\begin{subarray}{c}T\in\mathbb{T}\\

(4.30)

Proof.

For each

\operatorname{L-spread}(x)=0

C

(4.31)

(Note that the shading
Z(T)
is not necessarily a subset of

). We justify this claim as follows. Since there are at most

\theta

, if we could not select a

), and also make angle

\mathbb{T}
are essentially distinct, we conclude that

can satisfy

6
Kakeya estimates for tubes satisfying the Convex Wolff Axioms at every scale

Our goal in this section is to prove Theorem
5.2
. Theorem
5.2
is closely related to the discretized analogue of
[
16
, Theorem 1.1]
, and specifically the estimate
\sigma_{3}=0
from that paper; the latter result says that if
\mathbb{T}
is a collection of
\delta
-tubes in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
of cardinality roughly
\delta^{-2}
that point in
\delta
-separated directions, and if
\mathbb{T}
satisfies a property called “stickiness,” then the union of the tubes must have volume
\gtrapprox 1
. Stickiness is a technical property, which roughly speaking says that for each
\rho\in[\delta,1]
, the tubes in
\mathbb{T}
can be covered by a collection of
\rho
-tubes that point in
\rho
-separated directions (more accurately, the tubes point in
\rho
-separated directions up to multiplicity
\lessapprox 1
).

Stickiness is a multi-scale property, in the following sense: if
\mathbb{T}
is sticky, then for each
\rho\geq\delta
we can cover
\mathbb{T}
by a sticky collection of
\rho
-tubes. This multi-scale property was exploited throughout the arguments in
[
16
]
. Theorem
5.2
differs from
[
16
, Theorem 1.1]
, because the stickiness hypothesis has been replaced by the hypothesis that the tubes satisfy the Convex Wolff axioms at every scale. As we will see below, however, this latter hypothesis is sufficient in order to repeat the arguments from
[
16
]
. Indeed, with two exceptions that will be explained below, the arguments from
[
16
]
can be repeated mutatis mutandis to obtain Theorem
5.2
.

The proof of
[
16
, Theorem 1.1]
is divided into several sections. The paper begins by supposing that the result is false, and studying the structure of a (hypothetical) counter-example. In Section 2, the authors define what it means for a pair
(\mathbb{T},Y)
of tubes and their associated shading to be a worst possible counter-example to
[
16
, Theorem 1.1]
; this is called an
\varepsilon
-extremal pair, and we will adopt this terminology in our discussion below. In
[
16
]
, Section 3, it is shown that an
\varepsilon
-extremal pair
(\mathbb{T},Y)
must look coarsely self similar at every intermediate scale
\rho\in[\delta,1]
. More precisely, for every such
\rho
it is possible to cover the tubes in
\mathbb{T}
by a collection of
\rho
-tubes, so that the corresponding collection of
\rho
-tubes is a worst possible counter-example to
[
16
, Theorem 1.1]
at scale
\rho
, and the
\delta
-tubes inside each
\rho
-tube also form a (rescaled) worst-possible counter-example at scale
\delta/\rho
. The analogous statement and proof in our setting are nearly identical; we will state the former and briefly sketch the latter.

In Sections 3, 4, and 5 of
[
16
]
, the authors use the multi-scale self-similarity established in Section 2 of
[
16
]
to show that an extremal pair
(\mathbb{T},Y)
must have certain structural properties called planiness and graininess, and in particular there must exist local grains that are described by a Lipschitz plane map, and global grains that are described by a
C^{2}
slope function
f
; this will be described in greater detail below. The arguments from
[
16
]
can be repeated without modification to establish the same conclusions in the present setting. In particular, the hypothesis from
[
16
]
that the tubes point in
\delta
-separated directions has not been used up until this step.

In Section 6 of
[
16
]
, the authors show that the derivative
f^{\prime}
of the slope function has magnitude roughly 1 (and in particular, this quantity is bounded away from 0). It is during this step that the authors use the hypothesis that the tubes point in
\delta
-separated directions. With some small modifications, we can instead use the hypothesis that the tubes satisfy the Convex Wolff axioms at every scale; we will elaborate on this step in Section
6.4
.

Finally, in Section 7 of
[
16
]
, the authors recall an analogue of Wolff’s circular maximal function estimate
[
19
,
14
]
, and they show how this estimate contradicts the assumption that there exists a collection of sticky tubes whose union has volume much smaller than 1, thereby completing the proof of the theorem. This section of
[
16
]
uses the assumption that the tubes point in different directions in order to obtain a rich planar arrangement of curves to which Wolff’s circular maximal theorem can be applied. With some modifications, the direction-separated assumption can be replaced with a weaker ball condition. We will explain this in detail in Sections
6.5
and
7
.

Let
(\mathbb{T},Y)_{\delta}

\delta

The plane map.

In the arguments in

Discretized Ahlfors-David regular sets.

6.2
Extremal collection of tubes, and multi-scale structure

We are ready to begin the proof of Theorem
5.2
. Suppose to the contrary that the result was false. Then there exists
\sigma^{\prime}>0
so that for all
\eta,\delta_{0}>0
, there exists
\delta\in(0,\delta_{0}]
; a set
\mathbb{T}
of
\delta
-tubes that satisfy the Convex Wolff Axioms at every scale with error
\delta^{-\eta}
; and a
\delta^{\eta}
-dense shading
Y(T)
, so that

\Big|\bigcup_{T\in\mathbb{T}}Y(T)\Big|\leq\delta^{\sigma^{\prime}}.

Let
\sigma>0
be the supremum of all numbers
\sigma^{\prime}
with the above property; this supremum exists, since by hypothesis the set of admissible
\sigma^{\prime}
is non-empty, and it is bounded above by 3. Our goal is to show that we must in fact have
\sigma=0
, and thereby obtain a contradiction.

The following definition is the analogue of
[
16
, Definition 3.1]
.

Definition 6.1
.

Let
\varepsilon,\delta>0
. We say a pair
(\mathbb{T},Y)_{\delta}
is
\varepsilon
-

if the following holds:

1.

\mathbb{T}
satisfies the Convex Wolff Axioms at all scales with error
\delta^{-\varepsilon}
.

2.

Y
is
\delta^{\varepsilon}
-dense.

3.

\Big|\bigcup_{\mathbb{T}}Y(T)\Big|\leq\delta^{\sigma-\varepsilon}.

It is immediate from the above definitions that for every
\varepsilon,\delta_{0}>0
, there exists an
\varepsilon
-extremal collection of tubes
(\mathbb{T},Y)_{\delta}
for some
\delta\in(0,\delta_{0}]
; cf.
[
16
, Lemma 3.1]
.

Our next task is to prove an analogue of
[
16
, Proposition 3.2]
. The precise statement is as follows.

Proposition 6.2
.

For all
\varepsilon>0
, there exists
\eta>0
and
\delta_{0}>0
so that the following holds for all
\delta\in(0,\delta_{0}]
. Let
(\mathbb{T},Y)_{\delta}
be an
\eta
-extremal collection of tubes, and let
\rho\in[\delta^{1-\varepsilon},\delta^{\varepsilon}]
. Then there is a refinement
(\mathbb{T}^{\prime},Y^{\prime})_{\delta}
of
(\mathbb{T},Y)_{\delta}
and a balanced cover
(\tilde{\mathbb{T}},\tilde{Y})_{\rho}
of
(\mathbb{T}^{\prime},Y^{\prime})_{\delta}
with the following properties:

(i)

(\tilde{\mathbb{T}},\tilde{Y})_{\rho}
is
\varepsilon
-extremal.

(ii)

For each
\tilde{T}\in\tilde{\mathbb{T}}
, the unit rescaling of
(\mathbb{T}^{\prime}[\tilde{T}],Y^{\prime})_{\delta}
relative to

\varepsilon
-extremal.

(iii)

For each

,

(iv)

For each

and each

,
\#\mathbb{T}^{\prime}[\tilde{T}](p)\leq(\delta/\rho)^{2-\sigma-\varepsilon}(\#\mathbb{T}^{\prime}[\tilde{T}]).

Proof.

The proof is nearly identical to the proof of the corresponding statement in
[
16
, Proposition 3.2]
. In
[
16
]
, the first major step was to establish the existence of a cover
\mathbb{T}_{\rho}
of
\mathbb{T}
(or more accurately, a large subset
\mathbb{T}^{\prime}\subset\mathbb{T}
), with the property that both
\mathbb{T}_{\rho}
and the rescaled sets
\mathbb{T}^{\prime}[T_{\rho}]
are sticky. In our case, this step was handled by Lemma
2.13
. The second major step was to establish a shading on
\mathbb{T}_{\rho}
and a refinement of the shadings on the sets
\mathbb{T}^{\prime}[T_{\rho}]
so that not too many tubes pass through each point (and as a consequence, the corresponding collections are
\varepsilon
-extremal).

Next we will give a slightly more detailed sketch, though we refer the reader to the proof of
[
16
, Proposition 3.2]
for full details. We begin by reducing to the case where each
T\in\mathbb{T}
satisfies
|Y(T)|\geq\frac{1}{2}\delta^{\eta}|T|
. This can be done by discarding those tubes with
|Y(T)|<\frac{1}{2}\delta^{\eta}|T|
and then repeatedly pigeonholing to re-establish a collection of tubes that satisfies the Convex Wolff Axioms at every scale (with a slightly worse constant, say
\delta^{-2\eta}
); after this process, the cardinality of our collection of tubes has been reduced by at most a factor of
\delta^{\eta}
.

Next, we apply Lemma
2.13
with
\rho_{0}=\delta^{2\eta}\rho
. We get a number
\rho^{\prime}\in[\delta^{2\eta}\rho,\rho]
and a collection
\mathbb{T}_{\rho^{\prime}}
of
\rho^{\prime}
-tubes, as described by that Lemma. We may replace each
\rho^{\prime}
tube by its corresponding
\rho
-tube, and denote the resulting collection by
\tilde{\mathbb{T}}
. After pigeonholing to re-establish the property that the tubes are essentially distinct, it is still the case that
\tilde{\mathbb{T}}
satisfies the Convex Wolff Axioms at every scale with error
\delta^{-O(\eta)}
, and each of the rescaled collections
\mathbb{T}[\tilde{T}]
satisfies the Convex Wolff Axioms at every scale with error
\delta^{-O(\eta)}
.

After refining the shadings
Y(T)
, we may suppose that for each collection
\mathbb{T}[\tilde{T}]
, the same number of tubes pass through each
\delta
-cube in
E_{\mathbb{T}[\tilde{T}]}
in the sense of (
6.1
); after further pigeonholing we may suppose that this number is the same (up to a multiplicative factor of 2) for each set
\mathbb{T}[\tilde{T}]
; call this number
\mu_{\operatorname{fine}}
. Since
\mathbb{T}[\tilde{T}]
is a (rescaled) collection of tubes satisfying the Convex Wolff Axioms at every scale, if
\eta
and
\delta_{0}
are chosen sufficiently small then we must have
\mu_{\operatorname{fine}}\leq(\delta/\rho)^{2-\sigma-\varepsilon/10}(\#\mathbb{T}^{\prime}[\tilde{T}])
; this is Conclusion (iv) above.

Our next task is to define a shading
\tilde{Y}
on the tubes of
\tilde{\mathbb{T}}
. To begin, for each
\tilde{T}
we consider the union of all
\rho
-cubes
\tilde{Q}\subset\tilde{T}
that intersect
E_{\mathbb{T}[\tilde{T}]}
. After pigeonholing, we can refine this shading so that each
\rho
-cube intersects
E_{\mathbb{T}[\tilde{T}]}
in a set of roughly the same size, and the same number of tubes from
\tilde{\mathbb{T}}
pass through each cube of
E_{\tilde{\mathbb{T}}}
; call this number
\mu_{\operatorname{coarse}}
. We refine the corresponding shadings
Y(T),\ T\in\mathbb{T}
so that
Y(T)\subset\tilde{Y}(\tilde{T})
whenever
T\in\mathbb{T}[\tilde{T}]
; in particular
(\tilde{\mathbb{T}},\tilde{Y})_{\rho}
is now a balanced cover of
(\mathbb{T},Y)_{\delta}
. Note that Conclusion (iv) remains true after this procedure. Since
\tilde{\mathbb{T}}
satisfies the Convex Wolff Axioms with error
\delta^{-O(\eta)}
, if
\eta
and
\delta_{0}
are chosen sufficiently small then we must have
\mu_{\operatorname{coarse}}\leq\rho^{2-\sigma-\varepsilon/10}(\tilde{\mathbb{T}})
; this is Conclusion (iii) above.

Finally, observe that
\mu_{\operatorname{fine}}\mu_{\operatorname{coarse}}\leq\delta^{2-\sigma-\varepsilon/5}(\#\mathbb{T})
, but on the other hand since
\mathbb{T}
was
\eta
-extremal, we must have
\mu_{\operatorname{fine}}\mu_{\operatorname{coarse}}\gtrsim\delta^{2-\sigma+\eta}(\#\mathbb{T})
. We conclude that

\displaystyle(\delta/\rho)^{2-\sigma+\varepsilon/10}(\#\mathbb{T}^{\prime}[\tilde{T}])\leq

\displaystyle\mu_{\operatorname{fine}}\leq(\delta/\rho)^{2-\sigma-\varepsilon/10}(\#\mathbb{T}^{\prime}[\tilde{T}]),\quad\tilde{T}\in\tilde{\mathbb{T}},

\displaystyle\rho^{2-\sigma+\varepsilon/10}(\#\tilde{\mathbb{T}})\leq

\displaystyle\mu_{\operatorname{coarse}}\leq\rho^{2-\sigma-\varepsilon/10}(\#\tilde{\mathbb{T}}),

and in particular Conclusions (i) and (ii) are satisfied. We refer the reader to
[
16
]
for complete details.
∎

Definition 7.2

Recall that the

(In

In light of Lemma

Proof of Corollary
