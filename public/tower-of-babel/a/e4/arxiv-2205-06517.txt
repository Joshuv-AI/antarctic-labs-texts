Simulations of astrometric planet detection in Alpha Centauri by intensity interferometry
Km Nitu Rai, Subrata Sarangi, Prasenjit Saha, Soumen Basak

Abstract
Recent dynamical studies indicate that the possibility of an Earth-like planet around α \alpha\; Cen A or B should be taken seriously. Such a planet, if it exists, would perturb the orbital astrometry by < 10 µ <$10\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro}$ , which is 10 − 6 10^{-6} of the separation between the two stars. We assess the feasibility of detecting such perturbations using ground-based intensity interferometry. We simulate a dedicated setup consisting of four 40-cm telescopes equipped with photon counters and correlators with time resolution 0.1 ​ ns 0.1\,\rm ns , and a sort of matched filter implemented through an aperture mask. The astrometric error from one night of observing α \alpha\; Cen AB is ≈ 0.5 ​ mas \approx 0.5\,\rm mas . The error decreases if longer observing times and multiple spectral channels are used, as ( channels × nights ) − 1 / 2 (\hbox{channels}\times\hbox{nights})^{-1/2} .

In this paper, we propose intensity interferometry as a novel way of

\alpha\;

2
The Signal

In this section we will recall the well-known interferometric signal
of a stellar binary, explain the difficulty caused by the wide
separation of stars, and introduce the idea of a matched aperture mask to
overcome it. We will also derive the expected signal to noise ratio in
terms of instrument parameters.

2.1
The interference pattern

A featureless star of radius
R
at distance
D
has interferometric
visibility

V(\rho)=2J_{1}(\eta)/\eta\quad\hbox{where}\ \eta=2\pi\rho R/D

(1)

and
\rho
is the radial coordinate in the usual interferometric

(u,v)
plane. In a realistic situation, Limb darkening (
Klinglesmith
& Sobieski 1970
;
Claret &
Hauschildt 2003
) of the star would modify the expression.

For a binary system, taking
X,Y
as the physical separation of the
stars perpendicular to the line of sight, the visibility becomes

V(u,v)=\frac{I_{A}V_{A}(\rho)+I_{B}V_{B}(\rho)\exp\left(2\pi i\,(uX+vY)/D\right)}{I_{A}+I_{B}}

(2)

where
I_{A}
and
I_{B}
are the brightnesses of the stars A and B. By
construction,
V(\rho=0)=1
. We will not consider a third star here,
as
\alpha\;
Cen C is far-enough away to be easily excluded by the telescope.

Here, we are concerned with inferring the sky-projected
relative position
(X,Y)
of the stars, assuming the distance is
known. It is straightforward to include additional parameters to be
fitted to data (the radii of both stars, parameters of a
limb-darkening model) as done in

(
Rai
et al. 2021
, cf.)
. Such an approach is suitable
for close binaries. On the other hand, in case of
\alpha\;
Cen  the stars are
far-enough apart to study individually to determine their radii and
limb-darkening coefficients. This has been done using Michelson interferometry by

Kervella et al. 2017a
, and in principle should be possible using
intensity interferometry as well. However, for this work we assume
the stellar radii are known, and disregard limb darkening.

In intensity interferometry the visibility
V(u,v)
is a complex number and hence,
cannot be observed. Instead the square of magnitude of the visibility
V(u,v)

g(\textbf{{u}})=|V(\textbf{{u}})|^{2}\quad\hbox{where}\ \textbf{{u}}\equiv(u,v)

(3)

is observable as the excess intensity correlation. This expression,
upon substitution of Eq. (
2
) is equivalent to Eq. (1) in

Herbison-Evans et al. 1971
.

As the Earth rotates, a pair of telescopes fixed on the ground will
trace a path in
u,v
and the perpendicular coordinate
w
along the
line of sight to the source. If the separation on the ground is

(\Delta_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm E},\Delta_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm N},\Delta_{\rm up})
then
u,v,w
are given by the slightly
complicated rotation

\begin{pmatrix}u\\
v\\
w\end{pmatrix}=\frac{1}{\lambda}R_{1}(\delta)\,R_{2}(h)\,R_{1}(-l)\begin{pmatrix}\Delta_{\scriptscriptstyle{\rm E}}\\
\Delta_{\scriptscriptstyle{\rm N}}\\
\Delta_{\rm up}\end{pmatrix}

(4)

where
l
is the latitude of the setup,
\delta
is the declination
and
h
is the hour angle of the source, while

R_{1}(\delta)=\begin{pmatrix}1&0&0\\
0&\cos\delta&-\sin\delta\\
0&\sin\delta&\cos\delta\end{pmatrix}

(5)

and similarly
R_{1}(-l)

R_{2}(h)=\begin{pmatrix}\cos h&0&\sin h\\
0&1&0\\
-\sin h&0&\cos h\end{pmatrix}

(6)

are the usual rotation matrices. The
w
coordinate does not change
the interference pattern, but it produces a time delay of
\lambda w/c
.

Starspots in either or both of the binary stars present an unknown
source of astrometric noise.
Catanzarite
et al. 2008

estimate that the effect would not severely affect astrometric
planet detection at Earth masses. The largest sunspots would produce
a detectable astrometric signal
(
Hatzes 2002
, cf.)
but
the effect would have a very different time dependence than orbital
perturbations. Therefore, it is ignored in this work.

A masked aperture

The signal (
3
) applies if

(a)

the time resolution (say
\Delta t
) is much finer than the
coherence time (say
\Delta\tau
), and

(b)

the interference pattern does not vary significantly over
the light bucket.

Of these, (a) is never true in practice, with
\Delta t

\Delta\tau

(b), on the other hand, does hold in previous applications, but not
for
\alpha\;
Cen, because the fringes are only a few centimetres apart.
Hence we have to account for the finite aperture of the light bucket.

Now, the objective plane of a telescope is obviously perpendicular to
the line of sight, and hence parallel to the
(u,v)
plane. We can
therefore express the aperture of a telescope as
W(\textbf{{u}})
. The
finite-aperture signal is then
g(\textbf{{u}})
convolved with the apertures
of both telescopes. Thus we have

g_{\scriptscriptstyle W}(\textbf{{u}})=\frac{\int g(\textbf{{u}}+\textbf{{u}}_{2}-\textbf{{u}}_{1})\,W(\textbf{{u}}_{1})\,W(\textbf{{u}}_{2})\;d^{2}\textbf{{u}}_{1}\,d^{2}\textbf{{u}}_{2}}{\int W(\textbf{{u}}_{1})\,W(\textbf{{u}}_{2})\;d^{2}\textbf{{u}}_{1}\,d^{2}\textbf{{u}}_{2}}

(7)

for the finite aperture, and then

g^{\rm obs}=\frac{\Delta\tau}{\Delta t}\,g_{\scriptscriptstyle W}(\textbf{{u}})

(8)

will be the measured intensity correlation.

The convolution (
7
) suggests that making
W(\textbf{{u}})

similar to the fringe pattern itself will prevent the fringes from being
washed out. Accordingly we choose

W(\textbf{{u}})=\begin{cases}\cos^{2}\Big((\pi\lambda/b)\,\textbf{{u}}\cdot\hat{\textbf{{u}}}_{0}\Big)&\text{for}\ \rho\lambda<\tilde{a}\\
0&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

(9)

which looks complicated, but really just describes a set of sinusoidal
stripes inside a circle. Here
\tilde{a}
denotes the radius of the telescope
aperture,
\hat{\textbf{{u}}}_{0}
is a unit vector and hence, specifies the orientation of the stripes, and
b
is
the stripes width (the length of a stripe will vary according to its
position on aperture).

Computation of the convolution (
7
) is done on a

1024\times 1024
grid.

Figs.
1
and
2
illustrate the dependence
of the signal
g_{\scriptscriptstyle W}(\textbf{{u}})
on the stripe width
b
and the orientation

\hat{\textbf{{u}}}_{0}

600\text{\,}\mathrm{nm}

apparent in

for stripe width
b=$2.25\text{\,}\mathrm{cm}$

and
\hat{\textbf{{u}}}_{0}
oriented at
104.3^{\circ}
with respect to
u
. The
signal degrades noticeably when
b
and
\hat{\textbf{{u}}}_{0}
are even a few percent
from the optimal values. Hence, advance knowledge of the current
relative positions of the stars to
\sim 1\%
is desired, in order to
optimise the aperture mask. This is unlikely to be a problem in
practice for a wide binary such as the
\alpha\;
Cen AB.

A possible additional complication, depending on the type of
telescope mounting, is that the mask may need to be rotated as the
telescope tracks the target across the sky, to compensate for field
rotation. Equatorial mounting would eliminate this issue.

2.3
Signal to Noise

In a resolution time
\Delta t
the number of HBT correlated photon
pairs will be

S=(A\Phi)^{2}\;g_{\scriptscriptstyle W}(\textbf{{u}})\times\frac{\Delta t}{\Delta\tau}

(10)

where

A=\int W(\textbf{{u}})\;d^{2}\textbf{{u}}

(11)

is the effective area of each telescope,
\Phi
is the spectral photon
density, and
\Delta\tau
is the coherence time.

The coherence time is the reciprocal of the frequency bandwidth, thus

1/\Delta\nu=\lambda^{2}/(c\Delta\lambda)
. For the assumed observing
wavelength of
600\text{\,}\mathrm{nm}
the wave period is

\lambda/c=$2.0\text{\,}\mathrm{fs}$
. No specific
\Delta\lambda
is
assumed, but narrow bandwidths are generally desirable. A bandwidth
of
\Delta\lambda=$1\text{\,}\mathrm{nm}$
gives

\Delta\tau=$1.2\text{\,}\mathrm{ps}$
. In practice,
\Delta t
will be
orders of magnitude larger than
\Delta\tau
. The photon spectral
density denotes the photons per unit area in a coherence time

\Delta\tau
in one polarisation, which for a thermal source is given
by

\Phi=\frac{(\nu/c)^{2}}{e^{z}-1}\times\Delta\Omega\qquad z=\frac{h\nu}{kT}

(12)

where
\Delta\Omega
is the solid-angle area of the source, and
T
is
the effective temperature. The spectral energy flux (measured in Jy) is

2h\nu\Phi
.

In an interval
\Delta t
, the noise due to chance coincidences will
be

N=A\Phi\times\frac{\Delta t}{\Delta\tau}\,.

(13)

Dividing equations (
10
) and (
13
) gives the
signal to noise of

{\rm SNR}_{\Delta t}=A\Phi\;g_{\scriptscriptstyle W}(\textbf{{u}})

(14)

in one time slice
\Delta t
. We assume
\Delta t=$100\text{\,}\mathrm{ps}$
.

In practice, the correlation would be done in hardware and averaged
over an
t_{\rm av}
during which
u
can be assumed constant. We
take
t_{\rm av}=$1\text{\,}\mathrm{s}$
. This constitutes a data point for
fitting. The SNR of such a data point will be

{\rm SNR}_{t_{\rm av}}=A\Phi\;g_{\scriptscriptstyle W}(\textbf{{u}})\;(t_{\rm av}/\Delta t)^{1/2}\,.

(15)

For simulations, one can take the signal to be
g_{\scriptscriptstyle W}
and the noise to be

N=(\Delta t/t_{\rm av})^{1/2}(A\Phi)^{-1}\,.

(16)

It is noted that
g_{\scriptscriptstyle W}(\textbf{{u}})
is several times smaller than
g(\textbf{{u}})
.
That is, the aperture mask sacrifices a lot of signal. Let us
therefore consider what the SNR would be, if small light buckets
without aperture masking were used. For a given effective
temperature,
\Phi\propto(R/D)^{2}
, where
R
is the radius of the
larger star and
D
the distance. If
a
is the separation between
the stars, the width of the interference fringes will be
\propto D/a
.
Without aperture masking, the mirror diameter must to kept smaller than
the fringe width, implying
A\propto(D/a)^{2}
. This would give

{\rm SNR}\propto(R/a)^{2}
, which becomes hopelessly small for wide binaries.
Pointing error is not considered.

3.1
The Orbital Integration

The equations of motion of an
N
-body system with masses
m_{i}

(
i=0,1,\ldots,N-1
) in terms of the positions
\textbf{{X}}_{i}
and velocities
\textbf{{V}}_{\!i}

in an inertial reference frame are given by:

\ddot{\textbf{{X}}_{i}}\equiv\dot{\textbf{{V}}_{\!i}}=-\sum_{j=0;j\neq i}^{N-1}Gm_{j}\frac{\textbf{{X}}_{i}-\textbf{{X}}_{j}}{|\textbf{{X}}_{i}-\textbf{{X}}_{j}|^{\hbox to0.0pt{$\scriptstyle 3$\hss}}}

(17)

where
G
is the gravitational constant.

We use the well known Leapfrog algorithm, also otherwise known as
Drift-Kick-Drift, or the Verlet method, to solve for the orbits of
this system. Under this algorithm, the set of phase space vectors

\{(\textbf{{X}}_{i}(t),\textbf{{V}}_{\!i}(t)),i=0,1,\ldots,N-1\}
are advanced by the following set
of steps:

\displaystyle\textbf{{X}}_{i}^{\rm int}

\displaystyle=\textbf{{X}}_{i}^{\rm old}+\ \textbf{{V}}_{\!i}^{\rm old}\times{\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}t_{\rm step}

(18)

\displaystyle\textbf{{V}}_{\!i}^{\rm new}

\displaystyle=\textbf{{V}}_{\!i}^{\rm old}-{\sum_{j=0,j\neq i}^{N-1}Gm_{j}}\frac{\textbf{{X}}_{i}^{\rm int}-\textbf{{X}}_{j}^{\rm int}}{|\textbf{{X}}_{i}^{\rm int}-\textbf{{X}}_{j}^{\rm int}|^{\hbox to0.0pt{$\scriptstyle 3$\hss}}}\,\times t_{\rm step}

\displaystyle\textbf{{X}}_{i}^{\rm new}

\displaystyle=\textbf{{X}}_{i}^{\rm int}+\ \textbf{{V}}_{\!i}^{\rm new}\times{\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}t_{\rm step}.

The first step (Drift) in equation (
18
) produces an interim position vector
\textbf{{X}}_{i}^{\rm int}
using the present values of the position vector
\textbf{{X}}_{i}^{\rm old}
and the velocity vector
\textbf{{V}}_{\!i}^{\rm old}
over half the chosen time step,
i.e.,

\frac{1}{2}t_{\rm step}
. The second step (Kick) is produced by generating the next set of velocity vectors
\textbf{{V}}_{\!i}^{\rm new}
over a full time step
t_{\rm step}
by using the accelerations evaluated at the interim position vectors
\textbf{{X}}_{i}^{\rm int}
. Finally, in the last step (a second Drift), the next set of position vectors
\textbf{{X}}_{i}^{\rm new}
are evaluated with the remaining half a time step
\frac{1}{2}t_{\rm step}
using the set of velocity vectors
\textbf{{V}}_{\!i}^{\rm new}
at the next time step.
More efficient algorithms are available
(
Mikkola 2020
)
, but Leapfrog is sufficient for our purposes. It is known to conserve momentum and angular momentum exactly. The total energy has an oscillatory variation proportional to
(t_{\rm step})^{2}
and no secular variation.

Light-second units have been used for the computation. That is done
by making the code variables
\textbf{{X}}_{i}/c
and
\textbf{{V}}_{\!i}/c
, and mass parameters in
the program
Gm_{i}/c^{3}
. The time step is
t_{\rm step}=$1\text{\times}{10}^{6}\text{\,}\mathrm{s}$
.

Initial conditions

Orbital elements such as in Table

(

, for a

.

First we solve Kepler’s equation

\psi-e\sin\psi=\frac{2\pi}{P}(t-t_{\rm peri})

(19)

for the eccentric anomaly
\psi
with
t_{\rm peri}
being the epoch of periastron of the system. We then use the value of
\psi
to
compute the position in the orbital plane, and then we apply
three rotations

\begin{pmatrix}x\\
y\\
z\end{pmatrix}=a\times R_{3}(\Omega)\,R_{1}(\iota)\,R_{3}(\omega)\begin{pmatrix}\cos\psi-e\\
\sqrt{1-e^{2}}\sin\psi\\
0\end{pmatrix}

(20)

so as to orient the coordinates conveniently with
z
along the line
of sight. In equation (
20
), the set of parameters {
a
,
e
,
\iota
,
\Omega
and
\omega
} represent the standard Keplerian orbit elements noted in table
1
. The
R_{1}
matrices are as in equation (
5
), and
R_{3}
is
given by cyclically permuting the indices. The analogous expression
for velocities follows by noting

\frac{d\textbf{{x}}}{dt}=\frac{2\pi}{P}\frac{d\textbf{{x}}}{d\psi}

(21)

which itself follows from equation (
19
).

Applying the above procedure to the orbital elements, gives us three
positions
\textbf{{x}}_{1},\textbf{{x}}_{2},\textbf{{x}}_{3}
and three corresponding
velocities. These are two-body coordinates and velocities, and
correspond to pairs of bodies, not individual bodies. We now
interpret these as Jacobi variables. That is,
\textbf{{x}}_{1}
is
considered as the position of the second body (the fictitious planet)
with respect to the first body (
\alpha\;
Cen A),
\textbf{{x}}_{2}
is taken from
the third body (
\alpha\;
Cen B) to the barycentre of the earlier bodies, and
so on. Inertial coordinates
\textbf{{X}}_{i}^{I}
are then given by

\displaystyle\textbf{{X}}_{0}^{I}

We now simulate the measurement of the sky-projected relative position

(X,Y)
of
\alpha\;
Cen A and B from intensity interferometry at an arbitrary
epoch in 2023, at various SNR levels. We will then compare the errors
on such a measurement with the astrometric wobble in
(X,Y)
due to a
planet with different assumed masses. The distance to the system is
assumed known.

A full analysis pipeline would fit for the masses and initial
conditions as
X(t),Y(t)
evolve, rather than for a single-epoch

X,Y
. From astrometric data alone, the inferred parameters will have
a degeneracy depending on the assumed value of
D

kinematic data (not necessarily at very high resolution) would allow

D
to be measured as well. A many-parameter fit combining
astrometric and kinematic data would be rather complicated, but is not
expected to be intractable. Indeed, an early example of this type of
fit appears already in
Herbison-Evans et al. 1971
.

In this work, however, our main aim is simply to estimate the
uncertainty in
X
and
Y
, and how it depends on the SNR. Thus we do
not include the orbital variation of
X
and
Y
in the astrometric
fitting process. We consider a single-epoch
X,Y
even though the
observing campaign runs over a long time.

4.5
Inferring the host star

The observable signature of a planet around one of
\alpha\;
Cen A or B,
would be that the relative vector between the two stars exhibits an
approximately Keplerian orbit with (say) semi-major axis
a_{w}
and
angular frequency
\omega
. (There will be an uncertainty in
a_{w}

because the inclination is not known a priori, but let us suppose the
inclination is adequately constrained by stability arguments.) These
observable quantities are given by

a_{w}=\frac{m}{M_{1}}a_{p}

(29)

and

\omega^{2}=\frac{G(M_{1}+m)}{a_{p}^{3}}

(30)

where
m
denotes the mass of the planet,
M_{1}
that of the host star,
and
a_{p}
the orbital semi-major axis. Combining these we have

Gm(1+m/M_{1})^{1/3}=(GM_{1}\omega)^{2/3}\,a_{w}

(31)

which determines the mass of the planet provided
M_{1}
is known. The
trouble is, while the masses of
\alpha\;
Cen A and B are known, it is not
known which of them is
M_{1}
, which leaves a curious degeneracy in

m
.

Nevertheless, it seems plausible that the true host star could be
inferred, because of the perturbations of the other star. The
apparent orbit would show three-body perturbations from Keplerian.
The strength of the perturbations would depend on
a_{p}/r_{\rm tid}
where

r_{\rm tid}
is a characteristic scale for tidal perturbations. Let us
put

r_{\rm tid}=(M_{1}/M_{2})^{1/3}\,r_{\scriptscriptstyle\!AB}

(32)

where
r_{\scriptscriptstyle\!AB}
is the current distance of the other star and
M_{2}
is
its mass. The expression for
r_{\rm tid}
is simply the usual Hill radius,
apart from a factor of
3^{1/3}
. Combining with
equations (
29
) and (
30
) and neglecting the tiny
term
m/M_{1}
, we have

\frac{a_{p}}{r_{\rm tid}}=\left(\frac{GM_{2}}{\omega^{2}}\right)^{1/3}\frac{1}{r_{\scriptscriptstyle\!AB}}

(33)

for the amplitude of perturbations.

The above considerations suggest that determining which star is the
host from three-body perturbations would be much harder still than
detecting a planet via astrometry, if indeed a planet is present, but
not hopeless.

5
DISCUSSION

Astrometry to accuracies beyond what is currently available would be a
means to detect or rule out a habitable planet in the
\alpha\;
Cen system.
This paper studies intensity interferometry as a possible means to
this end. As usual with intensity interferometry, high-end photon
detectors and correlators are required, and accumulating enough SNR
takes a long time. But set against these is the advantage of a very
simple optical setup, requiring only amateur-grade telescope and no
special infrastructure, because coherence between optical elements is
not required.

Herbison-Evans et al. 1971
used intensity interferometry for
astrometry of
\alpha\,
Virginis (Spica). Recently in

Rai
et al. 2021
an extension to measure the radii of
both stars together has been suggested. But this will not work for wide
binary like
\alpha\;
Cen. As explained in Section
2
the
requirement that the light buckets are smaller than the fringe width
implies that
{\rm SNR}\propto(R/a)^{2}
where
a
is the separation
between the stars and
R
is the radius of the larger star. For Spica

a/R\sim 10
whereas for
\alpha\;
Cen
a/R>10^{3}
.

To evade this problem, we suggest a sort of matched filter in the form
of a mask placed on the objective. For large apertures, the mask
could also be placed in secondary optics.

With a declination of
-60^{\circ}\,50^{\prime}

\alpha\;
Cen is observable year-round
from latitudes further South than
30^{\circ}\,
S. The

1.8\text{\,}\mathrm{m}
-MOA telescope
(
Hearnshaw
et al. 2006
, see
e.g.,)
at Mt. John Observatory in New Zealand
can observe
\alpha\;
Cen throughout the year, but at present is not set up
for interferometry. Hence we consider a different scenario, with
small portable telescopes at a nearby location, but dedicated to

\alpha\;
Cen.
Horch et al. 2022
have recently demonstrated
intensity interferometry with movable 60 cm telescopes,
including multi-channel correlation, so a basis for our assumed setup
is already available. Adding aperture masks with appropriate stripe
width and orientations to do orbital astrometry by

<$10\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro}$
levels, is still a problem to be addressed; 3D printing may be a route to achieve this goal.
