Abstract




Bayesian Inference for the Multinomial Probit Model under Gaussian Prior Distribution

Abstract

Multinomial probit (
mnp
) models are fundamental and widely-applied regression models for categorical data.

[
1
]
proved that the class of unified skew-normal distributions is conjugate to several
mnp
sampling models.
This allows to develop Monte Carlo samplers and accurate variational methods to perform Bayesian inference.
In this paper, we adapt the above-mentioned results for a popular special case: the discrete-choice
mnp
model under zero-mean and independent Gaussian priors.
This allows to obtain simplified expressions for the parameters of the posterior distribution and an alternative derivation for the variational algorithm that gives a novel understanding of the fundamental results in
[
1
]
as well as computational advantages in our special settings.

Key Words
:
Multinomial Probit Model, Variational Inference, Unified Skew-Normal Distribution, Bayesian inference, Categorical Data, Classification

Introduction

Multinomial probit (
mnp
) models constitute a fundamental tool for categorical data regression, thanks to their interpretability and flexibility
[
2
]
.
Originally introduced by
[
3
]
to avoid the restrictive assumption of the
independence of irrelevant alternatives
typical of multinomial logit models, such models have faced the growth of many different specifications.
Among them, we consider the Bayesian formulation of the discrete choice
mnp
model with class-specific effects
[
4
]
, under a zero-mean and independent Gaussian prior for the parameters, adapting the results of
[
1
]
obtained under more general prior specifications and for a wider range of models.
In such a construction, originally developed in the econometrics literature, to each possible choice (or class)
\ell=1,\ldots,L
that individual
i=1,\ldots,n
faces, a corresponding random latent utility
z_{i\ell}={\bf x}_{i}^{\intercal}{\bm{\beta}}_{\ell}+\epsilon_{i\ell}
is associated, where
{\bf x}_{i}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{p}
is the covariate vector for observation
i
,
{\bm{\beta}}_{\ell}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{p}
is the class-specific vector of the covariate effects and
\bm{\epsilon}_{i}=(\epsilon_{i1},\ldots,\epsilon_{iL})^{\intercal}\sim\textsc{n}_{L}\left(\bm{0},{\bm{\Sigma}}\right)
, independently across units
i=1,\ldots,n
.
Note that the error terms for different alternatives can be correlated, since
{\bm{\Sigma}}
, which is assumed to be known, is not necessarily diagonal.
Among all the possible choices
1,\ldots,L
, individual
i
chooses the one giving her the maximal utility, meaning that
y_{i}=\ell
if and only if
z_{\ell}=\max\{z_{1},\ldots,z_{L}\}
.
Thus, for each
i=1,\ldots,n
, independently

\begin{split}\Pr(y_{i}=\ell\mid{\bm{\beta}}_{1},\ldots,{\bm{\beta}}_{L},{\bf x}_{i})&=\Pr(z_{i\ell}>z_{ik},\forall k\neq\ell)\\
&=\Pr({\bf x}^{\intercal}_{i}{\bm{\beta}}_{\ell}+\varepsilon_{i\ell}>{\bf x}^{\intercal}_{i}{\bm{\beta}}_{k}+\varepsilon_{ik},\forall k\neq\ell).\end{split}

(1)

Since in (
1
) only pairwise differences between the parameters matter, we set
{\bm{\beta}}_{L}=\bm{0}
for identifiability purposes.
In order to perform Bayesian inference, we complete the model by specifying a multivariate Gaussian prior distribution for the vector of parameters
{\bm{\beta}}=\left({\bm{\beta}}_{1}^{\intercal},\ldots,{\bm{\beta}}_{L-1}^{\intercal}\right)^{\intercal}
centered in zero, with independent and homoscedastic components. Thus,

{\bm{\beta}}\sim\textsc{n}_{p(L-1)}\left(\bm{0},\nu^{2}{\bf I}_{p(L-1)}\right).

(2)

The more general results under a unified skew-normal (
sun
) prior have been developed in
[
1
]
for a broader class of
mnp
models.
In fact, it is shown that in that case the posterior belongs again to the class of
sun
distributions, with updated dimensionalities and parameters. This allows to perform posterior inference via i.i.d. samples in small-to-moderate
n
settings, while a blocked variational procedure is developed to avoid the computational bottlenecks that one may encounter in large
n
scenarios.
The prior specification (
2
), however, constitutes a popular choice in case there are no reasons to
a priori
assume any dependence between the parameters or asymmetry in their distribution, and it is worth a separate treatment as it allows the simplification of the expression of some important parameters in the posterior distribution and the derivation of an alternative proof for the variational algorithm that can have computational advantages.
These two important aspects represent the main focus of the present article and will be the focus of Sections
2
and
3
below, while Section
4
is dedicated to the discussion of possible future research directions.

Posterior inference via Monte Carlo samples

Recently,
[

We specify here results in Section 2.2 in

Before doing that, we briefly recap the definition and the main properties of the

Further details can be found, for instance, in

A random vector
{\bm{\beta}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{q}
has
sun
distribution,
{\bm{\beta}}\sim\mbox{{sun}}_{q,h}({\bm{\xi}},{\bm{\Omega}},{\bm{\Delta}},{\bm{\gamma}},{\bm{\Gamma}})
, if its density function
p({\bm{\beta}})
can be expressed as

p({\bm{\beta}})=\phi_{q}({\bm{\beta}}-{\bm{\xi}};{\bm{\Omega}})\frac{\Phi_{h}[{\bm{\gamma}}+{\bm{\Delta}}^{\intercal}\bar{{\bm{\Omega}}}^{-1}{\bm{\omega}}^{-1}({\bm{\beta}}-{\bm{\xi}});{\bm{\Gamma}}{-}{\bm{\Delta}}^{\intercal}\bar{{\bm{\Omega}}}^{-1}{\bm{\Delta}}]}{\Phi_{h}({\bm{\gamma}};{\bm{\Gamma}})},

where the covariance matrix
{\bm{\Omega}}
of the Gaussian density
\phi_{q}({\bm{\beta}}-{\bm{\xi}};{\bm{\Omega}})
can be decomposed as
{\bm{\Omega}}={\bm{\omega}}\bar{{\bm{\Omega}}}{\bm{\omega}}
, i.e. by rescaling the correlation matrix
\bar{{\bm{\Omega}}}
via the diagonal scale matrix
{\bm{\omega}}=({\bm{\Omega}}\odot{\bf I}_{q})^{1/2}
, with
\odot
denoting the element-wise Hadamard product.
Moreover,
\Phi_{h}({\bf u};{\bf W})
denotes the cumulative distribution function of a
\textsc{n}_{h}(\bm{0},{\bf W})
evaluated at
{\bf u}
.
The following additive characterization constitutes a fundamental property to further understand the role of the parameters and to develop an i.i.d. sampler. If
{\bm{\beta}}\sim\textsc{sun}_{q,h}({\bm{\xi}},{\bm{\Omega}},{\bm{\Delta}},{\bm{\gamma}},{\bm{\Gamma}})
, then
{\bm{\beta}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\small d}}}{{=}}{\bm{\xi}}+{\bm{\omega}}({\bf V}_{0}+{\bm{\Delta}}{\bm{\Gamma}}^{-1}{\bf V}_{1})
, with

{\bf V}_{0}\sim\textsc{n}_{q}(\bm{0},\bar{{\bm{\Omega}}}-{\bm{\Delta}}{\bm{\Gamma}}^{-1}{\bm{\Delta}}^{\intercal}),\ {\bf V}_{1}\sim\textsc{tn}_{h}(\bm{0},{\bm{\Gamma}};A_{-{\bm{\gamma}}}),

where
A_{-{\bm{\gamma}}}=\left\{{\bf a}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{h}\colon a_{i}\geq-\gamma_{i}\ \forall i\right\}
and
\textsc{tn}_{h}({\bf m},{\bf W};A)
denotes the
h
-variate normal distribution with mean
{\bf m}
and covariance matrix
{\bf W}
, truncated in the region
A
.

Partially-factorized blocked mean-field approximation (PFM-B)

Basic manipulations of the posterior (
3
) show that
p({\bm{\beta}}\mid{\bf y},{\bf X})\propto p({\bm{\beta}})\cdot\Pr[\bar{{\bf z}}>\bm{0}\mid{\bm{\beta}},\bar{{\bf X}}]
, where
\bar{{\bf z}}=\left(\bar{{\bf z}}_{1},\ldots,\bar{{\bf z}}_{n}\right)^{\intercal}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n(L-1)}
and
\bar{{\bf z}}\mid{\bm{\beta}},\bar{{\bf X}}\sim\textsc{n}_{n(L-1)}\left(\bar{{\bf X}}{\bm{\beta}},{\bm{\Lambda}}\right)
.
Thus,
p({\bm{\beta}}\mid{\bf y},{\bf X})
can be seen as the marginal posterior distribution of the dual model

\displaystyle{\bm{\beta}}

\displaystyle\sim\textsc{n}_{p(L-1)}\left(\bm{0},\nu^{2}{\bf I}_{p(L-1)}\right)

(4a)

\displaystyle\bar{{\bf z}}_{i}\mid{\bm{\beta}},\bar{{\bf X}}

\displaystyle\overset{ind}{\sim}\textsc{n}_{L-1}\left(\bar{{\bf X}}_{[i]}{\bm{\beta}},{\bm{\Lambda}}_{[ii]}\right),\hskip 9.24994pti=1,\ldots,n,

(4b)

\displaystyle\bar{{\bf y}}_{i}

\displaystyle=\mathbbm{1}\left[\bar{{\bf z}}_{i}>\bm{0}\right],\hskip 9.24994pt\hskip 9.24994pt\hskip 9.24994pt\hskip 9.24994pt\hskip 9.24994pti=1,\ldots,n,

(4c)

in which one observes
\bar{{\bf y}}_{i}=\left(1,\ldots,1\right)^{\intercal}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{L-1}
for
i=1,\ldots,n
and
\mathbbm{1}[\cdot]
in (
4c

Thus, it holds
p({\bm{\beta}}\mid{\bf y},{\bf X})=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n(L-1)}}p({\bm{\beta}},\bar{{\bf z}}\mid\bar{{\bf y}},\bar{{\bf X}})d\bar{{\bf z}}
, with
\bar{{\bf y}}=\left(\bar{{\bf y}}_{1},\ldots,\bar{{\bf y}}_{n}\right)^{\intercal}
an
n(L-1)

Since direct sampling from
p({\bm{\beta}}\mid{\bf y},{\bf X})
can run into computational issues when
n(L-1)
is large due to step 2 in Algorithm
1

[
7

q^{*}({\bm{\beta}},\bar{{\bf z}})
in a given class of tractable density functions
\mathcal{Q}
.
This optimal solution is the minimizer of the Kullback-Leibler divergence
[
8
]

\textsc{kl}\left[q({\bm{\beta}},\bar{{\bf z}})\mid\mid p({\bm{\beta}},\bar{{\bf z}}\mid\bar{{\bf y}},\bar{{\bf X}})\right]={\mathbb{E}}_{q({\bm{\beta}},\bar{{\bf z}})}\left[\log\left(q({\bm{\beta}},\bar{{\bf z}})/p({\bm{\beta}},\bar{{\bf z}}\mid\bar{{\bf y}},\bar{{\bf X}})\right)\right]
.

In order to get a tractable approximation, but retain as much structure of the posterior distribution as possible, following
[
1
]
, we take
\mathcal{Q}=\mathcal{Q}_{\textsc{pfm-b}}=\{q({\bm{\beta}},\bar{{\bf z}}):q({\bm{\beta}},\bar{{\bf z}})=q({\bm{\beta}}\mid\bar{{\bf z}})\prod_{i=1}^{N}q(\bar{{\bf z}}_{i})\}
.
This class of densities leverages on the partially-factorized mean field approximation developed for the classical probit model in
[
9
]
, but allows to maintain the intra-correlations between the components of the
\bar{{\bf z}}_{i}
’s,
i=1,\ldots,n
, while enforcing the inter-correlations between them to be zero.
A first result about the optimal approximating density is obtained by the chain rule for the
kl

\textsc{kl}[q({\bm{\beta}},\bar{{\bf z}})||p({\bm{\beta}},\bar{{\bf z}}\mid\bar{{\bf y}},\bar{{\bf X}})]=\textsc{kl}[q(\bar{{\bf z}})||p(\bar{{\bf z}}\mid\bar{{\bf y}},\bar{{\bf X}})]+\mathbb{E}_{q(\bar{{\bf z}})}\{\textsc{kl}[q({\bm{\beta}}\mid\bar{{\bf z}})||p({\bm{\beta}}\mid\bar{{\bf z}},\bar{{\bf y}},\bar{{\bf X}})]\}
.
Thus, whatever is the value for
q(\bar{{\bf z}})
, the second summand is zero, and hence minimal, if and only if, calling
{\bf V}=(\nu^{-2}{\bf I}_{p(L-1)}+\bar{{\bf X}}^{\intercal}{\bm{\Lambda}}^{-1}\bar{{\bf X}})^{-1}
,

q({\bm{\beta}}\mid\bar{{\bf z}})=q^{*}({\bm{\beta}}\mid\bar{{\bf z}})=p({\bm{\beta}}\mid\bar{{\bf z}},\bar{{\bf y}},\bar{{\bf X}})=\phi_{p(L-1)}({\bm{\beta}}-{\bf V}\bar{{\bf X}}^{\intercal}{\bm{\Lambda}}^{-1}\bar{{\bf z}};{\bf V}),

which follows by standard properties of multivariate normals applied to model (
4

This means that in order to find the
kl

q^{*}({\bm{\beta}},\bar{{\bf z}})=q^{*}({\bm{\beta}}\mid\bar{{\bf z}})\prod_{i=1}^{N}q^{*}(\bar{{\bf z}}_{i})
in
\mathcal{Q}_{\textsc{pfm-b}}
, we just have to find
q^{*}(\bar{{\bf z}})=\prod_{i=1}^{N}q^{*}(\bar{{\bf z}}_{i})
minimizing
\textsc{kl}[q(\bar{{\bf z}})||p(\bar{{\bf z}}\mid\bar{{\bf y}},\bar{{\bf X}})]

See
[
1

4

Discussion

This article provides a novel derivation for the
pfm-b
variational method for a relevant class of
mnp
models with independent Gaussian priors.
As shown in Section
3
, this provides a novel understanding of the results in
[
1
]
as well as computational advantages in our special settings.
Future works include deriving results for the posterior also for the dynamic
mnp
, which allows to model sequential decisions in the time series framework.
In such a way, we plan to extend closed-form expressions, and the related samplers, for the filtering, predictive and smoothing distributions of multivariate dynamic probit models for binary time series in
[
10
]
.
Finally, the variational Bayes approach in Section
3
can be generalized to perform inference for the smoothing distribution of the dynamic
mnp
extending the results in
[
11
]
.
