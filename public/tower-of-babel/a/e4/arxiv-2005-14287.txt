Mechanobiology predicts raft formations triggered by ligand-receptor activity across the cell membrane
A. R. Carotenuto, L. Lunghi, V. Piccolo, M. Babaei, K. Dayal, N. M. Pugno, M. Zingales, L. Deseri, M. Fraldi

Abstract
Clustering of ligand-binding receptors of different types on thickened isles of the cell membrane, namely lipid rafts, is an experimentally observed phenomenon. Although its influence on cell’s response is deeply investigated, the role of the coupling between mechanical processes and multiphysics involving the active receptors and the surrounding lipid membrane during ligand-binding has not yet been understood. Specifically, the focus of this work is on G-protein-coupled receptors (GPCRs), the widest group of transmembrane proteins in animals, which regulate specific cell processes through chemical signalling pathways involving a synergistic balance between the cyclic Adenosine Monophosphate (cAMP) produced by active GPCRs in the intracellular environment and its efflux, mediated by the Multidrug Resistance Proteins (MRPs) transporters. This paper develops a multiphysics approach based on the interplay among energetics, multiscale geometrical changes and mass balance of species, i.e. active GPCRs and MRPs, including diffusion and kinetics of binding and unbinding. Because the obtained energy depends upon both the kinematics and the changes of species densities, balance of mass and of linear momentum are coupled and govern the space-time evolution of the cell membrane. The mechanobiology involving remodelling and change of lipid ordering of the cell membrane allows to predict dynamics of transporters and active receptors –in full agreement with experimentally observed cAMP levels– and how the latter trigger rafts formation and cluster on such sites. Within the current scientific debate on Severe Acute Respiratory Syndrome CoronaVirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2) and on the basis of the ascertained fact that lipid rafts often serve as an entry port for viruses, it is felt that approaches accounting for strong coupling among mechanobiological aspects could even turn helpful in better understanding membrane-mediated phenomena such as COVID-19 virus-cell interaction.

3
The biomechanical model

The mechanobiology of lipid raft formation is investigated by accounting for deformation localization phenomena during binding of receptors, namely GPCRs compounds, with the incoming ligand. To this aim, the cell membrane is modelled as a thin hyperelastic soft body on which
active receptors
with density
\displaystyle\xi
and
transporters
(i.e. MRPs-Multidrug Resistant Proteins) with density
\displaystyle\zeta
mediate the communication between the extra-cellular and the intra-cellular environment. Receptor-Ligand (RL) compounds and MRPs directly initiate different signaling pathways involved in different cell processes, regulated by the transport and compartmentation of chemicals such as cyclic-AMP. Measuring cAMP concentrations then allows to indirectly estimate the amount of surface proteins involved, as explained in Section
2
. There the experiments used to trace the levels of receptors and transporters are described. In the proposed model, the cell membrane is characterized according to the constitutive framework proposed by Deseri and Zurlo in
[
55
,
54
,
56
]
. Here, this is explicitly coupled with the dynamics of GPCRs and MRPs activation, apt to describe the growth of active domains on the membrane surface by means of flow rules that include the receptor-transporter interplay. This dynamics is modelled according to a theory of interspecific growth mechanics recently proposed by Fraldi and Carotenuto in
[
81
,
82
]
and applied to model the growth of solid tumors. Here, the same framework is adapted to model the kinetics and diffusion of transmembrane proteins triggered by ligand-binding and mediated by the membrane elasticity. Upon utilizing this approach, the evolution equations for the species provide Lotka-Volterra-inspired interspecific terms aimed to model the response of GPCRs to affine ligands and the growth of RL complex. The latter stimulate the activation of MRPs through the intracellular cAMP production. In turn, it is observed a localization of islands of active receptors and transporters on thickened domains of the cell membrane, known as lipid rafts. Such remodelling phenomenon is then governed by kinetics, diffusion and energetics, where binding/unbinding events turn out to be strongly coupled to the mechanical response of the plasma membrane through the energetics. Indeed, the mechanical interaction between the membrane and the active proteins during their remodelling and the conformational changes of their domains can be traced through the growth of their corresponding fractions.

3.2

A thermodynamically consistent framework is provided in this section with the aim of evaluating the chemo-mechanical coupling between transmembrane proteins and membrane elasticity. To this end, a total Helmoltz free energy per unit volume is introduced, say
\displaystyle W^{*}

3.2.1
Mass conservation equations and interspecific terms

The one-dimensional balance of mass for each constituents can be written as

\dot{n}_{i}+M_{i,x}=\Gamma_{i}

(3.24)

where the dot notation denotes the material time derivative,
\displaystyle M_{i}
describes the material species flux and
\displaystyle\Gamma_{i}
is the rate term describing the evolutionary behavior of each species. This is done by taking into account intrinsic rates as well as mutual interactions with other fields characterizing the system by means of suitable coupling terms. The present model mainly focuses on the active receptors and transport proteins on the cell membrane. In particular, in the presence of a specific ligand, binding and activation GPCRs kindles cytoplasmatic signaling pathways. The latter allow for both regulating some intracellular processes and communicating with the extra-cellular environment by expelling molecules through the activation of specific transport proteins, such as MRPs. This coupled mechanism can be followed in practice by measuring the levels of intracellular and extracellular concentrations of cAMP, involved in many physiological processes of the cell. In particular, intracellular cAMP production follows the response of the GPCRs to certain ligands, such as epinephrine. The level of cytoplasmatic cAMP is modulated by MRPs that permit the efflux of cAMP from the interior of the cell to the extracellular fluid. In this way, MRPs control cell homeostasis and determine an agonist interaction with membrane receptors.
In order to trace the interplay of the transmembrane proteins, we introduce mass balances for two selected species,
\displaystyle n_{1}=\xi
, representing the G proteins, and
\displaystyle n_{2}=\zeta
, namely the transport proteins. The main physiological aspects associated to their activity have been described by means of the following interspecific (Volterra-Lotka like) equations:

\displaystyle\displaystyle\dot{\xi}+M_{\xi,x}=\alpha_{\xi}\,\xi-\delta_{\xi}\,\xi-\beta_{\xi\zeta}\,\zeta\,\xi

(3.25)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\dot{\zeta}+M_{\zeta,x}=\beta_{\zeta\xi}\,\xi\,\zeta-\delta_{\zeta}\,\zeta.

(3.26)

Physical meaning of the species rates parameters.

In equation (
3.25
), the term
\displaystyle\alpha_{\xi}
denotes
intrinsic activation in response to a given ligand concentration

(such as the epinephrine), modelled by means of a specific uptake function. In particular, it is likely assumed that the ligand precipitation rate obeys a generic Gamma distribution with probability density function
\displaystyle\Upsilon(t)=a\,t\,e^{-bt}
. The coefficients
\displaystyle a
and
\displaystyle b
are calibrated by means of experiments. By observing the time at which
\displaystyle\Upsilon
is maximum – i.e.
\displaystyle\dot{\Upsilon}(t_{m})=0
– it readily follows that
\displaystyle b=t_{m}^{-1}
. Also, the total quantity of ligand precipitated over the experiment time
\displaystyle T
then will be

Q=\int_{0}^{T}\Upsilon(t)dt=ab^{-2}(1-e^{-bT}(1+bT))

(3.27)

that allows to estimate the parameter
\displaystyle a={Q}/(t_{m}^{2}-e^{-\frac{T}{t_{m}}}t_{m}\left(t_{m}+T\right))
. Given this, the coefficient
\displaystyle\alpha_{\xi}
results the relative uptake function multiplied by a suitable binding constant:

\alpha_{\xi}=k_{b}\,Q^{-1}\Upsilon(T).

(3.28)

The coefficient
\displaystyle\delta_{\xi}
denotes an
intrinsic deactivation constant
, while the
interspecific term

\displaystyle\beta_{\xi\zeta}
is introduced in the light that the efflux of extracellular cyclic Adenosyne Monophospate,
\displaystyle cAMP_{e}
(whose amount is proportional to the active MRP on lipidic membrane through (
2.4
)), is assumed to reduce the activity of receptor-ligand compounds by establishing chemical equilibrium between the extra- and the intra-cellular space. Dually, in equation (
3.26
), the rate of activation of transport proteins is stimulated by the level of intracellular cyclic Adenosyne Monophospate,
\displaystyle cAMP_{i}
(which is proportional to the activated G-proteins at time
\displaystyle t
through (
2.2
)). This is accounted for through the
interspecific positive term

\displaystyle\beta_{\zeta\xi}
governing the growth of activating MRPs. Model parameters have been therefore estimated through the experimental evaluation of intracellular and extracellular cAMP concentrations (see
Section 2

3.3
The Helmoltz free-energy of the membrane proteins

The energy
\displaystyle W^{*}
accounts for the hyperelastic contribution of the lipid membrane and for the Helmoltz free energy of the active proteins. The latter interact with the membrane by exchanging mechanical forces and showing remodelling changes during their activity. We start from the isothermal dissipation inequality. Here, we single out the contribution of mass transport of the species over the generic elementary volume, say
\displaystyle M_{i}
for the
\displaystyle i^{th}
species, through the action of the correspoinding (thermodynamically conjugate) chemical potential
\displaystyle\mu_{i}^{*}
, as well as the presence of thermodynamic driving forces
\displaystyle F_{r}^{*}
expending power against the rate of change of remodelling (see e.g.
[
87
,
98
]
). Hence, for an incompressible material and under isothermal assumptions, combined energy-entropy equations yield the following form of the second law of thermodynamics:

\int_{V^{0}}{P^{*}\dot{\lambda}}\,dV^{0}-\int_{V^{0}}\left(\mu^{*}_{i}\,M_{i}\right)_{x}\,dV^{0}+\int_{V^{0}}F_{r}^{*}\dot{K}_{r}\,dV^{0}\geq\frac{d}{dt}\int_{V^{0}}W^{*}_{0}\,dV^{0}

(3.29)

where repeated index
\displaystyle i
means summation over that index. By appealing to relation (
3.22
), in agreement with the dimension reduction performed in (
3.23
), quantities
\displaystyle X
per unit area can be introduced by considering that
\displaystyle\int_{V^{0}}X^{*}dV^{0}=\int_{\Omega}X\,d\Omega
, with
\displaystyle X=h_{0}X^{*}
for any field
\displaystyle X^{*}
per unit volume in the virgin configuration.

Provided that

W=W(\lambda,\lambda_{x},n_{i})

(3.30)

from (
3.23
), one also obtains the dimensionally reduced version of (
3.29
):

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}{P\dot{\lambda}}\,dV^{0}-\int_{\Omega}\left(\mu_{i}\,M_{i}\right)_{x}\,d\Omega+\int_{\Omega}F_{r}\dot{K}_{r}\,d\Omega\geq\int_{\Omega}\left[K_{r}\left(\frac{\partial W}{\partial\lambda}\dot{\lambda}+\frac{\partial W}{\partial\lambda_{x}}\dot{\lambda}_{x}+\frac{\partial W}{\partial n_{i}}\dot{n}_{i}\right)+W\,\dot{K}_{r}\right]\,d\Omega.

(3.31)

By substituting the mass balance (
3.24
) in the second integral of the left-hand side and by integrating by parts the second term on the right-hand side, equation (
3.31
) can be rearranged as follows:

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}{\left[P\dot{\lambda}-\mu_{i,x}M_{i}+\mu_{i}\,\dot{n}_{i}-\mu_{i}\,\Gamma_{i}+F_{r}\dot{K}_{r}\right]}\,d\Omega\geq\left[\frac{\partial W_{0}}{\partial\lambda_{x}}\dot{\lambda}\right]_{\partial\Omega}+\int_{\Omega}\left\{K_{r}\left[\left(\frac{\partial W}{\partial\lambda}-\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\left(\frac{\partial W}{\partial\lambda_{x}}\right)\right)\dot{\lambda}+\frac{\partial W}{\partial n_{i}}\dot{n}_{i}\right]+W\,\dot{K}_{r}\right\}\,dV^{0}

(3.32)

Provided the condition

\left[\frac{\partial W}{\partial\lambda_{x}}\dot{\lambda}\right]_{\partial\Omega}=0

(3.33)

is satisfied, grouping and localization of the terms in (
3.32
) yields the following inequality

\displaystyle\displaystyle\left[P-K_{r}\left(\frac{\partial W}{\partial\lambda}-\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\frac{\partial W}{\partial\lambda_{x}}\right)\right]\dot{\lambda}+\left[\mu_{i}-K_{r}\frac{\partial W}{\partial n_{i}}\right]\dot{n}_{i}+\left[F_{r}-W\right]\dot{K}_{r}-\mu_{i,x}M_{i}-\mu_{i}\,\Gamma_{i}\,\geq\,0.

(3.34)

Following the standard Coleman and Noll’s procedure, it is then possible to obtain the following constitutive relations for the Piola-Kirchhoff stress, the chemical potential and the remodelling driving force, which turns out to be entirely identifiable with the mechanical energy involved in the process:

\displaystyle\displaystyle P=K_{r}\left(\frac{\partial W}{\partial\lambda}-\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\frac{\partial W}{\partial\lambda_{x}}\right),

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mu_{i}=K_{r}\frac{\partial W}{\partial n_{i}},

(3.35)

\displaystyle\displaystyle F_{r}=W.

Inequality (
3.36
) thus reduces to:

-\mu_{i,x}M_{i}-\mu_{i}\,\Gamma_{i}\geq 0

(3.36)

that can be fulfilled by expressing proteins movement term as proportional to the gradient of the chemical potential, i.e.
\displaystyle M_{i}=-L_{i}\,n_{i}\,\mu_{i,x}
, where
\displaystyle L_{i}\geq 0
are suitable mobilities.
A reduced dissipation inequality in the form
\displaystyle\mu_{i}\,\Gamma_{i}\leq 0
remains to be fulfilled in order to ensure thermodynamic compatibility. In otehr words, the evolution of the system is thermodynamically consistent when the chemical potential becomes negative in the presence of increasing binding proteins on the membrane. An example of a similar instance was encountered in

in which an
ad hoc
flow rule respecting such condition was introduced.

Constitutive equations for the membrane

In presence of the uniaxial kinematics discussed in Section

In agreement with (

\displaystyle W=W(\lambda,\lambda_{x},n_{i})
represents the energy per unit area in the active (reference) configuration

an additive decompositon is assumed between the (hyperelastic)energy of the membrane and a second potential associated to the energetics of the transmembrane proteins.
This assumption is stated in the following form:

W=W_{hyp}(\lambda,\lambda_{x})-W_{n_{i}}(\phi,n_{i})=W_{hyp}(\lambda,\lambda_{x})-W_{n_{i}}(\lambda^{-1},n_{i}).

(3.37)

it is worth notning that the second term account for the possibility that activation and conformational changes of GPCRs may affect the membrane thickness, measured through
\displaystyle\lambda^{-1}

\displaystyle W

(3.38)

Because of relations (
3.3
), the following equations for the stress and for the chemical potentials relative to the active (reference) configurations follow:

\displaystyle\displaystyle\tilde{P}=K_{r}^{-1}P=\frac{\partial W}{\partial\lambda}-\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\frac{\partial W}{\partial\lambda_{x}}

\displaystyle\displaystyle\tilde{\mu}_{i}=K_{r}^{-1}\mu_{i}=\frac{\partial W}{\partial n_{i}}=-\frac{\partial W_{n_{i}}}{\partial n_{i}}.

(3.40)

A Legendre transformation of (
3.38

\displaystyle d(n_{i}\tilde{\mu}_{i})=\tilde{\mu}_{i}\,dn_{i}+n_{i}\,d\tilde{\mu}_{i}

d\hat{W}=d(W_{hyp}(\lambda,\lambda_{x})-\hat{W}_{\mu_{i}}(\lambda^{-1},\mu_{i}))=\tilde{P}\,d\lambda-n_{i}\,d\tilde{\mu}_{i}

(3.41)

from which it follows that

n_{i}=-\frac{\partial\hat{W}}{\partial\tilde{\mu}_{i}}=\frac{\partial\hat{W}_{\mu_{i}}}{\partial\tilde{\mu}_{i}}.

(3.42)

Because
\displaystyle W_{hyp}
is independent of the chemical potentials and since
\displaystyle\hat{W}_{\mu_{i}}
depends on the variables
\displaystyle\lambda^{-1}
and
\displaystyle\tilde{\mu}_{i}

\displaystyle\displaystyle dn_{i}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=-d\left(\frac{\partial\hat{W}}{\partial\tilde{\mu}_{i}}\right)=\frac{1}{\lambda^{-2}}\frac{\partial\tilde{P}}{\partial\tilde{\mu}_{i}}\,d\lambda^{-1}-\frac{\partial^{2}\hat{W}}{\partial\tilde{\mu}_{i}^{2}}\,d\tilde{\mu}_{i}=-c_{\lambda_{i}}\,d\lambda^{-1}+c_{\mu_{i}}d\tilde{\mu}_{i}.

(3.43)

Herein, the coefficient
\displaystyle c_{\lambda_{i}}
and
\displaystyle c_{\mu_{i}}
relate the variation of the
\displaystyle i^{th}
species density
\displaystyle n_{i}
to the change of the membrane stretch and of the associated chemical potential
\displaystyle\tilde{\mu}_{i}
.
It is assumed that the terms
\displaystyle c_{\lambda_{i}}
and
\displaystyle c_{\mu_{i}}
can be modelled as first order variation coefficients that depend on the current amount of active proteins, i.e.
\displaystyle c_{\lambda_{i}}\approx n_{i}\overline{c}_{\lambda_{i}}
and, in the light of the antagonism (

\displaystyle c_{\mu_{i}}\approx-n_{i}\overline{c}_{\mu_{i}}

\frac{dn_{i}}{n_{i}}=-\overline{c}_{\lambda_{i}}\,d\lambda^{-1}-\overline{c}_{\mu_{i}}d\tilde{\mu}_{i}

Therefore,
integration of Equation (

-\log\frac{n_{i}}{n_{i}^{0}}\simeq\,\overline{c}_{\lambda_{i}}\,\left(\lambda^{-1}-1\right)+\overline{c}_{\mu_{i}}\left(\tilde{\mu}_{i}-\tilde{\mu}_{i}^{0}\right)

(3.45)

which allows for evaluating the chemical potential of the species as follows

Results and discussion

The outcomes of the numerical simulations derived by the biomechanical model introduced above show the lipid bilayer undergoing dynamic remodelling triggered by the evolution of transmembrane protein activity. In particular, here strain localization within the cell membrane leading to the coexistence of thicker (ordered
\displaystyle L_{o}
-phases) and thinner lipid zones (disordered
\displaystyle L_{d}
-phases) is predicted.

As shown in Figure

[
108

), on such sites a difference of about
\displaystyle 0.9
nm between raft and non-raft membrane zones is detected. In particular, in stress-free simulations, there is a pure chemically-induced thickening of the membrane that forms a single raft and multiple rafts according to the analysed cases (Figure
6
). Under the same initial conditions, rafts also occur in the tensed membrane, where a transverse contraction and initial longitudinal elongation additionally take place in the
\displaystyle L_{d}
-phase as elastic response of the applied traction.

In Figure
7
A
the time-evolution of both the type of transmembrane protein fractions occurring on a raft site is reported. The interplay between RL proteins
\displaystyle\xi
and transporters
\displaystyle\zeta
is studied as a result of the introduced interspecific dynamics. More specifically, an initial growth of the GPCRs binding with ligands first occurs. Upon comparing this finding with the experimentally determined intracellular cAMP (cAMP
i
) normalized concentration, an estimate of the binding Receptor-Ligand (RL) compounds is provided according to (
2.2
). This is because GPCRs activation directly triggers the cell response by initiating the cAMP signaling pathway. Homeostatic levels of cAMP
i
are restored thanks to the activation of the Multidrug Resistant transport Proteins (MRPs) driving the efflux of part of the produced cAMP towards the extracellular environment. It is worth noting that a very good agreement is found between the predicted MRPs fraction and the (normalized) increase of cAMP
e
.

Furthermore, Figure
7

displays the simultaneous evolution of the ordered (thicker) phases. The latter evolve with similar trends in both the unconfined and the tensed membranes, exception made for the load-induced shrinkage in the initial stage, when chemical stress
\displaystyle P_{n}\,=\,K_{r}\,\omega_{i}\,(n_{i}^{0}-n_{i})/\lambda^{2}
(introduced in (
3.49

\displaystyle t\simeq 642s
in the simulations, a clear spatial correspondence between active domains and raft formation can be recognized in all situations, see Figure

This predicts that active GPCRs localize on lipid rafts
as an effect of the chemo-mechanical coupling between:

•

the chemical pressure arising from the deformation of the lipid bilayer;

•

the configurational changes of TMs domains, to which a decrease chemical potential as in Figure

Although the model and the associated numerics is carried out for a one-dimensional problem, the findings are fully consistent with the experimentally observed clustering of ligand-binding receptors on lipid rafts across the cell membrane (see e.g.

In order to investigate how model coefficients influence the formation of lipid rafts in high coupling with the dynamics of active receptors, the robustness of the model was tested by considering the variation of both interspecific and chemo-mechanical coefficients. In this regard, more than two thousands numerical simulations were performed by assigning values within the ranges reported in Table

Conclusions

Predictions and interpretation of the findings provided in this paper allow for explaining
why active receptors
of the GPCRs family
tend to cluster on lipid rafts
. Furthermore,
active receptors are predicted to enhance rafts formation
.

Such predictions are shown to be in relation with:

i) stress reduction arising within rafts because of the presence of active receptors and transporters that need local compliant zones to be able to perform conformational changes;

ii) decrease of chemical potential owing (1) binding of the GPRCs with the ligand, thereby favoring the formation of higher density regions of RL complex and (2) uptaking of intracellular cAMP from the MRPs transporters.

It is shown how the mechanobiology involved at the level of the cell membrane does entail strong coupling among (a) mechanical equilibrium governed by the (entropic, conformational, elastic) energy, (b) the kinetics of RL-binding and unbinding, regulated by the chemical potential and traced through the diffusion of GPCRs and MRPs (related to cAMP
i
and cAMP
e
respectively), and (c) the consistency requirement that the effective chemical potential of the RL-compound must agree both with the energetics and with the fact that lateral pressure arising between lipids surrounding the domains of the active species and confining their conformational changes. The strict correlation between how cAMP is triggered by RL-compounds and modulated by MRPs and the tendency of GPCRs to locate on lipid rafts has been shown by the simulations. It is worth notning that on the basis of the boundary and limit conditions of interest and by considering the interspecific terms taken into account in the model, coalescence due to Cahn-Hilliard-like dynamics was not expected, this phenomenon being object of future investigations.
The entire analysis has be done with special regard to an existing set of experiments involving HTR-8/SVneo cells and
\displaystyle\beta
2AR. Diffusive phenomena have been considered and the arising chemo-mechanical coupling has been accounted for through the kinetics of ligand binding. The outcomes of the model highlight the mechanobiology of lipid membrane remodelling by providing a mechanical explanation for the observed correspondence between the membrane regions where distribution density of active receptors is higher and locations of lipid rafts. These theoretical predictions actually confirm experimental findings by Nobel Prize 2012 Kobilka
[
10
]
and Lefkovitz
[
11
]
. Importantly, the predictive potential of the obtained model can help to provide a key support to quantitative diagnostics, owing for evaluation of cell response to endogenous and exogenous ligands regulated by GPCRs under different operating conditions of the cells.

When it comes to considering the membrane stress, formed by a hyperelastic part added on a reactive one, the lateral pressure is indeed such a term in the expression for the stress.
