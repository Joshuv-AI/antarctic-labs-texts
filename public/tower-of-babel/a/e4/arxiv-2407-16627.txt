Hardness of sampling solutions from the Symmetric Binary Perceptron
Ahmed El Alaoui, David Gamarnik

Abstract
We show that two related classes of algorithms, stable algorithms and Boolean circuits with bounded depth, cannot produce an approximate sample from the uniform measure over the set of solutions to the symmetric binary perceptron model at any constraint-to-variable density. This result is in contrast to the question of finding a solution to the same problem, where efficient (and stable) algorithms are known to succeed at sufficiently low density. This result suggests that the solutions found efficiently—whenever this task is possible—must be highly atypical, and therefore provides an example of a problem where search is efficiently possible but approximate sampling from the set of solutions is not, at least within these two classes of algorithms.



1
Introduction

We consider the problem of approximately sampling from the uniform distribution over the set of solutions of the Symmetric Binary Perceptron (SBP) model. The model is described
as follows.
Let
G=(g_{a})_{a=1}^{m}
be
m
independent random vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
defined over a probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\operatorname{\mathbb{P}})
, and let
\alpha,\kappa>0
. We let
m=\lfloor\alpha n\rfloor
and denote by
S(G,\kappa)
the set of binary solutions
x\in\{-1,+1\}^{n}
to the system of inequalities

\big|\langle g_{a},x\rangle\big|\leq\kappa\sqrt{n}~~~\mbox{for all}~1\leq a\leq m\,.

(1.1)

We consider two distributions over the input
G
: the Gaussian case
where each entry is distributed independently according to
N(0,1)
,
and the Rademacher case where each entry is an independent symmetric
random sign. In both cases (in fact under
more general distributional assumptions) a sharp and simple characterization of the
probability that
S(G,\kappa)\neq\emptyset

was recently derived in
[
ALS22a
,
PX21
]
.
For every
\kappa>0
,
this set is non-empty when
\alpha<\alpha_{\mbox{\tiny\sf SAT}}(\kappa):=-(\log 2)/\log\operatorname{\mathbb{P}}(|Z|\leq\kappa)
and empty when
\alpha>\alpha_{\mbox{\tiny\sf SAT}}(\kappa)
, both with high probability (whp)
as
n\to\infty
. Here
Z
denotes a standard Gaussian random variable.

It was further established in
[
ALS22a
,
PX21
]
that the set of solutions
exhibits the following interesting geometric property: For every
\kappa>0
at any positive constraint-to-variable density
\alpha>0

the space of solutions consists mostly (i.e., with exponentially small size exception)
of isolated points separated by linear
distance.
Specifically, for every
\alpha>0
, whp as
n\to\infty

there exists a subset
S_{0}(G,\kappa)\subset S(G,\kappa)
, such that
(a) for every
x\neq y\in S_{0}(G,\kappa)
,
d_{H}(x,y)=\Omega(n)
and
(b)
|S_{0}^{c}(G,\kappa)|/|S(G,\kappa)|\leq\exp(-\Omega(n))
. Here
A^{c}
denotes
the set-theoretic complement of
A
.

Based on similarities to other randomly generated constraint satisfaction
problems, such as the random K-SAT problem
[
ACORT11
]
, this suggests
an algorithmic difficulty of finding at least one satisfying solution.
Surprisingly, this is not the case and for every
\kappa>0
at small enough density
\alpha<\alpha_{\mbox{\tiny\sf ALG}}(\kappa)<\alpha_{\mbox{\tiny\sf SAT}}(\kappa)
, polynomial time algorithms for finding
solutions whp as
n\to\infty
exist
[
BS20
,
ALS22b
]
.
This apparent discrepancy was explained in
[
GKPX22
]
where it was shown that a provable barrier to algorithms finding
a solution appears in the form of a Overlap Gap Property (OGP), which occurs
near
\alpha_{\mbox{\tiny\sf ALG}}(\kappa)
when
\kappa
is small.

In this paper we consider the question of whether it is possible to efficiently generate
typical
solutions from
S(G,\kappa)
. In particular whether it is possible to sample
from it nearly uniformly at random.
We provide a strong evidence that the answer
is ‘no’ for all positive densities
\alpha>0
. Specifically, for two
classes of algorithms we describe below, we show that the distribution produced
by these algorithms deviates from the uniform distribution on
S(G,\kappa)

by a significant margin, and thus such algorithms fail
to sample nearly uniformly from the set of solutions.
This is the main result of our paper. One interpretation of this result is that the solutions produced by efficient algorithms as previously mentioned must be highly atypical.

The two algorithmic classes we consider are (a) stable algorithms (appropriately
defined) and (b) a related class of bounded depth Boolean circuits. Stable algorithms are loosely speaking
algorithms which have low noise sensitivity. Specifically, small changes in the input
matrix
G
results in a small change in the output of the algorithm. This
is quantified more precisely in Section
3
.
Many algorithms which find solutions in random constraint satisfaction
problems are known to be stable
[
Gam21
]
and some
are known to be effective for approximate sampling, as was shown recently
in
[
AMS22
,
AMS23a
,
HMP24
]

in the context of mean-field spin glass models.

As an illustration of stability in the context of sampling, we mention
(i)

sequential sampling
where
x_{1}
is sampled from its marginal, then for every
i\geq 2
,
x_{i}
is sampled from (an approximation of) its conditional distribution given
x_{1},\cdots,x_{i-1}
, and
(ii)
the class of
score-based diffusion algorithms

[
SE19
]
or algorithms based on discretizations of the
stochastic localization process

[
CE22
]
which are in a certain sense continuous-time versions of the sequential sampling procedure; see
[
CE22
,
Mon23
]
for an exposition of a common framework, as well as other sampling schemes from the same family. Such algorithms can be shown to be stable as long as the subroutine estimating the sequence of conditional distributions in the case
(i)
or for approximating the ‘denoising’ or ‘score’ function in the case
(ii)
is suitably Lipschitz as a function of its input.

As we mentioned earlier, when one is interested in search rather than sampling, i.e., one is only interested in finding a solution, and not necessarily a typical one, then OGP can be used to argue impossibility of the search task by stable algorithms.
Another class of algorithms ruled out by OGP in the context of the search problem is our (b) example, namely bounded
depth polynomial size Boolean circuits. This class of algorithms exhibits a form of ‘average’ stability, in contrast to the usual definition of stability used to argue against search algorithms, which is uniform in the input.
Technically, this comes in the form of low average noise sensitivity, specifically in the form of the celebrated Linial-Mansour-Nisan (LMN) Theorem
[
O’D14
]
and framed in terms of the underlying Fourier coefficients.
This average notion turns out to be sufficient for our sampling problem, and we use it to rule out two special classes of Boolean circuits :
polynomial size circuits
with depth
c\log n/\log\log n

for small enough constant
c
,
which depends on
\kappa
and
\alpha
, and circuits with bounded
depth and size
at most
\exp(O(n^{c}))
,
where
c
again depends on
\kappa
and
\alpha
.

Our proof approach is similar to the one of
[
AMS22
]

and is based on establishing that the model exhibits the so-called

Transport Disorder Chaos
(TDC). Roughly speaking this property says
that the measure is unstable to small perturbation to the input
G
when
a transport (Wasserstein-2) distance is used between measures.
The proof of the TDC property is somewhat technical and uses an associated planted model; an instrumental tool previously used in deriving
the satisfiability threshold
\alpha_{\mbox{\tiny\sf SAT}}(\kappa)

in
[
ALS22a
,
PX21
]
. TDC implies failure
of stable algorithms via a simple triangle inequality. On the other hand we show that Boolean circuits are stable via the LMN Theorem.
Part of this argument is similar to the one found
in
[
GJW20
]
where it was used to show the limitation of
circuits for solving search problems.

While our result rules out only certain classes of
sampling algorithms, we believe that the hardness of this problem is genuine, and in
fact no polynomial time algorithms for sampling exist for this problem at any
density
\alpha>0
. Needless to say, this is not within a near reach as such a
result would imply separation between
P
and
\#P
classes of algorithms.
An interesting extension of this work would be showing a failure of broader
classes of algorithm. We note
that a canonical choice of sampling technique, namely Monte Carlo Markov Chain/Glauber dynamics is not a viable
option here since the solution space is not connected and as such is not ergodic.
It seems plausible though to achieve sampling using Simulated Annealing (SA) technique. We believe though that the time for SA to
reach steady-state is exponentially large
and leave it as an open question.

Finally, another very interesting question which remains open is whether it is
possible to find any solution in the bulk
S_{0}(G,\kappa)
(the set of isolated solutions) at least
within the same classes of algorithms (stable or low-depth circuits). We suspect
that the answer is ‘no’, but our current proof technique does not extend to this result.
We leave it as a very interesting open problem.

2
Instability of the set of solutions

In this section we establish a certain structural property for this uniform measure called Transport Disorder Chaos in
[
AMS23b
]
, and then show that this presents a barrier to approximate sampling for the family of stable algorithms and for Boolean circuits of bounded depth and/or bounded size. At a high level, TDC is the property that the measure is unstable to small perturbations of the input data, in the sense that it moves a substantial fraction of its mass to a linear distance away from its initial location. Conversely, stable algorithms are insensitive to such small perturbations to their input, and are thus unable to produce a typical sample from the measure.
We will define the notion of perturbation differently depending on the distribution of the input:

1.

The Gaussian case:
If
G
has independent standard normal entries, we consider a perturbation of
G
defined by the interpolation

G_{t}=t\,G+\sqrt{1-t^{2}}\,G^{\prime},~~~t\in[0,1]\,,

where
G^{\prime}
is an independent copy of
G
.

2.

The Rademacher case:
If
G
has independent random sign entries, we instead construct
G_{t}
by resampling each entry of
G
independently with probability
1-t\in[0,1]
.

In both cases,
G
and
G_{t}
have marginally the same law and are correlated entrywise with correlation
t
.
Next, we measure displacement of mass due to perturbation via the Wasserstein-2 distance defined as

W_{2}(\mu,\nu)^{2}=\inf_{\pi\in\mathcal{C}(\mu,\nu)}\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}_{\pi}\Big[\big\|X-Y\big\|_{2}^{2}\Big]\,,

where
\mu,\nu
are any two probability
measures on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with finite second moments, and the infimum is over all couplings
(X,Y)\sim\pi
with marginals
X\sim\mu
and
Y\sim\nu
.
Finally, for a finite set
S
,
{\sf Unif}(S)
denotes the uniform distribution over
S
.

We now state our main structural result that
{\sf Unif}\big(S(G,\kappa)\big)
has transport disorder chaos:

Theorem 2.1
.

Let
G
be either drawn from the Gaussian or Rademacher distribution and let
G_{t}
be defined accordingly. Assume that
\kappa>0
and
\alpha<\alpha_{\mbox{\tiny\sf SAT}}(\kappa)
.
Let
\mu_{G}={\sf Unif}\big(S(G,\kappa)\big)
if
S(G,\kappa)\neq\emptyset
, and define
\mu_{G}
arbitrarily otherwise.
Then there exists
\delta,C>0
and
t_{0}\in[0,1)
, all depending on
\alpha,\kappa
such that for any sequence
t_{n}\in[t_{0},1)
satisfying

•

n\sqrt{1-t_{n}}\geq C\log n
is the Gaussian disorder case, or

•

\sqrt{n(1-t_{n})}\geq C\log n
is the Rademacher disorder case,

we have

\lim_{n\to\infty}\,\operatorname{\mathbb{P}}\Big(W_{2}\big(\mu_{G},\mu_{G_{t_{n}}}\big)\geq\sqrt{\delta n}\Big)=1\,.

(2.1)

Remark 1
.

The assumptions on
t_{n}
essentially require that
t_{n}
approaches
1
not faster than order
(\log n/n)^{2}
for the Gaussian disorder case,
and
(\log n)^{2}/n
for the Rademacher disorder case. The weaker rate we obtain in the latter is due to the use of a central limit theorem where additional errors are incurred relative to the Gaussian case. We also note that the logarithmic term can be dispensed with, i.e., a sufficient condition should be
n\sqrt{1-t_{n}}\to\infty
, at least in the Gaussian case in the presence of a contiguity statement between two probability models as we describe below.

The planted model

As a preliminary to the proof we introduce a
planted
distribution on pairs
(G,G_{t})
and state a few useful results from
[
PX21
,
ALS22a
]
.
We first let
\operatorname{\mathbb{P}}_{\mbox{\rm\tiny rd}}
denote our
null
or
random
distribution on pairs
(G,G_{t})
as in the description in
1
in the Gaussian case, or the description in
2
in the Rademacher case. Next we let
\operatorname{\mathbb{P}}_{\mbox{\rm\tiny pl}}
be the joint distribution on pairs
(G,G_{t})
defined by the density

\frac{\mathrm{d}\operatorname{\mathbb{P}}_{\mbox{\rm\tiny pl}}}{\mathrm{d}\operatorname{\mathbb{P}}_{\mbox{\rm\tiny rd}}}(G,G_{t})=\frac{\big|S(G,\kappa)\big|}{\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}_{\mbox{\rm\tiny rd}}\big|S(G,\kappa)\big|}\,.

(2.2)

Note that the above density does not depend on its second argument
G_{t}
. An equivalent way of describing the planted distribution is to first generate a
planted configuration

x
uniformly from
\{-1,+1\}^{n}
, then generate
G
from the Gaussian or Rademacher distribution conditionally on the event
x\in S(G,\kappa)
, and then generate
G_{t}
according to either description
1
or
2
.

The first result is about the magnitude of the likelihood ratio in Eq. (
2.2
) which will allows to transfer statements established under the planted model to equivalent statements under the null model.

Lemma 2.2

(
[
PX21
,
ALS22a
]
)
.

Let
L=\mathrm{d}\operatorname{\mathbb{P}}_{\mbox{\rm\tiny pl}}/\mathrm{d}\operatorname{\mathbb{P}}_{\mbox{\rm\tiny rd}}
.
For all
\alpha<\alpha_{\mbox{\tiny\sf SAT}}(\kappa)
there exists a constant
C>0
such that

\lim_{n\to\infty}\operatorname{\mathbb{P}}_{\mbox{\rm\tiny rd}}\Big(|\log L|\leq C\log n\Big)=1\,,

in both the Rademacher and the Gaussian cases.

The above result was proved by Perkins and Xu
[
PX21
]
under a numerical assumption, first identified in
[
APZ19
]
, about the possible maxima of a certain univariate function. Subsequent work of Abbé, Li and Sly
[
ALS22a
]
verified this condition and proved the stronger result that
L
has a log-normal limit (of constant order) when the disorder is Rademacher. Subsequent work of Sah and Sawhney
[
SS23
]
extended the latter result to the Gaussian case very close to the satisfiability threshold, i.e., when
m=\alpha_{\mbox{\tiny\sf SAT}}(\kappa)n-\eta\log n
,
\eta>0
. Log-normality of
L
implies that the planted and null distributions are mutually
contiguous
, meaning that any (sequence of) rare event(s) under one distribution is also rare under the other
[
VdV98
, Lemma 6.4]
.
For our purposes the weak statement in the above lemma is enough, as we will only need to transfer statements about events which are super-polynomially rare as we clarify in the next lemma.

Lemma 2.3
.

Suppose
(E_{n})
a sequence of events in
\mathcal{F}
such that
\operatorname{\mathbb{P}}_{\mbox{\rm\tiny pl}}(E_{n})=o(n^{-C})
where
C
is the constant appearing in Lemma
2.2
. Then
\operatorname{\mathbb{P}}_{\mbox{\rm\tiny rd}}(E_{n})=o_{n}(1)
.

Proof.

We have

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathbb{P}}_{\mbox{\rm\tiny rd}}(E_{n})

\displaystyle=\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}_{\mbox{\rm\tiny pl}}\big[\mathds{1}\{E_{n},L>n^{-C}\}L^{-1}\big]+\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}_{\mbox{\rm\tiny rd}}\big[\mathds{1}\{E_{n},L<n^{-C}\}\big]

\displaystyle\leq n^{C}\operatorname{\mathbb{P}}_{\mbox{\rm\tiny pl}}(E_{n})+\operatorname{\mathbb{P}}_{\mbox{\rm\tiny rd}}(L<n^{-C})\,.

The above tends to zero since
n^{C}\operatorname{\mathbb{P}}_{\mbox{\rm\tiny pl}}(E_{n})\to 0
and as a consequence of Lemma
2.2
for the second term.
∎

Now we are ready to prove the main theorem.

2.1
Proof of Theorem
2.1

The main idea is that conditional on
S(G,\kappa)
not being empty and for

not too close to 1, with high probability there is no solution

For

and two arrays

E_{\delta}=\Big\{x\in S(G,\kappa)\,:\,\forall\,y\in S(G_{t},\kappa)\,,~d_{H}(x,y)>\delta n\Big\}\,,

(2.3)

where

again denotes the Hamming distance between binary vectors.
We observe that under any coupling
\pi
of
\mu_{G}
and
\mu_{G_{t}}
, we have

\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}_{(X,Y)\sim\pi}\Big[\big\|X-Y\big\|_{2}^{2}\Big]=4\mu_{G}\Big(\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\big[d_{H}(X,Y)\,|\,X\big]\Big)\geq 4\delta n\,\mu_{G}(E_{\delta})\,.

(2.4)

Therefore it suffices to lower-bound
\mu_{G}(E_{\delta})
. We will in fact show that
\mu_{G}(E_{\delta})\to 1
in probability for some
\delta>0
not depending on
t
as long as
n\sqrt{1-t}\geq C\log n
in the Gaussian case and
\sqrt{n}\sqrt{1-t}\geq C\log n
in the Rademacher case.
We achieve this via a first moment method in the planted distribution.
Our goal will be to show that
\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}_{\mbox{\rm\tiny pl}}\big[\mu_{G}(E_{\delta}^{c})\big]\to 0
as
n\to\infty
.
By using Eq. (
2.2
) we have

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}_{\mbox{\rm\tiny pl}}\big[\mu_{G}(E_{\delta}^{c})\big]

\displaystyle=\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}_{\mbox{\rm\tiny pl}}\Big[\frac{1}{|S(G,\kappa)|}\sum_{x\in\{-1,+1\}^{n}}\mathds{1}\Big\{x\in S(G,\kappa)\,,\exists\,y\in S(G_{t},\kappa)\,\,\mbox{s.t.}\,\,d_{H}(x,y)\leq\delta n\Big\}\Big]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}|S(G,\kappa)|}\sum_{x\in\{-1,+1\}^{n}}\operatorname{\mathbb{P}}_{\mbox{\rm\tiny rd}}\Big(x\in S(G,\kappa)\,,~\exists\,y\in S(G_{t},\kappa)\,\,\mbox{s.t.}\,\,d_{H}(x,y)\leq\delta n\Big)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2^{n}}\sum_{x\in\{-1,+1\}^{n}}\operatorname{\mathbb{P}}_{\mbox{\rm\tiny rd}}\Big(\exists\,y\in S(G_{t},\kappa)\,\,\mbox{s.t.}\,\,d_{H}(x,y)\leq\delta n\,\Big|\,x\in S(G,\kappa)\Big)\,.

(2.5)

By sign symmetry of the distribution of
(G,G_{t})
, the probability in the above display does not depend on
x
, which we may assume fixed in all subsequent calculations. We also omit the subscript ‘
\mathrm{rd}
’. Let
y\in\{-1,1\}^{n}
,

be distributed as any row of
G_{t}
and
g
be distributed as a row of
G
(under
\operatorname{\mathbb{P}}_{\mbox{\rm\tiny rd}}
). By Markov’s inequality and independence of the rows of
G
and
G_{t}
, we obtain from Eq. (
2.1
)

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}_{\mbox{\rm\tiny pl}}\big[\mu_{G}(E_{\delta}^{c})\big]

\displaystyle\leq\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\Big[\Big|\Big\{y\in S(G_{t},\kappa)\,:\,d_{H}(x,y)\leq n\delta\Big\}\Big|\,\big|\,x\in S(G,\kappa)\Big]

\displaystyle=\sum_{y:d_{H}(x,y)\leq n\delta}\operatorname{\mathbb{P}}\Big(\big|\langle g_{t},y\rangle\big|\leq\kappa\sqrt{n}\,\Big|\,\big|\langle g,x\rangle\big|\leq\kappa\sqrt{n}\Big)^{m}\,.

(2.6)

The Gaussian case:

When the disorder is Gaussian the conditional probability in the above reads

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathbb{P}}\Big(\big|\langle g_{t},y\rangle\big|\leq\kappa\sqrt{n}\,\Big|\,\big|\langle g,x\rangle\big|\leq\kappa\sqrt{n}\Big)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{\mathbb{P}}\Big(\big|\langle g^{(t)},y\rangle\big|\leq\kappa\sqrt{n}\,,\big|\langle g,x\rangle\big|\leq\kappa\sqrt{n}\Big)/\operatorname{\mathbb{P}}\Big(\big|\langle g,x\rangle\big|\leq\kappa\sqrt{n}\Big)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{\mathbb{P}}\big(|Z_{0}|\leq\kappa\,,|Z_{r}|\leq\kappa\big)/\operatorname{\mathbb{P}}\big(|Z_{0}|\leq\kappa\big)\,,

(2.7)

where

, and
(Z_{0},Z_{r})
is a pair of centered Gaussian random variables with unit variances and correlation
\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}[Z_{0}Z_{r}]=r
.
Let us use the notation

p(\kappa)=\operatorname{\mathbb{P}}\big(|Z_{0}|\leq\kappa\big)\,,~~~~\mbox{and}~~~~q_{\kappa}(r)=\operatorname{\mathbb{P}}\big(|Z_{0}|\leq\kappa\,,\,|Z_{r}|\leq\kappa\big)\,.

We will need facts about the monotonicity of
q_{\kappa}
and estimates on its behavior near

:

Lemma 2.4
.

The map
r\mapsto q_{\kappa}(r)
is even and non-decreasing on
[0,1]
. Furthermore, there exists
r_{0}\in(0,1)
and

both depending on
\kappa>0
such that for all

q_{k}(r)-p(\kappa)\leq-c\sqrt{1-r}\,.

(2.8)

We delay the proof of the above lemma to the end of this section.
Returning to our main argument, for the case
y=x
in the sum (
2.6
), we have for all

,

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathbb{P}}\Big(\big|\langle g_{t},x\rangle\big|\leq\kappa\sqrt{n}\,\Big|\,\big|\langle g,x\rangle\big|\leq\kappa\sqrt{n}\Big)^{m}

\displaystyle=\Big(q_{\kappa}(t)\big/p(\kappa)\Big)^{m}\,.

Next, to treat the remaining terms, since

.
Therefore,

\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}_{\mbox{\rm\tiny pl}}\big[\mu_{G}(E_{\delta}^{c})\big]\leq\Big(q_{\kappa}(t)\big/p(\kappa)\Big)^{m}+\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\Big[\Big|\Big\{y\in S(G,\kappa)\setminus\{x\}\,:\,d_{H}(x,y)\leq n\delta\Big\}\Big|\,\big|\,x\in S(G,\kappa)\Big]\,.

The second term counts the number of solutions near a typical one in the planted model. Perkins and Xu

For all

(2.9)

This yields desired result. It remains to prove Lemma

Proof of Lemma
2.4
.

Monotonicity follows from the formula

q_{\kappa}^{\prime}(t)=\frac{1}{\pi}\frac{e^{-\kappa^{2}/2}}{\sqrt{1-t^{2}}}\left(e^{-\kappa^{2}(1-t)/2(1+t)}-e^{-\kappa^{2}(1+t)/2(1-t)}\right)\,,

. This is obtained by differentiating

.
Now we show the bound (

). Letting

\displaystyle p(\kappa)-q_{\kappa}(t)=\mathbb{P}(|Z_{t}|>\kappa,|Z_{0}|\leq\kappa)

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{P}\left(Z_{t}>\kappa,Z_{0}\in\left(\kappa-\varepsilon,\kappa\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\int_{\kappa-\varepsilon}^{\kappa}\mathbb{P}(Z_{r}\geq\kappa|Z=z)\phi(z)dz.

(2.10)

We use the fact that

is distributed

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(Z_{t}\geq\kappa|Z=z)=\mathbb{P}\left(X\geq\frac{\kappa-tz}{\sqrt{1-t^{2}}}\right).

When

\displaystyle{\kappa-tz\over\sqrt{1-t^{2}}}

\displaystyle\leq\kappa{1-t\over\sqrt{1-t^{2}}}+{\varepsilon t\over\sqrt{1-t^{2}}}

\displaystyle=\kappa{\sqrt{1-t}\over\sqrt{1+t}}+{\varepsilon t\over\sqrt{1-t^{2}}}

\displaystyle\leq\kappa+{\varepsilon\over\sqrt{1-t^{2}}}.

Now taking
\varepsilon=\kappa\sqrt{1-t^{2}}
,
the integral in (
2.10
) is at least

\mathbb{P}(X\geq 2\kappa)\mathbb{P}(\kappa-\varepsilon<Z<\kappa)\geq C(\kappa)\varepsilon
if
t
is close enough to
1
. The proof of Eq. (
2.8
) is complete.
∎

The Rademacher case:

When the disorder is Rademacher we restart the argument from Eq. (
2.6
) and follow the same outline by invoking the following two Berry-Esseen bounds:

Lemma 2.6
.

Let
X_{1},\cdots,X_{n}
be i.i.d. vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
where
X_{i}=(X_{i,1},X_{i,2})
is a bivariate vector of two unbiased Rademacher r.v.’s having correlation
t
. Let
\xi\in\{-1,+1\}^{n}
be fixed and
let
S_{n,1}=(1/\sqrt{n})\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i,1}
,
S_{n,2}=(1/\sqrt{n})\sum_{i=1}^{n}\xi_{i}X_{i,2}
.
There exists a universal constant
C>0
such that for all
\kappa>0
and
t\in[0,1]
we have

\displaystyle|\operatorname{\mathbb{P}}\big(S_{n,1}\in[-\kappa,\kappa]\big)-p(\kappa)|\leq\frac{C}{\sqrt{n}}\,,~~~\mbox{and}~~~\operatorname{\mathbb{P}}\big(S_{n}\in[-\kappa,\kappa]^{2}\big)-q_{\kappa}\Big(\frac{t}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\xi_{i}\Big)\leq\frac{C\log n}{\sqrt{n}}\,,

(2.11)

where
S_{n}=(S_{n,1},S_{n,2})
.

The first statement is the classical Berry-Esseen theorem, see e.g.
[
Dud18
]
. As for the second statement, while there are many proofs of the multivariate Central Limit Theorem adapted to various situations, we provide here a short proof based on the arguments of
[
Zha18
,
Rio09
]
at the end of this section.

Now going back to the main argument, we start from Eq. (
2.6
) which we rewrite here:

\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}_{\mbox{\rm\tiny pl}}\big[\mu_{G}(E_{\delta}^{c})\big]\leq\sum_{y:d_{H}(x,y)\leq n\delta}\operatorname{\mathbb{P}}\Big(\big|\langle g_{t},y\rangle\big|\leq\kappa\sqrt{n}\,\Big|\,\big|\langle g,x\rangle\big|\leq\kappa\sqrt{n}\Big)^{m}\,.

(2.12)

We will split the above sum based on the distance
d_{H}(x,y)
.
In the notation of Lemma
2.6
with
\xi=(x_{i}y_{i})_{i=1}^{n}
, and letting
r=(t/n)\sum_{i=1}^{n}\xi_{i}
we have

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathbb{P}}\Big(\big|\langle g_{t},y\rangle\big|\leq\kappa\sqrt{n}\,\Big|\,\big|\langle g,x\rangle\big|\leq\kappa\sqrt{n}\Big)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{\mathbb{P}}\big(S_{n}\in[-\kappa,\kappa]^{2}\big)\big/\operatorname{\mathbb{P}}\big(S_{n,1}\in[-\kappa,\kappa]\big)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{q_{\kappa}(r)+Cn^{-1/2}\log n}{p(\kappa)-Cn^{-1/2}}\,.

(2.13)

If
d_{H}(x,y)\leq K=n^{1/2-\epsilon_{0}}
,
\epsilon_{0}>0
small enough, we use the bound
q_{\kappa}(r)\leq q_{\kappa}(t)\leq p(\kappa)-c\sqrt{1-t}
as per Lemma
2.4
.
Restricting
t
so that

c\sqrt{1-t}\geq 2C\frac{\log n}{\sqrt{n}}\,,

Eq. (
2.13
) raised to the power
m
is bounded by

\left(\frac{p(\kappa)-c\sqrt{1-t}/2}{p(\kappa)-Cn^{-1/2}}\right)^{m}\leq e^{-c_{0}(\kappa)m\sqrt{1-t}+c_{1}(\kappa)m/\sqrt{n}}\leq e^{-c^{\prime}\sqrt{n}\log n}\,,

with
c^{\prime}=c^{\prime}(\alpha,\kappa)>0
. Therefore,

\displaystyle\sum_{y:d_{H}(x,y)\leq K}\operatorname{\mathbb{P}}\Big(\big|\langle g_{t},y\rangle\big|\leq\kappa\sqrt{n}\,\Big|\,\big|\langle g,x\rangle\big|\leq\kappa\sqrt{n}\Big)^{m}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k=0}^{\lfloor K\rfloor}\binom{n}{k}\cdot e^{-c^{\prime}\sqrt{n}\log n}

\displaystyle\leq K\binom{n}{\lfloor K\rfloor}e^{-c^{\prime}\sqrt{n}\log n}

\displaystyle\leq e^{-c^{\prime}\sqrt{n}(\log n)/2}\,.

(2.14)

Next for the terms where
n^{1/2-\epsilon_{0}}<d_{H}(x,y)\leq K=\epsilon n
, we use the bound

q_{\kappa}(r)\leq q_{\kappa}(\langle x,y\rangle/n)\leq p(\kappa)-c\sqrt{d_{H}(x,y)/n}\,.

Since
\sqrt{d_{H}(x,y)/n}>1/n^{1/4+\epsilon_{0}}\gg(\log n)/\sqrt{n}
for
\epsilon_{0}<1/4
, we similarly obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{y:n^{1/2-\epsilon_{0}}<d_{H}(x,y)\leq\epsilon n}\operatorname{\mathbb{P}}\Big(\big|\langle g_{t},y\rangle\big|\leq\kappa\sqrt{n}\,\Big|\,\big|\langle g,x\rangle\big|\leq\kappa\sqrt{n}\Big)^{m}

\displaystyle\leq n\binom{n}{\lfloor K\rfloor}\cdot e^{-c\sqrt{Kn}}\leq e^{-c^{\prime}n}\,,

(2.15)

for
\epsilon
small enough and
c^{\prime}=c^{\prime}(\alpha,\kappa,\epsilon)>0
.
Finally for
\epsilon n<d_{H}(x,y)\leq\delta n
, we still use the bound
q_{\kappa}(r)\leq q_{\kappa}(\langle x,y\rangle/n)
and compare the quantity in Eq. (
2.13
) to its Gaussian analogue
q_{\kappa}(\langle x,y\rangle/n)/p(\kappa)
. By a Taylor expansion we have

\displaystyle\frac{p(\kappa)}{q_{\kappa}(\langle x,y\rangle/n)}\operatorname{\mathbb{P}}\Big(\big|\langle g_{t},y\rangle\big|\leq\kappa\sqrt{n}\,\Big|\,\big|\langle g,x\rangle\big|\leq\kappa\sqrt{n}\Big)

\displaystyle\leq 1+c(\log n)/\sqrt{n}\leq e^{cn^{-1/2}\log n}\,,

where
c=c(\kappa)>0
. Therefore

\displaystyle\sum_{y:\epsilon n<d_{H}(x,y)\leq\delta n}\operatorname{\mathbb{P}}\Big(\big|\langle g_{t},y\rangle\big|\leq\kappa\sqrt{n}\,\Big|\,\big|\langle g,x\rangle\big|\leq\kappa\sqrt{n}\Big)^{m}

\displaystyle\leq e^{cmn^{-1/2}\log n}\hskip-14.22636pt\sum_{y:\epsilon n<d_{H}(x,y)\leq\delta n}\Big(\frac{q_{\kappa}(\langle x,y\rangle/n)}{p(\kappa)}\Big)^{m}\,.

(2.16)

This last inequality puts as back in the setting of Gaussian disorder, at the price of the factor
e^{cmn^{-1/2}\log n}
as above. The last term was shown to be exponentially small in
[
PX21
]
:

Lemma 2.7

(Consequence of Lemma 5 and Lemma 8 in
[
PX21
]
)
.

Under Gaussian disorder
G
, for all
\alpha<\alpha_{\mbox{\tiny\sf SAT}}(\kappa)
, there exists
\epsilon<\delta
and
c>0
depending on
\alpha,\kappa
such that

\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\Big[\Big|\Big\{y\in S(G,\kappa)\,:\,\epsilon n<d_{H}(x,y)\leq n\delta\Big\}\Big|\,\big|\,x\in S(G,\kappa)\Big]=\hskip-14.22636pt\sum_{y:\epsilon n<d_{H}(x,y)\leq\delta n}\Big(\frac{q_{\kappa}(\langle x,y\rangle/n)}{p(\kappa)}\Big)^{m}\leq e^{-cn}\,.

Using the result of Lemma
2.7
in Eq. (
2.16
) we obtain

\sum_{y:\varepsilon n<d_{H}(x,y)\leq\delta n}\operatorname{\mathbb{P}}\Big(\big|\langle g_{t},y\rangle\big|\leq\kappa\sqrt{n}\,\Big|\,\big|\langle g,x\rangle\big|\leq\kappa\sqrt{n}\Big)^{m}\leq e^{-c^{\prime}n}\,.

(2.17)

Combining the bounds (
2.14
), (
2.15
) and (
2.17
) we obtain

\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}_{\mbox{\rm\tiny pl}}\big[\mu_{G}(E_{\delta}^{c})\big]\leq e^{-c\sqrt{n}\log n}\,,~~~c>0\,,

and this allows us to conclude once more.

Finally, it remains to prove Lemma
2.6
to conclude the argument in the Rademacher case.

Proof of Lemma
2.6
.

We concentrate on proving the second bound in Eq. (
2.11
). For pedagogical reasons we first present a version of our argument which obtains the slower rate
1/n^{1/4}
, and then use a variation of this argument which achieves the rate
(\log n)/\sqrt{n}
.

Let
G=(G_{1},G_{2})
be a bivariate Gaussian random vector distributed as
(Z_{0},Z_{r})
,
r=(t/n)\sum_{i=1}^{n}\xi_{i}
. (Note that
\operatorname{\mathbb{P}}(G\in[-\kappa,\kappa]^{2})=q_{\kappa}(r)
.) For
\varepsilon>0
and under any coupling of
(S_{n},G)
we have

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathbb{P}}\big(S_{n}\in[-\kappa,\kappa]^{2}\big)

\displaystyle\leq\operatorname{\mathbb{P}}\big(\|S_{n}\|_{\infty}\leq\kappa,\|S_{n}-G\|_{\infty}\leq\varepsilon\big)+\operatorname{\mathbb{P}}\big(\|S_{n}-G\|_{\infty}>\varepsilon\big)

\displaystyle\leq\operatorname{\mathbb{P}}\big(\|G\|_{\infty}\leq\kappa+\varepsilon\big)+\operatorname{\mathbb{P}}\big(\|S_{n}-G\|_{\infty}>\varepsilon\big)\,.

On the one hand by Nazarov’s inequality
[
Naz03
,
CCK17
]
(or direct calculations) we have

\operatorname{\mathbb{P}}(\|G\|_{\infty}\leq\kappa+\varepsilon)\leq\operatorname{\mathbb{P}}(\|G\|_{\infty}\leq\kappa)+C\varepsilon

for an absolute constant
C>0
.
On the other hand by a union bound and Markov’s inequality,

\operatorname{\mathbb{P}}\big(\|S_{n}-G\|_{\infty}>\varepsilon\big)\leq\varepsilon^{-1}\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\big[\|S_{n}-G\|_{1}\big]\,.

(2.18)

Now consider coupling
S_{n}
and
G
coordinate-wise via increasing rearrangements: letting
F_{n}
and
\Phi
the cumulative distribution functions of the (real-valued) r.v.’s
S_{n,1}
and
G_{1}
respectively, we let
S_{n,j}=F_{n}(\Phi^{-1}(G_{j}))
,
j=1,2
. Then since the coordinates are identically distributed,

\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\big[\|S_{n}-G\|_{1}\big]=W_{1}(S_{n,1},G_{1})+W_{1}(S_{n,2},G_{2})=2W_{1}(S_{n,1},G_{1})\,,

where
W_{1}(X,Y)
is the Wasserstein-1 distance between
X
and
Y
, i.e., the infimum of
\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}[|X-Y|]
over all couplings with the correct marginals. This infimum is achieved by the increasing rearrangement mentioned above.
A CLT in the
W_{1}
distance, equivalently a Berry-Esseen bound for the
L_{1}
distance (rather than
L_{\infty}
) between the distribution functions is classical:

W_{1}(S_{n,1},G_{1})\leq\frac{C}{\sqrt{n}}\,,

for some absolute constant
C>0
, see
[
Ess58
,
Gol10
]
. Combining these bounds we obtain

\operatorname{\mathbb{P}}\big(S_{n}\in[-\kappa,\kappa]^{2}\big)\leq\operatorname{\mathbb{P}}\big(G\in[-\kappa,\kappa]^{2}\big)+C\varepsilon+\frac{2C}{\varepsilon\sqrt{n}}\,.

(2.19)

We obtain the bound
1/n^{1/4}
by taking
\varepsilon=1/n^{1/4}
.

Let us now improve the convergence rate
1/n^{1/4}
obtained above to
(\log n)/\sqrt{n}
. We use a stronger result of
[
Rio09
]
on a Wasserstein CLT in
\psi
-Orliz norm with
\psi(x)=e^{|x|}-1
: Define the
\psi
-Orliz norm of a real-valued random variable
X
as

\|X\|_{\psi}=\inf\{a>0\,:\,\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\psi(X/a)\leq 1\}\,.

This is also known as the
sub-exponential norm
of the random variable
X

[
Ver18
]
.
Define the Wasserstein-
\psi
distance between two r.v.’s
X
and
Y
as

W_{\psi}(X,Y)=\inf\,\,\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\|X-Y\|_{\psi}\,,

where the infimum is taken over all couplings of
X
and
Y
.
Now we follow the same argument as before and replace the bound (
2.18
) by the following improved bound

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathbb{P}}\big(\|S_{n}-G\|_{\infty}>\varepsilon\big)

\displaystyle\leq\operatorname{\mathbb{P}}\big(|S_{n,1}-G_{1}|>\varepsilon\big)+\operatorname{\mathbb{P}}\big(|S_{n,2}-G_{2}|>\varepsilon\big)

\displaystyle\leq 2e^{-\varepsilon/a}\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\big[e^{|S_{n,1}-G_{1}|/a}\big]\,,

valid for any
a>0
. We take
a=\|S_{n,1}-G_{1}\|_{\psi}
to obtain

\operatorname{\mathbb{P}}\big(\|S_{n}-G\|_{\infty}>\varepsilon\big)\leq 4e^{-\varepsilon/\|S_{n,1}-G_{1}\|_{\psi}}\,.

We now choose the optimal coupling achieving the
W_{\psi}
distance (this turns out to be the increasing rearrangement mentioned above), and use the univariate CLT for
W_{\psi}
in
[
Rio09
, Theorem 2.1]
:

W_{\psi}(S_{n,1},G_{1})\leq\frac{C}{\sqrt{n}}\,,

to obtain the bound

\operatorname{\mathbb{P}}\big(S_{n}\in[-\kappa,\kappa]^{2}\big)\leq\operatorname{\mathbb{P}}\big(G\in[-\kappa,\kappa]^{2}\big)+C\varepsilon+4e^{-\varepsilon\sqrt{n}/C}\,,

(2.20)

to be compared with (
2.19
). Now we can take
\varepsilon=C(\log n)/\sqrt{n}
to conclude.
∎

3
Consequences for stable algorithms and circuits

Let
{\sf ALG}_{n}:\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}\times\Omega
be a family of randomized algorithms taking the matrix
G\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
with
m=\lfloor\alpha n\rfloor
and an independent random seed
\omega\in\Omega
as input. The margin
\kappa>0
is also an input to the algorithm, but we omit it from our notation since it will be considered fixed throughout.

Definition 3.1
.

Let
\mu^{\mbox{\rm\tiny alg}}_{G}
be the law of the output of the algorithm
x^{\mbox{\rm\tiny alg}}={\sf ALG}_{n}(G,\omega)\in\{-1,+1\}^{n}
, conditional on
G
:
\mu^{\mbox{\rm\tiny alg}}_{G}:={\rm Law}(x^{\mbox{\rm\tiny alg}}\,|\,G)
.
For a sequence
t_{n}\to 0
, we say that
({\sf ALG}_{n})_{n\geq 1}
is
t_{n}
-stable if

\lim_{n\to\infty}\,\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}W_{2}\big(\mu^{\mbox{\rm\tiny alg}}_{G},\mu^{\mbox{\rm\tiny alg}}_{G_{t_{n}}}\big)=0\,~~~\mbox{in prob.}

(3.1)

A straightforward consequence of this definition, as observed in
[
AMS22
]
, is that
t_{n}
-stable algorithms cannot approximately sample from a distribution exhibiting transport disorder chaos with the same parameter
t_{n}
, in the following sense:

Lemma 3.2
.

If the pairs
(\mu_{G},\mu_{G_{t_{n}}})_{n\geq 1}
satisfy the conclusion of Theorem
2.1
and
({\sf ALG}_{n})_{n\geq 1}
is
t_{n}
-stable, then

\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}W_{2}(\mu_{G},\mu^{\mbox{\rm\tiny alg}}_{G})\geq\sqrt{\delta n}\,,

(3.2)

for some
\delta>0
.

Proof.

This follows from the triangle inequality

W_{2}(\mu_{G},\mu_{G_{t}})\leq W_{2}(\mu_{G},\mu^{\mbox{\rm\tiny alg}}_{G})+W_{2}(\mu^{\mbox{\rm\tiny alg}}_{G},\mu^{\mbox{\rm\tiny alg}}_{G_{t}})+W_{2}(\mu^{\mbox{\rm\tiny alg}}_{G_{t}},\mu_{G_{t}})\,,

for any
t
, and the fact that the first and third terms on the right-hand side have the same distribution.
∎

Many known algorithmic schemes are stable as per the above definition. Proving that a sampling algorithm
{\sf ALG}_{n}
is
t_{n}
-stable for some
t_{n}
usually reduces to showing that the map
G\mapsto{\sf ALG}_{n}(G,\omega)
is suitably Lipschitz with a constant independent of
n
. In the case of iterative algorithms the Lipschitz constant will usually depend on the number of iterations. As already mentioned in the introduction, it was shown, e.g., in
[
AMS22
, Proposition 5.2]
that the class of iterative algorithms using Lipschitz non-linearities is
t_{n}
-stable for some
t_{n}\to 0
when ran for a constant number of iterations.

Another class of approximate sampling algorithms we are especially interested in this paper are Boolean circuits of small depth and small size. We will show that circuits are
t_{n}
-stable for some
t_{n}\to 0
depending on the depth and size.

Boolean circuits

We begin by recalling some properties of Boolean circuits with controlled sizes and depth. Let
\Sigma_{r}=\{-1,+1\}^{r}
. Fix any
N,n
and consider
a Boolean circuit
\mathcal{C}:\Sigma_{N}\to\Sigma_{n}
. We write
C_{1},\ldots,C_{n}:\Sigma_{N}\to\{-1,+1\}
for the coordinates of the circuit associated with each of the
n

output nodes.
Let
s(\mathcal{C})
denote the size of the circuit, namely the number of gates and let
d(\mathcal{C})
denote the depth of the circuit which is the length of the longest path from the input layer to the output layer.

Let
m,n
be as in our setting of symmetric perceptron model with Rademacher disorder. Fix
R
with
\omega=(\omega_{i},i\in[R])\in\Sigma_{R}
representing the random seeds.
We consider a Boolean circuit
\mathcal{C}:\Sigma_{mn+R}\to\Sigma_{n}
where the first
mn
entries are the entries of the matrix
G\in\{-1,+1\}^{m\times n}
representing the instance of the perceptron model, and the remaining entries
\omega=(\omega_{i},i\in[R])
are the random seeds i.i.d. Rademacher, independently generated from
G
. The total input is described as a pair
(G,\omega)
.

Theorem 3.3
.

Fix
\alpha>0
,
m=\lfloor\alpha n\rfloor
and
R>0
and let
{\sf ALG}_{n}=\mathcal{C}:\Sigma_{mn+R}\to\Sigma_{n}
be a Boolean circuit.
For every
\gamma\in(0,1)
there exist
\beta,A>0
such that

({\sf ALG}_{n})_{n\geq 1}
is
t_{n}
-stable
as per Definition
3.1
for any
t_{n}\geq 1-n^{-1+\gamma}
, where both
G
and
G_{t}
are Rademacher with
entrywise correlation equal to
t_{n}
, provided that at least
one of the following conditions on
the depth and size of the circuit holds:

1.

Polynomial size and (sub)logarithmic depth:
s(\mathcal{C})\leq n^{A}
,
d(\mathcal{C})\leq\beta\log n/\log\log n
,

2.

Sub-exponential size and bounded depth:
s(\mathcal{C})\leq\exp(n^{\beta})
,
d(\mathcal{C})\leq A
.

We note that the dependence on
R
in the above statements is implicit since
N=mn+R
is a trivial lower bound on the circuit size
s({\mathcal{C}})
.
An immediate corollary is the following:

Corollary 3.4
.

Boolean circuits under either of the above depth and size constraints cannot approximately sample from
\mu_{G}
, in the sense of Lemma
3.2

Proof.

Indeed, Boolean circuits are
t_{n}
-stable if
\sqrt{n(1-t_{n})}\leq n^{\gamma/2}
as per Theorem
3.3
, and we recall that the pair
(\mu_{G},\mu_{G_{t_{n}}})
satisfies the conclusion of Theorem
2.1
, i.e., has transport disorder chaos, with Rademacher disorder if
\sqrt{n(1-t_{n})}\geq C\log n
. The two conditions are compatible.
∎

Theorem
3.3
is a consequence of a more precise sensitivity bound for Boolean circuits given below and used with
c<1
.

Theorem 3.5
.

For every
c,\beta,\gamma>0
satisfying
0<\beta+\gamma<c
the following holds for all
A>0
and all sufficiently large
n
.
Let
\mathcal{C}:\Sigma_{mn+R}\to\Sigma_{n}
be a Boolean circuit with
d(\mathcal{C})\leq\beta\log n/\log\log n
and
s(\mathcal{C})\leq n^{A}
. Suppose further that

\displaystyle t\geq 1-n^{-1+\gamma}.

(3.3)

Let
X=(G,\omega),X_{t}=(G_{t},\omega)
.
Then

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}{\|{\mathcal{C}}(X_{t})-{\mathcal{C}}(X)\|_{2}^{2}}\leq n^{c}.

(3.4)

Similarly, fix
c,A>0
and
\gamma<c
. Suppose
0<\beta<(c-\gamma)/A
. Then (
3.4
)
holds for all sufficiently large
n
, provided

s({\mathcal{C}})\leq\exp(n^{\beta})
,
d({\mathcal{C}})\leq A
and
t
satisfies (
3.3
) again.

The way to read this claim in a “friendly” way is as follows. Suppose we want to guarantee algorithmic

t_{n}
-stability with any fixed stability parameter
\gamma\in(0,1)
(so that
t_{n}=1-n^{-1+\gamma})
.
We then fix any
c<1
such that
\gamma<c
, and find
\beta
small enough so that
\beta<c-\gamma
.
Then per theorem above, poly-size circuits with depth at most
\beta\log n/\log\log n
are
t_{n}
-stable, since
\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}[W_{2}(\mu^{\mbox{\rm\tiny alg}}_{G},\mu^{\mbox{\rm\tiny alg}}_{G_{t}})^{2}]
is upper-bounded by the left-hand side of Eq. (
3.4
)

Similarly, fixing
A>0
arbitrarily and letting
\beta
be small enough so that
\beta<(c-\gamma)/A
, we obtain
that the circuit is
t_{n}
-stable (again with parameter
\gamma
), provided the circuit has depth at most
A
and size at most
\exp(n^{\beta})
. Therefore Theorem
3.5
implies Theorem
3.3
.

Proof of Theorem
3.5
.

Let us briefly recall the following notions from Boolean analysis/Fourier analysis on
\{-1,+1\}^{N}
; see e.g.
[
O’D14
]
for a reference.
Consider the standard Fourier expansion of functions on
\{-1,+1\}^{N}
associated with the
uniform measure on
\{-1,+1\}^{N}
. The basis of this expansion are monomials of the form

x_{S}\triangleq\prod_{i\in S}x_{i},\,S\subset[N]
. For every function
g:\{-1,+1\}^{N}\to\mathbb{R}
, the associated
Fourier coefficients are

\displaystyle\hat{g}_{S}=\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}[g(x)x_{S}],\qquad S\subset[N],

where the expectation is with respect to the uniform measure on
x=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{N})\in\{-1,+1\}^{N}
.
Then the Fourier expansion of
g
is

g=\sum_{S\subset[N]}\hat{g}_{S}x_{S}
, and the Parseval (or Walsh) identity states that
\sum_{S}\hat{g}_{S}^{2}=\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}{g(x)^{2}}
.
For
i\in[N]
, let
L_{i}
denote the Laplacian operator:

\displaystyle L_{i}g(x)\triangleq\sum_{S\ni i}\hat{g}_{S}x_{S},

and let

I(g)
be the total influence

\displaystyle I(g)=\sum_{i\in[N]}\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}{L_{i}g(x)^{2}}=\sum_{S\subset[N]}|S|\,\hat{g}_{S}^{2}.

(3.5)

Also note that

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}{L_{i}g(x)^{2}}={1\over 2}\left(\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}{L_{i}g(x_{-i,-1})^{2}}+\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}{L_{i}g(x_{-i,1})^{2}}\right),

(3.6)

where for
\ell=\pm 1
,
x_{-i,\ell}\in\{-1,+1\}^{N}
is obtained from
x
by fixing
the
i
-th coordinate of
x
to
\ell
.

Fix
\delta>0
. Let
\hat{C}_{S,j},S\subset[N]
be the Fourier coefficients of the function
C_{j}
, which
we recall is the
j
-component of
{\mathcal{C}}
. Fix a constant
c>0
and let

\displaystyle D

\displaystyle=\log(1/\delta)\left(c\log{s({\mathcal{C}})\over\delta}\right)^{d({\mathcal{C}})-1}.

(3.7)

We use the Linial-Mansour-Nisan Theorem
[
O’D14
]
(the version we take here is the version from page 93 in
[
O’D14
]
)
which states for some universal constant
c
the spectrum of a depth-
d
circuit with size
s

is
\delta
-concentrated on degree
\leq D
. More precisely, in our context, it states that for each
j=1,2,\ldots,n
,

\displaystyle\sum_{S\subset[N]\,:\,|S|>D}\left(\hat{C}_{S,j}\right)^{2}\leq\delta.

Thus for the influence function we have for each
j
,

\displaystyle I(C_{j})

\displaystyle=\sum_{S\subset[N]}|S|\left(\hat{C}_{S,j}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{S\,:\,|S|\leq D}|S|\left(\hat{C}_{S,j}\right)^{2}+N\delta

\displaystyle\leq D\sum_{S\,:\,|S|\leq D}\left(\hat{C}_{S,j}\right)^{2}+N\delta

\displaystyle\leq D\sum_{S}\left(\hat{C}_{S,j}\right)^{2}+N\delta

\displaystyle=D+N\delta,

(3.8)

where in the last equation we use the fact
C_{j}(x)\in\{-1,+1\}
and thus
1=\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}{C_{j}^{2}}=\sum_{S}\hat{C}_{S,j}^{2}
.

Combining (
3.8
),(
3.5
) and (
3.6
) we obtain for each
j
,

\displaystyle D+N\delta\geq\sum_{i\in[N]}{1\over 2}\left(\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}{L_{i}C_{j}(x_{-i,-1})^{2}+L_{i}C_{j}(x_{-i,1})^{2}}\right).

Using the inequality
a^{2}+b^{2}\geq\frac{1}{2}(a-b)^{2}
, the right-hand side is at least

\displaystyle{1\over 4}\sum_{i\in[N]}\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}{\left(L_{i}C_{j}(x_{-i,-1})-L_{i}C_{j}(x_{-i,1})\right)^{2}}.

Note that for any
g
,

\displaystyle L_{i}g(x_{-i,-1})-L_{i}g(x_{-i,1})=g(x_{-i,-1})-g(x_{-i,1}).

We obtain that the previous expression is

\displaystyle{1\over 4}\sum_{i\in[N]}\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}{\left(C_{j}(x_{-i,-1})-C_{j}(x_{-i,1})\right)^{2}}\,.

Summing over
j\in[n]
we obtain

\displaystyle(D+N\delta)n

\displaystyle\geq{1\over 4}\sum_{i\in[N]}\sum_{1\leq j\leq n}\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}{\left(C_{j}(x_{-i,-1})-C_{j}(x_{-i,1})\right)^{2}}\,.

Denote the expectation term by
\Delta_{i,j}
so that

\displaystyle(D+N\delta)n\geq{1\over 4}\sum_{i\in[N]}\sum_{1\leq j\leq n}\Delta_{i,j}\,.

(3.9)

Now consider the process of obtaining
X_{t}
from
X
. Let

[N_{0}]=[mn]
be the first
mn
coordinates of
X
corresponding to the matrix
G
,
and let
{\mathcal{S}}\subset[N_{0}]
denote the random subset of coordinates of
X
which are
flipped in the transition
X\to X_{t}
.
This is because every resampled entry of
X
is actually flipped with probability
1/2
.
Conditional on
{\mathcal{S}}=S
we have
X_{t}=X_{-S}
where for any
x\in\Sigma_{N}
and any set
R\subset[N]

x_{-R}
is obtained from
x

by flipping entries
x_{i}
for
i\in R
and keeping the remaining entries intact. Writing
S=\{i_{1},\ldots,i_{|S|}\}
we have

\displaystyle C_{j}(X)-C_{j}(X_{S})=\sum_{1\leq r\leq|S|}C_{j}(X_{-(i_{1},\ldots,i_{r-1})})-C_{j}(X_{-(i_{1},\ldots,i_{r})})\,,

where
X_{-(i_{1},\ldots,i_{r-1})}=X
by default when
r=1
.
Noting that
C_{j}(X)-C_{j}(X_{S})\in\{-2,0,2\}
we have by union bound

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}[(C_{j}(X)-C_{j}(X_{S}))^{2}|{\mathcal{S}}=S]

\displaystyle=4\mathbb{P}\left(C_{j}(X)\neq C_{j}(X_{S})|{\mathcal{S}}=S\right)

\displaystyle\leq 4\sum_{1\leq r\leq|S|}\mathbb{P}\left(C_{j}(X_{-(i_{1},\ldots,i_{r})})\neq C_{j}(X_{-(i_{1},\ldots,i_{r-1})})|{\mathcal{S}}=S\right)\,.

We note however that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(C_{j}(X_{-(i_{1},\ldots,i_{r}}))\neq C_{j}(X_{-(i_{1},\ldots,i_{r-1})})|{\mathcal{S}}=S\right)

\displaystyle=(1/4)\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}{\left(C_{j}(x_{-i_{r},-1})-C_{j}(x_{-i_{r},1})\right)^{2}}

\displaystyle=(1/4)\Delta_{i_{r},j}\,,

because flipping entries in
i_{1},\ldots,i_{r-1}
does not change
X_{-(i_{1},\ldots,i_{r-1})}
from
X
distributionally.
We conclude

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}[(C_{j}(X)-C_{j}(X_{t}))^{2}|{\mathcal{S}}=S]\leq\sum_{i\in S}\Delta_{i,j}\,.

Taking an expectation over
\mathcal{S}
we obtain

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}{(C_{j}(X)-C_{j}(X_{t}))^{2}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{S\subset[N_{0}]}\mathbb{P}({\mathcal{S}}=S)\sum_{i\in S}\Delta_{i,j}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\in[N_{0}]}\Delta_{i,j}\sum_{S\subset[N]}\mathbb{P}({\mathcal{S}}=S){\bf 1}(i\in S)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\in[N_{0}]}\Delta_{i,j}\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}{{\bf 1}(i\in{\mathcal{S}})}

\displaystyle={1-t\over 2}\sum_{i\in[N_{0}]}\Delta_{i,j},

since the entries are resampled with probability
1-t
and flipping occurs with probability
1/2
.

Summing over
j\in[n]
and using (
3.9
) we obtain

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}{\|C(X)-C(X_{t})\|_{2}^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq{1-t\over 2}\sum_{j\in[n]}\sum_{i\in[N_{0}]}\Delta_{i,j}

\displaystyle\leq 4(D+N\delta)n{1-t\over 2}

\displaystyle=2Dn(1-t)+2N\delta n(1-t)\,.

We now set
\delta=n^{-A-2}
(any exponent larger than
A+1
will do).
We first control the second term namely
2N\delta nt
.
Recall the assumed bounds on
s({\mathcal{C}})
and
d({\mathcal{C}})
. In particular
N\leq s({\mathcal{C}})\leq n^{A}
.
We obtain
N\delta n(1-t)\leq N\delta n=O(1/n)=o(1)
.

We now control the first term, namely
2Dn(1-t)
.
From (
3.7
) we obtain

\displaystyle D\leq(A+2)\log(n)\left(c(2A+2)\log n\right)^{d({\mathcal{C}})}.

We have

\displaystyle\left(\log n\right)^{d({\mathcal{C}})}\leq\left(\log n\right)^{\beta\log n/\log\log n}=n^{\beta},

implying
D\leq n^{\beta(1+o(1))}
.
Thus recalling our bound on
1-t\leq 1/n^{1-\gamma}
we obtain

\displaystyle Dn(1-t)=n^{\beta(1+o(1))}n^{\gamma}.

Since
\beta<c-\gamma
we have
Dn(1-t)=o(n^{c})
and the first claimed bound
n^{c}
is obtained for
n
large enough.

To prove the second part recall bounds on
s({\mathcal{C}})
and
d({\mathcal{C}})
.
We now choose
\delta=1/(e^{n^{\beta}}n)
(any function decaying faster than
1/(e^{n^{\beta}})
would do).
Then from (
3.7
) we obtain

\displaystyle D

\displaystyle\leq\left(n^{\beta}+\log n\right)\left(c\log\left(s({\mathcal{C}})e^{n^{\beta}}n\right)\right)^{A-1}

\displaystyle\leq\left(n^{\beta}+\log n\right)\left(2cn^{\beta}+c\log n\right)^{A-1}.

Thus
D=n^{\beta A(1+o(1))}
, implying

Dn(1-t)=n^{(\beta A+\gamma)(1+o(1))}
, again due to our bound on
t
.
Since
\beta A<c-\gamma
we obtain
Dn(1-t)=o(n^{c})
. Additionally

\displaystyle Nn\delta(1-t)\leq Nn\delta\leq s({\mathcal{C}})n\delta\leq e^{n^{\beta}}n\delta\leq 1\,,

by our choice of
\delta
.

∎

Appendix A
The derivative of
q_{\kappa}(t)

Here we show that

q_{\kappa}^{\prime}(t)=\frac{1}{\pi}\frac{e^{-\kappa^{2}/2}}{\sqrt{1-t^{2}}}\left(e^{-\kappa^{2}(1-t)/2(1+t)}-e^{-\kappa^{2}(1+t)/2(1-t)}\right)\,.

Indeed, let
\phi
denote standard normal density,
Z,Z^{\prime}\sim N(0,1)
i.i.d., and define for
0\leq t<1
,

a_{\pm}(t)=\frac{\pm\kappa-tZ}{\sqrt{1-t^{2}}}\,.

We first have

a_{\pm}^{\prime}(t)=-\frac{Z}{\sqrt{1-t^{2}}}+\frac{(\pm\kappa-tZ)t}{(1-t^{2})^{3/2}}=\frac{\pm\kappa t-Z}{(1-t^{2})^{3/2}}\,.

Next,

\displaystyle q_{\kappa}^{\prime}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\operatorname{\mathbb{P}}\left(|Z|\leq\kappa\,,|tZ+\sqrt{1-t^{2}}Z^{\prime}|\leq\kappa\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\Big[\mathds{1}\{|Z|\leq\kappa\}\int_{a_{-}(t)}^{a_{+}(t)}\phi(y)\mathrm{d}y\Big]

\displaystyle=\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\Big[\mathds{1}\{|Z|\leq\kappa\}\big(a_{+}^{\prime}(t)\phi(a_{+}(t))-a_{-}^{\prime}(t)\phi(a_{-}(t))\big)\Big]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}(1-t^{2})^{3/2}}\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\Big[\mathds{1}\{|Z|\leq\kappa\}\Big((\kappa t-Z)e^{-(\kappa-tZ)^{2}/2(1-t^{2})}

\displaystyle\hskip 162.18062pt+(\kappa t+Z)e^{-(\kappa+tZ)^{2}/2(1-t^{2})}\Big)\Big]\,.

Next we observe that the two terms inside the expectation are images of each other by a sign flip of
Z
, so

\displaystyle q_{\kappa}^{\prime}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{\sqrt{2\pi}(1-t^{2})^{3/2}}\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\Big[\mathds{1}\{|Z|\leq\kappa\}(\kappa t-Z)e^{-(\kappa-tZ)^{2}/2(1-t^{2})}\Big]

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{\sqrt{2\pi}(1-t^{2})^{3/2}}\int_{-\kappa}^{\kappa}(\kappa t-z)e^{-(\kappa-tz)^{2}/2(1-t^{2})-z^{2}/2}\frac{\mathrm{d}z}{\sqrt{2\pi}}

\displaystyle=\frac{2e^{-\kappa^{2}/2}}{\sqrt{2\pi}(1-t^{2})^{3/2}}\int_{-\kappa}^{\kappa}(\kappa t-z)e^{-(\kappa t-z)^{2}/2(1-t^{2})}\frac{\mathrm{d}z}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\,,

where the last line follows from the identity
(\kappa-tz)^{2}+(1-t^{2})z^{2}=\kappa^{2}(1-t^{2})+(\kappa t-z)^{2}
.
By the change of variables
z^{\prime}=(\kappa t-z)/\sqrt{1-t^{2}}
we obtain

\displaystyle q_{\kappa}^{\prime}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{2e^{-\kappa^{2}/2}}{\sqrt{2\pi(1-t^{2})}}\int_{-\kappa(1-t)/\sqrt{1-t^{2}}}^{\kappa(1+t)/\sqrt{1-t^{2}}}z^{\prime}e^{-z^{\prime 2}/2}\frac{\mathrm{d}z^{\prime}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}

\displaystyle=\frac{2e^{-\kappa^{2}/2}}{\sqrt{2\pi(1-t^{2})}}\Big(\phi\Big(\kappa\sqrt{\frac{1-t}{1+t}}\Big)-\phi\Big(\kappa\sqrt{\frac{1+t}{1-t}}\Big)\Big)\,.
