Kinetic derivation of an inviscid compressible Leslie–Ericksen equation for rarified calamitic gases Thanks: Submitted to the editors DATE.
Patrick E. Farrell, Giovanni Russo, Umberto Zerbinati

Abstract
Nematic ordering describes the phenomenon where anisotropic molecules tend to locally align, like matches in a matchbox. This ordering can arise in solids (as nematic elastomers), liquids (as liquid crystals), and in gases. In the 1940s, Onsager described how nematic ordering can arise in dilute colloidal suspensions from the molecular point of view. However, the kinetic theory of nonspherical molecules has not, thus far, accounted for phenomena relating to the presence of nematic ordering. In this work we develop a kinetic theory for the behavior of rarified calamitic (rodlike) gases in the presence of nematic ordering. Building on previous work by Curtiss, we derive from kinetic theory the rate of work hypothesis that forms the starting point for Leslie–Ericksen theory. We incorporate ideas from the variational theory of nematic liquid crystals to create a moment closure that preserves the coupling between the laws of linear and angular momentum. The coupling between these laws is a key feature of our theory, in contrast to the kinetic theory proposed by Curtiss & Dahler, where the couple stress tensor is assumed to be zero. This coupling allows the characterization of anisotropic phenomena arising from the nematic ordering. Furthermore, the theory leads to an energy functional that is a compressible variant of the classical Oseen–Frank energy (with a pressure-dependent Frank constant) and to an inviscid compressible analogue of the Leslie–Ericksen equations. The emergence of compressible aspects in the theory for nematic fluids enhances our understanding of these complex systems.

In recent years there has been much interest in the mathematical modeling of liquid crystals

In many liquid crystals, nematic ordering arises due to the calamitic (rodlike) nature of the constituent molecules; the short-range interaction potential causes nearby particles to be aligned, or the molecules are aligned by the action of external fields.

In this work we develop a kinetic theory for rarified calamitic gases in the presence of nematic ordering. This is a first step towards a kinetic theory of nematic ordering in calamitic fluids, substances with very low shear modulus whose constituent molecules are rodlike. 3
Leslie–Ericksen rate of work hypothesis

To take into account the anisotropic behaviour that stems from the equation of angular momentum, our objective will be to find a coupling between (

\mathbb{M}
is identically zero.
If
\mathbb{M}

It is worth mentioning that such coupling exists in the standard dynamic theory of liquid crystals, arising as the Ericksen tensor in the Leslie–Ericksen equations.

For this reason, our first task will be to connect (

where we employ the chain rule, the Leibniz rule, (

We now substitute the previous equation into (

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\Big(n\langle\!\langle\theta\rangle\!\rangle\Big)+\nabla_{\bm{q}}\cdot\Big[n\bm{v}_{0}\langle\!\langle\theta\rangle\!\rangle\Big]+(\rho\mathbb{P}):\nabla_{\bm{q}}\bm{v}_{0}

Last we would like to point out that (

4
Maxwellian distribution

In this section we derive constitutive relations for the stress tensor, the couple stress tensor, and the internal energy from the Maxwellian distribution for the Boltzmann–Curtiss equation (
1
), i.e. that
f^{(0)}
satisfying
C[f^{(0)},f^{0}]=0
. In seminal work by Curtiss
[
7
,
8
]
, this distribution was obtained using formal computations from statistical mechanics.
The Maxwellian is given by

f^{(0)}(\bm{\alpha},\bm{V},\bm{\Omega})=\frac{nQ\sin(\alpha_{2})}{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}Q\sin(\alpha_{2})\,d\alpha}\frac{m^{\frac{3}{2}}(I_{1}I_{2}I_{3})^{\frac{1}{2}}}{(\frac{4}{\mathcal{N}}\pi\langle\!\langle\theta\rangle\!\rangle)^{3}}\exp\Big[-m\frac{\lvert\bm{V}\rvert^{2}}{\frac{4}{\mathcal{N}}\langle\!\langle\theta\rangle\!\rangle}-\frac{\bm{\Omega}\cdot\mathbb{I}\bm{\Omega}}{\frac{4}{\mathcal{N}}\langle\!\langle\theta\rangle\!\rangle}\Big]

(37)

where
Q=\exp(\frac{\bm{\omega}_{0}\cdot\mathbb{I}\bm{\omega}_{0}}{\frac{2}{3}\langle\!\langle\theta\rangle\!\rangle})
and both
\omega_{0}
and
\theta
are assumed to remain constant at equilibrium.
Notice that the distribution here presented is the “absolute Maxwellian” since it is spatially homogeneous, i.e. we are assuming that at equilibrium
n,\bm{V},\bm{\Omega},\theta
are spatially independent quantities.

The only difference between the formulation of the Maxwellian considered here and the one presented in
[
7
,
8
]
is that (
37
) is expressed in terms of internal energy
\langle\!\langle\theta\rangle\!\rangle
rather than in terms of kinetic temperature.
If one is interested in obtaining (
37
) from
[
7
,
8
]
one only has to apply the equipartition of energy theorem, i.e.

\langle\!\langle\theta\rangle\!\rangle=\frac{\mathcal{N}}{2}k_{B}T,

(38)

where
T
is the kinetic temperature,
\mathcal{N}
is the number of degrees of freedom appearing in the Hamiltonian of a single molecule and
k_{B}
is the Boltzmann constant.

What is the correct value of
\mathcal{N}
for a polyatomic gas? To answer this question we consider linear diatomic gases, such as
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
,
\text{N}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
and
\text{O}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
.
We can think of these polyatomic gases as two spheres connected by a spring and therefore the Hamiltonian of this model would have seven degrees of freedom: the moments of the centers of mass of the two spheres, and the length of the spring connecting the two centers of mass.
Yet we know that vibration modes for the previously mentioned diatomic molecules are excited only above
2000K
, a temperature well above the regime of interest here.
This suggests that we can ignore the degrees of freedom corresponding to the spring connecting the center of mass of each atom and model the polyatomic gas molecule as a rigid body.
Lastly, in a later section, the hypothesis that our molecules are slender bodies will be essential to ignore some terms in the expansion of the inertia tensor.
In the slender body limit, we can regard our molecules as segments in space, thus losing one rotational degree of freedom in the kinetic energy; more details are provided in the supplementary material,
A
.
This suggests that in our regime of interest, we have
\mathcal{N}=5
and the correct relation arising from the equipartition of energy theorem would be

\langle\!\langle\theta\rangle\!\rangle=\frac{5}{2}k_{B}T.

(39)

We notice that the emergence of a nematic ordering implies that the remaining five rotational degrees of freedom are not independent among molecules and therefore the heat capacity of the nematic gas should be less than
\frac{5}{2}R
per mole, where
R
denotes the gas constant.
These difficulties in understanding the correct way to apply the equipartition of energy theorem are reflected in the experimental observation of the heat capacity of nematic fluids such as liquid crystals
[
29
]
.
In particular, in
[
29
]
the author observes a sharp increase in the specific heat capacity of the liquid crystal MBBA at constant pressure when the temperature is increased from
319K
to
324K
.
Such behaviour is anomalous compared to what is observed for polyatomic gases such as
\text{N}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
, for which the specific heat capacity at constant pressure is constant between
123K
and
573K

[
5
]
.
For this reason, we decided to treat non-spherical rarefied gases in the presence of nematic alignment in a standard manner, i.e. assuming (
39
), and devote future work to this particular issue.

We now proceed to use the Maxwellian distribution (
37
) to obtain constitutive relations for the stress tensor, the couple stress tensor, and the internal energy.
We can compute the pressure tensor near equilibrium by evaluating
\langle\!\langle\bm{V}\otimes\bm{V}\rangle\!\rangle
using the Maxwellian
f^{(0)}
as a probability distribution.
It turns out that the pressure tensor near equilibrium is nothing more than the variance of the Maxwellian distribution, i.e.

\mathbb{P}^{(0)}=\langle\!\langle\bm{V}\otimes\bm{V}\rangle\!\rangle_{\sim f^{(0)}}=\frac{2(I_{1}I_{2}I_{3})^{\frac{1}{2}}}{5m}\langle\!\langle\theta\rangle\!\rangle I.

(40)

Following the same reasoning we can compute also the couple stress tensor, which turns out to be zero:

\mathbb{M}^{(0)}=\langle\!\langle\bm{V}(\mathbb{I}\bm{\omega})\rangle\!\rangle_{\sim f^{(0)}}=\mathbb{E}_{f^{(0)}_{\bm{\Omega}}}\big[\mathbb{I}\bm{\omega}\big]\mathbb{E}_{f^{(0)}_{\bm{V}}}\big[\bm{V}\big]=0.

(41)

This follows from the Fubini–Tonelli Theorem, the fact that
f^{(0)}=f^{(0)}_{\bm{V}}f^{(0)}_{\bm{\Omega}}f^{(0)}_{\bm{\alpha}}
, and that
f^{(0)}_{\bm{V}}
has zero mean.
In particular, since we know
\bm{\xi}
is also zero, we can use (
22c
) to deduce that
\dot{\bm{\eta}}=0
.
In what follows, we will be interested in weighting the pressure tensor and the couple stress tensor by the fluid density, and for this reason, we introduce the kinetic pressure

p_{K}\coloneqq tr[\rho\mathbb{P}^{(0)}]=\frac{6}{5}\frac{\rho}{m}(I_{1}I_{2}I_{3})^{\frac{1}{2}}\langle\!\langle\theta\rangle\!\rangle.

(42)

5
Oseen–Frank energy

Since we now know that energy stored in the particle ensemble is given by
\psi=\langle\!\langle\frac{1}{2}m\lvert\bm{v}\rvert^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\bm{\omega}\cdot\mathbb{I}\bm{\omega}\rangle\!\rangle
we can proceed by rewriting,

\displaystyle\psi=\frac{1}{2}\langle\!\langle m\lvert\bm{v}\rvert^{2}+\bm{\omega}\cdot\mathbb{I}\bm{\omega}\rangle\!\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{m}{2}\langle\!\langle\lvert\bm{V}\rvert^{2}\rangle\!\rangle+\frac{m}{2}\lvert\bm{v}_{0}\rvert^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\langle\!\langle\bm{\Omega}\cdot\mathbb{I}\bm{\Omega}\rangle\!\rangle+\frac{1}{2}\langle\!\langle\bm{\omega}\cdot\mathbb{I}\rangle\!\rangle\cdot\bm{\omega}_{0}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\Big[\langle\!\langle m\lvert\bm{V}\rvert^{2}\rangle\!\rangle+\langle\!\langle\bm{\Omega}\cdot\mathbb{I}\bm{\Omega}\rangle\!\rangle\Big]+\frac{1}{2}\Big[m\lvert\bm{v}_{0}\rvert^{2}+\bm{\omega_{0}}\cdot\overline{\mathbb{I}}\bm{\omega}_{0}\Big]

\displaystyle=:\psi_{0}+\psi_{K},

(43)

where we make use of the fact that both
\langle\!\langle V\rangle\!\rangle
and
\langle\!\langle\mathbb{I}\bm{\Omega}\rangle\!\rangle
are zero, and that
\bm{\omega}_{0}=\langle\!\langle\mathbb{I}\rangle\!\rangle^{{}^{-1}}\bm{\eta}
.
The first term in (
43
) is the internal energy
\langle\!\langle\theta\rangle\!\rangle
, from now on denoted
\psi_{0}
to avoid using the
\langle\!\langle\cdot\rangle\!\rangle
notation.
The second term is the kinetic energy. It may be counterintuitive to identify
\psi_{0}(\bm{q},t)
as the internal energy, but from the decomposition (
43
) we can obtain:

\psi_{0}=\psi-\psi_{K}

(44)

which can be interpreted as the well-known decomposition of internal energy into total energy minus macroscopic kinetic energy.

We will next derive a slightly different decomposition in order to relate some terms of
\psi(\bm{q},t)
to the classical Oseen–Frank energy functional
[
15
,
30
]
.
It is a well-known fact that the inertia tensor of a needle-shaped molecule, i.e. the vanishing-girth limit of a calamitic molecule, can be decomposed as:

\mathbb{I}=\lambda_{1}\Big(I-\bm{\nu}\otimes\bm{\nu}^{T}\Big)

(45)

with
\bm{\nu}\in\mathbb{S}^{2}
.
Motivated by (
45
) we assume the following decomposition holds for calamitic molecules:

\mathbb{I}=\lambda_{1}\Big(I-\bm{\nu}\otimes\bm{\nu}\Big)+\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon),

(46)

where now
\bm{\nu}(\bm{q},t)\in\mathbb{S}^{2}
is the nematic vector, first introduced in
[
13
]
, and
\varepsilon\approx\left(\delta/\ell\right)^{2}
, where
\delta
and
\ell
are respectively the diameter of the spherical hemisphere at the end of the calamitic molecule and the height of the calamitic molecule.
Notice that
\bm{\nu}
is normally defined in terms of the orientation distribution function (ODF), i.e.
\bm{\nu}=\langle\!\langle\bm{\mathcal{N}}(\bm{\alpha})\rangle\!\rangle
, where
\bm{\mathcal{N}}(\bm{\alpha})
is the unit vector corresponding to the Euler angles
\bm{\alpha}
.

The introduction of a nematic director allows for substantial simplifications of computations involving the average of the inertia tensor, since under the hypothesis of the emergence of a nematic ordering we are allowed to move the inertia tensor expressed as in (
46
) outside the
\langle\!\langle\cdot\rangle\!\rangle
.
Since by theorem
9
the material time derivative
\dot{\bm{\nu}}
can be computed as
\bm{\omega}\times\bm{\nu}=\dot{\bm{\nu}}
, we can obtain:

\displaystyle\langle\!\langle\bm{\omega}_{0}\cdot\mathbb{I}\bm{\omega}_{0}\rangle\!\rangle

\displaystyle=\lambda_{1}\Big[\bm{\omega}_{0}\cdot\bm{\omega}_{0}-\langle\!\langle(\bm{\omega}_{0}\cdot\bm{\nu})(\bm{\omega}_{0}\cdot\bm{\nu})\rangle\!\rangle\Big]+\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon)

(47)

\displaystyle=\lambda_{1}\dot{\bm{\nu}}\cdot\dot{\bm{\nu}}{\,+\,\overset{\circ}{\bm{\nu}}\cdot\overset{\circ}{\bm{\nu}}-2(\dot{\bm{\nu}}\cdot\overset{\circ}{\bm{\nu}})}+\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon),

(48)

using the triple product identity
(\bm{\omega}_{0}\times\bm{\nu})\cdot(\bm{\omega}_{0}\times\bm{\nu})=(\bm{\omega}_{0}\cdot\bm{\omega}_{0})(\bm{\nu}\cdot\bm{\nu})-(\bm{\omega}_{0}\cdot\bm{\nu})(\bm{\nu}\cdot\bm{\omega}_{0})
, together with the definition of the corotational time derivative of the director, i.e.
\overset{\circ}{\bm{\nu}}\coloneqq\dot{\bm{\nu}}-\bm{\omega}_{0}\times\bm{\nu}
.
This simplifies the kinetic energy

\psi_{K}=\frac{1}{2}m(\bm{v}_{0}\cdot\bm{v}_{0})+\frac{\lambda_{1}}{2}\Big(\dot{\bm{\nu}}\cdot\dot{\bm{\nu}}+{\overset{\circ}{\bm{\nu}}\cdot\overset{\circ}{\bm{\nu}}-2(\dot{\bm{\nu}}\cdot\overset{\circ}{\bm{\nu}})}\Big)+\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon)

(49)

which in the vanishing girth limit, i.e.
\varepsilon\searrow 0
, becomes
\psi_{K}(\bm{q},t)=\frac{1}{2}m(\bm{v}_{0}\cdot\bm{v}_{0})+\frac{\lambda_{1}}{2}(\dot{\bm{\nu}}\cdot\dot{\bm{\nu}}+\overset{\circ}{\bm{\nu}}\cdot\overset{\circ}{\bm{\nu}}-2\dot{\bm{\nu}}\cdot\overset{\circ}{\bm{\nu}})
.
We proceed with our analysis of
\psi_{0}(\bm{q},t)
, making use of the fact that by theorem
9

\bm{\omega}\times\bm{\nu}=\partial_{t}\bm{\nu}+(\nabla_{\bm{q}}\bm{\nu})\bm{v}
, hence we can expand the
\langle\!\langle\bm{\omega}\cdot\mathbb{I}\bm{\omega}\rangle\!\rangle
in
\psi
as

\displaystyle 2\lambda_{1}^{-1}\langle\!\langle\bm{\omega}\cdot\mathbb{I}\bm{\omega}\rangle\!\rangle

\displaystyle=\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\langle\!\langle(\bm{\omega}\times\bm{\nu})\cdot(\bm{\omega}\times\bm{\nu})\rangle\!\rangle=\langle\!\langle\Big(\partial_{t}\bm{\nu}+(\nabla_{\bm{q}}\bm{\nu})\bm{v}\Big)^{T}\Big(\partial_{t}\bm{\nu}+(\nabla_{\bm{q}}\bm{\nu})\bm{v}\Big)\rangle\!\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\!\langle\lvert\partial_{t}\bm{\nu}\rvert\rangle\!\rangle^{2}+2\langle\!\langle(\partial_{t}\bm{\nu})^{T}\Big((\nabla_{\bm{q}}\bm{\nu})\bm{v}\Big)\rangle\!\rangle+\tr\langle\!\langle\nabla_{\bm{q}}{\bm{\nu}}(\bm{v}\otimes\bm{v})\nabla_{\bm{q}}\bm{\nu}^{T}\rangle\!\rangle,

where the last term has been obtained by
tr(\bm{a}\bm{b}^{T})=\bm{a}^{T}\bm{b}
.
Furthermore, we notice that in the mixed-term

\langle\!\langle 2(\partial_{t}\bm{\nu})^{T}\Big((\nabla_{\bm{q}}\bm{\nu})\bm{v}\Big)\rangle\!\rangle=-2\langle\!\langle\lvert\partial_{t}\bm{\nu}\rvert^{2}\rangle\!\rangle+\langle\!\langle 2\partial_{t}{\bm{\nu}}\cdot\dot{\bm{\nu}}\rangle\!\rangle,

(50)

the last term vanishes because
\partial_{t}{\bm{\nu}}\cdot\dot{\bm{\nu}}=\partial_{t}\bm{\nu}\cdot(\bm{\omega}\times\bm{\nu})=\bm{\omega}\cdot(\partial_{t}\bm{\nu}\times\bm{\nu})=0
, as
\bm{\nu}\times\bm{\nu}=0
.
We are now left with the following expression for the internal energy:

\displaystyle\psi_{0}

\displaystyle=\psi-\psi_{K}=\psi-\frac{1}{2}m(\bm{v}_{0}\cdot\bm{v}_{0})-\frac{\lambda_{1}}{2}(\dot{\bm{\nu}}\cdot\dot{\bm{\nu}})+{\frac{\lambda_{1}}{2}(\overset{\circ}{\bm{\nu}}\cdot\overset{\circ}{\bm{\nu}})-\lambda(\dot{\bm{\nu}}\cdot\overset{\circ}{\bm{\nu}})}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}m\langle\!\langle\lvert\bm{v}\rvert^{2}\rangle\!\rangle+\frac{\lambda_{1}}{2}\tr\langle\!\langle\nabla_{\bm{q}}{\bm{\nu}}(\bm{v}\otimes\bm{v})\nabla_{\bm{q}}\bm{\nu}^{T}\rangle\!\rangle-\frac{1}{2}m(\bm{v}_{0}\cdot\bm{v}_{0})

\displaystyle-\frac{\lambda_{1}}{2}(\dot{\bm{\nu}}\cdot\dot{\bm{\nu}})-{\frac{\lambda_{1}}{2}}\langle\!\langle(\partial_{t}\bm{\nu}\cdot\partial_{t}\bm{\nu})\rangle\!\rangle+{\frac{\lambda_{1}}{2}(\overset{\circ}{\bm{\nu}}\cdot\overset{\circ}{\bm{\nu}})-\lambda(\dot{\bm{\nu}}\cdot\overset{\circ}{\bm{\nu}})}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\langle\!\langle\lvert\bm{v}^{2}\rvert\rangle\!\rangle+\frac{\lambda_{1}}{2}\tr\Big[\nabla_{\bm{q}}\bm{\nu}\langle\!\langle\Pi\rangle\!\rangle\nabla_{\bm{q}}\bm{\nu}^{T}\Big]-\frac{1}{2}m(\bm{v}_{0}\cdot\bm{v}_{0})

\displaystyle-\frac{\lambda_{1}}{2}(\dot{\bm{\nu}}\cdot\dot{\bm{\nu}})-{\frac{\lambda_{1}}{2}}\langle\!\langle(\partial_{t}\bm{\nu}\cdot\partial_{t}\bm{\nu})\rangle\!\rangle+{\frac{\lambda_{1}}{2}(\overset{\circ}{\bm{\nu}}\cdot\overset{\circ}{\bm{\nu}})-\lambda(\dot{\bm{\nu}}\cdot\overset{\circ}{\bm{\nu}})}

Using the Maxwellian to compute

Such a dependence is a consequence of the fact that the principle of virtual work we started from and the constitutive relation for the pressure tensor we used were derived from the Boltzmann–Curtiss equation, which describes the dynamics of compressible fluids.

7
Conclusions

In this work we have derived a set of equations describing the evolution of a rarefied gas subject to a nematic ordering near thermodynamic equilibrium, using a novel moment closure technique inspired by the Noll–Coleman procedure.
At the heart of the proposed moment closure technique remains the Chapman–Enskog expansion, which approximates the distribution function as a perturbation from the Maxwellian with order corresponding to increasing powers of the Knudsen number.
We considered the first-order approximation, which is only valid near thermodynamic equilibrium, i.e. for extremely small Knudsen numbers.
The derived equations are an inviscid compressible variant of the Leslie–Ericksen equations, which describe the evolution of the density, the velocity, the pressure, the nematic director, the Lagrange multiplier enforcing the unit length constraint on the nematic director, and the internal energy.

We remark that the main differences between the derived equations and the classical Leslie–Ericksen equations are the compressibility of the fluid and the lack of viscosity.
This last point is a consequence of the well-known fact that the first-order Chapman–Enskog expansion does not capture viscous effects.

In future work, we plan to extend our derivation using a higher-order Chapman–Enskog expansions or the van Kampen elimination procedure to include viscous effects
[
12
]
.
We hope such an extension will allow us to derive a viscous compressible variant of the Leslie–Ericksen equations.

Lastly, we plan to numerically investigate the behavior of the derived equations and their viscous extension, to better understand the role of the nematic ordering in the dynamics of rarefied gases and to compare the results with the classical Leslie–Ericksen equations.

Rational mechanics of rigid bodies

Here we give a brief summary of the basic notions of the rational mechanics of rigid bodies.
In particular, we will discuss our choice of generalized coordinates used to describe the configuration space of a rigid body and what are the corresponding conjugate momenta.
We will assume our calamitic molecules can be modeled as a discrete rigid system of
N
material points and limit our focus to this specific case. It is trivial to extend the notions here presented to the case
N\to\infty

The presentation is primarily drawn from
[
14
]
.
We redirect the reader interested in a more in-depth study of this topic to Goldstein et al. or Whittaker
[
17

40
]

Definition 6
.

We call a rigid body a set of material points
\{(P_{1},m_{1})\}_{i=1}^{N}
, that at all time satisfy a set of so-called rigidity constraints:

\lVert P_{i}-P_{j}\rVert=R_{ij},\qquad 1\leq i\leq j\leq N.

(71)

where
\lVert\cdot\rVert
denotes the standard Euclidean norm in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
.
Furthermore, we require the rigidity constraints to satisfy the Euclidean compatibility conditions, i.e.

1.

R_{ij}\geq 0
and
R_{ij}=0
if and only if
P_{i}=P_{j}
,

2.

if
P_{i^{\prime}}=\lambda P_{i}
and
P_{j^{\prime}}=\lambda P_{j}
then
R_{i^{\prime}j^{\prime}}=\lambda R_{ij}
,

3.

R_{ij}\leq R_{ik}+R_{kj}
for all
1\leq k<j\leq N
.

Lastly, we require that if
N>3
, not all
\frac{N(N-1)}{2}
equations are independent. In particular we require the system of the rigidity equations to have rank at most
3
.

The system of rigidity constraints having at most rank 3 implies that once the position of three points are determined, all other points of the material body can be located using the rigidity constraints.

If we have a rigid body consisting of three unaligned material points,
P_{1}
,
P_{2}
, and
P_{3}
, and a fixed reference frame
(O,\bm{e}_{1},\bm{e}_{2},\bm{e}_{3})
we can identify the position of the three points assigning three coordinates to
P_{1}
, two coordinates to
P_{2}
and one coordinate to
P_{3}
.
Therefore, any three unaligned points can be identified by six coordinates.
Notice also that this tells us that any rigid body can be identified by six coordinates provided that it contains at least three unaligned points. In the case where all points in the system are aligned, we lose one degree of freedom because we only need five coordinates to identify two key material points and then all the other material points of the rigid body can be located using the rigidity constraints.

yields

\displaystyle\bm{\omega}

\displaystyle=\Big(\dot{\alpha_{2}}\cos(\alpha_{3})+\dot{\alpha_{1}}\sin(\alpha_{2})\sin(\alpha_{3})\Big)\hat{\bm{x}}

(79)

\displaystyle+\Big(-\dot{\alpha_{2}}\sin(\alpha_{3})+\dot{\alpha_{1}}\sin(\alpha_{2})\cos(\alpha_{3})\Big)\hat{\bm{y}}+\Big(\dot{\alpha_{3}}+\dot{\alpha_{1}}\cos(\alpha_{2})\Big)\hat{\bm{z}}.

We can rewrite the previous expression in matrix form as follows,

\bm{\omega}=\begin{bmatrix}\sin(\alpha_{2})\sin(\alpha_{3})&\cos(\alpha_{3})&0\\
\sin(\alpha_{2})\cos(\alpha_{3})&-\sin(\alpha_{3})&0\\
\cos(\alpha_{2})&0&1\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\dot{\alpha_{1}}\\
\dot{\alpha_{2}}\\
\dot{\alpha_{3}}\end{bmatrix}=\Xi\dot{\bm{\alpha}}.

(80)

We now introduce notions related to the dynamic aspects of rigid bodies, rather than the kinematic aspects.

Definition 10
.

We define the
center of mass
of a rigid body as

O_{M}=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{i=1}^{N}m_{i}P_{i},

(81)

where
M
is the total mass of the rigid body, i.e.
M=\sum_{i=1}^{N}m_{i}
.

Once the center of mass has been defined we can introduce the notion of
inertia moment
with respect to the planes identified respectively by the orthogonal normals
\bm{n}
and
\bm{n}^{\prime}
, i.e.

\Gamma_{\bm{n}\bm{n}^{\prime}}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}m_{i}\Big[(P_{i}-O_{M})\cdot\bm{n}\Big]\Big[(P_{i}-O_{M})\cdot\bm{n}^{\prime}\Big].

(82)

Once we are equipped with this definition we can introduce the notion of inertia tensor with respect to a reference frame
(O,\bm{x},\bm{y},\bm{z})
, i.e.

\mathbb{I}=\begin{bmatrix}\Gamma_{\bm{x}\bm{x}}&\Gamma_{\bm{x}\bm{y}}&\Gamma_{\bm{x}\bm{z}}\\
\Gamma_{\bm{y}\bm{x}}&\Gamma_{\bm{y}\bm{y}}&\Gamma_{\bm{y}\bm{z}}\\
\Gamma_{\bm{z}\bm{x}}&\Gamma_{\bm{z}\bm{y}}&\Gamma_{\bm{z}\bm{z}}\end{bmatrix}.

(83)

In particular, we notice that since
\mathbb{I}
is a symmetric matrix it can be orthogonally diagonalised, i.e.

\mathbb{I}=Q\begin{bmatrix}\Gamma_{1}&&\\
&\Gamma_{2}&\\
&&\Gamma_{3}\end{bmatrix}Q^{T},\qquad Q\in SO(3).

(84)

Notice that
\Gamma_{i}
are positive because they can be expressed as
\Gamma_{\bm{n}\bm{n}}
for a certain
\bm{n}
.
We will refer to
\Gamma_{1}
,
\Gamma_{2}
and
\Gamma_{3}
as the
principal moments of inertia
.
We call the principal reference frame the reference frame obtained by applying to
(O,\bm{x},\bm{y},\bm{z})
the rotation
Q
.

Lastly, we would like to apply the Hamiltonian formalism to the study of rigid motion. To achieve this we first observe that the Lagrangian corresponding to a rigid motion is only composed by the kinetic energy term,

T=\frac{1}{2}\lvert\bm{v}\rvert^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\bm{\omega}\cdot\mathbb{I}\bm{\omega}.

(85)

We chose as Lagrangian coordinates the position in space and the Euler angles. We therefore need to express the above kinetic energy in terms of the total derivatives of the position and of the Euler angles. To achieve this we resort to (
80
),

\mathcal{L}(\bm{q},\bm{\alpha},\dot{\bm{q}},\dot{\bm{\alpha}})=\frac{1}{2}m\lvert\dot{\bm{q}}\rvert^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\dot{\bm{\alpha}}\cdot\Xi^{T}\mathbb{I}\Xi\dot{\bm{\alpha}}.

(86)

By Sylvester’s law of inertia
[
37
]
, the congruent transformation
D_{\dot{\bm{\alpha}}}^{2}\mathcal{L}=\Xi^{T}\mathbb{I}\Xi

preserves the sign of the eigenvalues, i.e. the eigenvalues of
\Xi^{T}\mathbb{I}\Xi
have the same signs as the eigenvalues of
\mathbb{I}
, which are all positive. The Hessian
D^{2}\mathcal{L}
of the Lagrangian
\mathcal{L}
expressed in terms of position, Euler angles and their total time derivative therefore remains positive definite. Thus the Legendre transformation that defines the Hamiltonian coordinates is well-posed:

\bm{p}=\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\dot{\bm{q}}}=m\dot{\bm{q}},\qquad\bm{\varsigma}=\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\dot{\bm{\alpha}}}=\Xi^{T}\mathbb{I}\Xi\dot{\bm{\alpha}}.

(87)

Notice now that given the fact that
D^{2}\mathcal{L}
is positive definite then
\varsigma
is well-defined even if it depends on both
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{\alpha}}
and
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\dot{\bm{\alpha}}}
, and we can proceed to define the Hamiltonian of a rigid motion as

\mathcal{H}(\bm{q},\bm{\alpha},\bm{p},\bm{\varsigma})=\frac{1}{2m}\lvert\bm{p}\rvert^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\bm{\varsigma}\cdot(\Xi^{T}\mathbb{I}\Xi)^{-1}\bm{\varsigma}.

(88)

Hence our choice of coordinates in (
1
) is perfectly admissible.

Appendix B
The Boltzmann–Curtiss equation

In this section we provide a detailed derivation of the Boltzmann–Curtiss equation for convex non-spherical symmetric-top rigid molecules, using the modern BBGKY hierarchy approach
[
19
]
.
We assume the calamitic rarefied gas under consideration is an ensemble of
N
convex non-spherical symmetric-top rigid bodies.
The state of each particle can be identified by the coordinates
\bm{\Gamma}=(\bm{q},\bm{\alpha},\bm{p},\bm{\varsigma})
, where
\bm{q}
represents the position of the center of mass of the particle,
\bm{\alpha}
are the Euler angles representing its orientation while
\bm{p}
and
\bm{\varsigma}
are the conjugate moments to
\bm{q}
and
\bm{\alpha}
respectively.
The configuration of the rarefied calamitic fluid at time
t
is given by
\{\bm{\Gamma}_{i}^{*}(t)\}_{i=1}^{N}=\{(\bm{q}_{i}(t),\bm{\alpha}_{i}(t),\bm{p}_{i}(t),\bm{\varsigma}_{i}(t))\}_{i=1}^{N}
.
We can now introduce the Klimontovich distribution
\pi
and the reduced particle distribution
f_{s}
as follows,

\displaystyle\pi\Big(\{\bm{\Gamma}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N},t\Big)=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta\Big(\bm{\Gamma}_{i}-\bm{\Gamma}_{i}^{*}(t)\Big),

(89)

\displaystyle f_{s}(\bm{\Gamma}_{1},\dots,\bm{\Gamma}_{s},t)=\int\pi(\bm{\Gamma}_{1},\dots,\bm{\Gamma}_{s},\bm{\Gamma}^{(s)},t)\,d\bm{\Gamma}^{(s)},

(90)

where
d\bm{\Gamma}^{(s)}=d\bm{\Gamma}_{s+1}\dots d\bm{\Gamma}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}N}}
.
From (
88
) we know that the Hamiltonian of the particle ensemble is given by

\mathcal{H}=\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{\lvert\bm{p}_{i}\rvert^{2}}{2m}+\frac{1}{2}\bm{\varsigma}_{i}\cdot(\Xi^{T}\mathbb{I}\Xi)^{-1}\bm{\varsigma}_{i}\right)+\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq N}\phi(\bm{q}_{i},\bm{q}_{j},\bm{\alpha}_{i},\bm{\alpha}_{j}),

(91)

where
\mathbb{I}
is the inertia tensor of a single molecule,
\Xi
is the tensor used to pass from angular velocity to the total time derivative of the Euler angles in (
80
), and
\phi:\mathbb{R}^{3}\to\mathbb{R}
is the interaction potential we are considering in addition to the Hamiltonian corresponding to the rigid motion described in (
88
).
We decompose the Hamiltonian into three terms: two containing respectively contributions from particles
1,\dots,s
and
s+1,\dots,N
and a third term containing mixed terms,

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{s}=\left(\sum_{i=1}^{s}\frac{\lvert\bm{p}_{i}\rvert^{2}}{2m}+\frac{1}{2}\bm{\varsigma}_{i}\cdot(\Xi^{T}\mathbb{I}\Xi)^{-1}\bm{\varsigma}_{i}\right)+\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq s}\phi(\bm{q}_{i},\bm{q}_{j},\bm{\alpha}_{i},\bm{\alpha}_{j}),

(92)

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{N-s}=\left(\sum_{i=s+1}^{N}\frac{\lvert\bm{p}_{i}\rvert^{2}}{2m}+\frac{1}{2}\bm{\varsigma}_{i}\cdot(\Xi^{T}\mathbb{I}\Xi)^{-1}\bm{\varsigma}_{i}\right)+\sum_{s+1\leq i<j\leq N}\phi(\bm{q}_{i},\bm{q}_{j},\bm{\alpha}_{i},\bm{\alpha}_{j}),

(93)

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{s}=\sum_{i=1}^{s}\sum_{j=s+1}^{N}\phi(\bm{q}_{i},\bm{q}_{j},\bm{\alpha}_{i},\bm{\alpha}_{j}).

(94)

We then differentiate
f_{s}
with respect to time and use Liouville’s theorem

\frac{\partial\pi}{\partial t}=-\{\pi,\mathcal{H}\}

(95)

to obtain the following integro-differential equation for the reduced particle distribution
f_{s}
,

\frac{\partial f_{s}}{\partial t}=\int\frac{\partial\pi}{\partial t}\,d\bm{\Gamma}^{(s)}=-\int\left(\{\pi,\mathcal{H}_{s}\}+\{\pi,\mathcal{H}_{n-s}\}+\{\pi,\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{s}\}\right)\,d\Gamma^{(s)}.

(96)

We notice that since
\mathcal{H}_{s}
is independent of
\bm{\Gamma}_{s+1},\dots,\bm{\Gamma}_{n}
we can bring the integral inside the Poisson brackets to obtain the following equation,

\frac{\partial f_{s}}{\partial t}=\int\frac{\partial\pi}{\partial t}\,d\bm{\Gamma}^{(s)}=-\{f_{s},\mathcal{H}_{s}\}-\int\{\pi,\mathcal{H}_{n-s}\}\,d\Gamma^{(s)}-\int\{\pi,\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{s}\}\,d\Gamma^{(s)}.

(97)

We then notice that the second term on the right-hand side vanishes given the fact it is an exact divergence and we have appropriate boundary conditions:

\displaystyle\int\!\!\{\pi

\displaystyle,\mathcal{H}_{N-s}\}d\bm{\Gamma}^{(s)}\!=\!\!\!\int\!\!\sum_{i=1}^{n}\!\Big(\!\frac{\partial\pi}{\partial\bm{q}_{i}}\frac{\partial\mathcal{H}_{N-s}}{\partial\bm{p}_{i}}\!+\!\frac{\partial\pi}{\partial\bm{\alpha}_{i}}\frac{\partial\mathcal{H}_{N-s}}{\partial\bm{\varsigma}_{i}}\!-\!\frac{\partial\pi}{\partial\bm{p}_{i}}\frac{\partial\mathcal{H}_{N-s}}{\partial\bm{q}_{i}}\!-\!\frac{\partial\pi}{\partial\bm{\varsigma}_{i}}\frac{\partial\mathcal{H}_{N-s}}{\partial\bm{\alpha}_{i}}\!\Big)d\bm{\Gamma}^{(s)}

(98)

\displaystyle=\int\sum_{i=s+1}^{N}\Big(\frac{\partial\pi}{\partial\bm{q}_{i}}\frac{\bm{p}_{i}}{m}+\frac{\partial\pi}{\partial\bm{\alpha}}(\Xi^{T}\mathbb{I}\Xi)^{-1}\bm{\varsigma}\Big)\,d\bm{\Gamma}^{(s)}

\displaystyle\;\;\;\;-\int\sum_{i=s+1}^{N}\Big(\frac{\partial\pi}{\partial\bm{p}_{i}}\sum_{j=i+1}^{N}\frac{\partial\phi(\bm{q}_{i},\bm{q}_{j},\bm{\alpha}_{i},\bm{\alpha}_{j})}{\partial\bm{q}_{i}}+\frac{\partial\pi}{\partial\bm{\varsigma}_{i}}\sum_{j=i+1}^{N}\frac{\partial\phi(\bm{q}_{i},\bm{q}_{j},\bm{\alpha}_{i},\bm{\alpha}_{j})}{\partial\bm{\alpha}_{i}}\Big)\,d\bm{\Gamma}^{(s)}

\displaystyle=\int\sum_{i=s+1}^{N}\Bigg(\frac{\partial}{\partial\bm{q}_{i}}\cdot\Big(\pi\frac{\bm{p}_{i}}{m}\Big)+\frac{\partial}{\partial\bm{\alpha}}\cdot\Big(\pi(\Xi^{T}\mathbb{I}\Xi)^{-1}\bm{\varsigma}\Big)\Bigg)d\bm{\Gamma}^{(s)}

\displaystyle\;\;\;\;\;\;\;-\int\sum_{i=s+1}^{N}\frac{\partial}{\partial\bm{p}_{i}}\cdot\Big(\pi\sum_{j=i+1}^{N}\frac{\partial\phi(\bm{q}_{i},\bm{q}_{j},\bm{\alpha}_{i},\bm{\alpha}_{j})}{\partial\bm{q}_{i}}\Big)\,d\bm{\Gamma}^{(s)}

\displaystyle\;\;\;\;\;\;\;-\int\sum_{i=s+1}^{N}\frac{\partial}{\partial\bm{\varsigma}_{i}}\cdot\Big(\pi\sum_{j=i+1}^{N}\frac{\partial\phi(\bm{q}_{i},\bm{q}_{j},\bm{\alpha}_{i},\bm{\alpha}_{j})}{\partial\bm{\alpha}_{i}}\Big)\,d\bm{\Gamma}^{(s)}=0.

It remains to study the last term in (
97
):

\displaystyle\int\!\{\pi,\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{s}\}d\bm{\Gamma}^{(s)}

\displaystyle=\!\int\!\sum_{i=1}^{N}\!\Big(\frac{\partial\pi}{\partial\bm{q}_{i}}\frac{\partial\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{s}}{\partial\bm{p}_{i}}\!+\!\frac{\partial\pi}{\partial\bm{\alpha}_{i}}\frac{\partial\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{s}}{\partial\bm{\varsigma}_{i}}\!-\!\frac{\partial\pi}{\partial\bm{p}_{i}}\frac{\partial\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{s}}{\partial\bm{q}_{i}}\!-\!\frac{\partial\pi}{\partial\bm{\varsigma}_{i}}\frac{\partial\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{s}}{\partial\bm{\alpha}_{i}}\Big)\,d\bm{\Gamma}^{(s)}

(99)

\displaystyle=\int\sum_{i=1}^{s}\frac{\partial\pi}{\partial\bm{p}_{i}}\cdot\sum_{j=s+1}^{N}\frac{\partial\phi(\bm{q}_{i},\bm{q}_{j},\bm{\alpha}_{i},\bm{\alpha}_{j})}{\partial\bm{q}_{i}}\,d\bm{\Gamma}^{(s)}

\displaystyle+\int\sum_{j=s+1}^{N}\frac{\partial\pi}{\partial\bm{p}_{j}}\cdot\sum_{i=1}^{s}\frac{\partial\phi(\bm{q}_{i},\bm{q}_{j},\bm{\alpha}_{i},\bm{\alpha}_{j})}{\partial\bm{q}_{j}}\,d\bm{\Gamma}^{(s)}

\displaystyle+\int\sum_{i=1}^{s}\frac{\partial\pi}{\partial\bm{\varsigma}_{i}}\cdot\sum_{j=s+1}^{N}\frac{\partial\phi(\bm{q}_{i},\bm{q}_{j},\bm{\alpha}_{i},\bm{\alpha}_{j})}{\partial\bm{\alpha}_{i}}\,d\bm{\Gamma}^{(s)}

\displaystyle+\int\sum_{j=s+1}^{N}\frac{\partial\pi}{\partial\bm{\varsigma}_{i}}\cdot\sum_{i=1}^{s}\frac{\partial\phi(\bm{q}_{i},\bm{q}_{j},\bm{\alpha}_{i},\bm{\alpha}_{j})}{\partial\bm{\alpha}_{j}}\,d\bm{\Gamma}^{(s)}.

We notice that the second and last term in the previous equation vanish since they are exact divergences, which leaves us with

\displaystyle\int\!\{\pi,\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{s}\}d\bm{\Gamma}^{(s)}\!

\displaystyle=\int\sum_{i=1}^{s}\frac{\partial\pi}{\partial\bm{p}_{i}}\cdot\sum_{j=s+1}^{N}\frac{\partial\phi(\bm{q}_{i},\bm{q}_{j},\bm{\alpha}_{i},\bm{\alpha}_{j})}{\partial\bm{q}_{i}}\,d\bm{\Gamma}^{(s)}

(100)

\displaystyle+\int\sum_{i=1}^{s}\frac{\partial\pi}{\partial\bm{\varsigma}_{i}}\cdot\sum_{j=s+1}^{N}\frac{\partial\phi(\bm{q}_{i},\bm{q}_{j},\bm{\alpha}_{i},\bm{\alpha}_{j})}{\partial\bm{\alpha}_{i}}\,d\bm{\Gamma}^{(s)}

\displaystyle=(N-s)\int\sum_{i=1}^{s}\frac{\partial\pi}{\partial\bm{p}_{i}}\cdot\frac{\partial\phi(\bm{q}_{i},\bm{q}_{s+1},\bm{\alpha}_{i},\bm{\alpha}_{s+1})}{\partial\bm{q}_{i}}\,d\bm{\Gamma}^{(s)}

\displaystyle+(N-s)\int\sum_{i=1}^{s}\frac{\partial\pi}{\partial\bm{\varsigma}_{i}}\cdot\frac{\partial\phi(\bm{q}_{i},\bm{q}_{s+1},\bm{\alpha}_{i},\bm{\alpha}_{s+1})}{\partial\bm{\alpha}_{i}}\,d\bm{\Gamma}^{(s)}

\displaystyle=(N-s)\int\sum_{i=1}^{s}\frac{\partial f_{s+1}}{\partial\bm{p}_{i}}\cdot\frac{\partial\phi(\bm{q}_{i},\bm{q}_{s+1},\bm{\alpha}_{i},\bm{\alpha}_{s+1})}{\partial\bm{q}_{i}}\,d\bm{\Gamma}_{s+1}

\displaystyle+(N-s)\int\sum_{i=1}^{s}\frac{\partial f_{s+1}}{\partial\bm{\varsigma}_{i}}\cdot\frac{\partial\phi(\bm{q}_{i},\bm{q}_{s+1},\bm{\alpha}_{i},\bm{\alpha}_{s+1})}{\partial\bm{\alpha}_{i}}\,d\bm{\Gamma}_{s+1}.

We have the BBGKY hierarchy for the Boltzmann–Curtiss equation, i.e.

\displaystyle\frac{\partial f_{s}}{\partial t}+\{f_{s},\mathcal{H}_{s}\}

\displaystyle=(N-s)\int\sum_{i=1}^{s}\frac{\partial f_{s+1}}{\partial\bm{p}_{i}}\cdot\frac{\partial\phi(\bm{q}_{i},\bm{q}_{s+1},\bm{\alpha}_{i},\bm{\alpha}_{s+1})}{\partial\bm{q}_{i}}\,d\bm{\Gamma}_{s+1}

(101)

\displaystyle+(N-s)\int\sum_{i=1}^{s}\frac{\partial f_{s+1}}{\partial\bm{\varsigma}_{i}}\cdot\frac{\partial\phi(\bm{q}_{i},\bm{q}_{s+1},\bm{\alpha}_{i},\bm{\alpha}_{s+1})}{\partial\bm{\alpha}_{i}}\,d\bm{\Gamma}_{s+1}.

By abuse of notation, we will denote
f_{s}
the function
f_{s}
multiplied by the factor
\frac{N!}{(N-s)!}
to obtain the following expression for the BBGKY hierarchy, i.e.

\displaystyle\frac{\partial f_{s}}{\partial t}+\{f_{s},\mathcal{H}_{s}\}

\displaystyle=\int\sum_{i=1}^{s}\frac{\partial f_{s+1}}{\partial\bm{p}_{i}}\cdot\frac{\partial\phi(\bm{q}_{i},\bm{q}_{s+1},\bm{\alpha}_{i},\bm{\alpha}_{s+1})}{\partial\bm{q}_{i}}\,d\bm{\Gamma}_{s+1}

(102)

\displaystyle+\int\sum_{i=1}^{s}\frac{\partial f_{s+1}}{\partial\bm{\varsigma}_{i}}\cdot\frac{\partial\phi(\bm{q}_{i},\bm{q}_{s+1},\bm{\alpha}_{i},\bm{\alpha}_{s+1})}{\partial\bm{\alpha}_{i}}\,d\bm{\Gamma}_{s+1}.

In particular, we focus our attention on the first two terms of the BBGKY hierarchy, i.e.

\displaystyle\frac{\partial f_{1}}{\partial t}+\frac{\bm{p}_{1}}{m}\cdot\frac{\partial f_{1}}{\partial\bm{q}_{1}}+(\Xi^{T}_{1}\mathbb{I}\Xi_{1})^{-1}\bm{\varsigma}_{1}\cdot\frac{\partial f_{1}}{\partial\bm{\alpha}_{1}}

\displaystyle=\int\frac{\partial\phi(\bm{q}_{1},\bm{q}_{2},\bm{\alpha}_{1},\bm{\alpha}_{2})}{\partial\bm{q}_{1}}\frac{\partial f_{2}}{\partial\bm{p}_{1}}\,d\bm{\Gamma}_{2}

(103)

\displaystyle+\int\frac{\partial\phi(\bm{q}_{1},\bm{q}_{2},\bm{\alpha}_{1},\bm{\alpha}_{2})}{\partial\bm{\alpha}_{1}}\frac{\partial f_{2}}{\partial\bm{\varsigma}_{1}}\,d\bm{\Gamma}_{2}

\displaystyle\frac{\partial f_{2}}{\partial t}+\frac{\bm{p}_{1}}{m}\cdot\frac{\partial f_{2}}{\partial\bm{q}_{1}}

\displaystyle+(\Xi^{T}_{1}\mathbb{I}\Xi_{1})^{-1}\bm{\varsigma}_{1}\cdot\frac{\partial f_{2}}{\partial\bm{\alpha}_{1}}+\frac{\bm{p}_{2}}{m}\cdot\frac{\partial f_{2}}{\partial\bm{q}_{2}}+(\Xi^{T}_{2}\mathbb{I}\Xi_{2})^{-1}\bm{\varsigma}_{2}\cdot\frac{\partial f_{2}}{\partial\bm{\alpha}_{2}}

(104)

\displaystyle-\frac{\partial\phi(\bm{q}_{1},\bm{q}_{2},\bm{\alpha}_{1},\bm{\alpha}_{2})}{\partial\bm{q}_{1}}\frac{\partial f_{2}}{\partial\bm{p}_{1}}-\frac{\partial\phi(\bm{q}_{1},\bm{q}_{2},\bm{\alpha}_{1},\bm{\alpha}_{2})}{\partial\bm{\alpha}_{1}}\frac{\partial f_{2}}{\partial\bm{\varsigma}_{1}}=0

where the right-hand side of the last equation vanishes because we have assumed
f_{3}\equiv 0
since we are working under the assumption that we only have binary collisions.
Indeed under the Boltzmann–Grad limit it is possible to justify more precisely that
f_{3}
is negligible; we redirect the interested reader to
[
20

21

19
]
.
Notice now that we can add under the integral appearing on the right-hand side of (

104

\frac{\partial\phi(\bm{q}_{1},\bm{q}_{2},\bm{\alpha}_{1},\bm{\alpha}_{2})}{\partial\bm{q}_{2}}\frac{\partial f_{2}}{\partial\bm{p}_{2}}

\frac{\partial\phi(\bm{q}_{1},\bm{q}_{2},\bm{\alpha}_{1},\bm{\alpha}_{2})}{\partial\bm{\alpha}_{2}}\frac{\partial f_{2}}{\partial\bm{\varsigma}_{2}}

We focus our attention on the first two terms of the BBGKY hierarchy, i.e.

\displaystyle\frac{\partial f_{1}}{\partial t}+\frac{\bm{p}_{1}}{m}\cdot\frac{\partial f_{1}}{\partial\bm{q}_{1}}+(\Xi^{T}_{1}\mathbb{I}\Xi_{1})^{-1}\bm{\varsigma}_{1}\frac{\partial f_{1}}{\partial\bm{\alpha}_{1}}

\displaystyle=\int\frac{\partial\phi(\bm{q}_{1},\bm{q}_{2},\bm{\alpha}_{1},\bm{\alpha}_{2})}{\partial\bm{q}_{1}}\Big(\frac{\partial f_{2}}{\partial\bm{p}_{1}}-\frac{\partial f_{2}}{\partial\bm{p}_{2}}\Big)\,d\bm{\Gamma}_{2}

(105)

\displaystyle+\int\frac{\partial\phi(\bm{q}_{1},\bm{q}_{2},\bm{\alpha}_{1},\bm{\alpha}_{2})}{\partial\bm{\alpha}_{1}}\frac{\partial f_{2}}{\partial\bm{\varsigma}_{1}}d\bm{\Gamma}_{2}

\displaystyle+\int\frac{\partial\phi(\bm{q}_{1},\bm{q}_{2},\bm{\alpha}_{1},\bm{\alpha}_{2})}{\partial\bm{\alpha}_{2}}\frac{\partial f_{2}}{\partial\bm{\varsigma}_{2}}d\bm{\Gamma}_{2},

\displaystyle\frac{\partial f_{2}}{\partial t}+\frac{\bm{p}_{1}}{m}\cdot\frac{\partial f_{2}}{\partial\bm{q}_{1}}+(\Xi^{T}_{1}\mathbb{I}\Xi_{1})^{-1}\bm{\varsigma}_{1}\cdot\frac{\partial f_{2}}{\partial\bm{\alpha}_{1}}

\displaystyle+\frac{\bm{p}_{2}}{m}\cdot\frac{\partial f_{2}}{\partial\bm{q}_{2}}+(\Xi^{T}_{2}\mathbb{I}\Xi_{2})^{-1}\bm{\varsigma}_{2}\cdot\frac{\partial f_{2}}{\partial\bm{\alpha}_{2}}

(106)

\displaystyle-\frac{\partial\phi(\bm{q}_{1},\bm{q}_{2},\bm{\alpha}_{1},\bm{\alpha}_{2})}{\partial\bm{q}_{1}}\Big(\frac{\partial f_{2}}{\partial\bm{p}_{1}}-\frac{\partial f_{2}}{\partial\bm{p}_{2}}\Big)

\displaystyle-\frac{\partial\phi(\bm{q}_{1},\bm{q}_{2},\bm{\alpha}_{1},\bm{\alpha}_{2})}{\partial\bm{\alpha}_{1}}\frac{\partial f_{2}}{\partial\bm{\varsigma}_{1}}

\displaystyle-\frac{\partial\phi(\bm{q}_{1},\bm{q}_{2},\bm{\alpha}_{1},\bm{\alpha}_{2})}{\partial\bm{\alpha}_{2}}\frac{\partial f_{2}}{\partial\bm{\varsigma}_{2}}=0,

We notice that there are two different length scales in both (

).
In particular, the left-hand side of (

We introduce fast varying coordinates, i.e.
\bm{q}=\bm{q}_{2}-\bm{q}_{1}

\bm{\sigma}=\bm{\alpha}_{2}-\bm{\alpha}_{1}
together with slowly varying coordinates, i.e.
\bm{Q}=\frac{\bm{q}_{2}+\bm{q}_{1}}{2}

\bm{\Sigma}=\frac{\bm{\alpha}_{2}+\bm{\alpha}_{1}}{2}

Now we can rewrite (
104
) in terms of the new coordinates as,

\displaystyle\frac{\partial f_{2}}{\partial t}\!+\frac{1}{2}\frac{\bm{p}_{2}\!+\!\bm{p}_{1}}{m}\cdot\frac{\partial f_{2}}{\partial\bm{Q}}+{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{A}}\cdot\frac{\partial f_{2}}{\partial{\bm{\Sigma}}}\boxed{\!+\frac{\bm{p}_{2}\!-\!\bm{p}_{1}}{m}\cdot\frac{\partial f_{2}}{\partial\bm{q}}\!+\!{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{B}}\cdot\frac{\partial f_{2}}{\partial\bm{\sigma}}}

(107)

{\bm{A}=\frac{1}{2}\left((\Xi_{1}^{T}\mathbb{I}\Xi_{1})^{-1}\bm{\varsigma}_{1}+(\Xi_{2}^{T}\mathbb{I}\Xi_{2})^{-1}\bm{\varsigma}_{2}\right)}
,
{\bm{B}=\left((\Xi_{2}^{T}\mathbb{I}\Xi_{2})^{-1}\bm{\varsigma}_{2}-(\Xi_{1}^{T}\mathbb{I}\Xi_{1})^{-1}\bm{\varsigma}_{1}\right)}
and we have boxed all the terms acting on the microscopic scale length. We proceed to treat the terms acting on different scale lengths separately, and we notice that the terms in the previous equation corresponding to the microscopic scale length can be treated as steady with respect to the collisional time scale,

\displaystyle\frac{\bm{p}_{2}\!-\!\bm{p}_{1}}{m}\cdot\frac{\partial f_{2}}{\partial\bm{q}}+{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{B}}\cdot\frac{\partial f_{2}}{\partial\bm{\sigma}}=\frac{\partial\phi(\bm{q}_{1},\bm{q}_{2},\bm{\alpha}_{1},\bm{\alpha}_{2})}{\partial\bm{q}_{1}}\Big(\frac{\partial f_{2}}{\partial\bm{p}_{1}}-\frac{\partial f_{2}}{\partial\bm{p}_{2}}\Big)

(108)

\displaystyle+\frac{\partial\phi(\bm{q}_{1},\bm{q}_{2},\bm{\alpha}_{1},\bm{\alpha}_{2})}{\partial\bm{\alpha}_{1}}\frac{\partial f_{2}}{\partial\bm{\varsigma}_{1}}+\frac{\partial\phi(\bm{q}_{1},\bm{q}_{2},\bm{\alpha}_{1},\bm{\alpha}_{2})}{\partial\bm{\alpha}_{2}}\frac{\partial f_{2}}{\partial\bm{\varsigma}_{2}}.

Substituting (

\displaystyle\frac{\partial f_{1}}{\partial t}+\frac{\bm{p}_{1}}{m}\cdot\frac{\partial f_{1}}{\partial\bm{q}_{1}}+(\Xi^{T}_{1}\mathbb{I}\Xi_{1})^{-1}\bm{\varsigma}_{1}\frac{\partial f_{1}}{\partial\bm{\alpha}_{1}}=\left.\frac{d\,f_{1}}{d\,t}\right\lvert_{\vskip 0.5pt\text{collision}},

(109)

\displaystyle\left.\frac{d\,f_{1}}{d\,t}\right\lvert_{\vskip 0.5pt\text{collision}}\!\!\!\!=\int\left(\frac{\bm{p}_{2}\!-\!\bm{p}_{1}}{m}\cdot\frac{\partial f_{2}}{\partial\bm{q}}+{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{B}}\cdot\frac{\partial f_{2}}{\partial\bm{\sigma}}\,d\bm{\Gamma}_{2}\right),

(110)

where the right-hand side of (
110
) represents the total variation of
f_{1}
due to collisions between two molecules.

Appendix C
The rigid collision operator

In the previous section, we derived a hierarchy of equations to describe the evolution of the reduced distribution functions
f_{s}
.
In particular, each equation in the hierarchy depended on a higher-order reduced particle distribution, hence the system of equations was not closed. To solve this issue we removed the dependence on
f_{3}
from the second equation in the hierarchy, i.e. (
104
), by using the physical assumption that we are only considering binary collisions.
We will now focus our attention on a way to remove the dependence on
f_{2}
from the first equation in the hierarchy, i.e. (
103
) when considering the collision between two hard convex molecules.

Notice that since
f_{3}
vanishes we are only considering collisions between two molecules. We will from now on label these two molecules as molecule 1 and molecule 2 and use the notation
\bm{\Gamma}_{1}
and
\bm{\Gamma}_{2}
to denote the phase space coordinates of the two molecules respectively.
In particular, we will consider a reference frame having origin in the center of mass of molecule 1 and imagine that molecule 2 is approaching, colliding and then moving away from molecule 1.

We begin expanding
d\bm{\Gamma}_{2}
in (
110
) and making use of the fact that since we are only considering collisions between rigid bodies
f_{2}
will vanish outside of the translated excluded volume
V=V_{ex}^{1,2}(\sigma)+\bm{q}_{1}
, hence we can restrict the domain of integration to
V
, i.e.

\displaystyle\left.\frac{d\,f_{1}}{d\,t}\right\lvert_{\vskip 0.5pt\text{collision}}\!\!\!\!\!\!\!

\displaystyle=\int d\bm{\varsigma}_{2}d\bm{p}_{2}d\bm{\alpha}_{2}\int_{V}\frac{\bm{p}_{2}\!-\!\bm{p}_{1}}{m}\cdot\frac{\partial f_{2}}{\partial\bm{q}}\,{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}d\bm{q}}+\!\!\int d\bm{\varsigma}_{2}d\bm{p}_{2}d\bm{\alpha}_{2}\!\!\int_{V}\!\!\!{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{B}}\cdot\frac{\partial f_{2}}{\partial\bm{\sigma}}\,{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}d\bm{q}}

(111)

\displaystyle=\!\!\!\int d\bm{\varsigma}_{2}d\bm{p}_{2}d\bm{\alpha}_{2}\int_{\partial V}\frac{\bm{p}_{2}\!-\!\bm{p}_{1}}{m}\cdot\bm{k}f_{2}d\bm{S}+\!\!\int d\bm{\varsigma}_{2}d\bm{p}_{2}d\bm{\alpha}_{2}\int_{V}\!\!\!{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{B}}\cdot\frac{\partial f_{2}}{\partial\bm{\sigma}}\,d{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{q}}.\;\;

(112)

We now observe that the translated excluded volume
V
is a strictly convex set with smooth boundary, hence we can apply results from differential geometry of surfaces with positive curvature.
In particular, we can transform the integral on the boundary of
V
into an integral on the surface of the unit sphere
\mathbb{S}^{2}
, making use of the element of surface area per solid angle
dS
that can be computed explicitly
[
8
]
, and rewrite (
112
) as

\displaystyle\left.\frac{d\,f_{1}}{d\,t}\right\lvert_{\vskip 0.5pt\text{collision}}\!\!\!\!

\displaystyle=\int d\bm{\varsigma}_{2}d\bm{p}_{2}d\bm{\alpha}_{2}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}\frac{\bm{p}_{2}\!-\!\bm{p}_{1}}{m}\cdot\bm{k}f_{2}dS\,d\bm{k}

(113)

\displaystyle+\int d\bm{\varsigma}_{2}d\bm{p}_{2}d\bm{\alpha}_{2}\int_{V}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{B}}\cdot\frac{\partial f_{2}}{\partial\bm{\sigma}}\,{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}d\bm{q}}.

(114)

We now need to focus on the second term in the previous equation. In particular we need to rewrite it in terms of the surface element of the unit sphere
\mathbb{S}^{2}
. Notice that
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{B}=\dot{\bm{\sigma}}}
, and so we can rewrite the second term in the previous equation as

\displaystyle\int\!\!d\bm{\varsigma}_{2}d\bm{p}_{2}d\bm{\alpha}_{2}\int_{V}\dot{\bm{\sigma}}\cdot\frac{\partial f_{2}}{\partial\bm{\sigma}}\,d\bm{q}_{2}

\displaystyle=\!\!\int d\bm{\varsigma}_{2}d\bm{p}_{2}d\bm{\alpha}_{2}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}\dot{\bm{\sigma}}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\frac{\partial\bm{q}}{\partial\bm{\sigma}}}\cdot\frac{\partial f_{2}}{\partial\bm{q}}\,{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}d\bm{q}}

(115)

By virtue of proposition
9
we can express
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\dot{\bm{\sigma}}\frac{\partial\bm{q}}{\bm{\partial\sigma}}}
as
\bm{\omega}_{1}\times\bm{g}_{1}-\bm{\omega}_{2}\times\bm{g}_{2}
, where
\bm{g}_{1}
and
\bm{g}_{2}
are the vectors connecting
\bm{q}_{1}
and
\bm{q}_{2}
with the contact point
\bm{\zeta}
. In particular we can rewrite (
112
) as

\left.\frac{d\,f_{1}}{d\,t}\right\lvert_{\vskip 0.5pt\text{collision}}\!\!\!\!=\int d\bm{\varsigma}_{2}d\bm{p}_{2}d\bm{\alpha}_{2}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}\bm{\mathfrak{g}}\cdot\bm{k}f_{2}\,dS\,d\bm{k},

(117)

where
\bm{\mathfrak{g}}
is the relative velocity of the contact point
\bm{\zeta}
, i.e.

\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{\mathfrak{g}}=\frac{1}{m}\left(\bm{p}_{1}-\bm{p}_{2}\right)+\bm{\omega}_{1}\times\bm{g}_{1}-\bm{\omega}_{2}\times\bm{g}_{2}.

(118)

We decompose
\mathbb{S}^{2}
into two surfaces:
\mathbb{S}_{+}
is the portion of the surface
\mathbb{S}^{2}
such that along the collision trajectory molecule 2 is coming towards molecule 1, i.e.
\mathfrak{g}\cdot\bm{k}
is positive, while
\mathbb{S}_{-}
is the portion of the surface
\mathbb{S}^{2}
such that along the collision trajectory molecule 2 is moving away from molecule 1, i.e.
\mathfrak{g}\cdot\bm{k}
is negative.
With this we split the integral in (
117
) into two integrals on
\mathbb{S}_{+}
and
\mathbb{S}_{-}
respectively, i.e.

\displaystyle\left.\frac{d\,f_{1}}{d\,t}\right\lvert_{\vskip 0.5pt\text{collision}}\!\!\!\!

\displaystyle=\int d\bm{\varsigma}_{2}d\bm{p}_{2}d\bm{\alpha}_{2}\int_{\mathbb{S}_{+}}\bm{\mathfrak{g}}\cdot\bm{k}f_{2}\,dS\,d\bm{k}+\int d\bm{\varsigma}_{2}d\bm{p}_{2}d\bm{\alpha}_{2}\int_{\mathbb{S}_{-}}\bm{\mathfrak{g}}\cdot\bm{k}f_{2}\,dS\,d\bm{k}.

(119)

The first term in the previous equation is known as the
gain term
, while the second term is known as the
loss term
.
Notice now that we can make use of the
molecular chaos assumption

[
26
]
, i.e.
f_{2}(\bm{\Gamma}_{1},\bm{\Gamma}_{2},t)=f_{1}(\bm{\Gamma}_{1},t)f_{1}(\bm{\Gamma}_{2},t)
on the loss term to obtain

\displaystyle\left.\frac{df_{1}}{dt}\right|_{\vskip 0.5pt\text{collision}}\!\!\!\!

\displaystyle=\int\!d\bm{p}_{2}\,d\bm{\varsigma}_{2}\,d\bm{\alpha}\!\int_{\mathbb{S}_{+}}\!\!(\bm{k}\cdot\bm{\mathfrak{g}})f_{2}\,dSd\bm{k}

(120)

\displaystyle+\int\!d\bm{p}_{2}\,d\bm{\varsigma}_{2}\,d\bm{\alpha}\!\int_{\mathbb{S}_{-}}\!\!(\bm{k}\cdot\bm{g})f_{1}(\bm{q}_{1},\bm{\alpha}_{1},\bm{p}_{1},\bm{\varsigma}_{1})f_{1}(\bm{q}_{2},\bm{\alpha}_{2},\bm{p}_{2},\bm{\varsigma}_{2})\,S(\bm{k})d\bm{k}.

As discussed in
[
36
]
, since energy and linear and angular momentum are conserved after the collision we can trace back the post-collisional coordinates in the gain term of (
119
) to the corresponding pre-collisional coordinates. Applying the molecular chaos assumption once again we obtain,

\displaystyle\left.\frac{df_{1}}{dt}\right|_{\vskip 0.5pt\text{collision}}\!\!\!\!

\displaystyle=\int\!d\bm{p}_{2}\,d\bm{\varsigma}_{2}\,d\bm{\alpha}\!\int_{\mathbb{S}_{+}}\!\!(\bm{k}\cdot\bm{\mathfrak{g}})f_{1}(\bm{q}_{1}^{\prime},\bm{\alpha}_{1}^{\prime},\bm{p}_{1}^{\prime},\bm{\varsigma}_{1}^{\prime},t)f_{1}(\bm{q}_{2}^{\prime},\bm{\alpha}_{2}^{\prime},\bm{p}_{2}^{\prime},\bm{\varsigma}_{2}^{\prime},t)\,dSd\bm{k}

(121)

\displaystyle+\int\!d\bm{p}_{2}\,d\bm{\varsigma}_{2}\,d\bm{\alpha}\!\int_{\mathbb{S}_{-}}\!\!(\bm{k}\cdot\bm{\mathfrak{g}})f_{1}(\bm{q}_{1},\bm{\alpha}_{1},\bm{p}_{1},\bm{\varsigma}_{1},t)f_{1}(\bm{q}_{2},\bm{\alpha}_{2},\bm{p}_{2},\bm{\varsigma}_{2},t)\,dSd\bm{k}.

Lastly, we shorten the notation by writing the collision operator in the familiar way:

\displaystyle\left.\frac{df_{1}}{dt}\right|_{\vskip 0.5pt\text{collision}}=C[f_{1},f_{1}],

(122)

with

C[f,g]\coloneqq\int\!\!\!\!\int\!\!\!\!\int\!\!\!\!\int(g^{\prime}f^{\prime}-gf)(\bm{k}\cdot\bm{\mathfrak{g}})dSd\bm{k}d\bm{p}_{2}d\bm{\alpha}_{2}d\bm{\varsigma}_{2},

(123)

where the arguments of
f
are
(\bm{q}_{1},\bm{\alpha}_{1},\bm{p}_{1},\bm{\varsigma}_{1},t)
and the arguments of
g
are
(\bm{q}_{2},\bm{\alpha}_{2},\bm{p}_{2},\allowbreak\bm{\varsigma}_{2},t)
.
