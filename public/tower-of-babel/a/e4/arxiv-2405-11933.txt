Jinhyung Park

Abstract
. The purpose of this note is twofold. First, we give a quick proof of Ballico–Chiantini’s theorem stating that a Fano or Calabi–Yau variety of dimension at least 4 4 in codimension two is a complete intersection. Second, we improve Barth–Van de Ven’s result asserting that if the degree of a smooth projective variety of dimension n n is less than approximately 0.63 ⋅ n 1 / 2 0.63\cdot n^{1/2} , then it is a complete intersection. We show that the degree bound can be improved to approximately 0.79 ⋅ n 2 / 3 0.79\cdot n^{2/3} .

14N25, 14E25

1.
Introduction

Throughout the note,
X\subseteq\mathbf{P}^{r}
is a non-degenerate smooth projective complex variety of dimension
n
, codimension
e=r-n
, and degree
d
, and
H
is its hyperplane section. In 1970’s, Barth and Hartshorne independently observed that if
e
is small compared with
n
, then
X
is homologically like a complete intersection. The fact that no projective manifolds in small codimension were known other than complete intersections led Hartshorne to make the following famous conjecture.

Hartshorne Conjecture

(
[
16
]
)
.

If
n\geq 2e+1
or
n=4
and
e=2
, then
X\subseteq\mathbf{P}^{r}
is a complete intersection.

This conjecture is widely open even when
e=2
or
X
is a Fano variety. However, based on Ran’s deep result
[
22
]
, Ballico–Chiantini
[
2
]
verified Hartshorne conjecture when
e=2
and
X
is a Fano variety (or a Calabi–Yau variety).

Theorem 1.1

(
[
2
, Theorem 6]
)
.

If
X
is a Fano or Calabi–Yau variety, then
X\subseteq\mathbf{P}^{n+2}
is a complete intersection. In particular, if
d\leq 2n+2
, then
X\subseteq\mathbf{P}^{n+2}
is a complete intersection.

In this note, we give a quick proof of the theorem. We do not use Ran’s result
[
22
]
. Instead, we exploit the projection method developed by Lazarsfeld
[
17
]
for the Castelnuovo–Mumford regularity (see also
[
13
]
). Consider a general projection
\pi\colon X\rightarrow\overline{X}\subseteq\mathbf{P}^{n+1}
. We use Ballico–Chiantini’s argument (this is the only part where we use their argument) to prove that
X
is contained in a hypersurface of degree
n
(Lemma
3.3
). This yields a surjective map

\mathscr{O}_{\mathbf{P}^{n+1}}(-n+1)\oplus\cdots\oplus\mathscr{O}_{\mathbf{P}^{n+1}}(-1)\oplus\mathscr{O}_{\mathbf{P}^{n+1}}\longrightarrow\pi_{*}\mathscr{O}_{X}

whose kernel
E
is a vector bundle of rank
n
such that
H^{i}(\mathbf{P}^{n+1},E^{\vee}(m))=0
for
1\leq i\leq n-1
and
m\in\mathbf{Z}
. By Evans–Griffith theorem
[
7
]
,
E
is splitting. This implies that
X\subseteq\mathbf{P}^{n+2}
is projectively normal, and it is well-known that it suffices to conclude the theorem.

Now, recall a famous theorem of Hartshorne
[
16
]
that there exists a function
\mathcal{N}(n)
such that if
d\leq\mathcal{N}(n)
, then
X\subseteq\mathbf{P}^{r}
is a complete intersection. Subsequently, Barth–Van de Ven
[
3
]
proved that one can take

\mathcal{N}(n)=\sqrt{\frac{2}{5}n+\frac{1}{4}}+\frac{1}{2}.

Note that the Segre embedding of
\mathbf{P}^{n-1}\times\mathbf{P}^{1}
into
\mathbf{P}^{2n-1}
is not a complete intersection but has dimension
n
and degree
n
. Moreover, the Plücker embedding of the Grassmannian
\mathbf{G}(1,4)
into
\mathbf{P}^{9}
is not a complete intersection but has dimension
6
and degree
5
. Considering these examples, the following conjecture seems the best possible.

Barth–Ionescu Conjecture

(cf.
[
12
, Conjecture 3.5]
)
.

If
d\leq n-1
, then
X\subseteq\mathbf{P}^{r}
is a complete intersection unless it is the Plücker embedding
\mathbf{G}(1,4)\subseteq\mathbf{P}^{9}
.

This conjecture suggests that one can take
\mathcal{N}(n)=n-2
. In this note, we show that one can take

\mathcal{N}(n)=\min\left\{n-2,\sqrt[3]{\frac{(n+2)^{2}}{2}}+2\right\}.

When
n\gg 0
, aforementioned Barth–Van de Ven’s bound is approximately
0.63\cdot n^{1/2}
, but our bound is approximately
0.79\cdot n^{2/3}
. It is elementary to see that if
d\leq n-1
, then
n\geq 2e+1
unless
X\subseteq\mathbf{P}^{r}
is the Plücker embedding
\mathbf{G}(1,4)\subseteq\mathbf{P}^{9}
(Corollary
2.5
). Thus Barth–Ionescu conjecture is a special case of Hartshorne conjecture. Moreover, our assertion on
\mathcal{N}(n)
raised above is a consequence of the following theorem.

Theorem 1.2
.

Assume that
n\geq 2e+1
. If

d\leq\sqrt[3]{\frac{(n+e^{2}-2e+2)^{2}}{2}}+2,

then
X\subseteq\mathbf{P}^{r}
is a complete intersection.

Our approach to proving the theorem is completely different from Barth–Van de Ven’s approach which is based on the geometry of the varieties of lines contained in
X
. We first establish that if
d\leq(n+e)/e+e
, then
X\subseteq\mathbf{P}^{r}
is a complete intersection except for a few trivial cases of
d=e+1
and
d=e+2
(Theorem
4.4
). This is an improvement of Bertram–Ein–Lazarsfeld’s result
[
4
, Corollary 3]
which is the same assertion without the exceptional cases but replaces the degree condition with
d\leq(n+e)/2e
. For proving Theorem
4.4
, we basically follow the proof of
[
4
, Corollary 3]
, but instead of applying the main result of
[
4
]
, we use Noma’s theorem
[
21
, Theorem 9]
to show that
X\subseteq\mathbf{P}^{r}
is arithmetically Cohen–Macaulay. We then argue that the defining ideal
I_{X|\mathbf{P}^{r}}
is generated by
(e-1)(d-1-e/2)
generators (Lemma
4.1
). As
(e-1)(d-1-e/2)\leq n
and we may assume
n\geq 3e-2
, Netsvetaev’s theorem
[
20
]
yields that
X\subseteq\mathbf{P}^{r}
is a complete intersection. To deduce Theorem
1.2
, we establish that if
n\geq 2e+1
, then
d\geq e^{2}/2+2
(Lemma
2.6
). This result might be of independent interest. We refer to Section
2
for other results on degree of a smooth projective variety of small codimensio). Now, the degree condition in Theorem
1.2
implies
d\leq(n+e)/e+e
, so
X\subseteq\mathbf{P}^{r}
is a complete intersection by Theorem
4.4
.

The organization of the note is as follows. After discussing some properties of the degree of a smooth projective variety of small codimension using a general projection in Section
2
, we prove Theorem
1.1
in Section
3
and Theorem
1.2
in Section
4
.

2.
Degree of a smooth projective variety of small codimension

2.1.
Remarks on degree of Fano manifolds

In this subsection, we suppose the following:

(a)

H^{i}(X,\mathscr{O}_{X}(m))=0
for
1\leq i\leq n-1
and
m\in\mathbf{Z}
.

(b)

K_{X}=kH
for an integer
k
(i.e.,
X\subseteq\mathbf{P}^{r}
is subcanonical).

(c)

X\subseteq\mathbf{P}^{r}
is linearly normal.

When
X
is Fano, the condition
(a)
holds by Kodaira vanishing theorem. When
n\geq e+2
, the condition
(b)
holds by Barth–Larsen theorem (cf.
[
18
, Corollary 3.2.3]
). When
n\geq 2e-1
, the condition
(c)
holds by Zak’s theorem on linear normality (cf.
[
18
, Theorem 3.4.25]
).

Now, take a general projection

\pi\colon X\longrightarrow\mathbf{P}^{n}

centered at a general
(e-1)
-dimensional linear subspace
\mathbf{P}^{r}
disjoint from
X
. Note that
\pi
is a finite flat morphism. Then
\pi_{*}\mathscr{O}_{X}
is a vector bundle of rank
d
on
\mathbf{P}^{n}
. By Horrocks criterion, the condition
(a)
implies that
\pi_{*}\mathscr{O}_{X}
is splitting. The duality for a finite flat morphism and the condition
(b)
show that

(\pi_{*}\mathscr{O}_{X})^{\vee}=\pi_{*}\omega_{X/\mathbf{P}^{n}}=\pi_{*}\mathscr{O}_{X}(n+1+k).

Thus we may write

\pi_{*}\mathscr{O}_{X}=\mathscr{O}_{\mathbf{P}^{n}}^{\oplus a_{0}}\oplus\mathscr{O}_{\mathbf{P}^{n}}(-1)^{\oplus a_{1}}\oplus\cdots\oplus\mathscr{O}_{\mathbf{P}^{n}}(-(n+1+k))^{\oplus a_{n+1+k}},

where
a_{0},a_{1},\ldots,a_{n+1+k}
are nonnegative integers such that

a_{0}=1,a_{1}=e
,
a_{i}=a_{n+1+k-i}
for all
0\leq i\leq n+1+k
, and
a_{0}+a_{1}+\cdots+a_{n+1+k}=d
.

Here we use the condition
(c)
to get
a_{1}=e
.
As
n+1+k\geq 0
, we get
k\geq-n-1
. The following are immediate:

(1)

k=-n-1\Longleftrightarrow d=1
. In this case,
X=\mathbf{P}^{n}
.

(2)

k=-n\Longleftrightarrow d=2
. In this case,
X
is a quadric hypersurface in
\mathbf{P}^{n+1}
.

(3)

k=-n+1\Longleftrightarrow d=e+2
. In this case,
X
is a del Pezzo manifold when
n\geq 2
.

(4)

k=-n+2\Longleftrightarrow d=2e+2
. In this case,
X
is a Mukai manifold when
n\geq 3
.

Del Pezzo manifolds (resp. Mukai manifolds) are classified by Fujita
[
9
,
10
]
(resp. Mukai
[
19
]
). If
-K_{X}=(n-3)H
(resp.
-K_{X}=(n-4)H
), then
d=2e+a_{2}+2
(resp.
d=2e+2a_{2}+2
). It would be interesting to find all possible values of
e
and
a_{2}
. The following are partial results.

Lemma 2.1
.

If
k\geq-n+3
, then
d\geq 3e+2
. In particular, if
k=-n+3
, then
a_{2}\geq e
.

Proof.

We have
d\geq 2e+2+a_{2}
. Take a general curve section
C\subseteq\mathbf{P}^{e+1}
, and let
g
be the genus of
C
. Then
K_{C}=(k+n-1)H|_{C}
with
k+n-1\geq 2
, so
2g-2\geq 2d
. Suppose that
d\leq 3e+1
. By Castelnuovo’s genus bound (see e.g.,
[
15
]
),
g\leq 2d-3e-2
.
As
d+1\leq g
, we get
3e+3\leq d
, which is a contradiction. Thus
d\geq 3e+2
.
∎

Lemma 2.2
.

Suppose that
-K_{X}=(n-3)H
with
n\geq 2
. If
e=2
, then
a_{2}=2
or
3
.

Proof.

We have
d=a_{2}+6
. Take a general surface section
S\subseteq\mathbf{P}^{4}
. Then
K_{S}=H|_{S}=:H_{S}
. Recall the self-intersection formula:

d^{2}-10d-5H_{S}.K_{S}-2K_{S}^{2}+12+12p_{a}(S)=0
.
As
p_{a}(S)=p_{g}(S)=h^{0}(S,K_{S})=5
, we have

d^{2}-17d+72=(d-8)(d-9)=0.

Thus
d=8
(
a_{2}=2
) or
d=9
(
a_{2}=3
).
∎

Proposition 2.3
.

Suppose that
-K_{X}=(n-3)H
with
n\geq 2e-1
and
e\geq 3
. If
n\geq 10
, then
e\leq 6
and
d\leq 4e+15\leq 39
.

Proof.

By Barth–Larsen theorem (cf.
[
18
, Corollary 3.2.3]
),
\operatorname{Pic}(X)
is generated by
H
. As
n-3>2n/3
,
[
11
, Proposition 4.3]
implies that
X
is a conic-connected manifold. Then
[
11
, Proposition 3.2]
shows that

n-3\leq\frac{n+\delta(X)}{2}=\frac{2n-e+1}{2},

where
\delta(X)=(2n+1)-(n+e)
is the secant defect which can be computed by Zak’s theorem on linear normality (cf.
[
18
, Theorem 3.4.26]
). Thus
e\leq 7
. If
e=7
, then
n-3=(n+\delta(X))/2
. By
[
11
, Proposition 3.2]
,
X
is a local quadratic entry locus manifold (LQELM) of type
\delta(X)
, but it is impossible by Russo’s classification
[
23
, Corollary 3.1]
. Thus
e\leq 6
. Now, recall from
[
8
, Lemma 3.1]
that

n+e+1=h^{0}(X,H)=\frac{d}{24}(-n^{2}+7n-8)+\frac{c_{2}(X)H^{n-2}}{12}+n-3.

Since
n-3>(n+1)/2
, it follows that
T_{X}
is
H
-semistable. Applying the Bogomolov inequality as in
[
8
, Proof of Proposition 3.3]
, we get

(2.1)

e+4\geq\frac{7n-9}{24n}d>\frac{1}{4}d.

Thus
d<4e+16
, so
d\leq 4e+15\leq 39
.
∎

2.2.
Smallest possbile degree

As
X\subseteq\mathbf{P}^{r}
is non-degenerate, we have
d\geq e+1
. We say
X\subseteq\mathbf{P}^{r}
is a
variety of minimal degree
if
d=e+1
. A famous classical theorem of del Pezzo and Bertini says that a variety of minimal degree is either a rational normal scroll or the second Veronse surface
v_{2}(\mathbf{P}^{2})\subseteq\mathbf{P}^{5}
. Observe that if
d=e+1
, then
e\geq n-1
. It is natural to wonder what the smallest degree is among all non-degenerate smooth projective varieties of dimension
n
in
\mathbf{P}^{n+e}
with
e\leq n-2
. The following is a first step to solving this problem.

Proposition 2.4
.

Assume that
2\leq e\leq n-2
. We have the following:

(1)

d\geq e+2
, and
d=e+2
if and only if
X\subseteq\mathbf{P}^{r}
is a complete intersection of type
(2,2)
, or the Plücker embedding
\mathbf{G}(1,4)\subseteq\mathbf{P}^{9}
or its hyperplane section
(n=5,6,e=3
).

(2)
Suppose that
d\geq e+3
. Then
d\geq 2e+2
, and
d=2e+2
if and only if
X\subseteq\mathbf{P}^{r}
is a complete intersection of type
(2,3)
or
(2,2,2)
, a hyperquadric section of the cone of
\mathbf{G}(1,4)\subseteq\mathbf{P}^{9}

(n=6,e=4)
, the Plücker embedding
\mathbf{G}(1,5)\subseteq\mathbf{P}^{14}

(n=8,e=6)
, or the spinor variety in
\mathbf{P}^{15}
or its linear sections
(n=7,8,9,10,e=5)
.

(3)
Suppose that
d\geq 2e+3
. Then
d\geq 3e+2
unless
X\subseteq\mathbf{P}^{r}
is not an isomorphic projection of the Plücker embedding
\mathbf{G}(1,5)\subseteq\mathbf{P}^{14}
.

Proof.

By Barth–Larsen theorem (cf.
[
18
, Corollary 3.2.3]
),
\operatorname{Pic}(X)
is generated by
H
. If
X
is not Fano, then
[
14
, Theorem 1.2]
says that
d\geq ne+2\geq 4e+2
. Thus we may assume that
X
is Fano, so we may write
-K_{X}=\ell H
for an integer
1\leq\ell\leq n-1
. Recall that
d=e+2
if and only if
\ell=n-1
and
X\subseteq\mathbf{P}^{r}
is linearly normal. Then
(1)
follows from Fujita’s classification
[
9
,
10
]
. Suppose that
d\geq e+3
. By Zak’s theorem (cf.
[
18
, Theorem 3.4.26]
), all varieties in
(1)
do not have an isomorphic projection. Thus
\ell\leq n-2
, so
d\geq 2e+2
. Recall that
d=2e+2
if and only if
\ell=n-2
and
X\subseteq\mathbf{P}^{r}
is linearly normal. Then
(2)
follows from Mukai’s classification
[
19
]
. Suppose that
d\geq 2e+3
. By Zak’s theorem (cf.
[
18
, Theorem 3.4.26 and Remark 3.4.27]
), all varieties in
(2)
do not have an isomorphic projection except the Plücker embedding
\mathbf{G}(1,5)\subseteq\mathbf{P}^{14}
. Thus we may assume that
\ell\leq n-3
. Then
(3)
follows from Lemma
2.1
.
∎

Corollary 2.5
.

If
d\leq n-1
, then
d\geq 2e+2

(
hence
n\geq 2e+3
)
or
X\subseteq\mathbf{P}^{r}
is a complete intersection of type
(2,2)
or the Plücker embedding
\mathbf{G}(1,4)\subseteq\mathbf{P}^{9}
.

Proof.

As
e+1\leq d\leq n-1
, we get
e\leq n-2
. By Proposition
2.4
,
d\geq 2e+2
unless
X\subseteq\mathbf{P}^{r}
is a complete intersection of type
(2,2)
or the Plücker embedding
\mathbf{G}(1,4)\subseteq\mathbf{P}^{9}
. If
d\geq 2e+2
, then
2e+2\leq d\leq n-1
, so
n\geq 2e+3
.
∎

Lemma 2.6
.

Assume that
n\geq 2e+1
. Then

d\geq\frac{e^{2}}{2}+2.

Proof.

We may assume that
e\geq 2
.
Suppose that
d<e^{2}/2+2
. As
e^{2}/2+2\leq ne+1
,
[
14
, Theorem 1.2]
shows that
X
is a Fano variety so that we may write
-K_{X}=\ell H
for some integer
1\leq\ell\leq n+1
. Let
k
be an integer such that
ke+2\leq d\leq(k+1)e+1
. Notice that
k<e/2
. Take a general linear section
Y\subseteq\mathbf{P}^{k+1+e}
of dimension
k+1
. We have
K_{Y}=(n-\ell-k-1)H|_{Y}
. By
[
14
, Theorem 1.2]
(see also
[
15
, p.44]
),
H^{0}(Y,K_{Y})=0
, so
n-\ell-k-1<0
, i.e.,
\ell\geq n-k
. If
(2/3)n\geq n-k
(i.e.,
k\geq n/3
), then

\frac{ne}{3}+2\leq ke+2\leq d<\frac{e^{2}}{2}+2\leq\frac{2e^{2}}{3}+2.

Thus
n\leq 2e
, which is a contradiction. Hence
\ell\geq n-k>(2/3)n
. By
[
11
, Proposition 4.3]
,
X
is a conic-connected manifold, and then by
[
11
, Proposition 3.2]
, we have

n-k\leq\ell\leq\frac{n+\delta(X)}{2}=\frac{2n-e+1}{2},

where
\delta(X)=(2n+1)-(n+e)=n-e+1
is the secant defect, which can be computed by Zak’s theorem on linear normality (cf.
[
18
, Theorem 3.4.26]
). Suppose that
\ell=(2n-e+1)/2
. Then
[
11
, Proposition 3.2]
says
X
is a local quadratic entry locus manifold (LQELM) of type
\delta(X)
. Note that
n/2<\delta(X)<n
. By Russo’s classification
[
23
, Corollary 3.1]
, it is impossible to satisfy
n\geq 2e+1
. Thus

n-k\leq\ell\leq\frac{2n-e}{2}.

This implies that
e/2\leq k
, which is a contradiction.
∎

3.
Proof of Theorem
1.1

In this section, we prove Theorem
1.1
. We assume
e=2
and
n\geq 4
.

Lemma 3.1
.

Suppose that
H^{i}(X,\mathscr{O}_{X}(m))=0
for
1\leq i\leq n-1
and
m\in\mathbf{Z}
and
\operatorname{length}(\langle P,x\rangle\cap X)\leq n
for all
x\in X
and a fixed general point
P\in\mathbf{P}^{n+2}
. Then
X\subseteq\mathbf{P}^{n+2}
is a complete intersection.

Proof.

We take a general projection

\pi\colon X\longrightarrow\overline{X}\subseteq\mathbf{P}^{n+1}

centered the fixed general point
P\in\mathbf{P}^{n+2}
. Then there is a surjective map

\nu\colon\mathscr{O}_{\mathbf{P}^{n+1}}(-(n-1))\oplus\cdots\oplus\mathscr{O}_{\mathbf{P}^{n+1}}(-1)\oplus\mathscr{O}_{\mathbf{P}^{n+1}}\longrightarrow\pi_{*}\mathscr{O}_{X}

such that
E:=\ker(\nu)
is a vector bundle of rank
n
on
\mathbf{P}^{n+1}
(see
[
13
]
and
[
17
]
).
Observe that

H^{i}(\mathbf{P}^{n+1},E^{\vee}(m))=H^{n+1-i}(\mathbf{P}^{n+1},E(-m-n-2))=0~~\text{ for $1\leq i\leq n-1$ and $m\in\mathbf{Z}$}.

By Evans–Griffith theorem
[
7
]
(see
[
5
, Proposition 1.4]
),
E^{\vee}
is splitting, and so is
E
. Then

H^{0}(\mathbf{P}^{n+1},\mathscr{O}_{\mathbf{P}^{n+1}}(m-(n-1))\oplus\cdots\oplus\mathscr{O}_{\mathbf{P}^{n+1}}(m-1)\oplus\mathscr{O}_{\mathbf{P}^{n+1}}(m))\xrightarrow{H^{0}(\nu\otimes\mathscr{O}_{\mathbf{P}^{n+1}}(m))}H^{0}(X,\mathscr{O}_{X}(m))

is surjective for any
m\geq 0
, so
X\subseteq\mathbf{P}^{n+2}
is projectively normal. By Evans–Griffith theorem
[
7
]
(see
[
5
, Corollary 1.5]
),
X\subseteq\mathbf{P}^{n+2}
is a complete intersection.
∎

Remark 3.2
.

Let
X\subseteq\mathbf{P}^{n+3}
be a non-degenerate smooth projective Fano variety of dimension
n\geq 7
. Suppose that there exists a positive integer
k
with
(k+2)(k+1)/2\leq n
such that
\operatorname{length}(\langle L\cap x\rangle\cap X)\leq k+1
(or
\operatorname{reg}(\langle L\cap x\rangle\cap X)\leq k+1
) for all
x\in X
and a fixed general line
L\subseteq\mathbf{P}^{n+3}
. By the arguments in the proof of Lemma
3.1
,
X\subseteq\mathbf{P}^{n+2}
is projectively normal. Then
[
6
, Proposition 1.4]
implies that
X\subseteq\mathbf{P}^{n+3}
is a complete intersection.

Now, we further suppose that
X
is Fano or Calabi–Yau. By Kodaira vanishing and Serre duality,
H^{i}(X,\mathscr{O}_{X}(m))=0
for
1\leq i\leq n-1
and
m\in\mathbf{Z}
. To get
H^{i}(X,\mathscr{O}_{X})=0
for
1\leq i\leq n-1
when
X
is Calabi–Yau, we also need to apply Barth–Larsen theorem (cf.
[
18
, Theorem 3.2.1]
). Our proof of the following lemma is inspired by
[
2
, Proof of Theorem 6]
but much simpler.

Lemma 3.3
.

Suppose that
X
is Fano or Calabi–Yau so that
-K_{X}=\ell H
for
0\leq\ell\leq n-1
. Then
H^{0}(\mathbf{P}^{n+2},\mathscr{I}_{X|\mathbf{P}^{n+2}}(n))\neq 0
.

Proof.

Recall from Subsection
2.1
that
d=a_{2}+6
.
Suppose
\ell\geq n-2
. Then
\ell=n-1
or
n-2
, so
a_{2}\leq 2
. We find

\begin{array}[]{rcl}h^{0}(X,\mathscr{O}_{X}(2))&=&h^{0}(\mathbf{P}^{n},\mathscr{O}_{\mathbf{P}^{n}}(2))+2h^{0}(\mathbf{P}^{n},\mathscr{O}_{\mathbf{P}^{n}}(1))+a_{2}h^{0}(\mathbf{P}^{n},\mathscr{O}_{\mathbf{P}^{n}})\\
&<&h^{0}(\mathbf{P}^{n},\mathscr{O}_{\mathbf{P}^{n}}(2))+2h^{0}(\mathbf{P}^{n},\mathscr{O}_{\mathbf{P}^{n}}(1))+3h^{0}(\mathbf{P}^{n},\mathscr{O}_{\mathbf{P}^{n}})\\
&=&h^{0}(\mathbf{P}^{n+2},\mathscr{O}_{\mathbf{P}^{n+2}}(2)).\end{array}

Thus
H^{0}(\mathbf{P}^{n+2},\mathscr{I}_{X|\mathbf{P}^{n+2}}(2))\neq 0
. Suppose
\ell=n-3
. By Lemma
2.2
,
a_{2}=2
or
3
. We find

\begin{array}[]{rcl}h^{0}(X,\mathscr{O}_{X}(3))&=&h^{0}(\mathbf{P}^{n},\mathscr{O}_{\mathbf{P}^{n}}(3))+2h^{0}(\mathbf{P}^{n},\mathscr{O}_{\mathbf{P}^{n}}(2))+a_{2}h^{0}(\mathbf{P}^{n},\mathscr{O}_{\mathbf{P}^{n}}(1))+2h^{0}(\mathbf{P}^{n},\mathscr{O}_{\mathbf{P}^{n}})\\
&<&h^{0}(\mathbf{P}^{n},\mathscr{O}_{\mathbf{P}^{n}}(3))+2h^{0}(\mathbf{P}^{n},\mathscr{O}_{\mathbf{P}^{n}}(2))+3h^{0}(\mathbf{P}^{n},\mathscr{O}_{\mathbf{P}^{n}}(1))+4h^{0}(\mathbf{P}^{n},\mathscr{O}_{\mathbf{P}^{n}})\\
&=&h^{0}(\mathbf{P}^{n+2},\mathscr{O}_{\mathbf{P}^{n+2}}(3)).\end{array}

This means that
H^{0}(\mathbf{P}^{n+2},\mathscr{I}_{X|\mathbf{P}^{n+2}}(3))\neq 0
.

Suppose that
0\leq\ell\leq n-4
. As is well-known, there is a rank two vector bundle
F
on
\mathbf{P}^{n+2}
fitting into a short exact sequence

0\longrightarrow\mathscr{O}_{\mathbf{P}^{n+2}}\longrightarrow F\longrightarrow\mathscr{I}_{X|\mathbf{P}^{n+2}}(n+3-\ell)\longrightarrow 0.

Note that
c_{1}(F)=n+3-\ell
and
c_{2}(F)=d
. Let
a,b\in\mathbf{C}
be two roots of
x^{2}-(n+3-\ell)x+d=0
. Then
a+b=n+3-\ell=c_{1}(F)
and
ab=d=c_{2}(F)
.
We claim that

a=\begin{cases}2,3,\ldots,n+1&\text{ when $n$ is odd}\\
1,2,\ldots,n+2&\text{ when $n$ is even}.\end{cases}

Granting the claim, we see that

\chi(F(-3))={a-3+n+2\choose n+2}+{b-3+n+2\choose n+2}={a+n-1\choose n+2}+{2n+2-a-\ell\choose n+2}>0.

As
\chi(F(-3))=h^{0}(\mathbf{P}^{n+2},F(-3))-h^{1}(\mathbf{P}^{n+2},F(-3))
, we get

h^{0}(\mathbf{P}^{n+2},\mathscr{I}_{X|\mathbf{P}^{n+2}}(n-\ell))=h^{0}(\mathbf{P}^{n+2},F(-3))>0.

To prove the claim, taking a general linear section of dimension
(n-\ell)
, we may assume that
\ell=0
. We replace
n
by
n-\ell\geq 4
, so we still have
n\geq 4
.
First, consider the case when
n
is odd. Note that

\chi(F(-n-2))=\chi(\mathscr{O}_{\mathbf{P}^{n+2}}(-n-2))+\chi(\mathscr{I}_{X|\mathbf{P}^{n+2}}(1))=0+0=0.

We find

\chi(F(-n-2))={a\choose n+2}+{n+3-a\choose n+2}=\frac{(2a-(n+3))(a-2)\cdots(a-n-1)}{(n+1)!}=0,

so
a=2,3,\ldots,n+1
. Next, consider the case when
n
is even. Note that

\chi(F(-n-3))=\chi(\mathscr{O}_{\mathbf{P}^{n+2}}(-n-3))+\chi(\mathscr{I}_{X|\mathbf{P}^{n+2}})=1+(-1)=0.

We find

\chi(F(-n-3))={a-1\choose n+2}+{n+2-a\choose n+2}=\frac{2(a-1)(a-2)\cdots(a-n-2)}{(n+2)!}=0,

so
a=1,2,\cdots,n+2
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
1.1
.

Note that
H^{i}(X,\mathscr{O}_{X}(m))=0
for
1\leq i\leq n-1
and
m\in\mathbf{Z}
. Since
X
is contained in a hypersurface of degree
n
in
\mathbf{P}^{n+2}
by Lemma
3.3
, we have
\operatorname{length}(\langle P,x\rangle\cap X)\leq n
for all
x\in X
and a fixed general point
P\in\mathbf{P}^{n+2}
. Thus Lemma
3.1
yields that
X\subseteq\mathbf{P}^{n+2}
is a complete intersection. For the last statement, recall from
[
14
, Theorem 1.2]
that if
d\leq 2n+2
, then
X
is Fano or Calabi–Yau.
∎

4.
Proof of Theorem
1.2

In this section, we prove Theorem
1.2
after proving some preliminary results.

Lemma 4.1

(cf.
[
4
, Lemma 2.2]
)
.

Assume that
n\geq 3
and
X\subseteq\mathbf{P}^{r}
is arithmetically Cohen–Macaulay and subcanonical with
K_{X}=kH
for an integer
k
. If
T
is the number of minimal generators of the defining ideal
I_{X|\mathbf{P}^{r}}
, then

T\leq(e-1)\left(d-1-\frac{e}{2}\right)=ed-e-d+1-\frac{e^{2}-e}{2}.

Proof.

Let
M_{H}
be the kernel of the evaluation map
H^{0}(X,H)\otimes\mathscr{O}_{X}\to\mathscr{O}_{X}(1)
. Recall that the Koszul cohomology
K_{p,q}(X,H)
is the complex vector space of
p
-th syzygies of weight
q
of the section ring
R(X,H)=\bigoplus_{m\in\mathbf{Z}}H^{0}(X,mH)
and
K_{p,q}(X,H)=H^{1}(X,\wedge^{p+1}M_{H}\otimes\mathscr{O}_{X}(q-1))
for
p\geq 0
and
q\geq 1
(see e.g.,
[
1
, Chapter 2]
). As
X\subseteq\mathbf{P}^{r}
is projectively normal and
\mathscr{O}_{X}
is
(k+n+1)
-regular with respect to
\mathscr{O}_{X}(1)
(see e.g.,
[
18
, Subsection 1.8]
), we have

T=\kappa_{1,1}+\cdots+\kappa_{1,k+n+1},

where
\kappa_{1,m+1}:=\dim K_{1,m+1}(X,H)
is the graded Betti number for
0\leq m\leq k+n
. By Serre duality,
\kappa_{1,k+n+1}=0
, so we only need to consider the cases
0\leq m\leq k+n-1
. We have

H^{2}(X,\wedge^{2}M_{H}\otimes\mathscr{O}_{X}(m-1))=H^{1}(X,M_{H}\otimes\mathscr{O}_{X}(m))=K_{0,m+1}(X,H)=0~~\text{ for $m\geq 0$}.

Now, suppose that a general projection
\pi\colon X\to\mathbf{P}^{n}
is given by a subspace
V\subseteq H^{0}(X,\mathscr{O}_{X}(1))
of dimension
n+1
. If
M_{V}
is the kernel of the evaluation map
V\otimes\mathscr{O}_{X}\to\mathscr{O}_{X}(1)
, then
M_{V}=\pi^{*}M_{\mathscr{O}_{\mathbf{P}^{n}}(1)}=\pi^{*}\Omega_{\mathbf{P}^{n}}^{1}(1)
. Then
\pi_{*}M_{V}=\Omega_{\mathbf{P}^{n}}^{1}(1)\otimes\pi_{*}\mathscr{O}_{X}
and
\pi_{*}\wedge^{2}M_{V}=\Omega_{\mathbf{P}^{n}}^{2}(2)\otimes\pi_{*}\mathscr{O}_{X}
, where
\pi_{*}\mathscr{O}_{X}=\mathscr{O}_{\mathbf{P}^{n}}^{\oplus a_{0}}\oplus\mathscr{O}_{\mathbf{P}^{n}}(-1)^{\oplus a_{1}}\oplus\cdots\oplus\mathscr{O}_{\mathbf{P}^{n}}(-(n+1+k))^{\oplus a_{n+1+k}}
as in Subsection
2.1
. By Bott vanishing,

\begin{array}[]{l}h^{1}(X,M_{V}\otimes\mathscr{O}_{X}(m))=a_{m+1}~~\text{ and }~~h^{2}(X,M_{V}\otimes\mathscr{O}_{X}(m))=0;\\[3.0pt]
h^{1}(X,\wedge^{2}M_{V}\otimes\mathscr{O}_{X}(m))=0~~\text{ and }~~h^{2}(X,\wedge^{2}M_{V}\otimes\mathscr{O}_{X}(m))=a_{m+2}.\end{array}

Put
\overline{V}:=H^{0}(X,\mathscr{O}_{X}(1))/V
. Note that
\dim\overline{V}=e
. We have a short exact sequence

0\longrightarrow M_{V}\longrightarrow M_{H}\longrightarrow\overline{V}\otimes\mathscr{O}_{X}\longrightarrow 0.

There exists a vector bundle
K
on
X
fitting in short exact sequences

0\longrightarrow\wedge^{2}M_{V}\longrightarrow\wedge^{2}M_{H}\longrightarrow K\longrightarrow 0~~\text{ and }~~0\longrightarrow M_{V}\otimes\overline{V}\longrightarrow K\longrightarrow\wedge^{2}\overline{V}\otimes\mathscr{O}_{X}\longrightarrow 0.

We have

\kappa_{1,m+1}=h^{1}(X,\wedge^{2}M_{H}\otimes\mathscr{O}_{X}(m))=h^{1}(X,K\otimes\mathscr{O}_{X}(m))-h^{2}(X,\wedge^{2}M_{V}\otimes\mathscr{O}_{X}(m)),

and

\begin{array}[]{rcl}h^{1}(X,K\otimes\mathscr{O}_{X}(m))&=&\dim\overline{V}\cdot h^{1}(X,M_{V}\otimes\mathscr{O}_{X}(m))\\
&&-\operatorname{corank}\big(H^{0}(X,K\otimes\mathscr{O}_{X}(m))\longrightarrow\wedge^{2}\overline{V}\otimes H^{0}(X,\mathscr{O}_{X}(m))\big).\end{array}

When
m=0
, since
H^{0}(X,K)=0
, we get
\kappa_{1,1}=ea_{1}-a_{2}-{e\choose 2}
.
When
1\leq m\leq k+n-1
, we get
\kappa_{1,m+1}\leq ea_{m+1}-a_{m+2}
.
Then we find

T\leq e(a_{1}+\cdots+a_{k+n})-(a_{2}+\cdots+a_{k+n+1})-{e\choose 2}=e(d-2)-(d-e-1)-{e\choose 2},

which completes the proof.
∎

The proof of the lemma also yields the following.

Corollary 4.2
.

Suppose that
X
is cut out scheme-theoretically in
\mathbf{P}^{r}
by hypersurfaces of degree at most
d_{0}\geq 3
. If

f(e):=(e-1)\sum_{i=2}^{d_{0}-1}{e+i-1\choose i}+\frac{e^{2}+e}{2}\leq n+1,

then
X\subseteq\mathbf{P}^{r}
is a complete intersection.

Proof.

Notice that
f(e)
is a polynomial of degree
d_{0}
in
e
and
f(e)\geq e(d_{0}-1)+1
. As
f(e)\leq n+1
,
[
4
, Corollary 2]
shows that
X\subseteq\mathbf{P}^{r}
is arithmetically Cohen–Macaulay. Note that
f(e)\geq e^{2}(e+1)/2\geq 3e
. Thus
n\geq 3e-1\geq e+3
. By Barth–Larsen theorem (cf.
[
18
, Corollary 3.2.3]
),
K_{X}=kH
for some integer
k
. We may assume that
3\leq d_{0}\leq k+n+1
. Notice that

a_{i}\leq{e+i-1\choose i}~~\text{ for all $0\leq i\leq n+1+k$}.

If
T
is the minimal number of hypersurfaces in
\mathbf{P}^{r}
cutting out
X
scheme-theoretically, then

\begin{array}[]{l}\displaystyle T\leq\kappa_{1,1}+\cdots+\kappa_{1,d_{0}-1}\leq e(a_{1}+\cdots+a_{d_{0}-1})-(a_{2}+\cdots+a_{d_{0}})-{e\choose 2}\\
\displaystyle\leq(e-1)(a_{2}+\cdots+a_{d_{0}-1})+\frac{e^{2}+e}{2}\leq(e-1)\sum_{i=2}^{d_{0}-1}{e+i-1\choose i}+\frac{e^{2}+e}{2}=f(e)\leq n+1.\end{array}

Now,
[
20
, Theorem 3.2]
implies that
X\subseteq\mathbf{P}^{r}
is a complete intersection.
∎

Remark 4.3
.

When
d_{0}=2
in the situation of the corollary, Ionescu–Russo
[
12
, Theorem 3.8]
proved that if
n\geq 2e+1
, then
X\subseteq\mathbf{P}^{r}
is a complete intersection.

Theorem 4.4

(cf.
[
4
, Corollary 3]
)
.

If

d\leq\frac{n+2}{e}+e,

then
X\subseteq\mathbf{P}^{r}
is a complete intersection unless it is projectively equivalent to a variety of minimal degree with
n-1\leq e\leq n+2
or the Plücker embedding
\mathbf{G}(1,4)\subseteq\mathbf{P}^{9}
or its linear section with
4\leq n\leq 6
.

Proof.

We only need to consider the case that
e\geq 2
.
First, suppose that
e=2
. If
n\leq 3
, then
d\leq 4
and
X\subseteq\mathbf{P}^{r}
is a complete intersection of type
(2,2)
or a variety of minimal degree. If
n\geq 4
, then the assertion follows from Theorem
1.1
. Next, suppose that
e\geq 3
. If
d=e+1
, then
e\leq n+2
and clearly
e\geq n-1
. If
d=e+2
, then
2e\leq n+2
so that
n\geq 4
. By Fujita’s classification
[
9
,
10
]
, if
X\subseteq\mathbf{P}^{r}
is not a complete intersection, then it is the Plücker embedding
\mathbf{G}(1,4)\subseteq\mathbf{P}^{9}
or its linear section.
Now, we assume that
d\geq e+3
. As
e+3\leq(n+2)/e+e
, we get
n\geq 3e-2\geq 2e+1
. Since
d\leq ne+1
, Barth–Larsen theorem (cf.
[
18
, Corollary 3.2.3]
) and
[
14
, Theorem 1.2]
show that
X
is a Fano variety with
-K_{X}=\ell H
for a positive integer
\ell
. By
[
21
, Theorem 9]
,
X\subseteq\mathbf{P}^{r}
is
k
-normal for
k\geq e(d-e+1)-(n+e)
, but the given condition says
e(d-e+1)-(n+e)\leq 2
. Together with Zak’s theorem on linear normality (cf.
[
18
, Theorem 3.4.25]
), we see that
X\subseteq\mathbf{P}^{r}
is projectively normal. Thus
X\subseteq\mathbf{P}^{r}
is arithmetically Cohen–Macaulay. Now, applying Lemma
2.6
and the given condition, we find

ed-e-d+1-\frac{e^{2}-e}{2}\leq ed-e-\frac{e^{2}}{2}-1-\frac{e^{2}-e}{2}=ed-e^{2}-1-\frac{e}{2}\leq ed-e^{2}-2\leq n.

Then Lemma
4.1
says that
I_{X|\mathbf{P}^{r}}
can be generated by
n
generators. As
n\geq 3e-2
,
[
20
, Theorem 3.2]
implies that
X\subseteq\mathbf{P}^{r}
is a complete intersection.
∎

We are ready to prove Theorem
1.2
.

Proof of Theorem
1.2
.

By Lemma
2.6
and the given condition,

\frac{e^{2}}{2}\leq d-2\leq\sqrt[3]{\frac{(n+e^{2}-2e+2)^{2}}{2}},

so

\frac{e^{3}}{2}\leq n+e^{2}-2e+2.

This implies that

d\leq\sqrt[3]{\frac{(n+e^{2}-2e+2)^{2}}{2}}+2\leq\frac{n+e^{2}-2e+2}{e}+2=\frac{n+2}{e}+e.

Now, the theorem follows from Theorem
4.4
since in our case
X\subseteq\mathbf{P}^{r}
is neither a variety of minimal degree nor the Plücker embedding
\mathbf{G}(1,4)\subseteq\mathbf{P}^{9}
or its linear section.
∎
