On discrete analogues of potential vorticity for variational shallow water systems
Elizabeth L. Mansfield

Abstract
We outline how discrete analogues of the conservation of potential vorticity may be achieved in Finite Element numerical schemes for a variational system which has the particle relabelling symmetry, typically shallow water equations. We show that the discrete analogue of the conservation law for potential vorticity converges to the smooth law for potential vorticity, and moreover, for a strong solution, is the weak version of the potential vorticity law. This result rests on recent results by the author with T. Pryer concerning discrete analogues of conservation laws in Finite Element variational problems, together with an observation by P. Hydon concerning how the conservation of potential vorticity in smooth systems arises as a consequence of the linear momenta. The purpose of this paper is to provide all the necessary information for the implementation of the schemes and the necessary numerical tests. A brief tutorial on Noether’s theorem is included to demonstrate the origin of the laws and to demonstrate that the numerical method follows the same basic principle, which is that the law follows directly from the Lie group invariance of the Lagrangian.

Noether’s laws for smooth variational systems

A Lie group is a group which is also a manifold, a topological space with an atlas of smooth Euclidean coordinate charts. In practice, this means the group elements depend smoothly on real parameters.
The two simplest examples occurring in physical and engineering problems are:

1.

the real numbers under addition,
(\mathbb{R},+)
, acting on
a variable
x
by either translation,
x\mapsto x+\epsilon
, or scaling,
x\mapsto\exp(\lambda)x

2.

the special orthogonal group,
SO(n)
, acting on a point
\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
linearly, as
\mathbf{v}\mapsto g\mathbf{v}
, that is, as a rotation.

Any action on variables induces an action on functions of those variables,
g\cdot F(x)=F(g\cdot x)
. The induced action on derivatives is obtained via the chain rule,

g\cdot\frac{\partial u}{\partial x}:=\frac{\partial\left(g\cdot u\right)}{\partial\left(g\cdot x\right)}=\frac{\displaystyle\frac{\partial g\cdot u}{\partial x}}{\displaystyle\frac{\partial g\cdot x}{\partial x}},

where the second equality follows from implicit differentiation in the case here of a single independent variable.
Further, the induced action on integrands of line, surface and volume forms, is also induced by the chain rule, for example,

g\cdot{\rm d}x={\rm d}\left(g\cdot x\right)=\frac{\partial\left(g\cdot x\right)}{\partial x}\,{\rm d}x.

Example 2.1

Consider the action of
SE(2)=S)(2)\ltimes\mathbb{R}^{2}
, given by rotation and translation in the plane. If we take a curve in the plane to be
(x,u(x))
, then we have

g\cdot(x,u(x))=\left(g\cdot x,g\cdot u\right)=\left(\cos(\theta)\,x-\sin(\theta)\,u(x)+a,\sin(\theta)\,x+\cos(\theta)\,u(x)+b\right)

The induced action on
u_{x}
is

g\cdot u_{x}=\frac{{\rm d}(g\cdot u)}{{\rm d}(g\cdot x)}=\frac{{\rm d}(g\cdot u)}{{\rm d}x}\Big/\frac{{\rm d}(g\cdot x)}{{\rm d}x}=\frac{\sin(\theta)+\cos(\theta)u_{x}}{\cos(\theta)-\sin(\theta)u_{x}},

while that on
u_{xx}
is

g\cdot u_{xx}=\frac{{\rm d}(g\cdot u_{x})}{{\rm d}x}\Big/\frac{{\rm d}(g\cdot x)}{{\rm d}x}=\frac{u_{xx}}{\left(\cos(\theta)-\sin(\theta)\,u_{x}\right)^{3}}.

The induced action on the integrand,
f(x,u(x))\,{\rm d}x
is given by

\begin{array}[]{rcl}g\cdot\left(F(x,u(x))\,{\rm d}x\right)&=&F(g\cdot x,g\cdot u)\,{\rm d}(g\cdot x)\\
&=&F(g\cdot x,g\cdot u)\,\left(\cos(\theta)-\sin(\theta)\,u_{x}\right){\rm d}x.\end{array}

We are interested in the induced action on a Lagrangian, which for a one dimensional problem, takes the form,

g\cdot\int_{\Omega}L(x,u,u_{x})\,{\rm d}x:=\int_{g\cdot\Omega}L\left(g\cdot x,g\cdot u,g\cdot u_{x}\right)\,{\rm d}\left(g\cdot x\right)=\int_{\Omega}L\left(g\cdot x,g\cdot u,g\cdot u_{x}\right)\frac{\partial g\cdot x}{\partial x}\,{\rm d}x

(1)

where the second equality follows by considering all quantities with respect to the original independent variable
This is the same as transforming back to the original variables on the base space (
[
7
]
, page 249) via the group action map on the base space.
It is important to note that as
g\cdot x
is a function of
(x,u(x),g)

\Omega

If
g\cdot\int L\,{\rm d}x=\int L\,{\rm d}x
for all
g\in G

\gamma(0)=e

\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}\epsilon}\Big|_{\epsilon=0}\gamma(\epsilon)\cdot\int L\,{\rm d}x=0.

(2)

The result is the so-called
Infinitesimal Criterion of Invariance
. As we show below, Noether’s Theorem is the straightforward result of this criterion for an invariant Lagrangian.

We now turn to the statement of Noether’s Theorem.
Noether’s theorem is, in fact, “nothing but" a rearrangement of the infinitesimal criterion of invariance.

We show only the simplest low order cases, and then those that pertain to our systems of interest. The text by Olver, gives a comprehensive description and proof of recurrence formulae for the infinitesimals, and the proof of the Noether’s Theorem, in the general case. We record here the result for some
simple examples.

Consider the simplest, lowest order, lowest dimension, Lagrangian,
L(x,u,u_{x})\,{\rm d}x
,
and suppose this Lagrangian is invariant under a Lie group action with parameter
\epsilon
. If the infinitesimals are

\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}\epsilon}\Big|_{\epsilon=0}g(\epsilon)\cdot x=\xi(x,u),\qquad\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}\epsilon}\Big|_{\epsilon=0}g(\epsilon)\cdot u=\phi(x,u)

(3)

then the infinitesimal criterion for invariance yields,

\frac{\partial L}{\partial x}\xi(x,u)+\frac{\partial L}{\partial u}\phi(x,u)+\frac{\partial L}{\partial u_{x}}\left(\frac{{\rm d}\phi}{{\rm d}x}-u_{x}\frac{{\rm d}\xi}{{\rm d}x}\right)+L\frac{{\rm d}\xi}{{\rm d}x}=0.

(4)

Adding and subtracting the terms needed to convert
\partial L/\partial x
into
{\rm d}L/{\rm d}x
in the Infinitesimal Criterion of Invariance, Equation (
4
) and rearranging, we obtain

\left(\phi-u_{x}\xi\right)E(L)+\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}x}\left(L\xi+\frac{\partial L}{\partial u_{x}}\left(\phi-u_{x}\xi\right)\right)=0

(5)

where

E(L)=\frac{\partial L}{\partial u}-\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}x}\frac{\partial L}{\partial u_{x}}

is the Euler Lagrange equation. Therefore, on solutions of
E(L)=0
, we will have

\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}x}\left(L\xi+\frac{\partial L}{\partial u_{x}}\left(\phi-u_{x}\xi\right)\right)=0

which is Noether’s conservation law for this case.

where

\begin{array}[]{rclcl}\phi_{[x]}&=&\displaystyle\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}\epsilon}\big|_{\epsilon=0}\widetilde{u_{x}}&=&\displaystyle\frac{{\rm d}\phi}{{\rm d}x}-u_{x}\frac{{\rm d}\xi}{{\rm d}x}\\[7.5pt]
\phi_{[xx]}&=&\displaystyle\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}\epsilon}\big|_{\epsilon=0}\widetilde{u_{xx}}&=&\displaystyle\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}x}\left(\frac{{\rm d}\phi}{{\rm d}x}-u_{x}\frac{{\rm d}\xi}{{\rm d}x}\right)-u_{xx}\frac{{\rm d}\xi}{{\rm d}x}\end{array}

2.1.1
The particle relabelling symmetry and the conservation of potential vorticity

We consider again the problem of Example

(a,b,t)
where

(a,b)

The dependent variables

\widetilde{a}=A(a,b),\qquad\widetilde{b}=B(a,b),\qquad A_{a}B_{b}-A_{b}B_{a}=1.

Further,
\widetilde{t}=t

A first order Lagrangian which is invariant under this

(10)

A higher order Lagrangian with this symmetry will have additional arguments of the form of invariant derivatives of the generating invariant
\Delta

\frac{\partial^{m+n}}{\partial x^{m}\partial y^{n}}\Delta,\qquad\frac{\partial}{\partial x}=\frac{1}{\Delta}\left(y_{b}\frac{\partial}{\partial a}-y_{a}\frac{\partial}{\partial b}\right),\quad\frac{\partial}{\partial y}=\frac{1}{\Delta}\left(-x_{b}\frac{\partial}{\partial a}+x_{a}\frac{\partial}{\partial b}\right)

(11)

as well as derivatives with respect to

\displaystyle\left[\frac{\partial}{\partial t},\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\right]
and
\displaystyle\left[\frac{\partial}{\partial t},\frac{\partial}{\partial y}\right]

and

The formula for the potential vorticity of the smooth problem is obtained by cross-differentiation of the formulae for the linear momenta, an observation by Peter Hydon,
[
4
]

The Noether identities for the smooth linear momenta of a first order Lagrangian, are given by

\begin{array}[]{rcl}x_{a}E^{x}+y_{a}E^{y}&=&\displaystyle\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}a}\left(L+\Delta\frac{\partial L}{\partial\Delta}\right)+\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}\left(x_{a}\frac{\partial L}{\partial x_{t}}+y_{a}\frac{\partial L}{\partial y_{t}}\right)\\[7.5pt]
x_{b}E^{x}+y_{b}E^{y}&=&\displaystyle\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}b}\left(L+\Delta\frac{\partial L}{\partial\Delta}\right)+\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}\left(x_{b}\frac{\partial L}{\partial x_{t}}+y_{b}\frac{\partial L}{\partial y_{t}}\right).\end{array}

Cross differentiation of these, on solution of the Euler Lagrange equations, yields the conservation of potential vorticity, given explicitly as

\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}\left(\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}b}\left(x_{a}\frac{\partial L}{\partial x_{t}}+y_{a}\frac{\partial L}{\partial y_{t}}\right)-\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}a}\left(x_{b}\frac{\partial L}{\partial x_{t}}+y_{b}\frac{\partial L}{\partial y_{t}}\right)\right)=0,

(12)

which can be simplified to

\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial y}\frac{\partial L}{\partial x_{t}}-\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\frac{\partial L}{\partial y_{t}}\right)=0

(13)

using the definitions of the operators in Equation (
11
).
The exactly conserved density in Equation (
13
) is known as potential vorticity
\Psi
,

\Psi=\frac{\partial}{\partial y}\frac{\partial L}{\partial x_{t}}-\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\frac{\partial L}{\partial y_{t}}

and the references
therein).

If we let
u=\dot{x}
and
v=\dot{y}
, then the SW equations (
17
) can be
written as

\displaystyle\dot{x}

\displaystyle=u,

(20a)

\displaystyle\dot{y}

\displaystyle=v,

(20b)

\displaystyle\dot{u}

\displaystyle+gh(y_{b}h_{a}-y_{a}h_{b})-fv=0,

(20c)

\displaystyle\dot{v}

\displaystyle+gh(x_{a}h_{b}-x_{b}h_{a})+fu=0,

(20d)

where
h
is given by (
16
), which we shall refer to as the
potential form
of the SW equations (
17
).

Salmon
[
14
]
showed that there is a first-order Lagrangian

\mathcal{L}=(u-R)\dot{x}+(v+P)\dot{y}-\tfrac{1}{2}\left(u^{2}+v^{2}+gh\right),

(21)

where the functions
P=P(x,y)
and
R=R(x,y)
satisfy

P_{x}+R_{y}=f,

with

given by (
16
) and
f
constant, for which the associated
Euler-Lagrange equations represent (up to a sign) the SW equations (

It can be checked that the formula for the conserved quantity is indeed that given in Equation (

Example 2: the semi-geostrophic SW equations

In the past thirty years, the semi-geostrophic equations have become a
model for describing atmospheric motions on a synoptic scale, including
the presence of fronts
[
9
,
12
]
.

The semi-geostrophic approximation to equations (
17
) is the
replacement of the true acceleration by the time derivative of the
vector

u_{g}=-\frac{g}{f}h_{y},\qquad v_{g}=\frac{g}{f}h_{x},

(22)

following the particle. This vector field
is called the
geostrophic velocity
. Thus, the
semi-geostrophic approximation seeks to find motions satisfying

\displaystyle\dot{u}_{g}+gh_{x}-f\dot{y}

\displaystyle=0,

(23a)

\displaystyle\dot{v}_{g}+gh_{y}+f\dot{x}

\displaystyle=0.

(23b)

It is also known that the
semi-geostrophic potential
vorticity
, given by

\Omega^{*}=\frac{1}{h}\left(f+\frac{\partial v_{g}}{\partial x}-\frac{\partial u_{g}}{\partial y}+\frac{1}{f}\frac{\partial(u_{g},v_{g})}{\partial(x,y)}\right),

(24)

is conserved on particles
[
11
]
.

The
classical form
of the SG equations (
23
) are

\displaystyle hh_{b}\dot{x_{a}}-hh_{a}\dot{x_{b}}+(\dot{h}h_{b}+h\dot{h_{b}})x_{a}-(\dot{h}h_{a}+h\dot{h_{a}})x_{b}+fhh_{b}y_{a}-fhh_{a}y_{b}

\displaystyle=-\frac{f^{2}}{g}\dot{y},

(25a)

\displaystyle hh_{b}\dot{y_{a}}-hh_{a}\dot{y_{b}}+(\dot{h}h_{b}+h\dot{h_{b}})y_{a}-(\dot{h}h_{a}+h\dot{h_{a}})y_{b}-fhh_{b}x_{a}+fhh_{a}x_{b}

\displaystyle=\frac{f^{2}}{g}\dot{x},

(25b)

with

h
given by (
16
). This system is obtained after substituting
the functions
u_{g}
and
v_{g}
given by (
22
) into (
23
).

The potential form of the SG equations is given by (
22
) and (
23
), i.e.

\displaystyle u_{g}

\displaystyle=-\frac{g}{f}h(x_{a}h_{b}-x_{b}h_{a}),

(26a)

\displaystyle v_{g}

\displaystyle=\frac{g}{f}h(y_{b}h_{a}-y_{a}h_{b}),

(26b)

\displaystyle\dot{u}_{g}

\displaystyle+gh(y_{b}h_{a}-y_{a}h_{b})-f\dot{y}=0,

(26c)

\displaystyle\dot{v}_{g}

\displaystyle+gh(x_{a}h_{b}-x_{b}h_{a})+f\dot{x}=0,

(26d)

where
f
is a nonzero constant and
h
is given by (
16
).

The
SW equations (
26
) represent the Euler-Lagrange equations
associated with the first-order Lagrangian

[
5
]

\mathcal{L}=(u_{g}-R)\dot{x}+(v_{g}+P)\dot{y}-\tfrac{1}{2}(u_{g}^{2}+v_{g}^{2}+gh)-r\dot{u}_{g}+p\dot{v}_{g},

(27)

where
P(x,y)
,
R(x,y)
,
p(u_{g},v_{g})
and
r(u_{g},v_{g})
are arbitrary
functions satisfying

P_{x}+R_{y}=f,\qquad p_{u_{g}}+r_{v_{g}}={1/f},

(28)

and
h
is given by (
16
).

This case requires an extension of the theory elaborated above as we have additional invariants with time derivatives. Working through the calculations of the conserved quantity, we obtain

\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}\left(\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}b}\left(x_{a}\frac{\partial L}{\partial x_{t}}+y_{a}\frac{\partial L}{\partial y_{t}}+u_{g,a}\frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot{u_{g}}}+v_{g,a}\frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot{v_{g}}}\right)-\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}a}\left(x_{b}\frac{\partial L}{\partial x_{t}}+y_{b}\frac{\partial L}{\partial y_{t}}+u_{g,b}\frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot{u_{g}}}+v_{g,b}\frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot{v_{g}}}\right)\right)=0,

(29)

The conserved quantity is straightforwardly shown to be that given in Equation
24
, using the definitions of the operators in Equation (
11
) and noting that, for example,

\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot{u_{g}}}=\frac{\partial r}{\partial x}=\frac{\partial r}{\partial u_{g}}\frac{\partial u_{g}}{\partial x}+\frac{\partial r}{\partial v_{g}}\frac{\partial v_{g}}{\partial x}

and similarly for

\frac{\partial}{\partial y}\frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot{u_{g}}},\qquad\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot{v_{g}}},\qquad\frac{\partial}{\partial y}\frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot{v_{g}}}

appearing in Equation (
29
), and finally using Equation (
28
).

3
Noether identities for numerical variational problems – Finite Element

We now look at the Finite Element Noether identity, developed by the author with T. Pryer,
[
6
]
. We treat the approximate Lagrangian as an exact problem, and require invariance of the Finite Element Lagrangian.
We no longer have point wise invariance, and so the Finite Element Noether identity will be an integral expression.

We consider the Lagrangian,
\int L(x,u,u_{x})\,{\rm d}x
. The Finite Element Euler Lagrange equations for the Finite Element solution
U
are

0=\int_{\Omega}E^{u}\big|_{u=U}V+\int_{\mathcal{F}}\left[\kern-3.0pt\left[{\frac{\partial L}{\partial U_{x}}}\right]\kern-3.0pt\right]V,\qquad\forall V\in\mathbb{V}.

(30)

where
\mathbb{V}
is an appropriate space of piecewise continuous polynomial functions over a
triangulation, and where
E^{u}|_{u=U}=-\left({{\rm d}}/{{\rm d}x}\right){\partial L}/{\partial U_{x}}+{\partial L}/{\partial U}.

The Finite Element Noether Identity is

\begin{split}0&=\int_{\Omega}E^{u}\big|_{u=U}\left(\phi-U_{x}\xi\right)+\int_{\mathcal{F}}\left[\kern-3.0pt\left[{\frac{\partial L}{\partial U_{x}}\left(\phi-U_{x}\xi\right)+L\xi}\right]\kern-3.0pt\right]\end{split}

(31)

where
\xi
and
\phi
are the infinitesimals defined in Equation (
3
).
Thus, for example, for the conservation of linear momentum, where the group action is translation in
x
, so that
\widetilde{x}=x+\epsilon
, we must have that
L
does not depend explicitly on
x
. Then the infinitesimals of Equation (
8
) are given by

(\xi,\phi)=(1,0)
and the Noether identity for this symmetry is given by

\begin{split}0&=\int_{\Omega}E^{u}\big|_{u=U}\left(-U_{x}\right)+\int_{\mathcal{F}}\left[\kern-3.0pt\left[{\frac{\partial L}{\partial U_{x}}\left(-U_{x}\right)+L}\right]\kern-3.0pt\right].\end{split}

(32)

This compares to the smooth case where we would have

{\rm d}/{\rm d}x\left(L-u_{x}\partial L/\partial u_{x}\right)=0
. We note that the term incorporating the Euler Lagrange equations in the FE identity, cannot be set to zero, as it is not the case that
\phi-U_{X}\xi
is in the test space in general.

We now turn to the FE case and consider our FE Noether identities given by the particle relabelling symmetry, for the Lagrangian in (
10
).
The infinitesimals needed to express the identity, analogous to those in Equation (
8
), for
(a,b,t,x,y)
are
(\xi^{a},\xi^{b},\xi^{t},\phi^{x},\phi^{y})
, and satisfy

\frac{\partial\xi^{a}}{\partial a}+\frac{\partial\xi^{b}}{\partial b}=0,\quad\xi^{t}=0,\quad\phi^{x}=0,\quad\phi^{y}=0.

We suppose that the domain

\overline{\Omega}
is made up of domains of the form
\overline{\Omega}_{i,n}=\Omega_{i}\times[t_{n},t_{n+1}]
where
\Omega_{i}\subset(a,b)
-space.

Locally we may set

\xi^{a}=\partial_{b}\varphi=\varphi_{b}
and
\xi^{b}=-\partial_{a}\varphi=\varphi_{a}
for some smooth function
\varphi
, and then the FE Noether identity in this case, takes the form

\begin{array}[]{rcl}0&=&\displaystyle\sum_{i}\int_{\overline{\Omega}_{i,n}}\left[\left(\varphi_{a}X_{b}-\varphi_{b}X_{a}\right)E^{x}|_{x=X}+\left(\varphi_{a}Y_{b}-\varphi_{b}Y_{a}\right)E^{y}|_{y=Y}\right]\,{\rm d}a\,{\rm d}b\,{\rm d}t\\[12.0pt]
&&\displaystyle\qquad+\left(\int_{\Omega_{i}\times\{t_{n+1}\}}-\int_{\Omega_{i}\times\{t_{n}\}}\right)\left(\left(\varphi_{a}X_{b}-\varphi_{b}X_{a}\right)\frac{\partial L}{\partial X_{t}}+\left(\varphi_{a}Y_{b}-\varphi_{b}Y_{a}\right)\frac{\partial L}{\partial Y_{t}}\right)\,{\rm d}a\,{\rm d}b\\[12.0pt]
&&\displaystyle\qquad+\int_{t_{n}}^{t_{n+1}}\int_{\gamma_{i}(s)=\partial\Omega_{i}}\left[\kern-2.0pt\left[L+\Delta\frac{\partial L}{\partial\Delta}\right]\kern-2.0pt\right]\Big|_{\gamma_{i}}\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}s}\varphi(\gamma_{i}(s))\,{\rm d}s\,{\rm d}t.\end{array}

(33)

This is simply the three dimensional analogue of the above equation (
31
), adapted to two dependent variables and the given infinitesimals for this group action.

We now show that the continuum limit of the identity (
33
) is indeed the conservation law (
13
).

If
|t_{n+1}-t_{n}|
is small, we have for any continuous integrand
F
, that there exists
\tau
,
t_{n}<\tau<t_{n+1}
such that

\int_{t_{n}}^{t_{n+1}}F(t)\,{\rm d}t=(t_{n+1}-t_{n})F(\tau),

by the mean value theorem. We apply this result to the first and third integrals in Equation (
33
).
Doing this, then dividing through by
(t_{n+1}-t_{n})
and sending
t_{n+1}\rightarrow t_{n}
, the first and third intervals limit to the integral over
\Omega_{i}
at
t=t_{n}
. The second integral in Equation (
33
)
limits to a derivative with respect to
t
. We thus obtain, for any smooth function
\varphi
, that

\begin{split}0&=\displaystyle\sum_{i}\int_{\bar{\Omega}_{i}}\left[\left(\varphi_{a}X_{b}-\varphi_{b}X_{a}\right)E^{x}|_{x=X}+\left(\varphi_{a}Y_{b}-\varphi_{b}Y_{a}\right)E^{y}|_{y=Y}\right]\,{\rm d}a\,{\rm d}b\\
&\displaystyle\qquad+\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}\int_{\Omega_{i}}\left\{\varphi_{a}\left(X_{b}\frac{\partial L}{\partial X_{t}}+Y_{b}\frac{\partial L}{\partial Y_{t}}\right)-\varphi_{b}\left(X_{a}\frac{\partial L}{\partial X_{t}}+Y_{a}\frac{\partial L}{\partial Y_{t}}\right)\right\}{\rm d}a\,{\rm d}b\\
&\displaystyle\qquad+\int_{\gamma_{i}(s)=\partial\Omega_{i}}\left.\left[\kern-2.0pt\left[L+\Delta\frac{\partial L}{\partial\Delta}\right]\kern-2.0pt\right]\right|_{\gamma_{i}}\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}s}\varphi(\gamma_{i}(s))\,{\rm d}s.\end{split}

(34)

Finally, we observe that Equation (
34
) is the weak form of the smooth law. Indeed, for a strong solution of the Euler Lagrange equations, the
E^{x}
,
E^{y}
terms are zero, as is the jump term in the third integrand. Taking
\varphi
to be a bump function with support anywhere inside a mesh simplex, then
integration by parts yields the same cross-derivative as that yielding the smooth potential vorticity, to be zero.

Equation (
34
) is straightforwardly extended to the potential forms of the equations and their Lagrangians in the examples in Section
2.2
.
