Analysis of fluid flow models
Guy Latouche, Giang T. Nguyen

Abstract
Markov-modulated fluids have a long history. They form a simple class of Markov additive processes, and were initially developed in the 1950s as models for dams and reservoirs, before gaining much popularity in the 1980s as models for buffers in telecommunication systems, when they became known as fluid queues . More recent applications are in risk theory and in environmental studies. In telecommunication systems modelling, the attention focuses on determining the stationary distribution of the buffer content. Early ODE resolution techniques have progressively given way to approaches grounded in the analysis of the physical evolution of the system, and one only needs now to solve a Riccati equation in order to obtain several quantities of interest. To the early algorithms proposed in the Applied Probability literature, numerical analysts have added new algorithms, improved in terms of convergence speed, numerical accuracy, and domain of applicability. We give here a high-level presentation of the matrix-analytic approach to the analysis of fluid queues, briefly address computational issues, and conclude by indicating how this has been extended to more general processes.

In many applications,

C_{\mbox{\tiny$+$}}^{-1}T_{{\mbox{\tiny$+$}}{\mbox{\tiny$-$}}}(s)+C_{\mbox{\tiny$+$}}^{-1}T_{{\mbox{\tiny$+$}}{\mbox{\tiny$+$}}}(s)\widehat{\Psi}(s)+\widehat{\Psi}(s)|C_{\mbox{\tiny$-$}}|^{-1}T_{{\mbox{\tiny$-$}}{\mbox{\tiny$-$}}}(s)+\widehat{\Psi}(s)|C_{\mbox{\tiny$-$}}|^{-1}T_{{\mbox{\tiny$-$}}{\mbox{\tiny$+$}}}(s)\widehat{\Psi}(s)=0,

where

Q_{{\mbox{\tiny$-$}}{\mbox{\tiny$+$}}}&Q_{{\mbox{\tiny$-$}}{\mbox{\tiny$-$}}}-sI\end{bmatrix}+\begin{bmatrix}Q_{{\mbox{\tiny$+$}}0}\\
Q_{{\mbox{\tiny$-$}}0}\end{bmatrix}(sI-Q_{00})^{-1}\begin{bmatrix}Q_{0{\mbox{\tiny$+$}}}&Q_{0{\mbox{\tiny$-$}}}\end{bmatrix}.

The Riccati equation
may be solved for any given

by the same algorithms as discussed in

, and this makes it feasible to compute the
distributions themselves by numerical inversion procedures.

Moments of
first passage times are obtained by taking the derivatives of

and evaluating it for

. Derivatives of

are more involved, as shown in Bean

]
, and are expressed as solutions of nonsingular

The

may be obtained in a more straightforward manner,
as we show in (

Section

to a given level. Here, we deal with

We define as follows the matrices

\displaystyle={\rm P}[\tau_{b}^{\mbox{\tiny$+$}}<\tau_{a}^{\mbox{\tiny$-$}},\varphi(\tau_{b}^{\mbox{\tiny$+$}})=j|X(0)=0,\varphi(0)=i],\qquad\mbox{$i\in\mathcal{S}$, $j\in\mathcal{S}_{\mbox{\tiny$+$}}$.}

\begin{bmatrix}B^{(a,b)}&A^{(a,b)}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}B_{{\mbox{\tiny$+$}}{\mbox{\tiny$+$}}}^{(a,b)}&A_{{\mbox{\tiny$+$}}{\mbox{\tiny$-$}}}^{(a,b)}\\

If
\varphi(0)
is in
\mathcal{S}_{0}

\mathcal{S}_{\mbox{\tiny$+$}}
or

\begin{bmatrix}B_{0{\mbox{\tiny$+$}}}^{(a,b)}&A_{0{\mbox{\tiny$-$}}}^{(a,b)}\end{bmatrix}=(-Q_{00})^{-1}\begin{bmatrix}Q_{0{\mbox{\tiny$+$}}}&Q_{0{\mbox{\tiny$-$}}}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}B_{{\mbox{\tiny$+$}}{\mbox{\tiny$+$}}}^{(a,b)}&A_{{\mbox{\tiny$+$}}{\mbox{\tiny$-$}}}^{(a,b)}\\

Remark 8.1

We see here why we defined
\tau_{x}^{\mbox{\tiny$-$}}
in Section

as

Lemma 8.2

For
\varphi(0)
in
\mathcal{S}_{\mbox{\tiny$+$}}
or
\mathcal{S}_{\mbox{\tiny$-$}}

are solutions of the linear system

\begin{bmatrix}B_{{\mbox{\tiny$+$}}{\mbox{\tiny$+$}}}^{(a,b)}&A_{{\mbox{\tiny$+$}}{\mbox{\tiny$-$}}}^{(a,b)}\\

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}0&\Psi e^{U(a+b)}\\
\widehat{\Psi}e^{\widehat{U}(a+b)}&0\end{bmatrix},

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}e^{\widehat{U}b}&\Psi e^{Ua}\\
\widehat{\Psi}e^{\widehat{U}b}&e^{Ua}\end{bmatrix}.

Proof

Assume
\varphi(0)
is in

. We have

(37)

Indeed, the left-hand side gives the distribution of the phase when

without going down to

and the probability that it goes down to

from below, and eventually goes

to
b
.
Similarly,

\Psi e^{Ua}=A_{{\mbox{\tiny$+$}}{\mbox{\tiny$-$}}}^{(a,b)}+B_{{\mbox{\tiny$+$}}{\mbox{\tiny$+$}}}^{(a,b)}\Psi e^{U(a+b)}.

(38)

Equations (
37
,
38
) form the first row of the system (
36
). The
argument for the second row is similar.

\square

If
\mu\not=0
, then (

or

(see da Silva Soares and Latouche
[

for details). If

and

are
stochastic matrices,

is singular and we need to add one

Equation (

. This equation is identical to the

in

[

in a comment after Theorem 3.1, referring to a

et al.

[

by a spectral

Lemma 8.3

If
\mu=0
, then the escape probability matrix is such that

\begin{bmatrix}B_{{\mbox{\tiny$+$}}{\mbox{\tiny$+$}}}^{(a,b)}&A_{{\mbox{\tiny$+$}}{\mbox{\tiny$-$}}}^{(a,b)}\\
B_{{\mbox{\tiny$-$}}{\mbox{\tiny$+$}}}^{(a,b)}&A_{{\mbox{\tiny$-$}}{\mbox{\tiny$-$}}}^{(a,b)}\end{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{\beta}=\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{h}_{\mbox{\tiny$+$}}\\
\boldsymbol{h}_{\mbox{\tiny$-$}}\end{bmatrix},

where

\boldsymbol{\beta}=\begin{bmatrix}\ \ b\boldsymbol{1}_{\mbox{\tiny$+$}}+\boldsymbol{h}_{\mbox{\tiny$+$}}\\

Proof

We know from Coolen-Schrijner and van Doorn
[

that the deviation matrix of

, that is,

\int_{0}^{\infty}(e^{Qu}-\boldsymbol{1}\cdot\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{\textnormal{\tiny T}})\,\mathrm{d}u=-Q^{\#}
.
Therefore,

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{h}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}(e^{Qu}-\boldsymbol{1}\cdot\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{\textnormal{\tiny T}})\,\mathrm{d}u\ \boldsymbol{c}

\displaystyle=\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\int_{0}^{t}(e^{Qu}-\boldsymbol{1}\cdot\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{\textnormal{\tiny T}})\,\mathrm{d}u\ \boldsymbol{c}

\displaystyle=\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\int_{0}^{t}(e^{Qu}\boldsymbol{c}-\boldsymbol{1}\cdot\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{\textnormal{\tiny T}}\boldsymbol{c})\,\mathrm{d}u

\displaystyle=\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\int_{0}^{t}e^{Qu}\boldsymbol{c}\,\mathrm{d}u

(41)

as

and

Furthermore,

and we write

By conditioning on the first time the process escapes from

\displaystyle={\rm E}_{0}[X_{\infty}\mathds{1}\{\tau_{b}^{\mbox{\tiny$+$}}<\tau_{a}^{\mbox{\tiny$-$}}\}|\varphi(0)]+{\rm E}_{0}[X_{\infty}\mathds{1}\{\tau_{a}^{\mbox{\tiny$-$}}<\tau_{b}^{\mbox{\tiny$+$}}\}|\varphi(0)]

\square

10
Extensions

After the publication of Ramaswami’s seminal paper
[
44
]
,
the basic fluid queue model defined through Equations (
1
) and (
3
) has been
extended in many ways. We cite some of these, without getting into
details, the list is far from exhaustive.

Finite buffers

In some applications,
Y(t)
represents the content of some finite
buffer, so that the level may only take values in some interval
0\leq Y(t)\leq B<\infty
(see da Silva Soares and Latouche
[
23
,
24
]

and Bean
et al.

[
13
]
).
In that case, it is natural to
choose as regeneration points the epochs of return to the upper
boundary in addition to the returns to level 0, and the transition
matrix
H
between regeneration points makes use of
first passage probabilities from boundary to boundary. These may be obtained from Lemma
8.2
with

a=0
and
b=B
, if the process starts from the lower boundary, or

a=B
and
b=0
, if the process starts from the upper boundary.

The final expression for the stationary density is a mixture of two matrix
exponentials,
e^{Kx}
and
e^{\widehat{K}(B-x)}
. Details may be found in

[
25
]
and in
[
38
]
.

Level feedback

In our presentation so far, the level is driven by the phase, subject
to the boundary constrains that
Y(t)\geq 0
, or
0\leq Y(t)\leq B
, while the phase evolves independently of the level. In many
cases, the level has a direct influence on the evolution of the phase;
for instance, one might reduce the flow into the buffer as the level gets
nears the upper boundary, so as to avoid spillage.

In Bean
et al.

[
9
]
and da Silva Soares
[
25
]

(and other references cited there), one defines a number of threshold
values
0\leq b_{1}<b_{2}<\cdots<b_{N}\leq\infty
such that the
parameters
C
and
Q
of the phase process change upon the crossing of a
threshold. Here, regenerations occur when the fluid reaches any of
the threshold, and the analysis of such systems may be based on a
systematic extension of the results for the system with a finite buffer.

Fluid with jumps

In Remiche
[
45
]

Y(t)

represents the supply of tokens in a leaky bucket system. It increases
linearly in time and drops by a positive amount each time a file is
transmitted.
In Bean
et al.

[
12
]
,
Y(t)
represents the amount of
wear of a power generator and it may jump from
B
(indicating that
the generator is unusable) to 0 (indicating that it has been replaced
by a new equipment).
As discussed in Badescu
et
al.

[
7
]
and Stanford
et al.

[
47
]
,
risk processes may be analysed as fluid
queues with jumps.

If the jumps have a
phase-type distribution, then the analysis of the process requires
very little adaptation from the material presented in the present paper.

Markov modulated Brownian motion

The definition of these processes is very similar to that of fluid
flows. The difference is that the fluid evolves like a Brownian
motion with parameters (drift and variance) which depend on

\varphi(t)
. Recent references are
d’Auria
et al.

[
26
,
28
]
, Ivanovs
[
36
]
,
Gribaudo
et al.

[
31
]
where the authors focused on
obtaining time-dependent distributions and first hitting times using
different approaches: stochastic ODE resolution, spectral decomposition
and martingale theory. Breuer
[
18
]
determined the occupation time of the process in an interval before a one- or two-sided exit.
Latouche and Nguyen
[
38
,
39
]
are two recent papers that
follow a regenerative approach similar to the one developed here.

Two-dimensional fluid

A few authors have considered systems where the component
X
is
two-dimensional: Bean and O’Reilly
[
10
,
11
]
, Foss and
Miyazawa
[
29
]
and Latouche
et al.

[
40
]

among others. The area of two- or higher-dimensional fluids is still
wide open, with many exciting unanswered questions.
