Low-Rank Tensor Decomposition over Finite Fields
Jason Yang

Abstract
We show that finding rank- R R decompositions of a 3D tensor, for R ≤ 4 R\leq 4 , over a fixed finite field can be done in polynomial time. However, if some cells in the tensor are allowed to have arbitrary values, then rank-2 is NP-hard over the integers modulo 2. We also explore rank-1 decomposition of a 3D tensor and of a matrix where some cells are allowed to have arbitrary values.



1
Introduction

Given a
p\times q\times s
tensor (multidimensional array)
T
, a rank-
R
decomposition is a collection of variables
A_{a,b},B_{c,d},C_{e,f}
such that

T_{i,j,k}=\sum_{r=0}^{R-1}A_{r,i}B_{r,j}C_{r,k}\ \forall i,j,k.

This equation is also written as
T=\sum_{r=0}^{R-1}A_{r,:}\times B_{r,:}\times C_{r,:}
, where
M_{r,:}
denotes the
r
-th row of
M
and
\times
denotes the outer product: for arbitrary tensors
X
and
Y
,
(X\times Y)_{i_{0},\dots i_{k-1},j_{0},\dots j_{\ell-1}}:=X_{i_{0},\dots i_{k-1}}Y_{j_{0},\dots j_{\ell-1}}
.

Tensor decomposition is at the heart of fast matrix multiplication, a problem that is the primary bottleneck of numerous linear algebra and graph combinatorics algorithms, such as matrix inversion and triangle detection. All asymptotically fast algorithms for matrix multiplication use a divide-and-conquer scheme, and finding an efficient scheme is equivalent to finding a decomposition of a certain tensor with low rank
[
2
]
.

While significant progress has been made on this problem over the decades, most of the asymptotically fastest algorithms discovered use indirect constructions and convergence arguments that result in enormously large divide-and-conquer schemes, making the constant factors for these algorithms far too big for them to be useful in practice.
For this reason, there has also been interest in finding explicit decompositions for the tensors of small divide-and-conquer schemes. With the introduction of AlphaTensor
[
3
]
and flip graphs
[
5
]
just over a year ago, several new low-rank decompositions have been discovered, especially over finite fields of characteristic 2
[
1
]
.

Decompositions using only integers are especially preferred, since they avoid having to use floating point arithmetic, and most decompositions of small divide-and-conquer schemes that have been found have happened to use only integers. This makes it useful to search for decompositions over finite fields (e.g., integers modulo 2), as doing so makes the search space finite and allows one to rule out large classes of integer solutions.

It is known that deciding whether a given tensor has a decomposition of rank
\leq R
is NP-hard over finite fields via a reduction from 3SAT
[
4
]
. However, since we only want low-rank decompositions, we are more interested in hardness results for small fixed
R
.

1.1
Results

Our main result in this report is a polynomial-time algorithm for 3D tensor decomposition with rank
\leq 4
.
For simplicity of asymptotic analysis, we focus on
n\times n\times n
tensors
T
, although our algorithm easily generalizes to non-cube tensors.

Theorem 1
.

For fixed
R\leq 4
, finding a rank-
R
decomposition over a finite field
\mathbb{F}
of an
n\times n\times n
tensor, or determining that it does not exist, can be done in
O(n^{3}+f(|\mathbb{F}|,R)n^{2})
time, for some function
f
.

However, when allowing certain cells of the target tensor to have arbitrary value (“wildcards”), we have a hardness result over the integers mod 2 via a reduction from Not-All-Equal 3SAT (NAE-3SAT):

Theorem 2
.

If some cells of a
p\times q\times s
tensor
T
are allowed to have arbitrary value, then determining if
T
has a rank-2 decomposition over
\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}
is NP-hard.

We also sketch out polynomial-time algorithms for rank-1 decomposition with wildcards over a 3D tensor and a matrix, over an arbitrary fixed finite field.

2.1
Preliminary results about matrix factorizations

Before describing our algorithm for Theorem
1
, we present several lemmas on
matrix
rank decompositions that we will use extensively.

The first lemma is a direct result of Gaussian elimination:

Lemma 1
.

Given any matrix
M\in\mathbb{F}^{m\times n}
,
\exists T\in\mathrm{GL}\left(m,\ \mathbb{F}\right)
s.t.
TM=\mathrm{rref}\left(M\right)
.

It follows that inverting a
n\times n
matrix can be done in
O(n^{3})
time.

The next lemma is the uniqueness of rref (up to all-zero rows):

Lemma 2
.

Two rref matrices with the same rowspan must be equal, up to the presence of all-zero rows.

Proof.

Consider an rref matrix
W=\left[\begin{array}[]{c}w_{0}\\
\hline\cr\vdots\\
\hline\cr w_{k-1}\\
\hline\cr O\end{array}\right]
, where each
w_{i}\neq\overrightarrow{0}
has its leading 1 at column
\ell_{i}
, with
\ell_{0}<\dots<\ell_{k-1}
. Consider a linear combination of the rows,
v:=\sum_{i}\alpha_{i}w_{i}
, where the
\alpha_{i}
are not all zero: then
v_{\ell_{i}}=\alpha_{i}
, since by definition of rref, every column of
W
that has a leading 1 has all of its other elements set to 0.
As a consequence, if we are instead given some
v
that is known to be a linear combination of
w_{i}
, then the list of coefficients
\alpha_{i}
of that linear combination are unique and can immediately be deduced by reading each
v_{\ell_{i}}
element.

Additionally, the leading nonzero term of
v
must be at some
\ell_{i}
-th column, as we can use the following casework: if
\alpha_{0}\neq 0
, the leading nonzero term of
v
is
\ell_{0}
, since each
w_{i}
is all zeros to the left of its leading 1: else if
\alpha_{1}\neq 0
, the leading nonzero term of
v
is
\ell_{1}
: etc.

Now suppose there is another rref matrix
W^{\prime}
s.t.
\mathrm{rowspan}\left(W\right)=\mathrm{rowspan}\left(W^{\prime}\right)
: then
W^{\prime}
cannot have a leading 1 at a column that is not one of the
\ell_{i}
-th, otherwise
\mathrm{rowspan}\left(W^{\prime}\right)
would contain a row-vector that is not in
\mathrm{rowspan}\left(W\right)
. Conversely,
W^{\prime}
must contain a leading 1 at every
\ell_{i}
-th column, since
w_{i}\in\mathrm{rowspan}\left(W\right)
for each
i
.
Thus, for each
0\leq i<k
,
W^{\prime}_{i,:}
has a leading 1 at column
\ell_{i}
.

Finally, since
w_{i}\in\mathrm{rowspan}\left(W^{\prime}\right)
and, by definition of rref,
w_{i}
contains a 1 at column
\ell_{i}
and a 0 at all other leading columns
\ell_{j}
for
j\neq i
, the only way for
w_{i}
to be a linear combination of the rows of
W^{\prime}
is if
w_{i}=W^{\prime}_{i,:}
. Thus,
W_{:k,:}=W^{\prime}_{:k,:}
.
∎

The previous two lemmas yield an important result about
full-rank factorizations
, i.e. rank-
r
factorizations of a rank-
r
matrix:

Lemma 3
.

Consider an
m\times n
matrix
M
over an arbitrary field with rank
r:=\mathrm{rank}\left(M\right)>0
.
Then there exists
C_{0}\in\mathbb{F}^{m\times r},\ F_{0}\in\mathbb{F}^{r\times n}
s.t.
C_{0}F_{0}=M
.
Furthermore, for any
C\in\mathbb{F}^{m\times r},\ F\in\mathbb{F}^{r\times n}
s.t.
CF=M
,
\exists X\in\mathrm{GL}\left(r,\ \mathbb{F}\right)
s.t.
C=C_{0}X
and
F=X^{-1}F_{0}
.

Proof.

By Lemma
1
,
\exists T\in\mathrm{GL}\left(m,\ \mathbb{F}\right)
s.t.
TM=\mathrm{rref}\left(M\right)
, so
T^{-1}\mathrm{rref}\left(M\right)=M
. Since all except the top
r
rows of
\mathrm{rref}\left(M\right)
are all-zeros,
(T^{-1})_{:,:r}\mathrm{rref}\left(M\right)_{:r,:}=M
, so we can set
(C_{0},F_{0})=((T^{-1})_{:,:r},\mathrm{rref}\left(M\right)_{:r,:})
.

Now suppose there are
C\in\mathbb{F}^{m\times r},\ F\in\mathbb{F}^{r\times n}
s.t.
CF=M
.
Since
\mathrm{rowspan}\left(M\right)=\mathrm{rowspan}\left(CF\right)\subseteq\mathrm{rowspan}\left(F\right)
and
\mathrm{rank}\left(F\right)\leq r=\mathrm{rank}\left(M\right)
, we have
\mathrm{rowspan}\left(M\right)=\mathrm{rowspan}\left(F\right)
and
\mathrm{rank}\left(F\right)=r
.
Since row reduction preserves rowspan,
\mathrm{rowspan}\left(\mathrm{rref}\left(M\right)\right)=\mathrm{rowspan}\left(\mathrm{rref}\left(F\right)\right)
, so by Lemma
2
,
\mathrm{rref}\left(F\right)=\mathrm{rref}\left(M\right)_{:r,:}=F_{0}
.

Using Lemma
1
,
\exists X\in\mathrm{GL}\left(r,\ \mathbb{F}\right)
s.t.
XF=F_{0}
. Solving for
F
and substituting it in the original equation
CF=M
yields
(CX^{-1})F_{0}=M
. Since
\mathrm{rank}\left(F_{0}\right)=r
, its rows are linearly independent, so there is at most one solution for
CX^{-1}
. We know
C_{0}
is one possible solution, so
CX^{-1}=C_{0}
. Thus,
C=C_{0}X
and
F=X^{-1}F_{0}
.

Since invertible matrices form a group, it follows that all pairs of full-rank factorizations of
M
are reachable from each other via the transformation
(C,F)\mapsto(CX,X^{-1}F)
.
∎

The upshot of this result is that there are exactly
|\mathrm{GL}\left(r,\ \mathbb{F}\right)|\in O(|\mathbb{F}|^{r^{2}})
many rank-
r
decompositions of a rank-
r
matrix,
no matter how large
m
and
n
are
. This is the core reason why our main algorithm will run in polynomial time w.r.t. the size of the tensor.
In contrast, the number of rank-
(r+1)
decompositions of a rank-
r
matrix
M
might be exponential w.r.t. the dimensions of
M
, e.g., when
M
is all-zeros.

Finally, we deduce a property of matrix factorizations that are not necessarily full-rank:

Lemma 4
.

For
U\in\mathbb{F}^{m\times r}
and
V\in\mathbb{F}^{r\times n}
, let
r^{\prime}:=\mathrm{rank}\left(UV\right)
. Then
\mathrm{rank}\left(U\right)\leq r^{\prime}
or
\mathrm{rank}\left(V\right)<r
.

Proof.

By Lemma
1
,
\exists

T\in\mathrm{GL}\left(m,\ \mathbb{F}\right)
s.t.
TUV=\mathrm{rref}\left(UV\right)
. In particular, all but the topmost
r^{\prime}
rows of
TUV
are all-zeros.

If
\mathrm{rank}\left(V\right)=r
, then the rows of
V
are linearly independent.
Because
(TUV)_{i,:}=(TU)_{i,:}V=\overrightarrow{0}\ \forall i\geq r^{\prime}
, this forces
(TU)_{i,:}=\overrightarrow{0}\ \forall i\geq r^{\prime}
, so all except the top
r^{\prime}
rows of
TU
are all-zeros. We then have
\mathrm{rank}\left(TU\right)\leq r^{\prime}
and thus
\mathrm{rank}\left(U\right)\leq r^{\prime}
due to
T
being invertible.

We have thus proven that
(\mathrm{rank}\left(V\right)=r)\Rightarrow(\mathrm{rank}\left(U\right)\leq r^{\prime})
, which is logically equivalent to the final statement of the lemma.
∎

Using the well-known properties
\mathrm{rank}\left(A\right)=\mathrm{rank}\left(A^{\intercal}\right)
and
\mathrm{rank}\left(AB\right)\leq\mathrm{min}\left(\mathrm{rank}\left(A\right),\mathrm{rank}\left(B\right)\right)
for arbitrary matrices
A
and
B
, we can combine Lemma
4
with its transpose to get a stronger condition:

Corollary 1
.

For
U\in\mathbb{F}^{m\times r}
and
V\in\mathbb{F}^{r\times n}
, let
r^{\prime}:=\mathrm{rank}\left(UV\right)
.
Then
\left(\mathrm{rank}\left(U\right)\leq r^{\prime}\ \lor\ \mathrm{rank}\left(V\right)<r\right)\ \land\ \left(\mathrm{rank}\left(U\right)<r\ \lor\ \mathrm{rank}\left(V\right)\leq r^{\prime}\right)\ \land\ \mathrm{rank}\left(U\right)\geq r^{\prime}\ \land\ \mathrm{rank}\left(V\right)\geq r^{\prime}
.

2.2
Algorithm

We now present our main algorithm to solve
T_{i,j,k}=\sum_{r=0}^{R-1}A_{r,i}B_{r,j}C_{r,k}\ \forall i,j,k
, for an arbitrary
n\times n\times n
tensor
T
, where
R\leq 4
is fixed.

Our main insight is to fix
A
and then solve for
B,C
in a clever manner. To ensure we do not enumerate too many
A
, we extract a basis on the slices
T_{i,:,:}
, denoted as
\{T^{\prime}_{j,:,:}\}_{j}
, which can be obtained by flattening the slices
T_{i,:,:}
and row-reducing the matrix
\widetilde{T}:=\begin{bmatrix}\vdots\\
\hline\cr\overrightarrow{T_{i,:,:}}\\
\hline\cr\vdots\end{bmatrix}_{i}
. Let
J:=\mathrm{rank}\left(\widetilde{T}\right)
: then
T^{\prime}
contains
J
slices, and by Lemma
1
,
\exists M\in\mathrm{GL}\left(n,\ \mathbb{F}\right)
s.t.
\sum_{0\leq i<n}M_{j,i}T_{i,:,:}=\begin{cases}T^{\prime}_{j,:,:}&j<J\\
O&\textrm{else}\end{cases}
, implying
T_{i,:,:}=\sum_{0\leq j<J}(M^{-1})_{i,j}T^{\prime}_{j,:,:}
.

These relations allow us to convert an arbitrary rank-
R
decomposition of
T
into a rank-
R
decomposition of
T^{\prime}
and vice versa:

\displaystyle T^{\prime}=\sum_{r=0}^{R-1}A^{\prime}_{r}\times B_{r,:}\times C_{r,:}

\displaystyle\Rightarrow T=\sum_{r=0}^{R-1}\left[\sum_{0\leq j<J}(M^{-1})_{i,j}A^{\prime}_{r,j}\right]_{0\leq i<n}\times B_{r,:}\times C_{r,:}

\displaystyle T=\sum_{r=0}^{R-1}A_{r}\times B_{r,:}\times C_{r,:}

\displaystyle\Rightarrow T^{\prime}=\sum_{r=0}^{R-1}\left[\sum_{0\leq i<n}M_{j,i}A_{r,i}\right]_{0\leq j<J}\times B_{r,:}\times C_{r,:}

Thus, our original problem is equivalent to solving
T^{\prime}=\sum_{r}A^{\prime}_{r,:}\times B_{r,:}\times C_{r,:}
.

The upshot of constructing
T^{\prime}
is that if the original problem
T=\sum_{r}A_{r}\times B_{r,:}\times C_{r,:}
has a solution, then every
T_{i}
is a linear combination of
\left\{B_{r,:}\times C_{r,:}\right\}_{0\leq r<R}
, so all
T_{i,:,:}
collectively are in a linear subspace (of
\mathbb{F}^{n\times n}
) of rank at most
R
. This forces
T^{\prime}
to have at most
R
many slices; otherwise, the original problem has no solution and we can immediately terminate. Thus, there are at most
|\mathbb{F}|^{R^{2}}
possible
A^{\prime}
to consider, which is constant w.r.t.
n
.

If
J=0
, i.e.
T^{\prime}
is empty, then
T
must be all-zeros, so it trivially has a rank-
R
decomposition (e.g., with all elements in
A,B,C
set to 0).

Otherwise, we proceed by treating each
B_{r,:}\times C_{r,:}
as an unknown matrix
M_{r}
with the condition that
\mathrm{rank}\left(M_{r}\right)\leq 1

Now we split casework over the matrix

3.1
Rank 1 with wildcards

For rank-

\mathbb{F}

First, we rewrite the problem as follows: given an arbitrary subset
S\subseteq\left\{0,\dots n-1\right\}^{3}
of tensor indices and a set of elements

a_{i}b_{j}c_{k}=T_{i,j,k}\ \forall(i,j,k)\in S
.

When
\mathbb{F}=\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}
,
abc=1
implies
(a,b,c)=(1,1,1)
, so a solution exists if and only if setting

a_{i}=\lor_{j,k:(i,j,k)\in S}T_{i,j,k},\ b_{j}=\lor_{i,k:(i,j,k)\in S}T_{i,j,k},\ c_{k}=\lor_{i,j:(i,j,k)\in S}T_{i,j,k}

yields a solution; this is because this construction produces the minimal set of elements in
a\times b\times c
that are forced to be 1s by the values
T_{i,j,k}
, and this set covers all
(i,j,k)\in S
that have
T_{i,j,k}=1
.
Thus for
\mathbb{F}=\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}
there is an algorithm for rank-1 wildcard decomposition that runs in
O(n^{3})

For an arbitrary fixed finite field
\mathbb{F}

Let
S^{\prime}:=\left\{(i,j,k)\in S:T_{i,j,k}\neq 0\right\}

T_{i,j,k}\neq 0\ \forall(i,j,k)\in(I\times J\times K)\cap S

Otherwise, we observe that all the equations

Indeed, it is well-known that the multiplicative group of any finite field is cyclic, so there exists some

\mathbb{F}\setminus\left\{0\right\}

\mathbb{Z}/(|\mathbb{F}|-1)\mathbb{Z}

|S^{\prime}|\leq n^{3}
.

The main bottleneck in this algorithm is finding a primitive element of
\mathbb{F}
, for which an efficient algorithm is not known

.
On the other hand, for
\mathbb{F}=\mathbb{F}_{p^{k}}
, the total input size of a problem instance would be linear w.r.t.
\log(p)
and
k

\mathbb{F}_{p^{k}}

1,\alpha,\dots,\alpha^{k-1}

, so every element can be specified with a

3.2
Rank-1 with wildcards for matrices

One may wonder whether
matrix
rank factorization with wildcards is NP-hard. The issue is that matrix factorizations have an immense degree of freedom, as
CF=M
implies
(CX^{-1})(XF)=M
, so constructing gadgets seems very difficult even for rank
R=2
.

Additionally, for rank
R=1
, we sketch an algorithm that runs in
O(n^{3})
time even when
\mathbb{F}
is not fixed (assuming each operation between elements of
\mathbb{F}
runs in
O(1)
time), in contrast to the analogous problem for 3D tensors.
Again we formulate the problem as follows: given a subset
S\subseteq\left\{0,\dots n-1\right\}^{2}
and set of elements
T_{i,j}\in\mathbb{F}\ \forall(i,j)\in S
, find variables
a_{0,\dots n-1},b_{0,\dots n-1}
s.t.
a_{i}b_{j}=T_{i,j}
.

Like before, define
S^{\prime}:=\left\{(i,j)\in S:T_{i,j}\neq 0\right\}
,
I:=\left\{i:(i,j)\in S^{\prime}\right\}
,
J:=\left\{j:(i,j)\in S^{\prime}\right\}
. Then
a_{i}\neq 0\ \forall i\in I
and
b_{j}\neq 0\ \forall j\in J
, so we check whether
T_{i,j}\neq 0\ \forall(i,j)\in(I\times J)\cap S
; if the check fails then there is no solution.

Otherwise, we notice that since every equation
a_{i}b_{j}=T_{i,j}
constrains the product of two variables, knowing one variable immediately forces the value of the other variable.
This motivates us to construct a graph
G
with vertex set
S^{\prime}
where there is an edge between every pair of vertices
(i,j),(i^{\prime},j^{\prime})
s.t.
i=i^{\prime}
or
j=j^{\prime}
.

Choose an arbitrary
(i_{0},j_{0})\in S^{\prime}
: WLOG we can set
a_{i_{0}}=1,b_{j_{0}}=T_{i_{0},j_{0}}
because any solution
(a,b)
to the original problem can be scaled to
(\alpha a,\frac{1}{\alpha}b)
for any
\alpha\neq 0
. Then do a breadth-first search over
S^{\prime}
starting from
(i_{0},j_{0})
: when we visit each new vertex
(i,j)\in S
, we will have already determined
a_{i}
and
b_{j}
, so for each neighbor of the form
(i^{\prime},j)
we set
a_{i^{\prime}}=T_{i^{\prime},j}/b_{j}
, and for each neighbor of the form
(i,j^{\prime})
we set
b_{j^{\prime}}=T_{i,j^{\prime}}/a_{i}
. Each neighbor that was not already visited is added to a queue of vertices to visit.
Note that because every
T_{i,j}
we deal with is nonzero, we will never encounter division by 0 in this procedure.

If at any point we update a variable
a_{i}
or
b_{j}
that already has a assigned value and set it to a different value, then we have encountered an inconsistency and there is no solution to the original problem. Otherwise, at the end of this procedure we will have searched through the entire connected component of
G
that contains
(i_{0},j_{0})
, so we do another breadth-first search starting at an arbitrary unvisited vertex in
G
, and repeat until all vertices in
G
have been visited. If no inconsistencies were ever encountered, we can set all variables
a_{i}
and
b_{j}
that were not already assigned a value to 0, and we have a solution.

Since breadth-first search over a graph with
V
vertices and
E
edges takes
O(V+E)
time, and
G
has
O(n^{2})
vertices and
O(n^{3})
edges, our algorithm runs in
O(n^{3})
time.

The key for correctness is that if two vertices
(i,j),(i^{\prime},j^{\prime})
are in different connect components, then
(i^{\prime},j^{\prime})
is not adjacent to any vertex
(x,y)
in the connected component containing
(i,j)
, so
i^{\prime}\neq x
and
j^{\prime}\neq y
. This means that
a_{i^{\prime}}
and
b_{j^{\prime}}
are not directly or indirectly involved in any equations with elements
T_{x,y}
for each
(x,y)
in the connected component of
(i,j)
. Thus, after we finish searching over each connected component, all remaining unassigned variables
a_{i}
and
b_{j}
are completely independent of all previously assigned variables, so as we search through the next connected component we will never encounter inconsistencies with variables assigned during a previous connected component. This independence between connected components also allows us to assume WLOG that
a_{i_{0}}=1
and
b_{j_{0}}=T_{i_{0},j_{0}}
at each individual connected component.

If we tried generalizing this algorithm to 3D tensors, we would encounter a problem: two vertices would have to match 2 out of 3 coordinates in order to be adjacent to each other, because each equation
a_{i}b_{j}c_{k}=T_{i,j,k}
constrains the product of three variables instead of two, so two variables have to be known beforehand to force the value of the third one.
But then if
(i,j,k)
and
(i^{\prime},j^{\prime},k^{\prime})
occupy different components, it is still possible for
(i^{\prime},j^{\prime},k^{\prime})
to share 1 out of 3 coordinates with a vertex contained in the connected component containing
(i,j,k)
, so different connected components can still share common variables and thus interact with each other in a nontrivial manner.

4
Future directions

We conclude with some open questions:

•

For what
R\geq 5
can rank-
R
tensor decomposition (without wildcards) be computed in polynomial time over a fixed finite field?

•

For
R\leq 4
, how low can the constant factor of rank-
R
decomposition be w.r.t. to the size of the finite field and w.r.t.
R
?

•

How asymptotically low can the number of wildcards in a tensor be while keeping rank-2 wildcard decomposition NP-hard?

•

What is the parameterized complexity of rank-1 and rank-2 wildcard decomposition w.r.t. the number of wildcard elements in the target tensor?

•

Can we have efficient tensor decomposition over number systems beyond finite fields, such as the integers?
