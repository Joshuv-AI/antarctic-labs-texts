1.
Introduction

Let
T:X\to X
be a conservative ergodic measure-preserving transformation of a
\sigma
-finite measure space
(X,{\mathcal{B}},\mu)
and consider an observable
f
, that is a measurable function
f:X\to\mathbb{R}
. The almost sure asymptotic behaviour of the
Birkhoff sums of
f

S_{N}f(x):=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\,(f\circ T^{j-1})(x)

is the main result of the famous Birkhoff’s Ergodic Theorem. It states that if
\mu
is a probability measure and
f\in L^{1}(X,\mu)
, then
S_{N}f(x)
is asymptotic to
N\int_{X}f\,d\mu
as
N\to\infty
for
\mu
-a.e.
x\in X
. On the other hand, if
f\not\in L^{1}(X,\mu)
the almost sure asymptotic behaviour of
S_{N}f
is not described by any sequence
(b(N))
. In particular, letting
f:X\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
, one can apply
[
A97
, Cor. 2.3.4]
to show that given a sequence
(b(N))
of positive numbers with
b(N)/N\to\infty
, for
\mu
-almost every (a.e.)
x\in X
either
\limsup_{N}S_{N}f(x)/b(N)=\infty
or
\liminf_{N}S_{N}f(x)/b(N)=0
.

The situation is different if the measure
\mu
is
\sigma
-finite but infinite, that is
\mu(X)=\infty
. In this case if
f\in L^{1}(X,\mu)
then
S_{N}f(x)=o(N)
for
\mu
-a.e.
x\in X
, but again the almost sure asymptotic behaviour of
S_{N}f
is not described by any sequence
(b(N))
slower than
N
. It is the content of
[
A97
, Thm. 2.4.2]
that given a sequence
(b(N))
of positive numbers, even with
b(N)/N\to 0
, for
\mu
-a.e.
x\in X
either
\liminf_{N}S_{N}f(x)/b(N)=0
for all
f\in L^{1}(X,\mu)
, or there exists a sequence
(N_{k})
such that
\lim_{k}S_{N_{k}}f(x)/b(N_{k})=\infty
for all
f\in L^{1}(X,\mu)
. For infinite measure-preserving dynamical systems, an information about the almost sure asymptotic behaviour of the Birkhoff sums of summable observables is given in Hopf’s Ratio Ergodic Theorem (see
[
A97
, Thm 2.2.5]
). It is shown that for
f,g\in L^{1}(X,\mu)
with
g\geq 0
and
\int_{X}g\,d\mu>0
, the ratio
S_{N}f(x)/S_{N}g(x)
converges to
\int_{X}f\,d\mu/\int_{X}g\,d\mu
as
N\to\infty
for
\mu
-a.e.
x\in X
. That is, the almost sure asymptotic behaviour of
S_{N}f
is the same for all
f\in L^{1}(X,\mu)
, and its variability which leads to the negative results we have recalled above, depends on the initial condition
x\in X
. As far as we know, the almost sure asymptotic behaviour of Birkhoff sums has not been studied for non-summable observables in the context of infinite ergodic theory, with the sole exception of
[
LM18
]
.

In this paper we restrict our attention to the case that
\mu
is infinite and study the almost sure asymptotic behaviour of Birkhoff sums for both summable and non-summable observables with respect to sequences. We consider dynamical systems with strong mixing assumptions, namely
\psi
-mixing
(see Definition
2.1
), for the induced map on a finite measure subset
E\subset X
(see Section
2
for the detailed assumptions), and prove two main results. First we prove that for
f\in L^{1}(X,\mu)
there exist sequences of positive real numbers
(\alpha(N))
and
(m(N,E,x))
, the second sequence depending additionally on
x
, such that
S_{N+m(N,E,x)}f(x)
is asymptotic to
\alpha(N)\int_{X}f\,d\mu
as
N\to\infty
for
\mu
-a.e.
x\in X
. That is to obtain the same asymptotic behaviour for
\mu
-a.e.
x\in X
we have to change the number of terms to consider in the Birkhoff sums (see Theorem
2.3
). Then we prove that for a non-summable observable
f:X\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
, under suitable assumptions on its induced version
f^{E}
(see (
2.12
)) it is possible to find a sequence
(G(N))
which depends on
f
such that
S_{N}f(x)\sim G(N)
as
N\to\infty
for
\mu
-a.e.
x\in X
(see Theorem
2.7
). The main difference is that in this non-summable case the properties of
f
play an important role.

The proofs rely on the method of
trimmed sums
(see (
2.8
)), that is sums from which a number of largest entries is deleted. The method to trim sums of independent random variables to prove (pointwise and distributional) limit theorems which fail to hold for the untrimmed sum is long established and most results were developed in the 80th and 90th of the last century. One generally differentiates between different strengths of trimming, according to the number
r
of largest entries deleted. In the light trimming case the number of deleted entries
r
is constant for all
n
, while in the intermediate trimming case the number of deleted entries
r(n)
depends on
n
and tends to infinity, but with
r(n)=o(n)
.

The first result using trimming in the dynamical systems context was given by Diamond and Vaaler
[
DV86
]
providing a strong law of large numbers for the continued fraction digits under the use of light trimming. More precisely, if
(a_{n}(x))
are the coefficients of the continued fraction expansion of a point
x\in[0,1]
, it is proved in
[
DV86
]
that
\lim_{n\to\infty}(\sum_{k=1}^{n}a_{k}(x)-\max_{1\leq k\leq n}a_{k}(x))/(n\log n)=\log 2
holds for Lebesgue-a.e.
x\in[0,1]
. Hence in this case it is enough to trim the sum of the coefficients
(a_{n}(x))
by deleting only the largest entry, so
r=1
. These results were generalised by Aaronson and Nakada
[
AN03
]
giving strong laws of large numbers under light trimming for sufficiently fast
\psi
-mixing random variables, thus giving the analog to some statement for independent random variables from
[
KM92
]
. Haynes further quantified in
[
H14
]
the results by Aaronson and Nakada by giving precise error terms.

Kesseböhmer and the second author of this paper proved strong laws of large numbers under intermediate trimming using a spectral gap property of the transfer operator, see
[
KS19b
]
and
[
KS20a
]
for an application of these results to subshifts of finite type and
[
HM87
,
H93
,
KS19a
]
for these and further reaching results in the independent case. See further
[
KS20b
]
for a convergence in mean result concerning the same intermediately trimmed sums.

The paper is organised as follows. In Section
2
we describe the setting we consider and state the main results, Theorems
2.3
and
2.7
, whose proofs are collected in Section
4
. In Section
3
we describe two classes of dynamical systems to which our results are applicable, and discuss the assumptions on the observables for Theorem
2.7
. We also study in more detail our results for the Farey map, a well-known dynamical system on the interval, also in relation to the results in
[
LM18
]
which are shown to be a particular case of ours for this system. Finally, two appendices complete the paper. In particular Appendix
B
contains definitions and results on slowly varying functions, an important class of functions used in the statement of Theorem
2.7
.

We conclude with some comments and a discussion on possible directions of future research. As we have stated above, our main results hold for dynamical systems for which there exists a finite measure set
E
on which the induced map satisfies a
\psi
-mixing condition. It would be interesting to study if this
\psi
-mixing condition could be relaxed. This seems to be highly unlikely in general. Aaronson and Nakada already gave in
[
AN03
]
an example of a mixing though not
\psi
-mixing system which does not fulfil a strong law of large numbers after trimming, even though a sufficiently fast
\psi
-mixing system with the same distribution functions would fulfil a trimmed strong law. Haynes gave another simple example with even stronger mixing properties which does not fulfil a lightly trimmed strong law, see
[
H14
, Thm. 4]
. However, this particular system still fulfils an intermediately trimmed strong law, see
[
S18
]
. Thus, there might be possibilities to generalise Theorems
2.3
and
2.7
in this way. In particular, in this paper we have considered assumptions on the system
(X,T,\mu)
for which light trimming is enough to get the results, actually it has been enough to trim the sums by deleting only the largest entry. As shown in Section
3.1
for maps of the interval with indifferent fixed points, this is related with the order with which the derivative of the map converges to 1 in the indifferent fixed points. Using light trimming we have been able to consider only the so-called “barely infinite” situation. It is possible that we can obtain similar results for systems with a different “order of infinity” by using intermediate trimming.

2.
The setting and main results

Let
T:X\to X
be a conservative ergodic measure-preserving transformation of the measure space
(X,{\mathcal{B}},\mu)
with
\mu
a
\sigma
-finite measure with
\mu(X)=\infty
. Let
E\in{\mathcal{B}}
with
\mu(E)=1
, and denote by
\varphi_{{}_{E}}
the
first return time

\varphi_{{}_{E}}:E\to\mathbb{N}\,,\qquad\varphi_{{}_{E}}(x):=\min\left\{k\geq 1\,:\,T^{k}(x)\in E\right\}.

The first return time is finite for
\mu
-a.e.
x\in E
, so we define
T_{{}_{E}}(x):=T^{\varphi_{{}_{E}}(x)}(x)
to be the
induced map

T_{{}_{E}}:E\to E
, which is an ergodic measure-preserving transformation of the probability space
(E,{\mathcal{B}}|_{E},\mu)
. The first return time generates two families of sets, the level sets

(2.1)

A_{n}:=\left\{x\in E\,:\varphi_{{}_{E}}(x)=n\right\}

which are a measurable partition of
E
, and the super-level sets

(2.2)

A_{>n}:=\left\{x\in E\,:\,\varphi_{{}_{E}}(x)>n\right\}=\bigsqcup_{k>n}\,A_{k}.

We also use the notation
A_{\geq n}=A_{n}\cup A_{>n}
. Applying Kac’s Theorem one has

(2.3)

\sum_{k\geq 1}\,k\,\mu(A_{k})=\sum_{n\geq 0}\,\mu(A_{>n})=\mu(X)=\infty.

For this reason the vanishing order of the sequence
(\mu(A_{>n}))
is called the
order of infinity
of the system
(X,T,\mu)
. It is known that this order is independent of the finite measure subset
E
we have fixed.

A useful notion is the
longest excursion out of
E
beginning in the first
N
-steps
defined for
\mu
-a.e.
x\in X
as

(2.4)

m(N,E,x):=1+\max\left\{k\geq 1\,:\,\exists\,\ell\in\{1,\dots,N+1\}\text{ s.t. }T^{\ell+j}(x)\not\in E,\,\forall\,j=0,\dots,k-1\right\}.

If we denote by
R_{{}_{E,N}}(x)
the
number of visits to
E
up to time
N
along the orbit of a point
x
, that is

(2.5)

R_{{}_{E,N}}(x):=\sum_{k=1}^{N+1}\,(\mathbbm{1}_{{}_{E}}\circ T^{k-1})(x)

it is immediate to verify that for
\mu
-a.e.
x\in E

(2.6)

m(N,E,x)=\max\left\{(\varphi_{{}_{E}}\circ T_{{}_{E}}^{k-1})(x)\,:\,k=1,\dots,R_{{}_{E,N}}(x)\right\}.

A notion related to
m(N,E,x)
is that of the
longest excursion out of
E
seen up to time
N
,
defined for
\mu
-a.e.
x\in E
by

(2.7)

w(N,E,x):=1+\max\left\{k\geq 1\,:\,\exists\,\ell\in\{1,\dots,N-k\}\text{ s.t. }T^{\ell+j}(x)\not\in E,\,\forall\,j=0,\dots,k-1\right\}.

Notice that
w(N,E,x)
can correspond to an excursion which does not return to
E
up to time
N
.

In this paper we are interested in studying the pointwise asymptotic behaviour of the Birkhoff sums

S_{N}f(x):=\sum_{n=1}^{N}\,(f\circ T^{n-1})(x)

for summable and non-summable observables
f:X\to\mathbb{R}
. For
f\in L^{1}(X,\mu)
it is a straightforward consequence of Hopf’s Ratio Ergodic Theorem
[
A97
, Thm 2.2.5]
that
\mu
-a.s.
S_{N}f(x)=o(N)
. Moreover, the exact asymptotic pointwise behaviour of
S_{N}f
cannot be recovered for all
f\in L^{1}(X,\mu)
by changing the normalising sequence due to Aaronson’s Ergodic Theorem
[
A97
, Thm 2.42]
, which basically states that for any sequence of positive real numbers the growth rate of the Birkhoff sums of a non-negative summable observable will almost surely be either over- or under-estimated infinitely often. In this paper we obtain some results for the pointwise asymptotic behaviour of
S_{N}f
under strong mixing assumptions for the induced system
(E,T_{{}_{E}},\mu)
.

We now state the definitions and the results we need in the general case of random variables. We refer to
[
B05
]
for more definitions.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
(Y_{n})
be a sequence of random variables on a probability space
(Y,\mathbb{P})
, and let
{\mathcal{F}}_{h}^{k}
, for
0\leq h<k\leq\infty
, be the
\sigma
-field generated by
(Y_{n})_{h\leq n\leq k}
. The sequence
(Y_{n})
is
\psi
-mixing
if

\psi(n):=\sup\left\{\Big|\frac{\mathbb{P}(B\cap C)}{\mathbb{P}(B)\mathbb{P}(C)}-1\Big|\,:\,B\in{\mathcal{F}}_{0}^{j},\,C\in{\mathcal{F}}_{j+n}^{\infty},\,\mathbb{P}(B)>0,\,\mathbb{P}(C)>0,\,j\in\mathbb{N}\right\}

satisfies
\psi(n)\to 0
as
n\to\infty
.

We now recall the notion of
lightly trimmed sums
. Given a sequence
(Y_{n})
of random variables on a probability space
(Y,\mathbb{P})
and a point
y\in Y
, for each
N\in\mathbb{N}
we choose a permutation
\pi
of
\{1,2,\dots,N\}
such that
Y_{\pi(1)}(y)\geq Y_{\pi(2)}(y)\geq\dots\geq Y_{\pi(N)}(y)
. For a given
r\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, the lightly trimmed sum of the
(Y_{n})
is defined by

(2.8)

S_{N}^{r}(y):=\sum_{n=r+1}^{N}\,Y_{\pi(n)}(y)

that is the sum of the first
N
random variables trimmed by the largest
r
entries.

The next result is adapted from
[
AN03
]
as shown in Appendix
A
.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
(Y_{n})
be a sequence of non-negative, identically distributed random variables on a probability space
(Y,\mathbb{P})
which is
\psi
-mixing with coefficient
\psi(n)
fulfilling
\sum_{n\geq 1}\psi(n)/n<\infty
. Let
F
be the distribution function of the
(Y_{n})
, that is
F(y)=\mathbb{P}(Y_{1}\leq y)
, and let for some
y_{0}>0

(2.9)

\displaystyle W\coloneqq\min\left\{r\in\mathbb{N}\colon\int_{y_{0}}^{\infty}\left(\frac{y\left(1-F(y)\right)}{\int_{0}^{y}\left(1-F(t)\right)\mathrm{d}t}\right)^{r+1}\frac{1}{y}\,\mathrm{d}y<\infty\right\},

where we set the
\min
as
\infty
if such an
r
does not exist.
Then there exists a sequence
(d(N))
such that

\displaystyle\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{S_{N}^{W}(y)}{d(N)}=1,\quad\text{$\mathbb{P}$-a.s.}

If we set
a(y):=y/\int_{0}^{y}(1-F(t))\mathrm{d}t
, then
d(n)
can be set as the inverse function of
a(n)
.

Furthermore, if we denote by
M^{r}_{N}(y)
the
r
-th maximum in
\{Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{N}\}
then

(2.10)

\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{M^{r}_{N}(y)}{d(N)}=0,\quad\text{$\mathbb{P}$-a.s.}

for all
r>W
.

We now state our main results. We first consider the Birkhoff sums of an observable
f\in L^{1}(X,\mu)
. We prove that
S_{N}f
converges almost surely to the spatial mean of the observable if we consider the average with respect to a sequence
\alpha(n)
growing slower than
n
and consider more iterates for the observable. The dependence on the point
x
is given by the number
m(N,E,x)
, as defined in (
2.4
), of iterates for the observable that we have to add. We remark that the sequence
\alpha(n)
coincides with that found in the Weak Law of Large Numbers for dynamical system which are pointwise dual ergodic and have wandering rate given by a slowly varying sequence (see
[
A97
, Chapter 3]
).

Theorem 2.3
.

Let
T:X\to X
be a conservative ergodic measure-preserving transformation of the measure space
(X,{\mathcal{B}},\mu)
with
\mu
a
\sigma
-finite measure with
\mu(X)=\infty
, and let
E\in{\mathcal{B}}
with
\mu(E)=1
. Let’s assume that the first return time to
E
satisfies:

(i)

the sequence of random variables
(\varphi_{{}_{E}}\circ T_{{}_{E}}^{n-1})
with
n\geq 1
defined on
(E,\mu)
is
\psi
-mixing with coefficient
\psi(n)
fulfilling
\sum_{n\geq 1}\psi(n)/n<\infty
;

(ii)

the super-level sets of
\varphi_{{}_{E}}
satisfy

\sum_{n\geq 1}\frac{n\,(\mu(A_{>n}))^{2}}{(\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\mu(A_{>j}))^{2}}<\infty.

Then for all
f\in L^{1}(X,\mu)
and for
\mu
-a.e.
x\in X

\lim_{N\to\infty}\,\frac{1}{\alpha(N)}\,\sum_{k=1}^{N+m(N,E,x)}\,(f\circ T^{k-1})(x)=\int_{X}\,f\,\mathrm{d}\mu

where

(2.11)

\alpha(n):=\frac{n}{\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\,\mu(A_{>j})}\,.

In the following we also give a slightly different formulation of Theorem
2.3
which uses
w(N,E,x)
. The main difference is that the number of iterates for the observable is fixed and the dependence on
x
is found in the renormalising sequence. Thus, this second formulation is more similar to Hopf’s Ratio Ergodic Theorem in the spirit.

Theorem 2.4
.

Under the same assumptions as in Theorem
2.3
, for all
f\in L^{1}(X,\mu)
and for
\mu
-a.e.
x\in X
it holds

\lim_{N\to\infty}\,\frac{1}{\alpha(N-w(N,E,x))}\,\sum_{k=1}^{N}\,(f\circ T^{k-1})(x)=\int_{X}\,f\,\mathrm{d}\mu

where
w(N,E,x)
is defined in (
2.7
) and
\alpha(n)
is given in (
2.11
).

Remark 2.5
.

The asymptotic behaviour of the sequence
(w(N,E,x))
has been considered in
[
GHPZ
]
for some intermittent maps of the interval with infinite measure. Following the same argument and using the relations between
m(N,E,x)
and
w(N,E,x)
one can prove that under the conditions of the two previous theorems

\lim_{N\to\infty}\,\frac{\log m(N,E,x)}{\log N}=\lim_{N\to\infty}\,\frac{\log w(N,E,x)}{\log N}=1

for
\mu
-a.e.
x\in X
.

We have shown that in the case of summable observables the assumptions only depend on the set
E
and on its first return time. This is in accordance with what one could expect with Hopf’s Ratio Ergodic Theorem in mind. We now study the Birkhoff sums of non-negative observables
f\not\in L^{1}(X,\mu)
. In this case we need assumptions also on the observable itself.

Given
T:X\to X
and
E\in{\mathcal{B}}
with
\mu(E)=1
as above, for a measurable function
f:X\to\mathbb{R}
, a standard approach is to introduce the induced observable
f^{E}
defined on the full measure set of points
x\in E
with finite first return time by

(2.12)

f^{E}(x):=\sum_{k=1}^{\varphi_{{}_{E}}(x)}\,(f\circ T^{k-1})(x)\,.

The induced observable simply gives the contribution to the Birkhoff sums
S_{N}f
of the excursions out of
E
for the orbit of a point
x
. For
\mu
-a.e.
x\in E
let’s define the time
\tau_{{}_{E,x}}(N)
of the
N
-th return to
E
, which corresponds to the Birkhoff sum of
\varphi_{{}_{E}}
for the system
(E,T_{{}_{E}})
. We have

(2.13)

\tau_{{}_{E,x}}(N):=\sum_{k=1}^{N}\,(\varphi_{{}_{E}}\circ T_{{}_{E}}^{k-1})(x)\,.

Recalling now the notion
R_{{}_{E,N}}(x)
of the number of visits to
E
up to time
N
along the orbit of
x
defined in (
2.5
), the last visit to
E
for
x
up to time
N
is
T_{{}_{E}}^{R_{{}_{E,N}}(x)-1}(x)
and the time of this last visit is then given by
\tau_{{}_{E,x}}(R_{{}_{E,N}}(x)-1)\leq N
. Then the following relation follows for
\mu
-a.e.
x\in E

(2.14)

S_{N}f(x)=\sum_{n=1}^{R_{{}_{E,N}}(x)-1}\,(f^{E}\circ T^{n-1}_{{}_{E}})(x)+\sum_{k=\tau_{{}_{E,x}}(R_{{}_{E,N}}(x)-1)+1}^{N}\,(f\circ T^{k-1})(x)

with the standard convention that a summation vanishes if the upper index is smaller than the lower index. Moreover, using that
f\geq 0
and
\tau_{{}_{E,x}}(R_{{}_{E,N}}(x))>N
, being
\tau_{{}_{E,x}}(R_{{}_{E,N}}(x))
the time of the first visit to
E
after time
N
, we immediately obtain

\sum_{n=1}^{R_{{}_{E,N}}(x)-1}\,(f^{E}\circ T^{n-1}_{{}_{E}})(x)\leq S_{N}f(x)\leq\sum_{n=1}^{R_{{}_{E,N}}(x)}\,(f^{E}\circ T^{n-1}_{{}_{E}})(x)

for
\mu
-a.e.
x\in E
. Thus the pointwise convergence of the Birkhoff sums of
f
could in principle be obtained by the behaviour of the Birkhoff sums of
f^{E}
for the induced system
(E,T_{{}_{E}},\mu)
. However
f\not\in L^{1}(X,\mu)
implies
f^{E}\not\in L^{1}(E,\mu)
(see for examples
[
Z09
, Lemma 2]
), hence we cannot use Birkhoff’s Theorem to obtain the pointwise convergence for the Birkhoff sums of
f^{E}
. A solution is to apply Lemma

We recall that

Lemma 2.6

.

3.
Examples and applications

We first describe two classes of dynamical systems to which our results apply. Then we discuss the assumptions and the result of Theorem

3.1.
Maps of the interval with an indifferent fixed point

This class of maps is considered the easiest example of non-uniformly hyperbolic systems, and thus the easiest model of a system with “anomalous” dynamical phenomena. Here we use the general approach introduced in
[
Z00
]
.

Let
T:[0,1]\to[0,1]
be a piecewise monotonic
C^{2}
map with respect to a partition
{\mathcal{P}}
of open subintervals, that is
{\mathcal{P}}
is a finite or countable family of open subintervals
(P_{j})_{j\in J}
such that
m([0,1]\setminus\cup_{j}P_{j})=0
, being
m
the Lebesgue measure, and
T|_{P_{j}}
is
C^{2}
and strictly monotonic for all
j\in J
.

Definition 3.1
.

A map
T:[0,1]\to[0,1]
which is piecewise monotonic and
C^{2}
with respect to a partition
{\mathcal{P}}=(P_{j})_{j\in J}
of open subintervals defines a
basic AFN-system
if it is conservative and ergodic with respect to
m
and if

(A)

T^{\prime\prime}/(T^{\prime})^{2}
is bounded on
\cup_{j}P_{j}
;

(F)

T{\mathcal{P}}=\{T(P_{j})\,:\,j\in J\}
is a finite collection;

(N)

there exists a non-empty finite collection
\tilde{\mathcal{P}}\subset{\mathcal{P}}
such that each
P_{j}\in\tilde{\mathcal{P}}
has an
indifferent fixed point

x_{j}
, that is

\lim_{x\to x_{j},\,x\in P_{j}}\,T(x)=x_{j}\quad\text{and}\quad T^{\prime}(x_{j}):=\lim_{x\to x_{j},\,x\in P_{j}}\,T^{\prime}(x)=1\,,

which is a
one-sided regular source
, that is
T^{\prime}
decreases on
P_{j}\cap(0,x_{j})
and increases on
P_{j}\cap(x_{j},1)
. Moreover
T
is uniformly expanding away from the indifferent fixed points, that is for all
\epsilon>0
there exists
\rho_{\epsilon}>1
such that

|T^{\prime}(x)|\geq\rho_{\epsilon}\,,\quad\forall\,x\in[0,1]\setminus\bigcup_{P_{j}\in\tilde{\mathcal{P}}}\,P_{j}\cap(x_{j}-\epsilon,x_{j}+\epsilon)\,.

By
[
Z00
, Thm A]
a basic AFN-system admits an absolutely continuous invariant measure
\mu
with a lower semicontinuous density
h(x)
of the form

h(x)=c(x)\,g(x)\,,\quad\text{with}\quad g(x)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\frac{x-x_{j}}{x-(T|_{P_{j}})^{-1}(x)}\,,&\text{for }\,x\in P_{j}\in\tilde{\mathcal{P}}\\[5.69046pt]
1\,,&\text{for }\,x\in P_{j}\not\in\tilde{\mathcal{P}}\end{array}\right.

and
c(x)
satisfying
0<C^{-1}\leq c(x)\leq C
for some constant
C>0
.

Examples of basic AFN-systems are the Pomeau-Manneville maps, the Liverani-Saussol-Vaienti maps and the Farey map. They all share the following structure. Let
{\mathcal{T}}
be the family of maps of
[0,1]
for which there is a partition
{\mathcal{P}}=\{P_{0},P_{1}\}
of
[0,1]
into two open intervals with respect to which they are piecewise monotonic and
C^{2}
, and
T(P_{0})=T(P_{1})=(0,1)
. Moreover the collection
\tilde{\mathcal{P}}
contains only the set
P_{0}=(0,\bar{x})
, and the maps have an indifferent fixed point at
x_{0}=0
at which they satisfy

(3.1)

T(x)=x+C\,x^{1+p}+o(x^{1+p})\,,\quad\text{for $x\in P_{0}$ and $x\to 0^{+}$}

with
p\geq 1
for some constant
C>0
, and are uniformly expanding away from
x_{0}
.

By
[
Z00
, Lemma 8]
there is a set
E\subset[0,1]
with
\mu(E)<\infty
such that the induced map
T_{{}_{E}}
is piecewise monotonic and
C^{2}
with respect to a partition
{\mathcal{P}}_{E}
, satisfies conditions (A) and (F), and is uniformly expanding (U). It is then called an AFU-system. Finally
[
AN05
, Thm 1]
gives that if
T_{{}_{E}}
is weakly mixing and
\inf_{n\geq 1,Q\in{\mathcal{P}}^{n}_{E}}m(T_{{}_{E}}^{n}(Q))>0
, then the sequence of random variables
(\varphi_{{}_{E}}\circ T_{{}_{E}}^{n-1})
with
n\geq 1
defined on
(E,\mu)
is
\psi
-mixing with coefficient
\psi(n)
fulfilling
\psi(n)\leq K\,\theta^{n}
for some constants
K>0
and
\theta\in(0,1)
. Thus assumption (i) of Theorems
2.3
and
2.7
is satisfied.

For the maps in the family
{\mathcal{T}}
one can choose
E=P_{1}
to obtain an exact AFU-system
T_{{}_{E}}
which satisfies the assumptions of
[
AN05
, Thm 1]
.

Moreover we need information on the super-level sets
(A_{>n})
.

Definition 3.2
.

We say that a basic AFN-system
T
admits
regularly varying nice expansions
if for all
P_{j}\in\tilde{\mathcal{P}}
there are
p_{j}\geq 1
and
L_{j}
slowly varying functions at infinity (see Appendix
B
) such that

T(x)\sim x+\left|x-x_{j}\right|^{1+p_{j}}\,\left(L_{j}\left(1/\left|x-x_{j}\right|\right)\right)^{-1}\,,\quad\text{for $x\in P_{j}$ and $x\to x_{j}$.}

Using then computations similar to those in
[
T83
, Lemma 2]
, one can show that a basic AFN-system with regularly varying nice expansions satisfies assumption (ii) of Theorem
2.3
if
p:=\max_{P_{j}\in\tilde{\mathcal{P}}}p_{j}=1
and
L(y):=\max_{P_{j}\in\tilde{\mathcal{P}}}|L_{j}(y)|
satisfies

(3.2)

\int_{1}^{\infty}\,\left(\frac{L(y)}{\int_{1}^{y}\frac{L(t)}{t}\mathrm{d}t}\right)^{2}\,\frac{1}{y}\,\mathrm{d}y<\infty\,.

Indeed if
p=1
the super-level sets satisfy
\mu(A_{>n}\cap P_{j})\sim|L_{j}(n)|/n
as
n\to\infty
for all
P_{j}\in\tilde{\mathcal{P}}
, so that by (
3.2
) assumption (ii) is satisfied.

Maps in
{\mathcal{T}}
have
L_{0}(y)\equiv C
, so that the previous condition is easily satisfied.

3.2.
Fibred systems related to multi-dimensional continued fraction expansions

The class of systems in dimension greater than one for which the
\psi
-mixing property has been studied includes the so-called
fibred systems
on compact and connected subsets
E
of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

The notion of fibred system was introduced in
[
S95
]
in relation to the theory of real number expansions. Here we use results from
[
NN03
]
. We first recall the definition of a fibred system.

Definition 3.3
.

Let
E
be a compact and connected subset of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, with the
Borel
\sigma
-algebra
{\mathcal{B}}
and let
m
denote the

d
-dimensional normalised Lebesgue measure on
E
. Let
V
be a
measurable map of
E
onto itself. The pair
(E,V)
is called a

fibred system
if it satisfies the following properties.

(h1)

There exists a finite or countable measurable
partition
{\mathcal{C}}=\{C_{j}\}_{j\in J}
of
E
into open sets such that the
restriction of
V
to
C_{j}
is injective for all
j\in J
.

(h2)

The map
V
is measurable and non-singular. To simplify the next conditions we assume that
V|_{C_{j}}
is differentiable for all
j\in J
.

For
j\in J
, we denote by
\phi_{j}
the inverse of the
restriction
V|_{C_{j}}
. Given the iterated
partition
{\mathcal{C}}^{n}=\bigvee_{k=0}^{n-1}\,V^{-k}{\mathcal{C}}
, we denote by
\phi_{j_{1},\,\dots,\,j_{n}}
the local inverse of

V^{n}
restricted to
C_{j_{1},\,\dots,\,j_{n}}\in{\mathcal{C}}^{n}
.

(h3)

There exists a sequence
(\sigma(n))_{n\geq 0}
with

\sigma(n)\rightarrow 0
as
n\rightarrow\infty
and such
that

\sup_{(j_{1},\,\dots,\,j_{n})\in J^{n}}\,\diam C_{j_{1},\,\dots,\,j_{n}}\leq\sigma(n).

(h4)

There exist a finite number of measurable subsets

U_{1},\,\ldots,\,U_{N}
of
E
such that for any cylinder

C_{j_{1},\,\dots,\,j_{n}}
of positive measure, there exists

U_{i}
with
1\leq i\leq N
such that
V^{n}(C_{j_{1},\,\dots,\,j_{n}})=U_{i}
up to measure-zero sets.

(h5)

There exists a constant
\lambda\geq 1
such that

\esssup_{V^{n}(C_{j_{1},\,\dots,\,j_{n}})}|D\phi_{j_{1},\,\dots,\,j_{n}}|\leq\lambda\,\essinf_{V^{n}(C_{j_{1},\,\dots,\,j_{n}})}|D\phi_{j_{1},\,\dots,\,j_{n}}|

where
D\phi_{j_{1},\dots,j_{n}}
denotes the Jacobian determinant
of
\phi_{j_{1},\,\dots,\,j_{n}}
.

(h6)

For any
1\leq i\leq N
,
U_{i}
contains a proper
cylinder.

(h7)

There is a constant
r_{1}>0
such that

\left|D\phi_{j_{1},\,\dots,\,j_{n}}(p_{1})-D\phi_{j_{1},\,\dots,\,j_{n}}(p_{2})\right|\leq r_{1}m(C_{j_{1},\,\dots,\,j_{n}})\|p_{1}-p_{2}\|

for any
p_{1},p_{2}\in U_{i}
and all
i
.

(h8)

There is a constant
r_{2}>0
such that

\left\|\phi_{j_{1},\,\dots,\,j_{n}}(p_{1})-\phi_{j_{1},\,\dots,\,j_{n}}(p_{2})\right\|\leq r_{2}\sigma(n)\|p_{1}-p_{2}\|

for any
p_{1},p_{2}\in U_{i}
and all
i
.

(h9)

Let
\mathcal{F}
be a finite partition generated by

U_{1},\,\dots,\,U_{N}
and denote by
{\mathcal{F}}^{c}_{\ell}
the
cylinders in
{\mathcal{C}}^{\ell}
that are not contained in any element of

\mathcal{F}
. Then, as
\ell\rightarrow\infty

\gamma(\ell)\coloneqq\sum_{C(j_{1},\,\dots,\,j_{\ell})\in{\mathcal{F}}^{c}_{\ell}}\,m(C(j_{1},\,\dots,\,j_{\ell}))\rightarrow 0.

By
[
NN03
, Thm 2]
, a fibred system
(E,V)
is
\psi
-mixing, with respect to the iterated partition
{\mathcal{C}}^{n}
, and there exist constants
K>0
and
\theta\in(0,1)
such that

(3.3)

\psi(n)\leq K\,\left(\theta^{\sqrt{n}}+\sigma(\sqrt{n})+\gamma(\sqrt{n})\right)\,,

where
\sigma(k)
and
\gamma(k)
are defined in (h3) and (h9).

Examples of fibred systems are obtained in the study of multi-dimensional continued fractions algorithms. Here we consider the two-dimensional system studied in
[
BDM21
]
, which is related to the two-dimensional continued fractions algorithm introduced in
[
G01
]
. Let

\triangle\coloneqq\left\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\,:\,1\geq x\geq y\geq 0\right\}

and define the partition
\{\Gamma_{0},\Gamma_{1}\}
of
\triangle
by

\Gamma_{0}\coloneqq\left\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\,:\,1\geq x\geq y>1-x\right\},

and

\Gamma_{1}\coloneqq\left\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\,:\,1-y\geq x\geq y\geq 0\right\}.

We consider the map
S:\triangle\rightarrow\triangle
defined as

S(x,y)\coloneqq\begin{cases}\left(\frac{y}{x},\,\frac{1-x}{x}\right)&\text{if }(x,y)\in\Gamma_{0}\\[5.69046pt]
\left(\frac{x}{1-y},\,\frac{y}{1-y}\right)&\text{if
}\,(x,y)\in\Gamma_{1}\end{cases}.

In
[
BDM21
]
it is proved that
S
admits an absolutely continuous invariant measure
\mu
with density
h(x,y)=\frac{1}{xy}
, so that
\mu(\triangle)=\infty
, and that the system
(\triangle,S,\mu)
is ergodic and conservative.

The next result shows that there exists a set
E\subset\triangle
for which assumption (i) of Theorem
2.3
holds. The proof of the proposition is in Section
5
.

Proposition 3.4
.

Let
E\subset\triangle
be the triangle with vertices

Q_{1}=\left(\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2}\right)
,
Q_{2}=\left(\frac{2}{3},\frac{1}{3}\right)
and
Q_{3}=(1,1)
, with the sides

Q_{1}Q_{2}
and
Q_{2}Q_{3}
not included. Then
\mu(E)<\infty
, the induced map
S_{{}_{E}}
on
E
is
\psi
-mixing with respect to the level sets
(A_{n})
of the return time function
\varphi_{{}_{E}}
, and
\sum_{n\geq 1}\,\psi(n)/n<\infty
.

Finally, assumption (ii) of Theorem
2.3
is indirectly studied in
[
BDM21
, Appendix B]
but not proved to hold. Its validity implies the wandering rate
w_{n}(E):=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\,\mu(A_{>n})
of
E
to be slowly varying as discussed in
[
AN03
]
. This property of
w_{n}(E)
is studied in
[
BDM21
]
where it is proved that
w_{n}(E)\asymp(\log n)^{2}
, from which one could conjecture
\mu(A_{>n})=O(\frac{\log n}{n})
, and assumption (ii) would follow. Unfortunately we don’t have a proof of this asymptotic behaviour for
\mu(A_{>n})
.

3.3.
Discussions on Theorem
2.7

As previously remarked, in the case of non-summable observables the pointwise asymptotic behaviour of the Birkhoff sums depends on some properties of the observable itself. We have been able to show that for
f\geq 0
it holds
S_{N}f(x)\sim G(N)
for
\mu
-a.e.
x\in X
, where
G(n)
is the asymptotic behaviour of the induced observable
f^{E}
on the level sets
(A_{n})
, if
f
can be bounded by functions
g_{1}
and
g_{2}
with induced functions
g_{i}^{E}
of the form
nL_{i}(n)
for suitable normalised slowly varying functions (see Appendix
B
for definitions and details on slowly varying functions). The assumptions on the functions
L_{i}(n)
concern their asymptotic behaviour, as in assumptions (ii)-(b) and (c), and their link with the first return time function to the set
E
, as in assumptions (ii)-(d) and (e).

First we discuss assumptions (ii)-(b) and (c), showing sufficient conditions for
f
and for a slowly varying function to satisfy them. Then we discuss assumptions (ii)-(d) and (e) in a specific example, and consider the necessity of (ii)-(e). Further we consider the existing literature on this problem.

Proposition 3.5
.

Let
f:X\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
be constant on the level sets
(E_{n})
of the hitting time function (
2.15
) and assume that the induced function
f^{E}
satisfies
f^{E}|_{A_{n}}=n\,L(n)
for all
n\geq 0
, with
L
slowly varying. Then there exist
g_{1},g_{2}:X\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
satisfying (ii)-(a) and (b) of Theorem
2.7
.

Proof.

Apply Lemma
B.3
to
f^{E}
to find
g_{1}^{E}
and
g_{2}^{E}
. Then
g_{1}
and
g_{2}
are obtained using relation (
2.17
) between a function which is constant on the level sets
(E_{n})
and its induced function.
∎

We can thus reduce our attention to the case of a function
f
with induced function which is asymptotically equivalent to a function
G(n)=n\,L(n)
with
L
normalised slowly varying. Hence using normalised slowly varying functions does not reduce generality.

Let us now consider (ii)-(c). First notice that if
\xi(n)
is bounded then assumption (ii)-(c) is easily obtained by the properties of slowly varying functions. Moreover, if
h(n)
is a function for which
\xi(n)\leq h(n)
and assumption (ii)-(c) is satisfied for
h
, then it is immediately satisfied also for
\xi
.

Fixed a slowly varying function
h
, in Lemma
B.4
we give a sufficient condition on slowly varying functions
L
to satisfy
L(n\,\tilde{h}(n))\sim L(n)
for all
\tilde{h}
such that
c\leq\tilde{h}(n)\leq h(n)
. Notice that in Lemma
B.4
we assume that
h
is bounded below from 1. If
h
does not satisfy this assumption, simply apply the lemma to
k(n):=\min\{1+h_{0},h(n)\}
for some
h_{0}>0
, which is still slowly varying.

To study assumption (ii)-(c) using Lemma
B.4
for the function
\xi
it is thus sufficient to show that it is slowly varying or that there exists a slowly varying function
h
such that
\xi(n)\leq h(n)
. It follows from computations in Proposition
3.7
that
\xi(n)\sim\log^{2}(\log n)
, and therefore is slowly varying, for the class
{\mathcal{T}}
of maps of the interval considered in Section
3.1
. For the map studied in Section
3.2
instead, it is shown in
[
BDM21
]
that
\mu(A_{>n})=O(\frac{\log^{2}n}{n})
and
\alpha(n)=O(n/\log^{2}n)
, hence in Lemma
2.6
one finds
q(t)=O(t\,\log^{2}t)
, and

\xi(n)=O\Big(\log n\,\log^{2}(\log\,n)\Big)\,.

Thus we estimate
\xi
with a slowly varying function and Lemma
B.4
gives a sufficient condition on the slowly varying function
L
to satisfy assumption (ii)-(c). Hence if the measure of the super-level sets
(A_{>n})
is bounded by a regular varying function with index -1, and the sequence
\alpha(n)
defined in (
2.11
) is bounded by a regular varying function with index 1, then
\xi
is bounded by a slowly varying function. In Remark
4.6
we show that it is the case if (
2.18
) holds.

Finally we discuss the relation between the observable
f
and the first return time function to
E
. In particular we show that assumptions (ii)-(d) and (e) simplify when the two limiting functions
g_{1}
and
g_{2}
have induced functions which increase asymptotically linearly. The proof of the following proposition is in Section
5
.

Proposition 3.6
.

Let
T
,
(X,{\mathcal{B}},\mu)
and

be defined as in Theorem
2.7
. Let the sequence of random variables
(\varphi_{{}_{E}}\circ T_{{}_{E}}^{n-1})
with

satisfy assumption (i) of Theorem

. Let
f:X\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
be a non-negative measurable function such that

and let
f^{E}
be its induced version on
E
defined in (
2.12
). We assume that:

(c1)

there exist
g_{1},g_{2}:X\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
constant on the level sets
(E_{n})

Example 3.8

Let

Furthermore we assume

\lim_{N\to\infty}\,\frac{1}{N\,\log^{c}(\log N)}\,\sum_{n=1}^{N}\,(f\circ T^{n-1})(x)=1\,,\quad\text{for $\mu$-a.e.\ $x\in X$.}

We will use Theorem
2.7
to verify this statement. Obviously, conditions (i), (ii)-(a), and (ii)-(b) are already fulfilled by assumption.
To verify condition (ii)-(c) we notice that
q(n)\sim n\,\log^{b}(\log n)
(using
[

\alpha(n)\sim n/(\log n\,\log^{b}(\log n))

\log^{c}(\log(n\,\xi(n)))\sim\log^{c}(\log n)

To verify condition (ii)-(d) we notice that

\displaystyle\sim\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\log^{2(b+c)}(\log y)}{y\left(\int_{0}^{y}\,\log^{b+c}(\log t)/t)\mathrm{d}t\right)^{2}}\mathrm{d}y\sim\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{y\,\log^{2}y}\mathrm{d}y<\infty.

On the other hand

L(n)\,\sum_{k=1}^{n}\mu(A_{>k})\sim\Big(\log^{c}(\log n)\Big)\,\Big(\log n\,\log^{b}(\log n)\Big)\sim\ell(n)

giving all properties of Theorem
2.7
and (

Finally, we discuss the existing literature. The pointwise convergence of Birkhoff averages for non-summable observables and infinite measure-preserving dynamical systems has been recently discussed in
[

]
. The authors of
[

]
have proved that (

) holds under strong assumptions on the observable

and very weak assumptions on the system. Basically they considered

observables for which it is possible to control in a very strong way the contributions to the Birkhoff sums from the excursions out of a finite measure set

, the set on which we have considered the induced map. In this paper we have less stringent assumptions on the observables but have considered more particular dynamical systems, those for which an induced map exists which is

-mixing. We finish this section by discussing the relations of our results with those in
[
LM18
]
for the Farey map, a system in the class

.

Remark 3.10
.

Let us consider the particular case of the Farey map
F:[0,1]\to[0,1]
which belongs to the class
{\mathcal{T}}
considered in Section
3.1
and is defined as

F(x)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\frac{x}{1-x}\,,&\text{if $x\in\left[0,\frac{1}{2}\right]$,}\\[5.69046pt]
\frac{1-x}{x}\,,&\text{if $x\in\left[\frac{1}{2},1\right]$.}\end{array}\right.

It is well known that the absolutely continuous invariant measure is
d\mu(x)=\frac{1}{x\log 2}\,dx
. If
E=(\frac{1}{2},1)
, for which
\mu(E)=1
, it holds
E_{n}=(\frac{1}{n+1},\frac{1}{n})
. Thus
\mu(E_{n})=\mu(A_{>n})=\log_{2}(1+\frac{1}{n})\sim\frac{1}{n\log 2}
.

A particular class of observables studied in the context of infinite mixing theory is that of
global observables
. For maps in
{\mathcal{T}}
, we recall that a global observable is a function
f\in L^{\infty}([0,1],\mu)
such that the limit

\lim_{a\to 0^{+}}\,\frac{1}{\mu(a,1)}\,\int_{a}^{1}\,f\,d\mu

exists and is finite (see
[
BGL18
,
BL21
]
for more details).
We first show that observables
f\in L^{\infty}(X,\mu)
which are constant on the level sets
(E_{n})
with
f_{n}:=f|_{E_{n}}
and for which the induced function satisfies
f^{E}|_{A_{n}}\sim n
as
n\to\infty
, are global observables. The definition of global observable for the Farey map and for observables constant on the sets
E_{n}
reduces to show that the limit

(3.6)

\lim_{n\to\infty}\,\frac{1}{\mu\left(\frac{1}{k+1},1\right)}\,\int_{\frac{1}{k+1}}^{1}\,f(x)\,d\mu=\lim_{n\to\infty}\,\frac{1}{\sum_{k=1}^{n}\,\log_{2}(1+\frac{1}{k})}\,\sum_{k=1}^{n}\,f_{k}\,\log_{2}\left(1+\frac{1}{k}\right)

exists and is finite. To prove this, it is enough to recall that
\sum_{k=1}^{n}\,\log_{2}(1+\frac{1}{k})\sim\log_{2}n
and to apply Abel’s summation formula to get

\sum_{k=1}^{n}\,f_{k}\,\log_{2}\left(1+\frac{1}{k}\right)\sim\left(\sum_{k=1}^{n}\,f_{k}\right)\,\log_{2}\left(1+\frac{1}{n}\right)+\frac{1}{\log 2}\int_{1}^{n}\,\left(\sum_{k=1}^{t}\,f_{k}\right)\,\frac{1}{t^{2}+t}\,dt\sim

\sim f^{E}|_{A_{n}}\,\log_{2}\left(1+\frac{1}{n}\right)+\int_{1}^{n}\,\frac{f^{E}|_{A_{t}}}{\log 2}\,\frac{1}{t^{2}+t}\,dt\sim n\,\log_{2}\left(1+\frac{1}{n}\right)+\frac{1}{\log 2}\int_{1}^{n}\,\frac{t}{t^{2}+t}\,dt\sim\log_{2}(n+1)\,.

On the other hand let’s consider a global observable
f:X\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
which is constant on the level sets
(E_{n})
with
f_{n}:=f|_{E_{n}}
. By rescaling we can assume that the limit in (
3.6
) exists and is equal to 1. In general it is not possible to conclude about the asymptotic behaviour of
f^{E}|_{A_{n}}
, however some sufficient conditions can be obtained by the following argument (see
[
M06
]
). With
\ell_{n}:=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\,\log_{2}(1+\frac{1}{k})
let

\tau_{n}:=\frac{1}{\ell_{n}}\,\sum_{k=1}^{n}\,f_{k}\,\log_{2}\left(1+\frac{1}{k}\right)\,.

Then
f_{1}=\ell_{1}\tau_{1}
and

f_{n}=\frac{1}{\log_{2}\left(1+\frac{1}{n}\right)}\,(\ell_{n}\tau_{n}-\ell_{n-1}\tau_{n-1})\,,\quad\text{for $n\geq 2$,}

from which

f^{E}|_{A_{n}}=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\,f_{k}=\frac{\ell_{n}\tau_{n}}{\log_{2}\left(1+\frac{1}{n}\right)}-\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}\,\ell_{k}\tau_{k}\left(\frac{1}{\log_{2}\left(1+\frac{1}{k+1}\right)}-\frac{1}{\log_{2}\left(1+\frac{1}{k}\right)}\right)\,.

Since
\ell_{n}\sim\log_{2}n
, it follows for example that if
\tau_{n}\sim 1+o((\log_{2}n)^{-1})
then
f^{E}|_{A_{n}}\sim n
. Hence we can check if Proposition
3.7
is applicable to a given global observable.

Let us now compare the conditions on
f
in Proposition
3.7
with the statement in
[
LM18
, Theorem 2.5]
. In our setting and with proper norming, the assumptions in
[
LM18
, Theorem 2.5]
can be stated as follows:
f\in L^{\infty}(X,\mu)
and for all
\epsilon>0
there exist
N,K\in\mathbb{N}
such that for all
x\in\bigcup_{k\geq K}E_{k}
we have that

(3.7)

\displaystyle\left|\frac{\sum_{j=1}^{N}\left(f\circ T^{j-1}\right)(x)}{N}-1\right|<\epsilon.

First we show that if the assumptions for an observable
f
in
[
LM18
, Theorem 2.5]
are satisfied, then we obtain a good asymptotic behaviour for the induced observable
f^{E}
, so that we are basically in a good situation to apply Theorem
2.7
.

Lemma 3.11
.

Let
f:[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
be in
L^{\infty}(X,\mu)
and such that for all
\epsilon>0
there exist
N,K\in\mathbb{N}
such that for all
x\in\bigcup_{k\geq K}E_{k}
we have that (
3.7
) holds. Then
f^{E}|_{A_{k}}\sim k
for
k\to\infty
.

Proof.

Let
\epsilon>0
be given and consider
N,K
fixed as in (
3.7
). We set
f_{N}(x)\coloneqq\sum_{j=1}^{N}(f\circ T^{j-1})(x)
. For
x\in A_{R}
with
R>K
, writing
R=K+rN+s
with
r\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
and
0\leq s\leq N-1
, we get

f^{E}(x)=\sum_{j=1}^{R+1}\,(f\circ T^{j-1})(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{r}\,f_{N}(T^{N(i-1)}(x))+\sum_{j=rN+1}^{R+1}\,(f\circ T^{j-1})(x)\,.

Since
T^{N(i-1)}(x)\in\bigcup_{k\geq K}E_{k}
for all
i=1,\dots,r
, we apply (
3.7
), and the fact that
f\in L^{\infty}(X,\mu)
and
f\geq 0
, to obtain

rN\,(1-\epsilon)\leq f^{E}(x)\leq rN\,(1+\epsilon)+(N+K)\,\|f\|_{\infty}\,,\quad\forall\,x\in A_{R}\,.

Since
R\sim rN\to\infty
as
R\to\infty
, the lemma is proved.
∎

Finally we give two examples to which Theorem
2.7
is applicable, but which fail to fulfil the conditions in
[
LM18
, Theorem 2.5]
.
The first is an example with
f\notin L^{\infty}(X,\mu)
, the second one with
f\in L^{\infty}(X,\mu)
but not satisfying (
3.7
).

Example 3.12
.

Let us consider a strictly monotonic sequence of natural numbers
\gamma_{n}
defined by
\gamma_{1}=4
and
\gamma_{n+1}=\gamma_{n}+\left\lfloor\gamma_{n}^{1/2}\right\rfloor
. Then we set
f
to be constant on the intervals
E_{k}
with

\displaystyle f(x)=\begin{cases}\gamma_{n}-\gamma_{n-1}\,,&\text{if $x\in E_{\gamma_{n}}$ for some $n\in\mathbb{N}$;}\\[5.69046pt]
0\,,&\text{else}.\end{cases}

Since
\gamma_{n}-\gamma_{n-1}
tends to infinity,
f\notin L^{\infty}(X,\mu)
. On the other hand the induced function satisfies
f^{E}|_{A_{k}}=\gamma_{n}
for
\gamma_{n}\leq k<\gamma_{n+1}
, so that for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
and all
x\in A_{k}
we have
k+1-\sqrt{k+1}\leq f^{E}(x)\leq k
, implying that
f^{E}|_{A_{k}}\sim k
as

.

Example 3.13
.

First we define a strictly monotonic sequence of even numbers
\kappa_{n}
by setting
\kappa_{1}=4
and
\kappa_{n+1}=\kappa_{n}+2\left\lfloor\kappa_{n}^{1/2}/2\right\rfloor
.
Furthermore, we set

\displaystyle U\coloneqq\left\{\kappa_{n},\kappa_{n}+1,\ldots,\kappa_{n}+1/2\left(\kappa_{n+1}-\kappa_{n}\right),\,n\in\mathbb{N}\right\}.

We define

\displaystyle f(x)=\begin{cases}2\,,&\text{if $x\in E_{k}$ and $k\in U$;}\\[5.69046pt]
0\,,&\text{else}.\end{cases}

Then
0\leq f(x)\leq 2
and thus
f\in L^{\infty}(X,\mu)
. Furthermore, if
x\in A_{k}
then
k-2\sqrt{k}\leq f^{E}(x)\leq k+2\sqrt{k}
implying that
f^{E}|_{A_{k}}\sim k
as
k\to\infty
.

On the other hand, for each
K\in\mathbb{N}
there is a positive measure set

such that for all

we have that

implying that (

) cannot be fulfilled.

4.
Proofs of the main results

4.1.
Proof of Theorem
2.3

We prove the theorem for
\mu
-a.e.
x\in E
. The result for
\mu
-a.e.
x\in X
follows since
T
is conservative and ergodic. Let
f\in L^{1}(X,\mu)
and let
R_{{}_{E,N,m}}(x):=R_{{}_{E,N+m(N,E,x)}}(x)
denote the number of visits to
E
in the first
N+m(N,E,x)
steps of the orbit of
x
. Thus for each
N
we add a number of steps depending on
x
and being non-decreasing in
N
.

Lemma 4.1
.

For
\mu
-a.e.
x\in E
it holds

\lim_{N\to\infty}\,\frac{1}{R_{{}_{E,N,m}}(x)}\,\sum_{k=1}^{N+m(N,E,x)}\,(f\circ T^{k-1})(x)=\int_{X}\,f\,\mathrm{d}\mu\,.

Proof.

We apply Hopf’s Ratio Ergodic Theorem to
f(x)
and
\mathbbm{1}_{{}_{E}}(x)
and obtain for
\mu
-a.e.
x\in E

\lim_{N\to\infty}\,\frac{\sum_{k=1}^{N+m(N,E,x)}\,(f\circ T^{k-1})(x)}{R_{{}_{E,N,m}}(x)}=

=\lim_{N\to\infty}\,\frac{\sum_{k=1}^{N+m(N,E,x)}\,(f\circ T^{k-1})(x)}{(\mathbbm{1}_{{}_{E}}\circ T^{N+m(N,E,x)})(x)+\sum_{k=1}^{N+m(N,E,x)}\,(\mathbbm{1}_{{}_{E}}\circ T^{k-1})(x)}=

=\frac{\int_{X}\,f\,d\mu}{\int_{X}\,\mathbbm{1}_{{}_{E}}\,d\mu}=\int_{X}\,f\,\mathrm{d}\mu

where we have used that
\sum_{k=1}^{N+m(N,E,x)}\,(\mathbbm{1}_{{}_{E}}\circ T^{k-1})(x)
is divergent.
∎

Let us now recall for
\mu
-a.e.
x\in E
the definition of the time
\tau_{{}_{E,x}}(N)
of the
N
-th return to
E
given in (
2.13
). Then as in (
2.8
), we consider its trimmed version

(4.1)

\tau^{1}_{{}_{E,x}}(N):=\tau_{{}_{E,x}}(N)-\max_{1\leq k\leq N}\,(\varphi_{{}_{E}}\circ T_{{}_{E}}^{k-1})(x)

where we are trimming
\tau_{{}_{E,x}}
by deleting the largest entry.

Lemma 4.2
.

For
\mu
-a.e.
x\in E
it eventually holds

N<\tau^{1}_{{}_{E,x}}(R_{{}_{E,N,m}}(x))\leq N+M^{2}_{{}_{N,m}}(x)

where
M^{2}_{{}_{N,m}}(x)
denotes the 2-nd maximum in
\{(\varphi_{{}_{E}}\circ T_{{}_{E}}^{k-1})(x)\,:\,k=1,\dots,R_{{}_{E,N,m}}(x)\}
which in case of equality might coincide with the maximum.

Proof.

We first give some remarks on
\tau_{{}_{E,x}}
and
R_{{}_{E,N}}(x)
. Given
N\geq 1
,
R_{{}_{E,N}}(x)
is the number of visits to
E
up to time
N
, hence
R_{{}_{E,N}}(x)\leq N+1
and
T_{{}_{E}}^{R_{{}_{E,N}}(x)-1}(x)
is the last visit to
E
for
x
up to time
N
. It follows that

\tau_{{}_{E,x}}(R_{{}_{E,N}}(x))=\sum_{k=1}^{R_{{}_{E,N}}(x)}\,(\varphi_{{}_{E}}\circ T_{{}_{E}}^{k-1})(x)>N

as the
(R_{{}_{E,N}}(x))
-th return to
E
, equivalently the
(R_{{}_{E,N}}(x)+1)
-th visit to
E
, happens after time
N
. Moreover

\sum_{k=1}^{R_{{}_{E,N}}(x)}\,(\varphi_{{}_{E}}\circ T_{{}_{E}}^{k-1})(x)=\varphi_{{}_{E}}\circ T_{{}_{E}}^{R_{{}_{E,N}}(x)-1}(x)+\tau_{{}_{E,x}}(R_{{}_{E,N}}(x)-1)\leq\varphi_{{}_{E}}\circ T_{{}_{E}}^{R_{{}_{E,N}}(x)-1}(x)+N

as the
(R_{{}_{E,N}}(x)-1)
-th return to
E
, equivalently the
(R_{{}_{E,N}}(x))
-th visit to
E
, happens at time less than or equal to
N
.

Using now
R_{{}_{E,N,m}}(x)
, we have proved

(4.2)

N+m(N,E,x)<\tau_{{}_{E,x}}(R_{{}_{E,N,m}}(x))\leq N+m(N,E,x)+\varphi_{{}_{E}}\circ T_{{}_{E}}^{R_{{}_{E,N,m}}(x)-1}(x).

We now show that

(4.3)

\max\left\{(\varphi_{{}_{E}}\circ T_{{}_{E}}^{k-1})(x)\,:\,k=1,\dots,R_{{}_{E,N,m}}(x)\right\}=\max\left\{m(N,E,x),\,\varphi_{{}_{E}}\circ T_{{}_{E}}^{R_{{}_{E,N,m}}(x)-1}(x)\right\}.

From (
2.6
),
m(N,E,x)
maximises the return times
(\varphi_{{}_{E}}\circ T_{{}_{E}}^{k-1})(x)
for
k=1,\dots,R_{{}_{E,N}}(x)
, that is up to time
\tau_{{}_{E,x}}(R_{{}_{E,N}}(x))
. Moreover, repeating the same argument as above, the return times
(\varphi_{{}_{E}}\circ T_{{}_{E}}^{k-1})(x)
with
k=R_{{}_{E,N}}(x)+1,\dots,R_{{}_{E,N,m}}(x)-1
are concerned with excursions outside
E
for the orbit of
x
with time in
(N,N+m(N,E,x)]
. Then

m(N,E,x)=\max\left\{(\varphi_{{}_{E}}\circ T_{{}_{E}}^{k-1})(x)\,:\,k=1,\dots,R_{{}_{E,N}}(x)-1\right\}

and (
4.3
) is proved.

Then, if

\max\left\{(\varphi_{{}_{E}}\circ T_{{}_{E}}^{k-1})(x)\,:\,k=1,\dots,R_{{}_{E,N,m}}(x)\right\}=m(N,E,x)

it immediately follows from (
4.2
) that

N<\tau_{{}_{E,x}}(R_{{}_{E,N,m}}(x))-\max_{1\leq k\leq R_{{}_{E,N,m}}(x)}\,(\varphi_{{}_{E}}\circ T_{{}_{E}}^{k-1})(x)=\tau^{1}_{{}_{E,x}}(R_{{}_{E,N,m}}(x))\,,

otherwise, we obtain

\tau^{1}_{{}_{E,x}}(R_{{}_{E,N,m}}(x))=\tau_{{}_{E,x}}(R_{{}_{E,N,m}}(x)-1)>N

since
x
necessarily visits
E
at some time
k
in
(N,N+m(N,E,x)]
. In both cases we have thus obtained the first inequality to prove.

Moreover from (
4.2
) and (
4.3
) we obtain

\tau_{{}_{E,x}}(R_{{}_{E,N,m}}(x))-\max_{1\leq k\leq R_{{}_{E,N,m}}(x)}\,(\varphi_{{}_{E}}\circ T_{{}_{E}}^{k-1})(x)\leq N+\min\left\{m(N,E,x),\,\varphi_{{}_{E}}\circ T_{{}_{E}}^{R_{{}_{E,N,m}}(x)-1}(x)\right\}

and by (

Appendix B

In this section we collect the results on slowly varying functions used in the proof of Theorem
2.7
. We recall that a function
L:\mathbb{R}_{>0}\to\mathbb{R}
is called
regularly varying (at infinity) with index
\gamma\in\mathbb{R}
if for all
r>0
it holds

\lim_{x\to\infty}\,\frac{L(rx)}{L(x)}=r^{\gamma}\,.

If
\gamma=0
,
L
is called a
slowly varying (at infinity)
. We refer to
[
BGT87
]
for more details. By Karamata’s representation theorem (see for example
[
BGT87
, Thm. 1.3.1]
) we have that each slowly varying function
L
can be written as

(B.1)

\displaystyle L(x)=c(x)\,\exp\left(\int_{\kappa}^{x}\frac{\eta(t)}{t}\mathrm{d}t\right),

where
c(x)
tends to a constant
C
as
x\to\infty
,
\kappa\geq 0
and
\eta(x)
tends to zero as
x\to\infty
, and a function
L
is called
normalized slowly varying
if it can be written as

(B.2)

\displaystyle L(x)=C\,\exp\left(\int_{\kappa}^{x}\frac{\eta(t)}{t}\mathrm{d}t\right).

It is immediately clear that for each slowly varying function
L
there exists a normalised slowly varying function
\widetilde{L}
such that
L(x)\sim\widetilde{L}(x)
.

In the proof of our results we need
Potter’s bound
, see for example
[
BGT87
, Thm. 1.5.6]
. If
L
is a slowly varying function at infinity, then for all constants
\delta>0
and
A>1
there exists
C=C\left(\delta,A\right)
such that for all
x,y\geq C
we have

(B.3)

\frac{L\left(x\right)}{L\left(y\right)}\leq A\,\max\left\{\left(\frac{x}{y}\right)^{\delta}\,,\left(\frac{y}{x}\right)^{\delta}\right\}.

Lemma B.1
.

If
(a_{n})
and
(b_{n})
are two sequences of non-negative reals, at least one of them diverging, and
L
is non-negative and slowly varying such that
(n\,L(n))
is non-decreasing, then

a_{n}\,L(a_{n})+b_{n}\,L(b_{n})\sim(a_{n}+b_{n})\,L(a_{n}+b_{n})\,.

Proof.

We begin writing

\displaystyle a_{n}L(a_{n})+b_{n}L(b_{n})=\left(a_{n}+b_{n}\right)L(a_{n})-b_{n}\,\left(L(a_{n})-L(b_{n})\right).

Without restriction of generality we assume that
a_{n}>b_{n}
for all
n
. (Otherwise we could just define two new sequences
c_{n}:=\max\{a_{n},b_{n}\}
and
d_{n}:=\min\{a_{n},b_{n}\}
and continue with these sequences.)
By Potter’s bound (
B.3
) we have for all
\epsilon>0
that there exists
N\in\mathbb{N}
such that for
n\geq N

L(a_{n}+b_{n})\leq\left(1+\epsilon\right)L(a_{n})\,\left(\frac{a_{n}+b_{n}}{a_{n}}\right)^{\epsilon}\leq(1+\epsilon)\,2^{\epsilon}\,L(a_{n})

and

L(a_{n}+b_{n})\geq\left(1-\epsilon\right)L(a_{n})\,\left(\frac{a_{n}}{a_{n}+b_{n}}\right)^{\epsilon}\geq(1-\epsilon)\,2^{-\epsilon}\,L(a_{n})\,,

implying
L(a_{n}+b_{n})\sim L(a_{n})
and thus
\left(a_{n}+b_{n}\right)L(a_{n})\sim\left(a_{n}+b_{n}\right)L(a_{n}+b_{n})
.

Next, we have a closer look at
b_{n}\,\left(L(a_{n})-L(b_{n})\right)
distinguishing two cases. For a given
\epsilon>0
we first look at the subsequence
n_{k}
fulfilling
b_{n_{k}}\leq\epsilon\,a_{n_{k}}
for which we have

\displaystyle b_{n_{k}}\,\left(L(a_{n_{k}})-L(b_{n_{k}})\right)<\epsilon\,\left(a_{n_{k}}+b_{n_{k}}\right)L(a_{n_{k}})\sim\epsilon\,\left(a_{n_{k}}+b_{n_{k}}\right)L\left(a_{n_{k}}+b_{n_{k}}\right)\,.

On the other hand,
n\,L(n)
being non-decreasing and non-negative we have

\displaystyle b_{n_{k}}\,\left(L(a_{n_{k}})-L(b_{n_{k}})\right)

\displaystyle\geq-b_{n_{k}}\,L\left(b_{n_{k}}\right)\geq-\epsilon\,\left(a_{n_{k}}+b_{n_{k}}\right)\,L\left(\epsilon\,\left(a_{n_{k}}+b_{n_{k}}\right)\right)

\displaystyle\sim-\epsilon\,\left(a_{n_{k}}+b_{n_{k}}\right)\,L\left(a_{n_{k}}+b_{n_{k}}\right).

Next, we look at the subsequence fulfilling
b_{n_{k}}>\epsilon\,a_{n_{k}}
. Applying again (
B.3
), we have that for
k
big enough

L(a_{n_{k}}+b_{n_{k}})\leq(1+\epsilon)\,L(b_{n_{k}})\,\left(\frac{a_{n_{k}}+b_{n_{k}}}{b_{n_{k}}}\right)^{\epsilon}\leq\frac{(1+\epsilon)^{1+\epsilon}}{\epsilon^{\epsilon}}\,L(b_{n_{k}})

and

L(a_{n_{k}}+b_{n_{k}})\geq\frac{1}{1+\epsilon}\,L(b_{n_{k}})\,\left(\frac{b_{n_{k}}}{a_{n_{k}}+b_{n_{k}}}\right)^{\epsilon}\geq\frac{\epsilon^{\epsilon}}{(1+\epsilon)^{1+\epsilon}}\,L(b_{n_{k}})\,.

Since
L(a_{n_{k}}+b_{n_{k}})\sim L(a_{n_{k}})
we also have that for
k
big enough

\frac{1}{1+\epsilon}\leq\frac{L(a_{n_{k}}+b_{n_{k}})}{L(a_{n_{k}})}\leq 1+\epsilon\,,

therefore there is a constant
c>0
such that choosing
k
big enough we have

\displaystyle b_{n_{k}}\,\left(L(a_{n_{k}})-L(b_{n_{k}})\right)

\displaystyle\leq(a_{n_{k}}+b_{n_{k}})\left((1+\epsilon)L(a_{n_{k}}+b_{n_{k}})-\frac{\epsilon^{\epsilon}}{(1+\epsilon)^{1+\epsilon}}\,L(a_{n_{k}}+b_{n_{k}})\right)

\displaystyle\leq c\,\epsilon^{1/2}\,(a_{n_{k}}+b_{n_{k}})\,L(a_{n_{k}}+b_{n_{k}})

for
\epsilon\in(0,1)
, and similarly

\displaystyle b_{n_{k}}\,\left(L(a_{n_{k}})-L(b_{n_{k}})\right)

\displaystyle\geq b_{n_{k}}\left(\frac{1}{1+\epsilon}\,L(a_{n_{k}}+b_{n_{k}})-\frac{(1+\epsilon)^{1+\epsilon}}{\epsilon^{\epsilon}}\,L(a_{n_{k}}+b_{n_{k}})\right)

\displaystyle\geq-c\,\epsilon^{1/2}\,(a_{n_{k}}+b_{n_{k}})\,L(a_{n_{k}}+b_{n_{k}})

for
\epsilon\in(0,1)
.

Since
\epsilon
was chosen arbitrarily, we have that

b_{n_{k}}\,\left(L(a_{n_{k}})-L(b_{n_{k}})\right)=o\left((a_{n_{k}}+b_{n_{k}})\,L(a_{n_{k}}+b_{n_{k}})\right)\,,

and the lemma is proved.
∎

Lemma B.2
.

Let
(a_{n})
and
(b_{n})
be two sequences of non-negative reals with
a_{n}>b_{n}
and
(a_{n})
tending to infinity. Further let
L
be a non-negative normalized slowly varying function. Then

(B.4)

\displaystyle a_{n}L(a_{n})-b_{n}L(b_{n})\sim(a_{n}-b_{n})L(a_{n})\,.

Proof.

First we look at a subsequence
n_{j}
for which
b_{n_{j}}
is bounded, in this case (
B.4
) immediately holds.
Hence, we may assume without loss of generality that also
b_{n}
tends to infinity. We have

\displaystyle a_{n}L(a_{n})-b_{n}L(b_{n})

\displaystyle=\left(a_{n}-b_{n}\right)L(a_{n})+b_{n}\,\left(L(a_{n})-L(b_{n})\right)

\displaystyle=b_{n}\left(\frac{a_{n}}{b_{n}}-1\right)L(a_{n})+b_{n}\,\left(1-\frac{L(b_{n})}{L(a_{n})}\right)\,L(a_{n}).

Hence, we have to show that

\displaystyle 1-\frac{L(b_{n})}{L(a_{n})}=o\left(\frac{a_{n}}{b_{n}}-1\right).

To do so, using (
B.2
) we note that

\displaystyle\frac{L(a_{n})}{L(b_{n})}

\displaystyle=\exp\left(\int_{b_{n}}^{a_{n}}\frac{\eta(t)}{t}\mathrm{d}t\right)\leq\exp\left(\sup_{y\in[b_{n},a_{n}]}\eta(y)\int_{b_{n}}^{a_{n}}\frac{1}{t}\mathrm{d}t\right)

and similarly

\displaystyle\frac{L(a_{n})}{L(b_{n})}

\displaystyle=\exp\left(\int_{b_{n}}^{a_{n}}\frac{\eta(t)}{t}\mathrm{d}t\right)\geq\exp\left(\inf_{y\in[b_{n},a_{n}]}\eta(y)\int_{b_{n}}^{a_{n}}\frac{1}{t}\mathrm{d}t\right).

Since
(b_{n})
tends to infinity and
\eta
tends to zero we can conclude that for all
\epsilon>0
there exists
N\in\mathbb{N}

such that for all
n\geq N
we have

\displaystyle\left(\frac{a_{n}}{b_{n}}\right)^{-\epsilon}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{L(a_{n})}{L(b_{n})}\leq\left(\frac{a_{n}}{b_{n}}\right)^{\epsilon}.

Hence, for all
\epsilon>0
there exists
N\in\mathbb{N}
such that for all
n\geq N

\displaystyle\frac{\left|1-\frac{L(b_{n})}{L(a_{n})}\right|}{\left|\frac{a_{n}}{b_{n}}-1\right|}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\left(\frac{a_{n}}{b_{n}}\right)^{\epsilon}-1}{\frac{a_{n}}{b_{n}}-1}.

Since the function
g\colon(1,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
given by
(x^{\epsilon}-1)/(x-1)
is monotonically decreasing and tends to
\epsilon
for
x\to 1^{+}
, it follows that for all
\epsilon>0
there exists
N\in\mathbb{N}
such that for all
n\geq N

\displaystyle\left|1-\frac{L(b_{n})}{L(a_{n})}\right|\leq\epsilon\,\left|\frac{a_{n}}{b_{n}}-1\right|\,.

Since
\epsilon
was arbitrary, the statement is proven.
∎

Lemma B.3
.

Let
L
be a slowly varying function at infinity. Then there exist two normalised slowly varying functions
L^{-}
and
L^{+}
such that
L^{-}(n)\leq L(n)\leq L^{+}(n)
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
L^{-}(n)\sim L(n)\sim L^{+}(n)
as
n\to\infty
.

Proof.

Let
L
be represented as in (
B.1
) for fixed
c(x)
,
\kappa
and
\eta(x)
. Our approach is to find two normalised slowly varying functions
c^{-},c^{+}
fulfilling
c^{-}\leq c\leq c^{+}
and
c^{-}(n)\sim c(n)\sim c^{+}(n)
. Then we may set

L^{\pm}(n)=c^{\pm}(n)\,\exp\left(\int_{\kappa}^{n}\eta(t)/t\mathrm{d}t\right)\,.

Since a product of two normalised slowly varying functions is still normalised slowly varying, the functions
L^{\pm}
fulfil all the required properties. We will in the following only give the construction of
c^{-}
as the construction of
c^{+}
follows analogously.

We notice that a function
\ell
is normalised slowly varying if it fulfils
\ell^{\prime}(x)=o\left(\ell(x)/x\right)
almost everywhere. This follows immediately by taking the derivative of (
B.2
), but see also
[
BGT87
, p. 15]
. We construct a function
c^{-}
on a line
[\gamma_{1},\infty)
which is continuous and piecewise differentiable.

In order to define
c^{-}
let
\Gamma\coloneqq\left\{n\in\mathbb{N}\colon c(n)<c(k)\text{ for all }k>n\right\}
and let
(\gamma_{n})
be the ordered sequence of elements in
\Gamma
, that is we have
\gamma_{1}<\gamma_{2}<\ldots
and
c(\gamma_{1})<c(\gamma_{2})<\ldots
. The set
\Gamma
contains infinitely many elements unless
\min\left\{c(n),C\right\}=C
for sufficiently large
n
which is a trivial case in which we might simply set
c^{-}(n)=\min\left\{c(n),C\right\}
. In this case
(c^{-})^{\prime}(x)=0
for
x
sufficiently large and thus
(c^{-})^{\prime}(x)\leq(x\log x)^{-1}
holds immediately.

As we are only interested in the limit behaviour, it is sufficient to define
c^{-}
at
\mathbb{R}_{\geq\gamma_{1}}
and we set

c^{-}(\gamma_{i})=c(\gamma_{i})
, for all
i\in\mathbb{N}
.
For
x\in(\gamma_{i},\gamma_{i}+1)
we set

\displaystyle c^{-}(x):=\max\left\{(\log\,x)^{1/2}+c(\gamma_{i})-(\log\,\gamma_{i})^{1/2},c(\gamma_{i+1})\right\}.

The function
c^{-}
is then either piecewise constant and on those parts its derivative equals zero, or has derivative equal to
1/2(\log\,x)^{-1/2}\,x^{-1}=o(x^{-1})
. This is sufficient since in our case,
c^{-}
is bounded by
C
. Thus, at those points at which
c^{-}
is differentiable, it fulfils
\left(c^{-}\right)^{\prime}(x)=o\left(c^{-}(x)/x\right)
and there are only countably many points on which it is not differentiable. On the other hand, since
(\log\,x)^{1/2}
tends to infinity,
c^{-}
tends to
C
.
∎

Lemma B.4
.

Let
h
be a slowly varying function with
h(t)\geq 1+h_{0}
for all
t
and for some
h_{0}>0
, and let
L
be a slowly varying function written as in (
B.1
) with
\eta(t)=o(1/\log\xi(t))
as
t\to\infty
. Then for all functions
\tilde{h}
which satisfy
c\leq\tilde{h}(n)\leq h(n)
for some constant
c>0
and all
n
, it holds
L(n\,\tilde{h}(n))\sim L(n)
as
n\to\infty
.

The proof of the lemma follows by applying results in the theory of functions with regular variations for which we first need to introduce some definitions.

Definition B.5
.

Given a function
h:\mathbb{R}_{>0}\to\mathbb{R}_{>0}
, a function
\ell:\mathbb{R}_{>0}\to\mathbb{R}
is called
super-slowly varying at infinity with rate function

h
if

\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{\ell\left(x\,h(x)^{\delta}\right)}{\ell(x)}=1\text{ uniformly in }0\leq\delta\leq 1.

Definition B.6
.

A function
\phi:\mathbb{R}_{>0}\to\mathbb{R}
is called
self-controlled
if there exist positive constants
\gamma,\Gamma,T
such that
\gamma\phi(t)\leq\phi(t+\delta\phi(t))\leq\Gamma\phi(t)
, for all
t\geq T
and
\delta\in[0,1]
.

Lemma B.7

(
[
BGT87
, Thm. 3.12.5]
)
.

Let
h:\mathbb{R}_{>0}\to\mathbb{R}_{>0}
be such that
\phi(t):=\log(h(e^{t}))
is self-controlled and there exists
T>0
such that
1/\phi
is locally integrable over
[T,\infty)
.
Then
L:\mathbb{R}_{>0}\to\mathbb{R}
is super-slowly varying with rate function
h
if and only if

L
can be written as in (
B.1
) with
\eta(t)=o\left(1/\log h(t)\right)
as
t\to\infty
.

Proof of Lemma
B.4
.

Let us use Lemma
B.7
. Since
h
is a slowly varying function and
h(t)\geq 1+h_{0}
for all
t
, the function
\phi(t):=\log(h(e^{t}))
is such that
1/\phi
is locally integrable. Moreover we notice that
\phi(t+\delta\phi(t))=\log(h(e^{t}\,h(e^{t})^{\delta}))
and by using Potter’s bound (
B.3
) for
h
we obtain for all
\epsilon>0
that there exists
T>0
such that for
t>T
we have

\frac{h(e^{t}\,h(e^{t})^{\delta})}{h(e^{t})}\leq(1+\epsilon)\,\max\left\{h(e^{t})^{\delta\epsilon}\,,\,h(e^{t})^{-\delta\epsilon}\right\}\,.

Since
h(t)\geq 1+h_{0}
, we have
\phi(t)\geq\log(1+h_{0})
, hence for all
\epsilon\in(0,h_{0})

\phi(t+\delta\phi(t))\leq\log(1+\epsilon)+(1+\delta\epsilon)\,\phi(t)\leq(2+\delta\epsilon)\,\phi(t)\,.

Analogously, we obtain
\phi(t+\delta\phi(t))\geq\left(\left(1-\delta\epsilon\right)+\log(1-\epsilon)/\log(1+h_{0})\right)\phi(t)
. If we choose
\epsilon
sufficiently small, then the factor before
\phi(t)
is bounded away from zero which implies that
\phi
is self-controlled.

Then by Lemma
B.7
, we obtain that if
L
is a slowly varying function written as in (
B.1
) with
\eta(t)=o(1/\log h(t))
as
t\to\infty
, it is super-slowly varying with rate function
h
. Let now
\tilde{h}
be a function satisfying
c\leq\tilde{h}(n)\leq h(n)
for all
n
and for a constant
c>0
. Up to dividing by
c
and using that
\eta(t)=o(1/\log(h(t)/c))
as
t\to\infty
, we can set
c=1
. Then we can write
\tilde{h}(n)=h(n)^{\delta(n)}
where
\delta(n)\in[0,1]
for all
n
. By Definition
B.5
it follows

\left|\frac{L(n\,\tilde{h}(n))}{L(n)}-1\right|=\left|\frac{L(n\,h(n)^{\delta(n)})}{L(n)}-1\right|\leq\sup_{\delta\in[0,1]}\left|\frac{L(n\,h(n)^{\delta})}{L(n)}-1\right|\to 0\quad\text{as $n\to\infty$.}

and the lemma is proved.
∎
