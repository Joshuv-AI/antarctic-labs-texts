Possible origin for the similar phase transitions in k k -core and interdependent networks
Shengling Gao, Leyang Xue, Bnaya Gross, Zhikun She, Daqing Li, Shlomo Havlin

Abstract
The models of k k -core percolation and interdependent networks (IN) have been extensively studied in their respective fields. A recent study has revealed that they share several common critical exponents. However, several newly discovered exponents in IN have not been explored in k k -core percolation, and the origin of the similarity still remains unclear. Here, we investigate k-core percolation in random networks. We find that for k-core percolation, the fractality of the giant component fluctuations is manifested by a fractal fluctuation dimension, d ~ f = 3 / 4 \widetilde{d}_{f}=3/4 , within a correlation size N ′ N^{\prime} that scales as N ′ ∝ ( p − p c ) − ν ~ N^{\prime}\propto(p-p_{c})^{-\widetilde{\nu}} , with ν ~ = 2 \widetilde{\nu}=2 , same as found in IN. Indeed, here, ν ~ ≡ d ⋅ ν ′ \widetilde{\nu}\equiv d\cdot\nu^{\prime} and d ~ f ≡ d f ′ / d \widetilde{d}_{f}\equiv d^{\prime}_{f}/d , where ν ′ \nu^{\prime} and d f ′ d^{\prime}_{f} are respectively the same as the correlation length exponent and the fractal fluctuation dimension observed in d d -dimensional IN spatial networks. These two new exponents found here for k k -core percolation demonstrate the same scaling behaviors as found for IN with the same critical exponents, reinforcing the similarity between the two models. Furthermore, we suggest that these two models are similar since both have two types of interactions: short-range (SR) connectivity and long-range (LR) influences. In IN the LR are the influences of dependency links while in k-core we find here that for k = 1 k=1 and k = 2 k=2 the influences are short range while for k ≥ 3 k\geq 3 the influence is long range. In addition, analytical arguments for a universal hyper-scaling relation for the fractal fluctuation dimension of the k k -core giant component and for IN as well as for any mixed-order transition are established. Our analysis enhances the comprehension of k-core percolation and supports the generalization of the concept of fractal fluctuations in mixed-order phase transitions.

2.1
Short-range and long-range interactions in k-core percolation

The
k
-core is the maximal subgraph of the original graph, where every node in the
k
-core giant component has at least a degree
k

1

To obtain the
k
-core giant component of the original network,
we first remove randomly a fraction of
1-p
nodes from the original network.
We now iteratively remove nodes, where at each iteration all nodes with a degree below
k
are removed until there are no such nodes.
If a
k

N
nodes remains, we are above the transition
p_{c}
and if such a giant component does not exist, we are below
p_{c}

To test the range of influence, we randomly remove a node from the
k

where

By analyzing this distribution, we expect to distinguish between short range and long range interactions.
We plot in Fig.
1

1

the maximum shortest path between subsequently removed nodes and the initially removed
node, while the maximum possible propagation distance corresponds to the maximum shortest path between the nodes in the k-core giant component and the initially removed node.
Small ratios that decrease with network size correspond to short range (SR) interactions and large ratios which do not change with network size, correspond to long range (LR) interactions.
We can clearly see in Fig.

that for

4
Hyper-scaling relation for fluctuations in mixed-order transition

In this Section, we establish analytical arguments for a universal hyper-scaling relation for the fractal fluctuations dimension of the order parameter in a mixed-order transition in spatial networks of size,
N=L^{d}
, which also provides insight for non-spatial random networks.

We start with the scaling of the fraction of
k
-core giant component,
S
, close to criticality for both second-order and mixed-order transitions,

\displaystyle S(p)-S_{c}\propto(p-p_{c})^{\beta}.

(17)

Here, for second order transition
S_{c}=0
while for mixed order
S_{c}
is finite.
Next, we substitute the size (number of sites) of the giant component,
M_{c}
, at
p_{c}
and
M(p)
away (but closeby) from the critical threshold

\displaystyle\frac{M(p)}{L^{d}}-\frac{M_{c}}{L^{d}}\propto(p-p_{c})^{\beta}.

(18)

Based on the general case, Eq. (
18
),
one can obtain the well-known hyperscaling relation
for continuous second-order transitions
[
40
,
42
]
.
Substituting
M_{c}=0
and
M(p)\propto L^{d_{f}}
into Eq. (
18
),
yield
L^{d_{f}}/L^{d}\propto(p-p_{c})^{\beta}
and since
\xi\propto(p-p_{c})^{-\nu}
, the hyperscaling relation

\displaystyle d_{f}=d-\beta/\nu

(19)

is derived.

Different from the continuous phase transition case, in the mixed-order phase transition case, for Eq. (
18
), we evaluate instead the standard deviation of both sides

\displaystyle\sigma\left(\frac{M(p)}{L^{d}}-\frac{M_{c}}{L^{d}}\right)\propto\sigma\left((p-p_{c})^{\beta}\right).

(20)

Using the known relation
\sigma(X^{a})\sim X^{a-1}\sigma(X)

[
43
]
and assuming that the dominant variations between different realizations are in
p_{c}
and
M_{c}
, we get

\displaystyle\frac{\sigma\left(M_{c}\right)}{L^{d}}\propto(p-p_{c})^{\beta-1}\sigma\left(p-p_{c}\right).

(21)

Assuming,
\sigma\left(M_{c}\right)\propto L^{d^{\prime}_{f}}
, for
L<\xi^{\prime}
, we get

\displaystyle\frac{L^{d^{\prime}_{f}}}{L^{d}}\propto(p-p_{c})^{\beta-1}\sigma\left(p_{c}\right).

(22)

Substituting into Eq. (
22
) the scaling
\xi^{\prime}\propto(p-p_{c})^{-\nu^{\prime}}
and assuming that the variation of
p_{c}
are controlled by
\xi^{\prime}
, we obtain,

\displaystyle L^{d^{\prime}_{f}-d}=\xi^{\prime-\frac{\beta-1}{\nu^{\prime}}}\xi^{\prime-\frac{1}{\nu^{\prime}}}.

(23)

Taking the limit
L\to\xi^{\prime}
, we obtain,

\displaystyle\xi^{\prime d^{\prime}_{f}-d}=\xi^{\prime-\frac{\beta}{\nu^{\prime}}}.

(24)

Thus, deriving the new but analogous to Eq. (
19
) hyperscaling relation, Eq. (
3
), for the fractal fluctuations dimension,
d^{\prime}_{f}=d-\beta\nu^{\prime}
.

Note that the hyperscaling relations given by Eq. (
3
) (for mixed-order phase transitions) and Eq. (
19
) (for second-order phase transitions) are similar,
but the interpretation of the fractal dimensions
d^{\prime}_{f}
and
d_{f}
are different. For second-order continuous phase transitions,
d_{f}
describes the fractal dimension of the bulk
[
40
,
42
]
, while for mixed-order phase transitions,
d^{\prime}_{f}
describes the fractal dimension of the fluctuations of
M_{c}
, i.e., how
\sigma(M_{c})
, scales with
L
. Below the correlation
length
the fluctuations are fractals, but above they behave normally. Note that the hyperscaling relation, Eq. (
3
) holds for spatial networks of different dimension
d
. Correspondingly, it also holds for random networks like the ER networks.
Substituting Eq. (
6
) and Eq. (
9
) into Eq. (
3
), we can obtain the hyperscaling relation for random networks,

\displaystyle\widetilde{d}_{f}=1-\beta/\widetilde{\nu}.

(25)

As expected, the evaluated critical exponents
\beta=1/2
,
\widetilde{\nu}=2
and
\widetilde{d}_{f}=3/4
found here satisfy this hyperscaling relation, Eq. (
25
). Noteworthy, the hyperscaling relation Eq. (
3
) with identical exponents as found here for k-core, is also valid for interdependent networks
[
36
]
.

5
Discussions and Summary

In the present study, we investigate systematically the
k
-core percolation phase transitions in ER random networks to uncover the similarities of percolation in the k-core and interdependent networks models, along with identifying the possible underlying mechanisms for the similarities.
We examine several new critical exponents found recently in IN
[
36
]
, such as the critical exponents of fluctuations of
p_{c}
, representing the diverging correlation
size
,
N^{\prime}
, and of the order parameters in different realizations of size,
N
, representing the fluctuation fractal dimension.
The findings are summarized in Table 1, where the common critical exponents of
k
-core percolation and interdependent networks reveal intriguing similarities between the two models.

Our results suggest that the similarity of the criticality between the two systems (and probably to many other systems) originates from the two types of interactions that exist in both systems, one is the connectivity links which are short range and the other is the
k
-core
(k\geq 3)
or the dependency (large
\ell
) which are both long-range.
When plotting the distributions of distances
caused by a failure in different cores,
we found that for
k\geq 3
the interactions are LR, while for
k=1
and
k=2
the interactions are SR (see Fig.
1
).
Similar to
k
-core percolation, percolation results of the CD model again show that SR interactions exist for
\ell=0
and
\ell=1
and thus yield a second order phase transition,
while LR interactions, e.g.,
\ell=D
yields a mixed order phase transition.
In both models (and potentially also for other models) the mechanisms behind the different phase transitions seem the same, and short-range and long-range influences are the origin.
Moreover, we demonstrate that
the fractal fluctuations dimension of the order parameter in the mixed-order phase transition satisfies the hyperscaling relation,
d^{\prime}_{f}=d-\beta/\nu^{\prime}
.
Our study offers valuable insights into the striking similarities between
k
-core percolation and percolation in interdependent networks, shedding light on the phenomenon of fractal fluctuations dimension observed in mixed-order phase transitions.
