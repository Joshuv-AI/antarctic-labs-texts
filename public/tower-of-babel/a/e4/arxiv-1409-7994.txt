Randomness is valid at large numbers
Yeseul Kim, Byung Mook Weon

Abstract
Randomness is a central concept to statistics and physics. Here, a statistical analysis shows experimental evidence that tossing coins and finding last digits of prime numbers are identical regarding statistics for equally likely outcomes. This analysis explains why randomness in equally likely outcomes can be valid only at large numbers.

There are many examples for equally likely outcomes: representatively, coin tossing is believed to occur with a probability of 50

The frequency of each outcome (

N

R\sim N^{\alpha}
where

;
Frank
. Additionally, the range of relative frequency (
R/N
) between equally likely outcomes is defined as
R/N=(f_{i}^{max}{-}f_{i}^{min})
, which is equivalent to
(n_{i}^{max}{-}n_{i}^{min})/N
. From
R\sim N^{\alpha}
,
R/N
should have a simple relation as
R/N\sim N^{\beta}

\beta=\alpha{-}1

In conclusion, we introduced a simple expression for randomness at large numbers. From statistical analyses of coin tosses and last digits of primes, we showed that the range of relative frequency between equally likely outcomes (
R/N

R/N\sim N^{\beta}

N\rightarrow\infty

explains why randomness is valid only at large numbers. This result consequently supports that finding last digits of primes is intrinsically identical to tossing coins in statistics: both cases are the same problems of equally likely outcomes. Finally our finding of the power-law relation between the range of relative frequency among equally likely outcomes and the total number of repetition would be significant to understand the validity of randomness at large numbers (as known as the law of large numbers), which would be important in statistics, physics, and mathematics.
