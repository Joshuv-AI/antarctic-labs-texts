Generalized integrals and point interactions
Jan Dereziński 1, Christian Gaß 1 and Błażej Ruba 2

Abstract
First we recall a method of computing scalar products of eigenfunctions of a Sturm-Liouville operator. This method is then applied to Macdonald and Gegenbauer functions, which are eigenfunctions of the Bessel, resp. Gegenbauer operators. The computed scalar products are well defined only for a limited range of parameters. To extend the obtained formulas to a much larger range of parameters, we introduce the concept of a generalized integral. The (standard as well as generalized) integrals of Macdonald and Gegenbauer functions have important applications to operator theory. Macdonald functions can be used to express the integral kernels of the resolvent (Green functions) of the Laplacian on the Euclidean space in any dimension. Similarly, Gegenbauer functions appear in Green functions of the Laplacian on the sphere and the hyperbolic space. In dimensions 1,2,3 one can perturb these Laplacians with a point potential, obtaining a well defined self-adjoint operator. Standard integrals of Macdonald and Gegenbauer functions appear in the formulas for the corresponding Green functions. In higher dimensions the Laplacian perturbed by point potentials does not exist. However, the corresponding Green function can be generalized to any dimension by using generalized integrals.

Bilinear integrals of eigenfunctions of Sturm-Liouville operators

Consider a
Sturm-Liouville operator

\displaystyle{\mathcal{C}}:=-\rho(r)^{-1}\Big(\partial_{r}p(r)\partial_{r}+q(r)\Big)

acting on functions on an interval
]a,b[
.
{\mathcal{C}}
is formally

symmetric
for the bilinear scalar product with the
density

\rho
:

\displaystyle\langle f|g\rangle:=\int_{a}^{b}f(r)g(r)\rho(r)\mathrm{d}r.

Let us describe a method to compute the scalar product of two

eigenfunctions
of
{\mathcal{C}}
.

First, consider eigenfunctions
f_{i}
corresponding to two
distinct
eigenvalues

E_{i}
,
i=1,2
.
That is, suppose that
{\mathcal{C}}f_{i}=E_{i}f_{i}.

Then the following is true:

\displaystyle\int_{a}^{b}f_{1}(r)f_{2}(r)\rho(r)\mathrm{d}r

\displaystyle=\frac{{\mathcal{W}}(b)-{\mathcal{W}}(a)}{E_{1}-E_{2}}

(1)

\displaystyle\text{where }{\mathcal{W}}(r):=f_{1}(r)p(r)f_{2}^{\prime}(r)

\displaystyle-f_{1}^{\prime}(r)p(r)f_{2}(r)\text{ is the
Wronskian}.

(
1
) is sometimes called

Green’s identity
, or the
integrated Lagrange identity
.
Note that if
a,b
are
singular points
of the
corresponding differential equation, then the right hand side of
(
1
) can often be easily evaluated.

Using an appropriate limiting procedure we can also often
evaluate (
1
) for
f=f_{1}=f_{2}
:

\displaystyle\langle f|f\rangle=\int_{a}^{b}f(r)^{2}\rho(r)\mathrm{d}r.

(2)

Bilinear integrals of Macdonald and Gegenbauer functions

The following two families of Sturm-Liouville operators
are especially important for applications
[
17
,
14
,
13
]
:

\displaystyle\text{the Bessel operator:}\qquad{\mathcal{B}}_{\alpha}

\displaystyle:=-\frac{1}{r}\partial_{r}r\partial_{r}+\frac{\alpha^{2}}{r^{2}},

\displaystyle\text{and the Gegenbauer operator}:\quad{\mathcal{G}}_{\alpha}

\displaystyle:=-(1-w^{2})^{-\alpha}\partial_{w}(1-w^{2})^{\alpha+1}\partial_{w}.

The
modified Bessel equation
is the eigenequation
of
{\mathcal{B}}_{\alpha}

with eigenvalue
-1
. The
(standard) Bessel
equation
is its eigenequation for eigenvalue
1
.
The separation
of variables in the Laplacian
on the
Euclidean space

\mathbb{R}^{d}
leads to the Bessel operator
on
[0,\infty[
with density
\rho=2r
and
\alpha=\frac{d-2}{2}
.

The
Gegenbauer equation
is the eigenequation of

{\mathcal{G}}_{\alpha}
with eigenvalue

\lambda^{2}-\big(\alpha+\frac{1}{2}\big)^{2}
. The Gegenbauer operator
on
[-1,1]
with density
\rho(w)=(1-w^{2})^{\frac{d-2}{2}}

arises when we separate variables
in the Laplacian on the
sphere

\mathbb{S}^{d}
.
We obtain the Gegenbauer operator
on
[1,\infty[
with density
\rho(w)=(w^{2}-1)^{\frac{d-2}{2}}

when we separate variables
in the Laplacian on the
hyperbolic space

\mathbb{H}^{d}
.

Scaled
Macdonald functions

K_{\alpha}(br)
are exponentially decaying eigenfunctions of the Bessel operator with
eigenvalue
-b^{2}
.
Applying the above method for

a>0
,
b>0
we find

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}K_{\alpha}(ar)K_{\alpha}(br)2r\mathrm{d}r

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\pi\big((a/b)^{\alpha}-(b/a)^{\alpha}\big)}{\sin(\pi\alpha)(a^{2}-b^{2})},\quad\begin{array}[]{c}|\operatorname{Re}(\alpha)|<1,\\
\alpha\neq 0;\end{array}

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}K_{0}(ar)K_{0}(br)2r\mathrm{d}r

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{2\ln\frac{a}{b}}{a^{2}-b^{2}},

(6)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}K_{\alpha}(br)^{2}2r\mathrm{d}r

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\pi\alpha}{b^{2}\sin(\pi\alpha)},\qquad\begin{array}[]{c}|\operatorname{Re}(\alpha)|<1,\\
\alpha\neq 0;\end{array}

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}K_{0}(br)^{2}2r\mathrm{d}r

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{b^{2}}.

(10)

The first formula follows directly by Green’s identity.
The next three identities are obtained by
applying the
de l’Hôpital rule
to
\alpha=0
and
a=b
.

The identities (
2
)–(
10
) can be found in standard collections of
integrals, such as
[
9
]
.

The
Gegenbauer equation
is the special case of the hypergeometric
equation
with the symmetry
w\to-w
and the singular points at
-1,1,\infty
:

\displaystyle\Bigg((1-w^{2})\partial_{w}^{2}-2(1+\alpha)w\partial_{w}+\lambda^{2}-\Big(\alpha+\frac{1}{2}\Big)^{2}\Bigg)f(w)=0.

In the present context, the Gegenbauer equation is arguably more convenient
than the equivalent, but more frequently encountered
associated Legendre
equation
.

We will use two kinds of
Gegenbauer functions
: one
is characterized by its asymptotics
\sim\frac{1}{\Gamma(\alpha+1)}
at
1
:

\displaystyle{\bf S}_{\alpha,\pm\lambda}(w)

\displaystyle:=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{\big(\frac{1}{2}+\alpha+\lambda\big)_{j}\big(\frac{1}{2}+\alpha-\lambda)\big)_{j}}{\Gamma(\alpha+1+j)j!}\Big(\frac{1-w}{2}\Big)^{j},

where
(z)_{n}
is the
Pochhammer symbol.

The other has the asymptotics
\sim\frac{1}{w^{\frac{1}{2}+\alpha+\lambda}\Gamma(\lambda+1)}
at
\infty
:

\displaystyle\mathbf{Z}_{\alpha,\lambda}(w):

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(w\pm 1)^{\frac{1}{2}+\alpha+\lambda}}\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{\big(\frac{1}{2}+\lambda\big)_{j}\big(\frac{1}{2}+\lambda+\alpha\big)_{j}}{\Gamma(\lambda+1)(1+2\lambda)_{j}j!}\Big(\frac{2}{1\pm w}\Big)^{j}.

We note the identities

\displaystyle{\bf S}_{\alpha,\lambda}(w)={\bf S}_{\alpha,-\lambda}(w),

\displaystyle\quad{\bf Z}_{\alpha,\lambda}(w)=\frac{{\bf Z}_{-\alpha,\lambda}(w)}{(w^{2}-1)_{\bullet}^{\alpha}},

as well as the slightly more subtle
Whipple identity
:

\displaystyle{\bf Z}_{\alpha,\lambda}(w)

\displaystyle:=(w^{2}-1)_{\bullet}^{-\frac{1}{4}-\frac{\alpha}{2}-\frac{\lambda}{2}}{\bf S}_{\lambda,\alpha}\left(\frac{w}{(w^{2}-1)_{\bullet}^{\frac{1}{2}}}\right),

\displaystyle{\bf S}_{\alpha,\lambda}(w)

\displaystyle:=(w^{2}-1)_{\bullet}^{-\frac{1}{4}-\frac{\alpha}{2}-\frac{\lambda}{2}}{\bf Z}_{\lambda,\alpha}\left(\frac{w}{(w^{2}-1)_{\bullet}^{\frac{1}{2}}}\right),\qquad\operatorname{Re}(w)>0,

where
(w^{2}-1)_{\bullet}^{\alpha}:=(w-1)^{\alpha}(w+1)^{\alpha}
and we
use the principal branch of the power function.

Here are the basic bilinear integrals of Gegenbauer functions.
We assume
|\operatorname{Re}(\alpha)|<1
,
\alpha\neq 0
and
\operatorname{Re}(\lambda)>0
:

\displaystyle\int_{-2}^{2}{\bf S}_{\alpha,\mathrm{i}\beta_{1}}(w){\bf S}_{\alpha,\mathrm{i}\beta_{2}}(w)(1-w^{2})^{\alpha}\mathrm{d}2w

(11)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{2^{2\alpha+2}}{(\beta_{1}^{2}-\beta_{2}^{2})\sin\pi\alpha}\Big(\frac{\cosh(\pi\beta_{1})}{\Gamma(\frac{1}{2}+\alpha-\mathrm{i}\beta_{2})\Gamma(\frac{1}{2}+\alpha+\mathrm{i}\beta_{2})}-(\beta_{1}\leftrightarrow\beta_{2})\Big)

\displaystyle\int_{2}^{\infty}{\bf Z}_{\alpha,\lambda_{1}}(w){\bf Z}_{\alpha,\lambda_{2}}(w)(w^{2}-1)^{\alpha}\mathrm{d}2w

(12)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{2^{\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{2}+1}}{(\lambda_{1}^{2}-\lambda_{2}^{2})\sin\pi\alpha}\Big(\frac{1}{\Gamma(\frac{1}{2}-\alpha+\lambda_{1})\Gamma(\frac{1}{2}+\alpha+\lambda_{2})}-(\lambda_{1}\leftrightarrow\lambda_{2})\Big).

Applying the de l’Hôpital rule we extend these identities to
\alpha=0
,

\lambda_{1}=\lambda_{2}
, and
\beta_{1}=\beta_{2}
, see
[
7
]
.
In contrast to the well-known integrals over Macdonald functions, the integrals
(
11
) and (
12
) seem to be new.

3
Generalized integral

The integrals (
2
)–(
10
) and
(
11
)–(
12
) are divergent for
|\operatorname{Re}(\alpha)|\geq 1
.
Using the
generalized integral
,
(
2
)–(
10
) and (
12
) can be extended to
all
\alpha\in\mathbb{C}
, and (
11
) can be extended to

\operatorname{Re}(\alpha)>-1
. The concept of the generalized integral can be
traced back to Hadamard
[
10
,
11
]
and Riesz

[
16
]
; see
[
12
,
15
]
for modern expositions.

Note that several variations of the generalized integral are
possible. In our note we restrict ourselves to functions which
are non-integrable near a finite point and have a finite number
of homogeneous singularities at this point. In some
of the above references one can also find other variations of the
generalized integral applicable to functions which are non-integrable
near infinity and/or exhibit
almost homogeneous
singularities, i.e., singularities that are homogeneous up to powers of
logarithms.

Without loss of generality, we can put the singular point to 0. We then say
that a function

f
on
]0,\infty[
is
integrable in the generalized sense
if it is
integrable on
]1,\infty[
and if there exists a finite set
\Omega\subset\mathbb{C}

and
f_{k}\in\mathbb{C}
,
k\in\Omega
, such that

f-\sum\limits_{k\in\Omega}f_{k}r^{k}

is integrable on
]0,1[
. We define the
generalized integral
as

\displaystyle\mathrm{gen}\int_{0}^{\infty}f(r)\mathrm{d}r:=

\displaystyle\sum_{k\in\Omega\backslash\{-1\}}\frac{f_{k}}{k+1}+\int_{0}^{1}\Big(f(r)-\sum_{k\in\Omega}f_{k}r^{k}\Big)\mathrm{d}r+\int_{1}^{\infty}f(r)\mathrm{d}r.

For
f\in L^{1}[0,\infty[
the generalized and standard integrals
coincide:

\displaystyle\mathrm{gen}\int_{0}^{\infty}f(r)\mathrm{d}r=\int_{0}^{\infty}f(r)\mathrm{d}r.

If
f_{-1}\neq 0
, the generalized integral has a
scaling
anomaly
but it is always invariant with respect to a power transformation:

\displaystyle\mathrm{gen}\int_{0}^{\infty}f(r)\mathrm{d}r

\displaystyle=\mathrm{gen}\int_{0}^{\infty}f(\alpha u)\,\alpha\mathrm{d}u+f_{-1}\ln(\alpha),\quad

\displaystyle\qquad\mathrm{gen}\int_{0}^{\infty}f(r)\mathrm{d}r

\displaystyle=\mathrm{gen}\int_{0}^{\infty}f(u^{\alpha})\,\alpha u^{\alpha-1}\mathrm{d}u.

Under more general coordinate transformations
g(u)
, which are smooth and
preserve the leading scaling behavior of the integrand (i.e.,
g(0)=0
and

g^{\prime}(0)\neq 0
), the generalized integral transforms as

\displaystyle\mathrm{gen}\int_{0}^{\infty}f(g(u))g^{\prime}(u)\mathrm{d}u-\mathrm{gen}\int_{0}^{\infty}f(r)\mathrm{d}r

(13)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-f_{-1}\ln g^{\prime}(0)+\sum_{l\in(\mathbb{N}+1)\cap\Omega}\frac{f_{-l}}{(l-1)(l-1)!}\left.\frac{\mathrm{d}^{l-1}}{\mathrm{d}u^{l-1}}\left(\frac{u}{g(u)}\right)^{l-1}\right|_{u=0}.

That is to say, the generalized integral transforms non-trivially under such
transformations if there is an
n\in\mathbb{N}
such that
f_{-n}\neq 0
.
For this reason, we call the generalized integral
anomalous
if
f_{-n}\neq 0

for some
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

Non-anomalous generalized integrals have much better properties than
anomalous ones. They are often easy to compute: one just applies analytic
continuation.

A systematic analysis of properties of the generalized integral and a proof
of (
13
) can be found in
[
7
]
. To our knowledge,
the formula (
13
) is new.

4
Bilinear generalized integrals of Macdonald and Gegenbauer functions

Let
a,b>0
. For
\alpha\not\in\mathbb{Z}
generalized integrals
of Macdonald functions are analytic
continuations
of standard integrals:

\displaystyle\mathrm{gen}\int_{0}^{\infty}K_{\alpha}(ar)K_{\alpha}(br)2r\mathrm{d}r

\displaystyle=\frac{\pi}{\sin(\pi\alpha)}\frac{\big(\frac{a}{b}\big)^{\alpha}-\big(\frac{b}{a}\big)^{\alpha}}{a^{2}-b^{2}},

\displaystyle\mathrm{gen}\int_{0}^{\infty}K_{\alpha}(br)^{2}2r\mathrm{d}r

\displaystyle=\frac{\pi\alpha}{b^{2}\sin(\pi\alpha)}.

They have poles at
\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}
.

For
\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}
the generalized integrals are anomalous and
more complicated to compute. In particular, they do not coincide with
the
finite parts
of the above expressions:

\displaystyle\mathrm{gen}\int_{0}^{\infty}K_{\alpha}(ar)K_{\alpha}(br)

\displaystyle 2r\mathrm{d}r=(-1)^{\alpha}2\frac{\big(\frac{a}{b}\big)^{\alpha}\ln\big(\frac{a}{2}\big)-\big(\frac{b}{a}\big)^{\alpha}\ln\big(\frac{b}{2}\big)}{a^{2}-b^{2}}

\displaystyle\hskip-77.49976pt-\frac{(-1)^{\alpha}}{ab}\sum_{k=0}^{|\alpha|-1}\Big(\frac{a}{b}\Big)^{2k-|\alpha|+1}\big(\psi(1+k)+\psi(|\alpha|-k)\big);

\displaystyle\mathrm{gen}\int_{0}^{\infty}K_{\alpha}(br)^{2}2r\mathrm{d}r=

\displaystyle\frac{(-1)^{\alpha}}{b^{2}}\Big(|\alpha|\ln\big(\tfrac{b^{2}}{4}\big)+1+2|\alpha|\big(1-\psi(1+|\alpha|)\big)\Big).

Similarly we can be compute generalized bilinear
integrals of
{\bf S}_{\alpha,\mathrm{i}\beta}
,
{\bf Z}_{\alpha,\lambda}
. All these
formulas together with their derivations can be found in
[
7
]
.

5
Convergence of Gegenbauer functions and their integrals

As
\beta,\lambda\to\infty
, Gegenbauer functions converge to
Macdonald functions in the following sense:

\displaystyle\frac{\pi\mathrm{e}^{-\pi\beta}(\sin\theta)^{\alpha+\frac{1}{2}}}{2^{\alpha}\theta^{\alpha+\frac{1}{2}}}{\bf S}_{\alpha,\pm\mathrm{i}\beta}(-\cos\theta)

\displaystyle=(\theta\beta)^{-\alpha}K_{\alpha}(\beta\theta)\big(1+O(\beta^{-1})\big);

\displaystyle\frac{\sqrt{\pi}\Gamma(\tfrac{1}{2}-\alpha+\lambda)(\sinh\theta)^{\alpha+\frac{1}{2}}}{2^{\lambda+\frac{1}{2}}\theta^{\alpha+\frac{1}{2}}}{\bf Z}_{\alpha,\lambda}(\cosh\theta)

\displaystyle=(\lambda\theta)^{-\alpha}K_{\alpha}(\lambda\theta)\big(1+O(\lambda^{-1})\big).

The generalized integrals of Gegenbauer functions converge to
the corresponding generalized integrals of Macdonald functions:

\displaystyle\frac{\pi^{2}{\mathrm{e}}^{-2\pi\beta}\beta^{2\alpha}}{2^{2\alpha}}\;\mathrm{gen}\int_{-2}^{2}{\bf S}_{\alpha,\mathrm{i}\beta}(w)^{2}(1-w^{2})^{\alpha}\mathrm{d}2w=\Big(1+\mathcal{O}\big(\tfrac{1}{\beta}\big)\Big)\;\mathrm{gen}\int_{0}^{\infty}K_{\alpha}(\beta r)^{2}2r\mathrm{d}r;

\displaystyle\frac{\pi\Gamma\big(\tfrac{1}{2}+\alpha+\lambda\big)^{2}}{2^{2\lambda+1}\lambda^{2\alpha}}\;\mathrm{gen}\int_{2}^{\infty}{\bf Z}_{\alpha,\lambda}(w)^{2}(w^{2}-1)^{\alpha}\mathrm{d}2w=\Big(1+\mathcal{O}\big(\tfrac{1}{\lambda}\big)\Big)\;\mathrm{gen}\int_{0}^{\infty}K_{\alpha}(\lambda r)^{2}2r\mathrm{d}r.

The convergence of these generalized integrals
is straightforward in the non-anomalous case. In the anomalous
case one has to choose the variables carefully, which we did:

\displaystyle 2r\mathrm{d}r=\mathrm{d}r^{2},\quad 2(\cosh r-1)\simeq r^{2},\quad 2(1-\cos r)\simeq r^{2}.

Note that the generalized integral is invariant with respect to the change of
variables
r\to r^{2}
, but not with respect to scaling.
Proofs of these convergence statements can be found
in
[
7
]
.

6
Laplacian on the Euclidean space, the hyperbolic space and the sphere

In the remaining part of our manuscript we describe an application of
generalized integrals to operator theory. These applications will
involve point interactions of the Laplacian. We start by recalling
some basic information about Green functions of Laplacians. The following
formulas are well-known, confer for example
[
5
,
8
]
.

Consider the
Laplacian

\Delta_{d}
on the
Euclidean space

\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
Let
G_{d}(z;x,x^{\prime})
be the
Euclidean Green function
, that is
the integral kernel of the resolvent

(-z-\Delta_{d})^{-1}
. For
\operatorname{Re}\beta>0
, we have

G_{d}(-\beta^{2};x,x^{\prime})=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{\frac{d}{2}}}\Big(\frac{\beta}{|x-x^{\prime}|}\Big)^{\frac{d}{2}-1}K_{\frac{d}{2}-1}\big(\beta|x-x^{\prime}|\big).

The
hyperbolic space
is

\mathbb{H}^{d}:=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{1,d}\mkern 5.0mu|\mkern 5.0mu[x|x]=1\}

where

[x|y]=x^{0}y^{0}-x^{1}y^{1}-\dots-x^{d}y^{d}
is the
Minkowskian pseudoscalar product
.
The hyperbolic distance between
x,x^{\prime}\in\mathbb{H}^{d}
is given by

\cosh(r)=[x|x^{\prime}]
.

Let
\Delta_{d}^{\mathrm{h}}
denote the

Laplace-Beltrami operator
on
\mathbb{H}^{d}
and let

G_{d}^{\mathrm{h}}(z;x,x^{\prime})
be the
hyperbolic Green
function
, that is, the integral kernel of

\big(-z-\Delta_{d}^{\mathrm{h}}-\big(\tfrac{d-1}{2}\big)^{2}\big)^{-1}
. Then

G_{d}^{\mathrm{h}}\Big(-\beta^{2};x,x^{\prime}\Big)=\frac{\sqrt{\pi}\Gamma(\frac{d-1}{2}+\beta)}{\sqrt{2}(2\pi)^{\frac{d}{2}}2^{\beta}}{\bf Z}_{\frac{d}{2}-1,\beta}\big([x|x^{\prime}]\big).

The
unit sphere
is

\mathbb{S}^{d}:=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{1+d}\mkern 5.0mu|\mkern 5.0mu(x|x)=1\},

where
(x|y)=x^{0}y^{0}+x^{1}y^{1}+\dots+x^{d}y^{d}

is the
Euclidean scalar product
.
The spherical distance between
x,x^{\prime}\in\mathbb{S}^{d}
is given by

\cos(r)=(x|x^{\prime})
. Let
\Delta_{d}^{\mathrm{s}}
denote the

Laplace-Beltrami operator
on
\mathbb{S}^{d}
. Let

G_{d}^{\mathrm{s}}(z;x,x^{\prime})
be the
spherical Green
function
, that is, the integral kernel of

\big(-z-\Delta_{d}^{\mathrm{s}}+\big(\tfrac{d-1}{2}\big)^{2}\big)^{-1}
. Then

G_{d}^{\mathrm{s}}(-\beta^{2};x,x^{\prime})=\frac{\Gamma(\frac{d}{2}-\frac{1}{2}+\mathrm{i}\beta)\Gamma(\frac{d}{2}-\frac{1}{2}-\mathrm{i}\beta)}{2^{d}\pi^{\frac{d}{2}}}{\bf S}_{\frac{d}{2}-1,\mathrm{i}\beta}\big(-(x|x^{\prime})\big).

We remark that the integral kernels of spectral projections of
the discussed operators may be expressed explicitly in terms
of special functions of the same type as for resolvents, see e.g.
[
8
]
.

Let us also mention some basic properties of operators.
Let
H
be a self-adjoint operator and let
G(-\rho):=(\rho+H)^{-1}
be its resolvent. Then
G(-\rho)
satisfies

\displaystyle(H+\rho)G(-\rho)

\displaystyle={\mathchoice{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.5mul}{\rm 1\mskip-5.0mul}},

(14)

\displaystyle G(-\rho)^{*}

\displaystyle=G(-\overline{\rho}),

(15)

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\rho}G(-\rho)

\displaystyle=-G(-\rho)^{2}.

(16)

(

7
Laplacian with point interactions

Let us now try to define the Laplacian with a perturbation localized
in a single point. For simplicity, we display details only for the
case of the Euclidean space. We look for an operator

-\Delta_{d}^{\gamma}
, which is a self-adjoint extension of
-\Delta_{d}

restricted to
C_{\mathrm{c}}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\backslash\{0\})
. The
parameter
\gamma\in\mathbb{R}\cup\{\infty\}
will parametrize these
self-adjoint extensions.

Actually, instead
of
-\Delta_{d}^{\gamma}
it is more convenient to look for its resolvent

\displaystyle G_{d}^{\gamma}(-\rho)=(-\Delta_{d}^{\gamma}+\rho)^{-1}.

By the conditions (
14
), (
15
) and (
16
)
its integral kernel
G_{d}^{\gamma}(-\rho,x,x^{\prime})
should satisfy

\displaystyle(-\Delta_{x}+\rho)G_{d}^{\gamma}(-\rho,x,x^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\delta(x-x^{\prime}),\quad x\neq 0,

\displaystyle G_{d}^{\gamma}(-\rho,x,x^{\prime})

\displaystyle=G_{d}^{\gamma}(-\rho,x^{\prime},x),

\displaystyle\partial_{\rho}G_{d}^{\gamma}(-\rho,x,x^{\prime})

\displaystyle=-\int G_{d}^{\gamma}(-\rho,x,y)G_{d}^{\gamma}(-\rho,y,x^{\prime})\mathrm{d}y.

These conditions are solved by a
Krein-type resolvent

\displaystyle G_{d}^{\gamma}(-\rho,x,x^{\prime})

\displaystyle=G_{d}(-\rho,x,x^{\prime})+\frac{G_{d}(-\rho,x,0)G_{d}(-\rho,0,x^{\prime})}{\gamma+\Sigma_{d}(\rho)},

where

\partial_{\rho}\Sigma_{d}(\rho)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}G_{d}(-\rho,0,y)^{2}\mathrm{d}y,

and
\gamma
is an arbitrary constant.
In dimensions
d=1,2,3
the above integral is finite and we obtain

\Sigma_{d}(\beta^{2})=\begin{cases}-\frac{1}{2\beta}&d=1;\\
\frac{\ln(\beta^{2})}{4\pi}&d=2;\\
\frac{\beta}{4\pi}&d=3.\end{cases}

Thus we obtain formulas for the Green functions with a point
potential in dimensions
d=1,2,3
parametrized by a
real parameter
\gamma\in\mathbb{R}
:

G_{d}^{\gamma}(-\beta^{2};x,x^{\prime})=\begin{cases}\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-\beta|x-x^{\prime}|}}{2\beta}+\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-\beta|x|}\mathrm{e}^{-\beta|x^{\prime}|}}{(2\beta)^{2}\big(\gamma-\frac{1}{2\beta}\big)},&d=1;\\[8.61108pt]
\frac{K_{0}(\beta|x-x^{\prime}|)}{2\pi}+\frac{K_{0}(\beta|x|)K_{0}(\beta|x^{\prime}|)}{(2\pi)^{2}(\gamma+\frac{\ln\beta^{2}}{4\pi})},&d=2;\\[8.61108pt]
\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-\beta|x-x^{\prime}|}}{4\pi|x-x^{\prime}|}+\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-\beta|x|}\mathrm{e}^{-\beta|x^{\prime}|}}{(4\pi)^{2}|x||x^{\prime}|(\gamma+\frac{\beta}{4\pi})},&d=3.\end{cases}

For dimensions
d=1,3
we used the fact that for half-integer parameters
the Macdonald function reduces to elementary functions.
Of course, the above construction is well known from the literature

[
1
,
2
,
3
]

and often used in the physics literature.

The operators
-\Delta_{d}^{\gamma}
, strictly speaking, have no analogs
for
d\geq 4
.
However, the functions
G_{d}^{\gamma}(-\rho;x,x^{\prime})
can be generalized to
d\geq 4

using
generalized integrals
:

\partial_{\rho}\Sigma_{d}(\rho)=\frac{(\beta^{2})^{\frac{d}{2}-1}\pi^{\frac{d}{2}}}{(2\pi)^{d}\Gamma(\frac{d}{2})}\mathrm{gen}\int_{0}^{\infty}K_{\frac{d}{2}-1}(\sqrt{\rho}r)^{2}2r\mathrm{d}r.

We obtain

\Sigma_{d}(\beta^{2})=\begin{cases}\frac{(-1)^{\frac{d+1}{2}}\beta^{d-2}}{(4\pi)^{\frac{d-1}{2}}2(\frac{1}{2})_{\frac{d-1}{2}}}&d\text{ odd};\\
\frac{(-1)^{\frac{d}{2}+1}\beta^{d-2}}{(4\pi)^{\frac{d}{2}}\big(\frac{d}{2}-1\big)!}\left(2-2\psi\big(\tfrac{d}{2}\big)+\ln\tfrac{\beta^{2}}{4}\right)&d\text{ even}.\end{cases}

Thus for each dimension
d
we obtain a family of Green functions

\displaystyle G_{d}^{\gamma}(-\beta^{2};x,x^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{\frac{d}{2}}}\Big(\frac{\beta}{|x-x^{\prime}|}\Big)^{\frac{d}{2}-1}K_{\frac{d}{2}-1}\big(\beta|x-x^{\prime}|\big)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d}}\Big(\frac{\beta^{2}}{|x||x^{\prime}|}\Big)^{\frac{d}{2}-1}\frac{K_{\frac{d}{2}-1}(\beta|x|)K_{\frac{d}{2}-1}(\beta|x^{\prime}|)}{\gamma+\Sigma_{d}(\beta^{2})}.

(17)

describing point interaction of strength controlled by the
parameter
\gamma
.

A similar analysis can be performed for the hyperbolic and spherical
Green functions in all dimensions
[
8
]
.

One can ask what is the meaning of

G_{d}^{\gamma}(-\beta^{2};x,x^{\prime})
in dimensions
d\geq 4
, for wich it is
not the kernel of a bounded operator (and in particular, not a kernel
of a resolvent of a self-adjoint operator). Our expectation is as follows.
Suppose that
V
is a
potential
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
\mathbb{H}^{d}
or
\mathbb{S}^{d}
, possibly strong but with a small
support. Consider the
the
Schrödinger operator

-\Delta_{d}+V
.
Let

G^{V}(-\beta^{2})=(\beta^{2}-\Delta_{d}+V)^{-1}

be its resolvent with the integral kernel

G^{V}(-\beta^{2};x,x^{\prime})
. Then far from the support of
V

we can approximate
G^{V}(-\beta^{2};x,x^{\prime})
by
G^{\gamma}(-\beta^{2};x,x^{\prime})

as in (
17
),
possibly adding to
\Sigma_{d}(\beta^{2})

a polynomial in the energy
of degree
<\frac{d-1}{2}
if
d
is odd and
<\frac{d-2}{2}
if
d

is even. Thus possible infrared behaviors of
G^{V}(-\beta^{2};x,x^{\prime})
are
controlled by a finite number of parameters (coefficients of the above
mentioned polynomials).

The choice given by the zero polynomial that is defined by the above
generalized integrals can be viewed as a “standard reference point”.
The generalized integral computed in different coordinates, which are
related to
r^{2}
by a well-behaved coordinate transformation in the sense of
(
13
), will yield a different choice of polynomial.

The observation that a well-behaved change of variables will, by (
13
), only change this polynomial is particularly important
in the curved cases, where we chose
2(\cosh r-1)
, resp.
2(1-\cos r)
as
integration variables instead of
r^{2}
.

This is analogous to the renormalization of quantum quantum field theory using
dimensional regularization, see e.g.
[
4
]
.

The above situation resembles the idea often expressed
in the context of
quantum field theory
and of
the theory of
critical phenomena
, attributed to Keneth Wilson: for large distances
correlation functions have a
universal behavior
independent of the
details of the interaction, described by few parameters.
