Fluorescence quenching in plasmonic dimers due to electron tunneling
Henrikh M. Baghramyan 1, Cristian Ciracì 1

Abstract
Plasmonic nanoparticles provide an ideal environment for the enhancement of fluorescent emission. On the one hand, they locally amplify the electromagnetic fields, increasing the emitter excitation rate, and on the other hand, they provide a high local density of states that accelerates spontaneous emission. However, when the emitter is placed in close proximity to a single metal nanoparticle, the number of nonradiative states increases dramatically, causing the fluorescence to quench. It has been predicted theoretically that, through a judicious placing of the emitter, fluorescence in plasmonic nanocavities can be increased at monotonically. In this article, we show that such monotonic increase is due to the use of local response approximation in the description of the plasmonic response of metal nanoparticles. We demonstrate that taking into account the electron tunneling and the nonlocality of the surrounding system via the quantum hydrodynamic theory results eventually in a quenching of fluorescence enhancement also when the emitter is placed in a nanocavity, as opposed to local response and Thomas-Fermi hydrodynamic theory results. This outcome marks the importance of considering the quantum effects, in particular, the electron tunneling to correctly describe the emission effects in plasmonic systems at nanoscale.



I
Introduction

A quantum emitter in an excited states can undergo a decay by emitting a photon into one of the modes provided by the surrounding electromagnetic environment
Purcell et al. 1946
;
Goy et al. 1983
.
Due to plasmonic effects, metal nanoparticles (NPs) can provide a high local density of electromagnetic states in their vicinity.
The emission of a quantum emitter than can be highly amplified at the proximity of plasmonic NPs
Tam et al. 2007
.
Similarly, the fields of an external electromagnetic wave can get localized and enhanced at the metal NP surface well beyond the diffraction limit.
A quantum emitter placed in the vicinity of a metal NP then can also be more easily excited
Novotny and Hecht 2012
.
These two processes – the excitation and the emission – contribute to the fluorescence of the emitter
Lakowicz 2006a
.
Plasmonic-enhanced fluorescence has several applications
Gaponenko 2015
, spanning through biomedicine
Lakowicz 2006b
;
Lakowicz et al. 2006
;
Puerto et al. 2021
, sensors
Li et al. 2015
;
Langer et al. 2015
, quantum information
Fernández-Domínguez et al. 2018
and bionanotechnolgies
Darvill et al. 2013
. In particular, a hybrid plasmonic nanoantenna has been suggested in
Yang et al. 2020a
for the efficient enhancement of emission directionality and keeping high quantum efficiency.
Also, more than 100 times fluorescence enhancements have been observed in
Cruz et al. 2020
, which is critical for readout by inexpensive point-of-care detectors.

At very short distances from a single metal NP, however, fluorescence quenches since the emitter couples mostly to nonradiative modes
Dulkeith et al. 2002
;
Kühn et al. 2006
;
Anger et al. 2006
;
Bharadwaj and Novotny 2007
.
To suppress plasmonic quenching, one could place the emitter in a nanocavity
Faggiani et al. 2015
;
Kongsuwan et al. 2018a
;
Kongsuwan et al. 2018b
;
Yang et al. 2020b
;
Cuartero-González and Fernández-Domínguez 2020
;
Cuartero-González and Fernández-Domínguez 2018
.
In the cavity formed by an NP coupled to a metal film, for example, quenching disappears because higher-order modes gain a radiative nature while the excitation rate increases
Kongsuwan et al. 2018b
;
Yang et al. 2020b
.
Within the local response approximation (LRA), it is predicted that, as the NP-film distance is reduced, the fluorescence rate monotonically increases, and quenching never occurs
Kongsuwan et al. 2018b
;
Yang et al. 2020b
.
This, however, could be the result of a simplistic description rather than a physical consequence.
In fact, local theories do not account for the nonlocal nature of the dielectric function of the metal at short distances
Ciracì et al. 2012
;
Raza et al. 2013
;
Gonçalves et al. 2020
.
Indeed, the hydrodynamic theory (HT) for the plasmonic response predicts bound to the maximum field enhancement achievable in nanocavities
Ciracì et al. 2012
.
This is obtained by considering the electron pressure term when evaluating the permittivity of the metal. The pressure term originates from the Thomas-Fermi (TF) kinetic energy (KE)
E. Fermi 1927
;
Thomas 1927
.
However, the TF-HT is usually used with hard-wall boundary conditions at the metal surface, which does not allow electrons to spill outside of the metal
Raza et al. 2011
;
Ciracì et al. 2013
. In addition, TF KE is only a local functional of the density, i.e. it depends on the value of the electron density at the point considered.

In Ref.
Tserkezis et al. 2017
, the authors consider the generalized nonlocal optical response (GNOR) by accounting for both, nonlocal response and surface-enhanced Landau damping, and, in fact, predict a reduction of the fluorescence enhancement as compared to LRA calculations. However, even using the GNOR description there is no evidence of quenching.
A more accurate approach would require taking into account the KE dependence on higher-order derivatives of the density.
The quantum hydrodynamic theory (QHT) adds such correction to the TF contribution, namely the von Weizäcker (vW) KE term
C. F. von Weizsäcker 1935
, which is equivalent to the quantum potential
Bohm 1952a
;
Bohm 1952b
and depends on the gradient of the electron density.
In the framework of TF and vW KE functionals, the spatial dependence of the electron density in the metal is naturally taken into account and spill-out, and electron tunneling effects can be easily considered in the calculation of plasmon energy
Toscano et al. 2015
;
Ciracì and Della
Sala 2016
while still retaining retardation effects
Ciracì and Della
Sala 2016
.
Nonetheless, the QHT in the TF-vW approximation may result in unstable resonances due to the propagation nature of the asymptotic solutions at high energies
Ciracì and Della
Sala 2016
;
Baghramyan et al. 2021
.
This behaviour can be amended by considering a Laplacian level correction in the KE
Constantin et al. 2018
;
Baghramyan et al. 2021
.

In this article, we apply the QHT to evaluate the fluorescence enhancement of the emitter in the gap of the Na jellium sphere dimer in the weak coupling regime.
We show that for small enough gaps (
\approx 0.5
nm) the QHT predicts fluorescence quenching, as opposed to the LRA
Kongsuwan et al. 2018b
;
Yang et al. 2020b
calculations where the fluorescence enhancement monotonically increases as the gap closes.
Our formalism allows distinguishing the two quantum corrections that are often discussed in the literature: namely, spatial dispersion (or nonlocality) and electronic spill-out. In this paper, we show that the former does not produce any significant quenching
Christensen et al. 2014
;
Tserkezis et al. 2016
;
Tserkezis et al. 2017
;
Jurga et al. 2017
, while the latter does. Therefore, both mechanisms manifest in fluorescence in a very different way.
Finally, we analyze the impact of Laplacian-level KE correction to the fluorescence spectra.

After calculating the plasmonic response of the dimer to the incident radiation, and hence the field that excites the emitter, we proceed to the second step that is the evaluation of the decay rates.

The rate of spontaneous emission is a sum of the radiative and nonradiative decays, i. e.
\gamma_{\rm sp}=\gamma_{\rm r}+\gamma_{\rm nr}
, and can be found from the relation:

\gamma_{\mathrm{sp}}=\frac{2\pi^{2}c}{\hbar\varepsilon_{0}}\rho_{0}\left[{\mathbf{p}}\cdot\operatorname{Im}\{\mathbf{G}(\mathbf{r}_{e},\mathbf{r}_{e})\}\cdot{\mathbf{p}}\right],

where

\mathbf{p}

\mathbf{E_{\rm{II}}}(\mathbf{r},\omega)=\mu_{0}\omega^{2}\mathbf{G}\left(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}_{e}\right)\cdot\mathbf{p}_{c},

(9)

\mathbf{p}_{c}=2\mathbf{p}

2

the radiated power in vacuum. Having found

q=\frac{\gamma_{\mathrm{r}}}{\gamma_{\mathrm{sp}}}.

(11)

\eta_{em}=\eta_{ex}q.

(12)

In this article, we will consider the following cases with increasing complexity in the description of the electron dynamics in the Na spheres:

I:

LRA, where the metal is described through the conventional Drude model, corresponding to
G\left[n\right]=0
in Eq. (
2b

II:

TF-HT, in which we consider the TF pressure term in the hydrodynamic equation, i.e.
G\left[n\right]=T_{\rm TF}[n]
;

III:

QHT, where we account for the vW KE and XC corrections,
G\left[n\right]=T_{\rm TF}[n]+T_{\rm vW}[n,w]+E_{\rm XC}[n]

IV:

QHT-PGSL, in which the KE functional of the QHT is improved by adding the PGS and Laplacian terms, i. e.,
\tau=\tau^{\mathrm{vW}}+\tau^{\mathrm{PGS}}+\tau^{\mathrm{TF}}\beta q_{r}^{2}
;

V:

QHT-PGSLN, where we add the Laplacian-dependent logarithm correction to the QHT-PGSL, i. e.,
\tau=\tau^{\mathrm{vW}}+\tau^{\mathrm{PGS}}+\tau^{\mathrm{TF}}\left[\beta q_{r}^{2}+2\beta q_{0}^{2}\ln\left(1+q_{r}/q_{0}\right)\right]

III
Methods

The system of Eqs. (
2
), where
{\bf E}_{0}
is either a plane wave or a dipole filed, is solved using a finite element method through a customized implementation into a commercial software.
Moreover, to reduce the computational cost, we take advantage of the symmetry of the system, by expanding the fields in a series of cylindrical harmonics, each of which can propagate independently
Ciracì et al. 2013
;
Khalid and Ciracì 2019
.
Following this method, it is possible to reduce a three-dimensional calculation to few two-dimensional ones.
In fact, because the systems considered here are very small it is possible to limit the maximum azimuthal number
|m_{max}|\leq 1
.
Note that since the field associated with a vertically oriented dipole is axially symmetric, the emission properties can be exactly evaluated by only considering the azimuthal number
m=0

Ciracì et al. 2019
. More detail on the FEM implementation is given in the Appendix Section
A
.

As the gap size shrinks,
\eta_{ex}
reaches very high values for the LRA and TF-HT compared to the QHT as expected.
In addition, for QHT, we observe a drop at very small values of the gap since the field is mostly concentrated off the location of the emitter (see Fig.

An opposite behavior is observed for the quantum yield.
The smaller the gaps, the closer the emitter is to the NPs.
At short distances, the evanescent field of the emitter can more easily coupled to plasmonic nonradiative modes channeling more energy toward Ohmic losses, and so reducing the quantum yield.
Note that the nonmonotonic behavior in

Having seen singularly the behavior of

For gaps

and TF-HT

;
Tserkezis et al. 2016
;

;

Namely, the maximum fluorescence enhancement is found in the correspondence of the dipolar plasmon resonance (DPR).
At smaller gaps (
g\simeq 0.5

This result however is not general and should be material-dependent. We would like to mention that charge transfer modes can be observed in dimers, but they appear at energies below 1 eV

and are not present in the spectral range shown in Figs.
4

5

As the gap closes, in fact, the DPR is shifted to much longer wavelengths for which Na ohmic losses increase, and both quantum yield and field enhancement reduce (see Fig. S1 and Fig. S2 in the Supplemental Material (SM).
This is clearly visible for the LRA, although the TF-HT and QHT show similar results but with more spread-out resonances due to the effect of nonlocality.
Note that for a fixed gap, increasing the NP radius has the same effect as reducing the gap

This is formally true for the LRA in the quasi-static limit, where spectra are exactly the same except for an overall factor (related to the size of the cavity)

Aubry et al. 2010

At

In addition, the LRA predicts almost more than an order of magnitude higher values as compared to the TF-HT and QHT. Also, the LRA results in more peaks than the TF-HT and QHT for fixed radius. This could be attributed to the fact that the

The key difference of the QHT from the LRA and TF-HT is the quenching of fluorescence at gap sizes below

The QHT then predicts the maximum fluorescence enhancement to occur at

V
Conclusions and Future Perspectives

To conclude, we demonstrated that the consideration of nonlocality and tunneling in the framework of the QHT is crucial for the correct evaluation of fluorescence enhancement in plasmonic sphere dimers.
While the LRA predicts increasing enhancement
Kongsuwan et al. 2018b
;
Yang et al. 2020b
as the NPs distance is decreased, the use of the QHT results in the quenching of the fluorescence of the emitter. In addition, only accounting for the nonlocality in the framework of the TF-HT still results in an increasing rate of fluorescence.
Results similar to the QHT are obtained if the second-order derivatives of the density are included in the definition of the KE functional associated to the electron dynamic in the metallic volumes in the form of PGSL and PGSLN functionals. The fluorescence quenching happens at approximately
g=0.5
nm for all scenarios.
These results should also be valid for similar systems, such as the NP on film
Kongsuwan et al. 2018b
.

More generally, our method has allowed to introduce a fundamental mechanism for fluorescence quenching, which marks the limits for the local and hydrodynamic theories.
Even though our work is purely theoretical, experimental validation of such mechanism could be in principle achieved by using analogous larger scale systems, such as a heavily-doped semiconductor systems, or by using single atom emitters at cryogenic temperatures, by exploiting the strong gradients of the electric field at the dimer gap in order to create an optical trap. Another possibility, is that of exploiting the weaker transverse dipole of traditional molecules, which would allow the molecule to lay flat in the gap, thus reaching distances below
\sim 0.5
nm.

Although we have considered only quantum effects in the dielectric response of the NPs, while assuming a point-dipole approximation for the emitter, we believe that equally important contribution could be introduced by taking into account the spatial extension of the quantum emitter beyond the point-dipole approximation
Neuman et al. 2018
and the quantum nature of light
Feist et al. 2020
.
