Classifying Tree Topology Changes along Tropical Line Segments
Shelby Cox

Abstract
. The space of phylogenetic trees arises naturally in tropical geometry as the tropical Grassmannian. Tropical geometry therefore suggests a natural notion of a tropical path between two trees, given by a tropical line segment in the tropical Grassmannian. It was previously conjectured that tree topologies along such a segment change by a combinatorial operation known as Nearest Neighbor Interchange (NNI). We provide counterexamples to this conjecture, but prove that changes in tree topologies along the tropical line segment are either NNI moves or “four clade rearrangement” moves for generic trees. In addition, we show that the number of NNI moves occurring along the tropical line segment can be as large as n 2 n^{2} , but the average number of moves when the two endpoint trees are chosen at random is O ⁡ ( n ​ ( log ⁡ n ) 4 ) O(n(\log n)^{4}) . This is in contrast with O ⁡ ( n ​ log ⁡ n ) O(n\log n) , the average number of NNI moves needed to transform one tree into another.



1.
Introduction

Phylogenetic trees are leaf-labelled, metric trees that encode evolutionary relationships between a collection of organisms. In this paper, phylogenetic trees are also rooted. The root represents the common ancestor of the organisms, the leaves represent present day organisms, and the lengths of the edges represent the amount of mutation that has occurred. The space of phylogenetic trees with
n

\mbox{Tree}_{n}

The space

Under a certain embedding,

Question 1.1

How do tree topologies (i.e. tree structures) change along the tropical line segment?

2.
Background

2.1.
Tropical Semi-ring

The tropical semi-ring turns out to be a good place to work with trees. We will work in the tropical max-plus semi-ring
\left(\mathbb{R}\cup\{-\infty\},\oplus,\odot\right)
, where tropical addition and multiplication are given by
\max
and classical addition, respectively:

a\oplus b:=\max\{a,b\},~~~a\odot b:=a+b.

Note that
-\infty
is the identity element for addition, and
0
is the identity element for multiplication. Tropical division is classical subtraction; tropical additive inverses usually do not exist. Geometrically, we will work in the tropical projective space
\mathbb{R}^{n}/\mathbb{R}\mathbbm{1}
, where
\mathbbm{1}=(1,1,\ldots,1)
is the all 1’s vector.

Definition 2.1

(
[
12
]
)
.

The
tropical convex hull
of
u_{1},\ldots,u_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is the collection of all tropical linear combinations of the
u_{i}
:

\mbox{tconv}(u_{1},\ldots,u_{k})=\{\lambda_{1}\odot u_{1}\oplus\cdots\oplus\lambda_{k}\odot u_{k}\mid\lambda_{i}\in\mathbb{R}\}.

Note that if
u_{i}^{\prime}=\alpha_{i}\odot u_{i}
, then

\displaystyle\mbox{tconv}(u_{1},\ldots,u_{k})

\displaystyle=\{\lambda_{1}\odot u_{1}\oplus\cdots\oplus\lambda_{k}\odot u_{k}\mid\lambda_{i}\in\mathbb{R}\}

\displaystyle=\{(\lambda_{1}-\alpha_{1})\odot(\alpha_{1}\odot u_{1})\oplus\cdots\oplus(\lambda_{k}-\alpha_{k})\odot(\alpha_{k}\odot u_{k})\mid\lambda_{i}\in\mathbb{R}\}

\displaystyle=\{\lambda_{1}^{\prime}\odot u_{1}^{\prime}\oplus\cdots\oplus\lambda_{k}^{\prime}\odot u_{k}^{\prime}\mid\lambda_{i}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}\}

\displaystyle=\mbox{tconv}(u_{1}^{\prime},\ldots,u_{k}^{\prime})

i.e., the tropical convex hull is well defined in the tropical projective torus,
\mathbb{R}^{m}/\mathbb{R}\mathbbm{1}
.

A tropical line segment is the tropical convex hull of two points
u
,
v
. It is a concatenation of at most
n-1
classical (i.e. Euclidean) line segments, and it can be computed with the algorithm in
2.2
.

Definition 2.2

(Algorithm for the Tropical Line Segment)
.

(1)

input:

u,v\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

(2)

\Lambda(u,v)=[u_{i}-v_{i}\mid i\in[n]]
, sorted from least to greatest.

(3)

w_{i}=u\oplus(\lambda_{i}\odot v)
, where
\lambda_{i}
is the
i
th smallest element of
\Lambda
.

(4)

output:
the concatenation of Euclidean line segments from
w_{i}
to
w_{i+1}
.

Definition 2.3
.

In the algorithm above,
\Lambda(u,v)=\{u_{i}-v_{i}\mid i\in[n]\}
is the
set of turning point scalars
, and
w_{i}
is a
turning point
for the tropical line segment from
u
to
v
.

Example 2.4
.

Let

and

\displaystyle u\oplus(-2\odot v)

\displaystyle=(3,3,1)\oplus(1,0,1)=(3,3,1)

\displaystyle=u

\displaystyle u\oplus(0\odot v)

\displaystyle=(3,3,1)\oplus(3,2,3)=(3,3,3)

\displaystyle=(0,0,0)

\displaystyle u\oplus(1\odot v)

\displaystyle=(3,3,1)\oplus(4,3,4)=(4,3,4)

A

Definition 2.6

Given two leaves

We are especially interested in

Definition 2.7

A phylogenetic tree is an

For rooted equidistant trees, the height of an internal vertex is well-defined and it will be useful to have a notation for the height of an internal vertex.

Definition 2.8

Let

h_{T}(x)=\frac{1}{2}d_{T}(i,j)

We may associate to each phylogenetic tree

u^{T}

u=u^{T}=(d_{T}(1,2),d_{T}(1,3),\ldots,d_{T}(n-1,n))\in\mathbb{R}^{\binom{n}{2}}.

This is called the

Conversely, given a vector in
\mathbb{R}^{n\choose 2}
, we want to know when it is a vector coming from the leaf distances on a phylogenetic tree, and when it is a vector coming from the leaf distances on an equidistant tree. These are the four point and three point conditions, respectively:

Theorem 2.9

(Four Point Condition
[
13
]
)
.

u\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{n\choose 2}
is the tree metric for a phylogenetic tree

\max\{u_{ij}+u_{kl},u_{ik}+u_{jl},u_{il}+u_{jk}\}
is achieved at least twice for all distinct
i,j,k,l\in[n]
.

Theorem 2.10

(Three Point Condition
[
13
]
)
.

u\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{n\choose 2}
is the tree metric for an equidistant tree
T
if
\max\{u_{ij},u_{ik},u_{jk}\}
is achieved at least twice for all distinct
i,j,k\in[n]
.

It will be convenient when counting trees later to add a leaf labelled 0 adjacent to the root
\rho
, so that all internal vertices of a generic tree have degree exactly 3. However, we will not include distances to the 0 leaf in the tree metric vector.

Definition 2.11
.

An
ultrametric
is a vector in
\mathbb{R}^{n\choose 2}
satisfying the three-point condition.

Remark 1
.

An ultrametric
u\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{n\choose 2}
is a metric on
[n]
.

We will often refer to an equidistant tree by giving its tree metric as a vector (an ultrametric) in
\mathbb{R}^{n\choose 2}
. Furthermore, it is natural to view ultrametrics in a tropical projective space, i.e. adding
\lambda\mathbbm{1}
to the ultrametric vector doesn’t change the structure of the equidistant tree. When we add
\lambda\mathbbm{1}
to the ultrametric vector, the change in the corresponding equidistant tree is a change in the external (leaf-adjacent) edge lengths.

The following theorem is essential to our study of tropical tree space:

Theorem 2.12

(
[
13
]
)
.

The space of ultrametrics is tropically convex.

This means that the tropical line between two ultrametrics (or equidistant trees) stays in the space of ultrametrics.

2.3.
The Space of Phylogenetic Trees

By

, we mean the combinatorial structure of a tree as a leaf-labelled graph. We will define it in three ways: using edge splits (BHV definition), using the argmax function (metric definition), and finally using a fan structure on the tropical Grassmannian (tropical definition). Starting with the BHV definition:

Definition 2.13
.

Given a phylogenetic tree
T

Definition 2.14

(BHV Tree Topology)

Two trees have the same

We can also use the tree metric to determine the tree topology. If

which achieve the

S
. Formally, we define the

Definition 2.15

Let
\mbox{argmax}_{u}(S)=\{s\in S:u_{s}=\max_{x\in S}u_{x}\}
for any
S\subseteq\binom{[n]}{2}
.

Definition 2.16
.

Two phylogenetic trees
u

v
have the same

\mbox{argmax}_{u}\{ij,ik,jk\}=\mbox{argmax}_{v}\{ij,ik,jk\}

Finally, we define tree topology using a fan structure on the tropical Grassmannian. A tropicalized linear space has many fan structures. Here we consider the fan structure first described by Speyer and Sturmfels in

Definition 2.17

Two equidistant trees

(1)

\mbox{argmax}_{u^{1}}\{ij,ik,jk\}=\mbox{argmax}_{u^{2}}\{ij,ik,jk\}

\displaystyle=\{u\in\mathbb{R}^{n\choose 2}/\mathbb{R}\mathbbm{1}\mid\max(u_{ij},u_{ik},u_{jk})\text{ is achieved at least twice for }i<j<k.\}

A

The NNI graph for

The NNI graph is a well-studied graph. In 1997, DasGupta et al showed that computing shortest paths in the NNI graph is NP-complete

Another type of move that can occur along the tropical line segment is the four clade rearrangement move, where four branches may come together at once. An example is given in

Redefining Turning Points for Trees

We begin by reinterpreting the turning point scalars in the tropical line segment algorithm (

Definition 3.1

Given a pair of phylogenetic trees

\Pi(T_{1},T_{2}):=\{(x_{1},x_{2})\mid x_{k}\in T_{k},\exists i,j\in[n],i\neq j\mbox{ s.t. }x_{k}=\mbox{LCA}_{T_{k}}(i,j)\}

Definition 3.2

An equidistant tree

Let

\lambda_{ij}=2(h_{T_{1}}(x_{1})-h_{T_{2}}(x_{2})),\text{ where }x_{k}=\mbox{LCA}_{T_{k}}(i,j)

Conversely, given internal vertices

\mu_{x_{1}x_{2}}=2(h_{T_{1}}(x_{1})-h_{T_{2}}(x_{2}))

∎

4.
The Tropical Line and NNI

Now we come to the first main question of the paper: what kinds of tree topology changes can occur along the tropical line segment between two trees

and
T_{2}
?

It turns out that even for a generic pair of trees, there may be a turning point which is not a single NNI. For example, the endpoint trees in

In this section, we prove that when the endpoint trees are chosen to be sufficiently general, the only moves that can occur are: a single NNI, or a single four clade rearrangement.

4.1.
Possible Turning Points

The main theorem for this section is the following:

Theorem 4.1

At each turning point of the tropical line segment between a generic pair of trees, the intermediate tree takes one of the following three forms:

(1)

(2)

(3)

a generic tree, (remaining in a top-dimensional cell), see

6.
Expected Length of the Tropical Line Segment

Now that we know tree topologies along the tropical line segment change by single NNI or four clade rearrangement moves, we can ask how many NNI moves occur along the tropical line segment between two randomly chosen trees. The goal of this section is to prove
Theorem

6.1
bounding the number of turning points. A pair of randomly chosen phylogenetic trees on
[n]
leaves means that the topologies of the trees are uniformly randomly chosen from the (finitely many) binary tree topologies on
[n]
leaves, and the corresponding ultrametrics form a generic pair.

Theorem 6.1
.

The expected number of turning points of the tropical line segment between two randomly chosen phylogenetic trees on
n
leaves is
O\left(n\left(\log(n)\right)^{4}\right)
.

Corollary 6.2
.

The expected tropical NNI number between two randomly chosen phylogenetic trees on
n
leaves is
O\left(n\left(\log(n)\right)^{4}\right)
.

6.1.
Sample spaces, Notation, and Equivalence of Probabilities

We want to bound the number of turning points of the tropical line segment between two phylogenetic trees on
n
leaves. After a series of translations, we reduce this to a question on the space of unlabelled rooted binary planar trees with one marked internal vertex.

Definition 6.3
.

A rooted tree is

if the children of each vertex are assigned an ordering. For rooted binary planar trees, we will refer to the
left child
and
right child
of an internal vertex, and denote the descendants of the left (respectively right) child by

(resp.

and

We will consider the following sample spaces of trees:

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{[n]}

\displaystyle=\{T:T\text{ is a rooted binary tree with }n\text{ labeled leaves}\}.

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{[n]}^{\text{planar}}

\displaystyle=\{T:T\text{ is a planar rooted binary planar trees with }n\text{ labeled leaves}\}.

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{[n],v}^{\text{planar}}

\displaystyle=\{(T,v):T\in\mathcal{T}_{[n]}^{\textup{planar}},v\text{ is an internal vertex of }T\}.

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{n}^{\text{planar}}

\displaystyle=\{T:T\text{ is a rooted binary planar tree with }n\text{ unlabeled leaves}\}.

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{n,v}^{\text{planar}}

\displaystyle=\{(T,v):T\in\mathcal{T}_{n}^{\textup{planar}},v\text{ is an internal vertex of }T\}.

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{n,v}^{\text{planar}}(a,b)

\displaystyle=\{(T,v)\in\mathcal{T}_{n,v}^{\textup{planar}}:\ell(v)=a,r(v)=b\}.

We now re-frame the number of turning points in terms of the last tree sample space above. This proposition makes it clear that the number of turning points is reduced from the naive bound of

only because different leaf pairs

can have the same common ancestor in

Lemma 6.12
.

Let
A_{1}
and
A_{2}
be subsets of
[n]
, chosen uniformly at random from all subsets of size
a_{1}
and
a_{2}
respectively. Then,

P(A_{1}\cap A_{2}\neq\emptyset)\leq\frac{a_{1}a_{2}}{n}

Proof.

The probability that
1\in A_{1}\cap A_{2}
is
(a_{1}/n)\cdot(a_{2}/n)
. Thus, the probability that at least one element of
[n]
lies in the intersection of
A_{1},A_{2}
is at most
n(a_{1}/n)(a_{2}/n)=a_{1}a_{2}/n
.
∎

Corollary 6.13

Q(a_{1},b_{1},a_{2},b_{2})\leq\min\left(\frac{a_{1}a_{2}}{n},\frac{b_{1}b_{2}}{n}\right)\leq\frac{(a_{1}a_{2}b_{1}b_{2})^{1/2}}{n}

\tilde{Q}(a_{1},b_{1},a_{2},b_{2};n):=\frac{(a_{1}a_{2}b_{1}b_{2})^{1/2}}{n}.

Proposition 6.15
.

(12)

S_{n}\leq\left(\phantom{.}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a+b\leq n\\
a,b\geq 1\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{\left(a-\frac{1}{2}\right)\left(b-\frac{1}{2}\right)\left(c+\frac{1}{4}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}}\right)^{2}.

Proof.

We plug in the bounds we computed in
6.11

6.13

allows us to express the sum in six variables as the square of a sum in three variables.

\displaystyle S_{n}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a_{i}+b_{i}+c_{i}=n\\
a_{i},b_{i}\geq 1,c_{i}\geq 0\end{subarray}}Q(a_{1},b_{1},a_{2},b_{2})P(a_{1},b_{1};n)P(a_{2},b_{2};n)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a_{i}+b_{i}+c_{i}=n\\
a_{i},b_{i}\geq 1,c_{i}\geq 0\end{subarray}}\tilde{Q}(a_{1},b_{1},a_{2},b_{2};n)P(a_{1},b_{1};n)P(a_{2},b_{2};n)

\displaystyle=\left(\phantom{.}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a+b\leq n\\
a,b\geq 1\end{subarray}}\tilde{Q}_{0}(a,b;n)P(a,b;n)\right)^{2}

Now we expand and bound the terms of the sum in the square above using the bound on

\displaystyle\tilde{Q}_{0}(a,b;n)P(a,b;n)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{a^{\frac{1}{2}}b^{\frac{1}{2}}}{\sqrt{n}}\frac{\sqrt{n}}{a\left(a-\frac{3}{4}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}b\left(a-\frac{3}{4}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\left(c+\frac{1}{4}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{a^{\frac{1}{2}}\left(a-\frac{3}{4}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}b^{\frac{1}{2}}\left(b-\frac{3}{4}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\left(c+\frac{1}{4}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}}

We can simplify further by applying the AM-GM inequality:

S_{n}\leq\left(\phantom{.}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a+b\leq n\\

Lemma 6.16
.

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a+b=d\\
a,b\geq 1\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{\left(a-\frac{1}{2}\right)\left(b-\frac{1}{2}\right)}\leq\frac{2(2+\log\left(d-\frac{3}{2}\right))}{d-1}.

Proof.

First, when
a+b=d
we have:

\frac{1}{\left(a-\frac{1}{2}\right)\left(b-\frac{1}{2}\right)}=\frac{1}{d-1}\left(\frac{1}{a-\frac{1}{2}}+\frac{1}{b-\frac{1}{2}}\right).

Also,

\sum_{a=1}^{d-1}\frac{1}{a-\frac{1}{2}}=2+\frac{1}{2-\frac{1}{2}}+\cdots+\frac{1}{d-\frac{3}{2}}\leq 2+\log\left(d-\frac{3}{2}\right).

It follows that

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a+b=d\\
a,b\geq 1\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{\left(a-\frac{1}{2}\right)\left(b-\frac{1}{2}\right)}=\frac{1}{d-1}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a+b=d\\
a,b\geq 1\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{a-\frac{1}{2}}+\frac{1}{b-\frac{1}{2}}=\frac{2}{d-1}\sum_{a=1}^{d-1}\frac{1}{a-\frac{1}{2}}\leq\frac{2(2+\log\left(d-\frac{3}{2}\right))}{d-1}.

∎

Proof.

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a+b\leq n\\

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{c=0}^{n-2}\frac{2\left(2+\log\left(n-c-\frac{3}{2}\right)\right)}{\left(c+\frac{1}{4}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}(n-c-1)}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{c=0}^{n-2}\frac{2\left(2+\log\left(n-c-1\right)\right)}{\left(c+\frac{1}{4}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}(n-c-1)}

\displaystyle(\ast)

The sequence
\left(c+\frac{1}{4}\right)^{-\frac{1}{2}}
is decreasing in

(2+\log(n-c-1))/(n-c-1)

]

\displaystyle\sum_{c=0}^{n-2}\frac{1}{\left(c+\frac{1}{4}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}}\frac{2\left(2+\log\left(n-c-1\right)\right)}{(n-c-1)}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{n-1}\left(\sum_{c=0}^{n-2}\left(c+\frac{1}{4}\right)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\right)\left(\sum_{c=0}^{n-2}\frac{2(2+\log(n-c-1))}{(n-c-1)}\right)

\displaystyle\sim\frac{1}{n-1}\left(\sum_{c=0}^{n-2}\left(c+\frac{1}{4}\right)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\right)\left(\sum_{c=0}^{n-2}\frac{\log(n-c-1)}{(n-c-1)}\right)

7.
Conclusion

This paper classifies the tree topology changes that can occur along the tropical line segment between two trees, which is an important step towards interpreting statistical learning models in the space of ultrametrics. We also provide a bound for the average number of topology changes on the tropical line segment between two trees, which allows us to roughly compare distance in the NNI graph to the number of tree topology changes occurring along the tropical line segment. However, this bound is likely not tight. In particular, we suspect that the bound we employ for
Q(a_{1},b_{1},a_{2},b_{2};n)
in
6.13
can be improved. Nonetheless, this is a step towards comparing tree topology changes over geodesics in different realizations of
\mbox{Tree}_{n}
.
