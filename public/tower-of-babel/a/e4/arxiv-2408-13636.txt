Gravity or turbulence? VI. The physics behind the Kennicutt-Schmidt relations
Javier Ballesteros-Paredes, Manuel Zamora-Avilés, Carlos Román-Zúñiga, Aina Palau, Bernardo Cervantes-Sodi, Karla Gutiérrez-Dávila, Vianey Camacho, Eric Jiménez-Andrade, and Adriana Gazol

Abstract
We explain the large variety of star formation laws in terms of one single, simple law that can be inferred from the definition of the star formation rate and basic algebra. The resulting equation, SFR = ϵ ff ​ M collapse / τ ff {\rm SFR}={\epsilon_{\mathrm{ff}}}\ M_{\mathrm{collapse}}/{\tau_{\rm ff}} , although it has been presented elsewhere, is interpreted in terms of clouds undergoing collapse rather than being turbulence-supported, an idea that different groups have pursued this century. Under such assumption, one can explain the constancy of ϵ ff {\epsilon_{\mathrm{ff}}} , the different intra-cloud correlations observed in Milky Way’s molecular clouds, as well as the resolved and unresolved extragalactic relationships between SFR and a measurement of the mass in CO, HCN, and CO+HI. We also explain why the slope of the correlation changes when the orbital time τ orb {\tau_{\rm orb}} is considered instead of the free-fall time, and why estimations of the free-fall time from extragalactic observations skew the correlation, providing a false sublinear correlation. We furthermore show that the apparent nearly linear correlation between the star formation rate and the dynamical equilibrium pressure in the midplane of the galaxies, P DE P_{\rm DE} , is just a consequence of P DE P_{\rm DE} values being dominated by the variation of the column density of molecular gas. All in all, we argue that the star formation law is driven by the collapse of cold, dense gas, which happens to be primarily molecular in the present Universe, and that the role of stellar feedback is just to shut down the star formation process, not to shape the star formation law.

2
The fundamental equation of star formation

As summarized by
Genzel
et al. (2010)
, a typical approach to explain the Kennicutt-Schmidt relation is to assume that the volume density of star formation rate,
\rho_{\mathrm{SFR}}
, scales linearly with the ratio between the volume density of the gas,
\rho_{\mathrm{gas}}
and the free-fall time,
{\tau_{\rm ff}}

(
Schmidt, 1959
;
Kennicutt, 1998
;
Krumholz &
McKee, 2005
)
. Furthermore, noticing that

{\tau_{\rm ff}}=\bigg(\frac{3\pi}{32G\rho}\bigg)^{1/2}

(7)

(with
G
the universal gravitational constant and
\rho
the mass density), one could postulate

\rho_{\mathrm{SFR}}\propto\rho_{\mathrm{gas}}^{3/2}.

(8)

There are two problems with this approach. On the one hand, the relationship between
\rho_{\mathrm{SFR}}
and the ratio
\rho_{\mathrm{gas}}/{\tau_{\rm ff}}
is a postulate, but there is no formal proof. On the other hand, observations show a correlation between column densities, not volume densities. Thus, eq. (
8
) is valid simultaneously with eq. (
2
) for
N\sim 3/2
only if all the observed regions have the same depth along the line of sight. Although, in principle, one can accept that galaxy disks have similar scale heights, it is not necessarily true, provided their large dynamical range of masses, sizes, and surface brightness.

2.1
Derivation

By definition, the star formation rate is the time derivative of the mass of newborn stars,

\mathrm{SFR}=\frac{dM_{*}}{dt}.

(9)

Given the impossibility of estimating the instantaneous star formation rate observationally, astronomers have estimated the mean star formation rate by averaging over a finite arbitrary time interval
\Delta t
,

\langle{\mathrm{SFR}}\rangle_{\Delta t}=\frac{1}{\Delta t}\int_{\Delta t}\frac{dM_{*}}{dt}dt=\frac{M_{*,\Delta t}}{\Delta t}

(10)

This general equation can be applied to any timescale
\Delta t
. In particular,
some authors have assumed that there is a characteristic timescale for star formation,
{\tau_{\mathrm{YSO}}}
, depending on the young stellar objects (YSOs) under scrutiny used to estimate the star formation rate
3
3

3

In the estimations of the star formation rate for local molecular cloud studies,
{\tau_{\mathrm{YSO}}}\sim 2
Myr
(
Evans
et al., 2009
;
Heiderman et al., 2010
;
Lada
et al., 2010
)
when Class 0, I and II YSOs are considered, but
{\tau_{\mathrm{YSO}}}\sim 0.3
–0.5 Myr if only Class 0 and I YSOs are involved in the scrutiny
(
Lada et al., 2013
;
Lombardi et al., 2014
;
Lada et al., 2017
;
Pokhrel
et al., 2021
, e.g.,)
.
, such that one can estimate the star formation rate as

\langle{\mathrm{SFR}}\rangle_{\tau_{\mathrm{YSO}}}=\frac{{M_{\mathrm{*,{\tau_{\mathrm{YSO}}}}}}}{{\tau_{\mathrm{YSO}}}},

(11)

where we have explicitly written the subscript
{\tau_{\mathrm{YSO}}}
to indicate that these quantities, and the mass converted into stars, have been computed by integrating over the characteristic star formation timescale.

Since we want to relate this quantity to the mass in gas, and since the units of the star formation rate are mass per unit of time, we now multiply and divide this equation by the mass in gas,
M_{\mathrm{gas}}
, and a characteristic timescale of the gas,
\tau
. Thus, defining
\epsilon
as the ratio between this time and the depletion time,

\epsilon\equiv\frac{\tau}{\tau_{\rm depl}},

(12)

where the depletion time is the time to exhaust the available mass
M_{\mathrm{gas}}
by forming stars at the rate
{M_{\mathrm{*,{\tau_{\mathrm{YSO}}}}}}/{\tau_{\mathrm{YSO}}}
, and is given by

\tau_{\rm depl}=\frac{M_{\mathrm{gas}}}{{M_{\mathrm{*,{\tau_{\mathrm{YSO}}}}}}/{\tau_{\mathrm{YSO}}}},

(13)

we obtain,

\langle{\mathrm{SFR}}\rangle_{\tau_{\mathrm{YSO}}}=\epsilon\ \bigg(\frac{M_{\mathrm{gas}}}{\tau}\bigg).

(14)

This equation is not new. It has already been proposed heuristically in previous works
(
Silk, 1997
;
Elmegreen, 1997
;
Tan, 2000
;
Elmegreen, 2002
;
Genzel
et al., 2010
;

;
Elmegreen, 2018
;
Pokhrel
et al., 2021
, e.g.,)
. The relevance here is that it follows mathematically from the very definition of the star formation rate. We now notice the following points:

1.

Eq. (
14
) is general. Thus, it is valid for any gas mass
M_{\mathrm{gas}}
(molecular, atomic, or even ionized

4

In deriving eq. (

, where

If

If a linear function between (a measurement of)

2.2
Is collapse the reason for the constancy of
{\epsilon_{\mathrm{ff}}}
?

Although
{\epsilon_{\mathrm{ff}}}
varies over two orders of magnitude, from
{\epsilon_{\mathrm{ff}}}\sim 3\times
10^{-3}
to 0.3
(see Fig. 10 in
Krumholz et al., 2019
, and references therein)
, it is frequently assumed that it is nearly constant, around
{\epsilon_{\mathrm{ff}}}\sim 0.01
–0.03
(
Krumholz &
McKee, 2005
;
Krumholz
et al., 2012
;
Elmegreen, 2018
)
. In particular,
Pokhrel
et al. (2021)
found
{\epsilon_{\mathrm{ff}}}\sim 0.026
with a spread of 0.2 dex for a set of different clouds in the Solar Neighbrohood. In principle, one can show that if MCs are collapsing and thus undergoing Jeans fragmentation, then the expected values for
{\epsilon_{\mathrm{ff}}}
do not vary too much for a large sample of clouds. For this purpose, we first notice that the total mass in newborn stars can be estimated as the product of the total number of stars multiplied by their mean mass,

{M_{\mathrm{*,{\tau_{\mathrm{YSO}}}}}}={N_{\mathrm{*,{\tau_{\mathrm{YSO}}}}}}\langle{M_{*}}\rangle.

(18)

Thus, the ‘efficiency per free-fall time’ (
16
) can be rewritten in terms of two constants: the mean mass of the newborn star,
\langle{M_{*}}\rangle
, and the timescale in which the stars are formed. The justification for the first one is that the initial mass function is nearly universal and has a characteristic mass
\langle{M}\rangle\sim 0.45

M_{\odot}

(
Kirkpatrick
et al., 2024
)
. The justification for the second one is that the different evolutionary stages of a protostar have their characteristic timescales. For instance, if the objects to count are YSOs in general, the assumed characteristic timescale is
\sim
2 Myr. In contrast, when considering embedded protostellar objects, the characteristic timescale is of the order of 0.5 Myr
(
Evans
et al., 2009
;

;
Lada
et al., 2010
;
Lada et al., 2013
;
Pokhrel
et al., 2021
, e.g.,)
.

Having
\langle{M_{*}}\rangle
and
{\tau_{\mathrm{YSO}}}
constant, we notice that the requirement for keeping

constant, is that the factor

\frac{N_{*,{\tau_{\mathrm{YSO}}}}}{M_{\mathrm{gas}}}{\tau_{\rm ff}}

(19)

remains constant. Let us assume now that we have a super-Jeans, collapsing, nearly isothermal molecular cloud. After a given time, this cloud will form some number
N_{*}
of stars. Suppose now that we vary the system’s mass by an arbitrary factor
f
, keeping the same density (and thus, the same free-fall time). In that case, there will also be a factor
f
of difference in the number of Jeans masses and, thus, one can expect, statistically speaking
5
5

5

By “statistically speaking” we mean that different realizations of the same experiment may have different outcomes, but the number of protostars oscillates around a mean characteristic value.
, that the number of protostars will also vary by the same factor
f
, such that the ratio (
19
) remains constant. Mathematically, if the gas is super-Jeans and collapsing, one can expect the number of stars formed in a gravitationally-dominated cloud to be proportional to the inverse of the Jeans mass
(
Bate &
Bonnell, 2005
;
Palau
et al., 2015
;
Morii
et al., 2024
, see, e.g., )
,

N_{*}\propto N_{\rm Jeans}=\frac{M_{\mathrm{gas}}}{M_{\rm Jeans}},

(20)

with

M_{\rm Jeans}\sim 2~M_{\odot}\ \bigg(\frac{c_{s}}{0.2~\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}}\bigg)^{3}\ \bigg(\frac{n}{10^{3}~\mathrm{cm^{-3}}}\bigg)^{-1/2}.

(21)

Since also
{\tau_{\rm ff}}\propto n^{-1/2}
, then the ratio (
19
) should not depend on the density, confirming the constancy of
{\epsilon_{\mathrm{ff}}}
, provided the velocity dispersion
c_{s}
is constant.

We can make the reverse thought experiment. For this purpose, let us now take the same cloud, adding a factor
f
in mass to maintain the same density. But now let us assume that the added gas is strongly stirred by supersonic turbulence with some virial parameter
\alpha_{\rm vir}>2
. In that case, the number of stars that will be formed in the same free-fall time will depend on
{{\alpha_{\rm vir}}}
, as shown by, e.g.,
Padoan et al. (2012)
and
Kim
et al. (2021)
. Consequently, the ‘efficiency per free-fall time’
{\epsilon_{\mathrm{ff}}}
would not remain constant.

In what follows, a basic point to understand the different correlations is that the dense and cold gas collapses faster than the less dense and/or warmer gas. Thus, although the gas does not need to be molecular to collapse
(
Clark &
Glover, 2014
)
, molecular gas tends to be colder and denser and then to collapse faster. Consequently, since clouds are density segregated
(
Alfaro &
Román-Zúñiga, 2018
;
Morii
et al., 2023
, e.g., )
, in the presence of both cold, dense molecular gas and warmer and less dense atomic gas, the former will collapse faster and dominate the star formation. In a sense, molecular gas is a proxy for cold and dense gas prone to collapse, and consequently, it has to have a better correlation with the star formation than the warmer gas.

We notice that considering tracers with different characteristic densities and free-fall timescales hides the fundamental law of star formation (
15
). Indeed, in a several-tracer measurement, the gas that mainly drives the correlation is the dense and cold, typically the molecular gas. Thus, including additional gas that will not end up forming stars will only hide the star formation law. In fact, it is worth noticing how the clear linear correlations between the
{\rm SFR}
and the mass in molecular gas become messy when adding atomic gas
(
Bigiel et al., 2008
, e.g., )
.

3
The Galactic K-S correlations as a consequence of the fundamental law

The demonstration shown in §
2.2
allows us to understand the variety of correlations observed for the Milky Way and external galaxies quoted in§
1
with a single explanation, as we discuss in what follows.

3.1
The intra-cloud
\Sigma_{\rm{SFR}}-\Sigma_{\rm gas}/{\tau_{\rm ff}}
correlation

The correlation

\Sigma_{\rm{SFR}}={\epsilon_{\mathrm{ff}}}\ \bigg(\frac{\Sigma_{\rm gas}}{{\tau_{\rm ff}}}\bigg)

(22)

observed for individual clouds by
Pokhrel
et al. (2021)
, follows directly from the fundamental equation of star formation since it is obtained from eq. (
15
) by simply dividing it by the projected area of the cloud. In this case, the constant of proportionality is the ‘efficiency per free-fall time’.

3.2
The Lada’s inter-cloud correlation
{N_{\rm YSO}}-M_{\mathrm{dense~gas}}

The correlation,

{{N_{\rm YSO}}\propto M_{\mathrm{dense~gas}}}

(23)

shown by
Lada
et al. (2010)
, is also a direct consequence of the fundamental law of star formation. The demonstration is simple. We first notice that in Solar Neighborhood’s clouds studies
(
Evans
et al., 2009
;
Heiderman et al., 2010
;
Lada
et al., 2010
;
Gutermuth et al., 2011
;
Lombardi
et al., 2013
;
Lombardi et al., 2014
;
Lada et al., 2017
;
Pokhrel
et al., 2021
, e.g., )
, observers typically estimate the mass in young stellar objects as described in eq.
18
, i.e., as the number of observed young stellar objects
{N_{\rm YSO}}
multiplied by their mean mass,
\langle{M_{*}}\rangle

(
Kennicutt &
Evans, 2012
, see also)
. In this case, the fundamental law of star formation can be written as:

\langle{\mathrm{SFR}}\rangle_{\tau_{\mathrm{YSO}}}=\frac{{N_{\rm YSO}}\,\langle{M_{*}}\rangle}{{\tau_{\mathrm{YSO}}}}={\epsilon_{\mathrm{ff}}}\ \bigg(\frac{M_{\mathrm{collapse}}}{{\tau_{\rm ff}}}\bigg),

(24)

with
\langle{M_{*}}\rangle
and
{\tau_{\mathrm{YSO}}}
being constant for clouds under scrutiny.
Furthermore, according to
Lada
et al. (2010)
, the clouds in that study have nearly the same volume density. If so, these clouds should have nearly the same free-fall timescale
{\tau_{\rm ff}}
. Therefore, in the equation

{N_{\rm YSO}}=\bigg(\frac{{\epsilon_{\mathrm{ff}}}\,{\tau_{\mathrm{YSO}}}}{\langle{M_{*}}\rangle\,{\tau_{\rm ff}}}\bigg)\,M_{\mathrm{collapse}},

(25)

the quantities inside the parenthesis are constant as long as
{\epsilon_{\mathrm{ff}}}
remains constant.

Thus, if the clouds under analysis are collapsing, then the correlation
{N_{\rm YSO}}\propto M_{\mathrm{gas}}
follows from the constancy of the ‘efficiency per free-fall time’. In a sense, this correlation is an example of our very deduction of the constancy of
{\epsilon_{\mathrm{ff}}}
, since different collapsing clouds may exhibit different amounts of Jeans masses.

3.3
The Galactic intra-cloud
\Sigma_{\rm{SFR}}-\Sigma_{\rm gas}^{2}
correlation for most local clouds, but
\Sigma_{\rm{SFR}}-\Sigma_{\rm gas}^{3.3}
for California MC

The previous correlations are nothing but the direct application of eq. (
15
). We now want to understand the fact that, internally, most molecular clouds in the Solar Neighborhood do exhibit a power-law correlation between their surface densities of star formation and gas mass (eq.
4
), where most of the clouds show a power-law index of
N=2

(
Pokhrel
et al., 2021
;
Spilker et al., 2021
)
, but the California MC exhibits
N=3.3
. The understanding of these correlations is more subtle since we want to make them work simultaneously along with eq. (
22
), and thus, it involves understanding how clouds’ physical properties are measured and how they are related to the internal structure of the cloud through its column density. Indeed, as
Lada et al. (2013)
showed, “the cloud’s structure plays a fundamental role in setting the level of its star formation activity.”

With this idea in mind, we now want to find a relation between the power-law index of the column density PDF and the power-law index of the KS relation. Let us assume then that
{\epsilon_{\mathrm{ff}}}
is constant. We first notice that since

{\tau_{\rm ff}}\propto\rho^{-1/2}\propto\bigg(\frac{M}{L^{3}}\bigg)^{-1/2},

(26)

the KS relation (
22
) can be rewritten in terms of the mass
M
and size
L
of the cloud, quantities that are directly read from the maps at different levels of the hierarchy:

\Sigma_{\rm{SFR}}\propto{\epsilon_{\mathrm{ff}}}\frac{M^{3/2}}{L^{7/2}}.

(27)

We further notice that if the column density of star formation rate is found to be proportional to some power-law of the column density of the gas,
\Sigma_{\rm{SFR}}\propto\Sigma_{\rm gas}^{N}
, then

\frac{M^{3/2}}{L^{7/2}}\propto\frac{M^{N}}{L^{2N}}

(28)

Eq. (
28
) imposes a constraint on the observed mass-size relation,

M\propto L^{{\alpha_{1}}}

(29)

which is

{\alpha_{1}}=\frac{7-4N}{3-2N}.

(30)

We can explore further by recalling that the area
S
(and thus its size
L\propto S^{1/2}
) and the cloud’s mass
M
depend on the column density PDF of the cloud,
P(\Sigma_{\rm gas})
as

S(\Sigma_{\rm gas,0})\propto\int_{\Sigma_{\rm gas,0}}^{\infty}P(\Sigma_{\rm gas})d\Sigma_{\rm gas}

(31)

and

M(\Sigma_{\rm gas,0})\propto\int_{\Sigma_{\rm gas,0}}^{\infty}\Sigma_{\rm gas}\ P(\Sigma_{\rm gas})\ d\Sigma_{\rm gas},

(32)

with
\Sigma_{\rm gas,0}
the column density contour above which we define the cloud
(
Lombardi
et al., 2010
;
Ballesteros-Paredes et al., 2012
, see)
. Assuming that the column density PDF
P(\Sigma_{\rm gas})
of collapsing clouds is a power-law
(
Kainulainen et al., 2009
;
Kritsuk
et al., 2011
;
Ballesteros-Paredes
et al., 2011b
;
Ballesteros-Paredes et al., 2012
;
Girichidis et al., 2014
)
, and labeling
-q
the power-law index,

P(\Sigma_{\rm gas})\ d\Sigma_{\rm gas}=\bigg(\frac{\Sigma_{\rm gas}}{\Sigma_{\rm gas,*}}\bigg)^{-q}d\Sigma_{\rm gas}

(33)

where
\Sigma_{\rm gas,*}
is only a normalization constant, one can prove that

{M}\propto L^{\alpha_{2}},

(34)

with

{\alpha_{2}}=\frac{4-2q}{1-q}.

(35)

Thus, eqs. (
30
) and (
35
) give us the relation between the power-law index of the KS relation,
N
, and the power-law index of the column density PDF,
q
, of a given cloud:

q=4N-5.

(36)

We can now get back to the KS correlations found in local clouds. On the one hand, with the possible exception of Cygnus-X and AFGL-490, each cloud in the
Pokhrel
et al. (2021)
sample exhibits a quite straight
\Sigma_{\rm{SFR}}\propto\Sigma_{\rm gas}^{2}
correlation, as well as a quite constant ‘efficiency per free-fall time’ (see their Figs. 2 and 3) as a function of the column density. Given the fact that these clouds follow the KS-relation with
N=2
, eq. (
36
) tells us that the power-law index of the PDF of these clouds must be
q=-3
. This is precisely the index found for the column density PDF of some of them, as shown by
Lombardi et al. (2014)
for Orion A and B clouds, and by
Zari et al. (2016)
for the Perseus molecular cloud. We speculate, thus, that the PDF of the column density for the remaining clouds (with the possible exception of Cygnus-X and AFGL-490, see above) observed with Herschel in the
Pokhrel
et al. (2021)
study must follow nearly the same trend.

Let us now analyze the California cloud. Interestingly, this cloud is not present in the analysis of
Pokhrel
et al. (2021)
, even though it is one of the closest and better-known clouds, and it has been observed by
Herschel
. Fortunately, both the column density PDF and the KS relation of this cloud were studied by these authors
(
Lada et al., 2017
)
. They found a KS-type relation with
N=3.3
, i.e.,
\Sigma_{\rm{SFR}}\propto\Sigma_{\rm gas}^{3.3}
. This implies that if the ‘efficiency per free-fall time’ were constant, the power-law index of its PDF should be
q=8.2
, substantially steeper than the actual value of
q=3
reported by
Lada et al. (2017)
. This implies that the California molecular cloud must not have a constant ‘efficiency per free-fall time’ as a function of column density but an efficiency that should increase with column density.

We speculate that the California cloud must have a non-constant ‘efficiency per free-fall time’, and that this is consistent with the cloud being a more diffuse, more extended, and less dense cloud than, e.g., Orion A cloud, as found by
Rezaei Kh. &
Kainulainen (2022)
. Indeed, the cloud has a mass comparable to Orion’s A or B clouds
(
Lada
et al., 2010
, e.g.,)
. Still, Gaia analyses of the distances to the stars associated with California and Orion complexes suggest that the California cloud is substantially more extended in the line of sight and, thus, substantially less dense. Furthermore, its star formation activity is substantially smaller than in other clouds of the
Pokhrel
et al. (2021)
sample, hosting only one significant stellar group, LkH
\alpha
-101, in addition to a few sparse YSOs
(
Lada et al., 2017
)
. Thus, one can expect that not all the gas in the California cloud is as gravitationally bound and, thus, not necessarily involved in the process of collapse. If this is the case, there is no reason for the ‘efficiency per free-fall time’ to be constant with column density.

3.4
The lack of a correlation between
\Sigma_{\rm{SFR}}
and
\Sigma_{\rm gas}
for local clouds observed at a given column density threshold

Lada et al. (2013)
have shown that there is no correlation between the column densities of star formation rate and molecular gas mass for a set of clouds in the Solar Neighbrohood. These results appear to be at odds with their own correlation between the
{N_{\rm YSO}}\propto{\rm SFR}
and
M_{\mathrm{gas}}
discussed above, as well as with the existence of a
\Sigma_{\rm{SFR}}\propto\Sigma_{\rm gas}^{2}
correlation for the inner structure of molecular clouds studied by
Pokhrel
et al. (2021)
.
On the contrary, at the same column density, different clouds have a
\Sigma_{\rm{SFR}}
that varies about one order of magnitude among them. The reason
for this has been discussed by
Krumholz
et al. (2012)
. At a given column density threshold, MCs may very well have different masses and densities. Thus, the star formation rate should have a natural scatter at every column density. In other words, the lack of a correlation found by
Lada et al. (2013, see their fig. 8)
is nothing but a single cut in column density of the result found by
(
Pokhrel
et al., 2021
, see their Fig. 1)
. The different‘efficiencies per free-fall time’ for each cloud result from a natural scatter in the mass and free-fall timescale involved in eq. (
15
). Individual differences in the geometry and velocity field may also contribute to this scatter
(
Hartmann &
Burkert, 2007
;
Zavala-Molina et al., 2024
, e.g., )
.

4
The extra-galactic K-S correlations as a consequence of the fundamental law

Our original motivation was mainly to give a simple, basic explanation for the variety of galactic correlations found in the literature
(
Lada
et al., 2010
;
Lada et al., 2013
;
Lada et al., 2017
;
Lombardi et al., 2014
;
Zari et al., 2016
;
Pokhrel
et al., 2021
, e.g., )
. However, being so basic, the fundamental law of star formation (
15
) can also explain the variety of extragalactic correlations, as we discuss in what follows.
Similar and/or alternative explanations have already been provided by
Elmegreen (2018)
. We discuss this issue in §
6
.

4.1
The Gao-Solomon correlation

The fundamental equation of star formation (
15
) shows a natural and immediate explanation for the
Gao &
Solomon (2004)
correlation and its extension shown by
Wu et al. (2005)
. Since for that correlation, massive dense cores were observed using the same tracer, HCN, we can assume that all of these cores have nearly the same density at which the HCN is excited, regardless of its actual value
6
6

6

Note, however, that recent suggestions that HCN may be sub-thermally excited in especially active star-forming regions
(
Barnes
et al., 2020
, e.g., the LEGO Survey)
.
. Thus, we can expect
{\tau_{\rm ff}}
to be nearly the same for all clouds. Finally, since HCN is a tracer of dense gas
(n\sim 10^{4}
cm
-3
), we can assume that most of that gas is in the process of collapse, producing thus the correlation. This is similar to the argument we gave in §
3.2
to explain the correlation found by
Lada
et al. (2010)
between the number of YSOs and the mass in dense gas.

4.2
The extragalactic
\Sigma_{\rm{SFR}}-\Sigma_{\rm mol}
correlation (the mKS correlation)

An important ingredient in understanding this correlation is that even though MCs have a wide range of densities distributed in a
hierarchical way (i.e., they have internal structure), the filling factor of the dense structures is so small
7
7

7

Another way of picturing this fact is that the column density PDFs of molecular clouds decrease fast.
that the mean density of a cloud is always at the lower levels of its hierarchy, a prediction made more than ten years ago
(
Ballesteros-Paredes et al., 2012
)
and confirmed observationally recently by
Cahlon et al. (2023)
. Thus, since this correlation has been performed using CO
(
Bigiel et al., 2008
;
Sun et al., 2023
;
Jiménez-Donaire et al., 2023
;
Ellison
et al., 2024
, e.g., )
, and since CO is excited at around
100-400
cm
-3
, the observed CO clouds have mean densities around these values. Consequently, all CO clouds have similar free-fall timescales, and thus, the linear correlation between the column densities of star formation and of molecular gas follows naturally from the fundamental law of star formation. In other words, this correlation is explained in the same terms we explained the
Gao &
Solomon (2004)
,
Wu et al. (2005)
and
Lada
et al. (2010)
correlations: we are analyzing collapsing gas that traces basically the same density and thus has the same free-fall time.

4.3
The shift in
\Sigma_{\rm{SFR}}
between CO and HCN correlations

A reassuring feature regarding the relevance of eq. (
15
) is the displacement of one order of magnitude in
\Sigma_{\rm{SFR}}
between the correlations obtained using CO with typical densities of 100 cm
-3

(
Bigiel et al., 2008
;
Sun et al., 2023
;
Ellison
et al., 2024
, e.g., )
, and those using gas that is at 10
4
cm
-3

(
Gao &
Solomon, 2004
;
Wu et al., 2005
;
Lada
et al., 2010
)
. This displacement of a factor of 10 in
\Sigma_{\rm{SFR}}
is consistent with CO collapsing in timescales 10 times longer than the HCN clouds and the
Lada
et al. (2010)
clouds, which have densities 100 times larger.

4.4
The super-linear correlation
\Sigma_{\rm{SFR}}-\Sigma_{\rm gas}

The explanation of the super-linear KS relation for resolved galaxies
(
Bigiel et al., 2008
;
Leroy et al., 2008
)
, when using total gas column density (HI
+
H
2
) has been already hinted in §
2.2
. The fundamental point is that atomic gas in the ISM is typically less dense and warmer than molecular gas, and thus, it is less prone to collapse. In addition, once self-shielding against the dissociating UV radiation is achieved, the mass fraction of molecular gas correlates with total surface density
(
Park
et al., 2023
, e.g., )
, because the larger the column density, the larger the self-shielding against the UV dissociating radiation
(
Hartmann et al., 2001
;
Bergin et al., 2004
)
.

Let us assume, thus, that we can first measure a particular star formation rate for a small column density of total gas,
\Sigma_{\rm gas,1}
, and that at these column densities, the molecular gas fraction
f_{\rm mol,1}
is smaller than that of the HI,
f_{\rm HI,1}
. We now observe another parcel of the galaxy where the column density is larger by, e.g., a factor of two,
\Sigma_{\rm gas,2}=2\ \Sigma_{\rm gas,1}
. We know that the larger the density, the more easily the cloud is shielded against the dissociating UV radiation, and the more likely the H
2
can be formed. Consequently, not only the mass of H
2
does increase, but it does it at the expense of the HI, i.e., the fraction of molecular gas also increases such that
f_{\rm mol,1}<f_{\rm mol,2}
. In other words, although we have doubled the total column density, the amount of molecular gas in the second case is larger than twice its original value. Thus, even if the column density of total gas (HI+H
2
) is twice the former, the column density of star formation increases faster because the column density of cold and dense molecular gas has also increased faster, producing the super-linear power-law for the K-S correlation.

It should be stressed, nonetheless, that although the global correlation between the column densities of star formation and total gas (HI+H
2
) is superlinear, in the regime
\Sigma_{\rm gas}>
10
M_{\odot}
pc
-2
, i.e., in the regime where typically the gas becomes predominantly molecular,
(
Hartmann et al., 2001
;
Park
et al., 2023
, see, e.g.,)
, the tendencies shown by
Bigiel et al. (2008, see the right panels of Fig. 4)
become flatter, substantially close to unity.

4.5
The lack of a correlation at sub-kpc scales

As commented in the introduction, the correlation is broken, or at least more sparse, for extragalactic resolved objects at smaller scales
\lesssim
0.5 kpc as has been observed by, e.g.,
Schruba et al. (2010)
;
Williams
et al. (2018)
;
Pessa
et al. (2022)
;
Pan et al. (2022)
. As these authors explained, some of their target fields may focus on a molecular cloud that obscures the H
α
emission, accounting for smaller
\Sigma_{\rm{SFR}}
and larger
\Sigma_{\rm gas}
. At the same time, other beams may point to regions with more significant H
α
emission but few or no CO emission
(
Schruba et al., 2010
)
. This means that, given the methodology in extragalactic studies, small volumes have sampling problems. Extragalactic observations require large averaging volumes to track simultaneously the star formation and the presence of molecular gas.

4.6
The sub-linear
\Sigma_{\rm{SFR}}-\Sigma_{\rm mol}/{\tau_{\rm ff}}
correlation for galaxies

The result provided by
Sun et al. (2023)
,

\log{\Sigma_{\rm{SFR}}}=\alpha+\beta\log{\Sigma_{\rm mol}/{\tau_{\rm ff}}}

(37)

with
\beta\sim 0.7
, appears to contradict our analysis that this correlation should be linear because molecular gas is in the state of collapse, and CO clouds have basically the same volumetric density. This inconsistency has its origin, however, in the way in which
Sun et al. (2023)
estimated column densities and free-fall times of molecular gas. These authors computed the column density at 1.5 kpc resolution by integrating the CO(2-1) intensity from the moment-0 maps, and assume a common line-of-sight length of
l=
150 pc for computing the volumetric density,

\rho\propto{\Sigma}/{l}

(38)

and thus, the free-fall time
(
Leroy
et al., 2016
, see eqs. (3) and (4) in)
. This methodology introduces the bias that produces the sub-linear power-law. To understand this point, we first recall that molecular clouds are highly fractal
(
Falgarone et al., 1991
)
and have sizes in the range
0.1-100
pc
(
Larson, 1981
;
Solomon et al., 1987
;
Blitz et al., 2007
, e.g., )
. This means that the filling factor of MCs within 1.5 kpc size boxes is substantially small. Imagine that we have a (1.5 kpc)
2
box of a disk galaxy containing only one CO cloud, with a size of, say,
l\sim
60 pc. Its mean density will be around a few hundred cm
-3
, and its free-fall time will be around three million years. Let us assume now that we have ten other clouds in the same (1.5 kpc)
2
box. The total mass in the box will be ten times larger, and so it will be the estimated column and volumetric densities. However, the real mean density of molecular gas will still be a few hundred cm
-3
, because all the clouds considered have the same density. As it can be seen, the methodology used by
Sun et al. (2023)
introduces a bias: for each order of magnitude larger column density, the estimated free-fall time will be a factor
\sqrt{10}\sim 3
shorter. Consequently, the one-to-one correlation between
\Sigma_{\rm{SFR}}
and
\Sigma_{\rm gas}
implies that the power-law index of the
\Sigma_{\rm{SFR}}-\Sigma_{\rm gas}/{\tau_{\rm ff}}
correlation will be 1/3 smaller because we are stretching the
x
axis by a factor of
\sim
3. Indeed, the power-law found by
Sun et al. (2023)
exhibits power-law indexes of about 2/3
(
Sun et al., 2023
, see the third section of Table 2 of)
, confirming our explanation.

4.7
The sub-linear Elmegreen-Silk correlation
\Sigma_{\rm{SFR}}-\Sigma_{\rm mol}/{\tau_{\rm orb}}

As mentioned before,
Sun et al. (2023)
found a sublinear Elmegreen-Silk correlation
(
Elmegreen, 1997
;
Silk, 1997
)
,

\Sigma_{\rm{SFR}}\propto\bigg(\frac{\Sigma_{\rm mol}}{{\tau_{\rm orb}}}\bigg)^{N}.

(39)

with
N\sim 0.8
. These authors already explained the origin of this correlation, arguing that the orbital time
{\tau_{\rm orb}}
is smaller in the inner regions of galaxies, where the column density is larger, stretching the correlation to the right. To quantify the level of stretching, we first recall that the orbital time used in the correlation is related to the circular velocity
V_{\rm circ}
as

{\tau_{\rm orb}}=\frac{2\pi R}{V_{\rm circ}},

(40)

and that the column density of gas in galaxies typically has a decreasing exponential shape

\Sigma_{\rm mol}=\Sigma_{0}\exp\bigg(-{\frac{R}{R_{\rm 0,mol}}}\bigg)

(41)

where
R_{\rm 0,mol}
is the scale length of the column density of the gas, and
\Sigma_{0}
is the central density of the exponential
(for the CO clouds in the Milky Way,
Miville-Deschênes et al., 2017
, estimate
R_{\rm 0,mol}\sim 2
kpc, and
\Sigma_{0}\sim 100

M_{\odot}
pc
-2
)
. Solving for
R
in (
41
) and substituting it in (
40
), one obtains

{\tau_{\rm orb}}\ =\ -~2\pi~\bigg(\frac{R_{\rm 0,mol}}{V_{\rm circ}}\bigg)\ \ln\bigg({\frac{\Sigma_{\rm mol}}{\Sigma_{0}}}\bigg).

(42)

Close to the centre of the galaxy,
\Sigma_{\rm mol}\to\Sigma_{0}
. Far from the center, i.e.,
R\in\ \sim~(4,20)
kpc, the ratio
{\Sigma_{\rm mol}}/{\Sigma_{0}}
decreases from
10^{-1}
to
10^{-3}
. Although the slope of the logarithmic function changes continuously, in this column density regime, we notice that

-\ln{\bigg(\frac{\Sigma_{\rm mol}}{\Sigma_{0}}}\bigg)\simeq 1.3\bigg(\frac{\Sigma_{\rm mol}}{\Sigma_{0}}\bigg)^{-0.25}.

(43)

This means that by dividing
\Sigma_{\rm mol}
by
{\tau_{\rm orb}}
, in practice we will compute
\Sigma_{\rm mol}^{1.25}
instead of
\Sigma_{\rm mol}
for each
\Sigma_{\rm{SFR}}
. Thus, we are stretching the
x
axis from
\Sigma_{\rm mol}
to
\Sigma_{\rm mol}^{1.25}
, which will convert the linear correlation into a sublinear power law, with a power-law index of the order of
\sim 1/1.25=0.8

\Sigma_{\rm{SFR}}\propto\Sigma_{\rm mol}^{0.8}

4.8
The slightly sublinear correlation between the star formation rate and the dynamical pressure
\Sigma_{\rm{SFR}}
-
P_{\rm DE}
correlation

It has been argued that star formation may be regulated by the necessary pressure in the midplane of disk galaxies to achieve (vertical) dynamical equilibrium
(
Blitz &
Rosolowsky, 2004
;
Blitz &
Rosolowsky, 2006
;
Ostriker
et al., 2010
, e.g, )
. The idea is quite interesting, and it is schematically depicted in
Ostriker
et al. (2010, Fig. 1)
. Here, we offer a summarized explanation: if the ISM is overpressurized, it will compress the diffuse gas, favouring the formation of dense, cold clouds. This will lower the typical pressure of the ISM. By the same token, the larger amount of cold gas will increase the star formation rate, increasing the UV radiation (i.e., increasing the heating) and, thus, increasing the mean pressure of the thermal equilibrium curve. Then, the ISM de-pressurizes itself, and the thermal equilibrium curve increases its mean pressure. The reverse argument also applies: if the ISM is underpressurized, dense clouds will expand, increasing the mean pressure of the ISM. The star formation will decrease, reducing the UV radiation (i.e., reducing the heating) and then reducing the mean pressure of the thermal equilibrium curve. In this way,
Ostriker
et al. (2010)
propose that star formation regulates itself.

This pressure, which must correlate with the star formation rate, must be the pressure in the midplane due to the weight of the gas . A commonly used approximation, which according to
Ostriker &
Kim (2022)
, is accurate within 20% can be written as

P_{\rm DE}=\frac{\pi G}{2}\Sigma_{\rm gas}^{2}+\Sigma_{\rm gas}\sqrt{2G\rho_{*}}\sigma_{{\rm gas,}z},

(46)

where
\sigma_{{\rm gas,}z}
is the velocity dispersion of the gas perpendicular to the plane of the disk,
\Sigma_{\rm gas}
is the column density of the gas, and
\rho_{*}
is the stellar density in the midplane
(
Suárez-Madrigal et al., 2012
;
Ramírez-Galeano
et al., 2022
, note that we did not consider the contribution of the dark matter to the vertical structure of the disk, since we have found it to be negligible, e.g., )
.

Different authors have found, indeed, a correlation between
\Sigma_{\rm{SFR}}
and
P_{\rm DE}

(
Barrera-Ballesteros et al., 2021
;
Sun et al., 2023
;
Ellison
et al., 2024
, e.g., )
. In particular, in their recent survey of
\sim 80
galaxies,
Sun et al. (2023)
have shown that the midplane pressure (
46
) exhibits a slightly sublinear correlation with the column density of star formation rate, with an intrinsic scatter around the correlation not much different to the scatter given by the column densities of star formation rate and molecular gas mass.

There are several caveats to this approach. On the one hand, star formation is a process that occurs on scales of some Myr
(
Leisawitz et al., 1989
;
Ballesteros-Paredes
et al., 1999b
;
Hartmann et al., 2001
;
Ballesteros-Paredes &
Hartmann, 2007
;
Kruijssen et al., 2018
;
Chevance
et al., 2020
, e.g., )
. In contrast, considering that the scale length of the HI can be of the order of
l_{z}\sim 300
pc
(
Kalberla &
Kerp, 2009
)
, and the velocity dispersion of the order of
\sigma_{{\rm gas,}z}\sim
7-11  km s
-1

(

Troland, 2003
;
Haud &
Kalberla, 2007
;
Caldú-Primo & Schruba, 2016
)
, the dynamical time
\tau_{\rm dyn}=l_{z}/\sigma_{{\rm gas,}z}
is of the order of some tens of Myr, substantially larger than the free-fall time. Then, although vertical equilibrium must be relevant for the assembly of dense clouds, it appears irrelevant for regulating star formation itself.

In addition, at least for the Milky Way, the interstellar pressure is of the order of
P/k\sim 3000
K cm
-3
, and thus, the required pressures for reaching dynamical equilibrium,
P_{\rm DE}/k\sim
10
4

-3

(

are substantially larger. While it is true that the total interstellar pressure (thermal, magnetic and cosmic ray pressure) in the Solar Neighborhood is ten times larger than the thermal pressure
(
Boulares &
Cox, 1990

,
Ostriker
et al. (2010)
recognize that the scale height of the magnetic field and cosmic rays are substantially larger than the scale height of the thermal pressure, making fields and cosmic rays negligible for vertical force balance. This suggests that, in order to achieve vertical equilibrium due to changes in the thermal pressure, average midplane thermal pressures have to increase by several orders of magnitude, which may occur locally (e.g., inside a supernova explosion), but will be hard to meet on kiloparsec scales.

Our interpretation of this correlation is that, with eq. (
46
), we are mostly sampling the same linear correlation
\Sigma_{\rm{SFR}}-\Sigma_{\rm mol}
for molecular gas, disguised by additional factors.
To understand this argument, we notice three points:

1.

As for the additional factors in eq. (

4.9
The
Kennicutt (1998)
laws for whole galaxies

Kennicutt (1998)
presented two correlations. The so-called Kennicutt-Schmidt relation

\Sigma_{\rm{SFR}}-\Sigma_{\rm gas}^{1.4},

(47)

and the so-called Elmegreen-Silk relation,

\Sigma_{\rm{SFR}}-\Sigma_{\rm gas}/{\tau_{\rm orb}}

(48)

(
Elmegreen, 1997
;
Silk, 1997
)
, both discussed above in terms of resolved galaxies at scales of
\sim 1
kpc, and using total (HI+H
2
) gas. We interpret these two correlations in a similar way as before. As for the first one, the actual, linear correlation between the star formation rate and gas mass density is with the dense, cool molecular gas. However, the correlation becomes superlinear because of the inclusion of more diffuse, warmer atomic gas. This gas either does not collapse or does it in longer timescales because, statistically speaking, it is warmer and more diffuse. However, the larger the column density, the more easily the molecules are formed at expense of the atomic gas
(
Bergin et al., 2004
, e.g., )
, and then, as before (§
4.4
), a linear increase in the total column density of the galaxy translates it into a superlinear increase of the total cold, dense molecular gas, and consequently, in a superlinear increase of the column density of star formation rate.

As for the second correlation, we recall that

{\tau_{\rm orb}}\propto\frac{R_{0,{\rm mol}}}{V_{\rm circ}}\ln\bigg(\frac{\Sigma_{\rm mol}}{\Sigma_{0}}\bigg)

(49)

(see eq. (
42
)). In this case, we just have to realize that we are analyzing global properties, and thus, each galaxy will have a single value for
{\tau_{\rm orb}}
, such that we will use again eq. (
42
) for the ensemble of galaxies.

With this in mind, we first notice, as before, that the ratio
\Sigma_{\rm mol}/\Sigma_{0}
is quite constant for the variety of galaxies
(
Barrera-Ballesteros et al., 2023
, see e.g., )
. On the other hand, it is likely that the ratio
R_{0,{\rm mol}}/V_{\rm circ}
remains constant for the ensemble of galaxies or does not vary more than a factor of two for most of them. To picture this, we notice that, in fact, the scale length of the stellar disk exhibits a linear correlation with the circular velocity
(
Courteau
et al., 2007
)
, which implies a constant orbital time, close to 1 Gyr
(
Meurer et al., 2018
)
, with a scatter dominated by the intrinsic scatter of the spin parameter of the configuration
(
Mo
et al., 1998
;
Hernandez
& Cervantes-Sodi, 2006
)

and thus, one can expect a similar result between the scale length of the molecular gas,
R_{0,{\rm mol}}
and the circular velocity
V_{\rm circ}
.

To further understand the Elmegreen-Silk correlation for whole galaxies as shown by
Kennicutt (1998, see his Fig. 7)
, we now note that, in reality, the regular galaxies (solid circles) exhibit a behaviour steeper compared to circumnuclear regions (open circles) and starburst galaxies (squares). Still, the scatter is such that all the points seem to follow the same global tendency. Consequently, we analyze regular galaxies independently from the starburst galaxies and circumnuclear regions.

As it can be guessed, the orbital times of the starburst and circumnuclear regions are substantially smaller than those of regular galaxies. This means that in transiting from eq. (
47
) to (
48
), we will stretch more the
x
axis for the starburst galaxies and circumnuclear regions than for regular galaxies. In addition, the density contrast
\Sigma_{\rm gas}/\Sigma_{0}\sim 0.2-0.05
for starburst and circumnuclear regions is substantially larger than that for disk galaxies. In this regime,
-\ln{\Sigma_{\rm gas}/\Sigma_{0}}\sim(\Sigma_{\rm gas}/\Sigma_{0})^{-0.4}
. Thus, we will stretch the
x
axis from
\Sigma_{\rm gas}
to
\Sigma_{\rm gas}/{\tau_{\rm orb}}\propto\Sigma_{\rm gas}^{1.4}
for the same range of
\Sigma_{\rm{SFR}}
. The new dependency becomes linear since the original dependency was already
\Sigma_{\rm{SFR}}\propto\Sigma_{\rm gas}^{1.4}
.

The above analysis implies that disk galaxies, for which we have found that
-\ln\Sigma_{\rm gas}/\Sigma_{0}\propto(\Sigma_{\rm gas}/\Sigma_{0})^{-0.25}
, should exhibit a superlinear power-law index. Indeed, by looking only at the solid points in Fig. 7 of
Kennicutt (1998)
, one can clearly see that they do have an index larger than unity, although these authors did not notice it since the whole correlation is skewed by the tendency of the circumnuclear and starburst points, a fact noticed by
Kennicutt &
De Los Reyes (2021, see their Fig. 3)
.

5
Discussion

5.1
Gravity or turbulence? Collapse, overvirial CO clouds, and the KS relation

In the previous sections, we have presented arguments to understand the different forms of the Kennicutt-Schmidt correlation in terms of a single star formation law that rises naturally as a consequence of the collapse of the gas under analysis. This is at odds with the common belief that turbulence supports clouds over many free-fall timescales and regulates the star formation.

Indeed, one of the main arguments against the idea of molecular clouds collapsing within a (few) free-fall times is that the time to exhaust all molecular gas in the galaxy is of the order of 1 Gyr, i.e,
\sim
300 times the free-fall time of a CO cloud. This number has been used to argue that star formation is a slow process
(
Zuckerman &
Evans, 1974
;
Blitz &
Shu, 1980
;
Shu
et al., 1987
;
Krumholz &
Tan, 2007
;
Kennicutt &
Evans, 2012
;
Krumholz et al., 2019
;
Evans et al., 2021
;
Evans
et al., 2022
, e.g., )
. In such scenario, molecular clouds last many free-fall times, forming stars at a low pace, and CO clouds are mostly supervirial
(
Evans et al., 2021
, e.g., )
, confined by external pressure
(
Keto, 2024
, e.g., )
and, thus, not collapsing.

To support this argument, it also has been argued that even those clouds exhibiting virial parameters around
\alpha_{\rm vir}\sim
1–2, cannot be granted to be self-gravitating, since there are so many approximations in the estimations of the gravitational energy
(
Chevance et al., 2023
)
. In addition, these authors made the argument that, while turbulence scales with size as
\sigma_{v}^{2}\propto l
, where
\sigma_{v}
is the velocity dispersion of the cloud at the characteristic size
l
, gravitational energy scales as
M/l\propto l^{2}
, implying that the virial parameter would scale as
\alpha_{\rm vir}\propto l^{-1}
, and thus, smaller scales will be necessarily supported by turbulence. In other words,
Chevance
et al. (2020)
argue that clouds with virial parameters around unity are not necessarily bound, but even if they were, smaller portions of them would be supported against collapse. In summary, MCs would not be collapsing.

There are, however, several problems with such a classical view of molecular clouds being supported by turbulence against gravity. On the one hand, the studies for molecular clouds in the Solar Neighbourhood by different authors
(
Lada et al., 2013
;
Lombardi et al., 2014
;
Zari et al., 2016
;
Lada et al., 2017
;
Pokhrel
et al., 2021
)
show that star formation occurs fundamentally in the densest regions of molecular clouds, where the column density is of the order of
\Sigma_{\rm mol}\gtrsim
500
M_{\odot}
pc
-2
. In this regime, the depletion time is about 10 Myr, 100 times faster than what typically assumed. Thus, the long depletion timescales of molecular gas at galactic scale cannot be taken as a proof of star formation being slow.

It should be noticed, furthermore, that large efficiencies cannot be taken as an argument against fast star formation, since the final star formation efficiency is reduced by: (i) the fact that the denser regions, which collapse and form stars faster than the rest of the cloud, contain only a small fraction of the total mass
(
Ballesteros-Paredes
et al., 2011b
)
; (ii) the fact that geometry factors do play a role
(
Hartmann &
Burkert, 2007
;
Pon
et al., 2012
)
, where filaments develop an efficiency
\sim 10
times lower than spheres
(
Zavala-Molina et al., 2024
)
; and (iii) the large efficiency of stellar feedback (winds and UV radiation) in disrupting the parental cloud
(
Kruijssen et al., 2018
;
Krause
et al., 2020
;
Grudić et al., 2021
;
Kim
et al., 2021
, e.g., )
. Thus, the fact that CO clouds are mostly collapsing
(
Hartmann et al., 2001
)
does not imply a large star formation rate.

Consistent with these short timescales is the observational evidence that molecular clouds have no stars older than 5 Myr
(
Herbig, 1978
;
Leisawitz et al., 1989
;
Ballesteros-Paredes
et al., 2009
;
Ballesteros-Paredes &
Hartmann, 2007
;
Kruijssen et al., 2018
)
implying that newborn stars manage to get rid of their parental molecular clouds after two free-fall times. This result points to a fast star formation scenario in which star formation is rapid and stellar feedback is efficient in destroying the parental cloud
(
Ballesteros-Paredes
et al., 1999b
;
Elmegreen, 2000
;
Hartmann et al., 2001
;
Ballesteros-Paredes &
Hartmann, 2007
;
Bournaud et al., 2010
;
Colín et al., 2013
;
Hopkins
et al., 2011
;
Román-Zúñiga
et al., 2015
;
Kruijssen et al., 2018
;
Vázquez-Semadeni
et al., 2019
;
Krause
et al., 2020
;
Kim
et al., 2021
;
Grudić et al., 2021
, e.g., )
.

Regarding the virial state of molecular (and in particular, CO) clouds, there are several points to stress:

1.

While this is true that most of the CO clouds exhibit overvirial values, most of the mass is in the larger clouds. These exhibit values of
\alpha_{\rm vir}\sim 1-2

(
Miville-Deschênes et al., 2017
;
Evans et al., 2021
, e.g.,)
.

2.

Regarding the concerns on overestimating the gravitational energy due to simplifications
(
Chevance et al., 2023
)
, we found the opposite effect: typically, self-gravity is underestimated. In fact, we found that collapsing clouds may exhibit overvirial values if their actual density structure is not properly taken into account
(
Ballesteros-Paredes
et al., 2018
)
.

As a simple example, one can reconsider the derivation by
Chevance et al. (2023, see above)
. Since clumps and cores do have higher densities than their parent clouds, one should consider not only the scaling of the velocity dispersion but also that of the density, when estimating the virial parameter. For instance, assuming that the density profile scales as
\rho\propto l^{-\beta}
, then the virial parameter should scale as

\alpha_{\rm vir}\propto l^{\beta-1}.

(50)

As it can be seen, flat density profiles (
\beta<1
) will satisfy the
Chevance et al. (2023)
condition that smaller structures are overvirial. But clumps and cores with density profiles steeper than
\beta>1
will become more gravitationally bound than their parental cloud. Indeed,
Kauffmann et al. (2013, see their Fig. 1)
have shown that for different tracers, there are always clouds, clumps and cores with
\alpha_{\rm vir}\simeq 2
at all scales.

One of the problems with assuming turbulence as the physical agent supporting clouds against collapse is that turbulence, a highly dissipative phenomenon, must be driven continuously and homogeneously. The apparent longevity of virial or pressure-confined supervirial clouds is, in a way, a wrong extrapolation from stellar cluster dynamics, where clusters are virial and long-lived because they are not dissipative.

We can analyze how a stellar system achieves virial balance to understand this misconception. Let us first assume super-virial initial conditions. The long-term evolution of such a system will be its expansion, never reaching virial balance. If, on the contrary, the stellar system has initial sub-virial conditions, it will collapse under its self-gravity and reach virial values after one free-fall time
(
Noriega-Mendoza & Aguilar, 2018
)
.

In the case of molecular clouds the evolution is different: for a subvirial cloud, as collapse proceeds it will reach virial values as a consequence of the collapse itself
(
Ballesteros-Paredes
et al., 2018
, e.g., )
. Thus, its kinetic motions are due to the collapse itself and not to any form of kinetic support.

In the case of an initially super-virial cloud
(
Camacho
et al., 2016
;
Camacho et al., 2023
;
Ballesteros-Paredes
et al., 2018
;
Ganguly et al., 2022
, as it can be the case of a cloud being formed by the convergence of flows, see, e.g., )
, one cannot give for granted that the whole cloud will avoid collapse. Statistically speaking, half of the motions will be converging
(
Vázquez-Semadeni
et al., 2008
;
Camacho
et al., 2016
;
Appel et al., 2023
, a fact actually measured in numerical simulations, see)
, and will not produce support or expansion, but collapse
(
Ballesteros-Paredes, 2006
, see)
. Indeed, it should be recalled that, in the current model of molecular cloud formation
(
Ballesteros-Paredes et al., 1999a
;
Hennebelle &
Pérault, 1999
;
Hartmann et al., 2001
;
Vázquez-Semadeni et al., 2007
;
Heitsch &
Hartmann, 2008
;
Mac Low et al., 2017
;
Vázquez-Semadeni
et al., 2019
;
Ibáñez-Mejía et al., 2022
)
, clouds are formed by the confluence of overvirial diffuse gas streams
9
9

5.2
The role of feedback in the KS relation

Pokhrel
et al. (2021)
had argued that magnetic supersonic turbulence produced by stellar feedback is an adequate candidate to explain the tight correlation within individual molecular clouds spanning several orders of magnitude in masses and SFR with constant
{\epsilon_{\mathrm{ff}}}
. However, it should be noticed that the low values of
{\epsilon_{\mathrm{ff}}}
estimated by these authors in local molecular clouds involve not only places where there is active star formation but also where there are no newborn stars
(
Zamora-Avilés et al., 2024
, see also)
.

It should be stressed that in deriving the fundamental equation (
15
), we have not assumed the nature of the feedback and have shown that the constancy of
{\epsilon_{\mathrm{ff}}}
is the very consequence of the gas that is involved in the collapse to form stars. Conversely, if turbulence dominates the star formation process, the natural timescale used in the fundamental equation should be the dynamical crossing time
\tau_{\rm dyn}
, rather than the free-fall time. In reality, the relevance of the stellar feedback is not to support clouds over many free-fall times, maintaining low the ‘efficiency per free-fall’ time but to shut down the star formation process
(
Hartmann et al., 2001
;
Kruijssen et al., 2018
;
Grudić et al., 2021
)
.

It is important to mention that the fact that the mass in eq. (
16
) is the mass involved in the process of collapse of the cloud does not imply that all that mass will end up forming stars within one free fall time with a huge star formation rate. The reason is that MCs are highly inhomogeneous and fractal
(
Falgarone et al., 1991
, e.g.,)
, and then, the free-fall timescales within clouds have substantial variations from place to place. Then, although most of the mass of a molecular cloud is at
n\sim 100
cm
-3
, dense cores (
n\gtrsim 10^{5}
cm
-3
) containing only a small fraction of the total mass of the cloud, will form stars in a much shorter timescale, initiating the termination of the star formation process and dispersing the otherwise collapsing cloud well before the less-dense regions significantly develop the collapse.

5.3
Clouds with lognormal PDFs cannot have a constant ‘efficiency per free-fall time’

The results from section
3.3
have an important implication for models of molecular cloud dynamics. As we have shown,
{\epsilon_{\mathrm{ff}}}
is constant as long as the power-law index of the column density and the power-law index of the KS relation follows eq. (
36
). In more general terms, it can be demonstrated that

{\epsilon_{\mathrm{ff}}}\propto\frac{M^{N-3/2}}{L^{2N-7/2}}

(51)

which becomes constant if
\alpha_{1}
in eq. (
30
), and
\alpha_{2}
in eq. (
35
), are equal, as we showed in §
3.3
. But strictly speaking, the mass and the size are given respectively by eqs. (
32
) and the square root of (
31
). If the column density PDF of a cloud is a lognormal function, the ‘efficiency per free-fall time’, eq. (
51
), cannot be a constant but a quantity that will depend on the column density of the cloud. Thus, clouds with lognormal column density PDFs cannot have a constant ‘efficiency per free-fall time’. In a sense, this is another demonstration that turbulence cannot be responsible for keeping
{\epsilon_{\mathrm{ff}}}
constant, since turbulence-regulated clouds exhibit lognormal column density PDFs.

5.4
Some comments about the ‘efficiency per free-fall time’

Strictly speaking, the so-called ‘efficiency-per-free-fall-time’ is the ratio between the free-fall time
{\tau_{\rm ff}}
and the depletion time, i.e., the time in which the cloud will convert all its mass into stars,

\tau_{\rm depl}=\bigg(\frac{{M_{\mathrm{*,{\tau_{\mathrm{YSO}}}}}}/{\tau_{\mathrm{YSO}}}}{M_{\mathrm{gas}}}\bigg)^{-1}.

(52)

There are some caveats, however, with this name and definition. On the one hand, it is implicit in its name that clouds could last many free-fall times. Indeed, if clouds do not last long, there will not be a need to define an efficiency other than the final efficiency of star formation. In addition, it also suggests that this quantity should remain constant over those many free-fall times, although there is no warranty that this will be the case. Finally, there are some problems with the observational estimations of this quantity, at least in the case of local molecular cloud observations. For instance, to compute
{\epsilon_{\mathrm{ff}}}
for local clouds, one needs to know the timescales
{\tau_{\mathrm{YSO}}}
of the different evolutionary stages of protostars (Class 0, I, and II). Those timescales have been estimated by assuming stationarity, i.e., that the ratio of objects in two different stages is proportional to the ratio of timescales in those stages, i.e.,

\frac{N_{1}}{N_{2}}=\frac{\tau_{1}}{\tau_{2}}

(53)

(
Lee &
Myers, 1999
;
Ward-Thompson
et al., 2007
, see, e.g., )
. However, star formation may not be stationary. For instance, the embedded phase of a protostar may depend on its proximity to a massive star, and thus, there is no guarantee that the timescale in a particular stage is always the same for all objects. This will affect the estimations of the depletion time and, thus, the estimations of the ‘efficiency per free-fall time’.

Finally, the very purpose of defining efficiencies is to estimate, given a physical process, how much of a certain physical property can be obtained from an original pool. By definition, this quantity has to be smaller than one. Thus, the efficiency of star formation is, then, the final mass of stars compared to the total mass of gas that was part of the cloud during the process of star formation. However, in the case of the ‘efficiency per free-fall time’,
{\epsilon_{\mathrm{ff}}}
, there can be a situation in which
{\epsilon_{\mathrm{ff}}}
is larger than unity
(
Clark &
Glover, 2014
, see, e.g., Fig. 7 of)
, just because the rate of star formation at a given time may be larger than the rate of collapse of the cloud,
M_{\mathrm{dense~gas}}/{\tau_{\rm ff}}
. Thus, we argue that the ‘efficiency per free-fall time’ is a misleading name for the parameter
{\epsilon_{\mathrm{ff}}}
, and it should be called simply the ratio between the star formation rate and the gas infall rate. In fact, naming it in this way will allow a better understanding of its meaning. For instance, a constant
{\epsilon_{\mathrm{ff}}}
will imply that the star formation rate changes at the same rate as the infall rate, basically because both of them are deeply related, and because the latter is the cause of the former.

5.5
Collapsing clouds also exhibit low values of ‘efficiency per free-fall time’

It is well known that a collapsing cloud may have large values of the final star formation efficiency if it has no stellar feedback. Interestingly, using numerical simulations,
Zamora-Avilés et al. (2024)
show that collapsing clouds exhibit low values of
{\epsilon_{\mathrm{ff}}}
(
\sim 0.03
), compatible with the inferred observational estimations
(
Krumholz et al., 2019
;
Pokhrel
et al., 2021
, e.g., )
. These authors also show that
{\epsilon_{\mathrm{ff}}}
remains constant at different levels of the hierarchy of the cloud,
(
Lombardi et al., 2014
;
Zari et al., 2016
;
Pokhrel
et al., 2021
, just as the clouds in the Solar Neighbrohood,)
, and that it remains constant in time, confirming our analytic result that collapsing clouds must exhibit a constant
{\epsilon_{\mathrm{ff}}}
.

These results are at odds with the intuition. This apparent inconsistency has been explained by
Vázquez-Semadeni
et al. (2019)
and
Bonilla-Barroso
et al. (2022)
as a consequence of how estimations of
{\epsilon_{\mathrm{ff}}}
are performed. Indeed, the ‘efficiency per free-fall time’ contains the mass in gas and its free-fall timescale. Both quantities are computed from observations at the present day. But suppose clouds form from the diffuse medium and become gravitationally unstable as they reach larger densities and smaller temperatures
(
Hartmann et al., 2001
)
. In that case, the current free-fall time is not representative, as it changes every instant. As pointed out by
Vázquez-Semadeni
et al. (2019)
and
Bonilla-Barroso
et al. (2022)
, the current free-fall timescale of cores severely underestimates the time lapse that a larger and less dense cloud has to spend to achieve its present state.

6
Gravity or turbulence: What is new?

In the framework of the collapse scenario
(
Hartmann et al., 2001
;
Ballesteros-Paredes
et al., 2011a
;
Elmegreen, 2018
;
Vázquez-Semadeni
et al., 2019
;
Ibáñez-Mejía et al., 2022
, and references therein)
, it is natural to understand eq. (
15
) as the fundamental equation of star formation because, indeed, it is hard to form a molecular cloud from the diffuse ISM that can last several free-fall times without collapsing into different centres, forming stars and dispersing. The idea that this equation is fundamental has already been proposed before. For instance,
Krumholz
et al. (2012)
show that this correlation explains the observational data (mainly extragalactic) substantially better. In addition,
Clark &
Glover (2014)
have found that any region that is cold and dense enough to collapse follows eq. (
15
). These authors showed that the molecules are only a proxy for the cold and dense regions that collapse because they tend to be dissociated at larger temperatures or lower densities.

There are, however, important differences between our approach and previous work favouring the fundamental equation (
15
). For instance, the small values of
{\epsilon_{\mathrm{ff}}}
, have led different authors to argue that turbulence must keep clouds forming stars at a low pace over many free-fall times. This idea has been taken to the extreme to consider that star formation is a slow process
(
Krumholz &
McKee, 2005
;
Krumholz &
Tan, 2007
;
Krumholz et al., 2019
;
Evans
et al., 2022
, see also)
. In contrast, our interpretation is that, being dense and cold, molecular clouds collapse, form stars, and disperse rapidly. There are either observational and theoretical evidences of this
(
Herbig, 1978
;
Leisawitz et al., 1989
;
Ballesteros-Paredes
et al., 1999b
;
Ballesteros-Paredes &
Hartmann, 2007
;
Heitsch &
Hartmann, 2008
;
Kruijssen et al., 2018
;
Vázquez-Semadeni
et al., 2019
;
Kim
et al., 2021
)
.

Another relevant difference with
Krumholz
et al. (2012)
is between their approach and ours. For us, all star-forming clouds (most of them molecular) are at similar densities (
n\sim 100
cm
-3
). That is precisely the reason for the linear correlations between
\Sigma_{\rm{SFR}}
and
\Sigma_{\rm mol}
observed by different authors in CO
(
Bigiel et al., 2008
;
Sun et al., 2023
;
Ellison
et al., 2024
, e.g., )
. For them, observations in CO may trace different densities, according to
\rho_{\mathrm{gas}}=\Sigma_{\rm gas}/h_{z}
, and thus, different free-fall times. To give an example, from eqs. (6) and (8) in
Krumholz
et al. (2012)
, one can obtain volumetric densities

By assuming the density of MCs to be the characteristic density from which most of the CO emission comes, our estimation of the free-fall time is local. In contrast,
Krumholz

are deriving local quantities (density, free-fall time) from global ones, as the column density and the Toomre parameter at a given place in the galaxy. This approach would be valid if the free-fall time of molecular clouds were comparable to the crossing time of the galaxy in those places. But as we pointed out in §
4.8
, this is not the case. For instance, the vertical crossing timescale
h_{z}/\sigma_{z}

h_{z}

. As for the Toomre instability, the crossing time is even longer since the distances to travel at similar velocities are even larger.

It should be noted that if the Toomre instability played a role in the collapse of molecular clouds, the gravitational energy contribution from the stellar disk would be comparable to the gravitational energy of the cloud itself. But the contribution from the stellar disk to the gravitational budget of molecular clouds is negligible
(
Suárez-Madrigal et al., 2012
;
Ramírez-Galeano
et al., 2022
, e.g., )
. The stellar disk matters in terms of increasing the background density of the atomic gas from where molecular clouds would be formed, allowing fast MC formation
(
Ramírez-Galeano
et al., 2022
)
.

Our work, however, resembles that of
Elmegreen (2018)
, who tried to understand the different KS correlations in terms of the same fundamental law. In principle, both works agree that clouds must be collapsing. There are also some differences between our work and his, which should be clarified. In particular,

1.

Elmegreen (2018)
argues that all gas is collapsing, but at different rates. In contrast, we argue that eq. (
15
) has to be considered basically for collapsing gas only. When including gas that is not collapsing (or collapsing at substantially different rates), the linearity of eq. (
15
) is necessarily broken, and the power-law index of the KS relation would depend on how the HI/H
2
ratio varies in each galaxy.

2.

We argue that the linear relation is valid in all its forms, but care has to be taken when different comparisons are made. For instance, for us, the linear correlation between the star formation rate and the gas mass
(
Gao &
Solomon, 2004
;
Wu et al., 2005
;
Lada
et al., 2010
)
is a direct application of eq. (
15
), provided that the observed regions in each work have similar mean densities. However,
Elmegreen (2018)
argues that it may be a consequence of observational artefacts.

3.

Our approach explaining the linear SF law for molecular gas in resolved galaxies
(
Elmegreen, 2018
, relation KS-1a in)
is basically the same: cold dense gas collapses within 1–2 free-fall timescales. Nonetheless, we argue that there is no need to consider total gas to understand this relation since HI is substantially less dense than CO. Thus, its process of collapse is most likely to be disrupted by the star formation event produced by the cold, dense gas, long before it significantly develops the collapse.

4.

As for the non-linear correlations,
Elmegreen (2018)
distinguishes between the KS-1.5 and the KS-2 relations (the power laws with power-law indexes of 1.5 and 2). So, in order to explain the KS-1.5 relation
(i.e., the original result by
Kennicutt, 1998
, with a power-law index of 1.5)
for whole galaxies,
Elmegreen (2018, § 2.2)
invokes the density
\rho=\Sigma_{\rm gas}/2H
, with
H
the scale-height of the disk. And since
{\tau_{\rm ff}}\propto\rho^{-0.5}
, if the scale height is constant through the galaxy or between galaxies, then the KS relation with power-law 1.5 follows naturally. We believe that there is no need to invoke the constancy of the scale height of disk galaxies. Especially, knowing that the gas disks are flared
(
Kalberla &
Kerp, 2009
;
Yim et al., 2014
, e.g., )
and that there is not a single universal scale-height of the gaseous disk galaxies
(
Mancera Piña
et al., 2022
)
. Our proposal is, instead, that galaxies with larger column densities are more likely to have a larger fraction of dense, cold (molecular) gas
(
Park
et al., 2023
, e.g., )
, which will collapse faster. Thus, the increase in total column density implies a superlinear increase of such a dense, cold gas, which implies a superlinear increase in star formation rate.

5.

With the previous interpretation of the relative abundance of dense
vs.
non-dense gas, we try to explain not only these galaxies with index 1.5 but also the variety of non-linear correlations with power-law indexes between 1.5 and 2.3 found when total gas is analyzed
(
Bigiel et al., 2008
, e.g., )
. We argue that the actual increase of
\Sigma_{\rm{SFR}}
with total
\Sigma_{\rm gas}
depends upon the local conditions of extinction, UV radiation and abundances that will allow (or not) the gas to cool down and become dense enough to collapse faster
(
Clark &
Glover, 2014
, see)
. In contrast,
Elmegreen (2018, see § 2.4)
focus on the power-law index of 2 in terms of pressure equilibrium provided mainly by the self-gravity of the disk.

6.

We also show that the internal structure of molecular clouds in intimately related to the KS relation they exhibit, and thus, that the power-law index of the column density PDF that each cloud has is intimately related to the power-law index of its KS relation.

7.

Elmegreen (2018)
shows that a cloud with a density profile of a single isothermal sphere in virial equilibrium does follow the quadratic KS relation
\Sigma_{\rm{SFR}}\propto\Sigma_{\rm mol}^{2}
, a result found by
Pokhrel
et al. (2021)
. Indeed, it is easy to demonstrate that a cloud with such a profile will have a free-fall time
{\tau_{\rm ff}}\propto\Sigma_{\rm mol}^{-1}
, producing the quadratic correlation. And although the virialization of the cloud is not needed for these clouds to follow the KS relation, it should be recalled that virial balance is the natural outcome of collapse
(
Vázquez-Semadeni et al., 2007
;
Noriega-Mendoza & Aguilar, 2018
;
Ballesteros-Paredes et al., 2020
)
.

There are other differences between our work and that of
Elmegreen (2018)
, being the main ones (a) our interpretation of the Silk-Elmegreen and Pressure-regulated correlations as basically the same fundamental law disguised by different factors; (b) our deduction that the column density PDF of individual molecular clouds is intimately related to their KS relation, as
Lada
et al. (2010)
conjectured.

7
Conclusions

We started this project aimed at understanding the variety of Kennicut-Schmidt correlations in the literature, since not all seemed compatible or consistent with each other. To do so, we first derived the relation

\langle{\mathrm{SFR}}\rangle_{\tau_{\mathrm{YSO}}}={\epsilon_{\mathrm{ff}}}\ \bigg(\frac{M_{\mathrm{collapse}}}{{\tau_{\rm ff}}}\bigg).

(54)

Since this was done from the pure definition of star formation rate and basic algebra, we call it the fundamental law of star formation. We then demonstrate that if the ‘efficiency per free-fall time’ is constant, it is because the mass under consideration is in the process of Jeans fragmentation and, thus, must be collapsing.

We further have shown that the different correlations observed in the literature, galactic or extragalactic, can be inferred from the fundamental law, provided there is an understanding of the object’s structure in question. The first one,
\Sigma_{\rm{SFR}}\propto\Sigma_{\rm gas}/{\tau_{\rm ff}}
, shown by
Pokhrel
et al. (2021)
, is just a direct application of the law. The following two, namely
{N_{\rm YSO}}-M_{\mathrm{dense~gas}}

(
Lada
et al., 2010
)
and the linear correlation between the infrared luminosity as a tracer of star formation rate, and the HCN luminosity as a tracer of the mass in dense gas
L_{\rm IR}-L_{\rm HCN}

(
)

(
Gao &
Solomon, 2004
)
, can also be understood in terms of the fundamental equation of star formation (
14
) by noticing that the objects under consideration in each correlation have the same density regime and, thus, have similar free-fall times. But there is a third type of correlation, more similar to the
Kennicutt (1998)
correlation,
\Sigma_{\rm{SFR}}\propto\Sigma_{\rm gas}^{N}
, which, in order to be compatible with the fundamental law, requires an understanding of the internal structure of the cloud, namely, the column density PDF of the cloud, and its mass-size relation at different levels of the hierarchy of the cloud.

We then moved to the extragalactic correlations. We found that these could also be explained through the fundamental equation of star formation. In particular, we interpret the superlinear behaviour of the Kennicutt-Schmidt relationship as a consequence of the correlation between the molecular gas abundance and the column density of gas (HI+H
2
)
(
Park
et al., 2023
, see also)
caused by the increasing self-shielding of gas against the dissociating UV radiation, as column density increases. The remaining correlations were explained in terms of the different methodologies or assumptions without the need to explain further physical processes.

It should be noticed, furthermore, that the observed constancy of
{\epsilon_{\mathrm{ff}}}
implies that star formation is regulated by the collapse of the cloud only. The role of galactic rotation is not to regulate the star formation but to regulate the formation of HI clouds and, thus, only indirectly, to regulate the formation of molecular clouds. On the other hand, the role of stellar feedback is to shut down the local star formation event
(
Bournaud et al., 2010
;
Colín et al., 2013
;
Kim
et al., 2021
;
Hopkins
et al., 2011
, e.g., )
, and thus, to keep low the global star formation efficiency. But the rate at which molecular gas is transformed into stars is only regulated by the gravitational collapse itself, and the fact that
{\epsilon_{\mathrm{ff}}}
is found to be constant implies that the ratio of the star formation rate over the gas infall rate is the same during the star formation process.
