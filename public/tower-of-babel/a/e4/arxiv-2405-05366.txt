Approximation of skew Brownian motion by snapping-out Brownian motions
Adam Bobrowski, Elżbieta Ratajczyk

Abstract
. We elaborate on the theorem saying that as permeability coefficients of snapping-out Brownian motions tend to infinity in such a way that their ratio remains constant, these processes converge to a skew Brownian motion. In particular, convergence of the related semigroups, cosine families and projections is discussed.

1.
Introduction

1.1.
Skew Brownian motion

As described in
[
36
]
‘The skew Brownian Motion appeared in the ’70 in
[
27
,
43
]
as a natural generalization of the Brownian motion: it is a process that behaves like a Brownian motion except that the sign of each excursion is chosen using an independent Bernoulli random variable’. This characteristic property of skew Brownian motion is expressed in the boundary conditions

f^{\prime\prime}(0+)=f^{\prime\prime}(0-)\quad\text{ and }\quad\beta f^{\prime}(0-)=\alpha f^{\prime}(0+),

(1.1)

where
\alpha
and
\beta
are non-negative parameters such that
\alpha+\beta>0
.
More precisely, the skew Brownian motion is an honest Feller process on
\mathbb{R}
, and as such can be described by means of
its generator, that is, a Laplace operator, say,
A^{\textnormal{{skew}}}_{\alpha,\beta}
, in the space
\mathfrak{C}[-\infty,\infty]
(
[-\infty,\infty]
is a two-point compactification of
\mathbb{R}
) of continuous functions on
\mathbb{R}
that have finite limits at
\infty
and
-\infty
. The domain of
A^{\textnormal{{skew}}}_{\alpha,\beta}
is composed of functions satisfying the following three conditions:

(a)

f
is twice continuously differentiable in both

(-\infty,0]
and
[0,\infty)
, separately, with left-hand and
right-hand derivatives at
x=0
, respectively,

(b)

both the limits
\lim_{x\to\infty}f^{\prime\prime}(x)
and

\lim_{x\to-\infty}f^{\prime\prime}(x)
exist and are finite (it follows that, in fact, they
have to be equal to
0
), and

(c)

conditions (
1.1
) hold; note that the first of them implies that, although
f^{\prime}(0)
need not exist, it is meaningful to speak of
f^{\prime\prime}(0)
.

Furthermore, we
define

A^{\textnormal{{skew}}}_{\alpha,\beta}f\coloneqq f^{\prime\prime}.

Even though, for reasons that will become obvious in what follows, it is convenient to consider all non-negative parameters
\alpha
and
\beta
in (
1.1
) (such that
\alpha+\beta>0
), their probabilistic meaning is more clear when they are normalized by dividing both of them by
\alpha+\beta
. Then the new
\alpha
is the probability that the sign of excursion is chosen to be positive, and the new
\beta
is the probability that the sign is negative.

The paper by A. Lejay cited above discusses a number of constructions of the skew Brownian motion that appeared in the literature since the process was first discovered, and various contexts in which this process and its generalizations are studied; see also
[
44
]
*p. 107 and
[
38
]
*pp. 115–117. It is well-known, for example, that skew Brownian motion can be obtained in the limit procedure of Friedlin and Wentzell’s averaging principle
[
24
]
—see
[
23
]
*Thm. 5.1, comp.
[
12
]
*Eq. (3.1). Much more recently, in
[
6
]
, a link has been provided between skew Brownian motion and kinetic models of motion of a phonon involving an interface, of the type studied in

[
4
,
33
,
32
,
31
]
, and the telegraph
process with elastic boundary at the origin
[
18
,
19
]
.

1.2.
Snapping-out Brownian motion

The goal of this paper is to use the recent results of
[
8
]
to strengthen the limit theorem of
[
13
]
(see also
[
14
]
*Chapter 11) saying that the skew Brownian motion can be obtained as a limit of
snapping out Brownian motions
.

The snapping out Brownian motion is a diffusion on two half-lines,
(-\infty,0)
and
(0,\infty)
, separated by a semi-permeable membrane located at
0
. It can be described by a Feller semigroup of operators in the space

\mathfrak{C}(\mathbb{R}_{\sharp})

of continuous functions on

\mathbb{R}_{\sharp}\coloneqq[-\infty,0-]\cup[0+,\infty]

where
0-
and
0+
are two distinct points, representing
positions to the immediate left and to the immediate right of the membrane; alternatively, members of
\mathfrak{C}(\mathbb{R}_{\sharp})

can be seen as continuous functions on
(-\infty,0)\cup(0,\infty)
that have finite limits at
\pm\infty
and one-sided finite limits at
0
. The membrane, in turn, is characterized by two non-negative parameters, say,
\alpha
and
\beta
, describing its permeability for a particle diffusing from the left to the right and from the right to the left, respectively. More precisely, given such
\alpha
and
\beta
, we define the generator of the snapping out Brownian motion as follows: it is the operator
A_{\alpha,\beta}^{\textnormal{s-o}}
in
\mathfrak{C}(\mathbb{R}_{\sharp})
given by

A_{\alpha,\beta}^{\textnormal{s-o}}f\coloneqq f^{\prime\prime},

on the domain composed of twice continuously differentiable
f\in\mathfrak{C}(\mathbb{R}_{\sharp})
such that
f^{\prime},f^{\prime\prime}\in\mathfrak{C}(\mathbb{R}_{\sharp})
and

\displaystyle\begin{split}f^{\prime}(0-)&=\alpha(f(0+)-f(0-)),\\
f^{\prime}(0+)&=\beta(f(0+)-f(0-)).\end{split}

(1.2)

These
transmission conditions
are well-known in the literature and can be plausibly interpreted: according to Newton’s Law of Cooling, the temperature at
x=0
changes at a rate proportional to the difference of temperatures on
either sides of the membrane, see
[
17
, p. 9]
. In this context, J. Crank uses the term
radiation boundary condition
. In the context of passing or diffusing through membranes, analogous transmission conditions were introduced by J.E. Tanner
[
42
, eq. (7)]
, who studied diffusion of particles through a sequence of permeable barriers (see also Powles et al.
[
41
, eq. (1.4)]
, for a continuation of the subject). In
[
1
]
(see e.g. eq. (4) there) similar conditions are used in describing absorption and desorption phenomena. We refer also to
[
22
]
, where a compartment model with permeable walls (representing e.g., cells, and axons in the white matter of the brain in particular) is analyzed, and to eq. [42] there. Among more recent literature involving relatives of (
1.2
), one should mention the model of inhibitory synaptic receptor dynamics of P. Bressloff

[
15
]
; see also
[
16
]
and references given in these papers.

In
[
7
]
(see also
[
14
]
*Chapters 4 and 11), relations (
1.2
) were put in the context of Feller–Wentzel boundary conditions, and this allowed describing the related process by means of the
Lévy local time
. Later, in

[
37
]
, a stochastic construction was provided, and the term
snapping out Brownian motion
was coined. To summarize these findings, we note that conditions (
1.2
) are akin to the
elastic barrier condition: An elastic Brownian motion on
\mathbb{R}^{+}:=[0,\infty)
(see e.g.
[
27
,
29
]
) is the process with generator

Gf=\frac{1}{2}f^{\prime\prime}

defined on the domain composed of
f\in\mathfrak{C}^{2}[0,\infty]
satisfying the
Robin boundary condition
(known also as
elastic barrier condition
):

f^{\prime}(0+)=\beta f(0+).

In this process, the state-space is
\mathbb{R}^{+}
, and each particle performs a standard Brownian motion while away from the barrier
x=0.
Each time a particle touches the barrier it is reflected, but its time spent at the boundary, the local time mentioned above, is measured and after an exponential time with parameter
\beta
with respect to the local time, the particle is killed and no longer observed. The second condition in (
1.2
) expresses the fact that in the snapping out Brownian motion, each particle diffusing on the right half-axis, instead of being killed after the time described, is transferred to the other side of the membrane
x=0
. In other words, the particle after some time spent ‘at’ the right side of the boundary, permeates to the the left side. Similarly, a particle on the left side of the boundary permeates through the membrane after a random time characterized by the coefficient
\alpha
. In particular, we see that
\alpha
and
\beta
are permeability coefficients: the larger they are the shorter is the time for particles to diffuse through the membrane.

1.3.
Approximation of skew B.M. by snapping out B.M.

Since
\alpha
and
\beta
in (
1.2
) are permeability coefficients, it may be thought reasonable that letting them to be infinite should lead to the case in which the membrane is completely permeable, that is, to the standard Brownian motion. As it turns out, however, the matter is not so simple: in the limit some kind of asymmetry in the way particles pass to the left and to the right through the membrane remains.

To explain this phenomenon in more datail, let us replace
\alpha
and
\beta
in the transmission conditions (
1.2
) by
n\alpha
and
n\beta
, respectively, and let
n\to\infty
. Heuristically, it is then clear that the limit conditions should read

f(0+)=f(0-)\quad\text{ and }\quad\beta f^{\prime}(0-)=\alpha f^{\prime}(0+).

The first of these relations tells us that we should work with
f\in\mathfrak{C}(\mathbb{R}_{\sharp})
that are continuous at
x=0
, that is with
f\in\mathfrak{C}[-\infty,\infty]
, and the other is precisely the second of conditions that characterize the skew Brownian motion, see (
1.1
). Hence, we anticipate a theorem saying that a skew Brownian motion is a limit of snapping out Brownian motions, provided that permeability coefficients
\alpha
and
\beta
of the semi-permeable membrane converge to infinity whereas their ratio remains constant.

As we know from
[
13
]
, these intuitions can be transformed into a formal theorem saying that
for any
s>0
,

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\sup_{t\in[0,s]}\big\|\mathrm{e}^{tA_{n\alpha,n\beta}^{\textnormal{s-o}}}f-\mathrm{e}^{tA^{\textnormal{{skew}}}_{\alpha,\beta}}f\big\|=0,\qquad f\in\mathfrak{C}[-\infty,\infty],

(1.3)

where
\mathfrak{C}[-\infty,\infty]
is seen as the subspace of
\mathfrak{C}(\mathbb{R}_{\sharp})
composed of functions
f
such that
f(0-)=f(0+)
,
\{\mathrm{e}^{tA_{\alpha,\beta}^{\textnormal{s-o}}},\,t\geq 0\}
is the Feller semigroup describing snapping-out Brownian motion, and
\{\mathrm{e}^{tA^{\textnormal{{skew}}}_{\alpha,\beta}},\,t\geq 0\}
is that describing the skew Brownian motion. In view of the Trotter–Sova–Kurtz–Mackevic̆ius theorem
[
28
]
, this result can be seen as expressing a convergence of the random processes involved.

1.4.
The goal of the paper

The goal of the paper is to strengthen the result of
[
13
]
by
exhibiting a number of phenomena accompanying convergence (
1.3
). These can be summarized as follows.

(A)

Convergence of cosine families.
As proved in
[
13
]
, each
A_{\alpha,\beta}^{\textnormal{s-o}}
generates not only a semigroup but also a cosine family, and the latter is in a sense more fundamental than the semigroup. We will show that on
\mathfrak{C}[-\infty,\infty]
(but not outside of this space) not only the semigroups generated by
A_{n\alpha,n\beta}^{\textnormal{s-o}}
converge; so do also the related cosine families. Moreover, the limit is in fact uniform with respect to
t\in\mathbb{R}
, and for Lipschitz continuous functions the rate of convergence can be estimated—see Section
2
, and Theorem
2.1
(b) and Remarks
1
and
3
in particular.

(B)

Convergence of semigroups outside of
\mathfrak{C}[-\infty,\infty]
.
As a consequence of (A), for
f\not\in\mathfrak{C}[-\infty,\infty]
the limit
\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathrm{e}^{tA_{n\alpha,n\beta}^{\textnormal{s-o}}}f
still exists for all
t>0
, but is merely uniform with respect to
t
in compact subintervals of
(0,\infty)
—see Theorem
2.1
(c).

(C)

Asymptotic behavior.
We will show that the cosine families generated by
A_{\alpha,\beta}^{\textnormal{s-o}}
have the same time averages as those generated by
A^{\textnormal{{skew}}}_{\alpha,\beta}
—see Section
3
.

(D)

Convergence of projections.
It is also proved in
[
8
]
that each transmission condition (
1.2
) shapes unequivocally an extension of each member of
\mathfrak{C}(\mathbb{R}_{\sharp})
to a pair of members of
\mathfrak{C}[-\infty,\infty]
, that is, to a member of the Cartesian product

\mathcal{C}\coloneqq(\mathfrak{C}[-\infty,\infty])^{2}.

The subspace
\mathcal{C}_{\alpha,\beta}^{\textnormal{s-o}}\subset\mathcal{C}
of such extensions, besides being invariant under the Cartesian product basic cosine family in
\mathcal{C}
(see (
2.7
)), is
complemented
. Therefore, there are related projections
P_{\alpha,\beta}^{\textnormal{s-o}}\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{C})
on
\mathcal{C}_{\alpha,\beta}^{\textnormal{s-o}}
. We prove that

\lim_{n\to\infty}P_{n\alpha,n\beta}^{\textnormal{s-o}}
exists and is a projection on the subspace of extensions unequivocally shaped by the skew Brownian motion boundary condition (
1.1
). See Section
4
.

(E)

Convergence of complementary cosine families and semigroups.

As also proved in
[
8
]
, the complementary subspace of
\mathcal{C}_{\alpha,\beta}^{\textnormal{s-o}}
, say,
\mathcal{C}_{\alpha,\beta}^{\textnormal{o-s}}
, in
\mathcal{C}
is unequivocally shaped by certain transmission conditions too. As a result there are complementary cosine families and semigroups to those generated by
A_{\alpha,\beta}^{\textnormal{s-o}}
. We prove that these cosine families and semigroups converge similarly as those generated by
A_{\alpha,\beta}^{\textnormal{s-o}}
.
Surprisingly, the limit semigroup and the limit cosine family turn out to be isomorphic copies of the semigroup and cosine family related to another skew Brownian motion, with the role of coefficients
\alpha
and
\beta
reversed—see Section
5
.

Additional results completing the paper are presented in Section
6
.

2.
Convergence of cosine families and semigroups describing snapping-out Brownian motions

Before our main theorem in this section is presented we need to explain the nature of the result it generalizes, i.e., to put (
1.3
) in the context of general theory of convergence of semigroups. Also, in Section
2.2
we recall the notion of cosine family.

2.1.
Theory of convergence of semigroups of operators

The main idea of the Trotter–Kato–Neveu convergence theorem
[
2
,
20
,
25
,
40
]
, a cornerstone of the theory of convergence of semigroups
[
14
,
9
]
, is that convergence of resolvents of
equibounded
semigroups in a Banach space
\mathsf{F}
, gives an insight into convergence of the semigroups themselves. Hence, in studying the limit of semigroups, say,
\{\mathrm{e}^{tB_{n}},t\geq 0\},n\geq 1
, generated by the operators
B_{n},n\geq 1
we should first establish the existence of
the strong limit

R_{\lambda}\coloneqq\lim_{n\to\infty}\big(\lambda-B_{n}\big)^{-1}.

The general theory of convergence (see
[
14
]
*Chapter 8) covers also the case in which, unlike in the classical version of the Trotter–Kato–Neveu theorem, the (common) range of the operators
R_{\lambda},\lambda>0
so-obtained is
not
dense in
\mathsf{F}
, and stresses the role of the so-called
regularity space
, defined as the closure of the range of
R_{\lambda}
:

\mathsf{F}_{\textnormal{reg}}\coloneqq cl(RangeR_{\lambda})\subset\mathsf{F}.

(2.1)

Namely,
\mathsf{F}_{\textnormal{reg}}
turns out to be composed of
f\in\mathsf{F}
such the limit
T(t)f\coloneqq\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathrm{e}^{tB_{n}}f
exists and is uniform with respect to
t
in compact subintervals of
[0,\infty)
; then
\{T(t),\,t\geq 0\}
is a strongly continuous semigroup in
\mathsf{F}_{\textnormal{reg}}
.

Condition (
1.3
) (more precisely, the theorem proved in
[
13
]
) is thus a typical result of convergence theory: it characterizes the regularity space for
B_{n}\coloneqq A_{n\alpha,n\beta}^{\textnormal{s-o}}
as equal to
\mathfrak{C}[-\infty,\infty]\subset\mathfrak{C}(\mathbb{R}_{\sharp})
, and identifies
\{T(t),\,t\geq 0\}
as
\{\mathrm{e}^{A^{\textnormal{{skew}}}_{\alpha,\beta}},t\geq 0\}
. It should be stressed, though, that this statement does not exclude the possibility of the existence of
f\not\in\mathfrak{C}[-\infty,\infty]
such that
\big(\mathrm{e}^{tA_{n\alpha,n\beta}^{\textnormal{s-o}}}f\big)_{n\geq 1}
converges for all
t>0
. Such
irregular
convergence of semigroups, which is known to be always uniform with respect to
t
in compact subsets of
(0,\infty)
— see
[
11
]
or
[
14
]
*Thm 28.4 — is not so uncommon, especially in the context of singular perturbations
[
39
,
3
,
14
]
, but needs to be established by different means. We will prove that the limit
\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathrm{e}^{tA_{n\alpha,n\beta}^{\textnormal{s-o}}}f
exists for all
t>0
and
f\in\mathfrak{C}(\mathbb{R}_{\sharp})
, by examining the related cosine families.

2.2.
Cosine families

A strongly continuous family
\{C(t),t\in\mathbb{R}\}
of operators in a Banach space
\mathsf{F}
is said to be a cosine family iff
C(0)
is the identity operator and

2C(t)C(s)=C(s+t)+C(t-s),\qquad t,s\in\mathbb{R}.

The generator of such a family is defined by

Af=\lim_{t\to 0}2t^{-2}{(C(t)f-f)}

for all
f\in\mathsf{F}
such the limit on the right-hand side exists.
For example, in
\mathfrak{C}[-\infty,\infty]
there is the
basic cosine family
given by

C(t)f(x)={\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}[f(x+t)+f(x-t)],\qquad x\in\mathbb{R},t\in\mathbb{R}.

(2.2)

Its generator is the one-dimensional Laplace operator
f\mapsto f^{\prime\prime}
with domain composed of twice continuously differentiable functions on
\mathbb{R}
such that
f^{\prime\prime}\in\mathfrak{C}[-\infty,\infty]
.

Each cosine family generator, say,
A
, is automatically the generator of a strongly continuous semigroup (but not vice versa). The semigroup such
A
generates is given by the
Weierstrass formula
(see e.g.
[
2
]
*p. 219)

T(0)f=f\quad\text{and}\quad T(t)f={\textstyle\frac{1}{2\sqrt{\pi t}}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{s^{2}}{4t}}C(s)f\,\mathrm{d}s,\qquad t>0,f\in\mathsf{F}.

(2.3)

This formula expresses the fact that the cosine family is then a more fundamental object than the semigroup, and properties of the semigroup can be hidden in those of the cosine family.

In our main theorem of this section, we will show that (
1.3
) can be strengthened by proving that not only the semigroups generated by
A_{\alpha,\beta}^{\textnormal{s-o}}
converge but so also do the related cosine families. The fact that each
A_{\alpha,\beta}^{\textnormal{s-o}}
is a generator of a cosine family was proved in
[
13
]
, but from the analysis presented there it is not clear that the cosine families generated by
A_{\alpha,\beta}^{\textnormal{s-o}}
’s are equibounded or that they do converge (see Remark 6.1 in that paper).

2.3.
Convergence theorem for solution families

We are finally ready to present our main theorem in this section.

.

The limit

Proposition 2.2

3.
Asymptotic behavior

It is well-known (see
[
5
]
*Corollary 1 which extends
[
2
]
*Prop. 3.14.6) that, if
\{C(t),t\in\mathbb{R}\}
is a strongly continuous family in a Banach space
\mathsf{F}
, then the limit

\lim_{t\to\infty}C(t)f

exist for an
f\in\mathsf{F}
only in the trivial case, that is, iff
C(t)f=f,t\in\mathbb{R}
. On the other hand, for rather non-trivial cosine families it often happens that
the mean

Mf\coloneqq\lim_{t\to\infty}t^{-1}\int_{0}^{t}C(s)f\,\mathrm{d}s

exists for all
f\in\mathsf{F}
. For example, for the basic cosine family this limit exists in the bounded pointwise topology and we have

Mf={\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}(f(-\infty)+f(\infty)),\qquad f\in\mathfrak{C}[-\infty,\infty].

(3.1)

(Here, and in the rest of this section, for simplicity of notations, we do not distinguish between a real number, like

{\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}(f(-\infty)+f(\infty))
, and a function that is everywhere equal to this number.)
In this section we show that the mean operator
M
for
\cosso
remains the same when
\alpha
and
\beta
are replaced by
n\alpha
and
n\beta
respectively, and in fact coincides with the mean operator for
\cskew
. As a result, in view of Theorem
2.1
(b), for
f\in\mathfrak{C}[-\infty,\infty]
we can write

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to\infty}t^{-1}\int_{0}^{t}\lim_{n\to\infty}\cosson(s)f\,\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle=\lim_{t\to\infty}t^{-1}\int_{0}^{t}\cskew(s)f\,\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle=\lim_{n\to\infty}\lim_{t\to\infty}t^{-1}\int_{0}^{t}\cosson(s)f\,\mathrm{d}s.

Proposition 3.1
.

We have

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to\infty}t^{-1}\int_{0}^{t}\cosso(s)f\,\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle=M_{\alpha,\beta}f,\qquad f\in\mathfrak{C}(\mathbb{R}_{\sharp}),

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to\infty}t^{-1}\int_{0}^{t}\cskew(s)f\,\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle=M_{\alpha,\beta}f,\quad f\in\mathfrak{C}[-\infty,\infty],

where

M_{\alpha,\beta}f\coloneqq{\textstyle\frac{\beta}{\alpha+\beta}}f(-\infty)+{\textstyle\frac{\alpha}{\alpha+\beta}}f(\infty),\qquad f\in\mathfrak{C}(\mathbb{R}_{\sharp}).

Proof.

Because of (
3.1
), for
\widetilde{f_{\ell}}
of (
2.13
), we have

\lim_{t\to\infty}t^{-1}\int_{0}^{t}C(s)\widetilde{f_{\ell}}\,\mathrm{d}s={\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}(f(-\infty)+{\textstyle\frac{\beta-\alpha}{\alpha+\beta}}f(-\infty)+\textstyle{\frac{2\alpha}{\alpha+\beta}}f(\infty))=M_{\alpha,\beta}f.

and a similar calculation based on (
2.14
) shows that
\lim_{t\to\infty}t^{-1}\int_{0}^{t}C(s)\widetilde{f_{\textnormal{r}}}\,\mathrm{d}s

=M_{\alpha,\beta}f
too. Hence,
\lim_{t\to\infty}t^{-1}\int_{0}^{t}C_{\textnormal{D}}(s)E_{\alpha,\beta}^{\textnormal{skew}}f\,\mathrm{d}s=(M_{\alpha,\beta}f,M_{\alpha,\beta}f)\in\mathcal{C}
,
and thus

\lim_{t\to\infty}t^{-1}\int_{0}^{t}\cskew(s)f\,\mathrm{d}s=R(M_{\alpha,\beta}f,M_{\alpha,\beta}f)=M_{\alpha,\beta}f,

showing the second relation to be proved.

The proof of the first of these relations is rather similar. For
\widetilde{f_{\ell}}
of (
2.9
)

\lim_{t\to\infty}t^{-1}\int_{0}^{t}C(s)\widetilde{f_{\ell}}\,\mathrm{d}s=f(-\infty)+{\textstyle\frac{\alpha}{\alpha+\beta}}(f(\infty)-f(-\infty))=M_{\alpha,\beta}f,

because

\lim_{x\to\infty}e_{\alpha+\beta}*[f-f^{\textnormal{T}}](x)={\textstyle\frac{1}{\alpha+\beta}}(f(\infty)-f(-\infty))
.
Since the calculation for
\widetilde{f_{\textnormal{r}}}
gives the same result, we argue as above to complete the proof.
∎

4.
Convergence of projections

The abstract Kelvin formula (
2.8
) works well because the extension operator

E_{\alpha,\beta}^{\textnormal{s-o}}
is shaped by transmission conditions (
1.2
) in a very specific manner. In particular, the space

\mathcal{C}_{\alpha,\beta}^{\textnormal{s-o}}\subset\mathcal{C}

of extensions of all elements of
\mathfrak{C}(\mathbb{R}_{\sharp})
is invariant under the basic cartesian product cosine family
\{C_{\textnormal{D}}(t),t\in\mathbb{R}\}
,

It is one of the main findings of
[
8
]
that
\mathcal{C}_{\alpha,\beta}^{\textnormal{s-o}}
is
complemented
. In fact, Section 3 in
[
8
]
provides an explicit form of a natural projection of
\mathcal{C}
on this subspace. A ‘nearly orthogonal’ projection, say,
P_{\alpha,\beta}^{\textnormal{s-o}}
on
\mathcal{C}_{\alpha,\beta}^{\textnormal{s-o}}
is given by

P_{\alpha,\beta}^{\textnormal{s-o}}(f_{1},f_{2})=(g_{1},g_{2})

where

\displaystyle\begin{split}g_{1}(x)&=f_{1}^{e}(x)+{\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}(\gamma+2\alpha)\int_{-\infty}^{x}\big[\textstyle{\frac{1}{\gamma}}k_{1}(y)-{\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}k_{2}(y)\big]\mathrm{e}^{-\gamma(x-y)}\,\mathrm{d}y\\
&\phantom{=}-{\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}(\gamma-2\alpha)\int_{x}^{\infty}\big[\textstyle{\frac{1}{\gamma}}k_{1}(y)+{\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}k_{2}(y)\big]\mathrm{e}^{\gamma(x-y)}\,\mathrm{d}y,\\
g_{2}(x)&=f_{2}^{e}(x)+{\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}(\gamma+2\beta)\int_{x}^{\infty}\big[\textstyle{\frac{1}{\gamma}}k_{1}(y)+{\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}k_{2}(y)\big]\mathrm{e}^{\gamma(x-y)}\,\mathrm{d}y\\
&\phantom{=}-{\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}(\gamma-2\beta)\int_{-\infty}^{x}\big[\textstyle{\frac{1}{\gamma}}k_{1}(y)-{\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}k_{2}(y)\big]\mathrm{e}^{-\gamma(x-y)}\,\mathrm{d}y,\qquad x\in\mathbb{R},\end{split}

(4.1)

and

\displaystyle k_{1}\coloneqq\alpha f_{1}^{o}+\beta f_{2}^{o},\qquad k_{2}\coloneqq f_{1}^{e}-f_{2}^{e}.

We will show that convergence of solution families described in the previous section is accompanied by convergence of the related projections. We will show namely that projections
P_{n\alpha,n\beta}^{\textnormal{s-o}}
converge strongly to a projection on the space of extensions related to the skew Brownian motion.

Theorem 4.1
.

(a)

For every
(f_{1},f_{2})\in\mathcal{C}
, we have

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}P_{n\alpha,n\beta}^{\textnormal{s-o}}(f_{1},f_{2})=P_{\alpha,\beta}^{\textnormal{skew}}(f_{1},f_{2}),

where

P_{\alpha,\beta}^{\textnormal{skew}}(f_{1},f_{2})\coloneqq\big(f_{1}^{e}+\textstyle{\frac{2\alpha}{\gamma^{2}}}k_{1}-{\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}k_{2},f_{2}^{e}+\textstyle{\frac{2\beta}{\gamma^{2}}}k_{1}+{\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}k_{2}\big).

(b)

The map
P_{\alpha,\beta}^{\textnormal{skew}}
is a projection on the space

\mathcal{C}_{\alpha,\beta}^{\textnormal{skew}}\coloneqq\{(g_{1},g_{2})\in\mathcal{C}\colon g_{1}^{e}=g_{2}^{e},\beta g_{1}^{o}=\alpha g_{2}^{o}\}.

(c)

The space
\mathcal{C}_{\alpha,\beta}^{\textnormal{skew}}
is precisely the subspace of extensions related to the skew Brownian motion generated by
A^{\textnormal{{skew}}}_{\alpha,\beta}
.

Proof.

(a)
From the first formula in (
4.1
)
it follows that the first coordinate of
P_{n\alpha,n\beta}^{\textnormal{s-o}}(f_{1},f_{2})
is

\displaystyle g_{1,n}(x)

\displaystyle=f_{1}^{e}(x)+{\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}(\gamma+2\alpha)n\int_{-\infty}^{x}\big[\textstyle{\frac{1}{\gamma}}k_{1}(y)-{\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}k_{2}(y)\big]\mathrm{e}^{-n\gamma(x-y)}\,\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle\phantom{=}-{\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}(\gamma-2\alpha)n\int_{x}^{\infty}\big[\textstyle{\frac{1}{\gamma}}k_{1}(y)+{\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}k_{2}(y)\big]\mathrm{e}^{n\gamma(x-y)}\,\mathrm{d}y,\qquad x\in\mathbb{R}.

Lemma
7.1
in Appendix implies now that
g_{1,n}
converges in the norm of
\mathfrak{C}[-\infty,\infty]
, as
n\to\infty
, to

f_{1}^{e}+\frac{2\alpha}{\gamma^{2}}k_{1}-\frac{1}{2}k_{2}
, as desired.

For the proof of convergence of the second coordinate of
P_{n\alpha,n\beta}^{\textnormal{s-o}}(f_{1},f_{2})
, denoted
g_{2,n}
, we proceed analogously. Namely, by (
4.1
), we have

\displaystyle g_{2,n}(x)

\displaystyle=f_{2}^{e}(x)+{\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}(\gamma+2\beta)n\int_{x}^{\infty}\big[\textstyle{\frac{1}{\gamma}}k_{1}(y)+{\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}k_{2}(y)\big]\mathrm{e}^{n\gamma(x-y)}\,\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle\phantom{=}-{\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}(\gamma-2\beta)n\int_{-\infty}^{x}\big[\textstyle{\frac{1}{\gamma}}k_{1}(y)-{\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}k_{2}(y)\big]\mathrm{e}^{-n\gamma(x-y)}\,\mathrm{d}y,\qquad x\in\mathbb{R}.

Hence, by Lemma
7.1
,
g_{2,n}
converges in the norm of
\mathfrak{C}[-\infty,\infty]
to

f_{2}^{e}+\frac{2\beta}{\gamma^{2}}k_{1}+\frac{1}{2}k_{2}
.

(b)
As a strong limit of projections,
P_{\alpha,\beta}^{\textnormal{skew}}
is a projection also. To characterize its range we consider
(f_{1},f_{2})\in\mathcal{C}
and
(g_{1},g_{2})=P_{\alpha,\beta}^{\textnormal{skew}}(f_{1},f_{2})
.
Since
f_{1}^{e},f_{2}^{e},

k_{1}
and
k_{2}
all belong to
\mathfrak{C}[-\infty,\infty]
, it follows that so do
g_{1}
and
g_{2}
. Furthermore,

g_{1}^{e}=f_{1}^{e}-{\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}k_{2}={\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}(f_{1}^{e}+f_{2}^{e})=f_{2}^{e}+{\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}k_{2}=g_{2}^{e}
, because
k_{1}
is odd and
k_{2}
is even.
Similarly, we see that
g_{1}^{o}=\frac{2\alpha}{\gamma^{2}}k_{1}
and
g_{2}^{o}=\frac{2\beta}{\gamma^{2}}k_{1}
.
This shows that
(g_{1},g_{2})\in\mathcal{C}_{\alpha,\beta}^{\textnormal{skew}}
, that is, that the range of
P_{\alpha,\beta}^{\textnormal{skew}}
is contained in
\mathcal{C}_{\alpha,\beta}^{\textnormal{skew}}
.

To prove the other inclusion, we assume that
\beta f_{1}^{o}=\alpha f_{2}^{o}
and
f_{1}^{e}=f_{2}^{e}.

Then,
2\alpha k_{1}=\gamma^{2}f_{1}^{o}
,
2\beta k_{1}=\gamma^{2}f_{2}^{o}
and
k_{2}=0
. Hence the definition of
g_{1}
and
g_{2}
simplifies to
g_{1}=f_{1}^{e}+f_{1}^{o}=f_{1}
and
g_{2}=f_{2}^{e}+f_{2}^{o}=f_{2}
. This means that for
(f_{1},f_{2})\in\mathcal{C}_{\alpha,\beta}^{\textnormal{skew}}
,
P_{\alpha,\beta}^{\textnormal{skew}}(f_{1},f_{2})=(f_{1},f_{2})
. Therefore, the range of
P_{\alpha,\beta}^{\textnormal{skew}}
contains
\mathcal{C}_{\alpha,\beta}^{\textnormal{skew}}
and the proof of (b) is complete.

(c)
Relations (
2.13
) and (
2.14
) show that the subspace of extensions related to the skew Brownian motion can be characterized as the space of
(g_{1},g_{2})\in\mathcal{C}
satisfying
g_{1}(x)=\frac{\beta-\alpha}{{\alpha+\beta}}g_{1}(-x)+\frac{2\alpha}{{\alpha+\beta}}g_{2}(x)
and
g_{2}(-x)=\frac{2\beta}{{\alpha+\beta}}g_{1}(-x)+\frac{\alpha-\beta}{{\alpha+\beta}}g_{2}(x)
for
x\geq 0
.
Multiplying by
\alpha+\beta
and rearranging (using (
1.4
)) we obtain the following equivalent form of these equations:

\displaystyle\beta g_{1}^{o}

\displaystyle=\alpha(g_{2}-g_{1}^{e}),\qquad\alpha g_{2}^{o}=\beta(g_{2}^{e}-g_{1}^{\textnormal{T}})\quad\textrm{on }\mathbb{R}^{+}.

(4.2)

These, in turn, are equivalent to

\displaystyle g_{1}^{e}

\displaystyle=g_{2}^{e},\qquad\beta g_{1}^{o}=\alpha g_{2}^{o}\quad\textrm{on }\mathbb{R}^{+}.

(4.3)

Indeed, summing sides of (
4.2
) and rearranging once again yields the first relation in (
4.3
), and then the first relation in (
4.2
) renders the second in (
4.3
). Conversely, a straightforward argument leads from (
4.3
) to (
4.2
). Finally, equations featured in (
4.3
) hold on
\mathbb{R}^{+}
whenever they hold on
\mathbb{R}
.
Hence, the space of extensions equals
\mathcal{C}_{\alpha,\beta}^{\textnormal{skew}}
. ∎

Remark 4
.

The operator
P_{\alpha,\beta}^{\textnormal{skew}}
mimics orthogonal projection in the Hilbert space of square integrable functions on
\mathbb{R}
. More precisely, for
(f_{1},f_{2})\in\mathcal{C}
, the pair
P_{\alpha,\beta}^{\textnormal{skew}}(f_{1},f_{2})
minimizes the value of

L(g_{1},g_{2})\coloneqq\int_{-y}^{y}(g_{1}(x)-f_{1}(x))^{2}+(g_{2}(x)-f_{2}(x))^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x

over all
(g_{1},g_{2})\in\mathcal{C}_{\alpha,\beta}^{\textnormal{skew}}
for any positive
y
.
To prove this claim, we assume for a moment that
\alpha\neq\beta
. Summing the sides of the first equation in (
4.3
) multiplied by
\alpha
with the sides of the second, we check that
g_{1}(-x)=\frac{{\alpha+\beta}}{\beta-\alpha}g_{1}(x)-\frac{2\alpha}{\beta-\alpha}g_{2}(x)
for
(g_{1},g_{2})\in\mathcal{C}_{\alpha,\beta}^{\textnormal{skew}}
and
x\geq 0
. Also, repeating these calculations with
\alpha
replaced by
\beta
yields
g_{2}(-x)=\frac{2\beta}{\beta-\alpha}g_{1}(x)-\frac{{\alpha+\beta}}{\beta-\alpha}g_{2}(x)
for
x\geq 0
. It follows that

\displaystyle L(g_{1},g_{2})=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{y}(g_{1}(x)-f_{1}(x))^{2}+\Big({\textstyle\frac{{\alpha+\beta}}{\beta-\alpha}}g_{1}(x)-{\textstyle\frac{2\alpha}{\beta-\alpha}}g_{2}(x)-f_{1}(-x)\Big)^{2}

\displaystyle+(g_{2}(x)-f_{2}(x))^{2}+\Big({\textstyle\frac{2\beta}{\beta-\alpha}}g_{1}(x)-{\textstyle\frac{{\alpha+\beta}}{\beta-\alpha}}g_{2}(x)-f_{2}(-x)\Big)^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x.

We can minimize the integrand for each
x\in(0,y)
by finding the minimum of a quadratic function in two variables (
f_{1}(\pm x)
and
f_{2}(\pm x)
are treated as fixed parameters). Direct calculations verify that such solution coincides with
P_{\alpha,\beta}^{\textnormal{skew}}(f_{1},f_{2})
on the interval
(-y,y)
.

The case
\alpha=\beta
is straightforward because it comes down to the fact that
(g_{1},g_{2})=({\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}(f_{1}+f_{2}),{\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}(f_{1}+f_{2}))=P^{\textnormal{skew}}_{\alpha,\alpha}(f_{1},f_{2})
miminizes

L(g_{1},g_{2})=\int_{-y}^{y}(g_{1}(x)-f_{1}(x))^{2}+(g_{1}(x)-f_{2}(x))^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x
over all
(g_{1},g_{2})\in\mathcal{C}
satisfying
g_{1}=g_{2}
.

Remark 5
.

Arguing as in Remark
3
, we can estimate the rate of convergence in Theorem
4.1
(a); we omit the details.

5.
Convergence of ‘complementary’ families

5.1.
Definition of ‘complementary’ cosine families

The fact that
P_{\alpha,\beta}^{\textnormal{s-o}}
of Section
4
is a projection on
\mathcal{C}_{\alpha,\beta}^{\textnormal{s-o}}\subset\mathcal{C}
implies the existence of the subspace
\mathcal{C}_{\alpha,\beta}^{\textnormal{o-s}}
that complements
\mathcal{C}_{\alpha,\beta}^{\textnormal{s-o}}
to the entire
\mathcal{C}
:

\mathcal{C}=\mathcal{C}_{\alpha,\beta}^{\textnormal{s-o}}\oplus\mathcal{C}_{\alpha,\beta}^{\textnormal{o-s}};

\mathcal{C}_{\alpha,\beta}^{\textnormal{o-s}}
is defined as the kernel of
P_{\alpha,\beta}^{\textnormal{s-o}}
or, equivalently, as the image of
\mathcal{C}
via

P_{\alpha,\beta}^{\textnormal{o-s}}\coloneqq I_{\mathcal{C}}-P_{\alpha,\beta}^{\textnormal{s-o}}.

(5.1)

Interestingly,
\mathcal{C}_{\alpha,\beta}^{\textnormal{o-s}}
turns out to be invariant under the cartesian product basic cosine family also, and in fact is unequivocally shaped by certain boundary conditions.

Elaborating on this succinct statement, we first define the space

\mathfrak{C}_{\textnormal{ov}}(\mathbb{R}_{\sharp})

as the subspace of functions
f\in\mathfrak{C}(\mathbb{R}_{\sharp})
satisfying
f(0+)=-f(0-)
(‘
ov
’ stands for ‘opposite values’). As it transpires, each member
f
of
\mathfrak{C}_{\textnormal{ov}}(\mathbb{R}_{\sharp})
can be identified with the pair

E_{\alpha,\beta}^{\textnormal{o-s}}f=(\widetilde{f_{\ell}},\widetilde{f_{\textnormal{r}}})\in\mathcal{C}_{\alpha,\beta}^{\textnormal{o-s}}

determined by

\displaystyle\widetilde{f_{\ell}}(x)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}f(x),&$x<0$,\\
-f(-x)-2f(0+)e_{\alpha+\beta}(x)-2e_{\alpha+\beta}*[\beta f-\alpha f^{\textnormal{T}}](x),&$x>0,$\end{cases}

and

\displaystyle\widetilde{f_{\textnormal{r}}}(x)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}-f(-x)+2f(0+)e_{\alpha+\beta}(-x)+2e_{\alpha+\beta}*[\beta f-\alpha f^{\textnormal{T}}](-x),&$x<0$,\\
f(x),&$x>0$.\end{cases}

As a result, the formula

\cossot(t)\coloneqq RC_{D}(t)E_{\alpha,\beta}^{\textnormal{o-s}},\qquad t\in\mathbb{R},

(5.2)

defines a cosine family in
\mathfrak{C}_{\textnormal{ov}}(\mathbb{R}_{\sharp})
. Since
\|E_{\alpha,\beta}^{\textnormal{o-s}}\|\leq 5
, we have

\|\cossot(t)\|\leq 5,\qquad\alpha,\beta\geq 0,t\in\mathbb{R}.

It is also proved in
[
8
]
that the generator, say,
A^{\textnormal{o-s}}_{\alpha,\beta}
, of this cosine family can be characterized as follows.
Its domain
D(A^{\textnormal{o-s}}_{\alpha,\beta})
consists of functions
f\in\mathfrak{C}_{\textnormal{ov}}(\mathbb{R}_{\sharp})
satisfying the following
conditions:

(a)

f
is twice continuously differentiable in both

(-\infty,0-]
and
[0+,\infty)
, separately, with left-hand and
right-hand derivatives at
x=0\mp
, respectively,

(b)

both the limits
\lim_{x\to\infty}f^{\prime\prime}(x)
and

\lim_{x\to-\infty}f^{\prime\prime}(x)
exist and are finite, and

(c)

f^{\prime\prime}(0+)=\alpha f^{\prime}(0-)+\beta f^{\prime}(0+)\quad\text{ and }\quad f^{\prime\prime}(0+)=-f^{\prime\prime}(0-).

(5.3)

Moreover, for such
f
we have

A^{\textnormal{o-s}}_{\alpha,\beta}f=f^{\prime\prime};

we note in passing that the second condition in (
5.3
) is necessary for
f^{\prime\prime}
to belong to
\mathfrak{C}_{\textnormal{ov}}(\mathbb{R}_{\sharp})
.

It is worth stressing two points here. First of all, because of the way
\cossot
was defined, it can be seen as a ‘complementary’ cosine family to
\cosso
. Moreover, it can be shown that if (
5.2
) is to define the cosine family generated by
A^{\textnormal{o-s}}_{\alpha,\beta}
, the extension operator
E_{\alpha,\beta}^{\textnormal{o-s}}
has to be defined as above. In this sense,
E_{\alpha,\beta}^{\textnormal{o-s}}
, and thus
\mathcal{C}_{\alpha,\beta}^{\textnormal{o-s}}
also, is shaped by the boundary conditions (
5.3
).

5.2.
Convergence of ‘complementary’ solution families

We know from Theorem
2.1
that cosine families
\cosson
converge to the cosine families
\cskew
as
n\to\infty
, and that implies convergence of the related semigroups. In this section, we want to complement the aforementioned theorem with the result concerning convergence of
\{\cossot,\,t\in\mathbb{R}\}
introduced in Section
5.1
. We will show that (see Theorem
5.1
), in contrast to
\cosson
which converge only on a subspace of
\mathfrak{C}(\mathbb{R}_{\sharp})
,
\cossotn
converge, as
n\to\infty
, on the entire
\mathfrak{C}_{\textnormal{ov}}(\mathbb{R}_{\sharp})
. Moreover, perhaps more surprisingly, the limit cosine family is a mirror image of that related to skew Brownian motion (see Proposition
5.2
).

Theorem 5.1
.

We have

\lim_{n\to\infty}\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}\|\cossotn(t)f-\cweks(t)f\|=0,\qquad f\in\mathfrak{C}_{\textnormal{ov}}(\mathbb{R}_{\sharp}),

(5.4)

where
\cweks(t)\coloneqq RC_{\textnormal{D}}(t)E_{\alpha,\beta}^{\textnormal{weks}}
and
E_{\alpha,\beta}^{\textnormal{weks}}f=(\widetilde{f_{\ell}},\widetilde{f_{\textnormal{r}}})
is defined by

\displaystyle\widetilde{f_{\ell}}(x)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}f(x),&$x<0$,\\
\frac{\alpha-\beta}{{\alpha+\beta}}f(-x)-\frac{2\beta}{{\alpha+\beta}}f(x),&$x>0,$\end{cases}

(5.5)

and

\displaystyle\widetilde{f_{\textnormal{r}}}(x)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}-\frac{2\alpha}{{\alpha+\beta}}f(x)+\frac{\beta-\alpha}{{\alpha+\beta}}f(-x),&$x<0$,\\
f(x),&$x>0$.\end{cases}

(5.6)

As a consequence,

\lim_{n\to\infty}\sup_{t\geq 0}\big\|\mathrm{e}^{tA^{\textnormal{o-s}}_{n\alpha,n\beta}}-T(t)f\big\|=0,\qquad f\in\mathfrak{C}_{\textnormal{ov}}(\mathbb{R}_{\sharp}),

where
T(0)f=f
and

T(t)f={\textstyle\frac{1}{2\sqrt{\pi t}}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{s^{2}}{4t}}\cweks(s)f\,\mathrm{d}s,\qquad t>0,f\in\mathfrak{C}_{\textnormal{ov}}(\mathbb{R}_{\sharp}).

Proof.

It suffices to show (
5.4
), because the rest then can be proved as in Remark
1
. Indeed,
A^{\textnormal{o-s}}_{n\alpha,n\beta}
is the generator of
\cossotn
, and
\cweks
is a cosine family as a limit of cosine families. But, to establish (
5.4
) it suffices to check that

\lim_{n\to\infty}E_{n\alpha,n\beta}^{\textnormal{o-s}}f=E_{\alpha,\beta}^{\textnormal{weks}}f,\qquad f\in\mathfrak{C}_{\textnormal{ov}}(\mathbb{R}_{\sharp})

(5.7)

for
E_{\alpha,\beta}^{\textnormal{o-s}}
defined in the preceding section.

To this end, we note that, for
f\in\mathfrak{C}_{\textnormal{ov}}(\mathbb{R}_{\sharp})
, the continuous function
\phi
on
\mathbb{R}^{+}
determined by
\phi(x)=\beta f(x)-\alpha f(-x),x>0
, (in particular
\phi(0)=(\alpha+\beta)f(0+)
) belongs to
\mathfrak{C}[0,\infty]
. Therefore, by
Lemma
7.1
(c),

f(0+)e_{n(\alpha+\beta)}(x)+ne_{n({\alpha+\beta})}*[\beta f-\alpha f^{\textnormal{T}}](x)

converges, as
n\to\infty
, to
(\alpha+\beta)^{-1}[\beta f(x)-\alpha f^{\textnormal{T}}(x)]
uniformly in
x\geq 0.

It follows that the first coordinate of
E_{n\alpha,n\beta}^{\textnormal{o-s}}f
converges in the norm of
\mathfrak{C}[-\infty,\infty]
to
\widetilde{f_{\ell}}
defined by (
5.5
).
Since, similarly, the second coordinate of
E_{n\alpha,n\beta}^{\textnormal{o-s}}f
converges to
\widetilde{f_{\textnormal{r}}}
of (
5.6
), the proof is complete.
∎

The space
\mathfrak{C}_{\textnormal{ov}}(\mathbb{R}_{\sharp})
in which
\cweks
is defined is somewhat unusual, and it is perhaps the main reason why
\cweks
remains a bit mysterious even if a direct characterization of its generator is provided (that characterization can be given, but does not seem to be very informative). Fortunately,
\mathfrak{C}_{\textnormal{ov}}(\mathbb{R}_{\sharp})
is isometrically isomorphic to the space
\mathfrak{C}[-\infty,\infty]
where a number of semigroups and cosine families can be interpreted probabilistically, and it turns out that the image of
\cweks
in
\mathfrak{C}[-\infty,\infty]
coincides with
\cskewr
—that is, describes the skew Brownian motion with the roles of
\alpha
and
\beta
exchanged.

To make these remarks more precise, we introduce the isometric isomorphism

J\colon\mathfrak{C}_{\textnormal{ov}}(\mathbb{R}_{\sharp})\to\mathfrak{C}[-\infty,\infty]

of
\mathfrak{C}_{\textnormal{ov}}(\mathbb{R}_{\sharp})
and
\mathfrak{C}[-\infty,\infty]
, given by
Jf(x)=-f(x),x<0
,
Jf(x)=f(x),x>0
and
Jf(0)=f(0+)
. In terms of
J
our result takes the following form.

Proposition 5.2
.

For all
t\in\mathbb{R}
,

J\cweks(t)J^{-1}=\cskewr(t).

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{J}
be the isometric automorphism of
\mathcal{C}
given by
\mathcal{J}(f_{1},f_{2})=(-f_{1},f_{2})
. It is clear that
\mathcal{J}
is its own inverse. We note the following three identities that can be established by a straightforward calculation.

(i)

E_{\alpha,\beta}^{\textnormal{weks}}J^{-1}=\mathcal{J}E_{\beta,\alpha}^{\textnormal{skew}}
.

(ii)

\mathcal{J}C_{\textnormal{D}}(t)\mathcal{J}=C_{\textnormal{D}}(t),t\in\mathbb{R}
.

(iii)

JR(f_{1},f_{2})=R\mathcal{J}(f_{1},f_{2}),
provided that
f_{1}(0)=-f_{2}(0)
.

Hence,

\displaystyle J\cweks(t)J^{-1}

\displaystyle=(JR)C_{\textnormal{D}}(t)(E_{\alpha,\beta}^{\textnormal{weks}}J^{-1})=(R\mathcal{J})C_{\textnormal{D}}(t)(\mathcal{J}E_{\beta,\alpha}^{\textnormal{skew}})

\displaystyle=RC_{\textnormal{D}}(t)E_{\beta,\alpha}^{\textnormal{skew}}=\cskewr(t),

as desired.
∎

We have established that, as
n\to\infty
, along with
\cosson
converge also complementary families
\cossotn
; the first of these families converge to
\cskew
, the second to a mirror image of
\cskewr
.

Remark 6
.

In Theorem
5.1
(a) the rate of convergence can also be estimated. Arguing as in Remark
3
we can prove that, provided that
Jf
is Lipschitz continuous with Lipschitz constant
L
,
\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}\|\cossotn(t)f-\cweks(t)f\|\leq\frac{K}{n}
for
K\coloneqq\frac{2L}{\alpha+\beta}
.

6.
Additional results

To complete the paper, we need to answer two natural questions. First of all, as a direct consequence of Theorem
4.1
(a),

\lim_{n\to\infty}P_{n\alpha,n\beta}^{\textnormal{o-s}}(f_{1},f_{2})=P_{\alpha,\beta}^{\textnormal{weks}}(f_{1},f_{2})

where
P_{\alpha,\beta}^{\textnormal{o-s}}
was defined in (
5.1
) whereas

P_{\alpha,\beta}^{\textnormal{weks}}(f_{1},f_{2})\coloneqq\big(f_{1}^{o}-\textstyle{\frac{2\alpha}{\gamma^{2}}}k_{1}+{\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}k_{2},f_{2}^{o}-\textstyle{\frac{2\beta}{\gamma^{2}}}k_{1}-{\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}k_{2}\big).

It is natural to ask whether
P_{\alpha,\beta}^{\textnormal{weks}}
has anything to do with
\cweks
. As the following result shows, the answer is in the affirmative:
P_{\alpha,\beta}^{\textnormal{weks}}
projects on the invariant space of extensions related to
\cweks
, that is, on the range of the operator
E_{\alpha,\beta}^{\textnormal{weks}}
.

Proposition 6.1
.

The map
P_{\alpha,\beta}^{\textnormal{weks}}
is a projection on the space

\mathcal{C}_{\alpha,\beta}^{\textnormal{weks}}\coloneqq\{(g_{1},g_{2})\in\mathcal{C}\colon g_{1}^{e}=-g_{2}^{e},\alpha g_{1}^{o}=-\beta g_{2}^{o}\}.

Moreover,
\mathcal{C}_{\alpha,\beta}^{\textnormal{weks}}
is the range of
E_{\alpha,\beta}^{\textnormal{weks}}
.

Proof.

We start by noting that
P_{\alpha,\beta}^{\textnormal{weks}}
is a projection, being the limit of projections. Hence, to prove the first statement it suffices to find the range of
P_{\alpha,\beta}^{\textnormal{weks}}
. To this end, for
(f_{1},f_{2})\in\mathcal{C}
, let
(g_{1},g_{2})=P_{\alpha,\beta}^{\textnormal{weks}}(f_{1},f_{2})
, that is,

g_{1}=f_{1}^{o}-\textstyle{\frac{2\alpha}{\gamma^{2}}}k_{1}+{\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}k_{2}
and
g_{2}=f_{2}^{o}-\textstyle{\frac{2\beta}{\gamma^{2}}}k_{1}-{\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}k_{2}
.
The fact that
g_{1},g_{2}\in\mathfrak{C}[-\infty,\infty]
follows from point (b) in Theorem
4.1
. Also,
as in the proof of that result, we obtain

g_{1}^{e}={\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}(f_{1}^{e}-f_{2}^{e})=-g_{2}^{e}
and
\alpha g_{1}^{o}=\frac{2\alpha\beta}{\gamma^{2}}(\beta f_{1}^{o}-\alpha f_{2}^{o})=-\beta g_{2}^{o}

implying that
(g_{1},g_{2})\in\mathcal{C}_{\alpha,\beta}^{\textnormal{weks}}.

Moreover, it is easy to check that, if
(f_{1},f_{2})\in\mathcal{C}_{\alpha,\beta}^{\textnormal{weks}}
, then
g_{1}=f_{1}
and
g_{2}=f_{2}
. This proves the first statement in the proposition.

Identity (i) presented in the proof of Theorem
5.2
can be rewritten as
E_{\alpha,\beta}^{\textnormal{weks}}=\mathcal{J}E_{\beta,\alpha}^{\textnormal{skew}}J
. Since the range of
J
is the entire
\mathfrak{C}[-\infty,\infty]
, the range of
E_{\alpha,\beta}^{\textnormal{weks}}
is the image of the range of
E_{\beta,\alpha}^{\textnormal{skew}}
under
\mathcal{J}
. However, the range of
E_{\beta,\alpha}^{\textnormal{skew}}
is characterized in Theorem
4.1
(b). This characterization reveals that the image of the range of
E_{\beta,\alpha}^{\textnormal{skew}}
is precisely
\mathcal{C}_{\alpha,\beta}^{\textnormal{weks}}
as defined above. ∎

Corollary 6.2
.

The space
\mathcal{C}
is a direct sum of two spaces that are invariant under the basic Cartesian product cosine family:

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}=\mathcal{C}_{\alpha,\beta}^{\textnormal{skew}}\oplus\mathcal{C}_{\alpha,\beta}^{\textnormal{weks}}.

The second, and final, question one need to answer in this paper reads: is there a counterpart of Proposition
3.1
pertaining to the complementary cosine families
\cossot
? The answer is given below; we omit the proof of this statement, since it is very similar to that of Proposition
3.1
.

Proposition 6.3
.

For all
f\in\mathfrak{C}_{\textnormal{ov}}(\mathbb{R}_{\sharp})
,

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to\infty}t^{-1}\int_{0}^{t}\cossot(s)f\,\mathrm{d}s=\lim_{t\to\infty}t^{-1}\int_{0}^{t}\cweks(s)f\,\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle=N_{\alpha,\beta}f,

where
N_{\alpha,\beta}f\in\mathfrak{C}_{\textnormal{ov}}(\mathbb{R}_{\sharp})
is defined as follows

N_{\alpha,\beta}f(x)\coloneqq\begin{cases}{\textstyle\frac{\alpha}{\alpha+\beta}}f(-\infty)-{\textstyle\frac{\beta}{\alpha+\beta}}f(\infty),&x<0,\\
{\textstyle\frac{\beta}{\alpha+\beta}}f(\infty)-{\textstyle\frac{\alpha}{\alpha+\beta}}f(-\infty),&x>0.\end{cases}

We note that this result agrees nicely with Proposition
3.1
and Proposition
5.2
, for we have

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to\infty}t^{-1}\int_{0}^{t}\cskewr(s)f\,\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle=J\lim_{t\to\infty}t^{-1}\int_{0}^{t}\cweks(t)J^{-1}f\,\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle=JN_{\alpha,\beta}J^{-1}f=M_{\beta,\alpha}f.

7.
Appendix

The following lemma is indispensable in proving our convergence theorems.

Lemma 7.1
.

Let
a>0
,
\phi\in\mathfrak{C}[-\infty,\infty]
and
\varphi\in\mathfrak{C}[0,\infty]
be given. Then,

(a)

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}na\int_{-\infty}^{x}\mathrm{e}^{-na(x-y)}\phi(y)\,\mathrm{d}y=\phi(x),

(b)

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}na\int_{x}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{na(x-y)}\phi(y)\,\mathrm{d}y=\phi(x)
,

(c)

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\left[na\int_{0}^{x}\mathrm{e}^{-na(x-y)}\varphi(y)\,\mathrm{d}y+\mathrm{e}^{-nax}\varphi(0)\right]=\varphi(x).

The first two limits are uniform with respect to
x\in\mathbb{R}
, the third is uniform with respect to
x\geq 0.

Proof.

To prove (a), we apply a typical argument involving so-called Dirac sequences (see e.g.
[
35
]
*pp. 227–235 or
[
21
]
*pp. 219–220). Namely, given
\varepsilon>0
we can find a
\delta
such that
|\phi(x)-\phi(y)|<\frac{\varepsilon}{2}
as long as
|x-y|<\delta
; this is because members of
\mathfrak{C}[-\infty,\infty]
are uniformly continuous. Having chosen such a
\delta
we can also find an
n_{0}
so large that
2\mathrm{e}^{-na\delta}\|\phi\|<\frac{\varepsilon}{2}
, provided that
n>n_{0}
, where
\|\phi\|\coloneqq\|\phi\|_{\mathfrak{C}[-\infty,\infty]}
.

Next, for any
x\in\mathbb{R}
we have

na\int_{-\infty}^{x}\mathrm{e}^{-na(x-y)}\phi(y)\,\mathrm{d}y-\phi(x)=na\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{-nay}[\phi(x-y)-\phi(x)]\,\mathrm{d}y.

It follows that for
n>n_{0}
, the absolute value of the left-hand side above does not exceed

na\int_{0}^{\delta}\mathrm{e}^{-nay}{\textstyle\frac{\varepsilon}{2}}\,\mathrm{d}y+na\int_{\delta}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{-nay}2\|\phi\|\,\mathrm{d}y={\textstyle\frac{\varepsilon}{2}}(1-\mathrm{e}^{-na\delta})+2\mathrm{e}^{-na\delta}\|\phi\|<\varepsilon.

This completes the proof,
\varepsilon>0
being arbitrary.

Condition (b) is (a) in disguise: (a) becomes (b) if
\phi
and
x
are replaced by
\phi^{\textnormal{T}}
and
-x
, respectively. Likewise, to obtain (c) we use (a) for
\phi\in\mathfrak{C}[-\infty,\infty]
defined as follows:
\phi(x)=\varphi(x),x\geq 0
and
\phi(x)=\varphi(0),x<0.

∎

Remark 7
.

The proof presented above provides no rate of convergence in (a)–(c), but suggests that this rate depends on modulus of continuity of
\phi
and
\varphi
. If we restrict ourselves to
\phi
that are Lipschitz continuous, (a) and (b) can be expressed in a more qualitative form. To wit, if
|\phi(x)-\phi(y)|\leq L|x-y|
for all
x,y\in\mathbb{R}
and certain
L>0
, we have

\displaystyle\left|na\int_{-\infty}^{x}\mathrm{e}^{-na(x-y)}\phi(y)\,\mathrm{d}y-\phi(x)\right|

\displaystyle=\left|na\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{-naz}[\phi(x-z)-\phi(x)]\,\mathrm{d}z\right|

\displaystyle\leq Lna\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{-naz}z\,\mathrm{d}z=\frac{L}{na}.

Similarly,
|na\int_{x}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{na(x-y)}\phi(y)\,\mathrm{d}y-\phi(x)|\leq\frac{L}{na}
. Moreover, if
|\varphi(x)-\varphi(y)|\leq L
for
x,y\geq 0
, then
|na\int_{0}^{x}\mathrm{e}^{-na(x-y)}\varphi(y)\,\mathrm{d}y+\mathrm{e}^{-nax}\varphi(0)-\varphi(x)|\leq\frac{L}{na}
.
