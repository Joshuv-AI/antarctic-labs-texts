Bounds for the Bergman kernel and the sup-norm of holomorphic Siegel cusp forms
Soumya Das, Hariram Krishna

Abstract
. We prove ‘polynomial in k k ’ bounds on the size of the Bergman kernel for the space of holomorphic Siegel cusp forms of degree n n and weight k k . When n = 1 , 2 n=1,2 our bounds agree with the conjectural bounds on the aforementioned size, while the lower bounds match for all n ≥ 1 n\geq 1 . For an L 2 L^{2} -normalised Siegel cusp form F F of degree 2 2 , our bound for its sup-norm is O ϵ ​ ( k 9 / 4 + ϵ ) O_{\epsilon}(k^{9/4+\epsilon}) . Further, we show that in any compact set Ω \Omega (which does not depend on k k ) contained in the Siegel fundamental domain of Sp ⁡ ( 2 , ℤ ) \operatorname{Sp}(2,\mathbb{Z}) on the Siegel upper half space, the sup-norm of F F is O Ω ​ ( k 3 / 2 − η ) O_{\Omega}(k^{3/2-\eta}) for some η > 0 \eta>0 , going beyond the ‘generic’ bound in this setting.

{\mathchoice{\mathopen{\hbox to5.00002pt{\hss\vrule height=7.5pt,depth=2.5pt,width=1.50002pt\hss}}F\mathclose{\hbox to5.00002pt{\hss\vrule height=7.5pt,depth=2.5pt,width=1.50002pt\hss}}}{\mathopen{\hbox to5.00002pt{\hss\vrule height=7.5pt,depth=2.5pt,width=1.50002pt\hss}}F\mathclose{\hbox to5.00002pt{\hss\vrule height=7.5pt,depth=2.5pt,width=1.50002pt\hss}}}{\mathopen{\hbox to5.00002pt{\hss\vrule height=5.25pt,depth=1.75pt,width=1.50002pt\hss}}F\mathclose{\hbox to5.00002pt{\hss\vrule height=5.25pt,depth=1.75pt,width=1.50002pt\hss}}}{\mathopen{\hbox to5.00002pt{\hss\vrule height=3.75pt,depth=1.25pt,width=1.50002pt\hss}}F\mathclose{\hbox to5.00002pt{\hss\vrule height=3.75pt,depth=1.25pt,width=1.50002pt\hss}}}}_{2}

Recall that one expression for the Bergman kernel
B_{k}(Z,W)
(

) is given by (we suppress the dependence on

B_{k}(Z,W)=\sum_{G\in\mathcal{B}^{n}_{k}}G(Z)\overline{G(W)}

where

\Gamma_{n}

(1.4)

B_{k}(Z,W)=\frac{a(n,k)}{2}\,\sum\nolimits_{\gamma\in\Gamma_{n}}\det\Big(\frac{Z-\overline{W}}{2i}\Big)^{-k}\Big|_{k}^{(W)}\gamma\quad\quad(k\geq 2n+2);

where
a(n,k)
is a constant (cf. (
7.2
)). The Bergman kernel appears in the theory of automorphic functions in many connections, e.g., as a reproducing kernel for the space
S^{n}_{k}
(cf.
[
Kli90
]
) and is used in the theory of metrization, for dimension formulae, as the ratio of the ‘canonical’ and the hyperbolic metrics on the quotient space
\Gamma_{n}\backslash\mathcal{H}_{n}
(cf.
[
JK04
]
) etc. Basic properties of

have been studied for e.g. in
[

Kli90
]

\mathbb{B}_{k}(Z,Z):=\sum\nolimits_{F\in\mathcal{B}_{k}^{n}}{|F(Z)|^{2}\det(Y)^{k}}\quad\quad(=\det(Y)^{k}B_{k}(Z,Z)),

1.1.
Remarks and discussions about proofs

When
n=1
, Conjecture
1.2
is known from the works
[
FJK19
,
JK04
]
by the heat-kernel method (also see
[
AB18
]
for the case of Hilbert modular forms), but it appears with
O_{\epsilon}(k^{3/2-\epsilon})
as the lower bound. It also follows from (
1.2
) by summing over a basis, with
k^{\pm\epsilon}
defects in the upper and lower bound respectively.
When
n=1
, our lower bound in (
1.3
) gives
k^{3/2}
, and in subsection
7.2
, we show how to obtain the upper bound in (
1.6
) without any recourse to the techniques using the heat-kernel (as in
[
FJK19
]
) or using the Hoffstein-Lockhart bound for the upper and lower bounds for the Fourier coefficient
a_{f}(1)
of an
L^{2}
normalised Hecke eigenform of level one (as in
[
Xia07
]
). For the upper bound in (
1.6
) we just use a uniform (with respect to
k
) Hecke-bound obtained by using trivial bounds for Kloosterman sums and Bessel functions; the purpose is to demonstrate that with such minimal hypotheses, the proof might generalise to higher degrees.

To prove Theorem
1.3
, as is well-known, we will use two kinds of estimates:
one from the Fourier expansion of
F
, and another from the analysis of the Bergman kernel for
S^{n}_{k}
.
However, as mentioned above, due to the intractability of the Fourier expansion,
we essentially use it only to obtain the cut-off for the region
(roughly it is of the form
\{Z\in\mathcal{F}_{n}\mid\det(Y)\gg k^{n}\}
, with
\mathcal{F}_{n}
being the Siegel fundamental domain for
\operatorname{Sp}(n,\mathbb{Z})
)
in which the quantity
\mathbb{B}_{k}(Z,Z)
decays exponentially (see Theorem
4.5
). When
n=2
, however, the Fourier expansion can be handled somewhat better, see section
6
.
In the complementary region, our primary tool would be the Bergman kernel, defined above. It will be clear that the Bergman kernel occupies a major portion of this paper.
In the following, we will briefly discuss the main points pertaining to the techniques leading to the each kind of estimates discussed above.

We start with some highlights on the analysis of the Fourier expansion.
Suppose that we have an average bound for the Fourier coefficients of
F\in\mathcal{B}_{k}^{n}
in the following form:

(1.18)

p_{n,k}(T):=\sum\nolimits_{F\in\mathcal{B}_{k}^{n}}|a_{F}(T)|^{2}\ll_{n}\big(\frac{(4\pi)^{nk/2}k^{\alpha}\det(T)^{k/2-\beta}}{\Gamma(k)^{n/2}}\big)^{2},

where
\alpha,\beta
are real parameters.
We comment here that it is in principle possible (at least when
k>2n
) to obtain values for
\alpha,\beta

in terms of
n
by estimating the Fourier coefficient
a_{P_{T}}(T)
(expressed in terms of Bessel functions and Kloosterman sums of matrix index)
of the
T
-th Siegel Poincaré series
P_{T}:=P^{n}_{k,T}\in S^{n}_{k}
, from the Petersson trace formula

(1.19)

\sum\nolimits_{G\in\mathcal{B}^{n}_{k}}|a_{G}(T)|^{2}=c_{n,k}^{-1}\det(T)^{k-\frac{n+1}{2}}a_{P_{T}}(T)

with the constant

(1.20)

c_{n,k}=\pi^{n(n-1)/2}(4\pi)^{n(n+1)/4-nk}\prod\nolimits_{j=1}^{n}\Gamma(k-(n+j)/2).

Unfortunately, very little is known about
\beta
(which should depend only on
n
) – we do not even know
if we can take
\beta\geq 0

with respect to this specific choice of dependence on
k
, this is crucial for us.

So we take a different route and first prove Lemma
4.1
towards this, in order to set the ball rolling. At present we do not know of any other means to obtain a result like Lemma
4.1
.
This is done using bounds for the Bergman kernel. However this only shows that
\beta\geq-\gamma_{n}
for some quadratic polynomial
\gamma_{n}>0
in
n
. However, we emphasise here that such a weak lower bound for
\beta

is sufficient
for the problem at hand, in the sense that it leads to the same bound as one would have obtained if we knew that
\beta=0
via the method of Lemma
4.1
, perhaps at the cost of
k
being larger. In fact, we can show
\beta=0
with the knowledge of Theorem
1.3
(cf. Remark
4.3
(2)). Any better result by this method is contingent on a value of
\beta
being strictly positive. Moreover, as far as we could see, it seems that the only way to get
\beta>0
in our situation is to estimate directly
a_{P_{T}}(T)
, something which seems not possible at the moment due to insufficient knowledge of the matrix argument Bessel functions and Kloosterman sums. See Remark
4.7
for more on this matter.

Ignoring the dependence on
F
, one can indeed find a
\beta>0
in (
1.18
),
as can be seen from a variety of ways, for instance by considering the Rankin-Selberg series
with respect to the Fourier coefficients, or by using Jacobi Poincaré series (see e.g.
[
BK93
]
,

[
BR88
]
). However, finding a
\beta>0
which does not go to

0
as
n\to\infty
(even ignoring the dependence on
F
) is a well-known long-standing
open problem (see
[
BK93
]
for the best-known result to date).

The other (i.e., apart from using (
1.19
)) known methods for estimation of Fourier coefficients of Siegel cusp forms seem not suitable for us, as the implied constants in these methods cannot be controlled satisfactorily.
When one looks at the Fourier coefficients on average, one can work with them. Thus
our artefact for working with the Fourier series is the quantity

p_{n,k}(T)
(cf. (
1.18
)) for which Lemma
4.1
provides a bound. The idea then is to link this information with the Fourier expansions of the constituents
F
of
\mathcal{B}_{k}^{n}
. We do this in Lemma
4.5
by employing the Cauchy-Schwartz inequality judiciously and reduce our attention to a smooth function

q_{k}(Y)
by showing that
\mathbb{B}_{k}(Z,Z)\leq q_{k}(Y)^{2}
; where

q_{k}(Y)=q_{n,k}(Y):=\sum_{T\in\Lambda_{n}}p(T)^{1/2}\,\det(Y)^{k/2}\,\exp(-2\pi\tr(TY))
.

The rest of the work on Fourier series is then to bound
q_{k}(Y)
, and this is the content of Proposition
4.6
, which is at the heart of the matters.
For the pair
(\alpha,\beta)
as in (
1.18
), the Fourier expansion of
F
yields for all
Z\in\mathcal{F}_{n}

(1.21)

\displaystyle q_{k}(Y)\ll_{n}\big(k^{n}\det(Y)^{-1}\big)^{(n+1)/4-\beta}\cdot k^{\frac{n}{4}+\alpha+\epsilon};

We show that only finitely many
T
’s contribute significantly to the size of
q_{k}(Y)
, and estimate the size of this set in Lemma
4.9
,
from which (
1.21
) follows.
We also have a version of (
1.21
) in Proposition
4.6
where we only assume that
Y
is Minkowski-reduced.

We would only use (
1.21
) when
\det(Y)\gg k^{n}
or more generally when
y_{n}\gg k
with
y_{n}
being the largest diagonal element of
Y
. In such a region, one gets the upper bound in Theorem
1.3
by appropriate values of
(\alpha,\beta)
.
By a careful choice of constant

, one can also prove that in a region

,

decays exponentially, see Lemma

.
(
1.21
) can be improved as soon as we get better values of the pair

in (

), this is discussed in Remark

.

Moreover note that by
[

, Hilfssatz 3.11]
, for any

, the quantity
\det(Y)^{k/2}|F(Z)|
attains its supremum at a point in
\mathcal{F}_{n}
. Our results (cf. Proposition
4.5
) suggest that this is attained ‘high in the cusp’, at least in the average.
This can be compared with the work of Brumley-Templier
[
BT20
]
. From Theorem
1.3
, we see that the supremum of

grows as at least

, Theorem

and the second bound in Theorem

show that such a value can only be attained in the following region:

(1.22)

\mathbb{B}_{k}(Z_{0},Z_{0})=\sup\nolimits_{Z\in\mathcal{F}_{n}}\mathbb{B}_{k}(Z,Z)\implies k^{n/3}\ll_{n}\det(\Im({Z_{0}}))\ll_{n}k^{n}

This is also supported by the results of section
8
.

When
n=2
(discussed in sections
5
,
6
), we have a somewhat better handle on the Fourier expansion. In this case instead of Lemma
4.1
, we can rely on the Fourier coefficients of Poincaré series directly to bound

; these have been worked out in a remarkable paper of Kitaoka
[
Kit84
]
.

Lastly, the bound
\sup_{Z\in\mathcal{H}_{2}}\mathbb{B}_{k}(Z,Z)\ll_{\epsilon}k^{9/2+\epsilon}
deserves special mention. The reason behind this better bound is that on the one hand we adapt, and estimate
\#\mathcal{C}_{Y}
(the finite set
\mathcal{C}_{Y}
being the effective support of the Fourier expansion) more precisely using the results in
[
Blo15
]
stemming from the fact that when
n=2,
one can express the eigenvalues of a matrix in terms of its entries. On the other hand, we crucially make use of the decay (with respect to
k
) of the ‘off-diagonal’ terms of the expression for the
T
-th Fourier coefficient of the Poincaré series
P_{T}
. A key idea that we have used while bounding the
T
-th Fourier coefficient of Poincaré series

is to keep two natural terms which arise in this course to be treated separately rather than merge them into a uniform single estimate. The resulting bound then looks like

(1.23)

p_{2,k}(T)\ll_{n}c_{2,k}^{-1}\det(T)^{k}(k^{\alpha_{1}}\det(T)^{-\beta_{1}}+k^{\alpha_{2}}\det(T)^{-\beta_{2}})

where
\alpha_{j},\beta_{j}
are certain explicit constants. This creates the window for obtaining robust bounds when interacted with the bound on
\#\mathcal{C}_{Y}
.
See Lemma
6.2
.
These, when combined with our Bergman kernel estimates (cf. Theorem
7.5
), yields the desired bound after a delicate analysis, all of these can be found in section
6
yielding Theorem
6.3
.

Let us now highlight some of the notable points in the Bergman kernel analysis. First of all let us mention that here the situation is somewhat better than that with the Fourier expansion.

We break the sum in (
1.4
) into three pieces by writing any element
\gamma\in\Gamma_{n}
as

\gamma=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1_{n}&S\\
0&1_{n}\\
\end{smallmatrix}\right)\cdot\allowbreak\left(\begin{smallmatrix}U&0\\
0&(U^{t})^{-1}\\
\end{smallmatrix}\right)\cdot\allowbreak\left(\begin{smallmatrix}*&*\\
C&D\\
\end{smallmatrix}\right)
, where the notation is explained below.
The first sum is over
\operatorname{Sym}(n,\mathbb{Z})
which is handled by the Lipschitz formula. Here we present a new method of bounding the sum
\sum\nolimits_{S\in\operatorname{Sym}(n,\mathbb{Z})}\det(Z+S)^{-k}

through its Fourier expansion (see Lemma
7.2
):

(1.24)

\sum_{S\in\operatorname{Sym}(n,\mathbb{Z})}\det(Z+S)^{-k}\ll_{n}\begin{cases}k^{\frac{n(n+1)}{4}}\det(Y)^{-k}\quad\quad&(Z\in\mathcal{H}_{n}),\\
\det(Y)^{-k+\frac{n+1}{4}}\quad\quad&(Z\in\mathcal{F}_{n}).\end{cases}

The second sum
\sum_{U}\det(Y+U\widetilde{Y}U^{t})^{-k}
over
\operatorname{GL}(n,\mathbb{Z})
, is quite subtle (see Lemma
7.4
), and the third one is over the set of all in-equivalent coprime symmetric pairs
\{C,D\}
(see section
7
). Coincidentally the bounds ensuing from the first and second summations are just enough to handle the third, which is a majorant of the classical Siegel Eisenstein series:
\sum_{\{C,D\}}|\det(CZ+D)|^{-\rho}
for suitable
\rho>0
, one also has to check that the bound is independent of
Y
. The results are summarised in Theorem
7.5
. We obtain the following bounds towards the Bergman kernel for all
Z\in\mathcal{F}_{n}
:

(1.25)

\mathbb{B}_{k}(Z,Z)=B_{k}(Z,Z)\det(Y)^{k}\ll_{n,\epsilon}k^{\frac{n(n+1)}{2}}(\det(Y)/y_{1}^{n})^{\frac{n+1}{2}+\epsilon}\min\left\{k^{\frac{n(n+1)}{4}},\,\det(Y)^{\frac{n+1}{4}}\right\}.

Here
y_{1}
is the smallest diagonal entry of
Y
. Expression (
1.25
) could perhaps be improved if we do not forgo cancellations as much as we did, but it is a non-trivial prospect. However, a promising approach worth investigating seems to be the generalisation of section
7.2
revolving around the setting of amplification – see the discussion there. Some calculations towards this effect can already be found in section
8
when we deal with amplification.
This approach, and the treatment of the level aspect of the same problem, will be the content of a forthcoming paper by the authors.

We end the paper by proving a bound for the sup-norm of a Hecke eigenform
F
when it is restricted to a fixed compact subset, say
\Omega\subset\mathcal{F}_{n}
which does not depend on
k
. Here the preliminary bound is
O_{\Omega}(k^{n(n+1)/2})
, which follows from the work of Cogdell-Luo
[
CL11
]
. When
n=2
, we go beyond this bound by employing the amplification method: based on previous works by
[
BP16
]
,
[
CL11
]
,
[
DS15
]
and
[
IS95
]
.
The construction of the amplifier follows that in
[
BP16
]
, but proceeding further one needs to count the number of double cosets in the similitude Hecke operators
T_{m}
, see Lemma
8.2
.
We show that the bound can be improved to
O_{\Omega}(k^{3/2-\eta})
for some absolute constant
\eta>0
. See subsection
8
, Theorem
8.3
. Some of the calculations here might be useful in considering the same problem in the non-compact setting.

Lastly let us mention that for

, on compact subsets of the form
\mathcal{F}_{a,b}=\mathcal{F}_{a,b;n}:=\{Z\in\mathcal{F}_{n}\mid k^{a}\ll\det(Y)\ll k^{b}\}

2.
Notation and setting

In this paper, we will mostly use standard notation, some of which are collected below, and the rest will be introduced as and when necessary. For standard facts about Siegel modular forms, we refer the reader to
[
Fre83
]
,
[
Kli90
]
.

(1)

We use the standard conventions in analytic number theory. We note
A\ll B
and
A=O(B)
are the Vinogradov and Landau notations respectively. By
A\asymp B
we mean that there exists a constant
c\geq 1
such that
B/c\leq A\leq cB
. Any subscripts under them (e.g.
A\ll_{n}B
) indicates the dependence of any implicit constants on those parameters. Throughout,
\epsilon
will denote a small positive number which can vary from one line to another.

(2)

\mathbb{Z},\mathbb{Q},\mathbb{R},
and
\mathbb{C}
denote the integers,
rationals, reals and complex numbers respectively. For a commutative ring
R
with unit,
M(n,R)
denotes the set of
n\times n
matrices over
R
.
\operatorname{GL}(n,R)
denotes the group of invertible elements in
M(n,R)
. We will denote the transpose of
A
by
A^{t}
. Moreover,
1_{n}
and
0_{n}
will be the identity and zero matrices respectively.

(3)

\operatorname{Sym}(n,R)
is the set of symmetric matrices with entries in
R
. For a square matrix
A
, we denote by
A_{D}
the diagonal matrix with the diagonal elements of
A
on the diagonal. For a tuple
(a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{n})\in R^{n}
, we put
\mathrm{diag}(a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{n})
to be the diagonal matrix with these elements on the diagonal.
From now on
R\subset\mathbb{R}
. We denote by
\operatorname{Sym}(n,R)^{+}
to be the cone of positive-definite matrices over
R
.

(4)

\mathrm{GSp}^{+}(n,\mathbb{R})\subseteq\operatorname{GL}(2n,\mathbb{R})
is the symplectic similitude group over
\mathbb{R}
. That is, we put
\mathrm{GSp}^{+}(n,\mathbb{R})=\left\{M=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}A&B\\
C&D\end{smallmatrix}\right)\in\operatorname{GL}(2n,\mathbb{R})\mid M^{t}\left(\begin{smallmatrix}0_{n}&-1_{n}\\
1_{n}&0_{n}\end{smallmatrix}\right)M=\mu(M)\left(\begin{smallmatrix}0_{n}&-1_{n}\\
1_{n}&0_{n}\end{smallmatrix}\right)\right\}
,
\mu(M)>0
. Then
\operatorname{Sp}(n,R)
is the subgroup for which
\mu(M)=1
for all
M
. In particular,
\Gamma_{n}=\operatorname{Sp}(n,\mathbb{Z})
is the Siegel modular group. Throughout the paper, we would use the above block representation for
M\in\operatorname{Sp}(n,R)
; with
A,B,C,D
having size
n
. Equivalently
M
satisfies the relations
AD^{t}-BC^{t}=1_{n},\,AB^{t}=BA^{t},\,CD^{t}=DC^{t}
.

(5)

\mathcal{H}_{n}:=\{X+iY\in\operatorname{Sym}(n,\mathbb{C})\mid Y>0\}
is the Siegel half-space.
M\in\operatorname{Sp}(n,\mathbb{R})
acts on
\mathcal{H}_{n}
by
Z\mapsto(AZ+B)(CZ+D)^{-1}
. Moreover, for
M\in\mathrm{GSp}^{+}(n,\mathbb{R})
and
F
holomorphic on
\mathcal{H}_{n}
we put
F|_{k}M:=\det(CZ+D)^{-k}F(M\langle Z\rangle)
where
\det(M)^{-1/2}M=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}*&*\\
C&D\end{smallmatrix}\right)
. When
F
depends on more than one variable like in
F(Z,W)
, we will use
|_{k}^{(W)}
to indicate that the action is to performed for the
W
variable.
We denote by
Y=\Im(Z)>0
to denote the imaginary part of
Z\in\mathcal{H}_{n}
, and often use the formula
\Im(M\langle Z\rangle)={(CZ+D)^{t}}^{-1}Y\overline{(CZ+D)}^{-1}
.

(6)

S_{k}^{n}
is the space of Siegel cusp forms of weight
k
on
\Gamma_{n}
, i.e., those which are holomorphic and satisfy
f|_{k}\gamma=F
for all
\gamma\in\Gamma_{n}
, with the usual boundedness condition when
n=1
.
We will use
\mathcal{B}_{k}^{n}
to denote an orthonormal basis for this with
F_{k,j}
as its elements.

(7)

\Lambda_{n}:=\{T\in\operatorname{Sym}(n,\mathbb{Q})\mid T>0;2T=(2t_{ij})\in\operatorname{Sym}(n,\mathbb{Z});t_{ii}\in\mathbb{Z}\}
. Throughout the paper, for two symmetric matrices
A,B
we write
A>B
to denote
A-B
is positive definite.
Note that
\operatorname{GL}(n,\mathbb{Z})
acts on
\Lambda_{n}
from the left by
T\mapsto UTU^{t}
.

(8)

The Fourier expansion of
F\in S_{k}^{n}
is written as

F(Z)=\sum_{T\in\Lambda_{n}}a_{F}(T)e(\mathrm{tr}(TZ))

and
a_{F}(T)\in\mathbb{C}
are its Fourier coefficients. Further
e(\mathrm{tr}(A))=e^{2\pi i\mathrm{tr}(A)}.

(9)

\mathcal{F}_{n}
will denote the Siegel fundamental domain of

\mathcal{H}_{n}
under the action of
\Gamma_{n}
.
If
Z=X+iY\in\mathcal{F}_{n}
, then the coordinates of
X=(x_{ij})
satisfy

-\frac{1}{2}\leq x_{ij}\leq\frac{1}{2}
; moreover,
Y\in R_{n}
the Minkowski-reduced domain,
and
|\det(CZ+D)|\geq 1
for all
\{C,D\}
being lower blocks of elements in
\Gamma_{n}
. Note that there exist constants
r_{n}\geq 1
such that for a Minkowski-reduced matrix
A
of size
n
, we have (cf.
[
Kli90
, p. 20 Lemma 2]
)

(2.1)

A_{D}/r_{n}\leq A\leq r_{n}A_{D}.

Moreover if
Z\in\mathcal{F}_{n}
, then
Y_{D}\gg_{n}1_{n}
(cf.
[
Kli90
, p. 30 Lemma 2]
).
For
F,G\in S^{n}_{k}
we define the Petersson inner product
\langle F,G\rangle
of
F
and
G
by

\langle F,G\rangle=\int_{\mathcal{F}_{n}}F(Z)\overline{G(Z)}\det(Y)^{k}\frac{dX\,dY}{\det(Y)^{n+1}}.

(10)

For each
T\in\Lambda_{n}
we define the
T
-th Poincaré series
P^{n}_{k,T}
by the infinite series
P^{n}_{k,T}=\sum_{\gamma\in\Gamma_{\infty}\backslash\Gamma_{n}}\allowbreak e(\mathrm{tr}(TZ))|_{k}\gamma
for
k>n+1
; where
\Gamma_{\infty}=\{\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1_{n}&S\\
0_{n}&1_{n}\end{smallmatrix}\right)\mid S\in\operatorname{Sym}(n,\mathbb{Z})\}
is the subgroup of
\Gamma_{n}
consisting of translations, and
\Gamma_{0,n}
denotes the Siegel parabolic subgroup defined by
\{\gamma\in\Gamma_{n}\mid C=0\}
. The most important property of
P_{T}
is that it is dual to the Fourier coefficients
A_{F}(T)
for all
F\in S^{n}_{k}
, and this results in the very useful Petersson’s formula enunciated in (
1.19
).

(11)

If
\gamma=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}*&*\\
C&D\\
\end{smallmatrix}\right)\in\Gamma_{n}
, then
\{C,D\}
is called a coprime symmetric pair. Conversely, such a pair satisfies
CD^{t}=DC^{t}
and can be completed to an element of
\Gamma_{n}
. Moreover,
\operatorname{GL}(n,\mathbb{Z})
acts on such pairs from the left by left multiplication – the representatives of the distinct equivalence classes are called ‘in-equivalent’.

3.
Proof of Theorem

In this section we prove Theorem

by invoking suitable results from later parts of the paper. We feel this allows for an easier presentation of the proof.

3.1.
Lower bound for the Bergman kernel

We first prove the lower bounds in Theorem
1.3
.
Note that the lower bound for claimed for
\mathbb{B}_{k}(Z,Z)
is uniformly
O(k^{3n(n+1)/4})
for all

.
We start with the observation that for any fixed

\displaystyle\sup_{Z^{\prime}\in\mathcal{F}_{n}}\sum_{F\in\mathcal{B}_{k}^{n}}\det(Y^{\prime})^{k}\left\lvert F(Z^{\prime})\right\rvert^{2}

\displaystyle\geq\int_{X\bmod 1}\,\sum_{F\in\mathcal{B}_{k}^{n}}\det(Y_{0})^{k}\left\lvert F(X+iY_{0})\right\rvert^{2}\,dX

\displaystyle\geq\det(Y_{0})^{k}\sum_{F\in\mathcal{B}_{k}^{n}}\,\left\lvert\int_{X\bmod 1}\frac{F(X+iY_{0})}{e(\tr(X+iY_{0}))}\,dX\right\rvert^{2}\exp\left(-4\pi\tr(Y_{0})\right)

(3.1)

\displaystyle=\det(Y_{0})^{k}\exp\left(\tr(-4\pi Y_{0})\right)\sum_{F\in\mathcal{B}_{k}^{n}}\,|a_{F}(1_{n})|^{2}.

As a consequence of the Petersson formula (see (
1.19
), (
1.20
)), we know that

(3.2)

\displaystyle\sum_{F\in\mathcal{B}_{k}^{n}}\,|a_{F}(1_{n})|^{2}=c_{n,k}^{-1}\,a_{1_{n}}(1_{n}),\quad\quad\quad\quad(c_{n,k}\text{ as in }\eqref{cnk})

where

denotes the
1_{n}
-th Fourier coefficient of the Poincaré series

. From
[
KST11
, Thm. 4]
we see that for all even

with

depending only on

(3.3)

where

. Of course, (
3.3
) is related to the difficult problem of non-vanishing of Poincaré series – see
[
DS12
]
for the case when
T
varies and

is fixed but large.

Putting together (
3.1
), (
3.2
) and (
3.3
), and taking

we see that

\displaystyle\sup_{Z^{\prime}\in\mathcal{F}_{n}}\sum_{F\in\mathcal{B}_{k}^{n}}\det(Y^{\prime})^{k}|F(Z^{\prime})|^{2}

\displaystyle\gg\left(\frac{k}{4\pi e}\right)^{nk}c_{n,k}^{-1}\gg_{n}\frac{\Gamma(k)^{n}k^{n/2}}{\prod_{\nu=1}^{n}\Gamma(k-(n+\nu)/2)}

(3.4)

\displaystyle\gg_{n}k^{n/2+(3n^{2}+n)/4}=k^{3n(n+1)/4}.

In the above,

may not be effective, as we rely on the result from
[

]
. However, when

, we can use

from
[
KST12
, Proposition 3.3]
or from
[
Blo19
, (1.8)]
.
This proves the lower bound for all

.

4.
Bounding the sup-norm using the Fourier Series

In this section, we will obtain a bound on the size of a Siegel cusp form via its Fourier expansion by using certain bounds on its Fourier coefficients. The method rests on the observation that in the Fourier expansion of
F
, only certain finitely many
T
contribute to its mass.

Recall the explicit bound on the Fourier coefficients from (
1.18
) with a saving
\beta
over the Hecke’s bound. This saving cannot be completely arbitrary, as follows from below. Of course, one expects
\beta\geq 0
, but we could not find a proof.
As far as we are aware, the lemma below is not available in the literature if the uniformity with respect to
k
is requested.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
n\geq 2
and put
\gamma_{n}:=(n+1)(2n-3)/4
.
Then with the above notation, (
1.18
) is satisfied by the Fourier coefficients of any Siegel cusp form of unit Petersson norm for the pair
(\alpha,\beta)
such that

\alpha=(5n^{2}+3n)/8;\quad\,\beta=-\gamma_{n}\quad\quad(k\geq 2(n+1)).

Remark 4.2
.

The sharp upper bound
\beta\leq(n+1)/4+\epsilon
has been shown in
[
BD14
, Remark 5.3]

by using the Rankin-Selberg
L
-series
R_{F}(s)=\sum_{T\in\operatorname{GL}(n,\mathbb{Z})\backslash\Lambda_{n}}\frac{|a_{F}(T)|^{2}}{\varepsilon(T)\det(T)^{s}}
attached to
F
. Here
\varepsilon(T)
denote the number of units of
T
.
Namely if one has
a_{F}(T)=O_{k,\epsilon}(\det(T)^{k/2-\beta+\epsilon})
for all
\epsilon>0
,
then one must have
k-2\beta+(n+1)/2>k
as
R_{F}(s)

has a pole at
s=k
. That the bound is sharp follows from
[
Das18
, Theorem 1.2]
.

Proof.

A value of
\beta
could in principle be obtained by bounding the

T
-th Fourier coefficient of the Poincaré series. Unfortunately, this
is apparently very hard – as very little is known about
the underlying Bessel functions of order
\geq 3
.
Instead, we take a different route and employ the Bergman kernel for this purpose.
First note that

\displaystyle\sum\nolimits_{T\in\Lambda_{n}}|a_{F}(T)|^{2}\exp\left(-4\pi\tr(TY)\right)=\int_{X\bmod 1}|F(Z)|^{2}dX,

and therefore, by summing over any orthonormal basis
\mathcal{B}_{k}^{n}
of
S^{n}_{k}
,
we get

(4.1)

\displaystyle\sum\nolimits_{F\in\mathcal{B}_{k}^{n}}\,\sum\nolimits_{T\in\Lambda_{n}}\det(Y)^{k}|a_{F}(T)|^{2}\exp\left(-4\pi\tr(TY)\right)=\int_{X\bmod 1}\sum\nolimits_{F}\det(Y)^{k}|F(Z)|^{2}dX.

The integrand on the r.h.s. of (
4.1
) is the Bergman kernel,
and in the notation of this paper (see (
1.25
)), equals
\mathbb{B}_{k}(Z,Z)
.
From Theorem
7.5
, one of the bounds for
\mathbb{B}_{k}(Z,Z)
reads
\mathbb{B}_{k}(Z,Z)\ll_{n,\epsilon}k^{3n(n+1)/4}\det(Y)^{(n+1)/2+\epsilon}

for all
Z\in\mathcal{F}_{n}
. We will use this bound.

Let
Z_{0}=X_{0}+iY_{0}\in\mathcal{H}_{n}
, we can find an

M=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}A&B\\
C&D\end{smallmatrix}\right)\in\Gamma_{n}

such that
Z=M\langle Z_{0}\rangle\in\mathcal{F}_{n}
. From the above mentioned bound for the Bergman kernel thus,

(4.2)

\displaystyle\mathbb{B}_{k}(Z_{0},Z_{0})=\mathbb{B}_{k}(Z,Z)\ll_{n}k^{3n(n+1)/4}\det(\Im(M\langle Z_{0}\rangle))^{(n+1)/2+\epsilon}=\frac{k^{3n(n+1)/4}\,\det(Y_{0})^{(n+1)/2+\epsilon}}{|\det(CZ_{0}+D)|^{n+1+\epsilon}}.

Let
\mathrm{rank}(C)=r
. Then from Siegel
[
Sie35
]
(see also
[
DK15
, Lemma 3.1]
)
we can find
U,W\in\operatorname{GL}(n,\mathbb{Z})
and
C_{1},D_{1}\in\operatorname{M}(r,\mathbb{Z})
such that

\displaystyle(UC,UD)=\left(\begin{pmatrix}C_{1}&0\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}W^{t},\begin{pmatrix}D_{1}&0\\
0&I_{n-r}\end{pmatrix}W^{-1}\right).\quad\quad(\mathrm{rank}(C_{1})=r)

Using this we see that

\displaystyle|\det(CZ_{0}+D)|

\displaystyle=\left|\det\left(\begin{pmatrix}C_{1}&0\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}Z_{0}[W]+\begin{pmatrix}D_{1}&0\\
0&I_{n-r}\end{pmatrix}\right)\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\det(C_{1}Z_{0}[W]_{*}+D_{1})\right|,

where
Z_{0}[W]_{*}
is the leading
r\times r
sub-matrix of
Z_{0}[W]
.

Now take any
T_{0}\in\Lambda_{n}
.
Dropping all but one term in (
4.1
) we get from (
4.2
) that
\sum_{F\in\mathcal{B}_{k}^{n}}|a_{F}(T_{0})|^{2}
is

\displaystyle\ll_{n}\frac{k^{3n(n+1)/4}\exp\left(4\pi\tr(T_{0}Y_{0})\right)\det(Y_{0})^{-k+(n+1)/2+\epsilon}}{|\det(C_{1}Z_{0}[W]_{*}+D_{1})|^{n+1+\epsilon}}.

Note the inequality
|\det(C_{1}Z_{0}[W]_{*}+D_{1})|\geq\det(\Im(Z_{0}[W]_{*}))=\det(Y_{0}[W]_{*})
, since
\Im(A_{*})=\Im(A)_{*}
for any
A\in\mathcal{H}_{n}
.

We choose
Y_{0}=\frac{k}{4\pi}\cdot T_{0}^{-1}

(the choice of the constant is dictated by the exponential term above)
and then see from the above that

(4.3)

\displaystyle\sum_{F\in\mathcal{B}_{k}^{n}}|a_{F}(T_{0})|^{2}

\displaystyle\ll_{n}\frac{k^{5n(n+1)/4-nk+\epsilon}\exp\left(nk\right)(4\pi)^{nk}\det(T_{0})^{k-(n+1)/2+\epsilon}}{\det\big((W^{t}\frac{k}{4\pi}T_{0}^{-1}W)_{*}\big)^{n+1+\epsilon}}

\displaystyle\ll_{n}\frac{k^{(n+1)(\frac{5n}{4}-r)-nk+\epsilon}\exp\left(nk\right)(4\pi)^{nk}\det(T_{0})^{k-(n+1)/2+\epsilon}}{\det\big((W^{t}\det(T_{0})^{-1}\Adj(T_{0})W)_{*}\big)^{n+1+\epsilon}}

(4.4)

\displaystyle\ll_{n}\exp\left(nk\right)(4\pi)^{nk}k^{(n+1)(\frac{5n}{4}-r)-nk}\det(T_{0})^{k+(n+1)(r-1/2+\epsilon)}.

Here
\Adj(T)=\det(T)T^{-1}
is the adjoint matrix to
T
and the last inequality follows
since
\det\big((W^{t}\Adj(T_{0})W)_{*}\big)\geq 2^{-r}
, as
\Adj(T_{0})
is positive definite and has half-integer entries.

When
r=n
we obviously have a better exponent of

\det(T_{0})
as
W=1_{n}
in this case.
From (
4.3
) directly, we have the exponent
\det(T_{0})^{k+(n+1)/2+\epsilon}
.
Otherwise, the worst case with respect to
\det(T_{0})
occurs when
r=n-1
.
Here the final exponent can be bounded by
\det(T_{0})^{k+(n+1)(n-3/2)+\epsilon}
.

The worst exponent with respect to
k
occurs when
r=0
. In this case,
it is
k^{5n(n+1)/4-nk+\epsilon}
. Comparing these with (
1.18
), we see that

\displaystyle\big(\sum_{F\in\mathcal{B}_{k}^{n}}|a_{F}(T_{0})|^{2}\big)^{1/2}\ll_{n}\frac{(4\pi)^{nk/2}k^{5n(n+1)/8-n/4+\epsilon}|T_{0}|^{k/2+(n+1)(n/2-3/4)+\epsilon}}{\Gamma(k)^{n/2}},

which gives the lemma.
∎

(4.10)

p(T)^{1/2}\ll_{n}\frac{(4\pi)^{nk/2}k^{\alpha}\det(T)^{k/2-\beta}}{\Gamma(k)^{n/2}}.

Then for the function
q_{k}\colon\operatorname{Sym}(n,\mathbb{R})^{+}\to\mathbb{C}
defined in (
4.9
), we have

(4.11)

q_{k}(Y)\ll_{n,\epsilon}\left(\frac{k^{n}}{\det(Y)}\right)^{(n+1)/2-\beta}\cdot k^{\frac{n}{4}+\alpha}+\exp\left(-c_{0}k^{\epsilon}\right)\det(Y)^{-(n+1)/2},

for

reduced. Additionally, if we restrict to

, we have the better bound

(4.12)

q_{k}(Y)\ll_{n,\epsilon}\left(\frac{k^{n}}{\det(Y)}\right)^{(n+1)/4-\beta}\cdot k^{\frac{n}{4}+\alpha+\epsilon}+\exp\left(-c_{0}k^{\epsilon}\right)\det(Y)^{-(n+1)/2}.

Here
c_{0}>0
depends only on
n
and
0<\epsilon<1
.
Finally, if the largest diagonal entry
y_{n}>nk\,r_{n}/(2\pi)
we have the decay:

(4.13)

q_{k}(Y)\ll_{n}\exp\left(-c_{0}y_{n}\right)\det(Y)^{-(n+1)/2}.

Remark 4.7
.

(1)

If we estimate
p(T)
using Poincaré series, we would get

for all

.

(2)

Extending the above analysis for values of

\mathbb{B}_{k}(Z,Z)\ll_{n}k^{\frac{n(n+1)}{4}\left(\frac{19(n+1)-40\beta}{5(n+1)-8\beta}\right)+\epsilon}

At

) alone, we would get
the conjectural upper bound
k^{3n(n+1)/8+\epsilon}
for

(cf. (
1.2
)) up to the
\epsilon
.

Proof.

Taking (
1.18
) as a bound for
p(T)^{1/2}
, we see that

(4.15)

q_{k}(Y)\ll_{n}\sum_{T\in\Lambda_{n}}{\frac{(4\pi)^{\frac{nk}{2}}\det(TY)^{\frac{k}{2}}e^{-2\pi\tr(TY)}k^{\alpha}\det(T)^{-\beta}}{\Gamma(k)^{\frac{n}{2}}}}.

Let
Y^{1/2}
be the unique symmetric positive definite square root of
Y
.
The matrix
TY
is conjugate to the positive definite symmetric matrix

Y^{1/2}TY^{1/2}
. So it is diagonalizable in
\mathbb{R}
with
positive eigenvalues. Let these eigenvalues be
\lambda_{j},j\in\{1,...,n\}
.
Replacing
\det(TY)
with the product of these eigenvalues and
\tr(TY)
with their sum
and applying the Stirling’s approximation

\Gamma(k)\asymp\sqrt{\frac{2\pi}{k}}(k/e)^{k}
, we get

q_{k}(Y)\ll_{n}\sum_{T\in\Lambda_{*}}{\left(\prod_{j}{\left(\sqrt{\frac{4\pi\lambda_{j}}{k}}\right)}^{k}\exp\left(\frac{nk}{2}-2\pi\tr(TY)\right)k^{\alpha+\frac{n}{4}}\det(T)^{-\beta}\right)}.

We now look at a function
m
defined as follows which captures the contribution of each summand in terms of the eigenvalues of
TY
:

(4.16)

q_{k}(Y)\ll_{n}\sum_{T\in\Lambda_{*}}{\left(\left(\prod_{j}{m(\lambda_{j})}\right)k^{\alpha+\frac{n}{4}}\det(T)^{-\beta}\right)};\quad\quad m(x):=\left(\frac{4\pi x}{k}\right)^{k/2}\exp\left((k-4\pi x)/2\right).

This function
m
attains a maximum value of
1

when
x
is equal to
k/4\pi
.
If
|\frac{x-k}{4\pi}|\gg_{\epsilon}k^{\frac{1}{2}+\epsilon}
, then

m(x)\ll\exp\left(\frac{-k^{2\epsilon}}{4}+O(k^{-1/2+3\epsilon})\right)
for

0<\epsilon<1/2
.
We claim that the total contribution from all
T
for which any of the eigenvalues
of
TY
lie sufficiently away from
\frac{k}{4\pi}
has exponential decay
with respect to
k
. We will prove this claim later in Lemma
4.10
.
So, the values of
T
that make a significant contribution
to the summation in (
4.15
),
are those for which the eigenvalues of
TY
satisfy the following criterion

(4.17)

\lambda_{i}\in\frac{k}{4\pi}+O(k^{\frac{1}{2}+\epsilon})\quad\text{ for all }1\leq i\leq n.

Let us define the subset
\mathcal{C}_{Y}
of
\Lambda_{n}
by

(4.18)

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{Y}:=\left\{T\in\Lambda_{n}\;\middle|\;\text{ all eigenvalues of }TY\text{ are of magnitude }\frac{k}{4\pi}+O(k^{\frac{1}{2}+\epsilon})\right\}.

From the above discussion, we can write

(4.19)

q_{k}(Y)\ll_{n}\left(\sum_{T\in\mathcal{C}_{Y}}{1}+\sum_{T\notin\mathcal{C}_{Y}}{\prod_{j}m(\lambda_{j})}\right)k^{\alpha+\frac{n}{4}}\det(T)^{-\beta}.

For the first summation above, i.e., when
T\in\mathcal{C}_{Y}
,
we have
\det(TY)\asymp(k/(4\pi))^{n}
from the eigenvalue condition.
Moreover since
\det(T)\geq 2^{-n}
,
\mathcal{C}_{Y}
is empty if
\det(Y)\gg_{n}k^{n}
.
The contribution from the first sum is

(4.20)

\displaystyle\sum_{T\in\mathcal{C}_{Y}}k^{\alpha+\frac{n}{4}}\det(T)^{-\beta}\ll_{n}\,\#\mathcal{C}_{Y}\cdot\det(Y)^{\beta}k^{-n\beta}k^{\alpha+\frac{n}{4}}.

Now we treat this as a counting problem on
T
.
We provide two bounds, which are given in Lemma
4.8
and Lemma
4.9
.
For the first one we simply note that
\mathcal{C}_{Y}
is contained in the set

\{T\mid\tr(TY)\asymp\frac{nk}{4\pi}\}
where the implied
constant may depend only on
n
. And then we proceed using Lemma
4.8
to get
\#\mathcal{C}_{Y}\ll_{n}k^{n(n+1)/2}\det(Y)^{-(n+1)/2}
which gives (
4.11
). If we take into account
Lemma
4.10
below, we see that the contribution of the second summation in (
4.19
) is negligible (having a sub-exponential decay).

If we are also given

, similarly we can use Lemma
4.9
to get (
4.12
).

Finally we use Lemma
4.11
when
\det(Y)
is large getting exponential decay. This will complete the proof of the theorem.
∎

Lemma 4.8
.

For
Y\in R_{n}
the Minkowski reduced domain, we can bound the size of the set

(4.21)

\#\{T\in\Lambda_{n}|\tr(TY)\leq ck\}\ll_{n,c}k^{\frac{n(n+1)}{2}}\det(Y)^{-\frac{n+1}{2}}.

Proof.

Since
Y
is reduced, we can assume that it is almost equal to its diagonal
Y_{D}
.
If the diagonal elements of
T
are
\{t_{1},t_{2},\cdots,t_{n}\}
, then we have

\displaystyle\tr(TY_{D})=\sum_{i}t_{i}y_{i}\ll\tr(TY)\ll k\implies 1\leq t_{i}\ll k/y_{i}.

Since
T>0
, when
i\neq j
, one has
|2t_{i,j}|\leq(t_{i}t_{j})^{1/2}
, from which it follows that the number of choices for
T
is, up to a constant depending only on
n
,

\ll_{n}\prod_{i}\frac{k}{y_{i}}\cdot\prod_{i<j}\frac{k}{(y_{i}y_{j})^{1/2}}=\frac{k^{n+n(n-1)/2}}{\det(Y)^{1+(n-1)/2}}=\frac{k^{n(n+1)/2}}{\det(Y)^{(n+1)/2}}.\qed

The following lemma gives a different bound for the quantity
\mathcal{C}_{Y}
. It generalises
[
Blo15
, Lemma 4]
to higher degrees – uniform in
\det(Y)
.

Lemma 4.9
.

Let
\mathcal{C}_{Y}
be as in (
4.18
),
Y
is Minkowski reduced and
Y\gg 1_{n}
.
Then we have the following bound on its size:

(4.22)

\displaystyle\#\mathcal{C}_{Y}

\displaystyle\ll_{n}k^{\frac{n(n+1)}{4}+\epsilon}\det(Y)^{-\frac{n+1}{4}}.

Proof.

Put
\kappa=O(k^{1/2+\epsilon})
so that
all eigenvalues of
Y^{1/2}TY^{1/2}
are in the range
(\frac{k}{4\pi}-\kappa,\frac{k}{4\pi}+\kappa)
.

Put
J:=(\frac{k}{4\pi}-\kappa)1_{n}
and
K=(k_{i,j}):=Y^{1/2}TY^{1/2}-J
.

K
is still positive definite.
Its operator norm is bounded above by
2\kappa
as well.
By equivalence of operator norm and sup norm for positive definite matrices,
there exist a constant
c_{1}
only dependent on
n
such that

\left\lVert K\right\rVert_{\infty}\leq 2c_{1}\kappa
. i.e.,
|k_{i,j}|<2c_{1}\kappa
for all
i,j
.
We can write

T-Y^{-1/2}JY^{-1/2}=Y^{-1/2}KY^{-1/2}.

Let
(m_{i,j})
be the entries of
Y^{-1/2}JY^{-1/2}
and
(w_{i,j})
be the entries of
Y^{-1/2}
.
Then we calculate

\displaystyle(Y^{-1/2}KY^{-1/2})_{i,j}=\sum\nolimits_{1\leq p,q\leq n}w_{i,p}k_{p,q}w_{q,j}.

As
Y^{-1/2}
is positive definite,
2|w_{i,j}|\leq\sqrt{w_{i,i}w_{j,j}}
.
Further as
Y
is reduced in the Minkowski sense, from (
2.1
),
we have
r_{n}^{-1}Y<Y_{D}<r_{n}Y
.
Using the fact the if
A<B
then
\sqrt{A}\leq\sqrt{B}
(cf.
[
Bap99
]
) we have

r_{n}^{-1/2}Y^{-1/2}\leq Y_{D}^{-1/2}\leq r_{n}^{1/2}Y^{-1/2}
and we get

r_{n}^{-1/2}w_{i,i}\leq y_{i}^{-1/2}\leq r_{n}^{1/2}w_{i,i}
. This yields

(4.23)

\displaystyle|(Y^{-1/2}KY^{-1/2})_{i,j}|<\sum_{1\leq p,q\leq n}2c_{1}r_{n}\kappa(y_{i}y_{j}y_{p}y_{q})^{-1/4}.

Recall that
Y\gg 1_{n}
and use this to bound
y_{p}
and
y_{q}
. From this we conclude

|t_{i,j}-m_{i,j}|\leq 2n^{2}c_{1}r_{n}\kappa(y_{i}y_{j})^{-1/4}
.

The number of possible values
t_{i,j}

can take is
\ll_{n}\kappa/\sqrt[4]{y_{i}y_{j}}+1
. Put back
\kappa=O(k^{1/2+\epsilon})
,
and observe that if any
y_{i}\gg k
, then
\tr(TY)\gg k

and
\mathcal{C}_{Y}
would be empty.
Hence
k^{1/2+\epsilon}/\sqrt[4]{y_{i}y_{j}}\gg 1
.
Multiplying the count for all
t_{i,j}
gives us the total size of
\mathcal{C}_{Y}
and proves the lemma.
∎

Lemma 4.10
.

With
\mathcal{C}_{Y},m
and
\lambda_{j}
as defined in Proposition
4.6
, we have for all
0<\epsilon<1
, and for some absolute constant
c_{0}>0
a ‘sub-exponential’ type decay as follows.

(4.24)

\sum\nolimits_{T\notin\mathcal{C}_{Y}}{\prod\nolimits_{j}m(\lambda_{j})}k^{\alpha+\frac{n}{4}}\det(T)^{-\beta}\ll_{n}\exp\left(-c_{0}k^{\epsilon}\right)\det(Y)^{-\frac{n+1}{2}}.

Proof.

We will bound the summation by dividing the set
\Lambda_{n}\setminus\mathcal{C}_{Y}
into regions
\mathcal{C}_{Y}^{t},t\geq 0
.
We will find the maximum value the summand can attain in each region and a bound on the number of elements in each region.
The regions will be characterised by the largest eigenvalue
\lambda_{n}
of
TY
.
Define

(4.25)

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{Y}^{0}

\displaystyle:=\left\{T\in\Lambda_{n}\setminus\mathcal{C}_{Y}\;\middle|\;4\pi\lambda_{n}\leq 2k\right\}

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{Y}^{t}

\displaystyle:=\left\{T\in\Lambda_{n}\setminus\mathcal{C}_{Y}\;\middle|\;2^{t}<4\pi\lambda_{n}\leq 2^{t+1}k\right\},\quad t\geq 1.

If
T\in\mathcal{C}_{Y}^{0}
, then let
\lambda
be an eigenvalue of
TY
such that
|\lambda-\frac{k}{4\pi}|\geq\frac{c}{4\pi}k^{1/2+\epsilon}
for a constant
c>0
depending only on
\epsilon
. Recall that the function
m(x)
has a single maximum at
x=\frac{k}{4\pi}
with a value equal to
1
.
In either side, by monotonicity of
m
, we see that
\prod_{j}m(\lambda_{j})\ll_{n}m(\lambda)
. Now

\displaystyle m

\displaystyle\left(\frac{k\pm ck^{\frac{1}{2}+\epsilon}}{4\pi}\right)=\exp\left(\frac{k}{2}\log\left(1\pm ck^{-\frac{1}{2}+\epsilon}\right)\mp\frac{c}{2}k^{\frac{1}{2}+\epsilon}\right)

\displaystyle=\exp\left(\frac{k}{2}\big(\pm ck^{-\frac{1}{2}+\epsilon}-c^{2}\frac{k^{-1+2\epsilon}}{2}\pm O(k^{-\frac{3}{2}+3\epsilon})\big)\mp\frac{c}{2}k^{\frac{1}{2}+\epsilon}\right)

\displaystyle=\exp\left(-c^{2}k^{2\epsilon}/4+O(k^{-\frac{1}{2}+3\epsilon})\right)\ll\exp\left(-c^{2}k^{2\epsilon}/4\right),

Therefore, we get the following sub-exponential decay

(4.26)

\displaystyle\prod\nolimits_{j}m(\lambda_{j})\ll\exp\left(-c^{2}k^{2\epsilon}/4\right).

Further for any
t\geq 1
, we have
m\left(\frac{2^{t}k}{4\pi}\right)=\sqrt{2^{kt}e^{k(1-2^{t})}}=e^{\frac{k}{2}(t\log(2)+1-2^{t})}
.
If
\beta<0
, in
\mathcal{C}_{Y}^{t}
,
\det(T)^{-\beta}\leq(2^{t+1}k)^{-n\beta}
.
Otherwise, we can simply use
\det(T)^{-\beta}\leq 2^{n\beta}
.
The number of points in these
\mathcal{C}_{Y}^{t}
are bounded by (using Lemma
4.8
)

\#(\mathcal{C}_{Y}^{t})\ll_{n}(2k)^{\frac{(t+1)n(n+1)}{2}}\det(Y)^{-\frac{n+1}{2}}.

We bound the summation by summing over count of points in each region
\mathcal{C}_{Y}^{t}

times a bound on the maximum value
\prod_{j}m(\lambda_{j})\det(T)^{-\beta}
could take in that region.

\sum\nolimits_{T\in\Lambda_{n}\setminus\mathcal{C}_{Y}}{\prod\nolimits_{j}{m(\lambda_{j})}\det(T)^{-\beta}}\leq\sum\nolimits_{t=0}^{\infty}{\left(\#(\mathcal{C}_{Y}^{t})\max\nolimits_{T\in\mathcal{C}_{Y}^{t}}{\prod\nolimits_{j}{m(\lambda_{j})}\det(T)^{-\beta}}\right)}\\
\ll\biggl(\exp(-c_{1}k^{2\epsilon})(2k)^{n(n+1)}\max\left((2k)^{-n\beta},2^{n\beta}\right)\\
+\sum\nolimits_{t=1}^{\infty}\exp(k(\log(2)t+1-2^{t}))(2k)^{\frac{(t+1)n(n+1)}{2}}\max\left((2^{t+1}k)^{-n\beta},2^{n\beta}\right)\biggr)\det(Y)^{-\frac{n+1}{2}}

It is easy to check that the above sum is bounded by
\exp(-c_{0}k^{2\epsilon})\det(Y)^{-(n+1)/2}
. Replacing
\epsilon
by
\epsilon/C
for some
C>0
, if necessary, gives us the lemma.
∎

Lemma 4.11
.

For the constant
C_{n}=\frac{2\pi}{n\,r_{n}}
, in the region
\{\,Z\in R_{n}\mid y_{n}\geq k/C_{n}\,\}
, we have
q_{k}(Y)\ll_{n}\exp\left(-c_{1}y_{n}\right)\det(Y)^{-(n+1)/2}
for some constant
c_{1}>0
depending only on
n
.

Alternatively in the region
\{\,Y\in R_{n}\mid\det(Y)\geq(\frac{k}{C_{n}})^{n}\,\}
the quantity
q_{k}(Y)
decays exponentially: that is,
q_{k}(Y)\allowbreak\ll_{n}\exp(-c_{1}\sqrt[n]{\det(Y)})
.

We note that this also means exponential decay with respect to
k
from the condition on
Y
.

Proof.

Let
\det(Y)=(bk/C_{n})^{n}
for some
b\geq 1
.
In the above-mentioned region, by Hadamard’s inequality we also have
y_{1}y_{2}\cdots y_{n}\geq(\frac{bk}{C_{n}})^{n}
. Therefore, we must have
y_{n}\geq\frac{bk}{C_{n}}
.

As in the proof of Proposition
4.6
, if
\lambda_{1}\leq\lambda_{2}\leq\cdots\leq\lambda_{n}
denote the eigenvalues of
TY
, then it is easy to see
n\lambda_{n}\geq\tr(TY)\geq r_{n}^{-1}\sum_{j=1}^{n}t_{i}y_{i}\geq y_{n}/r_{n}
. Here we have used that
Y\geq Y_{D}/r_{n}
from (
2.1
).
Therefore, we get that
\lambda_{n}\geq\frac{bk}{n\,C_{n}\,r_{n}}
.

If we choose
C_{n}
such that
\frac{1}{n\,C_{n}\,r_{n}}\geq\frac{1}{2\pi}
, i.e.,
c_{n}\leq\frac{2\pi}{n\,r_{n}}
, then we must have
\lambda_{n}\geq\frac{bk}{2\pi}
. This would mean that for all
T\in\Lambda_{n}
,
T\not\in\mathcal{C}_{Y}
. In fact,

\displaystyle m(\lambda_{n})\leq m(bk/(2\pi))=\exp\left(-k(2b-1-\log(2b))/2\right)\leq\exp\left(-c^{\prime\prime}bk\right)

for some appropriate constant
c^{\prime\prime}
and
k
large enough. We proceed in the same way as that of Lemma
4.10
by considering the regions similar to
\mathcal{C}_{Y}^{t}
(
t\geq 1
) characterised by
2^{t}bk<4\pi\lambda_{n}\leq 2^{t+1}bk
to get
q_{k}(Y)\ll_{n}\exp\left(-c_{0}bk\right)\det(Y)^{-(n+1)/2}
. We do not repeat the arguments again.
We do note the absence of the
\mathcal{C}_{Y}^{0}
region and hence we get full exponential decay
(as opposed to a sub-exponential decay).
Put back

to finish the proof.
∎

5.
Using the Petersson trace formula when
n=2

In this section, we will get hold of a better bound on the Fourier coefficients, when
n=2
. Since in degree
2
there is sufficient information about Bessel functions and Kloosterman sums; we bound
a_{F}(T)
simply by bounding the
T
-th Fourier coefficient
a_{P_{T}}(T)
of the Siegel Poincaré series
P_{T}
and use the Petersson trace formula (
1.19
).

We recall the definition of the Poincaré series
P_{Q}(Z)
(
Q\in\Lambda_{n}
):

(5.1)

P_{Q}(Z):=\sum_{\gamma\in\Gamma_{\infty}\backslash\Gamma_{n}}{\det(j(\gamma,Z))^{-k}e^{2\pi i\tr(Q\gamma(Z))}}.

Here
\Gamma_{\infty}
is the subgroup of
\operatorname{Sp}(n,\mathbb{Z})
with elements of the form

\left(\begin{smallmatrix}I&B\\
0&I\\
\end{smallmatrix}\right)
.
Work towards bounding its Fourier coefficients
a_{P_{Q}}(T)
has been done in

[
Kit84
]
when
n=2
. It shows

|a_{P_{Q}}(T)|\ll_{k,Q}\det(T)^{k/2-1/4+\epsilon}.

However, this result does not provide an explicit dependence on the weight
k

for these Fourier coefficients, which is crucial for us.
The dependence on
k
has been tracked in
[
KST12
, Proposition 3.3]
and
[
Dic15
, Theorem 5.11]
but
only for certain special type of matrices
Q
and
T
. Also,
Q
was treated as fixed.
We are interested in the case when
Q=T
, with
T
varying.
We follow the approach in
[
KST12
, p.355-363]
with this additional bookkeeping.

Lemma 5.1
.

The
T
-th Fourier coefficient of the
T
-th Poincaré series can be bounded as follows

(5.2)

a_{P_{T}}(T)=\delta(T,T)+O_{\epsilon}(k^{-2/3}\det(T)^{1+\epsilon}).

Here
\delta(T,Q)=\#\{U\in\operatorname{GL}(2,\mathbb{Z})\mid UQU^{t}=T\}
.

Proof.

From
[
Kit84
, p. 158]
one can write

P_{Q}(Z)=\sum\nolimits_{M\in\Gamma_{\infty}\backslash\Gamma_{n}/\Gamma_{\infty}\hskip 8.19447pt}\sum\nolimits_{T\in\Lambda_{n}}h_{Q}(M,T)e^{2\pi i\tr(TZ)},

where

a_{P_{Q}}(T)=\sum\nolimits_{M\in\Gamma_{\infty}\backslash\Gamma_{n}/\Gamma_{\infty}}h_{Q}(M,T).

Note that we deliberately write
h_{Q}
instead of
h_{T}
to be consistent with the notation in
[
Kit84
]
,
[
KST12
]
. We reiterate, for us
Q=T
. In the rest of this section, we will closely follow these two references and indicate only the appropriate changes required to achieve our goal.

Then if
M
is expressed as
\left(\begin{smallmatrix}A&B\\
C&D\\
\end{smallmatrix}\right)
, the above summation
is divided into cases depending on the rank of
C
. When
\rk C=0
,

h_{Q}(M,T)=\delta(Q,T)
and so their sum over all
M
with
C=0

will be the size of the orthogonality group
\#\mathrm{Aut}(T)=O(1)
.

When
\rk C=1
, it has been shown in
[
KST12
, (3.1.8)]
that

\sum_{\rk C=1}h_{Q}(m,T)\ll\sum_{c,m\geq 1}\left(\frac{\det(T)}{\det(Q)}\right)^{k/2-3/4}m^{-1/2}(c,m)^{1/2}\left|J_{k-3/2}\left(\frac{\pi\sqrt{\det(TQ)}}{mc}\right)\right|

(5.3)

\sum_{\rk C=1}h_{T}(m,T)\ll\sum_{c,m\geq 1}m^{-1/2}(c,m)^{1/2}\left|J_{k-3/2}\left(\frac{\pi\det(T)}{mc}\right)\right|.

Here
J_{k}
is the Bessel function.
Using various approximations for the Bessel function, it was shown that
the the r.h.s. of (
5.3
) is
\ll k^{-5/6}
but with implicit constants dependent on
\det(T)
.
In all cases,
d
appears in the aforementioned calculation and for our situation one has to replace
d
with
\det(T)
.
This allows us to easily track the dependence to be
\det(T)^{1+\delta}
for any
\delta>0
. Hence

(5.4)

\sum\nolimits_{\rk C=1}h_{T}(m,T)\ll\det(T)^{1+\delta}k^{-\frac{5}{6}}.

Finally, when
\rk C=2
, from
[
KST12
, (3.1.11)]
for a fixed value of
C
,

\sum\nolimits_{D(\operatorname{mod}C)}h_{Q}(M,T)\ll\left(\frac{|T|}{|Q|}\right)^{\frac{k}{2}-\frac{3}{4}}\lvert C\rvert^{-\frac{3}{2}}K(Q,T;C)\mathcal{J}_{k}(P(C))

\sum\nolimits_{D(\operatorname{mod}C)}h_{T}(M,T)\ll\lvert C\rvert^{-\frac{3}{2}}K(T,T;C)\int_{0}^{1}J_{k-\frac{3}{2}}(4\pi s_{1}t)J_{k-\frac{3}{2}}(4\pi s_{2}t)\frac{tdt}{\sqrt{1-t^{2}}}.

Here
K(Q,T;C)
is a Kloosterman type sum and
(s_{1}^{2},s_{2}^{2})
are the
eigenvalues of
T{C^{t}}^{-1}TC^{-1}
. We refer the reader to
[
Kit84
, p. 165]
for the definition of
\mathcal{J}_{k}(P(C))
.
First from
[
Kit84

7.
Bounds using the Bergman Kernel

From
[
CL11
, eqn. 6]
,
for an orthonormal basis
\mathcal{B}_{k}^{n}=\{F_{k,j}\}_{j}
for Siegel Cusp forms of weight
k
, we have for
Z\in\mathcal{H}_{n}

(7.1)

\mathbb{B}_{k}(Z,Z)=\sum_{j}{|F_{k,j}(Z)|^{2}\det(Y)^{k}}=B_{k}(Z,Z)|\det(Y)|^{k}=2^{-1}a(n,k)R_{k}(Z),

where we have put

R_{k}(Z):=\sum_{\gamma\in\Gamma_{n}}{h_{\gamma}(Z)^{k}};\quad h_{\gamma}(Z)=\frac{\det(Y)}{\det\left(\frac{Z-\gamma\overline{Z}}{2i}\right)\det(C\overline{Z}+D)}

and
a(n,k)
defined below satisfies

(7.2)

a(n,k):=2^{-n(n+3)/2}\pi^{-n(n+1)/2}\prod_{v=1}^{n}{\frac{\Gamma(k-\frac{v-1}{2})}{\Gamma(k-\frac{v+n}{2})}}\ll_{n}k^{\frac{n(n+1)}{2}}.

Let us write
\gamma\in\Gamma_{n}
as

\gamma=\gamma_{\infty}\gamma_{U}\widetilde{\gamma}
where

\gamma_{\infty}=\begin{pmatrix}1_{n}&T\\
0&1_{n}\end{pmatrix};\gamma_{U}=\begin{pmatrix}U&0\\
0&(U^{t})^{-1}\end{pmatrix};T\in\operatorname{Sym}(n,\mathbb{Z}),U\in\operatorname{GL}(n,\mathbb{Z}).

For both
\gamma_{\infty}
and
\gamma_{U}
, we have (with
j(\gamma,Z)=CZ+D
)

\det(j(\gamma_{\infty},Z))=\det(j(\gamma_{U},Z))=1.

Thus we have

h_{\gamma}(Z)=\frac{\det(Y)}{\det\left(\frac{Z-U\overline{\widetilde{\gamma}\langle Z\rangle}U^{t}-T}{2i}\right)\det(j(\widetilde{\gamma},Z))}

and so

(7.3)

\sum_{\gamma\in\Gamma_{n}}h_{\gamma}(Z)^{k}=(2i)^{nk}\det(Y)^{k}\sum_{\{C,D\}}\det(CZ+D)^{-k}\sum_{U}\sum_{T}\det\left(Z-U\overline{\widetilde{\gamma}\langle Z\rangle}U^{t}-T\right)^{-k};

where in the above sum,
T
varies over
\operatorname{Sym}(n,\mathbb{Z})
,
U
varies over
\operatorname{GL}(n,\mathbb{Z})
and
\{C,D\}
vary over inequivalent coprime symmetric pairs in degree
n
(see section
2
).
Next, we shall introduce a couple of lemmas to help us estimate this nested summation.

7.1.
Bounding
\mathbb{B}_{k}(Z,Z)
using Lipschitz formula

Lemma 7.1
.

Let
k\geq n+1
and
Z\in\mathcal{H}_{n}
be such that
Y\geq c1_{n}
for some
c
depending only on
n
. Then

(7.4)

\sum\nolimits_{S\in\operatorname{Sym}(n,\mathbb{Z})}|\det(Z+S)|^{-k}\ll_{n}\det(Y)^{-k+(n+1)/2}.

Note the subtle point here, that it is not sufficient to estimate the l.h.s. of (
7.4
) term by term as it is. This would entail bad bounds in terms of the exponent
k
(cf.
[
Bra39
, p. 393-395]
, also see
[
Kri86
, Lemma V.1.2, Corollary V.1.5]
, where the relevant constants are exponential in
k
), but we can avoid this by separating the exponent. We will not use this lemma in this subsection but in section
7.2
(cf. (
7.33
)). The argument given below using the comparison of higher dimensional sum and integral may be useful in other situations.

Proof.

Let us write
k=k_{1}+k_{2}
where
k_{1}
will depend only on
n
. First, we note that for any
Z\in\mathcal{H}_{n}
and
R\in\operatorname{Sym}(n,\mathbb{R})
,
|\det(Z+R)|\geq\det(Y)
, where
Y=\Im(Z)
. This follows from the computation

|\det(Z+R)|=|\det(iY+(X+R))|\geq\det(Y)|\det(1_{n}-iY^{-1/2}(X+R)Y^{-1/2})|.

From the above discussion, we can write

(7.5)

\displaystyle\sum\nolimits_{S\in\operatorname{Sym}(n,\mathbb{Z})}|\det(Z+S)|^{-k}\leq\det(Y)^{-k_{2}}\sum\nolimits_{S\in\operatorname{Sym}(n,\mathbb{Z})}|\det(Z+S)|^{-k_{1}}.

Quoting
[
Bra39
, p. 393-395]
we have

(7.6)

\displaystyle\sum\nolimits_{S\in\operatorname{Sym}(n,\mathbb{Z})}|\det(Z+S)|^{-k_{1}}

\displaystyle\leq\exp\left(nk_{1}\tr(Y^{-1})\right)\int_{\mathcal{S}=\mathcal{S}^{\prime}}|\det(Z+\mathcal{S})|^{-k_{1}}d\mathcal{S}

(7.7)

\displaystyle\ll_{n,k_{1}}\det(Y)^{(n+1)/2-k_{1}},

provided
k_{1}\geq n+1
and that
Y\geq c1_{n}
for some
c
depending only on
n
. Combining (
7.5
) and (
7.6
) we get upon choosing
k_{1}=n+1
that

(7.8)

\displaystyle\sum\nolimits_{S\in\operatorname{Sym}(n,\mathbb{Z})}|\det(Z+S)|^{-k}\ll_{n}\det(Y)^{-k+k_{1}}\cdot\det(Y)^{(n+1)/2-k_{1}}=\det(Y)^{-k+(n+1)/2}.

This finishes the proof of the 1emma.
∎

We now give a couple of other bounds for the sum in Lemma
7.1
using the Fourier expansion of the function
\sum_{S\in\operatorname{Sym}(n,\mathbb{Z})}\det(Z+S)^{-k}
.

Lemma 7.2
.

For
Z\in\mathcal{H}_{n}
we have the bounds

(7.9)

\displaystyle\sum\nolimits_{S\in\operatorname{Sym}(n,\mathbb{Z})}\det(Z+S)^{-k}\ll_{n}\begin{cases}k^{\frac{n(n+1)}{4}}\det(Y)^{-k}\quad\quad&(Z\in\mathcal{H}_{n}),\\
k^{\epsilon}\det(Y)^{-k+\frac{n+1}{4}}\quad\quad&(Z\in\mathcal{F}_{n}).\end{cases}

Proof.

First of all we can assume that
Y=\Im(Z)
is reduced, by replacing
Z
by
Z[U]
for a suitable
U\in\operatorname{GL}(n,\mathbb{Z})
, since the sum over
S
is invariant under
\operatorname{GL}(n,\mathbb{Z})
.

By the Lipschitz formula, which we quote from
[
Sie35
, Hilfssatz 38]
: for any
Z\in\mathcal{H}_{n}
and any
k>n(n+1)/2

(7.10)

\mathcal{S}_{k}:=\sum\nolimits_{S\in\operatorname{Sym}(n,\mathbb{Z})}\det(Z+S)^{-k}=C_{n}\frac{e^{-\pi ink/2}}{\gamma_{n,k}}\sum\nolimits_{T\in\Lambda_{n}}\det(T)^{k-(n+1)/2}e(TZ),

where
C_{n}=(2\sqrt{\pi})^{-n(n-1)/2},\gamma_{n,k}=(2\pi)^{-nk}\prod_{\nu=0}^{n-1}\Gamma(k-\nu/2)
. Asymptotically, we get the following

\displaystyle\left\lvert C_{n}\frac{e^{-\pi ink/2}}{\gamma_{n,k}}\right\rvert\asymp_{n}\frac{(2\pi)^{nk}k^{\frac{n(n-1)}{4}}}{\Gamma(k)^{n}}\asymp_{n}\frac{(4\pi)^{nl/2}l^{\frac{-n(n+2)}{4}}}{\Gamma(l)^{n/2}}.

Here we have put
l=2k-n-1
. Thus we have

(7.11)

\left\lvert\mathcal{S}_{k}\det(Y)^{l/2}\right\rvert\asymp_{n}\sum_{T\in\Lambda_{n}}\frac{(4\pi)^{nl/2}l^{\frac{-n(n+2)}{4}}\det(TY)^{l/2}e^{2\pi\tr(TY)}}{\Gamma(l)^{n/2}}.

We now apply Proposition
4.6
to the above with equation with
\alpha=\frac{-n(n+2)}{4}
,

\beta=0
and
k
there being
l
here. With the notation of (
4.11
), the r.h.s of the above display is just
q_{l}(Y)
. Thus we have

(7.12)

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{k}\det(Y)^{l/2}\ll_{n,\epsilon}\det(Y)^{-(n+1)/2}(k^{\frac{n(n+1)}{4}}+\exp\left(-c_{0}k^{-\epsilon}\right))

which implies that

\mathcal{S}_{k}\ll_{n}k^{\frac{n(n+1)}{4}}\det(Y)^{-k}
.

If we also have
Y\gg 1_{n}
, then we can also use (
4.12
). This gives us

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{k}\det(Y)^{l/2}\ll_{n,\epsilon}\det(Y)^{-(n+1)/4}k^{\epsilon}+\det(Y)^{-(n+1)/2}\exp\left(-c_{0}k^{-\epsilon}\right)

which implies that
\mathcal{S}_{k}\ll_{n,\epsilon}k^{\epsilon}\det(Y)^{-k+\frac{n+1}{4}}
.
∎

This yields the following result.

Lemma 7.3
.

Let
Z\in\mathcal{H}_{n}
and
k\geq n+1
. For
\gamma\in\Gamma_{n}
, we put
\widetilde{Y}:=-\Im(\overline{\gamma\langle Z\rangle})
. Put
\mathbb{Y}_{U}:=Y+U\widetilde{Y}U^{t}

\sum_{S\in\operatorname{Sym}(n,\mathbb{Z})}\det\left(Z-U\overline{\widetilde{\gamma}\langle Z\rangle}U^{t}-S\right)^{-k}
is

(7.13)

\displaystyle\ll_{n,\epsilon}\begin{cases}k^{n(n+1)/4}\det(\mathbb{Y}_{U})^{-k}\quad\quad&(Z\in\mathcal{H}_{n})\\
k^{\epsilon}\det(\mathbb{Y}_{U})^{-k+\frac{n+1}{4}}\quad\quad&(Z\in\mathcal{F}_{n}).\end{cases}

Proof.

We apply Lemma
7.2
to
W:=Z-U\overline{\widetilde{\gamma}\langle Z\rangle}U^{t}
. Notice that

\Im(W)=Y+U\widetilde{Y}U^{t}\geq Y
. Since

∎

Lemma 7.4
.

For
k>(n+1)^{2},n\geq 2
and
Y,\widetilde{Y}\in\operatorname{Sym}(n,\mathbb{R})^{+}
, with
\lambda_{1}
as the smallest eigenvalue of
Y
, we have for any
\epsilon>0
:

(7.14)

\sum\nolimits_{U\in\operatorname{GL}(n,\mathbb{Z})}{\det(Y+U\widetilde{Y}U^{t})^{-k}}\ll_{n,\epsilon}2^{-nk}\det(Y)^{-\frac{k}{2}}\det(\widetilde{Y})^{\frac{-k+n+1}{2}+\epsilon}\lambda_{1}^{-\frac{n(n+1)}{2}-n\epsilon}.

Before we start the proof, we would like to call attention to the quantity
2^{nk}
sitting in the front of the summations in (
7.3
). We must be able to cancel this exponential factor – as our quest is to obtain polynomial bounds on the sup-norm. The proof given below proceeds by taking this into account.

Proof.

We first notice that the roles of
Y
and
\widetilde{Y}
can be swapped without affecting the sum. Further, if
\widetilde{Y}_{1}
satisfying
V\widetilde{Y}_{1}V^{t}=\widetilde{Y}
is the Minkowski reduction of
\widetilde{Y}
for some
V\in\operatorname{GL}(n,\mathbb{Z})
we can write

\sum\nolimits_{U}\det(Y+U\widetilde{Y}U^{t})^{-k}=\sum_{U}\det((UV)^{-1}Y((UV)^{-1})^{t}+\widetilde{Y}_{1})^{-k}=\sum\nolimits_{U}\det(\widetilde{Y}_{1}+U^{t}YU)^{-k}.

We start with the obvious equality for any
\kappa>0

(7.15)

\displaystyle\det(\widetilde{Y}_{1}+U^{t}YU)^{-\kappa}=\det(Y)^{-\kappa}\det(1_{n}+\widetilde{Y}_{1}^{-1/2}U^{t}YU\widetilde{Y}_{1}^{-1/2})^{-\kappa}.

We now put
A=\widetilde{Y}_{1}^{-1/2}U^{t}YU\widetilde{Y}_{1}^{-1/2}>0
.
Observe that for
A>0
the following inequality holds:

\det(1_{n}+A)\geq 2^{n}\det(A)^{1/2}.

And thus from (
7.15
), we have

(7.16)

\det(\widetilde{Y}_{1}+U^{t}YU)^{-\kappa}\leq 2^{-n\kappa}\det(\widetilde{Y}_{1})^{-\kappa}\det(A)^{-\kappa/2}=2^{-n\kappa}\det(\widetilde{Y}_{1})^{-\kappa/2}\det(Y)^{-\kappa/2}.

Also, using the inequality
\det(1_{n}+A)\geq 1+\tr(A)+\det(A)
for a positive semi-definite matrix
A
(which follows easily by diagonalising
A
), we get

\det(1_{n}+A)\geq(1+\tr(\widetilde{Y}_{1}^{-1}U^{t}YU))+\det(A).

Then applying the A.M.-G.M. inequality, we get

(7.17)

\displaystyle\det(1_{n}+A)\geq 2\left(1+\tr(\widetilde{Y}_{1}^{-1}U^{t}YU)\right)^{1/2}\det(A)^{1/2}.

From (
7.15
) and (
7.17
) we get

(7.18)

\displaystyle\det(\widetilde{Y}_{1}+U^{t}YU)^{-\kappa}\leq 2^{-\kappa}\det(\widetilde{Y}_{1})^{-\kappa/2}\det(Y)^{-\kappa/2}\left(1+\tr(\widetilde{Y}_{1}^{-1}UYU^{t})\right)^{-\kappa/2}.

Like in the proof of Lemma
7.1
, we write
k=k^{\prime\prime}+k^{\prime}
with
k^{\prime}
depending only on
n
, to be specified later. This will also tell us how large
k
needs to be. We use (
7.16
) for the exponent
k^{\prime\prime}
and (
7.18
) for
k^{\prime}
to get that:

\displaystyle\sum\nolimits_{U}

\displaystyle\det(\widetilde{Y}+U^{t}YU)^{-k}=\sum\nolimits_{U}\det(\widetilde{Y}+U^{t}YU)^{-k^{\prime}}\det(\widetilde{Y}+U^{t}YU)^{-k^{\prime\prime}}

(7.19)

\displaystyle\leq 2^{-nk+(n-1)k^{\prime}}\det(\widetilde{Y})^{-k/2}\det(Y)^{-k/2}\sum\nolimits_{U}\left(1+\tr(\widetilde{Y}_{1}^{-1}UYU^{t})\right)^{-k^{\prime}/2}.

For the rest of the proof, we will focus on the quantity
\mathbb{S}:=\sum\nolimits_{U}(1+\tr(\widetilde{Y}_{1}^{-1}UYU^{t}))^{-k^{\prime}/2}
.
As
\widetilde{Y}_{1}
is reduced, we have
\widetilde{Y}_{1}\gg_{n}\widetilde{Y}_{D}
, where
\widetilde{Y}_{D}=\mathrm{diag}(\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{n})
is the matrix made by the diagonal of
\widetilde{Y}_{1}
.
Since
k^{\prime}
depends only on
n
, we can thus bound
\mathbb{S}
by

(7.20)

\displaystyle\mathbb{S}\ll_{n}\sum\nolimits_{U}\left(1+\tr(\widetilde{Y}_{D}^{-1}U^{t}YU)\right)^{-k^{\prime}/2}\ll\sum\nolimits_{U}\left(1+\lambda_{1}\tr(\widetilde{Y}_{D}^{-1}U^{t}U)\right)^{-k^{\prime}/2}.

Here we have used
Y\geq\lambda_{1}1_{n}
where
\lambda_{1}
is the smallest diagonal eigenvalue of
Y
.

In (
7.20
), we introduce a new variable
W
such that
U^{t}U=W
. Note that
W
lies in
\operatorname{Sym}(n,\mathbb{Z})^{+}\cap\operatorname{SL}(n,\mathbb{Z})
. The new summation thus becomes

(7.21)

\displaystyle\mathbb{S}\ll_{n}\sum\nolimits_{W}\#\{U\mid U^{t}U=W\}\left(1+\lambda_{1}\tr(\widetilde{Y}_{D}^{-1}W)\right)^{-k^{\prime}/2}.

For the counting function in (
7.21
), which we will call
h(W)
, we note that if
U_{0}=U^{-1}\in\operatorname{GL}(n,\mathbb{Z})
is such that
U_{0}^{t}WU_{0}=1_{n}
and so

(7.22)

\displaystyle h(W)=\#\{U\mid(UU_{0}^{-1})^{t}UU_{0}^{-1}=W_{1}\}=h(1_{n}),

so we can
assume that
W
is reduced
. This clearly implies that

(7.23)

\displaystyle h(W)\ll_{n}1.

Then from (
7.21
) and (
7.23
), we have

(7.24)

\displaystyle\mathbb{S}\ll_{n}\sum\nolimits_{W}\left(1+\lambda_{1}\tr(\widetilde{Y}_{D}^{-1}W)\right)^{-k^{\prime}/2}\ll\sum\nolimits_{W}\left(1+\lambda_{1}\sum\nolimits_{j=1}^{n}\mu_{j}^{-1}w_{j}\right)^{-k^{\prime}/2}.

We execute the sum over
W
by grouping together
W
such that
\prod_{j}w_{j}=m
for integers
m\geq 1
. Let us define the higher divisor function
\sigma^{(n)}(m)

and the number theoretic function
g(\underline{t})
where
\underline{t}=(t_{1},t_{2},\cdots,t_{n})\in\mathbb{N}^{n}
by

\displaystyle\sigma^{(n)}(m)

\displaystyle=\#\left\{(s_{1},s_{2},\ldots,s_{n})\in\mathbb{N}^{n}\;\middle|\;\prod\nolimits_{j}s_{j}=m\right\},

(7.25)

\displaystyle g(\underline{t})

\displaystyle=\#\left\{V\in\operatorname{SL}(n,\mathbb{Z})\cap\operatorname{Sym}(n,\mathbb{Z})^{+}\;\middle|\;V_{D}=\mathrm{diag}(t_{1},t_{2},\ldots,t_{n})\right\},

where
V_{D}
denotes the diagonal matrix with the diagonal elements of
V
. It is well-known (see e.g.
[
Ten95
]
) that
\sigma^{(n)}(m)\ll_{\epsilon}m^{\epsilon}
and that
g(\underline{t})\ll(\prod_{j}t_{j})^{(n-1)/2}
. The latter can be checked as follows.

If
V=(v_{i,j})\in g(\underline{t})
, then
v_{i}:=v_{i,i}=t_{i}
for all
1\leq i\leq n
. Further since
V>0
, one has
2|v_{i,j}|\leq(t_{i}t_{j})^{1/2}
. Since
t_{i}\geq 1
for all
i
this implies that the number of choices for
V
is

\displaystyle\ll\prod\nolimits_{i<j}(t_{i}t_{j})^{1/2}=(\prod\nolimits_{j}t_{j})^{(n-1)/2}.

Then (
7.24
) can be written as

\displaystyle\mathbb{S}

\displaystyle\ll_{n}\sum\nolimits_{m=1}^{\infty}\,\sum\nolimits_{\underline{t}\in\mathbb{N}^{n},\,\prod_{j}t_{j}=m}g(\underline{t})\big(1+\lambda_{1}\sum\nolimits_{j=1}^{n}\mu_{j}^{-1}t_{j}\big)^{-k^{\prime}/2}.

Applying the A.M.-G.M. inequality to the sum over
j
above, we get

\displaystyle\mathbb{S}

\displaystyle\ll_{n}\lambda_{1}^{-\frac{nk^{\prime}}{2(n+1)}}\sum\nolimits_{m=1}^{\infty}\,\,\sum\nolimits_{\underline{t}\in\mathbb{N}^{n},\,\prod_{j}t_{j}=m}(\prod\nolimits_{j}t_{j})^{(n-1)/2}(\prod\nolimits_{j}\mu_{j})^{\frac{k^{\prime}}{2(n+1)}}(\prod\nolimits_{j}t_{j})^{-\frac{k^{\prime}}{2(n+1)}}

\displaystyle=\left(\widetilde{Y}_{D}/\lambda_{1}^{n}\right)^{\frac{k^{\prime}}{2(n+1)}}\sum\nolimits_{m=1}^{\infty}m^{-\frac{k^{\prime}}{2(n+1)}+\frac{n-1}{2}}\sigma^{(n)}(m)

(7.26)

\displaystyle\ll_{n,\epsilon}\left(\widetilde{Y}_{1}/\lambda_{1}^{n}\right)^{k^{\prime}/(2(n+1))}\sum\nolimits_{m=1}^{\infty}m^{(-k^{\prime}+n^{2}-1)/(2(n+1))+\epsilon}\ll_{n}\left(\widetilde{Y}/\lambda_{1}^{n}\right)^{k^{\prime}/(2(n+1))},

provided
(k^{\prime}-n^{2}+1)/(2(n+1))>1+\epsilon
i.e.,
k^{\prime}>(1+\epsilon)(n+1)^{2}
. Thus, as mentioned at the onset of the proof, we choose

k^{\prime}=(n+1)^{2}+\epsilon.

With this choice, we see that
\mathbb{S}\ll(\widetilde{Y}/\lambda_{1}^{n})^{(n+1)/2+\epsilon}
. Putting this in (
7.1
) finishes the proof.
∎

Theorem 7.5
.

The Bergman kernel
\mathbb{B}_{k}(Z,Z)
, for
k>(n+1)^{2}
and for all
Z\in\mathcal{F}_{n}
is bounded by

(7.27)

\displaystyle\mathbb{B}_{k}(Z,Z)\ll_{n,\epsilon}\begin{cases}k^{3n(n+1)/4}\det(Y)^{(n+1)/2+\epsilon}y_{1}^{-n(n+1)/2-n\epsilon}\\
k^{n(n+1)/2}\det(Y)^{3(n+1)/4+\epsilon}y_{1}^{-n(n+1)/2-n\epsilon},\end{cases}

where
y_{1}
is the smallest diagonal entry of
Y
. The above result can also be stated in terms of
\lambda_{1}
, the smallest eigenvalue of
Y
in place of
y_{1}
.

Proof.

We look at the expression for
\mathbb{B}_{k}(Z,Z)
from (
7.3
) for
Z\in\mathcal{F}_{n}
. We only explain the first of the above bounds, and the rest is similar.
For the sum over
T
and
U
respectively, we use the bounds from Lemma
7.3
and Lemma
7.4
. Namely, after executing the sum over
T
as in Lemma
7.3
, we apply Lemma
7.4
with appropriate
k
and take
\widetilde{Y}=\Im(M\langle Z\rangle)
. Then notice that the final bound for the sum over
U
(as in (
7.3
)) Lemma
7.4
becomes

\sum\nolimits_{U}\det(\widetilde{Y}+U^{t}YU)^{-k}\ll 2^{-nk}\frac{\det(Y)^{-k+(n+1)/2+\epsilon}\lambda_{1}^{-n(n+1)/2-n\epsilon}}{|\det(CZ+D)|^{-k+n+1+\epsilon}}.

Again looking at (
7.3
), we see that the exponential factor and the power of
\det(Y)^{k}
cancel off – and the remaining sum over the co-prime symmetric pairs
\{C,D\}
converges:

(7.28)

\displaystyle\mathbb{B}_{k}(Z,Z)\ll k^{3n(n+1)/4}\det(Y)^{(n+1)/2+\epsilon}\lambda_{1}^{-n(n+1)/2-n\epsilon}\cdot\sum\nolimits_{\{C,D\}}\frac{1}{|\det(CZ+D)|^{n+1+\epsilon}}.

That the sum over
\{C,D\}
–
\mathbb{E}_{s}(Z):=\sum_{\{C,D\}}|\det(CZ+D)|^{-s}
(being a majorant of the Siegel Eisenstein series) converges for
s>n+1
is well-known (see e.g.
[
Fre83
]
). But we also need to know that it can be bounded independently of
Y
.

To see this, we define the region
W(\delta)=\{Z\in\mathcal{H}_{n}\mid\tr(\Re(Z)^{2})\leq\delta^{-1},\,\Im(Z)\geq\delta 1_{n}\}
. Such regions are often used to establish analytic properties of automorphic forms. We
divide
\mathcal{F}_{n}
into two parts –
K=W(2/n)\cap\mathcal{F}_{n}
and
V=\mathcal{F}_{n}-K
. Note that
K=\{Z\in\mathcal{F}_{n}\mid\det(Y)\geq 2/n\}
.

In
K
we invoke
[
Fre83
, p. 68,
5.4_{2}
Hilfssatz]
to
Z_{0}=i1_{n}
to obtain that for all
Z\in K
, one has
|\det(CZ+D)|\geq c|\det(Ci1_{n}+D)|
for some constant
c>0
depending only on
n
. Then clearly
\mathbb{E}_{n+1+\epsilon}(Z)\ll\mathbb{E}_{n+1+\epsilon}(i1_{n})
and we are done.

For the region
V
, note that
V\subset K^{\prime}=\{Z\in\mathcal{F}_{n}\mid\det(Y)\leq 2\}
. Since
K^{\prime}
is compact, the continuous function
\mathbb{E}_{n+1+\epsilon}(Z)
attains a maximum
Z_{*}
in
K^{\prime}
which depends only on
n
.

Because
Y
is positive definite, we have
\lambda_{1}\leq y_{1}
and from the reduction conditions (
2.1
), we have
y_{1}\leq r_{n}\,\lambda_{1}
. So the two can be swapped freely.
For the second bound, we proceed exactly as above, but with the second bound from Lemma
7.3
and using Lemma
7.4
. This finishes the proof.
∎

.

Let

,

and consider
F\in S^{n}_{k}
with

. Then

\det(Y)^{k/2}|F(Z)|\ll_{n,\epsilon}\begin{cases}k^{\frac{3n(n+1)}{8}}\det(Y)^{\frac{n+1}{4}+\epsilon}\\
k^{\frac{n(n+1)}{4}}\det(Y)^{\frac{3(n+1)}{8}+\epsilon}.\end{cases}

Remark 7.7
.

If we follow the proof of Theorem
7.5
but use Lemma
7.1
instead of Lemma

, then the absolute sum is bounded by

for

.

7.2.
The case

: bound for the sup-norm using minimal hypotheses

When

, we of course have H. Xia’s sharp result
[
Xia07
]
that for a Hecke eigenform

with
{\mathchoice{\mathopen{\hbox to5.00002pt{\hss\vrule height=7.5pt,depth=2.5pt,width=1.50002pt\hss}}f\mathclose{\hbox to5.00002pt{\hss\vrule height=7.5pt,depth=2.5pt,width=1.50002pt\hss}}}{\mathopen{\hbox to5.00002pt{\hss\vrule height=7.5pt,depth=2.5pt,width=1.50002pt\hss}}f\mathclose{\hbox to5.00002pt{\hss\vrule height=7.5pt,depth=2.5pt,width=1.50002pt\hss}}}{\mathopen{\hbox to5.00002pt{\hss\vrule height=5.25pt,depth=1.75pt,width=1.50002pt\hss}}f\mathclose{\hbox to5.00002pt{\hss\vrule height=5.25pt,depth=1.75pt,width=1.50002pt\hss}}}{\mathopen{\hbox to5.00002pt{\hss\vrule height=3.75pt,depth=1.25pt,width=1.50002pt\hss}}f\mathclose{\hbox to5.00002pt{\hss\vrule height=3.75pt,depth=1.25pt,width=1.50002pt\hss}}}}_{2}=1
,
k^{1/4-\epsilon}\ll_{\epsilon}{\mathchoice{\mathopen{\hbox to5.00002pt{\hss\vrule height=7.5pt,depth=2.5pt,width=1.50002pt\hss}}f\mathclose{\hbox to5.00002pt{\hss\vrule height=7.5pt,depth=2.5pt,width=1.50002pt\hss}}}{\mathopen{\hbox to5.00002pt{\hss\vrule height=7.5pt,depth=2.5pt,width=1.50002pt\hss}}f\mathclose{\hbox to5.00002pt{\hss\vrule height=7.5pt,depth=2.5pt,width=1.50002pt\hss}}}{\mathopen{\hbox to5.00002pt{\hss\vrule height=5.25pt,depth=1.75pt,width=1.50002pt\hss}}f\mathclose{\hbox to5.00002pt{\hss\vrule height=5.25pt,depth=1.75pt,width=1.50002pt\hss}}}{\mathopen{\hbox to5.00002pt{\hss\vrule height=3.75pt,depth=1.25pt,width=1.50002pt\hss}}f\mathclose{\hbox to5.00002pt{\hss\vrule height=3.75pt,depth=1.25pt,width=1.50002pt\hss}}}}_{\infty}\ll_{\epsilon}k^{1/4+\epsilon}
for any
\epsilon>0
. This result is reliant on Deligne’s bound and deep properties of zeros of
L
-functions, namely the non-existence of Landau-Siegel zeros for the symmetric-square
L
-function attached to
f
. This directly implies
\mathbb{B}_{k}(Z,Z)\ll k^{3/2+\epsilon}
.
Here our aim is modest: we want to illustrate a simpler way of proving the bound
\mathbb{B}_{k}(Z,Z)\ll k^{3/2}
. This bound has been obtained by Kramer et al.
[
FJK19
]
by analysing the heat kernel corresponding to the weight-
k
Laplacian on
\mathcal{H}_{1}
.

Keeping in view of our limited information in higher degrees, the point is that we would only use
the Hecke bound on Fourier coefficients along with a somewhat careful Bergman kernel analysis. This might give some hope of bounding the Bergman kernel in higher degrees to obtain a reasonable sup-norm bound (viz.
{\mathchoice{\mathopen{\hbox to5.00002pt{\hss\vrule height=7.5pt,depth=2.5pt,width=1.50002pt\hss}}F\mathclose{\hbox to5.00002pt{\hss\vrule height=7.5pt,depth=2.5pt,width=1.50002pt\hss}}}{\mathopen{\hbox to5.00002pt{\hss\vrule height=7.5pt,depth=2.5pt,width=1.50002pt\hss}}F\mathclose{\hbox to5.00002pt{\hss\vrule height=7.5pt,depth=2.5pt,width=1.50002pt\hss}}}{\mathopen{\hbox to5.00002pt{\hss\vrule height=5.25pt,depth=1.75pt,width=1.50002pt\hss}}F\mathclose{\hbox to5.00002pt{\hss\vrule height=5.25pt,depth=1.75pt,width=1.50002pt\hss}}}{\mathopen{\hbox to5.00002pt{\hss\vrule height=3.75pt,depth=1.25pt,width=1.50002pt\hss}}F\mathclose{\hbox to5.00002pt{\hss\vrule height=3.75pt,depth=1.25pt,width=1.50002pt\hss}}}}_{\infty}\ll_{\epsilon}k^{3n(n+1)/8+\epsilon}
) and seems to us the most promising approach to be considered in the future. We first recall a simple lemma.

Lemma 7.8
.

Let
\alpha,d>0
.

(7.30)

\displaystyle\sum\nolimits_{t\geq 1}t^{\alpha-1}\exp\left(-td\right)\ll d^{-\alpha+1}\Gamma(\alpha)(d^{-1}+(\alpha-1)^{-1/2}).

Proof.

For this note that the function
h(x):=x^{\alpha-1}\exp\left(-xd\right)
attains its maximum at
x=(\alpha-1)/d
and so the l.h.s. of (
7.30
) is bounded by

(7.31)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}

\displaystyle x^{\alpha-1}\exp\left(-xd\right)dx+2h(d/(\alpha-1))=d^{-\alpha}\Gamma(\alpha)+2\left(\frac{\alpha-1}{d}\right)^{\alpha-1}\exp\left(-\alpha+1\right)

which is bounded as

\ll d^{-\alpha+1}\Gamma(\alpha)(\frac{1}{d}+\frac{1}{(\alpha-1)^{1/2}})

up to an implied absolute constant.
∎

From the Petersson formula and bounding the
n
-th Fourier coefficient of the Poincaré series
P_{n}
with the trivial bound for Kloosterman sums and Bessel functions (i.e.,
J_{k-1}(x)\leq\min\{1,(x/2)^{k-1}\}
for all
x>0
), we have the following uniform bound for any orthonormal basis
\mathcal{B}_{k}^{1}
of
S_{k}
:

\sqrt{\sum\nolimits_{f\in\mathcal{B}_{k}^{1}}|a_{f}(n)|^{2}}\leq\frac{(4\pi n)^{k/2}}{\Gamma(k-1)^{1/2}}.

Then from the lemma above with
\alpha=1+k/2
and
d=2\pi y
, and Lemma
4.4
, we get

(7.32)

\displaystyle\sqrt{\mathbb{B}_{k}(Z,Z)}\ll\frac{\Gamma(k/2+1)\,(y^{-1}+k^{-1/2})\,(4\pi)^{k/2}}{\Gamma(k-1)^{1/2}\,(2\pi)^{k/2}}\ll k^{5/4}(y^{-1}+k^{-1/2})\ll k^{3/4}

in the region
\{z\in\mathcal{F}_{1}\mid y\geq k^{1/2}\}
.

In the complementary region, we look at the Bergman kernel directly. We already know the shape of the bound: for any
z\in\mathcal{F}_{1}
,

\displaystyle\frac{\mathbb{B}_{k}(Z,Z)}{k}

\displaystyle\ll\sum_{\gamma\in\Gamma_{1}\colon\left\lvert\gamma(z)-z\right\rvert>\sqrt{\delta}y}|h_{\gamma}(z)|^{k}+\sum_{\gamma\in\Gamma_{1}\colon\left\lvert\gamma(z)-z\right\rvert\leq\sqrt{\delta}y}|h_{\gamma}(z)|^{k}

\displaystyle\ll\max_{\gamma\in\Gamma_{1}\colon\left\lvert\gamma(z)-z\right\rvert>\sqrt{\delta}y}|h_{\gamma}(z)|^{k-k_{0}}\big(\sum_{\gamma\in\Gamma_{1}}|h_{\gamma}(z)|^{k_{0}}\big)+\#\{\gamma\in\Gamma_{1}\colon\left\lvert\gamma(z)-z\right\rvert\leq\sqrt{\delta}y\}

(7.33)

\displaystyle\ll(1+\delta)^{-k+k_{0}}y^{2}+X_{\delta},

where
X_{\delta}=\#\{\gamma\in\Gamma_{1}\colon|\gamma(z)-z|\leq\sqrt{\delta}y\}
and use Remark
7.7
. For the bound on
\max|h_{\gamma}(z)|
we have used (
8.15
) (see also
[
CL11
, Lemma 1]
, keeping in mind the dependence on
y
).
We now estimate the size of
X_{\delta}
carefully. We follow the setting of section
8.1
and
[
DS15
, section 3]
. We make things explicit because it is possible and useful, even though this may be a little repetition to section
8.1
.

Let us recall that the condition
|\gamma(z)-z|\leq\alpha
implies that

\left\lvert\gamma_{0}^{-1}\gamma\gamma_{0}-\mathsf{k}\right\rvert\ll(\alpha/y)^{1/2}
. Here
\mathsf{k}_{z}=\gamma_{0}\mathsf{k}\gamma_{0}^{-1}
, where
\gamma_{0}=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}y^{1/2}&xy^{-1/2}\\
0&y^{-1/2}\end{smallmatrix}\right)
. For us,
\alpha=\sqrt{\delta}y
. Thus

\left\lvert\gamma_{0}^{-1}\gamma\gamma_{0}-\mathsf{k}\right\rvert\ll\delta^{1/4}
, i.e.,
\gamma_{0}^{-1}\gamma\gamma_{0}=\mathsf{k}+O(\delta^{1/4})
.

We see that if we put coordinates

c&d\end{smallmatrix}\right)
,
\mathsf{k}=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}p&q\\
-q&p\end{smallmatrix}\right)
such that
p^{2}+q^{2}=1
, then
from the analysis done in section
8.1
(see also
[
DS15
, section 3]
)

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=\gamma_{0}\begin{pmatrix}p&q\\
-q&p\end{pmatrix}\gamma_{0}^{-1}+O\left(\gamma_{0}O(\delta^{1/4})\gamma_{0}^{-1}\right)

(7.34)

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}p-qx/y&qx^{2}/y+qy\\
-q/y&qx/y+p\end{pmatrix}+O\begin{pmatrix}\delta^{1/4}&\delta^{1/4}y\\
\delta^{1/4}/y&\delta^{1/4}\end{pmatrix}.

The
O(\cdots)
term can be seen simply by "pretending" that the quantities
p,q
were

.

Our choice for

is
\delta=\delta_{0}:=k^{\epsilon}/k
. With this choice, we see that the number of choices of

in (

) is absolutely bounded. Further the number of choices for

is at most

. Since we are considering the region

, we get

(7.35)

\displaystyle X_{\delta_{0}}\ll k^{1/2}.

The first term in (
7.33
) decays exponentially in

.
Thus from (
7.32
), (
7.33
) and (

) we conclude that for all

,

(7.36)

\mathbb{B}_{k}(z,z)=\sum\nolimits_{f}y^{k}|f(z)|^{2}\ll k^{3/2}.

8.
Amplification: over compact sets

In this section, we want to go beyond the preliminary bound on the Bergman kernel over a compact subset of
\mathcal{F}_{n}
and prove a power saving bound for the same. For this, we would use the setting of the amplification method considered in
[
BP16
]
and
[
DS15
]
along with some of our earlier results.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathcal{F}_{n}
be a compact set which does not depend on
k
. By the results of
[
CL11
]

we see that for any
\Omega\subset\mathcal{F}_{n}^{\circ}
(
\mathcal{F}_{n}^{\circ}
being the interior of
\mathcal{F}_{n}
) as above, and some

,

(8.1)

\displaystyle\mathbb{B}_{k}(Z,Z)=2^{-1}a(n,k)R_{k}(Z)=a(n,k)+O(e^{-\delta k})\asymp_{n}k^{n(n+1)/2}.

This is actually worked out in a few lines before section 5 in
[
CL11
]
. Otherwise, and also generally one argues that

(8.2)

\displaystyle\mathbb{B}_{k}(Z,Z)\ll a(n,k)\sum\nolimits_{\gamma\in\Gamma_{n}}|h_{\gamma}(Z)|^{k}\ll a(n,k)\sum\nolimits_{\gamma\in\Gamma_{n}}|h_{\gamma}(Z)|^{2n+1}\ll_{\Omega}a(n,k),

since by
[
CL11
, Lemma 1]
,

and by Godement’s theorem as given in
[
Kli90
, p. 79, Chap. 6, Prop. 2(iii)]
, for all

(

even), the series

\det(Y)^{\kappa/2}\sum\nolimits_{\gamma\in\Gamma_{n}}|h_{\gamma}(Z)|^{\kappa}

converges uniformly for all

as above.
However,
\det(Y)
is bounded below (even absolutely, since

), and so we have (
8.2
). This can also be derived easily from the bound in Remark
7.7
. Dropping all but one term in (
8.2
) gives the ‘generic’ or the trivial bound

(8.4)

{\mathchoice{\mathopen{\hbox to5.00002pt{\hss\vrule height=7.5pt,depth=2.5pt,width=1.50002pt\hss}}F\mathclose{\hbox to5.00002pt{\hss\vrule height=7.5pt,depth=2.5pt,width=1.50002pt\hss}}}{\mathopen{\hbox to5.00002pt{\hss\vrule height=7.5pt,depth=2.5pt,width=1.50002pt\hss}}F\mathclose{\hbox to5.00002pt{\hss\vrule height=7.5pt,depth=2.5pt,width=1.50002pt\hss}}}{\mathopen{\hbox to5.00002pt{\hss\vrule height=5.25pt,depth=1.75pt,width=1.50002pt\hss}}F\mathclose{\hbox to5.00002pt{\hss\vrule height=5.25pt,depth=1.75pt,width=1.50002pt\hss}}}{\mathopen{\hbox to5.00002pt{\hss\vrule height=3.75pt,depth=1.25pt,width=1.50002pt\hss}}F\mathclose{\hbox to5.00002pt{\hss\vrule height=3.75pt,depth=1.25pt,width=1.50002pt\hss}}}}_{\Omega}=\sup_{\Omega}\,\det(Y)^{k/2}|F(Z)|\ll_{n}k^{n(n+1)/4}.

This also follows from the second bound in Corollary
7.6
. Our aim is to improve (
8.4
).

Henceforth in this subsection let us assume that
F
is an
L^{2}
normalised Hecke eigenform which is not a Saito-Kurokawa (SK) lift. (SK lifts will be discussed separately, see
[
AD22
]
.) Accordingly, we embed
F
in a Hecke (ortho-normalised) basis, say
\mathcal{B}^{*}_{k}
.
Our choice of the amplifier is the same as constructed in
[
BP16
]
. We quote the inequality at the heart of the amplification:

(8.5)

\displaystyle|\lambda(p,F)|+\frac{1}{p^{3/2}}|\lambda(p^{2},F)|+\frac{1}{p^{9/2}}|\lambda(p^{4},F)|\gg p^{3/2},

which says that the three eigenvalues above cannot be simultaneously small. For the reader’s convenience we recall a proof of (

by the work of Weissauer
[
Wei09
]
. In particular this implies that
x=|\lambda(p,F)|,y=|\lambda(p^{2},F)|,z=|\lambda(p^{4},F)|
are

for

respectively and the implied constant is absolute.
For future reference note the normalisation

Fix an

(8.6)

\displaystyle A_{F}:=(\sum_{p\in\mathcal{P}}x(p)\lambda(p,F))^{2}+(\sum_{p\in\mathcal{P}}x(p^{2})p^{-3/2}\lambda(p^{2},F))^{2}+(\sum_{p\in\mathcal{P}}x(p^{4})p^{-9/2}\lambda(p^{4},F))^{2}\geq 0.

\displaystyle A(Z,W)=\sum_{r=1,2,4}\Bigg(\sum_{p_{1}\neq p_{2}}\frac{x(p^{r}_{1}p^{r}_{2})}{(p_{1}p_{2})^{3(r-1)/2}}

\displaystyle\sum_{F}F(Z)\overline{(F|T_{p^{r}_{1}p^{r}_{2}})(W)}

\displaystyle+\sum_{p}\frac{1}{p^{3(r-1)}}\sum_{F}F(Z)\overline{F|(T_{p^{r}})^{2}(W)}\Bigg).

\displaystyle A(Z,W)\ll\sum_{r=1,2,4}\sum_{p_{1}\neq p_{2}}(p_{1}p_{2})^{3(1-r)/2}|B^{p_{1}^{r}p_{2}^{r}}(Z,W)|+\sum_{0\leq s\leq 4}\sum_{p\in\mathcal{P}}p^{3-2s}|B^{p^{2s}}(Z,W)|

\mathbb{B}^{m}(Z,W):=\det(Y)^{k}B^{m}(Z,W)

when
Z=W
.
For
\gamma\in S(m)

where we have put

Since the action of

8.1.

For a matrix

A

.

First of all from
[
CL11
, Lemma 1]
, we know that
|h_{\gamma}(Z)|\leq 1
for all

. Furthermore, from the same lemma, given any

\displaystyle|h_{\gamma}(Z)|\leq(1+\rho^{2})^{-1/2}\text{ provided }\left\lVert\gamma(Z)-Z\right\rVert>c\,\rho\,\tr(Y).

Accordingly, we define

(with

\delta_{0}=:c\,\rho\tr(Y).

Throughout the rest of this subsection, we put
Y^{1/2}
to be the unique positive definite (symmetric) square-root of

and define

0&Y^{-1/2}\end{pmatrix}\in\operatorname{Sp}(2,\mathbb{R}),

so that
\gamma_{0}(i1_{2})=Z

Lemma 8.1
.

With
\gamma_{0}
as above, and any
Z_{1},Z_{2}\in\mathcal{H}_{n}
, one has

(8.16)

\left\lVert\gamma_{0}(Z_{1})-\gamma_{0}(Z_{2})\right\rVert\leq a\implies\left\lVert Z_{1}-Z_{2}\right\rVert\leq a\,\tr(Y^{-1}).

Proof.

One observes that
\gamma_{0}(Z_{j})=Y^{1/2}Z_{j}Y^{1/2}+X
for
j=1,2
and so the hypothesis gives us
\left\lVert Y^{1/2}WY^{1/2}\right\rVert\leq a
; where we have put
W=Z_{1}-Z_{2}
.

Recall that
\left\lVert A\right\rVert^{2}=\tr(A\overline{A}^{t})
. Let
P
be an orthogonal matrix diagonalizing
Y
:
PYP^{t}=D
(say) with eigenvalues
d_{1},\cdots,d_{n}
. Then

\displaystyle\left\lVert Y^{1/2}WY^{1/2}\right\rVert^{2}=\tr(YWY\overline{W})=\tr(DW_{P}D\overline{W}_{P}),

where we have put
W_{P}=P^{t}WP
. Then it follows that

\sum_{i}d_{i}^{2}s_{i}\leq a^{2},\text{ where }s_{i}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}|W_{i,j}|^{2}

Now it is easy to see that
\left\lVert W\right\rVert^{2}=\left\lVert W_{P}\right\rVert^{2}=\sum_{i}s_{i}\leq a^{2}(\sum_{i}d_{i}^{-2})=a^{2}\,\tr(Y^{-1})^{2}
from which the lemma follows.
∎

Our next aim is to show that for small enough
\delta_{0}
, the condition
\left\lVert\gamma(Z)-Z\right\rVert\leq\delta_{0}
(
\gamma\in\operatorname{Sp}(n,\mathbb{R})
) implies that
\gamma
is ‘close’ to the maximal compact subgroup
K_{Z}=\gamma_{0}K\gamma_{0}^{-1}
fixing
Z
, in a suitable sense. For this we recall the Iwasawa decomposition of
\operatorname{Sp}(n,\mathbb{R})
: for
g\in\operatorname{Sp}(n,\mathbb{R})
,

\displaystyle g=\mathcal{N}\mathcal{A}\mathcal{K};\quad\mathcal{N}=\begin{pmatrix}B&C\\
0&{B^{t}}^{-1}\end{pmatrix},\mathcal{A}=\begin{pmatrix}L&0\\
0&L^{-1}\end{pmatrix},\mathcal{K}=\begin{pmatrix}P&Q\\
-Q&P\end{pmatrix};

where
B
is unipotent and upper triangular,
L=\mathrm{diag}(e^{t_{1}},\cdots,e^{t_{n}})
, and
\mathcal{K}\in K=\operatorname{SO}(2n,\mathbb{R})\cap\operatorname{Sp}(n,\mathbb{R})
.

Put
\tilde{\gamma_{1}}=\gamma_{0}^{-1}\tilde{\gamma}\gamma_{0}
so that
\left\lVert\gamma(Z)-Z\right\rVert=\left\lVert\gamma_{0}\tilde{\gamma_{1}}(i1_{n})-\gamma_{0}(i1_{n})\right\rVert\leq\delta_{0}
implies, by Lemma
8.1
that

(8.17)

\displaystyle\left\lVert\tilde{\gamma_{1}}(i1_{n})-i1_{n}\right\rVert=\left\lVert\mathcal{N}\mathcal{A}(i1_{n})-i1_{n}\right\rVert\leq\delta_{0}\,\tr(Y^{-1})=:\eta.

Here we have used the Iwasawa decomposition for
\tilde{\gamma_{1}}
, along with th fact that
\mathcal{K}
fixes
i1_{n}
. We see that

\displaystyle\eta^{2}\geq\left\lVert CB^{t}+i(BL^{2}B^{t}-1_{n})\right\rVert^{2}=\tr\left((N+iM)(N-iM)\right)=\tr(N^{2})+\tr(M^{2});

where we have put
N=CB^{t}
and
M=(m_{i,j})=BL^{2}B^{t}-1_{n}
.

From now on, we will assume for simplicity that
n=2
, but this part of the analysis, up to the end of this subsection, carries over to any
n
.

Further put
G=(g_{i,j})=BL
, so that

M=GG^{t}-1_{2}=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}g^{2}_{1,1}+g^{2}_{1,2}-1&g_{1,2}g_{2,2}\\
g_{1,2}g_{2,2}&g^{2}_{2,2}-1\\
\end{smallmatrix}\right).

Then
\tr(M^{2})\leq\eta^{2}
shows that
\sum_{i}\sum_{j}m_{i,j}^{2}\leq\eta^{2}
. In particular
m_{i,i}^{2}\leq\eta^{2}
.
Thus we have for all
1\leq i\leq 2
,

\displaystyle\left\lvert m_{i,i}\right\rvert=|\sum\nolimits_{j\geq i}g_{i,j}^{2}-1|\leq\eta\implies 1-\eta\leq\sum\nolimits_{j\geq i}g_{i,j}^{2}\leq 1+\eta.

Note that
G
is upper triangular, and
g_{i,j}=b_{i,j}l_{j}
for
j\geq i
and
0
otherwise, where obviously
B=(b_{i,j})
and
l_{j}=e^{t_{j}}
are the entries of
L
. We have
b_{1,1}=b_{2,2}=1
. Then from
m_{2,2}=g^{2}_{2,2}-1=l^{2}_{2}-1
we see that

1-\eta\leq l^{2}_{2}\leq 1+\eta
. Moving on, next consider
m_{1,2}=g_{1,2}g_{2,2}
. From
|m_{1,2}|\leq\eta

we infer that
|g_{1,2}|\leq\eta/(1-\eta)
. Since
g_{1,2}=b_{1,2}l_{2}
, we get
|b_{1,2}|\leq\eta/(1-\eta)^{3/2}\ll\eta
for
\eta
small enough. Finally from
|m_{1,1}|\leq\eta
we get
l_{1}=g_{1,1}

satisfies
1-2\eta\leq l^{2}_{1}\leq 1+2\eta
if

.

Now

and so
\left\lVert C\right\rVert\leq\left\lVert N\right\rVert\left\lVert B^{-1}\right\rVert\ll\eta
.
In our notation now note that

\mathcal{N}\mathcal{A}-I_{4}=\begin{pmatrix}BL&CL^{-1}\\
0&{B^{t}}^{-1}L^{-1}\end{pmatrix}-I_{4}=\begin{pmatrix}G-I_{2}&CL^{-1}\\
0&{G^{t}}^{-1}-I_{2}\end{pmatrix}

which, by the above analysis implies that

for
\eta
small enough. This is easy to see since

\end{smallmatrix}\right)
and with

are close to

whereas

is close to

. Moreover,
C
is also close to

. Thus
\mathcal{N}\mathcal{A}=I_{4}+O(\eta^{1/2})
. Multiplying by

on both sides gives
\tilde{\gamma_{1}}=\mathcal{K}+O(\eta^{1/2})
.
Conjugating with
\gamma_{0}
gives (recall

and (

))

\displaystyle\left\lVert\tilde{\gamma}-K_{Z}\right\rVert\ll\eta^{1/2}=(c\,\rho\,\tr(Y)\tr(Y^{-1}))^{1/2}\quad\quad(\delta_{0}=:c\,\rho\tr(Y)).

) and the estimate (

\displaystyle 2a(2,k)^{-1}\mathbb{B}^{m}(Z,Z)\ll(1+\rho^{2})^{-k+k_{0}}\sum\nolimits_{\gamma\in S(m)}|h_{\gamma}(Z)|^{k_{0}}+\#\mathscr{S}_{m}(Z,u\rho^{1/2}),

where

, which depends only on

.

8.2.
Back to the proof

To bound the quantity
\sum_{\gamma\in S(m)}|h_{\gamma}(Z)|^{k_{0}}
we use the method in

]
. Unfolding, we can write

\displaystyle\sum\nolimits_{\gamma\in S(m)}|h_{\gamma}(Z)|^{k_{0}}\leq\sum\nolimits_{j}(\sum\nolimits_{\gamma\in\Gamma_{2}}|h_{\gamma M_{j}}(Z)|^{k_{0}}).

Put
h_{\gamma}(Z,W)^{k_{0}}=\det\left(\frac{Z-\overline{W}}{2i}\right)\lvert^{(W)}_{k_{0}}\gamma
.
From
[
Kli90
, p. 79,81]
(Godement’s theorem) we know that (with
V=\Im(W)
) for

,

\displaystyle\sum\nolimits_{\gamma\in S(m)}|h_{\gamma}(Z,W)|^{k_{0}}

\displaystyle=\sum\nolimits_{j}\sum\nolimits_{\gamma\in\Gamma_{2}}\left.\left\lvert h_{\gamma}(Z,W)\right\rvert^{k_{0}}\right|_{k_{0}}^{(W)}M_{j}\ll_{\Omega}\sum\nolimits_{j}\left\lvert\det(V)^{-k_{0}/2}|_{k_{0}}M_{j}\right\rvert

(8.20)

\displaystyle\ll\#\{\Gamma_{2}\backslash S(m)\},

since
\det(V)^{-k_{0}/2}
is invariant under the
|_{k_{0}}
action of
\operatorname{Sp}(2,\mathbb{R})
in absolute value.

The following lemma perhaps is known, but since we could not find a proof in the literature, so we include it here for convenience.

Lemma 8.2
.

With the above notation,

\#\{\Gamma_{2}\backslash S(m)\}\leq m^{3}\sigma_{0}(m)^{2}
.

Proof.

From
[
And74
]
or
[
Kod67
]
, we see that a set of right coset representatives of the set
\Gamma_{2}\backslash S(m)
can be chosen in the form
\left(\begin{smallmatrix}A&B\\
0&C\end{smallmatrix}\right)
(
A,B,D\in\operatorname{M}(2,\mathbb{Z})
) where

\displaystyle AC^{t}=mI_{2},AB^{t}=BA^{t},\quad A=(a_{i,j}),B=(b_{i,j}),C=(c_{i,j}),

\displaystyle a_{2,1}=0,0\leq a_{i,j}<a_{j,j}\text{ if }i<j;\quad 0\leq b_{i,j}<c_{j,j}\text{ if }i\leq j.

From the equality
AD^{t}=mI_{2}
we get

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}a_{1,1}c_{1,1}+a_{1,2}c_{1,2}&a_{1,1}c_{2,1}+a_{1,2}c_{2,2}\\
a_{2,2}c_{1,2}&a_{2,2}c_{2,2}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}m&0\\
0&m\end{pmatrix},

which shows that
a_{2,2}c_{1,2}=0=a_{1,1}c_{2,1}+a_{1,2}c_{2,2}
;
a_{2,2}c_{2,2}=m=a_{1,1}c_{1,1}+a_{1,2}c_{1,2}
. Clearly
c_{1,2}=0
and so
a_{1,1}c_{1,1}=m
as well.

From the second equality
AB^{t}=BA^{t}
we get the relation
b_{2,1}a_{1,1}+b_{2,2}a_{1,2}=a_{2,2}b_{1,2}
. So
b_{2,1}
is determined from the rest of the quantities.

The number of choices for
A
is at most
m/c_{2,2}
given
C
; similarly
the number of choices for
B
is at most
c_{1,1}c_{2,2}^{2}
. Therefore, the total number of coset representatives is at most

\displaystyle\sum\nolimits_{c_{1,1}\mid m,\,c_{2,2}\mid m}\frac{m}{c_{2,2}}c_{1,1}c_{2,2}^{2}\leq m^{3}\sigma_{0}(m)^{2},

since
c_{2,1}
is determined from
a_{1,1}c_{2,1}+a_{1,2}c_{2,2}=0
.
∎

\displaystyle a(2,k)^{-1}\mathbb{B}^{m}(Z,Z)\ll(1+\rho^{2})^{-k+k_{0}}m^{3+\epsilon}+\mathscr{S}_{m}(Z,u\rho^{1/2}).

Quoting from

Finally, with (
8.21

and obtain

(8.23)

\displaystyle a(2,k)^{-1}\mathbb{B}^{m}(Z,Z)\ll(1+\rho^{2})^{-k+k_{0}}m^{3+\epsilon}+m^{1+\epsilon}(1+\rho^{B_{1}}m^{B_{2}}),

From (
8.11
) we now see that
\mathbb{A}(Z,Z)
is

\displaystyle\ll_{\epsilon,\Omega}a(2,k)\Big(\sum_{r=1,2,4}\sum_{p_{1}\neq p_{2}}(p_{1}p_{2})^{3(1-r)/2}|\mathbb{B}^{p_{1}^{r}p_{2}^{r}}(Z,Z)|+\sum_{0\leq s\leq 4}\sum_{p\in\mathcal{P}}p^{3-2s}|\mathbb{B}^{p^{2s}}(Z,Z)|\Big)

\displaystyle\ll a(2,k)\left(\sum_{r=1,2,4}\sum_{p_{1}\neq p_{2}}(p_{1}p_{2})^{3(1-r)/2}\big((1+\rho^{2})^{-k+k_{0}}(p_{1}p_{2})^{3r+\epsilon}+(p_{1}p_{2})^{r+\epsilon}(1+\rho^{B_{1}}(p_{1}p_{2})^{rB_{2}})\big)\right.

(8.24)

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\left.+\sum_{0\leq s\leq 4}\sum_{p\in\mathcal{P}}p^{3-2s}\big((1+\rho^{2})^{-k+k_{0}}p^{6s+\epsilon}+p^{2s+\epsilon}(1+\rho^{B_{1}}p^{2sB_{2}})\big)\right)

We now choose
\rho
such that
\rho^{B_{1}}(2L)^{8B_{2}}=1
, and

such that
(1+\rho^{2})^{-k}
decays exponentially:

With these choices (

\displaystyle a(2,k)^{-1}\mathbb{A}(Z,Z)\ll_{\epsilon,\Omega}

\displaystyle\sum_{r=1,2,4}\sum_{p_{1}\neq p_{2}}(p_{1}p_{2})^{3(1-r)/2}\big((p_{1}p_{2})^{3r+\epsilon}e^{-k^{\epsilon}}+(p_{1}p_{2})^{r+\epsilon}\big)

(8.25)

\displaystyle+\sum_{0\leq s\leq 4}\sum_{p\in\mathcal{P}}p^{3-2s}\big(p^{6s+\epsilon}e^{-k^{\epsilon}}+p^{2s+\epsilon}\big)\ll_{\epsilon}\frac{L_{0}^{3+\epsilon}}{\log(L_{0})}.

Comparing (

) and (

)

\displaystyle\frac{L_{0}^{5}}{(\log L_{0})^{2}}\det(Y)^{k}|F_{0}(Z)|^{2}\ll\mathbb{A}(Z,Z)\ll_{\epsilon,\Omega}k^{3}\frac{L_{0}^{3+\epsilon}}{\log(L_{0})}

which finally gives us

\sup_{Z\in\Omega}\det(Y)^{k}|F_{0}(Z)|^{2}\ll_{\epsilon,\Omega}k^{3}\frac{\log(L_{0})}{L_{0}^{2-\epsilon}}\ll k^{3-\omega+\epsilon}

for some absolute constant
\omega>0
since
B_{1},B_{2}
can be effectively computed. Summarising, we have proved

Theorem 8.3
.

Let
F\in S^{2}_{k}
,
\left\lVert F\right\rVert_{2}=1
and
F
be an eigenfunction of all Hecke operators. Assume further that

is orthogonal to the space of Saito-Kurokawa lifts. Then

{\mathchoice{\mathopen{\hbox to5.00002pt{\hss\vrule height=7.5pt,depth=2.5pt,width=1.50002pt\hss}}F\mathclose{\hbox to5.00002pt{\hss\vrule height=7.5pt,depth=2.5pt,width=1.50002pt\hss}}}{\mathopen{\hbox to5.00002pt{\hss\vrule height=7.5pt,depth=2.5pt,width=1.50002pt\hss}}F\mathclose{\hbox to5.00002pt{\hss\vrule height=7.5pt,depth=2.5pt,width=1.50002pt\hss}}}{\mathopen{\hbox to5.00002pt{\hss\vrule height=5.25pt,depth=1.75pt,width=1.50002pt\hss}}F\mathclose{\hbox to5.00002pt{\hss\vrule height=5.25pt,depth=1.75pt,width=1.50002pt\hss}}}{\mathopen{\hbox to5.00002pt{\hss\vrule height=3.75pt,depth=1.25pt,width=1.50002pt\hss}}F\mathclose{\hbox to5.00002pt{\hss\vrule height=3.75pt,depth=1.25pt,width=1.50002pt\hss}}}}_{\Omega}\ll_{\Omega}k^{3/2-\eta}
for some absolute constant
