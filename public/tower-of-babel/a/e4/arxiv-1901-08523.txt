Curvature-Exploiting Acceleration of Elastic Net Computations
Vien V. Mai and Mikael Johansson

Abstract
This paper introduces an efficient second-order method for solving the elastic net problem. Its key innovation is a computationally efficient technique for injecting curvature information in the optimization process which admits a strong theoretical performance guarantee. In particular, we show improved run time over popular first-order methods and quantify the speed-up in terms of statistical measures of the data matrix. The improved time complexity is the result of an extensive exploitation of the problem structure and a careful combination of second-order information, variance reduction techniques, and momentum acceleration. Beside theoretical speed-up, experimental results demonstrate great practical performance benefits of curvature information, especially for ill-conditioned data sets.



1
Introduction

Lasso, ridge and elastic net regression are fundamental problems in statistics and machine learning, with countless applications in science and engineering
[
33
]
.
Elastic net regression amounts to solving the following convex optimization problem

\displaystyle\underset{\bm{x}\in\mathbbmss{R}^{d}}{\mbox{minimize}}\,\bigg\{\frac{1}{2n}\left\|{\bm{A}\bm{x}-\bm{b}}\right\|_{2}^{2}+\frac{\gamma_{2}}{2}\left\|{\bm{x}}\right\|_{2}^{2}+\gamma_{1}\left\|{\bm{x}}\right\|_{1}\bigg\},

(1)

for given data matrices
\bm{A}\in\mathbbmss{R}^{n\times d}
and
\bm{b}\in\mathbbmss{R}^{n}
and regularization parameters
\gamma_{1}
and
\gamma_{2}
. Setting
\gamma_{1}=0
results in ridge regression,
\gamma_{2}=0
yields lasso and letting
\gamma_{1}=\gamma_{2}=0
reduces the problem to the classical least-squares. Lasso promotes sparsity of the optimal solution, which sometimes helps to improve interpretability of the results. Adding the additional
l_{2}
-regularizer helps to improve the performance when features are highly correlated
[
28
,
33
]
.

The convergence rates of iterative methods for solving (
1
) are typically governed by the condition number of the Hessian matrix of the ridge loss,
\bm{C}+\gamma_{2}\mathbf{I}
, where
\bm{C}=\frac{1}{n}\bm{A}^{\top}\bm{A}
is the sample correlation matrix. Real-world data sets often have few dominant features, while the other features are highly correlated with the stronger ones
[
28
,
13
]
.
This translates to a rapidly decaying spectrum of
\bm{C}
. In this paper, we demonstrate how this property can be exploited to reduce the effect of ill-conditioning and to design faster algorithms for solving the elastic net regression problem (
1
).

1.1
Related work

Over the past few years, there has been a great attention to developing efficient optimization algorithms for minimizing composite objective functions

\displaystyle\min_{\bm{x}\in\mathbbmss{R}^{d}}F\left(\bm{x}\right)\triangleq f\left(\bm{x}\right)+h\left(\bm{x}\right),

(2)

where

f\left(\bm{x}\right)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}f_{i}\left(\bm{x}\right)

is a finite sum of smooth and convex component functions
f_{i}\left(\bm{x}\right)
, and
h\left(\bm{x}\right)
is a possibly non-smooth convex regularizer.
In machine learning applications, the function
f
typically models the empirical data loss and the regularizer
h
is used to promote desired properties of a solution. For example, the elastic net objective can fit to this form with

f_{i}(\bm{x})=\frac{1}{2}\left(\bm{a}_{i}^{\top}\bm{x}-\bm{b}_{i}\right)^{2}+\gamma_{2}\left\|{\bm{x}}\right\|_{2}^{2},

and
h(\bm{x})=\gamma_{1}\left\|{\bm{x}}\right\|_{1}.

1.1.1
First-Order methods

Standard deterministic first-order methods for solving (
2
), such as proximal gradient descent, enjoy linear convergence for strongly convex objective functions and are able to find an
\epsilon
-approximate solution in time
O\left(dn\kappa\log\frac{1}{\epsilon}\right)
, where
\kappa
is the condition number of
f
. This runtime can be improved to
O\left(dn\sqrt{\kappa}\log\frac{1}{\epsilon}\right)
if it is combined with Nesterov acceleration
[
4
,
23
]
.
However, the main drawback of these methods is that they need to access the whole data set in every iteration, which is too costly in many machine learning tasks.

For large-scale problems, methods based on stochastic gradients have become the standard choice for solving (
2
).
Many linearly convergent proximal methods such as,
SAGA

[
9
]
and
Prox-SVRG

[
31
]
, have been introduced and shown to outperform standard first-order methods under certain regularity assumptions. These methods improve the time complexity to
O\left(d\left(n+\tilde{\kappa}\right)\log\frac{1}{\epsilon}\right)
, where
\tilde{\kappa}
is a condition number satisfying
\tilde{\kappa}\geq\kappa
.
When the component functions do not vary substantially in smoothness,
\tilde{\kappa}\approx\kappa
, and this complexity is far better than those of deterministic methods above. By exploiting Nesterov momentum in different ways (see, e.g.,
[
11
,
19
,
2
,
8
]
), one can improve the complexity to
O\big(d\big(n+\sqrt{n\tilde{\kappa}}\big)\log\frac{1}{\epsilon}\big)
, which is also optimal for this class of problems
[
30
]
.

1.1.2
Second-order methods

Second-order methods are known to have superior performance compared to their first-order counterparts both in theory and practice, especially when the problem at hand is highly nonlinear and/or ill-conditioned. However, such methods often have very high computational cost per iteration.
Recently, there has been an intense effort to develop algorithms which use second-order information with a more reasonable computational burden (see, e.g.,
[
10
,
21
,
26
,
1
,
31
,
32
,
6
]
and references therein). Those methods use techniques such as random sketching, matrix sampling, and iterative estimation to construct an approximate Hessian matrix. Local and global convergence guarantees have been derived under various assumptions. Although many experimental results have shown excellent performance of those methods on many machine learning tasks, current second-order methods for finite-sum optimization tend to have much higher time-complexities than their first-order counterparts (see
[
32
]
for a detailed comparison).

Apart from having high time complexities, none of the methods cited above have any guarantees in the composite setting since their analyses hinge on differentiability of the objective function. Instead, one has to rely on methods that build on
proximal Newton
updates (see, e.g.,
[
18
,
20
,
12
,
25
]
). However, these methods still inherit the high update and storage costs of conventional second-order methods or require elaborate tuning of several parameters and stopping criteria depending on a phase transition which occurs in the algorithm.

1.1.3
Ridge regression

For the smooth ridge regression problem, the authors in
[
13
]
have developed a preconditioning method based on linear sketching which, when coupled with
SVRG
, yields a significant speed-up over stochastic first-order methods. This is a rare second-order method that has a comparable or even better time complexity than stochastic first-order methods. More precisely, it has a guaranteed running time of

O(d(n+\kappa_{{}_{\bm{H}}})\log\frac{1}{\epsilon})
, where
\kappa_{{}_{\bm{H}}}
is a new condition number that can be dramatically smaller than
\tilde{\kappa}
, especially when the spectrum of
\bm{C}
decays rapidly. When
d\ll n
, the authors in
[
29
]
combine sub-sampled Newton methods with the mini-batch
SVRG
to obtain some further improvements.

1.2
Contributions

Recently, the work
[
3
]
shows that under some mild
algorithmic assumptions, and if the dimension is sufficiently large, the iteration complexity of second-order methods for
smooth
finite-sum problems composed of quadratics is no better than first-order methods.
Therefore, it is natural to ask whether one can develop a second-order method for solving the elastic net problem which has improved practical performance but still enjoys a strong
worst-case
time complexity like the stochastic first-order methods do?
It should be emphasized that due to the non-smooth objective, achieving this goal is much more challenging than for ridge regression. The preconditioning approach in
[
13
]
is not applicable, and the current theoretical results for second-order methods are not likely to offer the desired running time.

In this paper, we provide a positive answer to this question. Our main contribution is the design and analysis of a simple second-order method for the elastic net problem which has a strong theoretical time complexity and superior practical performance.
The convergence bound adapts to the problem structure and is governed by the spectrum and a
statistical measure
of the data matrix. These quantities often yield significantly stronger time complexity guarantees for practical datasets than those of stochastic first-order methods (see Table
1
). To achieve this, we first leverage recent advances in randomized low-rank approximation to generate a simple, one-shot approximation of the Hessian matrix. We then exploit the composite and finite-sum structure of the problem to develop a variant of the
ProxSVRG
method that builds upon Nesterov’s momentum acceleration and inexact computations of
scaled proximal
operators, which may be of independent interest. We provide a simple convergence proof based on an explicit Lyapunov function, thus avoiding the use of sophisticated
stochastic estimate sequences
.

In the paper, we shall frequently use the notions of strong convexity and smoothness in the
\bm{H}
-norm, introduced in the next two assumptions.

Assumption 1
.

The function
h\left(\bm{x}\right)
is lower-semicontinous and convex and
\mathrm{dom}\,h:=\{\bm{x}\in\mathbbmss{R}^{d}\,|\,h\left(\bm{x}\right)<\infty\}
, is closed. Each function

is

-smooth w.r.t the
\bm{H}
-norm, i.e, there exists a positive constant
L_{i}
such that

\displaystyle\left\|{\nabla f_{i}\left(\bm{x}\right)-\nabla f_{i}\left(\bm{y}\right)}\right\|_{\bm{H}^{-1}}\leq L_{i}\left\|{\bm{x}-\bm{y}}\right\|_{\bm{H}},\,\,\forall\bm{x},\bm{y}\in\mathbbmss{R}^{d}.

Assumption
1
implies that
\nabla f
is

\displaystyle\left\|{\nabla f\left(\bm{x}\right)-\nabla f\left(\bm{y}\right)}\right\|_{\bm{H}^{-1}}\leq L\left\|{\bm{x}-\bm{y}}\right\|_{\bm{H}}

for some
L\leq L_{\mathrm{avg}}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}L_{i}
. As a consequence, we have the following bound:

\displaystyle f\left(\bm{y}\right)\leq f\left(\bm{x}\right)+\left\langle\nabla f\left(\bm{x}\right),\bm{y}-\bm{x}\right\rangle+\frac{L_{\mathrm{avg}}}{2}\left\|{\bm{y}-\bm{x}}\right\|_{\bm{H}}^{2}.

Assumption 2
.

The function
f\left(\bm{x}\right)
is
\mu
-strongly convex w.r.t the
\bm{H}
-norm, i.e, there exists a positive constant
\mu
such that

\displaystyle f\left(\lambda\bm{x}+\left(1-\lambda\right)\bm{y}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\lambda f\left(\bm{x}\right)+\left(1+\lambda\right)f\left(\bm{y}\right)-\frac{\mu\lambda\left(1-\lambda\right)}{2}\left\|{\bm{x}-\bm{y}}\right\|_{\bm{H}}^{2}

holds for all
x,y\in\mathbbmss{R}^{n}
and
\lambda\in\left[0,1\right]
.

Assumption
2
is equivalent to the requirement that

\displaystyle f\left(\bm{y}\right)\geq f\left(\bm{x}\right)+\left\langle\nabla f\left(\bm{x}\right),\bm{y}-\bm{x}\right\rangle+\frac{\mu}{2}\left\|{\bm{y}-\bm{x}}\right\|_{\bm{H}}^{2},

holds for all
\bm{x},\bm{y}\in\mathbbmss{R}^{n}
. We will use both of these definitions of strong convexity in our proofs.

At the core of our method is the concept of

, defined as follows:

Definition 1

(Scaled Proximal Mapping)
.

For a convex function
h

\displaystyle{\prox}_{h}^{\bm{H}}\left(\bm{y}\right)=\argmin_{\bm{x}\in\mathbbmss{R}^{d}}\big\{h\left(\bm{x}\right)+\frac{1}{2}\left\|{\bm{x}-\bm{y}}\right\|_{\bm{H}}^{2}\big\}.

(3)

The scaled proximal mappings generalize the conventional ones:

\displaystyle{\prox}_{h}\left(\bm{y}\right)=\argmin_{\bm{x}\in\mathbbmss{R}^{d}}\big\{h\left(\bm{x}\right)+\frac{1}{2}\left\|{\bm{x}-\bm{y}}\right\|_{2}^{2}\big\}.

(4)

Fortunately, scaled proximal mappings do share some key properties with the conventional ones. We collect a few of them in the following result:

Building Block 1: Randomized Low-Rank Approximation

The computational cost of many Newton-type methods is dominated by the time required to compute the update direction
\bm{d}=\bm{H}^{-1}\bm{g}
for some vector
\bm{g}\in\mathbbmss{R}^{d}
and approximate Hessian
\bm{H}

O\left(nd^{2}\right)
flops, which is prohibitive for large-scale data sets. A natural way to reduce this cost is to use truncated SVD. However, standard deterministic methods such as the power method and the Lanzcos method have run times that scale inversely with the gap between the eigenvalues of the input matrix. This gap can be arbitrarily small for practical data sets, thereby preventing us from obtaining the desired time complexity. In contrast, randomized sketching schemes usually admit gap-free run times
[
15
]
. However, unlike other methods, the block Lanczos method, detailed in Algorithm
1
, admits both fast run times and
strong guarantees on the errors between the true and the computed approximate singular vectors. This property turns out to be critical for deriving bounds on the condition number of the elastic net.

3.2
Bounding the Condition Number

We now turn our attention to studying how the approximate Hessian affects the relevant condition number of the elastic net problem.
We first introduce a condition number that usually determines the iteration complexity of stochastic first-order methods under non-uniform sampling.

Definition 3
.

The average condition number of (
1.1
) is

\displaystyle\kappa_{{}_{\bm{H}}}=\frac{L_{\mathrm{avg}}}{\mu}=\frac{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}L_{i}}{\mu}.

For the elastic net problem (
1
), the smooth part of the objective is the ridge loss

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\underbrace{\frac{1}{2}\left(\bm{a}_{i}^{\top}\bm{x}-\bm{b}_{i}\right)^{2}+\gamma_{2}\left\|{\bm{x}}\right\|_{2}^{2}}_{f_{i}(\bm{x})}.

Since we define smoothness and strong convexity of
f_{i}(\bm{x})
in the
\bm{H}
-norm, the relevant constants are

\displaystyle L_{i}

\displaystyle=\|\bm{H}^{-1}\big(\bm{a}_{i}\bm{a}_{i}^{\top}+\gamma_{2}\mathbf{I}\big)\|_{2}

\displaystyle\mu

\displaystyle=\lambda_{d}\big(\bm{H}^{-1/2}\left(\bm{C}+\gamma_{2}\mathbf{I}\right)\bm{H}^{-1/2}\big).

For comparison, we also define the conventional condition number
\tilde{\kappa}
, which characterizes the smoothness and strong convexity of
f_{i}(\bm{x})
in the Euclidean norm. In this case
\tilde{\kappa}=\sum_{i}L_{i}/(n\mu)
, where

\displaystyle L_{i}=\|\bm{a}_{i}\bm{a}_{i}^{\top}+\gamma_{2}\mathbf{I}\|^{2}_{2}\quad\mbox{and}\quad\mu=\lambda_{d}\left(\bm{C}+\gamma_{2}\mathbf{I}\right).

It will become apparent that
\kappa_{{}_{\bm{H}}}
can be expressed in terms of a statistical measure of the ridge loss and that it may be significantly smaller than
\tilde{\kappa}
.
We start by introducing a statistical measure that has been widely used in the analysis of ridge regression (see, e.g.,
[
16
]
and the references therein).

Definition 4

(Effective Dimension)
.

For a positive constant
\lambda
, the effective dimension of
\bm{C}
is defined as

\displaystyle\mathsf{d}_{\lambda}=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\frac{\lambda_{i}}{\lambda_{i}+\lambda}.

The effective dimension generalizes the ordinary dimension and satisfies
\mathsf{d}_{\lambda}\leq d
with equality if and only if
\lambda=0
. It is typical that when
\bm{C}
has a rapidly decaying spectrum, most of the
\lambda_{i}

\lambda
, and hence
\mathsf{d}_{\lambda}
can be much smaller than
d
.

The following lemma bounds the eigenvalues of the matrix
\bm{H}^{-1/2}\left(\bm{C}+\gamma_{2}\mathbf{I}\right)\bm{H}^{-1/2}
, which can be seen as the effective Hessian matrix.

13
]

Since
\tilde{\kappa}=(\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}+d\gamma_{2})/\gamma_{2}
,

\displaystyle\frac{\sum_{i\leq r}\lambda_{i}+\sum_{i>r}\lambda_{i}}{r\lambda_{r}+\sum_{i>r}\lambda_{i}},

compared to

decays rapidly, then the terms

are negligible and the ratio is approximately
\sum_{i\leq r}\lambda_{i}/(r\lambda_{r})
. If the first eigenvalues are much larger than

, this ratio will be large. For example, for the australian data set

and

This indicates that it is possible to improve the iteration complexity of stochastic first-order methods if one can capitalize on the notions of strong convexity and smoothness w.r.t the
\bm{H}
-norm in the optimization algorithm. Of course, this is only meaningful if there is an efficient way to inject curvature information into the optimization process without significantly increasing the computational cost. In the smooth case, i.e.,
\gamma_{1}=0
, this task can be done by a preconditioning step
[
13
]
. However, this approach is not applicable for the elastic net, and we need to make use of another building block.

To motivate our algorithm, we first recall the

For the

\displaystyle\bm{v}_{k}=\left(\nabla f_{i_{k}}\left(\bm{x}_{k}\right)-\nabla f_{i_{k}}\left(\tilde{\bm{x}}_{s}\right)\right)/\left(np_{i_{k}}\right)+\nabla f\left(\tilde{\bm{x}}_{s}\right)

Since we are provided with an approximate Hessian matrix

\bm{v}_{k}

This suggests to use large mini-batches, i.e., instead of using a single component function

\displaystyle\bm{v}_{k}=\frac{1}{b}\sum_{i_{k}\in\mathcal{B}_{k}}\big(\nabla f_{i_{k}}\left(\bm{x}_{k}\right)-\nabla f_{i_{k}}\left(\tilde{\bm{x}}_{s}\right)\big)/\left(np_{i_{k}}\right)+\nabla f\left(\tilde{\bm{x}}_{s}\right),

It is easy to verify that

5
Warm-Start

The overall time complexity of Algorithm
2
depends strongly on our ability to solve problem (
10
) in a reasonable computational time. If one naively starts the solver at a random point, it may take many iterations to meet the target precision. Thus, it is necessary to have a well-designed warm-start procedure for initializing the solver. Intuitively, the current iterate
\bm{x}_{k}
can be a reasonable starting point since the next iterate
\bm{x}_{k+1}
should not be too far away from
\bm{x}_{k}
.
However, in order to achieve a strong theoretical running time, we use a rather different scheme inspired by
[
19
]
. Let us first define the vector
\bm{u}_{k}=\bm{y}_{k}-\eta\bm{H}^{-1}\bm{v}_{k}
for
k\in\{0,1,\ldots,T-1\}
and the function

\displaystyle p\left(\bm{z},\bm{u}\right)=h\left(\bm{z}\right)+\frac{1}{2\eta}\left\|{\bm{z}-\bm{u}}\right\|_{\bm{H}}^{2}.

Then, the
k
th subproblem seeks for
\bm{x}_{k+1}
such that

\displaystyle p\left(\bm{x}_{k+1},\bm{u}_{k}\right)-p\left(\bm{x}_{k+1}^{\star},\bm{u}_{k}\right)\leq\epsilon_{k},

(13)

where
\bm{x}_{k+1}^{\star}
is the exact solution.
We consider the initialization policy

\displaystyle\bm{z}_{0}=\prox_{\gamma h}\big(\bm{x}_{k}-\frac{\gamma}{\eta}\bm{H}\left(\bm{x}_{k}-\bm{u}_{k-1}\right)\big),

(14)

which can be seen as one step of the proximal gradient method applied to
p\left(\bm{z},\bm{u}_{k-1}\right)
starting at the current
\bm{x}_{k}
with step size
\gamma
.

The following proposition characterizes the difference in objective realized by
\bm{z}_{0}
and
\bm{x}_{k+1}^{\star}
.

Proposition 2
.

Let
\bm{z}_{0}
be defined by (
14
) with
\gamma=\eta/\lambda_{1}\left(\bm{H}\right)
. Let
\kappa_{\mathrm{sub}}=\lambda_{1}\left(\bm{H}\right)/\lambda_{r}\left(\bm{H}\right)
be the condition number of the subproblems. Assume that the errors in solving the subproblems satisfy
\epsilon_{k}\leq\left(1-\rho\right)^{k}V_{0}
for all
k\in\{0,1,\ldots,T-1\}
. Then,

\displaystyle p\left(\bm{z}_{0},\bm{u}_{k}\right)-p\left(\bm{x}_{k+1}^{\star},\bm{u}_{k}\right)\leq\frac{\kappa_{\mathrm{sub}}}{1-\rho}\epsilon_{k}.

Proof.

See Appendix
E
.
∎

The proposition, together with (
13
), implies that it suffices to find
\bm{x}_{k+1}
such that

\displaystyle p\left(\bm{x}_{k+1},\bm{u}_{k}\right)-p\left(\bm{x}_{k+1}^{\star},\bm{u}_{k}\right)\leq\frac{1-\rho}{\kappa_{\mathrm{sub}}}\left(p\left(\bm{z}_{0},\bm{u}_{k}\right)-p\left(\bm{x}_{k+1}^{\star},\bm{u}_{k}\right)\right).

(15)

This is significant since one only needs to reduce the residual error by a constant factor independent of the target precision. Note also that the condition number
\kappa_{\mathrm{sub}}
is much smaller than
\kappa\approx\lambda_{1}(\bm{H})/(\lambda_{d}+\gamma_{2})
, and computing the gradient of the smooth part of
p\left(\bm{z},\bm{u}\right)
only takes time
O\left(rd\right)
instead of
O\left(nd\right)
as in the original problem. Those properties imply that the subproblems can be solved efficiently by iterative methods, where only a small (and known) constant number of iterations is needed. The next section develops the final details of convergence proof.

6
Global Time Complexity

We start with the time complexity of Algorithm
2
.
Let
\mathcal{T}\left(\alpha\right)
be the number of gradient evaluations that a subproblem solver takes to reduce the residual error by a factor
\alpha
. Then, by Proposition
2
, one can find an
\epsilon_{k}
-optimal solution to the
k
th subproblem by at most
\mathcal{T}\left(\kappa_{\mathrm{sub}}/\left(1-\rho\right)\right)
gradient evaluations, where one gradient evaluation is equivalent to
O\left(d\right)
flops.
Consider the same setting of Theorem
2
and suppose that the subproblems are initialized by (
14
). Then, the time complexity of Algorithm
2
is given by:

\displaystyle O\big(d\big(n+\kappa_{{}_{\bm{H}}}+\sqrt{\kappa_{{}_{\bm{H}}}}\,\mathcal{T}\big(\frac{\kappa_{\mathrm{sub}}}{1-\rho}\big)\big)\log\frac{1}{\epsilon}\big),

(16)

where the first summand is due to the full gradient evaluation at each outer loop; the second one comes from the fact that one needs
O(\sqrt{\kappa_{{}_{\bm{H}}}})
inner iterations, each of which uses a mini-batch of size
O(\sqrt{\kappa_{{}_{\bm{H}}}})
; and the third one is the result of
O(\sqrt{\kappa_{{}_{\bm{H}}}})
inner iterations, each of which solves a subproblem that needs

\mathcal{T}\left(\kappa_{\mathrm{sub}}/\left(1-\rho\right)\right)
gradient evaluations.
We can now put things together and state our main result.

Proposition 3
.

Suppose that the approximate Hessian matrix
\bm{H}
is given by (
6
) and that Algorithm
2
is invoked with
f\left(\bm{x}\right)=\frac{1}{2n}\left\|{\bm{A}\bm{x}-\bm{b}}\right\|_{2}^{2}+\frac{\gamma_{2}}{2}\left\|{\bm{x}}\right\|_{2}^{2}
and
h\left(\bm{x}\right)=\left\|{\bm{x}}\right\|_{1}
. Assume further that the subproblems are solved by the accelerated proximal gradient descent method
[
23
,
4
]
. Our method can find an
\epsilon
-optimal solution in time

\displaystyle O\big(d\left(n+\kappa_{{}_{\bm{H}}}\right)\log\frac{1}{\epsilon}\big).

Proof.

The task reduces to evaluating the term
\mathcal{T}\left(\kappa_{\mathrm{sub}}/\left(1-\rho\right)\right)
in (
16
). Recall that the iteration complexity of the accelerated proximal gradient descent method for minimizing the function
F(x)=f(x)+h(x)
, where
f
is a smooth and strongly convex function and
h
is a possibly non-smooth convex regularizer, initiliazed at
\bm{x}_{0}
is given by
\sqrt{\kappa}\log\frac{F(\bm{x}_{0})-F(\bm{x}^{\star})}{\epsilon}
, where
\kappa
is the condition number. By invoking the about result with
F(\bm{x})=p\left(\bm{x},\bm{u}_{k}\right)
,
\bm{x}^{\star}=\bm{x}_{k+1}^{\star}
,
\bm{x}_{0}=\bm{z}_{0}
,
\kappa=\kappa_{\mathrm{sub}}
, and
\epsilon
is the right-hand side of (
15
), it follows that the number of iterations for each subproblem can be bounded by

\displaystyle O\big(\sqrt{\kappa_{\mathrm{sub}}}\log\frac{\kappa_{\mathrm{sub}}}{1-\rho}\big).

(17)

In addition, each iteration takes time
O\left(rd\right)
to compute the gradient implying the time complexity

\displaystyle O\big(d\big(n+\kappa_{{}_{\bm{H}}}+r\sqrt{\kappa_{\mathrm{sub}}}\sqrt{\kappa_{{}_{\bm{H}}}}\log\frac{\kappa_{\mathrm{sub}}}{1-\rho}\big)\log\frac{1}{\epsilon}\big)

Finally, since
\kappa_{{}_{\bm{H}}}\gg\kappa_{\mathrm{sub}}
, the proof is complete.
∎

We can easily recognize that this time complexity has the same form as the stochastic first-order methods discussed in Section 1.2.1 with the condition number
\tilde{\kappa}
replaced by
\kappa_{{}_{\bm{H}}}

that

can be much smaller than
\tilde{\kappa}
, especially, when
\bm{C}
has a rapidly decaying spectrum. We emphasize that the expression in (

is required to set

8
Conclusions

We have proposed and analyzed a novel second-order method for solving the elastic net problem. By carefully exploiting the problem structure, we demonstrated that it is possible to deal with the non-smooth objective and to efficiently inject curvature information into the optimization process without (significantly) increasing the computational cost per iteration. The combination of second-order information, fast iterative solvers, and a well-designed warm-start procedure results in a significant improvement of the total runtime complexity over popular first-order methods. An interesting direction for future research would be to go beyond the quadratic loss. We believe that the techniques developed in this work can be extended to more general settings, especially when the smooth part of the objective function is self-concordant.

\displaystyle\E V_{k}

\displaystyle\leq 20\left(1-\tau\right)^{k}V_{0}+\frac{50}{9}\sum_{t=0}^{k-1}\left(1-\tau\right)^{k-t-1}\epsilon_{t}+\frac{2\tau}{9}\sum_{t=0}^{k-1}\left(1-\tau\right)^{k-t-1}e_{t}+\frac{2}{9}\tilde{e}_{s}

\displaystyle\hskip 14.22636pt+40\left(1-\tau\right)^{k}\left(\sum_{t=0}^{k}\sqrt{\epsilon_{t}}\left(1-\tau\right)^{-t/2-1}\right)^{2}.

(36)

With our choice of
\epsilon_{t}
, it follows that

\displaystyle\sum_{t=0}^{k}\sqrt{\epsilon_{t}}\left(1-\tau\right)^{-t/2-1}\leq\frac{1}{1-\tau}\sum_{t=0}^{k}\left(\sqrt{\frac{1-\rho}{1-\tau}}\,\right)^{t}\sqrt{V_{0}}\leq\frac{\left(\sqrt{\frac{1-\rho}{1-\tau}}\,\right)^{k+1}}{\sqrt{1-\rho}-\sqrt{1-\tau}}\sqrt{\frac{V_{0}}{1-\tau}}.

Thus, the last term on the right-hand side of (

\displaystyle\frac{40\left(1-\rho\right)}{\left(\sqrt{1-\rho}-\sqrt{1-\tau}\right)^{2}\left(1-\tau\right)^{2}}\left(1-\rho\right)^{k}V_{0}\leq\frac{160\left(1-\rho\right)}{\left(\tau-\rho\right)^{2}\left(1-\tau\right)^{2}}\left(1-\rho\right)^{k}V_{0},

where the last inequality follows since the function
\sqrt{1-x}+x/2
is decreasing on the interval
\left[0,1\right]
.
Similarly, for the second term in (
D
), we have

\displaystyle\sum_{t=0}^{k-1}\left(1-\tau\right)^{k-t-1}\epsilon_{t}\leq\left(1-\tau\right)^{k-1}\sum_{t=0}^{k-1}\left(\frac{1-\rho}{1-\tau}\right)^{t}V_{0}\leq\frac{\left(1-\rho\right)^{k}V_{0}}{\tau-\rho}.

Thus, we can further bound
\E V_{k}
as

\displaystyle\E V_{k}

\displaystyle\leq c_{2}\left(1-\rho\right)^{k}V_{0}+\frac{2\tau}{9}\sum_{t=0}^{k-1}\left(1-\rho\right)^{k-t-1}e_{t}+\frac{2}{9}\tilde{e}_{s},

where
c_{2}=20+\frac{160\left(1-\rho\right)}{\left(\tau-\rho\right)^{2}\left(1-\tau\right)^{2}}+\frac{50}{9\left(\tau-\rho\right)}
and we have also used the fact that
1-\tau<1-\rho
.

Now, if we let
\delta_{k}
be the right-hand side of the above inequality, then it holds that

\displaystyle\delta_{k}

\displaystyle=c_{2}\left(1-\rho\right)^{k}V_{0}+\frac{2\tau}{9}\sum_{t=0}^{k-1}\left(1-\rho\right)^{k-t-1}e_{t}+\frac{2}{9}\tilde{e}_{s}

\displaystyle=\left(1-\rho\right)\left(c_{2}\left(1-\rho\right)^{k-1}V_{0}+\frac{2\tau}{9}\sum_{t=0}^{k-2}\left(1-\rho\right)^{k-t-2}e_{t}+\frac{2}{9}\tilde{e}_{s}\right)+\frac{2\tau}{9}e_{k-1}+\frac{2\rho}{9}\tilde{e}_{s}

\displaystyle=\left(1-\rho\right)\delta_{k-1}+\frac{2\tau}{9}\delta_{k-1}+\frac{2\rho}{9}V_{0}

\displaystyle=\left(1-\frac{7\rho}{9}\right)\delta_{k-1}+\frac{2\rho}{9}V_{0}

\displaystyle\leq\left(1-\frac{3\rho}{4}\right)\delta_{k-1}+\frac{2\rho}{9}V_{0},

where the third equality follows since by definition,
e_{k-1}\leq\E V_{k-1}\leq\delta_{k-1}
, and since
\tilde{e}_{s}=e_{0}\leq V_{0}
, and the last inequality follows since
\rho=0.9\tau

By applying the above inequality recursively, we obtain

\displaystyle\E V_{T}\leq\delta_{T}

\displaystyle\leq\left(1-\frac{3\rho}{4}\right)^{T}\delta_{0}+\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\left(1-\frac{3\rho}{4}\right)^{T-t-1}\frac{2\rho}{9}V_{0},

\displaystyle\leq\left(c_{3}\left(1-\frac{3\rho}{4}\right)^{T}+\frac{8}{27}\right)V_{0},

where
c_{3}=c_{2}+2/9

\displaystyle\E V_{T}\leq\delta_{T}\leq\left(\frac{1}{27}+\frac{8}{27}\right)V_{0}=\frac{1}{3}V_{0}.

Finally, using the definition of

\displaystyle\E\left\{{F\left(\tilde{\bm{x}}_{s+1}\right)-F\left(\bm{x}^{\star}\right)}\right\}\leq\E V_{T}\leq\frac{1}{3}V_{0}\leq\frac{2}{3}\left(F\left(\tilde{\bm{x}}_{s}\right)-F\left(\bm{x}^{\star}\right)\right),

completing the proof.

Appendix E
Proof of Proposition

Recall that

\displaystyle\bm{z}_{0}={\prox}_{\gamma h}\left(\bm{x}_{k}-\frac{\gamma}{\eta}\bm{H}\left(\bm{x}_{k}-\bm{u}_{k-1}\right)\right),

which can be seen as one step of the proximal gradient method applied to

starting at the current
\bm{x}_{k}

\displaystyle\bm{u}_{k}

\displaystyle=\bm{y}_{k}-\eta\bm{H}^{-1}\bm{v}_{k}

\displaystyle\triangleq h\left(\bm{z}\right)+\frac{1}{2\eta}\left\|{\bm{z}-\bm{u}}\right\|_{\bm{H}}^{2}.

By the optimality condition of
\bm{z}_{0}
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\gamma}\left[-\bm{z}_{0}+\bm{x}_{k}-\frac{\gamma}{\eta}\bm{H}\left(\bm{x}_{k}-\bm{u}_{k-1}\right)\right]\in\partial h\left(\bm{z}_{0}\right).

\displaystyle\bm{\zeta}_{k}\triangleq\frac{1}{\gamma}\left(\bm{x}_{k}-\bm{z}_{0}\right)+\frac{1}{\eta}\bm{H}\left(\bm{z}_{0}-\bm{x}_{k}\right)\in\partial p\left(\bm{z}_{0},\bm{u}_{k-1}\right).

We see that
\bm{\zeta}_{k}
is independent of the second argument of
p\left(\bm{z}_{0},\cdot\right)

\bm{\zeta}_{k}\in\partial p\left(\bm{z}_{0},\bm{u}_{k}\right)
.
Since
p\left(\bm{z},\cdot\right)
is strongly convex in
\bm{z}
with parameter

\lambda_{r}\left(\bm{H}\right)/\eta
and
\bm{\zeta}_{k}\in\partial p\left(\bm{z}_{0},\bm{u}_{k}\right)
, it holds that

\displaystyle p\left(\bm{x}_{k+1}^{\star},\bm{u}_{k}\right)

\displaystyle\geq p\left(\bm{z}_{0},\bm{u}_{k}\right)+\left\langle\bm{\zeta}_{k},\bm{x}_{k+1}^{\star}-\bm{z}_{0}\right\rangle+\frac{\lambda_{r}\left(\bm{H}\right)}{2\eta}\left\|{\bm{x}_{k+1}^{\star}-\bm{z}_{0}}\right\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\geq p\left(\bm{z}_{0},\bm{u}_{k}\right)-\frac{\eta}{2\lambda_{r}\left(\bm{H}\right)}\left\|{\bm{\zeta}_{k}}\right\|^{2},

(38)

where the last inequality follows from Young’s inequality. We next bound

\displaystyle\left\|{\bm{\zeta}_{k}}\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\left\|{\frac{1}{\gamma}\left(\bm{x}_{k}-\bm{z}_{0}\right)+\frac{1}{\eta}\bm{H}\left(\bm{z}_{0}-\bm{x}_{k}\right)}\right\|_{2}^{2}

where the last step follows from the definition of

\displaystyle\left\langle\bm{x}_{k}-\bm{z}_{0},\bm{H}\left(\bm{x}_{k}-\bm{z}_{0}\right)\right\rangle=\left(\bm{x}_{k}-\bm{z}_{0}\right)^{\top}\bm{H}\bm{H}^{-1}\bm{H}\left(\bm{x}_{k}-\bm{z}_{0}\right)\geq\frac{1}{\lambda_{1}\left(\bm{H}\right)}\left\|{\bm{H}\left(\bm{x}_{k}-\bm{z}_{0}\right)}\right\|_{2}^{2}.

Thus, combining (
E
) and (
E
) yields

\displaystyle p\left(\bm{z}_{0},\bm{u}_{k}\right)-p\left(\bm{x}_{k+1}^{\star},\bm{u}_{k}\right)\leq\frac{\eta}{2\gamma^{2}\lambda_{r}\left(\bm{H}\right)}\left\|{\bm{x}_{k}-\bm{z}_{0}}\right\|_{2}^{2}

Note that the quantity
\frac{1}{\gamma}\left(\bm{x}_{k}-\bm{z}_{0}\right)
is nothing but the gradient mapping of

[
23

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\gamma}\left\|{\bm{x}_{k}-\bm{z}_{0}}\right\|_{2}^{2}\leq p\left(\bm{x}_{k},\bm{u}_{k-1}\right)-p\left(\bm{z}_{0},\bm{u}_{k-1}\right)\leq p\left(\bm{x}_{k},\bm{u}_{k-1}\right)-p\left(\bm{x}_{k}^{\star},\bm{u}_{k-1}\right)\leq\epsilon_{k-1}.

\displaystyle p\left(\bm{z}_{0},\bm{u}_{k}\right)-p\left(\bm{x}_{k+1}^{\star},\bm{u}_{k}\right)\leq\kappa_{\mathrm{sub}}\epsilon_{k-1}=\frac{\kappa_{\mathrm{sub}}}{1-\rho}\epsilon_{k},

as desired.

Lemma 5

(Bounding Variance)
.

Assume that the indices in the mini-batch
\mathcal{B}_{k}
are sampled independently from
\{1,\ldots,n\}
with probabilities
P_{i}=\frac{L_{i}}{nL_{\mathrm{avg}}}
, then conditioned on
\bm{x}_{k}
and
\bm{y}_{k}
, it holds that

\displaystyle\E{\left\|{\Delta_{k}}\right\|_{\bm{H}^{-1}}^{2}}\leq\frac{2L_{\mathrm{avg}}^{2}}{b}\left\|{\bm{x}_{k}-\bm{y}_{k}}\right\|_{\bm{H}}^{2}+\frac{8L_{\mathrm{avg}}}{b}\left(F\left(\bm{x}_{k}\right)-F\left(\bm{x}^{\star}\right)+F\left(\tilde{\bm{x}}_{s}\right)-F\left(\bm{x}^{\star}\right)\right).

Proof.

We follow the original proofs in
[
31
,
24
]
with a few modifications to take the Mahalanobis norm into account. For any
i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
, consider the function
\phi_{i}
defined by

\displaystyle\phi_{i}\left(\bm{x}\right)=f_{i}\left(\bm{x}\right)-f_{i}\left(\bm{x}^{\star}\right)-\left\langle\nabla f_{i}\left(\bm{x}^{\star}\right),\bm{x}-\bm{x}^{\star}\right\rangle.

Then,
\phi_{i}\left(\bm{x}^{\star}\right)=\min_{\bm{x}}\phi_{i}\left(\bm{x}\right)
since
\nabla\phi_{i}\left(\bm{x}^{\star}\right)=0
and
\phi_{i}
is convex.
It can be checked that
\nabla\phi_{i}
is
L_{i}
-Lipschitz, we thus have

\displaystyle 0=\phi_{i}\left(\bm{x}^{\star}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\min_{\eta}\phi_{i}\left(\bm{x}-\eta\bm{H}^{-1}\nabla\phi_{i}\left(\bm{x}\right)\right)

\displaystyle\leq\min_{\eta}\phi_{i}\left(\bm{x}\right)-\eta\left\|{\nabla\phi_{i}\left(\bm{x}\right)}\right\|_{\bm{H}^{-1}}^{2}+\frac{\eta^{2}L_{i}}{2}\left\|{\nabla\phi_{i}\left(\bm{x}\right)}\right\|_{\bm{H}^{-1}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\phi_{i}\left(\bm{x}\right)-\frac{1}{2L_{i}}\left\|{\nabla\phi_{i}\left(\bm{x}\right)}\right\|_{\bm{H}^{-1}}^{2},

which is equivalent to

\displaystyle\left\|{\nabla f_{i}\left(\bm{x}\right)-\nabla f_{i}\left(\bm{x}^{\star}\right)}\right\|_{\bm{H}^{-1}}^{2}\leq 2L_{i}\left(f_{i}\left(\bm{x}\right)-f_{i}\left(\bm{x}^{\star}\right)-\left\langle\nabla f_{i}\left(\bm{x}^{\star}\right),\bm{x}-\bm{x}^{\star}\right\rangle\right).

Multiplying both sides of the previous inequality by
\frac{L_{\mathrm{avg}}}{L_{i}}
and averaging from
1,\ldots,n
gives

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{L_{\mathrm{avg}}}{L_{i}}\left\|{\nabla f_{i}\left(\bm{x}\right)-\nabla f_{i}\left(\bm{x}^{\star}\right)}\right\|_{\bm{H}^{-1}}^{2}\leq 2L_{\mathrm{avg}}\left(f\left(\bm{x}\right)-f\left(\bm{x}^{\star}\right)\right)-\left\langle\nabla f\left(\bm{x}^{\star}\right),\bm{x}-\bm{x}^{\star}\right\rangle.

Since
F\left(\bm{x}\right)=f\left(\bm{x}\right)+h\left(\bm{x}\right)
, by the optimality of
\bm{x}^{\star}
,
-\nabla f\left(\bm{x}^{\star}\right)\in\partial h\left(\bm{x}^{\star}\right)
. Therefore, it follows from the convexity of
h
that

-\left\langle\nabla f\left(\bm{x}^{\star}\right),\bm{x}-\bm{x}^{\star}\right\rangle\leq h\left(\bm{x}\right)-h\left(\bm{x}^{\star}\right),

which implies that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{L_{\mathrm{avg}}}{L_{i}}\left\|{\nabla f_{i}\left(\bm{x}\right)-\nabla f_{i}\left(\bm{x}^{\star}\right)}\right\|_{\bm{H}^{-1}}^{2}\leq 2L_{\mathrm{avg}}\left(F\left(\bm{x}\right)-F\left(\bm{x}^{\star}\right)\right).

(44)

We are now ready to bound
\E{\left\|{\Delta_{k}}\right\|_{\bm{H}^{-1}}^{2}}
, we have

\displaystyle\E\left\|{\bm{v}_{k}-\nabla f\left(\bm{y}_{k}\right)}\right\|_{\bm{H}^{-1}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{b^{2}}\E\bigg\{\sum_{i\in\mathcal{B}_{k}}\left\|{\frac{\nabla f_{i}\left(\bm{y}_{k}\right)-\nabla f_{i}\left(\tilde{\bm{x}}_{s}\right)}{L_{i}/L_{\mathrm{avg}}}-\left(\nabla f\left(\bm{y}_{k}\right)-\nabla f\left(\tilde{\bm{x}}_{s}\right)\right)}\right\|_{\bm{H}^{-1}}^{2}\bigg\}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{L_{\mathrm{avg}}}{b}\E\frac{1}{L_{i}}\left\|{\nabla f_{i}\left(\bm{y}_{k}\right)-\nabla f_{i}\left(\tilde{\bm{x}}_{s}\right)}\right\|_{\bm{H}^{-1}}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2L_{\mathrm{avg}}}{b}\E\frac{1}{L_{i}}\left\|{\nabla f_{i}\left(\bm{y}_{k}\right)-\nabla f_{i}\left(\bm{x}_{k}\right)}\right\|_{\bm{H}^{-1}}^{2}

\displaystyle\hskip 14.22636pt+\frac{4L_{\mathrm{avg}}}{b}\E\frac{1}{L_{i}}\left\|{\nabla f_{i}\left(\bm{x}_{k}\right)-\nabla f_{i}\left(\bm{x}^{\star}\right)}\right\|_{\bm{H}^{-1}}^{2}

\displaystyle\hskip 14.22636pt+\frac{4L_{\mathrm{avg}}}{b}\E\frac{1}{L_{i}}\left\|{\nabla f_{i}\left(\tilde{\bm{x}}_{s}\right)-\nabla f_{i}\left(\bm{x}^{\star}\right)}\right\|_{\bm{H}^{-1}}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2L_{\mathrm{avg}}^{2}}{b}\left\|{\bm{x}_{k}-\bm{y}_{k}}\right\|_{\bm{H}}^{2}+\frac{8L_{\mathrm{avg}}}{b}\left(F\left(\bm{x}_{k}\right)-F\left(\bm{x}^{\star}\right)+F\left(\tilde{\bm{x}}_{s}\right)-F\left(\bm{x}^{\star}\right)\right),

where the first inequality follows from the fact that
\E\left\|{X-\E X}\right\|^{2}\leq\E\left\|{X}\right\|^{2}
; we have used the inequality
\left\|{\bm{x}+\bm{y}}\right\|_{\bm{H}^{-1}}^{2}\leq 2\left\|{\bm{x}}\right\|_{\bm{H}^{-1}}^{2}+2\left\|{\bm{x}}\right\|_{\bm{H}^{-1}}^{2}
to derive the second inequality; and the last step follows by evaluating the expectations, and using Assumption
1
and (
44
). This completes the proof of Lemma
5
.
∎

The following definition of
\epsilon
-subgradients is very useful for analyzing how ineaxact proximal evaluations affect the convergence of the algorith.

Definition 5

(
\epsilon
-Subgradients
[
5
]
)
.

Given a convex function
f:\mathbbmss{R}^{d}\to\mathbbmss{R}
and a positive constant
\epsilon
, we say that a vector
\bm{\zeta}\in\mathbbmss{R}^{d}
is an
\epsilon
-subgradient of
f
at
\bm{x}\in\mathbbmss{R}^{d}
if

\displaystyle f\left(\bm{z}\right)\geq f\left(\bm{x}\right)+\left\langle\bm{\zeta},\bm{z}-\bm{x}\right\rangle-\epsilon,\quad\forall\bm{z}\in\mathbbmss{R}^{d}.

The set of all
\epsilon
-subgradients of
f
at
\bm{x}
is called the
\epsilon
-subdifferential of
f
at
\bm{x}
, and is denoted by
\partial_{\epsilon}f\left(\bm{x}\right)
.

The following lemma characterizes the property of the
\epsilon
-differential of the function
h
, where its proof for the case of the Euclidean norm can be found in
[
27
, Lemma 2]
. We provide the proof here for completeness.

Lemma 6
.

If
\bm{x}^{+}
is an
\epsilon
-optimal solution to problem (
40
) in the sense of (
5
), then there exists a vector
\bm{\xi}\in\mathbbmss{R}^{d}
such that
\left\|{\bm{\xi}}\right\|_{\bm{H}^{-1}}\leq\sqrt{2\eta\epsilon}
and

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{\eta}\left(\bm{H}\left(\bm{x}^{+}-\bm{y}\right)+\eta\bm{v}+\bm{\xi}\right)\in\partial_{\epsilon}h\left(\bm{x}^{+}\right).

Proof.

We start by noting that
\mathbf{0}\in\partial_{\epsilon}q\left(\bm{x}^{+}\right)
when
\bm{x}^{+}
is an
\epsilon
-optimal solution to problem (
40
). Consider the function
f\left(\bm{x}^{+}\right)=\frac{1}{2\eta}\left\|{\bm{x}^{+}-\bm{t}}\right\|_{\bm{H}}^{2}
, then it can be verified that

\displaystyle\partial_{\epsilon}f\left(\bm{x}^{+}\right)

\displaystyle=\big\{\bm{\zeta}\in\mathbbmss{R}^{d}\,\big|\,\frac{1}{2\eta}\left\|{\bm{H}\left(\bm{x}^{+}-\bm{t}\right)-\eta\bm{\zeta}}\right\|_{\bm{H}^{-1}}^{2}\leq\epsilon\}

\displaystyle=\big\{\bm{\zeta}\in\mathbbmss{R}^{d},\bm{\zeta}=\frac{1}{\eta}\bm{H}\left(\bm{x}^{+}-\bm{t}\right)+\frac{1}{\eta}\bm{\xi}\,\big|\,\frac{1}{2\eta}\left\|{\bm{\xi}}\right\|_{\bm{H}^{-1}}^{2}\leq\epsilon\}.

We have for convex functions
f_{1},f_{2}
that
\partial_{\epsilon}\left(f_{1}+f_{2}\right)\left(\bm{x}\right)\subset\partial_{\epsilon}f_{1}\left(\bm{x}\right)+\partial_{\epsilon}f_{2}\left(\bm{x}\right)

[
5
]
. Therefore, if we let
f_{1}=f
,
f_{2}=h
, and
\bm{t}=\bm{y}-\eta\bm{H}^{-1}\bm{v}
, then
\mathbf{0}\in\partial_{\epsilon}q\left(\bm{x}^{+}\right)
. Since
\partial_{\epsilon}q\left(\bm{x}^{+}\right)\subset\partial_{\epsilon}f\left(\bm{x}^{+}\right)+\partial_{\epsilon}h\left(\bm{x}^{+}\right)
, it follows that
\mathbf{0}
must be a sum of an element of
\partial_{\epsilon}f\left(\bm{x}^{+}\right)
and an element of
\partial_{\epsilon}h\left(\bm{x}^{+}\right)
. Thus, there is a vector
\bm{\xi}\in\mathbbmss{R}^{d}
such that

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{\eta}\bm{H}\left(\bm{x}^{+}-\bm{t}\right)-\frac{1}{\eta}\bm{\xi}\in\partial_{\epsilon}h\left(\bm{x}^{+}\right)\quad\text{with}\quad\left\|{\bm{\xi}}\right\|_{\bm{H}^{-1}}\leq\sqrt{2\eta\epsilon},

completing the proof.
∎
