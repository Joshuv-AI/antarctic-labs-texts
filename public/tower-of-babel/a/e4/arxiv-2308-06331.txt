Interaction energies in paranematic colloids
Dmitry Golovaty, Jamie Taylor, Raghavendra Venkatraman, Arghir Zarnescu

Abstract
We consider a system of colloidal particles embedded in a paranematic—an isotropic phase of a nematogenic medium above the temperature of the nematic-to-isotropic transition. In this state, the nematic order is induced by the boundary conditions in a narrow band around each particle and it decays exponentially in the bulk. We develop rigorous asymptotics of the linearization of the appropriate variational model that allow us to describe weak far-field interactions between the colloidal particles in two dimensional paranematic suspensions. We demonstrate analytically that decay rates of solutions to the full nonlinear and linear problems are similar and verify numerically that the interactions between the particles in these problems have similar dependence on the distance between the particles. Finally, we perform Monte-Carlo simulations for a system of colloidal particles in a paranematic and describe the statistical properties of this system.

As mentioned before, the proof of this lemma proceeds similarly to that of Lemma
2.2
. Without loss of generality, fix
j=1.

By the definition of
H^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}(\partial\Omega_{\varepsilon})
(see Appendix
A
) we have

\displaystyle\biggl\|\frac{\partial Z_{n}^{(1)}}{\partial\nu}\biggr\|_{H^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}(\partial\Omega_{\varepsilon})}=\sup_{\phi\in H^{1}(\Omega_{\varepsilon}):\|\phi\|_{H^{1}(\Omega_{\varepsilon})}\leqslant 1}\biggl[\int_{\Omega_{\varepsilon}}\nabla Z_{n}^{(1)}\cdot\nabla\phi+\Delta Z_{n}^{(1)}\phi\,dx\biggr]

(3.8)

\displaystyle=\sup_{\phi\in H^{1}(\Omega_{\varepsilon}):\|\phi\|_{H^{1}(\Omega_{\varepsilon})}\leqslant 1}\biggl[\int_{\Omega_{\varepsilon}}\nabla Z_{n}^{(1)}\cdot\nabla\phi+Z_{n}^{(1)}\phi\,dx\biggr]

\displaystyle\leqslant\|Z_{n}^{(1)}\|_{H^{1}(\Omega_{\varepsilon})}=\sqrt{2F_{\varepsilon}(Z_{n}^{(1)})},

and therefore, the desired estimate follows by constructing a competitor to the variational problem of minimizing the energy
F_{\varepsilon}
subject to the boundary conditions of
Z_{n}^{(1)}.
Our competitor construction and estimation of its energy proceeds as before.

Construction of competitor
\zeta
for
Z_{n}^{(1)}
and estimating the energy of the
\zeta
:
Our competitor
\zeta\in H^{1}(\Omega_{\varepsilon})
must be constructed satisfying the boundary conditions for
\zeta=Z_{n}^{(1)}
on
\partial\Omega_{\varepsilon},
so that
\zeta=0
on
\partial B_{1}
and
\zeta=-\Psi_{n}^{(1)}
on
\partial B_{2}.
We let
\eta:(0,\infty)\to[0,1]
be a
C^{1}
function that satisfies
\eta(t)\equiv 1
for
t\in\bigl[\nicefrac{{1}}{{\varepsilon^{2}}},\nicefrac{{1}}{{\varepsilon^{2}}}+\nicefrac{{b}}{{2}}\bigr],

\eta(t)\equiv 0
when
t\geqslant\nicefrac{{1}}{{\varepsilon^{2}}}+b,
and
|\eta^{\prime}|\leqslant\frac{2}{b},
and set

\displaystyle\zeta(x):=-\Psi_{n}^{(1)}(x)\eta(|x-a_{2}|).

Then

\displaystyle\nabla\zeta=-\eta(|x-a_{2}|)\nabla\Psi_{n}^{(1)}(x)-\Psi_{n}^{(1)}(x)\eta^{\prime}(|x-a_{2}|)\frac{x-a_{2}}{|x-a_{2}|},

so that, pointwise, we have the bound

\displaystyle|\nabla\zeta(x)|\leqslant|\nabla\Psi_{n}^{(1)}(x)|+\frac{2}{b}|\Psi_{n}^{(1)}(x)|,

with support in the set
\nicefrac{{1}}{{\varepsilon^{2}}}\leqslant|x-a_{2}|\leqslant\nicefrac{{1}}{{\varepsilon^{2}}}+b.
Then, the energy of
\zeta
is easily calculated:

\displaystyle F_{\varepsilon}(\zeta)\leqslant C\int_{\nicefrac{{1}}{{\varepsilon^{2}}}\leqslant|x-a_{2}|\leqslant\nicefrac{{1}}{{\varepsilon^{2}}}+b}\bigl(|\Psi_{n}^{(1)}|^{2}+|\nabla\Psi_{n}^{(1)}|^{2}\bigr)\,dx

(3.9)

\displaystyle=C\biggl|2\mathfrak{Re}\biggl[-\int_{|x-a_{2}|=\nicefrac{{1}}{{\varepsilon^{2}}}}\Psi_{n}^{(1)}(x)\overline{\frac{\partial\Psi_{n}^{(1)}(x)}{\partial\nu}}\,d\mathcal{H}^{1}+\int_{|x-a_{2}|=\nicefrac{{1}}{{\varepsilon^{2}}}+b}\Psi_{n}^{(1)}(x)\overline{\frac{\partial\Psi_{n}^{(1)}(x)}{\partial\nu}}\,d\mathcal{H}^{1}\biggr]\biggr|,

where we plugged in the PDE satisfied by
\Psi_{n}^{(1)}
and integrated by parts as before; the signs in front of the boundary integrals reflect our choice that the corresponding unit normals point towards
a_{2}.
Recalling that

\displaystyle\Psi_{n}^{(1)}(x)=\frac{K_{n}(|x-a_{1}|)}{K_{n}(\nicefrac{{1}}{{\varepsilon^{2}}})}e^{in\theta_{1}},

it is clear that

\displaystyle|\nabla\Psi_{n}^{(1)}(x)|\leqslant\frac{|K_{n}^{\prime}(|x-a_{1}|)|}{K_{n}(\nicefrac{{1}}{{\varepsilon^{2}}})}+\frac{|n|}{|x-a_{1}|}\frac{K_{n}(|x-a_{1}|)}{K_{n}(\nicefrac{{1}}{{\varepsilon^{2}}})},\quad\quad x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\overline{B}_{2}.

Estimating the first boundary integral in (
3.9
).
For this term, as before, we estimate
|\frac{\partial\Psi_{n}^{(1)}}{\partial\nu}|\leqslant|\nabla\Psi_{n}^{(1)}|,
and use the geometric observation that when
|x-a_{2}|=\nicefrac{{1}}{{\varepsilon^{2}}},
parametrizing
x=a_{2}+\nicefrac{{1}}{{\varepsilon^{2}}}(\cos\theta,\sin\theta),\theta\in(-\pi,\pi]
with
\theta=0
along the vertical, we have the lower bound

\displaystyle|x-a_{1}|\geqslant\nicefrac{{1}}{{\varepsilon^{2}}}+2b+\nicefrac{{1}}{{\varepsilon^{2}}}(1-\cos\theta).

As
K_{n}
and
-K_{n}^{\prime}
are both monotone decreasing functions, and moreover, since on the circle
|x-a_{2}|=\nicefrac{{1}}{{\varepsilon^{2}}},
we have
\,d\mathcal{H}^{1}=\nicefrac{{1}}{{\varepsilon^{2}}}\,d\theta
in the above parametrization, we find

\displaystyle\biggl|\int_{|x-a_{2}|=\nicefrac{{1}}{{\varepsilon^{2}}}}\Psi_{n}^{(1)}\overline{\frac{\partial\Psi_{n}^{(1)}(x)}{\partial\nu}}\,d\mathcal{H}^{1}\,d\mathcal{H}^{1}\biggr|

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{2}{\varepsilon K_{n}^{2}(\nicefrac{{1}}{{\varepsilon^{2}}})}\Biggl|\int_{0}^{\pi}K_{n}\bigl(\nicefrac{{1}}{{\varepsilon^{2}}}+2b+\nicefrac{{1}}{{\varepsilon^{2}}}(1-\cos\theta)\bigr)\biggl[-K_{n}^{\prime}(\nicefrac{{1}}{{\varepsilon^{2}}}+2b+\nicefrac{{1}}{{\varepsilon^{2}}}(1-\cos\theta))

\displaystyle+\frac{|n|}{\nicefrac{{1}}{{\varepsilon^{2}}}+2b+\nicefrac{{1}}{{\varepsilon^{2}}}(1-\cos\theta)}K_{n}(\nicefrac{{1}}{{\varepsilon^{2}}}+2b+\nicefrac{{1}}{{\varepsilon^{2}}}(1-\cos\theta){\color[rgb]{1,0,0})}\biggr]\,d\theta\Biggr|

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{C}{\varepsilon}(1+|n|)K_{n}^{2}(2b),

by an easy computation similar to that in the proof of Lemma
2.2
. By a similar argument, the second term in (
3.9
) satisfies the bound

\biggl|\int_{|x-a_{2}|=\nicefrac{{1}}{{\varepsilon^{2}}}+b}\Psi_{n}^{(1)}(x)\overline{\frac{\partial\Psi_{n}^{(1)}(x)}{\partial\nu}}\,d\mathcal{H}^{1}\biggr|\leqslant\frac{C}{\varepsilon}(1+|n|)K_{n}^{2}(2b).

Putting these together with the bound in (
3.9
) and (
3.8
), the proof of the Lemma is completed.
∎

In the next lemma we obtain the
\varepsilon\to 0^{+}
asymptotic expansion for the diagonal blocks in the matrix in (
3.4
).

Lemma 3.3
.

For every
m,n\in\mathbb{Z},
the
(m,n)
entry of the diagonal block of the quadratic form (
3.4
) satisfy the expansion

\displaystyle 2\mathfrak{Re}\biggl[\int_{\partial B_{1}}e^{im\theta_{1}}\overline{\frac{\partial}{\partial\nu_{1}}(\Psi_{n}^{(1)}+Z_{n}^{(1)})}\,d\mathcal{H}^{1}\biggr]=-\frac{4\pi}{\varepsilon^{2}}\frac{K_{n}^{\prime}(\nicefrac{{1}}{{\varepsilon^{2}}})}{K_{n}(\nicefrac{{1}}{{\varepsilon^{2}}})}\delta_{mn}+R_{mn},

(3.10)

where

|R_{mn}|\leqslant\frac{C}{\varepsilon}K_{m}(2b)K_{n}(2b)\sqrt{(1+|m|)(1+|n|)}.

(3.11)

The same expansion holds for the bottom right diagonal block.

Proof.

Let us note that since the normal
\nu_{1}
points towards the center
a_{1},
it follows that in the polar coordinates about
a_{1},
the normal derivative
\nicefrac{{\partial}}{{\partial\nu_{1}}}=-\nicefrac{{\partial}}{{\partial r}},
so that, on
\partial B_{1},

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial\nu_{1}}\Psi_{n}^{(1)}=-\frac{K_{n}^{\prime}(\nicefrac{{1}}{{\varepsilon^{2}}})}{K_{n}(\nicefrac{{1}}{{\varepsilon^{2}}})}e^{in\theta_{1}},

and, we have

\displaystyle\int_{\partial B_{1}}e^{im\theta_{1}}\overline{\frac{\partial}{\partial\nu_{1}}\Psi_{n}^{(1)}}\,d\mathcal{H}^{1}=-\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\frac{K_{n}^{\prime}(\nicefrac{{1}}{{\varepsilon^{2}}})}{K_{n}(\nicefrac{{1}}{{\varepsilon^{2}}})}\int_{0}^{2\pi}e^{i(m-n)\theta_{1}}\,d\theta=-\frac{2\pi}{\varepsilon^{2}}\frac{K_{n}^{\prime}(\nicefrac{{1}}{{\varepsilon^{2}}})}{K_{n}(\nicefrac{{1}}{{\varepsilon^{2}}})}\delta_{mn}.

As usual, the Kronecker’s delta
\delta_{mn}:=1
if
m=n
and
\delta_{mn}:=0
if
m\neq n.

We focus on the second term, i.e., on estimating

2\mathfrak{Re}\int_{\partial B_{1}}e^{im\theta_{1}}\overline{\frac{\partial}{\partial\nu_{1}}Z_{n}^{(1)}}\,d\mathcal{H}^{1}.

(3.12)

The natural idea to estimate this is to directly use Lemma
3.2
; however, this direct estimate misses the observation that
Z_{n}^{(1)}
vanishes on
\partial B_{1}.
To obtain a better estimate, we use Green’s second identity which, specialized to our setting, asserts that for any pair of suitably smooth functions
U_{1},U_{2}
that decay at infinity sufficiently fast satisfy the identity

\int_{\partial B_{1}}U_{1}\frac{\partial U_{2}}{\partial\nu_{1}}-U_{2}\frac{\partial U_{1}}{\partial\nu_{1}}\,d\mathcal{H}^{1}+\int_{\partial B_{2}}U_{1}\frac{\partial U_{2}}{\partial\nu_{2}}-U_{2}\frac{\partial U_{1}}{\partial\nu_{2}}\,d\mathcal{H}^{1}=\int_{\Omega_{\varepsilon}}U_{1}\Delta U_{2}-U_{2}\Delta U_{1}\,dx.

Applying this identity to
U_{1}=\Psi_{m}^{(1)}
and
U_{2}=\overline{Z_{n}^{(1)}},
and subsequently to the choice
U_{1}=\overline{\Psi_{m}^{(1)}}
, and
U_{2}={Z_{n}^{(1)}}
, adding the results in order to obtain the real parts in (
3.12
), we notice that the bulk terms on the right-hand side cancel, these choices of
U_{1}
and
U_{2}
are all equal to their respective Laplacians. We are consequently only left with boundary integrals, and we get

\displaystyle 2\mathfrak{Re}\biggl[\int_{\partial B_{1}}e^{im\theta_{1}}\overline{\frac{\partial}{\partial\nu_{1}}Z_{n}^{(1)}}\,d\mathcal{H}^{1}\biggr]=2\mathfrak{Re}\biggl[\int_{\partial B_{1}}\Psi_{m}^{(1)}\overline{\frac{\partial}{\partial\nu_{1}}Z_{n}^{(1)}}\,d\mathcal{H}^{1}\biggr]

(3.13)

\displaystyle=2\mathfrak{Re}\biggl[\int_{\partial B_{1}}\frac{\partial\Psi_{m}^{(1)}}{\partial\nu_{1}}\overline{Z_{n}^{(1)}}\,d\mathcal{H}^{1}+\int_{\partial B_{2}}\frac{\partial\Psi_{m}^{(1)}}{\partial\nu_{1}}\overline{Z_{n}^{(1)}}-\Psi_{m}^{(1)}\overline{\frac{\partial Z_{n}^{(1)}}{\partial\nu_{1}}}\,d\mathcal{H}^{1}\biggr]

\displaystyle=-2\mathfrak{Re}\biggl[\int_{\partial B_{2}}\overline{\Psi_{n}^{(1)}}\frac{\partial\Psi_{m}^{(1)}}{\partial\nu_{1}}+\Psi_{m}^{(1)}\overline{\frac{\partial Z_{n}^{(1)}}{\partial\nu_{1}}}\,d\mathcal{H}^{1}\biggr]

using the boundary conditions satisfied by
Z_{n}^{(1)}.
At this point, estimating as before and using Lemma
3.2
, it is easily seen that

\displaystyle\biggl|2\mathfrak{Re}\biggl[\int_{\partial B_{1}}e^{im\theta_{1}}\overline{\frac{\partial}{\partial\nu_{1}}Z_{n}^{(1)}}\,d\mathcal{H}^{1}\biggr]\biggr|\leqslant\frac{C(1+|n|)^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}(1+|m|)^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}K_{m}(2b)K_{n}(2b)}{\varepsilon},

since
\|\Psi_{m}^{(1)}\|_{H^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}(\partial B_{2})}+\|\tfrac{\partial}{\partial\nu_{1}}\Psi_{m}^{(1)}\|_{H^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}(\partial B_{2})}\leqslant\frac{C}{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}(1+|m|)^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}K_{m}(2b),
and the proof of the lemma is completed.

∎

Finally, we turn to evaluating the off-diagonal blocks in (

). The evaluation of these is not as straightforward since the terms involved do not have a straightforward separation of scales. To overcome this difficulty, we manipulate the boundary integrals that occur in the off-diagonal blocks using integrations by parts and the PDE solved by the functions involved, and this provides for a representation where the terms involved do have a separation of scales. At that point we can proceed very similarly to the proof of Lemma

in the case of constant boundary conditions.

Lemma 3.4
.

For each
m,n\in\mathbb{Z},
the
(m,n)
term in each of the off-diagonal blocks satisfies the expansion

\displaystyle\Biggl|2\mathfrak{Re}\biggl[\int_{\partial B_{1}}e^{im\theta_{1}}\overline{\frac{\partial}{\partial\nu_{1}}(\Psi_{n}^{(2)}+Z_{n}^{(2)})}\,d\mathcal{H}^{1}\biggr]-c_{m+n}\frac{e^{-2b}\sqrt{\pi}}{\varepsilon}\Biggr|\leqslant\frac{(1+|m|)(1+|n|)e^{-4b}}{\varepsilon}\,,

(3.14)

where

\displaystyle=2\mathfrak{Re}\biggl[\int_{\partial B_{2}}\frac{\partial\Psi_{m}^{(1)}}{\partial\nu_{2}}\overline{\Psi_{n}^{(2)}}\,d\mathcal{H}^{1}\biggr]

Using (

) and arguing as before using the

(3.16)

). Toward this end, we parametrize
\partial B_{2}
via

and notice that in this parametrization

and

The main contribution then is that it remains to evaluate the first term in (
3.15
). We proceed identically as in the proof of Lemma
2.4
. As in that argument, it suffices once again, to evaluate the portion of the integral on
\partial B_{2}^{+},

\partial B_{2}^{+}
as in the proof of that lemma (as a function of

it follows by rewriting

in terms of

that

\Psi_{n}^{(2)}(x)=\Bigl(T_{n}(\varepsilon^{2}x),\varepsilon^{2}\sqrt{1-x^{2}}U_{n-1}(\varepsilon^{2}x)\Bigr)\,.

Before computing
\nu_{2}\cdot\nabla\Psi_{m}^{(1)},
we record that for
(x,y)\in\partial B_{2}^{+},
arguing as in Step 3 of the proof of Lemma
2.4

\displaystyle(\cos\theta_{1},\sin\theta_{1})

\displaystyle=\frac{(x,y)-(0,\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}+b)}{\sqrt{x^{2}+\Bigl(y-\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}-b\Bigr)^{2}}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\Bigl(x,-2b-\frac{2}{\varepsilon^{2}}+\sqrt{\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{4}}-x^{2}}\Bigr)}{\sqrt{\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{4}}+C_{M}(x)}}

It follows that for
(x,y)\in\partial B_{2}^{+},
we have

\displaystyle\Psi_{m}^{(1)}(x,y)=\frac{K_{m}\Bigl(\sqrt{x^{2}+\Bigl(y-\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}-b\Bigr)^{2}}\Bigr)}{K_{m}(\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}})}(\cos(m\theta_{1}),\sin(m\theta_{1}))

\displaystyle\quad=\frac{K_{m}\Bigl(\sqrt{x^{2}+\Bigl(y-\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}-b\Bigr)^{2}}\Bigr)}{K_{m}(\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}})}

\displaystyle\quad\times\Biggl(T_{m}\Biggl(\frac{\varepsilon^{2}x}{\sqrt{x^{2}+\Bigl(y-\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}-b\Bigr)^{2}}}\Biggr),U_{m-1}\Biggl(\frac{\varepsilon^{2}x}{\sqrt{x^{2}+\Bigl(y-\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}-b\Bigr)^{2}}}\Biggr)\frac{y-\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}-b}{\sqrt{x^{2}+\Bigl(y-\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}-b\Bigr)^{2}}}\Biggr)\,,

\displaystyle=O(\varepsilon^{4})\,.

(x,y)\in\partial B_{2}^{+},

\displaystyle\nu_{2}\cdot\nabla\sqrt{x^{2}+\Bigl(y-\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}-b\Bigr)^{2}}

\displaystyle=-(\varepsilon^{2}x,\sqrt{1-\varepsilon^{4}x^{2}})\cdot\frac{(x,\sqrt{\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{4}}-x^{2}}-\frac{2}{\varepsilon^{2}}-2b)}{\sqrt{\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{4}}+C_{M}(x)}}

\displaystyle=-(\varepsilon^{2}x,\sqrt{1-\varepsilon^{4}x^{2}})\cdot\frac{(\varepsilon^{2}x,\sqrt{1-\varepsilon^{4}x^{2}}-2-2b\varepsilon^{2})}{\sqrt{1+\varepsilon^{4}C_{M}(x)}}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1-(2+2b\varepsilon^{2})\sqrt{1-\varepsilon^{4}x^{2}}}{\sqrt{1+\varepsilon^{4}C_{M}(x)}}\approx 1\,,

when
|x|\leqslant\frac{C}{\varepsilon}.

We turn to computing
\frac{\partial(\Psi_{m}^{(1)})_{2}}{\partial\nu_{2}}.
For this we observe that

\displaystyle\nu_{2}\cdot(\nabla\Psi_{m}^{(1)})_{2}(x,y)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{K_{m}(\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}})}\nu_{2}\cdot\nabla\Biggl[K_{m}\Bigl(\sqrt{x^{2}+\bigl(y-\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}-b\bigr)^{2}}\Bigr)U_{m-1}\Biggl(\frac{\varepsilon^{2}x}{\sqrt{x^{2}+\Bigl(y-\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}-b\Bigr)^{2}}}\Biggr)

\displaystyle\quad\quad\times\frac{y-\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}-b}{\sqrt{x^{2}+\Bigl(y-\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}-b\Bigr)^{2}}}\Biggr]

\displaystyle\quad\quad\approx-\frac{1}{K_{m}(\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}})}U_{m-1}\Biggl(\frac{\varepsilon^{2}x}{\sqrt{\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{4}}+C_{M}(x)}}\Biggr)\frac{\sqrt{\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{4}}-x^{2}}-\frac{2}{\varepsilon^{2}}-2b}{\sqrt{\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{4}}+C_{M}(x)}}

\displaystyle\quad\quad\times K_{m}^{\prime}\Bigl(\sqrt{\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{4}}+C_{M}(x)}\Bigr)\frac{(x,\sqrt{\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{4}}-x^{2}}-\frac{2}{\varepsilon^{2}}-2b)}{\sqrt{\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{4}}+C_{M}(x)}}\cdot(\varepsilon^{2}x,\sqrt{1-\varepsilon^{4}x^{2}})

\displaystyle\quad\quad=-\frac{1}{K_{m}(\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}})}U_{m-1}\Biggl(\frac{\varepsilon^{4}x}{\sqrt{1+\varepsilon^{4}C_{M}(x)}}\Biggr)\frac{\sqrt{1-\varepsilon^{4}x^{2}}-2-2b\varepsilon^{2}}{\sqrt{1+\varepsilon^{4}C_{M}(x)}}

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\times K_{m}^{\prime}\Bigl(\sqrt{\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{4}}+C_{M}(x)}\Bigr)\frac{1-(2+2b\varepsilon^{2})\sqrt{1-\varepsilon^{4}x^{2}}}{\sqrt{1+\varepsilon^{4}C_{M}(x)}}

\displaystyle\quad\quad\approx-U_{m-1}(0)\frac{K_{m}^{\prime}\Bigl(\sqrt{\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{4}}+C_{M}(x)}\Bigr)}{K_{m}(\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}})}\,,

and

(\Psi_{n}^{(2)})_{2}(x,y)=\varepsilon^{2}\sqrt{1-x^{2}}U_{n-1}(\varepsilon^{2}x)\approx\varepsilon^{2}U_{n-1}(0)\,.

In the above,
\approx
means that the left and right-hand sides of the equality differ by
O(\varepsilon^{2})
in magnitude, as can be checked from a straightforward calculation (by noting that
\nu_{2}\approx(0,-1),
and so only the derivatives along the ”radial” direction contribute)

Therefore, arguing exactly as before, and recalling that

U_{m}(0)=T_{m}(0)=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\begin{aligned} 1&\quad m\equiv 0(\mathrm{mod}\,4)\\
0&\quad m\equiv 1\mbox{ or }3(\mathrm{mod}\,4)\\
-1&\quad m\equiv 2(\mathrm{mod}\,4)\end{aligned}}}\right.\,,

we obtain

\displaystyle 2\mathfrak{Re}\int_{\partial B_{2}^{+}}\frac{\partial\Psi_{m}^{(1)}}{\partial\nu_{2}}\overline{\Psi_{n}^{(2)}}\,d\mathcal{H}^{1}

6. Monte Carlo simulation of the pairwise energy

In this section we will consider a many-body system of identical particles satisfying given canonical degree
d

g_{k}(\theta)=e^{i(d\theta-(d-1)\omega_{k})}
and which we assume are free to move and rotate, so that each particle has degrees of freedom corresponding to its centre of mass and the angle
\omega_{k}
. Following our discussion in the preceding section, we assume that there are only pairwise interactions in this system. Assuming that the distances between particles are large enough and plugging in special choices of
g_{1}(\theta)=e^{i(d\theta-(d-1)\omega_{1})}
and
g_{2}(\theta)=e^{i(d\theta-(d-1)\omega_{2})}
in the statement of Theorem
1.3
, we obtain that the interaction energy between a pair of particles of radius
1
at distance
2b\varepsilon^{2}
is given by

V_{12}=-(-1)^{d}\frac{2e^{-2b}\sqrt{\pi}}{\varepsilon}\cos((d-1)(\omega_{1}-\omega_{2}))\,.

(6.1)

We note a qualitative difference in behaviour depending on the parity of
d
. When
d
is even, the energy is minimised at parallel configurations, with particles at relative angle of
0
. Thus, we expect it to be favourable for particles to be closely packed with similar orientations. If
d
is odd, however, then the energy is minimised at anti-parallel configurations, where particles are at a relative angle of
\frac{\pi}{d-1}
, modulo
\frac{2\pi}{d-1}
. As the interactions are short-range, we expect only interactions with nearest neighbours to be significant. Heuristically, it seems clear that configurations of square-like lattices with second-nearest-neighbours having the same orientation, whilst nearest neighbours are at a relative angle of
\frac{\pi}{d-1}
, should be relatively stable.

We will consider the pairwise interaction energy for particles with orientations
\omega_{1},\omega_{2}
and centres of mass separated by
r
as given by

V(r,\omega_{1},\omega_{2})=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\begin{array}[]{l l}(-1)^{d+1}\exp\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(-\frac{|r|-2}{\epsilon^{2}}}}\right)\cos((d-1)(\omega_{1}-\omega_{2}))&|\widehat{r}|>2\\
+\infty&|\widehat{r}|\leq 2\end{array}}}\right.

(6.2)

Multiplicative factors that do not affect minimisers of the energy are neglected for simplicity. The infinite energy for
|\widehat{r}|\leq 2
corresponds to the the particles being unable to interpenetrate. Of course, the pairwise interaction in (
6.1
) corresponds to an asymptotic limit, and thus we are required to introduce an appropriate length-scale for the simulation, corresponding to the choice of
\epsilon
, which we take to be
\sqrt{\frac{2}{5}}
, as we found in preliminary studies that even marginally smaller values of
\epsilon
lead to interactions too weak to produce any noticable structure. The total pairwise energy is then given by

\frac{1}{2}\sum\limits_{i\neq j}V(x_{i}-x_{j},\omega_{i},\omega_{j}).

We employ a simulated annealing algorithm, with the transition probabilities taken from the corresponding Gibbs’ distribution of the system, that is, those of a Metropolis-Hastings algorithm. At each iteration step, we perturb the centre of mass of the particle with index
i
according to a Gaussian distribution with mean
0
and standard deviation given by
\min(\max(0.025,\delta_{i}),0.5)
, where
\delta_{i}=\min\limits_{j\neq i}(|x_{i}-x_{j}|-2)
is the minimal contact distance to another particle. We perturb the angle according to a normal distribution with mean 0 and standard deviation
\frac{2\pi}{50}
. For each temperature, we trial each particle once, and linearly decrease the temperature from
\frac{1}{4}
to
0
over 25000 iterations. We take a total of 256 particles, initialised as a perturbation of a square lattice with nearest-neighbour separation of
2.2
, and orientations taken according to a uniform distribution. Finally, due to the short-range nature of the interactions, at higher temperatures it is easy for particles to drift large distances, at which point their behaviour becomes a random walk and ceases to effectively interact with the rest of the system, so we impose that particles cannot leave a box of size
46\times 46
.

Whilst simulated annealing is generally used to find global minimisers, as the particles are very weakly interacting, we expect a relatively flat energy landscape that permits large fluctuations away from the global minimiser.

Appendix A
Sobolev spaces and trace theory

There are various definitions for the norms of trace spaces of functions in
H^{1}(\Omega)
, which are equivalent for sufficiently regular
\Omega

[
8
]
. In this work we work with the following definitions.

Definition A.1
.

Let
\Omega
be a Lipschitz, possibly unbounded, domain with boundary
\partial\Omega
. We define
H^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}(\partial\Omega)
to be the range of the trace operator on
H^{1}(\Omega)
. For
u_{0}\in H^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}(\partial\Omega)
, we define its norm as

\displaystyle\|u_{0}\|_{H^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}(\partial\Omega)}=\inf\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\|u\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}:u|_{\partial\Omega}=u_{0}}}\right\}.

(A.1)

It is then immediate that if
u\in H^{1}(\Omega)
, is the weak solution to
\Delta u=u
on
\Omega
, then
\|u\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|u|_{\partial\Omega}}}\right|}}\right|_{{H^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}}(\partial\Omega)}
. We note that this definition is distinct from the typical one employing the Gagliardo (semi-)norm,

\displaystyle\|u_{0}\|_{H^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}_{G}(\partial\Omega)}^{2}=

\displaystyle\|u_{0}\|_{L^{2}(\partial\Omega)}^{2}+\int_{\partial\Omega}\int_{\partial\Omega}\frac{|u_{0}(x)-u_{0}(y)|^{2}}{|x-y|^{2}}\,dx\,dy,

and instead corresponds to the interpretation of the trace space of
H^{1}(\Omega)
as the quotient space
H^{1}(\Omega)/H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
, where (
A.1
) corresponds to the induced norm on the quotient space. In the case of bounded and Lipschitz domains, these norms are known to be equivalent
[
8
]
, however in the case of unbounded domains, relevant in this work, this appears to be a folklore theorem, so we include a proof for completeness.

Proposition A.2
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be an exterior domain, i.e.,
\Omega^{c}
is a bounded, Lipschitz domain. Then
||\cdot||_{H^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}_{G}(\Gamma)}\sim||\cdot||_{H^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}(\partial\Omega)}
.

Proof.

Take
B
to be a ball such that
\Omega^{c}\subset\subset B
. We define
\widehat{\Omega}=\Omega\cap B
, which is then a bounded, Lipschitz domain. Given
u_{0}\in H^{\frac{1}{2}}(\partial\Omega)
, define
Eu_{0}
to be its extension by
0
to
\partial\widehat{\Omega}=\widehat{\Gamma}
, so that
Eu_{0}|_{\partial\Omega}=u_{0}
and
Eu_{0}|_{\partial B}=0
. Our proof strategy is to show the chain of equivalences,

||u_{0}||_{H^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}_{G}(\Gamma)}\sim||Eu_{0}||_{H^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}_{G}(\widehat{\Gamma})}\sim||Eu_{0}||_{H^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}(\partial\widehat{\Omega})}\sim||u_{0}||_{H^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}(\partial\Omega)},

(A.2)

where
A\sim B
implies the existence of some
C>1
with
\frac{1}{C}A\leq B\leq CA
.

First, we turn to
||u_{0}||_{H^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}_{G}(\Gamma)}\sim||Eu_{0}||_{H^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}_{G}(\widehat{\Gamma})}
. It is immediate, following the definition of the norm, that
||Eu_{0}||_{H^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}_{G}(\widehat{\Gamma})}\geq||u_{0}||_{H^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}_{G}(\Gamma)}
. To obtain the converse estimate, we note that

\begin{split}||Eu_{0}||_{H^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}_{G}(\widehat{\Gamma})}^{2}=&||u_{0}||_{L^{2}(\Gamma)}^{2}+\int_{\Gamma}\int_{\Gamma}\frac{|u(x)-u(y)|^{2}}{|x-y|^{2}}\,dx\,dy+2\int_{\Gamma}\int_{\partial B}\frac{|u(x)|^{2}}{|x-y|^{2}}\,dx\,dy\\
\leq&||u_{0}||_{L^{2}(\Gamma)}^{2}+\int_{\Gamma}\int_{\Gamma}\frac{|u(x)-u(y)|^{2}}{|x-y|^{2}}\,dx\,dy+\frac{2|\partial B|}{d(\partial B,\Gamma)^{2}}\int_{\Gamma}|u(x)|^{2}\,dx\\
\leq&\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1+\frac{2|\partial B|}{d(\partial B,\Gamma)^{2}}}}\right)||u_{0}||_{H^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}_{G}(\Gamma)}.\end{split}

(A.3)

The relationship
||Eu_{0}||_{H^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}_{G}(\widehat{\Gamma})}\sim||Eu_{0}||_{H^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}(\partial\widehat{\Omega})}
is given in
[
8
]
, as
\widehat{\Omega}
is a bounded, Lipschitz domain.

Finally, we demonstrate
||Eu_{0}||_{H^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}(\partial\widehat{\Omega})}\sim||u_{0}||_{H^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}(\partial\Omega)}
. For
u_{0}
in the trace space of
H^{1}(\widehat{\Omega})
with
u_{0}|_{\partial B}=0
, there exists a minimiser for the infima that defines
||u_{0}||_{H^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}(\partial\widehat{\Omega})}
, which has trace equal to zero on
\partial B
. In particular, it may be extended by zero to give a

||u_{0}||_{H^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}(\partial\Omega)}\leq||Eu_{0}||_{H^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}(\partial\widehat{\Omega})}

For the converse estimate, let

\Gamma

\text{supp }(\varphi)\subset\subset B

Appendix E
A brief introduction to the Landau-de Gennes model

The main characteristic feature of the nematic liquid crystals is the local preferred orientation of the rod-like molecules. A comprehensive way of modeling this is through a a probability measure
\mu(x,\cdot):\mathcal{L}(\mathbb{S}^{2})\to\mathbb{[}0,1]
for each material point
x
in the region
\Omega
occupied
by the liquid crystal. Thus
\mu(x,A)
assigns a number between
0
and
1
denoting the probability that the molecules with centre of mass in a very small neighborhood of the point
x\in\Omega
are pointing in a direction contained in
A\subset\mathbb{S}^{2}
.

The significant numerical and analytical challenges associated generated by dealing with parametrised probability measures
have lead Pierre Gilles de Gennes in the 70s to propose replacing the probability measure by one of its moments. Due to the physical head-to-tail symmetry of the molecules the first order moment vanishes (see for details
[
3
,
11
]
). Thus the first nontrivial information on
\mu
comes from the
tensor of second moments:

M_{ij}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rm{def}}}{{=}}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}p_{i}p_{j}\,d\mu(p),\,i,j=1,2,3.

We have
M=M^{T}
and

\textrm{tr}\,M=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}d\mu(p)=1
.

If the orientation of the molecules is equally distributed in
all directions we say that the distribution is
isotropic
and
then
\mu=\mu_{0}
where
d\mu_{0}(p)=\frac{1}{4\pi}dA
. The
corresponding second moment tensor is

M_{0}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rm{def}}}{{=}}\frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}p\otimes p\,dA=\frac{1}{3}Id

(since
\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}p_{1}p_{2}\,d\mu(p)=0,\,\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}p_{1}^{2}\,d\mu(p)=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}p_{2}^{2}\,d\mu(p)=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}p_{3}^{2}\,d\mu(p)

and
\textrm{tr}\,M_{0}=1
).

The de Gennes order-parameter tensor
Q
is defined as

Q\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rm{def}}}{{=}}M-M_{0}=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p\otimes p-\frac{1}{3}Id}}\right)\,d\mu(p)

(E.1)

and measures the deviation of the second moment
tensor from its isotropic value.

By extension we call a
Q
-tensor any symmetric, traceless, three-by-three real-valued matrix and denote the space of such
Q
-tensors by
\mathcal{S}_{0}
. The
configuration of the nematic material is then described by maps
Q:\Omega\to\mathcal{S}_{0}
. The simplest theory that produces physically meaningful predictions is a variational one. In it equilibrium configurations of liquid crystals are obtained, for instance, as
energy minimizers, subject to suitable boundary conditions. The simplest commonly used energy functional is

\mathcal{F}_{LG}[Q]=\int_{\Omega}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\frac{L}{2}\sum_{i,j,k=1}^{3}Q_{ij,k}Q_{ij,k}+\frac{a}{2}\textrm{tr}\,Q^{2}+\frac{b}{3}\textrm{tr}\,Q^{3}+\frac{c}{4}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\textrm{tr}\,Q^{2}}}\right)^{2}}}\right]\,dx

(E.2)

where
a,b,c
are temperature and material dependent constants and

is the elastic constant. The “elastic part”
