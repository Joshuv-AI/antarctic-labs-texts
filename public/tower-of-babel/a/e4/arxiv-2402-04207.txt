Detector-tuned overlap catastrophe in quantum dots
Sarath Sankar, Corentin Bertrand, Antoine Georges, Eran Sela, Yigal Meir

Abstract
The Anderson overlap catastrophe (AOC) is a many-body effect arising as a result of a shakeup of a Fermi sea due to an abrupt change of a local potential, leading to a power-law dependence of the density of states on energy. Here we demonstrate that a standard quantum-dot detector can be employed as a highly tunable probe of the AOC, where the power law can be continuously modified by a gate voltage. We show that signatures of the AOC have already appeared in previous experiments, and give explicit predictions allowing to tune and pinpoint their nonperturbative aspects.



I
Introduction

Quantum dots (QDs) are highly-tunable mesoscopic systems, which allow detailed exploration of many-body effects in electronic systems. The quintessential example is the demonstration of the Kondo effect in transport through a QD
Goldhaber-Gordon
et al. 1998a
;
Goldhaber-Gordon
et al. 1998b
, where, by tuning the dot energy level, one can explore the localized moment, mixed valence, and the Kondo regimes in a single sample. Another many-body effect that has been explored in such a system is the Anderson overlap catastrophe (AOC)
Anderson 1967
, where a sudden change in the local potential seen by a Fermi sea changes the ground state of the system into another one, such that its overlap with the initial ground-state decays as a power-law of the number of electrons. This effect was first discovered in the context of x-ray absorption in metals
Mahan 1967
;
Nozieres and
De Dominicis 1969
.
Manifestation of the AOC as a Fermi edge singularity in the transport through a QD has been pointed out early on
Matveev and Larkin 1992
, and has been extensively explored theoretically
Bascones et al. 2000
;
Muzykantskii et al. 2003
;
Abanin and Levitov 2004
;
Hentschel et al. 2005
;
Wunsch et al. 2008
;
Goldstein et al. 2010
;
Mkhitaryan and Raikh 2011
;
Chernii et al. 2014
;
Goremykina and Sukhorukov 2017
;
Borin and Sukhorukov 2017
.
Indeed, signatures of Fermi-edge singularity have been reported in tunneling experiments through single levels and QDs
Geim et al. 1994
;
Benedict et al. 1998
;
Thornton et al. 1998
;
Hapke-Wurst et al. 2000
;
Frahm et al. 2006
;
Rüth et al. 2008
;
Krähenmann
et al. 2017
and also in optical absorption of QDs
Latta et al. 2011
.
However, none of these studies have demonstrated the desired tunability expected in QD structures.

Here we propose using a nearby QD detector (QDD)
to continuously tune the overlap catastrophe exponent. While it was suggested in
Abanin and Levitov 2004
that one may tune this parameter by modifying the QD shape or its scattering matrix, such modifications are experimentally nontrivial. In our approach one merely has to change the QDD level position, easily controllable by an appropriate gate voltage, thus allowing continuous modification of the AOC exponent. Such quantum-point-contact or QD detectors have been employed extensively in the last 30 years
Field et al. 1993
, and thus the setups we explore here [see, e.g., Fig.
1
(a)] are readily realizable. Moreover, as the bias voltage on the QDD can be easily changed, one can also probe experimentally the AOC out of equilibrium. We demonstrate below that the puzzles raised in such a recent experiment
Ferguson et al. 2023
can be quantitatively explained by our approach, and offer, in fact, initial indication of AOC physics in these systems. We make specific predictions of unequivocal signatures of the AOC in these devices.

The basic setup consists of a mesoscopic system, comprising of one or several QDs, where some of them are coupled electrostatically to a QDD, tunnel coupled to its own leads, see Fig.
1
(a). When an electron tunnels to a QD that is coupled to the QDD, it shifts the QDD energy, and this modifies the current through it. Accordingly, the QDD serves as a charge detector. It was already pointed out in
Aleiner et al. 1997
(see also
Goldstein et al. 2010
) that such a system displays AOC physics since the scattering potential of the QDD changes abruptly when an electron tunnels into the mesoscopic system. The corresponding power-law exponent depends on the change in the phase shift of the scattering amplitude through the QDD. Since this phase shift depends continuously on the QDD energy position, the latter can be tuned to change the power-law exponent.

The standard approach to study the effect of the detector on the system - the measurement backaction (MBA) - is the popular
P(E)
theory
Ingold and Nazarov 1992
;
Aguado and Kouwenhoven 2000
;
Gustavsson et al. 2007
, describing photon assisted tunneling
Kouwenhoven et al. 1994
;
Platero and Aguado 2004
, where the current fluctuations in the detector are considered as an electro-magnetic environment which exchanges energy with the system. However, the
P(E)
approach fails to take into account the nonperturbative effect of the system state on the environment modes, which is the one leading to AOC. Below, we present an exact treatment of the electrostatic interaction [
\lambda
in Fig.
1
(a)] by numerically implementing the approach described in Ref.
Nozieres and
De Dominicis 1969
. We also show that the
P(E)
approach is a perturbative limit of our theory.

II
General formalism

The main idea is to first, following Refs.
Nozieres and
De Dominicis 1969
;
Ng 1996
;
Aleiner et al. 1997
, treat exactly the
detector, consisting of two voltage-biased leads and a central scattering region, that is capacitively coupled to the system QD [orange contour in Fig.
1
(a)]. Then we treat the tunneling to the rest of the system,
\Gamma
, perturbatively. In other words, as displayed in Fig.
1
(b), we consider high temperature
T
compared to quantum coherent scales associated with
\Gamma
(such as Kondo effects).

Since the system QD has no dynamics at
\Gamma=0
, we can, without loss of generality, describe it by a single spinless level with energy
\epsilon
. The dynamics of the many-body state of the detector is then governed by some Hamiltonian,
H_{n}
, that depends on the charge of the QD,
n=\{0,1\}
. The detector affects the rates of electron tunneling in and out of the QD to the rest of the system,
\Gamma^{{{\rm{(in/out)}}}}
, due to inelastic processes. The “rest of the system” in Fig.
1
(a) is arbitrary and various examples are considered below. As a representative case, we now consider it to be a lead whose bare tunneling rate to the QD is
\Gamma
. In the weak tunneling regime
\Gamma\ll T
, the tunnelling rates
can be written as (see Appendix
A
for a derivation based on the Fermi golden rule)

\displaystyle\Gamma^{({\rm{in}})}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Gamma\int dE\,f(E+\epsilon)A^{+}(E),~

\displaystyle\Gamma^{({\rm{out}})}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Gamma\int dE\,[1-f(E+\epsilon)]A^{-}(E),

(1)

where
f(E)
is the Fermi function of the lead and we set
\hbar,k_{B}=1
. The functions
A^{\pm}(E)
are independent of what the “rest of the system” is, and
A^{\pm}(E)=\int dte^{iEt}A^{\pm}(t)
, with

A^{\text{\tiny+/}\text{\small-}}(t)={\rm{Tr}}\left[\rho_{0/1}e^{itH_{0}}e^{-itH_{1}}\right],

(2)

where
\rho_{n}\propto e^{-H_{n}/T}
denote the detector density matrix for QD charge
n
. The functions
A^{\pm}(t)
describe the effect of a sudden change of the Hamiltonian of the detector, and their Fourier transforms
A^{\pm}(E)
give the probability of the detector to absorb/emit energy
E
in the tunneling-in/out process.

The QDD has a Lorentzian density of states associaed with the
\Gamma_{\rm{d}}-
broadened level. For a detector with constant density of states, explicit expressions were derived in the wide-band limit (WBL)
Aleiner et al. 1997
(also see Appendix
C
); At
T=0
one has
A_{{\rm{WBL}}}^{\pm}(t)\sim 1/t^{\alpha}\times e^{-\Gamma_{\varphi}t}
, where
\alpha
is the AOC exponent and
\Gamma_{\varphi}=\gamma V
is a voltage induced dephasing.

P(E)
-theory also expresses tunneling rates within the system in terms of a function characterizing the detector
Aguado and Kouwenhoven 2000
. It is important to compare it with our formalism: denoting
A^{\pm}(t)=e^{C^{\pm}(t)}
,
P(E)
theory corresponds to a perturbative approximation of
C^{\pm}(t)
to second order in
\lambda
(see Appendix
D
).
It thus includes the MBA of the detector on the system but ignores the back-reaction of the system on the detector, which lies at the heart of the AOC.

Either within
P(E)
theory or within the WBL,
the MBA is described by a single function

A^{+}(E)=A^{-}(-E)\equiv P(E).

(3)

Our treatment of AOC in general, gives
A^{+}(E)\neq A^{-}(-E)
. As we show below, this inequality has smoking gun signatures in experiments.

II.1
QD properties for
\Gamma\to 0

All the physically relevant quantities associated with the weakly tunnel coupled QD can be expressed in terms of the
A^{\pm}
correlators which thus carry all the information regarding MBA. For example, the QD occupation probability,
\langle n\rangle
, is determined by imposing steady state to the rate equation. For instance, in the QD-lead system, the steady state condition is

(1-\langle n\rangle)\Gamma^{({\rm{in}})}+\langle n\rangle\Gamma^{({\rm{out}})}=0\implies\langle n\rangle=\frac{\Gamma^{({\rm{in}})}}{\Gamma^{({\rm{in}})}+\Gamma^{({\rm{out}})}}.

(4)

The Green functions of the QD
can also be expressed in terms of
A^{\pm}(t)
. In particular the Green functions
G^{<}(t)=i\langle c^{\dagger}c(t)\rangle
and
G^{>}(t)=-i\langle c(t)c^{\dagger}\rangle
are given by

G^{>/<}(t)=i\left(\langle n\rangle-\frac{1}{2}\mp\frac{1}{2}\right)e^{-i\epsilon t}A^{\pm}(t),

(5)

and the retarded Green function can be obtained using
G^{R}(t)=\theta(t)\left[G^{>}(t)-G^{<}(t)\right]
. Note that in the frequency domain, the function
A_{R}^{\pm}(\omega)
, which is the Fourier transform of
\Theta(t)A^{\pm}(t)
enters into the expression of
G^{R}(\omega)
. Since by definition we have,
A^{\pm}(-t)=\left(A^{\pm}(t)\right)^{*}
, it follows that
A^{\pm}(\omega)=2{\rm{Re}}\left[A^{\pm}_{R}(\omega)\right]
. One can then express the DoS,
\nu(\omega)=-(1/\pi){\rm{Im}}[G^{R}(\omega)]
, as

\nu(\omega;\epsilon)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\left[(1-\langle n\rangle)A^{+}(\omega-\epsilon)+\langle n\rangle A^{-}(\omega-\epsilon)\right].

(6)

This expression for the density of states is used in Fig. 1(c,d).

\displaystyle H_{n}

-U/2>\epsilon_{1,2}>-U
the tunneling in rate dominates for
\{01\}\to\{11\}
, giving a preference to
\{10\}
.

First consider the spinless case effectively obtained at a large Zeeman field on the DD. Indeed, for
s=1
the above two tunnel rates become identical at
\epsilon_{1}=-U/2
. For example, one can verify that
\Gamma_{01\to 11}^{(\rm{in})}(\epsilon_{1})=\Gamma_{10\to 00}^{(\rm{out})}(-U-\epsilon_{1})
,
where
\epsilon\to-U-\epsilon
is a reflection around the mid-valley point

\{10\}
and
\{01\}
exchange roles, together with the closest excited state. As a result, the S-shape would be exactly symmetric, passing through the point
\epsilon_{1}=\epsilon_{2}=-U/2
, in agreement with Ref.
Ferguson et al. 2023
.
Thus, in the spinless case, any difference
\delta S_{1}\neq\delta S_{2}
is a smoking gun signature of MBA described by different functions
A^{+}(\omega)\neq A^{-}(-\omega)
, which goes beyond
P(E)
physics. Fig.

vs

\lambda_{1}
(here

(see Appendix
B.1

\delta S_{1}
and
\delta S_{2}
gets interchanged.

In the spinful case, as long as

in Eq. (

\{10\}
is more prominent than
\{01\}
at

As a nontrivial prediction of AOC beyond the

theory, as seen in Fig.

Appendix A
Fermi’s golden rule rates and spectral functions
A^{\pm}(\omega)

In this section we detail the derivation of the various detector assisted tunneling rates, Eqs. (1), (6) and a generalization of Eq. (8) , for different choices of the “rest of the system” in Fig. 1(a) of the main text.

A.1
QD-lead system

Here we derive Eq. (1) of the main text. Consider the spinless QD-lead system coupled to a charge detector. The detector is governed by some Hamiltonian,
H_{n}
, that depends on the charge
n=\{0,1\}
of the system QD with energy level
\epsilon
. Let
|\psi_{i}^{n}\rangle
denote the eigenbasis of
H_{n}
. The detector state is assumed to be given by the density matrix,
\rho_{n}=e^{-\beta H_{n}}/{\rm{Tr}}e^{-\beta H_{n}}=\sum_{i}P_{i}^{n}|\psi_{i}^{n}\rangle\langle\psi_{i}^{n}|
where
P_{i}^{n}=\frac{e^{-\beta E_{i}^{n}}}{\sum_{i}e^{-\beta E_{i}^{n}}}
and
\beta=T^{-1}
is the inverse temperature. The tunnel rates given by the Fermi golden rule can be expressed as

\displaystyle\Gamma^{{(\rm{in})}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Gamma\int dEf(E)\left[\sum_{i,j}P_{j}^{0}\langle\psi_{i}^{1}|\psi_{j}^{0}\rangle\langle\psi_{j}^{0}|\psi_{i}^{1}\rangle 2\pi\delta(E_{j}^{0}-E_{i}^{1}+E-\epsilon)\right],

(15)

\displaystyle\Gamma^{{(\rm{out})}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Gamma\int dE(1-f(E))\left[\sum_{i,j}P_{i}^{1}\langle\psi_{j}^{0}|\psi_{i}^{1}\rangle\langle\psi_{i}^{1}|\psi_{j}^{0}\rangle 2\pi\delta(E_{i}^{1}-E_{j}^{0}+\epsilon-E)\right].

(16)

Using
2\pi\delta(E)=\int\,dte^{itE}

these expressions become

\displaystyle\Gamma^{{(\rm{in})}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Gamma\int dEf(E)\int dt\,\left[\sum_{i,j}P_{j}^{0}\langle\psi_{j}^{0}|e^{itH_{0}}e^{-itH_{1}}|\psi_{i}^{1}\rangle\langle\psi_{i}^{1}|\psi_{j}^{0}\rangle\right]e^{it(E-\epsilon)},

(17)

\displaystyle\Gamma^{{(\rm{out})}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Gamma\int dE(1-f(E))\int dt\,\left[\sum_{i,j}P_{i}^{1}\langle\psi_{i}^{1}|e^{-itH_{1}}e^{itH_{0}}|\psi_{j}^{0}\rangle\langle\psi_{j}^{0}|\psi_{i}^{1}\rangle\right]e^{it(E-\epsilon)}.

(18)

Next we use
\sum_{i}|\psi_{i}^{1}\rangle\langle\psi_{i}^{1}|=\sum_{j}|\psi_{j}^{0}\rangle\langle\psi_{j}^{0}|=1
to obtain

\displaystyle\Gamma^{{(\rm{in})}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Gamma\int dEf(E)\int dt\,\text{Tr}\left[\rho_{0}e^{iH_{0}t}e^{-iH_{1}t}\right]e^{it(E-\epsilon)}=\Gamma\int dE\,f(E)A^{+}(E-\epsilon),

(19)

\displaystyle\Gamma^{{(\rm{out})}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Gamma\int dE(1-f(E))\int dt\,\text{Tr}\left[\rho_{1}e^{-iH_{1}t}e^{iH_{0}t}\right]e^{it(E-\epsilon)}=\Gamma\int dE\,(1-f(E))A^{-}(E-\epsilon).

(20)

We used the Lehman representation of the detector spectral functions, for example

\displaystyle A^{+}(E)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int dte^{iEt}{\rm{Tr}}\rho_{0}e^{iH_{0}t}e^{-iH_{1}t}

(21)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int dt\sum_{i,j}P^{0}_{j}|\langle\psi^{0}_{j}|\psi^{1}_{i}\rangle|^{2}e^{iE_{j}^{0}t}e^{-iE_{i}^{1}t}e^{iE}=2\pi\sum_{i,j}P^{0}_{j}|\langle\psi^{0}_{j}|\psi^{1}_{i}\rangle|^{2}\delta(E_{j}^{0}-E_{i}^{1}+E).

Thus, the rates of tunneling into the QD,
n=0\to 1
, are accompanied by excitations in the detector as described by the spectral function
A^{+}
, and similarly for the tunneling out of the QD in terms of
A^{-}
. Note that the definitions of
A^{\pm}(E)
in Eq. (
19
) are consistent with those in the main text

A^{\text{\tiny+/}\text{\small-}}(t)={\rm{Tr}}\left[\rho_{0/1}e^{itH_{0}}e^{-itH_{1}}\right],

(22)

since the exponentials
{}^{itH_{0}},e^{-itH_{1}}
do commute inside this trace (one of them commutes with
\rho_{0/1}
). Note also that it follows that
A^{\pm}(-t)=\left(A^{\pm}(t)\right)^{*}
.

A.2
Tunnel coupled double dot

Here we derive Eq. (6) of the main text. Consider a double dot Hamiltonian
H_{DD}=\epsilon_{1}c^{\dagger}_{1}c_{1}+\epsilon_{2}c^{\dagger}_{2}c_{2}+(vc^{\dagger}_{1}c_{2}+h.c.)
where
n=d^{\dagger}_{1}d_{1}
. Using the exact same treatment we obtain for the tunneling rate into dot 1

\Gamma^{{(\rm{in})}}_{{\rm{DD}}}=2\pi|v|^{2}\sum_{i,j}P_{j}^{0}\langle\psi_{i}^{1}|\psi_{j}^{0}\rangle\langle\psi_{j}^{0}|\psi_{i}^{1}\rangle\delta(E_{j}^{0}-E_{i}^{1}+\epsilon_{2}-\epsilon_{1}),

(23)

which is the same as Eq. (
15
) with
E\to\epsilon_{2},\epsilon\to\epsilon_{1}
, with no Fermi function (since we consider the conditional transition probability to tunnel into dot 1 given that an electron is in dot 2), and no integral over
E
. As a result we obtain

\Gamma^{({\rm{in}})}_{{\rm{DD}}}=|v|^{2}A^{+}(\epsilon_{2}-\epsilon_{1}),~~~\Gamma^{{(\rm{out})}}_{{\rm{DD}}}=|v|^{2}A^{-}(\epsilon_{2}-\epsilon_{1}).

(24)

Namely, by scanning the DD level detuning, a measurement of the tunneling rates directly measures the detector spectral functions.

A.3
Capacitively coupled double dot

\lambda_{1}
and
\lambda_{2}

\lambda_{1}
and
\lambda_{2}
the detector Hamiltonian takes one out of four possible values,
H_{n_{1},n_{2}}=H_{{\rm{det}}}+H_{\lambda}(n_{1},n_{2})
and we define for the tunneling into or out of dot 1

\displaystyle A^{+}_{1,n_{2}}={\rm{Tr}}[e^{-\beta H_{0,n_{2}}}e^{itH_{0,n_{2}}}e^{-itH_{1,n_{2}}}],

(25)

\displaystyle A^{-}_{1,n_{2}}={\rm{Tr}}[e^{-\beta H_{1,n_{2}}}e^{itH_{0,n_{2}}}e^{-itH_{1,n_{2}}}],

and similarly for tunneling in or out of dot 2,

\displaystyle A^{+}_{2,n_{1}}={\rm{Tr}}[e^{-\beta H_{n_{1},0}}e^{itH_{n_{1},0}}e^{-itH_{n_{1},1}}],

\displaystyle A^{-}_{2,n_{1}}={\rm{Tr}}[e^{-\beta H_{n_{1},1}}e^{itH_{n_{1},0}}e^{-itH_{n_{1},1}}].

\Gamma^{({\rm{in}})}_{0n_{2}\to 1n_{2}}=s\Gamma\int d\omega\,f(\omega)A_{1,n_{2}}^{+}(\omega-\epsilon_{1}-Un_{2}),

(28)

where the factor in tunneling-in rates is

\Gamma^{({\rm{in}})}_{n_{1}0\to n_{1}1}=s\Gamma\int d\omega\,f(\omega)A_{2,n_{1}}^{+}(\omega-\epsilon_{2}-Un_{1}),

(29)

\Gamma^{({\rm{out}})}_{n_{1}1\to n_{1}0}=\Gamma\int d\omega\,f(-\omega)A_{2,n_{1}}^{-}(\omega-\epsilon_{2}-Un_{1}).

Appendix B
Exact numerical method

In this section we provide details for our numerically exact method to compute the correlators
A^{\pm}(t)
when the detector Hamiltonians,
H_{n}
, are non-interacting.We focus on the QDD model,

\displaystyle H_{n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle H_{{\rm{det}}}+\lambda\left(d^{\dagger}d-\frac{1}{2}\right)\left(n-\frac{1}{2}\right),

(31)

\displaystyle H_{\rm{det}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\epsilon_{\rm{d}}d^{\dagger}d+\sum_{k,\mu=L,R}\epsilon_{k}\varphi_{k\mu}^{\dagger}\varphi_{k\mu}

(32)

\displaystyle+\sum_{\mu=L/R}v_{\mu}\sum_{k}(d^{\dagger}\varphi_{k,\mu}+h.c.).

The QDD leads are voltage biased with
\pm V/2
. We assume that the QDD leads have a constant DoS,
\nu
, and then the line width function is
\Gamma_{d}=2\pi\nu(v_{L}^{2}+v_{R}^{2})
. The local DoS of the QDD dot is then given by the Lorentzian form,

\nu_{d}(\omega;\epsilon_{\rm{d}})=\frac{1}{\pi}\frac{\Gamma_{d}}{(\omega-\epsilon_{\rm{d}})^{2}+\Gamma_{d}^{2}}.

(33)

The lesser and greater Green functions of the QDD with respect to
H_{\rm{det}}
are given by

g^{>}(\omega;\epsilon_{\rm{d}})=2\pi i\,\nu(\omega;\epsilon_{\rm{d}})[f(\omega-V/2)+f(\omega+V/2)-2],

(34)

g^{<}(\omega;\epsilon_{\rm{d}})=2\pi i\,\nu(\omega;\epsilon_{\rm{d}})[f(\omega-V/2)+f(\omega+V/2)].

(35)

Using the linked-cluster theorem we can write
A^{\pm}(t)=e^{C^{\pm}(t)}
, where
C^{\pm}(t)
is the sum of all single loop diagrams
Nozieres and
De Dominicis 1969
,

C^{\pm}(t)=-\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{(\pm\lambda)^{k}}{k}\int_{0}^{\pm t}dt_{1}\ldots\int_{0}^{\pm t}dt_{k}\,g^{(n)}(t_{1}-t_{2})\ldots g^{(n)}(t_{k}-t_{1}),

(36)

with
n=\{0,1\}
for
C^{\pm}
, respectively, and
g^{(n)}(t_{1}-t_{2})
denotes the time-ordered Green function with respect to
H_{n}
,

g^{(n)}(t_{1}-t_{2})=-i\text{Tr}\left[\rho_{n}\mathcal{T}d(t_{1})d^{\dagger}(t_{2})\right].

(37)

We now describe the calculation of
C^{+}(t)
(with a similar calculation of
C^{-}(t)
), and for simplicity we drop the superscripts of
g^{(n)}
such that
g\equiv g^{(0)}
. To proceed, it is useful to define a special Green function
\psi_{t}(u,u^{\prime})
, which is the sum of all connected diagrams with two external legs at times
u
and
u^{\prime}
and the vertices are restricted to lie in the range
[0,t]
. It obeys a quasi-Dyson equation,

\psi_{t}(u,u^{\prime})=g(u-u^{\prime})+\lambda\int_{0}^{t}dt_{1}\,g(u-t_{1})\psi_{t}(t_{1},u^{\prime}).

(38)

Comparing Eqs. (
36
) and (
38
) we get

\frac{dC^{+}}{dt}=-\lambda\psi_{t}(t,t),~~~C^{+}(0)=0.

(39)

The numerical method proceeds in two steps: the first step is to obtain
\psi_{t}(u,u^{\prime})
by solving the quasi-Dyson equation and the second step is to integrate Eq. (
39
). Since we only need the special case,
u^{\prime}=t
, it suffices to solve for
\psi_{t}(u,t)
, which can be expressed by splitting the time ordered Green functions into lesser/greater Green functions as

\displaystyle\psi_{t}(u,t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle g^{<}(u-t)+\lambda\left[\int_{0}^{u}dt_{1}\,g^{>}(u-t_{1})\psi_{t}(t_{1},t)\right.

\displaystyle\left.+\int_{u}^{t}dt_{1}\,g^{<}(u-t_{1})\psi_{t}(t_{1},t)\right].

(40)

The functions
g^{>}(t)
and
g^{<}(t)
are respectively obtained by taking a numerical inverse Fourier-transform of Eq. (
34
) and (
35
) with
\epsilon_{\rm{d}}
replaced by
\epsilon_{\rm{d}}-\lambda/2
.
Discretizing the time-interval,
[0,t]
, to a regular grid of step
dt=t/N
:
\{u_{0},\ldots,u_{N}\}
, and denoting
\psi_{t}(u_{i},t)\to h(u_{i})
, the above equation becomes

\displaystyle h(u_{i})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle g^{<}(u_{i}-t)+\lambda\left[\sum_{r=0}^{i-1}\int_{u_{r}}^{u_{r+1}}dv\,g^{>}(u_{i}-v)h(v)\right.

\displaystyle\left.+\sum_{r=i}^{N-1}\int_{u_{r}}^{u_{r+1}}dv\,g^{<}(u_{i}-v)h(v)\right].

(41)

The integrals can be performed by any quadrature and the discretization of the grid is chosen according to the desired accuracy. Specifically we choose the following quadrature, written compactly as,

\displaystyle\int_{u_{r}}^{u_{r+1}}dv\,g(u_{i}-v)h(v)=\frac{dt}{3}(g_{i-r-1}h_{r+1}+g_{i-r}h_{r})

\displaystyle+\frac{dt}{6}(g_{i-r-1}h_{r}+g_{i-r}h_{r+1})+O(dt^{2}),

(42)

which is the result of approximating
g
and
h
locally as linear functions.
Eq. (
B
) then becomes a linear problem
\sum_{j}M_{ij}h(u_{j})=g^{<}(u_{i}-t)
which is solved efficiently with the GMRES algorithm.
We exploit the fact that the matrix
M
is almost of Toeplitz form (up to two rows and two columns) to perform fast matrix-vector product with the Fast Fourier Transform algorithm.

Note that to obtain
\psi_{t}(u,t)
for different
t
, a linear problem has to be solved separately for each
t
. For this, another grid discretization is applied in the interval
[0,t_{max}]
, with
t_{max}
chosen such that
A^{\pm}(t>t_{max})
is given by a simple exponential decay, which always happens due to a finite bias or temperature. The step size is defined adaptively to ensure that a desired accuracy is reached all along the interval.
The function
\psi_{t}(t,t)
obtained by solving the quasi-Dyson equation for each
t
in the grid is then used to numerically integrate Eq. (
39
) with a Simpson rule. Finally a numerical Fourier transform is applied for
A^{\pm}(t)
to obtain
A^{\pm}(\omega)
, taking care to properly account for the exponential decay for
t>t_{max}
.

We typically neglect any Hartree shift. It enters as Im
(C^{\pm}(t))=\Delta_{H}t
in the calculation. We simply subtract out this linear contribution and then take the numerical Fourier transform.

B.1
Particle-hole transformation

Here we show that, a particle-hole transformation relates
A^{\pm}(t)
for QDD levels
\epsilon_{\rm{d}}
and
-\epsilon_{\rm{d}}
.
It is convenient to express
A^{+}(t)
using the many-body formulation

\displaystyle A^{+}(t)={\rm{Tr}}\{\rho_{0}e^{iH_{0}t}e^{-iH_{1}t}\}={\rm{Tr}}\{e^{-\beta H}c(t)c^{\dagger}\},

(43)

where
H=H_{0}cc^{\dagger}+H_{1}c^{\dagger}c
.
Now we want to express

\displaystyle A^{+}(t)|_{-\epsilon_{{\rm{d}}}}={\rm{Tr}}\{e^{-\beta H|_{-\epsilon_{{\rm{d}}}}}c(t)c^{\dagger}\}

(44)

in terms of the detector spectral functions at
+\epsilon_{{\rm{d}}}
. For this purpose, we apply the particle hole transformation

d\to d^{\dagger},~~~c\to c^{\dagger},~~~\epsilon_{{\rm{d}}}\to-\epsilon_{{\rm{d}}}.

(45)

This gives

\displaystyle A^{+}(t)|_{-\epsilon_{{\rm{d}}}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\rm{Tr}}\{e^{-\beta H|_{+\epsilon_{{\rm{d}}}}}c^{\dagger}(t)c\}

(46)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\rm{Tr}}\{e^{-\beta H|_{+\epsilon_{{\rm{d}}}}}c^{\dagger}(0)c(-t)\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle A^{-}(-t)|_{\epsilon_{{\rm{d}}}}.

In the last equation we used the many-body formulation of
A^{-}(t)
,

\displaystyle A^{-}(t)={\rm{Tr}}\{\rho_{1}e^{iH_{0}t}e^{-iH_{1}t}\}={\rm{Tr}}\{e^{-\beta H}c^{\dagger}(0)c(t)\}.

(47)

Therefore

\displaystyle A^{+}(E)|_{-\epsilon_{{\rm{d}}}}=\int dte^{iEt}A^{+}(t)|_{-\epsilon_{{\rm{d}}}}=\int dte^{iEt}A^{-}(-t)|_{+\epsilon_{{\rm{d}}}}

\displaystyle=\int dte^{-iEt}A^{-}(t)|_{+\epsilon_{{\rm{d}}}}=A^{-}(-E)|_{+\epsilon_{{\rm{d}}}}.

(48)

Appendix C
Analytical form in the wide band limit

In this section, we refer to analytic formulas for
A^{\pm}(t)
in the wide-band-limit (to keep notation concise, we drop the subscript
\rm{WBL}
in all subsequent expressions)
Aleiner et al. 1997

A^{\pm}(t)=\left(\frac{\pi T}{\pm i\xi\sinh(\pi Tt)}\right)^{\alpha}e^{-\Gamma_{\varphi}|t|+\gamma h(t)},~~~(t\gg 1/\xi).

(49)

Here the high energy cut-off
\xi\sim\Gamma_{\rm{d}}
is introduced to cure the short-time divergence. The function
h(t)
is given by
h(t)=\int_{0}^{t}d\tau\frac{\pi^{2}T^{2}\tau(1-\cos(V\tau))}{\sinh^{2}(\pi T\tau)}
. We can see that in the WBL
A^{-}(t)=A^{+}(-t)
(and
A^{\pm}(-t)=(A^{\pm}(t))^{*}
by definition) yielding
A^{+}(\omega)=A^{-}(\omega)
. As emphasized, this is not a general property, which is violated by finite bandwidth effects such as finite
\Gamma_{\rm{d}}
. Taking the Fourier transform neglecting
h(t)
, we get,

A^{\pm}(\omega)\xrightarrow{|\omega|<\xi}\chi(\alpha)\cdot\,{\rm{Re}}\left[\frac{e^{\pm i\pi\alpha/2}\Gamma(\frac{i\omega+\Gamma_{\varphi}}{2\pi T}+\frac{\alpha}{2})}{\Gamma(1+\frac{i\omega+\Gamma_{\varphi}}{2\pi T}-\frac{\alpha}{2})}\right],

(50)

with
\chi(\alpha)=\frac{2^{\alpha}}{\xi}\left(\frac{\pi T}{\xi}\right)^{\alpha-1}\Gamma(1-\alpha)
.

Appendix D
Perturbative calculation of
A^{\pm}(t)
and connection to
P(E)
theory

We consider a generic model
Aleiner et al. 1997
where the detector is governed by a Hamiltonians
H_{n}
depending on the charge state
n=\{0,1\}
of the QD,

\displaystyle H_{0}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k}\epsilon_{k}\left[L_{k}^{\dagger}L_{k}+R_{k}^{\dagger}R_{k}\right],

\displaystyle H_{1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle H_{0}+\lambda_{1}\sum_{k_{1},k_{2}}(L_{k_{1}}^{\dagger}L_{k_{2}}+R^{\dagger}_{k_{1}}R_{k_{2}})

(51)

\displaystyle+\lambda_{LR}\sum_{k_{1},k_{2}}(L^{\dagger}_{k_{1}}R_{k_{2}}+h.c.),

written in the basis of scattering eigenstates for the case,
n=0
. Here, for simplicity, we consider the spinless case and so
m=1
. The
L
and
R
states have chemical potentials
\mu_{L},\mu_{R}
with
\mu_{R}-\mu_{L}=V
. For the
n=1
charge state, the additional potential at the detector induces both forward scattering and backscatternig between the eigenstates of
H_{0}
. Eq. (
49
) was derived within this model. Below we outline the essence of that calculation emphasizing the relation to
P(E)
theory.

We have
A^{+}(t)=e^{C^{+}(t)}
, with
C^{+}(t)
given by Eq. (
36
). We are interested in calculating
C^{+}(t)
up to quadratic order in the scattering coefficients. Note that the first order term could lead to a Hartree shift, which we ignore. The second order term is (we drop the
+
superscript and use the shortcut,
\int_{1}=\int_{0}^{t}dt_{1}
),

C^{(2)}(t)=-\frac{\lambda_{1}^{2}}{2}\sum_{i=L/R}\int_{1}\int_{2}g_{i}(t_{1}-t_{2})g_{i}(t_{2}-t_{1})-\frac{\lambda_{LR}^{2}}{2}\left[\int_{1}\int_{2}g_{L}(t_{1}-t_{2})g_{R}(t_{2}-t_{1})+\int_{1}\int_{2}g_{R}(t_{1}-t_{2})g_{L}(t_{2}-t_{1})\right].

(52)

We now write the time-ordered Green functions in terms of greater and lesser Green functions,

g(\tau)=\theta(\tau)g^{>}(\tau)+\theta(-\tau)g^{<}(\tau),

(53)

and obtain,

C^{(2)}(t)=-\sum_{\{i,j\}=\{L,R\}}\lambda_{ij}^{2}\int_{0}^{t}dt_{1}\int_{0}^{t_{1}}dt_{2}\,g_{i}^{>}(t_{1}-t_{2})g_{j}^{<}(t_{2}-t_{1}),

(54)

where we defined
\lambda_{LL}=\lambda_{RR}=\lambda_{1}
and
\lambda_{RL}=\lambda_{LR}
. Next, writing the time domain Green functions as inverse Fourier transforms of the frequency domain ones, we obtain,

\displaystyle C^{(2)}(t)=-\sum_{\{i,j\}=\{L,R\}}\frac{\lambda_{ij}^{2}}{4\pi^{2}}\left[\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}d\Omega\,\chi_{ij}(\Omega)\right.

\displaystyle\left.\int_{0}^{t}dt_{1}\int_{0}^{t_{1}}dt_{2}\,e^{i\Omega(t_{1}-t_{2})}\right],

(55)

where

\chi_{ij}(\Omega)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}d\omega\,g_{i}^{>}(\omega)g_{j}^{<}(\omega+\Omega).

(56)

The time integrals in Eq. (
D
) give

\int_{0}^{t}dt_{1}\int_{0}^{t_{1}}dt_{2}\,e^{i\Omega(t_{1}-t_{2})}=\frac{it}{\Omega}+\frac{1-e^{i\Omega t}}{\Omega^{2}}.

(57)

The first term only contributes to the Hartree shift and we neglect it. Then,

C^{(2)}(t)=-\sum_{\{i,j\}=\{L,R\}}\frac{\lambda_{ij}^{2}}{4\pi^{2}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}d\Omega\,\chi_{ij}(\Omega)\frac{1-e^{i\Omega t}}{\Omega^{2}}.

(58)

In the wide band limit with an energy independent DoS,
\nu
, the local Green function is

g_{j}^{>}(\omega)=-2\pi\nu i(1-f(\omega-\mu_{j})),\quad g_{j}^{<}(\omega)=2\pi\nu if(\omega-\mu_{j}).

(59)

Plugging it in Eq. (
56
) we obtain

\chi_{ij}(\Omega)=4\pi^{2}\nu^{2}\frac{\Omega+\mu_{i}-\mu_{j}}{-1+\exp[\frac{\Omega+\mu_{i}-\mu_{j}}{T}]}.

(60)

We now make the connection to the
P(E)
description in Ref.
Aguado and Kouwenhoven 2000
. For this, compare our expressions in Eqs. (
58
) and (
60
) with analogous expressions, equations (3) and (7) in Ref.
Aguado and Kouwenhoven 2000
. They are identical once the impedance function
Z(\omega)
is assumed to be frequency independent (which is usually assumed in the applications of

P(E)

Now we proceed with the evaluation of the integral in Eq. (
58
). Let us consider the case of
T=0
, for which Eq. (
60

\chi_{ij}(\Omega)\xrightarrow{T=0}4\pi^{2}\nu^{2}(\mu_{j}-\mu_{i}-\Omega)\Theta(\mu_{j}-\mu_{i}-\Omega).

(61)

Let us split,
C^{(2)}(t)=C_{1}(t)+C_{2}(t)
where
C_{1}(t)=C_{LL}(t)+C_{RR}(t)
and
C_{2}(t)=C_{LR}(t)+C_{RL}(t)
. Let us also define
g_{1/LR}=\nu\lambda_{1/LR}
. We get,

C_{1}(t)=2g_{1}^{2}\int_{-\xi}^{0}d\Omega\,\frac{1-e^{i\Omega t}}{\Omega}\xrightarrow{t\gg 1/\xi}-2g_{1}^{2}\log(\xi t),

(62)

where the high-energy cutoff,
\xi

C^{(2)}(t)=-\alpha\log(t),

(63)

with,

\alpha=2g_{1}^{2}.

(64)

For the second term, we have,

\displaystyle C_{2}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-g_{LR}^{2}\left[\int_{-\infty}^{V}d\Omega\,\frac{V-\Omega}{\Omega^{2}}(1-e^{i\Omega t})+\int_{-\infty}^{-V}d\Omega\,\frac{-V-\Omega}{\Omega^{2}}(1-e^{i\Omega t})\right]

(65)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-g_{LR}^{2}\left[V\int_{-V}^{V}d\Omega\,\frac{1-e^{i\Omega t}}{\Omega^{2}}-2\int_{-\infty}^{-V}d\Omega\,\frac{1-e^{i\Omega t}}{\Omega}-\int_{-V}^{V}d\Omega\,\frac{1-e^{i\Omega t}}{\Omega}\right]

(66)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-g_{LR}^{2}\left[2V\int_{0}^{V}d\Omega\,\frac{1-\cos\Omega t}{\Omega^{2}}-2\int_{-\xi}^{0}d\Omega\,\frac{1-e^{i\Omega t}}{\Omega}+2\int_{-V}^{0}d\Omega\,\frac{1-e^{i\Omega t}}{\Omega}+2i\int_{0}^{V}d\Omega\,\frac{\sin\Omega t}{\Omega}\right].

(67)

The imaginary part of the third last term cancels the fourth term and we then have,

C_{2}(t)=-2g_{LR}^{2}\left[A+B+C\right].

(68)

Let us evaluate each term separately:

\displaystyle A

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle Vt\int_{0}^{Vt}dx\,\frac{1-\cos x}{x^{2}}\xrightarrow{t\gg 1/V}\frac{\pi}{2}Vt,

(69)

\displaystyle B

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\int_{-\xi t}^{0}dx\,\frac{1-e^{ix}}{x}\xrightarrow{t\gg 1/\xi}\log(\xi t),

(70)

\displaystyle C

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\int_{0}^{Vt}dx\,\frac{1-\cos x}{x}.

(71)

Note that the

of Eq. (

C_{2}(t)\xrightarrow{t\gg 1/V}-\pi g_{LR}^{2}Vt=-\Gamma_{\varphi}t,

(72)

\Gamma_{\varphi}=\pi g_{LR}^{2}V.
