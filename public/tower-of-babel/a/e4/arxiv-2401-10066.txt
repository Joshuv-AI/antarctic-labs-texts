L p L^{p} continuity of eigenprojections for 2-d Dirichlet Laplacians under perturbations of the domain
Ryan L. Acosta Babb, James C. Robinson

Abstract
We generalise results by Lamberti and Lanza de Cristoforis, 2005 concerning the continuity of projections onto eigenspaces of self-adjoint differential operators with compact inverses as the (spatial) domain of the functions is perturbed in ℝ 2 \mathbb{R}^{2} . Our main case of interest is the Dirichlet Laplacian on a square. We extend their results from bounds from H 0 1 H_{0}^{1} to H 0 1 H_{0}^{1} to bounds from L p L^{p} to L p L^{p} , under the assumption that ( − Δ − 1 − z ) − 1 (-\Delta^{-1}-z)^{-1} is L p L^{p} bounded when z z lies outside of the spectrum of − Δ − 1 -\Delta^{-1} . We show that this assumption is met if the initial domain is a square or a rectangle.



1
Introduction

It is a classical consequence of the Uniform Boundeness Principle
that the norm convergence of Fourier series is equivalent to
the uniform boundedness of the operator norms of the partial sums

(see
Grafakos, 2014a
, Chap. 4)
.
In dimensions 2 or higher, Fefferman’s celebrated theorem
(
Fefferman, 1971
)
on the unboundedness of the ball multiplier in
L^{p}

has as a consequence that we cannot choose the partial sums in any way we want,
since convergence is sensitive to the choice of “truncation” for these infinite series.

For concreteness, let us fix our dimension
d=2
and consider the lattice
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
,
which serves to index the Fourier coefficients of a periodic function
f:[0,1]^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
.
Then there are many ways in which we can group these indices in order to determine
the cutoff point for a partial sum of the series

\sum_{(m,n)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}}\widehat{f}(m,n)\mathrm{e}^{2\pi\mathrm{i}(mx+ny)}.

A natural choice would be to truncate by the size of the eigenvalues associated to each
exponential (viewed as an eigenfunction of the Laplacian).
Up to constants, this would mean fixing
N>0
and summing over all
(m,n)
with
m^{2}+n^{2}<N^{2}
.
Geometrically, we are using expanding concentric circles in the lattice to determine the cutoff.
As we mentioned earlier, Fefferman’s theorem shows that this method never yields
L^{p}
convergence
outside the “trivial” case
p=2
.
It is a straightforward extension of this result that other “curved” cutoffs,
such as ellipses, will also fail to yield convergence

(see
Grafakos, 2014b
, Chap. 5 and its exercises for a discussion)
.

On the other hand, taking squares or rectangles to mark the cutoff does work for all
1<p<\infty
,
as is well-known
(see
Grafakos, 2014a
, Chap.  4)
.
Results by
Córdoba, 1977
show that other polygonal regions also generate “good” cutoffs
within the range
4/3<p<4
.

So far we have been interested in the geometry of “frequency space”, i.e. the lattice
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
.
All results above concern functions defined on the torus, i.e. periodic functions on the square.
The scaling properties of Fourier series imply that all results carry over to functions
defined on rectangles
[0,a]\times[0,b]
.
Recently, one of the authors has transferred
L^{p}
convergence results
to functions defined on a certain class of triangular domains
(
Acosta Babb, 2023a
)
.
Another case of interest is functions defined on the disc,
which, to the best of our knowledge, remains open

(see
Acosta Babb, 2023b
, for a discussion of some of the issues)
.

One way in which we could explore the issue of convergence in other planar domains
is by perturbation.
The idea is to take a planar region
\Omega
and deform it into another region
\widetilde{\Omega}
.
We then study how the eigenfunctions of the Laplacian
and the projection operators onto eigenspaces
change alongside this change to
\Omega
.
The hope is that, for sufficiently small perturbations
we can carry over convergence results on a domain where they are known
(e.g. a square, a triangle)
to new domains where the problem is still unsolved.
The importance of having such
L^{p}
convergence results for problems in PDE
is discussed in
Fefferman et al., 2022
.
They note that a general understanding of
L^{p}
convergence for eigenfunction expansions
is still open, prompting our current efforts.

We now discuss the setup of the perturbation approach in some more detail,
following
Lamberti and Lanza de Cristoforis, 2005
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
be a bounded, open and connected domain in the plane
(so, in particular, the Poincaré inequality holds on
\Omega
).
We perturb
\Omega
by a map
\varphi\colon\Omega\to\widetilde{\Omega}

close to the identity in a sense to be made precise later.
We are interested in relating the Dirichlet eigenproblem on
\widetilde{\Omega}
back to that on
\Omega
.
We wish to compare the two operators by applying them to functions defined on
\Omega
.
Thus, “
\Delta
” will denote the Laplacian acting on
u\in H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)
,
while “
\Delta_{\varphi}
” denotes the Laplacian acting on
u\circ\varphi^{-1}\in H_{0}^{1}(\widetilde{\Omega})
,
and then “pulled back” via
\varphi
to
H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)
.
Thus,
\Delta-\Delta_{\varphi}
is well-defined as an operator acting on functions defined on
\Omega
.

We list the (positive) eigenvalues of
-\Delta
,
including multiplicities
, as

0<\lambda_{1}\leqslant\lambda_{2}\leqslant\lambda_{3}\leqslant\ldots.

Similarly, we denote the eigenvalues of
-\Delta_{\varphi}
by “
\widetilde{\lambda}_{j}
”.

Note that, since
0
is not an eigenvalue,
the Laplacian and its inverse yield equivalent eigenvalue problems:

-\Delta u=\lambda u\quad\text{if and only if}\quad-\Delta^{-1}u=\lambda^{-1}u.

(1)

We will therefore concentrate on the latter problem, since the inverse operators
are compact and self-adjoint
(see
Lamberti and Lanza de Cristoforis, 2005
, Lemma 3.5)
.

We will need to impose a further technical condition on the eigenspaces we consider.
Fix a finite subset
F\subset\mathbb{N}
and denote by “
P_{F}
” the orthogonal projection in
L^{2}

onto the eigenfunctions
u_{j}
associated to the
\lambda_{j}
with
j\in F
.
Note, in particular, that
u_{j}\neq u_{j+1}
with
\lambda_{j}=\lambda_{j+1}

if
\lambda_{j}
has a multiplicity of at least 2.

Definition 1
.

We say that
F

splits

an eigenvalue
\lambda
if there are indices
j,k\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\lambda=\lambda_{j}=\lambda_{k}

and
j\in F
but
k\notin F
.

In terms of the projection
P_{F}
, this means that
P_{F}u_{j}=u_{j}
but
P_{F}u_{k}=0

even though
-\Delta u_{j}=\lambda u_{j}
and
-\Delta u_{k}=\lambda u_{k}
.
We need to rule out eigenvalue splitting in order to apply Kato’s projection formula
(see the remark following
Theorem

2
for further details).

In
Section

2
we set up the necessary notation
and discuss the perturbation of the domain in some detail.
In
Section

3
we prove that under certain assumptions on
\varphi
,

F
will not split eigenvalues of
\Delta_{\varphi}
if it did not split eigenvalues of
\Delta
.

Section

4
contains the main estimates.
In
Section

5
we state and prove our main result for the Dirichlet Laplacian:

L^{p}
to
L^{p}
boundedness of the projections under perturbations of the domain
\Omega
,
assuming boundedness of the resolvents of
\Delta^{-1}
.
The brief
Section

6
is devoted to showing that this assumption is met
for square and rectangular domains.

Throughout we will use the notation
A(s)\lesssim B(s)
to mean that there is a constant
C>0
,
which does not depend on
s
, such that

A(s)\leqslant B(s)\quad\text{for all}\quad s.

2
Preliminaries

We begin with a homeomorphism
\varphi\colon\Omega\to\widetilde{\Omega}
and we wish to study
operators on both of these domains. Since these operators act on different
function spaces, e.g.
H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)
and
H_{0}^{1}(\widetilde{\Omega})
,
we cannot compare them directly.

Let
u,v\in H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)
. Their
L^{2}
inner product is

\left\langle{u,v}\right\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)}=\int_{\Omega}uv\,\mathrm{d}{x}.

(2)

On the other hand,
u\circ\varphi^{-1},v\circ\varphi^{-1}\in H_{0}^{1}(\widetilde{\Omega})
,
with
L^{2}
inner product

\displaystyle\left\langle{u\circ\varphi^{-1},v\circ\varphi^{-1}}\right\rangle_{L^{2}(\widetilde{\Omega})}

\displaystyle=\int_{\varphi(\Omega)}u(\varphi^{-1}(y))v(\varphi^{-1}(y))\,\mathrm{d}{y}

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}u(x)v(x)\left|\det{D\varphi}(x)\right|\,\mathrm{d}{x}=:Q_{\varphi}[u,v].

(3)

If we denote the standard inner product on
L^{2}(\Omega)
by “
Q
”,
then we can compare the inner products from
Eqs.

2
and
2
:

(Q-Q_{\varphi})[u,v]=\int_{\Omega}uv(1-\left|\det{D\varphi}\right|)\,\mathrm{d}{x}\quad\text{for all}\quad u,v\in H_{0}^{1}(\Omega).

(4)

We begin by recording a key result in
Lamberti and Lanza de Cristoforis, 2005
which we are going to generalise in this paper.
Let
H
be a Hilbert space equipped with two different bilinear forms,
Q
and
\widetilde{Q}
.
Suppose that
T
and
\widetilde{T}
are two compact operators from
H
to itself
and self-adjoint on
(H,Q)
and
(H,\widetilde{Q})
, respectively.
Let
F\subset\mathbb{N}
be a finite set of indices and denote by
P_{F}[Q,T]
the projection

P_{F}[Q,T]u:=\sum_{j\in F}Q[u,u_{j}]u_{j}

where
u_{j}
are eigenfunctions of
T
corresponding to the indices
j\in F
.

Theorem 2

(
Lamberti and Lanza de Cristoforis, 2005
)
.

Let
F\subset\mathbb{N}
be a finite set of indices that does not split eigenvalues of
T
or
\widetilde{T}
.
Then, there is a positive constant
C=C(T,\widetilde{T},F)
such that

\left\lVert P_{F}[Q,T]-P_{F}[\widetilde{Q},\widetilde{T}]\right\rVert_{H\to H}\leqslant C\left(\left\lVert Q-\widetilde{Q}\right\rVert_{H\times H\to\mathbb{R}}+\left\lVert T-\widetilde{T}\right\rVert_{H\to H}\right).

(5)

Remark
.

The main tool in the proof is Kato’s integral formula for a projection:

P_{F}[Q,T]=\mathrm{Re}\left(-\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\int_{\gamma[F,T]}(T-zI)^{-1}\,\mathrm{d}{z}\right).

Here
\gamma[F,T]
is a contour encircling the portion of the spectrum of
T

which contains the eigenvalues in
F
.

As we can see, the integral formula is “blind” to multiplicities:
it only differentiates the values of the eigenvalues, and there is no way
of keeping track of exactly which eigenfunctions we want to keep in our projection.
This is the reason behind the no-splitting condition, since if our set
F
were to split
an eigenvalue, then Kato’s formula would not actually give us the projection
P_{F}[T,Q]
,
but rather a projection onto a larger space which includes all eigenfunctions for every eigenvalue “in
F
”.

We will use a similar argument in which
T=\Delta^{-1}
and
\widetilde{T}

is a pullback to
\Omega
of the inverse Laplacian on
\widetilde{\Omega}
.
Thus, there are three Laplacians in play here:
the Laplacian
\Delta=\Delta_{\Omega}
acting on
C_{c}^{\infty}(\Omega)
;
the Laplacian
\Delta_{\widetilde{\Omega}}
acting on
C_{c}^{\infty}(\widetilde{\Omega})
;
and a transformed Laplacian
\Delta_{\varphi}
acting also on
C_{c}^{\infty}(\Omega)
as follows:

\Delta_{\varphi}f(x):=(\Delta_{\widetilde{\Omega}}(f\circ\varphi^{-1}))(\varphi(x))\quad\text{for all}\quad f\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\Omega).

That is: the function
f\circ\varphi^{-1}
is defined on
\widetilde{\Omega}
,
which we then differentiate using
\Delta_{\widetilde{\Omega}}
,
and finally pull back to
\Omega
by composing the result with
\varphi
.

We will denote the eigenfunctions of
\Delta_{\Omega}
and
\Delta_{\widetilde{\Omega}}

respectively by
u_{j}
and
\widetilde{u}_{j}
.
The eigenfunctions of
\Delta_{\varphi}
are
u^{\varphi}_{j}:=\widetilde{u}_{j}\circ\varphi

(see
Lamberti and Lanza de Cristoforis, 2005
, Theorem 3.10)
.

We are now in a position to distinguish three “partial sum” operators of interest:

(i)

Partial sums on
\Omega
, which are expansions in
L^{2}(\Omega)

in terms of the eigenfunctions
u_{j}
of
\Delta_{\Omega}
relative to
\Omega
:

P_{F}f=\sum_{j\in F}\left\langle{u_{j},f}\right\rangle_{\Omega}u_{j}\quad\text{for all}\quad f\in L^{2}(\Omega).

(6)

(ii)

Partial sums on
\widetilde{\Omega}
, which are expansions in
L^{2}(\widetilde{\Omega})

in terms of the eigenfunctions
\widetilde{u}_{j}
of
\Delta_{\widetilde{\Omega}}
relative to
\widetilde{\Omega}
:

\widetilde{P}_{F}g=\sum_{j\in F}\left\langle{\widetilde{u}_{j},g}\right\rangle_{\widetilde{\Omega}}\widetilde{u}_{j}\quad\text{for all}\quad g\in L^{2}(\widetilde{\Omega}).

(7)

(iii)

Transformed partial sums on
\Omega
which are “pull-backs” of
\widetilde{S}_{F}
to
L^{2}(\Omega)
.
These are expansions with respect to the eigenfunctions
u^{\varphi}_{j}
of
\Delta_{\varphi}
in
L^{2}(\Omega)
,
now equipped with the inner product
Q_{\varphi}
:

P^{\varphi}_{F}f=\sum_{j\in F}Q_{\varphi}[u^{\varphi}_{j},f]u^{\varphi}_{j}\quad\text{for all}\quad f\in L^{2}(\Omega).

(8)

Most of the work will go into obtaining bounds
\left\lVert T-\widetilde{T}\right\rVert_{L^{p}({\Omega})}

which depend (i) on the change of variables map
\varphi\colon\Omega\to\widetilde{\Omega}
,
and (ii) on the set
F
.
In order to do so, we first need to make sense of
\Delta^{-1}
as a bounded operator on
L^{p}
.

2.1
The inverse of the Laplacian

Under certain assumptions on the regularity of
\Omega
,
the Laplacian

\Delta\colon W^{2,p}(\Omega)\cap W^{1,p}_{0}(\Omega)\to L^{p}(\Omega)

is invertible; see, for example, Theorem 9.15 in
Gilbarg and Trudinger, 2001
.

Theorem 3
.

Let
\Omega
be a
C^{1,1}
domain in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
.
Then, if
f\in L^{p}(\Omega)
with
1<p<\infty
, the Dirichlet problem
\Delta u=f
in
\Omega

with
u\in W_{0}^{1,p}(\Omega)
has a unique solution
u\in W^{2,p}(\Omega)
.

A similar result also holds for a square, as follows from Theorem 4.4.3.7 in
Grisvard, 1985

(whose statement we slightly adapt).

Theorem 4
.

Assume that
\Omega
is a polygon with straight boundary edges meeting at right angles.
Then, for each
f\in L^{p}(\Omega)
there is a unique solution
u\in W^{2,p}(\Omega)\cap W_{0}^{1,p}(\Omega)

to the Poisson problem
\Delta u=f
with zero boundary conditions.

These are the more “obvious” examples; for a discussion on more general conditions on
\partial\Omega

for the
W^{2,p}
-solvability of the Poisson equation with
L^{p}
initial data,
see Chapter 7 of
Maz’ya and Shaposhnikova, 1985
.

In other words: for squares, rectangles and
C^{1,1}
domains (among others),
the Dirichlet Laplacian is invertible with inverse
\Delta^{-1}\colon L^{p}\to W^{2,p}\cap W_{0}^{1,p}
.

For brevity, we will henceforth write
X_{p}
for the Banach space
W^{2,p}\cap W_{0}^{1,p}

equipped with the
W^{2,p}
norm.

Definition 5
.

Let
\varphi\colon\Omega\to\widetilde{\Omega}
be an invertible map
with bounded weak derivatives up to second order.
Furthermore, we assume that

\inf_{\Omega}\left|\det{D\varphi}\right|>0.

We call the triple
(\Omega,\varphi,\widetilde{\Omega})

p
-admissible

if
\varphi
is as above and
\Delta\colon X_{p}\to L^{p}
is invertible
on both
\Omega
and
\widetilde{\Omega}
.
We will often simplify this to “
\varphi\colon\Omega\to\widetilde{\Omega}
is
p
-admissible”
or even “
\varphi
is
p
-admissible”.

Remark
.

We require one more order of differentiation than
Lamberti and Lanza de Cristoforis, 2005
,
as well as the invertibility of
\Delta
from
X_{p}
to
L^{p}
,
which comes “for free” on bounded domains in
\mathbb{R}^{2}

when working with
\Delta\colon H^{1}_{0}\to H^{-1}
.

Henceforth, we will assume that
(\Omega,\varphi,\widetilde{\Omega})
is
p
-admissible.

Definition 6
.

Let
Y(\cdot)
be a function space on
\Omega
and
\widetilde{\Omega}
, and let
\varphi\colon\Omega\to\widetilde{\Omega}

be a change of coordinates.
We define the following operators:

\displaystyle C_{\varphi}\colon Y(\widetilde{\Omega})

\displaystyle\longrightarrow Y(\Omega)

\displaystyle v\quad

\displaystyle\longmapsto v\circ\varphi

and

\displaystyle C_{\varphi^{-1}}\colon Y(\Omega)

\displaystyle\longrightarrow Y(\widetilde{\Omega})

\displaystyle u\quad

\displaystyle\longmapsto u\circ\varphi.

Remark
.

Note that
(C_{\varphi})^{-1}=C_{\varphi^{-1}}
and vice versa.

We will denote by
T
the inverse Laplacian

T=\Delta^{-1}\equiv(\Delta_{\Omega})^{-1}\colon L^{p}(\Omega)\to X_{p}(\Omega)\hookrightarrow L^{p}(\Omega).

We will also denote by
T_{\varphi}
the inverse of
\Delta_{\varphi}
, i.e. the map

T_{\varphi}=C_{\varphi}\circ\Delta_{\widetilde{\Omega}}^{-1}\circ C_{\varphi^{-1}}\colon L^{p}(\Omega)\to L^{p}(\Omega).

Thus, we have the following diagram:

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces X_{p}(\widetilde{\Omega})}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces L^{p}(\widetilde{\Omega})}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces L^{p}(\Omega)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces X_{p}(\Omega)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces L^{p}(\Omega)}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\Delta_{\widetilde{\Omega}}^{-1}}

\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\Delta_{\Omega}^{-1}}

where
T_{\varphi}
is the “up–over–down” path from
L^{p}(\Omega)
to
L^{p}(\Omega)
.

We now introduce a slightly modified version of the operator
\Delta_{\varphi}
.

Definition 7
.

Let
\mathcal{L}_{\varphi}\colon L^{p}(\Omega)\to L^{p}(\Omega)
be the operator defined by

\mathcal{L}_{\varphi}:=C_{\varphi^{-1}}^{t}\circ\Delta_{\widetilde{\Omega}}\circ C_{\varphi^{-1}},

where
C_{\varphi^{-1}}^{t}\colon L^{p}(\widetilde{\Omega})\to L^{p}(\Omega)
is the operator defined, for
v\in L^{p}(\widetilde{\Omega})
, by

\left\langle{C_{\varphi^{-1}}^{t}v,w}\right\rangle:=\left\langle{v,C_{\varphi^{-1}}w}\right\rangle\equiv\int_{\widetilde{\Omega}}v(y)w(\varphi^{-1}(y))\,\mathrm{d}{y}\quad\text{for all}\quad w\in L^{q}(\Omega).

Lemma 8
.

The operator
C_{\varphi^{-1}}^{t}
is given by

\left[C_{\varphi^{-1}}^{t}v\right](x)=\left|\det{D\varphi(x)}\right|[C_{\varphi}v](x)\quad\text{for all}\quad v\in L^{p}(\widetilde{\Omega}),

with inverse

\left[(C_{\varphi^{-1}}^{t})^{-1}u\right](y)=C_{\varphi}^{-1}\left(\frac{u(x)}{\left|\det{D\varphi(x)}\right|}\right)(y)\quad\text{for all}\quad u\in L^{p}(\Omega).

In particular,

(C_{\varphi^{-1}}^{t})^{-1}=C_{\varphi}^{t}.

(9)

Definition 9
.

Define the operator
\mathcal{J}_{\varphi}\colon L^{p}(\Omega)\to L^{p}(\Omega)
by

\mathcal{J}_{\varphi}u(x):=\left|\det{D\varphi(x)}\right|u(x)\quad\text{for all}\quad u\in L^{p}(\Omega).

If
\inf_{\Omega}\left|\det{D\varphi}\right|>0
, then

\mathcal{J}_{\varphi}^{-1}u(x)=\frac{u(x)}{\left|\det{D\varphi(x)}\right|}.

We can therefore conclude the following.

Corollary 10
.

We have
T_{\varphi}=\mathcal{L}_{\varphi}^{-1}\circ\mathcal{J}_{\varphi}
.

Proof.

First, note that
\mathcal{L}_{\varphi}=C_{\varphi^{-1}}^{t}\circ\Delta_{\widetilde{\Omega}}\circ C_{\varphi^{-1}}

implies that

\displaystyle(\mathcal{L}_{\varphi})^{-1}

\displaystyle=(C_{\varphi^{-1}})^{-1}\circ(\Delta_{\widetilde{\Omega}})^{-1}\circ(C_{\varphi^{-1}}^{t})^{-1}

\displaystyle=C_{\varphi}\circ\Delta_{\widetilde{\Omega}}^{-1}\circ C_{\varphi}^{-1}\circ\mathcal{J}_{\varphi}^{-1}

\displaystyle=(\Delta_{\varphi})^{-1}\circ\mathcal{J}_{\varphi}^{-1},

and so composing with
\mathcal{J}_{\varphi}
finishes the proof.
∎

These rather formal calculations are justified by the admissibility conditions on
\varphi
.

Remark
.

The crucial detail to observe is that we factor the (transformed) inverse Laplacian

T_{\varphi}
into two operators:
the inverse “Laplacian”
\mathcal{L}_{\varphi}^{-1}\colon L^{p}(\Omega)\to L^{p}(\Omega)

and the embedding
\mathcal{J}_{\varphi}\colon L^{p}(\Omega)\to L^{p}(\Omega)
.
This circuitous route, which is inspired by
Lamberti and Lanza de Cristoforis, 2005
,
will vastly simplify our proof of
19
below.
Essentially, we will need to transfer integration by parts
from
\Omega
to
\widetilde{\Omega}
.
If we were simply to use
\Delta_{\varphi}
, we would have

\displaystyle\left\langle{\Delta_{\varphi}u,w}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\left\langle{C_{\varphi}\Delta_{\widetilde{\Omega}}C_{\varphi^{-1}}u,w}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}(\Delta_{\widetilde{\Omega}}(u\circ\varphi^{-1}))(\varphi(x))w(x)\,\mathrm{d}{x}

\displaystyle=\int_{\widetilde{\Omega}}\Delta_{\widetilde{\Omega}}(u\circ\varphi^{-1})(y)w(\varphi^{-1}(y))\left|\det D\varphi^{-1}(y)\right|\,\mathrm{d}{y}

\displaystyle=-\int_{\widetilde{\Omega}}D_{y}(u\circ\varphi^{-1})(y)\cdot D_{y}\left[(w\circ\varphi^{-1})\left|\det{D\varphi^{-1}(y)}\right|\right]\,\mathrm{d}{y}.

Using the operator
\mathcal{L}_{\varphi}
instead will allow us to circumvent differentiating the determinant,
which is then reintroduced by
\mathcal{J}_{\varphi}
in a more convenient place.

3
Continuity and splitting of eigenvalues

In order to apply Kato’s projectio formula, we must assume that the set
F

does not split eigenvalues of either operator.
In this section, we show that, under reasonable assumptions on
\varphi
,
it is enough to assume that
F
does not split eigenvalues of
\Delta
on
\Omega
.
For in this case, continuity results of the eigenvalues with respect to changes of the domain
will ensure that
F
will not split the eigenvalues of
\Delta_{\widetilde{\Omega}}
on
\widetilde{\Omega}
.
The next lemma guarantees that
\Delta_{\widetilde{\Omega}}
and its pull-back
to
\Omega
have the same eigenvalues.
Thus, if
F
does not split eigenvalues of
T
, then
it will not split eigenvalues of
T_{\varphi}
,
and we can apply Kato’s projection formula.
(Recall that
\lambda>0
is an eigenvalue of
-\Delta
if, and only if,

\frac{1}{\lambda}>0
is an eigenvalue of
-\Delta^{-1}
.)

Lemma 11
.

Let
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
and
v\in L^{2}(\widetilde{\Omega})\setminus\{0\}
.
Then,

\Delta_{\widetilde{\Omega}}^{-1}v=\lambda v\quad\text{if, and only if,}\quad T_{\varphi}(C_{\varphi}v)=\lambda C_{\varphi}v.

Proof.

Immediate from the definitions; see Theorem 3.10 in
Lamberti and Lanza de Cristoforis, 2005
.
∎

Lemma

11
tells us that the eigenvalues of
\Delta_{\widetilde{\Omega}}^{-1}

remain unchanged after we “pull them back to
\Omega
”—that is the purpose of
T_{\varphi}
.

We next check that the eigenvalues of
\Delta^{-1}

are close to the eigenvalues of
\Delta_{\widetilde{\Omega}}^{-1}
.
This we do via a theorem due to
Courant and Hilbert, 1989
(see p. 423).

Theorem 12
.

Let
\varphi:\Omega\to\widetilde{\Omega}
be a bijection with
\inf_{x\in\Omega}\left|\det{D\varphi}\right|>0
.
Then, for any
\eta>0
there is an
\varepsilon>0
such that if

\left\lVert\operatorname{id}-\varphi\right\rVert_{W^{1,\infty}({\Omega})}\leqslant\varepsilon,

then

\left|\frac{\widetilde{\lambda}_{j}}{\lambda_{j}}-1\right|<\eta\quad\text{for all}\quad j\in\mathbb{N}.

Remark
.

Strictly speaking, this result applies to
-\Delta
, not
-\Delta^{-1}
.
But we have seen that the eigenvalue problems for both operators are equivalent
(see
Eq.

1
and the surrounding discussion in the Introduction).
The proof of this result uses the variational formulation of the eigenvalue problem.
As
Lamberti and Lanza de Cristoforis, 2005
point out, the “variational eigenvalues” need not coincide
with the true eigenvalues.
However, this is the case in bounded domains.
(More generally, when the embedding
H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)\hookrightarrow L^{2}(\Omega)
is compact;
see their discussion on p. 289.)

Lemma 13
.

Let
j,k\in F
be such that
\lambda_{j}\neq\lambda_{k}
.
Assume that

\gamma:=\inf\{\lambda_{l}-\lambda_{m}:l>m>0\}>0.

Then,
\left|\widetilde{\lambda}_{j}-\widetilde{\lambda}_{k}\right|>\gamma/2
,
provided that
\left\lVert\operatorname{id}-\varphi\right\rVert_{W^{1,\infty}({\Omega})}
is small enough.

Proof.

Without loss of generality, we may assume that
\lambda_{j}>\lambda_{k}
,
and set
\delta:=\lambda_{j}-\lambda_{k}\geqslant\gamma
.
Note that

\widetilde{\lambda}_{j}-\widetilde{\lambda}_{k}=\lambda_{j}\cdot\frac{\widetilde{\lambda}_{j}}{\lambda_{j}}-\lambda_{k}\cdot\frac{\widetilde{\lambda}_{k}}{\lambda_{k}}=(\lambda_{j}-\lambda_{k})\cdot\frac{\widetilde{\lambda}_{j}}{\lambda_{j}}+\lambda_{k}\left(\frac{\widetilde{\lambda}_{j}}{\lambda_{j}}-\frac{\widetilde{\lambda}_{k}}{\lambda_{k}}\right).

By
Theorem

12
,

1-\eta<\frac{\widetilde{\lambda}_{j}}{\lambda_{j}}<1+\eta,\quad\left|\frac{\widetilde{\lambda}_{j}}{\lambda_{j}}-\frac{\widetilde{\lambda}_{k}}{\lambda_{k}}\right|<2\eta\quad\text{and}\quad\left|\lambda_{k}\right|\leqslant\max_{l\in F}\left|\lambda_{l}\right|.

Hence,

\widetilde{\lambda}_{j}-\widetilde{\lambda}_{k}\geqslant(\lambda_{j}-\lambda_{k})(1-\eta)-2\max_{l\in F}\left|\lambda_{l}\right|\eta\geqslant\gamma-\eta(\delta+2\max_{l\in F}\left|\lambda_{l}\right|),

which is larger than
\gamma/2
, provided that
\eta<\frac{\gamma}{\delta+2\max_{l\in F}\left|\lambda_{l}\right|}
.
∎

Since there are only finitely many indices in
F
, it follows that, for a fixed
F

and a sufficiently small perturbation
\varphi
,
the multiplicities of the eigenvalues of
\Delta_{\widetilde{\Omega}}
(in
F
)
will not decrease with respect to the multiplicities of the eigenvalues of
\Delta
(in
F
).

Combining
Lemma

13
with
Lemma

11
,
we deduce that
F
does not split eigenvalues for
T
nor
T_{\varphi}
.

Corollary 14
.

If
\varphi
is
p
-admissible and

\left\lVert\operatorname{id}-\varphi\right\rVert_{W^{1,\infty}({\Omega})}
sufficiently small,
then
F
will not split eigenvalues of
T
nor
T_{\varphi}
.

4
Bounds on the norms of the operators

4.1
Auxiliary bounds

We need bounds on the operators
C_{\varphi}\colon L^{p}(\widetilde{\Omega})\to L^{p}(\Omega)

and
C_{\varphi}^{t}\colon L^{p}(\Omega)\to L^{p}(\widetilde{\Omega})
.

Lemma 15
.

If
\varphi
is
p
-admissible, then

\left\lVert C_{\varphi}\right\rVert_{L^{p}(\widetilde{\Omega})\to L^{p}(\Omega)}\leqslant\frac{1}{\inf_{\Omega}\left|\det{D\varphi}\right|^{1/p}},

and, with
1/p+1/q=1
,

\left\lVert C_{\varphi}^{t}\right\rVert_{L^{p}(\Omega)\to L^{p}(\widetilde{\Omega})}\leqslant\frac{1}{\inf_{\Omega}\left|\det{D\varphi}\right|^{1/q}}.

(10)

Proof.

For any
v\in L^{p}(\widetilde{\Omega})
we have

\displaystyle\left\lVert C_{\varphi}v\right\rVert_{L^{p}({\Omega})}^{p}

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\left|v(\varphi(x))\right|^{p}\,\mathrm{d}{x}

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\left|v(\varphi(x))\right|^{p}\frac{\left|\det{D\varphi}\right|}{\left|\det{D\varphi}\right|}\,\mathrm{d}{x}

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{1}{\inf_{\Omega}\left|\det{D\varphi}\right|}\int_{\Omega}\left|v(\varphi(x))\right|^{p}\left|\det{D\varphi}\right|\,\mathrm{d}{x}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\inf_{\Omega}\left|\det{D\varphi}\right|}\int_{\widetilde{\Omega}}\left|v(y)\right|^{p}\,\mathrm{d}{y},

by the change of variables formula, and
(
10
) immediately follows by a simple duality argument.
∎

Corollary 16
.

If
\varphi
is
p
-admissible, then

\left\lVert\mathcal{L}_{\varphi}^{-1}\right\rVert_{L^{p}(\Omega)\to L^{p}(\Omega)}\lesssim\frac{1}{\inf_{\Omega}{\left|\det{D\varphi}\right|}}.

Proof.

Recall that, by
Eq.

9
, we have

\mathcal{L}_{\varphi}^{-1}=C_{\varphi}\circ\Delta_{\widetilde{\Omega}}^{-1}\circ C_{\varphi}^{t}.

Hence, the result follows from
Lemma

15
and the estimate

\left\lVert\Delta_{\widetilde{\Omega}}^{-1}\right\rVert_{L^{p}(\widetilde{\Omega})\to L^{p}(\widetilde{\Omega})}\lesssim 1,

by the
p
-admissibility of
\widetilde{\Omega}
.
∎

Next we need some technical lemmas concerning
\varphi
and its derivatives.
We will write
\varphi=\mathrm{id}+(f,g)
to mean

\varphi(x,y)=(x+f(x,y),y+g(x,y)).

We want to consider
\varphi
“close to the identity” in the sense that

\left\lVert\mathrm{id}-\varphi\right\rVert_{W^{2,\infty}({\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{2}})}
is small.
In other words,
f,g
and their derivatives up to second order have small
L^{\infty}
norms.

Lemma 17
.

Suppose that
\left\lVert\mathrm{id}-\varphi_{k}\right\rVert_{W^{1,\infty}({\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{2}})}\to 0
as
k\to\infty
.
Then,

\left\lVert 1-\det{D\varphi_{k}}\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}({\Omega})}\to 0.

Proof.

Let

D\varphi_{k}=\left(\begin{matrix}a_{k}&b_{k}\\
c_{k}&d_{k}\end{matrix}\right).

Then, by assumption,

D\varphi_{k}-I=\left(\begin{matrix}a_{k}-1&b_{k}\\
c_{k}&d_{k}-1\end{matrix}\right)\to 0\quad\text{in}\quad L^{\infty}.

Hence,
\det{(D\varphi_{k}-I)}\to 0

and it follows that
1-\det{(D\varphi_{k}-I)}\to 0
.
∎

Remark
.

Note that we only required
\mathrm{id}-\varphi\in W^{1,\infty}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{2})
for this lemma.
The next result will require convergence in
W^{2,\infty}
.

Lemma 18
.

Suppose that
\left\lVert\mathrm{id}-\varphi_{k}\right\rVert_{W^{2,\infty}({\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{2}})}\to 0
as
k\to\infty

and write

M_{k}:=D\varphi_{k}^{-1}D\varphi_{k}^{-t}\left|\det{D\varphi}\right|-I\equiv\left(\begin{matrix}a_{k}&b_{k}\\
c_{k}&d_{k}\end{matrix}\right).

Then, as
k\to\infty
,

(i)

\left\lVert M_{k}\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}({\Omega})}\to 0
, and

(ii)

\left\lVert\frac{\partial a_{k}}{\partial x}+\frac{\partial b_{k}}{\partial y}\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}({\Omega})}
,

\left\lVert\frac{\partial c_{k}}{\partial x}+\frac{\partial d_{k}}{\partial y}\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}({\Omega})}\to 0
.

Proof.

Recall that
\varphi_{k}=\mathrm{id}+(f_{k},g_{k})
.
For simplicity, we will drop the
k
subscripts on
\varphi
,
f
and
g
,
writing
f_{x}:=\frac{\partial f_{k}}{\partial x}
,
and so on.
Thus, e.g., “
f_{x}\to 0
” will mean “
\frac{\partial f_{k}}{\partial x}\to 0
as
k\to\infty
”.
Note that our assumption means precisely that
f_{x},f_{y},g_{x},g_{y}\to 0
,
as well as all second partial derivatives.

By
Lemma

17
, eventually
\det{D\varphi}>0
a.e.,
so we may drop the absolute value signs.
A simple calculation shows that

D\varphi^{-1}D\varphi^{-t}=\frac{1}{(\det{D\varphi})^{2}}\left(\begin{matrix}g_{x}^{2}+(1+g_{y})^{2}&-(f_{y}+g_{x}+f_{x}g_{y}+f_{y}g_{x})\\
-(f_{y}+g_{x}+f_{x}g_{y}+f_{y}g_{x})&(1+f_{x})^{2}+f_{y}^{2}\end{matrix}\right)

and that

\det{D\varphi}=1+f_{x}+g_{y}+f_{x}g_{y}+f_{y}g_{x}.

Hence, for example, the top-left entry of
M_{k}
is

a_{k}=\frac{g_{x}^{2}+g_{y}^{2}-g_{y}-f_{x}-f_{x}g_{y}-f_{y}g_{x}}{\det{D\varphi}}.

By
Lemma

17
, the denominator remains bounded,
while the numerator vanishes as
k\to\infty
by assumption.
Similar calculations for
b,c
and
d
establish (i).

For (ii), we differentiate the last display with respect to
x
to find

\frac{\partial a_{k}}{\partial x}=\frac{p(Df,Dg,D^{2}f,D^{2}g)}{(\det{D\varphi})^{2}},

where
p
is a homogeneous polynomial in the first and second derivatives of
f
and
g
.
Thus, as above,
\partial a_{k}/\partial x\to 0
. We omit the remaining cases.
∎

4.2
Bounds on
T-T_{\varphi}

With these estimates, we can now control
T-T_{\varphi}
.

Proposition 19
.

If
\varphi\colon\Omega\to\widetilde{\Omega}
is
p
-admissible, then

\displaystyle\left\lVert T-T_{\varphi}\right\rVert_{L^{p}(\Omega)\to L^{p}(\Omega)}\lesssim\frac{1}{\inf_{\Omega}\left|\det{D\varphi}\right|}\left(\left\lVert 1-\left|\det{D\varphi}\right|\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}({\Omega})}+K_{\varphi}\right),

where
K_{\varphi}
is a constant depending on
D\varphi
and its derivatives
which vanishes as
\left\lVert\mathrm{id}-\varphi\right\rVert_{W^{2,\infty}({\Omega})}\to 0
.

Proof.

Fix
u\in L^{p}(\Omega)
.
Recall that
T_{\varphi}=\mathcal{L}_{\varphi}^{-1}\circ\mathcal{J}_{\varphi}
.
Hence, it follows from
16
that

\displaystyle\left\lVert Tu-T_{\varphi}u\right\rVert_{L^{p}({\Omega})}

\displaystyle\leqslant\left\lVert\mathcal{L}_{\varphi}^{-1}\right\rVert_{L^{p}(\Omega)\to L^{p}(\Omega)}\left\lVert\mathcal{J}_{\varphi}u-\mathcal{L}_{\varphi}\Delta^{-1}u\right\rVert_{L^{p}({\Omega})}

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{1}{\inf_{\Omega}\left|\det{D\varphi}\right|}\left\lVert\mathcal{J}_{\varphi}u-\mathcal{L}_{\varphi}\Delta^{-1}u\right\rVert_{L^{p}({\Omega})}.

(11)

To bound the last
L^{p}
norm, we use duality.
For convenience, let
Au:=\mathcal{J}_{\varphi}u-\mathcal{L}_{\varphi}\Delta^{-1}u
,
and compute, for
w\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\Omega)
with
\left\lVert w\right\rVert_{L^{q}({\Omega})}\leqslant 1
:

\displaystyle\left\langle{Au,w}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\left\langle{\mathcal{J}_{\varphi}u,w}\right\rangle-\left\langle{\mathcal{L}_{\varphi}\Delta^{-1}u,w}\right\rangle

\displaystyle\begin{split}&=\int_{\Omega}uw\left|\det{D\varphi}\right|\,\mathrm{d}{x}-\left\langle{\mathcal{L}_{\varphi}\Delta^{-1}u,w}\right\rangle.\end{split}

(12)

We now unravel the definition of
\mathcal{L}_{\varphi}
, integrate by parts and change variables:

\displaystyle\left\langle{\mathcal{L}_{\varphi}[\Delta^{-1}u],w}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\left\langle{C_{\varphi^{-1}}^{t}\Delta_{\widetilde{\Omega}}C_{\varphi}^{-1}[\Delta^{-1}u],w}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\left\langle{\Delta_{\widetilde{\Omega}}C_{\varphi^{-1}}[\Delta^{-1}u],C_{\varphi^{-1}}w}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=-\int_{\widetilde{\Omega}}D_{y}\left[\Delta^{-1}u(\varphi^{-1}(y))\right]\cdot D_{y}\left[w(\varphi^{-1}(y))\right]\,\mathrm{d}{y}

\displaystyle=-\int_{\widetilde{\Omega}}D_{x}(\Delta^{-1}u)(\varphi^{-1}(y))D\varphi^{-1}(y)D\varphi^{-t}(y)(D_{x}w)^{t}(\varphi^{-1}(y))\,\mathrm{d}{y}

\displaystyle=-\int_{\Omega}D(\Delta^{-1}u)D\varphi^{-1}(D\varphi^{-1})^{t}(Dw)^{t}\left|\det{D\varphi}\right|\,\mathrm{d}{x},

where for a matrix
M
,
M^{-t}=(M^{-1})^{t}
, and
Dv\cdot Dw=Dv(Dw)^{t}
.
We will now add and subtract

\pm\int_{\Omega}D(\Delta^{-1}u)\cdot Dw\,\mathrm{d}{x}

from (
4.2
) to obtain:

\displaystyle\left\langle{Au,w}\right\rangle=

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}uw\left|\det{D\varphi}\right|\,\mathrm{d}{x}+\int_{\Omega}D(\Delta^{-1}u)\cdot Dw\,\mathrm{d}{x}

(13)

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\int_{\Omega}D(\Delta^{-1}u)D\varphi^{-1}D\varphi^{-t}(Dw)^{t}\left|\det{D\varphi}\right|\,\mathrm{d}{x}\\
&\quad-\int_{\Omega}D(\Delta^{-1}u)\cdot Dw\,\mathrm{d}{x}.\end{split}

(14)

To estimate (
13
), we integrate by parts in the second summand to see that

(
13
)

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}uw\left|\det{D\varphi}\right|\,\mathrm{d}{x}-\int_{\Omega}\Delta(\Delta^{-1}u)w\,\mathrm{d}{x}

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}uw(\left|\det{D\varphi}\right|-1)\,\mathrm{d}{x}

\displaystyle\leqslant\left\lVert 1-\left|\det{D\varphi}\right|\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}({\Omega})}\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{L^{p}({\Omega})}\left\lVert w\right\rVert_{L^{q}({\Omega})}.

(15)

For the second term, note that

\eqref{eqn:II}=\int_{\Omega}D(\Delta^{-1}u)\left\{D\varphi^{-1}(D\varphi^{-1})^{t}\left|\det{D\varphi}\right|-I\right\}\cdot Dw\,\mathrm{d}{x},

where
I
denotes the
2\times 2
identity matrix.
Writing the expression in braces as

M_{\varphi}=\left(\begin{matrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{matrix}\right)

and integrating by parts yields

\eqref{eqn:II}=-\int_{\Omega}\left[(a_{x}+b_{y},c_{x}+d_{y})\cdot D(\Delta^{-1}u)+M_{\varphi}\cdot D^{2}(\Delta^{-1}u)\right]w\,\mathrm{d}{x},

where
A\cdot B
is the dot product of the matrices
A
and
B
,
seen as vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{4}
.
Then,

(
14
)

\displaystyle\lesssim K_{\varphi}\left(\left\lVert D^{2}(\Delta^{-1}u)\right\rVert_{L^{p}({\Omega})}+\left\lVert D(\Delta^{-1}u)\right\rVert_{L^{p}({\Omega})}\right)\left\lVert w\right\rVert_{L^{q}({\Omega})}

\displaystyle\leqslant K_{\varphi}\left\lVert\Delta^{-1}u\right\rVert_{W^{2,p}({\Omega})}\left\lVert w\right\rVert_{L^{q}({\Omega})}

\displaystyle\lesssim K_{\varphi}\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{L^{p}({\Omega})}\left\lVert w\right\rVert_{L^{q}({\Omega})}.

(16)

The last line follows from the boundedness of
\Delta^{-1}\colon L^{p}(\Omega)\to X_{p}(\Omega)
,
while
K_{\varphi}
is a constant depending on
D\varphi
and its derivatives,
which vanishes in the required manner by
Lemma

18
.

Hence, combining the estimates (
15
) and (
16
)
with (
4.2
) and (
11
) yields the result.
∎

Remark
.

If
\varphi\colon\Omega\to\widetilde{\Omega}
is conformal,
then we can regard it as a holomorphic map from
\mathbb{C}
to
\mathbb{C}

whose complex derivative
\varphi^{\prime}(x+\mathrm{i}y)
does not vanish at any
(x,y)\in\Omega
.
In such a case we have

\det{D\varphi}=\left|\varphi^{\prime}\right|^{2}\quad\text{and}\quad\frac{1}{\left|\det{D\varphi}\right|}D\varphi D\varphi^{t}=I,

whence it follows that

D\varphi^{-1}D\varphi^{-t}\left|\det{D\varphi}\right|=I,

and so
Section

4.2
simplifies to

\displaystyle\left\langle{Au,w}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}uw\left|\det{D\varphi}\right|\,\mathrm{d}{x}+\int_{\Omega}D[\Delta^{-1}u]\cdot Dw\,\mathrm{d}{x}

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}uw\left(\left|\det{D\varphi}\right|-1\right)\,\mathrm{d}{x}.

Corollary 20
.

Suppose that
\varphi\colon\Omega\to\widetilde{\Omega}
is
p
-admissible, conformal and differentiable.
Then,

\left\lVert T-T_{\varphi}\right\rVert_{L^{p}(\Omega)\to L^{p}(\Omega)}\lesssim\frac{1}{\inf_{\Omega}\left|\det{D\varphi}\right|}\left\lVert 1-\left|\det{D\varphi}\right|\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}({\Omega})}.

5
Continuity of projections under domain perturbations

We will now combine the estimates for
T-T_{\varphi}

with Kato’s integral formula to obtain
L^{p}
a continuity theorem
for the projections as
\mathrm{id}-\varphi\to 0
in
W^{2,\infty}
,
provided one can establish
L^{p}
bounds on the resolvents on
T=\Delta^{-1}
.
In the next section we will show that this is possible when
\Omega
is a square of rectangular domain.

Before we state the theorem, we note that
the projection operators for
\Delta
and its inverse
T

are identical, since they share the same eigenfunctions;
and similarly for
\Delta_{\varphi}
and
T_{\varphi}
.

Theorem 21
.

Suppose that
\varphi\colon\Omega\to\widetilde{\Omega}
is
p
-admissible,
and
F
does not split eigenvalues of
T
or
T_{\varphi}
.
Suppose further that

\left\lVert(T-z)^{-1}\right\rVert_{L^{p}\to L^{p}}\leqslant C(T,z)

(17)

for all
z\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\sigma(\widetilde{T})
.
Then,

\left\lVert P_{F}-P_{F}^{\varphi}\right\rVert_{L^{p}(\Omega)\to L^{p}(\Omega)}\lesssim C(F,\Omega,\varphi)\left(\frac{\left\lVert 1-\left|\det{D\varphi}\right|\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}({\Omega})}+K_{\varphi}}{\inf_{\Omega}\left|\det{D\varphi}\right|}\right)

where
K_{\varphi}
is the constant from
Lemma

18

and the implicit constants depend only on
p
.

Furthermore, if
\varphi_{k}
is a sequence of
p
-admissible transformations,
such that
\left\lVert\mathrm{id}-\varphi_{k}\right\rVert_{W^{2,\infty}({\Omega})}\to 0
,
then
\left\lVert P_{F}-P_{F}^{\varphi_{k}}\right\rVert_{L^{p}(\Omega)\to L^{p}(\Omega)}\to 0

for each fixed
F
that does not split eigenvalues of
\Delta
.

Proof.

Under the assumptions on
\Omega
and
\varphi
,

T
and
T_{\varphi}
are well-defined compact operators on
L^{2}(\Omega)
.
Label their eigenvalues as
(\mu_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
and
(\widetilde{\mu}_{j})_{j}
,
respectively, and recall that
\mu_{j}=1/\lambda_{j}
, etc.

For any
u\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\Omega)
, we have the
L^{2}(\Omega)
expansions

Tu=\sum_{j\in\mathbb{N}}\mu_{j}\left\langle{u_{j},u}\right\rangle_{\Omega}u_{j}\quad\text{and}\quad T_{\varphi}=\sum_{j\in\mathbb{N}}\widetilde{\mu}_{j}Q_{\varphi}[u^{\varphi}_{j},u]u^{\varphi}_{j}.

For any
z\in\mathbb{C}
distinct from any
\mu_{j}
and
\widetilde{\mu}_{j}
, we have (still in
L^{2}
)

(T-z)^{-1}u=\sum_{j\in\mathbb{N}}\frac{\left\langle{u_{j},u}\right\rangle_{\Omega}}{\mu_{j}-z}u_{j}\quad\text{and}\quad(T_{\varphi}-z)^{-1}=\sum_{j\in\mathbb{N}}\frac{Q_{\varphi}[u^{\varphi}_{j},u]}{\widetilde{\mu}_{j}-z}u^{\varphi}_{j}.

Now select a contour
\gamma
in the complex plane enclosing precisely the

\mu_{j}
and
\widetilde{\mu_{j}}
with
j\in F
and no others.
This is possible by
Theorem

12
.

By Kato’s projection formula,

\displaystyle P_{F}u-P_{F}^{\varphi}u

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\int_{\gamma}\left\{(T-z)^{-1}-(T_{\varphi}-z)^{-1}\right\}u\,\mathrm{d}{z}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\int_{\gamma}(T_{\varphi}-z)^{-1}(T_{\varphi}-T)(T-z)^{-1}u\,\mathrm{d}{z}

(18)

We want to replace the first resolvent,
(T_{\varphi}-z)^{-1}
, with a truncated version:

R^{\varphi}_{F}(z)f:=\sum_{j\in F}\frac{Q_{\varphi}[u^{\varphi}_{j},u]}{\widetilde{\mu}_{j}-z}u_{j}^{\varphi}.

Indeed, we claim that:

\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\int_{\gamma}(T_{\varphi}-z)^{-1}f\,\mathrm{d}{z}=\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\int_{\gamma}R_{F}^{\varphi}(z)f\,\mathrm{d}{z}\quad\text{for all}\quad f\in L^{2}(\Omega).

(19)

It suffices to check that
(
Conway, 2007
, VII,§4)

\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\int_{\gamma}Q_{\varphi}[(T_{\varphi}-z)^{-1}f,g]\,\mathrm{d}{z}=\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\int_{\gamma}Q_{\varphi}[R_{F}^{\varphi}(z)f,g]\,\mathrm{d}{z}

for all
f
and
g
in
L^{2}(\Omega)
.

For
f\in L^{2}(\Omega)
, we can write
f=\sum_{j}Q_{\varphi}[u^{\varphi}_{j},f]u^{\varphi}_{j}

and apply the bounded operator
(T_{\varphi}-z)^{-1}
:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\int_{\gamma}Q_{\varphi}[(T_{\varphi}-z)^{-1}f,g]\,\mathrm{d}{z}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\int_{\gamma}\sum_{j}\frac{Q_{\varphi}[(T_{\varphi}-z)^{-1}f,g]}{\widetilde{\mu}_{j}-z}Q_{\varphi}[u_{j}^{\varphi},g]\,\mathrm{d}{z}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j}Q_{\varphi}[u_{j}^{\varphi},f]Q_{\varphi}[u_{j}^{\varphi},g]\left(\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\int_{\gamma}\frac{1}{\widetilde{\mu}_{j}-z}\,\mathrm{d}{z}\right).

By the classical Cauchy integral formula, the integral in brackets is

1
if
j\in F
and
0
otherwise. Hence, the last expression
is equal to

\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\int_{\gamma}Q_{\varphi}\left[\sum_{j\in F}\frac{Q_{\varphi}[u_{j}^{\varphi},u_{j}]}{\widetilde{\mu}_{j}-z}u_{j}^{\varphi},g\right]\,\mathrm{d}{z},

and the claim follows.

The next step is to obtain
L^{p}
bounds on the operator norm of
R_{F}^{\varphi}(z)
.
Taking
f\in C_{c}^{\infty}
, which dense in both
L^{2}
and
L^{p}
, we have

\left\lVert R_{F}^{\varphi}(z)f\right\rVert_{L^{p}({\Omega})}\leqslant\sum_{j\in F}\frac{\left|Q_{\varphi}[u_{j}^{\varphi},f]\right|}{\left|\widetilde{\mu}_{j}-z\right|}\left\lVert u_{j}^{\varphi}\right\rVert_{L^{p}({\Omega})}.

The change of variables formula immediately shows that (for
1/p+1/q=1
)

\left\lVert u_{j}^{\varphi}\right\rVert_{L^{p}({\Omega})}\leqslant\frac{\left\lVert\widetilde{u}_{j}\right\rVert_{L^{p}({\widetilde{\Omega}})}}{\inf_{\Omega}{\left|\det{D\varphi}\right|^{1/p}}}

and

\left|Q_{\varphi}[u_{j}^{\varphi},f]\right|\leqslant\frac{\left\lVert\det{D\varphi}\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}({\Omega})}\left\lVert\widetilde{u}_{j}\right\rVert_{L^{q}({\widetilde{\Omega}})}}{\inf_{\Omega}{\left|\det{D\varphi}\right|^{1/q}}}\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{L^{p}({\Omega})}.

Hence, it follows that

\left\lVert R_{F}^{\varphi}(z)\right\rVert_{L^{p}(\Omega)\to L^{p}(\Omega)}\leqslant\frac{1}{\min_{F}\left|\widetilde{\mu}_{j}-z\right|}\frac{\left\lVert\det{D\varphi}\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}({\Omega})}}{\inf_{\Omega}{\left|\det{D\varphi}\right|}}\sum_{j\in F}\left\lVert\widetilde{u}_{j}\right\rVert_{L^{p}({\widetilde{\Omega}})}\left\lVert\widetilde{u}_{j}\right\rVert_{L^{q}({\widetilde{\Omega}})}.

Let us write

C(F,\varphi):=\frac{\left\lVert\det{D\varphi}\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}({\Omega})}}{\inf_{\Omega}{\left|\det{D\varphi}\right|}}\sum_{j\in F}\left\lVert\widetilde{u}_{j}\right\rVert_{L^{p}({\widetilde{\Omega}})}\left\lVert\widetilde{u}_{j}\right\rVert_{L^{q}({\widetilde{\Omega}})}.

(20)

Then, applying (
19
) to (
5
), and recalling assumption (
17
)
yields:

\left\lVert P_{F}u-P_{F}^{\varphi}u\right\rVert_{L^{p}({\Omega})}\lesssim C(F,\varphi)\int_{\gamma}\frac{C(T,z)}{\min_{F}{\left|\widetilde{\mu}_{j}-z\right|}}\,\mathrm{d}{z}\left\lVert T-T_{\varphi}\right\rVert_{L^{p}(\Omega)\to L^{p}(\Omega)}\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{L^{p}({\Omega})}.

We can package the first two terms into a constant
C(F,T,\varphi)
.
On the other hand,
19
gave us the estimate

\displaystyle\left\lVert T-T_{\varphi}\right\rVert_{L^{p}(\Omega)\to L^{p}(\Omega)}\lesssim\frac{1}{\inf_{\Omega}\left|\det{D\varphi}\right|}\left(\left\lVert 1-\left|\det{D\varphi}\right|\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}({\Omega})}+K_{\varphi}\right),

and the first result immediately follows.

The continuity statement is an immediate consequence of
Lemma

17

and
Lemma

18
.
∎

20
gives us the following simplification when
\varphi
is conformal.

Corollary 22
.

Suppose, in addition, that
\varphi
is holomorphic. Then,

\left\lVert P_{F}-P_{F}^{\varphi}\right\rVert_{L^{p}(\Omega)\to L^{p}(\Omega)}\lesssim C(F)\left\lVert 1-\left|\det{D\varphi}\right|\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}({\Omega})}\times E_{\varphi},

where
E_{\varphi}
remains bounded as
\mathrm{id}-\varphi\to 0
in
W^{2,\infty}
.

Remark
.

When
p=2
, the term

\sum_{j\in F}\left\lVert u_{j}\right\rVert_{L^{p}}\left\lVert u_{j}\right\rVert_{L^{q}}

appearing in
Eq.

20
can be improved to
1

(see
Lamberti and Lanza de Cristoforis, 2005
, eq. (13))
.
Indeed, for any compact operator
T
,

\left\lVert(T-z)^{-1}f\right\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}}=\sum_{j}\frac{\left|\left\langle{u_{j},f}\right\rangle\right|^{2}}{\left|\mu_{j}-z\right|^{2}}\leqslant\frac{1}{\min{\left|\mu_{j}-z\right|}^{2}}\left\lVert f\right\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}}.

In their paper,
Lamberti and Lanza de Cristoforis, 2005
claim to obtain this bound from
the following inequality
(
Taylor and Lay, 1986
, VI.3, Theorem 3.1)
:

\left\lVert(T-z)^{-1}\right\rVert\leqslant\frac{1}{d(z,V(T))},

where
d(z,V(T))
is the distance of
z\in\gamma

to the
numerical range
of the operator
T
,

V(T):=\left\{(Tx,x):\left\lVert x\right\rVert=1\right\}.

However, in a Hilbert Space
(
Halmos, 1982
, p. 116)
,
V(T)

is the closed convex hull of the spectrum of
T
.
Since
T
is compact,
0
lies in the closure of the spectrum,
and so the contour
\gamma
as described will necessarily intersect
the numerical range when it crosses the real axis.
Our alternative calculation has the advantage of generalising to

L^{p}
for the truncated operators
R_{F}^{\varphi}(z)
,
at the cost of increasing the size of the constants.

Under suitable assumptions on
\varphi
, one can use
P^{\varphi}_{F}
to transfer bounds on
P_{F}\colon L^{p}(\Omega)\to L^{p}(\Omega)

to bounds on
\widetilde{P}_{F}\colon L^{p}(\widetilde{\Omega})\to L^{p}(\widetilde{\Omega})
.
This “transference” result is encapsulated in the following lemma.

Lemma 23

(Transference from
\Omega
to
\widetilde{\Omega}
)
.

Let
\varphi\colon\Omega\to\widetilde{\Omega}
be (weakly) differentiable, invertible and satisfy the bounds

0<\inf_{\Omega}\left|\det{D\varphi}\right|\leqslant\sup_{\Omega}\left|\det{D\varphi}\right|<\infty.

Suppose that we have bounds of the form

\left\lVert P_{F}\right\rVert_{L^{p}(\Omega)\to L^{p}(\Omega)}\leqslant A

and

\left\lVert P_{F}-P_{F}^{\varphi}\right\rVert_{L^{p}(\Omega)\to L^{p}(\Omega)}\leqslant B.

Then, we have the bound

\left\lVert\widetilde{P}_{F}\right\rVert_{L^{p}(\widetilde{\Omega})\to L^{p}(\widetilde{\Omega})}\leqslant\left(\frac{\sup\left|\det{D\varphi}\right|}{\inf\left|\det{D\varphi}\right|}\right)^{1/p}(A+B).

Proof.

Fix
f\in L^{p}(\Omega)
and let
g:=f\circ\varphi^{-1}\in L^{p}(\widetilde{\Omega})
.
Then, by a change of variables in the integral defining the inner product on
\widetilde{\Omega}
, we have

\widetilde{P}_{F}g=\sum_{j\in F}\left(\int_{\Omega}\widetilde{u}_{j}(\varphi(x))f(x)\left|\det{D\varphi(x)}\right|\,\mathrm{d}{x}\right)\widetilde{u}_{j}=\sum_{j\in F}Q_{\varphi}[\widetilde{u}_{j}\circ\varphi,f]\widetilde{u}_{j}.

Taking
L^{p}(\widetilde{\Omega})
norms and changing variables again:

\displaystyle\left\lVert\widetilde{P}_{F}f\right\rVert_{L^{p}({\widetilde{\Omega}})}^{p}

\displaystyle=\int_{\widetilde{\Omega}}\left|\sum_{j\in F}Q_{\varphi}[\widetilde{u}_{j}\circ\varphi,f]\widetilde{u}_{j}(y)\right|^{p}\,\mathrm{d}{y}

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\left|\sum_{j\in F}Q_{\varphi}[\widetilde{u}_{j}\circ\varphi,f]\widetilde{u}_{j}(\varphi(x))\right|^{p}\left|\det{D\varphi(x)}\right|\,\mathrm{d}{x}

\displaystyle\leqslant\left\lVert\det{D\varphi(x)}\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}({\Omega})}\left\lVert\sum_{j\in F}Q_{\varphi}[u^{\varphi}_{j},f]u^{\varphi}_{j}\right\rVert_{L^{p}({\Omega})}^{p},

whence we arrive at

\left\lVert\widetilde{P}_{F}g\right\rVert_{L^{p}({\widetilde{\Omega}})}\leqslant\left\lVert\det{D\varphi(x)}\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}({\Omega})}^{1/p}\left\lVert P^{\varphi}_{F}f\right\rVert_{L^{p}({\Omega})}.

The result now follows by applying the triangle inequality to

P^{\varphi}_{F}f=P_{F}f+P^{\varphi}_{F}f-P_{F}f

and noticing that

\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{L^{p}({\Omega})}^{p}\leqslant\sup_{\widetilde{\Omega}}\left|\det{D\varphi}^{-1}\right|\left\lVert g\right\rVert_{L^{p}({\widetilde{\Omega}})}^{p}.\qed

6
Resolvent bounds for the square

In this section we will verify that
Eq.

17
holds
when
\Omega
is a square.

Recall that the Dirichlet eigenfunctions of
-\Delta
on
[0,\pi]^{2}
are

u_{m,n}(x,y)=\sin(mx)\sin(ny)

for
(m,n)\in\mathbb{N}
,
with corresponding eigenvalues
\lambda_{m,n}=m^{2}+n^{2}
.

Thus, in
L^{2}
, we have

(T-z)^{-1}u=\sum_{m,n}\frac{\widehat{u}(m,n)}{\frac{1}{m^{2}+n^{2}}-z}u_{m,n},

where
\widehat{u}(m,n)
are the double-sine Fourier coefficients of
u\in L^{2}([0,\pi]^{2})
,
provided that
z\in\mathbb{C}
is not a sum of two squares.

Thus, the operator
(T-z)^{-1}
can be viewed as a Torus Fourier multiplier with symbol

\sigma_{z}(m,n):=\frac{m^{2}+n^{2}}{1-z(m^{2}+n^{2})}\quad\text{for all}\quad m,n\in\mathbb{N}.

Combining the Mihlin–Hörmander Multiplier Theorem
(
Grafakos, 2014a
, Theorem 6.2.7)

and a Transference Theorem
(
Grafakos, 2014a
, Theorem 4.3.7)
, we obtain the following result.

Proposition 24
.

Suppose that
\chi\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{2})\cap C^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
satisfies

\left|\partial^{\alpha}\chi(\xi)\right|\leqslant A\left|\xi\right|^{-\left|\alpha\right|}

for all
\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
and all multiindices
\alpha
with
\left|\alpha\right|\leqslant 2
.
Then, for all
1<p<\infty
, we have

\left\lVert(\chi\widehat{f})^{\lor}\right\rVert_{L^{p}({\mathbb{R}^{2}})}\leqslant C_{p}A\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{L^{p}({\mathbb{R}^{2}})}.

Hence, for
1<p<\infty
, the operator

f\mapsto\sum_{m,n}\chi(m,n)\widehat{f}(m,n)u_{m,n}

is bounded on
L^{p}([0,\pi]^{2})
with norm at most
C_{p}A
.

We cannot apply
24
directly to
\sigma_{z}
,
since
\sigma_{z}(\xi)
is unbounded as
\xi\to\frac{1}{\sqrt{z}}
in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
.
However, since we are only interested in
\sigma_{z}
at integer lattice points
(m,n)
,
and by construction
1/z
cannot be a sum of squares (since
z
is not an eigenvalue of
-\Delta
),
it suffices to find another function that is an
L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
multiplier
and agrees with
\sigma_{z}
on
\mathbb{N}^{2}
.

The solution is to select a smooth, radial cut-off function
\rho_{z}:[0,\infty)\to[0,1]

which is zero on an annulus
\frac{1}{\sqrt{z}}-\varepsilon<\left|\xi\right|<\frac{1}{\sqrt{z}}+\varepsilon
,
and
\rho_{z}\equiv 1
outside the larger annulus

\sqrt{\lambda_{\max F}}+\delta<\left|\xi\right|<\sqrt{\lambda_{\max F+1}}-\delta

(see
Fig.

1
).

Figure 1:
Graph of the smooth cut-off
\rho_{z}
centred at
\left|\xi\right|=\frac{1}{\sqrt{z}}.

Finally, define
\chi_{z}(\xi):=\sigma_{z}(\xi)\rho_{z}(\left|\xi\right|)
.
This new symbol is smooth and bounded.
Furthermore, it agrees with
\sigma_{z}
on lattice points, since
\rho_{z}=1

at all such points.
Tedious calculations show that
\chi_{z}
satisfies the assumptions of
24
,
and so it follows that
(T-z)^{-1}
is bounded from
L^{p}
to
L^{p}
.

Remark
.

A note on the size of the constant
A
in
24
.
If
F=\{1,\ldots,N\}
, then the “worse” value of
z

along the contour
\gamma
in the proof of
Theorem

21

occurs when
\frac{1}{\lambda_{N+1}}<z<\frac{1}{\lambda_{N}}
,
so the width of the “window” around
\frac{1}{\sqrt{z}}
in
Fig.

1

will have to be comparable to
\sqrt{\lambda_{N+1}}-\sqrt{\lambda_{N}}
.
In
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, as a consequence of Weyl’s Law,

\lambda_{N}\approx N
and
\lambda_{N+1}-\lambda_{N}\approx 1
as
N\to\infty
.
(Here
A\approx B
means that
A/B
remains bounded above and below by constants.)
Therefore, the window is shrinking like
1/N
as
N\to\infty
.
This dependence on
N
(i.e. on
F
) manifests in the size of the derivatives of
\rho
.
By our calculations,
A=O(N^{5})
when estimating the derivatives of
\chi_{z}
up to second order.

Finally, we note that we may also take
\Omega
to be a rectangle,
by appropriate rescaling each direction.

7
Conclusions and open problems

This approach has two important limitations.
The first is the assumption that the resolvents
(-\Delta^{-1}-z)^{-1}
be
L^{p}

bounded.
As far as we know, the only general results for Banach spaces yield bounds
in terms of the distance of
z
to the numerical range
(see
Carvalho et al., 2012
, Theorem 6.11)
.
However, the numerical range contains the closed convex hull of the spectrum
(
Zenger, 1968
, see)
,
which is
[0,1/\lambda_{1}]
for
-\Delta^{-1}
.
Thus, the distance from
z\in(1/\lambda_{N+1},1/\lambda_{N})
to the numerical range
is 0.

Another assumption we have to make is
that the projections
P_{F}
map onto eigenspaces which do not split eigenvalues.
Eigenvalue splitting is not allowed because of Kato’s projection formula,
as discussed in the remark immediately after
Theorem

2
.

The following example motivates our interest in relaxing the notion of splitting.

Example 25
.

Let
\Omega=[0,1]^{2}
, a unit square; then the eigenvalues of the Dirichlet Laplacian are

\pi^{2}(m^{2}+n^{2})
for
(m,n)
running over
\mathbb{N}^{2}
. Fix any
N\in\mathbb{N}
.
The following results are classical
(
Grafakos, 2014a
, see)
:

(i)

If we take
F_{N}=\{(m,n):0<m,n\leqslant N\}
, then
P_{F_{N}}
is the partial sum operator
corresponding to “square cutoff” truncations of a double sine-series on the torus.
For any value of
1<p<\infty
, these projections are uniformly bounded in
N
,
which is equivalent to saying that the partial sums
P_{F_{N}}u
converge to
u
for any
u\in L^{p}
.

(ii)

If we take
B_{N}=\{(m,n):m^{2}+n^{2}\leqslant N^{2}\}
, then
P_{B_{N}}
is the partial sum operator
corresponding to “circular cutoff” truncations of a double sine-series on the torus.
These partial sums do not converge in any
L^{p}
space other than
p=2
.

It would be interesting to transfer the convergence results for (i) to other domains
obtained by perturbing the square by some suitable
\varphi
, as described here.
However, by definition, the sets
B_{N}
will not split eigenvalues,
while the sets
F_{N}
will often split eigenvalues.
Take, for instance, the pairs
(5,5)
and
(1,7)
,
both corresponding to the eigenvalue
50\pi^{2}
.
Then
(5,5)\in F_{6}
but
(1,7)\notin F_{6}
.

Denote this “bad region” by
\Gamma_{N}
, i.e.

\Gamma_{N}=\{(m,n)\in F_{N}:m^{2}+n^{2}=k^{2}+l^{2}\text{ for some }(k,l)\notin F_{N}\}.

\Gamma_{N}\neq\varnothing

It is rather surprising that this “small” region
\Gamma_{N}

lying between the circle and the square is responsible for such
dramatic differences in the convergence of the partial sums.
Indeed, Fefferman’s proof shows that curvature is a key part of the failure
of
L^{p}
convergence.
On the other hand, Córdoba’s results show that varying the number of vertices,
we can still guarantee convergence in
L^{p}
at least in the range
4/3<p<4
.

Our work in this paper has been motivated by the “failure” of the circle
to yield “well-behaved” (i.e. convergent) cutoffs

(see
Fefferman et al., 2022
, concluding remarks)
.
Indeed, it would seem that circular cutoffs are the “natural choice”
to truncate the partial sums since they correspond to choosing
all eigenfunctions whose eigenvalues are bounded by some value.
The square truncations, on the other hand, exploit the “artificial”
labelling of these eigenvalues by pairs of integers.
Fefferman himself remarks on his surprise at his own theorem,
since it was conjectured that circular cutoffs would yield convergence
in the region
\frac{4}{3}<p<4

(
Fefferman, 1971
, see the introduction to)
.

Thus, two open questions of especial interest remain:

(i)

Can we eliminate the “no-splitting” requirement in order to apply our results
to the square cutoffs?

(ii)

Can we improve the constant
C(F)
in order to get uniform estimates on the operator norms?

Regarding (ii), we observe that in some simple examples, there is
no
constant
C(F)
,
and so we can immediately recover
L^{p}
convergence on
\widetilde{\Omega}
from
L^{p}
convergence on
\Omega
.

Example 26
.

Let
\Omega=[0,\pi]^{2}
with eigenfunctions
u_{m,n}=\sin(mx)\sin(ny)
,
and
\varphi(x,y)=(ax,by)
mapping onto
\widetilde{\Omega}=[0,\pi a]\times[0,\pi b]
.
The eigenfunctions on the new domain are
\widetilde{u}_{m,n}=\sin(mx/a)\sin(ny/b)
.
A simple calculation then shows that

\left\lVert P_{F}u-P_{F}^{\varphi}u\right\rVert_{L^{p}({\Omega})}\leqslant\left|1-ab\right|\left\lVert P_{F}u\right\rVert_{L^{p}({\Omega})},

and so we can recover
L^{p}
boundedness of the
square
projections.
