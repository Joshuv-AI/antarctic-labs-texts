Hebbian Learning from First Principles
Linda Albanese, Adriano Barra, Pierluigi Bianco, Fabrizio Durante, Diego Pallara

Abstract
Recently, the original storage prescription for the Hopfield model of neural networks – as well as for its dense generalizations – has been turned into a genuine Hebbian learning rule by postulating the expression of its Hamiltonian for both the supervised and unsupervised protocols. In these notes, first, we obtain these explicit expressions by relying upon maximum entropy extremization à la Jaynes. Beyond providing a formal derivation of these recipes for Hebbian learning, this construction also highlights how Lagrangian constraints within entropy extremization force network’s outcomes on neural correlations: these try to mimic the empirical counterparts hidden in the datasets provided to the network for its training and, the denser the network, the longer the correlations that it is able to capture. Next, we prove that, in the big data limit, whatever the presence of a teacher (or its lacking), not only these Hebbian learning rules converge to the original storage prescription of the Hopfield model but also their related free energies (and, thus, the statistical mechanical picture provided by Amit, Gutfreund and Sompolinsky is fully recovered). As a sideline, we show mathematical equivalence among standard Cost functions ( Hamiltonian ), preferred in Statistical Mechanical jargon, and quadratic Loss Functions, preferred in Machine Learning terminology. Remarks on the exponential Hopfield model (as the limit of dense networks with diverging density) and semi-supervised protocols are also provided.

2.1
The Hopfield neural network in statistical mechanics

To define the Hebbian prescription for pattern storage, we need at first to introduce the
K
patterns that we want the network to store and retrieve: these are chosen as
N
-bits random vectors whose entries are drawn from a Rademacher distribution as stated by the next

Definition 1
.

(Random structureless patterns)
Let
K,N\in\mathbb{N}
. The patterns in the Hopfield model are denoted by
\bm{\xi}^{\mu}=(\xi_{1}^{\mu},\ldots,\xi_{N}^{\mu})
, for
\mu=1,\ldots,K
and their entries are i.i.d. Rademacher random variables with probability mass function given by

\mathbb{P}(\xi_{i}^{\mu}=x)=\frac{1}{2}\left(\delta(x-1)+\delta(x+1)\right),

(1)

for any
i=1,\ldots,N
and
\mu=1,\ldots,K
.

Next, we introduce the
N
neurons and their dichotomous neural activities
\sigma_{i}\in\{-1,+1\},i=1,...,N
(such that
\sigma_{i}=+1
represents the
i^{th}
neuron firing, while
\sigma_{i}=-1
accounts for its quiescence), that we use to introduce the Hopfield neural network
Amit
:

Definition 2
.

(Hebbian storing)
Let
J_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
and
h\in\mathbb{R}
and let
\bm{\sigma}=(\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2},...,\sigma_{N})\in\{-1,+1\}^{N}
be a vector of
N\in\mathbb{N}
binary Ising neurons. The Cost function (or Hamiltonian) of the Hopfield model is defined as

\displaystyle H_{N}^{\textnormal{(Hop)}}(\bm{\sigma}|J_{0},h,\bm{\xi})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\sum_{i,j=1,i<j}^{N}J_{ij}\sigma_{i}\sigma_{j}-\sum_{i=1}^{N}h_{i}\sigma_{i}

(2)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\dfrac{J_{0}}{2N}\sum_{i,j=1}^{N}\sum_{\mu=1}^{K}\xi_{i}^{\mu}\xi_{j}^{\mu}\sigma_{i}\sigma_{j}-h\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{\mu=1}^{K}\xi_{i}^{\mu}\sigma_{i},

where we have defined
h_{i}:=h\displaystyle\sum\limits_{\mu=1}^{K}\xi_{i}^{\mu},\ i=1,\ldots N
.

Here, the information about the patterns is allocated in the synaptic matrix, namely in the couplings
\bm{J}=\left(J_{ij}\right)_{i,j=1,...,N}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}
among neurons, defined according to the so-called Hebb’s rule

J_{ij}=\frac{J_{0}}{N}\sum_{\mu=1}^{K}\xi_{i}^{\mu}\xi_{j}^{\mu}.

(3)

Note that, in this model, neurons interact in couples, whose strength is ruled by
J_{0}
, and they perceive the external world, e.g, the presence of a pattern to recognize, through the one-body term (i.e., the second term at the r.h.s. of eq. (
2
)), whose strength is ruled by
h
.

In order to inspect the network’s information processing capabilities, we introduce also the concept of
control parameters
and
order parameters
as follows

Definition 3
.

(Control and order parameters)
Given the Hopfield model, the control parameters are
\beta\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
, the noise (see below in eq. (
6
)), and
\alpha:=K/N\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
, the storage capacity of the network.

Moreover, the order parameters (required to quantify the success of storing and retrieval by the Hopfield model (
2
)) are the
K
Mattis magnetizations (or overlaps) denoted by

\displaystyle m_{\mu}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}(\bm{\sigma}|\bm{\xi})}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\xi^{\mu}_{i}\sigma_{i},

(4)

for
\mu=1,\ldots,K
.

Roughly speaking, control parameters are parameters that we freely tune in the network (and we expect that –as for the former– the higher the noise in the network, the harder the computation for its neurons –whatever it is, storage or retrieval– much as for the latter, because the larger the storage, the harder the task).
On the contrary
, order parameters are response functions, that is, they provide a quantitative measure of network’s performances as control parameters vary.

Note that, working at
h=0

2

\displaystyle H_{N}^{\textnormal{(Hop)}}(\bm{\sigma}|J_{0}=1,h=0,\bm{\xi})=-\frac{N}{2}\sum_{\mu=1}^{K}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}(m_{\mu}(\bm{\sigma}|\bm{\xi}))^{2}}.

(5)

Hence it is trivial to realize that the minima of this Cost function are those provided by neural configurations that result in
|m_{\mu}|=1
for some

. Those configurations are said to be “retrieval states” because they match perfectly the patterns they are meant to retrieve.
Further, these configurations can also be attractors for the neural dynamics that we now introduce: once a Hamiltonian is provided, it is possible to construct a Markov process for the neural dynamics

\mathbb{P}(\bm{\sigma}(t+1)|\bm{\xi})=\sum_{\bm{\sigma^{\prime}}}W(\bm{\sigma},\bm{\sigma^{\prime}})\mathbb{P}(\bm{\sigma^{\prime}}(t)|\bm{\xi}),

where the transition rates

are dictated by eq. (
2

For instance, a popular implementation of the above neural dynamics (that can be explicitly coded on a computer), reads

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\bm{\sigma}(t+1)|\bm{\xi})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\prod_{i=1}^{N}\frac{1}{2}\left(1+\sigma_{i}(t+1)\tanh(\beta\theta_{i}(\bm{\sigma}(t)))\right),

(6)

\displaystyle\theta_{i}(\bm{\sigma}(t))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{N}J_{ij}\sigma_{j}(t)+h_{i},

(7)

where the term

is the

field acting on neuron

and the parameter

\lim_{t\to\infty}\mathbb{P}(\bm{\sigma}(t)|\bm{\xi})=\mathbb{P}(\bm{\sigma}|\bm{\xi})=\frac{e^{-\beta H_{N}^{\textnormal{(Hop)}}(\bm{\sigma}|\bm{\xi})}}{\sum_{\bm{\sigma}}e^{-\beta H_{N}^{\textnormal{(Hop)}}(\bm{\sigma}|\bm{\xi})}}=\frac{e^{\frac{N\beta}{2}\sum_{\mu}^{K}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}(m_{\mu}(\bm{\sigma}|\bm{\xi}))^{2}}}}{Z_{N}(\bm{\xi})}

where the normalization factor

Dealing with pattern storage, the interest lies in constructing neural networks with the largest possible storage capacity and in developing techniques to estimate these values as summarized below

Remark 1

Let

The free energy of the Hopfield model is defined as

\mathcal{A}(\alpha,\beta)=\lim_{N\to\infty}\mathcal{A}_{N}(\alpha,\beta),\quad\textnormal{where}\quad\mathcal{A}_{N}(\alpha,\beta)=\dfrac{1}{N}\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\xi}}\log Z_{N}(\bm{\xi}),

where

The analysis of the free energy is not the focus of the present investigation, we just need to introduce its definition because we aim to prove that the above expression related to the Hebbian storing can be recovered, in suitable limits (that we inspect in Sec.

2.2
The Hopfield neural network from statistical inference

In this Section we aim to explore the Maximum Entropy Principle (MEP) to obtain the Boltzmann-Gibbs measure (see eq. (
8
)) related to the Hopfield Hamiltonian defined in eq. (
2
) from an inferential procedure rather than as the relaxation of a stochastic process.

In an experimental scenario, to check retrieval performances of the neural network, we should measure at least two numbers per pattern: the mean values of the overlap between the network’s configuration (once relaxed toward a steady state) and the stored pattern, i.e. the Mattis magnetizations, and their variances.

In other words, the experimental setup requires the observation of the following quantities

\langle m_{\mu}\rangle_{{\textnormal{exp}}}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\xi^{\mu}_{i}\langle\sigma_{i}\rangle_{{\textnormal{exp}}},\qquad\langle m_{\mu}^{2}\rangle_{{\textnormal{exp}}}=\frac{1}{N^{2}}\sum_{i,j=1}^{N}\xi^{\mu}_{i}\xi^{\mu}_{j}\langle\sigma_{i}\sigma_{j}\rangle_{\textnormal{exp}},

(13)

where the subscript
exp
means that we are considering experimentally evaluated quantities. Data collection should happen as follows: upon presentation of the new pattern, say
\bm{\xi}^{k}
, the network is left free to evolve toward relaxation within a stable configuration. Then, skipped the initial transient, neural activity is recorded over time
t=1,...,T
and suitably averaged such that

\langle m_{k}\rangle_{{\textnormal{exp}}}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i}^{N}\xi_{i}^{k}\langle\sigma_{i}\rangle_{{\textnormal{exp}}}=\lim_{T\to\infty}\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i}^{N}\xi_{i}^{k}\sigma_{i}(t).

As discussed in Remark
1
, if the network is handling a moderate number of patterns, then the empirical average is informative as
\langle\sigma_{i}\rangle_{\textnormal{exp}}=\xi^{k}_{i}
.

Further, to set the maximum entropy optimization procedure, we need to rely also on the theoretical outcomes from the network for means and variances of the Mattis magnetizations and these are achieved via standard Boltzmann-Gibbs sampling and indicated by the brackets (i.e.,
\langle m_{\mu}\rangle
and
\langle m_{\mu}^{2}\rangle
) that read as

\displaystyle\langle m_{k}\rangle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\xi}}\frac{\sum_{\bm{\sigma}}(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\xi_{i}^{k}\sigma_{i})e^{-\beta H_{N}^{\textnormal{(Hop)}}(\bm{\sigma}|J_{0},h,\bm{\xi})}}{\sum_{\bm{\sigma}}e^{-\beta H_{N}^{\textnormal{(Hop)}}(\bm{\sigma}|J_{0},h,\bm{\xi})}},

\displaystyle\langle m^{2}_{k}\rangle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\xi}}\frac{\sum_{\bm{\sigma}}(\frac{1}{N^{2}}\sum_{i,j=1}^{N}\xi_{i}^{k}\xi_{k}^{k}\sigma_{i}\sigma_{j})e^{-\beta H_{N}^{\textnormal{(Hop)}}(\bm{\sigma}|J_{0},h,\bm{\xi})}}{\sum_{\bm{\sigma}}e^{-\beta H_{N}^{\textnormal{(Hop)}}(\bm{\sigma}|J_{0},h,\bm{\xi})}}.

The brackets represent the Boltzmann average over the ensemble
\omega(.)
and over the quenched patterns
\xi
, namely –given a generic observable

(function of both the neural activities and the patterns lying in the synaptic coupling)– we write

\langle O(\sigma,\xi)\rangle:=\mathbb{E}\omega\left(O(\sigma,\xi)\right)=\mathbb{E}\frac{\sum_{\sigma}O(\sigma,\xi)e^{-\beta H_{N}^{Hop}(\bm{\sigma}|J_{0},h,\bm{\xi})}}{Z_{N}(\bm{\xi})}\equiv\mathbb{E}\frac{\sum_{\sigma}O(\sigma,\xi)e^{-\beta H_{N}^{Hop}(\bm{\sigma}|J_{0},h,\bm{\xi})}}{\sum_{\sigma}e^{-\beta H_{N}^{Hop}(\bm{\sigma}|J_{0},h,\bm{\xi})}}.

The goal is then to determine the less structured probability distribution
\mathbb{P}_{N}(\bm{\sigma}|\bm{\xi})

\langle m_{\mu}\rangle=\langle m_{\mu}\rangle_{\textnormal{exp}}
and
\langle m_{\mu}^{2}\rangle=\langle m_{\mu}^{2}\rangle_{\textnormal{exp}}

\mu=1,...,K
. To do so,

.

\mathbb{P}_{N}(\bm{\sigma}|\bm{\xi})=\frac{e^{-\beta H_{N}^{(Hop)}(\bm{\sigma}|J_{0},h,\bm{\xi})}}{Z(\bm{\xi})}=\frac{\exp\left(\frac{\beta J_{0}}{2N}\sum_{i,j=1}^{N}\sum_{\mu=1}^{K}\xi^{\mu}_{i}\xi^{\mu}_{j}\sigma_{i}\sigma_{j}+\beta h\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{\mu=1}^{K}\xi^{\mu}_{i}\sigma_{i}\right)}{\sum_{\bm{\sigma}}\exp\left(\frac{\beta J_{0}}{2N}\sum_{i,j=1}^{N}\sum_{\mu=1}^{K}\xi^{\mu}_{i}\xi^{\mu}_{j}\sigma_{i}\sigma_{j}+\beta h\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{\mu=1}^{K}\xi^{\mu}_{i}\sigma_{i}\right)},

(14)

Proof.

The key argument is to obtain

by maximizing the Shannon entropy

S[\mathbb{P}_{N}(\bm{\sigma}|\bm{\xi})]=-\sum_{\bm{\sigma}}\mathbb{P}_{N}(\bm{\sigma}|\bm{\xi})\log\mathbb{P}_{N}(\bm{\sigma}|\bm{\xi})

constraining the latter, via Lagrange multipliers, to have lowest order momenta

and

that reproduce the empirical ones (i.e.,

and

).

We should thus maximize the quantity

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\dfrac{\partial S[\mathbb{P}_{N,M}(\bm{\sigma}|\bm{\eta})]}{\partial\lambda_{0}}=&\sum_{\bm{\sigma}}\mathbb{P}_{N,M}(\bm{\sigma}|\bm{\eta})-1=0\\
\dfrac{\partial S[\mathbb{P}_{N,M}(\bm{\sigma}|\bm{\eta})]}{\partial\lambda_{1}^{\mu,a}}=&\sum_{\bm{\sigma}}\mathbb{P}_{N,M}(\bm{\sigma}|\bm{\eta})\dfrac{r}{\mathcal{R}N}\displaystyle\sum\limits_{i=1}^{N}\eta_{i}^{\mu,a}\sigma_{i}-\langle n_{\mu,a}\rangle_{\exp}=0\\
\dfrac{\partial S[\mathbb{P}_{N,M}(\bm{\sigma}|\bm{\eta})]}{\partial\lambda_{2}^{\mu,a}}=&\sum_{\bm{\sigma}}\mathbb{P}_{N,M}(\bm{\sigma}|\bm{\eta})\left(\dfrac{r}{\mathcal{R}N}\right)^{2}\displaystyle\sum\limits_{i,j=1,1}^{N,N}\eta_{i}^{\mu,a}\eta_{j}^{\mu,a}\sigma_{i}\sigma_{j}-\langle n_{\mu,a}^{2}\rangle_{\exp}=0\\
\dfrac{\delta S[\mathbb{P}_{N,M}(\bm{\sigma}|\bm{\eta})]}{\delta\mathbb{P}_{N,M}(\bm{\sigma}|\bm{\eta})}=&-\log\mathbb{P}_{N,M}(\bm{\sigma}|\bm{\eta})-1+\lambda_{0}+\displaystyle\sum\limits_{\mu=1}^{K}\displaystyle\sum\limits_{a=1}^{M}\lambda_{1}^{\mu,a}\dfrac{r}{\mathcal{R}N}\displaystyle\sum\limits_{i=1}^{N}\eta_{i}^{\mu,a}\sigma_{i}\\
&+\displaystyle\sum\limits_{\mu=1}^{K}\displaystyle\sum\limits_{a=1}^{M}\lambda_{2}^{\mu,a}\left(\dfrac{r}{\mathcal{R}N}\right)^{2}\displaystyle\sum\limits_{i,j=1}^{N}\eta_{i}^{\mu,a}\eta_{j}^{\mu,a}\sigma_{i}\sigma_{j}=0\end{cases}

The first three equations return the requirement for

to be a discrete probability distribution and guarantee the matching of the theoretical averages of
n_{\mu,a}
and
n_{\mu,a}^{2}
to their experimental counterparts. The last equation, instead, gives us the explicit expression of the probability distribution, namely

\displaystyle\log\mathbb{P}_{N,M}(\bm{\sigma}|\bm{\eta})=

\displaystyle{\left(\lambda_{0}-1+\displaystyle\sum\limits_{\mu=1}^{K}\displaystyle\sum\limits_{a=1}^{M}\lambda_{1}^{\mu,a}\dfrac{r}{RN}\displaystyle\sum\limits_{i=1}^{N}\eta_{i}^{\mu,a}\sigma_{i}+\displaystyle\sum\limits_{\mu=1}^{K}\displaystyle\sum\limits_{a=1}^{M}\lambda_{2}^{\mu,a}\left(\dfrac{r}{RN}\right)^{2}\displaystyle\sum\limits_{i,j=1}^{N}\eta_{i}^{\mu,a}\eta_{j}^{\mu,a}\sigma_{i}\sigma_{j}\right)}.

(32)

If we fix the normalization by setting
Z(\bm{\eta})=\exp\left(1-\lambda_{0}\right)
we recover a Boltzmann-Gibbs distribution and, in particular, by requiring
\lambda_{1}^{\mu,a}=0
and
\lambda_{2}^{\mu,a}=\dfrac{\mathcal{R}N}{2r^{2}M}

\mu=1,...,K

\mathbb{P}_{N,M}(\bm{\sigma}|\bm{\eta})
reproduces all the details of the unsupervised Hebbian learning scheme.
∎

3.2
Recovering the free energy of Hebbian storage in the big data limit

Plan of this Section is to prove that the whole free energy of these learning protocols approaches the free energy of the Hopfield model in the
M\to\infty
limit (the so-called
big data
limit), thus, as a consequence all the related properties of that model are recovered
Amit
. To do so, we rely upon Guerra’s interpolation technique
GuerraNN
. The idea is to write a generalized free energy that interpolates between the free energies of Hebbian learning and Hebbian storing and then prove that its derivative w.r.t. the interpolation parameter goes to zero as
M
goes to infinity. This allows a straightforward application of the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus that makes the proof extremely simple.

As a sideline, we also perform the big data limit to the Boltzmann-Gibbs probability distributions stemming by these learning rules to prove that, in the
M\to\infty
limit, these approach the Boltzmann-Gibbs measure corresponding to standard Hopfield storage: this implies that these Cost functions for Hebbian learning ultimately collapse to the Hopfield Hamiltonian for Hebbian storing, as intuitive in the big data limit.

3.2.1
Supervised Hebbian learning

Let us start showing how the free energy of Hebbian learning, within the supervised setting, approaches the free energy of the Hopfield model, in the big data limit.

It is useful to write explicitly the next

Definition 13
.

(Free energy)

(46)

where
\mathbb{E}

\bm{\eta}
,
\bm{\eta}=\bm{\eta}(\xi,r)
.

\mathcal{A}_{N}(\alpha,\beta)=\dfrac{1}{N}\mathbb{E}\log\sum_{\bm{\sigma}}\exp\left(\dfrac{\beta}{2N}\sum_{\mu=1}^{K}\sum_{i,j=1}^{N}\xi_{i}^{\mu}\xi_{j}^{\mu}\sigma_{i}\sigma_{j}\right),

We can now introduce the following interpolating free energy:

(Interpolating free energy)

Remark 6

A generalized average follows from this generalized measure linked to the Boltzmann factor

\omega_{t}(\cdot)\coloneqq\frac{1}{Z_{N,M}(t|\bm{\xi},\bm{\eta})}\,\sum_{\bm{\sigma}}\penalty\ \cdot\penalty\ B_{N,M}(t|\bm{\sigma},\bm{\xi},\bm{\eta})

With these premises, we can now state the main Theorem of this subsection as

Theorem 1

In the asymptotic (big data) limit,

To prove the Theorem, we need to put beforehand the following

Lemma 1
.

In the limit
M\to+\infty
, the derivative of the interpolating free energy (
14

is null, namely

\displaystyle\lim_{M\to\infty}\dfrac{d\mathcal{A}_{N,M}(t|\alpha,\beta)}{dt}=\lim_{M\to\infty}\dfrac{\beta}{2}\sum_{\mu=1}^{K}\left(\langle m_{\mu}^{2}\rangle-\dfrac{1}{\mathcal{R}}\langle\tilde{n}_{\mu}^{2}\rangle\right)=0.

(51)

Proof.

Let us start with the computation of the derivative:

\displaystyle d_{t}\mathcal{A}_{N,M}(t|\alpha,\beta)=

\displaystyle\dfrac{1}{N}\mathbb{E}\Bigg[\dfrac{1}{Z_{N,M}(t|\bm{\sigma},\bm{\xi},\bm{\eta})}\sum_{\bm{\sigma}}B(t|\bm{\sigma},\bm{\xi},\bm{\eta})\left(\dfrac{\beta}{2N}\sum_{i,j=1}^{N}\sum_{\mu=1}^{K}\xi_{i}^{\mu}\xi_{j}^{\mu}\sigma_{i}\sigma_{j}\right.

\displaystyle\left.-\dfrac{\beta}{2N\mathcal{R}}\sum_{\mu=1}^{K}\sum_{i,j=1}^{N}\left(\dfrac{1}{M}\sum_{a=1}^{M}\eta_{i}^{\mu,a}\right)\left(\dfrac{1}{M}\sum_{b=1}^{M}\eta_{j}^{\mu,b}\right)\sigma_{i}\sigma_{j}\right)\Bigg]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\dfrac{\beta}{2N}\sum_{\mu=1}^{K}\left(\langle m_{\mu}^{2}\rangle-\dfrac{1}{\mathcal{R}}\langle\tilde{n}_{\mu}^{2}\rangle\right).

(52)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left(\dfrac{1}{M}\sum_{a=1}^{M}\eta_{i}^{\mu,a}\Big|\bm{\xi}\right)

\displaystyle=\xi^{\mu}_{i}

(53)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\xi}}\left(\dfrac{1}{M}\sum_{a=1}^{M}\eta_{i}^{\mu,a}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=r^{2}+\dfrac{1-r^{2}}{M}=\mathcal{R}.

(54)

∎

(of Theorem

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{N,M}(t=1|\alpha,\beta)=\mathcal{A}_{N,M}(t=0|\alpha,\beta)+\int_{0}^{1}\dfrac{d\mathcal{A}_{N,M}(t|\alpha,\beta)}{dt}|_{t=s}ds,

Let us start with the following definition

Definition 19

Cost functions vs Loss functions in dense Hebbian networks

Building on

Let us start with the following definition

Definition 29
.

\displaystyle\mathcal{Ls}^{\mu}(\bm{\sigma}|\bm{\eta}^{\mu})=Ls_{-}^{\mu}(\bm{\sigma}|\bm{\eta}^{\mu})Ls_{+}^{\mu}(\bm{\sigma}|\bm{\eta}^{\mu})

where

such that

\displaystyle X_{i_{1},\ldots,i_{P/2}}^{\mu}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\left(\dfrac{1}{M}\sum_{a_{1}=1}^{M}\eta_{i_{1}}^{\mu,a_{1}}\right)\cdots\left(\dfrac{1}{M}\sum_{a_{P/2}=1}^{M}\eta_{i_{P}}^{\mu,a_{P/2}}\right)

(117)

\displaystyle Ls_{\pm}^{\mu}(\bm{\sigma}|\bm{\eta}^{\mu})=

\displaystyle\dfrac{1}{2N^{P/2}}\sum_{i_{1},\ldots,i_{P/2}=1,\ldots,1}^{N,\ldots,N}\left[\left(X^{\mu}_{i_{1},\ldots,i_{P/2}}\right)^{2}+\left(S_{i_{1},\ldots,i_{P/2}}\right)^{2}-2S_{i_{1},\ldots,i_{P/2}}X^{\mu}_{i_{1},\ldots,i_{P}}\right]=

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\dfrac{1}{2}\pm\tilde{n}_{\mu}^{P/2}+\dfrac{1}{2N^{P/2}}\left(\dfrac{1}{M}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{a=1}^{M}\eta_{i}^{\mu,a}\right)^{P/2}

4

Conclusion and outlook

In this paper we consistently provided a derivation for all the Cost functions (and, in a cascade fashion, for all the Loss functions) that have been recently introduced in the Literature regarding Hebbian learning from examples, with or without the presence of a teacher. To be sharp, we systematically derived, by relying upon the maximum entropy inferential criterion à la Jaynes, the expression for the Cost function of Hebbian storing and Hebbian learning (supervised, unsupervised and semi-supervised) and then we proved a duality of expression among these Cost functions (preferred by the Statistical Mechanics community) and standard
L^{2}
Loss functions (preferred by the Machine Learning community).

Once provided an exhaustive derivation for the shallow case of neural networks equipped with pairwise interactions among neurons, we enlarged the analysis to the dense counterpart, namely to neural networks with many-body interactions (up to the limit of infinite interactions, that we have shown to collapse on the so-called exponential Hopfield model).

Beyond providing an analytical derivation for these expressions (that were solely heuristically assumed in the Literature so far), the usage of the maximum entropy inferential tool allowed also to deepen how many and what kind of correlations these networks look for when inspecting databases: the higher the order of interactions (i.e. the broader the neural assembly at work), the longer the correlations that the network as a whole can detect. This possibly contribute to explain why dense neural networks outperform their pairwise shallow limit at work with structured datasets.
