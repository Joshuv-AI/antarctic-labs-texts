Obstructions to extension of Wasserstein distances for variable masses
Luca Lombardini, Francesco Rossi

Abstract
. We study the possibility of defining a distance on the whole space of measures, with the property that the distance between two measures having the same mass is the Wasserstein distance, up to a scaling factor. We prove that, under very weak and natural conditions, if the base space is unbounded, then the scaling factor must be constant, independently of the mass. Moreover, no such distance can exist, if we include the zero measure. Instead, we provide examples with non-constant scaling factors for the case of bounded base spaces.

28A33, 49Q22

1.
Introduction and main results

Wasserstein distances are crucial modern tools in mathematics, raising an enormous interest from the community of mathematical analysis (see e.g.
[
17
,
1
,
3
]
). Their applications are also extremely varied, ranging from crowd dynamics
[
6
,
13
]
, to economics
[
12
,
9
]
, to computer science
[
14
]
.

Yet, Wasserstein distances have several limitations, the most apparent being that they are defined between measures with the same mass only. This issue led to the definition of different possible generalizations of the Wasserstein distance between measures with different masses. The first attempt in this direction, described in
[
8
]
, was related to the heat equation on a domain
\Omega
with Dirichlet boundary condition, that clearly does not preserve mass. A second attempt, see
[
7
]
, was given by the so-called optimal partial transport problem: given two measures
\mu,\nu
with different masses, one fixes a smaller mass and computes the Wasserstein distance between optimal submeasures.

A third group of contributions, known under the name of
generalized Wasserstein distance

[
15
,
16
]
, was based on the optimization of a mixed Wasserstein-
L^{1}
cost, in which a part of the measure is transported (with Wasserstein cost) and the remaining part is removed (with a
L^{1}
cost). This contribution was then followed by several “generalizations of the generalization”, see
[
5
,
10
,
11
]
.

One of the main drawbacks of the distances given above is that they do not coincide, in general, with the standard Wasserstein distance when the two measures have the same mass. The only exception is clearly the partial optimal transportation problem when one chooses to transport the whole mass. This observation raises a natural question:
is it possible to define a distance between measures of different masses, that coincides with the Wasserstein distance in the case measures have the same mass?

Our main result provides a negative answer, under quite general hypotheses. We show that it is not possible to extend the
p
-Wasserstein distance
W_{p}
to a distance function
d
defined on the whole space of measures in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and satisfying the property

(1.1)

d(\mu_{1},\mu_{2})=f(m)W_{p}\Big(\frac{\mu_{1}}{m},\frac{\mu_{2}}{m}\Big),

whenever the mass
|\mu|:=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}d\mu
satisfies
|\mu_{1}|=|\mu_{2}|=m>0
.

More precisely, let
\mathcal{M}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
be the space of non-negative measures in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
having finite mass and compact support and
\mathcal{M}^{*}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
the space of measures with positive finite mass and compact support. Then,
\mathcal{M}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{n})=\{0\}\cup\mathcal{M}^{*}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, where
0
denotes the zero measure. Let also
\mathcal{P}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
be the space of probability measures on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, i.e. of mass 1, having compact support. We use the notation
\mathbb{R}_{>0}
for the space of positive real numbers
(0,+\infty)
, endowed with the Euclidean distance.
We stress that, as sets, we can identify

(1.2)

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}^{*}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\simeq\mathbb{R}_{>0}\times\mathcal{P}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{n}),

via the bijection
\mu\mapsto(|\mu|,\mu/|\mu|)
.

We investigate the possibility of defining a distance function
d
on
\mathcal{M}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
satisfying (
1.1
), i.e. with the property that on each fiber
\{m\}\times\mathcal{P}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
the distance
d
coincides with the
p
-Wasserstein distance, up to a multiplicative scaling factor depending on
m>0
only.

Our main result is a strong obstruction to a general definition of such a distance, as it provides a very weak condition forcing the function
f
in (
1.1
) to be constant. Indeed, we have the following first result.

Theorem 1.1

(Unbounded space)
.

Let
p\in[1,+\infty)
and let
d_{\mathcal{M}}:\mathcal{M}^{*}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\times\mathcal{M}^{*}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\to[0,+\infty)
be a distance function satisfying the following two properties:

(1)

there exists a function
f:\mathbb{R}_{>0}\to\mathbb{R}_{>0}
such that

d_{\mathcal{M}}(m\mu_{1},m\mu_{2})=f(m)W_{p}(\mu_{1},\mu_{2}),

for every
m\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
and every
\mu_{1},\mu_{2}\in\mathcal{P}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
.

(2)

There exists an unbounded subset
\Sigma\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that for every
m_{0}\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
there exist a radius
r>0
and a positive constant
C>0
, both depending only on
m_{0}
, with

(1.3)

\sup_{x\in\Sigma}d_{\mathcal{M}}(m_{0}\delta_{x},m\delta_{x})\leq C

for every
m\in(m_{0}-r,m_{0}+r)
, where
\delta_{x}
is the Dirac delta centered at
x
.

Then,
f\equiv\lambda
, for some positive constant
\lambda>0
.

As a consequence, it is not possible to extend such a distance to the whole space
\mathcal{M}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, i.e. when the zero measure is added.

Corollary 1.2

(Unbounded space with zero)
.

Let
d_{\mathcal{M}}
be a distance function on
\mathcal{M}^{*}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
as in Theorem
1.1
. Then, there exists no distance function
d_{\mathcal{M}_{0}}
defined on
\mathcal{M}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, which agrees with
d_{\mathcal{M}}
on
\mathcal{M}^{*}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, and such that either

(1.4)

\sup_{x\in\Sigma}d_{\mathcal{M}_{0}}(0,m_{0}\delta_{x})<+\infty,

for some
m_{0}\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
, or

(1.5)

\lim_{m\searrow 0}d_{\mathcal{M}_{0}}(0,m\mu_{i})=0,

at least for two different
\mu_{1}\not=\mu_{2}\in\mathcal{P}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
.

A few observations are in order. First of all, simple examples of distance functions on
\mathcal{M}^{*}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
satisfying (
1.1
), with
f
constant, are given by the product metrics on the decomposed space (
1.2
), see Example
3.3
below.
Roughly speaking, these distances measure separately the costs of changing mass and of transporting two equal masses, then sum up the two costs.

We also stress that condition (
1.3
) is very weak. It is an assumption regarding only the point masses located within
\Sigma
, and it represents the fact that the cost of “destroying” or “creating” mass, going from
m\delta_{x}
to
m_{0}\delta_{x}
, is uniformly bounded with respect to the position
x\in\Sigma
, locally around
m_{0}
.
Furthermore, we point out that (
1.3
) is implied by other very natural conditions, like the invariance of
d_{\mathcal{M}}
with respect to isometries together with the compatibility of
d_{\mathcal{M}}
with weak convergence when varying the mass, see Proposition
3.2
for the precise statement.

In Theorem
1.1
and Corollary
1.2
, the specific properties of the
p
-Wasserstein distance actually play no role, except for the fact that
W_{p}
is unbounded on
\mathcal{M}^{*}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
—and in particular on the subset consisting of Dirac measures with base points belonging to
\Sigma
, an unbounded subset of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
As a consequence, Theorem
1.1
and Corollary
1.2
can be extended to much more general settings: in place of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
we can consider a Polish space
(X,d_{X})
and in place of the Wasserstein distance
W_{p}
we can consider any distance function
d_{\mathcal{P}}
defined on the space of compactly supported probability measures
\mathcal{P}_{c}(X)
.

If
d_{\mathcal{P}}
is unbounded, we can then translate Theorem
1.1
to this setting in a straightforward way: in point (ii) simply consider a subset
S\subseteq\mathcal{P}_{c}(X)
which is unbounded with respect to
d_{\mathcal{P}}
, in place of the set
\{\delta_{x}\,:\,x\in\Sigma\}
.

On the contrary, if
d_{\mathcal{P}}
is bounded—e.g., if
d_{\mathcal{P}}
is the bounded Lipschitz distance in
\mathcal{P}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
(see, e.g.,
[
18
,
16
]
) or if
d_{\mathcal{P}}
is the
p
-Wasserstein distance in
\mathcal{P}_{c}(X)
, when
(X,d_{X})
is bounded—the picture is completely different.

Proposition 1.3

(Bounded space)
.

Let
(X,d_{X})
be a Polish space and let
d_{\mathcal{P}}:\mathcal{P}_{c}(X)\times\mathcal{P}_{c}(X)\to[0,+\infty)
be a bounded distance function. Let
f:\mathbb{R}_{>0}\to\mathbb{R}_{>0}
be a Lipschitz, increasing, function such that

\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}m_{1},m_{2}\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}\\
m_{1}\not=m_{2}\end{subarray}}\frac{|f(m_{1})-f(m_{2})|}{|m_{1}-m_{2}|}\leq\frac{1}{\diam\mathcal{P}_{c}(X)}\quad\mbox{and}\quad\lim_{m\searrow 0}f(m)=0.

Then, the function
d_{\mathcal{M}_{0}}:\mathcal{M}_{c}(X)\times\mathcal{M}_{c}(X)\to[0,+\infty)
defined by setting

d_{\mathcal{M}_{0}}(m_{1}\mu_{1},m_{2}\mu_{2}):=|m_{1}-m_{2}|+\min\{f(m_{1}),f(m_{2})\}d_{\mathcal{P}}(\mu_{1},\mu_{2}),

for every
m_{1},m_{2}\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
and
\mu_{1},\mu_{2}\in\mathcal{P}_{c}(X)
,
and

d_{\mathcal{M}_{0}}(0,m\mu)=d_{\mathcal{M}_{0}}(m\mu,0):=m,\qquad d_{\mathcal{M}_{0}}(0,0):=0,

is a distance function on
\mathcal{M}_{c}(X)
.

Clearly, the distance function
d_{\mathcal{M}_{0}}
defined in Proposition
1.3
is such that

d_{\mathcal{M}_{0}}(m\mu_{1},m\mu_{2})=f(m)d_{\mathcal{P}}(\mu_{1},\mu_{2}),

for every
m\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
and every
\mu_{1},\mu_{2}\in\mathcal{P}_{c}(X)
.
Moreover,

\sup_{\mu\in\mathcal{P}_{c}(X)}d_{\mathcal{M}_{0}}(m_{1}\mu,m_{2}\mu)=|m_{1}-m_{2}|,

for every
m_{1},m_{2}\in[0,+\infty)
, which is a stronger property than assumption (ii) of Theorem
1.1
, and also implies (
1.5
) for every
\mu\in\mathcal{P}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows.
In Section
2
, we first prove some general results about distance functions defined on the direct product of two metric spaces. Then, in Section
2.1
, we exploit these results to prove our main theorems, stated here above in Section
1
. In Section
3
we prove some further results, which are natural consequences of Theorem
1.1
, and we provide some examples of meaningful distance functions defined on
\mathcal{M}_{c}^{*}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
.

2.
Metrics on product spaces

We begin with the following result, which is a generalization of Theorem
1.1
to product spaces
M=X\times Y
.

Theorem 2.1
.

Let
(X,d_{X})
and
(Y,d_{Y})
be two metric spaces, and let
M:=X\times Y
be equipped with a distance function
d_{M}:M\times M\to[0,+\infty)
satisfying the following two properties:

(1)

there exists a function
f:X\to(0,+\infty)
such that

(2.1)

d_{M}((x,y_{1}),(x,y_{2}))=f(x)d_{Y}(y_{1},y_{2}),

for every
x\in X
and every
y_{1},y_{2}\in Y
.

(2)

For every
x_{0}\in X
there exist a radius
r>0
and a positive constant
C>0
, both depending only on
x_{0}
, such that

(2.2)

\sup_{y\in Y}d_{M}((x_{0},y),(x,y))\leq C

for every
x\in B_{r}^{X}(x_{0})
.

If
X
is connected and
\diam Y=+\infty
, then
f\equiv\lambda
, for some positive constant
\lambda>0
.

Proof.

Fix
x_{0}\in X
and let
r
and
C
be as in point (ii). Notice that by (
2.2
) and the triangle inequality of
d_{M}
we have

\sup_{y\in Y}d_{M}((x_{1},y),(x_{2},y))\leq 2C,

for every
x_{1},x_{2}\in B_{r}^{X}(x_{0})
.
Thus, exploiting again the triangle inequality of
d_{M}
and also (
2.1
), we obtain

\displaystyle f(x_{1})d_{Y}(y_{1},y_{2})

\displaystyle=d_{M}((x_{1},y_{1}),(x_{1},y_{2}))

\displaystyle\leq d_{M}((x_{1},y_{1}),(x_{2},y_{1}))+d_{M}((x_{2},y_{1}),(x_{2},y_{2}))+d_{M}((x_{2},y_{2}),(x_{1},y_{2}))

\displaystyle\leq 4C+f(x_{2})d_{Y}(y_{1},y_{2}),

for every
x_{1},x_{2}\in B_{r}^{X}(x_{0})
and every
y_{1},y_{2}\in Y
. This implies that

|f(x_{1})-f(x_{2})|d_{Y}(y_{1},y_{2})\leq 4C,

and hence

\sup_{x_{1},x_{2}\in B_{r}^{X}(x_{0})}|f(x_{1})-f(x_{2})|\leq\frac{4C}{\diam Y}.

In particular, if
\diam Y=+\infty
then
f
must be constant in
B_{r}^{X}(x_{0})
, hence it is locally constant in
X
. Therefore, if we also assume
X
to be connected we conclude that
f
is globally constant, as claimed.
∎

An example of such a distance function is given by

d_{M}((x_{1},y_{1}),(x_{2},y_{2})):=d_{X}(x_{1},x_{2})+\lambda d_{Y}(y_{1},y_{2}),

for any fixed
\lambda>0
.

Remark 2.2
.

On the other hand, if
\diam Y<+\infty
, then we can easily find distances on
M
satisfying points (i) and (ii) of Theorem
2.1
, with
f
not constant. For example, let
f:X\to\mathbb{R}_{>0}
be such that

M:=\sup_{x\in X}f(x)<+\infty.

Then, the function

\displaystyle d_{M}((x_{1},y_{1}),(x_{2},y_{2})):=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}f(x_{1})d_{Y}(y_{1},y_{2})&\mbox{if }x_{1}=x_{2},\\
M\,\diam Y&\mbox{if }x_{1}\not=x_{2},\end{array}\right.

is such a distance function.

We now restrict our attention to the case in which
(X,d_{X})=\mathbb{R}_{>0}
.

Proposition 2.3
.

Let
(Y,d_{Y})
be a metric space with
\diam Y<+\infty
and let
f:\mathbb{R}_{>0}\to\mathbb{R}_{>0}
be a Lipschitz, increasing, function such that

(2.3)

\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}x_{1},x_{2}\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}\\
x_{1}\not=x_{2}\end{subarray}}\frac{|f(x_{1})-f(x_{2})|}{|x_{1}-x_{2}|}\leq\frac{1}{\diam Y}.

Then, the function

\displaystyle d_{M}((x_{1},y_{1}),(x_{2},y_{2})):=|x_{1}-x_{2}|+\min\{f(x_{1}),f(x_{2})\}d_{Y}(y_{1},y_{2}),

is a distance function on
M:=\mathbb{R}_{>0}\times Y
satisfying point (i) of Theorem
2.1
and

(ii’)

for every
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
there exists a radius
r>0
and a positive constant
C>0
, both depending only on
x_{0}
, such that

\sup_{y\in Y}d_{M}((x_{1},y),(x_{2},y))\leq C|x_{1}-x_{2}|,

for every
x_{1},x_{2}\in(x_{0}-r,x_{0}+r)
—which is more restrictive than point (ii) of Theorem
2.1
.

Proof.

We only need to verify that
d_{M}
satisfies the triangle inequality. By symmetry we can assume without loss of generality that
x_{1}\leq x_{2}
, so that

d_{M}((x_{1},y_{1}),(x_{2},y_{2}))=x_{2}-x_{1}+f(x_{1})d_{Y}(y_{1},y_{2}).

Let us now consider a third point
(x_{3},y_{3})\in M
. If
x_{3}\geq x_{1}
, then

f(x_{1})\leq\min\{f(x_{1}),f(x_{3})\}\quad\mbox{and}\quad f(x_{1})\leq\min\{f(x_{2}),f(x_{3})\},

hence, exploiting also the triangle inequality of
|\,\cdot\,|
and
d_{Y}
, we have

\displaystyle d_{M}((x_{1},y_{1})

\displaystyle,(x_{2},y_{2}))=x_{2}-x_{1}+f(x_{1})d_{Y}(y_{1},y_{2})

\displaystyle\leq x_{3}-x_{1}+|x_{2}-x_{3}|+f(x_{1})d_{Y}(y_{1},y_{3})+f(x_{1})d_{Y}(y_{2},y_{3})

\displaystyle\leq x_{3}-x_{1}+\min\{f(x_{1}),f(x_{3})\}d_{Y}(y_{1},y_{3})+|x_{2}-x_{3}|+\min\{f(x_{2}),f(x_{3})\}d_{Y}(y_{2},y_{3})

\displaystyle=d_{M}((x_{1},y_{1}),(x_{3},y_{3}))+d_{M}((x_{2},y_{2}),(x_{3},y_{3})).

Suppose now that
x_{3}<x_{1}
, so that

d_{M}((x_{1},y_{1}),(x_{3},y_{3}))=x_{1}-x_{3}+f(x_{3})d_{Y}(y_{1},y_{3}),

and

d_{M}((x_{2},y_{2}),(x_{3},y_{3}))=x_{2}-x_{3}+f(x_{3})d_{Y}(y_{2},y_{3}).

Therefore the triangle inequality is equivalent to

(2.4)

f(x_{1})d_{Y}(y_{1},y_{2})-f(x_{3})\big(d_{Y}(y_{1},y_{3})+d_{Y}(y_{2},y_{3})\big)\leq 2(x_{1}-x_{3}).

Notice that by the triangle inequality of
d_{Y}
we have

-f(x_{3})\big(d_{Y}(y_{1},y_{3})+d_{Y}(y_{2},y_{3})\big)\leq-f(x_{3})d_{Y}(y_{1},y_{2}).

Thus, by exploiting also assumption (
2.3
), we obtain

\displaystyle f(x_{1})d_{Y}(y_{1},y_{2})-f(x_{3})\big(d_{Y}(y_{1},y_{3})+d_{Y}(y_{2},y_{3})\big)

\displaystyle\leq\big(f(x_{1})-f(x_{3})\big)d_{Y}(y_{1},y_{2})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{x_{1}-x_{3}}{\diam Y}\,\diam Y,

establishing (
2.4
) and concluding the proof of the Proposition.
∎

Corollary 2.4
.

Let
(Y,d_{Y})
be a metric space,
M:=\mathbb{R}_{>0}\times Y
, and let
M_{0}:=\{0\}\cup M
be equipped with a distance function
d_{M_{0}}:M_{0}\times M_{0}\to[0,+\infty)
such that the restriction of
d_{M_{0}}
to
M
satisfies points (i) and (ii) of Theorem
2.1
. If there exist at least two distinct points
p_{1}\not=p_{2}\in Y
such that

(2.5)

\lim_{x\to 0}d_{M_{0}}(0,(x,p_{i}))=0\qquad\mbox{for }i=1,2,

then
\diam Y<+\infty
.

Proof.

We argue by contradiction and we assume that
\diam Y=+\infty
. Then, by Theorem
2.1
we know that

d_{M_{0}}((x,y_{1}),(x,y_{2}))=\lambda d_{Y}(y_{1},y_{2}),

for every
x\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
and every
y_{1},y_{2}\in Y
, for some fixed constant
\lambda>0
.
Let
x_{k}\searrow 0
. Then, by (
2.5
) we have

\displaystyle 0<d_{Y}(p_{1},p_{2})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\lambda}d_{M_{0}}((x_{k},p_{1}),(x_{k},p_{2}))

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\lambda}\big(d_{M_{0}}((x_{k},p_{1}),0)+d_{M_{0}}(0,(x_{k},p_{2}))\big)\xrightarrow{k\to\infty}0,

giving a contradiction.
∎

Lemma 2.5
.

Let
(Y,d_{Y})
be a metric space,
M:=\mathbb{R}_{>0}\times Y
, and let
M_{0}:=\{0\}\cup M
be equipped with a distance function
d_{M_{0}}:M_{0}\times M_{0}\to[0,+\infty)
. Suppose that there exist
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
and
\lambda_{0}>0
such that

d_{M_{0}}((x_{0},y_{1}),(x_{0},y_{2}))=\lambda_{0}d_{Y}(y_{1},y_{2}),

for every
y_{1},y_{2}\in Y
, and

\Lambda:=\sup_{y\in Y}d_{M_{0}}((x_{0},y),0)<+\infty.

Then
\diam Y<+\infty
.

Proof.

It is enough to notice that by the triangle inequality we have

\displaystyle d_{Y}(y_{1},y_{2})=\frac{1}{\lambda_{0}}d_{M_{0}}((x_{0},y_{1}),(x_{0},y_{2}))\leq\frac{1}{\lambda_{0}}\big(d_{M_{0}}((x_{0},y_{1}),0)+d_{M_{0}}((x_{0},y_{2}),0)\big),

for every
y_{1},y_{2}\in Y
.
This implies that

\diam Y\leq\frac{2\Lambda}{\lambda_{0}},

concluding the proof.
∎

Remark 2.6
.

If
\diam Y<+\infty
, then we can easily find many such distances
d_{M_{0}}
on
M_{0}=\{0\}\cup\mathbb{R}_{>0}\times Y
.
Indeed, let
f:\mathbb{R}_{>0}\to\mathbb{R}_{>0}
be a Lipschitz, increasing, function such that

\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}x_{1},x_{2}\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}\\
x_{1}\not=x_{2}\end{subarray}}\frac{|f(x_{1})-f(x_{2})|}{|x_{1}-x_{2}|}\leq\frac{1}{\diam Y}\quad\mbox{and}\quad\lim_{x\searrow 0}f(x)=0.

Define the function
d_{M_{0}}:M_{0}\times M_{0}\to[0,+\infty)
by setting

\displaystyle d_{M_{0}}((x_{1},y_{1}),(x_{2},y_{2})):=|x_{1}-x_{2}|+\min\{f(x_{1}),f(x_{2})\}d_{Y}(y_{1},y_{2}),

for every
(x_{1},y_{1}),(x_{2},y_{2})\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}\times Y

and

d_{M_{0}}(0,(x,y))=d_{M_{0}}((x,y),0):=x,\qquad d_{M_{0}}(0,0):=0.

Then, by arguing as in Proposition
2.3
, we can easily verify that
d_{M_{0}}
is a distance function on
M_{0}
.

2.1.
Proofs of the main results

We apply the results that we obtained in the previous Section to prove Theorem
1.1
, Corollary
1.2
and Proposition
1.3
.

Proof of Theorem
1.1
.

We consider the metric space
(Y,d_{Y}):=(S,W_{p})
, where
S\subseteq\mathcal{P}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
is the subspace given by

S:=\{\delta_{x}\,:\,x\in\Sigma\},

and the subspace

M:=\{m\delta_{x}\,:\,x\in\Sigma,m>0\}\simeq\mathbb{R}_{>0}\times S\subseteq\mathcal{M}^{*}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{n}).

Then,
(M,d_{\mathcal{M}})
satisfies the hypothesis of Theorem
2.1
. Since
W_{p}(\delta_{x},\delta_{y})=|x-y|
and
\Sigma
is unbounded, also the space
(Y,d_{Y})
is not bounded. Thus, by Theorem
2.1
we conclude that
f\equiv\lambda
, as claimed.
∎

We observe that Corollary
1.2
, with hypothesis (
1.4
), means that we cannot extend a distance function
d_{\mathcal{M}}
as in Theorem
1.1
to a distance on the whole space
\mathcal{M}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, while preserving the boundedness assumption of point (ii) also around the zero measure.
Similarly, such an extension is not possible under the very weak continuity hypothesis (
1.5
)—which, roughly speaking, translates the fact that when we fix any probability measure
\mu
and we decrease its mass, considering
m\mu
, with
m\searrow 0
, we expect to be “approaching” the zero measure. This can be seen, e.g., as a compatibility of
d_{\mathcal{M}_{0}}
with the weak convergence
m\mu\rightharpoonup 0
as
m\searrow 0
. A related result is given in Proposition
3.1
, assuming invariance of the distance with respect to isometries.

Proof of Corollary
1.2
.

The claims of the Corollary follow by Lemma
2.5
and by arguing as in the proof of Corollary
2.4
.
∎

Proof of Proposition
1.3
.

The proof follows from Proposition
2.3
and Remark
2.6
. ∎

3.
Further results and examples

In this Section, we discuss other obstructions to the extension of the Wasserstein distances to general measures, based on invariance with respect to isometries.

We prove that there exists no distance function on
\mathcal{M}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
which is invariant with respect to isometries and which satisfies (
1.1
) even on a single fiber
\{m_{0}\}\times\mathcal{P}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
. More precisely:

Proposition 3.1

(Unbounded space with zero, isometries invariance)
.

Let
p\in[1,+\infty)
. There exists no distance function
d
on
\mathcal{M}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
such that

d(\mu_{1},\mu_{2})=\lambda_{0}W_{p}\Big(\frac{\mu_{1}}{m_{0}},\frac{\mu_{2}}{m_{0}}\Big),

whenever
|\mu_{1}|=|\mu_{2}|=m_{0}
, for some
m_{0}\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
and
\lambda_{0}>0
, and which is invariant with respect to the isometries of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, i.e.

(3.1)

d(T\#\mu_{1},T\#\mu_{2})=d(\mu_{1},\mu_{2})

for every isometry
T:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
and for every
\mu_{1},\mu_{2}\in\mathcal{M}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
.

Proof.

Notice that for every
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
the function
\tau_{x}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
defined by
\tau_{x}(y):=y+x
is an isometry, and
\delta_{x}=\tau_{x}\#\delta_{0}
.
By (
3.1
) we thus obtain

\displaystyle d(m_{0}\delta_{x},0)=d(m_{0}\tau_{x}\#\delta_{0},\tau_{x}\#0)=d(\tau_{x}\#(m_{0}\delta_{0}),\tau_{x}\#0)=d(m_{0}\delta_{0},0),

for every
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. The conclusion then follows from Lemma
2.5
, by considering
(Y,d_{Y})=(S,W_{p})
, with
S:=\{\delta_{x}\,:\,x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\}
, which is not bounded, and
d_{M_{0}}=d
on
M_{0}:=\{0\}\cup\mathbb{R}_{>0}\times S\subseteq\mathcal{M}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
.
∎

Once again, in Proposition
3.1
the obstruction to the existence of such a distance function
d
is given by the combination of the presence of the zero measure with the unboundedness of
W_{p}
.
On a related note, we also observe that hypothesis (ii) in Theorem
1.1
is weaker than the combination of other very natural assumptions, like the invariance with respect to isometries and the compatibility with weak convergence when varying the mass. Thus, we obtain the following:

Proposition 3.2

(Unbounded space, isometries invariance)
.

Let
p\in[1,+\infty)
and let
d_{\mathcal{M}}:\mathcal{M}^{*}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\times\mathcal{M}^{*}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\to[0,+\infty)
be a distance function such that

d_{\mathcal{M}}(m\mu_{1},m\mu_{2})=f(m)W_{p}(\mu_{1},\mu_{2}),

for every
m\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
and every
\mu_{1},\mu_{2}\in\mathcal{P}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, for some function
f:\mathbb{R}_{>0}\to\mathbb{R}_{>0}
. Suppose that
d_{\mathcal{M}}
is invariant with respect to the isometries of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, i.e.

(3.2)

d_{\mathcal{M}}(T\#\mu_{1},T\#\mu_{2})=d_{\mathcal{M}}(\mu_{1},\mu_{2})

for every isometry
T:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
and for every
\mu_{1},\mu_{2}\in\mathcal{M}_{c}^{*}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
. Assume moreover that

(3.3)

\lim_{|m-m_{0}|\to 0}d_{\mathcal{M}}(m\mu,m_{0}\mu)=0,

for every
m_{0}\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
and every
\mu\in\mathcal{P}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
. Then,
f\equiv\lambda
, for some
\lambda>0
.

Proof.

Fix any
m_{0}\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
. By the continuity hypothesis in (
3.3
), we know in particular that there exists
r>0
, depending only on
m_{0}
, such that

d_{\mathcal{M}}(m\delta_{0},m_{0}\delta_{0})\leq 1,

for every
m\in(m_{0}-r,m_{0}+r)
. Moreover, exploiting the isometric invariance (
3.3
) and arguing as in the proof of Proposition
3.1
, we have

\displaystyle d_{\mathcal{M}}(m\delta_{x},m_{0}\delta_{x})

\displaystyle=d_{\mathcal{M}}(m\tau_{x}\#\delta_{0},m_{0}\tau_{x}\#\delta_{0})=d_{\mathcal{M}}(\tau_{x}\#(m\delta_{0}),\tau_{x}\#(m_{0}\delta_{0}))

\displaystyle=d_{\mathcal{M}}(m\delta_{0},m_{0}\delta_{0}),

for every
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Therefore,
d_{\mathcal{M}}
satisfies hypothesis (ii) in Theorem
1.1
, with
\Sigma=\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and the conclusion follows.
∎

3.1.
Examples

The simplest examples of distance functions defined on
\mathcal{M}_{c}^{*}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
which satisfy (
1.1
) (with
f
constant) are given by product metrics. Indeed, if
d
is any distance function on
(0,+\infty)
, then

d_{\mathcal{M}}(\mu_{1},\mu_{2}):=\sqrt{d^{2}(|\mu_{1}|,|\mu_{2}|)+\lambda^{2}W_{p}^{2}\Big(\frac{\mu_{1}}{|\mu_{1}|},\frac{\mu_{2}}{|\mu_{2}|}\Big)},

is such a distance function.
Of particular interest are the cases given in Example
3.3
and Example
3.4
here below, which also satisfy the boundedness condition (
1.3
).

Example 3.3
.

Let
q\in[1,+\infty)
and define

d_{\mathcal{M},q}(\mu_{1},\mu_{2}):=\left(\big||\mu_{1}|-|\mu_{2}|\big|^{q}+\lambda^{q}W_{p}^{q}\Big(\frac{\mu_{1}}{|\mu_{1}|},\frac{\mu_{2}}{|\mu_{2}|}\Big)\right)^{\frac{1}{q}},

and also

d_{\mathcal{M},\infty}(\mu_{1},\mu_{2}):=\max\left\{\big||\mu_{1}|-|\mu_{2}|\big|,\lambda W_{p}\Big(\frac{\mu_{1}}{|\mu_{1}|},\frac{\mu_{2}}{|\mu_{2}|}\Big)\right\},

for every
\mu_{1},\mu_{2}\in\mathcal{M}_{c}^{*}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
. Then,
d_{\mathcal{M},q}
is a distance function on
\mathcal{M}_{c}^{*}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
satisfying points (i) and (ii) of Theorem
1.1
, for every
q\in[1,+\infty]
. Actually, each
d_{\mathcal{M},q}
is such that

\sup_{\mu\in\mathcal{P}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}d_{\mathcal{M},q}(m_{0}\mu,m\mu)=|m_{0}-m|,

for every
m,m_{0}\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
, which is more restrictive than point (ii).

Example 3.4
.

Let
d
be a bounded distance function on
(0,+\infty)
, with
d(m_{1},m_{2})\leq C_{d}
for every
m_{1},m_{2}\in(0,+\infty)
. Then,

d_{\mathcal{M}}(\mu_{1},\mu_{2}):=d(|\mu_{1}|,|\mu_{2}|)+\lambda W_{p}\Big(\frac{\mu_{1}}{|\mu_{1}|},\frac{\mu_{2}}{|\mu_{2}|}\Big),

is a distance function on
\mathcal{M}^{*}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, satisfying point (i) of Theorem
1.1
and the global boundedness property

\sup_{\mu\in\mathcal{P}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}d_{\mathcal{M}}(m_{0}\mu,m\mu)\leq C_{d},

for every
m_{0},m\in(0,+\infty)
, which is clearly stronger than point (ii). In particular, by considering as
d
the discrete metric on
(0,+\infty)
, we obtain an example of distance function
d_{\mathcal{M}}
for which we do not have continuity in the mass, in the sense that

\lim_{|m-m_{0}|\to 0}d_{\mathcal{M}}(m_{0}\mu,m\mu)=0,

does not hold true for any
\mu\in\mathcal{P}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
.

Other less trivial examples can be obtained as the so-called warped product metrics, by exploiting the fact that
(\mathcal{P}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{n}),W_{p})
is a geodesic space, see
[
17
]
.

Example 3.5

(Warped products)
.

Let
d_{\mathcal{M},2}
be the product metric on
\mathcal{M}_{c}^{*}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
defined in Example
3.3
. Given a Lipschitz continuous curve
\gamma:I\to(\mathcal{M}_{c}^{*}(\mathbb{R}^{n}),d_{\mathcal{M},2})
, where
I:=[0,1]\subseteq\mathbb{R}
, we denote by
m_{\gamma}:I\to\mathbb{R}_{>0}
and
\rho_{\gamma}:I\to(\mathcal{P}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{n}),W_{p})
its projections, defined respectively as

m_{\gamma}(t):=|\gamma(t)|\quad\mbox{and}\quad\rho_{\gamma}(t):=\frac{\gamma(t)}{|\gamma(t)|},

which are Lipschitz continuous curves. Let
\Gamma
denote the set of all such curves.
Given a continuous function
g:(\mathcal{P}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{n}),W_{p})\to\mathbb{R}_{>0}
, we define the warped product metric, with warping function
g
, as

d_{\mathcal{M},g}(\mu_{1},\mu_{2}):=\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}\gamma\in\Gamma\\
\gamma(0)=\mu_{1},\gamma(1)=\mu_{2}\end{subarray}}\int_{I}\sqrt{g^{2}(\rho_{\gamma}(t))|m_{\gamma}^{\prime}(t)|^{2}+|\rho_{\gamma}^{\prime}(t)|^{2}}\,dt.

Here above we have denoted, with a slight abuse of notation,

|\rho_{\gamma}^{\prime}(t)|:=\lim_{s\to 0}\frac{W_{p}(\rho_{\gamma}(t),\rho_{\gamma}(t+s))}{|s|},

which exists for almost every
t\in I
, since
\rho_{\gamma}
is Lipschitz, see, e.g.,
[
2
, Theorem 2.7.6]
. This definition can be found, e.g., in
[
2
, Section 3.6.4]
, and it is easy to check that it coincides with the one given in
[
4
, Section 3.1]
.
By
[
4
, Proposition 3.1]
,
d_{\mathcal{M},g}
is a distance function on
\mathcal{M}_{c}^{*}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, and, by
[
4
, Lemma 3.2]
, it satisfies (
1.1
), as indeed

(3.4)

d_{\mathcal{M},g}(\mu_{1},\mu_{2})=W_{p}\Big(\frac{\mu_{1}}{|\mu_{1}|},\frac{\mu_{2}}{|\mu_{2}|}\Big),

for every
\mu_{1},\mu_{2}\in\mathcal{M}_{c}^{*}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
such that
|\mu_{1}|=|\mu_{2}|
. It is easy to find examples of warping functions
g
for which the corresponding distance
d_{\mathcal{M},g}
does not satisfy point (ii) of Theorem
1.1
, such as
g(\mu):=1+W_{p}(\mu,\delta_{0})
. Similarly, if we consider the warping function

g(\mu):=1+\inf_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}W_{p}(\mu,\delta_{x}),

then,
d_{\mathcal{M},g}
satisfies point (ii) of Theorem
1.1
, with
\Sigma=\mathbb{R}^{n}
. However, since we can find a sequence
\{\mu_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\subseteq\mathcal{P}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
such that
g(\mu_{k})\geq k
for every
k
, it is easy to verify that (
1.3
) does not hold on the whole of
\mathcal{P}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
. That is, there exist no
m_{0},r,C>0
for which

\sup_{\mu\in\mathcal{P}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}d_{\mathcal{M},g}(m_{0}\mu,m\mu)\leq C,

for every
m\in(m_{0}-r,m_{0}+r)
.

Furthermore, we observe that for every choice of warping function
g
, the distance
d_{\mathcal{M},g}
is compatible with weak convergence when varying the mass, as indeed

d_{\mathcal{M},g}(m_{0}\mu,m\mu)\leq g(\mu)|m-m_{0}|,

for every
\mu\in\mathcal{P}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
. Nevertheless, since (
3.4
) holds true, by arguing as in the proof of Corollary
2.4
we see that no such distance can be extended to a distance
d
defined on the whole of
\mathcal{M}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
and with the property that

\lim_{m\searrow 0}d(0,m\mu_{i})=0,

for at least two different
\mu_{1}\not=\mu_{2}\in\mathcal{P}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
.
