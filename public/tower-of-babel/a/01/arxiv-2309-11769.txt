A Finiteness Theorem in Galois Cohomology
Dylon Chow

Abstract
. We prove the finiteness of the kernel of the localization map in the Galois cohomology of a connected reductive group over a global field.



1.
Introduction

The purpose of this note is to prove:

Theorem 1
.

(Borel-Serre, Borel-Prasad, Nisnevich). Let
G
be a connected reductive group defined over a global field
F
. Let
V
denote the set of places of
F
. The fibers of the canonical map

\lambda\colon H^{1}(F,G)\to\prod_{v\in V}H^{1}(F_{v},G)

are finite.

In more geometric language, this says that given a principal homogeneous space
X
over
F
for
G
, the principal homogeneous spaces over
F
for
G
which are isomorphic to
X
over
F_{v}
for all
v
form a finite number of isomorphism classes over
F
. In the case when
F
is a number field, this is
[
4
, Theorem 7.1]
. By a twisting argument, it suffices to prove that the kernel of
\lambda
is finite. A proof that the kernel of
\lambda
is finite is given in
[
1
, Theorem 6.8]
. As Platonov and Rapinchuk point out (see p.316 of
[
11
]
), there is a fairly standard proof in the case when
G
is a torus, but the proof of Borel and Serre in the general case is very different and appeals to the reduction theory of adele groups.

On p.176 of
[
3
]
, Borel and Prasad gave a proof in the function field case for any connected reductive group
G
. They first prove the theorem for semisimple groups in
[
2
, Theorem B.1]
. For arbitrary reductive groups, they use the finiteness of the class number of a semisimple group
[
2
, Proposition 3.9]
, the proof of which uses lower bounds on covolumes of arithmetic subgroups.

In this note, we give a totally different proof of the theorem. We take as given the case when
G
is a torus. We prove the theorem in two steps. The first step is the case when the derived group of
G
is simply connected. Then we use a
z
-extension to prove the theorem in general.

2.
Proof of the Theorem

First we record the statement of Theorem
1
for tori:

Proposition 2
.

Theorem
1
holds when
G
is a torus.

Proof.

This follows from the global Nakayama-Tate duality theorem. See
[
11
]
, Theorem 6.3 and the discussion preceding it. This reference only states Nakayama-Tate duality for number fields, but the same proof works for function fields. See
[
13
]
for a proof of Nakayama-Tate duality.
∎

In the statement below, a local field is a finite extension of either
{\mathbb{R}},{\mathbb{Q}}_{p}
, or
\mathbb{F}_{p}((t))
for a prime
p
.

Proposition 3
.

(1)

If
H
is a connected reductive group over a local field
k
, then
H^{1}(k,H)
is finite.

(2)

If
H
is a semisimple simply connected group over a nonarchimedean local field
k
, then
H^{1}(k,H)=1
.

(3)

Let
H
be a semisimple, simply connected algebraic group over a global field
k
. The canonical map

H^{1}(k,H)\to\prod_{v}H^{1}(k_{v},H)

is injective.

Proof.

(1)

In characteristic
0
, this is proven in
[
4
]
. For a textbook reference, see
[
11
, Theorem 6.14]
. In characteristic
p>0
, see
[
12
, III.4.3, Remark 2]
.

(2)

This is due to Bruhat-Tits. See
[
5
, Thm. in section 4.7]
.

(3)

In the number field case, this was proved in
[
7
]
except for the case when
G
has a factor of type
E_{8}
, which was proved in
[
6
]
. The function field case is proved in
[
8
]
- in fact, in this case,
H^{1}(k,H)=0
.

∎

Now we proceed to the proof of Theorem
1
. The first step is to handle the case when the derived group of
G
is simply connected.

Proposition 4
.

If the derived group of
G
is simply connected, then Theorem 1 holds.

Proof.

We consider the short exact sequence

1\to G_{\text{der}}\to G\to D\to 1,

where
D
denotes the quotient of
G
by its derived group
G_{\text{der}}
. This gives us a commutative diagram with exact rows

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces H^{1}(F,G_{\text{der}})}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces H^{1}(F,G)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces H^{1}(F,D)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\prod_{v}H^{1}(F_{v},G_{\text{der}})}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\prod_{v}H^{1}(F_{v},G)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\prod_{v}H^{1}(F_{v},D).}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\rho}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\alpha}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\sigma}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\beta}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\gamma}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\rho^{\prime}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\sigma^{\prime}}

We want to show that
\text{ker}(\beta)
is finite. We have

\text{ker}(\beta)\subset\sigma^{-1}(\text{ker}(\gamma)),

and by Proposition
2
,
\text{ker}(\gamma)
is finite. Proposition
3
tells us that
H^{1}(F_{v},G_{\text{der}})
is trivial for all finite places
v
, that
H^{1}(F_{v},G_{\text{der}})
is finite for all infinite places
v
, and that
\alpha
is injective. Together, these statements imply that
H^{1}(F,G_{\text{der}})
is finite. Thus
\text{ker}(\sigma)=\text{im}(\rho)
is also finite. Now we use a standard twisting argument (see section I.5.4 of
[
12
]
). Let
\zeta\in\text{ker}(\gamma)
, and choose a 1-cocycle
z
that represents a class in
\sigma^{-1}(\zeta)
. By twisting the sequence above by
z
, we obtain a short exact sequence

1\to(G_{z})_{\text{der}}\to G_{z}\to D\to 1.

This induces a map
H^{1}(F,G_{z})\to H^{1}(F,D)
whose kernel is
\sigma^{-1}(\zeta)
(after identifying
H^{1}(F,G)
with
H^{1}(F,G_{z})
). Therefore, for every element
\zeta\in\text{ker}(\gamma)
,
\sigma^{-1}(\zeta)
is finite. and so
\text{ker}(\beta)
is finite.

∎

We have proved Theorem
1
when
G_{\text{der}}
is simply connected. Now we use
z
-extensions to prove it in general. We choose a finite Galois extension
E/F
splitting
G
and an extension

1\to Z\to G^{\prime}\to G\to 1

such that
Z
is an induced torus (i.e., it is obtained by Weil restriction of scalars from a split
E
-torus) and the derived group of
G^{\prime}
is simply connected. Such an extension exists - see, for instance, the discussion in the proof of Lemma A.1 of
[
9
]
. This gives a commutative diagram with exact rows

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces 0}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces H^{1}(F,G^{\prime})}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces H^{1}(F,G)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces H^{2}(F,Z)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces 0}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\prod_{v}H^{1}(F_{v},G^{\prime})}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\prod_{v}H^{1}(F_{v},G)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\prod_{v}H^{2}(F_{v},Z).}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\alpha}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\gamma_{1}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\beta}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\gamma_{2}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\gamma_{3}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\alpha^{\prime}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\beta^{\prime}}

The
0
on the leftmost term in the top row comes from the fact that the
F
-torus
Z
is induced. Since
Z\times_{\text{Spec}(F)}\text{Spec}(F^{\prime})
is also induced over
F^{\prime}
for all fields
F^{\prime}\supset F
, the leftmost term in the bottom row is also
0
. We will use the following lemma:

Lemma 5
.

The homomorphism
H^{2}(F,Z)\to\prod_{v}H^{2}(F_{v},Z)
is injective.

Proof.

The torus
Z
is the Weil restriction of some split torus over
E
, say
Z=R_{E/F}(S)
where
S\cong\mathbb{G}_{m}^{r}
. By Shapiro’s lemma we have

H^{2}(F,Z)\cong H^{2}(E,(\mathbb{G}_{m,E})^{r}).

Let
v
be a place of
F
. Then we have (see
[
10
]
, p.58)

\displaystyle(R_{E/F}(S))_{F_{v}}

\displaystyle\cong R_{E\otimes_{F}F_{v}}(S_{E\otimes_{F}F_{v}})

\displaystyle\cong\left(\prod_{w\lvert v}R_{E_{w}/F_{v}}(\mathbb{G}_{m})\right)^{r}.

Therefore,

\displaystyle H^{2}(F_{v},Z)

\displaystyle\cong\prod_{w\lvert v}H^{2}(F_{v},R_{E_{w}/F_{v}}(\mathbb{G}_{m})^{r})

\displaystyle=\prod_{w\lvert v}H^{2}(E_{w},\mathbb{G}_{m})^{r}.

Thus the map
H^{2}(F,Z)\to\prod_{v}H^{2}(F_{v},Z)
becomes

(\text{Br}(E))^{r}\to(\prod_{w\in V_{E}}\text{Br}(E_{w}))^{r},

which is injective by global class field theory.
∎

Now we return to the proof of the theorem. We want to show that
\text{ker}(\gamma_{2})
is finite. Suppose that
x\in\text{ker}(\gamma_{2})
. Then
\gamma_{3}(\beta(x))=0
. By Lemma
5
,
\beta(x)=0
. Thus
x\in\text{im}(\alpha)
, say
\alpha(w)=x
. But then
\alpha^{\prime}(\gamma_{1}(w))=0
, and so
\gamma_{1}(w)=0
. Therefore,
\text{ker}(\gamma_{2})\subset\alpha(\text{ker}(\gamma_{1}))
. Since
\text{ker}(\gamma_{1})
is finite by Proposition
4
,
\text{ker}(\gamma_{2})
is also finite. This completes the proof of Theorem
1
.
