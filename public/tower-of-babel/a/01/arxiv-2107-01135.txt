Fractional boundary value problems
Mirko D’Ovidio

Abstract
. We study some functionals associated with a process driven by a fractional boundary value problem (FBVP for short). By FBVP we mean a Cauchy problem with boundary condition written in terms of a fractional equation, that is an equation involving time-fractional derivative in the sense of Caputo. We focus on lifetimes and additive functionals characterizing the boundary conditions. We show that the corresponding additive functionals are related to the fractional telegraph equations. Moreover, the fractional order of the derivative gives a unified condition including the elastic and the sticky cases among the others.



Keywords:
fractional boundary conditions, Robin conditions, Wentzell conditions, elastic Brownian motions, sticky Brownian motions, lifetimes, telegraph processes, Mittag-Leffler random variables

1.
Introduction

In this work we focus on the fractional boundary value problem

(1.1)

\begin{cases}\displaystyle\frac{\partial u}{\partial t}=\Delta u,\quad t>0,\;x\in[0,\infty)\\
\displaystyle\eta D^{\alpha/2}_{t}u(t,0)=\sigma\frac{\partial u}{\partial x}(t,0)-c\,u(t,0),\quad t>0\\
\displaystyle u(0,x)=f(x),\quad x>0\end{cases}

(we denote by
\Delta
the operator
\partial^{2}_{xx}
) with
\alpha\in(0,1]
,
\eta\geq 0

\sigma\geq 0
,
c\geq 0
and

\displaystyle D^{\alpha/2}_{t}u(t,x)=\frac{1}{\Gamma(1-\alpha/2)}\int_{0}^{t}\frac{\partial u}{\partial s}(s,x)\,(t-s)^{-\alpha/2}ds,

that is the Caputo derivative. We are interested in the probabilistic representation of the solution and the associated functionals.

This problem has been inspired by the following observation. Let us consider the Cauchy problem

\displaystyle\frac{\partial u}{\partial t}(t,x)=\Delta u(t,x),\quad t\geq 0,\;x\in[0,\infty),\quad u(0,x)=f(x)

with the boundary condition

\displaystyle\eta D^{\beta}_{t}u(t,0)=\sigma\frac{\partial u}{\partial x}(t,0)-c\,u(t,0),\quad t>0

where
\beta\in(0,1]
. The Caputo derivative enjoys the continuity property w.r. to the order
\beta
. In particular, as
\beta\uparrow 1
the Caputo derivative becomes
D^{1}_{t}u=\partial u/\partial t
and the problem (
1.1
) can be intuitively regarded as the Cauchy problem associated with the domain

(1.2)

\displaystyle\left\{\varphi\in C^{2}([0,\infty))\to\mathbb{R}:\eta\varphi^{\prime\prime}(0^{+})=\sigma\varphi^{\prime}(0^{+})-c\varphi(0^{+})\right\}.

As
\beta\to 1/2
we have that (
1.1
) must be associated with the set of functions

(1.3)

\displaystyle\left\{\varphi\in C^{2}([0,\infty))\to\mathbb{R}:-\eta\varphi^{\prime}(0^{+})=\sigma\varphi^{\prime}(0^{+})-c\varphi(0^{+})\right\}

where
-\eta\varphi^{\prime}
is somehow obtained from the fact that
\eta D^{1}_{t}u=\eta\Delta u
and
\eta D^{\beta}_{t}u
coincides, for
\beta=1/2
, with
-\eta\partial_{x}u
(at
x=0
). Thus, the fractional boundary condition involving the Caputo derivative introduces a characterization of the associated process given in terms of an elastic Brownian motion for
\beta\in(0,1/2]
and in terms of a Sticky Brownian motion for
\beta\in(1/2,1]
. The domains (
1.2
) and (
1.3
) are often associated with the Wentzell and Robin boundary conditions. The domain (
1.3
) suggests also an interesting balance between
\eta
and
\sigma
in terms of reflection, especially for negative
\sigma
. Concerning (
1.2
) and the problem with
\beta\in(1/2,1]
, the probabilistic representation is still an open problem. The author is working in this direction.

In order to give a clear picture about our results we first underline the main aspects relating such results with the well-known theory of time-changed processes. Let us consider the Cauchy problem

(1.4)

\begin{cases}\displaystyle\frac{\partial u}{\partial t}=\Delta u\\
\displaystyle u(0,x)=f(x)\end{cases}

for which we have the probabilistic representation of the solution given by
\mathbf{E}_{x}[f(\widetilde{X}_{t})]
. The process
\widetilde{X}
is a Brownian motion with generator
(\Delta,D(\Delta))
and the boundary conditions can be associated with a multiplicative functional
M_{t}=\exp A_{t}
where
A_{t}
is an additive functional (
[
4
]
). Thus, the solution to (
1.4
) has the representation

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}_{x}[f(\widetilde{X}_{t})]=\mathbf{E}_{x}[f(X_{t})\,M_{t}]

where
\widetilde{X}
is the part process of
X
in a domain according with
M_{t}
. For
\alpha\in(0,1]
, the fractional Cauchy problem

(1.5)

\begin{cases}\displaystyle D^{\alpha}_{t}u=\Delta u\\
\displaystyle u(0,x)=f(x)\end{cases}

has been extensively investigated by many researchers. It is well-known that the time-changed process
\widetilde{X}\circ L
can be considered in order to solve (
1.5
) where
\widetilde{X}
has generator
(\Delta,D(\Delta))
and the independent random time
L
is an inverse to a stable subordinator
H
. The random clock
L
for the base process
X
seems to introduce a delaying effect along the paths of
X
. The additive functional turns out to be time-changed as well, in place of
A
we have to consider
A\circ L
, that is

(1.6)

\displaystyle A_{L_{t}}

which characterizes the boundary behaviour of the time-changed process. As
\alpha\to 1
the problem (
1.5
) becomes the problem (
1.4
). This is due to the fact that
L_{t}\to t
almost surely as
\alpha\to 1
. In the special case
\alpha/2\to 1
, the problem (
1.1
) takes a completely different face. In the literature the following slightly modified version of (
1.1
) has been considered (
[
34
;
35
]
)

(1.7)

\begin{cases}\displaystyle\frac{\partial u}{\partial t}=\mu\nabla u+\Delta u\\
\displaystyle\frac{\partial u}{\partial t}(t,0)=\sigma\frac{\partial u}{\partial x}(t,0),\quad t>0\\
\displaystyle u(0,x)=f(x)\end{cases}

with
\mu,\sigma\in\mathbb{R}
(we denote by
\nabla u
the first derivative of
u
). The ideal interpretation of (
1.7
) can be given by considering the set of functions

(1.8)

\displaystyle\left\{\varphi\in C^{2}([0,\infty))\to\mathbb{R}:\mu\varphi^{\prime}(0^{+})+\varphi^{\prime\prime}(0^{+})=\sigma\varphi^{\prime}(0^{+})\right\}

from which the sticky condition immediately emerges. For
\mu=0
, the sticky condition introduces the time change

(1.9)

\displaystyle V_{t}=t+\frac{1}{\sigma}\gamma_{t}

where
\gamma_{t}
is a local time at zero. In particular, a reflecting Brownian motion time-changed by the inverse
V^{-1}_{t}
can be considered in order to solve the heat equation with sticky condition at zero.

In the present work, the first result we provide is that the formula (
1.6
) does not hold. Indeed, the functional associated with the fractional boundary condition in (
1.1
) is a composition as expected but it turns out to be

(1.10)

\displaystyle\bar{L}_{A_{t}}

where
\bar{L}
is an inverse process playing the role of
L
in (
1.6
). Moreover, we show that
\bar{L}
is an inverse to the process

\displaystyle\bar{H}_{t}=\frac{\sigma}{\eta}t+H_{t}

which is therefore very close to the probabilistic interpretation of the problem (
1.7
). The process
\bar{L}
can be associated to a fractional telegraph equation. We recall that
L
in (
1.6
) is an inverse to
H
, thus the composition (
1.10
) maintains the same structure of (
1.6
). In particular, if
\sigma=0
, then the random times
\bar{L}
and

are identical in law. Only in this case,
\sigma=0
, we have that
L\circ A
equals in law
A\circ L
. In (
1.1
),
\alpha/2\in(0,1/2]
and therefore we are not able (up to now) to obtain (
1.7
) as a special case of (
1.1
). We study the lifetimes of the processes associated with (
1.1
) and provide a connection with the fractional telegraph equation.

2.
Fractional derivatives and random times

Let us consider
b>0
and the set
AC([0,b])
of continuous functions with derivative in
L_{1}([0,b])
. Denote by
v^{\prime}
the derivative of
v
as usual. Thus,
v\in AC([0,b])
is such that
v^{\prime}=g\in L_{1}([0,b])
, that is
v
has the representation

\displaystyle v(t)=v(0)+\int_{0}^{t}g(s)ds.

We notice that the Sobolev space
W^{1,1}([0,b])=\{\varphi\in L_{1}([0,b])\,:\,\varphi^{\prime}\in L_{1}([0,b])\}
endowed with the norm
\|\varphi\|_{W^{1,1}}=\|\varphi\|_{L_{1}}+\|\varphi^{\prime}\|_{L_{1}}
coincides with the space
AC([0,b])
for
b<\infty
. Let
v\in AC([0,b])
and
\alpha\in(0,1)
. For the Riemann-Liouville derivative

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}_{t}v(t):=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\Gamma(1-\alpha)}\frac{d}{dt}\int_{0}^{t}v(s)(t-s)^{-\alpha}ds

(2.1)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\Gamma(1-\alpha)}\left(\frac{v(0)}{t^{\alpha}}+\int_{0}^{t}v^{\prime}(s)(t-s)^{-\alpha}ds\right)

we have that (
2.1
) exists a. e. on
[0,b]
and
\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}_{t}v\in L_{p}([0,b])
with
1\leq p\leq 1/\alpha
(see
[
13
, page 28]
). The formula (
2.1
) introduces the Caputo derivative

(2.2)

\displaystyle D^{\alpha}_{t}v(t):=\frac{1}{\Gamma(1-\alpha)}\int_{0}^{t}v^{\prime}(s)(t-s)^{-\alpha}ds=\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}_{t}\big(u(t)-u(0)\big).

In the literature, the convolution-type operator (
2.2
) is also known as Caputo-Dzherbashian derivative. Indeed the second author actively investigated this operator starting from the papers
[
17
;
18
]
. We immediately see that
\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}_{t}
and
D^{\alpha}_{t}
coincide for compactly supported functions in
C^{1}_{c}([0,b))
. We also observe that the derivative
D^{\alpha}_{t}v
is well-defined on the set of functions for which

v^{\prime}(s)(t-s)^{-\alpha}\in L_{1}([0,t))\quad\textrm{for any}\quad t>0.

In particular, for a function
v
such that
|v^{\prime}(s)|\leq s^{\beta-1}
,

\displaystyle|D^{\alpha}_{t}v(t)|\leq D^{\alpha}_{t}t^{\beta}=\frac{\Gamma(\beta+1)}{\Gamma(\beta-\alpha+1)}t^{\beta-\alpha},\quad t>0

which can be easily obtained from the Beta integral

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}s^{\beta-1}(1-s)^{-\alpha}ds=\frac{\Gamma(\beta)\Gamma(1-\alpha)}{\Gamma(\beta-\alpha+1)}.

This fact will be considered below in the definition of the spaces
C^{\alpha}_{L}
and
D^{\alpha}_{L}
.

The fractional derivatives above are directly related with stable subordinators and their inverses. For
\alpha\in(0,1)
, let
H
be the stable subordinator for which

(2.3)

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}_{x}[e^{-\lambda H_{t}}]=e^{-t\lambda^{\alpha}},\quad\lambda\geq 0.

The inverse process
L
is defined as
L_{t}:=\inf\{s\geq 0\,:\,H_{s}>t\}
. The subordinator
H
may have jumps, so that the inverse
L
may have plateaux. This gives a clear picture about the well-known behaviours of the time-changed processes obtained by considering
H
and
L
as random times. We denote by
h
and
l
the densities

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}_{0}(H_{t}\in dx)=h(t,x)\,dx\quad\mathbf{P}_{0}(L_{t}\in dx)=l(t,x)\,dx.

After some calculation, by exploiting the equality

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}_{0}(H_{s}>t)=\mathbf{P}_{0}(s>L_{t}),

we obtain

(2.4)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\lambda t}l(t,x)\,dt=\lambda^{\alpha-1}e^{-x\lambda^{\alpha}},\quad\lambda>0.

We recall the following result which will be useful further on,

(2.5)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\lambda t}\,h(t,x)\,dt=x^{\alpha-1}E_{\alpha,\alpha}(-\lambda x^{\alpha})=-\frac{1}{\lambda}\frac{d}{dx}E_{\alpha}(-\lambda x^{\alpha}).

We also recall that the solution to
D^{\alpha}_{t}u(t)=-cu(t)
with
u(0)=1
is the analytic function
u(t)=E_{\alpha}(-ct^{\alpha})
, that is
u\in C^{\infty}((0,\infty))
is the Mittag-Leffler function

(2.6)

\displaystyle E_{\alpha}(-ct^{\alpha})=\sum_{k\geq 0}\frac{(-ct^{\alpha})^{k}}{\Gamma(\alpha k+1)},\quad t\geq 0,\quad c\geq 0.

As we can see (
2.6
) is not an element of
L_{1}((0,\infty))
but it is in
AC([0,\infty))
.

Let us introduce the space

\displaystyle W^{1,1}_{0}([0,\infty))=\{\varphi\in L_{1}([0,\infty)\,:\,\varphi^{\prime}\in L_{1}([0,\infty),\,\varphi(0)=0\}.

The Riemann-Liouville derivative
-\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}_{x}
gives the generator of the
\alpha
-stable subordinator
H
. In particular,
h\in D(-\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}_{x})
only if
h\in W^{1,1}_{0}([0,\infty))
. It is well-known that for the inverse process
L
, the density satisfies the fractional equation

\displaystyle D^{\alpha}_{t}\,l=-\frac{\partial l}{\partial x}.

For the sake of simplicity we now set
\eta=1
and we consider the process
\bar{H}_{t}=\sigma t+H_{t}
and its inverse
\bar{L}_{t}=\inf\{s\geq 0\,:\,\bar{H}_{s}>t\}
. The drift coefficient
\sigma
is assumed to be non negative. However, we have interesting applications of our results in case we allow
\sigma\in(-1,0)
, that is the case of subordinators with negative drift. Let us denote by
\bar{h}
and
\bar{l}
the corresponding densities. For the sake of all-inclusive presentation we provide the following results which have been similarly obtained and stated in the literature.

Theorem 2.1
.

Let us consider

\displaystyle\alpha\in(0,1],\quad\sigma\geq 0.

The solution on
C^{1,1}((0,\infty)\times W^{1,1}_{0}([0,\infty)),[0,\infty))
of the problem

(2.7)

\begin{cases}\displaystyle\frac{\partial v}{\partial t}=-\sigma\frac{\partial v}{\partial x}-\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}_{x}v\\
\displaystyle v(0,x)=f(x),\quad x>0,\quad f\in W^{1,1}_{0}([0,\infty))\end{cases}

is written as

\displaystyle v(t,x)=\int_{0}^{x}f(x-y)\,\bar{h}(t,y)\,dy=\mathbf{E}[f(x-\bar{H}_{t})\mathbf{1}_{(t<\bar{L}_{x})}].

The solution on
C^{1,1}(AC((0,\infty))\times(0,\infty),(0,\infty))
to the problem

(2.8)

\begin{cases}\displaystyle\sigma\frac{\partial w}{\partial t}+D^{\alpha}_{t}w=-\frac{\partial w}{\partial x}\\
\displaystyle w(0,x)=f(x),\quad x>0,\quad f\in C_{b}(0,\infty)\\
\displaystyle w(t,0)=0,\quad t>0\end{cases}

is written as

\displaystyle w(t,x)=\int_{0}^{x}f(x-y)\,\bar{l}(t,y)\,dy=\mathbf{E}[f(x-\bar{L}_{t})\mathbf{1}_{(t<\bar{H}_{x})}].

Proof.

Since
v(t,\cdot)\in W^{1,1}_{0}([0,\infty))
,
t>0
, we have that

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\xi x}\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}_{x}v(t,x)\,dx=\xi^{\alpha}\,\widetilde{v}(t,\xi).

From the problem (
2.7
) we get the double Laplace transform

\displaystyle\widetilde{v}(\lambda,\xi)\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(\lambda+\sigma\xi+\xi^{\alpha})^{-1}\widetilde{f}(\xi)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\lambda t}e^{-t(\sigma\xi+\xi^{\alpha})}dt\right)\widetilde{f}(\xi)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\lambda t}\mathbf{E}_{0}[e^{-\xi\bar{H}_{t}}]dt\right)\widetilde{f}(\xi)

where

(2.9)

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}_{0}[e^{-\xi\bar{H}_{t}}]=\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\xi x}\bar{h}(t,x)\,dx.

Thus,

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}_{0}[e^{-\xi\bar{H}_{t}}]\widetilde{f}(\xi)=\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\xi x}\int_{0}^{x}f(x-y)\bar{h}(t,y)\,dy\,dx

and the result follows. Concerning the probabilistic representation, we only notice that

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{x}f(x-y)\bar{h}(t,y)\,dy=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}f(x-y)\bar{h}(t,y)\,\mathbf{1}_{(y<x)}dy

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}_{0}[f(x-\bar{H}_{t})\mathbf{1}_{(\bar{H}_{t}<x)}]

with
(\bar{H}_{t}<x)\equiv(t<\bar{L}_{x})
under
\mathbf{P}_{0}
.

From the problem (
2.8
) we have that
w(\cdot,x)\in AC((0,\infty))
for any
x\in(0,\infty)
, thus the derivatives in time are well-defined. In particular, we have the extra-condition
\partial_{t}w\in C((0,\infty))\cap L_{1}((0,\infty))
. We get that

\displaystyle\widetilde{w}(\lambda,\xi)\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(\sigma\lambda+\lambda^{\alpha}+\xi)^{-1}\frac{\sigma\lambda+\lambda^{\alpha}}{\lambda}\widetilde{f}(\xi)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\xi x}\frac{\sigma\lambda+\lambda^{\alpha}}{\lambda}e^{-x(\sigma\lambda+\lambda^{\alpha})}\right)\widetilde{f}(\xi)

where

\displaystyle\frac{\sigma\lambda+\lambda^{\alpha}}{\lambda}e^{-x(\sigma\lambda+\lambda^{\alpha})}=

\displaystyle-\frac{d}{dx}\frac{1}{\lambda}e^{-x(\sigma\lambda+\lambda^{\alpha})}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{d}{dx}\frac{1}{\lambda}\mathbf{E}_{0}[e^{-\lambda\bar{H}_{x}}]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{d}{dx}\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\lambda t}\mathbf{P}_{0}(\bar{H}_{x}<t)\,dt

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{d}{dx}\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\lambda t}\mathbf{P}_{0}(x<\bar{L}_{t})\,dt

(2.10)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\lambda t}\bar{l}(t,x)\,dt.

From this, we write

\displaystyle w(t,x)=\int_{0}^{x}f(x-y)\bar{l}(t,y)\,dy

that is

\displaystyle w(t,x)=\mathbf{E}_{0}[f(x-\bar{L}_{t})\mathbf{1}_{(\bar{L}_{t}<x)}].

As before, by noticing that
(\bar{L}_{t}<x)\equiv(t<\bar{H}_{x})
under
\mathbf{P}_{0}
we get the result.
∎

We observe that

\displaystyle\bar{h}(t,x)>0,\quad t\geq 0,\quad x\in(0,\infty)

and

\displaystyle\bar{h}(t,x)=0,\quad t\geq 0,\quad x\in(-\infty,0]

whereas

\displaystyle\bar{l}(t,x)>0,\quad t\geq 0,\quad x\in\left[0,\frac{t}{\sigma}\right)

and

\displaystyle\bar{l}(t,x)=0,\quad t\geq 0,\quad x\in(-\infty,0)\cup\left[\frac{t}{\sigma},\infty\right).

Indeed, by construction,

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}_{0}(\bar{L}_{t}>t/\sigma)=\mathbf{P}_{0}(t>\bar{H}_{t/\sigma})=\mathbf{P}_{0}(0>H_{t/\sigma})=0.

Moreover, from (
2.9
) in the previous proof, we have that,
\forall\,t>0
,

\displaystyle\xi^{n}\widetilde{\bar{h}}(t,\xi)\to 0\quad\textrm{as}\quad\xi\to\infty\quad\forall\,n\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}

and

\displaystyle\xi^{n}\widetilde{\bar{h}}(t,\xi)\to 0\quad\textrm{as}\quad\xi\to 0\quad\forall\,n\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}.

In particular,
\forall t>0
,
\forall n\in\mathbb{N}
,
\xi^{n}\widetilde{\bar{h}}(t,\xi)\in C_{0}((0,\infty))
and

\displaystyle\exists M_{H}>0\;:\;\forall t>0,\;|\xi^{n}\widetilde{\bar{h}}(t,\xi)|\leq M_{H}.

Thus,
\forall t>0
,
\bar{h}(t,\cdot)\in C^{\infty}((0,\infty))
. Furthermore, we easily see that
\forall t>0
,
\bar{h}(t,\cdot)\in L_{1}((0,\infty)
. Concerning the inverse process, from (
2
) we have that,
\forall\,x>0
,

\displaystyle\lambda^{n}\widetilde{\bar{l}}(\lambda,x)\to 0\quad\textrm{as}\quad\lambda\to\infty\quad\forall\,n\in\mathbb{N}

and

\displaystyle\lambda^{n}\widetilde{\bar{l}}(\lambda,x)\to 0\quad\textrm{as}\quad\lambda\to 0\quad\forall\,n\in\mathbb{N}.

Thus,

\displaystyle\exists M_{L}>0\;:\;\forall x>0,\;|\lambda^{n}\widetilde{\bar{l}}(\lambda,x)|\leq M_{L}.

We conclude that
\forall x>0
,
\bar{l}(\cdot,x)\in C^{\infty}((0,\infty))
and
\bar{l}(\cdot,x)\notin L_{1}((0,\infty))
. As
x=0
in (
2
), we have the Laplace transform of the tail of a Lévy measure, that is the measure associated with
\bar{H}
.

We also notice that
\bar{H}_{t}
exhibits non-decreasing paths, then

\displaystyle\bar{L}_{t}=\inf\{s\geq 0\,:\,\bar{H}_{s}\notin(0,t)\}

can be also regarded as an exit time. On the other hand, the processes
\bar{L}_{x}
and
\bar{H}_{x}
can be respectively regarded as the lifetimes for
\bar{H}_{t}
,
t\geq 0
and
\bar{L}_{t}
,
t\geq 0
both with respect to the level
x\in[0,\infty)
.

The general theory on PDEs and subordinators with drift can be found in the book

. The fractional telegraph equation has been studied also in

where

, if we assume that

. The process

3.
Lifetimes for processes driven by a FBVP

Let
\widetilde{X}=(\widetilde{X}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
be the process with generator
(G,D(G))
where
G=\Delta
and

\displaystyle D(G)=\{\varphi\,:\,\varphi,\Delta\varphi\in C((0,\infty))\,,\,\partial_{x}\varphi(0)=c\,\varphi(0)\}

with elastic coefficient
c\geq 0
. The semigroup associated with the elastic Brownian motion
\widetilde{X}
can be written by considering the couple
(X_{t},M_{t})_{t\geq 0}
as discussed in the introduction, that is

4.
An intuitive case

An helpful intuitive reading is given by the fact that, from the heat equation

D^{1}_{t}u=\Delta u,

we have that the boundary condition involving
D^{1}_{t}u(t,0)
introduces a second-order boundary condition
\Delta u(t,0)
. On the other hand, the condition
D^{1/2}_{t}u(t,0)
should introduce a condition involving the operator

\displaystyle-(-\Delta)^{1/2}u(t,0)=-\frac{\partial u}{\partial x}(t,0).

The last identity may hold for functions extended with zero on the negative part of the real line, that is we are dealing with the Riesz operator
d\varphi/d|x|
acting on
\varphi:[0,\infty)\to(0,\infty)
such that
\varphi=0
on
(-\infty,0)
. The definition of fractional Laplacian can be understood in the sense of Phillips (Bochner subordination). Thus, for
\beta\in(0,1]
,

\displaystyle(-\partial_{x})^{\beta}\varphi(x):=\int_{0}^{\infty}\left(e^{-y\partial_{x}}\varphi(x)-\varphi(x)\right)\frac{y^{-\beta-1}}{\Gamma(1-\beta)}dy

is an operator of order
\beta
where
e^{-y\partial_{x}}\varphi(x)=\varphi(x-y)
is the translation semigroup and

\displaystyle(-\Delta)^{\beta}\varphi(x):=\int_{0}^{\infty}\left(e^{-y\Delta}\varphi(x)-\varphi(x)\right)\frac{y^{-\beta-1}}{\Gamma(1-\beta)}dy

is an operator of order
2\beta
. For
\varphi
extended with zero on
(-\infty,0)
the operator
(-\partial_{x})^{\beta}
corresponds to the Marchaud derivative of order
\beta\leq 1
whereas, as
\beta=1/2
the operator
(-\Delta)^{\beta}
corresponds to a first order derivative. Thus, in the special case
\beta=1/2
, the equality

D^{1/2}_{t}u(t,0)=-\frac{\partial u}{\partial x}(t,0),

implies that the condition

D^{1/2}_{t}u(t,0)=-c\,u(t,0)

plays the role of

\frac{\partial u}{\partial x}(t,0)=c\,u(t,0)

which is indeed a Robin boundary condition.

As
\alpha\to 1
in Theorem
3.1
we obtain the following helpful and intuitive result.

Corollary 1
.

Let us consider

\displaystyle\eta\geq 0,\quad\sigma\geq 0,\quad c\geq 0.

The solution
u\in C^{1,2}((0,\infty)\times[0,\infty);[0,\infty))\cap D^{1}_{L}
to the problem

(4.1)

\begin{cases}\displaystyle\frac{\partial u}{\partial t}(t,x)=\Delta u(t,x),\quad(t,x)\in(0,\infty)\times(0,\infty)\\

\displaystyle u(0,x)=\mathbf{1}_{[0,\infty)}(x),\quad x>0\end{cases}

X_{t}
started at

Since

∎

6.
Some asymptotic results

We discuss the following results moving from the fact introduced by Feller (1958) as a conjecture (Feller conjecture) which has been well-studied after its statement. The boundary condition

\displaystyle(1-\rho)\,u^{\prime}(0)=\rho\,u(0),\quad\rho\in[0,1]

can be considered in order to study the Robin boundary condition and therefore, the limit cases corresponding to the Dirichlet and the Neumann boundary conditions. By restating the previous condition as

\displaystyle u^{\prime}(0)=\varrho\,u(0),\quad\varrho=\frac{\rho}{1-\rho}

we formally get the Dirichlet condition as
\varrho\to\infty
and the Neumann condition as
\varrho\to 0
. We now use similar arguments in case of the fractional boundary condition

\displaystyle\eta D^{\alpha/2}_{t}u(t,0)=\sigma\frac{\partial u}{\partial x}(t,0)-c\,u(t,0).

We have the following formal cases (for the sake of simplicity we set
\eta=1
):

-

\forall\,c>0
, as
\sigma\to 0
we get the result in the previous Corollary
2
;

-

\forall\,c>0
, as
\sigma\to\infty
we formally get the Neumann condition
\partial_{x}u(t,0)=0
. The solution takes the form

\displaystyle u(t,x)=Q^{D}_{t}\mathbf{1}_{[0,\infty)}+\int_{0}^{t}\frac{x}{\tau}g(\tau,x)\,d\tau.

Since

(6.1)

\displaystyle Q^{D}_{t}\mathbf{1}_{[0,\infty)}=1-\int_{0}^{t}\frac{x}{\tau}g(\tau,x)\,d\tau

we obtain that

\displaystyle 1=u(t,x)=\mathbf{P}_{x}(\zeta>t),\;t\geq 0,\;x\in[0,\infty).

The process has infinite lifetime;

-

\forall\sigma\geq 0
as
c\to 0
we have that

\displaystyle u(t,x)=\mathbf{E}_{x}[\mathbf{1}_{[0,\infty)}(X_{t})]=1

that is
\forall x

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}_{x}(\zeta>t)=1,\quad\forall t

The lifetime is infinite. The process behaves like a Brownian motion reflected at
x=0
;

-

\forall\,\sigma\geq 0
, as
c\to\infty
we get

\displaystyle u(t,x)=\mathbf{E}_{x}[\mathbf{1}_{[0,\infty)}(X_{t}),\gamma_{t}=0]=\mathbf{E}_{x}[\mathbf{1}_{[0,\infty)}(X_{t}),t<\tau_{0}]

where
\tau_{0}
is the first time the process
X_{t}
hits the point zero. Thus the reflecting Brownian motion
X
is stopped at the random time
\tau_{0}
. We get
u(t,x)=\mathbf{P}_{x}(\tau_{0}>t)
and the corresponding process behaves like a Brownian motion killed on the boundary point
x=0
;

-

Suppose that

as

, then

\displaystyle u(t,x)=\mathbf{E}_{x}[\mathbf{1}_{[0,\infty)}(X_{t})\,e^{-\rho\gamma_{t}}]

and

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}_{x}(\zeta>t)=\mathbf{P}_{x}(\gamma_{t}<\chi)

where
\chi\sim Exp(\rho)
. The corresponding process behaves like an elastic Brownian motion.

Moreover, we may consider the following cases (for
\sigma,c\in(0,\infty)
):

-

as
\eta\to 0
, we have the Robin boundary condition;

-

as
\eta\to\infty
, we have the Neumann boundary condition corresponding to

\displaystyle D^{\alpha/2}_{t}u(t,0)=0,\quad t>0.

Proof of the results

Let us consider

(7.3)

\displaystyle Q^{D}_{t}f(x)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\big(g(t,x-y)-g(t,x+y)\big)\,f(y)\,dy

is the Gaussian kernel. The semigroup (

\displaystyle Q_{t}f(x)=\mathbf{E}_{x}[f(X_{t})M_{t}]

X_{t}

. The process

is related to the additive functional

. In case of the elastic Brownian motion for instance,

where
\gamma_{t}
is the local time at
\{0\}
for
X_{t}
. Let us write

(7.4)

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}f(y)\mathbf{P}_{x}(X_{t}\in dy,\gamma_{t}\in dw)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}f(y)\,\frac{x+y+w}{t}\,g(t,x+y+w)\,dy\,dw+Q^{D}_{t}f(x)\,\delta(w)dw.

and

(7.5)

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}f(y)\mathbf{P}_{x}(X_{t}\in dy,\bar{L}_{\gamma_{t}}\in dw)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}f(y)\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{x+y+z}{t}\,g(t,x+y+z)\,\bar{l}(z,w)\,dz\right)dy\,dw

\displaystyle+Q^{D}_{t}f(x)\,\delta(w)dw.

From (

\displaystyle Q_{t}f(x)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty}f(y)\,e^{-cw}\,\mathbf{P}_{x}(X_{t}\in dy,\gamma_{t}\in dw)+Q_{t}^{D}f(x)

for which

. Indeed, the elastic Brownian motion is a (strong) Markov process for which

is gievn by

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}_{x}[f(X_{t+s})g(\gamma_{t+s})]=

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}_{x}[\mathbf{E}_{0}[f(X_{t+s}-X_{s}+X_{s})g(\gamma_{t+s}-\gamma_{s}+\gamma_{s})|X_{s}]]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}_{x}[\mathbf{E}_{0}[f(X_{t}+X_{s})g(\gamma_{t}+\gamma_{s})|X_{s}]]

Lemma 1
.

For the densities
l
and
h
of the processes
L
and
H
, it holds that

\displaystyle\alpha\frac{x}{t}l(t,x)=h(x,t),\quad t>0,\;x>0.

Proof.

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\lambda t-\xi x}\alpha\,l(t,x)\,dx\,dt=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\lambda t}\alpha\,E_{\alpha}(-\xi t^{\alpha})\,dt

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\alpha\frac{\lambda^{\alpha-1}}{\xi+\lambda^{\alpha}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\xi x}\alpha\,\lambda^{\alpha-1}e^{-x\lambda^{\alpha}}\,dx

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{d}{d\lambda}\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\xi x}\frac{1}{x}e^{-x\lambda^{\alpha}}\,dx

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{d}{d\lambda}\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\xi x-\lambda t}\frac{1}{x}h(x,t)\,dt\,dx

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\xi x-\lambda t}\frac{t}{x}h(x,t)\,dt\,dx

Lemma 2

We have that

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\frac{x}{t-\tau}g(t-\tau,x)\,g(\tau,y)\,d\tau=g(t,x+y)

Formula (

Proof of Theorem

.

Let us assume that the solution to (

) has the probabilistic representation

. Observe that

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}_{x}[f(X_{t})\bar{M}_{t}]=\mathbf{E}_{x}[f(X_{t})\bar{M}_{t},\gamma_{t}=0]+\mathbf{E}_{x}[f(X_{t})\bar{M}_{t},\gamma_{t}>0]

which can be written in terms of the first time the process
X

x=0
, that is

\displaystyle\tau_{0}=\inf\{t\geq 0\,:\,X_{t}=0\}.

Let us introduce the
\lambda

\displaystyle R_{\lambda}f(x)=\mathbf{E}_{x}\left[\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\lambda t}f(X_{t})\bar{M}_{t}\,dt\right],\quad\lambda>0

for which we write

\displaystyle R_{\lambda}f(x)=R^{D}_{\lambda}f(x)+\bar{R}_{\lambda}f(x)

where

\displaystyle R^{D}_{\lambda}f(x)=\mathbf{E}_{x}\left[\int_{0}^{\tau_{0}}e^{-\lambda t}f(X_{t})\bar{M}_{t}\,dt\right]

and

\displaystyle\bar{R}_{\lambda}f(x)=\mathbf{E}_{x}\left[\int_{\tau_{0}}^{\infty}e^{-\lambda t}f(X_{t})\bar{M}_{t}\,dt\right].

We have that

\displaystyle\Delta R_{\lambda}f(x)=

\displaystyle\lambda R_{\lambda}f(x)-f(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\lambda R^{D}_{\lambda}f(x)-f(x)\right)+\lambda\bar{R}_{\lambda}f(x)

with

\displaystyle\Delta R^{D}_{\lambda}f(x)=\lambda R^{D}_{\lambda}f(x)-f(x)\quad\textrm{and}\quad\Delta\bar{R}_{\lambda}f(x)=\lambda\bar{R}_{\lambda}f(x).

On the other hand

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\lambda t}D^{\alpha/2}_{t}u(t,x)\,dt=

\displaystyle\lambda^{\alpha/2}R_{\lambda}f(x)-\lambda^{\alpha/2-1}f(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lambda^{\alpha/2}R^{D}_{\lambda}f(x)+\lambda^{\alpha/2-1}\left(\lambda\bar{R}_{\lambda}f(x)-f(x)\right)

with

\displaystyle R^{D}_{\lambda}f(x)\big|_{x=0}=0.

Thus, the potential
\bar{R}_{\lambda}
completely characterizes the boundary value problem in terms of the fractional derivative.

The Laplace transform of (
7.2
) is given by

\displaystyle\widetilde{u}(\lambda,x)=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\lambda t}u(t,x)\,dt

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\lambda t}Q^{D}_{t}f(x)\,dt+\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\lambda t}\int_{0}^{t}\frac{x}{\tau}g(\tau,x)\,u(t-\tau,0)\,d\tau\,dt

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\lambda t}\int_{0}^{t}\frac{x}{\tau}g(\tau,x)\,u(t-\tau,0)\,d\tau\,dt=e^{-x\sqrt{\lambda}}\,\widetilde{u}(\lambda,0)=\bar{R}_{\lambda}f(x).

We obviously have that

\displaystyle\lambda R^{D}_{\lambda}f(x)\to f(x)\quad\textrm{as}\quad\lambda\to\infty

whereas, as
\lambda\to\infty

\displaystyle\lambda\bar{R}_{\lambda}f(x)=e^{-x\sqrt{\lambda}}\lambda R_{\lambda}f(0)\to\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\displaystyle f(0),&x=0\\
\displaystyle 0,&x>0\end{array}\right.

assuming that

as

. Our assumption is therefore on the continuity of the paths of the involved process, that is

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to 0^{+}}\mathbf{E}_{x}[f(X_{t})e^{-c\bar{L}_{\gamma_{t}}}]=f(x)
