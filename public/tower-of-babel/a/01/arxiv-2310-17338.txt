Abstract
Optimizing strongly convex functions subject to linear constraints is a fundamental problem with numerous applications. In this work, we propose a block (accelerated) randomized Bregman-Kaczmarz method that only uses a block of constraints in each iteration to tackle this problem. We consider a dual formulation of this problem in order to deal in an efficient way with the linear constraints. Using convex tools, we show that the corresponding dual function satisfies the Polyak-Lojasiewicz (PL) property, provided that the primal objective function is strongly convex and verifies additionally some other mild assumptions. However, adapting the existing theory on coordinate descent methods to our dual formulation can only give us sublinear convergence results in the dual space. In order to obtain convergence results in some criterion corresponding to the primal (original) problem, we transfer our algorithm to the primal space, which combined with the PL property allows us to get linear convergence rates. More specifically, we provide a theoretical analysis of the convergence of our proposed method under different assumptions on the objective and demonstrate in the numerical experiments its superior efficiency and speed up compared to existing methods for the same problem.

Introduction

In this work, we consider the fundamental problem of approximating solutions of large-scale consistent linear systems of the form:

\mathbf{A}x=b

(1.1)

with matrix
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
and right hand side
b\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
. We consider the case that the full matrix is not accessible simultaneously, but that one
can only work with a single or block of rows of the system (
1.1
) at a time. Given the possibility of multiple solutions of (
1.1
), we set out to find the unique solution, characterized by the function
f
, i.e.,

\hat{f}\overset{\text{def}}{=}\min_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}f(x)\quad\text{subject to}\quad\mathbf{A}x=b,

(1.2)

with a general strongly convex function
f
. However, we will not assume smoothness (not even differentiability) of
f
. One possible example is
f(x)=\lambda\cdot\|x\|_{1}+\frac{1}{2}\|x\|^{2}_{2}
and it is known that this function favors sparse solutions for appropriate choices of
\lambda>0
, see
cai2009linearized
;
LSW14
;
LWSM14
;
LS19
;
yin2010analysis
. Similarly, by dividing
the components of
x
into
M
groups,
x=(x_{(1)},\dots,x_{(M)})
with
x_{(j)}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{j}}
, the function
f(x)=\lambda\cdot\sum_{j=1}^{M}\|x_{(j)}\|_{2}+\frac{1}{2}\cdot\|x\|_{2}^{2}
favours group sparsity. We assume throughout the paper that both
m
and
n
are large and that the system is consistent. The
i
-th row of
\mathbf{A}
is denoted by
a_{i}^{\top}
and we assume
a_{i}\neq 0
for all
i\in[m]:=\{1,\dots,m\}
. Since

is strongly convex, problem (
1.2
) has a unique solution
\hat{x}
. In applications, it is usually sufficient to find a point which is not too far from
\hat{x}
. In particular, one chooses the error tolerance
\varepsilon>0
and aims to find a point

satisfying
\|x-\hat{x}\|^{2}_{2}\leq\varepsilon
. Since our method is of a stochastic nature, the iterates

are random vectors. Hence, our goal will be to compute approximate solutions of (
1.2
) that fulfills
\mathbb{E}[\|x-\hat{x}\|^{2}_{2}]\leq\varepsilon

1.1
Related work

The linear system (
1.1
) may be so large that full matrix operations are very expensive or even infeasible. Then, it appears desirable to use iterative algorithms
with low computational cost and storage per iteration that produce good approximate solutions of (
1.2

gower2019adaptive
;
gower2015randomized
;
necoara2019faster
;

a_{i}^{\top}

is chosen at random from the system (
1.1

is projected onto the solution space of that equation to obtain

\mathcal{O}(n)
. Recently, a new variant of the randomized Kaczmarz (RK) method namely the randomized sparse Kaczmarz method (RSK)
LWSM14
;
LS19
with almost the same low cost and storage requirements has shown good performance in approximating sparse solutions of large consistent linear systems. The papers
LSW14
;
LS19
analyze this method by interpreting it as a sequential, randomized Bregman projection method (where the Bregman projection is done with respect to the function
f
), while
P15
connects it with the coordinate descent method via duality.
Variations of RSK including block/averaging variants can be found in
du2020randomized
;
necoara2019faster
;
needell2014paved
;
P15
;
tondji2023faster
;
tondji2023adaptive
, momentum variants
lorenz2023minimal
, sampling scheme
tondji2021linear
and adaptations to least squares problems are given in
Du19
;
schopfer2022extended
;
zouzias2013randomized
. In those variants, one usually needs to have access to more than one row of the matrix
\mathbf{A}
at the cost of increasing the memory. Coordinate descent methods are often considered in the context of minimizing composite convex objective functions
fercoq2015accelerated
;
necoara2016
;
nesterov2012efficiency
. At each iteration, they update only one coordinate of the vector of variables using partial derivatives and proximal operators rather than the whole
gradient and the full proximal operator, respectively. A randomized accelerated coordinate descent method was proposed in
nesterov2012efficiency
for smooth convex functions and later it was extended to composite convex functions in e.g.,
fercoq2015accelerated
;
necoara2016
. Note that accelerated methods transform the coordinate descent method, for which the optimality gap decreases as
\mathcal{O}(1/k)

algorithm with optimal
\mathcal{O}(1/k^{2})
complexity under mild additional computational cost
fercoq2015accelerated
;
nesterov2012efficiency

to solve problem (

and expands upon them with an additional restart algorithm (see Algorithm

and

1.2
Contributions

To the best of our knowledge, blocks accelerated Bregman-Kaczmarz methods for solving linearly constrained optimization problems with strongly convex (possibly nonsmooth) objective functions have not been considered before in the literature. In this work, we provide such methods for solving this type of problems. Hence, this study has the following main contributions:

(a)

Beyond interpreting the Bregman-Kaczmarz method as a dual coordinate descent method, we propose a primal accelerated coordinate gradient algorithm using only blocks of rows of the matrix defining the constraints. We refer to this as the Block Accelerated Randomized Bregman-Kaczmarz method (ARBK) with more details in Algorithm
2
. We have also extended the method and propose a restart scheme for Algorithm
2
that exhibits faster convergence.

(b)

Using tools from convex theory, we show that the corresponding dual function satisfies the Polyak-Lojasiewicz (PL) property, provided that the primal objective function is strongly convex and verifies additionally some other mild assumptions. By exploiting the connection between primal and dual updates, we obtain convergence as a byproduct and convergence rates under different assumptions on the objective function which have not been available so far. In particular, we prove that our restart accelerated method leads to faster (i.e., linear rate) convergence than its standard counterpart.

(c)

We also validate the superior efficiency of our approach empirically and we provide an implementation of our algorithm in
Python
.

2.1
Basic notions

Let
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
be convex (we assume that
f
is finite everywhere, hence it is also continuous). The
subdifferential
of
f
at any
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is defined by

\partial f(x)\overset{\text{def}}{=}\{x^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}|f(y)\geq f(x)+\langle x^{*},y-x\rangle\;\;\forall y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\},

which is a nonempty, compact and convex set. The function
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
is said to be
\sigma
-strongly convex
with constant
\sigma>0
, if for all
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and subgradients
x^{*}\in\partial f(x)
we have

f(y)\geq f(x)+\langle x^{*},y-x\rangle+\tfrac{\sigma}{2}\cdot\|y-x\|_{2}^{2}\,.

If
f
is
\sigma
-strongly convex, then
f
is coercive, i.e.

\lim_{\|x\|_{2}\to\infty}f(x)=\infty\,.

The Fenchel conjugate of a
\sigma
-strongly convex function
f
, denoted
f^{*}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
, given by

f^{*}(x^{*})\overset{\text{def}}{=}\sup_{y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\langle x^{*}\,,\,y\rangle-f(y),

is also convex, finite everywhere and coercive. Additionally,
f^{*}
is differentiable with a
Lipschitz-continuous gradient
with constant
L_{f^{*}}=\frac{1}{\sigma}
, i.e., for all
x^{*},y^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
we have

\|\nabla f^{*}(x^{*})-\nabla f^{*}(y^{*})\|_{2}\leq L_{f^{*}}\cdot\|x^{*}-y^{*}\|_{2}\,,

which implies the estimate

\displaystyle f^{*}(y^{*})\leq f^{*}(x^{*})\!+\langle\nabla f^{*}(x^{*})\,,\,y^{*}-x^{*}\rangle+\tfrac{L_{f^{*}}}{2}\|x^{*}-y^{*}\|_{2}^{2}.

(2.2)

The function
f^{*}
is said to have block-coordinate Lipschitz continuous gradient if there exists
L_{f^{*},i}>0
such that

\displaystyle\|\nabla_{(i)}f^{*}(x^{*}+V_{i}h_{(i)})-\nabla_{(i)}f^{*}(x^{*})\|\leq L_{f^{*},i}\cdot\|h_{(i)}\|_{2},

(2.3)

for all
x^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{n},h_{(i)}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{i}},i\in[M].

Definition 1

The

D_{f}^{x^{*}}(x,y)
between

with respect to a convex function

and a subgradient

is defined as

D_{f}^{x^{*}}(x,y)\overset{\text{def}}{=}f(y)-f(x)-\langle x^{*}\,,\,y-x\rangle\,.

Fenchel’s equality states that
f(x)+f^{*}(x^{*})=\langle x\,,\,x^{*}\rangle
if
x^{*}\in\partial f(x)
and implies that the Bregman distance can be written as

D_{f}^{x^{*}}(x,y)=f^{*}(x^{*})-\langle x^{*}\,,\,y\rangle+f(y)\,.

Calmness, linear regularity and linear growth conditions

In this section, we are going to define notions such as calmness, linear regularity, and linear growth

denote the closed unit ball of the

Definition 2

The (set-valued) subdifferential mapping
\partial f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightrightarrows\mathbb{R}^{n}

if there are constants

\partial f(x)\subset\partial f(\hat{x})+L\cdot\|x-\hat{x}\|_{2}\cdot B_{2}\quad\mbox{for any $x$ with}\quad\|x-\hat{x}\|_{2}\leq\varepsilon\,.

(2.6)

Note that calmness is a local growth condition akin to Lipschitz-continuity
of a gradient mapping, but for fixed
\hat{x}
. Of course, any Lipschitz-continuous
gradient mapping is calm everywhere.

Example 2

(a)

(b)

The subdifferential mapping of

Definition 3

Let

Then, the collection

(2.7)

The collection

We say the subdifferential mapping of

Definition 5

Let us consider the class of convex-constrained optimization problems

3
Coordinate descent and Bregman-Kaczmarz methods

Note that by a proper scalling of
f
we can always assume the strong convexity constant
\sigma=1
. Therefore, in the
sequel we consider 1-strongly convex objective function
f
. To connect the Kaczmarz method to the coordinate descent algorithm, we consider the dual problem to problem (
1.2
). Using the conjugate of
f
we can write the dual objective as the infimum of the Lagrangian
\mathcal{L}(x,y)\overset{\text{def}}{=}f(x)-y^{\top}(\mathbf{A}x-b)
as

\displaystyle\inf_{x}\mathcal{L}(x,y)

\displaystyle=\inf_{x}(f(x)-y^{\top}(\mathbf{A}x-b))=b^{\top}y-f^{*}(\mathbf{A}^{\top}y).

Hence, the dual problem of (
1.2
) is given by:

\hat{\Psi}\overset{\text{def}}{=}\min_{y\in\mathbb{R}^{m}}\left[\Psi(y):=f^{*}(\mathbf{A}^{\top}y)-b^{\top}y\right].

(3.1)

The primal-dual optimality system

\displaystyle\mathbf{A}\hat{x}=b,\quad\hat{x}=\nabla f^{*}(\mathbf{A}^{\top}\hat{y}),

(3.2)

couples the primal and dual optimal solutions
\hat{x}
and
\hat{y}
respectively. Our objective function in Eq. (
1.2
) is strongly convex and the linear system is consistent, therefore there exists a primal solution
\hat{x}
and it is unique and strong duality holds in this case. The primal-dual problems can be defined as follows:
\Phi(x)=f(x)+\delta_{\{0\}}(b-\mathbf{A}x)
and
\Psi(y)=-f^{*}(\mathbf{A}^{\top}y)+b^{\top}y
. Hence, the duality gap is given by:

\displaystyle\Psi(\hat{y})-\Phi(\hat{x})=-f^{*}(\mathbf{A}^{\top}\hat{y})+b^{\top}\hat{y}-f(\hat{x})-\delta_{\{0\}}(b-\mathbf{A}\hat{x})=-D^{\hat{d}}_{f}(\hat{x},\hat{x})=0.

Therefore, the primal and the dual solutions exist and satisfy equation (
3.2
) and thus the optimal solution set
\mathcal{Y}^{*}
of (
3.1
) is nonempty. Further, the dual objective
\Psi
is unconstrained, differentiable and its gradient is given by the following expression

\nabla\Psi(y)=\mathbf{A}\nabla f^{*}(\mathbf{A}^{\top}y)-b.

(3.3)

Moreover, the gradient
\nabla\Psi
of the dual function is Lipschitz and block-wise Lipschitz continuous w.r.t. the Euclidean norm
\|\cdot\|_{2}
, with constants
necoara2022linear
:

L_{\Psi}\overset{\text{def}}{=}\|\mathbf{A}\|^{2}_{2}\ \text{ and }\ L_{\Psi,i}\overset{\text{def}}{=}L_{i}\overset{\text{def}}{=}\|\mathbf{A}_{(i)}\|_{2}^{2},

(3.4)

respectively. The following lemma relates the Bregman distance to the optimal primal solution to the distance to optimality of the dual problem.

Lemma 1

Let
b\in\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{A})
and
(y^{k},d^{k},x^{k})
be three sequences such that
d^{k}=\mathbf{A}^{\top}y^{k}
and
x^{k}=\nabla f^{*}(d^{k})
. Then, it holds

\displaystyle D_{f}^{d^{k}}(x^{k},\hat{x})=\Psi(y^{k})-\hat{\Psi}.

(3.5)

Proof

By Definition
1
we have that for
d^{k}=\mathbf{A}^{\top}y^{k}
and
x^{k}=\nabla f^{*}(d^{k})
. The subgradient inversion formula
Roc:70
implies that
d^{k}\in\partial f(x^{k})
and the following is valid:

\displaystyle D_{f}^{d^{k}}(x^{k},\hat{x})

\displaystyle=f^{*}(d^{k})+f(\hat{x})-\langle d^{k},\hat{x}\rangle

\displaystyle=f^{*}(\mathbf{A}^{\top}y^{k})-\langle b,y^{k}\rangle+f(\hat{x})

\displaystyle=\Psi(y^{k})+\hat{f},

and since
\hat{\Psi}=-\max-\Psi=-\hat{f}
(by strong duality), the assertion follows.

The randomized block coordinate descent update applied to minimize
\Psi
from (
3.1
) reads, see
necoara2016
;
nesterov2012efficiency
:

\displaystyle y^{k+1}

\displaystyle=y^{k}-\frac{1}{\|\mathbf{A}_{(i)}\|_{2}^{2}}\mathbf{U}_{i}\bigg(\mathbf{A}_{(i)}\nabla f^{*}(\mathbf{A}^{\top}y^{k})-b_{(i)}\bigg),

Using the notations

\displaystyle d^{k}=\mathbf{A}^{\top}y^{k},\quad x^{k}=\nabla f^{*}(d^{k}),

(3.8)

and the fact that

the block dual coordinate descent iterates transfer in the primal space to block Bregman-Kaczmarz iterates as shown in Algorithm

A more general version of randomized coordinate descent, namely APPROX has been proposed in
fercoq2015accelerated
to accelerate proximal coordinate descent methods for the minimization of composite functions. The APPROX update applied to solve our dual problem (

) is given by:

\displaystyle v^{k}

\displaystyle=(1-\theta_{k})y^{k}+\theta_{k}z^{k}

(3.9)

\displaystyle z^{k+1}_{(i)}

\displaystyle=z^{k}_{(i)}-\frac{1}{\theta_{k}M\|\mathbf{A}_{(i)}\|_{2}^{2}}\cdot\bigg(\mathbf{A}_{(i)}\nabla f^{*}(\mathbf{A}^{\top}v^{k})-b_{(i)}\bigg)

\displaystyle y^{k+1}

\displaystyle=v^{k}+M\theta_{k}(z^{k+1}-z^{k})

\displaystyle\theta_{k+1}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sqrt{\theta_{k}^{4}+4\theta_{k}^{2}}-\theta_{k}^{2}}{2}.

In the updates (

We transfer this algorithm to the primal space using the relation

and this results in a primal block accelerated randomized Bregman Kaczmarz scheme, see Algorithm

(

The sequence

\displaystyle\frac{(2-\theta_{0})}{k+(2-\theta_{0})/\theta_{0}}

\displaystyle\leq\theta_{k}\leq\frac{2}{k+2/\theta_{0}},

\displaystyle\frac{1-\theta_{k+1}}{\theta^{2}_{k+1}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\theta^{2}_{k}},\quad\forall k=0,1,\dots

\displaystyle\theta_{k+1}

\displaystyle\leq\theta_{k},\quad\forall k=0,1,\dots

The next lemma gives a recurrence relation for the dual sequences generated by Algorithm

Lemma 3
(
(

, see Eq. (52))

Consider the linearly constrained optimization problem (

) with

-strongly convex

Theorem 4.1

) with

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}\big[\|x^{k}-\hat{x}\|^{2}_{2}\big]\leq\mathbb{E}\big[D_{f}^{d^{k}}(x^{k},\hat{x})\big]\leq\frac{2M\bar{L}_{\alpha}}{k+4}R^{2}_{1-\alpha}(y^{0}).

Proof

Let

be the sequence generated by iteration (

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\theta^{2}_{k-1}}\mathbb{E}\big[D_{f}^{d^{k}}(x^{k},\hat{x})\big]

4.2

Consider the feasible, convex and linearly constrained optimization problem (
1.2

4.10
) give us :

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[D_{f}^{d^{k}}(x^{k},\hat{x})]\leq\bigg(1-\frac{1}{2M\gamma(\hat{x})\bar{L}_{\alpha}\bar{L}^{1-\alpha}}\bigg)^{k}\cdot D_{f}^{d^{0}}(x^{0},\hat{x}).

The second estimate follows from the fact that
f
is
1-
strongly convex, hence satisfies
\frac{1}{2}\|x^{k}-\hat{x}\|^{2}_{2}\leq D^{d^{k}}_{f}(x^{k},\hat{x})
.

Remark 4

The linear convergence rate from the previous theorem depends on the number of blocks
M
and shows that considering blocks is beneficial for BK (Algorithm
1
). Indeed, Theorem
4.4
tells us that taking
M=m
gives us the slowest convergence rate and thus one should consider updating the blocks having a size greater than 1, i.e.,
M<m
. However, small
M
implies large block sizes which increases the computational time of the subproblem. From our computational results from Section
5
one can see that small
M
indeed is beneficial in terms of number of full iterations. Note that Theorem
4.4
generalizes the convergence results from
P15
;
LS19
. In fact, to get the linear convergence rate in
P15
the authors needed the objective function to be smooth which is not the case in our theorem. The linear convergence result in
LS19
can be obtained from our Theorem
4.4
by setting
\alpha=1,M=m
. In this case
M\bar{L}=\|\mathbf{A}\|^{2}_{F}
, which corresponds to the result in
(
LS19
, Theorem 3.2)
.

A well-known drawback of accelerated first-order methods like Algorithm
2
is their oscillating behavior that slows them down (see e.g Figure
2
and
4
below). As a first path towards solving these issues, restart schemes have been proposed in the literature, which have been demonstrated to improve convergence in practice by removing the oscillatory behavior
necoara2019linear
. Practically speaking in a restart scheme, Algorithm
2
is stopped when a certain criterion is met and then restarted using the last value provided by the method as the new initial condition. In the next section, we present a restart scheme for ARBK (Algorithm
2
) that exhibits linear convergence for our problem (
1.2
).

4.3
Restarted ARBK

It has been shown that under the quadratic growth property accelerated gradient
methods can have improved complexity bounds in combination with an appropriate restarting procedure, see e.g.
fercoq2020restarting
;
necoara2019linear
;
necoara2022linear
. Our goal is to design a restarted ARBK akin to the restart strategies in
fercoq2020restarting
;
necoara2022linear
with similar properties based on the results in the previous sections. Having defined a set of integers
\{K_{0},K_{1},...\}
of frequencies with which one wishes to restart the method, we can write the restarted version of ARBK. In the following version of ARBK in Algorithm
3
we restart with these frequencies, but only update the dual variable if we have decreased the objective during the last restart period.

Algorithm 3

Restarted-ARBK (RARBK)

1:

Choose
y^{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
and set
\tilde{y}^{0}=y^{0}
.

2:

choose restart periods
\{K_{0},\dots,K_{r},\dots\}\subseteq\mathbb{N}
.

3:

initialize
r=0

4:

repeat

5:

\bar{y}^{r+1}=\text{ARBK}(f,\tilde{y}^{r},K_{r})

6:

update
\tilde{y}^{r+1}=\begin{cases}\bar{y}^{r+1},&\text{if }\Psi(\bar{y}^{r+1})\leq\Psi(\tilde{y}^{r})\\
\\
\tilde{y}^{r},&\text{if }\Psi(\bar{y}^{r+1})>\Psi(\tilde{y}^{r})\end{cases}

7:

increment
r=r+1

8:

until
a stopping criterion is satisfied

9:

return

\tilde{x}^{r+1}=\nabla f^{*}(\mathbf{A}^{\top}\tilde{y}^{r+1})
.

The next lemma gives an estimate of the progress we make in the primal variable in one restart period.

Lemma 4
(Conditional restarting at
y^{k}
)

Let
(y^{k},x^{k})
be the iterates of ARBK (Algorithm
2
). Let

\displaystyle\tilde{y}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}y^{k},&\text{if }\Psi(y^{k})\leq\Psi(y^{0})\\
y^{0},&\text{if }\Psi(y^{k})>\Psi(y^{0})\end{cases}

(4.12)

and set
\tilde{d}=\mathbf{A}^{\top}\tilde{y}
,
\tilde{x}=\nabla f^{*}(\tilde{d})
.
Then, we have

\mathbb{E}[D^{\tilde{d}}_{f}(\tilde{x},\hat{x})]\leq\theta^{2}_{k-1}\Bigg(\frac{1-\theta_{0}}{\theta^{2}_{0}}+\frac{L_{\text{max}}}{\gamma(\hat{x})\theta^{2}_{0}}\Bigg)\cdot D^{d^{0}}_{f}(x^{0},\hat{x}),

(4.13)

with
L_{\text{max}}=\max\limits_{i\in[M]}\|\mathbf{A}_{(i)}\|_{2}^{2},\theta_{0}=\frac{1}{M}
. Moreover, given
\zeta<1
, if

k\geq\frac{2}{\theta_{0}}\Bigg(\sqrt{\frac{L_{\text{max}}+\gamma(\hat{x})}{\zeta\gamma(\hat{x})}}-1\Bigg)+1,

(4.14)

then
\mathbb{E}[D^{\tilde{d}}_{f}(\tilde{x},\hat{x})]\leq\zeta\cdot D^{d^{0}}_{f}(x^{0},\hat{x})
.

Proof

Since
\Psi
satisfies now the PL inequality (
2.9
) it also satisfies the quadratic growth condition
(
karimi2016linear
, Theorem 2)
, i.e.,

\Psi(y^{k})-\hat{\Psi}\geq\frac{\gamma(\hat{x})}{2}\text{dist}(y^{k},\mathcal{Y}^{*})^{2}.

(4.15)

From Lemma
3
, the following holds for the iterates of ARBK:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\Psi(y^{k})-\hat{\Psi}]

\displaystyle\leq\theta^{2}_{k-1}\Bigg(\frac{1-\theta_{0}}{\theta^{2}_{0}}(\Psi(y^{0})-\hat{\Psi})+\frac{1}{2\theta^{2}_{0}}\text{dist}_{\mathbf{B}}(y^{0},\mathcal{Y}*)^{2}\Bigg)

\displaystyle\leq\theta^{2}_{k-1}\Bigg(\frac{1-\theta_{0}}{\theta^{2}_{0}}(\Psi(y^{0})-\hat{\Psi})+\frac{L_{\text{max}}}{2\theta^{2}_{0}}\text{dist}(y^{0},\mathcal{Y}*)^{2}\Bigg)

\displaystyle\leq\theta^{2}_{k-1}\Bigg(\frac{1-\theta_{0}}{\theta^{2}_{0}}+\frac{L_{\text{max}}}{\gamma(\hat{x})\theta^{2}_{0}}\Bigg)(\Psi(y^{0})-\hat{\Psi}),

where
\text{dist}_{\mathbf{B}}(y^{0},\mathcal{Y}*)
denotes the distance of
y^{0}
to the set
\mathcal{Y}*
with respect to the norm
\|\cdot\|_{\mathbf{B}}
with
{\mathbf{B}}=\textbf{Diag}(\|\mathbf{A}_{(1)}\|_{2}^{2},\|\mathbf{A}_{(2)}\|_{2}^{2},\dots,\|\mathbf{A}_{(M)}\|_{2}^{2})
and
L_{\text{max}}=\max_{i\in[M]}\|\mathbf{A}_{(i)}\|_{2}^{2}
. Furthermore from Lemma
1
, it holds
D_{f}^{d^{k}}(x^{k},\hat{x})=\Psi(y^{k})-\hat{\Psi}
and from condition (
4.12
), we have
D^{\tilde{d}}_{f}(\tilde{x},\hat{x})\leq D^{d}_{f}(x^{k},\hat{x})
which gives Eq. (
4.13
). Condition (
4.14
) is equivalent to:

\frac{4}{(k-1+2M)^{2}}\Bigg(\frac{1-\theta_{0}}{\theta^{2}_{0}}+\frac{L_{\text{max}}}{\gamma(\hat{x})\theta^{2}_{0}}\Bigg)\leq\zeta.

Corollary 1

Denote
K(\zeta)=\bigg\lceil{2M\bigg(\sqrt{\frac{L_{\text{max}}+\gamma(\hat{x})}{\zeta\gamma(\hat{x})}}-1\bigg)+1\bigg\rceil}
. If the restart periods
\{K_{0},\dots,K_{r},\dots\}
are all equal to
K(\zeta)
, then the iterates of Algorithm
3
satisfy:

\mathbb{E}[D^{\tilde{d}^{r}}_{f}(\tilde{x}^{r},\hat{x})]\leq\zeta^{r}\cdot D^{d^{0}}_{f}(x^{0},\hat{x}),

where the iterate
\tilde{x}^{r}
is obtained after restarting
r
times ARBK performing each time
K(\zeta)
iterations.

Proof

By the definition of
\tilde{x}^{r}
, we know that for all
r
,
D^{\tilde{d}^{r+1}}_{f}(\tilde{x}^{r+1},\hat{x})\leq\zeta\cdot D^{\tilde{d}^{r}}_{f}(\tilde{x}^{r},\hat{x})
. Applying recursively Lemma
4
gives us the desired linear convergence.

Note that the choice
K(\zeta)
from the previous corollary is not known to be optimal, but such a choice guarantees linear convergence. From relation (
2.5
) and Corollary
1
we can also derive a linear rate in terms of
the expected quadratic distance of this primal sequence
\tilde{x}^{r}
to the optimal solution
\hat{x}

\mathbb{E}[\|\tilde{x}^{r}-\hat{x}\|_{2}^{2}]\leq 2\zeta^{r}\cdot D^{d^{0}}_{f}(x^{0},\hat{x})

The iterate
\tilde{x}^{r}
is obtained after running
r
times Algorithm
3
of
K(\zeta)
iterations, which suggests
\zeta=e^{-2}
, or equivalently a fixed restart period (see also
necoara2019linear
):

K^{*}=\bigg\lceil{2eM\bigg(\sqrt{\frac{L_{\text{max}}+\gamma(\hat{x})}{\gamma(\hat{x})}}-1\bigg)+1\bigg\rceil}

(4.16)

Even though the quantity
\gamma(\hat{x})
is not known, Corollary
1
shows that expected linear convergence can be obtained for restarted ARBK with a long enough period
K
. In fact, we can easily see that those restarting periods depend on the number of blocks
M
and hence block variants, i.e.,
M<m
, have shorter restart periods, meaning faster convergence. Hence, it is beneficial to work with block of coordinates. Further, we present a restarting variation of ARBK as proposed in
fercoq2020restarting
, which does not require explicit knowledge of
\gamma(\hat{x})
. Finally, we fix the length of the first epoch,
K_{0}
, to

K_{0}=\bigg\lceil{2eM\bigg(\sqrt{\frac{L_{\text{max}}+\bar{\gamma}(\hat{x})}{\bar{\gamma}(\hat{x})}}-1\bigg)+1\bigg\rceil}

where
\bar{\gamma}(\hat{x})
is an estimate of the unknown constant
\gamma(\hat{x})
, which cannot be chosen arbitrarily. The true value of
\gamma(\hat{x})
can be estimated by some
\bar{\gamma}(\hat{x})
in Theorem
4.3
using Eq. (
4.9
).

Theorem 4.5

Let the sequence
\{K_{j}\}_{j\geq 0}\subset\mathbb{N}
satisfy:

(a)

K_{2^{j}-1}=2^{j}K_{0}
for all
j\in\mathbb{N}

(b)

|\{0\leq r<2^{p}-1|\,\,K_{r}=2^{j}K_{0}\}|=2^{p-1-j}
for all
j\in\{0,1,\dots,p-1\}
.

For example, we can consider,
K_{0}=K_{0},K_{1}=2^{1}K_{0},K_{2}=K_{0},K_{3}=2^{2}K_{0}
and so on. Then, after
p
epochs of Algorithm
3
we have the following linear rate in expectation:

\mathbb{E}[D^{\tilde{d}^{k}}_{f}(\tilde{x}^{k},\hat{x})]\leq\bigg(e^{-\frac{4}{(p+2)2^{i}K_{0}}}\bigg)^{k}\cdot D^{d^{0}}_{f}(x^{0},\hat{x}),

with
i=\lceil\max(0,\text{log}_{2}(K^{*}/K_{0}))\rceil
and
k=\sum\limits_{j=0}^{2^{p}-1}K_{j}
is the total number of iterations.

Proof

Let us define

\Delta_{r}=\mathbb{E}[D^{\tilde{d}^{r}}_{f}(\tilde{x}^{r},\hat{x})]

and

c_{i}(p)=|\{l<2^{p}-1|\,\,K_{l}\geq 2^{i}K_{0}\}|+1=1+\sum\limits_{k=i}^{p-1}2^{p-1-k}=2^{p-i}.

Note that
c_{i}(p)
represents the number of restarts such that
K_{l}\geq K^{*}
, i.e. the number of restarts for
which Lemma
4
applies. When

K_{l}<K^{*}
, we invoke the guaranteed Bregman distance decrease enforced in Algorithm
3
. Then it follows from Lemma
4
that

\Delta_{2^{p}-1}\leq e^{-2c_{i}(p)}\cdot\Delta_{0}.

(4.17)

Moreover, we have :

\displaystyle k=\sum\limits_{j=0}^{2^{p}-1}K_{j}

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{j=0}^{p-1}|\{0\leq r<2^{p}-1|\,\,K_{r}=2^{j}K_{0}\}|\cdot 2^{j}K_{0}+K_{2^{p}-1}

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{j=0}^{p-1}2^{p-1-j}\cdot 2^{j}K_{0}+K_{2^{p}-1}

\displaystyle=(p+2)2^{p-1}K_{0}

and the relation (
4.17
) implies:

\Delta_{k}\leq\bigg(e^{-2\frac{c_{i}(p)}{\sum_{j=0}^{2^{p}-1}K_{j}}}\bigg)^{k}\Delta_{0}=\bigg(e^{-\frac{2^{p-i+1}}{(p+2)2^{p-1}K_{0}}}\bigg)^{k}\Delta_{0}

which confirms the above result.

Remark 5

(a)

The linear convergence rate stated in Theorem
4.4
clearly implies the following estimate on the total number of iterations required by BK to obtain an
\varepsilon
-suboptimal solution in expectation:

\mathcal{O}\bigg(2M\gamma(\hat{x})\bar{L}_{\alpha}\bar{L}^{1-\alpha}\text{log}\bigg(\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\bigg)\bigg).

(b)

It follows from Theorem
4.5
that an upper bound on the total number of iterations performed by the Restarted-ARBK scheme to attain an
\varepsilon
-suboptimal solution in expectation is given by:

\mathcal{O}\bigg(M\sqrt{\frac{L_{\text{max}}}{\gamma(\hat{x})}}\text{log}\bigg(\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\bigg)\text{log}_{2}\bigg(\text{log}\bigg(\frac{f(\hat{x})}{\varepsilon}\bigg)\sqrt{\frac{\bar{\gamma}(\hat{x})}{\gamma(\hat{x})}}\bigg)\bigg).

(c)

If we assume that
f(\hat{x})\leq 1
and
\gamma(\hat{x})
is known, then the Restarted-ARBK is better than BK when
\text{log}_{2}(\text{log}(1/\varepsilon))\leq 2\gamma\sqrt{L_{\text{max}}\gamma}
. For instance, let’s suppose that the rows of the matrix are normalized and set
M=m
, then
L_{\text{max}}=1
. If
\gamma=10^{1/6}
, then Restarted-ARBK has a better worst-case complexity than ARBK for all accuracies
\varepsilon>10^{-6}
,
\gamma=10^{1/3}
for
\varepsilon>10^{-35}
and
\gamma=10^{2/3}
for
\varepsilon>10^{-455391}
.

6
Conclusions

In this work, we have proposed block (accelerated) randomized Bregman-Kaczmarz methods for solving convex linearly constrained optimization problems. Our methods are of Kaczmarz-type, i.e., they use a block of constraints in each iteration. We have used a recently proposed acceleration variant for the randomized coordinate descent method and transferred it to the primal space. A theoretical analysis of the convergence of the proposed nonaccelerated and accelerated algorithms was given. Numerical experiments have shown that the proposed methods are more efficient and faster than the existing methods for solving the same problem.
