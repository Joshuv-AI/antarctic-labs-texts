Long time stability and instability in the two-dimensional Boussinesq system with kinematic viscosity Thanks: Acknowledgements . The author acknowledges the support of the SNSF Ambizione grant No. 216083.
Jaemin Park

Abstract
. In this paper, we investigate the long-time behavior of the two-dimensional incompressible Boussinesq system with kinematic viscosity in a periodic channel, focusing on instability and asymptotic stability near hydrostatic equilibria. Firstly, we prove that any hydrostatic equilibrium reveals long-time instability when the initial data are perturbed in Sobolev spaces of low regularity. Secondly, we establish asymptotic stability of the stratified density, which is strictly decreasing in the vertical direction, under sufficiently regular perturbations, proving that the solution converges to the unique minimizer of the total energy. Our analysis is based on the energy method. Although the total energy dissipates due to kinematic viscosity, such mechanism cannot capture the stratification of the density. We overcome this difficulty by discovering another Lyapunov functional which exhibits the density stratification in a quantitative manner.

1.
Introduction

Our aim in this paper is to study long-time behavior of the two-dimensional Boussinesq system in a periodic channel,
\Omega:=\mathbb{T}\times(0,1)
,

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\rho_{t}+u\cdot\nabla\rho=0,\\
u_{t}+u\cdot\nabla u=-\nabla p-g\begin{pmatrix}0\\
\rho\end{pmatrix}+\nu\Delta u,&\text{ $t\geq 0$, $x=(x_{1},x_{2})\in\Omega$}\\
\nabla\cdot u=0,\end{cases}

(1.1)

combined with the boundary and initial condition,

\displaystyle u=0\text{ on $\partial\Omega$},\quad(\rho(0,x),u(0,x))=(\rho_{0}(x),u_{0}(x)).

(1.2)

Here,
\rho
represents a scalar-valued function denoting the temperature density of fluids, while
u
represents the velocity field that drives the fluids. The positive constants
g
and
\nu
denote the gravitational acceleration and the kinematic viscosity coefficient, respectively. For simplicity we will assume that

g=\nu=1.

The system (
1.1
) describes the evolution of the temperature distribution of a viscous, heat-conducting fluid under the effect of gravitational force (see, for example,
[
2
,
12
,
17
,
21
]
for physical derivations of the model).

1.1.
A brief overview of background

In regard to long-time behavior of the Boussinesq system, there are numerous results available in the literature. Perhaps the most necessary result in our theme is a global well-posedness theory to ensure the existence of solutions for large time. Considering (
1.1
) in a general bounded domain with smooth boundary, it was proved in
[
14
]
that if
(\rho_{0},u_{0})\in H^{k-1}\times H^{k}
for
k\geq 2
, there exists a global in time solution
(\rho,u)\in L^{\infty}_{loc}([0,\infty);H^{k-1}\times H^{k})
(to be more precise, a detailed proof in
[
14
]
is provided only for the case
k=2
. However, as noted in their paper, the same method can be generalized to higher regularity class by adapting standard techniques to deal with the viscosity effect on the boundary (see e.g.,
[
11
, Chapter 5]
for such methods used in the well-posedness analysis of the Navier–Stokes equations in a bounded domain). When the Boussinesq system is considered in the spatial domain
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, the global existence of solutions in
(\rho,u)\in L^{\infty}_{loc}([0,\infty);H^{k}\times H^{k})
for
k\geq 3
is available in a more explicit statement
[
4
,
13
]
.

We are particularly interested in asymptotic stability and instability of a class of steady states known as
hydrostatic
equilibria:

\displaystyle(\rho_{s}(x),u_{s}(x))=(\rho_{s}(x_{2}),0).

(1.3)

That is, the density
\rho_{s}
is stratified in the sense that it is independent of the horizontal variable
x_{1}
, and the fluids are at rest. It is straightforward to see that any solution of the form (
1.3
) is a stationary solution to the Boussinesq system. Moreover, the converse also holds true: any steady state of the system (
1.1
) must be a hydrostatic equilibrium. To see this, we consider the potential, kinetic and total energies:

E_{P}(\rho):=\int_{\Omega}\rho(x)x_{2}dx,\quad E_{K}(u):=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}|u(x)|^{2}dx,\quad E_{T}(\rho,u):=E_{P}(\rho)+E_{K}(u).

In the system (
1.1
), the loss of total energy is solely attributed to the velocity dissipation. Denoting by
(\rho(t),u(t))
a classical solution, a standard energy estimate reads (see Section
3
for detailed computations)

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}E_{T}(\rho(t),u(t))=-\rVert\nabla u(t)\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}.

(1.4)

Thus, in order for
(\rho_{s},u_{s})
to be a steady state, the energy dissipation must be zero, yielding
\nabla u_{s}=0
. Due to the non-slip boundary condition (
1.2
), it must hold that
u_{s}\equiv 0
. Hence the velocity equation in (
1.1
) tells us the external force field,
(0,\rho_{s})^{T}
, must be a gradient field. Consequently
\rho_{s}
is stratified. We note that the above argument might not be justified when the system is considered in an unbounded domain. Allowing the solution to have infinite (total) energy or the fluids to slip on the boundary, one can mathematically derive non-hydrostatic equilibria such as shear flows (see, for instance,
[
18
,
23
]
).

Concerning the stability analysis near hydrostatic equilibria, most of the available research has been focused on a particular density profile:

\displaystyle\rho_{s}(x_{2})=1-\alpha x_{2},\text{ for some $\alpha>0$}.

(1.5)

since such a linear density profile reduces much of the technical difficulty in the analysis of the linearized system. Also, the sign-condition on the coefficient
\alpha>0
is clearly necessary to establish stability; otherwise instability is observed even in the linearized system
[
7
, Theorem 1.4 (3)]
(such instability, resulting from the density gradient, is often referred to as
Rayleigh–Bénard instability
). As an extension of the analysis on the linearized system conducted in
[
7
]
, the authors in
[
22
]
investigated nonlinear stability in the spatial domain
\mathbb{T}^{2}
, proving that the density
\rho(t)
will eventually converge to a stratified one as
t\to\infty
, under the additional assumption that
\sup_{t>0}\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}}|\hat{\rho}(k_{1},k_{2},t)|<\infty,

where
\hat{\rho}
denotes the usual Fourier coefficient of
\rho
.

There are several results concerning the stability of slightly different models. The authors in
[
3
]
replaced
\Delta u
by
-u
in the velocity equation of (
1.1
), and established asymptotic stability of hydrostatic equilibria with the linear density as in (
1.5
). We also mention the work
[
8
]
, where the authors established asymptotic stability of the system (
1.1
) but with a different boundary condition which prevents the creation of vorticity on the boundary. We will give a slightly more detailed comparison between these models and ours in Remark
1.4
.

1.2.
Some observations for the energy structure

A trivial observation from (
1.4
) is that the total energy is always decreasing. Integrating (
1.4
) over time, we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{1}{t}\int_{0}^{t}\rVert\nabla u(s)\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}ds=\frac{1}{t}\left(E_{T}(\rho_{0},u_{0})-E_{T}(\rho(t),u(t))\right)=O(t^{-1}),\text{ for all $t\gg 1$}

(1.6)

As
t\to\infty
, we observe
\rVert\nabla u(s)\rVert_{L^{2}}
decays like
O(t^{-1/2})
, in an average sense. However the velocity converging to
0
is not enough to conclude that the solution converges to a steady state. The density may induce velocity again, unless it is exactly stratified. Indeed, the main difficulty is that the energy balance (
1.4
) neither captures the energy transfer between the potential and kinetic energies nor provides any information about the density stratification.

In order to overcome this difficulty, we introduce an auxiliary vector field
v=v(t)
, a solution to the so-called steady Stokes equation:

\displaystyle\begin{cases}-\Delta v=-\nabla q-\begin{pmatrix}0\\
\rho(t)\end{pmatrix},\quad\nabla\cdot v=0,&\text{ in $\Omega$}\\
v=0.&\text{ on $\partial\Omega$.}\end{cases}

(1.7)

The motivation behind this definition is as follows: In the velocity equation in (
1.1
), we formally neglect the terms
u_{t}
and
u\cdot\nabla u
, since they are expected to become much smaller, compared to the other terms, as the solution approaches to a hydrostatic equilibrium. The resulting velocity equation coincides with (
1.7
). In other words, we expect
u-v
to vanish throughout the evolution. To quantify this expectation, we consider a quantity
S
defined by

S(t):=\int_{\Omega}|u(t)-v(t)|^{2}dx.

Our key observation is that a suitable linear combination of
S(t)
and the total energy is a Lyapunov functional (see Proposition
3.2
for a precise statement):

\frac{d}{dt}\left(E_{T}(\rho,u)+S\right)\lesssim-(\rVert\nabla v\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}+\rVert\nabla u\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}+\rVert\nabla(u-v)\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}).

Integrating this over time, similar computations as in (
1.6
) now give us

\frac{1}{t}\int_{0}^{t}\rVert\nabla v(s)\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}+\rVert\nabla u(s)\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}+\rVert\nabla(u-v)\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}ds=O(t^{-1}),\text{ for $t\gg 1$.}

This reveals that the kinetic energies stored in both vector fields
u
and
v
decay over time in a time average sense,

\displaystyle\rVert\nabla u\rVert_{L^{2}}+\rVert\nabla v\rVert_{L^{2}}=O(t^{-1/2}).

(1.8)

We emphasize that the new Lyapunov functional
E_{T}+S
is certainly more useful than the total energy in that it provides quantitative information for the density stratification. The Biot-Savart law for
v
in (
1.7
) corresponds to
v=\nabla^{\perp}\Delta_{\Omega}^{-2}\partial_{1}\rho(t)
. Hence the decay rate (
1.8
) formally yields

\displaystyle\rVert\partial_{1}\rho\rVert_{H^{-2}}=O(t^{-1/2}),

(1.9)

indicating that the dependence on
x_{1}
of
\rho
vanishes in a weak sense as
t\to\infty
.

1.3.
Main results for instability and the asymptotic stability

As a first application of this energy structure, we present a long-time instability of arbitrary equilibrium under small perturbations in
H^{2-}
. This is motivated by the instability mechanism introduced in
[
15
, Theorem 1.5]
for the incompressible porous media equation.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
\rho_{s}
be a smooth stratified density, that is,
\rho_{s}(x)=\rho_{s}(x_{1},x_{2})
is independent of
x_{1}
. For any
\gamma,\epsilon>0
and initial velocity
u_{0}\in C^{\infty}(\Omega)
such that
\nabla\cdot u_{0}=0
and
u_{0}=0
on
\partial\Omega
, there exists an initial density
\rho_{0}\in H^{2-\gamma}\cap C^{\infty}(\Omega)
such that

\rVert\rho_{0}-{\rho}_{s}\rVert_{H^{2-\gamma}(\Omega)}\leq\epsilon

and

\limsup_{t\to\infty}t^{-k/4}\rVert\partial_{1}\rho(t)\rVert_{H^{k}(\Omega)}=\infty,\text{ for all $k>0$.}

Remark 1.2
.

The proof of Theorem
1.1
heavily relies on the argument made in
[
15
, Section 4-5]
in the context of the incompressible porous media equation (IPM), where the authors connected the decay of an anisotropic negative Sobolev norm to a growth of high Sobolev norms. We also mention that the same idea was adapted to prove instability in the Stokes transport system (Stokes) in
[
16
, Theorem 3.7.2]
. The only difference in our setting, compared to these models, is that the potential energy is not monotone decreasing. Our theorem will be established by rigorously justifying the estimate given in (
1.9
).

Our second main result of this paper is to establish asymptotic stability near stable hydrostatic equilibria. In this stability analysis, we restrict the class of steady states to prevent the Rayleigh–Bénard instability. More precisely, we will consider
\rho_{s}
such that

\displaystyle\gamma:=\inf_{x_{2}\in[0,1]}\left(-\partial_{2}{\rho}_{s}(x_{2})\right)>0,\text{ and }\partial_{2}{\rho}_{s}\in H^{4}([0,1]).

(1.10)

Let us consider a solution
(\rho(t),u(t))
whose initial data is a small perturbation from a hydrostatic equilibrium. A trivial but crucial property of the Boussinesq system (
1.1
) is that the density is transported by an incompressible flow, which preserve the area of each super level set. If
\rho(t)
indeed asymptotically converges to another stratified density in a sufficiently strong topology (strong enough to preserve topological properties of the super level sets), the limit density must be given by a push-forward of a measure-preserving diffeomorphism of
\rho_{0}
. This motivates us to define a vertical (decreasing) rearrangement as

\displaystyle\rho\mapsto\rho^{*}(x):=\int_{0}^{\infty}1_{\left\{0\leq x_{2}\leq|\left\{\rho>s\right\}|\right\}}ds.

(1.11)

From the above consideration, it should come as no surprise that the vertical rearrangement of the initial density,
\rho_{0}^{*}
, is the unique candidate for the long-time limit of the density,
\lim_{t\to\infty}\rho(t)
.

Another important property of the vertical rearrangement is that
\rho^{*}
is the unique minimizer of the potential energy, among all the densities which can be obtaind by a push-forward of a measure-preserving diffeomorphism of
\rho
. For a solution
(\rho(t),u(t))
to the Boussinesq system, this observation implies
E_{P}(\rho(t))\geq E_{P}(\rho_{0}^{*})
, resulting in

E_{P}(\rho(t))+E_{K}(u(t))\geq E_{P}(\rho_{0}^{*}),\text{ for all $t>0$}.

Hence, the asymptotic stability in the Boussinesq system can be interpreted as the convergence in the solution curve
t\to(\rho(t),u(t))
, which minimizes the total energy. The precise statement is as follows:

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
(\rho_{s},0)
be a hydrostatic equilibrium satisfying (
1.10
). There exist
\epsilon=\epsilon(\gamma,\rVert\rho_{s}\rVert_{H^{4}(\Omega)})
and
C=C(\gamma,\rVert\rho_{s}\rVert_{H^{4}})
such that
if
(\rho_{0}-{\rho}_{s},u_{0})\in\left(H^{2}_{0}(\Omega)\cap H^{4}(\Omega)\right)^{2}
and

\displaystyle\rVert\rho_{0}-{\rho}_{s}\rVert_{H^{4}}+\rVert u_{0}\rVert_{H^{4}}\leq\epsilon,

then the solution
(\rho(t),u(t))
to the Boussinesq system (
1.1
) satisfies

\displaystyle\rVert\rho(t)-{\rho}_{s}\rVert_{H^{4}(\Omega)}+\rVert u(t)\rVert_{H^{4}}\leq C\epsilon,\text{ for all $t>0$}.

(1.12)

Furthermore, the total energy decays as

\displaystyle E_{P}(\rho(t))-E_{P}(\rho_{0}^{*})+E_{K}(u(t))\leq C\epsilon^{2}t^{-2},

where
\rho_{0}^{*}
is the vertical rearrangement of the initial density. Consequently, the solution
(\rho(t),u(t))
converges to
(\rho_{0}^{*},0)
, satisfying

\displaystyle\rVert\rho(t)-\rho_{0}^{*}\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\rVert u(t)\rVert_{L^{2}}\leq C\frac{\epsilon}{t},\text{ for all $t>0$.}

(1.13)

Remark 1.4
.

In the aforementioned works
[
3
,
8
]
, asymptotic stability near hydrostatic equilibrium with
\rho_{s}(x)=1-x_{2}
was established for slight variations of our Boussinesq system. In
[
3
]
, the authors considered a model where the viscous damping
\Delta u
is replaced by
-u
in the velocity equation in (
1.1
), establishing asymptotic stability under perturbations in
H^{k}
for
k\geq 16
. We note that our damping term
\Delta u
cannot be viewed stronger than
-u
, simply counting the number of derivatives. Although our model induces stronger damping effect on the velocity, it induces strictly weaker damping effect on the density, which can be detected from the linearized system. In
[
8
]
, the authors modified the boundary condition (
1.2
) so that the flow slips and the vorticity always vanishes on the boundary, preventing the boundary layer formation. In this setting, asymptotic stability was established under perturbations in
H^{k}
for
k>32
. The main difference of Theorem
1.3
, compared to these works, is that our model reveals nontrivial difficulty due to the viscosity effect on the boundary. Another difference is that our method does not rely on the analysis of the linearized system, allowing us to achieve asymptotic stability in much weaker regularity class of perturbations.

Remark 1.5
.

The decay rate of the total energy
E_{T}(t)=O(t^{-2})
obtained in Theorem
1.3
matches with the numerical experiment in
[
7
]
(note that the the initial data considered in their paper are not close to a hydrostatic equilibrium, and the simulation was performed in a spatial domain
[0,1]^{2}
instead of the periodic channel). Moreover, our proof exhibits that the rate of the density stratification in (
1.9
) can be improved. Indeed we will obtain

\frac{2}{t}\int_{t/2}^{t}\rVert\nabla v(s)\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}+\rVert\nabla u(s)\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}ds\leq_{C}\epsilon^{2}t^{-3},\text{ for all $t>0$},

thus the long-time averages of
\rVert\partial_{1}\rho(t)\rVert_{H^{-2}}
and
\rVert\nabla u(t)\rVert_{L^{2}}
decay like
O(t^{-3/2})
at least. We note that such decay rates are not directly deduced from interpolating (
1.12
) and (
1.13
).

Although the proof of Theorem
1.3
is slightly lengthy, the key idea is simple. Adapting our observations described in Subsection
1.2
, we treat the velocity difference
u-v
as a perturbation. In the extreme case where the two velocities
u
and
v
are exactly equal, the transport equation for the density can be read as

\displaystyle\rho_{t}+v\cdot\nabla\rho=0,\quad\text{$v$ is given by \eqref{sB22vec}.}

(1.14)

This is an active scalar equation, often referred to as the Stokes transport system (see, for example,
[
19
]
for a physical motivation of this model). Although the damping effect on the density in (
1.14
) may not be immediately apparent, an inviscid damping mechanism of this system was established in the work
[
6
]
, motivated by the stability analysis for the IPM equation
[
9
]
(here, to avoid any potential confusion regarding the terminology, we note that the term “inviscid damping” is used because the density in (
1.14
) does not exhibit any viscosity-induced dissipation, although the flow determined by (
1.7
) is viscous). We adapt this inviscid damping mechanism based on the proof in
[
20
]
for the Stokes transport system. Meanwhile the perturbative quantity,
u-v
, will be effectively estimated throughout the proof.

2.
Preliminaries

2.1.
The steady Stokes equation and the Leray projection

We recall classical results concerning the steady Stokes equation and the Leray projection. Although the following lemmas hold true in general bounded domains, we will always assume that
\Omega=\mathbb{T}\times(0,1)
is a periodic channel in our paper.

Given a vector field
f
in
\Omega
, let us consider the steady Stokes equation for
(v,q)
:

\displaystyle\begin{cases}-\Delta v=-\nabla q-f,\quad\nabla\cdot v=0&\text{ in $\Omega$},\\
v=0&\text{ on $\partial\Omega$},\\
\int_{\Omega}q(x)dx=0.\end{cases}

(2.1)

Lemma 2.1
.

[
10
, Theorem IV 6.1]

Let
f\in H^{k}(\Omega)
for
k\geq-1
. The system (
2.1
) has a unique solution
(v,q)
satisfying

\rVert v\rVert_{H^{k+2}}+\rVert q\rVert_{H^{k+1}}\leq C_{k}\rVert f\rVert_{H^{k}}.

Let
(v,q)
be a solution to the steady Stokes equation (
2.1
). Thanks to the divergence free condition, the velocity
v
can be represented in terms of a stream function,
v=\nabla^{\perp}\Psi
for some
\Psi:\Omega\mapsto\mathbb{R}
. In order to characterize the the stream function, we recall the following lemma concerning the bilaplacian operator:

Lemma 2.2
.

[
6
, Lemma B.1]

Let
h\in H^{k}(\Omega)
for
k\geq-2
. Then the bilaplacian equation,

\begin{cases}\Delta^{2}\Psi=h&\text{ in $\Omega$},\\
\Psi=\nabla\Psi=0&\text{ on $\partial\Omega$,}\end{cases}

admits a unique solution
\Psi\in H_{0}^{2}(\Omega)\cap H^{k+4}(\Omega)
and it satisfies

\rVert\Psi\rVert_{H^{k+4}}\leq C_{k}\rVert h\rVert_{H^{k}}.

Combining the above two lemmas, we obtain the following:

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
f\in H^{k}
for
k\geq-1
. Let
(v,q)
be the unique solution to (
2.1
). Then

v=\nabla^{\perp}\Psi:=(-\partial_{2}\Psi,\partial_{1}\Psi)^{T},

where
\Psi
is the unique solution to

\begin{cases}\Delta^{2}\Psi=\partial_{1}f_{2}-\partial_{2}f_{1}&\text{ in $\Omega$},\\
\Psi=\nabla\Psi=0,&\text{ on $\partial\Omega$.}\end{cases}

Proof.

It is straightforward to see that

\nabla\times\Delta(v-\nabla^{\perp}\Psi)=\nabla\times(f-\nabla^{\perp}\Delta\Psi)=\nabla\times f-\nabla\times\nabla^{\perp}\Delta\Psi=0.

Since
\Omega
is simply connected, the vector field
\Delta(v-\nabla^{\perp}\Psi)
must be a gradient field. Therefore there exists
p:\Omega\mapsto\mathbb{R}
such that
(v-\nabla^{\perp}\Psi,p)
solves

\begin{cases}-\Delta(v-\nabla^{\perp}\Psi)=-\nabla p,\quad\nabla\cdot(v-\nabla^{\perp}\Psi)=0,&\text{ in $\Omega$},\\
v-\nabla^{\perp}\Psi=0&\text{ on $\partial\Omega$,}\\
\int_{\Omega}p(x)dx=0.\end{cases}

Applying Lemma
2.1
, we conclude that
(v-\nabla^{\perp}\Psi,p)=(0,0)
is the unique solution. In other words,
v=\nabla^{\perp}\Psi
.
∎

Another simple application of Lemma
2.2
is given below:

Lemma 2.4
.

For
f\in H^{4}(\Omega)
such that
f=\partial_{2}f=0
on
\partial\Omega
and
\int_{\Omega}f(x)dx=0
, it holds that

\rVert f\rVert_{{H}^{2}(\Omega)}\leq_{C}\rVert\Delta f\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega)},\quad\rVert f\rVert_{H^{4}(\Omega)}\leq_{C}\rVert\Delta^{2}f\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega)}.

Proof.

The first estimate concerning the Laplacian operator follows trivially from integration by parts and the Poincaré inequality. For the second estimate, it is tautological that
f
solves

\Delta^{2}f=\Delta^{2}f\text{ in $\Omega$ and }f=\nabla f=0,\text{ on $\partial\Omega$.}

Hence, Lemma
2.2
gives
\rVert f\rVert_{H^{4}}\leq_{C}\rVert\Delta^{2}f\rVert_{L^{2}}
.
∎

Lastly, we recall the Leray projection in a bounded domain.

Lemma 2.5
.

[
5
, Proposition 1.8]

Given a vector field
f\in L^{2}(\Omega)
, the differential equation for
(v,q)
given by

\begin{cases}v=-\nabla q-f,\quad\nabla\cdot v=0,&\text{ in $\Omega$}\\
v\cdot\vec{n}=0,&\text{ on $\partial\Omega$}\\
\int_{\Omega}q(x)dx=0,\end{cases}

has a unique solution
(v,q)
satisfying

\rVert v\rVert_{L^{2}}+\rVert q\rVert_{H^{1}}\leq\rVert f\rVert_{L^{2}}.

Given a vector field
f
, we denote the solution
v
by

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}f:=v.

(2.2)

3.
Lyapunov functional in the Boussinesq system

In the rest of the paper, we denote
(\rho,u)
, solution to the Boussinesq system (
1.1
). Given the solution, let us define an auxiliary vector field
v
as

\displaystyle\begin{cases}-\Delta v=-\nabla q-\begin{pmatrix}0\\
\rho\end{pmatrix},\quad\nabla\cdot v=0&\text{ in $\Omega$,}\\
v=0&\text{ on $\partial\Omega$.}\end{cases}

(3.1)

Note that the existence of such a vector field
v
(also the pressure
q
) is ensured by Lemma
2.1
.
Differentiating (
3.1
) in time, we observe that
v_{t}
satisfies

\displaystyle-\Delta v_{t}=-\nabla q_{t}-\begin{pmatrix}0\\
-\nabla\cdot(u\rho)\end{pmatrix},\quad\nabla\cdot v_{t}=0,\text{ in $\Omega$ and },v_{t}=0\text{ on $\partial\Omega$.}

(3.2)

Denoting

\displaystyle w:=u-v,\quad F:=u\cdot\nabla u+v_{t},

(3.3)

we see from the velocity equation in (
1.1
) that

\Delta w=\Delta u-\Delta v=\nabla p+u\cdot\nabla u+u_{t}=\nabla p+u\cdot\nabla u+v_{t}+w_{t}=\nabla p+F+w_{t}.

Combining this with the boundary condition and the incompressibility condition for
w
, we see that
w
solves

\displaystyle\begin{cases}w_{t}=-\nabla p-F+\Delta w,\quad\nabla\cdot w=0,&\text{ in $\Omega$}\\
w=0&\text{ on $\partial\Omega$}.\end{cases}

(3.4)

We define the following energy quantities:

E_{P}(t):=\int(\rho(t)-\rho_{0}^{*})x_{2}dx,\quad E_{K}(t):=\frac{1}{2}\int|u(t)|^{2}dx,\quad E_{T}(t):=E_{P}(t)+E_{K}(t),

and

S(t):=\frac{1}{2}\int|w(t)|^{2}dx.

It follows straightforwardly from (
1.1
) that

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}E_{P}(t)

\displaystyle=\int\rho_{t}x_{2}dx=-\int u\cdot\rho x_{2}dx=\int u_{2}\rho dx,

(3.5)

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}E_{K}(t)

\displaystyle=\int u\cdot u_{t}dx=-\int u_{2}\rho dx-\int|\nabla u|^{2}dx.

(3.6)

Summing them up, we obtain the conservation of the total energy:

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}(E_{P}(t)+E_{K}(t))=-\rVert\nabla u\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2},\text{ thus }E_{T}(t)+\int_{0}^{t}\rVert\nabla u\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}dt=E_{T}(0).

(3.7)

To derive an estimate for
S(t)
, we need an estimate for
F
:

Lemma 3.1
.

There exists a universal constant
C>0
such that

\rVert F\rVert_{H^{-1}}\leq C(\rVert\rho\rVert_{L^{4}}+\rVert u\rVert_{L^{2}})\rVert\nabla u\rVert_{L^{2}}.

Proof.

For any smooth test vector field
\varphi
, we have

\int F\cdot\varphi dx=\int(u\cdot\nabla u)\cdot\varphi dx+\int v_{t}\cdot\varphi dx=-\int u\otimes u:\nabla\varphi dx+\int v_{t}\cdot\varphi dx,

where the second equality is due to the integration by parts with the incompressibility and the boundary condition of
u
. Therefore, using the usual density argument of
C^{\infty}(\Omega)
in
H^{1}(\Omega)
, we get

\rVert F\rVert_{H^{-1}}\leq\rVert u\otimes u\rVert_{L^{2}}+\rVert v_{t}\rVert_{H^{-1}}\leq_{C}\rVert u\rVert_{L^{4}}^{2}+\rVert v_{t}\rVert_{H^{-1}}.

For the first term in the right-hand side, Ladyzhenskaya’s inequality gives us that

\displaystyle\rVert u\rVert_{L^{4}}^{2}\leq C\rVert u\rVert_{L^{2}}\rVert\nabla u\rVert_{L^{2}}\leq C\rVert\nabla u\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2},

(3.8)

where the last inequality follows from the Poincaré inequality.
We apply Lemma
2.1
to (
3.2
), yielding that

\rVert v_{t}\rVert_{H^{-1}}\leq C\rVert v_{t}\rVert_{H^{1}}\leq C\rVert\nabla\cdot(u\rho)\rVert_{H^{-1}}\leq\rVert u\rho\rVert_{L^{2}}\leq\rVert\rho\rVert_{L^{4}}\rVert u\rVert_{L^{4}}\leq C\rVert\rho\rVert_{L^{4}}\rVert\nabla u\rVert_{L^{2}},

where the last inequality is due to the estimate for
\rVert u\rVert_{L^{4}}
obtained above.
Combining this with the first inequality in (
3.8
), we obtain the desired estimate for
F
.
∎

Now we are ready to prove the main result of this section, that is, a suitable linear combination of
S(t)
and the total energy
E_{T}(t)
is a Lyapunov functional.

Proposition 3.2
.

There exist constants
C_{1},C_{2}>0
depending on
\rVert\rho_{0}\rVert_{L^{4}}
and
E_{T}(0)
such that

\frac{d}{dt}\left(C_{1}E_{T}(t)+S(t)\right)\leq-C_{2}\left(\rVert\nabla u\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}+\rVert\nabla w\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}+\rVert\nabla v\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}\right).

Proof.

Let us estimate the evolution of
S(t)
, using the equation for
w
in (
3.4
):

\frac{d}{dt}S(t)=\int ww_{t}dx=\int F\cdot wdx+\int w\cdot\Delta wdx\leq\rVert\nabla w\rVert_{L^{2}}\rVert F\rVert_{H^{-1}}-\rVert\nabla w\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}\leq C\rVert F\rVert_{H^{-1}}^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\rVert\nabla w\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2},

where the first inequality is due to the Poincaré inequality,
\rVert w\rVert_{H^{1}}\leq_{C}\rVert\nabla w\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}
, and we used the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality in the last inequality. Then Lemma
3.1
gives us

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}S(t)

\displaystyle\leq C(\rVert\rho(t)\rVert_{L^{4}}+\rVert u(t)\rVert_{L^{2}})^{2}\rVert\nabla u\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}-C\rVert\nabla w\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq C(1+\rVert\rho(t)\rVert_{L^{4}}+\rVert u(t)\rVert_{L^{2}})^{2}\rVert\nabla u\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}-C\left(\rVert\nabla w\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}+\rVert\nabla v\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}\right),

where the last inequality is due to the following elementary application of the triangular inequality,

\rVert\nabla w\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}\geq C(\rVert\nabla w\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}+\rVert\nabla(w-u)\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2})-C\rVert\nabla u\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}\geq C(\rVert\nabla w\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}+\rVert\nabla v\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2})-C\rVert\nabla u\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}.

Since
\rho
is transported by incompressible flows, we have a conservation of
\rVert\rho(t)\rVert_{L^{p}}
for any
p\in[1,\infty]
. Especially we have
\rVert\rho(t)\rVert_{L^{4}}=\rVert\rho_{0}\rVert_{L^{4}}
. Combining this with the energy conservation (
3.7
), we arrive at

\frac{d}{dt}S(t)\leq C(1+\rVert\rho_{0}\rVert_{L^{4}}+E_{T}(0))^{2}\rVert\nabla u\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}-C\left(\rVert\nabla w\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}+\rVert\nabla v\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}\right).

Therefore, we can choose
C_{1}
large enough depending on
\rVert\rho_{0}\rVert_{L^{4}}
and
E_{T}(0)
so that the above inequality and (
3.7
) give us

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\left(C_{1}E_{T}(t)+S(t)\right)

\displaystyle\leq-\left(C_{1}-C(1+\rVert\rho_{0}\rVert_{L^{2}}+E_{T}(0))^{2}\right)\rVert\nabla u\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}-C\left(\rVert\nabla w\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}+\rVert\nabla v\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\leq-C_{2}\left(\rVert\nabla u\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}+\rVert\nabla w\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}+\rVert\nabla v\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}\right),

for some constant
C_{2}>0
, which also depend only on
\rVert\rho_{0}\rVert_{L^{4}}
and
E_{T}(0)
. This finishes the proof.
∎

4.
Long-time instability for an arbitrary steady state

In this section, we investigate an application of the Lyapunov functional derived in the earlier section yielding that a long-time instability of an arbitrary steady state.
The main strategy is based on the proof of instability for the incompressible porous media equation in
[
15
, Section 4,5]
and its application to the Stokes transport system in
[
16
, Section 3]
.

We first recall the definition of
bubble type
density profile, which possesses a simple closed regular level curve.

Definition 4.1
.

[
16
, Definition 3.7.3]

A function
f:\Omega\mapsto\mathbb{R}
is said to be of bubble type, if there exists a closed curve
\Gamma_{0}\subset\Omega
enclosing a simply connected set such that
f
is constant on
\Gamma_{0}
and
\inf_{x\in\Gamma_{0}}|\nabla f|>0
.

Noting that
H^{2-\gamma}
for any
\gamma>0
fails to embed into
C^{1}
, it is possible to design an arbitrarily small perturbation of any stratified density
\rho_{s}
in
H^{2-\gamma}
destroying its stratified level set structure. More precisely, we recall the following:

Lemma 4.2
.

[
16
, Lemma 3.7.7]

For any stratified density
\rho_{s}\in C^{\infty}(\Omega)
and
\epsilon,\gamma>0
, there exists
\rho_{0}\in C^{\infty}(\Omega)
such that
\rVert\rho_{s}-\rho_{0}\rVert_{H^{2-\gamma}}\leq\epsilon
and
\rho_{0}
is of bubble type.

The following lemma addresses a uniform lower bound of an anisotropic norm of solutions to a general transport equation when the initial data is of bubble type. While the detailed proof provided in
[
15
]
is presented in the context of the IPM equation, the proof remains essentially the same. Therefore, we will omit the proof here.

Lemma 4.3
.

[
15
, Proposition 4.1, Corollary 4.2]
Let
f
be a solution to the transport equation

f_{t}+U\cdot\nabla f=0,\quad f(0,x)=f_{0}(x),

for some smooth divergence free vector field
U
. If
f_{0}
is a smooth function of bubble type, then there exists a positive constant
C=C(f_{0})
such that

\rVert\partial_{1}f(t)\rVert_{L^{2}}\geq C,\text{ for all $t>0$}.

Now, the proof of Theorem
1.1
follows straightforwardly.

Proof of Theorem
1.1
.

Let
\rho_{s}
be a smooth stratified density and let
\gamma,\epsilon>0
be fixed. Let us fix a smooth initial velocity
u_{0}
such that
\nabla\cdot u_{0}=0
and
u_{0}=0
on
\partial\Omega
. Thanks to Lemma
4.2
, we can find a bubble type smooth initial density
\rho_{0}
such that
\rVert\rho_{s}-\rho_{0}\rVert_{H^{2-\gamma}}<\epsilon
. Since the initial data
(\rho_{0},u_{0})
, the Boussinesq system
1.1
admits a unique smooth solution
(\rho(t),u(t))
,
\rho(t)
is transported by a smooth divergence free vector field. Then Lemma
4.3
tells us that there exists a constant
C=C(\rho_{0})>0
such that

\displaystyle\rVert\partial_{1}\rho(t)\rVert_{L^{2}}\geq C,\text{ for all $t>0$.}

(4.1)

Moreover, Proposition
3.2
yields that for some constants
C_{1},C_{2}
depending on the initial data, we have

\displaystyle C_{1}E_{T}(t)+S(t)+C_{2}\int_{0}^{t}\rVert\nabla u(s)\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}+\rVert\nabla w(s)\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}+\rVert\nabla v(s)\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}ds\leq\underbrace{C_{1}E_{T}(0)+S(0)}_{=:C(\rho_{0},u_{0})},\text{ for all $t>0$.}

(4.2)

Taking
t\to\infty
, we obtain

\int_{0}^{\infty}\rVert\nabla v(s)\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}ds\leq C(\rho_{0},u_{0}).

This implies that

\displaystyle\liminf_{t\to\infty}t\rVert\nabla v(t)\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}=0.

(4.3)

Applying Lemma
2.3
to the steady Stokes equation (
3.1
), we observe that the velocity
v
admits a stream function
\Psi
such that
v=\nabla^{\perp}\Psi
and

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\Delta^{2}\Psi=\partial_{1}\rho&\text{ in $\Omega$}\\
\Psi=\nabla\Psi=0&\text{ on $\partial\Omega$.}\end{cases}

(4.4)

Therefore, the Gagliardo-Nirenberg interpolation inequality give us

\rVert\partial_{1}\rho\rVert_{L^{2}}\leq C\rVert\Delta^{2}\Phi\rVert_{L^{2}}\leq C\rVert\Delta\Phi\rVert_{L^{2}}^{k/(k+2)}\rVert\Delta^{2}\Phi\rVert_{H^{k}(\Omega)}^{2/(k+2)}\leq C\rVert\nabla v\rVert_{L^{2}}^{k/(k+2)}\rVert\partial_{1}\rho\rVert_{H^{k}}^{2/(k+2)},\text{ for all $k>0$.}

Combining this with (
4.1
), we obtain
\rVert\partial_{1}\rho(t)\rVert_{H^{k}}\geq C\rVert\nabla v(t)\rVert_{L^{2}}^{-k/2}
. Together with (
4.3
), we conclude that

\limsup_{t\to\infty}t^{-k/4}\rVert\partial_{1}\rho(t)\rVert_{H^{k}}\geq\limsup_{t\to\infty}\left(t\rVert\nabla v\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}\right)^{-k/4}=\infty,\text{ for all $k>0$,}

which proves the theorem.
∎

\displaystyle\leq_{C}\rVert u_{tt}\rVert_{L^{2}}+\rVert u_{t}\rVert_{H^{2}}\rVert u\rVert_{H^{1}}+\rVert u\rVert_{L^{\infty}}\left(\rVert w\rVert_{H^{2}}+\rVert F\rVert_{L^{2}}+\rVert u\rVert_{H^{1}}\right),

Lemma 5.3

Proof.

Recalling

Lemma 5.5

[

Let

The proof of the lemma is identical to

The main result of this subsection is the next proposition.

Proposition 5.6
.

Suppose that a solution
(\theta(t),u(t))
to (
5.2
). Then there exists
\delta_{0}=\delta(\rho_{s})>0
such that if (
5.6
) holds for
\delta_{0}
, then it holds that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{dt}\left(\rVert\Delta^{2}\theta\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}+\rVert w_{t}\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}+\rVert w_{tt}\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\leq-C\left(\rVert\partial_{1}\Delta\theta\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}+\rVert u\rVert_{H^{5}}^{2}+\rVert w\rVert_{H^{5}}^{2}\right)+C(A_{1}+A_{2}),

(5.50)

where

\displaystyle A_{1}

\displaystyle:=\left(\rVert u_{2}\rVert_{W^{2,\infty}}+\rVert u_{2}\rVert_{H^{3}}+\rVert\Delta^{2}w\rVert_{L^{2}}\right)\rVert\Delta^{2}\theta\rVert_{L^{2}}

(5.51)

\displaystyle A_{2}

\displaystyle:=\rVert\partial_{1}\theta\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}+\rVert u\rVert_{H^{1}}^{2}+\rVert w_{tt}\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}+\rVert w\rVert_{H^{2}}^{2}.

Proof.

Let us compute the evolution of
\rVert\Delta^{2}\theta\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2},\rVert w_{t}\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2},\rVert w_{tt}\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}
separately.

Computation for
\rVert\Delta^{2}\theta\rVert_{L^{2}}
.

From (
5.2
), we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{dt}\rVert\Delta^{2}\theta\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}=-\int\Delta^{2}(u\cdot\nabla\theta)\Delta^{2}\theta dx+\int\Delta^{2}(-\partial_{2}\rho_{s}u_{2})\Delta^{2}\theta dx

(5.52)

We simplify the linear term first. We rewrite it as

\displaystyle\int\Delta^{2}(-\partial_{2}\rho_{s}u_{2})\Delta^{2}\theta dx

\displaystyle=\int\left(\Delta^{2}(-\partial_{2}\rho_{s}u_{2})-(-\partial_{2}\rho_{s}\Delta^{2}u_{2})\right)\Delta^{2}\theta dx+\int-\partial_{2}\rho_{s}\Delta^{2}u_{2}\Delta^{2}\theta dx=:I_{1}+I_{2},

(5.53)

while the second integral can be further decomposed into

\displaystyle I_{2}=\int-\partial_{2}\rho_{s}\Delta^{2}(u_{2}-v_{2})\Delta^{2}\theta dx+\int-\partial_{2}\rho_{s}\Delta^{2}v_{2}\Delta^{2}\theta dx=:I_{21}+I_{22}.

(5.54)

Since
\rho_{s}
is independent of
x_{1}
, the the stream function of
v
in (
4.4
) can be restated as

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\Delta^{2}\Psi=\partial_{1}\theta&\text{ in $\Omega$,}\\
\Psi=\nabla\Psi=0&\text{ on $\partial\Omega$.}\end{cases}

(5.55)

Thus we can rewrite
I_{22}
as

\displaystyle I_{22}

\displaystyle=\int-\partial_{2}\rho_{s}\partial_{1}\Delta^{2}\Psi\Delta^{2}\theta dx=\int\partial_{2}\rho_{s}\partial_{1}\theta\partial_{1}\Delta^{2}\theta dx=\int\Delta(\partial_{2}\rho_{s}\partial_{1}\theta)\partial_{1}\Delta\theta dx

\displaystyle=\int\partial_{2}\rho_{s}|\partial_{1}\Delta\theta|^{2}dx+\int(\partial_{222}\rho_{s}\partial_{1}\theta+2\partial_{22}\rho_{s}\partial_{12}\theta)\partial_{1}\Delta\theta dx

\displaystyle=:I_{221}+I_{222},

(5.56)

where the integration by parts in the third equality is justified by the boundary condition of
\theta
in Lemma
5.5
. Hence, it follows from (
5.53
), (
5.54
) and (
5.56
) that the linear term can be decomposed as

\displaystyle\int\Delta^{2}(-\partial_{2}\rho_{s}u_{2})\Delta^{2}\theta dx-I_{221}=I_{1}+I_{21}+I_{222}.

(5.57)

For
I_{1}
in (
5.53
), we estimate

|I_{1}|\leq\left(\rVert\partial_{2}\rho_{s}\rVert_{H^{4}}\rVert u_{2}\rVert_{L^{\infty}}+\rVert\partial_{2}\rho_{s}\rVert_{L^{\infty}}\rVert u_{2}\rVert_{H^{3}}\right)\rVert\Delta^{2}\theta\rVert_{L^{2}}\leq C\rVert u_{2}\rVert_{H^{3}}\rVert\Delta^{2}\theta\rVert_{L^{2}},

where the last inequality follows from (
1.10
) and (

).
For
I_{21}
, we apply the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality and the definition of

to obtain

|I_{21}|\leq\rVert\partial_{2}\rho_{s}\rVert_{L^{\infty}}\rVert w\rVert_{H^{4}}\rVert\Delta^{2}\theta\rVert_{L^{2}}\leq C\rVert w\rVert_{H^{4}}\rVert\Delta^{2}\theta\rVert_{L^{2}}.

For

|I_{222}|\leq\rVert\partial_{222}\rho_{s}\rVert_{L^{\infty}}\rVert\partial_{1}\theta\rVert_{H^{1}}\rVert\partial_{1}\Delta\theta\rVert_{L^{2}}\leq C\rVert\partial_{1}\theta\rVert_{H^{1}}\rVert\partial_{1}\Delta\theta\rVert_{L^{2}}.

Using the interpolation inequality, we have
\rVert\partial_{1}\theta\rVert_{H^{1}}\leq C\rVert\partial_{1}\theta\rVert_{L^{2}}^{1/2}\rVert\partial_{1}\theta\rVert_{H^{2}}^{1/2}\leq\rVert\partial_{1}\theta\rVert_{L^{2}}^{1/2}\rVert\partial_{1}\Delta\theta\rVert_{L^{2}}^{1/2}

|I_{222}|\leq C\rVert\partial_{1}\theta\rVert_{L^{2}}^{1/2}\rVert\partial_{1}\Delta\theta\rVert_{H^{2}}^{3/2}\leq C_{\eta}\rVert\partial_{1}\theta\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}+\eta\rVert\partial_{1}\theta\rVert_{H^{2}}^{2},\text{ for any $\eta>0$,}

where the last inequality follows from Young’s inequality. Collecting the above estimates, we arrive at

\displaystyle|I_{1}|+|I_{21}|+|I_{222}|\leq C(\rVert u_{2}\rVert_{H^{3}}+\rVert w\rVert_{H^{4}})\rVert\Delta^{2}\theta\rVert_{L^{2}}+C_{\eta}\rVert\partial_{1}\theta\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}+\eta\rVert\partial_{1}\Delta\theta\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2},\text{ for any $\eta>0$}.

Note that
I_{221}\leq-\gamma\rVert\partial_{1}\Delta\theta\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}\leq_{C}-\rVert\partial_{1}\Delta\theta\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}
which follows from the upper bound of

in (

\displaystyle\int\Delta^{2}(-\partial_{2}\rho_{s}u_{2})\Delta^{2}\theta dx\leq-C\rVert\partial_{1}\Delta\theta\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}+C(\rVert u_{2}\rVert_{H^{3}}+\rVert w\rVert_{H^{4}})\rVert\Delta^{2}\theta\rVert_{L^{2}}+C\rVert\partial_{1}\theta\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}.

(5.59)

This gives an estimate for the second integral in the right-hand side of (
5.52
).

In order to estimate the nonlinear term
\int\Delta^{2}(u\cdot\nabla\theta)\Delta^{2}\theta dx
in (

), we recall the following Lemma:

Lemma 5.7
.

[
20
, Lemma 4.5]

Let
u
be a smooth divergence free vector field such that
U=0
on
\partial\Omega
and

be a smooth scalar-valued function such that

\displaystyle\left|\int_{\Omega}\Delta^{2}(u\cdot\nabla f)\Delta^{2}fdx\right|\leq_{C}\left(\rVert U_{2}\rVert_{H^{3}(\Omega)}+\rVert U_{2}\rVert_{W^{2,\infty}(\Omega)}\right)\rVert\Delta^{2}f\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\rVert U\rVert_{H^{5}(\Omega)}\rVert\partial_{1}\Delta f\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\rVert\Delta^{2}f\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega)},

where

is a universal constant.

Thanks to Lemma
5.5
and the boundary condition of

in (

), we can apply the above lemma with

and
f=\theta
which immediately gives us

\displaystyle\left|\int\Delta^{2}(u\cdot\nabla\theta)\Delta^{2}\theta dx\right|

\displaystyle\leq_{C}\left(\rVert u_{2}\rVert_{H^{3}}+\rVert u_{2}\rVert_{W^{2,\infty}}\right)\rVert\Delta^{2}\theta\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}+\rVert u\rVert_{H^{5}}\rVert\partial_{1}\Delta\theta\rVert_{L^{2}}\rVert\Delta^{2}\theta\rVert_{L^{2}}.

(5.60)

To control the highest order term,
\rVert u\rVert_{H^{5}}
, we rewrite it as
u=w+v
, thus

\rVert u\rVert_{H^{5}}\leq\rVert w\rVert_{H^{5}}+\rVert v\rVert_{H^{5}}.

Applying Lemma
2.2
to (
5.55
), we have

\displaystyle\rVert v\rVert_{H^{5}}\leq\rVert\Phi\rVert_{H^{6}}\leq C\rVert\partial_{1}\theta\rVert_{H^{2}}\leq C\rVert\partial_{1}\Delta\theta\rVert_{L^{2}},

(5.61)

where the last inequality is due to Lemma
2.4
. With these estimates, the last term in (
5.60
) can be bounded as

\displaystyle\rVert u\rVert_{H^{5}}\rVert\partial_{1}\Delta\theta\rVert_{L^{2}}\rVert\Delta^{2}\theta\rVert_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\rVert w\rVert_{H^{5}}\rVert\partial_{1}\Delta\theta\rVert_{L^{2}}\rVert\Delta^{2}\theta\rVert_{L^{2}}+C\rVert\partial_{1}\Delta\theta\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}\rVert\Delta^{2}\theta\rVert_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\rVert w\rVert_{H^{5}}^{2}\rVert\Delta^{2}\theta\rVert_{L^{2}}+C\rVert\partial_{1}\Delta\theta\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}\rVert\Delta^{2}\theta\rVert_{L^{2}},

where we used Young’s inequality for the last inequality. Plugging this into (
5.60
), we get

\left|\int\Delta^{2}(u\cdot\nabla\theta)\Delta^{2}\theta dx\right|\leq_{C}\left(\rVert u_{2}\rVert_{H^{3}}+\rVert u_{2}\rVert_{W^{2,\infty}}\right)\rVert\Delta^{2}\theta\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}+\rVert w\rVert_{H^{5}}^{2}\rVert\Delta^{2}\theta\rVert_{L^{2}}+\rVert\partial_{1}\Delta\theta\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}\rVert\Delta^{2}\theta\rVert_{L^{2}}.

Plugging this and (
5.59
) into (
5.52
), we arrive at

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{dt}\rVert\Delta^{2}\theta\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq-C\left(1-C\rVert\Delta^{2}\theta\rVert_{L^{2}}\right)\rVert\partial_{1}\Delta\theta\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle\ +C\left(\rVert u_{2}\rVert_{H^{3}}+\rVert u_{2}\rVert_{W^{2,\infty}}\right)\rVert\Delta^{2}\theta\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}+C\left(\rVert u_{2}\rVert_{H^{3}}+\rVert\Delta^{2}w\rVert_{L^{2}}\right)\rVert\Delta^{2}\theta\rVert_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle\ +C\rVert w\rVert_{H^{5}}^{2}\rVert\Delta^{2}\theta\rVert_{L^{2}}+C\rVert\partial_{1}\theta\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}.

Using (
5.6
), we make this slightly simpler, that is,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{dt}\rVert\Delta^{2}\theta\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq-C\rVert\partial_{1}\Delta\theta\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle\ +C\left(\rVert u_{2}\rVert_{W^{2,\infty}}+\rVert u_{2}\rVert_{H^{3}}+\rVert\Delta^{2}w\rVert_{L^{2}}\right)\rVert\Delta^{2}\theta\rVert_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle\ +C\rVert w\rVert_{H^{5}}^{2}\rVert\Delta^{2}\theta\rVert_{L^{2}}+C\rVert\partial_{1}\theta\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}.

(5.62)

Computation for
\rVert w_{t}\rVert_{L^{2}}
.

Now we turn to derive an estimate for
\rVert w_{t}\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}
. Using (
5.4
), we compute

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{dt}\rVert w_{t}\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle=-\int F_{t}w_{t}dx-\int|\nabla w_{t}|^{2}dx\leq C\rVert F_{t}\rVert_{L^{2}}\rVert w_{t}\rVert_{L^{2}}-\rVert\nabla w_{t}\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq_{C}\rVert F_{t}\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}-C\rVert\nabla w_{t}\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq_{C}\left(\rVert w\rVert_{H^{2}}^{2}+\rVert u\rVert_{H^{1}}^{2}+\rVert w_{tt}\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}\right)-C\rVert\nabla w_{t}\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2},

(5.63)

where the second last inequality is due to Young’s inequality and the last inequality is from (
5.33
) and (
5.6
).

Computation for
\rVert w_{tt}\rVert_{L^{2}}
.

Next step, we estimate for
\rVert w_{tt}\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}
in a similar way. Using (
5.5
), we compute

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{dt}\rVert w_{tt}\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle=-\int F_{tt}w_{tt}dx-\int|\nabla w_{tt}|^{2}dx\leq C\rVert F_{tt}\rVert_{H^{-1}}\rVert\nabla w_{tt}\rVert_{L^{2}}-\rVert\nabla w_{tt}\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq_{C}\rVert F_{tt}\rVert_{H^{-1}}^{2}-C\rVert\nabla w_{tt}\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2},

where the first inequality is due to the Poincaré inequality,
\rVert w_{tt}\rVert_{H^{1}}\leq_{C}\rVert\nabla w_{tt}\rVert_{L^{2}}
and the last inequality is due to Young’s inequality. Using (
5.35
), we arrive at

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{dt}\rVert w_{tt}\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}\leq_{C}\rVert w_{tt}\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}+\rVert w\rVert_{H^{2}}^{2}+\rVert u\rVert_{H^{1}}^{2}-C\rVert\nabla w_{tt}\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}.

(5.64)

Again using
\rVert w_{tt}\rVert_{H^{1}}\leq C\rVert\nabla w_{tt}\rVert_{L^{2}}
and combining this with (
5.10
), we get

\displaystyle\rVert\nabla w_{tt}\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle\geq C\rVert w\rVert_{H^{5}}^{2}-C\left(\rVert F\rVert_{H^{3}}^{2}+\rVert F_{t}\rVert_{H^{1}}^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\geq C\rVert w\rVert_{H^{5}}-C\left(\rVert u\rVert_{H^{4}}^{2}\rVert w_{tt}\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}+\rVert w\rVert_{H^{2}}^{2}+\rVert u\rVert_{H^{3}}^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\geq C\rVert w\rVert_{H^{5}}-C\left(\rVert w_{tt}\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}+\rVert w\rVert_{H^{2}}^{2}+\rVert u\rVert_{H^{3}}^{2}\right)

where the second last inequality is due to (
5.32
) and (
5.34
), and the last inequality is due to (
5.6
). Thus, from (
5.64
), we arrive that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{dt}\rVert w_{tt}\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}\leq_{C}\rVert w_{tt}\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}+\rVert w\rVert_{H^{2}}^{2}+\rVert u\rVert_{H^{3}}^{2}-C\rVert w\rVert_{H^{5}}^{2}.

(5.65)

Now, we combine all the estimates (
5.62
), (
5.63
) and (
5.65
), we arrive at

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{dt}\left(\rVert\Delta^{2}\theta\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}+\rVert w_{t}\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}+\rVert w_{tt}\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\underbrace{-C\left(\rVert\partial_{1}\Delta\theta\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}+\rVert w\rVert_{H^{5}}^{2}\right)+C\rVert w\rVert_{H^{5}}^{2}\rVert\Delta^{2}\theta\rVert_{L^{2}}+\rVert u\rVert_{H^{3}}^{2}}_{=:J_{1}}

(5.66)

\displaystyle\ +C\left(\rVert u_{2}\rVert_{W^{2,\infty}}+\rVert u_{2}\rVert_{H^{3}}+\rVert\Delta^{2}w\rVert_{L^{2}}\right)\rVert\Delta^{2}\theta\rVert_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle\ +C\left(\rVert\partial_{1}\theta\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}+\rVert w_{tt}\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}+\rVert w\rVert_{H^{2}}^{2}\right).

Let us simplify the terms
J_{1}
. From (
5.6
), we can assume
\rVert\Delta^{2}\theta\rVert_{L^{2}}
is sufficiently small so that

\displaystyle J_{1}\leq-C\left(\rVert\partial_{1}\Delta\theta\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}+\rVert w\rVert_{H^{5}}^{2}\right)+\rVert u\rVert_{H^{3}}^{2}.

(5.67)

Using
w=u-v
, we also have

\rVert u\rVert_{H^{5}}\leq_{C}\rVert w\rVert_{H^{5}}+\rVert v\rVert_{H^{5}}\leq_{C}\rVert w\rVert_{H^{5}}+\rVert\Psi\rVert_{H^{6}}\leq_{C}\rVert w\rVert_{H^{5}}+\rVert\partial_{1}\theta\rVert_{H^{2}}\leq_{C}\rVert w\rVert_{H^{5}}+\rVert\partial_{1}\Delta\theta\rVert_{L^{2}},

where the second and the third inequality are due to (
5.55
) and Lemma
2.2
, and the last inequality follows from Lemma
2.4
. With this, we rewrite the estimate (
5.67
) as

J_{1}\leq-C\left(\rVert\partial_{1}\Delta\theta\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}+\rVert w\rVert_{H^{5}}^{2}+\rVert u\rVert_{H^{5}}\right)+\rVert u\rVert_{H^{3}}^{2}.

Also, the Gagliardo-Nirenberg interpolation theorem tells us

\rVert u\rVert_{H^{3}}^{2}\leq_{C}\rVert u\rVert_{H^{1}}\rVert u\rVert_{H^{5}}\leq_{C}\eta\rVert u\rVert_{H^{5}}^{2}+C_{\eta}\rVert u\rVert_{H^{1}}^{2},\text{ for any $\eta>0$,}

where the last inequality is due to Young’s inequality. Thus by choosing
\eta
sufficiently small, we can estimate
J_{1}
as

J_{1}\leq-C\left(\rVert\partial_{1}\Delta\theta\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}+\rVert w\rVert_{H^{5}}^{2}+\rVert u\rVert_{H^{5}}\right)+C\rVert u\rVert_{H^{1}}^{2}.

Hence plugging this into (
5.66
), we obtain the desired estimate.
∎

5.3.
Derivation of decay rates via energy structure

In this subsection, the main goal is to derive sufficient decay rates in time for the terms
A_{1}
and
A_{2}
in (
5.50
), under the assumption (
5.6
). We will achieve this as an application of the Lyapunov functional constructed in Section
3
. As a first step, we derive an explicit decay rate of the total energy and the enstrophy (in a time average sense).

As a preparation, let us recall the following lemma, showing that the vertical rearrangement is indeed a minimizer of the potential energy:

Lemma 5.8
.

[
20
, Proposition 2.5]

Suppose
\rho_{s}
satisfies (
1.10
). There exists
\delta=\delta(\gamma,\rVert\rho_{s}\rVert_{H^{4}})>0
such that if
f={\rho}_{s}
on
\partial\Omega
and
\rVert f-{\rho}_{s}\rVert_{H^{3}(\Omega)}\leq\delta
, then

\displaystyle C^{-1}\rVert f-f^{*}\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}\leq\int_{\Omega}(f(x)-f^{*}(x))x_{2}dx\leq C\rVert f-f^{*}\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2},

(5.68)

where
f^{*}
is the vertical rearrangement of
f
, defined in (
1.11
).
Moreover, we have

\displaystyle\rVert\partial_{1}f\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\geq C\rVert f-f^{*}\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega)}.

(5.69)

The constant
C
depends only on
\gamma
and
\rVert\rho_{s}\rVert_{H^{4}}
.

Proposition 5.9
.

Let
(\rho(t),u(t))
be a solution to the Boussinesq system (
1.1
) for
t\in[0,T]
for some
T>0
. Then there exists a small constant
\delta_{0}=\delta_{0}(\rho_{s})>0
such that if (
5.6
) holds, then

\displaystyle E_{T}(t)+S(t)

\displaystyle\leq C\frac{\delta}{t^{2}},

(5.70)

\displaystyle\frac{2}{t}\int_{t/2}^{t}\rVert\nabla u(s)\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}+\rVert\nabla v\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}+\rVert\nabla w(s)\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}ds

\displaystyle\leq C\frac{\delta}{t^{3}}.

(5.71)

Proof.

Assuming (
5.6
) for sufficiently small
\delta_{0}\ll 1
, it is trivial that
\rVert\rho_{0}\rVert_{L^{4}}
and
E_{T}(0)
is bounded by a universal constant. Therefore Proposition
3.2
tells us that

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\left(CE_{T}(t)+S(t)\right)\leq-C\left(\rVert\nabla w\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}+\rVert\nabla v\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}+\rVert\nabla u\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}\right)\leq-C\left(\rVert u\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}+\rVert w\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}+\rVert\nabla v\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}\right),

(5.72)

where the last inequality follows from the Poincaré inequality.

Now, let us estimate the lower bound of
\rVert\nabla v\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}.
From the stream function formulation in (
4.4
), we have

\displaystyle\rVert\nabla v\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}=\rVert\Phi\rVert_{H^{2}}^{2}.

(5.73)

Note that (
4.4
) also implies

\displaystyle\rVert\partial_{1}\rho\rVert_{L^{2}}=\rVert\Delta^{2}\Phi\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}\leq_{C}\rVert\Delta^{2}\Phi\rVert_{H^{2}}^{1/2}\rVert\Delta\Phi\rVert_{L^{2}}^{1/2},

(5.74)

where the last inequality is due to the Gagliardo-Nirenberg interpolation inequality. Also, Lemma
5.8
, under the assumption (
5.6
) for sufficiently small
\delta_{0}
, implies

\displaystyle C^{-1}\rVert\rho(t)-\rho_{0}^{*}\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}\leq E_{P}(t)\leq C\rVert\rho(t)-\rho^{*}\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}\leq C\rVert\partial_{1}\rho\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}.

(5.75)

Hence, combining this estimate and (
5.74
), we obtain

\rVert\Phi\rVert_{H^{2}}^{2}\geq\rVert\Delta\Phi\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}\geq_{C}\rVert\partial_{1}\rho\rVert_{L^{2}}^{4}\rVert\Delta^{2}\Phi\rVert_{H^{2}}^{-2}\geq_{C}\rVert\rho-\rho^{*}\rVert_{L^{2}}^{4}\rVert\Delta^{2}\Phi\rVert_{H^{2}}^{-2}\geq_{C}E_{P}(t)^{2}\rVert\Delta^{2}\Phi\rVert_{H^{2}}^{-2}.

Plugging this into (
5.73
), we arrive at

\rVert\nabla v\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}\geq_{C}E_{P}(t)^{2}\rVert\Delta^{2}\Phi\rVert_{H^{2}}^{-2}.

This implies that the energy estimate in (
5.72
) can be written as

\frac{d}{dt}\left(CE_{P}(t)+CE_{K}(t)+S(t)\right)\leq_{C}-\left((CE_{P}(t))^{2}\rVert\Delta^{2}\Phi(t)\rVert_{H^{2}}^{-2}+CE_{K}(t)+S(t)\right).

Then applying Lemma
A.1
with
f(t):=CE_{P}(t)
,
g(t):=CE_{K}(t)+S(t)
and
\alpha(t):=\rVert\Delta^{2}\Phi(t)\rVert_{H^{2}}^{2}
, we obtain

\displaystyle CE_{P}(t)+CE_{K}(t)+S(t)\leq C\frac{1}{t^{2}}\left(\int_{0}^{T}\rVert\Delta^{2}\Phi\rVert_{H^{2}}^{2}dt+\left(E_{P}(0)+E_{K}(0)+S(0)\right)\right)\text{ for all $t\in[0,T]$}.

(5.76)

On the other hand, (
4.4
), (
5.6
) and Lemma
2.4
implies

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\rVert\Delta^{2}\Phi\rVert_{H^{2}}^{2}dt\leq_{C}\int_{0}^{T}\rVert\partial_{1}\theta\rVert_{H^{2}}^{2}dt\leq_{C}\delta.

(5.77)

From (
5.75
) and (
5.73
), we have

\displaystyle E_{P}(t)+\rVert\nabla v\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}\leq_{C}\rVert\partial_{1}\rho\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}+\rVert\Phi\rVert_{H^{2}}^{2}\leq_{C}\rVert\partial_{1}\theta\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}\leq_{C}\delta,

(5.78)

where the second last inequality is due to (
4.4
) and Lemma
2.2
and the last inequality is due to (
5.6
). Moreover, we have

E_{K}(t)+S(t)\leq_{C}\rVert u(t)\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}+\rVert w(t)\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}\leq_{C}\rVert u\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}+\rVert v\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}\leq_{C}\delta,

were the last inequality is from (
5.6
) and (
5.78
). Hence, plugging this, (
5.78
) and (
5.77
) into (
5.76
), we obtain (
5.70
).

Lastly, integrating (
5.72
) in time over
[t/2,t]
, we get

CE_{T}(t)+S(t)+C\int_{t/2}^{t}\rVert\nabla u\rVert_{L^{2}}+\rVert\nabla v\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}+\rVert\nabla w\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}ds\leq CE_{T}(t/2)+S(t/2)\leq\frac{C\delta}{t^{2}},

where the last inequality is due to (
5.70
). Since
E_{T}(t),S(t)\geq 0
,
this gives us (
5.71
).
∎

In view of Proposition
5.6
, the decay rates obtained in the above proposition is not sufficient, since they cannot control high Sobolev norms such as
\rVert u_{2}\rVert_{H^{3}},\rVert u_{2}\rVert_{W^{2,\infty}},\rVert\Delta^{2}w\rVert_{L^{2}}
and
\rVert w_{tt}\rVert_{L^{2}}
. Sufficient decay rates for these high norms will be achieved in the next proposition.

Proposition 5.10
.

Let
(\rho(t),u(t))
be a solution to the Boussinesq system (
1.1
). Then there exists a small constant
\delta_{0}=\delta_{0}(\rho_{s})>0
such that if (
5.6
) holds for
\delta<\delta_{0}
, then

\displaystyle\frac{2}{t}\int_{t/2}^{t}\rVert\partial_{1}\theta(s)\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}ds

\displaystyle\leq_{C}\frac{\delta}{t^{2}},

\displaystyle\frac{2}{t}\int_{t/2}^{t}\rVert w_{tt}(s)\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}+\rVert w(s)\rVert_{H^{4}}^{2}+\rVert u_{2}(s)\rVert_{H^{2}}^{2}ds

\displaystyle\leq_{C}\frac{\delta}{t^{3}},

for all
t\in[0,T]
.

Proof.

Estimate for
\rVert w_{tt}\rVert_{L^{2}}
.

Taking the Leray projection
\mathbb{P}
in (
3.4
) and differentiating it, we observe

\nabla w_{t}=-\nabla\mathbb{P}F+\nabla\mathbb{P}\Delta w.

A standard energy estimate gives us

\frac{d}{dt}\rVert\nabla w\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}=-\int\nabla\mathbb{P}F\nabla wdx+\int\nabla\mathbb{P}\Delta w\nabla wdx=\int F\mathbb{P}\Delta wdx-\int|\mathbb{P}\Delta w|^{2}dx\leq-C\rVert\mathbb{P}\Delta w\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}+C\rVert F\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2},

where the last inequality follows from Young’s inequality. Using (
5.30
), we arrive at

\frac{d}{dt}\rVert\nabla w\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}\leq-C\rVert\mathbb{P}\Delta w\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}+C\rVert\nabla u\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}.

Then, applying Lemma
A.2
to the above differential inequality, noting the estimates for
\nabla u
and
\nabla w
in (
5.71
), we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{2}{t}\int_{t/2}^{t}\rVert\mathbb{P}\Delta w\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}ds\leq_{C}\frac{\delta}{t^{3}}.

(5.79)

Again, applying the Leray projection to (
3.4
), we have
w_{t}=-\mathbb{P}F+\mathbb{P}\Delta w,

hence

\rVert w_{t}\rVert_{L^{2}}\leq_{C}\rVert F\rVert_{L^{2}}+\rVert\mathbb{P}\Delta w\rVert_{L^{2}}.

Applying Lemma
2.1
to (
3.4
), we also have

\rVert w\rVert_{H^{2}}\leq\rVert w_{t}\rVert_{L^{2}}+\rVert F\rVert_{L^{2}}\leq_{C}\rVert F\rVert_{L^{2}}+\rVert\mathbb{P}\Delta w\rVert_{L^{2}}\leq_{C}\rVert\nabla u\rVert_{L^{2}}+\rVert\mathbb{P}\Delta w\rVert_{L^{2}},

where the last inequality follows from (
5.30
).
Hence, using (
5.79
) and (
5.71
), we arrive at

\displaystyle\frac{2}{t}\int_{t/2}^{t}\rVert w\rVert_{H^{2}}^{2}ds\leq\frac{2}{t}\int_{t/2}^{t}\rVert\nabla u\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}+\rVert\mathbb{P}\Delta w\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}ds\leq_{C}\frac{\delta}{t^{3}}.

(5.80)

Towards the higher derivative estimate for
w
, we take the Leray projection
\mathbb{P}
in the equation of
w_{tt}
in (
5.4
), yielding that

\displaystyle w_{tt}=-\mathbb{P}F_{t}+\mathbb{P}\Delta w_{t}.

(5.81)

A standard energy estimate gives us

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\frac{1}{2}\rVert\nabla w_{t}\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle=-\int\nabla\mathbb{P}(F_{t})\nabla w_{t}dx+\int\nabla w_{t}\nabla\mathbb{P}\Delta w_{t}dx=-\int F_{t}\mathbb{P}\Delta w_{t}dx-\int|\mathbb{P}\Delta w_{t}|^{2}dx

\displaystyle\leq C\rVert F_{t}\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\rVert\mathbb{P}\Delta w_{t}\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq C\rVert F_{t}\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}-C\rVert\mathbb{P}\Delta w_{t}\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}-C\rVert w_{t}\rVert_{H^{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq C\rVert F_{t}\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}-C\rVert\mathbb{P}\Delta w_{t}\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}-C\rVert\nabla w_{t}\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2},

(5.82)

where the second inequality follows from (
5.11
).
From the boundedness of the Leray projection (see Lemma
2.5
), we notice that (
5.81
) implies

\rVert\mathbb{P}\Delta w_{t}\rVert_{L^{2}}\geq\rVert w_{tt}\rVert_{L^{2}}-\rVert F_{t}\rVert_{L^{2}}.

Thus (
5.82
) yields

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\frac{1}{2}\rVert\nabla w_{t}\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}\leq-C\rVert\nabla w_{t}\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}-C\rVert w_{tt}\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}+C\rVert F_{t}\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}

(5.83)

Using (
5.33
) we see that

-C\rVert w_{tt}\rVert_{L^{2}}+C\rVert F_{t}\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}\leq-C(1-C\rVert u\rVert_{H^{3}})\rVert w_{tt}\rVert_{L^{2}}+C\left(\rVert w\rVert_{H^{2}}+\rVert u\rVert_{H^{1}}\right)\leq-C\rVert w_{tt}\rVert_{L^{2}}+C\left(\rVert w\rVert_{H^{2}}+\rVert u\rVert_{H^{1}}\right),

where the last inequality is due to (
5.6
). Plugging this into (
5.83
), we arrive at

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\frac{1}{2}\rVert\nabla w_{t}\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}\leq-C\rVert\nabla w_{t}\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}-C\rVert w_{tt}\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}+C\left(\rVert w\rVert_{H^{2}}^{2}+\rVert u\rVert_{H^{1}}^{2}\right).

(5.84)

We notice that the last term in the right-hand side satisfies
\frac{2}{t}\int_{t/2}^{t}\rVert w(s)\rVert_{H^{2}}^{2}+\rVert u(s)\rVert_{H^{1}}^{2}ds\leq_{C}\frac{\delta}{t^{3}}
, which is due to (
5.80
) and (
5.71
). Thus applying Lemma
A.3
to (
5.84
), we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{2}{t}\int_{t/2}^{t}\rVert\nabla w_{t}\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}ds\leq\frac{C}{t^{3}}\left(\rVert\nabla w_{t}(0)\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}+\int_{0}^{T}\rVert w(s)\rVert_{H^{2}}^{2}+\rVert u(s)\rVert_{H^{1}}^{2}ds\right).

(5.85)

Applying Lemma
2.1
to (
5.4
) and (
5.5
), we see that

\displaystyle\rVert\nabla w_{t}\rVert_{L^{2}}\leq_{C}\rVert w_{t}\rVert_{H^{2}}\leq_{C}\rVert w_{tt}\rVert_{L^{2}}+\rVert F_{t}\rVert_{L^{2}},\quad\rVert w\rVert_{H^{2}}\leq_{C}\rVert F\rVert_{L^{2}}+\rVert w_{t}\rVert_{L^{2}}.

(5.86)

Hence, (
5.33
) tell us that

\displaystyle\rVert\nabla w_{t}(t)\rVert_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq_{C}(1+\rVert u\rVert_{H^{3}})\rVert w_{tt}\rVert_{L^{2}}+\rVert w\rVert_{H^{2}}+\rVert u\rVert_{H^{1}}

\displaystyle\leq_{C}(1+\rVert u\rVert_{H^{3}})\rVert w_{tt}\rVert_{L^{2}}+\rVert F\rVert_{L^{2}}+\rVert w_{t}\rVert_{L^{2}}+\rVert u\rVert_{H^{1}}

\displaystyle\leq_{C}(1+\rVert u\rVert_{H^{3}})\rVert w_{tt}\rVert_{L^{2}}+\rVert w_{t}\rVert_{L^{2}}+\rVert u\rVert_{H^{1}}

\displaystyle\leq_{C}\sqrt{\delta},

where the second inequality is due to the second inequality in (
5.86
), and the third inequality follows from (
5.30
), and the last inequality is due to (
5.6
). Together with (
5.6
), this implies that

\rVert\nabla w_{t}(0)\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}+\int_{0}^{T}\rVert w(s)\rVert_{H^{2}}^{2}+\rVert u(s)\rVert_{H^{1}}^{2}ds\leq_{C}\delta.

Thus, (
5.85
) reads as

\displaystyle\frac{2}{t}\int_{t/2}^{t}\rVert\nabla w_{t}\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}ds\leq_{C}\frac{\delta}{t^{3}}.

(5.87)

Noting that (
5.80
) and (
5.71
) imply

5.103
) must imply

M\epsilon^{2}\leq C\epsilon^{2}M\left(\frac{(\log M)^{C}}{M}+(\log\log M)^{-1/10}\right),

in other words,

1\leq C\left((\log\log M)^{-1/10}+\frac{(\log M)^{C}}{M}\right).

Since
C
is a constant that depends on only the steady state
\rho_{s}
, we can choose
M
large enough so that this inequality leads to a contradiction. This proves the claim (
5.102
) and finishes the proof.

Appendix A
ODE estimates

In the appendix, we collect elementary ODE estimates.

Lemma A.1
.

Let
f,g,\alpha:[0,T]\mapsto\mathbb{R}^{+}
be smooth functions for some
T>0
. Suppose it holds that

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}(f(t)+g(t))\leq-\left(f(t)^{2}\alpha(t)^{-1}+g(t)\right),\text{ for $t\in[0,T]$.}

(A.1)

Then we have

f(T)+g(T)\leq C\frac{A}{T^{2}},\text{ where $A:=\max\left\{\int_{0}^{T}\alpha(s)ds,f(0)+g(0)\right\}$}.

Proof.

Let us decompose the interval
[0,T]
as

[0,T]=:I\cup J,\text{ where }I:=\left\{t\in[0,T]:g(t)\leq\alpha(t)\right\},\quad J:=\left\{t\in[0,T]:g(t)>\alpha(t)\right\}.

Since
f,g,\alpha
are smooth functions, we can decompose each of
I_{1},I_{2}
into a union of disjoint intervals:

I=\cup_{i\in\mathbb{N}\cup\left\{0\right\}}[t_{2i},t_{2i+1}]=:\cup_{i\in\mathbb{N}\cup\left\{0\right\}}I_{i},\quad J=\cup_{i\in\mathbb{N}\cup\left\{0\right\}}[t_{2i+1},t_{2i+2}]=:\cup_{i\in\mathbb{N}\cup\left\{0\right\}}J_{i},\text{ for $t_{i}\leq t_{i+1}$.}

Let us consider two cases, either
|I|\geq|J|
or
|I|\leq|J|
. We suppose first
|I|\geq|J|
. On each
I_{i}
, we have

g(t)^{2}\alpha^{-1}=g(t)\times(g(t)\alpha(t)^{-1})\leq g(t),\text{ for $t\in I$.}

Hence, the differential inequality (
A.1
) reads as

\frac{d}{dt}(f(t)+g(t))\leq-(f(t)^{2}\alpha(t)^{-1}+g(t)^{2}\alpha(t)^{-1})=-C\left(f(t)+g(t)\right)^{2}\alpha(t)^{-1},\text{ for $t\in I$.}

Denoting
h(t):=f(t)+g(t)
, we have

\frac{d}{dt}h(t)\leq-Ch(t)^{2}\alpha^{-1}(t),\text{ for $t\in I$.}

Integrating the both sides over
t\in I_{i}
, we get

\frac{1}{h(t_{2i+1})}-\frac{1}{h(t_{2i})}\geq C\int_{t_{2i}}^{t_{2i+1}}\alpha(s)^{-1}ds.

Summing the both sides over
i
and using that
\frac{1}{h(t_{2i+2})}-\frac{1}{h(t_{2i+1})}\geq 0
for all
i
due to the monotonicity of
t\mapsto h
, we get, denoting
t_{I,\infty}:=\sup_{t\in I}t\leq T
,

\frac{1}{h({t_{I,\infty}})}-\frac{1}{h(t_{0})}\geq\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\int_{I_{i}}\alpha(s)^{-1}ds=\int_{I}\alpha(s)^{-1}ds\geq\frac{|I|^{2}}{\int_{I}\alpha(s)ds},

where the last inequality follows from Jensen’s inequality. Again since
h
is nonnegative and monotone decreasing, the left-hand side satisfies

\frac{1}{h({t_{I,\infty}})}-\frac{1}{h(t_{0})}\leq\frac{1}{h(t_{I,\infty})}\leq\frac{1}{h(T)}.

On the other hand, the assumption that
|I|\geq|J|
implies
|I|\geq\frac{1}{2}T
, therefore,

\frac{|I|^{2}}{\int_{I}\alpha(s)ds}\geq\frac{CT^{2}}{\int_{I}\alpha(s)ds}\geq\frac{CT^{2}}{\int_{0}^{T}\alpha(s)ds}.

Thus we arrive at
\frac{1}{h(T)}\geq\frac{CT^{2}}{\int_{0}^{T}\alpha(s)ds}
, that is,

\displaystyle{h(T)}\leq\frac{\int_{0}^{T}\alpha(s)ds}{CT^{2}},\text{ if $|I|\geq|J|$.}

(A.2)

Now, let us consider the case where
|I|\leq|J|
. On the interval
J_{i}
, Young’s inequality yields

f(t)=f(t)\alpha(t)^{-1/2}\alpha^{1/2}\leq\frac{1}{2}f(t)^{2}\alpha^{-1}(t)+\frac{1}{2}\alpha(s)\leq\frac{1}{2}f(t)^{2}\alpha(t)^{-1}+\frac{1}{4}g(t),\text{ for $t\in J$.}

Therefore,

\frac{1}{2}f(t)^{2}\alpha(t)^{-1}+g(t)\geq_{C}f(t)+g(t),\text{ for $t\in J$.}

With this, the differential inequality (
A.1
) reads as

\frac{d}{dt}h(t)\leq-Ch(t),\text{ for $t\in J$.}

Solving this, we obtain
h(t)\leq h(t_{2i+1})e^{-C(t-t_{2i+1})}
for
t\in J_{i}
. In other words, we get

\frac{h(t_{2i+2})}{h(t_{2i+1})}\leq e^{-C|J_{i}|},\text{ for all $i$.}

Since
t\mapsto h(t)
is monotone decreasing, we have
\frac{h(t_{2i})}{h(t_{2i-1})}\leq 1
for
i\geq 1
, hence, denoting
t_{J,\infty}:=\sup_{t\in J}\leq T
. we obtain

\frac{h(T)}{h(0)}\leq\frac{h(T)}{h({t_{1}})}\leq\Pi_{i=0}^{\infty}\frac{h({t_{2i+2}})}{h(t_{2i+1})}\leq\Pi_{i=0}^{\infty}e^{-C|J_{i}|}\leq e^{-C|J|}\leq e^{-CT},

where the last inequality follows from the assumption that
|J|\geq|I|
, thus
|J|\geq\frac{1}{2}|T|
. Thus we arrive at

h(T)\leq h(0)e^{-CT}\leq\frac{Ch(0)}{T^{2}},\text{ if $|J|\geq|I|$.}

Combining this with (
A.2
), we obtain the desired result.
∎

Lemma A.2
.

Let
f,g,h:[0,T]\mapsto\mathbb{R}^{+}
be smooth functions for some
T>0
. Suppose it holds that

\frac{d}{dt}f(t)\leq-g(t)+h(t),\text{ for $t\in[0,T]$},

and
f,h
satisfy decay conditions:

\frac{2}{t}\int_{t/2}^{t}f(s)ds\leq\frac{A}{t^{n-1}},\quad\frac{2}{t}\int_{t/2}^{t}h(s)ds\leq\frac{A}{t^{n}},\text{ for some $n\in\mathbb{N},A>0$ and for all $t\in[0,T]$.}

Then,

\frac{2}{t}\int_{t/2}^{t}g(s)ds\leq\frac{A}{t^{n}},\text{ for all $t\in[0,T]$.}

Proof.

Integrating the differential inequality in time over
[s,t]
for
0<s<t<T
, we get
f(t)-f(s)+\int_{s}^{t}g(u)du\leq\int_{s}^{t}h(u)du
, thus

\int_{s}^{t}g(u)du\leq\int_{s}^{t}h(u)du+f(s).

Integrating the both sides in
s
over
[t/4,t/2]
, we see that the left-hand side can be estimated as

\displaystyle\int_{t/4}^{t/2}\int_{s}^{t}g(u)duds

\displaystyle=\int_{t/4}^{t/2}\int_{0}^{t}g(u)1_{u\geq s}duds=\int_{0}^{t}g(u)\int_{t/4}^{t/2}1_{u\geq s}duds\geq\int_{0}^{t}g(u)\int_{t/4}^{t/2}1_{u\geq t/2}dsdu

\displaystyle=\int_{t/2}^{t}g(u)\frac{t}{4}du.

For the right-hand side estimate, we compute

\int_{t/4}^{t/2}\int_{s}^{t}h(u)duds\leq\int_{t/4}^{t/2}\int_{t/4}^{t}h(u)duds=\frac{t}{4}\left(\int_{t/2}^{t}h(u)du+\int_{t/4}^{t/2}h(u)du\right)\leq Ct\frac{A}{t^{n-1}}\leq\frac{CA}{t^{n-2}},

where we used the assumption on
h
for the second last inequality. Also the following estimate is trivial:

\int_{t/4}^{t/2}f(s)ds\leq\frac{CA}{t^{n-2}}.

Putting them together, we arrive at

t\int_{t/2}^{t}g(u)du\leq\frac{CA}{t^{n-2}}.

Dividing the both sides by
t^{2}
, we obtain the desired result.
∎

Lemma A.3
.

Let
f,g:[0,T]\mapsto\mathbb{R}^{+}
be smooth functions for some
T>0
. Suppose it holds that

\frac{d}{dt}f(t)\leq-f(t)+g(t),\text{ for $t\in[0,T]$},

and
g
satisfies a decay condition:

\frac{2}{t}\int_{t/2}^{t}g(s)ds\leq\frac{A}{t^{n}},\text{ for some $n\in\mathbb{N},A>0$ and for all $t\in[0,T]$.}

Then,

\frac{2}{t}\int_{t/2}^{t}f(s)ds\leq\frac{A+B}{t^{n}},\text{ for all $t\in[0,T]$, where $B:=f(0)+\int_{0}^{T}g(s)ds$.}

Proof.

Let
F
be a solution to

\frac{d}{dt}F(t)=-F(t)+g(t),\ F(0)=f(0).

The comparison principle tells us that

f(t)\leq F(t),\text{ for all $t\in[0,T]$.}

On the other hand, the method of integrating factor tells us

\displaystyle F(t)

\displaystyle=e^{-t}F(0)+\int_{0}^{t}g(s)e^{s-t}ds=e^{-t}f(0)+\int_{0}^{t/2}g(s)e^{s-t}ds+\int_{t/2}^{t}g(s)e^{s-t}ds

\displaystyle\leq e^{-t}f(0)+e^{-t/2}\int_{0}^{t/2}g(s)ds+\int_{t/2}^{t}g(s)ds

\displaystyle\leq e^{-t/2}\left(f(0)+\int_{0}^{t/2}g(s)ds\right)+\int_{t/2}^{t}g(s)ds.

Denoting

B:=f(0)+\int_{0}^{t}g(s)ds,

and using the decay assumption on
g
, we obtain

F(t)\leq e^{-t/2}B+\frac{A}{t^{n-1}}\leq\frac{A+B}{t^{n-1}}.

Hence,
f(t)\leq\frac{A+B}{t^{n-1}}
, and thus
\frac{2}{t}\int_{t/2}^{t}f(s)ds\leq_{C}\frac{A+B}{t^{n-1}}
. Applying Lemma
A.2
, we arrive at

\frac{2}{t}\int_{t/2}^{t}f(s)ds\leq\frac{A+B}{t^{n}},\text{ for all $t\in[0,T]$.}

∎
