Fractal catastrophes
J. Meibohm, K. Gustavsson, J. Bec, B. Mehlig

Abstract
We analyse the spatial inhomogeneities (‘spatial clustering’) in the distribution of particles accelerated by a force that changes randomly in space and time. To quantify spatial clustering, the phase-space dynamics of the particles must be projected to configuration space. Folds of a smooth phase-space manifold give rise to catastrophes (‘caustics’) in this projection. When the inertial particle dynamics is damped by friction, however, the phase-space manifold converges towards a fractal attractor. It is believed that caustics increase spatial clustering also in this case, but a quantitative theory is missing. We solve this problem by determining how projection affects the distribution of finite-time Lyapunov exponents. Applying our method in one spatial dimension we find that caustics arising from the projection of a dynamical fractal attractor (‘fractal catastrophes’) make a distinct and universal contribution to the distribution of spatial finite-time Lyapunov exponents. Our results explain a projection formula for the spatial fractal correlation dimension, and how a fluctuation relation for the distribution of finite-time Lyapunov exponents for white-in-time Gaussian force fields breaks upon projection. We explore the implications of our results for heavy particles in turbulence, and for wave propagation in random media.

2
Problem formulation and background

2.1
Phase-space finite-time Lyapunov exponents

Consider the dynamics of the position
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{t}
and momentum
{\boldsymbol{p}}_{t}
of a particle of mass
m
in a random force field
\boldsymbol{f}(\boldsymbol{x},t)
in
d
spatial dimensions,

\displaystyle\tfrac{\text{d}}{\text{d}t}{\boldsymbol{x}}_{t}=\frac{\boldsymbol{p}_{t}}{m}\,,\quad\tfrac{\text{d}}{\text{d}t}{\boldsymbol{p}}_{t}=-\gamma\boldsymbol{p}_{t}+\boldsymbol{f}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t},t)\,.

(1)

Here
\gamma
is a damping coefficient. Equation (
1
) is a widely used model for the dynamics of small, heavy particles in turbulence
[
7
]
. In this case the damping is due to viscous friction, and the random force field
\boldsymbol{f}(\boldsymbol{x},t)
represents the turbulent fluid-velocity field
\boldsymbol{u}(\boldsymbol{x},t)
.

In what follows we use the dimensionless units
t^{\prime}=t\sqrt{f_{0}/(m\ell)}
,
\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}=\boldsymbol{x}/\ell
,
\boldsymbol{p}^{\prime}=\boldsymbol{p}/\sqrt{m\ell f_{0}}
, and
\boldsymbol{f}^{\prime}=\boldsymbol{f}/f_{0}
, where
\ell
and
f_{0}
are the correlation length and standard deviation of the force
\boldsymbol{f}
. We drop the primes for notational convenience, and write:

\displaystyle\tfrac{\text{d}}{\text{d}t}{\boldsymbol{x}}_{t}={\boldsymbol{p}_{t}}\,,\quad\tfrac{\text{d}}{\text{d}t}{\boldsymbol{p}}_{t}=-\zeta\boldsymbol{p}_{t}+\boldsymbol{f}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t},t)\,.

(2)

Here
\zeta=\gamma\sqrt{m\ell/f_{0}}
is a dimensionless damping coefficient. To describe fractal clustering in phase space, we analyse the dynamics of a small neighbourhood of phase-space trajectories around a reference trajectory
({\boldsymbol{x}}_{t},{\boldsymbol{p}}_{t})
. The phase-space separation between
({\boldsymbol{x}}_{t},{\boldsymbol{p}}_{t})
and a neighbouring trajectory
({\boldsymbol{x}}_{t}^{\prime},{\boldsymbol{p}}_{t}^{\prime})
is denoted by
\boldsymbol{R}_{t}=(\delta{\boldsymbol{x}}_{t},\delta{\boldsymbol{p}}_{t})\equiv({\boldsymbol{x}_{t}}^{\prime}-{\boldsymbol{x}_{t}},{\boldsymbol{p}_{t}}^{\prime}-{\boldsymbol{p}_{t}})
. We consider separations at large times
t
, yet small enough so that the separations are always much smaller than the correlation length of the forcing,
|\delta\boldsymbol{x}_{t}|\ll 1
. Then we can linearise the force field around
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{t}
to obtain

\displaystyle\tfrac{\text{d}}{\text{d}t}\delta{\boldsymbol{x}}_{t}=\delta{\boldsymbol{p}}_{t}\,,\quad\tfrac{\text{d}}{\text{d}t}\delta{\boldsymbol{p}}_{t}=-\zeta\delta{\boldsymbol{p}}_{t}+\mathbb{F}({\boldsymbol{x}}_{t},t)\delta{\boldsymbol{x}}_{t}\,,

(3)

where
{\symAMSb F}
is the random force-gradient matrix with elements
F_{ij}=\partial_{j}f_{i}
. The phase-space dynamics in the vicinity of a trajectory
(\boldsymbol{x}_{t},\boldsymbol{p}_{t})
becomes

\displaystyle\tfrac{\text{d}}{\text{d}t}{\boldsymbol{R}}_{t}={\mathbb{A}}(t)\,{\boldsymbol{R}}_{t}\,,\ {\mathbb{A}}(t)=\begin{pmatrix}0&\phantom{-}{\symAMSb I}_{d\times d}\\
\mathbb{F}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t},t)&-\zeta\,{\symAMSb I}_{d\times d}&\end{pmatrix}\,,

(4)

where
{\symAMSb I}_{d\times d}
is the
d\times d
unit matrix. The solution of Eq. (
4
) is expressed in terms of the Green function
\mathbb{J}(t)
by

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{R}}_{t}=\mathbb{J}(t)\,{\boldsymbol{R}}_{t=0}\quad\mbox{with}\quad\mathbb{J}(t)=\mathscr{T}\!\exp\int_{0}^{t}\text{d}t^{\prime}\mathbb{A}(t^{\prime})\,.

(5)

Here
\mathscr{T}\!\exp
denotes the time-ordered exponential evaluated along
({\boldsymbol{x}}_{t},{\boldsymbol{p}}_{t})
. Writing
\mathbb{J}=\mathbb{V}\,\mathbb{R}
, we decompose
\mathbb{J}
into a rotation
\mathbb{R}
and a stretch tensor
\mathbb{V}
with positive and real eigenvalues
(\text{e}^{\sigma^{(1)}_{t}t},\ldots,\text{e}^{\sigma^{(2d)}_{t}t})
. The exponents
({\sigma^{(1)}_{t}},\ldots,{\sigma^{(2d)}_{t}})
are the phase-space FTLEs we intend to calculate. They can be computed as follows. One defines the left Cauchy-Green tensor
\mathbb{B}\equiv\mathbb{J}{\mathbb{J}}^{\sf T}=\mathbb{V}^{2}
with eigenvalues
(\text{e}^{2\sigma^{(1)}_{t}t},\ldots,\text{e}^{2\sigma^{(2d)}_{t}t})
. Analysing
\mathbb{B}
instead of
\mathbb{V}
is convenient since
\mathbb{B}
obeys a closed equation,
\mathbb{\dot{B}}=\mathbb{B}\mathbb{A}^{\sf T}+\mathbb{A}\mathbb{B}
, while
\mathbb{V}
does not. As shown in Ref.
[
41
]
this allows to derive evolution equations for the FTLEs and for the orthogonal matrix
\mathbb{O}
that diagonalises
\mathbb{B}
. The elements of
\mathbb{O}
can be written as
\mathbb{O}_{ij}=[\boldsymbol{e}^{(i)}_{t}]_{j}
, where
\boldsymbol{e}^{(i)}_{t}
is the eigenvectors of
{\symAMSb B}
corresponding to
\sigma^{(i)}_{t}
. For long enough times, and given a non-degenerate spectrum of Lyapunov exponents, the dynamics of the eigenvectors
\boldsymbol{e}^{(i)}_{t}
decouples from that of the FTLEs, leading to a closed set of stochastic equations for
\boldsymbol{e}^{(i)}_{t}

[
41
]
:

\displaystyle\tfrac{\text{d}}{\text{d}t}\boldsymbol{e}^{(i)}_{t}=\mathbb{A}(t){\boldsymbol{e}}^{(i)}_{t}-({\boldsymbol{e}}^{(i)}_{t}\cdot\mathbb{A}(t){\boldsymbol{e}}^{(i)}_{t}){\boldsymbol{e}}^{(i)}_{t}-\sum_{j<i}^{2d}[{\boldsymbol{e}}^{(j)}_{t}\cdot(\mathbb{A}(t)+\mathbb{A}(t)^{\sf T}){\boldsymbol{e}}^{(i)}_{t}]{\boldsymbol{e}}^{(j)}_{t}\,.

(6)

The phase-space FTLEs are obtained as integrals over
\boldsymbol{e}^{(i)}_{t}\cdot\mathbb{A}\boldsymbol{e}^{(i)}_{t}
:

\displaystyle\sigma^{(i)}_{t}=\frac{1}{t}\int^{t}_{0}\!\!\text{d}{t^{\prime}}\,\boldsymbol{e}^{(i)}_{t^{\prime}}\cdot\mathbb{A}(t)\boldsymbol{e}^{(i)}_{t^{\prime}}\,,

(7)

It is convenient to arrange the FTLEs in non-increasing order
[
42
]
:

\displaystyle\sigma^{(1)}_{t}\geq\sigma^{(2)}_{t}\geq\cdots\geq\sigma^{(2d)}_{t}\,.

(8a)

Since the trace of
{\symAMSb A}
is constant,
\text{tr}\,\mathbb{A}=-\zeta d
, the phase-space FTLEs obey the sum rule

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{2d}\sigma^{(i)}_{t}=-\zeta d\,.

(8b)

For ergodic dynamics, the FTLEs have definite limits,

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to\infty}\sigma^{(i)}_{t}=\lambda_{i}\,,

(9)

the Lyapunov exponents
[
68
]
. In the limit
t\to\infty
, their cumulative sums,
\sum^{n}_{i=1}\lambda_{i}
, describe the expansion or contraction rates of
n
-dimensional phase-space volumes spanned by
n+1
nearby particles. The distributions of
\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sigma^{(i)}_{t}
, by contrast, describe transient fluctuations of the magnitudes of phase-space (sub-)volumes.

2.2
Large-deviation principle

At large but finite times
t
, the phase-space FTLEs obey a large-deviation principle
[
37
,
39
]
. Their joint density has the large-deviation form
[
41
,
43
,
42
]

\displaystyle P\left(\sigma^{(1)}_{{t}}=s_{1},\ldots,\sigma^{(2d)}_{{t}}=s_{2d}\right){\propto}1_{s_{1}\geq\ldots\geq s_{2d}}\,\delta\left(\sum_{j=1}^{2d}s_{j}+\zeta d\right)\,\text{e}^{-tI(\boldsymbol{s})}\,,

(10)

with rate function
I(\boldsymbol{s})
, and
\boldsymbol{s}=(s_{1},\ldots,s_{2d-1})
. The indicator function
1_{x}
ensures the ordering of FTLEs, while the Dirac delta function
\delta(x)
enforces the constraint Eq. (
8b
). Instead of calculating
I(\boldsymbol{s})
directly, it is often easier to compute the scaled cumulant-generating function (SCGF)
[
37
,
39
,
69
,
70
]

\displaystyle\Lambda(\boldsymbol{k})=\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{1}{t}\log\left\langle\exp\left[t\sum_{i=1}^{2d-1}k_{i}\sigma^{(i)}_{t}\right]\right\rangle\,,

(11)

where
\boldsymbol{k}=(k_{1},\ldots,k_{2d-1})
. If
\Lambda(\boldsymbol{k})
exists and provided that it is differentiable with respect to
\boldsymbol{k}
, then
I(\boldsymbol{s})
is given by the Legendre transform

[
71
,
37
]
,

\displaystyle I({\boldsymbol{s}})=\sup_{\boldsymbol{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{2d-1}}\left\{{\boldsymbol{k}}\cdot{\boldsymbol{s}}-\Lambda(\boldsymbol{k})\right\}\,.

(12)

In Section
4
we derive stochastic differential equations that allow, in principle, to compute the large-deviation statistics of phase-space FTLEs for a one-dimensional random force field
f(x,t)
. In Section
7
we show how to solve these equations explicitly, for white-in-time Gaussian force fields.

2.3
Catastrophes

Catastrophe theory
[
72
,
73
,
74
]
is a branch in mathematics that concerns the description and classification of singularities in dynamical systems. The theory explains, for instance, the sensitive parameter dependence of steady-state solutions of differential equations.
Within the theory, singularities arising from folds of a manifold of steady-state solutions over parameter space are categorised into so-called normal forms
[
74
]
. An important property of a catastrophe is its codimension, given by the dimension of the space under consideration, minus the dimensionality of the singularity. Catastrophes of codimension one are called cuspoids (including fold and cusp catastrophes). Although cuspoid catastrophes are the most common ones, catastrophes of higher codimension play an important role in optics
[
49
,
50
]
.
In optics, catastrophes lead to caustics, singularities in the light intensity due to partial focusing. Caustics arise from the projection of folds of a smooth phase-space manifold onto configuration space.

Fig.
1
illustrates that similar folds, albeit of a
fractal
attractor, are created by the phase-space dynamics (
2
). In Section
3
we show how these fractal catastrophes arise from the spatial projection to configuration space. In Section
5
we demonstrate how they affect the distribution of spatial FTLEs.

2.4
Fractal attractors

When the dynamics is dissipative (
\zeta>0)
, the steady-state phase-space attractor is fractal. This means that the cumulative probability distribution of phase-space separations
R_{t}=|\boldsymbol{R}_{t}|=\sqrt{|\delta\boldsymbol{x}_{t}|^{2}+{|\delta\boldsymbol{p}_{t}|^{2}}}
exhibits a power law

\displaystyle P(R_{t}\leq r)\sim r^{D_{2}}\quad{\mbox{for}\quad r\ll 1}\,.

(13)

The exponent
D_{2}
defines the phase-space correlation dimension. As can be seen from Eq. (
13
), the phase-space correlation dimension measures the probability of finding two particles within a distance
r
in phase-space. For a homogeneous distribution of particles, this probability scales as
\sim r^{2d}
. For fractal particle distributions, on the other hand, the probability scales as
\sim r^{D_{2}}
with
D_{2}<2d
.

The correlation dimension is not the only quantity that measures the fractal properties of particle distributions. Often the Kaplan-Yorke dimension
D_{\text{KY}}

[
75
]
is used to characterise the fractal nature of attractors, because
D_{\text{KY}}
is defined in terms of the Lyapunov exponents
\lambda_{i}
. The Kaplan-Yorke dimension
D_{\text{KY}}
is thus insensitive to the transient fluctuations determined by
\sigma_{t}^{(i)}
. For generic non-linear dynamics,
D_{\text{KY}}
equals the information dimension
D_{1}
, but counterexamples can be constructed
[
76
]
.

For the phase-space dynamics (
1
) that generate the dynamical fractal attractor illustrated in Fig.
1
, the distribution of phase-space FTLEs determines not only
D_{1}
and
D_{2}
but the whole spectrum of fractal phase-space dimensions
D_{q}

[
34
,
35
,
36
]
for any value of
q
. For the analysis of
D_{q}
, one considers the moments of the probability
{\mathscr{M}}_{r}
contained in a small phase-space ball of radius
r
around
(\boldsymbol{x}_{t},\boldsymbol{p}_{t})
. The
r
-scaling of the
n
-th moment of
\mathscr{M}_{r}
measures the probability of finding
n+1
particles within a distance
r
, thus generalising Eq. (
13
). The
r
-scaling of
\langle{\mathscr{M}}_{r}^{n}\rangle
is given by the exponent
\xi_{n}

[
34
,
77
]
,

\displaystyle\langle{\mathscr{M}}_{r}^{n}\rangle\sim r^{\xi_{n}}\quad\text{for}\quad r\ll 1\,.

(14)

Note in particular that
\langle\mathscr{M}_{r}\rangle=P(R_{t}\leq r)
, so that
\xi_{1}=D_{2}
. More generally, the singularity exponents
\xi_{n}
are, by definition, related to the fractal dimensions
D_{q}
by
\xi_{n}=nD_{n+1}
.
If trajectories do not cross, the local phase-space mass is conserved. In this case the singularity exponents can be computed from the rate function of FTLEs
[
34
,
35
,
36
]
. We show in Sections
6
and
7
how to obtain the fractal phase-space dimensions
D_{q}
in this way.

The cumulative probability distribution of
spatial
separations
|\delta x_{t}|
has the form

\displaystyle P(|\delta x_{t}|\leq r){\propto}r^{\hat{D}_{2}}\quad\mbox{for}\quad r\ll 1\,,

(15)

where
\hat{D}_{2}
is the spatial correlation dimension
[
7
]
1
1

1

In what follows, we denote all spatially projected quantities by a hat.
. The spatial correlation dimension
\hat{D}_{2}
measures the probability of finding two particles within a spatial distance
r
. This dimension therefore plays an important role for particle interactions that require spatial proximity.

More generally, spatial clustering is characterised by the spatial fractal dimensions
\hat{D}_{q}
which describe how the particles fill out configuration space. There is no general formula that connects a spatial fractal dimension
\hat{D}_{q}
to its phase-space counterpart
D_{q}
. However, it was conjectured on the basis of numerical investigations of spatial clustering
[
77
,
53
,
7
]
that the correlation dimension obeys a projection formula of the form

\displaystyle\hat{D}_{2}=\min\{D_{2},d\}\,.

(16)

For typical projections of generic attractors this relation can be proven to hold for
\hat{D}_{q}
for
0\leq q\leq 2

[
78
,
79
,
80
]
. For
q>2
one can show that
\hat{D}_{q}\leq\min\{D_{q},d\}

[
80
]
. But an important point is that the dynamics (
2
) is not isotropic in
2d
-dimensional phase space. Therefore, it is not at all clear whether the projection from phase-space to configuration space is typical, or not.
Using our results for the distribution of the spatial FTLE for one spatial dimension, we show in Section
6
that the spatial correlation dimension
\hat{D}_{2}
obeys the projection formula (
16
). More importantly, our theory explains that
\hat{D}_{2}
saturates at unity (for
d=1
) because of caustics. However, this does not necessarily mean
that caustics give rise to a spatially uniform distribution of particles, because, possibly,
\hat{D}_{q}<1
for

.

3
Projection to configuration space

In this Section we explain how to project the distribution of phase-space FTLEs to configuration space, taking into account the effect of catastrophes. As mentioned above, the phase-space FTLEs describe how (sub-)volumes evolve in phase space. To understand how they project to configuration space, consider a small
n
-dimensional (
n\leq d
) phase-space volume around a phase-space trajectory
(\boldsymbol{x}_{t},\boldsymbol{p}_{t})
. Assume that the initial volume lies entirely within configuration space, so that for any vector
\boldsymbol{w}
in that volume we have
\boldsymbol{w}_{{t=0}}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}w_{j}\boldsymbol{\hat{e}}^{(j)}
, where
\boldsymbol{\hat{e}}^{(1)},\ldots,\boldsymbol{\hat{e}}^{(n)}
is the Cartesian basis in configuration space. At long times the volume aligns with the
n
eigenvectors

\boldsymbol{e}^{(1)}_{t},\ldots,\boldsymbol{e}^{(n)}_{t}
of
{\symAMSb B}
that correspond to the largest FTLEs,
\sigma_{t}^{(1)},\ldots,\sigma_{t}^{(n)}
. As a consequence, the projection of
\boldsymbol{w}_{t}
to configuration space evolves as

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\hat{w}}_{t}=\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\boldsymbol{\hat{e}}^{(i)}W^{(n)}_{ij}{\boldsymbol{e}_{t}^{(j)}\cdot\boldsymbol{w}_{t=0}}\quad\mbox{with}\quad W^{(n)}_{ij}=\text{e}^{\sigma^{(j)}_{t}t}\,\boldsymbol{\hat{e}}^{(i)}\cdot\boldsymbol{e}_{t}^{(j)}\,.

(17)

The absolute value of the determinant of
{\symAMSb W}_{t}^{(n)}
,

\displaystyle|\det\mathbb{W}_{t}^{(n)}|=\text{e}^{t\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sigma^{(i)}_{t}}|\det\mathbb{O}_{t}^{(n)}|\,,

(18)

determines how
n
-dimensional
spatial
volumes expand and contract. Here
\mathbb{O}_{t}^{(n)}
is the
n\times n
sub-matrix of
\mathbb{O}_{t}
corresponding to
\boldsymbol{e}^{(1)}_{t},\ldots,\boldsymbol{e}^{(n)}_{t}
. Eq. (
18
) shows that
|\det\mathbb{W}_{t}^{(n)}|
factorises into a phase-space volume factor,
\text{e}^{t\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sigma^{(i)}_{t}}
, and a spatial volume factor,
|\det\mathbb{O}_{t}^{(n)}|
. Since
0\leq|\det\mathbb{O}_{t}^{(n)}|\leq 1
, we can write
|\det\mathbb{O}_{t}^{(n)}|=\cos\alpha^{(n)}_{t}
and assign periodic boundary conditions to the angle
\alpha^{(n)}_{t}
in
[-\pi/2,\pi/2)
. Eq. (
18
) allows us to express the spatial FTLEs in terms of the phase-space FTLEs as

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\hat{\sigma}^{(i)}_{t}\equiv\frac{1}{t}\log\Bigg|\frac{\det\mathbb{W}_{t}^{(n)}}{\det\mathbb{W}_{{t=0}}^{(n)}}\Bigg|=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sigma^{(i)}_{t}+\frac{1}{t}\log\left(\frac{\cos\alpha^{(n)}_{t}}{\cos\alpha^{(n)}_{{t=0}}}\right)\,.

(19)

Using Eq. (
7
) we obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\hat{\sigma}^{(i)}_{t}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{t}\int^{t}_{0}\!\!\text{d}{t^{\prime}}\,\,\sum_{i=1}^{n}\boldsymbol{e}^{(i)}_{t}\cdot\mathbb{A}(t)\boldsymbol{e}^{(i)}_{t}-\frac{1}{t}\int_{0}^{t}\!\text{d}\alpha^{(n)}_{t}\tan\alpha^{(n)}_{t}\,,

(20a)

\displaystyle\tfrac{\text{d}}{\text{d}t}\alpha_{t}^{(n)}

\displaystyle=-\left[\tan\alpha_{t}^{(n)}\right]^{-1}\Tr\left(\tfrac{\text{d}}{\text{d}t}\mathbb{O}^{(n)}_{t}\Big[\mathbb{O}^{(n)}_{t}\Big]^{-1}\right)\,.

(20b)

Here
\Tr(\ldots)
denotes the trace of the matrix. Equations (
20
) describe how folds of the phase-space manifold (catastrophes) affect the spatial FTLEs.
A catastrophe of spatial codimension
d-n+1
or larger occurs in the spatial subspace spanned by
\boldsymbol{\hat{e}}^{(1)},\ldots,\boldsymbol{\hat{e}}^{(n)}
when
\cos\alpha^{(n)}_{t}\to 0
. We denote by
J^{(n)}
the rate at which
\cos\alpha^{(n)}_{t}\to 0
, i.e.,
\alpha^{(n)}_{t}
transitions from
-\pi/2
to
[\pi/2]^{+}
. Most importantly,
J\equiv J^{(d)}
is the rate of formation of catastrophes of codimension one or larger, often called simply ‘rate of caustic formation’
[
45
,
47
]
. Note that the second integral in Eq. (
20a
) diverges as
\alpha_{t}^{(n)}\to-\pi/2
. The time derivative
\tfrac{\text{d}}{\text{d}t}\alpha_{t}^{(n)}
, however, remains finite because the factor
\big[\tan\alpha_{t}^{(n)}\big]^{-1}
in Eq. (
20b
) cancels the divergence of
\big[\mathbb{O}^{(n)}_{t}\big]^{-1}\propto\big[\cos\alpha^{(n)}_{t}\big]^{-1}
.

In the next Sections we formulate the equations of motion (
20
) explicitly for
d=n=1
. In this case caustics occur at isolated points in configuration space (see Fig.
1
).

4
FTLEs in two-dimensional phase space

We now apply the methods outlined in the Section

in one spatial dimension. For

there are two eigenvectors
\boldsymbol{e}^{(1)}_{t}
and
\boldsymbol{e}^{(2)}_{t}
which can be parametrised by a single angle

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{e}^{(1)}_{t}=\begin{pmatrix}\cos\alpha_{t}\\
\sin\alpha_{t}\end{pmatrix}\,,\qquad\boldsymbol{e}^{(2)}_{t}=\begin{pmatrix}-\sin\alpha_{t}\\
\cos\alpha_{t}\end{pmatrix}\,.

(21)

The constraint (
8b
) implies that there is only one independent phase-space FTLE, which we take to be
\sigma^{(1)}_{t}
. In order to derive the dynamics for
\alpha_{t}
and
\sigma_{t}^{(1)}
, we start with Eqs. (
6
) and (
7
):

\displaystyle\tfrac{\text{d}}{\text{d}t}(t\sigma^{(1)}_{t})=\boldsymbol{e}^{(1)}_{t}\cdot\mathbb{A}\boldsymbol{e}^{(1)}_{t}\quad\mbox{and}\quad\tfrac{\text{d}}{\text{d}t}\boldsymbol{e}^{(1)}_{t}=\mathbb{A}\boldsymbol{e}^{(1)}_{t}-(\boldsymbol{e}^{(1)}_{t}\cdot\mathbb{A}\boldsymbol{e}^{(1)}_{t})\boldsymbol{e}^{(1)}_{t}\,.

(22)

We use the parametrisation (
21
) together with Eqs. (
6
) and (
7
) to obtain equations of motion for
\alpha_{t}
and
\sigma^{(1)}_{t}
:

\displaystyle\tfrac{\text{d}}{\text{d}t}\alpha_{t}

\displaystyle=-\sin\alpha_{t}(\sin\alpha_{t}+\zeta\cos\alpha_{t})\!+\!F_{t}\cos^{2}\alpha_{t}\,,

(23a)

\displaystyle\sigma^{(1)}_{t}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{t}\int_{0}^{t}\text{d}t^{\prime}\tan\alpha_{t^{\prime}}+\frac{1}{t}\int_{0}^{t}\text{d}\alpha_{t^{\prime}}\,\tan\alpha_{t^{\prime}}\,,

(23b)

with
F_{t}\equiv F(x_{t},t)
. As explained in the previous Section, caustics occur as
\alpha_{t}
transitions from
-\pi/2
to
[\pi/2]^{+}
. From Eq. (
23a
) we see that
\text{d}\alpha_{t}|_{\alpha_{t}=-\pi/2}=-\text{d}t
, which implies that the transition from
-\pi/2
to
[\pi/2]^{+}
is deterministic with angular velocity
-1
. At the point
\alpha_{t}=-\pi/2
, both integrands in Eq. (

) diverge. However, because
\text{d}\alpha_{t}|_{\alpha_{t}=-\pi/2}=-\text{d}t
the divergencies in Eq. (
23b
) cancel, so that the phase-space FTLE
\sigma^{(1)}_{t}
remains finite for all times. Equations (
23
) admit the following interpretation, illustrated in Fig.
2
: An initial two-dimensional phase-space disc is deformed by
\text{e}^{t\sigma^{(1)}_{t}}
along
\boldsymbol{e}^{(1)}_{t}
and by
\text{e}^{t\sigma^{(2)}_{t}}
along
\boldsymbol{e}^{(2)}_{t}
. The initial disc is thus squeezed into an ellipse with decreased phase-space volume
\mathscr{V}_{t}\sim\mathscr{V}_{t=0}\text{e}^{-\zeta t}
, due to the dissipative nature of the dynamics. At the same time, the eigensystem of
\mathbb{B}
rotates by the angle
\alpha_{t}
.

at the boundaries of the interval

The system (
23
) is more conveniently written in terms of the variable
Z_{t}=\tan\alpha_{t}=\delta{p}_{t}/\delta{x}_{t}

,

]
, which measures the local particle-velocity gradient along the reference trajectory. We obtain

(25a)

\displaystyle\sigma^{(1)}_{t}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{t}\int_{0}^{t}\!\!{\text{d}t^{\prime}}\,Z_{t^{\prime}}+\frac{1}{t}\int_{0}^{t}\!\!\text{d}Z_{{t^{\prime}}}\,\frac{Z_{t^{\prime}}}{Z_{t^{\prime}}^{2}+1}\,.

In terms of the coordinate
Z_{t}
, a caustic corresponds to
Z_{t}\to-\infty

Z_{t}=+\infty

[
7
]
. The stochastic dynamics (
25
) determines the distribution of the phase-space FTLE
\sigma^{(1)}_{t}
, of the form (
10
) with rate function
I(s)
. In one spatial dimension (

) discussed in Section

reads:

\displaystyle P\left(\sigma^{(1)}_{{t}}=s,\sigma^{(2)}_{{t}}=s^{\prime}\right){\propto}\delta(s+s^{\prime}+\zeta)\theta(s-s^{\prime})\text{e}^{-tI(s)}\,.

(26)

Integrating over
s^{\prime}
we obtain the marginal distribution of
\sigma^{(1)}_{t}
:

\displaystyle P\left(\sigma^{(1)}_{{t}}=s\right){\propto}\theta\left(s+\frac{\zeta}{2}\right)\text{e}^{-tI(s)}\,.

(27)

The convex rate function
I(s)
attains its minimum at
\lambda_{1}
, where
I(\lambda_{1})=0
so that
\lambda_{1}=\lim_{t\to\infty}\sigma^{(1)}_{t}
is the maximal Lyapunov exponent, Eq. (

5
Distribution of the spatial FTLE

We outlined in Section
3
how to calculate the spatial FTLE
\hat{\sigma}_{t}
by projection. In one spatial dimension this projection is illustrated as the dashed line in Fig.
2
. From Eq. (
20b
) we obtain the equation of motion for the projected spatial FTLE
\hat{\sigma}_{t}
:

\displaystyle\hat{\sigma}_{t}=\frac{1}{t}\int_{0}^{t}\text{d}t^{\prime}\tan\alpha_{t^{\prime}}=\frac{1}{t}\int_{0}^{t}\text{d}t^{\prime}Z_{t^{\prime}}\,.

(28)

It is easy to see by comparing Eqs. (
28
) and (
23b
) that the cancellation of the divergencies of the integrals for
\sigma_{t}^{(1)}
does not take place for
\hat{\sigma}_{t}
. Instead, the spatial FTLE runs into a logarithmic divergence
\hat{\sigma}_{t}\sim\log(|t-t_{\text{c}}|)
in the vicinity of a caustic at time
t_{\text{c}}
.

In the remainder of this Section we present the two main results of this paper, the spatial rate function
\hat{I}(s)
and the spatial SCGF
\hat{\Lambda}(k)
. These quantities describe the transient fluctuations of the spatial FTLE
\hat{\sigma}_{t}
.

5.1
Spatial rate function
\hat{I}(s)

We see from Eq. (
28
) that the spatial projection leads to finite-time divergencies of the spatial FTLE
\hat{\sigma}_{t}
. In Section
4
we have demonstrated that the phase-space FTLE obeys a large-deviation principle with rate function
I(s)
, Eq. (
49
). The question is how the finite-time divergencies of
\hat{\sigma}_{t}
affect the large-deviation principle. In
A
we show that the distribution of
\hat{\sigma}_{t}
has indeed a large-deviation form

\displaystyle P({\hat{\sigma}}_{t}=s){\propto}\text{e}^{-t\hat{I}(s^{\prime})}\,,

(29)

but with an altered,
spatial
rate function
\hat{I}(s)
. The spatial rate function
\hat{I}(s)
is given by

\displaystyle\hat{I}(s)=\begin{cases}I(s)\,,&s\geq\max\{s^{*},-\zeta/2\}\,,\\
-s-\hat{I}_{0}\,,&\text{otherwise}\,,\end{cases}

(30)

which depends on two constants,
s^{*}
and
\hat{I}_{0}
. Both these constants depend on the properties of the phase-space rate function
I(s)
. Namely
s^{*}
is given by the position of the infimum of
I(s)+s
,

\displaystyle s^{*}=\text{argmin}_{s^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}}\{I(s^{\prime})+s^{\prime}\}\,.

(31)

while
\hat{I}_{0}
depends on
I(s)
and the location of
s^{*}
relative to
-\zeta/2
:

\displaystyle\hat{I}_{0}=\begin{cases}\Lambda(-1)\,,&s^{*}\geq-\zeta/2\,,\\
\zeta/2-I(-\zeta/2)\,,&\text{otherwise}\,.\end{cases}

(32)

Equation (
30
) shows that the spatial rate function
\hat{I}(s)
is a continuous and convex function of
s
which coincides with the phase-space rate function
I(s)
for
s\geq\max\{s^{*},-\zeta/2\}
, and is linear otherwise. However, if
s^{*}<-\zeta/2
then
\hat{I}(s)
is not differentiable at
s=-\zeta/2
. Fig.
3
shows the form of
\hat{I}(s)
schematically for these two cases.

The linear part in the spatial rate function
\hat{I}(s)
implies that the probability of large, negative values of
\hat{\sigma}_{t}
is exponentially enhanced, by a factor of
\sim\exp(t[I(s)-\hat{I}(s)])
. The shape of the linear part does not depend upon the details of
F_{t}
and is thus a universal contribution due to caustic catastrophes. Put differently, caustics created by fold catastrophes of the phase-space attractor cause additional spatial clustering, with universal properties.

5.2
Spatial scaled cumulant-generating function
\hat{\Lambda}(k)

Schematic plot of the phase-space SCGF
\Lambda(k)

s^{*}\geq-\zeta/2

s^{*}<-\zeta/2

\hat{\Lambda}(k)

As the second main result of the paper, we derive the spatial SCGF
\hat{\Lambda}(k)
. Using Eq. (
28
) and
Z_{t}=\tfrac{\text{d}}{\text{d}t}\log|\delta{x}_{t}|
,
\hat{\Lambda}(k)
can be expressed in terms of the spatial separation
|\delta x_{t}|
according to

\displaystyle\hat{\Lambda}(k)=\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{1}{t}\log\left\langle\text{e}^{k\int_{0}^{t}\!\!{\text{d}t^{\prime}}\,Z_{t^{\prime}}}\right\rangle=\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{1}{t}\log\left\langle\left|\frac{\delta{x}_{t}}{\delta{x}_{{t=0}}}\right|^{k}\right\rangle\,.

(33)

This expression shows that
\hat{\Lambda}(k)
is equal to the generalised Lyapunov exponent
[
81
]
in one spatial dimension. Since the spatial separations
|\delta x_{t}|
contract to zero in finite time, the ensemble average in Eq. (
33
) contains singularities that affect
\hat{\Lambda}(k)
for negative
k
. In the next Section we show that
P(|\delta x_{t}/{\delta}x_{{t=0}}|=0)>0
for large enough
t
. This means that
\langle|\delta x_{t}/\delta x_{{t=0}}|^{k}\rangle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\text{d}r\,r^{k}P(|\delta x_{t}/{\delta}x_{{t=0}}|=r)=\infty
, for
k\leq-1
, which implies
\hat{\Lambda}(k)=\infty
for
k\leq-1
. To obtain
\hat{\Lambda}(k)
for
k>-1
we perform a Legendre transform of
\hat{I}(s)
:

\displaystyle\hat{\Lambda}(k)=\sup_{s\in\mathbb{R}}\{sk-\hat{I}(s)\}\,.

(34)

This calculation is carried out in
B
. We find that the spatial SCGF
\hat{\Lambda}(k)
reads

\displaystyle\hat{\Lambda}(k)=\begin{cases}\Lambda(k)\,,&k>k^{*}\,,\\
-k\zeta/2-I(-\zeta/2)\,,&k^{*}\geq k>-1\,,\\
\infty\,,&\text{otherwise}\,.\end{cases}

(35)

The constant
k^{*}
is given by

\displaystyle k^{*}=\begin{cases}-1\,,&s^{*}\geq-\zeta/2\,,\\
I^{\prime}([-\zeta/2]^{+})\,,&\text{otherwise}\,.\end{cases}

(36)

Here
I^{\prime}([-\zeta/2]^{+})
is the right derivative of
I(s)
at
s=-\zeta/2
, which enters Eq. (
36
) because
I(s)
is not continuously differentiable at
s=-\zeta/2
for
s^{*}<-\zeta/2
.

Equation (
35
) shows that
\hat{\Lambda}(k)
coincides with the phase-space SCGF
\Lambda(k)
for
k>k^{*}
, has a linear part in the interval
k\in(-1,k^{*}]
, and diverges for
k\leq-1
. Note that the linear part vanishes when
s^{*}\geq-\zeta/2
. In Fig.
4
the spatial SCGF is shown schematically for the two cases
s^{*}\geq-\zeta/2
and
s^{*}<-\zeta/2
.

The divergence of
\hat{\Lambda}(k)
is the universal analogue of the linear part in the spatial rate function
\hat{I}(s)
. In view of Eq. (
33
) the divergence of
\hat{\Lambda}(k)
for
k\leq-1
describes a divergence of the negative moments of spatial separations. This divergence is due to a finite value of the probability density of
|\delta x_{t}/\delta x_{{t=0}}|
at zero spatial separation
P(|\delta x_{t}/\delta x_{{t=0}}|=0)>0
, which we derive in the next Section. In other words, the caustic catastrophes allow the particle positions in a neighbourhood to coincide with finite probability.

6
Relation between phase-space and spatial clustering

Let us analyse the consequences of Eqs. (
30
) and (
35
) for the relation between phase-space and spatial clustering. Recall that the large-deviation statistics of the phase-space FTLEs allows to compute the singularity exponents
\xi_{n}
, and thus the fractal phase-space dimensions
D_{q}
. Adapting the formalism described in Ref.
[
34
]
to our model, we find that, for a positive maximum Lyapunov exponent
\lambda_{1}>0
, the exponents
\xi_{n}
are given by

\displaystyle\sup_{s\geq-\zeta/2}\{(\xi_{n}-2n)s-{I}(s)\}

\displaystyle=-\zeta(\xi_{n}-n)\,,\ \text{if}\ \xi_{n}>n\,,

(37a)

\displaystyle\sup_{s\geq-\zeta/2}\{-\xi_{n}s-{I}(s)\}

\displaystyle=0\,,\quad\quad\quad\quad\text{otherwise}.

(37b)

This result determines the phase-space singularity spectrum
\xi_{n}
in terms of the phase-space rate function
I(s)
. Equations (
37
) show how the long-time distribution of phase-space FTLEs determines the fractal properties of the phase-space attractor, as discussed in Section
2.4
.

For spatial clustering, no formula as general as Eq. (
37
) has been derived, because caustics cause trajectories to cross in configuration space. Therefore, the masses of infinitesimal phase-space volumes are in general not conserved. With the help of Eq. (
13
) one can, however, obtain an analogous formula at least for
D_{2}=\xi_{1}
. It reads
[
82
,
83
,
55
]

\displaystyle\sup_{s\in\mathbb{R}}\left\{-\hat{D}_{2}s-{\hat{I}}(s)\right\}=0\,.

(38)

Comparing with the corresponding condition for the phase-space correlation dimension, Eqs. (
37
) and the projection relation (
30
) between
I(s)
and
\hat{I}(s)
we deduce that

\displaystyle\hat{D}_{2}=\min\{D_{2},1\}\,.

(39)

This proves that the projection formula (
16
) holds for
q=2
.
We can also conclude that the saturation of
\hat{D}_{2}
at unity is caused by the linear part of the projected rate function,
{\hat{I}}(s)=-s-\hat{I}_{0}
for
s<s^{*}
. This means
that the saturation of
\hat{D}_{2}
is a direct consequence of the formation of caustics.

The behaviour of
\hat{D}_{2}
described by Eq. (
39
) can be explained by comparing the scalings of
P(R_{t}\leq r)
and
P(|\delta x_{t}|\leq r)
for
r\ll 1
at large but finite times. In the infinite-time limit, we must then recover Eqs. (
13
) and (
15
), thereby confirming the relation (
39
) between
D_{2}
and
\hat{D}_{2}
. We start by considering
P(R_{t}\leq r)
for
r\geq\text{e}^{-\zeta t/2}
and
D_{2}<1
. We write

\displaystyle P(R_{t}\leq r){\propto}\exp\big[-t\inf_{-\zeta/2\leq s\leq t^{-1}\log r}I(s)\big]\,.

(40)

Since
\lambda_{1}>0
, typical separations grow exponentially.
In an infinite system the probability of observing any fixed
value of
r
must tend to zero in the infinite-time limit.
In order to find the scaling form of the separation distribution that
defines the correlation dimension, we therefore
demand
\tfrac{\text{d}}{\text{d}t}P(R_{t}\leq t^{-1}\log r)=0
.
This yields the condition
\sup_{-\zeta/2\leq s<\lambda_{1}}\{I^{\prime}(t^{-1}\log r_{0})s-I(s)\}=0
in a small range of separations around a small value
r_{0}
. Comparing with Eq. (
37
) for
D_{2}<1
reveals that
I^{\prime}(t^{-1}\log r_{0})=-D_{2}
. Using this condition we obtain the cumulative separation
distribution by expanding Eq. (
40
) around
r_{0}
. For
|\log(r/r_{0})|\ll t
, we find
P(R_{t}\leq r)\sim r^{-I^{\prime}(t^{-1}\log r_{0})}=r^{D_{2}}
for the cumulative distribution of phase-space separations. This shows how the scaling (
13
) of the cumulative distribution
of phase-space separations emerges from our result for the distribution of phase-space FTLEs.

Now consider the cumulative distribution of spatial separations
P(|\delta x_{t}|\leq r)
. From Eq. (
30
) we find that for
r\geq\exp(t\max\{s^{*},-\zeta/2\})
,
P(|\delta x_{t}|\leq r)=P(R_{t}\leq r)
. Differences between the distributions only arise for
r<\exp(t\max\{s^{*},-\zeta/2\})
, because this is the regime of the linear part of the spatial rate function
\hat{I}(s)
. We have in this case
P(|\delta x_{t}|\leq r)\sim\exp\left[-t\inf_{s\leq t^{-1}\log r}\left\{-s-\hat{I}_{0}\right\}\right]=r\text{e}^{\hat{I}_{0}t}
.
As for the phase-space separations, the power-law in
P(|\delta x_{t}|\leq r)
, Eq. (
15
), builds up in a range of separations around a small value
\hat{r}_{0}
, given by
\sup_{s\leq\hat{\lambda}}\{\hat{I}^{\prime}(t^{-1}\log\hat{r}_{0})s-\hat{I}(s)\}=0
. Comparing this with Eq. (
39
) we conclude that
I^{\prime}(t^{-1}\log\hat{r}_{0})=-D_{2}
for
\hat{r}_{0}\geq\exp(t\max\{s^{*},-\zeta/2\})
and
I^{\prime}(t^{-1}\log\hat{r}_{0})=-1
otherwise. When
D_{2}
transitions from
D_{2}<1
to
D_{2}>1
,
\hat{r}_{0}
moves into the caustic regime with linear
r
-scaling, so that
\hat{D}_{2}=1
for
D_{2}>1
. Hence we recover Eq. (
15
) in the long-time limit.
For a finite system with boundaries, the cumulative distribution of spatial separations must contain both scalings at small separations,
P(|\delta x_{t}|\leq r)\approx C_{1}r^{D_{2}}+C_{2}r
, with time-independent constants
C_{1}
and
C_{2}

[
84
]
. The small-
r
scaling of
P(|\delta x_{t}|\leq r)
is therefore determined by which of the two powers is dominant. This is consistent with Eq. (
39
).

In conclusion, caustics affect the behaviour
of the distribution of spatial separations
r
at small values of
r
,
namely that the cumulative distribution depends linearly upon
r
,

P(|\delta x_{t}|\leq r)\propto r
at small separations
r
. This means that
the distribution of separations,
P(|\delta x_{t}|=r)
, approaches a constant at small separations.
For
\hat{D}_{2}<1
, by contrast, this ‘caustic regime’ is well separated from a self-similar regime with power-law exponent
D_{2}
, so that
\hat{D}_{2}=D_{2}
. For larger values of
D_{2}
, the self-similar regime lies within the caustic regime. As a consequence the
\hat{D}_{2}
must equal unity. This shows how
Eq. (
39
) follows from our results for the distributions of phase-space and spatial FTLEs.

. This makes it possible to compute the steady-state probability distribution of

,

, the phase-space SCGF

and the phase-space rate function

of the phase-space FTLE
\sigma^{(1)}_{t}
in explicit form. The explicit results derived in this Section allow us to study in more detail the impact of the spatial projection upon
{\hat{I}}(s)
, and to evaluate the singularity spectrum
\xi_{n}
in Eqs. (
37
).

\alpha_{t}

7.3
Fluctuation relation and spatial rate function

With help of (

\displaystyle\Lambda(k-1)-\Lambda(-k-1)

\displaystyle{I}(s-\zeta/2)-{I}(-s-\zeta/2)

\displaystyle\sup_{s\in\mathbb{R}}\{-D_{2}s-I(s)\}=\Lambda(-D_{2})=0\,.

Second, Eq. (

in Eq. (

A mathematical interpretation of the broken fluctuation relation is that the spatially projected system loses its Markov property, because the momentum is a hidden variable in the projected space.

We note that the authors of Ref.

8
Discussion

In this Section, we discuss the implications of our findings for a range of physical systems, starting with heavy particles in turbulence.

8.1
Heavy particles in turbulence

Equation (
2
) is a model for small heavy particles in turbulence
subject to viscous friction. For small particles Stokes’ law determines
the viscous damping parameter
\gamma
. The force field
\boldsymbol{f}(\boldsymbol{x},t)

represents the incompressible turbulent fluid-velocity field
\boldsymbol{u}(\boldsymbol{x},t)
. Its
correlation length
\ell
is related to the Kolmogorov length
[
7
]
.
This model has been used to study spatial clustering and caustic formation for particles
in turbulence, and their consequences for collisions in turbulent aerosols
[
44
,
45
,
6
,
77
,
52
,
46
,
47
,
53
,
54
,
48
,
84
,
90
,
51
]
.
Spatial clustering affects rate of particle collisions through the radial distribution function
g(r)
evaluated at the contact distance (equal
to
2a
for two particles of radius
a
)
[
91
]
.

In
d
spatial dimensions the radial distribution function reads
g(r)=P(\hat{R}_{t}=r)/r^{d-1}

[
77
,
84
,
90
]
, where
\hat{R}_{t}=|\delta\boldsymbol{x}_{t}|
is the spatial separation between the centers of mass of the two particles. In one spatial dimension,
g(r)
is identical to the distribution of separations
P(|\delta x_{t}|=r)
discussed in Section
6
. Our analysis of the one dimensional case shows that in an expanding system out of equilibrium, the radial distribution function is constant, for small enough spatial separations and finite times. Therefore we expect a competition between the two different scalings in the radial distribution function, that may affect the collision rate. For small times, so that
2a<\text{e}^{-\zeta t/2}
, the plateau in
P(\hat{R}_{t}=r)
gives
g(2a)\sim\text{const}
in one dimension, while for larger times
g(2a)\sim(2a)^{\hat{D}_{2}-d}
.

For heavy particles in turbulence, there may be sub-regions of high particle concentration that temporarily expand into particle void regions, without being affected by the boundaries of the system. These are in a transient (non-steady) state, where caustics contribute to collision rates between particles not only through the rate of caustic formation
J
and an increased collision velocity
[
84
,
90
]
, but possibly also through finite-time contributions to the radial distribution function
g(r)
.

8.2
Wave propagation in disordered media

Now we explore the connection between our results for the dissipative problem (
2
) and wave propagation in random media. Random focusing and spatial patterns of optical
[
49
,
50
]
, acoustic
[

,
64
,

, and quantum-mechanical
[

waves in disordered media can be understood in terms of their ray dynamics, governed by the dissipation-free limit,

[

]
, and the distribution of local stretching factors
[
61

, simply
|\delta x_{t}/\delta x_{{t=0}}|
in one spatial dimension. The dissipation-free limit of Eq. (

[

[

Equation (
30

\hat{I}(s)
appears for

. Our discussion of the distribution of spatial separations in Section

then implies a linear scaling in the cumulative distribution of the stretching factor
P(|\delta x_{t}/\delta x_{t=0}|\leq r)\sim r\text{e}^{t\hat{I}_{0}}

P(|\delta x_{t}/\delta x_{t=0}|=r)=\tfrac{\text{d}}{\text{d}r}P(|\delta x_{t}/\delta x_{t=0}|\leq r)

It has been argued that the distribution of stretching factors in the dissipation-free limit is approximately log-normal
[
61

, meaning that
\log|\delta x_{t}/\delta_{t=0}|
is normally distributed. This is motivated by expanding
\hat{I}(s)
around its minimum,
\hat{I}(s)\sim\hat{I}^{\prime\prime}(\hat{\lambda})(s-\hat{\lambda})^{2}/2
, hence neglecting non-Gaussian fluctuations. As we have shown, this approximation fails to describe the statistics of stretching factors
|\delta x_{t}/\delta x_{t=0}|
smaller than or equal to unity.

Furthermore, the spatial SCGF that describes the moments of the stretching factor,
\hat{\Lambda}(k)=\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{1}{t}\log\langle|\delta x_{t}/\delta x_{t=0}|^{k}\rangle

, for all

[

. Our result for the spatial SCGF, Eq. (

\hat{\Lambda}(k)
diverges for

\zeta=0
. While the assumption that
\hat{\Lambda}(k)=\Lambda(k)
does take into account the non-Gaussian fluctuations of the phase-space FTLEs, it does not consider the non-Gaussian fluctuations induced by the caustics. Therefore, the assumption fails to describe the long-term behaviour of the negative moments of the stretching factor.

8.3
Deterministic chaos

Related questions are of importance in classical systems that exhibit deterministic chaos with a positive maximal Lyapunov exponent. In such systems, although
\lambda_{1}>0
, trajectories may nevertheless stay close together in configuration space for some time, when the local stretching factors are small
[

. In Ref.
[

the probability of zero spatial stretching, i.e.,

are determined by the behaviour of

at

[

found that taking into account phase-space folds leads to

\displaystyle\hat{I}(0)<\hat{\lambda}\,,\quad\text{and}\quad\hat{I}^{\prime}(0)\geq-1\,.

These results for

. Using
\hat{I}(\hat{\lambda})=\hat{I}^{\prime}(\hat{\lambda})=0
, we first find that
\hat{I}(s)\leq\hat{\lambda}-s
for

and

. Because

. Hence, we extend Eqs. (

\displaystyle\hat{I}(s)<\hat{\lambda}-s\,,\quad\text{for}\ s<\hat{\lambda}\quad\text{and}\quad\hat{I}^{\prime}(s)\geq-1\,,\quad\text{for all}\ s\,.

(48)

The inequalities in Eqs. (
47

s=0
. In conclusion, our results for the spatial FTLE appear to apply also to deterministic, chaotic dynamics. This is perhaps not unexpected, as statistical descriptions are suitable for chaotic systems that are sufficiently mixing
[

]
. We conclude that the rate function
\hat{I}(s)
for the Hamiltonian systems analysed in Ref.
[

has a linear part starting at

and extending to

.

9
Conclusions

In this paper we quantified the effects of fractal catastrophes (caustics that arise in the projection of a dynamical fractal attractor) upon spatial clustering of inertial particles in a random force field. For one spatial dimension, we showed that these caustics lead to an exponential increase of the probability to observe large negative spatial FTLE, resulting in a universal law of spatial clustering.

We demonstrated that caustics give rise to a universal negative tail in the rate function for
the distribution of the spatial FTLE distribution (the rate function is essentially the logarithm of this distribution). This universal linear part of the rate function implies that the moments of spatial separations below a critical order diverge in finite time, and that the spatial correlation dimension
\hat{D}_{2}
obeys the projection formula
\hat{D}_{2}=\min\{D_{2},1\}
.
Our theory shows how the distribution of spatial separations evolves as a function of time,
and how it approaches its steady state. Folds of the phase-space manifold and fractal clustering affect this distribution in two distinct ways: caustics cause the distribution of spatial separations to become constant
at small separations, and fractal clustering gives rise to a self-similar regime. When the spatial correlation dimension is less than the spatial dimension then these two regimes are well separated. Otherwise
the regimes overlap.

For white-in-time Gaussian force fields we calculated the distribution of phase-space FTLEs explicitly. This distribution exhibits a fluctuation relation, associating the probabilities of phase-space regions with large positive and large negative FTLE, voids and clusters. Our exact results imply that this balance is destroyed in the distribution of the spatial FTLE, a consequence of increased clustering due to caustics.

We showed that our results have implications for different problems in statistical physics and chaos theory, where they allow to explain and extend existing results and put into question some of the approximations.

We obtained all results that characterise spatial quantities from the spatial rate function
\hat{I}(s)
, which acquires a universal linear part under spatial projection. Therefore, the expressions for the spatial rate function
\hat{I}(s)
and of its Legendre transform
\hat{\Lambda}(k)
are the main results of this paper.

An open question is how the conceptual insights obtained in one dimension carry over to systems in higher dimensions. A complete analysis, as provided for
d=1
in this paper, is challenging because it involves a much more complex dynamics. However, our results should extend to the growth rate
t^{-1}\log\mathscr{\hat{V}}_{t}
of an infinitesimal
d
-dimensional spatial volume
\mathscr{\hat{V}}_{t}
quantifying the long-time probability of observing local particle-rich regions in configuration space. We speculate that the rate function of this growth rate has a universal linear negative tail, resulting in an exponentially increased probability of particle clusters similar to the one-dimensional case.

Important insights into how catastrophes shape the divergence of
\hat{\Lambda}(k)
for
k\leq-1
could be obtained by studying the effect of a finite cutoff upon
\hat{\Lambda}(k)
.
Berry
[
49
]
considered the effect of a cutoff given by the wavelength of
light upon the patterns of light intensity
I(\boldsymbol{x},t)
focused by a random medium.
The cutoff removes the divergencies for non-zero values of
\lambda
. Berry calculated
how the intensity moments diverge as
\lambda\to 0
, and computed the
critical exponents
\nu_{n}
associated with the
n
-th intensity moment. He
showed that as
n
increases, the critical exponents are dominated by contributions from catastrophes of increasing codimensions.
In our model the physical origin of a cutoff is different, for example due to a finite number of particles.
We expect that such a cutoff regularises the divergence of
\hat{\Lambda}(k)
for
k\leq-1
, and that
it should be possible to calculate the corresponding critical exponents.
They could give further insights into the divergencies caused by caustics in dissipative systems of the kind discussed here, and will yield a better understaning of the effect of fractal catastrophes on spatial clustering.

Appendix A
Derivation of
\hat{I}(s)

We now derive a large deviation principle for
\hat{\sigma}_{t}
with rate function
\hat{I}(s)
without making explicit use of the equation of motion (
28
). This allows us to circumvent the difficulties associated with the finite-time divergences of
\hat{\sigma}_{t}
. The large-deviation form of the cumulative distribution of the spatial FTLE
\hat{\sigma}_{t}
reads

\displaystyle P({\hat{\sigma}}_{t}\leq s){\propto}\exp\big[-t\inf_{s^{\prime}\leq s}\hat{I}(s^{\prime})\big]\,,

(49)

with the spatial rate function
\hat{I}(s)
yet to be determined. To find
\hat{I}(s)
, we first write for
P(\hat{\sigma}_{t}\leq s)
:

\displaystyle P(\hat{\sigma}_{t}\leq s)

\displaystyle=P\left(\text{e}^{t\hat{\sigma}_{t}}\leq\text{e}^{ts}\right)=P\left(\frac{\cos\alpha_{t}}{\cos\alpha_{t=0}}\text{e}^{t\sigma_{t}^{(1)}}\leq\text{e}^{ts}\right)=P\left(\cos\alpha_{t}\leq\exp[t(s-\sigma^{(1)}_{t})]\right)\,.

(50)

Here we used that
|\delta{x}_{t}/\delta{x}_{{t=0}}|=\cos\alpha_{t}\,\text{e}^{t\sigma^{(1)}_{t}}=\text{e}^{t\hat{\sigma}_{t}}
, and that
\cos\alpha_{t=0}=1
.
Now, as discussed in Section
4
,
\alpha_{t}
passes
-\pi/2
with rate
J
. The large-deviation principle (
27
), on the other hand, implies that
\sigma^{(1)}_{t}
stabilises in the vicinity of the maximal phase-space Lyapunov exponent
\lambda_{1}
. Therefore, the joint distribution of
\alpha_{t}
and
\sigma^{(1)}_{t}
factorises. As a consequence, the cumulative distribution function of
\cos\alpha_{t}
conditional on a
\sigma^{(1)}_{t}
simplifies to

\displaystyle P\left(\cos\alpha_{t}\leq\exp[t(s-s^{\prime})]\big|\sigma^{(1)}_{t}=s^{\prime}\right)

\displaystyle\approx P_{\text{st}}\left(\cos\alpha_{t}\leq\exp[t(s-s^{\prime})]\right)\,.

(51)

We now condition Eq. (
50
) on
\sigma^{(1)}_{t}=s^{\prime}
and use Eq. (
51
) to simplify the expression:

\displaystyle P(\hat{\sigma}_{t}\leq s)

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\text{d}s^{\prime}P\left(\hat{\sigma}_{t}\leq s|\sigma_{t}^{(1)}=s^{\prime}\right)P(\sigma^{(1)}_{t}=s^{\prime})\,

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\text{d}s^{\prime}P\left(\cos\alpha_{t}\leq\exp[t(s-s^{\prime})]\big|\sigma_{t}^{(1)}=s^{\prime}\right)P(\sigma^{(1)}_{t}=s^{\prime})\,

\displaystyle\propto\int_{-\tfrac{\zeta}{2}}^{\infty}\text{d}s^{\prime}P_{\text{st}}\left(\cos\alpha_{t}\leq\exp[t(s-s^{\prime})]\right)\text{e}^{-tI(s^{\prime})}.

(52)

In the second step we used Eq. (
51
), and in the last step we inserted the large-deviation form of
\sigma^{(1)}_{t}
, Eq. (
27
). We now split the integral in Eq. (
A
) into two parts:
s^{\prime}<s
and
s^{\prime}>s
, so that for for large times,
\exp[t(s-s^{\prime})]\gg 1
and
\exp[t(s-s^{\prime})]\ll 1
, respectively. For
\exp[t(s-s^{\prime})]\gg 1
we have, trivially,
P_{\text{st}}(\cos\alpha_{t}\leq\exp[t(s-s^{\prime})])=1
. For
\exp[t(s-s^{\prime})]\ll 1
, i.e.
s^{\prime}>s
, we find using Eq. (
24
),
P_{\text{st}}(\cos\alpha_{t}\leq\exp[t(s-s^{\prime})])\sim 2J\exp[t(s-s^{\prime})]
. Putting these results together we obtain

\displaystyle P(\hat{\sigma}_{t}\leq s)

\displaystyle\propto\int_{-\zeta/2}^{s}\text{d}s^{\prime}\exp[-tI(s^{\prime})]+2J\exp(ts)\int_{s}^{\infty}\text{d}s^{\prime}\exp(-t[I(s^{\prime})+s^{\prime}])

\displaystyle\propto\exp\left[-t\inf_{s^{\prime}\leq s_{+}}I(s^{\prime})\right]+2J\exp\left[-t\left(-s+\inf_{s^{\prime}\geq s_{+}}\{I(s^{\prime})+s^{\prime}\}\right)\right]\,,

(53)

where we defined
s_{+}=\max\{s,-\zeta/2\}
. Although we are interested only in the exponential growth rate of
P(\hat{\sigma}_{t}\leq s)
, we kept the prefactor
2J
in Eq. (
A
) to show that the second term vanishes when
J=0
. For
J>0
the relative size of the two exponents determines which of the two terms in Eq. (
A
) is the leading one. This, in turn, is determined by the location
s^{*}
of the infimum of
I(s)+s
,

\displaystyle s^{*}=\text{argmin}_{s^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}}\{I(s^{\prime})+s^{\prime}\}\,.

(54)

Assuming that
I(s)
is differentiable and convex,
s^{*}
is determined uniquely by the implicit equation

\displaystyle I^{\prime}(s^{*})=-1\,.

(55)

Now if
s^{*}<s_{+}
, then
\inf_{s^{\prime}\geq s_{+}}\{I(s^{\prime})+s^{\prime}\}=I(s_{+})+s_{+}
. It follows that the first term in Eq. (
A
) is the leading one as
t\to\infty
. This implies that

\displaystyle P(\hat{\sigma}_{t}\leq s)\propto\exp\left[-t\inf_{s^{\prime}\leq s_{+}}I(s^{\prime})\right]\,.

(56)

If, on the other hand,
s_{+}\leq s^{*}
, then the second term is the leading one, so that

\displaystyle P(\hat{\sigma}_{t}\leq s)\propto 2J\exp\left[-t(-s-\hat{I}_{0})\right]=2J\exp\left[-t\inf_{s^{\prime}\leq s}\{-s^{\prime}-\hat{I}_{0}\}\right]\,.

(57)

The value of the constant
\hat{I}_{0}
depends on the location of
s^{*}
relative to
-\zeta/2
:

\displaystyle\hat{I}_{0}=\begin{cases}\Lambda(-1)\,,&s^{*}\geq-\zeta/2\,,\\
\zeta/2-I(-\zeta/2)\,,&s^{*}<-\zeta/2\,.\end{cases}

(58)

The scaled cumulant-generating function at
k=-1
,
\Lambda(-1)
, appears in Eq. (
58
) because by definition
\Lambda(-1)=\sup_{s\in\mathbb{R}}\left\{-s-I(s)\right\}=-\inf_{s\in\mathbb{R}}\left\{I(s)+s\right\}
. We conclude, for general
s
and using Eq. (
49
),

\displaystyle\hat{I}(s)=\begin{cases}I(s)\,,&s\geq\max\{s^{*},-\zeta/2\}\,,\\
-s-\hat{I}_{0}\,,&\text{otherwise}\,.\end{cases}

(59)

This is Eq. (
30
) in the main text.

Appendix B
Derivation of
\hat{\Lambda}(k)
by Legendre transform

We compute
\hat{\Lambda}(k)
from the Legendre transform (
34
). When
\hat{I}(s)
is differentiable in
s
, then
\hat{\Lambda}(k)
is uniquely determined by inverting
k=\hat{I}^{\prime}(s)
. This is the case for
s^{*}\geq-\zeta/2
where we find using Eqs. (
30
) and (
34
):

\displaystyle\hat{\Lambda}(k)=\begin{cases}\Lambda(k)\,,&k>-1\\
\infty\,,&k\leq-1\,.\end{cases}

(60)

The case
s^{*}<-\zeta/2
is slightly more complicated. Since
\hat{I}(s)
is not continuously differentiable at
s=-\zeta/2
, we must consider the left and right limits of the derivative at this point. Approaching
s=-\zeta/2
from the left we find
\hat{I}^{\prime}([\zeta/2]^{-})=-1
, a direct consequence of the linear part. For the derivative approaching from the right we obtain
\hat{I}^{\prime}([-\zeta/2]^{+})=I^{\prime}([-\zeta/2]^{+})>-1
and we denote the value of the right derivative by
k^{*}
. The gap between the left and the right derivative,
-1<{k^{*}}
, implies that the equation
k=\hat{I}^{\prime}(s)
does not have a solution for
k
-values in the interval
k\in(-1,{k^{*}})
. Therefore, the value for
s
which maximises the right-hand side of Eq. (
34
) stays equal to
s=-\zeta/2
over the interval
k\in(-1,{k^{*}})
. Hence, we find for
k\in(-1,{k^{*}})
,
\hat{\Lambda}(k)=-\zeta k/2-\hat{I}(-\zeta/2)
, which is linear in
k
. In conclusion, we find for
s^{*}<-\zeta/2
:

\displaystyle\hat{\Lambda}(k)=\begin{cases}\Lambda(k)\,,&k\geq{k^{*}}\\
-k\zeta/2-I(-\zeta/2)\,,&{k^{*}}>k>-1\\
\infty\,,&k\leq-1\,.\end{cases}

(61)

Putting Eqs. (
60
) and (
61
) together in one equation we obtain Eqs. (
35
) and (
36
) in the main text.

Appendix C
Calculation of
\Lambda(k)
for even integer
k

We find exact expressions for the spectrum of the tilted generator
\mathscr{L}_{k}
for even integer
k
and show that
\Lambda(k)
is real for even values of
k
. The tilted generator and its adjoint obey the eigenvalue equations (
42
), with the phase-space SCGF
\Lambda(k)
as the leading eigenvalue. We apply to Eqs. (
42
) the transformations

\displaystyle r_{k}(z)

\displaystyle=(z^{2}+1)^{-k/2}\tilde{r}_{k}(z)\,,

(62)

\displaystyle l_{k}(z)

\displaystyle=(z^{2}+1)^{k/2}\tilde{l}_{k}(z)\,,

(63)

and introduce the change of variables
z\to y=z+\zeta/2
. The functions
\tilde{r}_{k}(y)
and
\tilde{l}_{k}(y)
then obey the eigenvalue equations

\displaystyle\varepsilon^{2}\zeta^{3}\tilde{r}_{k}^{\prime\prime}(y)-\left(y^{2}-\frac{\zeta^{2}}{4}\right)\tilde{r}_{k}^{\prime}(y)+ky\tilde{r}_{k}(y)

\displaystyle=\left(\Lambda(k)+\frac{\zeta k}{2}\right)\tilde{r}_{k}(y)\,,

(64a)

\displaystyle\varepsilon^{2}\zeta^{3}\tilde{l}_{k}^{\prime\prime}(y)+\left(y^{2}-\frac{\zeta^{2}}{4}\right)\tilde{l}^{\prime}_{k}(y)+(k+2)y\tilde{l}_{k}(y)

\displaystyle=\left(\Lambda(k)+\frac{\zeta k}{2}\right)\tilde{l}_{k}(y)\,.

(64b)

Now, we rescale
y
by
y\to\zeta y
and follow a method described in Ref.
[
85
]
. That is, we first write
\tilde{r}_{k}(y)
as a polynomial in
y

\displaystyle\tilde{r}_{k}(y)=\sum_{n=0}^{N}a_{n}y^{n}\,,

(65)

where we choose
a_{N}=1
. Substituting (
65
) into (
64a
), we obtain a recurrence relation for
a_{n}
which terminates at
N=k
, for positive integer
k
. In order to satisfy the boundary conditions that
r_{k}(z)
must to be symmetric for large argument, we need to restrict
N
to positive
even
integer
k
. The recurrence relation (
65
) can be written as an eigenvalue problem for the vector
\mathbf{a}=(a_{0},a_{1},\ldots,a_{k-1},1)
:

\displaystyle\Lambda(k)\mathbf{a}=\mathbb{L}\mathbf{a}\,,

(66)

with the
(k+1)\times(k+1)
-dimensional matrix
\mathbb{L}
given by the matrix:

\displaystyle\mathbb{L}=\begin{pmatrix}-\frac{k}{2}&\frac{1}{4}&2\varepsilon^{2}&0&\cdots&\cdots&\cdots&\cdots&0\\
k&-\frac{k}{2}&\frac{2}{4}&6\varepsilon^{2}&0&\cdots&\cdots&\cdots&\vdots\\
0&k-1&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&\cdots&\cdots&\vdots\\
\vdots&0&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&\frac{k-1}{4}&k(k-1)\varepsilon^{2}\\
0&\ddots&\cdots&\cdots&\cdots&0&2&-\frac{k}{2}&\frac{k}{4}\\
0&\cdots&\cdots&\cdots&\cdots&\cdots&0&1&-\frac{k}{2}\end{pmatrix}

(67)

The matrix
\mathbb{L}
is of the Metzler type, which means that all its off-diagonal entries are non-negative. For this kind of matrix one can prove that its largest eigenvalue (and thus
\Lambda(k)
for positive, even integer
k
), is strictly real
[
95
]
. Using the fluctuation relation Eq. (
44a
), we can extend this result to negative even integer
k
. We conclude that
\Lambda(k)
is real for all even integer
k
, as stated in the main text. The approach described here can be used to write
\Lambda(k)
as the dominant root of a polynomial of order
k+1
for all finite and even values of
k
. For
k=2
and
k=4
we obtain after reversing the rescaling
\Lambda(k)\to\Lambda(k)/\zeta
:

\displaystyle 0=

\displaystyle-\frac{\Lambda(2)^{3}}{2}-\frac{3\zeta\Lambda(2)^{2}}{2}-\zeta^{2}\Lambda(2)+2\varepsilon^{2}\zeta^{3}\,,

(68a)

\displaystyle 0=

\displaystyle-\frac{\Lambda(4)^{5}}{24\zeta^{2}}-\frac{5\Lambda(4)^{4}}{12\zeta}-\frac{35\Lambda(4)^{3}}{24}+\zeta\Lambda(4)^{2}\left(\frac{7\varepsilon^{2}}{2}-\frac{25}{12}\right)+\zeta^{2}\Lambda(4)\left(14\varepsilon^{2}-1\right)+12\varepsilon^{2}\zeta^{3}\,.

(68b)

The largest solutions of Eqs. (
68
) are real and yield implicit expressions for
\Lambda(2)
and
\Lambda(4)
.

Appendix D
Derivation of fluctuation relation (
44
) using the tilted generator

We derive the fluctuation relations Eq. (
44
) from the eigenvalue equations Eq. (
42
). We start from the transformed Eqs. (
64
). The idea is to bring the first equation into the same form as the second one by a suitable change of variables and then compare the corresponding largest eigenvalues
\Lambda(k)
. To this end we transform
y\to-y
and
k\to-k-2
in Eq. (
64b
) to obtain

\displaystyle\varepsilon^{2}\zeta^{3}\tilde{l}_{-k-2}^{\prime\prime}(-y)-\left(y^{2}-\frac{\zeta^{2}}{4}\right)\tilde{l}^{\prime}_{-k-2}(-y)+ky\tilde{l}_{-k-2}(-y)

\displaystyle=\left(\Lambda(-k-2)-\frac{\zeta k}{2}-\zeta\right)\tilde{l}_{-k-2}(-y)\,.

(69)

Assuming non-degeneracy of the leading eigenvalue, we compare this equation to (
64a
) and obtain:

\displaystyle\Lambda(k)+\frac{\zeta k}{2}=\Lambda(-k-2)-\frac{\zeta k}{2}-\zeta\,.

(70)

The fluctuation relation [Eq. (
44a
) in the main text] follows directly from the shift
k\to k-1
:

\displaystyle\Lambda(k-1)+\zeta\frac{k-1}{2}

\displaystyle=\Lambda(-k-1)-\zeta\frac{k-1}{2}-\zeta\,,

\displaystyle\Lambda(k-1)-\Lambda(-k-1)

\displaystyle=-\zeta k

(71)

The fluctuation relation for
I(s)
, Eq. (
44b
) in the main text, is obtained from that of
\Lambda(k)
by Legendre transform:

\displaystyle I(s-\zeta/2)

\displaystyle=\sup_{k\in\mathbb{R}}\left\{k(s-\zeta/2)-\Lambda(k)\right\}\,,

\displaystyle=\sup_{k\in\mathbb{R}}\left\{-(k+1)(s-\zeta/2)-\Lambda(-k-1)\right\}\,,

\displaystyle=\sup_{k\in\mathbb{R}}\left\{(k-1)(-s-\zeta/2)+\zeta k-2s-\Lambda(k-1)-\zeta k\right\}\,,

\displaystyle=I(-s-\zeta/2)-2s\,.

(72)

Appendix E
Connection between fluctuation relations (
44
) and time-reversal invariance

We derived the fluctuation relations (
44
) from the eigenvalue problem of the tilted generator
\mathscr{L}^{\dagger}
in phase space. More generally it can be shown that fluctuation relations of the type (
44
) follow from the statistical invariance under time reversal
[
87
,
56
]
. To make the connection we use the method described in Ref.
[
56
]
for Markov processes to show that the fluctuation relations (
44
) have their origin in the time-reversal invariance of the shifted process
Y_{t}=Z_{t}+\zeta/2
, which obeys the dynamics

\displaystyle\dot{Y}_{t}=-{Y_{t}}^{2}+\frac{\zeta^{2}}{4}+F_{t}\,.

(73)

If the statistics of the force gradient
F_{t}
is invariant under time-reversal, the equation of motion (
73
) is invariant under the transformation
(t,Y_{t})\to(-t,-Y_{-t})
. Using the procedure described in Ref.
[
56
]
we identify the observable associated with this symmetry as

\displaystyle\mathscr{W}_{t}=2\int^{t}_{0}{\text{d}t^{\prime}}\,(Z_{{t^{\prime}}}+\tfrac{\zeta}{2})-\Delta\varphi\,,

(74)

where

\Delta\varphi=\log\rho_{t}(Z_{t}+\zeta/2)-\log\rho_{{t=0}}(Z_{{t=0}}+\zeta/2)\,.

(75)

Here
\rho_{t}
and
\rho_{{t=0}}
are the initial and final densities of
Z_{t}
. According to Ref.
[
56
]
this implies that the rate function
I_{\mathscr{W}_{t}}
corresponding to the observable
\mathscr{W}_{t}/t
has the symmetry

\displaystyle I_{\mathscr{W}_{t}}({s})-I_{\mathscr{W}_{t}}(-{s})=-{s}\,.

(76)

Having identified the observable (
74
), and using the general result (
76
), we can now show the fluctuation relation for
\sigma^{(1)}_{t}
. To this end, we use Eq. (
25b
) in the main text to express
\sigma^{(1)}_{t}
in terms of
\mathscr{W}_{t}
as

\displaystyle\sigma^{(1)}_{t}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{t}\int_{0}^{t}\text{d}t^{\prime}Z_{t^{\prime}}+\frac{1}{t}\int_{0}^{t}\text{d}t^{\prime}\tfrac{\text{d}}{\text{d}t^{\prime}}\log\sqrt{Z_{t^{\prime}}^{2}+1}\,,

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\mathscr{W}_{t}}{t}-\zeta\right)+\frac{\Delta\varphi}{t}\,.

(77)

To obtain the second equality, we choose
\rho_{t}(Z_{t}+\tfrac{\zeta}{2})
and
\rho_{t=0}(Z_{t=0}+\tfrac{\zeta}{2})
in Eq. (
75
) as follows:

\displaystyle\rho_{t}(Z_{t}+\tfrac{\zeta}{2})=\frac{1}{\pi(Z_{t}^{2}+1)}\quad\mbox{and}\quad\rho_{t=0}(Z_{t=0}+\tfrac{\zeta}{2})=\frac{1}{\pi(Z_{t=0}^{2}+1)}\,.

(78)

From Eqs. (
76
) and (
E
) we then conclude that the rate function
I(s)
of
\sigma^{(1)}_{t}
must obey:

\displaystyle I_{\mathscr{W}_{t}}(s)=I\left({s/2-\zeta/2}\right)\,,\quad I_{\mathscr{W}_{t}}(-s)=I\left({-s/2-\zeta/2}\right)\,,

(79)

so that Eq. (
76
) implies the the fluctuation relation for
I(s)
, Eq. (
44b
) in the main text. This shows that the phase-space fluctuation relations (
44
) follow from statistical invariance of the Markov dynamics under time-reversal symmetry, as outlined in Ref.
[
56
]
.
