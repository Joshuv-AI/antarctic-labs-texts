Minimax Converse for Identification via Channels
Shun Watanabe

Abstract
A minimax converse for the identification via channels is derived. By this converse, a general formula for the identification capacity, which coincides with the transmission capacity, is proved without the assumption of the strong converse property. Furthermore, the optimal second-order coding rate of the identification via channels is characterized when the type I error probability is non-vanishing and the type II error probability is vanishing. Our converse is built upon the so-called partial channel resolvability approach; however, the minimax argument enables us to circumvent a flaw reported in the literature.

Introduction

The identification is one of typical functions such that randomization significantly reduces the amount
of communication necessary to compute those functions; eg. see
[

.
Inspired by the work by Ja’Ja’
[
18

, Ahlswede and Dueck studied
the problem of identification via noisy channels in
[
3
,
2
]
; they have shown that,
with randomization, messages of doubly exponential size of the block-length can be identified, and the optimal
coefficient is given by Shannon’s transmission capacity. Since then, the problem of identification in the context of
information theory has been studied extensively in the literature
[
11
,
12
,
24
,

,
4

13
,
20
,
33
,

; see
[

]
for a thorough review.

In many cases, the difficulties of identification problems arise in proving converse coding theorems.
Initially, the so-called soft converse was proved in
[
3
]
; the converse coding theorem was only proved
under the assumption that the identification error probabilities converge to zero exponentially fast in the block-length.
Later, Han and Verdú proved the strong converse coding theorem of the identification via channels in
[
11
]
.
The crucial step of the proof in
[
11
]
is that we replace general stochastic encoders with stochastic encoders having
specific forms, termed “
M
-types.”
In
[
12
]
, Han and Verdú further studied this step as a separate problem, which they termed the
channel resolvability, by introducing the information spectrum approach.

The information spectrum approach provides effective tools to investigate coding problems for
general non-ergodic sources/channels; see
[
10
]
for a thorough treatment.
For the channel resolvability, the optimal rate is upper bounded by the spectral sup-mutual information rate
maximized over input processes. On the other hand, the identification capacity of general channels can be
lower bounded by the spectral inf-mutual information rate maximized over input processes.
When those upper bound and lower bound coincide, which is termed the strong converse property,
it was shown in
[
12
]
that the identification capacity and the optimal rate of the channel resolvability
coincide with the transmission capacity of the same channels. Later, it was proved in
[
13
]
that,
without the assumption of the strong converse property,
the optimal rate of the channel resolvability is characterized by the spectral sup-mutual information rate
maximized over input processes.

In an attempt to determine the identification capacity without the assumption of the strong converse property,
Steinberg introduced the partial channel resolvability
[
24
]
.
In the partial channel resolvability, we consider a truncated channel so that
the tail probability of information spectrum is not accumulated twice
in the argument of relating the channel resolvability to the identification code.
It should be noted that, in the modern terminology, considering the partial response is
essentially equivalent to the technique termed “smoothing”
[
23
]
. For instance,
the channel resolvability for smoothed channels has been effectively used to derive second-order
bounds on coding problems with side-information
[
32
]
.

Using the partial channel resolvability, it was claimed in
[
24
]
that
the identification capacity of general channels coincides with the transmission capacity of the same channels.
However, there is a flaw in the proof of
[
24
, Lemma 2]
,
which has been reported in
[
13
, Remark 2]
.
Thus, without the assumption of the strong converse property, the identification capacity of general channels has been
an open problem so far. The main purpose of this paper is to provide a remedy to the result claimed in
[
24
]
.
In fact, our converse is built upon the partial channel resolvability; however, in order to circumvent the aforementioned flaw,
we leverage the minimax argument described below.

In the past few decades, the argument based on the hypothesis testing has been successfully used to derive
a converse bound on transmission codes of general channels

22
]
, which is termed the meta converse.

II
Problem Formulation of Identification via Channels

In this section, we describe the problem formulation of the identification via channels, and review basic results.
We start with the problem formulation for the single-shot regime.
Given a channel
W
from
{\cal X}
to
{\cal Y}
, the sender tries to transmit one of

N
messages; then the receiver shall identify if message
i\in\{1,\ldots,N\}
was transmitted or not.
The encoder is given by stochastic mappings
P_{1},\ldots,P_{N}\in{\cal P}({\cal X})
, and the decoder is given by
acceptance regions
{\cal D}_{1},\ldots,{\cal D}_{N}\subset{\cal Y}
for each message. Note that, unlike the standard
transmission code, the acceptance regions of an identification code need not be disjoint.
In other words, if the receiver is intended to identify message
i
, there is no need to distinguish
messages other than
i
.

For a given identification code
\{(P_{i},{\cal D}_{i})\}_{i=1}^{N}
, the first type error probability is given by

\displaystyle\mathrm{P}_{\mathtt{I}}:=\max_{1\leq i\leq N}P_{i}W({\cal D}_{i}^{c}),

and the second type error probability is given by

\displaystyle\mathrm{P}_{\mathtt{II}}:=\max_{1\leq i\neq j\leq N}P_{i}W({\cal D}_{j}),

where

\displaystyle P_{i}W(y)=\sum_{x\in{\cal X}}P_{i}(x)W(y|x)

is the output distribution of the channel
W
corresponding to the input distribution
P_{i}
.
For given error probabilities
0\leq\varepsilon,\delta<1
with
\varepsilon+\delta<1
,
an identification code
\{(P_{i},{\cal D}_{i})\}_{i=1}^{N}
is called
(N,\varepsilon,\delta)
-ID code for channel
W
if
\mathrm{P}_{\mathtt{I}}\leq\varepsilon
and
\mathrm{P}_{\mathtt{II}}\leq\delta

are satisfied.
Then, the optimal code size of identification via channel
W
is defined by

\displaystyle N^{\star}(\varepsilon,\delta|W):=\sup\big\{N:\mbox{$(N,\varepsilon,\delta)$-ID code for $W$ exists}\big\}.

When we consider the block coding over
n
uses
W^{n}
of a channel,
it is known that the optimal code size
N^{\star}(\varepsilon,\delta|W^{n})
grows doubly exponentially
in the block length
n
. For a discrete memoryless channel, it has been known that
the identification capacity

\displaystyle C_{\mathtt{ID}}(\varepsilon,\delta|W):=\liminf_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log\log N^{\star}(\varepsilon,\delta|W^{n})

coincide with the transmission capacity
[
3
,
11
]
, i.e.,

\displaystyle C_{\mathtt{ID}}(\varepsilon,\delta|W)=C(W):=\max_{P_{X}}I(X\wedge Y)

as long as
\varepsilon+\delta<1
, where
I(X\wedge Y)
is the mutual information between
(X,Y)
distributed
according to
P_{XY}(x,y)=P_{X}(x)W(y|x)
.
It should be noted that the identification capacity is infinite
when
\varepsilon+\delta\geq 1

[
11
]
.

III
Hypothesis Testing

In this section, we summarize known facts on the hypothesis testing and the meta converse
that are needed in the rest of the paper.
Consider a binary hypothesis testing with a null hypothesis
Z\sim P_{Z}

and an alternative hypothesis
Z\sim Q_{Z}
, where
P_{Z}
and
Q_{Z}
are distribution
on the same alphabet
{\mathcal{Z}}
. Upon observing
Z=z
, we shall decide whether the
value was generated by the distribution
P_{Z}
or the distribution
Q_{Z}
.
Most general test can be described by a channel
T
from
{\mathcal{Z}}
to
\{0,1\}
,
where
0
indicates the null hypothesis and
1
indicates the alternative hypothesis.
When
z\in{\mathcal{Z}}
is observed, the test
T
chooses the null hypothesis with probability
T(0|z)

and the alternative hypothesis with probability
T(1|z)=1-T(0|z)
.
Then, the type I error probability of the test is defined by

\displaystyle\mathrm{P}_{\mathtt{I}}[T]:=\sum_{z}P_{Z}(z)T(1|z),

and the type II error probability of the test is defined by

\displaystyle\mathrm{P}_{\mathtt{II}}[T]:=\sum_{z}Q_{Z}(z)T(0|z).

For a given
0\leq\varepsilon<1
, denote by
\beta_{\varepsilon}(P_{Z},Q_{Z})
the
optimal type II error probability under the condition that the type I error probability is less than
\varepsilon
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\beta_{\varepsilon}(P_{Z},Q_{Z}):=\inf_{T:\atop\mathrm{P}_{\mathtt{I}}[T]\leq\varepsilon}\mathrm{P}_{\mathtt{II}}[T].

In fact, since
\beta_{\varepsilon}(P_{Z},Q_{Z})
can be described as a linear programming when
{\mathcal{Z}}
is finite,
the infimum can be attained.

For a threshold parameter
\gamma\in\mathbb{R}
, the test given by

\displaystyle T(0|z)=\mathbf{1}\bigg[\log\frac{P_{Z}(z)}{Q_{Z}(z)}>\gamma\bigg]

is termed the likelihood ratio test, also known as the Neyman-Pearson test.
For given
0\leq\varepsilon<1
, let

\displaystyle D_{\mathtt{s}}^{\varepsilon}(P_{Z}\|Q_{Z}):=\sup\bigg\{\gamma\in\mathbb{R}:\Pr\bigg(\log\frac{P_{Z}(Z)}{Q_{Z}(Z)}\leq\gamma\bigg)\leq\varepsilon\bigg\},

where the probability is with respect to
Z\sim P_{Z}
. Note that
the quantity is the supremum of thresholds such that the type I error probability of the likelihood ratio test is less than
\varepsilon
,
and it is referred to as
\varepsilon
-information spectrum divergence
[
28
]
.
This quantity and the optimal type II error probability defined above have the following relationship (eg. see
[
27
, Lemma 2.4]
);
it can be understood as a variant of the Neyman-Pearson lemma claiming that the likelihood ratio test is essentially optimal.

Lemma 1

For a given
0\leq\varepsilon<1
, it holds that

\displaystyle D_{\mathtt{s}}^{\varepsilon}(P_{Z}\|Q_{Z})\leq-\log\beta_{\varepsilon}(P_{Z},Q_{Z})\leq D_{\mathtt{s}}^{\varepsilon+\zeta}(P_{Z}\|Q_{Z})+\log(1/\zeta)

for any
0<\zeta<1-\varepsilon
.

This lemma enables us to use the two quantities almost interchangeably.

As we have mentioned in Section
I
, in the past few decades,
the hypothesis testing has become a useful tool to derive a converse bound on transmission codes
over a channel
W
from
{\mathcal{X}}
to
{\mathcal{Y}}
.
For such an application, we consider the hypothesis testing between the null hypothesis

\displaystyle P\times W(x,y):=P(x)W(y|x)

and the alternative hypothesis

\displaystyle P\times Q(x,y):=P(x)Q(y),

where
P\in{\mathcal{P}}({\mathcal{X}})
and
Q\in{\mathcal{P}}({\mathcal{Y}})
are given input/output distributions.
More specifically, the optimal coding rate of transmission codes is bounded in terms of

\displaystyle\inf_{P\in{\mathcal{P}}({\mathcal{X}})}\sup_{Q\in{\mathcal{P}}({\mathcal{Y}})}\beta_{\varepsilon}(P\times W,P\times Q).

(1)

It can be easily verified from the definition that
\beta_{\varepsilon}(P\times W,P\times Q)
is concave
with respect to the output distribution
Q\in{\mathcal{P}}({\mathcal{Y}})
. On the other hand, it was proved in
[
21
]
that
\beta_{\varepsilon}(P\times W,P\times Q)

is convex with respect to the input distribution
P\in{\mathcal{P}}({\mathcal{X}})
. Thus,
\beta_{\varepsilon}(P\times W,P\times Q)
is a convex-concave function
on
{\mathcal{P}}({\mathcal{X}})\times{\mathcal{P}}({\mathcal{Y}})
, and regularity conditions on the saddle-point property were discussed in
[
21
]
;
particularly, since
{\mathcal{P}}({\mathcal{X}})
and
{\mathcal{P}}({\mathcal{Y}})
are compact for finite alphabets
{\mathcal{X}}
and
{\mathcal{Y}}
, the following saddle-point property
follows from the classic min-max theorem.

Lemma 2

(
[
21
]
)

For a given
0\leq\varepsilon<1
, the optimal value in (
1
) is attainable and

\displaystyle\min_{P\in{\cal P}({\cal X})}\max_{Q\in{\cal P}({\cal Y})}\beta_{\varepsilon}(P\times W,P\times Q)=\max_{Q\in{\cal P}({\cal Y})}\min_{P\in{\cal P}({\cal X})}\beta_{\varepsilon}(P\times W,P\times Q).

When we evaluate asymptotic behavior of coding rates for a DMC, it is more convenient to
use the
\varepsilon
-information spectrum divergence. Particularly, we will use
the following symbol-wise relaxation (eg. see
[
29
]
):

\displaystyle D_{\mathtt{s}}^{\varepsilon}(P\times W\|P\times Q)\leq\max_{x\in{\cal X}}D_{\mathtt{s}}^{\varepsilon}(W(\cdot|x)\|Q)

(2)

for any
Q\in{\mathcal{P}}({\mathcal{Y}})
.

IV
Main Result: Minimax Converse for Identification via Channels

In this section, we present our main result, i.e., the minimax converse bound on the identification via channels.
To that end, we first explain the problem of channel resolvability.

For an integer
M
, a distribution
P\in{\mathcal{P}}({\mathcal{X}})
is said to be an
M
-type
if
P(x)
is an integer multiple of
1/M
for every
x\in{\mathcal{X}}
.
Then,
(N,\varepsilon,\delta)
-ID code
\{(P_{i},{\mathcal{D}}_{i})\}_{i=1}^{N}
is said to be
M
-canonical if

P_{i}
is an
M
-type for every
1\leq i\leq N
. For
M
-canonical
(N,\varepsilon,\delta)
-ID code
with
\varepsilon+\delta<1
, it is not difficult to see that all
P_{i}
s are distinct; in fact, if there exist
i
and
j

such that
P_{i}=P_{j}
, then

\displaystyle 1-\varepsilon\leq P_{i}W({\mathcal{D}}_{i})=P_{j}W({\mathcal{D}}_{i})\leq\delta,

which contradict
\varepsilon+\delta<1
. Since the number of
M
-types on
{\mathcal{X}}

is at most
|{\mathcal{X}}|^{M}
, we must have
N\leq|{\mathcal{X}}|^{M}
for
M
-canonical ID code.

In
[
12
]
, among other motivations, the channel resolvability was introduced as a tool handle
general ID codes by relating their analysis to that of
M
-canonical codes.
In the channel resolvability problem, we shall approximate the output distribution
PW

of an arbitrarily given input distribution
P\in{\mathcal{P}}({\mathcal{X}})
by the output distribution
\tilde{P}W
of an
M
-type
\tilde{P}

so that

\displaystyle d(\tilde{P}W,PW)\leq\zeta

is satisfied for a prescribed approximation error
\zeta
. If such an approximation is realized, then we can replace
each
P_{i}
with an
M
-type
\tilde{P}_{i}
, and use the above mentioned counting argument for
M
-canonical codes.

In an attempt to derive a tighter converse bound than that in
[
12
]
,
the partial channel resolvability was introduced in
[
24
]
.
For a given subset
{\cal S}\subset{\cal X}\times{\cal Y}
, let us introduce the truncated channel

\displaystyle W^{\mathcal{S}}(y|x):=W(y|x)\mathbf{1}[(x,y)\in{\cal S}]

and the truncated output distribution

\displaystyle PW^{\cal S}(y):=\sum_{x\in{\cal X}}P(x)W(y|x)\mathbf{1}[(x,y)\in{\cal S}].

Note that
PW^{\mathcal{S}}
is a sub-distribution, i.e., it may not add up to
1
, and
it is referred to as the partial response of the input distribution
P
.
It can be immediately verified that

\displaystyle d(PW^{\cal S},PW)=\frac{P\times W({\cal S}^{c})}{2}.

(3)

In the partial channel resolvability problem, we shall approximate the partial response
PW^{\mathcal{S}}

of an arbitrarily given input distribution
P\in{\mathcal{P}}({\mathcal{X}})
by the partial response
\tilde{P}W^{\mathcal{S}}
of
an
M
-type
\tilde{P}
so that

\displaystyle d(\tilde{P}W^{\mathcal{S}},PW^{\mathcal{S}})\leq\zeta

is satisfied for a prescribed approximation error
\zeta
.

A standard approach of constructing the (partial) channel resolvability code
is to randomly generate
M
symbols
x_{1},\ldots,x_{M}
according to distribution
P
.
The performance analysis of such a random code construction is referred to as the soft covering lemma
[
9
]
.
The following lemma is a variant of the soft covering lemma, and it can be derived in almost the same manner
as
[

with a simple modification.
Even though the modified version is available in the literature

Lemma 3

For arbitrarily given
Q\in{\cal P}({\cal Y})
and
\gamma\in\mathbb{R}

\displaystyle{\cal S}={\cal S}(Q,\gamma):=\bigg\{(x,y)\in{\cal X}\times{\cal Y}:\log\frac{W(y|x)}{Q(y)}\leq\gamma\bigg\}.

(4)

Then, for a given input distribution
P\in{\cal P}({\cal X})

\tilde{P}

\displaystyle d(\tilde{P}W^{\cal S},PW^{\cal S})\leq\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\frac{e^{\gamma}}{M}}

(5)

Proof.

Let
{\mathcal{C}}=\{X_{1},\ldots,X_{M}\}
be a codebook such that each
X_{i}
is randomly generated with distribution
P
.
Then, we define
M
-type
\tilde{P}=\tilde{P}_{\mathcal{C}}
by

\displaystyle\tilde{P}(x)=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{i=1}^{M}\mathbf{1}[X_{i}=x].

We shall evaluate the approximation error averaged over the random generation of the codebook
{\mathcal{C}}

By Jensen’s inequality and the convexity of

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{{\mathcal{C}}}\big[d(\tilde{P}W^{\mathcal{S}},PW^{\mathcal{S}})\big]^{2}\leq\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{C}}\big[d(\tilde{P}W^{\mathcal{S}},PW^{\mathcal{S}})^{2}\big].

(6)

Then, we have

\displaystyle 4\mathbb{E}\big[d(\tilde{P}W^{\mathcal{S}},PW^{\mathcal{S}})^{2}\big]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{{\mathcal{C}}}\bigg[\bigg(\sum_{y}\big|\tilde{P}W^{\mathcal{S}}(y)-PW^{\mathcal{S}}(y)\big|\bigg)^{2}\bigg]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{{\mathcal{C}}}\bigg[\bigg(\sum_{y}\sqrt{Q(y)}\sqrt{Q(y)}\bigg|\frac{\tilde{P}W^{\mathcal{S}}(y)-PW^{\mathcal{S}}(y)}{Q(y)}\bigg|\bigg)^{2}\bigg]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}_{{\mathcal{C}}}\bigg[\sum_{y}Q(y)\bigg|\frac{\tilde{P}W^{\mathcal{S}}(y)-PW^{\mathcal{S}}(y)}{Q(y)}\bigg|^{2}\bigg],

where the summation

VII
Discussion

In this paper, we have derived a minimax converse bound for the identification via channel.
By using this converse bound, we have derived the general formula for the identification capacity without the assumption
of the strong converse property; the problem has been unsolved for a long time.
Our converse is built upon the partial channel resolvability introduced in
[
24
]
.
When we derive the converse bound for the identification code using the channel resolvability, a crucial observation
is the counting argument in which the number of messages of the identification code is bounded by the number of
M
-types.
Even though the partial channel resolvability approach have had a potential to improve the bound based on
the channel resolvability, there was a difficulty that the counting argument does not work for the partial channel resolvability, at least without an additional trick;
cf. Remark
2
.
We have overcome this difficulty by utilizing the auxiliary output distribution,
the idea that has become popular in the past decade.
As a future direction, it is tempting to apply the auxiliary output distribution approach
to other problems of identification code.
