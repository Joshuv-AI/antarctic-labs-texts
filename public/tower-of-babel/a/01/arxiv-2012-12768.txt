Near field propulsion forces from nonreciprocal media
David Gelbwaser-Klimovsky, Noah Graham, Mehran Kardar, Matthias Krüger

Abstract
Arguments based on symmetry and thermodynamics may suggest the existence of a ratchet-like lateral Casimir force between two plates at different temperatures and with broken inversion symmetry. We find that this is not sufficient, and at least one plate must be made of nonreciprocal material. This setup operates as a heat engine by transforming heat radiation into mechanical force. Although the ratio of the lateral force to heat transfer in the near field regime diverges inversely with the plates separation, d d , an Onsager symmetry, which we extend to nonreciprocal plates, limits the engine efficiency to the Carnot value η c \eta_{c} . The optimal velocity of operation in the far field is of the order of c ​ η c c\eta_{c} , where c c is the speed of light. In the near field regime, this velocity can be reduced to the order of ω ¯ ​ d ​ η c \bar{\omega}d\eta_{c} , where ω ¯ \bar{\omega} is a typical material frequency.

Introduction

Fluctuations of electromagnetic fields lead to a variety of phenomena, from Planck’s law of thermal radiation
Planck 1901
, to the fluctuation-induced normal forces predicted by Casimir in 1948
Casimir 1948
and later generalized by Lifshitz
Lifshitz 1956
. In 1971, Polder and van Hove discussed near field effects in radiative heat transfer between closely spaced objects
Polder and Van Hove 1971
, demonstrating a dramatic increase from the far field.
These effects where observed experimentally for normal forces at the end of the last century
Lamoreaux 1997
;
Mohideen and Roy 1998
, and roughly ten years later for radiative heat transfer
Shen et al. 2009
;
Rousseau et al. 2009
;
Biehs et al. 2020
. A variety of related effects arise for Casimir forces in systems out of equilibrium
Antezza et al. 2008
;
Bimonte 2009
;
Messina and Antezza 2011a
;
Krüger et al. 2011
;
Messina and Antezza 2011b
;
Krüger et al. 2012
;
Narayanaswamy and Zheng 2014
;
Bimonte et al. 2017
, such as torques
Reid et al. 2017
;
Guo and Fan 2020
and propulsion forces
Müller and Krüger 2016
, in both near and far field regimes.

Lateral Casimir forces, which can propel plates with respect to each other, were also predicted
between corrugated plates in equilibrium
Golestanian and Kardar 1997
;
Emig et al. 2001
,
and observed experimentally
Chen et al. 2002
;
Banishev et al. 2013
.
However, in these and several other
equilibrium and non-equilibrium settings
Ashourvan et al. 2007
;
Chiu et al. 2010
;
Müller and Krüger 2016
;
Hassani Gangaraj et al. 2018
,
either the shape or rotation of an object establishes the direction of the propulsive force.
In contrast, we consider here a
translationally invariant object
,
and demonstrate that a lateral (potentially propulsive) force can arise by taking advantage of radiative heat transfer involving
non-reciprocal
materials.
Nonreciprocal media have indeed been shown to give rise to a variety of other interesting phenomena
Zhu and Fan 2016
;
Herz and Biehs 2019
;
Latella and Ben-Abdallah 2017
;
Ben-Abdallah 2016
.

In this Letter, we demonstrate that non-equilibrium propulsive forces can be used to build a heat engine without any contact between its parts and whose optimal operation strongly depends on the type of radiation driving its heat transfer. In the far-field limit, the on-shell photon energy-momentum relation bounds the ratio of propulsive force to heat transfer, requiring operational velocities of the order of the speed of light, limiting the usefulness of such an engine. In the near field, the on-shell relation does not hold, allowing the engine to operate at efficiencies close to the Carnot bound at much lower velocities.

In summary, we have shown that for the case of two smooth plates at different temperatures, non-equilibrium fluctuations will cause a motive force if at least one of the plates is made of a nonreciprocal material. Although for large plate separation the on-shell energy-momentum photon relation limits the ratio between motive force and heat transfer, at small separations, the heat transfer and propulsion force are dominated by evanescent modes, allowing this ratio to grow inversely with the distance between the two plates. The velocity scale corresponding to maximal efficiency and power is thus linear in that distance.

\displaystyle=\frac{-2\hbar}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}\!\!\!\!\!d\omega\frac{1}{e^{\frac{\hbar\omega}{k_{B}T_{1}}}-1}\mbox{Tr}\left\{i\partial_{y}\left[\frac{\mathbb{T}_{2}-\mathbb{T}_{2}^{\dagger}}{2i}-\mathbb{T}_{2}^{\dagger}\imaginary[\mathbb{G}_{0}]\mathbb{T}_{2}\right]\frac{1}{1-\mathbb{G}_{0}\mathbb{T}_{1}\mathbb{G}_{0}\mathbb{T}_{2}}\mathbb{G}_{0}\left[\frac{\mathbb{T}_{1}-\mathbb{T}_{1}^{\dagger}}{2i}-\mathbb{T}_{1}\imaginary[\mathbb{G}_{0}]\mathbb{T}_{1}^{\dagger}\right]\mathbb{G}^{*}_{0}\frac{1}{1-\mathbb{T}^{\dagger}_{2}\mathbb{G}^{*}_{0}\mathbb{T}^{\dagger}_{1}\mathbb{G}^{*}_{0}}\right\}

(22)

This is Eq. (1) in the main text.

I.2
Positivity of
\hat{\mathbb{R}}_{2}

We consider an object with
\mathbb{V}=\tilde{\mathbb{V}}(x,x^{\prime},z,z^{\prime})\delta(y-y^{\prime})
. We can write, using an expansion in Fourier modes along
y
,

\displaystyle\frac{\mathbb{V}-\mathbb{V}^{\dagger}}{2i}=\int\frac{dk_{y}}{2\pi}\frac{\tilde{\mathbb{V}}-\tilde{\mathbb{V}}^{\dagger}}{2i}e^{ik_{y}(y-y^{\prime})}

(23)

Importantly, we have
l\mbox{Tr}[\mathbb{V}-\mathbb{V}^{\dagger}]=\mbox{Tr}[\tilde{\mathbb{V}}-\tilde{\mathbb{V}}^{\dagger}]
, where
l
is a length scale that formally goes to zero. It can be understood when introducing an upper cut off
\sim{1/l}
in Eq. (
23
). We thus have
\mbox{Tr}[(\tilde{\mathbb{V}}-\tilde{\mathbb{V}}^{\dagger})/2i]\geq 0
.

Furthermore, for such potential,
\mathbb{G}=\mathbb{G}(y-y^{\prime})
, so that

\displaystyle\mathbb{G}

\displaystyle=\int\frac{dk_{y}}{2\pi}\tilde{\mathbb{G}}(k_{y})e^{ik_{y}(y-y^{\prime})}

(24)

\displaystyle\mathbb{G}^{\dagger}

\displaystyle=\int\frac{dk_{y}}{2\pi}[\tilde{\mathbb{G}}(k_{y})]^{\dagger}e^{ik_{y}(y-y^{\prime})}.

(25)

We finally have
\tilde{\mathbb{R}}_{2}
defined in the main text,

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathbb{R}}_{2}=\tilde{\mathbb{G}}\frac{\tilde{\mathbb{V}}-\tilde{\mathbb{V}}^{\dagger}}{2i}\tilde{\mathbb{G}}^{\dagger}\geq 0.

(26)

And

\displaystyle\hat{\mathbb{R}}_{2}=e^{ik_{y}y}\tilde{\mathbb{R}}_{2}e^{-ik_{y}y^{\prime}}\geq 0.

(27)

I.3
Onsager relation and friction

Generalizing the results of Ref.
Golyk et al. 2013
for non-reciprocal objects yields the following result for the friction tensor, relating the force acting on object 2 in response to a velocity of object 1 (both for
y
-direction, both objects and environment have same temperature
T_{2}
)

{\Gamma}_{1}^{(2)}=-\frac{2\hbar}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}d\omega\frac{\partial n_{2}}{\partial\omega}\mbox{Tr}\left\{\partial^{2}_{y}\left[\frac{\mathbb{T}_{2}-\mathbb{T}_{2}^{\dagger}}{2i}-\mathbb{T}_{2}^{\dagger}\imaginary[\mathbb{G}_{0}]\mathbb{T}_{2}\right]\frac{1}{1-\mathbb{G}_{0}\mathbb{T}_{1}\mathbb{G}_{0}\mathbb{T}_{2}}\mathbb{G}_{0}\left[\frac{\mathbb{T}_{1}-\mathbb{T}_{1}^{\dagger}}{2i}-\mathbb{T}_{1}\imaginary[\mathbb{G}_{0}]\mathbb{T}_{1}^{\dagger}\right]\mathbb{G}^{*}_{0}\frac{1}{1-\mathbb{T}^{\dagger}_{2}\mathbb{G}^{*}_{0}\mathbb{T}^{\dagger}_{1}\mathbb{G}^{*}_{0}}\right\}.\\

(28)

We used that both object are translationally invariant in direction
y
, so that
\partial_{y}
can be moved through. We continue by regarding only effects for the inside between plates, for which forces are equal and opposite. The part given here
{\Gamma}_{1}^{(2)}=-\Gamma
is thus the negative of
\Gamma
in Eq. (18). The relation between
F_{y}
and
\Gamma
given in the main text is apparent.

Similarly, the heat transfer absorbed by object two changes with
v
in the following way,

\displaystyle\left.\frac{\partial{\cal H}^{(2)}}{\partial v}\right|_{v=0}=-\frac{2\hbar}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}d\omega\omega\frac{\partial n_{2}}{\partial\omega}\mbox{Tr}\left\{i\partial_{y}\left[\frac{\mathbb{T}_{2}-\mathbb{T}_{2}^{\dagger}}{2i}-\mathbb{T}_{2}^{\dagger}\imaginary[\mathbb{G}_{0}]\mathbb{T}_{2}\right]\frac{1}{1-\mathbb{G}_{0}\mathbb{T}_{1}\mathbb{G}_{0}\mathbb{T}_{2}}\mathbb{G}_{0}\left[\frac{\mathbb{T}_{1}-\mathbb{T}_{1}^{\dagger}}{2i}-\mathbb{T}_{1}\imaginary[\mathbb{G}_{0}]\mathbb{T}_{1}^{\dagger}\right]\mathbb{G}^{*}_{0}\frac{1}{1-\mathbb{T}^{\dagger}_{2}\mathbb{G}^{*}_{0}\mathbb{T}^{\dagger}_{1}\mathbb{G}^{*}_{0}}\right\}.

(29)

The force
F_{y}
can be expanded in temperature difference (again using simplifications of symmetry arising in the setup of two parallel plates). Observing
n_{1}-n_{2}=-\frac{\partial n_{2}}{\partial T_{2}}(T_{2}-T_{1})+\dots=\frac{\omega}{T_{2}}\frac{\partial n_{2}}{\partial\omega}(T_{2}-T_{1})+\dots

I.4
Derivation of Eq. (11)

We calculate
r^{NN}
at
k_{\perp}\gg\frac{\omega}{c}|\epsilon_{ij}|
for an interface between the vacuum and a plane with normal vector parallel to
\hat{z}
.
r^{NN}=\frac{n_{r}}{n_{i}}
where
n_{r}
is the amplitude of the electric field of the reflected wave,
\mathbf{E_{r}}=n_{r}\frac{c}{\omega}\{k_{z}k_{x},k_{z}k_{y},k^{2}_{\perp}\}
and
n_{i}
is the amplitude of the electric field of the incoming wave,
\mathbf{E_{i}}=n_{i}\frac{c}{\omega}\{-k_{z}k_{x},-k_{z}k_{y},k^{2}_{\perp}\}
.
\mathbf{k_{i}}
and
\mathbf{k_{r}}
are the corresponding wave vectors. At this point we assume that there could be two different transmitted waves
Chen 1981
with wave vectors
\mathbf{k_{+}}
and
\mathbf{k_{-}}
and electric fields
\mathbf{E_{+}}
and
\mathbf{E_{-}}
respectively. For
k_{\perp}\gg\frac{\omega}{c}|\epsilon_{ij}|
the
z
-component of the transmitted wave vectors can be approximated as
ik_{\perp}
and
\sqrt{-k_{y}^{2}-k_{x}^{2}\frac{\epsilon_{p}}{\epsilon_{d}}}
. Using the fact that the wave vector is perpendicular to the electric field,
\mathbf{k_{+}}\cdot\mathbf{E_{+}}=\mathbf{k_{-}}\cdot\mathbf{E_{-}}=0
with the standard boundaries conditions that are used for calculating the Fresnel coefficients, that is:

\displaystyle\left(\mathbf{E_{i}}+\mathbf{E_{r}}-\bbeps\mathbf{E_{+}}-\bbeps\mathbf{E_{-}}\right)\cdot\hat{z}=0

(30)

\displaystyle\left(\mathbf{k_{i}}\times\mathbf{E_{i}}+\mathbf{k_{r}}\times\mathbf{E_{r}}-\mathbf{k_{+}}\times\mathbf{E_{+}}-\mathbf{k_{-}}\times\mathbf{E_{-}}\right)\cdot\hat{z}=0

(31)

\displaystyle\left(\mathbf{E_{i}}+\mathbf{E_{r}}-\mathbf{E_{+}}-\mathbf{E_{-}}\right)\times\hat{z}=0

(32)

\displaystyle\left(\mathbf{k_{i}}\times\mathbf{E_{i}}+\mathbf{k_{r}}\times\mathbf{E_{r}}-\mathbf{k_{+}}\times\mathbf{E_{+}}-\mathbf{k_{-}}\times\mathbf{E_{-}}\right)\times\hat{z}=0

(33)

one can find all the components of
\mathbf{E_{+}}
and
\mathbf{E_{-}}
and also Eq. (11) in the main text.

I.7
Symmetry relations for heat transfer and consequences for motive forces

I.7.1
General

We introduce the following exact expressions for the heat
H_{i}^{(2)}
emitted by object
i
(including environment) and absorbed by object 2. These expressions are generalizations of the ones given in Ref.
Krüger et al. 2011
, and are valid for objects of arbitrary shapes and reciprocal or nonreciprocal media. (with
\mathbb{R}_{1}
,
\mathbb{R}_{2}
and
\mathbb{W}
given in the main text)

\displaystyle H_{1}^{(2)}(T)

\displaystyle=\frac{2\hbar}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}d\omega\frac{\omega}{e^{\frac{\hbar\omega}{k_{B}T}}-1}\mbox{Tr}\left\{\mathbb{R}_{2}\mathbb{W}\mathbb{R}_{1}\mathbb{W}^{\dagger}\right\}

(35)

\displaystyle H_{2}^{(2)}(T)

\displaystyle=\frac{2\hbar}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}d\omega\frac{\omega}{e^{\frac{\hbar\omega}{k_{B}T}}-1}\mbox{Tr}\left\{\frac{\mathbb{G}_{1}-\mathbb{G}^{\dagger}_{1}}{2i}\frac{1}{1-\mathbb{T}_{2}\mathbb{G}_{0}\mathbb{T}_{1}\mathbb{G}_{0}}\left[\frac{\mathbb{T}_{2}-\mathbb{T}_{2}^{\dagger}}{2i}-\mathbb{T}_{2}\imaginary[\mathbb{G}_{0}]\mathbb{T}_{2}^{\dagger}\right]\frac{1}{1-\mathbb{G}^{*}_{0}\mathbb{T}^{\dagger}_{1}\mathbb{G}^{*}_{0}\mathbb{T}_{2}^{\dagger}}\right\}

(36)

\displaystyle=\frac{-2\hbar}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}d\omega\frac{\omega}{e^{\frac{\hbar\omega}{k_{B}T}}-1}\mbox{Tr}\left\{\mathbb{R}_{2}\mathbb{W}\frac{\mathbb{G}_{1}-\mathbb{G}^{\dagger}_{1}}{2i}\mathbb{W}^{\dagger}\right\}

(37)

\displaystyle H_{env}^{(2)}(T)

\displaystyle=\frac{2\hbar}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}d\omega\frac{\omega}{e^{\frac{\hbar\omega}{k_{B}T}}-1}\mbox{Tr}\left\{\mathbb{R}_{2}\mathbb{W}(1+\mathbb{G}_{0}\mathbb{T}_{1})\imaginary[\mathbb{G}_{0}](1+\mathbb{T}_{1}^{\dagger}\mathbb{G}_{0}^{*})\mathbb{W}^{\dagger}\right\}

(38)

The equality of Eqs. (
36
) and (
37
) can be shown in a tedious, but straightforward calculation. (Note that the operator in square brackets of Eq. (
36
) is different from
\mathbb{R}_{2}
.)
We now transform all operators in Fourier space along
y
,

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathbb{O}}(k_{y})

\displaystyle=\int d{y}e^{-ik_{y}y}\mathbb{O}(y)

(39)

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathbb{O}}^{\dagger}(k_{y})

\displaystyle=\int d{y}e^{-ik_{y}y}\mathbb{O}^{\dagger}(y)

(40)

So that

\displaystyle\mbox{Tr}\left\{\mathbb{R}_{2}\mathbb{W}\mathbb{R}_{1}\mathbb{W}^{\dagger}\right\}

\displaystyle=\int\frac{dk_{y}}{2\pi}\mbox{Tr}\left\{e^{-ik_{y}y}{\tilde{\mathbb{R}}}_{2}\tilde{\mathbb{W}}\tilde{\mathbb{R}}_{1}\tilde{\mathbb{W}}^{\dagger}e^{ik_{y}y^{\prime}}\right\}=\int\frac{dk_{y}}{2\pi}S_{1}^{(2)}(k_{y})

(41)

\displaystyle\mbox{Tr}\left\{\mathbb{R}_{2}\mathbb{W}\frac{\mathbb{G}_{1}-\mathbb{G}^{\dagger}_{1}}{2i}\mathbb{W}^{\dagger}\right\}

\displaystyle=\int\frac{dk_{y}}{2\pi}\mbox{Tr}\left\{e^{-ik_{y}y}{\tilde{\mathbb{R}}}_{2}\tilde{\mathbb{W}}\frac{\tilde{\mathbb{G}}_{1}-\tilde{\mathbb{G}^{\dagger}_{1}}}{2i}\tilde{\mathbb{W}}^{\dagger}e^{ik_{y}y^{\prime}}\right\}=-\int\frac{dk_{y}}{2\pi}S_{2}^{(2)}(k_{y})

(42)

\displaystyle\mbox{Tr}\left\{\mathbb{R}_{2}\mathbb{W}\mathbb{G}_{1}\imaginary[\mathbb{G}_{0}]\mathbb{G}^{\dagger}_{1}\mathbb{W}^{\dagger}\right\}

\displaystyle=\int\frac{dk_{y}}{2\pi}\mbox{Tr}\left\{e^{-ik_{y}y}{\tilde{\mathbb{R}}}_{2}\tilde{\mathbb{W}}\tilde{\mathbb{G}}_{1}\frac{\tilde{\mathbb{G}}_{0}-\tilde{\mathbb{G}^{\dagger}_{0}}}{2i}\tilde{\mathbb{G}}^{\dagger}_{1}\tilde{\mathbb{W}}^{\dagger}e^{ik_{y}y^{\prime}}\right\}=\int\frac{dk_{y}}{2\pi}S_{env}^{(2)}(k_{y})

(43)

we note that all operators, even if reciprocal in real speace, may not be reciprocal for mode
k_{y}
, such as
\tilde{\mathbb{G}}_{0}
.

I.7.2
Absence of equilibrium transfer per wavevector for reciprocal and nonreciprocal media

Another tedious, but straight forward computation shows that

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1,2,env}S_{i}^{(2)}(k_{y})=0.

(44)

This shows that, in equilibrium, no object heats up or cools down,
per wavevector
k_{y}
. One may interpret that the second law of thermodynamics holds per wavevector
k_{y}

Fan et al. 2020
. This statement, which holds for reciprocal and non-reciprocal objects, was proven for two parallel plates in
Fan et al. 2020
. Notably, the generalization for arbitrary objects involves the environment, and does not (at least not obviously) hold pairwise for the two objects involved. In case that radiation to or from the environment can be neglected, we have

\displaystyle S_{1}^{(2)}(k_{y})=-S_{2}^{(2)}(k_{y})=S_{2}^{(1)}(k_{y}).

(45)

In the last equality, we used conservation of energy for two objects.

I.7.3
Detailed balance per wavevector for reciprocal media

For reciprocal media, all operators involved are symmetric. For such symmetric, translationally invariant operator
\mathbb{O}
(free Green’s function or
\mathbb{T}
), one has

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathbb{O}}(k_{y})

\displaystyle=\int d{y}e^{-ik_{y}y}\mathbb{O}(y)=\int d{y}e^{ik_{y}y}\mathbb{O}(-y)=\int d{y}e^{ik_{y}y}\mathbb{O}^{T}(y)=\tilde{\mathbb{O}}^{T}(-k_{y}).

(46)

We introduce slightly different notation compared to the main text: The emission operator

\mathbb{M}_{i}=\mathbb{G}_{0}\left[\frac{\mathbb{T}_{i}-\mathbb{T}_{i}^{\dagger}}{2i}-\mathbb{T}_{i}\imaginary[\mathbb{G}_{0}]\mathbb{T}_{i}^{\dagger}\right]\mathbb{G}^{*}_{0}
and the absorption operator
\mathbb{{A}}_{i}=\mathbb{G}_{0}^{*}\left[\frac{\mathbb{T}_{i}-\mathbb{T}_{i}^{\dagger}}{2i}-\mathbb{T}_{i}^{\dagger}\imaginary[\mathbb{G}_{0}]\mathbb{T}_{i}\right]\mathbb{G}_{0}

and the multiple scattering operator
\mathbb{W}_{ij}=\mathbb{G}_{0}^{-1}(1-\mathbb{G}_{0}\mathbb{T}_{i}\mathbb{G}_{0}\mathbb{T}_{j})^{-1}
.
We have, in Fourier space

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathbb{M}}_{i}^{*}(-k_{y})

\displaystyle=\tilde{\mathbb{A}}_{i}(k_{y})

(47)

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathbb{A}}_{i}^{*}(-k_{y})

\displaystyle=\tilde{\mathbb{M}}_{i}(k_{y})

(48)

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathbb{W}}_{21}^{*}(-k_{y})

\displaystyle=\tilde{\mathbb{W}}_{12}^{\dagger}(k_{y})

(49)

\displaystyle(\tilde{\mathbb{W}}_{21}^{\dagger})^{*}(-k_{y})

\displaystyle=\tilde{\mathbb{W}}_{12}(k_{y})

(50)

Using it, and the fact that the expression is real, we have

\displaystyle S_{1}^{(2)}(k_{y})=\mbox{Tr}\left\{\tilde{\mathbb{A}}_{2}(k_{y})\tilde{\mathbb{W}}_{12}(k_{y})\tilde{\mathbb{M}}_{1}(k_{y})\tilde{\mathbb{W}}^{\dagger}_{12}(k_{y})\right\}=\mbox{Tr}\left\{\tilde{\mathbb{M}}_{2}(-k_{y})\tilde{\mathbb{W}}^{\dagger}_{21}(-k_{y})\tilde{\mathbb{A}}_{1}(-k_{y})\tilde{\mathbb{W}}_{21}(-k_{y})\right\}=S_{2}^{(1)}(-k_{y}).

(51)

I.7.4
Consequences for forces

If radiation from environment can be neglected, for reciprocal media, we have, combining Eqs. (
45
) and (
51
),

\displaystyle S_{1}^{(2)}(k_{y})=S_{1}^{(2)}(-k_{y}).

(52)

Using Eq. (3) in the main text shows that in this case, the motive force vanishes. Motive forces for reciprocal media, in a setting of translational invariance along
y
, can only, if at all, result from far field radiation from the environment. It is thus not possible to obtain near field propulsion forces for nonreciprocal media.
