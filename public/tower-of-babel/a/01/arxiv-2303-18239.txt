Well-posedness results for a new class of stochastic spatio-temporal SIR-type models driven by proportional pure-jump Lévy noise
Mohamed Mehdaoui †

Abstract
. This paper provides a first attempt to incorporate the massive discontinuous changes in the spatio-temporal dynamics of epidemics. Namely, we propose an extended class of epidemic models, governed by coupled stochastic semilinear partial differential equations, driven by pure-jump Lévy noise. Based on the considered type of incidence functions, by virtue of semi-group theory, a truncation technique and Banach fixed point theorem, we prove the existence and pathwise uniqueness of mild solutions, depending continuously on the initial datum. Moreover, by means of a regularization technique, based on the resolvent operator, we acquire that mild solutions can be approximated by a suitable converging sequence of strong solutions. With this result at hand, for positive initial states, we derive the almost-sure positiveness of the obtained solutions. Finally, we present the outcome of several numerical simulations, in order to exhibit the effect of the considered type of stochastic noise, in comparison to Gaussian noise, which has been used in the previous literature. Our established results lay the ground-work for investigating other problems associated with the new proposed class of epidemic models, such as asymptotic behavior analyses, optimal control as well as identification problems, which primarily rely on the existence and uniqueness of biologically feasible solutions.

1.
Introduction and motivation

As a scientific tool, mathematical modeling of infectious diseases has attracted the attention of several researchers throughout the decades. Primarily, due to its capacity of using several mathematical objects, in order to convert the biological real problem into a mathematical one, which once solved, through a deductive reasoning, provides valuable information that can be interpreted and analyzed, leading to the better understanding and identification of different factors altering the evolution of a given disease within a studied population. In this framework, to describe the propagation of the Plague, compartmental modeling was introduced in 1927 by Kermack and McKendrick
[
1
]
. The primal idea of their approach was to divide the population under study into three compartments, denoted as
S(t)
,
I(t)
and
R(t)
, which stand, at a given time
t>0
, for the individuals susceptible to the disease, those infected with it, and last, those who recover or die from the disease and are thus removed from the studied population. Then, by introducing two positive parameters
\beta
and
\gamma,
which stand for the disease transmission and recovery rates, respectively, they proposed a model which is governed by the following system of coupled nonlinear ordinary differential equations:

(1.1)

\begin{cases}\begin{split}&\dfrac{dS(t)}{dt}=-\beta S(t)I(t),\\[6.45831pt]
&\dfrac{dI(t)}{dt}=\beta S(t)I(t)-\gamma I(t),\\[6.45831pt]
&\dfrac{dR(t)}{dt}=\gamma I(t).\end{split}\end{cases}

Compartmental epidemic models which are governed by coupled nonlinear ordinary differential equations, follow a deterministic approach which assumes that the susceptible, infected and recovered populations’ densities, are completely and surely determined, provided that the parameters and initial data are known. However, as it has been demonstrated by May
[
2
]
and Mao
et al.

[
3
]
, the random fluctuations play a major role in the dynamics of ecological and epidemiological systems. Taking this fact into account, Jiang
et al.

[
4
]
extended the standard deterministic SIR model by taking the random fluctuations into account and by introducing three additional positive parameters
\Lambda,\mu,
and
\epsilon
, standing for the birth rate, natural death rate and the rate of death caused by the disease. Namely, the authors considered the following stochastic model:

(1.2)

\begin{cases}\begin{split}&{dS(t)}=(\Lambda-\beta S(t)I(t)-\mu S(t))dt+\sigma_{1}S(t)dB_{1}(t),\\[6.88889pt]
&{dI(t)}=(\beta S(t)I(t)-(\gamma+\mu+\epsilon)I(t))dt+\sigma_{2}I(t)dB_{2}(t),\\[6.88889pt]
&{dR(t)}=(\gamma I(t)-\mu R(t))dt+\sigma_{3}R(t)dB_{3}(t),\end{split}\end{cases}

where
(B_{i}(t))_{i\in\{1,2,3\}}
are independent Brownian motions with corresponding noise intensities
(\sigma_{i}^{2})_{i\in\{1,2,3\}}
.

The sudden severe environmental changes, which are exhibited for instance by volcanoes, hurricanes and tornadoes, may cause the discontinuity of solutions. In this case, Gaussian noise is no longer suitable. To overcome this limitation, Zhang and Wang
[
5
]
extended Model (
1.2
) by considering, in addition to multiplicative Gaussian noise, a Lévy-type jump process, as follows:

(1.3)

\begin{cases}\begin{split}&\displaystyle{dS(t)}=(\Lambda-\beta S(t)I(t)-\mu S(t))dt+\sigma_{1}S(t)dB_{1}(t)+\int_{\mathbb{Z}}C_{1}(z)S(t-)\tilde{N}(dt,dz),\\
&{dI(t)}=(\beta S(t)I(t)-(\gamma+\mu+\epsilon)I(t))dt+\sigma_{2}I(t)dB_{2}(t)+\int_{\mathbb{Z}}C_{2}(z)I(t-)\tilde{N}(dt,dz),\\
&{dR(t)}=(\gamma I(t)-\mu R(t))dt+\sigma_{3}R(t)dB_{3}(t)+\int_{\mathbb{Z}}C_{3}(z)R(t-)\tilde{N}(dt,dz),\end{split}\end{cases}

where
S(t-)
,
I(t-)
and
R(t-)
stand for the right limits of
S(t),

I(t)
and
R(t),
respectively.
\tilde{N}(dt,dz)
is the compensated Poisson process, with characteristic measure
\nu
defined on a measurable subset
\mathbb{Z}\subset[0,+\infty),
such that
\nu(\mathbb{Z})<+\infty
. Additionally,
C_{i}(z)>-1
for
i\in\{1,2,3\}.

For infectious diseases, the movements of individuals within the population contribute to a great extent. Hence, time-dependent epidemic models are no longer qualified due to omitting the spatial factor. Taking this fact into account, Webb
[
6
]
considered in a one spatial dimension, the following adapted SIR model:

(1.4)

\begin{cases}\begin{split}&\partial_{t}S(x,t)-\partial_{xx}S(x,t)=-\beta S(x,t)I(x,t),&\quad-L<x<L,\quad t>0,\\[6.45831pt]
&\partial_{t}I(x,t)-\partial_{xx}I(x,t)=\beta S(x,t)I(x,t)-\gamma I(x,t),&\quad-L<x<L,\quad t>0,\\[6.45831pt]
&\partial_{t}R(x,t)-\partial_{xx}R(x,t)=\gamma I(x,t),&\quad-L<x<L,\quad t>0,\end{split}\end{cases}

equipped with the positive initial conditions:

(1.5)

S(x,0)\geq 0,\quad I(x,0)\geq 0\quad\text{and}\quad R(x,0)\geq 0,\quad-L<x<L,

and the following homogeneous Neumann boundary conditions:

(1.6)

\partial_{x}S(\pm L,t)=\partial_{x}I(\pm L,t)=\partial_{x}R(\pm L,t)=0,\quad t>0,

where
L>0
,
\partial_{x}
and
\partial_{t}
stand for the first-order partial derivatives with respect to the spatial and time variables, respectively, while
\partial_{xx}
stands for the second-order derivative with respect to the spatial variable.

In order to incorporate the spatial factor in stochastic epidemic models, Nguyen
et al.

[
7
]
considered the following SIS model:

(1.7)

\begin{cases}\begin{split}&\begin{split}dS(t,x)&=\big[k_{1}\Delta S(t,x)+\Lambda(x)-\mu_{1}(x)S(t,x)-\frac{\alpha(x)S(t,x)I(t,x)}{S(t,x)+I(t,x)}\\[6.45831pt]
&+\gamma(x)I(t,x)\big]dt+S(t,x)dW_{1}(t,x),\end{split}&\;\text{in}\;\mathbb{R}^{+}\times\mathcal{O},\\[6.45831pt]
&\begin{split}dI(t,x)&=\big[k_{2}\Delta I(t,x)-\mu_{2}(x)I(t,x)+\frac{\alpha(x)S(t,x)I(t,x)}{S(t,x)+I(t,x)}\\
&-\gamma(x)I(t,x)\big]dt+I(t,x)dW_{2}(t,x),\end{split}&\;\text{in}\;\mathbb{R}^{+}\times\mathcal{O},\end{split}\end{cases}

equipped with the following homogeneous Neumann boundary conditions:

(1.8)

\partial_{\nu}S(t,x)=\partial_{\nu}I(t,x)=0,\quad(t,x)\in\mathbb{R}^{+}\times\partial\mathcal{O},

and the initial conditions:

(1.9)

S(0,x)\geq 0\quad\text{and}\quad I(0,x)\geq 0,\quad x\in\mathcal{O},

where
\mathcal{O}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}(n\geq 1)
is an open bounded set with boundary
\partial\mathcal{O}
of class
C^{2}
,
\partial_{\nu}
is the outward normal derivative on
\partial\mathcal{O}
while
W_{1}(t,x)
and
W_{2}(t,x)
are
L^{2}(\mathcal{O},\mathbb{R})
-valued Wiener processes. For a detailed biological signification of the remaining positive parameters in the stochastic system (
1.7
), we refer to
[
7
]
.

We outline that due to Lyapunov method developed by Mao
[
3
]
, the approach used to establish well-posedness results for a variety of stochastic epidemic models, governed by coupled stochastic nonlinear differential equations, is now well-understood and the main challenge resides in addressing other problems such as extinction, persistence, stability, stationary distribution and periodic solutions (see e.g.
[
8
,
4
,
9
,
10
]
). However, in the case of epidemic models governed by coupled stochastic semilinear partial differential equations, the existence of pathwise unique biologically feasible solutions, alone, can be challenging, since the aforementioned approach fails. This is due to the fact that it is based on the existence and uniqueness of local strong solutions, which are not guaranteed. Furthermore, it requires the application of the infinite dimensional Itô rule
[
11
, Theorem 3.8]
, which is not directly applicable to mild solutions.

In the context of Gaussian noise, the above limitations have been addressed by Nguyen
et al.

[
7
]
in their pioneering work, laying the ground-work for some recently-extended epidemic models developed by Nguyen
et al.

[
12
]
, Hu
et al.

[
13
]
and Shao
et al.

[
14
]
. However, in all the aforementioned models, the chosen type of noise is suitable to model only small fluctuations, and it cannot be used to capture sudden environmental changes with discontinuous arrivals. Therefore, an interesting question is to investigate the mathematical well-posedness and biological feasibility of a new class of spatio-temporal stochastic epidemic models, in which Gaussian noise is replaced with a suitable stochastic jump process, describing such discontinuous random changes. It is worth mentioning that a few researchers have addressed the question of extending deterministic partial differential equations, describing phenomena arising in other fields such as physics and engineering, to the stochastic case, driven by pure-jump Lévy processes. In this regard, we mention the pioneering results of Brzeźniak and Zhu
[
15
]
, for the extension of the deterministic beam model, Jiang
et al.

[
16
]
for the extension of the deterministic wave equation, Liang and Gao
[
17
]
, for the extension of the deterministic wave equation with memory, and Bessaih
et al.

[
18
]
for the extension of equations arising in hydrodynamics.

Taking the above discussion into account, the focal point of this paper is to address the questions of mathematical well-posedness and biological feasibility for a new class of stochastic epidemic models, governed by coupled stochastic semilinear partial differential equations, which are driven by pure-jump Lévy noise. To the very best of our knowledge, this is the first paper to rigorously address these questions and the only existing results in the literature, when it comes to the stochastic case, are the ones where Gaussian noise has been used
[
7
,
12
,
14
,
13
]
.

Let
(\Omega,(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{t\geq 0},\mathbb{P})
be a probability space, with a filtration
\mathbb{F}:=(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
satisfying the usual conditions. The model in question is expressed as follows:

(1.10)

\begin{cases}\begin{split}&\begin{split}dS(t,x)&=\left[d_{1}\Delta S(t,x)+\Lambda(x)-\mu(x)S(t,x)-F(S(t,x),I(t,x))\right]dt\\
&+\int_{\mathbb{Z}}\mathcal{C}_{1}(z,x)S(t-,x)\;\tilde{N}(dt,dz),\end{split}&\quad(t,x)\in\mathbb{R}^{+}\times\mathcal{U},\\[5.59721pt]
&\begin{split}dI(t,x)&=\left[d_{2}\Delta I(t,x)-\mu(x)I(t,x)-\gamma(x)I(t,x)+{F(S(t,x),I(t,x))}\right]dt\\[5.59721pt]
&+\int_{\mathbb{Z}}\mathcal{C}_{2}(z,x)I(t-,x)\;\tilde{N}(dt,dz),\end{split}&\quad(t,x)\in\mathbb{R}^{+}\times\mathcal{U},\\[5.59721pt]
&\begin{split}dR(t,x)&=\left[d_{3}\Delta R(t,x)-\mu(x)R(t,x)+{\gamma(x)I(t,x)}\right]dt\\[2.15277pt]
&+\int_{\mathbb{Z}}\mathcal{C}_{3}(z,x)R(t-,x)\;\tilde{N}(dt,dz),\end{split}&\quad(t,x)\in\mathbb{R}^{+}\times\mathcal{U},\end{split}\end{cases}

equipped with the following homogeneous Neumann boundary conditions:

(1.11)

\partial_{\nu}S(t,x)=\partial_{\nu}I(t,x)=\partial_{\nu}R(t,x)=0,\quad(t,x)\in\mathbb{R}^{+}\times\partial\mathcal{U},

and the positive initial conditions:

(1.12)

S(0,x)=S_{0}(x)\geq 0,\quad I(0,x)=I_{0}(x)\geq 0,\quad\text{and}\quad R(0,x)=R_{0}(x)\geq 0,\quad x\in\mathcal{U},

where
\mathcal{U}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}(n\geq 1)
is an open bounded set with smooth boundary
\partial\mathcal{U}
of class
C^{2},

\Delta
denotes the Laplace operator with respect to the spatial variable and
\partial_{\nu}
denotes the outward normal derivative on
\partial\mathcal{U}.

We mention that the boundary condition (
1.11
) has been used by several authors for a wide range of deterministic epidemic models (see e.g.
[
19
,
20
,
21
,
22
]
and the references therein). From the biological point of view, this condition is suitable when the population under study is assumed to be at lock-down. That is, individuals are not allowed to enter nor cross the boundary of the spatial domain. On the other hand, the motivation behind the initial conditions (
1.12
) is purely biological, since we are dealing with populations’ densities. For more details on this subject, we refer the reader to the interesting monograph given in
[
23
]
.

In this paper, the discontinuous random changes are mathematically modeled by a pure-jump Lévy process. Namely, given a measure space
(\mathbb{Z},\mathcal{Z},\nu),
we denote by
\tilde{N},
the compensated Poisson random measure, defined by:

\tilde{N}((0,t]\times\mathbb{S}):=N((0,t]\times\mathbb{S})-t\nu(\mathbb{S}),\quad\forall t>0,\quad\forall\mathbb{S}\in\mathcal{Z},

where
N
is a Poisson random measure, with a corresponding intensity measure
\nu(.),
which is assumed to be
\sigma
-finite. For a detailed terminology on compensated Poisson random measures, we refer the reader to the monographs given in
[
24
,
25
]
.

In System (
1.10
),
d_{1},d_{2}
and
d_{3}
are the positive spatial diffusion rates of the susceptible, infected and recovered individuals,
\Lambda,

\mu,
and
\gamma
are essentially bounded positive functions, depending on the spatial variable, and their biological signification is the same one considered for Model (
1.2
). For
i\in\{1,2,3\},
the functions
\mathcal{C}_{i}\;:\mathbb{Z}\times\mathcal{U}\times\Omega\longrightarrow\mathbb{R},
model the intensities of the jump noise and are assumed to be
(\mathcal{Z}\times\mathcal{F}_{t})
-measurable and essentially bounded with respect to
(z,x)\in\mathbb{Z}\times\mathcal{U}
.

The disease incidence rate is mathematically modeled by the function

whose expression depends on the incorporated biological characteristics. In this paper, we distinguish two types, based on the following mathematical properties:

(
P
1)

F:[0,+\infty)^{2}\longrightarrow[0,+\infty)
is a globally Lipschitz function. That is,

\exists L_{F}>0,\;\forall s_{1},i_{1},s_{2},i_{2}\in[0,+\infty),\quad|F(s_{1},i_{1})-F(s_{2},i_{2})|\leq L_{F}{\left(|s_{1}-s_{2}|^{2}+|i_{1}-i_{2}|^{2}\right)}^{\frac{1}{2}}.

(
P

F:[0,+\infty)^{2}\longrightarrow[0,+\infty)
is a locally Lipschitz function, satisfying the following growth condition:

\exists C_{F}>0,\quad F(s,i)\leq C_{F}s.

Remark 1.1
.

Below are some examples corresponding to incidence functions satisfying
(P1)
and
(P2)
, respectively:

•

Standard incidence rate
(P1)
:
let

and
\mathbf{D}:=\{(s,i)\in[0,+\infty)^{2}|\;s+i\neq 0\}.
Then, define

0,\quad&\text{otherwise}.\end{split}\end{cases}

•

Holling-type and Crowley-Martin incidence rates

:
let

Then, define

are non-negative real constants satisfying

.

2.
Preliminaries and abstract formulation

Dealing with the mathematical well-posedness and the biological feasibility of Model (
1.10
)-(
1.12
) requires its reformulation in an abstract compact form, which is expressed by a stochastic semilinear evolution equation.

For
p\in(1,\infty],
we begin by setting

\mathbb{L}^{p}(\mathcal{U}):=(L^{p}(\mathcal{U}))^{3}\quad\text{and}\quad\mathbb{W}^{2,p}(\mathcal{U}):=(W^{2,p}(\mathcal{U}))^{3},

as the standard product Lebesgue and Sobolev spaces (see e.g.
[
29
, Chapter 5, p. 245-247]
).

For the particular case
p=2,
we set

\mathbb{H}^{2}(\mathcal{U}):=\mathbb{W}^{2,2}(\mathcal{U})\quad\text{and}\quad\mathbb{H}:=\mathbb{L}^{2}(\mathcal{U}).

The Hilbert space
\mathbb{H}
is equipped with its scalar-product-induced norm:

\|u\|_{\mathbb{H}}^{2}:=\langle u,u\rangle_{\mathbb{H}\times\mathbb{H}}:=\sum_{i=1}^{3}\langle u_{i},u_{i}\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathcal{U})\times L^{2}(\mathcal{U})}:=\sum_{i=1}^{3}\int_{\mathcal{U}}u_{i}^{2}(x)\;dx.

In order to unify the notation, overall throughout the paper, we adopt the following setting:

u\overset{\Delta}{=}(u_{1},u_{2},u_{3})\overset{\Delta}{=}(S,I,R).

Now, consider the following linear operator:

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}:\;

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{A})\subset\mathbb{L}^{p}(\mathcal{U})\longrightarrow\mathbb{L}^{p}(\mathcal{U})

\displaystyle u\mapsto(d_{1}\Delta u_{1},d_{2}\Delta u_{2},d_{3}\Delta u_{3}),

defined on the domain:

(2.1)

\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{A}):=\{u\in\mathbb{W}^{2,p}(\mathcal{U})|\quad\partial_{\nu}u_{i}=0,\;\text{on}\;\partial\mathcal{U},\;\forall i\in\{1,2,3\}\},

equipped with the following graph norm:

\|u\|_{\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{A})}:=\|\mathcal{A}u\|_{\mathbb{L}^{p}(\mathcal{U})}+\|u\|_{\mathbb{L}^{p}(\mathcal{U})},\quad\forall u\in\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{A}).

As a direct consequence of Hille-Yosida theorem
[
30
, Theorem 3.1]
, it is known that the operator
\mathcal{A}
generates in
\mathbb{L}^{p}(\mathcal{U})\;(p\in(1,\infty]),
an analytic
C_{0}
-semigroup
(\mathcal{S}_{p}(t))_{t\geq 0}
. Moreover, it holds that (see e.g.
[
31
, Chapter 4, p. 109]
)

\mathcal{S}_{p}(t)u=\mathcal{S}_{q}(t)u,\quad\forall u\in\mathbb{L}^{p}(\mathcal{U})\cap\mathbb{L}^{q}(\mathcal{U}),\quad\forall p,q\in(1,\infty].

Additionally, the operator

(2.2)

\begin{split}(\mathbb{L}^{p}(\mathcal{U}),&\|.\|_{\mathbb{L}^{p}(\mathcal{U})})\longrightarrow(\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{A}),\|.\|_{\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{A})})\\
&u\mapsto\mathcal{S}_{p}(t)u,\end{split}

is bounded for
t>0
(see e.g.
[
31
, Chapter 4, p. 109]
).

Henceforth, for
p\in(1,\infty],
the semigroup
(\mathcal{S}_{p}(t))_{t\geq 0}
will be simply denoted
(\mathcal{S}(t))_{t\geq 0}
and we shall omit the explicit notation.

For
x\in\mathcal{U},
consider the following functions defined on
\mathbb{R}^{3}
by:

\begin{cases}f_{1}(s,i,r):=\Lambda(x)-F(s,i)-\mu(x)s,\\
f_{2}(s,i,r):=F(s,i)-(\mu(x)+\gamma(x))i,\\
f_{3}(s,i,r):=\gamma(x)i-\mu(x)r.\end{cases}

Additionally, consider the following functions defined on
\mathbb{Z}\times\mathcal{U}\times\mathbb{R}
by:

\begin{cases}g_{1}(z,x,s):=\mathcal{C}_{1}(z,x)s,\\
g_{2}(z,x,i):=\mathcal{C}_{2}(z,x)i,\\
g_{3}(z,x,r):=\mathcal{C}_{3}(z,x)r.\end{cases}

Then, define the operator
\mathcal{F}
by:

•

In the case of incidence functions satisfying
(P1)
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}\;:\;

\displaystyle\mathbb{H}\longrightarrow\mathbb{H}

\displaystyle u\mapsto\textbf{f}(u).

•

In the case of incidence functions satisfying
(P2)
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}\;:\;

\displaystyle\mathbb{L}^{\infty}(\mathcal{U})\longrightarrow\mathbb{L}^{\infty}(\mathcal{U})

\displaystyle u\mapsto\textbf{f}(u).

Additionally, we define

In the case of incidence functions satisfying

:

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}\;:

\displaystyle\;\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{H}\longrightarrow\mathbb{H}

•

In the case of incidence functions satisfying

:

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}\;:

\displaystyle\;\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{L}^{\infty}(\mathcal{U})\longrightarrow\mathbb{L}^{\infty}(\mathcal{U})

\displaystyle(z,u)\mapsto(\textbf{g}_{1}(z,u_{1}),\textbf{g}_{2}(z,u_{2}),\textbf{g}_{3}(z,u_{3})),

where

\displaystyle\textbf{f}_{i}(u):\;

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}\;\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}

(\omega,z)\in\Omega\times\mathbb{Z}
, the function
u_{\omega,z}:\;\mathbb{R}_{+}\ni t\mapsto u_{\omega,z}(t):=u(t,\omega,z)\in\mathbb{R}
is left-continuous.

Finally, we denote by
\mathbb{B}(\mathcal{X}),
the
\sigma
-field generated by open sets in a Banach space
\mathcal{X}
.

Definition 2.1
.

A stochastic process
u:\;\mathbb{R}^{+}\times\Omega\longrightarrow\mathcal{X}
is said to be progressively measurable, if it is
(B([0,t])\times\mathcal{F}_{t}/\mathbb{B(\mathcal{X})})
-measurable for all
t\geq 0
.

Definition 2.2
.

A stochastic process
u:\;\mathbb{R}^{+}\times\Omega\longrightarrow\mathcal{X},
is said to be predictable, if the mapping

u_{t}:\;\Omega\times\mathbb{Z}\ni(\omega,z)\mapsto u_{t}(\omega,z):=u(t,\omega,z)\in\mathcal{X},

is
(\mathbb{P}/\mathbb{B}(\mathcal{X}))
-measurable.

Definition 2.3
.

A mapping
u:\mathbb{R}^{+}\times\Omega\times\mathbb{Z}\longrightarrow\mathcal{X}
is said to be
\mathbb{F}
-predictable (or simply predictable) if it is
(\overline{\mathbb{P}}/\mathbb{B}(\mathcal{X}))
-measurable.

For
T>0,
we define
\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{F}}^{T}
as the space of progressively measurable processes

u:\;\mathbb{R}^{+}\times\Omega\longrightarrow\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{A}),

such that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\int_{0}^{T}\|u(t)\|_{\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{A})}^{2}\;dt<+\infty.

On the other hand, we define
\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{Z}}^{T}
as the space of progressively measurable predictable processes

u:\;\mathbb{R}^{+}\times\Omega\times\mathbb{Z}\longrightarrow{\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{A})},

such that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{Z}}\|u(t,z)\|_{\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{A})}^{2}\;\nu(dz)dt<+\infty.

Here and overall throughout the paper,
\mathbb{E}
will stand for the mathematical expectation.

Definition 2.4
.

Given a time-horizon
(0,T),
we define a global mild solution to Problem (
2.4
) as an
\mathbb{H}
-valued stochastic process
(u(t))_{0\leq t\leq T}
, which is
\mathcal{F}_{t}
-adapted, satisfies the càd-làg property and for which the following assertions are fulfilled:

(1)

\mathcal{S}(t-.)\mathcal{F}(u(.))\in\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{F}}^{t}
and
\quad\mathcal{S}(t-.)\mathcal{G}(z,x,u(.))\in\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{Z}}^{t},\quad\forall 0<t\leq T.

(2)

The following equation:

\displaystyle u(t)=\mathcal{S}(t)u_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}\mathcal{S}(t-s)\mathcal{F}(u(s))ds+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{Z}}\mathcal{S}(t-s)\mathcal{G}(z,x,u(s-))\tilde{N}(ds,dz),\\

holds
\forall 0\leq t\leq T
,
\mathbb{P}
-almost surely (
\mathbb{P}
-a.s).

The global mild solution
u
is said to be pathwise unique, if for every other mild solution
v
, it holds that

(2.5)

\mathbb{P}(u(t)=v(t),\;\forall 0\leq t\leq T)=1.

Definition 2.5
.

Given a time-horizon
(0,T),
we define a global strong solution to Problem (
2.4
) as a
\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{A})
-valued stochastic process
(u(t))_{0\leq t\leq T}
, which is
\mathcal{F}_{t}
-adapted, satisfies the càd-làg property and for which the following assertions are fulfilled:

(1)

\mathcal{S}(t-.)\mathcal{F}(u(.))\in\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{F}}^{t}
and
\quad\mathcal{S}(t-.)\mathcal{G}(z,x,u(.))\in\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{Z}}^{t},\quad\forall 0<t\leq T.

(2)

The following equation:

\displaystyle u(t)=u_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}\mathcal{A}u(s)ds+\int_{0}^{t}\mathcal{F}(u(s))ds+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{Z}}\mathcal{G}(z,x,u(s-))\tilde{N}(ds,dz),\\

holds
\forall 0\leq t\leq T
,
\mathbb{P}
-a.s.

The global strong solution is said to be pathwise unique if Property (
2.5
) is satisfied, given two strong solutions
u
and
v
.

3.
Mathematical well-posedness of Model (
1.10
)-(
1.12
)

Given a time-horizon
(0,T),
an initial state and input parameters, the mathematical well-posedness of Model (
1.10
)-(
1.12
) relies on the existence of a pathwise unique mild solution to Problem (
2.4
), depending continuously on the initial state.

3.1.
Well-posedness results in the case of incidence functions satisfying
(P1)

Throughout this subsection, the incidence function is assumed to satisfy
(P1)
while the initial condition
u_{0}
is assumed to belong to the space
\mathbb{H}
. The main tool that we employ to address the existence and pathwise uniqueness of a global mild solution in this case, is Banach fixed point theorem. The idea is to consider a mapping, defined on a suitable Banach space, and whose fixed point characterizes the mild solution.

Consider the following Banach space:

\mathbf{E}_{T,\lambda}:=\{u:\mathbb{R}^{+}\times\Omega\longrightarrow\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{A}),\;\text{progressively measurable, such that}\;\underset{0\leq t\leq T}{\sup}{\mathbb{E}\|u(t)\|_{\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{A})}}<+\infty\},

equipped with the following norm:

(3.1)

\|u\|_{\mathbf{E}_{T,\lambda}}^{2}:=\underset{0\leq t\leq T}{\sup}e^{-\lambda t}{\mathbb{E}\|u(t)\|_{\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{A})}^{2}},\quad\forall u\in\mathbf{E}_{T,\lambda},

where
\lambda>0
will be chosen thereafter accordingly, given that the norms
\left(\|.\|_{\mathbf{E}_{T,\lambda}}\right)_{\lambda\geq 0}
are equivalent.

It is clear that the mild solution to Problem (
2.4
) is characterized by the fixed point of the following mapping:

\begin{split}\mathcal{L}:\;&(\mathbf{E}_{T,\lambda},\|.\|_{\mathbf{E}_{T,\lambda}})\longrightarrow(\mathbf{E}_{T,\lambda},\|.\|_{\mathbf{E}_{T,\lambda}})\\
&u\mapsto\mathcal{L}(u):=\mathcal{S}(.)u_{0}+\int_{0}^{.}\mathcal{S}(.-s)\mathcal{F}(u(s))ds+\int_{0}^{.}\int_{\mathbb{Z}}\mathcal{S}(.-s)\mathcal{G}(z,x,u(s-))\tilde{N}(ds,dz).\end{split}

Before proceeding to prove that the mapping
\mathcal{L}
has a unique fixed point, we first establish that it is well-defined, which is assured by the following lemma:

Lemma 3.1
.

Assume that
(u(t))_{t\geq 0}
is progressively measurable. Then, the
\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{A})
-valued stochastic processes:

\displaystyle\left(\int_{0}^{t}\mathcal{S}(t-s)\mathcal{F}(u(s))ds\right)_{t\geq 0}

and

\displaystyle\left(\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{Z}}\mathcal{S}(t-s)\mathcal{G}(z,x,u(s-))\tilde{N}(ds,dz)\right)_{t\geq 0},

are well-defined and progressively measurable.

Proof.

First, note that since
\mathcal{F}
is continuous, then it is
(\mathbb{B}(\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{A}))/\mathbb{B}(\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{A})))
-measurable.
On the other hand, since
(\mathcal{S}(t))_{t\geq 0}
is strongly continuous; then
\mathcal{S}(.-s)\mathcal{F}(u(.))
is progressively measurable, as a composing of progressively measurable processes. Thus, as a consequence of Fubini’s theorem, the stochastic process

\left(\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\mathcal{S}(t-s)\mathcal{F}(u(s))ds\right)_{t\geq 0},

is
\mathcal{F}_{t}
-adapted. By using the same preceding arguments, it can be proved that the stochastic process

\displaystyle\left(\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{Z}}\mathcal{S}(t-s)\mathcal{G}(z,x,u(s-))\tilde{N}(ds,dz)\right)_{t\geq 0},

is also
\mathcal{F}_{t}
-adapted.

Next, we claim that

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\mathcal{S}(t-s)\mathcal{F}(u(s))ds,\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{Z}}\mathcal{S}(t-s)\mathcal{G}(z,x,u(s-))\tilde{N}(ds,dz)\in\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{A})),\quad\forall t>0.

This is established primarily by employing some properties of the resolvent operator, defined as follows:

(3.2)

\forall\theta>0,\quad\mathcal{R}:=\left(-\mathcal{A}+\theta\mathcal{I}\right)^{-1},

where
\mathcal{I}
denotes the identity operator.

Note that
\mathcal{R}
is compact. Moreover, it holds that (see e.g.
[
30
, Proof of Theorem 3.1]
)

(3.3)

\mathbf{R}(\mathcal{R})=\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{A}),

where
\mathbf{R}(.)
stands for the range of a given operator.

With Property (
3.3
) in mind, it suffices to prove that
\exists\chi,\overline{\chi}\in\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{R})

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\mathcal{S}(t-s)\mathcal{F}(u(s))ds=\mathcal{R}\chi,

and

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{Z}}\mathcal{S}(t-s)\mathcal{G}(z,x,u(s-))\tilde{N}(ds,dz)=\mathcal{R}\overline{\chi}.

Now, since the operator
\mathcal{R}
is closed, by Hille’s theorem (see e.g.
[
32
, Theorem 3.7.12]
), the operator
\mathcal{R}
and the integral operator commute. With this in mind, we acquire that

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}\int_{0}^{t}\mathcal{A}\mathcal{S}(t-s)\mathcal{F}(u(s))ds

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}\mathcal{R}\mathcal{A}\mathcal{S}(t-s)\mathcal{F}(u(s))ds

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}\theta\mathcal{R}\mathcal{S}(t-s)\mathcal{F}(u(s))ds-\int_{0}^{t}\mathcal{S}(t-s)\mathcal{F}(u(s))ds

\displaystyle=\mathcal{R}\int_{0}^{t}\theta\mathcal{S}(t-s)\mathcal{F}(u(s))ds-\int_{0}^{t}\mathcal{S}(t-s)\mathcal{F}(u(s))ds.

By the same arguments, it can be shown that

\begin{split}\mathcal{R}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{Z}}\mathcal{A}\mathcal{S}(t-s)\mathcal{G}(z,x,u(s-))\tilde{N}(ds,dz)&=\mathcal{R}\int_{0}^{t}\theta\mathcal{S}(t-s)\mathcal{G}(z,x,u(s-))\tilde{N}(ds,dz)\\
&-\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{Z}}\mathcal{S}(t-s)\mathcal{G}(z,x,u(s-))\tilde{N}(ds,dz).\end{split}

The claim follows by setting

\displaystyle\chi:=\int_{0}^{t}\left(\theta\mathcal{I}-\mathcal{A}\right)\mathcal{S}(t-s)\mathcal{F}(u(s))ds,

and

\displaystyle\overline{\chi}:=\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{Z}}\left(\theta\mathcal{I}-\mathcal{A}\right)\mathcal{S}(t-s)\mathcal{G}(z,x,u(s-))\tilde{N}(ds,dz).

Now, note that as a direct consequence of
[
33
, Theorem 4.14]
, the following Itô isometry holds:

(3.4)

\mathbb{E}\Bigg\|\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{Z}}\mathcal{S}(t-s)\mathcal{G}(z,x,u(s-))\tilde{N}(ds,dz)\Bigg\|_{\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{A})}^{2}=\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{Z}}\mathbb{E}\|\mathcal{S}(t-s)\mathcal{G}(z,x,u(s))\|_{\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{A})}^{2}\nu(dz)ds.

On the other hand, we infer that

(3.5)

\mathbb{E}\Bigg\|\int_{0}^{t}\mathcal{S}(t-s)\mathcal{F}(u(s))ds\Bigg\|_{\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{A})}^{2}\leq\int_{0}^{t}\mathbb{E}\|\mathcal{S}(t-s)\mathcal{F}(u(s))\|_{\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{A})}^{2}ds.

Finally, we indicate that the right hand side of Equality (
3.4
) and that of Inequality (
3.5
) are finite due to the boundedness of the mapping defined by (
2.2
).

This concludes the proof.
∎

Remark 3.1
.

Note that Lemma
3.1
ensures that

\mathcal{S}(t-.)\mathcal{F}(u(.))\in\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{F}}^{t},\;\forall 0<t\leq T.

Furthermore, as a consequence of
[
34
, Theorem 1.1]
, we further acquire that the process

\displaystyle\left(\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{Z}}\mathcal{S}(t-s)\mathcal{G}(z,x,u(s-))\tilde{N}(ds,dz)\right)_{t\geq 0}

satisfies the càd-làg property. Hence,

\mathcal{S}(t-.)\mathcal{G}(z,x,u(.))\in\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{Z}}^{t},\;\forall 0<t\leq T.

Consequently, the first assertion of Definition
2.4
is fulfilled.

We are now in a position to state the following first well-posedness result:

Theorem 3.1
.

For every
u_{0}\in\mathbb{H},
Problem (
2.4
) admits a pathwise unique global mild solution, depending continuously on the initial condition.

Proof.

With Remark
3.1
and Lemma
3.1
in mind, it remains to establish that
\mathcal{L}
is a contraction from
(\mathbf{E}_{T,\lambda},\|.\|_{\mathbf{E}_{T,\lambda}})
into itself. To this end, let
u,v\in\mathbf{E}_{T,\lambda},
we infer that

(3.6)

\begin{split}\|\mathcal{L}(u)-\mathcal{L}(v)\|_{\mathbf{E}_{T,\lambda}}^{2}&:=\underset{0\leq t\leq T}{\sup}e^{-\lambda t}\mathbb{E}\Bigg\|\int_{0}^{t}\mathcal{S}(t-s)(\mathcal{F}(u(s))-\mathcal{F}(v(s)))ds\\[-2.15277pt]
&+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{Z}}\mathcal{S}(t-s)(\mathcal{G}(z,x,u(s-))-\mathcal{G}(z,x,v(s-)))\tilde{N}(ds,dz)\Bigg\|_{\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{A})}^{2}\\[-2.15277pt]
&\leq 2\underset{0\leq t\leq T}{\sup}\left(Te^{-\lambda t}\int_{0}^{t}\mathbb{E}\|\mathcal{F}(u(s))-\mathcal{F}(v(s))\|_{\mathbb{H}}^{2}ds\right.\\[-2.15277pt]
&+\left.e^{-\lambda t}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{Z}}\mathbb{E}\|(\mathcal{G}(z,x,u(s))-\mathcal{G}(z,x,v(s)))\|_{\mathbb{H}}^{2}\nu(dz)ds\right)\\[-2.15277pt]
&\leq 2\underset{0\leq t\leq T}{\sup}\left(TL_{\mathcal{F}}^{2}e^{-\lambda t}\int_{0}^{t}\mathbb{E}\|u(s)-v(s)\|_{\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{A})}^{2}ds\right.\\[-2.15277pt]
&+\left.\max_{i\in\{1,2,3\}}\|\mathcal{C}_{i}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{Z}\times\mathcal{U})}^{2}e^{-\lambda t}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{Z}}\mathbb{E}\|(u(s)-(v(s))\|_{\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{A})}^{2}\nu(dz)ds\right)\\[-2.15277pt]
&\leq 2TL_{\mathcal{F}}^{2}\underset{0\leq t\leq T}{\sup}\int_{0}^{t}e^{-\lambda(t-s)}\mathbb{E}\|u(s)-v(s)\|_{\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{A})}^{2}e^{-\lambda s}ds\\[-2.15277pt]
&+2\max_{i\in\{1,2,3\}}\|\mathcal{C}_{i}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{Z}\times\mathcal{U})}^{2}\nu(\mathbb{Z})\underset{0\leq t\leq T}{\sup}\int_{0}^{t}e^{-\lambda(t-s)}\mathbb{E}\|u(s)-v(s)\|_{\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{A})}^{2}e^{-\lambda s}ds\\[-2.15277pt]
&\leq 2e^{-\lambda T}\int_{0}^{T}e^{\lambda s}ds\left(TL_{M}^{2}+\max_{i\in\{1,2,3\}}\|\mathcal{C}_{i}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{Z}\times\mathcal{U})}^{2}\nu(\mathbb{Z})\right)\underset{0\leq s\leq T}{\sup}e^{-\lambda s}\mathbb{E}\|u(s)-v(s)\|_{\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{A})}\\
&\leq\dfrac{2}{\lambda}\left(TL_{\mathcal{F}}^{2}+\max_{i\in\{1,2,3\}}\|\mathcal{C}_{i}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{Z}\times\mathcal{U})}^{2}\nu(\mathbb{Z})\right)\|u-v\|_{\mathbf{E}_{T,\lambda}}^{2},\end{split}

where we have used the definition of
\mathcal{G},
Property (
2.2
), Isometry (
3.4
) and Cauchy-Schwartz inequality.

Now, by choosing
\lambda>0
large enough such that

(3.7)

\lambda>4\left(TL_{\mathcal{F}}^{2}+\max_{i\in\{1,2,3\}}\|\mathcal{C}_{i}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{Z}\times\mathcal{U})}^{2}\nu(\mathbb{Z})\right),

it follows that

\|\mathcal{L}(u)-\mathcal{L}(v)\|_{\mathbf{E}_{T,\lambda}}\leq\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\|u-v\|_{\mathbf{E}_{T,\lambda}}.

Thus, by Banach fixed point theorem, it follows that

(3.8)

\exists!\textbf{u}\in\mathbf{E}_{T,\lambda},\quad\text{such that}\quad(\mathcal{L}(\textbf{u}))(t)=\textbf{u}(t),\quad\mathbb{P}\text{-a.s,}\;\forall t\in(0,T).

Here, we precise that the uniqueness is in the sense of modifications. That is,

(3.9)

\left(\exists v\in\mathbf{E}_{T,\lambda},\quad\mathcal{L}(v)=v\right)\Longrightarrow\textbf{u}(t)=v(t),\quad\mathbb{P}\text{-a.s},\;\forall t\in(0,T).

However, to achieve that the uniqueness in the sense of modifications is equivalent to pathwise uniqueness, we still have to prove that the mild solution has a càd-làg modification. Note that thanks to the global Lipschitzity of the operator
\mathcal{F}
, the process

\displaystyle(u(t))_{t\geq 0}

\displaystyle\overset{\Delta}{=}\left(\mathcal{S}(t)\textbf{u}_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}\mathcal{S}(t-s)\mathcal{F}(\textbf{u}(s))ds+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{Z}}\mathcal{S}(t-s)\mathcal{G}(z,x,\textbf{u}(s-))\tilde{N}(ds,dz)\right)_{t\geq 0}

\displaystyle=:(\mathcal{L}(\textbf{u}))_{t\geq 0},

satisfies the càd-làg property. Furthermore, it holds that

(3.10)

\mathbb{E}\|\textbf{u}(t)-u(t)\|_{\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{A})}^{2}=0.

Now, it remains to prove that

\mathcal{L}(u)(t)=u(t),\quad\mathbb{P}\text{-a.s},\;\forall t\in(0,T),

which immediately follows by combining (
3.8
) and (
3.10
).

Thus, the pathwise uniqueness of the mild solution is an immediate consequence of (
3.9
) together with the càd-làg property (see e.g.
[
35
, Chapter 1]
).

To conclude the proof, we still have to establish the continuity of the pathwise unique mild solution with respect to the initial condition. To this end, let
u
and
\hat{u}
be the pathwise unique mild solutions corresponding to the initial conditions
u_{0}
and
\hat{u}_{0},
respectively. Then, it follows that

\mathbb{E}\|u(t)-\hat{u}(t)\|_{\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{A})}^{2}\leq\|u_{0}-\hat{u}_{0}\|_{\mathbb{H}}^{2}+\left(L_{\mathcal{F}}+\underset{i\in\{1,2,3\}}{\max}\|\mathcal{C}_{i}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{Z}\times\mathcal{U})}\nu(\mathbb{Z})\right)\int_{0}^{t}\mathbb{E}\|u(s)-\hat{u}(s)\|_{\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{A})}^{2}ds.

By virtue of the integral form of Gronwall’s inequality (see e.g.
[
29
, Appendix B]
) yields

\|u-\hat{u}\|_{\mathbf{E}_{T,0}}^{2}\leq\|u_{0}-\hat{u}_{0}\|_{\mathbb{H}}^{2}\left(1+Te^{\left(L_{\mathcal{F}}+\underset{i\in\{1,2,3\}}{\max}\|\mathcal{C}_{i}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{Z}\times\mathcal{U})}\nu(\mathbb{Z})\right)T}\right).

The result follows by the equivalence of the norms
\left(\|.\|_{\mathbf{E}_{T,\lambda}}\right)_{\lambda\geq 0}
.
∎

Remark 3.2
.

Observe that the importance of the parameter
\lambda>0
relies on the fact that for every assigned values to the biological parameters
\Lambda,\mu,\gamma
and
\mathcal{C}_{i}\;(i\in\{1,2,3\}),
one can always find a suitable parameter
\lambda(\Lambda,\mu,\gamma,\mathcal{C}_{1},\mathcal{C}_{2},\mathcal{C}_{3})>0,
satisfying Inequality (
3.7
), which guarantees that the mapping
\mathcal{L}
defines a contraction. This induces the incorporation of variate scenarios, without a restriction on the assigned values to the biological parameters, especially when it comes to numerical simulations.

3.2.
Well-posedness results in the case of incidence functions satisfying
(P2)

In this subsection, the incidence function is assumed to satisfy
(P2)
. The Lipschitz property of the operator
\mathcal{F}
in this case is merely local, which prohibits the direct use of Banach fixed point theorem. In light of this difficulty, we proceed by a standard truncation technique (see e.g.
[
36
, p. 86]
).
Given
M>0,
choose a
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}
- function
\Pi_{M}
such that

\quad\Pi_{M}(r):=\begin{cases}\begin{split}&1,&\quad\text{if}\quad 0\leq r\leq M,\\
&0,&\quad\text{if}\quad r>2M.\end{split}\end{cases}

Additionally, set

\mathcal{F}^{M}(u):=\mathcal{F}(\Pi_{M}(\|u\|_{\mathbb{L}^{\infty}(\mathcal{U})})u),\quad\forall u\in\mathbb{L}^{\infty}(\mathcal{U}).

Now, introduce the following intermediate problem:

(3.11)

\begin{cases}\displaystyle du(t)=\left(\mathcal{A}u(t)+\mathcal{F}^{M}(u(t)\right)dt+\int_{\mathbb{Z}}\mathcal{G}(z,x,u(t-))\tilde{N}(dt,dz),\\
u(0)=u_{0}.\end{cases}

Then, consider the following Banach space:

\hat{\mathbf{E}}_{T,\lambda}:=\{u:\mathbb{R}^{+}\times\Omega\longrightarrow\mathbb{L^{\infty}}(\mathcal{U}),\;\text{progressively measurable, such that}\;\underset{0\leq t\leq T}{\sup}{\mathbb{E}\|u(t)\|_{\mathbb{L^{\infty}}(\mathcal{U})}}<+\infty\},

equipped with the following norm:

\|u\|_{\hat{\mathbf{E}}_{T,\lambda}}^{2}:=\underset{0\leq t\leq T}{\sup}e^{-\lambda t}{\mathbb{E}\|u(t)\|_{\mathbb{L}^{\infty}(\mathcal{U})}^{2}},\quad\forall u\in\hat{\mathbf{E}}_{T,\lambda},\quad\forall\lambda>0.

Since the operator
\mathcal{F}^{M}
is globally Lipschitz, by proceeding analogously to the proofs of Lemma
3.1
and Theorem
3.1
, it can be proved that Problem (
3.11
) admits a pathwise unique mild solution, which we shall henceforth denote by
u^{M}
.

Theorem 3.2
.

For every
u_{0}\in\mathbb{L}^{\infty}(\mathcal{U}),
Problem (
2.4
) admits a pathwise unique global mild solution, depending continuously on the initial condition.

Proof.

Let
T>0.
With the càd-làg property taken into account, consider the following well-defined sequence of stopping times:

\tau_{M}:=\underset{t\in[0,T]}{\inf}\{\|u^{M}(t)\|_{\mathbb{L}^{\infty}(\mathcal{U})}\geq M\}.

Obviously,
(\tau_{M})_{M>0}
is an increasing sequence. Hence, there exists
\tau_{\infty}
such that

\underset{M\rightarrow+\infty}{\lim}\tau_{M}(\omega)=\tau_{\infty}(\omega),\quad\forall\omega\in\Omega.

Remark that

\mathcal{F}^{M}(u^{M}(t))=\mathcal{F}(u^{M}(t)),\quad\forall t\in(0,\tau_{M}).

Consequently, to conclude the proof it suffices to prove that
\tau_{\infty}=+\infty.

To this end, recall that

\displaystyle u^{M}(t)=\mathcal{S}(t)u_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}\mathcal{S}(t-s)\mathcal{F}(u^{M}(s))ds+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{Z}}\mathcal{S}(t-s)\mathcal{G}(z,x,u^{M}(s-))\tilde{N}(ds,dz),\quad\forall t\in(0,\tau_{M}).

Let
t\in(0,\tau_{M})
, by employing the growth condition (
2.3
), we infer that

\mathbb{E}\|u^{M}(t)\|_{\mathbb{L^{\infty}}(\mathcal{U})}\leq\|u_{0}\|_{\mathbb{L^{\infty}}(\mathcal{U})}+\left(C_{\mathcal{F}}+\underset{i\in\{1,2,3\}}{\max}\|\mathcal{C}_{i}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{Z}\times\mathcal{U})}\nu(\mathbb{Z})\right)\int_{0}^{t}\mathbb{E}\|u^{M}(s)\|_{\mathbb{L}^{\infty}(\mathcal{U})}ds.

Hence, by virtue of the integral form of Gronwall’s inequality (see e.g.
[
29
, Appendix B]
), we obtain

\mathbb{E}\|u^{M}(t)\|_{\mathbb{L}^{\infty}(\mathcal{U})}\leq\|u_{0}\|_{\mathbb{L^{\infty}}(\mathcal{U})}\left(1+Te^{\left(C_{\mathcal{F}}+\underset{i\in\{1,2,3\}}{\max}\|\mathcal{C}_{i}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{Z}\times\mathcal{U})}\nu(\mathbb{Z})\right)T}\right).

Now, remark that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\{\tau_{M}(\omega)<t\}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\mathbbm{1}_{\left\{\tau_{M}(\omega)<t\right\}}=\int_{\Omega}\dfrac{\|u^{M}(\tau_{M})\|_{\mathbb{L^{\infty}}(\mathcal{U})}}{\|u^{M}(\tau_{M})\|_{\mathbb{L^{\infty}}(\mathcal{U})}}\mathbf{1}_{\left\{\tau_{M}(\omega)<t\right\}}d\mathbb{P}

\displaystyle\leq\dfrac{1}{M}\|u_{0}\|_{\mathbb{L^{\infty}}(\mathcal{U})}\left(1+Te^{\left(C_{\mathcal{F}}+\underset{i\in\{1,2,3\}}{\max}\|\mathcal{C}_{i}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{Z}\times\mathcal{U})}\nu(\mathbb{Z})\right)T}\right).

Define the following decreasing sequence of sets:

A_{M}:=\{\tau_{M}<t\},\quad\forall M\in\mathbb{N}^{*}.

By the below-continuity property, we infer that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\{\tau_{\infty}<t\}=\mathbb{P}\left\{\underset{M\in\mathbb{N}^{*}}{\bigcap}A_{M}\right\}

\displaystyle=\underset{M\rightarrow+\infty}{\lim}\mathbb{P}(A_{M})

4.

)-(

Now that the mathematical well-posedness of Model (

)-(

) has been addressed, we still have to address its biological feasibility, which consists of proving the positiveness of the pathwise unique mild solution. Such a property can be established in the framework of partial differential equations, by relying on the maximum principle. On the other hand, in the framework of stochastic partial differential equations driven by Gaussian noise, when the obtained solution is strong, one can proceed by applying an infinite dimensional suitable Itô rule
[
11

[

Thus, from Equality (
4
), we obtain

(4.7)

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}(u_{\epsilon}^{\theta}(t))

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{Z}}\left(\langle\eta_{\epsilon}(u_{\epsilon}^{\theta}(s)+\mathcal{G}_{\epsilon}(z,x,u_{\epsilon}^{\theta}(s)))-\eta_{\epsilon}(u_{\epsilon}^{\theta}(s)),\eta_{\epsilon}^{\prime}(u_{\epsilon}^{\theta}(s))\rangle_{\mathbb{H}\times\mathbb{H}}\right)\tilde{N}(dt,dz).

Evaluating the mathematical expectation on both sides of Inequality (
4.7
) yields

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\mathcal{J}(u_{\epsilon}^{\theta}(t))

\displaystyle\leq 0.

Hence

\mathbb{E}\int_{\mathcal{U}}\eta_{\epsilon}((u_{\epsilon}^{\theta}(t,x))_{i})dx\leq 0,\quad\forall i\in\{1,2,3\}.

By definition of
\eta_{\epsilon},
we acquire that

(u_{\epsilon}^{\theta}(t,x))_{i}\geq 0,\quad\forall i\in\{1,2,3\},\;\mathbb{P}\text{-a.s.}

The proof is concluded by using Lemma
4.3
.
∎

6.
Conclusion and open questions

In this work, we have proposed a new class of stochastic epidemic models which, as far as we know, is the first to incorporate the spatial dependency together with the massive discontinuous noise on the dynamics. Moreover, we have addressed its mathematical well-posedness and biological feasibility. It should be noted that the results established in this paper can be extended to other epidemic models falling into the same class and incorporating other biological characteristics, such as the temporary immunity, the incubation period, the vaccination procedure, the co-infection, etc; we mention for instance the models developed in
[
48
,
49
,
50
,
51
,
52
,
53
]
. We should indicate that this was our first attempt to incorporate the discontinuous noise on the spatio-temporal dynamics of epidemics, and thus our focus was to mainly address the questions of mathematical well-posedness and biological feasibility which, now that they have been addressed, lay the ground-work for some further important emerging questions, which need to be tackled. These are briefly outlined as follows:

•

The case of the bilinear incidence function:
In this case, the Lipschitz property of the incidence function is merely local and the growth condition given in
(P2)
is no longer verified. Hence, the truncation technique used in this paper only allows to prove the existence of local biologically feasible mild solutions. This result is not practical since, in general, there is no explicit information about the lifespan of the obtained solution. Hence, to address the global well-posedness, one needs to proceed with other techniques, such as variational methods (see e.g.
[
54
]
).

•

The asymptotic behavior of the susceptible and infected populations:
Once the mathematical well-posedness and biological feasibility of a given epidemic model have been achieved, an interesting topic is deriving sufficient conditions guaranteeing the persistence and extinction of the susceptible and infected populations. Here, we mention that due to the result presented in Theorem
3.3
, the difficulty arising in the lack of using Itô formula
[
40
, Theorem 3.5.3]
for mild solutions has been addressed. Indeed, one can proceed to establish the aforementioned conditions for the sequence of strong solutions to Problem (
3.21
), then retrieve the desired result for mild solutions by a convergence argument. Thus, an interesting future direction would be to study the persistence and extinction for given explicit incidence functions satisfying
(P1)
or
(P2)
, such as the ones given by (
1.13
) and (
1.14
).

•

The optimal control problem:
When the susceptible and infected populations are persistent, an emerging question is deriving the optimal control strategies steering the dynamics to a desired state, in which the densities of susceptible and infected populations are minimized. For instance, we refer to
[
19
,
22
]
where such results have been acquired in the deterministic case. However, as far as we know, the only existing result in the stochastic case driven by Gaussian noise can be found in the recent work of Shao
et al.

[
14
]
and there are no results when it comes to stochastic spatio-temporal epidemic models driven by Lévy noise.

•

The parameter identification problem:
Once the mathematical well-posedness, biological feasibility and asymptotic analyses have been conducted, one focuses on the question of calibrating Model (
1.10
)-(
1.12
) using real data, which leads to what is known as the inverse problem. Namely, given observations of the susceptible, infected and recovered populations at time
T>0,
one seeks the values assigned to the model parameters, such that the obtained solution provides the best approximation of the given observations. Such results have been derived in the case of deterministic spatio-temporal epidemic models by Xiang and Liu
[
55
]
as well as Coronel
et al.

[
56
]
. On the other hand, in the case of stochastic time-dependent epidemic models driven by Gaussian noise, this problem has been addressed by Mummert and Otunuga
[
57
]
. However, as far as we know, for stochastic spatio-temporal epidemic models driven by Gaussian or Lévy noise, this is still an open question.

Due to the merit of the aforementioned questions, we will treat them independently in our next future works.
