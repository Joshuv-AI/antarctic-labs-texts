Spin polarization of fermions at local equilibrium: Second-order gradient expansion
Xin-Li Sheng, Francesco Becattini, Xu-Guang Huang, Zhong-Hua Zhang

Abstract
We present a calculation of the spin polarization of spin-1/2 fermions in a relativistic fluid at local thermodynamic equilibrium at the second order in the gradient expansion, including second-order derivatives. The second-order derivative terms vanish if the local equilibrium hypersurface is the hyperplane t = c ​ o ​ n ​ s ​ t t=const in the collision center-of-mass frame. However, since the freeze-out hypersurface has a non-trivial space-time structure, these terms may result in a non-vanishing contribution to the spin polarization, whose magnitude needs to be assessed with numerical computations.

Introduction

Spin polarization in a relativistic fluid is a subject of major phenomenological relevance in
relativistic heavy-ion collisions, where the Quark Gluon Plasma (QGP) is formed; for recent
reviews on spin polarization, see, e.g.,
[
1
,
2
,
3

Global and local polarization of
\Lambda
hyperons have been measured by the STAR collaboration
at RHIC energies
[
4
,
5
,
6
]
, and by the ALICE collaboration
at the LHC energy
[
7
,
8
]
. The hydrodynamic model with statistical
hadronic freeze-out proved to be successful in reproducing the data for several observables.
Global spin polarization in heavy ion collision at high energy was quantitatively and successfully
predicted by this model
[
9
,
10
,
11
]
with the spin-thermal vorticity
coupling
[
12
]
, whereas its momentum dependence could not be reproduced
[
13
,
6
,
14
]
.
This motivated further theoretical investigations on other possible sources of spin polarization
in heavy-ion collisions, which led to the finding that also the (thermal) shear tensor and the gradient
of chemical potentials contribute
[
15
,
16
,
17
]
at the linear
order in the gradients of the hydro-thermodynamic fields (see also studies in refs.

[
18
,
19
,
20
,
21
,
22
]
). By
including the spin polarization induced by the thermal shear tensor, the models can
reproduce the experimental data
[
23
,
24
]
with a noteworthy
sensitivity on the initial hydrodynamic conditions as well as on the transport coefficients

[
19
,
24
]
.

In hydrodynamics and non-equilibrium statistical mechanics, any physical observable can
be expressed in terms of the hydro-thermodynamic fields through a so-called gradient expansion

[
25
,
26
]
, which provides a good approximation when these fields
are slowly varying in spacetime. More precisely, the expansion in gradients is appropriate when
the Knudsen number
K_{n}
, that is the ratio between the interaction length and the typical scale
of the inhomogeneity of the fields satisfies
K_{n}\ll 1
, the so-called hydrodynamic limit

1
1

1

The Knudsen number can be defined as
K_{n}=\lambda/|\beta/\partial\beta|
where

\lambda
is the interaction length and
\beta
the four-temperature field.
.
However, there are two kinds of terms in the general gradient expansion which are
conceptually distinct: those stemming from the expansion of the local equilibrium part of the
density operator, which are non-dissipative, and those stemming from the expansion of the
dissipative part of the density operator, which are associated to the so-called transport
coefficients
[
27
]
, such as shear viscosity.

It is known that the gradient expansion of the local equilibrium density
operator gives rise to terms proportional to thermal vorticity tensor
\varpi_{\mu\nu}
, thermal
shear tensor
\xi_{\mu\nu}
and gradient of chemical potential for the spin polarization, which
are first order in the gradient expansion
[
15
]
.
In heavy-ion collisions, numerical simulations show that components of these fields are small enough
[
9
,
28
,
10
,
29
,
30
,
31
,
32
,
33
,
23
]
to ensure the validity of the hydrodynamic limit, hence of the gradient expansion,
up to first order. However, some components, such as the
\varpi_{tz}
component as shown
in ref.
[
10
]
, could have significant variations over the freeze-out hypersurface.
A naive estimation according to ref.
[
10
]
shows that
\partial_{x}\varpi_{tz}

could be as large as
0.3\,\mathrm{fm}^{-1}\approx 0.05\,\mathrm{GeV}
at some points on the
hypersurface. Assuming a typical energy scale
E\approx 1\,\mathrm{GeV}
, we will find that

\partial_{x}\varpi_{tz}/E\approx 0.05
, which is the same order of
\varpi_{tz}
itself.
This might be an indication of a sizeable polarization induced by second order terms such as

\partial_{\rho}\varpi_{\mu\nu}
and
\partial_{\rho}\xi_{\mu\nu}
. Nevertheless, we do not
have any knowledge on the magnitude of second order spin polarizations in the gradient expansion
due to the lack of theoretical calculations and numerical simulations. In this work, we aim at
partially filling this gap by analytically calculating, in the linear response approximation,
the spin polarization to the second order gradient of hydrodynamic quantities at local thermodynamic
equilibrium. On the other hand, we will not delve into the possible dissipative contributions
(i.e. arising from the expansion in the dissipative part of the density operator), which might
be as important as the non-dissipative contributions but are certainly more difficult to estimate
numerically because they generally depend on unknown coefficients.

II
Local thermodynamic equilibrium and its expansion

For a relativistic fluid at Local Thermodynamic Equilibrium (LTE), the density operator is given by
[
34
,
35
,
36
]

\widehat{\rho}_{\text{LE}}=\frac{1}{Z_{\text{LE}}}\exp\left[-\int_{\Sigma}{\rm d}\Sigma_{\mu}\;\left(\widehat{T}^{\mu\nu}(y)\beta_{\nu}(y)-\frac{1}{2}\widehat{S}^{\mu\nu\lambda}(y)\Omega_{\nu\lambda}(y)-\widehat{j}^{\mu}(y)\zeta(y)\right)\right]\,,

(1)

where
Z_{\text{LE}}
is the normalization factor ensuring
{\rm Tr}\,\widehat{\rho}_{\text{LE}}=1
,

\beta_{\nu}=u_{\nu}/T
is the four-temperature vector,
\Omega_{\nu\lambda}
is the spin potential,

\zeta=\mu/T
is the ratio between a chemical potential and temperature, and
y
is the point
on the hypersurface
\Sigma
. For heavy ion collisions, we are mostly interested on calculations
at the freeze-out, so the hypersurface is the
\Sigma_{\text{FO}}
featured in fig.
1
.
The formula (
1
) includes the possibility of a spin potential
\Omega_{\nu\lambda}
not
coinciding with thermal vorticity:

\varpi_{\nu\lambda}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\partial_{\lambda}\beta_{\nu}-\partial_{\nu}\beta_{\lambda}\right)\,,

which makes it certainly pseudo-gauge dependent
[
37
]
. Thus, the operator (
1
)
pertains to a particular choice of the pseudo-gauge, for instance the so-called canonical one
where
\widehat{T}^{\mu\nu}
and
\widehat{S}^{\mu\nu\lambda}
are obtained from the Lagrangian
of the theory by applying the Nöther theorem.

When calculating the mean value of a local operator
\widehat{O}(x)
at LTE:

\langle\widehat{O}(x)\rangle_{\rm LE}={\rm Tr}\left(\widehat{O}(x)\widehat{\rho}_{\text{LE}}\right)

in a relativistic fluid with slowly varying thermodynamic fields
\beta,\zeta
(small Knudsen
number), it is expected that the above mean value will be mostly determined by the value of those
fields at the point
x
while their values far from
x
will give rise to corrections. One can then
expand the fields
\beta_{\nu}(y)
,
\Omega_{\nu\lambda}(y)
, and
\zeta(y)
in Taylor series around the point

x
where the operator
\widehat{O}
is to be evaluated, thereby generating a gradient expansion.
At the leading order, one can approximate:

\beta_{\nu}(y)-\beta_{\nu}(x)\simeq(y-x)^{\lambda}\partial_{\lambda}\beta_{\nu}(x)\,,\qquad\qquad\zeta(y)-\zeta(x)\simeq(y-x)^{\lambda}\partial_{\lambda}\zeta(x)\,,

implying (
\varpi=\Omega
for the sake of simplicity)
[
36
]
:

\widehat{\rho}_{\text{LE}}\simeq\frac{1}{Z_{\text{LE}}}\exp\left[-\beta_{\nu}(x){\widehat{P}}^{\nu}+\frac{1}{2}\varpi_{\nu\lambda}(x)\widehat{J}_{x}^{\nu\lambda}-\frac{1}{2}\xi_{\nu\lambda}(x)\widehat{Q}_{x}^{\nu\lambda}+\zeta(x){\widehat{Q}}+\partial_{\nu}\zeta(x)\widehat{D}_{x}^{\nu}\right]\,,

(2)

where
\xi
is the thermal shear tensor:

\xi_{\nu\lambda}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\partial_{\lambda}\beta_{\nu}+\partial_{\nu}\beta_{\lambda}\right)\,,

{\widehat{P}}^{\mu}
are the four-momentum operators,
{\widehat{J}}_{x}^{\lambda\nu}
are the angular momentum-boost operators with respect to the point
x
, and the operators
\widehat{Q}_{x}
and
\widehat{D}_{x}
read:

{\widehat{Q}}_{x}^{\lambda\nu}=\int_{\Sigma}{\rm d}\Sigma_{\mu}(y)\;\left[(y-x)^{\lambda}{\widehat{T}}^{\mu\nu}+(y-x)^{\nu}{\widehat{T}}^{\mu\lambda}\right]\,,\qquad\qquad\widehat{D}_{x}^{\lambda}=\int_{\Sigma}{\rm d}\Sigma_{\mu}(y)\;(y-x)^{\lambda}{\widehat{j}}^{\mu}\,.

(3)

The operators
{\widehat{Q}}_{x}
and
\widehat{D}_{x}
are responsible for the shear-induced polarization

[
15
,
16
]
and spin Hall effect
[
17
]
respectively
and, unlike
{\widehat{P}}
and
{\widehat{J}}
, are dependent on the hypersurface
\Sigma
; this is a crucial
difference because the corrections stemming from
{\widehat{Q}}_{x}
and
\widehat{D}_{x}
will, in general,
be dependent on the normal vector field to the hypersurface besides the thermo-hydrodynamic fields.

It is the purpose of this paper to go beyond the leading order Taylor expansion of the thermodynamic
fields and to include the second derivatives of
\beta
and
\zeta
in the expansion (
2
),
which will eventually be associated to integral operators of the same sort of
{\widehat{Q}}_{x}
and
\widehat{D}_{x}
.
To maintain a general viewpoint, it is convenient to recast
\widehat{\rho}_{\text{LE}}
as:

\widehat{\rho}_{\text{LE}}=\frac{1}{Z_{\text{LE}}}\exp\left[\widehat{A}_{x}+\widehat{B}_{x}\right]\,,

(4)

where the operators
\widehat{A}_{x}
and
\widehat{B}_{x}
are defined as:

\displaystyle\widehat{A}_{x}

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle-\beta_{\nu}(x)\widehat{P}^{\mu}+\zeta(x)\widehat{Q}\,,

\displaystyle\widehat{B}_{x}

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle-\int_{\Sigma}{\rm d}\Sigma_{\mu}\;\widehat{T}^{\mu\nu}(y)\left[\beta_{\nu}(y)-\beta_{\nu}(x)\right]+\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Sigma}{\rm d}\Sigma_{\mu}\;\widehat{S}^{\mu\nu\lambda}(y)\Omega_{\nu\lambda}(y)

(5)

\displaystyle+\int_{\Sigma}{\rm d}\Sigma_{\mu}\;\widehat{j}^{\mu}(y)\left[\zeta(y)-\zeta(x)\right]\,,

with
\widehat{P}^{\nu}\equiv\int_{\Sigma}{\rm d}\Sigma_{\mu}\,\widehat{T}^{\mu\nu}
being the
four-momentum operator and
\widehat{Q}\equiv\int_{\Sigma}{\rm d}\Sigma_{\mu}\widehat{j}^{\mu}

being the total charge. Due to energy-momentum conservation and current conservation, the
operator
\widehat{A}_{x}
is independent of the hypersurface
\Sigma
unlike
\widehat{B}_{x}
.
In eq. (
5
) we have not extracted the spin potential at the point
x
.
Indeed, since the spin potential coincides with the thermal vorticity at global equilibrium,
we have considered the spin potential
\Omega_{\nu\lambda}(y)\approx\Omega_{\nu\lambda}(x)

as a first order term. In this power counting scheme, the contribution from
\widehat{B}_{x}

is at least
\mathcal{O}(\partial)
and therefore is assumed to be much smaller than the contribution
from
\widehat{A}_{x}
, allowing us to expand the exponential in (
4
) up to
the quadratic term in
\widehat{B}_{x}
using the Kubo identity,

\displaystyle{\rm e}^{\widehat{A}_{x}+\widehat{B}_{x}}

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle{\rm e}^{\widehat{A}_{x}}+\int_{0}^{1}{\rm d}z\;{\rm e}^{z\widehat{A}_{x}}\widehat{B}_{x}{\rm e}^{-z\widehat{A}_{x}}

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{1}{\rm d}z_{1}\int_{0}^{z_{1}}{\rm d}z_{2}\;{\rm e}^{z_{1}\widehat{A}_{x}}\widehat{B}_{x}{\rm e}^{(z_{1}-z_{2})\widehat{A}_{x}}\widehat{B}_{x}{\rm e}^{-z_{1}\widehat{A}_{x}}+\mathcal{O}(\widehat{B}^{3})\,.

For the local operator
\widehat{O}(x)
, the mean value at LTE is thus given by:

\displaystyle\left\langle\widehat{O}(x)\right\rangle_{\text{LE}}

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle\left\langle\widehat{O}(0)\right\rangle_{A}+\int_{0}^{1}{\rm d}z\;\left\langle\widehat{O}(0),\,{\rm e}^{z\widehat{A}_{x}}\widehat{B}_{0}{\rm e}^{-z\widehat{A}_{x}}\right\rangle_{A,C}

(6)

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{1}{\rm d}z_{1}\int_{0}^{z_{1}}{\rm d}z_{2}\left\langle\widehat{O}(0),\,{\rm e}^{z_{2}\widehat{A}_{x}}\widehat{B}_{0}{\rm e}^{-z_{2}\widehat{A}_{x}},\,{\rm e}^{z_{1}\widehat{A}_{x}}\widehat{B}_{0}{\rm e}^{-z_{1}\widehat{A}_{x}}\right\rangle_{A,C}+\mathcal{O}(\widehat{B}^{3})\,,

where
\left\langle\;\;\right\rangle_{A}
denotes the mean value at the homogeneous global
thermodynamic equilibrium described by
\widehat{A}
:

\left\langle\widehat{O}\right\rangle_{A}\equiv\frac{\text{Tr}\left[\widehat{O}\,{\rm e}^{\widehat{A}_{x}}\right]}{\text{Tr}\left[{\rm e}^{\widehat{A}_{x}}\right]}\,.

In eq. (
6
) we have shifted the operator
\widehat{O}(x)
by means of
the identity
\widehat{O}(0)\equiv{\rm e}^{-ix\cdot\widehat{P}}\widehat{O}(x){\rm e}^{ix\cdot\widehat{P}}

and
\widehat{B}_{x}
likewise. The connected mean values are defined as:

\displaystyle\left\langle\widehat{O}_{1},\,\widehat{O}_{2}\right\rangle_{A,C}

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\left\langle\widehat{O}_{1}\widehat{O}_{2}\right\rangle_{A}-\left\langle\widehat{O}_{1}\right\rangle_{A}\left\langle\widehat{O}_{2}\right\rangle_{A}\,,

\displaystyle\left\langle\widehat{O}_{1},\,\widehat{O}_{2},\,\widehat{O}_{3}\right\rangle_{A,C}

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\left\langle\widehat{O}_{1}\widehat{O}_{2}\widehat{O}_{3}\right\rangle_{A}-\ \left\langle\widehat{O}_{1}\widehat{O}_{2}\right\rangle_{A}\left\langle\widehat{O}_{3}\right\rangle_{A}-\left\langle\widehat{O}_{2}\widehat{O}_{3}\right\rangle_{A}\left\langle\widehat{O}_{1}\right\rangle_{A}

(7)

\displaystyle-\left\langle\widehat{O}_{1}\widehat{O}_{3}\right\rangle_{A}\left\langle\widehat{O}_{2}\right\rangle_{A}+2\left\langle\widehat{O}_{1}\right\rangle_{A}\left\langle\widehat{O}_{2}\right\rangle_{A}\left\langle\widehat{O}_{3}\right\rangle_{A}\,,

for arbitrary operators
\widehat{O}_{1,2,3}
.

We now focus on free spin-1/2 fermions, for which the canonical energy-stress tensor, canonical spin
tensor, and the current are given by
[
38
]
,

\displaystyle\widehat{T}^{\mu\nu}(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{i}{2}\overline{\psi}(x)\gamma^{\mu}(\overrightarrow{\partial}^{\nu}-\overleftarrow{\partial}^{\nu})\psi(x)\,,

\displaystyle\widehat{S}^{\mu\nu\lambda}(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\epsilon^{\mu\nu\lambda\rho}\overline{\psi}(x)\gamma_{\rho}\gamma^{5}\psi(x)\,,

\displaystyle\widehat{j}^{\mu}(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\overline{\psi}(x)\gamma^{\mu}\psi(x)\,,

(8)

where
\psi(x)
is the Dirac field.
By substituting (
8
) into eq. (
5
) and taking the 4D Fourier transforms,
it is possible to express
\widehat{B}_{0}
as follows:

\widehat{B}_{0}={\rm e}^{-ix\cdot\widehat{P}}\widehat{B}_{x}{\rm e}^{ix\cdot\widehat{P}}=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{5}}\int{\rm d}^{4}k_{1}{\rm d}^{4}k_{2}\;\overline{\psi}(k_{2})\mathcal{B}(k_{2},k_{1})\psi(k_{1})\,,

(9)

where
\psi(k)
is the Fourier component of the Dirac field,

\psi(k)\equiv\int{\rm d}^{4}x\;\psi(x){\rm e}^{ik\cdot x},\qquad\overline{\psi}(k)\equiv\psi^{\dagger}(k)\gamma^{0}=\int{\rm d}^{4}x\;\overline{\psi}(x){\rm e}^{-ik\cdot x}\,.

(10)

Since
\widehat{B}_{0}
is Hermitian,
\mathcal{B}(k_{2},k_{1})
is a
4\times 4
Dirac matrix
fulfilling
\gamma^{0}\mathcal{B}^{\dagger}(k_{2},k_{1})\gamma^{0}=\mathcal{B}(k_{1},k_{2})
, whose
explicit form reads:

\mathcal{B}(k_{2},k_{1})=-\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{3}}\int_{\Sigma}{\rm d}\Sigma_{\mu}\;{\rm e}^{-iq\cdot(y-x)}\bigg\{\gamma^{\mu}k^{\nu}\left[\beta_{\nu}(y)-\beta_{\nu}(x)\right]+\frac{1}{4}\epsilon^{\mu\nu\lambda\sigma}\gamma_{\sigma}\gamma^{5}\Omega_{\nu\lambda}(y)-\gamma^{\mu}\left[\zeta(y)-\zeta(x)\right]\bigg\}\,,

(11)

where
k\equiv(k_{1}+k_{2})/2
is the average momentum and
q\equiv k_{1}-k_{2}
is the relative
momentum, respectively. The fields
\beta_{\nu}(y)
,
\Omega_{\nu\lambda}(y)
, and
\zeta(y)

can be expressed as Taylor series with respect to
y-x
. For instance:

\beta_{\nu}(y)=\beta_{\nu}(x)+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n!}\left[\prod_{j=1}^{n}\left(y^{\lambda_{j}}-x^{\lambda_{j}}\right)\partial_{\lambda_{j}}\right]\beta_{\nu}\Big|_{y=x},

where
\partial_{\lambda_{j}}
acts on
\beta_{\nu}
. Then:

\displaystyle{\rm e}^{-iq\cdot(y-x)}\left[\beta_{\nu}(y)-\beta_{\nu}(x)\right]={\rm e}^{-iq\cdot(y-x)}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n!}\left[\prod_{j=1}^{n}\left(y^{\lambda_{j}}-x^{\lambda_{j}}\right)\partial_{\lambda_{j}}\right]\beta_{\nu}\Big|_{y=x}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n!}\left[\prod_{j=1}^{n}\partial_{\lambda_{j}}\right]\beta_{\nu}\Big|_{y=x}\left[\prod_{j=1}^{n}i\frac{\partial}{\partial_{q_{j}}}\right]{\rm e}^{-iq\cdot(y-x)}\equiv[\beta_{\nu}(x+i\partial_{q})-\beta_{\nu}(x)]\left({\rm e}^{-iq\cdot(y-x)}\right)\,.

The other terms in (
11
) involving
\Omega
and
\zeta
can be
expressed likewise. Finally, all the functions
\beta(x+i\partial_{q})
etc. can
be moved out of the integral, so that eq. (
11
) turns into:

\mathcal{B}(k_{2},k_{1})=-\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{3}}\bigg\{\gamma^{\mu}k^{\nu}\left[\beta_{\nu}(x+i\partial_{q})-\beta_{\nu}(x)\right]+\frac{1}{4}\epsilon^{\mu\nu\lambda\sigma}\gamma_{\sigma}\gamma^{5}\Omega_{\nu\lambda}(x+i\partial_{q})-\gamma^{\mu}\left[\zeta(x+i\partial_{q})-\zeta(x)\right]\bigg\}\int_{\Sigma}{\rm d}\Sigma_{\mu}\;{\rm e}^{-iq\cdot(y-x)}\,,

(12)

where it is understood that the term within the curl brackets acts on the integral
over
\Sigma
. Now, with the help of the Gauss’s theorem, that integral can be converted
to an integral over a portion of a hyperplanes
\Sigma_{B}
with constant normal vector

\hat{t}^{\mu}=(1,0,0,0)
and an integral over a 4D region
\Omega_{B}
enclosed by
two space-like hyperplanes and a part of the hypersurface
\Sigma
(see fig.
2
in
Appendix
A
). Assuming that the space extent of
\Sigma_{B}
and
\Omega_{B}

are sufficiently large, we obtain (see Appendix
A
for the derivation):

\displaystyle\int_{\Sigma}{\rm d}\Sigma_{\mu}\;{\rm e}^{-iq\cdot(y-x)}

\displaystyle\simeq

\displaystyle(2\pi)^{3}\left[\hat{t}_{\mu}{\rm e}^{-iq^{0}(y_{B}^{0}-x^{0})}-iq_{\mu}\int_{y_{B}^{0}}^{y_{\Sigma}^{0}(q)}{\rm d}y^{0}\;{\rm e}^{-iq^{0}(y^{0}-x^{0})}\right]\delta^{3}({\bf q})

(13)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(2\pi)^{3}{\rm e}^{-iq^{0}(y_{\Sigma}^{0}(q)-x^{0})}\delta^{3}({\bf q})\,\hat{t}_{\mu}\,,

where
y_{B}^{0}
denotes the constant time for
\Sigma_{B}
and
y_{\Sigma}^{0}(q)
denotes a
costant time, dependent on
q
, which can be considered as an average time for the space-like
part of the hypersurface
\Sigma
(see again Appendix
A
for the precise
definition).
By plugging eq. (
13
) into eq. (
12
),
we can finally express
\mathcal{B}(k_{2},k_{1})
as follows:

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}(k_{2},k_{1})

\displaystyle\simeq

\displaystyle-\hat{t}_{\mu}\bigg\{\gamma^{\mu}k^{\nu}\left[\beta_{\nu}(x+i\partial_{q})-\beta_{\nu}(x)\right]+\frac{1}{4}\epsilon^{\mu\nu\lambda\rho}\gamma_{\rho}\gamma^{5}\Omega_{\nu\lambda}(x+i\partial_{q})\bigg.

(14)

\displaystyle\bigg.-\gamma^{\mu}\left[\zeta(x+i\partial_{q})-\zeta(x)\right]\bigg\}\left[{\rm e}^{-iq^{0}(y_{\Sigma}^{0}(q)-x^{0})}\delta^{3}({\bf q})\right]\,,

with the same proviso as for eq. (
12
).

III
The Wigner function at local equilibrium

The general formula to calculate the mean spin vector of spin 1/2 fermions reads
[
39
]
:

S^{\mu}(p)=\frac{1}{2}\frac{\int_{\Sigma}{\rm d}\Sigma\cdot p_{+}\,{\rm tr}[\gamma^{\mu}\gamma^{5}W(x,p)]}{\int_{\Sigma}{\rm d}\Sigma\cdot p_{+}\,{\rm tr}[W(x,p)]}\,,

(15)

where
{\rm tr}
stands for the trace over the spinorial indices and
p_{+}^{\mu}\equiv p^{\mu}\theta(p^{0})

selects the positive-energy part of the Wigner function, removing antiparticle contribution.
The covariant Wigner function is defined as
[
40
,
41
]
:

W_{ab}(x,p)\equiv\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{4}}\int{\rm d}^{4}y\;{\rm e}^{-ip\cdot y}\left\langle\overline{\psi}_{b}\left(x+\frac{y}{2}\right)\psi_{a}\left(x-\frac{y}{2}\right)\right\rangle\,,

(16)

where
a,b
are spinorial Dirac indices and
\left\langle(\cdots)\right\rangle\equiv\text{Tr}[\widehat{\rho}(\cdots)]
denotes the mean value with a specific density matrix.
It should be stressed that, even though the argument
p
of the Wigner function is not
necessarily on-shell, in the formula (
15
), for a free field,
p
becomes
an on-shell four-vector.
The reason is that, for the Dirac free field, the divergence of the integrand
in equation (
15
) vanishes, that is the Wigner function, either restricted to
particles or not, fulfills the equation
p^{\mu}\partial_{\mu}W(x,p)=0
. This condition
makes it possible to choose an arbitrary hypersurface to perform integration e.g. in the equation
(
15
) or in any other similar expression and to show, by means of an explicit calculation
for a free field, that only on-shell four-vectors
p
contribute to the integral
[
39
]
.

The goal of this section is the calculation of the Wigner function at LTE by using the
expansion in eq. (
6
). For this purpose, we first make use of the
Fourier decomposition in eq. (
10
) to convert the definition
(
16
) to the momentum space:

W_{ab}(x,p)=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{8}}\int{\rm d}^{4}k_{1}{\rm d}^{4}k_{2}\,\delta^{4}\left(p-\frac{k_{1}+k_{2}}{2}\right){\rm e}^{-i(k_{1}-k_{2})\cdot x}\left\langle\overline{\psi}_{b}(k_{2})\psi_{a}(k_{1})\right\rangle\,,

(17)

then we apply eq. (
6
) by setting
\widehat{O}=\overline{\psi}_{b}(k_{2})\psi_{a}(k_{1})
.
The result can be in general written as a series in powers of
\widehat{B}
:

W_{\text{LE}}(x,p)=W_{0}(x,p)+W_{\text{lin}}(x,p)+W_{\text{quad}}(x,p)+\mathcal{O}(\widehat{B}^{3})\,.

(18)

We are now going to determine the form of the Wigner function up to the quadratic term.

III.1
Main term

The
\widehat{B}
-independent part of the Wigner function is given by:

W_{0,ab}(x,p)=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{8}}\int{\rm d}^{4}q\;{\rm e}^{-iq\cdot x}\left\langle\overline{\psi}_{b}(p_{2})\psi_{a}(p_{1})\right\rangle_{A}\,,

(19)

where

p\equiv(p_{1}+p_{2})/2\,,\qquad q\equiv p_{1}-p_{2}\implies p_{1,2}\equiv p\pm q/2

(20)

and
\widehat{A}_{x}
is given by eq. (
5
). Noting that the field operators
in coordinate space obey the equal-time anticommutating relation

(47)

Plugging the above expression into
S^{(2)\mu}_{\text{quad}}
in eq. (
46
),
one can obtain the spin polarization induced by quadratic terms of first order quantities.
Note that, according to eq. (
39
), if
\Sigma
is a flat 3D hypersurface
with normal vector
\hat{t}^{\mu}=(1,0,0,0)
,
{\rm tr}\left[W^{(1)}(x,p)\right]=0
because of
vanishing
y^{0}_{\Sigma}(0)-x^{0}
for any point
x
on
\Sigma
, leading to vanishing
S_{\text{quad}}
.
Therefore, for such a flat hypersurface, the total spin polarization at second order, given by the sum
S^{(2)\mu}=S^{(2)\mu}_{\text{lin}}+S^{(2)\mu}_{\text{quad}}
, vanishes. This is in agreement
with calculations at the global equilibrium, which are notably independent of the hypersurface,
which show that there is no quadratic contribution in thermal vorticity for the spin polarization
vector
[
46
]
.

V
Summary

In summary, we have derived an expression of the average spin polarization vector of spin-1/2
fermions at local thermodynamic equilibrium up to the second order in the gradient expansion of
hydro-thermodynamic fields, upgrading previous results at the leading order. We have calculated
both the contributions from the quadratic term in the gradients and the linear in the second
order derivatives.

The calculations in this work are based on the local thermodynamic equilibrium density operator
and therefore have an expected dependence on the freeze-out hypersurface
\Sigma
.
Under some geometric approximation, we find that the second order correction
S^{(2)\mu}
depends
on the time coordinate
x^{0}
of the centre-of-mass frame for any point
x
on
\Sigma
. Notably, for
the case of
\Sigma
coinciding with a hyperplane with a constant normal vector
\hat{t}^{\mu}=(1,0,0,0)
,
the term
S^{(2)\mu}
vanishes. Nevertheless, in relativistic heavy ion collision
\Sigma
is neither
flat nor purely space-like, resulting in a non-vanishing
S^{(2)\mu}
.

In relativistic heavy ion collisions, while the quadratic term is expected to be a small correction
to the leading order in view of the smallness of
\varpi
and
\xi
at the freeze-out, as shown
by numerical simulations, the impact of the second order derivative term is not easy to estimate
and requires numerical computations.

Appendix A
Integral over the hypersurface

The aim of this section is to calculate the integral over the freeze-out hypersurface:

\int_{\Sigma}{\rm d}\Sigma_{\mu}\;{\rm e}^{-iq\cdot(y-x)}\,.

(48)

The hypersurface
\Sigma
can be split into a space-like part
\Sigma_{S}
and a time-like
part
\Sigma_{T}
(there is also a light-like part, but it is 2D surface with vanishing

\Sigma
at
some 2D boundary. Obviously, for any point

on
\Sigma^{\prime}
, the time component

is a constant. The idea is that, in general, it is possible to approximate:

(49)

and that

can be chosen on
\Sigma^{\prime}
so as to make this approximation an equality
for the time component of the equation (
49

\Sigma_{S}

component which is a function of the space Cartesian coordinates
{\bf y}
, that is

. Thus, the
\mu=0
component of eq. (
49

\int{\rm d}^{3}{\rm y}\;{\rm e}^{-iq^{0}(y_{\Sigma}^{0}-x^{0})}{\rm e}^{i{\bf q}\cdot({\bf y}-{\bf x})}=\int{\rm d}^{3}{\rm y}\;{\rm e}^{-iq^{0}\left(y^{0}({\bf y})-x^{0}\right)}{\rm e}^{i{\bf q}\cdot({\bf y}-{\bf x})}

\Sigma_{L}

\Sigma_{B}

\displaystyle\int_{\Sigma}{\rm d}\Sigma_{\mu}\;{\rm e}^{-iq\cdot(y-x)}

\displaystyle\simeq\int_{\Sigma^{\prime}\cup\Sigma_{L}}{\rm d}\Sigma_{\mu}\;{\rm e}^{-iq\cdot(y-x)}\simeq\hat{t}_{\mu}{\rm e}^{-iq^{0}(y_{B}^{0}-x^{0})}\int{\rm d}^{3}{\rm y}\;{\rm e}^{i{\bf q}\cdot({\bf y}-{\bf x})}+\int_{y_{B}^{0}}^{y_{\Sigma}^{0}(q)}\!\!{\rm d}y^{0}\int{\rm d}^{3}{\rm y}\;\frac{\partial}{\partial y^{\mu}}{\rm e}^{-iq\cdot(y-x)}\,

\displaystyle n_{F}(p_{1})[1-n_{F}(p_{3})]\int_{0}^{1}{\rm d}z_{1}\int_{0}^{z_{1}}{\rm d}z_{2}\ \left\{-{\rm e}^{z_{2}\beta\cdot q+z_{1}\beta\cdot q^{\prime}}n_{F}(p_{2})+{\rm e}^{z_{2}\beta\cdot q^{\prime}+z_{1}\beta\cdot q}[1-n_{F}(p_{2})]\right\}

(60)

\displaystyle\simeq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}n_{F}(p)[1-n_{F}(p)][1-2n_{F}(p)]\left[1+\frac{1-4{\rm e}^{\beta\cdot p}+{\rm e}^{2\beta\cdot p}}{6\left({\rm e}^{2\beta\cdot p}-1\right)}\beta\cdot(q-q^{\prime})+\mathcal{O}(q^{2},q^{\prime 2},q\cdot q^{\prime})\right]\,,

where
\mathcal{O}(q^{2},q^{\prime 2},q\cdot q^{\prime})
terms can be dropped because
they will not contribute to later calculations. In eq. (

),

\Gamma_{1,\lambda}
is multiplied
with a momentum derivative acting on
\delta^{3}({\bf q})
or
\delta^{3}({\bf q}^{\prime})
,
while
\Gamma_{2}
does not. The momentum derivative gives rise to complicated expressions when trying
to integrate out the delta functions, thus we separate
W_{\text{quad}}
into three parts:

W_{\text{quad}}=W_{\text{quad},11}+W_{\text{quad},12}+W_{\text{quad},22}\,,

with:

\displaystyle W_{\text{quad,11}}(x,p)=\int{\rm d}Q\;(\not{p}_{3}+m)\left\{\left[\Gamma_{1,\lambda}(p-q/2)i\partial_{q^{\prime}}^{\lambda}\right]\left[{\rm e}^{-iq^{\prime 0}(y_{\Sigma}^{0}-x^{0})}\delta^{3}({\bf q}^{\prime})\right]\right\}

\displaystyle\times(\not{p}_{2}+m)\left\{\left[\Gamma_{1,\sigma}(p+q^{\prime}/2)i\partial_{q}^{\sigma}\right]\left[{\rm e}^{-iq^{0}(y_{\Sigma}^{0}-x^{0})}\delta^{3}({\bf q})\right]\right\}(\not{p}_{1}+m)\,,

\displaystyle W_{\text{quad,12}}(x,p)=\int{\rm d}Q\;(\not{p}_{3}+m)\left\{\left[\Gamma_{1,\lambda}(p-q/2)i\partial_{q^{\prime}}^{\lambda}\right]\left[{\rm e}^{-iq^{\prime 0}(y_{\Sigma}^{0}-x^{0})}\delta^{3}({\bf q}^{\prime})\right]\right\}(\not{p}_{2}+m)\Gamma_{2}(\not{p}_{1}+m)\left[{\rm e}^{-iq^{0}(y_{\Sigma}^{0}-x^{0})}\delta^{3}({\bf q})\right]

\displaystyle+\int{\rm d}Q\;(\not{p}_{3}+m)\Gamma_{2}(\not{p}_{2}+m)\left\{\left[\Gamma_{1,\sigma}(p+q^{\prime}/2)i\partial_{q}^{\sigma}\right]\left[{\rm e}^{-iq^{0}(y_{\Sigma}^{0}-x^{0})}\delta^{3}({\bf q})\right]\right\}(\not{p}_{1}+m)\left[{\rm e}^{-iq^{\prime 0}(y_{\Sigma}^{0}-x^{0})}\delta^{3}({\bf q}^{\prime})\right]\,,

\displaystyle W_{\text{quad,22}}(x,p)=\int{\rm d}Q\;(\not{p}_{3}+m)\Gamma_{2}(\not{p}_{2}+m)\Gamma_{2}(\not{p}_{1}+m)\left[{\rm e}^{-iq^{\prime 0}(y_{\Sigma}^{0}-x^{0})}\delta^{3}({\bf q}^{\prime})\right]\left[{\rm e}^{-iq^{0}(y_{\Sigma}^{0}-x^{0})}\delta^{3}({\bf q})\right]\,.

It is hard to obtain explicit formulae for the various
W_{\text{quad}}
, especially

W_{\text{quad,11}}
. Notwithstanding, calculating the axial-vector
component is relatively easy with the help of FeynCalc
[
47
]
.

We first focus on the first contribution to the
W_{\rm quad}
. Working out the trace of
Dirac matrices, we find:

\displaystyle{\rm tr}\left[\gamma^{\mu}\gamma^{5}W_{\text{quad,11}}(x,p)\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 8p^{0}\epsilon^{\mu\nu\alpha\rho}p_{\nu}\int{\rm d}Q\;\left\{\hat{t}_{\rho}(q_{\alpha}+q_{\alpha}^{\prime})-\frac{1}{2p^{0}}\left[q_{\alpha}q_{\rho}^{\prime}+\hat{t}\cdot(q-q^{\prime})(q_{\alpha}+q_{\alpha}^{\prime})\hat{t}_{\rho}\right]\right\}

(61)

\displaystyle\times\left[(p^{\nu^{\prime}}-q^{\nu^{\prime}}/2)\partial_{\lambda^{\prime}}\beta_{\nu^{\prime}}(x)-\partial_{\lambda^{\prime}}\zeta(x)\right]\left[(p^{\nu}+q^{\prime\nu}/2)\partial_{\lambda}\beta_{\nu}(x)-\partial_{\lambda}\zeta(x)\right]

\displaystyle\times\left\{\partial_{q}^{\lambda}\partial_{q^{\prime}}^{\lambda^{\prime}}\sin\left[(q^{0}+q^{\prime 0})(y_{\Sigma}^{0}-x^{0})\right]\delta^{3}({\bf q}^{\prime})\delta^{3}({\bf q})\right\}\,,

taking advantage of the fact that (
61
) is a real function. Integrating by parts,
we can rewrite it as:

{\rm tr}\left[\gamma^{\mu}\gamma^{5}W_{\text{quad,11}}(x,p)\right]=\frac{4p^{0}}{(2\pi)^{3}}n_{F}(p)[1-n_{F}(p)][1-2n_{F}(p)]\epsilon^{\mu\nu\alpha\rho}p_{\nu}(I_{\alpha\rho}^{(1)}+I_{\alpha\rho}^{(2)}+I_{\alpha\rho}^{(3)}+I_{\alpha\rho}^{(4)})\,,

(62)

where:

\displaystyle I_{\alpha\rho}^{(1)}

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\int{\rm d}^{4}q\,{\rm d}^{4}q^{\prime}\;g_{1}(p;q,q^{\prime})g_{2}(p;q,q^{\prime})\left[\partial_{q}^{\lambda}\partial_{q^{\prime}}^{\lambda^{\prime}}g_{3,\alpha\rho\lambda\lambda^{\prime}}(p;q,q^{\prime})\right]\,,

\displaystyle I_{\alpha\rho}^{(2)}

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\int{\rm d}^{4}q\,{\rm d}^{4}q^{\prime}\;g_{1}(p;q,q^{\prime})\left[\partial_{q}^{\lambda}\partial_{q^{\prime}}^{\lambda^{\prime}}g_{2}(p;q,q^{\prime})\right]g_{3,\alpha\rho\lambda\lambda^{\prime}}(p;q,q^{\prime})\,,

\displaystyle I_{\alpha\rho}^{(3)}

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\int{\rm d}^{4}q\,{\rm d}^{4}q^{\prime}\;g_{1}(p;q,q^{\prime})\left[\partial_{q}^{\lambda}g_{2}(p;q,q^{\prime})\right]\left[\partial_{q^{\prime}}^{\lambda^{\prime}}g_{3,\alpha\rho\lambda\lambda^{\prime}}(p;q,q^{\prime})\right]\,,

\displaystyle I_{\alpha\rho}^{(4)}

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\int{\rm d}^{4}q\,{\rm d}^{4}q^{\prime}\;g_{1}(p;q,q^{\prime})\left[\partial_{q^{\prime}}^{\lambda^{\prime}}g_{2}(p;q,q^{\prime})\right]\left[\partial_{q}^{\lambda}g_{3,\alpha\rho\lambda\lambda^{\prime}}(p;q,q^{\prime})\right]\,,

with auxiliary functions

\displaystyle g_{1}(p;q,q^{\prime})

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\sin\left[(q^{0}+q^{\prime 0})(y_{\Sigma}^{0}-x^{0})\right]\delta^{3}({\bf q}^{\prime})\delta^{3}({\bf q})\text{sgn}(p_{1}^{0})\text{sgn}(p_{2}^{0})\text{sgn}(p_{3}^{0})\,,

\displaystyle g_{2}(p;q,q^{\prime})

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\delta(p_{1}^{2}-m^{2})\delta(p_{2}^{2}-m^{2})\delta(p_{3}^{2}-m^{2})\,,

\displaystyle g_{3,\alpha\rho\lambda\lambda^{\prime}}(p;q,q^{\prime})

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\left[1+\frac{1-4{\rm e}^{\beta\cdot p}+{\rm e}^{2\beta\cdot p}}{6\left({\rm e}^{2\beta\cdot p}-1\right)}\beta\cdot(q-q^{\prime})\right]

\displaystyle\times\left\{\hat{t}_{\rho}(q_{\alpha}+q_{\alpha}^{\prime})-\frac{1}{2p^{0}}\left[q_{\alpha}q_{\rho}^{\prime}+\hat{t}\cdot(q-q^{\prime})(q_{\alpha}+q_{\alpha}^{\prime})\hat{t}_{\rho}\right]\right\}

\displaystyle\times\left[(p^{\tau^{\prime}}-q^{\tau^{\prime}}/2)\partial_{\lambda^{\prime}}\beta_{\tau^{\prime}}(x)-\partial_{\lambda^{\prime}}\zeta(x)\right]\left[(p^{\tau}+q^{\prime\tau}/2)\partial_{\lambda}\beta_{\tau}(x)-\partial_{\lambda}\zeta(x)\right]\,.

We note that we have neglected momentum derivatives of the sign functions because they
will give terms like
\delta(p_{1}^{0})
, a constraint that in general cannot be fulfilled.

In the term
I_{\alpha\rho}^{(1)}
, the delta functions in
g_{1}
and
g_{2}
yield:

\delta(p_{1}^{2}-m^{2})\delta(p_{2}^{2}-m^{2})\delta(p_{3}^{2}-m^{2})\delta^{3}({\bf q})\delta^{3}({\bf q}^{\prime})=\frac{\delta(p^{2}-m^{2})}{4(p^{0})^{2}}\delta^{4}(q)\delta^{4}(q^{\prime})\,,

(63)

forcing
q^{\mu}
and
q^{\prime\mu}
to be vanishing vectors. Therefore
I_{\alpha\rho}^{(1)}

vanishes because:

I_{\alpha\rho}^{(1)}\propto\lim_{q^{\mu},q^{\prime\mu}\rightarrow 0}\sin\left[(q^{0}+q^{\prime 0})(y_{\Sigma}^{0}-x^{0})\right]=0\,.

In the term
I_{\alpha\rho}^{(2)}
, the momentum derivatives act on
g_{2}
, which can be
expressed as:

g_{2}(p;q,q^{\prime})=\frac{1}{4}\delta\left(p^{2}-m^{2}+\frac{q^{2}+q^{\prime 2}}{4}+\frac{q\cdot q^{\prime}}{2}\right)\delta\left[q\cdot\left(p+\frac{q^{\prime}}{2}\right)\right]\delta\left[q^{\prime}\cdot\left(p-\frac{q}{2}\right)\right]\,.

If both
\partial_{q}^{\lambda}
and
\partial_{q^{\prime}}^{\lambda^{\prime}}
act on

\delta\left(p^{2}-m^{2}+\frac{q^{2}+q^{\prime 2}}{4}+\frac{q\cdot q^{\prime}}{2}\right)
,
the remaining two delta functions in
g_{2}
, with the delta functions in
g_{1}
, will
lead to
\delta^{4}(q)\delta^{4}(q^{\prime})
and thus
g_{1}=0
because:

\lim_{q^{\mu},q^{\prime\mu}\rightarrow 0}\sin\left[(q^{0}+q^{\prime 0})(y_{\Sigma}^{0}-x^{0})\right]=0.

If
\partial_{q}^{\lambda}
or
\partial_{q^{\prime}}^{\lambda^{\prime}}
(or both of them)
acts on
\delta\left[q\cdot\left(p+\frac{q^{\prime}}{2}\right)\right]
or

\delta\left[q^{\prime}\cdot\left(p-\frac{q}{2}\right)\right]

derivative with respect to the time component
q^{0}
or
q^{\prime 0}
and then integrate

\displaystyle I_{\alpha\rho}^{(2)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4}\int{\rm d}^{4}q{\rm d}^{4}q^{\prime}\;g_{1}(p;q,q^{\prime})\left(p^{\lambda}+\frac{q^{\prime\lambda}}{2}\right)\left(p^{\lambda^{\prime}}-\frac{q^{\lambda^{\prime}}}{2}\right)g_{3,\alpha\rho\lambda\lambda^{\prime}}(p;q,q^{\prime})

\displaystyle\times\delta\left(p^{2}-m^{2}+\frac{q^{2}+q^{\prime 2}}{4}+\frac{q\cdot q^{\prime}}{2}\right)\delta^{\prime}\left[q\cdot\left(p+\frac{q^{\prime}}{2}\right)\right]\delta^{\prime}\left[q^{\prime}\cdot\left(p-\frac{q}{2}\right)\right]\,.

Integrating again by parts, we find:

I_{\alpha\rho}^{(2)}\propto\lim_{q,q^{\prime}\rightarrow 0}\frac{\partial}{\partial q^{0}}\frac{\partial}{\partial q^{\prime 0}}\sin\left[(q^{0}+q^{\prime 0})(y_{\Sigma}^{0}-x^{0})\right]\hat{t}_{\rho}(q_{\alpha}+q_{\alpha}^{\prime})\propto\hat{t}_{\alpha}\hat{t}_{\rho}\,,

which is symmetric with respect to its indices
\alpha,\rho
.

The calculation of
I_{\alpha\rho}^{(3)}
is similar to that of
I_{\alpha\rho}^{(2)}
,
and the only term that survive reads:

\displaystyle I_{\alpha\rho}^{(3)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4}p^{\lambda}\int{\rm d}^{4}q\,{\rm d}^{4}q^{\prime}\;g_{1}(p;q,q^{\prime})\delta\left(p^{2}-m^{2}+\frac{q^{2}+q^{\prime 2}}{4}+\frac{q\cdot q^{\prime}}{2}\right)

\displaystyle\times\delta^{\prime}\left[q\cdot\left(p+\frac{q^{\prime}}{2}\right)\right]\delta\left[q^{\prime}\cdot\left(p-\frac{q}{2}\right)\right]\left[\partial_{q^{\prime}}^{\lambda^{\prime}}g_{3,\alpha\rho\lambda\lambda^{\prime}}(p;q,q^{\prime})\right]\,.

Then,
\delta^{\prime}\left[q\cdot\left(p+\frac{q^{\prime}}{2}\right)\right]

gives rise to a derivative with respect to
q^{0}
, and after integrating by parts, it turns out:

\displaystyle I_{\alpha\rho}^{(3)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{4}p^{\lambda}\lim_{q^{\mu},q^{\prime\mu}\rightarrow 0}\frac{\partial}{\partial q^{0}}\sin\left[(q^{0}+q^{\prime 0})(y_{\Sigma}^{0}-x^{0})\right]\text{sgn}(p_{1}^{0})\text{sgn}(p_{2}^{0})\text{sgn}(p_{3}^{0})

\displaystyle\times\frac{\delta\left[p^{2}-m^{2}+\frac{q^{2}+q^{\prime 2}}{4}+\frac{q\cdot q^{\prime}}{2}\right]}{\left(p^{0}+\frac{q^{\prime 0}}{2}\right)\left|p^{0}-\frac{q^{0}}{2}\right|\left|p^{0}+\frac{q^{\prime 0}}{2}\right|}\left[\partial_{q^{\prime}}^{\lambda^{\prime}}g_{3,\alpha\rho\lambda\lambda^{\prime}}(p;q,q^{\prime})\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{\delta(p^{2}-m^{2})\text{sgn}(p^{0})}{4(p^{0})^{3}}(y_{\Sigma}^{0}-x^{0})\hat{t}_{\rho}g_{\alpha}^{\lambda^{\prime}}\left[p^{\tau^{\prime}}\partial_{\lambda^{\prime}}\beta_{\tau^{\prime}}(x)-\partial_{\lambda^{\prime}}\zeta(x)\right]p^{\lambda}\left[p^{\tau}\partial_{\lambda}\beta_{\tau}(x)-\partial_{\lambda}\zeta(x)\right]\,.

\displaystyle{\rm tr}\left[\gamma^{\mu}\gamma^{5}W_{\text{quad,11}}(x,p)\right]

\displaystyle-\frac{2\delta(p^{2}-m^{2})\text{sgn}(p^{0})}{(2\pi)^{3}(p^{0})^{2}}n_{F}(p)[1-n_{F}(p)][1-2n_{F}(p)](y_{\Sigma}^{0}-x^{0})

\displaystyle\times\epsilon^{\mu\nu\alpha\rho}p_{\nu}\hat{t}_{\rho}g_{\alpha}^{\lambda}\left[p^{\tau}\partial_{\lambda}\beta_{\tau}(x)-\partial_{\lambda}\zeta(x)\right]p^{\lambda^{\prime}}\left[p^{\tau^{\prime}}\partial_{\lambda^{\prime}}\beta_{\tau^{\prime}}(x)-\partial_{\lambda^{\prime}}\zeta(x)\right]\,.

\displaystyle\delta(p_{1}^{2}-m^{2})\delta(p_{2}^{2}-m^{2})\delta(p_{3}^{2}-m^{2})\delta^{3}({\bf q})

\displaystyle W_{\text{quad,12}}(x,p)

\displaystyle W_{\text{quad,12}}(x,p)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\delta(p^{2}-m^{2})}{32(2\pi)^{3}|p^{0}|}n_{F}(p)[1-n_{F}(p)][1-2n_{F}(p)][p^{\nu}\partial_{\lambda}\beta_{\nu}(x)-\partial_{\lambda}\zeta(x)]\hat{t}_{\tau}\epsilon^{\tau\nu\alpha\sigma}\Omega_{\nu\alpha}(x)

\displaystyle{\rm tr}\left[\gamma^{\mu}\gamma^{5}W_{\text{quad,12}}(x,p)\right]

\displaystyle\times p^{\lambda^{\prime}}\partial_{\lambda^{\prime}}\left[p^{\tau^{\prime}}\beta_{\tau^{\prime}}(x)-\zeta(x)\right]
