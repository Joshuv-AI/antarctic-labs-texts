Beyond Wilson–Cowan dynamics: oscillations and chaos without inhibition
Vincent Painchaud, Nicolas Doyon, Patrick Desrosiers

Abstract
Fifty years ago, Wilson and Cowan developed a mathematical model to describe the activity of neural populations. In this seminal work, they divided the cells in three groups: active, sensitive and refractory, and obtained a dynamical system to describe the evolution of the average firing rates of the populations. In the present work, we investigate the impact of the often neglected refractory state and show that taking it into account can introduce new dynamics. Starting from a continuous-time Markov chain, we perform a rigorous derivation of a mean-field model that includes the refractory fractions of populations as dynamical variables. Then, we perform bifurcation analysis to explain the occurance of periodic solutions in cases where the classical Wilson–Cowan does not predict oscillations. We also show that our mean-field model is able to predict chaotic behavior in the dynamics of networks with as little as two populations.

Introduction

Differential equations have been successfully used to model the activity of neurons for more than a century now, ever since the works of
Lapicque 1907
. One of the most important examples of such a model, published fifty years ago by
Wilson and Cowan 1972
, describes the average firing rates of coupled neural populations using a set of ordinary differential equations. This model, of significant historical importance, has been the starting point for many extensions and is still highly relevant today
(
Bressloff et al 2016
;
Chow and Karimipanah 2020
;
Cowan et al 2016
;
Destexhe and Sejnowski 2009
;
Wilson and Cowan 2021
)
. An important achievement of this model was to predict oscillations and bistability in the neural activity of a network made of an excitatory and an inhibitory population.

Inhibition is a key ingredient in the dynamics of the Wilson–Cowan model. Indeed, it is well known that their equations for a pair of populations can only predict oscillatory solutions if one of them is excitatory while the other is inhibitory
(
Ermentrout and Terman 2010
, section 11.3.2)
, forming a so-called Wilson–Cowan oscillator. The model may also lead to more complicated dynamical behavior in the presence of inhibition. For instance, chaotic behaviors have been shown to arise from their equations, for example by
Borisyuk et al 1995
and by
Maruyama et al 2014
, but only—at least to our knowledge—in systems of at least two coupled Wilson–Cowan oscillators. Thus, inhibition plays a crucial role in Wilson–Cowan’s model in the formation of oscillatory and chaotic behaviors, which have both been observed experimentally for a long time in the activity of neural networks and are thought to play important biological roles
(
Buzsáki 2006
;
Breakspear 2017
;
Rabinovich and Abarbanel 1998
)
. In similar models as well, the role of inhibition is crucial for chaotic behavior to arise
(
Fukai and Shiino 1990
;
Sompolinsky et al 1988
)
. While it has been demonstrated experimentally that inhibition is important to several types of oscillations in neural networks
(
Bartos et al 2007
;
Whittington et al 2000
)
, it does not explain all oscillatory behavior. For example, it has been shown in experiments that excitatory neurons in the pre-Bötzinger complex can exhibit oscillatory behavior
(
Butera et al 1999a
;
Butera et al 1999b
;
Duan et al 2017
)
. Another example of this are theta oscillations
(
Buzsáki 2002
;
Buzsáki and Draguhn 2004
)
, which are thought to arise as a result of the activity of excitatory neurons only
(
Budd 2005
;
Chagnac-Amitai and
Connors 1989
)
. Thus, there are still oscillatory behaviors that the classical model cannot explain.

One of the key steps in the construction of Wilson–Cowan’s model is a time coarse graining, which has the effect of setting the refractory fraction of a neural population as always proportional to its active fraction. Wilson and Cowan originally argued that, at least when the parameters of the model have physiologically reasonable values, this should not have any important impact on the model. Later on, others even argued that the refractory period of neurons should not have an impact on the dynamics, and chose to neglect it completely
(
Curtu and Ermentrout 2001
;
Ermentrout and Terman 2010
, section 11.3)
. However, it has been noticed experimentally that refractoriness can have an impact on neuronal activity. For instance,
Berry and Meister 1998
have shown that a longer refractory period in neurons improves the precision in the response of ganglion cells, suggesting that refractoriness improves neural signaling. Similarly,
Avissar et al 2013
have shown that refractoriness enhances precision in the timing and synchronization of neurons’ spikes, which once again suggests that it makes neurons more precise. Refractoriness is also known to be related to oscillations in neural networks, since it can help neurons to synchronize their spikes
(
Sanchez-Vives and McCormick 2000
;
Wiedemann and Lüthi 2003
)
. In theoretical works as well, it has recently been suggested that the refractory state of neurons could be essential in some cases to provide a complete description of the dynamics of a biological neural network
(
Rule et al 2019
;
Weistuch et al 2021
)
.

In this paper, we propose a simple extension of Wilson–Cowan’s classical model where the refractory state is explicitly considered with the same importance as the active and sensitive states. Indeed, using a different approach than Wilson–Cowan, we derive a dynamical system closely related to their original model, in the sense that it includes it as a subsystem. Then, we show that our dynamical system can predict oscillations and chaotic behaviors in the activity of neural networks of excitatory populations. This contrasts with the original Wilson–Cowan model, in which inhibition plays a crucial role for such phenomena to arise.

First, in section
2
, we present an explicit construction of the model. We start by defining a continuous-time Markov chain to describe the evolution of a large network’s state in a way that mimics the behavior of biological neurons. The resulting stochastic process is similar to one already proposed—but not extensively studied—by
Cowan 1990
, and is also reminiscent of a similar process proposed by
Zarepour et al 2019
. Then, we reduce this Markov chain, which describes the evolution of the network’s state from a microscopic point of view, to a dynamical system of small dimension that describes the dynamics from a macroscopic point of view. To do so, we split the network into a small number of neural populations, and we obtain a dynamical system that describes the evolution of the average active and refractory fractions of each population.

Then, in section
3
, we study the relationship between our model and Wilson–Cowan’s classical model. In fact, we show that Wilson–Cowan’s dynamical system can be seen as a subsystem of ours. We then argue that the simplification of our model to Wilson–Cowan’s is not trivial. Indeed, the domain to which corresponds the subsystem is not invariant by the flow of the full dynamical system, so that in particular, it cannot be an attracting set.

Finally, in section
4
, we present a detailed study of three examples where our model succeeds in predicting the qualitative dynamical behavior of the underlying Markov chain, while the classical Wilson–Cowan model fails to do so. In particular, the first example shows that our model allows oscillations in the activity of a single excitatory population, and the second shows that it allows chaotic behavior in the activity of a pair of excitatory populations.

2
The model

We consider a network of

\ignorespaces N

neurons labelled with integers from

\ignorespaces 1

to

\ignorespaces N

. Links between neurons are described by a weight matrix

\ignorespaces W\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}

whose element

\ignorespaces W_{jk}

represents the weight of the connection from neuron

\ignorespaces k

to neuron

\ignorespaces j

. If neuron

\ignorespaces k

is excitatory, then

\ignorespaces W_{jk}>0

, and if it is inhibitory, then

\ignorespaces W_{jk}<0

.

Our goal is to build an approximate macroscopic description of the network’s dynamics starting from a precise microscopic description. To do so, we consider a partition

\ignorespaces\mathscr{P}

of the set of neurons

\ignorespaces\{1,\ldots,N\}

. Each element

\ignorespaces J\in\mathscr{P}

then represents a population of the network, that is, a set of neurons that share similar properties in a sense that will be made precise later. We will start by defining a continuous-time Markov process to provide a precise description of the evolution of the network’s state, and then we will construct a mean-field model to describe the evolution of the population’s macroscopic states.

2.1
The microscopic model

2.1.1
Stochastic process

In order to model biological neurons, we assume that neurons can take three states:

•

\ignorespaces 0

: the
sensitive
state,

•

\ignorespaces 1

: the
active
state,

•

\ignorespaces i

: the
refractory
state,

where

\ignorespaces i

denotes the imaginary unit. The active state is that of a neuron undergoing an action potential. Following an action potential, neurons typically enter a hyperpolarized state during which another action potential cannot occur even if the neuron receives a stimulus that would be otherwise sufficient to trigger spiking
(
Purves et al 2018
)
. This state is what we call the refractory state. When a neuron is neither active nor refractory, we say that it is sensitive, as if it receives a high enough input it can spike in response.

We see the transitions between these states as random: a sensitive neuron activates at a rate that increases nonlinearly with its input, and then gets to the refractory state and then back to the sensitive state at constant rates. This intuitive process describes the evolution of the whole network’s state, and is defined rigorously as a continuous-time Markov chain

\ignorespaces\{X_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}

taking values in the state space

\ignorespaces E\vcentcolon=\{0,1,i\}^{N}

.

\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces 0

\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces 1

\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces i

\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\alpha

\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\beta

\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\gamma

Allowed transitions between states of neurons and corresponding characteristic rates.

For each neuron

\ignorespaces j

, let

\ignorespaces\alpha_{j},\beta_{j},\gamma_{j}>0

. These parameters characterize the transition rates from a state to another as illustrated in Fig.
1
. While

\ignorespaces\beta_{j}

and

\ignorespaces\gamma_{j}

both describe the actual transition rates,

\ignorespaces\alpha_{j}

rather represents the activation rate of

\ignorespaces j

if it is given an infinite excitation. Indeed, we assume a soft threshold dynamics: the activation rate is given by the function

a_{j}(x)\vcentcolon=\alpha_{j}F_{J}\biggl(\sum_{k=1}^{N}W_{jk}\operatorname{Re}x_{k}+Q_{J}\biggr),

where

\ignorespaces F_{J}\colon\mathbb{R}\to[0,1]

is a function of the neuron’s input,

\ignorespaces Q_{J}\in\mathbb{R}

is an input in

\ignorespaces J

that is external to the network, and

\ignorespaces J

is the population to which belongs

\ignorespaces j

. Here, we assume that

\ignorespaces F_{J}

is a continuous and increasing function that tends to

\ignorespaces 1

at infinity. Biologically speaking,

\ignorespaces\beta_{j}

and

\ignorespaces\gamma_{j}

can be interpreted as the inverse of the average times neuron

\ignorespaces j

spends in the active and refractory states, respectively. Thus, for example, a higher value for

\ignorespaces\beta_{j}

than for

\ignorespaces\gamma_{j}

translates the idea that the duration of an action potential is less than the refractory period. However, the interpretation of

\ignorespaces\alpha_{j}

is less direct. Indeed,

\ignorespaces\alpha_{j}

is the activation rate of neuron

\ignorespaces j

only when it is given an infinite input. Hence, the inverse of

\ignorespaces\alpha_{j}

cannot be seen directly as the average time neuron

\ignorespaces j

spends in the sensitive state; this time depends on the activity of the whole network. Moreover, the average and maximal firing rates vary greatly from situation to situation or according to the neural type
(
Roxin et al 2011
;
Wang et al 2016
)
. This implies that the model can be relevant for a wide range of values of

\ignorespaces\alpha_{j}

.

From the rates associated to the state transitions of single neurons, we can now define a generator for the Markov chain

\ignorespaces\{X_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}

, which will allow to define the process correctly. This generator is a matrix

\ignorespaces M

indexed over the state space

\ignorespaces E

whose entry

\ignorespaces m(x,y)

gives the transition rate from a state

\ignorespaces x

to another state

\ignorespaces y

. We define this rate as

m(x,y)\vcentcolon=\sum_{j=1}^{N}m_{j}(x,y)\smash{\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}k=1\\
k\neq j\end{subarray}}^{N}}\delta_{x_{k}y_{k}},

where

\ignorespaces\delta_{x_{k}y_{k}}

is a Kronecker delta and where

\displaystyle m_{j}(x,y)\vcentcolon=a_{j}(x)

\displaystyle(1-\lvert x_{j}\rvert)\bigl(\operatorname{Re}y_{j}-(1-\lvert y_{j}\rvert)\bigr)

\displaystyle{}+\beta_{j}

\displaystyle\operatorname{Re}(x_{j})(\operatorname{Im}y_{j}-\operatorname{Re}y_{j})

\displaystyle{}+\gamma_{j}

\displaystyle\operatorname{Im}(x_{j})\bigl((1-\lvert y_{j}\rvert)-\operatorname{Im}y_{j}\bigr).

To make sense of

\ignorespaces m_{j}(x,y)

, recall that a component

\ignorespaces x_{j}

of a state vector

\ignorespaces x\in E

is either

\ignorespaces 0

,

\ignorespaces 1

or

\ignorespaces i

, so that exactly one of

\ignorespaces\operatorname{Re}x_{j}

,

\ignorespaces\operatorname{Im}x_{j}

and

\ignorespaces 1-\lvert x_{j}\rvert

is

\ignorespaces 1

while the others are

\ignorespaces 0

.

Now, it is a simple calculation to see that the matrix

\ignorespaces M\vcentcolon=\{m(x,y):x,y\in E\}

is the generator of a continuous-time Markov chain. Thus, it follows from the Kolmogorov extension theorem (for details, see e.g. the books by
Doob 1990
or
Norris 1997
) that a probability measure

\ignorespaces\mathbb{P}

exists on the space

\ignorespaces(E,2^{E})^{[0,\infty)}

such that for any

\ignorespaces x,y\in E

, as

\ignorespaces\Delta t\downarrow 0

,

\mathbb{P}[X_{t+\Delta t}=y\nonscript\>|\nonscript\>\mathopen{}X_{t}=x]=\delta_{xy}+m(x,y)\Delta t+o(\Delta t).

In particular, if

\ignorespaces X_{t}^{j}

denotes the

\ignorespaces j

th component of

\ignorespaces X_{t}

and if

\ignorespaces x

denotes a state with

\ignorespaces x_{j}=0

, then

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}[X_{t+\Delta t}^{j}=1\nonscript\>|\nonscript\>\mathopen{}X_{t}=x]

\displaystyle=a_{j}(x)\Delta t+o(\Delta t),

(1a)

while in general

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}[X_{t+\Delta t}^{j}=i\nonscript\>|\nonscript\>\mathopen{}X_{t}^{j}=1]

\displaystyle=\beta_{j}\Delta t+o(\Delta t),

(1b)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}[X_{t+\Delta t}^{j}=0\nonscript\>|\nonscript\>\mathopen{}X_{t}^{j}=i]

\displaystyle=\gamma_{j}\Delta t+o(\Delta t),

(1c)

and the transitions

\ignorespaces 0\mapsto i

,

\ignorespaces i\mapsto 1

and

\ignorespaces 1\mapsto 0

all have

\ignorespaces o(\Delta t)

rates.

In principle, the system is then completely described, since the transition probabilities

\ignorespaces\mathbb{P}[X_{t+\Delta t}=y\nonscript\>|\nonscript\>\mathopen{}X_{t}=x]

for

\ignorespaces x,y\in E

can be obtained from the solution of the Kolmogorov forward equation

\dot{P}(t)=P(t)M,

(2)

where the dot denotes a derivative. Indeed, the solution

\ignorespaces P(t)

of (
2
) is the matrix whose entries are the probabilities that the system makes a transition from a state to another during an interval of time

\ignorespaces t

. More explicitely, the transition probability

\ignorespaces\mathbb{P}[X_{t+\Delta t}=y\nonscript\>|\nonscript\>\mathopen{}X_{t}=x]

is equal to the element

\ignorespaces x,y

of

\ignorespaces P(\Delta t)

. However, since there are

\ignorespaces 3^{N}

possible states in

\ignorespaces E

, this differential equation is enormous when the network has a large number of neurons, so that it cannot be studied directly in practice.

2.1.2
Dynamical system

Now, we want to use the stochastic process constructed above to obtain a macroscopic description of the evolution of the network’s state, in the form of a dynamical system. To do this, we first introduce functions

\ignorespaces p_{j},q_{j},r_{j}\colon[0,\infty)\to[0,1]

given by

\displaystyle\SwapAboveDisplaySkip p_{j}(t)

\displaystyle\vcentcolon=\mathbb{P}[X_{t}^{j}=1],

(3a)

\displaystyle q_{j}(t)

\displaystyle\vcentcolon=\mathbb{P}[X_{t}^{j}=0],

(3b)

\displaystyle r_{j}(t)

\displaystyle\vcentcolon=\mathbb{P}[X_{t}^{j}=i].

(3c)

Since

\ignorespaces X_{t}^{j}

takes values in

\ignorespaces\{0,1,i\}

, it is easy to see that

\ignorespaces p_{j}+q_{j}+r_{j}\equiv 1

and that

\displaystyle p_{j}(t)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[\operatorname{Re}X_{t}^{j}],

\displaystyle q_{j}(t)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[1-\lvert X_{t}^{j}\rvert],

\displaystyle r_{j}(t)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[\operatorname{Im}X_{t}^{j}],

where

\ignorespaces\mathbb{E}

denotes the expectation with respect to

\ignorespaces\mathbb{P}

. Using these relations, it is possible to find expressions for the derivatives of these variables. Indeed, with

\ignorespaces\Delta t>0

,

\displaystyle p_{j}(t+\Delta t)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}[X_{t+\Delta t}^{j}=1]

\displaystyle=\sum_{x\in E}\mathbb{P}[X_{t+\Delta t}^{j}=1\nonscript\>|\nonscript\>\mathopen{}X_{t}=x]\mathbb{P}[X_{t}=x].

Using the transition rates introduced earlier, we get from (
1
) that

\displaystyle\begin{split}p_{j}(t+\Delta t)&=\sum_{x\in E}\Bigl(\operatorname{Re}(x_{j})(1-\beta_{j}\Delta t)\\[-5.69054pt]
&\hskip 34.1433pt+\bigl(1-\lvert x_{j}\rvert\bigr)a_{j}(x)\Delta t\\
&\hskip 62.59605pt+o(\Delta t)\Bigr)\mathbb{P}[X_{t}=x]\end{split}

\displaystyle\begin{split}&=(1-\beta_{j}\Delta t)p_{j}(t)\\
&\hskip 17.07164pt+\Delta t\mathbb{E}\big[\bigl(1-\lvert X_{t}^{j}\rvert\bigr)a_{j}(X_{t})\big]+o(\Delta t).\end{split}

Taking

\ignorespaces\Delta t\to 0

, it follows that

\displaystyle\dot{p}_{j}(t)

\displaystyle=-\beta_{j}p_{j}(t)+\mathbb{E}\big[\bigl(1-\lvert X_{t}^{j}\rvert\bigr)a_{j}(X_{t})\big].

(4a)

Using the same method, we also find that

\displaystyle\dot{q}_{j}(t)

\displaystyle=-\mathbb{E}\big[\bigl(1-\lvert X_{t}^{j}\rvert\bigr)a_{j}(X_{t})\big]+\gamma_{j}r_{j}(t),

(4b)

\displaystyle\dot{r}_{j}(t)

\displaystyle=-\gamma_{j}r_{j}(t)+\beta_{j}p_{j}(t).

(4c)

At first glance, one might think that at this point, the dimension of the system has been reduced from

\ignorespaces 3^{N}

to

\ignorespaces 2N

. However, the system (
4
) is not closed, in the sense that the derivatives of

\ignorespaces p_{j}

,

\ignorespaces q_{j}

and

\ignorespaces r_{j}

are not given as functions of the same variables, but rather involve other expectations. Hence, the Kolmogorov forward equation (
2
) is still needed to solve (
4
).

2.2
The macroscopic model

To study the network’s dynamics from a macroscopic point of view, we introduce for each population

\ignorespaces J\in\mathscr{P}

and each

\ignorespaces t\geq 0

the random variables

\displaystyle A_{t}^{J}

\displaystyle\vcentcolon=\frac{1}{\lvert J\rvert}\sum_{j\in J}\operatorname{Re}X_{t}^{j},

(5a)

\displaystyle R_{t}^{J}

\displaystyle\vcentcolon=\frac{1}{\lvert J\rvert}\sum_{j\in J}\operatorname{Im}X_{t}^{j},

(5b)

\displaystyle S_{t}^{J}

\displaystyle\vcentcolon=\frac{1}{\lvert J\rvert}\sum_{j\in J}\bigl(1-\lvert X_{t}^{j}\rvert\bigr),

(5c)

which can be understood as state variables for populations. Thus, the expected values of these variables describe the expected behavior of the network from a macroscopic point of view. We will use the system (
4
) to find a dynamical system that describes the expected behavior of the network in that sense.

Since it drastically simplifies the reduction of (
4
) to the macroscopic point of view, we assume that all parameters (the weights and transition rates) are constant over populations. However, we stress that this is not necessary if populations are large: we could instead assume that parameters are independent random variables, identically distributed over populations, and the resulting macroscopic model would be the same. This more general approach, which is also much more technical, is discussed in appendix
A
.

Using the assumption described above, we see that the input in a neuron

\ignorespaces j

of a population

\ignorespaces J

becomes

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{N}W_{jk}

\displaystyle\operatorname{Re}X_{t}^{k}+Q_{J}

\displaystyle=\sum_{K\in\mathscr{P}}\sum_{k\in K}W_{JK}\operatorname{Re}X_{t}^{k}+Q_{J}

\displaystyle=\sum_{K\in\mathscr{P}}\lvert K\rvert W_{JK}A_{t}^{K}+Q_{J},

where we replaced

\ignorespaces W_{jk}

with

\ignorespaces W_{JK}

for

\ignorespaces k\in K

, as we assume that weights are constant over populations. To simplify notation, we will write such an input as

B_{t}^{J}\vcentcolon=\sum_{K\in\mathscr{P}}c_{JK}A_{t}^{K}+Q_{J}\quad\text{with}\quad c_{JK}\vcentcolon=\lvert K\rvert W_{JK}.

It is then easy to obtain expressions for the derivatives of the average macroscopic state variables

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{J}(t)

\displaystyle\vcentcolon=\mathbb{E}[A_{t}^{J}],

(6a)

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{J}(t)

\displaystyle\vcentcolon=\mathbb{E}[R_{t}^{J}],

(6b)

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{J}(t)

\displaystyle\vcentcolon=\mathbb{E}[S_{t}^{J}].

(6c)

Indeed, if

\ignorespaces j\in J

, then

\ignorespaces a_{j}(X_{t})=\alpha_{J}F_{J}(B_{t}^{J})

, so it directly follows from (
4
) that

\displaystyle\dot{\mathcal{A}}_{J}(t)

\displaystyle=-\beta_{J}\mathcal{A}_{J}(t)+\alpha_{J}\mathbb{E}[F_{J}(B_{t}^{J})S_{t}^{J}],

(7a)

\displaystyle\dot{\mathcal{S}}_{J}(t)

\displaystyle=-\alpha_{J}\mathbb{E}[F_{J}(B_{t}^{J})S_{t}^{J}]+\gamma_{J}\mathcal{R}_{J}(t),

(7b)

\displaystyle\dot{\mathcal{R}}_{J}(t)

\displaystyle=-\gamma_{J}\mathcal{R}_{J}(t)+\beta_{J}\mathcal{A}_{J}(t),

(7c)

where we replaced

\ignorespaces\alpha_{j}

with

\ignorespaces\alpha_{J}

and followed the same pattern for other transition rates.

Finally, to close the above dynamical system, we add the mean-field assumption and we neglect covariances between state variables. Thus, we obtain the mean-field dynamical system

\displaystyle\dot{\mathcal{A}}_{J}

\displaystyle=-\beta_{J}\mathcal{A}_{J}+\alpha_{J}F_{J}(\mathcal{B}_{J})\mathcal{S}_{J},

(8a)

\displaystyle\dot{\mathcal{S}}_{J}

\displaystyle=-\alpha_{J}F_{J}(\mathcal{B}_{J})\mathcal{S}_{J}+\gamma_{J}\mathcal{R}_{J},

(8b)

\displaystyle\dot{\mathcal{R}}_{J}

\displaystyle=-\gamma_{J}\mathcal{R}_{J}+\beta_{J}\mathcal{A}_{J}

(8c)

where

\ignorespaces\mathcal{B}_{J}(t)\vcentcolon=\mathbb{E}[B_{t}^{J}]

.

Remark that for each

\ignorespaces J

, one of the equations (
8
) is redundant since

\ignorespaces\mathcal{A}_{J}+\mathcal{R}_{J}+\mathcal{S}_{J}\equiv 1

. Hence, the above dynamical system has dimension

\ignorespaces 2n

, where

\ignorespaces n

is the number of populations of the network. Moreover, the variables

\ignorespaces\mathcal{A}_{J}

,

\ignorespaces\mathcal{R}_{J}

and

\ignorespaces\mathcal{S}_{J}

must be contained in

\ignorespaces[0,1]

to make sense. Therefore, the system (
8
) can be studied using only the active and refractory fractions of populations, on the domain

\begin{multlined}\mathscr{D}_{n}\vcentcolon=\bigl\{(\mathcal{A}_{J},\mathcal{R}_{J})_{J\in\mathscr{P}}\in[0,1]^{2n}:\\
\qquad\qquad\forall J\in\mathscr{P},0\leq\mathcal{A}_{J}+\mathcal{R}_{J}\leq 1\bigr\}.\end{multlined}

(9)

This domain enjoys a simple invariance property.

Proposition 1
.

The domain

\ignorespaces\mathscr{D}_{n}

is invariant by the flow of the dynamical system (
8
).

Proof:
Recall that all transition rates

\ignorespaces\alpha_{J},\beta_{J},\gamma_{J}

are positive and that all functions

\ignorespaces F_{J}

are nonnegative. If

\ignorespaces Y=(\mathcal{A}_{J},\mathcal{R}_{J})_{J\in\mathscr{P}}

is a point at the boundary of

\ignorespaces\mathscr{D}_{n}

, then one of the fractions

\ignorespaces\mathcal{A}_{J}

,

\ignorespaces\mathcal{R}_{J}

or

\ignorespaces\mathcal{S}_{J}

is zero at

\ignorespaces Y

for some population

\ignorespaces J

; call this fraction

\ignorespaces\mathcal{X}_{J}

. Then it is clear from the equations (
8
) that the derivative of

\ignorespaces\mathcal{X}_{J}

must be nonnegative at

\ignorespaces Y

, so that the vector field corresponding to the dynamical system is directed inwards

\ignorespaces\mathscr{D}_{n}

at

\ignorespaces Y

.

This invariance property is crucial to the meaning of solutions of the mean-field dynamical system (
8
). Indeed, for the variables

\ignorespaces\mathcal{A}_{J}

,

\ignorespaces\mathcal{R}_{J}

and

\ignorespaces\mathcal{S}_{J}

to represent proportions of neurons, all of them must remain in the interval

\ignorespaces[0,1]

at all times. Thus, Proposition
1
confirms that it is always possible to interpret the components of a solution of (
8
) as proportions of neurons in each of the three states, as long as the initial state can also be interpreted in this way. In other words, any solution of (
8
) that starts from a physiologically meaningful initial state continues to carry a physiological interpretation at all times.

3
Relationship with the Wilson–Cowan model

The dynamical system (
8
) can be seen as a generalization of the classical system introduced by
Wilson and Cowan 1972
. Indeed, both our model and Wilson–Cowan’s describe the activities of populations of a biological neural network. Wilson–Cowan’s classical equations, which are formulated for a network split into an excitatory and an inhibitory population, are given by

\displaystyle\tau_{e}\dot{E}

\displaystyle=-E+(1{-}r_{e}E)f_{e}(w_{ee}E{-}w_{ei}I{+}Q_{e}),\!\!

(10a)

\displaystyle\tau_{i}\dot{I}

\displaystyle=-I+(1{-}r_{i}I)f_{i}(w_{ie}E{-}w_{ii}I{+}Q_{i}),

(10b)

where

\ignorespaces E

and

\ignorespaces I

are the average firing rates of the populations,

\ignorespaces\tau_{e}

and

\ignorespaces\tau_{i}

are time constants,

\ignorespaces r_{e}

and

\ignorespaces r_{i}

are the refractory periods of neurons,

\ignorespaces f_{e}

and

\ignorespaces f_{i}

are functions that describe the response of both populations,

\ignorespaces Q_{e}

and

\ignorespaces Q_{i}

are external inputs, and

\ignorespaces w_{JK}

are nonnegative coefficients that describe the links between the populations.

To obtain these equations, Wilson and Cowan use a time coarse graining that leads to see the refractory fraction of a population as proportional to its active fraction. Remark that in our model, such a reduction amounts to fixing each refractory fraction

\ignorespaces\mathcal{R}_{J}

to its equilibrium solution in the system (
8
):

\dot{\mathcal{R}}_{J}=0\iff\mathcal{R}_{J}=\frac{\beta_{J}}{\gamma_{J}}\mathcal{A}_{J}.

Using this simplification, the system (
8
) simply becomes

\displaystyle\dot{\mathcal{A}}_{J}

\displaystyle=-\beta_{J}\mathcal{A}_{J}

(11)

\displaystyle+\alpha_{J}\biggl(1-\Bigl(1+\frac{\beta_{J}}{\gamma_{J}}\Bigr)\mathcal{A}_{J}\biggr)F_{J}(\mathcal{B}_{J}),

and is completely equivalent to Wilson–Cowan’s in the case of a pair of populations. Indeed, we can introduce

\ignorespaces x_{J}\vcentcolon=\beta_{J}\mathcal{A}_{J}

, which is the average proportion of active neurons per unit time—that is, the firing rate—of population

\ignorespaces J

. Then, the above equation leads to

\frac{1}{\beta_{J}}\dot{x}_{J}=-x_{J}+\biggl(1-\Bigl(\frac{1}{\beta_{J}}+\frac{1}{\gamma_{J}}\Bigr)x_{J}\biggr)\alpha_{J}F_{J}(\mathcal{B}_{J}),

where we can write

\ignorespaces\mathcal{B}_{J}=\sum_{K\in\mathscr{P}}\frac{c_{JK}}{\beta_{K}}x_{K}+Q_{J}

. The parallel with Wilson–Cowan’s original model (
10
) is then quite clear if we set

\ignorespaces\tau_{J}=\nicefrac{{1}}{{\beta_{J}}}

,

\ignorespaces r_{J}=\nicefrac{{1}}{{\beta_{J}}}+\nicefrac{{1}}{{\gamma_{J}}}

,

\ignorespaces f_{J}=\alpha_{J}F_{J}

and

\ignorespaces w_{JK}=\nicefrac{{c_{JK}}}{{\beta_{K}}}

.

The above arguments show that Wilson–Cowan’s dynamical system can be seen as the subsystem (
11
) of the mean-field system (
8
), which corresponds to the domain

\mathscr{D}_{n}^{\textsc{wc}}\vcentcolon=\Bigl\{(\mathcal{A}_{J},\mathcal{R}_{J})_{J\in\mathscr{P}}\in\mathscr{D}_{n}:\forall J,\mathcal{R}_{J}=\frac{\beta_{J}}{\gamma_{J}}\mathcal{A}_{J}\Bigr\}.

It is then justified to ask whether the reduction of the system (
8
) to the subsystem (
11
) leads to a loss of richness in the dynamics.

A first hint that dynamical behaviors can be lost by the reduction of (
8
) to the subsystem (
11
) is given in the following proposition.

Proposition 2
.

The domain

\ignorespaces\mathscr{D}_{n}^{\textsc{wc}}

is not invariant by the flow of the dynamical system (
8
).

Proof:
Let

\ignorespaces f\colon\mathscr{D}_{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{2n}

denote the vector field corresponding to the differential equation (
8
). Notice that

\ignorespaces\mathscr{D}_{n}^{\textsc{wc}}

is a subset of an euclidean subspace of dimension

\ignorespaces n

in

\ignorespaces\mathbb{R}^{2n}

. Thus, for

\ignorespaces\mathscr{D}_{n}^{\textsc{wc}}

to be invariant by the flow of (
8
),

\ignorespaces f

must be tangent to

\ignorespaces\mathscr{D}_{n}^{\textsc{wc}}

at each of its points. However, this cannot be the case, because on

\ignorespaces\mathscr{D}_{n}^{\textsc{wc}}

,

\ignorespaces f

has no component along the axes associated with the refractory fractions, whereas

\ignorespaces\mathscr{D}_{n}^{\textsc{wc}}

is not orthogonal to these axes.

To be more explicit, fix a population

\ignorespaces K\in\mathscr{P}

, and choose

\ignorespaces x\in(0,1)

such that

\frac{\alpha_{K}\gamma_{K}}{\alpha_{K}\beta_{K}+\alpha_{K}\gamma_{K}+\beta_{K}\gamma_{K}}<x<\frac{\gamma_{K}}{\beta_{K}+\gamma_{K}},

which is always possible since

\ignorespaces\alpha_{K},\beta_{K},\gamma_{K}>0

. Now, define the vectors

\ignorespaces Y=(\mathcal{A}_{J},\mathcal{R}_{J})_{J\in\mathscr{P}}

and

\ignorespaces Y^{\perp}=(\mathcal{A}_{J}^{\perp},\mathcal{R}_{J}^{\perp})_{J\in\mathscr{P}}

by setting

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{K}

\displaystyle=x,

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{K}

\displaystyle=\frac{\beta_{K}}{\gamma_{K}}x,

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{K}^{\perp}

\displaystyle=1,

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{K}^{\perp}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\gamma_{K}}{\beta_{K}},

and then

\ignorespaces\mathcal{A}_{J}=\mathcal{R}_{J}=\mathcal{A}_{J}^{\perp}=\mathcal{R}_{J}^{\perp}=0

for all

\ignorespaces J\neq K

. Then

\ignorespaces Y\in\mathscr{D}_{n}^{\textsc{wc}}

and

\ignorespaces Y^{\perp}

is orthogonal to

\ignorespaces\mathscr{D}_{n}^{\textsc{wc}}

. An example of a phase plane with

\ignorespaces Y

and

\ignorespaces Y^{\perp}

is illustrated in Fig.
2
, in a simple case where the network has a single population.

Figure 2:
Example of phase plane of the mean-field system (
8
) in a case of a single population, to illustrate the proof of Proposition
2
. Note that the nullcline

\ignorespaces\dot{\mathcal{R}}=0

is the set

\ignorespaces\mathscr{D}_{1}^{\textsc{wc}}

Suppose

\ignorespaces f

is tangent to

\ignorespaces\mathscr{D}_{n}^{\textsc{wc}}

at

\ignorespaces Y

. Then

\ignorespaces f(Y)

must be orthogonal to

\ignorespaces Y^{\perp}

. Now, all components of

\ignorespaces Y^{\perp}

are zero except along the axes of population

\ignorespaces K

, and

\ignorespaces\dot{\mathcal{R}}_{K}\bigr\rvert_{Y}=0

because

\ignorespaces Y\in\mathscr{D}_{n}^{\textsc{wc}}

. Thus,

\ignorespaces\langle f(Y),Y^{\perp}\rangle=\dot{\mathcal{A}}_{K}\bigr\rvert_{Y}

. Since

\ignorespaces F_{K}

is nonnegative, we can easily compute the estimate

\displaystyle\dot{\mathcal{A}}_{K}\Bigr\rvert_{Y}

\displaystyle=-\beta_{K}x+\alpha_{K}F_{K}(\mathcal{B}_{K})\mathcal{S}_{K}

\displaystyle\leq-\beta_{K}x+\alpha_{K}\Bigl(1-\frac{\beta_{K}+\gamma_{K}}{\gamma_{K}}x\Bigr)

\displaystyle=\alpha_{K}\Bigl(1-\frac{\alpha_{K}\beta_{K}+\alpha_{K}\gamma_{K}+\beta_{K}\gamma_{K}}{\alpha_{K}\gamma_{K}}x\Bigr).

By our choice of

\ignorespaces x

, it follows that

\ignorespaces\langle f(Y),Y^{\perp}\rangle<0

, which contradicts the requirement for

\ignorespaces f(Y)

to be orthogonal to

\ignorespaces Y^{\perp}

. Hence,

\ignorespaces f

is not tangent to

\ignorespaces\mathscr{D}_{n}^{\textsc{wc}}

at

\ignorespaces Y

, so

\ignorespaces\mathscr{D}_{n}^{\textsc{wc}}

is not invariant by the flow of the dynamical system (
8
).

Since an attracting set is invariant by definition, Proposition
2
directly implies the following corollary.

Corollary
.

The domain

\ignorespaces\mathscr{D}_{n}^{\textsc{wc}}

is not an attracting set of the dynamical system (
8
), so its subsystem (
11
) is not stable.

Proposition
2
and its corollary give information about the relationship between the solutions of the mean-field system (
8
) and its Wilson–Cowan subsystem (
11
). If any solution of the mean-field system did eventually converge to a solution of the Wilson–Cowan subsystem—which would happen if

\ignorespaces\mathscr{D}_{n}^{\textsc{wc}}

was an attracting set from

\ignorespaces\mathscr{D}_{n}

—then we would expect that both models actually lead to the same predictions. However, the corollary to Proposition
2
shows that this is not true, so that we can expect to see different long-term behaviors predicted by the two models, at least in some cases. In fact, Proposition
2
shows that in general, a solution of the Wilson–Cowan subsystem is not even a solution of the mean-field system. This means that if a solution of the mean-field system meets the condition that

\ignorespaces\mathcal{R}_{J}(t)=\frac{\beta_{J}}{\gamma_{J}}\mathcal{A}_{J}(t)

for all populations

\ignorespaces J

at some time

\ignorespaces t

, this condition does not need to remain true afterwards, and the behavior predicted from that point by the Wilson–Cowan subsystem might not be the same as the behavior predicted by the mean-field system.

Nevertheless, the systems (
8
) and (
11
) share some properties: for instance, it is easy to see that their fixed points are exactly the same, since the only way for

\ignorespaces\dot{\mathcal{R}}_{J}=0

is that

\ignorespaces\mathcal{R}_{J}=\frac{\beta_{J}}{\gamma_{J}}\mathcal{A}_{J}

. However, it is not clear at all if the stability of these fixed point remains the same. In fact, we will see in the next section that it is not always the case.

An important difference between these two models is the disparity in the dimension of the dynamical system for the same number of populations. Indeed, in order to model the dynamics of a network of

\ignorespaces n

neural populations, our mean-field system uses a system of

\ignorespaces 2n

equation whereas the Wilson–Cowan system only uses

\ignorespaces n

. In particular, this means that our mean-field system has enough dimensions to allow oscillations in the activity of a single neural population, unlike the Wilson–Cowan system. Indeed, we will see in section
4.1
an example of an excitatory population whose activity oscillates due to the refractory period. However, it is still not possible with our model to predict oscillations in the activity of a single inhibitory population.

Proposition 3
.

In the case of a single population, if

\ignorespaces\beta+\gamma>\alpha c\sup F^{\prime}

, then the mean-field system (
8
) has no cycles in the domain

\ignorespaces\mathscr{D}_{1}

.

Remark
.

Since the transition rates

\ignorespaces\alpha

,

\ignorespaces\beta

and

\ignorespaces\gamma

are positive and

\ignorespaces F^{\prime}

is nonnegative, the hypothesis of Proposition
3
is always satisfied for a single inhibitory population, since in that case

\ignorespaces c<0

.

Proof:
Let

\ignorespaces f\colon\mathscr{D}_{1}\to\mathbb{R}^{2}

denote the vector field corresponding to the mean-field system (
8
), so that

\displaystyle\dot{\mathcal{A}}

\displaystyle=f_{1}(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{R}),

\displaystyle\dot{\mathcal{R}}

\displaystyle=f_{2}(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{R}).

Direct calculations show that

\partial_{\mathcal{A}}f_{1}(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{R})=-\beta-\alpha F(\mathcal{B})+\alpha F^{\prime}(\mathcal{B})c\mathcal{S}

and that

\partial_{\mathcal{R}}f_{2}(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{R})=-\gamma.

Since

\ignorespaces F

takes its values between

\ignorespaces 0

and

\ignorespaces 1

, it follows that

\nabla\cdot f(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{R})\leq-\beta-\gamma+\alpha F^{\prime}(\mathcal{B})c\mathcal{S}.

(12)

Now, recall that

\ignorespaces\alpha,\beta,\gamma>0

, that

\ignorespaces\mathcal{S}\geq 0

since we assume that the state

\ignorespaces(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{R})\in\mathscr{D}_{1}

, and that

\ignorespaces F

is increasing. Therefore, the divergence (
12
) is always negative when

\ignorespaces c\leq 0

. On the other hand, when

\ignorespaces c>0

,

\nabla\cdot f(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{R})\leq-\beta-\gamma+\alpha c\sup F^{\prime},

which is always negative provided that

\ignorespaces\beta+\gamma>\alpha c\sup F^{\prime}

. As stated in the remark above, this condition includes the case where

\ignorespaces c\leq 0

. Thus, if

\ignorespaces\beta+\gamma>\alpha c\sup F^{\prime}

, then the divergence of the vector field is always negative in

\ignorespaces\mathscr{D}_{1}

, and the criterion of
Bendixson 1901
guarantees that there are no cycles in

\ignorespaces\mathscr{D}_{1}

.

In the same way, the Wilson–Cowan system cannot predict chaotic behavior with two populations, since it has only two dimensions while existence of chaotic solutions requires at least three dimensions in continuous dynamical systems. However, there is no dimensional argument to rule out this possibility with our mean-field system, since it has four dimensions for two populations. We will indeed see in section
4.2
an example of a pair of two excitatory populations that exhibit chaotic behavior.

To further compare the mean-field system and its Wilson–Cowan subsystem, we can add an extra parameter

\ignorespaces\varepsilon

to the mean-field system (
8
), and consider the dynamical system

\displaystyle\dot{\mathcal{A}}_{J}

\displaystyle=-\beta_{J}\mathcal{A}_{J}+\alpha_{J}F_{J}(\mathcal{B}_{J})\mathcal{S}_{J},

(13a)

\displaystyle\varepsilon\dot{\mathcal{R}}_{J}

\displaystyle=-\gamma_{J}\mathcal{R}_{J}+\beta_{J}\mathcal{A}_{J}.

(13b)

The parameter

\ignorespaces\varepsilon

can then be used to study the transition between the models. First, the system (
8
) corresponds to the case

\ignorespaces\varepsilon=1

. Then, in the regime where

\ignorespaces 0<\varepsilon\ll 1

, (
13
) is a slow-fast system with two time scales, where the active fractions of populations are the slow variables whereas the refractory fractions are the fast variables. Ultimately, in the limit where

\ignorespaces\varepsilon

goes to zero, the fast components can be considered to be at equilibrium, so that each refractory fraction is forced to

\ignorespaces\mathcal{R}_{J}=\frac{\beta_{J}}{\gamma_{J}}\mathcal{A}_{J}

, and we retrieve the Wilson–Cowan subsystem (
11
). This suggests that the reduction of the mean-field system to the Wilson–Cowan subsystem is valid when the refractory fractions of populations vary much faster than their active fractions, which is the case when the firing rates of populations are small compared to the rates

\ignorespaces\beta_{J}

and

\ignorespaces\gamma_{J}

. However, this approximation is no longer valid for regimes of larger firing rates where the activation can occur on a time scale comparable to the transitions in and out of the refractory state. It follows that our model can be seen as an extension of the Wilson–Cowan model that is still valid when the firing rate cannot be taken as tending to 0.

5
Conclusion

The Wilson–Cowan model has played an important role in the description of neural systems at the macroscopic level. It has been shown that when considering several excitatory and inhibitory populations, the Wilson–Cowan model can exhibit rich dynamics such as oscillations, bistability and chaos. Furthermore, it is a useful tool in better understanding biological neural networks, especially sensory systems.

One of the assumptions on which the Wilson–Cowan model relies is that the ratio of the numbers of active and refractory neurons is constant in a single population. In this work, we showed that lifting this assumption can reveal novel dynamics in the model, such as oscillations in the activity of a single population or chaotic behavior in the activity of two populations.

An interesting byproduct of our method is that when constructing the model as done in section
2.2
, the way in which the dynamics might be affected by correlations between the activities of different populations becomes quite clear. Indeed, to obtain a closed dynamical system, we chose to neglect covariances in equations (
7
), which led us to approximate the expectations

\ignorespaces\mathbb{E}[F_{J}(B_{J}^{t})S_{t}^{J}]

by the corresponding functions where the variables

\ignorespaces S_{t}^{J}

and

\ignorespaces B_{t}^{J}

are replaced with their expectations. Using a higher-order moment closure, we could take into account the correlations between activities of different populations. We intend to investigate this in future work.

Appendix A
Alternative construction of the model

In this section, we provide an alternative construction of the model presented in section
2
, in which we allow parameters to be random, independent and identically distributed over populations. As long as we keep the mean-field assumption, the resulting macroscopic is still the dynamical system (
8
). This construction is presented in greater detail and for a more general Markov chain in chapter 2 of
(
Painchaud 2021
)
; the special case of the Markov chain presented here is discussed in section 4.2 of the latter reference. For basic definitions and results about stochastic processes and Markov chains, we refer to the books by
Doob 1990
and
Norris 1997
.

We start from a similar setting as that described at the beginning of section
2
. We consider a network of

\ignorespaces N

neurons labelled from integers from 1 to

\ignorespaces N

, and split into a collection

\ignorespaces\mathscr{P}

of populations, which are assumed to be made of a large number of neurons. The weight matrix

\ignorespaces W

is now random, defined on a probability space

\ignorespaces(H,\mathscr{H},\mu)

, where

\ignorespaces\mathscr{H}

and

\ignorespaces\mu

respectively are a

\ignorespaces\sigma

-algebra and a probability measure on the set

\ignorespaces H

. We still interpret links between neurons as being either excitatory or inhibitory according to the sign of the corresponding entry of

\ignorespaces W

. Then, for each neuron

\ignorespaces j

, we consider real-valued random variables

\ignorespaces\alpha_{j}

,

\ignorespaces\beta_{j}

,

\ignorespaces\gamma_{j}

and

\ignorespaces\theta_{j}

, defined on

\ignorespaces(H,\mathscr{H},\mu)

as well, the first three being nonnegative. We assume that all parameters

\ignorespaces\alpha_{j}

,

\ignorespaces\beta_{j}

,

\ignorespaces\gamma_{j}

,

\ignorespaces\theta_{j}

and

\ignorespaces W_{jk}

are independent random variables, and that they are identically distributed over populations in

\ignorespaces\mathscr{P}

.

We still see the parameters

\ignorespaces\beta_{j}

and

\ignorespaces\gamma_{j}

as being the transition rates of neuron

\ignorespaces j

as in Fig.
1
. For the reduction to the macroscopic model to be possible, we will use a different activation rate:

a_{j}(\eta,x)\vcentcolon=\alpha_{j}\mathbbone_{T_{j}(x)}(\eta)

where

\ignorespaces\mathbbone_{T_{j}(x)}

is the indicator function of the set

T_{j}(x)\vcentcolon=\Bigl\{\eta\in H:\sum_{k=1}^{N}W_{jk}(\eta)\operatorname{Re}x_{k}+Q_{J}>\theta_{j}(\eta)\Bigr\},

where the external input

\ignorespaces Q_{J}

is still assumed to be constant over population

\ignorespaces J

.

Now, any

\ignorespaces\eta\in H

fixes a choice of parameters, allowing to construct a Markov chain as in section
2.1.1
. To do so, we define the matrix

\ignorespaces M^{\eta}\vcentcolon=\{m^{\eta}(x,y):x,y\in E\}

with entries

m^{\eta}(x,y)\vcentcolon=\sum_{j=1}^{N}m_{j}^{\eta}(x,y)\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}k=1\\
k\neq j\end{subarray}}^{N}\delta_{x_{k}y_{k}}

where

\displaystyle m_{j}^{\eta}(x,y)\vcentcolon=a_{j}(\eta,x)

\displaystyle(1-\lvert x_{j}\rvert)\bigl(\operatorname{Re}y_{j}-(1-\lvert y_{j}\rvert)\bigr)

\displaystyle{}+\beta_{j}(\eta)

\displaystyle\operatorname{Re}(x_{j})(\operatorname{Im}y_{j}-\operatorname{Re}y_{j})

\displaystyle{}+\gamma_{j}(\eta)

\displaystyle\operatorname{Im}(x_{j})\bigl((1-\lvert y_{j}\rvert)-\operatorname{Im}y_{j}\bigr).

Again,

\ignorespaces M^{\eta}

is the generator of a continuous-time Markov chain. Hence, it follows from the Kolmogorov extension theorem that a probability measure

\ignorespaces\mathbb{P}^{\eta}

exists on

\ignorespaces(\Omega,\mathscr{F})\vcentcolon=(E,2^{E})^{[0,\infty)}

such that for any

\ignorespaces x,y\in E

, as

\ignorespaces\Delta t\downarrow 0

,

\mathbb{P}^{\eta}[X_{t+\Delta t}=y\nonscript\>|\nonscript\>\mathopen{}X_{t}=x]=\delta_{xy}+m^{\eta}(x,y)\Delta t+o(\Delta t),

where

\ignorespaces\{X_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}

is the coordinate mapping process on

\ignorespaces(\Omega,\mathscr{F})

, defined as

\ignorespaces X_{t}(\omega)\vcentcolon=\omega(t)

. Similar relations to (
1
) with a dependence on

\ignorespaces\eta

also hold. The measures

\ignorespaces\mathbb{P}^{\eta}

can be combined in a single measure

\ignorespaces\mathbb{P}

on the product space

\ignorespaces(H\times\Omega,\mathscr{H}\otimes\mathscr{F})

such that

\mathbb{E}[Z]=\int_{H}\int_{\Omega}Z(\eta,\omega)\operatorname{d\!}{}\mathbb{P}^{\eta}(\omega)\operatorname{d\!}{}\mu(\eta)

(23)

for any random variable

\ignorespaces Z

on

\ignorespaces(H\times\Omega,\mathscr{H}\otimes\mathscr{F})

, where

\ignorespaces\mathbb{E}

is the expectation with respect to

\ignorespaces\mathbb{P}

.

To study the average behavior of the Markov chain, we start by defining

\displaystyle p_{j}^{\eta}(t)

\displaystyle\vcentcolon=\mathbb{P}[X_{t}^{j}=1\nonscript\>|\nonscript\>\mathopen{}\eta],

\displaystyle q_{j}^{\eta}(t)

\displaystyle\vcentcolon=\mathbb{P}[X_{t}^{j}=0\nonscript\>|\nonscript\>\mathopen{}\eta],

\displaystyle r_{j}^{\eta}(t)

\displaystyle\vcentcolon=\mathbb{P}[X_{t}^{j}=i\nonscript\>|\nonscript\>\mathopen{}\eta],

where we condition on the projection onto

\ignorespaces H

. Then, in the same way as in section
2.1.2
, it can be shown that

\displaystyle\dot{p}_{j}^{\eta}(t)

\displaystyle=-\beta_{j}(\eta)p_{j}^{\eta}(t)+\mathbb{E}\big[\bigl(1-\lvert X_{t}^{j}\rvert\bigr)a_{j}(\eta,X_{t})\nonscript\>|\nonscript\>\mathopen{}\eta\big],

\displaystyle\dot{q}_{j}^{\eta}(t)

\displaystyle=-\mathbb{E}\big[\bigl(1-\lvert X_{t}^{j}\rvert\bigr)a_{j}(\eta,X_{t})\nonscript\>|\nonscript\>\mathopen{}\eta\big]+\gamma_{j}(\eta)r_{j}^{\eta}(t),

\displaystyle\dot{r}_{j}^{\eta}(t)

\displaystyle=-\gamma_{j}(\eta)r_{j}^{\eta}(t)+\beta_{j}(\eta)p_{j}^{\eta}(t).

Now, we can define

\displaystyle p_{j}(t)

\displaystyle\vcentcolon=\mathbb{P}[X_{t}^{j}=1],

\displaystyle q_{j}(t)

\displaystyle\vcentcolon=\mathbb{P}[X_{t}^{j}=0],

\displaystyle r_{j}(t)

\displaystyle\vcentcolon=\mathbb{P}[X_{t}^{j}=i].

Then

\ignorespaces\dot{p}_{j}(t)=\frac{\operatorname{d\!}{}}{\operatorname{d\!}{}t}\int_{H}p_{j}^{\eta}(t)\operatorname{d\!}{}\mu(\eta)

. The functions

\ignorespaces\eta\mapsto p_{j}^{\eta}(t)

can be dominated by the function

\ignorespaces\sum_{x\in E}\lvert\beta_{j}+\alpha_{j}\mathbbone_{T_{j}(x)}\rvert

, which is integrable over

\ignorespaces H

, so that the derivative can be passed under the integral sign. Similar bounds hold for

\ignorespaces q_{j}^{\eta}(t)

and

\ignorespaces r_{j}^{\eta}(t)

, and it follows that

\displaystyle\dot{p}_{j}(t)

\displaystyle=-\mathbb{E}[\beta_{j}\operatorname{Re}X_{t}^{j}]+\mathbb{E}\big[\bigl(1-\lvert X_{t}^{j}\rvert\bigr)a_{j}(\cdot,X_{t})\big],

\displaystyle\dot{q}_{j}(t)

\displaystyle=-\mathbb{E}\big[\bigl(1-\lvert X_{t}^{j}\rvert\bigr)a_{j}(\cdot,X_{t})\big]+\mathbb{E}[\gamma_{j}\operatorname{Im}X_{t}^{j}],

\displaystyle\dot{r}_{j}(t)

\displaystyle=-\mathbb{E}[\gamma_{j}\operatorname{Im}X_{t}^{j}]+\mathbb{E}[\beta_{j}\operatorname{Re}X_{t}^{j}].

To pass to the macroscopic point of view, we consider for

\ignorespaces J\in\mathscr{P}

the fractions of population

\ignorespaces A_{t}^{J}

,

\ignorespaces R_{t}^{J}

and

\ignorespaces S_{t}^{J}

defined as in section
2.2
, that is,

\displaystyle A_{t}^{J}

\displaystyle\vcentcolon=\frac{1}{\lvert J\rvert}\sum_{j\in J}\operatorname{Re}X_{t}^{j},

\displaystyle R_{t}^{J}

\displaystyle\vcentcolon=\frac{1}{\lvert J\rvert}\sum_{j\in J}\operatorname{Im}X_{t}^{j},

\displaystyle S_{t}^{J}

\displaystyle\vcentcolon=\frac{1}{\lvert J\rvert}\sum_{j\in J}\bigl(1-\lvert X_{t}^{j}\rvert\bigr).

Now that parameters are random, we cannot simply sum over populations to obtain the differential equations (
7
) from the expressions we obtained for the derivatives of

\ignorespaces p_{j}

,

\ignorespaces q_{j}

and

\ignorespaces r_{j}

. However, we can still argue that the same macroscopic dynamical system describes the average behavior of the network’s activity, at least approximatively. To do so, we define the subpopulations

J_{t}^{\xi}\vcentcolon=\{j\in J:X_{t}^{j}=\xi\}

for

\ignorespaces\xi\in\{0,1,i\}

. The size of these subpopulations can easily be related to the macroscopic state variables. Indeed, since

\ignorespaces\operatorname{Re}X_{t}^{j}=1

if and only if

\ignorespaces X_{t}^{j}=1

, then

\ignorespaces\lvert J_{t}^{1}\rvert=\lvert J\rvert A_{t}^{J}

. Similarly,

\ignorespaces\lvert J_{t}^{0}\rvert=\lvert J\rvert S_{t}^{J}

and

\ignorespaces\lvert J_{t}^{i}\rvert=\lvert J\rvert R_{t}^{J}

. Now, notice that

\frac{1}{\lvert J\rvert}\sum_{j\in J}\beta_{j}\operatorname{Re}X_{t}^{j}=\frac{1}{\lvert J\rvert}\sum_{j\in J_{t}^{1}}\beta_{j}=\frac{A_{t}^{J}}{\lvert J_{t}^{1}\rvert}\sum_{j\in J_{t}^{1}}\beta_{j}.

Assuming that the random variables

\ignorespaces\beta_{j}

are independent and identically distributed over populations, if the size of

\ignorespaces J

is very large, the law of large numbers leads to expect the average

\ignorespaces\frac{1}{\lvert J\rvert}\sum_{j\in J}\beta_{j}

to be very close to the expectation

\ignorespaces\beta_{J}\vcentcolon=\mathbb{E}[\beta_{j}]

. In the same way, since the value of

\ignorespaces\beta_{j}

should not influence the probability that

\ignorespaces j

is active at time

\ignorespaces t

, we expect the average

\ignorespaces\frac{1}{\lvert J_{t}^{1}\rvert}\sum_{j\in J_{t}^{1}}\beta_{j}

to be very close to

\ignorespaces\beta_{J}

. This leads to approximate

\frac{1}{\lvert J\rvert}\sum_{j\in J}\beta_{j}\operatorname{Re}X_{t}^{j}\approx\beta_{J}A_{t}^{J},

(24)

for a large population

\ignorespaces J

. In the same way, we approximate

\frac{1}{\lvert J\rvert}\sum_{j\in J}\gamma_{j}\operatorname{Im}X_{t}^{j}=\frac{R_{t}^{J}}{\lvert J_{t}^{i}\rvert}\sum_{j\in J_{t}^{i}}\gamma_{j}\approx\gamma_{J}R_{t}^{J}

(25)

where

\ignorespaces\gamma_{J}\vcentcolon=\mathbb{E}[\gamma_{j}]

for

\ignorespaces j\in J

, and

\sum_{k=1}^{N}W_{jk}\operatorname{Re}X_{t}^{k}=\sum_{K\in\mathscr{P}}\sum_{k\in K_{t}^{1}}W_{jk}\\
\approx\sum_{K\in\mathscr{P}}\lvert K\rvert A_{t}^{K}W_{JK}=\sum_{K\in\mathscr{P}}c_{JK}A_{t}^{K}

where

\ignorespaces c_{JK}\vcentcolon=\lvert K\rvert W_{JK}

and

\ignorespaces W_{JK}\vcentcolon=\mathbb{E}[W_{jk}]

for

\ignorespaces j\in J

and

\ignorespaces k\in K

. The last case requires more attention. Using the last approximation, we start by approximating

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\lvert J\rvert}\sum_{j\in J}

\displaystyle\bigl(1-\lvert X_{t}^{j}\rvert\bigr)a_{j}(\cdot,X_{t})

\displaystyle=\frac{S_{t}^{J}}{\lvert J_{t}^{0}\rvert}\sum_{j\in J_{t}^{0}}\alpha_{j}\mathbbone_{\bigl\{\sum_{k=1}^{N}W_{jk}\operatorname{Re}X_{t}^{k}+Q_{J}>\theta_{j}\bigr\}}

\displaystyle\approx\frac{S_{t}^{J}}{\lvert J_{t}^{0}\rvert}\sum_{j\in J_{t}^{0}}\alpha_{j}\mathbbone_{\{B_{t}^{J}>\theta_{j}\}},

where

\ignorespaces B_{t}^{J}\vcentcolon=\sum_{K\in\mathscr{P}}c_{JK}A_{t}^{K}+Q_{J}

. Using the same argument as in the last cases, we approximate by replacing the rate

\ignorespaces\alpha_{j}\mathbbone_{\{B_{t}^{J}>\theta_{j}\}}

with its expectation given the state of the network. Since

\ignorespaces\alpha_{j}

and

\ignorespaces\theta_{j}

are independent, we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\lvert J\rvert}\sum_{j\in J}

\displaystyle\bigl(1-\lvert X_{t}^{j}\rvert\bigr)a_{j}(\cdot,X_{t})\approx\alpha_{J}F_{J}(B_{t}^{J})S_{t}^{J}

(26)

where

\ignorespaces\alpha_{J}\vcentcolon=\mathbb{E}[\alpha_{j}]

for

\ignorespaces j\in J

and where

\ignorespaces F_{J}

denotes the cumulative distribution function of

\ignorespaces\theta_{j}

for

\ignorespaces j\in J

, which is the expectation of

\ignorespaces\mathbbone_{\{B_{t}^{J}>\theta_{j}\}}

given the state of the network. We remark that this approximation is based on idea that the set

\ignorespaces J_{t}^{0}

is large, and that the approximate activation rate

\ignorespaces\alpha_{j}\mathbbone_{\{B_{t}^{J}>\theta_{j}\}}

has no influence on the probability for neuron

\ignorespaces j

to be sensitive at time

\ignorespaces t

. However, one could expect this to be false, especially if the standard deviation of

\ignorespaces\theta_{j}

is high: if the threshold

\ignorespaces\theta_{j}

is higher, it is harder for

\ignorespaces j

to reach its threshold input and to activate. Therefore, we expect this last approximation to be valid only when the standard deviation of

\ignorespaces\theta_{j}

is small.

Now, consider the macroscopic state variables

\ignorespaces\mathcal{A}_{J}(t)\vcentcolon=\mathbb{E}[A_{t}^{J}]

,

\ignorespaces\mathcal{R}_{J}(t)\vcentcolon=\mathbb{E}[R_{t}^{J}]

and

\ignorespaces\mathcal{S}_{J}(t)\vcentcolon=\mathbb{E}[S_{t}^{J}]

. By linearity of the derivative,

\dot{\mathcal{A}}_{J}=\frac{1}{\lvert J\rvert}\sum_{j\in J}\dot{p}_{j}.

Hence, the approximations (
24
) and (
26
) lead to approximate

\displaystyle\dot{\mathcal{A}}_{J}(t)

\displaystyle\approx\mathbb{E}\big[-\beta_{J}A_{t}^{J}+\alpha_{J}F_{J}(B_{t}^{J})S_{t}^{J}\big]

\displaystyle=-\beta_{J}\mathcal{A}_{J}(t)+\alpha_{J}\mathbb{E}[F_{J}(B_{t}^{J})S_{t}^{J}].

In the same way, the approximations (
24
), (
25
) and (
26
) lead to

\displaystyle\dot{\mathcal{R}}_{J}(t)

\displaystyle\approx-\gamma_{J}\mathcal{R}_{J}(t)+\beta_{J}\mathcal{A}_{J}(t)

and

\displaystyle\dot{\mathcal{S}}_{J}(t)

\displaystyle\approx-\alpha_{J}\mathbb{E}[F_{J}(B_{t}^{J})S_{t}^{J}]+\gamma_{J}\mathcal{R}_{J}(t).

Thus, the dynamical system (
7
) describes approximatively the evolution of the network’s activity. In the same way as in section
2.2
, the mean-field assumption yields the dynamical system (
8
).

We conclude by noticing that, even though the approximations we made here seem reasonable, it would be far from obvious to make the argument fully rigorous by properly taking a limit as the sizes of the populations grow infinitely large. This is because the probability measure

\ignorespaces\mathbb{P}

defined in (
23
) depends on the measures

\ignorespaces\mathbb{P}^{\eta}

, which are defined from the generators

\ignorespaces M^{\eta}

and thus depend on the network’s size. Hence, to vary the network’s size, we would need to vary the probability measure, and it is not clear how these different measures can be related. Moreover, the sets over which averages of parameters are approximated to their expectations are subpopulations of neurons that are in a given state at a given time, which are random. Thus, to use correctly some sort of law of large numbers, we would need to ensure that these subpopulations become arbitrarily large with a high probability.

\displaystyle=1-\mathcal{A}-\frac{\beta\mathcal{A}}{\alpha F(\mathcal{B})}

and

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}\Bigr\rvert_{\dot{\mathcal{R}}=0}

\displaystyle=\frac{\beta}{\gamma}\,\mathcal{A}.

An illustration of these nullclines in the case where

\ignorespaces\alpha

and

\ignorespaces\beta

have the values of Example
4.1
is given on the phase plane on the right panel of Fig.
3
. The fixed points of dynamical system are the points where these nullclines intersect, so they correspond to the zeros of

g(\mathcal{A})\vcentcolon=1-\mathcal{A}-\frac{\beta\mathcal{A}}{\alpha F(\mathcal{B})}-\frac{\beta}{\gamma}\mathcal{A}.

Notice that

\ignorespaces g(0)=1

while

g\Bigl(\frac{\gamma}{\beta+\gamma}\Bigr)=-\frac{\beta\gamma}{\alpha(\beta+\gamma)F(\frac{c\gamma}{\beta+\gamma}+Q)}<0.

Thus, the intermediate value theorem shows that the system has at least one fixed point

\ignorespaces(\mathcal{A}^{*},\mathcal{R}^{*})

with

\ignorespaces\mathcal{A}^{*}\in[0,\frac{\gamma}{\beta+\gamma}]

. At such a fixed point, the refractory fraction is

\ignorespaces\mathcal{R}^{*}=\frac{\beta}{\gamma}\mathcal{A}^{*}=\frac{\beta}{\beta+\gamma}

, and

\ignorespaces(\mathcal{A}^{*},\mathcal{R}^{*})\in\mathscr{D}_{1}

.

Now, assuming that

\ignorespaces F

is given by the sigmoidal function (
14
), it has the property that

F^{\prime}(\mathcal{B})=\frac{1}{s_{\theta}}F(\mathcal{B})\bigl(1-F(\mathcal{B})\bigr).

Using this result, it is straightforward to compute

g^{\prime\prime}(\mathcal{A})=\frac{\beta c}{\alpha s_{\theta}}\biggl(\frac{1}{F(\mathcal{B})}-1\biggr)\biggl(2-\frac{c\mathcal{A}}{s_{\theta}}\biggr).

Since

\ignorespaces F<1

, it follows that

\ignorespaces g^{\prime\prime}

has a single zero. Hence,

\ignorespaces g

has a single inflection point, and it cannot have more than three zeros.

The above discussion shows that the system has between one and three fixed points. It would be hard to determine precisely the location and the number of fixed points analytically, but it can be done numerically. Indeed, for a given value of

\ignorespaces\alpha

, it is possible to compute the values

\ignorespaces\beta

for which the nullclines are tangent to each other. These values of

\ignorespaces\beta

corresponds to the boundary between the domains of the

\ignorespaces\alpha

–

\ignorespaces\beta

space where the system admits one and three fixed points. Then, when there is only one fixed point, it is possible to find it numerically, and to compute the eigenvalues of the Jacobian matrix of the system to determine its stability. The result is illustrated in Fig.
15
. The regions
A
,
B
and
C
of the bifurcation diagram are those where the system admits a single fixed point, and region
D
is that where it admits three fixed points. The boundary of region
D
corresponds to the points where the nullclines are tangent.

Two important conclusions can be understood from these numerical results. First, in region
B
, the system admits a single fixed point which is unstable, since at least one eigenvalue of the Jacobian matrix of the system has positive real part. Since the domain

\ignorespaces\mathscr{D}_{1}

is always invariant, it then follows from the theorem of Poincaré–
Bendixson 1901
that a limit cycle exists somewhere in

\ignorespaces\mathscr{D}_{1}

. Moreover, on the curves that bound region
B
, where the real part of the eigenvalue

\ignorespaces\lambda_{+}

is zero, the imaginary part of

\ignorespaces\lambda_{+}

is nonzero. Hence, on these curves, the eigenvalues of the Jacobian matrix are conjugates, and their real part changes sign. This implies that the system undergoes Hopf bifurcations on these curves.

Therefore, if the values of

\ignorespaces\alpha

and

\ignorespaces\beta

lie in region
B
in Fig
15
, then a limit cycle exists. We remark however that, a priori, this condition is sufficient but not necessary. Indeed, if the Hopf bifurcations on the bounds of region
B
are subcritical, the limit cycle may very well continue to exist outside of region
B
. Further analysis would be needed to determine with more precision the values of

\ignorespaces\alpha

and

\ignorespaces\beta

which yield a limit cycle.
