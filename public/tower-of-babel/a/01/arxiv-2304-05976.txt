Bayesian Causal Inference in Doubly Gaussian DAG–probit Models
Rasool Tahmasbi, Keyvan Tahmasbi

Abstract
We consider modeling a binary response variable together with a set of covariates for two groups under observational data. The grouping variable can be the confounding variable (the common cause of treatment and outcome), gender, case/control, ethnicity, etc. Given the covariates and a binary latent variable, the goal is to construct two directed acyclic graphs (DAGs), while sharing some common parameters. The set of nodes, which represent the variables, are the same for both groups but the directed edges between nodes, which represent the causal relationships between the variables, can be potentially different. For each group, we also estimate the effect size for each node. We assume that each group follows a Gaussian distribution under its DAG. Given the parent nodes, the joint distribution of DAG is conditioanlly independent due to the Markov property of DAGs. We introduce the concept of Gaussian DAG–probit model under two groups and hence doubly Gaussian DAG–probit model. To estimate the skeleton of the DAGs and the model parameters, we took samples from the posterior distribution of doubly Gaussian DAG–probit model via MCMC method. We validated the proposed method using a comprehensive simulation experiment and applied it on two real datasets. Furthermore, we validated the results of the real data analysis using well-known experimental studies to show the value of the proposed grouping variable in the causality domain.

Bayesian Causal Inference; Causal Effects;
\mathrm{do}

Introduction

The goal of etiological research is to uncover causal effects, whilst prediction research aims to predict an outcome with the best accuracy. Causal and prediction research usually require different methods, and yet their findings may be meaningless if the right method is not used. Machine-learning (ML) systems have made astounding progress in analyzing data patterns, but ML algorithms cannot tell whether a crowing rooster makes the sun rise, or the other way around (
Pearl & Mackenzie (2020)
). So by just training a model on historical data we cannot say anything about the causes or the direction of causation, which is crucial as well. More granular distiction between the causality asnd prediction methods can be found in
Ramspek et al. (2021)
and
Gische et al. (2021)
.

Type of data availibility is also important for picking the right causality model. The gold standard data for answering the ”what if” questions, which are related to the causal effect discovery, is
randomized
data. However, it is often not feasible to run randomized experiments due to the ethical issues, costs, or running time. In this case, causal inferences can be obtained from
observational
data (
Hernán & Robins (2010)
).

Estimating causality from observational data is essential in many data science questions but can be a challenging task.Some mathematical models such as Directed Acyclic Graph (DAG) and Structural Equation Model (SEM) are important tools to infer the causal effects based on observational data. DAGs have been extensively used to construct statistical models embodying conditional independence relations in graphical models (
Lauritzen (1996)
) while causal DAGs will be instrumental to define the notion of causal effect (
Pearl et al. (2000)
). Some models such as DoWhy (
Sharma & Kiciman (2020)
) assume that the causal graph is known and the goal is to estimate the effect sizes.

Gaussian graphical models (GGM) are also used in many causal discovery problems. GGM is a statistical framework defined with respect to a graph, in which the nodes index a collection of jointly Gaussian random variables and the edges represent the conditional independence relations among the variables. A number of papers have studied covariance estimation in the context of GMM selection. For example,
M. Chen et al. (2016)
estimated the covariate-adjusted GMM using asymptotic theory. They showed that for each finite subgraph, their estimator is asymptotically normal and efficient.
Cai et al. (2013)
introduced a sparse high dimensional multivariate regression model for studying conditional independence relationships among a set of genes adjusting for possible genetic effects. They presented a covariate-adjusted precision matrix estimation method using a constrained
\ell_{1}
minimization.

The problem of latent variables, where all the variables (both observed and
latent) are jointly Gaussian, is studied by
Chandrasekaran et al. (2012)
.
Chandrasekaran et al. (2012)
studied the case when the graph is sparse and there are a few additional latent components in the DAG structure. They proposed a convex program based on
\ell_{1}
and nuclear norm regularized maximum likelihood for latent-variable graphical model selection.
Wu et al. (2017)
proposed a method for learning latent variable graphical models via
\ell_{1}
and trace penalized D-trace loss. Gaussian latent variables also have many applications in the generative models such as variational autoencoder (VAE) models, introduced by
Kingma & Welling (2013)
.
Learning individual-level causal effects (ILCE) from observational data, which is important for the policy makers, is studied by
Louizos et al. (2017)
. Examples of ILCE include understanding the effect of medications on a
patient’s health, or of teaching methods on a student’s chance of graduation.
Their approach is based on the VAE to estimate the causal effects of the latent variables on large datasets. For a comprehensive review on theoretical properties and optimalities of the estimation of structured covariance and precision matrices, see
Cai, Ren & Zhou (2016)
and the references therein.

Motivated by the probit regression model,
Guo et al. (2015)
introduced the probit graphical model.
Castelletti & Consonni (2021)
extended the probit graphical models by introducing DAG–probit models. For DAG–probit model, they considered a binary response which is potentially affected by a set of continuous variables. Their model assumes that there is just one DAG.

In reality, there are many cases when we should model our data for different groups, separately. The grouping variable can be gender, different ethnicities, or case/control studies. For example, is well known that the physiological differences between men and women affect drug activity, including pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics. If we pick a model that does not properly take genders into account, then the results will be bias, a serious problem which is called
gender bias in research
(see
Holdcroft (2007)
and
Aragón et al. (2023)
). As another example, our metabolism changes with age (
Pontzer et al. (2021)
) and therefore a method that can model the age differences is preferred. The results of comparing the outcome between two groups can sometimes be confounded and even reversed by an unrecognised third variable. This concept is known as
Simpson’s Paradox
and confounding variable can be gender, ethnicity, etc.

In this paper, we introduce the
doubly Gaussian DAG–probit model
in Section
2
by allowing groups to have different DAGs while some of the model parameters are shared between the groups. Allowing a model with the flexibility to have different structures for different groups can potentially take care of Simpson paradox and can reduce gender bias in research. The proposed model is drived for binary grouping variables but can be extended for any non-binary grouping variable as well. The shared parameteres can be the common edges between the two groups, a common cut-off parameter or nodes variance. As an example,
Qiao et al. (2020)
studied the network of EEG (electroencephalogram) signals on alcoholic and control groups. They expressed that since the graphical structures for alcoholic and non-alcoholic groups share some common edges, it is advantageous to jointly estimate two networks. For estimating the heritability using geneome data, it is common to assume that the effect of SNPs (single-nucleotide polymorphism) are the same for all individuals (check our paper
Evans et al. (2018)
). Therefore, we assume that the effect of a gene for all individuals (even in different groups) is the same and changes in the phenotyps are due the differences in the gene network, which enables us to jointly estimate this parameter from all groups.

Choice of priors can speed up the MCMC algorithm and simplify the posterior formula. Our priors for the model parameters are presented in Section
3
. After computing the posteriors for the parameters, we present our MCMC algorithm in Section
4
.
Dawid & Musio (2022)
expressed and contrasted two distinct problem areas for statistical causality: studying the likely effects of an intervention (effects of causes) and studying whether there is a causal link between the observed exposure and outcome in an individual case (causes of effects). The effect of interventions in terms of the observed probabilities using
do calulus
is computed in section
5
and causes of effects can be estimated from the last step of our MCMC Algotithm.

To assess the performance of the proposed method, a comprehensive simulation study is performed in Section
6
. We provide several evaluation metrics to check the MCMC algorithm and model performance. We apply or method on two well-known real datasets and illustrate the results in Section
7
. We use the genome network data of breast cancer for the first example and compare our results with the well-known published genetics papers to validated our outputs in Section
7.1
. For the second real data analysis, we study the impact of airborne particles on the cardiovascular mortality rate (CMR) in Section
7.2
. The finals points for discussion are presented in Section
8
. Some proof of the posteriors together with more simulation results are provided in Appendix.

Methodology

Let
\mathcal{D}=(V,E)
be a DAG, where
V=\{1,\dots,q\}
is a set of vertices (or nodes) and
E\subseteq V\times V
a set of edges whose elements are
(u,v)\equiv u\to v
, such that if
(u,v)\in E

then
(v,u)\notin E
. In addition,
\mathcal{D}
contains no cycles. For a given node
v
, if
u\to v\in E
, we say that
u
is a
parent
of

v
(conversely
v
is a
child
of
u
). The parent set of
v
in
\mathcal{D}
is denoted by
\mathrm{pa}(v)
, the set of children by
\mathrm{ch}(v)
. Moreover, we denote by
\mathrm{fa}(v)=v\cup\mathrm{pa}(v)
the
family
of
v
in
\mathcal{D}
. Finally, we say that a DAG is complete if all vertices are joined by an edge. For further theory and notation on DAGs we refer to
Lauritzen (1996)
.

A DAG can be expressed by a probabilistic model via conditional dependence structure between its random variables. We consider a collection of random variables
(X_{1},\dots,X_{q})
and assume that their joint probability density function
f(\boldsymbol{x})
is Markov w.r.t.
\mathcal{D}
, so that it admits the following factorization

f(x_{1},\dots,x_{q})=\prod_{j=1}^{q}f\left(x_{j}|\boldsymbol{x}_{\mathrm{pa}(j)}\right).

(1)

2.1
Gaussian Graphical Models

Graphical models provide a suitable language to decompose many complex real-world problems through conditional independence constraints. Gaussian Graphical Models (GGMs) are extensively used in many research areas, such as genomics, proteomics, neuroimaging, and psychology, to study the partial correlation structure of a set of variables. This structure is visualized by drawing an undirected network, in which the variables constitute the nodes and the partial correlations the edges.

GGMs are tightly linked to precision matrices. Suppose
\boldsymbol{X}=(X_{1},\dots,X_{q})^{\prime}
follows a multivariate Gaussian distribution
\mathcal{N}_{q}(\boldsymbol{\mu},\boldsymbol{\Sigma})
with dimention
q
. Without loss of generality, we can assume the mean of
X_{i}
is zero. The precision matrix
\boldsymbol{\Omega}=\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}
with
\boldsymbol{\Omega}=[\omega_{ij}]_{i,j=1,\dots,q}
describes the graphical structure of its corresponding Gaussian graph. If the
(i,j)
th entry of the precision matrix
\omega_{ij}
is equal to zero, then
X_{i}
and
X_{j}
are independent conditioning on all other variables
X_{k},k\neq i,j
. Correspondingly, no edge exists between
X_{i}
and variable
X_{j}
in the graphical structure of Gaussian graphical model. If
\omega_{ij}\neq 0
, then
X_{i}
and
X_{j}
are conditionally dependent and they are therefore connected in the graphical structure. Partial correlation can be written in terms of the precision matrix. For the precision matrix
\boldsymbol{\Omega}
, the partial correlation between two variables
X_{i}
and
X_{j}
given all other nodes, i.e.,

V\setminus\{i,j\}
is given by

\rho_{ij}:=\rho_{ij\,|\,V\setminus\{i,j\}}=-\frac{\omega_{ij}}{\sqrt{\omega_{ii}\omega_{jj}}}.

(2)

Lafit et al. (2019)
used a partial correlation screening approach for controlling the false positive rate in sparse GMMs.
Under Gaussian assumption with mean zero, i.e.,
X_{1},\dots,X_{q}|\boldsymbol{\Omega}\sim\mathcal{N}_{q}(\boldsymbol{0},\boldsymbol{\Omega}^{-1})
, we can rewrite (
1
) as

f(x_{1},\dots,x_{q}|\boldsymbol{\Omega})=\prod_{j=1}^{q}f_{\mathcal{N}}\left({x}_{j}|\mu_{j}(\boldsymbol{x}_{\mathrm{pa}(j)}),\sigma^{2}_{j}\right),

(3)

where
f_{\mathcal{N}}(.|\mu,\sigma^{2})
denotes the normal density having mean
\mu
and variance
\sigma^{2}
. In this section, we will show how to compute
\mu_{j}(\boldsymbol{x}_{\mathrm{pa}(j)})
and
\sigma^{2}_{j}
.

For a given DAG, we can always reorder the nodes using topological sorting to have a
parent ordering
of the nodes which numerically relabels the nodes so that if
i\to j
(
i
is a child of
j
) then
i<j
, for any
i,j\in V
(
Knuth (1997)
). Clearly node
1
will not have any children.

Equation (
3
) can be written as a
structural equation model

\boldsymbol{L}^{\prime}\boldsymbol{X}=\boldsymbol{\epsilon},

(4)

where
\boldsymbol{X}=(X_{1},\dots,X_{q})^{\prime}
and
\boldsymbol{L}=[L_{ij}]_{i,j\in 1,\dots,q}
is a lower-triangular matrix of coefficients with
L_{ij}\in\mathbb{R}
,
L_{ii}=1
and
L_{ij}\neq 0
if and only if
i\rightarrow j
.
\boldsymbol{L}
is a lower-triangular matrix because of the parent ordering. So
L_{ij}=0
if and only if
i>j
or
i\not\rightarrow j
. Moreover,
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}
is a
q\times 1
vector of error terms,
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}\sim\mathcal{N}_{q}(\boldsymbol{0},\boldsymbol{D})
, where
\boldsymbol{D}=\mathrm{diag}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{2})
and
\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{2}
is the
q\times 1
vector of conditional variances whose
j
-th element is

\sigma^{2}_{j}=\mathrm{Var}(X_{j}|\boldsymbol{x}_{\mathrm{pa}(j)},\boldsymbol{\Omega})
.

Taking variance on both sides of (
4
), we can show that

\boldsymbol{\Omega}=\boldsymbol{L}\boldsymbol{D}^{-1}\boldsymbol{L}^{\prime},

(5)

which is the
modified Cholesky decomposition
of
\boldsymbol{\Omega}
(see
Pourahmadi (2007)
). In fact any positive definite matrix
\boldsymbol{\Omega}
can be uniquely decomposed as
\boldsymbol{\Omega}=\boldsymbol{L}\boldsymbol{D}^{-1}\boldsymbol{L}^{\prime}
, where
\boldsymbol{L}
is a lower triangular matrix with unit diagonal entries, and
\boldsymbol{D}
is a diagonal matrix with positive diagonal entries (see, e.g.,
Golub & Van Loan (2013)
).
Rothman et al. (2010)
presented a new approach for the Cholesky-based covariance regularization in high dimensions.
Guo et al. (2011)
considered an automated approach using the lasso to estimate sparse graphical models by selecting sets of edges common to all groups, as well as group-specific edges.

Since the joint probability density function
f(\boldsymbol{x})
is Markov w.r.t.
\mathcal{D}
, we can use multivariate Gaussian conditional distribution to compute
\mu_{j}(\boldsymbol{x}_{\mathrm{pa}(j)})
and
\sigma^{2}_{j}
in (
3
). For any given
q\times q
matrix
\boldsymbol{A}
, denote by
\boldsymbol{A}_{\preceq j\succeq}
the
|\mathrm{pa}(j)|\times|\mathrm{pa}(j)|
submatrix of
\boldsymbol{A}
with indexes coresponding to
\mathrm{pa}(j)
,
\boldsymbol{A}_{\preceq j\succ}
the
|\mathrm{pa}(j)|\times 1
submatrix of
\boldsymbol{A}
, and
\boldsymbol{A}_{\prec j\succ}:=\boldsymbol{A}_{jj}
where the parent nodes,
\mathrm{pa}(j)
, can be extracted from
\mathcal{D}
. Therefore,

\mu_{j}(\boldsymbol{x}_{\mathrm{pa}(j)})=-\boldsymbol{L}_{\preceq j\succ}\boldsymbol{x}_{\mathrm{pa}(j)},

(6)

where
\boldsymbol{L}_{\preceq j\succ}=-\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\preceq j\succeq}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\preceq j\succ}
and

\sigma^{2}_{j}=\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\prec j\succ}-\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\preceq j\succ}^{\prime}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\preceq j\succeq}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\preceq j\succ}.

(7)

The proof is based on the Gaussian conditional distributions of node
j
given its parent nodes,
\mathrm{pa}(j)
. For the cases when
\mathrm{pa}(j)
is empty set, we set
\mu_{j}(\boldsymbol{x}_{\mathrm{pa}(j)})=\boldsymbol{0}
and
\sigma^{2}_{j}=\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\prec j\succ}
.

2.2
DAG–Probit Model

DAG–Probit model is defined in
Castelletti & Consonni (2021)
. They assumed that
X_{1}
is a latent variable and the binary variable
Y\in\{0,1\}
is observed. For a given threshold
\theta\in\mathbb{R}
, define

Y=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}0&\mathrm{if\penalty\ }X_{1}<\theta,\\
1&\mathrm{if\penalty\ }X_{1}\geq\theta.\\
\end{array}\right.

(8)

Without loss of generality, they assumed that the variance of
X_{1}
is 1, i.e.,
\sigma^{2}_{1}=1
. In reality,
X_{1}
is not observed and we are interested in distribution of
(Y,X_{2},\dots,X_{q})
.
By including the latent variable
X_{1}
in the model and using (
3
) and (
8
), the joint density of
(Y,X_{1},\dots,X_{q})
becomes

f(y,x_{1},\dots,x_{q}|\mathcal{D},\boldsymbol{D},\boldsymbol{L},\theta)=\prod_{j=1}^{q}f_{\mathcal{N}}\left({x}_{j}|\mu_{j}(\boldsymbol{x}_{\mathrm{pa}(j)}),\sigma^{2}_{j}\right).\mathbbm{1}(\theta_{y_{i}-1}<x_{1}\leq\theta_{y_{i}}),

(9)

where the notation
\theta_{-1}:=-\infty,\theta_{0}:=\theta
and
\theta_{1}:=\infty
is used.

For a sample size of
n
with observations
(y_{i},x_{i,1},\dots,x_{i,q})
, the
augmented
likelihood can be written as

\displaystyle f(\boldsymbol{y},\boldsymbol{X}|\mathcal{D},\boldsymbol{D},\boldsymbol{L},\theta)

\displaystyle=\prod_{i=1}^{n}f(y_{i},x_{i,1},\dots,x_{i,q}|\mathcal{D},\boldsymbol{D},\boldsymbol{L}).\mathbbm{1}(\theta_{y_{i}-1}<x_{i,1}\leq\theta_{y_{i}})

\displaystyle=\prod_{j=1}^{q}f_{\mathcal{N}_{n}}\left(\boldsymbol{X}_{j}|-\boldsymbol{X}_{\mathrm{pa}(j)}\boldsymbol{L}_{\preceq j\succ},\sigma^{2}_{j}\boldsymbol{I}_{n}\right).\prod_{i=1}^{n}\mathbbm{1}(\theta_{y_{i}-1}<x_{i,1}\leq\theta_{y_{i}})

(10)

where
\boldsymbol{y}=(y_{1},\dots,y_{n})^{\prime}
,
\boldsymbol{X}:=(\boldsymbol{X}_{1},\dots,\boldsymbol{X}_{q})
is the
n\times q
augmented data matrix,
\boldsymbol{X}_{s}
is the submatrix of
\boldsymbol{X}
with columns
s
and
\boldsymbol{X}_{-1}:=(\boldsymbol{X}_{2},\dots,\boldsymbol{X}_{q})
.

2.3
Doubly Gaussian DAG-Probit Model

We assume there are two groups of data,
\boldsymbol{X}^{(1)}
and
\boldsymbol{X}^{(2)}
with the coresponding DAG’s
\mathcal{D}^{(1)}=(V,E^{(1)})
and
\mathcal{D}^{(2)}=(V,E^{(2)})
. The set of vertices are the same for both groups but potentially they can have different sets of edges.
For each group
k\in\{1,2\}
,
\boldsymbol{X}^{(k)}
follows a Gaussian distribution with sample size
n_{k}
. Furthermore, we also have observed the binary random vector
\boldsymbol{y}^{(k)}
of size
n_{k}
, related to the latent variables
X_{1}^{(k)}
defined in (
8
). We assume that
\sigma_{j}^{2}
is equal for both groups, i.e.,
\mathrm{Var}(X_{j}|\boldsymbol{x}^{(1)}_{\mathrm{pa}(j)})=\mathrm{Var}(X_{j}|\boldsymbol{x}^{(2)}_{\mathrm{pa}(j)})
, therefore
\boldsymbol{D}:=\boldsymbol{D}^{(1)}=\boldsymbol{D}^{(2)}
. The skeleton of
\mathcal{D}^{(k)}
can have effect on the
\boldsymbol{L}^{(k)}
as well, because
L_{ij}=0
if and only if
i\not\rightarrow j
, so they can be different as well as the precision matrices
\boldsymbol{\Omega}^{(1)}
and
\boldsymbol{\Omega}^{(2)}
.

The augmented likelihood for the doubly Gaussian DAG-probit model can be written as

\displaystyle f\left(\boldsymbol{y}^{(1)},\boldsymbol{y}^{(2)},\boldsymbol{X}^{(1)},\boldsymbol{X}^{(2)}\big|\mathcal{D}^{(1)},\mathcal{D}^{(2)},\boldsymbol{D},\boldsymbol{L}^{(1)},\boldsymbol{L}^{(2)},\theta\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\prod_{k=1}^{2}f\left(\boldsymbol{y}^{(k)},\boldsymbol{X}^{(k)}\big|\mathcal{D}^{(k)},\boldsymbol{D},\boldsymbol{L}^{(k)},\theta\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\prod_{j=1}^{q}f_{\mathcal{N}_{n_{1}}}\left(\boldsymbol{X}^{(1)}_{j}\Big|\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}^{(1)},\sigma^{2}_{j}\boldsymbol{I}_{n_{1}}\right)f_{\mathcal{N}_{n_{2}}}\left(\boldsymbol{X}^{(2)}_{j}\Big|\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}^{(2)},\sigma^{2}_{j}\boldsymbol{I}_{n_{2}}\right)\times\prod_{i=1}^{n_{1}}\mathbbm{1}(\theta_{y_{i}-1}<x_{i,1}^{(1)}\leq\theta_{y_{i}})\prod_{i=1}^{n_{2}}\mathbbm{1}(\theta_{y_{i}-1}<x_{i,1}^{(2)}\leq\theta_{y_{i}}),

(11)

where
\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}^{(k)}:=-\boldsymbol{X}^{(k)}_{\mathrm{pa}(j)}\boldsymbol{L}^{(k)}_{\preceq j\succ}
and we assumed the cut-off parameter
\theta
is the same for both groups.

3
Bayesian Inference

This section concerns priors for (
\mathcal{D}^{(1)},\mathcal{D}^{(2)},\boldsymbol{D},\boldsymbol{L}^{(1)},\boldsymbol{L}^{(2)},\theta
) and we review different models in the litrature. We try to pick the conjugate priors to make the equations simpler and the computations faster. In section
4
, we will compute the posteriors for each of the parameters.

3.1
Prior on DAG
\mathcal{D}^{(k)}

Random graph famously studied by
Erdős and Rényi
Erdős et al. (1960)
. For generating random graphs on DAGs, we refer to
Karrer & Newman (2009)
.
Let
\boldsymbol{A}^{\mathcal{D}}
be the 0–1 adjacency matrix of the skeleton of the parent ordering DAG
\mathcal{D}
whose
(i,j)
th element is denoted by
\boldsymbol{A}_{ij}^{\mathcal{D}}
. Clearly,
\boldsymbol{A}_{ij}^{\mathcal{D}}=0
for
i\leq j
because of the parent ordering and no self–loops.

Assuming the probability of edge inclusion is
\xi
, we assign a Bernoulli prior independently to each element
\boldsymbol{A}_{ij}^{\mathcal{D}}
, that is

f(\boldsymbol{A}^{\mathcal{D}})=\xi^{|\boldsymbol{A}^{\mathcal{D}}|}(1-\xi)^{\frac{q(q-1)}{2}-|\boldsymbol{A}^{\mathcal{D}}|},

(12)

where
|\boldsymbol{A}^{\mathcal{D}}|
denotes the number of edges in the skeleton.
Cao et al. (2019)
also used
independent identically distributed Bernoulli random variables as the prior on the probability of edges which corresponds to an Erdős–Rényi type of distribution.

For the prior on DAG’s
\mathcal{D}^{(k)}
, we independentely set

f(\mathcal{D}^{(k)})\propto f(\boldsymbol{A}^{\mathcal{D}^{(k)}}),

(13)

where
k\in\{1,2\}
.

3.2
Prior on the Modified Cholesky Decomposition

Since
\boldsymbol{L}_{ij}=0
eighter if there is no edge from
i
to
j
or
i>j
, so a Gaussian DAG model restricts
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}
(and
\boldsymbol{\Omega}
) to a lower-dimensional space by imposing sparsity constraints encoded in
\mathcal{D}
on
\boldsymbol{L}
. This constraint is important for both frequentist and Bayesian methods.

On the frequentist side, a variety of penalized likelihood methods for sparse estimation of
\boldsymbol{L}
exist in the literature; see the refrences in
Cao et al. (2019)
. Some of those methods, such as those in
Shojaie & Michailidis (2010)
;
Yu & Bien (2017)
, constrain the sparsity pattern in
\boldsymbol{L}
to be banded. We assume no constraints on the sparsity pattern in this paper.
Gaskins & Daniels (2013)
used a nonparametric prior for covariance estimation.

On the Bayesian side, the first class of priors on the restricted space of covariance matrices corresponding to a Gaussian DAG model was initially developed in
Geiger & Heckerman (2002)
;
Smith & Kohn (2002)
.

Li & Zhang (2019)
reparameterized the likelihood of a matrix of Gaussian graphical model and obtained the full conditional distribution of the parameters in Cholesky factor. Using the asymptotic distribution of all parameters in the Cholesky factor, they opbtained a shrinkage Bayesian estimator for large precision matrix.
Cao et al. (2019)
considered a hierarchical Gaussian DAG model with DAG-Wishart priors on the covariance matrix and independent Bernoulli priors for each edge in the DAG. A standard choice of conjugate prior is the Wishart distribution, i.e.,
\boldsymbol{\Omega}\sim W_{q}(a,\boldsymbol{U})
having expectation
a\,\boldsymbol{U}^{-1}
, where
a>q-1
. The priors in
Geiger & Heckerman (2002)
can be considered as analogs of the G-Wishart distribution for concentration graph models.
Ben-David et al. (2015)
introduced a class of DAG-Wishart distributions with multiple shape parameters. Their class of distributions is defined for arbitrary DAG models and offers a flexible framework for Bayesian inference in Gaussian DAG models, and generalizes previous Wishart–based priors for DAG models.

For the cases when
\mathcal{D}
is complete,
(
Ben-David et al. (2015)
, Supplemental Section B)
derived the Hyper Morkov propertirties of the DAG–Wishart as

\sigma_{j}^{2}\sim\mathrm{I\text{-}Ga}\left(\frac{a_{j}}{2}-\frac{|\mathrm{pa}(j)|}{2}-1,\frac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{U}_{\prec j\succ}\right),

(14)

and

\boldsymbol{L}_{\preceq j\succ}|\sigma_{j}^{2}\sim\mathcal{N}_{|\mathrm{pa}(j)|}\Big(\boldsymbol{0},\sigma_{j}^{2}\,\boldsymbol{U}^{-1}_{\preceq j\succeq}\Big),

(15)

for the Cholesky parameters, where
a_{j}=a+q-2j+3
and
\mathrm{I\text{-}Ga}(\alpha,\beta)
is an Inverse–Gamma distribution with shape
\alpha>0
and rate
\beta>0
having expectation
\beta/(\alpha-1)
. When
\mathcal{D}
is not complete, by setting
\boldsymbol{U}=g\boldsymbol{I}_{q}
in equations (
14
) and (
15
),
(
Castelletti & Consonni (2021)
, Supplementary)
showed that

\sigma_{j}^{2}\sim\mathrm{I\text{-}Ga}\left(\frac{a_{j}}{2},\frac{g}{2}\right),

(16)

\boldsymbol{L}_{\preceq j\succ}|\sigma_{j}^{2}\sim\mathcal{N}_{|\mathrm{pa}(j)|}\left(\boldsymbol{0},\frac{1}{g}\sigma_{j}^{2}\boldsymbol{I}_{|\mathrm{pa}(j)|}\right),

(17)

where
a_{j}=a+|\mathrm{pa}(j)|-q+1
and
\boldsymbol{I}_{q}
is the identity matrix and
g>0
is a hyperparameter.

For the case where there are 2 sparse DAGs, we need to properly define the priors for
\boldsymbol{D}
,
\boldsymbol{L}^{(1)}
and
\boldsymbol{L}^{(2)}
. Lets define the prior for
\sigma_{j}^{2}
as

4
MCMC for Doubly Gaussian DAG–Probit Models

We assume there are two groups of data,
\boldsymbol{X}^{(1)}
and
\boldsymbol{X}^{(2)}
with the coresponding DAGs
\mathcal{D}^{(1)}
and
\mathcal{D}^{(2)}
. We first present the full likelihood distribution of the model gevin the input data
\left\{\boldsymbol{y}^{(1)},\boldsymbol{y}^{(2)},\boldsymbol{X}_{-1}^{(1)},\boldsymbol{X}_{-1}^{(2)}\right\}
and then find the posterior distributions for the model parameters. The goal is to estimate some of the model parameters using the joint distribution. The schematic view of the algorithm is presented in Figure
1
. We will provide more info on the MCMC algorithm in Section
4.8
.

4.1
Full Posterior Distribution

The full posterior distribution is used to find the posteriors or acceptance rates for
\mathcal{D}^{(k)}
,
\boldsymbol{L}^{(k)}
,
\boldsymbol{D}
,
\boldsymbol{X}_{1}^{(k)}
and
\theta
in the sebsequent sections.

Given
\left\{\boldsymbol{y}^{(1)},\boldsymbol{y}^{(2)},\boldsymbol{X}_{-1}^{(1)},\boldsymbol{X}_{-1}^{(2)}\right\}
as the input, the full posterior distribution is

\displaystyle f\left(\mathcal{D}^{(1)},\mathcal{D}^{(2)},\boldsymbol{L}^{(1)},\boldsymbol{L}^{(2)},\boldsymbol{D},\boldsymbol{X}_{1}^{(1)},\boldsymbol{X}_{1}^{(2)},\theta\big|\boldsymbol{y}^{(1)},\boldsymbol{y}^{(2)},\boldsymbol{X}_{-1}^{(1)},\boldsymbol{X}_{-1}^{(2)}\right)

\displaystyle\propto\prod_{k=1}^{2}f\left(\boldsymbol{y}^{(k)},\boldsymbol{X}^{(k)}\Big|\mathcal{D}^{(k)},\boldsymbol{L}^{(k)},\boldsymbol{D},\theta\right)f\left(\boldsymbol{L}^{(k)}\big|\boldsymbol{D},\mathcal{D}^{(k)}\right)f\left(\mathcal{D}^{(k)}\right)\times f\left(\boldsymbol{D}\big|\mathcal{D}^{(1)},\mathcal{D}^{(2)}\right),

(20)

where the first term is defined in (
11
),
f\left(\boldsymbol{D}\big|\mathcal{D}^{(1)},\mathcal{D}^{(2)}\right)
and
f(\boldsymbol{L}^{(k)}\big|\boldsymbol{D},\mathcal{D}^{(k)})
are defined in equations (
18
) and (
19
), and
f(\mathcal{D}^{(k)})
is defined in (
13
). The proof of the full posterior distribution is presented in Appendix
A
.

4.2
Update of
\mathcal{D}^{(k)}

The first step at each MCMC iteration is to propose two new DAG’s
\mathcal{D^{\prime}}^{(1)}
and
\mathcal{D^{\prime}}^{(2)}
from suitable proposal distributions
q(\mathcal{D}^{\prime(1)}|\mathcal{D}^{(1)})
and
q(\mathcal{D}^{\prime(2)}|\mathcal{D}^{(2)})
. Given the current DAG
\mathcal{D}^{(k)}
, we construct
\mathcal{D}^{\prime(k)}
by randomly selecting one of the following operators:
\texttt{Insert}(i\to j)
,
\texttt{Delete}(i\to j)
or
\texttt{Reverse}(i\to j)
. The operator
Insert
adds the edge
i\to j
with probability
\xi
if this edge does not exist in
\mathcal{D}^{(k)}
,
Delete
removes the exsisting edge
i\to j
with probability
1-\xi
, and the operator
Reverse
changes the direction of an existing edge, i.e., converting
i\to j
to
i\leftarrow j
.
\texttt{Reverse}(i\to j)
operator is equivalent to removing
i\to j
followed by adding the reverse edge, i.e.,
\texttt{Reverse}(i\to j):=\texttt{Delete}(i\to j)+\texttt{Insert}(j\to i)
.

All of these operators must not violate the DAG asumptions, especially they should not create any loop, so we check if the proposed DAG is valid or not at each MCMC iteration. If the proposed DAG has loop, we simply reject it and continue proposing new DAGs until we end up with a valid one. This process ensures that the proposed DAG is valid.

The next step is to accept or reject the proposed valid DAG
\mathcal{D}^{\prime(k)}
. We use the algorithm proposed by
Wang & Li (2012)
for Bayesian model determination in Gaussian graphical models under G-Wishart prior distributions. This algorithm is based on PAS (Partial Analytic Structure) algorithm proposed by
Godsill (2001)
, which is based on the reversible jump proposal schemes and takes into account the partial analytic structure of the DAG model.

It is important to mention that
\texttt{Insert}(i\to j)
and
\texttt{Delete}(i\to j)
operators just make changes in the
\mathrm{pa}(j)
, so the Cholesky parameters under the new and old DAGs differ only with respect to their
j
-th component, but for the
\texttt{Reverse}(i\to j)
operator, both
\mathrm{pa}(i)
and
\mathrm{pa}(j)
will change. As a result, the acceptance probabilities for these two cases are different and we present them separetely.

4.2.1

and

Under
\texttt{Insert}(i\to j)
and

\mathcal{D}^{\prime(k)}
for

is given by

(21)

where for
\mathcal{D}\in\{\mathcal{D}^{(1)},\mathcal{D}^{(2)},\mathcal{D}^{{}^{\prime}(1)},\mathcal{D}^{{}^{\prime}(2)}\}

\displaystyle m(\boldsymbol{X}_{j}^{(k)}|\boldsymbol{X}_{\mathrm{pa}_{\mathcal{D}}(j)}^{(k)},\mathcal{D})

\displaystyle=(2\pi)^{-\frac{n_{k}}{2}}\frac{|\boldsymbol{T}_{j}^{(k)}|^{1/2}}{|\bar{\boldsymbol{T}}_{j}^{(k)}|^{1/2}}\,.\,\frac{\Gamma(a_{j}^{\mathcal{D}}/2+n_{k}/2)}{\Gamma(a_{j}^{\mathcal{D}}/2)}\left[\frac{1}{2}g_{k}\right]^{a_{j}^{\mathcal{D}}/2}\left[\frac{1}{2}\left(g_{k}+\boldsymbol{X}^{\prime(k)}_{j}\boldsymbol{X}^{(k)}_{j}-\hat{\boldsymbol{L}}^{\prime(k)}_{j}\bar{\boldsymbol{T}}^{(k)}_{j}\hat{\boldsymbol{L}}^{(k)}_{j}\right)\right]^{-(a_{j}^{\mathcal{D}}+n_{k})/2},

with

For

\displaystyle m(\boldsymbol{X}_{1}^{(k)}|\boldsymbol{X}_{\mathrm{pa}_{\mathcal{D}}(1)}^{(k)},\mathcal{D})=(2\pi)^{-\frac{n_{k}}{2}}\frac{|\boldsymbol{T}_{1}^{(k)}|^{1/2}}{|\bar{\boldsymbol{T}}_{1}^{(k)}|^{1/2}}\,.\,\exp\left\{-\frac{1}{2}\left(\boldsymbol{X}^{\prime(k)}_{1}\boldsymbol{X}^{(k)}_{1}-\hat{\boldsymbol{L}}^{\prime(k)}_{1}\bar{\boldsymbol{T}}^{(k)}_{1}\hat{\boldsymbol{L}}^{(k)}_{1}\right)\right\}.

(24)

The number of different combinations to initialize

is equal to

We showed in Equation (

Proposition 1

5
Causal Effects

Given two disjoint sets of variables,
X
and
Y
, the
causal effect
of
X
on
Y
, denoted by
f(y|\,\mathrm{do}(x))
, is a function from
X
to the space of probability distributions on
Y
. The goal of
\mathrm{do}(x)
calculus is to generate probabilistic formulas for the effect of interventions in terms of the observed probabilities. For each realization
x
of
X
,
f(y|\,\mathrm{do}(x))
gives the probability of
Y=y
induced by deleting all equations corresponding to variables in
X
and substituting
X=x
in the remaining equations (
Pearl (2009)
).
In fact,
\mathrm{do}(.)
operator marks an action or an intervention in the model. In an algebraic model, we replace certain functions with a constant
X=x
, and in a graph we remove edges going into the target of intervention, but preserve edges going out of the target.
The graph corresponding to the reduced set of equations is a subgraph of
\mathcal{D}
from which all arrows entering
x
have been pruned.

The effect of interventions of
\mathrm{do}(X_{i}=x_{i})
for
s\in\{2,\dots,q\}
can be expressed in a simple
truncated factorization
formula as following

f(x_{1},\dots,x_{q}\,|\,\mathrm{do}(X_{s}=\tilde{x})=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\prod_{j=1,j\neq s}^{q}f(x_{j}\,|\,\mathrm{pa}(x_{j}))\,\big|_{x_{s}=\tilde{x}}&\mathrm{if\penalty\ }x_{s}=\tilde{x},\\
0&\mathrm{otherwise}.\end{array}\right.

(39)

This quation reflects the removal of the term
f(x_{i}|\mathrm{pa}(x_{i}))
from the product of (
1
), since
\mathrm{pa}(x_{i})
no longer influences
X_{i}
and can be seen as a transformation between the pre- (
1
) and post-intervention (
39
) distributions.

For the latent variable
X_{1}
, the post-intervention distribution can be calculated as

f(x_{1}\,|\,\mathrm{do}(X_{s}=\tilde{x}))=\int f(x_{1}\,|\,\tilde{x},\boldsymbol{x}_{\mathrm{pa}(s)})f(\boldsymbol{x}_{\mathrm{pa}(s)})d\boldsymbol{x}_{\mathrm{pa}(s)}.

(40)

For more details on the post-intervention distribution, take a look at the Theorem 3.2.2 in
Pearl (2009)
.

Using the conditional Gaussian distribution,
Castelletti & Consonni (2021)
showed that

f(x_{1}\,|\,\mathrm{do}(X_{s}=\tilde{x}),\boldsymbol{\Sigma})=f_{\mathcal{N}}\big(x_{1}\,|\,\gamma_{s}\tilde{x},\sigma^{2}_{\mathrm{do}}\big),

(41)

where

\displaystyle\sigma^{2}_{\mathrm{do}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\delta_{1}^{2}}{1-(\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{\prime}\boldsymbol{T}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\gamma})/\delta_{1}^{2}},

\displaystyle\delta_{1}^{2}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{1|\mathrm{fa}(s)},

\displaystyle(\gamma_{s},\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{\prime})^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{1,\mathrm{fa}(s)}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\mathrm{fa}(s),\mathrm{fa}(s)})^{-1},

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{T}

\displaystyle=(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\mathrm{fa}(s),\mathrm{fa}(s)})^{-1}+\frac{1}{\delta_{1}^{2}}\boldsymbol{\gamma}\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{\prime}.

Inspired by the Bartlett’s decomposition,
Silva & Ghahramani (2009)

8
Conclusion

We considered modeling a binary response variable together with a set of independent variables for two groups under observational data. This work can be extended for groups with more than two elements simply be using
k>2
in all equations. We could speed up the MCMC convergence by picking better initial graphs for the DAGs using modified Cholesky decomposition.

In this work, we assumed equal edge inclusion of the edges while we can improve it by adding some weights to the edge inclusion. For example, we can add more weights to the edges coming into node 1, or we can decrease the probability of node inclusion inversely proportionate to the degree of its parent node. This method is useful if someone wants to keep the proposed graph as a sparse DAG at each MCMC iteration.

There are many real examples where there are more than one binary variables in data. This method can model just one binary response variable. Categorical or nominal response variables also have many applications which are not under the scope of this paper and need more research. Normality assumption of the independent variables is also a limitation of this method which may produce biased outputs.

Regardless of those limitations which are not under the proposed model assumptions, we were able to show that this method could estimate the DAG structure and the model parameters accurately. We also demonstrated its value in real well-known datasets, especially using two DAGs.

\displaystyle=\prod_{k=1}^{2}f\left(\boldsymbol{y}^{(k)},\boldsymbol{X}^{(k)}\Big|\mathcal{D}^{(k)},\boldsymbol{L}^{(k)},\boldsymbol{D},\theta\right)f\left(\boldsymbol{L}^{(k)}\big|\boldsymbol{D},\mathcal{D}^{(k)}\right)f\left(\mathcal{D}^{(k)}\right)\times f\left(\boldsymbol{D}\big|\mathcal{D}^{(1)},\mathcal{D}^{(2)}\right)f\left(\theta\right),

(46)

We summerize the PAS algorithm here at first. Consider

, each one with set of parameters
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\nu}

, where

\mathcal{M}_{\nu^{\prime}}

there exists a subvector
(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\nu^{\prime}})_{\mathcal{U}}
of the parameter
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\nu^{\prime}}
for a new model
\mathcal{M}_{\nu^{\prime}}
such that
f\left((\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\nu^{\prime}})_{\mathcal{U}}\,\big|\,(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\nu^{\prime}})_{-\mathcal{U}},\mathcal{M}_{\nu^{\prime}},\boldsymbol{X}\right)
is available in closed form,

b)

in the current model
\mathcal{M}_{\nu}

with the same dimension as
(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\nu^{\prime}})_{-\mathcal{U}}

The PAS algorithm is

1.

Propose
\mathcal{M}_{\nu^{\prime}}\sim q(\mathcal{M}_{\nu^{\prime}}|\mathcal{M}_{\nu})

(48)

\texttt{Insert}(i\to j)
,
\texttt{Delete}(i\to j)
and
\texttt{Reverse}(i\to j)
operators, separetely in the following subsections.

B.1
One Parent Node is Changed

In algorithm
1
, new
\mathcal{D}^{{}^{\prime}(1)}
and
\mathcal{D}^{{}^{\prime}(2)}
are proposed seperately, so we need to compute the integral in equation (
48
) for
k\in\{1,2\}
independentey. For the operastors
\texttt{Insert}(i\to j)
and
\texttt{Delete}(i\to j)
, the difference between the proposed DAG
\mathcal{D}^{{}^{\prime}(k)}
and
\mathcal{D}^{(k)}
is just one parent node, denoted by node
j
, so
\mathcal{U}=(\sigma^{2}_{j}
,
\boldsymbol{L}_{\preceq j\succ})
and the set of parameters in PAS algorithm is
\boldsymbol{\theta}=(\boldsymbol{D}^{(k)},\boldsymbol{L}^{(k)})
. For
j>1
and using Bayes theorem, equation (
48
) becomes

\displaystyle f\left(\mathcal{D}^{(k)}\,|\,\boldsymbol{X}^{(k)},\boldsymbol{\theta}_{-\mathcal{U}}\right)\propto f\left(\boldsymbol{X}^{(k)},\boldsymbol{\theta}_{-\mathcal{U}}\,|\,\mathcal{D}^{(k)}\right)f\left(\mathcal{D}^{(k)}\right)

\displaystyle=f\left(\mathcal{D}^{(k)}\right)\int_{\mathbb{R}^{|\mathrm{pa}(j)|}}\int_{0}^{\infty}f\left(\boldsymbol{X}^{(k)},\boldsymbol{D}^{(k)},\boldsymbol{L}^{(k)}|\mathcal{D}^{(k)}\right)\,d\boldsymbol{L}^{(k)}_{\preceq j\succ}\,d\sigma^{2}_{j}

\displaystyle=f\left(\mathcal{D}^{(k)}\right)\prod_{r\neq j}f\left(\boldsymbol{X}^{(k)}_{r},\sigma^{2}_{r},\boldsymbol{L}^{(k)}_{\preceq r\succ}\Big|\mathcal{D}^{(k)}\right)\times\int\int f\left(\boldsymbol{X}^{(k)}_{j}\Big|\sigma^{2}_{j},\boldsymbol{L}^{(k)}_{\preceq j\succ}\right)f\left(\boldsymbol{L}^{(k)}_{\preceq j\succ}|\sigma^{2}_{j}\right)f\left(\sigma^{2}_{j}|\mathcal{D}^{(k)}\right)\,d\boldsymbol{L}^{(k)}_{\preceq j\succ}\,d\sigma^{2}_{j},

(49)

where
f\left(\sigma^{2}_{j}|\mathcal{D}^{(k)}\right)
is the prior for
\sigma^{2}_{j}
assuming there is just one DAG
\mathcal{D}^{(k)}
, i.e.,
\sigma^{2}_{j}|\mathcal{D}^{(k)}\sim\mathrm{I\text{-}Ga}\left(\frac{a_{j}^{(k)}}{2},\frac{g_{k}}{2}\right)
.

To simplify the equations, we define

\displaystyle m(\boldsymbol{X}^{(k)}_{j}|\boldsymbol{X}^{(k)}_{\mathrm{pa}(j)},\mathcal{D}^{(k)}):=\int\int f\left(\boldsymbol{X}^{(k)}_{j}\Big|\sigma^{2}_{j},\boldsymbol{L}^{(k)}_{\preceq j\succ}\right)f\left(\boldsymbol{L}^{(k)}_{\preceq j\succ}|\sigma^{2}_{j}\right)f\left(\sigma^{2}_{j}|\mathcal{D}^{(k)}\right)\,d\boldsymbol{L}^{(k)}_{\preceq j\succ}\,d\sigma^{2}_{j}.

(50)

The integrand in (
50
) can be written as

\displaystyle f\left(\boldsymbol{X}^{(k)}_{j}\Big|{\sigma^{2}_{j}},\boldsymbol{L}^{(k)}_{\preceq j\succ}\right)f\left(\boldsymbol{L}^{(k)}_{\preceq j\succ}\Big|{\sigma^{2}_{j}}\right)f\left({\sigma^{2}_{j}}|\mathcal{D}^{(k)}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{\frac{n_{k}}{2}}}{|\sigma^{2}_{j}\boldsymbol{I}_{n_{k}}|}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\exp\left\{-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}_{j}}\left(\boldsymbol{X}^{(k)}_{j}+\boldsymbol{X}^{(k)}_{\mathrm{pa}(j)}\boldsymbol{L}^{(k)}_{\preceq j\succ}\right)^{\prime}\left(\boldsymbol{X}^{(k)}_{j}+\boldsymbol{X}^{(k)}_{\mathrm{pa}(j)}\boldsymbol{L}^{(k)}_{\preceq j\succ}\right)\right\}

\displaystyle\times\frac{1}{{(2\pi)^{\frac{{|\mathrm{pa}^{(k)}(j)|}}{2}}}}\Big|\frac{1}{g_{k}}\sigma^{2}_{j}\boldsymbol{I}_{|\mathrm{pa}^{(k)}(j)|}\Big|^{-\frac{1}{2}}\exp\left\{-\frac{g_{k}}{2\sigma^{2}_{j}}(\boldsymbol{L}^{(k)}_{\preceq j\succ})^{\prime}\boldsymbol{L}^{(k)}_{\preceq j\succ}\right\}

\displaystyle\times C\,.\,(\sigma^{2}_{j})^{-\frac{a^{(k)}_{j}}{2}-1}\exp\left\{-\frac{\frac{g_{k}}{2}}{\sigma^{2}_{j}}\right\},

(51)

where
C={(\frac{g_{k}}{2})}^{\frac{a^{(k)}_{j}}{2}}/\Gamma(\frac{a^{(k)}_{j}}{2})
.

By rearranging the terms containing
\boldsymbol{L}_{\preceq j\succ}^{(k)}
in (
51
), it is easy to show that

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{L}_{\preceq j\succ}^{(k)}|\sigma^{2}_{j},\boldsymbol{X}^{(k)}\sim\mathcal{N}_{|\mathrm{pa}^{(k)}(j)|}\left(-\hat{\boldsymbol{L}}^{\prime(k)}_{j},\sigma^{2}_{j}(\bar{\boldsymbol{T}}^{(k)}_{j})^{-1}\right),

(52)

so, the inner integral becomes

\displaystyle\int f\left(\boldsymbol{X}^{(k)}_{j}\Big|\sigma^{2}_{j},\boldsymbol{L}^{(k)}_{\preceq j\succ}\right)f\left(\boldsymbol{L}^{(k)}_{\preceq j\succ}|\sigma^{2}_{j}\right)\,d\boldsymbol{L}^{(k)}_{\preceq j\succ}=(2\pi)^{\frac{|\mathrm{pa}^{(k)}(j)|}{2}}\,\,{\left|\frac{\bar{\boldsymbol{T}}_{j}^{(k)}}{\sigma^{2}_{j}}\right|}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\exp\left\{\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}_{j}}\hat{\boldsymbol{L}}^{{}^{\prime}(k)}_{j}\bar{\boldsymbol{T}}^{(k)}_{j}\hat{\boldsymbol{L}}^{(k)}_{j}\right\},

(53)

which is the constant terms in a random variable that follows
\mathcal{N}_{|\mathrm{pa}^{(k)}(j)|}\left(-\hat{\boldsymbol{L}}^{\prime(k)}_{j},\sigma^{2}_{j}(\bar{\boldsymbol{T}}^{(k)}_{j})^{-1}\right)
. Furthermore, using (
53
) and other terms containg
\sigma^{2}_{j}
in (
51
) together with the prior distribition for
\sigma^{2}_{j}|\mathcal{D}^{(k)}
, we can show that

\displaystyle f\left(\sigma^{2}_{j}|\boldsymbol{X}^{(k)}_{j},\mathcal{D}^{(k)}\right)

\displaystyle\propto({\sigma^{2}_{j}})^{-\frac{n_{k}}{2}-\frac{a^{(k)}_{j}}{2}-1-\frac{|\mathrm{pa}^{(k)}(j)|}{2}+\frac{|\mathrm{pa}^{(k)}(j)|}{2}}

\displaystyle\times\exp\left\{\frac{-\frac{1}{2}(g_{k}+{\boldsymbol{X}^{{}^{\prime}(k)}_{j}}\boldsymbol{X}^{(k)}_{j}-\hat{\boldsymbol{L}}^{{}^{\prime}(k)}_{j}\bar{\boldsymbol{T}}^{(k)}_{j}\hat{\boldsymbol{L}}^{(k)}_{j})}{\sigma^{2}_{j}}\right\},

(54)

where we substitute
{\left|\frac{\bar{\boldsymbol{T}}_{j}^{(k)}}{\sigma^{2}_{j}}\right|}^{-\frac{1}{2}}={\sigma^{2}_{j}}^{\frac{|\mathrm{pa}^{(k)}(j)|}{2}}{\left|\bar{\boldsymbol{T}}_{j}^{(k)}\right|}^{-\frac{1}{2}}
. It is easy to see that the posterior is

\displaystyle\sigma^{2}_{j}|\boldsymbol{X}^{(k)}_{j},\mathcal{D}^{(k)}\sim\mathrm{I-Ga}\left(\frac{a^{(k)}_{j}}{2}+\frac{n_{k}}{2},\frac{1}{2}K^{(k)}\right),

(55)

where
K^{(k)}=g_{k}+{\boldsymbol{X}^{{}^{\prime}(k)}_{j}}\boldsymbol{X}^{(k)}_{j}-\hat{\boldsymbol{L}}^{{}^{\prime}(k)}_{j}\bar{\boldsymbol{T}}^{(k)}_{j}\hat{\boldsymbol{L}}^{(k)}_{j}
.

Finally by bringing back the constant terms from (
51
), the outer integral in (
50
) becomes

\displaystyle m(\boldsymbol{X}^{(k)}_{j}|\boldsymbol{X}^{(k)}_{\mathrm{pa}(j)},\mathcal{D}^{(k)})

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}f\left(\sigma^{2}_{j}|\boldsymbol{X}^{(k)}_{j},\mathcal{D}^{(k)}\right)\,d\sigma^{2}_{j}

\displaystyle=(2\pi)^{-\frac{n_{k}}{2}}\,\frac{{|\boldsymbol{T}}_{j}^{(k)}|^{1/2}}{|\bar{\boldsymbol{T}}_{j}^{(k)}|^{1/2}}\,\frac{\Gamma(\frac{a^{(k)}_{j}}{2}+\frac{n_{k}}{2})}{\Gamma(\frac{a^{(k)}_{j}}{2})}\,{(\frac{g_{k}}{2})}^{a^{(k)}_{j}}\,\left[\frac{1}{2}(g_{k}+{\boldsymbol{X}^{{}^{\prime}(k)}_{j}}\boldsymbol{X}^{(k)}_{j}-\hat{\boldsymbol{L}}^{{}^{\prime}(k)}_{j}\bar{\boldsymbol{T}}^{(k)}_{j}\hat{\boldsymbol{L}}^{(k)}_{j})\right]^{-(a^{(k)}_{j}+n_{k})/2}.

(56)

For
j=1
, since
\sigma^{2}_{j}=1
then ther would be just one integral, so

\displaystyle m(\boldsymbol{X}^{(k)}_{1}|\boldsymbol{X}^{(k)}_{\mathrm{pa}(1)},\mathcal{D}^{(k)}):=\int f\left(\boldsymbol{X}^{(k)}_{1}\Big|\boldsymbol{L}^{(k)}_{\preceq 1\succ}\right)f\left(\boldsymbol{L}^{(k)}_{\preceq 1\succ}\right)\,d\boldsymbol{L}^{(k)}_{\preceq 1\succ},

(57)

which we computed in (
53
). Bringing back the constant terms from (
51
) we have

\displaystyle m(\boldsymbol{X}^{(k)}_{1}\,|\,\boldsymbol{X}^{(k)}_{\mathrm{pa}(1)},\mathcal{D}^{(k)})=(2\pi)^{-\frac{n_{k}}{2}}\frac{|\boldsymbol{T}_{1}^{(k)}|^{1/2}}{|\bar{\boldsymbol{T}}_{1}^{(k)}|^{1/2}}\,.\,\exp\left\{-\frac{1}{2}\left(\boldsymbol{X}^{\prime(k)}_{1}\boldsymbol{X}^{(k)}_{1}-\hat{\boldsymbol{L}}^{\prime(k)}_{1}\bar{\boldsymbol{T}}^{(k)}_{1}\hat{\boldsymbol{L}}^{(k)}_{1}\right)\right\}.

(58)

Substituting
f\left(\mathcal{D}^{(k)}\,|\,\boldsymbol{X}^{(k)},\boldsymbol{\theta}_{-\mathcal{U}}\right)
into equation (
47
) completes the proof.

B.2
Two Parent Nodes are Changed

As we explaned beforre, for the
\texttt{Reverse}(i\to j)
operator, both
\mathrm{pa}(i)
and
\mathrm{pa}(j)
will change. So, we need to account for both nodes
i
and
j
. In this case,
\mathcal{U}=(\sigma^{2}_{i},\sigma^{2}_{j},\boldsymbol{L}_{\preceq i\succ},\boldsymbol{L}_{\preceq j\succ})
and the set of parameters in PAS algorithm is
\boldsymbol{\theta}=(\boldsymbol{D}^{(k)},\boldsymbol{L}^{(k)})
. For
j>1
, equation (
48
) becomes

\displaystyle f\left(\mathcal{D}^{(k)}\,|\,\boldsymbol{X}^{(k)},\boldsymbol{\theta}_{-\mathcal{U}}\right)\propto f\left(\boldsymbol{X}^{(k)},\boldsymbol{\theta}_{-\mathcal{U}}\,|\,\mathcal{D}^{(k)}\right)f\left(\mathcal{D}^{(k)}\right)

\displaystyle=f\left(\mathcal{D}^{(k)}\right)\int\int\int\int f\left(\boldsymbol{X}^{(k)},\boldsymbol{D}^{(k)},\boldsymbol{L}^{(k)}|\mathcal{D}^{(k)}\right)\,d\boldsymbol{L}^{(k)}_{\preceq i\succ}\,d\boldsymbol{L}^{(k)}_{\preceq j\succ}\,d\sigma^{2}_{i}d\sigma^{2}_{j}

\displaystyle=f\left(\mathcal{D}^{(k)}\right)\prod_{r\neq i,j}f\left(\boldsymbol{X}^{(k)}_{r},\sigma^{2}_{r},\boldsymbol{L}^{(k)}_{\preceq r\succ}\Big|\mathcal{D}^{(k)}\right)\times\int\int f\left(\boldsymbol{X}^{(k)}_{i}\Big|\sigma^{2}_{i},\boldsymbol{L}^{(k)}_{\preceq i\succ}\right)f\left(\boldsymbol{L}^{(k)}_{\preceq i\succ}|\sigma^{2}_{i}\right)f\left(\sigma^{2}_{i}|\mathcal{D}^{(k)}\right)\,d\boldsymbol{L}^{(k)}_{\preceq i\succ}\,d\sigma^{2}_{i}

\displaystyle\times\int\int f\left(\boldsymbol{X}^{(k)}_{j}\Big|\sigma^{2}_{j},\boldsymbol{L}^{(k)}_{\preceq j\succ}\right)f\left(\boldsymbol{L}^{(k)}_{\preceq j\succ}|\sigma^{2}_{j}\right)f\left(\sigma^{2}_{j}|\mathcal{D}^{(k)}\right)\,d\boldsymbol{L}^{(k)}_{\preceq j\succ}\,d\sigma^{2}_{j}

\displaystyle=f\left(\mathcal{D}^{(k)}\right)\prod_{r\neq i,j}f\left(\boldsymbol{X}^{(k)}_{r},\sigma^{2}_{r},\boldsymbol{L}^{(k)}_{\preceq r\succ}\Big|\mathcal{D}^{(k)}\right)\times m(\boldsymbol{X}^{(k)}_{i}|\boldsymbol{X}^{(k)}_{\mathrm{pa}(i)},\mathcal{D}^{(k)})\,m(\boldsymbol{X}^{(k)}_{j}|\boldsymbol{X}^{(k)}_{\mathrm{pa}(j)},\mathcal{D}^{(k)}),

(59)

where
m(.)
is defined in (
50
).

For
j=1
, we apply the same process that we used before to compute
m(\boldsymbol{X}^{(k)}_{1}\,|\,\boldsymbol{X}^{(k)}_{\mathrm{pa}(1)},\mathcal{D}^{(k)})
, which is defined in (
57
). Substituting
f\left(\mathcal{D}^{(k)}\,|\,\boldsymbol{X}^{(k)},\boldsymbol{\theta}_{-\mathcal{U}}\right)
in equation (
47
) completes the proof for the
\texttt{Reverse}(i\to j)
operator.

C
Posteriors for
\boldsymbol{D}
and
\boldsymbol{L}^{(k)}

Assuming
\mathcal{D}^{(1)}
and
\mathcal{D}^{(2)}
are given, to compute the posteriors for
\boldsymbol{D}
and
\boldsymbol{L}^{(k)}
, we need to start with the joint probability distribution

\displaystyle f\left(\boldsymbol{X}^{(1)},\boldsymbol{X}^{(2)},\boldsymbol{D},\boldsymbol{L}^{(1)},\boldsymbol{L}^{(2)}\right)

\displaystyle=f\left(\boldsymbol{X}^{(1)},\boldsymbol{X}^{(2)}\Big|\boldsymbol{D},\boldsymbol{L}^{(1)},\boldsymbol{L}^{(2)}\right)f\left(\boldsymbol{L}^{(1)},\boldsymbol{L}^{(2)}|\boldsymbol{D}\right)f\left(\boldsymbol{D}\right)

\displaystyle=\prod_{j=1}^{q}f\left(\boldsymbol{X}^{(1)}_{j},\boldsymbol{X}^{(2)}_{j}\Big|{\sigma^{2}_{j}},\boldsymbol{L}^{(1)}_{\preceq j\succ},\boldsymbol{L}^{(2)}_{\preceq j\succ}\right)f\left(\boldsymbol{L}^{(1)}_{\preceq j\succ},\boldsymbol{L}^{(2)}_{\preceq j\succ}\Big|{\sigma^{2}_{j}}\right)f\left({\sigma^{2}_{j}}\right),

(60)

and because of the Markov property of Gaussian DAG’s, we just need to find the posterior for node
j
. So

\displaystyle f\left(\boldsymbol{X}^{(1)}_{j},\boldsymbol{X}^{(2)}_{j}\Big|{\sigma^{2}_{j}},\boldsymbol{L}^{(1)}_{\preceq j\succ},\boldsymbol{L}^{(2)}_{\preceq j\succ}\right)f\left(\boldsymbol{L}^{(1)}_{\preceq j\succ},\boldsymbol{L}^{(2)}_{\preceq j\succ}\Big|{\sigma^{2}_{j}}\right)f\left({\sigma^{2}_{j}}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{\frac{n_{1}}{2}}}{|\sigma^{2}_{j}\boldsymbol{I}_{n_{1}}|}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\exp\left\{-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}_{j}}\left(\boldsymbol{X}^{(1)}_{j}+\boldsymbol{X}^{(1)}_{\mathrm{pa}(j)}\boldsymbol{L}^{(1)}_{\preceq j\succ}\right)^{\prime}\left(\boldsymbol{X}^{(1)}_{j}+\boldsymbol{X}^{(1)}_{\mathrm{pa}(j)}\boldsymbol{L}^{(1)}_{\preceq j\succ}\right)\right\}

\displaystyle\times\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{\frac{n_{2}}{2}}}{|\sigma^{2}_{j}\boldsymbol{I}_{n_{2}}|}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\exp\left\{-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}_{j}}\left(\boldsymbol{X}^{(2)}_{j}+\boldsymbol{X}^{(2)}_{\mathrm{pa}(j)}\boldsymbol{L}^{(2)}_{\preceq j\succ}\right)^{\prime}\left(\boldsymbol{X}^{(2)}_{j}+\boldsymbol{X}^{(2)}_{\mathrm{pa}(j)}\boldsymbol{L}^{(2)}_{\preceq j\succ}\right)\right\}

\displaystyle\times\frac{1}{{(2\pi)^{\frac{{|\mathrm{pa}^{(1)}(j)|}}{2}}}}\Big|\frac{1}{g_{1}}\sigma^{2}_{j}\boldsymbol{I}_{|\mathrm{pa}^{(1)}(j)|}\Big|^{-\frac{1}{2}}\exp\left\{-\frac{g_{1}}{2\sigma^{2}_{j}}(\boldsymbol{L}^{(1)}_{\preceq j\succ})^{\prime}\boldsymbol{L}^{(1)}_{\preceq j\succ}\right\}

\displaystyle\times\frac{1}{{(2\pi)^{\frac{{|\mathrm{pa}^{(2)}(j)|}}{2}}}}\Big|\frac{1}{g_{2}}\sigma^{2}_{j}\boldsymbol{I}_{|\mathrm{pa}^{(2)}(j)|}\Big|^{-\frac{1}{2}}\exp\left\{-\frac{g_{2}}{2\sigma^{2}_{j}}(\boldsymbol{L}^{(2)}_{\preceq j\succ})^{\prime}\boldsymbol{L}^{(2)}_{\preceq j\succ}\right\}

\displaystyle\times C\,.\,(\sigma^{2}_{j})^{-\frac{a^{(1)}_{j}+a^{(2)}_{j}}{2}-1}\exp\left\{-\frac{\frac{g_{1}+g_{2}}{2}}{\sigma^{2}_{j}}\right\},

(61)

where
C={(\frac{g_{1}+g_{2}}{2})}^{\frac{a^{(1)}_{j}+a^{(2)}_{j}}{2}}/\Gamma(\frac{a^{(1)}_{j}+a^{(2)}_{j}}{2})
, which is not a function of
\boldsymbol{D}^{(k)}
and
\boldsymbol{L}^{(k)}
.

C.1
Posterior for
\boldsymbol{L}^{(k)}_{\preceq j\succ}|\sigma^{2}_{j},\boldsymbol{X}^{(k)}

For each
k\in\{1,2\}
, the terms with
\boldsymbol{L}^{(k)}_{\preceq j\succ}
in equation (
61
) are

\displaystyle A^{(k)}:=\exp\left\{-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}_{j}}\left(\boldsymbol{X}^{{}^{\prime}(k)}_{j}\boldsymbol{X}^{(k)}_{\mathrm{pa}(j)}\boldsymbol{L}^{(k)}_{\preceq j\succ}+{\boldsymbol{L}^{{}^{\prime}(k)}_{\preceq j\succ}}\boldsymbol{X}^{{}^{\prime}(k)}_{\mathrm{pa}(j)}\boldsymbol{X}^{(k)}_{j}+\boldsymbol{L}^{{}^{\prime}(k)}_{\preceq j\succ}\boldsymbol{X}^{{}^{\prime}(k)}_{\mathrm{pa}(j)}\boldsymbol{X}^{(k)}_{\mathrm{pa}(j)}\boldsymbol{L}^{(k)}_{\preceq j\succ}+g_{k}\boldsymbol{L}^{{}^{\prime}(k)}_{\preceq j\succ}\boldsymbol{L}^{(k)}_{\preceq j\succ}\right)\right\}.

(62)

Using notations defined in (
23
), the exponent term in
A^{(k)}
can be rewritten as

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}_{j}}\left[\boldsymbol{L}^{{}^{\prime}(k)}_{\preceq j\succ}\left(\boldsymbol{X}^{{}^{\prime}(k)}_{\mathrm{pa}(j)}\boldsymbol{X}^{(k)}_{\mathrm{pa}(j)}+g_{k}\boldsymbol{I}_{|\mathrm{pa}^{(k)}(j)|}\right)\boldsymbol{L}^{(k)}_{\preceq j\succ}+\boldsymbol{L}^{{}^{\prime}(k)}_{\preceq j\succ}\bar{\boldsymbol{T}}^{(k)}_{j}\left({\bar{\boldsymbol{T}}^{(k)}_{j}}\right)^{-1}\boldsymbol{X}^{{}^{\prime}(k)}_{\mathrm{pa}(j)}\boldsymbol{X}^{(k)}_{j}+\boldsymbol{X}^{{}^{\prime}(k)}_{j}\boldsymbol{X}^{(k)}_{\mathrm{pa}(j)}\left({\bar{\boldsymbol{T}}^{{}^{\prime}(k)}_{j}}\right)^{-1}\bar{\boldsymbol{T}}^{(k)}_{j}\boldsymbol{L}^{(k)}_{\preceq j\succ}\right]

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}_{j}}\left[\boldsymbol{L}^{{}^{\prime}(k)}_{\preceq j\succ}\bar{\boldsymbol{T}}^{(k)}_{j}\boldsymbol{L}^{(k)}_{\preceq j\succ}+\boldsymbol{L}^{{}^{\prime}(k)}_{\preceq j\succ}\bar{\boldsymbol{T}}^{(k)}_{j}\hat{\boldsymbol{L}}^{(k)}_{j}+\hat{\boldsymbol{L}}^{{}^{\prime}(k)}_{j}\bar{\boldsymbol{T}}^{{}^{\prime}(k)}_{j}\boldsymbol{L}^{(k)}_{\preceq j\succ}\right],

(63)

which is proportional to the exponent of a normal distribution, i.e.,

\displaystyle A^{(k)}\propto\exp\left\{-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}_{j}}\left(\boldsymbol{L}^{(k)}_{\preceq j\succ}+\hat{\boldsymbol{L}}^{(k)}_{j}\right)^{\prime}\bar{\boldsymbol{T}}^{(k)}_{j}\left(\boldsymbol{L}^{(k)}_{\preceq j\succ}+\hat{\boldsymbol{L}}^{(k)}_{j}\right)\right\}.

(64)

In other words, equation (
62
) can be written as

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{L}_{\preceq j\succ}^{(k)}|\sigma^{2}_{j},\boldsymbol{X}^{(k)}\sim\mathcal{N}_{|\mathrm{pa}^{(k)}(j)|}\left(-\hat{\boldsymbol{L}}^{\prime(k)}_{j},\sigma^{2}_{j}(\bar{\boldsymbol{T}}^{(k)}_{j})^{-1}\right),

(65)

which completes the proof.

For case when
j=1
, because we set
\sigma^{2}_{1}=1
, we have

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{L}_{\preceq 1\succ}^{(k)}|\boldsymbol{X}^{(k)}\sim\mathcal{N}_{|\mathrm{pa}^{(k)}(1)|}\left(-\hat{\boldsymbol{L}}^{\prime(k)}_{1},(\bar{\boldsymbol{T}}^{(k)}_{1})^{-1}\right).

(66)

C.2
Posterior for
\sigma^{2}_{j}|\boldsymbol{X}^{(1)}_{j},\boldsymbol{X}^{(2)}_{j}

For
j=1
, we set
\sigma^{2}_{1}=1
, so we assume
j>1
in this section. The terms with
\sigma^{2}_{j}
in equation (
61
) are

\displaystyle(\sigma^{2}_{j})^{-\frac{n_{1}}{2}-\frac{n_{2}}{2}-\frac{a^{(1)}_{j}}{2}-\frac{a^{(2)}_{j}}{2}-1-\frac{|\mathrm{pa}^{(1)}(j)|}{2}-\frac{|\mathrm{pa}^{(2)}(j)|}{2}}

\displaystyle\times\exp\left\{-\frac{1}{2{\sigma^{2}_{j}}}\left({\boldsymbol{X}^{{}^{\prime}(1)}_{j}}\boldsymbol{X}^{(1)}_{j}+{\boldsymbol{X}^{{}^{\prime}(2)}_{j}}\boldsymbol{X}^{(2)}_{j}+g_{1}+g_{2}\right)\right\}

\displaystyle\times\exp\left\{-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}_{j}}\left(\boldsymbol{X}^{{}^{\prime}(1)}_{j}\boldsymbol{X}^{(1)}_{\mathrm{pa}(j)}\boldsymbol{L}^{(1)}_{\preceq j\succ}+{\boldsymbol{L}^{{}^{\prime}(1)}_{\preceq j\succ}}\boldsymbol{X}^{{}^{\prime}(1)}_{\mathrm{pa}(j)}\boldsymbol{X}^{(1)}_{j}+\boldsymbol{L}^{{}^{\prime}(1)}_{\preceq j\succ}\boldsymbol{X}^{{}^{\prime}(1)}_{\mathrm{pa}(j)}\boldsymbol{X}^{(1)}_{\mathrm{pa}(j)}\boldsymbol{L}^{(1)}_{\preceq j\succ}+g_{1}\boldsymbol{L}^{{}^{\prime}(1)}\boldsymbol{L}^{(1)}_{\preceq j\succ}\right)\right\}

\displaystyle\times\exp\left\{-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}_{j}}\left(\boldsymbol{X}^{{}^{\prime}(2)}_{j}\boldsymbol{X}^{(2)}_{\mathrm{pa}(j)}\boldsymbol{L}^{(2)}_{\preceq j\succ}+{\boldsymbol{L}^{{}^{\prime}(2)}_{\preceq j\succ}}\boldsymbol{X}^{{}^{\prime}(2)}_{\mathrm{pa}(j)}\boldsymbol{X}^{(2)}_{j}+\boldsymbol{L}^{{}^{\prime}(2)}_{\preceq j\succ}\boldsymbol{X}^{{}^{\prime}(2)}_{\mathrm{pa}(j)}\boldsymbol{X}^{(2)}_{\mathrm{pa}(j)}\boldsymbol{L}^{(2)}_{\preceq j\succ}+g_{2}\boldsymbol{L}^{{}^{\prime}(2)}\boldsymbol{L}^{(2)}_{\preceq j\succ}\right)\right\}

(67)

Lets denote by
A^{(k)}
the last two terms in the above equations for
k=1,2
, which are a function of
\boldsymbol{L}^{(k)}_{\preceq j\succ}
. To obtain the marginal posterior for
\sigma^{2}_{j}
, we need to integrate
A^{(k)}
with respect to
\boldsymbol{L}^{(k)}_{\preceq j\succ}
. We showed that
A^{(k)}
is proportional to the p.d.f of a normal distribution in equation (
64
), so

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{|\mathrm{pa}^{(k)}(j)|}}{A^{(k)}}\,\,d\boldsymbol{L}^{(k)}_{\preceq j\succ}=(2\pi)^{\frac{|\mathrm{pa}^{(k)}(j)|}{2}}\,\,{\left|\frac{\bar{\boldsymbol{T}}_{j}^{(k)}}{\sigma^{2}_{j}}\right|}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\exp\left\{\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}_{j}}\hat{\boldsymbol{L}}^{{}^{\prime}(k)}_{j}\bar{\boldsymbol{T}}^{(k)}_{j}\hat{\boldsymbol{L}}^{(k)}_{j}\right\}.

(68)

Substituting (
68
) in (
67
) and using the fact
{\left|\frac{\bar{\boldsymbol{T}}_{j}^{(k)}}{\sigma^{2}_{j}}\right|}^{-\frac{1}{2}}={\sigma^{2}_{j}}^{\frac{|\mathrm{pa}^{(k)}(j)|}{2}}{\left|\bar{\boldsymbol{T}}_{j}^{(k)}\right|}^{-\frac{1}{2}}
, we have

\displaystyle f(\sigma^{2}_{j}|\boldsymbol{X}^{(1)}_{j},\boldsymbol{X}^{(2)}_{j})

\displaystyle\propto({\sigma^{2}_{j}})^{-\frac{n_{1}}{2}-\frac{n_{2}}{2}-\frac{a^{(1)}_{j}}{2}-\frac{a^{(2)}_{j}}{2}-1-\frac{|\mathrm{pa}^{(1)}(j)|}{2}-\frac{|\mathrm{pa}^{(2)}(j)|}{2}+\frac{|\mathrm{pa}^{(1)}(j)|}{2}+\frac{|\mathrm{pa}^{(2)}(j)|}{2}}

\displaystyle\times\exp\left\{\frac{-\frac{1}{2}(g_{1}+g_{2}+{\boldsymbol{X}^{{}^{\prime}(1)}_{j}}\boldsymbol{X}^{(1)}_{j}+{\boldsymbol{X}^{{}^{\prime}(2)}_{j}}\boldsymbol{X}^{(2)}_{j}-\hat{\boldsymbol{L}}^{{}^{\prime}(1)}_{j}\bar{\boldsymbol{T}}^{(1)}_{j}\hat{\boldsymbol{L}}^{(1)}_{j}-\hat{\boldsymbol{L}}^{{}^{\prime}(2)}_{j}\bar{\boldsymbol{T}}^{(2)}_{j}\hat{\boldsymbol{L}}^{(2)}_{j})}{\sigma^{2}_{j}}\right\}.

(69)

Therefore the posterior for
\sigma^{2}_{j}|\boldsymbol{X}^{(1)}_{j},\boldsymbol{X}^{(2)}_{j}
becomes

\sigma^{2}_{j}|\boldsymbol{X}^{(1)}_{j},\boldsymbol{X}^{(2)}_{j}\sim\mathrm{I-Ga}\left(\frac{a^{(1)}_{j}}{2}+\frac{a^{(2)}_{j}}{2}+\frac{n_{1}}{2}+\frac{n_{2}}{2},\frac{1}{2}K\right),

(70)

where
K=\frac{1}{2}(g_{1}+g_{2}+{\boldsymbol{X}^{{}^{\prime}(1)}_{j}}\boldsymbol{X}^{(1)}_{j}+{\boldsymbol{X}^{{}^{\prime}(2)}_{j}}\boldsymbol{X}^{(2)}_{j}-\hat{\boldsymbol{L}}^{{}^{\prime}(1)}_{j}\bar{\boldsymbol{T}}^{(1)}_{j}\hat{\boldsymbol{L}}^{(1)}_{j}-\hat{\boldsymbol{L}}^{{}^{\prime}(2)}_{j}\bar{\boldsymbol{T}}^{(2)}_{j}\hat{\boldsymbol{L}}^{(2)}_{j})
.

D
Posterior for
\theta\big|\boldsymbol{y}^{(1)},\boldsymbol{y}^{(2)},\boldsymbol{X}^{(1)}_{-1},\boldsymbol{X}^{(2)}_{-1},\boldsymbol{D},\boldsymbol{L}^{(1)},\boldsymbol{L}^{(2)},\mathcal{D}^{(1)},\mathcal{D}^{(2)}

The posterior for
\theta
can be written as

f\left(\theta\big|\boldsymbol{y}^{(1)},\boldsymbol{y}^{(2)},\boldsymbol{X}^{(1)}_{-1},\boldsymbol{X}^{(2)}_{-1},\boldsymbol{D},\boldsymbol{L}^{(1)},\boldsymbol{L}^{(2)},\mathcal{D}^{(1)},\mathcal{D}^{(2)}\right)\propto f\left(\boldsymbol{y}^{(1)},\boldsymbol{y}^{(2)},\boldsymbol{X}^{(1)}_{-1},\boldsymbol{X}^{(2)}_{-1}\big|\boldsymbol{D},\boldsymbol{L}^{(1)},\boldsymbol{L}^{(2)},\mathcal{D}^{(1)},\mathcal{D}^{(2)},\theta\right)f(\theta),

(71)

where
f(\theta)\propto 1
.
To simplify the equations, we omit
\mathcal{D}^{(1)}
and
\mathcal{D}^{(2)}
. By seperating
\boldsymbol{X}^{(k)}_{1}
, the full likelihood function can be written as

\displaystyle f\left(\boldsymbol{y}^{(1)},\boldsymbol{y}^{(2)},\boldsymbol{X}^{(1)},\boldsymbol{X}^{(2)}\big|\boldsymbol{D},\boldsymbol{L}^{(1)},\boldsymbol{L}^{(2)},\theta\right)

\displaystyle=\prod_{j=2}^{q}f_{\mathcal{N}_{n_{1}}}\left(\boldsymbol{X}^{(1)}_{j}|-\boldsymbol{X}^{(1)}_{\mathrm{pa}(j)}\boldsymbol{L}^{(1)}_{\preceq j\succ},\sigma^{2}_{j}\boldsymbol{I}_{n_{1}}\right).f_{\mathcal{N}_{n_{1}}}\left(\boldsymbol{X}^{(1)}_{1}|-\boldsymbol{X}^{(1)}_{\mathrm{pa}(1)}\boldsymbol{L}^{(1)}_{\preceq 1\succ},\boldsymbol{I}_{n_{1}}\right).\prod_{i=1}^{n_{1}}\mathbbm{1}\left(\theta_{y^{(1)}_{i}-1}<x^{(1)}_{i,1}\leq\theta_{y^{(1)}_{i}}\right)

\displaystyle\times\prod_{j=2}^{q}f_{\mathcal{N}_{n_{2}}}\left(\boldsymbol{X}^{(2)}_{j}|-\boldsymbol{X}^{(2)}_{\mathrm{pa}(j)}\boldsymbol{L}^{(2)}_{\preceq j\succ},\sigma^{2}_{j}\boldsymbol{I}_{n_{2}}\right).f_{\mathcal{N}_{n_{2}}}\left(\boldsymbol{X}^{(2)}_{1}|-\boldsymbol{X}^{(2)}_{\mathrm{pa}(1)}\boldsymbol{L}^{(2)}_{\preceq 1\succ},\boldsymbol{I}_{n_{2}}\right).\prod_{i=1}^{n_{2}}\mathbbm{1}\left(\theta_{y^{(2)}_{i}-1}<x^{(2)}_{i,1}\leq\theta_{y^{(2)}_{i}}\right)

\displaystyle=\prod_{k=1}^{2}f\left(\boldsymbol{X}^{(k)}_{-1}\big|\boldsymbol{D},\boldsymbol{L}^{(k)}\right)\,f_{\mathcal{N}_{n_{k}}}\left(\boldsymbol{X}^{(k)}_{1}|-\boldsymbol{X}^{(k)}_{\mathrm{pa}(1)}\boldsymbol{L}^{(k)}_{\preceq 1\succ},\boldsymbol{I}_{n_{k}}\right).\prod_{i=1}^{n_{k}}\mathbbm{1}\left(\theta_{y^{(k)}_{i}-1}<x^{(k)}_{i,1}\leq\theta_{y^{(k)}_{i}}\right),

(72)

where
f\left(\boldsymbol{X}^{(k)}_{-1}\big|\boldsymbol{D},\boldsymbol{L}^{(k)}\right)
does not depend on
\theta
.

In order to get the marginal distribution, we need to take integral from both sides of (
72
) with respect to
\boldsymbol{X}^{(1)}_{1}
and
\boldsymbol{X}^{(2)}_{1}
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\int\int f\left(\boldsymbol{y}^{(1)},\boldsymbol{y}^{(2)},\boldsymbol{X}^{(1)},\boldsymbol{X}^{(2)}\big|\boldsymbol{D},\boldsymbol{L}^{(1)},\boldsymbol{L}^{(2)},\theta\right)\,\,d\boldsymbol{X}^{(1)}_{1}\,\,d\boldsymbol{X}^{(2)}_{1}

\displaystyle\propto\prod_{k=1}^{2}\int f_{\mathcal{N}_{n_{k}}}\left(\boldsymbol{X}^{(k)}_{1}|-\boldsymbol{X}^{(k)}_{\mathrm{pa}(1)}\boldsymbol{L}^{(k)}_{\preceq 1\succ},\boldsymbol{I}_{n_{k}}\right).\prod_{i=1}^{n_{k}}\mathbbm{1}\left(\theta_{y^{(k)}_{i}-1}<x^{(k)}_{i,1}\leq\theta_{y^{(k)}_{i}}\right)\,\,d\boldsymbol{X}^{(k)}_{1}

\displaystyle=\prod_{k=1}^{2}\prod_{i=1}^{n_{k}}\int f_{\mathcal{N}}\left({x}^{(k)}_{i,1}|-\boldsymbol{x}^{(k)}_{\mathrm{pa}(1)}\boldsymbol{L}^{(k)}_{\preceq 1\succ},1\right)\mathbbm{1}\left(\theta_{y^{(k)}_{i}-1}<x^{(k)}_{i,1}\leq\theta_{y^{(k)}_{i}}\right)dx^{(k)}_{i,1},

(73)

where
\theta_{-1}:=-\infty,\theta_{0}:=\theta
,
\theta_{1}:=\infty
and

\displaystyle\int f_{\mathcal{N}}\left({x}^{(k)}_{i,1}|-\boldsymbol{x}^{(k)}_{\mathrm{pa}(1)}\boldsymbol{L}^{(k)}_{\preceq 1\succ},1\right)\mathbbm{1}\left(\theta_{y^{(k)}_{i}-1}<x^{(k)}_{i,1}\leq\theta_{y^{(k)}_{i}}\right)dx^{(k)}_{i,1}=\int_{\theta_{y^{(k)}_{i}-1}}^{\theta_{y^{(k)}_{i}}}f_{\mathcal{N}}\left({x}^{(k)}_{i,1}|-\boldsymbol{x}^{(k)}_{\mathrm{pa}(1)}\boldsymbol{L}^{(k)}_{\preceq 1\succ},1\right)\,dx^{(k)}_{i,1}

is either the CDF or the survival function of a
\mathcal{N}(-\boldsymbol{x}^{(k)}_{\mathrm{pa}(1)}\boldsymbol{L}^{(k)}_{\preceq 1\succ},1)
ditsribution for
y_{i}^{(k)}=0
and
y_{i}^{(k)}=1
, respectively.

Therefore,

\displaystyle f\left(\theta\big|\boldsymbol{y}^{(1)},\boldsymbol{y}^{(2)},\boldsymbol{X}^{(1)}_{-1},\boldsymbol{X}^{(2)}_{-1},\mathcal{D}^{(1)},\mathcal{D}^{(2)}\right)\propto\prod_{k=1}^{2}\prod_{i=1}^{n_{k}}\Psi\big(y^{(k)}_{i},\theta|-\boldsymbol{x}^{(k)}_{\mathrm{pa}(1)}\boldsymbol{L}^{(k)}_{\preceq 1\succ},1\big),

(74)

where
\Psi\big(y,\eta|\mu,\sigma^{2}\big):=|y-\Phi(\eta|\mu,\sigma^{2}\big)|
. In fact,
\Psi\big(y,\eta|\mu,\sigma^{2}\big)
is a notation for the CDF or the survival function of a
\mathcal{N}(\mu,\sigma^{2})
ditsribution for
y=0
and
y=1
, respectively.

E
Proof of proposition 1

We first need to reorder the matrix
\boldsymbol{X}
as
\boldsymbol{X}^{*}=(\boldsymbol{X}_{-1},\boldsymbol{X}_{1})
and let
\text{cov}(\boldsymbol{X}^{*})=\boldsymbol{\Omega}^{-1}
.
The matrix
\boldsymbol{\Omega}
can also be partitioned as

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Omega}

\displaystyle=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{11}&\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{12}\\
\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{21}&\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{22}\end{array}\right).

Using Cholesky decomposition, we can decompose
\boldsymbol{\Omega}
into

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Omega}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{L}\boldsymbol{L}^{\prime}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\boldsymbol{L}_{11}&\boldsymbol{0}\\
\boldsymbol{L}_{21}&\boldsymbol{L}_{22}\end{array}\right)\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\boldsymbol{L}^{\prime}_{11}&\boldsymbol{L}^{\prime}_{21}\\
\boldsymbol{0}&\boldsymbol{L}^{\prime}_{22}\end{array}\right).

By equating the submatrices in (
E
) and (
E
), we have
\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{11}=\boldsymbol{L}_{11}\boldsymbol{L}^{\prime}_{11}
,
\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{12}=\boldsymbol{L}_{11}\boldsymbol{L}^{\prime}_{21}
and
\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{22}=\boldsymbol{L}_{21}\boldsymbol{L}^{\prime}_{21}+\boldsymbol{L}_{22}\boldsymbol{L}^{\prime}_{22}
.These equations can be written as

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{L}_{11}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{11}^{1/2}

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{L}^{\prime}_{21}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{11}^{{}^{\prime}-1/2}\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{12}

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{L}_{22}

\displaystyle=\left(\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{22}-\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{11}^{-1/2}\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{12}^{\prime}\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{12}\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{11}^{-1/2}\right)^{1/2},

(81)

where the square root for the matrix
A
is defined by
A=A^{1/2}A^{{}^{\prime}1/2}
.
On the other hand,
\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{-1}:=\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{11}=\text{cov}(\boldsymbol{X}_{-1})
can be decomposed to
\boldsymbol{L}_{-1}\boldsymbol{L}^{\prime}_{-1}
, so
\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{11}^{1/2}=\boldsymbol{L}_{-1}
, which completes the proof that
\boldsymbol{L}_{11}=\boldsymbol{L}_{-1}
.
