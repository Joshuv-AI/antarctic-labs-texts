Hugo Araújo, Carlos Gustavo Moreira, Alex Zamudio Espinosa

Abstract
. In this paper we prove that among pairs K , K ′ ⊂ ℂ K,\,K^{\prime}\subset\mathbbm{C} of conformal dynamically defined Cantor sets with sum of Hausdorff dimensions H ​ D ​ ( K ) + H ​ D ​ ( K ′ ) > 2 HD(K)+HD(K^{\prime})>2 , there is an open and dense subset of such pairs verifying int ​ ( K − K ′ ) ≠ ∅ \text{int}(K-K^{\prime})\neq\emptyset . This is motivated by the work [ 1 ] , where Moreira and Yoccoz proved a similar statement for dynamically defined Cantor sets in the real line. Here we adapt their argument to the context of conformal Cantor sets in the complex plane, this requires the introduction of several new concepts and a more detailed analysis in some parts of the argument.



1.
Introduction

The aim of this paper is to prove a complex version of the main theorem in
[
1
]
. We will prove that among pairs
K,\,K^{\prime}\subset\mathbbm{C}
of conformal dynamically defined Cantor sets with sum of Hausdorff dimensions
HD(K)+HD(K^{\prime})>2
, there is an open and dense subset of such pairs verifying
\text{int}(K-K^{\prime})\neq\emptyset
. The analogous statement for dynamically defined Cantor sets in the real line was proved by Moreira and Yoccoz in
[
1
]
. Here we adapt their argument to the context of conformal Cantor sets, this requires the introduction of several new concepts and a more detailed analysis in some parts of the argument. The main new difficulties come from the fact that smooth real maps are naturally conformal, in the sense that their derivative preserves angles, and this is not true for maps in dimension two. A rough version of our main theorem is the following.

Theorem 1.1
.

There exists an open and dense subset
\mathcal{V}
of the space of pairs
(K_{1},K_{2})
of conformal Cantor sets in the complex plane such that
HD(K_{1})+HD(K_{2})>2
satisfying the following. If
(K,K^{\prime})\in\mathcal{V}
, then

\mathcal{I}_{s}(K,K^{\prime})=\{\lambda\in\mathbbm{C}:(K+\lambda,K^{\prime})\text{ has stable intersection}\}

is dense in

\mathcal{I}=\{\lambda\in\mathbbm{C}:(K+\lambda)\cap K^{\prime}\neq\emptyset\}.

In particular,
\text{int}(K-K^{\prime})\neq\emptyset
.

The work of Moreira and Yoccoz was motivated by a conjecture of Palis, according to which generic pairs of dynamically defined Cantor sets in the real line
K,\,K^{\prime}
either verify that their arithmetic difference
K-K^{\prime}=\{x-y:x\in K,\,y\in K^{\prime}\}
has zero Lebesgue measure or non-empty interior. Palis conjecture emerged from his work with Takens (
[
2
]
,
[
3
]
), where, in their study of homoclinic bifurcations for surface diffeomorphisms, they used the crucial fact that if
HD(K)+HD(K^{\prime})<1
, then
K-K^{\prime}
has zero Lebesgue measure. Looking for a converse, Palis proposed its conjecture.

The study of homoclinic bifurcations has proved to be fruitful in the understanding of dynamics for surface diffeomorphisms. Complicated dynamical phenomena arise from them. For example, arbitrarily close to any diffeomorphism exhibiting a generic homoclinic tangency, there are open regions in which any diffeomorphism belonging to a residual set has an infinite number of sinks- this is the so called Newhouse phenomenon. Looking for analogous results and using similar ideas to those of Newhouse, Buzzard
[
4
]
proved the existence of an open set of automorphisms of
\mathbbm{C}^{2}
with stable homoclinic tangencies. Buzzard’s strategy was very similar to the work of Newhouse [9], constructing a “very thick” horseshoe, such that the Cantor sets, this time living in the complex plane, associated to it would also be “very thick”. However, the concept of thickness does not have a simple extension to this complex setting and so the argument to guarantee intersections between the Cantor sets after a small pertubation is different.

Furthermore, Moreira and Yoccoz were able to use their solution to Palis conjecture in the study of homoclinic bifurcations for surface diffeomorphisms (see
[
5
]
). They proved that given a surface diffeomorphism
F
with a homoclinic quadratic tangency associated to a horseshoe with dimension larger than one, the set of diffeomorphisms close to
F
presenting a stable tangency has positive density at
F
.

The authors of this paper have been trying to push all this theory to the context of Cantor sets in the complex plane and apply it to the study of homoclinic bifurcations of automorphisms of
\mathbbm{C}^{2}
. In
[
6
]
, Araújo and Moreira introduced the concept of conformal Cantor sets and also extended the concept of recurrent compact sets to this context, which serves as a tool to obtain stable intersection. More importantly, Araújo and Moreira showed that given a complex horseshoe of an automorphism of
\mathbbm{C}^{2}
, one can associate to it a conformal Cantor set. Using all this, they were able to recast Buzzard’s example in terms of the theory of conformal Cantor sets.

In the paper
[
7
]
, Moreira and Zamudio proved a multidimensional version of the scale recurrence lemma for conformal Cantor sets. The real version of this lemma was a key step in the solution, by Moreira and Yoccoz, of Palis conjecture. Moreover, Moreira and Zamudio used the multidimensional conformal version of the scale recurrence lemma to prove a dimension formula relating the Hausdorff dimension of the image of a product of conformal Cantor sets
h(K_{1}\times...\times K_{n})
, where
h
is a
C^{1}
function, and the sum of the Hausdorff dimensions
HD(K_{1})+...+HD(K_{n})
.

In this paper we intend to use the results in
[
6
]
,
[
7
]
and obtain a version of Palis conjecture for conformal Cantor sets. In a future paper we plan to use the concepts and ideas laid down in this paper to study homoclinic bifurcations for automorphisms of
\mathbbm{C}^{2}
. We plan to obtain results analogous to the ones in
[
5
]
. We expect that conformal regular Cantor sets in
\mathbbm{C}
will play a role in the study of homoclinic bifurcations of automorphisms of
\mathbbm{C}^{2}
, similar to regular Cantor sets in
\mathbbm{R}
in the study of homoclinic bifurcations of surface diffeomorphisms.

2.1.
Notations

Here we introduce some of the notations we will use along the text:

•

We will work with the space of complex numbers
\mathbbm{C}
. We identify it with
\mathbbm{R}^{2}
in the usual way. In this space we will use the Euclidean metric, given by the norm
|(x,y)|=\sqrt{x^{2}+y^{2}}
.

•

We will also work with the space of non-zero complex numbers
\mathbbm{C}^{*}
. We will sometimes identify
\mathbbm{C}^{*}
with
J=\mathbbm{R}\times\mathbbm{T}
, where
\mathbbm{T}=\mathbbm{R}/(2\pi\mathbbm{Z})
, through the map
(t,v)\to e^{t+iv}
. Note that
J
has the structure of a commutative group. We will endow
J
with the metric coming from the inclusion
J=\mathbbm{C}^{*}\subset\mathbbm{C}
.

•

Given a linear map
A:\mathbbm{R}^{2}\to\mathbbm{R}^{2}
we denote its norm by
|A|
, its minimum norm by
m(A)
. They are given by

|A|=\sup_{v\neq 0}\frac{|Av|}{|v|},\,\,m(A)=\inf_{v\neq 0}\frac{|Av|}{|v|}.

We will say that
A
is conformal if
|A|=m(A)
.

•

We will use
Id
to denote the identity matrix, sometimes we will use the same symbol for the identity function. Each case will be clear from the context.

•

Let
(X,d)
be a metric space and
A\subset X
. We will use the notation
V_{\delta}(A)
for the open
\delta
neighborhood around
A
, i.e.
V_{\delta}(A)=\{x\in X:\,d(x,A)<\delta\}
. For a point
x\in X
and a positive real number
r
we use the notation
B(x,r)=V_{r}(\{x\})
.

•

We will have to deal with many inequalities and several parameters. In order to reduce the number of constants introduced along the text we will use the following notations: Given expressions
f(x)
and
g(x)
depending in the parameter
x
, when we write
f(x)\lesssim g(x)
this means that there is a constant
C>0
such that
f(x)\leq Cg(x)
for all
x
. The constant
C
can only depend on other constants when those have already been fixed. This ensures that we will not get any contradiction between the different constants that will appear.
f\gtrsim g
will mean
g\lesssim f
,
f\approx g
will mean
f\lesssim g
and
g\gtrsim f
both hold.

•

Derivative of a
C^{l}
function, we use two notations
D^{j}f(x)
or
D^{j}_{x}f
, both mean derivative of order
j
of
f
at the point
x
. They are
j
-linear functions.

•

The uniform norm of functions
f:X\to Y
, where
X
and
Y
are subsets of normed vector spaces, will be denoted by
\left\lVert f\right\rVert\coloneqq\sup_{x\in X}\left\lvert f(x)\right\rvert
. In many occasions,
f
will be the derivative of order
j
of a
C^{l}
function.

•

Given a real number
m
we will denote by
[m]
its integer part, this is
[m]=\sup\{r\in\mathbbm{Z}:r\leq m\}
.

2.2.
The space of conformal regular Cantor Sets

We begin by the very concept of
conformal regular Cantor set
. First we remember that a
C^{m}

regular Cantor set
, also called
dynamically defined Cantor set
, is given by the following data.

•

A finite set
\mathbb{A}
of letters and a set
B\subset\mathbb{A}\times\mathbb{A}
of admissible pairs.

•

For each
a\in\mathbb{A}
a compact connected set
G(a)\subset\mathbb{C}
.

•

A
C^{m}
map
g:V\to\mathbb{C}
, for
m>1
, defined on an open neighbourhood
V
of
\bigsqcup_{a\in\mathbb{A}}G(a)
.

These data must verify the following assumptions:

•

The sets
G(a)
,
a\in\mathbb{A}
, are pairwise disjoint.

•

(a,b)\in B
implies
G(b)\subset g(G(a))
, otherwise
G(b)\cap g(G(a))=\emptyset
.

•

For each
a\in\mathbb{A}
, the restriction
g|_{G(a)}
can be extended to a
C^{m}
diffeomorphism from an open neighborhood of
G(a)
onto its image such that
m(Dg)>\mu
for some constant
\mu>1
, where
m(A):=\displaystyle{\inf_{v\neq 0}\frac{\lvert Av\rvert}{\lvert v\rvert}}
is the minimum norm of the linear operator
A
on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
.

•

The subshift
(\Sigma,\sigma)
induced by
B
, called the type of the Cantor set,

\Sigma=\{\underline{a}=(a_{0},a_{1},a_{2},\dots)\in\mathbb{A}^{\mathbb{N}}:(a_{i},a_{i+1})\in B,\forall i\geq 0\},

\sigma(a_{0},a_{1},a_{2},\dots)=(a_{1},a_{2},a_{3},\dots)
, is topologically mixing.

Once we have all these data we can define a Cantor set (i.e. a totally disconnected, perfect compact set) on the complex plane:

K=\bigcap_{n\geq 0}g^{-n}\left(\bigsqcup_{a\in\mathbb{A}}G(a)\right).

We say that such a set is
conformal
if, for all
x\in K
, the derivative of
g
at
x
, denoted by
Dg(x):\mathbbm{R}^{2}\to\mathbbm{R}^{2}
, is a conformal linear operator.

Notice that we always consider the degree of differentiability of the map
g
,
m
, to be a real number larger than one. This means that
g
has derivatives up to order
[m]
and
D^{[m]}g
is Hölder with exponent
m-[m]
. This hypothesis, as we will precise later in this section, allows us to control the geometry of small parts of the Cantor set
K
. All Cantor sets in this paper will be conformal regular Cantor sets.

Besides, as we mentioned on the introduction, an important family of dynamically defined sets are contained in the class of
C^{m}
conformal regular Cantor sets. Let
G
be an automorphism of
\mathbbm{C}^{2}
exhibiting a horseshoe
\Lambda
and
p
be a hyperbolic periodic point in it. Then, there is a subset
U\subset W^{s}(p)
, open in the topology of
W^{s}(p)
as an immersed manifold, and some
\varepsilon>0
sufficiently small such that
\Lambda\cap U
is, after some parametrization, a
C^{1+\varepsilon}
conformal regular Cantor set. See
Theorem A
of
[
6
]
.

We will usually write only
K
to represent all the data that defines a particular dynamically defined Cantor set. Of course, the compact set
K
can be described in multiple ways as a Cantor set constructed with the objects above, but whenever we say that
K
is a Cantor set we assume that one particular set of data as above is fixed. In this spirit, we may represent the Cantor set
K
by the map
g
that defines it as described above, since all the data can be inferred if we know
g
.

Notice that in our definition we did not require the pieces
G(a)
to have non-empty interior. To circumvent this, we introduce the following sets.

Lemma 2.1
.

There is
\delta>0
sufficiently small such that the sets
G^{*}(a)\coloneqq V_{\delta}(G(a))
satisfy:

(i)

G^{*}(a)
is open and connected.

(ii)

G(a)\subset G^{*}(a)
and
g|_{G^{*}(a)}
can be extended to an open neighbourhood of
\overline{G^{*}(a)}
, such that it is a
C^{m}
embedding (with
C^{m}
inverse ) from this neighbourhood to its image and
m(Dg)>\mu
.

(iii)

The sets
\overline{G^{*}(a)}
,
a\in\mathbb{A}
, are pairwise disjoint.

(iv)

(a,b)\in B
implies
\overline{G^{*}(b)}\subset g(G^{*}(a))
, and
(a,b)\notin B
implies
\overline{G^{*}(b)}\cap\overline{g(G^{*}(a))}=\emptyset
.

The sets
\overline{G^{*}(a)}
could substitute the pieces
G(a)
in our definition as to make the hypothesis of open interiors be true. These changes do not enlarge the Cantor set. To see this, we introduce more notation and a previous result.

Associated to a Cantor set
K
we define the sets

\displaystyle\Sigma^{fin}

\displaystyle=\{(a_{0},\dots,a_{n}):(a_{i},a_{i+1})\in B\ \forall i,\,0\leq i<n\},

\displaystyle\Sigma^{-}

\displaystyle=\{(\dots,a_{-n},a_{-n+1},\dots,a_{-1},a_{0}):(a_{i-1},a_{i})\in B\ \forall i\leq 0\}.

Given
\underline{a}=(a_{0},\dots,a_{n})
,
\underline{b}=(b_{0},\dots,b_{m})
,
\underline{\theta}^{1}=(\dots,\theta^{1}_{-2},\theta^{1}_{-1},\theta^{1}_{0})
and
\underline{\theta}^{2}=(\dots,\theta^{2}_{-2},\theta^{2}_{-1},\theta^{2}_{0})
, we write:

•

if
a_{n}=b_{0}
,
\underline{ab}=(a_{0},\dots,a_{n},b_{1},\dots,b_{m})
;

•

if
\theta^{1}_{0}=a_{0}
,
\underline{\theta^{1}a}=(\dots,\theta^{1}_{-2},\theta^{1}_{-1},a_{0},\dots,a_{n})

•

if
\underline{\theta}^{1}\neq\underline{\theta}^{2}
and
\theta^{1}_{0}=\theta^{2}_{0}
,
\underline{\theta}^{1}\wedge\underline{\theta}^{2}=(\theta_{-j},\theta_{-j+1},\dots,\theta_{0})
, in which
\theta_{-i}=\theta^{1}_{-i}=\theta^{2}_{-i}
for all
i=0,\dots,j
and
\theta^{1}_{-j-1}\neq\theta^{2}_{-j-1}
.

•

Define the distance between
\underline{\theta}^{1}
and
\underline{\theta}^{2}
by
d(\underline{\theta}^{1},\underline{\theta}^{2})=diam(G(\underline{\theta}^{1}\wedge\underline{\theta}^{2}))
.

•

if
\underline{a}
starts with
\underline{b}
, we define
\underline{a}/\underline{b}
as the unique finite word such that
\underline{a}=\underline{b}(\underline{a}/\underline{b})
.

For
\underline{a}=(a_{0},a_{1},\dots,a_{n})\in\Sigma^{fin}
we say that it has length
n
and define:

G(\underline{a})=\{x\in\bigsqcup_{a\in\mathbb{A}}G(a),\;g^{j}(x)\in G(a_{j}),\;j=0,1,\dots,n\}

and the function
f_{\underline{a}}:G(a_{n})\to G(\underline{a})
by:

f_{\underline{a}}=g|^{-1}_{G(a_{0})}\circ g|^{-1}_{G(a_{1})}\circ\dots\circ(g|^{-1}_{G(a_{n-1})})|_{G(a_{n})}.

Notice that
f_{(a_{i},a_{i+1})}=g|^{-1}_{G(a_{i})}
. Furthermore, we can consider the sets
G^{*}(\underline{a})
defined in the same way

G^{*}(\underline{a})=\{x\in\bigsqcup_{a\in\mathbb{A}}G^{*}(a),\;g^{j}(x)\in G^{*}(a_{j}),\;j=0,1,\dots,n\}

but using the
*
version of the pieces and consider the function
f_{\underline{a}}
to be defined in the larger set
G^{*}(a_{n})
having image equal to
G^{*}(\underline{a})
.

Now we have the following lemma.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
K
be a dynamically defined Cantor set and
G^{*}(\underline{a})
the sets defined above. There exists a constant
C>0
such that:

diam(G^{*}(\underline{a}))<C\mu^{-n}.

As a consequence of this lemma we can see that

K=\bigcap_{n\geq 0}g^{-n}\left(\bigsqcup_{a\in\mathbb{A}}G^{*}(a)\right)

since
G(\underline{a})\subset G^{*}(\underline{a})
and
\text{diam}(G^{*}(\underline{a}))\rightarrow 0
, and so the Cantor set has not been enlarged. Another consequence is that
K
is contained in the interior of the union of the pieces
G^{*}(a)
. From now on we will work with the assumption that the sets
G(a)
have non-empty interior and that they contain
K
in the interior of their union. We keep the definition
G^{*}(a)\coloneqq V_{\delta}(G(a))
as before because it will be useful in the definition of limit geometries.

The following definition will be useful in the future.

Definition 2.3
.

For every Cantor set
K
we define the homeomorphism

H:K\to\Sigma

that carries each point
x\in K
to its itinerary along the pieces
G(a)
, that is

H(x)=(a_{0},a_{1},\dots,a_{n},\dots)

if and only if
g^{i}(x)\in G(a_{i})
for all
i\geq 0
.

Our main result concerns generic Cantor sets. So now we fix the topology on the space of Cantor sets. We remind that any Cantor set we are considering is given by a map
g
that is, at least,
C^{1+\varepsilon}
for some
\varepsilon>0
.

Definition 2.4
.

(The space
\Omega^{m}_{\Sigma}
) For a fixed symbolic space
\Sigma
and real number
m>1
(we also allow m=
\infty
). The set of all
C^{m}
conformal regular Cantor sets
K
with the type
\Sigma
is defined as the set of all
C^{m}
conformal Cantor sets described as above whose set of data includes the alphabet
\mathbb{A}
and the set
B
of admissible pairs used in the construction of
\Sigma
. We denote it by
\Omega^{m}_{\Sigma}
.

The topology on
\Omega^{m}_{\Sigma}
is generated by a basis of neighbourhoods
U_{K,\delta}\subset\Omega^{m}_{\Sigma}
where
K
is any
C^{m}
Cantor set in
\Omega^{m}_{\Sigma}
and
\delta>0
. The neighborhood
U_{K,\delta}
is the set of all
C^{m}
conformal regular Cantor sets
K^{\prime}
given by
g^{\prime}:V^{\prime}\to\mathbbm{C},\,V^{\prime}\supset\bigsqcup_{a\in\mathbb{A}}G^{\prime}(a)
such that
G(a)\subset V_{\delta}(G^{\prime}(a))
,
G^{\prime}(a)\subset V_{\delta}(G(a))
(that is, the pieces are close in the Hausdorff topology) and the restrictions of
g^{\prime}
and
g
to
V\cap V^{\prime}
are
\delta
close in the
C^{m}
metric. The topology on
\Omega^{\infty}_{\Sigma}
is the one such that a sequence of
C^{\infty}
Cantor sets
K_{n}
converges to
K
if and only if the sequence converges to
K
in the topology of
\Omega^{m}_{\Sigma}
for every
m\in(1,\infty)
.

We also consider the union
\Omega_{\Sigma}:=\bigcup_{m>1}\Omega^{m}_{\Sigma}
, the topology in
\Omega_{\Sigma}
is the finest topology such that the inclusions
\Omega^{m}_{\Sigma}\subset\Omega_{\Sigma}
are continuous maps, the so called inductive limit topology. Thus, a set
U\subset\Omega_{\Sigma}
is open if and only if
U\cap\Omega^{m}_{\Sigma}
is open in
\Omega^{m}_{\Sigma}
for all
m>1
. It is not difficult to prove that an open set
U\subset\Omega_{\Sigma}
can be written as a union
U=\bigcup_{m>1}U_{m}
, where each
U_{m}
is open in
\Omega^{m}_{\Sigma}
and
U_{m}\subset U_{m^{\prime}}
if
m>m^{\prime}
.

2.3.
Limit geometries

To study the geometry of small parts of our Cantor sets, we introduce more objects. For each
\underline{a}=(a_{0},\,\dots,\,a_{n})\in\Sigma^{fin}
, denote by
K(\underline{a})
the set
K\cap G(\underline{a})
. For each
a\in\mathbb{A}
, fix a point
c_{a}\in K(a)
. We will refer to these points as base points. Define
c_{\underline{a}}\in K(\underline{a})
by

c_{\underline{a}}\coloneqq f_{\underline{a}}(c_{a_{n}}).

Additionally, given
\underline{\theta}=(\dots,\theta_{-n},\dots,\theta_{0})\in\Sigma^{-}
we write
\underline{\theta}_{n}\coloneqq(\theta_{-n},\dots,\theta_{0})
and
r_{{\underline{\theta}}_{n}}:=\text{diam}(G(\underline{{\theta}}_{n}))
.

Given
\underline{\theta}\in\Sigma^{-}
and
n\geq 1
, define
\Phi_{\underline{\theta}_{n}}
as the unique map in

Aff(\mathbbm{C})\coloneqq\{\alpha z+\beta,\ \alpha\in\mathbbm{C}^{*},\,\beta\in\mathbbm{C}\}

such that

\Phi_{\underline{\theta}_{n}}(c_{\underline{\theta}_{n}})=\left(\Phi_{\underline{\theta}_{n}}\circ f_{\underline{\theta}_{n}}\right)(c_{\theta_{0}})=0\qquad\text{and}\qquad D\left(\Phi_{\underline{\theta}_{n}}\circ f_{\underline{\theta}_{n}}\right)(c_{\theta_{0}})=Id.

The maps
\Phi_{\underline{\theta}_{n}}
act as a
normalization
of small parts of the Cantor set
K
. For that purpose, we define the maps
k^{\underline{\theta}}_{n}
by

k^{\underline{\theta}}_{n}\coloneqq\Phi_{\underline{\theta}_{n}}\circ f_{\underline{\theta}_{n}}.

Through them we have the first result that allows control over the sets
G(\underline{\theta}_{n})
.

In what follows we consider some
m>1
fixed and
K
being a Cantor set in the space
\Omega_{\Sigma}^{m}
.

Proposition 2.5
.

(Limit Geometries) For each
\underline{\theta}\in\Sigma^{-}
the sequence of
C^{m}
embeddings
k^{\underline{\theta}}_{n}:G^{*}(\theta_{0})\to\mathbbm{C}
converges in the
C^{m}
topology to a
C^{m}
embedding
k^{\underline{\theta}}:G^{*}(\theta_{0})\to\mathbbm{C}
. The convergence is uniform over all
\underline{\theta}\in\Sigma^{-}
and in a small neighbourhood of
g
in
\Omega^{m}_{\Sigma}
.

The
k^{\underline{\theta}}:G(\theta_{0})\to\mathbbm{C}
defined for any
\underline{\theta}\in\Sigma^{-}
are called the
limit geometries
of
K
.

Remark 2.6
.

Define
\Sigma^{-}_{a}=\{\underline{\theta}\in\Sigma^{-},\,\underline{\theta}_{0}=a\}
and consider in this set the topology given by the metric
d(\underline{\theta}^{1},\underline{\theta}^{2})=\text{diam}(G(\underline{\theta}^{1}\wedge\underline{\theta}^{2}))
. Likewise, for
m>1
, let
\text{Emb}_{m}(G^{*}(a),\mathbbm{C})
be the space of
C^{m}
embeddings from
G^{*}(a)
to
\mathbbm{C}
with
C^{m}
inverse equipped with the topology given by the
C^{1}
metric

d(g_{1},g_{2})=\max\{||g_{1}-g_{2}||,||Dg_{1}-Dg_{2}||\}.

For fixed
0<\varepsilon<1
and a
C^{1+\varepsilon}
Cantor set
K
, the map
k:\Sigma^{-}_{a}\to\text{Emb}_{1+\varepsilon}(G^{*}(a),\mathbbm{C}),\;\underline{\theta}\mapsto k^{\underline{\theta}}
is
\varepsilon
-Hölder, if we consider the metrics described above for both spaces. In case the Cantor set
K
is
C^{m}
, for
m\geq 2
, then there is a constant
C>0
such that
d(k^{\underline{\theta}^{1}},k^{\underline{\theta}^{2}})\leq Cd(\underline{\theta}^{1},\underline{\theta}^{2})
. The constant
C
can be chosen uniformly in a neighborhood of the Cantor set.

Since the convergence is uniform with respect to
\underline{\theta}
and in a neighborhood of
\Omega^{m}_{\Sigma}
, the limit geometries
k^{\underline{\theta}}
depend continuously in
\underline{\theta}
and the Cantor set
K
.

We also remark that the derivative
Dk^{\underline{\theta}}(x)
is conformal for all
x\in K(\theta_{0})
.

Remark 2.7
.

It is important to observe that limit geometries depend on the choice of base points. Nonetheless, different choice of base points do not change the resultant limit geometries by much, only by an affine transformation that is bounded by some constant
C
depending on
K
. Here we mean that such transformations are given by maps
A(z)=\alpha z+\beta
, where
\left\lvert\alpha\right\rvert,\left\lvert\beta\right\rvert<C
. This bound is, as before, uniform for Cantor sets
\tilde{K}
sufficiently close to
K
. See the paragraph after Corollary 3.2 of
[
6
]
.

For reasons that will become more clear in the future, from now on we assume that for each
a\in\mathbb{A}
the corresponding base point
c_{a}
is a pre-periodic point.

Before proceeding, we fix some more notation. For
\underline{\theta}\in\Sigma^{-}
and
\underline{a}\in\Sigma^{fin}
we write

\displaystyle G^{\underline{\theta}}(\underline{a})\coloneqq k^{\underline{\theta}}(G(\underline{a})),\qquad K^{\underline{\theta}}(\underline{a})\coloneqq k^{\underline{\theta}}(K(\underline{a})),\qquad c^{\underline{\theta}}_{\underline{a}}\coloneqq k^{\underline{\theta}}(c_{\underline{a}}).

Furthermore, to establish stable intersections, we are going to analyse very small parts of the Cantor sets, whose size will be controlled by a real number
\rho
. This number should be regarded as a variable that we are going to assume in various instances to be very small, as to make the various estimates we are going to find in the future to fit all together. This being said, let
c_{0}
be a sufficiently large constant.

Definition 2.8
.

For
0<\rho<1
, the set
\Sigma(\rho)
is defined as the set of words
\underline{a}\in\Sigma^{fin}
such that

c_{0}^{-1}\rho\leq\text{diam}(G(\underline{a}))\leq c_{0}\rho.

We say that the set
G(\underline{a})
has an
approximate size

\rho
.

Using standard techniques (see
[
2
]
and
[
8
]
), one can prove that there is a constant
C
depending only in the Cantor set and the parameter
c_{0}
such that

(1)

C^{-1}\rho^{-d}\leq\#\Sigma(\rho)\leq C\rho^{-d},

where
d=HD(K)
is the Hausdorff dimension of
K
.

Remark 2.9
.

Notice that, because the set of limit geometries represent a compact subset of
\cup_{a\in\mathbb{A}}{Emb_{m}(G(a))}
, every piece of
approximate size

\rho
also contains the ball
B(c_{\underline{a}},(c_{0}^{\prime})^{-1}\rho)
for some
c_{0}^{\prime}>0
depending only on
K
. The result remains valid for perturbations
\tilde{K}
sufficiently close to
K
. Even more, by maybe enlarging
c_{0}^{\prime}
a little bit, because of Corollaries 3.3 and 3.4 of
[
6
]
, it follows that for any
\underline{a}=(a_{0},\,\dots,\,a_{n})\in\Sigma^{fin}

(2)

{(c^{\prime}_{0})}^{-1}\leq\frac{\left\lvert Df_{\underline{a}}(c_{a_{n}})\right\rvert}{\text{diam}(G(\underline{a}))}\leq c^{\prime}_{0}.

This allows us to control the approximate size of the sets
G(\underline{a})
through the derivative of the map
f_{\underline{a}}
at
c_{a_{n}}
.

2.4.
Recurrent compact criterion for stable intersections

Our next objects are called
configurations
. They are a way of moving a Cantor set in the plane without changing its internal structure.

Definition 2.10
.

A
C^{m}
-
configuration
of a piece
G(a)
of a Cantor set is a
C^{m}
,
m>1
, diffeomorphism

h:G(a)\to U\subset\mathbbm{C}.

The space of all
C^{m}
configurations of a piece
G(a)
is denoted by
\mathcal{P}^{m}(a)
and we equip it with the
C^{m}
topology. The space of all configurations is denoted by
\mathcal{P}(a)=\cup_{m>1}\mathcal{P}^{m}(a)
and we equip it with the inductive limit topology. This is,
U\subset\mathcal{P}(a)
is open if and only
U\cap\mathcal{P}^{m}(a)
is open in the topology of
\mathcal{P}^{m}(a)
, for all
m>1
.

If
h
is an affine map, we call it an
affine configuration
. Observe that a limit geometry is a configuration of a piece. Configurations of the type
A\circ k^{\underline{\theta}}
, where
A\in Aff(\mathbbm{C})
and
\underline{\theta}\in\Sigma^{-}
, are of great importance to our work and so are called
affine configurations of limit geometries
.

The renormalization operators represent a way of looking into smaller parts of the Cantor set.

Definition 2.11
.

Let
K
and
K^{\prime}
be two Cantor sets. Choose any pair of words
\underline{a}=(a_{0},\,a_{1},\,\dots,\,a_{n})\in\Sigma^{fin}
and
\underline{a}^{\prime}=(a^{\prime}_{0},\,a^{\prime}_{1},\,\dots,\,a^{\prime}_{m})\in{\Sigma^{\prime}}^{fin}
. Then, the renormalization operator
T_{\underline{a}}T^{\prime}_{\underline{a}^{\prime}}
acts on any pair of configurations
h:G(a_{0})\to\mathbbm{C}
and
h^{\prime}:G(a^{\prime}_{0})\to\mathbbm{C}
by

T_{\underline{a}}T^{\prime}_{\underline{a}^{\prime}}(h,h^{\prime})\coloneqq(h\circ f_{\underline{a}},h^{\prime}\circ f_{\underline{a}^{\prime}}).

The notation above clearly indicates that we can consider the operators
T_{\underline{a}}
and
T^{\prime}_{\underline{a^{\prime}}}
as separate, each acting on configurations of
K
and
K^{\prime}
respectively. In a very similar way to the proposition
2.5
, the one defining limit geometries, one can show (see Lemma 3.11 of
[
6
]
) that the set of affine configurations of limit geometries form an attractor in the space of configurations under the action of renormalizations. Even more, see lemma 3.8 of
[
6
]
, the renormalization operators act in a very simple manner over limit geometries:

Lemma 2.12
.

For any
\underline{\theta}\in\Sigma^{-}
and
\underline{a}\in\Sigma^{fin}
,
\underline{a}=(a_{0},...,a_{m})
, there is an affine transformation
F^{\underline{\theta}}_{\underline{a}}\in Aff(\mathbbm{C})
such that

k^{\underline{\theta}}\circ f_{\underline{a}}=F^{\underline{\theta}}_{\underline{a}}\circ k^{\underline{\theta}\underline{a}}.

Moreover, this transformation can be calculated by

\displaystyle DF^{\underline{\theta}}_{\underline{a}}

\displaystyle=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\left(Df_{\underline{\theta}_{n}}(c_{\theta_{0}})\right)^{-1}\cdot Df_{(\underline{\theta}\underline{a})_{n+m}}(c_{a_{m}})\quad\text{and}

\displaystyle F^{\underline{\theta}}_{\underline{a}}(0)

\displaystyle=c^{\underline{\theta}}_{\underline{a}}=k^{\underline{\theta}}(c_{\underline{a}}).

Now we properly establish the notion of
stable intersection
between Cantor sets. Given two Cantor sets
K,\,K^{\prime}
and any pair of configurations
(h_{a},h^{\prime}_{a^{\prime}})\in\mathcal{P}(a)\times\mathcal{P}^{\prime}(a^{\prime})
we say that it is:

•

linked
whenever
h_{a}(G(a))\cap h^{\prime}_{a^{\prime}}(G(a^{\prime}))\neq\emptyset
.

•

intersecting
whenever
h_{a}(K(a))\cap h^{\prime}_{a^{\prime}}(K^{\prime}(a^{\prime}))\neq\emptyset
.

•

has stable intersections
whenever
\tilde{h}_{a}(\tilde{K}(a))\cap\tilde{h^{\prime}}_{a^{\prime}}(\tilde{K^{\prime}}(a^{\prime}))\neq\emptyset
for any pairs of Cantor sets
(\tilde{K},\tilde{K^{\prime}})\in\Omega_{\Sigma}\times\Omega_{\Sigma^{\prime}}
in a small neighbourhood of
(K,K^{\prime})
and any configuration pair
(\tilde{h}_{a},\tilde{h^{\prime}}_{a^{\prime}})
that is sufficiently close to
(h_{a},h^{\prime}_{a^{\prime}})
in the topology of
\mathcal{P}(a)\times\mathcal{P}^{\prime}(a^{\prime})
.

The set
\mathcal{I}_{s}(K,K^{\prime})
in the statement of theorem
1.1
represents the set of all
\lambda\in\mathbbm{C}
such that
(\tau_{\lambda},Id)
is a pair of configurations having stable intersections in the sense just described, where
\tau_{z}
is the translation by
z
on
\mathbbm{C}
.

The main theorem in the introduction is a consequence of theorem
2.23
, because it guarantees stable intersections for affine configurations of Cantor sets. In turn, theorem
2.23
is a consequence of theorem
2.22
. The statement of this theorem requires that we recall some more concepts.
First, notice that the space of affine configurations of limit geometries of a Cantor set can be seen as the image of the continuous association

\displaystyle I:\mathcal{A}\coloneqq Aff(\mathbbm{C})\times\Sigma^{-}

\displaystyle\to\mathcal{P}

\displaystyle(A,\underline{\theta})

\displaystyle\mapsto A\circ k^{\underline{\theta}}.

Definition 2.13
.

The space of relative affine configurations of limit geometries will be denoted by
\mathcal{C}
. It is the quotient of
\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{A^{\prime}}
by the action of the affine group by composition on the left, that is,
\left((A,\underline{\theta}),(A^{\prime},\underline{\theta}^{\prime})\right)\mapsto\left((B\circ A,\underline{\theta}),(B\circ A^{\prime},\underline{\theta}^{\prime})\right)
, where
B
ranges in
Aff(\mathbbm{C})
.

The concepts of
linking
,
intersection
and
stable intersection
were well defined for pairs of affine configurations of limit geometries, and since they are invariant by the action of
Aff(\mathbbm{C})
, they are also defined for relative configurations in
\mathcal{C}
.

Also, since the renormalization operator acts by composition on the right on
(A,\underline{\theta})
, its action commutes with the multiplication on the left by affine transformations and so it can be naturally defined on
\mathcal{C}
. This space can be identified with
\Sigma^{-}\times{\Sigma^{\prime}}^{-}\times Aff(\mathbbm{C})
by the identification
[(A,\underline{\theta}),(A^{\prime},\underline{\theta}^{\prime})]\equiv(\underline{\theta},\underline{\theta}^{\prime},{A^{\prime}}^{-1}\circ A)
and, in this manner, the topology on
\mathcal{C}
is the product topology on
\Sigma^{-}\times{\Sigma^{\prime}}^{-}\times Aff(\mathbbm{C})
. The action of the renormalization operator on a relative configuration can be described by

T_{\underline{a}}T^{\prime}_{\underline{a}^{\prime}}(\underline{\theta},\underline{\theta}^{\prime},A)=(\underline{\theta}\underline{a},\underline{\theta}^{\prime}\underline{a}^{\prime},\left(F^{\underline{\theta}^{\prime}}_{\underline{a}^{\prime}}\right)^{-1}\circ A\circ F^{\underline{\theta}}_{\underline{a}}),

and if
A=sz+t
, then

(3)

\left(F^{\underline{\theta}^{\prime}}_{\underline{a}^{\prime}}\right)^{-1}\circ A\circ F^{\underline{\theta}}_{\underline{a}}(z)=\frac{DF^{\underline{\theta}}_{\underline{a}}}{DF^{\underline{\theta}^{\prime}}_{\underline{a}^{\prime}}}\,sz+\frac{1}{DF^{\underline{\theta}^{\prime}}_{\underline{a}^{\prime}}}\left(sc^{\underline{\theta}}_{\underline{a}}+t-c^{{\underline{\theta}}^{\prime}}_{\underline{a}^{\prime}}\right).

It is more convenient to see the space
\mathcal{C}
through one more identification:

\displaystyle\Sigma^{-}\times{\Sigma^{\prime}}^{-}\times Aff(\mathbbm{C})

\displaystyle\equiv\Sigma^{-}\times{\Sigma^{\prime}}^{-}\times\mathbbm{C}^{*}\times\mathbbm{C}

\displaystyle(\underline{\theta},\underline{\theta}^{\prime},A)

\displaystyle\equiv(\underline{\theta},\underline{\theta}^{\prime},s,t),

where
A(z)=sz+t
. We will call
s
the
scale
part of the relative configuration and
t
the
translation
part. The equation (
3
) provides to us a formula for the renormalization under this identification if we analyse the scale and translation parts separately:

s\mapsto\frac{DF^{\underline{\theta}}_{\underline{a}}}{DF^{\underline{\theta}^{\prime}}_{\underline{a}^{\prime}}}\,s\qquad\text{and}\qquad t\mapsto\frac{1}{DF^{\underline{\theta}^{\prime}}_{\underline{a}^{\prime}}}\left(sc^{\underline{\theta}}_{\underline{a}}+t-c^{{\underline{\theta}}^{\prime}}_{\underline{a}^{\prime}}\right).

The space of relative scales is given by
\mathcal{S}=\Sigma^{-}\times{\Sigma^{\prime}}^{-}\times J
, where
J=\mathbbm{C}^{*}
. We identify
J
with
\mathbbm{R}\times\mathbbm{T}
through the map
(t,v)\to e^{t+iv}
. It acts on
\mathbbm{C}
by complex multiplication. The space
\mathcal{C}
of relative configurations projects to
\mathcal{S}
by the map

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}

\displaystyle\to\mathcal{S}

\displaystyle[(\underline{\theta},A),(\underline{\theta}^{\prime},A^{\prime})]

\displaystyle\to(\underline{\theta},\underline{\theta}^{\prime},DA/DA^{\prime}),

where
DA
means derivative of the affine map
A
(which is an element in
J
). We trivialize
\mathcal{C}\to\mathcal{S}
in the following way: we map
[(\underline{\theta},A),(\underline{\theta}^{\prime},A^{\prime})]\in\mathcal{C}
to
(\underline{\theta},\underline{\theta}^{\prime},s,\lambda)
such that
s=DA/DA^{\prime}
and
\lambda=(A^{\prime})^{-1}\circ A(0)
. In this sense we can think of
\mathcal{C}
as
\mathcal{S}\times\mathbbm{C}
. The renormalization operators act on the space of relative scales by

T_{\underline{a}}T^{\prime}_{\underline{a}^{\prime}}(\underline{\theta},\underline{\theta}^{\prime},s)=\left(\underline{\theta}\underline{a},\underline{\theta}^{\prime}\underline{a}^{\prime},s\cdot(DF^{\underline{\theta}}_{\underline{a}}/DF^{\underline{\theta}^{\prime}}_{\underline{a}^{\prime}})\right).

Most of the time we will work with scales which are bounded away from zero and infinity, for this purpose we introduce the notation

J_{R}=\{s\in J:\,e^{-R}\leq|s|\leq e^{R}\},

where
R
is a positive real number.

The object which we present in the following definition will play a central role in the proof of our main theorems. It is a useful tool to get stable intersection between pairs of Cantor sets.

Definition 2.14

(Recurrent compact)
.

Let
K
and
K^{\prime}
be a pair of Cantor sets. Let
\mathcal{L}
be a compact set in
\mathcal{C}
. We say that
\mathcal{L}
is
recurrent
(for the pair
(K,K^{\prime})
) if for any relative affine configuration of limit geometries
v\in\mathcal{L}
, there are finite words
\underline{a}
,
\underline{a}^{\prime}
such that
u=T_{\underline{a}}T^{\prime}_{\underline{a^{\prime}}}(v)
satisfies
u\in\text{int }\mathcal{L}
, where the
T_{\underline{a}}T^{\prime}_{\underline{a^{\prime}}}
are renormalization operators associated to the pair of Cantor sets
K
and
K^{\prime}
.

If such a renormalization can be done using words
\underline{a}
and
\underline{a}^{\prime}
such that their total size combined is equal to one, we say that such a set is
immediately recurrent
.

Theorem B
of
[
6
]
states that if
u
belongs to a recurrent compact set associated to a pair of Cantor sets
K
and
K^{\prime}
, then it represents pairs of affine configurations of limit geometries of these Cantor sets that have stable intersections. For the convenience of the reader, we copy its statement below.

Theorem
.

The following properties are true:

(1)

Every recurrent compact set is contained in an immediately recurrent compact set.

(2)

Given a recurrent compact set
\mathcal{L}
(resp. immediately recurrent) for
g
,
g^{\prime}
, for any
\tilde{g}
,
\tilde{g}^{\prime}
in a small neighbourhood of
(g,g^{\prime})\in\Omega_{\Sigma}\times\Omega_{{\Sigma}^{\prime}}
we can choose base points
\tilde{c}_{a}\in\tilde{G}(a)\cap\tilde{K}
and
\tilde{c}_{a^{\prime}}\in\tilde{G}(a^{\prime})\cap\tilde{K}^{\prime}
respectively close to the pre-fixed
c_{a}
and
c_{a^{\prime}}
, for all
a\in\mathbb{A}
and
a^{\prime}\in\mathbb{A^{\prime}}
, in a manner that
\mathcal{L}
is also a recurrent compact set for
\tilde{g}
and
\tilde{g}^{\prime}
.

(3)

Any relative configuration contained in a recurrent compact set has stable intersections.

Remark 2.15
.

For each pair of maps
(\tilde{g},\tilde{g}^{\prime})
in the small neighbourhood of
(g,g^{\prime})
in the theorem above, let
\tilde{H}
and
\tilde{H}^{\prime}
be the corresponding homeomorphisms defined in
2.3
. The base points
\tilde{c}_{a}\in\tilde{G}(a)\cap\tilde{K}
and
\tilde{c}_{a^{\prime}}\in\tilde{G}(a^{\prime})\cap\tilde{K}^{\prime}
in the theorem above are chosen so that
\tilde{H}(\tilde{c}_{a})=H(c_{a})
and
\tilde{H}^{\prime}(\tilde{c}_{a^{\prime}})=H^{\prime}(c_{a^{\prime}})
for all
a\in\mathbb{A}
and all
a^{\prime}\in\mathbb{A}^{\prime}
, meaning that their itineraries under the action of
\tilde{g}
and
\tilde{g}^{\prime}
are the same for all pairs of maps in this neighbourhood. In subsequent contexts, the base points will be chosen in the same way.

2.5.
Perturbation of Conformal Cantor Sets

Let
K
be a conformal Cantor set defined by a
C^{m}
map
g
. We show that if
K
is non-essentially real then arbitrarily close to
K
, in the
C^{[m]}
topology, there is a
C^{\infty}
conformal Cantor set
\tilde{K}
defined by a map
\tilde{g}
that is holomorphic on a small open neighbourhood of
\tilde{K}
. This is an important property that will allow us to perturb more freely the conformal Cantor sets and adapt the random perturbation argument from
[
1
]
to our context.

We begin with the following lemma:

Lemma 2.16
.

Let
K
be a
C^{m}
conformal Cantor set given by
g
. For
x\in K
consider the set

K^{dir}_{x}:=\bigcap_{\delta>0}\overline{\left\{\frac{y-x}{|y-x|}:y\in B(x,\delta)\cap(K\setminus\{x\})\right\}}.

Assume that, for all
x\in K
,
K^{dir}_{x}
has two linearly independent vectors (over
\mathbbm{R}
). Then, for all
1\leq l\leq[m]
and
x\in K
the
l
-linear map
D^{l}_{x}g:\mathbbm{R}^{2}\times...\times\mathbbm{R}^{2}\to\mathbbm{R}^{2}
is conformal, i.e. there is a complex number
c^{l}_{x}
such that

D^{l}_{x}g(z_{1},...,z_{l})=c^{l}_{x}\cdot z_{1}\cdot z_{2}\cdot\dots\cdot z_{l}.

The operation
\cdot
in the right hand side of the last equality corresponds to complex multiplication.

Proof.

Notice that the case
l=1
is just the definition of conformality for the Cantor set. Now we proceed by induction, assume the result for
l-1
. Let
w\in K^{dir}_{x}
, then there are sequences
t_{n}\to 0
and
w_{n}\to w
such that
x+t_{n}w_{n}\in K
. Hence

\displaystyle D^{l}_{x}g(w,z_{1}...,z_{l-1})

\displaystyle=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{D^{l-1}_{x+t_{n}w_{n}}g(z_{1},...,z_{l-1})-D^{l-1}_{x}g(z_{1},...,z_{l-1})}{t_{n}}

\displaystyle=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{c^{l-1}_{x+t_{n}w_{n}}\cdot z_{1}\cdots z_{l-1}-c^{l-1}_{x}\cdot z_{1}\cdots z_{l-1}}{t_{n}}

\displaystyle=\left(\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{c^{l-1}_{x+t_{n}w_{n}}-c^{l-1}_{x}}{t_{n}}\right)\cdot z_{1}\cdots z_{l-1}.

This shows that the limit
\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{c^{l-1}_{x+t_{n}w_{n}}-c^{l-1}_{x}}{t_{n}}
exists, denote it by
c^{l}_{x}(w)
. Moreover

D^{l}_{x}g(w,z_{1}...,z_{l-1})=c^{l}_{x}(w)\cdot z_{1}\cdots z_{l-1}.

If we take another vector
\tilde{w}\in K^{dir}_{x}
, and using the symmetry of the operator
D^{l}_{x}g
, we would have

c^{l}_{x}(w)\tilde{w}=D^{l}_{x}g(w,\tilde{w},1,...,1)=D^{l}_{x}g(\tilde{w},w,1,...,1)=c^{l}_{x}(\tilde{w})w.

This shows that
\frac{c^{l}_{x}(w)}{w}
does not depend on
w
, denote it by
c^{l}_{x}
. Since we can choose
w,\tilde{w}
generating
\mathbbm{R}^{2}
, we conclude that

D^{l}_{x}g(z_{1}...,z_{l})=c^{l}_{x}\cdot z_{1}\cdots z_{l}.

∎

To use this lemma we need to consider Cantor sets that are indeed two dimensional. This concept is precised by the following definition.

Definition 2.17
.

We will say that a Cantor set
K
is
essentially real
if there exists
\underline{\theta}\in\Sigma^{-}
such that the limit Cantor set
K^{\underline{\theta}}(\theta_{0})
is contained in a straight line. Otherwise, we say it is
non-essentially real
.

It is not difficult to prove that
K
is essentially real if and only if for every
\underline{\theta}\in\Sigma^{-}
the limit Cantor set
K^{\underline{\theta}}(\theta_{0})
is contained in a straight line. Moreover, one can prove that
K
being essentially real is equivalent to
K
being contained in a
C^{1}
one dimensional manifold embedded on the plane.

Lemma 1.4.1 from
[
8
]
can be adapted to our context and it can be used to prove that every point
x
belonging to a non-essentially real Cantor set
K
verifies that
K^{dir}_{x}
has two linearly independent vectors (over
\mathbbm{R}
). We now show that being non-essentially real is an open property.

Lemma 2.18
.

Let
K
be a
C^{m}
non-essentially real conformal Cantor set. Every conformal Cantor set, close enough to
K
in the
C^{m}
topology, is also non-essentially real.

Proof.

If the lemma does not hold, we would have a sequence
K_{n}
of conformal Cantor sets converging to
K
and such that every
K_{n}
is essentially real. Let
\underline{\theta}\in\Sigma^{-}
, denote by
k^{\underline{\theta},n}
the limit geometry associated to
\underline{\theta}
and the Cantor set
K_{n}
. Since all
K_{n}
are essentially real then, for all
n
,
K^{\underline{\theta},n}(\theta_{0})=k^{\underline{\theta},n}(K_{n})
is contained in a line passing through the origin. Taking a subsequence we can assume that, as
n
goes to infinity,
K^{\underline{\theta},n}(\theta_{0})
converges to a a set contained in a line passing through the origin. Using the fact that limit geometries depend continuously on the Cantor set, we conclude that
K^{\underline{\theta}}(\theta_{0})
is contained in a line and therefore
K
is essentially real, contradicting the hypothesis in the lemma.
∎

Lemma 2.19
.

Let
(K,g)
be a
C^{m}
non-essentially real conformal Cantor set. Arbitrarily close to
K
, in the
C^{[m]}
topology, we can construct a
C^{\infty}
conformal Cantor set
(\tilde{K},\tilde{g})
such that
\tilde{g}
is holomorphic on a neighbourhood of
\tilde{K}
.

Proof.

Since
K
is non-essentially real then the
l
-derivative
D^{l}_{x}g
, at a point
x
in the Cantor set, is determined by a complex number, which we denote by
g^{(l)}(x)
, i.e.

D^{l}_{x}g(z_{1}...,z_{l})=g^{(l)}(x)\cdot z_{1}\cdots z_{l}.

In this situation, the Taylor approximation of
g
at the point
x
is

\displaystyle g(x+z)

\displaystyle=g(x)+\sum_{j=1}^{[m]}\dfrac{1}{j!}\left(D^{j}_{x}g\right)(z,\dots,z)\;+

\displaystyle+\int_{[0,1]^{[m]}}t^{[m]-1}_{1}t^{[m]-2}_{2}\cdots t_{[m]-1}\left(\left[D^{[m]}_{x+t_{1}t_{2}\dots t_{[m]}z}-D^{[m]}_{x}\right]g\right)(z,\dots,z)dt_{1}\dots dt_{[m]}

\displaystyle=g(x)+\sum_{j=1}^{[m]}\dfrac{1}{j!}g^{(j)}(x)z^{j}\;+

\displaystyle+\int_{[0,1]^{[m]}}t^{[m]-1}_{1}t^{[m]-2}_{2}\cdots t_{[m]-1}\left(\left[D^{[m]}_{x+t_{1}t_{2}\dots t_{[m]}z}-D^{[m]}_{x}\right]g\right)(z,\dots,z)dt_{1}\dots dt_{[m]}.

Hence,
g
is approximated (close to
x
) by a complex polynomial, which is an holomorphic function. Now, to globally aproximate
g
by a function
\tilde{g}
which is holomorphic in a neighborhood of its Cantor set
\tilde{K}
, we are going to take many of the previous polynomial approximations and glue them together.

Choose any real number
\rho
larger than zero. Consider
\Lambda\subset\Sigma(\rho)
such that
\{G(\underline{a})\cap K\}_{\underline{a}\in\Lambda}
is a partition of
K
. For each
\underline{a}\in\Lambda
we choose a point
x_{\underline{a}}\in G(\underline{a})\cap K
and define the polynomial

p_{\underline{a}}(z)=\sum_{j=0}^{[m]}\dfrac{1}{j!}g^{(j)}(x_{\underline{a}})(z-x_{\underline{a}})^{j}.

We can also consider
C^{\infty}
functions
\phi_{\underline{a}}:\mathbbm{C}\to\mathbbm{R}
,
\underline{a}\in\Lambda
, with the following properties:

•

\phi_{\underline{a}}(z)=1
for all
z\in G(\underline{a})
.

•

supp(\phi_{\underline{a}})\subset V_{\tilde{c}\rho}(G(\underline{a}))
, for a constant
\tilde{c}
independent of
\rho
.

•

supp(\phi_{\underline{a}})\cap supp(\phi_{\underline{b}})=\emptyset
, for all
\underline{a}\neq\underline{b}
.

•

\|D^{j}\phi_{\underline{a}}\|\leq\tilde{C}\rho^{-j}
, for a constant
\tilde{C}
independent of
\rho
.

Indeed, to be able to construct these bump functions, we only need to show that given
\underline{a}^{0}\neq\underline{a}^{1}
both in
\Lambda\subset\Sigma(\rho)
, the distance between the pieces
G(\underline{a}^{0})
and
G(\underline{a}^{1})
is at least
3\tilde{c}\rho
for some constant
\tilde{c}>0
independent of
\rho
. For that, we can suppose that
\underline{a}^{0}=\underline{a}a_{0}
and
\underline{a}^{1}=\underline{a}a_{1}
for some
a_{0}\neq a_{1}\in\mathbb{A}
, since this would be the worst scenario. If
\underline{\theta}\in\Sigma^{-}
ends with
\underline{a}
, the distance between these sets is comparable to

\text{diam}\left(G(\underline{a})\right)\cdot d\left(k^{\underline{\theta}}(G(a_{0})),k^{\underline{\theta}}(G(a_{1}))\right).

Hence the existence of
\tilde{c}
follows from the compactness of the space of limit geometries.

Now, let
\hat{g}
be
C^{\infty}
and very close, in the
C^{[m]}
topology, to
g
. Define
\tilde{g}
, with the same domain as
g
, by

\tilde{g}(z)=\sum_{\underline{a}\in\Lambda}\phi_{\underline{a}}(z)p_{\underline{a}}(z)+\left(1-\sum_{\underline{a}\in\Lambda}\phi_{\underline{a}}(z)\right)\hat{g}(z).

Notice that

\hat{g}-\tilde{g}=\sum_{\underline{a}\in\Lambda}\phi_{\underline{a}}\cdot(\hat{g}-p_{\underline{a}}).

Therefore, the
C^{[m]}
norm of
\hat{g}-\tilde{g}
will be small provided

\|D^{j}\phi_{\underline{a}}\|\cdot\|D^{k-j}(\hat{g}-p_{\underline{a}})|_{V_{\tilde{c}\rho}(G(\underline{a}))}\|\leq\|D^{j}\phi_{\underline{a}}\|\cdot\left[\|D^{k-j}(g-p_{\underline{a}})|_{V_{\tilde{c}\rho}(G(\underline{a}))}\|+\|D^{k-j}(g-\hat{g})|_{V_{\tilde{c}\rho}(G(\underline{a}))}\|\right]

is small, for all
0\leq k\leq[m]
,
0\leq j\leq k
(remember that support of
\phi_{\underline{a}}
is contained in
V_{\tilde{c}\rho}(G(\underline{a}))
). We already know that
\|D^{j}\phi_{\underline{a}}\|\leq\tilde{C}\rho^{-j}
. On the other hand, Taylor approximation implies that

\lim_{\rho\to 0}\sup_{\underline{a}\in\Lambda}\frac{\|D^{k-j}(g-p_{\underline{a}})|_{V_{\tilde{c}\rho}(G(\underline{a}))}\|}{\rho^{j}}=0.

We conclude that taking
\rho
small enough and
\hat{g}
close enough to
g
, we get
\tilde{g}
as
C^{[m]}
close to
g
as we want. Notice that thanks to the way in which we defined
\tilde{g}
, it is
C^{\infty}
and by lemma
2.18
we can suppose it is non-essentially real. Moreover, in the set
\sqcup_{\underline{a}\in\Lambda}G(\underline{a})
the function
\tilde{g}
is holomorpic.
We can also guarantee that
\tilde{g}
verifies the hypothesis necessary to define a dynamically defined Cantor set (with the same sets
G(a)
,
a\in\mathbb{A}
), we just need to take
\tilde{g}

C^{1}
-close enough to
g
. Even more, the Cantor set
\tilde{K}
, generated by
\tilde{g}
, is contained in
\sqcup_{\underline{a}\in\Lambda}G(\underline{a})
.
∎

To prove our main theorems we will use the scale recurrence lemma (see subsection
2.7
). To use this lemma we need that our Cantor sets are non-essentially affine. A
C^{m}
Cantor set
K
, with
m\geq 2
, is said to be
non-essentially affine
when there is a pair of limit geometries
\underline{\theta}^{0}
and
\underline{\theta}^{1}
in
\Sigma^{-}
such that
\theta^{0}_{0}=\theta^{1}_{0}
and a point
x_{0}\in K^{\underline{\theta}^{0}}(\theta_{0})
such that

D^{2}\left[k^{\underline{\theta}^{1}}\circ(k^{\underline{\theta}^{0}})^{-1}\right](x_{0})\neq 0.

The following lemma allow us to perturb and get a non-essentially affine Cantor set.

Lemma 2.20
.

Let
K
be a
C^{m}
non-essentially real conformal Cantor set. Arbitrarly close to
K
, in the
C^{[m]}
topology, there is a
C^{\infty}
Cantor set
\tilde{K}
which is non-essentially real, non-essentially affine and such that its expanding function
\tilde{g}
is holomorphic in a neighborhood of
\tilde{K}
.

Proof.

Let
(\hat{K},\hat{g})
be the perturbed Cantor set from lemma
2.19
. If
\hat{K}
is non-essentially affine we are done. Otherwise, choose a piece
G(a)
,
a\in\mathbb{A}
, and let
c_{a}
be the corresponding base point. As previously mentioned, it is pre-periodic.

Claim
.

If
\rho>0
is sufficiently small, we can chose
\underline{a}\in\Sigma(\rho)
ending with
a
so that no word in
\Sigma(\rho^{1/3})
appears more than once in
\underline{a}
. Given any word
\underline{a}
with this property, if
\rho>0
is sufficiently small, there is
\underline{\theta}^{0}\in\Sigma^{-}
ending with
\underline{a}
and such that
\underline{a}
does not appear elsewhere in
\underline{\theta}^{0}
. Furthermore, there is
\underline{\theta}^{1}\in\Sigma^{-}
ending with
a
such that no subword of
\underline{a}
belonging to
\Sigma(\rho^{1/3})
appears in it.

Proof.

Since the shift is mixing over
\Sigma
, there must be at least two sequences of distinct lengths (both larger than 1)
\underline{a}^{1},\underline{a}^{2}\in\Sigma^{fin}
such that both end and begin with
a
and no other letter in these words is
a
. Now construct
\underline{a}
as

\underline{a}=\underbrace{\underline{a}^{1}\,\underline{a}^{1}\,\dots\,\underline{a}^{1}}_{N_{1}\text{ times}}\underbrace{\underline{a}^{1}\,\underline{a}^{2}\,\dots\,\underline{a}^{1}\underline{a}^{2}}_{N_{2}\text{ times}}\underbrace{\underline{a}^{1}\,\underline{a}^{2}\underline{a}^{2}\,\dots\,\underline{a}^{1}\underline{a}^{2}\underline{a}^{2}}_{N_{3}\text{ times}}\underbrace{\underline{a}^{2}\,\dots\underline{a}^{2}\underline{a}^{2}}_{N_{4}\text{ times}}.

If
\rho
is sufficiently small, a suitable choice of
N_{1}
,
N_{2}
,
N_{3}
and
N_{4}
can be done so that no subword in
\Sigma(\rho^{1/3})
appears more than once. This can be seen by analysing the behaviour of the distance between two consecutive letters
a
in any two subwords of
\underline{a}
.

Now, choose a subword
\underline{a}^{\prime}\in\Sigma(\rho^{1/3})
such that
\underline{a}
begins with
\underline{a}^{\prime}
. If
\underline{a}^{\prime}
never appears in
\underline{\theta}^{\prime}\in\Sigma^{-}
, then we can make
\underline{\theta}^{0}=\underline{\theta}^{\prime}\underline{a}
. Indeed, for
\underline{a}
to appear more than once, the word
\underline{a}/\underline{a}^{\prime}
must be contained in
\underline{a}
but in another position. This implies that a subword in
\Sigma(\rho^{1/3})
, corresponding to the beginning of
\underline{a}/\underline{a}^{\prime}
for example, appears more than once in
\underline{a}
. But this does not happen.

Now, suppose we are given a word
\underline{b}\in\Sigma(\rho)
. We want to prove there is some
\underline{\theta}\in\Sigma^{-}
such that
\underline{b}
never appears in
\underline{\theta}
. Choose a beginning
\underline{b}_{1}\in\Sigma({\rho}^{1/3})
of
\underline{b}
and an ending
\underline{b}_{2}\in\Sigma({\rho}^{1/3})
. Let
\underline{b}^{\prime}\in\Sigma({\rho}^{2})
be such that
\underline{b}_{1}
and
\underline{b}_{2}
never appear in it and also suppose that the first letter of
\underline{b}^{\prime}
is the same as its last letter. Then, similarly to the analysis before, we can make
\underline{\theta}=\dots\underline{b}^{\prime}\dots\underline{b}^{\prime}\in\Sigma^{-}
.

The existence of
\underline{b}^{\prime}
comes from a counting argument, in which we show that the words in which
\underline{b}_{1}
or
\underline{b}_{2}
appear do not account for all possible words
\underline{b}^{\prime}\in\Sigma(\rho^{2})
. Remember that if
d
is the Hausdorff dimension of
K
, then
\#\Sigma(\rho)\approx\rho^{-d}
.

The number of words in
\Sigma(\rho^{2})
ending with
\underline{b}_{1}
is
\lesssim\rho^{-5d/3}
. More than that, if we fix a starting position for the appearance of
\underline{b}_{1}
, such as it beginning in the
1000^{th}
letter of
\underline{b}^{\prime}
(remember
\rho
is very small), then the same estimate remains true. Notice however that the number of letters of
\underline{b}^{\prime}
is
\lesssim\log{\rho^{-1}}
, and so the number of words in
\Sigma(\rho)
that fail our requirements is
\lesssim\rho^{-5d/3}\log{\rho^{-1}}
, thus, for
\rho
small enough, there must be
\underline{b^{\prime}}\in\Sigma(\rho^{2})
that satisfies our requirements.

To construct
\underline{\theta}^{1}
, we can use the same argument, all we need to observe is that the number of subwords of
\underline{a}
in
\Sigma(\rho^{1/3})
is also
\lesssim\log{\rho}
.

∎

By maybe shrinking
\rho
even further, we can assume that the periodic part of the itinerary of
c_{a}
is a word very small when compared to
\underline{a}
. The combinatorial conditions above imply that
f_{\underline{\theta}^{0}_{n}}(c_{a})
belongs to
G(\underline{a})
only when
\underline{\theta}^{0}_{n}=\underline{a}
. Besides,
f_{\underline{\theta}^{1}_{n}}(c_{a})
never belongs to this set.

Let
\phi:G(\underline{a})\to\mathbbm{C}
be an holomorphic map
C^{[m]}
close to the identity, and suppose it fixes the point
c_{\underline{a}}
and has derivative equal to the identity at this point. Similar to the construction on lemma
2.19
, define a new Cantor set given by an expanding map
\tilde{g}
that is equal to
\hat{g}
outside a small neighborhood of
G(\underline{a})
and equal to
\hat{g}\circ\phi
in
G(\underline{a})
. Note that the perturbed base point
\tilde{c}_{a}
is equal to
c_{a}
, thanks to the pre-periodicity of
c_{a}
and the combinatorial properties of
\underline{a}
. Moreover, the limit geometry corresponding to
\underline{\theta}^{1}
stays the same close to
c_{a}
, that is, we can choose a neighbourhood
V=G(\underline{b})
of the base point
\tilde{c}_{a}=c_{a}
, with
\underline{b}\in\Sigma^{fin}
sufficiently large, such that
\tilde{k}^{\underline{\theta}^{1}}|_{V}=\hat{k}^{\underline{\theta}^{1}}|_{V}
. On the other hand, for
\underline{\theta}^{0}
,

\tilde{k}^{\underline{\theta}^{0}}|_{V}=\hat{k}^{\underline{\theta}^{0}}|_{V}\circ f_{\underline{a}}^{-1}\circ\phi^{-1}\circ f_{\underline{a}},

since the affine reescalings
\Phi_{\underline{\theta}_{n}^{0}}
stay the same.
Notice that the map
\tilde{g}
is still holomorphic in a neighbourhood of
\tilde{K}
and, because of lemma
2.18
, it is non-essentially real if
\phi
is
C^{1}
sufficiently close to the identity. However, we can still choose
\phi
so that

D^{2}\left(f_{\underline{a}}^{-1}\circ\phi^{-1}\circ f_{\underline{a}}\right)(c_{a})\neq 0.

Hence
D^{2}\tilde{k}^{\underline{\theta}^{0}}(c_{a})\neq D^{2}\hat{k}^{\underline{\theta}^{0}}(c_{a})
. This implies that

D^{2}\left[\tilde{k}^{\underline{\theta}^{1}}\circ(\tilde{k}^{\underline{\theta}^{0}})^{-1}\right](0)\neq 0

and so the new Cantor set is also non-essentially affine.

∎

Remark 2.21
.

We observe that the
C^{\infty}
Cantor set
\tilde{K}
constructed in lemma
2.19
can be also assumed to be close to
K
in the
C^{m^{\prime}}
topology for all
m^{\prime}\in(1,m)
. All one needs to do is to choose
\hat{g}
close to
g
in this topology. This can be done using a mollifier
\varphi
supported in a very small neighbourhood of the origin and making
\hat{g}=g*\varphi
.
Notice that the
C^{m}
proximity between the maps
p_{\underline{a}}
and
g
in
supp(\phi_{\underline{a}})
comes from the fact that
g
is
C^{m}
and
supp(\phi_{\underline{a}})
has diameter of order
\rho
. Moreover, using the fact that
D^{[m]}g
is
\varepsilon
-Holder, for
\varepsilon=m-[m]
, one gets the improved estimate
\|D^{m-j}(g-p_{\underline{a}})|_{V_{\tilde{c}\rho}(G(\underline{a}))}\|=O(\rho^{j+\varepsilon})
for all
0\leq j\leq[m]
. This can be seen analysing the Taylor approximation of the derivatives of
g
in this domain.

It follows that, given any Cantor set
K\in\Omega_{\Sigma}
, essentially real or not, arbitrarily close to
K
in the topology of
\Omega_{\Sigma}
, there is a Cantor set
\tilde{K}\in\Omega^{\infty}_{\Sigma}\subset\Omega_{\Sigma}
that satisfies the conclusion of lemma
2.20
. Indeed, suppose that
K
is
C^{1+\varepsilon}
for some
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
. Then the only part of the argument (polynomial approximation in the
C^{1+\varepsilon}
topology) in lemma
2.19
which uses the non-essentially real hypothesis can be done using the conformality of the Cantor set. Moreover, one can also choose
\tilde{K}
to be non-essentially real. To do so, we first suppose that the expanding function
\tilde{g}
is already holomorphic in a neighborhood of
\tilde{K}
. Then one chooses a periodic point
p\in\tilde{K}
, of period
n
, and observes that if
D\tilde{g}^{n}(p)\notin\mathbbm{R}
then
\tilde{K}
can not be essentially real. Thus, if
D\tilde{g}^{n}(p)\notin\mathbbm{R}
we are done, otherwise we perturb
\tilde{K}
along the periodic orbit
\{p,...,\tilde{g}^{n-1}(p)\}
to get such property. One execution of this idea of perturbing along a periodic orbit can be found in the proof of theorem
2.23
.

2.6.
Main theorems

Here we state our main theorems. The proof of theorem
2.22
will be given throughout the remaining sections. Using this theorem we will prove theorem
2.23
. In particular, we will get that there is an open and dense set, among pairs of conformal Cantor sets
K
,
K^{\prime}
with
HD(K)+HD(K^{\prime})>2
, such that all elements in this set verify
\text{int}(K-K^{\prime})\neq\emptyset
. Before stating the theorems, we remark that the Hausdorff dimension varies continuously with the Cantor set. This is proven in
[
9
]
for Cantor sets in the real line and the argument there can be adapted to our context.

Theorem 2.22
.

Given a pair of non-essentially real conformal Cantor sets
(K,K^{\prime})
in
\Omega^{\infty}_{\Sigma}\times\Omega^{\infty}_{\Sigma^{\prime}}
, such that
HD(K)+HD(K^{\prime})>2
. Arbitrarily close to
K
,
K^{\prime}
, in the
C^{\infty}
topology, we can find conformal Cantor sets
\tilde{K}
,
\tilde{K}^{\prime}
in
\Omega^{\infty}_{\Sigma}
,
\Omega^{\infty}_{\Sigma^{\prime}}
respectively, such that
\tilde{K}
,
\tilde{K}^{\prime}
has a non empty recurrent compact set.

Define the set
U
as the pairs of conformal Cantor sets
(K,K^{\prime})
in
\Omega_{\Sigma}\times\Omega_{\Sigma^{\prime}}
such that for every relative configuration
(\underline{\theta},\underline{\theta}^{\prime},s,t)
, there is
\tilde{t}\in\mathbbm{C}
such that the configuration
(\underline{\theta},\underline{\theta}^{\prime},s,\tilde{t})
has stable intersection.

Theorem 2.23
.

The set
U
is open in
\Omega_{\Sigma}\times\Omega_{\Sigma^{\prime}}
and
U\cap\Omega^{\infty}_{\Sigma}\times\Omega^{\infty}_{\Sigma^{\prime}}
is dense, in the
C^{\infty}
topology, in
\{(K,K^{\prime})\in\Omega^{\infty}_{\Sigma}\times\Omega^{\infty}_{\Sigma^{\prime}}:\,HD(K)+HD(K^{\prime})>2,\,K,\,K^{\prime}\text{ are non-essentially real}\}
. Moreover, for any
(K,K^{\prime})
in
U
and
(h,h^{\prime})\in\mathcal{P}\times\mathcal{P^{\prime}}
such that
Dh(z)
and
Dh^{\prime}(z^{\prime})
are conformal for all
(z,z^{\prime})\in K\times K^{\prime}
, the set

\mathcal{I}_{s}=\{\lambda\in\mathbbm{C}:(h+\lambda,h^{\prime})\text{ has stable intersection}\}

is dense in

\mathcal{I}=\{\lambda\in\mathbbm{C}:(h+\lambda,h^{\prime})\text{ is intersecting}\}.

In particular,
\text{int}(K-K^{\prime})\neq\emptyset
.

Proof.

The proof is very similar to the one for the corresponding result in
[
1
]
, except for the use of lemma
2.25
. For the openness of
U
, one observes that, if
R
is big enough, then any relative configuration
(\underline{\theta},\underline{\theta}^{\prime},s,t)
can be transported, using a renormalization operator, to the set
\Sigma^{-}\times\Sigma^{\prime-}\times\{e^{-R}\leq|s|\leq e^{R}\}\times\mathbbm{C}
. Given
(K,K^{\prime})
in
U
, from compactness of the set
\Sigma^{-}\times\Sigma^{\prime-}\times\{e^{-R}\leq|s|\leq e^{R}\}
one sees that there is a neighborhood of
(K,K^{\prime})
such that for any pair in this neighborhood, we have
(\underline{\theta},\underline{\theta}^{\prime},s,t)\in\Sigma^{-}\times\Sigma^{\prime-}\times\{e^{-R}\leq|s|\leq e^{R}\}\times\mathbbm{C}
implies there is
\tilde{t}\in\mathbbm{C}
such that
(\underline{\theta},\underline{\theta}^{\prime},s,\tilde{t})
has stable intersection. Thus the same happens for the whole
\Sigma^{-}\times\Sigma^{\prime-}\times\mathbbm{C}^{*}\times\mathbbm{C}
, and the neighborhood is contained in
U
.

Furthermore, in the same context of the previous paragraph, from compactness of the set
\Sigma^{-}\times\Sigma^{\prime-}\times\{e^{-R}\leq|s|\leq e^{R}\}
, for each
r>1
there is some
\delta>0
such that if
\tilde{h}
and
\tilde{h}^{\prime}
are maps
\delta
-close to the identity in the
C^{r}
metric, then
(\tilde{h}\circ B\circ k^{\underline{\theta}},\tilde{h}^{\prime}\circ k^{\underline{\theta}^{\prime}})
has stable intersections, where
B(z)=sz+\tilde{t}
and
e^{-R}\leq|s|\leq e^{R}
. We will need this later.

For the denseness we use theorem
2.22
. Given
(\tilde{K},\tilde{K}^{\prime})\in\Omega^{\infty}_{\Sigma}\times\Omega^{\infty}_{\Sigma^{\prime}}
, with
HD(K)+HD(K^{\prime})>2
and both of them non-essentially real, arbitrarily close to it there is
(K,K^{\prime})\in\Omega^{\infty}_{\Sigma}\times\Omega^{\infty}_{\Sigma^{\prime}}
having a non empty recurrent compact set
\mathcal{L}
. Perturbing, we may assume that
(K,K^{\prime})
also has periodic points
p
,
p^{\prime}
, associated to finite words
\underline{a}
,
\underline{a}^{\prime}
, that is
f_{\underline{a}}(p)=p
,
f_{\underline{a}^{\prime}}(p^{\prime})=p^{\prime}
, such that if we write

a_{i,j}=Df_{\underline{a}^{i}}(p)/Df_{\underline{a}^{\prime j}}(p^{\prime})=DF^{\underline{\theta}_{0}}_{\underline{a}^{i}}/DF^{\underline{\theta}_{0}^{\prime}}_{\underline{a}^{\prime j}}\in\mathbbm{C}^{*},

where
\underline{a}^{i}
is concatenation of
\underline{a}
with itself
i
times, similarly for
\underline{a}^{\prime}
,
\underline{\theta}_{0}=(...,\underline{a},\underline{a})
and
\underline{\theta}^{\prime}_{0}=(...,\underline{a}^{\prime},\underline{a}^{\prime})
, then the set
\{a_{i,j}\}_{i,j\in\mathbbm{Z}^{+}}
is dense in
\mathbbm{C}^{*}
(see lemma
2.25
).

More precisely, in order to get the density of
\{a_{i,j}\}
, we need to perturb
(g,g^{\prime})
such that
Dg^{m}(p),\,Dg^{\prime m^{\prime}}(p^{\prime})\in\mathbbm{C}^{*}\approx\mathbbm{R}\times\mathbbm{T}
have the property in lemma
2.25
, where
m
,
m^{\prime}
are the periods of
p
,
p^{\prime}
, respectively. To do this, we define a family of conformal Cantor sets given by expanding functions
(g_{x},g^{\prime}_{y})
depending in complex parameters
x
,
y
, such that the pairs
(Dg_{x}^{m}(p_{x}),Dg_{y}^{\prime m^{\prime}}(p_{y}))\in\mathbbm{C}^{*}\times\mathbbm{C}^{*}
form an open set. Choose a word
\underline{d}\in\Sigma(\alpha)
such that
p\in G(\underline{d})
,
\alpha
is small enough such that there are not more points of the periodic orbit of
p
contained in
G(\underline{d})
and
g
is holomorphic in
V_{\alpha}(K)
(by lemma
2.19
we may assume this). For
0<c<1
small enough one has that
K\cap V_{c\alpha}(G(\underline{d}))=K\cap G(\underline{d})
. We choose a
C^{\infty}
function
\psi_{x}
such that

\psi_{x}(z)=\begin{cases}x\cdot(z-p)+p&\text{ if }z\in V_{c\alpha/3}(G(\underline{d})),\\
z&\text{ if }z\notin V_{2c\alpha/3}(G(\underline{d})),\end{cases}

where
x
is in the ball of center

and radius
\delta^{\prime}
in
\mathbbm{C}
. Define

, notice that we can take

as close as we want to

in the

topology by choosing

small enough. If we choose

, associated to

and therefore

, which implies that

is conformal for all

. This proves that

is a conformal Cantor set. Moreover, notice that

is still a periodic point of

with the same period

, and

. Doing the same construction for

one sees that for some value of

satisfy the hypothesis of lemma

.

Using equation (
3
), the denseness of the set

and the fact that

has non-empty interior, one concludes that for any
(\underline{\theta},\underline{\theta}^{\prime},s,t)
there is

,
\underline{b}
,
\underline{b}^{\prime}
in

,
\Sigma^{\prime fin}

(K,K^{\prime})\in U\cap\Omega^{\infty}_{\Sigma}\times\Omega^{\infty}_{\Sigma}
.

For the final part, let
\lambda\in\mathbbm{C}
be such that

is intersecting and take any

. Then there is at least one pair of words
\underline{a}=(a_{0},\,a_{1},\,\dots,\,a_{n})\in\Sigma^{fin}

are still intersecting and the diameters of the sets

and

are smaller than

. Indeed, if it was not the case, the sets
(h+\lambda)(G(\underline{a}))
and

would be disjoint for all
\underline{a}
and
\underline{a}^{\prime}

Lemma 2.25
.

Let
(t,v),(t^{\prime},v^{\prime})\in(\mathbbm{R}\setminus\{0\})\times(\mathbbm{R}/(2\pi\mathbbm{Z}))
, and consider the subgroup
E=\{m(t,v)+n(t^{\prime},v^{\prime}):\,m,n\in\mathbbm{Z}\}
. Let
w,\,w^{\prime}\in\mathbbm{R}
be representatives of
v,\,v^{\prime}
, respectively. Then
E\subset\mathbbm{R}\times\mathbbm{T}
is dense if and only there is not
(\beta_{1},\beta_{2})\in\mathbbm{Z}^{2}\setminus\{0\}
such that

\beta_{1}\cdot\frac{t}{t^{\prime}}+\beta_{2}\left(w-w^{\prime}\frac{t}{t^{\prime}}\right)\in\mathbbm{Z}.

Moreover, if
E
is dense and
t/t^{\prime}>0
then
\{m(t,v)-n(t^{\prime},v^{\prime}):\,m,n\in\mathbbm{Z}^{+}\}
is dense. The set of pairs
((t,v),(t^{\prime},v^{\prime}))\in(\mathbbm{R}\times\mathbbm{T})^{2}
such that
E
is dense is a countable intersection of open and dense sets.

Proof.

The lemma is proved using Kronecker’s theorem. It states that a vector
(w_{1},...,w_{k})\in\mathbbm{T}^{k}
generates a dense subgroup if and only if there is not
(a_{1},...,a_{k})\in\mathbbm{Z}^{k}\setminus\{0\}
such that
a_{1}w_{1}+...+a_{k}w_{k}=0
.

Let
p:\mathbbm{R}\to\mathbbm{T}
be the canonical projection and choose
w,\,w^{\prime}\in\mathbbm{R}
such that
p(w)=v
,
p(w^{\prime})=v^{\prime}
. Note that
E
is dense if and only if the set

\{(t,w),(t^{\prime},w^{\prime}),(0,1)\}

generates a dense subgroup in
\mathbbm{R}^{2}
.

Moreover, this last property is invariant under invertible linear transformations in
\mathbbm{R}^{2}
. Let
A:\mathbbm{R}^{2}\to\mathbbm{R}^{2}
be the linear map such that
A(t^{\prime},w^{\prime})=(1,0)
and
A(0,1)=(0,1)
. Then,
E
is dense if and only if the set

\{A(t,w),(1,0),(0,1)\}

generates a dense subgroup in
\mathbbm{R}^{2}
. It is clear that this happens if and only if the projection of
A(t,w)
to
\mathbbm{T}^{2}
generates a dense subgroup in
\mathbbm{T}^{2}
. Thus, using Kronecker’s theorem we see that
E
is dense if and only if there is not
(\beta_{1},\beta_{2})\in\mathbbm{Z}^{2}\setminus\{0\}
such that

\langle(\beta_{1},\beta_{2}),A(t,w)\rangle\in\mathbbm{Z}.

Moreover, it is not difficult to see that
A(t,w)=(t/t^{\prime},w-(w^{\prime}t/t^{\prime}))
. This proves the first part of the lemma.

Notice that the set of pairs
((t,v),(t^{\prime},v^{\prime}))
such that
E
is dense corresponds to the intersection, varying
a\in\mathbbm{Z}^{2}\setminus\{0\}
, of the sets

\{((t,p(w)),(t^{\prime},p(w^{\prime}))):\langle a,A(t,w)\rangle\notin\mathbbm{Z}\},

and each one of these sets is open and dense.

Finally,
\{m(t,v)-n(t^{\prime},v^{\prime}):\,m,n\in\mathbbm{Z}^{+}\}
will be dense if and only if

\{mA(t,w)-n(1,0)+r(0,1):\,m,n\in\mathbbm{Z}^{+},\,r\in\mathbbm{Z}\}

is dense in
\mathbbm{R}^{2}
. If
E
is dense, then the projection of
\{mA(t,w):\,m\in\mathbbm{Z}^{+}\}
to
\mathbbm{T}^{2}
is dense. If we also have
t/t^{\prime}>0
, then from the expression

mA(t,w)-n(1,0)+r(0,1)=(m(t/t^{\prime})-n,m[w-w^{\prime}(t/t^{\prime})]+r),

it is not difficult to see that
\{m(t,v)-n(t^{\prime},v^{\prime}):\,m,n\in\mathbbm{Z}^{+}\}
is dense in this case.
