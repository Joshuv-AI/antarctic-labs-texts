Strong Normalization for the Calculus of Constructions
Chris Casinghino

Abstract




1
Introduction

The calculus of constructions (CC) is a core theory for dependently
typed programming and higher-order constructive logic. Originally
introduced in Coquand’s 1985 thesis
[
4
]
, CC has
inspired 25 years of research in programming languages and type
theory. Today, extensions of CC form the basis of languages like
Coq
[
17
]
and Agda
[
15
,
16
]
.

The popularity of CC can be attributed to the combination of its
expressiveness and its pleasant metatheoretic properties. Among these
properties, one of the most important is
strong normalization
,
which means that there are no infinite reduction sequences from
well-typed expressions. This result has two important consequences.
First, it implies that CC is consistent as a logic. This makes it an
attractive target for the formalization of mathematics. Second, it
implies that there is an algorithm to check whether two expressions
are
\beta
-convertible. Thus, type checking is decidable and CC
provides a practical basis for programming languages.

The strong normalization theorem has traditionally been considered
difficult to
prove
[
5
,
2
]
. Coquand’s
original proof was found to have at least two errors, but a number of
later papers give different, correct
proofs
[
5
]
. In subsequent years, many authors
considered how to extend this result for additional programming
constructs like inductive datatypes with recursion, a predicative
hierarchy of universes, and large
eliminations
[
18
,
9
,
11
]
. Many of these
proofs are even more challenging, and several span entire theses.

This document reviews three proofs of strong normalization for CC.
Each paper we have chosen proves the theorem by constructing a model
of the system in a different domain, and each contributes something
novel to the theory of CC and its extensions. The technical details
of the models are often complicated and intimidating. Rather than
comprehensively verifying and reproducing the proofs, we have focused
on painting a clear picture of the beautiful and fascinating
mathematical structures that underpin them.

The first proof, originally presented by
Geuvers and Nederhof 1991
and
subsequently popularized by
Barendregt 1992
, models
CC in the simpler theory of F
ω
. It demonstrates that the
strong normalization theorems for CC and F
ω
are equivalent
by giving a reduction-preserving translation from the former to the
latter. The second, by
Geuvers 1995
, models CC’s types
with sets of expressions. The paper demonstrates how the model may be
extended to cope with several popular language features, aiming for
flexibility. The last proof, from
Melliès and Werner 1996
,
uses realizability semantics to consider a large class of type
theories, known as the pure type systems, which include CC. The
authors’ goal is to prove strong normalization for any pure type
system that enjoys a particular kind of realizability model.

Though each paper has a unique focus and models CC in a different
semantic system, the overall structures are very similar. After
unifying the syntax, the correspondences between certain parts of the
proofs are quite striking. Readers are encouraged, for example, to
compare the interpretation functions defined in
Sections
6.2
and
6.3
with those in
Section
5.2
. The similarities between the papers speak
to the fundamental underlying structure of CC, while their differences
illustrate how design choices can push the proof towards varying
goals.

The paper is structured as follows: In Sections
2

and
3
we review the definition and basic
metatheory of pure type systems and the calculus of constructions. We
present the high-level structure of a strong normalization argument in
Section
4
, then the proofs of

Geuvers and Nederhof 1991
,
Geuvers 1995
and

Melliès and Werner 1996
in Sections
5
,

6
and
7
, respectively. We
compare the proofs and conclude in Section
8
.

Pure Type Systems

The calculus of constructions is one example of a
pure type

(PTS). This very general notion, introduced by Berardi and
popularized by Barendregt

us to simultaneously study the properties of several systems.

\mathcal{S}

these sorts is given by a collection of
axioms

\mathcal{A}\subseteq\mathcal{S}^{2}
. Finally, the way product types may be formed is
specified by the set of
rules

\mathcal{R}\subseteq\mathcal{S}^{3}

Definition of Pure Type Systems

Choosing to explain CC as a PTS settles several questions of
presentation. The terms, types and kinds are collapsed into
one grammar. Some authors choose to separate these levels
syntactically for easier identification, but we find this version more
economical and it is more closely aligned with the three papers under
consideration. For the same reasons, we have used
\beta
-conversion
in the
Conv
rule instead of using a separate judgemental
equality (as is done, for example,
in
[
2
]
). Here,
=_{\beta}
is the symmetric,
transitive, reflexive closure of
\leadsto
. We do not consider

\eta
-conversion.

This context also permits a clean and compartmentalized explanation of
CC’s features. In most of the systems we consider, the sorts and
axioms are given by the sets:

\mathcal{S}=\{\ast,\Box\}\hskip 15.00002pt\mathcal{A}=\{(\ast,\Box)\}

Intuitively,
\ast
classifies types and
\Box
classifies
kinds. The lone axiom says that
\ast
is itself a kind. The
rule
(\ast,\ast,\ast)
permits standard function
types, whose domain and codomain are both types. The system with only
this rule is the simply-typed lambda calculus:

\mathcal{R}=\{(\ast,\ast,\ast)\}

The rule
(\Box,\ast,\ast)
permits functions whose
domain is a kind. For example, when the domain is
\ast
these
are functions which takes types as arguments (i.e., polymorphism).
Thus, adding this rule yields System F:

\mathcal{R}=\{(\ast,\ast,\ast),\,(\Box,\ast,\ast)\}

The rule
(\Box,\Box,\Box)
effectively duplicates STLC at
the type level. It allows functions that take and return types.
Adding it yields System F
ω
, which has type-level
computation:

\mathcal{R}=\{(\ast,\ast,\ast),\,(\Box,\ast,\ast),\,(\Box,\Box,\Box)\}

CC adds
dependent types
to System F
ω
. The rule

(\ast,\Box,\Box)
permits types to depend on terms by
allowing functions which take terms as arguments but return types.
Thus, the complete specification of CC is:

\mathcal{S}=\{\ast,\Box\}\hskip 15.00002pt\mathcal{A}=\{(\ast,\Box)\}\hskip 15.00002pt\mathcal{R}=\{(\ast,\ast,\ast),\,(\Box,\ast,\ast),\,(\Box,\Box,\Box),\,(\ast,\Box,\Box)\}

3
Simple Metatheory

For completeness, we review a few basic metatheoretic results. We
will write
\Gamma\vdash\mathit{a}:\mathit{A}
for the typing judgement of an arbitrary
PTS or when it is clear what system we are discussing, and otherwise
will label the turnstile as in
\Gamma\vdash_{CC}\mathit{a}:\mathit{A}
for CC’s typing
relation in particular.

The first result, confluence, can be proven using the standard
Tait–Martin-Löf
technique
[
12
,
11
]
.

Theorem 3.1

(Confluence)
.

If
\mathit{a}\leadsto^{*}\mathit{a_{{\mathrm{1}}}}
and
\mathit{a}\leadsto^{*}\mathit{a_{{\mathrm{2}}}}
then there is
a
\mathit{b}
such that
\mathit{a_{{\mathrm{1}}}}\leadsto^{*}\mathit{b}
and
\mathit{a_{{\mathrm{2}}}}\leadsto^{*}\mathit{b}
.

The second property, preservation, is proved by induction on typing
derivations, using a substitution lemma.

Theorem 3.2

(Preservation)
.

If
\Gamma\vdash\mathit{a}:\mathit{A}
and
\mathit{a}\leadsto\mathit{b}
then
\Gamma\vdash\mathit{b}:\mathit{A}
.

The last property is not usually considered for less expressive lambda
calculi because they are presented with separate syntax for terms,
types and kinds. The theorem says that CC expressions can still be
classified in this way with the typing judgement. It is proved by a
straightforward induction on typing derivations.

Theorem 3.3

(Classification)
.

If
\Gamma\vdash_{CC}\mathit{A}:\mathit{B}
, then exactly one of the following holds:

•

B
is
\Box
. In this case, we call
A
a
kind
.

•

\Gamma\vdash_{CC}\mathit{B}:\Box
. In this case, we call
A
a

\Gamma
-constructor
.

•

\Gamma\vdash_{CC}\mathit{B}:\ast
. In this case, we call
A
a

\Gamma
-term
.

When
B
is
\ast
, we will call
A
a
\Gamma
-type
.
This is a special case of the second bullet above. In this document
we use the word “expression” to refer to any element of CC’s grammar
and reserve the word “term” for the subclass of expressions
identified here.

Notice that we need a context to distinguish between constructors and
terms, but can identify kinds without one. The ambiguity comes from
variables, and some authors avoid it by splitting them into two
syntactic classes (typically
x,y,z
for term variables and

\alpha,\beta
for type variables). Distinguishing the variables in
this way forces duplication or subtle inaccuracy when discussing
binders at different levels. For that reason, we prefer to mix the
variables and use a context to identify the terms and constructors.

Finally, we define the central notion considered below:

Definition 3.4
.

An expression is called
strongly normalizing
if there are no
infinite
\leadsto
reduction sequences beginning at it. We write

\mathsf{SN}
for the collection of all such expressions.

4
Structure of the proofs

The three proofs we consider each model CC in a different domain, but
they share a similar overall structure. In this section we describe
the technique at a high level.

Step 1: Define the interpretations

Each proof begins by defining two interpretations. A “type”
interpretation, usually written
\llbracket A\rrbracket
, captures the static
meaning of types, kinds and sorts. For example, in the second proof
we will model types as sets of expressions so that
\llbracket A\rrbracket

contains all the terms of type
A
. Then a “term” interpretation is
defined to capture the run-time behavior of terms, types and kinds.
This is usually written
[a]
. In the example where types are
interpreted as sets of expressions, the term interpretation might pick
a canonical inhabitant with the right reduction behavior from the set.

Step 2: Relate the interpretations

After defining the term and type interpretations, we prove a theorem
that relates them. For example, in the second proof we will show that
if
\Gamma\vdash\mathit{a}:\mathit{A}
, then
[a]\in\llbracket A\rrbracket
. This theorem is
usually called “soundness”.

Step 3: Declare success

After proving the soundness theorem we observe that one of the
interpretations has some important property. This property will mean
that strong normalization is a direct consequence of the soundness
theorem. In the running example,
\llbracket A\rrbracket
will turn out to
contain only strongly normalizing expressions. Then, since
[a]\in\llbracket A\rrbracket
and
[a]
models
a
’s run-time behavior,
a\in\mathsf{SN}
.

A clarification about the interpretations

Though we have called
\llbracket\cdot\rrbracket
the “type” interpretation
and
[\cdot]
the “term” interpretation, we do not mean that the
former is only defined on types and the later on terms, in the sense
of the classification theorem. Rather,
\llbracket\cdot\rrbracket
is meant
to model the static meaning of any expression that can be used to
classify other expressions. In each proof it will be defined on all
constructors, kinds and sorts of CC. Correspondingly,
[\cdot]
is
meant to model the dynamic behavior of any expression which can take
reduction steps. It will be defined on the terms, constructors and
kinds of CC.

5
Modeling CC in F
ω

The first proof we consider translates CC expressions to System
F
ω
in a way that preserves reduction. System F
ω

is known to be strongly normalizing (see
[
6
]
for
a detailed proof), so the correctness of this translation will imply
that CC is strongly normalizing as well. The idea to prove strong
normalization of an expressive type theory by translation to a
better-understood system has been used in a variety of contexts. For
example,
Harper et al. 1993
demonstrated that LF is strongly
normalizing by giving a reduction-preserving translation to the simply
typed lambda calculus. This technique was originally applied to CC
by
Geuvers and Nederhof 1991
, and their proof is reproduced
in
Barendregt 1992
.

While this development does not have the same focus on extensibility
or generality as the later two, it has at least two advantages.
First, the proof is modular. The other two proofs we will see are
monolithic in that they must explain the unique features of CC while
recapitulating and extending a complicated semantic argument. Here we
may focus on the ways in which CC extends F
ω
and can rely on
the somewhat simpler semantics of that system. Second, the
translation itself is simple and can be verified in Peano arithmetic.
Thus, this technique demonstrates that the proof-theoretic complexity
of CC’s strong normalization argument is no greater than that of
F
ω
.

5.1
Intuition for the translation

The calculus of constructions extends System F
ω
with dependency in
the form of the rule
(\ast,\Box,\Box)
. This rule
permits type-level abstractions which create types but take terms as
arguments. The difficulty comes from modeling these functions in
System F
ω
without erasing any possible reduction sequences.

To do this, we will translate expressions in two distinct ways. The
“type” translation
\llbracket\cdot\rrbracket
erases the dependencies to
create
F_{\omega}
types from CC types. The “term” translation

[\cdot]
keeps the dependencies to avoid erasing any
possible reductions, but lowers type functions to the level of terms.
The soundness theorem of our translation will state

\text{if}\;\Gamma\vdash_{CC}\mathit{a}:\mathit{A}\;\text{ then }\;\llbracket\Gamma\rrbracket\vdash_{\text{F}_{\omega}}[\mathit{a}]:\llbracket\mathit{A}\rrbracket.

We follow
Geuvers and Nederhof 1991
in exhibiting how these translations
handle several examples before specifying them in full detail.
Consider a simple example of dependency where
F
is a dependent type
function,
A
a type and
a
a term, so that:

\frac{\Gamma\vdash_{CC}\mathit{F}:\mathit{A}\to\ast\quad\Gamma\vdash_{CC}\mathit{a}:\mathit{A}}{\Gamma\vdash_{CC}\mathit{F}\,\mathit{a}:\ast}\quad\textsc{right=}

The subderivation which checks the type of
F
will need to make use
of rule
(\ast,\Box,\Box)
. We must somehow erase this
use of dependency so that, in F
ω
:

\frac{\llbracket\Gamma\rrbracket\vdash_{\text{F}_{\omega}}[\mathit{F}]:\llbracket\mathit{A}\to\ast\rrbracket\quad\llbracket\Gamma\rrbracket\vdash_{\text{F}_{\omega}}[\mathit{a}]:\llbracket\mathit{A}\rrbracket}{\llbracket\Gamma\rrbracket\vdash_{\text{F}_{\omega}}[\mathit{F}\,\mathit{a}]:\llbracket\ast\rrbracket}\quad\textsc{right=}

To solve this, we take
\llbracket\mathit{A}\to\ast\rrbracket=\llbracket\mathit{A}\rrbracket\to 0
where

0:\ast
is a fixed type variable that is added to the context
by
\llbracket\Gamma\rrbracket
. We set
\llbracket\ast\rrbracket=0
and
[\mathit{F}\,\mathit{a}]=[\mathit{F}]\,[\mathit{a}]
. Now when checking the translated
F
we have a
term-level function rather than one which returns a type.

For our second example, suppose
A
and
B
are types and
a
is a
term of type
A
. When translating the application
(\lambda\mathit{x}:\mathit{A}.\mathit{B})\,\mathit{a}
,
we must erase
A
to an F
ω
type using the type translation

\llbracket\mathit{A}\rrbracket
. However, this admits the possibility that by erasing
dependency we will delete redexes. This is solved by inserting an
extra redex which does nothing but provide a spot to hold
A
’s
translation as a term. That is, for some fresh variable
y

[(\lambda\mathit{x}:\mathit{A}.\mathit{B})\,\mathit{a}]=(\lambda\mathit{y}:0.\lambda\mathit{x}:\llbracket\mathit{A}\rrbracket.[\mathit{B}])\,[\mathit{A}]\,[\mathit{a}].

The situation for polymorphism is similar. Consider a constructor
F

with kind
(\mathit{x}:\ast)\to\mathit{x}\to\mathit{x}
(for example, the polymorphic
identity function) and a type
A:*
. In translating the term
\mathit{F}\,\mathit{A}
, we must preserve
A
’s static meaning as a type without
erasing any possible reduction sequences. The solution is to use
both translations, again:

\llbracket(\mathit{x}:\ast)\to\mathit{x}\to\mathit{x}\rrbracket

=
(\mathit{x}:\ast)\to 0\to\mathit{x}\to\mathit{x}

[\mathit{F}\,\mathit{A}]

=
[\mathit{F}]\,\llbracket\mathit{A}\rrbracket\,[\mathit{A}]

The theme of these examples is that the two translations accomplish
different tasks. The type translation
\llbracket\cdot\rrbracket
erases
dependencies to make F
ω
types out of CC types. The term
translation
[\cdot]
preserves reduction behavior but
lowers CC types to F
ω
terms in order to accommodate the
weaker type system. We translate parts of expressions twice so that
we can achieve both goals.

5.2
The translation of types and contexts

Now we give the complete definition of the translation functions. We
begin by owning up to a slight simplification in the last section. To
distinguish term variables from type variables, the translations must
be indexed by contexts. Thus, the translation for types becomes

\llbracket\cdot\rrbracket_{\Gamma}
, and the translation for terms becomes

[\cdot]_{\Gamma}
. The translation for contexts,
\llbracket\cdot\rrbracket
,
remains unindexed.

In addition to these functions we define
V
which translates CC sorts
and kinds to F
ω
kinds:

\displaystyle V(\Box)

\displaystyle=\ast

\displaystyle V(\ast)

\displaystyle=\ast

\displaystyle V((\mathit{x}:\mathit{A})\to\mathit{B})

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}V(A)\to V(B)&\text{ if $A$ is a kind}\\
V(B)&\text{ otherwise }\end{cases}

This function is not indexed by a context because CC kinds may be
distinguished without one, by the classification theorem. The reason
for the case split in the last clause is that we are erasing
dependency.

The translation of types from CC to F
ω
follows the examples
from the previous section. The domain of
\llbracket\cdot\rrbracket_{\Gamma}
is the
sorts, kinds, and
\Gamma
-constructors of CC. We pick a unique type
variable
0
and assume it is never used in an input to this
function.

\displaystyle\llbracket\Box\rrbracket_{\Gamma}

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\llbracket\ast\rrbracket_{\Gamma}

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\llbracket\mathit{x}\rrbracket_{\Gamma}

\displaystyle=\mathit{x}

\displaystyle\llbracket(\mathit{x}:\mathit{A})\to\mathit{B}\rrbracket_{\Gamma}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}(\mathit{x}:V(\mathit{A}))\to\llbracket\mathit{A}\rrbracket_{\Gamma}\to\llbracket\mathit{B}\rrbracket_{\Gamma,\mathit{x}\!:\!\mathit{A}}&\text{ if $A$ is a kind }\\
(\mathit{x}:\llbracket\mathit{A}\rrbracket_{\Gamma})\to\llbracket\mathit{B}\rrbracket_{\Gamma,\mathit{x}\!:\!\mathit{A}}&\text{ if $A$ is a $\Gamma$-type }\end{cases}

\displaystyle\llbracket(\lambda\mathit{x}:\mathit{A}.\mathit{B})\rrbracket_{\Gamma}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\lambda\mathit{x}:V(\mathit{A}).\llbracket\mathit{B}\rrbracket_{\Gamma,\mathit{x}\!:\!\mathit{A}}&\text{ if $A$ is a kind }\\
\llbracket\mathit{B}\rrbracket_{\Gamma}&\text{ if $A$ is a $\Gamma$-type }\end{cases}

\displaystyle\llbracket\mathit{A}\,\mathit{B}\rrbracket_{\Gamma}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\llbracket\mathit{A}\rrbracket_{\Gamma}\,\llbracket\mathit{B}\rrbracket_{\Gamma}&\text{ if $B$ is a $\Gamma$-constructor }\\
\llbracket\mathit{A}\rrbracket_{\Gamma}&\text{ if $B$ is a $\Gamma$-term }\end{cases}

This function inserts duplication in product types as we discussed in
the examples section. Otherwise, it is straightforward with the
intuition that we are erasing dependency. The cases of the type
translation that deal with functions take into account the level of
the function’s domain (just as we saw with
V
). This distinction is
justified by the classification lemma and is reflected in the
substitution lemma for the translation:

(

.

Suppose
A

\Gamma

•

\llbracket[b/x]A\rrbracket_{\Gamma}=[\llbracket b\rrbracket_{\Gamma}/x]\llbracket A\rrbracket_{\Gamma}

•

\llbracket[b/x]A\rrbracket_{\Gamma}=\llbracket A\rrbracket_{\Gamma}
, if
B

\Gamma

This lemma can be shown by induction on the typing derivation. It
follows that the translation of types preserves
\beta

Lemma 5.2

(

.

Suppose
A
and

\Gamma

\llbracket\mathit{A}\rrbracket_{\Gamma}=_{\beta}\llbracket\mathit{A^{\prime}}\rrbracket_{\Gamma}
.

Before we can state that the results of
\llbracket\cdot\rrbracket_{\Gamma}
are
classified by
V
, we must extend the translation to contexts. As
mentioned,
\llbracket\cdot\rrbracket
will add a type variable
0:\ast
to the context. There are two additional changes. First, a
variable
z:(\mathit{x}:\ast)\to\ast
will be added to help provide a
canonical inhabitant for each type. Second, for each kind variable

x
which appears in
\Gamma
, the translation will add another
variable
w^{x}:x
. This last change simply ensures that contexts
match up with the translation of product types, where we add an extra
argument in the case of kinds as discussed above.

We define the translation of contexts in two parts. First, a function

\llbracket x:A\rrbracket_{\Gamma}
maps each context binding to one or two translated
bindings:

\llbracket x:A\rrbracket_{\Gamma}=\begin{cases}x:\llbracket A\rrbracket_{\Gamma},w^{x}:x&\text{ if $A$ is a $\Gamma$-kind }\\
x:\llbracket A\rrbracket_{\Gamma}&\text{ if $A$ is a $\Gamma$-type }\end{cases}

The translation of a context simply maps this last function onto each
binding and adds
0
and
z
to the front, as mentioned.
Suppose
\Gamma=x_{1}:A_{1},\ldots,x_{n}:A_{n}
, then:

\llbracket\Gamma\rrbracket=0:*,\;z:(\mathit{x}:\ast)\to\ast,\;\llbracket x_{1}:A_{1}\rrbracket_{\Gamma},\;\ldots,\;\llbracket x_{n}:A_{n}\rrbracket_{\Gamma}

Now the soundness of the translation of types follows
straightforwardly by induction on typing derivations

Lemma 5.3

(Soundness of
\llbracket\cdot\rrbracket_{\Gamma}
)
.

Suppose
A
is a sort, kind or
\Gamma
-type of CC such that

\Gamma\vdash_{CC}\mathit{A}:\mathit{B}
. Then
\llbracket\Gamma\rrbracket\vdash_{\text{F}_{\omega}}\llbracket\mathit{A}\rrbracket_{\Gamma}:V(\mathit{B})
.

5.3
The translation of terms

As mentioned in the last section, the translation of contexts permits
the construction of a canonical inhabitant of each type or kind in F
ω
.
In particular, for any expression
B
such that
\llbracket\Gamma\rrbracket\vdash_{\text{F}_{\omega}}\mathit{B}:\mathit{s}
, we will define a term
c^{B}
of type
B
in the same context.
That is,
\llbracket\Gamma\rrbracket\vdash_{\text{F}_{\omega}}c^{\mathit{B}}:\mathit{B}
. If
s=\ast
, then we may use
the term
z
to construct
c^{\mathit{B}}
:

c^{\mathit{B}}=\mathit{z}\,\mathit{B}\hfill\text{ when $B$ is a type}

Otherwise,
B
is a kind and we define:

\displaystyle c^{\ast}

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle c^{(\mathit{x}:\mathit{A})\to\mathit{B}}

\displaystyle=\lambda\mathit{x}:\mathit{A}.c^{\mathit{B}}

The evaluation behavior of these canonical inhabitants is not very
important. The chief purpose of

is to help in the term
translation of product types. The problem is that when
(\mathit{x}:\mathit{A})\to\mathit{B}

is a valid CC type, its translation
(\mathit{x}:[\mathit{A}]_{\Gamma})\to[\mathit{B}]_{\Gamma}
is not
necessarily well-typed in F
ω
. The translation
\llbracket\cdot\rrbracket_{\Gamma}

handles this by erasing dependency, but
[\cdot]_{\Gamma}
must retain
all the possible reductions which begin at
(\mathit{x}:\mathit{A})\to\mathit{B}
. Instead
of translating it as a product, we use
c
to construct a function
whose application to
A
and
B
is well-typed. In particular,
c^{0\to 0\to 0}\,[\mathit{A}]_{\Gamma}\,[\mathit{B}]_{\Gamma}
will be a valid F
ω
expression. Since

[\cdot]_{\Gamma}
does not erase the terms from
A
and
B
, this
retains all the possible reduction sequences.

We now present the full translation of terms:

\displaystyle[\ast]_{\Gamma}

\displaystyle=c^{0}

\displaystyle[\mathit{x}]_{\Gamma}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}w^{x}&\text{ if $x$ is a $\Gamma$-type }\\
x&\text{ if $x$ is a $\Gamma$-term }\end{cases}

\displaystyle[(\mathit{x}:\mathit{A})\to\mathit{B}]_{\Gamma}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}c^{0\to 0\to 0}\,[\mathit{A}]_{\Gamma}\,([c^{V(\mathit{A})}/\mathit{x}][c^{\llbracket\mathit{A}\rrbracket_{\Gamma}}/w^{\mathit{x}}][\mathit{B}]_{\Gamma,\mathit{x}\!:\!\mathit{A}})&\text{ if $A$ is a kind }\\
c^{0\to 0\to 0}\,[\mathit{A}]_{\Gamma}\,([c^{\llbracket\mathit{A}\rrbracket_{\Gamma}}/\mathit{x}][\mathit{B}]_{\Gamma,\mathit{x}\!:\!\mathit{A}})&\text{ if $A$ is a $\Gamma$-type }\end{cases}

\displaystyle[\lambda\mathit{x}:\mathit{A}.\mathit{b}]_{\Gamma}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}(\lambda\mathit{y}:0.\lambda\mathit{x}:V(\mathit{A}).\lambda w^{\mathit{x}}:\llbracket\mathit{A}\rrbracket_{\Gamma}.[\mathit{b}]_{\Gamma,\mathit{x}\!:\!\mathit{A}})\,[\mathit{A}]_{\Gamma}&\text{if $A$ is a kind, picking $y$ fresh }\\

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}[\mathit{A}]_{\Gamma}\,\llbracket\mathit{B}\rrbracket_{\Gamma}\,[\mathit{B}]_{\Gamma}&\text{ if $B$ is a $\Gamma$-type}\\

(Soundness of
[\cdot]_{\Gamma}
)
.

If

5.4
Strong Normalization

The final step in this proof is to relate reductions from CC
expressions with reductions from their translations. The following
result says that the term translation does not drop any reduction
steps.

Theorem 5.6

(
[\cdot]_{\Gamma}
preserves reduction)
.

Suppose
\Gamma\vdash_{CC}\mathit{a}:\mathit{A}
.

\mathit{a}\leadsto\mathit{a^{\prime}}\hskip 10.00002pt\Rightarrow\hskip 10.00002pt[\mathit{a}]_{\Gamma}\leadsto^{*}_{\neq 0}[\mathit{a^{\prime}}]_{\Gamma}

Here,
\leadsto^{*}_{\neq 0}
denotes reduction in at least one step.

Proof.

The proof is by induction on the derivation that
\mathit{a}\leadsto\mathit{a^{\prime}}
. The
case of beta reduction uses Lemma
5.5
. Each
congruence case follows quickly by using an inversion lemma on the
typing assumption and applying the induction hypothesis.
∎

Strong normalization for CC now follows quickly, using the same result
for F
ω
.

Theorem 5.7

(Strong normalization)
.

If
\Gamma\vdash_{CC}\mathit{a}:\mathit{A}
, then
a\in\mathsf{SN}
.

Proof.

Assume for a contradiction that there is an infinite reduction
sequence starting at
a
:

\mathit{a}\leadsto\mathit{a_{{\mathrm{1}}}}\leadsto\mathit{a_{{\mathrm{2}}}}\leadsto\ldots

By preservation,
\Gamma\vdash_{CC}\mathit{a_{\mathit{n}}}:\mathit{A}
for each
n
. Thus, by
Lemma
5.6
, there is another infinite sequence of
reductions:

[\mathit{a}]_{\Gamma}\leadsto^{*}_{\neq 0}[\mathit{a_{{\mathrm{1}}}}]_{\Gamma}\leadsto^{*}_{\neq 0}[\mathit{a_{{\mathrm{2}}}}]_{\Gamma}\leadsto^{*}_{\neq 0}\ldots

But by the soundness of the term interpretation, we have
\llbracket\Gamma\rrbracket\vdash_{\text{F}_{\omega}}[\mathit{a}]_{\Gamma}:\llbracket\mathit{A}\rrbracket_{\Gamma}
. This is a contradiction because the
well-typed terms of F
ω
are strongly normalizing.
∎

Modeling types as sets of expressions

The second proof we consider, from
Geuvers 1995
, will be
the most familiar to readers acquainted with the Girard--Tait method of
reducibility candidates or saturated sets. The paper places a special
emphasis on making the proof easy to extend with additional
programming language constructs. To this end, only the metatheory we
have introduced so far is required.

1

1

In fact, Geuvers requires
a little less: he claims preservation isn’t necessary. He still

6.1
Basic definitions

We begin with a few definitions and results relating to reduction.
Intuition for these ideas is important to understanding the main
proof, so we discuss them in some detail.

Definition 6.1
.

Any expression of the form
\mathit{x}\,\mathit{a_{{\mathrm{1}}}}\,\ldots\,\mathit{a_{\mathit{n}}}
is called a
base expression
. The set of base expressions is denoted
\mathsf{BASE}
.
Note that variables are base expressions (i.e.,
n=0
is allowed).

Definition 6.2
.

With some expressions we associate another expression, called a
key redex
.

•

The expression
(\lambda\mathit{x}:\mathit{A}.\mathit{b})\,\mathit{a}
is its own key redex.

•

If
A
has a key redex, then
\mathit{A}\,\mathit{B}
has the same key redex.

We denote by
\mathrm{red}_{\mathrm{k}}(A)
the expression obtained by reducing
A
’s key
redex, when it has one. Note that base expressions don’t have key
redexes. The intuition behind key redexes is that they can not be
avoided. Reducing an expression without reducing its key redex leaves
the redex in place. This intuition and the importance of key
reduction is captured by the following two lemmas. They are not
difficult to prove, but they rely on a few other simple properties of
beta reduction.

Lemma 6.3
.

Suppose
a
has a key redex and
\mathit{a}\leadsto\mathit{b}
without reducing that
redex. Then
b
has a key redex, and
\mathrm{red}_{\mathrm{k}}(a)\leadsto^{*}\mathrm{red}_{\mathrm{k}}(b)
.

It is helpful to visualize this lemma:

a

\leadsto

\mathit{b}

\leadsto

\leadsto

\mathrm{red}_{\mathrm{k}}(a)

\leadsto^{*}

\mathrm{red}_{\mathrm{k}}(b)

Lemma 6.4
.

Suppose
a,b\in\mathsf{SN}
and
\mathrm{red}_{\mathrm{k}}(\mathit{a}\,\mathit{b})\in\mathsf{SN}
. Then
\mathit{a}\,\mathit{b}\in\mathsf{SN}
.

Proof.

Suppose for a contradiction that there is an infinite reduction
sequence starting at
\mathit{a}\,\mathit{b}
. Since we know
a
and
b
are in

\mathsf{SN}
, this means the application must beta-reduce in some finite
number of steps. That is, the infinite sequence has a prefix of
the form:

\mathit{a}\,\mathit{b}\leadsto^{*}(\lambda\mathit{x}:\mathit{A}.\mathit{a^{\prime}})\,\mathit{b^{\prime}}\leadsto[\mathit{b^{\prime}}/\mathit{x}]\mathit{a^{\prime}}\leadsto\ldots

Note that this last step reduces a key redex. Thus, by multiple
applications of lemma
6.3
, we have
\mathrm{red}_{\mathrm{k}}(\mathit{a}\,\mathit{b})\leadsto^{*}[\mathit{b^{\prime}}/\mathit{x}]\mathit{a^{\prime}}
. This is a contradiction, since
\mathrm{red}_{\mathrm{k}}(\mathit{a}\,\mathit{b})\in\mathsf{SN}
but we found an infinite reduction sequence starting
at it.
∎

Saturated sets and their closure properties are the key technical
device in the interpretation. Originally introduced by Tait, they are
closely related to Girard’s
candidates of reducibility
(for
detailed comparisons, see
[
11
]

and
[
6
]
). The idea is pervasive, and we will
see it again in the second proof.

Definition 6.5
.

A set of expressions
S
is called a
saturated set
if the
following three conditions hold:

•

S\subseteq\mathsf{SN}

•

(\mathsf{SN}\cap\mathsf{BASE})\subseteq S

•

If
A\in\mathsf{SN}
and
\mathrm{red}_{\mathrm{k}}(A)\in S
then
A\in S
.

The third condition states that saturated sets are “closed under the
expansion of key redexes”. We write
\mathsf{SAT}
for the collection of all
saturated sets. Note that
\mathsf{SN}\in\mathsf{SAT}
and that every saturated set
is non-empty.

Lemma 6.6
.

If
S
is a non-empty
collection of saturated sets, then
\displaystyle\bigcap S\in\mathsf{SAT}
.

Definition 6.7
.

If
S_{1}
and
S_{2}
are sets of expressions, define:

\Pi(S_{1},\,S_{2}):=\{a\,|\,\forall b\in S_{1},\mathit{a}\,\mathit{b}\in S_{2}\}

It helps to have some intuition for this last definition: An
expression
a
is in
\Pi(S_{1},\,S_{2})
if whenever
a
is applied to
an expression in
S_{1}
, you get an expression in
S_{2}
. Thus, when
these sets model types,
\Pi(S_{1},\,S_{2})
will contain the functions
from the first type to the second. The next lemma, that

\Pi(\cdot,\,\cdot)
preserves saturation, involves the most
intricate reasoning about reduction that appears in this proof.

Lemma 6.8
.

If
S_{1},S_{2}\in\mathsf{SAT}
, then
\Pi(S_{1},\,S_{2})\in\mathsf{SAT}
.

Proof.

There are three conditions to verify:

•

(
\Pi(S_{1},\,S_{2})\subseteq\mathsf{SN}
)  Suppose
a\in\Pi(S_{1},\,S_{2})
. Saturated sets are non-empty, so let
b\in S_{1}
be given. Then
\mathit{a}\,\mathit{b}\in S_{2}
, and so
\mathit{a}\,\mathit{b}\in\mathsf{SN}
.
Thus,
\mathit{a}\in\mathsf{SN}
.

•

(
(\mathsf{SN}\cap\mathsf{BASE})\subseteq\Pi(S_{1},\,S_{2})
)  Let
a\in\mathsf{SN}\cap\mathsf{BASE}
be given. For any
b\in S_{1}
, since
b\in\mathsf{SN}
,

\mathit{a}\,\mathit{b}\in\mathsf{SN}\cap\mathsf{BASE}
. Thus
\mathit{a}\,\mathit{b}\in S_{2}
. So,
a\in\Pi(S_{1},\,S_{2})
.

•

(
\Pi(S_{1},\,S_{2})
is closed under key redex expansion)
Suppose
a\in\mathsf{SN}
and
\mathrm{red}_{\mathrm{k}}(a)\in\Pi(S_{1},\,S_{2})
. We must
show
a\in\Pi(S_{1},\,S_{2})
, so let

be given. We
have
\mathrm{red}_{\mathrm{k}}(a)\;b\in S_{2}
, and must show

. But

\mathrm{red}_{\mathrm{k}}(a)\;b=\mathrm{red}_{\mathrm{k}}(\mathit{a}\,\mathit{b})
. Since
S_{2}
is closed under
expansion of key redexes, it is enough to show that
\mathit{a}\,\mathit{b}\in\mathsf{SN}
. This follows immediately by lemma
6.4
.
∎

6.2
Interpreting kinds

The interpretation of types comes in two steps. First we define a
function
V(\,\cdot\,)
on the sorts and kinds of CC. This is,
roughly, the type of the main interpretation: if
B
is a kind or type
such that
\Gamma\vdash\mathit{B}:\mathit{A}
, then
B
’s interpretation will be an
element of the set
V(A)
.

\displaystyle V(\Box)={}

\displaystyle\mathsf{SAT}

\displaystyle V(\ast)={}

\displaystyle\mathsf{SAT}

\displaystyle V((\mathit{x}:\mathit{A})\to\mathit{B})={}

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\{f\,|\,f:V(A)\to V(B)\}&\text{when $A$ is a kind}\\
V(B)&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

By
\{f\,|\,f:V(A)\to V(B)\}
, we mean the collection of all
(set-theoretic) functions from
V(A)
to
V(B)
.

Lemma 6.9
.

If
A
is a kind, then
V(A)
is non-empty.

As an example, consider the type
\ast\to\ast
. Notice that
V(\ast\to\ast)
is the collection of all functions from saturated sets to
saturated sets. So, when we interpret an expression with this type
(say
\lambda\mathit{x}:\ast.\mathit{x}
), we will expect to get a function that takes
collections of expressions to other collections of expressions. This
makes sense, since it is a function from types to types.

The observant reader will notice that this definition of
V
mirrors
the one from Section
5.2
. Just as there, it indicates
that we will ignore dependency in the interpretation of types. This
will work because of the limited ways in which CC may use terms in
types. For example, CC lacks large eliminations: even though we can
encode natural numbers, we can not define types by pattern matching on
them.

6.3
Interpreting types

Because our interpretation is not restricted to closed types, we begin
by defining an environment that interprets the variables. Later, we
will consider another similar environment for terms.

Definition 6.10
.

Given a context
\Gamma
such that
\vdash\Gamma
, a
constructor
environment

\sigma
for
\Gamma
is a function that maps the type
variables of
\Gamma
to appropriate sets according to
V
. It
should satisfy the condition:

\text{if }(\mathit{x}:\mathit{A})\in\Gamma\,\wedge\,\mathit{A}\text{ is a kind, then }\sigma(x)\in V(A)

We’ll write
\sigma\models\Gamma
for this relation and

\sigma[x\mapsto S]
for the constructor environment which maps

to

and otherwise agrees with

6.4
From the interpretation to Strong Normalization

The key fact about the function
\llbracket\cdot\rrbracket
is that every
CC expression is in the interpretation of its type. Before we can
prove this, we need a notion of environment for terms corresponding to

\sigma
for types.

Definition 6.14
.

We call a mapping on variables
\rho
a
term environment for

\Gamma
with respect to
\sigma

and:

We write

for this relation and

for the term environment that sends

and otherwise agrees with

We show only the trickiest case of the key theorem—the complete
proof may be found in the appendix. Though there are a number of
details to keep track of, all the cleverness is in the definition of
the interpretation; the result here is straightforward by induction.

Theorem 6.15

(Soundness of

.

Suppose

and

. Then

.

Proof.

By induction on the derivation

of

Case:

\mathcal{D}=\raisebox{-7.56006pt}{$\frac{\frac{}{\mathcal{D}_{1}\hskip 7.3612pt\hskip 7.3612pt\Gamma\vdash\mathit{A}:\mathit{s}}\hskip 8.19447pt\textsc{right=}\hskip 8.19447pt\frac{}{\mathcal{D}_{2}\hskip 7.3612pt\hskip 7.3612pt\Gamma,\mathit{x}\!:\!\mathit{A}\vdash\mathit{b}:\mathit{B}}\hskip 8.19447pt\textsc{right=}}{\Gamma\vdash\lambda\mathit{x}:\mathit{A}.\mathit{b}:(\mathit{x}:\mathit{A})\to\mathit{B}}\quad\textsc{right=TLam}$}

.

6.5
Extensions

We conclude the presentation of this development by describing how it
changes to accommodate several extensions to CC. We sketch each
addition at a high level to give a sense of the proof’s flexibility.
Adding small
\Sigma
-types and W-types is encouragingly
straightforward. Unfortunately, changes at the kind level turn out to
be considerably more complicated.

Small
\Sigma
-types

Small
\Sigma
-types classify dependent pairs where the first component is
a term. We extend the syntax of CC with four new constructs

A,B,a,b:=\ldots\,|\,\Sigma\mathit{x}:\mathit{A}.\mathit{B}\,|\,(\mathit{a},\mathit{b})\,|\,\mathsf{proj}_{1}\;\mathit{a}\,|\,\mathsf{proj}_{2}\;\mathit{a}

and we add straightforward corresponding typing rules:

\frac{\Gamma\vdash\mathit{A}:\ast\quad\Gamma,\mathit{x}\!:\!\mathit{A}\vdash\mathit{B}:\mathit{s}}{\Gamma\vdash\Sigma\mathit{x}:\mathit{A}.\mathit{B}:\mathit{s}}\quad\textsc{right=TSigma}\hskip 30.00005pt\frac{\Gamma\vdash\mathit{a}:\mathit{A}\quad\Gamma\vdash\mathit{b}:[\mathit{a}/\mathit{x}]\mathit{B}\quad\quad\Gamma\vdash\Sigma\mathit{x}:\mathit{A}.\mathit{B}:\mathit{s}}{\Gamma\vdash(\mathit{a},\mathit{b}):\Sigma\mathit{x}:\mathit{A}.\mathit{B}}\quad\textsc{right=TPair}

\frac{\Gamma\vdash\mathit{a}:\Sigma\mathit{x}:\mathit{A}.\mathit{B}}{\Gamma\vdash\mathsf{proj}_{1}\;\mathit{a}:\mathit{A}}\quad\textsc{right=TProj1}\hskip 30.00005pt\frac{\Gamma\vdash\mathit{a}:\Sigma\mathit{x}:\mathit{A}.\mathit{B}}{\Gamma\vdash\mathsf{proj}_{2}\;\mathit{a}:[\mathsf{proj}_{1}\;\mathit{a}/\mathit{x}]\mathit{B}}\quad\textsc{right=TProj2}

The reduction judgement must also change. The obvious congruence
rules are needed for each construct, and there are two reduction rules
to handle the case where the projection operations meet pairs:

\frac{}{\mathsf{proj}_{1}\;(\mathit{a},\mathit{b})\leadsto\mathit{a}}\quad\textsc{right=SProj1}\hskip 30.00005pt\frac{}{\mathsf{proj}_{2}\;(\mathit{a},\mathit{b})\leadsto\mathit{b}}\quad\textsc{right=SProj2}

We make some simple changes to the definitions of base expressions and
key reduction. These ensure that certain pair constructions will
always appear in our interpretations. In particular, we extend
Definition
6.1
with the following clause:

•

If
a\in\mathsf{BASE}
then
\mathsf{proj}_{1}\;\mathit{a}\in\mathsf{BASE}
and
\mathsf{proj}_{2}\;\mathit{a}\in\mathsf{BASE}
.

And we extend Definition
6.2
with the following clause:

•

If
a
has a key redex, then
\mathsf{proj}_{1}\;\mathit{a}
and
\mathsf{proj}_{2}\;\mathit{a}

have the same key redex.

The definition of saturated sets remains the same, and we define a new
construction
X\otimes Y
that is a saturated set whenever
X
and
Y

are:

X\otimes Y:=\{a\;|\;\mathsf{proj}_{1}\;\mathit{a}\in X\wedge\mathsf{proj}_{2}\;\mathit{a}\in Y\}

This construction is used to extend the interpretation of types for
dependent sums. Here, since we know
x
is a term variable, we do not
need to extend
\sigma
in the interpretation of
B
(just as in the
interpretation for product types and functions).

\llbracket\Sigma\mathit{x}:\mathit{A}.\mathit{B}\rrbracket_{\sigma}=\llbracket A\rrbracket_{\sigma}\otimes\llbracket B\rrbracket_{\sigma}

This new clause doesn’t significantly alter the proofs of
Lemmas
6.11
,
6.12
and

6.13
. Similarly, a quick inspection of the four new
typing rules reveals that the soundness of the term interpretation
(Theorem
6.15

cases.

W-types

W-types add well-founded trees and recursion to the calculus of
constructions. They are common in the literature as a small change
that adds much of the expressive power of simple datatypes. We do not
present their details, but a comprehensive introduction may be found
in
[
14
]
.

Extending the proof to support W-types is only a little harder than
the previous example. Once again, the definitions of the base
expressions and key reduction each get an extra clause. The main
difficulty comes in defining a new construction on saturated sets to
model the
\mathsf{W}\mathit{x}:\mathit{A}.\mathit{B}
type constructor. The typing rule for this
constructor is:

\frac{\Gamma\vdash\mathit{A}:\ast\quad\Gamma,\mathit{x}\!:\!\mathit{A}\vdash\mathit{B}:\ast}{\Gamma\vdash\mathsf{W}\mathit{x}:\mathit{A}.\mathit{B}:\ast}\quad\textsc{right=TW}

The type
\mathsf{W}\mathit{x}:\mathit{A}.\mathit{B}
classifies well-founded trees where
A

describes the ways a tree may be formed and
B
describes the contents
of the tree for each possible
A
. Unsurprisingly, the interpretation
of this type involves a least fixed point over a particular monotone
operator on saturated sets. Geuvers demonstrates that a suitable
class of operators on saturated sets has least fixed points (an
interesting exercise in set theory, but somewhat outside the scope of
the current project).

After proving this property of saturated sets, the rest of the proof
hardly changes. An extra case is added to the interpretation of types
which uses a fixed point to interpret
\mathsf{W}\mathit{x}:\mathit{A}.\mathit{B}
. The cases
involving the new typing rules for W-types are then straightforward by
induction.

Large
\Sigma
-types

Large
\Sigma
-types classify dependent pairs where the first component is
a constructor. Adding them to small
\Sigma
-types involves only one
additional typing rule:

\frac{\Gamma\vdash\mathit{A}:\Box\quad\Gamma,\mathit{x}\!:\!\mathit{A}\vdash\mathit{B}:\mathit{s}}{\Gamma\vdash\Sigma\mathit{x}:\mathit{A}.\mathit{B}:\Box}\quad\textsc{right=TSigmaL}

A
is a kind, because
\sigma
must contain
interpretations for each of the type variables in

right-hand side.

This requires significant changes to the kind and type
interpretations. Currently,
\llbracket A\rrbracket_{\sigma}
is a saturated set when
A

is a kind. Instead,
\llbracket A\rrbracket_{\sigma}
will be function from elements of

to
\mathsf{SAT}
. Lemma
6.13
and

6.15
change as follows:

7
Modeling pure type systems with realizability semantics

Note to the reader: The proof presented in this section is the
most complicated of the three. The model it uses is substantially
more complex than the previous two, and there are several technical
problems with the paper under consideration. This section is
included for completeness and to document some of the issues we
encountered in reproducing the results. Casual readers are
encouraged to skip the details.

Melliès and Werner 1996
consider the question of strong
normalization for a subset of the pure type systems. They define a
“realizability” semantics parameterized in the same way as a PTS and
show that, when such a model exists, the corresponding PTS is strongly
normalizing. Their proof identifies four specific properties that the
model must satisfy in order to guarantee strong normalization, and the
paper exhibits suitable models for several systems. The idea of using
realizability semantics to model CC was originally introduced
by
Altenkirch 1993
.

In this section we present their development. The results are
particularly interesting in that the authors consider pure type
systems which are more expressive than CC. For example, ECC (the
extended calculus of constructions) adds an infinite hierarchy of
predicative sorts to CC. Proving strong normalization for this system
has traditionally been somewhat harder
[
11
]
.

This proof is considerably more involved than the one in the previous
section. In particular, we must define realizability models and lift
many of the ideas already explored in the context of CC to this new
domain, suitably generalized to work with any pure type system. The
situation is additionally complicated because some of the theorems and
proofs given in the paper are false or inadequate. We still believe
the technique is worth presenting because of its promised generality
and because it seems possible the problems here could be repaired. We
will focus on giving intuition for the model constructions and avoid
getting caught up in the proofs.

We begin by introducing
labeled
pure type systems with tight
reduction (Section
7.1
). The basic structures used in
the interpretation are defined in Section
7.2
, and in
this context Section
7.3
illustrates one of the
paper’s errors. Section
7.4
identifies the four
key properties that must hold of a model for the strong normalization
proof to apply, and examples of suitable constructions are given for
System F (Section
7.5
) and CC
(Section
7.7
). The interpretation of expressions into
these models is given in Section
7.8
. Finally,
Section
7.9
discusses the paper’s attempt to prove strong
normalization when a satisfactory model exists and to lift the result
back to a PTS without the extra labels.

7.3
A problem

As mentioned in the introduction, there are several problems with this
proof. The first comes from the definition of
\Lambda
-set products.
The authors claim to prove that this operation preserves saturation:

Let
\mathfrak{A}_{1}
and
\mathfrak{A}_{2}
be
\mathfrak{E}
-sets. Suppose
X\in\mathfrak{A}_{1}
and

Y_{\alpha}\in\mathfrak{A}_{2}
for each
\alpha\sqsubset X
. If
X
and each

Y_{\alpha}
are saturated
\Lambda
-sets, then so is
\Pi(X,\,Y)
.

While a similar result holds for for saturated sets, this proposition
is false. A saturated
\Lambda
-set must have a carrier which
realizes every element of
\mathsf{BASE}\cap\mathsf{SN}
, but
\Pi(X,\,Y)
may have
no carriers at all.

As an example, consider three
\Lambda
-sets,
X,Y_{\beta}
and

Y_{\gamma}
, such that
X
has two carriers and the others have one,
and each carrier is realized by every strongly normalizing expression.
That is:

\displaystyle X:=

\displaystyle(\{\beta,\gamma\},\,\mathsf{SN}\times\{\beta,\gamma\})

\displaystyle Y_{\beta}:=

\displaystyle(\{\beta^{\prime}\},\,\mathsf{SN}\times\{\beta^{\prime}\})

\displaystyle Y_{\gamma}:=

\displaystyle(\{\gamma^{\prime}\},\,\mathsf{SN}\times\{\gamma^{\prime}\})

These
\Lambda
-sets are saturated. Suppose
\mathfrak{E}
is a singleton set

\{1\}
, and define two
\mathfrak{E}
-sets:

\mathfrak{A}_{1}

:=

\{X\}

\mathfrak{A}_{2}

:=

\{Y_{\beta},\,Y_{\gamma}\}

\cong^{1}_{\mathfrak{A}_{1}}

:=

\{(X,X)\}

\cong^{1}_{\mathfrak{A}_{2}}

:=

\mathfrak{A}_{2}\times\mathfrak{A}_{2}

\equiv^{1}_{\mathfrak{A}_{1}}

:=

X_{0}\times X_{0}

\equiv^{1}_{\mathfrak{A}_{2}}

:=

\{(\beta^{\prime},\beta^{\prime}),(\gamma^{\prime},\gamma^{\prime})\}

Notice in particular that
\beta
and
\gamma
are related by

\equiv^{1}_{\mathfrak{A}_{1}}
but that
\beta^{\prime}
and
\gamma^{\prime}
are not
related by
\equiv^{1}_{\mathfrak{A}_{2}}
. Any carrier of
\Pi(X,\,Y)

would have to map
\beta
to
\beta^{\prime}
and
\gamma
to
\gamma^{\prime}
, so no
carrier can preserve the equivalence relation. Thus,
\Pi(X,\,Y)

has no carriers and is not saturated. The (non-)proof given in the
paper misses this problem because it neglects to reason carefully
about which functions preserve the equivalence relation.

We could fix the example given here by demanding that if two carriers
of elements of a
\mathfrak{E}
-set realize the same expressions, they must be
related by
\equiv^{i}_{\mathfrak{A}}
. However, it would still be
possible to construct a similar counter example by picking a smaller
set of realizers for
\gamma^{\prime}
.

One can imagine more complicated restrictions on
\Lambda
-sets and

\mathfrak{E}
-sets which restore this property, but it is not clear how they
would influence the rest of the proof. It is also possible that all
the specific uses of the product construction later in the paper
result in saturated
\Lambda
-sets. However, because this regularity
property is implicitly relied on in countless places, tracking it
completely is beyond the scope of this survey.

7.4
Models

We will now describe the model into which we interpret a labeled PTS.
This will be followed by four conditions, parameterized by the sets

\mathcal{S}
,
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{R}
. The main result of the paper is
that when there exists a model satisfying the four conditions
instantiated with parameters corresponding to a particular PTS,
that system is strongly normalizing.

The four conditions are somewhat involved. However, when using a
model where the set
\mathfrak{E}
is empty, conditions 2-4 are trivially
satisfied. This is the case for System F, so we recommend beginning
by understanding condition 1 and the model of System F in the next
section. Then Section
7.6
explains why this
construction does not suffice for CC, which may help motivate the
remaining conditions.

For each sort
s\in\mathcal{S}
, a model consists of

•

an
\mathfrak{E}
-set
\mathfrak{A}^{\Uparrow}(s)
,

•

a saturated
\Lambda
-set
\mathfrak{A}_{\Downarrow}(s)
, and

•

a bijection
\Uparrow_{s}:\mathfrak{A}_{\Downarrow}(s)_{0}\to\mathfrak{A}^{\Uparrow}(s)

When
\Gamma\vdash\mathit{A}:\mathit{s}

\mathfrak{A}^{\Uparrow}(s)
to contain a model of
A

as a “type” and for
\mathfrak{A}_{\Downarrow}(s)
to contain a model of
A

\Uparrow_{s}

\Lambda

\Downarrow_{s}:\mathfrak{A}^{\Uparrow}(s)\to\mathfrak{A}_{\Downarrow}(s)_{0}

The interpretation in Section
7.8
comes in two
corresponding levels. Each type classified by a sort

has a

\mathfrak{A}^{\Uparrow}(s)

expression also has a
term
interpretation as a carrier of the

\Lambda
-set associated with its type. Finally, each expression will
realize its term interpretation. By condition 1.1 below, this will
imply the expression is strongly normalizing.

7.6
Does this model work for CC?

It is instructive to consider why the model selected for System F is
not sufficient for CC. This will motivate the use of
\mathfrak{E}
-sets and
their equivalence relations. Recall that CC is the PTS given by:

\mathcal{S}=\{\ast,\Box\}\hskip 40.00006pt\mathcal{A}=\{(\ast,\Box)\}\hskip 40.00006pt\mathcal{R}=\{(\ast,\ast,\ast),\,(\ast,\Box,\Box),\,(\Box,\ast,\ast),\,(\Box,\Box,\Box)\}

The only difference from System F is the addition of two new rules.
We find the problem in satisfying property 1.3 for the rule

(\Box,\Box,\Box)
.

Suppose
X\in\mathfrak{A}^{\Uparrow}(\Box)
and
Y_{\alpha}\in\mathfrak{A}^{\Uparrow}(\Box)

for each

.

Condition 1.3 demands that we find an element of
\mathfrak{A}^{\Uparrow}(\Box)

which is isomorphic to
\Pi(X,\,Y)
. The only such element is

J(\mathsf{DG})
, so we are being asked to show
J(\mathsf{DG})
is isomorphic to

\Pi(X,\,Y)
. But the carrier set of

is
\mathsf{DG}
, and the
carrier set of
\Pi(X,\,Y)
is all the set theoretic functions from

\mathsf{DG}
to
\mathsf{DG}
. Plainly, there is no bijection between these sets.

This problem is fundamental, since the axiom

means
\mathfrak{A}^{\Uparrow}(\Box)
must contain
\mathfrak{A}_{\Downarrow}(\ast)
. To resolve
it, we use a non-empty
\mathfrak{E}
. The equivalence relations will break
our model of

situations like this where self-reference and set-theoretic size
constraints are a problem, we will simply move to a “higher” level.

7.9
Strong Normalization

Strong normalization follows from two key theorems. The first says
that when an expression is well-typed, its term interpretation is a
carrier of the interpretation of its type. The second says that each
expression realizes its term interpretation. Since condition 1.1
guarantees that all
\Lambda
-sets in the model are saturated, the
realizers are all strongly normalizing.

We do not go into the details of the proofs of these theorems.
Indeed, the proofs given seem inadequate. For example, consider the
first lemma the paper gives after the interpretation:

All that remains is to relate strong normalization for a labeled PTS
to strong normalization for an ordinary PTS. To do this, define an
operation

on labeled expressions which simply erases the extra
annotations to obtain an ordinary PTS expression. Extend this

by applying it to each type. We would

8
Discussion and Conclusion

The original goal of this project was to survey several very different
strong normalization proofs for the calculus of constructions. To
that end, we picked three attempts that target different structures
(sets of expressions, realizability semantics, and F
ω
).
Each paper’s proof was targeted toward different additional goals.
The first considered extensions with various datatypes and recursion.
The second tried to model a large class of pure type systems. The
last gave a relatively straightforward translation to simpler system,
demonstrating that the proof-theoretic complexity of CC’s strong
normalization argument is no greater than that of F
ω
.

Despite this, the three proofs are remarkably similar. Each gives a
type interpretation
\llbracket\mathit{A}\rrbracket
followed by a term interpretation

[\mathit{a}]
. Then, when
\Gamma\vdash\mathit{a}:\mathit{A}
, a simple relationship
between the two translations is demonstrated (for example,
[\mathit{a}]\in\llbracket\mathit{A}\rrbracket
). Finally, this relationship is shown to imply that
the expression
a
itself is strongly normalizing. Though the models
targeted by these functions are different in each proof, they share a
considerable amount of structure. For example, saturated sets of
expressions are very useful in both the first and second proofs. They
are often used in proofs of strong normalization for F
ω
as
well.

There are many commonalities even in the specifics of the
interpretations. Compare the kind interpretation
V
from
Section
6.2
with
V
in Section
5.2
.
Though one targets collections saturated sets and the other
F
ω
, both cope with the kind structure of CC in the same way.
Moreover, examining this similarity yields a cleaner understanding of
both proofs. In the context of saturated sets, it is tricky to
motivate the definition of the kind interpretation
V((\mathit{x}:\mathit{A})\to\mathit{B})

when
A
is a type. For example, we might plausibly have picked
functions from values or expressions to
V(B)
instead of just
V(B)

itself. But in the translation to F
ω
we find a snappy
explanation: this definition simply erases dependency. By unifying
the presentations and providing a common narrative through the three
proofs, we found additional clarity in each.

The papers have differences, too. These typically result from the
motivations of the authors. For example, the translation to
F
ω
is certainly the simplest of the developments and
provides the most confidence in the result. It achieves this by
relying on the existing strong normalization result for the simpler
system. On the downside, the authors do not consider extensions.

The most complicated proof uses realizability semantics. The
structure of the
\mathfrak{E}
-sets is somewhat intimidating, and in the end
the model of CC uses relatively little of its expressiveness. The
authors aim to provide a technique which extends to ECC, but perhaps
in this they overreach: the reasoning about the structures involved is
sometimes mistaken, and it is not clear how simple it would be to
repair.

The traditional approach using saturated sets falls somewhere between
these two. Though this technique must be extended to cope with CC,
the proof is somewhat familiar and does not require any new
structures. The authors succeed in extending the approach to various
common datatypes with recursion, but do not consider more complicated
additions like a predicative hierarchy of universes or large
eliminations.

The popularity of dependently-typed programming languages continues to
grow. So, too, do their lists of features. Our understanding of
their metatheory and of strong normalization in particular has not
quite kept pace. Thus, while the strong normalization of CC has been
considered a settled issue for more than two decades, understanding
its fundamentals is more important now than ever.

is a bound variable, we may pick it to be fresh for the domain
and range of
\rho
.

–

Suppose
s
is
\ast
. Then we must show
\lambda x:[A]_{\rho}.[b]_{\rho}\in\Pi(\llbracket A\rrbracket_{\sigma},\,\llbracket B\rrbracket_{\sigma})
. So
let
a\in\llbracket A\rrbracket_{\sigma}
be given, and observe it is enough to
show
(\lambda x:[A]_{\rho}.[b]_{\rho})\;a\in\llbracket B\rrbracket_{\sigma}
.

We have
\sigma\models\rho[x\mapsto a]:G,x:A
. Thus, the
IH for
\mathcal{D}_{2}
gives us
[b]_{\rho[x\mapsto a]}\in\llbracket B\rrbracket_{\sigma}
. But we also know:

(\lambda x:[A]_{\rho}.[b]_{\rho})\;a\leadsto[a/x][b]_{\rho}=[b]_{\rho[x\mapsto a]}

And this step contracts a key redex. So, it suffices to show
that
(\lambda x:[A]_{\rho}.[b]_{\rho})\;a\in\mathsf{SN}
.

Lemma
6.13
gives us that
\llbracket A\rrbracket_{\sigma}
is a
saturated set, so
a\in\mathsf{SN}
. We already observed that

[A]_{\rho}\in\mathsf{SN}
. By the second IH,
[b]_{\rho}=[b]_{\rho[x\mapsto x]}\in\llbracket B\rrbracket_{\sigma}
. But the
classification lemma and lemma
6.13
imply that

\llbracket B\rrbracket_{\sigma}
is a saturated set, so
[b]_{\rho}\in\mathsf{SN}
. Thus,
by lemma
6.4
,
(\lambda x:[A]_{\rho}.[b]_{\rho})\;a\in\mathsf{SN}
as desired.

–

Suppose instead that
s
is
\Box
. We must show

\lambda x:[A]_{\rho}.[b]_{\rho}\in\Pi(\llbracket A\rrbracket_{\sigma},\,\displaystyle\bigcap_{S\in V(A)}\llbracket B\rrbracket_{\sigma[x\mapsto S]})
. Let an expression
a\in\llbracket A\rrbracket_{\sigma}
and a saturated set
S\in V(A)
be given. It is
enough to show
(\lambda x:[A]_{\rho}.[b]_{\rho})\;a\in\llbracket B\rrbracket_{\sigma[x\mapsto S]}
.

Because
\sigma[x\mapsto S]\models\rho[x\mapsto a]:\Gamma,\mathit{x}\!:\!\mathit{A}
, the IH for
\mathcal{D}_{2}
gives us that

[b]_{\rho[x\mapsto a]}\in\llbracket B\rrbracket_{\sigma[x\mapsto S]}
. As in the previous subcase, we
can observe that:

(\lambda x:[A]_{\rho}.[b]_{\rho})\;a\leadsto[a/x][b]_{\rho}=[b]_{\rho[x\mapsto a]}

This step contracts a key redex, and by reasoning as before we
find
(\lambda x:[A]_{\rho}.[b]_{\rho})\;a\in\llbracket B\rrbracket_{\sigma[x\mapsto S]}
as desired.

Case:

\mathcal{D}=\raisebox{-7.56006pt}{$\frac{\frac{}{\mathcal{D}_{1}\hskip 7.3612pt\hskip 7.3612pt\Gamma\vdash\mathit{a}:(\mathit{x}:\mathit{A})\to\mathit{B}}\hskip 8.19447pt\textsc{right=}\hskip 8.19447pt\frac{}{\mathcal{D}_{2}\hskip 7.3612pt\hskip 7.3612pt\Gamma\vdash\mathit{b}:\mathit{A}}\hskip 8.19447pt\textsc{right=}}{\Gamma\vdash\mathit{a}\,\mathit{b}:[\mathit{b}/\mathit{x}]\mathit{B}}\quad\textsc{right=App}$}

We must show
[a]_{\rho}\;[b]_{\rho}\in\llbracket[b/x]B\rrbracket_{\sigma}
, and the
IH for
\mathcal{D}_{2}
is that
[b]_{\rho}\in\llbracket A\rrbracket_{\sigma}
. We consider two
cases: by the classification lemma and inversion,
A
is either a
kind or a
\Gamma
-type.

–

Suppose first that
A
is a kind. Then the IH for
\mathcal{D}_{1}

gives us that

[a]_{\rho}\in\Pi(\llbracket A\rrbracket_{\sigma},\,\displaystyle\bigcap_{S\in V(A)}\llbracket B\rrbracket_{\sigma[x\mapsto S]})

In particular, expanding the definition of
\Pi(\cdot,\,\cdot)

and applying lemma
6.13
, we have:

[a]_{\rho}\;[b]_{\rho}\in\llbracket B\rrbracket_{\sigma[x\mapsto\llbracket b\rrbracket_{\sigma}]}

By lemma
6.11
, this is just what we wanted to

–

The case where

Case:

\mathcal{D}=\raisebox{-7.56006pt}{$\frac{\frac{}{\mathcal{D}_{1}\hskip 7.3612pt\hskip 7.3612pt\Gamma\vdash\mathit{a}:\mathit{A}}\hskip 8.19447pt\textsc{right=}\hskip 8.19447pt\frac{}{\mathcal{D}_{2}\hskip 7.3612pt\hskip 7.3612pt\Gamma\vdash\mathit{B}:\mathit{s}}\hskip 8.19447pt\textsc{right=}\hskip 8.19447pt\mathit{A}=_{\beta}\mathit{B}}{\Gamma\vdash\mathit{a}:\mathit{B}}\quad\textsc{right=Conv}$}

The IH for

. By

, both

[a]_{\rho}\in\llbracket B\rrbracket_{\sigma}
