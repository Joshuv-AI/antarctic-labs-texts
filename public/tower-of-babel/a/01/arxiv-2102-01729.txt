Majorizing Measures, Sequential Complexities, and Online Learning
Adam Block, Yuval Dagan, Alexander Rakhlin

Abstract
We introduce the technique of generic chaining and majorizing measures for controlling sequential Rademacher complexity. We relate majorizing measures to the notion of fractional covering numbers, which we show to be dominated in terms of sequential scale-sensitive dimensions in a horizon-independent way, and, under additional complexity assumptions establish a tight control on worst-case sequential Rademacher complexity in terms of the integral of sequential scale-sensitive dimension. Finally, we establish a tight contraction inequality for worst-case sequential Rademacher complexity. The above constitutes the resolution of a number of outstanding open problems in extending the classical theory of empirical processes to the sequential case, and, in turn, establishes sharp results for online learning.



Keywords:

Online learning, Majorizing Measures, Sequential Rademacher Complexity, Chaining

1
Introduction

One of the primary goals of learning theory is to understand how the sample complexity of learning depends on the complexity of the model. Classically, much of the research focused on the case where data are drawn independently from some population distribution. The mismatch between sample and population inevitably leads to questions of uniform convergence. To answer such questions, the theory of empirical processes was developed, with seminal papers establishing non-asymptotic rates of convergence in terms of covering numbers, chaining
(
Dudley, 1973
)
, VC dimension
(
Vapnik & Chervonenkis, 1971
)
, and scale-sensitive combinatorial parameters
(
Bartlett et al. , 1996
;
Kearns & Schapire, 1994
)
.

A central notion of complexity is the (empirical) Rademacher complexity
(
Giné & Zinn, 1984
)
, defined for a data set
z_{1},\dots,z_{n}\in{\mathcal{Z}}
and a real-valued function class
\mathcal{F}
on
{\mathcal{Z}}
as

\widehat{\mathcal{R}}_{n}(\mathcal{F})=\mathbb{E}_{\varepsilon}\left[\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{t=1}^{n}\varepsilon_{t}f(z_{t})\right],

(1)

where
\varepsilon_{t}
are independent Rademacher random variables.
It is a classical result that
\widehat{\mathcal{R}}_{n}(\mathcal{F})
determines the rate of convergence of uniform laws of large numbers over the function class
\mathcal{F}
. Sharp upper and lower bounds on sample complexity of prediction and estimation have been established via Rademacher complexity and its localized versions
(
Bartlett & Mendelson, 2002
)
.

Just as important as the introduction of these notions of complexity has been the development of the relationships among them. In particular, it is a result of Dudley
(
Dudley, 1973
)
that the Rademacher complexity is controlled by integrating a function of the covering number and this, in turn, can be bounded by the integral of the square root of the scale-sensitive combinatorial dimension, as shown by
Mendelson & Vershynin, 2003
;
Rudelson & Vershynin, 2006
. On the other hand, closely related Gaussian averages, obtained by replacing Rademacher with Gaussian random variables in (
1
), are known to be tightly (up to a constant) controlled via generic chaining and majorizing measures
(
Fernique, 1975
;
Talagrand, 1996
)
, and may be strictly smaller than the bounds provided by Dudley’s chaining technique. It is fair to say that the foundations of statistical learning when the data are i.i.d. and presented all at once are fairly well-understood.

In contrast to statistical learning, in online learning the data arrive sequentially. The learner is tested on the new datum, observes the outcome, and updates the model with the new information. In the common formulation of the online learning problem, the sequence as treated as non-stochastic (individual), or even adaptively and adversarially chosen
(
Cesa-Bianchi & Lugosi, 2006
)
. For online binary classification, the fundamental result of
Littlestone, 1988
(and the agnostic generalization of
Ben-David et al. , 2009
) characterizes learnability in terms of finiteness of Littlestone dimension, the counterpart to VC dimension in statistical learning. Motivated by these results,
Rakhlin et al. , 2010
developed
sequential
analogues of Rademacher averages, covering numbers, and scale-sensitive dimensions, and showed that sample complexity of online learning can be related to these quantities. For example, for online supervised learning with indicator or absolute value loss, the minimax regret
V_{n}(\mathcal{F})
, defined as the average loss of an algorithm minus the average prediction loss of the best model in
\mathcal{F}
, is—up to a multiplicative factor of
2
—equal to the worst-case sequential Rademacher complexity of
\mathcal{F}

(
Rakhlin et al. , 2010
)
, a quantity we shall define below. Furthermore, in parallel to the close relationship between statistical learning and uniform laws of large numbers, the sample complexity of online learning can be viewed through the lens of uniform
martingale
laws of large numbers
(
Rakhlin et al. , 2015
)
.

In recent years, sequential complexities have been used in an increasing range of topics, including private learning

(
Alon et al. , 2019
;
Bun et al. , 2020
;
Jung et al. , 2020
;
Ghazi et al. , 2020
)
, adversarial robustness of sampling streaming data
(
Alon et al. , 2021
)
, denoising in autoregressive models
(
Hall et al. , 2016
;
Foster et al. , 2020
)
,
contextual bandits
(
Foster & Rakhlin, 2020
)
, among others.

While many of the parallels between the classical results in empirical process theory and their sequential (or martingale) counterparts have been established, a number of the fundamental relationships and techniques (such as generic chaining, a sharp contraction principle) are still missing. This paper addresses some of these gaps.

Before stating our contributions, we define the main object of study, sequential Rademacher averages. We are primarily interested in certain dyadic martingales. As before, let
\varepsilon_{1},\ldots,\varepsilon_{n}
be independent Rademacher random variables, and let
{\boldsymbol{z}}_{t}(\varepsilon)_{1\leq t\leq n}
be a predictable process with respect to the filtration
\mathcal{A}_{t}=\sigma(\varepsilon_{1},\dots,\varepsilon_{t})
; equivalently, we can think of
{\boldsymbol{z}}
as a complete binary tree of depth
n
with each vertex
{\boldsymbol{z}}_{t}(\varepsilon)
labelled by an element of
\mathcal{Z}
according to the path
\varepsilon_{1},\dots,\varepsilon_{t-1}
(see the bottom of this section for complete definitions). Given a real-valued function class
\mathcal{F}
on
\mathcal{Z}
, we follow
(
Rakhlin et al. , 2010
)
and define the sequential Rademacher complexity as

\mathcal{R}_{n}(\mathcal{F},{\boldsymbol{z}})=\mathbb{E}_{\varepsilon}\left[\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{t=1}^{n}\varepsilon_{t}f({\boldsymbol{z}}_{t}(\varepsilon))\right]

(2)

We note that if
{\boldsymbol{z}}
is a tree whose labels are constant as a function of
t
, i.e.,
{\boldsymbol{z}}_{t}(\varepsilon)
is independent of
\varepsilon
, we recover the classical notion of Rademacher complexity in (
1
). Much of
(
Rakhlin et al. , 2010
)
is devoted to demonstrating that this is, in some sense, the “correct” extension to the online case and this paper is primarily concerned with developing upper bounds for this quantity in terms of various complexity measures of
\mathcal{F}
.

The structure of the paper is given by the order of the following contributions:

1.

We introduce a notion of majorizing measure in the online setting and show that the sequential Rademacher complexity is bounded:

\mathcal{R}_{n}(\mathcal{F},{\boldsymbol{z}})~\lesssim~\inf_{\supp\mu\subseteq\mathcal{F}({\boldsymbol{z}})}\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\boldsymbol{v}}\in\mathcal{F}({\boldsymbol{z}})\\
\varepsilon\in\{\pm 1\}^{n}\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\int_{0}^{1}\sqrt{\log\frac{1}{\mu(B_{\delta}({\boldsymbol{v}},\varepsilon))}}d\delta

(3)

The upper bound can be viewed as an analogue of the corresponding upper bound via majorizing measures
(
Fernique, 1975
;
Talagrand, 1996
)
.
In proving the above, we introduce a new concentration inequality for martingales.

2.

We extend the notion of fractional cover introduced by
Alon et al. , 2021
to real-valued function classes and show that this notion of complexity controls the upper bound given by a majorizing measure.

3.

We prove a sequential analogue of the results of
Mendelson & Vershynin, 2003
for fractional covers, showing that

N^{\prime}(\mathcal{F},\delta)~\lesssim~\left(\frac{C}{\delta}\right)^{\mathsf{fat}_{c\delta}(\mathcal{F})}

(4)

thereby resolving an open question raised in
(
Rakhlin et al. , 2015
)
. Here
\mathsf{fat}
is a sequential scale-sensitive dimension, defined below.

4.

We prove a sequential analogue of the results of
Rudelson & Vershynin, 2006
, showing that

\mathcal{R}_{n}(\mathcal{F})~\lesssim~\int_{0}^{1}\sqrt{\mathsf{fat}_{\delta}(\mathcal{F})}d\delta

(5)

where
\mathcal{R}_{n}(\mathcal{F})=\sup_{{\boldsymbol{z}}}\mathcal{R}_{n}(\mathcal{F},{\boldsymbol{z}})
is the worst-case Rademacher complexity over all trees
{\boldsymbol{z}}
. For sufficiently simple classes
\mathcal{F}
, we show that this inequality can be reversed and apply the result to prove a dimension-independent contraction inequality for sequential Rademacher complexity, thereby resolving another open problem from
(
Rakhlin et al. , 2015
)
.

Notation.

Bold lower-case letters will denote complete binary trees. Given a depth
n
binary tree
{\boldsymbol{v}}
, denote by
\varepsilon\in\{\pm 1\}^{n}
a path from the root to a leaf of the tree, where
\varepsilon_{t}=-1
signifies that node
t+1
of the path is the left child of node
t
. A
real-valued tree
is a tree labelled by real numbers. If
{\boldsymbol{v}},{\boldsymbol{v}}^{\prime}
are real-valued trees, define by
{\boldsymbol{v}}(\varepsilon)=({\boldsymbol{v}}_{1}(\varepsilon),\dots,{\boldsymbol{v}}_{n}(\varepsilon))
the values of
{\boldsymbol{v}}
on the path
\varepsilon
, and let

\left|\left|{\boldsymbol{v}}(\varepsilon)-{\boldsymbol{v}}^{\prime}(\varepsilon)\right|\right|^{2}=\sum_{t=1}^{n}\left({\boldsymbol{v}}_{t}(\varepsilon)-{\boldsymbol{v}}_{t}^{\prime}(\varepsilon)\right)^{2}.

(6)

Given a real-valued tree
{\boldsymbol{v}}
, we let
\mathbb{T}_{\boldsymbol{v}}
be the associated tree process, the random variable

\mathbb{T}_{\boldsymbol{v}}(\varepsilon)=\sum_{t=1}^{n}\varepsilon_{t}{\boldsymbol{v}}_{t}(\varepsilon).

(7)

If
{\boldsymbol{z}}
is a
\mathcal{Z}
-valued tree that is clear from context and
f
is a real-valued function on
\mathcal{Z}
, we often abbreviate
\mathbb{T}_{f}=\mathbb{T}_{f({\boldsymbol{z}})}
and write
\mathcal{F}({\boldsymbol{z}})=\{f\circ{\boldsymbol{z}}:f\in\mathcal{F}\}
. For two quantities
a,b
, we say that
a\lesssim b
if there exists a universal constant
C
, independent of the quantities that determine
a,b
, such that
a\leq Cb
.

2
Majorizing Measures and Upper Bounds

In the classical setting, one of the most fundamental quantities that controls the size of the supremum of a stochastic process is the covering number of the underlying index set, i.e., the minimal number of points such that every member of the index set is within
\delta
of one of the chosen points. In
(
Rakhlin et al. , 2015
)
, this definition is extended to the sequential setting:

Definition 1
.

Let
{\boldsymbol{z}}
be a
\mathcal{Z}
-valued tree of depth
n
and let
\mathcal{F}\subset\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{Z}}
. A covering at scale
\delta
is a set of binary real-valued trees
{\boldsymbol{v}}^{1},\cdots,{\boldsymbol{v}}^{N}
such that for all
f\in\mathcal{F}
and all
\varepsilon\in\{\pm 1\}^{n}
, there is some
j
such that

\left|\left|f({\boldsymbol{z}}(\varepsilon))-{\boldsymbol{v}}^{j}(\varepsilon)\right|\right|^{2}\leq n\delta^{2}.

(8)

The covering number
N(\mathcal{F},\delta,{\boldsymbol{z}})
of a class
\mathcal{F}
for a tree
{\boldsymbol{z}}
at scale
\delta
is the minimal
N
such that there exists a
\delta
-covering of size
N
.

As noted in
(
Rakhlin & Sridharan, 2015
;
Rakhlin et al. , 2010
)
, we can recover the classical definition by restricting to trees
{\boldsymbol{z}}
, whose values are path-independent. In analogy with the statistical learning regime, we may apply the chaining technique to bound the sequential Rademacher complexity in terms of these covering numbers; in fact, for uniformly bounded function classes,
(
Rakhlin et al. , 2015
, Theorem 3)
guarantees

\mathcal{R}_{n}(\mathcal{F},{\boldsymbol{z}})~\lesssim~\inf_{\alpha>0}\left(\alpha+\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\int_{\alpha}^{1}\sqrt{\log N(\mathcal{F},\delta,{\boldsymbol{z}})}d\delta\right).

(9)

Looking back to the classical case, we note that the generic chaining and majorizing measure approaches
(
Talagrand, 2014
;
Talagrand, 1996
;
Fernique, 1975
)
would suggest that the bound in (
9
) is not tight in all cases. In the classical setting, generic chaining approaches give significant improvements over the Dudley entropy integral, as can be seen, for example, from the case of ellipsoids in Hilbert space
(
Talagrand, 2014
, Sec 2.5)
. Such improvements go well beyond logarithmic factors. Furthermore,
Rakhlin et al. , 2015
note that a clean,
n
-independent upper bound for the sequential covering number in terms of the sequential scale-sensitive dimension in the style of
(
Mendelson & Vershynin, 2003
)
is not yet known, resulting in additional looseness when using (
9
).

We now introduce a generalization of majorizing measures to the sequential case.

Definition 2
.

For a collection of
[0,1]
-valued binary trees
\mathcal{V}
of depth
n
, for
{\boldsymbol{v}}\in\mathcal{V}
and for a fixed path
\varepsilon
, let
B_{\delta}({\boldsymbol{v}},\varepsilon)
be the set of trees
{\boldsymbol{v}}^{\prime}
in
\mathcal{V}
such that
\left|\left|{\boldsymbol{v}}(\varepsilon)-{\boldsymbol{v}}^{\prime}(\varepsilon)\right|\right|^{2}\leq n\delta^{2}
. For a measure
\mu
on the set of
[0,1]
-valued trees of depth
n
, and
0\leq\alpha\leq 1
, let

I_{\mu}^{\alpha}({\boldsymbol{v}},\varepsilon)=\alpha+\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\int_{\alpha}^{1}\sqrt{\log\frac{1}{\mu(B_{\delta}({\boldsymbol{v}},\varepsilon))}}d\delta.

(10)

We let

\displaystyle I_{\mu}^{\alpha}=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\boldsymbol{v}}\in\mathcal{V}\\
\varepsilon\in\{\pm 1\}^{n}\end{subarray}}I_{\mu}^{\alpha}({\boldsymbol{v}},\varepsilon)

\displaystyle I_{\mathcal{F},{\boldsymbol{z}}}^{\alpha}=\inf_{\supp\mu\subseteq\mathcal{F}({\boldsymbol{z}})}I_{\mu}^{\alpha}

\displaystyle I_{\mathcal{F}}^{\alpha}=\sup_{\boldsymbol{z}}I^{\alpha}_{\mathcal{F},{\boldsymbol{z}}}

(11)

To see that this definition extends the classical one, consider the special case where
\mathcal{V}
is restricted to contain only trees that are constant on vertices of equal depth, corresponding to the batch setting; thus, the path
\varepsilon
in the above definition becomes irrelevant and we recover the classical notion of a majorizing measure
(
Talagrand, 2014
, p. 177)
. This notion of complexity is not very useful unless it actually controls the size of our stochastic process; fortunately, we have:

Theorem 3
.

Let
\mathcal{F}
be a separable
[0,1]
-valued function class on
\mathcal{Z}
. Let
{\boldsymbol{z}}
be a
\mathcal{Z}
-valued binary tree of depth
n
. Then with probability at least
1-\rho
over
\varepsilon
and for any
\alpha\geq 0

\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\left(\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{T}_{f({\boldsymbol{z}})}(\varepsilon)\right)~\lesssim~I_{\mathcal{F},{\boldsymbol{z}}}^{\alpha}+\sqrt{\frac{\log\frac{1}{\rho}}{n}}

(12)

In particular, the sequential Rademacher complexity is bounded by

\mathcal{R}_{n}(\mathcal{F},{\boldsymbol{z}})~\lesssim~\inf_{\alpha>0}\left(\alpha+\inf_{\supp\mu\subset\mathcal{F}({\boldsymbol{z}})}\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\boldsymbol{v}}\in\mathcal{F}({\boldsymbol{z}})\\
\varepsilon\in\{\pm 1\}^{n}\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\int_{\alpha}^{1}\sqrt{\log\frac{1}{\mu(B_{\delta}({\boldsymbol{v}},\varepsilon))}}d\delta\right)

(13)

The proof of Theorem
3
is somewhat technical and thus deferred to Appendix
A
. The proof rests on the following new martingale concentration inequality that can be viewed as a stronger version of
(
Bartlett et al. , 2008
, Lemma 2)
:

Lemma 4
.

Let
{\boldsymbol{v}}
be a
[-1,1]
-labelled binary tree of depth
n
and
\varepsilon_{1},\dots,\varepsilon_{n}
independent Rademacher variables. Then there is a constant
C
such that with probability at least
1-\rho
over the
\varepsilon_{t}
,

\frac{\left|\sum_{t=1}^{n}\varepsilon_{t}{\boldsymbol{v}}_{t}(\varepsilon)\right|}{\left|\left|{\boldsymbol{v}}(\varepsilon)\right|\right|\sqrt{\log\frac{1}{\rho}+\log\log\frac{e\sqrt{n}}{\left|\left|{\boldsymbol{v}}(\varepsilon)\right|\right|}}}\leq C

(14)

For the probabilist, an equivalent rephrasing of Lemma
4
is to let
M_{t}
be a martingale sequence whose differences are conditionally symmetric and bounded in absolute value by
1
. Let
[M]_{t}
be its quadratic variation process. Then the above says that with high probability,

\frac{\left|M_{n}\right|}{\sqrt{[M]_{n}\left(\log\frac{1}{\rho}+\log\log\frac{en}{[M]_{n}}\right)}}\lesssim 1

(15)

One way of thinking about this is to consider it as a non-asymptotic version of the martingale Laws of the Iterated Logarithm proved in
(
de la Peña, 1999
;
Bercu & Touati, 2008
)
.

Much like the classical case, structural results of the function class can help control the majorizing measure’s upper bound. One example of such a result concerns Lipschitz compositions:

Proposition 5
.

Let
\mathcal{F}\subseteq[0,1]^{\mathcal{Z}}
and let
\ell:[0,1]\to[0,1]
be
L
-Lipschitz. Then
I_{\ell\circ\mathcal{F},{\boldsymbol{z}}}^{\alpha}\leq LI_{\mathcal{F},{\boldsymbol{z}}}^{\alpha}
.

While majorizing measures in the classical case fully characterize the associated Gaussian process, constructing these multi-scale measures for a given class of functions at hand is notoriously difficult. In the following section, inspired by
(
Alon et al. , 2021
)
for the binary-valued case, we introduce a single-scale measure that is easier to analyze than the multi-scale construction.

3
Fractional Covers

As we just remarked, the multi-scale nature of the majorizing measure makes it difficult to apply the technique to practical function classes. Thus we introduce the fractional covering number for real-valued function classes as a single-scale alternative:

Definition 6
.

Let
{\boldsymbol{z}}
a binary,
\mathcal{Z}
-valued tree of depth
n
and let
\mathcal{F}\subset\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{Z}}
. A measure
\mu
on the space of binary real-valued trees of depth
n
is a fractional covering at scale
\delta
of size
\gamma
if for all
f\in\mathcal{F}
and
\varepsilon\in\{\pm 1\}^{n}
, we have

\mu\left(\left\{{\boldsymbol{v}}:\left|\left|{\boldsymbol{v}}(\varepsilon)-f({\boldsymbol{z}}(\varepsilon))\right|\right|^{2}\leq n\delta^{2}\right\}\right)\geq\frac{1}{\gamma}.

(16)

The fractional covering number
N^{\prime}(\mathcal{F},\delta,{\boldsymbol{z}})
of
\mathcal{F}
at scale
\delta
is the smallest
\gamma
such that there exists a fractional cover of
\mathcal{F}
at scale
\delta
of size
\gamma
. Let
N^{\prime}(\mathcal{F},\delta)=\sup_{\boldsymbol{z}}N^{\prime}(\mathcal{F},\delta,{\boldsymbol{z}})
.

We see immediately (and prove in Appendix
D
) that this new notion of size is controlled by the sequential covering number introduced in
(
Rakhlin et al. , 2010
)
:

Lemma 7
.

For any
{\boldsymbol{z}},\mathcal{F},\delta
, we have
N^{\prime}(\mathcal{F},\delta,{\boldsymbol{z}})\leq N(\mathcal{F},\delta,{\boldsymbol{z}})
.

Interestingly, at least in the classical case, the reverse inequality holds as well:

Lemma 8
.

Let
{\boldsymbol{z}}
be a tree whose labels are constant as a function of depth. Then
N\left(\mathcal{F},2\delta,{\boldsymbol{z}}\right)\leq N^{\prime}(\mathcal{F},\delta,{\boldsymbol{z}})\leq N(\mathcal{F},\delta,{\boldsymbol{z}})
.

The proof of Lemma
8
is deferred to Appendix
D
. Thus, at least in the classical case, the fractional covering number does not provide any improvement over the classical notion of covering number; whether such a result holds in the sequential case remains open, due to the lack of packing-covering duality in this setting.

To understand the connection between fractional covers and majorizing measures, we note that Definition
6
can be rephrased to say that
\mu_{\delta}
is a
\delta
-fractional cover of size
\gamma
if

\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}f\in\mathcal{F}\\
\varepsilon\in\{\pm 1\}^{n}\end{subarray}}\log\frac{1}{\mu_{\delta}\left(B_{\delta}(f({\boldsymbol{z}}),\varepsilon)\right)}=\log\gamma.

(17)

Applying Theorem
3
and setting
\alpha=0
for ease of exposition, we see that, after putting the supremum inside of the integral,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\mathbb{T}_{f}(\varepsilon)\right]

\displaystyle\lesssim\sqrt{n}\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}f\in\mathcal{F}\\
\varepsilon\in\left\{\pm 1\right\}^{n}\end{subarray}}\int_{0}^{1}\sqrt{\log\frac{1}{\mu(B_{\delta}(f({\boldsymbol{z}}),\varepsilon)}}d\delta

(18)

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{n}\int_{0}^{1}\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}f\in\mathcal{F}\\
\varepsilon\in\left\{\pm 1\right\}^{n}\end{subarray}}\sqrt{\log\frac{1}{\mu(B_{\delta}(f({\boldsymbol{z}}),\varepsilon))}}d\delta

(19)

for all measures
\mu
. The last integrand above
almost
looks like the left-hand side of (
17
) and so we might hope that we can replace this integrand with
\sqrt{\log N^{\prime}(\mathcal{F},\delta,{\boldsymbol{z}})}
. The problem with this last step is that the measure
\mu_{\delta}
of the fractional cover is allowed to depend on the scale while the majorizing measure is not; thus it is not obvious that a chaining bound with fractional covers follows from Theorem
3
. Fortunately, as the following proposition demonstrates, we are still able to control the majorizing measure by fractional covers and thus the above critique does not apply:

Proposition 9
.

Let
{\boldsymbol{z}}
be a
\mathcal{Z}
-valued binary tree of depth
n
and let
\mathcal{F}
be a class of real-valued functions on
\mathcal{Z}
. Then

I_{\mathcal{F},{\boldsymbol{z}}}^{\alpha}~\lesssim~\alpha+\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\int_{\alpha}^{1}\sqrt{\log N^{\prime}(\mathcal{F},\delta,{\boldsymbol{z}})}d\delta

(20)

In other words, fractional covers dominate majorizing measures, up to a constant.

A proof of Proposition
9
can be found in Appendix
D
. An immediate corollary of the above, which follows from combining Theorem
3
and Proposition
9
is

Corollary 10
.

Let
\mathcal{F}
be a separable
[0,1]
-valued function class on
\mathcal{Z}
. Let
{\boldsymbol{z}}
be a
\mathcal{Z}
-valued binary tree of depth
n
. Let
N^{\prime}(\mathcal{F},\delta)
be the fractional covering number at scale
\delta
. Then with probability at least
1-\rho
over
\varepsilon
, for all
\alpha\geq 0
,

\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\left(\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{T}_{f({\boldsymbol{z}})}(\varepsilon)\right)~\lesssim~\alpha+\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\int_{\alpha}^{1}\sqrt{\log N^{\prime}(\mathcal{F},\delta,{\boldsymbol{z}})}d\delta+\sqrt{\frac{\log\frac{1}{\rho}}{n}}.

(21)

In particular, the sequential Rademacher complexity is bounded by the Dudley integral:

\mathcal{R}_{n}(\mathcal{F},{\boldsymbol{z}})~\lesssim~\inf_{\alpha>0}\left(\alpha+\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\int_{\alpha}^{1}\sqrt{\log N^{\prime}(\mathcal{F},\delta,{\boldsymbol{z}})}d\delta\right).

(22)

If we apply Lemma
7
to Corollary
10
, we recover the chaining bound with respect to sequential non-fractional covers of
(
Rakhlin et al. , 2015
)
. As a first application of our results, using the techniques of
Rakhlin et al. , 2015
we get a high-probability uniform deviations bound:

Corollary 11
.

Let
Z_{1},\dots,Z_{t},\dots
be a sequence of
\mathcal{Z}
-valued random variables adapted to a filtration
\mathcal{A}_{t}
and let
\mathcal{F}
be a
[0,1]
-valued function class on
\mathcal{Z}
. Then with probability at least
1-4\rho
,

\displaystyle\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\left|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{t=1}^{n}f(Z_{t})-\mathbb{E}[f(Z_{t})|\mathcal{A}_{t}]\right|

\displaystyle~\lesssim~\inf_{\alpha>0}I_{\mathcal{F}}^{\alpha}+\sqrt{\frac{\log\frac{1}{\rho}}{n}}

(23)

\displaystyle~\lesssim~\inf_{\alpha>0}\left(\alpha+\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\int_{\alpha}^{1}\sqrt{\log N^{\prime}(\mathcal{F},\delta)}d\delta\right)+\sqrt{\frac{\log\frac{1}{\rho}}{n}}

(24)

Corollary
11
significantly improves on the results of
(
Rakhlin et al. , 2015
, Section 7)
in two ways: first, we chain with respect majorizing measures and fractional covering numbers which are guaranteed to be at least as good as sequential covering numbers by Lemma
7
; second, our bound removes an extraneous factor polynomial in
\log n
and now matches the form of uniform concentration bounds from the classical regime. In the following section, we show how combinatorial properties of
\mathcal{F}
can help us bound fractional covering numbers.

4
Fractional Covering and Fat-Shattering Dimension

Thus far, we have been focused on somewhat abstract results, providing upper bounds on stochastic processes in terms of majorizing measures and fractional covering numbers without any evidence that these new quantities improve on the sequential covering numbers introduced in
Rakhlin et al. , 2010
; here, we rectify that and focus on how to control the fractional covering numbers with combinatorial and structural properties of the function class.

One of the cornerstones in classical learning theory was the development of Vapnik-Chernovenkis theory and how it relates to uniform convergence over a binary-valued function class
(
Vapnik & Chervonenkis, 1981
)
. This theory was then extended to real-valued functions with the development of fat-shattering or scale-sensitive dimension
(
Bartlett et al. , 1996
;
Kearns & Schapire, 1994
)
. In the online world,
Littlestone, 1988
introduced an analogue of the VC dimension and
Rakhlin et al. , 2015
extended this definition to a sequential notion of the fat-shattering dimension. Here, we recall the definition of the fat-shattering dimension and then use it to bound the fractional covering numbers independently of
n
in a sequential analogue of the results of
Mendelson & Vershynin, 2003
. We then show that, when integrated in the chaining upper bound of Corollary
10
, we can further improve the bound and provide a sequential analogue of the results of
Rudelson & Vershynin, 2006
, which is tight in the Donsker case. Finally, we apply these results to prove a tight (up to constants) Lipschitz contraction bound on worst-case Rademacher complexity, improving on the structural results of
Rakhlin et al. , 2015
.

We begin by recalling the fat-shattering dimension from
(
Rakhlin et al. , 2015
, Definition 7)
:

Definition 12
.

Let

a

(25)

The (sequential) fat-shattering dimension

is the maximal

of depth

that shatters

A sequential version of the Sauer-Shelah lemma was also proven in

and this was used to bound the

where

denotes the covering number with respect to

. As noted

, this method cannot provide

regime because the

covering numbers cannot be independent of dimension in general. In the classical setting, Dudley extraction, pioneered in

, allows for dimension-independent bounds, but, due to the lack of covering-packing duality in the sequential case, this method cannot be applied in the online regime. A major advantage of the fractional covering number is that we are able to eliminate the dependence on
n
and control the

covering numbers by the fat-shattering dimension. We have the following bound:

Theorem 13

Let

such that for all
\delta>0
, and all trees
{\boldsymbol{z}}

N^{\prime}(\mathcal{F},\delta,{\boldsymbol{z}})\leq\left(\frac{C}{\delta}\right)^{3\mathsf{fat}_{c\delta}(\mathcal{F})}

(27)

The details of the proof of Theorem

, but we provide a sketch here.

We follow
Rakhlin et al. , 2015
and discretize the unit interval into sub-intervals of length proportional to

into one that takes on finitely many values. Using this transformation, it suffices to bound the fractional covering number of a simpler class of functions

by

, i.e., it suffices to prove

Proposition 14
.

Suppose that
\mathcal{F}:\mathcal{Z}\to\mathcal{Y}=\{0,1,\dots,m\}
and that
\mathsf{fat}_{2}(\mathcal{F})=d
. Then there is a universal constant

such that
N^{\prime}\left(\mathcal{F},\frac{2}{3},{\boldsymbol{z}}\right)\leq\left(Cm\right)^{3d}

We provide full details in Appendix
B
, but provide a sketch here:

Sketch.

We fix the tree
{\boldsymbol{z}}
and construct the fractional cover recursively. The idea of the construction is to partition the function class
\mathcal{F}
into sub-classes
\mathcal{F}_{j}
taking the value

on the root. Lemma

, tells us that there are at most two such sub-classes such that
\mathsf{fat}_{2}(\mathcal{F}_{j})=\mathsf{fat}_{2}(\mathcal{F}_{j^{\prime}})=\mathsf{fat}_{2}(\mathcal{F})
and that
\left|j-j^{\prime}\right|\leq 1
. Let

be the minimal

such that
\mathsf{fat}_{2}(\mathcal{F}_{j})=\mathsf{fat}_{2}(\mathcal{F})
and notice that
j^{*}
and

are potentially the only values of

such that
\mathsf{fat}_{2}(\mathcal{F}_{j})=\mathsf{fat}_{2}(\mathcal{F})
. We construct the fractional cover

recursively as the following mixture: with probability

, where

is a small number to be specified, we label the root as

and label the two subtrees of the root independently, using the fractional cover with respect to the complete class
\mathcal{F}
. Otherwise, with probability

j
and each of the two subtrees independently from the fractional covers with respect to the subclass
\mathcal{F}_{j}
, which satisfies
\mathsf{fat}_{2}(\mathcal{F}_{j})\leq\mathsf{fat}_{2}(\mathcal{F})-1
.
An induction proof relying on the recursive construction, found in Appendix

as Lemma

\mathsf{fat}_{2}(\mathcal{F})\leq d
and

is as constructed above, then for any integer

\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}f\in\mathcal{F}\\
\varepsilon\in\{\pm 1\}^{n}\end{subarray}}\mu\left(\left\{{\boldsymbol{v}}|\sum_{t=0}^{n}\mathbf{1}_{\left|{\boldsymbol{v}}_{t}(\varepsilon)-f({\boldsymbol{z}}(_{t}\varepsilon))\right|\geq 1}\leq k\right\}\right)\geq(1-p)^{n+1+k}\left(\frac{p}{m}\right)^{d}\binom{k+d}{d}\kern 5.0pt.

Setting

,

, the right hand side is at least

. By the construction, if

then it is at most
\frac{1}{2}
and otherwise,
\left|{\boldsymbol{v}}_{t}(\varepsilon)-f({\boldsymbol{z}}_{t}(\varepsilon))\right|\leq m

{\boldsymbol{v}}
is in the above set, then
\left|\left|{\boldsymbol{v}}(\varepsilon)-f({\boldsymbol{z}}(\varepsilon))\right|\right|^{2}\leq m^{2}\delta n+\frac{1}{4}(1-\delta)n

∎

If we plug the result of Theorem

\mathcal{R}_{n}(\mathcal{F})=\sup_{\mathsf{depth}({\boldsymbol{z}})=n}\mathcal{R}_{n}(\mathcal{F},{\boldsymbol{z}})~\lesssim~\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\int_{0}^{1}\sqrt{\mathsf{fat}_{\delta}(\mathcal{F})\log\frac{1}{\delta}}d\delta

Let

There exist universal constants

\mathcal{F}
be a class of functions from
\mathcal{Z}

sketch.

Similarly to the proof of Theorem

is a

recursively, in the same spirit as the proof of Proposition

\mathcal{F}
into sub-classes
\mathcal{F}_{j}

While admittedly still somewhat technical, we observe that the proof of Proposition

(

)
.

Miraculously, for function classes
\mathcal{F}
that are not too complex, Proposition

gives a tight bound on the worst-case sequential Rademacher complexity, up to constants. In order to state such a result, we need to define what it means for a function class to be simple.

Definition 17
.

For a constant
c>0
and
0\leq p<2
, we say that a function class is
(c,p)
-bounded if for all
0<\delta<1
,

\mathsf{fat}_{\delta}(\mathcal{F})\leq c\delta^{-p}.

(34)

We note that many practical function classes are
(c,p)
-bounded; for example, Littlestone classes satisfy the above for
c=\mathsf{Ldim}(\mathcal{F})
and

. We have the following corollary:

Corollary 18
.

Let
\mathcal{F}
be a
(c,p)
-bounded class of functions. Then

\int_{0}^{1}\sqrt{\mathsf{fat}_{\delta}(\mathcal{F})}d\delta~\lesssim~\sqrt{n}\mathcal{R}_{n}(\mathcal{F})~\lesssim~\int_{0}^{1}\sqrt{\mathsf{fat}_{\delta}(\mathcal{F})}d\delta

(35)

where the constants depend on
\mathcal{F}
only through
c,p
. The upper bound holds with no restriction on the complexity of the class
\mathcal{F}
.

Thus for many classes of interest, we have a tight characterization of sequential Rademacher complexity, matching the characterization in the offline case. A challenging open problem remains, however: in the classical setting, majorizing measures provide both an upper and lower bound on the supremum of a Gaussian process. While our results can be extended to give an upper bound for the sequential Gaussian complexity, where the path is determined by the signs of independent Gaussian multipliers, the lower bound remains to be shown.

As an application of our results, we prove a tight Lipschitz contraction statement for worst-case Rademacher complexity in the
(c,p)
-bounded regime that is dimension-independent, the first such statement in the online case.

Corollary 19
.

Let
\mathcal{F}
be a
(c,p)
-bounded class of functions. If
\ell
is an
L
-Lipschitz function, we have

\mathcal{R}_{n}\left(\ell\circ\mathcal{F}\right)~\lesssim~L\mathcal{R}_{n}(\mathcal{F})

(36)

where, again, the constant depends on
\mathcal{F}
only through
c
and
p
.

Importantly, the statement of Corollary

is one about
worst-case
Rademacher complexity as opposed to
tree-dependent
Rademacher complexity. A similar statement with a factor polynomial in

was proved as
(
Rakhlin et al. , 2015
, Lemma 7)
. Whether an analogous result holds in the tree-dependent case remains a challenging open question.

5
Sample Complexity of Online Learning

We finish this paper with a discussion of online learning, showing that our techniques lead to sharp estimates on minimax regret.

The online supervised learning problem is defined as follows. We fix a class of functions
\mathcal{F}\subseteq[-1,1]^{\mathcal{X}}
, known to the learner. The online prediction problem proceeds over
n
rounds. On round
t
, the learner observes
x_{t}\in\mathcal{X}
, makes a prediction
\widehat{y}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}
, and observes the outcome
y_{t}\in\mathcal{Y}\subseteq\mathbb{R}
. Predictions may be randomized, in which case the learner selects a distribution
q_{t}
on round
t
and draws
\widehat{y}_{t}\sim q_{t}
. Regret is defined as

\displaystyle\mathsf{Reg}_{n}(\mathcal{F})=\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{n}\sum_{t=1}^{n}\ell(\widehat{y}_{t},y_{t})-\inf_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{t=1}^{n}\ell(f(x_{t}),y_{t})\right].

(37)

In turn, the minimax regret is defined as

\displaystyle V_{n}(\mathcal{F})=\min_{\tau}\max_{\pi}~\mathsf{Reg}_{n}(\mathcal{F})

(38)

where minimum is taken over all randomized strategies of the learner, and maximum is over all strategies of Nature for selecting the sequence (potentially adaptively and adversarially). While this minimax object appears to be complicated, by writing down a repeated sequence of minima and maxima per time step, it is possible to upper bound the minimax regret by a supremum of a collection of martingales indexed by
\mathcal{F}

(
Abernethy et al. , 2009
;
Rakhlin et al. , 2010
)
. In particular, it is shown in
(
Rakhlin et al. , 2010
)
that for binary classification with
\mathcal{Y}=\{0,1\}
,
\mathcal{F}\subseteq\mathcal{Y}^{\mathcal{X}}
, and
\ell(a,b)=\mathbb{I}\{a\neq b\}
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{n}(\mathcal{F})\leq V_{n}(\mathcal{F})\leq 2\mathcal{R}(\mathcal{F})

(39)

where
\mathcal{R}_{n}(\mathcal{F})=\sup_{{\boldsymbol{x}}}\mathcal{R}_{n}(\mathcal{F},{\boldsymbol{x}})
. The tight characterization (
39
) also holds for prediction with absolute value loss (
\ell(a,b)=|a-b|
,
\mathcal{Y}=[-1,1],
and
\mathcal{F}\subseteq\mathcal{Y}^{\mathcal{X}}
), as well as for prediction with linear loss (
\ell(a,b)=-ab
,
\mathcal{Y}=[-1,1],\mathcal{F}\subseteq\mathcal{Y}^{\mathcal{X}}
)
(
Rakhlin et al. , 2010
)
.

For the online learning problems mentioned above, we conclude that

\displaystyle V_{n}(\mathcal{F})~\lesssim~\inf_{\alpha\geq 0}\left\{\alpha+\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\int_{\alpha}^{1}\sqrt{\mathsf{fat}_{\delta}(\mathcal{F})}d\delta\right\},

(40)

improving on the corresponding estimates in
(
Rakhlin et al. , 2010
)
. In particular, from Corollary
18
, for classes satisfying Definition
17
, the minimax regret

\displaystyle V_{n}(\mathcal{F})~\asymp~\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\int_{0}^{1}\sqrt{\mathsf{fat}_{\delta}(\mathcal{F})}d\delta~\asymp~\sqrt{\frac{c}{n}}.

(41)

In particular, for binary
classification, this recovers the sharp result of
(
Alon et al. , 2021
)
, as
\mathsf{fat}_{\delta}(\mathcal{F})
reduces to the Littlestone dimension in this case. More generally, (
40
) provides a succinct and sharp characterization of optimal regret in terms of combinatorial parameters of the class. Using the techniques developed in this paper, we can sharpen and remove extraneous logarithmic factors in a number of bounds in
(
Rakhlin et al. , 2010
)
. We mention that for online regression with square loss, optimal regret is characterized in terms of a localized notion of sequential Rademacher complexity (offset complexity), and the study of its behavior in terms of the notions introduced in this paper is left for future work.

is summable, then pulling the square out of the log factor tells us that with probability at least
1-\frac{\rho}{6}
, we have, uniformly in
j

\mu\left(\left\{{\boldsymbol{v}}\colon\left|\mathbb{T}_{\boldsymbol{v}}(\varepsilon)\right|>C\sqrt{n\log\frac{\gamma_{j}}{\rho}}\right\}\right)\leq\frac{1}{\gamma_{j}}

(60)

Now we look at the second event. The identical argument serves to allow us to only consider
\gamma_{j}
. We now apply Lemma
4
to Lemma
21
instead of Lemma
20
. In particular, note that for any
\gamma_{j}
, and any
{\boldsymbol{v}},{\boldsymbol{v}}^{\prime}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\left|\mathbb{T}_{{\boldsymbol{v}},{\boldsymbol{v}}^{\prime}}(\varepsilon)\right|>C\left|\left|{\boldsymbol{v}}(\varepsilon)-{\boldsymbol{v}}^{\prime}(\varepsilon)\right|\right|\sqrt{\log\frac{6\gamma_{j}^{2}}{\rho}+\log\log\frac{6\sqrt{n}}{\left|\left|{\boldsymbol{v}}(\varepsilon)-{\boldsymbol{v}}^{\prime}(\varepsilon)\right|\right|}}\right)\leq\frac{\rho}{6\gamma_{j}^{2}}

(61)

by Lemma
4
. Thus, applying Lemma
21
with the measure
\nu
, we have that with probability at least
1-\frac{\rho}{\gamma_{j}}
,

\displaystyle\nu\left(\left\{({\boldsymbol{v}},{\boldsymbol{v}}^{\prime})\colon\left|\mathbb{T}_{{\boldsymbol{v}},{\boldsymbol{v}}^{\prime}}(\varepsilon)\right|>C\left|\left|{\boldsymbol{v}}(\varepsilon)-{\boldsymbol{v}}^{\prime}(\varepsilon)\right|\right|\sqrt{\log\frac{\gamma_{j}^{2}}{\rho}+\log\log\frac{3\sqrt{n}}{\left|\left|{\boldsymbol{v}}(\varepsilon)-{\boldsymbol{v}}^{\prime}(\varepsilon)\right|\right|}}\right\}\right)\leq\frac{1}{\gamma_{j}}

(62)

Applying a union bound and taking out the square from the logarithm and putting it into the constant
C
concludes the proof.
∎

For the remainder, assume that the event of Lemma
22
holds and we will show how to bound the supremum over
\mathbb{T}_{f({\boldsymbol{z}})}
.
Define
\delta_{j}=2^{-j}
and for any
f,\varepsilon
, define
\gamma_{j}(f,\varepsilon)=1/\mu(B_{\delta_{j}}(f,\varepsilon))
. Let
N
be the maximal integer such that
\delta^{N}\geq 2\alpha
.

Notice that it suffices to prove that (under the event of Lemma
22
), for any
f\in\mathcal{F}
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{T}_{f}(\varepsilon)

\displaystyle\leq C\left(\sqrt{n\log\frac{1}{\rho}}+n\delta_{N}+\sqrt{n}\sum_{j=0}^{N}\delta_{j}\sqrt{\log\gamma_{j}(f,\varepsilon)}\right)

(63)

\displaystyle\leq C\left(\sqrt{n\log\frac{1}{\rho}}+n\alpha+\sqrt{n}\sum_{j=0}^{N}2^{-j}\sqrt{\log\frac{1}{\mu(B_{2^{-j}}(f,\varepsilon))}}\right).

(64)

Define

\displaystyle r_{j}(f,\varepsilon)=C\delta_{j}\sqrt{n}\sqrt{\log\frac{\gamma_{j+1}(f,\varepsilon)}{\rho}+\log\log\frac{3}{\delta_{j}}}

(65)

for a sufficiently large
C>0
and
j\geq 0
integer. We write
r_{j}
and
\gamma_{j}
when
f,\varepsilon
are obvious from context.
We have that the following holds for any
f,\varepsilon
and
j\geq 1
(we refer to these inequalities as
(\dagger)
):

\displaystyle\mu\left(\left\{{\boldsymbol{v}}\colon\left|\mathbb{T}_{\boldsymbol{v}}(\varepsilon)\right|>r_{0}(f,\varepsilon)\right\}\right)\leq\frac{1}{4\gamma_{0}(f,\varepsilon)}

(66)

\displaystyle\nu\left(\left\{({\boldsymbol{v}},{\boldsymbol{v}}^{\prime})\colon\left|\mathbb{T}_{{\boldsymbol{v}},{\boldsymbol{v}}^{\prime}}(\varepsilon)\right|>r_{j}(f,\varepsilon)\text{ and }\left|\left|{\boldsymbol{v}}(\varepsilon)-{\boldsymbol{v}}(\varepsilon^{\prime})\right|\right|\leq 2\delta_{j}\sqrt{n}\right\}\right)

(67)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{9\gamma_{j}(f,\varepsilon)\gamma_{j+1}(f,\varepsilon)}.

(68)

Indeed, the first inequality is obtained from Lemma
22
by substituting
\gamma=4\gamma_{0}(f,\varepsilon)
and the second inequality is obtained by taking
\gamma=9\gamma_{j}(f,\varepsilon)\gamma_{j+1}(f,\varepsilon)\leq 9\gamma_{j+1}(f,\varepsilon)^{2}
.

Our goal is to construct a chain of elements
{\boldsymbol{v}}_{f,0},\dots,{\boldsymbol{v}}_{f,N}
that provide finer and finer approximations for
f({\boldsymbol{z}})
on the path
\varepsilon
, and obtain the bound

\mathbb{T}_{f({\boldsymbol{z}})}\leq\mathbb{T}_{{\boldsymbol{v}}_{f,0}}+\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}\mathbb{T}_{{\boldsymbol{v}}_{f,j},{\boldsymbol{v}}_{f,j+1}}+\mathbb{T}_{{\boldsymbol{v}}_{f,N},f({\boldsymbol{z}})}.

The approximations will be chosen from the sets
A^{\prime}_{j}(f,\varepsilon)
defined below:

\displaystyle A_{j}(f,\varepsilon)

\displaystyle=\left\{{\boldsymbol{v}}|\left|\left|{\boldsymbol{v}}(\varepsilon)-f({\boldsymbol{z}}(\varepsilon))\right|\right|\leq\sqrt{n}\delta_{j}\right\}

(69)

\displaystyle A_{j}^{\prime}(f,\varepsilon)

\displaystyle=\left\{{\boldsymbol{v}}\in A_{j}(f,\varepsilon)\colon\mu\left(\left\{{\boldsymbol{v}}^{\prime}|\left|\mathbb{T}_{{\boldsymbol{v}},{\boldsymbol{v}}^{\prime}}(\varepsilon)\right|>r_{j}(f,\varepsilon)\text{ and }\left|\left|{\boldsymbol{v}}(\varepsilon)-{\boldsymbol{v}}^{\prime}(\varepsilon)\right|\right|\leq 2\delta_{j}\sqrt{n}\right\}\right)\leq\frac{1}{3\gamma_{j+1}}\right\}

(70)

As a first step, we would like to lower bound
\mu(A_{j}^{\prime}(f,\varepsilon))
assuming we have
\varepsilon
such that
(
\dagger
)
holds.
By construction,
\mu(A_{j}(f,\varepsilon))\geq\frac{1}{\gamma_{j}(f,\varepsilon)}
.
For any
{\boldsymbol{v}}
, let

B({\boldsymbol{v}})=\left\{{\boldsymbol{v}}^{\prime}|\left|\mathbb{T}_{{\boldsymbol{v}},{\boldsymbol{v}}^{\prime}}(\varepsilon)\right|>r_{j}(f,\varepsilon)\text{ and }\left|\left|{\boldsymbol{v}}(\varepsilon)-{\boldsymbol{v}}^{\prime}(\varepsilon)\right|\right|\leq 2\delta_{j}\sqrt{n}\right\}

(71)

and let

B=\bigcup_{\boldsymbol{v}}\bigcup_{{\boldsymbol{v}}^{\prime}\in B({\boldsymbol{v}})}\left\{({\boldsymbol{v}},{\boldsymbol{v}}^{\prime})\right\}

(72)

Let

\widetilde{B}=\left\{{\boldsymbol{v}}|\mu(B({\boldsymbol{v}}))>\frac{1}{3\gamma_{j+1}}\right\}

(73)

From
(
\dagger
)
, we know that
\nu(B)\leq\frac{1}{9\gamma_{j}\gamma_{j+1}}
. By construction of
\nu
, we have

\displaystyle\nu(B)\geq\mu\left(B({\boldsymbol{v}})|{\boldsymbol{v}}\in\widetilde{B}\right)\mu\left(\widetilde{B}\right)

(74)

By construction, if
{\boldsymbol{v}}\in\widetilde{B}
, then

\mu\left(B({\boldsymbol{v}})\right)\geq\frac{1}{3\gamma_{j+1}}

(75)

Thus we have

\displaystyle\mu\left(\widetilde{B}\right)\leq\frac{\nu(B)}{\mu\left(B({\boldsymbol{v}})|{\boldsymbol{v}}\in\widetilde{B}\right)}\leq\frac{\frac{1}{9\gamma_{j}\gamma_{j+1}}}{\frac{1}{3\gamma_{j+1}}}=\frac{1}{3\gamma_{j}}

(76)

Thus we see that

\mu(A_{j}^{\prime}(f,\varepsilon))\geq\mu(A_{j}(f,\varepsilon))-\mu(\widetilde{B})\geq\frac{1}{\gamma_{j}}-\frac{1}{3\gamma_{j}}=\frac{2}{3\gamma_{j}}\kern 5.0pt.

(77)

As a final remark before we construct the chain, note that if
{\boldsymbol{v}}\in A_{j}(f,\varepsilon)
and
{\boldsymbol{v}}^{\prime}\in A_{j+1}(f,\varepsilon)
, then by the triangle inequality,

\left|\left|{\boldsymbol{v}}(\varepsilon)-{\boldsymbol{v}}^{\prime}(\varepsilon)\right|\right|\leq\left|\left|{\boldsymbol{v}}(\varepsilon)-f({\boldsymbol{z}}(\varepsilon))\right|\right|+\left|\left|{\boldsymbol{v}}^{\prime}(\varepsilon)-f({\boldsymbol{z}}(\varepsilon))\right|\right|\leq\delta_{j}\sqrt{n}+\delta_{j+1}\sqrt{n}\leq 2\delta_{j}\sqrt{n}

(78)

We are now ready to construct the chain. Fix
\varepsilon
such that
(
\dagger
)
holds. For notational simplicity, we suppress the dependence of
{\boldsymbol{v}}_{f,j,\varepsilon}
on
\varepsilon
and simply write
{\boldsymbol{v}}_{f,j}
; this causes no confusion as we have fixed
\varepsilon
. For any
f
, we have

\displaystyle\mu\left(A_{j}^{\prime}(f,\varepsilon)\cap\left\{{\boldsymbol{v}}|\left|\mathbb{T}_{\boldsymbol{v}}(\varepsilon)\right|>r_{j}(f,\varepsilon)\right\}^{c}\right)\geq\frac{2}{3\gamma_{j}}-\frac{1}{3\gamma_{j}}=\frac{1}{3\gamma_{j}}>0

(79)

Thus, letting
j=0
, we see that there exists a
{\boldsymbol{v}}_{f,0}\in A_{0}^{\prime}(f,\varepsilon)
such that

\left|\mathbb{T}_{{\boldsymbol{v}}_{f,0}}(\varepsilon)\right|\leq r_{0}(f,\varepsilon).

(80)

Now suppose that we have chosen
{\boldsymbol{v}}_{f,j}\in A_{j}^{\prime}(f,\varepsilon)
. We wish to select an element
{\boldsymbol{v}}_{f,j+1}\in A_{j+1}^{\prime}(f,\varepsilon)
such that
\left|\mathbb{T}_{{\boldsymbol{v}}_{f,j},{\boldsymbol{v}}_{f,j+1}}(\varepsilon)\right|
is small. To see that this is possible, we remark that, as
{\boldsymbol{v}}_{f,j}\in A_{j}^{\prime}(f,\varepsilon)
, we have by definition

\displaystyle\mu\left(\left\{{\boldsymbol{v}}^{\prime}|\left|\mathbb{T}_{{\boldsymbol{v}}_{f,j},{\boldsymbol{v}}^{\prime}}(\varepsilon)\right|>r_{j}(f,\varepsilon)\text{ and }\left|\left|{\boldsymbol{v}}_{f,j}(\varepsilon)-{\boldsymbol{v}}^{\prime}(\varepsilon)\right|\right|\leq 2\delta_{j}\sqrt{n}\right\}\right)\leq\frac{1}{3\gamma_{j+1}}

(81)

But we have already seen that

\displaystyle\mu(A_{j+1}^{\prime}(f,\varepsilon))\geq\frac{2}{3\gamma_{j+1}}

(82)

and as noted, for any
{\boldsymbol{v}}^{\prime}\in A_{j+1}^{\prime}(f,\varepsilon)
,
\left|\left|{\boldsymbol{v}}_{f,j}(\varepsilon)-{\boldsymbol{v}}^{\prime}(\varepsilon)\right|\right|\leq 2\delta_{j}\sqrt{n}
. Thus
\mu
takes measure at least
\frac{1}{3\gamma_{j+1}}
on the set of such desirable next links in the chain and so such a
{\boldsymbol{v}}_{f,j+1}
exists. Thus for any
f
, on this
\varepsilon
, we have a chain
{\boldsymbol{v}}_{f,j}
for
j\geq 0
such that
{\boldsymbol{v}}_{f,j}\in A_{j}^{\prime}(f,\varepsilon)
. Note that, by (
78
) and the way we have constructed the chain, we have that for all
f
,

\left|\mathbb{T}_{{\boldsymbol{v}}_{f,j},{\boldsymbol{v}}_{f,j+1}}(\varepsilon)\right|\leq r_{j}(f,\varepsilon).

(83)

We also have for any
f
that

\left|\mathbb{T}_{{\boldsymbol{v}}_{f,0}}\right|\leq r_{0}(f,\varepsilon).

(84)

Thus we see that on this
\varepsilon
, for every
f
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{T}_{f}(\varepsilon)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{T}_{{\boldsymbol{v}}_{f,0,\varepsilon}}(\varepsilon)+\sum_{N\geq j\geq 0}\mathbb{T}_{{\boldsymbol{v}}_{f,j,\varepsilon},{\boldsymbol{v}}_{f,j+1,\varepsilon}}(\varepsilon)+\mathbb{T}_{f({\boldsymbol{z}}),{\boldsymbol{v}}_{f,N,\varepsilon}}(\varepsilon)

(85)

\displaystyle\leq r_{0}(f,\varepsilon)+\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}r_{j}(f,\varepsilon)+Cn\delta_{n}(f,\varepsilon)

(86)

\displaystyle\leq C\sqrt{n\log\frac{1}{\rho}}+\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}C\sqrt{n}\left(\sqrt{\log\gamma_{j}(f,\varepsilon)}+\sqrt{\log\log\frac{3}{\delta_{j}}}\right)\delta_{j}+\delta_{N}(f,\varepsilon)n

(87)

\displaystyle\leq C\sqrt{n\log\frac{1}{\rho}}+\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}C\sqrt{n}\sqrt{\log\gamma_{j}(f,\varepsilon)}\delta_{j}+\delta_{N}(f,\varepsilon)n

(88)

where the inequality
\mathbb{T}_{f({\boldsymbol{z}}),{\boldsymbol{v}}_{f,N,\varepsilon}}(\varepsilon)
follows from Cauchy-schwarz and the fact that
{\boldsymbol{v}}_{f,N}\in A_{N}(f,\varepsilon)
. As
(
\dagger
)
occurs with probability at least
1-\rho
, we have with the same probability that the above is bounded. This concludes the proof.

Lemma 23

(
Rakhlin et al. , 2015
)
.

Let
{\boldsymbol{z}}
be a
\mathcal{Z}
-valued binary tree,
\mathcal{F}
a class of functions
\mathcal{Z}\to\{0,1,\dots,m\}
,
{\boldsymbol{z}}_{0}
the root of the tree, and
\mathcal{F}_{j}=\{f\in\mathcal{F}|f({\boldsymbol{z}}_{0})=j\}
. Let
r\geq 1
be an integer. If
j,j^{\prime}\in\{0,\dots,m\}
such that
\mathsf{fat}_{r}(\mathcal{F}_{j})=\mathsf{fat}_{r}(\mathcal{F}_{j^{\prime}})=\mathsf{fat}_{r}(\mathcal{F})
, then
|j-j^{\prime}|<r
. In particular, there can be at most
r
values of
j
such that
\mathsf{fat}_{r}(\mathcal{F}_{j})=\mathsf{fat}_{r}(\mathcal{F})
.

Proof.

Suppose that
j,j^{\prime}\in\{0,\dots,m\}
such that
j\geq j^{\prime}+r
. Let
{\boldsymbol{z}}^{(-1)}
and
{\boldsymbol{z}}^{(1)}
denote trees of depth
\mathsf{fat}_{r}(\mathcal{F})
that shatter at scale
r
the classes
\mathcal{F}_{j}
and
\mathcal{F}_{j^{\prime}}
. Then, we can construct a tree of depth
\mathsf{fat}_{r}(\mathcal{F})+1
that
r
-shatters
\mathcal{F}
by taking
z_{0}
as a root and taking
z^{(\pm 1)}
as subtrees of the root. This contradicts the fact that
\mathsf{fat}_{r}(\mathcal{F})
is the maximal depth of a tree that shatters
\mathcal{F}
at scale
r
. The result follows.

In order to prove the claim, for a depth

\varepsilon\in\{\pm 1\}^{n}\end{subarray}}\mu\left(\left\{{\boldsymbol{v}}\colon\sum_{t=0}^{n}\mathbf{1}_{\left\{|{\boldsymbol{v}}_{t}(\varepsilon)-f({\boldsymbol{z}}_{t}(\varepsilon))|\geq 1\right\}}\leq k\right\}\right)

, we note that for

then

We now prove the technical lemma used above:

Lemma 24
.

Let
\phi(k,d,n)
be as defined in the above proof. Then for all
0<p<1
, and
n\geq-1
(where
n=-1
corresponds to an empty tree),

\phi(k,d,n)\geq(1-p)^{n+1+k}\left(\frac{p}{m-1}\right)^{d}\binom{k+d}{d}

(95)

Proof.

We prove the result by induction on
n
.
If
n=-1
then the left hand side of (
95
) equals
1
, while the right hand side equals

(1-p)^{k}\left(\frac{p}{m-1}\right)^{d}\binom{k+d}{d}\leq(1-p)^{k}p^{d}\binom{k+d}{d}\leq\sum_{\ell=0}^{k+d}(1-p)^{k+d-\ell}p^{\ell}\binom{k+d}{\ell}\\
=((1-p)+p)^{k+d}=1.

Thus, we may assume that
n\geq 0
. We prove the following recursive formula:

\phi(k,d,n)\geq\min\begin{pmatrix}(1-p)\phi(k,d,n-1),\\
\begin{cases}(1-p)\phi(k-1,d,n-1)+\frac{p}{m-1}\phi(k,d-1,n-1)&\text{if }k,d>0\\
\frac{p}{m-1}\phi(k,d-1,n-1)&\text{if }k=0,d>0\\
\infty&ifd=0\end{cases}\end{pmatrix}

To see this, consider a fixed
f,\varepsilon
. We divide into cases:

If
f({\boldsymbol{z}}_{0})\in\{j^{*},j^{*}+1\}
.

Recall that
\mu_{{\boldsymbol{z}},\mathcal{F}}
samples
\mu_{{\boldsymbol{z}},\mathcal{F},j^{*}+1/2}
with probability
1-p
. For any
{\boldsymbol{v}}\in\mathrm{support}(\mu_{{\boldsymbol{z}},\mathcal{F},j^{*}+1/2})
it holds that
{\boldsymbol{v}}_{0}=j^{*}+1/2
and so
\left|{\boldsymbol{v}}_{0}-f({\boldsymbol{z}}_{0})\right|\leq\frac{1}{2}<1
. Hence,

\mu_{{\boldsymbol{z}},\mathcal{F}}\left(\left\{{\boldsymbol{v}}\colon\sum_{t=0}^{n}\mathbf{1}_{\left\{|{\boldsymbol{v}}_{t}(\varepsilon)-f({\boldsymbol{z}}_{t}(\varepsilon))|\geq 1\right\}}\leq k\right\}\right)\\
\geq(1-p)\mu_{{\boldsymbol{z}},\mathcal{F},j^{*}+1/2}\left(\left\{{\boldsymbol{v}}\colon\sum_{t=0}^{n}\mathbf{1}_{\left\{|{\boldsymbol{v}}_{t}(\varepsilon)-f({\boldsymbol{z}}_{t}(\varepsilon))|\geq 1\right\}}\leq k\right\}\right)\geq(1-p)\phi(k,d,n-1).

If
f({\boldsymbol{z}}_{0})=j\not\in\{j^{*},j^{*}+1\}
and

.

Here, differently from the previous case, for
{\boldsymbol{v}}\in\mathrm{support}(\mu_{{\boldsymbol{z}},\mathcal{F},j^{*}+1/2})

\left|{\boldsymbol{v}}_{0}-f({\boldsymbol{z}}_{0})\right|\geq 1
. Additionally, recall that
\mu_{{\boldsymbol{z}},\mathcal{F}}
samples
\mu_{{\boldsymbol{z}},\mathcal{F}_{j},j}
with probability
\frac{p}{m-1}
. For any
{\boldsymbol{v}}\in\mathrm{support}(\mu_{{\boldsymbol{z}},\mathcal{F}_{j},j})
, it holds that
{\boldsymbol{v}}_{0}=f({\boldsymbol{z}}_{0})
. Further, recall that
\mathsf{fat}_{2}(\mathcal{F}_{j})\leq\mathsf{fat}_{2}(\mathcal{F})-1
. Hence, we see that in this case,

\mu_{{\boldsymbol{z}},\mathcal{F}}\left(\left\{{\boldsymbol{v}}\colon\sum_{t=0}^{n}\mathbf{1}_{\left\{|{\boldsymbol{v}}_{t}(\varepsilon)-f({\boldsymbol{z}}_{t}(\varepsilon))|\geq 1\right\}}\leq k\right\}\right)\geq(1-p)\mu_{{\boldsymbol{z}},\mathcal{F},j^{*}+1/2}\left(\left\{{\boldsymbol{v}}\colon\sum_{t=0}^{n}\mathbf{1}_{\left\{|{\boldsymbol{v}}_{t}(\varepsilon)-f({\boldsymbol{z}}_{t}(\varepsilon))|\geq 1\right\}}\leq k\right\}\right)\\

. This concludes the proof.

(128)

Next, we combine the above bound by taking an integral, and switching the order of integration:

\displaystyle\int_{b}^{1}\sqrt{\log N^{\prime}(\mathcal{F},\delta)}d\delta

\displaystyle\leq C\int_{\delta=b}^{1}\int_{x=c\delta}^{1}\frac{\sqrt{\log(x/\delta)\mathsf{fat}_{x}(\mathcal{F})}}{x}dxd\delta

\displaystyle=C\int_{x=cb}^{1}\sqrt{\mathsf{fat}_{x}(\mathcal{F})}\int_{\delta=b}^{x/c}\frac{\sqrt{\log(Cx/\delta)}}{x}d\delta dx

\displaystyle\leq C^{\prime}\int_{x=cb}^{1}\sqrt{\mathsf{fat}_{x}(\mathcal{F})}dx.

∎

Proof of Proposition

By scaling, it suffices to consider

Proof of Lemma

.

The upper bound follows from Lemma

{\boldsymbol{z}}

(

2\delta
-packed and consider the balls

Proof of Proposition
9
.

A majorizing measure is multi-scale, while a fractional cover is single-scale; to turn the latter into the former, we consider the following mixture distribution. If the integral of the fractional cover is infinite then there is nothing to prove; thus, assume that the this quantity is finite. Suppose
\alpha=0
. Let
\delta_{j}
be the smallest
\delta
such that
N^{\prime}(\mathcal{V},\delta)\leq 2^{2^{j}}
and let
\mu_{j}
be an optimal
\delta_{j}
-fractional cover. Let

\mu=c\sum_{j}j^{-2}\mu_{j}

(131)

Note that the above sum is over all
j
sufficiently large by the assumption that the integral is finite and we let
c
be a normalizing constant. Now, we have for any
{\boldsymbol{v}},\varepsilon
,

\displaystyle I_{\mu}({\boldsymbol{v}},\varepsilon)=\int_{0}^{1}\sqrt{\log\frac{1}{\mu(B_{\delta}({\boldsymbol{v}},\varepsilon))}}d\delta\leq\sum_{j}\int_{\delta_{j}}^{\delta_{j-1}}\sqrt{\log\frac{1}{\mu(B_{\delta}({\boldsymbol{v}},\varepsilon))}}d\delta

(132)

By construction, for any
\delta\geq\delta_{j}
,

\displaystyle\mu(B_{\delta}({\boldsymbol{v}},\varepsilon))\geq\mu(B_{\delta_{j}}({\boldsymbol{v}},\varepsilon))\geq\frac{1}{j^{2}}\mu_{j}(B_{\delta_{j}}({\boldsymbol{v}},\varepsilon))\geq\frac{1}{j^{2}2^{2^{j}}}\geq\frac{1}{2^{2\times 2^{j}}}

(133)

Thus we have

\displaystyle\int_{\delta_{j}}^{\delta_{j-1}}\sqrt{\log\frac{1}{\mu(B_{\delta}({\boldsymbol{v}},\varepsilon))}}d\delta\leq c\delta_{j-1}2^{\frac{j}{2}}\leq c^{\prime}\delta_{j-1}2^{\frac{j-1}{2}}

(134)

Thus,

\displaystyle I_{\mu}({\boldsymbol{v}},\varepsilon)\leq\sum_{j}c^{\prime}\delta_{j-1}2^{\frac{j-1}{2}}\leq C\int_{0}^{1}\sqrt{\log N^{\prime}(\mathcal{V},\delta)}d\delta

(135)

If
\alpha>0
, simply cut off
j
such that
\delta_{j}\geq\alpha
. The same technique applies, concluding the proof.
∎

Proof of Corollary
18
.

The upper bound follows immediately from setting
\alpha=0
in the second statement of Theorem
10
and then bounding the resulting integral by Proposition
15
.

For the lower bound, we first need to show that if
\mathcal{F}
is
(c,p)
-bounded, then

\int_{0}^{1}\sqrt{\mathsf{fat}_{\delta}(\mathcal{F})}d\delta\leq\frac{2\sqrt{c}}{1-2^{\frac{p}{2}-1}}

(137)

To do this, we note that the function
\delta\mapsto\mathsf{fat}_{\delta}(\mathcal{F})
is monotone non-increasing. Thus we have

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\sqrt{\mathsf{fat}_{\delta}(\mathcal{F})}d\delta

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\int_{2^{-j}}^{2^{-j+1}}\sqrt{\mathsf{fat}_{\delta}(\mathcal{F})}d\delta\leq\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}2^{-j}\sqrt{\mathsf{fat}_{2^{-j}}(\mathcal{F})}

(138)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}2^{-j}\sqrt{c2^{jp}}\leq\frac{2\sqrt{c}}{1-2^{\frac{p}{2}-1}}

(139)

Now, noting that
\mathsf{fat}_{1}(\mathcal{F})\geq 1
, we see that the above implies that for any
\alpha<1
,

\int_{0}^{1}\sqrt{\mathsf{fat}_{\delta}(\mathcal{F})}d\delta\leq\frac{2\sqrt{c}}{1-2^{\frac{p}{2}-1}}\leq\frac{2\sqrt{c}}{1-2^{\frac{p}{2}-1}}\sup_{\delta>\alpha}\delta\sqrt{\mathsf{fat}_{\delta}(\mathcal{F})}

(140)

Note that the definition of a
(c,p)
-bounded class ensures that the right hand side of Proposition
15
tends to a finite limit as
b\downarrow 0
; call this finite limit
A
. To get the lower bound, we apply
(
Rakhlin et al. , 2015
, Lemma 2)
, which says that

\sup_{\delta>0}\delta\sqrt{\frac{\min(\mathsf{fat}_{\delta}(\mathcal{F}),n)}{32}}\leq\sqrt{n}\mathcal{R}_{n}(\mathcal{F})

(141)

and, moreover, that if
\delta>2\mathcal{R}_{n}(\mathcal{F})
, then
\mathsf{fat}_{\delta}(\mathcal{F})<n
.
Thus, using the upper bound on
\mathcal{R}_{n}(\mathcal{F})
just proven, we see that

\displaystyle\sqrt{n}\mathcal{R}_{n}(\mathcal{F})

\displaystyle\geq C^{\prime}\sup_{\delta>2\mathcal{R}_{n}(\mathcal{F})}\delta\sqrt{\mathsf{fat}_{\delta}(\mathcal{F})}\geq C^{\prime}\sup_{\delta>\frac{c^{\prime}}{\sqrt{n}}\int_{0}^{1}\sqrt{\mathsf{fat}_{\delta}(\mathcal{F})}d\delta}\delta\sqrt{\mathsf{fat}_{\delta}(\mathcal{F})}

(142)

\displaystyle\geq C^{\prime}\sup_{\delta>\frac{c^{\prime}A}{\sqrt{n}}}\delta\sqrt{\mathsf{fat}_{\delta}(\mathcal{F})}\geq C^{\prime}\frac{1-2^{\frac{p}{2}-1}}{2\sqrt{c}}\int_{0}^{1}\sqrt{\mathsf{fat}_{\delta}(\mathcal{F})}d\delta

(143)

The result follows.
∎

Proof of Corollary

By scaling it suffices to consider the case
