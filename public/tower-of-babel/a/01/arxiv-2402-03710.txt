Listen, Chat, and Edit: Text-Guided Soundscape Modification for Enhanced Auditory Experience
Xilin Jiang, Cong Han, Yinghao Aaron Li, Nima Mesgarani

Abstract
In daily life, we encounter a variety of sounds, both desirable and undesirable, with limited control over their presence and volume. Our work introduces “Listen, Chat, and Edit” (LCE), a novel multimodal sound mixture editor that modifies each sound source in a mixture based on user-provided text instructions. LCE distinguishes itself with a user-friendly chat interface and its unique ability to edit multiple sound sources simultaneously within a mixture, without needing to separate them. Users input open-vocabulary text prompts, which are interpreted by a large language model to create a semantic filter for editing the sound mixture. The system then decomposes the mixture into its components, applies the semantic filter, and reassembles it into the desired output. We developed a 160-hour dataset with over 100k mixtures, including speech and various audio sources, along with text prompts for diverse editing tasks like extraction, removal, and volume control. Our experiments demonstrate significant improvements in signal quality across all editing tasks and robust performance in zero-shot scenarios with varying numbers and types of sound sources. An audio demo is available at: https://listenchatedit.github.io.

Introduction

Imagine yourself at a cocktail party, listening to a conversation between two people. Amidst their conversation, a soft guitar melody gracefully wafts through the air, however woefully accompanied by the annoying noises of passing cars outside. While you might find their conversation intriguing, one of the speakers talks too loudly, making it difficult to hear the other person clearly. Moreover, the persistent outdoor traffic adds an extra layer of distraction. At this moment, you open the smart sound editor and type
“Hey. Can you reduce the volume of the excited male speaker and remove the background traffic noise completely?”
Instantly, you find it much easier to follow their conversation, and the soothing music remains hovering in the air.

The abovementioned scenario where people want to focus on specific sounds among multiple concurrent ones is known as the cocktail party problem
(
Cherry 1953
;
Haykin & Chen 2005
;
McDermott 2009
)
. Numerous studies have been conducted for enhancing the hearing experience in multi-sound environments. Traditional hearing aids
(
Kates 2008
;
Clark & Swanepoel 2014
;
Launer et al. 2016
)
can amplify or suppress a wide spectrum of sounds within a mix, but they fall short in targeting specific sound sources. Some recent models can extract a target sound source based on neural auditory attention signals
(
Van Eyndhoven et al. 2016
;
Han et al. 2019
)
or sound word labels
(
Kilgour et al. 2022
;
Liu et al. 2022
;
Veluri et al. 2023
)
. However, these models concentrate on isolating a single source and lack the versatility to modify multiple sound sources. Additionally, most of these models do not provide a user-friendly interface for specifying the target. Therefore, we need a solution that allows for soundscape modification by easily specifying arbitrary sound components we would like to edit.

Figure 1:
An overview of
Listen, Chat, and Edit
.

In this study, we introduce text-guided sound mixture-to-mixture editing, a new problem that aims to simultaneously extract, remove, or control the volume of various sounds in a mix based on the user’s open-vocabulary instruction. We propose the first text-guided sound mixture editor:
Listen, Chat, and Edit
(LCE). Figure
1
shows the schematic of LCE: To begin with, a user and LCE both
Listen
to the sound mixture. Next, the user
Chat
s with a text prompt in natural language to specify which sound sources to be edited and in which manner. Finally, LCE
Edit
s the sound mixture according to the text prompt and outputs a new sound mixture that aligns with the user’s command. LCE differs from previous works in three distinctive features:

•

Selectivity
: LCE recognizes and edits target talkers by semantic descriptions. A user describes speaking styles for human speakers, including gender, pitch, tempo, energy, and emotion, and the category for non-speech sounds, such as the name of an animal or a musical instrument.

•

Accessibility
: LCE employs a large language model (LLM) to interpret users’ open-vocabulary text prompts for different editing tasks. This textual instruction is more user-friendly than other forms of instruction like neural signals, speaker features, sound labels, and so on.

•

Sound mixture-to-mixture editing
: LCE simultaneously edits multiple sound sources in the sound mixture in one step, without separating them in advance and editing each sound one by one.

We propose 16 sound mixture-to-mixture editing tasks as shown in Table
1
and evaluate LCE on them. Since the sound mixtures and text prompts required to train LCE did not exist, we have curated a new sound mixture dataset, comprising around 160 hours or over 100k of speech and audio mixtures with over 500k of text prompts written for these 16 editing tasks. Our experimental results show that LCE trained on this dataset enhances the signal quality in 94.4% of sound mixtures and improves the signal-to-noise ratio by 10.4 dB averaged across all editing tasks. Notably, LCE surpasses previous expert models on target speech or audio extraction as one of the editing tasks. Moreover, LCE can perform zero-shot editing on sound mixtures containing unseen types of sounds or different numbers of sources.

Related Works

Sound Separation and Extraction.
Sound mixture understanding and processing
(
Settle et al. 2018
;
Turpault et al. 2020
)
relies on sound separation and extraction, as the latter can provide the computational groundwork to isolate each sound source in a mixture. There have been sound separation models separating speeches
(
Hershey et al. 2015
;
Luo & Mesgarani 2019
;
Subakan et al. 2021
)
, music
(
Bryan et al. 2013
;
Défossez et al. 2021
;
Luo & Yu 2022
)
, and universal sounds
(
Kavalerov et al. 2019
;
Wisdom et al. 2021
;
Petermann et al. 2022
)
in a mixture. However, they are not selective since they unconditionally separate all sources in the mixture. On the other hand, sound extraction models selectively extract one target sound from a sound mixture
(
Elminshawi et al. 2022
;
Zmolikova et al. 2023
)
. A clue, which can take multiple forms, is provided to identify the target. Some common clues include lip movement videos
(
Afouras et al. 2018
;
Ma et al. 2021
)
, videos recording the sound production
(
Zhao et al. 2018
;
Tzinis et al. 2021
)
, target speaker embeddings
(
Žmolíková et al. 2019
;
Wang et al. 2019
)
, neural auditory attention signals
(
Van Eyndhoven et al. 2016
;
Han et al. 2019
)
, locations
(
Gu et al. 2019
;
Heitkaemper et al. 2019
)
, the language spoken
(
Borsdorf et al. 2021
)
, and recently emerging text prompts.

Text-Guided Audio Applications.
Recent advancements in NLP and text-audio learning have led to the use of text as the guidance for various audio applications. In target sound extraction, semantic labels, descriptions of audio sources
(
Kilgour et al. 2022
;
Liu et al. 2022
;
Liu et al. 2023c
;
Veluri et al. 2023
)
, transcriptions, or speaker attributes
(
Hao et al. 2023
)
has been used as a new form of clue to extract the target sound source. Text prompts have also been introduced in sound generation tasks, such as TextrolSpeech
(
Ji et al. 2023
)
, AudioGen
(
Kreuk et al. 2022
)
, and Diffsound
(
Yang et al. 2023a
)
. Text-guided sound editing is similar to sound generation but provides a noisy or corrupt sound as an additional input. Relevant work includes SpeechX
(
Wang et al. 2023a
)
for speech enhancement, deletion, extraction, and content editing, AUDIT
(
Wang et al. 2023b
)
for audio addition, removal, inpainting, replacement, and super-resolution, and uSee
(
Yang et al. 2023b
)
for noise and reverberation addition and deletion. The latest sound generation and editing models, AudioLDM
(
Liu et al. 2023a
;
Liu et al. 2023b
)
, Voicebox
(
Le et al. 2023
)
, and Audiobox
(
Vyas et al. 2023
)
can perform style transfer, noise reduction, and audio inpainting.

This work focuses on text-guided sound mixture-to-mixture editing, a new category of text-guided audio applications. The problem we address here is a generalized version of the target sound extraction, one of many tasks LCE can solve. In addition, LCE is inherently different from existing sound generation or editing models, which focus on generating a new sound or modifying sound properties. LCE focuses on keeping every sound’s integrity and controls its presence and volume for an enhanced hearing experience in a multi-sound environment. The applications of LCE include hearing assistance, audio engineering, and ambient sound control.

Multimodal Text-Audio Learning.
The performance of text-guided audio applications relies on the relevance of the text embedding to the audio content. Given that most language models are trained without audio, achieving better performance in text-guided audio tasks often involves a subsequent language model finetuning step using paired text and audio data. There are two common approaches for this. One approach is to use Contrastive Language-Audio Pretraining (CLAP)
(
Elizalde et al. 2023a
;
Elizalde et al. 2023b
)
or similar methods such as Wav2clip
(
Wu et al. 2022
)
, Audioclip
(
Guzhov et al. 2022
)
, and CLAPSpeech
(
Ye et al. 2023
)
, which maximizes the similarity between embeddings of paired text and audio. A pretrained language model is first finetuned with a pretrained audio encoder
using the CLAP objective. Then, the language model is frozen and its text embeddings are used for various downstream tasks including audio captioning and classification
(
Wang et al. 2023c
;
Liang et al. 2023
)
, sound extraction
(
Liu et al. 2023c
)
, and audio generation
(
Liu et al. 2023a
)
. Another approach does not incorporate the intermediate CLAP step. Instead, it directly optimizes the language model and the audio processing model, such as a sound separation model
(
Kilgour et al. 2022
;
Hao et al. 2023
)
, together with the task-specific objective. We opt for the second approach since the sound mixtures and text prompts in this work contain multiple sources, and to our knowledge, there is no work using CLAP to learn the text embedding corresponding to a mixture of sounds due to a lack of relevant datasets and an increased complexity to learn every combination of sources in a mixture. Nevertheless, we still implemented the CLAP method trained on single sources for target speech or audio extraction and included the results in Table
2
as a baseline.

3
Sound Mixture-to-Mixture Editing

We formulate sound mixture-to-mixture editing in this section. An example editing is shown in Figure
2
.

3.1
Sources and Actions

Consider a sound mixture
x
, we can express it as the sum of
N
different sources:

\displaystyle x=\sum_{i=1}^{N}s_{i}

(1)

Each source can have different energy and can be either a human speech or a non-speech sound:
s_{i}\in\mathcal{S}=\mathcal{S}_{\text{speech}}\cup\mathcal{S}_{\text{audio}}
. For the remainder of this paper, we will use the term
audio
for non-speech sounds, including animal voices, sound effects, music, and noises. We can describe each sound source
s_{i}
by a semantic description
\tilde{s}_{i}
, which can be the speaking style measured in one or multiple of gender, pitch, tempo, energy, and emotion for a speech source or the class label for an audio source. Let us define
\tilde{\mathcal{S}}
as the set of all semantic descriptions. In this work, we assume that no two sound sources share the same semantic description in the sound mixture. In other words, no two speech sources share the same speaking style, and no two audio sources share the same class label. Therefore, every sound mixture is a combination of
N
different sources of different descriptions
\{\tilde{s}_{1},\tilde{s}_{2},...,\tilde{s}_{N}\}
, with
\tilde{s}_{i}\neq\tilde{s}_{j},\forall i\neq j\large
, and a description
\tilde{s}_{i}
can identify an unique source
s_{i}
in the mixture.

Our goal is to edit the mixture
x
to yield a new mixture
y
:

\displaystyle y=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\alpha_{i}s_{i}

(2)

which is made up by the same sources but different scaling factors applied to them. The values of
\alpha_{i}
are determined by the user’s desired editing actions, which can be selected from the action space denoted as
\mathcal{A}=\{\textit{removing}\hskip 3.00003pt(0),\textit{keeping}\hskip 3.00003pt(1),\textit{increasing the volume}\hskip 3.00003pt(\uparrow),\\
\textit{decreasing the volume}\hskip 3.00003pt(\downarrow)\}
. These actions correspond to scaling factors represented by
\mathcal{A}_{\alpha}=\{0,1,2,0.5\}
. For example, a scaling factor
\alpha_{i}=2
implies increasing the volume of
s_{i}
by 6 dB. If only one
\alpha_{i}
is equal to 1, and all others are equal to 0, it results the extraction of
s_{i}
.

3.2
Editing Tasks

To specify both how and what to modify for a single source, we require a tuple
(a_{i},\tilde{s}_{i})\in\mathcal{A}\times\tilde{\mathcal{S}}
to specify both the action
a_{i}
and the source
\tilde{s}_{i}
. To specify how and what to modify for a mixture of
N
sources, we require a collection of
N
such tuples:
\pi=\{(a_{1},\tilde{s}_{1}),...,(a_{N},\tilde{s}_{N})\}
. We call
\pi
a sound mixture-to-mixture editing instruction. If the composition
\{\tilde{s}_{1},\tilde{s}_{2},...,\tilde{s}_{N}\}
of the sound mixture is fixed,
\mathcal{A}^{N}
represents the set of all possible edits to this mixture. For instance, if a mixture contains four sources, there is a total of
|\mathcal{A}|^{4}-2=254
possible edits, excluding the trivial identity and silence cases. One example editing
[0,\downarrow,\uparrow,1]\in\mathcal{A}^{4}
instructs the following: remove
s_{1}
, turn down the volume of
s_{2}
, turn up the volume of
s_{3}
, and keep
s_{4}
.

Notice that many elements in
\mathcal{A}^{N}
are similar. For example,
[0,\downarrow,\uparrow,1]
and
[\downarrow,0,1,\uparrow]
specify same actions but applied to different sources. To more accurately evaluate the performance of LCE and to maintain consistency with existing literature, we categorize all elements in
\mathcal{A}^{N}
into the task space
\mathcal{T}
. For any sound mixture containing at least two speech sources and at least two audio sources, there are 16 different tasks in
\mathcal{T}
. In Table
1
, we present
\mathcal{T}
based on which source(s) are edited (in columns) and the manner in which they are edited (in rows). Details and examples of each task are provided in Appendix
C
.

The task space
\mathcal{T}
is a super set of the target speech or audio extraction and removal tasks studied in previous works
(
Liu et al. 2023c
;
Hao et al. 2023
;
Veluri et al. 2023
)
. It further includes volume control and multiple sound editing tasks.

3.3
Text Prompts

Although
\pi=\{(a_{1},\tilde{s}_{1}),...,(a_{N},\tilde{s}_{N})\}
itself can accurately specify a sound mixture-to-mixture editing instruction, writing an instruction in this format can be inconvenient and difficult for humans, because we prefer to use natural language rather than numbers and symbols. As a result, we adopted using
p=\mathcal{H}(\pi)
as the input for LCE. Here,
p
is an open-vocabulary text prompt written in natural language by
\mathcal{H}
, which can be either a human or a large language model (LLM). Given the impracticality of hiring humans to write hundreds of thousands of prompts, we leveraged a LLM to generate text prompts
p
from
\pi
. The text prompt generation pipeline can be found in Section
5
and Appendix
B
.

4
Listen, Chat, and Edit

Listen, Chat, and Edit (LCE) is our solution to the sound mixture-to-mixture editing problem. LCE edits a sound mixture
x
given a natural language text prompt
p
:

\displaystyle\hat{y}=\text{LCE}(x,p)

(3)

LCE includes two models: a
PromptReader
to read text prompts and a
SoundEditor
to edit sound mixtures. A graphical illustration of it in details is depicted in Figure
3
.

4.1
Prompt Reader

The
PromptReader
is a language model that behaves as the inverse of
\mathcal{H}
: It generates a
D
-dimensional text embedding
z\in\mathbb{R}^{D}
from a text prompt
p
, which encodes the information about the sources and actions specified in
\pi
. We call
z
a
semantic filter
because it is generated from text to guide the
SoundEditor
to execute proper filtering of sounds. We analyze and show the correspondence between the semantic filter and the resulting sound filter in Appendix
A
.

\displaystyle z=\textit{PromptReader}(p)

(4)

We employed a pretrained language model GPT-2
(
Radford et al. 2019
)
or LLaMA2
(
Touvron et al. 2023
)
in the role of the
PromptReader
. We finetuned it, with full parameters or low-rank approximation
(
Hu et al. 2022
)
, using the gradients backpropagated from the
SoundEditor
. While the pretrained language model only grasps textual information, further joint training with
SoundEditor
enables it to generate text embeddings that exhibit awareness of the acoustic characteristics (e.g., pitch, emotion, animal voices, and instrument timbres) written in the descriptions (see Appendix
A
). Consequently, the enhanced embeddings facilitate the
SoundEditor
in more easily identifying the corresponding sources noted in the text prompt.

4.2
Sound Editor

The
SoundEditor
is an acoustic model responsible for editing the sound mixture
x
based on the semantic filter
z
:

\displaystyle\hat{y}=\textit{SoundEditor}(x,z)

(5)

We adopted the architecture of source separation models for the
SoundEditor
due to their abilities to isolate individual sources in a sound mixture. However, it is essential to clarify that
SoundEditor
does not explicitly (in any stage) separate all the sources in the mixture, individually editing them, and sum them up. Instead, the model functions similar to a sound extraction model to directly output a single sound that is the mix of all the edited sources.

In particular, we followed the design of time-domain source separation network Conv-TasNet
(
Luo & Mesgarani 2019
)
and SepFormer
(
Subakan et al. 2021
)
. Both models incorporate a learnable linear encoder
\mathcal{E}:\mathbb{R}^{1\times T}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{C\times L}
for mapping the input waveform
x
of
T
samples into a
C
-dimensional latent representation
h_{x}
and a learnable linear decoder
\mathcal{D}:\mathbb{R}^{C\times L}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{1\times T}
for mapping the output sound from the latent space
h_{\hat{y}}
back to the waveform
\hat{y}
.

\displaystyle h_{x}=\mathcal{E}(x),\quad\hat{y}=\mathcal{D}(h_{\hat{y}})

(6)

Between the encoder and the decoder, a mask network denoted as
\mathcal{M}
estimates a single editing mask
h_{m}\in\mathbb{R}^{C\times L}
that selectively extracts, removes, amplifies, or reduces samples in
h_{x}
according to
z
through element-wise multiplication on
h_{x}
:

\displaystyle h_{m}=\mathcal{M}(x,z),\quad h_{\hat{y}}=h_{m}\otimes h_{x}

(7)

\mathcal{M}
consists of
R
editing blocks with the same structure: temporal convolutional network for Conv-TasNet or dual-path transformer for SepFormer.
We guide the behavior of every editing block by the semantic filter
z
using Feature-wise Linear Modulation (FiLM)
(
Perez et al. 2018
)
. Before the
i
th block, the text-guided feature
\tilde{h}_{i}
is computed by:

\displaystyle\tilde{h}_{i}=\gamma_{i}h_{i}+\beta_{i},\quad 0\leq i\leq R-1

(8)

\displaystyle\gamma_{i}=\mathcal{F}_{i}(z),\quad\beta_{i}=\mathcal{G}_{i}(z)

(9)

\mathcal{F}_{i}:\mathbb{R}^{D}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{C}
and
\mathcal{G}_{i}:\mathbb{R}^{D}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{C}
are modeled by two two-layer perceptrons which project the
z
to the same dimension of
h_{i}
. Afterwards,
\gamma_{i}
and
\beta_{i}
are replicated along the time axis to modulate
h_{i}
. This ensures that the text prompt maintains time-invariant influence over the duration of the mixture signal.

4.3
Training Objective

Each training instance is a tuple
(x,y,p)
including an input mixture
x
, a target mixture
y
, and a text prompt
p
. The sources in the mixture
\{s_{1},s_{2},...,s_{N}\}
and the underlying symbolic instruction
\pi
used in the creation of
y
and
p
are hidden from the system. LCE estimates a mixture
\hat{y}
given
x
and
p
, following Equation
4
and
5
. All model components are jointly optimized by maximizing the signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) between the target and the estimated mixture:

\displaystyle\text{SNR}(\hat{y},y)=10\log_{10}\Biggl(\frac{\|y\|^{2}}{\|y-\hat{y}\|^{2}}\Biggr)

(10)

5
Dataset

Due to the absence of natural language instruction-prompted sound editing datasets, we generated our own sound mixtures and text prompts. This section highlights key features of our dataset. See more details in Appendix
B
.

Sound Sources

Speech sources were from TextrolSpeech
(
Ji et al. 2023
)
and audio sources were from VGGSound
(
Chen et al. 2020
)
(for training and in-domain evaluation) or FSD50K
(
Fonseca et al. 2021
)
(for zero-shot evaluation). Speakers and audios were split into training, validation, and testing sets. All sources were resampled to 16 kHz and cropped or padded to 5 seconds.

Sound Mixtures

We generated 100k, 5k, and 10k mixtures of 2 speech + 2 audio sources from TextrolSpeech and VGGSound for training, validation, and testing. These four-source mixtures, featuring a variety of categories, simulate acoustic scenes commonly encountered in daily life. We also generated 5,000 mixtures of 2 speech, 2 audio, 2 speech + 1 audio, 1 speech + 2 audio for zero-shot evaluation on unseen numbers of sources, and 5,000 mixtures of 2 speech + 2 audio sources from FSD50K with either all classes or unseen classes for zero-shot evaluation on unseen sounds.

Editing Tasks
For every mixture, we first evenly assigned a task
t\in\mathcal{T}
(e.g. target speech extraction), and then assigned a particular instruction
\pi
of task
t
(e.g. extracting a particular speaker). The reported performance for each task was calculated by averaging the editing performance of all mixtures assigned to that task.

Text Prompts
We requested GPT-3.5 Turbo to write a text prompt five times from the assigned instruction
\pi
for each mixture. To improve the diversity of the text prompts, we asked it to try imperative or interrogative sentences and substitute synonyms for class labels and style keywords such as
female
,
male
,
low
,
high
,
pitch
, and
tempo
. To improve the naturalness of the text prompts, we omitted to mention a source if the action was
keeping
, unless we specifically wanted to extract it. Furthermore, we described a speaker by a random subset instead of all of five style attributes. More efforts to make the text prompts more diverse and natural are discussed in Appendix
B
.

7
Conclusion

We develop
Listen, Chat, and Edit
, the first text-guided sound mixture editor that can handle a variaty of editing tasks for speech and audio mixtures. Our development also introduces the first text-prompted sound mixture dataset of 160 hours, featuring speakers with diverse styles, audio from hundreds of classes, and five generic text prompts written for each mixture. LCE trained on this dataset demonstrates an SNR improvement of over 10 dB averaged across all 16 tasks, superior performance than ad-hoc sound extraction models, and a generalization ability to unfamiliar sound mixtures of unseen sounds or different numbers of sound sources. Our future efforts will target two directions: One is to scale up the size of the model and the dataset to reach a higher performance on the current 16-task benchmark. The other is to introduce new tasks to sound mixture-to-mixture editing, such as dereverberation, restoration, and content editing of speech and audio.

8
Broader Impact

In noisy environments with multiple sound sources present, human listeners often find their listening abilities reduced
(
Schäfer et al. 2018
;
Vanthornhout et al. 2018
)
. This issue intensifies for those with hearing impairments, making it hard to distinguish and focus on specific sounds
(
Marrone et al. 2008
;
Shinn-Cunningham & Best 2008
;
Dai et al. 2018
)
. LCE, as the first text-guided sound mixture editor, is capable of adjusting the presence and volume of specific sound sources according to textual instructions from listeners. Therefore, LCE could serve as an effective tool in assisting those with hearing challenges. Importantly, LCE is safe to use. It operates using general descriptions of speaker styles and audio categories without retaining or employing specific characteristics of any speaker. Furthermore, it only edits existing sources within the mix, ensuring it does not generate malicious sounds that are not initially present. LCE offers a promising and safe enhancement for auditory experience without any evident ethical concerns or negative social impacts.

Appendix B
Text-Prompted Sound Mixture Editing Dataset

Due to the absence of text-prompted sound mixture editing datasets, we have curated a 160-hour dataset of more than 100k sound mixtures and more than 500k text prompts. We will make the mixing scripts and the text prompts publicly available to promote future research. In this section, we used sound mixtures of 2 Speech + 2 Audio for illustration. Other compositions of sound mixtures can be generated and prompted in the same way.

B.1
Sound Sources

The speech sources were sourced from TextrolSpeech
(
Ji et al. 2023
)
. TextrolSpeech consists of 330 hours of speeches gathered from 1327 speakers, with each speech source being tagged with five style attributes:
gender
(female or male),
pitch
(low, normal, or high),
tempo
(low, normal, or high),
volume
(low, normal, or high), and
emotion
(angry, contempt, disgusted, fear, happy, sad, surprised, or neutral). The audio sources were sourced from VGGSound
(
Chen et al. 2020
)
for training and in-domain evaluation and FSD50K
(
Fonseca et al. 2021
)
for out-of-domain evaluation. VGGSound contains audio clips from YouTube, with each clip being associated with one or more audio class labels. We filtered out clips with multiple labels, indicating a mixture of sounds, and those with labels related to human voices, such as
people
,
children
, and
police radio chatter
. The filtered VGGSound dataset contains 430 hours of audio from 248 distinct classes. FSD50K is collected from the Freesound database, and we only used the
Eval
set for testing. We filtered out clips with labels under
Human_voice
or
Human_group_actions
, resulting in 22 hours of audio data from 171 different classes. Furthermore, we filtered out clips with labels that overlapped (considering synonyms) with those in VGGSound, resulting in 8 hours of audio data of 67 different classes. We also shorten labels that contain parenthesis or are too long, e.g.
vehicle horn and car horn and honking
to
vehicle horn
and
keyboard (musical)
to
musical keyboard
.

We partitioned 1327 speakers from TextrolSpeech into three subsets: 1177 for training, 50 for validation, and 100 for testing. We applied the same ratio to partition the VGGSound into three sets. All remaining sources in FSD50K
Eval
are reserved for zero-shot evaluation. Next, we resampled both speech and audio sources to a 16 kHz sampling rate and adjusted their duration to be 5 seconds through cropping or padding as necessary.

To facilitate later discussion, we define a style vector
\vec{s}=[\textit{gender},\textit{pitch},\textit{tempo},\textit{volume},\textit{emotion}]
, which is the ordered sequence of values corresponding to the style attributes of a speech source. Additionally, we use the symbol
c
to denote the class label associated to an audio source.

B.2
Mixture Generation

We randomly sampled two speech sources of different style vectors (i.e.
\vec{s}_{1}\neq\vec{s}_{2}
, meaning at least one of the five style attributes is different) and two audio sources of different class labels (i.e.
c_{3}\neq c_{4}
). This makes sure that every source can be uniquely identified by speaking style or class label in words.

We mixed audio sources with signal-to-noise ratios (SNR) that were uniformly sampled from [-3, 3] dB. For speech sources, we used SNRs that were uniformly sampled from three different ranges: [-3, -2] dB, [-1, 1] dB, and [2, 3] dB, depending on
volume
values: low, normal, and high, respectively. In total, we created 100,000 mixtures (equivalent to 139 hours), 5000 mixtures (7 hours), and 10,000 mixtures (14 hours) for the training, validation, and testing sets, respectively.

B.3
Instruction Generation

We assigned an editing instruction to every mixture in the dataset. Instead of distributing instructions uniformly across
254=|\mathcal{A}|^{4}-2
possible edits, we opted to assign the instruction to every mixture uniformly across the 16 tasks. Our intention is to ensure that all 16 tasks carry equal importance and have an equal share in the dataset. Randomly selecting from 254 possible edits leads to training imbalances and evaluation unfairness, because some tasks, such as SE and SR have significantly fewer edits associated compared to other tasks like MVC and MEVC (as shown Appendix
C
).

For every mixture
x
in the dataset, we first selected a task
t\in\mathcal{T}
from the task space. Subsequently, we uniformly sampled an edit from all possible edits with the task
t
, resulting in
[a_{1},a_{2},a_{3},a_{4}]\in\mathcal{A}^{4}(t)
. Finally, we translated the actions into scaling factors
[\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\alpha_{3},\alpha_{4}]\in\mathcal{A}_{\alpha}^{4}
and generated a target mixture
y
according to Equation
2
.

An editing instruction can be expressed as
\pi=\{(a_{1},\vec{s}_{1}),(a_{2},\vec{s}_{2}),(a_{3},c_{3}),(a_{4},c_{4})\}
. However, when people writing text prompts, we tend to
not
include every detail in
\pi
. For instance, we might not mention sources we prefer not to change, and might not describe a speaker using all five style attributes. To align with human’s behavior, we built
\pi^{\prime}
which contains a subset of information from
\pi
in two steps. First, we skipped sources marked as
a_{i}=\textit{keeping}
in
\pi
, except for extraction and removal tasks, where we alternated to say extracting sources (dropping all
removing
) and removing other sources (dropping all
keeping
) with equal probabilities. Second, we simplified the speaker description by only using a subset of style attributes that can distinguish one speaker from the other. For example, if two speakers have different
gender
,
pitch
, and
tempo
, we might only describe
gender
and
tempo
and omit the other three attributes for both speakers.

E.1
Text Prompt Quality

By default, we train the LCE with open-vocabulary
generic
prompts. Here, we instead use “closed-vocabulary”
template
prompts for training but still used
generic
prompts for testing. The differences between
generic
and
template
prompts are elaborated in Appendix
B.4
. In brief,
template
prompts are generated by a program that fills in words within one of three predefined templates, whereas
generic
prompts are rephrased from
template
prompts by GPT-3.5 Turbo and have greater variability in both word choices and sentence structures, thus closer to prompts written by humans. The advantage of using
template
prompts lies in their ease of generation from a computational and time-efficiency perspective. Compared to
generic
prompts which were written offline for only one selected edit per sound mixture
12
12

12

Due to resource constraints, we only generated five text prompts corresponding to one of 254 possible edits per sound mixture.
,
template
prompts can be written on the fly for all 254 possible edits for each sound mixture with minimal computational overhead.

In Table
7
, the first row “TEMPLATE 100%” used 100% of
template
prompts for training. The second row “TEMPLATE 100% +” also used 100% of
template
prompts for training, but it generated additional training samples: For every mixture, we randomly sampled a new editing instruction, which might differ from the one assigned and written the
generic
prompts, and generated a new
template
prompt and target sound mixture on the fly. The third row “TEMPLATE 50% +” used 50% of
template
prompts and 50% of
generic
prompts for training. For the
generic
half, we used one of the five
generic
prompts written offline for the assigned edit. For the
template
half, we did the same as the second row to randomly sample a new edit, resulting in a new
template
prompt and a new target mixture.

We observe that the LCE trained on
template
prompts of lower quality exhibits an average SNRi 1.4 dB lower compared to the LCE trained on
generic
prompts. However, when we introduce the generation of new editing instructions and new target mixtures, the average SNRi increases by 0.5 dB. The best performance is achieved when using a combination of 50%
generic
prompts and 50%
template
prompts with the generation of new editing instructions and new target mixtures, on par with the performance of using 100%
generic
prompts. We have three insights from these results: First, including
generic
or real prompts in training is crucial for good performance on open-vocabulary instructions. Second, it is not necessary to train exclusively with 100%
generic
or real prompts to achieve comparable results. Third, the performance of LCE can be further improved by generating new editing instructions and target mixtures.

E.2
Multiple Masks

Recall that
SoundEditor
estimates a single editing mask
h_{m}\in\mathbb{R}^{C\times L}
for sound mixtures containing any number of sources, as shown in Equation
7
. In this section, we explore estimating
N
masks for
N
sources in the mixture, i.e.
h_{m}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times C\times L}
. This is equivalent to first separate all the sources in the mixture, individually edit each of them with one of the mask, and sum the edited sources up. We show two variants with multiple editing masks: In one experiment, we only changed the output dimension of the output layer of the mask network
\mathcal{M}
to be
N
channels to output
N
masks. In the other experiment, we further introduced a permutation invariant SNR
(
Yu et al. 2017
)
(PIT-SNR) loss between the estimated edited sources
\{\hat{s_{i}}\}
and the ground truth edited sources
\{\bar{s_{i}}\}
. The total loss in this case is shown in Equation
11
and
\hat{s_{i}}\sim\Pi
denotes selecting the optimal permutation of the sources that maximizes the sum of SNR for all sources:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}\bigl(\{\hat{s_{i}}\},\{\bar{s_{i}}\},\hat{y},y\bigr)=-\bigl(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{\hat{s_{i}}\sim\Pi}\text{SNR}(\hat{s_{i}},\bar{s_{i}})\bigr)-\text{SNR}(\hat{y},y)

(11)

The performance with multiple editing masks, both without and with the PIT-SNR loss on sources, is shown in the fourth and fifth rows of Table
7
. Switching from one mask to multiple (
N
) masks has a negligible impact on performance. However, incorporating an additional PIT-SNR loss leads to an improvement of 0.2 dB in average performance. While this represents a slight improvement over the default system with one mask, it necessitates access to each of the ground truth edited sources (
\{\bar{s_{i}}\}
), in addition to the target mixture
y
, and presumes knowledge of the number of sources (
N
) in the mixture. Therefore, this approach is only included in this discussion for comparison purposes.

E.3
Model Configurations

We tried two other configurations of
SoundEditor
Conv-TasNet. In one experiment, the kernel size and the stride of both the encoder and decoder are increased to 64 samples (4 milliseconds) and 32 samples (2 milliseconds), respectively, which is four times the size used in the standard configuration. This adjustment results in a receptive field that is four times larger but with features in the latent space that are four times smaller. Despite this configuration being less effective than the default in terms of speech separation
(
Luo & Mesgarani 2019
)
, we want to explore if a longer context of the sound input can compensate for the shortcoming of the reduced feature size in better comprehending and executing editing instructions. The performance with this four times larger kernel and stride size is only marginally lower by an average of 0.2 dB, yet the inference speed of Conv-TasNet is approximately four times faster. In another experiment, we develop a causal system targeting real-time hearing assistance. The performance of the causal system is 1.1 dB lower than the performance of the non-causal system. The inference speed is around 90 times faster than real-time on a NVIDIA L40 GPU. The average memory usage is about 14 GB, with the majority of the memory attributed to instantiating and running
PromptReader
LLAMA2. Further research is needed to reduce the memory usage for a commodity GPU or mobile devices.

Appendix F
Models and Training

We jointly optimized either Conv-TasNet or Sepformer, implemented by SpeechBrain
(
Ravanelli et al. 2021
)
with the default model architecture (in particular a kernel size of 16 samples or one millisecond for the encoder and decoder) in papers
(
Luo & Mesgarani 2019
;
Subakan et al. 2021
)
, as the
SoundEditor
, and either GPT-2 (base)
(
Radford et al. 2019
)
or LLaMA2 (7b-chat-hf)
(
Touvron et al. 2023
)
as the
PromptReader
. The
SoundEditor
is trained from scratch and the
PromptReader
is finetuned from pretrained checkpoints
13
13

13

LLaMA2: https://huggingface.co/meta-llama/Llama-2-7b-chat-hf. GPT-2: https://huggingface.co/gpt2
. We trained LCE for 100 epochs using 4 NVIDIA L40 GPUs with brain 16-bit floating point precision. The total batch size was set to 16 for Conv-TasNet and 8 for Sepformer. We used an Adam
(
Kingma & Ba 2014
)
optimizer with a learning rate of
5e-4
for Conv-TasNet and
1e-4
for Sepformer. GPT-2 and LLaMA2 were finetuned with a learning rate of
1e-4
. GPT-2 was finetuned with all parameters, and LLaMA2 was finetuned using LoRA with a rank of 16 and a dropout rate of 0.05 for the query and value matrices in each self-attention layer. In addition, we applied a linear learning rate warm-up for the first 5000 updates, and in case the validation Signal-to-Noise Ratio (SNR) did not improve for 3 epochs, we halved the learning rate for both the
SoundEditor
and
PromptReader
.

The training time is around 3 days for Conv-TasNet & GPT-2 system, 4 days for Conv-TasNet & LLaMA2 system, 7 days for Sepformer & GPT-2 system, and 9 days for Sepformer & LLaMA2 system.
