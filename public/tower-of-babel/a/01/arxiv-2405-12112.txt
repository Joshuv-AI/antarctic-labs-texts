Abstract
. Metaplectic Wigner distributions are joint time-frequency representations that are parametrized by a symplectic matrix and generalize the short-time Fourier transform and the Wigner distribution. We investigate the question which metaplectic Wigner distributions satisfy an uncertainty principle in the style of Benedicks and Amrein-Berthier. That is, if the metaplectic Wigner distribution is supported on a set of finite measure, must the functions then be zero? While this statement holds for the short-time Fourier transform, it is false for some other natural time-frequency representations. We provide a full characterization of the class of metaplectic Wigner distributions which exhibit an uncertainty principle of this type, both for sesquilinear and quadratic versions. Keywords. Uncertainty principle, metaplectic Wigner distributions, support of time-frequency representations, symplectic group, metaplectic group, oscillator representation AMS subject classifications. 81S07, 81S30, 22E46

Introduction

The uncertainty principle refers to a huge collection of inequalities
that assert that a function and its Fourier transform cannot be small
simultaneously. Measuring the size of a function
f
on the real line by its second moment
\Delta f=\int_{\mathbb{R}}x^{2}|f(x)|^{2}\,dx
, one obtains the
classical uncertainty principle, the Heisenberg-Pauli-Weyl inequality

\Delta f\,\Delta\hat{f}\geq\frac{1}{16\pi^{2}}\|f\|_{2}^{4}\,.

Here we use the normalization
\hat{f}(\omega)={\mathcal{F}}f(\omega)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}f(x)e^{-2\pi ix\omega}\,dx
for the Fourier transform of
f
.

Measuring the size of
f
by the Lebesgue measure of its support,
one obtains a qualitative uncertainty principle:

If both
f
and
\hat{f}
have a
support of finite measure,

then
f
is the zero function.

This uncertainty principle is due to Benedicks
[
3
]
and Amrein and
Berthier
[
1
]
and is often referred to as
Benedicks’s uncertainty principle (because of an early preprint
of
[
3
]
).

In general, for every measure of size, one can obtain a version of the uncertainty principle.
For excellent surveys of such uncertainty principles for the Fourier
pair
(f,\hat{f})
, we recommend the surveys
[
15
,
22
,
31
,
5
]
.

In quantum mechanics and signal processing, one often prefers to study joint time-frequency representations, such as the short-time Fourier transform or the Wigner distribution, in place of the Fourier pair
(f,\hat{f})
.
The basic time-frequency representation is the short-time Fourier transform of a function
f
on

{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}
with respect to a window
g
, which is
defined as

{V}_{g}f(x,\omega)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f(t)\bar{g}(t-x)e^{-2\pi i\omega\cdot t}\,dt\,.

In the standard interpretation of the short-time Fourier transform,
{V}_{g}f(x,\omega)
is
the amplitude of the frequency
\omega
of
f
locally at time
x
. In this sense,
the short-time Fourier transform carries information simultaneously about
f

and

\hat{f}
, and therefore one expects a corresponding uncertainty principle. Indeed, the analog of Benedicks’s uncertainty principle holds for the short-time Fourier transform
[
25
,
28
,
19
,
36
]
:

If
{V}_{g}f
is supported on a set of finite measure, then
f\equiv 0
or

g\equiv 0
.
(*)

The same result holds for close relatives of the short-time Fourier transform, such as the ambiguity function and the Wigner
distribution. The proof of (*) uses Benedicks’s result for the Fourier
pair
(f,\hat{f})
. In fact,
there is now an almost systematic technique to translate an uncertainty principle from the Fourier pair
(f,\hat{f})
to an uncertainty principle of a joint time-frequency representation, e.g.,
{V}_{g}f
, see the survey
[
18
]
.

The short-time Fourier transform, the ambiguity function, and the Wigner distribution are undoubtedly the most important
and most frequently used time-frequency representations.
They are used for the definition of
function spaces
[
12
,
20
]
, in the calculus of pseudodifferential operators
[
14
,
20
,
7
,
4
]
, in many
applications in signal processing
[
37
,
17
,
33
,
6
,
13
]
, and in the phase space formulation
of quantum mechanics
[
10
,
11
]
.
Yet there is a whole zoo of equally
interesting time-frequency representations available
[
23
]
.
For instance, the popular
\tau
-Wigner distribution goes back at least to
[
27
]
.
Further extensions were
studied as linear perturbations of the
Wigner distribution
[
2
]
, as canonical linear
transforms
[
39
]
, free metaplectic transformations
[
38
]
,
and as symplectic Wigner
distributions
[
8
]
.

For this huge class of time-frequency representations, one expects many uncertainty principles to carry over
from the short-time Fourier transform. This is the topic of our work.

To set up the results, we first recall the symplectic group and the
associated metaplectic representation.
Let

(1.1)

{\mathcal{J}}=\begin{pmatrix}0&I\\
-I&0\end{pmatrix}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2d\times 2d}

be the standard skew-symmetric matrix in

\mathrm{GL}(2d,\mathbb{R})
. Here
0=0_{d}
and
I=I_{d}

denote the zero and identity matrix in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d\times d}

respectively
1
1

1

We include the dimension in the index only when necessary, but will otherwise omit it.
.
Then the symplectic group
\mathrm{Sp}(2d,\mathbb{R})

consists of all
2d\times 2d
-matrices
\mathcal{A}
such that
\mathcal{A}^{t}{\mathcal{J}}\mathcal{A}={\mathcal{J}}
. Associated to
\mathcal{A}\in\mathrm{Sp}(2d,\mathbb{R})
is a
unitary operator
\mu(\mathcal{A})
which intertwines the symmetric
phase-space shifts and acts on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})

[
20
,
14
,
11
]
.

Now we double the dimension
and assume that
{\bm{\mathcal{A}}}\in\mathrm{Sp}(4d,\mathbb{R})
. Then
\mu({\bm{\mathcal{A}}})
acts
on
L^{2}({\mathbb{R}^{2d}})
, and in particular on the elementary tensors
(f\otimes g)(x,y)=f(x)g(y)
for
f,g\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. Motivated by a factorization of the short-time Fourier transform and
the Wigner distribution
[
20
, Lem. 3.1.2, 4.3.3]
, one can introduce a time-frequency representation as

(1.2)

\mathrm{W}_{{\bm{\mathcal{A}}}}(f,g)=\mu({\bm{\mathcal{A}}})(f\otimes\bar{g})\qquad f,g\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}).

Following Cordero and Rodino
[
8
,
9
]
, who studied pseudodifferential operators and modulation spaces by means of
these transforms, we call this time-frequency representation a metaplectic time-frequency representation or an
{\bm{\mathcal{A}}}
-Wigner
distribution.
All common time-frequency representations, in particular, the short-time Fourier transform, the ambiguity function, all
\tau
-Wigner
distributions, the canonical linear transforms, and their
extension in
[
2
]
can be written as a symplectic time-frequency representation with a
particular
4d\times 4d
symplectic matrix
{\bm{\mathcal{A}}}
, see Section 7. The question
arises whether the usual uncertainty principles can be extended and formulated for
a general symplectic time-frequency representation. One would expect an affirmative answer,
but after checking a few examples, one begins to understand that this question
leads to an interesting problem, whose answer is by far not
obvious.

Examples.

Since the intertwining operators
\mu(\mathcal{A})\in{\mathrm{Sp}(2d,{\mathbb{R}})}
are unique up to a multiplication with a global phase
\tau\in{\mathbb{T}}

[
11
,
14
,
20
]
, we omit the global phase in the examples below.

(i) Let
{\bm{\mathcal{A}}}=\bm{\mathcal{J}}
be the standard symplectic matrix in
{\mathrm{Sp}(4d,{\mathbb{R}})}
. Then
\mu(\bm{\mathcal{J}})F=\hat{F}
for
F\in L^{2}({\mathbb{R}^{2d}})
is just the Fourier transform. Evidently,

\mathrm{W}_{\bm{\mathcal{J}}}(f\otimes\overline{g})=\hat{f}\otimes\hat{\overline{g}}
is supported on a set of finite measure
(or on a compact set) if and only if both
\hat{f}
and
\hat{g}

have a support of finite measure (compact support) in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. So there is
no Benedicks-type uncertainty principle.

(ii) Let
L\in\mathrm{GL}(2d,\mathbb{R})
and

{\bm{\mathcal{A}}}=\begin{pmatrix}L&0\\
0&L^{-t}\end{pmatrix}\in\mathrm{Sp}(4d,\mathbb{R})\,.

The corresponding metaplectic operator is given by a unitary coordinate
transform
\mu({\bm{\mathcal{A}}})f(\lambda)=|\det L|^{-1/2}F(L^{-1}\lambda)
for

\lambda\in{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}
and
F\in L^{2}({\mathbb{R}^{2d}})
. Clearly, a coordinate transform
changes the support and its measure, but the compactness and the
finiteness of the measure are preserved. Thus, if we assume that the
time-frequency representation
\mathrm{W}_{{\bm{\mathcal{A}}}}(f\otimes\overline{g})
has compact support,
then also
f\otimes{g}
has compact support and every pair
(f,g)

of compactly supported functions in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
yields a counter-example
to the expected version of Benedicks’s theorem.

(iii) Let
Q=Q^{t}\in\mathrm{GL}(2d,\mathbb{R})
be symmetric and

{\bm{\mathcal{A}}}=\begin{pmatrix}I&0\\
Q&I\end{pmatrix}\in\mathrm{Sp}(4d,\mathbb{R})\,.

Then the operator
\mu({\bm{\mathcal{A}}})F(\lambda)=e^{i\pi\lambda\cdot Q\lambda}F(\lambda)
for
F\in L^{2}({\mathbb{R}^{2d}})
amounts to the
multiplication by a “chirp”. Thus,
\supp\,\mathrm{W}_{{\bm{\mathcal{A}}}}(f\otimes\overline{g})=\supp\,(f\otimes\overline{g})
, and again every pair
(f,g)

of compactly supported functions in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
yields a counter-example
to the analogous version of Benedicks’s theorem.

(iv) By contrast, we can represent the short-time Fourier transform, the ambiguity function,
and the Wigner distribution in the form
\mathrm{W}_{\bm{\mathcal{A}}}(f,g)
for some
symplectic matrix
{\bm{\mathcal{A}}}\in\mathrm{Sp}(4d,\mathbb{R})
. In this case,
if

\supp\,\mathrm{W}_{\bm{\mathcal{A}}}(f,g)
has finite measure, then
f\equiv 0
or

g\equiv 0
by the results in
[
25
,
28
,
19
,
36
]
. Thus a Benedicks-type uncertainty principle holds.

These examples show that some metaplectic time-frequency representations obey an uncertainty principle in the
style of Benedicks, whereas others violate this uncertainty principle. These initial examples look rather puzzling and have struck our
curiosity and form the motivation for our work.

We investigate the Benedicks-type uncertainty principle for general metaplectic time-frequency representations
and will provide a complete description of those
time-frequency representations that yield a Benedicks-type uncertainty principle. As this description requires
some structure theory of the symplectic group and many technical
preliminaries, we formulate the most important special case of
Benedicks’s uncertainty principle in order to offer a flavour of our general result.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
{\bm{\mathcal{A}}}=\begin{pmatrix}A&B\\
C&D\end{pmatrix}\in\mathrm{Sp}(4d,\mathbb{R})
, and assume that
B
is
invertible. Consider the
symmetric matrix
P=B^{-1}A=\begin{pmatrix}P_{11}&P_{12}\\
P_{21}&P_{22}\end{pmatrix}
.

(i)

If
P
is not block-diagonal, i.e.,
P_{12}=P_{21}^{t}\neq 0
, then
\mathrm{W}_{{\bm{\mathcal{A}}}}(f,g)

satisfies the
Benedicks-type uncertainty principle:

If
\mathrm{W}_{{\bm{\mathcal{A}}}}(f,g)
is supported on a set of finite measure,

then
f\equiv 0
or
g\equiv 0
.

(ii)

If
P
is block-diagonal, i.e.,
P_{12}=P_{21}^{t}=0
,
then there exist non-zero functions
f,g\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, such that the support of
\mathrm{W}_{{\bm{\mathcal{A}}}}(f,g)
is compact.

The matrices addressed in Theorem
1.1
are called
free symplectic matrices
.
In the full treatment of Benedicks’s uncertainty principle we will remove the
condition
\det B\neq 0
with a trick that might be useful in
other contexts as well. Multiplying
{\bm{\mathcal{A}}}
with an orthogonal symplectic matrix of the form

\bm{\mathcal{R}}_{\tau I}=\begin{pmatrix}aI&bI\\
-bI&aI\end{pmatrix}\in{\mathrm{Sp}(4d,{\mathbb{R}})}\cap\mathrm{O}(4d,{\mathbb{R}}),

with
\tau=a+ib\in{\mathbb{T}}
, we can guarantee that the resulting
matrix
{\bm{\mathcal{A}}}\bm{\mathcal{R}}_{\tau}
is free for all but
finitely many
\tau\in{\mathbb{T}}
, and
Theorem
1.1
is applicable, see Lemma
4.3
.

The proof of Theorem
1.1
is based on several key elements.

(i) We use a version of the Iwasawa decomposition of
symplectic matrices that yields a factorization of a symplectic matrix
into three matrices of the type that were discussed in the examples
(i) — (iii). In view of the (counter-)examples above, the main problem is
the maximal compact subgroup of
\mathrm{Sp}(4d,\mathbb{R})
, see Section
4
. A main role in
the investigation will be played by a recent singular value
decomposition of orthogonal symplectic matrices. In this respect we
would like to acknowledge inspiration from the note
of Jaming who implicitly used matrix factorizations
to prove uncertainty principles for the fractional Fourier transform
[
26
]
.

(ii) We introduce a new notion of a partial short-time Fourier transform by
freezing some variables and taking the short-time Fourier transform only over a subset of
variables.

Our final result will be an algorithm for deciding
which metaplectic Wigner distributions
W_{\bm{\mathcal{A}}}(f,g)
obey the
Benedicks-type uncertainty principle. Although our main theorem contains a complete
description of all metaplectic time-frequency representations for which
Benedicks’s uncertainty principle holds, the deeper geometric meaning of the conditions
still remains unclear to us.

In the last part, we will investigate quadratic time-frequency representations of the form
\mathrm{W}_{\mathcal{A}}(f,{f})=\mu({\bm{\mathcal{A}}})(f\otimes\overline{f})
. It may seem that nothing new is gained by
studying quadratic time-frequency representations. However, the example of the so-called
Rihaczek distribution defined by

(1.3)

R(f,g)(x,\omega)=e^{-2\pi ix\cdot\omega}f(x)\overline{\hat{g}(\omega)}

points again to some mystery. Again
R(f,g)
can be written as a
symplectic time-frequency representation.
For the sesquilinear Rihaczek
distribution
R(f,g)
, clearly there are
f,g\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, such
that
R(f,g)
has compact support, and Benedicks-type uncertainty principle cannot be
true. However, if the quadratic
expression
R(f,f)
is supported on a set of finite measure, then

f=0
. This is just the classical uncertainty principle of Benedicks and Amrein-Berthier. Thus there is
a subtle difference between quadratic and sesquilinear time-frequency representations.
In
Theorem
6.3
, we will characterize all quadratic symplectic time-frequency representation
\mathrm{W}_{{\bm{\mathcal{A}}}}(f,f)
that
satisfy Benedicks’s uncertainty principle.

The paper is organized as follows: in Section
2
we
introduce the symplectic group and the metaplectic group. In Section

1.1

We then address the structure of the symplectic

\tau

7
.

2.
The symplectic group and the metaplectic group

2.1.
The symplectic group

The symplectic group

{\mathrm{Sp}(2d,{\mathbb{R}})}

consists of the matrices
\mathcal{A}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2d\times 2d}

which preserve
the standard skew-symmetric bilinear form, i.e.,

(2.1)

{\mathrm{Sp}(2d,{\mathbb{R}})}=\left\{\mathcal{A}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2d\times 2d}:\mathcal{A}^{t}{\mathcal{J}}\mathcal{A}={\mathcal{J}}\right\},

where
{\mathcal{J}}
denotes the standard skew-symmetric matrix defined in (
1.1
). The symplectic group is a subgroup of the special linear group, and only when
d=1
do these two groups coincide.
The simplest symplectic matrices are of the following type:
2
2

2

We use the convention
A+iB\in{\mathbb{C}}^{d\times d}
with
A={\mathrm{Re}\,}(A+iB)
,
B={\mathrm{Im}\,}(A+iB)
.

(2.2)

{\mathcal{V}}_{Q}=\begin{pmatrix}I&0\\
Q&I\end{pmatrix},\quad\mathcal{D}_{L}=\begin{pmatrix}L&0\\
0&L^{-T}\end{pmatrix},\quad\mathcal{R}_{A+iB}=\begin{pmatrix}A&B\\
-B&A\end{pmatrix}.

The block structure of
{\mathcal{J}}
provides four algebraic equations that characterize the blocks of symplectic matrices
[
14
]
. With these equations, one can easily verify that
\mathcal{D}_{L}
are the only block-diagonal symplectic matrices,
{\mathcal{V}}_{Q}
is symplectic if and only if
Q
is symmetric, and
\mathcal{R}_{A+iB}
is symplectic if and only if
U=A+iB
is unitary on
{{\mathbb{C}}^{d}}
.

The symplectic group is a classical Lie group. It possesses a
double cover, the so-called
metaplectic group

{\mathrm{Mp}(2d,{\mathbb{R}})}

[
29
,
21
]
.

2.2.
Metaplectic operators

Let
T_{x}f(t)=f(t-x)
and
M_{\omega}f(t)=e^{2\pi i\omega\cdot t}
,
t,x,\omega\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
be the operators of translation and modulation. The
symmetric time-frequency shifts

\rho(x,\omega)=T_{x/2}M_{\omega}T_{x/2}

are a projective unitary representation of
{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}
, as they satisfy

(2.3)

\rho(\lambda+\gamma)=e^{\pi i\lambda\cdot{\mathcal{J}}\gamma}\rho(\lambda)\rho(\gamma),\qquad\lambda,\gamma\in{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}.

This relation extends to a unitary representation of the Heisenberg group
[
14
,
20
,
11
]
.
From this perspective,
the symplectic group is precisely the subgroup of
\mathrm{GL}(2d,{\mathbb{R}})
which preserve the phase factor
e^{\pi i\lambda\cdot{\mathcal{J}}\gamma}
in (
2.3
).

By results in representation theory of the Heisenberg group, specifically, the Stone-von-Neumann theorem
[
14
,
20
,
11
]
,
there exist unitary operators
\mu(\mathcal{A})
with

(2.4)

\rho(\mathcal{A}\lambda)=\mu(\mathcal{A})\rho(\lambda)\mu(\mathcal{A})^{-1},

that is,
\mu(\mathcal{A})
arises as an intertwining operator. These operators
are called
metaplectic operators
. The operator is unique up to
a constant phase factor: it is obvious that any
\tau\mu(\mathcal{A})
,
|\tau|=1
,
also satisfies (
2.4
). The mapping
\mathcal{A}\mapsto\mu(\mathcal{A})
is a projective, unitary representation of the symplectic
group called the
oscillator representation

[
34
]
.

We already encountered the standard examples: the unitary dilations, the linear chirps, and the Fourier transform;

(2.5)

\begin{split}\mu(\mathcal{D}_{L})f(x)&=\lvert\det{L}\rvert^{-1/2}f(L^{-1}x),\\
\mu({\mathcal{V}}_{Q})f(x)&=e^{\pi ix^{t}Qx}f(x),\\
\mu({\mathcal{J}})f(\omega)&=\hat{f}(\omega).\end{split}

In this work, we will work with this particular choice of
\mu(V_{Q}),\mu(\mathcal{D}_{L})
and
\mu({\mathcal{J}})
, but the results are independent of the
particular choice of the global phase. In addition, one can show that
with a suitable choice of the phase the metaplectic operators
generate a group that is isomorphic to the twofold cover of the symplectic group

{\mathrm{Sp}(2d,{\mathbb{R}})}
. This is the metaplectic group. The construction is rather subtle
[
34
]
, but is
not needed in the sequel. In the following we identify the abstract
twofold cover
{\mathrm{Mp}(2d,{\mathbb{R}})}
with the group of operators generated by the
intertwining operators
\mu(\mathcal{A})

with the correct phase factors
.

With this designation, the projection

\pi^{\mathrm{Mp}}:{\mathrm{Mp}(2d,{\mathbb{R}})}\to{\mathrm{Sp}(2d,{\mathbb{R}})}

is a surjective group homomorphism, with kernel

\mathrm{ker}(\pi^{\mathrm{Mp}})=\left\{\mathrm{id},-\mathrm{id}\right\}
, and we often say that the metaplectic
operator
\mu(\mathcal{A})\in{\mathrm{Mp}(2d,{\mathbb{R}})}
projects to the matrix
\mathcal{A}\in{\mathrm{Sp}(2d,{\mathbb{R}})}
.

2.3.
Free symplectic matrices and quadratic Fourier transforms

An important subset of
{\mathrm{Sp}(2d,{\mathbb{R}})}
is the set
of
free symplectic matrices
, i.e., the symplectic matrices with
an invertible upper-right
d\times d
block. These possess a factorization into the elementary symplectic matrices (
2.2
) as follows.

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
\mathcal{A}\in{\mathrm{Sp}(2d,{\mathbb{R}})}
be a
free symplectic matrix
, i.e.,
\mathcal{A}
has a block structure

(2.6)

\mathcal{A}=\begin{pmatrix}A&B\\
C&D\end{pmatrix}

with
A,B,C,D\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d\times d}
and invertible

B
. Then

\mathcal{A}={\mathcal{V}}_{DB^{-1}}\mathcal{D}_{B}{\mathcal{J}}{\mathcal{V}}_{B^{-1}A}.

The proof is a short computation and can be found in
[
10
, Prop. 2.39.]
.

As a consequence, metaplectic operators
corresponding to a free symplectic matrix can then be factored into the
elementary operators (Fourier transform, linear coordinate change, and
multiplication by a chirp) up to a phase factor. The composition of
these elementary operators yields
integral operators with a Gaussian
kernel
[
32
]
, which are sometimes called

quadratic Fourier transforms

[
11
]
. Although we do
not need this fact, we mention that every symplectic matrix is the
product of two free symplectic matrices. Thus the matrices
{\mathcal{V}}_{Q},\mathcal{D}_{L},
and
{\mathcal{J}}
generate the symplectic group.

Notation.
We distinguish matrices in
{\mathrm{Sp}(2d,{\mathbb{R}})}
and
{\mathrm{Sp}(4d,{\mathbb{R}})}
by
the following notation where the basic matrices on
{\mathbb{R}}^{4d}
are in
bold face:

(2.7)

\displaystyle\mathcal{A},\quad{\mathcal{V}}_{Q},\mathcal{D}_{L},\mathcal{R}_{U}

\displaystyle\in{\mathrm{Sp}(2d,{\mathbb{R}})},\ Q,L\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d\times d},U\in{\mathbb{C}}^{d\times d},

(2.8)

\displaystyle\bm{\mathcal{A}},\quad\bm{\mathcal{V}}_{Q},\bm{\mathcal{D}}_{L},\bm{\mathcal{R}}_{U}

\displaystyle\in{\mathrm{Sp}(4d,{\mathbb{R}})},\ Q,L\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2d\times 2d},U\in{\mathbb{C}}^{2d\times 2d}.

The blocks of a symplectic matrix are in general denoted with

A,B,C,D
. For special matrices we use the following convention:
L

is used for invertible matrices,
W,V
denote
orthogonal matrices,
P,Q
denote symmetric matrices,
U
is always a
unitary matrix, and
\Sigma
stands for a unitary diagonal
matrix. Note that only
U
and
\Sigma
are
complex-valued
. For

A,B\in{\mathbb{C}}^{d\times d}
we denote with
\mathrm{diag}(A,B)=\begin{pmatrix}A&0\\
0&B\end{pmatrix}
block-diagonal matrices with
d\times d
blocks.

Remark 2.2
.

One can easily verify that
{\mathrm{Sp}(2d,{\mathbb{R}})}\times{\mathrm{Sp}(2d,{\mathbb{R}})}
can be embedded in

{\mathrm{Sp}(4d,{\mathbb{R}})}
by embedding the generators
\mathcal{D}_{L},{\mathcal{V}}_{Q}
and
\mathcal{R}_{U}
in the following way:

(2.9)

\begin{split}(\mathcal{D}_{L_{1}},\mathcal{D}_{L_{2}})&\mapsto\bm{\mathcal{D}}_{\mathrm{diag}(L_{1},L_{2})},\quad L_{1},L_{2}\in\mathrm{GL}(d,{\mathbb{R}}),\\
({\mathcal{V}}_{Q_{1}},{\mathcal{V}}_{Q_{2}})&\mapsto\bm{\mathcal{V}}_{\mathrm{diag}(Q_{1},Q_{2})},\quad Q_{1}=Q_{1}^{t},Q_{2}=Q_{2}^{t}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d\times d},\\
(\mathcal{R}_{U_{1}},\mathcal{R}_{U_{2}})&\mapsto\bm{\mathcal{R}}_{\mathrm{diag}(U_{1},U_{2})},\quad U_{1},U_{2}\in\mathrm{U}(d,{\mathbb{C}}).\end{split}

On the operator level, the action on the elementary tensors is given by

\displaystyle\mu(\bm{\mathcal{D}}_{\mathrm{diag}(L_{1},L_{2})})(f\otimes g)

\displaystyle=\mu(\mathcal{D}_{L_{1}})f\otimes\mu(\mathcal{D}_{L_{2}})g,

(2.10)

\displaystyle\mu(\bm{\mathcal{V}}_{\mathrm{diag}(Q_{1},Q_{2})})(f\otimes g)

\displaystyle=\mu({\mathcal{V}}_{Q_{1}})f\otimes\mu({\mathcal{V}}_{Q_{2}})g,

\displaystyle\mu(\bm{\mathcal{R}}_{\mathrm{diag}(U_{1},U_{2})})(f\otimes g)

\displaystyle=\mu(\mathcal{R}_{U_{1}})f\otimes\mu(\mathcal{R}_{U_{2}})g.

We will frequently use the following formulas for the elementary
symplectic matrices
{\mathcal{V}}_{Q}
,
\mathcal{D}_{L}
,
\mathcal{R}_{U}
.

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
Q,Q_{1},Q_{2}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d\times d}
be symmetric,
L,L_{1},L_{2}\in\mathrm{GL}(d,{\mathbb{R}})
,
U,U_{1},U_{2}\in\mathrm{U}(d,{\mathbb{C}})
. Then:

(i)

If
U
is real-valued, i.e.,
U\in\textrm{O}(d,{\mathbb{R}})
,
then
\mathcal{R}_{U}=\mathcal{D}_{U}
.

(ii)

Interaction with the Fourier transform:
{\mathcal{J}}\mathcal{D}_{L}=\mathcal{D}_{L^{-t}}{\mathcal{J}}
.

(iii)

For
W\in\mathrm{O}(d,{\mathbb{R}})
we have

(2.11)

\mathcal{D}_{W}{\mathcal{V}}_{Q}\mathcal{D}_{W^{t}}={\mathcal{V}}_{WQW^{t}}\,.

(iv)

Homomorphisms:

(2.12)

{\mathcal{V}}_{Q_{1}}{\mathcal{V}}_{Q_{2}}={\mathcal{V}}_{Q_{1}+Q_{2}},\quad\mathcal{D}_{L_{1}}\mathcal{D}_{L_{2}}=\mathcal{D}_{L_{1}L_{2}},\quad\mathcal{R}_{U_{1}}\mathcal{R}_{U_{2}}=\mathcal{R}_{U_{1}U_{2}}.

Proof.

These properties follow immediately from the definition of the
elementary symplectic matrices (
2.2
).
∎

3.
Uncertainty principles for free symplectic
matrices

In this section, we will prove a
Benedicks-type uncertainty principle for the
{\bm{\mathcal{A}}}
-Wigner distribution of
a free
symplectic matrix
{\bm{\mathcal{A}}}\in{\mathrm{Sp}(4d,{\mathbb{R}})}
. The general case will be reduced to
this case in Section 5.

Given a function
f\in{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})}
and writing
x=(x_{1},x_{2})
for
x_{1}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{k}
and
x_{2}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d-k}

for some
1\leq k<d
, we denote with
f_{x_{2}}
the restriction

(3.1)

f_{x_{2}}:{\mathbb{R}}^{k}\to{\mathbb{C}},\quad x_{1}\mapsto f(x_{1},x_{2}).

By Fubini’s theorem, for almost all
x_{2}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d-k}
, the function

f_{x_{2}}
is square-integrable. This fact allows us to define the partial short-time Fourier transform in
k
variables almost everywhere.

Definition 3.1
.

Let
f,g\in{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})},\,g\not\equiv 0
, and let
k\in\{1,\dots,d\}
. We define the partial short-time Fourier transform in the first
k
variables of the function
f
with respect to
g
as the function

(3.2)

\begin{split}{V}^{k}_{g}f(x_{1},x_{2},\omega_{1},\omega_{2})&=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{k}}f(t,x_{2})\overline{g(t-x_{1},-\omega_{2})}e^{-2\pi it\cdot\omega_{1}}\,dt\\
&=\langle f_{x_{2}},M_{\omega_{1}}T_{x_{1}}g_{-\omega_{2}}\rangle\\
&={V}_{g_{-\omega_{2}}}f_{x_{2}}(x_{1},\omega_{1}),\end{split}

for
(x_{1},x_{2},\omega_{1},\omega_{2})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{k}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d-k}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{k}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d-k}
.

If
k=d
,
{V}^{d}_{g}={V}_{g}
is the classical short-time Fourier transform. Otherwise, the integral in (
3.2
) is well-defined almost everywhere because
f_{x_{2}}
and
g_{\omega_{2}}
are square-integrable for almost all
x_{2},\omega_{2}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d-k}
.
For such
x_{2},\omega_{2}
,
{V}_{g}^{k}f
is continuous in
x_{1},\omega_{1}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{k}
.

Like the short-time Fourier transform, the partial short-time Fourier transform can be interpreted as a metaplectic
time-frequency representation. To see this, we note that

(x_{1},x_{2},t,\omega_{2})\mapsto(t,x_{2},t-x_{1},-\omega_{2})
is a linear
mapping
L_{k}^{-1}\in\mathrm{GL}(2d,{\mathbb{R}})
on
{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}
, and the
partial Fourier transform is the metaplectic operator that
projects to the matrix

(3.3)

\bm{\mathcal{J}}_{k}=\bm{\mathcal{R}}_{\mathrm{diag}(I_{k},I_{d-k},iI_{k},I_{d-k}})\in{\mathrm{Sp}(4d,{\mathbb{R}})}.

Therefore the partial short-time Fourier transform
V^{k}_{g}f
is the metaplectic time-frequency distribution
\mathrm{W}_{\bm{\mathcal{J}}_{k}\bm{\mathcal{D}}_{L_{k}}}(f,g)
.

The following lemma is a Benedicks-type uncertainty principle for the partial short-time Fourier transform,
and is a first step towards a general result.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
f,g\in{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})}
and let
k\in\{1,\dots,d\}
.
If
{V}^{k}_{g}f
is supported on a set of finite measure, then
f\equiv 0
or
g\equiv 0
.

Proof.

If
k=d
, then this is Benedicks’s uncertainty principle for the
short-time Fourier transform
[
28
,
25
,
19
,
36
]
. Therefore, we may assume
that
1\leq k<d
.

By Fubini’s theorem,
there exists a set
N_{1}\subseteq{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}
of measure zero such that for all
(x_{1},x_{2},\omega_{1},\omega_{2})\in{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}\setminus N_{1}
, the functions
f_{x_{2}},g_{\omega_{2}}

are square-integrable and the representation (
3.2
) holds pointwise on
{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}\setminus N_{1}
.

Let
\mathcal{S}\subseteq{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}\setminus N_{1}
be the support of
{V}^{k}_{g}f
, where we have already omitted the null set of restrictions
f_{x_{2}},\,g_{\omega_{2}}
,
x_{2},\omega_{2}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d-k}
that are meaningless or not in
L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{k})
. By
Fubini’s theorem,
there exists a further set
N_{2}\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{d-k}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d-k}
of measure zero
such that for fixed
(x_{2},\omega_{2})\in({\mathbb{R}}^{d-k}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d-k})\setminus N_{2}
every section

\mathcal{S}_{x_{2},\omega_{2}}\coloneqq\left\{(x_{1},\omega_{1})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{k}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{k}:\ (x_{1},x_{2},\omega_{1},\omega_{2})\in\mathcal{S}\right\}

(i)

is measurable and
has finite measure in
{\mathbb{R}}^{k}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{k}
,

(ii)

the measure of
\mathcal{S}
is given by

(3.4)

\displaystyle|\mathcal{S}|=\int_{({\mathbb{R}}^{d-k}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d-k})\setminus N_{2}}|\mathcal{S}_{x_{2},\omega_{2}}|\,dx_{2}d\omega_{2},

(iii)

\mathcal{S}_{x_{2},\omega_{2}}=\supp{V}_{g_{-\omega_{2}}}f_{x_{2}}
.

Since
\mathcal{S}_{x_{2},\omega_{2}}
has finite measure in
{\mathbb{R}}^{k}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{k}
, Benedicks’s uncertainty principle for the short-time Fourier transform
[
28
,
25
,
19
,
36
]
implies that
f_{x_{2}}\equiv 0
or
g_{\omega_{2}}\equiv 0
, and as a consequence,
|\mathcal{S}_{x_{2},\omega_{2}}|=0
.

Going back to (
3.4
), this implies that
|\mathcal{S}|=0
, hence
{V}^{k}_{g}f\equiv 0
.
As already noted in Definition
3.1
,
{V}^{k}_{g}f=\mu({\bm{\mathcal{A}}}_{k})(f\otimes\overline{g})
for a suitable symplectic matrix
{\bm{\mathcal{A}}}_{k}\in{\mathrm{Sp}(4d,{\mathbb{R}})}
.
Since
\mu({\bm{\mathcal{A}}}_{k})
is unitary, it follows that
f\otimes\overline{g}\equiv 0
, or equivalently,
f\equiv 0
or
g\equiv 0
.
This proves the claim.
∎

As noted in the introduction, the operators
\mu(\mathcal{D}_{L})
and
\mu({\mathcal{V}}_{Q})
are not relevant for the validity of a Benedicks-type uncertainty principle. Since we will use this fact frequently, we state it formally.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
{\bm{\mathcal{A}}}=\bm{\mathcal{V}}_{Q}\bm{\mathcal{D}}_{L}{\bm{\mathcal{B}}}\in{\mathrm{Sp}(4d,{\mathbb{R}})}
and
f,g\in{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})}
non-zero. Then

(3.5)

|\supp\,\mathrm{W}_{{\bm{\mathcal{A}}}}(f,g)|<\infty

if and only if

|\supp\,\mathrm{W}_{{\bm{\mathcal{B}}}}(f,g)|<\infty.

Proof.

By (
2.5
), for

f,g\in{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})}
,
\lambda\in{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}
, we have

(3.6)

\begin{split}|\mathrm{W}_{\bm{\mathcal{A}}}(f,g)(\lambda)|&=|\mu(\bm{\mathcal{V}}_{Q})\mu(\bm{\mathcal{D}}_{L})\mu({\bm{\mathcal{B}}})(f\otimes\overline{g})(\lambda)|\\
&=|\det L|^{-1/2}|e^{\pi i\lambda\cdot Q\lambda}\mu({\bm{\mathcal{B}}})(f\otimes\overline{g})(L^{-1}\lambda)|.\end{split}

Therefore,

(3.7)

\supp\,\mathrm{W}_{{\bm{\mathcal{A}}}}(f,g)=L\cdot\supp\,\mathrm{W}_{{\bm{\mathcal{B}}}}(f,g).

Clearly
\supp\,\mathrm{W}_{{\bm{\mathcal{A}}}}(f,g)
has finite measure,
if and only if

\supp\,\mathrm{W}_{{\bm{\mathcal{B}}}}(f,g)
has finite measure.
∎

We now prove an uncertainty principle for the
{\bm{\mathcal{A}}}
-Wigner
distribution of a special class of free symplectic matrices.

Theorem 3.4
.

Let
{\bm{\mathcal{A}}}=\bm{\mathcal{J}}\bm{\mathcal{V}}_{P}=\begin{pmatrix}P&I\\
-I&0\end{pmatrix}\in{\mathrm{Sp}(4d,{\mathbb{R}})}
be a free symplectic matrix with

P=\begin{pmatrix}P_{11}&P_{12}\\
P_{21}&P_{22}\end{pmatrix}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2d\times 2d},

such that the
d\times d
blocks on the side diagonal
P_{12}=P_{21}^{t}\neq 0
are not zero. Then Benedicks’s uncertainty principle holds:

If
\mathrm{W}_{\bm{\mathcal{A}}}(f,g)
is supported on a set of finite measure for some
f,g\in{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})}
,

then
f\equiv 0
or

g\equiv 0
.

Proof.

Let
f,g\in{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})}
be two functions such that
\mathrm{W}_{{\bm{\mathcal{A}}}}(f,g)
is supported on a set of finite measure. We want to show that
f\equiv 0
or
g\equiv 0
.
We begin with a reduction to metaplectic operators with a simple action on elementary tensors.

Since

P_{12}=P_{21}^{t}\neq 0
, it
has a singular value decomposition

(3.8)

P_{12}=W_{1}\cdot\Gamma\cdot W_{2}^{t},

where
W_{1},W_{2}\in\mathrm{O}(d,{\mathbb{R}})
and
\Gamma
is a diagonal
matrix with non-negative entries, i.e.,

\Gamma=\begin{pmatrix}\Gamma_{1}&{0}_{k\times k}\\
{0}_{d-k\times k}&{0}_{d-k\times d-k}\end{pmatrix},\quad\Gamma_{1}\in\mathrm{GL}(k,{\mathbb{R}}),\quad k=\mathrm{rank}(P_{12})\geq 1.

We write

\displaystyle P

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}0&P_{12}\\
P_{12}^{t}&0\end{pmatrix}+\begin{pmatrix}P_{11}&0\\
0&P_{22}\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}W_{1}&0\\
0&W_{2}\end{pmatrix}\,\begin{pmatrix}0&\Gamma\\
\Gamma&0\end{pmatrix}\,\begin{pmatrix}W_{1}^{t}&0\\
0&W_{2}^{t}\end{pmatrix}+\begin{pmatrix}P_{11}&0\\
0&P_{22}\end{pmatrix}.

Using Lemma
2.3
, we can write
{\bm{\mathcal{A}}}
as

(3.9)

\begin{split}{\bm{\mathcal{A}}}&=\bm{\mathcal{J}}\bm{\mathcal{V}}_{P}=\bm{\mathcal{J}}\bm{\mathcal{D}}_{\mathrm{diag}(W_{1},W_{2})}\bm{\mathcal{V}}_{\begin{pmatrix}0&\Gamma\\
\Gamma&0\end{pmatrix}}\bm{\mathcal{D}}_{\mathrm{diag}(W_{1},W_{2})^{t}}\bm{\mathcal{V}}_{\mathrm{diag}(P_{11},P_{22})}\\[4.30554pt]
&=\bm{\mathcal{D}}_{\mathrm{diag}(W_{1},W_{2})}\bm{\mathcal{J}}\bm{\mathcal{V}}_{\begin{pmatrix}0&\Gamma\\
\Gamma&0\end{pmatrix}}\bm{\mathcal{D}}_{\mathrm{diag}(W_{1}^{t},W_{2}^{t})}\bm{\mathcal{V}}_{\mathrm{diag}(P_{11},P_{22})}.\end{split}

We note that
\mu(D_{W_{1}^{t}}{\mathcal{V}}_{P_{11}}),\mu(D_{W_{2}^{t}}{\mathcal{V}}_{-P_{22}})

belong to
{\mathrm{Mp}(2d,{\mathbb{R}})}
and set
f_{1}\coloneqq\mu(\mathcal{D}_{W_{1}^{t}}{\mathcal{V}}_{P_{11}})f
,
g_{1}\coloneqq\mu(D_{W_{2}^{t}}{\mathcal{V}}_{-P_{22}})g\in{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})}
.
Using Remark
2.2
and
\mu({\mathcal{J}})={\mathcal{F}}
, we can rewrite
the absolute value of
\mathrm{W}_{{\bm{\mathcal{A}}}}(f,g)
as

(3.10)

\displaystyle|\mathrm{W}_{{\bm{\mathcal{A}}}}(f,g)|=\big|\mu(\bm{\mathcal{D}}_{\mathrm{diag}(W_{1},W_{2})}){\mathcal{F}}\mu\big(\bm{\mathcal{V}}_{\begin{pmatrix}0&\Gamma\\
\Gamma&0\end{pmatrix}}\big)(f_{1}\otimes\overline{g_{1}})\big|.

By Lemma
3.3
,
{\mathcal{F}}\mu(\bm{\mathcal{V}}_{\begin{pmatrix}0&\Gamma\\
\Gamma&0\end{pmatrix}})(f_{1}\otimes\overline{g_{1}})

is also supported on a set of finite measure.

By the definition of
{\mathcal{V}}_{P}
in (
2.5
),

(3.11)

\mu(\bm{\mathcal{V}}_{\begin{pmatrix}0&\Gamma\\
\Gamma&0\end{pmatrix}})(f_{1}\otimes\overline{g_{1}})(x,y)=e^{2\pi i\Gamma x\cdot y}f_{1}(x)\overline{g_{1}}(y).

This function is in
L^{2}({\mathbb{R}^{2d}})
, and, with a suitable interpretation the
integrals, its Fourier transform is

\displaystyle\phantom{=}{\mathcal{F}}\mu(\bm{\mathcal{V}}_{\begin{pmatrix}0&\Gamma\\
\Gamma&0\end{pmatrix}})(f_{1}\otimes\overline{g_{1}})(\omega,\xi)

\displaystyle=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}f_{1}(x)e^{-2\pi i\omega\cdot x}\,\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\overline{g_{1}}(y)e^{2\pi i\Gamma x\cdot y}\,e^{-2\pi i\xi\cdot y}\,dy\,dx

(3.12)

\displaystyle=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}f_{1}(x)e^{-2\pi i\omega\cdot x}\,\overline{\hat{g_{1}}}(\Gamma x-\xi)\ dx.

If
\Gamma
were invertible, then we could identify the above expression, after a coordinate change, as a short-time Fourier transform with a support of finite measure, and we could finalize the proof.
However, in general, the off-diagonal blocks
P_{12}
,
P_{21}=P_{12}^{t}
, and thus
\Gamma
, are
not
invertible.
This is the reason why we have to use the partial short-time Fourier transform in (
3.2
).
To process the integral in (
3.12
) further, we evaluate
\mathrm{W}_{\bm{\mathcal{A}}}(f,g)
at the points

(3.13)

\big(\mathrm{diag}(\Gamma_{1},I)\omega,\xi\big)=(\Gamma_{1}\omega_{1},\omega_{2},\xi)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{k}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d-k}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}

instead of at
(\omega,\xi)
. With this coordinate change, the final expression in (

) becomes

3
3

3

If

{\mathbb{R}}^{d-k}

(3.14)

\begin{split}&\phantom{=}{\mathcal{F}}\mu(\bm{\mathcal{V}}_{\begin{pmatrix}0&\Gamma\\
\Gamma&0\end{pmatrix}})(f_{1}\otimes\overline{\hat{g_{1}}})\big(\mathrm{diag}(\Gamma_{1},I)\omega,\xi\big)\\
&=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}f_{1}(x)e^{-2\pi i\omega\cdot\mathrm{diag}(\Gamma_{1},I)x}\,\overline{\hat{g_{1}}}(\Gamma x-\xi)\ dx\\
&=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{k}}\overline{\hat{g_{1}}}(\Gamma_{1}x_{1}-\xi_{1},-\xi_{2})e^{-2\pi i\omega_{1}\cdot\Gamma_{1}x_{1}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d-k}}f_{1}(x_{1},x_{2})e^{-2\pi i\omega_{2}\cdot x_{2}}\,dx_{2}\ dx_{1}.\end{split}

We set

and

(3.15)

\begin{split}f_{2}(\Gamma_{1}x_{1},\omega_{2})&\coloneqq\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d-k}}f_{1}(x_{1},x_{2})e^{-2\pi i\omega_{2}\cdot x_{2}}\,dx_{2},\\

Both

and

are (rescaled) partial Fourier transforms of

, (

\begin{split}&\phantom{=}{\mathcal{F}}\mu(\bm{\mathcal{V}}_{\begin{pmatrix}0&\Gamma\\

&\overset{t=\Gamma_{1}x_{1}}{=}|\det\Gamma_{1}|^{-1}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{k}}f_{2}(t,\omega_{2})\overline{g_{2}}(t-\xi_{1},-\xi_{2})e^{-2\pi i\omega_{1}\cdot t}\,dt\\
&=|\det\Gamma_{1}|^{-1}{V}_{g_{2}}^{k}f_{2}(\xi_{1},\omega_{2},\omega_{1},\xi_{2}).\end{split}

Since
\mathrm{W}_{\bm{\mathcal{A}}}(f,g)
has a support of finite measure, the transformed expression (

) also has a support of finite measure.
By Lemma
3.2
, this implies that
f_{2}\equiv 0
or
g_{2}\equiv 0
.
Since
f_{2}
and

are the image of

Proof of Theorem
1.1
.

The theorem formulated in the introduction is a direct consequence of Theorem
3.4
. Let
{\bm{\mathcal{A}}}=\begin{pmatrix}A&B\\
C&D\end{pmatrix}\in{\mathrm{Sp}(4d,{\mathbb{R}})}

be a free symplectic matrix. We set

and

By Proposition
2.1
,
{\bm{\mathcal{A}}}
factors as

{\bm{\mathcal{A}}}=\bm{\mathcal{V}}_{Q}\bm{\mathcal{D}}_{B}\bm{\mathcal{J}}\bm{\mathcal{V}}_{P}.

By Lemma

,
\mathrm{W}_{\bm{\mathcal{A}}}(f,g)

\mathrm{W}_{\bm{\mathcal{J}}\bm{\mathcal{V}}_{P}}(f,g)
is

(i)
Assume

implies that

or

, as claimed.

(ii)
If

4.
The structure of the symplectic group

Before treating
uncertainty principles for arbitrary symplectic matrices,
we need a deeper
grasp
of the structure of the symplectic group and its factorizations. Our
main tool is a decomposition of symplectic matrices that resembles
the Iwazawa decomposition and is called the pre-Iwazawa
decomposition
[
10
]
.

Lemma 4.1
.

Every symplectic matrix
\mathcal{A}\in{\mathrm{Sp}(4d,{\mathbb{R}})}
can be factored as

(4.1)

\mathcal{A}={\mathcal{V}}_{Q}\mathcal{D}_{L}\mathcal{R}_{U}=\begin{pmatrix}I&0\\
Q&I\end{pmatrix}\cdot\begin{pmatrix}L&0\\
0&L^{-t}\end{pmatrix}\cdot\begin{pmatrix}A&B\\
-B&A\end{pmatrix}

with
L\in\mathrm{GL}(d,{\mathbb{R}})
,
Q=Q^{t}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d\times d}
, and
U=A+iB\in\mathrm{U}(d,{\mathbb{C}})
. This decomposition is unique if
L
is
symmetric.

Proof.

The decomposition can be derived easily from the Iwazawa
decomposition of
{\mathrm{Sp}(4d,{\mathbb{R}})}

[
24
,
29
]
. A nice
and direct proof was given by deGosson
[
10
, Sec. 2.2.2]
. It has the
additional advantage that it does not require any knowledge of Lie
group theory and provides explicit formulas for
Q,L,A,B
.
∎

The advantage of (
4.1
) is the fact that
the operators
\mu({\mathcal{V}}_{Q}),\,\mu(\mathcal{D}_{L})
do not change the nature of the
support of a function, so that a support of
finite measure (or compact support) is preserved, as observed in
Lemma
3.3
. Thus the factor for the validity of an uncertainty principle is played by the symplectic rotations
\mathcal{R}_{U}\in{\mathrm{Sp}(2d,{\mathbb{R}})}
.
Since we have already established an uncertainty principle for free
symplectic matrices, we aim to reduce the general case to this special case.

We make use of the following result

[
16
, Thm. 5.1.]
, which is a version of a joint singular value decomposition over
{\mathbb{R}}
of the real and imaginary part of
U\in\mathrm{U}(d,{\mathbb{C}})
.

Lemma 4.2
.

For every
U=A+iB\in\mathrm{U}(d,{\mathbb{C}})
there exist orthogonal matrices
W,V\in\mathrm{O}(d,{\mathbb{R}})
and a diagonal unitary matrix
\Sigma=\Sigma_{r}+i\Sigma_{i}\in\mathrm{U}(d,{\mathbb{C}})
such that

U=W\Sigma V^{t},\quad\text{i.e., }\quad A=W\Sigma_{r}V^{t},\ B=W\Sigma_{i}V^{t}.

To move from free symplectic matrices to general symplectic matrices, we use the following trick.

Lemma 4.3
.

For every
U=A+iB\in\mathrm{U}(d,{\mathbb{C}})
there exists a
\tau\in{\mathbb{T}}
such that the orthogonal symplectic matrix

\mathcal{R}_{\tau U}=\mathcal{R}_{\tau I}\mathcal{R}_{U}=\mathcal{R}_{U}\mathcal{R}_{\tau I}

is
free
and
{\mathrm{Im}\,}(\tau U)
is invertible.
Furthermore, for a fixed
U
,
{\mathrm{Im}\,}(\tau U)
fails to be invertible
for at most
2d
choices of
\tau\in{\mathbb{T}}
.

Proof.

By Lemma
4.2
,
U
factors as
U=W\Sigma V^{t}

with
W,V\in\mathrm{O}(d,{\mathbb{R}})
and a diagonal unitary matrix
\Sigma=\mathrm{diag}(\Sigma_{kk})_{1\leq k\leq d}\in\mathrm{U}(d,{\mathbb{C}})

with
|\Sigma_{kk}|=1
. Then for all
\tau\in{\mathbb{T}}
,

\begin{split}{\mathrm{Im}\,}(\tau U)&=W\cdot{\mathrm{Im}\,}(\tau\Sigma)\cdot V^{t}.\end{split}

This shows that the
diagonal matrix
{\mathrm{Im}\,}(\tau\Sigma)
has zeros on the diagonal if
and only if
\tau=\pm\overline{\Sigma_{kk}}
for some
1\leq k\leq d
. Thus
{\mathrm{Im}\,}(\tau U)

is singular if and only if
\tau
assumes one of the (at most

2d
) values
\tau=\pm\,\overline{\Sigma_{kk}}
for some
1\leq k\leq d
.

This proves both the existence of
\tau\in{\mathbb{T}}
as desired as well as the upper bound on the number of exceptions.

Note that for
U=A+iB\in\mathrm{U}(d,{\mathbb{C}})
, the corresponding orthogonal symplectic matrix
\mathcal{R}_{U}=\begin{pmatrix}A&B\\
-B&A\end{pmatrix}
is free if and only if
B={\mathrm{Im}\,}(U)
is invertible. Thus,
\mathcal{R}_{\tau U}
is free if and only if
{\mathrm{Im}\,}(\tau U)
is invertible.
∎

4.1.
Fractional Fourier transforms

Before proceeding with the reductions, we take a closer look at the metaplectic operators which project to
\mathcal{R}_{\Sigma}
for some
diagonal
unitary matrix
\Sigma\in\mathrm{U}(d,{\mathbb{C}})
.

In the one-dimensional case, the maximal compact subgroup of
\mathrm{Sp}(1,\mathbb{R})
consists of the matrices
\mathcal{R}_{\sigma}=\mathcal{R}_{e^{i\alpha}}=\begin{pmatrix}\cos(\alpha)&\sin(\alpha)\\
-\sin(\alpha)&\cos(\alpha)\end{pmatrix}
and is thus isomorphic to the torus
{\mathbb{T}}
. The corresponding metaplectic operator is (up to a phase factor) the
fractional Fourier transform
{\mathcal{F}}_{\sigma}=\mu(\mathcal{R}_{\sigma})
, and in the literature, it is often identified with the corresponding angle
\alpha\in[0,2\pi)
.
In particular,
{\mathcal{F}}_{1}f=f
,
{\mathcal{F}}_{-1}f=f(-\cdot)
, and
{\mathcal{F}}_{i}f={\mathcal{F}}f
is the classical Fourier transform
[
30
,
35
]
.
If
\sigma\not\in\{-1,1\}
, then
{\mathcal{F}}_{\sigma}
is a quadratic Fourier transform with the explicit
formula

(4.2)

{\mathcal{F}}_{\sigma}f(\omega)=k_{\sigma}\int_{\mathbb{R}}f(t)e^{\pi i\cot(\alpha)\omega^{2}-2\pi i\csc(\alpha)\omega t+\pi i\cot(\alpha)t^{2}}\,dt,

where

k_{\sigma}\in{\mathbb{C}}
is a normalizing constant.

In higher dimensions, the correspondence of diagonal unitary matrices
to fractional Fourier transforms generalizes as follows. Given a diagonal matrix
\Sigma=\mathrm{diag}(\Sigma_{jj})_{1\leq j\leq d}\in\mathrm{U}(d,{\mathbb{C}})
, let
\Sigma_{j}
be the diagonal matrix with
\Sigma_{jj}
on the
j
-th diagonal entry and otherwise
1
. Then
\mu(\mathcal{R}_{\Sigma_{j}})={\mathcal{F}}_{\Sigma_{j}}
acts as the partial fractional Fourier transform in the
j
-th coordinate, and

(4.3)

\Sigma=\prod\limits_{j=1}^{d}\Sigma_{j},\qquad\mathcal{R}_{\Sigma}=\prod\limits_{j=1}^{d}\mathcal{R}_{\Sigma_{j}},\qquad\mu(\mathcal{R}_{\Sigma})={\mathcal{F}}_{\Sigma}=\prod\limits_{j=1}^{d}{\mathcal{F}}_{\Sigma_{j}}.

In particular,
{\mathcal{F}}_{\Sigma}
acts on elementary tensors as

(4.4)

{\mathcal{F}}_{\Sigma}(f_{1}\otimes\dots\otimes f_{d})={\mathcal{F}}_{\Sigma_{11}}f_{1}\otimes\dots\otimes{\mathcal{F}}_{\Sigma_{dd}}f_{d}.

All
{\mathcal{F}}_{\Sigma}
commute. Furthermore, from the integral
representation (
4.2
) follows that there exists a constant
c_{\Sigma}\in{\mathbb{T}}
such that

(4.5)

{\mathcal{F}}_{\Sigma}\overline{f}=c_{\Sigma}\,\overline{{\mathcal{F}}_{\overline{\Sigma}}f}\,.

We conclude the section with an uncertainty principle for pairs
(f,{\mathcal{F}}_{\Sigma}f)
for diagonal
\Sigma\in\mathrm{U}(d,{\mathbb{C}})
. For
\Sigma=\mathrm{diag}(i,i,\dots,i)
, the corresponding operators is just the
ordinary Fourier transform
{\mathcal{F}}_{\Sigma}={\mathcal{F}}
, and the result is just Benedicks’s and
Amrein-Berthier’s original uncertainty principle.

Theorem 4.4
.

Let
\Sigma\in\mathrm{U}(d,{\mathbb{C}})
be a diagonal unitary matrix with a non-zero imaginary part. Then Benedicks’s uncertainty principle holds:

If both
f
and
{\mathcal{F}}_{\Sigma}f
are supported on a set of finite measure, then
f
is the zero function.

Proof.

Assume
f
and
{\mathcal{F}}_{\Sigma}f
are supported on a set of finite measure. We want to show that
f
is the zero function.

Let
\Sigma=A+iB
. If
B
is invertible, then

\mathcal{R}_{\Sigma}=\begin{pmatrix}A&B\\
-B&A\end{pmatrix}
is a free symplectic matrix with a factorization

(4.6)

\mathcal{R}_{\Sigma}={\mathcal{V}}_{AB^{-1}}\mathcal{D}_{B}{\mathcal{J}}{\mathcal{V}}_{B^{-1}A}={\mathcal{V}}_{AB^{-1}}{\mathcal{J}}\mathcal{D}_{B^{-t}}{\mathcal{V}}_{B^{-1}A}\,,

according to Proposition
2.1
.
By (
2.5
),
\mu(V_{Q})
and
\mu(D_{L})
have no substantial effect on the support, in particular, both
f_{1}=\mu(\mathcal{D}_{B^{-t}}{\mathcal{V}}_{B^{-1}A})f
and

(4.7)

\mu({\mathcal{V}}_{AB^{-1}})^{-1}{\mathcal{F}}_{\Sigma}f={\mathcal{F}}\mathcal{D}_{B^{-t}}{\mathcal{V}}_{B^{-1}A}f={\mathcal{F}}f_{1}

are supported on a set of finite measure. By the classical Benedicks’s uncertainty principle
[
3
,
1
]
,
f_{1}
is the zero function, thus
f=\mu(\mathcal{D}_{B-1}{\mathcal{V}}_{B^{-t}A})^{-1}f_{1}\equiv 0
.

If
B
is not invertible, then we consider restrictions
f_{x}
to the range of
B
analogously to the proof of Theorem
3.4
.
∎

In essence, this proof is the same as Jaming’s
[
26
]
. Our
contribution is to make the underlying factorization of metaplectic
operators explicit and visible and then use it for other uncertainty principles.
The numerous uncertainty principles for the fractional Fourier transform and the
canonical linear transforms all work with the explicit
formula (
4.2
). Predictably, the proofs tend to become complicated
quickly, and so different methods seem preferable.

5.
General versions of Benedicks’s uncertainty principle

In this section,
we provide a criterion for the validity of an uncertainty principle
for
\mathrm{W}_{\bm{\mathcal{A}}}(f,g)
. Technically, we will reduce the general case to the special case of free symplectic matrices.

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
\bm{\mathcal{R}}_{U}\in{\mathrm{Sp}(4d,{\mathbb{R}})}
for some
U\in\mathrm{U}(2d,{\mathbb{C}})
.
Then the following are equivalent.

(i)

There exist non-zero
f,g\in{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})}
with

|\supp\,\mathrm{W}_{\bm{\mathcal{R}}_{U}}(f,g)|<\infty.

(ii)

For all
\tau\in{\mathbb{T}}

there exist non-zero
f_{\tau},g_{\tau}\in{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})}
with

|\supp\,\mathrm{W}_{\bm{\mathcal{R}}_{\tau U}}(f_{\tau},g_{\tau})|<\infty.

Proof.

Let
\tau\in{\mathbb{T}}
. Then for all
f,g\in{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})}
holds

(5.1)

\displaystyle|\mathrm{W}_{\bm{\mathcal{R}}_{U}}(f,g)|

\displaystyle=|\mu(\bm{\mathcal{R}}_{\tau U})\mu(\bm{\mathcal{R}}_{\overline{\tau}I})(f\otimes\overline{g})|

(5.2)

\displaystyle\overset{\eqref{eq:_frac_tensor}}{\underset{\eqref{eq:frac_inv_rule}}{=}}|\mu(\bm{\mathcal{R}}_{{\tau}U})(\mu(\mathcal{R}_{\overline{\tau}I})f\otimes\overline{\mu(\mathcal{R}_{\tau I})g})|.

The equivalence follows by choosing

f_{\tau}=\mu(\mathcal{R}_{\overline{\tau}I})f,\quad g_{\tau}=\mu(\mathcal{R}_{\tau I})g.\qed

The characterization of
\mathrm{W}_{\bm{\mathcal{A}}}
,
{\bm{\mathcal{A}}}\in{\mathrm{Sp}(4d,{\mathbb{R}})}
, which obey Benedicks’s uncertainty principle is a direct consequence of Theorem
3.4
, Lemma
3.3
, and Lemma
5.1
.

Theorem 5.2
.

Let

{\bm{\mathcal{A}}}\in\mathrm{Sp}(4d,{\mathbb{R}})

be a symplectic matrix with pre-Iwasawa decomposition

(5.3)

{\bm{\mathcal{A}}}=\bm{\mathcal{V}}_{Q}\bm{\mathcal{D}}_{L}\bm{\mathcal{R}}_{U}.

Let
\tau\in{\mathbb{T}}
such that
{\mathrm{Im}\,}(\tau U)
is invertible.
If
P={\mathrm{Im}\,}(\tau U)^{-1}{\mathrm{Re}\,}(\tau U)
is not block-diagonal, then Benedicks’s uncertainty principle holds:

If
\mathrm{W}_{\bm{\mathcal{A}}}(f,g)
is supported on a set of finite measure for some
f,g\in{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})}
,

then
f\equiv 0
or
g\equiv 0
.

Proof.

Assume that
\mathrm{W}_{{\bm{\mathcal{A}}}}(f_{0},g_{0})
with non-zero
f_{0},g_{0}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
has a
support of finite measure. Then
\mathrm{W}_{\bm{\mathcal{R}}_{U}}(f_{0},g_{0})
has
also a support of finite measure by Lemma
3.3
.

Now choose
\tau\in\mathbb{T}
such that
\bm{\mathcal{R}}_{\tau U}
is a free
symplectic matrix; this is possible by
Lemma
4.3
. Then by Lemma
5.1
there exist
non-zero
f,g\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, such that
\mathrm{W}_{\bm{\mathcal{R}}_{\tau U}}(f,g)
is supported on a set of finite
measure.
Set
A={\mathrm{Re}\,}(\tau U)
and
B={\mathrm{Im}\,}(\tau U)
, and
P=B^{-1}A
.

By Proposition
2.1
,

(5.4)

\bm{\mathcal{R}}_{\tau U}=A+iB=\bm{\mathcal{V}}_{AB^{-1}}\bm{\mathcal{D}}_{B}\bm{\mathcal{J}}\bm{\mathcal{V}}_{B^{-1}A}.

By Lemma
3.3
again,
\mathrm{W}_{\bm{\mathcal{J}}\bm{\mathcal{V}}_{P}}(f,g)
is also supported on a set of finite measure. Since

P=B^{-1}A
is not block-diagonal, we can apply Theorem

3.4
and conclude that
f\equiv 0
or
g\equiv 0
. Consequently, the same
conclusion holds for
W_{{\bm{\mathcal{A}}}}(f,g)
as claimed.
∎

A special case of Benedicks’s uncertainty principle was already obtained
in
[
2
]
for the class of metaplectic time-frequency representations of the form
\mathrm{W}(f,g)=\mathcal{F}_{2}\bm{\mathcal{D}}_{L}(f\otimes\bar{g})=\bm{\mathcal{R}}_{\mathrm{diag}(I,iI)}\bm{\mathcal{D}}_{L}(f\otimes\bar{g})
for
L=\begin{pmatrix}A&B\\
C&D\end{pmatrix}
with invertible blocks
B
and
D
. These time-frequency representations
can be related directly to the short-time Fourier transform and require less work.

By Lemma
4.3
, there always exists a
\tau\in{\mathbb{T}}
such that
{\mathrm{Im}\,}(\tau U)
is invertible, so Theorem

5.2
can be applied to every symplectic matrix.
However, for an arbitrary symplectic matrix
{\bm{\mathcal{A}}}\in{\mathrm{Sp}(4d,{\mathbb{R}})}
, it is not
obvious how to check the assumptions of Theorem
5.2
directly for

{\bm{\mathcal{A}}}
. In the following we elaborate on this question and will
present an algorithm to decide whether or not a Benedicks-type uncertainty principle holds for
W_{{\bm{\mathcal{A}}}}
.

Lemma 5.3
.

Let
U=A+iB\in\mathrm{U}(2d,{\mathbb{C}})
be a unitary matrix with an invertible imaginary part
B\in\mathrm{GL}(2d,{\mathbb{R}})
and let
P=B^{-1}A
. Then the following are equivalent.

(i)

There exist symmetric matrices
P_{1},P_{2}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d\times d}

such that
P
is block-diagonal,

P=\mathrm{diag}(P_{1},P_{2}).

(ii)

There exist orthogonal matrices
W\in\mathrm{O}(2d,{\mathbb{R}})
,

V_{1},V_{2}\in\mathrm{O}(d,{\mathbb{R}})
, and diagonal unitary matrices
\Sigma_{1},\Sigma_{2}\in\mathrm{U}(d,{\mathbb{C}})
with

(5.5)

U=W\cdot\mathrm{diag}(\Sigma_{1},\Sigma_{2})\cdot\mathrm{diag}(V_{1},V_{2})^{t}=W\cdot\mathrm{diag}(\Sigma_{1}V_{1}^{t},\Sigma_{2}V_{2}^{t}).

(iii)

The unitary matrix
U^{t}U
is block-diagonal.

Proof.

We will show the chain of implications (ii)
\Rightarrow
(iii)
\Rightarrow
(i)
\Rightarrow
(ii).

A simple computation shows that (ii) implies (iii).

(iii)
\Rightarrow
(ii) Since
U=A+iB
is unitary, its real and imaginary parts satisfy

(5.6)

I={\mathrm{Re}\,}(U^{*}U)=A^{t}A+B^{t}B\qquad\text{and}\qquad 0={\mathrm{Im}\,}(U^{*}U)=A^{t}B-B^{t}A.

Then
B^{t}A
is symmetric and

(5.7)

\begin{split}U^{t}U=A^{t}A-B^{t}B+i(A^{t}B+B^{t}A)=I-2B^{t}B+2iB^{t}A.\end{split}

By assumption,
U^{t}U
is block-diagonal, thus both its real part
I-2B^{t}B
and its imaginary part
2B^{t}A
are block-diagonal. Therefore,
B^{t}B
is block-diagonal, and invertible by assumption on
B
.
From this, we conclude that

(5.8)

P=B^{-1}A=B^{-1}B^{-t}B^{t}A=(B^{t}B)^{-1}\,(B^{t}A)

is block-diagonal and symmetric as a product of block-diagonal,
symmetric matrices.

(i)
\Rightarrow
(ii). Since the matrix
P=B^{-1}A
is symmetric, the matrix
I+P^{2}
is positive definite, and the following matrix is well-defined, with invertible imaginary part:

(5.9)

U^{\prime}=(I+P^{2})^{-1/2}(P+iI)=A^{\prime}+iB^{\prime}.

The matrix
U^{\prime}
is also unitary, because
U^{\prime}U^{\prime*}=U^{\prime*}U^{\prime}=I
.
Furthermore, we also have
B^{\prime-1}A^{\prime}=(1+P^{2})^{1/2}[(1+P^{2})^{-1/2}P]=P
.
Since
B,B^{\prime}
are invertible,
W^{\prime}=BB^{\prime-1}
is invertible, and consequently,

(5.10)

A=BP=W^{\prime}B^{\prime}P=W^{\prime}A^{\prime}.

Thus
U=W^{\prime}U^{\prime}
. By assumption,
P
is block-diagonal, hence
U^{\prime}

is block-diagonal as well.
By Lemma
4.2
, there exist matrices
W_{1},W_{2},V_{1},V_{2}\in\mathrm{O}(d,{\mathbb{R}})
and diagonal matrices
\Sigma_{1},\Sigma_{2}\in\mathrm{U}(d,{\mathbb{C}})
such that

(5.11)

U^{\prime}=\begin{pmatrix}W_{1}&0\\
0&W_{2}\end{pmatrix}\cdot\begin{pmatrix}\Sigma_{1}&0\\
0&\Sigma_{2}\end{pmatrix}\cdot\begin{pmatrix}V_{1}&0\\
0&V_{2}\end{pmatrix}

We set

(5.12)

W=W^{\prime}\cdot\mathrm{diag}(W_{1},W_{2})

to obtain the claimed factorization.
∎

We now formulate the main result.

Theorem 5.4
.

Let

{\bm{\mathcal{A}}}\in\mathrm{Sp}(4d,{\mathbb{R}})

be a symplectic matrix with pre-Iwasawa decomposition

(5.13)

{\bm{\mathcal{A}}}=\bm{\mathcal{V}}_{Q}\bm{\mathcal{D}}_{L}\bm{\mathcal{R}}_{U}.

Then the following alternative holds.

(i)

If
U^{t}U
is not block-diagonal, then Benedicks’s uncertainty principle holds:

If
\mathrm{W}_{\bm{\mathcal{A}}}(f,g)
is supported on a set of finite measure for some
f,g\in{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})}
,

then
f\equiv 0
or
g\equiv 0
.

(ii)

If
U^{t}U
is block-diagonal, then there exist non-zero
f,g\in{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})}
, such that
\mathrm{W}_{\bm{\mathcal{A}}}(f,{g})
is supported on a compact set, in particular, on a set of finite measure.

Proof.

(i) The uncertainty principle is a direct consequence of Theorem

5.2
and Lemma

5.3
. To see this, let
f,g\in{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})}
such
that
\mathrm{W}_{\bm{\mathcal{A}}}(f,g)
is supported on a set of finite measure.

By Lemma
4.3
, there exists a
\tau\in{\mathbb{T}}
such that
{\mathrm{Im}\,}(\tau U)
is invertible. By assumption, the matrix

(5.14)

(\tau U)^{t}(\tau U)=\tau^{2}U^{t}U

is not-block-diagonal. Consequently,
by Lemma
5.3
, the symmetric matrix
P={\mathrm{Im}\,}(\tau U)^{-1}{\mathrm{Re}\,}(\tau U)
is not block-diagonal either. Theorem

5.2
now implies that
f\equiv 0
or
g\equiv 0
.

(ii) We now prove the second statement. If
U^{t}U
is block-diagonal, so is the matrix
(\tau U)^{t}(\tau U)=\tau^{2}U^{t}U
for any
\tau\in{\mathbb{T}}
.
Using Lemma
4.3
, we can choose
\tau\in{\mathbb{T}}

such that
{\mathrm{Im}\,}(\tau U)
is invertible and apply Lemma

5.3
to write
\tau U
as

(5.15)

\tau U=W\cdot\mathrm{diag}(\Sigma_{1,\tau}V_{1}^{t},\Sigma_{2,\tau}V_{2}^{t})

for some
W\in\mathrm{O}(2d,{\mathbb{R}})
,
V_{1},V_{2}\in\mathrm{O}(d,{\mathbb{R}})
and diagonal unitary matrices
\Sigma_{1,\tau},\Sigma_{2,\tau}\in\mathrm{U}(d,{\mathbb{C}})
.
Set
\Sigma_{1}=\overline{\tau}\Sigma_{1,\tau}
and
\Sigma_{2}=\overline{\tau}\Sigma_{2,\tau}
, then

(5.16)

U=W\cdot\mathrm{diag}(\Sigma_{1}V_{1}^{t},\Sigma_{2}V_{2}^{t}).

By Remark
2.2
,
for all
f,g\in{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})}
,

(5.17)

\displaystyle|\mathrm{W}_{\bm{\mathcal{A}}}(f,{g})|

\displaystyle=|\mu(\bm{\mathcal{V}}_{Q})\mu(\bm{\mathcal{D}}_{L})\mu(\bm{\mathcal{D}}_{W})\mu(\bm{\mathcal{R}}_{\mathrm{diag}(\Sigma_{1}V_{1}^{t},\Sigma_{2}V_{2}^{t})})\,(f\otimes\overline{g})|

(5.18)

\displaystyle=|\det L|^{-1/2}\,|(\mu(\mathcal{R}_{\Sigma_{1}V_{1}^{t}})f\otimes\overline{\mu(\mathcal{R}_{\overline{\Sigma_{2}}V_{2}^{t}})g})(W^{t}L^{-1}\,\cdot\,)|.

Choose non-zero
f_{0},g_{0}\in{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})}
with compact support, then the functions

f=\mu(\mathcal{R}_{\Sigma_{1}V_{1}^{t}})^{-1}f_{0}\quad\text{ and }\quad g=\mu(\mathcal{R}_{\overline{\Sigma_{2}}V_{2}^{t}})^{-1}g_{0}

are non-zero and square-integrable, since
\mu(\mathcal{R}_{\Sigma_{1}V_{1}^{t}}),\mu(\mathcal{R}_{\overline{\Sigma_{2}}V_{2}^{t}})
are unitary operators. Their

{\bm{\mathcal{A}}}
-Wigner distribution is

|\mathrm{W}_{\bm{\mathcal{A}}}(f,g)|=|\det L|^{-1/2}\,|(f_{0}\otimes\overline{g_{0}})(W^{t}L^{-1}\,\cdot\,)|

and has compact support as well.
∎

Theorem
5.4
characterizes all symplectic
matrices for which the metaplectic time-frequency distribution
W_{{\bm{\mathcal{A}}}}

satisfies a Benedicks-type uncertainty principle and yields a simple algorithm to implement this characterization.

Input:
{\bm{\mathcal{A}}}\in{\mathrm{Sp}(4d,{\mathbb{R}})}
.

(1)

Compute the pre-Iwasawa decomposition
{\bm{\mathcal{A}}}=\bm{\mathcal{V}}_{Q}\bm{\mathcal{D}}_{L}\bm{\mathcal{R}}_{U}.

(2)

Compute
U^{t}U
.

(3)

If
U^{t}U
is block-diagonal, then there exist non-zero

f,g\in{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})}
,

such that
\mathrm{W}_{\bm{\mathcal{A}}}(f,{g})
is supported on a compact set.

(4)

Otherwise, whenever
\mathrm{W}_{\bm{\mathcal{A}}}(f,{g})
is supported on a set of finite measure, then
f\equiv 0
or
g\equiv 0
.

To determine the pre-Iwasawa decomposition one can use the explicit formulas
given in
[
10
, Sec. 2.2.2]
. It would be interesting to understand the geometric meaning of the
condition on
U^{t}U
.

6.
Quadratic uncertainty principles

This section is dedicated to uncertainty principles with an additional restriction on the pair
(f,g)
, namely,
g=f
. The
motivation stems from the Rihaczek distribution

\mathrm{R}(f,g)(x,\omega)=e^{-2\pi ix\cdot\omega}f(x)\overline{{\mathcal{F}}g(\omega)},

in which case we can clearly find non-zero functions
f,g\in{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})}
, such that
\mathrm{R}(f,g)
is compactly supported. However, with the additional assumption
g=f
,
\mathrm{R}(f,f)
cannot be supported on a set of finite measure unless
f
is the zero function. This is precisely a reformulation of Benedicks’s uncertainty principle
[
3
,
1
]
.

Before we state the main result, we prove a version of Lemma

5.3
which is better suited for quadratic
time-frequency distributions.

Lemma 6.1
.

Let
U=A+iB\in\mathrm{U}(2d,{\mathbb{C}})
be a unitary matrix. Then the following statements are equivalent.

(i)

There exist an orthogonal matrix
W\in\mathrm{O}(2d,{\mathbb{R}})
and a unitary matrix
\Delta\in\mathrm{U}(d,{\mathbb{C}})
such that

(6.1)

U=W\cdot\mathrm{diag}(\Delta,\overline{\Delta}).

(ii)

There exist an orthogonal matrix
W\in\mathrm{O}(2d,{\mathbb{R}})
and
unitary matrices
\Delta_{1},\Delta_{2}\in\mathrm{U}(d,{\mathbb{C}})
such
that
\Delta_{1}\Delta_{2}^{t}\in\mathrm{U}(d,{\mathbb{C}})
is real-valued and

(6.2)

U=W\cdot\mathrm{diag}(\Delta_{1},\Delta_{2}).

(iii)

There exists a unitary matrix
\bm{\Delta}\in\mathrm{U}(d,{\mathbb{C}})
such that

(6.3)

U^{t}U=\mathrm{diag}(\bm{\Delta},\overline{\bm{\Delta}}).

Proof.

We show the following chain of implications

(6.4)

\text{(i)}\ \Rightarrow\ \text{(iii)}\ \Rightarrow\ \text{(ii)}\ \Rightarrow\ \text{(i)}.

A simple computation shows that (i) implies (iii) with

\bm{\Delta}=\Delta^{t}\Delta
.

(iii)
\Rightarrow
(ii) Let
\tau\in{\mathbb{T}}

such that
{\mathrm{Im}\,}(\tau U)
is invertible according to Lemma
4.3
. By Lemma
5.3
, there exist
W\in\mathrm{O}(2d,{\mathbb{R}})
,
V_{1},V_{2}\in\mathrm{O}(d,{\mathbb{R}})
and diagonal unitary matrices
\Sigma_{1,\tau},\Sigma_{2,\tau}\in\mathrm{U}(d,{\mathbb{C}})
with

(6.5)

\tau U=W\cdot\mathrm{diag}(\Sigma_{1,\tau}V_{1},\Sigma_{2,\tau}V_{2}^{t}).

Set
\Delta_{1}=\bar{\tau}\Sigma_{1}V_{1}^{t},\,\Delta_{2}=\bar{\tau}\Sigma_{2}V_{2}^{t}\in\mathrm{U}(d,{\mathbb{C}})
. Then

(6.6)

U=W\cdot\mathrm{diag}(\Delta_{1},\Delta_{2}).

By assumption,

(6.7)

\mathrm{diag}(\bm{\Delta},\overline{\bm{\Delta}})=U^{t}U=\begin{pmatrix}\Delta_{1}^{t}\Delta_{1}&0\\
0&\Delta_{2}^{t}\Delta_{2}\end{pmatrix},

hence

(6.8)

\overline{\Delta_{1}^{t}\Delta_{1}}=\Delta_{2}^{t}\Delta_{2}\,.

We need to show that
\Delta_{1}\Delta_{2}^{t}
is real-valued.
Indeed, by multiplying (
6.8
) with

\Delta_{1}
from the left and with
\Delta_{2}^{-1}=\Delta_{2}^{*}=\overline{\Delta_{2}^{t}}
from the right, we obtain

(6.9)

\Delta_{1}\Delta_{1}^{*}\overline{\Delta_{1}}{\Delta_{2}^{*}}=\Delta_{1}\Delta_{2}^{t}\Delta_{2}\Delta_{2}^{*}\,,

or equivalently,
\overline{\Delta_{1}\Delta_{2}^{t}}=\Delta_{1}\Delta_{2}^{t}
, as claimed.

(ii)
\Rightarrow
(i) The assumption says that there exist
W^{\prime}\in\mathrm{O}(2d,{\mathbb{R}})
and

\Delta_{1},\Delta_{2}\in\mathrm{U}(d,{\mathbb{C}})
such that

(6.10)

U=W^{\prime}\cdot\mathrm{diag}(\Delta_{1},\Delta_{2})

and the matrix
W_{0}=\Delta_{1}\Delta_{2}^{t}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d\times d}
is
real-valued and hence orthogonal. Using
\Delta_{1}^{-1}=\overline{\Delta_{1}^{t}}=\Delta_{1}^{*}
,

(6.11)

\Delta_{2}=(\Delta_{1}^{-1}W_{0})^{t}=W_{0}^{t}\overline{\Delta_{1}}.

We set
W=W^{\prime}\cdot\mathrm{diag}(I,W_{0}^{t})\in\mathrm{O}(2d,{\mathbb{R}})
and
\Delta=\Delta_{1}\in\mathrm{U}(d,{\mathbb{C}})
. Then

(6.12)

U=W\cdot\mathrm{diag}(\Delta,\overline{\Delta})

and (i) follows.
∎

Before we formulate the main result for quadratic metaplectic Wigner
distributions, we prove an extension of
Theorem
4.4
for a pair

(\mu(\mathcal{R}_{U_{1}})f,\mu(\mathcal{R}_{U_{2}})f)
.

Theorem 6.2
.

Let
\mathcal{R}_{U_{1}},\mathcal{R}_{U_{2}}\in{\mathrm{Sp}(2d,{\mathbb{R}})}
be a orthogonal symplectic matrices such that the matrix
U_{1}U_{2}^{*}
is not real-valued. Then Benedicks’s uncertainty principle holds for the pair
(\mu(\mathcal{R}_{U_{1}})f,\mu(\mathcal{R}_{U_{2}})f)
:

If both
\mu(\mathcal{R}_{U_{1}})f
and
\mu(\mathcal{R}_{U_{2}})f
are supported on a set of finite measure,

then
f\equiv 0
.

Proof.

The proof idea relies on Theorem
4.4

and the matrix decomposition of Lemma
4.2
.

Assume
f\in{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})}
such that both
\mu(\mathcal{R}_{U_{1}})f
and
\mu(\mathcal{R}_{U_{2}})f
are supported on a set of finite measure. We aim to show that
f
is the zero function.

We set
U=U_{1}U_{2}^{*}
and
f_{1}=\mu(\mathcal{R}_{U_{2}})f
.
Then by Remark
2.2

(6.13)

|\mu(\mathcal{R}_{U_{1}})f|=|\mu(\mathcal{R}_{U_{1}})\mu(\mathcal{R}_{U_{2}})^{-1}f_{1}|=|\mu(\mathcal{R}_{U_{1}}\mathcal{R}_{U_{2}}^{-1})f_{1}|=|\mu(\mathcal{R}_{U_{1}U_{2}^{*}})f_{1}|=|\mu(\mathcal{R}_{U})f_{1}|\,.

By construction,
f_{1}
and
\mu(\mathcal{R}_{U})f_{1}
are supported on a set of finite measure.

We now decompose
U
with Lemma
4.2
as
U=W\Sigma V^{t}
with

W,V\in\mathrm{O}(d,{\mathbb{R}})
and a diagonal matrix

\Sigma\in\mathrm{U}(d,{\mathbb{C}})
. By
Lemma
2.3
,
\mathcal{R}_{W}=\mathcal{D}_{W}
and
\mathcal{R}_{V^{t}}=\mathcal{D}_{V^{t}}
,
and
\mu(\mathcal{D}_{W}),\,\mu(\mathcal{D}_{V^{t}})
act as dilation operators,
therefore both
f_{2}=\mu(\mathcal{D}_{V^{t}})f_{1}
and

(6.14)

\mu(\mathcal{R}_{\Sigma}\mathcal{R}_{V^{t}})f_{1}=\mu(\mathcal{R}_{\Sigma})f_{2}={\mathcal{F}}_{\Sigma}f_{2}

are supported on a set of finite measure. Furthermore, since
W,V

are real-valued but
U=U_{1}U_{2}^{*}
is not real-valued by
assumption, we conclude that
\mathrm{Im}\,(\Sigma)\neq 0
.
By Theorem
4.4
(Benedicks’s
uncertainty principle for fractional Fourier transforms),
f_{2}
is the zero function, hence

(6.15)

f=\mu(\mathcal{R}_{U_{2}})^{-1}f_{1}=\mu(\mathcal{R}_{U_{2}})^{-1}\mu(\mathcal{D}_{V^{t}})^{-1}f_{2}\equiv 0,

which concludes the proof.
∎

Finally, we prove the uncertainty principle for quadratic metaplectic Wigner distributions.

Theorem 6.3
.

Let
{\bm{\mathcal{A}}}\in{\mathrm{Sp}(4d,{\mathbb{R}})}
be a symplectic matrix with pre-Iwasawa decomposition

(6.16)

{\bm{\mathcal{A}}}=\bm{\mathcal{V}}_{Q}\bm{\mathcal{D}}_{L}\bm{\mathcal{R}}_{U}.

Then the following alternative holds.

(i)

If
U^{t}U\neq\mathrm{diag}(\Delta,\overline{\Delta})
for all unitary matrices
\Delta\in\mathrm{U}(d,{\mathbb{C}})
, then Benedicks’s uncertainty principle holds:

If
\mathrm{W}_{\bm{\mathcal{A}}}(f,f)
is supported on a set of finite measure for some
f\in{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})}
,

then
f\equiv 0
.

(ii)

If
U^{t}U=\mathrm{diag}(\Delta,\overline{\Delta})
for some unitary matrix
\Delta\in\mathrm{U}(d,{\mathbb{C}})
,
then there exists a non-zero function
f\in{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})}
, such that the support of
\mathrm{W}_{\bm{\mathcal{A}}}(f,f)
is compact, in particular, has finite measure.

Proof.

By Lemma
3.3
, we may assume that
Q=0
and
L=I
and that
{\bm{\mathcal{A}}}=\bm{\mathcal{R}}_{U}
.

We first prove (i) and assume that
\mathrm{W}_{\bm{\mathcal{A}}}(f,f)=\mathrm{W}_{\bm{\mathcal{R}}_{U}}(f,f)
is supported on a set of finite measure for
f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. We distinguish two cases.

Case 1.

U^{t}U
is not block-diagonal. Then Theorem

5.4
with
g=f
implies that
f=0
.

Case 2.

U^{t}U
is block-diagonal, but
U^{t}U\neq\mathrm{diag}(\Delta,\Delta^{t})
. Then by Lemma
6.1
, there exists a matrix
W\in\mathrm{O}(2d,{\mathbb{R}})
and unitary matrices
\Delta_{1},\Delta_{2}\in\mathrm{U}(d,{\mathbb{C}})
with
\Delta_{1}\Delta_{2}^{t}\notin{\mathbb{R}}^{d\times d}
such that

U=W\cdot\mathrm{diag}(\Delta_{1},\Delta_{2}).

Recall that by Lemma
2.3

\mu(\bm{\mathcal{R}}_{W})=\mu(\bm{\mathcal{D}}_{W})
and by Remark
2.2
the action of

\mu(\bm{\mathcal{R}}_{\mathrm{diag}(\Delta_{1},\Delta_{2})})
tensorizes. Consequently,

\displaystyle|\mu(\bm{\mathcal{R}}_{U})(f\otimes\bar{f})|

\displaystyle=|\mu(\bm{\mathcal{D}}_{W})\mu(\bm{\mathcal{R}}_{\mathrm{diag}(\Delta_{1},\Delta_{2})}(f\otimes\bar{f})|

\displaystyle=\big|\mu(\bm{\mathcal{D}}_{W})\big(\mu(\mathcal{R}_{\Delta_{1}}f\otimes\mathcal{R}_{\Delta_{2}}\bar{f})\big)\big|\,.

Since
\mu(\bm{\mathcal{D}}_{W})F
amounts to a rotation of the support of
F
, we
may ignore it and are left with the fact that the tensor product
\mu(\mathcal{R}_{\Delta_{1}})f\otimes\mu(\mathcal{R}_{\Delta_{2}})\bar{f}=\mu(\mathcal{R}_{\Delta_{1}}f\otimes\overline{\mathcal{R}_{\bar{\Delta}_{2}}f)}
is supported on a set of finite measure.

By assumption,
U^{t}U
is not block-diagonal of the form

\mathrm{diag}(\Delta,\bar{\Delta})
, thus condition (ii) of
Lemma
6.1
says that
\Delta_{1}\Delta_{2}^{t}=\Delta_{1}\bar{\Delta}_{2}^{*}

cannot be real-valued. This is precisely the assumption of
Theorem
6.2
, and we conclude that
f=0
.

We now prove (ii). By Lemma
6.1
, there
exists an orthogonal matrix
W\in\mathrm{O}(2d,{\mathbb{R}})
and a unitary
matrix
\Delta\in\mathrm{U}(d,{\mathbb{C}})
such that

U=W\cdot\mathrm{diag}(\Delta,\overline{\Delta}).

Choose non-zero
f_{0}\in{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})}
with compact support
and set

f=\mu(\mathcal{R}_{{{\Delta}}})^{-1}f_{0}.

Then
f\in{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})}
and
f\not\equiv 0
because

\mu(\mathcal{R}_{{{\Delta}}})^{-1}
is a unitary operator.
Using again that
\bm{\mathcal{R}}_{W}=\bm{\mathcal{D}}_{W}
, we obtain

(6.17)

\displaystyle|\mathrm{W}_{\bm{\mathcal{A}}}(f,f)|

\displaystyle=\mu(\bm{\mathcal{D}}_{W})|\mu(\bm{\mathcal{R}}_{\mathrm{diag}(\Delta,\bar{\Delta})})(f\otimes\bar{f})|

(6.18)

\displaystyle=\mu(\bm{\mathcal{D}}_{W})(|\mu(\mathcal{R}_{{\Delta}})f|\otimes|\overline{\mu(\mathcal{R}_{{\Delta}})f}|)

(6.19)

\displaystyle=\mu(\bm{\mathcal{D}}_{W})(|f_{0}|\otimes|{f_{0}}|),

and this function is clearly supported on a compact set.
∎

Remark 6.4
.

Let
\iota f
be either complex conjugation
f\mapsto\bar{f}

or the involution
f\mapsto f^{*},f^{*}(t)=\bar{f}(-t)
. Then
Theorem
6.3
also holds for the quadratic
time-frequency distribution
W_{\mathcal{A}}(f,\iota f)
. The condition
\Delta_{1}\Delta_{2}^{t}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d\times d}
needs to be replaced by the
condition
\Delta_{1}\Delta_{2}^{-1}=\Delta_{1}\overline{\Delta_{2}^{t}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d\times d}
, and the proof is the same.

7.
Examples

In this section, we show how to apply Theorems
5.4
and
6.3
, and
we recover the known uncertainty principles for the short-time Fourier transform, the
ambiguity function, and the
\tau
-Wigner distribution. The symplectic
matrices associated to the short-time Fourier transform and the
\tau
-Wigner distributions
were already derived in
[
9
]
, here we test them with
regard to the validity of the uncertainty principle.

The short-time Fourier transform is given by

\mathrm{V}_{g}f(x,\omega)=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}f(t)\overline{g(t-x)}e^{2\pi i\omega\cdot t}\,dt.

Let

(7.1)

L_{\lx@scalerel@obj{\mathrm{STFT}}}=\begin{pmatrix}I&-I\\
I&0\end{pmatrix}\in\mathrm{GL}(2d,{\mathbb{R}})\quad\text{with inverse}\quad L_{\lx@scalerel@obj{\mathrm{STFT}}}^{-1}=\begin{pmatrix}0&I\\
-I&I\end{pmatrix}

(the matrix
L_{\lx@scalerel@obj{\mathrm{STFT}}}^{-1}
corresponds
to the linear map
(x,t)\mapsto(t,t-x)
). Since the coordinate
change by
L_{\lx@scalerel@obj{\mathrm{STFT}}}
and the partial
Fourier transform are metaplectic operators,

{V}_{g}f
can be written as
the
{\bm{\mathcal{A}}}
-Wigner distribution

{V}_{g}f=\mu({\bm{\mathcal{A}}}_{\lx@scalerel@obj{\mathrm{STFT}}})(f\otimes\overline{g})=\mu(\bm{\mathcal{R}}_{\mathrm{diag}(I,iI)}\bm{\mathcal{D}}_{L_{\mathrm{STFT}}})(f\otimes\overline{g})\text{\ \ with the matrix}

{\bm{\mathcal{A}}}_{\lx@scalerel@obj{\mathrm{STFT}}}=\bm{\mathcal{R}}_{\mathrm{diag}(I,iI)}\bm{\mathcal{D}}_{L_{\mathrm{STFT}}}=\begin{pmatrix}I&-I&0&0\\
0&0&I&I\\
0&0&0&-I\\
-I&0&0&0\end{pmatrix}.

The pre-Iwasawa decomposition of
{\bm{\mathcal{A}}}_{\lx@scalerel@obj{\mathrm{STFT}}}
is given by

(7.2)

{\bm{\mathcal{A}}}_{\lx@scalerel@obj{\mathrm{STFT}}}=\bm{\mathcal{V}}_{-\frac{1}{2}\begin{pmatrix}0&I\\
I&0\end{pmatrix}}\cdot\bm{\mathcal{D}}_{\sqrt{2}I}\cdot\bm{\mathcal{R}}_{U_{\mathrm{STFT}}},\quad U_{\lx@scalerel@obj{\mathrm{STFT}}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{pmatrix}I&-I\\
iI&iI\end{pmatrix}\in\mathrm{U}(2d,{\mathbb{C}}).

The deciding product is

U_{\lx@scalerel@obj{\mathrm{STFT}}}^{t}U_{\lx@scalerel@obj{\mathrm{STFT}}}=\begin{pmatrix}0&-I\\
-I&0\end{pmatrix},

which is not block-diagonal, thus the Benedicks-type uncertainty
principle holds for the short-time Fourier transform, which is known from
[
25
,
28
,
19
,
36
]
.

The ambiguity function is a more symmetric version of the short-time Fourier transform and is defined as

\displaystyle\mathrm{A}(f,g)(x,\omega)

\displaystyle=\int_{{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}}f(t+\tfrac{x}{2})\overline{g(t-\tfrac{x}{2})}e^{-2\pi i\omega\cdot t}\,dt

\displaystyle=e^{\pi ix\cdot\omega}{V}_{g}f(x,\omega)=\mu(\bm{\mathcal{V}}_{\frac{1}{2}\begin{pmatrix}0&I\\
I&0\end{pmatrix}}){V}_{g}f(x,\omega),\quad x,\omega\in{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}},

thus is given by
\mathrm{A}(f,g)=\mu({\bm{\mathcal{A}}}_{\lx@scalerel@obj{\mathrm{A}}})(f,g)
, where

(7.3)

{\bm{\mathcal{A}}}_{\lx@scalerel@obj{\mathrm{A}}}=\bm{\mathcal{V}}_{\frac{1}{2}\begin{pmatrix}0&I\\
I&0\end{pmatrix}}\cdot{\bm{\mathcal{A}}}_{\lx@scalerel@obj{\mathrm{STFT}}}=\begin{pmatrix}I&-I&0&0\\
0&0&I&I\\
0&0&\tfrac{1}{2}I&-\tfrac{1}{2}I\\
-\tfrac{1}{2}I&-\tfrac{1}{2}I&0&0\end{pmatrix}.

By (
7.2
), the pre-Iwasawa decomposition of
{\bm{\mathcal{A}}}_{\lx@scalerel@obj{\mathrm{A}}}
is given by

(7.4)

{\bm{\mathcal{A}}}_{\lx@scalerel@obj{\mathrm{A}}}=\bm{\mathcal{V}}_{0}\cdot\bm{\mathcal{D}}_{\sqrt{2}I}\cdot\bm{\mathcal{R}}_{U_{\mathrm{A}}},\quad U_{\lx@scalerel@obj{\mathrm{A}}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{pmatrix}I&-I\\
iI&iI\end{pmatrix}\in\mathrm{U}(2d,{\mathbb{C}}),

and as above

U^{t}_{\lx@scalerel@obj{\mathrm{A}}}U_{\lx@scalerel@obj{\mathrm{A}}}=\begin{pmatrix}0&-I\\
-I&0\end{pmatrix},

hence it obeys the Benedicks-type uncertainty principle

[
25
,
28
,
19
,
36
]
.

Finally, the
\tau
-Wigner distribution is given by

(7.5)

\begin{split}\mathrm{W}_{\tau}(f,g)(x,\omega)&=\int_{{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}}f(x+\tau t)\overline{g(x-(1-\tau)t)}e^{-2\pi i\omega t}\,dt.\end{split}

This family of time-frequency representations includes the Wigner distribution for
\tau=\frac{1}{2}
and
the Rihaczek distribution for
\tau=0
.
By choosing

(7.6)

L_{\tau}=\begin{pmatrix}I-\tau I&\tau I\\
I&-I\end{pmatrix}\in\mathrm{GL}(2d,{\mathbb{R}})\quad\text{with inverse}\quad L_{\tau}^{-1}=\begin{pmatrix}I&\tau I\\
I&-(1-\tau)I\end{pmatrix},

\mathrm{W}_{\tau}(f,g)
can be written as

\mathrm{W}_{\tau}(f,g)=\mu(\bm{\mathcal{R}}_{\mathrm{diag}(I,iI)}\bm{\mathcal{D}}_{L_{\tau}})(f\otimes\overline{g})=\mu({\bm{\mathcal{A}}}_{\tau})(f\otimes\overline{g})\text{\ \ with the matrix}

{\bm{\mathcal{A}}}_{\tau}=\begin{pmatrix}(1-\tau)I&\tau I&0&0\\
0&0&\tau&-(1-\tau)I\\
0&0&I&I\\
-I&I&0&0\end{pmatrix}.

With the parameter
\alpha_{\tau}\coloneqq(1-2\tau+2\tau^{2})^{1/2}=((1-\tau)^{2}+\tau^{2})^{1/2}

the pre-Iwasawa decomposition of
{\bm{\mathcal{A}}}_{\tau}
is given by

(7.7)

{\bm{\mathcal{A}}}_{\tau}=\bm{\mathcal{V}}_{-\frac{(1-2\tau)}{\alpha_{\tau}^{2}}\begin{pmatrix}0&I\\
I&0\end{pmatrix}}\cdot\bm{\mathcal{D}}_{\alpha_{\tau}I}\cdot\bm{\mathcal{R}}_{U_{\tau}},\quad U_{\tau}=\frac{1}{\alpha_{\tau}}\begin{pmatrix}1-\tau&\tau\\
i\tau&-i(1-\tau)\end{pmatrix}\in\mathrm{U}(2d,{\mathbb{C}}).

The deciding product is given by

U_{\tau}^{t}U_{\tau}=\frac{1}{\alpha_{\tau}^{2}}\begin{pmatrix}(1-2\tau)I&2(1-\tau)\tau I\\
2(1-\tau)\tau I&-(1-2\tau)I\end{pmatrix}.

The matrix is block-diagonal if and only if
\tau=0
or
\tau=1
, thus a Benedicks-type uncertainty principle holds for the
\tau
-Wigner
distribution precisely when
\tau\neq 0,1
, and fails for the
Rihaczek distributions
\tau=0
and
\tau=1
.

Concerning the uncertainty principle for quadratic time-frequency representations in Theorem
6.3
, if
\tau\in\{0,1\}
, then

(7.8)

U_{0}^{t}U_{0}=\begin{pmatrix}I&0\\
0&-I\end{pmatrix},\qquad U_{1}^{t}U_{1}=\begin{pmatrix}-I&0\\
0&I\end{pmatrix}.

These are not of the form
U_{\tau}^{t}U_{\tau}=\mathrm{diag}(\bm{\Delta},\overline{\bm{\Delta}})
. Hence, for all
\tau\in{\mathbb{T}}
,
the only function
f\in{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})}
such that
\mathrm{W}_{\tau}(f,{f})
is supported on a set of finite measure is the zero function.
