How arithmetic and geometry make error correcting codes better
Alain Couvreur

Abstract
This note completes a talk given at the conference Curves over Finite Fields: past, present and future celebrating the publication the book Rational Points on Curves over Finite Fields by J.-P. Serre and organised at Centro de ciencias de Benasque in june 2021. It discusses a part of the history of algebraic geometry codes together with some of their recent applications. A particular focus is done on the “multiplicative” structure of these codes, i.e. their behaviour with respect to the component wise product. Some open questions are raised and discussed.



Introduction

The story of algebraic geometry codes is a fascinating one for both
mathematicians and computer scientists. It lies at the crossroad of
coding theory, number theory and algebraic geometry and testifies of
the need for such interactions between different communities to
provide efficient but also elegant solutions to practical problems.

Coding theory began in the 50’s after the works of Claude Shannon

[
Sha48
]
and Richard Hamming
[
Ham50
]
. The former
developing a stochastic approach for error correction and building the
foundations of information theory and the latter proposing a more
combinatorial point of view. The original motivations came from the need
for error correction in computations and communications. Our history
is full of technologies who would never have been as efficient as they
were without error correcting codes. One can for instance mention
NASA’s program Mariner 9 whose objective was to transmit pictures from
Mars and where the error tolerance has been guaranteed by Reed–Muller
codes.

Today, coding theory has become a rich and wide mathematical theory
with strong connections with combinatorics, probabilities, arithmetic,
geometry and algorithmics. More importantly, the field of applications
of coding theory impressively broadened in 70 years and introducing
codes as objects permitting good communications across noisy channels
would be indisputably a very restrictive description. Coding theory
finds now numerous other applications, for instance in symmetric
cryptography, in public key cryptography or in cloud storage. Results
from coding theory are also used to address problems in other
mathematical areas such as combinatorics or number theory, or in
theoretical computer science with famous results such as the
celebrated PCP theorem
[
ALM+98
]
.

Inside coding theory, algebra always played a particular rule. The
use of algebraic structures to design codes is almost as old as coding
theory itself. Reed–Muller codes
[
Ree54
]
have been designed
using multivariate polynomials over
\mathbf{F}_{2}
, then Reed–Solomon ones

[
RS60
]
are constructed thanks to univariate polynomials and
Berlekamp
[
Ber68
,
Ber15
]
developed a decoding algorithm based on
algebraic features. Interestingly, Berlekamp’s interest on algebraic
codes encouraged him to focus on some computational aspects of finite
fields which lead him to discover his famous algorithm for factorizing
polynomials over finite fields. In summary, algebra had an
indisputable impact on the development of coding theory but the
converse is also true.

Algebraic geometry enters the game in the early 80’s with Goppa’s
seminal article
[
Gop81
]
, followed by the unexpected
and rather counter intuitive result proved by Tsfasman, Vlăduţ
and Zink
[
TVZ82
]
showing that, some codes from modular
or Shimura curves have better parameters than random codes. This
breakthrough motivated the impressive development of the theory of
algebraic geometry codes which today represent a broad and rich
research area with a significant number of independent applications.
The present article presents a part of this story. Then a particular
focus is done on the behaviour of algebraic geometry codes with respect
to the component wise product and its
various applications, to decoding, cryptanalysis or secret sharing.

3
Component wise product

In the last decades, an apparently elementary operation which took an
increasing importance in coding theory is the component wise product
of vectors or codes,
i.e.
the multiplication operation in

\mathbf{F}_{q}^{n}
regarded as a product of rings :

\forall\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{b}\in\mathbf{F}_{q}^{n},\quad\boldsymbol{a}\star\boldsymbol{b}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}(a_{1}b_{1},\dots,a_{n}b_{n}).

This operation extends to codes where, to keep a linear structure,
the product of two codes is defined as the span of the products of
their vectors

Definition 4
.

Given two codes
\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B}\subseteq\mathbf{F}_{q}^{n}
,

\mathcal{A}\star\mathcal{B}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\text{Span}_{\mathbf{F}_{q}}\left\{\boldsymbol{a}\star\boldsymbol{b}\penalty\ |\penalty\ \boldsymbol{a}\in\mathcal{A},\boldsymbol{b}\in\mathcal{B}\right\}.

Importing such a ring structure over
\mathbf{F}_{q}^{n}
is actually rather
natural when studying codes such as AG codes which are obtained by
evaluation of functions living in a ring. Reconsidering
Definition
2
and denoting by
\mathcal{O}_{\mathscr{X},\mathcal{P}}
the
semi-local ring
\bigcap_{P\in\mathcal{P}}\mathcal{O}_{\mathscr{X},P}
, the evaluation map

\text{ev}_{\mathcal{P}}:\left\{\begin{array}[]{ccc}\mathcal{O}_{\mathscr{X},\mathcal{P}}&\longrightarrow&\mathbf{F}_{q}^{n}\\
f&\longmapsto&(f(P_{1}),\dots,f(P_{n}))\end{array}\right.

is a ring homomorphism from
\mathcal{O}_{\mathscr{X},\mathcal{P}}
to
\mathbf{F}_{q}^{n}
equipped
with the
\star
product. Moreover,
\mathcal{C}_{L}({\mathscr{X}},{\mathcal{P}},{G})
is nothing but
the image of
L(G)
by this map. In summary, considering the
\star

product is natural in order to take into account the rich algebraic
structure of AG codes coming from the parent curve.

In the last decades the
\star
product took an increasing importance
in coding theory. For instance, we explain in §
4

and
6
that the design of algebraic decoding algorithms or
the properties of some secret sharing schemes can easily be
interpreted in terms of this operation. The reader interested in an
in–depth study of the arithmetic of this operation together with some
of its applications is encouraged to look at the excellent survey

[
Ran14
]

For now, and because of the underlying algebraic geometric structure,
AG codes have a very peculiar behaviour with respect to the
\star

product. Indeed, for a general code
\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathbf{F}_{q}^{n}
, we have

\dim\mathcal{C}\star\mathcal{C}\leqslant\min\left\{n,\ {\dim\mathcal{C}+1\choose 2}\right\}.

(4)

Moreover, it is proved in
[
CCMZ15
]
that for a random code the
above inequality is an equality with a high probability. Typically,
the
\star

or has the same dimension as the symmetric square
S^{2}\mathcal{C}
of
\mathcal{C}

(note that there is a surjective canonical map

S^{2}\mathcal{C}\rightarrow\mathcal{C}\star\mathcal{C}
). For non symmetric products, one
can observe very similar behaviours. Namely, given two codes

\mathcal{C},\mathcal{D}\subseteq\mathbf{F}_{q}^{n}
, we have

\dim\mathcal{C}\star\mathcal{D}\leqslant\min\left\{n,\ \dim\mathcal{C}\cdot\dim\mathcal{D}-{\dim\mathcal{C}\cap\mathcal{D}\choose 2}\right\},

where again the inequality is typically an equality when
\mathcal{C},\mathcal{D}

are random.

The design of efficient decoding algorithms for AG codes is an
important part of the theory starting in the late 80’s with the work
of Justesen, Larsen, Jensen, Havemose and Høholdt

[
JLJ+89

Interestingly, the first works on the decoding of AG codes

[

[

to an observation, the very reason why AG codes are equipped with
polynomial time decoders while arbitrary codes are not is their
peculiar behaviour with respect to the
\star

4.1
Algebraic decoding in a nutshell

The decoding paradigm presented by Pellikaan, is now known as
Error
Correcting Pairs
. We give here a rather unusual presentation of this
algorithm that we expect to be somehow more elementary to understand.

Consider a code
\mathcal{C}
and a received vector
{\boldsymbol{y}}=\boldsymbol{c}+\boldsymbol{e}
where

\boldsymbol{c}\in\mathcal{C}
and
\boldsymbol{e}\in\mathbf{F}_{q}^{n}
has weight
w_{H}(\boldsymbol{e})\leqslant t
,
where
w_{H}(\cdot)
is defined in §
1
and
t
is
a constant that will be clarified further.
Obviously,
{\boldsymbol{y}}
should be considered as known while
\boldsymbol{c},\boldsymbol{e}
are not.
The key of algebraic decoding is to introduce an auxiliary code

\mathcal{A}
in order to localise the error positions. The code
\mathcal{A}

should be chosen so that
\dim\mathcal{A}>t
and the product

\mathcal{A}\star\mathcal{C}
is “not too large”. In particular

\mathcal{A}\star\mathcal{C}\varsubsetneq\mathbf{F}_{q}^{n}
.

The first step of the algorithm consists in
computing the space

K\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\left\{\boldsymbol{a}\in\mathcal{A}\penalty\ |\penalty\ \boldsymbol{a}\star{\boldsymbol{y}}\in\mathcal{A}\star\mathcal{C}\right\}.

This space can be computed by Gaussian elimination with the very knowledge
of
{\boldsymbol{y}}
and
\mathcal{A},\mathcal{C}
while, by definition of
\mathcal{A}\star\mathcal{C}

it actually equals

K=\left\{\boldsymbol{a}\in\mathcal{A}\penalty\ |\penalty\ \boldsymbol{a}\star\boldsymbol{e}\in\mathcal{A}\star\mathcal{C}\right\}.

Next, the hope is that for any
\boldsymbol{a}\in K
, the product

\boldsymbol{a}\star\boldsymbol{e}
is
0
. This is in particular what should happen
when the minimum distance of
\mathcal{A}\star\mathcal{C}
exceeds
t
since

w_{H}(\boldsymbol{a}\star\boldsymbol{e})\leqslant w_{H}(\boldsymbol{e})\leqslant t
. In this situation, any

\boldsymbol{a}\in K
actually vanishes at any nonzero position of
\boldsymbol{e}
and
hence one can expect that the common zero positions of the elements of

K
permit to identify the nonzero positions of
\boldsymbol{e}
. Once the
errors are localised, decoding turns out to be solvable by the
resolution of a linear system.

In summary, correcting
t
errors for a code
\mathcal{C}
is possible as soon
as there exists an auxiliary code
\mathcal{A}
such that

(i)

\dim\mathcal{A}>t
;

(ii)

d_{\text{min}}(\mathcal{A}\star\mathcal{C})>t
;

(iii)

d_{\text{min}}(\mathcal{A})+d_{\text{min}}(\mathcal{C})>n
.

Sketch of proof.

First, note that
K
contains the so–called
shortening

of
\mathcal{A}
at the error positions,
i.e.
the subcode of

\mathcal{A}
of words vanishing where
\boldsymbol{e}
does not. Under
Condition (
i
), this shortening of
\mathcal{A}
is nonzero
and hence
K\neq 0
. Next, because of this previous discussion,
Condition (
ii
) asserts that
K
actually equals this

shortening
of
\mathcal{A}
. Finally, Condition
(
iii
) permits to make sure that, once the error
positions are known, the resolution of a linear system will provide

\boldsymbol{e}
as the unique solution. See
[
HP95
, Prop. 2.4]
for
further details.
∎

Remark 6
.

Actually, Condition (
ii
) is rather restrictive and
can be relaxed to

\dim\mathcal{A}-t+\dim\mathcal{A}\star\mathcal{\mathcal{C}}\leqslant n
which
permits to typically expect that

(K\star\boldsymbol{e})\cap\mathcal{A}\star\mathcal{C}=\{0\}
, which is
sufficient to obtain that
K
is the shortening of
\mathcal{A}
we look
for. Similarly, Condition (
iii
) may be
removed. Relaxing these conditions is possible at the cost that the
algorithm may fail for some rare error patterns
\boldsymbol{e}
.

Remark 7
.

Pellikaan’s original description of the so–called
Error
Correcting pairs
involves two auxiliary codes
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{B}

(see for instance
[
HP95

prove that the code

satisfies
the conditions of Error Correcting Pairs.

“can one correct
errors beyond half the minimum distance at the cost of possibly
returning a list of codewords instead of a single one”
. A positive
answer has been given by Sudan for Reed–Solomon codes

[
Sud97
]
and Guruswami and Sudan for AG codes

[
GS99
]
. The latter paper providing a polynomial time
algorithm correcting up to the so–called
Johnson bound
, a
radius under which any decoder is guaranteed to return a list whose
size is polynomial in the code length.

The reason why all the aforementioned algorithms succeed to decode AG
codes and not arbitrary ones, can somehow be understood in terms of
the peculiar behaviour of these codes with respect to the
\star

product. See for instance

[
Ros18
,
CP20
,
PRS21
]
.

5
Code–based cryptography and McEliece encryption
scheme

Code–based cryptography dates back to 1978 with the seminal paper of
McEliece
[
McE78
]
proposing a public key encryption
scheme whose security is related to the hardness of the decoding problem.
Informally, the principle is to take a code for which a decoding
algorithm correcting up to
t
errors is known and to publish one of
its bases. Encryption consists in encoding the plaintext into a
codeword and add some randomly chosen error pattern of weight
t

while decryption consists in decoding, which is a possible task for
the legitimate receiver.

In terms of security, one expects the published version of the
code to be computationally indistinguishable from a random code. Thus,
under this indistinguishability assumption an attacker cannot do
better than trying to solve the decoding problem for an apparently
random code. The latter problem is proved to be NP–complete

[
BMvT78
]
but more importantly, it is supposed to be hard

in average
,
i.e.
hard for randomly chosen inputs. Still,
up to now the best known algorithms for generic decoding have a time
complexity which is exponential in the weight of the error.

Among the various proposals to instantiate McEliece scheme,
Reed–Solomon and AG codes have been proposed

[
Nie86
,
JM96
]
. It turns out that the
algebraic structure of these codes and in particular their structure
with respect to the component wise product mentioned in
§
3
, which is the key of the algebraic decoding as
explained in §
4
, also represents a strong weakness
of these codes. Indeed, because of
Theorem
5
and the subsequent discussion, the
operation
\mathcal{C}\mapsto\mathcal{C}\star\mathcal{C}
precisely provides a polynomial
time distinguisher between AG codes and random ones making the latter
ones unusable for public key encryption. To turn this distinguisher
into a full key recovery attack, we refer the reader to

[
CMCP17
]
.

Therefore, if the raw use of AG codes is not a secure, it is still
possible to use “deteriorate” versions of them for which decoding is
still possible but with a much lower decoding radius
t
. For
instance, one can consider some random subcodes of AG codes with a
large enough codimension or take
subfield subcodes
, that is to
say, start from an AG code in
\mathbf{F}_{q^{m}}^{n}
and only keep vectors whose
entries are all in the subfield
\mathbf{F}_{q}
. In some sense, McEliece’s
original proposal was based on the latter idea with a genus 0
underlying curve.
We refer the reader to
[
CR21
, § 15.7]

for further details on the history of cryptanalysis of AG codes.

6
Secret sharing

In
[
Sha79
]
, A. Shamir proposes a secure scheme for a set of
players to share a secret so that a coalition of
k
of them can get
the secret while any coalition of less than
k
players cannot get
even a partial information on the secret. The scheme is based on
Lagrange interpolation and can actually be interpreted in terms of
codes. For
n-1
players and a given secret
s\in\mathbf{F}_{q}
, take an

[n,k,d]_{q}
code
\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathbf{F}_{q}^{n}
, choose an arbitrary vector

\boldsymbol{c}\in\mathcal{C}
whose last entry
c_{n}=s
and distribute the
n-1

other entries to the players. The reconstruction and security
properties of the scheme can actually be interpreted in terms of
the minimum distance of
\mathcal{C}
and its dual
\mathcal{C}^{\perp}
.

Lemma 8
.

A coalition of
r>n-d_{\text{min}}(\mathcal{C})
players can
recover the secret while any coalition of

r^{\prime}<d_{\text{min}}(\mathcal{C}^{\perp})-1

players cannot get any information on the secret.

Démonstration.

Given
I\subseteq\{1,\dots,n\}
, denote by
\mathcal{C}_{I}
the image
of
\mathcal{C}
under the canonical projection on the entries with
index in
I
:

\boldsymbol{c}\longmapsto\boldsymbol{c}_{I}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}{(c_{i})}_{i\in I}\penalty\ .

The first assertion is due to the fact that if

|I|>n-d_{\text{min}}(\mathcal{C})
, then this canonical projection

\mathcal{C}\rightarrow\mathcal{C}_{I}
is injective. Hence, the knowledge of

\boldsymbol{c}_{I}
is enough to recover the whole vector
\boldsymbol{c}
and in
particular the secret
s=c_{n}
.

On the other hand, if
|I|<d_{\text{min}}(\mathcal{C}^{\perp})-1
, then
one can prove that
\mathcal{C}_{I\cup\{n\}}=\mathbf{F}_{q}^{|I|+1}
and hence for
any
s^{\prime}\in\mathbf{F}_{q}
there exists
\boldsymbol{c}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}
such that

\boldsymbol{c}^{\prime}_{n}=s^{\prime}
and
\boldsymbol{c}^{\prime}_{I}=\boldsymbol{c}_{I}
. Thus, with the only knowledge of

\boldsymbol{c}_{I}
, the possible values of the secret are uniformly
distributed.
∎

The coding theoretic interpretation of Shamir’s scheme rests on a

k
–dimensional Reed–Solomon code. It permits the secret recovery by
any
k
–tuple of users while coalitions of
r<k
users cannot get
any information on the secret.

Another interesting feature of Shamir’s scheme is that it is
linear
: if one wants to perform linear combinations of secrets, the
users only have to perform separately the linear combination of their
own shares and the result of a legal coalition will be the expected
linear combination. Next, one could expect to be able to perform
further arithmetic operations on the secrets such as
multiplications. In such situations the code
\mathcal{C}\star\mathcal{C}
enters
the game again. This is at the core of the so–called
arithmetic
secret sharing schemes
which can serve as a basis for secure
multiparty computation
protocols
[
CDM00
]
. Here we refer the
reader to
[
CDN15
, Chap. 12]
and

[
CR21
, § 15.8.4]
for further details on the expected
properties a code should have to provide a good arithmetic secret
sharing scheme. Note that very similarly to other applications,
Reed–Solomon codes — yielding Shamir’s scheme — are somehow
optimal but suffer from the length restriction : the number of players
cannot exceed the size of the ground field
i.e.
the cardinality
of the set of secrets. Next, AG codes appear to be the best solution
to address this limitation.

In this context, an open question remains :
which codes may be
suitable for arithmetic secret sharing ?
At least a first question
could be
which codes
\mathcal{C}
satisfy the property that

\mathcal{C}\star\mathcal{C}
is “small” ?
. According to

[
CDM00
, Thm. 12.19]
, the coding theoretic requirements to
design a multiplicative secret sharing scheme consist in lower bounds
on the minimum distances of
\mathcal{C}^{\perp}
and
\mathcal{C}\star\mathcal{C}
. According
to Singleton bound (
), this entails a lower bound on
the dimension of
\mathcal{C}
and an upper bound on that of
\mathcal{C}\star\mathcal{C}
. Then, finding codes
\mathcal{C}
of fixed dimension
k
such that

\mathcal{C}\star\mathcal{C}
has the least possible dimension is a question of
interest and classifying such codes is a crucial question which
remains widely open up to now and that is discussed in the sequel.

6.1
Which codes have small squares ?

Let us focus on the following problem,
which codes

\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathbf{F}_{q}^{n}
of dimension
k
have a square of small
dimension ?
We first need to clarify what is meant by
“small”. For this we need the following definition.

Definition 9
.

A code
\mathcal{A}\subseteq\mathbf{F}_{q}^{n}
is said to be
degenerate
if
it is the direct sum of two subcodes with disjoint supports.
Equivalently, there exist subcodes

\mathcal{A}_{1},\mathcal{A}_{2}\subseteq\mathbf{F}_{q}^{n}
such that

\mathcal{A}=\mathcal{A}_{1}\oplus\mathcal{A}_{2}
and

\mathcal{A}_{1}\star\mathcal{A}_{2}=\{0\}
.

Now, according to
[
HLX02
, Thm. 2.4]
, as soon as
\mathcal{C}\star\mathcal{C}

is
non degenerate
, there is an analog of Cauchy–Davenport
formula in additive combinatorics which asserts that

\dim\mathcal{C}\star\mathcal{C}\geqslant 2\dim\mathcal{C}-1.

Next, for any positive integer
\gamma
, what can one say about codes
satisfying

\dim\mathcal{C}\star\mathcal{C}=2\dim\mathcal{C}-1+\gamma?

(6)

The quantity
\gamma
should be “small” and inspired by additive
combinatorics, one can first target Freiman–like results

[
TV06
, Thm. 5.11]
and study the case where

\gamma\leqslant\dim\mathcal{C}-3
.

First, one can note that AG codes yield a solution of the raised
problem. Indeed, Theorem
5
asserts that an AG
code
\mathcal{C}_{L}({\mathscr{X}},{\mathcal{P}},{G})
from a curve of genus
\gamma
with

\deg G>2\gamma
precisely satisfies this property. However, they are
not the only ones. It is also possible for some subcodes of AG codes
from curves of genus
<\gamma
to satisfy (
). For
instance, for
\gamma=1
, Property (
) is clearly
satisfied by AG codes from elliptic curves but also by some codes from
a curve of genus
0
as explained in the example below.

Example 10
.

Consider the projective line
\mathbf{P}^{1}
, denote by
P_{\infty}
the
place at infinity and by
\mathcal{P}
an ordered sequence of distinct degree

1
finite places. The code
\mathcal{C}_{L}({\mathbf{P}^{1}},{\mathcal{P}},{kP_{\infty}})

is nothing but a
(k+1)
–dimensional Reed–Solomon code and
let
\mathcal{C}
be its subcode spanned by the evaluations of the
functions
1,x^{2},x^{3},\dots,x^{k}
, that is to say, removing
x
.
The code
\mathcal{C}\star\mathcal{C}
will be spanned by the evaluations of

1,x^{2},x^{3},\dots,x^{2k}
and hence will have dimension

2k=2\dim\mathcal{C}
while being constructed from a genus
0
curve.

Question 1
.

Let
k
be a positive integer
and
0<\gamma\leqslant k-3
. Are there
k
–dimensional codes

\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathbf{F}_{q}^{n}
, such that
\mathcal{C}\star\mathcal{C}
is non degenerate,
satisfies

\dim\mathcal{C}\star\mathcal{C}=2\dim\mathcal{C}-1+\gamma

and
\mathcal{C}
is
not
contained in an AG code from a curve of genus

g\leqslant\gamma
?

For
\gamma=0
, it has been proved by Mirandola and Zémor

[
MZ15
]
that MDS codes (
i.e.
codes whose minimum distance
reaches the Singleton bound (
)) which satisfy

\dim\mathcal{C}\star\mathcal{C}=2\dim\mathcal{C}-1
are equivalent to Reed–Solomon
codes. Interestingly, this fact was already known from
an algebraic geometry point of view as Castelnuovo’s Lemma (see for instance

[
ACGH85
, § III.2, p. 120]
).

For larger
\gamma
’s, the problem is still open. The following
paragraphs discuss a possible geometric approach to study this
problem. We acknowledge that this discussion raises more questions
than answers.

6.2
The quadratic hull of a code

At least a first question could be :
do such codes always arise
from curves ?
In
[
Ran21
]
a notion called
quadratic
hull
of a code, associates an algebraic variety to an arbitrary
code in the following manner. Given a
k
–dimensional code

\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathbf{F}_{q}^{n}
, consider a generator matrix
G
for
\mathcal{C}
,
i.e.
a
k\times n
matrix whose rows span
\mathcal{C}
. Assuming that
the columns of
G
are pairwise independent, and regarding these
columns as points of
\mathbf{P}^{k-1}
, we associate to
\mathcal{C}
the
corresponding
n
–tuple
V(\mathcal{C})
of elements of
\mathbf{P}^{k-1}(\mathbf{F}_{q})
. By
this manner,
\mathcal{C}
can be regarded as an evaluation code : it is the
code of evaluations of
H^{0}(\mathbf{P}^{k-1},\mathcal{O}(1))
at the points of

V(\mathcal{C})
. Moreover,
\mathcal{C}\star\mathcal{C}
can be regarded in this way as
the code of evaluations of
H^{0}(\mathbf{P}^{k-1},\mathcal{O}(2))
at these
same points ! Let
I_{2}(V(\mathcal{C}))
be the space of quadratic forms
vanishing on
V(\mathcal{C})
, we have the following exact sequence

0\longrightarrow I_{2}(V(\mathcal{C}))\longrightarrow H^{0}(\mathbf{P}^{k-1},\mathcal{O}(2))\longrightarrow\mathcal{C}\star\mathcal{C}\longrightarrow 0.

(7)

The
quadratic hull

\mathscr{H}_{2}(\mathcal{C})
of
\mathcal{C}
is the variety defined by the
homogeneous ideal of
\mathbf{F}_{q}[X_{0},\dots,X_{k-1}]
spanned by

I_{2}(V(\mathcal{C}))
. Equivalently, it is the intersection of quadrics containing

V(\mathcal{C})
. Clearly,
\mathscr{H}_{2}(\mathcal{C})
contains
V(\mathcal{C})
but may contain further
elements.

\propname

\the\smf@thm
.

Let
\mathscr{X}
be a curve of genus
g
,
\mathcal{P}
be an ordered
n
–tuple of
rational points of
\mathscr{X}
and
G
be a divisor such that

2g+2\leqslant\deg G<\frac{n}{2}
. Then, the quadratic hull of

\mathcal{C}_{L}({\mathscr{X}},{\mathcal{P}},{G})
equals
\mathscr{X}
.

Démonstration.

The proof can be found in
[
MCMMP14
, Thm. 2]
, we adapt it here
to the current notation and terminology. The condition

\deg G\geqslant 2g+2
entails the very ampleness of
G
and, up to
projective equivalence the corresponding embedding

\varphi_{G}:\mathscr{X}\rightarrow\mathbf{P}(L(G))\simeq\mathbf{P}^{k-1}
sends
precisely
\mathcal{P}
onto
V(\mathcal{C})
. The curve
\varphi_{G}(\mathscr{X})

has embedding degree
\deg G
in
\mathbf{P}^{k-1}
and hence, any quadric
hypersurface containing
V(\mathcal{C})
contains the whole
\mathscr{X}
.
Indeed, if not, the quadric hypersurface would intersect

\varphi_{G}(\mathscr{X})
at
n>2\deg G
points (by assumption on

\deg G
) which would contradict Bézout’s Theorem. Therefore, the
quadratic hull of
\mathcal{C}
contains
\varphi_{G}(\mathscr{X})
. Next, using Saint
Donat’s result
[
SD72
]
, such an embedding of
\mathscr{X}
is
an intersection of quadrics, which concludes the proof.
∎

Now the question is,
what if
\mathcal{C}
is not supposed to arise from a
curve but
\mathcal{C}\star\mathcal{C}
is non degenerate and
\dim\mathcal{C}\star\mathcal{C}\leqslant 2\dim\mathcal{C}-1+\gamma
with

\gamma\leqslant\dim\mathcal{C}-3
?
Is it possible that this code is not
contained in an AG code from a curve of genus

? The
point is that if
\dim\mathcal{C}\star\mathcal{C}
is small, then

\dim I_{2}(V(\mathcal{C}))
is large and one could reasonably expect the quadratic
hull to have a small dimension. Actually, one can prove the following
statement.

Theorem 11
.

Let
\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathbf{F}_{q}^{n}
such that
\mathcal{C}\star\mathcal{C}
is non

\dim\mathcal{C}\star\mathcal{C}\leqslant 3\dim\mathcal{C}-4

\mathscr{H}_{2}(\mathcal{C})

is a reduced absolutely

\mathscr{H}_{2}(\mathcal{C})
is a curve.

Remark 12
.

The above statement is mentioned in
[

Under the hypotheses of Theorem

Using the hypothesis that

Question 2
.

Given a code
\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathbf{F}_{q}^{n}
such that
\mathcal{C}\star\mathcal{C}
is non

\dim\mathcal{C}\star\mathcal{C}\leqslant 3\dim\mathcal{C}-4
, under which

\mathcal{C}

Example 14
.

This example appears in
[
Ran21

.
Let
\mathscr{Y}\subseteq\mathbf{P}^{3}
be the union of a plane and a line not
contained in it. Let
\mathcal{P}
be the set of rational points of
\mathscr{Y}
and
consider the code
\mathcal{C}
obtained as the set of evaluations of linear

i.e.
the elements of
H^{0}(\mathbf{P}^{3},\mathcal{O}(1))

\mathcal{P}
. For

\mathscr{Y}
. Hence, the quadratic

. Moreover, the dimension of the space of

. On the other hand, for

while Weil’s bound asserts that curves
of genus
\leqslant 1
have at most

7
A concluding remark

The present note dealt with codes from curves while one could also
consider codes from higher dimensional varieties. The latter
topic, without being as investigated in the literature as codes from
curves remains of deep interest : we refer the reader to the survey

[
Lit08
]
.
However, our focus was on the behaviour of codes with respect to the
component wise product and codes from curves are very particular from
this point of view. It can be related to the fact that Hilbert
polynomials of curves are linear while they are quadratic for
surfaces. Using this point of view, it seems that one cannot expect to
decode codes from higher dimensional varieties as efficiently as codes
from curves. Similarly, higher dimensional varieties are probably much
less interesting for secret sharing applications.
On the other hand, codes from higher dimensional varieties may remain of deep
interest for other applications such as cloud storage and the design
of locally recoverable codes.
