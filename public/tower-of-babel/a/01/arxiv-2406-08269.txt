Analyzing constrained LLM through PDFA-learning Thanks: Presented at LearnAut 2024, July 7, 2024.
M. Carrasco F. Mayr S. Yovine J. Kidd M. Iturbide J. da Silva A. Garat

Abstract
We define a congruence that copes with null next-symbol probabilities that arise when the output of a language model is constrained by some means during text generation. We develop an algorithm for efficiently learning the quotient with respect to this congruence and evaluate it on case studies for analyzing statistical properties of LLM.

Introduction

Many works have studied neural language models, such as Recurrent Neural Networks (RNN) and Transformers, through the analysis of surrogate automata of different sorts obtained from the former in a variety of ways, with the purpose of verifying or explaining their behavior (e.g.
[
12
,
13
,
4
,
6
,
8
]
).
A few have proposed to somehow compose neural language models with automata or regular expressions in order to verifying properties on-the-fly while learning (
[
7
]
), assessing the existence of memorization, bias, or toxicity (
[
5
]
), and guiding text generation (
[
14
]
).

In this paper, we first study theoretical questions that arise when applying this last approach in the context of active learning of probabilistic deterministic finite automata (PDFA) (
[
11
]

2

3
, we adapt the learning algorithm of
[

to efficiently learn the quotient PDFA. In Sec.

[

,
14
]
when generating text with GPT2. Experimental results show the interest of our approach.

Sampling

\mathcal{L}
can be used to generate random strings
x\in\Sigma^{\ast}
with
x_{i}\sim\mathcal{L}(x_{<i})
, for
i\geq 1
, where
x_{i}
is the
i
-th symbol and
x_{<i}=x_{1}\ldots x_{i-1}
with
x_{<1}\triangleq\lambda
.
That is, by sampling the next symbol to concatenate from the distribution of the prefix until the termination symbol is selected. In general, this procedure may not terminate.
Indeed,
\mathcal{L}
uniquely defines a probability distribution over
\Sigma^{\ast}\cup\Sigma^{\omega}
, where
\Sigma^{\omega}
denotes the set of all infinite strings.
More formally, let
P:\Sigma^{\ast}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{+}
be:

P(\lambda)\triangleq 1
and

P(u\sigma)\triangleq P(u)\cdot\mathcal{L}(u)(\sigma)
.
We expect
P(w)
to represent the probability of
w
being a prefix. We also define
P_{\$}:\Sigma^{\ast}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{+}
by
P_{\$}(u)\triangleq P(u)\cdot\mathcal{L}(u)(\$)
. In this case, we expect
P_{\$}(w)
to represent the probability of occurrence of the finite string
w
. Proposition
F.1
guarantees the existence of a unique probability distribution
\boldsymbol{P}
over
\Sigma^{\ast}\cup\Sigma^{\omega}
whose prefix probabilities are given by
P
and whose restriction to
\Sigma^{\ast}
is given by
P_{\$}
: if
x
is a random string in
\Sigma^{\ast}\cup\Sigma^{\omega}
with distribution
\boldsymbol{P}
, then
\boldsymbol{P}\left\{w\in\mathsf{pref}(x)\right\}=P(w)
and
\boldsymbol{P}\left\{x=w\right\}=P_{\$}(w)
. Here
\mathsf{pref}(x)
denotes the set of all prefixes in
\Sigma^{\ast}
of the string
x
, including
x
itself.
In general,
P_{\$}
is not a probability distribution over
\Sigma^{\ast}
as it may not sum 1 (
[
11
]
).
In fact,
\sum_{u\in\Sigma^{\ast}}P_{\$}(u)=1
iff
\boldsymbol{P}\left\{|x|<\infty\right\}=1
.
Necessary and sufficient conditions for termination of the sampling process involve statements about the probabilities of the terminal symbol (
[
2
]
).

Not every occurrence of a zero probability is problematic.
For
\mathcal{A}
in Fig.
1
, the fact that
\pi_{\mathcal{A}}(q_{1})(b)=0
is harmless:

\sum_{u\in\Sigma^{\ast}}P_{\$}(u)=0.3\cdot 0.4\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}0.6^{n}+0.7\cdot 0.2\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}0.8^{n}=0.3+0.7=1
.
However, for
\mathcal{B}
,
\pi_{\mathcal{A}}(q_{2})(\$)=0
is troublesome:

\sum_{u\in a\Sigma^{\ast}}P_{\$}(u)+\sum_{u\in b\Sigma^{\ast}}P_{\$}(u)=0.3\cdot 0.4\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}0.6^{n}=0.3\neq 1
.

\mathcal{B}
can actually be obtained from
\mathcal{A}
by constraining the set of symbols to sample from to the top-
2
most likely ones:

\mathsf{top}_{2}(\pi_{\mathcal{A}}(q_{2}))=\{a,b\}
, and normalizing the probabilities.
It results in that no finite string starting with symbol

can be sampled in
\mathcal{B}
with distribution
P_{\$}
.
We deal with the general situation in this paper since the possibility of non-termination in the sampling process is harmless for our purposes.
Using
\mathsf{top}_{r}
or
\mathsf{top}_{p}
(most likely symbols with a cumulative probability cutoff of
p
) is usual practice when sampling from an LLM. So, it is relevant to formalize the effect of these constraints on
\mathcal{L}
.
A
sampling strategy

\textsf{samp}:\Delta(\Sigma_{\$})\rightarrow\Delta(\Sigma_{\$})
is s.t. for all
\rho\in\Delta(\Sigma_{\$})
,
\textsf{supp}(\textsf{samp}(\rho))\subseteq\textsf{supp}(\rho)
.

\textsf{samp}(\mathcal{L})
is the language model obtained by applying
samp
to
\mathcal{L}(u)

\forall u\in\Sigma^{\ast}
and normalizing the probabilities.
In Fig.

\mathcal{B}=\mathsf{samptop}_{2}(\mathcal{A})
, where
\mathsf{samptop}_{r}(\rho)(\sigma)=\rho(\sigma)
if
\sigma\in\mathsf{top}_{r}(\rho)

Learning algorithm

Based on the results of Sec.
2
, we develop the algorithm
Omit-Zero
, to learn
\equiv
-minimal PDFA. It is a variant of
\mathrm{QNT}
(
[
6
]
) that differs in specific steps indicated with boxes in Alg

.
For lack of space, we focus on these.

Omit-zero
maintains a tree
T
whose nodes are strings which are partitioned in two sets, namely,
\mathit{Acc}\subset\Sigma^{\ast}
and
\mathit{Dis}\subset\Sigma^{\ast}
of

distinguishing

\mathit{Acc}
is the set of
leafs
. Each

is labelled with the distribution
\mathcal{L}(u)
.

\mathit{Dis}
is the set of non-leaf nodes.
Both
\mathit{Acc}
and
\mathit{Dis}
contain
\lambda
, which is also the root and a leaf of
T
.
Arcs in
T
are labeled with classes in
\left[\Delta(\Sigma_{\$})\right]

Every outgoing arc from a non-leaf node is labeled with a different class.

\forall u\neq u^{\prime}\in\mathit{Acc}

, is such that
\mathcal{L}(uw)\neq\mathcal{L}(u^{\prime}w)

A difference with
\mathrm{QNT}
is that

satisfies the following properties:

\displaystyle\forall u\in\mathit{Acc}:\qquad\qquad

\displaystyle\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{L}}(u)=1

(7)

\displaystyle\forall w\in\zeta_{u}.\ w\neq\lambda\implies w_{1}\in\textsf{supp}(\mathcal{L}(u))

where

is the path of distinguishing strings from the root to the leaf

.
Notice that (

of undefined strings.

5
Conclusions

This work was motivated by the need of understanding LLM when their operation is controlled by external artifacts, such as grammars, to generate text following a specific format. An important question that arise in this context is how to deal with 0-probabilities that appear when restricting their output.
To start up with, we revised the congruence (
1
) in order to make constructing the quotient less dependent on
P
by expressing it in terms of the output of the language model. The first consequence of this operational view is to allow a generalization of the congruence capable of dealing with equivalences on distributions. Besides, it led to developing a variant of the
\mathrm{QNT}
active-learning algorithm to efficiently learn PDFA by avoiding to check for 0-probability transitions as much as possible. This is essential to make it computationally feasible by reducing the number of queries to the LLM.
The experimental results
2
2

2

https://github.com/neuralchecker/analyzing_constrained_LLM_through_PDFA_learning
support the viability of our approach for analyzing and validating statistical properties of LLM, such as bias in text generation. Besides, they provided evidence that distributions resulting from generation of a guided LLM could be well approximated by a learnt PDFA. This opens the door to make these analyses less dependent on sampling by studying properties of the PDFA.
