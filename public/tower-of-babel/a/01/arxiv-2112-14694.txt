On proper extensions of the conformal group of sphere diffeomorphisms Thanks: U. Lakatos was partially supported by CNPq, Grant No. 159129/2015-0 Thanks: F.A. Tal was supported by Fapesp and CNPq – Brazil
Ulisses Lakatos de Mello, Fábio Armando Tal

Abstract
. In this paper, we prove that any group of diffeomorphisms acting on the 2-sphere and properly extending the conformal group of Möbius transformations must be at least 4-transitive or, more precisely, arc 4-transitive. In addition, we show that any such group must always contain an element of positive topological entropy. We also provide an elementary characterization, in terms of transitivity, of the Möbius transformations within the full group of sphere diffeomorphisms.

1.
Introduction

Let
M
be a closed and oriented topological manifold, and consider the set of all its
orientation preserving homeomorphisms
, denoted simply by
\homeos_{\,}\!(M)
. The usual
uniform convergence metric
turns this set – endowed with the composition operation
\circ
– into a topological group, the subgroups of which one can try to understand and classify.

In his early 2000s’ essay
[
3
]
, É. Ghys proposed such a classification scheme for closed and transitive groups acting on the unit circle
\mathds{S}^{1}
. Here, closed refers to the uniform topology mentioned above, while
transitive
means that any given point
p
can be mapped onto another given point
q
via some transformation in the group. The corresponding result, which we quote later on this paper, was proven in 2006 by J. Giblin and V. Markovic, see
[
4
]
.

A relevant part of understanding the closed subgroups of
\homeos_{\,}\!(M)
is to deal not only with their inclusions, but also with questions of
maximality
. In other words, determining whether or not between a given subgroup and the full group of homeomorphisms one may find proper intermediate subgroups, up to their closures. For example, F. Le Roux proved in
[
8
]
that, in triangulable manifolds of dimension two or higher, the group of area preserving homeomorphisms is always maximal.

Specializing in the unit sphere
\mathds{S}^{2}
, F. Kwakkel and the second author derived in
[
7
]
a number of results concerning important subgroups of
\homeos_{\,}\!(\mathds{S}^{2})
, one of which is
\Mob(\mathds{S}^{2})
, the
Möbius group
of conformal 2-sphere diffeomorphisms. Among others, they left the question of whether the Möbius group is maximal within the full group of homeomorphisms, meaning that there would be no proper intermediate group of diffeomorphisms between
\Mob(\mathds{S}^{2})
and
\homeos_{\,}\!(\mathds{S}^{2})
, up to uniform closure.
1
1

1

[
7
]
is an unpublished work. Due to a difference of opinion between its authors, it remains as an unsubmitted preprint. Note that we reference here the v2 in arXiv, not the v3. In any case, this note, being mostly self-contained, makes no direct use of any result in that work.

This question is known to have a positive answer in the case of the circle, and the proof there is related to higher orders of transitivity. In this paper we provide insight, from the transitivity viewpoint, into extensions of the Möbius group acting on the sphere, as stated below.

Theorem A
.

Let
G\subset\difeo^{1}_{\,}\!(\mathds{S}^{2})
be a group that is a proper extension of
\Mob(\mathds{S}^{2})
. Then, its identity component
G_{0}
is arc
4
-transitive. In particular,
G
is at least
4
-transitive.

Above, the stronger concept of
arc-transitivity
is introduced. Although more precisely defined in the text to follow, it essentially means that not only ordered lists
\left(p_{1},\ldots,p_{4}\right)
and
\left(q_{1},\ldots,q_{4}\right)
of four points can be mapped one onto another via a single transformation in the group
G
, each
q_{i}
is actually the endpoint of
p_{i}
’s trajectory under a isotopy in
G
starting at the identity map.

A straightforward application of Theorem A, using both the techniques developed in its proof and some basics of Nielsen-Thurston classification theory, is the following result. It could also be derived with Theorem A and an abstract result from
[
7
]
).

Theorem B
.

Let
G\subset\difeo^{1}_{\,}\!(\mathds{S}^{2})
be a proper extension of
\Mob(\mathds{S}^{2})
. Then,
G
contains an element
f
fixing at least 4 points, and such that the restriction of
f
to the complement of these points is isotopic to a pseudo-Anosov map relative to these 4 points. In particular,
f
has strictly positive topological entropy.

Finally, we also include an interesting result – albeit not new – steaming from the same sort of techniques developed in the proofs of Theorems A and B. It is stated in terms of the general definition given below, which plays a key role in this paper.

Definition 1.1
.

Given
k\in\mathds{N}
, the action of a subgroup
G\subset\homeos_{\,}\!(M)
is said to be
k
-transitive
if, for every pair of
k
-tuples
\left(p_{1},\ldots,p_{k}\right)
and
\left(q_{1},\ldots,q_{k}\right)
, each of them composed by mutually distinct points, there exists some transformation
g\in G
such that
q_{i}=g(p_{i})
for each
i\in\left\{1,\ldots,k\right\}
. When it holds, in addition, that for such a given
k
-tuple the only transformation fixing its every point is the identity map, the group is said to be
sharply
k
-transitive
.

It is a widely known fact of complex analysis that
\Mob(\mathds{S}^{2})
is sharply 3-transitive. As it turns out, this is a
defining property
of such group: in their 2014 work, Kwakkel and the second author show that any sharply
3
-transitive subgroup of
\difeo^{1}_{\,}\!(\mathds{S}^{2})
extending the rotations group
\Rot(\mathds{S}^{2})
must be
\Mob(\mathds{S}^{2})
. For completeness, and given that the cited work has not been published, we also present herein a new simple – and in our opinion rather enjoyable – direct proof of this fact, formally stated below.

Theorem C
.

Let
G\subset\difeo^{1}_{\,}\!(\mathds{S}^{2})
be a homogeneous group of diffeomorphisms. That is,
G
is an extension of the rotations group
\Rot(\mathds{S}^{2})
. Then,
G
sharply 3-transitive implies
G=\Mob(\mathds{S}^{2})
.

1.1.
Preliminaries

Let us start by agreeing upon terminology. As aforementioned,
\homeos_{\,}\!(M)
denotes the set of orientation preserving homeomorphisms of a closed and oriented topological manifold
M
, on which the uniform metric

d_{\infty}\left(f,g\right)=\max_{p\in M}\left\{d\left(f(p),g(p)\right)\right\}

is well defined. Above,
d
is some distance function generating the manifold’s topology. If
d
comes from a Riemannian structure on
M
, we may also consider the subset
\difeo^{1}_{\,}\!(M)\subset\homeos_{\,}\!(M)
of orientation preserving diffeomorphisms of class
{C}^{1}
and its finer
{C}^{1}
topology
– or
compact-open
– which takes into account the local expressions of differentials as well.

Consider the unit circle,
M=\mathds{S}^{1}
. It can be thought of as the set of complex points at unit distance from the origin or as the compactification
\mathds{R}\cup\left\{\infty\right\}
. In the latter case, identification is provided by stereographic projection from the point
(0,1)
. Each such description is linked to a canonical group acting on
\mathds{S}^{1}
.

In the first case, we have the
rotations group
,
\Rot(\mathds{S}^{1})
. This group is naturally identified with the circle itself, the rotation of angle
\alpha
being given as
z\mapsto\mathrm{e}^{2\pi\mathrm{i}\,\alpha}z
. It is thus a compact Lie group. In the second case, we have the
\emph{Möbiusgroup}
,
\Mob(\mathds{S}^{1})
, obtained by transposing to the circle the action of
\projspecgroup\!\left(2;\mathds{R}\right)
on the extended real line by
linear fractional transformations
.

The rotations group can be realized as a proper subgroup of the Möbius group. Interestingly enough, these two groups yield a full description of the closed and transitive subgroups of
\homeos_{\,}\!(\mathds{S}^{1})
, which is the content of the theorem by Giblin and Markovic.

Theorem

(Giblin and Markovic,
[
4
]
)
.

Let
G
be a closed and transitive subgroup of
\homeos_{\,}\!(\mathds{S}^{2})
containing a nontrivial path connected component. Then, it is conjugate to one, and only one, of the following:

(1)

\Rot(\mathds{S}^{1})
,

(2)

\Mob_{k}(\mathds{S}^{1})
,

(3)

or
\homeos_{\,k}\!(\mathds{S}^{1})
.

Above, the subscript
k\in\mathds{N}
indicates cyclic covering of order
k
. Furthermore,
\Mob(\mathds{S}^{1})
is maximal among the closed and transitive groups of
\homeos_{\,}\!(\mathds{S}^{1})
. This means that there is no such a subgroup properly containing
\Mob(\mathds{S}^{1})
and properly contained in
\homeos_{\,}\!(\mathds{S}^{1})
.

Consider now the unit 2-sphere,
M=\mathds{S}^{2}
. It can be thought of either as the points of Euclidean 3-space
\mathds{R}^{3}
at unit distance from the origin or as the compactification
\mathds{C}\cup\left\{\mathbf{\infty}\right\}
. In the latter case, identification is provided by stereographic projection from the North Pole
(0,0,1)
. Points on the plane and their stereographic images will be denoted by the same letters and confounded without notice. Accordingly, any sphere mapping that fixes
\mathbf{\infty}
defines, by stereographic conjugation, a mapping of the plane with the same degree of regularity, also denoted by the same letter.

The group of
sphere rotations
,
\Rot(\mathds{S}^{2})
, consists of Euclidean isometries preserving
\mathds{S}^{2}
. It is realized by the orthogonal matrices
\specorthgroup\!\left(3;\mathds{R}\right)
, and is thus also a compact Lie group. Each such transformation amounts to prescribing an axis and a rotation angle around that axis. From these two facts, it can be seen that
\Rot(\mathds{S}^{2})
is closed and transitive.

Rotations are
minimal
among transitive groups in the following sense: any compact subgroup
G\subset\textrm{Homeo}(\mathds{S}^{2})
is conjugate to a closed subgroup of
\Rot(\mathds{S}^{2})
– a result known to B. Kerékjártó since the 40s, and for which a proof according to contemporary standards is given by B. Kolev in
[
6
]
. If
G
is also transitive, then it is conjugate to
\Rot(\mathds{S}^{2})
. The problem of classifying the groups contained between
\Rot(\mathds{S}^{2})
and
\homeos_{\,}\!(\mathds{S}^{2})
is thus called the
kernel subgroup problem
, and such groups are hereafter called
homogeneous
, following the terminology established in
[
7
]
.

The
Möbius group

\Mob(\mathds{S}^{2})
is an example of a homogeneous group. It is defined similarly to its circle counterpart, by the action of
\projspecgroup\!\left(2;\mathds{C}\right)
on the extended plane via linear fractional transformations. More precisely, one associates to (the class of) the matrix
A
a mapping
M_{A}
as follows:

\text{if }A=\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{pmatrix}\in\projspecgroup\!\left(2;\mathds{C}\right)\text{ , then }M_{A}(z)=\frac{a\,z+b}{c\,z+d}\text{ for every }z\in\mathds{C}\cup\left\{\infty\right\}\text{.}

This procedure not only induces a group of orientation preserving homeomorphisms of the sphere, it characterizes the
conformal diffeomorphisms
of
\mathds{S}^{2}
endowed with its canonical smooth structure. The Möbius group is thoroughly understood: each transformation has either one or two fixed points, and is conjugated to one out of four prototypical mappings – called parabolic, elliptical, hyperbolic or loxodromic – depending on the relation between the coefficients
a
and
d
. In particular, rotations of the sphere are induced by the subgroup
\projspecunigroup\!\left(2;\mathds{C}\right)
.

These are classical results, some of them already known to Gauss, that can be found, for instance, in Chapter 3 of
[
10
]
. Kwakkel and the second author left open the question of whether the inclusion of
\Mob(\mathds{S}^{2})
in
\homeos_{\,}\!(\mathds{S}^{2})
is maximal. Our contribution to this problem was stated in
Theorem A
above. It should be stressed that, in the circle setting, 4-transitivity is sufficient to ensure
k
-transitivity for any
k\geq 4
(Theorem 6.4 in
[
4
]
).

The constructions in this paper are mostly based upon
isotopies
, here understood as jointly continuous maps
f\colon I\times M\to M
, where
I
is a (possibly unbouded) real interval, such that the functions
f_{t}=f(t,\!\cdot)
are homeomorphisms of
M
. We can either prescribe
f
or a family
\left(f_{t}\right)_{t\in I}
for which
t\mapsto f_{t}
is continuous with respect to
d_{\infty}
. When the interval in question is the
standard unit interval
, we denote it by
\mathds{I}=\left[0,\!1\right]
.

It will often be the case that
f_{t}\in\difeo^{1}_{\,}\!(M)
for every
t\in I
, but
t\mapsto f_{t}
can only be assured to be continuous with respect to
d_{\infty}
. To avoid confusions, isotopies for which this association is actually continuous with respect to the
{C}^{1}
-topology will be explicitly referred to as
{C}^{1}
-isotopies
.

Definition 1.2
.

Let
G\subset\homeos_{\,}\!(M)
. An isotopy
\left(f_{t}\right)_{t\in I}
such that
0\in I
,
f_{0}=\mathrm{id}
and
f_{t}\in G
for every
t\in I
will be referred to as an
\mathcal{I}G
-isotopy
. By letting
\sup I=+\infty
and
\inf I=-\infty
whenever suitable, we then define:

(1)

The
trajectory
of a point
x\in M
under
f
as
\gamma_{f}\left(x\right)=\left\{f_{t}(x)\,:\,t\in I\right\}
. Although this is
a priori
just a set, it will often be thought of as a curve oriented according to its natural direction of travel.

(2)

Its
\omega
-
limit
as the following set of accumulation points:

\omega_{f}\left(x\right)=\left\{y\in M\,:\,\text{ there exists some sequence }t_{n}\nearrow\sup I\text{ such that }f_{t_{n}}(x)\to y\right\}\text{.}

A similar notion of
\alpha
-
limit
is defined for sequences such that
t_{n}\searrow\inf I
.

We can now introduce the concept of arc transitivity, mentioned in
Theorem A
.

Definition 1.3
.

Given
k\in\mathds{N}
, a subgroup
G\subset\homeos_{\,}\!(M)
is said to be
arc
k
-transitive
if, for every pair of
k
-tuples as in
Definition

1.1
, there exists an
\mathcal{I}G
-isotopy
\left(g_{t}\right)_{t\in\mathds{I}}
such that
g_{1}(p_{i})=q_{i}
for each
i\in\left\{1,\ldots,k\right\}
.

The suggestive terminologies above are useful in the developments that follow. Although analogies with flows are limited, the following establishes some parallels.

Definition 1.4
.

For a fixed a subgroup
G\subset\homeos_{\,}\!(M)
and given
z,w\in M
, say that

z\sim_{G}w\iff\text{ there exists an $\mathcal{I}G$-isotopy $\left(f_{t}\right)_{t\in\mathds{I}}$ in $G$ such that $f_{1}(z)=w$.}

This is an equivalence relation, under which the class of a point
z\in M
is denoted by
\mathcal{A}_{z}
, and referred to as the
points accessible from
z
(in
G
).

Lemma 1.5
.

Let
\left(f_{t}\right)_{t\in I}
and
\left(h_{t}\right)_{t\in J}
be
\mathcal{I}G
-isotopies, where
I
and
J
are intervals of any kind. Then, for any two
z,w\in M
such that
\gamma_{f}\left(z\right)\cap\gamma_{h}\left(w\right)\neq\emptyset
we have that
\mathcal{A}_{w}=\mathcal{A}_{z}
.

Proof.

Say that
f_{a}(z)=h_{b}(w)
, for some
a\in I
,
b\in J
. Given
y\in\mathcal{A}_{w}
, there exists an
\mathcal{I}G
-isotopy
\left(g_{t}\right)_{t\in\mathds{I}}
such that
g_{1}(w)=y
. Then,

k_{t}=\begin{cases}f_{3at}&\text{if }0\leq t\leq\dfrac{1}{3}\text{, }\\[7.5pt]
h_{\left(3t-2\right)b}\circ h_{b}^{-1}\circ f_{a}&\text{if }\dfrac{1}{3}\leq t\leq\dfrac{2}{3}\text{, }\\[7.5pt]
g_{3t-2}\circ h_{b}^{-1}\circ f_{a}&\text{if }\dfrac{2}{3}\leq t\leq 1\text{, }\end{cases}

is an
\mathcal{I}G
-isotopy satisfying
k_{1}(z)=y
. This shows that
\mathcal{A}_{w}\subset\mathcal{A}_{z}
, and the converse inclusion follows by symmetry.
∎

We finish this introduction by fixing some reference points that play a key role in the arguments that follow:

•

\mathbf{\infty}
is the North Pole,

•

\mathbf{0}
is the South Pole, and corresponds to the plane’s origin,

•

\mathbf{1}
is the point
(1,0,0)
, which corresponds to its counterpart on the real axis.

The meridian through
\mathbf{0}
,
\mathbf{1}
and
\mathbf{\infty}
– which is the stereographic image of the
x
-axis (or real axis) – is denoted by
\Gamma
. This meridian defines on its left a
western hemisphere

\mathcal{H}^{+}
, corresponding to the upper half-plane, and on its right an
eastern hemisphere

\mathcal{H}^{-}
, corresponding to the lower half-plane. For any group
G
we also adopt the following notations for stabilizers:

G_{1}=\Stab_{G}\left(\mathbf{\infty}\right)\;\text{, }\;G_{2}=\Stab_{G}\left(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{\infty}\right)\;\text{ and }\;G_{3}=\Stab_{G}\left(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{1},\mathbf{\infty}\right)\text{.}

1.2.
Paper outline

Given a group
G
extending
\Mob(\mathds{S}^{2})
, we consider the subgroups
G_{k}
,
1\leq k\leq 3
, as above. Our objective is to conclude that
G_{3}
is (one) transitive.

We begin by establishing an
Extension Lemma
at the end of
Section
2
, which states that
G_{2}
must contain an isotopy between the identity and a map having a hyperbolic saddle point at the (fixed) South Pole. This is achieved by starting with a nonconformal map and continuously parametrizing rotations and homotheties, both of which are Möbius transformations, in such a way as to create eigendirections and modulate the corresponding eigenvalues.

From this starting point, we fix the privileged reference meridian
\Gamma
and promote two parallel processes. In
Section
3
, we prove that points outside of it admit full time isotopies in
G_{3}
accumulating at the poles. In
Section
4
, we prove that there is a finite time isotopy in
G_{3}
for which some point on this meridian starts at one side of it and ends at the other side.

In
Section
5
we show our main theorems.
Theorem A
is derived upon combining isotopies of the types described and concluding that all but three points of the sphere are actually arc connected in the sense of
Definition
1.3
, which yields arc transitivity of
G_{3}
.
Theorem B
is also derived by convenient combinations of segments of such isotopies, but to produce a “topological figure 8”, a device that implies positive entropy due to the Nielsen-Thurston classification theory.

Lastly, in
Section
6
we derive
Theorem C
. First, a purely topological argument shows that, if
G
is a sharply
3
-transitive and homogeneous group of homeomorphisms, then
G_{2}
must permute parallels. This fact, when combined with differentiability, yields conformality – first at the poles, and then at every point.

2.
Extensions of Möbius

Definition 2.1
.

For a given
0\leq\theta<2\pi
,
R_{\theta}
denotes the counterclockwise rotation of angle
\theta

•

around the
z
-axis, if it is thought of as a mapping of the 2-sphere,

•

around the origin, if it is thought of as a planar mapping. In this case, it may be written as
R_{\theta}(z)=\mathrm{e}^{2\pi\mathrm{i}\,\theta}\,z
.

Also, for a given
\rho>0
, we let
H_{\rho}\in\Mob_{2}(\mathds{S}^{2})
denote the transformation induced by the plane homothety
z\mapsto\rho z
. Since the tangent space
T_{\mathbf{0}}\mathds{S}^{2}
can be identified with the horizontal subspace
\mathds{R}^{2}\times\left\{0\right\}\simeq\mathds{R}^{2}
, generated by the canonical basis
\{{}^{\partial}{\mskip-10.0mu\;/\,\mskip-8.0mu}_{\partial x},{}^{\partial}{\mskip-10.0mu\;/\,\mskip-8.0mu}_{\partial y}\}
, we may write, in a slight abuse of notation:

\mathrm{D}R_{\theta}\!\left(\mathbf{0}\right)=R_{\theta}\quad\text{ and }\quad\mathrm{D}H_{\rho}\!\left(\mathbf{0}\right)=\rho\,\mathrm{id}\,\text{.}

We remark that, if
u,v
are nonzero vectors, the
angle

0\leq\aangle\!\left(z,w\right)\leq\pi
between them is defined in the usual way, as
\arccos\left[\left\langle u,v\right\rangle/\left(\left|u\right|\left|v\right|\right)\right]
.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
G\subset\difeo^{1}_{\,}\!(\mathds{S}^{2})
be a proper extension of
\Mob(\mathds{S}^{2})
. Then, there exists
\hat{g}\in G_{2}
for which
\mathbf{0}
is a hyperbolic saddle point. More precisely,
\mathrm{D}\hat{g}\!\left(\mathbf{0}\right)=\diaag\!\left[\lambda,\lambda^{-1}\right]
with respect to the canonical basis, where
0<\lambda<1
.

Proof.

Since
G
is a proper extension of the conformal group, it contains at least one mapping
\hat{h}
which is nonconformal at some point of the sphere. By precomposing and postcomposing with suitable Möbius transformations, it can be assumed that such map lies in
G_{2}
(that is, it fixes the poles) and that
A=\mathrm{D}\hat{h}\!\left(\mathbf{0}\right)
is nonconformal.

This means that there exists a pair of unit vectors
u,v
such that
\aangle\!\left(Au,Av\right)\neq\aangle\!\left(u,v\right)
. Let
R_{(1)}
be a planar rotation such that
R_{(1)}\left(Au\right)
is a positive multiple of
u
. Then,
A_{1}=R_{(1)}A
is a linear mapping having at least one eigenvalue
\lambda_{1}>0
, for which
u
is an unit eigenvector. It cannot be the case that
\lambda_{1}
is of maximal geometric multiplicity, for that would imply
A
conformal. This leaves two possibilities.

Case 1
.

A_{1}
is a defective matrix.

Then, we may fix an orthogonal chain of generalized eigenvectors
\left\{u,w\right\}
. That is,
A_{1}w=u+\lambda_{1}w
and
\left\langle u,w\right\rangle=0
. For each
\phi\in\left[0,\!\pi/2\right]
we define

x_{\phi}=\cos\phi\;u+\sin\phi\;w/\left|w\right|\;\text{ and }\;y_{\phi}=-\sin\phi\;u+\cos\phi\;w/\left|w\right|\text{, }

obtaining an orthonormal frame
\left\{x_{\phi},y_{\phi}\right\}
of
T_{\mathbf{0}}\mathds{S}^{2}
. Consider now the continuous function

\xi(\phi)=\left\langle A_{1}x_{\phi},A_{1}y_{\phi}\right\rangle/\left|A_{1}x_{\phi}\right|\left|A_{1}y_{\phi}\right|\text{.}

It satisfies
\xi(0)\phantom{\cdot}\xi(\pi/2)<0
. We thus have
\xi({\phi}_{0})=0
for some
0<{\phi}_{0}<\pi/2
. This means that, if
\hat{x}=x_{{\phi}_{0}}
and
\hat{y}=x_{{\phi}_{0}}
, then the orthonormal frame
\left\{A_{1}\hat{x}/|A_{1}\hat{x}|,A_{1}\hat{y}/\left|A_{1}\hat{y}\right|\right\}
can be applied onto
\left\{\hat{x},\hat{y}\right\}
by a certain planar rotation
R_{(2)}
, because
A_{1}
preserves orientation. Thus,
R_{(2)}\left(A_{1}\hat{x}\right)=\nu_{1}\,\hat{x}
and
R_{(2)}\left(A_{1}\hat{x}\right)=\nu_{2}\,\hat{y}
, where
\nu_{1},\nu_{2}>0
.

Once again, we must have
\nu_{1}\neq\nu_{2}
, or
A
would be conformal. Let us assume, without loss of generality, that
0<\nu_{1}<\nu_{2}
. By defining
A_{2}=R_{(2)}A_{1}
,
\rho=1/\sqrt{\nu_{1}\nu_{2}}
and
\lambda=\sqrt{\nu_{1}/\nu_{2}}<1
, we have
\left(\rho\,A_{2}\right)\hat{x}=\lambda\hat{x}
and
\left(\rho\,A_{2}\right)\hat{y}=\lambda^{-1}\,\hat{y}
.
Lastly, let
R
be a planar rotation such that
R^{-1}\,\hat{x}=\partial/\partial x
. Then,
R^{-1}\,\hat{y}=\varepsilon\;\partial/\partial y
, where
\varepsilon=1
if
\left\{\hat{x},\hat{y}\right\}
is a positive basis, or
\varepsilon=-1
otherwise. Defining
\hat{A}=\rho\,R^{-1}A_{2}R
:

\displaystyle\hat{A}\left({}^{\partial}{\mskip-10.0mu\;/\,\mskip-8.0mu}_{\partial x}\right)=\lambda\;{}^{\partial}{\mskip-10.0mu\;/\,\mskip-8.0mu}_{\partial x}\quad\text{ and }\quad\hat{A}\left({}^{\partial}{\mskip-10.0mu\;/\,\mskip-8.0mu}_{\partial y}\right)=\varepsilon^{2}\,\lambda^{-1}\;{}^{\partial}{\mskip-10.0mu\;/\,\mskip-8.0mu}_{\partial y}=\lambda^{-1}\;{}^{\partial}{\mskip-10.0mu\;/\,\mskip-8.0mu}_{\partial y}\,\text{.}

Therefore,
\hat{A}
has the form described in the Lemma’s statement with respect to the canonical basis. But, if
\hat{g}=R^{-1}\circ H_{\rho}\circ R_{(2)}\circ R_{(1)}\circ\hat{h}\circ R\in G_{2}
, then
\mathrm{D}\hat{g}\!\left(\mathbf{0}\right)=\hat{A}

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
g

\mathrm{D}g\!\left(\mathbf{0}\right)=\diaag\!\left[\lambda,\lambda^{-1}\right]

0<\left|z\right|<\delta\;\text{ and }\;z\notin\mathcal{C}_{\alpha}\;\text{ imply }\;R_{\omega}\left(g(z)\right)\notin\mathcal{C}_{\alpha}\,\text{, }\text{whenever }\,\left|\omega\right|<\tau\text{.}

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
\left(g_{t}\right)_{t\in\mathds{I}}

{C}^{1}

Proof.

For a fixed

]

.

Let

Let

4.
A Crossing Lemma

Given
r>0
, we let
\gamma_{r}:\mathds{I}\to\mathds{R}
be the path
\gamma_{r}\left(\theta\right)=r\mathrm{e}^{2\pi\mathrm{i}\,\theta}
and
S_{r}
be its image. Then, if
g\in\difeo^{1}_{\,}\!(\mathds{R}^{2})
is a planar diffeomorphism fixing the origin, we can check by a calculation that:

(11)

\frac{g\circ\gamma_{r}}{r}\xrightarrow{{C}^{1}\left(\mathds{I}\right)}\mathrm{D}g\!\left(\mathbf{0}\right)\circ\gamma_{1}\quad\text{ as }\quad r\to 0^{+}\text{.}

Assume further that
\mathrm{D}g\!\left(\mathbf{0}\right)
is a hyperbolic saddle matrix. Then, it maps
S_{1}
onto an ellipse having semiminor axis of length strictly smaller than one and semimajor axis of length strictly greater than one. Thus,
S_{1}\cap\mathrm{D}g\!\left(\mathbf{0}\right)\left(S_{1}\right)
consists of four nontangential intersections. It then follows from (
11
), along with the stability of nontangential intersections, that for a sufficiently small
r_{0}>0
(indeed, for infinitely many),
S_{r_{0}}\cap g\left(S_{r_{0}}\right)
contains exactly four points.

This remark allows us to now analyze the behaviour of
\Gamma
under the action of
G
. Before doing so, we agree that given three distinct points
a,b,c
, we denote by
\hat{M}\!\left[a,b,c\right]
the unique Möbius transformation mapping
a
to
\mathbf{0}
,
b
to
\mathbf{1}
and
c
to
\mathbf{\infty}
. If the points are all finite and nonzero, this transformation can be explicitly written as a certain cross ratio, from which it is seen that the association
\left(a,b,c\right)\mapsto\hat{M}\!\left[a,b,c\right]
is continuous.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
G\subset\difeo^{1}_{\,}\!(\mathds{S}^{2})
be a proper extension of
\Mob(\mathds{S}^{2})
. Then, there exists an
\mathcal{I}G_{3}
-isotopy
\left(k_{t}\right)_{t\in\mathds{I}}
such that
\left|\,k_{1}\left(\Gamma\right)\cap\Gamma\,\right|=4
.

Proof.

By the
Extension Lemma
, it is known that there exists an
\mathcal{I}G_{2}
-isotopy
\left(g_{t}\right)_{t\in\mathds{I}}
such that
g\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=g_{1}
has a hyperbolic saddle fixed point at the origin. So, we may fix any sufficiently small

such that
\left|\,S_{r_{0}}\cap g\left(S_{r_{0}}\right)\,\right|=4
.

Let

be four consecutive points in the usual anticlockwise cyclic order, and such that
S_{r_{0}}\cap g\left(S_{r_{0}}\right)=\left\{g(a),g(b),g(c),g(d)\right\}
. Then, we may consider the unique
M_{0}\in\Mob(\mathds{S}^{2})
mapping the ordered triple
\left(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{1},\mathbf{\infty}\right)
onto
\left(a,b,c\right)
. Notice that
M_{0}=\hat{M}\!\left[a,b,c\right]^{-1}
and that
M_{0}\left(\Gamma\right)=S_{r_{0}}
. By defining

we obtain an
\mathcal{I}G_{3}
-isotopy
\left(k_{t}\right)_{t\in\mathds{I}}
. Since
M_{0}
is a bijection of the sphere:

\displaystyle\left|\,k_{1}\left(\Gamma\right)\cap\Gamma\,\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\,\left(M_{0}\circ\hat{M}\!\left[g(a),g(b),g(c)\right]\right)\circ g\left(M_{0}\left(\Gamma\right)\right)\cap M_{0}\left(\Gamma\right)\,\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\,g\left(S_{r_{0}}\right)\cap S_{r_{0}}\,\right|=4\text{.}\qed

Finite points
a,b
on the meridian
\Gamma
are identified with their real counterparts on the
x
-axis. This induces a natural ordering, for which one may speak of the oriented interval with endpoints
a,b
. Whenever
a\leq b
with respect to this ordering,

•

\left[a,\!b\right]
denotes the arc of the meridian
\Gamma
with endpoints

and not containing
\mathbf{\infty}
, which corresponds to the compact interval of the

-axis with the associated endpoints;

•

\left[b,\!a\right]
denotes the arc of the meridian
\Gamma
with endpoints

and containing
\mathbf{\infty}
, which projects onto
\left(\left.-\infty,\!a\right]\right.\cup\left[\left.b,\!+\infty\right)\right.
.

If

, then the corresponding arcs are defined via stereographic projection as
\left[\mathbf{\infty},\!a\right]=\left(\left.-\infty,\!a\right]\right.\cup\left\{\mathbf{\infty}\right\}
and
\left[a,\!\mathbf{\infty}\right]=\left[\left.a,\!+\infty\right)\right.\cup\left\{\mathbf{\infty}\right\}
. Lastly, open and half-open arcs of

are defined accordingly by deletion of the suitable endpoints from the corresponding closed arcs.

.

Lemma 4.3

(The Crossing Lemma)
.

\left(\mathbf{0},\!\mathbf{1}\right)

\left(J_{t}\right)_{t\in\left[-1,1\right]}

\left(\mathbf{0},\!\mathbf{1}\right)

\,J_{\,1}\left(\hat{z}\right)\in\mathcal{H}^{+}

By

, we may fix an

such that

We have that

With respect to

, we now evoke the Nielsen-Thurston Classification Theorem, as presented in Section 7.5 of
[
2
]
. It takes as input the homeomorphism
f:\mathds{S}^{2}\to\mathds{S}^{2}
and the set
P
, for which the four punctured sphere
\mathds{S}^{2}\setminus P
has negative Euler characteristic. Then, it yields

•

a homeomorphism
\Phi:\mathds{S}^{2}\to\mathds{S}^{2}
such that
f
and
\Phi
are isotopic relative to
P
or, in other words, such that every point of
P
is fixed throughout the isotopy between

and
\Phi
,

•

a (possibly empty) system of closed simple loops
\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{r}
, called

.

Such curves come equipped with pairwise disjoint tubular neighbourhoods
V_{i}
, disjoint from
P
, such that the connected components of
\mathds{S}^{2}\setminus\bigcup_{i=1}^{r}V_{i}
group into invariant cycles, restricted to which either
\Phi
is of

– meaning some power of it equals the identity – or
\Phi
is
pseudo-Anosov
relative to
P
.

Briefly, pseudo-Anosov means that
\Phi
(or its restriction to the appropriate component) admits a pair of invariant transverse foliations – one of which is expanded with ratio
\beta>1
and the other of which is contracted with ratio
\beta^{-1}
(in a measure-theoretical precise sense).
Relative to
P
means that the points in
P
are kept fixed under

, and manifest as one-prong singularities of the foliations.

If the reducing system of curves is empty, we notice that no power

can be isotopic to the identity relative to
P
. Indeed, let
\alpha
be any simple closed loop separating two pairs of points of
P

would have to be (freely) homotopic to

relative to

. But, considering the
\mathcal{I}G_{3}
isotopy
\left(F^{m}_{t}\right)_{t\in\mathds{I}}
, the family

would have to enclose the figure 8 in a fashion incompatible with such fact.

Upon interpreting points in
P
as punctures (as implied by
[
9
]
and Section 7.6 of
[
2
]
), each connected component of
\mathds{S}^{2}\setminus\bigcup_{i=1}^{r}V_{i}
must also have negative Euler characteristic. Thus, if the reducing system of curves is nonempty,
\hat{w}
must be enclosed by
\alpha_{i}
along with some other reference point
a\in\left\{\mathbf{0},\mathbf{1},\mathbf{\infty}\right\}
. Then, the argument from the previous paragraph also shows that
\Phi
cannot be of finite order in any component.

In short,
\Phi
must be a pseudo-Anosov map, which is classically known to have strictly positive topological entropy
h_{\text{top}}\left(\Phi\right)=\log\beta>0
. But the behaviour of mappings isotopic to pseudo-Anosov homeomorphisms is described in
[
5
]
. More specifically, Theorem 2 therein implies the statement of

, and also

. Thus,

must have positive topological entropy as well.

6.
Characterization of the conformal group in terms of transitivity

In this section, we show that sharp 3-transitivity is a defining property of
\Mob(\mathds{S}^{2})
among the homogeneous groups of diffeomorphisms, as more precisely stated in
Theorem C
. Before proving it, we stablish two auxiliary lemmas. The first shows that the subgroup
G_{2}
fixing the poles possesses a property that we already know
a priori
to be held by the actual
\Mob(\mathds{S}^{2})
. The second establishes conformality at the poles in this subgroup.

Lemma 6.1
.

Let
G\subset\homeos_{\,}\!(\mathds{S}^{2})
be a sharply 3-transitive homogeneous group. Then, the subgroup
G_{2}
permutes parallels.

Proof.

Recalling that
G_{2}
is the subgroup fixing
\mathbf{0}
and
\mathbf{\infty}
,
g\in G_{2}
translates to a planar homeomorphism fixing the origin, for which we must prove that circles centered at the origin are mapped onto circles centered at the origin. Given one such circle
\gamma
, let
\lambda=g\left(\gamma\right)
be its image. Then,
\lambda
is a Jordan curve, containing the origin in its interior. If
\lambda
is
not
a circle, it contains points
p_{m}
and
p_{M}
such that

\left|p_{m}\right|=\min_{p\in\lambda}\left|p\right|<\max_{p\in\lambda}\left|p\right|=\left|p_{M}\right|\text{.}

For each polar angle
0\leq\theta<2\pi
, the semiradius
\vec{r}_{\theta}=\left\{t\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\,\theta}\,:\,t\geq 0\right\}
intercepts
\lambda
in a compact set
\lambda_{\theta}
, in such a way that
\lambda=\bigcup_{0\leq\theta<2\pi}\lambda_{\theta}
. For some
\theta_{0}
, it must be the case that
\left|p\right|<\left|p_{M}\right|
for every
p\in\lambda_{\theta_{0}}
.

Now, if
\theta_{M}
is such that
p_{M}\in\lambda_{\theta_{M}}
, let
R
be a planar rotation mapping the semiradius
\vec{r}_{\theta_{M}}
onto the semiradius
\vec{r}_{\theta_{0}}
. Then,
R\left(p_{M}\right)\in R\left(\lambda\right)
, but
R\left(p_{M}\right)\in\exter\lambda=\left(\overline{\inter\lambda}\right)^{\mathsf{c}}
, where
\inter
and
\exter
are used in the Jordan Curve Theorem sense, as the bounded and unbounded open connected components of
\lambda^{\mathsf{c}}
, sharing
\lambda
as their common boundary. We remark that
\left(\overline{\inter\lambda}\right)^{\mathsf{c}}\subset\left\{z\,:\,\left|z\right|>\left|p_{m}\right|\right\}
. So, it cannot be the case that
R\left(\lambda\right)
is fully contained within
\exter\lambda
, since
\left|R\left(p_{m}\right)\right|=\left|p_{m}\right|
. Therefore, we also must have
R\left(\lambda\right)\cap\overline{\inter\lambda}\neq\emptyset
. It follows that
R\left(\lambda\right)\cap\lambda\neq\emptyset
.

That said, we may obtain
p,q\in\gamma
such that
R\left(g(p)\right)=g(q)
, implying
\left(g^{-1}\circ R\circ g\right)(p)=q
. Since
p,q
lie on the same circle, there exists a planar rotation
U
such that
p=U(q)
. But then,
g^{-1}\circ R\circ g\circ U
defines an element of
G_{2}
fixing
q
. By sharp 3-transitivity, it must be the identity. In particular,
g^{-1}\circ R\circ g\circ U\;\left(\gamma\right)=\gamma
. Since rotations leave
\gamma
invariant, the above implies
R\left(\lambda\right)=\lambda
, which is a contradiction. Therefore,
\lambda
has to be a circle.
∎

Lemma 6.2
.

Let
G\subset\homeos_{\,}\!(\mathds{S}^{2})
be a sharply 3-transitive homogeneous group of diffeomorphisms. Then, every
g\in G_{2}
is conformal at the poles.

Proof.

Given such
g
, we know from
Lemma
6.1
that it permutes paralllels. It thus suffices to consider the case of a planar diffeomorphism fixing the origin and mapping circles centered at the origin onto circles centered at the origin. We know that
A=\mathrm{D}g\!\left(\mathbf{0}\right)
is a linear isomorphism, so we may fix
z_{m},z_{M}\in\mathds{S}^{1}
such that

0<\left|Az_{m}\right|=\min_{z\in\mathds{S}^{1}}\left|Az\right|\leq\max_{z\in\mathds{S}^{1}}\left|Az\right|=\left|Az_{M}\right|\text{.}

Since
g
preserves circles, for each
t\in\left(0,\!1\right)
:

1=\frac{\left|g\left(tz_{m}\right)\right|}{\left|g\left(tz_{M}\right)\right|}=\frac{\left|A\left(tz_{m}\right)+o\left(\left|tz_{m}\right|\right)\right|}{\left|A\left(tz_{M}\right)+o\left(\left|tz_{M}\right|\right)\right|}=\frac{\left|A\left(z_{m}\right)+\frac{o\left(\left|tz_{m}\right|\right)}{t}\right|}{\left|A\left(z_{M}\right)+\frac{o\left(\left|tz_{M}\right|\right)}{t}\right|}\to\frac{\left|A\left(z_{m}\right)\right|}{\left|A\left(z_{M}\right)\right|}\quad\text{ as }t\to 0^{+}\text{.}

It follows that
A\left(\mathds{S}^{1}\right)
is a circle. Since
A
is orientation preserving, this is enough to conclude that it is a conformal matrix. In other words,
g
is conformal at
\mathbf{0}
.
∎

Before proceeding to the proof of
Theorem C
, let us make a small remark: if
G
is a 2-transitive homogeneous group of diffeomorphisms and
\delta>0
is given, we may find
h_{\delta}\in G
such that
h_{\delta}
fixes
\mathbf{0}
, but not
\mathbf{\infty}
, and
\mathrm{D}h_{\delta}\!\left(\mathbf{0}\right)
is
\delta
-close to
\mathrm{id}
.

Indeed, this can be verified as follows: by 2-transitivity, we may fix
h\in G
such that
h\left(\mathbf{0}\right)=\mathbf{0}
and
h\left(\mathbf{\infty}\right)=\mathbf{1}
. Then, as long as
0\leq t<2\pi
,
h_{t}=h^{-1}\circ R_{t}\circ h
fixes
\mathbf{0}
, but not
\mathbf{\infty}
, since
R_{t}\left(\mathbf{1}\right)
is a point on the equator distinct from
\mathbf{1}
. It thus suffices to take
h_{\delta}=h_{\hat{t}}
, for sufficiently small
\hat{t}
. This might seem like an underuse of the 3-transitivity hypothesis. But, as it turns out, a result from
[
7
]
implies that any 2-transitive homogeneous group must actually be 3-transitive. Either way, we are now ready to establish
Theorem C
.

6.1.
Proof of Theorem C

Suppose, for the sake of contradiction, that
G
contains a nonconformal mapping
g
. By precomposing and postcomposing with suitable rotations, it may be assumed that
g
fixes
\mathbf{0}
and that
A=\mathrm{D}g\!\left(\mathbf{0}\right)
is a nonconformal matrix. This means that, for some pair of unit vectors
u_{0}
and
v_{0}
,

(15)

\text{if }\alpha=\left\langle u_{0},v_{0}\right\rangle\text{ and }\beta=\left\langle\frac{Au_{0}}{\left|Au_{0}\right|},\frac{Au_{0}}{\left|Au_{0}\right|}\right\rangle\text{, }\text{ then }\alpha\neq\beta\text{.}

Given
\varepsilon=\left|\beta-\alpha\right|/2
there exists
\delta>0
such that, for every
u,v,w,z
unit vectors,
\left|u-z\right|<\delta
and
\left|v-w\right|<\delta
imply
|\left\langle u,v\right\rangle-\left\langle z,w\right\rangle|<\varepsilon
. We then fix
h_{\delta}\in G
such that

•

h_{\delta}(\mathbf{0})=\mathbf{0}
,

•

h_{\delta}^{-1}\left(\mathbf{\infty}\right)=p_{\delta}\neq\mathbf{\infty}
, and

•

C_{\delta}=\mathrm{D}h_{\delta}\!\left(\mathbf{0}\right)
is
\delta/2
-close to
\mathrm{id}
.

Since
G
is 3-transitive, we may fix
f_{\delta}\in G_{2}
such that
f_{\delta}\left(g(\mathbf{\infty})\right)=p_{\delta}
. From
Lemma
6.2
, we know that
B=\mathrm{D}f_{\delta}\!\left(\mathbf{0}\right)
is conformal. If we let
g=h_{\delta}\circ f_{\delta}\circ g
, then also
g\in G_{2}
, and thus
D=\mathrm{D}g\!\left(\mathbf{0}\right)
is conformal. But, by the Chain Rule,
D=C_{\delta}BA
. We remark that, if
w_{0}
is any unit vector, then

\displaystyle\left|\frac{C_{\delta}w_{0}}{\left|C_{\delta}w_{0}\right|}-w_{0}\right|

\displaystyle=\left|C_{\delta}w_{0}-w_{0}-\frac{\left|C_{\delta}w_{0}\right|-1}{\left|C_{\delta}w_{0}\right|}\,C_{\delta}w_{0}\right|

\displaystyle\leq 2\,\left|C_{\delta}w_{0}-w_{0}\right|<\delta\text{.}

Since
B
is conformal,
\displaystyle\left|\frac{Du_{0}}{\left|Du_{0}\right|}-\frac{Au_{0}}{\left|Au_{0}\right|}\right|<\delta
and
\displaystyle\left|\frac{Dv_{0}}{\left|Dv_{0}\right|}-\frac{Av_{0}}{\left|Av_{0}\right|}\right|<\delta
. Accordingly,

\left|\left\langle\frac{Du_{0}}{\left|Du_{0}\right|},\frac{Dv_{0}}{\left|Dv_{0}\right|}\right\rangle-\left\langle\frac{Au_{0}}{\left|Au_{0}\right|},\frac{Av_{0}}{\left|Av_{0}\right|}\right\rangle\right|=\left|\left\langle\frac{Du_{0}}{\left|Du_{0}\right|},\frac{Dv_{0}}{\left|Dv_{0}\right|}\right\rangle-\beta\right|<\varepsilon\text{.}

By the choice of
\varepsilon
, this ensures that the angle between
Du_{0}
and
Dv_{0}
is different from the angle between
u_{0}
and
v_{0}
, contradicting
D
conformal. Thus, such nonconformal
g\in G
cannot exist, and
G
is a subgroup of
\Mob(\mathds{S}^{2})
.

Lastly, let
M\in\Mob(\mathds{S}^{2})
. Then, there exists
h\in G\subset\Mob(\mathds{S}^{2})
such that
h\left(\mathbf{0}\right)=M(\mathbf{0})
,
h\left(\mathbf{1}\right)=M\left(\mathbf{1}\right)
and
h\left(\mathbf{\infty}\right)=M\left(\mathbf{\infty}\right)
. By the sharp 3-transitivity of
\Mob(\mathds{S}^{2})
, this implies

. Since
M
was arbitrary,
G=\Mob(\mathds{S}^{2})
follows.
