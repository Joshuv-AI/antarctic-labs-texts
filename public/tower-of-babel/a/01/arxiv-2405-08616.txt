Abstract




Efficient computation of Fourier-Bessel transforms

for transverse-momentum dependent parton distributions

and other functions

Markus Diehl and Oskar Grocholski

Deutsches Elektronen-Synchrotron DESY, Notkestr. 85, 22607 Hamburg, Germany

We present a method for the numerical computation of Fourier-Bessel transforms
on a finite or infinite interval. The function to be transformed needs to be
evaluated on a grid of points that is independent of the argument of the Bessel
function. We demonstrate the accuracy of the algorithm for a wide range of
functions, including those that appear in the context of transverse-momentum
dependent parton distributions in Quantum Chromodynamics.

1
Introduction

The Fourier-Bessel transform — also known as Hankel transform — appears in
many branches of physics; in particular it arises from the multi-dimensional
Fourier transform of functions with rotational symmetry. It has the form

\displaystyle I(q)

\displaystyle=\int_{z_{a}}^{z_{b}}dz\,J_{\nu}(qz)\,\tilde{f}(z)\,,

(1.1)

where
J_{\nu}
is a Bessel function of the first kind. In general the
integration runs from
0
to
\infty
, but it may be reduced to a finite
interval by the support properties of
\tilde{f}(z)
.

In Quantum Chromodynamics, the Fourier-Bessel transform prominently appears in
the context of transverse-momentum dependent parton distributions (TMDs), where
cross sections are proportional to integrals of the form (
1.1
) with

\nu=0,1,2,3
and
\tilde{f}(z)
equal to
z^{\nu+1}
times the product of two
TMDs,
1
1

1

We note that in the TMD literature, the spatial distance is
usually called
b
rather than
z
.

see e.g. section 2.11 in
[
1
]
.
The integration limits are
0
and
\infty
in that case. The method proposed
in
[
2
]
involves similar integrals over TMDs from
0
to some
finite value, and from that finite value to
\infty
.
A more involved application is given by two-parton TMDs, which appear in the
description of double parton scattering
[
3
]
. In this case,
two nested Fourier transforms occur, the radial parts of which can be converted
into two nested Fourier-Bessel integrals,
\int dz_{1}\int dz_{2}\,J_{\nu_{1}}(q_{1}z_{1})\,J_{\nu_{2}}(q_{2}\mskip 1.5muz_{2})\,\tilde{f}(z_{1},z_{2})
.

In the cases just mentioned, one typically needs to compute the transform
(
1.1
) for many settings of external parameters, namely longitudinal
parton momentum fractions and hard scales. For uncertainty estimates on TMDs
fitted to data (using the Hessian method or replicas) one must repeat the
calculation for many TMD sets. The numerical computation of the integrands
itself can be rather costly; at small transverse distances
z
it involves
Mellin convolutions in the longitudinal momentum fractions, with lengthy kernels
at higher perturbative orders (see section 2.8 in
[
1
]
and
references therein).

In the TMD context, the variable
q
in (
1.1
) is a measured
transverse momentum, for instance of the produced lepton pair in the Drell-Yan
process. The process to be described is characterised by a hard scale
Q
,
which can range from a few
\operatorname{GeV}
for semi-inclusive deep inelastic scattering
or non-resonant Drell-Yan production to about
100\operatorname{GeV}
for the production
of a
W
,
Z
, or Higgs boson. For the production of a pair of such bosons, the
hard scale
Q
is given by the invariant mass of the pair and can be
significantly larger.
The TMD formalism describes cross sections in the region
q\ll Q
, whilst a
description in terms of conventional collinear parton distributions is adequate
for
q\sim Q
. A smooth transition between the two regimes typically
requires evaluating the TMD expression for

up to order

, see
section 4.7 in
[
1
]
for an overview and
[
4
]

for a recent example.

range of

values. In such a scenario, integration methods with nodes that

to be computed many times. This is the case for the method of Ogata

\displaystyle I(q)

\displaystyle=\int_{z_{a}}^{z_{b}}dz\,J_{\nu}(qz)\,\biggl(\frac{1+z}{z}\biggr)^{\nu}\,f(z)\,,

2
Developing the method

2.1
The original Levin method

To begin with, let us present the original method of Levin
[
13
]
for
the case of Fourier-Bessel integrals. We use a slightly different notation than
the cited work. The integral to be evaluated has the form

\displaystyle I(q)

\displaystyle=\int_{z_{a}}^{z_{b}}dz\,\Bigl[\,\widetilde{\omega}_{1}(z,q)\,\tilde{f}_{1}(z)+\widetilde{\omega}_{2}(z,q)\,\tilde{f}_{2}(z)\,\Bigr]

(2.1)

with weights

\displaystyle\widetilde{\omega}_{1}(z,q)

\displaystyle=J_{\nu}(qz)\,,

\displaystyle\widetilde{\omega}_{2}

\displaystyle=J_{\nu+1}(qz)\,,

(2.2)

where it is assumed that the functions
\tilde{f}_{1}(z)
and
\tilde{f}_{2}(z)
are

not
strongly oscillating.
The Fourier-Bessel transform (
1.1
) is obtained by setting
\tilde{f}_{1}=\tilde{f}
and
\tilde{f}_{2}=0
. Setting
\tilde{f}_{1}=0
and
\tilde{f}_{2}=\tilde{f}
instead,
one obtains the analogue of (
1.1
) for
J_{\nu+1}
instead of

J_{\nu}
. This requires only a minor additional computational effort; a bonus
that is preserved by our adaptation of Levin’s method.

To proceed, we introduce the vector notation

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\widetilde{\omega}}

\displaystyle=\bigl(\widetilde{\omega}_{1}\,,\widetilde{\omega}_{2}\bigr)^{T}\,,

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\tilde{f}}

\displaystyle=\bigl(\tilde{f}_{1}\,,\tilde{f}_{2}\bigr)^{T}\,,

(2.3)

where a superscript
T
denotes transposition. Using the relations
(
A.1
), one readily derives

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dz}\,\boldsymbol{\widetilde{\omega}}(z,q)

\displaystyle=\widetilde{A}^{\,T}(z,q)\cdot\boldsymbol{\widetilde{\omega}}(z,q)

(2.4)

with the matrix

\displaystyle\widetilde{A}(z,q)

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}\nu/z\,,&q\\
-q\,,&-(\nu+1)/z\end{pmatrix}\,.

(2.5)

If one finds a solution for the system

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\tilde{f}}(z)

\displaystyle=\biggl[\frac{d}{dz}+\widetilde{A}(z,q)\biggr]\,\boldsymbol{\tilde{h}}(z,q)

(2.6)

of ordinary differential equations (ODEs), one can evaluate the integral as

\displaystyle I(q)

\displaystyle=\int_{z_{a}}^{z_{b}}dz\,\boldsymbol{\widetilde{\omega}}^{T}(z,q)\cdot\boldsymbol{\tilde{f}}(z)=\Bigl[\mskip 1.5mu\boldsymbol{\widetilde{\omega}}^{T}(z,q)\cdot\boldsymbol{\tilde{h}}(z,q)\mskip 1.5mu\Bigr]_{z_{a}}^{z_{b}}\;\;,

(2.7)

where we use the common notation

\displaystyle\bigl[F(z,q)\bigr]_{z_{a}}^{z_{b}}

\displaystyle=F(z_{b},q)-F(z_{a},q)\,.

(2.8)

This is readily seen by inserting (
2.6
) in the first expression
of (
2.7
), integrating by parts, and then using
(
2.4
).

As argued in
[
12
,
13
]
, the system (
2.6
) has
one particular solution with functions
\tilde{h}_{i}(z,q)
that are not rapidly
oscillating.
One can find this solution by collocation, which consists in approximating

\tilde{h}_{1}(z,q)
and
\tilde{h}_{2}(z,q)
by a linear combination of
n
basis
functions
b_{i}(z)
and by determining the coefficients of these functions by
evaluating the system (
2.6
) at a set of
n
suitably chosen
collocation points
z_{i}
. This yields a coupled system of linear equations that
can be solved by standard linear algebra methods (see section
3.2.1

for details).
For the method to give accurate results, it is essential that the approximation
of
\tilde{h}_{1}
and
\tilde{h}_{2}
is sufficiently precise, in particular at the
end points
z_{a}
and
z_{b}
, where these functions are evaluated in the final
result (
2.7
).

As it stands, this method is not suitable for a lower integration boundary
z_{a}=0
, where the diagonal elements of
\widetilde{A}
diverge. We circumvent this
problem by rescaling, as described in the next subsection.

2.2
Rescaling: general form

The representation (
2.7
) of
I(q)
remains valid if one replaces

\boldsymbol{\tilde{f}}
,
\boldsymbol{\tilde{h}}
and
\boldsymbol{\widetilde{\omega}}
by the rescaled functions

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{f}(z)

\displaystyle=r^{\nu}(z)\,\boldsymbol{\tilde{f}}(z)\,,

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{h}(z)

\displaystyle=r^{\nu}(z)\,\boldsymbol{\tilde{h}}(z)\,,

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\omega}(z,q)

\displaystyle=r^{-\nu}(z)\,\boldsymbol{\widetilde{\omega}}(z,q)\,,

(2.9)

where
r(z)
will be specified below.
Using the explicit form (
2.5
) of
\widetilde{A}
, one can rewrite the system of
ODEs (
2.6
) as

\displaystyle f_{1}

\displaystyle=\frac{d}{dz}\,h_{1}+qh_{2}+\nu\mskip 1.5mu\bigg[\mskip 1.5mu\frac{1}{z}-\frac{r^{\prime}}{r}\mskip 1.5mu\biggr]\,h_{1}\,,

\displaystyle f_{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{d}{dz}\,h_{2}-qh_{1}-\biggl[\mskip 1.5mu\frac{\nu+1}{z}+\nu\,\frac{r^{\prime}}{r}\mskip 1.5mu\biggr]\,h_{2}\,,

(2.10)

where we have omitted the arguments
z
or
(z,q)
of the functions and
abbreviated
r^{\prime}=dr/dz
.
The singularity at
z=0
in the first equation of (
2.10
) is removed if

\displaystyle r(z)

\displaystyle\sim z

\displaystyle\text{ for }z\to 0\,,

(2.11)

and the singularity in the second equation is avoided by setting

\displaystyle h_{2}(z,q)

\displaystyle=r(z)\,h_{3}(z,q)\,.

(2.12)

The final form of the ODE system then reads

\displaystyle f_{1}

\displaystyle=\frac{d}{dz}\,h_{1}+q\mskip 1.5mur\mskip 1.5muh_{3}+\nu\mskip 1.5mu\bigg[\mskip 1.5mu\frac{1}{z}-\frac{r^{\prime}}{r}\mskip 1.5mu\biggr]\,h_{1}\,,

\displaystyle f_{2}

\displaystyle=r\,\frac{d}{dz}\,h_{3}-qh_{1}-\biggl[\mskip 1.5mu(\nu+1)\,\frac{r}{z}+(\nu-1)\,r^{\prime}\mskip 1.5mu\biggr]\,h_{3}\,,

(2.13)

and the integral
I(q)
is given by

\displaystyle I(q)

\displaystyle=\int_{z_{a}}^{z_{b}}dz\;\,r^{-\nu}(z)\,\Bigl[J_{\nu}(qz)\,f_{1}(z)+J_{\nu+1}(qz)\,f_{2}(z)\Bigr]

\displaystyle=\biggl[\mskip 1.5muJ_{\nu}(qz)\,r^{-\nu}(z)\,h_{1}(z,q)+J_{\nu+1}(qz)\,r^{-\nu+1}(z)\,h_{3}(z,q)\mskip 1.5mu\biggr]_{z_{a}}^{z_{b}}\,.

(2.14)

We will show that if
f_{1}(z)
and
f_{2}(z)
are sufficiently well-behaved
at
z\to 0
, the solutions
h_{1}(z,q)
and
h_{3}(z,q)
of (
2.13
) are
well behaved at
z\to 0
as well. This enables one to solve the system by
collocation with basis functions that have a Taylor expansion in
z
around

z=0
. We anticipate that our method will use basis functions with this
property, postponing details to section
3.2
.

2.3
Rescaling with
r(z)=z

The main purpose of this subsection is to explicitly solve the system
(
2.13
) of ODEs for particular choices of
f_{1}
and
f_{2}
. This is an
intermediate step, and a reader mainly interested in final version of our method

In the present subsection, we set

\displaystyle r(z)

\displaystyle=z\,,

(2.15)

such that the system of ODE takes the rather simple form

\displaystyle f_{1}

\displaystyle=\frac{d}{dz}\,h_{1}+qz\mskip 1.5muh_{3}\,,

(2.16)

\displaystyle f_{2}

\displaystyle=z\mskip 1.5mu\frac{d}{dz}\,h_{3}-qh_{1}-2\nu\mskip 1.5muh_{3}\,.

One can eliminate

by taking the derivative of (

) and

\displaystyle z^{2}\mskip 1.5mu\frac{d^{2}}{dz^{2}}\,(z^{-\nu}\mskip 1.5muh_{3})+z\mskip 1.5mu\frac{d}{dz}\,(z^{-\nu}\mskip 1.5muh_{3})+\bigl(q^{2}z^{2}-\nu^{2}\bigr)\,(z^{-\nu}\mskip 1.5muh_{3})

\displaystyle=z^{1-\nu}\mskip 1.5mu\bigl(qf_{1}+f^{\mskip 1.5mu\prime}_{2}\mskip 1.5mu\bigr)

If one sets

. The

solution of equations (

)
and (
2.17
) is therefore given by

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}h_{1}\\
h_{3}\end{pmatrix}\Bigg|_{\text{hom}}

\displaystyle=c_{J}\mskip 1.5mu\begin{pmatrix}{}-(qz)^{\nu+1}\,J_{\nu+1}(qz)\\
q\,(qz)^{\nu}\,J_{\nu}(qz)\end{pmatrix}+c_{Y}\mskip 1.5mu\begin{pmatrix}{}-(qz)^{\nu+1}\,Y_{\nu+1}(qz)\\

with arbitrary coefficients

and

At small

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}a_{k}\mskip 1.5mux^{k}

2.23
) or
(
2.26
) must be large enough if
h_{1}
and
h_{3}
are to be sufficiently
well approximated by basis functions that have a Taylor expansion around
z=0
.
In particular, a finite first derivative of
h_{1}
and
h_{3}
at
z=0
requires
\mu+\nu\geq 0
for
f_{1}
and
\mu+\nu\geq 1
for
f_{2}
.

2.3.2
Behaviour of solutions at large
z

We now turn to the behaviour of the functions
h_{1}
and
h_{3}
in the limit
z\to\infty
. If
f_{1}(z)
or
f_{2}(z)
is proportional to
z^{\mu+\nu}
, we can
use the the solutions (
2.25
) or (
2.27
) derived
in the previous subsection. According to (
A.19
), they behave
like

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}h_{1}\\
h_{3}\end{pmatrix}\Bigg|_{\text{$f_{1}$}}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}z^{\mu+\nu-1}\;\sigma(z^{-2})\\
z^{\mu+\nu-1}\;\sigma(z^{-2})\end{pmatrix}\,,

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}h_{1}\\
h_{3}\end{pmatrix}\Bigg|_{\text{$f_{2}$}}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}z^{\mu+\nu}\;\sigma(z^{-2})\\
z^{\mu+\nu-2}\;\sigma(z^{-2})\end{pmatrix}

(2.30)

for
z\gg 1
. We postpone the discussion of this result to
section
3.1
.

Let us now consider functions with an exponential falloff in
z
, namely

\displaystyle f_{1}(z)

\displaystyle=c_{1}\mskip 1.5muz^{\mu+\nu}\,\exp(-\kappa z)\,,

\displaystyle f_{2}(z)

\displaystyle=c_{2}\mskip 1.5muz^{\mu+\nu}\,\exp(-\kappa z)\,.

(2.31)

with
\kappa>0
. To solve the system of ODEs in (
2.16
) and
(
2.17
), we make the ansatz

\displaystyle h_{i}(z,q)

\displaystyle=\hat{h}_{i}(z,q)\,\exp(-\kappa z)

\displaystyle(i=1,3).

(2.32)

This leads to the new system

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}c_{1}\mskip 1.5muz^{\mu+\nu}\\
c_{2}\mskip 1.5muz^{\mu+\nu}\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=-\begin{pmatrix}\kappa&-q\\
q&\kappa\end{pmatrix}\cdot\begin{pmatrix}\hat{h}_{1}\\
z\hat{h}_{3}\end{pmatrix}+\begin{pmatrix}d\mskip 1.5mu\hat{h}_{1}/dz\\
z\mskip 1.5mud\mskip 1.5mu\hat{h}_{3}/dz-2\nu\mskip 1.5mu\hat{h}_{3}\end{pmatrix}\,,

(2.33)

which can be solved with the ansatz

\displaystyle\hat{h}_{1}(z,q)

\displaystyle=z^{\mu+\nu}\,\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}b_{1,k}(q)\mskip 1.5muz^{-k}\,,

\displaystyle\hat{h}_{3}(z,q)

\displaystyle=z^{\mu+\nu-1}\,\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}b_{3,k}(q)\mskip 1.5muz^{-k}\,.

(2.34)

For every single
k
in the sums, the first term on the r.h.s. of
(
2.33
) is leading at large
z
, whereas the second term on the
r.h.s. is down by one power of
z
.
For the leading coefficients, we readily get

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}b_{1,0}\\
b_{3,0}\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{\kappa^{2}+q^{2}}\begin{pmatrix}\kappa&q\\
-q&\kappa\end{pmatrix}\cdot\begin{pmatrix}c_{1}\\
c_{3}\end{pmatrix}\,,

(2.35)

whereas the subleading coefficients can be obtained recursively as

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}b_{1,k+1}\\
b_{3,k+1}\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\kappa^{2}+q^{2}}\begin{pmatrix}\kappa&q\\
-q&\kappa\end{pmatrix}\cdot\begin{pmatrix}(\mu+\nu-k)\,b_{1,k}\\
(\mu-\nu-1-k)\,b_{3,k}\end{pmatrix}\,,

(2.36)

starting with
k=0
.

The upshot of this calculation is that if the functions
f_{1}
and
f_{2}
are of
the form “power times exponential” at large
z
, then the solutions at large

z
have the same form, with the same damping factor
\kappa
in the
exponential.

In appendix
B
we show that a similar statement holds if
f_{1}

and
f_{2}
have a Gaussian decrease in
z
(possibly modified by a power and an
exponential in
z
).

3
Final form of the method

In the previous section, we have shown that the rescaling function
r(z)=z

leads to well behaved solutions
h_{1}
and
h_{3}
of the differential equations
(
2.13
) in the limit
z=0
. This choice is however not optimal for the
opposite limit
z\to\infty
, where the requirement that the functions
f_{1}

and
f_{2}
in (
2.14
) must remain finite at the end points of
the integration interval would reduce the class of integrals that can be
evaluated. Our final choice of rescaling functions is therefore

\displaystyle r(z)

\displaystyle=\frac{z}{1+z}\,,

(3.1)

which interpolates smoothly between the choice
r(z)=z
at small
z
and no
rescaling as
z\to\infty
. A more general version would be
r(z)=z/(1+\alpha z)
with
\alpha>0
, but we shall not explore this option here.

We can then compute the integral

\displaystyle I(q)

\displaystyle=\int_{z_{a}}^{z_{b}}dz\;\biggl(\frac{1+z}{z}\biggr)^{\nu}\,\biggl[J_{\nu}(qz)\,f_{1}(z)+J_{\nu+1}(qz)\,f_{2}(z)\biggr]

(3.2)

as

\displaystyle I(q)

\displaystyle=\biggl[J_{\nu}(qz)\,\biggl(\frac{1+z}{z}\biggr)^{\nu}\,h_{1}(z,q)+J_{\nu+1}(qz)\,\biggl(\frac{1+z}{z}\biggr)^{\nu-1}\,h_{3}(z,q)\biggr]_{z_{a}}^{z_{b}}\;,

(3.3)

where
h_{1}
and
h_{3}
solve the system

\displaystyle f_{1}(z)

\displaystyle=\frac{d}{dz}\,h_{1}(z,q)+q\,\frac{z}{1+z}\,h_{3}(z,q)+\frac{\nu}{1+z}\,h_{1}(z,q)\,,

\displaystyle f_{2}(z)

\displaystyle=\frac{z}{1+z}\,\frac{d}{dz}\,h_{3}(z,q)-qh_{1}(z,q)-\biggl[\mskip 1.5mu\frac{\nu-1}{(1+z)^{2}}+\frac{\nu+1}{1+z}\mskip 1.5mu\biggr]\,h_{3}(z,q)

(3.4)

of ODEs. For convenience, we will call (
3.4
) the
Levin
equations
in the following.

For the reasons given in section
2.3
, the relations
(
3.3
) and (
3.4
) are used for
\nu\geq 1
. To
evaluate integrals with Bessel functions of order below
1
, we instead use the
relation (
A.2
) and subsequent integration by parts, which gives

\displaystyle\int_{z_{a}}^{z_{b}}dz\;J_{\nu-1}(qz)\,\biggl(\frac{1+z}{z}\biggr)^{\nu-1}\,f_{0}(z)

\displaystyle\qquad=\frac{1}{q}\int_{z_{a}}^{z_{b}}dz\;\frac{d}{dz}\mskip 1.5mu\biggl[z^{\nu}\mskip 1.5muJ_{\nu}(qz)\biggr]\;\frac{1}{z^{\nu}}\,\biggl(\frac{1+z}{z}\biggr)^{\nu-1}\,f_{0}(z)

\displaystyle\qquad=\frac{1}{q}\biggl[J_{\nu}(qz)\,\biggl(\frac{1+z}{z}\biggr)^{\nu-1}\,f_{0}(z)\biggr]_{z_{a}}^{z_{b}}-\frac{1}{q}\int_{z_{a}}^{z_{b}}dz\;J_{\nu}(qz)\,\biggl(\frac{1+z}{z}\biggr)^{\nu}\,f_{1}(z)

(3.5)

with

\displaystyle f_{1}(z)

\displaystyle=\frac{z}{1+z}\mskip 1.5mu\frac{d}{dz}\mskip 1.5muf_{0}(z)-\biggl[\frac{\nu-1}{(1+z)^{2}}+\frac{\nu}{1+z}\biggr]\mskip 1.5muf_{0}(z)\,.

(3.6)

Since we require
f_{0}(z)
to be finite at the integration limits, the
boundary terms on the r.h.s. of (
3.5
) vanish for
z_{a}=0
and
z_{b}=\infty
. The derivative in (
3.6
) is computed numerically in our method, as specified in equation (
3.25
).

3.1
Behaviour of solutions at small and large

z

From equation (
2.14
) it is clear that one obtains the same
integral with different rescaling factors by a suitable transformation of the
functions
f_{i}
and
h_{i}
in the system of ODEs. For the choices made in
the present and in the previous section, we have

\displaystyle f_{i}(z)\mskip 1.5mu\big|_{r=z/(1+z)}

\displaystyle=(1+z)^{-\nu}\;f_{i}(z)\mskip 1.5mu\big|_{r=z}

\displaystyle(i=1,2),

\displaystyle h_{1}(z,q)\mskip 1.5mu\big|_{r=z/(1+z)}

\displaystyle=(1+z)^{-\nu}\;h_{1}(z,q)\mskip 1.5mu\big|_{r=z}\,,

\displaystyle h_{3}(z,q)\mskip 1.5mu\big|_{r=z/(1+z)}

\displaystyle=(1+z)^{-\nu+1}\;h_{3}(z,q)\mskip 1.5mu\big|_{r=z}\,.

(3.7)

We thus readily obtain the small and large
z
behaviour of
h_{i}
with our final rescaling choice from our results in section
2.3
.

For the limit
z\to 0
, we deduce from (
2.28
) and
(
2.29
) that

\displaystyle f_{1}(z)

\displaystyle\sim z^{\mu+\nu}\,\sigma(z)

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\;h_{3}(z,q)

\displaystyle\sim\sigma(z)+z^{\mu+\nu+1}\,\sigma(z)+z^{2\nu}\,\sigma(z)+z^{2\nu}\mskip 1.5mu\ln(z)\,\sigma(z)

(3.8)

and

\displaystyle f_{2}(z)

\displaystyle\sim z^{\mu+\nu}\,\sigma(z)

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\;h_{3}(z,q)

\displaystyle\sim\sigma(z)+z^{\mu+\nu}\,\sigma(z)+z^{2\nu}\,\sigma(z)+z^{2\nu}\mskip 1.5mu\ln(z)\,\sigma(z)\,,

(3.9)

where we recall that
\sigma(z)
denotes a power series in
z
in the generic
sense explained after equation (
2.20
).
The behaviour of
h_{1}(z,q)
is as the one of
h_{3}(z,q)
in both
(
3.8
) and (
3.9
), except that the
power
z^{2\nu}
is replaced with
z^{2\nu+2}
. It is understood that
\mu+\nu\geq 0
, which ensures that
f_{1}
and
f_{2}
remain finite at
z=0
and that
the integral (
3.2
) with
z_{a}=0
actually exists.

We recall from our discussion in section
2.3.1
that small
non-integer values of
\mu+\nu
lead to a non-analytic behaviour of
h_{1}
and

h_{3}
at
z=0
that is difficult to approximate by functions having a Taylor
expansion around that point. We therefore expect our method to become less
accurate in this case. The comparison between (
3.8
)
and (
3.9
) reveals that for an integrand behaving like

z^{\mu}J_{\rho}(qz)
around
z=0
, one should use (
3.2
) with

\nu=\rho
and
f_{2}=0
if
1<\mu+\rho<2
, rather than with
\nu=\rho-1
and
f_{1}=0
. The same holds if
0\leq\mu+\rho<1
. For
\mu+\rho=1

or
\mu+\rho\geq 2
, both options are possible.

For the large-
z
limit, we can discuss several cases. From
(
2.30
) we deduce that for a power-law behaviour of
f_{i}
we
have

\displaystyle f_{1}(z)

\displaystyle\sim z^{\mu}\,\sigma(z^{-1})\quad\Rightarrow\;\begin{pmatrix}h_{1}(z,q)\\
h_{3}(z,q)\end{pmatrix}\sim\begin{pmatrix}z^{\mu-1}\mskip 1.5mu\sigma(z^{-1})\\
z^{\mu}\mskip 1.5mu\sigma(z^{-1})\end{pmatrix}\,,

\displaystyle f_{2}(z)

\displaystyle\sim z^{\mu}\,\sigma(z^{-1})\quad\Rightarrow\;\begin{pmatrix}h_{1}(z,q)\\
h_{3}(z,q)\end{pmatrix}\sim\begin{pmatrix}z^{\mu}\mskip 1.5mu\sigma(z^{-1})\\
z^{\mu-1}\mskip 1.5mu\sigma(z^{-1})\end{pmatrix}\,.

(3.10)

Both the functions
f_{i}
and the solutions
h_{i}
remain finite for
z\to\infty
provided that
\mu\leq 0
. Our method thus allows the computation of
Fourier-Bessel integrals not only for functions
f_{i}
with a power-law
decrease but also for functions that tend to a finite value at
z\to\infty
.

For functions with an exponential falloff at large
z
, we get

\displaystyle f_{1}(z)\text{ or }f_{2}(z)

\displaystyle\sim z^{\mu}\,\sigma(z^{-1})\mskip 1.5mu\exp(-\kappa z)\quad\Rightarrow\;\begin{pmatrix}h_{1}(z,q)\\
h_{3}(z,q)\end{pmatrix}\sim\begin{pmatrix}z^{\mu}\mskip 1.5mu\sigma(z^{-1})\\
z^{\mu}\mskip 1.5mu\sigma(z^{-1})\end{pmatrix}\exp(-\kappa z)

(3.11)

for
\kappa>0
according to section
2.3.2
, whereas for
functions with a Gaussian falloff, the results of appendix
B
imply
that

\displaystyle f_{1}(z)

\displaystyle\sim z^{\mu}\,\sigma(z^{-1})\mskip 1.5mu\exp(-\lambda^{2}z^{2}-\kappa z\mskip 1.5mu\bigr)\;\quad\Rightarrow\;\begin{pmatrix}h_{1}(z,q)\\
h_{3}(z,q)\end{pmatrix}\sim\begin{pmatrix}z^{\mu-1}\mskip 1.5mu\sigma(z^{-1})\\
z^{\mu-2}\mskip 1.5mu\sigma(z^{-1})\end{pmatrix}\exp(-\lambda^{2}z^{2}-\kappa z\mskip 1.5mu\bigr)\;\,,

\displaystyle f_{2}(z)

\displaystyle\sim z^{\mu}\,\sigma(z^{-1})\mskip 1.5mu\exp(-\lambda^{2}z^{2}-\kappa z\mskip 1.5mu\bigr)\;\quad\Rightarrow\;\begin{pmatrix}h_{1}(z,q)\\
h_{3}(z,q)\end{pmatrix}\sim\begin{pmatrix}z^{\mu-2}\mskip 1.5mu\sigma(z^{-1})\\
z^{\mu-1}\mskip 1.5mu\sigma(z^{-1})\end{pmatrix}\exp(-\lambda^{2}z^{2}-\kappa z\mskip 1.5mu\bigr)\;

(3.12)

for
\lambda>0
.
Both in the exponential and in the Gaussian case, the solutions
h_{1}
and
h_{3}

thus have the same leading behaviour for
z\to 0
as the functions
f_{1}
or

f_{2}
, except for a possible downward shift of the power
z^{\mu}
by one or
two units.

Using integration by parts.

It remains to discuss the case where one uses integration by parts (
3.5
)
and then evaluates the integral on its r.h.s. by Levin’s method.

With (
3.6
) we find

\displaystyle f_{0}(z)

\displaystyle\sim z^{\mu+\nu-1}\,\sigma(z)\quad\Rightarrow\;f_{1}(z)\sim z^{\mu+\nu-1}\,\sigma(z)

(3.13)

for
z\to 0
, so that our method can be applied to integrands behaving like

z^{\mu}\mskip 1.5muJ_{\rho}(qz)
with
\mu+\rho\geq 0
for all
\rho\geq 0
.

At large
z
we have

\displaystyle f_{0}(z)

\displaystyle\sim z^{\mu}\,\sigma(z^{-1})\mskip 1.5mu\exp(-\lambda^{2}z^{2}-\kappa z\mskip 1.5mu\bigr)\;\quad\Rightarrow\;f_{1}(z)\sim z^{\rho}\,\sigma(z^{-1})\,\exp(-\lambda^{2}z^{2}-\kappa z\mskip 1.5mu\bigr)\;

(3.14)

with

\displaystyle\rho

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\mu-1&\text{ if }\lambda=\kappa=0\,,\\
\mu&\text{ if }\lambda=0\text{ and }\kappa>0\,,\\
\mu+1&\text{ if }\lambda>0\,.\end{cases}

(3.15)

One thus gets the same leading exponential or Gaussian behaviour for
f_{0}
and

f_{1}
. A pure power law
f_{0}\sim z^{\mu}
with
\mu\leq 0
implies a power-law
decrease
f_{1}\sim z^{\mu-1}
.

Grand summary.

As an upshot of our detailed discussion, we retain
that if a set of basis functions is good for approximating the functions
f_{1}

or
f_{2}
in (
3.2
), or
f_{0}
in (
3.5
), it can be expected
to be good for approximating the solutions
h_{1}
and
h_{3}
of the Levin
equations (
3.4
) and hence allow for an accurate computation of the
integrals. A small-
z
behaviour like
z^{1-\delta}
with
\delta<1
is tolerable for
f_{0}
or
f_{1}
, because the corresponding power of
z

for
h_{1}
and
h_{3}
is larger by one unit.

3.2
Discretisation on Chebyshev grids

We now discuss in detail how we implement the method of collocation for solving
the Levin equations (
3.4
).

In most examples of Levin’s original work
[
12
,
13
]
, the
chosen basis functions are polynomials
b_{i}(z)=(z-z_{0})^{i}
, and the
system of ODEs is evaluated at
equispaced
points between
z_{a}
and
z_{b}
.
It was found that the accuracy of the method could be improved by dividing the
integration domain into several subintervals. Other options for the basis
functions are mentioned in
[
12
]
.

It is well known that polynomial approximation on equispaced grids suffers from
Runge’s phenomenon: the approximation becomes poor in the vicinity of the
interval end points when one increases the number of grid points (i.e. the
polynomial order). This problem is avoided by using suitable grids that are not
equispaced. A prominent example is
Chebyshev interpolation
, which is
described in detail in
[
17
]
.
This was indeed used in the context of Levin’s method in
[
14
,
15
]
.

Motivated by our experience with interpolating (single and double) parton
distributions on Chebyshev grids
[
18
,
19
]
, we adopt
Chebyshev interpolation to our present case. Our method involves three steps.

Variable transform.

We first define a transformation from
z
to the variable
u
that will be used
for interpolation with Chebyshev polynomials. The values
u_{a}=u(z_{a})
and

u_{b}=u(z_{b})
must both be finite. We require
u(z)
to be an analytic
function with
du/dz>0
for all
z\in[z_{a},z_{b}]
, except for the point

z_{b}=\infty
, where necessarily
du/dz=0
.

A primary purpose of this transformation is to map an infinite integration
interval
[z_{a},\infty]
onto a finite interval in
u
. On a finite interval in

z
, one may take the trivial transformation
u=z
. However, even in this
case a non-trivial variable transformation can be beneficial, for instance when

f_{i}(z)
rises or decreases very steeply.

Depending on the behaviour of
f_{i}(z)
, different variable transformations

u(z)
lead to good results of our method. A selection of such transformations
is presented in section
3.2.2
.

Subintervals.

We find that it is often useful to split
[z_{a},z_{b}]
into
k
subintervals

[z_{0},z_{1}]
,
[z_{1},z_{2}]
, …,
[z_{k-1},z_{k}]
with
z_{0}=z_{a}
and

z_{k}=z_{b}
and to evaluate the integral on each of them separately. We
will see that this benefits both the integration accuracy and the computation
time. Following the notation introduced in
[
18
,
19
]
,
we write

\displaystyle[z_{0},z_{1},\ldots,z_{k}]_{(n_{1},\ldots,n_{k})}

(3.16)

to characterise a grid and its subgrids, where
z_{j}
are the interval
boundaries, and
n_{j}
is the number of points on each subgrid. The total number
of grid points is then
\sum_{j=1}^{k}n_{j}-(k-1)
, because adjacent subgrids
share their end points. In the notation (
3.16
) it is understood
that one and the same variable transform is used on the full interval from
z_{0}

to
z_{k}
.

For simplicity, we will continue to present our method for a single integration
interval
[z_{a},z_{b}]
, bearing in mind that this may be a subinterval in the
sense just described.

Chebyshev interpolation.

On each subinterval, we approximate the functions
h_{1}(z,q)
and
h_{3}(z,q)

using Chebyshev interpolation. A compendium of this method with the formulae
needed for our method is given in appendix
C
. In brief, we
approximate

\displaystyle h_{i}(z,q)

\displaystyle\approx p_{i}\bigl(u(z),q\bigr)\,,

(3.17)

where
p_{i}(u,q)
is the unique polynomial of order
N
that reproduces the
function

\displaystyle h_{i}(z_{j},q)

\displaystyle=p_{i}\bigl(u_{j},q\bigr)\,,

\displaystyle u_{j}

\displaystyle=u(z_{j})

(3.18)

at the
n=N+1
points
z_{j}
that correspond to the Chebyshev points
u_{j}
on
the interval
[u_{a},u_{b}]
, with
u_{j}
given in (
C.11
). Note that
these points include the interval boundaries,
z_{a}=z_{0}
and
z_{b}=z_{N}
,
where the functions
h_{i}
are required for evaluating the Fourier-Bessel
integral with the master formula (
3.3
) of our method.

The derivatives of
dh_{i}/dz
appearing in the Levin equations
(
3.4
) can be approximated by the derivatives of the interpolants
dp_{i}/dz
. When evaluating the equations at the points
z_{j}
, these derivatives
can be evaluated via a matrix multiplication. In summary, we discretise
(
3.4
) by making the following replacements:

\displaystyle f_{i}(z)

\displaystyle\to f_{i}(z_{j})

\displaystyle(i=1,2)\,,

\displaystyle h_{i}(z,q)

\displaystyle\to p_{i}(u_{j},q)

\displaystyle(i=1,3)\,,

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dz}\mskip 1.5muh_{i}(z,q)

\displaystyle\to\frac{du}{dz}(z_{j})\sum_{k}D^{u}_{jk}\,p_{i}(u_{k},q)

\displaystyle(i=1,3)\,,

(3.19)

where
D^{u}_{jk}
is given in (
C.12
) and
du/dz
is the Jacobian of the variable transform.
The Levin equations are thus replaced by a system of linear equations, which can
be written as

\displaystyle B(q)\cdot\boldsymbol{P}(q)

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{F}

(3.20)

with the
2n
-dimensional vectors

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{F}

\displaystyle=\bigl(f_{1}(z_{0}),f_{1}(z_{1}),\ldots,f_{1}(z_{N}),\;f_{2}(z_{0}),f_{2}(z_{1}),\ldots,f_{2}(z_{N})\bigr)^{T}\,,

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{P}(q)

\displaystyle=\bigl(p_{1}(z_{0},q),p_{1}(z_{1},q),\ldots,p_{1}(z_{N},q),\;p_{3}(z_{0},q),p_{3}(z_{1},q),\ldots,p_{3}(z_{N},q)\bigr)^{T}

(3.21)

and the
2n
-dimensional square matrix

\displaystyle B(q)

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}B^{aa}&B^{ab}(q)\\
B^{ba}(q)&B^{bb}\end{pmatrix}

(3.22)

with diagonal blocks

\displaystyle B^{aa}_{jk}

\displaystyle=\frac{du}{dz}(z_{j})\,D^{u}_{jk}+\frac{\nu}{1+z_{j}}\,\delta_{jk}\,,

\displaystyle B^{bb}_{jk}

\displaystyle=\frac{z_{j}}{1+z_{j}}\,\frac{du}{dz}(z_{j})\,D^{u}_{jk}-\biggl[\mskip 1.5mu\frac{\nu-1}{(1+z_{j})^{2}}+\frac{\nu+1}{1+z_{j}}\mskip 1.5mu\biggr]\mskip 1.5mu\delta_{jk}

(3.23)

and off-diagonal blocks

\displaystyle B^{ab}_{jk}(q)

\displaystyle=q\,\frac{z_{j}}{1+z_{j}}\,\delta_{jk}\,,

\displaystyle B^{ba}_{jk}(q)

\displaystyle=-q\mskip 1.5mu\delta_{jk}\,,

(3.24)

where
j
and
k
run from
0
to
N
in all cases.

Note that up to this point, the only approximation we have made was to replace
the derivatives of
h_{i}
at the points
z_{j}
by the derivatives of their
approximants
p_{i}
.
If we use the integration-by-parts identity (
3.5
), then we also
invoke Chebyshev interpolation to approximately compute the derivative

df_{0}/dz
in the definition (
3.6
) of
f_{1}
. The values of
f_{1}

at the grid points are thus obtained from the corresponding values of
f_{0}
as

\displaystyle f_{1}(z_{j})

\displaystyle\approx\sum_{k}C_{jk}\,f_{0}(z_{k})\,,

\displaystyle C_{jk}

\displaystyle=\frac{z_{j}}{1+z_{j}}\mskip 1.5mu\frac{du}{dz}(z_{j})D^{u}_{jk}-\biggl[\frac{\nu-1}{(1+z_{i})^{2}}+\frac{\nu}{1+z_{i}}\biggr]\mskip 1.5mu\delta_{jk}\,.

(3.25)

In section
3.1
we investigated in detail how the
small-
z
behaviour of
h_{1}
and
h_{3}
is related to the small-
z
behaviour of

f_{1}
or
f_{2}
. Since we require the variable transformation between
u
and

to be analytic and to have a finite derivative at
z=0
, the interpolation
polynomials
p_{i}\bigl(u(z),q\bigr)
in

have a Taylor expansion around

z=0
. To obtain a good approximation when discretising the Levin equations, we
hence need that
h_{1}
and
h_{3}
can be sufficiently well approximated by a
Taylor series around
z=0
, with the first
n
Taylor coefficients being free
and the remaining ones fixed by the variable transform.

A corresponding statement holds for the small-
z
behaviour of
f_{0}(z)
when
using integration by parts. Notice that if one has
f_{0}\sim z^{1-\delta}

with
0<\delta<1
, one gets a divergent first derivative of
f_{0}
at
z=0
, which is however multiplied with
z
in (
3.6
) and thus can be
accommodated within our setup.

Using quadrature.

We will see that the matrix
B
in (
3.20
) becomes ill-conditioned
when
q
becomes small. As described in the next subsection, this can in part
be dealt with by linear algebra methods. On the other hand, there is no
compelling need to use the Levin method when the integrand in
(
3.2
) is not rapidly oscillating in the integration interval

[z_{a},z_{b}]
. In our algorithm, we therefore replace the Levin method with
Clenshaw-Curtis quadrature if for a given

one has

\displaystyle qz_{b}

\displaystyle\leq j_{\nu}\,,

(3.26)

where
j_{\nu}
is the first zero of the Bessel function
J_{\nu}(x)
. We find that
this yields a high integration accuracy and also saves computing time. The
quadrature result is obtained as the sum

\displaystyle I(q)

\displaystyle\approx\sum_{j}w^{z}_{j}\;\biggl(\frac{1+z_{j}}{z_{j}}\biggr)^{\nu}\,\biggl[J_{\nu}(qz_{j})\,f_{1}(z_{j})+J_{\nu+1}(qz_{j})\,f_{2}(z_{j})\biggr]\,,

(3.27)

where
I(q)
is defined in (
3.2
) and the weights are given by

\displaystyle w^{z}_{j}

\displaystyle=\biggl(\frac{du}{dz}(z_{j})\biggr)^{-1}\;w^{u}_{j}

(3.28)

with

from (

3.2.1
Solving the linear equation system

The system (
3.20
) has a unique solution if the matrix
B
is
nonsingular. By taking increasingly fine interpolation grids, i.e. increasingly high order of the interpolation polynomials
p_{i}(u,q)
, one should
eventually approach the limit of the original Levin equations, which has an
infinity of solutions. One can hence expect that for sufficiently fine
interpolation grids, the matrix
B
is close to singular. This poses a
challenge to the numerical solution of (
3.20
).

The work in
[
14
,
15
]
contains a detailed analysis of the linear
equation system for a different set of integrals, along with a strategy for its
solution. We follow a rather similar strategy in the present work, selecting one
out of two algorithms to solve (
3.20
).

Our default algorithm uses the LU decomposition (see e.g.
[
20
, Chapter
2.3]
), which represents
B
in the form

\displaystyle B

\displaystyle=\Pi\cdot L\cdot U\,,

(3.29)

where
\Pi
is a permutation matrix,
L
is a lower diagonal matrix with all
diagonal elements equal to
1
, and
U
an upper diagonal matrix. The product
of the diagonal elements
U_{ii}
is equal to
\det(B)
, so that at least one
of these elements is zero when
B
is singular. If this is not the case, the
solution of
B\cdot\boldsymbol{P}=\boldsymbol{F}
is obtained by successively solving

\displaystyle L\cdot\boldsymbol{G}

\displaystyle=\Pi^{-1}\cdot\boldsymbol{F}\,,

\displaystyle U\cdot\boldsymbol{P}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{G}\,,

(3.30)

which can be done by forward and backward substitution, respectively. The
second step involves the inverse diagonal elements
1/U_{ii}
of
U
and
becomes numerically unstable when
B
is close to singular. As criterion for
this case, we use

\displaystyle\min_{i}\bigl(|U_{ii}|\bigr)

\displaystyle\;\leq\;r_{LU}^{\text{max}}\,\max_{i}\bigl(|U_{ii}|\bigr)\,,

(3.31)

where
r_{LU}^{\text{max}}
can be chosen by the user.

If (
3.31
) is satisfied for a given matrix
B
, we use the
singular value (SV) decomposition. In this case,
B
is represented as

\displaystyle B

\displaystyle=U\cdot S\cdot V^{T}\,,

(3.32)

where
U
and
V
are orthogonal
4
4

4

Note that
U
denotes different matrices in (
3.32
) and
(
3.29
).

and
S=\operatorname{diag}(S_{ii})
is diagonal and positive semidefinite.

For nonsingular
B
, the solution of
B\cdot\boldsymbol{P}=\boldsymbol{F}
is then
readily given by

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{P}

\displaystyle=V\cdot\operatorname{diag}(1/S_{ii})\cdot U^{T}\cdot\boldsymbol{F}\,.

(3.33)

If
B
is singular, then some of the
S_{ii}
vanish, and one replaces the
corresponding entries
1/S_{ii}
in (
3.33
) with zero. As shown
e.g. in
[
20
, Chapter 2.6]
, this yields an approximate
solution of (
3.20
) in the sense that
\boldsymbol{P}
obtained in this way
minimises
|B\cdot\boldsymbol{P}-\boldsymbol{F}|
, i.e. the distance between the
vectors on the two sides of the equation. The numerical stability of this
procedure is greatly enhanced by also replacing
1/S_{ii}
with zero when
S_{ii}
is very small; this is called a
truncated
SV decomposition. We
choose to replace an entry
1/S_{ii}
with zero if it satisfies

\displaystyle S_{ii}

\displaystyle\;<\;r_{SV}^{\text{max}}\,\max_{j}(S_{jj})\,,

(3.34)

with
r_{SV}^{\text{max}}
to be chosen by the user.

A numerical computation is typically much faster for the LU decomposition
(
3.29
) than for the SV decomposition (
3.32
). One should
hence take
r_{LU}^{\text{max}}
as small as can be done without compromising
the accuracy of the result. Our choice of the control parameters

r_{LU}^{\text{max}}
and
r_{SV}^{\text{max}}
is discussed in
section
4.1
.

The computation time for both the LU and the SV decomposition grows very quickly
with the dimension of the matrix
B
. From this point of view, it is beneficial
to split the overall integration region
[z_{a},z_{b}]
into subintervals, since
this allows one to work with smaller matrices
B_{k}
on each subinterval
k
.

3.3
Summary of the algorithm

Our complete algorithm naturally proceeds in several steps.

1.

The user must specify an interpolation grid, i.e. a variable transform

u(z)
, a subinterval division in
z
, and the number of grid points per
subinterval. Then

(a)

compute the grid points
z_{j}
,

(b)

evaluate the values at the grid points
for the function(s) to be integrated over, i.e. for
f_{1}(z),f_{2}(z)
or

f_{0}(z)
in (
3.2
) or (
3.5
),

(c)

perform steps
2
to
4
for each
subinterval and add up the integrals from these subintervals.

2.

For a given value of
\nu

(a)

compute the values of
\bigl[z_{j}/(1+z_{j})\bigr]^{\rho}
with

\rho=1
,
\nu
, and
\nu-1
for each grid point,

(b)

compute the diagonal blocks (
3.23
) of the matrix
B
, as
well as the matrix
C
in (
3.25
),

(c)

compute the Clenshaw-Curtis weights
w^{z}_{j}
in
(
3.28
).

We recall that for a given
\nu
, the method allows the computation of
integrals with
J_{\nu-1}(qz)
,
J_{\nu}(qz)
, and
J_{\nu+1}(qz)
.

3.

For a given value of
q

(a)

determine from (
3.26
) whether to use quadrature or
the Levin method,

(b)

compute the values of

\bigl[(1+z)/z\mskip 1.5mu\bigr]^{\nu}\,J_{\nu}(qz)
and

\bigl[(1+z)/z\mskip 1.5mu\bigr]^{\nu}\,J_{\nu+1}(qz)
, for all grid
points
z_{j}
if quadrature is used, and for the interval boundaries otherwise,

(c)

if the Levin method is used: compute the off-diagonal blocks
(
3.24
) of the matrix
B
,

(d)

if the Levin method is used: set up the
linear equation solver by computing the LU and — if needed — the SV
decomposition of
B
.

4.

For given values
f_{i}(z_{j})
on the interpolation grid (
i=0,1
, or
2
)

(a)

for integrals with
J_{\nu-1}(qz)\mskip 1.5mu
:
evaluate the boundary terms at
z_{a}
and
z_{b}
in (
3.5
) and compute

f_{1}(z_{j})
from
f_{0}(z_{j})
using (
3.25
),

(b)

if the Levin method is used: solve the matrix equation
(
3.20
),

(c)

evaluate the integral from the master formula (
3.3
)
or (
3.5
).

The time required for step
1b
crucially depends on the
functions to be integrated; an important feature of our method is that the
functions need not be evaluated separately for different values of
q
. The
evaluation of Bessel functions is limited to step
3b
and
required only once for a given
q
.

The operations in step
2
need to be done only once for a given
choice of
\nu
and the interpolation grid – they should be irrelevant for the
overall computing time in a typical workflow. We find step

3d
to be by far the most time consuming one. By
comparison, all operations in step
4
are fast.

Recalling the use cases mentioned in the introduction, we see that our
method (as well as the Levin method in its original form) is particularly
efficient for a workflow in which one needs the Fourier-Bessel transform of

many
similar functions at a given value of
q
. With functions of
sufficiently similar shape, one can take a common grid for discretisation. The
computationally expensive step
3d
has to be carried out
once for a given
q
, after which the much faster steps in
4

can be repeated for each discretised function in turn.

The methods of

5
Numerical studies for TMD-like functions

In this section, we explore in some detail how our method performs for integrals
that appear in TMD cross sections. Up to a global factor, such cross sections
are proportional to

\displaystyle I(q)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\!dz\,J_{0}(qz)\,z\mskip 1.5muW(z,Q)

(5.1)

where
W(z,Q)
is product of TMDs evolved to the scale
Q
. For brevity, we do
not display the additional dependence on longitudinal momentum fractions. We
will also consider the cumulative
q
spectrum

\displaystyle K(q)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int d^{2}\mskip-1.5mu\tilde{q}\;\theta(q-\tilde{q})\,I(\tilde{q})=\int_{0}^{q}d\tilde{q}\;\tilde{q}\,I(\tilde{q})=q\int_{0}^{\infty}\!dz\,J_{1}(qz)\,W(z,Q)\,.

(5.2)

Notice that the integrands in (
5.1
) and (
5.2
) have the
same behaviour at
z\to 0
.

We recall that
q
is a measured transverse momentum and
z
is a distance
between parton fields in the plane transverse to the collision axis in the
process. We will therefore give
q
in units of
\operatorname{GeV}
and
z
in units in

\operatorname{GeV}^{-1}
(using a system in which the speed of light is unity).

5.1
Integrands

In this subsection, we specify different forms of
W(z,Q)
used in our numerical
study. A reader not familiar with TMDs (but interested in Fourier-Bessel
transforms) may skip over the details, but should note two important features of
the integrand:
(i)
it is non-analytic at
z=0
, and
(ii)
its leading
behaviour at large
z
is either given by an exponential
\exp(-\kappa z)
or
by a Gaussian
\exp(-\lambda^{2}z^{2})
.

Our purpose is not a detailed phenomenological investigation of TMDs, but to
understand the interplay between their qualitative behaviour and our integration
algorithm. We therefore use leading-order evolution and tree-level
short-distance matching for the TMDs. For details and references, we refer to

[
1
, chapter 2]
. The product of two evolved TMDs
has the form

\displaystyle W(z,Q)

\displaystyle=\bigl[f_{\text{np}}(z)\bigr]^{2}\,\exp\bigl[-2S(z,Q)\bigr]\,,

(5.3)

where

\displaystyle S(z,Q)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mu_{z}}^{Q}\frac{d\mu}{\mu}\,\biggl[4C_{F}\,\frac{\alpha_{s}(\mu)}{2\pi}\,\ln\frac{Q}{\mu}-3C_{F}\,\frac{\alpha_{s}(\mu)}{2\pi}\biggr]

\displaystyle=\biggl[\,\frac{c_{1}}{\alpha_{s}(Q)}-c_{2}\,\biggr]\,\ln\frac{\alpha_{s}(\mu_{z})}{\alpha_{s}(Q)}-c_{1}\biggl[\,\frac{1}{\alpha_{s}(Q)}-\frac{1}{\alpha_{s}(\mu_{z})}\,\biggr]

(5.4)

with coefficients

\displaystyle c_{1}

\displaystyle=8\pi C_{F}/\beta_{0}^{2}\,,

\displaystyle c_{2}

\displaystyle=3C_{F}/\beta_{0}\,,

\displaystyle\beta_{0}

\displaystyle=11-2n_{f}/3\,,

\displaystyle C_{F}=4/3

(5.5)

and with one-loop running of the strong coupling:

\displaystyle\alpha_{s}(\mu)

\displaystyle=\frac{2\pi}{\beta_{0}\ln(\mu/\Lambda)}\,.

(5.6)

The scale parameter
\Lambda
is chosen such that
\alpha_{s}(M_{Z})=0.13
at the
mass of the
Z
boson. For simplicity, we take
n_{f}=5
flavours everywhere; a
more realistic treatment would not affect the qualitative behaviour of our
integrands.
The initial conditions for evolution are formulated at the scale

\displaystyle\mu_{z}

\displaystyle=b_{0}\mskip 1.5mu\sqrt{1/z^{2}+1/z^{2}_{\text{max}}\rule{0.0pt}{7.66385pt}}\,,

\displaystyle b_{0}

\displaystyle=2e^{-\gamma}\approx 1.12\,,

\displaystyle z_{\text{max}}

\displaystyle=0.5\operatorname{GeV}^{-1}\,.

(5.7)

For
z\to 0
, we have a non-analytic behaviour in
z
, namely

\displaystyle\exp\bigl[-2S(z,Q)\bigr]

\displaystyle\;\sim\;\biggl(\frac{z\Lambda}{b_{0}}\biggr)^{8C_{F}/\beta_{0}}\biggl(\ln\frac{b_{0}}{z\Lambda}\biggr)^{2c_{1}/\alpha_{s}(Q)}\approx\biggl(\frac{z\Lambda}{b_{0}}\biggr)^{1.4}\;\biggl(\ln\frac{b_{0}}{z\Lambda}\biggr)^{1.14/\alpha_{s}(Q)}\,.

(5.8)

Note that if
f_{\text{np}}(z)
is sufficiently regular at
z=0
, the function

z\mskip 1.5muW(z)
appearing in the integral (

) has a finite first
derivative at that point.

We performed all numerical studies for three values of

, namely

\displaystyle Q

\displaystyle=2\operatorname{GeV},\,20\operatorname{GeV},\,100\operatorname{GeV}\,,

(5.9)

with corresponding values
\alpha_{s}(2\operatorname{GeV})\approx 0.33
,
\alpha_{s}(20\operatorname{GeV})\approx 0.17
, and
\alpha_{s}(100\operatorname{GeV})\approx 0.13

\mu_{z}

\displaystyle=\cosh\biggl[\biggl(\frac{2c^{2}}{\kappa}-\frac{\kappa}{4}\biggr)z\,\biggr]\,\bigg/\cosh\biggl[\biggl(\frac{2c^{2}}{\kappa}+\frac{\kappa}{4}\biggr)z\,\biggr]\,,

\displaystyle\kappa

\displaystyle=0.642\operatorname{GeV}\,,

\displaystyle c

\displaystyle\lambda

6
Formally divergent integrals

In this section, we take a closer look at Fourier-Bessel integrals from
0
to

\infty
with integrands that do not decrease at large
z
. The integrals
we have considered so far are either absolutely convergent, or they can be
defined as

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}dz\,J_{\nu}(z)\,\tilde{f}(z)

\displaystyle=\lim_{z_{b}\to\infty}\int_{0}^{z_{b}}dz\,J_{\nu}(z)\,\tilde{f}(z)\,.

(6.1)

The situation is more complicated if
\tilde{f}(z)
grows like
\sqrt{z}
or faster
at large
z
. An example is the case
\nu=0
and
\tilde{f}(z)=z
, where

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{z_{b}}dz\,J_{0}(qz)\,z

\displaystyle=\frac{z_{b}}{q}\,J_{1}(qz_{b})

(6.2)

diverges at
z_{b}\to\infty
for any
q>0
, whereas the integral up to

\infty
can be defined as a distribution

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}dz\,J_{0}(qz)\,z

\displaystyle=\frac{\delta(q)}{q}=2\mskip 1.5mu\delta(q^{2})\,.

(6.3)

The latter relation can be obtained from the representation of a two-dimensional

\delta
distribution as the Fourier transform of a constant.

In the context of TMD studies, one encounters Fourier-Bessel transforms with
functions
\tilde{f}(z)
increasing even faster than
z
. Writing the fixed-order
perturbative cross section up to order
\alpha_{s}^{n}
as a Fourier-Bessel
integral, one gets an integrand of the form
J_{0}(qz)
times

\displaystyle\tilde{f}(z)

\displaystyle=z\sum_{k=0}^{2n}c_{k}\ln^{k}(zQ)

(6.4)

with coefficients
c_{k}
that are independent of
z
.
The corresponding integral can be defined in the sense of distributions, see

[
25
, appendix C]
for a systematic investigation and

[
26
, appendix B]
for an earlier reference.

As shown in the papers just quoted, the Fourier-Bessel transform of
(
6.4
) can be carried out analytically, but this is no longer true
for more complicated cases. For instance, the method of
[
4
]

involves a smooth interpolation between the form (
6.4
) at
q\sim Q
and an expression including all-order resummation at
q\ll Q
. In this
scheme, the function
\tilde{f}
depends on both
z
and
q
. It generically has a
rather complicated form, and numerical integration is required to compute its
Fourier-Bessel transform.

These considerations motivate us to extend our method to integrands that
increase with
z
. We do this for general
\nu\geq 0
, which presents only a
modest overhead compared with the case
\nu=0
required for the physics
application just sketched.

6.1
Defining the integral

In the following we consider functions that satisfy

\displaystyle z^{\nu}\,\Bigl(z\mskip 1.5mu\frac{d}{dz}\Bigr)^{k}\mskip 1.5mu\tilde{f}(z,q)

\displaystyle\;\underset{z\to 0}{\rightarrow}\;\mathit{const}\,,

\displaystyle z^{-3/2}\,\Bigl(z\mskip 1.5mu\frac{d}{dz}\Bigr)^{k}\,\tilde{f}(z,q)

\displaystyle\;\underset{z\to\infty}{\rightarrow}\;0

\displaystyle\qquad\text{for }k=0,1,2,

(6.5)

where it is understood that
\mathit{const}
may be finite or zero. If
\tilde{f}

depends on
q
, we also require that

\displaystyle z^{\nu}\mskip 1.5mu\frac{d}{dq}\mskip 1.5mu\tilde{f}(z,q)

\displaystyle\;\underset{z\to 0}{\rightarrow}\;\mathit{const}\,,

\displaystyle z^{-3/2}\,\frac{d}{dq}\mskip 1.5mu\tilde{f}(z,q)

\displaystyle\;\underset{z\to\infty}{\rightarrow}\;0

(6.6)

and

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dz}\mskip 1.5mu\frac{d}{dq}\,\tilde{f}(z,q)

\displaystyle=\frac{d}{dq}\mskip 1.5mu\frac{d}{dz}\,\tilde{f}(z,q)\,.

(6.7)

The integral of interest is

\displaystyle I(q)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}dz\,J_{\nu}(qz)\,\tilde{f}(z,q)

(6.8)

with
\nu\geq 0
. The relation (
A.2
) for Bessel functions of the
first kind implies

\displaystyle J_{\nu}(qz)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{z}\,\frac{1}{q^{\nu+1}}\,\frac{d}{dq}\,\bigl[q^{\nu+1}\,J_{\nu+1}(qz)\bigr]\,,

(6.9)

which gives

\displaystyle I(q)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{q^{\nu+1}}\int_{0}^{\infty}dz\,\frac{d}{dq}\,\Biggl[\mskip 1.5muq^{\nu+1}\,J_{\nu+1}(qz)\,\frac{\tilde{f}(z,q)}{z}\Biggr]-\int_{0}^{\infty}dz\,J_{\nu+1}(qz)\;\frac{d}{dq}\,\frac{\tilde{f}(z,q)}{z}

(6.10)

when inserted into (
6.8
). Interchanging differentiation and
integration, we get

\displaystyle I(q)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{q^{\nu+1}}\,\frac{d}{dq}\Biggl[\mskip 1.5muq^{\nu+1}\!\int_{0}^{\infty}dz\,J_{\nu+1}(qz)\,\frac{\tilde{f}(z,q)}{z}\Biggr]-\int_{0}^{\infty}dz\,J_{\nu+1}(qz)\;\frac{d}{dq}\,\frac{\tilde{f}(z,q)}{z}\,.

(6.11)

The conditions (
6.5
) and (
6.6
) guarantee the convergence of
(
6.11
), but not the one of (
6.8
). If
\tilde{f}
grows so
fast that the integral in (
6.8
) is divergent, then we
define

that integral by (
6.11
).

Obviously, (
6.11
) cannot be used at
q=0
. In
appendix
D
we show how (
6.11
) can be extended to
a distribution in
q
, with a plus-prescription and a
\delta
distribution at

q=0
. In the remainder of this section we consider the region
q>0
.

Let us rewrite (
6.11
) such that explicit differentiation w.r.t.
q
is not necessary. We start by inserting

\displaystyle J_{\nu+1}(qz)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{q}\,\frac{1}{z^{\nu+2}}\,\frac{d}{dz}\,\bigl[z^{\nu+2}\,J_{\nu+2}(qz)\bigr]

(6.12)

into (
6.11
) and use integration by parts in both terms. Together
with the limiting behaviour of the Bessel functions at small and large
z
, the
conditions (
6.5
) and (
6.6
) ensure that the boundary terms
vanish, so that we get

\displaystyle I(q)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{q^{\nu+1}}\,\frac{d}{dq}\Biggl[\,q^{\nu}\!\int_{0}^{\infty}dz\,J_{\nu+2}(qz)\,z^{\nu+2}\,\frac{d}{dz}\,\frac{\tilde{f}(z,q)}{z^{\nu+3}}\Biggr]

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1}{q}\int_{0}^{\infty}dz\,J_{\nu+2}(qz)\;z^{\nu+2}\frac{d}{dz}\,\frac{d}{dq}\,\frac{\tilde{f}(z,q)}{z^{\nu+3}}\,.

(6.13)

Using the product rule for the
q
derivative in the first term, we get

\displaystyle I(q)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{q^{\nu+1}}\int_{0}^{\infty}dz\;\frac{d}{dq}\biggl[q^{\nu}J_{\nu+2}(qz)\biggr]\,z^{\nu+2}\,\frac{d}{dz}\,\frac{\tilde{f}(z,q)}{z^{\nu+3}}\,,

(6.14)

where we have used the condition (
6.7
). Remarkably, terms that
involve the
q
dependence of
\tilde{f}
have disappeared. In this sense, our
procedure can be regarded as local in
q
.
The two relations in (
A.1
) imply

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dq}\,J_{\nu+2}(qz)

\displaystyle=zJ_{\nu+1}(qz)-\frac{\nu+2}{q}\,J_{\nu+2}(qz)\,,

(6.15)

which allows us to replace the remaining
q
derivative in (
6.14
).
The result is

\displaystyle I(q)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{q^{2}}\int_{0}^{\infty}dz\,\bigl[2J_{\nu+2}(qz)-qz\mskip 1.5muJ_{\nu+1}(qz)\bigr]\,z^{\nu+2}\,\frac{d}{dz}\,\frac{\tilde{f}(z,q)}{z^{\nu+3}}\,.

(6.16)

We now use (
6.12
) and integrate by parts once more. The
boundary terms vanish again by virtue of (
6.5
) and (
6.6
),
so that

\displaystyle I(q)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{q^{2}}\int_{0}^{\infty}dz\,J_{\nu+2}(qz)\,z^{\nu+2}\;\frac{d}{dz}\biggl[\frac{2\mskip 1.5mu\tilde{f}(z,q)}{z^{\nu+3}}+z\,\frac{d}{dz}\,\frac{\tilde{f}(z,q)}{z^{\nu+3}}\biggr]\,.

(6.17)

This can be brought into the standard form (
3.2
),

\displaystyle I(q)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{q^{2}}\int_{0}^{\infty}dz\,J_{\nu+2}(qz)\,\biggl(\frac{1+z}{z}\biggr)^{\nu+2}f_{1}(z)

(6.18)

with

\displaystyle f_{1}(z)

\displaystyle=\frac{z^{\nu}}{(1+z)^{\nu+2}}\,\biggl[z^{2}\frac{d^{2}}{dz^{2}}\,\tilde{f}(z)-(2\nu+3)\,z\mskip 1.5mu\frac{d}{dz}\,\tilde{f}(z)+(\nu+3)(\nu+1)\,\tilde{f}(z)\biggr]\,,

(6.19)

where from now on we drop the
q
dependence of
\tilde{f}
again, as we do in the
rest of this paper. With the conditions in (
6.5
), the function

f_{1}
is finite or zero at the integration boundaries, so that the integral
(
6.18
) exists in the usual sense and can be evaluated using our
adaptation of Levin’s method in (
3.2
) and
(
3.3
).

Discretisation.

For discretising the integrand, we introduce the scaled function

\displaystyle g(z)

\displaystyle=\biggl(\frac{z}{1+z}\biggr)^{\nu}\,\;\frac{\tilde{f}(z)}{(1+z)^{2}}\,,

(6.20)

which according to (
6.5
) is finite or zero at the integration
boundaries. Inserting this into (
6.19
), we get

\displaystyle f_{1}(z)

\displaystyle=\Bigl(z\mskip 1.5mu\frac{d}{dz}\Bigr)^{2}\;g(z)-2\,\biggl[\nu+\frac{\nu+2}{1+z}\biggr]\,z\mskip 1.5mu\frac{d}{dz}\,g(z)

\displaystyle\quad+\biggl[\nu^{2}-1+\frac{(2\nu+1)(\nu+2)}{1+z}+\frac{(\nu+1)(\nu+2)}{(1+z)^{2}}\biggr]\,\,g(z)\,.

(6.21)

For the variable transform from
z
to
u
used for discretisation, we require
that

\displaystyle z\,\frac{du}{dz}

\displaystyle\,\underset{z\to\infty}{\longrightarrow}\,0\,,

(6.22)

which is fulfilled for all cases in table
1
. This allows us to
approximate the derivatives in (
6.21
) using Chebyshev interpolation:

\displaystyle z\,\frac{d}{dz}\,g(z)\,\biggr|_{z=z_{j}}

\displaystyle\approx\sum_{k}D^{(1)}_{jk}\;g(z_{k})\,,

\displaystyle\biggl(z\mskip 1.5mu\frac{d}{dz}\biggr)^{2}\;g(z)\,\biggr|_{z=z_{j}}

\displaystyle\approx\sum_{k}D^{(2)}_{jk}\;g(z_{k})\,,

(6.23)

with

\displaystyle D^{(1)}_{jk}

\displaystyle=z_{j}\,\frac{du}{dz}(z_{j})\,D^{u}_{jk}\,,

\displaystyle D^{(2)}_{jk}

\displaystyle=\sum_{l}D^{(1)}_{jl}\,D^{(1)}_{lk}\,.

(6.24)

In close analogy with (
3.25
), we can thus use
(
6.21
) to compute
f_{1}(z_{j})
by multiplying
g(z_{k})
with a matrix.
This matrix depends only on
\nu
and the interpolation grid and can hence be
precomputed.

Generalisation.

We note that using the relation

\displaystyle J_{\nu}(qz)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{z^{j}}\,\frac{1}{q^{\nu}}\biggl(\frac{1}{q}\,\frac{d}{dq}\biggr)^{j}\bigl[q^{\nu+j}\,J_{\nu+j}(qz)\bigr]

(6.25)

with a suitable value of
j
, it is straightforward to generalise the previous
method to functions
\tilde{f}
growing like
z^{3/2}
or faster as
z\to\infty
.
We checked explicitly that with
j=2
and two steps of integration by parts, one
obtains a representation of (
6.8
) as an integral over
J_{\nu+3}(qz)
times a function depending on
(d/dz)^{k}\,\tilde{f}
with
k=0,1,2,3
.

6.2
Numerical accuracy

The evaluation of the integral (
6.18
) using the discretised
version of (
6.20
) and (
6.21
) is implemented as a separate
class for “special integrals” in
BestLime
. The integration is performed using
the
J_{\nu}
call of the standard Bessel integrator of the library.

The timing of integration calls for the special integrator agrees within
10\%

with the timing of
J_{\nu-1}
calls for the standard integrator. This is
plausible, because in both cases the discretised integrand is multiplied by a
matrix for differentiation, before being passed to the algorithm that implements
Levin’s method.

Let us investigate the accuracy of our method for the functions
\tilde{f}(z)=z\ln^{k}(z^{2}\mskip 1.5muQ^{2}/b_{0}^{2})
, which form a basis of the fixed-order perturbative
expression (
6.4
). The constant
b_{0}
is defined in
(
5.7
) and gives a simpler form of the Fourier-Bessel transform. The
exact result for
I(q)
at
q>0
is readily obtained by evaluating
(
6.11
) analytically, which can be done using

\displaystyle(\ln z)^{k}

\displaystyle=(d/dz)^{k}\,z^{\mu}\,\bigr|_{\mu=0}

(6.26)

and

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}dz\;J_{\nu+1}(qz)\,z^{\mu}

\displaystyle=\frac{\Gamma\bigl(\frac{\nu}{2}+\frac{\mu}{2}+1\bigr)}{\Gamma\bigl(\frac{\nu}{2}-\frac{\mu}{2}+1\bigr)}\,\frac{2^{\mu}}{q^{\mu+1}}\,.

(6.27)

Explicit results can also be found in
[
25
, appendix C]
. In the
following, we set the hard scale to
Q=100\operatorname{GeV}
.

We find that for
k\leq 4
, quite good integration accuracy can be achieved
with the
inv pow
transformation that we already used for the benchmark
integrals in section
4.2
. For higher powers of the logarithm,
the results become however increasingly inaccurate unless one takes a very large
number of grid points. A much better performance for all
k
is obtained with
the
log pow
transformation in table
1
.
For
\nu=0
and
k
up to
8
(corresponding to a perturbative calculation up
to
\alpha_{s}^{4}
in the TMD context), we take a

\displaystyle\text{{log pow} grid with }[0,0.05,\infty]_{(n_{1},n_{2})}\,,

\displaystyle\alpha=0.2,\quad z_{\text{lo}}=10^{-8},\quad z_{\text{hi}}=0.1

7
Summary

We have presented a method to compute Fourier-Bessel transforms,
\int dz\,J_{\nu}(qz)\,\tilde{f}(z)
, with
z
ranging from
0
to
\infty
or being
restricted to a smaller interval. The method works for general index
\nu\geq 0
of the Bessel function. The function
\tilde{f}(z)
may be non-analytic at

z=0
(details are given in section
3.1
), and for

z\to\infty
it should decrease or tend to a constant.

Our method is a modification of an algorithm by Levin
[
13
]
. For

\nu\geq 1
the integration problem is transformed to the system of ODEs given
in (
3.4
), and integration by parts provides access to Bessel
functions of order
\nu<1
as specified in (
3.5
). The system of ODEs is
solved by collocation, with Chebyshev polynomials in a transformed variable

u(z)
as basis functions. The resulting matrix equation is handled with
standard linear algebra methods. The derivative required for integration
by parts is evaluated by approximating
\tilde{f}(z)
with the same Chebyshev
polynomials. As a complement, we use Clenshaw-Curtis
quadrature in
z
intervals where
qz
does not exceed the first zero of

J_{\nu}(x)
. This avoids most of the cases in which this matrix equation is
poorly conditioned, so that its solution would require a numerically expensive
singular value decomposition.
The details of our algorithm are laid out in section
3.3
and
implemented in the C++ library
BestLime
, which can be downloaded from

[
16
]
.

The method requires a number of presets to be decided by the user. The first
one is a variable transform
u(z)
that maps the integration interval in
z

onto a finite interval in
u
and should be adapted to the large-
z
behaviour
of
\tilde{f}(z)
. The
z
-grid on which
\tilde{f}(z)
must be evaluated is then
specified by the number of points, with the possibility of dividing the total
integration range into several subintervals. As we have shown, such a
subdivision is beneficial for both the accuracy and the computational cost of
integration. This set of choices is admittedly more involved than for other
methods (the Ogata method
[
5
]
for instance requires a step
parameter
h
and the total number
N
of function evaluations). On the other
hand, we found that with about
50
to
80
grid points, a high integration
accuracy can be reached for a wide range of grid parameters and over a wide
range of
q
, so that a detailed tuning of the grid settings should in general
not be necessary. Settings that we found to be satisfactory are given in
section
4.2
for a wide class of functions, and in
equations (
5.15
) to (
5.17
) for integrands
appearing in TMD cross sections.

In fixed-order perturbative TMD calculations one encounters functions

\tilde{f}(z)
that grow with
z
, such that the corresponding Fourier-Bessel
integrals do not converge. As shown in section
6
and
appendix
D
, these integrals can be defined in the
distributional sense, and their regular parts at
q>0
can be rewritten such
that they can be evaluated numerically with our method. We obtain good accuracy
for
\tilde{f}(z)=z\,\ln^{k}\!z
with
k
up to
8
, which is the highest power
appearing at order
\alpha_{s}^{4}
.

As explained in section
3.3
, our method is most efficient
for computing Bessel-weighted integrals of several
functions
\tilde{f}(z)
at the same value of
q
. Changing the value of
q

requires somewhat more computation time for linear algebra manipulations, but
does
not
necessitate a new evaluation of the function(s)
\tilde{f}(z)
,
since the grid in
z
does not depend on
q
. This presents an important
advantage if the function
\tilde{f}(z)
is expensive to compute. In an ongoing
separate project, we found that computing two-fold Fourier-Bessel transforms

\int dz_{1}\int dz_{2}\,J_{\nu_{1}}(q_{1}z_{1})\,J_{\nu_{2}}(q_{2}\mskip 1.5muz_{2})\,\tilde{f}(z_{1},z_{2})
can be done accurately and efficiently with the implementation
of our method in
BestLime
.

Appendix A
Useful relations for Bessel and Lommel functions

In this appendix, we collect results about Bessel and Lommel functions that are
used in the main body of our work.

A.1
Bessel functions

The properties of Bessel functions are well documented in the literature. The
following relations can for instance be found in

[
27
,
Chapter 10
]
.

Relations.

Functions of different order are related by

\displaystyle Z_{\nu}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{x}{2\nu}\,\Bigl[Z_{\nu-1}(x)+Z_{\nu+1}(x)\Bigr]\,,

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dx}\,Z_{\nu}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\,\Bigl[Z_{\nu-1}(x)-Z_{\nu+1}(x)\Bigr]\,,

(A.1)

which implies

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dx}\mskip 1.5mu\Bigl[x^{\nu}Z_{\nu}(x)\Bigr]

\displaystyle=x^{\nu}\,Z_{\nu-1}(x)

(A.2)

and

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dx}\mskip 1.5mu\Bigl[x^{-\nu}Z_{\nu}(x)\Bigr]

\displaystyle=-x^{-\nu}\,Z_{\nu+1}(x)\,,

(A.3)

where
Z_{\nu}
stands for
J_{\nu}
or
Y_{\nu}
, i.e. for the Bessel
functions of the first or the second kind. The two sets of functions are
related by

\displaystyle Y_{\nu}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{\cos(\nu\pi)\,J_{\nu}(x)-J_{-\nu}(x)}{\sin(\nu\pi)}

(A.4)

for non-integer
\nu
. For
n\in\mathbb{Z}
, one can take the limit
\nu\to n
of the r.h.s. to define
Y_{n}
.

Behaviour at large arguments.

The leading asymptotic behaviour for
x\to\infty
of
J_{\nu}(x)
and
Y_{\nu}(x)

consists of oscillations and a global decrease like
x^{-1/2}
, namely

\displaystyle J_{\nu}(x)

\displaystyle\sim\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi x}}\,\cos\Bigl(x-\nu\mskip 1.5mu\frac{\pi}{2}-\frac{\pi}{4}\Bigr)\,,

\displaystyle Y_{\nu}(x)

\displaystyle\sim\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi x}}\,\sin\Bigl(x-\nu\mskip 1.5mu\frac{\pi}{2}-\frac{\pi}{4}\Bigr)\,.

(A.5)

Series representations.

For
\nu\geq 0
one has

\displaystyle x^{\nu}\mskip 1.5muJ_{\nu}(x)

\displaystyle=x^{2\nu}\,\sigma(x^{2})\,,

\displaystyle x^{\nu}\,Y_{\nu}(x)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\sigma(x^{2})+x^{2\nu}\mskip 1.5mu\ln(x)\,\sigma(x^{2})&\text{ for }\nu\in\mathbb{Z}\,,\\
\sigma(x^{2})+x^{2\nu}\,\sigma(x^{2})&\text{ for }\nu\notin\mathbb{Z}\,,\end{cases}

(A.6)

where we use our generic notation
\sigma(x^{2})
for a power series in
x^{2}
,
explained after equation (
2.20
).

Fourier-Bessel transform.

The Fourier-Bessel transform of order
\nu

\displaystyle I(q)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}dz\mskip 1.5muz\,J_{\nu}(qz)\,W(z)

(A.7)

is its own inverse:

\displaystyle W(z)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}dq\mskip 1.5muq\,J_{\nu}(qz)\,I(q)\,.

(A.8)

This can be expressed in terms of the closure relation

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}dq\mskip 1.5muq\,J_{\nu}(qy)\,J_{\nu}(qz)

\displaystyle=\frac{\delta(y-z)}{z}\,.

(A.9)

Equations (
A.7
) to (
A.9
) require that
\nu\geq-1/2
.

A.2
Lommel functions

The literature about Lommel functions is much more sparse than for Bessel
functions. This holds especially for the Lommel functions of the second kind,
which are relevant for the discussion in section
2.3
. We
therefore provide some more detail here and in particular derive a
representation in terms of power series.
Unless explicitly mentioned, the following relations can be found in

[
27
,
Chapter 11.9
]
or in

[
28
, Chapter 8.57]
.

The Lommel functions of the first and second kind,
s_{\mu,\nu}(x)
and

S_{\mu,\nu}(x)
, solve the second-order differential equation

\displaystyle x^{2}\,w^{\prime\prime}(x)+x\,w^{\prime}(x)+\bigl[x^{2}-\nu^{2}\bigr]\,w(x)

\displaystyle=x^{\mu+1}\,.

(A.10)

The Lommel functions of first kind are given in terms or a power series as

\displaystyle s_{\mu,\nu}(x)

\displaystyle=x^{\mu+1}\,\sigma(x^{2})

(A.11)

and oscillate like the Bessel functions for large
x
. For the Lommel functions
of the second kind one has

\displaystyle S_{\mu,\nu}(x)

\displaystyle=s_{\mu,\nu}(x)+2^{\mu-1}\,\Gamma\biggl(\frac{\mu+\nu+1}{2}\biggr)\,\Gamma\biggl(\frac{\mu-\nu+1}{2}\biggr)

\displaystyle\quad\times\biggl[\sin\Bigl(\mskip-1.5mu(\mu-\nu)\frac{\pi}{2}\,\Bigr)\,J_{\nu}(x)-\cos\Bigl(\mskip-1.5mu(\mu-\nu)\frac{\pi}{2}\,\Bigr)\,Y_{\nu}(x)\biggr]\,.

(A.12)

If
\mu+\nu
or
\mu-\nu
is a negative odd integer, several coefficients of
the power series in (
A.11
) become infinite, as do the
\Gamma

functions in (
A.12
). The function
S_{\mu,\nu}
can be defined
by a limiting procedure in these cases, as shown in
[
29
, Chapter
3.4]
or
[
30
, Chapter 10.73]
.

Relations.

The Lommel functions of the second kind satisfy

\displaystyle S_{\mu,\nu}(x)

\displaystyle=S_{\mu,-\nu}(x)

(A.13)

and

\displaystyle S_{\mu,\nu}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{x}{2\nu}\,\Bigl[(\mu+\nu-1)\,S_{\mu-1,\nu-1}(x)-(\mu-\nu-1)\,S_{\mu-1,\nu+1}(x)\Bigr]\,,

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dx}\,S_{\mu,\nu}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\,\Bigl[(\mu+\nu-1)\,S_{\mu-1,\nu-1}(x)+(\mu-\nu-1)\,S_{\mu-1,\nu+1}(x)\Bigr]\,,

(A.14)

which implies

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dx}\mskip 1.5mu\Bigl[x^{\nu}S_{\mu,\nu}(x)\Bigr]

\displaystyle=(\mu+\nu-1)\,x^{\nu}\,S_{\mu-1,\nu-1}(x)\,.

(A.15)

Notice the close analogy of these relations with (
A.1
) and
(
A.2
).
The identity

\displaystyle S_{\mu+2,\nu}(x)

\displaystyle=x^{\mu+1}-(\mu+\nu+1)(\mu-\nu+1)\,S_{\mu,\nu}(x)

(A.16)

implies that

\displaystyle S_{\nu+1,\nu}(x)

\displaystyle=x^{\nu}\,,

\displaystyle S_{-\nu+1,\nu}(x)

\displaystyle=x^{-\nu}\,,

(A.17)

and by recursion

\displaystyle x^{-\nu}\,S_{\nu+1+2k,\nu}(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{k}c_{\nu,\mskip 1.5muk,\mskip 1.5muj}\,x^{2j}\,,

\displaystyle x^{\nu}\,S_{-\nu+1+2k,\nu}(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{k}c_{-\nu,\mskip 1.5muk,\mskip 1.5muj}\,x^{2j}

(A.18)

for
k\in\mathbb{N}
, with coefficients
c_{\nu,\mskip 1.5muk,\mskip 1.5muj}
whose explicit
form we do not need.

Behaviour at large arguments.

For
x\to\infty
, the Lommel functions of the second kind have an asymptotic
expansion

\displaystyle x^{\nu}\mskip 1.5muS_{\mu,\nu}(x)

\displaystyle\sim x^{\mu+\nu-1}\,\sigma(x^{-2})\,,

(A.19)

where the first term in the series
\sigma
is equal to
1
. This expansion is
also valid for the special cases mentioned below equation (
A.12
).

Series representation.

As in the case of Bessel functions, we assume
\nu\geq 0
. Combining
(
A.6
), (
A.11
), and (
A.12
),
one obtains a series representation

\displaystyle x^{\nu}\mskip 1.5muS_{\mu,\nu}(x)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\sigma(x^{2})+x^{\mu+\nu+1}\,\sigma(x^{2})+x^{2\nu}\mskip 1.5mu\ln(x)\,\sigma(x^{2})&\text{ for }\nu\in\mathbb{Z}\,,\\
\sigma(x^{2})+x^{\mu+\nu+1}\,\sigma(x^{2})+x^{2\nu}\,\sigma(x^{2})&\text{ for }\nu\notin\mathbb{Z}\,,\end{cases}

(A.20)

provided that neither
\mu+\nu
nor
\mu-\nu
is a negative odd integer.
For these exceptional values of
\mu
and
\nu
, we need separate derivations.
We limit ourselves to values satisfying

\displaystyle\mu+\nu>-1\,,

(A.21)

which is sufficient for the discussion in section
2.3
.
This leaves us with the possibility that
\mu-\nu
is a negative odd integer.
We then can distinguish two cases:

•

\nu\in\mathbb{Z}
. Then
\mu+\nu
is a positive odd integer, because
we impose (
A.21
). We can therefore use the second
representation in (
A.18
) and find that
x^{\nu}\,S_{\mu,\nu}(x)
is a polynomial in
x^{2}
.

•

\nu\notin\mathbb{Z}
. In this case we can use a representation derived
in
[
29
, Chapter 3.4]
and also in
[
30
, Chapter 10.73]
.
It has the form

\displaystyle x^{\nu}\mskip 1.5muS_{\nu-1,\nu}(x)

\displaystyle=x^{2\nu}\mskip 1.5mu\sigma(x^{2})+2^{\nu-1}\mskip 1.5mu\Gamma(\nu)\biggl[x^{\nu}J_{\nu}(x)\,\ln\mskip-1.5mu\frac{x}{2}-\frac{\pi}{2}\,x^{\nu}\mskip 1.5muY_{\nu}(x)\biggr]

\displaystyle=\sigma(x^{2})+x^{2\nu}\,\sigma(x^{2})+x^{2\nu}\mskip 1.5mu\ln(x)\,\sigma(x^{2})

(A.22)

and is valid for all
\nu>0
. Starting from this, we can repeatedly apply the
recursion relation (
A.16
) to obtain

\displaystyle x^{\nu}\mskip 1.5muS_{\mu=\nu-1-2k,\mskip 1.5mu\nu}(x)

\displaystyle=d_{\mu,\nu}\,x^{\nu}\,S_{\nu-1,\nu}(x)+x^{\mu+\nu+1}\mskip 1.5mu\sigma(x^{2})

\displaystyle\text{for }k=1,2,3,\ldots,

(A.23)

where
d_{\mu,\nu}
is some number and the series multiplying
x^{\mu+\nu+1}
is in fact a polynomial.

Putting everything together, we find that one can always write

\displaystyle x^{\nu}\mskip 1.5muS_{\mu,\nu}(x)

\displaystyle=\sigma(x^{2})+x^{\mu+\nu+1}\,\sigma(x^{2})+x^{2\nu}\,\sigma(x^{2})+x^{2\nu}\mskip 1.5mu\ln(x)\,\sigma(x^{2})

(A.24)

for
\mu+\nu>-1
and
\nu\geq 0
. If
\mu+\nu
is a positive odd integer,
then the r.h.s. reduces to a polynomial in
x^{2}
. Otherwise, the term with

\ln(x)
is present if
\nu
is an integer or if
\nu-\mu
is a negative odd
integer.

Appendix C
Chebyshev interpolation

In this appendix, we collect formulae for Chebyshev interpolation that are
relevant in our method. We use the same notation as in
[
18
,
19
]
, where references or derivations for the following results can be
found.

We start with functions defined on the interval
[-1,1]
, as is customary in
the discussion of Chebyshev polynomials. The generalisation to any other finite
interval is trivial and given at the end of this appendix.

Functions with support
t\in[-1,1]
.

The Chebyshev polynomials of the first kind,
T_{k}(t)
, can be defined by

\displaystyle T_{k}(\cos\theta)

\displaystyle=\cos(k\mskip 1.5mu\theta)\,,

(C.1)

where
k\geq 0
is an integer. Using the multiple-angle formula for the cosine,
one readily verifies that
T_{k}(t)
is a polynomial in
t
of order
k
. The set
of all
T_{k}(t)
is orthonormal with respect to the integration measure

1/\sqrt{1-t^{2}}
.

For a given integer
N
, the
Chebyshev points
are given by

\displaystyle t_{j}

\displaystyle=\cos\theta_{j}\,,

\displaystyle\theta_{j}

\displaystyle=\frac{j\pi}{N}

\displaystyle\text{ with }j=0,\ldots,N\,.

(C.2)

They form a descending series from
t_{0}=1
to
t_{N}=-1
and obey the
reflection property
t_{N-j}=-t_{j}
. The polynomial
T_{N}(t)
assumes its
maxima
+1
and minima
-1
at the Chebyshev points, as is easily seen from
(
C.1
). We note that the density of Chebyshev points increases from the
centre toward the end points of the interval
[-1,1]
. This feature is crucial
to avoid Runge’s phenomenon for equispaced interpolation grids.

The
Chebyshev interpolant

p_{N}(t)
of a function
f(t)
is the unique
polynomial that satisfies

\displaystyle p_{N}(t_{j})

\displaystyle=f(t_{j})

\displaystyle\text{ for }j=0,\ldots,N

(C.3)

at the Chebyshev points
t_{j}
. It can be written in the form

\displaystyle p_{N}(t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{N}\,\beta_{k}\mskip 1.5muc_{k}\mskip 1.5muT_{k}(t)

(C.4)

with coefficients

\displaystyle c_{k}

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{N}\sum_{j=0}^{N}\,\beta_{j}\mskip 1.5muT_{k}(t_{j})\,f(t_{j})\,,

(C.5)

where
\beta_{0}=\beta_{N}=1/2
and
\beta_{j}=1
otherwise.

For functions
f(t)
that are sufficiently regular in the interval
[-1,1]

(including the end points), one can show that the difference
|f(t)-p_{N}(t)|

uniformly tends to zero on the full interval in the limit
N\to\infty
. A
precise statement of this convergence theorem can be found in

[
18
, section 2]
. We note that a function is
not

sufficiently regular for the purpose of this theorem if its first derivative
diverges at an endpoint of the interval. This explains our insistence on a
finite first derivative of
h_{1}
and
h_{3}
in the discussion of
section
2.3
.

Differentiation.

Given the Chebyshev interpolant
p_{N}(t)
for a function
f(t)
, one can
approximate the derivative
f^{\prime}(t)=df/dt
of the function by the derivative

dp_{N}/dt
of its interpolant. Note that in general
df/dt
is not equal
to
dp_{N}/dt
at the Chebyshev points; this is obvious because one cannot
compute the exact values of
f^{\prime}(t_{j})
from the discrete set of function values

f(t_{j})
.
At the Chebyshev points, the derivative of the interpolating polynomial can be
computed as

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\mskip 1.5mup_{N}(t_{j})

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{N}\,D_{jk}\,f(t_{k})

(C.6)

with a differentiation matrix given by

\displaystyle D_{00}

\displaystyle=-D_{NN}=\frac{2N^{2}+1}{6}\,,

\displaystyle D_{jj}

\displaystyle=-\dfrac{\cos\theta_{j}}{2\sin^{2}\theta_{j}}

\displaystyle\text{for}\quad j\neq 0,N,

\displaystyle D_{jk}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{\beta_{k}}{\beta_{j}}\,\dfrac{(-1)^{j+k}}{t_{j}-t_{k}}

\displaystyle\text{for}\quad j\neq k\,.

(C.7)

Note that the matrix multiplication (
C.6
) maps a vector
f(t_{k})=\text{\it const}
onto the zero vector, as it must be because the derivative
of a constant function is zero.

Integration.

Approximating a function by its Chebyshev interpolant, one can derive an
integration rule

\displaystyle\int_{-1}^{1}dt\,f(t)

\displaystyle\approx\sum_{\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{k=0}{\text{even}}}^{N}\,\frac{2\mskip 1.5mu\beta_{k}\mskip 1.5muc_{k}}{1-k^{2}}=\sum_{j=0}^{N}\,w_{j}\mskip 1.5muf(t_{j})

(C.8)

with weights

\displaystyle w_{j}

\displaystyle=\frac{4\beta_{j}}{N}\,\sum_{\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{k=0}{\text{even}}}^{N}\,\beta_{k}\,\frac{\cos(k\mskip 1.5mu\theta_{j})}{1-k^{2}}\,.

(C.9)

This is known as
Clenshaw-Curtis quadrature
. A detailed discussion of
its accuracy, as well as a comparison with Gauss quadrature, is given in

[
17
, chapter 19]
and in
[
31
]
.

Functions with support
u\in[u_{a},u_{b}]
.

It is easy to generalise the preceding results to an arbitrary finite interval
in the variable
u
by introducing the linear transformation

\displaystyle u(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{u_{a}-u_{b}}{2}\,t+\frac{u_{a}+u_{b}}{2}\,.

(C.10)

With the Chebyshev points

\displaystyle u_{j}

\displaystyle=u(t_{j})=\frac{u_{a}-u_{b}}{2}\,\cos\frac{j\pi}{N}+\frac{u_{a}+u_{b}}{2}

(C.11)

in the new variable, we obtain the following rules for differentiation and for integration:

\displaystyle\frac{d}{du}\mskip 1.5muf(u_{j})

\displaystyle\approx\sum_{k=0}^{N}\,D^{u}_{jk}\,f(u_{k})\,,

\displaystyle D^{u}_{jk}

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{u_{a}-u_{b}}\,D_{jk}

(C.12)

with
D_{jk}
from (
C.7
) and

\displaystyle\int_{u_{a}}^{u_{b}}du\,f(u)

\displaystyle\approx\sum_{j=0}^{N}\,w^{u}_{j}\mskip 1.5muf(u_{j})\,,

\displaystyle w^{u}_{j}

\displaystyle=\frac{u_{b}-u_{a}}{2}\,w_{j}

(C.13)

with
w_{j}
from (
C.9
).

Appendix D
Fourier-Bessel integrals as distributions

In this appendix, we show how the formally divergent integrals discussed in
section
6
can be defined in the distributional sense.

For a given
\nu
and a function
g(q)
, we define the plus-distribution
[g(q)]_{+}^{q_{0}}
by its action on a test function, which we require to be of the
form
q^{\nu+1}\mskip 1.5mu\varphi(q)
:

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}dq\,q^{\nu+1}\mskip 1.5mu\varphi(q)\,\bigl[g(q)\bigr]_{+}^{q_{0}}=\int_{0}^{\infty}dq\,q^{\nu+1}\mskip 1.5mu\bigl[\varphi(q)-\theta(q_{0}-q)\mskip 1.5mu\varphi(0)\bigr]\mskip 1.5mu\,g(q)\,.

(D.1)

This can also be written as

\displaystyle\bigl[g(q)\bigr]_{+}^{q_{0}}

\displaystyle=\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\,\biggl[\theta(q-\epsilon)\,g(q)-\frac{\delta(q)}{q^{\nu+1}}\,\int_{\epsilon}^{q_{0}}d\tilde{q}\;\tilde{q}^{\nu+1}\mskip 1.5mug(\tilde{q})\biggr]\,,

(D.2)

where it is understood that the limit
\epsilon\to 0
should be taken

after
integrating over
q
with a test function.
Note that the factor
1/q^{\nu+1}
in (
D.2
) does not give rise to
singularities because we consider test functions proportional to
q^{\nu+1}
.
Alternatively, one can write
\delta(q)/q^{\nu+1}=(\nu+2)\,\delta(q^{\nu+2})
and integrate over
q^{\nu+2}
in (
D.1
).

If
g(q)
decreases faster than
1/q^{\nu+2}
at large
q
, then the upper
integration limit
q_{0}
in the subtraction term of (
D.1
) and
(
D.2
) can be set to
\infty
. Otherwise,
q_{0}
must be finite.

For
\nu=0
our definition is equivalent to the one in

[
25
, appendix C]
if the latter is applied to two-dimensional test
functions
\varphi(\smash{\vec{\mskip 0.5muq}}\mskip 1.5mu)
with azimuthal symmetry.

We now extend our previous definition (
6.11
) to the distributional
form

\displaystyle I(q)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}dz\,J_{\nu}(qz)\,\tilde{f}(z,q)

\displaystyle=\biggl[\frac{1}{q^{\nu+1}}\,\frac{d}{dq}\,K(q)\biggr]_{+}^{q_{0}}+\frac{\delta(q)}{q^{\nu+1}}\,K(q_{0})-\int_{0}^{\infty}dz\,J_{\nu+1}(qz)\;\frac{d}{dq}\,\frac{\tilde{f}(z,q)}{z}\,,

(D.3)

where

\displaystyle K(q)

\displaystyle=q^{\nu+1}\!\int_{0}^{\infty}dz\,J_{\nu+1}(qz)\;\frac{\tilde{f}(z,q)}{z}\,.

(D.4)

Using (
D.2
), we can rewrite (
D.3
) as

\displaystyle I(q)

\displaystyle=\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\,\Biggl[\frac{\theta(q-\epsilon)}{q^{\nu+1}}\,\frac{d}{dq}\,K(q)+\frac{\delta(q)}{q^{\nu+1}}\,\biggl(K(q_{0})-\int_{\epsilon}^{q_{0}}d\tilde{q}\;\frac{d}{d\tilde{q}}\mskip 1.5muK(\tilde{q})\biggr)\Biggr]

\displaystyle\quad-\int_{0}^{\infty}dz\,J_{\nu+1}(qz)\;\frac{d}{dq}\mskip 1.5mu\frac{\tilde{f}(z,q)}{z}

\displaystyle=\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\,\Biggl[\frac{\theta(q-\epsilon)}{q^{\nu+1}}\,\frac{d}{dq}\,K(q)+\frac{\delta(q)}{q^{\nu+1}}\,K(\epsilon)\Biggr]-\int_{0}^{\infty}dz\,J_{\nu+1}(qz)\;\frac{d}{dq}\mskip 1.5mu\frac{\tilde{f}(z,q)}{z}\,.

(D.5)

This makes it explicit that the dependence on
q_{0}
cancels, as it must be.
If
K(0)=0
, one can take the
\epsilon=0
limit for each individual term on
the r.h.s. The distributional part proportional to
\delta(q)
then disappears,
and
I(q)
is an ordinary function.

If
\tilde{f}
is independent of
q
, one readily finds

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{q}d\tilde{q}\;\tilde{q}^{\nu+1}\,I(\tilde{q})

\displaystyle=K(q)\,.

(D.6)

The representation in (
D.3
) and (
D.4
) thus generalises the
relation between the differential and cumulative
q
spectra in (
5.1
)
and (
5.2
) to distributions and to
\nu\neq 0
.

As a simple illustration let us set
\tilde{f}(z)=z
and
\nu=0
. One then has

K(q)=1
, and our definition (
D.3
) gives
I(q)=\delta(q)/q
in
agreement with (
6.3
).

Inverse Fourier-Bessel transform

If
\tilde{f}
is independent of
q
, then (
D.3
) is an extension of the
ordinary Fourier-Bessel transform. We now show that, in accordance with the
relations (
A.7
) and (
A.8
) for ordinary functions, the
Fourier-Bessel transform of
I(q)
gives back
\tilde{f}(z)/z
, provided that

\displaystyle q^{2}\mskip 1.5muK(q)

\displaystyle\underset{q\to 0}{\rightarrow}0\,,

\displaystyle q^{-1/2-\nu}\,K(q)

\displaystyle\underset{q\to\infty}{\rightarrow}0\,.

(D.7)

We do not investigate here for which functions
\tilde{f}(z)
these conditions are
satisfied, but we note that they do hold for the case
\tilde{f}(z)=z\,\ln^{k}\!z
that we studied in section
6
.

Taking the Fourier-Bessel transform of (
D.3
) and using (
D.1
) with
\varphi(q)=J_{\nu}(qy)/q^{\nu}
, we get

\displaystyle\tilde{I}(y)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}dq\,q\,J_{\nu}(qy)\,I(q)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}dq\,\biggl[\frac{J_{\nu}(qy)}{q^{\nu}}-\theta(q_{0}-q)\mskip 1.5muJ_{\nu}^{(0)}\biggr]\frac{d}{dq}\mskip 1.5muK(q)+J_{\nu}^{(0)}\,K(q_{0})

(D.8)

with

\displaystyle J_{\nu}^{(0)}

\displaystyle=\lim_{q\to 0}\,\frac{J_{\nu}(qy)}{q^{\nu}}=\biggl(\frac{y}{2}\biggr)^{\nu}\frac{1}{\Gamma(1+\nu)}\,.

(D.9)

Integration by parts gives

\displaystyle\tilde{I}(y)

\displaystyle=-\int_{0}^{\infty}dq\;K(q)\,\frac{d}{dq}\mskip 1.5mu\frac{J_{\nu}(qy)}{q^{\nu}}\,,

(D.10)

where the boundary terms have vanished by virtue of (
D.7
). Using
(
A.3
) and (
A.9
), we finally obtain the desired
result

\displaystyle\tilde{I}(y)

\displaystyle=y\int_{0}^{\infty}\!dq\,q\,J_{\nu+1}(qy)\int_{0}^{\infty}\!dz\,J_{\nu+1}(qz)\,\frac{\tilde{f}(z)}{z}=\frac{\tilde{f}(y)}{y}\,.

(D.11)
