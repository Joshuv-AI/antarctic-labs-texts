Flow views and infinite interval exchange transformations for recognizable substitutions
Natalie Priebe Frank

Abstract
. A flow view is the graph of a measurable conjugacy Φ \displaystyle\Phi between a substitution or S-adic subshift ( Σ , σ , μ ) \displaystyle({\Sigma},\shift,\mu) and an exchange of infinitely many intervals in ( [ 0 , 1 ] , 𝔉 , m ) \displaystyle([0,1],\mathfrak{F},m) , where m \displaystyle m is Lebesgue measure. A natural refining sequence of partitions of Σ \displaystyle{\Sigma} is transferred to ( [ 0 , 1 ] , m ) \displaystyle([0,1],m) using a canonical addressing scheme, a fixed dual substitution 𝒮 ∗ \displaystyle{\mathcal{S}_{*}} , and a shift-invariant probability measure μ \displaystyle\mu . On the flow view, 𝝉 ∈ Σ \displaystyle\boldsymbol{\tau}\in{\Sigma} is shown horizontally at a height of Φ ⁡ ( 𝝉 ) \displaystyle\Phi(\boldsymbol{\tau}) using colored unit intervals to represent the letters. The infinite interval exchange transformation 𝔉 \displaystyle\mathfrak{F} is well approximated by exchanges of finitely many intervals, making numeric and graphic methods possible. We prove that in certain cases a choice of dual substitution guarantees that Φ \displaystyle\Phi is self-similar. We discuss why the spectral type of Φ ∈ L 2 ​ ( Σ , μ ) , \displaystyle\Phi\in L^{2}({\Sigma},\mu), is of particular interest. As an example of utility, some spectral results for constant-length substitutions are included.

In this paper we provide a canonical method for constructing measurable conjugacies between a given recognizable substitution or
\displaystyle S

\displaystyle({\Sigma},\shift,\mu)

The current paper is situated within the field of Aperiodic Order, to which Uwe Grimm gave the major works
[

Two things that characterize Uwe’s work are the thorough analysis of examples and extensive use of visualization to understand physical and mathematical phenomena. This paper carries on some of those traditions.
Not only does the flow view allow us to “see” the full sequence spaces, the graphs of the IIETs produce a visual representation of the shift action. We show how our scheme is applied to the iconic examples such as the Fibonacci, Thue-Morse, period-doubling, and Rudin-Shapiro substitutions along with sequences that arise from procedures like Chacon’s cutting-and-stacking

2.1.
Symbolic dynamics

A finite set
\displaystyle{\mathcal{A}}
is taken to be the

with elements
\displaystyle\alpha\in{\mathcal{A}}
known as
letters
. A

is a function
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\omega}:\{j,j+1,\ldots,k\}\to{\mathcal{A}}
where
\displaystyle j\leq k\in\mathbb{Z}
. The
length
of a word is
\displaystyle|\boldsymbol{\omega}|=k-j+1
. We use the notation
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\omega}[i,...,n]
for a
subword
of
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\omega}
, where
\displaystyle j\leq i\leq n\leq k
is assumed. We use ordinary function notation
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\omega}(i)
and refer to the element of
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\omega}
at
\displaystyle i
. A sequence in
\displaystyle{\mathcal{A}}^{\mathbb{Z}}
is an infinite word.

The set
\displaystyle{\mathcal{A}}^{+}
is the set of all words of finite length and
\displaystyle{\mathcal{A}}^{\mathbb{Z}}
is the set of all (bi)infinite sequences on
\displaystyle{\mathcal{A}}
. We endow
\displaystyle{\mathcal{A}}^{\mathbb{Z}}
with a metric that determines the distance between
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\tau},\boldsymbol{\tau}^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{A}}^{\mathbb{Z}}
based on the largest interval around the origin on which they are identical. Any choice will generate the product topology on
\displaystyle{\mathcal{A}}^{\mathbb{Z}}
. We use the following.

Definition 2.1
.

For any
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\tau}
and
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\tau}^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{A}}^{\mathbb{Z}}
with
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\tau}\neq\boldsymbol{\tau}^{\prime}
, define
\displaystyle N(\boldsymbol{\tau},\boldsymbol{\tau}^{\prime})=\inf\{n\geq 0:\boldsymbol{\tau}[-n,\ldots,n]\neq\boldsymbol{\tau}^{\prime}[-n,\ldots,n]\}.

Then

d(\boldsymbol{\tau},\boldsymbol{\tau}^{\prime})=\begin{cases}\exp(-N(\boldsymbol{\tau},\boldsymbol{\tau}^{\prime}))&\boldsymbol{\tau}\neq\boldsymbol{\tau}^{\prime}\\
0&\boldsymbol{\tau}=\boldsymbol{\tau}^{\prime}\end{cases}.

There is a
shift
action
\displaystyle\shift:{\mathcal{A}}^{\mathbb{Z}}\to{\mathcal{A}}^{\mathbb{Z}}
by elements of
\displaystyle\mathbb{Z}
given by

(1)

\left(\shift(\boldsymbol{\omega})\right)(k)=\boldsymbol{\omega}(k+1)\quad\text{ for all }k\in\mathbb{Z}.

The shift moves
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\omega}
one unit to the left, so that whatever was at
\displaystyle 1
in
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\omega}
is at
\displaystyle 0
in
\displaystyle\shift(\boldsymbol{\omega})
. The shift is continuous and the resulting dynamical system is known as the
full shift

\displaystyle({\mathcal{A}}^{\mathbb{Z}},\shift)

\displaystyle{\Sigma}\subset{\mathcal{A}}^{\mathbb{Z}}
inherits the metric topology and shift action; the pair
\displaystyle({\Sigma},\shift)
is known as a
subshift
.

Substitutions and their dynamical systems (see

A

When the set

2.3.
The address scheme

Here we develop the address scheme needed for our construction. It is fairly standard, appearing for instance in
[
12
]
, but we give it notation adapted to our purposes. For each
\displaystyle n=0,1,2,...
we know that if
\displaystyle\mathcal{S}
is recognizable then every
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\tau}\in{\Sigma}
is an element of
\displaystyle\shift^{k}(\mathcal{S}^{n}([\alpha])
for a unique
\displaystyle\alpha\in{\mathcal{A}}
and
\displaystyle k\in\{0,1,\ldots,|\mathcal{S}^{n}(\alpha)|-1\}
. This means that the set of all
\displaystyle n
-cylinders forms a partition of
\displaystyle{\Sigma}
which we denote

(5)

\mathcal{B}_{n}=\left\{\shift^{k}(\mathcal{S}^{n}([\alpha])),\alpha\in{\mathcal{A}}\text{ and }0\leq k\leq|\mathcal{S}^{n}(\alpha)|-1\right\}.

The fact that the partition
\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{n+1}
is a refinement of the partition of
\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{n}
can be seen as follows. Any two elements of the
\displaystyle(n+1)
-cylinder

\displaystyle\shift^{k}(\mathcal{S}^{n+1}([\alpha])
have the origin in precisely the same location of the same
\displaystyle(n+1)
-supertile. That immediately means that they must also share the same
\displaystyle n
-supertile at the origin. Thus any given
\displaystyle n
-cylinder either completely contains or is completely disjoint from every
\displaystyle(n+1)
-cylinder.

Definition 2.7
.

Let
\displaystyle{\Sigma}
be given by a recognizable substitution. The refining sequence of partitions
\displaystyle\left(\mathcal{B}_{n}\right)_{n=0}^{\infty}
is called the

of

Proposition 3.4
.

Let
\displaystyle\mathcal{S}
be a recognizable substitution whose subshift
\displaystyle({\Sigma},\mu)
is minimal, and let
\displaystyle\Phi:({\Sigma},\mu)\to([0,1],m)
be a canonical isomorphism. Then
\displaystyle\Phi
is measure preserving, well-defined and uniformly continuous everywhere, bijective almost everywhere, and at most
\displaystyle 2|{\mathcal{A}}|
:1.

Proof.

We know
\displaystyle\Phi
is well-defined because every tiling has a unique address and each infinite series converges by definition.

For uniform continuity, given
\displaystyle\epsilon>0
, suppose
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\tau}
and
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\tau}^{\prime}
have the same address out to
\displaystyle N
, where
\displaystyle\epsilon>\lambda^{-N}
. Then
\displaystyle\Phi(\boldsymbol{\tau})
and
\displaystyle\Phi(\boldsymbol{\tau}^{\prime})
map into the same element of
\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{N}
, which has length smaller than
\displaystyle\lambda^{-N}
, and thus
\displaystyle|\Phi(\boldsymbol{\tau})-\Phi(\boldsymbol{\tau}^{\prime})|<\epsilon
.
There is a common recognizability radius
\displaystyle R_{N}
to determine the
\displaystyle N
th supertile at the origin for any element of
\displaystyle{\Sigma}
. To ensure
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\tau}
and
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\tau}^{\prime}
agree on that supertile we need only that
\displaystyle d(\boldsymbol{\tau},\boldsymbol{\tau}^{\prime})<1/R_{N}
. This proves uniform continuity. Moreover, since
\displaystyle{\Sigma}
is compact its image must be also, so

\displaystyle\mathfrak{F}

All of the IIETs shown in this document exhibit repetitive properties that appear to be a form of self-similarity, perhaps via a graph-directed IFS. This reveals geometrically the self-inducing structure of

Definition 4.7
.

We call a substitution
proper
if there are
\displaystyle\beta,\gamma\in{\mathcal{A}}
such that
\displaystyle\mathcal{S}(\alpha)
begins with
\displaystyle\beta
and ends with
\displaystyle\gamma
for all
\displaystyle\alpha\in{\mathcal{A}}

\displaystyle\alpha\in{\mathcal{A}},\mathcal{S}(\alpha)(0)=\beta
and
\displaystyle\mathcal{S}(\alpha)(|\mathcal{S}(\alpha)|-1)=\gamma

Proposition 4.8
.

Let
\displaystyle\mathcal{S}
be a recognizable and proper substitution with minimal subshift, and suppose
\displaystyle|\mathcal{S}(\alpha)|>1
for each
\displaystyle\alpha\in{\mathcal{A}}

Then
\displaystyle\mathcal{S}
has a canonical IIET
\displaystyle\mathfrak{F}
for which there is a constant
\displaystyle\kappa\in[0,1)
such that

(17)

\mathfrak{F}(x)=\lambda(\mathfrak{F}(x/\lambda)+\kappa)\text{ for a.e. }x\in[0,1].

Proof.

Suppose every substituted letter begins with
\displaystyle\beta
and ends with
\displaystyle\gamma
and construct the initial partition
\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{0}
so that
\displaystyle{\mathbf{I}}(\gamma)=[0,\mu([\gamma]))
. No other restrictions on
\displaystyle\Phi_{0}
and
\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{0}
are required.

Next, we need to choose
\displaystyle{\phi}
and
\displaystyle{\mathcal{S}_{*}}
.
Since every supertile ends in a
\displaystyle\gamma

\displaystyle{\mathcal{S}_{*}}(\gamma)
contains all the letters in
\displaystyle{\mathcal{A}}
at least once. That immediately tells us that
\displaystyle\mu(\mathcal{S}([\gamma]))\geq 1/\lambda

\displaystyle{\mathcal{S}_{*}}(\gamma)
be given by the letters of
\displaystyle{\mathcal{A}}
in whatever order was chosen for
\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{0}
so that
\displaystyle[0,1/\lambda)
is partitioned the same way as
\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{0}
except scaled by
\displaystyle 1/\lambda
. We will use the interval
\displaystyle[0,1/\lambda)
to be associated to the maximal address of each 1-supertile.

By similar logic we know that
\displaystyle{\mathcal{S}_{*}}(\beta)
contains all of the letters of
\displaystyle{\mathcal{A}}
at least once
and so we include a copy of
\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{0}
scaled by
\displaystyle\lambda
in
\displaystyle{\mathbf{I}}(\beta)
for the supertiles with minimal addresses. Denote this copy of
\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{0}

\displaystyle[\kappa,\kappa+1/\lambda)

\displaystyle\kappa

\displaystyle[1/\lambda,1)

\displaystyle|\mathcal{S}(\alpha)|>1
for each
\displaystyle\alpha\in{\mathcal{A}}

\displaystyle{\mathcal{S}_{*}}(\gamma)
and
\displaystyle{\mathcal{S}_{*}}(\beta)

\displaystyle{\mathcal{S}_{*}}

Consider the canonical isomorphism
\displaystyle\Phi
and IIET
\displaystyle\mathfrak{F}
for this choice of initial partition and refinement maps. We show that
\displaystyle\mathfrak{F}
is self-similar.

The square
\displaystyle Q=[0,1/\lambda)\times[\kappa,\kappa+1/\lambda)
contains all transitions from maximal 1-supertiles to minimal 1-supertiles and is a scaled-down copy of the graph of
\displaystyle\mathfrak{F}

\displaystyle\mathbf{n}(x)\geq 1

\displaystyle[1/\lambda^{2},1/\lambda)

\displaystyle\mathfrak{F}(x)=\lambda^{-1}\mathfrak{F}(\lambda x)+\kappa

\displaystyle x\in[1/\lambda,1)

\displaystyle\mathfrak{F}(x)=\lambda(\mathfrak{F}(x/\lambda)-\kappa)

\displaystyle[1/\lambda^{2},1/\lambda)

\displaystyle[0,1/\lambda^{2})
contains all
\displaystyle x
for which
\displaystyle\mathbf{n}(x)\geq 2
; it corresponds to the 2-supertiles with maximal addresses. It must therefore be mapped to the interval of length
\displaystyle 1/\lambda^{2}
corresponding to the 2-supertiles with minimal addresses, which begins at
\displaystyle\kappa(1+1/\lambda)
. The transitions between non-maximal 3-supertiles are the same as the transitions between non-maximal tiles within their supertiles, so

\mathfrak{F}(x)=\lambda^{-2}\mathfrak{F}(\lambda^{2}x)+\kappa(1+1/\lambda).

Since
\displaystyle\lambda^{2}x\in[1/\lambda,1)
, that means

\mathfrak{F}(\lambda^{2}x)=\lambda(\mathfrak{F}(\lambda^{2}x/\lambda)-\kappa)=\lambda(\mathfrak{F}(\lambda x)-\kappa).

Plugging into the expression for
\displaystyle\mathfrak{F}(x)
yields

\mathfrak{F}(x)=\lambda^{-2}\left(\lambda(\mathfrak{F}(\lambda x)-\kappa)\right)+\kappa(1+1/\lambda)=\lambda^{-1}\mathfrak{F}(\lambda x)+\kappa.

Since
\displaystyle\lambda x\in[1/\lambda^{2},1/\lambda)
this extends the result (
17
) to
\displaystyle[1/\lambda^{2},1)
. An analogous induction argument will find the result to hold for all intervals of the form
\displaystyle[1/\lambda^{n},1)
.
∎

5.
Spectral analysis of
\displaystyle({\Sigma},\shift,\mu)

5.1.
Review: spectral analysis in a general system (see e.g.
[
28
]
)

One way to study the behavior of a dynamical system
\displaystyle(X,T,\nu)
is through the measurable functions it supports. It is particularly convenient to consider
\displaystyle L^{2}(X,\nu)
because it is a Hilbert space.
The “dynamical spectrum” is the spectrum of the so-called Koopman operator
\displaystyle U
on
\displaystyle L^{2}(X,\nu)
given by
\displaystyle U(h)=h\circ T
. It is natural to ask what happens under powers of the operator, which leads to the Fourier analysis approach we describe here.

The
spectral coefficient

\displaystyle\hat{h}(j)
is defined as the inner product

\hat{h}(j)=\left\langle U^{j}(h),\,h\right\rangle=\int_{X}h(T^{j}(x))\overline{h(x)}d\nu(x).

This expression compares the measurements
\displaystyle h
takes at each pair
\displaystyle(x,T^{j}(x))
and averages the result over all
\displaystyle x
.
It is well-known that the sequence of spectral coefficients is positive definite and so there is a
spectral measure

\displaystyle z_{h}
on the circle
\displaystyle S^{1}
for which

\hat{h}(j)=\int_{S^{1}}z^{j}dz_{h}\quad\text{ for all }\quad j\in\mathbb{Z}.

An
eigenfunction
for
\displaystyle T
is an
\displaystyle h\in L^{2}(X,\nu)

for which there exists an
eigenvalue

\displaystyle R\in\mathbb{R}
with
\displaystyle h(T(x))=R\,h(x)
for all
\displaystyle x\in X
. All constant functions are eigenfunctions and so it is customary to consider
\displaystyle h
for which
\displaystyle\int_{X}h\,d\nu=0
when looking at spectral measures.

5.2.
The spectral measure of
\displaystyle\Phi

In
\displaystyle\Phi
we have an extraordinary function in
\displaystyle L^{2}({\Sigma},\mu)
. It the best ‘observable’ there is: it measures the features of
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\tau}\in{\Sigma}
so accurately that it can almost always place it in a unique location in
\displaystyle[0,1]
. Moreover, that location is close to other
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\tau}
s that strongly ‘resemble’ it as measured using any other test function in
\displaystyle L^{2}(X,\mu)
. We conjecture that this implies that the spectral measure of
\displaystyle\Phi
is the maximal spectral type of the system.

For each
\displaystyle j\in\mathbb{Z}
the spectral coefficient is computed using Corollary
3.5
to be

\hat{\Phi}(j)=\int_{\Sigma}\Phi(\shift^{j}(\boldsymbol{\tau}))\overline{\Phi(\boldsymbol{\tau})}\mu(d\boldsymbol{\tau})=\int_{0}^{1}\,x\,\mathfrak{F}^{j}(x)dx,

and since
\displaystyle\mathfrak{F}^{k}(x)=x+c
, where
\displaystyle c
depends only on any sufficiently nonmaximal partition element containing
\displaystyle x
, the integrand is a piecewise sum of upward-facing quadratics
\displaystyle x^{2}+cx
.

It is known
[
22
]
that shifting by sequences of the form
\displaystyle(|\mathcal{S}^{n}(\alpha)|)_{n=1}^{\infty}
reveals the presence or absence of nonconstant eigenfunctions. Figures
11
through
15
in the discussions below exhibit a variety of behaviors of
\displaystyle\mathfrak{F}^{|\mathcal{S}^{n}(\alpha)|}(x)
for different substitutions, beginning with the constant-length case.

Proposition 5.1
.

If
\displaystyle\mathcal{S}
is a primitive recognizable constant-length substitution with expansion factor
\displaystyle K
then
\displaystyle\left(\mathfrak{F}^{K^{n}}(x)\right)_{n=1}^{\infty}
has at most
\displaystyle|{\mathcal{A}}|
accumulation points for a.e.
\displaystyle x\in[0,1]
.

Proof.

Let
\displaystyle x
be such that
\displaystyle\mathfrak{F}^{j}(x)
is defined for all
\displaystyle j\in\mathbb{N}
. Choose
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\tau}\in\Phi^{-1}(x)
for which
\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{{\mathsf{a}}}}(x)={\boldsymbol{{\mathsf{a}}}}(\boldsymbol{\tau})
. Since
\displaystyle K^{n}
is the length of an
\displaystyle n
-supertile, the
\displaystyle n
-supertile at the origin in
\displaystyle\shift^{K^{n}}(\boldsymbol{\tau})
and in
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\tau}
are the same position modulo
\displaystyle K^{n}
but may be of different types. There are
\displaystyle|{\mathcal{A}}|
possible
\displaystyle n
-subintervals that could contain
\displaystyle\mathfrak{F}^{K^{n}}(x)
.

For
\displaystyle N>n
,
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\tau}(0)
and
\displaystyle\shift^{K^{N}}(\boldsymbol{\tau})(0)
now are in the same position modulo
\displaystyle K^{N}
, so as before there are exactly
\displaystyle|{\mathcal{A}}|
subintervals that could contain
\displaystyle\Phi(\shift^{K^{N}}(\boldsymbol{\tau}))
. Shifting by
\displaystyle K^{N}
matches up
\displaystyle n
-supertiles as well, so the possible intervals for
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\tau}
and
\displaystyle\shift^{K^{N}}(\boldsymbol{\tau})
are contained in the intervals for
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\tau}
and
\displaystyle\shift^{K^{n}}(\boldsymbol{\tau})
. Thus there are
\displaystyle|{\mathcal{A}}|
nested sequences of intervals of vanishing length that
\displaystyle\mathfrak{F}^{K^{n}}(x)
can visit as
\displaystyle n\to\infty
. The limits of their left endpoints are the possible accumulation points for
\displaystyle\left\{\mathfrak{F}^{K^{n}}(x),\,n\in\mathbb{N}\right\}
.
∎

Definition 5.2
.

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}

\displaystyle N\in\mathbb{N},\beta\in{\mathcal{A}}

\displaystyle\mathsf{A}

Theorem 5.3
.

Let
\displaystyle\mathcal{S}
be a primitive substitution of constant length
\displaystyle K>1
with canonical isomorphism
\displaystyle\Phi:({\Sigma},\shift,\mu)\to([0,1],\mathfrak{F},m)
. Then
\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathfrak{F}^{K^{n}}(x)=x
almost everywhere if and only if
\displaystyle\mathcal{S}
has a coincidence.

Proof.

Suppose there is a coincidence, which by taking powers if necessary can be assumed to occur
at
\displaystyle j_{c}\in\{0,\ldots,K-1\}
. Consider
\displaystyle{\Sigma}_{c}
, the set of all
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\tau}\in{\Sigma}
such that
\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{{\mathsf{a}}}}(\boldsymbol{\tau})
contains infinitely many coincidence addresses. This is a set of full measure by Lemma
2.10
since it contains
\displaystyle{\Sigma}_{0}
.

Let
\displaystyle x=\Phi(\boldsymbol{\tau})
for
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\tau}\in{\Sigma}_{c}
and let
\displaystyle\epsilon>0
. Find some
\displaystyle n>1+|\ln(\epsilon)|
at which
\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{{\mathsf{a}}}}(\boldsymbol{\tau})
has a coincidence label, i.e.

{\boldsymbol{{\mathsf{a}}}}(\boldsymbol{\tau})=\left({\boldsymbol{{\mathsf{a}}}}_{{n-1}}(\boldsymbol{\tau}),(\alpha_{n},j_{c}),(\alpha_{n+1},j_{n+1}),\ldots\right).

Then
\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{{\mathsf{a}}}}_{{n-1}}(\boldsymbol{\tau})
is independent of
\displaystyle\alpha_{n}
and depends only on the positions
\displaystyle j_{k}
for
\displaystyle 1\leq k\leq n-1
. Any shift of
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\tau}
by
\displaystyle K^{N}
where
\displaystyle N\geq n
aligns the
\displaystyle n
-supertiles since
\displaystyle K^{N}\equiv 0\pmod{K^{n}}
, so
\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{{\mathsf{a}}}}(\shift^{K^{N}}(\boldsymbol{\tau}))
also has a coincidence label at
\displaystyle n
.
That is,
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\tau}
and
\displaystyle\shift^{K^{N}}(\boldsymbol{\tau})
have the same
\displaystyle n-1
supertile with 0 in the same location. Their images under
\displaystyle\Phi
must both be in
\displaystyle{\mathbf{I}}({\boldsymbol{{\mathsf{a}}}}_{{n-1}}(\boldsymbol{\tau}))

which has length
\displaystyle=1/K^{n-1}<\epsilon
. Since
\displaystyle\Phi(\shift^{K^{N}}(\boldsymbol{\tau}))=\mathfrak{F}^{K^{n}}(\Phi(\boldsymbol{\tau}))\to\Phi(\boldsymbol{\tau})
this shows
\displaystyle\mathfrak{F}^{K^{n}}(x)\to x
for any
\displaystyle x\in\Phi({\Sigma}_{c})
.

Now suppose
\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathfrak{F}^{K^{n}}(x)=x
almost everywhere and let

[\alpha\beta]:=\{\boldsymbol{\tau}\in{\Sigma}\,|\,\boldsymbol{\tau}(0)=\alpha\text{ and }\boldsymbol{\tau}(1)=\beta\}.

Let
\displaystyle\delta>0
such that
\displaystyle\delta<\mu([\alpha\beta])
for all legal two-letter words
\displaystyle\alpha\beta:\{0,1\}\to{\mathcal{A}}
. By Egorov’s theorem there is a set
\displaystyle{D_{\delta}}\subset[0,1]
on which the convergence is uniform and
\displaystyle m({D_{\delta}})\geq 1-\delta
. Choose
\displaystyle M
so that if
\displaystyle n\geq M
then
\displaystyle|\mathfrak{F}^{K^{n}}(x)-x|<\delta
for all
\displaystyle x\in{D_{\delta}}
.

For all
\displaystyle j=0,1,\ldots,K^{M}-1
, let
\displaystyle{E}(j)
be the set of all sequences in the
\displaystyle j
th spot of their
\displaystyle M
supertile in the sense that

{E}(j)=\bigcup_{\alpha\in{\mathcal{A}}}\shift^{j}(\mathcal{S}^{M}([\alpha)]).

This has measure
\displaystyle\mu({E}(j))=1/K^{M}
. Let
\displaystyle{E_{\delta}}=\Phi^{-1}({D_{\delta}})
and
\displaystyle{E_{\delta}}(j)={E}(j)\cap{E_{\delta}}
, and suppose
\displaystyle j_{c}
is an index for which
\displaystyle\mu({E_{\delta}}(j))\leq\mu({E_{\delta}}(j_{c}))
for all
\displaystyle j\in\{0,...,K^{M}-1\}
. We claim
\displaystyle j_{c}
is a coincidence for
\displaystyle\mathcal{S}
.

For the sake of contradiction suppose there are
\displaystyle\hat{\alpha}
and
\displaystyle\hat{\beta}
that differ at their
\displaystyle j_{c}
th spot:
\displaystyle\mathcal{S}^{M}(\hat{\alpha})(j_{c})\neq\mathcal{S}^{M}(\hat{\beta})(j_{c})
. Without loss of generality we may assume that
\displaystyle\hat{\alpha}
and
\displaystyle\hat{\beta}
are chosen so that the word
\displaystyle\hat{\alpha}\hat{\beta}
is a legal word since if no such choice was possible then
\displaystyle j_{c}
is a coincidence and we are done.

Suppose
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\tau}\in\shift^{j_{c}}(\mathcal{S}^{M}([\hat{\alpha}\hat{\beta}]))
. Since
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\tau}\in\shift^{j_{c}}(\mathcal{S}^{M}([\hat{\alpha}]))
and
\displaystyle\shift^{K^{M}}(\boldsymbol{\tau})\in\shift^{j_{c}}(\mathcal{S}^{M}([\hat{\beta}]))
we know that
\displaystyle\Phi(\boldsymbol{\tau})
and
\displaystyle\Phi(\shift^{K^{M}}(\boldsymbol{\tau}))=\mathfrak{F}^{K^{M}}(\Phi(\boldsymbol{\tau}))
are in disjoint subintervals of
\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{M}
.
Thus
\displaystyle|\Phi(\boldsymbol{\tau})-\mathfrak{F}^{K^{M}}(\Phi(\boldsymbol{\tau}))|>\delta
.
That means
\displaystyle\mu({E_{\delta}}(j_{c}))\leq\mu({E}(j_{c}))-\mu(\shift^{j_{c}}(\mathcal{S}^{M}[\hat{\alpha}\hat{\beta}]))=1/K^{M}-\mu([\hat{\alpha}\hat{\beta}]))/K^{M}.
Since
\displaystyle\mu({E_{\delta}}(j_{c}))
is the maximum measure over the
\displaystyle j

\mu({E_{\delta}})=\sum_{j=1}^{K^{M}}\mu({E_{\delta}}(j))\leq K^{M}\mu({E_{\delta}}(j_{c}))\leq 1-\mu([\hat{\alpha}\hat{\beta}])<1-\delta.

Since
\displaystyle\mu({E_{\delta}})=m({D_{\delta}})
, this contradicts the definition of
\displaystyle{D_{\delta}}
and completes the proof.
∎

Self-similar tilings, S-adic sequences and fusion tilings of

Our construction works in a number of other situations. We provide a relatively complete description of the one-dimensional case below. Extension to higher dimensions could be of interest but we will not discuss that here.

6.1.
S-adic systems

These systems are generated with substitutions that can vary at each level. A general S-adic system is generated by a
directive sequence
of morphisms
\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{n}:{\mathcal{A}}_{n}\to{\mathcal{A}}_{n-1}^{+}
on a sequence
\displaystyle{\mathcal{A}}_{0},{\mathcal{A}}_{1},{\mathcal{A}}_{2},{\mathcal{A}}_{3},...
of finite alphabets. The
\displaystyle n
-supertiles are of the form
\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{1}(\mathcal{S}_{2}(...(\mathcal{S}_{n}(\alpha))...)
, where
\displaystyle\alpha\in{\mathcal{A}}_{n}
. A subshift of all admissible sequences is obtained as in definition
2.2
.
A classic type of S-adic system is made by taking all substitutions with a given transition matrix
\displaystyle M
on a single alphabet
\displaystyle{\mathcal{A}}

[
4
]
. In that situation the estimates from corollary
4.6
still hold, and a single dual substitution can be chosen to construct the flow view.

For S-adic symbolic systems the notion of recognizability becomes more nuanced
[
9
]
. We assume the strongest form:
full recognizability
, where each substitution in the directive sequence is recognizable.
We also assume that the subshift admitted by the supertiles is minimal. In this case there is no direct Perron-Frobenius theorem to give us information about the natural lengths or frequencies. However, there is a sequence of transition matrices, and any invariant measure for the subshift must obey a type of transition-consistency that forces the left equation of (
10
) to be true at each level.

There are two adaptations needed to our proof. First, we need an address set
\displaystyle\mathsf{A}_{n}
for each
\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{n}
, constructed as before (
6
). Addresses for
\displaystyle n
-cylinders will be those elements of
\displaystyle\mathsf{A}_{1}\times\mathsf{A}_{2}\times...\times\mathsf{A}_{n}
that represent allowable supertile inclusions. Those inclusions are given at each level as in equation
7
as follows. For each
\displaystyle\alpha\in{\mathcal{A}}_{n}
,

{\mathsf{T}_{n}({\alpha})}=\{(\beta,j)\in\mathsf{A}_{n+1}\,|\,\mathcal{S}_{n+1}(\beta)(j)=\alpha\}=\{(\beta,j)\in\mathsf{A}_{n+1}\,|\,\alpha\text{ is the }j\text{th letter of }\mathcal{S}_{n+1}(\beta)\}.

These provide the edge sets for the Bratteli diagram. The refining sequence
\displaystyle\{\mathcal{B}_{n}\}
of
\displaystyle{\Sigma}
is canonical, as is the order on the Bratteli diagram.

The second adaptation is that at every level we need to produce a new left endpoint function
\displaystyle{\phi}_{n}:{\mathsf{T}_{n}({\alpha})}\to[0,1)
for each
\displaystyle\alpha\in{\mathcal{A}}_{n}
. Because the measure is transition-consistent, we can partition the intervals for
\displaystyle n
-supertiles into the subintervals corresponding to their
\displaystyle(n+1)
-tiles and define
\displaystyle{\phi}_{n}

The rest of the proofs are made with these adaptations.

7.
Questions, comments, observations

The author has fallen down numerous rabbit holes of high (to her) entertainment value. The following is a somewhat random assortment of her favorites.

✤ Any substitution whose subshift is minimal and recognizable has numerous IIET/flow view combinations that represent it. In the simplest case we could consider only the set of canonical IIETs given by all possible dual substitutions. This produces a finite list of
\displaystyle(\Phi_{j},\mathfrak{F}_{j})
s that all represent
\displaystyle({\Sigma},\shift,\mu)
.

•

What information is contained in the geometry of the maps

\displaystyle\Phi_{2}\circ\Phi_{1}^{-1}
of
\displaystyle[0,1]
?

•

Many substitutions have a
periodic dual substitution
which, if treated as recognizable, would be an odometer. What is the significance of this?

•

Is there an
optimal choice
of dual substitution to represent a system?

✤
The flow view construction provides a link between all substitutions and S-adic systems that share a common primitive transition matrix
\displaystyle M
.

•

For any given substitution, an S-adic system using any or all of the choices of dual substitution could be used, in which case the representation would be non-canonical.

•

Alternatively, S-adic systems in which the directive sequence shares a transition matrix can be modeled with a dual substitution for its flow view.

•

Take two subshifts with coordinate maps given by the same dual substitution. This gives a measure-theoretic bijection between the subshifts. How do the properties of the matrix affect the properties of this bijection?

✤ The Fibonacci substitution subshift is known to correspond to an
exchange of two intervals
, but the
\displaystyle\mathfrak{F}
that appears in figure
1
of our introduction exchanges infinitely many. That’s because there are only two choices of dual substitution for
\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{fib}
, and both yield exchanges of infinitely many intervals. There are two main ways to enlarge the scope. One is to allow all canonical
\displaystyle(\Phi_{j},\mathfrak{F}_{j})
s for all powers of the substitution. The other is to allow the subdivisions to vary at each stage, producing an S-adic structure of dual substitutions. The latter has not been investigated so far, but the former allows the Fibonacci two-interval exchange to appear from our process. With
\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{fib}^{2}
and the correct choice of dual substitution, figure

If a substitution subshift is measurably conjugate to a

One can define the

For instance, since the Fibonacci subshift can be represented with an IET of two intervals, the efficiency of any other canonical representation can be compared.

✤ The destination under
\displaystyle\Phi
