An Empirical Study of Automated Vulnerability Localization with Large Language Models
Jian Zhang, Chong Wang, Anran Li, Weisong Sun, Cen Zhang, Wei Ma, Yang Liu

Abstract
Recently, Automated Vulnerability Localization (AVL) has attracted much attention, aiming to facilitate diagnosis by pinpointing the lines of code responsible for discovered vulnerabilities. Large Language Models (LLMs) have shown potential in various domains, yet their effectiveness in vulnerability localization remains underexplored. In this work, we perform the first comprehensive study of LLMs for AVL. Our investigation encompasses 10+ leading LLMs suitable for code analysis, including ChatGPT and various open-source models, across three architectural types: encoder-only, encoder-decoder, and decoder-only, with model sizes ranging from 60M to 16B parameters. We explore the efficacy of these LLMs using 4 distinct paradigms: zero-shot learning, one-shot learning, discriminative fine-tuning, and generative fine-tuning. Our evaluation framework is applied to the BigVul-based dataset for C/C++, and an additional dataset comprising smart contract vulnerabilities. The results demonstrate that discriminative fine-tuning of LLMs can significantly outperform existing learning-based methods for AVL, while other paradigms prove less effective or unexpectedly ineffective for the task. We also identify challenges related to input length and unidirectional context in fine-tuning processes for encoders and decoders. We then introduce two remedial strategies: the sliding window and the right-forward embedding, both of which substantially enhance performance. Furthermore, our findings highlight certain generalization capabilities of LLMs across Common Weakness Enumerations (CWEs) and different projects, indicating a promising pathway toward their practical application in vulnerability localization.



I
Introduction

Software vulnerabilities are critical risks that require swift action to prevent attacks
[
1
]
. Although various vulnerability detection approaches have been proposed
[
2
,
3
,
4
]
, developers often face delays in resolving these issues, hindered by high false positives and unclear origins of the weaknesses
[
5
,
6
]
. Hence, there has been a shift towards Automated Vulnerability Localization (AVL)
[
7
]
, which pinpoints the exact location within the code where a vulnerability exists in terms of statements. Different from vulnerability detection, AVL aids in efficiently diagnosing vulnerabilities, and thus reduces the manual effort required from developers.

While static analysis tools is feasible for reporting line information, they often suffer from high false positives
[
8
]
. Therefore, recent work mainly concentrates on utilizing Deep Learning (DL) techniques to tackle the problem
[
9
,
10
,
11
,
7
,
12
]
. Initially, some of studies consider vulnerability localization as an accessory of function-level detection models, where the vulnerable statements are predicted via inner layer features of trained models. IVDetect
[
9
]
and LineVul
[
10
]
fall into this category, which measure the contributions of the features such as subgraph or token weights in the detection models. Due to the misalignment between the features and the vulnerable statements, subsequent works transform it as a node classification problem via supervised learning
[
11
,
7
,
12
]
. For example, VulTeller
[
12
]
utilizes taint analysis to prioritize the control flow paths of the vulnerable function code and encodes them into vectors via a rank-aware path encoder, among which the nodes are gathered and classified as vulnerable or not. In summary, existing approaches devote much effort into the application of structural or dependency information within the function while restricted in learning from scratch.

Meanwhile, Large Language Models (LLMs), particularly those pre-trained on vast amounts of code and its documents from repositories like GitHub, possess an inherent domain knowledge that traditional models lack. This allows them to excel in addressing various tasks related to general bugs, where fault localization
[
13
,
14
]
and program repair
[
15
,
16
]
have been extensively studied, and have demonstrated the effectiveness of LLMs. Recently, Wu et al.
[
17
]
study Java vulnerability repair capabilities of LLMs, and show the strengths over DL-based models.

However, when it comes to vulnerability localization, LLMs have yet to be touched. The missed opportunities are in twofold. First, existing approaches typically focus on traditional neural models like graph neural network
[
18
]
, the potential of LLMs remains unknown in understanding statement-level vulnerabilities. Second, there has been little comprehensive, in-depth work analyzing and comparing LLMs’ capabilities in the AVL domain. Therefore, a significant gap exists between the recent advances in LLMs and the crucial software engineering problem of AVL.

In this paper, we comprehensively investigate the capabilities of LLMs in vulnerability localization to fill the gap aforementioned. We evaluate the efficacy of 10 kinds of state-of-the-art LLMs in the context of AVL. These LLMs include both commercial LLMs like ChatGPT, and open-source LLMs like CodeLlama. For open-source LLMs, we consider all 3 types of their pre-training architectures, including encoder-only, encoder-decoder, and decoder-only ones. We focus on the following key aspects to evaluate the selected LLMs and present the main findings.

Benchmarks
. We initialize the evaluation by collecting the commonly-used dataset (BV-LOC) for AVL based on BigVul
[
19
]
, which provided by VulTeller and consists 10,811 pairs of the vulnerable C/C++ function and the line numbers responsible for the vulnerability. Nevertheless, due to the overlap of programming languages and release dates between the dataset and most LLMs, there could be a bias caused by the data leakage problem. To mitigate it, we provide a new dataset (SC-LOC) of 1,369 entries in the language of Solidity, accumulated from the recent audit activities of smart contracts.

Prompting
. As a fundamental approach, we explore the viability of directly prompting LLMs as an alternative to current DL-based methods. We utilize zero-shot and one-shot learning with a series of instruction-tuned LLMs, including ChatGPT, Llama 2, CodeLlama, and WizardCoder. For zero-shot learning, we prompt LLMs with vulnerable code alongside optimized instructions to predict the locations of vulnerabilities by line numbers. In one-shot learning, we enhance the prompt with an example to aid in prediction. Surprisingly, nearly all LLMs struggle to accurately identify vulnerability locations compared to existing DL-based methods in the BV-LOC dataset. While GPT-4 achieves a precision of 31.9%, it still lags 7 percentage points behind the most recent DL-based work. Despite this, it remains a potent alternative in scenarios with limited data availability.

Fine-tuning
. We introduce two fine-tuning approaches for enhancing open-source LLMs: discriminative fine-tuning and generative fine-tuning. Discriminative fine-tuning converts the vulnerability localization challenge into a sequence labeling task, where each code statement is classified as vulnerable or not. In generative fine-tuning, models are trained to identify and generate the numbers of lines along with the statements that are vulnerable within a given function. Compared to zero/one-shot learning, generative fine-tuning significantly outperforms, with CodeLlama-7B showing a 26% increase in F1-score over the zero-shot approach. Even more impressively, under discriminative fine-tuning, CodeLlama achieves a remarkable 63.2% leap in absolute F1-score, surpassing DL-based methods by 36.2%.

Robustness Analysis
. Our robustness assessment of LLMs spans two aspects: (1) performance across various types of vulnerabilities, as classified by Common Weakness Enumerations (CWEs), and (2) their effectiveness in Cross-Project Analysis, which evaluates performance across different software projects. Notably, LLMs show promising adaptability and accuracy in identifying varied vulnerability types (CWEs) owing to their extensive pre-training on diverse codebases. In cross-project analysis, LLMs prove their ability to generalize effectively, though there is a noticeable dip in precision, from approximately 90% to 83%.
However, LLMs show limitations in addressing specific vulnerability types, such as Out-of-bounds Read, and in dealing with the novelty presented by previously unseen vulnerabilities. This highlights the necessity for further refinement in recognizing subtle and varied patterns to keep high levels of accuracy.

Improvement Strategies
. In our examination of discriminative fine-tuning across various LLM architectures, we identify two primary challenges. First, encoder-based LLMs like CodeT5 face a limitation with a 512 token length limit. This constraint means they cannot process all input tokens simultaneously, resulting in a lack of predictions for overflow tokens. Second, decoder-based LLMs such as CodeGen, restricted by their unidirectional attention mechanism, fail to consider context from both directions. Hence, we introduce two strategic improvements. For encoder-based models, we implement a sliding window approach that processes function chunks sequentially during both fine-tuning and inference phases. For decoder-based models, we enhance their capability by incorporating right-forward embeddings to ensure a broader context is considered. The application of these strategies has proven to significantly enhance performance, yielding improvements of up to 24.1% in F1-score.

In summary, this paper makes the following contributions.

•

We undertake the first comprehensive analysis of the capabilities of 16 LLMs for AVL, including both commercial (e.g., GPT-4) and open-source (e.g., CodeLlama) models, which vary in size from 60M to 16B parameters. This analysis spans 4 paradigms of vulnerability localization, demonstrating that with appropriate fine-tuning, LLMs can significantly enhance AVL performance, with improvements up to 41.2% in F1-score.

•

An in-depth examination of LLM robustness in AVL is conducted, covering various CWEs and a cross-project setting. These analysis confirms the utility of LLMs, yet also highlights the need for continuous model refinement to address specific vulnerability types.

•

The study identifies two principal challenges that constrain the effectiveness of fine-tuned LLMs and proposes practical solutions. Through extensive experiments, these strategies are shown to substantially alleviate the identified limitations.

As presented in Figure

II-C

Fine-Tuning

Prompting LLMs for AVL, despite its advantages, often faces challenges.
In particular, the static nature of pre-trained models may not effectively keep pace with the dynamic and continuously evolving landscape of software vulnerabilities, characterized by the emergence of new vulnerability types and patterns.

Fig. 2:
The paradigms of fine-tuning LLMs for AVL

To address these challenges, fine-tuning
[
31
]
emerges as a critical step to tailor LLMs more closely to the specific requirements of AVL. In general, by training on vulnerability-specific datasets, LLMs can achieve greater accuracy in identifying vulnerabilities.
It allows LLMs to deepen their understanding of software security, moving beyond general programming knowledge to nuanced insights into code safety.

Building on the foundation laid by prompting, fine-tuning LLMs for AVL can be undertaken through both discriminative and generative methods, each method specifically designed to make LLMs aligned with the task. The architectures for the two paradigms are presented in Figure
2
.

II-C
1
Discriminative Fine-Tuning

Discriminative fine-tuning is predicated on the model’s capacity to classify individual code statements (or lines) as either vulnerable or secure, utilizing sequence labeling techniques for this purpose.

For a token sequence
X=\{x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{L}\}
of the vulnerable function, the model’s encoder or decoder component, denoted as
M_{\text{encoder}}
or
M_{\text{decoder}}
, processes
X
to yield a sequence of output vectors:

O=M_{\text{component}}(X)=\{o_{1},o_{2},\ldots,o_{L}\},

where
O
represents the output tensor with dimensions
L\times H
. Here,
L
signifies the sequence length, and
H
denotes the hidden dimension size. During the process, the contextual information is captured by the self-attention or masked self-attention mechanisms in the encoder or decoder of LLMs, where masked self-attention limits the sight to preceding part of tokens. Each output vector
o_{i}
that represents the last token of one statement is subsequently associated with a vulnerability label (i.e., 0 or 1). The optimization process employs the binary cross-entropy as the loss function.

II-C
2
Generative Fine-Tuning

Conversely, generative fine-tuning aims to equip LLMs with the ability to directly generate the specific code lines related to vulnerabilities. This concept is inspired by program slicing
[
32
]
, which focuses on extracting relevant slices from a program. Instead of the specification of slicing criteria, our approach leverages learning to isolate vulnerable lines from the function.

Initially, the encoder component transforms the input
X
into a contextualized representation
C
, that is, C =
Encoder
(X).
Subsequently, the decoder undertakes the generation of the output sequence
D=M_{\text{decoder}}(C)=\{d_{1},d_{2},\ldots,d_{k}\}
,
where
D
encapsulates the vectors indicative of the generated sequence of vulnerable line numbers. The objective of sequence prediction is to minimize the negative log-likelihood for the target sequence of lines with numbers
L=\{l_{1},l_{2},\ldots,l_{k}\}
given
X
, as illustrated in Figure
2
(b).

II-D

Context Expansion

Fine-tuning approaches for LLMs, particularly discriminative fine-tuning, have proven effective for a range of tasks in AVL. However, one significant constraint is the
input length limit
imposed by the fixed-size context window of LLMs like CodeBERT
[
25
]
, which typically restricts the number of code tokens (i.e., 512) that can be processed in a single pass. This limitation can lead to suboptimal performance when attempting to localize vulnerabilities in lengthy code where context is critical for predict
complete
locations. In that case, the truncated sequences of code causes the model to miss vulnerabilities by simply predicting with negatives.

Additionally, as illustrated in Figure
2
(a), for decoder-only LLMs like CodeGen
[
29
]
, they commonly rely on
single directional attention
, specifically masked self-attention, which inherently focuses on predicting tokens based on previous context in the left-to-right manner
[
14
]
. It may overlook the full context available in a bidirectional manner, which is often necessary for understanding code semantics and structure.

To mitigate these limitations, we propose two key enhancements: the adoption of a sliding window technique and the integration of right-forward embedding.

II-D
1
Sliding Window

The sliding window technique is designed to mitigate the input length constraints of LLMs. For a given token sequence
X
defined previously, rather than processing the entire sequence at once, the sequence is divided into overlapping windows that allow the model to handle longer inputs effectively.

Let
W
be the window size, and
S
be the step size for the sliding window. The sequence
X
is partitioned into
n
overlapping windows
X^{(i)}
, each of which is processed separately:

\displaystyle X^{(i)}

\displaystyle=\{x_{i},x_{i+1},\ldots,x_{i+W-1}\},

\displaystyle\quad i=1,S+1,2S+1,\ldots,L-W+1

Each window
X^{(i)}
is then fed into the model’s component
M_{\text{component}}
to produce the output vectors for that window
O^{(i)}
:

O^{(i)}=M_{\text{component}}(X^{(i)})=\{o_{i},o_{i+1},\ldots,o_{i+W-1}\}

The final output sequence
O
is constructed by concatenating the output vectors from each window, with appropriate handling of overlaps to maintain contextual continuity.

The overlapping segments ensure that each part of the input sequence is considered within multiple contexts, improving the model’s ability to identify vulnerabilities without being constrained by the fixed-size input window.

II-D
2
Right-forward Embedding

Expanding context understanding within LLMs like CodeGen can be achieved by integrating a bidirectional attention layer atop the model’s frozen architecture, as suggested by recent advancements
[
14
]
. However, this approach also escalates the total number of parameters, potentially leading to overfitting issues
[
33
]
due to the added complexity of transformer layers.

Alternatively, we explore a right-forward embedding strategy that enriches the representation of each token by directly incorporating insights from the hidden states ahead in the sequence. Specifically, for each token’s hidden state
o_{i}
in the output tensor
O=\{o_{1},o_{2},\ldots,o_{L}\}
, we augment it with aggregated information from subsequent hidden states. This process can be formalized as follows:

\hat{o}_{i}=\text{Concat}\left(o_{i},\ \text{aggr}\left(o_{i+1:L}\right)\right)

In this formula,
\text{aggr}(\cdot)
represents an aggregation function applied to the sequence of hidden states from
i+1
to
L
, which can be the last hidden state (
\text{last}(o_{i+1:L})
), the mean pooling (
\text{mean}(o_{i+1:L})
), or the max pooling
\text{max}(o_{i+1:L})
of these states.
The last hidden state offers clues about the eventual direction of the narrative, the mean provides an overall sense of the forthcoming content, and the max highlights the most dominant features likely to influence subsequent tokens.

By integrating the right-forward embedding strategy, we can strike a balance between extending the model’s contextual horizon and maintaining model complexity.

Experimental Setup

III-A

Research Questions

We investigate the capabilities of LLMs for AVL by answering the following research questions (RQs).

•

RQ1:
Can directly prompting LLMs rival or even surpass the effectiveness of current state-of-the-art approaches dedicated to AVL?

•

RQ2:
How effectively do fine-tuned LLMs perform in AVL, and what factors influence their performance?

•

RQ3:
How robust are fine-tuned LLMs when confronted with various types of vulnerabilities, or applied to new projects?

•

RQ4:
Can the proposed context expansion strategies effectively mitigate the theoretical limitations of narrow context in fine-tuning LLMs?

Benchmarks

BV-LOC

III-C

Implementation

For the paradigm of prompting, we make GPT-3.5 as the reference and set the input limit of all LLMs to 4,096. When receiving responses, we set the token limit to 128 since it covers a majority of cases.
To make all the LLMs comparable when fine-tuning, we set the token number to 512, which is the minimum length limit among them. Likewise, for improving discriminative fine-tuning, we set the window size and the step size to 512 and 256 respectively. For generative fine-tuning, the output token limit is 256. We fine-tune all LLMs for 10 epochs, with the batch size of 8, which can achieve a good converge of these LLMs. The learning rate is
5\times 10^{-5}
using the AdamW optimizer
[
35
]
. We adopt the LoRA technique
[
36
]
for an efficient fine-tuning of billion-level CodeGen and CodeLlama. When applying LoRA, the Lora attention dimension is set to 12, the alpha parameter for scaling is 32, and the dropout probability for Lora layers is 0.1.

All the experiments were conducted on an Ubuntu 20.04 server with one AMD EPYC 7763 64-Core Processor, 256GB RAM, and one A100 GPU with 80GB memory.

For VulTeller, since we use the same training and test datasets, we cite the reported results in this paper. For the Transformer, we use the default hyper-parameters of VELVET and train it for 10 epochs as the LLMs do. Following previous work

12

IV-A

RQ1: Effectiveness of Prompting LLMs

When analyzing the results presented in Table
III
for RQ1, which assesses the effectiveness of directly prompting LLMs for AVL against specific baselines (VulTeller and Transformer), we can draw several insights about whether they are ready for practical use.

IV-A
1
Zero-shot Learning

In the zero-shot scenario, all LLMs show varying degrees of effectiveness across the BV-LOC and SC-LOC datasets. GPT-4 stands out with a Precision of 31.9% in the BV-LOC dataset, though its Recall is significantly lower at 10.6%. Notably, GPT-3.5 achieves highest performance on the SC-LOC dataset. This suggests ChatGPT’s strength in accurately identifying vulnerabilities when training data is limited.
For the SC-LOC dataset in the zero-shot setting, WizardCoder-15B shows an impressive Recall of 64.9%, the highest among LLMs. Nonetheless, its Precision is the lowest compared to LLMs specialized in code, such as CodeLlama-7B and CodeLlama-13B. Similarly, the recall metrics for CodeLlama-7B and WizardCoder-15B on both datasets. These observations suggest that even a single example can significantly impact the model’s ability to identify vulnerabilities.

In particular, the F1-score of CodeLlama-13B decreases from 25.6% to 17.9% on SC-LOC, which may be caused by the potential overfitting to the example provided. Finding 2:

Discriminative vs. Generative Fine-Tuning

In particular, CodeLlama-7B performs much worse in handling Solidity vulnerabilities other than C/C++. Discriminative fine-tuning is highly effective for unlocking the potential of LLMs, with up to 36.2% F1-score improvement over existing approaches. Generative fine-tuning is less effective, but also shows a promising direction. However, the extensive scale of LLMs may result in underfitting when applied to small datasets.

Full vs. Partial Evaluation

A notable distinction emerges between models fine-tuned with the full ground truths of the testset and those restricted to a partial length of 512 tokens. Models constrained to a 512-token input length, including CodeBERT, GraphCodeBERT, PLBART, and CodeT5, experience a marked performance decline when unable to process code beyond this limit. For instance, CodeT5’s F1-score drops from 86.9% in the Partial setting to 63.0% in the Full setting. In contrast, CodeGen and CodeLlama demonstrate more consistent performance across varying input lengths. Notably, in the Full BV-LOC dataset, CodeLlama-7B leads with an impressive 82.0% F1-score under discriminative fine-tuning. This robust performance is attributed to its capacity to process up to 4,096 tokens during testing, covering the majority of test cases comprehensively. Furthermore, maintaining peak performance in the Partial category suggests that CodeLlama-7B has effectively captured vulnerability patterns, exhibiting strong generalization to longer contexts.

Finding 4:
Models with larger input capacities, like CodeLlama-7B, demonstrate top performance across varying input lengths, contrasting sharply with models limited to 512 tokens which exhibit significant performance drops.

Impact of Model Architectures

The results of

Likewise, encoder-decoder architectures, such as those in PLBART and CodeT5, demonstrate a balanced blend of high precision and recall, with CodeT5-base reaching an F1-score of 86.9% in BV-LOC.

VI
Threats to Validity

Internal
: A primary internal threat to our study’s validity involves the dataset utilized for evaluating the LLMs. There exists a possibility of a data leak problem, which could inadvertently bias the models’ performance. However, certain LLMs, such as CodeBERT, which were not pre-trained on languages like C/C++ or Solidity, still work well. This suggests that these models can indeed learn and generalize underlying vulnerability patterns through fine-tuning. Furthermore, our careful collection of Solidity data, which has minimal time overlap with the pre-training datasets of most evaluated LLMs including GPT-3.5 and GPT-4, can also help accurately reflect their inherent capabilities. Another internal threat concerns the design and selection of prompts used in our experiments. While we have undertaken empirical exploration to devise prompts that ensure the correct response format and content relevance, we cannot guarantee that the chosen prompts are universally optimal. Future work will involve more extensive exploration into prompt engineering techniques to refine this aspect.

External
: On the external front, the threat to validity largely centers around the re-implementation of baseline models, such as the Transformer and LLMAO. To mitigate it, we have diligently reviewed relevant literature and available open-source implementations to guide our adaptation process. The potential for variability in the broader applicability of our findings also threatens the validity. The specific configurations, datasets, and models chosen for this study may not cover all possible scenarios or reflect the diversity of real-world software development environments. Therefore, while our results shed light on LLMs’ effectiveness and AVL strategies, we advise caution in generalizing these findings across varied contexts, vulnerability types, or programming languages not explicitly covered in this research.

VII
Conclusion

This study has systematically explored the capabilities of LLMs in the domain of Automated Vulnerability Localization, revealing significant insights into model performance across various architectures, paradigms, improvement strategies, and evaluation settings. The investigation demonstrates that fine-tuning LLMs is highly effective when the training data is sufficient, otherwise it downgrades to the performance of directly prompting ChatGPT. Moreover, fine-tuned LLMs have an overall good generalizability, yet a certain types of vulnerabilities with subtle semantics and novel patterns should be carefully treated. With respect to the limitations of narrow context during fine-tuning, we propose the context expansion strategies, which can further enhance their effectiveness. Our findings also highlight the critical role of LoRA and model size in optimizing LLMs for specific AVL tasks.

Looking ahead, future work will focus on expanding the diversity and scope of training datasets to cover a broader range of vulnerability types and programming languages, enhancing models’ ability to generalize across various software projects.
