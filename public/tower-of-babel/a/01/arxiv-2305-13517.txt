Statistical Guarantees of Group-Invariant GANs
Ziyu Chen, Markos A. Katsoulakis, Luc Rey-Bellet, Wei Zhu

Abstract
This work presents the first statistical performance guarantees for group-invariant generative models. Many real data, such as images and molecules, are invariant to certain group symmetries, which can be taken advantage of to learn more efficiently as we rigorously demonstrate in this work. Here we specifically study generative adversarial networks (GANs), and quantify the gains when incorporating symmetries into the model. Group-invariant GANs are a type of GANs in which the generators and discriminators are hardwired with group symmetries. Empirical studies have shown that these networks are capable of learning group-invariant distributions with significantly improved data efficiency. In this study, we aim to rigorously quantify this improvement by analyzing the reduction in sample complexity and in the discriminator approximation error for group-invariant GANs. Our findings indicate that when learning group-invariant distributions, the number of samples required for group-invariant GANs decreases proportionally by a factor of the group size and the discriminator approximation error has a reduced lower bound. Importantly, the overall error reduction cannot be achieved merely through data augmentation on the training data. Numerical results substantiate our theory and highlight the stark contrast between learning with group-invariant GANs and using data augmentation. This work also sheds light on the study of other generative models with group symmetries, such as score-based generative models.

Introduction

The machine learning community has shown a growing interest in generative models, which aim to understand the underlying distribution of data and generate new samples from it. Among the various generative models, Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs)
[
20
]
have garnered significant attention due to their ability to learn and sample from complex data distributions. Empirical evidence shows that GANs achieve remarkable performance in diverse applications such as image synthesis and text generation
[

,
51
,
53

. Such success, however, hinges upon the availability of
abundant
training data.

Recent research has proposed to leverage the group-invariant structure of underlying distributions to improve the data efficiency of GANs
[
15
,
7
]
. Additionally, related work has explored other generative models:
[

]
proposes equivariant flows, while
[

and
[

group symmetry
observed in various real-world distributions. For instance, in the case of medical images captured without orientation alignment, the distribution should be rotation-invariant, i.e., an image and its rotated copy are
equiprobable
. Furthermore, many physical, physicochemical, and biochemical systems possess intrinsic symmetries or equivariance structures, making structured probabilistic modeling crucial
[

,

36

[

generic

is expected to create
physics-informed
generative models that are more sample-efficient and reduce computational load and complexity. However, a clear theoretical understanding of these phenomena remains to be established.

In this paper, we provide statistical

[

,
7
]

\mathbb{E}\left[\mathcal{W}_{1}(S^{\Sigma}[(g_{n,m}^{*})_{\sharp}\rho],\mu)\right]\,\text{is controlled by}\,\left(\frac{1}{\mathinner{\!\left\lvert\Sigma\right\rvert}n}\right)^{1/d},

Related work

A burgeoning body of recent research on group-equivariant neural networks
[
12
,
13
,
48
]
has demonstrated remarkable empirical success achieved through leveraging group symmetries in various supervised machine learning tasks. These accomplishments have, in turn, fostered the development of group-invariant/equivariant generative models, enabling data-efficient unsupervised learning of group-invariant distributions. Notable endeavors in this area include equivariant normalizing flows
[
5
,
8
,
19
,
28
]
, group-equivariant GANs
[
15
]
, structure-preserving GANs
[
7
]
that rely on structure-customized divergences
[
6
]
, and discrete and continuous equivariant diffusion models
[
23
,
32
]
. Despite the intuitive understanding, the extent to which and the underlying reasons why group symmetry can enhance the data efficiency of those group-invariant generative models remain largely unknown.

On the other hand, recent research
[
9
,
24
,
31
]
has made significant progress in the sample-complexity analysis of
vanilla
GANs (i.e., GANs without group-symmetric generators and discriminators) in distribution learning. These works quantify the disparity, often measured using probability divergences, between the distribution learned by GANs with finite training samples and the underlying data distribution. Compared to these works, our analysis follows a similar oracle inequality and bounds each term of the inequality separately. However, the novelty of our work lies in: 1) proposing a version of group-invariant generator and discriminator architectures; 2) treating each error term with incorporated group-invariant network architectures and utilizing the fact that the target distribution is group-invariant. While recent work
[
10
,
44
]
has explored the enhanced sample complexity of divergence estimation under group symmetry, it remains unclear how these findings can be extended to the statistical estimation of group-invariant GANs aimed at learning group-symmetric probability distributions. Moreover,
[
10
]
in particular did not consider distributions with low-dimensional support, and their technical assumptions on the group actions are notably restrictive. In our work, we extend the analysis of GANs to include cases where distributions possess low-dimensional structure and we adopt a more flexible approach towards the assumptions on finite group actions compared to those in
[
10
]
.

While some other generative models such as diffusion models have gained traction recently, GANs as one-shot methods are still widely used in certain applications where computational efficiency and real-time generation are critical
[
33
,
26
,
41
]
. Moreover, the mathematical formulation of GANs is essentially the minimization of variational divergences. As the objective of general generative modeling can be viewed as minimizing the discrepancy between the generated and the target distribution in terms of certain divergences, our work can shed light on the study of other generative models by considering the performance guarantees of invariant models evaluated by symmetry-adapted divergences. For example, in recent work
[
11
]
, we provide performance guarantees for score-based generative models with symmetries, inspired by this work.

Background and problem setup

3.1
Integral Probability Metrics and GANs

Let
\mathcal{X}
be a measurable space, and
\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X})
be the set of probability measures on
\mathcal{X}
. Given
\Gamma\subset\mathcal{M}_{b}(\mathcal{X})
, where
\mathcal{M}_{b}(\mathcal{X})
is the space of bounded measurable functions on
\mathcal{X}
, the
\Gamma
-Integral Probability Metric (
\Gamma
-IPM)
[
34
,
43
]
between
\nu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X})
and
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X})
is defined as

d_{\Gamma}(\nu,\mu)\coloneqq\sup_{\gamma\in\Gamma}\left\{\mathbb{E}_{\nu}[\gamma]-\mathbb{E}_{\mu}[\gamma]\right\}.

(3)

For example, if
\Gamma=\text{Lip}_{H}(\mathcal{X})
, the space of
H
-Lipschitz functions
on
\mathcal{X}
, then by the Kantorovich-Rubinstein duality, we have

\mathcal{W}_{1}(\nu,\mu)=H^{-1}d_{\Gamma}(\nu,\mu).

(4)

For simplicity, we omit the factor
H^{-1}
in the following presentation.
When
\Gamma
is a class of neural networks,
d_{\Gamma}(\nu,\mu)
defined by (
3
) is called the “neural network distance”
[
2
]
.

GANs, originally proposed by Goodfellow et al.
[
20
]
, learn to approximate a target data distribution
\mu
by generating samples from a noise source distribution
\rho
(typically chosen as uniform or Gaussian) through a two-player game. More specifically, GANs aim to solve the following minmax problem,

\min_{g\in\mathcal{G}}\max_{f\in\mathcal{D}}\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mu}[f(x)]-\mathbb{E}_{z\sim\rho}[f(g(z))],

(5)

where
\mathcal{G}
is a class of functions called
generators
and
\mathcal{D}
is a class of functions called
discriminators
. This minmax problem can be equivalently formulated as

\min_{g\in\mathcal{G}}d_{\mathcal{D}}(\mu,g_{\sharp}\rho),

where
g_{\sharp}\rho\coloneqq\rho\circ g^{-1}
is the push-forward measure of
\rho
under
g
. For example, if
\mathcal{D}=\text{Lip}_{1}(\mathcal{X})
is the class of 1-Lipschitz functions, this minmax problem is the formulation of Wasserstein GANs (W-GANs)
[
1
]
.

Suppose we have only finite samples
\{x_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
from the target
\mu
and
\{z_{i}\}_{i=1}^{m}
from the source
\rho
, we define the optimal generator as the minimizer of the following optimization problem
[
9
,
24
]
,

g_{n,m}=\argmin_{g\in\mathcal{G}}d_{\mathcal{D}}(g_{\sharp}\widehat{\rho}_{m},\widehat{\mu}_{n}),

(6)

where
\widehat{\mu}_{n}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{x_{i}}
is the empirical distribution of the target
\mu
and
\widehat{\rho}_{m}=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\delta_{z_{i}}
is the empirical distribution of the source
\rho
.

3.2
Group invariance and symmetrization operators

A
group
is a set
\Sigma
equipped with a group product satisfying the properties of associativity, identity, and invertibility. Given a group
\Sigma
and a set
\mathcal{X}
, a map
\theta\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\Sigma\times\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{X}
is called a
group action on
\mathcal{X}
if
\theta_{\sigma}\coloneqq\theta(\sigma,\cdot)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{X}
is a bijection on
\mathcal{X}
for any
\sigma\in\Sigma
, and
\theta_{\sigma_{2}}\circ\theta_{\sigma_{1}}=\theta_{\sigma_{2}\cdot\sigma_{1}}
,
\forall\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2}\in\Sigma
. If the context is clear, we will abbreviate
\theta(\sigma,x)
as
\sigma x
.

A function
\gamma\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
is said to be
\Sigma
-invariant
if it is constant over group orbits of
\mathcal{X}
, i.e.,
\gamma\circ\theta_{\sigma}=\gamma
for all
\sigma\in\Sigma
. Let
\Gamma\subset\mathcal{M}_{b}(\mathcal{X})
be a set of bounded measurable functions
\gamma\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
. We define its subset,
\Gamma_{\Sigma}\subset\Gamma
, of
\Sigma
-invariant functions as

\Gamma_{\Sigma}\coloneqq\{\gamma\in\Gamma\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\gamma\circ\theta_{\sigma}=\gamma,\forall\sigma\in\Sigma\}.

(7)

Likewise, a probability measure
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X})
is said to be
\Sigma
-invariant
if
\mu=(\theta_{\sigma})_{\sharp}\mu
for all
\sigma\in\Sigma
. We define the set of all
\Sigma
-invariant distributions on
\mathcal{X}
as

\mathcal{P}_{\Sigma}(\mathcal{X})\coloneqq\{\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X})\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mu~\text{is}~\Sigma\text{-invariant}\}.

(8)

Finally, following
[
7
,
10
]
, we define two
symmetrization operators
,
S_{\Sigma}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathcal{M}_{b}(\mathcal{X})\to\mathcal{M}_{b}(\mathcal{X})
and
S^{\Sigma}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X})\to\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X})
, on functions and probability measures, respectively, as

S_{\Sigma}[\gamma](x)\coloneqq\int_{\Sigma}\gamma(\sigma x)\mu_{\Sigma}(d\sigma),~\forall\gamma\in\mathcal{M}_{b}(\mathcal{X}),

(9)

\mathbb{E}_{S^{\Sigma}[\mu]}\gamma\coloneqq\mathbb{E}_{\mu}S_{\Sigma}[\gamma],~\forall\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X}),\forall\gamma\in\mathcal{M}_{b}(\mathcal{X}),

(10)

where
\mu_{\Sigma}
is the unique Haar probability measure on a compact Hausdorff topological group
\Sigma

[
18
]
. One can easily verify that
S_{\Sigma}
and
S^{\Sigma}
are, respectively,
projection operators
onto their corresponding invariant subsets
\Gamma_{\Sigma}\subset\Gamma
and
\mathcal{P}_{\Sigma}(\mathcal{X})\subset\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X})

[
7
]
. One of the main results of
[
7
]
that motivates the construction of group-invariant GANs is summarized in the following Lemma.

Lemma 1

(paraphrased from
[
7
]
)
.

If
S_{\Sigma}[\Gamma]\subset\Gamma
and
\nu,\mu\in\mathcal{P}(X)
, then

d_{\Gamma}(S^{\Sigma}[\nu],S^{\Sigma}[\mu])=d_{\Gamma_{\Sigma}}(\nu,\mu)=\sup_{\gamma\in\Gamma_{\Sigma}}\left\{\mathbb{E}_{\nu}[\gamma]-\mathbb{E}_{\mu}[\gamma]\right\}.

In particular, if
\nu,\mu\in\mathcal{P}_{\Sigma}(\mathcal{X})
are
\Sigma
-invariant, then
d_{\Gamma}(\nu,\mu)=d_{\Gamma_{\Sigma}}(\nu,\mu)
.

In other words, to “tell the difference” between two
\Sigma
-invariant distributions
\nu,\mu\in\mathcal{P}_{\Sigma}(\mathcal{X})
, one only needs to optimize over all
\Sigma
-invariant discriminators
\gamma\in\Gamma_{\Sigma}
.
Group-invariant GANs
are thus a type of GANs where the discriminators are all
\Sigma
-invariant and the generators are parameterized in such a way that the generated distributions are always
\Sigma
-invariant. We will further elaborate on the exact construction of group-invariant GANs in Section
3.4
.

3.3
Assumptions on the group actions

We assume
\mathcal{X}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a bounded subset of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
equipped with the Euclidean metric
\|\cdot\|_{2}
. In addition, we make the following assumptions on the group
\Sigma
and its action on
\mathcal{X}
.

Assumption 1
.

The group
\Sigma
and its action on
\mathcal{X}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
satisfy

1.
\Sigma
is finite, i.e.,
\mathinner{\!\left\lvert\Sigma\right\rvert}<\infty
;

2.
\Sigma
acts linearly on
\mathcal{X}
; that is,
\sigma(ax_{1}+bx_{2})=a\sigma x_{1}+b\sigma x_{2},\,\forall x_{1},x_{2}\in\mathcal{X},\sigma\in\Sigma,a,b\in\mathbb{R}
;

3. the
\Sigma
-actions on
\mathcal{X}
are isometric; that is,

\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\sigma x_{1}-\sigma x_{2}\right\rVert}_{2}=\mathinner{\!\left\lVert x_{1}-x_{2}\right\rVert}_{2},\,\forall x_{1},x_{2}\in\mathcal{X},\sigma\in\Sigma.

Remark 1
.

In terms of the third condition, one can instead assume that the
\Sigma
-actions on
\mathcal{X}
are 1-Lipschitz: that is,
\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\sigma x_{1}-\sigma x_{2}\right\rVert}_{2}\leq\mathinner{\!\left\lVert x_{1}-x_{2}\right\rVert}_{2},\,\forall x_{1},x_{2}\in\mathcal{X},\sigma\in\Sigma
. Weyl’s unitary trick tells us that for every finite group action, there exists a basis in which the action is isometric, such that the third condition can always be satisfied with a simple change of basis.

The
\Sigma
action on
\mathcal{X}
induces a
fundamental domain

\mathcal{X}_{0}\subset\mathcal{X}
, a terminology which we adopt from
[
10
]
.

Definition 1

(Fundamental domain)
.

A subset
\mathcal{X}_{0}\subset\mathcal{X}
is called a fundamental domain of
\mathcal{X}
under the
\Sigma
-action if for any
x\in\mathcal{X}
, there exists a unique
x_{0}\in\mathcal{X}_{0}
such that
x=\sigma x_{0}
for some
\sigma\in\Sigma
.

We remark that the choice of the fundamental domain
\mathcal{X}_{0}
is generally not unique. Following
[
10
]
, we abuse the notation
\mathcal{X}=\Sigma\times\mathcal{X}_{0}
to denote
\mathcal{X}_{0}
is a fundamental domain of
\mathcal{X}
under the
\Sigma
-action. Given a specific choice of the fundamental domain
\mathcal{X}_{0}
, we define the projection
T_{0}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{X}_{0}
as

\displaystyle T_{0}(x)\coloneqq y\in\mathcal{X}_{0},~\text{if}~y=\sigma x~\text{for some}~\sigma\in\Sigma.

(11)

In other words,
T_{0}
maps any
x\in\mathcal{X}
to its unique orbit representative in
\mathcal{X}_{0}
. Given a
\Sigma
-invariant distribution
\mu\in\mathcal{P}_{\Sigma}(\mathcal{X})
on
\mathcal{X}
, the map
T_{0}
induces a distribution
\mu_{\mathcal{X}_{0}}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X}_{0})
on the fundamental domain
\mathcal{X}_{0}
defined by

\mu_{\mathcal{X}_{0}}=(T_{0})_{\sharp}\mu.

(12)

We next introduce an important concept, the
covering number
, which appears in many proofs throughout our paper.

Definition 2

(Covering number)
.

Let
(\mathcal{X},\tau)
be a metric space. A subset
S\subset\mathcal{X}
is called an
\epsilon
-cover of
\mathcal{X}
if for any
x\in\mathcal{X}
there is an
s\in S
such that
\tau(s,x)\leq\epsilon
. Define the
\epsilon
-covering number of
\mathcal{X}
as

\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{X},\epsilon,\tau)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\min\left\{\mathinner{\!\left\lvert S\right\rvert}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}S\text{ is an }\epsilon\text{-cover of }\mathcal{X}\right\}.

When
\tau(x,y)=\|x-y\|_{2}
is the Euclidean metric in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we abbreviate
\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{X},\epsilon,\tau)
as
\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{X},\epsilon)
.

With Definition
1
and Definition
2
, we specify below our second technical assumption on the group action over
\mathcal{X}
, which is motivated by
[
42
,
10
]
.

Assumption 2
.

There exists some constant
\epsilon_{\Sigma}
and a set-valued function
A_{0}(\epsilon)
, such that for any
\epsilon\in(0,\epsilon_{\Sigma})
, we have
A_{0}(\epsilon)\subset\mathcal{X}_{0}
, and

1.
\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\sigma x-\sigma^{\prime}x^{\prime}\right\rVert}_{2}>2\epsilon
,
\forall x,x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}_{0}\backslash A_{0}(\epsilon),\sigma\neq\sigma^{\prime}\in\Sigma
;

2.
\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\sigma x-\sigma x^{\prime}\right\rVert}_{2}=\mathinner{\!\left\lVert x-x^{\prime}\right\rVert}_{2}
,
\forall x,x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}_{0}\backslash A_{0}(\epsilon),\sigma\in\Sigma
.

Moreover, for some
0\leq r\leq d
, we have

\limsup_{\epsilon\to 0^{+}}\frac{\mathcal{N}(A_{0}(\epsilon),\epsilon)}{\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{X}_{0},\epsilon)\epsilon^{r}}<\infty.

(13)

Remark 2
.

Assumption
2
is notably less restrictive compared to the sufficient condition of Theorem 3 in
[

]
, which can be viewed as a special case of Assumption
2
when
A(\epsilon)=\emptyset
and

. Such relaxation is crucial to accommodate symmetries such as mirror reflections and circular rotations in the following examples.

(Mirror reflection in
\mathbb{R}^{2}

.

in Assumption

in Assumption

. See the right subfigure in Figure

.

The introduction of

3.4
Network architecture for group-invariant GANs

In GANs, both the generator class and the discriminator class are parameterized by neural networks. In this paper, we consider fully-connected feed-forward ReLU networks as the architecture for both generators and discriminators.

A fully-connected feed-forward ReLU neural network,
\phi\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathbb{R}^{d_{0}}\to\mathbb{R}^{d_{L+1}}
, can be formulated as

\phi(x)=W_{L}\cdot\text{ReLU}(W_{L-1}\cdots\text{ReLU}(W_{0}x+b_{0})\cdots+b_{L-1})+b_{L},

(14)

where
W_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{i+1}\times d_{i}}
and
b_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{i+1}}
are network weights,
i=0,1,\dots,L
, and the activation function is given by
\text{ReLU}(x)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\max\{0,x\}
,
x\in\mathbb{R}
. We call the numbers
W=\max\{d_{1},\dots,d_{L}\}
and

the width and the depth of the neural network
\phi
, respectively. When the input and output dimensions are clear from contexts, we denote by
\mathcal{NN}(W,L,N,K)
a ReLU
network architecture
that
consists of fully-connected feed-forward ReLU neural networks with width at most
W
,
depth at most
L
, the number of weights at most
N
and the magnitude of entries of
W_{i}
and
b_{i}
no more than
K
. We also write
\mathcal{NN}(W,L)
or
\mathcal{NN}(W,L,N)
when either
N
or
K
is not specified.

Let
\mathcal{D}_{NN}
and
\mathcal{G}_{NN}
, respectively, denote a class of discriminators and a class of generators realized by fully-connected ReLU networks, which are generally not
\Sigma
-invariant. We explain next how to construct
\Sigma
-invariant discriminators and generators from given
\mathcal{D}_{NN}
and
\mathcal{G}_{NN}
.

Since
\Sigma
acts linearly on
\mathcal{X}
(Assumption

), there is a matrix representation
W_{\sigma}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
for any
\sigma\in\Sigma
such that
\sigma x=W_{\sigma}x,\forall x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. We can formulate
\Sigma
-invariant discriminators, denoted by
\mathcal{D}_{NN}^{\Sigma}
, using the function symmetrization operator
S_{\Sigma}
introduced in (

).

Definition 3

(
\Sigma
-invariant discriminators)
.

For any class of discriminators with ReLU network architecture
\mathcal{D}_{NN}=\mathcal{NN}(W,L,N,K)
, its corresponding class of
\Sigma
-invariant discriminators
\mathcal{D}_{NN}^{\Sigma}
is defined as

\mathcal{D}_{NN}^{\Sigma}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\left\{\frac{1}{\mathinner{\!\left\lvert\Sigma\right\rvert}}\sum_{i=1}^{\mathinner{\!\left\lvert\Sigma\right\rvert}}\phi(\sigma_{i}^{-1}x)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\phi(x)\in\mathcal{D}_{NN}\right\}.

(15)

That is,
\mathcal{D}_{NN}^{\Sigma}
consists of neural networks of the form

\displaystyle\phi(x)=\frac{1}{\mathinner{\!\left\lvert\Sigma\right\rvert}}\sum_{i=1}^{\mathinner{\!\left\lvert\Sigma\right\rvert}}W_{L}\cdot\text{ReLU}(W_{L-1}\cdots\text{ReLU}(W_{0}(W_{\sigma_{i}}x)+b_{0})\cdots+b_{L-1})+b_{L},

(16)

where
\{W_{i},b_{i}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}i=0,\dots,L\}
is the same set of weights as that of
\mathcal{D}_{NN}
.

By Definition
3
, it is straightforward to verify that
S_{\Sigma}\left[\mathcal{D}_{NN}^{\Sigma}\right]=\mathcal{D}_{NN}^{\Sigma}
.

Remark 4
.

definition

3
requires
\mathinner{\!\left\lvert\Sigma\right\rvert}
passes through the network; however, in numerous practical scenarios of generative modeling, particularly in medical imaging, the foremost limitation is typically the scarcity of sufficient data, rather than computational resources. Consequently, while

3
increases computational demands, it represents a necessary trade-off to effectively address this significant challenge in sample-constrained applications.

Similarly, the
\Sigma
-invariant generators can be defined using the distribution symmetrization operator

introduced in (

).

Definition 4

(
\Sigma
-invariant generators)
.

For any class of generators with ReLU network architecture

, its corresponding class of
\Sigma
-invariant generators

is defined as

g_{\Sigma}\in\mathcal{G}_{NN}^{\Sigma}\iff\exists g(x)\in\mathcal{G}_{NN}~~s.t.~~g_{\Sigma}(\rho)=S^{\Sigma}[g_{\sharp}\rho],~~\forall~\text{noise source distribution}~\rho.

In other words, we add a
\Sigma
-symmetrization layer

that draws a random

according to the Haar measure

and transforms the output

of

to

In this paper, we consider learning a

-invariant target data distribution

-invariant GANs whose generators and discriminators are both

-invariant and the noise source probability measure

g_{n,m}^{*}=\argmin_{g\in\mathcal{G}_{NN}}d_{\mathcal{D}_{NN}^{\Sigma}}(S^{\Sigma}[g_{\sharp}\widehat{\rho}_{m}],\widehat{\mu}_{n}).

Note that

is a set of

-invariant distributions in

for any easy-to-sample source distribution

\mathbb{R}
.

4
Learning distributions in Euclidean spaces

In this section, we quantify the error between the generated distribution
S^{\Sigma}[(g_{n,m}^{*})_{\sharp}\rho]
and the target data distribution
\mu\in\mathcal{P}_{\Sigma}(\mathcal{X})
in terms of the Wasserstein-1 metric; that is,
\Gamma=\text{Lip}_{H}(\mathcal{X})
and we estimate
d_{\Gamma}(S^{\Sigma}[(g_{n,m}^{*})_{\sharp}\rho],\mu)
. All of the proofs can be found in the appendix. Since the value of the
\Gamma
-IPM does not change if we replace
\Gamma
by
\Gamma+c
for any
c\in\mathbb{R}
, by Lemma 2 in
[
10
]
, it is equivalent to replace
\Gamma
by
\Gamma=\{f\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}f\in\text{Lip}_{H}(\mathcal{X}),\mathinner{\!\left\lVert f\right\rVert}_{\infty}\leq M\}
if
\mathcal{X}
is bounded, where
M=H\cdot\text{diam}(\mathcal{X}_{0})
.

The proof of Theorem
1
hinges upon the following error decomposition based on the proof of Lemma 9 in
[
24
]
and Lemma
1
.
Compared to
[
24
]
, our analysis makes use of the group-invariant architectures of the generator and discriminator as well as the fact that the target distribution has group-invariant structure so that each error term will reveal the gain or no-extra-cost of group invariance.

Lemma 2

(Error decomposition)
.

Suppose
g_{n,m}^{*}
is the optimal GAN generator from (
17
), then for any function class
\Gamma
defined on
\mathcal{X}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we have

\displaystyle d_{\Gamma}(S^{\Sigma}[(g_{n,m}^{*})_{\sharp}\rho],\mu)\leq\underbrace{2\sup_{f\in\Gamma_{\Sigma}}\inf_{f_{\omega}\in\mathcal{D}_{NN}^{\Sigma}}\mathinner{\!\left\lVert f-f_{\omega}\right\rVert}_{\infty}}_{\begin{subarray}{c}\text{Invariant discriminator approximation error: $\Delta_{1}$}\end{subarray}}

\displaystyle\quad+\underbrace{\inf_{g\in\mathcal{G}_{NN}}d_{\mathcal{D}_{NN}^{\Sigma}}(S^{\Sigma}[g_{\sharp}\rho],\widehat{\mu}_{n})}_{\begin{subarray}{c}\text{Invariant generator approximation error: $\Delta_{2}$}\end{subarray}}+\underbrace{d_{\Gamma_{\Sigma}}(\widehat{\mu}_{n},\mu)}_{\text{Statistical error from target: $\Delta_{3}$}}

\displaystyle\quad+\underbrace{2d_{\mathcal{D}_{NN}^{\Sigma}\circ\mathcal{G}_{NN}}(\rho,\widehat{\rho}_{m})}_{\text{Statistical error from source: $\Delta_{4}$}}.

We provide lemmas that bound each of
\Delta_{i}
’s.

Lemma 3

(
\Sigma
-invariant discriminator approximation error)
.

Let

\Gamma=\{f\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}f\in\text{Lip}_{H}(\mathcal{X}),\mathinner{\!\left\lVert f\right\rVert}_{\infty}\leq H\cdot\text{diam}(\mathcal{X}_{0})\}.

For any
\epsilon\in(0,1)
, there exists a class of
\Sigma
-invariant discriminators

\mathcal{D}_{NN}^{\Sigma}=S_{\Sigma}\left[\mathcal{NN}(W_{1},L_{1},N_{1},K_{1})\right]

with
L_{1}\lesssim\log(1/\epsilon)
,
N_{1}\lesssim\epsilon^{-d}\log(1/\epsilon)
and
K_{1}
depends on
H,\sup_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\mathinner{\!\left\lVert x\right\rVert}_{\infty}
and
\text{diam}(\mathcal{X}_{0})
, such that

\sup_{f\in\Gamma_{\Sigma}}\inf_{f_{\omega}\in\mathcal{D}_{NN}^{\Sigma}}\mathinner{\!\left\lVert f-f_{\omega}\right\rVert}_{\infty}\leq\epsilon.

In addition to
Lemma

3
, we take a closer look at each individual approximation to illustrate the importance of using
\Sigma
-invariant discriminators in the next proposition, whose proof can be found in
Section

8.2
.

Proposition 1
.

Suppose
f
is a
\Sigma
-invariant function and
f_{\omega}
is not necessarily
\Sigma
-invariant. Assuming
\Sigma
has unitary matrix representations, i.e., the Jacobian of each
\theta_{\sigma}
is a unitary matrix, then we have

\displaystyle\mathinner{\!\left\lVert f-f_{\omega}\right\rVert}_{\infty}

\displaystyle\geq\sqrt{\frac{1}{\text{vol}(\mathcal{X})}\left(\mathinner{\!\left\lVert f-S_{\Sigma}[f_{\omega}]\right\rVert}_{L^{2}(\mathcal{X})}^{2}+\mathinner{\!\left\lVert f_{\omega}-S_{\Sigma}[f_{\omega}]\right\rVert}_{L^{2}(\mathcal{X})}^{2}\right)}

\displaystyle\geq\sqrt{\frac{1}{\text{vol}(\mathcal{X})}}\mathinner{\!\left\lVert f_{\omega}-S_{\Sigma}[f_{\omega}]\right\rVert}_{L^{2}(\mathcal{X})}.

Remark 7

(Deviation from invariance)
.

The right-hand side of
Proposition

1
can be viewed as a measurement of the non-invariance of
f_{\omega}
and can be large if
f_{\omega}
is “far from” being
\Sigma
-invariant. This lower bound vanishes whenever
f_{\omega}
is
\Sigma
-invariant, which suggests that we use
\Sigma
-invariant discriminators
\mathcal{D}_{NN}^{\Sigma}
instead of
\mathcal{D}_{NN}
to achieve a smaller discriminator approximation error. See also
Figure

4
for numerical illustrations. This measurement for the deviation from invariance seems general in generative models with symmetries. For example,
[
11
]
uses a similar notion to measure the non-equivariance of a vector field in the context of score-based generative models for score-matching approximations.

Lemma 4

(
\Sigma
-invariant generator approximation error)
.

Suppose
W_{2}\geq 7d+1,L_{2}\geq 2
. Let
\rho
be an absolutely
continuous probability measure on
\mathbb{R}
. If
n\leq\frac{W_{2}-d-1}{2}\lfloor\frac{W_{2}-d-1}{6d}\rfloor\lfloor\frac{L_{2}}{2}\rfloor+2
, we have

\inf_{g\in\mathcal{G}_{NN}}d_{\mathcal{D}_{NN}^{\Sigma}}(S^{\Sigma}[g_{\sharp}\rho],\widehat{\mu}_{n})=0
, where
\mathcal{G}_{NN}=\mathcal{NN}(W_{2},L_{2})
.

Remark 8
.

We also refer to Theorem 4.6 in
[
7
]
for the necessity of using
\Sigma
-invariant generators: suppose the discriminator architecture is
\Sigma
-invariant. Since
d_{\mathcal{D}_{NN}^{\Sigma}}(g_{\sharp}\rho,\widehat{\mu}_{n})=d_{\mathcal{D}_{NN}}(S^{\Sigma}[g_{\sharp}\rho],S^{\Sigma}[\widehat{\mu}_{n}])
, the generator may only learn to generate distributions whose symmetrization is the target distribution, thus causing “mode collapse”. That is,
d_{\mathcal{D}_{NN}^{\Sigma}}
is a probability metric on
\mathcal{P}_{\Sigma}(\mathcal{X})
rather than
\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X})
. We also refer to qualitative numerical examples of this phenomenon in
[
7
]
.

Lemma 5

(Statistical error from the target)
.

Let
\mathcal{X}=\Sigma\times\mathcal{X}_{0}
be a subset of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
satisfying the conditions in Assumptions
1
and
2
. Let
\Gamma=\{f\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}f\in\text{Lip}_{H}(\mathcal{X}),\mathinner{\!\left\lVert f\right\rVert}_{\infty}\leq M\}
. Suppose
\mu
is a
\Sigma
-invariant probability measure on
\mathcal{X}
. Then we have

(1) If
d\geq 2
, then for any
s>0
and
n
sufficiently large, we have

\mathbb{E}[d_{\Gamma_{\Sigma}}(\widehat{\mu}_{n},\mu)]\leq\quad C_{\mathcal{X},H,d,s}\left(\frac{1}{\mathinner{\!\left\lvert\Sigma\right\rvert}n}\right)^{\frac{1}{d+s}}+o\left(\left(\frac{1}{n}\right)^{\frac{1}{d+s}}\right),

where the coefficient
C_{\mathcal{X},H,d,s}
of the dominating term does not depend on
\Sigma
or
n
;

(2) If
d=1
and
\mathcal{X}_{0}
is an interval, then for
n
sufficiently large, we have

\mathbb{E}[d_{\Gamma_{\Sigma}}(\widehat{\mu}_{n},\mu)]\leq\frac{cH\cdot\text{diam}(\mathcal{X}_{0})}{\sqrt{n}}+o\left(\frac{1}{n}\right),

where
c>0
is an absolute constant independent of
\mathcal{X}
and
\mathcal{X}_{0}
.

Lemma 6

(Statistical error from the source)
.

Suppose
L_{1}\lesssim\log n
,
N_{1}\lesssim n\log n
,
W_{2}^{2}L_{2}\lesssim n
, then we have
d_{\mathcal{D}_{NN}^{\Sigma}\circ\mathcal{G}_{NN}}(\rho,\widehat{\rho}_{m})\lesssim\sqrt{\frac{n^{2}\log^{2}n\log m}{m}}
. In particular, if
m\gtrsim n^{2+2/d}\log^{3}n
, we have
\mathbb{E}[d_{\mathcal{D}_{NN}^{\Sigma}\circ\mathcal{G}_{NN}}(\rho,\widehat{\rho}_{m})]\lesssim n^{-1/d}
.

With those lemmas, we now state and prove the main theorem.

Theorem 1

(Main Theorem)
.

Let
\mathcal{X}=\Sigma\times\mathcal{X}_{0}
be a compact subset of
\mathbb{R}^{d}(d\geq 2)
satisfying Assumptions
1
and
2
, and let
\Gamma=\text{Lip}_{H}(\mathcal{X})
. Suppose the target distribution
\mu
is
\Sigma
-invariant on
\mathcal{X}
and the noise source distribution
\rho
is absolutely continuous on
\mathbb{R}
. Then there exists
\Sigma
-invariant discriminator architecture
\mathcal{D}_{NN}^{\Sigma}=S_{\Sigma}[\mathcal{D}_{NN}]
, where
\mathcal{D}_{NN}=\mathcal{NN}(W_{1},L_{1},N_{1})
as defined in (
16
) with
N_{1}\lesssim n\log n
and
L_{1}\lesssim\log n
, and
\Sigma
-invariant generator architecture
\mathcal{G}_{NN}^{\Sigma}
, where
\mathcal{G}_{NN}=\mathcal{NN}(W_{2},L_{2})
, with
W_{2}^{2}L_{2}\lesssim n
, such that if
m\gtrsim n^{2+2/d}\log^{3}n
, we have

\mathbb{E}\left[d_{\Gamma}(S^{\Sigma}[(g_{n,m}^{*})_{\sharp}\rho],\mu)\right]\leq C_{\mathcal{X},H,d,s}\left(\frac{1}{\mathinner{\!\left\lvert\Sigma\right\rvert}n}\right)^{\frac{1}{d+s}}+o\left(\left(\frac{1}{n}\right)^{\frac{1}{d+s}}\right),

(18)

for any
s>0
, where the coefficient
C_{\mathcal{X},H,d,s}
of the dominating term does not depend on
\Sigma
or
n
.
See (
8.4
) in the Appendix for details.

Proof.

We can choose
\epsilon\simeq n^{-1/d}
in Lemma
3
with
L_{1}\lesssim\log n^{1/d}\simeq\log n
and
N_{1}\lesssim n\log n
, so that
\sup_{f\in\Gamma_{\Sigma}}\inf_{f_{\omega}\in\mathcal{D}_{NN}^{\Sigma}}\mathinner{\!\left\lVert f-f_{\omega}\right\rVert}_{\infty}\lesssim n^{-1/d}
. On the other hand, we can make
\inf_{g\in\mathcal{G}_{NN}}d_{\mathcal{D}_{NN}^{\Sigma}}(S^{\Sigma}[g_{\sharp}\rho],\widehat{\mu}_{n})=0
with

\mathbb{E}[d_{\Gamma_{\Sigma}}(\widehat{\mu}_{n},\mu)]\lesssim_{s}(\mathinner{\!\left\lvert\Sigma\right\rvert}n)^{-1/(d+s)}
and
\mathbb{E}[d_{\mathcal{D}_{NN}^{\Sigma}\circ\mathcal{G}_{NN}}(\rho,\widehat{\rho}_{m})]\lesssim n^{-1/d}
by Lemma
5
and Lemma
6
respectively.
∎

Remark 9

(Data efficiency)
.

This improved training sample complexity can be interpreted as follows: For vanilla GANs without built-in group symmetry, the group size is
1
, representing the baseline sample complexity. However, for a group-invariant GAN with nontrivial group
\mathinner{\!\left\lvert\Sigma\right\rvert}>1
, its performance when utilizing

\mathinner{\!\left\lvert\Sigma\right\rvert}n
i.i.d. training samples.

5
Target distributions with low-dimensional structure

While Theorem
1
explains the improved data efficiency of group-invariant GANs, the error bound increases as the ambient dimension
d
increases. However, empirical evidence suggests that numerous probability distributions found in nature are concentrated on submanifolds of low
intrinsic dimensions
. In light of this, we present an enhanced analysis of sample complexity for group-invariant GANs, specifically when learning distributions of low intrinsic dimension under group symmetry. Suppose the target distribution
\mu
is supported on some compact
d^{*}
-dimensional smooth submanifold of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
We first prove the following bound for the covering number of a compact smooth submanifold of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, which reflects the intrinsic dimension of the submanifold.

Lemma 7
.

Let
\mathcal{M}
be a compact
d^{*}
-dimensional smooth submanifold of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, then for small
\epsilon>0
, we have

\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{M},\epsilon)\leq C_{\mathcal{M}}(\frac{1}{\epsilon})^{d^{*}},

where the metric in the covering number is the Euclidean metric on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and the constant
C_{\mathcal{M}}
depends on
\mathcal{M}
.

Proof.

For any point
x\in\mathcal{M}
, there exists a chart
(U_{x},\varphi_{x})
, where
U_{x}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
is an open set containing
x
and
\varphi_{x}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}U_{x}\cap\mathcal{M}\to\varphi_{x}(U_{x}\cap\mathcal{M})\subset\mathbb{R}^{d^{*}}
is a diffeomorphism such that
\varphi_{x}(U_{x}\cap\mathcal{M})
is an open set of
\mathbb{R}^{d^{*}}
. Without loss of generality, we can assume
\varphi_{x}(x)=0\in\mathbb{R}^{d^{*}}
. In addition, we can assume that
\varphi_{x}(U_{x}\cap\mathcal{M})=B_{d^{*}}(0,R_{x})
for some
R_{x}>0
, and
\mathinner{\!\left\lVert D\varphi_{x}^{-1}\right\rVert}_{2}\leq c_{x}
on
B_{d^{*}}(0,R_{x})
for some constant
c_{x}
, where
D\varphi_{x}^{-1}
is the Jacobian matrix of
\varphi_{x}^{-1}
. (This is possible since
\varphi_{x}(U_{x})
is open, we can pick some
r>0
such that
B_{d^{*}}(0,r)\subset\varphi_{x}(U_{x})
, so that
\overline{B_{d^{*}}(0,r/2)}
is compact, and
\mathinner{\!\left\lVert D\varphi_{x}^{-1}\right\rVert}_{2}
is bounded on
\overline{B_{d^{*}}(0,r/2)}
. Then we can set
U_{x}\cap\mathcal{M}=\varphi_{x}^{-1}(B_{d^{*}}(0,r/4))
.)

Let
\epsilon_{x}\leq\frac{R_{x}}{3}
, then any open ball in
\mathbb{R}^{d^{*}}
that intersects
B_{d^{*}}(0,\epsilon_{x})
must lie within
B_{d^{*}}(0,R_{x})
. For any small
\epsilon\in(0,1)
, we know that
B_{d^{*}}(0,\epsilon_{x})
can be covered by
\frac{(\epsilon_{x}+\epsilon/2)^{d^{*}}}{(\epsilon/2)^{d^{*}}}
balls with radius
\epsilon
in
\mathbb{R}^{d^{*}}
(c.f. Proposition 4.2.12 in
[
47
]
). Moreover, since
\mathinner{\!\left\lVert D\varphi_{x}^{-1}\right\rVert}_{2}\leq c_{x}
,
\varphi_{x}^{-1}
maps each of these
\epsilon
-balls to some open subset of
U_{x}
whose diameter is no more than
c_{x}\epsilon
and hence contained in some open ball of radius
2c_{x}\epsilon
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Therefore,
\varphi_{x}^{-1}(B_{d^{*}}(0,\epsilon_{x}))
can be covered by
\frac{(\epsilon_{x}+\epsilon/2)^{d^{*}}}{(\epsilon/2)^{d^{*}}}
balls with radius
2c_{x}\epsilon
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. By a rescaling,
\varphi_{x}^{-1}(B_{d^{*}}(0,\epsilon_{x}))
can be covered by
\frac{(\epsilon_{x}+\epsilon/4c_{x})^{d^{*}}}{(\epsilon/4c_{x})^{d^{*}}}
balls with radius
\epsilon
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Also note that
\cup_{x\in\mathcal{M}}[\varphi_{x}^{-1}(B_{d^{*}}(0,\epsilon_{x}))]
forms an open cover of
\mathcal{M}
, so there exist finitely many
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}
such that
\mathcal{M}=\cup_{i=1}^{n}[\varphi_{x_{i}}^{-1}(B_{d^{*}}(0,\epsilon_{x_{i}}))]
, since
\mathcal{M}
is compact. Thus
\mathcal{M}
can be covered by
\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{(\epsilon_{x_{i}}+\epsilon/4c_{x_{i}})^{d^{*}}}{(\epsilon/4c_{x_{i}})^{d^{*}}}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\frac{4c_{x_{i}}\epsilon_{x_{i}}}{\epsilon}+1\right)^{d^{*}}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\frac{4c_{x_{i}}\epsilon_{x_{i}}+1}{\epsilon}\right)^{d^{*}}\leq\frac{C_{\mathcal{M}}}{\epsilon^{d^{*}}}
balls with radius
\epsilon
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, where
C_{\mathcal{M}}=n\cdot\max_{i}\{(4c_{x_{i}}\epsilon_{x_{i}}+1)^{d^{*}}\}
. That is,
\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{M},\epsilon)\leq C_{\mathcal{M}}(\frac{1}{\epsilon})^{d^{*}}
.
∎

Theorem 2
.

Let
\mathcal{X}=\Sigma\times\mathcal{X}_{0}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\mathcal{X}_{0}
be a compact
d^{*}
-dimensional smooth submanifold of

and

\mu

\Sigma

\mathcal{X}

\mathbb{R}

\Sigma

\mathcal{D}_{NN}^{\Sigma}=S_{\Sigma}[\mathcal{D}_{NN}]
, where
\mathcal{D}_{NN}=\mathcal{NN}(W_{1},L_{1},N_{1})
as defined in (
16
) with
N_{1}\lesssim n\log n
and
L_{1}\lesssim\log n
, and
\Sigma

Proof.

7
Conclusion and future work

In this work, we quantitatively established the improved generalization guarantees of group-invariant GANs. Our results indicate that when learning group-invariant target distributions, potentially supported on a manifold with small intrinsic dimension, the required number of samples for group-invariant GANs is reduced by a factor of the group size. In essence, group-invariant GANs function effectively as though the input data is augmented in the statistical sense, without an increase in the number of parameters, compared to vanilla GANs. This becomes crucial when we face data scarcity. On the other hand, however, the overall improved bound cannot simply be derived from data augmentation since invariant GANs have a reduced discriminator approximation error, and our numerical results further support this distinction. Our findings present several potential avenues for further exploration. Firstly, it is essential to explore how the dimension of the support of the noise source
\rho
influences the performance of GANs. A recent experimental study
[
54
]
suggests that the dimension of the source should not be significantly smaller than the intrinsic dimension of the target distribution’s support.
Secondly, exploring the case of unbounded support for the target distribution, particularly when heavy-tailed distributions are involved, holds promise for further investigation. To our knowledge, the theoretical study of how the dimension of the support and the distribution characteristics of the noise source
\rho
influence the performance of GANs is still open.
Thirdly, it will also be worthwhile to study the statistical estimation and expressive power of group-invariant GANs constructed from equivariant convolutional neural networks (CNNs), based on the universality result of CNNs (cf.
[
52
]
). Lastly, our work assumes the group is finite, which is crucial for defining our group-invariant generators and discriminators. Extending the analysis to compact Lie groups should also be explored. While the statistical error from the target
\Delta_{3}
has been studied in
[
10
,
44
]
for continuous groups, it remains an open problem for a novel design of invariant discriminator architecture and its universality for infinite groups.
