Largest component of subcritical random graphs with given degree sequence
Matthew Coulson, Guillem Perarnau

Abstract
We study the size of the largest component of two models of random graphs with prescribed degree sequence, the configuration model (CM) and the uniform model (UM), in the (barely) subcritical regime. For the CM, we give upper bounds that are asymptotically tight for certain degree sequences. These bounds hold under mild conditions on the sequence and improve previous results of Hatami and Molloy on the barely subcritical regime. For the UM, we give weaker upper bounds that are tight up to logarithmic terms but require no assumptions on the degree sequence. In particular, the latter result applies to degree sequences with infinite variance in the subcritical regime.

Introduction

Let
[n]\coloneqq\{1,\dots,n\}
be a set of
n
vertices. Let
{\mathbf{d}}_{n}=(d_{1},\dots,d_{n})
be a degree sequence with
m\coloneqq\sum_{i\in[n]}d_{i}
an even positive integer. Without loss of generality, we will assume that
d_{1}\leq\dots\leq d_{n}
. Additionally, we may assume that
d_{1}\geq 1
; if there are elements with degree
0
we can remove them and study the remainder sequence. Let
\Delta=\Delta_{n}
be the maximum degree of
{\mathbf{d}}_{n}
.

The
configuration model
, denoted by
{{\mathbb{CM}}}_{n}={{\mathbb{CM}}}_{n}({\mathbf{d}}_{n})
, is
the random multigraph on
[n]
generated by giving
d_{i}

half-edges
(or stubs) to vertex
i
, and then pairing the half-edges uniformly at random.
The
uniform model
, denoted by
{\mathbb{G}}_{n}={\mathbb{G}}_{n}({\mathbf{d}}_{n})
, is the random simple graph on
[n]
obtained by choosing a simple graph uniformly at random among all graphs on
[n]
where vertex
i
has degree
d_{i}
. Throughout the paper, all the results on the uniform model will assume that the sequence
{\mathbf{d}}_{n}
is
graphical
; that is, there exists at least one graph on
[n]
with such degree sequence.

For any graph
G
on
[n]
, let
L_{1}(G)
denote the order of a largest component. A central problem in random graph theory is to find a parameter of the model
\alpha
such that
L_{1}
undergoes a phase transition at
\alpha=\alpha_{0}
. The set of parameters is then divided into subcritical (
\alpha<\alpha_{0}
), critical (
\alpha\sim\alpha_{0}
) and supercritical (
\alpha>\alpha_{0}
). A further problem is the study the critical window: that is, to find parameters
\beta^{-},\beta^{+}
, such that
L_{1}
behaves essentially the same for any
\alpha\in(\alpha_{0}-\beta^{-},\alpha_{0}+\beta^{+})
and the critical region is further divided into barely subcritical,
\alpha_{0}-\alpha>\beta^{-}
and barely supercritical, where
\alpha-\alpha_{0}>\beta^{+}
.

The main goal of this paper is to study the largest component phase transition
L_{1}({{\mathbb{CM}}}_{n})
and
L_{1}({{\mathbb{G}}}_{n})
in the subcritical and the barely subcritical regimes.
Generally speaking, the study of configuration model is simpler due to the existence of an explicit model with good independence properties. In contrast, most of the results existing for
{{\mathbb{G}}}_{n}
arise from
{{\mathbb{CM}}}_{n}
by observing that the probability that
{{\mathbb{CM}}}_{n}
generates a simple graph is sufficiently large.

In order to understand the phase transition, define

\displaystyle Q=Q_{n}({\mathbf{d}}_{n})\coloneqq\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i\in[n]}d_{i}(d_{i}-2)\;,

(1.1)

\displaystyle R=R_{n}({\mathbf{d}}_{n})\coloneqq\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i\in[n]}d_{i}(d_{i}-2)^{2}\;.

(1.2)

In the first part of the paper, we will focus on the case
Q_{n}\leq 0
. It is easy to check that the bound on
Q_{n}
implies
\Delta_{n}=O(\sqrt{n})
and
m\leq 2n
. Also note the implicit bound on the maximum degree
\Delta_{n}=O(n^{1/3}R_{n}^{1/3})
obtained by just considering the contribution of a vertex of maximum degree to
R_{n}
.

Let
D_{n}
be the degree of a uniform random vertex and let
\hat{D}_{n}
be its size-biased distribution; that is, for
k\geq 1
,

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}(\hat{D}_{n}=k)=\frac{k{\mathbb{P}}(D_{n}=k)}{m}\;.

(1.3)

For
b,h\in\mathbb{N}
, let
{\cal{L}}(b,h):=\{b+hk:k\in\mathbb{Z}\}
be the integer lattice containing
b
with step
h
. Let
h_{n}
be the largest integer

such that
{\mathbb{P}}(D_{n}\in{\cal{L}}(b,h))=1
for some
b\in\mathbb{N}
.

We will study the
{{\mathbb{CM}}}_{n}
under the following conditions on the degree sequence:

Assumption 1.1
.

There exists a discrete random variable
D
supported on
\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
such that

(i)

D_{n}\to D
in distribution;

(ii)

Q_{n}\to 0
;

(iii)

{\mathbb{P}}(D\not\in\{0,2\})>0
;

(iv)

if
h
is the largest integer such that
{\mathbb{P}}(D\in{\cal{L}}(b,h))
for some
b\in\mathbb{N}
, then
h_{n}=h
for all
n
.

(v)

\mathbb{E}(D_{n}^{4})\leq\Delta_{n}^{1/2}

Remark 1.2
.

Conditions (i)-(iii) are usual in this setting. In particular, they imply that
R_{n}
is bounded away from zero, which will be often used in the proofs.

Condition (iv) simply asks that the limiting degree distribution
D
has the same step as the random variables
D_{n}
which converge to it. This restriction is not particularly strong and forbids no limiting degree sequence, only the way in which we converge to it.

Condition (v) is the most restrictive one. As
Q_{n}=o(1)
, we have
\mathbb{E}[D^{2}_{n}]=O(1)
, which implies
\mathbb{E}[D^{4}_{n}]=O(\Delta_{n}^{2})
. Thus, this condition can be understood as a “polynomial limitation” on the contribution of large degree vertices to the fourth moment. It would be interesting to see up to which point a condition on the fourth moment is needed.

Our first result upper bounds the size of the largest component when

is not too large with respect to

.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
\epsilon>0
. Let
{\mathbf{d}}_{n}
be a degree sequence satisfying
1.1
and
\Delta|Q|=o(R)
. If
Q\leq-\omega(n)n^{-1/3}R^{2/3}
for some

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}\left(L_{1}({{\mathbb{CM}}}_{n}({\mathbf{d}}_{n}))\leq(1+\epsilon)\frac{2R}{Q^{2}}\log\left(\frac{|Q|^{3}n}{R^{2}}\right)\right)=1-o(1)\;.

(1.4)

Remark 1.4
.

As noted in
[
10
]
, under the condition
|Q|\Delta=o(R)
the critical window is
|Q|=O(n^{-1/3}R^{2/3})
. Therefore,
Theorem

1.3
bounds the largest component in the whole barely subcritical regime. See

Let
\eta\coloneqq\eta_{n}=\hat{D}_{n}-2

\displaystyle\varphi(\theta)\coloneqq\varphi_{n}(\theta)={\mathbb{E}}\left[e^{\theta\eta_{n}}\right]\;.

(1.5)

Theorem

Theorem 1.5
.

Let
\epsilon>0
. Let
{\mathbf{d}}_{n}
be a degree sequence satisfying

and

. Let

(1.6)

If
\theta_{0}m\geq\omega(n)T_{n}
for some
\omega(n)\to\infty
, then

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}(L_{1}({{\mathbb{CM}}}_{n}({\mathbf{d}}_{n}))\leq(1+\epsilon)T_{n})=1-o(1)\;.

(1.7)

Remark 1.6
.

The value
\theta_{0}
exists and is bounded as
n\to\infty
. We have
\varphi(0)=1
and
\varphi^{\prime}(0)=Q<0
.
Recall that
\eta
is supported in
\{-1,0,1,\dots\}
.
By
1.1
,
\{D\geq 3\}
happens with positive probability and so if we define

, then

and

.
So
\varphi(\theta)\to\infty
as
\theta\to\infty
and it must at some point have positive derivative. Thus, there exists
\theta_{0}\in(0,1)
such that
\varphi^{\prime}(\theta_{0})=0
.

It is interesting to understand if these results also hold in the uniform setting. One can use the following result to transfer from
{{\mathbb{CM}}}_{n}
to
{{\mathbb{G}}}_{n}
.

Theorem 1.7

(Janson
[
14
]
)
.

Let

. Then

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}({{\mathbb{CM}}}_{n}({\mathbf{d}}_{n})\text{ simple})=\exp\left(-\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i\in[n]}d_{i}^{2}\right)>0\;,

(1.8)

and conditioned on being simple,
{{\mathbb{CM}}}_{n}
has the same law as
{{\mathbb{G}}}_{n}
. Therefore, any result that holds with probability
1-o(1)
for
{{\mathbb{CM}}}_{n}({\mathbf{d}}_{n})
, also holds with probability
1-o(1)
for
{{\mathbb{G}}}_{n}({\mathbf{d}}_{n})
.

As
Theorem

1.3
and
Theorem

1.5
assume that

, we have that
{\mathbb{E}}\left[D^{2}_{n}\right]=O(1)
and we can use

1.7
to transfer their conclusions to
{{\mathbb{G}}}_{n}
, provided that their hypothesis are satisfied.

The second part of our paper focuses on the size of the largest component in the (barely) subcritical regime of
{{\mathbb{G}}}_{n}
without further assumptions on the degree sequence. The lack of a tractable model for
{{\mathbb{G}}}_{n}
hampers its analysis and the upper bounds obtained are weaker than the ones obtained for

and probably not of the right order.

Let
S_{*}
be a smallest set of vertices of largest degree that satisfies

\displaystyle\sum_{u\in[n]\setminus S_{*}}d_{u}(d_{u}-2)\leq 0

(1.9)

and define

\displaystyle m_{*}=\sum_{v\in S_{*}}d_{v}\;.

(1.10)

In particular, if

, then

and

For any

, we call

\displaystyle\frac{1}{m}\sum_{w\in[n]\setminus S}d_{w}(d_{w}-2)\leq Q_{0}\;.

Our most general result on
{{\mathbb{G}}}_{n}
is the following.

Theorem 1.8
.

Let
{\mathbf{d}}_{n}
be an
(m_{0},Q_{0})
-subcritical degree sequences for some parameters satisfying
m_{0}\geq 3m_{*}
,
m_{0}|Q_{0}|\geq(\Delta|Q_{0}|+R)\log\left(\frac{nQ_{0}^{2}}{\Delta|Q_{0}|+R}\right)
and
Q_{0}^{2}n\geq\omega(n)m_{0}
for some
\omega(n)\to\infty
. Then,

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}\left(L_{1}({{\mathbb{G}}}_{n}({\mathbf{d}}_{n}))=O(m_{0}/|Q_{0}|)\right)=1-o(1)\;.

Remark 1.9

(Infinite degree variance)
.

The main strength of
Theorem

1.8
is that it applies to degree sequences with subcritical behaviour but infinite degree variance. To our knowledge, the only results available in this setting are of the form

[
1
,
17
]
. Note that, even if such results were available for
{{\mathbb{CM}}}_{n}
,

1.7
is not strong enough to transfer them to

.

Remark 1.10

(The

.

To compare it with previous work, let us get more explicit results for the case

(i.e.

). In this case,

and we can choose

(a)

If

is bounded away from zero, then all conditions in

\displaystyle L_{1}({{\mathbb{G}}}_{n}({\mathbf{d}}_{n}))=O(\Delta\log n)\;.

(1.11)

(b)

If

, then we split depending on how

and
\Delta|Q|

(b.1)

If

, then for any

(b.2)

If

\displaystyle L_{1}({{\mathbb{G}}}_{n}({\mathbf{d}}_{n}))=O\left(\frac{\Delta}{|Q|}\log\left(\frac{nQ}{\Delta}\right)\right)\;.

If

.

For any

,
\Delta=o(\sqrt{n})

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}\left(L_{1}({{\mathbb{G}}}_{n}(\widehat{\mathbf{d}}_{n}))\geq(1+o(1))\frac{2R}{Q^{2}}\log\left(\frac{n}{R^{2}}\right)\right)=1-o(1)\;.

.

We can compare the lower bound in

The degree sequence

While

. Provided that

1.1
Previous work

The foundational paper of Erdős and Rényi
[
7
]
located the phase transition for the existence of a linear order component in a uniformly chosen graph on
n
vertices and
m
edges,
{\mathbb{G}}(n,m)
, showing that the order of the largest component undergoes a double jump at
m=n/2
, in particular
L_{1}({\mathbb{G}}(n,m))=O(\log{n})
if
m\leq cn
and
c<1/2
,
L_{1}({\mathbb{G}}(n,m))=\Theta(n^{2/3})
if
m=n/2
, and
L_{1}({\mathbb{G}}(n,m))=\Theta(n)
if
m\leq cn
and
c>1/2
. This result can be easily transferred to the Binomial random graph
{\mathbb{G}}(n,p)
with
p=2m/n
, which has become the reference model for random graphs. The size of the largest component in all regimes is well understood, see e.g. Sections 4 and 5 in
[
11
]
.

The study of the phase transitions in random graphs with given degree sequences was pioneered by Molloy and Reed
[
19
]
. The so-called
Molloy-Reed criterion
determines the phase transition at
Q=0
, provided that the degree sequence satisfies a number of technical conditions. The criterion has been extended to degree sequences with bounded degree variance
[
15
]
and uniformly integrable sequences
[
1
]
, providing the asymptotic value of
L_{1}
in the supercritical regime
Q>0
in terms of the survival probability of an associated branching process, similarly as in the
{\mathbb{G}}(n,p)
case. Interestingly, the criterion is no longer valid for general degree sequences due to the presence of high degree vertices (hubs) or an extremely large number of degree
2
vertices. Joos et al.
[
17
]
gave an extended criterion that determines whether any given degree sequence typically produces a linear order component.

While the behaviour of the largest component in the supercritical regime resembles the simpler Erdős-Rényi model, this does not happen in the subcritical one, when
Q<0
. Trivially, we have
L_{1}({{\mathbb{G}}}_{n})\geq\Delta+1
which could be much larger than logarithmic. In
[
19
]
the authors showed that
L_{1}({{\mathbb{CM}}}_{n})=O(\Delta^{2}\log{n})
for subcritical sequences. More precise results are known for power-law degree sequences. Durrett
[
5
]
conjectured
1
1

1

In fact, this was conjectured for a slightly different model where the degrees are i.i.d. copies of
D_{n}
conditioned on their sum being even.
that if
{\mathbb{P}}(D_{n}=k)\sim ck^{-\gamma}
for some
\gamma>3
and
c>0
, then
L_{1}({{\mathbb{CM}}}_{n})=O(\Delta)
. In this setting,
\gamma>3
implies
{\mathbb{E}}\left[D_{n}^{\gamma-1}\right]=O(1)
. Pittel
[
24
]
showed that
L_{1}({{\mathbb{CM}}}_{n})=O(\Delta\log n)
for subpower-law distributions. Janson
[
13
]
proved a strong version of the conjecture: if
{\mathbb{P}}(D_{n}\geq k)=O(k^{1-\gamma})
for some

, then

\displaystyle L_{1}({{\mathbb{CM}}}_{n})=\frac{\Delta}{|Q|}+o(n^{1/(\gamma-1)})

(1.14)

For power-law distributions, we have
\Delta=\Theta(n^{1/(\gamma-1)})
with high probability, and the second term is negligible. From the intuitive point of view, the largest component is obtained by starting a subcritical branching process with expected offspring
1+Q
from each vertex adjacent to the vertex of largest degree. The expected total progeny of such process is
1/|Q|
.
One can interpret the result of
Theorem

1.8
in a similar spirit: in the largest component there might be at most
O(m_{0})
edges and from each of these edges a piece of size
O(1/|Q_{0}|)
hangs. For
Q<0
, (
1.14
) can be compared to the weaker bound
Eq.

1.11
that holds regardless of the shape of the degree sequence tail. As shown in
Remark

, for general degree sequences

cannot be improved.

The critical regime has attracted a lot of interest in recent years
[
2
,
3
,
10
,
12
,
18
,
25
]
with several papers specialising on the finite second or finite third moment cases. Here we focus on the results known for the barely subcritical regime.

Riordan
[

]
showed that if
\Delta=O(1)
then
Eq.

1.4
holds, even more, one has asymptotic equality and control on the second order term.
Theorem

can be seen as a generalization of the upper bound in
[

]
to a wider class of sequences that allows
\Delta\to\infty
as

.

Hatami and Molloy
[

]
studied the critical window under some mild conditions on the degree sequence. They showed that
|Q|=O(n^{-1/3}R^{2/3})
is the critical window of
{{\mathbb{CM}}}_{n}
. Regarding the barely subcritical regime, for
Q\leq-\omega(n)n^{-1/3}R^{2/3}
with
\omega(n)\to\infty
, they showed that

\displaystyle L_{1}({{\mathbb{CM}}}_{n})

\displaystyle=O\left(\sqrt{\frac{n}{|Q|}}\right)\;.

(1.15)

One can check that
Eq.

1.15
coincides in order with
Eq.

1.4
at the boundary of the critical window

, while
Eq.

1.4
improves

Under infinite variance, the probability of
{{\mathbb{CM}}}_{n}
being simple can be exponentially small in

. Thus, only results that hold with exponentially high probability can be transferred from
{{\mathbb{CM}}}_{n}
to
{{\mathbb{G}}}_{n}
, see e.g.
[

. Another approach is to study

. In both cases, the best bound given in the subcritical regime is

.

Theorem

1.8
provides the first explicit general bound to

at subcriticallity for infinite variance degree sequences. As discussed in

, this bound cannot be substantially improved without further assumptions.

It thus remains as an open question to determine the exact size of the largest component in the (barely) subcritical regime.
Hofstad, Janson and Łuczak conjectured that

is concentrated in this regime

]
. Supported by the result of Riordan for constant maximum degree, we conjecture that the upper bound in

A local limit theorem

A local limit theorem estimates the probability distribution of a suitably rescaled sum of independent random variables, by the density function of a Gaussian random variable. Local limit theorems are a useful tool to determine the component size in random graphs
[
22
,
25
]
. For our application, we will need the step distribution to allow for the existence of very large degrees, as well as the fact that the degree sequence may be supported on an lattice with step different than
1
. This prevents us from using classical results such as Berry-Esseen Theorem (see
[
6
, Theorem 3.4.9]
). Our goal is to develop a precise local limit theorem which will allow us to deal with our step distributions. Our result is based on previous local limit theorems by Doney
[
4
]
and Mukhin
[
21
,
20
]
from which we derive more explicit error bounds. In particular the main result of this section is following,

Theorem 2.1
.

Let
X_{1},X_{2},\ldots,X_{n}
be independent and identically distributed random variables taking values on
{\cal{L}}(v_{0},h)
.
Define
S_{n}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}
.
Suppose that
\mu=\mathbb{E}(X_{1})=0
,
\sigma^{2}={\mathrm{Var}}(X_{1})
and
\gamma=\mathbb{E}|X_{1}|^{3}
, and let
\varphi(t)
be the characteristic function of
X_{1}
. Then,

\displaystyle\sup_{w\in{\cal{L}}(nv_{0},h)}\left|{\mathbb{P}}(S_{n}=w)-\frac{h}{\sqrt{2\pi n\sigma^{2}}}\exp\left(-\frac{w^{2}}{2n\sigma^{2}}\right)\right|\leq\frac{32h\gamma}{\sigma^{4}n}+\frac{h}{\pi}\int_{\frac{\sigma^{2}}{4\gamma}}^{\frac{\pi}{h}}|\varphi(t)|^{n}dt.

(2.1)

To prove this theorem, we will require the following Fourier inverse theorems which can be found in the book of Durett
[
6
]
.

Theorem 2.2

(Continuous Fourier Inverse Theorem)
.

Suppose that
X
is a random variable with characteristic function
\varphi_{X}(t)
.
Suppose further that
\varphi_{X}(t)
is integrable, then
X
is a continuous random variable with density function
f(y)
defined by

f(y)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{-ity}\varphi_{X}(t)dt.

Theorem 2.3

(Discrete Fourier Inverse Theorem)
.

Let
X
be a random variable with characteristic function
\varphi_{X}(t)
. Suppose that there exists
h>0
such that
{\mathbb{P}}(X\in h\mathbb{Z})=1
. Then for any
a\in h\mathbb{Z}
,

{\mathbb{P}}(X=a)=\frac{h}{2\pi}\int_{-\frac{\pi}{h}}^{\frac{\pi}{h}}e^{-ita}\varphi_{X}(t)dt.

Finally, as we are interested in local limit theorems it will be useful to note that the characteristic function of the standard normal distribution is given by
N(t)=e^{-\frac{t^{2}}{2}}
.

Proof of Theorem
2.1
.

Let
\varphi(t)
be the characteristic function of
X_{1}
and
\psi_{n}(t)
the characteristic function of
S_{n}
.
By basic properties of characteristic functions, it is easy to see that
\psi_{n}(t)=\varphi(t)^{n}
.
By Theorems
2.2
and
2.3
we may deduce that

{\mathbb{P}}(S_{n}=w)-\frac{h}{\sqrt{2\pi n\sigma^{2}}}\exp\left(-\frac{w^{2}}{2n\sigma^{2}}\right)=\frac{h}{2\pi}\int_{-\frac{\pi}{h}}^{\frac{\pi}{h}}e^{-itw}(\psi_{n}(t)-N(t\sigma\sqrt{n}))dt-\frac{h}{\pi}\int_{\frac{\pi}{h}}^{\infty}e^{-itw}N(t\sigma\sqrt{n})dt.

Therefore, by applying various forms of the triangle law we obtain the bound

\left|{\mathbb{P}}(S_{n}=w)-\frac{h}{\sqrt{2\pi n\sigma^{2}}}\exp\left(-\frac{w^{2}}{2n\sigma^{2}}\right)\right|\leq\frac{h}{2\pi}\int_{-\frac{\pi}{h}}^{\frac{\pi}{h}}|\psi_{n}(t)-N(t\sigma\sqrt{n})|dt+\frac{h}{\pi}\int_{\frac{\pi}{h}}^{\infty}N(t\sigma\sqrt{n})dt.

(2.2)

To bound the first integral in (
2.2
) we split it into three parts.
For
\epsilon>0
(which we shall pick later) we have

\int_{-\frac{\pi}{h}}^{\frac{\pi}{h}}|\psi_{n}(t)-N(t\sigma\sqrt{n})|dt\leq\int_{-\epsilon}^{\epsilon}|\psi_{n}(t)-N(t\sigma\sqrt{n})|dt+2\int_{\epsilon}^{\frac{\pi}{h}}N(t\sigma\sqrt{n})dt+2\int_{\epsilon}^{\frac{\pi}{h}}|\varphi(t)|^{n}dt

(2.3)

The idea of this bound being that both
\psi_{n}(t)
and
N(t\sigma\sqrt{n})
only contribute a non-trivial amount to the left hand side of (
2.3
) for
t
very close to
0
.
In bounding the first integral of (
2.3
) we will use the following lemma
[
23
, Page 109, Lemma 1]
.

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
be independent random variables with
\mathbb{E}(X_{i})=0
,
\sigma^{2}_{i}={\mathrm{Var}}(X_{i})
and
\gamma_{i}=\mathbb{E}|X_{i}|^{3}
.
Define

\displaystyle B_{n}:=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sigma^{2}_{i}

\displaystyle L_{n}:=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\gamma_{i}}{B_{n}^{3/2}}

\displaystyle T_{n}:=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}}{B_{n}^{1/2}}

Let
f_{n}(t)
be the characteristic function of
T_{n}
. Then,

|f_{n}(t)-e^{-\frac{t^{2}}{2}}|\leq 16L_{n}|t|^{3}e^{-\frac{t^{2}}{3}}\;\text{ for }\;|t|\leq\frac{1}{4L_{n}}.

(2.4)

Clearly this is applicable in our setting, however we need to rescale first which allows us to deduce

|\psi_{n}(t)-N(t\sigma\sqrt{n})|\leq 16\gamma n|t|^{3}e^{-\frac{t^{2}\sigma^{2}n}{3}}\;\text{ for }\;|t|\leq\frac{\sigma^{2}}{4\gamma}.

So, for any
\epsilon\leq\sigma^{2}/(4\gamma)
we have

\displaystyle\int_{-\epsilon}^{\epsilon}|\psi_{n}(t)-N(t\sigma\sqrt{n})|dt

\displaystyle\leq 16\gamma n\int_{-\epsilon}^{\epsilon}|t|^{3}e^{-\frac{t^{2}\sigma^{2}n}{3}}dt

\displaystyle\leq 16\gamma n\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}|t|^{3}e^{-\frac{t^{2}\sigma^{2}n}{3}}dt

\displaystyle=\frac{16\gamma}{\sigma^{4}n}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}|t|^{3}e^{-\frac{t^{2}}{3}}dt=\frac{144\gamma}{\sigma^{4}n}.

(2.5)

The next step is to bound the second term of (
2.3
).
Note that we can combine this with bounding the second term of (
2.2
), so we require to give an upper bound on

\int_{\epsilon}^{\infty}N(t\sigma\sqrt{n})dt=\frac{1}{\sigma\sqrt{n}}\int_{\epsilon\sigma\sqrt{n}}^{\infty}e^{-\frac{t^{2}}{2}}dt=\frac{\sqrt{2\pi}}{\sigma\sqrt{n}}{\mathbb{P}}({\cal{N}}(0,1)>\epsilon\sigma\sqrt{n}).

(2.6)

We use the Chernoff’s bound for the standard normal distribution,
{\mathbb{P}}({\cal{N}}(0,1)>x)\leq e^{-\frac{x^{2}}{2}}
and the simple inequality
e^{-x}\leq x^{-1/2}
for
x>0
, to obtain

\int_{\epsilon}^{\infty}N(t\sigma\sqrt{n})dt=\frac{\sqrt{2\pi}}{\sigma\sqrt{n}}{\mathbb{P}}({\cal{N}}(0,1)>\epsilon\sigma\sqrt{n})\leq\frac{2\sqrt{\pi}}{\epsilon\sigma^{2}n}

(2.7)

Choosing
\epsilon=\sigma^{2}/(4\gamma)
and combining (
2.2
), (
2.3
), (
2.5
) and (
2.7
), we find that for any
w\in{\cal{L}}(nv_{0},h)
,

\displaystyle\left|{\mathbb{P}}(S_{n}=w)-\frac{h}{\sqrt{2\pi n\sigma^{2}}}\exp\left(-\frac{w^{2}}{2n\sigma^{2}}\right)\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left(\frac{72}{\pi}+\frac{16}{\sqrt{\pi}}\right)\frac{h\gamma}{\sigma^{4}n}+\frac{h}{\pi}\int_{\frac{\sigma^{2}}{4\gamma}}^{\frac{\pi}{h}}|\varphi(t)|^{n}dt

\displaystyle\leq\frac{32h\gamma}{\sigma^{4}n}+\frac{h}{\pi}\int_{\frac{\sigma^{2}}{4\gamma}}^{\frac{\pi}{h}}|\varphi(t)|^{n}dt.

(2.8)

Concluding the proof of the theorem.
∎

For the remainder of this section we will focus on bounding the integral term in the RHS of (
2.1
).
To this end we introduce the parameter
H_{D}(X)
, which generalises a similar parameter introduced by Mukhin
[
20
,
21
]
.
For a real-valued random variable
X
, we define
X^{*}=X-X^{\prime}
to be the
symmetrisation
of
X
, where
X^{\prime}
is an independent copy of
X
.
Furthermore, for
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
define
\langle\alpha\rangle
to be the distance from
\alpha
to the nearest integer.
Then for a random variable
X
and
d\in\mathbb{R}
we define the following parameter

\displaystyle H(X,d)

\displaystyle:=\mathbb{E}\langle X^{*}d\rangle^{2}.

The parameter
H(X,d)
measures in a certain sense how close is
X^{*}
to be a random variable supported on a lattice with step
1/|d|
.

The following lemma from
[
20
]
will be useful.

Lemma 2.5
.

If
\varphi(t)
is the characteristic function of the random variable
X
then

4H\left(X,\frac{t}{2\pi}\right)\leq 1-|\varphi(t)|\leq 2\pi^{2}H\left(X,\frac{t}{2\pi}\right)

(2.9)

We provide a full proof of the statement, for the sake of completeness.

Proof.

We look at the characteristic function of
X^{*}
,
\varphi^{*}(t)
.
Note that
X^{*}
is by definition symmetric around the origin and hence so is
\varphi^{*}(t)
.
Writing
{\cal{D}}(X^{*})
for the domain of
X^{*}
which is discrete, we have

\varphi^{*}(t)=\frac{\varphi^{*}(t)+\varphi^{*}(-t)}{2}=\sum_{x\in{\cal{D}}(X^{*})}\frac{e^{itx}+e^{-itx}}{2}{\mathbb{P}}(X^{*}=x)=\sum_{x\in{\cal{D}}(X^{*})}\cos(tx){\mathbb{P}}(X^{*}=x).

(2.10)

As
\cos(x)
is symmetric around
\pi
and periodic with period
2\pi
, we have the identity

\cos(x)=\cos\left(2\pi\left\langle\frac{x}{2\pi}\right\rangle\right).

We can use this identity to rewrite (
2.10
) as

\varphi^{*}(t)=\sum_{x\in{\cal{D}}(X^{*})}\cos\left(2\pi\left\langle\frac{tx}{2\pi}\right\rangle\right){\mathbb{P}}(X^{*}=x).

(2.11)

Consider the following bounds on
\cos(x)
valid for
x\in[0,\pi]
,

1-\frac{x^{2}}{2}\leq\cos(x)\leq 1-\frac{2x^{2}}{\pi^{2}}.

We can use this in combination with (
2.11
) to deduce that

1-2\pi^{2}\mathbb{E}\left\langle\frac{X^{*}t}{2\pi}\right\rangle^{2}\leq\varphi^{*}(t)\leq 1-8\mathbb{E}\left\langle\frac{X^{*}t}{2\pi}\right\rangle^{2}

(2.12)

Finally, by definition of
X^{*}
, note that
\varphi^{*}(t)=\varphi(t)\varphi(-t)=|\varphi(t)|^{2}
.
As
|\varphi(t)|\in[0,1]
we may deduce that

1-2\pi^{2}H\left(X,\frac{t}{2\pi}\right)\leq|\varphi(t)|\leq 1-4H\left(X,\frac{t}{2\pi}\right)

(2.13)

which may easily be rearranged to give the statement of the lemma.
∎

For
D\in\mathbb{N}
and a random variable
X
we define

\displaystyle H_{D}(X):=\inf_{\frac{1}{4D}\leq d\leq\frac{1}{2D}}H(X,d)

(2.14)

The following lemma of Mukhin
[
21
]
bounds
H(X,d)
in terms of this new parameter,

Lemma 2.6
.

For any random variable
X
,
d\in\mathbb{R}
and

D\in\mathbb{N}
with
2D|d|\leq 1
we have

H(X,d)\geq 4D^{2}|d|^{2}H_{D}(X).

We may now apply Lemma
2.5
to give an explicit upper bound on the integral term in (
2.1
) as follows. Recall that
X_{1}
is a lattice random variable with step
h
. By Lemma
2.5
and using
\ln(1/x)\geq 1-x
for
x>0
,

\int_{\frac{\sigma^{2}}{4\gamma}}^{\frac{\pi}{h}}|\varphi(t)|^{n}dt\leq\int_{\frac{\sigma^{2}}{4\gamma}}^{\frac{\pi}{h}}e^{-n(1-|\varphi(t)|)}dt\leq\int_{\frac{\sigma^{2}}{4\gamma}}^{\frac{\pi}{h}}e^{-4nH(X,\frac{t}{2\pi})}dt.

(2.15)

Now, note that the upper limit of the integral in (
2.15
) is
\pi/h
.
So, as
(\pi/h)/(2\pi)=1/(2h)
we may apply Lemma
2.6
with
D=h
and
d=t/2\pi\leq 1/2h
to deduce that

\displaystyle\int_{\frac{\sigma^{2}}{4\gamma}}^{\frac{\pi}{h}}|\varphi(t)|^{n}dt

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\frac{\sigma^{2}}{4\gamma}}^{\frac{\pi}{h}}e^{-\frac{4nh^{2}H_{h}(X)t^{2}}{\pi^{2}}}dt\leq\int_{\frac{\sigma^{2}}{4\gamma}}^{\infty}e^{-\frac{4nh^{2}H_{h}(X)t^{2}}{\pi^{2}}}dt

\displaystyle=\frac{\pi^{3/2}}{2h(nH_{h}(X))^{1/2}}{\mathbb{P}}\left({\cal{N}}(0,1)>\frac{\sigma^{2}h(nH_{h}(X))^{1/2}}{\sqrt{2}\pi\gamma}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\pi^{3/2}}{2h(nH_{h}(X))^{1/2}}e^{-\frac{\sigma^{4}h^{2}nH_{h}(X)}{4\pi^{2}\gamma^{2}}},

(2.16)

where the final inequality follows by the Chernoff’s bound.
This allows us to deduce, once again using the inequality
e^{-x}<x^{-1/2}
, that this integral is bounded above as

\int_{\frac{\sigma^{2}}{4\gamma}}^{\frac{\pi}{h}}|\varphi(t)|^{n}dt\leq\frac{\pi^{5/2}\gamma}{h^{2}\sigma^{2}nH_{h}(X)}.

This allows us to state the following corollary to Theorem
2.1
.

Corollary 2.7
.

Let
X_{1},X_{2},\ldots,X_{n}
be independent and identically distributed random variables taking values on
{\cal{L}}(v_{0},h)
.
Define
S_{n}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}
.
Suppose that
\mu=\mathbb{E}(X_{1})=0
,
\sigma^{2}={\mathrm{Var}}(X_{1})
and
\gamma=\mathbb{E}|X_{1}|^{3}
.
Then,

\displaystyle\sup_{w\in{\cal{L}}(nv_{0},h)}\left|{\mathbb{P}}(S_{n}=w)-\frac{h}{\sqrt{2\pi n\sigma^{2}}}\exp\left(-\frac{w^{2}}{2n\sigma^{2}}\right)\right|\leq\frac{32h\gamma}{\sigma^{4}n}+\frac{6\gamma}{h\sigma^{2}nH_{h}(X_{1})}.

(2.17)

To give an explicit upper bound on the error probability, we need to deduce that
H_{h}(X_{1})
is bounded from below. For
\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{k})\in\mathbb{Z}^{k}
, define

w(\mathbf{x}):=\max_{i\neq\ell\neq j}\frac{|x_{i}-x_{\ell}|}{\gcd(|x_{i}-x_{\ell}|,|x_{j}-x_{\ell}|)}.

Then the fact that
H_{h}(X_{1})
is bounded from below is implied by the following lemma,

Lemma 2.8
.

Let
X
be an integer valued random variable supported on a lattice of step
h
and with atoms
x_{1},\ldots,x_{k}
not all contained in a non-trivial arithmetic progression of the lattice. Then there exists an absolute constant
C>0
such that

H_{h}(X)\geq C\cdot\frac{\min_{i\in[k]}{\mathbb{P}}(X=x_{i})}{k(w(\mathbf{x})h)^{2}}

Proof.

For
d\in\mathbb{R}
, consider

\displaystyle D(X,d)

\displaystyle:=\inf_{\alpha\in\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{E}\langle(X-\alpha)d\rangle^{2}.

(2.18)

By
[
21
, Lemma 1]
, we have that
D(X,d)\leq H(X,d)\leq 4D(X,d)
. Therefore,

H_{h}(X)\geq\min_{1/4h\leq d\leq 1/2h}D(X,d)

For all
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{Z}^{k}
and
\beta,d\in\mathbb{R}
, define

\displaystyle S(\mathbf{x},\beta,d):=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\langle\beta+x_{i}d\rangle

(2.19)

Cauchy-Schwartz’s inequality implies that

\displaystyle D(X,d)

\displaystyle:=\inf_{\alpha\in\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{E}\langle(X-\alpha)d\rangle^{2}

\displaystyle=\inf_{\beta\in\mathbb{R}}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\langle\beta+x_{i}d\rangle^{2}{\mathbb{P}}(X=x_{i})

\displaystyle\geq\min_{i\in[k]}{\mathbb{P}}(X=x_{i})\inf_{\beta\in\mathbb{R}}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\langle\beta+x_{i}d\rangle^{2}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\min_{i\in[k]}{\mathbb{P}}(X=x_{i})}{k}\inf_{\beta\in\mathbb{R}}S(\mathbf{x},\beta,d)^{2}.

(2.20)

It thus suffices to bound the infimum of
S
when
\mathbf{x}
and
d
are fixed. The derivative of
S
with respect to
\beta
satisfies the following properties:

(i)

it is well defined for all
\beta
such that
\langle\beta+x_{i}d\rangle\not\in\{0,1/2\}
for all
i\in[k]
;

(ii)

it is constant between any two consecutive values at which the derivative is undefined;

(iii)

it takes integer values in
\{-k,\dots,k\}
anywhere where it is defined.

Thus, the minimum of
S
is attained at
\beta_{0}
, for which the derivative is not defined. By relabelling the
x_{i}
, we may assume that
\langle\beta_{0}+x_{k}d\rangle\in\{0,1/2\}
. If
\langle\beta_{0}+x_{k}d\rangle=1/2
, plugging it in (
2.20
) we would get the desired bound and we are done. So we may assume that
\langle\beta_{0}+x_{k}d\rangle=0
, and, in fact, we can choose
\beta_{0}=-x_{k}d
. For
i\in[k-1]
, define
y_{i}:=x_{i}-x_{k}
.
As the
x_{i}
are not all contained in a non-trivial arithmetic progression then
\text{hcf}(y_{1},y_{2},\ldots,y_{k-1})=h
.
By a simple extension of Bézout’s Lemma there exist
\lambda_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}
with
|\lambda_{i}|\leq w(\mathbf{x})h
for all
i\in[k-1]
and
\lambda_{1}y_{1}+\lambda_{2}y_{2}+\ldots+\lambda_{k-1}y_{k-1}=h
.
Now, using the identities
\langle t\beta\rangle\leq|t|\langle\beta\rangle
for any
t\in\mathbb{Z}
and
\langle\beta_{1}+\beta_{2}\rangle\leq\langle\beta_{1}\rangle+\langle\beta_{2}\rangle
, we obtain

\displaystyle\inf_{\beta\in\mathbb{R}}S(\mathbf{x},\beta,d)=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\langle\beta_{0}+x_{i}d\rangle=\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}\langle y_{i}d\rangle\geq\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}\frac{\langle\lambda_{i}y_{i}d\rangle}{|\lambda_{i}|}\geq\dfrac{\left\langle\sum\limits_{i=1}^{k-1}\lambda_{i}y_{i}d\right\rangle}{w(\mathbf{x})h}=\frac{\langle hd\rangle}{w(\mathbf{x})h}

(2.21)

Observing that
\langle hd\rangle=hd
for all
d\leq 1/2h
, we obtain,

\displaystyle H_{h}(X)\geq\min_{1/4h\leq d\leq 1/2h}D(X,d)\geq\min_{1/4h\leq d\leq 1/2h}\frac{\min_{i\in[k]}{\mathbb{P}}(X=x_{i})}{k}\left(\frac{\langle hd\rangle}{w(\mathbf{x})h}\right)^{2}=\frac{\min_{i\in[k]}{\mathbb{P}}(X=x_{i})}{16k(w(\mathbf{x})h)^{2}}.

∎

3
Barely subcritical regime for the configuration model

3.1
Exploration process

In this section we introduce a process that given a vertex
v\in[n]
explores
{{\mathbb{CM}}}_{n}
starting by the component containing
v
. We set a total order of the half edges as follows. For every vertex
v
, consider an arbitrary order of its
d_{v}
half-edges. Then, the half edges are ordered, first by its corresponding vertex (using the total order on
[n]
) and then by the order given within the half-edges incident to a vertex.

We will denote by
{\cal{F}}_{t}
the history of the process at time
t
. With a slight abuse of notation, we will assume that
{\cal{F}}_{t}
is the subgraph formed by the partial matching at time
t
. Note that the order of the pairings is determined by the knowledge of the matching. The main random variable we would like to track is
X_{t}=X_{t}(v)
, defined as the number of unmatched half-edges incident to
V({\cal{F}}_{t})
when the process started at
v
. Note that if
X_{t}=0
, there are no unpaired half-edges and thus
{\cal{F}}_{t}
is a union of components of
{{\mathbb{CM}}}_{n}
containing the component of
v
.

The
exploration process of
{{\mathbb{CM}}}_{n}
starting at
v\in[n]
is defined as follows:

1)

Let
{\cal{F}}_{0}
be the single-vertex graph on
\{v\}
and
X_{0}=d_{v}
.

2)

While
V({\cal{F}}_{t})\neq[n]
,

2a)

If
X_{t}=0
, choose a uniformly unmatched half-edge and let
u
be the vertex incident to it. Let
{\cal{F}}_{t+1}
be constructed from
{\cal{F}}_{t}
by adding
\{u\}
as an isolated vertex, and let
X_{t+1}=d_{u}
.

2b)

Otherwise, choose the smallest unmatched half-edge
e
incident to
V({\cal{F}}_{t})
and pair it with a half-edge
f
chosen uniformly at random from all the unmatched ones. Let
u
be the vertex incident to
f
.

i)

If
u\notin V({\cal{F}}_{t})
, let
{\cal{F}}_{t+1}
be constructed from
{\cal{F}}_{t}
by adding vertex
u
and edge
ef
and let
X_{t+1}=X_{t}+d_{u}-2
.

ii)

Otherwise, let
{\cal{F}}_{t+1}
constructed from
{\cal{F}}_{t}
by adding edge
ef
and let
X_{t+1}=X_{t}-2
.

Note that
X_{t}
is measurable with respect to
{\cal{F}}_{t}
. We define the following parameters:

\displaystyle\begin{split}\eta_{t+1}&\coloneqq X_{t+1}-X_{t}\;,\\
M_{t}&\coloneqq X_{t}+\sum_{u\notin V({\cal{F}}_{t})}d_{u}\;,\\
Q_{t}&=\frac{1}{M_{t}-1}\sum_{u\notin V({\cal{F}}_{t})}d_{u}(d_{u}-2)\;,\\
R_{t}&=\frac{1}{M_{t}-1}\sum_{u\notin V({\cal{F}}_{t})}d_{u}(d_{u}-2)^{2}\;.\end{split}

(3.1)

It is straightforward to check that if
X_{t}>0
, then

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left[\eta_{t+1}\mid{\cal{F}}_{t}\right]=Q_{t}\quad\text{ and }\quad{\mathbb{E}}\left[(\eta_{t+1})^{2}\mid{\cal{F}}_{t}\right]=R_{t}\;,

(3.2)

and if
X_{t}=0
, then

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left[\eta_{t+1}\mid{\cal{F}}_{t}\right]=\frac{1}{M_{t}}\sum_{u\notin V({\cal{F}}_{t})}d_{u}^{2}\quad\text{ and }\quad{\mathbb{E}}\left[(\eta_{t+1})^{2}\mid{\cal{F}}_{t}\right]\geq\frac{R_{t}}{2}\;,

(3.3)

although we will never study the process for
t
such that
X_{t}=0
.

3.2
Stochastic domination and random sums

Recall the definition of
T=T_{n}
given in
Eq.

1.6
.

Define the distribution
\beta
as follows: for every
\ell\in L\coloneqq\{-1,0,1,\dots,n-3\}
,

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}(\beta=\ell)\coloneqq\begin{cases}\frac{m}{m-2T}\,{\mathbb{P}}(\eta=-1)-\frac{2T}{m-2T}&\text{if }\ell=-1\;,\\
\frac{m}{m-2T}\,{\mathbb{P}}(\eta=\ell)&\text{if }\ell\geq 0\;.\end{cases}

(3.4)

Let
\varphi_{\beta}(\theta)
,
\theta_{0}^{\beta}
,
Q_{\beta}
,
R_{\beta}
and
T_{\beta}
be defined as in
Eqs.

1.1
,
1.2
,
1.5
and
1.6
replacing
\eta
by
\beta
. By the choice of
\beta
all main parameters are asymptotically equal to the original ones, as the following result demonstrates.

Lemma 3.1
.

For every
k\geq 0
, we have
\varphi^{(k)}_{\beta}(\theta)=(1+o(\theta_{0}))\varphi^{(k)}(\theta)+o(\theta_{0})
.
Moreover,
T_{\beta}=(1+o(1))T
.

Proof.

The first part of the lemma follows directly from

\displaystyle\varphi^{(k)}_{\beta}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}(\beta^{k}e^{\theta\beta})

\displaystyle=\frac{m}{m-2T}\mathbb{E}(\eta^{k}e^{\theta\eta})-\frac{2T}{m-2T}(-1)^{k}e^{-\theta}

(3.5)

\displaystyle=(1+O(T/m))\varphi^{(k)}(\theta)+O(T/m)

\displaystyle=(1+o(\theta_{0}))\varphi^{(k)}(\theta)+o(\theta_{0}).

(3.6)

where in the last line we used the hypothesis
T=o(m\theta_{0})
in
Theorem

1.5
.

For the second part, we split into two cases. If
\Delta|Q|=o(R)
, we are in the setting of
Theorem

1.3
. In such case

\log(\varphi_\beta(\theta_0^\beta))^{-1}\sim\frac{Q_{\beta}^{2}}{2R_{\beta}}\sim\frac{Q^{2}}{2R}\sim\log(\varphi(\theta_0))^{-1},

(see
Section

3.4
for the first and third equivalences) and the result follows from the first part of the lemma.

Otherwise
R=O(\Delta|Q|)
. As
R
is bounded away from zero by
1.1
and
\Delta_{n}\leq n^{1/6}
, it follows that
|Q|
is of order at least
n^{-1/6}
. Again all we need to show is that
\log(\varphi_\beta(\theta_0^\beta))^{-1}=(1+o(1))\log(\varphi(\theta_0))^{-1}
and then the rest will follow by the first part of the lemma. We do this by bounding
\varphi(\theta_{0})-\varphi_{\beta}(\theta^{\beta}_{0})
.

Using
e^{x}\geq 1+x
, we have

0=\mathbb{E}[\eta e^{\theta_{0}\eta}]\geq\mathbb{E}[\eta(1+\theta_{0}\eta)]=Q+\theta_{0}R

and thus

\displaystyle 0<\theta_{0}\leq\frac{|Q|}{R}=o(1).

(3.7)

By construction,
\beta
stochastically dominates
\eta
and it follows that
\varphi^{(k)}_{\beta}(\theta)\geq\varphi^{(k)}(\theta)
for all
k\geq 0
and
\theta\geq 0
. In particular,

\displaystyle 0<\theta_{0}^{\beta}\leq\theta_{0}

(3.8)

Combining (
3.5
) for
k=0
, (
3.7
) and (

4.1

We will use the exploration process described in

We first describe the exploration process on a fixed graph where each vertex has an order in its adjacency list. Precisely, an

The

Lemma 4.2

Proof.

The proof uses an edge-switching argument. A

is a local operation that transforms an input into another one. Given an input

and two oriented edges

and

and

. Note that this operation preserves the degree of each vertex and does not modify the permutations of the adjacency lists. We will restrict to switchings that do not modify the edges within

in order to switch between inputs in
{\cal{F}}_{t}
.

Fix

. If
X_{t}=0
, then

is chosen with probability

, so we may assume that

. Let

,

and

as described in the process. Given
{\cal{F}}_{t}

and

is a random vertex. Let

be the set of inputs with

. We will estimate the number of switchings between

We first proof

. To switch from

to

for

. To switch from

0
for every
H
of order
\ell
. Since the size of the largest component is asymptotically equal to
\Delta L_{1}(\mathbb{G}_{*})
,
L_{1}({{\mathbb{CM}}}_{n}(\widehat{\mathbf{d}}_{n}))
and
L_{1}({{\mathbb{G}}}_{n}(\widehat{\mathbf{d}}_{n}))
are not concentrated.

Remark 5.3

(The case
Q=o(1)
)
.

The largest component of Erdős-Rényi is well-studied in the barely subcritical regime (see e.g. Theorem 5.6 in
[
16
]
). If
p=\frac{1-\epsilon(\ell)}{\ell}
with
\epsilon(\ell)>0
and
\ell^{-1/3}\ll\epsilon(\ell)\ll 1
, then

\displaystyle L_{1}(\mathbb{G}(\ell,p))\sim 2\epsilon^{2}\log(\epsilon^3 \ell)\;.

(5.7)

Let
\ell=(1-\epsilon(n))\frac{n}{\Delta^{2}}
and define the degree sequence
\widehat{\mathbf{d}}_{n}
as before. Again,
Q\sim-\epsilon
and
R\sim\Delta
. In particular
\Delta|Q|=o(R)
holds.

Set
p=\frac{1-\epsilon(n)}{\ell}
. The same argument as in the proof of
Proposition

1.12
and
Eq.

5.7
gives

\displaystyle L_{1}({{\mathbb{G}}}_{n}(\widehat{\mathbf{d}}_{n}))\geq\Delta L_{1}(\mathbb{G}(\ell,p))\geq(1+o(1))\frac{2\Delta}{\epsilon^{2}}\log\left(\frac{\epsilon^{3}n}{\Delta^{2}}\right)\geq(1+o(1))\frac{2R}{Q^{2}}\log\left(\frac{|Q|^{3}n}{R^{2}}\right)\;.

(5.8)

The condition
\epsilon(\ell)\gg\ell^{-1/3}
for the validity of
Eq.

5.7
is equivalent to
Q\leq-\omega(n)n^{-1/3}R^{2/3}
, for some
\omega(n)\to\infty
.
