Cycle Matroids of Graphings: From Convergence to Duality
Kristóf Bérczi, Márton Borbényi, László Lovász, László Márton Tóth

Abstract
A recent line of research has concentrated on exploring the links between analytic and combinatorial theories of submodularity, uncovering several key connections between them. In this context, Lovász initiated the study of matroids from an analytic point of view and introduced the cycle matroid of a graphing [ 23 ] . Motivated by the limit theory of graphs, the authors introduced a form of right-convergence, called quotient-convergence, for a sequence of submodular setfunctions, leading to a notion of convergence for matroids through their rank functions [ 5 ] . In this paper, we study the connection between local-global convergence of graphs and quotient-convergence of their cycle matroids. We characterize the exposed points of associated convex sets, forming an analytic counterpart of matroid independence- and base-polytopes. Finally, we consider dual planar graphings and show that the cycle matroid of one is the cocycle matroid of its dual if and only if the underlying graphings are hyperfinite. Keywords: Cycle matroid, Duality, Exposed points, Graphings, Matroid limits, Cost

Introduction

Cycle matroids bridge graph theory and matroid theory by providing matroid theoretic counterparts of graph concepts such as cycles, cuts, and spanning trees, thus leading to a powerful framework for understanding and solving various combinatorial and optimization problems. The matroidal perspective allows for a deeper analysis of graph properties and leads to efficient algorithms in network design, electrical engineering, and operations research. In particular, the cycle matroid of a graph
G
is characterized by its rank function, where the rank of a set
F\subseteq E(G)
of edges is
r_{G}(F)=|V(G)|-|\{\textrm{$F$-connected components of $G$}\}|
; see Section
2
for properties of matroid rank functions.

There has been an extensive line of research on extending the notion of cycle matroid to infinite graphs, for example for connected locally finite graphs. Probably the simplest notion for infinite graphs is the finitary cycle matroid whose circuits are exactly the finite cycles. However, an unfortunate feature of finitary matroids is that excluding infinite circuits spoils duality. As a workaround, Bruhn, Diestel, Kriesell, Pendavingh and Wollan
[
9
]
proposed five equivalent sets of axioms leading to a non-finitary theory of infinite matroids. These axioms were in fact shown to be equivalent to the notion of B-matroids introduced by Higgs
[
14
]
. Using these axioms, Bruhn and Diestel
[
8
]
provided further examples by defining the circuits of the matroid to be the edge sets of topological circuits of compactifications of infinite graphs obtained by adding the space of ends to the graph. Later, Bowler, Carmesin and Christian
[
7
]
introduced a class of infinite cycle matroids that unifies previous constructions. Motivated by the work of Nešetřil and Ossona de Mendez
[
26
]
on first order convergence of graphs, Kardoš, Král’, Liebenau, and Mach
[
15
]
introduced the notion of first order convergence of linearly representable matroids. Since graphic matroids are linearly representable, this also provides a convergence notion for such matroids.

In addition to the results discussed above, however, there have been no or only moderate work concerning graphs with an underlying measurable structure. In this paper we study the cycle matroids of graphings, as graphings are natural limit objects in the context of sparse graph limits. A
graphing
is a bounded degree graph
\mathbf{G}=(J,\mu,E)
, where
(J,\mu)
is a standard Borel probability space, the edge set
E
is a Borel subset of
\binom{J}{2}
with a certain measure preserving property; this property allows one to define the measure
\eta_{\mathbf{G}}
on the edge set, see Subsection
2.2
for the full definition. Graphings serve as limit objects in sparse graph limit theory. Recently, Lovász
[
23
]
initiated a limit theory of cycle matroids of bounded-degree graphs, and introduced the cycle matroid of a graphing
\mathbf{G}=(J,\mu,E)
by defining its rank function as

\rho_{\mathbf{G}}(F)=1-\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\frac{1}{|V(\mathbf{G}^{F}_{x})|}\right],

(1)

where
F\subseteq E
is Borel,
x
is a uniform random point from
\mu
,
\mathbf{G}^{F}_{x}
denotes its connected component in the graphing
\mathbf{G}^{Y}=(J,\mu,F)
, and
V(\mathbf{G}^{F}_{x})
denotes the set of vertices of the component. In
[
23
]
, it was shown that
\rho_{\mathbf{G}}
is an increasing submodular setfunction on the Borel subsets of
E
, and
\rho_{\mathbf{G}}\leq\eta_{\mathbf{G}}
. It is not difficult to verify that this definition generalizes the rank function of the cycle matroid of a finite graph, normalized by the number of vertices. In particular, if
\mathbf{G}
a forest with finite components, then
\rho_{\mathbf{G}}(E)
is half of the average degree. If
\mathbf{G}
is any graphing with infinite components, then
\rho_{\mathbf{G}}(E)=1
.

This raises several questions about cycle matroids of graphings. How to define and describe their independent sets, bases, and other basic properties known from the finite case? Does the convergence of a sequence of finite graphs to a graphing imply convergence of the corresponding matroids (in the sense introduced in
[
5
]
)? Does duality of matroids play any role? In this paper, we provide some answers to these questions. We develop the limit theory of cycle matroids of bounded-degree graphs, show that cycle matroids of graphings are their limit objects in the sense of
[
5
]
, we study their bases, and investigate how they behave under duality of planar graphings.

Limit theory.

Our first result connects local-global convergence of graphs with quotient-convergence of the rank function of cycle matroids, defined in
[
5
]
. A
k
-quotient
of a setfunction
\varphi
on a set-algebra
(J,\mathcal{B})
is
\varphi\circ F^{-1}
, where
F:J\to[k]
is a measurable map. We denote by
Q_{k}(\varphi)
the set of
k
-quotients of
\varphi
. A sequence of setfunctions
(\varphi_{1},\varphi_{2},\dots)

quotient converges
to
\varphi
, denoted
\varphi_{n}\rightarrowtail\varphi
, if
Q_{k}(\varphi_{n})\to Q_{k}(\varphi)
as
n\to\infty
in the Hausdorff distance for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
. Note that
Q_{k}(\varphi_{n})
and
Q_{k}(\varphi)
are subsets of the same
2^{k}
-dimensional linear space, so convergence in Hausdorff distance does not depend on the norm of this space. We refer to Subsection
2.4
for more details on this convergence notion. Our first main theorem concerns the case of cycle matroids of bounded-degree graphs.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
(G_{1},G_{2},\dots)
be a sequence of finite graphs with all degrees bounded by
D
that converges to the graphing
\mathbf{G}
in the local-global sense. Let
r_{G_{n}}
denote the rank function of the cycle matroid of
G_{n}
. Then

\frac{r_{G_{n}}}{|V(G_{n})|}\rightarrowtail\rho_{\mathbf{G}}

in the sense of quotient-convergence of submodular setfunctions.

We also exhibit an example showing that assuming local convergence of
G_{n}
is not enough: there exists a sequence
(H_{1},H_{2},\dots)
of finite graphs with all degrees bounded by
D
that converges to a graphing
\mathbf{G}
in the local sense, however,
\frac{r_{H_{n}}}{|V(G_{n})|}\centernot\rightarrowtail\rho_{\mathbf{G}}
; see Example
3.4
for details.

Independent sets.

Our goal is to define independent sets of the cycle matroid of a graphing. There are various ways to characterize independent sets in the cycle matroid of a finite graph, of which we consider the following four.

•

Independent sets in the cycle matroid of a finite graph are edge-sets of acyclic subgraphs (briefly, forests).

•

Bases of the cycle matroid of a finite graph are spanning forests, i.e. they are spanning trees in every connected component. The independent sets are their subsets.

•

Independent sets in any matroid can be recovered from the rank function as sets with
r(X)=|X|
.

•

In a finite matroid
(E,r)
, solutions of the system of inequalities
x_{e}\geq 0

(e\in E)
, and
\sum_{e\in A}x(a)\leq r(A)

(A\subseteq E)
are called
fractional independent sets
, and form the
matroid polytope
. Vertices of the matroid polytope are precisely the (indicator vectors of) independent sets.

Before generalizing these definitions for graphings and stating the theorem about a certain equivalence of them, we need to discuss some points where the generalizations are not quite straightforward.

In this section, we consider forests that are hyperfinite. A graphing
\mathbf{G}=(J,\mu,E)
is
hyperfinite
if, for every
\varepsilon>0
, there is a Borel set
S\subseteq E
with
\eta_{\mathbf{G}}(S)\leq\varepsilon
such that every connected component of
E\setminus S
is finite. A
forest
in
\mathbf{G}
is an acyclic subset
T\subseteq E
of the edges. An
essential spanning forest
is a forest that forms a spanning tree in every finite component of
\mathbf{G}
, and contains only infinite
T
-components in the infinite
\mathbf{G}
-components. Note that
T
might contain several components in an infinite component of
\mathbf{G}
, but all of those are required to be infinite; this is why
T
is called an essential spanning forest instead of a spanning tree. Let us emphasize that we will call a set of edges a “forest” or an “essential spanning forest” if they became one after adding or deleting a set of edges of measure
0
.

A vertex of a convex polytope can be generalized to the infinite setting in several ways. A point
v
of the convex set
C
in a Banach space
X
is
extreme
, if it cannot be written as
v=\frac{1}{2}(u+w)
, where
u,w\in C\backslash\{v\}
. The point
v
is
exposed
, if there is a
continuous linear functional
\Lambda
on
X
such that
\Lambda(y)\leq\Lambda(x)
for every
y\in C
, and
x
is the unique point of
C
with
equality. An exposed point is necessarily extreme, but not the other way around; see Section
4
for further comments.

It was suggested in
[
22
]
that for a general increasing submodular setfunction
\varphi
with
\varphi(\emptyset)=0
, the generalization of the matroid polytope should be the set of its minorizing charges, i.e. finitely additive measures
\alpha
such that
0\leq\alpha\leq\varphi
. For a graphing
\mathbf{G}=(J,\mu,E)
, we are in a simpler situation, since a finitely additive measure
\alpha
minorizing
\rho_{\mathbf{G}}
satisfies
\alpha\leq\eta_{\mathbf{G}}
, and so it is automatically countably additive (i.e., a proper measure). So the analytic version of the cycle matroid polytope will be

\text{\sf mm}_{+}(\rho_{\mathbf{G}})=\big\{\alpha\textrm{ measure on $E$}\mid 0\leq\alpha\leq\rho_{\mathbf{G}}\big\}.

The base polytope of a cycle matroid can be generalized as

\text{\sf bmm}_{+}(\rho_{\mathbf{G}})=\big\{\alpha\in\text{\sf mm}_{+}(\rho_{\mathbf{G}})\mid\alpha(E)=\rho_{\mathbf{G}}(E)\big\}.

First, we give a characterization of the exposed points of
\text{\sf mm}_{+}(\rho_{\mathbf{G}})
and
\text{\sf bmm}_{+}(\rho_{\mathbf{G}})
.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
\mathbf{G}=(J,\mu,E)
be a graphing.

(a)

\alpha\in\text{\sf mm}_{+}(\rho_{\mathbf{G}})
is exposed if and only if
\alpha=\eta_{\mathbf{G}}|_{F}
for a hyperfinite subforest
F\subseteq E
.

(b)

\alpha\in\text{\sf bmm}_{+}(\rho_{\mathbf{G}})
is exposed if and only if
\alpha=\eta_{\mathbf{G}}|_{F}
for a hyperfinite essential spanning forest
F\subseteq E
.

We also give characterization of these kind of subsets used in Theorem
1.2
.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
\mathbf{G}=(J,\mu,E)
be a graphing and
F\subseteq E
be a Borel subset of edges. Then the following are equivalent:

(i)

(J,\mu,F)
is a hyperfinite forest;

(ii)

F
is a Borel subset of the edge set of a hyperfinite essential spanning forest;

(iii)

\rho_{\mathbf{G}}(F)=\eta_{\mathbf{G}}(F)
.

Similarly, the following are equivalent:

(i’)

(J,\mu,F)\subseteq(J,\mu,E)
is a hyperfinite essential spanning forest;

(ii’)

\rho_{\mathbf{G}}(F)=\eta_{\mathbf{G}}(F)=\rho_{\mathbf{G}}(E)
.

Along the lines, we introduce the notion of
weakly exposed
points and prove structural properties of those, see Theorem
4.7
and Corollary
4.8
. In Section
4.5
, we show that hyperfinite forests satisfy an exchange property, which in turn leads to a conceptually new proof of the submodularity of the set function
\rho_{\mathbf{G}}
, different from the (somewhat lengthy) proof in
[
23
]
. The following question remains open.

Question 1.4
.

Are all extreme points of
\text{\sf bmm}_{+}(\rho_{\mathbf{G}})
exposed, and therefore hyperfinite spanning forests?

Duality.

Duality is an important concept of matroid theory, and it lines up nicely with duality of finite planar graphs: the dual of the cycle matroid of a planar map (embedded connected planar graph) is the cycle matroid of its planar dual. The question naturally arises: Does an analogous statement hold for planar graphings?

The notion of a “planar graphing” takes some work to set up. In order to talk about pairs of dual planar graphings, we define planar graphings
\mathbf{G}=(J,\mu,E)
in a rather restricted sense. The connected components of a
graphing map
are 2-vertex-connected graphs endowed with a specified planar embedding. We assume that the vertex sets of these planar maps have no accumulation points in the plane, and all faces are finite cycles, whose lengths are uniformly bounded. Then the dual graphing map
\mathbf{G}^{*}=(J^{*},\mu^{*},E^{*})
is well defined, and it has bounded degrees. For a dual pair of graphing maps, there is a measure preserving Borel bijection
\sigma\colon E\to E^{*}
such that the image of the star of a vertex of
\mathbf{G}
is a facial cycle in
\mathbf{G}^{*}
and vice versa. We also note that the hyperfiniteness of
\mathbf{G}
and of
\mathbf{G}^{*}
are equivalent
[
3
, Example 3.11, Proposition 3.12]
. See Section
5
for an exact definition, and also
[
3
]
and
[
10
]
for a more general treatment.

Remark 1.5
.

The above assumptions are made solely to avoid technical difficulties, not because they are necessary for the results presented here. Our aim in this paper is to showcase how duality works, and these assumptions allow us to do so with minimal distraction. The aforementioned works
[
3
]
and
[
10
]
demonstrate that, with dedicated treatment, such technical questions can be addressed to prove theorems for planar graphings without these restrictions.

The dual of the cycle matroid, called the
cocycle
matroid of
\mathbf{G}=(J,\mu,E)
, can be defined for any (not necessarily planar) graphing by its rank function

\rho^{*}_{\mathbf{G}}(A)=\rho_{\mathbf{G}}(E\setminus A)+\eta_{\mathbf{G}}(A)-\rho_{\mathbf{G}}(E).

For a general notion of duality for submodular setfunctions, see
[
6
]
. Our first result here shows that in the hyperfinite case duality works similarly as in the finite case.

Theorem 1.6
.

Let
\mathbf{G}
and
\mathbf{G}^{*}
be a hyperfinite pair of dual planar graphings. Then
\rho^{*}_{\mathbf{G}}=\rho_{\mathbf{G}^{*}}
and
\rho^{*}_{\mathbf{G}^{*}}=\rho_{\mathbf{G}}
.

However, when
\mathbf{G}
and
\mathbf{G}^{*}
are not hyperfinite, the two matroids do not form a dual pair. This is the case, for example, if the components of
\mathbf{G}
and
\mathbf{G}^{*}
are dual hyperbolic tilings. This example leads us to an interesting area, motivated by group theory, namely the theory of “cost”. The
cost
of a graphing
\textrm{\rm cost}(\mathbf{G})
is a nonnegative number, designed to measure the minimal amount of edges needed to generate the connected components of a graphing; see the precise definition in Subsection
2.2
. Every hyperfinite graphing has cost at most
1
, and exactly
1
if all components are infinite. On the other hand, if a planar graphing is not hyperfinite, then its cost is strictly more than
1

[
10
]
.

Theorem 1.7
.

Let
\mathbf{G}
and
\mathbf{G}^{*}
be a non-hyperfinite pair of dual planar graphings. Then
\rho^{*}_{\mathbf{G}^{*}}\neq\rho_{\mathbf{G}}
. Moreover,
\rho^{*}_{\mathbf{G}^{*}}
is a submodular function on
E
that attains the cost, i.e.
\rho^{*}_{\mathbf{G}^{*}}(E)=\textrm{\rm cost}(\mathbf{G})
.

Both submodular functions
\rho_{\mathbf{G}}
and
\rho^{*}_{\mathbf{G}^{*}}
are natural to consider, as supported by the following. Let
\mathbf{G}
be a graphing and
<
be an arbitrary Borel total order on the edges. We define the
free maximal spanning forest

\mathbf{T}_{f}
of
\mathbf{G}
by simultaneously deleting all edges
e
of
\mathbf{G}
for which there exists a cycle
C
containing
e
in which
e
is
<
-minimal. The
wired maximal spanning forest

\mathbf{T}_{w}
is similar to the free version, but we also remove any edge that is minimal with respect to the ordering on a two-way infinite path. It is straightforward to check that both
\mathbf{T}_{f},\mathbf{T}_{w}
are acyclic and
\mathbf{T}_{w}\subseteq\mathbf{T}_{f}
. We refer to
[
24
, Section 11]
for a thorough introduction. It is also true that
\mathbf{T}_{w}
is an essential spanning forest of
\mathbf{G}
. We show the following.

Theorem 1.8
.

Let
\mathbf{G}
and
\mathbf{G}^{*}
be a pair of dual planar graphings and
<
be an arbitrary Borel total order on the edges. The Wired Maximal Spanning Forest
\mathbf{T}^{<}_{w}
is an exposed point of
\text{\sf bmm}^{+}(\rho_{\mathbf{G}})
, while the Free Maximal Spanning Forest
\mathbf{T}^{<}_{f}
is an exposed point of
\text{\sf bmm}^{+}(\rho^{*}_{\mathbf{G}^{*}})
.

Matroids beyond the hyperfinite.

While the definition of the cycle matroid via (
1
) is a consistent generalization of the finite notion, it does have a drawback: the rank of the full edge set is at most
1
. This is natural for finite graphs, as spanning trees have average degree slightly below 2. For graphings, however, spanning trees can have higher average degree, so if there is a notion of a matroid whose bases are spanning trees instead of essential spanning trees, then the total rank should be larger than
1
. Theorems
1.7
and
1.8
above show that for planar graphings, the dual matroid of the dual graphing provides such a submodular rank function.

To further justify the study of
\rho_{\mathbf{G}}
, we highlight that even though
\rho_{\mathbf{G}}
only sees spanning forests that are hyperfinite, it still carries a lot of information about the graphing. In the finite case, Whitney proved that every 3-connected graph is uniquely determined by its cycle matroid. In an upcoming work
[
4
]
, we study analogous problems for graphings.

Question 1.9
.

Under what assumptions does
\rho_{\mathbf{G}}
determine the graphing
\mathbf{G}
?

As a first step in this direction, we could show that
\rho_{\mathbf{G}}
already determines acyclic graphings with minimum degree 3 up to
isomorphism
in the following sense: For a graphing
\mathbf{G}
and an acyclic graphing
\mathbf{T}
of minimum degree 3, if
\rho_{\mathbf{G}}\cong\rho_{\mathbf{T}}
then
\mathbf{G}\cong\mathbf{T}
. In both cases,
\cong
means that there exists a measure preserving bijection between the ground spaces, up to measure zero, that maps one structure to the other.

Preliminaries

2.1
Submodular setfunctions and matroids

A setfunction
\varphi\colon\mathcal{B}\to\mathbb{R}
on a set-algebra
(J,\mathcal{B})
is
submodular
if
\varphi(X)+\varphi(Y)\geq\varphi(X\cap Y)+\varphi(X\cup Y)
holds for
X,Y\in\mathcal{B}
. One of the most basic examples of a submodular function is the rank function of a matroid. A
matroid

M=(E,r)
is defined by its finite
ground set

E
and its
rank function

r\colon 2^{E}\to\mathbb{Z}_{+}
that satisfies the so-called
rank axioms
: (R1)
r(\emptyset)=0
, (R2)
X\subseteq Y\Rightarrow r(X)\leq r(Y)
, (R3)
r(X)\leq|X|
, and (R4)
r(X)+r(Y)\geq r(X\cap Y)+r(X\cup Y)
. Note that axiom (R4) requires submodularity of the rank function. A set
X\subseteq E
is called
independent
if
r(X)=|X|
and
dependent
otherwise. The
circuits
of the matroid are formed by its (inclusionwise) minimal dependent sets, while maximal independent sets are called
bases
. The rank axioms imply that any two bases have the same cardinality. For further details on matroids, the reader is referred to
[
27
]
.

2.2
Graphings and hyperfiniteness

We give the basic definitions, but point to
[
21
, Chapters 18 and 21]
for a thorough introduction. Let
(J,\mu)
be a standard Borel probability space.
A
graphing
is a graph
\mathbf{G}
with vertex set
V(\mathbf{G})=J
and Borel edge set
E\subseteq\binom{J}{2}
, in which all degrees are finite, and

\int_{A}\deg(B,x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu(x)=\int_{B}\deg(A,x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu(x)

for all measurable sets
A,B\subseteq J
, where
\deg(S,x)
is the number of edges from
x\in J
to
S\subseteq J
. This assumption allows one to meaningfully define the
edge measure

\eta_{\mathbf{G}}
on Borel subsets
F\subseteq E
by setting

\eta_{\mathbf{G}}(F)=\frac{1}{2}\int\deg_{F}(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu(x),

where
\deg_{F}(x)
is the degree of
x
in
F
. The measure
\eta_{\mathbf{G}}
is then concentrated on
E_{\mathbf{G}}
. It is worth noting that
F^{\prime}\subseteq F\subseteq E
implies
\eta_{\mathbf{G}^{F}}(F^{\prime})=\eta_{\mathbf{G}}(F^{\prime})
, where
\mathbf{G}^{F}=(J,\mu,F)
is the graphing obtained by deleting the edges of
\mathbf{G}
in
E\setminus F
.

An acyclic graphing, that is, a graphing in which almost all components are trees, is called a
treeing
. We will use the following characterizations of a treeing being hyperfinite.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
\mathbf{G}=(J,\mu,E)
be a graphing. Then the following are equivalent:

(i)

\mathbf{G}
is a hyperfinite treeing;

(ii)

almost every component of
\mathbf{G}
is a tree with at most
2
ends;

(iii)

\eta_{\mathbf{G}}(E)=\rho_{\mathbf{G}}(E)
.

(iv)

\eta_{\mathbf{G}}=\rho_{\mathbf{G}}
as setfunctions;

Proof.

\ref{levitti}\Leftrightarrow\ref{levittii}
: The equivalence was proved by Levitt
[
19
]
and Adams
[
1
]
; a more recent proof can be found in
[
2
]
.

\ref{levitti}\Rightarrow\ref{levittiii}
: The implication was proved in
[
22
]
.

\ref{levittiii}\Rightarrow\ref{levittiv}
: It was proved in
[
22
]
that
\rho_{\mathbf{G}}\leq\eta_{\mathbf{G}}
. Now, using the subadditivity of
\rho_{\mathbf{G}}
, we get

\rho_{\mathbf{G}}(F)+\rho_{\mathbf{G}}(E\setminus F)\geq\rho_{\mathbf{G}}(E)=\eta_{\mathbf{G}}(E)=\eta_{\mathbf{G}}(F)+\eta_{\mathbf{G}}(E\setminus F)\geq\rho_{\mathbf{G}}(F)+\rho_{\mathbf{G}}(E\setminus F).

Therefore equality holds throughout, implying
\rho_{\mathbf{G}}(B)=\eta_{\mathbf{G}}(B)
for any Borel set
F\subseteq E
.

\ref{levittiv}\Rightarrow\ref{levitti}
:
First we prove that
\mathbf{G}
is a treeing, and then we verify hyperfiniteness.

Assume that
\mathbf{G}
is not a treeing. Then there exists some positive integer
k
such that the set of vertices that lie on cycles of length
k
has positive measure. Since each vertex has bounded degree in
\mathbf{G}
, every vertex is contained in bounded many
k
-cycles. Hence there exists
C\subseteq E_{\mathbf{G}}
such that
C
is a vertex-disjoint union of
k
-cycles, and
\eta_{\mathbf{G}}(C)>0
. Let
C^{\prime}\subseteq C
be Borel such that
C^{\prime}
contains exactly one edge in each
k
-cycle. Then

\eta_{\mathbf{G}}(E\setminus C^{\prime})=\rho_{\mathbf{G}}(E\setminus C^{\prime})=\rho_{\mathbf{G}}(E)=\eta_{\mathbf{G}}(E)>\eta_{\mathbf{G}}(E\setminus C^{\prime})=\eta_{\mathbf{G}}(E\setminus C^{\prime}),

a contradiction.

Now we show that
G
is hyperfinite. Let
U=\{v\in J\mid|V(\mathbf{G}_{x})|=\infty\}
and let
F\subseteq E
denote the set of edges going between vertices in
U
. Then
\eta_{\mathbf{G}}(F)=\mu(U)
. A graphing is hyperfinite if and only if its restriction to the infinite components is hyperfinite;
let us denote the restriction by
\mathbf{G}|_{U}
. Although
\mathbf{G}|_{U}
is not necessarily a graphing as the underlying measure might not be probability measure, one can scale it back to
1
. Here the measure of the edges is
\eta_{\mathbf{G}}(F)=\rho_{\mathbf{G}}(F)=\int_{U}1d\mu(x)=\mu(U)
, thus
\mathbf{G}|_{U}
is hyperfinite, see
[
2
, Corollary 8.10]
.

Lemma
2.1
is also related to the theory of cost, which we introduce very briefly; see
[
11

\mathbf{G}=(J,\mu,E_{\mathbf{G}})

\mathbf{G}\sim\mathbf{H}

\textrm{\rm cost}(\mathbf{G})=\inf\big\{\eta_{\mathbf{H}}(E_{\mathbf{H}})\ \big|\ \mathbf{H}\sim\mathbf{G}\big\}.

For a graphing
\mathbf{G}=(J,\mu,E)
with all components of size
n
the cost is
1-\frac{1}{n}
, and for hyperfinite graphings with infinite components the cost is 1. In general, for any Borel subset
F\subseteq E
, we have
\rho_{\mathbf{G}}(F)\leq\textrm{\rm cost}(\mathbf{G}^{F})\leq\eta_{\mathbf{G}}(F)
. Levitt
[
19
]
characterized those cases when the first inequality holds with equality.

Theorem 2.3

(Levitt)
.

Let
\mathbf{G}=(J,\mu,E)
be a graphing and
F\subseteq E
be a Borel subset of edges. Then
\textrm{\rm cost}(\mathbf{G}^{F})
is attained and
\rho_{\mathbf{G}}(F)=\textrm{\rm cost}(\mathbf{G}^{F})
if and only if
\mathbf{G}^{F}
is hyperfinite.

It follows that
\rho_{\mathbf{G}}(F)
and
\textrm{\rm cost}(\mathbf{G}^{F})
, as functions in
F
, might be different. This also follows from the simple observation that while the rank is always submodular, the cost is not necessarily so: for an example, consider the Bernoulli graphing on
T_{3}\times\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{Z}
. Gaboriau
[
12
]
proved that one cannot “cut costs” on treeings, that is, their edge measure is the same as their cost. This property in fact characterizes treeings.

Theorem 2.4

(Gaboriau)
.

Let
\mathbf{G}=(J,\mu,E)
be a graphing and
F\subseteq E
be a Borel subset of edges. Then
\mathbf{G}^{F}
is a treeing if and only if
\textrm{\rm cost}(\mathbf{G}^{F})=\eta_{\mathbf{G}}(F)
.

For a graph
G
, we refer to its vertex and edge sets as
V(G)
and
E(G)
, respectively, and the same notation is used for graphings.

2.3
Local and local-global convergence

Intuitively, a sequence
(G_{1},G_{2},\dots)
of finite graphs is said to be
locally
(or
Benjamini-Schramm
)
convergent
if the local behaviour around a random vertex of
G_{n}
converges as
n\to\infty
. Formally, let
r\in\mathbb{N}
denote our radius of sight, and
\alpha
denote a rooted, connected graph of radius
r
. The
local statistics

P_{r}(G,\alpha)
of a finite graph
G
are the probabilities
\mathbb{P}_{v}\big[B_{G}(v,r)\cong\alpha\big]
, where the vertex
v\in V(G)
is chosen uniformly at random,
B_{G}(v,r)
denotes the
r
-ball around
v
in
G
, and the isomorphism has to respect the root. One can define
P_{r}(G,\alpha)
analogously for a graphing
\mathbf{G}
, with the random vertex being chosen according to
\mu
. A sequence
(G_{1},G_{2},\dots)
of finite graphs converges locally to a graphing
\mathbf{G}
if the local statistics converge, i.e.
P_{r}(G_{n},\alpha)\to P_{r}(\mathbf{G},\alpha)
for all
r
and
\alpha
.

Local-global convergence is a more refined notion of convergence, see
[
13
]
for an introduction. We use a colored version of the neighborhood statistics. For a fixed vertex-coloring
c\colon V(G)\to[k]
, the
colored neighborhood statistics

P_{r}(G,c,\alpha)
is obtained as before: we choose a vertex
v\in V(G)
uniformly at random, and then consider its colored
r
-neighborhood
\big(B_{G}(v,r),c|_{B_{G}(r,v)}\big)
. Then the probabilities
P_{r}[G,c]=\big(P_{r}(G,c,\alpha)\big)_{\alpha}
form a distribution on the set of rooted, colored, connected graphs
\alpha
of radius at most
r
. Let
Q_{k,r}(G)
denote the (finite) set of possible colored neighborhood statistics we can obtain by different colorings:

Q_{k,r}(G)=\big\{P_{r}[G,c]~\big|~c\colon V(G)\to[k]\big\}.

The set
Q_{k,r}(\mathbf{G})
is defined analogously for a graphing, with the assumption that the coloring
c\colon V(\mathbf{G})\to[k]
is required to be Borel. The sequence
G_{n}
locally-globally converges to
\mathbf{G}
, if
Q_{k,r}(G_{n})\to Q_{k,r}(\mathbf{G})
in the Hausdorff distance. The local-global convergence of
G_{n}
means that for any
r,k\in\mathbb{N}
and
\varepsilon>0
, there is a threshold such that, for
i
large enough, the colored neighborhood distribution
P_{r}[G_{i},c]
for any
k
-coloring
c
can be approximately modeled on
\mathbf{G}
. That is, we can find some Borel coloring
c^{\prime}
of
\mathbf{G}
such that
P_{r}[G_{i},c]
and
P_{r}[\mathbf{G},c^{\prime}]
are
\varepsilon
-close. Vice versa, Borel colorings of
\mathbf{G}
can be approximately modeled on
G_{i}
.

2.4
Quotient-convergence of setfunctions

The quotient-convergence of setfunctions was introduced in
[
5
]
. Let
\varphi\colon\mathcal{B}\to\mathbb{R}
be a setfunction on a set-algebra
(J,\mathcal{B})
with
\varphi(\emptyset)=0
. The
k
-quotient set

Q_{k}(\varphi)
is then defined as the set of all quotients of
\varphi
on
[k]
, that is, the set of all setfunctions
\varphi\circ F^{-1}
where
F\colon J\to[k]
is a measurable map. Note that
Q_{k}(\varphi)
is a subset of
\mathbb{R}^{2^{k}}
, with the two coordinates
\varphi\circ F^{-1}(\emptyset)=0
and
\varphi\circ F^{-1}(J)=\varphi(J)
being fixed. The
quotient profile
of
\varphi
is defined as the sequence
(Q_{1}(\varphi),Q_{2}(\varphi),\dots)
, while the sequence of closures
(\overline{Q}_{1}(\varphi),\overline{Q}_{2}(\varphi),\dots)
is the
closed quotient profile
of
\varphi
. A sequence
(\varphi_{1},\varphi_{2},\dots)
of setfunctions on set-algebras
(J_{1},\mathcal{B}_{1}),(J_{2},\mathcal{B}_{2}),\dots
is
quotient-convergent
if for every positive integer
k
, the quotient sets
(Q_{k}(\varphi_{1}),Q_{k}(\varphi_{2}),\dots)
form a Cauchy sequence in the Hausdorff distance
d_{{\text{\rm Haus}}_{2^{k}}}
on the
2^{k}
-dimensional Euclidean space. The sequence
(\varphi_{1},\varphi_{2},\dots)

quotient-converges to
\varphi
for some setfunction
\varphi
if the sequence
(Q_{k}(\varphi_{1}),Q_{k}(\varphi_{2}),\dots)
converges to
Q_{k}(\varphi)
in the Hausdorff distance, denoted by
\varphi_{n}\rightarrowtail\varphi
. For a finite
measurable partition

\mathcal{P}=\{P_{1},\dots,P_{q}\}
of
J
, that is, each
P_{i}
is measurable, then
\varphi/\mathcal{P}\colon 2^{[k]}\to\mathbb{R}
denotes the map defined by
(\varphi/\mathcal{P})(X)=\varphi(\cup_{i\in X}P_{i})
for
X\subseteq[k]
.

3
Connection to sparse graph limits

We first prove Theorem
1.1
in Section
3.1
, showing that if a sequence of finite bounded-degree graphs converges to the graphing in the local-global sense, then the corresponding sequence of rank functions quotient-converges to the rank function of the graphing. Then, in Section
3.2
, we present an example showing that assuming local convergence of the graph sequence is not enough.

3.1
Continuity for local-global convergence

The following result appeared in
[
23
]
.

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
(G_{1},G_{2},\dots)
be a sequence of finite graphs with all degrees bounded by
D
that converges to a graphing
\mathbf{G}
in the local sense. Then,
r_{G_{n}}(E(G_{n}))/|V(G_{n})|\to\rho_{\mathbf{G}}(E(\mathbf{G}))
.

We can strengthen this result to imply Theorem
1.1
by the following observation. Given any graphing parameter
f
, we can turn it into a setfunction
\varphi_{f}
on subsets of
E(\mathbf{G})
by setting

\varphi_{\mathbf{G},f}(F)=f(\mathbf{G}^{F}).

Theorem 3.2
.

Let
(G_{1},G_{2},\dots)
be a sequence of finite graphs with all degrees bounded by
D
that converges to a graphing
\mathbf{G}
in the local-global sense. If
f
is continuous with respect to local convergence then
\varphi_{\mathbf{G}_{n},f}\rightarrowtail\varphi_{\mathbf{G},f}
.

Proof.

For sake of brevity, let
\varphi_{n}=\varphi_{\mathbf{G}_{n},f}
,
\varphi=\varphi_{\mathbf{G},f}
,
Q^{n}_{k}=Q_{k}(\varphi_{n})
and
Q_{k}=Q_{k}(\varphi)
. We need to prove that for all
k
,
Q^{n}_{k}\to Q_{k}
in the Hausdorff distance. In other words, we need to show the following:

(1)

if
a\in Q_{k}
, then there exists
a_{n}\in Q^{n}_{k}
such that
a_{n}\to a
,

(2)

if
a_{n}\in Q^{n}_{k}
and
a_{n}\to a
, then
a\in\overline{Q}_{k}
.

We prove these using the following technical claim.

Claim 3.3
.

Let
(\mathbf{G}_{1},\mathcal{P}_{1}),(\mathbf{G}_{2},\mathcal{P}_{2}),\ldots
be a sequence of edge-coloured graphings with at most
k
colours that converges as coloured graphings to the coloured graphing
(\mathbf{G},\mathcal{P})
in the local sense. Then,
\varphi_{n}/\mathcal{P}_{n}
converges to
\varphi/\mathcal{P}
in
\mathbb{R}^{2^{k}}
.

Proof of claim..

For
A\subseteq[k]
, let
\tilde{A}_{n}
(respectively
\tilde{A}
) denote the set of edges of
\mathbf{G}_{n}
(respectively
\mathbf{G}
) with colour in
A
. Clearly,
\varphi_{n}(\tilde{A}_{n})=(\varphi_{n}/\mathcal{P}_{n})(A)
, and similarly for
\varphi
,
\mathcal{P}
, and
\tilde{A}
. We denote by
\mathcal{H}_{A,n}
the subgraphing of
\mathbf{G}_{n}
with edge set
\tilde{A}_{n}
, and define
\mathcal{H}_{A}
similarly. The coloured local convergence of the sequence
(\mathbf{G}_{n},\mathcal{P}_{n})
implies that
\mathcal{H}_{A,n}
locally converges to
\mathcal{H}_{A}
for all
A
. As
f
is continuous with respect to local convergence, we have

(\varphi_{n}/\mathcal{P}_{n})(A)=\varphi_{n}(\tilde{A}_{n})=f(\mathcal{H}_{A,n})\to f(\mathcal{H}_{A})=\varphi(\tilde{A})=(\varphi/\mathcal{P})(A),

concluding the proof of the claim.
∎

Proof of
(1)
. As
a\in Q_{k}
, there is a Borel partition
\mathcal{P}
of the edges of
\mathbf{G}
such that
\varphi/\mathcal{P}=a
. By
\mathcal{P}
being a Borel partition and by local-global convergence, we can find some Borel partition
\mathcal{P}_{n}
of the edges of
\mathbf{G}_{n}
such that the sequence
(\mathbf{G}_{n},\mathcal{P}_{n})
locally converges to
(\mathbf{G},\mathcal{P})
as coloured graphings
1
1

1

Usually one defines local-global convergence using vertex colorings, but edge colorings can also be approximated, see
[
21
, Proposition 19.15]
.
. By Claim
3.3
, the corresponding
a_{n}=\varphi_{n}/\mathcal{P}_{n}
converges to
a
.

Proof of
(2)
. Assume
a_{n}\to a
. For all
a_{n}
, there exists a partition
\mathcal{P}_{n}
such that
a_{n}=\varphi_{n}/\mathcal{P}_{n}
. By possibly taking a subsequence, we may assume that the sequence
(\mathbf{G}_{n},\mathcal{P}_{n})
converges locally as coloured graphings. Let
(\mathbf{G}^{*},\mathcal{P}^{*})
represent the limit. The graphing
\mathbf{G}^{*}
a priori has nothing to do with
\mathbf{G}
or the sequence
\mathbf{G}_{n}
because local-global convergence of the sequence
\mathbf{G}_{n}
does not imply a partition
\mathcal{P}
of the edges of
\mathbf{G}
such that
(\mathbf{G},\mathcal{P})
represents the limit of
(\mathbf{G}_{n},\mathcal{P}_{n})
. Nevertheless, by completeness, such a coloured graphing
(\mathbf{G}^{*},\mathcal{P}^{*})
exists. However, local-global convergence provides arbitrarily good approximations of
(\mathbf{G}^{*},\mathcal{P}^{*})
on
\mathbf{G}
. That is, there exist Borel-partitions
\mathcal{R}_{n}
of
\mathbf{G}
such that the

applied to the sequence
(\mathbf{G}_{n},\mathcal{P}_{n})
, we get that
a=\varphi_{\mathbf{G}^{*}}/\mathcal{P}^{*}
. Let
b_{n}=\varphi/\mathcal{R}_{n}

and applying the claim to

gives

as stated, finishing the proof of the theorem.

.

Let

be the graphing parameter that assigns to every graphing the total rank of its edge set. Then

, and Theorems

3.2
Non-continuity for local convergence

It was proved in
[
23
, Theorem 4.7]
that the rank of the whole edge set of a graph is locally estimable, i.e. continuous with respect to local convergence
[
21
, Theorem 22.3]
. We show by an example that local convergence does not guarantee quotient-convergence of the matroids.

Example 3.4
.

Let
(G_{1},G_{2},\dots)
be a sequence of
3
-regular,
c
-expander graphs with
|V(G_{n})|=2n
and
0<c<1
, such that their girth
g_{n}
tends to infinity. Let
H_{n}=G_{n}\cup G_{n}
, that is,
H_{n}
is the disjoint union of two copies of
G_{n}
. Clearly, the interlaced sequence
G_{1},H_{1},G_{2},H_{2},\dots
is locally convergent (it converges to any 3-regular treeing). Let
\rho_{G_{n}}=r_{G_{n}}/|V(G_{n})|
, and similarly for
H_{n}
. We claim that
Q_{2}(\rho_{G_{n}})
is far from
Q_{2}(\rho_{H_{m}})
if
n
and
m
are large, so the sequence
(\rho_{G_{1}},\rho_{H_{1}},\rho_{G_{2}},\rho_{H_{2}},\dots)
is not quotient-convergent.

The partition of
H_{n}
into two copies of
G_{n}
defines a quotient of the cycle matroid:

a_{n}\in Q_{2}(H_{n})\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{2^{2}},\ a_{n}(\emptyset)=0,\ a_{n}(\{1\})=a_{n}(\{2\})=\frac{2n-1}{4n},\ a_{n}(\{1,2\})=\frac{4n-2}{4n}.

The limit of
a_{n}
is

a\in\mathbb{R}^{2^{2}},\ a(\emptyset)=0,\ a(\{1\})=a(\{2\})=\frac{1}{2},\ a(\{1,2\})=1.

We show that if
n
is sufficiently large, then
G_{n}
cannot have
2
-quotients that are
\varepsilon
-approximations of
a
, where
\varepsilon=c/32
. This will imply that
d^{\text{\rm Haus}}(Q_{2}(\rho_{G_{n}}),Q_{2}(\rho_{H_{m}}))>\varepsilon
. We choose
n
large enough so that
g_{n}>32/c
.

By the definition of expander sequences, for every
S\subseteq V(G_{n})
with
|S|\leq n
, the number of edges connecting
S
to
V(G_{n})\setminus S
is at least
c\cdot|S|
. Let
E(G_{n})=E_{1}\cup E_{2}
be any 2-partition of the edges. For
i\in\{0,1,2,3\}
, we denote by
V_{i}
the set of vertices of
G_{n}
with degree
i
in
E_{1}
and let
m_{i}=|V_{i}|
. Suppose that this partition results in a quotient closer to
a
than
\varepsilon
. Then,
|\rho_{G_{n}}(E_{i})-\frac{1}{2}|<\varepsilon
for
i=1,2
, and so
|r_{G_{n}}(E_{i})-n|<2\varepsilon n
. Let us note that
r_{G_{n}}(E_{1})\leq 2n-m_{0}
, and the previous two inequalities imply
m_{0}\leq(1+2\varepsilon)n
. The same bound holds for
m_{3}
.

We bound the number of
E_{1}
-components as follows. The number of singletons is clearly
m_{0}
. If an
E_{1}
-component is not a tree, then it has at least
g_{n}
vertices, and hence the number of such components is at most
2n/g_{n}
. Finally, if a component is a non-singleton tree, then it has at least
2
vertices of degree
1
, so the number of such components is bounded by
m_{1}/2
. Hence

m_{0}+\frac{m_{1}}{2}\geq 2n-r_{G_{n}}(E_{1})-\frac{2n}{g_{n}}\geq\Big(1-2\varepsilon-\frac{2}{g_{n}}\Big)n\geq\Big(1-\frac{c}{8}\Big)n.

Adding the analogous inequality for
E_{2}
, we get

2m_{0}+m_{1}+m_{2}+2m_{3}\geq\Big(2-\frac{c}{4}\Big)n,

and using that
m_{0}+m_{1}+m_{2}+m_{3}=2n
, we obtain
m_{1}+m_{2}\leq cn/4
. Without loss of generality, we may assume that
m_{0}\leq m_{3}
, implying that
m_{0}\leq|V(G_{n})|/2
. The neighbours of
V_{0}
in
G
are contained in
V_{1}\cup V_{2}
, and hence by the expansion of
G_{n}
, we have
m_{1}+m_{2}\geq cm_{0}
. So,

m_{0}+m_{1}+m_{2}+m_{3}\leq\frac{c+1}{c}(m_{1}+m_{2})+m_{3}\leq\frac{c+1}{c}\,\frac{c}{4}\,n+\Big(1+\frac{c}{16}\Big)n=\Big(1+\frac{5c}{16}\Big)n<2n.

This is a contradiction.

4
Exposed minorizing measures and hyperfinite spanning forests

In this section, we focus on the set of minorizing finitely additive measures of the rank function of a graphing. In Section
4.1
, we establish a connection between the Radon-Nikodym derivatives of such measures and hyperfinite essential spanning forests. We characterize the exposed points of minorizing finitely additive measures in Section
4.2
, and then introduce the notion of weakly exposed points in Section
4.3
. Based on these observations, we prove Theorems
1.2
and
1.3
in Section
4.4
. Finally, in Section
4.5
, we give a conceptually new proof of the submodularity of the rank function
\rho_{\mathbf{G}}
of a graphing
\mathbf{G}
that might be of independent combinatorial interest.

4.1
Measures, functions and subsets

Let
\mathbf{G}=(J,\mu,E)
be a graphing and
\rho_{\mathbf{G}}
be the rank function of its cycle matroid. If
\mathbf{G}
has no finite components, then
\rho_{\mathbf{G}}(E)=1
. Since any
\alpha\in\text{\sf mm}_{+}(\rho_{\mathbf{G}})
is a
\sigma
-additive measure, it admits a Radon-Nikodym derivative with respect to
\eta_{\mathbf{G}}
, denoted by

f_{\alpha}(x)=\frac{\partial\alpha}{\partial\eta_{\mathbf{G}}}(x).

For a function
f\in L^{1}(\eta_{\mathbf{G}})
, let
\eta_{f}
denote the measure

\eta_{f}(A)=\int_{A}f\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\eta_{\mathbf{G}}.

By the above, we can identify the convex sets of measures
\text{\sf mm}_{+}(\rho_{\mathbf{G}})
and
\text{\sf bmm}_{+}(\rho_{\mathbf{G}})
with convex sets of measurable functions. By a slight abuse of notation, we say that a function
f\in L^{1}(\eta_{\mathbf{G}})
is in
\text{\sf mm}_{+}(\rho_{\mathbf{G}})
or
\text{\sf bmm}_{+}(\rho_{\mathbf{G}})
if the corresponding measure
\eta_{f}
is in
\text{\sf mm}_{+}(\rho_{\mathbf{G}})
or
\text{\sf bmm}_{+}(\rho_{\mathbf{G}})
, respectively. Note that
f_{\alpha}
takes values in
[0,1]
.

If
f_{\alpha}
is
0
-
1
valued
\eta_{\mathbf{G}}
-almost everywhere, then
\alpha
is clearly extreme in both
\text{\sf mm}_{+}(\rho_{\mathbf{G}})
and
\text{\sf bmm}_{+}(\rho_{\mathbf{G}})
. In such a case, let us consider the corresponding measurable set of edges
E_{\alpha}=\{e\in E_{\mathbf{G}}\mid f_{\alpha}(e)=1\}
. Our first observation is that
\mathbf{T}=(J,\mu,E_{\alpha})
is a hyperfinite essential spanning forest.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
G=(J,\mu,E)
be a graphing and
\mathbf{T}=(J,\mu,F)
be a spanning subgraphing. The following are equivalent:

(i)

\mathbf{T}
is a hyperfinite spanning forest;

(ii)

the measure
\alpha_{F}(A)=\eta_{\mathbf{G}}(A\cap F)
is in
\text{\sf mm}_{+}(\rho_{\mathbf{G}})
.

Similarly, the following are equivalent:

(i’)

\mathbf{T}
is a hyperfinite essential spanning forest;

(ii’)

the measure
\alpha_{F}(A)=\eta_{\mathbf{G}}(A\cap F)
is in
\text{\sf bmm}_{+}(\rho_{\mathbf{G}})
.

Proof.

\ref{him:i}\Rightarrow\ref{him:ii}
: Let
B
be a Borel subset of
E
. Then
B\cap F
is clearly a hyperfinite forest, so
\alpha_{F}(B)=\eta_{\mathbf{G}}(B\cap F)=\rho_{\mathbf{G}}(B\cap F)\leq\rho_{\mathbf{G}}(B)
, where the first equality is by the definition of
\alpha_{F}
, the second equality holds by Lemma
2.1
, and the inequality follows from
[
23
, Lemma 4.3]
.

\ref{him:ii}\Rightarrow\ref{him:i}
: From
\alpha_{F}\leq\rho_{\mathbf{G}}\leq\eta_{\mathbf{G}}
it follows that
\eta_{\mathbf{T}}\leq\rho_{\mathbf{G}}|_{\mathbf{T}}=\rho_{\mathbf{T}}\leq\eta_{\mathbf{T}}
. Therefore, we get
\eta_{\mathbf{T}}=\rho_{\mathbf{T}}
, implying that
\mathbf{T}
is a hyperfinite spanning forest by Lemma
2.1
.

\ref{him:i'}\Rightarrow\ref{him:ii'}
: By the equivalence of
(i)
and
(ii)
, we have
\alpha_{F}\in\text{\sf mm}_{+}(\rho_{\mathbf{G}})
. By Corollary
2.2
, we have
\alpha_{F}(E)=\eta_{\mathbf{G}}(F)=\rho_{\mathbf{G}}(E)
, showing
\alpha_{F}\in\text{\sf bmm}_{+}(\rho_{\mathbf{G}})
.

\ref{him:ii'}\Rightarrow\ref{him:i'}
: By the equivalence of
(i)
and
(ii)
,
\mathbf{T}
is a hyperfinite forest, hence
\rho_{\mathbf{G}}(F)=\eta_{\mathbf{G}}(F)
. Since
\eta_{\mathbf{G}}(F)=\alpha_{F}(E)=\rho_{\mathbf{G}}(E)
, we get
\rho_{\mathbf{G}}(E)=\eta_{\mathbf{G}}(F)
, implying that
\mathbf{T}
is essential by Corollary
2.2
.
∎

4.2
Exposed points

Let
C
be a compact, convex subset of a locally convex topological space. We say that
v\in C
is exposed if there exists
\varphi
linear, continuous functional such that
v
is the unique maximizer of
\varphi
on
C
, that is,
\varphi(v)>\varphi(u)
for any
u\in C
,
u\neq v
. In this case, we say that
v
is
exposed
by the function
\varphi
and call
\varphi
a
witness function
for
v
. It is not difficult to see that exposed points are extreme. In this section we characterize the exposed points of
\text{\sf mm}_{+}(\rho_{\mathbf{G}})
and
\text{\sf bmm}_{+}(\rho_{\mathbf{G}})
. We prove that all these points are of the form
\alpha_{F}
where
F
is the edge set of some hyperfinite spanning forest, or in other words, all the exposed
f_{\alpha}
are
0
-
1
valued.

While being extreme is a purely algebraic property, being exposed my depend on the underlying topology. Therefore, first we need to specify which space to embed
\text{\sf mm}_{+}(\rho_{\mathbf{G}})
and
\text{\sf bmm}_{+}(\rho_{\mathbf{G}})
into. A natural choice would be using
L^{p}
with the weak topology for some
p\in[1,\infty)
, or
L^{\infty}
with the weak
∗
topology. As it turns out, the choice of the space does not matter as
\text{\sf mm}_{+}(\rho_{\mathbf{G}})
and
\text{\sf bmm}_{+}(\rho_{\mathbf{G}})
are compact and the exposed points are exactly same in all cases. For ease of discussion, we use the space
L^{1}
with the weak topology.

Theorem 4.2
.

Let
f_{\alpha}\in\text{\sf bmm}_{+}(\rho_{\mathbf{G}})
be exposed by a function
g\in L^{\infty}(E,\eta_{\mathbf{G}})
. Then,
f_{\alpha}
is
0
-
1
valued
\eta_{\mathbf{G}}
-almost everywhere.

Proof.

We treat
g
as a weight function on the edges, and our goal is to find a maximum weight hyperfinite essential spanning forest
F
with respect to this weight function. Recall that
\alpha_{F}
denotes the measure in
\text{\sf bmm}_{+}(\rho_{\mathbf{G}})
defined as
\alpha_{F}(A)=\eta_{\mathbf{G}}(A\cap F)
for all measurable
A\subseteq E
. We show that there exists an
F
satisfying
\alpha_{F}(\{g\geq t\})=\rho_{\mathbf{G}}(\{g\geq t\})
for every
t\in\mathbb{R}
. For any
\beta\in\text{\sf bmm}_{+}(\rho_{\mathbf{G}})
, in a layer-cake integration this implies

\int_{E}\mathbb{1}_{F}\cdot g\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\eta_{\mathbf{G}}=\int_{E}g\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\alpha_{F}\geq\int_{E}g\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\beta=\int_{E}f_{\beta}\cdot g\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\eta_{\mathbf{G}}.

By the assumption that
f_{\alpha}
is the unique maximizer, this proves
f_{\alpha}=\mathbb{1}_{F}
in
L^{1}(E,\eta_{\mathbf{G}})
.

To verify the existence of a required
F
, we run a variant of Kruskal’s greedy algorithm
[
18
]
on
\mathbf{G}
, sometimes called Prim’s invasion algorithm. As a tie-breaking rule, we fix an arbitrary Borel ordering of
E
in advance, and whenever there is a tie among the
g
values of finitely many edges considered, we choose the maximal one according to the Borel ordering. The algorithm constructs Borel edge sets
F_{1}\subseteq F_{2}\subseteq\dots
, with
F=\bigcup F_{n}
being the final output. In the first step, every vertex simultaneously chooses the incident edge with the highest
g
-value to be included in
F_{1}
. During the
n^{\mathrm{th}}
step, every finite component of
F_{n-1}
adds the edge with the highest
g
-value from the set of edges leaving it if such an edge exists, and thus we obtain
F_{n}
. In case there is no edge leaving a finite component, observe that it is also a component in theoriginal graphing and
F_{n-1}
is already a spanning tree in it. Also note that infinite components do not add any edges, but it may happen that a finite component adds an edge that connects it to an infinite one.

It is not difficult to see that the sets
F_{n}
are Borel, cycles cannot be created, and
F
is essential.
We claim that
\eta_{\mathbf{G}}(F)=\rho_{\mathbf{G}}(E)
, so
F
is hyperfinite by Theorem
2.1
. Indeed, set up a payment function as follows. In the beginning, every vertex receives
1
token. In a general step, every finite connected component gives
1
token to the edge it chooses. After the addition of the edges, for every finite connected component there is exactly one edge who received
2
tokens. This edge gives
1
token back to the component, thus ensuring that every finite component has a token at the beginning of the next step. Should a finite
F
-component become spanning in a finite
\mathbf{G}
-component, and therefore has no use for its token, this token is redistributed equally among the vertices of this component. In the end, vertices in finite
\mathbf{G}
-components paid
1-\frac{1}{|\mathbf{G}_{x}|}
token, and vertices in infinite
\mathbf{G}
-components paid 1 token. On the other hand, every edge in
F
received 1 token, hence
\rho_{\mathbf{G}}(E)=\eta_{\mathbf{G}}(F)
by the Mass Transport Principle. As
F
is hyperfinite, by Lemma
4.1
, we have
\alpha_{F}\in\text{\sf bmm}_{+}(\rho_{\mathbf{G}})
.

Let
A_{t}=\{g\geq t\}
and
F_{\geq t}=F\cap A_{t}
. Observe that
F_{t}
is an essential spanning forest of
A_{t}
. That is, for
\mu
-almost every
x\in J
,

(1)

if the
A_{t}
-component of
x
is finite, then
F_{\geq t}
is a spanning tree inside this component, and

(2)

if the
A_{t}
-component of
x
is infinite, then its

-component is also infinite – though it might be smaller.

For
(1)
, note that the edges leaving the
A_{t}
-component all have
g
-values strictly less than
t
, while being an
A_{t}
-component means that it can be connected by edges with
g
-value at least
t
. So the algorithm will connect these points before growing the component any further. For
(2)
, the reasoning is analogous. The conclusion, however, is different, This is due to the fact that we cannot guarantee the connectedness of
F_{\geq t}
inside the
A_{t}
-component, because the
F_{\geq t}
-components stop growing once they are all infinite.

Observations
(1)
and
(2)
imply
\alpha_{F}(A_{t})=\rho_{\mathbf{G}}(A_{t})
, completing the proof of the theorem.
∎

4.3
Weakly exposed points

Even in finite dimension, extreme points are not necessarily exposed. To see this, consider the following set in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
:

S=\big\{(x,y)\mid-1\leq x\leq 1,\ -1\leq y\leq 1\big\}\cup\big\{(x,y)\mid x^{2}+y^{2}\leq 1\big\}.

Then
(-1,0)
and
(1,0)
are clearly extreme points. However, there is a unique supporting hyperplane through any of them:
x=-1
through
(-1,0)
and
x=1
through
(1,0)
, hence these points are not exposed. Nevertheless, these extreme points can be exposed in finitely many steps by iteratively restricting the problem to the intersection of the convex set and a supporting hyperplane. In this subsection, we use this idea to generalize Theorem
4.2
to extreme points that we call
weakly exposed
.

We say that an extreme point
x\in\text{\sf bmm}_{+}(\rho_{\mathbf{G}})
is
exposed in two steps
by
L^{1}
functions, if one can find
g_{1},g_{2}\in L^{1}(E,\eta_{\mathbf{G}})
such that
\varphi_{g_{1}}(x)
is maximal in
\text{\sf bmm}_{+}(\rho_{\mathbf{G}})
, and
x
strictly maximizes
\varphi_{g_{2}}
in
\{y\in\text{\sf bmm}_{+}(\rho_{\mathbf{G}})\mid\varphi_{g_{1}}(y)=\varphi_{g_{1}}(x)\}
. Note that the proof of Theorem
4.2
yields the same conclusion for points that are exposed in two steps. The only difference is that during the algorithm, we always choose an edge that is maximal with respect to
g_{1}
, and in case of ties, we choose the one maximal with respect to
g_{2}
. Finally, if there is still a tie, we decide according to the Borel ordering we fixed at the beginning.

More generally, let
\alpha\in\omega_{1}
be a countable ordinal and let
\mathbf{g}\colon\alpha\to L^{1}(E,\eta_{\mathbf{G}})
be a list of functions. Let us define
\mathbf{g}(x)\in\mathbb{R}^{\alpha}
by
f(x)_{\beta}=\mathbf{g}(\beta)(x)
. Then,
\mathbf{g}
defines a Borel partial ordering on
\text{\sf bmm}_{+}(\rho_{\mathbf{G}})
based on the lexicographical ordering of the images of points of
\text{\sf bmm}_{+}(\rho_{\mathbf{G}})
in
\mathbb{R}^{\alpha}
. We denote this ordering by

and call a point
x\in\text{\sf bmm}_{+}(\rho_{\mathbf{G}})

exposed in
\alpha
steps
by the list
\mathbf{g}
if
x
is the unique maxima of
<_{\mathbf{g}}
in
\text{\sf bmm}_{+}(\rho_{\mathbf{G}})
. In other words, for all
y\in\text{\sf bmm}_{+}(\rho_{\mathbf{G}})
there exists an ordinal
\beta_{y}\leq\alpha
such that for all
\gamma\leq\beta_{y}
, we have
\mathbf{g}(\gamma)(x)=\mathbf{g}(\gamma)(y)
and
\mathbf{g}(\beta_{y})(x)>\mathbf{g}(\beta_{y})(y)
. We say
x\in\text{\sf bmm}_{+}(\rho_{\mathbf{G}})
is

, if it is exposed in
\alpha

\alpha
.

Theorem 4.7
.

Let
f_{\alpha}\in\text{\sf bmm}_{+}(\rho_{\mathbf{G}})

is
0
-
1

\eta_{\mathbf{G}}
-almost everywhere.

Proof.

The proof of Theorem
4.2
holds for weakly exposed points as well by always choosing the

-maximal edge.
∎

Corollary 4.8

Weakly exposed points of
\text{\sf bmm}_{+}(\rho_{\mathbf{G}})

5
Duality

In this section, we present various observations based on the idea that in an infinite planar graph, the complement of an essential spanning forest is an essential spanning forest of the dual.

Roughly speaking, a
planar graphing
is a graphing
\mathbf{G}=(J,\mu,E)
whose connected components are planar and are also endowed with a concrete embedding in a measurable way. The embedding is encoded by specifying the finite facial cycles of the components, and we assume this collection of edge sets to be a Borel set inside the standard Borel space of finite subsets of
E
. For the precise definition, see
[
10
, Section 3.2]
. The component of a
\mu
-random vertex, together with the embedding is a
unimodular random map
in the sense of
[
3
]
.

We will, in fact, restrict our attention to the case when all facial cycles of
\mathbf{G}
are finite, and refer to them as
faces
. The assumption of
2
-vertex-connectivity and bounded size of faces mentioned in the Introduction also applies. The fact that the encoding is Borel ensures that we can talk about the dual graphing
\mathbf{G}^{*}=(J^{*},\mu^{*},E^{*})
, where
J^{*}
is the set of faces of
\mathbf{G}
. There is a measure preserving Borel bijection
\sigma\colon E\to E^{*}
that denotes the usual correspondence:
\sigma(e)
connects two distinct faces
x_{1}^{*}
and
x_{2}^{*}
if and only if
e
is a common boundary edge of
x_{1}^{*}
and
x_{2}^{*}
. For sake of simplicity, we write
e^{*}=\sigma(e)
. For an edge set
F\subseteq E
, we use the notation
F^{*}=\{e^{*}\mid e\in F\}
.

Examples of planar graphings include Schreier graphings of p.m.p. actions of finitely generated groups with planar Cayley graphs (in particular surface groups), as well as Palm graphings of planar factor graphs of free invariant point processes on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
or
\mathbb{H}^{2}

[
25
, Remark 17]
.

Remark 5.1
.

For graphings, we usually assume
\mu(J)=1
for convenience. For finite graphs this corresponds to normalization by the number of vertices. However, when duals are considered, this normalization is less natural, since the dual graph typically does not have the same number of vertices. It would probably be more natural to normalize by the number of edges which is the same for the two graphs. Nevertheless, to be consistent with the rest of the paper, we do not change the normalization, and simply accept that the vertex measure of the dual graphing is not necessarily 1.

Remark 5.2
.

Limits of sequences of finite planar graphs are hyperfinite by the Lipton-Tarjan Planar Separation Theorem
[
20
]
. Nevertheless, planar graphings need not be hyperfinite. In fact, there is a sharp dichotomy between hyperfinite and non-hyperfinite planar graphings, see
[
3
]
and
[
29
]
.

We define the
cocyclic
matroid of
\mathbf{G}
by the infinite analogue of the usual dual rank function formula:

\rho^{*}_{\mathbf{G}}(F)=\rho_{\mathbf{G}}(E\setminus F)+\eta_{\mathbf{G}}(F)-\rho_{\mathbf{G}}(E).

Note that
\rho^{*}_{\mathbf{G}}
is submodular as it is the sum of a submodular and a modular function. Furthermore,
\rho_{\mathbf{G}}(E)\leq 1
, and equality holds if and only if almost all components of
\mathbf{G}
are infinite. Moreover, the rank of the full edge set in the cocycle matroid is
\rho^{*}_{\mathbf{G}}(E)=\eta_{\mathbf{G}}(E)-1
. There is a nice correspondence between the elements of the associated minorizing measures:

\alpha\in\text{\sf bmm}^{+}(\rho_{\mathbf{G}})\iff\eta_{\mathbf{G}}-\alpha\in\text{\sf bmm}^{+}(\rho^{*}_{\mathbf{G}}).

5.1
Duality for hyperfinite planar graphings

For finite planar graphs, the cocycle matroid is the cycle matroid of the dual. In the Borel setting, this holds only for hyperfinite planar graphings. In fact, we will exploit exactly this discrepancy between
\rho_{\mathbf{G}}
and
\rho^{*}_{\mathbf{G}^{*}}
in Subsection
5.2
to prove Theorem
1.7
.

Remark 5.3
.

Theorem
1.6
is actually a reformulation of Euler’s formula, stating that for any embedding of a finite planar graph, the equality
n+f=m+2
holds, where
n
,
m
and
f
denote the number of vertices, edges and faces, respectively. For a planar graphing
\mathbf{G}
with infinite components, the special case
\rho^{*}_{\mathbf{G}^{*}}(E^{*})=\rho_{\mathbf{G}}(E)
translates into
\mu(J)+\mu^{*}(J^{*})=\eta_{\mathbf{G}}(E)
, that is, the
2
in the formula disappears in the limit.

We present two proofs for Theorem
1.6
, one relying on Theorem
4.2
, the other using the Mass Transport Principle.

Proof of Theorem
1.6
using Theorem
4.2
.

First we prove
\rho^{*}_{\mathbf{G}^{*}}=\rho_{\mathbf{G}}
. We noted earlier that
\text{\sf bmm}^{+}(\rho^{*}_{\mathbf{G}^{*}})=\eta_{\mathbf{G}^{*}}-\text{\sf bmm}^{+}(\rho_{\mathbf{G}^{*}})
. By Theorem
4.2
, we know that exposed points of
\text{\sf bmm}^{+}(\rho_{\mathbf{G}})
and
\text{\sf bmm}^{+}(\rho_{\mathbf{G}^{*}})
are hyperfinite essential spanning forests of
\mathbf{G}
and
\mathbf{G}^{*}
, respectively. Note also that they are in a one-to-one correspondence by complementation. Here we use that
\mathbf{G}
and
\mathbf{G}^{*}
are hyperfinite, to ensure that the complement is again hyperfinite.

These observations together show that
\text{\sf bmm}^{+}(\rho_{\mathbf{G}})
and
\text{\sf bmm}^{+}(\rho^{*}_{\mathbf{G}^{*}})
have the same set of exposed points. This implies
\text{\sf bmm}^{+}(\rho_{\mathbf{G}})=\text{\sf bmm}^{+}(\rho^{*}_{\mathbf{G}^{*}})
, as they are the weak* closure of the convex hull of the same set, see Remark
4.6
. Finally, this implies
\rho_{\mathbf{G}}=\rho^{*}_{\mathbf{G}^{*}}
by the fact that a submodular function
\varphi
is determined by
\text{\sf bmm}^{+}(\varphi)
in the sense that
\varphi(A)=\sup_{\alpha\in\text{\sf bmm}^{+}(\varphi)}\alpha(A)
, see
[
22
, Corollary 6.10]
. The proof of
\rho^{*}_{\mathbf{G}}=\rho_{\mathbf{G}^{*}}
is analogous.
∎

Proof of Theorem
1.6
using the Mass Transport Principle.

By using the definitions of
\rho_{\mathbf{G}}
and
\rho^{*}_{\mathbf{G}^{*}}
and the equalities
\eta_{\mathbf{G}^{*}}=\eta_{\mathbf{G}}
and
\rho_{\mathbf{G}^{*}}(E^{*})=\mu^{*}(J^{*})
, we obtain that
\rho_{\mathbf{G}}(F)=\rho^{*}_{\mathbf{G}^{*}}(F)
is equivalent to

\eta_{\mathbf{G}}(F)=\int_{x\in J}1-\frac{1}{|V(\mathbf{G}^{F}_{x})|}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu(x)+\int_{x^{*}\in J^{*}}\frac{1}{|V(\mathbf{G}^{*,E^{*}\setminus F^{*}}_{x^{*}})|}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu^{*}(x^{*}).

(2)

To verify the equality, we set up a payment between vertices, faces, and edges. Motivated by the second integral, every face
x^{*}
that is in a finite
(E^{*}\setminus F^{*})
-component pays
1/|V(\mathbf{G}^{*,E^{*}\setminus F^{*}}_{x^{*}})|
token to the component. This way every finite
(E^{*}\setminus F^{*})
-component collects 1 token in total, and this token is passed on to an edge (chosen in a Borel way) in the
F
-cycle surrounding the component. We can also make sure that the edges that get payed, oriented from the component that pays to them, form an acyclic oriented graph on the components of
E^{*}\setminus F^{*}
. This way the edges of
F
that do not receive a payment form a subforest
F^{\prime}\subseteq F
that has the same connected components in
\mathbf{G}
as
F
.

Next, every
x\in J
in a finite
F
-component pays
1/|V(\mathbf{G}^{F}_{x})|
token to every
F^{\prime}
-edge in its component. This way all such vertices pay
1-1/|V(\mathbf{G}^{F}_{x})|
, and such
F
-edges receive
1
token in total.

What remains are infinite
F^{\prime}
components and their vertices. Write
F^{\prime\prime}
for this edge set, and
J^{\prime\prime}
for the vertices. Then
\eta_{\mathbf{G}}(F^{\prime\prime})=\mu(J^{\prime\prime})
by Theorem
2.1
, as
F^{\prime\prime}
is a hyperfinite treeing on
J^{\prime\prime}
with infinite components. In a mass transport formulation, this means that we can ensure that every vertex in
J^{\prime\prime}
pays 1 token, and every edge in
F^{\prime\prime}
gets 1 token.

Concluding the above, vertices and faces payed in total the right hand side of (
2
), while every edge in
F
received 1, so (
2
) holds by the Mass Transport Principle.
∎

Proof of Theorem
1.7
.

As we are in the non-hyperfinite case, the dual
\mathbf{G}^{*}
has no
2
-ended components
\mu^{*}

[
10
]
, one can pick a 1-ended hyperfinite spanning forest
F^{*}\subseteq E^{*}
. The corresponding measure
\alpha_{F^{*}}
is in
\text{\sf bmm}^{+}(\rho_{\mathbf{G}^{*}})
, implying
\eta_{\mathbf{G}^{*}}-\alpha_{F^{*}}\in\text{\sf bmm}^{+}(\rho^{*}_{\mathbf{G}^{*}})

E\setminus F

F^{*}

∎

5.3
Free versus wired spanning forests

Let
\mathbf{G}
be a graphing with a Borel order
<
on the edges. We have already seen in Remark
4.9
that the algorithm for finding bases with respect to
\rho_{\mathbf{G}}
produces the wired maximal spanning forest, denoted by
\mathbf{T}_{w}
. Recall that the free maximal spanning forest is a natural counterpart. We again refer to
[
24
, Chapter 11]
for a thorough overview, with the caveat that here we treat arbitrary Borel orders on the edge set.

Proof of Theorem
1.8
.

When
\mathbf{G}
and
\mathbf{G}^{*}
are dual planar graphings and the Borel total ordering is the same on
E
and
E^{*}
, the free maximal spanning forest of
\mathbf{G}
is exactly the complement of the wired minimal spanning forest of
\mathbf{G}^{*}
.
∎

Remark 5.4
.

Based on Theorem
1.8
, one could hope to use the free maximal spanning forest for arbitrary, not necessarily planar graphings to define an interesting matroid that goes beyond hyperfinite spanning forests. The problem is that the free maximal spanning forest built on two different Borel orders might have different edge measure, and so they cannot form bases of the same matroid. For example, let
\mathbf{G}
be the Schreier graphing of a free action of
F_{2}\times\mathbb{Z}
. Let
<_{1}
be such that all
F_{2}
-edges are larger than all
\mathbb{Z}
-edges, and the ordering is reversed for
<_{2}
. Then,
\mathbf{T}^{<_{1}}_{f}
contains exactly the
F_{2}
-edges while
\mathbf{T}^{<_{2}}_{f}
contains exactly the
\mathbb{Z}
-edges, so their edge measures are
2
and
1
, respectively.
