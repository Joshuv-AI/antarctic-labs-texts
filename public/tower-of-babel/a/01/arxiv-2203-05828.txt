Four-point semidefinite bound for equiangular lines
Wei-Jiun Kao, Wei-Hsuan Yu

Abstract
A set of lines in ℝ d \mathbb{R}^{d} passing through the origin is called equiangular if any two lines in the set form the same angle. We proved an alternative version of the three-point semidefinite constraints developed by Bachoc and Vallentin 2008 , and the multi-point semidefinite constraints developed by Musin 2013 for spherical codes. The alternative semidefinite constraints are simpler when the concerned object is a spherical s s -distance set. Using the alternative four-point semidefinite constraints, we found the four-point semidefinite bound for equiangular lines. This result improves the upper bounds for infinitely many dimensions d d with prescribed angles. As a corollary of the bound, we proved the uniqueness of the maximum construction of equiangular lines in ℝ d \mathbb{R}^{d} for 7 ≤ d ≤ 14 7\leq d\leq 14 with inner product α = 1 / 3 \alpha=1/3 , and for 23 ≤ d ≤ 64 23\leq d\leq 64 with α = 1 / 5 \alpha=1/5 .

2
Semidefinite constraints

The semidefinite programming method is developed by
Bachoc and Vallentin 2008
. This method is a generalization of the linear programming method developed by
Delsarte et al. 1977
.

The two methods are initially developed to bound the kissing numbers in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, that is, the maximum cardinality of
X\subseteq\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
such that
A(X)\subseteq[-1,1/2]
. In
Odlyzko and Sloane 1979
,
Odlyzko and Sloane 1979
and
Levenshtein 1979
independently proved that the kissing numbers in
\mathbb{R}^{8}
and
\mathbb{R}^{24}
are
240
and
196560
using the linear programming method.
Musin 2008
proved that the kissing number in
\mathbb{R}^{4}
is
24
by a modification of the linear programming method. Meanwhile,
Bachoc and Vallentin 2008
gave the same result by the semidefinite programming method.

The linear programming method and the semidefinite programming method involve the following parts:

1.

Consider the
distance distribution
of a spherical set
X
.

2.

The distance distribution is related to the cardinality
N=|X|
.

3.

Meanwhile, the distance distribution satisfies some specific inequalities or semidefinite constraints.

4.

The upper bound for the cardinality
N
is given by considering a linear program or a semidefinite program with
N
being the objective function and the distance distribution being the variables.

This framework can also be applied to spherical
s
-distance sets, as well as spherical projections of sets of equiangular lines. See
[
7
,
8
,
15
,
36
,
43
]
for some upper bounds on spherical
2
-distance sets and equiangular lines proved by using the semidefinite programming method.

Bachoc and Vallentin 2008
’s semidefinite programming method involves the three-point distance distribution satisfying the so-called three-point semidefinite constraints.
Musin 2013
generalized the method and developed the multi-point semidefinite constraints of
(m+2)
-point distance distributions for
m\geq 1
. As an application,
de Laat et al. 2021
gave some numerical upper bounds on the cardinality of sets of equiangular lines by
(m+2)
-point semidefinite programming method for
1\leq m\leq 4
.

In this section, we review the linear inequalities and the semidefinite constraints and prove an alternative version of the semidefinite constraints. The alternative semidefinite constraints (Theorem
2.3
and Theorem
2.13
) are simpler than the original ones (Theorem
2.2
and Theorem
2.12
) when the concerned object
X
is a spherical
s
-distance set.

2.1
Two-point linear inequality

Part 1.
Consider the
two-point distance distribution

x(t)\coloneqq\#\{(c,c^{\prime})\in X^{2}:c\cdot c^{\prime}=t\}.

The value
x(t)
counts the number of pairs in
X
with inner product
t
. Clearly
x(1)=N
, and
x(t)>0
only when
t=1
or
t\in A(X)
.

Part 2.

\sum_{t\in A(X)}x(t)=N(N-1)
.

Part 3.
The inequalities for the distance distribution
x(t)
arise from the property of a series of polynomials called
Gegenbauer polynomials
. The Gegenbauer polynomials
\{P^{d}_{k}(t):k=0,1,2,\dots\}
are defined by

\displaystyle P^{d}_{0}(t)

\displaystyle=1,

\displaystyle P^{d}_{1}(t)

\displaystyle=t,

\displaystyle P^{d}_{k}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{k+d-3}\left((2k+d-4)P^{d}_{k-1}(t)-(k-1)P^{d}_{k-2}(t)\right),\ k\geq 2.

For
c,c^{\prime}\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
,
P^{d}_{k}(c\cdot c^{\prime})
can be realized as a specific inner product (
Delsarte et al. 1977
). Therefore

\sum_{c,c^{\prime}\in X}P^{d}_{k}(c\cdot c^{\prime})\geq 0\text{ for }k=0,1,2,\dots.

(2.1)

By rewriting the inequality (
2.1
) in terms of the two-point distance distribution, we have the following theorem.

Theorem 2.1
.

For a spherical set
X
in
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
with inner product set
A
,
\sum_{t\in A}x(t)=N(N-1)
and

N+\sum_{t\in A}x(t)P^{d}_{k}(t)\geq 0\text{ for }k\geq 1.

Part 4.
One can establish a linear program using Theorem
2.1
to bound the cardinality
N
.

2.2
Three-point semidefinite constraint

Part 1.
Consider the
three-point distance distribution

y(u,v,t)\coloneqq\#\{(b,c,c^{\prime})\in X^{3}:b\cdot c=u,b\cdot c^{\prime}=v,c\cdot c^{\prime}=t\}.

The value
y(u,v,t)
counts the number of triples in
X
with pairwise inner products
u
,
v
and
t
.

Part 2.
The distance distributions are related to each other and the cardinality
N
by

•

y(1,1,1)=x(1)=N
,

•

y(1,t,t)=y(t,1,t)=y(t,t,1)=x(t)
,

•

y(1,u,v)=y(u,1,v)=y(u,v,1)=0
for
u\neq v
, and

•

\sum_{u,v,t\in A(X)}y(u,v,t)=N(N-1)(N-2)
.

Part 3.

Bachoc and Vallentin 2008
proved that, there is a specific series of matrices
\{Y^{d}_{k,n}:k\geq 0,n\geq 0\}
such that

\sum_{c,c^{\prime}\in X}Y^{d}_{k,n}(b\cdot c,b\cdot c^{\prime},c\cdot c^{\prime})\succeq 0

(2.2)

for any fixed point
b\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
. The matrices
Y^{d}_{k,n}
are defined by

\displaystyle Y^{d}_{k,n}(u,v,t)

\displaystyle\coloneqq Q^{d}_{k}(u,v,t)\begin{pmatrix}1\\
u\\
\vdots\\
u^{n}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}1&v&\cdots&v^{n}\end{pmatrix},

\displaystyle Q^{d}_{k}(u,v,t)

\displaystyle\coloneqq(1-u^{2})^{k/2}(1-v^{2})^{k/2}P^{d-1}_{k}\left(\frac{t-uv}{\sqrt{(1-u^{2})(1-v^{2})}}\right).

The order of
Y^{d}_{k,n}(u,v,t)
is
n+1
, and in which every entry is a polynomial of
u,v,t
in degree
k
.

By summing
b
in (
2.2
) over elements of
X
, we have the following constraints about
y(u,v,t)
.

Theorem 2.2

(
Bachoc and Vallentin 2008
)
.

For a spherical set
X
in
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
with inner product set
A
,

\sum_{u,v,t\in A}y(u,v,t)=(N-2)\sum_{t\in A}x(t)

and

\displaystyle NY^{d}_{k,n}(1,1,1)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\sum_{t\in A}x(t)\Big(Y^{d}_{k,n}(1,t,t)+Y^{d}_{k,n}(t,1,t)+Y^{d}_{k,n}(t,t,1)\Big)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\sum_{u,v,t\in A}y(u,v,t)Y^{d}_{k,n}(u,v,t)

\displaystyle\succeq 0\text{ for }k,n\geq 0.

Part 4.
One can establish a semidefinite program using Theorem
2.2
to bound the cardinality
N
. In
Bachoc and Vallentin 2008
, the variables of the semidefinite program are
x(t)/N
and
y(u,v,t)/N
, and the objective function is
1+\sum_{t}x(t)/N=N
.

Below shows an alternative version of Theorem
2.2
.

Part 3 (alternative version).
Let
A^{\prime}=A\cup\{1\}
. The semidefinite constraint in Theorem
2.2
is equivalent to

\sum_{u,v,t\in A^{\prime}}y(u,v,t)Q^{d}_{k}(u,v,t)\begin{pmatrix}1\\
u\\
\vdots\\
u^{n}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}1&v&\cdots&v^{n}\end{pmatrix}\succeq 0.

The positive semidefiniteness means that

\textbf{a}^{\top}\left(\sum_{u,v,t\in A^{\prime}}y(u,v,t)Q^{d}_{k}(u,v,t)\begin{pmatrix}1\\
u\\
\vdots\\
u^{n}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}1&v&\cdots&v^{n}\end{pmatrix}\right)\textbf{a}\geq 0

for all
\textbf{a}\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
, or

\sum_{u,v,t\in A^{\prime}}y(u,v,t)Q^{d}_{k}(u,v,t)f(u)f(v)\geq 0

(2.3)

for all polynomials
f(x)
with degree at most
n
. Since
n
can be arbitrary, the inequality (
2.3
) holds for any polynomial
f(x)
.

Let
\textstyle\{e_{u}:u\in A^{\prime}\}
be the standard basis of
\mathbb{R}^{s+1}
. We rewrite (
2.3
) as

\sum_{u,v,t\in A^{\prime}}y(u,v,t)Q^{d}_{k}(u,v,t)(\mathbf{f}^{\top}e_{u})(e_{v}^{\top}\mathbf{f})\geq 0

where
\textstyle\mathbf{f}=\sum_{u\in A^{\prime}}f(u)e_{u}
for some polynomial
f(x)
. In fact,
\mathbf{f}
can be taken as arbitrary vector in
\mathbb{R}^{s+1}
; that is, given any vector
\textstyle\mathbf{f}=\sum_{u\in A^{\prime}}f_{u}e_{u}
, we can always find a polynomial
f(x)
such that

f(u)=f_{u},\ u\in A^{\prime}.

Therefore, the matrix

\sum_{u,v,t\in A^{\prime}}y(u,v,t)Q^{d}_{k}(u,v,t)e_{u}e_{v}^{\top}

(2.4)

is positive semidefinite. We denote the matrix (
2.4
) by
\mathcal{Q}^{d}_{k}(X)
, and call it by the
alternative three-point semidefinite constraint
.

Theorem 2.3

(Alternative three-point semidefinite constraint)
.

For a spherical
s
-distance set
X
in
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
with inner product set
A
,

\sum_{u,v,t\in A}y(u,v,t)=(N-2)\sum_{t\in A}x(t).

Let
\{e_{u}:u\in A\cup\{1\}\}
be the standard basis of
\mathbb{R}^{s+1}
. Then

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}^{d}_{k}(X)

\displaystyle=NQ^{d}_{k}(1,1,1)e_{1}e_{1}^{\top}

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\sum_{t\in A}x(t)\Big(Q^{d}_{k}(1,t,t)e_{1}e_{t}^{\top}+Q^{d}_{k}(t,1,t)e_{t}e_{1}^{\top}+Q^{d}_{k}(t,t,1)e_{t}e_{t}^{\top}\Big)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\sum_{u,v,t\in A}y(u,v,t)Q^{d}_{k}(u,v,t)e_{u}e_{v}^{\top}

\displaystyle\succeq 0

for any
k\geq 0
.

Remark 2.4
.

When the concerned object
X
has a finite number of distances, the alternative semidefinite constraints
\mathcal{Q}^{d}_{k}(X)
are simpler than the original ones in Theorem
2.2
. For a spherical
s
-distance set
X
, the original constraints consist of sums of
s^{3}+3s+1
matrices with order
(n+1)
; in the alternative constraints
\mathcal{Q}^{d}_{k}(X)
, there are only
s^{3}+3s+1
terms in a
(s+1)\times(s+1)
matrix.

Remark 2.5
.

One might think that the original semidefinite constraints are better with larger matrix size
(n+1)
. However, an argument similar to the proof of Theorem
2.3
indicates that the restricting ability on
y(u,v,t)
of the original semidefinite constraints is the same as the alternative constraints whenever
n\geq s
. Therefore, it suffices to take
n=s
when applying the original semidefinite constraints.

.

Consider a

-distance set

with

\mathcal{Q}^{d}_{k}(X)=\begin{pmatrix}[r]NQ^{d}_{k}(1,1,1)&x(\alpha)Q^{d}_{k}(1,\alpha,\alpha)&x(\beta)Q^{d}_{k}(1,\beta,\beta)\\[10.00002pt]

2.3
Multi-point semidefinite constraint

The multi-point semidefinite constraints are generalizations of the three-point semidefinite constraints. Fix
m
to be a positive integer with
1\leq m\leq d-2
. The multi-point constraints become the three-point constraints when
m=1
.

Part 1.
We use the notation of
Gram matrices
to define the
multi-point distance distribution
.

Definition 2.7
.

Suppose
B=\{b_{1},\dots,b_{m}\}\subseteq X
is a set of unit vectors, and
c,c^{\prime}\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
.

(a)

The Gram matrix of
B
is an
m\times m
matrix

G=G(B)\coloneqq\begin{pmatrix}b_{p}\cdot b_{q}\end{pmatrix}_{1\leq p,q\leq m}.

The Gram matrix
G
is symmetric and positive semidefinite. If
G
is positive definite, i.e.,
B
is linearly independent, we say
G
is a
proper Gram matrix
.

(b)

For
c\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
, denote the vector
B^{\top}\cdot c\coloneqq(b_{1}\cdot c,\dots,b_{m}\cdot c)^{\top}
in
\mathbb{R}^{m}
.

(c)

Suppose
\mathbf{u}=B^{\top}\cdot c\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
, then the Gram matrix of
B\cup\{c\}
is

G(B,c)=\begin{pmatrix}G(B)&B^{\top}\cdot c\\
c^{\top}\cdot B&c\cdot c\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}G&u\\
u^{\top}&1\end{pmatrix}.

We denote the
(m+1)\times(m+1)
Gram matrix by the pair
(G;\mathbf{u})
.

(d)

Suppose
\mathbf{u}=B^{\top}\cdot c
and
\mathbf{v}=B^{\top}\cdot c^{\prime}
, then the Gram matrix of
B\cup\{c,c^{\prime}\}
is

G(B,c,c^{\prime})=\begin{pmatrix}G(B)&B^{\top}\cdot c&B^{\top}\cdot c^{\prime}\\
c^{\top}\cdot B&c\cdot c&c\cdot c^{\prime}\\
c^{\prime\top}\cdot B&c^{\prime}\cdot c&c^{\prime}\cdot c^{\prime}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}G&\mathbf{u}&\mathbf{v}\\
\mathbf{u}^{\top}&1&t\\
\mathbf{v}^{\top}&t&1\end{pmatrix}.

We denote the
(m+2)\times(m+2)
Gram matrix by the tuple
(G;\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v},t)
.

Definition 2.8
.

Suppose
G
is an
m\times m
proper Gram matrix,
\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
and
t\in\mathbb{R}
. Define the
(m+2)
-point distance distribution by

3
Switching reduction

In this section, we give a simplification of the semidefinite constraints on the regime that
X
is a spherical projection of a set of equiangular lines, which we call by
switching reduction
.

The simplification is given by considering the mutual relations of different spherical projections. We say that two Gram matrices
G_{1}
,
G_{2}
are
switching equivalent
, if
G_{2}
is given by changing the sign of some columns and the corresponding rows in
G_{1}
. The Gram matrices of different spherical projections of the same set of equiangular lines are switching equivalent: let
X=\{c_{1},\dots,c_{N}\}
be a spherical projection with Gram matrix
G=G(X)
. Any possible spherical projection is of the form

\{\varepsilon_{1}c_{1},\dots,\varepsilon_{N}c_{N}\},\varepsilon_{i}\in\{\pm 1\}.

Let
\Lambda=\text{diag}(\varepsilon_{1},\dots,\varepsilon_{N})
be the diagonal matrix with diagonal entries
\varepsilon_{1},\dots,\varepsilon_{N}
. Then the Gram matrix
G_{\Lambda}
of the corresponding spherical projection is

G_{\Lambda}=\begin{pmatrix}\varepsilon_{i}c_{i}\cdot\varepsilon_{j}c_{j}\end{pmatrix}_{1\leq i,j\leq N}=\Lambda G\Lambda.

That is,
G_{\Lambda}
is given by changing the sign of the column
i
and the corresponding row
i
with
\varepsilon_{i}=-1
. Then
G_{\Lambda}
is switching equivalent to
G
.

In the following, we demonstrate the modification of the semidefinite constraints after switching reduction.

Part 1.
We can define the distance distribution
N_{m}(G;\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v},t)
for a spherical projection
X
of a set of equiangular lines. Meanwhile, we can never define
N_{m}(G;\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v},t)
for the set of equiangular lines itself, since the number of
(m+2)
-tuples with a fixed Gram matrix depends on the chosen spherical projection. However, the sum of
N_{m}(G;\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v},t)
over a switching class of the Gram matrix
(G;\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v},t)
is independent of the choice of spherical projections.

We define the
distance distribution of switching classes
, which do not rely on the spherical projections. We use square brackets to represent the sums over switching classes.

Definition 3.1
.

Suppose
G
is an
m\times m
proper Gram matrix and
u_{i},v_{j},t\in\{\pm\alpha\}
. Define the distance distribution of the switching class
[G;\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v},t]
by

N_{m}[G;\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v},t]\coloneqq\sum_{(G^{\prime};\mathbf{u}^{\prime},\mathbf{v}^{\prime},t^{\prime})\sim(G;\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v},t)}N_{m}(G^{\prime};\mathbf{u}^{\prime},\mathbf{v}^{\prime},t^{\prime}),

in which
\sim
means switching equivalence. The value
N_{m}[G;\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v},t]
is the number of
(m+2)
-tuples
(B,c,c^{\prime})
with Gram matrix switching equivalent to
(G;\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v},t)
. We also define

\displaystyle N_{m-1}[G;\mathbf{u}]

\displaystyle=\sum_{(G^{\prime};\mathbf{u}^{\prime})\sim(G;\mathbf{u})}N_{m-1}(G^{\prime};\mathbf{u}^{\prime}),

\displaystyle N_{m-2}[G]

\displaystyle=\sum_{G^{\prime}\sim G}N_{m-2}(G^{\prime}).

Algebraically,
(G^{\prime};\mathbf{u}^{\prime},\mathbf{v}^{\prime},t^{\prime})
is switching equivalent to
(G;\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v},t)
if and only if

(G^{\prime};\mathbf{u}^{\prime},\mathbf{v}^{\prime},t^{\prime})=\Lambda_{0}(G;\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v},t)\Lambda_{0}

for some
(m+2)\times(m+2)
diagonal matrix
\Lambda_{0}=\text{diag}(\varepsilon_{1},\dots,\varepsilon_{m+2})
with diagonal entries
\varepsilon_{1},\dots,\varepsilon_{m+2}\in\{\pm 1\}
. Write
\Lambda=\text{diag}(\varepsilon_{1},\dots,\varepsilon_{m})
, we have

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}G^{\prime}&\mathbf{u}^{\prime}&\mathbf{v}^{\prime}\\
(\mathbf{u}^{\prime})^{\top}&1&t^{\prime}\\
(\mathbf{v}^{\prime})^{\top}&t^{\prime}&1\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}\Lambda&0&0\\
0&\varepsilon_{m+1}&0\\
0&0&\varepsilon_{m+2}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}G&\mathbf{u}&\mathbf{v}\\
\mathbf{u}^{\top}&1&t\\
\mathbf{v}^{\top}&t&1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\Lambda&0&0\\
0&\varepsilon_{m+1}&0\\
0&0&\varepsilon_{m+2}\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}\Lambda G\Lambda&\varepsilon_{m+1}\Lambda\mathbf{u}&\varepsilon_{m+2}\Lambda\mathbf{v}\\
\varepsilon_{m+1}\mathbf{u}^{\top}\Lambda&\varepsilon_{m+1}^{2}&\varepsilon_{m+1}\varepsilon_{m+2}t\\
\varepsilon_{m+2}\mathbf{u}^{\top}\Lambda&\varepsilon_{m+2}\varepsilon_{m+1}t&\varepsilon_{m+2}^{2}\end{pmatrix},

therefore

G^{\prime}=\Lambda G\Lambda,\ \mathbf{u}^{\prime}=\varepsilon_{m+1}\Lambda\mathbf{u},\ \mathbf{v}^{\prime}=\varepsilon_{m+2}\Lambda\mathbf{v},\ t^{\prime}=\varepsilon_{m+1}\varepsilon_{m+2}t.

Proposition 3.2
.

Let
G
be an
m\times m
proper Gram matrix and
u_{i},v_{j},t\in\{\pm\alpha\}
. Then

(a)

N_{m}[G;\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v},t]=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\Lambda}\sum_{\varepsilon_{m+1},\varepsilon_{m+2}\in\{\pm 1\}}N_{m}(\Lambda G\Lambda;\varepsilon_{m+1}\Lambda\mathbf{u},\varepsilon_{m+2}\Lambda\mathbf{v},\varepsilon_{m+1}\varepsilon_{m+2}t)
.

(b)

N_{m-1}[G;\mathbf{u}]=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\Lambda}\sum_{\varepsilon_{m+1}\in\{\pm 1\}}N_{m-1}(\Lambda G\Lambda;\varepsilon_{m+1}\Lambda\mathbf{u})
.

(c)

N_{m-2}[G]=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\Lambda}N_{m-2}(\Lambda G\Lambda)
.

In all cases,
\Lambda
is summed over all
m\times m
diagonal matrices with diagonal entries
\varepsilon_{1},\dots,\varepsilon_{m}\in\{\pm 1\}
.

There is a multiple
1/2
in Proposition
3.2
, since two diagonal matrices define the same Gram matrix if and only if the matrices are opposite.

Part 2.
The distance distribution
N_{m}[G;\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v},t]
of switching classes are related to the cardinality
N
by the following equations.

Proposition 3.3
.

Let
\mathcal{G}
be a collection of representatives of switching classes of
m\times m
proper Gram matrices. Fix
u_{1}=v_{1}=\alpha
, and let
u_{p},v_{q},t\in\{\pm\alpha\}

\displaystyle\sum_{G\in\mathcal{G}}\sum_{u_{1}=v_{1}=\alpha}\sum_{t\in\{\pm\alpha\}}N_{m}[G;\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v},t]

\displaystyle=\frac{N!}{(N-m-2)!},

\displaystyle\sum_{G\in\mathcal{G}}\sum_{u_{1}=\alpha}N_{m-1}[G;\mathbf{u}]

\displaystyle=\frac{N!}{(N-m-1)!},

\displaystyle\sum_{G\in\mathcal{G}}N_{m-2}[G]

\displaystyle=\frac{N!}{(N-m)!}.

Proof.

The right hand side of the first equation is the sum of
N_{m}(G;\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v},t)
over all proper
(m+2)\times(m+2)

which is switching equivalent to

∎

There are several duplications of values in the distance distribution

Proposition 3.4

If

N_{m-1}[G^{\prime};\mathbf{u}^{\prime}]=N_{m-1}[G;\mathbf{u}]

Part 3.
The reduction in the alternative semidefinite constraints
\mathcal{Q}^{d,m}_{k}(G;X)
is due to the
switching property
of the polynomial
Q^{d,m}_{k}(G;\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v},t)
.

Proposition 3.7
.

If
(G^{\prime};\mathbf{u}^{\prime},\mathbf{v}^{\prime},t)
is switching equivalent to
(G;\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v},t)
by the relation

G^{\prime}=\Lambda G\Lambda,\ \mathbf{u}^{\prime}=\varepsilon_{m+1}\Lambda\mathbf{u},\ \mathbf{v}^{\prime}=\varepsilon_{m+2}\Lambda\mathbf{v},\ t^{\prime}=\varepsilon_{m+1}\varepsilon_{m+2}t,

then
Q^{d,m}_{k}(G^{\prime};\mathbf{u}^{\prime},\mathbf{v}^{\prime},t^{\prime})=(\varepsilon_{m+1}\varepsilon_{m+2})^{k}Q^{d,m}_{k}(G;\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v},t)
for any
k\geq 0
.

Proof.

Since

t^{\prime}-\mathbf{u}^{\prime}(G^{\prime})^{-1}(\mathbf{v}^{\prime})^{\top}=\varepsilon_{m+1}\varepsilon_{m+2}(t-\mathbf{u}G^{-1}\mathbf{v}^{\top})

and

(1-\mathbf{u}^{\prime}(G^{\prime})^{-1}(\mathbf{u}^{\prime})^{\top})(1-\mathbf{v}^{\prime}(G^{\prime})^{-1}(\mathbf{v}^{\prime})^{\top})=(1-\mathbf{u}G^{-1}\mathbf{u}^{\top})(1-\mathbf{v}G^{-1}\mathbf{v}^{\top}),

the only difference in
Q^{d,m}_{k}(G;\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v},t)
and
Q^{d,m}_{k}(G^{\prime};\mathbf{u}^{\prime},\mathbf{v}^{\prime},t^{\prime})
is the input of
P^{d-m}_{k}
in a multiple
\varepsilon_{m+1}\varepsilon_{m+2}
. Meanwhile,
P^{d-m}_{k}
is an odd function when
k
is odd and an even function when
k
is even, so the two values differ by a multiple of
(\varepsilon_{m+1}\varepsilon_{m+2})^{k}
.
∎

Due to Proposition
3.7
, we can safely sum over switching classes in the alternative semidefinite constraints
\mathcal{Q}^{d,m}_{k}(G;X)
, since the coefficients

for inputs

in the same switching class are identical. We also use the square bracket to represent the sum of the matrices over a switching class.

, if
f_{1},f_{2}
and all the principal minors of
F
are nonnegative, then

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle\leq\left\langle\mathcal{Q}^{d,2}_{0}[G;X],F\right\rangle+\left\langle\mathcal{Q}^{d,2}_{3}[G;X],\begin{pmatrix}f_{1}&0\\
0&f_{2}\end{pmatrix}\right\rangle=N(N-1)\left(\frac{1}{2}(a^{2}-1)(a^{2}-2)-N\right)

\displaystyle\Longrightarrow N\leq\frac{1}{2}(a^{2}-1)(a^{2}-2).

For
a=3
,
D_{4}(3)\approx 14.42
and
\lfloor D_{4}(3)\rfloor=14
; for
a\geq 5
, one can check that the leading coefficient of
g_{a}(x)
is negative, and the equation
g_{a}(x)=0
has four real roots in the disjoint intervals

\displaystyle\left[0,\frac{3}{2a^{2}}\right],\left[\frac{6}{7}a^{2}-8,\frac{6}{7}a^{2}\right],

\displaystyle\left[3a^{2}-\frac{12}{\sqrt{5}}a-\frac{948}{25}+\frac{30\sqrt{5}}{a},3a^{2}-\frac{12}{\sqrt{5}}a-\frac{948}{25}+\frac{45\sqrt{5}}{a}\right],

\displaystyle\left[3a^{2}+\frac{12}{\sqrt{5}}a-\frac{948}{25}-\frac{32\sqrt{5}}{a},3a^{2}+\frac{12}{\sqrt{5}}a-\frac{948}{25}+\frac{2\sqrt{5}}{a}\right],

which proves the asymptotic argument of
D_{4}(a)
. In addition, we have

3a^{2}+\frac{12}{\sqrt{5}}a-\frac{948}{25}-\frac{32\sqrt{5}}{a}-1\leq\lfloor D_{4}(a)\rfloor\leq 3a^{2}+\frac{12}{\sqrt{5}}a-\frac{948}{25}+\frac{2\sqrt{5}}{a}.

In either case, when taking
d=\lfloor D_{4}(a)\rfloor
, one can check that
f_{1}
,
f_{2}
and all the principal minors of
F
except
\det F
are positive. Therefore

N_{1/a}(d)\leq\frac{1}{2}(a^{2}-1)(a^{2}-2)

whenever
d\leq\lfloor D_{4}(a)\rfloor
.
∎

Remark 4.3
.

Theorem
4.1
explained the numerical results by
de Laat et al. 2021
partially. By the four-point semidefinite programming method, which is denoted by
\Delta_{4}(G)^{\ast}
by
[
13
]
,

N_{1/5}(65)\leq 276,\ N_{1/7}(145)\leq 1128,\ N_{1/9}(251)\leq 3160,\ N_{1/11}(381)\leq 7140,

while the largest applicable dimensions we found are

\lfloor D_{4}(5)\rfloor=64,\ \lfloor D_{4}(7)\rfloor=144,\ \lfloor D_{4}(9)\rfloor=250,\ \lfloor D_{4}(11)\rfloor=380.

Remark 4.4
.

Lemma
4.2
can be understood as the duality of semidefinite programming. The found dual matrix
F
paired with
\mathcal{Q}^{d,2}_{0}[G;X]
is a matrix with the null space

\mathbb{R}\begin{pmatrix}1\\
(a+1)^{3}(a-2)/4\\
(a-1)^{3}(a+2)/4\end{pmatrix}.

This is consistent with the fact that, all the known constructions attaining the bound in Theorem
4.1
satisfy

\mathcal{Q}^{d,2}_{0}[G;X]=N(N-1)\begin{pmatrix}1\\
(a+1)^{3}(a-2)/4\\
(a-1)^{3}(a+2)/4\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}1&\frac{(a+1)^{3}(a-2)}{4}&\frac{(a-1)^{3}(a+2)}{4}\end{pmatrix},

which is a rank-
1
matrix. On the other hand, the found dual matrix paired with
\mathcal{Q}^{d,2}_{3}[G;X]
is a diagonal matrix. The off-diagonal entries are irrelevant, since the off-diagonal entry in
\mathcal{Q}^{d,2}_{3}[G;X]
is

z_{2}\left(Q^{d,2}_{3}(G;\mathbf{u}_{1},\mathbf{u}_{2},\alpha)+Q^{d,2}_{3}(G;\mathbf{u}_{1},\mathbf{u}_{2},-\alpha)\right),

which must be zero.

4.2
Proof of Theorem
1.4

Glazyrin and Yu 2018
proved that the constructions attaining the three-point semidefinite bound must lie in an
(a^{2}-2)
-dimensional subspace. As a generalization, Theorem
1.4
states that the constructions attaining the four-point semidefinite bound must lie in an
(a^{2}-2)
-dimensional subspace as well. Therefore Theorem
1.4
gives restrictions for the constructions of equiangular lines with cardinality
(a^{2}-1)(a^{2}-2)/2
and inner product
1/a
for dimensions
d\in(D_{3}(a),D_{4}(a)]
.

If a spherical projection
X
is given by choosing an unit vector
b
along one line, and then choosing unit vectors with positive inner products with
b
along the other lines, then
X\setminus\{b\}
is called the
derived code
of the set of equiangular lines with respect to
b
.

The proof by
Glazyrin and Yu 2018
analyzes the structure of the derived codes, of which the two-point distance distributions are known from the linear programming method. However, our proof of Theorem
1.3
cannot determine the distance distributions of derived codes directly. Instead, we consider the
distance distribution with two fixed points
, and show that the distribution can be determined if the matrix
\mathcal{Q}^{d,2}_{0}[G;X]
in the semidefinite constraint has rank
1
, which is the case when
d<D_{4}(a)
.

Lemma 4.5
.

Let
a\geq 3
be an odd integer and
d<D_{4}(a)
. Suppose
X
is a spherical projection of a set of equiangular lines in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with cardinality
(a^{2}-1)(a^{2}-2)/2
and inner product
1/a
. Then the matrix
\mathcal{Q}^{d,2}_{0}[G;X]
is of rank
1
.

Proof.

Without the loss of generality, we may assume that
d=\lfloor D_{4}(a)\rfloor
. As in the proof of Theorem
4.1
,

0\leq\left\langle\mathcal{Q}^{d,2}_{0}[G;X],F\right\rangle+\left\langle\mathcal{Q}^{d,2}_{3}[G;X],\begin{pmatrix}f_{1}&0\\
0&f_{2}\end{pmatrix}\right\rangle=N(N-1)\left(\frac{1}{2}(a^{2}-1)(a^{2}-2)-N\right)=0,

therefore both
\left\langle\mathcal{Q}^{d,2}_{0}[G;X],F\right\rangle
and
\left\langle\mathcal{Q}^{d,2}_{3}[G;X],\begin{pmatrix}f_{1}&0\\
0&f_{2}\end{pmatrix}\right\rangle
are zero. Recall that for two positive semidefinite
n\times n
matrices
A,B
with
\langle A,B\rangle=0
, we have the inequality
\text{rank}(A)+\text{rank}(B)\leq n
, and all the eigenvectors of
A
with positive eigenvalues are in
\ker B
. For
d=\lfloor D_{4}(a)\rfloor
, all the principal minors of
F
except
\det F
are positive, hence
F
is a rank 2 matrix with null space

\mathbb{R}\begin{pmatrix}1\\
(a+1)^{3}(a-2)/4\\
(a-1)^{3}(a+2)/4\end{pmatrix},

and the rank of the matrix
\mathcal{Q}^{d,2}_{0}[G;X]
is at most
1
. Since the matrix is nonzero, the rank is
1
and

\mathcal{Q}^{d,2}_{0}[G;X]=N(N-1)\begin{pmatrix}1\\
(a+1)^{3}(a-2)/4\\
(a-1)^{3}(a+2)/4\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}1&(a+1)^{3}(a-2)/4&(a-1)^{3}(a+2)/4\end{pmatrix}.

∎

Let
b,b^{\prime}\in X
be different unit vectors. Consider the distance distribution with two fixed points

\displaystyle N_{b,b^{\prime}}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\#\{c\in X:G(b,b^{\prime},c)\sim\begin{pmatrix}1&\alpha&\alpha\\
\alpha&1&\alpha\\
\alpha&\alpha&1\end{pmatrix}\},

\displaystyle N^{\prime}_{b,b^{\prime}}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\#\{c\in X:G(b,b^{\prime},c)\sim\begin{pmatrix}1&\alpha&\alpha\\
\alpha&1&-\alpha\\
\alpha&-\alpha&1\end{pmatrix}\}.

Clearly, we have
N^{\prime}_{b,b^{\prime}}=(N-2)-N_{b,b^{\prime}}
. Furthermore, the two values can be determined when the matrix
\mathcal{Q}^{d,2}_{0}[G;X]
is of rank
1
.

Lemma 4.6
.

Suppose the matrix

\mathcal{Q}^{d,2}_{0}[G;X]=\begin{pmatrix}N(N-1)&y_{1}&y_{2}\\
y_{1}&y_{1}+z_{1}+z_{2}&2z_{2}\\
y_{2}&2z_{2}&y_{2}+z_{2}+z_{3}\end{pmatrix}

is of rank
1
. Then for any
b,b^{\prime}\in X
and
b\neq b^{\prime}
,

N_{b,b^{\prime}}=\frac{y_{1}}{N(N-1)}\text{ and }N^{\prime}_{b,b^{\prime}}=\frac{y_{2}}{N(N-1)}.

Proof.

Consider the matrix

\begin{pmatrix}1&N_{b,b^{\prime}}&N^{\prime}_{b,b^{\prime}}\\
N_{b,b^{\prime}}&(N_{b,b^{\prime}})^{2}&N_{b,b^{\prime}}N^{\prime}_{b,b^{\prime}}\\
N^{\prime}_{b,b^{\prime}}&N_{b,b^{\prime}}N^{\prime}_{b,b^{\prime}}&(N^{\prime}_{b,b^{\prime}})^{2}\end{pmatrix}.

It is not hard to see that the sum of such matrices over all
(b,b^{\prime})\in X^{2}
with
b\neq b^{\prime}
is
\mathcal{Q}^{d,2}_{0}[G;X]
. Since
\mathcal{Q}^{d,2}_{0}[G;X]
is of rank
1
, we have

0=N(N-1)(y_{1}+z_{1}+z_{2})-y_{1}^{2}=\left(\sum_{b\neq b^{\prime}}1\right)\left(\sum_{b\neq b^{\prime}}N_{b,b^{\prime}}^{2}\right)-\left(\sum_{b\neq b^{\prime}}N_{b,b^{\prime}}\right)^{2}.

Therefore, the values
N_{b,b^{\prime}}
for all
b\neq b^{\prime}
must be the same and equal
y_{1}/N(N-1)
. Similarly, we have
N^{\prime}_{b,b^{\prime}}=y_{2}/N(N-1)
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
1.4
.

Suppose
a\geq 3
is an odd integer,
d<D_{4}(a)
, and
X
is a spherical projection of a set of equiangular lines in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with cardinality
(a^{2}-1)(a^{2}-2)/2
and inner product
1/a
. Without the loss of generality, we assume that the spherical projection is chosen such that
X\setminus\{b\}
is a derived code for some
b\in X
. Consider a graph
H
with vertex set
V=X\setminus\{b\}
, and
(c,c^{\prime})\in V^{2}
forms an edge if and only if
c\cdot c^{\prime}=\alpha
. We first show that the graph
H
must be a strongly regular graph with parameters

\text{SRG}\left(\frac{a^{2}(a^{2}-3)}{2},\frac{(a+1)^{3}(a-2)}{4},\frac{(a+1)(a+2)(a^{2}-5)}{8},\frac{(a+1)^{3}(a-2)}{8}\right).

Indeed, the number of vertices is
v=|V|=N-1=a^{2}(a^{2}-3)/2
. By Lemma
4.5
and Lemma
4.6
, we have
N_{c,c^{\prime}}=(a+1)^{3}(a-2)/4
and
N^{\prime}_{c,c^{\prime}}=(a-1)^{3}(a+2)/4
for any
c,c^{\prime}\in X
and
c\neq c^{\prime}
. For any
b\in V
, the degree of
b
in
H
is

\deg_{H}(b)=\#\{c\in V:G(b_{0},b,c)=\begin{pmatrix}1&\alpha&\alpha\\
\alpha&1&\alpha\\
\alpha&\alpha&1\end{pmatrix}\}=N_{b_{0},b}=\frac{(a+1)^{3}(a-2)}{4}.

Therefore
H
is
k
-regular with
k=(a+1)^{3}(a-2)/4
. For nonadjacent vertices
b,b^{\prime}\in V
, let
\mu
be the number of the common neighbors of
b
and
b^{\prime}
in
H
. Then there are
(k-\mu)
neighbors of
b
which are not neighbors of
b^{\prime}
, and
(k-\mu)
neighbors of
b^{\prime}
which are not neighbors of
b
. Therefore

k=N_{b,b^{\prime}}=(k-\mu)+(k-\mu)\Longrightarrow\mu=\frac{k}{2}=\frac{(a+1)^{3}(a-2)}{8}.

By a similar argument, one can show that any two adjacent vertices has
\lambda=(3k-v-1)/2=(a+1)(a+2)(a^{2}-5)/8
common neighbors.

Let
M
be the adjacency matrix of
H
. By the theory of strongly regular graphs, the eigenvalues of
M
are
k
,
\frac{k}{a+1}
and
-\frac{a+1}{2}
with multiplicities
1
,
a^{2}-3
and
v-a^{2}+2
, respectively. Since the derived code
X\setminus\{b_{0}\}
is a spherical two-distance set in
\mathbb{S}^{d-2}
with inner product set

\left\{\frac{\pm\alpha-\alpha^{2}}{1-\alpha^{2}}\right\}=\left\{\frac{\pm a-1}{a^{2}-1}\right\}=\left\{\frac{1}{a+1},\frac{-1}{a-1}\right\},

the Gram matrix of the derived code is

I+\frac{1}{a+1}M-\frac{1}{a-1}(J-I-M).

The eigenvalues of the Gram matrix are
\frac{a^{2}}{2}
and
0
with multiplicities
a^{2}-3
and
v-a^{2}+3
, respectively. Therefore, the unit vectors in the derived code span an
(a^{2}-3)
-dimensional space, and the original set of equiangular lines lie in an
(a^{2}-2)
-dimensional space.

∎

The above proof indicates that the derived code of a construction attaining the four-point semidefinite bound must form a strongly regular graph with parameters

\text{SRG}\left(\frac{a^{2}(a^{2}-3)}{2},\frac{(a+1)^{3}(a-2)}{4},\frac{(a+1)(a+2)(a^{2}-5)}{8},\frac{(a+1)^{3}(a-2)}{8}\right).

Such strongly regular graphs are known to exist and are unique for
a=3
and
a=5
(see
Seidel 1968
,
Goethals and Seidel 1975
). Therefore, we can determine the uniqueness of some maximum constructions of equiangular lines.

Corollary 4.7
.

The following maximum constructions of equiangular lines are unique up to orthogonal transformations.

(a)

28
equiangular lines in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
for
7\leq d\leq 14
with
\alpha=1/3
.

(b)

276
equiangular lines in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
for
23\leq d\leq 64
with
\alpha=1/5
.

By
Glazyrin and Yu 2018
, Theorem 4, the uniqueness is only known for
7\leq d\leq 11
with
\alpha=1/3
, and
23\leq d\leq 59
with
\alpha=1/5
.

Remark 4.8
.

Delsarte et al. 1977
proved that
N_{1/3}(d)=28
for
7\leq d\leq 15
. There are at least two maximum constructions in
\mathbb{R}^{15}
. Meanwhile,
Cao et al. 2022
proved that
N_{1/5}(d)=276
for
23\leq d\leq 185
. It is known that there are at least two maximum constructions in
\mathbb{R}^{185}
, so it remains unknown that whether the maximum constructions in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
for
65\leq d\leq 184
are unique.

Further questions

We have established the four-point semidefinite bound by using the alternative four-point semidefinite constraints with switching reduction, namely, Theorem

.

Establish the five-point and the six-point semidefinite bounds for equiangular lines.

In the proof of the four-point semidefinite bound, we have also noticed some facts, but we fail to give the insights into them.

.

5.3
is equivalent to classify tight spherical

-designs and tight spherical

N(d)\geq\frac{2}{9}(d+1)^{2}
for
d=6\cdot 4^{i}-1
, which implies that

\frac{2}{9}\leq\mathop{\lim\sup}_{d\to\infty}\frac{N(d)}{d^{2}}\leq\frac{1}{2}.

Greaves et al. 2016
offered another construction, indicating that

N(d)\geq\frac{32d^{2}+328d+296}{1089}.

A.2
Previous results on
N_{\alpha}(d)

Neumann’s theorem starts the study on
N_{1/a}(d)
for odd integers
a
. Below is a brief review of the researches on
N_{\alpha}(d)
.

•

For
\alpha=1/3
,
Lemmens and Seidel 1973
proved that
N_{1/3}(d)=28
for
7\leq d\leq 15
, and
N_{1/3}(d)=2(d-1)
for
d\geq 15
.

•

For
\alpha=1/5
,
Lemmens and Seidel 1973
conjectured that
N_{1/5}(d)
equals
276
for
23\leq d\leq 185
and
\textstyle\lfloor\frac{3}{2}(d-1)\rfloor
for
d\geq 185
.

–

Neumaier 1989
proved the conjecture for sufficiently large
d
.

–

Yu 2017
proved the conjecture for
23\leq d\leq 60
.

–

Lin and Yu 2020a
proved that the conjecture is true when the base size
K=2,3,5
, as
Lemmens and Seidel 1973
claimed.

–

Recently,
Cao et al. 2022
completely proved the conjecture.

•

For the asymptotic behavior of
N_{\alpha}(d)
as
d\to\infty
,

–

Bukh 2016
proved that
N_{\alpha}(d)=O(d)
for any
\alpha\in(0,1)
.

–

Balla et al. 2018
proved that
N_{\alpha}(d)\leq 1.93d
if
\alpha\neq 1/3
.

–

Jiang and Polyanskii 2020
proved that
N_{1/5}(d)=(3/2)d+O(1)
,
N_{1/(1+2\sqrt{2})}(d)=(3/2)d+O(1)
, and
N_{\alpha}(d)\leq 1.49d
for every
\alpha\neq 1/3,1/5
and
1/(1+2\sqrt{2})
and for sufficiently large
d
.

–

Jiang et al. 2021
proved that
N_{\alpha}(d)=\lfloor\frac{k}{k-1}(d-1)\rfloor
for all sufficiently large
d
, where
k
is the spectral radius order of
(1-\alpha)/(2\alpha)
. In particular,
N_{1/a}(d)=\lfloor\frac{a+1}{a-1}(d-1)\rfloor
for all odd integers
a\geq 3
and all sufficiently large
d
. If
k=\infty
, then
N_{\alpha}(d)=d+o(d)
.

•

Some new relative bounds.

–

Okuda and Yu 2016
proved that

N_{1/a}(d)\leq 2+(d-2)\max\left(\frac{(a-1)^{3}}{d-(3a^{2}-6a+2)},\frac{(a+1)^{3}}{(3a^{2}+6a+2)-d}\right)

for
3a^{2}-6a+2<d<3a^{2}+6a+2
.

–

Glazyrin and Yu 2018
proved that

N_{1/a}(d)\leq\left(\frac{2}{3}a^{2}+\frac{4}{7}\right)d+2

for
a\geq 3
.

–

Balla 2021
proved that

N_{1/a}(d)\leq\frac{a^{3}}{2}\sqrt{d}+\frac{a+1}{2}d

and

N_{1/a}(d)\leq\max\left(2a^{5}+\frac{2a^{4}}{a-1},(2+\frac{8}{(a-1)^{2}})(d+1)\right).
