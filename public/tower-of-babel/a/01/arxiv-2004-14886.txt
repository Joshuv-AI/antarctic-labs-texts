Abstract
. We construct a calculus of functors in the spirit of orthogonal calculus, which is designed to study “functors with reality” such as the Real classifying space functor, BU ℝ ⁡ ( − ) \BU_{\mathbb{R}}(-) . The calculus produces a Taylor tower, the n n –th layer of which is classified by a spectrum with an action of C 2 ⋉ U ⁡ ( n ) C_{2}\ltimes\mathrm{U}(n) . We further give model categorical considerations, producing a zig–zag of Quillen equivalences between spectra with an action of C 2 ⋉ U ⁡ ( n ) C_{2}\ltimes\mathrm{U}(n) and a model structure on the category of input functors which captures the homotopy theory of the n n –th layer of the Taylor tower.

Introduction

The orthogonal and unitary calculi

\mathcal{J}

\mathcal{J}
is the category of finite–dimensional real inner product spaces or complex inner product spaces, respectively. The key idea behind these calculi is to approximate a given functor by a sequence of polynomial functors, similar to Taylor’s series from differential calculus. A natural question to ask is:
what can we say when the
\mathcal{J}
–spaces come with symmetry in the form of a group action?
For Goodwillie calculus, this equivariance has been studied by Dotto
[

,
7
,
8
]
, and Dotto and Moi
[
5
]
. An initial step in a much larger project to understand equivariant orthogonal calculus is the following calculus with reality. This is unitary calculus, constructed to take into account the
C_{2}
–action on the category of complex inner product spaces given by complex conjugation. In
[
24
,
25
]
, we explained the strong analogy of orthogonal and unitary calculi with real and complex topological
K
–theory. In fact, this analogy was the motivation behind the comparisons of
[
25

K
–theory with reality, due to Atiyah
[
2

, hence the ‘with reality’ appellation.

The idea is as in the orthogonal and unitary calculus, but suitably altered to take into account the precise equivariance which arises from complex conjugation. We define the notion of a polynomial functor with reality, and construct a polynomial approximation functor which is given as the fibrant replacement in a suitable model structure on the category of functors with reality, see Proposition

In detail, in the orthogonal calculus, Barnes and Oman
[

constructed a zig–zag of Quillen equivalences using only one intermediate step between their

[

\mathrm{U}(n)

As an interesting aside, we further strengthen the idea of calculus with reality being in analogy with the

1.
Real Stiefel Combinatorics

A theory of calculus is reliant on the notions of polynomials and derivatives. In the orthogonal and unitary calculus, the derivatives were constructed via relations between particular indexing categories, called the
n
–th jet categories (see
[
27
, Section 1]
and
[
24
, Definition 4.3]
). The relation between these categories gives the adjunctions used in the Quillen equivalences of
[
3
]
and
[
24
]
. This task is subtly more difficult when dealing with the calculus with reality. To have a well-defined theory we need to carefully choose our indexing category to fix a complex conjugation. We start by fixing such a universe, constructing the
n
–th jet categories, and demonstrating relationships between these categories.

1.1.
The universe

Unitary calculus is indexed on the universe
\mathbb{C}^{\infty}
, i.e. in
[
24
]
, we considered functors which take values on finite–dimensional inner product subspaces of
\mathbb{C}^{\infty}
. In the calculus with reality setting, we want the universe, and all its finite–dimensional subspaces, to be closed under complex conjugation, as without such a closure condition, we would be unable to assign a
C_{2}\T
–enrichment on the category of input functors. For this,
\mathbb{C}^{\infty}
is inappropriate, as is highlighted by the following example.

Example 1.1
.

Consider the complex line
\ell\subset\mathbb{C}^{2}
spanned by the vector
(1,i)
. This line is not closed under the inherited complex conjugation from
\mathbb{C}^{2}
, since
\overline{(1,i)}=(1,-i)
is not proportional to
(1,i)
. Moreover, there are many choices for a complex conjugation on the line spanned by
(1,i)
. If we consider the above complex conjugation, the set of fixed points is clearly the trivial inner product space
\{\underline{0}\}
. However, this line is isomorphic to
\mathbb{C}
, via the isomorphism
\varphi:\ell\longrightarrow\mathbb{C},(1,i)\mapsto 1
. This isomorphism defines a complex conjugation on the line
\ell
via
\overline{(1,i)}=\varphi^{-1}(\overline{\varphi(1,i)})
. This fixed point set of this complex conjugation is the real line. These complex conjugations do not agree, and there are several choices of the isomorphisms between
\ell
and
\mathbb{C}
, and hence serval different choices for the complex conjugation.

We choose the universe as
\mathbb{C}\otimes\mathbb{R}^{\infty}
. Within this universe we consider inner product spaces of the form
\mathbb{C}\otimes V
, for
V\subset\mathbb{R}^{\infty}
. Complex conjugation is then given by
\overline{c\otimes v}=\overline{c}\otimes v
for
c\in\mathbb{C},v\in V
, and the standard complex conjugation on
\mathbb{C}
.

Remark 1.2
.

The consideration of inner product spaces of the form
\mathbb{C}\otimes V
is equivalent to requiring complex inner product spaces which have a real basis, that is, finite–dimensional complex inner product spaces such that there exists a basis
\beta
consisting of only real inner products. For example, the standard basis on
\mathbb{C}^{n}
is real. It is not hard to show that
V=\mathbb{C}\otimes V^{\prime}
if and only if
V^{\prime}
has a real basis. Assume
V
is a complex finite–dimensional inner product space with real basis
\beta=\{\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{k}\}
. Then complex conjugation is given by

\overline{v}=\overline{\sum_{i=1}^{k}\lambda_{k}\beta_{k}}=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\overline{\lambda_{k}}\beta_{k}

where
\sum_{i=1}^{k}\lambda_{k}\beta_{k}
is the unique expression of
v
in terms of the basis vectors.

1.2.
The indexing categories

With the correct universe in place, the construction of the indexing categories is formal, and follows the orthogonal and unitary calculus versions,
[

24
]

Let

Remark 1.4

2.
Polynomial Functors with Reality

Any good theory of calculus, for example

,

These polynomial functors approximate a given functor in such a way to produce a Taylor tower which has strong analogy with Taylor series from differential calculus. Remark 2.3

The theory of a calculus with reality has previously been studied by Tynan in his thesis

4.
Derivatives of Functors with Reality

The other crucial ingredient in a theory of calculus is that of derivatives. Following the constructions of the derivatives in orthogonal and unitary calculus,
[
27
, Section 2]
,
[
3
, Section 4]

[
24
, Section 4]
, we construct the derivatives of a functor with reality, utilising the Real Stiefel combinatorics developed in Section
1
.
There is a strong analogy between the derivatives of a functor, and the derivatives of a function. For example, both compute the errors between successive polynomial approximations. Moreover, the derivative of any functor may be converted to a spectrum with an appropriate group action. This conversion of the derivative into a spectrum formed one aspect of the zig–zag of Quillen equivalences for orthogonal and unitary calculi,
[
3
, Proposition 8.3]
,
[
24
, Corollary 6.5, Proposition 6.7]
. In the ‘with reality’ setting, more work must be done to give a description of the derivative as a spectrum, and forms two steps in the three–step zig–zag of Quillen equivalences.

4.1.
The derivative

In the orthogonal and unitary calculus the derivative of a functor is constructed as a left Kan extension along a particular inclusion of categories. We follow a similar procedure here and define the derivative in this way. We first define categories,
C_{2}\ltimes\mathcal{E}^{\mathbf{R}}_{n}
, for all
n\geq 0
, which should be thought of as the unitary calculus categories with interwoven
C_{2}
–action coming from complex conjugation.

Definition 4.1
.

For all
n\geq 0
, define
C_{2}\ltimes\mathcal{E}^{\mathbf{R}}_{n}
to be the category of
C_{2}\T
–enriched functors from
\mathcal{J}^{\mathbf{R}}_{n}
to
C_{2}\T
.

For
m<n
, the inclusion
i_{m}^{n}:\mathbb{C}^{m}\longrightarrow\mathbb{C}^{n}
onto the first
m
–components is
C_{2}
–equivariant and induces a
C_{2}\T
–enriched functor
i_{m}^{n}:\mathcal{J}^{\mathbf{R}}_{m}\longrightarrow\mathcal{J}^{\mathbf{R}}_{n}
. On the level of vector bundles, the map is given by

i_{m}^{n}:\gamma^{\mathbf{R}}_{n}(U,V)\longrightarrow\gamma^{\mathbf{R}}_{n+1}(U,V),\quad(f,x)\mapsto(f,i_{m}^{n}\otimes\id(x)),

which is
C_{2}
–equivariant.

Definition 4.2
.

Define the
restriction functor

\res_{m}^{n}:C_{2}\ltimes\mathcal{E}^{\mathbf{R}}_{n}\longrightarrow C_{2}\ltimes\mathcal{E}^{\mathbf{R}}_{m}
by precomposition with
i_{m}^{n}
. Define the
induction functor

\ind_{m}^{n}:C_{2}\ltimes\mathcal{E}^{\mathbf{R}}_{m}\longrightarrow C_{2}\ltimes\mathcal{E}^{\mathbf{R}}_{m}
to be the (enriched) left Kan extension along
i_{m}^{n}
. When
m=0
, this induction functor defines the
n
–th derivative
of a functor with reality.

The definition of derivative is not amenable to calculations. The following gives the derivative as a particular homotopy fibre, allowing for calculations in some cases.

Proposition 4.3
.

For
F\in C_{2}\ltimes\mathcal{E}^{\mathbf{R}}_{n}
, there is a homotopy fibre sequence

\res_{n}^{n+1}\ind_{n}^{n+1}F(V)\longrightarrow F(V)\longrightarrow\Omega^{2n}F(V\oplus\mathbb{C})

in
C_{2}\T
for all
V\in\mathcal{J}_{n}
.

Proof.

This follows from
[
3
, Lemma 4.6]
upon noting the
C_{2}
–equivariance of Proposition
1.6
.
∎

In both the orthogonal and unitary calculi, relating polynomial functors to derivatives through fibres sequences was incredibly useful for computations of the derivatives and proving the zig–zag of Quillen equivalences. The first step in this process is the following
C_{2}
–equivariant homotopy cofibre sequence.

Lemma 4.4
.

There is a cofibre sequence

S\gamma^{\mathbf{R}}_{n+1}(V,W)_{+}\xrightarrow{p_{1}}\mathcal{J}^{\mathbf{R}}_{0}(V,W)\longrightarrow\mathcal{J}^{\mathbf{R}}_{n+1}(V,W)

in
C_{2}\T
where
p_{1}
is the projection onto the first component of
S\gamma_{n+1}(V,W)
.

Proof.

The mapping cone of
S\gamma_{n+1}(V,W)_{+}\xrightarrow{p_{1}}\mathcal{J}^{\mathbf{R}}_{0}(V,W)
is the pushout

\textstyle{S\gamma^{\mathbf{R}}_{n+1}(V,W)_{+}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{p_{1}}
\textstyle{\mathcal{J}^{\mathbf{R}}_{0}(V,W)\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{S\gamma^{\mathbf{R}}_{n+1}(V,W)_{+}\wedge[0,\infty]\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{\mathcal{P}}

where we use
[0,\infty]=[0,\infty)^{c}
(with basepoint
\infty
). This pushout consists of
3
–tuples
(f,x,t)
for
t\in[0,\infty]
and
(f,x)\in S\gamma^{\mathbf{R}}_{n+1}(V,W)_{+}
modulo the relations

\begin{split}(f,x,\infty)&=(f^{\prime},x^{\prime},\infty)\\
(f,x,0)&=(f^{\prime},x^{\prime},0).\\
\end{split}

The required map
\Phi:\mathcal{P}\longrightarrow\mathcal{J}^{\mathbf{R}}_{n+1}(V,W)
is given (away from the basepoint) by
\Phi(f,x,t)=(f,xt)
. This map is a
C_{2}
–equivariant homeomorphism.
∎

We can now state the desired fibre sequence, which gives a measure of the failure of a functor from being polynomial in terms of the derivative.

Proposition 4.5
.

For
F\in C_{2}\ltimes\mathcal{E}^{\mathbf{R}}_{0}
, there is a homotopy fibre sequence

\res_{0}^{n+1}\ind_{0}^{n+1}F(V)\longrightarrow F(V)\longrightarrow\tau_{n}F(V)

in
C_{2}\T
.

Proof.

By Lemma
4.4
, there is a cofibre sequence

S\gamma^{\mathbf{R}}_{n+1}(V,-)_{+}\xrightarrow{p_{1}}\mathcal{J}^{\mathbf{R}}_{0}(V,-)\longrightarrow\mathcal{J}^{\mathbf{R}}_{n+1}(V,-).

Applying the functor
C_{2}\ltimes\mathcal{E}^{\mathbf{R}}_{0}(-,F)
we obtain a homotopy fibre sequence

C_{2}\ltimes\mathcal{E}^{\mathbf{R}}_{0}(\mathcal{J}^{\mathbf{R}}_{n+1}(V,-),F)\longrightarrow C_{2}\ltimes\mathcal{E}^{\mathbf{R}}_{0}(\mathcal{J}^{\mathbf{R}}_{0}(V,-),F)\longrightarrow C_{2}\ltimes\mathcal{E}^{\mathbf{R}}_{0}(S\gamma^{\mathbf{R}}_{n+1}(V,-),F)

which reduces via definitions, the Yoneda Lemma and Proposition
2.9
to give a homotopy fibre sequence

\ind_{0}^{n}F(V)\longrightarrow F(V)\longrightarrow\tau_{n}F(V).\qed

As a corollary we see that the
(n+1)
–st derivative of an
n
–polynomial functor is trivial.

Corollary 4.6
.

If
F\in C_{2}\ltimes\mathcal{E}^{\mathbf{R}}_{0}
is
n
–polynomial, then
\res_{0}^{n}\ind_{0}^{n}F
is levelwise weakly contractible.

A useful result for showing an object is
n
–polynomial is the following result relating
n
–polynomial objects and homotopy fibres. The proof of which is an application of the Five Lemma. In particular the following result gives that the homotopy fibre of a map between
n
–polynomial objects is
n
–polynomial.

Lemma 4.7

(
[
27
, Lemma 5.5]
)
.

Let
E\in C_{2}\ltimes\mathcal{E}^{\mathbf{R}}_{0}
be
n
–polynomial,
g:E\longrightarrow F
a morphism in
C_{2}\ltimes\mathcal{E}^{\mathbf{R}}_{0}
and suppose that the
(n+1)
–th derivative of
F
is trivial Then the functor given by

V\mapsto\hofibre[E(V)\xrightarrow{g_{V}}F(V)]

is an
n
–polynomial.

An instant corollary is that the functor
V\mapsto\Omega F(V)
is
n
–polynomial whenever
F^{(n+1)}
vanishes.

Corollary 4.8
.

Let
F\in C_{2}\ltimes\mathcal{E}^{\mathbf{R}}_{0}
. If
F^{(n+1)}
is trivial, then the functor
V\mapsto\Omega F(V)
is
n
–polynomial.

Proof.

Apply Lemma
4.7
with
E=\ast
.
∎

Example 4.9
.

Let
\Theta\in\mathsf{Sp}^{\mathbf{O}}[C_{2}\ltimes\mathrm{U}(n)]
. Then the functor given by

V\longmapsto\Omega^{\infty}[(S^{nV}\wedge\Theta)_{h\mathrm{U}(n)}]

is
n
–polynomial.

Proof.

This follows from
[
24
, Example 4.12]
upon checking the
C_{2}
–equivariance.
∎

By the Quillen equivalence of the category of spectra with an action of
C_{2}\ltimes\mathrm{U}(n)
and
C_{2}\ltimes\mathcal{E}^{\mathbf{R}}_{1}[\mathrm{U}(n)]
, Theorem
5.8
, we achieve the following corollary.

Corollary 4.10
.

Let
\Theta\in C_{2}\ltimes\mathcal{E}^{\mathbf{R}}_{1}[\mathrm{U}(n)]
. Then the functor
F
given by

V\longmapsto\Omega^{\infty}[(S^{nV}\wedge\Theta)_{h\mathrm{U}(n)}]

is
n
–polynomial.

Proof.

We sketch the proof, full details of the argument can be found in
[
27
, Example 5.7]
or
[
24
, Example 4.12]
. Let
\Theta\in C_{2}\ltimes\mathcal{E}^{\mathbf{R}}_{1}[\mathrm{U}(n)]
and define a functor
F
as above. The sequence of derivatives of
F

F^{(n)}\longrightarrow F^{(n-1)}\longrightarrow\dots\longrightarrow F^{(i)}\longrightarrow\dots\longrightarrow F^{(1)}\longrightarrow F.

can be identified with the sequence of functors

F[n]\longrightarrow F[n-1]\longrightarrow\dots\longrightarrow F[i]\longrightarrow\dots\longrightarrow F[1]\longrightarrow F,

where
F[i](U)=\Omega^{\infty}[(S^{nU}\wedge\Theta)_{h\mathrm{U}(n-i)}]
, and
\mathrm{U}(n-i)
fixes the first
i
coordinates. Note that
F[i]
is an object of
C_{2}\ltimes\mathrm{U}(i)\mathcal{E}_{i}^{\mathbf{R}}
(see
[
24
, Example 4.12]
). The result then follows from Corollary
4.8
, and by noting that the first derivative of
F[i]
is
F[i+1]
.
∎

4.2.
The intermediate category

As with orthogonal calculus,
[
3
, Section 4]
, and unitary calculus,
[
24
, Section 4]
, the
n
–th derivative of a functor naturally lands in a category which is intermediate between the input category and spectra with an action of
C_{2}\ltimes\mathrm{U}(n)
. Utilising the theory of diagram spaces of Mandell, May, Schwede and Shipley
[
20
]
, we give the construction of such a category here, and its relation with the input category
C_{2}\ltimes\mathcal{E}^{\mathbf{R}}_{0}
.

Definition 4.11
.

Define
C_{2}\ltimes\mathrm{U}(n)\mathcal{E}_{n}^{\mathbf{R}}
to be the category of
(C_{2}\ltimes\mathrm{U}(n))\T
–enriched functors from
\mathcal{J}^{\mathbf{R}}_{n}
to
(C_{2}\ltimes\mathrm{U}(n))\T
.

This category comes with an equivalent description in terms of a category of modules. Let
\mathbf{R}
be the category with the same objects as
\mathcal{J}^{\mathbf{R}}
and linear isometric isomorphisms. Denote by
C_{2}\mathbf{R}\T
and
(C_{2}\ltimes\mathrm{U}(n))\mathbf{R}\T
the categories of
C_{2}
–equivariant
\mathbf{R}
–spaces and
(C_{2}\ltimes\mathrm{U}(n))
–equivariant
\mathbf{R}
–spaces, respectively. These are closed symmetric monoidal categories,
[
20
, Theorem 1.7]
, with product given by Day convolution
[
20
, Definition 21.4]
.

Define
n\mathbb{S}:\mathbf{R}\longrightarrow C_{2}\T
to be the functor given by
V\mapsto S^{nV}
, where
nV:=\mathbb{C}^{n}\otimes V
, with
C_{2}
acting on
S^{nV}
via complex conjugation on
V
. Following orthogonal and unitary calculus
[
3
, Lemma 7.3]
,
[
24
, Subsection 4.3]
, the functor
n\mathbb{S}
is a commutative monoid in
C_{2}\mathbf{R}\T
, and also in
(C_{2}\ltimes\mathrm{U}(n))\mathbf{R}\T
. We verify this claim for
C_{2}\mathbf{R}\T
, the other case follows from the unitary case
[
24
, Subsection 4.3]
.

Lemma 4.12
.

For each
n\geq 0
,
n\mathbb{S}
is a commutative monoid in the category
C_{2}\mathbf{R}\T
, of
C_{2}
–equivariant
\mathbf{R}
–spaces.

Proof.

The multiplication is identical to that of
[
3
, Lemma 7.3]
. It suffices to verify that the evaluation map

\mathrm{Ev}:\mathbf{R}(V,W)_{+}\wedge S^{V}\longrightarrow S^{W}

is
C_{2}
–equivariant. Away from the
C_{2}
-fixed basepoint, this map is given by
(f,v)\mapsto f(v)
. This is clearly
C_{2}
–equivariant since

\mathrm{Ev}(g\cdot(f,v))=\mathrm{Ev}(gfg,gv)=gf(ggv)=g(f(v))=g\cdot\mathrm{Ev}(f,v).\qed

With this, we get the description of the categories
C_{2}\ltimes\mathrm{U}(n)\mathcal{E}_{n}^{\mathbf{R}}
and
C_{2}\ltimes\mathcal{E}^{\mathbf{R}}_{n}
as categories of modules over
n\mathbb{S}
.

Proposition 4.13
.

The category
C_{2}\ltimes\mathcal{E}^{\mathbf{R}}_{n}
is equivalent to the category of
n\mathbb{S}
–modules in
C_{2}\mathbf{R}\T
, and the category
C_{2}\ltimes\mathrm{U}(n)\mathcal{E}_{n}^{\mathbf{R}}
is equivalent to the category of
n\mathbb{S}
–modules in
(C_{2}\ltimes\mathrm{U}(n))\mathbf{R}\T
.

Proof.

By
[
24
, Proposition 5.2]
it is enough to check that the isomorphism

\int^{U\in\mathbf{R}}\mathbf{R}(V\oplus U,W)_{+}\wedge S^{nU}\longrightarrow\mathcal{J}^{\mathbf{R}}_{n}(V,W)

is
C_{2}
–equivariant. It suffices to check this on the map

\Phi:\mathbf{R}(V\oplus U,W)_{+}\wedge S^{nU}\longrightarrow\mathcal{J}^{\mathbf{R}}_{n}(V,W),(f,u)\mapsto(f|_{V},(\mathbb{C}^{n}\otimes f)(u))

which construct the above isomorphism. Indeed, let
g\in C_{2}
and recall
g=g^{-1}
. Then

\begin{split}g\cdot\Phi(f,u)&=g\cdot(f|_{V},(\mathbb{C}^{n}\otimes f_{(}u)))=(g\cdot f|_{V},g((\mathbb{C}^{n}\otimes f)(u)))\\
&=((gfg)|_{V},g((\mathbb{C}^{n}\otimes f(ggu))))=\Phi(g\cdot f,gu)=\Phi(g\cdot(f,u)).\qed\end{split}

We now follow the procedure set by Barnes and Oman
[
3
, Section 4]
for orthogonal calculus and combine the restriction–induction adjunction with change of group functors of Mandell and May
[
21
, Section V.2]
to construct an adjunction between
C_{2}\ltimes\mathrm{U}(n)\mathcal{E}_{n}^{\mathbf{R}}
and
C_{2}\ltimes\mathrm{U}(m)\mathcal{E}_{m}^{\mathbf{R}}
similar to that of the above adjunction between
C_{2}\ltimes\mathcal{E}^{\mathbf{R}}_{n}
and
C_{2}\ltimes\mathcal{E}^{\mathbf{R}}_{m}
. Note that the same technique was employed in
[
24

.

This is as well defined functor since

to

4.3.
The
n
–stable model structure

With the description of
C_{2}\ltimes\mathrm{U}(n)\mathcal{E}_{n}^{\mathbf{R}}
as
n\mathbb{S}
–modules we can construct a stable model structure on
C_{2}\ltimes\mathrm{U}(n)\mathcal{E}_{n}^{\mathbf{R}}
. We start as is standard with the projective model structure, and suitably left Bousfield localise to produce the
n
–stable model structure. The existence of this model structure follows from
[
20
]
, taking
\mathcal{J}_{n}
as the diagram category.

Lemma 4.15
.

There is a cellular, proper and topological model structure on
C_{2}\ltimes\mathrm{U}(n)\mathcal{E}_{n}^{\mathbf{R}}
with weak equivalences and fibrations defined levelwise. The generating cofibrations are of the form

\mathcal{J}^{\mathbf{R}}_{n}(U,-)\wedge(C_{2}\ltimes\mathrm{U}(n))_{+}\wedge i

for
i
a generating cofibration of
\T
. The generating acyclic cofibrations are of the form

\mathcal{J}^{\mathbf{R}}_{n}(U,-)\wedge(C_{2}\ltimes\mathrm{U}(n))_{+}\wedge j

for
j
a generating acyclic cofibration of
\T
.

A left Bousfield localisation of this model structure results in the
n
–stable model structure. The construction is completely analogous to the
n
–stable model structure of
[
3
, Section 7]
or
[
24
, Subsection 4.3]
.

Definition 4.16
.

The
n
–homotopy groups
of
X\in C_{2}\ltimes\mathrm{U}(n)\mathcal{E}_{n}^{\mathbf{R}}
are defined as

n\pi_{k}X=\underset{q}{\colim}\penalty\ \pi_{2nq+k}X(\mathbb{C}^{q}).

A map
f:X\longrightarrow Y
in
C_{2}\ltimes\mathrm{U}(n)\mathcal{E}_{n}^{\mathbf{R}}
is an
n\pi_{*}
–isomorphism
if
n\pi_{k}:n\pi_{k}X\longrightarrow n\pi_{k}Y
is an isomorphism for all
k\geq 0
.

There is also a version of
n\Omega
–spectra defined analogously to the orthogonal or unitary calculus.

Definition 4.17
.

An element
X\in C_{2}\ltimes\mathrm{U}(n)\mathcal{E}_{n}^{\mathbf{R}}
is an
n\Omega
–spectrum
if the adjoint structure maps

X(U)\longrightarrow\Omega^{nV}X(U\oplus V)

are weak homotopy equivalences for all
U,V\in\mathcal{J}^{\mathbf{R}}_{n}
.

Denote by

\lambda_{V,W}^{n}:\mathcal{J}^{\mathbf{R}}_{n}(V\oplus W,-)\wedge S^{nW}\longrightarrow\mathcal{J}^{\mathbf{R}}_{n}(V,-)

the restricted composition map. We can factor this map, through it’s mapping cylinder, as a cofibration

k_{V,W}^{n}:\mathcal{J}^{\mathbf{R}}_{n}(V\oplus W,-)\wedge S^{nW}\longrightarrow M\lambda_{V,W}^{n}

and an acyclic fibration

r_{V,W}^{n}:M\lambda_{V,W}^{n}\longrightarrow\mathcal{J}^{\mathbf{R}}_{n}(V,-).

Adding the cofibrations
\{k_{V,W}^{n}\}
to the acyclic cofibrations of the projective model structure in a particular way yields the

5.2.
The equivalence between
C_{2}\ltimes\mathcal{E}^{\mathbf{R}}_{1}
and Real spectra

The above Quillen equivalence provides an equivalence of categories between the homotopy category of
C_{2}\ltimes\mathrm{U}(n)\mathcal{E}_{n}^{\mathbf{R}}
and the homotopy category of
C_{2}\ltimes\mathcal{E}^{\mathbf{R}}_{1}[\mathrm{U}(n)]
. In both orthogonal and unitary calculus, the categories
\mathcal{E}_{1}^{\mathbf{O}}
and
\mathcal{E}_{1}^{\mathbf{U}}
are equivalent to the categories of orthogonal and unitary spectra respectively. We now show that
C_{2}\ltimes\mathcal{E}^{\mathbf{R}}_{1}
is equivalent to the category of Real spectra of Schwede, see
[
23
, Example 7.11]
.

Definition 5.3
.

A Real spectrum
X
is a sequence of spaces
\{X_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
with an action of
C_{2}\ltimes\mathrm{U}(k)
together with structure maps

\sigma_{k}:X_{k}\wedge S^{2}\longrightarrow X_{k+1}

such that the iterated structure maps

\sigma_{k}^{m}:X_{k}\wedge S^{2m}\longrightarrow X_{k+m}

are
(C_{2}\ltimes(\mathrm{U}(k)\times\mathrm{U}(m)))
–equivariant.

A map of Real spectra
f:X\longrightarrow Y
is then a collection of maps
f_{k}:X_{k}\longrightarrow Y_{k}
which are compatible with the structure maps in the usual sense. Hence there is a category of Real spectra, denoted
C_{2}\ltimes\mathsf{Sp}^{\mathbf{U}}
. Again this notation is deliberate as one should think of Real spectra as unitary spectra with an interwoven
C_{2}
–action.

Proposition 5.4
.

The category of Real spectra,
C_{2}\ltimes\mathsf{Sp}^{\mathbf{U}}
is equivalent to the category
C_{2}\ltimes\mathcal{E}^{\mathbf{R}}_{1}
.

Proof.

We construct an inverse equivalence of categories. Define
\mathbb{U}:C_{2}\ltimes\mathcal{E}^{\mathbf{R}}_{1}\longrightarrow C_{2}\ltimes\mathsf{Sp}^{\mathbf{U}}
by

(\mathbb{U}X)_{n}=X(\mathbb{C}^{n}).

The space
(\mathbb{U}X)_{n}
inherits a
(C_{2}\ltimes\mathrm{U}(n))
–action from the evaluation maps

\mathcal{J}^{\mathbf{R}}(V,W)_{+}\wedge X(V)\longrightarrow X(W)

under the special case
V=W=\mathbb{C}^{n}
. The iterated structure maps

\sigma^{m}:(\mathbb{U}X)_{n}\wedge S^{m}\longrightarrow(\mathbb{U}X)_{n+m}

are induced by the structure maps for
X
, and are appropriately
(C_{2}\ltimes(\mathrm{U}(n)\times\mathrm{U}(m))
–equivariant by the special case
V=V^{\prime}=\mathbb{C}^{n}
and
W=W^{\prime}=\mathbb{C}^{m}
of the commuting of the diagram

\textstyle{\mathcal{J}^{\mathbf{R}}(V,V^{\prime})_{+}\wedge\mathcal{J}^{\mathbf{R}}(W,W^{\prime})_{+}\wedge X(V)\wedge S^{W}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{\mathcal{J}^{\mathbf{R}}(V\oplus W,V^{\prime}\oplus W^{\prime})_{+}\wedge X(V\oplus W)\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{X(V^{\prime})\wedge S^{W^{\prime}}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{X(V^{\prime}\oplus W^{\prime}).}

In the other direction, define
\mathbb{P}:C_{2}\ltimes\mathsf{Sp}^{\mathbf{U}}\longrightarrow C_{2}\ltimes\mathcal{E}^{\mathbf{R}}_{1}
, by

(\mathbb{P}Y)(V)=\mathcal{J}^{\mathbf{R}}(\mathbb{C}^{n},V)_{+}\wedge_{\mathrm{U}(n)}Y_{n}

whenever
\dim(V)=n
.
\mathrm{U}(n)
acts on
\mathcal{J}^{\mathbf{R}}(\mathbb{C}^{n},V)
by precomposition,
C_{2}
–acts diagonally on the smash product, and
(\mathbb{P}Y)(V)
is then the coequaliser of the two
\mathrm{U}(n)

[\varphi,-]:Y_{n}\longrightarrow(\mathbb{P}Y)(V),x\mapsto[\varphi,x].

The

. Moreover, the iterated structure maps

\sigma^{m}:Y_{n}\wedge S^{m}\longrightarrow X_{n+m}

are a special case of the generalised structure maps

\sigma_{V,W}:(\mathbb{P}Y)(V)\wedge S^{W}\longrightarrow(\mathbb{P}Y)(V\oplus W)

which are defined by setting
m=\dim(W)
and choosing an isometry

\sigma_{V,W}([\varphi,x],w)=[\varphi\oplus\psi,\sigma^{m}(x\wedge\psi^{-1}(w))].

By construction, the homeomorphism induced by

\mathbb{U}\mathbb{P}\cong\mathbbm{1}

\mathbb{P}\mathbb{U}\cong\mathbbm{1}

∎

5.3.
The Quillen equivalence between
C_{2}\ltimes\mathcal{E}^{\mathbf{R}}_{1}[\mathrm{U}(n)]
and spectra with an action of
C_{2}\ltimes\mathrm{U}(n)
.

Using the work of Schwede
[
23
, Example 7.11]
, we give a Quillen equivalence between
C_{2}\ltimes\mathcal{E}^{\mathbf{R}}_{1}
and the category of
C_{2}
–objects in orthogonal spectra,
\mathsf{Sp}^{\mathbf{O}}[C_{2}]

Let

, and define a functor
\psi:C_{2}\ltimes\mathcal{E}^{\mathbf{R}}_{1}\longrightarrow\mathsf{Sp}^{\mathbf{O}}[C_{2}]
by

\psi(X)(V)=\Omega^{iV}X(\mathbb{C}\otimes V)=\T(S^{iV},X(\mathbb{C}\otimes V)).

Assuming

, then the group
C_{2}\times\mathrm{O}(n)
acts on

by the sign representation for the
C_{2}
–factor and via the regular representation for the
\mathrm{O}(n)
–factor. Moreover
C_{2}\times\mathrm{O}(n)
acts on
X(\mathbb{C}\otimes V)
via restriction along the inclusion
C_{2}\times\mathrm{O}(n)\hookrightarrow C_{2}\ltimes\mathrm{U}(n)
, and hence
C_{2}\times\mathrm{O}(n)
acts on the mapping space via conjugation.

The description as a mapping space gives a clear description of the structure maps,

\begin{split}S^{1}\wedge\T(S^{iV},X(\mathbb{C}\otimes V))&\xrightarrow{\text{assemble}}\T(S^{iV},S^{1}\wedge X(\mathbb{C}\otimes V))\\
&\xrightarrow{S^{i\mathbb{R}}\wedge-}\T(S^{i\mathbb{R}}\wedge S^{iV},S^{iR}\wedge S^{1}\wedge X(\mathbb{C}\otimes V))\\
&\xrightarrow{\cong}\T(S^{i(\mathbb{R}\oplus V)},S^{2}\wedge X(\mathbb{C}\otimes V))\\
&\xrightarrow{(\sigma_{\mathbb{C}\otimes V})_{*}}\T(S^{i(\mathbb{R}\oplus V)},X(\mathbb{C}\otimes(V\oplus\mathbb{R})))\\
&\xrightarrow{=}\psi(X)(V\oplus\mathbb{R})\\
\end{split}

where we use the

to identity
S^{\mathbb{R}\oplus i\mathbb{R}}
with

. The functor

has a left adjoint, giving an adjunction between
C_{2}\ltimes\mathcal{E}^{\mathbf{R}}_{1}
and
\mathsf{Sp}^{\mathbf{O}}[C_{2}]

Proposition 5.5
.

There is an adjoint pair

\textstyle{L_{\psi}:\mathsf{Sp}^{\mathbf{O}}[C_{2}]\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces C_{2}\ltimes\mathcal{E}^{\mathbf{R}}_{1}:\psi}

where

A standard calculus of (co)ends argument verifies the claim.

This adjunction produces a Quillen equivalence between
C_{2}\ltimes\mathcal{E}^{\mathbf{R}}_{1}
and
\mathsf{Sp}^{\mathbf{O}}[C_{2}]

Proposition 5.6
.

The adjoint pair

\textstyle{L_{\psi}:\mathsf{Sp}^{\mathbf{O}}[C_{2}]\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces C_{2}\ltimes\mathcal{E}^{\mathbf{R}}_{1}:\psi}

is a Quillen equivalence when both categories are equipped with their stable model structures.

Proof.

By
[
16
, Proposition 8.5.4 and Lemma 7.7.1]
, in order to to exhibit a Quillen adjunction, it suffices to show that the right adjoint preserves acyclic fibrations and fibrant objects. Let
f:X\longrightarrow Y
be an acyclic fibration in
C_{2}\ltimes\mathcal{E}^{\mathbf{R}}_{1}
. Then,
f:X\longrightarrow Y
is a levelwise acyclic fibration. By construction
\psi(f):\psi X\longrightarrow\psi Y
will also be a levelwise acyclic fibration, and hence an acyclic fibration in
\mathsf{Sp}^{\mathbf{O}}[C_{2}]
.

Now, let
X
be a fibrant object in
C_{2}\ltimes\mathcal{E}^{\mathbf{R}}_{1}
, then
X(V)\simeq\Omega^{\mathbb{C}}X(V\oplus\mathbb{C})
for all
V\in\mathcal{J}^{\mathbf{R}}_{1}
. It follows that

\begin{split}\psi(X)(U)=\Omega^{iU}X(\mathbb{C}\otimes U)&\simeq\Omega^{iU}\Omega^{\mathbb{C}}X((\mathbb{C}\otimes U)\oplus\mathbb{C})\\
&\simeq\Omega^{iU}\Omega^{\mathbb{R}\oplus i\mathbb{R}}X(\mathbb{C}\otimes(U\oplus\mathbb{R}))\\
&\simeq\Omega^{\mathbb{R}}\Omega^{i(U\oplus\mathbb{R})}X(\mathbb{C}\otimes(U\oplus\mathbb{R}))\\
&\simeq\Omega^{\mathbb{R}}\psi(U\oplus\mathbb{R}),\\
\end{split}

and hence, the right adjoint preserves fibrant objects.

For the Quillen equivalence, it is left to show that the right adjoint reflects weak equivalences and that the derived unit is an isomorphism. For the first, suppose that
f:X\longrightarrow Y
is a map in
C_{2}\ltimes\mathcal{E}^{\mathbf{R}}_{1}
such that
\psi(f):\psi X\longrightarrow\psi Y
is a

is the derived image of
F
under the zig-zag of Quillen equivalences.

Proof.

Let
F
be cofibrant-fibrant in
n\homog C_{2}\ltimes\mathcal{E}^{\mathbf{R}}_{0}
, that is,
F
is
n
-homogeneous and projectively cofibrant. Define two new objects of
C_{2}\ltimes\mathcal{E}^{\mathbf{R}}_{0}
,

\begin{split}E:\mathcal{J}^{\mathbf{R}}_{0}\longrightarrow\T,\ \mathbb{C}\otimes V&\longmapsto(\ind_{0}^{n}F(\mathbb{C}\otimes V))_{h\mathrm{U}(n)}\\
G:\mathcal{J}^{\mathbf{R}}_{0}\longrightarrow\T,\ \mathbb{C}\otimes V&\longmapsto\Omega^{\infty}[(S^{\mathbb{R}^{2n}\otimes V}\wedge\Theta_{F}^{n})_{h\mathrm{U}(n)}]\end{split}

Since
E(\mathbb{C}\otimes V)\simeq\Theta_{F}^{n}(\mathbb{R}^{2n}\otimes V)
, there is a levelwise weak equivalence between
E
and the object of
C_{2}\ltimes\mathcal{E}^{\mathbf{R}}_{0}
defined by

\mathbb{C}\otimes V\longmapsto[\Omega^{\infty}(S^{\mathbb{R}^{2n}\otimes V}\wedge\Theta_{F}^{n})]_{h\mathrm{U}(n)}.

It follows by Example
6.6
, and the fact that
G
is
n
-polynomial (Example
4.9
) that there is a zig-zag of levelwise weak equivalence
T_{n}E\longrightarrow T_{n}G\longleftarrow G
. As derived functors preserve equivalences, there is a zig-zag of levelwise weak equivalences,

\ind_{0}^{n}\varepsilon^{*}T_{n}E\longrightarrow\ind_{0}^{n}\varepsilon^{*}T_{n}G\longleftarrow\ind_{0}^{n}\varepsilon^{*}G.

The
n
-th derivative of
G
is identified in Example
4.9
with the functor
G[n]\in C_{2}\ltimes\mathrm{U}(n)\mathcal{E}_{n}^{\mathbf{R}}
defined by

\mathbb{C}\otimes V\longmapsto\Omega^{\infty}(S^{\mathbb{R}^{2n}\otimes V}\wedge\Theta_{F}^{n}).

Since
G[n]
is levelwise weakly equivalent to
\ind_{0}^{n}\varepsilon^{*}F
, there is a zig-zag of levelwise weak equivalences between
\ind_{0}^{n}\varepsilon^{*}T_{n}E
and
\ind_{0}^{n}\varepsilon^{*}F
. A double application of Whitehead’s Theorem for (co)localisations of model structures,
[
16
, Theorem 3.2.13]
, yields a zig-zag of levelwise weak equivalences between
E
and
F
. The case for general
n
-homogeneous
F
then follows by projectively cofibrantly replacing
F
.
∎

Remark 7.2
.

In the above proof we used the identification
\mathbb{C}^{n}\otimes_{\mathbb{C}}\mathbb{C}\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}V\cong\mathbb{R}^{2n}\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}V
to identify their one-point compactifications. Such an identification between the one-point compactifications made the relationship to orthogonal spectra clearer.

The end result of this chapter is that given a functor with reality,
F\in C_{2}\ltimes\mathcal{E}^{\mathbf{R}}_{0}
, there exists a Taylor tower approximating
F
at
V\in\mathcal{J}^{\mathbf{R}}

\textstyle{F(V)\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{\cdots\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{r_{n+1}}
\textstyle{T_{n}F(V)\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{r_{n}}
\textstyle{\cdots\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{r_{2}}
\textstyle{T_{1}F(V)\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{r_{1}}
\textstyle{F(\mathbb{C}^{\infty})}
\textstyle{\Omega^{\infty}[(S^{nV}\wedge\Psi_{F}^{n})_{h\mathrm{U}(n)}]\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}

where the

8.
Examples

The general theory of such a calculus will be familiar to those with some knowledge of the orthogonal or unitary calculus. In this section we gather a series of examples for the reader, so those familiar with the general idea of the theory can see this new calculus in practice, and refer back to the relevant sections where necessary. There is a stark similarity between calculus with reality and unitary calculus. This should not be surprise as one may thing of unitary calculus as the resulting calculus after ‘forgetting’ the
C_{2}
–action which we have built into the calculus with reality.

8.1.
Representable functors with reality

We are particularly interested in the representable functors. They played a crucial role in understanding convergence results in orthogonal and unitary calculus
[

[

\mathcal{J}^{\mathbf{R}}_{n}(0,-)=n\mathbb{S}

\mathcal{J}^{\mathbf{R}}_{n}(V,-)
for all
V\in\mathcal{J}^{\mathbf{R}}_{n}
and all

Example 8.1
.

Let
n\mathbb{S}\in n\homog C_{2}\ltimes\mathcal{E}^{\mathbf{R}}_{0}
. This is the image of
\mathrm{U}(n)_{+}\wedge\mathcal{J}^{\mathbf{R}}_{n}(0,-)
under the derived left adjoint
\mathbbm{L}\res_{0}^{n}/\mathrm{U}(n)
. In turn, applying
\mathbbm{L}(\xi_{n})_{!}
to
\mathrm{U}(n)_{+}\wedge\mathcal{J}^{\mathbf{R}}_{n}(0,-)
gives
\mathrm{U}(n)_{+}\wedge\mathcal{J}_{1}^{\mathbf{R}}(0,-)
. Moreover,
\mathrm{U}(n)_{+}\wedge\mathcal{J}_{1}^{\mathbf{R}}(0,-)
is the image of
\Sigma_{+}^{\infty}\mathrm{U}(n)
under the derived left adjoint
\mathbbm{L}(L_{\psi})
. Diagrammatically, we have the following

n\mathbb{S}\longmapsfrom{\mathrm{U}(n)_{+}\wedge\mathcal{J}^{\mathbf{R}}_{n}(0,-)}\longmapsto\mathrm{U}(n)_{+}\wedge\mathcal{J}^{\mathbf{R}}_{1}(0,-)\longmapsfrom\Sigma_{+}^{\infty}\mathrm{U}(n).

It follows that the
n
–th derivative of
n\mathbb{S}
is the (naive)
(C_{2}\ltimes\mathrm{U}(n))
–spectrum
\Sigma_{+}^{\infty}\mathrm{U}(n)
.

Remark 8.2
.

In the above example we saw that the derived left adjoint,
\mathbbm{L}(\xi_{n})_{!}
‘changes’
\mathcal{J}^{\mathbf{R}}_{n}
to
\mathcal{J}^{\mathbf{R}}_{1}
. Intuitively, one should think of this functor as a ‘change of rings’ functor.

Remark 8.3
.

As an example of how the algebraic model of Greenlees and Shipley from Remark

reduces the complexity of computations, we see by
[

that the algebraic model for the

n
-th derivative of the algebraic model for
n\mathbb{S}
would be
\Sigma^{n^{2}}\mathbb{Q}
.

Calculating the

–th derivative in Example
8.1
allows us to calculate the
n
–polynomial approximation. The unitary version of the following is
[

.

Example 8.4
.

The functor
n\mathbb{S}
is
n

and an object of the localising set

n

[

(2n\dim(U)-1)

Example 8.5
.

As before, we have the following diagram, where each arrow is a derived left adjoint, as part of the zig–zag of Quillen equivalences.

\mathcal{J}^{\mathbf{R}}_{n}(\mathbb{C}\otimes U,-)\longmapsfrom{\mathrm{U}(n)_{+}\wedge\mathcal{J}^{\mathbf{R}}_{n}(\mathbb{C}\otimes U,-)}\longmapsto\mathrm{U}(n)_{+}\wedge\mathcal{J}^{\mathbf{R}}_{1}(\mathbb{C}\otimes U,-)\longmapsfrom\Sigma^{\infty}_{U}(\mathrm{U}(n)_{+}),

\Sigma^{\infty}_{U}(\mathrm{U}(n)_{+})

, left adjoint to evaluation at

\mathbbm{L}(L_{\psi})(\Sigma^{\infty}_{U}(\mathrm{U}(n)_{+})

\begin{split}(L_{\psi})(\Sigma^{\infty}_{U}(\mathrm{U}(n)_{+})(\mathbb{C}\otimes V)&=\int^{W\in\mathcal{J}_{1}^{\mathbf{O}}}\mathcal{J}_{1}^{\mathbf{R}}(\mathbb{C}\otimes W,\mathbb{C}\otimes V)\wedge\Sigma^{\infty}_{U}(\mathrm{U}(n)_{+})(W)\wedge S^{iW}\\

\end{split}

8.3.
The Real classifying space of the unitary group

Proposition
4.3
gives a homotopy fibre sequence which allows for the iterative calculation of the derivative of a functor with reality. We can apply this to the Real classifying space of the unitary group functor,
\BU_{\mathbb{R}}(-):V\mapsto\BU_{\mathbb{R}}(V)
, which is given by
\BU(V)
with
C_{2}
–action inherited from the complex conjugation on
V

Example 8.8
.

There is a homotopy fibre sequence

\BU_{\mathbb{R}}^{(1)}(V)\longrightarrow\BU_{\mathbb{R}}(V)\longrightarrow\BU_{\mathbb{R}}(V\oplus\mathbb{C}),

that is, a
C_{2}
–equivariant homotopy fibre sequence

\BU^{(1)}(V)\longrightarrow\BU(V)\longrightarrow\BU(V\oplus\mathbb{C}),

where
\BU(V)
had the induced
C_{2}
–action by complex conjugation on
3V
. As such, the first derivative of
\BU_{\mathbb{R}}(-)
is the shifted sphere spectrum
\Sigma^{\infty}S^{-1}\simeq\Omega\Sigma^{\infty}S^{0}
, with
C_{2}\ltimes\mathrm{U}(1)
–acting via the
C_{2}
–action on the inner product spaces, and
\mathrm{U}(1)
acting trivially.

We now consider a functor of which,
\BU_{\mathbb{R}}(-)
is an extension. The following functor is easier to understand since it has trivial
0
-polynomial approximation. This is
[
27
, Example 10.2]
, with added
C_{2}
–equivariance.

Example 8.9
.

Consider the functor
E
given by

V\longmapsto{\raisebox{1.99997pt}{$\mathrm{U}(V\oplus\mathbb{C}^{\infty})$}\left/\raisebox{-1.99997pt}{$\mathrm{U}(V)$}\right.}.

This functor is similar to
\BU_{\mathbb{R}}(-)
, as
\BU_{\mathbb{R}}(-)
is an extension of

T_{0}E(V)=E(\mathbb{C}^{\infty})\ast

D_{1}E(V)\longrightarrow T_{1}E(V)\longrightarrow T_{0}E(V)\simeq\ast

T_{1}E(V)\simeq\Omega^{\infty}[(S^{V}\wedge\Sigma^{\infty}S^{-1})_{h\mathrm{U}(1)}]

where we have identified the first derivative of
