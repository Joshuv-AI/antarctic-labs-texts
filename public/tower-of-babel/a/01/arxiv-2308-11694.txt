Modular curves X 0 ​ ( N ) X_{0}(N) with infinitely many quartic points
Maarten Derickx, Petar Orlić

Abstract
. We determine all modular curves X 0 ​ ( N ) X_{0}(N) with infinitely many quartic points. To do this, we define a pairing that induces a quadratic form representing all possible degrees of a rational morphism from X 0 ​ ( N ) X_{0}(N) to a positive rank elliptic curve.

1.
Introduction

Let
C
be a smooth, projective, and geometrically integral curve defined over a number field
k
. Determining whether the set of points of degree
\leq d
on
C
is finite or infinite is an important problem in arithmetic geometry. Faltings’ Theorem solves the base case
d=1
.

Theorem 1.1

(Faltings’ Theorem)
.

Let
k
be a number field and let
C
be a non-singular curve defined over
k
of genus
g\geq 2
. Then the set
C(k)
is finite.

Therefore, if
C(k)\neq\emptyset
, then it is infinite if and only if
C
is isomorphic to
\mathbb{P}^{1}
(
g=0
) or
C
is an elliptic curve (
g=1
) with positive
k
-rank. The next step is considering the same problem for
d>1
.

Definition 1.2
.

Let
C
be a curve defined over a number field
k
. The arithmetic degree of irrationality
\textup{a.irr}_{k}C
is the smallest integer
d
such that
C
has infinitely many closed points of degree
d
over
k
, i.e.

\textup{a.irr}_{k}C:=\min\left(d,\#\left\{\cup_{[F:k]\leq d}C(F)\right\}=\infty\right).

We also define

\textup{a.irr}_{\overline{k}}\ C:=\min_{[L:k]<\infty}\textup{a.irr}_{L}\ C.

It is obvious that
\textup{a.irr}_{k}C\geq\textup{a.irr}_{\overline{k}}\ C
.

Harris and Silverman
[
14
]
proved that if a curve of genus
g\geq 2
has infinitely many quadratic points, then it must be either hyperelliptic or bielliptic. Also, Abramovich and Harris
[
2
]
gave a conjecture

\textup{a.irr}_{\overline{k}}\ C\leq d\iff C\textup{ admits a map of degree}\leq d\textup{ to }\mathbb{P}^{1}\textup{ or an elliptic curve}

which they proved for
d=2,3
. However, Debarre and Fahlaoui constructed counterexamples for
d\geq 4

[
8
]
. One way to characterize when there are infinitely many points of degree
d
on
C
is the following theorem.

Theorem 1.3

(
[
6
, Theorem 4.2. (1)]
)
.

Let
C
be a curve over a number field. There are infinitely many degree
d
points on
C
if and only if either there exists a map
C\to\mathbb{P}^{1}
of degree
d
or the image of
\operatorname{Sym}^{d}C
in
\operatorname{Pic}^{d}C
contains a translate of a positive rank abelian variety.

It can be hard to check, however, whether the image of
\operatorname{Sym}^{d}C
in
\operatorname{Pic}^{d}C
contains a translate of a positive rank abelian variety. Kadets and Vogt gave a simpler characterization for
d=2,3
, which encompasses the previous results of Harris-Silverman
[
14
]
and Abramovich-Harris
[
2
]
.

Theorem 1.4

(
[
23
]
, Theorem 1.3)
.

Suppose
X/k
is a smooth, projective, and geometrically integral curve. Then the following statements hold:

(1)

If
\textup{a.irr}_{k}X=2
, then
X
is a double cover of
\mathbb{P}^{1}
or an elliptic curve of positive rank over
k
.

(2)

If
\textup{a.irr}_{k}X=3
, then one of the following three cases holds:

(a)

X
is a triple cover of
\mathbb{P}^{1}
or an elliptic curve of positive rank over
k
.

(b)

X
is a smooth plane quartic with no rational points, positive rank Jacobian, and at least one cubic point.

(c)

X
is a genus
4
Debarre-Fahlaoui curve.

(3)

If
\textup{a.irr}_{\overline{k}}X=d\leq 3
, then
X_{\overline{k}}
is a degree
d
cover of
\mathbb{P}^{1}
or an elliptic curve.

(4)

If
\textup{a.irr}_{\overline{k}}X=d=4,5
, then either
X_{\overline{k}}
is a Debarre-Fahlaoui curve, or
X_{\overline{k}}
is a degree
d
cover of
\mathbb{P}^{1}
or an elliptic curve.

Definition 1.5
.

For a curve
C
defined over a field
k
, the
k
-gonality
\textup{gon}_{k}C
is the smallest integer
d
such that there exists a morphism of degree
d
from
C
to
\mathbb{P}^{1}
defined over
k
.

The question of determining
\textup{a.irr}_{k}C
is closely related to the

-gonality of
C
and degree

maps to elliptic curves. Frey
[

proved that if a curve

defined over a number field

.

Regarding the curves

2.
Jacobians

2.1.
Notation and definitions

Let
C,C^{\prime}
be curves over a field
k
. A morphism
f:C\to C^{\prime}
induces maps
f_{*}:J(C)\to J(C^{\prime})
and
f^{*}:J(C^{\prime})\to J(C)
which are defined as follows. If
D=\sum n_{i}P_{i}
and
D^{\prime}=\sum n^{\prime}_{i}P^{\prime}_{i}
are divisors on
C
and
C^{\prime}
respectively, then
f_{*}([D])=[\sum n_{i}f(P_{i})]
and
f^{*}([D^{\prime}])=[\sum n^{\prime}_{i}f^{-1}(P^{\prime}_{i})]
. When seeing
J(C)
not as divisors modulo principal divisors but as
\operatorname{Pic}^{0}(C)
, the map
f^{*}
is sometimes also denoted as
f^{\vee}
or
\operatorname{Pic}(f)
.

Lemma 2.1
.

f_{*}\circ f^{*}=[\deg f]
.

Proof.

f_{*}(f^{*}([D^{\prime}]))=f_{*}(f^{*}([\sum n^{\prime}_{i}P^{\prime}_{i}]))=f_{*}([\sum n^{\prime}_{i}f^{-1}(P^{\prime}_{i})])=[\sum n^{\prime}_{i}\cdot(\deg f)P^{\prime}_{i}]=[(\deg f)D^{\prime}]
.
∎

By
[
35
]
, Theorem 6.6, the abelian variety
J(C)
comes with a canonical principal polarization

\phi_{\Theta_{C}}:J(C)\to J(C)^{\vee}

induced by the theta divisor of
C
. This map is an isomorphism.

If
P\in C(k)
, then we can define the embedding morphism

\displaystyle f_{P}:C

\displaystyle\to J(C),

\displaystyle x

\displaystyle\mapsto[x-P].

If
A
is an abelian variety over
k
, we can use this point to define

\displaystyle\operatorname{Hom}_{P}(C,A):=\left\{f\in\operatorname{Hom}(C,A)\mid f(P)=0\right\}.

With this definition, the universal property of the Jacobian (
[
35
]
, Theorem 6.1) states that the map

\displaystyle\iota_{P}:\operatorname{Hom}(J(C),A)

\displaystyle\to\operatorname{Hom}_{P}(C,A),

\displaystyle h

\displaystyle\mapsto h\circ f_{P}

is an isomorphism.

The map

\displaystyle s_{P}:\operatorname{Hom}(C,A)

\displaystyle\to\operatorname{Hom}_{P}(C,A),

\displaystyle f

\displaystyle\mapsto t_{-f(P)}\circ f,

where
t_{-f(P)}
denotes the translation by
-f(P)
map, is a retraction of the canonical inclusion
\operatorname{Hom}_{P}(C,A)\to\operatorname{Hom}(C,A)
whose kernel are the constant maps. Since the constant maps can be identified with
A(k)
, we have a direct sum decomposition

\displaystyle\operatorname{Hom}(C,A)\cong\operatorname{Hom}_{P}(C,A)\times A(k).

If
A
is an elliptic curve, then
f
and
s_{P}(f)
have the same degree because
t_{-f(p)}
is an isomorphism. In particular if one wants to study the possible degrees that occur for elements in
\operatorname{Hom}(C,A)
, it suffices to restrict to those in
\operatorname{Hom}_{P}(C,A)
.

Note that the maps
f_{P}^{\vee}:J(C)^{\vee}\to J(C)
and
\phi_{\Theta_{C}}:J(C)\to J(C)^{\vee}
are closely related to each other, namely
f_{P}^{\vee}\circ\phi_{\Theta_{C}}=-\operatorname{Id}_{J(C)}
. For elliptic curves, one often takes
P=0_{E}
to be the zero section of the elliptic curve, and then the map
f_{0_{E}}:E\to J(E)
is used to identify
E
with its Jacobian/dual. So the above means that this identification differs by the one coming from the polarization
\phi_{\Theta_{E}}:J(E)\to J(E)^{\vee}=E^{\vee\vee}\cong E
by a minus sign.

2.2.
Degree pairing

We already saw in the previous section that if
f:C\to C^{\prime}
is a map of curves, then
f_{*}\circ f^{*}=[\deg f]
. This motivates the following definition:

Definition 2.2
.

Let
C,E
be curves over a field
k
with
E
being an elliptic curve. The degree pairing is defined on
\operatorname{Hom}(C,E)
as

\displaystyle\left<\rule{6.99997pt}{0.4pt},\rule{6.99997pt}{0.4pt}\right>:\operatorname{Hom}(C,E)\times\operatorname{Hom}(C,E)

\displaystyle\to\operatorname{End}(J(E))

\displaystyle f,g

\displaystyle\mapsto f_{*}\circ g^{*}.

If
P\in C(k)
, then we can define the degree pairing on
\operatorname{Hom}(J(C),E)
as

\displaystyle\left<\rule{6.99997pt}{0.4pt},\rule{6.99997pt}{0.4pt}\right>:\operatorname{Hom}(J(C),E)\times\operatorname{Hom}(J(C),E)

\displaystyle\to\operatorname{End}(J(E)),

\displaystyle f,g

\displaystyle\mapsto(f\circ f_{P})_{*}\circ(g\circ f_{P})^{*}.

We will also write
\left<f,g\right>:=f_{*}\circ g^{*}
for
f,g\in\operatorname{Hom}(C,C^{\prime})
(this is not a pairing when
C^{\prime}
is not elliptic since
\operatorname{Hom}(C,C^{\prime})
is not an abelian group in that case). With this notation we have
\langle f,f\rangle=[\deg f]
for
f\in\operatorname{Hom}(C,C^{\prime})
.

Note that the definition on
\operatorname{Hom}(J(C),E)
is slightly unsatisfactory since a priori it seems to depend on the base point
P
. Additionally, it is not defined in terms of intrinsic properties of the abelian variety
J(C)
, but instead just defined by using
f_{P}:C\to J(C)
to transport the definition on
\operatorname{Hom}(C,E)
to that on
\operatorname{Hom}(J(C),E)
. So let’s try to give a more intrinsic definition.

Let
A
and
B
be two polarized abelian varieties over
k
with polarizations
\phi_{A}
and
\phi_{B}
respectively and assume the polarization
\phi_{A}
is principal. Then one can define the map

\displaystyle\rule{6.99997pt}{0.4pt}^{\dagger}:\operatorname{Hom}(A,B)

\displaystyle\to\operatorname{Hom}(B,A),

\displaystyle f

\displaystyle\mapsto\phi_{A}^{-1}\circ f^{\vee}\circ\phi_{B}.

When
A=B
this is just the Rosati involution, defined in Section 17 of
[
34
]
.

Definition 2.3
.

Let
(A,\phi_{A})
and
(B,\phi_{B})
be two polarized abelian varieties over
k
with
\phi_{A}
a principal polarization. Then the dagger pairing on
\operatorname{Hom}(A,B)
is defined as

\displaystyle\left<\rule{6.99997pt}{0.4pt},\rule{6.99997pt}{0.4pt}\right>_{\dagger}:\operatorname{Hom}(A,B)\times\operatorname{Hom}(A,B)

\displaystyle\to\operatorname{End}(B),

\displaystyle f,g

\displaystyle\mapsto f\circ g^{\dagger}.

The following lemma shows how the dagger pairing relates to the degree pairing.

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
C,E
be curves over a field
k
with
E
being an elliptic curve, and let
P\in C(k)
. Then for
f,g\in\operatorname{Hom}(J(C),J(E))
we have

\langle f,g\rangle_{\dagger}=\langle\,f_{0_{E}}^{-1}\circ f\circ f_{P},\,\,f_{0_{E}}^{-1}\circ g\circ f_{P}\,\rangle,

where the principal polarizations on
J(C)
and
J(E)
needed for the definition of
\left<\rule{6.99997pt}{0.4pt},\rule{6.99997pt}{0.4pt}\right>_{\dagger}
are taken to be those coming from the theta divisors on
C
and
E
.

Proof.

We prove this by showing
(f_{0_{E}}^{-1}\circ f\circ f_{P})_{*}=f
and
(f_{0_{E}}^{-1}\circ g\circ f_{P})^{*}=g^{\dagger}
.

For the equality
(f_{0_{E}}^{-1}\circ f\circ f_{P})_{*}=f
it suffices to show equality on points over the algebraic closure of
k
. So let
D=\sum n_{i}P_{i}
be a degree zero divisor representing a point in
J(C)(\overline{k})
. Then

\displaystyle(f_{0_{E}}^{-1}\circ f\circ f_{P})_{*}(\sum n_{i}P_{i})=\sum n_{i}(f_{0_{E}}^{-1}\circ f)(P_{i}-P)=(f_{0_{E}}^{-1}\circ f)(\sum n_{i}(P_{i}-P))=\ldots

\displaystyle\ldots=f_{0_{E}}^{-1}(f(\sum n_{i}P_{i})-f(\sum n_{i}P))=f_{0_{E}}^{-1}(f(\sum n_{i}P_{i})-f(0))=f(\sum n_{i}P_{i}).

The equality
(f_{0_{E}}^{-1}\circ g\circ f_{P})^{*}=g^{\dagger}
follows since
∗
and
∨
denote the same operation and

(f_{0_{E}}^{-1}\circ g\circ f_{P})^{\vee}=f_{P}^{\vee}\circ g^{\vee}\circ(f_{0_{E}}^{-1})^{\vee}=(-\phi_{\Theta_{C}})^{-1}\circ g^{\vee}\circ(-\phi_{\Theta_{E}})=\phi_{\Theta_{C}}^{-1}\circ g^{\vee}\circ\phi_{\Theta_{E}}=g^{\dagger},

where the second equality follows by applying Lemma 6.8 of
[
35
]
twice.

∎

Proposition 2.5
.

Let
C,E
be curves over
\mathbb{Q}
with
E
being an elliptic curve. Then the dagger pairing is a positive definite symmetric bilinear form on
\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathbb{Q}}(J(C),J(E))
taking values in
\operatorname{End}_{\mathbb{Q}}(J(E))=\mathbb{\mathbb{Z}}
.

Proof.

The dagger pairing is obviously bilinear. It is also symmetric because for
f,g\in\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathbb{Q}}(J(C),J(E))
we have

\left<f,g\right>_{\dagger}=f\circ g^{\dagger}=(g\circ f^{\dagger})^{\dagger}=g\circ f^{\dagger}.

Here the last equality holds because
g\circ f^{\dagger}\in\operatorname{End}_{\mathbb{Q}}(J(E))
is of the form
[n]
for some
n\in\mathbb{Z}
and
[n]^{\dagger}=[n]
. The positive definiteness follows from
2.4
since we can compute
\left<f,f\right>_{\dagger}
over
\overline{\mathbb{Q}}
by choosing a
P\in C(\overline{\mathbb{Q}})
as follows:

\left<f,f\right>_{\dagger}=\langle\,f_{0_{E}}^{-1}\circ f\circ f_{P},\,\,f_{0_{E}}^{-1}\circ f\circ f_{P}\,\rangle=[\deg f_{0_{E}}^{-1}\circ f\circ f_{P}],

and
\deg f_{0_{E}}^{-1}\circ f\circ f_{P}>0
if
f\neq 0
.
∎

Remark 2.6
.

If
E
is a CM elliptic curve over
\mathbb{C}
, we could also consider the dagger pairing
\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathbb{C}}(J(C),J(E))\times\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathbb{C}}(J(C),J(E))\to\operatorname{End}_{\mathbb{C}}(J(E))

†

2.3.
Degeneracy maps

Let
M
and
N
be positive integers such that
M\mid N
. For every divisor
d
of
\frac{N}{M}
there exists a degeneracy map

\iota_{d,N,M}:X_{0}(N)\to X_{0}(M),(E,G)\mapsto(E/G[d],(G/G[d])[M]).

The degeneracy map acts on
\tau\in\mathbb{H}^{*}
in the extended upper half-plane as

\iota_{d,N,M}(\tau)=d\tau.

From this or directly from the definition, we can easily see that when
dM\mid N
and
d^{\prime}N\mid N^{\prime}
, then

(1)

\displaystyle\iota_{d,N,M}\circ\iota_{d^{\prime},N^{\prime},N}=\iota_{dd^{\prime},N^{\prime},M}.

We want to describe
\left<\iota_{d_{1},N,M},\iota_{d_{2},N,M}\right>
for different divisors
d_{1}
,
d_{2}
of
\frac{N}{M}
in terms of Hecke operators on
J_{0}(M)
(the case
d_{1}=d_{2}
is solved by
2.1
).
We recall from Section 7.3 of
[
11
]
that Hecke operators
T_{n}
act on
Y_{0}(M)
as

T_{n}(E,G)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\#C=n\\
C\cap G=\{0\}\end{subarray}}(E/C,(G+C)/C)

and have the following properties:

\displaystyle T_{p^{r}}

\displaystyle=T_{p^{r-1}}T_{p}-pT_{p^{r-2}}

\displaystyle\textup{ for primes }p\nmid M\textup{ and }r>1,

\displaystyle T_{p^{r}}

\displaystyle=T_{p}^{r}

\displaystyle\textup{ for primes }p\mid M\textup{ and }r>0,

\displaystyle T_{mn}

\displaystyle=T_{m}T_{n}

\displaystyle\textup{ if }(m,n)=1.

We want to determine
\left<\iota_{d_{1},N,M},\iota_{d_{2},N,M}\right>
. When
N=Mp
for a prime
p
, we already know from Section 7.3 of
[
11
]
that
\left<\iota_{1,N,M},\iota_{p,N,M}\right>=T_{p}
. The remaining case is when
\frac{N}{M}
is a composite number. Before we consider that case, we prove a technical group theory lemma.

Lemma 2.7
.

Let
G
be an abelian group of order
N
that has a cyclic subgroup
G^{\prime}
of order
d
. If
dG\cong\mathbb{Z}/(N/d)\mathbb{Z}
, then
G
is cyclic.

Proof.

We know that
G\cong(\mathbb{Z}/(d_{1}\mathbb{Z}))\times\ldots\times(\mathbb{Z}/(d_{k}\mathbb{Z}))
, where
d_{i}
are integers such that
d_{1}\mid\ldots\mid d_{k}
,
d_{1}\ldots d_{k}=N
and
d\mid d_{k}
. Thus,

dG\leq(\mathbb{Z}/(d_{1}\mathbb{Z}))\times\ldots\times\left(\mathbb{Z}/\left(\frac{d_{k}}{d}\mathbb{Z}\right)\right).

However,
dG\cong\mathbb{Z}/\left(\frac{N}{d}\mathbb{Z}\right)
implying that
d_{1}=\ldots=d_{k-1}=1
and
d_{k}=N
.
∎

Lemma 2.8
.

If
\frac{N}{M}
is square-free, then
\left<\iota_{1,N,M},\iota_{N/M,N,M}\right>=T_{N/M}
.

Proof.

Suppose that
(E,G)
represents a point on
Y_{0}(M)
. We compute

\displaystyle\left<\iota_{1,N,M},\iota_{N/M,N,M}\right>(E,G)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}E^{\prime}/G^{\prime}[N/M]=E\\
G^{\prime}/G^{\prime}[N/M]=G\end{subarray}}(E^{\prime},G^{\prime}[M]),

\displaystyle T_{N/M}(E,G)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\#C=N/M\\
C\cap G=\{0\}\end{subarray}}(E/C,(G+C)/C)

In order to prove that these sums are equal, it is enough to find a bijection between the summands. We will now construct a map that sends
(E^{\prime},G^{\prime}[M])
to
(E/C,(G+C)/C)
(i.e. define
C
in terms of
E^{\prime}
and
G^{\prime}
) and prove that it is a bijection.

By definition, there is a map
f:E^{\prime}\to E
such that
\ker f=G^{\prime}[N/M]
. We set

C:=\ker f^{\vee}.

This means that for the map
f^{\vee}:E\to E^{\prime}
we have
E^{\prime}=E/C
. Further,

(G+C)/C=f^{\vee}(G)=f^{\vee}(f(G^{\prime}))=\frac{N}{M}G^{\prime}=G^{\prime}[M],

meaning that
G\cap C=\{0\}
(since
f^{\vee}(G)
is a group of order
M
) and
(E/C,(G+C)/C=(E^{\prime},G^{\prime}[M]))
.
To prove bijectivity, we define the inverse map, i.e. we define
E^{\prime}
and
G^{\prime}
in terms of
C
.

By definition, there is a map
g:E\to E/C
. We set

\displaystyle E^{\prime}

\displaystyle:=E/C,

\displaystyle G^{\prime}

\displaystyle:=(g^{\vee})^{-1}(G).

First, we need to prove that
G^{\prime}
is a cyclic subgroup of order
N
. It is obviously a group of order
N
. We have

\frac{N}{M}G^{\prime}=(g\circ g^{\vee})(g^{\vee})^{-1}(G)=g(G)=(G+C)/C.

Also,
\mathbb{Z}/\left(\frac{N}{M}\mathbb{Z}\right)\cong\ker g^{\vee}\leq G^{\prime}
so we can use
2.7
to conclude that
G^{\prime}
is a cyclic subgroup of order
N
. This further implies that
G^{\prime}[M]=\frac{N}{M}G^{\prime}=(G+C)/C
.

To prove that these two maps are inverse to each other it is enough to prove
g=f^{\vee}
. This holds because

\ker f=G^{\prime}[N/M]=(g^{\vee})^{-1}(G)[N/M]=M(g^{\vee})^{-1}(G)=(g^{\vee})^{-1}(MG)=(g^{\vee})^{-1}(0)=\ker g^{\vee}.

∎

Lemma 2.9
.

If
\frac{N}{M}
is square-free, then
\left<\iota_{N/M,N,M},\iota_{1,N,M}\right>=w_{M}\circ T_{N/M}\circ w_{M}
.

Proof.

We will prove the equivalent statement
w_{M}\circ\left<\iota_{N/M,N,M},\iota_{1,N,M}\right>=T_{N/M}\circ w_{M}
. Suppose that
(E,G)
represents a point on
Y_{0}(M)
. We compute (similarly as in the previous lemma)

\displaystyle\left<\iota_{N/M,N,M},\iota_{1,N,M}\right>(E,G)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\#G^{\prime}=N\\
G^{\prime}[M]=G\end{subarray}}(E/G^{\prime}[N/M],G^{\prime}/G^{\prime}[N/M])=\sum(E^{\prime},G^{\prime\prime}),

\displaystyle w_{M}\circ\left<\iota_{N/M,N,M},\iota_{1,N,M}\right>(E,G)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\#G^{\prime}=N\\
G^{\prime}[M]=G\end{subarray}}(E^{\prime}/G^{\prime\prime},E^{\prime}[M]/G^{\prime\prime}),

\displaystyle T_{N/M}\circ w_{M}(E,G)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\#H=N/M\\
(E[M]/G)\cap H=\{0\}\end{subarray}}(E/G/H,((E[M]/G)+H)/H).

It remains to prove that there is a bijection between the summands. We have the following situation:

E\mathop{\rightarrow}^{f_{1}}E^{\prime}\mathop{\rightarrow}^{f_{2}}E^{\prime}/G^{\prime\prime},

E\mathop{\rightarrow}^{g_{1}}E/G\mathop{\rightarrow}^{g_{2}}E/G/H

where we know that
G^{\prime}=\ker(f_{2}\circ f_{1})
because
\ker f_{2}=G^{\prime}/G^{\prime}[N/M]=G^{\prime}/\ker f_{1}
.

We can express
G^{\prime}
in terms of
H
as
G^{\prime}:=g_{1}^{-1}(H)=\ker(g_{2}\circ g_{1})
. By
2.7
, this is a cyclic group of order
N
because
\mathbb{Z}/(M\mathbb{Z})\cong G\leq G^{\prime}
and

MG^{\prime}=(g_{1}^{\vee}\circ g_{1})g_{1}^{-1}(H)=g_{1}^{\vee}(H)\cong H\cong\mathbb{Z}/\left(\frac{N}{M}\mathbb{Z}\right).

Here the third equality holds because
(E[M]/G)\cap H=\{0\}
. Now, since
G^{\prime}=g_{1}^{-1}(H)=\ker(g_{2}\circ g_{1})
, we get
f_{2}\circ f_{1}=g_{2}\circ g_{1}
. Further,
G=\ker g_{1}\subset G^{\prime}
implying that
G^{\prime}[M]=G
.

Let us now express
H
in terms of
G^{\prime}
. Since
G
is a subgroup of
G^{\prime}=\ker(f_{2}\circ f_{1})
, there exist isogenies
g_{1}
and
g_{2}
such that
g_{2}\circ g_{1}=f_{2}\circ f_{1}
and
G=\ker g_{1}
. We set
H:=\ker g_{2}
. It remains to prove that
(E[M]/G)\cap H=\{0\}
. This holds because

g_{2}(E[M]/G)=g_{2}(g_{1}(E[M]))=f_{2}(f_{1}(E[M]))=E[M]/(E[M]\cap G^{\prime})\cong E[M]/G.

∎

Remark 2.10
.

When
\frac{N}{M}
is not square-free, then the proof of
2.8
and
2.9
still work provided one replaces the Hecke operator

, which is defined as

C\textup{ cyclic}\\

Note that the only difference between

and

is that the sum in

is restricted to cyclic subgroups. If

is coprime to

, then

\left<\iota_{1,N,M},\iota_{N/M,N,M}\right>=T^{\prime}_{N/M}=\sum_{m^{2}\mid N/M}\mu(m)T_{N/(Mm^{2})},

\left<\iota_{N/M,N,M},\iota_{1,N,M}\right>=w_{M}\circ T^{\prime}_{N/M}\circ w_{M}=w_{M}\circ\sum_{m^{2}\mid N/M}\mu(m)T_{N/(Mm^{2})}\circ w_{M},

where

Proposition 2.11
.

Let
M,d_{1},d_{2}
be positive integers with
\gcd(d_{1},d_{2})=1
. Then

\displaystyle\iota_{1,d_{1}d_{2}M,d_{1}M,*}\circ\iota_{1,d_{1}d_{2}M,d_{2}M}^{*}

\displaystyle=\iota_{1,d_{1}M,M}^{*}\circ\iota_{1,d_{2}M,M,*}\text{\hskip 10.22217ptand}

\displaystyle\left<\iota_{d_{1},d_{1}d_{2}M,M},\iota_{d_{2},d_{1}d_{2}M,M}\right>

\displaystyle=\left<\iota_{d_{1},d_{1}M,M},\iota_{1,d_{1}M,M}\right>\circ\left<\iota_{1,d_{2}M,M},\iota_{d_{2},d_{2}M,M}\right>.

Proof.

Let
E
be an elliptic curve with a cyclic subgroup
G
of order
d_{2}M
. The first equality can be verified on a pair
(E,G)
since

\displaystyle\iota_{1,d_{1}d_{2}M,d_{1}M,*}\circ\iota_{1,d_{1}d_{2}M,d_{2}M}^{*}(E,G)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}H_{1}\supseteq G\textup{ cyclic}\\
\#H_{1}=d_{1}d_{2}M\end{subarray}}(E,H_{1}[d_{1}M])

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}H_{2}\supseteq G[M]\textup{ cyclic}\\
\#H_{2}=d_{1}M\end{subarray}}(E,H_{2})

\displaystyle=\iota_{1,d_{1}M,M}^{*}\circ\iota_{1,d_{2}M,M,*}(E,G).

Furthermore,
H_{1}
and
H_{2}
are related to each other via
H_{2}=H_{1}[d_{1}M]
and
H_{1}=H_{2}+G
.
The second equality follows from the first because

\displaystyle\left<\iota_{d_{1},d_{1}d_{2}M,M},\iota_{d_{2},d_{1}d_{2}M,M}\right>

\displaystyle=\iota_{d_{1},d_{1}d_{2}M,M,*}\circ\iota_{d_{2},d_{1}d_{2}M,M}^{*}

\displaystyle=\iota_{d_{1},d_{1}M,M,*}\circ\iota_{1,d_{1}d_{2}M,d_{1}M,*}\circ\iota_{1,d_{1}d_{2}M,d_{2}M}^{*}\circ\iota_{d_{2},d_{2}M,M}^{*}

\displaystyle=\iota_{d_{1},d_{1}M,M,*}\circ\iota_{1,d_{1}M,M}^{*}\circ\iota_{1,d_{2}M,M,*}\circ\iota_{d_{2},d_{2}M,M}^{*}

\displaystyle=\left<\iota_{d_{1},d_{1}M,M},\iota_{1,d_{1}M,M}\right>\circ\left<\iota_{1,d_{2}M,M},\iota_{d_{2},d_{2}M,M}\right>.

∎

Combining the previous results we get the following proposition.

Proposition 2.12
.

Assume that
\frac{N}{M}
is either squarefree or coprime to
M
and let
d_{1}
and
d_{2}
be divisors of
\frac{N}{M}
. We write just
\gcd
instead of
\gcd(d_{1},d_{2})
and
lcm
instead of
\textup{lcm}(d_{1},d_{2})
for simplicity. Then

\displaystyle\left<\iota_{d_{1},N,M},\iota_{d_{2},N,M}\right>=

\displaystyle\ w_{M}\circ\left(\sum_{m^{2}|d_{1}/\gcd}\mu(m)T_{d_{1}/(m^{2}\gcd)}\right)\circ w_{M}\circ

\displaystyle\circ\left(\sum_{m^{2}|d_{1}/\gcd}\mu(m)T_{d_{2}/(m^{2}\gcd)}\right)\circ[\deg\iota_{\gcd,N,M\textup{lcm}/\gcd}].

Proof.

Note that

\iota_{d_{1},N,M}=\iota_{d_{1}/\gcd,M\textup{lcm}/\gcd,M}\circ\iota_{\gcd,N,M\textup{lcm}/\gcd}

and similarly for
d_{2}
. This shows that
\iota_{d_{1},N,M}
and
\iota_{d_{2},N,M}
both factor through the map
\iota_{\gcd,N,M\textup{lcm}/\gcd}
allowing us to write

\displaystyle\left<\iota_{d_{1},N,M},\iota_{d_{2},N,M}\right>

\displaystyle=\iota_{d_{1}/\gcd,M\textup{lcm}/\gcd,M,*}\circ\iota_{\gcd,N,M\textup{lcm}/\gcd,*}\circ\iota_{\gcd,N,M\textup{lcm}/\gcd}^{*}\circ\iota_{d_{2}/\gcd,M\textup{lcm}/\gcd,M}^{*}

\displaystyle=\iota_{d_{1}/\gcd.M\textup{lcm}/\gcd,M,*}\circ[\deg\iota_{\gcd,N,M\textup{lcm}/\gcd}]\circ\iota_{d_{2}/\gcd,M\textup{lcm}/\gcd,M}^{*}

\displaystyle=\left<\iota_{d_{1}/\gcd,M\textup{lcm}/\gcd,M},\iota_{d_{2}/\gcd,M\textup{lcm}/\gcd,M}\right>\circ[\deg\iota_{\gcd,N,M\textup{lcm}/\gcd}].

Further, by
Proposition

2.11
we have

\left<\iota_{d_{1}/\gcd,M\textup{lcm}/\gcd,M},\iota_{d_{2}/\gcd,M\textup{lcm}/\gcd,M}\right>=\left<\iota_{d_{1}/\gcd,Md_{1}/\gcd,M},\iota_{1,Md_{1}/\gcd,M}\right>\circ\left<\iota_{1,Md_{2}/\gcd,M},\iota_{d_{2}/\gcd,Md_{2}/\gcd,M}\right>.

Now we get the desired result by applying
2.8
,
2.9
, and
Remark

2.10
.
∎

Theorem 2.13
.

Using the assumptions and notation of
2.12
, let
E
be an elliptic curve of conductor
M
with corresponding newform
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}a_{n}q^{n}
and let
f:X_{0}(M)\to E
be the modular parametrization of
E
. If we define

a=\left(\sum_{m^{2}\mid(d_{1}/\gcd)}\mu(m)a_{d_{1}/(\gcd m^{2})}\right)\left(\sum_{m^{2}\mid(d_{2}/\gcd)}\mu(m)a_{d_{2}/(\gcd m^{2})}\right),

where
\mu
is the Möbius function (when
\frac{d_{1}d_{2}}{\gcd^{2}}
is squarefree,
a
is equal to
a_{d_{1}d_{2}/\gcd^{2}}
, similarly as in
Remark

2.10
), then

\left<f\circ\iota_{d_{1},N,M},f\circ\iota_{d_{2},N,M}\right>=\left[a\cdot\frac{\psi\left(N\right)}{\psi\left(\frac{M\textup{lcm}}{\gcd}\right)}\cdot\deg f\right].

Here
\psi(N)=N\prod_{q\mid N}(1+\frac{1}{q})
, as in
5.3
.

Proof.

For the sake of simplicity, we will assume that
\frac{d_{1}d_{2}}{\gcd^{2}}
is squarefree. We have

\left<f\circ\iota_{d_{1},N,M},f\circ\iota_{d_{2},N,M}\right>=f_{*}\circ\iota_{d_{1},N,M,*}\circ\iota_{d_{2},N,M}^{*}\circ f^{*}=f_{*}\circ\left<\iota_{d_{1},N,M},\iota_{d_{2},N,M}\right>\circ f^{*}.

Let
E^{\prime}
be
f^{*}(E)\subset J_{0}(M)
. Then
E^{\prime}
is an elliptic curve isogenous to
E
. Since, up to isogeny,
E
occurs with multiplicity one in the factorization of
J_{0}(M)
(because
\textup{cond}(E)=M
), it follows that
\left<\iota_{d_{1},N,M},\iota_{d_{2},N,M}\right>
is a rational endomorphism of

. Therefore,
\left<\iota_{d_{1},N,M},\iota_{d_{2},N,M}\right>
is of the form
[k]
for some

and we get that

f_{*}\circ\left<\iota_{d_{1},N,M},\iota_{d_{2},N,M}\right>\circ f^{*}=f_{*}\circ f^{*}\circ\left<\iota_{d_{1},N,M},\iota_{d_{2},N,M}\right>=[\deg f]\circ\left<\iota_{d_{1},N,M},\iota_{d_{2},N,M}\right>.

2.12
now tells us that

(2)

\displaystyle\left<f\circ\iota_{d_{1},N,M},f\circ\iota_{d_{2},N,M}\right>=w_{M}\circ T_{d_{1}/\gcd}\circ w_{M}\circ T_{d_{2}/\gcd}\circ[\deg\iota_{\gcd,N,M\textup{lcm}/\gcd}]\circ[\deg f].

We see that here both the Atkin-Lehner involution

and Hecke operators act on

acts as

on

, the action of

cancels itself. Furthermore, the Hecke operators

act on

as multiplication by

(the coefficient in the corresponding newform). Therefore,

\displaystyle\left<f\circ\iota_{d_{1},N,M},f\circ\iota_{d_{2},N,M}\right>

\displaystyle=[a_{d_{1}/\gcd}]\circ[a_{d_{2}/\gcd}]\circ[\deg\iota_{\gcd,N,M\textup{lcm}/\gcd}]\circ[\deg f]

\displaystyle=[a_{d_{1}d_{2}/\gcd^{2}}\cdot\deg\iota_{\gcd,N,M\textup{lcm}/\gcd}\cdot\deg f].

The last equality holds due to the fact that

for relatively prime

. Finally, since the degrees of all degeneracy maps from

to

are equal to

, we get the desired formula.

If
\frac{d_{1}d_{2}}{\gcd^{2}}
is not squarefree, in
2
we will get the Möbius sums from

2.10
instead of

. We can then use the same argument to get the desired result since the sums

act on

as
\sum_{m^{2}\mid(d_{i}/\gcd)}\mu(m)a_{d_{i}/(\gcd m^{2})}
.
∎

and

3.
d
-elliptic modular curves

Definition 3.1
.

Let
d
be a positive integer. We call a curve
C
over a field
k

d
-elliptic if there exists an elliptic curve
E
over
k
and a morphism
C\to E
of degree
d
defined over
k
. If in addition
k
is a number field and
E
has positive Mordell-Weil rank, then we call
C
positive rank
d
-elliptic.

In this section, we will describe some ideas that allow one to determine for given integers
N
and
d
whether
X_{0}(N)
is
d
-elliptic over
\mathbb{Q}
.

If we fix a point
P\in X_{0}(N)(\mathbb{Q})
, then, as we have seen in
Section

2.1
, there exists an element of
\textup{Hom}_{\mathbb{Q}}(X_{0}(N),E)
of degree
d
if and only if there exists an element of
\textup{Hom}_{\mathbb{Q},P}(X_{0}(N),E)
of degree
d
. Furthermore, by the universal property of
J_{0}(N)
, every
f\in\textup{Hom}_{\mathbb{Q},P}(X_{0}(N),E)
factors uniquely through
J_{0}(N)
via the map
f_{P}
.

We define a map
\textup{Hom}_{\mathbb{Q}}(X_{0}(N),E)\to\textup{Hom}_{\mathbb{Q}}(J_{0}(N),E)
as follows:

f\mapsto t_{-f(P)}\circ f\mapsto\textup{homomorphism induced from }t_{-f(P)}\circ f\textup{ by the universal property of }J_{0}(N).

In this section, to make the text more readable, we sometimes use a slight abuse of notation. We will sometimes work with maps defined on
X_{0}(N)
as if they were defined on
J_{0}(N)
. For example, in the proof of
1.12
, we will say that the maps
f\circ d_{i}:X_{0}(N)\to E
form a basis for
\textup{Hom}_{\mathbb{Q}}(J_{0}(N),E)
, but this will actually hold for the images of
f\circ d_{i}
via the above map.

Definition 3.2
.

Let
A
and
B
be abelian varieties over a field
k
with
B
simple. An abelian variety
A^{\prime}
together with a quotient map
\pi:A\to A^{\prime}
is an optimal
B
-isogenous quotient if
A^{\prime}
is isogenous to
B^{n}
for some integer
n
and every morphism
A\to B^{\prime}
with
B^{\prime}
isogenous to
B^{m}
for some integer
m
uniquely factors via
\pi
.

Proposition 3.3
.

Optimal
B
-isogenous quotients exist, and are unique up to a unique isomorphism.

Proof.

By the Poincaré reducibility theorem (
[
34
, Chapter 10, Proposition 10.1]
or
[
5
, Theorem 5.3.7]
), there exists an integer
s
and simple abelian subvarieties
A_{1},\ldots,A_{s}
of
A
such that the sum map
A_{1}\times\cdots\times A_{s}\to A
is an isogeny. By reordering the
A_{i}
if necessary we can let
n\leq s
be the integer such that

are isogenous to

are not. Define

is an isogeny.

To show that the quotient

is an optimal

-isogenous quotient, let

be an abelian variety isogenous to

and let

be a morphism. Since

is isogenous to

but all the

for

are not isogenous to

, meaning that for

. However,

was obtained by quotienting out the

The uniqueness up to unique isomorphism follows formally because optimal
B
-isogenous quotients are defined using a universal property.
∎

The dual notion of optimal

Proposition 3.6

Maximal

-isogenous subvarieties exist, and are unique up to a unique isomorphism.

4.

In this section, we will prove that for levels

We will use two methods for obtaining quartic points on a curve

2
morphism to a curve

Proposition 4.1
.

Let
k
be a number field and let
d
be a positive integer. Suppose

and
C^{\prime}
are smooth, projective, and geometrically integral curves defined over
k
and let

has infinitely many points of degree
\frac{d}{d^{\prime}}
over

has infinitely many points of degree
\leq d
over

Proof.

Let

of degree
\frac{d}{d^{\prime}}
over

Therefore,

must be defined over some field

.

Proof of
1.12
.

The proofs of Propositions
4.5
,
4.7
and
Remark

4.6
tell us that
X_{0}(N)
is positive rank tetraelliptic for all levels
N
listed in
1.12
. Now we prove that for the other
N
the curve
X_{0}(N)
is not positive rank tetraelliptic. We only need to consider
N<408
not already eliminated in
5.5
for which there exists an elliptic curve of conductor
M\mid N
of positive
\mathbb{Q}
-rank. Further, if
M=N
, then any morphism from
X_{0}(N)
factors through the modular parametrization of a strong Weil curve in the corresponding isogeny class. However, the modular degree is strictly greater than
4
in all such cases. Therefore, we may suppose
M<N
.

Let
E
be a strong Weil curve of conductor
M\mid N
and positive
\mathbb{Q}
-rank,
n
the number of divisors of
N/M
, and
f:X_{0}(M)\to E
its modular parametrization. Since
\xi_{E,N}:J_{0}(N)\to E^{n}
for
N<408
is an
E
-isogenous optimal quotient by
3.9
, every map from
J_{0}(N)
to
E
uniquely factors through
E^{n}
. Therefore, we get that the maps
f\circ d_{i}
, where
d_{i}
runs over the degeneracy maps
X_{0}(N)\to X_{0}(M)
, form a basis for
\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathbb{Q}}(J_{0}(N),E)\cong\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathbb{Q}}(E^{n},E)\cong\mathbb{Z}^{n}
.
2.13
and
Remark

2.14
allow us to compute the degree pairing on this basis. Now, the degree of a map
\sum_{i\mid N/M}x_{i}(f\circ d_{i})
is given by a positive definite quadratic form

\sum_{i\mid N/M}\sum_{j\mid N/M}x_{i}x_{j}\left<f\circ d_{i},f\circ d_{j}\right>.

All
N
and strong Weil curves
E
which were considered in this proof are given in the
Table

2
.

Using the norm induced by this quadratic form, we can use the Fincke-Pohst algorithm for enumerating integer vectors of small norm
[
12
]
to determine that there are no nonconstant elements of
\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathbb{Q}}(J_{0}(N),E)
of degree
\leq 4
, and hence no elements of
\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathbb{Q}}(X_{0}(N),E)
of degree
\leq 4
. So this proves the statement for strong Weil curves.

If
E
is not a strong Weil curve, let
E^{\prime}
be a strong Weil curve in the isogeny class of
E
. Then by the
E
-isogenous optimality of
\xi_{E^{\prime},N}:J_{0}(N)\to(E^{\prime})^{n}
we have that any
g\in\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathbb{Q}}(J_{0}(N),E)
factors as
h\circ\xi_{E^{\prime},N}
for some
h\in\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathbb{Q}}((E^{\prime})^{n},E)
.

Also, the map

\pi:\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathbb{Q}}((E^{\prime})^{n},E)\to\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathbb{Q}}(E^{\prime},E)^{n},\ \pi(f)=(f\restriction_{E^{\prime}_{1}},\ldots,f\restriction_{E^{\prime}_{n}}),

where
E^{\prime}_{i}
is the
i
-th component of
(E^{\prime})^{n}
, is an isomorphism with an inverse map

\pi^{-1}((f_{1},\ldots,f_{n}))(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})=f_{1}(x_{1})+\ldots+f_{n}(x_{n}).

Furthermore, we have that
\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathbb{Q}}(E^{\prime},E)
is a free
\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathbb{Q}}(E^{\prime},E^{\prime})(\cong\mathbb{Z})
-module of rank
1
, generated by a single element
g_{2}
. In particular, any
f\in\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathbb{Q}}(E^{\prime},E)
can be written as
g_{2}\circ[m]
for some
m\in\mathbb{Z}
.

Therefore, we have
\pi(h)=(f_{1},\ldots,f_{n})=g_{2}\circ([m_{1}],\ldots,[m_{n}])
and
h(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})=g_{2}(m_{1}x_{1}+\ldots+m_{n}x_{n})
. This means that
h=g_{2}\circ m
for some
m\in\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathbb{Q}}((E^{\prime})^{n},E^{\prime})
. Returning back to our
g\in\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathbb{Q}}(J_{0}(N),E)
, we see that it factors as
g_{2}\circ m\circ\xi_{E^{\prime},N}
. It follows that

\deg g=\deg g_{2}\cdot\deg(m\circ\xi_{E^{\prime},N})\geq\deg(m\circ\xi_{E^{\prime},N})>4

since
E^{\prime}
is a strong Weil curve and
m\circ\xi_{E^{\prime},N}
is a rational map.
∎

In most cases, especially when we have only
2
degeneracy maps, we do not actually need the Fincke-Pohst algorithm to prove that there are no nonconstant elements of
\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathbb{Q}}(J_{0}(N),E)
of degree
\leq 4
. We show several examples where we prove that with elementary methods.

Example 1
.

We take
N=122
. There exist two elliptic curves
E
of positive
\mathbb{Q}
-rank and conductor
\textup{cond}(E)\mid N
. One of them has conductor equal to
N
and modular degree
8
and can therefore be eliminated. The other one is
E=X_{0}^{+}(61)
. Its modular parametrization
f
is the quotient map
X_{0}(61)\to X_{0}^{+}(61)
.

By the proof of
1.12
, the basis for
\textup{Hom}_{\mathbb{Q}}(J_{0}(122),E)
is
\{f\circ d_{1},f\circ d_{2}\}
and both of these maps have degree
2\cdot 3=6
. Further, by
2.13
, we have

\left<f\circ d_{1},f\circ d_{2}\right>=[a_{2}\cdot 1\cdot 2]=[-2].

This means that any map
J_{0}(122)\to E
must have degree equal to

6x^{2}-4xy+6y^{2}

for some integers
x,y
. It remains to prove that this expression can never be equal to
4
.

Let us suppose the contrary. If both
x
and
y
are not
0
, then
6x^{2}-4xy+6y^{2}=4x^{2}+2(x-y)^{2}+4y^{2}\geq 8
. Therefore, we may without loss of generality set
y=0
. However, the expression now becomes
6x^{2}
which cannot be equal to
4
, contradiction.

Example 2
.

We take
N=129
. There exist two elliptic curves
E
of positive
\mathbb{Q}
-rank and conductor
\textup{cond}(E)\mid N
. One of them has conductor equal to
N
and modular degree
8
and can therefore be eliminated. The other one is
E=X_{0}^{+}(43)
. Its modular parametrization
f
is the quotient map
X_{0}(43)\to X_{0}^{+}(43)
.

By the proof of
1.12
, the basis for
\textup{Hom}_{\mathbb{Q}}(J_{0}(129),E)
is
\{f\circ d_{1},f\circ d_{3}\}
and both of these maps have degree
2\cdot 4=8
. Further, by
2.13
, we have

\left<f\circ d_{1},f\circ d_{3}\right>=[a_{3}\cdot 1\cdot 2]=[-4].

This means that any map
J_{0}(129)\to E
must have degree equal to

8x^{2}-8xy+8y^{2}

for some integers
x,y
. This expression is divisible by
8
and cannot therefore be equal to
4
.

Example 3
.

We take
N=148
. There exist two elliptic curves
E
of positive
\mathbb{Q}
-rank and conductor
\textup{cond}(E)\mid N
. One of them has conductor equal to
N
and modular degree
12
and can therefore be eliminated. The other one is
E=X_{0}^{+}(37)
. Its modular parametrization
f
is the quotient map
X_{0}(37)\to X_{0}^{+}(37)
.

In this case
N/M
is not squarefree like in the previous two examples. However,
N/M
and
M
are coprime and we can still use
2.13
.

By the proof of
1.12
, the basis for
\textup{Hom}_{\mathbb{Q}}(J_{0}(148),E)
is
\{f\circ d_{1},f\circ d_{2},f\circ d_{4}\}
and these maps have degree
2\cdot 6=12
. Further, by
2.13
, we have

\left<f\circ d_{1},f\circ d_{2}\right>=[a_{2}\cdot 2\cdot 2]=[-8],

\left<f\circ d_{1},f\circ d_{4}\right>=[(a_{4}-a_{1}))\cdot 1\cdot 2]=[2],

\left<f\circ d_{2},f\circ d_{4}\right>=[a_{2}\cdot 2\cdot 2]=[-8].

This means that any map
J_{0}(148)\to E
must have degree equal to

12x^{2}+12y^{2}+12z^{2}-16xy+4xz-16yz

for some integers
x,y,z
. This expression is equal to

2(x+z-2y)^{2}+2(2x-y)^{2}+2(2z-y)^{2}+2x^{2}+2z^{2}.

Let us suppose that it is equal to
4
for some
x,y,z
. If both
x
and
z
are not
0
, then
2x^{2}+2z^{2}\geq 4
and the other terms must be equal to
0
. This would mean that
x+z-2y=2x-y=2z-y=0
. We can easily check that this is impossible.

Therefore, we may without loss of generality set
z=0
. The expression now becomes
12x^{2}-16xy+12y^{2}=8(x-y)^{2}+4x^{2}+4y^{2}
. As before, we see that
x
or
y
must be
0
(otherwise
4x^{2}+4y^{2}\geq 8
) and that
x=y
(otherwise
8(x-y)^{2}\geq 8
). This means that
x=y=z=0
and we get a contradiction.

.

.
