Theory of pressure acoustics with thermoviscous boundary layers and streaming in elastic cavities
Jonas Helboe Joergensen, Henrik Bruus

Abstract
We present an effective thermoviscous theory of acoustofluidics including pressure acoustics, thermoviscous boundary layers, and streaming for fluids embedded in elastic cavities. By including thermal fields, we thus extend the effective viscous theory by Bach and Bruus, J. Acoust. Soc. Am. 144, 766 (2018). ( Bach2018 ) The acoustic temperature field and the thermoviscous boundary layers are incorporated analytically as effective boundary conditions and time-averaged body forces on the thermoacoustic bulk fields. Because it avoids resolving the thin boundary layers, the effective model allows for numerical simulation of both thermoviscous acoustic and time-averaged fields in 3D models of acoustofluidic systems. We show how the acoustic streaming depends strongly on steady and oscillating thermal fields through the temperature dependency of the material parameters, in particular the viscosity and the compressibility, affecting both the boundary conditions and spawning additional body forces in the bulk. We also show how even small steady temperature gradients ( ∼ 1 ​ K / mm \sim 1\,\textrm{K}/\textrm{mm} ) induce gradients in compressibility and density that may result in very high streaming velocities ( ∼ 1 ​ mm / s \sim 1\,\textrm{mm}/\textrm{s} ) for moderate acoustic energy densities ( ∼ 100 ​ J / m 3 \sim 100\,\textrm{J}/\textrm{m}^{3} ).

II
Basic theory and model assumptions

We consider an acoustofluidic device consisting of an elastic solid containing a microchannel filled with a thermoviscous Newtonian fluid and actuated by an piezoelectric transducer at a single frequency in the
MHz
range. This time-harmonic actuation establishes an acoustic field in the system, which in the fluid, by the internal dissipation and hydrodynamic nonlinearities, results in a time-averaged response that leads to acoustic streaming.

II.2
Acoustic actuation and perturbation expansion

Following Ref.
Bach2018
, we actuate time-harmonically with angular frequency
\omega
by a displacement of a surface, so an element at equilibrium position
\bm{s}_{0}
, at time
t
will have the position
\bm{s}(\bm{s}_{0},t)=\bm{s}_{0}+\bm{s}_{1}(\bm{s}_{0})\mathrm{e}^{-{\mathrm{i}\omega t}}
. For models containing only a fluid, the displacement will be on the fluid boundary, whereas for models containing both a fluid and a solid domain, the actuation is on the solid boundary. For models including the piezoelectric transducer driving the system, the actuation parameter is the applied voltage.
Skov2019
However, this is not included in this work.

The acoustic response to the actuation parameter
\bm{s}_{1}
is linear, and the resulting fields will be complex fields
Q_{1}(\bm{r})\mathrm{e}^{-{\mathrm{i}\omega t}}
, the so-called first-order fields with subscript
"1"
. The non-linearity of the governing equation results in higher order responses to the actuation. We are only interested in the time-averaged second-order response and define
Q_{2}(\bm{r})=\big\langle Q_{2}(\bm{r},t)\big\rangle=\frac{\omega}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{\frac{2\pi}{\omega}}Q_{2}(\bm{r},t)\,\mathrm{d}t

\big\langle A_{1}B_{1}\big\rangle=\frac{1}{2}\re\big[A_{1}B_{1}^{*}\big]
, where the asterisk denote complex conjugation. Thus, a given field

in the model, such as density
\rho
, temperature
T

p

\bm{v}
, displacement
\bm{u}
, and stress
\bm{\sigma}
, is written as the sum of the unperturbed field, the acoustic response, and the time-averaged response,

Q(\bm{r},t)=Q_{0}(\bm{r})+Q_{1}(\bm{r})\,\mathrm{e}^{-{\mathrm{i}\omega t}}+Q_{2}(\bm{r}).

(8)

Similarly, through their dependency on temperature and density, all material parameters, such as thermal conductivity
k^{\mathrm{th}}
, compressibility
\kappa

\displaystyle\eta(\bm{r},t)

\displaystyle=\eta_{0}(T_{0})+\eta_{1}(T_{1},\rho_{1})\,\mathrm{e}^{-{\mathrm{i}\omega t}},

\displaystyle\eta_{1}(T_{1},\rho_{1})

\displaystyle=\Big(\frac{\partial\eta}{\partial T}\Big)_{T_{0}}T_{1}(\bm{r})+\Big(\frac{\partial\eta}{\partial\rho}\Big)_{T_{0}}\rho_{1}(\bm{r}).

(9b)

II.3
Separation of length scales

Acoustofluidic systems exhibits dynamics on two lengthscales, set by the acoustic wavelength and the thermoviscous boundary layer width. The boundary conditions on the temperature, heat flux, velocity, and stress at a fluid-solid interface result in the appearance of a thermal boundary layer (in fluids and solids) of width
\delta_{\mathrm{t}}
and in a viscous boundary layer (in fluids only) of width
\delta_{\mathrm{s}}
, localized near fluid-solid interfaces. Their dynamically-defined widths, jointly referred to as
\delta

d

\delta\ll d
,
(
Karlsen2015
)

\delta_{\mathrm{s}}=\sqrt{\frac{2\nu_{0}}{\omega}},\qquad\delta_{\mathrm{t}}=\sqrt{\frac{2D^{\mathrm{th}}_{0}}{(1-X)\omega}}\approx\sqrt{\frac{2D^{\mathrm{th}}_{0}}{\omega}},

(10)

where
X=0
for fluids and
X=(\gamma-1)\frac{4c^{2}_{\mathrm{tr}}}{3c^{2}_{\mathrm{lo}}}\lesssim 0.01
for solids,
\nu_{0}=\frac{\eta_{0}}{\rho_{0}}
, and
D^{\mathrm{th}}_{0}=\frac{k^{\mathrm{th}}_{0}}{\rho_{0}c_{p0}}
. Typically,
\delta_{\mathrm{t}}\lesssim\delta_{\mathrm{s}}\lesssim 500~\textrm{nm}
, which is more than two orders of magnitude smaller than
d\sim 100~\upmu\textrm{m}
. In this paper, the various fields are decomposed into a bulk field (

) and a boundary-layer field (
\delta
) that are connected by the boundary conditions. In Fig.
1

of the oscillating boundary.

II.5
Range of validity of the model

We briefly discuss the range of validity imposed by the main assumptions. Firstly, perturbation theory is valid when lower-order terms are much larger than and unaffected by higher-order terms, say,
\rho_{0}\gg\big|{\rho_{1}}\big|
and
\big|{\bm{v}_{1}}\big|\gg\big|{\bm{v}_{2}}\big|
, and when the latter can be neglected in the governing equations. For example, the zeroth-order heat equation (
18
) is only valid, if the time scale for advective heat transport
t_{\mathrm{adv}}=d_{t}/\big|{\bm{v}_{2}}\big|
is much longer than that of diffusion
t_{\mathrm{dif}}=d_{t}^{2}/D^{\mathrm{th}}_{0}
in a system with characteristic length
d_{t}
. For
d_{t}=1\,\textrm{mm}
this requires
\big|{\bm{v}_{2}}\big|\ll\frac{D^{\mathrm{th}}_{0}}{d_{t}}\approx 150\,\textrm{\textmu{}m}/\textrm{s}
.

Secondly, due to low oscillatory advection, we assume
\bm{\nabla}\cdot(q_{0}\bm{v}_{1})\approx q_{0}\bm{\nabla}\cdot\bm{v}_{1}
, where
q_{0}
is a parameter of the fluid. This requires
|q_{0}\bm{\nabla}\cdot\bm{v}_{1}|\gg|\bm{\nabla}q_{0}\cdot\bm{v}_{1}|
. By using the parameter
a_{q}
of Eq. (
7
), the validity of our theory is limited by,

\big|{\bm{\nabla}T_{0}}\big|\ll\bigg|\frac{k_{\mathrm{c}}}{a_{\eta}{\alpha_{p0}}}\bigg|\approx 5000~\frac{\textrm{K}}{\textrm{mm}}.

(17)

Here,
a_{\eta}
is used as the viscosity has the strongest temperature dependency. In conventional acoustofluidic systems
\big|{\bm{\nabla}T_{0}}\big|\lesssim 50~\textrm{K}/\textrm{mm}\ll 5000~\textrm{K}/\textrm{mm}
.

Thirdly, the effective boundary-layer theory requires the boundary-layer width to be much smaller than the bulk wavelength,
k_{0}\delta\ll 1
, see Section
II.3
, which is true for MHz acoustics in water.

\bm{v}_{0}=\bm{0}

IV.1
Acoustic equations and potential theory for fluids

The governing equations for the complex-valued acoustic field amplitudes in a fluid are given in Eq. (11) of Ref.

\displaystyle=\frac{\omega}{c_{0}}(1+\mathrm{i}\Gamma^{\mathrm{fl}}_{\mathrm{0c}}),

(21a)

\displaystyle\nabla^{2}T^{\delta}_{1}

\displaystyle=-k^{2}_{\mathrm{t}}T^{\delta}_{1},

\displaystyle k_{\mathrm{t}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1+\mathrm{i}}{\delta_{\mathrm{t}}}(1+\mathrm{i}\Gamma^{\mathrm{fl}}_{\mathrm{0t}}),

(21b)

\displaystyle\nabla^{2}\bm{v}^{\delta}_{1}

\displaystyle=-k^{2}_{\mathrm{s}}\bm{v}^{\delta}_{1},

\displaystyle k_{\mathrm{s}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1+\mathrm{i}}{\delta_{\mathrm{s}}}.

(21c)

Here,
\Gamma^{\mathrm{fl}}_{\mathrm{0c}}=\frac{1}{2}\big[\Gamma_{\mathrm{s}}+(\gamma-1)\Gamma_{\mathrm{t}}\big]
and

and

and

and temperature

are given by
p_{1}
,
\bm{v}^{\delta}_{1}
, and
T^{\delta}_{1}

\displaystyle\bm{v}_{1}

\displaystyle=\bm{v}^{d}_{1}+\bm{v}^{\delta}_{1}\qquad=

\displaystyle\bm{v}_{1}^{d,p}

\displaystyle\;+\bm{v}_{1}^{d,T}+\bm{v}^{\delta}_{1},

(22a)

\displaystyle\bm{v}_{1}^{d,p}

\displaystyle=\bm{\nabla}\big[-\mathrm{i}\mbox{$\frac{1-\mathrm{i}\Gamma_{\mathrm{s}}}{\omega\rho_{0}}$}\>p_{1}\big],\;\;

\displaystyle\bm{v}_{1}^{d,T}

\displaystyle=\bm{\nabla}\big[{\alpha_{p0}}D^{\mathrm{th}}_{0}T_{1}^{\delta}\big],

(22b)

\displaystyle T_{1}

\displaystyle=T^{d}_{1}+T^{\delta}_{1},\quad

\displaystyle T^{d}_{1}

\displaystyle=(\gamma-1)\frac{\kappa_{s0}}{{\alpha_{p0}}}\>p_{1}.

(22c)

(23)

IV.2
Acoustic equations and potential theory for solids

For a linear elastic isotropic solid with density
\rho_{0}
, longitudinal sound speed
c_{\mathrm{lo}}
, and transverse sound speed
c_{\mathrm{tr}}
, the governing equations is the linearized form of the momentum and heat equation (
1
) for the displacement field
\bm{u}_{1}
and the temperature
T_{1}
,
(
Karlsen2015
)

\displaystyle-\omega^{2}\rho_{0}\bm{u}_{1}=

\displaystyle-\frac{{\alpha_{p0}}}{\kappa_{T0}}\bm{\nabla}T_{1}

\displaystyle+(c^{2}_{\mathrm{lo}}-c^{2}_{\mathrm{tr}})\bm{\nabla}(\bm{\nabla}\cdot\bm{u}_{1})+c^{2}_{\mathrm{tr}}\nabla^{2}\bm{u}_{1},

(24a)

\displaystyle-\mathrm{i}\omega T_{1}-

\displaystyle\;\mathrm{i}\omega\frac{\gamma-1}{{\alpha_{p0}}}\>\bm{\nabla}\cdot\bm{u}_{1}=D^{\mathrm{th}}_{0}\nabla^{2}T_{1}.

(24b)

In analogy with the fluid, the governing equations for the solid are solved by potential theory, again following Ref.
Karlsen2015
. The displacement field is Helmholtz decomposed as
-\mathrm{i}\omega\bm{u}_{1}=\bm{\nabla}(\phi_{c}+\phi_{t})+\bm{\nabla}\times\bm{\Psi}=-\mathrm{i}\omega(\bm{u}^{\mathrm{lo}}_{1}+\bm{u}^{\mathrm{tr}}_{1})
, where
\phi_{c}
is the compressional potential,
\phi_{t}
is the thermal potential, and
\bm{\Psi}
is the shear vector potential, and where we have used
\bm{v}_{1}^{\mathrm{sl}}=-\mathrm{i}\omega\bm{u}_{1}

T_{1}=T^{d}_{1}+T^{\delta}_{1}=\frac{\mathrm{i}(\gamma-1)\omega}{{\alpha_{p0}}c_{0}^{2}}\phi_{c}+\frac{1}{\chi{\alpha_{p0}}D^{\mathrm{th}}_{0}}\phi_{t}

\phi_{c}

\phi_{t}

\bm{u}^{\mathrm{tr}}_{1}=\bm{\nabla}\times\bm{\Psi}
instead of
\bm{\Psi}
. The solution to Eq. (
24

\displaystyle\nabla^{2}\phi_{c}

\displaystyle=-k^{2}_{\mathrm{c}}\phi_{c},

\displaystyle\quad k_{\mathrm{c}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\omega}{c_{0}}(1+\mathrm{i}\Gamma^{\mathrm{sl}}_{\mathrm{0c}}),

(25a)

\displaystyle\nabla^{2}T^{\delta}_{1}

\displaystyle=-k^{2}_{\mathrm{t}}T^{\delta}_{1},

\displaystyle k_{\mathrm{t}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1+\mathrm{i}}{\delta_{\mathrm{t}}}(1+\mathrm{i}\Gamma^{\mathrm{sl}}_{\mathrm{0t}}),

(25b)

\displaystyle\nabla^{2}\bm{u}^{\mathrm{tr}}_{1}

\displaystyle=-k^{2}_{\mathrm{s}}\bm{u}^{\mathrm{tr}}_{1},

\displaystyle k_{\mathrm{s}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\omega}{c_{\mathrm{tr}}}.

(25c)

Here,

and

are given by Eq. (

. For a solid, only
T^{\delta}_{1}
is a dampened field confined to the boundary layer, whereas
\phi_{c}
and
\bm{u}^{\mathrm{tr}}_{1}
are bulk fields. The transverse waves in fluids and solids are qualitatively different:
\bm{v}^{\delta}_{1}
cannot propagate in a fluid and is restricted to the boundary layer, whereas
\bm{u}^{\mathrm{tr}}_{1}
can propagate in a solid and is not associated with a boundary layer.
The full displacement
\bm{u}_{1}
and temperature
T_{1}
are given by
\phi_{c}
,
\bm{u}^{\mathrm{tr}}_{1}
, and
T^{\delta}_{1}
as,

\displaystyle\bm{u}_{1}

\displaystyle=\bm{u}^{\mathrm{lo}}_{1}+\bm{u}^{\mathrm{tr}}_{1},\qquad

\displaystyle\bm{u}^{\mathrm{lo}}_{1}

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathrm{i}}{\omega}\>\bm{\nabla}\phi_{c},

(26a)

\displaystyle T_{1}

\displaystyle=T^{d}_{1}+T^{\delta}_{1},\qquad

\displaystyle T^{d}_{1}

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathrm{i}(\gamma-1)\omega}{{\alpha_{p0}}c_{0}^{2}}\phi_{c}.

(26b)

The explicit expression for the stress tensor
\bm{\sigma}^{\mathrm{xl}}_{1}
in the fluid (xl = fl) and in the solid (xl = sl) can be formulated jointly in potential theory as

Karlsen2015

\displaystyle\bm{\sigma}_{1}^{\mathrm{xl}}

\displaystyle=-p_{1}^{\mathrm{xl}}\mathbf{I}+\eta_{0}^{\mathrm{xl}}\big[(2k^{2}_{\mathrm{c}}-k^{2}_{\mathrm{s}})\phi_{\mathrm{c}}+(2k^{2}_{\mathrm{t}}-k^{2}_{\mathrm{s}})\phi_{\mathrm{t}}\big]\mathbf{I}

\displaystyle\quad+\eta_{0}^{\mathrm{xl}}\big[\bm{\nabla}\bm{v}_{1}^{\mathrm{sl}}+(\bm{\nabla}\bm{v}_{1}^{\mathrm{sl}})^{\dagger}\big],

(27)

where in the solid

The thermal boundary layer

\displaystyle=+\frac{1}{1+\tilde{Z}}\>\Delta T_{1}^{d0}(x,y)\>\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}k_{\mathrm{t}}^{\mathrm{sl}}z}.

(31b)

IV.5
The effective boundary condition for the velocity

Given the analytical solutions of the three boundary-layer fields, we only need to numerically solve the three bulk fields, namely
\phi_{c}
and
\bm{\Psi}
in the solid and
\phi_{c}
in the fluid, or equivalently, the displacement
\bm{u}_{1}
in the solid and the pressure
p_{1}
in the fluid. Therefore, we set two effective boundary conditions on these bulk fields using the analytical solutions for the boundary-layer fields: One effective boundary condition on the displacement
\bm{u}_{1}
in the solid derived from the condition on the stress, and another on the pressure in the fluid.

First, from the no-slip condition (
12a
), we derive the boundary condition for the first-order pressure field
p_{1}
, which takes the viscous and thermal boundary-layer effects into account through terms with
k_{\mathrm{s}}
,
k_{\mathrm{t}}
, and
T_{1}^{\delta 0}
. We express the compressional velocity
v^{d0,\mathrm{fl}}_{1,z}
on the fluid-solid interface through the no-slip condition (
33

\mathrm{i}k_{\mathrm{s}}v^{\delta 0,\mathrm{fl}}_{1,z}+\bm{\nabla}\cdot\bm{v}^{\delta 0,\mathrm{fl}}_{1}=0

z

, and finally introduce the bulk fields in the fluid,

\displaystyle v^{d0,\mathrm{fl}}_{1,z}

\displaystyle=v^{d0,\mathrm{sl}}_{1,z}-v^{\delta 0,\mathrm{fl}}_{1,z}=v^{d0,\mathrm{sl}}_{1,z}-\frac{\mathrm{i}}{k_{\mathrm{s}}}\bm{\nabla}\cdot\bm{v}^{\delta 0,\mathrm{fl}}_{1}

\displaystyle=v^{d0,\mathrm{sl}}_{1,z}-\frac{\mathrm{i}}{k_{\mathrm{s}}}\bm{\nabla}\cdot\Big[\bm{v}^{d0,\mathrm{sl}}_{1}-\bm{v}^{d0,\mathrm{fl}}_{1}\Big]

(34)

\displaystyle=\Big[v^{d0,\mathrm{sl}}_{1,z}\!-\!\frac{\mathrm{i}}{k_{\mathrm{s}}}\bm{\nabla}\!\cdot\!\bm{v}^{d0,\mathrm{sl}}_{1}\Big]+\frac{\mathrm{i}}{k_{\mathrm{s}}}\Big[\bm{\nabla}\!\cdot\!\bm{v}^{d,\mathrm{fl}}_{1}\!-\!\partial_{z}v^{d,\mathrm{fl}}_{1,z}\Big]_{z=0}.

Combining Eqs. (
19a
) and (
22c
), we obtain

\bm{\nabla}\cdot\bm{v}_{1}^{\mathrm{d}}=\mathrm{i}\frac{(1-\mathrm{i}\Gamma_{\mathrm{s}})k_{\mathrm{c}}^{2}}{\omega\rho_{0}}p_{1}-\mathrm{i}\omega{\alpha_{p0}}T_{1}^{\delta}.

(35a)

Then using Eq. (
22a
), we write
v^{d0,\mathrm{fl}}_{1,z}
and
\partial_{z}v^{d,\mathrm{fl}}_{1,z}
evaluated at the solid-fluid interface at
z=0
, and arrive at

\displaystyle v^{d0,\mathrm{fl}}_{1,z}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\mathrm{i}}{\omega\rho_{0}}(1-\mathrm{i}\Gamma_{\mathrm{s}})\partial_{z}p_{1}+{\alpha_{p0}}D^{\mathrm{th}}_{0}\partial_{z}T_{1}^{\delta},

(35b)

\displaystyle\partial_{z}v^{d,\mathrm{fl}}_{1,z}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\mathrm{i}}{\omega\rho_{0}}(1-\mathrm{i}\Gamma_{\mathrm{s}})\partial_{z}^{2}p_{1}+{\alpha_{p0}}D^{\mathrm{th}}_{0}\partial_{z}^{2}T_{1}^{\delta}.

(35c)

Inserting Eqs. (
35
) and (
12a
) into Eq. (
34
) leads to the final form of the effective boundary condition on
p_{1}
,

\displaystyle\partial_{z}p_{1}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{i}\frac{\omega\rho_{0}}{1-\mathrm{i}\Gamma_{\mathrm{s}}}\big(V_{1z}^{0}-\frac{\mathrm{i}}{k_{\mathrm{s}}}\bm{\nabla}\cdot\bm{V}_{1}^{0}\big)-\frac{\mathrm{i}}{k_{\mathrm{s}}}\big(k_{\mathrm{c}}^{2}+\partial_{z}^{2}\big)p_{1}

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{\mathrm{i}}{k_{\mathrm{t}}}\frac{{\alpha_{p0}}}{\kappa_{T0}}k^{2}_{0}\,T_{1}^{\delta 0},\;\text{ for }z=0.

(36a)

The first two terms on the right-hand were derived by Bach and Bruus,
(
Bach2018
)
whereas the last term is a new correction due to the thermal boundary layer. For
T_{1}^{d}\approx T_{1}^{\delta}
at
z=0
, this thermal correction is of the order
\frac{\gamma-1}{k_{\mathrm{t}}}\>k_{\mathrm{c}}^{2}\>p_{1}

IV.6
The effective boundary condition for the stress

Next, using the explicit expressions for
\bm{\sigma}_{1}^{\mathrm{sl}}
and
\bm{\sigma}_{1}^{\mathrm{fl}}
, we turn to the stress boundary condition (
13a
), the continuity of the stress
\bm{\sigma}_{1}
across the fluid-solid interface,
\bm{\sigma}_{1}^{\mathrm{sl}}\cdot\bm{e}_{z}=\bm{\sigma}_{1}^{\mathrm{fl}}\cdot\bm{e}_{z}
. For the fluid, we use that
k_{\mathrm{s}}\gg k_{\mathrm{c}}
,
\big|{\phi_{\mathrm{c}}}\big|\gg\big|{\phi_{\mathrm{t}}}\big|
, and
\big|{\partial_{z}\bm{v}_{1}^{\delta}}\big|\gg\big|{\nabla\bm{v}_{1}^{d}}\big|

(36b)

For the solid, we neglect in Eq. (
IV.2
) the derivative

along the surface, as it is a factor
\Gamma_{\mathrm{t}}
smaller than

Second order: Acoustic streaming

For the second-order fields in the fluid, we follow Eq. (

\displaystyle 0

VI
Numerical implementation and examples

We implement the effective thermoviscous model in the commercial finite-element software COMSOL Multiphysics.
(
Comsol56
)
It is validated by comparisons to full numerical simulations, and two examples of significant thermal effects in acoustofluidic devices are shown. All simulations are done in COMSOL 5.6
(
Comsol56
)
on a HP-G4 workstation with a processor Intel Core i9-7960X @

The effective thermoviscous model solver contains three steps: (1) the zeroth-order thermal field, (2) the acoustic pressure and displacement fields, and (3) the stationary streaming fields. The acoustic temperature field

is included analytically and therefore does not increase the numerical workload compared to the purely viscous model. The effective thermoviscous theory allows us to simulate acoustofluidic systems in 3D, which has prohibitive numerical costs for the full model.

In conclusion, the example highlights two important aspects: (1) The effective thermoviscous model enables 3D thermoviscous simulations in acoustofluidic systems, and (2) even moderate thermal gradients may create high streaming velocities in acoustofluidic systems. Such gradients can of course be created not only by heat generation in the transducer as in this example, but also more controllable by ohmic wires, Peltier elements, and external light sources. Notably, the validity of the perturbative approach breaks down at moderately high, but experimentally obtainable acoustic energy densities above
\sim 100~\textrm{J}/\textrm{m}^{3}
in combination with a moderate thermal gradient
\sim 1~\textrm{K}/\textrm{mm}
, and this calls for an extension beyond perturbation theory of the presented theory.

VII
Conclusion

We have derived an effective thermoviscous theory for a fluid embedded in an elastic solid. The steady zeroth order temperature field is governed by Eq. (
18
). The acoustic fields are governed by the Helmholtz equations (
21
) and (
25
), the decompositions (
22
) and (
26
), and the effective boundary conditions (
36
). The time-averaged acoustic streaming is governed by the effective Stokes equation (
54
) and the effective boundary conditions (
56
). The theory includes the thermoviscous boundary layers and the acoustic temperature field
T_{1}
analytically, and impose them as effective boundary conditions and time-averaged body forces on the thermoacoustic bulk fields.

The theory has been implemented in a numerical model, which because it avoids resolving numerically the boundary layers, allows for simulating both the first-order thermoviscous acoustic fields and second-order steady fields in 3D models of acoustofluidic systems. A conventional brute-force direct numerical simulations is very difficult, due to large memory requirements. In 2D, the model was validated by direct numerical simulations, and in 3D its self-consistency have been checked by mesh-convergence analyses.

We have applied the effective thermoviscous model in two numerical examples to demonstrate the importance of thermovisocus effects in microscale acoustofluidic devices. In particular, we have shown how the acoustic streaming depends strongly on the thermal fields: (1) The oscillating temperature field
T_{1}
impacts the streaming through the temperature dependency of the viscosity, causes corrections to the effective boundary condition, and spawns an additional body force in the bulk. In the 2D model of the square channel in Section
VI.1
and Fig.
2
, we have shown, how the thermoviscous effects are particularly important for the morphology and magnitude of the streaming in a rotating acoustic field. (2) The presence of an inhomogeneous stationary temperature field
T_{0}
affects the streaming through the induced gradients in compressibility and density. In the 3D model of the capillary glass tube in Section
VI.2
and Fig.
4
, we have shown, how the experimentally-observed characteristic horizontal streaming rolls in the standing acoustic resonance of Fig.
3
, are caused by heating from the actuation area. We have also shown, how very high streaming velocities (
\sim 1\,\textrm{mm}/\textrm{s}
) can be caused by small temperature gradients (
\sim 1\,\textrm{K}/\textrm{mm}
) for moderate acoustic energy densities (
\sim 100\,\textrm{J}/\textrm{m}^{3}
).

Our theoretical model enables 3D simulations of thermoviscous effects in microscale acoustofluidic devices. The results point to new ways for microscale handling of fluids and particles using a combination of acoustic and thermal fields. Although we have developed the effective thermoviscous theory within the narrow scope of microscale acoustofluidics, it is more general and may find wider use in other branches of thermoacoustics.
