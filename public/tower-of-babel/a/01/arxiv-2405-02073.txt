Iterative Reconstruction Methods for Cosmological X-Ray Tomography
Julianne Chung, Lucas Onisk, Yiran Wang

Abstract
We consider the imaging of cosmic strings by using Cosmic Microwave Background (CMB) data. Mathematically, we study the inversion of an X-ray transform in Lorentzian geometry, called the light ray transform. The inverse problem is highly ill-posed, with additional complexities of being large-scale and dynamic, with unknown parameters that represent multidimensional objects. This presents significant computational challenges for the numerical reconstruction of images that have high spatial and temporal resolution. In this paper, we begin with a microlocal stability analysis for inverting the light ray transform using the Landweber iteration. Next, we discretize the spatiotemporal object and light ray transform and consider iterative computational methods for solving the resulting inverse problem. We provide a numerical investigation and comparison of some advanced iterative methods for regularization including Tikhonov and sparsity-promoting regularizers for various example scalar functions with conormal type singularities.



keywords

cosmic microwave background, light ray transform, iterative methods, regularization, microlocal analysis, Landweber

Introduction

Our study is motivated by recent advances on the inverse problem of recovering gravitational anomalies such as cosmic strings from the Cosmic Microwave Background (CMB) radiation measurements. Since its discovery by Penzias and Wilson in 1965, the CMB has provided invaluable information regarding the early universe. Despite being highly isotropic, there are faint anisotropies in the CMB which have been mapped by sensitive detectors such as COBE, WMAP, and Planck Surveyor. It has been known for a long time
[
26
]
that on the first-order linearization level, the CMB anisotropy is connected to gravitational perturbations via a cosmological X-ray transform, also called the light ray transform.
Recently, the possibility of recovering such gravitational perturbations from the integral transform received lots of attention; see
[
20
,
21
,
29
,
31
,
32
]
. The mathematical studies are quite encouraging; however, research on the numerical inversion of the light ray transform remains preliminary. The primary goal of this work is to investigate the numerical inversion of the light ray transform, especially for recovering cosmic strings which are represented by singularities in the gravitational perturbations. We remark that the light ray transform naturally appears in other applications; see e.g. Section 5 of
[
21
]
.

We begin with a brief description of the inverse problem. Consider the Minkowski spacetime
({\mathbb{R}}^{n+1},g),n\geq 2
where in local coordinates
z=(t,x),t\in{\mathbb{R}},x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
,
g=-dt^{2}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}(dx^{i})^{2}
. We describe all null geodesics (or light-like geodesics) on
({\mathbb{R}}^{n+1},g)
as

\gamma_{x,v}(s)=(s,x+sv),\quad s\in{\mathbb{R}},x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n},v\in{\mathbb{S}}^{n-1}.

In particular, the set of null geodesics can be identified with
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\times{\mathbb{S}}^{n-1}
. The light ray transform for scalar functions can be defined as

Lf(x,v)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}f(s,x+sv)ds

(1)

where
f
is a function so that the integral makes sense. The inverse problem is to reconstruct
f
from
Lf
. See Figure
1
.

Figure 1:
Setup of the inverse problem. Each point at
t=t_{0}
plane is regarded as a light source. The light signal from
x
is recorded at
t=t_{1}
in the dashed circle. The light ray
\gamma
can be parametrized by
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n},v\in{\mathbb{S}}^{n-1}
. The inverse problem is to reconstruct
f
supported between
t=t_{0}
and
t=t_{1}
from
Lf
.

The integral transform
L
can be regarded as a Lorentzian analogue of the famous Radon transform which is the backbone of X-ray computed tomography; see for instance
[
23
]
. Perhaps the more precise analogue is the limited angle Radon transform, because only integrations along null geodesics are considered. Due to the limited available data, the inversion of
L
is severely ill-posed. As shown in several theoretical works
[
13
,
20
,
31
]
, not all information of
f
can be stably recovered, even though the transform is injective in some cases, see for instance
[
11
,
21
,
27
]
. Thus for numerical reconstruction, it is crucial to understand the mechanism of instability and find ways to improve it. Additionally, the discretized problem is highly ill-posed and regularization strategies have yet to be considered.

Overview of contributions

In this work, we develop numerical methods for inverting the light ray transform in relation to imaging cosmic strings in a two-dimensional universe over time from the CMB measurements. We investigate various iterative reconstruction methods and analyze the artefact phenomena. Our main contributions include the following.

•

In a functional regime, we analyze stability and instability of the Landweber iteration method for inverting the light ray transform and use microlocal analysis to investigate the artefact phenomena.

•

We discretize the linear inverse problem, formulating it as a large, sparse matrix equation and consider various regularization techniques for the reconstruction of cosmic strings in
\mathbb{R}^{2+1}
.

•

We provide a numerical comparison of various iterative methods, including iterative reconstruction methods like Landweber, iterative Krylov projection methods for Tikhonov regularization, and iterative shrinkage threshholding algorithms for
\ell_{1}
regularization. The computational methods that we use are state-of-the-art, but they are not new. For the tomography approach for the CMB inverse problems, these methods are only now becoming relevant.

An outline of the paper is as follows. In Sections
2
and
3
, we focus on the functional representation of the linearized CMB inverse problem. We describe the Landweber iteration and analyze its convergence behavior in an unstable regime. Then in Section
4
we describe the discrete inverse problem and describe various iterative methods that can be used for the reconstruction of cosmic strings. Several numerical examples are provided in Section
5
to illustrate the theory and demonstrate the potential benefits and challenges of incorporating different regularization terms. Conclusions and future work are provided in Section
6
.

2
The Landweber Iteration

We recall from Section 3 of
[
28
]
the Landweber’s iteration in the abstract setting. Let
A:H_{1}\rightarrow H_{2}
be a linear operator where
H_{1},H_{2}
are Hilbert spaces. For a given
m\in H_{2}
, we aim to solve the linear inverse problem
Af=m
for
f\in H_{1}
. It is well-known that Landweber iteration is a gradient descent method for the functional

f\rightarrow\|Af-m\|_{H_{2}}^{2}.

We can derive it from solving
Af=m
in the form

(\operatorname{Id}-(\operatorname{Id}-\gamma A^{*}A))f=\gamma A^{*}m

where
A^{*}
is the adjoint of
A
,
\operatorname{Id}
is the identity operator and
\gamma>0
is chosen properly. Let
K=\operatorname{Id}-\gamma A^{*}A
. If
K
is a strict contraction, then
f
can be solved by Neumann series,

f=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}K^{j}\gamma A^{*}m.

This implies the following Landweber iteration scheme:

\begin{gathered}f_{0}=0\text{ and }f_{k}=f_{k-1}-\gamma A^{*}(Af_{k-1}-m),\quad k=1,2,\ldots.\end{gathered}

(2)

Convergence of the iteration in the abstract setting was analyzed in Section 3 of
[
28
]
. For example, if the stability estimate

\mu\|f\|_{H_{1}}\leq\|Af\|_{H_{2}}

(3)

holds for some
\mu>0
, then
K
is a strict contraction for
\gamma\in(0,2/\|A\|^{2})
. In fact, the Neumann series converges uniformly. Even when
A
has a non-trivial kernel, convergence can be established under a stability assumption similar to (
3
), see Theorem 3.1 of
[
28
]
.

When applying the Landweber iteration to the light ray transform
L
, a serious issue is that (
3
) does not hold. For simplicity, let
{\mathcal{K}}
be a pre-compact set of
{\mathbb{R}}^{n+1}
and we consider
f
supported in
{\mathcal{K}}
. Then there is no stability estimate of the form

\|f\|_{H^{a}}\leq C\|Lf\|_{H^{b}},

for any
a,b\in{\mathbb{R}}
,
C>0
. Similar behavior is known for the limited angle Radon transform, see Section VI.2 of
[
23
]
. We remark that for
f\in L^{2}
with compact support,
Lf=0
implies
f=0
, see the discussion below. So the kernel of
L
is trivial and the instability is not caused by the kernel.

To further understand the issue, we discuss the injectivity and stability of the light ray transform. We will obtain a partial stability estimate and use it to show the convergence of the Landweber iteration.
We start from the injectivity proof of
L
, see for example
[
21
]
. Suppose
f\in C_{0}^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{n+1})
and
Lf(x,v)=0
for all
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n},v\in{\mathbb{S}}^{n-1}.
We denote the Fourier transform on
{\mathbb{R}}^{n+1}
by
{\mathscr{F}}
and the Fourier transform in the spatial variable by
\overline{{\mathscr{F}}}
. Taking the Fourier transform of (
1
) in the
x
variable, we get

\displaystyle\overline{{\mathscr{F}}}(Lf)(\xi,v)

\displaystyle=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{-\imath x\cdot\xi}f(t,x+tv)dtdx

(4)

\displaystyle=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{-\imath y\cdot\xi-\imath t(v\cdot\xi)}f(t,y)dtdy={\mathscr{F}}f(v\cdot\xi,\xi)

where
\imath^{2}=-1.

This is known as the
Fourier Slice Theorem
. Note that for
v\in{\mathbb{S}}^{n-1},\xi\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
,
(v\cdot\xi,\xi)
gives all space-like and light-like vectors on
({\mathbb{R}}^{n+1},g)
. Thus if
Lf=0
, we get
{\mathscr{F}}f=0
in the cone of space-like vectors. By analyticity of
{\mathscr{F}}f
, we conclude that
{\mathscr{F}}f=0
on
{\mathbb{R}}^{n+1}
hence
f=0.
Indeed, we see that the kernel of
L
acting on Schwartz functions
{\mathscr{S}}({\mathbb{R}}^{n+1})
consists of
f
whose Fourier transform vanishes on all space-like vectors.

The above argument is clearly not stable. However, there is some stable ingredient in the argument. We denote by
\Gamma^{sp}=\{(z,\zeta)\in T^{*}{\mathbb{R}}^{n+1}:\zeta=(\tau,\xi),\tau\in{\mathbb{R}},\xi\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n},|\xi|>|\tau|\}
the cone of space-like (co)vectors. For
\delta\geq 0
small, we let
\Gamma^{sp}_{\delta}=\{(z,\zeta)\in T^{*}{\mathbb{R}}^{n+1}:\zeta=(\tau,\xi),\tau\in{\mathbb{R}},\xi\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n},(1-\delta)|\xi|>|\tau|\}
so
\Gamma^{sp}_{\delta}\subset\Gamma^{sp}
. Let
\chi_{\delta}(\tau,\xi)
be the characteristic function of
\Gamma^{sp}_{\delta}
and let
\chi_{\delta}(D)={\mathscr{F}}^{-1}\chi_{\delta}{\mathscr{F}}
be defined as a Fourier multiplier. Below, we use the product measure to define the
L^{2}
space on
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\times{\mathbb{S}}^{n-1}.

Proposition 1
.

For any
f\in L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{n+1}),n\geq 2
, we have

\mu_{n}\|\chi_{\delta}(D)f\|_{L^{2}}\leq\|Lf\|_{H^{1/2}}.

(5)

where
\mu_{n}=C_{n}^{\frac{1}{2}}\delta^{(n-3)/4}>0
and
C_{n}=2\pi^{(n-1)/2}/\Gamma((n-1)/2)
is the surface area of
{\mathbb{S}}^{n-2},n\geq 2
.

Proof.

From (
4
), we get

\begin{gathered}\overline{{\mathscr{F}}}(Lf)(\xi,v)=\chi_{0}(v\cdot\xi,\xi){\mathscr{F}}f(v\cdot\xi,\xi)\end{gathered}

almost everywhere. By using the Plancherel theorem, we obtain that

\begin{gathered}\|Lf\|^{2}_{H^{1/2}}\geq\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\int_{{\mathbb{S}}^{n-1}}|\xi||\chi_{0}(v\cdot\xi,\xi){\mathscr{F}}f(v\cdot\xi,\xi)|^{2}dvd\xi.\end{gathered}

To compute the integral in
v
, without loss of generality, we can take
\xi=|\xi|(1,0,0)
and write
v=(\tau,\omega)
where
\tau\in[-1,1]
and
\omega(\sqrt{1-|\tau|^{2}})^{-1}\in{\mathbb{S}}^{n-2}
. Then we get

\displaystyle\|Lf\|^{2}_{H^{1/2}}

\displaystyle\geq\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\int_{{\mathbb{S}}^{n-2}}\int_{-1}^{1}|\xi||\chi_{0}(\tau|\xi|,\xi){\mathscr{F}}f(\tau|\xi|,\xi)|^{2}d\tau d\omega d\xi

\displaystyle=C_{n}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\int_{-1}^{1}(\sqrt{1-|\tau|^{2}})^{n-3}|\xi||\chi_{0}(\tau|\xi|,\xi){\mathscr{F}}f(\tau|\xi|,\xi)|^{2}d\tau d\xi

\displaystyle=C_{n}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\int_{-|\xi|}^{|\xi|}\frac{(|\xi|^{2}-|\tau|^{2})^{(n-3)/2}}{|\xi|^{n-3}}|\chi_{0}(\tau,\xi){\mathscr{F}}f(\tau,\xi)|^{2}d\tau d\xi

Using the support property of
\chi_{0},
we have

\displaystyle\|Lf\|^{2}_{H^{1/2}}

\displaystyle\geq C_{n}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\int_{-|\xi|}^{|\xi|}\frac{(|\xi|^{2}-|\tau|^{2})^{(n-3)/2}}{|\xi|^{n-3}}|\chi_{\delta}(\tau,\xi){\mathscr{F}}f(\tau,\xi)|^{2}d\tau d\xi

(6)

\displaystyle\geq C_{n}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\int_{-|\xi|}^{|\xi|}\frac{(\delta|\xi|^{2})^{(n-3)/2}}{|\xi|^{n-3}}|\chi_{\delta}(\tau,\xi){\mathscr{F}}f(\tau,\xi)|^{2}d\tau d\xi

\displaystyle\geq C_{n}\delta^{(n-3)/2}\|\chi_{\delta}(D)f\|^{2}_{L^{2}}

This completes the proof.

Based on the above results, we can set up the Landweber iteration to stably recover certain information of
f
. We aim to solve
Lf=m
for
f\in L^{2}
supported in
{\mathcal{K}}
. By the description of the kernel of
L
, we see that the equation is equivalent to
L\chi_{0}(D)f=m.
By the continuity of
L:L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{n+1})\rightarrow H^{1/2}({\mathbb{R}}^{n}\times{\mathbb{S}}^{n-1})
(see for example
[
31
]
), we know that the equation should be solved in
H^{1/2}
. As in
[
17
]
, it is convenient to further modify the equation to
L^{2}.
Let
L^{*}
be the
L^{2}
adjoint. We can solve
L^{*}L\chi_{0}(D)f=L^{*}m
in
H^{1}
. To get a stable reconstruction, we consider solving
\chi_{\delta}(D)L^{*}L\chi_{0}(D)f=\chi_{\delta}(D)L^{*}m
in
H^{1}
. Let
\Delta
be the Dirichlet realization of the positive Laplacian on
{\mathcal{K}}
. We can define the square root of
\Delta
. Then we solve

\Delta^{\frac{1}{2}}\chi_{\delta}(D)L^{*}L\chi_{0}(D)f=\Delta^{\frac{1}{2}}\chi_{\delta}(D)L^{*}m

(7)

in
L^{2}
by Landweber iteration.

Let
X=\Delta^{\frac{1}{2}}\chi_{\delta}(D)L^{*}L\chi_{\delta}(D)
. The corresponding Landweber iteration for solving (
7
) is

\begin{gathered}f_{0}=0\text{ and }f_{k}=f_{k-1}-\gamma X^{*}(Xf_{k-1}-\Delta^{\frac{1}{2}}L^{*}m),\quad k=1,2,\cdots.\end{gathered}

(8)

Note that
X:L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{n+1})\rightarrow L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{n+1})
is bounded. Also, from Proposition
1
, we have

\mu_{n}^{2}\|\chi_{\delta}(D)f\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\leq\|L\chi_{\delta}(D)f\|_{H^{1/2}}^{2}\leq\|Xf\|_{L^{2}}\|\chi_{\delta}(D)f\|_{L^{2}}.

Then
K=\operatorname{Id}-\gamma X^{*}X
is a strict contraction for
\gamma\in(0,2/\|X\|^{2})
, and we conclude that

\chi_{\delta}(D)f=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}K^{j}\gamma\Delta^{\frac{1}{2}}\chi_{\delta}(D)L^{*}m

converges uniformly. This means that
\chi_{\delta}(D)f
can be stably reconstructed from
Lf.

3
Analysis of the Instability

In this section, we analyze the behavior of the Landweber reconstruction in the unstable regime. We are particularly interested in the artefact phenomena. Note that the iteration (
8
) is basically for solving
Lf=m
in
H^{1/2}
. This is suitable for proving convergence of the scheme. However, as we will see soon, the iteration will produce stronger artefacts and it might be preferable to solve
Lf=m
in
L^{2}
to reduce the artefacts. We thus consider

\begin{gathered}f_{0}=0\text{ and }f_{k}=f_{k-1}-\gamma L^{*}(Lf_{k-1}-Lf),\quad k=1,2,\ldots.\end{gathered}

(9)

Let
N=L^{*}L
be the normal operator of
L
. It suffices to analyze the microlocal structure of
N
and powers of
N
. For example, the first few terms of the iteration (
9
) are

\begin{gathered}f_{0}=0,\quad f_{1}=\gamma Nf,\quad f_{2}=2\gamma Nf-\gamma^{2}N^{2}f.\end{gathered}

(10)

It is computed in
[
21
, Theorem 2.1]
that

Nf(t,x)=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n+1}}K_{N}(t,x,t^{\prime},x^{\prime})f(t^{\prime},x^{\prime})dt^{\prime}dx^{\prime}

(11)

where the Schwartz kernel is given by

K_{N}(t,x,t^{\prime},x^{\prime})=\frac{\delta(t-t^{\prime}-|x-x^{\prime}|)+\delta(t-t^{\prime}+|x-x^{\prime}|)}{|x-x^{\prime}|^{n-1}}.

(12)

In particular,
N
can be written as a Fourier multiplier

\begin{gathered}Nf(t,x)=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n+1}}e^{i(t\tau+x\cdot\xi)}k(\tau,\xi)\hat{f}(\tau,\xi)d\tau d\xi\end{gathered}

(13)

where

k(\tau,\xi)=C_{n}\frac{(|\xi|^{2}-\tau^{2})_{+}^{\frac{n-3}{2}}}{|\xi|^{n-2}},\quad C_{n}=2\pi|{\mathbb{S}}^{n-2}|.

(14)

Here, for
s\in{\mathbb{R}}
and
\operatorname{Re}a>-1
,
s_{+}^{a}
denotes the distribution defined by
s_{+}^{a}=s^{a}
if
s>0
and
s_{+}^{a}=0
if
s\leq 0
, see
[
19
, Section 3.2]
. Furthermore,

Nf(t,x)=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n+1}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n+1}}e^{i(t-t^{\prime},x-x^{\prime})\cdot(\tau,\xi)}k(\tau,\xi)f(t^{\prime},x^{\prime})d\tau d\xi dt^{\prime}dx^{\prime}.

(15)

Note that the amplitude
k
has conormal type singularities at
\tau^{2}=|\xi|^{2}
. So the operator is not a standard pseudo-differential operator. When restricting
k(\tau,\xi)
to
\Gamma^{sp}
, we indeed get an elliptic pseudo-differential operator which is the main theme in
[
20
,
21
]
. In particular, this means that one can recover space-like singularities in
f
from
Nf
. For time-like directions,
k(\tau,\xi)=0
thus time-like singularities of
f
are lost in
Nf
. For the borderline case of light-like singularities, we need the microlocal structure of
N
in
[
31
]
that the Schwartz kernel is a paired Lagrangian distribution. Such distributions were introduced in
[
14
,
22
]
. We briefly recall the relevant result for understanding
N.

Let
{\mathcal{X}}
be a
C^{\infty}
manifold of dimension
n
and
w_{\mathcal{X}}
be the simplectic form on
T^{*}{\mathcal{X}}
. Let
\Lambda_{0},\Lambda_{1}
be conic Lagrangian submanifolds of
T^{*}({\mathcal{X}}\times{\mathcal{X}})\backslash 0
with symplectic form
\pi_{1}^{*}w_{\mathcal{X}}+\pi_{2}^{*}w_{\mathcal{X}}
. Here,
\pi_{1},\pi_{2}:{\mathcal{X}}\times{\mathcal{X}}\rightarrow{\mathcal{X}}
denotes the projections to the first, second copy of
{\mathcal{X}}
. Suppose that
\Lambda_{1}
intersects
\Lambda_{0}
cleanly at a codimension
k
,
1\leq k\leq 2n-1
submanifold
\Sigma=\Lambda_{0}\cap\Lambda_{1}
, namely

T_{p}(\Lambda_{0}\cap\Lambda_{1})=T_{p}(\Lambda_{0})\cap T_{p}(\Lambda),\quad\forall p\in\Sigma.

It is proved in Theorem 3.1 of
[
31
]
that the Schwartz kernel of the normal operator
K_{N}\in I^{-n/2,n/2-1}({\mathbb{R}}^{n+1}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{n+1};\Lambda_{0},\Lambda_{1})
, in which
\Lambda_{0},\Lambda_{1}
are two cleanly intersecting Lagrangians defined as follows:

\begin{gathered}\Lambda_{0}=\{(t,x,\tau,\xi;t^{\prime},x^{\prime},\tau^{\prime},\xi^{\prime})\in T^{*}{\mathbb{R}}^{n+1}\backslash 0\times T^{*}{\mathbb{R}}^{n+1}\backslash 0:\\
t^{\prime}=t,x^{\prime}=x,\tau^{\prime}=-\tau,\xi^{\prime}=-\xi\}\end{gathered}

(16)

which is the punctured conormal bundle of the diagonal in
{\mathbb{R}}^{n+1}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{n+1}
and

\begin{gathered}\Lambda_{1}=\{(t,x,\tau,\xi;t^{\prime},x^{\prime},\tau^{\prime},\xi^{\prime})\in T^{*}{\mathbb{R}}^{n+1}\backslash 0\times T^{*}{\mathbb{R}}^{n+1}\backslash 0:x=x^{\prime}+(t-t^{\prime})\xi/|\xi|,\\
\tau=\pm|\xi|,\tau^{\prime}=-\tau,\xi^{\prime}=-\xi\}.\end{gathered}

(17)

To analyze the powers of
N
, we use the fact that
(\Lambda_{0},\Lambda_{1})
is the flow-out model, see
[
31
]
. We recall that a submanifold
\Gamma\subseteq T^{*}{\mathcal{X}}
is involutive if
\Gamma=\{(x,\xi):p_{i}(x,\xi)=0,i=1,2,\cdots,k\}
satisfies (i)
p_{i}
are defining functions of
\Gamma
that
dp\neq 0
on
p=0
, and (ii)
p_{i}
are in involution so the Poisson brackets
\{p_{i},p_{j}\}=0
at
\Gamma
. Let
H_{p_{i}}
be the Hamilton vector fields of
p_{i}
. The flow out of
\Gamma

\begin{gathered}\Lambda_{\Gamma}=\{(x,\xi,y,\eta)\in T^{*}{\mathcal{X}}\times T^{*}{\mathcal{X}}:(x,\xi)\in\Gamma,\\
(y,\eta)=\exp(\sum_{j=1}^{k}t_{j}H_{p_{j}})(x,\xi),t_{j}\in{\mathbb{R}}\}\end{gathered}

(18)

is a Lagranian submanifold of
T^{*}({\mathcal{X}}\times{\mathcal{X}})
and is a canonical relation if
\Gamma
is conic. Write
\Lambda_{1}=\Lambda_{\Gamma}
. In this case, we have the following result.

Theorem 2
(Theorem 1 of
[
1
]
).

Let
A\in I^{p,l}({\mathcal{X}}\times{\mathcal{X}};\Lambda_{0},\Lambda_{1})
and
B\in I^{r,s}({\mathcal{X}}\times{\mathcal{X}};\Lambda_{0},\Lambda_{1})
be properly supported. Then
A\circ B\in I^{p+r+k/2,l+s-k/2}({\mathcal{X}}\times{\mathcal{X}};\Lambda_{0},\Lambda_{1})
.

Now consider the two Lagrangians
\Lambda_{0}
and
\Lambda_{1}
in (
16
) and (
17
) respectively. Let
f(\tau,\xi)=\frac{1}{2}(\tau^{2}-|\xi|^{2})
and
\Sigma=\{(t,x,\tau,\xi;t,x,-\tau,-\xi)\in T^{*}{\mathbb{R}}^{n+1}\backslash 0\times T^{*}{\mathbb{R}}^{n+1}\backslash 0:f(\tau,\xi)=0\}
. Then
\Lambda_{1}
is the flow out of
\Sigma
under the Hamilton vector field
H_{f}
. Indeed, we have

H_{f}=\tau\frac{\partial}{\partial t}-\sum_{i=1}^{3}\xi_{i}\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{i}}.

Let
\gamma(s)=(t(s),x(s),\tau(s),\xi(s))
be a null bi-characteristic which satisfies

\begin{gathered}\dot{t}(s)=\tau,\quad\dot{x}_{i}(s)=-\xi_{i},\quad\dot{\tau}(s)=0,\quad\dot{\xi}_{i}(s)=0\quad s\geq 0\\
t(0)=t^{\prime},\quad x(0)=x^{\prime},\quad\tau(0)=\tau^{\prime},\quad\xi(0)=\xi^{\prime}\end{gathered}

with
f(\tau^{\prime},\xi^{\prime})=0.
We solve

t(s)=t^{\prime}+s\tau^{\prime},\quad x(s)=x^{\prime}-s\xi^{\prime},\quad\tau(s)=\tau^{\prime},\quad\xi(s)=\xi^{\prime},

which up to a re-parametrization gives (
17
). Now we can use Theorem
2
to conclude that for
j\in{\mathbb{N}}
,
N^{j}\in I^{-nj/2+(j-1)/2,nj/2-(j-1)/2}({\mathbb{R}}^{n+1}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{n+1};\Lambda_{0},\Lambda_{1})
. So all the terms
N^{j},j=1,2,\ldots
have similar microlocal structures but the orders are different.

It is known that for a paired Lagrangian distribution
N^{j}
, the wave front set
\text{WF}(N^{j})\subset\Lambda_{0}\cup\Lambda_{1}
. From wave front analysis see e.g.
[
19
]
, we observe that the wave front set on
\Lambda_{0}
does not produce new singularities in
N^{j}f
. However, the part on
\Lambda_{1}
does produce new singularities which correspond to the artefacts in the numerical reconstruction. In fact, away from
\Lambda_{0}
, we know that
N^{j}\in I^{-(n-1)j/2-1/2}({\mathbb{R}}^{n+1}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{n+1};\Lambda_{1})
is a Lagrangian distribution. By the definition of
\Lambda_{1}
and wave front analysis, we see that only light-like singularities are affected by
\Lambda_{1}
, and the new singularities (or artefacts) would appear in the flow-out under
\Lambda_{1}
. The analysis so far implies that in the iteration scheme (
9
), one can reconstruct space-like singularities of
f
but not time-like singularities, and there might be artefacts from light-like singularities of
f
.

Below, we give a more precise statement about the strength of the artefacts. As in
[
24
]
, we describe them using the notion of microlocal Sobolev regularity. We recall from Definition 2.2 of
[
24
]
that a distribution
f
is in
H^{s}
microlocally near
(z_{0},\zeta_{0})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n+1}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{n+1}\backslash 0
if and only if there exists a cut-off function
\mathbf{\varphi}\in C_{0}^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{n+1})
with
\mathbf{\varphi}(z_{0})\neq 0
and
u(\zeta)
homogeneous of degree zero and smooth on
{\mathbb{R}}^{n+1}\backslash 0
with
u(\zeta_{0})\neq 0
such that
(1+|\zeta|^{2})^{s/2}u(\zeta){\mathscr{F}}(\mathbf{\varphi}f)(\zeta)\in L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{n+1})
. It follows from the definition that if
(z_{0},\zeta_{0})\notin\text{WF}(f)
, then
f
is
H^{s}
microlocally near
(z_{0},\zeta_{0})
for all
s\in{\mathbb{R}}.

Theorem 3
.

Suppose
f
is
H^{s}
microlocally near
(z,\zeta)=(t,x,\tau,\xi)
with
\tau^{2}=|\xi|^{2}
.
Then for
j\in{\mathbb{N}}
,
N^{j}f
is
H^{s+(n-1)j/2}
microlocally near
(z^{\prime},\zeta^{\prime})
where
z^{\prime}=(t^{\prime},x-(t^{\prime}-t)\xi/|\xi|)
and
\zeta=(\tau,\xi)
.

Proof.

Let
C_{1}=\Lambda_{1}^{\prime}
be the canonical relation. We can verify that the differential of the projection
C_{1}\rightarrow T^{*}{\mathbb{R}}^{n+1}
has rank
2n+1
. Thus applying Theorem 4.3.2 of
[
18
]
, we get that
N^{j}:L^{2}_{\operatorname{comp}}({\mathbb{R}}^{n+1})\rightarrow H^{(n-1)j/2}_{\text{loc}}({\mathbb{R}}^{n+1})
. Then we can follow the arguments in Theorem 3.1 of
[
24
]
to get the microlocal result.

We note that as
j
increases, the produced artefacts are more regular in Sobolev regularity. For the iteration scheme (
8
), the corresponding artefacts are actually more singular after each iteration due to the
\Delta^{\frac{1}{2}}
terms. This is why we prefer the iterative scheme (
9
).

Finally, we use one example to demonstrate all the possible phenomena in the reconstruction based on the analysis. Consider
n=2
and let
f
be the characteristic function of the unit ball in
{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
, that is for
t,x,y\in{\mathbb{R}}
,

f(t,x,y)=1,\quad\text{ if }t^{2}+x^{2}+y^{2}<1

and otherwise
f=0.
In Figure
2
, we show the projection of
f
to the
t,x
-plane. The function has conormal type singularities at the unit sphere. We can identify the part of the boundary that can be reconstructed stably and the possible artefacts.

4
Computational Approaches for the Inverse Problem

We turn our attention to computational approaches for solving the linear inverse problem in a discrete setting. We begin in Section
4.1
with the forward problem, describing how the space-time is discretized and how the forward light ray transform model can be represented using a large, sparse matrix. Section
4.2
then provides an overview of iterative methods that can be used for string recovery. We consider the discrete Landweber method as an iterative regularization strategy, as well as variational regularization approaches, namely Tikhonov and
\ell_{1}
regularization. Numerical investigations are provided in Section
5
.

4.1
Discrete Forward Problem

The discrete inverse problem can be stated as follows. We consider a
2+1
spatial-temporal region, where space has been discretized using equally spaced
xy
-spatial grids and time has been discretized using
T
time points. We denote
\textbf{X}(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
to be the unknown (discretized) image plane at time point
t
where
t=1,2,\dots,T
. Upon vectorizing each spatial slice columnwise so that

\text{vec}\Big(\mathbf{X}(t)\Big)=\mathbf{x}(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{n^{2}},

the vector of unknowns is given by

\mathbf{x}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{x}(1)\\
\mathbf{x}(2)\\
\vdots\\
\mathbf{x}(T)\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{n^{2}T}.

Assume there are
N
source grid locations from which a light-cone emanates. For each source location
s_{i},i=1,2,\ldots,N
, there are
m_{i}
observations that are collected in vector

\mathbf{b}_{i}=\mathbf{A}_{i}\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{e}_{i}

(19)

where
\mathbf{b}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{m_{i}}
and
\mathbf{A}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{m_{i}\times n^{2}T}
with noise contamination
\mathbf{e}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{m_{i}}
. The values
m_{i}
correspond to the number of observable detector grid locations such that light-rays from source point
s_{i}
have a non-zero interaction with the spatial-temporal object.

Equation (
19
) models the linearized CMB problem outlined in Section
1
. Here, the rows of
\mathbf{A}_{i}
contain the weights of the intersection of the light-rays through the unknown spatial slices
\mathbf{X}(t)
corresponding to source point
s_{i}
. The weights are obtained through bilinear interpolation. More specifically, for each ray connecting the source
s_{i}
with one detector, we find the point of intersection of the ray with the plane
\mathbf{X}(t)
and identify the four nearest pixels
x_{i,j},\,x_{i+1,j},\,x_{i,j+1},
and
x_{i+1,j+1}
. Then the bilinear interpolation weights
w_{i,j},\,w_{i+1,j},\,w_{i,j+1},
and
w_{i+1,j+1}
are computed and placed in the corresponding columns of matrix
\mathbf{A}_{i}(t)
. See Figure
3
for an illustration.
Since the observations may contain contributions from multiple event planes, let
\mathbf{A}_{i}(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{m_{i}\times n^{2}}
denote the ray-trace matrix that represents contributions from the plane
\mathbf{X}(t)
when the source is located at
s_{i}
. Then we have for source point
s_{i},

\mathbf{A}_{i}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{A}_{i}(1)&\mathbf{A}_{i}(2)&\cdots&\mathbf{A}_{i}(T)\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{m_{i}\times n^{2}T}.

Figure 3:
Discrete problem setup. On the left is an illustration of the geometry of the discrete CMB problem, with the object of interest located between the source grid at the bottom and the detector grid on the top. For each source location
s_{i}
, detectors (determined by a cone of 45 degrees) receive contributions from event planes
X(t),t=1,\ldots,T
. The weights of contribution from
X(t)
are determined via bilinear interpolation, as shown in the right figure, where the red cross denotes the ray’s intersection with
X(t)
.

If we consider all
N
source grid locations, we obtain the following linear inverse problem,

\displaystyle\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{b}_{1}\\
\mathbf{b}_{2}\\
\vdots\\
\mathbf{b}_{N}\end{bmatrix}}_{\mathbf{b}}

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{A}_{1}(1)&\mathbf{A}_{1}(2)&\cdots&\mathbf{A}_{1}(T)\\
\mathbf{A}_{2}(1)&\mathbf{A}_{2}(2)&\cdots&\mathbf{A}_{2}(T)\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\mathbf{A}_{N}(1)&\mathbf{A}_{N}(2)&\cdots&\mathbf{A}_{N}(T)\end{bmatrix}}_{\mathbf{A}}\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{x}(1)\\
\mathbf{x}(2)\\
\vdots\\
\mathbf{x}(T)\end{bmatrix}}_{\mathbf{x}}+\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{e}_{1}\\
\mathbf{e}_{2}\\
\vdots\\
\mathbf{e}_{N}\end{bmatrix}}_{\mathbf{e}}

(20)

where
\mathbf{b}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
and
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n^{2}T}

with
m=\sum_{i=1}^{N}m_{i}
. As every
\mathbf{A}_{i}(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{m_{i}\times n^{2}}
has at most
4
column entries per row, we are able to construct and store
\mathbf{A}
as a sparse matrix; this enhances the efficiency of computing matrix-vector multiplications with
\mathbf{A}
and
\mathbf{A}^{\top}
.
Next we describe iterative methods to estimate
\mathbf{x}
, given
\mathbf{b}
and
\mathbf{A}
.

4.2
Iterative Methods and Regularization

Consider an inverse problem where noisy data is given by
\mathbf{b}=\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}_{\rm true}+\mathbf{e}
for some
\mathbf{x}_{\rm true}\in\mathbb{R}^{n^{2}T}.
We consider the least-squares (LS) problem,

\min_{\mathbf{x}}\|\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{b}\|^{2},

(21)

where unless specified otherwise,
\|\cdot\|
will denote the Euclidean norm. Since
\bf{b}
is contaminated by error and
\mathbf{A}
is ill-conditioned, the solution of (
21
) is usually a poor approximation of
\mathbf{x}_{\rm true}
due to the propagation of error into the computed solution.
However, a regularized solution can be obtained by applying an iterative method to (
21
) and terminating the iterations before the propagation of noise occurs.

One such method is the discrete Landweber method whose iterates are given by

\mathbf{x}^{(k+1)}=\mathbf{x}^{(k)}+\omega\,\mathbf{A}^{T}\mathbf{r}^{(k)},

(22)

where
\omega\in(0,2/\sigma_{1}^{2})
is a relaxation term with
\sigma_{1}
the largest singular value of
\mathbf{A}
. In the absence of noise and in exact arithmetic, we expect the errors to be nonincreasing, i.e.,
\|\mathbf{x}_{\rm true}-\mathbf{x}^{(k+1)}\|\leq\|\mathbf{x}_{\rm true}-\mathbf{x}^{(k)}\|
. However, due to the contaminating error in (
20
), the Landweber method can exhibit
semiconvergence
behavior, which manifests visually in the plot of relative reconstruction error norms per iteration. That is, the error norms decay in early iterations and are followed by a continual monotonic increase. As such, a good termination criterion is needed for the Landweber method. For the numerical experiments, we used a Landweber implementation from the software package AIR Tools II
[
16
]
where the default relaxation parameter
\omega=1.9/\sigma_{1}^{2}
was used.

Another approach to reduce semiconvergent behavior is to improve the conditioning of the problem by incorporating prior knowledge in the form of regularization. In variational form, the regularized problem may be given by

\min_{\mathbf{x}}\|\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{b}\|^{2}+\lambda\,R(\mathbf{x})

(23)

where
R
is a regularization term and
\lambda>0
is a regularization parameter that balances the trade-off between the data fit and regularization terms. In standard form Tikhonov regularization,
R(\bf{x})
is replaced with an
\ell_{2}
penalization term so that the penalized LS solution is given by

\mathbf{x}_{\lambda}=\argmin_{\mathbf{x}}\left\|\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{b}\right\|^{2}+\lambda\left\|\bf{x}\right\|^{2}.

(24)

For large-scale problems, iterative methods can be used to compute approximations to (
24
), where at the
k
th iteration, a solution is sought in a
k
dimensional Krylov subspace. That is, the
k^{th}
iterate is given by

\mathbf{x}_{\lambda}^{(k)}=\argmin_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{V}_{k})}\left\|\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{b}\right\|^{2}+\lambda\left\|\bf{x}\right\|^{2},

(25)

where
\mathcal{R}(\cdot)
denotes the range of a given matrix and
\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{V}_{k})=\mathcal{K}_{k}(\mathbf{A}^{T}\mathbf{A},\mathbf{A}^{T}\mathbf{b})
when the starting guess,
\mathbf{x}^{(0)}
, is the zero vector; see
[
5
,
6
]
for further exposition. We note that
\mathbf{x}^{(k)}\rightarrow\mathbf{x}_{\lambda}
as
k\rightarrow\min\{m,n\}
for
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
. For problems where the regularization parameter
\lambda
is not known in advance, hybrid projection methods can be used, where the regularization parameter is estimated during the iterative process, resulting in a sequence of projected problems of the form (
25
) with a different regularization parameter at each iteration,
\lambda^{(k)}
. See
[
5
]
for a survey on hybrid projection methods.

An alternative choice of
R
in (
23
) is
\ell_{1}
regularization,

\min_{\mathbf{x}}\left\|\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{b}\right\|^{2}+\lambda\left\|\bf{x}\right\|_{1}.

(26)

The choice of
\ell_{1}
regularization over
\ell_{2}
may stem from a desire to promote sparsity in solutions by reducing the sensitivity to outliers. A popular strategy to solve (
26
) is the iterative shrinkage-thresholding algorithm (ISTA) whose general iterative process is given by

\mathbf{x}^{(k+1)}=\Psi_{\lambda}\left(\mathbf{x}^{(k)}+2t\mathbf{A}^{T}\mathbf{r}^{(k)}\right)

(27)

where
t
is an appropriate step size,
\mathbf{r}^{(k)}=\mathbf{b}-\mathbf{Ax}^{(k)}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
denotes the residual at the
k
th iteration, and
\Psi_{\lambda}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
is the shrinkage operator defined by

\Psi_{\lambda}\left(x_{i}\right)=(|x_{i}|-\lambda)_{+}\text{sgn}\,(x_{i})

(28)

where
x_{i}
denotes the
i^{th}
entry of
\mathbf{x}
; see
[
9
]
for more details.
We use the seminal Fast-ISTA (FISTA) method from Beck and Teboulle
[
4
]
with backtracking to efficiently compute a solution to (
26
). FISTA differs from its predecessor through the choice of its shrinkage operator
\Psi_{\lambda}(\cdot)
which acts on a specific linear combination of the previous two iterates
\left\{\mathbf{x}^{(k-1)},\mathbf{x}^{(k)}\right\}
. This choice results in a superior worst-case complexity of
\mathcal{O}(1/k^{2})
versus
\mathcal{O}(1/k)
for ISTA. We use the FISTA implementation from the software package IR-Tools
[
12
]
.

Obtaining a suitable choice of the regularization parameter (
\lambda
for variational regularization and the stopping iteration
k
for Landweber) is a non-trivial task and has continued to receive considerable attention over the last four decades; see references within
[
3
,
2
,
25
]
.

Since our focus herein is on the performance of Landweber and other iterative regularization methods to recover cosmic strings, we have used the optimal regularization parameter
\lambda_{opt}
in each experiment. That is, given the true solution
\mathbf{x}_{\rm true}
, we determine the optimal regularization parameter to be that which minimizes the relative reconstruction error. For Landweber, this corresponds to finding
k_{opt}=\argmin_{k}\|\mathbf{x}^{(k)}-\mathbf{x}_{\rm true}\|
within a maximal number of iterations. For the solution of (
25
), we select at iteration
k

\lambda^{(k)}_{opt}=\argmin_{\lambda}\frac{\|\mathbf{x}^{(k)}_{\lambda}-\mathbf{x}_{\rm true}\|}{\|\mathbf{x}_{\rm true}\|}.

This process can be efficiently implemented at each iteration given that
\mathbf{x}_{\lambda}^{(k)}
is computed in the subspace
\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{V}_{k})
where typically
k\ll\min\{m,n\}
; see
[
5
,
6
]
for more details. A similar process was carried out involving the minimization of (
26
) to determine the regularization parameter that would result in the lowest relative error amongst the maximal number of iterations within a given implementation of FISTA.

5
Numerical Results

We illustrate the performance of Landweber, Tikhonov, and FISTA described in Section
4.2
for recovering cosmic strings in a two dimensional universe (i.e.
n=2
). To do this, we model a cosmic string as a closed loop in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
and consider its evolution in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2+1}
. As explained in the introduction, we aim to recover the string through the gravitational perturbations it produces. We take a scalar function
f
with conormal type singularities to be the string to the world sheet in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2+1}
. From the tomography data
Lf
we seek to recover
f
. The details of the model are described in Examples
\#1-\#3
below. We remark that although our model is highly idealized, it does capture some of the main features of the physical problem. We refer the reader to
[
30
]
for additional background.

In our experiments we assume knowledge of an upper bound on the error contaminating the problems, i.e.,

\left\|\bf{e}\right\|\leq\delta,

and utilize the discrepancy principle (DP) as a termination criterion for the iterative methods considered.
Let
\mathbf{x}^{(k)}
denote the solution determined by the appropriate algorithm at iteration
k
.
The DP prescribes that an iterative method
should be terminated as soon as an iterate is found that satisfies

\left\|\mathbf{Ax}^{(k)}-\bf{b}\right\|\leq\tau\delta.

(29)

Here,
\tau>1
is a user specified constant that is independent of

\delta
; see
[
10
,
15
]
. In our experiments we utilize
\tau=1.01
. We iteratively track the relative residual norm (RRN) defined by

\text{RRN}\left(\mathbf{x}^{(k)}\right)=\frac{\left\|\mathbf{Ax}^{(k)}-\mathbf{b}\right\|}{\left\|\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}_{\rm true}\right\|}

to determine when the normalized DP is satisfied. To evaluate the quality of the computed solutions,
we compute the relative reconstructive error (RRE) defined by

\text{RRE}\left(\mathbf{x}^{(k)}\right)=\frac{\left\|\mathbf{x}^{(k)}-\mathbf{x}_{\rm true}\right\|}{\left\|\mathbf{x}_{\rm true}\right\|}.

The reported numerical results of this work were carried out in MATLAB R2022b 64-bit on a MacBook Pro laptop
running MacOS Ventura with an Apple M2 Pro processor with @3.49 GHz and 16 GB of RAM. The codes for this paper will be made available at
https://github.com/lonisk1/CMB_InvProb
once the revision process is complete.

For our final example, we consider a string that appears at

&0,\quad t\in[0,t_{0})\cup(t_{1},4]\end{cases}

As in Example
\#1
, we take

. The main goal of the example is to investigate the reconstruction at the temporal interfaces
t=t_{0}
and
t=t_{1}
where
f_{3}
is singular in time-like directions. This should be quite challenging according to the analysis in Section
2
, but we will see that certain regularizers can reasonably capture the temporal singularity.

The discretization of the world sheet is the same as in the previous two examples. Since the string only exists from
t\in[t_{0},\,t_{1}]
, this corresponds to the top and bottom
6
temporal slices being empty. On
[0,\,4]
with
20

and

. The problem is considered with
5\%
added noise in the same manner as was done in the previous examples.

Since this example involves slices that we wish to avoid contaminating during reconstruction, it is natural to consider a regularizer
R
from (
23
) which penalizes interaction between sequential time slices,
\mathbf{X}(t)
, for
t=1,\dots,T
. Such a regularizer was discussed in
[
7
]
for Bayesian inverse problems. The penalized LS problem may be written as

\min_{\mathbf{x}}\|\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{b}\|^{2}+\lambda\|\mathbf{x}\|_{\mathbf{Q}^{-1}}^{2}

(30)

where
\|\mathbf{x}\|_{\mathbf{M}}^{2}=\mathbf{x}^{\top}\mathbf{M}\mathbf{x}
for any symmetric positive definite matrix
\mathbf{M}.

We note that for dynamic inverse problems, the prior covariance matrix
\mathbf{Q}\in\mathbb{R}^{n^{2}T\times n^{2}T}
may be very large and dense, and working with its inverse or square root can be computationally difficult. Generalized Golub-Kahan based iterative methods were described in
[
7
,
8
]
, where the
k^{th}
iterate is given by

\mathbf{x}^{(k)}=\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{y}_{k}\quad\mbox{where}\quad\mathbf{y}_{k}=\argmin_{\mathbf{y}\in\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{V}_{k})}\|\mathbf{A}\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{b}\|^{2}+\lambda\|\mathbf{y}\|_{\mathbf{Q}}^{2}

where
\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{V}_{k})=\mathcal{K}_{k}(\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{b})
.

For the numerical experiments, we let
\mathbf{Q}
represent a three-dimensional Matérn covariance kernel with
\nu=1.5
and
\ell=0.05
. This is a nonseparable covariance kernel that models space-time interactions of variability. Constructing such a matrix is not done explicitly; instead, circulant embedding and FFT-based techniques are used for efficient matrix-vector products. We will refer experimentally to this method as generalized Tikhonov (gen. Tikhonov) where like past examples, we will consider the optimal regularization parameter at each iteration.

6
Conclusion

In this paper, we provide a theoretical and numerical study of iterative methods for the inverse problem of the light ray transform, and discuss applications in cosmology. Specifically, we investigated the use of state-of-the-art iterative regularization methods. We provided a theoretical analysis of the Landweber method and its expected behavior for the recovery of cosmic strings. We numerically compared Landweber against Tikhonov and
\ell_{1}
iterative regularization methods. Various examples in Section
5
complement the analysis from Section
3
, demonstrating that the recovery of functions with conormal type singularities (e.g., strings to the world sheet) can be difficult and can lead to reconstruction artefacts.

Future work includes extensions to a three-dimensional universe over time (i.e., 4D reconstructions). This will require additional tools (e.g., structure exploiting methods and algorithms that can handle larger datasets).
Moreover, for a more realistic setting, the CMB data is measured near a freely falling observer, see for example
[
20
]
. We plan to investigate the partial data problem for the light ray transform in the near future. The problem is conceivably more challenging. However, recent studies in
[
29
,
32
]
have shown that stability of the inverse problem can be improved by taking into account the evolution of the universe. It would be interesting to investigate the effects on the numerical reconstruction.
