A Landen-type method for computation of Weierstrass functions
Matvey Smirnov, Kirill Malkov, Sergey Rogovoy

Abstract
. We establish a version of the Landen’s transformation for Weierstrass functions and invariants that is applicable to general lattices in complex plane. Using it we present an effective method for computing Weierstrass functions, their periods, and elliptic integral in Weierstrass form given Weierstrass invariants g 2 g_{2} and g 3 g_{3} of an elliptic curve. Similarly to the classical Landen’s method our algorithm has quadratic rate of convergence. Keywords. Weierstrass functions, Landen’s transformation.

Introduction

There are numerous applications of elliptic functions in various fields of mathematics and physics (see, e.g.
[
3
]
,
[
8
]
, and references therein). For efficient computations with elliptic functions various methods are known. Traditionally, the most frequently used approaches include either the Landen method (or equivalent methods based on the arithmetic-geometric mean) for Jacobi functions
[
9
]
, or summation of theta-series
[
5
]
. It is noteworthy that Weierstrass functions, even though they are the most convenient for theoretical framework of elliptic functions and curves, are not usually considered as an effective computational tool. In this paper we present an approach related to the Landen method that provides effective computation of all the Weierstrass functions, elliptic integral in the Weierstrass form, and periods of an elliptic curve given the Weierstrass invariants
g_{2}\mathchar 59\relax g_{3}
.

The part of the method concerning computation of periods and the Abel map was already presented in the work
[
4
]
in an equivalent form with the use of the complex version of arithmetic-geometric mean. Also a similar method for computation of the
\wp
-function was presented in
[
7
, Sec. 4.3]
. In this work we present a unified approach to these computations, as well as the computation of other Weierstrass functions. It is noteworthy that the calculation of all Weierstrass functions simultaneously allows not only to provide a framework to all possible computations with elliptic functions, but also to solve the problem that emerges in adaptations of the Landen or AGM-type methods to theta-functions. Namely, for the theta function (and the Weierstrass
\sigma
function as well) Landen’s transformation only allows to determine the squared value of the function. For example, in
[
6
]
this problem is solved by using a low-accuracy approximation of the theta-function by its Fourier series, which allows to determine the sign. Such an approach leads to a significant increase in computational complexity, so the method shows its efficiency only during calculations with very high precision. For the Weierstrass
\sigma
function, however, this difficulty can be overcome by computing simultaneously
\wp\mathchar 59\relax\wp^{\prime}
, and
\sigma^{2}
at
z/2
and using the duplication formula.

Similarly to the Landen and AGM-based methods the method presented here has quadratic rate of convergence. For example, in practice it is usually sufficient to perform at most
5
iterations of the Landen transformation in order to achieve the machine precision while computing with double precision floating point arithmetic. Moreover, due to the quadratic convergence, there is only mild increase in complexity with the use of the high precision arithmetic.
Finally, we note that the essence of this method implies that the computations are stable for curves that are close to being degenerate. To demonstrate this we compute parameters of a conformal mapping problem studied in
[
10
]
for domains, such that the corresponding elliptic curve is near degeneration.

The paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
we recall the notation and several facts from the theory of elliptic functions. In particular, we are interested in the Weierstrass functions that are associated not only with lattices but also with subgroups of
\CC
of rank
1
or
0
, which are not usually covered in literature. The main results of this paper are collected in Section
3
. Namely, we derive the Landen transformation for the Weierstrass functions, describe the optimal way to choose a subgroup of index
2
in a lattice, and analyze the rate of convergence of Weierstrass invariants under the iterations of the Landen transformation. Some of the results of Section
3
are known in literature, but it was decided to include their proofs for completeness and convenience. Section
4
contains the description of the method that computes periods and the Abel map (i.e. the elliptic integral in Weierstrass form). Since an equivalent version of this method was already studied in
[
4
]
, we do not include analysis of the convergence. In Section
5
we describe the algorithm to compute Weierstrass functions and give a simple proof of the quadratic rate of convergence for the
\wp
function. Finally, Section
6
contains numerical experiments that show the quadratic convergence and an application to a conformal mapping problem.

3.
Subgroups of index
2
in lattices

Throughout this section
\Gamma\subset\CC
denotes a lattice. Recall that the
index

[\Gamma:G]
of a subgroup

is the number of elements in the quotient group
\Gamma/G

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
\Gamma^{\prime}\subset\Gamma
be a subgroup of index
2
. Then the following statements hold.

(i)

\Gamma^{\prime}
is a lattice.

(ii)

2\Gamma\subset\Gamma^{\prime}\subset\Gamma
and
[\Gamma^{\prime}:2\Gamma]=2
.

(iii)

Let
\omega\in\Gamma^{\prime}\setminus 2\Gamma
. Then
\Gamma^{\prime}=\Span(2\Gamma\cup\{\omega\})
.

Proof.

The statement
(i)
is clear. To prove
(ii)
note that
2x=0
for all
x\in\Gamma/\Gamma^{\prime}
. Therefore,
2x+\Gamma^{\prime}=\Gamma^{\prime}
for all
x\in\Gamma
which implies
2x\in\Gamma^{\prime}
. The equality
[\Gamma^{\prime}:2\Gamma]=2
is easily derived using
[\Gamma:\Gamma^{\prime}]=2
and
[\Gamma:2\Gamma]=4
.

Now we prove
(iii)
. Clearly,
G=\Span(2\Gamma\cup\{\omega\})\subset\Gamma^{\prime}
. Since
2\Gamma\subset G\subset\Gamma
, the number
[\Gamma:G]
can be only equal to
1
,
2
, or
4
. Since
G
is strictly greater than
2\Gamma
, its index cannot be equal to
4
. On the other hand,
G
is contained in
\Gamma^{\prime}
, so
G\neq\Gamma
. Thus,
[\Gamma:G]=2
. It remains to note
\Gamma^{\prime}
has the same index in
\Gamma
as
G
and the equality
[\Gamma:G]=[\Gamma:\Gamma^{\prime}][\Gamma^{\prime}:G]
implies that
\Gamma^{\prime}/G
is a trivial group.
∎

Corollary 3.2
.

Let
\omega\in\Gamma\setminus 2\Gamma

of index
2

\omega

\Gamma^{\prime}=\Span(2\Gamma\cup\{\omega\})

Proof.

The formula for
\Gamma^{\prime}
(if it exists) is contained in Proposition

(iii)

has index

is strictly between

∎

Now assume that

Definition 3.15

\Gamma\setminus\omega_{1}\ZZ
. It is easy to conclude, that
|\omega_{2}|=\inf\{|w|:w\in\hat{\Gamma}\setminus\{0\}\}
. Now consider the case
2|\omega_{1}|=\inf\{|w|:w\in\hat{\Gamma}\setminus\{0\}\}
. Since
\hat{\Gamma}\setminus 2\omega_{1}\ZZ\subset\Gamma\setminus\omega_{1}\ZZ
we obtain that also
|\omega_{2}|=\inf\{|w|:w\in\hat{\Gamma}\setminus 2\omega_{1}\ZZ\}
, i.e. basis
2\omega_{1}\mathchar 59\relax\omega_{2}
is reduced. Finally, assume
|\omega_{2}|=\inf\{|w|:w\in\hat{\Gamma}\setminus\{0\}\}
. Without loss of generality we put
\omega_{1}=1
. Then
1\leq|\omega_{2}|\leq 2
and
|\mathrm{Re}\>\omega_{2}|\leq 1/2
. It is clear that there exists a reduced basis in
\hat{\Gamma}
of the form
\omega_{2}\mathchar 59\relax 2+k\omega_{2}
for some
k\in\ZZ
. Moreover, it is sufficient to satisfy
|\mathrm{Re}\>(2/\omega_{2}+k)|\leq 1/2
. The conditions on
\omega_{2}
easily imply that
|\mathrm{Re}\>(2/\omega_{2})|\leq 1
, so it is possible to find
k
from the set
\{-1\mathchar 59\relax 0\mathchar 59\relax 1\}
.

Now we prove
(iii)
. Suppose that
2|\omega_{1}|\leq|\omega_{2}|
. Then the basis
2\omega_{1}\mathchar 59\relax\omega_{2}
is reduced and the statement follows from Theorem
3.6

(i)
. To prove the converse assume that
|f_{1}-f_{2}|\geq|f_{2}-f_{3}|
and
|f_{1}-f_{3}|\geq|f_{2}-f_{3}|
. If
2|\omega_{1}|>|\omega_{2}|
, then by
(ii)

2\omega_{1}
is either the second element, or the sum of elements in a reduced basis in
\Gamma
. In both cases Theorem
3.6
combined with the assumption implies that
2\omega_{1}
has the smallest absolute value among the elements of
\hat{\Gamma}\setminus\{0\}
, which contradicts
2|\omega_{1}|>|\omega_{2}|
.
∎

Now we can formulate an algorithm that computes a reduced basis in a lattice
\Gamma
given
g_{2}(\Gamma)
and
g_{3}(\Gamma)
. The idea can be formulated as follows: to compute the smallest period just iterate the transformation
\widetilde{\mathscr{L}}
to choose an optimal subgroup of index 2 until the corresponding lattice is close enough to a rank-
1
group. Then we can use the formula that relates remaining period to the roots. In order to find the second period in the basis we iterate the Landen transformation to find not an optimal subgroup of index
2
(so the smallest period multiplies by
2
and the second period remains the same) until the smallest period of that lattice appears to be the required second period of
\Gamma
. After that we can just find the smallest period in the obtained lattice.

Algorithm 4.1
.

(1)

Calculate a properly ordered triple
(e_{1}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}e_{2}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}e_{3})
of distinct roots of the polynomial
4x^{3}-g_{2}(\Gamma)x-g_{3}(\Gamma)
.

(2)

Calculate
f^{(0)}=\mathscr{L}(e_{1}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}\{e_{2}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}e_{3}\})
and
h^{(0)}=\mathscr{L}(e_{2}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}\{e_{1}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}e_{3}\})
.

(3)

Calculate
f^{(n)}=\widetilde{\mathscr{L}}(\mathscr{F}(f^{(n-1)}))
until the difference between two closest roots in the triple
\mathscr{F}(f^{(n)})
is sufficiently small. Let
N
denote the number of iterations. So
f^{(N)}=(f^{(N)}_{1}\mathchar 59\relax\{f^{(N)}_{2}\mathchar 59\relax f^{(N)}_{3}\})
, where
f^{(N)}_{1}\approx-e/2
and
\{f^{(N)}_{2}\mathchar 59\relax f^{(N)}_{3}\}\approx\{e\mathchar 59\relax-e/2\}
. Now
\omega_{1}=i\pi/\sqrt{3f^{(N)}_{1}}
is an approximation for an element in
\Gamma\setminus\{0\}
with the smallest absolute value.

(4)

Assume that the conditions
|h^{(n-1)}_{1}-h^{(n-1)}_{2}|\geq|h^{(n-1)}_{2}-h^{(n-1)}_{3}|
and
|h^{(n-1)}_{1}-h^{(n-1)}_{3}|\geq|h^{(n-1)}_{2}-h^{(n-1)}_{3}|
hold. In this case we let
\tilde{h}^{(n-1)}\in\CC^{3}
denote a properly ordered triple of the same numbers as in
h^{(n-1)}
such that
h^{(n-1)}_{1}=\tilde{h}^{(n-1)}_{1}
and let
h^{(n)}=\mathscr{L}(\tilde{h}^{(n-1)}_{2}\mathchar 59\relax\{\tilde{h}^{(n-1)}_{1}\mathchar 59\relax\tilde{h}^{(n-1)}_{3}\})
. If the above conditions are not fulfilled we stop the iterations and let
k^{(0)}=\{h^{(n-1)}_{1}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}h^{(n-1)}_{2}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}h^{(n-1)}_{3}\}
.

(5)

Use the same calculations as in the step
3
to find an approximation for a nonzero element with the smallest absolute value in the lattice that corresponds to the roots
k^{(0)}
. This is an approximation for the second period of
\Gamma
.

We also present an algorithm to compute
\mathcal{A}_{\Gamma}(x\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}y)
given
g_{2}(\Gamma)
,
g_{3}(\Gamma)
, and a pair
(x\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}y)\in\CC^{2}
such that
y^{2}=4x^{3}-g_{2}(\Gamma)x-g_{3}(\Gamma)
. More precisely, the algorithm below computes some point
z\in\CC
such that
\mathcal{A}_{\Gamma}(x\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}y)=z\;\mathrm{mod}\;\Gamma
.

Algorithm 4.2
.

(1)

Calculate a properly ordered
(e_{1}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}e_{2}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}e_{3})
triple of distinct roots of the polynomial
4x^{3}-g_{2}(\Gamma)x-g_{3}(\Gamma)
.

(2)

Let
e^{(1)}=\mathscr{L}(e_{1}\mathchar 59\relax\{e_{2}\mathchar 59\relax e_{3}\})
and calculate
e^{(n)}=\widetilde{\mathscr{L}}(\mathscr{F}(e^{(n-1)}))
until
|e^{(n)}_{2}-e^{(n)}_{3}|
is sufficiently small. Let
N
denote the number of iterations and
\omega
denote the approximation for the smallest period of
\Gamma
(i.e.
\omega=i\pi/\sqrt{3e^{(N)}_{1}}
).

(3)

Denote
x_{0}=x
and
y_{0}=y
. Calculate a sequence
x_{n}
,
y_{n}
,
n=1\mathchar 59\relax\dots\mathchar 59\relax N
that satisfies

x_{n-1}=x_{n}+\frac{(e^{(n)}_{2}-e^{(n)}_{1})(e^{(n)}_{3}-e^{(n)}_{1})}{x_{n}-e^{(n)}_{1}}\mathchar 59\relax

y_{n-1}=y_{n}\left(1-\frac{(e^{(n)}_{2}-e^{(n)}_{1})(e^{(n)}_{3}-e^{(n)}_{1})}{(x_{n}-e^{(n)}_{1})^{2}}\right).

On each iteration there are two possibilities for choosing
x_{n}
(the value
y_{n}
is determined by the choice of
x_{n}
). In order to make these sequences converge we require
x_{n}
to be that solution of the first equation above, which is closer to
x_{n-1}
.

(4)

As an approximation to
\mathcal{A}_{\Gamma}(x\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}y)
we propose
z\;\mathrm{mod}\;\Gamma
, where

z=-\frac{\omega}{\pi}\arctan\left(\frac{6\pi\omega^{2}x_{N}+2\pi^{3}}{3\omega^{3}y_{N}}\right).

For the analysis (in particular, the analysis of convergence) of the similar algorithms formulated in the setting of the complex AGM (which is an equivalent form of the Landen transformation) we refer to
[
4
]
.

5.
Computation of Weierstrass functions

We are ready to give an algorithm to compute values of the Weierstrass functions
\wp(z\mathchar 59\relax\Gamma)
,
\wp^{\prime}(z\mathchar 59\relax\Gamma)
,
\zeta(z\mathchar 59\relax\Gamma)
,
\sigma(z\mathchar 59\relax\Gamma)
given
z\mathchar 59\relax g_{2}(\Gamma)\mathchar 59\relax g_{3}(\Gamma)
. We follow the ideas of the classical Landen method, that is, we compute a sequence of optimal subgroups of index
2
until the corresponding Weierstrass functions are approximated well by the functions corresponding to a rank-
1
additive subgroup. After that the approximations of the Weierstrass functions corresponding to
\Gamma
are obtained by repeated application of formulae (
3.1
)-(
3.4
). The only difficulty in this approach is that we can only compute
\sigma^{2}(z\mathchar 59\relax\Gamma)
instead of
\sigma(z\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}\Gamma)
. This problem can be solved by a following trick: values
\wp(z\mathchar 59\relax\Gamma)
,
\wp^{\prime}(z\mathchar 59\relax\Gamma)
,
\zeta(z\mathchar 59\relax\Gamma)
,
\sigma(z\mathchar 59\relax\Gamma)
can be recovered from values
\wp(z/2\mathchar 59\relax\Gamma)
,
\wp^{\prime}(z/2\mathchar 59\relax\Gamma)
,
\zeta(z/2\mathchar 59\relax\Gamma)
,
\sigma(z/2\mathchar 59\relax\Gamma)^{2}
using duplication formulae (see
[
1
, Eq. 18.4.5-8]
).

Algorithm 5.1
.

(1)

Calculate a properly ordered
(e_{1}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}e_{2}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}e_{3})
triple of distinct roots of the polynomial
4x^{3}-g_{2}(\Gamma)x-g_{3}(\Gamma)
.

(2)

Put
e^{(1)}=\mathscr{L}(e_{1}\mathchar 59\relax\{e_{2}\mathchar 59\relax e_{3}\})
and calculate
e^{(n)}=\widetilde{\mathscr{L}}(\mathscr{F}(e^{(n-1)}))
until
|e^{(n)}_{2}-e^{(n)}_{3}|
is sufficiently small. Let
N
denote the number of iterations and
\omega
denote the approximation for the smallest period of
\Gamma
(i.e.
\omega=i\pi/\sqrt{3e^{(N)}_{1}}
).

(3)

Initialize
\tilde{\wp}_{N}\mathchar 59\relax\tilde{\wp}^{\prime}_{N}\mathchar 59\relax\tilde{\zeta}_{N}\mathchar 59\relax\tilde{\sigma}_{N}
as

\tilde{\wp}_{N}=\frac{\pi^{2}}{\omega^{2}}\left(\frac{1}{\sin\left(\frac{\pi z}{2\omega}\right)^{2}}-\frac{1}{3}\right)\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}\;\;\tilde{\wp}^{\prime}_{N}=-\frac{2\pi^{3}\cos\left(\frac{\pi z}{2\omega}\right)}{\omega^{3}\sin\left(\frac{\pi z}{2\omega}\right)^{3}}

\tilde{\zeta}_{N}=\frac{\pi^{2}z}{6\omega^{2}}+\frac{\pi}{\omega}\cot\left(\pi\frac{z}{2\omega}\right)\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}\;\;\tilde{\sigma}_{N}=\frac{\omega}{\pi}\exp\left(\frac{\pi^{2}z^{2}}{24\omega^{2}}\right)\sin\left(\pi\frac{z}{2\omega}\right).

(4)

Compute
\tilde{\wp}_{n}\mathchar 59\relax\tilde{\wp}^{\prime}_{n}\mathchar 59\relax\tilde{\zeta}_{n}\mathchar 59\relax\tilde{\sigma}_{n}
for
n=N-1\mathchar 59\relax N-2\mathchar 59\relax\dots\mathchar 59\relax 0
by the rules

\tilde{\wp}_{n-1}=\tilde{\wp}_{n}+\frac{(e^{(n)}_{2}-e^{(n)}_{1})(e^{(n)}_{3}-e^{(n)}_{1})}{\tilde{\wp}_{n}-e^{(n)}_{1}}\mathchar 59\relax

\tilde{\wp}^{\prime}_{n-1}=\tilde{\wp}^{\prime}_{n}\left(1-\frac{(e^{(n)}_{2}-e^{(n)}_{1})(e^{(n)}_{3}-e^{(n)}_{1})}{(\tilde{\wp}_{n}-e^{(n)}_{1})^{2}}\right)\mathchar 59\relax

\tilde{\zeta}_{n-1}=2\tilde{\zeta}_{n}+\frac{1}{2}\frac{\tilde{\wp}^{\prime}_{n}}{\tilde{\wp}_{n}-e^{(n)}_{1}}+e^{(n)}_{1}\frac{z}{2}\mathchar 59\relax

\tilde{\sigma}_{n-1}=\exp\left(e^{(n)}_{1}\frac{z^{2}}{4}\right)(\tilde{\wp}_{n}-e^{(n)}_{1})\tilde{\sigma}_{n}^{2}.

(5)

Finally, we propose the following approximations for the Weierstrass functions:

\wp(z\mathchar 59\relax\Gamma)\approx-2\tilde{\wp}_{0}+\left(\frac{6\tilde{\wp}^{2}_{0}-g_{2}(\Gamma)/2}{2\tilde{\wp}^{\prime}_{0}}\right)^{2}\mathchar 59\relax

\wp^{\prime}(z\mathchar 59\relax\Gamma)\approx-\tilde{\wp}^{\prime}_{0}+\frac{6\tilde{\wp}^{2}_{0}-g_{2}(\Gamma)/2}{4\tilde{\wp}^{\prime}_{0}}\left(12\tilde{\wp}_{0}-\left(\frac{6\tilde{\wp}^{2}_{0}-g_{2}(\Gamma)/2}{\tilde{\wp}^{\prime}_{0}}\right)^{2}\right)\mathchar 59\relax

\zeta(z\mathchar 59\relax\Gamma)=\approx 2\tilde{\zeta}_{0}+\frac{6\tilde{\wp}^{2}_{0}-g_{2}(\Gamma)/2}{2\tilde{\wp}^{\prime}_{0}}\mathchar 59\relax

\sigma(z\mathchar 59\relax\Gamma)\approx-\tilde{\wp}^{\prime}_{0}\tilde{\sigma}^{2}_{0}

The proof of convergence of the foregoing approximations is rather technical and we will only consider the approximation of
\wp
-function. The main tool is the following elementary lemma.

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
x\in\CC
and let
\{f_{n}\}_{n\in\NN}
be a sequence of meromorphic functions on
\CC
. Assume that there exists a neighborhood
U
of
x
such that functions
f_{n}
do not have poles in
U
for large
n
and uniformly converge on
U
to a holomorphic function as
n\rightarrow\infty
. Also assume that the sequences
\{x_{n}\}_{n\in\NN}
,
\{y_{n}\}_{n\in\NN}
converge to
x
quadratically fast. Then the sequence
\{f_{n}(x_{n})-f_{n}(y_{n})\}
consists of finite complex numbers for large
n
and converges to
0
quadratically fast.

Proof.

Let
V
denote a compact convex neighborhood of
x
such that
\overline{V}\subset U
. Let
N\in\NN
denote a number such that
x_{n}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}y_{n}\in U
and
f_{n}
is holomorphic on
U
for
n\geq N
. It is clear that the values
f_{n}(x_{n})
,
f_{n}(y_{n})
, and
f_{n}(x)
belongs to
\CC
for
n\geq N
. Moreover, the functions
f^{\prime}_{n}
for
n\geq N
are uniformly bounded on
V
by some constant
C
. Thus,
|f_{n}(x_{n})-f_{n}(x)|\leq C|x_{n}-x|
for
n\geq N
and the sequence
\{f_{n}(x_{n})-f_{n}(x)\}
converges to
0
quadratically fast. The statement follows as
f_{n}(x_{n})-f_{n}(y_{n})=(f_{n}(x_{n})-f_{n}(x))-(f_{n}(y_{n})-f_{n}(x))
.
∎

Now we introduce the following notation. Let
e^{(n)}
,
N
, and
\omega
be the same as in the step
(2)
of the algorithm. To be more precise we use the notation
\omega_{N}
instead of
\omega

. We denote by
\Gamma_{n}
the lattice that corresponds to the roots

and by

we denote the function

\phi_{n}(x)=x+\frac{(e^{(n)}_{2}-e^{(n)}_{1})(e^{(n)}_{3}-e^{(n)}_{1})}{x-e^{(n)}_{1}}.

Finally, let
\Phi_{n}(x)=(\phi_{1}\circ\dots\circ\phi_{n})(x)
.

Proposition 5.2
.

Assume that
z\notin\Gamma
and let

x_{N}=\frac{\pi^{2}}{\omega_{N}^{2}}\left(\frac{1}{\sin\left(\frac{\pi z}{\omega_{N}}\right)^{2}}-\frac{1}{3}\right).

Then
\Phi_{N}(x_{N})
converges to
\wp(z\mathchar 59\relax\Gamma)
quadratically fast.

Proof.

It is clear that
\Gamma_{0}=\Gamma
and
\Gamma_{n}
is optimal and has index
2
in
\Gamma_{n-1}
. Thus, by Lemma
3.17

(i)
,
G=\bigcap_{n}\Gamma_{n}
is spanned by a complex number
\Omega
. Moreover, an appropriate choice of signs of
\omega_{N}
guarantees that
\omega_{N}
converges to
\Omega
quadratically fast (note that the
x_{N}
does not change, when
\omega_{N}
is replaced with
-\omega_{N}
). From this we can conclude that
x_{N}
converges quadratically fast to
x=\wp(z\mathchar 59\relax G)
. It is clear that the sequence
\wp(z\mathchar 59\relax\Gamma_{N})
also converges to
x
quadratically fast. The relation
\Phi_{N}(\wp(w\mathchar 59\relax\Gamma_{N}))=\wp(w\mathchar 59\relax\Gamma)
easily implies that the sequence of meromorphic functions
\Phi_{N}
converges on the set
\CC\setminus\wp(\Gamma\mathchar 59\relax G)
to a holomorphic function. In particular, it converges in some neighborhood of
x
. Since

\Phi_{N}(x_{N})-\wp(z\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}\Gamma)=\Phi_{N}(x_{N})-\Phi_{N}(\wp(z\mathchar 59\relax\Gamma_{N}))\mathchar 59\relax

Lemma
5.1

Remarks
.

(1)

All the algorithms that we have presented require to compute some number of optimal subgroups (more precisely, roots of the corresponding polynomials) of undex
2
. If it is required to perform significant amount of computations with a fixed elliptic curve, it is reasonable to precompute those roots and store them. Since usual number of iterations that is required to achieve good accuracy is quite small, the memory cost is negligible.

(2)

Algorithm
5.1
can be optimized if it is not required to approximate all four functions at once. At first we note that the duplication step is necessary only for computation of
\sigma
. In addition, the computation of
\wp
does not depend on other functions,
\wp^{\prime}
depends only on
\wp
, and both
\zeta
and
\sigma
depend on
\wp
and
\wp^{\prime}
(but are independent from each other). Thus, to compute only a subset of values
\{\wp(z\mathchar 59\relax\Gamma)\mathchar 59\relax\wp^{\prime}(z\mathchar 59\relax\Gamma)\mathchar 59\relax\zeta(z\mathchar 59\relax\Gamma)\mathchar 59\relax\sigma(z\mathchar 59\relax\Gamma)\}
one can perform a simpler version of the algorithm.

(3)

In practice of computations with special functions it is often required to calculate the derivatives of these functions with respect to their variables and parameters. Fortunately, the Weierstrass functions satisfy specific differential equations
[
1
, Eqs. 18.6.1-24]
that allow to compute all their derivatives with respect to
z
,
g_{2}
, and
g_{3}
given only their values.
