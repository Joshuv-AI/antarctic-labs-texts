Monogamy relations for relativistically causal correlations
Mirjam Weilenmann

Abstract
Non-signalling conditions encode minimal requirements that any (quantum) systems put into spatial arrangements must satisfy in order to be consistent with special relativity. Recent works have argued that in scenarios involving more that two parties, conditions compatible with relativistic causality do not have to satisfy all possible non-signalling conditions but only a subset of them. Here we show that correlations satisfying only this subset of constraints have to satisfy highly non-local monogamy relations between the effects of space-like separated random variables. These monogamy relations take the form of new entropic inequalities between the various systems and we give a general method to derive them. Using these monogamy relations we refute previous suggestions for physical mechanisms that could lead to relativistically causal correlations, demonstrating that such mechanisms would lead to superluminal signalling.



I
Introduction

The relation between quantum and relativity theory has been a topic of interest in the realm of quantum information theory in the last few decades. Investigations into whether consistency with special relativity is sufficient to restrict correlations between two parties to be quantum
Popescu and Rohrlich 1994
, has prompted extensive research in quantum foundations into the physical principles underlying it. At the same time, cryptographic tasks have been analysed against adversaries that are not necessarily restricted to act according to quantum theory but instead are restricted by relativistic principles; such adversaries are generally known as non-signalling adversaries. Security against such adversaries has been studied in the bipartite setting, in device-independent key distribution
Barrett et al. 2005
and randomness extraction
Hänggi et al. 2013
;
Ramanathan et al. 2021
.

As quantum technologies progress to involve larger systems with more parties, e.g. in quantum networks, the question of how to generalise and characterise the power of setups involving more parties becomes increasingly important. For cryptographic applications, as non-signalling adversaries are restricted directly by the conditions imposed by special relativity, a crucial preliminary question is how to generalise these conditions to the multi-partite setting. It was argued early on that the straightforward generalisation of non-signalling, which asks that the marginal distribution of any subset of the parties is independent of the inputs of the remaining parties (see e.g.
Masanes et al. 2006
), can be relaxed in specific setups. That is, if such a relaxation does not introduce causal inconsistencies such as causal loops
Shimony 1984
;
Grunhaus et al. 1996
. For instance, if two random variables, say
A,C
have their joint future within that of another,
B
, then
B
influencing the correlations of
A
and
C
does not introduce any causal loops, even if all three variables are space-like separated.
This led to a general description of
relativistically causal correlations

Horodecki and Ramanathan 2019
.

Subsequent work showed that information-theoretic and statistical challenges may arise when restricting correlations in the multi-party setting only by this notion of relativistic causality. Specifically, it leads to no-go theorems for cryptographic protocols
Salazar et al. 2021
as well as to challenges for causal modelling, which in this case relies on fine-tuning the causal dependencies
Vilasini and Colbeck 2022a
;
Vilasini and Colbeck 2022b
.

In this article, we demonstrate conceptual implications of allowing the most general relativistically causal correlations. While the examples considered in
Shimony 1984
;
Grunhaus et al. 1996
;
Horodecki and Ramanathan 2019
;
Salazar et al. 2021
seem to suggest that causality is relaxed in a somewhat innocent way, we show that in more complicated multi-party setups, these correlations have to satisfy a new type of monogamy relation. The special feature of these relations is that they relate the influences of independent, space-like separated variables. We argue that this shows that this type of correlation is highly non-local.

Furthermore, an absence of causal loops does not explain, what mechanisms may lead to general relativistically causal correlations. For understanding in what sense such correlations can be generated in an information theoretic task or used by an adversary to tamper with a cryptographic protocols, the mechanisms leading to such correlations and the control different parties may have over them are, however, important. Despite the lack of a full explanation, a partial intuition of such a mechanism was given in
Grunhaus et al. 1996
, where there is a “jammer”,
who has access to a
jamming device which he can activate, at will

Grunhaus et al. 1996
.

We show here, that our monogamy relations can be used to rule out the explanation of correlations in terms of a mechanism that an agent can partially control or activate, as such a mechanism would allow for a superluminal transfer of information. Thus the above intuition is inadequate in general. We go on to discuss further problems the monogamy relations create for other potential explanations of such a mechanism as well as for causal modelling. This work thus restricts the type of possible explanations of relativistically causal correlations, making it questionable that these correlations will be realised by a physical theory.

Our monogamy relations can be phrased in terms of entropic inequalities for which we propose a technique to derive them. We further show how this technique can be adapted to relax entropic constraints on non-signalling correlations to their analogues for more general relativistically causal correlations. We illustrate this with an example showing how the tradeoff between the bipartite non-locality that can be shared in a three-party setting between pairs of parties is altered in this paradigm as compared to imposing all non-signalling constraints
Chaves and Budroni 2016
.

This article is structured as follows. We will first introduce the notions of relativistic causality based on those of
Grunhaus et al. 1996
;
Horodecki and Ramanathan 2019
(in Section
II
).
We will continue by proving our monogamy relations (Section
III
) and use them to show how a mechanism that leads to relativistically causal correlations can in general not be controlled by an agent (Section
IV
). We discuss the implications of these insights for mechanisms explaining such relativistically causal correlations and for causal modelling (Section
IV.2
). We further show how a variation of the technique that leads to such monogamy relations can be used to derive entropy inequalities for relativistically causal correlations (Section
V
) and compare them to the non-signalling case. We conclude in Section
VI
.

II
Compatibility with relativistic causality

In this section we introduce the notion of compatibility with relativistic causality as it has previously appeared in the literature. This includes the bipartite setting, that has been intensively studied in the context of Bell inequality violations, as well as the multipartite setting
Shimony 1984
;
Grunhaus et al. 1996
;
Horodecki and Ramanathan 2019
.

II.1
Non-signalling conditions

In a bipartite (quantum) communication scenario we are usually concerned with two parties, Alice and Bob, who are supposed to perform some information processing task. Often, these parties are allowed to locally operate on systems that are shared with them by a common source.

Usually, the parties are allowed to perform measurements on the respective subsystems they hold. In particular, Alice, Bob make inputs
x,y
(that signify their choice of measurement) and obtain outcomes
a,b
respectively. Since inputs and outcomes are chosen with certain probabilities, we further associate random variables
X,Y,A,B
to them. It is commonly assumed that the parties can choose their inputs independently of any other variables.

Assuming further that the parties are space-like separated and choose their measurements
x,y
locally, compatibility with special relativity is formally phrased as the requirement that the outcome probabilities
P(ab|xy)
satisfy the relations

\displaystyle\sum_{a}P(ab|xy)

\displaystyle=P(b|y)\quad\forall\ b,x,y,

\displaystyle\sum_{b}P(ab|xy)

\displaystyle=P(a|x)\quad\forall\ a,x,y,

known as the
(bipartite) non-signalling conditions
.

The generalisation of the non-signalling conditions to the multi-partite setting is less established. In fact there are several possible generalisations
Masanes et al. 2006
;
Shimony 1984
;
Grunhaus et al. 1996
;
Horodecki and Ramanathan 2019
. The most straightforward one requires that no subset of the
n
space-like separated parties can signal to any subset of the others. Formally, for
n
space-like separated parties, where the
i
-th has input
x_{i}
and obtains outputs
a_{i}

Masanes et al. 2006

\displaystyle\sum_{a_{k}}

\displaystyle P(\{a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}\}|\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\})

\displaystyle=P(\{a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}\}\setminus a_{k}|\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\}\setminus x_{k})

(1)

\forall\ k,\{a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}\}\setminus a_{k},x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}
. We will call these in the following
full non-signalling conditions.

II.2
Relativistic causality constraints

The question of how relativistic causality affects the correlations observed by multiple parties, was discussed in
Shimony 1984
;
Grunhaus et al. 1996
and found to rely on the light-cone structure of the setup. This has been generalised to a multipartite framework for describing sets of correlations compatible with various space-time arrangements of the parties

We define
relativistic causality
as the requirement that no observer can send superluminal signals, in the sense that an input

can influence correlations of a set of variables
\{A_{i}\}_{i}

\cap_{j}\mathcal{K}_{a_{j}}
of the future light cones
\mathcal{K}_{a_{j}}

, otherwise it cannot, i.e., otherwise
P(\{a_{i}\}_{i}|x)=P(\{a_{i}\}_{i})
. This arguably corresponds to the notion captured by the mathematical formalisation from
Grunhaus et al. 1996
;
Horodecki and Ramanathan 2019
;
Salazar et al. 2021
.

Definition II.1

(Relativistic causality constraints)
:

Let there be

\emptyset\neq\mathcal{I}\subsetneq\{1,\ldots,n\}

Notice that this coincides with the description in one spatial dimension before. Scenarios where only subsystems are considered are furthermore naturally obtained by marginalising over the relevant parties (outcomes). In the following we call correlations that satisfy all relativistic causality constraints in a light-cone arrangement the
relativistically causal correlations
of that scenario.

While this formal definition is stated straightforwardly, its mathematical consistency as well as the physical notion behind it needs further explanation. First of all, this notion is incompatible with the operational reasoning that is often followed in quantum information theory (or in treatments in quantum foundations, like generalised probabilistic theories
Hardy 2001
;
Barrett 2007
), where the non-signalling conditions are naturally built into the framework (specifically into the way multipartite states and measurements are defined). In the quantum case this operational reasoning is followed by taking space-like separated measurements to be made up from commuting operators or to act on different Hilbert spaces in a tensor product. However, this notion of causality might appear more naturally in future physical theories, as was suggested in
Horodecki and Ramanathan 2019
.

Furthermore, to see whether this definition is meaningful for the correlations we may have, we need to show that there are constellations of events, where it leads to constraints that are weaker than the ones that one would obtain from (
1
). To check this for a specific arrangement, it is important to take
all
spatial dimensions into account when identifying whether a variable can potentially affect the correlations of others.
We show that such setups exist and illustrate the dimension dependency by means of the example from Figure
2
in Appendix
A

To see that Definition
II.1
makes sense in general, we further need to ensure that the relativistic causality conditions are frame-independent, since this is something we expect for correlations. This holds because any event in the future light cone of some event is in this future light cone in any reference frame. The analogous statement holds for events in the intersection of several light cones.
We discuss this in more detail in Appendix
A
.

While the picture of relativistically causal correlations appears thus consistent and differs from the full non-signalling paradigm (
1
), an underlying explanation, i.e., a mechanism leading to such correlations is not known.
The mechanism that may lead to such correlations is left open in
Grunhaus et al. 1996
;
Horodecki and Ramanathan 2019
. The intuition that is given is that an agent can activate a device that “jams” correlations of other parties
Grunhaus et al. 1996

Grunhaus et al. 1996

and considered among a larger number of parties in
Horodecki and Ramanathan 2019

Note that while the correlations remain valid independently of the frame, these underlying explanations
depend on the reference frame

can reach a point

is not Lorentz invariant. Indeed, if a signal can travel this way in one reference frame, there may be another reference frame where the order of events at

and

is reversed. Thus, such an explanation can only be retained in certain reference frames

and may be different in others.

IV
Using jamming to signal

We show that while the correlations themselves do not (by definition) feature a superluminal transfer of information, superluminal signalling can occur if we assume that there is a mechanism that an agent can turn on that leads to these relativistically causal effects. We call this a jamming mechanism.

IV.1
Further challenges for establishing a mechanism for relativistically causal correlations

As the above establishes that for some relativistically causal correlations, whether two variables are non-locally affected can not be established at the point where a jamming device is turned on, the question then arises, where this may happen.

One possibility is that relativistically causal influences, e.g. the influence of
X,X_{m}
and
Y,Y_{m}
on
B
, only takes shape where
B
is generated (or at one specific point in the intersection of the future light cones of
(X_{m},X)
and
(Y_{m},Y)
before that).
This seems to be a somewhat consistent way to explain such correlations in a local way using superluminal influences in Figure
4
(a).
However, such a mechanism can not be the whole story: If the influence really goes from
(X_{m},X)
and
(Y_{m},Y)
via
A
,
B
,
C
there needs to be a feature in that mechanism that causes that the scenarios in Figure
4

than in scenario (b).

Another possible explanation could be that
the space time location, where it is determined whether e.g.

is affected by the superluminal influences of
(X_{m},X)
and/or
(Y_{m},Y)
is where the future light cones of
(X_{m},X)
and
(Y_{m},Y)
intersect. However, with this interpretation there is a possible issue since in case of more than one spatial dimension the intersection of the future light cones of
(X_{m},X)
and
(Y_{m},Y)
is not a light cone and does thus not have a single point where the information can start to exist, this rather must be a delocalised region. Such an explanation seems unnatural as along this whole region there would then be a conspiratorial decision whether the effect of
(X_{m},X)
or
(Y_{m},Y)
should prevail. This would thus also require a non-local mechanism that ensures that this happens consistently in this space-time region.

Another way out of this difficulty would be to consider all jamming variables (

) to be determined in a conspiratorial way, so that it is already decided which one is to affect

However, this questions the whole approach of considering light cones, as it seems to make more sense then to consider a single hidden source affecting all variables, as the underlying mechanism that explains the correlations is such. This is in contrast with the relativistic causality framework, which relies on the premise that there are still independent variables (here
(X_{m},X)
,
(Y_{m},Y)
).

Thus mechanisms leading to such correlations have to be highly non-local. Seen from this viewpoint, this exhibits a stronger form of non-locality than is observed for monogamy relations in quantum theory, e.g. for entanglement
Coffman et al. 2000
or non-locality
Toner 2009
. In the case of entanglement and non-locality, the monogamy concerns measurement on a system that is shared with several parties that measure it in the future, in the present type of monogamy it concerns the influences of variables (
(X_{m},X)
,
(Y_{m},Y)
) that are independent and space-like separated.

IV.2
Challenging the possibility of causal inferences

Causal reasoning in the Bayesian sense
Pearl 2009
has been successfully adapted from its classical formulation for random variables to the quantum realm
Allen et al. 2017
;
Barrett et al. 2019
, where notions such as d-separation and the notion of interventions were formulated for quantum processes.
Since quantum theory may one-day be replaced by a more general theory (hopefully including gravity), a more general, theory independent framework to study causal relations has been developed for generalised probabilistic theories
Henson et al. 2014
– which as mentioned above satisfy all non-signalling constraints (
1
) – as well as more generally
Vilasini and Colbeck 2022b
. This latter framework is in particular able to incorporate causal influences as considered in this work, allowing for
fine-tuned causal relations

Vilasini and Colbeck 2022b
.

Even though there is thus a framework to model such causal influences, the monogamy relations considered here provide a conceptual problem for causal reasoning. Causal modelling presupposes that there is an underlying mechanism for correlations.
Our work suggests that such mechanisms have likely a global nature and can not be (partially) controlled by agents. This questions whether causal modelling makes sense for such correlations in more than one spatial dimension, i.e., whether
Vilasini and Colbeck 2022b
can be generalised to this regime.

Furthermore, by looking at a sub-scenario of the one above, say
A,B,X
, we can potentially not know whether the variables have to follow the non-signalling constraints (
1
) or whether they are only restricted by (
3
), which, according to the monogamy relations, depends on
Y
. Recall that, since we can have additional inputs to
A,B
and outcomes to
X
, this also holds for the original scenario of Figure
2
. This makes causal and also experimental reasoning about setups that allow such correlations challenging, in the sense that we have to be able to exclude that there exist any variables that may affect correlations of
A
or
B
respectively with other variables. This would pose new challenges to the way we do research by considering only an experimental setup of interest and some environmental interactions, but where we can assume that no unknown space-like separated variables control how we can operate our experiment.

V
Further applications of entropy inequalities for relativistic causality

Entropy inequalities further give us a method to distinguish properties of non-signalling from relativistically causal correlations. We show in this section that the latter correlations still satisfy non-trivial constraints that are albeit weaker than those for correlations satisfying the full non-signalling constraints, which were derived in
Chaves and Budroni 2016
.

That we can distinguish the correlations of different theories (e.g. classical and quantum) in networks with entropy inequalities, has only ever been shown for entropies where we condition on the inputs taking specific values
Braunstein and Caves 1988
;
Chaves and Fritz 2012
;
Weilenmann and Colbeck 2017
4
4

4

This is called the post-selection technique in
Weilenmann and Colbeck 2017
.
. This motivates us to also pursue such an approach for relativistically causal correlations.
Our method to obtain constraints for relativistically causal correlations is similar to the method for constraining full non-signalling correlations in
Chaves and Budroni 2016
, to which we compare our constraints.

The main new idea that makes our approach suitable for describing relativistically causal correlations, is to introduce additional causal links among the involved variables that a priori introduce too many dependencies and then to remove the unwanted parts of those dependencies. We describe the method in detail in Appendix
C
.

To illustrate the technique we show how conditions on the monogamy of non-locality in the scenario of Figure

Here we address this problem from the perspective of entropy inequalities, where we show how such inequalities are relaxed as compared to the full non-signalling setting.

For this purpose, let us consider the entropy of the distributions

VI
Conclusions

This work shows that relativistically causal correlations have to satisfy strong monogamy relations, which come about when multi-partite relativistically causal correlations in more than one spatial dimension are to be considered. Such scenarios arguably have to be included in the treatment if anyone is to take such correlations seriously.

This puts into question the explanation of these correlations via a jamming mechanism in terms of a device that an agent can voluntarily turn on, as such a device could be used to signal.
This is problematic
in any single reference frame
an explanation of these correlations may be attempted, not only when changing reference frames (which is known to lead to difficulties for explaining this type of correlation
Horodecki and Ramanathan 2019
).
This monogamy is also problematic for the way we perform experiments, where we can usually assume that systems at far away space-time localions do not influence the correlations of our outcomes.

This lack of control about a jamming mechanism also implies that it is not so obvious what the implications of relativistically causal correlations may be for cryptography, since it is not so clear to what extent anyone could actively make use of them. It further implies that, if there exists a mechanism that consistently jams the correlations of some parties, this may shield other parties from similar interferences, which may serve as an asset for salvaging some cryptographic protocols (of which several have already been shown to be compromised in this framework in
Horodecki and Ramanathan 2019
;
Salazar et al. 2021
).

Appendix C
Method for deriving entropy inequalities in jamming scenarios

Entropy allows us to formulate natural measures for quantifying the information some random variables hold about others, in terms of (conditional) mutual information. They furthermore have the convenient property that they linearise (conditional) independence constraints, which has made them one of the main tool to formulate causal compatibility constraints in networks in the past
Pearl 2009
.

In a similar vein, any relativistic causality constraint:
\sum_{a_{i}{\rm s.t.}i\in\mathcal{I}}P(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}|x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})=P(\mathcal{A}_{\mathcal{J}}|\mathcal{X}_{\mathcal{J}}),
for

\emptyset\neq\mathcal{I}\subsetneq\{1,\ldots,n\}
,
\mathcal{J}=\{1,\ldots,n\}\setminus\mathcal{I}
and
\mathcal{J}=\{1,\ldots,n\}\setminus\mathcal{I}
and
\mathcal{J}\subseteq\mathcal{K}\subseteq\{1,\ldots,n\}
,
\tilde{\mathcal{A}}_{\mathcal{J}}=\{A_{i}\mid i\in\mathcal{J}\}
,
\tilde{\mathcal{X}}_{\mathcal{K}}=\{X_{i}\mid i\in\mathcal{K}\}
(and analogously for
\mathcal{I}
), leads to an entropic constraint on

P(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n},x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
, namely

H(\tilde{\mathcal{A}}_{\mathcal{J}}\tilde{\mathcal{X}}_{\mathcal{I}}\tilde{\mathcal{X}}_{\mathcal{K}})=H(\tilde{\mathcal{A}}_{\mathcal{J}}\tilde{\mathcal{X}}_{\mathcal{K}})+H(\tilde{\mathcal{X}}_{\mathcal{I}}),

(13)

which can be concisely written as
I(\tilde{\mathcal{A}}_{\mathcal{J}}\tilde{\mathcal{X}}_{\mathcal{K}}:\tilde{\mathcal{X}}_{\mathcal{I}})=0
. It is well known that this holds iff
P(\mathcal{A}_{\mathcal{J}}\mathcal{X}_{\mathcal{K}}\mathcal{X}_{\mathcal{I}})=P(\mathcal{A}_{\mathcal{J}}\mathcal{X}_{\mathcal{K}})P(\mathcal{X}_{\mathcal{I}})
. Thus these entropic relations fully capture the relativistic causality constraints.
Recall that as inputs the
X_{i}
are all independent.

All such constraints, together with the so-called
Shannon inequalities

\displaystyle H(\mathcal{T})\geq 0

\displaystyle H(\mathcal{V}|\mathcal{W})\geq 0

(14)

\displaystyle I(\mathcal{T}:\mathcal{V}|\mathcal{W})\geq 0

for all non-intersecting sets of variables
\mathcal{T},\mathcal{V},\mathcal{W}\subset\{A_{1},\ldots,A_{n},X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}\}
, form the so-called
Shannon cone of the scenario

\mathcal{S}=\left\{{\bf{H}}=(H(A_{1}),\ldots,H(A_{n}),H(X_{1}),\ldots,H(X_{n}),H(A_{1}A_{2}),\ldots,H(A_{1}\ldots A_{n}X_{1}\ldots X_{n}))\in\mathbb{R}^{2^{2n}-1}|\eqref{eq:sh}\text{ and }\eqref{eq:caus}\text{ hold}\right\}.

Asking what these constraints impose on the relation of specific marginals can then be phrased as a variable elimination problem. Indeed we can identify components of
{\bf{H}}
that are of interest to us and then derive constraints only involving these variables by means of performing a Fourier-Motzkin elimination of all other variables on
\mathcal{S}
.
We illustrate this technique with an example.

Entropy inequalities can further be formulated where we condition on some of the variables, usually the inputs, to take specific values. Such methods have been called post-selection techniques
Weilenmann and Colbeck 2017
. Similar ideas have then also been applied to analysing networks without the use of entropy, where conditioning on certian variables taking specific values is now known as unpacking
Navascués and Wolfe 2020
. These are particularly useful to distinguish correlations from different theories in a specific framework. Here we formulate such inequalities for relativistically causal correlations. The method is inspired by
Chaves and Budroni 2016
, and allows us to directly compare the inequalities from this approach to those arising from the full non-signalling correlations.

For this, let us consider a network of input/output variables
(X_{i},A_{i})
that can take values
x_{i}\in\mathcal{X}_{i}
and
a_{i}\in\mathcal{A}_{i}
respectively. A simple way to see how this procedure works is by drawing a network involving all of these variables with arrows between inputs and outcomes.

Now for each
{X}_{i}
in the network we identify all variables
A_{j}
of which
{X}_{i}
influenced the correlations with some other variables
A_{k}
and we add arrows from
X_{i}
to all of these variables. After this, we consider each
A_{j}
and we create a copy of this variable for each combination of values the variables influencing it can take. For instance, if
A_{j}
is influenced in this network by
X_{j},X_{k},X_{l}
(meaning that there is an arrow pointing to it from those variables), then we create
|\mathcal{X}_{j}|\cdot|\mathcal{X}_{k}|\cdot|\mathcal{X}_{l}|
copies of
A_{j}
, indexed by the values
x_{j},x_{k},x_{l}
, namely
A_{j}(x_{j},x_{k},x_{l})
. These represent random variables with distributions
P(a_{j}|x_{j},x_{k},x_{l})
.

Now among all the variables
\{A_{i}(\cdot)\}_{i}
we define sets of coexisting variables, which are those that agree in the value that the
X_{j}
that they share as an index take and we drop all such sets that are subsets of others, thus leaving us with maximal coexisting sets. These are exactly the sets of variables for which we can define joint conditional distributions
P(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}|x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
in the network we built. For each of these we build an entropy vector.

Now, each of these entropy vectors has to satisfy all Shannon inequalities (see above). In addition, the relativistic causality constraints impose relations among the entropies: If a variable,
X_{j}
, affects
only
the correlations of another,
A_{i}
, the entropy of
A_{i}
will not depend on
X_{j}
, i.e.,
H(A_{i}(x))=H(A_{i}({x^{\prime}}))
for two values
x,x^{\prime}
of
X_{j}
. It may furthermore happen that some of the variables, say
A_{i},A_{j}
are independent (meaning in this case that they have no shared parent in the network), which leads to constraints
H(A_{i}(\cdot)A_{j}(\cdot))=H(A_{i}(\cdot))+H(A_{j}(\cdot))
. Note furthermore that the entropy vectors share some of the variables, so that we obtain an overall system of linear entropy inequalities by combining the inequalities from all the maximal coexisting sets.

Now we can use Fourier-Motzkin elimination to obtain constraints that only involve the variables we are interested in. We illustrate this whole procedure with an example.
