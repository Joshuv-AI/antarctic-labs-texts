Abstract
We study monotone P1 finite element methods on unstructured meshes for fully non-linear, degenerately parabolic Isaacs equations with isotropic diffusions arising from stochastic game theory and optimal control and show uniform convergence to the viscosity solution. Elliptic projections are used to manage singular behaviour at the boundary and to treat a violation of the consistency conditions from the framework by Barles and Souganidis by the numerical operators. Boundary conditions may be imposed in the viscosity or in the strong sense, or in a combination thereof. The presented monotone numerical method has well-posed finite dimensional systems, which can be solved efficiently with Howard’s method.



1
Introduction

Hamilton–Jacobi–Isaacs equations, in short Isaacs equations, arise from stochastic game theory and optimal control, in particular from stochastic two player zero-sum games
[
Sou99
]
. The solution of the Isaacs equation corresponds under appropriate assumptions to a value function of the game. The equations are of the structure

\displaystyle-\partial_{t}v+\adjustlimits{\inf}_{\beta}{\sup}_{\alpha}(L^{(\alpha,\beta)}v-f^{(\alpha,\beta)})=0,

(1)

where
\inf\sup
is taken over a family of second-order linear degenerately elliptic differential operators
L^{\alpha,\beta}
.

Isaacs equations combine a number of features which are challenging from the numerical point of view. They are fully nonlinear in non-divergence form and the nonlinearity is in general non-convex. Their spatial differential operators may only be degenerately elliptic and often do not exhibit smoothness and structure properties to make linearisation a usable tool. Furthermore, the theory of well-posedness relies, in the setting of viscosity solutions, on comparison principles, which in turn make it natural to impose monotonicity requirements on the numerical methods. Boundary conditions, such as of Dirichlet type, can be posed in multiple non-equivalent forms and comparison principles may rely on a particular form to ensure that they hold.

In this work we present a finite element method to solve time-dependent Isaacs equations with isotropic, possibly degenerate diffusions. We show the convergence to the viscosity solution on Lipschitz domains. Dirichlet boundary conditions are imposed in the viscosity or in the strong sense, or in a combination thereof. The presented analysis is a generalisation of
[
JS13
]
, where Bellman equations with homogeneous, strong Dirichlet boundary conditions were approximated.

Compared to the literature for typical quasilinear differential equations, the scientific discourse on the numerical approximation of second-order Isaacs equations consists of a small number of papers. A two-scale finite element method for Isaacs equations was proposed in
[
SZ19
]
, where Alexandrov–Bakelman–Pucci estimates are used to prove the convergence with rates of numerical approximations to solutions of uniformly elliptic Isaacs equations on convex domains. Such two-scale finite element methods are closely related to semi-Lagrangian schemes
[
LN18
]
, for which convergence rates for Isaacs operators on unbounded domains were given in
[
DJ13
]
, see also the series of works
[
Jak04
]
,
[
Jak06
]
,
[
JK02
]
,
[
JK05
]
as well as
[
MZB06
]
in this context. Finite difference methods for uniformly parabolic Isaacs equations on smooth bounded domains with Dirichlet boundary conditions were shown to converge with rates in
[
Kry15
]
and
[
Tur15
]
. Deterministic two-player zero-sum games lead to first-order Isaacs equations. We refer for the numerical approximation of deterministic problems to
[
BCJ19
]
,
[
BHT11
]
,
[
Fal06
]
,
[
FK14
]
and the references therein. An important field of application is
H^{\infty}
control, for which we highlight
[
BM98
]
,
[
FRS10
]
,
[
KKK20
]
,
[
Sor99
]
.

The main contributions of the paper are the following:

Convergence:
We present a finite element method which is guaranteed to converge to the viscosity solution of the Isaacs equation, including in the degenerate case. Domains only need to be Lipschitz regular.

Boundary conditions:
Dirichlet boundary conditions are treated in the viscosity and strong sense, so that the numerical analysis can follow the requirements arising from the construction of comparison principles.

Barrier functions:
Barrier functions are used to ensure that upper and lower semi-continuous envelopes of the numerical solutions locally satisfy the boundary conditions in the strong sense. The construction of barrier functions is characterized in the degenerate setting and on non-convex domains.

Stability:
Boundary data is mapped onto the approximation space with elliptic projections in order to avoid instabilities, which may arise for instance in the presence of re-entrant corners of the domain.

This paper is organised as follows. In Section
2
we specify the class of problems under consideration, introduce a notion of viscosity solution and discuss the two types of Dirichlet boundary conditions. In Section
3
we define the numerical scheme. In Section
4
we establish monotonicity properties; in Section
5
we show the existence and uniqueness of the numerical solutions; in Section
6
we ensure stability; in Section
7
we verify that the upper and lower semi-continuous envelopes of numerical solutions are sub- and supersolutions, respectively. In Section
8
we return to the different notions of the boundary conditions. In particular, we introduce barrier functions which ensure that boundary conditions can be satisfied in the strong sense at least on a part of the boundary. With that we can state the convergence result in Section
9
. In Section
10
we show the construction of barrier functions in uniformly parabolic as well as in degenerately parabolic cases. Finally, in Section
11
we conclude with numerical experiments verifying the convergence of the scheme and we show an application to a two-player stochastic game.

2
Isotropic Isaacs equations

We consider Isaacs equations on bounded Lipschitz domains
{\Omega}\in\mathds{R}^{d}
with
d\geq 2
. Let
A
and
B
be compact metric spaces and
(\alpha,\beta)\in A\times B
. We introduce the linear operators

L^{(\alpha,\beta)}:\;H^{2}({\Omega})\to L^{2}({\Omega}),\;w\mapsto-a^{(\alpha,\beta)}\,\Delta w-b^{(\alpha,\beta)}\cdot\nabla w+c^{(\alpha,\beta)}\,w

(2)

and data terms
f^{(\alpha,\beta)},v_{T}\in C(\overline{{\Omega}})
,
g\in\{w\in W^{1,\infty}(\mathds{R}^{d}):\Delta w\in L^{\infty}({\Omega})\}
. The mapping

\displaystyle A\times B\to C(\overline{{\Omega}})\times C(\overline{{\Omega}},\mathds{R}^{d})\times C(\overline{{\Omega}})\times C(\overline{{\Omega}}),

\displaystyle(\alpha,\beta)\mapsto(a^{(\alpha,\beta)},b^{(\alpha,\beta)},c^{(\alpha,\beta)},f^{(\alpha,\beta)})

is assumed to be continuous such that the families of functions
\{a^{(\alpha,\beta)}\}_{(\alpha,\beta)\in A\times B}
,
\{b^{(\alpha,\beta)}\}_{(\alpha,\beta)\in A\times B}
,
\{c^{(\alpha,\beta)}\}_{(\alpha,\beta)\in A\times B}
and
\{f^{(\alpha,\beta)}\}_{(\alpha,\beta)\in A\times B}
are equicontinuous. Moreover,
a^{(\alpha,\beta)}(x)\geq 0
for any
(\alpha,\beta)\in A\times B
and
x\in{\Omega}
. It follows that all
L^{\alpha,\beta}
are degenerate elliptic and that

\sup_{(\alpha,\beta)\in A\times B}\|\,(a^{(\alpha,\beta)},b^{(\alpha,\beta)},c^{(\alpha,\beta)},f^{(\alpha,\beta)})\,\|_{C(\overline{{\Omega}})\times C(\overline{{\Omega}},\mathds{R}^{d})\times C(\overline{{\Omega}})\times C(\overline{{\Omega}})}<\infty.

For any sufficiently smooth
w
we define the Hamiltonian

Hw:=\adjustlimits{\inf}_{\beta\in B}{\sup}_{\alpha\in A}(L^{(\alpha,\beta)}w-f^{(\alpha,\beta)}),

assuming that the supremum and the infimum are applied pointwise. We wish to study the Isaacs problems of the following form:

\displaystyle-\partial_{t}v+Hv

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\qquad\text{in }(0,T)\times{\Omega},

(3a)

\displaystyle v

\displaystyle=g

\displaystyle\qquad\text{on }(0,T)\times\partial{\Omega},

(3b)

\displaystyle v

\displaystyle=v_{T}

\displaystyle\qquad\text{on }\{T\}\times\overline{{\Omega}},

(3c)

where
v_{T}\in L^{\infty}({\Omega})
and
v_{T}|_{\partial{\Omega}}=g|_{\partial{\Omega}}
. Even though only the values of
g|_{\partial{\Omega}}
appear in (
3
) and
v_{T}|_{\partial{\Omega}}=g|_{\partial{\Omega}}
it remains valuable to distinguish
g
and
v_{T}
: While
v_{T}
is only assumed to be uniformly bounded, we require that
g
can be written as the trace of a function from the more regular space
\{w\in W^{1,\infty}(\mathds{R}^{d}):\Delta w\in L^{\infty}({\Omega})\}
.

Typically there does not exist a smooth solution allowing a classical interpretation of (
3
). Our aim is to construct a finite element method which approximates the viscosity solution of the Isaacs problem (
3
). We now formalise what is meant by a viscosity solution throughout this chapter. Firstly, let us reformulate (
3
) as
F=0
where
F
is the differential operator

\displaystyle F(t,x,q,p,r,s)=

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{rllll}\!\!\!-r+{\displaystyle\inf_{\beta}\sup_{\alpha}}\bigl(-a^{(\alpha,\beta)}(x)\,q-b^{(\alpha,\beta)}(x)\!\cdot\!p+c^{(\alpha,\beta)}(x)\,s-f^{(\alpha,\beta)}(x)\bigr)&:x\in{\Omega},t<T,\\
s-g(x)&:x\in\partial{\Omega},t<T,\\[5.69054pt]
s-v_{T}(x)&:t=T.\end{array}\right.

Given a bounded function
v:[0,T]\times\overline{{\Omega}}\to\mathds{R}
we define its upper and lower semi-continuous envelopes, respectively, as

v^{*}(t,x):=\limsup_{\begin{subarray}{c}(s,y)\to(t,x)\\
(s,y)\in[0,T]\times\overline{{\Omega}}\end{subarray}}v(s,y)

and

v_{*}(t,x):=\liminf_{\begin{subarray}{c}(s,y)\to(t,x)\\
(s,y)\in[0,T]\times\overline{{\Omega}}\end{subarray}}v(s,y).

We analogously extend the definition of lower- and upper semicontinuous envelopes to
F
.

Definition
2.2
below imposes Dirichlet boundary conditions in the viscosity sense on all of
\partial{\Omega}
in the flavour of the original Barles-Souganidis proof, see also
[
BS91
,
JS18
]
. For boundary value problems arising from concrete applications, such viscosity boundary conditions may not be strong enough to establish a comparison principle. We therefore introduce a subset
\omega\subset\partial{\Omega}
on which boundary conditions hold in a stronger sense.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
g:\partial{\Omega}\to\mathds{R}
and
v_{T}:\overline{{\Omega}}\to\mathds{R}
be bounded functions. We say that a bounded function
v:[0,T]\times\overline{{\Omega}}\to\mathds{R}
satisfies the Dirichlet boundary conditions
v(t,x)=g(x)
at
x\in\partial{\Omega}
strongly if, for all
t\in[0,T]
,

v^{*}(t,x)=g(x)\qquad\text{and}\qquad v_{*}(t,x)=g(x).

Similarly, we say that
v
satisfies the final time conditions at
x\in\overline{{\Omega}}
strongly if

v^{*}(T,x)=v_{T}(x)\qquad\text{and}\qquad v_{*}(T,x)=v_{T}(x).

Depending on the setting
\omega=\emptyset
,
\omega=\partial{\Omega}
or
\emptyset\subsetneqq\omega\subsetneqq\partial{\Omega}
may be most appropriate choices.

Definition 2.2
.

A bounded function
v
is a viscosity supersolution (respectively, subsolution) of (
3
) strongly on
\omega\subset\partial{\Omega}
if, for any test function
\psi\in C^{2}(\mathds{R}\times\mathds{R}^{d})
,

\displaystyle F^{*}(t,x,\Delta\psi(t,x),\nabla\psi(t,x),\partial_{t}\psi(t,x),v_{*}(t,x))\geq 0,

(4)

(respectively,

\displaystyle\left.F_{*}(t,x,\Delta\psi(t,x),\nabla\psi(t,x),\partial_{t}\psi(t,x),v^{*}(t,x))\leq 0,\right)

(5)

provided that
v_{*}-\psi
attains at
(t,x)\in[0,T)\times\overline{{\Omega}}
a local minimum relative to
[0,T)\times\overline{{\Omega}}
(respectively,
v^{*}-\psi
attains a local maximum) and additionally
v_{*}
(respectively
v^{*}
) satisfies the final time conditions and the boundary conditions on
\omega
in the strong sense.
A function which is simultaneously a viscosity super- and subsolution of (
3
) is called a viscosity solution.

The motivation for incorporating strong boundary conditions in the definition of viscosity solutions can be illustrated with a simple example.

Example 2.1
.

We investigate on
{\Omega}=(-1,1)
the Bellman equation

\displaystyle\sup_{\beta\in\mathds{R}}\beta\cdot v^{\prime}(x)-\beta^{2}/4-(x^{2}-x^{4})^{2}=0

(6)

with homogeneous Dirichlet boundary conditions. Identifying the structure of a Fenchel conjugate in the
\beta
-dependent terms, we can rewrite the PDE as
|v^{\prime}(x)|^{2}-(x^{2}-x^{4})^{2}=0
. Taking the square root, we may simplify the expression to the eikonal equation
|v^{\prime}(x)|=x^{2}-x^{4}
with a double-well potential on the right-hand side.

An elementary calculation shows that

v(x)=\frac{|x|^{5}}{5}-\frac{|x|^{3}}{3}+\frac{2}{15}

is twice continuously differentiable, solves (
6
) classically and satisfies the boundary conditions strongly.

For
c\leq 0
we define

v_{c}:\;\overline{{\Omega}},\;x\mapsto\begin{cases}v(x)\quad&:x\in{\Omega},\\
c\quad&:x\in\partial{\Omega}.\end{cases}

We initially assume that
\omega
of Definition
2.2
is equal to the empty set and show that
v_{c}
is in that case a viscosity solution for all
c\leq 0
. In particular the viscosity solution is then not unique as required by the construction in
[
BS91
]
and there cannot exists a corresponding comparison principle.

We observe that
(v_{c})^{*}=v
on
\overline{{\Omega}}
, whereas
(v_{c})_{*}=v_{c}
since
v_{c}
is lower semi-continuous. Thus it is clear that
v_{c}
is a viscosity subsolution of the problem.

We now prove that
v_{c}
is also a supersolution. We must show that for all
\psi\in C^{2}
such that
(v_{c})_{*}-\psi
has a local minimum
x
we have as in
[
CIL92
, equation (7.10)]

\displaystyle\textstyle\sup_{\beta}\beta\cdot\psi^{\prime}(x)-\beta^{2}/4-(x^{2}-x^{4})^{2}\phantom{\}}

\displaystyle\geq 0,

\displaystyle x\in{\Omega},

(7a)

\displaystyle\max\{(v_{c})_{*},\textstyle\sup_{\beta}\beta\cdot\psi^{\prime}(x)-\beta^{2}/4-(x^{2}-x^{4})^{2}\}

\displaystyle\geq 0,

\displaystyle x\in\partial{\Omega}.

(7b)

Inequality (
7a
) holds because
v_{c}
is a classical solution on
{\Omega}
. Remembering that
x^{2}-x^{4}
vanishes on
\partial{\Omega}
and the simplification to the eikonal form, the boundary case (
7b
) can be simplified to

\displaystyle\max\{c,|\psi^{\prime}(x)|^{2}\}\geq 0,\qquad x\in\partial{\Omega},

which holds trivially.

Instead, setting
\omega=\partial{\Omega}
we enforce
(v_{c})_{*}=v_{c}=0
on the boundary, which filters out all the spurious solutions with
c<0
and uniqueness is regained.

Similar examples can also be constructed with second-order equations arising from diffusive problems and in higher dimensions, e.g. see
[
JS18
, Section 2]
. While the loss of uniqueness of solutions directly interacts with the ability to formulate comparison principles, the flexibility of viscosity solutions to break continuity in the vicinity of the boundary can be helpful in some circumstances, e.g. when examining singularly perturbed diffusion-advection equations which exhibit a boundary layers for small diffusion coefficients
\varepsilon>0
and may fully detach from the Dirichlet data when
\varepsilon\to 0
.

We conclude this section by relating our definition of strong boundary conditions to that in
[
CIL92
, Section 7.A]
. There an upper semi-continuous function
v:\overline{{\Omega}}\to\mathds{R}
is a subsolution of the boundary conditions
B(x,v(x),Du(x),D^{2}u(x))=0
at
x\in\partial{\Omega}
if

\displaystyle B(x,v(x),p,X)\leq 0\quad\text{for}\quad(p,X)\in\overline{J}_{\overline{{\Omega}}}^{2,+}v(x).

(8)

Similarly, a lower semi-continuous
v:\overline{{\Omega}}\to\mathds{R}
is a subsolution of the boundary conditions at
x\in\partial{\Omega}
if

\displaystyle B(x,v(x),p,X)\geq 0\quad\text{for}\quad(p,X)\in\overline{J}_{\overline{{\Omega}}}^{2,-}v(x).

(9)

In our case
B(x,q,p,s)=s-g(x)
and we refer for the definition of the closed second-order jets
\overline{J}_{\overline{{\Omega}}}^{2,+}u(x)
,
\overline{J}_{\overline{{\Omega}}}^{2,-}u(x)
to
[
CIL92
]
.

Comparing (
8
) and (
9
) to Definitions
2.1
and
2.2
, we first of all make an observation which is not directly related to the boundary conditions: we adopt in this paper the notion of bounded, but not necessarily semi-continuous sub- and supersolutions, following
[
BP88
]
and
[
BS91
]
, see also
[
FS06
, Chapter VII]
. Hence, to bridge this difference, we can adapt the definition of
[
CIL92
]
to

\displaystyle B(x,v^{*}(x),p,X)\leq 0\quad\text{for}\quad(p,X)\in\overline{J}_{\overline{{\Omega}}}^{2,+}v(x)

(10)

and

\displaystyle B(x,v_{*}(x),p,X)\geq 0\quad\text{for}\quad(p,X)\in\overline{J}_{\overline{{\Omega}}}^{2,-}v(x).

(11)

Now if the closed second-order jets of
v
are non-empty, then (
10
) implies
v^{*}(x)\leq g(x)
and (
11
) implies
v_{*}(x)\geq g(x)
. Since
v^{*}\geq v_{*}
by construction, we arrive at
v^{*}(x)=v_{*}(x)=g(x)
, mirroring Definition
2.1
.

3
The numerical method

We consider a sequence
V_{i},i\in\mathds{N}
, of piecewise linear shape-regular finite element spaces. Let us denote the nodes of the finite element mesh by
{y}_{i}^{\ell}
where
\ell
is the index over the set of nodes. The associated hat functions are denoted
{\phi}_{i}^{\ell}
, i.e.
{\phi}_{i}^{\ell}\in V_{i}
such that
\phi_{i}^{\ell}(y_{i}^{\ell})=1
while
{\phi}_{i}^{\ell}(y_{i}^{s})=0
for all
\ell\neq s
. Set
\hat{\phi}_{i}^{\ell}:={\phi}_{i}^{\ell}/\|{\phi}_{i}^{\ell}\|_{L^{1}({\Omega})}
. Therefore, the
{\phi}_{i}^{\ell}
are normalised in the
L^{\infty}
norm whilst the
\hat{\phi}_{i}^{\ell}
are normalised in the
L^{1}
norm.

Let
V_{i}^{0}\subset V_{i}
be the subspace of functions which satisfy the homogeneous Dirichlet conditions on
\partial{\Omega}
. Throughout the text we let the index
\ell
range over the boundary nodes first, in other words,
{y}_{i}^{\ell}\in{\Omega}
for
\ell\leq N_{i}:=\dim V_{i}^{0}
.

In order to construct a finite element method which is both stable and consistent, care needs to be taken when imposing
non-homogeneous
boundary conditions. Due to the more delicate stability properties of Isaacs operators compared to Bellman operators, on non-convex domains a nodal interpolant is not suitable to map the boundary data onto the approximation space.

Given
w\in C(H^{1}({\Omega}))
, we consider therefore linear mappings
{P_{i}}
which map
w
into
V_{i}
such that, for all
\hat{\phi}_{i}^{\ell}\in V_{i}^{0}
,

\langle\nabla{P_{i}}w,\nabla\hat{\phi}_{i}^{\ell}\rangle=\langle\nabla w,\nabla\hat{\phi}_{i}^{\ell}\rangle.

(12)

Assumption 3.1
.

There are linear mappings
{P_{i}}
satisfying (
12
) and there is a constant
C\geq 0
such that for every
w\in C^{\infty}(\mathds{R}^{d})
and
i\in\mathds{N}
,

\left\|{P_{i}}w\right\|_{W^{1,\infty}({\Omega})}\leq C\left\|w\right\|_{W^{1,\infty}({\Omega})}\quad\text{and}\quad\lim_{i\rightarrow\infty}\left\|{P_{i}}w-w\right\|_{W^{1,\infty}({\Omega})}=0.

(13)

It is shown in
[
DLSW12
]
that Assumption
3.1
holds if we chose
{P_{i}}w
such that it interpolates
w
on the boundary, (
3.1
) is satisfied,
{\Omega}
is a bounded
convex polyhedral
domain in
\mathds{R}^{d}
,
d\in\{2,3\}
, and the mesh satisfies a local quasi-uniformity condition.

To apply the result for non-convex domains
{\Omega}
and general
w\in C^{\infty}(\mathds{R}^{d})
, we consider a convex polyhedral domain
B
containing
{\Omega}
and assume there is a locally quasi-uniform mesh on
B
which coincides with the original mesh on
{\Omega}
. Let
\eta
be a smooth cut-off function with relatively compact support in
B
such that
\eta\equiv 1
on
{\Omega}
. Then the classical elliptic projection on
B
, acting on
\eta w:B\to\mathds{R}
, has the required properties. Given this construction for
{P_{i}}
, it is natural to refer to it as an elliptic projection, see also
[
JS13
, Section 4]
. This construction provides on non-convex domains an approximation of the boundary data, which permits the proof of stability of numerical solutions, see Theorem
6.1
below, as well as of consistency, see Lemma
3.1
. In conclusion, let
V_{i}^{g}\subset V_{i}
be the subspace of functions which attain
{P_{i}}g
on the boundary.

The mesh size, i.e. the largest diameter of an element, is denoted
\Delta x_{i}
. It is assumed that
\Delta x_{i}\to 0
as
i\to\infty
. The uniform time step size is denoted
h_{i}
with the constraint that
T/h_{i}\in\mathds{N}
. It is assumed that
h_{i}\to 0
as
i\to\infty
. Let
{s}_{i}^{k}
be the
k
th time step at the refinement level
i
. Then the set of time steps is
S_{i}:=\bigl\{{s}_{i}^{k}:k=T/h_{i},\dots,0\bigr\}
.

The time derivative is approximated by
d_{i}
for which we let the
\ell
th entry of
d_{i}w({s}_{i}^{k},\cdot)
be

(d_{i}w({s}_{i}^{k},\cdot))_{\ell}=\frac{w({s}_{i}^{k+1},{y}_{i}^{\ell})-w({s}_{i}^{k},{y}_{i}^{\ell})}{h_{i}}.

For the discretisation of
L^{(\alpha,\beta)}
we allow a splitting into an explicit and an implicit part. For each pair
(\alpha,\beta)
and for each
i
, we introduce the explicit operator
{E}_{i}^{(\alpha,\beta)}
and the implicit operator
{I}_{i}^{(\alpha,\beta)}
such that
L^{(\alpha,\beta)}\approx{E}_{i}^{(\alpha,\beta)}+{I}_{i}^{(\alpha,\beta)}
and

\displaystyle{E}_{i}^{(\alpha,\beta)}

\displaystyle:\;H^{2}({\Omega})\to L^{2}({\Omega}),\;w\mapsto-\bar{a}_{i}^{(\alpha,\beta)}\,\Delta w-\bar{b}_{i}^{(\alpha,\beta)}\cdot\nabla w+\bar{c}_{i}^{(\alpha,\beta)}\,w,

\displaystyle{I}_{i}^{(\alpha,\beta)}

\displaystyle:\;H^{2}({\Omega})\to L^{2}({\Omega}),\;w\mapsto-\bar{\bar{a}}_{i}^{(\alpha,\beta)}\,\Delta w-\bar{\bar{b}}_{i}^{(\alpha,\beta)}\cdot\nabla w+\bar{\bar{c}}_{i}^{(\alpha,\beta)}\,w.

For each
i
we also seek a discretisation
{f}_{i}^{(\alpha,\beta)}
of
f^{(\alpha,\beta)}
. We now want to make the conceptual statements
L^{(\alpha,\beta)}\approx{E}_{i}^{(\alpha,\beta)}+{I}_{i}^{(\alpha,\beta)}
and

more precise.

Assumption 3.2
.

The splitting is consistent in the sense that

\displaystyle\lim_{i\to\infty}\sup_{(\alpha,\beta)\in A\times B}

\displaystyle\bigl(\sup_{0\leq\ell\leq N_{i}}\bigl\|a^{(\alpha,\beta)}-\bigl(\bar{a}_{i}^{(\alpha,\beta)}({y}_{i}^{\ell})+\bar{\bar{a}}_{i}^{(\alpha,\beta)}({y}_{i}^{\ell})\bigr)\bigr\|_{L^{\infty}({\rm supp}\,\hat{\phi}_{i}^{\ell})}

\displaystyle+\bigl\|b^{(\alpha,\beta)}-\bigl(\bar{b}_{i}^{(\alpha,\beta)}+\bar{\bar{b}}_{i}^{(\alpha,\beta)}\bigr)\bigr\|_{L^{\infty}({\Omega},\mathds{R}^{d})}

\displaystyle+\bigl\|c^{(\alpha,\beta)}-\bigl(\bar{c}_{i}^{(\alpha,\beta)}+\bar{\bar{c}}_{i}^{(\alpha,\beta)}\bigr)\bigr\|_{L^{\infty}({\Omega})}+\bigl\|f^{(\alpha,\beta)}-{f}_{i}^{(\alpha,\beta)}\bigr\|_{L^{\infty}({\Omega})}\bigr)=0.

The coefficients
\bar{c}_{i}^{(\alpha,\beta)}
and
\bar{\bar{c}}_{i}^{(\alpha,\beta)}
are non-negative and that there exists
\gamma\in\mathds{R}
such that

\displaystyle\|\bar{c}_{i}^{(\alpha,\beta)}\|_{L^{\infty}}+\|\bar{\bar{c}}_{i}^{(\alpha,\beta)}\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq\gamma,\qquad\forall\,i\in\mathds{N},\;\forall\,(\alpha,\beta)\in A\times B.

(14)

The family of mappings

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{ll}A\times B\to L^{\infty},\;(\alpha,\beta)\mapsto(\bar{a}_{i}^{(\alpha,\beta)},\bar{b}_{i}^{(\alpha,\beta)},\bar{c}_{i}^{(\alpha,\beta)},\bar{\bar{a}}_{i}^{(\alpha,\beta)},\bar{\bar{b}}_{i}^{(\alpha,\beta)},\bar{\bar{c}}_{i}^{(\alpha,\beta)},f_{i}^{(\alpha,\beta)})\end{array}

is continuous for each
i
.

The splitting into explicit and implicit part is used to define the internal numerical operators
{\mathsf{E}}_{{\Omega},i}^{(\alpha,\beta)}
and
{\mathsf{I}}_{{\Omega},i}^{(\alpha,\beta)}
as mappings from
H^{1}({\Omega})
to
\mathds{R}^{\dim V_{i}}
:

\displaystyle({\mathsf{E}}_{{\Omega},i}^{(\alpha,\beta)}w)_{\ell}

\displaystyle:=\bar{a}_{i}^{(\alpha,\beta)}({y}_{i}^{\ell})\langle\nabla w,\nabla\hat{\phi}_{i}^{\ell}\rangle+\langle-\bar{b}_{i}^{(\alpha,\beta)}\cdot\nabla w+\bar{c}_{i}^{(\alpha,\beta)}\,w,\hat{\phi}_{i}^{\ell}\rangle,

(15a)

\displaystyle({\mathsf{I}}_{{\Omega},i}^{(\alpha,\beta)}w)_{\ell}

\displaystyle:=\bar{\bar{a}}_{i}^{(\alpha,\beta)}({y}_{i}^{\ell})\langle\nabla w,\nabla\hat{\phi}_{i}^{\ell}\rangle+\langle-\bar{\bar{b}}_{i}^{(\alpha,\beta)}\cdot\nabla w+\bar{\bar{c}}_{i}^{(\alpha,\beta)}w,\hat{\phi}_{i}^{\ell}\rangle,

(15b)

\displaystyle({\mathsf{F}}_{{\Omega},i}^{(\alpha,\beta)})_{\ell}

\displaystyle:=\langle{f}_{i}^{(\alpha,\beta)},\hat{\phi}_{i}^{\ell}\rangle,

(15c)

where
\ell
ranges over all internal nodes, i.e.
\ell\leq N_{i}
. For boundary nodes

4
Monotonicity

Monotonicity properties of the numerical scheme play a crucial role in ensuring convergence to the viscosity solution.

Definition 4.1
.

An operator
F:V_{i}\to\mathds{R}^{N_{i}}
is said to satisfy the local monotonicity property (LMP) if for all
v\in V_{i}
such that
v
has a non-positive local minimum at the internal node
{y}_{i}^{\ell}
, we have
(Fv)_{\ell}\leq 0
. The operator
F
satisfies the weak discrete maximum principle (wDMP) provided that for any
v\in V_{i}
,

\text{if \,}\bigl(Fv\bigr)_{\ell}\geq 0\text{ for all }\ell\in\{1,\dots,N_{i}\},\quad\text{ then }\min_{{\Omega}}v\geq\min\{\min_{\partial{\Omega}}v,0\}.

(25)

Suppose an operator
F
satisfies the LMP. If
v\in V_{i}
has a negative local minimum at an internal node
{y}_{i}^{\ell}
, then
((F+\epsilon{\sf Id})v)_{\ell}<0
for any positive
\epsilon
. In particular, the contrapositive of (
25
) holds. Therefore
F+\epsilon{\sf Id}
satisfies the wDMP for any positive
\epsilon
.

Assumption 4.1
.

For each
(\alpha,\beta)\in A\times B
, we assume that
{\mathsf{E}}_{i}^{(\alpha,\beta)}
, restricted to
V_{i}
, has non-positive off-diagonal entries. We assume that
h_{i}
is small enough so that
h_{i}{\mathsf{E}}_{i}^{(\alpha,\beta)}-{\sf Id}
is monotone for every
(\alpha,\beta)
, i.e. so that all entries of all
h_{i}{\mathsf{E}}_{i}^{(\alpha,\beta)}-{\sf Id}
are non-positive. For each
(\alpha,\beta)\in A\times B
, we suppose that
{\mathsf{I}}_{i}^{(\alpha,\beta)}
satisfies the LMP.

We now turn to the matrices
h_{i}{\mathsf{I}}_{i}^{k,w}+{\sf Id}
and
h_{i}{\mathsf{E}}_{i}^{k,w}-{\sf Id}
which will later be used in the proof of the well-posedness of the scheme (

Lemma 4.1
.

Consider a

so that

for all

6
Stability

For Hamilton–Jacobi–Bellman equations one can bound the value function by the solution of any linear evolution problem associated to a fixed control. In contrast the value function of an Isaacs equation may lie in part above and in part below the solution of such a linear problem. This difference between the Bellman and Isaacs equations extends to the proof of stability of numerical solutions. We begin by adapting
[
JS13
, Lemma 3.2]
.

Lemma 6.1
.

One has
\lVert(h_{i}{\mathsf{I}}_{i}^{w}+{\sf Id})^{-1}\rVert_{\infty}\leq 1
and
\lVert h_{i}{\mathsf{E}}_{i}^{w}-{\sf Id}\rVert_{\infty}\leq 1
for all
i\in\mathds{N}
and
w\in V_{i}
, where the norms are the matrix
\infty
-norms of linear mappings from
V_{i}
into
V_{i}
and
V_{i}^{0}
into
V_{i}^{0}
, respectively.

Proof.

Define
v=\sum_{\ell=1}^{\dim V_{i}}{\phi}_{i}^{\ell}\equiv 1
, and
v_{0}=\sum_{\ell=1}^{N_{i}}{\phi}_{i}^{\ell}\in V_{i}^{0}
. By Lemma
4.1
,
h_{i}{\mathsf{I}}_{i}^{w}+{\sf Id}
is an invertible M-matrix on
V_{i}
. Thus,
(h_{i}{\mathsf{I}}_{i}^{w}+{\sf Id})^{-1}\geq 0
entrywise on
V_{i}
. Thus

\begin{split}\lVert(h_{i}{\mathsf{I}}_{i}^{w}+{\sf Id})^{-1}\rVert_{\infty}&=\max_{1\leq\ell\leq\dim V_{i}}\sum_{j=1}^{\dim V_{i}}(h_{i}{\mathsf{I}}_{i}^{w}+{\sf Id})^{-1}_{\ell j}=\max_{1\leq\ell\leq\dim V_{i}}\left((h_{i}{\mathsf{I}}_{i}^{w}+{\sf Id})^{-1}\mathbf{1}\right)_{\ell},\end{split}

(29)

where
\mathbf{1}\in\mathds{R}^{\dim V_{i}}
is the vector with all entries equal to
1
.

Since
\nabla v\equiv 0
(as
v\equiv 1
) we have for each
1\leq\ell\leq N_{i}
that

((h_{i}{\mathsf{I}}_{i}^{w}+{\sf Id})v)_{\ell}=1+h_{i}\langle\bar{\bar{c}}_{i}^{(\alpha_{i}^{\ell}(w),\beta^{\ell}(w))},\hat{\phi}_{i}^{\ell}\rangle\geq 1,

where we have used non-negativity of
\bar{\bar{c}}_{i}^{\alpha}
and defined
(\alpha_{i}^{\ell}(w),\beta^{\ell}(w))
analogously to
(\alpha_{i}^{k,\ell}(w),\beta_{i}^{k,\ell}(w))
in (
18
). Similarly, for each boundary node index
N_{i}<\ell\leq\dim V_{i}

((h_{i}{\mathsf{I}}_{i}^{w}+{\sf Id})v)_{\ell}=1+h_{i}\geq 1.

It follows that
(h_{i}{\mathsf{I}}_{i}^{w}+{\sf Id})v\geq\mathbf{1}
. Combining positive invertibility of
(h_{i}{\mathsf{I}}_{i}^{w}+{\sf Id})
with (
29
), we conclude that
\lVert(h_{i}{\mathsf{I}}_{i}^{w}+{\sf Id})^{-1}\rVert_{\infty}\leq 1
, as required.

Turning our attention to explicit operators, one has

\lVert h_{i}{\mathsf{E}}_{i}^{w}-{\sf Id}\rVert_{\infty}=\max_{1\leq\ell\leq N_{i}}\bigl(-(h_{i}{\mathsf{E}}_{i}^{w}-{\sf Id})\,v_{0}\bigr)_{\ell}

because all entries of the matrix
h_{i}{\mathsf{E}}_{i}^{w}-{\sf Id}
are non-positive. For each
1\leq\ell\leq N_{i}
,

\bigl((h_{i}{\mathsf{E}}_{i}^{w}-{\sf Id})\,v\bigr)_{\ell}=\bigl((h_{i}{\mathsf{E}}_{i}^{w}-{\sf Id})\,v_{0}\bigr)_{\ell}+\sum_{j=N_{i}+1}^{\dim V_{i}}\left(h_{i}{\mathsf{E}}_{i}^{w}-{\sf Id}\right)_{\ell j}\leq\bigl((h_{i}{\mathsf{E}}_{i}^{w}-{\sf Id})\,v_{0}\bigr)_{\ell},

so

(-(h_{i}{\mathsf{E}}_{i}^{w}-{\sf Id})\,v_{0})_{\ell}\leq(-(h_{i}{\mathsf{E}}_{i}^{w}-{\sf Id})\,v)_{\ell}=1-h_{i}\langle\bar{c}_{i}^{(\alpha_{i}^{\ell}(w),\beta^{\ell}(w))},\hat{\phi}_{i}^{\ell}\rangle\leq 1

because
\bar{c}_{i}^{(\alpha_{i}^{\ell}(w),\beta^{\ell}(w))}\geq 0
where
\bar{c}_{i}^{(\alpha_{i}^{\ell}(w),\beta^{\ell}(w))}
is defined analogously to
\bar{\bar{c}}_{i}^{(\alpha_{i}^{\ell}(w),\beta^{\ell}(w))}
above. Therefore,
-(h_{i}{\mathsf{E}}_{i}^{w}-{\sf Id})v_{0}\leq\mathbf{1}
. So
\lVert h_{i}{\mathsf{E}}_{i}^{w}-{\sf Id}\rVert_{\infty}\leq 1
.
∎

Theorem 6.1
.

The numerical solutions
v_{i}
are uniformly bounded in the
L^{\infty}

\displaystyle\left\|v_{i}\right\|_{L^{\infty}(S_{i}\times{\Omega})}\leq

\displaystyle\left\|{P_{i}}v_{T}\right\|_{L^{\infty}({\Omega})}+2\lVert P_{i}g\rVert_{L^{\infty}({\Omega})}

In order to understand the connection between convergence of the envelopes to the boundary conditions and the barrier functions let us consider the following example.

Example 8.1
.

We study a
1
-dimensional test problem with the homogeneous boundary conditions:

\displaystyle-\partial_{t}u+\nabla u-1

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle\text{in }[0,T)\times(0,1),

\displaystyle u

\displaystyle=x,

\displaystyle\text{on }\{T\}\times(0,1).

In order to find solution we consider a fully implicit numerical scheme with artificial diffusion. In this spirit, the exact solution of
-u_{t}-\upsilon\Delta u+\nabla u-1=0
, interpreting
\upsilon
as the artificial diffusion coefficient, is:

v_{\upsilon}(t,x)=x-1+\frac{e^{\frac{x}{\upsilon}}-e^{\frac{1}{\upsilon}}}{1-e^{\frac{1}{\upsilon}}}.

For a fixed
\upsilon
we can choose
\Delta x
small enough such that the numerical solution
\tilde{v}_{\upsilon}
with artificial diffusion
\upsilon
and uniform mesh with mesh size
\Delta x
satisfies
\left\|\tilde{v}_{\upsilon}-v_{\upsilon}\right\|_{W^{1,\infty}}<\upsilon
. We note that
v_{\upsilon}
attains its maximum at

x_{\mathrm{max}}=\upsilon\log\left[\upsilon\left(e^{\frac{1}{\upsilon}}-1\right)\right]

with maximal value equal to

v_{\upsilon}(t,x_{\mathrm{max}})=\frac{1}{e^{\frac{1}{\upsilon}}-1}+\upsilon\log\left[\upsilon\left(e^{\frac{1}{\upsilon}}-1\right)\right].

In particular, it follows that
x_{\mathrm{max}}\to 1
and
v_{\upsilon}(t,x_{\mathrm{max}})\to 1
as
\upsilon\to 0
. Since

\left\|\tilde{v}_{\upsilon}-v_{\upsilon}\right\|_{W^{1,\infty}}<\upsilon,

we conclude that for a decreasing artificial diffusion coefficient the sequence of numerical solutions
\tilde{v}_{\upsilon}
has the lower semi-continuous envelope

\underline{v}(t,x)=\begin{cases}x&\quad x\neq 1,\\
0&\quad x=1.\end{cases}

(35)

However, since
\underline{v}
has a discontinuity at
1
, there cannot exist a Lipschitz continuous barrier function as described in Assumption
8.1
– as we decrease
\upsilon
,
v_{\upsilon}
and hence
\tilde{v}_{\upsilon}
exhibit an arbitrarily large gradient in the vicinity of the boundary. As a result, for any barrier function
\xi
there exists

for which the maximum of
\tilde{v}_{\upsilon}-P_{i}\xi_{y,\epsilon}
does not lie on the boundary for all

. Note that we can construct both upper and lower barrier functions at

, e. g.
\zeta_{0}=x^{2}
and
\xi_{0}=(x+1)^{2}
.

Lemma 8.1
.

Given Assumption
8.1

\overline{v}(t,x)=\underline{v}(t,x)=g(t,x)

\displaystyle\limsup_{i\rightarrow\infty}v_{i}({s}_{i}^{k},{y}_{i}^{\ell})=

\displaystyle\xi_{y,\epsilon}(t,y)+\limsup_{i\rightarrow\infty}\sup_{z\in\partial{\Omega}}\Big[{P_{i}}g(t,z)-P_{i}\xi_{y,\epsilon}(t,z)\Big.

\displaystyle\left.+\left({P_{i}}g({s}_{i}^{k},z)-{P_{i}}g(t,z)\right)-\left(P_{i}\xi_{y,\epsilon}({s}_{i}^{k},z)-P_{i}\xi_{y,\epsilon}(t,z)\right)\right]

\displaystyle\xi_{y,\epsilon}(t,y)+\limsup_{i\rightarrow\infty}\sup_{z\in\partial{\Omega}}[\left(g(t,z)-\xi_{y,\epsilon}(t,z)\right)

\displaystyle+\left({P_{i}}g(t,z)-g(t,z)\right)-\left(P_{i}\xi_{y,\epsilon}(t,z)-\xi_{y,\epsilon}(t,z)\right)]

where we get (a) due to Lipschitz continuity of

and

) we obtain

\displaystyle\left((h_{i}{\mathsf{I}}_{i}^{k,v_{i}}+{\sf Id})(P_{i}\xi-v_{i}({s}_{i}^{k},\cdot))\right)_{\ell}

\displaystyle\geq(P_{i}\xi)_{\ell}-v_{i}({s}_{i}^{k+1},{y}_{i}^{\ell}).

(43)

Because both
(P_{i}\xi)_{\ell}
and
v_{i}({s}_{i}^{k},{y}_{i}^{\ell})
interpolate
g
on
\partial{\Omega}
, it follows
P_{i}\xi-v_{i}({s}_{i}^{k},\cdot)\in V_{i}^{0}
. Owing to the M-matrix property of
h_{i}{\mathsf{I}}_{i}^{k,v_{i}}+{\sf Id}
,

\displaystyle(P_{i}\xi)_{\ell}-v_{i}({s}_{i}^{k+1},{y}_{i}^{\ell})\geq 0

(44)

thus implies condition (
41
). Hence, by induction, (
41
) holds for all
k
as soon as (
44
) is shown at the final time
{s}_{i}^{k+1}=T
.

From (
40
) we know that
-\lambda\Delta\xi\geq M
. Then, using
v_{i}(T,\cdot)={P_{i}}v_{T}
and
\lambda>0
,

\displaystyle\langle\nabla\left({P_{i}}\xi^{T}-v_{i}(T,\cdot)\right),\nabla\hat{\phi}_{i}^{\ell}\rangle

\displaystyle=-\langle\Delta(\xi-v_{T}),\hat{\phi}_{i}^{\ell}\rangle

\displaystyle\geq\langle M/\lambda+\Delta v_{T},\hat{\phi}_{i}^{\ell}\rangle\geq M/\lambda-\lVert\Delta v_{T}\rVert_{L^{\infty}({\Omega})}.

The definition of
M
ensures that
\frac{M}{\lambda}\geq\lVert\Delta v_{T}\rVert_{L^{\infty}({\Omega})}
. Using M-matrix property of the discrete Laplacian formed with respect to the nodal basis
\{\hat{\phi}_{i}^{\ell}\}_{\ell}
we obtain (
44
) at the final time.

At this point we proved that the maximum of
v_{i}({s}_{i}^{k},\cdot)-P_{i}\xi
lies on the boundary as required. It remains to show that for
t\in[0,T]
we can select a maximiser
r_{t,y,\epsilon}
of
g-\xi_{y,\epsilon}
over
\{t\}\times\partial{\Omega}
such that
\lim_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0}\ r_{t,y,\epsilon}=y
and
\lim_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0}\xi_{y,\epsilon}(t,y)-\xi_{y,\epsilon}\ (t,r_{t,y,\epsilon})\leq 0
. Because
\xi
attains
g
on whole the boundary,
r_{t,y,\epsilon}:=y
is already such a choice.

10.1.2
IMEX numerical schemes

We now extend the above argument to general numerical schemes as defined in (
19
). Choosing
\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2},\lambda
and
M
as above, the inequality (
43
) generalises in the IMEX setting to

\displaystyle\left((h_{i}{\mathsf{I}}_{i}^{k,v_{i}}+{\sf Id})(P_{i}\xi-v_{i}({s}_{i}^{k},\cdot))\right)_{\ell}

\displaystyle\geq-\left(\!(h_{i}{\mathsf{E}}_{i}^{k,v_{i}}-{\sf Id})(P_{i}\xi-v_{i}({s}_{i}^{k+1},\cdot))\!\right)_{\ell}.

According to Lemma
4.1
we have
-(h_{i}{\mathsf{E}}_{i}^{k,v_{i}}-{\sf Id})\geq 0
. Therefore, if

P_{i}\xi-v_{i}({s}_{i}^{k+1},\cdot))\geq 0

implies (
41
). At this point the induction of the previous subsection can be adapted to show that maxima are attained on the boundary. Setting

completes the construction.

11
Numerical experiments

In this section we present two numerical experiments showing the viability of the presented method. In the first experiment, we analyse convergence rates of a fully nonlinear second order Isaacs problem with a known solution and we confirm at least linear convergence in
L^{2}
,
L^{\infty}
and
H^{1}
norms. In the second experiment we calculate the value function of a stochastic tag-chase game with asymmetric velocities and vanishing diffusion on a non-convex domain. The presented finite element method was implemented in Python with FEniCS
[
LMW+12
]
and is available from the public repository
[
Jar21
]
under the GNU Lesser General Public License.

11.1
Isaacs problem with exact solution

Let the spatial domain
{\Omega}
in
\mathds{R}^{2}
be the equilateral triangle with vertices
(\pm\sqrt{3},\ \frac{1}{2})
and
(0,\-1)
. We will study the following Isaacs problem:

-v_{t}-\inf_{\beta\in[\frac{1}{4},\ \frac{1}{2}]}\Bigl\{\beta\sqrt{\frac{x^{2}+y^{2}}{T-t+1}}\Delta v+\frac{1}{2}\frac{1}{\sqrt{T-t+1}}\left|\nabla v\right|\Bigr\}=-\frac{1}{2}\frac{\sqrt{x^{2}+y^{2}}}{(T-t+1)^{3/2}}.

(49)

Equation (
49
) is indeed of Isaacs type because the Euclidean norm of the gradient may be written alternatively as

\left|\nabla v\right|=\sup_{\{\alpha\in\mathds{R}^{2}:\left|\alpha\right|=1\}}\{\alpha\cdot\nabla v\}.

One can now verify through direct calculation that the function

\displaystyle v(x,y,t)=\exp\left(-\sqrt{\frac{x^{2}+y^{2}}{T-t+1}}\right)+\sqrt{\frac{x^{2}+y^{2}}{T-t+1}}

(50)

solves (
49
) exactly. The final and boundary data is also given by the right-hand side of (
50
).

We split the scheme so that the advection term is treated explicitly. Note that in order to ensure the monotonicity of the scheme we may also assign part of the diffusion to the explicit operator. In order to improve the rate of convergence this was done locally, i.e. on a node-wise basis. In case the naturally occurring diffusion at the node is not sufficient, we introduce artificial diffusion. Overall, this approach leads to artificial diffusion near the origin where the differential operator is degenerately elliptic, while for the majority of nodes of the mesh it is zero. We choose the largest timestep guaranteeing the monotonicity of the method which leads to
O(h_{i})=O(\Delta x_{i})
. Any amount of the diffusion left after ensuring the monotonicity of the explicit operators is treated implicitly.

The convergence of the scheme is reflected on Figure
1
which plots errors at
t=0
for different mesh sizes.

11.2
Tag-Chase Game with random noise

Imagine two players moving on the
R^{2}
plane. One player is the pursuer (we will denote them by P) and tries to catch the other one, who is the evader (we will denote them by E). Both of them are allowed to choose their direction freely:
\alpha,\beta\in[-\pi,\pi]
and
\alpha
is the direction chosen by the evader, while
\beta
is the direction chosen by the pursuer. The pursuer moves with speed
v^{\beta}_{P}
while the evader moves with speed
v^{\alpha}_{E}
. In particular, the respective speeds are functions of the angles. Additionally, some choices of a direction for the pursuer and the evader may be subject to a random noise behaving like a standard Brownian motion. Having specified the setting we are able to formulate the dynamics explicitly as

\displaystyle dx_{1}^{P}

\displaystyle=(v^{\beta}_{P})_{1}\,\sin(\beta)dt+\sigma^{\beta}_{P}(x_{1}^{P})\,dW_{(1,P)},

\displaystyle dx_{2}^{P}

\displaystyle=(v^{\beta}_{P})_{2}\,\cos(\beta)dt+\sigma^{\beta}_{P}(x_{2}^{P})\,dW_{(2,P)},

\displaystyle dx_{1}^{E}

\displaystyle=(v^{\alpha}_{E})_{1}\,\sin(\alpha)dt+\sigma^{\alpha}_{E}(x_{1}^{E})\,dW_{(1,E)},

\displaystyle dx_{2}^{E}

\displaystyle=(v^{\alpha}_{E})_{2}\,\cos(\alpha)dt+\sigma^{\alpha}_{E}(x_{2}^{E})\,dW_{(2,E)}.

Additionally, we assume that

\displaystyle W_{P}

\displaystyle=(W_{(1,P)},W_{(2,P)}),

\displaystyle W_{E}

\displaystyle=(W_{(1,E)},W_{(2,E)}),

are two
R^{2}
-valued, mutually independent standard Wiener processes. We reduce this
4
-dimensional problem to a
2
-dimensional one by allowing the origin of the coordinate system to move along with the pursuer. In this case our dynamics are

\displaystyle d\hat{x}_{1}

\displaystyle=((v^{\beta}_{P})_{1}\,\sin(\beta)-(v^{\alpha}_{E})_{1}\,\sin(\alpha))\,dt+\sigma^{(\alpha,\beta)}(\hat{x}_{1})\,d\hat{W}_{1},

\displaystyle d{\hat{x}_{2}}

\displaystyle=((v^{\beta}_{P})_{2}\,\cos(\beta)-(v^{\alpha}_{E})_{2}\,\cos(\alpha))\,dt+\sigma^{(\alpha,\beta)}(\hat{x}_{2})\,d\hat{W}_{2},

where
\sigma^{(\alpha,\beta)}(\hat{x}_{i})=\sqrt{\sigma^{\beta}_{P}(x^{P}_{i}))^{2}+(\sigma^{\alpha}_{E}(x^{E}_{i}))^{2}}
and
\hat{W}
is an
R^{2}
-valued standard Wiener process satisfying

\sigma^{(\alpha,\beta)}(\hat{x}_{i})\hat{W}_{i}(t)=\sigma^{\beta}_{P}(x_{i}^{P})W_{i,P}(t)-\sigma^{\alpha}_{E}(x_{i}^{E})W_{i,E}(t),\quad t\geq 0,\;i=1,2.

The pursuer catches the evader (and thus wins the game) if they manage to reduce their distance from the evader to some value
r\in R
. The evader wins the game when they manage to increase the distance to pursuer to some given
R>r
or if they manage to avoid the capture before some time
T
. Note that in this case the spatial domain of the problem becomes
\overline{{\Omega}}:=\overline{B}(0,R)\setminus B(0,r)
.

Mathematically, the evader receives the pay-out of
1
whenever they win the game and receive
0
otherwise. We write for the expected pay-out to the evader
\mathcal{J}(\vec{\alpha},\vec{\beta},x,t)
, where
\vec{\alpha}:[0,T]\to A
,
\vec{\beta}:[0,T]\to B
are functions of time so that
\vec{\alpha}(t)
,
\vec{\beta}(t)
are the controls chosen by the evader and pursuer at time
t
, respectively.

The value function

v(x,t):=\adjustlimits{\inf}_{\beta}{\sup}_{\alpha}\mathcal{J}(\alpha,\beta,x,t)

then solves the second-order Isaacs equation

\displaystyle-\partial_{t}v+\inf_{\beta}\sup_{\alpha}\big(-a^{(\alpha,\beta)}\Delta v-b^{(\alpha,\beta)}\cdot\nabla v\big)=0\qquad

\displaystyle\text{on }[0,T)\times{\Omega},

\displaystyle v=1\qquad

\displaystyle\text{on }[0,T)\times\{x:\|x\|=R\},

\displaystyle v=0\qquad

\displaystyle\text{on }[0,T)\times\{x:\|x\|=r\},

\displaystyle v=1\qquad

\displaystyle\text{on }\{T\}\times\overline{{\Omega}},

where

a^{(\alpha,\beta)}:=\frac{(\sigma^{(\alpha,\beta)})^{2}}{2},\qquad b^{\alpha,\beta}:=\begin{pmatrix}(v_{P}^{\beta})_{1}\,\sin(\beta)-(v_{E}^{\alpha})_{1}\,\sin(\alpha)\\
(v_{P}^{\beta})_{2}\,\cos(\beta)-(v_{E}^{\alpha})_{2}\,\cos(\alpha)\end{pmatrix}.

We shall assume that the pursuer is faster than the evader when moving in the horizontal direction. Moreover, we assume that the diffusion in the upper part of the domain scales with the vertical position
x_{2}
, while it is constant where
x_{2}\leq 0.1
. Specifically, this corresponds to the following choice of the diffusion and advection coefficients:

\displaystyle a^{(\alpha,\beta)}=\max\{x_{2},0.1\},\qquad b^{(\alpha,\beta)}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}4\sin(\beta)-0.5\sin(\alpha)\\
\cos(\beta)-\cos(\alpha)\end{pmatrix}.

The numerical approximation of the value function is displayed in Figure
2
. Note the asymmetric nature of the graph, due to the different speeds of the pursuer and the evader in the horizontal direction as well as a larger effect of the stochastic component of the equation in the upper part of the domain.
