The Combinatorial Loewner Property and super-multiplicativity inequalities for symmetric self-similar metric spaces
Riku Anttila, Sylvester Eriksson-Bique

Abstract
. This paper introduces a general construction of self-similar metric spaces as limits of discrete graphs. Our framework produces many classical examples, such as the Sierpiński carpet and the higher dimensional Menger sponges, but also a rich class of new examples. The main result of the work roughly speaking states: If the construction is sufficiently symmetric then the limiting object supports useful moduli estimates, namely the Combinatorial Loewner property of Bourdon–Kleiner and the super-multiplicativity inequalities. The latter are established on Menger sponges for which it had not been previously known. The main new technique the work offers is a general framework of flows and resistance estimates.

1.
Introduction

1.1.
Overview

This paper introduces a general construction of symmetric self-similar metric spaces as a limit of discrete graphs. We analyze the quasiconformal geometry and non-linear potential theory of these limit spaces using the fairly common tool in the related literature, the so-called
combinatorial
/
discrete modulus
.

We are particularly interested, in the general context, in understanding when a metric space supports a certain family of scale-invariant moduli estimates, namely the
Combinatorial Loewner property (CLP)
of Bourdon–Kleiner
[
14
]
. This question is motivated to deepen our understanding on the conjecture posed by Kleiner
[
34
]
, stating that a self-similar metric space carrying the combinatorial Loewner property is quasisymmetrically equivalent to a Loewner space of Heinonen–Koskela
[
29
]
.

The conjecture was recently disproved by the authors
[
2
]
but Kleiner’s question, nevertheless, remains open and relevant for many interesting examples. See for instance Bourdon–Kleiner
[
14
]
and Clais
[
23
]
for a large class of boundaries of hyperbolic groups where the conjecture is open, Bourdon–Pajot
[
15
]
for group boundaries where the answer is positive. If a group boundary is homeomorphic to the 2-sphere then, according to the results of Bonk–Kleiner
[
9
,
10
]
, Kleiner’s question is closely related to Cannon’s conjecture in geometric group theory.
See also the recent work of Murugan and Shimizu
[
48
]
which connects Kleiner’s question on the Sierpiński carpet, which remains open, to potential theory and Sobolev spaces.
Lastly, the recent work of the second author and G. C. David
[
24
]
shows that positive answer to Kleiner’s question on the pillow space (Example
1.3
) would lead to a quite pathological differentiability structure of the Euclidean plane.

The main goal of the present work is to produce new self-similar metric spaces and verify their combinatorial Loewner property, with the aim to support future research on Kleiner’s conjecture in the general context.
We introduce new resistance/flow methods and employ them to establish delicate moduli estimates, in particular the
super-multiplicativity inequality
.
Our flow techniques can be understood as a generalization of Barlow–Bass
[
5
]
and Kwapisz
[
38
]
.

1.2.
Basic framework

Let us begin by providing a rough description of our construction of the limit spaces through some examples;
the method is quite similar to the one recently introduced by the authors
[
2
]
, but therein construction is only able to produce topologically one dimensional objects.
It is also unable to produce the following model example of the present work, the Sierpiński carpet.
The general construction is described in Section
3
in terms of the language of
(vertex)-iterated graph systems
.

Example 1.1

(
Sierpiński carpet
)
.

Sierpiński carpet is a self-similar fractal which can be obtained by removing smaller squares from the unit square
[0,1]^{2}
in an iterative fashion. It is also an attractor of an iterated function system in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
consisting of eight contraction maps.

Figure 1.
Construction of the Sierpiński carpet as a limit of graphs.

However, in the present work, we are interested in a different construction; the first steps are drawn in Figure
1
. It goes as follows.
First, take the left most graph in Figure
1
which consists of eight vertices and eight edges, and name it
G_{1}
.
The graph in the middle of the figure, say
G_{2}
, is constructed by first replacing each vertex in
G_{1}
by
G_{1}
. Then each horizontal/vertical edge in
G_{1}
is replaced by three horizontal/vertical edges. This produces
G_{2}
. The third graph
G_{3}
is obtained by applying an analogous replacement procedure to
G_{2}
, namely we replace each vertex in
G_{2}
by
G_{1}
and edges in a simlar way.
By repeating, we obtain an infinite sequence
\{G_{n}=(V^{n},E_{n})\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of self-similar graphs.
The limit space is obtained by taking a suitable limit of the rescaled path metrics,

(X,d)=\lim_{n\to\infty}(V^{n},3^{-n}d_{G_{n}})

where the limit can be understood in a (subsequential) Gromov–Hausdorff sense.
The limit space is biLipschitz equivalent to the Sierpiński carpet.

Similar replacement construction can be used to produce many other classical examples. For instance, by adding dimensions to the graphs, we can produce higher dimensional carpets, namely the
d
-dimensional Menger sponges for
d\geq 3
, and also the broader class of generalized Sierpiński carpets; see
[
7
]
for the definition.
We can also consider other shapes as well. The present work introduces a new pentagonal self-similar metric space.

Example 1.2

(
Pentagonal carpet
)
.

The replacement rule in Figure
2
produces a pentagonal fractal space which we call the
pentagonal carpet
.

Figure 2.
First three steps in the construction of the pentagonal carpet

It is homeomorphic to the Sierpiński carpet, thus the name pentagonal carpet, but its geometry and analysis seems to be rather different from the standard carpet.
A driving reason is the difference of symmetry groups.
The limit can be obtained by taking a suitable rescaled limit,

(X,d)=\lim_{n\to\infty}(V^{n},2^{-n}d_{G_{n}}).

We are also interested in the following fractal space which can be understood as a two-dimensional variant of the Laakso diamond space; see
[
39
,
40
,
54
,
25
]
for some studies on the Laakso diamond.

Example 1.3

(
Pillow space
)
.

The
Pillow space
is self-similar metric space that can be constructed in a similar manner as the Sierpiński carpet with the difference that the middle squares are doubled instead of being removed. Of course, such a procedure is impossible to perform inside the Euclidean plane. Instead, the construction is done in an abstract setting.

Figure 3.
First two steps in the construction of the pillow space.

This idea was inspired by the Laakso diamond space, which can be constructed via similar doubling procedure of intervals.
In Figure
3
we construct the pillow space as a limit of graphs. The limit is, again, defined as a rescaled limit of graphs,

(X,d)=\lim_{n\to\infty}(V^{n},3^{-n}d_{G_{n}}).

3
provides the precise definitions and detailed descriptions of the constructions, and the rest of the main examples are introduced in Section

.

1.3.
Main results

First, we need some terminology; we remark that some of the notation and definitions below are slightly different from what we use in later sections for expository reasons.
Consider a general compact path-connected metric space
(X,d)
.
For a given positive integer
n\in\mathbb{N}
we fix a
2^{-n}
-net
N_{n}\subseteq X
, meaning the balls
B(x,2^{-n})
for
x\in N_{n}
cover
X
and
d(x,y)\geq 2^{-n}
for all distinct
x,y\in N_{n}
. Let us denote the collections of open balls
G_{n}:=\{B(x,2^{-n})\}_{x\in N_{n}}
. Note that
G_{n}
is always finite because
(X,d)
is compact.

Then take any family of (continuous and compact) curves
\Gamma
in
X
.
By the term
curve
we refer to a subset
\gamma\subseteq X
which is an image of a continuous map
[0,1]\to X
. Now, the
discrete modulus
of
\Gamma
is defined as follows.
For a given function
\rho:G_{n}\to[0,\infty)
, we say that
\rho
is
\Gamma
-admissible
if for all
\gamma\in\Gamma
,

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}v\in G_{n}\\
\gamma\cap v\neq\emptyset\end{subarray}}\rho(v)\geq 1.

Then, for a given
p\geq 1
, the
G_{n}
-discrete
p
-modulus
is

\Mod_{p}(\Gamma,G_{n}):=\inf_{\rho}\sum_{v\in G_{n}}\rho(v)^{p}

where the infimum is taken over all
\Gamma
-admissible functions
\rho
.
The value
\Mod_{p}(\Gamma,G_{n})
in general depends on the parameters
p,\,\Gamma
and
G_{n}
, but the dependence on
G_{n}
is very mild;
see
[
14
, Proposition 2.2]
which is also stated in Proposition
2.12
of the present work.

The most important discrete moduli for the present work are

(1.4)

\cM_{p,n}:=\Mod_{p}(\Gamma_{\delta},G_{n})\text{ where }\Gamma_{\delta}:=\{\gamma:\diam(\gamma)\geq\delta\},

where
\delta>0
is some fixed sufficiently small constant.
We are interested in the asymptotic behavior of
\cM_{p,n}
as
n\to\infty
.
By definition,
\Gamma_{\delta}
contains the large scale geometric data of the ambient space
(X,d)
, but fails to see the scales smaller than
\delta
. However, in a setting where small scale geometry is identical to large scale, or in other words when the ambient space is self-similar,
\Gamma_{\delta}
is particularly useful. For instance, Carrasco Piaggio and Keith–Kleiner showed that, by analyzing the asymptotic behavior of
\cM_{p,n}
for different
p\geq 1
, one can determine the
conformal dimension
of the ambient space; see
[
21
, Corollary 1.4]
, and this is stated in Proposition
2.15
of the present work as-well. We note that the result of Carrasco Piaggio is far more general than we indicate here.
See also
[
55
,
32
,
26
,
47
]
for related results.

We now state the first main result of the work, which regards an important regularity estimate of the discrete moduli, the
super-multiplicativity inequality
; see Theorem
8.1
for the general result and the proof.

Theorem 1.5
.

Let
(X,d)
be a
d
-dimensional Menger sponge for
d\geq 3
, the pentagonal carpet, the pillow space or more generally any metric space satisfying the assumptions in Theorem
8.1
. For
p\geq 1
let
\cM_{p,n}
be as we defined above. Then the following super-multiplicativity inequality holds. There is a constant
C\geq 1
such that for all
n,m\in\mathbb{N}
,

(1.6)

C^{-1}\cM_{p,n}\cdot\cM_{p,m}\leq\cM_{p,n+m}\leq C\cM_{p,n}\cdot\cM_{p,m}.

The constant
C
depends on the ambient space and continuously on
p\in[1,\infty)
.

Frankly, super-multiplicativity only refers to the lower inequality, and the upper inequality is called
sub-multiplicativity inequality
. We nevertheless wrote both for expository reasons, but also because sub-multiplicativity is virtually always true in self-similar setting; see
[
14
, Proposition 1.5]
.

Super-multiplicativity has numerous applications.
Let us review a few notable ones; we provide further details in Section
9
.
Bourdon–Kleiner proved the super-multiplicativity inequality for the Sierpiński carpet, and used it to establish the Combinatorial Loewner property.
They also verified the Combinatorial Loewner property for the higher dimensional Menger sponges, but using a weaker variant of the super-multiplicativity; see
[
14
, Section 4]
. The present work resolves super-multiplicativity of Menger sponges for all
p\geq 1
.
We note that, for exclusively
p=2
, this was already known by
[
44
]
.

In the recent work of Murugan and Shimizu
[
48
]
, where they constructed Sobolev spaces on the Sierpiński carpet using the Kusuoka–Zhou apporach
[
37
]
, the main reason why their methods did not generalize to Menger sponges was the lack of super-multiplicativity; see
[
48
, Remark 8.24]
. By combining the results of the present work with the techniques of Murugan–Shimizu, it should be possible to construct Sobolev spaces also on Menger sponges.
However, we note that some of their techniques have a certain dimension restriction, which is a problem when
p\in(1,\infty)
is very small; see
[
48
, Problem 10.1]
.
In
\mathbb{R}^{n}
this restriction can be understood as
p>n-1
and is related to the fact that in this range curves have positive capacity.
The present work does not resolve this deep issue.
We also note that, with even a more restrictive dimensional condition, namely
p
being strictly larger than the conformal dimension, the framework of Kigami
[
33
]
and the earlier work of Shimizu
[
57
]
already provides good Sobolev spaces also on the Menger sponges. This dimensional condition corresponds to
p>n
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, which is supported by the validity of Morrey-type inequality
[
33
, Theorem 3.21]
.

The last application we mention is a special case of what we discussed in the previous paragraph. When
p=2
and the dimensional restriction from Murugan–Shimizu
[
48
, Problem 10.1]
is satisfied, one can perform an analytic construction of diffusion processes and Dirichlet forms. In particular, the method works on the 3-dimensional Menger sponge.
This construction would coincide with the Brownian motion constructed by Barlow–Bass using a probabilistic method
[
4
,
6
]
, because a self-similar diffusion process on the Menger sponge is unique according to
[
7
]
. We note that the probabilistic method of Barlow–Bass does not suffer from the dimension restriction of
[
48
]
.

Next, we state our main result regarding the combinatorial Loewner property. The precise definition is postponed to Section
2
, but roughly speaking the combinatorial Loewner property is a quantitative formulation of the general principle that discrete modulus is scale/conformally invariant. More specifically, a metric space
(X,d)
is
combinatorially
Q
-Loewner
for
Q>1
if for any disjoint pair of connected and compact subsets
E,F\subseteq X
the discrete moduli
\Mod_{Q}(\Gamma(E,F),G_{n})
is controlled by the
relative distance

\Delta(E,F):=\frac{\dist(E,F)}{\diam(E)\land\diam(F)}.

Here
\Gamma(E,F)
denotes the continuous curves connecting
E
and
F
.
This definition was introduced in Bourdon–Kleiner
[
14
]
and it was inspired by the Loewner spaces of Heinonen–Koskela
[
29
]
. Unlike the Loewner condition, the combinatorial Loewner proeprty is a quasisymmetry invariant; see
[
14
, Theorem 2.6]
.

Theorem 1.7
.

Let
(X,d)
be the pentagonal carpet, the pillow space or more generally any metric space satisfying the assumption of Theorem
8.3
.
Then there is a unique
Q>1
such that

C^{-1}\leq\liminf_{n\to\infty}\cM_{Q,n}\leq\limsup_{n\to\infty}\cM_{Q,n}\leq C

for some constant
C\geq 1
. This
Q
is equal to the conformal dimension of
(X,d)
and
(X,d)
satisfies the combinatorial
Q
-Loewner property.

1.4.
Main techniques

The main new technique is the
replacement flow method
.
The general idea and the terminology “replacement flow” was inspired by the work of Kwapisz
[
38
]
where he derived impressive estimates on the conformal dimension of the Sierpiński carpet by studying flows on the graph approximations. See also the related work
[
43
]
.
The key idea is that flows provide lower bounds to discrete moduli through the standard duality; see
[
49
, Theorem 5.1]
and
[
1
]
. We also state a variant of it in Proposition
2.7
.

Flow methods on Sierpiński carpets go back to Barlow–Bass
[
5
]
which was later generalized to higher dimensional carpets by McGillivray
[
44
]
; see also
[
17
]
.
These works exclusively regard the case
p=2
and some of their methods use the classical Harmonic analysis of the Euclidean space. It seems unclear how well their techniques generalize to general exponents
p\in(1,\infty)
, let alone to cases where the ambient space is not a subset of the Euclidean space.
We note that, because we are interested in the Combinatorial Loewner property, we in particular need these techniques for
p
equal to the conformal dimension.
Kwapisz applied the flow method for general exponents
p
but exclusively in the case of the planar Sierpiński carpet.

The replacement flow method is by far the most technical part of the paper. But the basic idea is rather simple. Consider three graphs
G_{n},\,G_{m}
and
G_{n+m}
described in Example
1.1
, for
n,m\in\mathbb{N}
. Assume we are given a flow
\mathcal{J}_{n}
from
S_{n}
to
T_{n}
where
S_{n},\,T_{n}\subseteq V(G_{n})
is a pair of disjoint subsets. The idea of the replacement flow is to produce a flow on
\mathcal{J}_{n+m}
on
G_{n+m}
by suitably “lifting”
\mathcal{J}_{n}
.
This is performed by first constructing a family of flows
\mathcal{B}
on
G_{m}
so that for every pair of the four line segments (as in Figure
1
) in the boundary, there is a flow between them.
In later sections we call
\mathcal{B}
a
flow basis
.
Then the flow
\mathcal{J}_{n+m}
is obtained by suitably embedding the flows on
G_{m}
into
G_{n+m}
by using the self-similarity of the graphs.
We take suitably linear combinations of them and the coefficients are determined by
\mathcal{J}_{n}
. The construction of a flow basis is a bit delicate, and involves substantial use of symmetries to bend and reflect flows.

2.
Preliminaries

2.1.
Conformal dimension

Consider complete metric spaces
(X,d)
. All metric balls
B(x,r):=\{y\in X:d(x,y)<r\}
are open for
x\in X,r>0
.
We usually refer to the metric space
(X,d)
simply by
X
and omit the metric
d
.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
Q\in(0,\infty)
. We say that a metric space
X
is
Q
-
Ahlfors regular
, if there exists a Radon measure
\mu
of
(X,d)
and constant
C\geq 1
such that for all
r\in(0,2\diam(X))
and all
x\in X

C^{-1}\cdot r^{Q}\leq\mu(B(x,r))\leq C\cdot r^{Q}.

If a metric space is
Q
-Ahlfors regular for some
Q\in(0,\infty)
, we simply say that it is
Ahlfors regular
.
A metric space
(X,d)
is
metric doubling
, if there is a constant
N\in\mathbb{N}
such that for every ball
B(x,r)\subset X
there are
x_{1},\dots,x_{N}\in X
for which
B(x,r)\subset\bigcup_{i=1}^{N}B(x,r/2)
. A simple volume counting argument shows that every Ahlfors regular metric space is metric doubling.
It is also well-known that if
X
is Ahlfors regular, then any measure
\mu
as above is comparable to the
Q
-
Hausdorff measure

\cH^{Q}
given by

\cH^{Q}(A):=\lim_{\delta\to 0}\cH^{Q}_{\delta}(A),

where
\cH^{Q}_{\delta}
is the
Hausdorff content

\cH^{Q}_{\delta}(A):=\inf\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\diam(A_{i})^{Q}:A\subset\bigcup_{i=1}^{\infty}A_{i},\diam(A_{i})\leq\delta\right\}.

In particular, the
Hausdorff dimension
of
(X,d)
then is

Q=\dim_{H}(X):=\inf\left\{d>0:\cH^{d}(X)=0\right\}.

Definition 2.2
.

A metric space
X
is
quasiconvex
if there is a constant
C\geq 1
such that for every pair
x,y\in X
there is a continuous curve
\gamma:[0,1]\to X
with
\gamma(0)=x,\gamma(1)=1
and
\len(\gamma)\leq C\cdot d(x,y)
. Here the
length
of
\gamma
is given by

\len(\gamma):=\sup\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{N-1}d(\gamma(x_{i}),\gamma(x_{i+1})):0=x_{1}<x_{2}<\dots<x_{N}=1\right\}.

Next, we introduce the notion of self-similarity relevant to our work. See
[
52
]
for more background.

Definition 2.3
.

If
L\geq 1
a map
f:(X,d_{X})\to(Y,d_{Y})
is an
L
-
biLipschitz map
if for all
x,y\in X

\frac{1}{L}d_{X}(x,y)\leq d_{Y}(f(x),f(y))\leq Ld_{X}(x,y).

A metric space
X
is
approximately self-similar
, if there is a constant
L\geq 1
such that for every
x\in X
and every
r\in(0,\diam(X)]
, there exists an open set
U_{x,r}\subset X
and a
L
-biLipschitz map
f_{x,r}:(B(x,r),d/r)\to U_{x,r}
. Here the ball
B(x,r)
is equipped with the scaled metric
d/r
.

If
L=1
, such a biLipschitz scaling map is called a homothetic homeomorphism with scaling factor
1/r
.

Definition 2.4
.

Given a homeomorphism
\eta:[0,\infty)\to[0,\infty)
, we say that homeomorphism
f:(X,d_{X})\to(Y,d_{Y})
is an
\eta
-
quasisymmetry
if for every
x,y,z\in Z
with
x\neq z
, we have

\frac{d_{Y}(f(x),f(y))}{d_{Y}(f(x),f(z))}\leq\eta\left(\frac{d_{X}(x,y)}{d_{X}(x,z)}\right).

We say that
f:X\to Y
is a
quasisymmetry
, if it is an
\eta
-quasisymmetry for some
\eta
. In these cases, we say that
X
is quasisymmetric to
Y
. For more background on these mappings and the notions below, see
[
28
]
. We will usually omit the subscript of a metric
d
, where the space is clear from context.
The
conformal gauge
of a metric space
X
is given by

\cG(X,d):=\left\{d^{\prime}:d^{\prime}\text{ is a metric on }X\text{ and }\id:(X,d)\to(X,d^{\prime})\text{ is a quasisymmetry}\right\}.

We usually drop the metric from
\cG(X,d)
and simply write
\cG(X)
.

Understanding the structure of the conformal gauge allows one to study different types of
quasisymmetric uniformization problems
, i.e., finding a metric in the conformal gauge more suitable for the problem in question. An important example is to minimize the Hausdorff dimension among Ahlfors regular metrics in the conformal gauge. From this problem, naturally arises the notion of
(Ahlfors regular) conformal dimension
, which is given by

\dims_{\rm AR}(X):=\inf\left\{Q^{\prime}:d^{\prime}\in\cG(X,d)\text{ and }(X,d^{\prime})\text{ is $Q^{\prime}$-Ahlfors regular}\right\}.

Determining whether the above infimum is a minimum is called
the attainment problem of (Ahlfors regular) conformal dimension
.

The Ahlfors regular metrics in
\cG(X)
can be described by quasi-isometries on a certain hyperbolic space, and the conformal dimension is characterized as the critical dimension of suitable discrete moduli problems; see e.g.
[
32
,
55
,
21
]
. Despite this, the attainment problem is notoriously hard, and the answer is known only in some exceptional cases. Finding the exact value of the conformal dimension also tends to be challenging. See
[
38
]
for numerical estimates of the conformal dimension of the Sierpiński carpet, whose exact value of the conformal dimension remains unknown.
See
[
2
]
for examples where the conformal dimension is computable.

Other variants of the conformal dimensions have also been studied. The conformal Hausdorff dimension was first considered by Pansu
[
51
]
, the conformal Assouad dimension was studied in
[
47
]
, the conformal walk-dimension was introduced in
[
30
]
, and the present one, the Ahlfors regular conformal dimension, was introduced in
[
15
]
. For further background on the conformal dimension, see
[
42
]
. The various definitions (except the conformal walk-dimension) are equivalent in self-similar settings; see
[
26
]
. Since all the spaces considered in this paper are self-similar, we refer to the Ahlfors regular conformal dimension simply as the conformal dimension.

2.2.
Terminology of graphs

A
graph
is a pair
G=(V,E)
where
V(G):=V
is a non-empty finite set of
vertices
and
E(G):=E\subseteq V\times V
is the set of edges.
The set of edges are always assumed to satisfy: If
(x,y)\in E
then
(y,x)\notin E
. In particular,
(x,x)\notin E
for all
x\in V
.

Usually, the above definition of a graph is known as oriented simple graph. In our work the given orientations are more or less “artificial” in the sense that they are needed only for describing certain constructions in Subsection
3.2
and are mostly disregarded afterwards. Whenever we only want to emphasize that a pair of vertices is connected by an edge and the orientation is not important, we write the edge as
\{x,y\}
. If the choice of the orientation does not matter, we sometimes leave it unspecified which orientation we have chosen.

For a vertex
x\in V
we define its
degree
as the number of its neighbors, meaning

\deg(x):=\lvert\{y\in V(G):\{x,y\}\in E(G)\}\rvert.

The
degree
of
G
, denoted
\deg(G)
, is then the maximum of the degrees of its vertices.

Next we discuss concepts related to discrete modulus; see e.g.
[
56
,
1
]
for background.
First, a
path
in a graph
G
is a sequence
\theta=[x_{1},\dots,x_{k}]
and
\{x_{i},x_{i+1}\}\in E
for all
i=1,\dots,k-1
when
k>1
. If
k=1
then
\theta
consists of only one vertex. The
length
of a path
\theta
then is
\len(\theta):=k-1
.
Given non-empty subsets
A,B\subseteq V(G)
we denote

\Theta(A,B,G):=\{\theta:\theta=[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]\text{ is a path in }G\text{ and }x_{1}\in A,x_{n}\in B\}.

If
\Theta(A,B,G)\neq\emptyset
whenever
A,B\neq\emptyset
, we say that
G
is
connected
, and denote the
(shortest) path metric
by
d_{G}
.
We also say that a subset
U\subseteq V(G)
is
connected
if the induced subgraph
G[U]
is connected. Furthermore, given a path
[x_{1},\dots,x_{k}]
, without an explicit mention, we sometimes regard it as the connected subset
\{x_{1},\dots,x_{k}\}\subseteq G
.

Definition 2.5
.

Let
G
be a graph and
\rho:V(G)\to[0,\infty)
. If
\theta
is a path we define its
\rho
-length

L_{\rho}(\theta):=\sum_{x\in\theta}\rho(x),

namely we sum over all vertices appearing in the path
\theta
.
Note that each vertex is included at most once in the sum even if it appears multiple times in
\theta
.
Given a family of paths
\Theta
in
G
we say that
\rho:V(G)\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
is
\Theta
-admissible
if
L_{\rho}(\theta)\geq 1
for all
\theta\in\Theta
.
Given a density
\rho:V\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
and
p\geq 1
, the
p
-mass
of
\rho
is given by

\cM_{p}(\rho):=\sum_{x\in V}\rho(x)^{p}.

The
discrete
p
-modulus
of
\Theta
is then given by

\Mod_{p}(\Theta,G):=\inf_{\rho}\cM_{p}(\rho),

where the infimum is over all
\Theta
-admissible densities. For simplicity we write
\Mod_{p}(A,B,G):=\Mod_{p}(\Theta(A,B,G),G)
.

Discrete modulus is a convex minimization problem. When
p>1
then the minimum always exists and is unique.
Because it is a minimization problem, obtaining upper bound estimates is straightforward by constructing admissible densities with suitable mass.
Lower bounds are more involved. Our method for deriving these is to analyze the dual problem of resistance and flows; see
[
56
,
1
,
49
]
for background.

Definition 2.6
.

A function
\mathcal{J}:V\times V\to\mathbb{R}
is
antisymmetric
if it satisfies the following conditions.

(1)

\mathcal{J}(x,y)=-\mathcal{J}(y,x)
for all
x,y\in V
.

(2)

\mathcal{J}(x,y)=0
unless
\{x,y\}\in E
.

The
divergence
of
\mathcal{J}
at
x\in V
is given by

\divr(\mathcal{J})(x):=\sum_{\{x,y\}\in E}\mathcal{J}(x,y).

Given non-empty disjoint subsets
A,B\subseteq V
, an antisymmetric function
\mathcal{J}
is a
flow
from
A
to
B
if

\divr(\mathcal{J})(x)=0\text{ for all }x\notin A\cup B.

The
total flow
is given by

I(\mathcal{J}):=\sum_{x\in A}\divr(F)(x).

If
I(\mathcal{J})=1,
we say that
\mathcal{J}
is a
unit flow
from
A
to
B
.
For
p\in(1,\infty)
, the
p
-energy
of a flow
\mathcal{J}
is

\cE_{q}(\mathcal{J}):=\sum_{\{x,y\}\in E}\lvert\mathcal{J}(x,y)\rvert^{q},

where
q=p/(p-1)
.
The
p
-resistance
between
A
and
B
is

\cRes_{p}(A,B,G):=\inf_{\mathcal{J}}\cE_{q}(\mathcal{J}),

where the infimum is over all unit flows from
A
to
B
.

There is a well-known duality result between resistances and modulus; see
[
49
, Theorem 5.1]
and also
[
1
]
. In our setting, it reads as follows.

Proposition 2.7
.

Let
G
be a connected graph,
A,B\subseteq V(G)
be disjoint non-empty subsets and
p\in(1,\infty)
.
Write
C=2^{p}\deg(G)
. Then

(2.8)

C^{-1}\cRes_{p}(A,B,G)^{1-p}\leq\Mod_{p}(\Theta(A,B),G)\leq C\cRes(A,B,G)^{1-p}

Remark 2.9
.

The standard version of duality (
2.8
) concerns the edge modulus, instead of the vertex counterpart we use. The above version of the duality follows from the fact that edge-modulus and vertex-modulus are comparable;
see
[
2
, Lemma 4.3]
for details.

In our analysis, it will be convenient to expand the graph at sources and targets of the flow. The following lemma provides control over this procedure.

Lemma 2.10
.

Let
G
be a connected graph,
S,T\subseteq V(G)
be disjoint non-empty subsets and
\mathcal{J}
be a flow from
S
to
T
in
G
.
We define an expanded graph
G^{*}
, which is obtained by adding the vertices

V(G^{*}):=V(G)\cup\{x^{*}:x\in A\cup B\},

and the edges

E(G^{*}):=E(G)\cup\{\{x^{*},x\}:x\in A\cup B\}.

We denote
S^{*}:=\{x^{*}:x\in S\}\text{ and }T^{*}:=\{x^{*}:x\in T\}
.
Now, let
J^{*}
be a flow from
S^{*}
to
T^{*}
be a flow obtained by extending
\mathcal{J}
by

\mathcal{J}^{*}(x^{*},x):=\divr(\mathcal{J})(x)=\sum_{\{x,y\}\in E}\mathcal{J}(x,y).

Then there is a constant
C\geq 1
depending only on
p\in(1,\infty)
and
\deg(G)
such that

(2.11)

\cE_{q}(\mathcal{J}^{*})\leq 3\deg(G)^{\frac{1}{p-1}}\cE_{q}(\mathcal{J})

where
q=p/(p-1)
.

Proof.

The claim follows from Hölder’s inequality,

\displaystyle\cE_{q}(\mathcal{J}^{*})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\{x,y\}\in E}\lvert\mathcal{J}^{*}(x,y)\rvert^{q}+\sum_{x\in S\cup T}\left|\widehat{\mathcal{J}}(x^{*},x)\right|^{q}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\{x,y\}\in E}\lvert\mathcal{J}(x,y)\rvert^{q}+\sum_{x\in S\cup T}\left|\sum_{\{x,y\}\in E}\mathcal{J}(x,y)\right|^{q}

\displaystyle\leq\cE_{q}(\mathcal{J})+\deg(G)^{\frac{1}{p-1}}\sum_{x\in S\cup T}\sum_{\{x,y\}\in E}\lvert\mathcal{J}(x,y)\rvert^{q}

\displaystyle\leq(1+2\deg(G)^{\frac{1}{p-1}})\cE_{q}(\mathcal{J})

\displaystyle\leq 3\deg(G)^{\frac{1}{p-1}}\cE_{q}(\mathcal{J}).

∎

2.3.
Graph approximations

Let
X
be metric space. We say that a graph
G=(V,E)
is an
incidence graph
of
X
if
V
is a covering of
X
and
\{u,v\}\in E
if and only if
u\cap v\neq\emptyset
and
u\neq v
. For a given subset
F\subseteq X
we write

V[F]:=\{v\in V:v\cap F\neq\emptyset\}.

If
\Gamma
is a family of subsets of
X
we define

V[\Gamma]:=\{V[\gamma]:\gamma\in\Gamma\}.

In our analysis
\Gamma
is always a collection of (continuous and compact)
curves
, meaning a collection of subset
F=\gamma([0,1])\subseteq X
where
\gamma
is a continuous map
\gamma:[0,1]\to X
.
When
F_{1},F_{2}\subseteq X
are two non-empty sets we denote by
\Gamma(F_{1},F_{2})
the set of continuous curves
\gamma:[0,1]\to X
such that
\gamma(0)\in A
and
\gamma(1)\in B
.
We often identify a continuous curve
\gamma
by their image.

For
\alpha,L_{*}>1
, an
\alpha
-approximation
to a compact metric space
X
is a sequence of incidence graphs
\{G_{n}=(V^{n},E_{n})\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
, such that each graph
G_{n}
satisfies the following conditions.

(1)

For every
v\in V^{n}
there exists a
z_{v}\in v
for which

B(z_{v},\alpha^{-1}L_{*}^{-n})\subset v\subset B(z_{v},\alpha L_{*}^{-n}).

(2)

For every pair of distinct
v,w\in V^{n}
we have

B(z_{v},\alpha^{-1}L_{*}^{-n})\cap B(z_{w},\alpha^{-1}L_{*}^{-n})=\emptyset.

In
[
14
]
, the value
L_{*}=2
is used exclusively, but by adjusting the levels
n\in\mathbb{N}
(by repeating or skipping indices), and the constant
\alpha
, any
\alpha
approximation with
L_{*}>1
also yields an
\alpha^{\prime}
-approximation with
L_{*}=2
for some
\alpha^{\prime}>1
. In the following, it will be however convenient to allow for general
L_{*}
.

Fix
n\in\mathbb{N},p\geq 1
and let
\rho:V^{n}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
. Given a family of continuous curves
\Gamma
in
X
, we say that
\rho
is
\Gamma
-admissible
if

L_{\rho}(\gamma):=\sum_{v\in V^{n}[\gamma]}\rho(v)\geq 1

for all
\gamma\in\Gamma
. Here, the curves are identified by their image.
We define the
G_{n}
-discrete
p
-modulus
of
\Gamma
by

\Mod_{p}(\Gamma,G_{n}):=\inf_{\rho}\cM_{p}(\rho),

where the infimum is over all
\Gamma
-admissible densities
\rho:V^{n}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
.
If
\Gamma=\Gamma(F_{1},F_{2})
for
F_{1},F_{2}\subseteq X
we denote
\Mod_{p}(F_{1},F_{2},G_{n}):=\Mod_{p}(\Gamma(F_{1},F_{2}),G_{n})
.
We note that this notion of modulus is very similar to the one discussed the previous subsection but with the differences that the curves here live in a general metric space rather than on a graph.

The same metric space can have many approximations but a different choice only affects the discrete moduli by a multiplicative constant.

Proposition 2.12

(Proposition 2.2
[
14
]
)
.

Let
\alpha,\alpha^{\prime},L_{*}>1
and
G_{n}
and
G_{n}^{\prime}
be
\alpha
- and
\alpha^{\prime}
-approximations of a compact metric space
X
respectively, and assume
X
satisfies the metric doubling property. Then for all
p\geq 1
there is a constant
C\geq 1
depending only on
\alpha,\alpha^{\prime}
, continuously on
p
and the doubling constant of
X
, such that for all family of paths
\Gamma

(2.13)

C^{-1}\cdot\Mod_{p}(\Gamma,G_{n}^{\prime})\leq\Mod_{p}(\Gamma,G_{n})\leq C\cdot\Mod_{p}(\Gamma,G_{n}^{\prime}).

Keith and Kleiner described the conformal dimension of self-similar spaces as a critical exponent for discrete modulus. Carrasco Piaggio gave an independent proof of this fact in
[
21
]
. This idea appeared already in Pansu’s original paper, where conformal dimension is actually defined as a critical dimension for a certain discrete modulus
[
51
]
. For
\delta>0
we write
\Gamma_{\delta}=\{\gamma:\diam(\gamma)\geq\delta\}
and

(2.14)

\cM_{p,\delta}^{(n)}:=\Mod_{p}(\Gamma_{\delta},G_{n}).

Proposition 2.15

(Corollary 1.4
[
21
]
)
.

Let
X
be an approximately self-similar path-connected metric space. There exists
\delta_{0}>0
such that for all
\delta\in(0,\delta_{0})
we have

\dims_{\rm AR}(X)=\inf\left\{p\geq 1:\lim_{n\to\infty}\cM_{p,\delta}^{(n)}=0\right\}.

This characterization yields a numerical way to estimate conformal dimension. However, since the definition involves a limit, we need some further estimates to give rigorous bounds. Upper bounds for conformal dimension can be obtained through an argument using the sub-multiplicative inequality (
2.17
). Kwapisz used this strategy in
[
38
]
and obtained good numerical estimates on the conformal dimension of the standard Sierpiński carpet.

Proposition 2.16

(Proposition 3.6
[
14
]
)
.

Let
X
be compact, path-connected and approximately self-similar metric space. There exists
\delta_{0}>0
such that for all
p\in[1,\infty)
and
\delta\in(0,\delta_{0})
there is a constant
C
depending on
\delta
and continuously on
p\in[1,\infty)
such that for all
n,m\in\mathbb{N}
,

(2.17)

\cM_{p,\delta}^{(n+m)}\leq C\cM_{p,\delta}^{(n)}\cM_{p,\delta}^{(m)}.

for all
n,m\in\mathbb{N}
.

In Section
8
we consider the reverse inequality, the
super-multiplicative inequality
. In contrast to sub-multiplicativity, which is virtually always true in self-similar settings, establishing super-multiplicativity will be much more involved and requires additional assumptions.

Definition 2.18
.

Let
Q,L_{*},\alpha>1
and
\{G_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be an
\alpha
-approximation of a compact approximately self-similar metric space.
We say that
X
satisfies the
combinatorial

-Loewner property
if there exists two positive increasing functions
\phi,\psi
on
(0,\infty)
with
\lim_{t\to 0}\psi(t)=0
, which satisfies the following two conditions.

(CLP1)

If
F_{1},F_{2}\subseteq X
are two disjoint non-degenerate continua such that
L_{*}^{-n}\leq\diam(F_{1})\land\diam(F_{2})
, then for all
m\in\mathbb{N}

\phi(\Delta(F_{1},F_{2})^{-1})\leq\Mod_{Q}(F_{1},F_{2},G_{n+m}),

where
\Delta(F_{1},F_{2})
is the
relative distance

\Delta(F_{1},F_{2}):=\frac{\dist(F_{1},F_{2})}{\diam(F_{1})\land\diam(F_{2})}.

(CLP2)

If
x\in X
,
r\geq L_{*}^{-n}
and

then for all

\Mod_{Q}\left(B(x,r),X\setminus B(x,(C+1)r)\right),G_{n+m})\leq\psi(C^{-1}).

The definition of the combinatorial Loewener property given above slightly differs from the one in
[

]
. This version appeared first in
[

]
, although
(CLP2)
was written there in a slightly different, but equivalent, form.

3.
Iterated graph systems

In this section, we introduce the core concepts of the paper, the iterated graph systems (IGSs) and replacement graphs. Similar notions have been considered in many works with different names, among them hierarchical graph systems, hierarchical graph products and self-similar graphs
[
50
,
41
,
58
,
36
,
53
,
27
,
22
]
.
The term “iterated graph systems” originates from the work of Neroli Z.
[
50
]
.

3.1.
Definitions and notations

Definition 3.1
.

An
iterated graph system (IGS)
is a collection of different classes of data.
It consists of a connected graph
G
, which we refer to as the
generator
, a finite non-empty set
\mathcal{T}
of
types
, a surjective
typing function

\mathfrak{t}:E\to\cT
and, finally, each type
t\in\mathcal{T}
is associated a
gluing rule
.
For each
t\in\mathcal{T}
the gluing rule consists of a pair of non-empty disjoint sets
I_{t,+},I_{t,-}\subseteq V(G)
and bijective functions
\alpha_{t}:I_{t,+}\cup I_{t,-}\to I_{t,+}\cup I_{t,-}
which satisfies
\alpha_{t}(I_{t,+})=I_{t,-}
and
\alpha^{2}_{t}=\id_{V(G)}
.

When we specify the the data associated to a given IGS, we always write
\Xi:=(G,\mathcal{T},\ft,\{\alpha_{t}\}_{t\in\mathcal{T}})
. The sets
I_{t,\pm}
are omitted for brevity but are always understood to be part of the data.
Whenever there is no danger of confusion, we will always implicitly associate the notation
G,\mathcal{T},\ft,\alpha_{t}
to the given IGS without further mention.
We also implicitly always denote the vertex set
V:=V(G)
and the edge set
E:=E(G)
.

Remark 3.2
.

In
[
2
]
, we also discuss another type of IGSs. To distinguish the two, one can call the present ones
vertex-IGSs
and the ones there
edge-IGS
, according to whether the replacement procedure replaces vertices or edges.
In this paper we only discuss vertex-IGSs, and thus simplify the terminology.

We introduce some symbolic operations. Fix a finite non-empty set
V
; in our setting
V
is always the vertex set of the generator
G=(V,E)
of an IGS.

First symbolic operations are the
projections
. For
n\geq m
we denote
\pi_{n,m}:V^{n}\to V^{m},\,v_{1}\ldots v_{n}\mapsto v_{1}\ldots v_{m}
.
We abbreviate
\pi_{n}=\pi_{n,n-1}
.

The second are the
shift functions
.
For
n\geq m
we set
\sigma_{n,m}:V^{n}\to V^{n-m}
,
v_{1}\ldots v_{n}\mapsto v_{m+1}\ldots v_{n}
. If
m=n-1
we abbreviate
\sigma_{n}=\sigma_{n,n-1}
.

Lastly, for
x=v_{1}\ldots v_{n},\,y=w_{1}\ldots w_{m}
and
n,m\in\mathbb{N}
and
v_{i},w_{j}\in V
, we define the
product

x\cdot y:=v_{1}\ldots v_{m}\mapsto w_{1}\ldots w_{n}v_{1}\ldots v_{m}\in V^{n+m}
. In particular,
x=\pi_{n,m}(x)\cdot\sigma_{n,m}(x)
for all
x\in V^{n}
and
n\geq m
. Given a subset
L\subseteq V^{m}
and
x\in V^{n}
we denote
x\cdot L\subseteq V^{n+m}
the image of
L
under the map
y\mapsto x\cdot y,\,V^{m}\to V^{n+m}
. Note that
x\cdot V^{m}=\pi_{n+m,n}^{-1}(x)
.

3.2.
Replacement rule

For a given IGS and its data
\Xi:=(G,\cT,\ft,\{\alpha_{t}\}_{t\in\mathcal{T}})
, where
G=(V,E)
, we consider a recursive replacement rule which produces a sequence of graphs
\{G_{n}:=(V^{n},E_{n})\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
.
We refer to them as
replacement graphs
.
At each step in the recursion, we need a graph
G_{n}
and and a typing function
\ft_{n}
. In the first step we simply set
G_{1}:=G
and
\mathfrak{t}_{1}:=\mathfrak{t}
.
Next assume
G_{n-1}
and
\mathfrak{t}_{n-1}
have been defined, and let
x,y\in V^{n}
.
Then
(x,y)\in E_{n}
if and only if one of the following two conditions holds.

(1)

\pi_{n}(x)=\pi_{n}(y)
and
(\sigma_{n}(x),\sigma_{n}(y))\in E
.

(2)

(\pi_{n}(x),\pi_{n}(y))\in E_{n-1}
,
\sigma_{n}(x)\in I_{t,+}
and
\alpha_{t}(\sigma_{n}(x))=\sigma_{n}(y)\in I_{t,-}
where
t=\ft_{n-1}(\pi_{n}(x),\pi_{n}(y))
.

Finally, the new typing function is

\mathfrak{t}_{n}(x,y):=\begin{cases}\mathfrak{t}(\sigma_{n}(x),\sigma_{n}(y))&\text{ if }\pi_{n}(x)=\pi_{n}(y)\\
\mathfrak{t}_{n-1}(\pi_{n}(x),\pi_{n}(y))&\text{ if }(\pi_{n}(x),\pi_{n}(y))\in E_{n-1}.\end{cases}

Next, we review few simple lemmas. We begin by addressing the obvious self-similarity of our construction. We recall some standard terminology first.
Given two general graphs
G
and
G^{\prime}
we say that

simplicial
, and write
f:G\to G^{\prime}

\{x,y\}\in E(G)

. If
f
is also a bijective function and
f^{-1}:G^{\prime}\to G

is a

.
Given a subset
U\subseteq V(G)
the corresponding
induced subgraph
is the graph
\langle U\rangle
satisfying
V(\langle U\rangle)=U
and
E(\langle U\rangle)=E(G)\cap(U\times U)
.

Lemma 3.6
.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N},
and
x=v_{1}\ldots v_{n},\,y=w_{1}\ldots w_{n}\in V^{n}
be distinct. Fix
1\leq l\leq n

such that
\pi_{n,l}(x)\neq\pi_{n,l}(y)
. Then the following are equivalent.

(1)

(x,y)\in E_{n}
edge and is of type
t\in\mathcal{T}
.

(2)

(\pi_{n,l}(x),\pi_{n,l}(y))
is an edge of type
t
,
w_{k}\in I_{t,+}
and
\alpha_{t}(w_{k})=v_{k}
for all
l<k\leq n
.

Moreover, the replacement graphs are self-similar in the following sense. For all
n,m\in\mathbb{N}
and
x\in V^{m}
the mapping
V^{n}\to V^{n+m}
given by the product
y\mapsto x\cdot y
is a graph isomorphism onto the subgraph induced by
\pi_{n+m,m}^{-1}(x)
, and the inverse is given by the shift function
\sigma_{n+m,n}
.

Proof.

These follow from an induction on the steps of the replacement rule.
∎

The following lemma is needed to ensure that the replacement graphs do not grow uncontrollably.

Lemma 3.7
.

Assume that the following conditions hold:

(1)

For all
t\in\mathcal{T}
, if
v\in I_{t,+}
then there is no edge
(v,w)\in E(G)
of type
t
.

(2)

For all
t\in\mathcal{T}
, if
w\in I_{t,-}
then there is no edge
(v,w)\in E(G)
of type
t
.

Then the replacement graphs have uniformly bounded degree, meaning

\sup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\deg(G_{n})<\infty.

Proof.

For any
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
v\in V^{n}
define

\deg_{t,+}(x):=\lvert\{y\in V^{n}:(x,y)\in E_{n}\text{ and }\ft(x,y)=t\}\rvert.

We also define
\deg_{t,-}(x)
in an analogous way.

By the iterative construction of
G_{n}
, for
n\geq 2
we have the bound

\deg_{t,+}(x)\leq\deg_{t,+}(\sigma_{n}(x))+\deg_{t,+}(\pi_{n}(x))

Fix a vertex
x\in V^{n}
for
n\geq 2
. If
\sigma_{n}(x)\in I_{t,+}
, it follows from the previous inequality and the first assumption in the lemma,

\deg_{t,+}(x)\leq\deg_{t,+}(\sigma_{n}(x))+\deg_{t,+}(\pi_{n}(x))=\deg_{t,+}(\pi_{n}(x)).

If
\sigma_{n}(x)\notin I_{t,+}
, then it follows from the recursive construction of
G_{n}
,

\displaystyle\deg_{t,+}(x)

\displaystyle=\deg_{t,+}(\sigma_{n}(x))\leq\deg(G).

So in conclusion,
\deg_{t,+}(x)\leq\max\{\deg_{t,+}(\pi_{n}(x)),\deg(G)\}
. After applying this inequality
n-1
times, we get
\deg_{t,+}(x)\leq\deg(G)
.

By an identical argument and using the properties of the gluing rules
\alpha_{t}
in Definition
3.1
, we see that
\deg_{t,-}(x)\leq\deg(G)
.
Since this holds for all
t\in\mathcal{T}
and
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we can conclude the proof.
∎

Remark 3.8
.

The converse to Lemma
3.7
is also valid but we leave the details to an interested reader.

3.3.
Symmetric IGSs

Our methods in later sections heavily rely on suitable symmetries.
Here we describe what kind of conditions we are looking for.

Definition 3.9
.

Let
\Xi
be an IGS and
G
be its generator. We say that
\alpha:V\to V
is a
symmetry
of
\Xi
if the map
v_{1}\ldots v_{n}\mapsto\alpha(v_{1}),\ldots,\alpha(v_{n})
defines a graph isomorphism
G_{n}\to G_{n}
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
. For simplicity, we usually denote higher level maps by
\alpha
as well.

Symmetries of IGSs have an easily verifiable characterization, but it is given by a somewhat technical symbolic condition. We nevertheless state it for the convenience of the reader.

Lemma 3.10
.

Let
\Xi
be an IGS and
G
be its generator. Then a graph isomorphism
\alpha:G\to G
is a symmetry of
\Xi
if and only if the following conditions hold.

(1)

If
(v,w)\in E(G)
is an edge of type
t
and
(\alpha(v),\alpha(w))\in E(G)
is an edge of type
t^{\prime}
then
\alpha(I_{t,+})=I_{t^{\prime},+}
,
\alpha(I_{t,-})=I_{t^{\prime},-}
and

(\alpha_{t^{\prime}}\circ\alpha)(v)=(\alpha\circ\alpha_{t})(v)\text{ for all }v\in I_{t,+}\cup I_{t,-}.

(2)

If
(v,w)\in E(G)
is an edge of type
t
and
(\alpha(w),\alpha(v))\in E(G)
is an edge of type
t^{\prime}
then
\alpha(I_{t,+})=I_{t^{\prime},-}
,
\alpha(I_{t,-})=I_{t^{\prime},+}

(\alpha_{t^{\prime}}\circ\alpha)(v)=(\alpha\circ\alpha_{t})(v)\text{ for all }v\in I_{t,+}\cup I_{t,-}.

Proof.

The “if” part follows from Lemma
3.6
, and the “only if” part follows from the fact that
G_{2}\to G_{2}
,
v_{1}v_{2}\mapsto\alpha(v_{1})\alpha(v_{2})
is a graph isomorphism.
∎

Definition 3.11
.

We say that the gluing rules of an IGS
\Xi
are
symmetric
if for every
t\in\mathcal{T}
there is a symmetry
\beta_{t}:V\to V
of
\Xi
with

\text{$\beta_{t}|_{I_{t,+}\cup I_{t,-}}=\alpha_{t}$}\text{ and }\beta_{t}^{2}=\id_{V}.

Whenever the gluing rules are symmetric, we implicitly assume that the gluing rules
\alpha_{t}=\beta_{t}
are restrictions of symmetries of
\Xi
to the set
I_{t,+}\cup I_{t,-}
.
For simplicity we also denote the higher level map
v_{1}\ldots v_{n}\mapsto\beta_{t}(v_{1})\ldots\beta_{t}(v_{n})
by
\alpha_{t}
.

Symmetric gluing rules allow us to perform
foldings
which simplify moduli computations in later sections. These ideas were inspired by Bourdon–Kleiner
[
14
]
but similar techniques also appear in e.g.
[
48
,
33
]
.
Below, we describe an algorithm which takes
n,m\in\mathbb{N}
and a path
\theta=[x_{1},\dots,x_{k}]
in
G_{n+m}
as input and produces as output the
folding

\fold_{m}(\theta)
of
\theta
, which is also a path in
G_{n+m}
. The folded path has the additional property, that it lies entirely in
y\cdot V^{m}=\pi_{n+m,n}^{-1}(y)
for some
y\in V^{n}
.

Consider the sequence
y_{1},\ldots y_{k}\in V^{n}
where
y_{i}=\pi_{n+m,n}(x_{i})
. If
y_{i_{1}},\ldots y_{i_{l}}
is the maximal subsequence of
y_{1},\ldots y_{k}
with no consecutive tuples, then our algorithm has
l
iteration steps in total.

First, if
y_{i}
is a constant sequence, then we return
\theta=\fold_{m}(\theta)
.
If not, let
j
be the least index for which
y_{j}\neq y_{j+1}
.
Then, by definition of the projections,
x_{i}=y_{j}\cdot\tilde{x}_{i}
for some
\tilde{x}_{i}\in V^{m}
, for all
1\leq i\leq j
.
Because the shift maps are isomorphisms (onto their image) according to Lemma
3.6
, the sequence
[z_{1},\ldots z_{j}]
where
z_{i}=y_{j+1}\cdot\alpha_{t}(\tilde{x}_{i})
is a path in
G_{n+m}
.
Here
t
is the type of
\{y_{j},y_{j+1}\}\in E_{n}
.
By Lemma
3.6
,
z_{j}=x_{j+1}
.
We can define the new path

\tilde{\theta}:=[z_{1},\ldots,z_{j},x_{j+2},\ldots x_{k}].

Next, we replace
\theta
with
\tilde{\theta}
and repeat the previous procedure.
Note that
\tilde{\theta}
is shorter than the path
\theta
that we started with so this algorithm will always terminate. Moreover,
\tilde{\theta}\subseteq\pi_{n+m,n}^{-1}(\pi_{n+m,n}(x_{k}))
where
x_{k}
is the last vertex in the original path
\theta
.

The following somewhat technical lemma will be very useful later.
Roughly speaking it states: If the folded path is small, then the original path is also small.
In the statement, we use the same notation as above and also define
\mathcal{L}_{m}
to be the collection consisting of the sets
I_{t,+}^{m},I_{t,-}^{m}\subseteq V^{m}
.
Recall also the notation
x\cdot L
from Subsection
3.2
.

Lemma 3.12
.

Define the collection

\mathcal{C}:=\{\pi_{n+m,n}(x_{k})\cdot L\subseteq V^{n+m}:L\in\mathcal{L}_{m}\text{ and }\fold_{m}(\theta)\cap(\pi_{n+m,n}(x_{k})\cdot L)\neq\emptyset\}.

If all sets in
\mathcal{C}
have a common vertex, then there is a path
[h_{1},\ldots,h_{l}]
in
G_{n+m}
such that
\pi_{n+m}(h_{1})=y_{1},\,\pi_{n+m}(h_{k})=y_{k}
and
\pi_{n+m,n}(h_{i})\neq\pi_{n+m,n}(h_{i+1})
for all
i=1,\ldots,l-1
.

Proof.

Let
z_{*}
be a common vertex of all members in
\mathcal{C}
.
We will prove the claim by backtracking the construction of
\fold_{m}(\theta)
.

Suppose that
y_{i_{1}},\ldots y_{i_{l}}
is the maximal subsequence of
y_{1},\ldots y_{k}
with no consecutive tuples. By construction, the folding procedure had
l
iteration steps in total.
Also,
\{y_{i_{j}},y_{i_{j+1}}\}\in E_{n}
for all
j
, and
let
t_{j}
be its type.
We claim that
h_{j}
can be given inductively as
h_{k}=z_{*}
and
h_{j-1}=y_{i_{j-1}}\cdot\alpha_{t_{j}}(\sigma_{n+m,n}(h_{j}))
.

To see why, first note that
\{h_{k},h_{k-1}\}\in E_{n+m}
by construction and the definition of symmetric gluing rules.
Let
\theta^{\prime}
be the path in the beginning of the last round in the folding. Then
\theta^{\prime}
contains a vertex in
y_{i_{l-1}}\cdot I_{t_{l-2},\pm}^{m}
where the sign depends on the orientation of
\{y_{i_{l-2}},y_{i_{l-1}}\}
.
For simplicity, let the sign be
+
.
Note that
\alpha_{t_{l}}(I_{t_{l-2},+}^{m})\in\mathcal{L}_{m}
by Lemma
3.10
. After the last folding step,
x_{k}\cdot\alpha_{t_{l}}(I_{t_{l-2},+}^{m})\in\mathcal{C}
, which means that
z_{*}\in\alpha_{t_{l}}(I_{t_{l-2},+}^{m})
. Because
\alpha_{t_{j}}^{2}
is the identity by Definition
3.11
, we must have
\alpha_{t_{j}}(z_{*})\in I_{t_{l-2},+}^{m}
.
So we conclude that
\{h_{k-2},h_{k-1}\}\in E_{n+m}
. Then we repeat this backtracking process until we reach the beginning of the path.
∎

Symmetries are also used to “bend” flows, and the following definition captures when this is possible. It is applicable for general graphs
G
.

Definition 3.13
.

Let
G
be a general graph and
\alpha
be a graph isomorphism
G\to G
. We say that
\alpha
is a
reflectional symmetry of
G
if there is a partition
V(G)=C\cup D\cup\Delta
, where
\Delta:=\{v\in V(G):\alpha(v)=v\}
, so that
\alpha(C)=D
,
\alpha^{2}=\id_{V}
and there is no edge between vertices in
C
and
D
.
Here, partition refers to a pair-wise disjoint covering.

The model example of reflectional symmetries are the diagonal reflections of hypercubes, e.g. the reflection over the line
y=x
in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. Later, we will also apply reflections to the pentagonal carpet.
Their main application is to bend flows, which we state in following proposition; similar arguments were used by Barlow–Bass
[
5
]
and Kwapisz
[
38
]
.

Proposition 3.14
.

Let
G
be a graph and
S,T\subseteq V(G)
be disjoint non-empty subsets. Let
\alpha
be a reflectional symmetry of
G
and with the associated partition
V(G)=C\cup D\cup\Delta
. Lastly, we assume that
S\subseteq C,\,T\subseteq D
and that
S\cap\alpha(T)=\emptyset=\alpha(S)\cap T
. Then given a unit flow
\mathcal{J}
from
S
to
T
,

\widetilde{\mathcal{J}}(x,y):=\begin{cases}\mathcal{J}(x,y)&\text{ if }x,y\in\Delta\\
\mathcal{J}(x,y)+\mathcal{J}(\alpha(x),\alpha(y))&\text{ if }\{x,y\}\cap C\neq\emptyset\\
0&\text{ otherwise}\end{cases}

is a unit flow from
S
to
\alpha(T)
.

Proof.

We begin by checking the divergences at vertices in
x\notin S\cup\alpha(T)
. Fix such a vertex
x
.
First, assume
x\in C
. We note that
x,\alpha(x)\notin S\cup T
. This is because
x\notin S
is already assumed and
x\notin T
follows from
T\subseteq D
.
Also,
\alpha(x)\notin S
follows from
S\subseteq C
, and
\alpha(x)\notin T
from
x\notin\alpha(T)
and
\alpha^{2}=\id_{V}
.
Now, since there is no edge between
C
and
D
,

\divr(\widetilde{\mathcal{J}})(x)=\divr(\mathcal{J})(x)+\divr(\mathcal{J})(\alpha(x))=0+0=0.

Next, assume
x\in\Delta
. By the definition of
\widetilde{\mathcal{J}}
,

\displaystyle\divr(\widetilde{\mathcal{J}})(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\{x,y\}\in E\\
y\in\Delta\end{subarray}}\widetilde{\mathcal{J}}(x,y)+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\{x,y\}\in E\\
y\in C\end{subarray}}\widetilde{\mathcal{J}}(x,y)+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\{x,y\}\in E\\
y\in D\end{subarray}}\widetilde{\mathcal{J}}(x,y)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\{x,y\}\in E\\
y\in\Delta\end{subarray}}\mathcal{J}(x,y)+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\{x,y\}\in E\\
y\in C\end{subarray}}(\mathcal{J}(x,y)+\mathcal{J}(x,\alpha(y)))

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\{x,y\}\in E\\
y\in\Delta\end{subarray}}\mathcal{J}(x,y)+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\{x,y\}\in E\\
y\in C\end{subarray}}\mathcal{J}(x,y)+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\{x,y\}\in E\\
y\in D\end{subarray}}\mathcal{J}(x,y)

\displaystyle=\divr(\mathcal{J})(v)=0.

In the second to last row, we used the equality

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\{x,y\}\in E\\
y\in C\end{subarray}}\mathcal{J}(x,\alpha(y))=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\{x,y\}\in E\\
y\in D\end{subarray}}\mathcal{J}(x,y)

which follows from the properties of
\alpha
.
Lastly, assume
x\in D
. Since there is an edge between
x
and
C
, it trivially follows from the definition of
\mathcal{J}
that
\divr(\mathcal{J})(x)=0
.

Next, we verify that
\widetilde{\mathcal{J}}
is a unit flow. By the assumptions,
\alpha(x)\notin(S\cup T)
if
x\in S
. Therefore

\divr(\widetilde{\mathcal{J}})(x)=\divr(\mathcal{J})(x)+\divr(\mathcal{J})(\alpha(x))=\divr(\mathcal{J})(x),

and we get

I(\widetilde{\mathcal{J}})=\sum_{x\in S}\divr(\mathcal{J})(v)=1.

∎

In some cases of interest, such as the Sierpiński gasket, we need to consider symmetries that do not satisfy the assumptions above. Such symmetries enable us to reflect flows.

Proposition 3.15
.

Let
G
be a graph and
\alpha
a graph isomorphism
G\to G
such that there is a partition
V(G)=C\cup D\cup\Delta
where
\Delta=\{v\in V(G):\alpha(v)=v\}
,
\alpha(C)=D
and
\alpha^{2}=\id_{V(G)}
. We also assume that there is no vertex between two vertices in
\Delta
and that whenever
\{x,y\}\in E(G)
for
x\in C
and
y\in D
then
\alpha(x)=y
. Now let
\mathcal{J}
be a unit flow from
S
to
T
such that
S\subseteq C
and
T\subseteq D
. Then

\widetilde{\mathcal{J}}(x,y)=\begin{cases}\mathcal{J}(x,y)&\text{ if }\{x,y\}\cap C\neq\emptyset\\
-\mathcal{J}(\alpha(x),\alpha(y))&\text{ otherwise.}\end{cases}

Then
\widetilde{\mathcal{J}}
is a unit flow from
S
to
\alpha(S)
.

Proof.

We check the divergences at
x\notin S\cup\alpha(S)
.
If
x\in C
, then
\divr(\widetilde{\mathcal{J}})(x)=\divr(\mathcal{J})(x)=0
by the definition of
\widetilde{\mathcal{J}}
.
Next, let
x\in\Delta
. Because there are no edges between vertices in
\Delta
, we have

\displaystyle\divr(\widetilde{\mathcal{J}})(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\{x,y\}\in E(G)\\
y\in C\end{subarray}}\widetilde{\mathcal{J}}(x,y)+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\{x,y\}\in E(G)\\
y\in D\end{subarray}}\widetilde{\mathcal{J}}(x,y)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\{x,y\}\in E(G)\\
y\in C\end{subarray}}\mathcal{J}(x,y)-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\{x,y\}\in E(G)\\
y\in D\end{subarray}}\mathcal{J}(x,\alpha(y))

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\{x,y\}\in E(G)\\
y\in C\end{subarray}}\mathcal{J}(x,y)-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\{x,y\}\in E(G)\\
y\in C\end{subarray}}\mathcal{J}(x,y)=0.

The penultimate equality holds since
\alpha
is a graph isomorphism with
\alpha(C)=D
and
\alpha^{2}=\id_{V}
.
Lastly, assume
x\in D
.
Note that
\alpha(x)\notin S
because we assumed
x\notin\alpha(S)
and
\alpha^{2}=\id_{V(G)}
.
Also
\alpha(x)\notin T
because
\alpha(x)\in C
and
T\subseteq D
.
Now, if the neighbors of
x
are contained in
D\cup\Delta
,
\divr(\mathcal{J})(x)=-\divr(\mathcal{J})(\alpha(x))=0
by the definition of
\widetilde{\mathcal{J}}
.
If this is not the case, by the assumptions on
\alpha
, the only neighbor of
x
that is not in
D\cup\Delta
is
\alpha(x)
.
We then obtain

\displaystyle\divr(\widetilde{\mathcal{J}})(x)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{J}(x,\alpha(x))-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\{x,y\}\in E\\
y\in D\cup\Delta\end{subarray}}\mathcal{J}(\alpha(x),\alpha(y))

\displaystyle=-\mathcal{J}(\alpha(x),x)-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\{\alpha(x),y\}\in E\\
y\in C\cup\Delta\end{subarray}}\mathcal{J}(\alpha(x),y)=-\divr(\mathcal{J})(\alpha(x))=0.

Finally, to see that
\widetilde{\mathcal{J}}
is unit, this follows from
S\subseteq C
and the definition of
\widetilde{\mathcal{J}}
.
∎

Finally, we end with a simple property for symmetries.

Proposition 3.16
.

Let
G,\alpha
and
V(G)=C\cup D\cup\Delta
be as in Proposition
3.15
. Then the function
f:V(G)\to V(G)
given by

f(x):=\begin{cases}x&\text{ if }x\in C\cup\Delta\\
\alpha(x)&\text{ if }x\in D\end{cases}

is simplicial.

Proof.

This follows from the properties of
\alpha
.
∎

4.
Main Examples

This section introduces the main examples of IGSs that this work considers.
These are divided into two different classes; hypercube based constructions and regular polygon based constructions. The former generalizes the Sierpiński carpet and higher dimensional Menger sponges, and also includes the pillow space.
For the latter class we only consider two specific examples, the pentagonal carpet and Sierpiński gasket. We included a short discussion on further generalization in the end of the section.

4.1.
Hypercube based construction

Fix integer parameters
d\geq 2,L_{*}\geq 3
and
K\geq 1
.
Based on these choices, we first define a single large IGS, and the hypercube based IGSs are obtained as suitable “sub-objects” of these larger ones.
First, the set of types is
\mathcal{T}:=\{1,\ldots,d\}
.
To define the underlying graph
G(d,L_{*},K)
, we define the vertex set

V(d,L_{*},K):=V(G(d,L_{*},K)):=\{1,2,\dots,L_{*}\}^{d}\times\{1,\ldots K\}.

For the sake of clearer notation, given
v=\left((z_{i})_{i\in\mathcal{T}},s\right)\in V
, we write
c_{i}(v)=z_{i}
for
i\in\mathcal{T}
and
\xi(v)=s
.
The set of edges are defined so that

(w,v)\in E(d,L_{*},K):=E(G(d,L_{*},K))
is an edge of type
i\in\mathcal{T}
if and only if

c_{j}(v)=\begin{cases}c_{j}(w)&\text{ if }i\neq j\\
c_{j}(w)+1&\text{ if }i=j.\end{cases}

The typing function is denoted
\tilde{\ft}
.
To define the gluing rules
\tilde{\alpha}_{i}:\tilde{I}_{i,+}\cup\tilde{I}_{i,-}\to\tilde{I}_{i,+}\cup\tilde{I}_{i,-}
for
i\in\mathcal{T}
, we first fix the sets. We define
w\in I_{i,+}
if and only if
c_{i}(w)=L_{*}
, and
w\in I_{i,-}
if and only if
c_{i}(w)=1
. Finally, we define
\tilde{\alpha}_{i}(w)=v
if and only if

\begin{cases}c_{j}(w)=c_{j}(v)&\text{ for all }i\neq j\\
(c_{i}(w),c_{i}(v))=(L_{*},1)&\text{ and }\\
\xi(w)=\xi(v).\end{cases}

We denote the IGS
\Xi(d,L_{*},K):=(G(d,L_{*},K),\mathcal{T}(d),\tilde{\ft},\{\tilde{\alpha}_{t}\}_{\mathcal{T}(d)})
.

Definition 4.1
.

We say that
\Xi=(G,\mathcal{T},\ft,\{\alpha_{t}\}_{t\in\mathcal{T}})
is a
hypercubic
IGS if there are
d\geq 2
,
L_{*}\geq 3
and
K\geq 1
such that
\Xi
is a sub-ohject of the IGS described above in the following sense.

(1)

G
is a subgraph of
G(d,L_{*},K)
, meaning
V(G)\subseteq V(d,L_{*},K)
and the inclusion is simplicial.

(2)

\mathcal{T}=\mathcal{T}(d)
and
\ft=\tilde{\ft}|_{E(G)}
.

(3)

The gluing rules
\alpha_{i}:I_{i,+}\cup I_{i,-}\to I_{i,+}\cup I_{i,-}
satisfy
I_{i,\pm}\subseteq\tilde{I}_{i,\pm}
and
\alpha_{i}=(\tilde{\alpha}_{i})|_{I_{i,+}}
for all
i\in\mathcal{T}
.

Unless specified otherwise, we always associate the notation
d,\,L_{*},\,K
to the parameters of a hypercubic IGS.

Remark 4.2
.

Replacement graphs of hypercubic IGSs with parameters
d,L_{*}
and
K=1
have simplicial embeddings to the integer lattice graphs
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
.

We are not yet done with the definition because we need suitable (dihedral) symmetries to ensure good analytic properties. We define them on
\Xi(d,L_{*},K)
first.
We begin by extending the gluing rules of
\Xi(d,L_{*},K)
, namely
\tilde{\alpha}_{i}
for
i\in\mathcal{T}
, to a symmetry of
\Xi(d,L_{*},K)
in the sense of Definition
3.9
.
For
i\in\mathcal{T}(d)
we set
\tilde{\alpha}_{i}(w)=v
where
\xi(w)=\xi(v)
and

c_{j}(v)=\begin{cases}c_{j}(w)&\text{ if }i\neq j\\
L_{*}-c_{i}(w)+1&\text{ if }i=j.\end{cases}

We call
\tilde{\alpha}_{i}
a
horizontal symmetry
.

Next for
1\leq i<j\leq d
we define the
diagonal reflection

\alpha_{i,j}^{+}=\alpha_{j,i}^{+}=\alpha
such that
\alpha(w):=v
where
\xi(v)=\xi(w)
and

c_{k}(v)=\begin{cases}c_{k}(w)&\text{ if }k\notin\{i,j\}\\
c_{j}(w)&\text{ if }k=i\\
c_{i}(w)&\text{ if }k=j.\end{cases}

for all
k\in\mathcal{T}
. We also define the
anti-diagonal reflection

\alpha_{i,j}^{-}=\alpha_{j,i}^{-}=\alpha
such that
\alpha(w)=v
where

\xi(w)=\xi(v)
and

c_{k}(v)=\begin{cases}c_{k}(w)&\text{ if }k\notin\{i,j\}\\
L_{*}-c_{j}(w_{1})+1&\text{ if }k=i\\
L_{*}-c_{i}(w_{1})+1&\text{ if }k=j.\end{cases}

Finally, the set of the graph isomorphisms of
G(d,L_{*},K)
we defined above, namely
\tilde{\alpha}_{i}
and
\alpha_{i,j}^{\pm}
, is denoted
\mathcal{G}(d,L_{*},K)
.

Definition 4.3
.

We say that
\Xi=(G,\mathcal{T},\ft,\{\alpha_{t}\}_{t\in\mathcal{T}})
is a
symmetric hypercubic
IGS if
\Xi
is hypercubic IGS for some parameters
d,L_{*},K
and the following additional conditions are satisfied.

(
C1
)

For all
j\in\mathcal{T}
the vertex set
V(G)
contains all vertices
w
with

\begin{cases}c_{i}(w)\in\{1,L_{*}\}\text{ for all }i\neq j\\
\xi(w)=1,\end{cases}

(
C2
)

For all
j\in\mathcal{T}
the edge set
E(G)
contains all edges
(v,w)
with

\begin{cases}c_{i}(v)=c_{i}(w)\in\{1,L_{*}\}\text{ for all }i\neq j\\
c_{j}(v)=c_{j}(w)+1\\
\xi(v)=\xi(w)=1\end{cases}

(
C3
)

For all
j\in\mathcal{T}
we have
w\in I_{j,+}
and
v\in I_{j,-}
where

\begin{cases}c_{i}(w)=c_{i}(v)&\text{ for all }i\neq j\\
(c_{j}(w_{2}),c_{j}(v_{2}))=(L_{*},1)\\
\xi(w)=\xi(v)=1.\end{cases}

(
C4
)

For every
\alpha\in\mathcal{G}(d,L_{*},K)
the restriction
\alpha|_{V(G)}
is a symmetry of
\Xi
in the sense of Definition
3.9
.

Remark 4.4

Symmetrical hypercubic IGSs are symmetric also in the sense of Definition

4.2.
Constructions by Regular Polygons

We introduce the two other main examples of the work,
the
Sierpiński gasket
and the

The former is one of the model examples of fractal spaces in the mathematical literature, and it is also one of the most researched examples in analysis on fractals; see

The latter seems to be a new construction, although see

.

We again identify the gluing rules
\alpha_{t}
as a subset of

such that
(v,w)\in\alpha_{t}
if and only if

and

Example 4.6

(
Sierpiński gasket
)
.

Let
G=(V,E)
be the graph where
V=\{0,1,2\}

and
E=\{(0,1),(1,2),(0,2)\}
with different types
a,b,c
, respectively.
The gluing rules are given by

\alpha_{a}=\{(1,0)\}\,,\alpha_{b}=\{(2,1)\}\text{ and }\alpha_{c}=\{(2,0)\}.

We refer to this IGS as the

because the limit it produces is biLipschitz equivalent to the usual Sierpiński gasket with the Euclidean metric.

First four replacement graphs of the Sierpiński gasket.

Remark 4.7
.

The Sierpiński gasket is a symmetric IGS in the sense of Definition
3.11
. This is because the gluing rules
\alpha_{a},\,\alpha_{b},\,\alpha_{c}
can be extended to symmetries of the equilateral triangle that yields graph isomorphisms
G_{n}\to G_{n}
on the replacement graphs, according to Lemma
3.10
.
For instance, the non-trivial symmetry that fixes the vertex
i\in V
can be written as

\alpha(j):=\begin{cases}i&\text{ if }j=i\\
i+1&\text{ if }j=i-1\\
i-1&\text{ if }j=i+1.\\
\end{cases}

These are symmetries of the IGS Sierpiński gasket.

Example 4.8

(
Pentagonal carpet
)
.

Let
G=(V,E)
be the graph
V=\{0,1,2,3,4\}

and
E=\{(0,1),(1,2),(2,3),(3,4),(4,0)\}
with types
a,b,c,d,e
, respectively.
The gluing rules are given by

\displaystyle\alpha_{a}

\displaystyle:=\{(1,0),(2,4)\},\,\alpha_{b}:=\{(2,1),(3,0)\},\,\alpha_{c}:=\{(3,2),(4,1)\},

\displaystyle\alpha_{d}

\displaystyle:=\{(4,3),(0,2)\},\,\alpha_{e}:=\{(0,4),(1,3)\}.

We call this IGS the
pentagonal carpet
.
The first three replacement graphs can be found in Figure
2
.
We further introduce the notations

\displaystyle L_{0}:=\{2,3\}=I_{b,+}=I_{d,-},

\displaystyle L_{1}:=\{3,4\}=I_{c,+}=I_{e,-},

\displaystyle L_{2}:=\{0,4\}=I_{a,-}=I_{d,+},

\displaystyle L_{3}:=\{0,1\}=I_{b,-}=I_{e,+},

\displaystyle L_{4}:=\{1,2\}=I_{a,+}=I_{c,-}.

Notice that
L_{i}
is the line segment opposite to the vertex
i\in V
. We heavily employ these notations in our arguments.

Remark 4.9
.

The pentagonal carpet is also a symmetric IGS. To see this, it follows from Lemma
3.10
that the symmetries of the regular pentagon are symmetries also in the sense of Definition
3.11
. For instance, the non-trivial symmetry of the regular pentagon that fixes the vertex
i\in V
can be written as

\alpha(j):=\begin{cases}i&\text{ if }j=i\\
i+1&\text{ if }j=i-1\\
i-1&\text{ if }j=i+1\\
i+2&\text{ if }j=i-2\\
i-2&\text{ if }j=i+2.\\
\end{cases}

The arithmetic above is taken as modulo 5.

The construction of a pentagonal Sierpiński carpet is new, but it has a curious connection with a subdivision rule already studied in the literature.

Remark 4.10
.

There exists a planar pentagonal subdivision rule
[
16
]
which was first studied in
[
19
,
18
]
. This subdivision rule divides a pentagon into five pentagons recursively. This can be seen also from Figure
2
, if one imagines a reflected pentagon being placed at the center of the construction. In
[
18
]
it is shown, that this subdivision rule is realized by a rational map, see also
[
46
]
. In
[
19
]
and
[
16
]
it is shown that the pentagonal subdivision rule can be realized as a conformal subdivision rule of the complex plane. See
[
16
]
for visualizations of these conformal subdivision rules that are computed using circle packings. Such subdivision rules produce sequences of tilings of a pentagon. If one omits the central pentagon in the resulting tilings at every stage, one obtains a carpet. The remaining tiles of this carpet have the same combinatorial structure as in our graphs. It thus seems, that our pentagonal carpet is a subset of these subdivision rules equipped with an appropriately chosen visual metric in the sense of Bonk and Meyer
[
12
]
. Consequently, the carpets that we study may be interesting from a dynamical perspective, and this suggests that it may be useful to use the IGS framework in conjunction with the subdivision framework. This connection suggests many further problems, such as the extension of the rigidity work of Bonk and Merenkov; see
[
11
]
.

Further examples and discussions

While our class of regular polygon based construction only consists of two explicit examples, we of course could consider many other examples. In the two figures below we included two IGSs that also fall within this class of constructions.

The main reason for our decision to focus on only the two regular polygon based examples is that the analysis on these kinds of fractals seems to subtly depend on the particularities of each shape used. The cause of this seems to be the differences in the symmetry groups, and this complicates finding unified proofs for a larger class of examples.
Nevertheless, extensions to a broader class should be possible by adapting our methods. Later, we will also state a set of axioms on the replacement graphs, which seems to be quite general; see Section

. If one is interested in an example that does not fall directly into one of the examples of this section, one can attempt to verify these axioms instead. These verifications should be quite similar to the various special cases that we discuss.

5.
Replacement flow

This section studies flows on replacement graphs.
Subsection
5.1
is devoted to prove the main result of the section, which regards the general replacement flow technique (Theorem
5.2
). We state it for general IGSs with symmetric gluing rules and whose replacement graphs have uniformly bounded degrees.
This is the most important technique of the paper and it is a crucial ingredient in our proof of super-multiplicativity inequality and the combinatorial Loewner property in Section
8
.
In Subsection
5.2
we construct good flow bases for our main examples.

5.1.
Flow bases and replacement flow

Throughout the subsection, we consider a fixed IGS
\Xi=(G,\mathcal{T},\ft,\{\alpha_{t}\}_{t\in\mathcal{T}})
such that its replacement graphs
G_{n}=(V^{n},E_{n})
have uniformly bounded degree, meaning

C_{\deg}:=\sup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\deg(G_{n})<\infty.

We assume
\Xi
to be symmetric in the sense of Definition
3.11
. The notation
\alpha_{t}
for
t\in\mathcal{T}
denotes both the gluing rules and the graph isomorphisms
G_{n}\to G_{n}
. We also need to be careful with the orientations of the edges. If
G
is any oriented graph and
\{x,y\}\in E
is any edge we write

\sgn(x,y):=\begin{cases}+&\text{ if }(x,y)\in E\\
-&\text{ if }(y,x)\in E.\end{cases}

For
m\in\mathbb{N}
we define
\mathcal{L}_{m}
to be the collection consisting of the subsets
I_{t,+}^{m},I_{t,-}^{m}\subseteq V^{m}
for
t\in\mathcal{T}
.
For our flow method it will be useful to introduce the following
extended replacement graphs
.
For
m\in\mathbb{N}
, we set
G^{e}_{m}:=(V_{m}^{e},E_{m}^{e})
to be the graph that is obtained by extending the set of vertices

V_{m}^{e}:=V^{m}\cup\{x_{L}:L\in\cL_{m}\text{ and }x\in L\}

and by adding the edges

E_{m}^{e}:=E_{m}\cup\left(\bigcup_{L\in\cL_{m}}\{\{x_{L},x\}:x\in L\}\right).

Finally, for
L\in\cL_{m}
, we denote
L^{e}:=\{x_{L}:x\in L\}
. Note that
L^{e}_{1}\cap L_{2}^{e}=\emptyset
for all distinct pairs
L_{1},L_{2}\in\mathcal{L}_{m}
.
Also
\alpha_{t}(I_{t,\pm}^{m})=I_{t,\mp}^{m}
for all
t\in\mathcal{T}
.
In particular if
L=I_{t,-}^{m}
, then the notation
\alpha_{t}(x)_{L}
is sensible. We use this in
(FB3)
below.

Definition 5.1
.

A collection
\mathcal{B}
of flows in
G_{m}^{e}
is a
flow basis
if it satisfies the following conditions.

(
FB1
)

Each flow in
\mathcal{B}
is a unit flow from
L_{1}^{e}
to
L_{2}^{e}
for some distinct pair
L_{1},L_{2}\in\mathcal{L}_{m}
.
Furthermore, for each distinct pair
L_{1},L_{2}\in\mathcal{L}_{m}
there is exactly one unit flow from
L_{1}^{e}
to
L_{2}^{e}
in

\mathcal{J}
denoted as
\mathcal{J}_{[L_{1}\to L_{2}]}
.

(
FB2
)

If
L\neq L_{1},L_{2}
and
x\in L
,

\mathcal{J}_{[L\to L_{1}]}(x_{L},x)=\mathcal{J}_{[L\to L_{2}]}(x_{L},x)\text{ and }\mathcal{J}_{[L_{1}\to L]}(x,x_{L})=\mathcal{J}_{[L_{2}\to L]}(x,x_{L}).

(
FB3
)

It holds for all
t\in\mathcal{T},\,L_{1}=I_{t,+}^{m},\,L_{2}=I_{t,-}^{m}
and
x\in L_{1}
,

\mathcal{J}_{[L_{1}\to L_{2}]}(x_{L_{1}},x)=\mathcal{J}_{[L_{1}\to L_{2}]}(\alpha_{t}(x),\alpha_{t}(x)_{L_{2}})

and

\mathcal{J}_{[L_{1}\to L_{2}]}(x_{L_{1}},x)=\mathcal{J}_{[L_{2}\to L_{1}]}(x,x_{L_{1}}).

Here
\alpha_{t}
is the gluing rule/symmetry of type
t
.

The
p
-energy
of
\mathcal{B}
is
\cE_{q}(\mathcal{B}):=\max\left\{\cE_{q}(\mathcal{J}):\mathcal{J}\in\mathcal{B}\right\}
where
q=p/(p-1)
.

For
n\in\mathbb{N}
we fix a source and a target
S_{n},T_{n}\subseteq V^{n}
, which is a disjoint pair of non-empty subsets. For every
u\in S_{n}\cup T_{n}
we assign any choice of a pair
(\ft(u),\sgn(u))\in\mathcal{T}\times\{+,-\}
. Then we define the source and the target in
G_{n+m}
,

S_{n+m}:=\{u\cdot x\in V^{n+m}:u\in S_{n}\text{ and }x\in I_{\ft(u),\sgn(u)}^{m}\}\subseteq V^{n+m}.

Similarily we define

T_{n+m}:=\{u\cdot x\in V^{n+m}:u\in T_{n}\text{ and }x\in I_{\ft(u),\sgn(u)}^{m}\}\subseteq V^{n+m}.

We can now state the replacement flow theorem.

Theorem 5.2
.

Fix
n,m\in\mathbb{N}
and
p\in(1,\infty)
.
Let
\Xi
be an IGS with symmetric gluing rules and such that
\deg(G_{n})\leq C_{\deg}<\infty
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
.
Let
\mathcal{B}
be a flow basis of
G_{m}^{e}
, and
S_{n},T_{n},S_{n+m},T_{n+m}
be as above. Then there is a constant
C\geq 1
depending only on
C_{\deg}
and
p\in(1,\infty)
s.t. the following holds. For every unit flow
\mathcal{J}_{n}
from
S_{n}\to T_{n}
in
G_{n}
there exists a unit flow
\mathcal{J}_{n+m}
from
S_{n+m}
to
T_{n+m}
in
G_{n+m}
satisfying

\cE_{q}(\mathcal{J}_{n+m})\leq C\cdot\cE_{q}(\mathcal{B})\cE_{q}(\mathcal{J}_{n}).

The constant
C
can be chosen so that
C^{1-p}
is continuous in
p\in(1,\infty)
and has a limit when
p\to 1^{+}
.

Theorem
5.2
is quite powerful when we have good estimates for
\mathcal{E}_{q}(\mathcal{B})
. An important application is the
super-multiplicativity inequality
. For its proof in Section
8
, we need the following theorem.

Theorem 5.3
.

Let
\Xi
be an IGS with the same assumptions as in Theorem
5.2
and
p\in(1,\infty)
.
Assume that there is a constant
C_{1}=C_{1}(p)
such that
C_{1}^{1-p}
is continuous in
p\in(1,\infty)
, has a limit when
p\to 1^{+}
and for all
m\in\mathbb{N}
there is a flow basis
\mathcal{B}
on
G_{m}^{e}
satisfying

\mathcal{E}_{q}(\mathcal{B})\leq C_{1}\cRes_{p}(I_{t,+}^{m},I_{t,-}^{m},G_{n})

for all
t\in\mathcal{T}
.
Then there is a constant
C_{2}
depending on
C_{1},p
and
C_{\deg}
such that for all
n,m\in\mathbb{N}
and
t\in\mathcal{T}
,

\cRes_{p}(I_{t,+}^{n+m},I_{t,-}^{n+m},G_{n+m})\leq C_{2}\cRes_{p}(I_{t,+}^{n},I_{t,-}^{n},G_{n})\cdot\cRes_{p}(I_{t,+}^{m},I_{t,-}^{m},G_{m})

The constant
C_{2}
can be chosen so that
C_{2}^{1-p}
is continuous in
p\in(1,\infty)
and has a limit when
p\to 1^{+}
.

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{J}_{n}
be the minimizer for
\cRes_{p}(I_{t,+}^{n},I_{t,-}^{n},G_{n})
. In particular, its source is
S_{n}:=I_{t,+}^{n}
and target is
T_{n}:=I_{t,-}^{n}
. We choose the sets
S_{n+m}
and
T_{n+m}
as follows. For each
u\in S_{n}
we choose
(\ft(u),\sgn(u))=(t,+)
, and for
y\in T_{n}
we choose
(\ft(u),\sgn(u))=(t,-)
. Then
S_{n+m}=I_{t,+}^{n+m}
and
T_{n+m}=I_{t,-}^{n+m}
.
Then the super-multiplicative inequality, as in the claim, follows from the energy estimate on the flow basis and the replacement flow theorem ( Theorem
5.2
).
∎

The proof of Theorem
5.2
is quite technical due to the delicate nature of flows. We will introduce some additional notation to ease the computation.
We begin by performing an extension of
G_{n}
and
G_{n+m}
as well. Set
G_{n}^{*}:=(V_{n}^{*},E_{n}^{*})
where

V_{n}^{*}:=V^{n}\cup\{u^{*}:u\in S_{n}\cup T_{n}\}

and

E_{n}^{*}:=E_{n}\cup\{\{u^{*},u\}:u\in S_{n}\cup T_{n}\}.

To define the extension
G_{n+m}^{*}:=(V_{n+m}^{*},E_{n+m}^{*})
, for

we first set,
\ft(u^{*},u):=\ft(u)
and
\sgn(u^{*},u):=\sgn(u)
. We also choose
\sgn(u,u^{*})
so that

\{\sgn(u^{*},u),\sgn(u,u^{*})\}=\{+,-\}.

Lastly we introduce new vertices
u^{*}\cdot\alpha_{\ft(u,u^{*})}(x)
. This is only a formal notation; see the remark below.
We now define the extended graph so that the vertex set is

V_{n+m}^{*}:=V^{n+m}\cup\left\{u^{*}\cdot\alpha_{\ft(u,u^{*})}(x):u\in S_{n}\cup T_{n}\text{ and }x\in I_{\ft(u),\sgn(u)}^{m}\right\}

and the edge set

E^{*}_{n+m}:=E_{n+m}\cup\left\{\{u\cdot x,u^{*}\cdot\alpha_{\ft(u,u^{*})}(x)\}:u\in S_{n}\cup T_{n}\text{ and }x\in I_{\ft(u),\sgn(u)}^{m}\right\}.

The orientation of these added edges will play no role, but
\ft(u^{*}\cdot\alpha_{\ft(u,u^{*})}(x),u\cdot x):=\ft(u)
and
\sgn(u^{*}\cdot\alpha_{\ft(u,u^{*})}(x),u\cdot x):=\sgn(u)
.

.

Recall the notation
\sgn(x,y)
from the beginning of the section.

Let us now begin the construction of
\mathcal{J}_{n+m}
in Theorem

Lemma 5.6
.

For each pair
y\in N_{u}^{-}\,z\in N_{u}^{+}
the function
\mathcal{J}_{y,z}

\{z\cdot\alpha_{\ft(u,z)}(x):x\in L_{u,z}\}

(5.7)

\cE_{q}(\mathcal{J}_{y,z})\leq\cE_{q}(\mathcal{B}).

where

Proof.

First assume
L_{u,y}\neq L_{u,z}
. For vertices of the form

for

, we have by construction

\divr(\mathcal{J}_{y,z})(u\cdot x)=\divr(\mathcal{B}_{[L_{u,y}\to L_{u,z}]})(x)=0.

To see that
\divr(\mathcal{J}_{y,z})
vanishes at the rest of the claimed vertices, simply observe that
\mathcal{J}_{y,z}
vanishes at the edges containing them. The flow is unit because

I(\mathcal{J}_{y,z})=\sum_{x\in L_{u,y}}\mathcal{J}_{y,z}(y\cdot\alpha_{\ft(u,y)}(x),u\cdot x)=I(\mathcal{B}_{[L_{u,y}\to L_{u,z}]})=1,

where the last equality follows from
(FB1)
.
The energy estimate follows from

\cE_{q}(\mathcal{J}_{y,z})=\cE_{q}(\mathcal{B}_{[L_{u,y}\to L_{u,z}]})\leq\cE_{q}(\mathcal{B}).

Next assume
L_{u,y}=L_{u,z}
. By definition of
\mathcal{J}_{y,z}
,

I(\mathcal{J}_{y,z})=\sum_{x\in L_{u,y}}\mathcal{J}_{y,z}(y\cdot\alpha_{\ft(u,y)}(x),u\cdot x)=I(\mathcal{B}_{[L_{u,y}\to\widetilde{L}_{u,y}]})=1.

For
x\in L_{u,y}
we compute

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}_{y,z}(y\cdot\alpha_{\ft(u,y)}(x),u\cdot x)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{B}_{[L_{u,y}\to\widetilde{L}_{u,y}]}(x_{L_{u,y}},x)=\mathcal{B}_{[\widetilde{L}_{u,y}\to L_{u,y}]}(x,x_{L_{u,y}})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{B}_{[\widetilde{L}_{u,z}\to L_{u,z}]}(x,x_{L_{u,z}})=\mathcal{J}_{y,z}(u\cdot x,z\cdot\alpha_{\ft(u,z)}(x)),

where the second equality follows from
(FB3)
and the third from
(FB2)
. So we get

\divr(\mathcal{J}_{y,z})(u\cdot x)=\mathcal{J}_{y,z}(u\cdot x,y\cdot\alpha_{\ft(u,y)}(x))+\mathcal{J}_{y,z}(u\cdot x,z\cdot\alpha_{\ft(u,z)}(v))=0.

The divergences vanish at the other claimed vertices because, again, the flow vanishes at the edges containing them, and
\mathcal{J}_{y,z}
is a unit flow.
To obtain the energy estimate, we use
(FB3)
to compute

\cE_{q}(\mathcal{J}_{y,z})=2\sum_{x\in L_{u,y}}\left|\mathcal{B}_{[L_{u,y}\to\widetilde{L}_{u,y}]}(x_{L_{u,y}},x)\right|^{q}\leq\cE_{q}(\mathcal{B}_{[L_{u,y}\to\widetilde{L}_{u,y}]})\leq\cE_{q}(\mathcal{B}).

∎

Next we introduce a flow
\mathcal{J}_{u}
for the chosen

. To this end, we first set

Note that the latter equality follows from

if and only if

.

and
J_{u}>0
. The flow
\mathcal{J}_{u}

\mathcal{J}_{u}:=\sum_{z\in N_{u}^{+}}\left(\frac{\mathcal{J}_{n}^{*}(u,z)}{I_{u}}\sum_{y\in N_{u}^{-}}\mathcal{J}_{n}^{*}(y,u)\cdot\mathcal{J}_{y,z}\right).

Theorem 5.12
.

Let
\Xi
be a symmetric hypercubic IGS for parameters

Proof.

Fix
m\in\mathbb{N}
and write for simplicity
L_{i,\pm}:=I_{i,\pm}^{m}

\mathcal{B}_{[L_{i,\pm}\to L_{i,\mp}]}

\cRes_{p}(L_{i,+},L_{i,-},G_{m})

\mathcal{J}_{i}
to a flow
\mathcal{J}_{i}^{*}
on
G_{m}^{e}
in the natural way. More precisely, we set for
L=L_{i,\pm}

\mathcal{J}_{i}^{*}(x_{L},x):=\divr(\mathcal{J}_{i})(x),

\mathcal{J}_{i}^{*}(x_{L},x)=0
for
L\neq L_{i,\pm}
. Now, set
\mathcal{B}_{[L_{i,+}\to L_{i,-}]}=\mathcal{J}_{i}^{*}
,
\mathcal{B}_{[L_{i,-}\to L_{i,+}]}=-\mathcal{J}_{i}^{*}
.

Before constructing the other flows, let us first show that
\mathcal{J}_{i}^{*}
and
-\mathcal{J}_{i}^{*}
satisfy
(FB3)
where the symmetry
\alpha_{i}

\alpha_{i}(\mathcal{J}_{i})=-\mathcal{J}_{i}

.

G_{m}

L_{i,+}^{e}
to
L_{i,-}^{e}

\alpha_{i,j}^{+}(L_{k,\pm}^{e})\in\{L_{i,+}^{e},L_{i,-}^{e},L_{j,+}^{e},L_{j,-}^{e}\}
for

\alpha_{i,j}^{+}
on
\widetilde{G}_{m}
is a reflectional symmetry as in Definition
3.13
. The partition

\alpha(L_{i})=L_{j}

\{\alpha(L_{i+2}),\alpha(L_{i-2})\}=\{L_{j+2},L_{j-2}\}.

By the uniqueness of the energy minimizing unit flow, we then have

This implies that

For

be the non-trivial symmetry of the regular pentagon that fixes the vertex opposite to

. We observe that the isomorphisms

satisfy the assumptions in Proposition

. To see this, first note that

is clear.
The partition

is given as follows; see also Figure

.
Note that

only fixes one vertex, namely the constant sequence

for all

For

Then we define

For

The flows in

Proposition 5.14
.

Let

.

6.
Asymptotic geometric growth

This section studies the geometry of replacement graphs.
The main goal of the section is the theorem below.
When
x\in V^{n}
then
B(x,r)\subseteq V^{n}
always denotes the open ball with respect to the path metric
d_{G_{n}}
.
We also recall the notation
x\cdot L
from Subsection
3.2
.

Theorem 6.1
.

Let
\Xi
be a symmetric hypercubic IGS, the Sierpiński gasket or the pentagonal carpet. Then there are constants
M_{*},L_{*}\geq 1
and
c_{1},c_{2}>0
depending on the given IGS so that the following inclusions hold. For all
n,m\in\mathbb{N},\,x\in V^{n+m}
and
r\leq c_{1}L_{*}^{m}

B(x,r)\subseteq\bigcup_{z\in B(\pi_{n+m,n}(x),M_{*})}z\cdot V^{m},

and for all
R\geq c_{2}L_{*}^{m}

\bigcup_{z\in B(\pi_{n+m,n}(x),M_{*})}z\cdot V^{m}\subseteq B(x,R).

6.1.
Bounded geometry

The first inclusion of Theorem
6.1
requires quite delicate arguments, and is another reason for us to restrict our focus to particular examples of IGS’s instead of analyzing all cases simultaneously.
Our approach to this inclusion is based on studying the structure of a special class of paths that are defined below.

Through the section, unless specified otherwise,
\Xi
denotes a general IGS, and we refer to its data as stated in the paragraph below Definition
3.1
. The replacement graphs are denoted
G_{n}
, and we assume that they have uniformly bounded degrees.

For a given path
\theta=[x_{1},\ldots x_{k}]
in
G_{n}
and
1\leq m\leq n
the
projected path
, denoted
\pi_{n,m}(\theta)
, is the path in
G_{m}
obtained by removing the consecutive repeated tuples from the sequence
[\pi_{n,m}(x_{1}),\ldots\pi_{n,m}(x_{k})]
.

Definition 6.2
.

For
n>1
a path
\theta
on
G_{n}
is
non-collapsing
if
\len(\theta)=\len(\pi_{n}(\theta))
. We say that an IGS is of
bounded geometry
, if

\sup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\max_{\theta}\diam(\theta,d_{G_{n}})<\infty

where the maximum is taken over all non-collapsing paths in
G_{n}
. Here
\diam
refers to the diameter, with respect to the path metric, of the set of vertices contained in the path
\theta
.

Verifying the bounded geometry condition seems to be unfortunately technical and tedious, and this work verifies it for the main examples only. We do not, however, exclude the possibility that there exists some relatively simple algorithm that could decide whether it holds or not based on the data of IGS only.

6.2.
Bounded geometry

We begin with the following technical lemma. For a given edge
\{x,y\}\in E_{n}
we denote
\Lambda(\{x,y\})
the smallest number
m\geq 1
so that
\pi_{n,m}(x)\neq\pi_{n,m}(y)
. For a given path
\theta=[x_{1},\ldots x_{k}]
we denote

\Lambda(\theta):=\{\Lambda(\{x_{i},x_{i+1}\}):1\leq i\leq k-1\}.

Lemma 6.3
.

Let
\theta=[x_{1},\ldots x_{k}]
be a path in
G_{m}
and write
x_{i}:=v_{1}^{i}\ldots v_{m}^{i}
. Assume that
J\subseteq\{1,\dots,m\}
is a (discrete) sub-interval with
\Lambda(\theta)\cap J=\emptyset
. Then whenever

v_{i}^{1}=v_{j}^{1}
for some
i,j\in J
, we have
v_{i}^{l}=v_{j}^{l}
for all
l=1,\dots,k
.

Proof.

It is sufficient to prove the case where
k=2
, so we write
\theta=[x_{1},x_{2}]
. We may assume that
(x_{1},x_{2})\in E_{m}
because otherwise we can consider the reversed path.

Now, assume
v_{i}^{1}=v_{j}^{1}
for some
i,j\in J
. Since
J
is an interval with
\Lambda(\{x_{1},x_{2}\})\notin J
, either

\Lambda(\{x_{1},x_{2}\})>i,j
or
\Lambda(\{x_{1},x_{2}\})<i,j
.
In the former case, by the definition of
\Lambda(\{x_{1},x_{2}\})
,
v_{i}^{2}=v_{i}^{1}=v_{j}^{1}=v_{j}^{2}
.
In the latter case, by Lemma
3.6
, we have
v_{j}^{2}=\alpha_{t}(v_{j}^{1})=\alpha_{t}(v_{i}^{1})=v_{1}^{2}
.
∎

Proposition 6.4
.

Hypercubic IGSs are of bounded geometry.

Proof.

Let
\Xi
be a hypercubic IGS.
Fix a non-collapsing path
\theta=[x_{1},\ldots,x_{k}]
in
G_{m}
and write
x_{i}=v_{1}^{i}\ldots v_{m}^{i}
.
We first show that
\lvert\Lambda(\theta)\rvert\leq\lvert\mathcal{T}\rvert
. More specifically, we show that every edge of the same type in
\theta
have the same value in
\Lambda
.

We fix an edge on
\theta
. Without loss of generality assume that we chose
\{x_{1},x_{2}\}
and let its type be
t
.
Let
l
be the smallest integer so that
1<l\leq k
and
\{x_{l},x_{l+1}\}
is also of type
t
. We would be done if we can show that
\Lambda(\{x_{l},x_{l+1}\})=\Lambda(\{x_{1},x_{2}\})
.
To do this, we do a counter-assumption. Because we can reverse the path, there is no harm in assuming

\Lambda(\{x_{1},x_{2}\})<\Lambda(\{x_{l},x_{l+1}\})
.

Note that by the gluing rules of hypercubic IGSs and Lemma
3.6
, if
x=v_{1}\ldots v_{m},y=w_{1}\ldots w_{m}\in V^{m}
are neighbors in
G_{m}
and are connected by an edge of type
t^{\prime}\neq t
, we have
c_{t}(v_{i})=c_{t}(w_{i})
for all
i=1,\ldots m
. Here
c_{t}
are the coordinate functions. By the choice of
l
, it therefore holds that
c_{t}(v_{i}^{2})=c_{t}(v_{i}^{l})
for all
i
.
By Lemma
3.6
, the gluing rules of hypercubic IGSs and
\Lambda(\{x_{1},x_{2}\})<\Lambda(\{x_{l},x_{l+1}\})
,
c_{t}(v_{j}^{l})=c_{t}(v_{j}^{2})
are all the same value (either
1
or
L_{*}
) for all
j\geq\Lambda(\{x_{l},x_{l+1}\})
. This finally contradicts Lemma
3.6
, the definition of edges and gluing rules of hypercubic IGSs and the fact that
\{x_{l},x_{l+1}\}
is an edge of type
t
. We now conclude that
\lvert\Lambda(\theta)\rvert\leq\lvert\mathcal{T}\rvert
.

Now, fix
x=v_{1}\ldots v_{m},\,y=w_{1}\ldots w_{m}\in V^{m}
so that there is a non-collapsing path between
x
and
y
.
Write
\Lambda(\theta)=\{l_{1},\dots,l_{k}\}
so that
l_{1}<\dots<l_{k}
, and consider the discrete sub intervals

J_{j}:=\begin{cases}[1,l_{1})&\text{ if }j=0\\
(l_{k},m]&\text{ if }j=k\\
(l_{j},l_{j+1})&\text{ if }1\leq j\leq k-1.\end{cases}

Then write
U_{j}:=\{v_{i}:i\in J_{j}\}
and for each
v\in U_{j}
set
j_{v}
to be the smallest index in
J_{j}
so that
v_{i}=v
. By Lemma
6.3

y
is uniquely determined by
x
and
(w_{j_{v}})_{1\leq j\leq k,\,v\in U_{j}}
. Since
k\leq\lvert\cT\rvert
, there are at most
\lvert V\rvert^{\lvert\cT\rvert(\lvert V\rvert+1)}
many choices for
y\in V^{m}
. Because paths are connected, we get a bound for the diameters of non-collapsing paths.
∎

Next we deal with the Sierpiński gasket and the pentagonal carpet. The argument for the former is direct, but the latter is considerably more tedious.

Proposition 6.5
.

The Sierpiński gasket and the pentagonal carpet are of bounded geometry.

Proof.

We first deal with the gasket. Let
\theta
be a simple non-constant non-collapsing path in
G_{m}
. We will show that it contains exactly one edge. Let

x=v_{1}\ldots v_{m},\,y=w_{1}\ldots w_{m}\in V^{m}
be so that
(x,y)\in E_{m}
is an edge contained in
\theta
. Using the symmetries of the equilateral triangle, we may assume that
(v_{k},w_{k})=(0,1)
, where
k=\Lambda(\{x,y\})
.
By the gluing rules,
(v_{i},w_{i})=(1,0)
for all
k<i\leq n
.
From this, the fact that
\theta
is non-collapsing and the gluing rules of the gasket we see that
\theta
cannot contain other vertices than
x,y
.

Next we move on to the pentagonal carpet.
Let
\theta
be a simple non-collapsing path in
G_{m}
and
(x,y)\in E_{m}
an edge contained in the path with

(6.6)

k:=\Lambda(\{x,y\})=\min\Lambda(\theta).

By applying the symmetries of the regular pentagon, we may assume that
(v_{k},w_{k})=(0,1)
.
We will first prove that
\theta
is contained in

\left(\pi_{n,k}(x)\cdot I_{a,+}^{(n-k)}\right)\quad\bigcup\quad\left(\pi_{n,k}(y)\cdot I_{a,-}^{n-k}\right).

Recall that
z\cdot A
is the image of
A
under the map
w\mapsto z\cdot w
.
Note that
x
and
y
are contained in this set. Assume the converse, i.e., there is an edge
\{z_{1},z_{2}\}
in
\theta
so that

z_{1}\in\left(\pi_{n,k}(x)\cdot I_{a,+}^{n-k}\right)\quad\bigcup\quad\left(\pi_{n,k}(y)\cdot I_{a,-}^{n-k}\right).

and

z_{2}\notin\left(\pi_{n,k}(x)\cdot I_{a,+}^{n-k}\right)\quad\bigcup\quad\left(\pi_{n,k}(y)\cdot I_{a,-}^{n-k}\right).

Write
z_{j}=u^{j}_{1}\dots u^{j}_{n}
for
j=1,2
and, for simplicity, we assume that
z_{1}\in\pi_{n,k}(x)\cdot I_{a,+}^{n-k}
. Observe that

\pi_{n,k}(z_{1})=\pi_{n,k}(x)\neq\pi_{n,k}(z_{2}).

Hence
\Lambda(\{z_{1},z_{2}\})\leq k
. But since
\{z_{1},z_{2}\}
is contained in
\theta
, by (
6.6
) we must have
\Lambda(\{z_{1},z_{2}\})=k
.
Since
u_{k}^{1}=v_{k}=0
, we must have
u_{k}^{2}\neq 1
. Otherwise,
(u_{k}^{1},u_{k}^{2})=(0,1)
and

u^{1}\in\pi_{n,k}(x)\cdot I_{a,+}^{(n-k)}
.
In particular, we must have
u_{k}^{2}=4
and the edge is oriented as
(z_{1},z_{2})
.
However, then
u^{1}_{k+1}\in I_{a,+}\cap I_{e,+}=\emptyset
which yields the contradiction. We thus have that claimed inclusion.

We recall the notation
\langle A\rangle
for the induced subgraph of
A\subseteq V^{n}
. We consider the subgraphs

H_{1}=\left\langle\left(\pi_{n,k}(x)\cdot I_{a,+}^{n-k}\right)\quad\bigcup\quad\left(\pi_{n,k}(y)\cdot I_{a,-}^{(n-k)}\right)\right\rangle\subseteq G_{n}

and

H_{2}=\left\langle\pi_{n,k}(x)\cdot I_{a,+}^{n-k}\right\rangle\subseteq G_{n}.

Consider the simplicial map between graphs
f:H_{1}\to H_{2}
,

f(u\cdot v)=\begin{cases}\pi_{n,k}(x)\cdot v&\text{ if }u=\pi_{n,k}(x)\\
\pi_{n,k}(x)\cdot\alpha_{a}(v)&\text{ if }u=\pi_{n,k}(y).\end{cases}

Then the path
\theta^{\prime}
which is obtained by mapping
\theta
by
f
and removing the consequtive tuples, is a non-collapsing path contained in
\pi_{n,k}(x)\cdot I_{a,+}^{n-k}
. By proving that
\theta^{\prime}
contains at most 2 vertices, we can conclude that
\theta
contains at most 4 vertices and we would be done.

Let
(z_{1},z_{2})
be an edge in
\theta^{\prime}
and
z_{j}=u^{j}_{1}\dots u^{j}_{n}
and
l:=\Lambda(\{z_{1},z_{2}\})
.
Note that

\pi_{n,k}(z_{1})=\pi_{n,k}(x)=\pi_{n,k}(z_{2})

so we must have
l>k
. In particular,
u_{l}^{1},u_{l}^{2}\in I_{a,+}
, which gives
(u_{l}^{1},u_{l}^{2})=(1,2)
. Moreover,
u_{i}^{1}\in I_{a,+}\cap I_{b,+}=\{2\}
and
u^{2}_{i}\in I_{a,+}\cap I_{b,-}=\{1\}
for all
l<i\leq n
.
Now suppose
\theta^{\prime}
contains an edge
\{z_{1},z_{3}\}
. By replicating the previous argument, we have that
z_{3}=z_{2}
. Similarly, if
\theta^{\prime}
contains
\{z_{2},z_{3}\}
, we have

. We may now conclude that
\theta^{\prime}
contains at most two vertices, so we are done.
∎

6.3.
Asymptotic geometry

We move on to proving Theorem
6.1
. We first perform some case-by-case analysis and derive suitable diameter upper bounds.

Let us first consider a symmetric hypercubic IGS
\Xi
.
For each
k=1,\dots,L_{*}
denote with the bolded number
\bm{k}\in V
the vertex, for which

\begin{cases}c_{1}(\bm{k})=k\\
c_{i}(\bm{k})=1&\text{ for all }i\neq 1\\
\xi(\bm{k})=1.\end{cases}

Such a vertex
\bm{k}
is contained in
V
by
(C1)
.
By
(C2)
, there are edges
(\bm{k},\bm{k+1})\in E_{1}
of type
t=1
for all
1\leq k<L_{*}
.
By
(C3)
,
\bm{L_{*}}\in I_{1,+}
and
\alpha(\bm{L_{*}})=\bm{1}
.
The graph
G_{m+1}
contains the edges
(\bm{k}\cdot(\bm{L_{*}})^{m},(\bm{k+1})\bm{1}^{m})
.
Here
\bm{k}^{m}\in V^{m}
refers to the constant sequence whose all indices are equal to
\bm{k}
.

Lemma 6.7
.

Let
\Xi
be a symmetric hypercubic IGS.
Then for all
m\in\mathbb{N}
,

(6.8)

d_{G_{m}}(\bm{1}^{m},\bm{L_{*}}^{m})\leq L_{*}^{m}-1.

Proof.

Clear for
m=1
. If it holds for
m\in\mathbb{N}
(IH) then

\displaystyle d_{G_{m+1}}(\bm{1}^{m+1},\bm{L_{*}}^{m+1})

\displaystyle\leq\quad

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{L_{*}}d_{G_{m+1}}(\bm{k}\bm{1}^{m},\bm{k}\bm{L_{*}}^{m})+\sum_{k=1}^{L_{*}-1}d_{G_{m+1}}(\bm{k}\bm{L_{*}}^{m},(\bm{k+1})\bm{1}^{m})

\displaystyle\leq\quad

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{L_{*}}d_{G_{m}}(\bm{1}^{m},\bm{L_{*}}^{m})+L_{*}-1

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathmakebox[\widthof{=}]{\text{(IH)}}}}{{\leq}}\quad

\displaystyle L_{*}\cdot(L_{*}^{m}-1)+L_{*}-1=L_{*}^{m+1}-1.

In the second inequality we used
d_{G_{n+m}}(x\cdot y,x\cdot z)\leq d_{G_{n}}(y,z)
which follows from the self-similarity of the graphs, Lemma
3.6
.
∎

Corollary 6.9
.

Let
\Xi
be a symmetric hypercubic IGS.
Then for all
n,m\in\mathbb{N}
and
x,y\in V^{m}
,

(6.10)

d_{G_{n+m}}(x\cdot\bm{1}^{n},y\cdot\bm{1}^{n})\leq d_{G_{m}}(x,y)\cdot L_{*}^{n}.

Proof.

It is sufficient to prove the case where
(w,v)\in E(G_{m})
. Moreover, by
(C4)
, we may assume that this edge is of type
t=1
, since in the other cases one can use symmetries
\alpha_{1,i}^{+}
which preserves the distances and fixes the point
\bm{1}
in
V
. Then

\displaystyle d_{G_{n+m}}(x\cdot\bm{1}^{n},y\cdot\bm{1}^{n})

\displaystyle\leq d_{G_{n+m}}(x\cdot\bm{1}^{n},x\cdot\bm{L_{*}}^{n})+d_{G_{n+m}}(x\cdot\bm{L_{*}}^{n},y\cdot\bm{1}^{n})

\displaystyle\leq d_{G_{n}}(\bm{1}^{n},\bm{L_{*}}^{n})+1

\displaystyle\leq L_{*}^{n}-1+1=L_{*}^{n}.

The first inequality is triangle inequality, second is self-similarity and third is Lemma
6.7
.
∎

Proposition 6.11
.

Let
\Xi
be a symmetric hypercubic IGS.
Then for all
m\in\mathbb{N}
and
x,y\in V^{m}
,

(6.12)

d_{G_{m}}(x,y)\leq 4\diam(G_{1})\cdot L_{*}^{m}.

Proof.

We first set
x=\bm{1}^{m}
and also write
y=v_{1}\ldots v_{m}
. In the computation below we use the formal notations
\pi_{m,0}(y)\cdot z=z
and
z\cdot\bm{1}^{0}=z
. Now we estimate

\displaystyle d_{G_{m}}(\bm{1}^{m},y)\leq

\displaystyle\quad\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}d_{G_{m}}\left(\pi_{m,j}(y)\cdot\bm{1}^{m-j},\pi_{m,j+1}(y)\cdot\bm{1}^{m-(j+1)}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\quad\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}d_{G_{m}}(\pi_{m,j}(y)\cdot\bm{1}\cdot\bm{1}^{m-(j+1)},\pi_{m,j}(y)\cdot v_{j+1}\cdot\bm{1}^{m-(j+1)})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\quad\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}d_{G_{m-j}}(\bm{1}\cdot\bm{1}^{m-(j+1)},v_{j+1}\cdot\bm{1}^{m-(j+1)})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\quad\diam(G_{1})\cdot\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}L_{*}^{m-(j+1)}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\quad 2\diam(G_{1})\cdot L_{*}^{m}.

In the second inequality, we used self-similarity and Corollary
6.9
in the third.
Thus, the aimed for inequality follows from

d_{G_{m}}(x,y)\leq d_{G_{m}}(x,\textbf{1}^{m})+d_{G_{m}}(\textbf{1}^{m},y)\leq 4\diam(G_{1})\cdot L_{*}^{m}.

∎

Next we adjust the previous argument to get the desired upper bound for the Sierpiński gasket and pentagonal carpet.

Lemma 6.13
.

If
\Xi
is the Sierpiński gasket and
i,j\in\{0,1,2\}
,

d_{G_{m}}(i^{m},j^{m})\leq 2^{m}-1.

If
\Xi
is the pentagonal carpet and
i,j\in\{0,1,2,3,4\}
then

d_{G_{m}}(i^{m},j^{m})\leq 2^{m+1}-1.

Proof.

First we give the argument for the Sierpiński gasket. By symmetry we may assume
i=0
and
j=1
. Since
(0,1)\in E
is an edge of type
a
and
(1,0)\in I_{a}
, the upper bound follows from an identical computation as in the proof of Lemma
6.7
.

Next we consider the case of the pentagonal Sierpiński carpet. First we assume that
i,j\in\{0,1,2,3,4\}
so that
j=i\pm 1\mod 5
. By symmetry, we may assume that
i=0
and
j=1
. Again, since
(0,1)\in E
is an edge of type
a
and
(1,0)\in I_{a}
, we can yet again conclude that

d_{G_{m}}(0^{m},1^{m})\leq 2^{m}-1.

It now follows that if
j=i\pm 2\mod 5
and
k:=i\pm 1\mod 5
we have

d_{G_{m}}(i^{m},j^{m})\leq d_{G_{m}}(i^{m},k^{m})+d_{G_{m}}(k^{m},j^{m})\leq 2^{m}-1+2^{m}-1\leq 2^{m+1}-1.

∎

Proposition 6.14
.

If
\Xi
is the Sierpiński gasket or the pentagonal carpet, then for all
m\in\mathbb{N}
and
x,y\in V^{m}
,

(6.15)

d_{G_{m}}(x,y)\leq 32\cdot L_{*}^{m}.

Proof.

We derive (
6.15
) only using the estimate

d_{G_{m}}(i^{m},j^{m})\leq 2^{m+1}-1,

and some properties of the IGSs satisfied by both examples.
By the identical argument as given in Corollary
6.9
, we have

d_{G_{n+m}}(x\cdot 0^{n},y\cdot 0^{n})\leq d_{G_{m}}(x,y)\cdot 2^{m+1}.

By following the argument given in Proposition
6.11
, we obtain

d_{G_{m}}(0^{m},y)\leq 4\diam(G_{1})\cdot 2^{m+1}=8\cdot 2^{m+1}.

The desired inequality then follows from the triangle inequality.
∎

We now have suitable diameter upper bounds. Next we deal with some lower bounds.

Proposition 6.16
.

Let
\Xi
be a hypercubic IGS, Sierpiński gasket or pentagonal carpet. Then for all
m\in\mathbb{N}
and
t\in\mathcal{T}
,

d_{G_{m}}\left(I_{t,+}^{m},I_{t,-}^{m}\right)\geq 1/2\cdot L_{*}^{m}.

Proof.

We deal with the hypercubic case first. Our method uses the following simple observation. Let
c_{i}
be the coordinate functions for
i\in\mathcal{T}
and consider the interval graph
\mathbb{I}_{m}
whose vertex set is
\{0,1,\ldots L_{*}^{m}-1\}
and
\{k,l\}\in E(\mathbb{I}_{m})
if and only if
\lvert k-l\rvert=1
. Then the map
\varphi_{m,t}:V^{m}\to V(\mathbb{I}_{m})
,

\varphi_{m,t}(v_{1}\ldots v_{m}):=\sum_{i=1}^{m}(c_{t}(v_{i})-1)\cdot L_{*}^{m-i}

is simplicial
G_{m}\to\mathbb{I}_{m}
. These maps are counterparts of the coordinate projections
(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d})\mapsto x_{i}
of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
The fact that
\varphi_{m,t}
is simplicial is verified using the definition of the gluing rules and Lemma
3.6
.

Now, because
\varphi_{m}
is simplicial,

d_{G_{m}}(x,y)\geq d_{\mathbb{I}_{m}}(\varphi_{m}(x),\varphi_{m}(y)).

Since
\varphi_{m}(I_{t,-}^{m})=0
and
\varphi_{m}(I_{t,+}^{m})=L_{*}^{m}-1
, this concludes the hypercubic case.

For the gasket, we will show by induction on
m\in\mathbb{N}
that

d_{G_{m}}(0^{m},1^{m})\geq 2^{m}-1

for all
m\in\mathbb{N}
. By symmetry, this is sufficient.

The claim is clear for
m=1
. Assume that we have it for
m\in\mathbb{N}
and let
\theta
be a path connecting
0^{m+1}
to
1^{m+1}
.
Consider the non-trivial symmetry of the equilateral triangle
\alpha
which fixes
0^{m+1}
. This induces a graph isomorphism
G_{m}\to G_{m}
according to Remark
4.7
. It also satisfies the conditions in Proposition
3.16
where the partition
V(G)=C\cup D\cup\Delta
is
\Delta=\{0^{m+1}\}
,

C=\{v_{1}\ldots v_{m+1}\notin\Delta:v_{k}=1\text{ where }k=\min_{l}v_{l}\neq 0\}

and
D
contains the rest.
Now, let
f
be as in Proposition
3.16
and
\theta^{\prime}
be the path obtained by mapping
\theta
by
f
and removing the consecutive tuples.
We observe that
\theta^{\prime}
is contained in
0\cdot V^{m}\cup 1\cdot V^{m}
and that
\{0\cdot 1^{m},1\cdot 0^{m}\}
is the only edge connecting
0\cdot V^{m}
to
1\cdot V^{m}
. So
\theta^{\prime}
must contain disjoint sub-paths
\theta_{1}
connecting
0^{m+1}
to

and contained in
0\cdot V^{m}
, and
\theta_{2}
connecting

to
1^{m+1}
and contained in

. It also must contain the edge

.
By applying the induction hypothesis,

\len(\theta)\geq\len(\theta^{\prime})\geq\len(\theta_{1})+\len(\theta_{2})+1\geq 2^{m}-1+2^{m}-1+1=2^{m+1}.

We conclude the argument for the gasket.

Next, we deal with the pentagonal carpet; recall the notation from Example
4.8

We show by induction that

d_{G_{m}}(L_{0}^{m},L_{2}^{m}\cup L_{3}^{m})\geq 2^{m}-1,

which is sufficient by symmetry.
The base case is yet again trivial.
The inductive step is almost the same as for the gasket. We take the non-trivial symmetry of the regular pentagon that fixes the vertex
0^{m+1}
, and similarly fold the path onto a suitable smaller subset. Then we use induction hypothesis to find two disjoint paths with suitable length lower bound and add them together.
∎

Proof of Theorem
6.1
.

We begin with the first inclusion, and we shall prove it for

M_{*}:=\sup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\max_{\theta}\diam(\theta,G_{n})+1

where the maximum is taken over all non-collapsing paths
\theta
in

.
Now, let
y\in V^{m}
so that
d(\pi_{n+m,m}(x),y)\geq M_{*}
and choose any

with a path
\theta
from

to

. Let
\mathcal{C}
and
\fold_{m}(\theta)
be as in Lemma
3.12
. First note that the condition for
\mathcal{C}
in Lemma

must fail, namely there is no common vertex for all the members in

because otherwise, the path
[h_{1},\ldots,h_{k}]
in Lemma

would be non-collapsing. But this would contradict the choice of

For these three classes of examples, the only possibility for the members in

to not have a common vertex, is that the folded path

connects the sets

for some

. Since the folded path is contained in

The latter inclusion, which reads

∎

We need a couple more geometric properties. The first one regards the self-similarity of the path metric.

Proposition 6.17
.

Let
\Xi
be a symmetric hypercubic IGS, the Sierpiński gasket or the pentagonal carpet. Then for all
n,m\in\mathbb{N}
and
x\in V^{n}
,
y,z\in V^{m}
, the path metrics satisfy

d_{G_{n+m}}(x\cdot y,x\cdot z)=d_{G_{m}}(y,z).

Proof.

The inequality
d_{G_{n+m}}(x\cdot y,x\cdot z)\leq d_{G_{m}}(y,z)
follows from the self-similarity of the graphs, Lemma
3.6
. We are left to show the opposite inequality. By induction, we may assume
n=1
and
x\in V
.

We first check the symmetric hypercubic case. The dimension parameter is denoted
d
.
We consider the following folding map. For every
w\in V
we define the composition

\alpha_{w}:=\prod_{i=1}^{d}\alpha_{i}^{\lvert c_{i}(x)-c_{i}(w)\rvert}

of the of horizontal symmetries
\alpha_{i}
for
i\in\mathcal{T}
.
Note that the horizontal symmetries for different types commute with each other so the above definition is well-defined.

Set
f(w\cdot y):=x\cdot\alpha_{w}(y)
. This is a simplicial map
G\to G
onto
x\cdot V^{m}
satisfying
f|_{x\cdot V^{m}}=\id_{x\cdot V^{m}}
. To see that it is simplicial, let
\{y,z\}\in E_{n+1}
. If
y,z\in w\cdot V^{m}
for the same
w
, then
\{y,z\}
is mapped to an edge because horizontal symmetries are graph isomorphisms. If
y=w\cdot y^{\prime}
and
y=u\cdot z^{\prime}
for distinct
w,u\in V
, then
\alpha_{w}=\alpha_{u}\circ\alpha_{i}
where
i
is the type of
\{x,w\}
. It then follows from Lemma
3.6
that
\alpha_{w}(y^{\prime})=\alpha_{u}(z^{\prime})
, meaning
f(y)=f(z)
. Thus, the converse inequality follows because
f
maps any path between points in
x\cdot V^{m}
to path between the same vertices fully contained in
x\cdot V^{m}
whose length is less than or equal to the original length. Here, we use that the induced graph of
x\cdot V^{m}
is isomorphic to
G_{m}
, by Lemma
3.6
.

Next we deal with the pentagonal carpet.
We again assume
n=1
and
x\in V
, and perform a similar folding argument, i.e. construct a simplicial map
f:G_{m+1}\to G_{m+1}
onto
x\cdot V^{m}
with
f|_{x\cdot V^{m}}=\id_{x\cdot V^{m}}
.
By symmetry, we assume
x=0
. Let
\alpha
be the non-trivial symmetry of
G_{m+1}
that fixes
2^{m+1}
. Then
\alpha
satisfies the conditions in Proposition
3.16
and we can choose the partition so that
C\cup\Delta\subseteq 0\cdot V^{m}\cup 1\cdot V^{m}
. We first take the folding map
f_{1}
onto the subset
C\cup\Delta
. Then if
\beta
is the non-trivial symmetry of
G_{m+1}
that fixes
3^{m+1}
, we see that
f(0\cdot V^{m}\cup 1\cdot V^{m})=0\cdot V^{m}\cup 1\cdot V^{m}
. By applying Proposition
3.16
to
\beta
restricted to the subgraph induced by
0\cdot V^{m}\cup 1\cdot V^{m}
, we find a simplicial map
f_{2}
onto
0\cdot V^{m}
.
We then take
f=f_{2}\circ f_{1}
.

The argument for the gasket is very similar to the one for the pentagon and employs twice applying Proposition
3.16
to symmetries fixing vertices
1^{m+1}
and
2^{m+1}
.
∎

The following proposition yields the existence of an “interior point” to sets
G_{m}
which is far from the “boundary” of
G_{m}
.
For
m\in\mathbb{N}
we denote
\partial G_{m}\subseteq V^{m}
the union of all the sets
I_{t,+}^{m},\,I_{t,-}^{m}
for all
t\in\mathcal{T}
.

Proposition 6.18
.

Let
\Xi
be a symmetric hypercubic IGS, the Sierpiński gasket or the pentagonal carpet, and let
M_{*}\geq 1
be the constant in Theorem
6.1
. Then there is
m\in\mathbb{N}
and
v_{*}\in V^{m}
so that
B(v_{*},M_{*}+1)\cap\partial G_{m}=\emptyset
.

Proof.

We consider the symmetric hypercubic case first. We use the functions
\varphi_{m,t}
from the proof of Proposition
6.16
.
First fix some
m\in\mathbb{N}
.
Let
v,w\in V
so that

\begin{cases}c_{t}(v)=1&\text{ for all }t\in\mathcal{T}\\
\xi(v)=1\end{cases}\quad\text{ and }\quad\begin{cases}c_{t}(w)=L_{*}&\text{ for all }t\in\mathcal{T}\\
\xi(w)=1.\end{cases}

Then we choose the sequence
v_{3},\dots,v_{m+2}\in V
arbitrarily and set

v_{*}:=vwv_{3}\ldots v_{m+2}\in V^{m+2}.

Then for each

,

\displaystyle L_{*}^{m}

\displaystyle\leq(L_{*}-1)\cdot L_{*}^{m}=(c_{t}(w)-1)\cdot L_{*}^{m}\leq\varphi_{m+2,t}(v_{*}).

Because
c_{t}(v)=1
, we have

\varphi_{m+2,t}(v_{*})\leq\sum_{i=2}^{m+2}(L_{*}-1)L_{*}^{m+2-i}=L_{*}^{m+1}-1.

Because the maps
\varphi_{m,t}
are simplicial and
\varphi_{m,t}(I_{t,\pm}^{m+2})\in\{0,L_{*}^{m+2}-1\}
,

d_{G_{m+2}}(v_{*},\partial G_{m+2})\geq L_{*}^{m},

and we are therefore done by choosing
m\in\mathbb{N}
so that
L_{*}^{m}>M_{*}
.

Next we consider the gasket and the pentagonal carpet.
A similar argument works for both so we omit the gasket case. First fix
m\geq 1
and set
v_{*}:=02^{m}
. Consider the folding map
f
onto
0\cdot V^{m}
that was defined during the proof of Proposition
6.17
. Note that
f
satisfies

f(\partial G_{m+1})=\partial G_{m+1}\cap 0\cdot V^{m}=0\cdot L_{2}^{m}\cup 0\cdot L_{3}^{m}.

Because

and

simplicial onto the subgraph induced by the subset

,

d_{G_{m+1}}(v_{*},\partial G_{m+1})=d_{G_{m+1}}(v_{*},0\cdot L_{2}^{m}\cup 0\cdot L_{3}^{m}).

By combining Propositions
6.16
and
6.17
,

d_{G_{m+1}}(v_{*},0\cdot L_{2}^{m}\cup 0\cdot L_{3}^{m})=d_{G_{m}}(2^{m},L_{2}^{m}\cup L_{3}^{m})\geq 1/2L_{*}^{m}.

We are done by combining the previous two equations.
∎

Finally, we need one more technical condition for symmetric hypercubic IGSs and the pentagonal carpet.

Proposition 6.19
.

Let
\Xi
be a symmetric hypercubic IGSs or the pentagonal carpet. Then for all
m\in\mathbb{N}
and
t\in\mathcal{T}
the graph
G_{m}
contains
2^{m}
pair-wise disjoint paths from
I_{t,+}^{m}
to
I_{t,-}^{m}
.

Proof.

We argue the hypercubic case first. By symmetry we assume
t=1\in\mathcal{T}
. Note that we essentially constructed one path during the proof of Lemma
6.7
by constructing a “line segment” between “corner points”.
The construction is quite intuitive by examining Figure
1
. Let
\theta_{1}
be that path on
G_{1}
.
Then we take another similar line segment
\theta_{2}\in G_{1}
between two corner points. For instance it can be obtained by applying to
\theta_{2}
the horizontal symmetry
\alpha_{2}
for the type
2\in\mathcal{T}
. These are clearly disjoint paths. This gives the base case of the induction. Supposing now that we have constructed
2^{m}
paths in
G_{m}
, we can obtain
2^{m}
paths in
G_{m+1}
by gluing the self-similar copies of these in the subgraphs
v\cdot G_{m}
for
v\in\theta_{i}
for
i=1,2
. This way one can construct
2^{m+1}
disjoint paths between
I_{1,+}^{m+1}
to
I_{1,-}^{m+1}
.

Next we deal with the pentagonal carpet. By symmetry we assume
\{I_{t,+},I_{t,-}\}=\{L_{2},L_{4}\}
.
The idea is essentially the same as above but we make use of a symmetry trick.
We warmly recommend the reader to make heavy use of Figure
2
.

We prove by induction on
m
that for all
x\in L_{2}^{m}
there is a path connecting
x
to
L_{4}^{m}
so that these paths are disjoint for different
x,y\in L_{2}^{m}
.
The base case
m=1
is obvious so we make an inductive hypothesis (IH) for
m\in\mathbb{N}
.
By self-similarity, for every vertex in
x\in 0\cdot L_{2}^{m}
there is a path that connects it to some
y\in 1\cdot L_{2}^{m}
and the paths are pair-wise disjoint. By applying self-similarity and IH once again, we can find a path from
y
to
z\in 1\cdot L_{4}^{m}\subseteq L_{4}^{m+1}
. By connecting these two paths together, we obtain a disjoint family of
2^{m}
paths from
0\cdot L_{2}^{m}
to
1\cdot L_{4}^{m}
.
It is clear by construction that these paths are contained in
0\cdot V^{m}\cup 1\cdot V^{m}
.

Next we construct another
2^{m}
pair-wise disjoint paths so that they are contained in
2\cdot V^{m}\cup 3\cdot V^{m}\cup 4\cdot V^{m}
. Then we would be done because these do not intersect with the previously constructed ones.
By applying a suitable symmetry of the regular pentagon and the IH, we have
2^{m}
disjoint paths from
L_{2}^{m}
to
L_{0}^{m}
.
Thus, we have a path connecting
x\in 4\cdot L_{2}^{m}
to
y\in 3\cdot L_{2}^{m}
. Then by applying the IH again, we can connect
y\in 3\cdot L_{2}^{m}
to some
z\in 2\cdot L_{1}^{m}
. We use the IH one last time, and a suitable symmetry in the same way as in the beginning of the paragraph, to get a path from
z\in 2\cdot L_{1}^{m}
to
w\in 2\cdot L_{4}^{m}
. We have found additional
2^{m}
pair-wise disjoint paths. Because these are contained in
2\cdot V^{m}\cup 3\cdot V^{m}\cup 4\cdot V^{m}
, and the previously constructed once are contained in
0\cdot V^{m}\cup 1\cdot V^{m}
, the union of these two families is a family of paths we were looking for.
∎

Remark 6.20
.

In the previous proof, one may notice an interesting feature of the pentagonal carpet. The argument shows that the constructed family of paths is a partition of
V^{m}
, meaning not only that it is a disjoint family of paths but also that it covers the vertex set.
We do not make any use of this here but it may be useful in future works.

7.
Construction of the limit space

The rest of the work studies limit spaces produced by IGSs/replacement graphs. These are constructed by taking limits of rescaled path-metrics.
This choice is largely driven by a desire to give a simpler analysis and to avoid many technical issues.
For instance, it simplifies the proof of approximate self-similarity.
Other metrics could also be considered, such as visual metrics
[
12
]
that correspond to an associated iterative subdivision of the constructed fractal; see also
[
32
]
.
Conceptually, the main requirements for the construction is for it to produce a quasivisual approximation in the sense of
[
13
]
.
We do not need or use this language in the present paper since the rescaled path metrics offer a very convenient and simple framework for our results. More complex metrics may be needed in non-symmetric settings; compare for example with the well known example of the so called Rickman rug
[
35
]
.

7.2.
Geometry of the limit space

We are now well-prepared to define the limit space.
Throughout the rest of the section, we consider a fixed IGS satisfying Assumption
7.1
.

We first define the
symbol space

\Sigma:=V^{\mathbb{N}}
, and equip it with a metric
\delta
and a Radon measure
\mathfrak{m}
. The points in
\Sigma
are written with the sequence notation
\omega=\omega_{1}\omega_{2}\ldots\in\Sigma
.
We define the natural projections
\pi_{n}(\omega_{1}\omega_{2}\ldots):=\omega_{1}\omega_{2}\ldots\omega_{n}\in V^{n}
. For a given
v\in V^{n}
we denote
\Sigma_{v}:=\pi_{n}^{-1}(v)\subseteq\Sigma
.

The metric
\delta
is the
word metric

\delta(\omega,\tau):=\begin{cases}0&\text{ if }\omega=\tau\\
2^{-k}&\text{ if $\omega\neq\tau$ where }k=\min\{l:\pi_{l}(\omega)\neq\pi_{l}(\tau)\}.\end{cases}

The Radon measure
\mathfrak{m}
is defined as the measure which satisfies

\mathfrak{m}(\Sigma_{v}):=\lvert V\rvert^{-n}\text{ for all }v\in V^{n}.

In other words,
\mathfrak{m}
is a Bernoulli measure with uniform weights. It follows from Caratheodory’s extension theorem that the condition above uniquely determines a Radon measure on the metric space
(\Sigma,\delta)
.

Definition 7.5
.

Let
d
be the semi-metric on
\Sigma
given by

d(\omega,\tau):=\limsup_{n\to\infty}L_{*}^{-n}d_{G_{n}}(\pi_{n}(\omega),\pi_{n}(\tau)).

Here
L_{*}
is the same as in Assumption
7.1
.
We define the set
X
as the quotient
X:=\Sigma/\sim
where
\omega\sim\tau
if and only if
d(\omega,\tau)=0
.
Note that
d(x,y)
for
x,y\in X
does not depend on the choices of the representatives in
\Sigma
by the triangle inequality of path metrics.
We denote the canonical projection
\chi:\Sigma\to X
.
Lastly, we define the push-forward measure
\mu:=\chi_{*}(\mathfrak{m})
given by
\mu(A):=\mathfrak{m}(\chi^{-1}(A))
. The metric space
(X,d)
is the
limit space
of the IGS
\Xi
.

The following proposition shows that our limit spaces are self-similar.
For
v\in V^{n}
and
\omega\in\Sigma
we define the symbolic product
v\cdot\omega\in\Sigma
by appending the sequence
\omega
by
v
. This satisfies
\pi_{n+k}(v\cdot\omega)=v\cdot\pi_{k}(\omega)
for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
.

Proposition 7.6
.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
v\in V^{n}
.
The mapping
\chi(\omega)\mapsto\chi(v\cdot\omega)
is a well-defined
L_{*}^{-n}
-Lipschitz function
X\to X
.

Proof.

It follows from Lemma
3.6
,

\displaystyle d_{G_{n+m}}(\pi_{n+m}(v\cdot\omega),\pi_{n+m}(v\cdot\tau))

\displaystyle=d_{G_{n+m}}(v\cdot\pi_{m}(\omega),v\cdot\pi_{m}(\tau))

\displaystyle\leq d_{G_{m}}(\pi_{m}(\omega),\pi_{m}(\tau)).

The claim now follows from the definition of the metric.
∎

Remark 7.7
.

If the mappings defined in Proposition
7.6
are injective, then our construction would produce a self-similar structure in the sense of Kigami
[
31
]
. This for instance would hold if the conclusion of Proposition
6.17
is valid.

We state the main goal of the section. Recall the terminology from Section
2
.

Theorem 7.8
.

Let
(X,d)
be a metric space which can be realized as a limit space of an IGS
\Xi
satisfying Assumption
7.1
, and let
c_{1},c_{2},L_{*}
be as stated therein.

(1)

(X,d)
is compact, quasiconvex and
Q
-Ahlfors regular for

Q:=\frac{\log(\lvert V\rvert)}{\log(L_{*})}.

(2)

(X,d)
is approximately self-similar.

(3)

There exists
\alpha>1
so that for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
v\in V^{n}
the set
X_{v}:=\chi(\Sigma_{v})
contains a point
z_{v}\in X_{v}
satisfying the following properties.

(a)

B(z_{v},\alpha^{-1}L_{*}^{-n})\subseteq X_{v}\subseteq B(z_{v},\alpha L_{*}^{-n})
.

(b)

If
v,w\in V^{n}
are distinct,

B(z_{v},\alpha^{-1}L_{*}^{-n})\cap B(z_{w},\alpha^{-1}L_{*}^{-n})=\emptyset.

In particular, the coverings
\{X_{v}\}_{v\in V^{n}}
form an
\alpha
-approximation of
X
.

Before proving this theorem, we establish first some auxiliary lemmas.

Lemma 7.9
.

Let
M_{*}
be as in Assumption
7.1
and
x=\chi(\omega)\in X
.
If
x\in X_{v}
for
v\in V^{n}
, then
v\in B(\pi_{n}(\omega),M_{*})
.

Proof.

This follows from
(M1)
. Indeed, if
v\notin B(\pi_{n}(\omega),M_{*})
, then by the definition of the metric
d
,

d(x,y)\geq c_{1}L_{*}^{-n}\text{ for all }y\in X_{v}.

∎

Lemma 7.10
.

Let
c_{1},c_{2},M_{*},L_{*}
be as in Assumption
7.1
and
x:=\chi(\omega)\in X
.
Then for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
,

B(x,c_{1}L_{*}^{-n})\subseteq\bigcup_{w\in B(\pi_{n}(\omega),M_{*})}X_{w},

and also

\bigcup_{w\in B(\pi_{n}(\omega),M_{*})}X_{w}\subseteq B(x,c_{2}L_{*}^{-n}).

Proof.

Let
w\in V^{n}\setminus B(\pi_{n}(\omega,M_{*}))
and
y=\chi(\tau)
so that
\tau\in\Sigma_{w}
. To prove the first inclusion, it is sufficient to show
y\notin B(x,c_{1}L_{*}^{n})
.

Now, by
(M1)
,
d_{G_{n+m}}(\pi_{n+m}(\omega),\pi_{n+m}(\tau))\geq c_{1}L_{*}^{m}
. By letting
m\to\infty
,

d(x,y)=\limsup_{m\to\infty}L_{*}^{-(n+m)}d_{G_{n+m}}(\pi_{n+m}(\omega),\pi_{n+m}(\tau))\geq c_{1}L_{*}^{-n}.

The second inclusion follows from a similar argument using
(M2)
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
7.8
-(
1
).

Because
(\Sigma,\delta)
is compact, the compactess of
(X,d)
follows once we show that
\chi
is continuous. First, by applying
(M2)
for
n=1
, we see that there is a constant
C
satisfying
\diam(V^{m},d_{G_{m}})\leq CL_{*}^{m}
for all
m\in\mathbb{N}
. By the self-similarity of the graphs,
\diam(v\cdot V^{m},d_{G_{n+m}})\leq CL_{*}^{m}
for all
v\in V^{n}
and
n\in\mathbb{N}
. This then implies

\diam(\chi(\Sigma_{v}))\leq CL_{*}^{-n},

so the continuity of
\chi
follows from the definition of the word metric
\delta
.

We next show that
(X,d,\mu)
is
Q
-Ahlfors regular. Note that Lemma
7.9
implies for all
v\in V^{n}
,

\chi^{-1}(X_{v})\subseteq\bigcup_{w\in B(v,M_{*})}\Sigma_{w}.

We combine this with the first inclusion in Lemma
7.10
and the definition of
\mu,\,\mathfrak{m}
,

\mu(B(x,c_{1}L_{*}^{-n}))\leq\bigcup_{w\in B(\pi_{n}(\omega),2M_{*})}\mathfrak{m}(\Sigma_{w})\leq C\lvert V\rvert^{-n}=C(L_{*}^{-n})^{Q},

where
C
depends on
M_{*}
and the maximum degree of the replacement graphs. Now for a given
r\leq 2\diam(X)
, we get the upper inequality in the definition of
Q
-Ahlfors regularity by taking
n\in\mathbb{N}
with
CL_{*}^{-n-1}\leq r\leq CL_{*}^{-n}
for suitable
C
. The lower inequality is obtained similarily by using the second inclusion in Lemma
7.10
. This gives
Q
-Ahlfors regularity.

We now verify quasiconvexity. Let
x,y\in X
be distinct points and write
x=\chi(\omega),\,y=\chi(\tau)
. We also fix a constant
C\geq 1
which we determine during the proof. Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
so that
CL_{*}^{-n-1}\leq d(x,y)<CL_{*}^{-n}
. By a suitable choice of
C
, there is, according to Lemma
7.10
, another constant
K\geq 1
depending on
C
so that for all
m\in\mathbb{N}
,

\pi_{n+m}(\omega),\,\pi_{n+m}(\tau)\in\bigcup_{w\in B(\pi_{n}(\omega),K)}w\cdot V^{m}.

By the argument at the beginning of the proof,
d_{G_{n+m}}(\pi_{n+m}(\omega),\pi_{n+m}(\tau))\leq C_{1}L_{*}^{m}
for all
m\in\mathbb{N}
.

Next for all
m\in\mathbb{N}
we take discrete paths
\theta_{m}=[u_{1,m},\ldots,u_{k_{m}+1,m}]
in
G_{n+m}
connecting
\pi_{n+m}(\omega)
to
\pi_{n+m}(\tau)
and having length at most
k_{m}\leq C_{1}L_{*}^{m}
. We also fix arbitrary points
z_{i,m}\in X_{u_{i,m}}
. According to the second inclusion in Lemma
7.10
,
d(z_{i,m},z_{i+1,m})\leq c_{2}L_{*}^{-(n+m)}
. Thus we have

\sum_{i=1}^{k_{m}}d(z_{i,m},z_{i+1,m})\leq C_{1}c_{2}L_{*}^{-n}.

By letting
m\to\infty
, we obtain by Arzela–Ascoli theorem a continuous curve from
x
to
y
of length at most
C_{1}c_{2}L_{*}^{-n}
; see for instance
[
8
, Proof of Theorem 4.32]
for an analogous argument. By recalling how
n
was defined, quasiconvexity follows.
∎

Next, we deal with approximate self-similarity. To this end, we need a new definition. First, we fix a sufficiently large constant
N\geq 1
which is determined during the proof below. We let
\mathcal{N}
to be a certain finite collection of balls
B(v,N)
where
v\in V^{n}
for some
n\in\mathbb{N}
.
The number
n
can be different for different members.

The members of
\mathcal{N}
are chosen so that the following condition holds. For every
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
v\in V^{n}
there is
B\in\mathcal{N}
and a graph isomorphism between the subgraphs induced by
B
and
B(v,N)
that preserves types and orientations of the edges. Such isomorphism lifts to higher level graphs by Lemma
3.6
; the explicit description is given in the proof below.
The set
\mathcal{N}
can be chosen to be finite because the degrees of replacement graphs are assumed to be uniformly bounded.

Proof of Theorem
7.8
-(
3
).

Let
x=\chi(\omega)
and
r>0
. Recall that the goal is to find a suitable biLipschitz embedding
F:B(x,r)\to X
.
We may assume that
r
is suitably small because otherwise we may simply choose
F
to be the inclusion
B(x,r)\hookrightarrow X
.
Fix a constant
C\geq 1
, whose value is determined later in the proof, and let
n\in\mathbb{N}
with
CL_{*}^{-n-1}<r\leq CL_{*}^{-n}
.

Now, let
B(w,N)\in\mathcal{N}
for
w\in V^{m}
so that there exists an isomorphism
f:B(\pi_{n}(\omega),N)\to B(w,N)
as we discussed right before the proof. Then
f
has natural lifts
f_{k}:\pi_{n+k,n}^{-1}(B(\pi_{n}(\omega),N))\to\pi_{m+k,m}^{-1}(B(w,N))
given by

f_{k}(v\cdot z):=f(v)\cdot z\text{ for all }v\in B(\pi_{n}(\omega),N)\text{ and }z\in V^{k}.

We claim that the restriction of
f_{k}
onto
{\pi_{n+k,n}^{-1}(B(\pi_{n}(\omega),2M_{*}))}
is an isometry onto the image with respect to the path metrics if we choose
N
to be suitably large. To see this, we combine
(M1)
and
(M2)
,

\pi_{n+k,n}^{-1}(B(\pi_{n}(\omega),2M_{*}))\subseteq B(z,C_{1}L_{*}^{k})\subseteq B(z,3C_{1}L_{*}^{k})\subseteq\pi_{n+k,n}^{-1}(B(\pi_{n}(\omega),N))

where
z=\pi_{n+k}(\omega)
,
C_{1}
depends only on the fixed constant
M_{*}
and
N
is sufficiently large. In particular, the shortest paths between vertices in
\pi_{n+k,n}^{-1}(B(\pi_{n}(\omega),2M_{*}))
are contained in
\pi_{n+k,n}^{-1}(B(\pi_{n}(\omega),N))
. These paths are thus mapped by
f_{k}
to the
\pi_{n+k,n}^{-1}(B(w,N))
. This shows that
f_{k}
does not increase distances. By performing the same analysis in reverse, we conclude that the restriction of
f_{k}
to
\pi_{n+k,n}^{-1}(B(\pi_{n}(\omega),M_{*}))
is an isometry.

The final step is to take a limit of
f_{k}
. By the observation above,

F:\bigcup_{v\in B(\pi_{n}(\omega),2M_{*})}X_{v}\to\bigcup_{u\in B(w,2M_{*})}X_{u},\quad F(\chi(v\cdot\tau)):=\chi(f(v)\cdot\tau),

is a well-defined homemorphims.
Since the discrete functions were isometric embeddings,
F
satisfies

\lvert F(y)-F(z)\rvert=L_{*}^{n-m}d(y,z).

Recall that
n\in\mathbb{N}
was chosen so that
r\approx L_{*}^{-n}
. Also
m
has an upper bound because the set
\mathcal{N}
is finite.
We get

L^{-1}\frac{d(y,z)}{r}\leq\lvert F(y)-F(z)\rvert\leq L\frac{d(y,z)}{r}

where
L
depends on
m
and the other constants used during the proof.

To conclude the approximate self-similarity, what is left is to show that
F(B(x,r))\subseteq X
is an open set. To see this, notice that
F(B(x,r))=B(F(x),rL_{*}^{n-m})
. Indeed, this equality follows since

B(x,r)\subseteq\bigcup_{v\in B(\pi_{n}(\omega),M_{*})}X_{v},

and

B(F(x),r)\subset\bigcup_{u\in B(w,M_{*})}X_{u}

and
F
is an isometry on a set containing
\bigcup_{v\in B(\pi_{n}(\omega),M_{*})}X_{v}
onto a set that contains
\bigcup_{u\in B(w,M_{*})}X_{u}
(with the metric scaled by
L_{*}^{n-m}
).
∎

We are left to check the properties of an
\alpha
-approximation.

Proof of Theorem
7.8
-(
3
).

Let
n_{0},\,v_{*}
be as in
(M3)
. Fix
n\in\mathbb{N}
and choose
v\in V^{n}
. Then we choose
z_{v}=\chi(\omega_{v})\in X_{v\cdot v_{*}}
for any point
\omega_{v}\in\Sigma_{v\cdot v^{*}}
.

First, we claim that

(7.11)

B(v\cdot v_{*},M_{*}+1)\subseteq v\cdot(V^{n_{0}}\setminus\partial G_{n_{0}}).

This follows from the following observation. According to Lemma
3.6
, any path in
G_{n+n_{0}}
which exits
v\cdot V^{n_{0}}
goes through
v\cdot\partial G_{n_{0}}
.
Then
7.11
follows from
(M3)
.

Then we combine (
7.11
) with the first inclusion of Lemma
7.10
,

B(z_{v},c_{1}L_{*}^{-n})\subseteq\bigcup_{w\in B(\pi_{n}(\omega_{v}),M_{*})}X_{w}\subseteq X_{v}.

Also the combination of (
7.11
) and Lemma
7.9
yield

B(z_{v},c_{1}L_{*}^{-n}/2)\cap X_{w}=\emptyset

for all
w\in V^{n}\setminus\{v\}
.
Thus we have the desired result.
∎

We finish the section by establishing some properties of the natural incidence graphs associated to the closed coverings
\{X_{v}\}_{v\in V^{n}}
for
n\in\mathbb{N}
.
We denote these
\mathbb{G}_{n}:=(V^{n},\mathbb{E}_{n})
where we have identified the vertices
V^{n}
with their associated sets
X_{v}
.
Our covering is not open, but if one wishes to have an open covering, we could replace
X_{v}
with the interiors of

\bigcup_{w\in B(v,M_{*})}X_{w}.

This would result in adding some edges, and is only a matter of the preference. Both should work equally well because of Proposition
2.12
.

Corollary 7.12
.

If
\mathbb{G}_{n}
are as above then the identity map
\id_{V^{n}}:(V^{n},d_{G_{n}})\to(V^{n},d_{\mathbb{G}_{n}})
is a
2M_{*}
-biLipschitz simplicial map
G_{n}\to\mathbb{G}_{n}
. Moreover, there is a constant
C\geq 1
so that for all
p\geq 1
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
and any disjoint pair of non-empty subsets
A,B\subseteq V^{n}
,

C^{-1}\Mod_{p}(A,B,\mathbb{G}_{n})\leq\Mod_{p}(A,B,G_{n})\leq C\Mod_{p}(A,B,\mathbb{G}_{n}).

Proof.

To see that the identity is simplicial
G_{n}\to\mathbb{G}_{n}
, or in the other words
E_{n}\subseteq\mathbb{E}_{n}
, fix an edge
(v,w)\in E_{n}
. By Lemma
3.6
there are
\omega,\tau\in\Sigma
so that

(\pi_{m}(v\cdot\omega),\pi_{m}(w\cdot\tau))\in E_{m}\text{ for all }m\geq n.

Thus
\chi(v\cdot\omega)=\chi(w\cdot\tau)
, meaning that
X_{v}\cap X_{w}\neq\emptyset
. By definition,
\{v,w\}\in\mathbb{E}_{n}
, and
E_{n}\subseteq\mathbb{E}_{n}
is now clear.

Note that
d_{\mathbb{G}_{n}}\leq d_{G_{n}}
is obvious from
E_{n}\subseteq\mathbb{E}_{n}
. On the other hand, if
\{v,w\}\in\mathbb{E}_{n}
, then
d_{G_{n}}(v,w)<2M_{*}
. Indeed, according to Lemma
7.9
,
\chi(\omega)\in X_{v}\cap X_{w}
for some
\omega\in\Sigma
implies

d_{G_{n}}(v,w)\leq d_{G_{n}}(v,\pi_{n}(\omega))+d_{G_{n}}(\pi_{n}(\omega),w)<2M_{*}.

We conclude
d_{G_{n}}\leq 2M_{*}d_{\mathbb{G}_{n}}
.

Lastly, we deal with the modulus estimate.
Note that the upper inequality holds for
C=1
because
E_{n}\subseteq\mathbb{E}_{n}
. For the lower one, we argue as follows.
To prove the converse, let
\rho:V^{n}\to[0,\infty)
be
\Theta(A,B,G_{n})
-admissible. Define

\rho^{\prime}(v):=2M_{*}\max_{d_{G_{n}}(v,w)\leq 2M_{*}}\rho(w).

Because
d_{G_{m}}\leq 2M_{*}d_{\mathbb{G}_{m}}
,
\rho^{\prime}
is
\Theta(A,B,\mathbb{G}_{n})
-admissible, and it has the
p
-mass estimate

\cM_{p}(\rho^{\prime})\leq 2M_{*}C^{2M_{*}}\cM_{p}(\rho)

where
C\geq 1
is a constant bounding the degrees of
G_{n}
.
∎

8.
Combinatorial Loewner property

The final section of the paper regards the combinatorial Loewner property of the limit space, and the super-multiplicativity inequality of discrete modulus.

We recall the limit space
X:=(X,d)
of an IGS from Definition
7.5
, and also the notation related to discrete modulus from Subsection
2.3
.
In particular, we recall the discrete moduli (
2.14
), which are defined

\cM_{p,\delta}^{(n)}:=\Mod_{p}(\Gamma_{\delta},\mathbb{G}_{n})

where the
\alpha
-approximations
\mathbb{G}_{n}:=(V^{n},\mathbb{E}_{n})
are given by the incidence graphs of
\{X_{v}\}_{v\in V^{n}}
and
\Gamma_{\delta}=\{\gamma:\diam(\gamma)\geq\delta\}
.

The goal of the rest of the paper is to prove the following two theorems. The first one regards the
super-multiplicativity inequality
. For expository reason we include the sub-multiplicativity as well but note that it already follows from Bourdon–Kleiner (Proposition
2.16
).

Theorem 8.1
.

Let
(X,d)
be a metric space which can be realized as a limit space of an IGS
\Xi
satisfying Assumptions
7.1
and
7.2
.
There is a
\delta_{0}>0
so that for any given
p\in[1,\infty)
and
\delta\in(0,\delta_{0})
there exists a constant
C\geq 1
so that the super-multiplicativity inequality,

C^{-1}\cM_{p,\delta}^{(n)}\cdot\cM_{p,\delta}^{(m)}\leq\cM_{p,\delta}^{(n+m)}\leq C\cM_{p,\delta}^{(n)}\cdot\cM_{p,\delta}^{(m)},

holds for all
n,m\in\mathbb{N}
.
The constant
C
can be chosen to be continuous in
p\in[1,\infty)
.
In particular, there is a constant
\cM_{p}\in(0,\infty)
,

C^{-1}\cM_{p}^{n}\leq\cM_{p,\delta}^{(n)}\leq C\cM_{p}^{n}.

The constant
\cM_{p}
is continuous and non-increasing in
p\in[1,\infty)
.

Remark 8.2
.

We expect in many cases that
p\mapsto\cM_{p}
is strictly decreasing. However, this seems to be very difficult to prove in most cases.

The second result is the combinatorial Loewner property.

Theorem 8.3
.

Let
(X,d)
be a metric space which can be realized as a limit space of an IGS
\Xi
satisfying Assumptions
7.1
,
7.2
and
7.3
. For a given
p\in[1,\infty)
let
\cM_{p}\in(0,\infty)
be as in Theorem
8.1
.
Then there is a unique
Q>1
which solves
\cM_{Q}=1
and for which
(X,d)
satisfies the Combinatorial
Q
-Loewner property. This value
Q
is the (Ahlfors regular) conformal dimension of
(X,d)
.

8.1.
Super-multiplicativity

Throughout the section, we consider a fixed IGS
\Xi
satisfying Assumptions
7.1
and
7.2
. The limit space is denoted
(X,d)
.
We recall
\mathcal{A}
from Assumption
7.2
.

Lemma 8.4
.

For all
p\in[1,\infty)
there is a constant
C\geq 1
so that for any pair
\{L_{1},L_{2}\},\{L_{3},L_{4}\}\in\mathcal{A}
and
n\in\mathbb{N}
,

C^{-1}\Mod_{p}\left(L_{3}^{n},L_{4}^{n},G_{n}\right)\leq\Mod_{p}\left(L_{1}^{n},L_{2}^{n},G_{n}\right)\leq C\Mod_{p}\left(L_{3}^{n},L_{4}^{n},G_{n}\right).

The constant
C
can be chosen so that it is continuous in
p\in[1,\infty)
.

Proof.

The case
p\in(1,\infty)
follows from duality (Proposition
2.7
),
(S2)
and the trivial inequality

\cRes_{p}(L_{1}^{n},L_{2}^{n},G_{n})\leq\mathcal{E}_{q}(\mathcal{B})

which follows from the definition of flow basis. Note that the constant
C
in the claim can be chosen to have a limit when
p\to 1^{+}
. The claim for
p=1
then follows from the continuity of the optimization problem
\Mod_{p}
because it has only finitely many variables and the target function is continuous both in the variables and the parameter
p
.
∎

By the previous lemma, we adopt the simpler notation

\cM_{p,n}:=\max_{\{L_{1},L_{2}\}\in\mathcal{A}}\Mod_{p}\left(L_{1}^{n},L_{2}^{n},G_{n}\right).

While this is dangerously similar to
\cM_{p,\delta}^{(n)}
we justify the choice in Proposition
8.6
.

Lemma 8.5
.

For all
p\in[1,\infty)
there is a constant
C\geq 1
so that for any
n,k\in\mathbb{N}
and
v,w\in V^{n}
with
d_{G_{n}}(v,w)=M_{*}
,

C^{-1}\cM_{p,k}\leq\Mod_{p}(v\cdot V^{k},w\cdot V^{k},G_{n+k})\leq C\cM_{p,k}.

The constant
C
can be chosen to be continuous in
p\in[1,\infty)
.

Proof.

We first prove the lower estimate using replacement flow. Take a shortest path
\theta=[v_{1},\ldots,v_{M_{*}+1}]
from
v
to
w
in
G_{n}
and take
\mathcal{J}
to be the constant flow along this path, i.e.,
\mathcal{J}(v_{i},v_{i+1})=1
and zero on all other edges. Its
p
-energy is equal to
M_{*}
. Then we use Theorem
5.2
to construct a flow
\mathcal{J}^{\prime}
from
v\cdot V^{k}
to
w\cdot V^{k}
with the energy estimate

The following proposition is a key ingredient in the proof of super-multiplicativity.
A similar method was used in

Proposition 8.6
.

There is
\delta_{0}>0
so that for any given

We remark that in the above statement we compare two different notions of discrete moduli; one is defined for curves in the limit space and the other on the paths on the replacement graphs.

Proof of Theorem
8.1
.

We already have the sub-multiplicativity inequality by the result of Bourdon–Kleiner (Proposition
2.16
), and Theorem
7.8
. By the replacement flow argument (Theorem
5.3
) combined with Proposition
8.6
, Lemma
8.4
and
(S2)
, we get the super-multiplicativity inequality.
In conclusion, for all
p\in[1,\infty)
and
n,m\in\mathbb{N}
,

C^{-1}\cM_{p,\delta}^{(n)}\cdot\cM_{p,\delta}^{(m)}\leq\cM_{p,\delta}^{(n+m)}\leq C\cM_{p,\delta}^{(n)}\cdot\cM_{p,\delta}^{(m)}.

The continuity of
C
in
p
also follows.
We remark that the case
p=1
is obtained by a similar continuity argument as in Lemma
8.4
.

It is well-known that a sequence satisfying both sub- and super-multiplicativity is comparable to an exponential; see e.g.
[
5
, Theorem 5.1]
. We nevertheless sketch the argument.
Let
a_{n}:=\log(C\cM_{p,\delta}^{(n)})
. Then
a_{n}
is sub-additive, meaning
a_{n+m}\leq a_{n}+a_{m}
. It follows from Fekete’s subadditive lemma that

\rho_{1}:=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{a_{n}}{n}=\inf_{n>0}\frac{a_{n}}{n}

exists.
Also
b_{n}:=\log(C^{-1}\cM_{p}^{(n)})
is super-additive, so

\rho_{2}:=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{b_{n}}{n}=\sup_{n>0}\frac{b_{n}}{n}

exists as-well, and we clearly have
\rho_{1}=\rho=\rho_{2}
.
But then

\rho-\frac{\log(C)}{n}\leq\frac{\log(\cM_{p}^{(n)})}{n}\leq\rho-\frac{\log(C^{-1})}{n}.

By taking exponentials,

(8.10)

C^{-1}(e^{\rho})^{n}\leq\cM_{p,\delta}^{(n)}\leq C(e^{\rho})^{n},

and are done by choosing
\cM_{p}:=e^{\rho}
.

We show that
p\mapsto\cM_{p}
is continuous and non-increasing.
First, to see that
\cM_{p}
is non-increasing, assume it is not. Namely,
\cM_{p}<\cM_{p^{\prime}}
for some
p<p^{\prime}
. By (
8.10
), for large enough
n
we have
\cM_{p,\delta}^{(n)}<\cM_{p^{\prime},\delta}^{(n)}
. But this contradicts the fact that the discrete modulus is non-increasing.
Second, to see that
p\mapsto\cM_{p}
is continuous, again assume not. This means that there is
p\in[1,\infty)
and
\varepsilon>0
so that
\cM_{p}>\cM_{p^{\prime}}+\varepsilon
for all
p^{\prime}>p
or
\cM_{p}<\cM_{p^{\prime}}-\varepsilon
for all
1\leq p^{\prime}<p
.
Because the constant
C
in (
8.10
) is continuous in
p
, we can choose it to be uniform on any bounded interval. If either of the discontinuities would hold, by (
8.10
), for large enough
n\in\mathbb{N}
the discrete modulus
\cM_{p,\delta}^{(n)}
would not be continuous.
∎

Proposition 8.12
.

Let
x,y\in X
,

and
A>0
. Take two disjoint open balls
B_{1}=B(x,r),B_{2}=B(y,r)
with

.

depending on

satisfying the following condition.

\Gamma
is the family of continuous curves

\Gamma:=\{\gamma\in\Gamma(B_{1},B_{2}):\diam(\gamma)\leq L\cdot r\},

so that

,

\Mod_{Q}(\Gamma,\mathbb{G}_{n+m})\geq K.

The constants can be explicitly chosen

and

for some constant

depending on the geometry of
(X,d)

Proof.

Let
j\in\mathbb{Z}
so that
L_{*}^{-j}\leq r<L_{*}^{-j+1}
,

and
m\in\mathbb{N}
.
Note that

is compact.
By the second inclusion in Lemma
7.10
there is

with

Since the balls are disjoint, we obviously have

7.10

Take

Z_{n}:=\bigcup_{w\in\theta}w\cdot V^{n-j+m}.

By choosing
i
to be suitably large, the choices can be performed so that every continuous curve
\gamma
connecting
B(x,r)
to the complement of
B(x,2r)
intersects with
X_{u}
and
X_{v}
for some pair
u\in U_{1}
and
v\in U_{2}
with
d_{G_{n-k+i}}(u,v)=M_{*}
.
Thus, by the argument in the proof of Proposition
8.6
,

\displaystyle\quad\Mod_{Q}(B(x,r),X\setminus B(x,2r),\mathbb{G}_{n+m+i})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\quad\lvert U_{1}\rvert\lvert U_{2}\rvert\max_{u\in U_{1}}\max_{v}\Mod_{Q}(u\cdot V^{m+k},v\cdot V^{m+k},\mathbb{G}_{n+m+i})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\quad C\lvert U_{1}\rvert\lvert U_{2}\rvert\cM_{Q}^{m+k}\leq K,

where the last inequality follows from
\cM_{Q}=1
. We arrive to (
8.13
) by applying Proposition
2.12
.

Now we fix
C>0
and our objective is to estimate

\Mod_{Q}(B(x,r),X\setminus B(x,(C+1)r),\mathbb{G}_{n+m})

in terms of
C
.
We consider the cases
C\leq 1
and
C\geq 1
separately. Let us assume the former. We take a covering
\{B(y,Cr/4)\}_{y\in I}
of
B(x,r)
so that each
y\in B(x,r)
and
d(y_{1},y_{2})\geq Cr/4
for all distinct
y_{1},y_{2}\in I
. By using the
Q^{\prime}
-Ahlfors regularity of
(X,d)
(Theorem
7.8
), we have a bound
\lvert I\rvert\leq C_{1}C^{-Q^{\prime}}
. This follows from a volume counting argument. Now for
y\in I
we take

\rho_{y}:V^{n+j}\to[0,\infty),\,\text{$\Gamma(B(y,Cr/4),B(y,Cr/2)$)-admissible},

whose
Q
-mass can be bounded by
K
according to (
8.13
).
Then the vertex-wise maximum
\rho:=\max_{y\in I}\rho_{y}
is
\Gamma(B(x,r),X\setminus B(x,(C+1)r))
admissible whose
Q
-mass can be bounded by
K\cdot\lvert I\rvert\leq C_{2}C^{-Q^{\prime}}
. We can set
\psi(t):=C_{2}t^{Q^{\prime}}
when
t\geq 1
.

Next we consider the case
C>1
.
Our method here is a quite standard technique in quasiconformal geometry; see e.g.
[
29
, Main Lemma 4.12]
and
[
14
, Proposition 3.4]
.
Let
N:=\lceil\log_{2}(C)\rceil-1
, and for each
i=1,\dots,N-1
we take
\Gamma(B(x,2^{i}r),X\setminus B(x,2^{i+1}r))
-admissible density
\rho_{i}:V^{n+j}\to[0,\infty)
with
Q
-mass bounded by
K
.
Then we take the average
\rho:=A/(N-1)\sum_{i=1}^{N-1}\rho_{i}
where
A
is chosen so that every
X_{v}
for
v\in V^{n+i}
intersects at most
A
different balls
B(x,2^{i}r)
. Such choice is possible by Theorem
7.8
-(
3
).

Because every curve connecting
B(x,2^{i}r)
to
B(x,2^{i+1}r)
contains a sub-curve that is fully contained in the annuli
B(x,2^{i+1}r)\setminus B(x,2^{i}r)
, we can always choose
\rho_{i}
so that
\rho_{i}(v)=0
unless
X_{v}\cap(B(x,2^{i+1}r)\setminus B(x,2^{i}r))\neq\emptyset
. Because the members of the covering
\{X_{v}\}_{v\in V^{n+i}}
have bounded overlapping (by (
1
) and (
3
) of Theorem
7.8
), there is a constant
M
depending only on the geometry of
(X,d)
so that for every
v\in V^{n+i}
the value
\rho_{i}(v)
is non-zero for at most
M
different
i=1,\ldots l
. Using this, we get the
Q
-mass bound

\cM_{Q}(\rho)\leq\frac{AM^{Q-1}}{(N-1)^{Q}}\sum_{i=1}^{N-1}\cM_{Q}(\rho_{i})\leq\frac{C_{3}}{N^{Q-1}}\leq C_{4}\log(C)^{1-Q}

Thus, when
t\leq 1
, we set
\psi(t):=C_{4}\log(1/t)^{1-Q}
.

We have now shown the
Q
-CLP for any
Q
s.t.
\cM_{Q}=1
. By
[
26
, Lemma 4.2]
any such
Q
must be equal to the conformal dimension of
X
and is thus also unique.
∎

9.
Some further results

We finish this paper by discussing some further applications of our techniques to constructing Sobolev spaces,
p
-energies and diffusions on the limit spaces using the Kusuoka–Zhou method; we refer to
[
48
,
37
,
33
]
for further discussion.

Let us assume that
\Xi
is a fixed IGS satisfying Assumptions
7.1
and
7.2
.
We denote the Hausdorff dimension

Q:=\frac{\log(\lvert V\rvert)}{\log(L_{*})}.

We also define the
p
-walk dimension
by

d_{w,p}:=\frac{\log(\lvert V\rvert\cdot\cM_{p}^{-1})}{\log(L_{*})},

where
\cM_{p}
is as in Theorem
8.1
.
We first verify a suitable volume growth condition for the graphs.

Proposition 9.1
.

The replacement graphs
G_{n}
are uniformly
Q
-Ahlfors regular, i.e. there is constant
C\geq 1
so that for all
x\in V^{n}
and
r\in[1,2\diam(G_{n})]
,

C^{-1}r^{Q}\leq\lvert B(w,r)\rvert\leq Cr^{Q}.

Proof.

Follows from an identical argument as in the proof of
Q
-Ahlfors regularity of the limit space in Theorem
7.8
-(
1
).
∎

Next we have a natural upper estimate for the discrete moduli over annuli.

Proposition 9.2
.

Let
p\in[1,\infty)
. There is a constant
C\geq 1
so that for all
x\in V^{n}
and
r>0

(9.3)

\Mod_{p}(B(x,r),V^{n}\setminus B(x,2r),\mathbb{G}_{n})\leq C\frac{\lvert B(x,r)\rvert}{r^{d_{w,p}}}.

Proof.

Similar arguments as in the proof of Theorem
8.3
, combined with Theorem
8.1
, show that

\Mod_{p}(B(x,r),V^{n}\setminus B(x,2r),\mathbb{G}_{n})\leq C\cM_{p}^{m},

where
r\approx L_{*}^{m}
.
The desired upper bound now follows from the definition of
d_{w,p}
and Proposition
9.1
.
∎

The following proposition verifies the
p
-combinatorial Ball Loewner condition from
[
48
]
.

Proposition 9.4
.

Let
p\in[1,\infty)
and
A>0
. There are constants
L,K>0
depending on
A
so that for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
x,y\in V^{n}
and
r>0
the following implication holds.
If
B_{1}:=B(x,r),\,B_{2}:=B(y,r)\subseteq V^{n}
with
\dist(B_{1},B_{2})\leq A\cdot r
and

\Theta:=\{\theta\in\Theta(B_{1},B_{2}):\diam(\theta)\leq L\cdot r\}

then

(9.5)

\Mod_{p}(\Theta,G_{n})\geq K\cdot r^{Q-d_{w,p}}.

Proof.

The idea is the same as in the proof of Proposition
8.12
. First, we note that
r
can be assumed to be suitably large. This is because otherwise we have only finitely many combinatorially distinct cases, depending only on
A
and the maximum degree of the replacement graphs.
Then we choose
m\leq n
according to the given radius
r>0
. It is chosen as the smallest positive integer so that

\pi_{n,m}(x)\cdot V^{n-m}\subseteq B(x,r)\text{ and }\pi_{n,m}(y)\cdot V^{n-m}\subseteq B(y,r).

We choose a shortest path
\theta
from
\pi_{n,m}(x)
to
\pi_{n,m}(y)
. Then take the unit flow along this path and apply the replacement flow (Theorem
5.2
).
By the same argument as in the proof of Proposition
8.12
,

\Mod_{p}(\Theta,G_{n})\geq C\cdot\cM_{p}^{m}\geq Kr^{Q-d_{w,p}}.

∎

Let
p\in(1,\infty)
.
According to Murugan–Shimizu
[
48
]
, the previous three propositions and the dimension condition
Q-d_{w,p}<1
are sufficient for the uniform elliptic Harnack inequality of
p
-harmonic functions and the uniform
(p,p)
-Poincaré-inequality on the replacement graphs
G_{n}
. By combining these estimates with the methods of Murugan–Shimizu, it should be possible to extend the constructions of Sobolev spaces to higher dimensional Menger sponges, and also to the broader class of generalized Sierpiński carpets; see
[
48
, Section 7]
.
In particular, this construction works when
p
is equal to the conformal dimension of the ambient limit space. We also get a non-probabilistic construction of the self-similar Dirichlet form on the Menger sponge.
It is a difficult open problem whether the condition
Q-d_{w,p}<1
can be relaxed.

We expect the method of Murugan–Shimizu also applies on many other fractal spaces that we considered it the work, including the pillow space and the pentagonal carpet. A detailed study of this is left for future work.

Under the more restrictive dimensionality condition
d_{w,p}>Q
, it should be possible to verify the conductive homogeneity condition of Kigami
[
33
]
; note that
d_{w,p}>Q
is equivalent to
p
being strictly larger than the conformal dimension of the ambient limit space. This is because the statement in Proposition
9.4
is equivalent to the
p
-Knight move condition
of Kigami (see
[
33
,
57
]
and also
[
4
,
6
,
37
]
) which is equivalent to the conductive homogeneity condition. We note that, for this equivalence, the condition
d_{w,p}>Q
seems to be quite crucial.
