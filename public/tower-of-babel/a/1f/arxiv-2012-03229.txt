A general class of C 1 C^{1} smooth rational splines: Application to construction of exact ellipses and ellipsoids
Hendrik Speleers, Deepesh Toshniwal

Abstract
In this paper, we describe a general class of C 1 C^{1} smooth rational splines that enables, in particular, exact descriptions of ellipses and ellipsoids — some of the most important primitives for CAD and CAE. The univariate rational splines are assembled by transforming multiple sets of NURBS basis functions via so-called design-through-analysis compatible extraction matrices; different sets of NURBS are allowed to have different polynomial degrees and weight functions. Tensor products of the univariate splines yield multivariate splines. In the bivariate setting, we describe how similar design-through-analysis compatible transformations of the tensor-product splines enable the construction of smooth surfaces containing one or two polar singularities. The material is self-contained, and is presented such that all tools can be easily implemented by CAD or CAE practitioners within existing software that support NURBS. To this end, we explicitly present the matrices (a) that describe our splines in terms of NURBS, and (b) that help refine the splines by performing (local) degree elevation and knot insertion. Finally, all C 1 C^{1} spline constructions yield spline basis functions that are locally supported and form a convex partition of unity.

Introduction

Multivariate splines are used extensively for computer-aided design (CAD) and, more recently, for computer-aided engineering (CAE).
Smoothness of such splines is a particularly valuable trait.
When the aim is to create a (freeform) geometric model for a smooth object, it helps if the splines used for the task are smooth themselves.
For instance, this circumvents situations where small displacements to control points may produce ‘non-smooth’ features such as
C^{0}
kinks, loss of curvature continuity, etc.
Similarly, when the aim is to numerically approximate the solution to high-order partial differential equations (PDEs) using isogeometric analysis (IGA) — a generalization of classical finite elements
[
8
]
— high smoothness of the approximating spaces can be beneficial.
For instance, it can allow us to directly discretize the PDEs without any auxiliary variables, thus yielding simpler and more efficient implementations.

In this paper, we discuss a general class of
C^{1}
smooth rational splines that allow for the construction of
C^{1}
smooth curves and surfaces.
These are an extension of classical
C^{1}
non-uniform rational B-splines (NURBS) as they enjoy the flexibility of choosing locally unrelated weight functions as well as the option of local degree elevation — they can
be roughly
regarded as piecewise-NURBS. At the same time, they maintain intuitive control-point-based design.
Moreover, they enable simple (low-degree) and smooth descriptions of some of the most important primitives
for CAD and CAE (but also for computer vision, graphics and robotics): closed, real, non-degenerate quadrics — that is, ellipses in two dimensions and ellipsoids in three dimensions.

The ideas we present here build upon those from
[
25
]
, in multiple directions, and their presentation is motivated by our primary objectives:
self-contained, explicit, NURBS-compatible descriptions that can be easily and efficiently implemented within existing CAD software
.
The most important novel contributions are the following.

•

We describe the usage of classical univariate NURBS to assemble
C^{1}
rational multi-degree spline basis functions
using an extraction matrix.

The general framework was
explained in
[
25
]
, but we provide here a simplified exposition of the construction and a formal proof of the properties; see Remark
2.3
.

We mainly stick to parametric smoothness, but a construction centred around the notion of geometric smoothness can be formulated as well; see Remark
2.5
.

•

We describe efficient refinement of the
C^{1}
splines leveraging classical NURBS refinement.
The novelty here relies in an explicit and simple construction of the refinement matrices.

•

We describe how tensor-product bivariate
C^{1}
rational splines can be used to build
C^{1}
smooth geometries that may contain one or two polar singularities; the
C^{1}
smooth splines describing the geometries are called
polar splines
.
As above, the idea is based on building an extraction matrix.

•

We describe efficient refinement of polar splines.

In particular, we provide an explicit and simple construction of the refinement matrices.

•

We provide explicit descriptions of ellipses and ellipsoids built using low-degree
C^{1}
splines, and we detail their extraction in terms of NURBS so that they can be readily implemented and used in CAD or CAE software.

1.1
Extraction matrices

At the core of our approach is the notion of the so-called
design-through-analysis (DTA) compatible extraction matrix
.
1
1

1

Our notion of
DTA-compatible extraction matrix
has been called
IGA-suitable extraction matrix
in
[
25
]
. The name reflects the fact that both design and analysis may profit
from
the extraction operation.

Roughly speaking, such matrix helps us assemble ‘simple splines’ into ‘more general splines.’
Examples are the Bézier extraction matrix introduced to assemble Bernstein polynomials into B-/T-splines
[
3
,
19
]
; the multi-degree extraction matrix for assembling elements of extended Tchebycheff spaces into generalized Tchebycheffian B-splines
[
22
,
26
,
7
]
; and the unstructured spline extraction matrices for assembling tensor-product splines into splines on unstructured quadrilateral
meshes
[
25
,
27
,
24
]
.

Here, we apply the concept of extraction in the following context.
We start from multiple sets of (univariate or bivariate) NURBS basis functions defined on adjacent domains, and collect all of these functions in the set
\{b_{j}:j=1,\dots,m\}
.
Then, we assemble them into more general
C^{1}
rational (polar) splines using a matrix
{\boldsymbol{C}}
(with entries
C_{ij}
), called the
extraction matrix
.

Denote this new set of splines by
\{N_{i}:i=1,\dots,{n}\}
, where
{n}<m
. These are defined as follows,

N_{i}=\sum_{j=1}^{m}C_{ij}b_{j},\quad i=1,\dots,{n}.

(1)

We are particularly interested in matrices
{\boldsymbol{C}}
such that the functions
N_{i}

•

satisfy certain smoothness constraints that may or may not be satisfied by the
b_{j}
, and

•

possess the properties of non-negativity, locality, linear independence and partition of unity that the
b_{j}
already possess.

Such extraction matrices are called
DTA-compatible
.

Definition 1.1

(DTA-compatible extraction)
.

An extraction matrix
{\boldsymbol{C}}
is called DTA-compatible if

(a)

{\boldsymbol{C}}
is a full-rank matrix,

(b)

each column of
{\boldsymbol{C}}
sums to 1,

(c)

each entry in
{\boldsymbol{C}}
is non-negative, and

(d)

{\boldsymbol{C}}
imparts locality to the functions
N_{i}
through sparsity.

It is easy to see that the action of a DTA-compatible extraction matrix on a convex partition of unity, local basis gives rise to another local basis that also forms a convex partition of unity.
Indeed, by summing over
i
in Equation (
1
), we have

\sum_{i=1}^{{n}}N_{i}=\sum_{i=1}^{{n}}\sum_{j=1}^{m}{C}_{ij}b_{j}=\sum_{j=1}^{m}b_{j}\sum_{i=1}^{{n}}{C}_{ij}=1,

as the
b_{j}
form a partition of unity.
Since
{\boldsymbol{C}}
has non-negative entries and is a full-rank matrix, non-negativity and linear independence of
N_{i}
follow from the non-negativity and linear independence of
b_{j}
.

1.2
Related literature

As mentioned in the
previous section
, the construction of smooth univariate splines by joining simpler pieces has been recently explored in
[
25
,
22
,
26
]
for polynomial multi-degree splines, and in
[
7
]
for generalized Tchebycheffian splines.
These approaches have conceptual similarities with the notion of beta-splines
[
2
]
.
The main differences are that the former approaches do not rely on symbolic computations while the latter
does
, and the former approaches consider parametric continuity while the latter studies geometric continuity.

The use of smooth univariate rational splines for construction of circles has been previously explored in
[
1
,
12
,
13
]
.
It is known that a circle cannot be represented by a single (symmetric) periodic
C^{1}
quadratic NURBS curve
[
17
, Section 7.5]
nor a
C^{2}
cubic NURBS curve
[
5
, Section 13.7]
. However, it is possible to find
C^{p}
smooth descriptions using NURBS of degree
2(p+1)
, which is shown to be the minimal degree in
[
1
]
. On the other hand,
[
12
,
13
]
presented a
C^{1}
piecewise quadratic NURBS description of the circle and used it for IGA. Our rational multi-degree splines form a flexible extension of the latter framework, and allow for a variety of exact descriptions of circles using low
(multi-)
degrees, as indicated in Table
1
.

In two dimensions, closed quadrics or, more generally, smooth closed surfaces of genus zero can be built using tensor-product splines by introducing polar singularities.
For such polar surfaces, subdivision schemes producing
C^{1}
surfaces
[
10
,
14
]
and
C^{2}
surfaces
[
9
,
15
]
have been previously worked out.
The corresponding limit surfaces consist of an infinite sequence of surface rings where the faces shrink to a point in the limit.
A more CAD-friendly finite construction was developed in
[
16
]
; this approach constructs ‘shape’ basis functions for
C^{2}
polar splines with bi-degree
(6,3)
.
These basis functions correspond to unique Fourier frequencies in the polar expansion of a quadratic surface.
The ‘shape’ basis does not enjoy non-negativity and does not form a partition of unity, and extensions of it to higher smoothness leads to degrees of freedom that control non-intuitive shape parameters.
Similar recent constructions for obtaining
C^{1}
polar spline caps can be found in
[
11
]
.

Curvature continuous polar NURBS surfaces were discussed in
[
21
]
, and
[
20
]
presented a construction of polar caps using periodic B-spline surfaces with
G^{n}
continuity for arbitrary
n
.
On the CAE side, a standard circular serendipity-type element for IGA was proposed in
[
12
]
, and
C^{k}
smooth basis functions over singular parametrizations of triangular domains were constructed in
[
23
]
.
A design-through-analysis friendly construction of
C^{k}
smooth polar surfaces was recently proposed in
[
25
]
, and the current work builds further upon this construction.

A completely different approach for dealing with curves and surfaces is the use of implicit representations
[
6
]
. Such representations enjoy nice geometric properties (especially for simple shapes). For instance, they allow for a straightforward point membership classification. On the other hand, explicit smooth B-spline representations are more convenient for direct geometric modeling and (local) modification.

2
Piecewise-rational curves

In this section, we focus on a multi-degree extension of univariate NURBS splines.
The multi-degree spline space is defined as a collection of classical NURBS spaces (with possibly
different polynomial degrees and weight functions
) glued together
C^{1}
smoothly. For such space we present a construction of a set of basis functions, with similar properties to classical NURBS. After discussing some preliminary material on NURBS in Section
2.1
, we elaborate how these basis functions can be computed through a DTA-compatible extraction matrix in Section
2.2
.
A more general but also more complex algorithmic construction has been detailed in
[
25
, Section 2]
and further explored in
[
22
,
26
]
for polynomial multi-degree splines. Then, in Section
2.3
, we give an explicit procedure how to compute a refined representation of a given curve. Finally, in Section
2.4
, we illustrate how this tool can be used to describe arbitrary ellipses in a
C^{1}
smooth fashion using low-degree piecewise-rational curve representations suited for integrated design and analysis.

2.1
Preliminaries on NURBS

We start by defining notation for NURBS basis functions, and introduce some classical relations that can be found, e.g., in
[
18
,
17
]
.

Given a basic interval
I:=[x_{1},x_{2}]\subset{\mathbb{R}}
, let us denote with
{\boldsymbol{\xi}}
an
open knot vector
of degree
p\in{\mathbb{N}}
and length
{n}+p+1\in{\mathbb{N}}
, i.e.,

\begin{gathered}{\boldsymbol{\xi}}:=[\xi_{1},\xi_{2},\dots,\xi_{{n}+p+1}],\quad\xi_{i+1}\geq\xi_{i},\\
\xi_{1}=\dots=\xi_{p+1}=x_{1}<\xi_{p+2},\\
\xi_{{n}+1}=\dots=\xi_{{n}+p+1}=x_{2}>\xi_{{n}}.\end{gathered}

(2)

The number of times a knot value
\xi_{i}
is duplicated in the knot vector is called the knot’s
multiplicity
. The multiplicity of
\xi_{i}
is denoted with
m_{i}
, and we assume that
1\leq m_{i}\leq p-1
.
The corresponding set of B-splines
\{b_{j,p}:j=1,\dots,{n}\}
are defined using the recursive relation,

b_{j,p}(x):=\frac{x-\xi_{j}}{\xi_{j+p}-\xi_{j}}b_{j,p-1}(x)+\frac{\xi_{j+p+1}-x}{\xi_{j+p+1}-\xi_{j+1}}b_{j+1,p-1}(x),

starting from

b_{j,0}(x):=\begin{cases}1,&\text{if }\xi_{j}\leq x<\xi_{j+1},\\
0,&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

and under the convention that fractions with zero denominator have value zero. With the above definition, all the B-splines take the value zero at the end point
x_{2}
.
Therefore, in order to avoid asymmetry over the interval
I
, it is common to assume the B-splines to be left continuous at
x_{2}
. We will follow suit.

Let us denote with
{\boldsymbol{w}}
a
weight vector
of length
{n}
, i.e.,

{\boldsymbol{w}}:=[w_{1},w_{2},\dots,w_{n}],\quad w_{i}>0.

(3)

The corresponding set of NURBS
\{b^{w}_{j,p}:j=1,\dots,{n}\}
are defined by

b^{w}_{j,p}(x):=\frac{w_{j}b_{j,p}(x)}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}b_{i,p}(x)}.

Each
b^{w}_{j,p}
is non-negative on
I
and is locally supported on
[\xi_{j},\xi_{j+p+1}]
. Moreover, the functions
b^{w}_{j,p}
are linearly independent and form a partition of unity. They satisfy the following end-point conditions:

\displaystyle b^{w}_{1,p}(x_{1})=1,\quad b^{w}_{j,p}(x_{1})=0,\quad j=2,\ldots,{n},

\displaystyle b^{w}_{{n},p}(x_{2})=1,\quad b^{w}_{j,p}(x_{2})=0,\quad j=1,\ldots,{n}-1.

The NURBS space corresponding to
{\boldsymbol{\xi}}
and
{\boldsymbol{w}}
is denoted with
\mathcal{R}[{\boldsymbol{\xi}},{\boldsymbol{w}}]
and is defined as the span of
\{b^{w}_{j,p}:j=1,\dots,{n}\}
. This is a space of piecewise-rational functions of degree
p
with smoothness
C^{p-m_{i}}
at knot
\xi_{i}
and its dimension is
{n}
. The assumption on the multiplicity will ensure us global
C^{1}
smoothness.
Note that when
w_{1}=\dots=w_{n}
, the
members of this space are
piecewise-polynomial.

Remark 2.1
.

The structure of
{\boldsymbol{\xi}}
in Equation (
2
) is such that
p
,
m_{i}
,
{n}
and
I
are embedded in it. Therefore, we will assume that once a knot vector
{\boldsymbol{\xi}}
is known, so are the degree, smoothness, and dimension of the corresponding NURBS space
\mathcal{R}[{\boldsymbol{\xi}},{\boldsymbol{w}}]
.

We identify a function
f\in\mathcal{R}[{\boldsymbol{\xi}},{\boldsymbol{w}}]
with the vector of its coefficients
\left[f_{1},\dots,f_{n}\right]
,

f(x)=\sum_{j=1}^{{n}}f_{j}b^{w}_{j,p}(x).

Only the first (last)
k+1
basis functions contribute towards the
k
-th order derivative at the left (right) end point of
I
. In particular, we have

\displaystyle f(x_{1})

\displaystyle=f_{1},\quad\frac{df}{dx}(x_{1})=\frac{p}{\xi_{p+2}-x_{1}}\frac{w_{2}}{w_{1}}(f_{2}-f_{1}),

(4)

\displaystyle f(x_{2})

\displaystyle=f_{n},\quad\frac{df}{dx}(x_{2})=\frac{p}{x_{2}-\xi_{{n}}}\frac{w_{{n}-1}}{w_{n}}(f_{n}-f_{{n}-1}).

A NURBS curve embedded in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
,
{d}\geq 2
, can be constructed as

{\boldsymbol{f}}(x)=\sum_{j=1}^{n}{\boldsymbol{f}}_{j}b^{w}_{j,p}(x),

where
{\boldsymbol{f}}_{j}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{{d}}
are the control points assigned to each basis function. All coordinate functions of this curve belong to
\mathcal{R}[{\boldsymbol{\xi}},{\boldsymbol{w}}]
and therefore all the above relations hold for them.

Rational multi-degree B-splines

Consider
{m}
open knot vectors
{{\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{(i)}}
of degree
{p^{(i)}}
,

, defined as in Equation (

{\phi^{(i)}}(x):=x-{x_{1}^{(i)}}+{\tau_{1}^{(1)}}+\sum_{\ell=1}^{i-1}({x_{2}^{(\ell)}}-{x_{1}^{(\ell)}}),

for an arbitrarily chosen origin

Then,
{{}^{(i)}}:=[{\tau_{1}^{(i)}},{\tau_{2}^{(i)}}]:={\phi^{(i)}}([{x_{1}^{(i)}},{x_{2}^{(i)}}])\subset{\mathbb{R}}

\Omega:=[t_{1},t_{2}]:={{}^{(1)}}\cup\dots\cup{{}^{({m})}}.

Note that

Moreover, let

The space of rational multi-degree splines is defined as

\begin{split}\mathcal{R}[{\boldsymbol{\Xi}},{\boldsymbol{W}}]:=\bigl\{\,f\in C^{1}(\Omega):\;&f\circ{\phi^{(i)}}\in\mathcal{R}[{{\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{(i)}},{{\boldsymbol{w}}^{(i)}}],\\

The elements of

It is clear that classical NURBS spaces are a special case of the rational multi-degree spline spaces.

{\boldsymbol{0}}&{\boldsymbol{H}}^{c}&{\boldsymbol{0}}\\
0&{\boldsymbol{0}}&1\end{array}\right],\\
{{\boldsymbol{H}}}^{per}:=\left[\begin{array}[]{c|c|c}\frac{{\beta^{({m})}}}{{\alpha^{({m})}}+{\beta^{({m})}}}&\hbox{\multirowsetup${\boldsymbol{H}}^{c}$}&\frac{{\beta^{({m})}}}{{\alpha^{({m})}}+{\beta^{({m})}}}\\
{\boldsymbol{0}}&&{\boldsymbol{0}}\\
\frac{{\alpha^{({m})}}}{{\alpha^{({m})}}+{\beta^{({m})}}}&&\frac{{\alpha^{({m})}}}{{\alpha^{({m})}}+{\beta^{({m})}}}\end{array}\right].\end{gathered}

(9)

The
number of rows in the two matrices are denoted with
{n}:=\eta_{m}+2
and
{{n}}^{per}:=\eta_{m}
, respectively.
The sparse and simple structure of both matrices means that it is easy to verify that both have full rank.
Indeed, this conclusion can be directly deduced from the full rank of
{\boldsymbol{H}}^{c}
, which in turn is implied by Equation (
7
).
Moreover, their entries are non-negative, and the column sum is equal to one. Hence, we conclude that these matrices are DTA-compatible.

How these matrices help us build
C^{1}
splines can be understood by taking into account Equation (
4
) at any end point
{\tau_{2}^{(i)}}
.
Let us discuss the non-periodic setting and fix
i\in\{1,\dots,{m}-1\}
; the argument can be directly applied to the periodic setting
as well
.
Only four functions (
b_{{\mu}_{i}-1}
,
b_{{\mu}_{i}}
,
b_{{\mu}_{i}+1}
,
b_{{\mu}_{i}+2}
) are not
C^{1}
at
{\tau_{2}^{(i)}}
.
More precisely, only these four functions have non-vanishing values and first derivatives here.
In view of (
4
), a spline
f
given by

f(t)=\sum_{j=1}^{4}f_{j}b_{{\mu}_{i}-2+j}(t)

will be
C^{1}

at
{\tau_{2}^{(i)}}
if

f_{2}=f_{3},\quad{\alpha^{(i)}}(f_{2}-f_{1})={\beta^{(i)}}(f_{4}-f_{3}).

(10)

We can verify that the entries of
{\boldsymbol{H}}
satisfy exactly such relations.
Indeed, for some
j
, the matrix
{\boldsymbol{H}}
defines two new functions
B_{j}
and
B_{j+1}
such that

\begin{bmatrix}B_{j}\\
B_{j+1}\end{bmatrix}=\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}[c]1&\frac{{\alpha^{(i)}}}{{\alpha^{(i)}}+{\beta^{(i)}}}&\frac{{\alpha^{(i)}}}{{\alpha^{(i)}}+{\beta^{(i)}}}&0\\
0&\frac{{\beta^{(i)}}}{{\alpha^{(i)}}+{\beta^{(i)}}}&\frac{{\beta^{(i)}}}{{\alpha^{(i)}}+{\beta^{(i)}}}&1\end{bmatrix}}_{=:\,{\bar{{\boldsymbol{H}}}^{(i)}}}\begin{bmatrix}b_{{\mu}_{i}-1}\\
b_{{\mu}_{i}}\\
b_{{\mu}_{i}+1}\\
b_{{\mu}_{i}+2}\end{bmatrix}+\begin{bmatrix}{\bar{B}_{j}^{(i)}}\\
{\bar{B}_{j+1}^{(i)}}\end{bmatrix},

where
{\bar{B}_{j}^{(i)}}
and
{\bar{B}_{j+1}^{(i)}}
are at least
C^{1}
at
{\tau_{2}^{(i)}}
.
When setting
[f_{1},f_{2},f_{3},f_{4}]
equal to the first or the second row of
{\bar{{\boldsymbol{H}}}^{(i)}}
, we see that Equation (
10
) is satisfied.
The following result follows from the above discussion.

Theorem 2.2
.

The
C^{1}
smooth piecewise-rational functions in the sets
\{B_{i}:i=1,\dots,{n}\}
and
\{{B}^{per}_{i}:i=1,\dots,{{n}}^{per}\}
are linearly independent, locally supported, and form a convex partition of unity on
\Omega
.

Remark 2.3
.

In
[
25
, Section 2.3.5]
it was observed that the
C^{1}
smooth piecewise-rational basis functions enjoy the properties described in Theorem
2.2
. However, a formal proof was missing. It was also pointed out that the property of non-negativity is in general not present in case of
C^{2}
or higher smoothness. On the other hand, this is possible when restricting to polynomial pieces
[
26
]
.

Remark 2.4
.

With the aim of designing quadric curves, also called conics, it is natural to choose local NURBS spaces of the same degree
p
and defined on the same
uniform
knot vector
{\boldsymbol{\xi}}
.
Moreover, it is common to set
{w_{1}^{(i)}}={w_{{{n}^{(i)}}}^{(i)}}=1
. Under these circumstances, the ratios in Equation (
8
) read as

\begin{gathered}\frac{{\alpha^{(i)}}}{{\alpha^{(i)}}+{\beta^{(i)}}}={\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\frac{{w_{{{n}^{(i)}}-1}^{(i)}}}{{w_{{{n}^{(i)}}-1}^{(i)}}+{w_{2}^{(i+1)}}},}\\
\frac{{\beta^{(i)}}}{{\alpha^{(i)}}+{\beta^{(i)}}}=\frac{{w_{2}^{(i+1)}}}{{w_{{{n}^{(i)}}-1}^{(i)}}+{w_{2}^{(i+1)}}}.\end{gathered}

Finally, if there is additional symmetry in the choice of weights, so
{w_{{{n}^{(i)}}-1}^{(i)}}={w_{2}^{(i+1)}}
, we simply get

\frac{{\alpha^{(i)}}}{{\alpha^{(i)}}+{\beta^{(i)}}}=\frac{1}{2},\quad\frac{{\beta^{(i)}}}{{\alpha^{(i)}}+{\beta^{(i)}}}=\frac{1}{2}.

Once we have computed a DTA-compatible extraction matrix
{\boldsymbol{H}}
(or
{{\boldsymbol{H}}}^{per}
), given
{n}
control points
{\boldsymbol{f}}_{i}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{{d}}
,
{d}\geq 2
, we can construct a piecewise-rational curve
{\boldsymbol{f}}
embedded in
{\mathbb{R}}^{{d}}
,

{\boldsymbol{f}}(t)=\sum_{i=1}^{{n}}{\boldsymbol{f}}_{i}B_{i}(t).

For a fixed curve, the transpose of
{\boldsymbol{H}}
(or
{{\boldsymbol{H}}}^{per}
) defines the relationship between control points of the
b_{j}
(discontinuous at the segment joins) and control points of the smooth
B_{i}
. More precisely, if

\sum_{j=1}^{{\mu}_{{m}}}{\boldsymbol{g}}_{j}b_{j}(t)={\boldsymbol{f}}(t)=\sum_{i=1}^{{n}}{\boldsymbol{f}}_{i}B_{i}(t),

then

{\boldsymbol{g}}_{j}=\sum_{i=1}^{{n}}H_{ij}{\boldsymbol{f}}_{i}.

(11)

Remark 2.5
.

When dealing with curves, the proposed piecewise-NURBS framework can also be formulated in the context of geometric continuity
[
4
]
. In such case, the
C^{1}
smoothness condition at the segment join in (
10
) is replaced by the
G^{1}
smoothness condition

f_{2}=f_{3},\quad{\alpha^{(i)}}(f_{2}-f_{1})={{}^{(i)}}{\beta^{(i)}}(f_{4}-f_{3}),

for a given geometric shape parameter
{{}^{(i)}}>0
, resulting in the matrix

{\bar{{\boldsymbol{H}}}^{(i)}}=\begin{bmatrix}[c]1&\frac{{\alpha^{(i)}}}{{\alpha^{(i)}}+{{}^{(i)}}{\beta^{(i)}}}&\frac{{\alpha^{(i)}}}{{\alpha^{(i)}}+{{}^{(i)}}{\beta^{(i)}}}&0\\
0&\frac{{{}^{(i)}}{\beta^{(i)}}}{{\alpha^{(i)}}+{{}^{(i)}}{\beta^{(i)}}}&\frac{{{}^{(i)}}{\beta^{(i)}}}{{\alpha^{(i)}}+{{}^{(i)}}{\beta^{(i)}}}&1\end{bmatrix}.

It is clear that this matrix is still DTA-compatible.

2.3
Refinement of piecewise-rational curves

The rational spline spaces defined in the previous section can be refined in a multitude of ways.
We could reduce the smoothness at segment joins, raise the polynomial degrees of local NURBS spaces, and/or insert new knots in local NURBS spaces
[
25
, Section 2.4.3]
. A combination of these possibilities could be judiciously employed to achieve spline spaces that provide higher resolution or approximation power exactly where needed. In this section, we present an explicit construction of refined representations of a given piecewise-rational curve.

Before delving into the details of the refinement procedure, we first define two matrices
{\boldsymbol{G}}
and
{{\boldsymbol{G}}}^{per}
that can be regarded as right inverses of the extraction matrices
{\boldsymbol{H}}
and
{{\boldsymbol{H}}}^{per}
, respectively.
Looking at the structure of the matrix
{\boldsymbol{H}}^{c}
specified in Equations (
7
)–(
8
), we can define a sparse matrix
{\boldsymbol{G}}^{c}
of size
({\mu}_{m}-2)\times\eta_{m}
, whose non-zero entries
G^{c}_{ij}
are identified as follows: for
i=1,\ldots,{m}
and
j=1,\ldots,{{n}^{(i)}}-2
,

G^{c}_{{\mu}_{i-1}+j,\eta_{i-1}+j}:=1.

From its construction it is clear that the product
{\boldsymbol{H}}^{c}{\boldsymbol{G}}^{c}
is equal to the identity matrix.
Similarly, keeping in mind Equation (
9
), the matrices

{\boldsymbol{G}}:=\left[\begin{array}[]{ccc}1&{\boldsymbol{0}}&0\\
\hline\cr{\boldsymbol{0}}&{\boldsymbol{G}}^{c}&{\boldsymbol{0}}\\
\hline\cr 0&{\boldsymbol{0}}&1\end{array}\right],\quad{{\boldsymbol{G}}}^{per}:=\left[\begin{array}[]{ccc}{\boldsymbol{0}}\\
\hline\cr{\boldsymbol{G}}^{c}\\
\hline\cr{\boldsymbol{0}}\end{array}\right]

(12)

give rise to products
{\boldsymbol{H}}{\boldsymbol{G}}
and
{{\boldsymbol{H}}}^{per}{{\boldsymbol{G}}}^{per}
that are equal to identity matrices.

Now, let
\mathcal{R}
be a given spline space and let us denote the target refined space with
\tilde{\mathcal{R}}
.

For simplicity of notation, we drop the superscript
per
in case of periodicity.

Then, we consider the two unique representations of a curve
{\boldsymbol{f}}
with coordinate functions in
\mathcal{R}\subset\tilde{\mathcal{R}}
,

\sum_{i=1}^{\tilde{{n}}}\tilde{{\boldsymbol{f}}}_{i}\tilde{B}_{i}(t)={\boldsymbol{f}}(t)=\sum_{i=1}^{{n}}{\boldsymbol{f}}_{i}B_{i}(t).

(13)

Let us collect the control points in the row vectors
\tilde{{\boldsymbol{F}}}:=[\tilde{{\boldsymbol{f}}}_{1},\dots,\tilde{{\boldsymbol{f}}}_{\tilde{{n}}}]
and
{\boldsymbol{F}}:=[{\boldsymbol{f}}_{1},\dots,{\boldsymbol{f}}_{{n}}]
. We now seek the refinement matrix
{\boldsymbol{R}}
of size
{n}\times\tilde{{n}}
that helps us compute
\tilde{{\boldsymbol{F}}}
from
{\boldsymbol{F}}
, i.e.,

\tilde{{\boldsymbol{F}}}={\boldsymbol{F}}{\boldsymbol{R}}.

Assume that
{\boldsymbol{H}}
and
\tilde{{\boldsymbol{H}}}
are the extraction matrices corresponding to the spaces
\mathcal{R}
and
\tilde{\mathcal{R}}
, respectively.
Incorporating these matrices in the representations in Equation (
13
) results in

\tilde{{\boldsymbol{F}}}\tilde{{\boldsymbol{H}}}\tilde{{\boldsymbol{b}}}={\boldsymbol{F}}{{\boldsymbol{H}}}{{\boldsymbol{b}}},

where the column vectors
{{\boldsymbol{b}}}
and
\tilde{{\boldsymbol{b}}}
collect the local NURBS basis functions.
Let the matrix
{\boldsymbol{S}}
be such that
{{\boldsymbol{b}}}={\boldsymbol{S}}\tilde{{\boldsymbol{b}}}
; this matrix can be computed with standard NURBS refinement techniques.
Then, we have

\tilde{{\boldsymbol{F}}}\tilde{{\boldsymbol{H}}}\tilde{{\boldsymbol{b}}}={\boldsymbol{F}}{{\boldsymbol{H}}}{\boldsymbol{S}}\tilde{{\boldsymbol{b}}}.

This implies that we can compute
{\boldsymbol{R}}
by solving the following (overdetermined) linear system with a unique solution,

{\boldsymbol{R}}\tilde{{\boldsymbol{H}}}={\boldsymbol{H}}{\boldsymbol{S}}.

After multiplication of both sides of this system with the matrix
\tilde{{\boldsymbol{G}}}
(corresponding to
\tilde{{\boldsymbol{H}}}
) as defined in Equation (
12
), we arrive at

{\boldsymbol{R}}={\boldsymbol{H}}{\boldsymbol{S}}\tilde{{\boldsymbol{G}}}.

(14)

Note that the application of
\tilde{{\boldsymbol{G}}}
in Equation (
14
) means that a subset of
\tilde{{n}}
columns of
{\boldsymbol{H}}{\boldsymbol{S}}
are selected to form
{\boldsymbol{R}}
.

Remark 2.6
.

The definition of
\tilde{{\boldsymbol{G}}}
is done for the sake of simplicity of computation of
{\boldsymbol{R}}
in Equation (
14
), but is not unique. Any matrix that is a right inverse of
\tilde{{\boldsymbol{H}}}
would be a valid choice as well, such as the standard Moore–Penrose right inverse
\tilde{{\boldsymbol{H}}}^{T}(\tilde{{\boldsymbol{H}}}\tilde{{\boldsymbol{H}}}^{T})^{-1}
.

3
Piecewise-rational polar surfaces

In this section, we describe how to construct
C^{1}
smooth representations for
polar surfaces
containing single or double polar singularities (e.g., hemispheres and spheres, respectively).
Such surfaces can be obtained by starting from a bivariate tensor-product (piecewise-NURBS) spline patch and collapsing one or two of its edges, respectively, as illustrated in Figure
3
.
Each of such edge collapses creates a
polar point
and can be achieved by coalescing the control points related to basis functions with non-zero values on the edge.
In general, however, this control-point coalescing will introduce kinks at the poles and the surface representation will not be smooth.
To achieve overall smoothness, additional conditions need to be satisfied by the control points
[
25
, Section 3]
.
In Section
3.1
, we derive
C^{1}
smoothness conditions at a polar point, and they enable us to build
smooth polar splines
as linear combinations of bivariate tensor-product splines in Section
3.2
. Then, in Section
3.3
, we give an explicit procedure how to compute a refined representation of a given polar surface.
Finally, in Section
3.4
, we present explicit descriptions of arbitrary ellipsoids using
C^{1}
smooth low-degree polar spline representations suited for integrated design and analysis.

3.1
Smoothness conditions at the polar points

A polar surface will be smooth at a polar point if it can be locally (re)parameterized in a smooth way.
Such
parameterizations
can be specified in a constructive manner and we elaborate upon it in this section.
The resulting conditions will help us build smooth polar B-splines in the next section.

As shown in Figure
3
, we first describe the initial setup — a tensor-product spline space on a rectangular domain.
We start from two univariate
C^{1}
rational spline spaces
\mathcal{R}^{s},\mathcal{R}^{t}
defined on the univariate domains
{}^{s}:=[s_{1},s_{2}]
and
{}^{t}:=[t_{1},t_{2}]
, respectively; the superscripts of

and
t
are meant to indicate the symbols used for the respective coordinates.
Using a Cartesian product, we build the rectangular domain
\Omega:={}^{s}\times{}^{t}
, and on
\Omega
we define the tensor-product spline space
\mathcal{R}:=\mathcal{R}^{s}\otimes\mathcal{R}^{t}
.
Without loss of generality, we assume that
s_{1}=t_{1}=0
.
This tensor-product spline space is spanned by tensor-product B-spline basis functions
B_{ij}
,
i=1,\dots,{n}^{s}
;
j=1,\dots,{n}^{t}
.
Here,
{n}^{s}
and
{n}^{t}
denote the respective dimensions of the chosen univariate spline spaces; the basis functions spanning these spaces are denoted with
B_{i}^{s}
and
B_{j}^{t}
.
Then, the tensor-product basis function
B_{ij}
is simply the product
B_{i}^{s}B_{j}^{t}
.
The functions
B_{ij}
are assumed to be periodic in
s
and non-periodic in
t
.

Now, let us use the functions
B_{ij}
to map the domain
\Omega
to a polar surface using edge-collapse.
Then, the smoothness conditions at a collapsed edge will only involve those
B_{ij}
that have non-zero first derivatives there.
Observe that, if
{n}^{t}\geq 4
, then any
B_{ij}
with non-zero first derivatives at the bottom edge of
\Omega
will have zero first derivatives at its top edge, and vice versa.
The upshot is that, when we are collapsing both the bottom and top edges of
\Omega
into two polar points, as in Figure
3
(b), the smoothness conditions at those points are independent of each other and can be resolved separately for
{n}^{t}\geq 4
.

3.1.1
Single polar point

In light of the above discussion, in the following we first focus on the case of a single collapsed edge, i.e., the one shown in Figure
3
(a).
We derive smoothness conditions that will help us build smooth polar spline functions (and, using them, smooth polar surfaces).
This is done by explicitly specifying the
parameterization
with respect to which the spline functions are deemed smooth.
First, we construct a planar disk-like domain
pol
, called the
polar parametric domain
, via a suitable polar map
{\boldsymbol{F}}
; see Figure
3
(a).
Next, for an arbitrary
C^{1}
spline
f\in\mathcal{R}
, we define
{f}^{pol}:{}^{pol}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}
to be
f\circ{\boldsymbol{F}}^{-1}
. In general,
{f}^{pol}
will be multivalued at the pole.
Finally, we derive the required smoothness conditions by asking for
{f}^{pol}
to be
C^{1}
smooth at the polar point.

We start by building
{\boldsymbol{F}}
.
Assign the control point
{\boldsymbol{F}}_{ij}:=(\rho_{j}\cos(\theta_{i}),\rho_{j}\sin(\theta_{i}))\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
to the basis function
B_{ij}
, where

\rho_{j}:=\frac{j-1}{{n}^{t}-1}\in[0,1],

(19)

and

\theta_{i}:=2\pi+\frac{(1-2i)\pi}{{n}^{s}}\in[0,2\pi].

(20)

The above choice of control-point values has been made in the interest of standardization and is not unique.
Using these control points, we can construct the disk-like domain
pol
with the aid of the map

{\boldsymbol{F}}
from
\Omega
to
pol
,

\Omega\ni(s,t)\mapsto{\boldsymbol{F}}(s,t)=(u,v)\in{}^{pol},

defined as

{\boldsymbol{F}}(s,t):=(F_{u}(s,t),F_{v}(s,t)):=\sum_{i=1}^{{n}^{s}}\sum_{j=1}^{{n}^{t}}{\boldsymbol{F}}_{ij}B_{ij}(s,t).

(21)

Note that the above construction will not necessarily yield an exactly circular domain
pol
; its shape will depend on the choice of
\mathcal{R}
.
This domain will serve as the reference element for
the smoothness of
polar configurations, i.e., we will define polar splines such that they are
C^{1}
smooth functions over
pol
.
It is clear that for all
s\in{}^{s}
,

{\boldsymbol{F}}(s,0)=(0,0),

(22)

where
(0,0)\in{}^{pol}
is the polar point. Note that this implies

\frac{\partial F_{u}}{\partial s}\bigg|_{t=0}\equiv 0\equiv\frac{\partial F_{v}}{\partial s}\bigg|_{t=0}.

(23)

Let
{B}^{pol}_{ij}
be the image of
B_{ij}
under the polar map

{\boldsymbol{F}}:\Omega\mapsto{}^{pol}
in Equation (
21
)
so that

{B}^{pol}_{ij}(u,v)={B}^{pol}_{ij}({\boldsymbol{F}}(s,t))=B_{ij}(s,t).

Then, for given coefficients
f_{ij}
, a polar spline function
{f}^{pol}
over
pol
can be constructed as

{f}^{pol}(u,v)=\sum_{i=1}^{{n}^{s}}\sum_{j=1}^{{n}^{t}}f_{ij}{B}^{pol}_{ij}(u,v).

We can pull
{f}^{pol}
back to
\Omega
as follows,

\begin{split}f(s,t):={f}^{pol}({\boldsymbol{F}}(s,t))&=\sum_{i=1}^{{n}^{s}}\sum_{j=1}^{{n}^{t}}f_{ij}{B}^{pol}_{ij}\left({\boldsymbol{F}}(s,t)\right)\\
&=\sum_{i=1}^{{n}^{s}}\sum_{j=1}^{{n}^{t}}f_{ij}B_{ij}(s,t).\end{split}

(24)

Moreover, by using the chain rule we can also relate the partial derivatives of
f
and
{f}^{pol}
:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial f}{\partial s}(s,t)

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial{f}^{pol}}{\partial u}(u,v)\dfrac{\partial F_{u}}{\partial s}(s,t)+\frac{\partial{f}^{pol}}{\partial v}(u,v)\dfrac{\partial F_{v}}{\partial s}(s,t),

\displaystyle\frac{\partial f}{\partial t}(s,t)

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial{f}^{pol}}{\partial u}(u,v)\dfrac{\partial F_{u}}{\partial t}(s,t)+\frac{\partial{f}^{pol}}{\partial v}(u,v)\dfrac{\partial F_{v}}{\partial t}(s,t).

For
{f}^{pol}
to be
C^{1}
smooth at the polar point, there must exist real values
\alpha,\beta,\gamma
such that

\begin{gathered}\lim\limits_{(u,v)\rightarrow(0,0)}\left[{f}^{pol},\;\frac{\partial{f}^{pol}}{\partial u},\;\frac{\partial{f}^{pol}}{\partial v}\right](u,v)=[\alpha,\;\beta,\;\gamma].\end{gathered}

(25)

In view of (
22
) and (
23
) this means for all
s\in{}^{s}
,

f(s,0)=\alpha,\quad\frac{\partial f}{\partial t}(s,0)=\beta\dfrac{\partial F_{u}}{\partial t}(s,0)+\gamma\dfrac{\partial F_{v}}{\partial t}(s,0).

In particular, since only
B_{ij}
,
j\leq 2
, have non-zero values and derivatives when
t=0
, the above condition translates to the following requirement for all
s\in{}^{s}
,

\begin{gathered}\sum_{i=1}^{{n}^{s}}f_{i1}B_{i1}(s,0)=\alpha,\\
\sum_{i=1}^{{n}^{s}}\sum_{j=1}^{2}\left[f_{ij}-{\boldsymbol{F}}_{ij}\cdot(\beta,\gamma)\right]\frac{\partial B_{ij}}{\partial t}(s,0)=0.\end{gathered}

(26)

3.1.2
Double polar point

Equation (
26
) shows the required smoothness conditions when the bottom edge of
\Omega
is being collapsed.
Next, if we also want to collapse the top edge of
\Omega
, we can repeat the previous argument with minor changes.
We would, of course, need to choose a map
\hat{{\boldsymbol{F}}}
that collapses the edge
{}^{s}\times{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\{t_{2}\}}
instead.
One way of achieving this could be by choosing the control points
\hat{{\boldsymbol{F}}}_{ij}:=(\hat{\rho}_{j}\cos(\hat{\theta}_{i}),\hat{\rho}_{j}\sin(\hat{\theta}_{i}))\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
, where

\hat{\rho}_{j}:=1-\rho_{j},\quad\hat{\theta}_{i}:=2\pi-\theta_{i}.

Then, we can follow the same argument as in Section
3.1.1
. Asking for
C^{1}
smoothness of
{\hat{f}}^{pol}
is equivalent to asking that there exist real values
\hat{\alpha},\hat{\beta},\hat{\gamma}
such that for all
s\in{}^{s}
,

\begin{gathered}\sum_{i=1}^{{n}^{s}}f_{i,{n}^{t}}B_{i,{n}^{t}}(s,t_{2})=\hat{\alpha},\\
\sum_{i=1}^{{n}^{s}}\sum_{j={n}^{t}-1}^{{n}^{t}}\left[f_{ij}-\hat{{\boldsymbol{F}}}_{ij}\cdot(\hat{\beta},\hat{\gamma})\right]\frac{\partial B_{ij}}{\partial t}(s,t_{2})=0.\end{gathered}

(27)

Note once again that the smoothness at the polar point corresponding to
t=0
is imposed with respect to the
parameterization

{\boldsymbol{F}}(\Omega)
, while that at the polar point corresponding to
t=t_{2}
is imposed with respect to the
parameterization

\hat{{\boldsymbol{F}}}(\Omega)
. The
corresponding smoothness conditions in Equations (
26
) and (
27
) involve different coefficients

f_{ij}
for
{n}^{t}\geq 4
, and so can be resolved separately.

Remark 3.1
.

The choices of
\theta_{i},\rho_{j},\hat{\theta}_{i},\hat{\rho}_{j}
are such that the maps
{\boldsymbol{F}}
and
\hat{{\boldsymbol{F}}}
preserve the orientation of the parametric domain
\Omega
.

3.2
Rational polar B-splines at the polar points

We now elaborate how the derived
C^{1}
smoothness constraints at a polar point will enable the computation of a DTA-compatible extraction matrix.
This matrix represents a linear map to a set of polar spline basis functions that are
C^{1}
smooth on the polar parametric domain.

3.2.1
Single polar point

As before, we start by considering the case of a single collapsed edge, i.e., the one shown in Figure
3
(a).
Let us arrange the set of basis functions
\{{B}^{pol}_{ij}:i=1,\dots,{n}^{s};j=1,\dots,{n}^{t}\}
in a vector
{\boldsymbol{B}}
, where
{B}^{pol}_{ij}
occupies the
(i+(j-1){n}^{s})
-th entry. Our goal is to construct
C^{1}
smooth polar basis functions on the polar parametric domain
pol
as suitable linear combinations of the functions
{B}^{pol}_{ij}
. In other words, we are looking for an extraction matrix
{\boldsymbol{E}}
such that the polar spline basis functions in
\{{N}^{pol}_{l}:l=1,\dots,{n}\}
defined by the following relation,

{\boldsymbol{N}}:={\boldsymbol{E}}{\boldsymbol{B}},

(28)

are
C^{1}
at the polar point.
For fixed
j
, the set
\{{B}^{pol}_{ij}:i=1,\dots,{n}^{s}\}
is called the
(j-1)
-th polar ring of basis functions.
When
j>2
, all basis functions in the
(j-1)
-th ring already satisfy the
C^{1}
continuity conditions at the polar point (their derivatives are identically zero there), so they can be included without modifications in the set of polar basis functions being created. The others will be substituted by three smooth polar basis functions.
This dictates that
{\boldsymbol{E}}
will be a matrix, with
{n}:={n}^{s}({n}^{t}-2)+3
rows and
{n}^{s}{n}^{t}
columns, taking the following sparse block-diagonal form:

{\boldsymbol{E}}:=\begin{bmatrix}[c]\bar{{\boldsymbol{E}}}&\\
&{\boldsymbol{I}}\end{bmatrix},

(29)

where
{\boldsymbol{I}}
is the identity matrix of size
{n}^{s}({n}^{t}-2)\times{n}^{s}({n}^{t}-2)
and
\bar{{\boldsymbol{E}}}
is a matrix of size
3\times 2{n}^{s}
. The entry of
\bar{{\boldsymbol{E}}}
corresponding to its
l
-th row and
\left(i+(j-1){n}^{s}\right)
-th column is denoted with
\bar{E}_{l,(ij)}
. We can then rewrite Equation (
28
) as follows for
l=1,2,3
,

{N}^{pol}_{l}(u,v)=\sum_{i=1}^{{n}^{s}}\sum_{j=1}^{2}\bar{E}_{l,(ij)}{B}^{pol}_{ij}(u,v).

We can pull these back to
\Omega
using Equation (
24
) to obtain the equivalent representation for
l=1,2,3
,

N_{l}(s,t)=\sum_{i=1}^{{n}^{s}}\sum_{j=1}^{2}\bar{E}_{l,(ij)}B_{ij}(s,t).

(30)

We will enforce
C^{1}
continuity at the polar point by requiring the basis functions
{N}^{pol}_{l}
to satisfy a linearly independent Hermite data set at the polar point, in the spirit of Equation (
25
). To this end, we will use three
source basis functions

\{T_{l}:l=1,2,3\}
, that provide us with the appropriate Hermite data.
Given a non-degenerate triangle
\triangle
with vertices
{\boldsymbol{v}}_{1},{\boldsymbol{v}}_{2}
and
{\boldsymbol{v}}_{3}
, let
(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\lambda_{3})
be the unique barycentric coordinates of point
(u,v)
with respect to
\triangle
such that

\lambda_{1}{\boldsymbol{v}}_{1}+\lambda_{2}{\boldsymbol{v}}_{2}+\lambda_{3}{\boldsymbol{v}}_{3}=(u,v),\quad\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{2}+\lambda_{3}=1.

Then, we define

T_{l}(u,v):=\lambda_{l},\quad l=1,2,3.

These functions can be interpreted as triangular Bernstein polynomials of degree
1
. They are non-negative on the domain triangle
\triangle
. Moreover, they are linearly independent, form a partition of unity, and span the space of bivariate polynomials of total degree less than or equal to
1
.
Then, we require that
N_{l}
in Equation (
30
) is a spline function
f
such that it satisfies the continuity constraints in Equation (
26
), with

\alpha=T_{l}(0,0),\quad\beta=\frac{\partial T_{l}}{\partial u}(0,0),\quad\gamma=\frac{\partial T_{l}}{\partial v}(0,0),

for
l=1,2,3
.
In the interest of standardization, we choose the triangle
\triangle
as equilateral with vertices

{\boldsymbol{v}}_{1}=(2\rho_{2},0),\ {\boldsymbol{v}}_{2}=(-\rho_{2},\sqrt{3}\rho_{2}),\ {\boldsymbol{v}}_{3}=(-\rho_{2},-\sqrt{3}\rho_{2});

recall the definition of
\rho_{2}
from Equation (
19
).

After some calculations, we deduce that

\bar{E}_{1,(i1)}=\bar{E}_{2,(i1)}=\bar{E}_{3,(i1)}=\frac{1}{3},

and

\begin{bmatrix}[c]\bar{E}_{1,(i2)}\\
\bar{E}_{2,(i2)}\\
\bar{E}_{3,(i2)}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}[r]\frac{1}{3}&0&\frac{1}{3}\\
-\frac{1}{6}&\frac{\sqrt{3}}{6}&\frac{1}{3}\\
-\frac{1}{6}&-\frac{\sqrt{3}}{6}&\frac{1}{3}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}[c]\cos(\theta_{i})\\
\sin(\theta_{i})\\
1\end{bmatrix}.

This relation says that

\left(\bar{E}_{1,(i2)},\bar{E}_{2,(i2)},\bar{E}_{3,(i2)}\right)

are simply the barycentric coordinates of the control point
{\boldsymbol{F}}_{i2}:=(\rho_{2}\cos(\theta_{i}),\rho_{2}\sin(\theta_{i}))
with respect to
\triangle
.
It is easily checked that
\triangle
encloses the circle centred at
(0,0)
with a radius of
\rho_{2}
, and hence
\left(\bar{E}_{1,(i2)},\bar{E}_{2,(i2)},\bar{E}_{3,(i2)}\right)
are guaranteed to be non-negative.
In summary,
\bar{{\boldsymbol{E}}}
is specified as

\bar{{\boldsymbol{E}}}:=\begin{bmatrix}[c]\frac{1}{3}&\cdots&\frac{1}{3}&\bar{E}_{1,(12)}&\cdots&\bar{E}_{1,(i2)}&\cdots&\bar{E}_{1,({n}^{s}2)}\\
\frac{1}{3}&\cdots&\frac{1}{3}&\bar{E}_{2,(12)}&\cdots&\bar{E}_{2,(i2)}&\cdots&\bar{E}_{2,({n}^{s}2)}\\
\frac{1}{3}&\cdots&\frac{1}{3}&\bar{E}_{3,(12)}&\cdots&\bar{E}_{3,(i2)}&\cdots&\bar{E}_{3,({n}^{s}2)}\end{bmatrix}.

(31)

This matrix has full rank and the column sum is equal to one, thus confirming that
{\boldsymbol{E}}
is DTA-compatible. The following result follows from the above discussion.

Theorem 3.2
.

The
C^{1}
smooth polar spline functions in the set
\{{N}^{pol}_{l}:l=1,\dots,{n}\}
are linearly independent, locally supported, and form a convex partition of unity on
pol
.

Remark 3.3
.

As long as
\triangle
is chosen to be a triangle enclosing the first polar ring of control points
{\boldsymbol{F}}_{ij}
for a given configuration, we are guaranteed non-negative extraction coefficients.
It is only in the interest of standardization that we have chosen to fix
\triangle
as an equilateral triangle with a fixed pattern of vertices.

Given
{n}
control points
{\boldsymbol{f}}_{l}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{{d}}
,
{d}\geq 3
, we can construct a
C^{1}
polar surface
{\boldsymbol{f}}
embedded in
{\mathbb{R}}^{{d}}
,

{\boldsymbol{f}}(u,v)=\sum_{l=1}^{{n}}{\boldsymbol{f}}_{l}{N}^{pol}_{l}(u,v),

or, equivalently, after pulling back to
\Omega
,

{\boldsymbol{f}}(s,t)=\sum_{l=1}^{{n}}{\boldsymbol{f}}_{l}N_{l}(s,t).

(32)

The behavior of
{\boldsymbol{f}}
at the polar point is going to be fully specified by the first three control points
{\boldsymbol{f}}_{1}
,
{\boldsymbol{f}}_{2}
and
{\boldsymbol{f}}_{3}
. These control points can be thought of as forming a
control triangle
.
For a fixed surface, the transpose of the extraction matrix
{\boldsymbol{E}}
defines the relationship between control points of the
B_{ij}
and control points of the smooth
N_{l}
. More precisely, if

\sum_{i=1}^{{n}^{s}}\sum_{j=1}^{{n}^{t}}{\boldsymbol{g}}_{ij}B_{ij}(s,t)={\boldsymbol{f}}(s,t)=\sum_{l=1}^{{n}}{\boldsymbol{f}}_{l}N_{l}(s,t),

then

{\boldsymbol{g}}_{ij}=\sum_{l=1}^{n}E_{l,(ij)}{\boldsymbol{f}}_{l}.

(33)

In particular, the transpose of the extraction matrix
\bar{{\boldsymbol{E}}}
in Equation (
31
) essentially computes the control points of the zeroth and the first polar rings of basis functions
B_{ij}
as convex combinations of
{\boldsymbol{f}}_{1}
,
{\boldsymbol{f}}_{2}
and
{\boldsymbol{f}}_{3}
.
In other words, it forces the zeroth and the first polar rings of the control points
{\boldsymbol{g}}_{ij}
to be coplanar.
The plane in which they lie is the one passing through
{\boldsymbol{f}}_{1}
,
{\boldsymbol{f}}_{2}
and
{\boldsymbol{f}}_{3}
, and hence the control triangle must be tangent to the surface
{\boldsymbol{f}}
at the pole.

In light of
Theorem
3.2
we can immediately conclude the following properties for the pulled-back functions
N_{l}
,
l=1,\dots,{n}
.

Corollary 3.4
.

The spline functions in the set
\{N_{l}:l=1,\dots,{n}\}
are linearly independent, locally supported, and form a convex partition of unity on
\Omega
. Moreover, any polar surface
{\boldsymbol{f}}
as in Equation (
32
) with non-collinear control points
{\boldsymbol{f}}_{1}
,
{\boldsymbol{f}}_{2}
and
{\boldsymbol{f}}_{3}
will have a well-defined tangent plane at the pole.

Remark 3.5
.

According to Section
2.2
, the univariate sets of basis functions
\{B_{i}^{s}:i=1,\dots,{n}^{s}\}
and
\{B_{j}^{t}:j=1,\dots,{n}^{t}\}
are built from local NURBS basis functions through extraction matrices
{\boldsymbol{H}}^{s}
and
{\boldsymbol{H}}^{t}
, respectively. Hence, by combining the matrices
{\boldsymbol{E}}
,
{\boldsymbol{H}}^{s}
and
{\boldsymbol{H}}^{t}
, the spline basis functions in the set
\{N_{l}:l=1,\dots,{n}\}
can be directly expressed in terms of local tensor-product NURBS basis functions.

3.2.2
Double polar point

When dealing with double polar surfaces, the spline construction can be obtained by collapsing a pair of two opposite edges as illustrated in Figure
3
(b). As explained in Section
3.1
, the smoothness treatment of the two poles can be done separately for
{n}^{t}\geq 4
.
In this case, each pole leads to a local extraction matrix by applying the same procedure as in Section
3.2.1
and the combined global extraction matrix takes the following sparse block-diagonal form:

{\boldsymbol{E}}:=\begin{bmatrix}[c]{\bar{{\boldsymbol{E}}}^{(1)}}&&\\
&{\boldsymbol{I}}&\\
&&{\bar{{\boldsymbol{E}}}^{(2)}}\end{bmatrix},

(34)

where
{\boldsymbol{I}}
is the identity matrix of size
{n}^{s}({n}^{t}-4)\times{n}^{s}({n}^{t}-4)
and
{\bar{{\boldsymbol{E}}}^{(i)}}
,
i=1,2
, are matrices of size
3\times 2{n}^{s}
.
By choosing the two polar
parameterizations

{\boldsymbol{F}}(\Omega)
and
\hat{{\boldsymbol{F}}}(\Omega)
specified in Section
3.1
, it is easily verified that one can set

{\bar{{\boldsymbol{E}}}^{(1)}}:=\bar{{\boldsymbol{E}}},\quad{\bar{{\boldsymbol{E}}}^{(2)}}:={\boldsymbol{J}}_{3}\bar{{\boldsymbol{E}}}{\boldsymbol{J}}_{2{n}^{s}},

(35)

where
\bar{{\boldsymbol{E}}}
is the matrix defined in Equation (
31
) and
{\boldsymbol{J}}_{k}
is the exchange matrix of size
k\times k
, i.e., an anti-diagonal matrix of the form

{\boldsymbol{J}}_{k}:=\begin{bmatrix}[c]&&&1\\
&&\iddots&\\
&1&&\\
1&&&\end{bmatrix}.

The extraction matrix
{\boldsymbol{E}}
can then be used to compute the set of spline functions
\{N_{l}:l=1,\dots,{n}\}
in terms of the tensor-product functions
\{B_{ij}:i=1,\dots,{n}^{s};j=1,\dots,{n}^{t}\}
.
Similar to the single-pole result in Corollary
3.4
, these spline functions have the following properties.

Corollary 3.6
.

The spline functions in the set
\{N_{l}:l=1,\dots,{n}\}
are linearly independent, locally supported, and form a convex partition of unity on
\Omega
. Moreover, any polar surface
{\boldsymbol{f}}
as in Equation (
32
) with non-collinear control points
{\boldsymbol{f}}_{1}
,
{\boldsymbol{f}}_{2}
and
{\boldsymbol{f}}_{3}
and with non-collinear control points
{\boldsymbol{f}}_{{n}-2}
,
{\boldsymbol{f}}_{{n}-1}
and
{\boldsymbol{f}}_{{n}}
will have a well-defined tangent plane at both poles.

3.3
Refinement of piecewise-rational polar surfaces

A polar spline surface can be refined in a manner similar to the one discussed in Section
2.3
.
We begin with the following observation. Consider the
3\times 3
matrix

\bar{{\boldsymbol{M}}}:=\begin{bmatrix}[r]\frac{1}{3}&0&\frac{1}{3}\\
-\frac{1}{6}&\frac{\sqrt{3}}{6}&\frac{1}{3}\\
-\frac{1}{6}&-\frac{\sqrt{3}}{6}&\frac{1}{3}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}[c]0&\cos(\theta_{\iota})&\cos(\theta_{\kappa})\\
0&\sin(\theta_{\iota})&\sin(\theta_{\kappa})\\
1&1&1\end{bmatrix},

for some
angles
\theta_{\iota}
and
\theta_{\kappa}\not\in\{\theta_{\iota},\theta_{\iota}+\pi\}
selected from the set in Equation (
20
).
This a submatrix of
\bar{{\boldsymbol{E}}}
, defined in Equation (
31
), consisting of three linearly independent columns. Its inverse is given by

\displaystyle\bar{{\boldsymbol{M}}}^{-1}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}[c]0&\cos(\theta_{\iota})&\cos(\theta_{\kappa})\\
0&\sin(\theta_{\iota})&\sin(\theta_{\kappa})\\
1&1&1\end{bmatrix}^{-1}\begin{bmatrix}[r]\frac{1}{3}&0&\frac{1}{3}\\
-\frac{1}{6}&\frac{\sqrt{3}}{6}&\frac{1}{3}\\
-\frac{1}{6}&-\frac{\sqrt{3}}{6}&\frac{1}{3}\end{bmatrix}^{-1}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sin(\theta_{\kappa}-\theta_{\iota})}\bar{{\boldsymbol{L}}}\begin{bmatrix}[r]2&-1&-1\\
0&\sqrt{3}&-\sqrt{3}\\
1&1&1\end{bmatrix},

where

\bar{{\boldsymbol{L}}}:=\begin{bmatrix}[c]\sin(\theta_{\iota})-\sin(\theta_{\kappa})&\cos(\theta_{\kappa})-\cos(\theta_{\iota})&\sin(\theta_{\kappa}-\theta_{\iota})\\
\sin(\theta_{\kappa})&-\cos(\theta_{\kappa})&0\\
-\sin(\theta_{\iota})&\cos(\theta_{\iota})&0\end{bmatrix}.

Then, we define a sparse matrix
\bar{{\boldsymbol{D}}}
of size
2{n}^{s}\times 3
, whose non-zero entries
\bar{D}_{ij}
are identified as follows: for
j=1,2,3
,

\bar{D}_{1j}:=\bar{M}^{-1}_{1j},\quad\bar{D}_{{n}^{s}+\iota,j}:=\bar{M}^{-1}_{2j},\quad\bar{D}_{{n}^{s}+\kappa,j}:=\bar{M}^{-1}_{2j}.

From its construction it is clear that the product
\bar{{\boldsymbol{E}}}\bar{{\boldsymbol{D}}}
is equal to the identity matrix. Note that the product
({\boldsymbol{J}}_{3}\bar{{\boldsymbol{E}}}{\boldsymbol{J}}_{2{n}^{s}})({\boldsymbol{J}}_{2{n}^{s}}\bar{{\boldsymbol{D}}}{\boldsymbol{J}}_{3})
is also equal to the identity matrix. Hence, keeping in mind the definition of
{\boldsymbol{E}}
in Equations (
34
)–(
35
), the matrix

{\boldsymbol{D}}:=\begin{bmatrix}[c]\bar{{\boldsymbol{D}}}&&\\
&{\boldsymbol{I}}&\\
&&{\boldsymbol{J}}_{2{n}^{s}}\bar{{\boldsymbol{D}}}{\boldsymbol{J}}_{3}\end{bmatrix}

(36)

gives rise to a product
{\boldsymbol{E}}{\boldsymbol{D}}
that is equal to the identity matrix.
A similar matrix
{\boldsymbol{D}}
can be found (with only two diagonal blocks) for the matrix
{\boldsymbol{E}}
defined in Equation (
29
).

Any refinement matrix of polar splines can then be built using the following procedure.
First, we compute the control points of the tensor-product basis functions
B_{ij}
as in Equation (
33
).
Then, we refine the tensor-product control points using a tensor product of univariate refinement matrices (see Section
2.3
).
Denote this matrix with
{\boldsymbol{S}}
, and denote the polar spline extraction matrices before and after refinement with
{\boldsymbol{E}}
and
\tilde{{\boldsymbol{E}}}
, respectively.
Then, control points of the refined polar spline basis functions can be obtained by applying a matrix
{\boldsymbol{R}}
to the original set of polar control points, where
{\boldsymbol{R}}
is computed by solving the following (overdetermined) linear system with a unique solution,

{\boldsymbol{R}}\tilde{{\boldsymbol{E}}}={\boldsymbol{E}}{\boldsymbol{S}}.

After multiplication of both sides of this system with the matrix
\tilde{{\boldsymbol{D}}}
(corresponding to
\tilde{{\boldsymbol{E}}}
) as defined in Equation (
36
), we arrive at

{\boldsymbol{R}}={\boldsymbol{E}}{\boldsymbol{S}}\tilde{{\boldsymbol{D}}}.

(37)

Remark 3.7
.

Similar to Remark
2.6
, there is some flexibility in the definition of
\tilde{{\boldsymbol{D}}}
for the computation of
{\boldsymbol{R}}
in Equation (
37
), as long as it is a right inverse of
\tilde{{\boldsymbol{E}}}
. The current choice is again done for the sake of simplicity. For the computation of
\bar{{\boldsymbol{M}}}^{-1}
, from a numerical point of view, it is advised to select the angles
\theta_{\iota}
and
\theta_{\kappa}
from the set in Equation (
20
) such that their difference is as close as possible to
\pm\pi/2
. For instance, taking
\iota=\kappa+\lfloor{n}^{s}/4+1/2\rfloor
gives

\theta_{\kappa}-\theta_{\iota}=\frac{2\pi}{{n}^{s}}\left\lfloor\frac{{n}^{s}}{4}+\frac{1}{2}\right\rfloor,

and then
\det(\bar{{\boldsymbol{M}}})=\sin(\theta_{\kappa}-\theta_{\iota})\geq 0.86
for
{n}^{s}\geq 3
.

(0,0,0)

\left(\frac{f_{x}}{a_{x}}\right)^{2}+\left(\frac{f_{y}}{a_{y}}\right)^{2}+\left(\frac{f_{z}}{a_{z}}\right)^{2}=1.

0&0&0&0&-e_{+}&-e_{-}&e_{+}&e_{-}\\
0&0&0&0&e_{-}&e_{+}&-e_{-}&-e_{+}\end{bmatrix},

e_{+}=\frac{\sqrt{3}+1}{6},\quad e_{-}=\frac{\sqrt{3}-1}{6};

see Equations (

).
Here the compact notation of adding a scalar to a matrix means the entrywise operation of adding the scalar to each entry of the matrix.

Remark 3.11
.

The examples presented here have focused on the simplest possible
C^{1}
descriptions of quadrics, namely descriptions that either use lowest-degree splines — bi-degree
(2,2)
— or the smallest number of polynomial pieces — two.
Unsurprisingly, these simplest descriptions can lead to large control triangles since each control point influences a large portion of the spline surface.
Nevertheless, localized control is easily attained upon refinement (see Section
3.3
) and, in particular, refinement also leads to much smaller control triangles that offer much finer geometric control.
The surface shown in Figure
5
illustrates this point.
This surface has been obtained by refining and modifying the control points of the bi-degree
(2,2)
sphere from Figure
4
(a).
The smaller control triangle is visible near the top of the figure.

4
Conclusions

We have presented a general class of
C^{1}
smooth rational splines that allow for the construction and refinement of
C^{1}
smooth curves and (polar) surfaces. They are built by gluing together multiple sets of NURBS basis functions with
C^{1}
smoothness using a DTA-compatible extraction matrix. The main features of the
splines we have built
are the following:

•

all standard properties of NURBS, including support for intuitive control-point-based design,

•

(local) degree elevation and knot insertion based on classical NURBS refinement,

•

low-degree
C^{1}
descriptions of exact ellipses and ellipsoids, and

•

compatibility with CAD or CAE software through the explicit representation in terms of NURBS.

In particular, with regard to the last two bullets above,
we believe
that the explicit, NURBS-compatible
C^{1}
descriptions of ellipses and ellipsoids provided herein will be of
use to geometric modellers
[
11
]
and computational scientists
[
24
]
alike. For instance, the exact
C^{1}
(re)parameterizations at polar points may make the design of algorithms more stable and efficient; it may also avoid the need for special treatment of polar points.
