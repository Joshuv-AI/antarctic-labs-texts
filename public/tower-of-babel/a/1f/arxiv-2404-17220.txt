Christian Kuehn, Pascal Lehner, Jan-Eric Sulzbach

Abstract
. The aim of this expository paper is twofold. We start with a concise overview of the theory of invariant slow manifolds for fast-slow dynamical systems starting with the work by Tikhonov and Fenichel to the most recent works on infinite-dimensional fast-slow systems. The main part focuses on a class of linear fast-reaction PDE, which are particular forms of fast-reaction systems. The first result shows the convergence of solutions of the linear system to the limit system as the time-scale parameter ε \varepsilon goes to zero. Moreover, from the explicit solutions the slow manifold is constructed and the convergence to the critical manifold is proven. The subsequent result, then, states a generalized version of the Fenichel-Tikhonov theorem for linear fast-reaction systems.

1.
Introduction

In all natural sciences, one frequently observes dynamical systems that exhibit a multiple timescale behaviour. The fundamental reason for this is that natural systems tend to be influenced by many different processes, and the more processes are taken into account, the more likely it is to obtain systems that evolve on different timescales. The involved timescales can range, depending on the application, from nanoseconds, for example, due to fluctuations on atomic levels to years due to, for example, seasonal variability of the earth. The modeling of such systems often results in a coupled system of differential equations with many variables. These system can be computationally challenging, especially if the system is resolved on the fastest timescale.

One building block to model these multi-scale systems is to consider the case two timescales, so-called fast-slow systems. These differential equations can be written in their standard form abstractly as

(1.1)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\varepsilon\partial_{t}u^{\varepsilon}&=F(u^{\varepsilon},v^{\varepsilon},\varepsilon),\\
\partial_{t}v^{\varepsilon}&=G(u^{\varepsilon},v^{\varepsilon},\varepsilon),\end{split}

where
u^{\varepsilon}=u^{\varepsilon}(t)\in X
is the fast variable and
v^{\varepsilon}=v^{\varepsilon}(t)\in Y
the slow variable. The variables depend on time
t
belonging to some interval
\mathcal{I}
with
0\in\mathcal{I}
. The state spaces
X
and
Y
are often assumed to be Banach spaces, while the system (
1.1
) is an ODE if state spaces are finite dimensional, while PDEs are the most common examples if
X
and/or
Y
are infinite dimensional. With
F,G
we denote general maps, that may contain (differential) operators, which determine the evolution of the variables. The time scale parameter
\varepsilon>0
formally determines the quantitative amount of time scale separation between the two variables. Furthermore, suitable initial conditions can be added to the fast-slow system. We write the variables
u^{\varepsilon},v^{\varepsilon}
with a superscript
\varepsilon
to indicate the dependence of solutions on
\varepsilon
.

Since in many applications
\varepsilon
is assumed to be very small, it is natural to consider the limit
\varepsilon\to 0
of system (
1.1
) and try to reduce the system at hand. We obtain in this singular limit the system

(1.2)

\displaystyle\begin{split}0&=F(u^{0},v^{0},0),\\
\partial_{t}v^{0}&=G(u^{0},v^{0},0).\end{split}

This equation is a differential-algebraic differential equation, which is defined on the critical set

\displaystyle C_{0}:=\{(u^{0},v^{0})\in X\times Y:F(u^{0},v^{0},0)=0\}.

This limit system ignores the fast parts of the original system and only focuses on the dynamics of the slow variable and via this complexity reduction is therefore in general easier to study. For ODEs, the theory is quite well-understood and we briefly review it below. Yet, one major problem with this procedure for PDEs is that most differential operators that are defined on infinite dimensional spaces are not bounded, so taking the limit
\varepsilon\to 0
on the right-hand side of (
1.1
) may simply not be justified. The simplest example is the Laplace operator that is often used to model systems involving diffusion processes. Therefore, in the case of PDEs one has to be careful with obtaining the limit system, since taking the limit can potentially be much more singular than the ODE analog.

Next, we present a brief overview of the literature on fast-slow dynamical systems with a focus on the results in infinite dimensions. The theory for bounded operators, which leads to fast-slow ODEs dates back to the work by Tikhonov
[
20
]
and Fenichel
[
11
,
10
]
and is known in the literature as geometric singular perturbation theory (GSPT). Suppose
S_{0}\subseteq C_{0}
is a compact and normally hyperbolic submanifold, i.e., it is a sufficiently smooth manifold and the eigenvalues of the operator
A
in the fast direction have nonzero real values for all
p\in S_{0}
. Then the so called Fenichel-Tikhonov theory guarantees the existence of locally invariant slow manifolds
S^{\varepsilon}
. For more details and applications of this theory we refer to the books by Jones
[
17
]
and Kuehn
[
18
]
. Yet, for infinite-dimensional systems new methods are required since applying the classical theory to PDEs does not hold and leads to problems as shown in
[
15
]
.

One idea to overcome these problems is to adapt the theory of inertial manifolds
[
5
,
12
]
. The main goal of this theory is to construct a lower/finite-dimensional attracting invariant manifold. However, this approach requires a global dissipative structure of the PDE and compact embeddings to construct these lower-dimensional invariant manifolds. In addition, the inertial manifold theory relies on a spectral-gap type condition that allows the splitting of the underlying space into a finite-dimensional subspace and its complement.

The next approach which is more along the lines of the classical fast-slow theory for ODEs is due to Bates et al.
[
2
,
4
,
3
]
. Here, for the case of semiflows the authors prove a local existence and persistence result of the so called slow manifold. In particular, their theory includes the case of partially dissipative systems, where the dynamics for the fast variable
u
can be a PDE while the slow variable dynamics is an ODE. However, for fast-slow systems where both components are PDEs, this idea cannot be applied as was shown in
[
15
]
. A different approach focusing on the construction of a slow manifold for differential inclusions can be found in
[
13
,
1
]
.

In recent years progress was made for several classes of fast-slow PDEs. In
[
15
]
a general theory is developed for the case of
F(u,v,\varepsilon)=Au+f(u,v)
and
G(u,v,\varepsilon)=Bu+g(u,v)
with
A,B
being closed linear (differential) operators and
f,g
nonlinear functions. By introducing a modified notion of normal hyperbolicity the authors can prove, with few assumptions, the convergence of solutions of the system (
1.1
) to solutions of (
1.2
) in a suitable Banach space. Under slightly more stringent assumptions, one of which is a spectral-gap type condition for the operator
B
, the authors can also show the existence and persistence of slow manifolds. The assumption of normal hyperbolicity is key as was shown in
[
8
]
and some applications of the general theory can be found in
[
6
,
15
,
7
]
. For the class of fast-slow PDEs of the form
F(u,v,\varepsilon)=\varepsilon Au+f(u,v)
and
G(u,v,\varepsilon)=Bu+g(u,v)
the generalization of the classical results by Tikhonov and Fenichel was proven in
[
19
]
. In this work notion of normal hyperbolicity is adapted to account for the fact that the fast dynamics in this case are driven by the nonlinearity
f
. But again, a spectral-gap type condition is required to prove the existence and persistence of a slow invariant manifold.

In dynamical systems theory, understanding nonlinear systems is generally hard. However, the linear case is often easier to understand and it is usually a useful first step to gain deeper insights into the behavior of a system. In this work we study the linear case of the fast-slow systems analyzed in
[
19
]
, so called fast-reaction systems. Here we want to provide an explicit example, where all objects can be identified via formulas in contrast to the far more abstract setting in
[
19
]
, which turns out to be quite instructive to learn about the problems arising in the geometric analysis of multiscale fast-reaction systems. These fast-reaction equations are an important subclass of fast-slow systems and occur in combination with diffusion operators for example in biology, chemistry or population dynamics. The mathematical study of fast-reaction systems dates back at least to the work by Hilhorst et al.
[
14
]
. Over the last years there have been many results concerning the existence and uniqueness of solutions and the convergence to a limit system from a global functional-analytic perspective. A review on this topic can be found in
[
16
]
.

Now, for the linear case of an infinite dimensional fast-reaction system we assume
f,g
to be linear functions. Then, the aim of this work is to construct explicit solutions via Fourier methods and to show the convergence of solutions towards solutions of the limit system as the time scale parameter
\varepsilon
goes to zero. To do this we use typical Gronwall inequality estimates. Moreover, the slow manifold is
explicitly
constructed, without requiring a spectral gap condition on the operator, and it converges to the critical manifold on the space of Fourier coefficients. This shows clearly that the nonlinear terms generate the difficulty regarding the mixing of different scales in the problem, which one would also conjecture intuitively from a physical perspective.

2.
Analysis of a linear fast-reaction system

In the following, we present in detail the functional setting of the linear fast-reaction system at hand.
As state spaces we choose
X:=Y:=L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
and for the linear operators we set
A:=\Delta-\mu I:H^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\to L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
and
B:=\Delta-\nu I:H^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\to L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, where
I
denotes the identity on the Sobolev space
H^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
and
\mu,\nu\in\mathbb{R}
are additional parameters.
As initial conditions we take
u_{0}
and
v_{0}
, which are assumed to be in
H^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
.
Hence, we obtain a fully linear fast-reaction system the form

(2.1)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\varepsilon\partial_{t}u^{\varepsilon}(t)&=\varepsilon(\Delta-\mu I)u^{\varepsilon}(t)+\alpha u^{\varepsilon}(t)+\beta v^{\varepsilon}(t),\\
\partial_{t}v^{\varepsilon}(t)&=(\Delta-\nu I)v^{\varepsilon}(t)+\gamma u^{\varepsilon}(t)+\delta v^{\varepsilon}(t),\\
u^{\varepsilon}(0)&=u_{0}\in H^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n}),\quad v^{\varepsilon}(0)=v_{0}\in H^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n}),\end{split}

for
t\geq 0
, where the non zero parameters
\beta,\gamma,\delta\in\mathbb{R}\backslash\{0\}
and
\alpha<0
characterise the in this case linear functions
f
and
g
.
The associated limit system for
\varepsilon\to 0
is a differential-algebraic equation of the form

(2.2)

\displaystyle\begin{split}0&=\alpha u^{0}(t)+\beta v^{0}(t),\\
\partial_{t}v^{0}(t)&=(\Delta-\nu I)v^{0}(t)+\gamma u^{0}(t)+\delta v^{0}(t),\\
u^{0}(0)&=-\alpha^{-1}\beta v^{0}(0)\in H^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n}),\quad v^{0}(0)=v_{0}\in H^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\end{split}

defined on a linear critical set

\displaystyle S_{0}:=\{(u,v)\in H^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\times H^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n}):\alpha u+\beta v=0\}\subset L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\times L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n}).

2.1.
Explicit solutions of the linear system

Since both systems (
2.1
) and (
2.2
) are linear in
u^{\varepsilon}
and
v^{\varepsilon}
, the existence and uniqueness of solutions follows in a straightforward way and we refer to
[
9
]
for more details on the existence theory for linear PDEs.
However, using Fourier analysis we can easily write down the explicit solutions of the systems. We start with the simpler system, the limit system.

Lemma 2.1
.

The explicit solution to the limit system (
2.2
) has the form

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}u^{0}(t)\\
v^{0}(t)\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}-\alpha^{-1}\beta v^{0}(t)\\
{\text{e}}^{\kappa t}K_{n}(t)*v_{0}\end{pmatrix},

where
\kappa:=-\nu-\alpha^{-1}\beta\gamma+\delta
.
Here,
K_{n}(t):=(4\pi t)^{-\frac{n}{2}}{\text{e}}^{\frac{-|k|^{2}}{4t}}
denotes the
n
-dimensional heat kernel, where

*
is the usual convolution of measurable functions.

Proof.

Since
\alpha\neq 0
the first equation of (
2.2
) gives
u^{0}(t)=-\alpha^{-1}\beta v^{0}(t)
.
The Fourier transform to some integrable function
w=w(t,x)
is defined as

(\mathcal{F}w)(t):=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}w(t,x){\text{e}}^{-2\pi ikx}dx.

Applying this to the equation for
v^{0}
yields

\partial_{t}(\mathcal{F}v^{0})(t)=(-4\pi^{2}|k|^{2}-\nu-\alpha^{-1}\beta\gamma+\delta)\mathcal{F}v^{0}(t)

for all
k\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, since
\mathcal{F}(\Delta v^{0})=-4\pi^{2}|k|^{2}\mathcal{F}(v^{0})
.
So, we can infer that

(\mathcal{F}v^{0})(t)={\text{e}}^{(-4\pi^{2}|k|^{2}+\kappa)t}(\mathcal{F}v_{0}),

where
\kappa:=-\nu-\alpha^{-1}\beta\gamma+\delta
and
which leads upon an inverse Fourier transformation to the claimed form.
Recall that the space
H^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
can be defined via Fourier transforms

H^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n}):=\{f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\,:\,(1+|k|^{2})\mathcal{F}f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\}.

Hence applying Plancherel’s identity in the upcoming estimates it is sufficient to show that
\|(1+|k|^{2})\mathcal{F}f\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}
remains bounded.
Then, from the regularity of the heat kernel and the initial data
v_{0}
it follows that
v^{0}(t),u^{0}(t)\in H^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
.

∎

Lemma 2.2
.

For small enough
\varepsilon>0
the explicit solution to the complete system (
2.1
) is given by

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}u^{\varepsilon}(t)\\
v^{\varepsilon}(t)\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}[\rho_{1}^{\varepsilon}L^{\varepsilon}_{+}(t)+\rho_{2}^{\varepsilon}L^{\varepsilon}_{-}(t)]*u_{0}+[\rho_{3}^{\varepsilon}L^{\varepsilon}_{+}(t)+\rho_{4}^{\varepsilon}L^{\varepsilon}_{-}(t)]*v_{0}\\
[\rho_{5}^{\varepsilon}L^{\varepsilon}_{+}(t)+\rho_{6}^{\varepsilon}L^{\varepsilon}_{-}(t)]*u_{0}+[\rho_{7}^{\varepsilon}L^{\varepsilon}_{+}(t)+\rho_{8}^{\varepsilon}L^{\varepsilon}_{-}(t)]*v_{0}\end{pmatrix},

with

L^{\varepsilon}_{\pm}(t):={\text{e}}^{(\varepsilon^{-1}\alpha-\mu+\delta-\nu\pm\Omega^{\varepsilon})\frac{t}{2}}K_{n}(t)

and where

\Omega^{\varepsilon}:=\sqrt{(\delta-\nu-\varepsilon^{-1}\alpha+\mu)^{2}+4\varepsilon^{-1}\beta\gamma}\,\in\mathbb{R}\backslash\{0\}.

The coefficients
\rho^{\varepsilon}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
for
i\in\{1,...,8\}
depend only on the constants
\alpha,\beta,\gamma,\delta,\mu,\nu
and the parameter
\varepsilon
.

Proof.

First, applying the Fourier transform to the linear system (
2.1
) yields for
t>0

(2.3)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\varepsilon\partial_{t}(\mathcal{F}u^{\varepsilon})(t)&=(-\varepsilon 4\pi^{2}|k|^{2}-\mu\varepsilon+\alpha)(\mathcal{F}u^{\varepsilon})(t)+\beta(\mathcal{F}v^{\varepsilon})(t),\\
\partial_{t}(\mathcal{F}v^{\varepsilon})(t)&=\gamma(\mathcal{F}u^{\varepsilon})(t)+(\delta-4\pi^{2}|k|^{2}-\nu)(\mathcal{F}v^{\varepsilon})(t)\\
(\mathcal{F}u^{\varepsilon})(0)&=(\mathcal{F}u_{0})\in H^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n}),\quad(\mathcal{F}v^{\varepsilon})(0)=(\mathcal{F}{v_{0}})\in H^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\end{split}

for all
k\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
To simplify the notation write the above system as a two-dimensional ODE for each Fourier coefficient
k
, where we set

w_{k}^{\varepsilon}(t):=\begin{pmatrix}(\mathcal{F}u^{\varepsilon})(t)\\
(\mathcal{F}v^{\varepsilon})(t)\end{pmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}.

Then, multiplying the first equation of system (
2.3
) by
\varepsilon^{-1}
, we can reformulate the system as

\partial_{t}w_{k}^{\varepsilon}(t)=M_{k}^{\varepsilon}w_{k}^{\varepsilon}(t)

where

M_{k}^{\varepsilon}:=\begin{pmatrix}\varepsilon^{-1}\alpha-\mu-4\pi^{2}|k|^{2}&\varepsilon^{-1}\beta\\
\gamma&\delta-\nu-4\pi^{2}|k|^{2}\\
\end{pmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2}.

The eigenvalues of
M^{\varepsilon}_{k}
are given by

\lambda^{\varepsilon}_{\pm}(k):=\frac{1}{2}\left(\varepsilon^{-1}\alpha-\mu+\delta-\nu-8\pi^{2}|k|^{2}\mp\Omega^{\varepsilon}\right)\in\mathbb{R}.

with

\Omega^{\varepsilon}:=\sqrt{(\delta-\nu-\varepsilon^{-1}\alpha+\mu)^{2}+4\varepsilon^{-1}\beta\gamma}.

Regardless of the values of the parameters, one can always ensure that

(\delta-\nu-\varepsilon^{-1}\alpha+\mu)^{2}>-4\varepsilon^{-1}\beta\gamma

for all sufficiently small
\varepsilon
.
Hence, we can assume that
\varepsilon
is small enough such that
\Omega^{\varepsilon}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
and
\lambda^{\varepsilon}_{\pm}\in\mathbb{R}
.
Now, if
|k|^{2}=\frac{\varepsilon^{-1}\alpha-\mu+\delta-\nu\mp\Omega^{\varepsilon}}{8\pi^{2}}=:\rho^{\varepsilon}_{\pm}
we have
\lambda^{\varepsilon}_{\pm}(k)=0
. But since the sets

Q_{\pm}:=\{k\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:|k|^{2}=\rho^{\varepsilon}_{\mp}\}

are
(n-1)
-dimensional spheres of radius
\sqrt{\rho_{\pm}^{\varepsilon}}
if
\rho_{i}^{\varepsilon}>0
,
Q_{\pm}
are null sets with respect to the Lebesgue measure in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
So in the following we can assume that we have non-zero eigenvalues for almost every
k\in\mathbb{R}
.

Therefore, we can derive a formula for the solution of system (
2.3
) using matrix diagonalization of
M_{k}^{\varepsilon}
, i.e. we can write for almost every
k

M^{\varepsilon}_{k}=S^{\varepsilon}J^{\varepsilon}_{k}({S^{\varepsilon}})^{-1}

with

\displaystyle S^{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{2\gamma}\begin{pmatrix}\varepsilon^{-1}\alpha-\mu-\delta+\nu-\Omega^{\varepsilon}&\varepsilon^{-1}\alpha-\mu-\delta+\nu+\Omega^{\varepsilon}\\
2\gamma&2\gamma\end{pmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2}

and

\displaystyle J^{\varepsilon}_{k}

\displaystyle:=\begin{pmatrix}\lambda^{\varepsilon}_{-}(k)&0\\
0&\lambda^{\varepsilon}_{+}(k)\end{pmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2}.

Using the diagonalization the solution of system (
2.3
) can be written as

\begin{pmatrix}(\mathcal{F}u^{\varepsilon})(t)\\
(\mathcal{F}v^{\varepsilon})(t)\end{pmatrix}=S^{\varepsilon}{\text{e}}^{J_{k}^{\varepsilon}t}(S^{\varepsilon})^{-1}\begin{pmatrix}(\mathcal{F}u_{0})\\
(\mathcal{F}v_{0})\end{pmatrix}.

After performing the matrix multiplications we get

\displaystyle(\mathcal{F}u^{\varepsilon})(t)=\frac{1}{{2\Omega^{\varepsilon}}}\bigg(

\displaystyle{(\varepsilon^{-1}\alpha-\mu-\delta+\nu+\Omega^{\varepsilon})}{\text{e}}^{\lambda^{\varepsilon}_{-}(k)t}

\displaystyle-({\varepsilon^{-1}\alpha-\mu-\delta+\nu-\Omega^{\varepsilon}}){\text{e}}^{\lambda^{\varepsilon}_{+}(k)t}\bigg)(\mathcal{F}u_{0})

\displaystyle-\frac{\varepsilon^{-1}\beta}{\Omega^{\varepsilon}}\bigg(

\displaystyle{\text{e}}^{\lambda_{-}^{\varepsilon}(k)t}-{\text{e}}^{\lambda_{+}^{\varepsilon}(k)t}\bigg)(\mathcal{F}v_{0})

and

\displaystyle(\mathcal{F}v^{\varepsilon})(t)=\frac{\gamma}{\Omega^{\varepsilon}}\bigg(

\displaystyle{\text{e}}^{\lambda^{\varepsilon}_{-}(k)t}-{\text{e}}^{\lambda^{\varepsilon}_{+}(k)t}\bigg)(\mathcal{F}u_{0})

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{{2\Omega^{\varepsilon}}}\bigg(

\displaystyle{-(\varepsilon^{-1}\alpha-\mu-\delta+\nu+\Omega^{\varepsilon})}{\text{e}}^{\lambda^{\varepsilon}_{-}(k)t}

\displaystyle-{(\varepsilon^{-1}\alpha-\mu-\delta+\nu-\Omega^{\varepsilon})}{\text{e}}^{\lambda^{\varepsilon}_{+}(k)t}\bigg)(\mathcal{F}v_{0}).

Note that except for the initial conditions
\mathcal{F}u_{0}
and
\mathcal{F}v_{0}
, the only term in the above expressions that depends on
k
is the exponential term
{\text{e}}^{\lambda^{\varepsilon}_{\pm}(k)t}
. Thus, all that remains is to perform the inverse Fourier transform of this function, which evaluates to be
L^{\varepsilon}_{\pm}(t)
. By the linearity of the Fourier transform, we obtain the claimed solution for system (
2.1
). Since the Fourier transform involves integration over
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we can neglect that the solution formula for the Fourier transform is valid only for almost every
k\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

Moreover, we observe that
(1+|k|^{2})\rho_{i}^{\varepsilon}e^{\lambda^{\varepsilon}_{\pm}(t)}\mathcal{F}(u_{0})\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
for
t>0
and, respectively, the same holds for
v_{0}
.
Hence the solution satisfies
u^{\varepsilon}(t),v^{\varepsilon}(t)\in H^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
.
∎

Remark 2.3
.

We note that all coefficients
\rho_{i}^{\varepsilon}
introduced in the previous proof are well defined in the limit as
\varepsilon\to 0
.
Indeed, we have

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}(\Omega^{\varepsilon})^{-1}=0

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\varepsilon^{-1}(\Omega^{\varepsilon})^{-1}=\alpha^{-1}
, which yields the desired bounds for the coefficients.

2.2.
Convergence of solutions

As in
[
15
]
and
[
19
]
in order to solve the limit system (
2.2
) we have introduced a function

h^{0}:H^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\to H^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n}),\,y\mapsto h^{0}(y)

such that the critical manifold
S_{0}
can be written as

\displaystyle S_{0}=\{(h^{0}(y),y):y\in H^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\}.

In the case of system (
2.2
) the function
h^{0}
is linear and given by
h^{0}(v^{0}):=\alpha^{-1}\beta v^{0}=u^{0}
.

In addition, we observe that the two systems
are autonomous and hence we can write the solutions
(u^{\varepsilon},v^{\varepsilon})
and
(u^{0},v^{0})
as semi-flows.
That is, there exist continuous mappings

\displaystyle T_{\varepsilon}:[0,T]\times H^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\times H^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\to H^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\times H^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\text{ and }T_{0}:[0,T]\times S_{0}\to S_{0}

for any
T>0
such that the solutions can be written as

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}u^{\varepsilon}(t)\\
v^{\varepsilon}(t)\end{pmatrix}=T_{\varepsilon}(t)\begin{pmatrix}u_{0}\\
v_{0}\end{pmatrix}\text{ and }\begin{pmatrix}u^{0}(t)\\
v^{0}(t)\end{pmatrix}=T_{0}(t)\begin{pmatrix}h^{0}(v_{0})\\
v_{0}\end{pmatrix}.

The following theorem provides a convergence result of the semi-flows of the linear system (
2.1
) towards solutions of the limit system (
2.2
) as
\varepsilon\to 0
.

Theorem 2.4
.

Let
\varepsilon_{0}>0
be small enough such that
\varepsilon^{-1}_{0}\alpha-\mu<0
and
T>0
. Then, for all
\varepsilon\in(0,\varepsilon_{0}]
there exist a positive increasing function
C_{\nu,\kappa}(t)
depending on the parameters
\nu
and
\kappa:=-\nu-\alpha^{-1}\beta\gamma+\delta
such that

\displaystyle\left\|T_{\varepsilon}(t)\begin{pmatrix}u_{0}\\
v_{0}\end{pmatrix}-T_{0}(t)\begin{pmatrix}h^{0}(v_{0})\\
v_{0}\end{pmatrix}\right\|_{H^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\times H^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\leq

\displaystyle\quad\leq\varepsilon C_{\nu,\kappa}(t)\bigg(\|u_{0}-h^{0}(v_{0})\|_{H^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}+\|v_{0}\|_{H^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\bigg),

for all
t\in(0,T)
.
In the case
\nu>0
and
\kappa<0
we can set
C_{\nu,\kappa}(t)=C>0
.

Proof of Theorem
2.4

The proof of Theorem
2.4
is split into several steps.
The overall idea is to introduce two auxiliary systems that in some sense interpolate between the equation of the fast variable and its algebraic limit. Then, by using a variation of constants method in combination with the Fourier transform we obtain estimates for the Fourier coefficients and thus for the solutions themselves.

In the first step we introduce the two approximate systems in the fast component
u^{\varepsilon}
.
That is, we have
u^{\varepsilon,0}
as the solution to

(2.4)

\displaystyle\varepsilon\partial_{t}u^{\varepsilon,0}(t)

\displaystyle=\varepsilon(\Delta-\mu I)u^{\varepsilon,0}(t)+\alpha u^{\varepsilon,0}(t)+\beta v^{0}(t)+\varepsilon(\partial_{t}-\Delta+\mu I)h^{0}(v^{0}(t))

and
\tilde{u}^{\varepsilon}
solving

(2.5)

\displaystyle\varepsilon\partial_{t}\tilde{u}^{\varepsilon}(t)

\displaystyle=\varepsilon(\Delta-\mu I)\tilde{u}^{\varepsilon}(t)+\alpha\tilde{u}^{\varepsilon}(t)+\beta v^{0}(t),

where in both cases the equation in the slow variable
v
is given by

(2.6)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}v^{0}(t)

\displaystyle=(\Delta-\nu I)v^{0}(t)-\gamma\beta\alpha^{-1}v^{0}(t)+\delta v^{0}(t)

and the initial data satisfy

\displaystyle u^{\varepsilon,0}(0)=u_{0},\quad\tilde{u}^{\varepsilon}(0)=u_{0},\quad v^{0}(0)=v_{0}.

The motivation behind introducing this approximate system
u^{\varepsilon,0}
is that the solution gets close to the solution of the limit system (
2.2
).
A formal way to see this is to consider the different powers of
\varepsilon
in the equation.
In
\mathcal{O}(1)
we have

\alpha u^{\varepsilon,0}(t)+\beta v^{0}(t)=0

and in
\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon)
we get

\partial_{t}(u^{\varepsilon,0}-h^{0}(v^{0}(t)))=(\Delta-\mu I)(u^{\varepsilon,0}-h^{0}(v^{0}(t)))

which can be viewed as
\alpha u^{\varepsilon,0}+\beta v^{0}=0
, since for small
\varepsilon
it holds that
u^{\varepsilon,0}\approx h^{0}(v^{0})
. Thus, in both orders of
\varepsilon
we obtain an equation corresponding to an equation characterizing the critical manifold of system (
2.2
).

The well-posedness of the auxiliary systems follows from using the linearity of the systems and by noting that the equation in
v
is independent of the fast variable
u
.
Therefore, one can first solve the
v
-equation and then the equation in
u^{\varepsilon,0}
and
\tilde{u}^{\varepsilon}
respectively.

The next step of the proof is to estimate the fast variable.
By applying a triangle inequality we have

\displaystyle\|u^{\varepsilon}-h^{0}(v^{0})\|_{H^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\!\leq\|u^{\varepsilon}-\tilde{u}^{\varepsilon}\|_{H^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\!+\|\tilde{u}^{\varepsilon}-u^{\varepsilon,0}\|_{H^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\!+\|u^{\varepsilon,0}-h^{0}(v^{0})\|_{H^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}.

In the following proposition we give further estimates for each term.

Proposition 2.5
.

Let
\alpha<0
and assume that
\varepsilon>0
is small enough such that
\varepsilon^{-1}\alpha<\mu
. Then if
u^{\varepsilon}
is the solution of equation (
2.1
) and
\tilde{u}^{\varepsilon}
the solution to equation (
2.5
) it holds for
t\geq 0

\|u^{\varepsilon}(t)-\tilde{u}^{\varepsilon}(t)\|_{H^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\leq C\sup_{s\in[0,t]}\|v^{\varepsilon}(s)-v^{0}(s)\|_{H^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})},

for some constant
C>0
.

Proof.

Applying the Fourier transform to the first equation in (
2.1
) and multiplying by
\varepsilon^{-1}
gives

\partial_{t}(\mathcal{F}u^{\varepsilon})(t)=(-4\pi^{2}|k|^{2}-\mu+\varepsilon^{-1}\alpha)(\mathcal{F}u^{\varepsilon})(t)+\varepsilon^{-1}\beta(\mathcal{F}v^{\varepsilon})(t)

for
k\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. By variation of constants we have

(\mathcal{F}u^{\varepsilon})(t)={\text{e}}^{(-4\pi^{2}|k|^{2}-\mu+\varepsilon^{-1}\alpha)t}(\mathcal{F}u_{0})+\varepsilon^{-1}\beta\int_{0}^{t}{\text{e}}^{(-4\pi^{2}|k|^{2}-\mu+\varepsilon^{-1}\alpha)(t-s)}(\mathcal{F}v^{\varepsilon})(s)\textnormal{d}s.

On the other hand,

(\mathcal{F}\tilde{u}^{\varepsilon})(t)={\text{e}}^{(-4\pi^{2}|k|^{2}-\mu+\varepsilon^{-1}\alpha)t}(\mathcal{F}u_{0})+\varepsilon^{-1}\beta\int_{0}^{t}{\text{e}}^{(-4\pi^{2}|k|^{2}-\mu+\varepsilon^{-1}\alpha)(t-s)}(\mathcal{F}v^{0})(s)\textnormal{d}s.

So by Plancherel’s theorem and a generalized Hölder’s inequality we obtain

\displaystyle\|u^{\varepsilon}(t)-\tilde{u}^{\varepsilon}(t)\|_{H^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}

\displaystyle=\|(1+|k|^{2})\big((\mathcal{F}u^{\varepsilon})(t)-(\mathcal{F}\tilde{u}^{\varepsilon})(t)\big)\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}

\displaystyle\leq|\beta|\varepsilon^{-1}\int_{0}^{t}\bigg(\|{\text{e}}^{(-4\pi^{2}|k|^{2}-\mu+\varepsilon^{-1}\alpha)(t-s)}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\times\|(1+|k|^{2})\big((\mathcal{F}v^{\varepsilon})(s)-(\mathcal{F}v^{0})(s)\big)\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\bigg)\textnormal{d}s

\displaystyle\leq|\beta|\sup_{s\in[0,t]}\|(1+|k|^{2})\big((\mathcal{F}v^{\varepsilon})(s)-(\mathcal{F}v^{0})(s)\big)\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}

\displaystyle\quad\times\varepsilon^{-1}\int_{0}^{t}{\text{e}}^{(-\mu+\varepsilon^{-1}\alpha)(t-s)}\textnormal{d}s

\displaystyle=|\beta|\sup_{s\in[0,t]}\|(1+|k|^{2})\big((\mathcal{F}v^{\varepsilon})(s)-(\mathcal{F}v^{0})(s)\big)\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}

\displaystyle\quad\times\frac{1-{\text{e}}^{(-\mu+\varepsilon^{-1}\alpha)t}}{\mu-\varepsilon^{-1}\alpha}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{|\beta|}{\mu-\varepsilon^{-1}\alpha}\sup_{s\in[0,t]}\|v^{\varepsilon}(s)-v^{0}(s)\|_{H^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}

\displaystyle\leq C\sup_{s\in[0,t]}\|v^{\varepsilon}(s)-v^{0}(s)\|_{H^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}.

∎

Proposition 2.6
.

Let
\alpha<0
and assume that
\varepsilon>0
is small enough such that
-\mu+\varepsilon^{-1}\alpha<\kappa:=-\nu-\beta\alpha^{-1}\gamma+\delta
.
Then for
t\geq 0
there is a
C>0
such that

\displaystyle\|u^{\varepsilon,0}(t)-\tilde{u}^{\varepsilon}(t)\|_{H^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\leq\varepsilon C\textnormal{e}^{\kappa t}\|v_{0}\|_{H^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}.

Proof.

Since by equation (
2.6
)
(\partial_{t}-\Delta)v^{0}(t)=(-\nu-\beta\alpha^{-1}\gamma+\delta)v^{0}(t)=\kappa v^{0}(t)
, equation (
2.4
) can be written as

\displaystyle\varepsilon\partial_{t}u^{\varepsilon,0}(t)=\varepsilon(\Delta-\mu I)u^{\varepsilon,0}(t)+\alpha u^{\varepsilon,0}(t)+\left(\beta-\varepsilon\beta\alpha^{-1}(\mu+\kappa)\right)v^{0}(t).

Applying the Fourier transform and multiplying with
\varepsilon^{-1}
gives

\displaystyle\partial_{t}(\mathcal{F}u^{\varepsilon,0})(t)

\displaystyle=(-4\pi^{2}|k|^{2}-\mu+\varepsilon^{-1}\alpha)(\mathcal{F}u^{\varepsilon,0})(t)

\displaystyle\quad+\left(\varepsilon^{-1}\beta-\beta\alpha^{-1}(\mu+\kappa)\right){\text{e}}^{(-4\pi^{2}|k|^{2}+\kappa)t}(\mathcal{F}v_{0})

for all
k\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. By variation of constants we get

\displaystyle(\mathcal{F}u^{\varepsilon,0})(t)

\displaystyle={\text{e}}^{(-4\pi^{2}|k|^{2}-\mu+\varepsilon^{-1}\alpha)t}(\mathcal{F}u_{0})

\displaystyle+\left(\beta\varepsilon^{-1}-\alpha^{-1}\beta(\mu+\kappa)\right)\int_{0}^{t}{\text{e}}^{(-4\pi^{2}|k|^{2}-\mu+\varepsilon^{-1}\alpha)(t-s)}{\text{e}}^{(-4\pi^{2}|k|^{2}+\kappa)s}\textnormal{d}s(\mathcal{F}v_{0}).

By performing the integration in the latter expression we obtain

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}{\text{e}}^{(-4\pi^{2}|k|^{2}-\mu+\varepsilon^{-1}\alpha)(t-s)}{\text{e}}^{(-4\pi^{2}|k|^{2}+\kappa)s}\textnormal{d}s

\displaystyle={\text{e}}^{(-4\pi^{2}|k|^{2}-\mu+\varepsilon^{-1}\alpha)t}\int_{0}^{t}{\text{e}}^{(-\varepsilon^{-1}\alpha+\mu+\kappa)s}\textnormal{d}s

\displaystyle=\frac{{\text{e}}^{(-4\pi^{2}|k|^{2}-\mu+\varepsilon^{-1}\alpha)t}}{-\varepsilon^{-1}\alpha+\mu+\kappa}\left({\text{e}}^{(-\varepsilon^{-1}\alpha+\mu+\kappa)t}-1\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{{\text{e}}^{-4\pi^{2}|k|^{2}t}}{-\varepsilon^{-1}\alpha+\mu+\kappa}\left({\text{e}}^{\kappa t}-{\text{e}}^{(-\mu+\varepsilon^{-1}\alpha)t}\right).

Therefore, after an inverse Fourier transformation we obtain

\displaystyle u^{\varepsilon,0}(t)\!

\displaystyle={\text{e}}^{(-\mu+\varepsilon^{-1}\alpha)t}K_{n}(t)\!*\!u_{0}+\frac{\beta-\varepsilon\alpha^{-1}\beta(\mu+\kappa)}{-\alpha+\varepsilon\mu+\varepsilon\kappa}\left({\text{e}}^{\kappa t}-{\text{e}}^{(-\mu+\varepsilon^{-1}\alpha)t}\right)K_{n}(t)\!*\!v_{0}.

By similar calculations we can derive from equation (
2.5
) that

\tilde{u}^{\varepsilon}(t)={\text{e}}^{(-\mu+\varepsilon^{-1}\alpha)t}K_{n}(t)*u_{0}+\frac{\beta}{-\alpha+\varepsilon\mu+\varepsilon\kappa}\left({\text{e}}^{\kappa t}-{\text{e}}^{(-\mu+\varepsilon^{-1}\alpha)t}\right)K_{n}(t)*v_{0}.

Hence, using that we have
-\mu+\varepsilon^{-1}\alpha<\kappa
yields

\displaystyle\|u^{\varepsilon,0}(t)-\tilde{u}^{\varepsilon}(t)\|_{H^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}

\displaystyle=\frac{\varepsilon|-\alpha^{-1}\beta(\mu+\kappa)|}{|-\alpha+\varepsilon\mu+\varepsilon\kappa|}\left|{\text{e}}^{\kappa t}-{\text{e}}^{(-\mu+\varepsilon^{-1}\alpha)t}\right|\|v_{0}\|_{H^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}

\displaystyle\leq C\frac{\varepsilon|-\alpha^{-1}\beta(\mu+\kappa)|}{|-\alpha+\varepsilon\mu+\varepsilon\kappa|}{\text{e}}^{\kappa t}\|v_{0}\|_{H^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}

\displaystyle\leq\varepsilon C{\text{e}}^{\kappa t}\|v_{0}\|_{H^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}.

for some positive constant C.
∎

Proposition 2.7
.

Let
\varepsilon>0
be small enough such that
-\mu+\varepsilon^{-1}\alpha<0
. Then there exists a constant
C>0
such that

\|u^{\varepsilon,0}(t)-h^{0}(v^{0}(t))\|_{H^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\leq C{\text{e}}^{(-\mu+\varepsilon^{-1}\alpha)t}\|u_{0}-h^{0}(v_{0})\|_{H^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}.

Proof.

As in the previous proof we apply the variation of constant method along with the Fourier transform.
Hence, we rewrite the solution
u^{\varepsilon,0}
as

u^{\varepsilon,0}(t)={\text{e}}^{(-\mu+\varepsilon^{-1}\alpha)t}K_{n}(t)*u_{0}-\alpha^{-1}\beta\left({\text{e}}^{\kappa t}-{\text{e}}^{(-\mu+\varepsilon^{-1}\alpha)t}\right)K_{n}(t)*v_{0}

and obtain

\displaystyle\|u^{\varepsilon,0}(t)-h^{0}(v^{0}(t))\|_{H^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}=

\displaystyle=\|{\text{e}}^{(-\mu+\varepsilon^{-1}\alpha)t}(1+|k|^{2})K_{n}(t)\!*\!u_{0}-\alpha^{-1}\beta\left({\text{e}}^{\kappa t}-{\text{e}}^{(-\mu+\varepsilon^{-1}\alpha)t}\right)(1+|k|^{2})K_{n}(t)\!*\!v_{0}

\displaystyle\quad\quad+\alpha^{-1}\beta{\text{e}}^{\kappa t}(1+|k|^{2})K_{n}(t)*v_{0}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}

\displaystyle=\|{\text{e}}^{(-\mu+\varepsilon^{-1}\alpha)t}(1+|k|^{2})K_{n}(t)*\left[u_{0}+\alpha^{-1}\beta v_{0}\right]\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}

\displaystyle\leq C{\text{e}}^{(-\mu+\varepsilon^{-1}\alpha)t}\|u_{0}-h^{0}(v_{0})\|_{H^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}.

∎

Combining the three estimates from the previous propositions with
\varepsilon
small enough to fulfil the required conditions and
\alpha<0
yields for a
C>0

(2.7)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\|u^{\varepsilon}(t)-h^{0}(v^{0}(t))\|_{H^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}&\leq C\sup_{s\in[0,t]}\|v^{\varepsilon}(s)-v^{0}(s)\|_{H^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}+\varepsilon C{\text{e}}^{\kappa t}\|v_{0}\|_{H^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\\
&\quad+C{\text{e}}^{(-\mu+\varepsilon^{-1}\alpha)t}\|u_{0}-h^{0}(v_{0})\|_{H^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}.\end{split}

The next step in the proof is to estimate
\|v^{\varepsilon}(t)-v^{0}(t)\|_{H^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}
.
By variations of constants we obtain

(\mathcal{F}v^{\varepsilon})(t)={\text{e}}^{(-4\pi^{2}|k|^{2}-\nu)t}(\mathcal{F}v_{0})+\int_{0}^{t}{\text{e}}^{(-4\pi^{2}|k|^{2}-\nu)(t-s)}(\gamma(\mathcal{F}u^{\varepsilon})(s)+\delta(\mathcal{F}v^{\varepsilon})(s))\textnormal{d}s

and

(\mathcal{F}v^{0})(t)={\text{e}}^{(-4\pi^{2}|k|^{2}-\nu)t}(\mathcal{F}v_{0})+\int_{0}^{t}{\text{e}}^{(-4\pi^{2}|k|^{2}-\nu)(t-s)}(\delta-\gamma\alpha^{-1}\beta)(\mathcal{F}v^{0})(s)\textnormal{d}s.

Hence,

\displaystyle\|v^{\varepsilon}(t)-v^{0}(t)\|_{H^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{t}\bigg(\|{\text{e}}^{(-4\pi^{2}|k|^{2}-\nu)(t-s)}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}|\delta|\|v^{\varepsilon}(s)-v^{0}(s)\|_{H^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})})

\displaystyle+\|{\text{e}}^{(-4\pi^{2}|k|^{2}-\nu)(t-s)}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}(|\gamma|\|u^{\varepsilon}(s)-h^{0}(v^{0}(s))\|_{H^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\bigg)\textnormal{d}s.

Inserting the estimate for the fast variable (
2.7
) yields

\displaystyle\|v^{\varepsilon}(t)-v^{0}(t)\|_{H^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\leq

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{t}{\text{e}}^{-\nu(t-s)}\bigg(C{{\text{e}}^{(-\mu+\varepsilon^{-1}\alpha)s}\|u_{0}-h^{0}(v_{0})\|_{H^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}+\varepsilon C{\text{e}}^{\kappa s}\|v_{0}\|_{H^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}}

\displaystyle\qquad+|\delta|\|v^{\varepsilon}(s)-v^{0}(s)\|_{H^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}+C\sup_{r\in[0,s]}\|v^{\varepsilon}(r)-v^{0}(r)\|_{H^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\bigg)\textnormal{d}s.

Since the right-hand side of the inequality is increasing in time, we obtain

\displaystyle\sup_{s\in[0,t]}\|v^{\varepsilon}(s)-v^{0}(s)\|_{H^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}

\displaystyle\leq C\int_{0}^{t}{\text{e}}^{-\nu(t-s)}\big({{\text{e}}^{(-\mu+\varepsilon^{-1}\alpha)s}\|u_{0}-h^{0}(v_{0})\|_{H^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}+\varepsilon{\text{e}}^{\kappa s}\|v_{0}\|_{H^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}}\big)\textnormal{d}s

\displaystyle\quad+C\int_{0}^{t}{\text{e}}^{-\nu(t-s)}\sup_{r\in[0,s]}\|v^{\varepsilon}(r)-v^{0}(r)\|_{H^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\textnormal{d}s.

This expression can be further simplified to

\displaystyle\sup_{s\in[0,t]}\|v^{\varepsilon}(s)-v^{0}(s)\|_{H^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\leq

\displaystyle\leq C{\text{e}}^{-\nu t}\bigg(\|u_{0}-h^{0}(v_{0})\|_{H^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\frac{{\text{e}}^{(\varepsilon^{-1}\alpha-\mu+\nu)t}-1}{\varepsilon^{-1}\alpha-\mu+\nu}+\varepsilon\|v_{0}\|_{H^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\frac{{\text{e}}^{(-\alpha^{-1}\beta\gamma+\delta)t}-1}{-\alpha^{-1}\beta\gamma+\delta}\bigg)

\displaystyle\quad+C{\text{e}}^{-\nu t}\int_{0}^{t}{\text{e}}^{\nu s}\sup_{r\in[0,s]}\|v^{\varepsilon}(r)-v^{0}(r)\|_{H^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\textnormal{d}s.

This estimate is of the form

z(t)\leq A(t)+\int_{0}^{t}B(s)z(s)\textnormal{d}s.

Hence, applying Gronwall’s inequality yields

z(t)\leq A(t)+\int_{0}^{t}A(s)B(s)\exp\bigg(\int_{s}^{t}B(r)dr\bigg)\textnormal{d}s.

We observe that for
0\leq s\leq t

\displaystyle\exp\bigg(C\int_{s}^{t}{\text{e}}^{-\nu(t-r)}dr\bigg)=\exp C\nu^{-1}\big(1-{\text{e}}^{-\nu(t-s)}\big)\leq\exp C\nu^{-1}\big(1-{\text{e}}^{-\nu t}\big)\leq C_{\nu}(t),

where
C_{\nu}(t)=C_{\nu}\geq 0
is a constant if
\nu>0
and otherwise
C_{\nu}(t)
is a positive increasing function.
Therefore, we can estimate

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}A(s)B(s)\exp\bigg(\int_{s}^{t}B(r)dr\bigg)\textnormal{d}s\leq

\displaystyle\leq C_{\nu}(t)\int_{0}^{t}\|u_{0}-h^{0}(v_{0})\|_{H^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\frac{{\text{e}}^{(\varepsilon^{-1}\alpha-\mu)s}-{\text{e}}^{-\nu s}}{\varepsilon^{-1}\alpha-\mu+\nu}\textnormal{d}s

\displaystyle\quad+C_{\nu}(t)\int_{0}^{t}{\text{e}}^{-\nu s}\bigg(\varepsilon\|v_{0}\|_{H^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\frac{{\text{e}}^{(-\alpha^{-1}\beta\gamma+\delta)s}-1}{-\alpha^{-1}\beta\gamma+\delta}\bigg)\textnormal{d}s

\displaystyle\leq I_{1}+I_{2}.

Evaluating each integral yields

\displaystyle I_{1}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\varepsilon C_{\nu}(t)}{|\alpha-\varepsilon\mu+\varepsilon\nu|}\|u_{0}-h^{0}(v_{0})\|_{H^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\bigg(\frac{1-{\text{e}}^{(\varepsilon^{-1}\alpha-\mu)t}}{\mu-\varepsilon^{-1}\alpha}+\frac{{\text{e}}^{-\nu t}-1}{\nu}\bigg)

and

\displaystyle I_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\varepsilon C_{\nu}(t)}{|-\alpha^{-1}\beta\gamma+\delta|}\|v_{0}\|_{H^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\bigg(\frac{{\text{e}}^{\kappa t}-1}{\kappa}+\frac{{\text{e}}^{-\nu t}-1}{\nu}\bigg).

Hence we obtain

\displaystyle\sup_{s\in[0,t]}\|v^{\varepsilon}(s)-v^{0}(s)\|_{H^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\leq

\displaystyle\leq\varepsilon C_{\nu}(t)\|u_{0}-h^{0}(v_{0})\|_{H^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\bigg(\frac{{\text{e}}^{(\varepsilon^{-1}\alpha-\mu+\nu)t}-{\text{e}}^{-\nu t}}{\alpha-\varepsilon\mu+\varepsilon\nu}+\frac{1-{\text{e}}^{(\varepsilon^{-1}\alpha-\mu)t}}{\mu-\varepsilon^{-1}\alpha}+\frac{{\text{e}}^{-\nu t}-1}{\nu}\bigg)

\displaystyle\quad+\varepsilon C_{\nu}(t)\|v_{0}\|_{H^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\bigg(\frac{{\text{e}}^{\kappa t}-{\text{e}}^{-\nu t}}{-\alpha^{-1}\beta\gamma+\delta}+\frac{{\text{e}}^{\kappa t}-1}{\kappa}+\frac{{\text{e}}^{-\nu t}-1}{\nu}\bigg).

Again depending on the sign of
\nu
and
\kappa
we can write this estimate as

\displaystyle\sup_{s\in[0,t]}\|v^{\varepsilon}(s)-v^{0}(s)\|_{H^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\leq\varepsilon C_{\nu,\kappa}(t)\big(\|u_{0}-h^{0}(v_{0})\|_{H^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}+\|v_{0}\|_{H^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\big),

where
C_{\nu,\kappa}(t)
is a positive increasing function.
In the case that
nu>0
and
\kappa<0
we have
C_{\nu,\kappa}(t)=C
.
Moreover, we observe that the right hand side is increasing in time and thus

\displaystyle\|v^{\varepsilon}(t)-v^{0}(t)\|_{H^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\leq\varepsilon C_{\nu,\kappa}(t)\big(\|u_{0}-h^{0}(v_{0})\|_{H^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}+\|v_{0}\|_{H^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\big).

Plugging this into the estimate for the fast variable (
2.7
) yields

\displaystyle\|u^{\varepsilon}(t)-h^{0}(v^{0}(t))\|_{H^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\leq

\displaystyle\leq\varepsilon C_{\nu,\kappa}\bigg(\|u_{0}-h^{0}(v_{0})\|_{H^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}+\|v_{0}\|_{H^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\bigg)+{\text{e}}^{(\varepsilon^{-1}\alpha-\mu)t}\|u_{0}-h^{0}(v_{0})\|_{H^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}.

The final step in the proof is to combine the two estimates, resulting in

\displaystyle\left\|T_{\varepsilon}(t)\begin{pmatrix}u_{0}\\
v_{0}\end{pmatrix}-T_{0}(t)\begin{pmatrix}h^{0}(v_{0})\\
v_{0}\end{pmatrix}\right\|_{H^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\times H^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\leq

\displaystyle\leq\varepsilon C_{\nu,\kappa}\bigg(\big(1+\varepsilon^{-1}{\text{e}}^{(\varepsilon^{-1}\alpha-\mu)t}\big)\|u_{0}-h^{0}(v_{0})\|_{H^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}+\|v_{0}\|_{H^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\bigg),

for
t>0
.
This completes the proof of Theorem
2.4
.

3.
Dynamics of a linear fast-reaction system

As mentioned in the introduction finite dimensional fast-slow systems tend to have, under certain hyperbolicity assumptions, so called slow manifolds.
These slow manifolds are a family
S_{\varepsilon}
of locally invariant manifolds that converge to the critical manifold as
\varepsilon\to 0
with respect to the Hausdorff-distance, while also preserving the regularity. Moreover, the flow on those manifolds converges to the flow of the limit system as
\varepsilon\to 0
. This means that the existence of slow manifolds imply that the limit system is a reasonable good approximation of the whole system near the critical manifold.

It is therefore desirable to also have slow manifolds in the case of infinite dimensional systems. However, one should be reminded that infinite dimensional manifolds are much more difficult to handle.

In this section we show that slow manifolds can be obtained for the linear fast-reaction system (
2.1
) and provide an explicit formula. This means that the results from finite dimensional fast-slow systems generalize to the infinite dimensional linear fast-reaction systems.

The first step of adapting the Fenichel-Tikhonov theory to this linear fast-reaction system is to construct the invariant slow manifold of the system.
To bridge the gap between the finite-dimensional theory and its infinite-dimensional extension, we apply the Fourier transform to the linear system (
2.1
) and construct the slow manifolds for each Fourier coefficient
k\in\mathbb{R}
.
Then, the following lemma holds.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
\alpha<0
. Then, for all
\varepsilon>0
small enough satisfying
\varepsilon^{-1}\alpha-\mu<0
the slow manifolds of system (
2.3
) are given by

(3.1)

C^{\mathcal{F}}_{\varepsilon}:=\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:(\varepsilon(\delta-\nu+\mu)-\alpha-\varepsilon\Omega^{\varepsilon})x-2\beta y=0\},

where
\Omega^{\varepsilon}:=\sqrt{(\delta-\nu-\varepsilon^{-1}\alpha+\mu)^{2}+4\varepsilon^{-1}\beta\gamma}\in\mathbb{R}
.

Proof.

Recall that after applying a Fourier transform to system (
2.1
) we can write the system as

(3.2)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}w_{k}^{\varepsilon}(t)=M_{k}^{\varepsilon}w_{k}^{\varepsilon}(t),

where

\displaystyle M_{k}^{\varepsilon}:=\begin{pmatrix}\varepsilon^{-1}\alpha-\mu-4\pi^{2}|k|^{2}&\varepsilon^{-1}\beta\\
\gamma&\delta-\nu-4\pi^{2}|k|^{2}\\
\end{pmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2}.

The existence of slow manifolds follows directly from the theory for fast-slow ODEs, since in this case we have that the normal hyperbolicity condition is fulfilled since
\varepsilon^{-1}\alpha-\mu<0
.
Moreover, we can explicitly construct the slow manifolds by computing the eigenspaces corresponding to the linear ODE system.
Since system (
3.2
) is linear and finite-dimensional, the only locally invariant manifolds are submanifolds of the two eigenspaces
E^{\varepsilon}_{+}
and
E^{\varepsilon}_{-}
of
M^{\varepsilon}_{k}
.
As we have seen in the proof of Lemma
2.2
, for
\varepsilon
small enough there exist two non identical eigenspaces of
M_{k}^{\varepsilon}
for almost every
k
.
One possible representation of basis vectors
w^{\varepsilon}_{+}
and
w^{\varepsilon}_{-}
such that
E^{\varepsilon}_{+}=\text{span}\{w^{\varepsilon}_{+}\}
and
E^{\varepsilon}_{-}=\text{span}\{w^{\varepsilon}_{-}\}
is given by

w^{\varepsilon}_{\pm}=\begin{pmatrix}2\varepsilon^{-1}\beta\\
\delta-\nu-\varepsilon^{-1}\alpha+\mu\pm\Omega^{\varepsilon}\end{pmatrix},

where
\Omega^{\varepsilon}:=\sqrt{(\delta-\nu-\varepsilon^{-1}\alpha+\mu)^{2}+4\varepsilon^{-1}\beta\gamma}
.
We recall that the corresponding eigenvalues are given by

\lambda^{\varepsilon}_{\pm}(k):=\frac{1}{2}\left(\varepsilon^{-1}\alpha-\mu+\delta-\nu-8\pi^{2}|k|^{2}\mp\Omega^{\varepsilon}\right)\in\mathbb{R}.

From this we deduce that the attracting invariant slow manifold is given by
w^{\varepsilon}_{-}
.
∎

Since the eigenvectors completely determine the behaviour of system (
3.2
) we desire to compute
\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}w^{\varepsilon}_{\pm}
.
In order for the eigenspaces to maintain a finite basis, even in the limit as
\varepsilon\to 0
we scale
w^{\varepsilon}_{\pm}
with
\varepsilon
.
Now, we can take the limit and obtain

\displaystyle\varepsilon w^{\varepsilon}_{\pm}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}2\beta\\
\varepsilon\delta-\alpha\pm\varepsilon\Omega^{\varepsilon}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}2\beta\\
\varepsilon(\delta-\nu+\mu)-\alpha\pm\sqrt{(\varepsilon\delta-\varepsilon\nu+\varepsilon\mu-\alpha)^{2}+4\varepsilon\beta\gamma}\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle\to\begin{pmatrix}2\beta\\
-\alpha\pm|\alpha|\end{pmatrix}:=w_{\pm}^{0}

as
\varepsilon\to 0
.
That is we obtain

w_{-}^{0}=\begin{pmatrix}2\beta\\
-2\alpha\end{pmatrix},\quad w_{+}^{0}=\begin{pmatrix}2\beta\\
0\end{pmatrix}.

Thus,
w^{0}_{-}
spans a linear subspace corresponding to

C_{0}^{\mathcal{F}}:=\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:\alpha x+\beta y=0\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{2},

which is the critical manifold of system (
3.2
) in the limit as
\varepsilon\to 0
.
We have therefore found a family of invariant manifolds

C^{\mathcal{F}}_{\varepsilon}:=E_{+}^{\varepsilon}
, which is one eigenspace of
M_{k}^{\varepsilon}
, such that
C^{\mathcal{F}}_{\varepsilon}\to C^{\mathcal{F}}_{0}
for
\varepsilon\to 0
for almost every
k
.

This exact finite dimensional relation can be shown to also hold infinite dimensions.
Due to the identical structure of the solutions of (
2.1
) and (
2.3
) these systems have the same linear invariant manifolds.
We summarize this fact in the following lemma.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
a,b\in\mathbb{R}\backslash\{0\}
,
H:=H^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
and
\varepsilon
small enough such that
\Omega^{\varepsilon}\in\mathbb{R}\backslash\{0\}
. Then, the following two statements are equivalent.

(1)

There exists an invariant manifold, called the slow manifold,
S_{\varepsilon}\subset H\times H
to system (
2.1
);

(2)

C^{\mathcal{F}}_{\varepsilon}:=\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:(\varepsilon(\delta-\nu+\mu)-\alpha-\varepsilon\Omega^{\varepsilon})x-2\beta y=0\}
is an invariant manifold of system (
2.3
) for almost every
k\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

Proof.

Note that
(0,0)
is a steady state in both systems. We can therefore exclude it in what follows, since we are only interested in nontrivial invariant manifolds. We only show that (2) implies (1), but the other direction works exactly the same.

Let
w:=(u,v)\in C^{\mathcal{F}}_{\varepsilon}\backslash\{0\}
and choose any
k\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that we can construct the explicit solution via Fourier transforms. Denote
\phi^{t}_{k}(u,v)
as the flow of this system (
3.2
). Due to invariance, we have that
\phi_{k}^{t}(u,v)\in C^{\mathcal{F}}_{\varepsilon}
for all
t>0
. Expressed in formulas this means

\displaystyle a[(\rho^{\varepsilon}_{1}{\text{e}}^{\lambda^{\varepsilon}_{+}(k)t}+\rho^{\varepsilon}_{2}{\text{e}}^{\lambda^{\varepsilon}_{-}(k)t})u+(\rho_{3}^{\varepsilon}{\text{e}}^{\lambda^{\varepsilon}_{+}(k)t}+\rho^{\varepsilon}_{4}{\text{e}}^{\lambda^{\varepsilon}_{-}t(k)})v]

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle b[(\rho_{5}^{\varepsilon}{\text{e}}^{\lambda_{+}^{\varepsilon}(k)t}+\rho_{6}^{\varepsilon}{\text{e}}^{\lambda_{-}^{\varepsilon}(k)}t)u+(\rho_{7}^{\varepsilon}{\text{e}}^{\lambda^{\varepsilon}_{+}(k)t}+\rho^{\varepsilon}_{8}{\text{e}}^{\lambda^{\varepsilon}_{-}(k)t})v]=0

for all
t>0
. Since
\lambda_{+}(k)\neq\lambda_{-}(k)
it follows by equating coefficients that

\displaystyle(a\rho^{\varepsilon}_{1}+b\rho^{\varepsilon}_{5})u+(a\rho^{\varepsilon}_{3}+b\rho^{\varepsilon}_{7})v=(a\rho^{\varepsilon}_{2}+b\rho^{\varepsilon}_{6})u+(a\rho^{\varepsilon}_{4}+b\rho^{\varepsilon}_{8})v=0.

Since we have
u=-\frac{b}{a}v
, so we can also write the above equation as

\displaystyle\left(-b\rho^{\varepsilon}_{1}-\frac{b^{2}}{a}\rho^{\varepsilon}_{5}+a\rho^{\varepsilon}_{3}+b\rho^{\varepsilon}_{7}\right)v=\left(-b\rho^{\varepsilon}_{2}-\frac{b^{2}}{a}\rho^{\varepsilon}_{6}+a\rho^{\varepsilon}_{4}+b\rho^{\varepsilon}_{8}\right)v=0.

Since
w\neq(0,0)
we can assume
v\neq 0
. So we can deduce from the last equation that

\displaystyle\left(-b\rho^{\varepsilon}_{1}-\frac{b^{2}}{a}\rho^{\varepsilon}_{5}+a\rho^{\varepsilon}_{2}+b\rho^{\varepsilon}_{7}\right)=\left(-b\rho^{\varepsilon}_{2}-\frac{b^{2}}{a}\rho^{\varepsilon}_{6}+a\rho^{\varepsilon}_{4}+b\rho^{\varepsilon}_{8}\right)=0.

Therefore, for any
\tilde{v}\in H^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
we also have

\displaystyle\left(-b\rho^{\varepsilon}_{1}-\frac{b^{2}}{a}\rho^{\varepsilon}_{5}+a\rho^{\varepsilon}_{3}+b\rho^{\varepsilon}_{7}\right)*\tilde{v}=\left(-b\rho^{\varepsilon}_{2}-\frac{b^{2}}{a}\rho^{\varepsilon}_{6}+a\rho^{\varepsilon}_{4}+b\rho^{\varepsilon}_{8}\right)*\tilde{v}=0

and by defining
\tilde{u}:=-\frac{b}{a}\tilde{v}\in H^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
we can follow

\displaystyle(a\rho^{\varepsilon}_{1}+b\rho^{\varepsilon}_{5})*\tilde{u}+(a\rho^{\varepsilon}_{3}+b\rho^{\varepsilon}_{7})*\tilde{v}=(a\rho^{\varepsilon}_{2}+b\rho^{\varepsilon}_{6})*\tilde{u}+(a\rho^{\varepsilon}_{4}+b\rho^{\varepsilon}_{8})*\tilde{v}=0

for all
(\tilde{u},\tilde{v})\in S_{0}
. From this it follows that

\displaystyle a[(\rho^{\varepsilon}_{1}L^{\varepsilon}_{+}(x,t)+\rho^{\varepsilon}_{2}L^{\varepsilon}_{-}(x,t))*\tilde{u}+(\rho_{3}^{\varepsilon}L^{\varepsilon}_{+}(x,t)+\rho^{\varepsilon}_{4}L^{\varepsilon}_{-}(x,t))*\tilde{v}]

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle b[(\rho_{5}^{\varepsilon}L^{\varepsilon}_{+}(x,t)+\rho_{6}^{\varepsilon}L^{\varepsilon}_{-}(x,t)*\tilde{u}+(\rho_{7}^{\varepsilon}L^{\varepsilon}_{+}(x,t)+\rho^{\varepsilon}_{8}L^{\varepsilon}_{-}(x,t))*\tilde{v}]=0

for all
t>0
. So we have shown that
S_{\varepsilon}
is invariant.
∎

Similarly, we obtain that
C_{0}:=\{(u,v)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:au+bv=0\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
and
S_{0}:=\{(u,v)\in H^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\times H^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n}):au+bv=0\}\subset H\times H
are equivalent in the above sense.
In addition, from the above lemma it can be concluded that two maximal invariant manifolds of system (
2.1
) are given by

C^{\pm}_{\varepsilon}:=\{(u,v)\in H\times H:(\varepsilon\delta-\alpha\pm\varepsilon\Omega^{\varepsilon})u-2\beta v=0\}.

As in the finite dimensional case
C_{\varepsilon}^{-}
converges to the critical attracting manifold of the linear fast-reaction system (
2.1
) as
\varepsilon\to 0
if
\alpha<0
.
This along with the other properties of slow manifolds can be summarized in the following result which is an extension of the classical Fenichel-Tikhonov theory.

Theorem 3.3
.

Let
\alpha<0
and
\varepsilon
small enough such that
\varepsilon^{-1}\alpha-\mu<0
and
\Omega^{\varepsilon}\in\mathbb{R}\backslash\{0\}
. Then the following is true

i)

C^{-}_{\varepsilon}
is the attracting slow manifold of the linear fast-reaction system (
2.1
) which is invariant under the semi flow
T_{\varepsilon}
. Moreover, the slow manifold has the same regularity as the critical manifold;

ii)

The slow manifold
C^{-}_{\varepsilon}
converges to the critical manifold
C_{0}
as
\varepsilon\to 0
. In addition, on bounded subsets the distance between these two objects is given by
d_{H}(C^{-}_{\varepsilon},C_{0})=\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon)
with
d_{H}
being the Hausdorff-distance;

iii)

The semi flow
T_{\varepsilon}
restricted to
C^{-}_{\varepsilon}
converges to the semi flow
T_{0}
on
C_{0}
as
\varepsilon\to 0
.

Proof.

i) The invariance follows from Lemma
3.2
and regularity properties holds due to the linearity of the manifolds.

ii) Since
\varepsilon\delta-\alpha-\varepsilon\Omega^{\varepsilon}\to-2\alpha
as
\varepsilon\to 0
, the equation describing the attracting slow manifold
C_{\varepsilon}^{-}
converges to the equation describing
C_{0}
.
Next, we define the Hausdorff distance as

d_{H}(C_{0},C_{\varepsilon}^{-}):=\inf\{{\lambda\geq 0}:C_{0}\subset C_{\varepsilon}^{-}+B_{\lambda}(0),C_{\varepsilon}^{-}\subset C_{0}+B_{\lambda}(0)\}.

Here
+
denotes the Minkowski sum between sets.
That is for
U,V\subset H^{2}(R^{n})
we define

U+V:=\{u+v:u\in U,v\in V\}\subset H^{2}(R^{n}).

We first want to show
C_{\varepsilon}^{-}\subset C_{0}+B_{\lambda}(0)
.
To this end, let
w^{\varepsilon}=(u,v^{\varepsilon})\in C_{\varepsilon}^{-}
such that
\|u\|_{H^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\leq M
. Due to the convergence of
C^{-}_{\varepsilon}\to C_{0}
, we can find
\tilde{\varepsilon}>0
such that
|\frac{\tilde{\varepsilon}\delta-\alpha-\tilde{\varepsilon}\Omega^{\tilde{\varepsilon}}}{2\beta}+\frac{\alpha}{\beta}|<\varepsilon^{2}
.
Let
w^{0}=(u,-\frac{\beta}{\alpha}u)\in C_{0}
.
Since we consider an element on the slow manifold we have
v^{\varepsilon}=\frac{\tilde{\varepsilon}\delta-\alpha-\tilde{\varepsilon}\Omega^{\tilde{\varepsilon}}}{2\beta}u
and thus we can compute

\displaystyle\|w^{\varepsilon}-w^{0}\|^{2}_{H^{2}(R^{n})\times H^{2}(R^{n})}:

\displaystyle=\|u-u\|^{2}_{H^{2}(R^{n})}+\|-\frac{\beta}{\alpha}u-v^{\varepsilon}\|^{2}_{H^{2}(R^{n})}

\displaystyle=\left|\frac{\tilde{\varepsilon}\delta-\alpha-\tilde{\varepsilon}\Omega^{\tilde{\varepsilon}}}{2\beta}+\frac{\alpha}{\beta}\right|\|u\|^{2}_{H^{2}(R^{n})}

\displaystyle<\varepsilon^{2}\|u\|^{2}_{H^{2}(R^{n})}\leq\varepsilon^{2}M^{2}.

Hence we have shown that
C_{0}\subset C_{\varepsilon}^{-}+B_{\lambda}(0)
for
\lambda=\varepsilon M=\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon)
.
The other inclusion can be shown in a similar way.

iii) If
(u_{0},v_{0})\in C_{\varepsilon}^{-}
we know that the semi flow
T_{\varepsilon}(t)(u_{0},v_{0})\in C_{\varepsilon}^{-}
for all
t>0
due to i). Therefore, we can eliminate
u^{\varepsilon}
in the second equation of system (
2.1
) and obtain

\displaystyle\partial_{t}v^{\varepsilon}(t)=(\Delta-\nu I)v^{\varepsilon}(t)+\bigg(\gamma\frac{2\beta}{\varepsilon\delta-\alpha-\varepsilon\Omega^{\varepsilon}}+\delta\bigg)v^{\varepsilon}(t).

Passing to the limit we obtain
\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}v^{\varepsilon}=v^{0}
, where
v^{0}
is the second component of the semi flow
T_{0}
. Then,

u^{\varepsilon}=\frac{2\beta}{\varepsilon\delta-\alpha-\varepsilon\Omega^{\varepsilon}}v^{\varepsilon}\to-\frac{\beta}{\alpha}v^{0}=h^{0}(v^{0})\text{ as }\varepsilon\to 0.

∎

4.
Conclusion

The first observation we make is that the general results presented in
[
19
]
also apply in this linear fast-reaction setting.
However, the advantage of this work is that the slow manifold is explicitly constructed and the convergence to the critical manifold only depends on the separation of time scales parameter
\varepsilon
but not on any spectral gap type parameter.
Moreover, the results presented here hold for any dimension
N\in\mathbb{N}
and can also be applied to bounded domains with either zero Dirichlet or periodic boundary conditions.

Secondly, we want to compare the normal hyperbolicity assumptions used in this work.
When working on the level of Fourier transforms the PDE system turns into a two dimensional ODE in each of the Fourier coefficients.
Here, we can apply the normal hyperbolicity condition that the eigenvalues of the linearized matrix in the fast direction have a non-zero real part.
After taking the inverse Fourier transform, this translates to the normal hyperbolicity condition in the abstract Banach space setting, presented in
[
19
]
.

Thirdly, we comment on the influence of the parameter
\alpha
.
For
\alpha<0
the critical manifold is attracting normally hyperbolic and so is the slow manifold.
In addition, the convergence of solutions and the convergence of the semi flow on the manifolds holds for any
t\in(0,T)
for any
T>0
.
Now, since this is a linear system, the results would also hold for
\alpha>0
leading to a repelling slow manifold.

Lastly, note carefully that we could only disregard the loss of normal hyperbolicity in the Fourier domain for some modes
k
in our proofs as the measure of these modes is zero so it is not visible for the Fourier inversion. For nonlinear systems with a spectral gap, a similar mechanism is making the existence of invariant manifolds possible
[
19
]
. Yet, if the failure of formal hyperbolicity occurs on a set of positive measure in the Fourier domain, we conjecture that it is no longer possible by standard methods to derive invariant manifolds. In particular, this emphasizes the role of a nonlinearity in contrast to the expository linear case we have discussed here: the nonlinearity provides a possibility for mixing of the Fourier modes so that a positive measure set in the Fourier domain lacks normal hyperbolicity.
