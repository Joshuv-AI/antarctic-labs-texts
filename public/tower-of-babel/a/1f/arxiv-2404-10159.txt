Abstract
. Discontinuous Galerkin (DG) methods are widely adopted to discretize the radiation transport equation (RTE) with diffusive scalings. One of the most important advantages of the DG methods for RTE is their asymptotic preserving (AP) property, in the sense that they preserve the diffusive limits of the equation in the discrete setting, without requiring excessive refinement of the discretization. However, compared to finite element methods or finite volume methods, the employment of DG methods also increases the number of unknowns, which requires more memory and computational time to solve the problems. In this paper, when the spherical harmonic method is applied for the angular discretization, we perform an asymptotic analysis which shows that to retain the uniform convergence, it is only necessary to employ non-constant elements for the degree zero moment only in the DG spatial discretization. Based on this observation, we propose a heterogeneous DG method that employs polynomial spaces of different degrees for the degree zero and remaining moments respectively. To improve the convergence order, we further develop a spherical harmonics hybrid DG finite volume method, which preserves the AP property and convergence rate while tremendously reducing the number of unknowns. Numerical examples are provided to illustrate the effectiveness and accuracy of the proposed scheme.

1.
Introduction

The radiation transport equation (RTE) is a kinetic model that describes the flow of radiation through a material medium and plays an important role in a wide range of applications involving neutron
[
9
]
, photon
[
35
]
, and neutrino
[
32
]
transport processes.
The RTE is an integro-differential equation that is numerically challenging to solve due to its high-dimensional space and the multiscale nature of the solution induced by variations in the material medium. In particular, the nature of the RTE is hyperbolic when scattering is minimal and diffusive when scattering is very prominent. In cases where the scattering is prominent throughout the spatial domain of a particular problem, a diffusive approximation
[
24
,
4
]
can be used to approximate the particle density at a significantly reduced computational cost.
However, in true multiscale problems, the scattering may vary by orders of magnitude, requiring the use of the kinetic description provided by the RTE. Unfortunately, standard finite difference and finite volume (FV) methods designed for hyperbolic problems can become computationally expensive in scattering-dominated regimes. For stability and accuracy reasons, they must resolve the mean free path between particle collisions with the material. Thus care must be taken to construct numerical methods which accurately capture the asymptotic diffusion equation that arises in the limit of infinite scattering
[
26
,
25
,
28
,
23
,
18
]
. Such methods are often referred to as asymptotic preserving (AP)
[
22
,
21
]
.

The discretization of the RTE is usually carried out separately for the angular, spatial, and temporal variables. In this paper, we focus on the spherical harmonics (
P_{N}
) method for angular discretization. The
P_{N}
method
[
7
]
is one of the most widely employed angular discretizations due to its robustness, accuracy, and computational efficiency. It is a spectral method in the angle that seeks an approximate solution as a sum of spherical harmonics basis functions with coefficients that depend on space (and time in the time-dependent setting). These coefficients correspond to moments of the RTE; thus the
P_{N}
equations can be viewed as a type of moment model
[
13
]
. A major advantage of the
P_{N}
method is that it diagonalizes any scattering operator with a rotationally invariant kernel
[
27
]
, thereby reducing the cost of evaluating the operator. In addition, the method exhibits spectral convergence for smooth problems
[
12
]
, and preserves rotational invariance properties of the RTE
[
14
]
. Moreover, for steady-state problems, the
P_{1}
(
N=1
) method is equivalent to the first-order form of the diffusion limit, which makes the asymptotic analysis relatively simple.

For spatial discretization, we focus on discontinuous Galerkin (DG) finite element methods, which become a popular choice in solving the RTE due to their robustness, flexibility, and high accuracy. Importantly, with sufficiently rich spaces, these methods recover the interior diffusion limit
1
1

1

Here the
interior diffusion limit
refers to the correct interior discretization. To fully recover the limit, correct boundary conditions are also required. Boundary conditions are not addressed here.

of the RTE
[
25
,
1
,
15
,
30
]
. Roughly speaking, the necessary requirement for DG methods with upwind fluxes to achieve such property is that the finite element spaces support global linear polynomials. This requirement translates to local
\mathbb{P}_{1}
and
\mathbb{Q}_{1}
approximations for triangular and rectangular elements, respectively. The
\mathbb{Q}_{1}
requirement can be prohibitive, particularly in three dimensions where the added cost over an FV method increases by a factor of eight. As a consequence, while the AP property of the DG method is desirable, it comes at the expense of higher computational costs, including increased memory demands and longer computing times.

Inspired by the analysis in
[
31
,
15
]
, we propose a
P_{N}
-DG method with heterogeneous polynomial spaces for different moments that maintains the AP property of the DG method while significantly reducing the number of unknowns. Specifically, only the degree zero moment requires a
\mathbb{Q}_{1}
approximation in space, while the remaining moments can be approximated in space using piece-wise constants. As a result, the computational cost becomes comparable to that of an FV method. However, even though this method captures the interior diffusion limit, it is generally first-order in space. To alleviate this limitation, we propose a hybrid approach that combines the DG method with
\mathbb{Q}_{1}
elements for the degree zero moment and a second-order FV method for the remaining moments. Numerical experiments confirm that this hybrid method successfully captures the interior diffusion limit and achieves uniform second-order accuracy.

The concept of employing different spatial discretizations for different components of the RTE has been explored in several other contexts. In
[
6
]
, the
P_{N}
equations were split at the continuum level into a diffusive (
P_{1}
) and non-diffusive part; each part was then discretized with a different FV method. In
[
38
]
, a discrete ordinate DG (
S_{N}
-DG) method was proposed in which each ordinate is assigned a unique average but shares a common slope. In
[
20
,
19
]
, the RTE is separated into collided and uncollided components, and different discretizations are employed for the angular and spatial variables of each component.

2.
Preliminaries and problem setting

Throughout the paper,
we adopt the conventional notation
H^{r}(D)
to indicate Sobolev spaces on (possibly lower-dimensional) subdomain
D\subset X
with the norm
\|\cdot\|_{r,D}
. Clearly,
H^{0}(D)=L^{2}(D)
, and this norm is denoted by
\|\cdot\|_{D}
.

2.1.
Scaled radiative transfer/transport equations

Let
X=[0,1)^{3}
be the unit torus in three dimensions, and let
\mathbb{S}
be the unit sphere in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
.
We consider the following scaled versions of the steady-state and time-dependent RTEs with periodic boundary conditions for the unknown angular flux
u=u(\mathbf{x},\bm{\omega})
and
\psi=\psi(t,\mathbf{x},\bm{\omega})
, respectively:

(2.1)

\displaystyle\bm{\omega}\cdot\nabla u(\mathbf{x},\bm{\omega})+\frac{\sigma_{\mathrm{t}}}{\varepsilon}u(\mathbf{x},\bm{\omega})

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{\sigma_{\mathrm{t}}}{\varepsilon}-\varepsilon\sigma_{\mathrm{a}}\right)\left\langle u\right\rangle(\mathbf{x})+\varepsilon f,

\displaystyle\quad(\mathbf{x},\bm{\omega})\in X\times\mathbb{S},

and

\displaystyle\varepsilon\partial_{t}\psi(t,\mathbf{x},\bm{\omega})+\bm{\omega}\cdot\nabla\psi(t,\mathbf{x},\bm{\omega})

\displaystyle+\frac{\sigma_{\mathrm{t}}}{\varepsilon}\psi(t,\mathbf{x},\bm{\omega})

(2.2a)

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{\sigma_{\mathrm{t}}}{\varepsilon}-\varepsilon\sigma_{\mathrm{a}}\right)\left\langle\psi\right\rangle(t,\mathbf{x})+\varepsilon f,

\displaystyle\quad(\mathbf{x},\bm{\omega})\in X\times\mathbb{S},t>0

(2.2b)

\displaystyle\psi(0,\mathbf{x},{\bm{\omega}})

\displaystyle=\psi_{0}(\mathbf{x},\bm{\omega}),

\displaystyle\quad(\mathbf{x},{\bm{\omega}})\in X\times\mathbb{S},

where
\left\langle v\right\rangle=\int_{\mathbb{S}}v\,\mathrm{d}{\bm{\omega}}\,/\int_{\mathbb{S}}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{\omega}
is the average of
v
over
\mathbb{S}

and
\int_{\mathbb{S}}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{\omega}=4\pi
.
The functions
\left\langle u\right\rangle
and
\left\langle\psi\right\rangle
are called the scalar flux.

The parameter
\varepsilon
is a scaling constant that characterizes the amount of scattering in the problem. For simplicity, we assume
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
. The solutions
u
and
\psi
of (
2.1
) and (
2.2
), respectively, depend on
\varepsilon
. However, to reduce the complexity of the notations,
u
(or
\psi
) instead of
u^{\varepsilon}
(or
\psi^{\varepsilon}
) will be employed except in
Section

3
.

The functions
\sigma_{\mathrm{a}}=\sigma_{\mathrm{a}}(\mathbf{x})
and
\sigma_{\mathrm{t}}=\sigma_{\mathrm{t}}(\mathbf{x})
are (known) macroscopic absorption and total cross sections, respectively, and
f=f(\mathbf{x},\bm{\omega})
is a (known) source.
The scaled scattering cross-section
\sigma_{\mathrm{s},\varepsilon}
takes the form

(2.3)

\sigma_{\mathrm{s},\varepsilon}=\sigma_{\mathrm{t}}-\varepsilon^{2}\sigma_{\mathrm{a}}.

Here and below, a function is said to be isotropic if it is constant with respect to the angular variable
\bm{\omega}
.

We make the following assumptions:

(2.4a)

\displaystyle\sigma_{\mathrm{t}},\sigma_{\mathrm{a}}\in L^{\infty}(X),

(2.4b)

\displaystyle\sigma_{\mathrm{s},\varepsilon}=\sigma_{\mathrm{t}}-\varepsilon^{2}\sigma_{\mathrm{a}}\geq\sigma_{\mathrm{s}}^{\mathrm{min}}\text{ in }\mathbf{x}\in X\text{ for a constant }\sigma_{\mathrm{s}}^{\mathrm{min}}>0,

(2.4c)

\displaystyle\sigma_{\mathrm{a}}\geq\sigma_{\mathrm{a}}^{\mathrm{min}}\text{ in }X\text{ for a constant }\sigma_{\mathrm{a}}^{\mathrm{min}}>0,

(2.4d)

\displaystyle f(\mathbf{x},\bm{\omega})\in L^{2}(X\times\mathbb{S}).

The condition (
2.4c
), while not strictly necessary, is often used in a priori estimates.

The limit
\varepsilon\rightarrow 0
corresponds to the ratio of the mean free path of the particles to the diameter of
X
going to zero. In this regime, the media becomes optically thick and is dominated by scattering.
It can be shown that with isotropic initial conditions, the solutions
u
and
\psi
in the diffusion limit (i.e., when
\varepsilon\rightarrow 0
) satisfy the following diffusion equations, respectively
[
24
,
16
,
4
]
:

(2.5)

\displaystyle\nabla\cdot\left(\frac{1}{3\sigma_{\mathrm{t}}(\mathbf{x})}\nabla u(\mathbf{x})\right)+\sigma_{\mathrm{a}}(\mathbf{x})u(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=\langle f\rangle(\mathbf{x}),\quad\hskip 8.5359pt\mathbf{x}\in X,

and

(2.6a)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\psi(t,\mathbf{x})+\nabla\cdot\left(\frac{1}{3\sigma_{\mathrm{t}}(\mathbf{x})}\nabla\psi(t,\mathbf{x})\right)+\sigma_{\mathrm{a}}(\mathbf{x})\psi(t,\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=\langle f\rangle(\mathbf{x}),\;(t,\mathbf{x})\in[0,T]\times X,

(2.6b)

\displaystyle\psi(0,\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=\psi_{0}(\mathbf{x}),\quad\mathbf{x}\in X.

In the current work, we focus on periodic domains which can be easily treated in a variational framework
[
10
,
29
]
. More general boundary conditions are also possible but may be computationally inefficient due to the evaluation of half-space integrals that lead to dense matrices during assembly. An alternative approach
[
11
,
37
]
based on perfectly matched layers introduces a small modification that results in a negligible error in consistency but improves the sparsity of the matrix representation. With this fact in mind, we focus only on the periodic boundary conditions, although the challenge of recovering at the numerical level the correct boundary condition in the diffusion limit remains open.

2.2.
Equations in reduced geometry

While the analysis here is often applied to the full 3-D setting, the numerical results in
Section

5
are applied to reduced models with certain symmetries: a
1
-D slab geometry model and a
2
-D plane-parallel model. These (steady-state) reduced models
[
2
,
33
]
are briefly introduced here. The corresponding time-dependent equations are obvious and therefore omitted.

In a spherical coordinate system,
\bm{\omega}
is determined by the polar angle
\theta\in[0,\pi]
and azimuthal angle
\varphi\in[0,2\pi)
. Thus

(2.7)

\bm{\omega}=\begin{bmatrix}\omega_{x}&\omega_{y}&\omega_{z}\end{bmatrix}^{\mathsf{T}}=\begin{bmatrix}\sin\theta\cos\varphi&\sin\theta\sin\varphi&\cos\theta\end{bmatrix}^{\mathsf{T}}.

Reduced geometry problems will frequency be expressed in terms of these angles, as well as the variable
\mu=\cos\theta\in[-1,1]
.

One-dimensional slab geometry problem

Let
I=[0,1)
be the unit
1
-torus in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
.
In the slab geometry, the RTE (
2.1
) reduces to the following form

(2.8)

\mu\frac{\partial u}{\partial z}+\frac{\sigma_{\mathrm{t}}}{\varepsilon}u=\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\sigma_{\mathrm{t}}}{\varepsilon}-\varepsilon\sigma_{\mathrm{a}}\right)\int^{1}_{-1}u(z,\mu^{\prime})\,\mathrm{d}\mu^{\prime}+f,\quad\forall z\in I,

where
z\in I
,
u=u(z,\mu)
,
f=f(z,\mu)
, and
\,\mathrm{d}\mu^{\prime}
is the Lebesgue measure on
(-1,1)
.

Two-dimensional plane-parallel model

Let
J=[0,1)^{2}
be the unit
2
-torus in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
. In the parallel-plane model, the RTE (
2.1
) takes the form

(2.9)

\sqrt{1-\mu^{2}}\cos\varphi\frac{\partial u}{\partial x}+\mu\frac{\partial u}{\partial z}+\frac{\sigma_{\mathrm{t}}}{\varepsilon}u=\left(\frac{\sigma_{\mathrm{t}}}{\varepsilon}-\varepsilon\sigma_{\mathrm{a}}\right)\left\langle u\right\rangle+\varepsilon f,

where
(x,z)\in J
,
u=u(x,z,\mu,\varphi)
, and
f=f(x,z)
.

2.3.
Time discretion and translation to steady-state form

There are a variety of schemes to discretize the time variable in the RTE (
2.2a
). Implicit methods are common, and here we employ the backward differentiation formula (BDF). In detail, let
t_{n}=n\varDelta t
with
\Delta t
being the time step size, and
\psi^{(n)}(\mathbf{x},\bm{\omega})=\psi(t_{n},\mathbf{x},\bm{\omega})
. Then the
s
-step BDF method applied to the time-dependent RTE (
2.2a
) can be formed as

(2.10)

\varepsilon\psi^{(n)}=\varepsilon\sum_{p=1}^{s}\beta_{s,p}\psi^{(n-p)}+\gamma_{s}\varDelta t\bigg(-\bm{\omega}\cdot\nabla\psi^{(n)}-\frac{\sigma_{\mathrm{t}}(\mathbf{x})}{\varepsilon}\psi^{(n)}\\
+\left(\frac{\sigma_{\mathrm{t}}(\mathbf{x})}{\varepsilon}-\varepsilon\sigma_{\mathrm{a}}(\mathbf{x})\right)\left\langle\psi^{(n)}\right\rangle+\varepsilon f(\mathbf{x},\bm{\omega})\bigg),

where the spatial and angular variables remain continuous. The coefficients of
\beta_{s,p}
and
\gamma_{s}
for
s
-step BDFs with
p\leq s
can be found, e.g., in
[
39
]
for
s\leq 5
.

After some simple manipulations, the steps in (
2.10
) can be rewritten as the following steady-state RTE

(2.11)

\bm{\omega}\cdot\nabla\psi^{(n)}+\frac{\sigma^{(n)}_{\mathrm{t}}}{\varepsilon}\psi^{(n)}=\left(\frac{\sigma_{\mathrm{t}}}{\varepsilon}-\varepsilon\sigma_{\mathrm{a}}\right)\left\langle\psi^{(n)}\right\rangle+\varepsilon f^{(n)},

where

(2.12)

f^{(n)}=f+\frac{1}{\gamma\varDelta t}\sum_{p=1}^{s}\beta_{s,p}\psi^{(n-p)}

is a source depending on the values at previous steps and
\sigma^{(n)}_{\mathrm{t}}=\sigma_{\mathrm{t}}+\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{\gamma_{s}\varDelta t}
is the effective total cross-section.
After dropping the temporal index
n
, (
2.11
) takes the form of the scaled steady-state RTE (
2.1
), which will be the focus of the analysis in the rest of this paper.

2.4.
Spherical harmonic method for angular discretization

In this section, we briefly review the spherical harmonic functions and the
P_{N}
method. The interested readers are referred to
[

]
for more discussions. Formulas for the spherical harmonics are given in

Given integers
\ell\geq 0

\kappa\in[-\ell,\ell]
, let
m^{\kappa}_{\ell}
be the real-valued spherical harmonic of degree
\ell

\kappa
on
\mathbb{S}
, normalized such that
\int_{\mathbb{S}}(m^{\kappa}_{\ell}(\bm{\omega}))^{2}d\bm{\omega}=1

We collect the

\ell

\bm{m}_{\ell}=\begin{bmatrix}m^{-\ell}_{\ell}&m^{-\ell+1}_{\ell}&\cdots&m^{0}_{\ell}&\cdots&m^{\ell-1}_{\ell}&m^{\ell}_{\ell}\end{bmatrix}^{\mathsf{T}}
and then for any given

\bm{m}=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{m}_{0}^{\mathsf{T}}&\bm{m}_{1}^{\mathsf{T}}&\cdots&\bm{m}_{N}^{\mathsf{T}}\end{bmatrix}^{\mathsf{T}}

In all,
\bm{m}
has
L:=\sum^{N}_{\ell=0}n_{\ell}=(N+1)^{2}
components which form an orthogonal basis for the space

\mathbb{P}_{N}=\left\{\sum^{N}_{\ell=0}\sum^{\ell}_{\kappa=-\ell}c^{\kappa}_{\ell}m^{\kappa}_{\ell}\colon c^{\kappa}_{\ell}\in\mathbb{R},\text{ and }0\leq\ell\leq N,|\kappa|\leq\ell\right\}
.
Furthermore, the spherical harmonics fulfill a recursion relation of the form
[
3
]

(2.13)

\omega_{i}\bm{m}_{\ell}=\bm{A}^{(i)}_{\ell,\ell+1}\bm{m}_{\ell+1}+\bm{A}^{(i)}_{\ell,\ell-1}\bm{m}_{\ell-1},

where
\bm{A}^{(i)}_{\ell,\ell^{\prime}}=\int_{\mathbb{S}}\omega_{i}\bm{m}_{\ell}\bm{m}^{\mathsf{T}}_{\ell^{\prime}}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{\omega}
and
\big(\bm{A}^{(i)}_{\ell,\ell^{\prime}}\big)^{\mathsf{T}}=\bm{A}^{(i)}_{\ell^{\prime},\ell}
.

The
P_{N}
equations for the radiative transfer equation (
2.1
) are derived by approximating
u
by a function
u_{\mathrm{PN}}
of the form of

(2.14)

u_{\mathrm{PN}}(\mathbf{x},\bm{\omega})=\bm{u}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{m}=\sum_{\ell=0}^{N}\bm{u}^{\mathsf{T}}_{\ell}\bm{m}_{\ell}=\sum_{\ell=0}^{N}\sum_{|k|\leq\ell}{u}^{k}_{\ell}{m}_{\ell}^{k}

such that
\forall\,v\in\mathbb{P}_{N}
,

(2.15)

\Big\langle v\big(\bm{\omega}\cdot\nabla u_{\mathrm{PN}}(\mathbf{x},\bm{\omega})+\frac{\sigma_{\mathrm{t}}(\mathbf{x})}{\varepsilon}u_{\mathrm{PN}}(\mathbf{x},\bm{\omega})-\left(\frac{\sigma_{\mathrm{t}}(\mathbf{x})}{\varepsilon}-\varepsilon\sigma_{\mathrm{a}}(\mathbf{x})\right)\bar{u}_{\mathrm{PN}}\big)\Big\rangle=\varepsilon\langle vf\rangle.

Because the components
m_{l}^{\kappa}
are normalized, it follows that

u_{\ell}^{k}:=\int_{\mathbb{S}}m_{\ell}^{k}u_{\mathrm{PN}}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{\omega},\quad\ell=0,\cdots,N,\;|k|\leq\ell
.
The double index notation for
\bm{u}
is inherited from
\bm{m}
; that is

\bm{u}_{\ell}=\begin{bmatrix}u_{\ell}^{-\ell}&\cdots&u_{\ell}^{\ell}\end{bmatrix}^{\mathsf{T}}
and
\bm{u}=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{u}_{0}^{\mathsf{T}}&\bm{u}_{1}^{\mathsf{T}}&\cdots&\bm{u}_{N}^{\mathsf{T}}\end{bmatrix}^{\mathsf{T}}
. However, for convenience, we often use a single index to denote the components in
\bm{m}
and
\bm{u}
, e.g.,
\bm{u}=\begin{bmatrix}u_{1}&u_{2}&\cdots&u_{L}\end{bmatrix}^{\mathsf{T}}
, in which case the function
u_{\mathrm{PN}}
can be expressed as

u_{\mathrm{PN}}=\sum_{i=1}^{L}m_{i}u_{i}.

Setting
v=\bm{m}
in (
2.15
) and using (
2.14
) and the fact that
\int_{\mathbb{S}}\bm{m}\bm{m}^{\mathsf{T}}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{\omega}=\bm{I}
, we can reformulate (
2.15

(2.16)

\displaystyle\bm{A}\cdot\nabla\bm{u}(\mathbf{x})+\varepsilon\sigma_{\mathrm{a}}\bm{u}(\mathbf{x})+\left(\frac{\sigma_{\mathrm{t}}}{\varepsilon}-\varepsilon\sigma_{\mathrm{a}}\right)\bm{R}\bm{u}(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=\varepsilon\bm{f}(\mathbf{x})\qquad\text{ in }X,

Here
\bm{u}(\mathbf{x})\in\mathbb{R}^{L}
;

\bm{f}=\int_{\mathbb{S}}\bm{m}f\,\mathrm{d}\bm{\omega}
;
\bm{R}=\bm{I}-\diag(1,0,\cdots,0)=\diag(0,1,\cdots,1)
is diagonal and positive semi-definite;
and the dot product between

(2.17)

\bm{A}:=\begin{bmatrix}\left(\bm{A}^{(1)}\right)^{\mathsf{T}}&\left(\bm{A}^{(2)}\right)^{\mathsf{T}}&\left(\bm{A}^{(3)}\right)^{\mathsf{T}}\end{bmatrix}^{\mathsf{T}}

and the gradient is understood as

\bm{A}\cdot\nabla:=\sum^{3}_{i=1}\bm{A}^{(i)}\partial_{i}
with

\bm{A}^{(i)}=\int_{\mathbb{S}}\omega_{i}\bm{m}\bm{m}^{\mathsf{T}}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{\omega}
,

.
Note that

is symmetric and sparse. Moreover, the recursion relation (

\bm{A}^{(i)}=\begin{bmatrix}0&\bm{A}^{(i)}_{0,1}&0&0&\dots&0\\

\|\bm{u}\|_{r,D}=\left(\sum_{\ell=0}^{N}\sum_{|k|\leq\ell}\|u^{k}_{\ell}\|_{r,D}^{2}\right)^{1/2},\quad\text{ for }\bm{u}\in\left[H^{r}(D)\right]^{L}.

2.5.
Discontinuous Galerkin method for spatial discretization

Let
\mathcal{T}^{h}
be a regular family of partition of

K
.
The meshes on the boundary are assumed to be periodic to avoid unnecessary technicalities, i.e., the surface meshes on two opposite parallel faces of
\partial X
are identical.
Define
h_{K}:=\textrm{diam}(K)
and
h:=\max_{K\in\mathcal{T}^{h}}h_{K}
.
Denote by
\mathbf{n}^{K}=\begin{bmatrix}n_{1}^{K}&n_{2}^{K}&n_{3}^{K}\end{bmatrix}^{\mathsf{T}}
the unit outward normal to
\partial K
with respect to
K
.

Let
V_{h,k}
be a DG finite element space whose elements are polynomials of degree no more than
k
when restricted to any element
K
, i.e.,

(2.24)

V^{h,k}=\begin{cases}\{v\in L^{2}(\mathbb{S})\colon v|_{K}\in\mathbb{P}_{k}(K)\;\forall K\in\mathcal{T}^{h}\},&\text{ if }\mathcal{T}^{h}\text{ is triangular},\\
\{v\in L^{2}(\mathbb{S})\colon v|_{K}\in\mathbb{Q}_{k}(K)\;\forall K\in\mathcal{T}^{h}\},&\text{ if }\mathcal{T}^{h}\text{ is rectangular},\end{cases}

where
k
is a nonnegative integer,
\mathbb{P}_{k}(K)
denotes the set of all polynomials on
K
with maximum total degree
k
, and

\mathbb{Q}_{k}(K)
denotes the set of all polynomials on
K
with maximum degree
k
in each dimension.
Define
\bm{V}^{h,k_{\ell}}_{\ell}=[V^{h,k_{\ell}}]^{2\ell+1}
,
\ell=0,1,\cdots,N
,
and the Cartesian product

\bm{V}^{h}=\bm{V}^{h,k_{0}}_{0}\times\bm{V}^{h,k_{1}}_{1}\times\cdots\times\bm{V}^{h,k_{N}}_{N}
.

Given an element
K\in\mathcal{T}^{h}\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
, and any function
\bm{g}\in\bm{V}^{h}
, we define the inside (
-
) and outside (
+
) values of a function
\bm{g}
(with respect to
K
) as, respectively,

(2.25a)

\displaystyle\bm{g}^{\pm}(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=\lim\limits_{\epsilon\to 0}\bm{g}(\mathbf{x}\pm\epsilon\,\mathbf{n}^{K}(\mathbf{x})),\quad\;\;\;\forall\mathbf{x}\in\partial K.

The average
\{\!\!\{\bm{g}\}\!\!\}
and jump
[\![\bm{g}]\!]
in
\bm{g}\in\bm{V}^{h}
with respect to
K
are given by

(2.26)

\{\!\!\{\bm{g}\}\!\!\}=\frac{1}{2}(\bm{g}^{+}+\bm{g}^{-})\qquad\text{and}\qquad[\![\bm{g}]\!]=\bm{g}^{+}-\bm{g}^{-}.

For any
\bm{u}^{h},\bm{v}^{h}\in\bm{V}^{h}
, define

(2.27a)

\displaystyle\mathfrak{a}^{h}(\bm{u}^{h},\bm{v}^{h})

\displaystyle=\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}^{h}}\bigg\{\Big(\mathbf{n}^{K}\cdot\overrightarrow{\bm{A}\bm{u}^{h}},\,\bm{v}^{h-}\Big)_{\partial K}-\sum_{i=1}^{3}(\bm{A}^{(i)}\bm{u}^{h},\partial_{i}\bm{v}^{h})_{K}+(\bm{Q}\bm{u}^{h},\bm{v}^{h})_{K}\bigg\},

(2.27b)

\displaystyle\mathfrak{f}(\bm{v}^{h})

\displaystyle=\varepsilon\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}^{h}}(\bm{f},\bm{v}^{h})_{K}

where

(2.28)

\bm{Q}=\varepsilon\sigma_{\mathrm{a}}\bm{I}+\left(\frac{\sigma_{\mathrm{t}}}{\varepsilon}-\varepsilon\sigma_{\mathrm{a}}\right)\bm{R}=\begin{bmatrix}\varepsilon\sigma_{\mathrm{a}}&\\
&\frac{\sigma_{\mathrm{t}}}{\varepsilon}\bm{I}_{L-1}\end{bmatrix}

and
\bm{I}
is the identity matrix. Following
[
17
]
, we define the numerical flux as

(2.29)

n_{i}^{K}\overrightarrow{\bm{A}^{(i)}\bm{u}^{h}}=n_{i}^{K}\bm{A}^{(i)}\{\!\!\{\bm{u}^{h}\}\!\!\}-\frac{1}{2}\left|n_{i}^{K}\right|\,\bm{D}^{(i)}\,[\![\bm{u}^{h}]\!],\quad i=1,2,3,

and therefore

(2.30)

\mathbf{n}^{K}\cdot\overrightarrow{\bm{A}\bm{u}^{h}}=\mathbf{n}^{K}\cdot\bm{A}\{\!\!\{\bm{u}^{h}\}\!\!\}-\frac{1}{2}|\mathbf{n}^{K}|\cdot\bm{D}\,[\![\bm{u}^{h}]\!],

where
|\mathbf{n}^{K}|\cdot\bm{D}=\sum_{i=1}^{3}|n_{i}^{K}|\bm{D}^{(i)}
and, assuming the upwind flux,
\bm{D}^{(i)}=|\bm{A}^{(i)}|
.
The fully discrete spherical harmonic DG scheme for (
2.1
) can be cast as follows.

Problem 2.1

(Spherical harmonic DG scheme)
.

Find
\bm{u}^{h}\in\bm{V}^{h}
such that

(2.31)

\mathfrak{a}^{h}(\bm{u}^{h},\bm{v}^{h})=\mathfrak{f}(\bm{v}^{h})\quad\forall\bm{v}^{h}\in\bm{V}^{h},

where
\mathfrak{a}^{h}
and
\mathfrak{f}
are defined in (
2.27
).

To study the stability and well-posedness of
Problem

2.1
,
we define the norm

(2.32)

\|\bm{v}^{h}\|_{\mathfrak{a}^{h}}=\left(\frac{1}{4}\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}^{h}}\Big(|\mathbf{n}^{K}|\cdot\bm{D}\,[\![\bm{v}^{h}]\!],[\![\bm{v}^{h}]\!]\Big)_{\partial K}+(\bm{Q}\bm{v}^{h},\bm{v}^{h})\right)^{\frac{1}{2}},

for any
\bm{v}^{h}\in\bm{V}_{h}
.

Lemma 2.2

(Stability)
.

Under the assumptions (
2.4b
) and (
2.4c
),

(2.33)

\|\bm{v}^{h}\|^{2}_{\mathfrak{a}^{h}}=\mathfrak{a}^{h}(\bm{v}^{h},\bm{v}^{h}),\quad\forall\bm{v}^{h}\in\bm{V}^{h}.

Proof.

By the definition (
2.27a
), it follows that for any
\bm{v}^{h}\in\bm{V}^{h}
,
\mathfrak{a}^{h}(\bm{v}^{h},\bm{v}^{h})=\mathrm{I}+\mathrm{II}+\mathrm{III}
, where

\displaystyle\mathrm{I}

\displaystyle:=\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}^{h}}\sum_{i=1}^{3}\Big(n_{i}^{K}\bm{A}^{(i)}\{\!\!\{\bm{v}^{h}\}\!\!\}-\frac{1}{2}|n_{i}^{K}|\bm{D}^{(i)}[\![\bm{v}^{h}]\!],\bm{v}^{h-}\Big)_{\partial K},

\displaystyle\mathrm{II}

\displaystyle:=-\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}^{h}}\sum_{i=1}^{3}(\bm{A}^{(i)}\bm{v}^{h},\partial_{i}\bm{v}^{h})_{K},\quad\mathrm{III}:=\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}^{h}}(\bm{Q}\bm{v}^{h},\bm{v}^{h})_{K}.

For the first two terms, we have

\mathrm{I}=\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}^{h}}\frac{1}{2}\Big(\mathbf{n}^{K}\cdot\bm{A}\bm{v}^{h-},\bm{v}^{h-}\Big)_{\partial K}+\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}^{h}}\frac{1}{4}\Big(|\mathbf{n}^{K}|\cdot\bm{D}\,[\![\bm{v}^{h}]\!],[\![\bm{v}^{h}]\!]\Big)_{\partial K}.

\mathrm{II}=-\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}^{h}}\sum_{i=1}^{3}\frac{1}{2}(n_{i}^{K}\bm{\Lambda}^{(i)}\bm{U}_{i}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{v}^{h-},\bm{U}_{i}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{v}^{h-})_{\partial K}=-\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}^{h}}\frac{1}{2}(\mathbf{n}^{K}\cdot\bm{A}\bm{v}^{h-},\bm{v}^{h-})_{\partial K}.

Therefore, we have
\mathrm{I}+\mathrm{II}=\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}^{h}}\frac{1}{4}\left(|\mathbf{n}^{K}|\cdot\bm{D}\,[\![\bm{v}^{h}]\!],[\![\bm{v}^{h}]\!]\right)_{\partial K}
and (
2.33
) follows.
∎

Since
\bm{V}^{h}
is of finite dimension, all norms in
\bm{V}^{h}
are equivalent, which ensures continuity of the bilinear form. Due to the Lax–Milgram lemma, under the assumptions (
2.4b
) and (
2.4c
), the spherical harmonic DG method (
2.31
) has a unique solution.

3.
Asymptotic Analysis and Diffusion Limit

In this section, we first perform the asymptotic analysis of the spherical harmonic DG scheme
eq.

2.31
under the assumption
\varepsilon\rightarrow 0
, and discuss the approximation to the diffusion limit. Based on these observations, we then design a heterogeneous DG method, which can achieve the AP property while with less degree of freedom.

3.1.
Asymptotic analysis

The main result of this section is the following theorem on proving the AP property.
The proof follows the basic strategy in
[
15
]
, where instead the discrete-ordinate method was employed to discretize the angular variable. We remark that to indicate the implicit dependence of
\varepsilon
, we start to use
\bm{u}^{\varepsilon,h}
instead of
\bm{u}^{h}
to denote the solution for the analysis in this section. Similarly,
\bm{u}^{\varepsilon,h}_{\ell}
is used to denote the
\ell
th moment of
\bm{u}^{\varepsilon,h}
.

Define

(3.1)

\phi^{\varepsilon,h}=\int_{\mathbb{S}}u_{\mathrm{PN}}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{\omega}\quad\text{ and }\quad\bm{J}^{\varepsilon,h}=\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\int_{\mathbb{S}}\bm{\omega}\,u_{\mathrm{PN}}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{\omega}

so that

(3.2)

\bm{u}^{\varepsilon,h}_{0}=\frac{\phi^{\varepsilon,h}}{\sqrt{4\pi}}\qquad\text{and}\quad\bm{u}^{\varepsilon,h}_{1}={\sqrt{\frac{3}{4\pi}}}\varepsilon\bm{P}^{-1}\bm{J}^{\varepsilon,h},

where the matrix

(3.3)

\bm{P}=\begin{bmatrix}0&0&1\\
1&0&0\\
0&1&0\end{bmatrix}

is needed because the indexing of
\bm{\omega}
and
\bm{m}_{1}
is not consistent.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
\bm{u}^{\varepsilon,h}
be the solution to the spherical harmonic DG method (
2.31
) of the scaled RTE with parameter
\varepsilon>0
. Suppose the assumptions (
2.4b
)-(
2.4c
) hold.
Then as
\varepsilon\rightarrow 0
,

(3.4)

\phi^{\varepsilon,h}\rightarrow\phi^{0,h}\in C_{0}\cap\bm{V}^{h,k_{0}}_{0},\quad\bm{J}^{\varepsilon,h}\rightarrow\bm{J}^{0,h}\in\bm{V}^{h,k_{1}}_{1},

where
\phi^{0,h}
and
\bm{J}^{0,h}
solve the following system: For all
v_{0}\in C_{0}\cap\bm{V}^{h,k_{0}}_{0}
and
\bm{v}_{1}\in\bm{V}^{h,k_{1}}_{1}
,

(3.5a)

\displaystyle\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}^{h}}\left\{-(\bm{J}^{0,h},\nabla v_{0})_{K}+(\sigma_{\mathrm{a}}\phi^{0,h},v_{0})_{K}\right\}

\displaystyle=4\pi\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}^{h}}(\langle f\rangle,v_{0})_{K},

(3.5b)

\displaystyle\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}^{h}}\bigg\{\frac{1}{3}\left(\nabla\phi^{0,h},\bm{v}_{1}\right)_{K}+\left(\sigma_{\mathrm{t}}\bm{J}^{0,h},\bm{v}_{1}\right)_{K}\bigg\}

\displaystyle=0.

Proof.

The first step is to determine bounds for
\bm{u}^{\varepsilon,h}
.
Setting
v=\bm{u}^{\varepsilon,h}
in (
2.31
) with the bilinear form (
2.27
) and the flux (
2.30
), and employing the equality (
2.33
) yield the energy equation

(3.6)

\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}^{h}}\bigg\{\frac{1}{4}\big(|\mathbf{n}^{K}|\cdot\bm{D}\,[\![\bm{u}^{\varepsilon,h}]\!],[\![\bm{u}^{\varepsilon,h}]\!]\big)_{\partial K}\\
+\varepsilon(\sigma_{\mathrm{a}}\bm{u}^{\varepsilon,h},\bm{u}^{\varepsilon,h})_{K}+\left(\left(\frac{\sigma_{\mathrm{t}}}{\varepsilon}-\varepsilon\sigma_{\mathrm{a}}\right)\bm{R}\bm{u}^{\varepsilon,h},\bm{u}^{\varepsilon,h}\right)_{K}\bigg\}=\varepsilon\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}^{h}}(\bm{f},\bm{u}^{\varepsilon,h})_{K}.

Since the first and the third terms on the left-hand side of the above equation are non-negative and
\sigma_{\mathrm{a}}>\sigma_{\mathrm{a}}^{\mathrm{min}}
, it follows that

(3.7)

\sigma_{\mathrm{a}}^{\mathrm{min}}\|\bm{u}^{\varepsilon,h}\|^{2}\leq\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}^{h}}(\bm{f},\bm{u}^{\varepsilon,h})_{K}\leq\frac{1}{2}\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}^{h}}(\frac{1}{\sigma_{\mathrm{a}}^{\mathrm{min}}}\bm{f},\bm{f})_{K}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}^{h}}(\sigma_{\mathrm{a}}^{\mathrm{min}}\bm{u}^{\varepsilon,h},\bm{u}^{\varepsilon,h})_{K}.

Therefore
\sigma_{\mathrm{a}}^{\mathrm{min}}\|\bm{u}^{\varepsilon,h}\|^{2}\lesssim\|\bm{f}\|_{L^{2}(X)}
,
i.e., the sequence
\{\bm{u}^{\varepsilon,h}\}_{\varepsilon>0}
is uniformly bounded. Therefore it has a convergent sub-sequence, for the ease of presentation, still denoted by
\{\bm{u}^{\varepsilon,h}\}_{\varepsilon>0}
with limit
\bm{u}^{0,h}
.
Next, since

(3.8)

\sigma_{\mathrm{s}}^{\mathrm{min}}\|\bm{R}\bm{u}^{\varepsilon,h}\|^{2}_{X}\leq\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}^{h}}((\sigma_{\mathrm{t}}-\varepsilon^{2}\sigma_{\mathrm{a}})\bm{R}\bm{u}^{\varepsilon,h},\bm{u}^{\varepsilon,h})_{K}\leq\varepsilon^{2}\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}^{h}}(\bm{f},\bm{u}^{\varepsilon,h})_{K},

it follows
\|\bm{R}\bm{u}^{\varepsilon,h}\|^{2}_{X}\to 0
as
\varepsilon\rightarrow 0
.
Because
\bm{R}=\diag(0,1,1,\cdots,1,1)
and (
3.2
), we conclude that
\bm{u}^{0,h}_{\ell}=0
for all
\ell\geq 1
and that
\bm{J}^{\varepsilon,h}
is bounded and converges to a sub-sequential limit
\bm{J}^{0,h}
.
Finally (
3.6
) also implies that

(3.9)

\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}^{h}}(|n_{i}|\bm{D}^{(i)}[\![\bm{u}^{\varepsilon,h}]\!],[\![\bm{u}^{\varepsilon,h}]\!])_{\partial K}\leq 4\varepsilon\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}^{h}}(\bm{f},\bm{u}^{\varepsilon,h})_{K}.

As a result,
[\![\bm{u}^{0,h}]\!]=\lim_{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0}[\![\bm{u}^{\varepsilon,h}]\!]=0
. In particular,
u^{0,h}_{0}
is continuous.

The next step is to find equations for
\phi^{\varepsilon,h}
and
\bm{J}^{\varepsilon,h}
in the limit.
By setting

\bm{v}^{h}=\varepsilon^{-1}v_{0}\mathbf{e}_{0}
in (
2.31
), where
v_{0}\in C_{0}\cap\bm{V}^{h,k_{0}}_{0}
and
\mathbf{e}_{0}=\begin{bmatrix}1&0&0&\cdots&0\end{bmatrix}^{\mathsf{T}}\in\mathbb{R}^{L}
,

(3.10)

\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}^{h}}\bigg\{\sum_{i=1}^{3}\left(n_{i}\overrightarrow{\bm{A}^{(i)}\bm{u}^{\varepsilon,h}},\varepsilon^{-1}v_{0}^{-}\mathbf{e}_{0}\right)_{\partial K}-\sum_{i=1}^{3}\left(\bm{A}^{(i)}\bm{u}^{\varepsilon,h},\left(\varepsilon^{-1}\partial_{i}v_{0}\right)\mathbf{e}_{0}\right)_{K}\\
+\frac{1}{\sqrt{4\pi}}(\sigma_{\mathrm{a}}\phi^{\varepsilon,h},v_{0})_{K}-\sqrt{4\pi}(\left\langle f\right\rangle,v_{0})_{K}\bigg\}=0.

From (
2.18
) and (
3.2
), we have

(3.11)

\bm{A}^{(i)}\bm{u}^{\varepsilon,h}\cdot\mathbf{e}_{0}=\bm{A}^{(i)}_{0,1}\bm{u}^{\varepsilon,h}_{1}={\sqrt{\frac{3}{4\pi}}}\varepsilon\bm{A}^{(i)}_{0,1}\bm{P}^{-1}\bm{J}^{\varepsilon,h}.

Noting the definition of
\bm{J}^{\varepsilon,h}
in (
3.1
) and
\bm{P}
in (
3.3
), and
\bm{A}^{(i)}_{0,1}=\int_{\mathbb{S}}\omega_{i}\bm{m}_{1}\bm{m}^{\mathsf{T}}_{0}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{\omega}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}\begin{bmatrix}\delta_{i,2}&\delta_{i,3}&\delta_{i,1}\end{bmatrix}^{\mathsf{T}}
, we deduce by direct computation that

(3.12)

\bm{A}^{(i)}_{0,1}\bm{P}^{-1}\bm{J}^{\varepsilon,h}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}\left(\bm{J}^{\varepsilon,h}\right)^{i},

where
\left(\bm{J}^{\varepsilon,h}\right)^{i}
indicates the
i
th component of the vector
\bm{J}^{\varepsilon,h}
. Therefore,

(3.13)

\sum_{i=1}^{3}\left(\bm{A}^{(i)}\bm{u}^{\varepsilon,h},\left(\varepsilon^{-1}\partial_{i}v_{0}\right)\mathbf{e}_{0}\right)_{K}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{4\pi}}\sum_{i=1}^{3}\left(\left(\bm{J}^{\varepsilon,h}\right)^{i},\partial_{i}v_{0}\right)_{K}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{4\pi}}(\bm{J}^{\varepsilon,h},\nabla v_{0})_{K}.

Since
v_{0}
is continuous, and therefore
\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}^{h}}\sum_{i=1}^{3}\left(\overrightarrow{\bm{A}^{(i)}\bm{u}^{\varepsilon,h}},n_{i}\varepsilon^{-1}v_{0}^{-}\mathbf{e}_{0}\right)_{\partial K}=0
, the equation (
3.10
) reduces to

(3.14)

\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}^{h}}\left\{-(\bm{J}^{\varepsilon,h},\nabla v_{0})_{K}+(\sigma_{\mathrm{a}}\phi^{\varepsilon,h},v_{0})_{K}\right\}=4\pi\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}^{h}}(\left\langle f\right\rangle,v_{0})_{K}.

By using the fact that
\bm{J}^{\varepsilon,h}\rightarrow\bm{J}^{0,h}
and
\phi^{\varepsilon,h}\rightarrow\phi^{0,h}
, respectively, we can now pass to the limit as
\varepsilon\rightarrow 0
to obtain

(3.15)

\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}^{h}}\left\{-(\bm{J}^{0,h},\nabla v_{0})_{K}+(\sigma_{\mathrm{a}}\phi^{0,h},v_{0})_{K}\right\}=4\pi\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}^{h}}(\left\langle f\right\rangle,v_{0})_{K}.

Next, setting
\bm{v}^{h}=\begin{bmatrix}0&\bm{v}_{1}^{\mathsf{T}}&0&\cdots&0\end{bmatrix}^{\mathsf{T}}\in\mathbb{R}^{L}
in (
2.31
) with
\bm{v}_{1}\in\bm{V}_{1}^{h,k_{1}}
yields

(3.16)

\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}^{h}}\bigg\{\sum_{i=1}^{3}\left(n_{i}\overrightarrow{\bm{A}^{(i)}\bm{u}^{\varepsilon,h}},\bm{v}^{h-}\right)_{\partial K}-\sum_{i=1}^{3}\left(\bm{A}^{(i)}\bm{u}^{\varepsilon,h},\partial_{i}\bm{v}^{h}\right)_{K}\\
+\sqrt{\frac{3}{4\pi}}\varepsilon^{2}(\sigma_{\mathrm{a}}\bm{P}^{-1}\bm{J}^{\varepsilon,h},\bm{v}_{1})_{K}+\sqrt{\frac{3}{4\pi}}((\sigma_{\mathrm{t}}-\varepsilon^{2}\sigma_{\mathrm{a}})\bm{P}^{-1}\bm{J}^{\varepsilon,h},\bm{v}_{1})_{K}-\varepsilon(\bm{f},\bm{v}_{1})_{K}\bigg\}=0.

Since

(3.17)

\sum_{i=1}^{3}\bm{P}\bm{A}^{(i)}_{1,0}\partial_{i}\phi^{\varepsilon,h}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}\nabla\phi^{\varepsilon,h}

and
\bm{A}^{(i)}_{1,0}=\int_{\mathbb{S}}\omega_{i}\bm{m}_{1}\bm{m}^{\mathsf{T}}_{0}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{\omega}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}\begin{bmatrix}\delta_{i,2}&\delta_{i,3}&\delta_{i,1}\end{bmatrix}^{\mathsf{T}}
, it follows that

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{3}

\displaystyle\left(\left(n_{i}\overrightarrow{\bm{A}^{(i)}\bm{u}^{\varepsilon,h}},\bm{v}^{h-}\right)_{\partial K}-\left(\bm{A}^{(i)}\bm{u}^{\varepsilon,h},\partial_{i}\bm{v}^{h}\right)_{K}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{3}\left(\left(n_{i}\overrightarrow{\bm{A}^{(i)}_{1,0}\bm{u}^{\varepsilon,h}_{0}}+n_{i}\overrightarrow{\bm{A}^{(i)}_{1,2}\bm{u}^{\varepsilon,h}_{2}},\bm{v}^{-}_{1}\right)_{\partial K}-\left(\bm{A}^{(i)}_{1,0}\bm{u}^{\varepsilon,h}_{0}+\bm{A}^{(i)}_{1,2}\bm{u}^{\varepsilon,h}_{2},\partial_{i}\bm{v}_{1}\right)_{K}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{3}\bigg(\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{4\pi}}\bm{A}^{(i)}_{1,0}\phi^{\varepsilon,h},n_{i}\bm{v}^{-}_{1}\right)_{\partial K}-\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{4\pi}}\bm{A}^{(i)}_{1,0}\phi^{\varepsilon,h},\partial_{i}\bm{v}_{1}\right)_{K}\bigg)

\displaystyle\phantom{=}\quad+\sum_{i=1}^{3}\bigg(\left(n_{i}\overrightarrow{\bm{A}^{(i)}_{1,2}\bm{u}^{\varepsilon,h}_{2}},\bm{v}^{-}_{1}\right)_{\partial K}-\left(\bm{A}^{(i)}_{1,2}\bm{u}^{\varepsilon,h}_{2},\partial_{i}\bm{v}_{1}\right)_{K}\bigg)

(3.18)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{1}{12\pi}}(\bm{P}^{-1}\nabla\phi^{\varepsilon,h},\bm{v}_{1})_{K}+\sum_{i=1}^{3}\left(\left(n_{i}\overrightarrow{\bm{A}^{(i)}_{1,2}\bm{u}^{\varepsilon,h}_{2}},\bm{v}^{-}_{1}\right)_{\partial K}-\left(\bm{A}^{(i)}_{1,2}\bm{u}^{\varepsilon,h}_{2},\partial_{i}\bm{v}_{1}\right)_{K}\right).

Plugging (
3.18
) in (
3.16
), letting
\varepsilon\rightarrow 0
and noting
\bm{u}^{0,h}_{2}=0
yield the limit equation

(3.19)

\displaystyle\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}^{h}}\bigg\{\frac{1}{3}(\nabla\phi^{0,h},\bm{v}_{1})_{K}+(\sigma_{\mathrm{t}}\bm{J}^{0,h},\bm{v}_{1})_{K}\bigg\}

\displaystyle=0.

Together (
3.15
) and (
3.19
) recover (
3.5
), and the proof is complete.
∎

Theorem
3.1
states that
\phi^{\varepsilon,h}
converges to the function

\phi^{0,h}\in C_{0}\cap\bm{V}_{0}^{h,k_{0}}
, which solves the system (
3.5
).
This system is a mixed discretization of the diffusion equation (
2.5
) with a continuous primal variable.
An obvious conclusion from the above theorem is that we require
k_{0}\geq 1
to ensure that
C_{0}\cap\bm{V}_{0}^{h,k_{0}}
is not a trivial space only consisting of constant functions. Therefore, we have the following corollary.

Corollary 3.2
.

k_{0}\geq 1
is a necessary condition for the solution
\phi^{\varepsilon,h}
converges to the correct limit solution

3.2.
Diffusion limit

Let us further consider the limit equations (
3.5
) of the system (
2.31
). Assuming constant cross-sections on each element,
for the first and second moments
\phi^{0,h}
and
\bm{J}^{0,h}
, let us focus on
\mathbb{Q}_{1}
elements on rectangular meshes. Choose
\bm{W}_{0}^{h}:=C_{0}\cap\bm{V}_{0}^{h,1}
(i.e., continuous and piecewise
\mathbb{Q}_{1}
) and
\bm{W}^{h}_{1}:=\bm{V}_{1}^{h,1}
(piecewise
\mathbb{Q}_{1}
). If
v_{0}\in\bm{W}_{0}^{h}
, then
\bm{v}_{1}:=\nabla v_{0}\in\bm{W}^{h}_{1}
. Substitution yields a continuous Galerkin method for the diffusion equation (
2.5
):

(3.20)

\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}^{h}}\left\{\frac{1}{3\sigma_{\mathrm{t}}}\left(\nabla\phi^{0,h},\nabla v_{0}\right)_{K}+\sigma_{\mathrm{a}}(\phi^{0,h},v_{0})_{K}\right\}=4\pi\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}^{h}}(\left\langle f\right\rangle,v_{0})_{K}.

It can be shown
[
8
]
that this method (
3.20
) converges with the order of
\mathcal{O}(h)
in the standard
H^{1}
norm. This shows the AP property of the spherical harmonic DG method, i.e., the limit (as
\varepsilon\to 0
) of the discretization is the discretization of the limit equation. The same conclusion can also be drawn when
\mathbb{P}_{1}
elements are used on triangular meshes.

We could also use lower order elements for
\bm{J}^{0,h}
. If we keep
\bm{W}^{h}_{0}=C_{0}\cap\bm{V}^{h,1}_{0}
, but take
\bm{W}^{h}_{1}=\bm{V}^{h,0}_{1}
(the piecewise constant space) instead. On triangular meshes, we can take
\bm{V}^{h,0}_{1}
to be the piecewise
\mathbb{P}_{1}
space, hence, we still have
\bm{v}_{1}:=\nabla v_{0}\in\bm{W}^{h}_{1}
and following the same procedure of substitution gives the same limit equation (
3.20
), which implies the AP property on triangular meshes under this setup of approximation spaces.
In fact, in the case that
k_{0}=1
and
k_{1}=0
, the system (
3.5
) is a mixed discretization of the steady-state
P_{1}
equations (i.e. the diffusion equation in first-order form), using the method from
[
10
]
.

On rectangular meshes, we take
\bm{V}_{0}^{h,1}
to be the piecewise
\mathbb{Q}_{1}
space. Since
\nabla v_{0}
belongs to the piecewise
\mathbb{P}_{1}
space and is no longer an element of
\bm{W}^{h}_{1}=\bm{V}^{h,0}_{1}
, we choose
\bm{v}_{1}:=\overline{\nabla v_{0}}\in\bm{W}^{h}_{1}
in (
3.5b
), where
\overline{\nabla v_{0}}=(\nabla v_{0},1)_{K}
is the cell average.
Since
\bm{J}^{0,h}\in\bm{V}^{1}_{h,0}
, we can obtain

(3.21)

\left(\bm{J}^{0,h},\nabla v_{0}\right)_{K}=\bm{J}^{0,h}\left(1,\nabla v_{0}\right)_{K}=\left(\bm{J}^{0,h},\overline{\nabla v_{0}}\right)_{K}.

Substituting these again into (
3.5a
) to obtain

(3.22)

\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}^{h}}\left\{\frac{1}{3\sigma_{\mathrm{t}}}\left(\nabla\phi^{0,h},\overline{\nabla v_{0}}\right)_{K}+\sigma_{\mathrm{a}}(\phi^{0,h},v_{0})_{K}\right\}=4\pi\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}^{h}}(\left\langle f\right\rangle,v_{0})_{K},

or equivalently

(3.23)

\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}^{h}}\left\{\frac{1}{3\sigma_{\mathrm{t}}}\left(\overline{\nabla\phi^{0,h}},\overline{\nabla v_{0}}\right)_{K}+\sigma_{\mathrm{a}}(\phi^{0,h},v_{0})_{K}\right\}=4\pi\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}^{h}}(\left\langle f\right\rangle,v_{0})_{K}.

For the one-dimensional model, it is easy to observe that
eq.

3.22
is equivalent to
eq.

3.20
. For the multi-dimensional case, we can show that this method also converges with the order of
\mathcal{O}(h)
in the standard
H^{1}
norm. The proof follows from the standard error estimate in
H^{1}
norm for
eq.

3.20
, combined with the fact that
\|\nabla\phi^{0,h}-\overline{\nabla\phi^{0,h}}\|=\mathcal{O}(h)
, where the bar denotes the cell-wise average. The detailed steps are skipped here to save space. Therefore, the AP property of the spherical harmonic DG method can be achieved when lower order elements are used for
\bm{J}^{0,h}
, and this holds for both rectangular and triangular meshes.

3.3.
A discontinuous Galerkin method with heterogeneous polynomial spaces for different moments

In conventional spherical harmonics DG methods, it is standard that
k_{i}\equiv k
for all
0\leq i<N
. Furthermore, in order to capture the diffusion limit, local
\mathbb{P}_{1}
or
\mathbb{Q}_{1}
approximations for triangular or rectangular elements have to be employed, i.e.,
k\geq 1
.
However, a key observation from the asymptotic analysis for the DG method in
theorem

3.1
and the discussion in Section
3.2
indicates that, in order for numerical solutions of the DG method to have the correct asymptotic limit, the requirement
k\geq 1
can be placed on the spaces
\bm{V}_{0}^{h}
only. This means that non-constant elements have to be employed only for the space
\bm{V}^{h,k_{0}}_{0}
, i.e.,
k_{0}\geq 1
, while a trivial space only consisting of constant
\mathbb{P}_{0}
elements for simplices (and
\mathbb{P}_{0}
or
\mathbb{P}_{1}
for cuboids) can still be used for higher moments.
This fact can be leveraged to save memory, hence the computational cost, while maintaining the property of AP and uniform convergence. For example, we could use
\mathbb{P}_{1}
or
\mathbb{Q}_{1}
elements for
\bm{u}_{0}
only, and
\mathbb{P}_{0}
elements for all higher moments. Therefore, the improved DG method with heterogeneous polynomial spaces can be cast as follows.

Problem 3.1
.

For
k_{0}\geq 1
, find
\bm{u}^{h}\in\bm{V}^{h,k_{0}}_{0}\times\bm{V}^{h,0}_{1}\times\cdots\times\bm{V}^{h,0}_{N}
such that

(3.24)

\mathfrak{a}^{h}(\bm{u}^{h},\bm{v}^{h})=\mathfrak{f}(\bm{v}^{h})\quad\forall\bm{v}^{h}\in\bm{V}^{h,k_{0}}_{0}\times\bm{V}^{h,0}_{1}\times\cdots\times\bm{V}^{h,0}_{N}.

Remark 3.3
.

For the proposed DG methods with heterogeneous polynomial spaces, high accuracy can be expected in the case of highly diffusive regions (
\varepsilon\ll 1
). However, when
\varepsilon=\mathcal{O}(1)
, the hybrid method will lose the high order accuracy due to the
\mathbb{P}_{0}
elements employed. In fact, for simplicial elements, a uniform convergence (with respect to
\varepsilon
) of order
\mathcal{O}(h^{1/2})
can be proved when
\mathbb{P}_{1}
and
\mathbb{P}_{0}
elements are employed for
\bm{u}_{0}
and higher moments, respectively. The proof can be studied following the approach in
[
36
]
and is neglected here to save space.

4.
A hybrid discontinuous Galerkin/finite volume method

The proposed DG method with heterogeneous polynomial spaces in the previous section can save computational costs but might lose convergence order when
\varepsilon=\mathcal{O}(1)
.
To alleviate such a drawback, we proposed a hybrid method,
using reconstruction techniques from FV methods for all the

\mathbb{P}_{0}
elements approximating higher moments.

The proposed hybrid methods are a combination of DG methods and FV methods.
For the unknown variables, the first moment of
\bm{u}^{h}
is approximated by a piecewise polynomial from the DG solution space, and all the other moments of
\bm{u}^{h}
are approximated
by a piecewise constant in the FV fashion.
At each cell interface, we can compute the cell boundary values of
\bm{u}^{h}
via either polynomial evaluation (for the DG component) or reconstruction procedure (for the FV component).
Numerical fluxes at the cell interface are then computed based on these values.
Below, we take the 1-D slab geometry model (
2.8
) as an example to present the approach.
The derivation for the 2-D model or fully RTE follows the same approach, and we provide some details and implementations of the algorithm for the 2-D model in
Appendix

B
.

In 1-D slab geometry settings, the RTE model reduces to (
2.8
), and the corresponding
P_{N}
equation takes the form

(4.1)

\bm{B}\partial_{z}\bm{u}+\varepsilon\sigma_{\mathrm{a}}\bm{u}+\left(\frac{\sigma_{\mathrm{t}}}{\varepsilon}-\varepsilon\sigma_{\mathrm{a}}\right)\bm{R}\bm{u}=\varepsilon\bm{f}.

See
Section

A.1
for more details.
We take the domain
I=(0,1)\subset\mathbb{R}
.
Let
\mathcal{T}^{h}
be a regular family of meshes of
I
, i.e.,
I:=\cup_{i=1}^{M}I_{i}
with
I_{i}=(z_{i-1/2},z_{i+1/2})
.
Denote
z_{i}=(z_{i-\frac{1}{2}}+z_{i+\frac{1}{2}})/2
,
h_{i}=z_{i+1/2}-z_{i-1/2}
and
h:=\max_{1\leq i\leq M}h_{i}
. Let
\mathcal{E}^{h}
be the set of all interior points of
\mathcal{T}
.
The value of
\bm{u}^{h}
at
z=i+1/2
is denoted by
(\bm{u}^{h})_{i+1/2}
or just
\bm{u}^{h}_{i+1/2}
.

Let us set
\bm{u}^{h}_{0}\in\bm{V}^{h,1}_{0}
. Then for each cell
I_{i}
,

(4.2)

\bm{u}^{h}_{0}|_{I_{i}}=\overline{\bm{u}}_{0,i}+\widetilde{\bm{u}}_{0,i}\xi_{i}\in\mathbb{P}_{1},\quad\text{where $\xi_{i}=\frac{2(z-z_{i})}{h_{i}}\in[-1,1]$}.

For higher moments, let
\bm{u}^{h}_{\ell}\in\bm{V}^{h,0}_{\ell}
. Then for each cell
I_{i}
,

(4.3)

\bm{u}^{h}_{\ell}|_{I_{i}}=\overline{\bm{u}}_{\ell,i}\in[\mathbb{P}_{0}]^{2\ell+1},\quad\ell=1,\cdots,N.

Integration of (
4.1
) over
I_{i}
gives

(4.4)

\frac{1}{h_{i}}\left(\overrightarrow{\bm{B}\bm{u}}_{i+\frac{1}{2}}-\overrightarrow{\bm{B}\bm{u}}_{i-\frac{1}{2}}\right)+\varepsilon\sigma_{\mathrm{a}}\overline{\bm{u}}_{i}+\left(\frac{\sigma_{\mathrm{t}}}{\varepsilon}-\varepsilon\sigma_{\mathrm{a}}\right)\bm{R}\overline{\bm{u}}_{i}=\varepsilon\overline{\bm{f}}_{i}.

Here
\overrightarrow{\bm{B}\bm{u}}_{i+\frac{1}{2}}
and
-\overrightarrow{\bm{B}\bm{u}}_{i-\frac{1}{2}}
are the numerical fluxes and
\overline{\bm{f}}_{i}={\frac{1}{h_{i}}}\int_{I_{i}}\bm{f}\,\mathrm{d}z
.
Setting
\overrightarrow{\bm{B}\bm{u}}_{i+\frac{1}{2}}=\bm{B}\{\!\!\{\bm{u}\}\!\!\}_{i+\frac{1}{2}}-\frac{1}{2}\bm{D}[\![\bm{u}]\!]_{i+\frac{1}{2}}
, where
\bm{D}=|\bm{B}|
is a positive definite matrix, gives

(4.5)

\frac{1}{h_{i}}\Big(\bm{B}\{\!\!\{\bm{u}\}\!\!\}_{i+\frac{1}{2}}-\frac{1}{2}\bm{D}[\![\bm{u}]\!]_{i+\frac{1}{2}}\Big)-\frac{1}{h_{i}}\Big(\bm{B}\{\!\!\{\bm{u}\}\!\!\}_{i-\frac{1}{2}}+\frac{1}{2}\bm{D}[\![\bm{u}]\!]_{i-\frac{1}{2}}\Big)\\
+\varepsilon\sigma_{\mathrm{a}}\overline{\bm{u}}_{i}+\left(\frac{\sigma_{\mathrm{t}}}{\varepsilon}-\varepsilon\sigma_{\mathrm{a}}\right)\bm{R}\overline{\bm{u}}_{i}=\varepsilon\overline{\bm{f}}_{i},

The edge values to define jumps and averages in (
4.5
) are determined using (
4.2
) for
\ell=0
and local reconstructions for
\ell>0
. In the
\ell=0
case, an equation for
\widetilde{\bm{u}}_{0,i}
is required. To derive it, we multiply the
\ell=0
component of (
4.1
) by
\xi_{i}
and integrate over
I_{i}
.
With the definition of the numerical fluxes above, the result is

(4.6)

\frac{3}{h_{i}}\bigg(\Big(\bm{B}\{\!\!\{\bm{u}\}\!\!\}_{i+\frac{1}{2}}-\frac{1}{2}\bm{D}[\![\bm{u}]\!]_{i+\frac{1}{2}}\Big)_{0}+\Big(\bm{B}\{\!\!\{\bm{u}\}\!\!\}_{i-\frac{1}{2}}+\frac{1}{2}\bm{D}[\![\bm{u}]\!]_{i-\frac{1}{2}}\Big)_{0}\bigg)\\
-\frac{6}{h_{i}}(\bm{B}\overline{\bm{u}}_{i})_{0}+\varepsilon\sigma_{\mathrm{a}}\widetilde{\bm{u}}_{0,i}+\left(\frac{\sigma_{\mathrm{t}}}{\varepsilon}-\varepsilon\sigma_{\mathrm{a}}\right)\bm{R}\widetilde{\bm{u}}_{0,i}=\varepsilon\widetilde{\bm{f}}_{0,i},

where, for notational convenience, we often use an outer subscript to extract the
\ell=0
component of a vector, e.g.
(\bm{B}\overline{\bm{u}}_{i})_{0}=(\bm{B}\overline{\bm{u}})_{0,i}
.

For
\ell>0
, we only know the cell average value of
\bm{u}_{\ell}^{h}
. To recover uniform second-order accuracy, we employ Fromm’s method to approximate slopes with center differences to find edge values and then apply upwind fluxes at cell interfaces. Let us assume a uniform mesh size
h
. Then

(4.7)

\bm{u}^{h}_{\ell}\big|_{I_{i}}=\overline{\bm{u}}_{\ell,i}+\frac{\overline{\bm{u}}_{\ell,i+1}-\overline{\bm{u}}_{\ell,i-1}}{2h}(z-z_{i}),\quad{\ell>0},

so that for
\ell>0
,

(4.8)

\bm{u}^{h-}_{\ell}\big|_{z_{i+\frac{1}{2}}}=\overline{\bm{u}}_{\ell,i}+\frac{\overline{\bm{u}}_{\ell,i+1}-\overline{\bm{u}}_{\ell,i-1}}{4},\quad\bm{u}^{h+}_{\ell}\big|_{z_{i+\frac{1}{2}}}=\overline{\bm{u}}_{\ell,i+1}-\frac{\overline{\bm{u}}_{\ell,i+2}-\overline{\bm{u}}_{\ell,i}}{4}.

Hence, for
\ell>0
,

(4.9a)

\{\!\!\{\bm{u}^{h}_{\ell}\}\!\!\}_{i+\frac{1}{2}}=\frac{-\overline{\bm{u}}_{\ell,i+2}+5\overline{\bm{u}}_{\ell,i+1}+5\overline{\bm{u}}_{\ell,i}-\overline{\bm{u}}_{\ell,i-1}}{8},

(4.9b)

[\![\bm{u}^{h}_{\ell}]\!]_{i+\frac{1}{2}}=\frac{-\overline{\bm{u}}_{\ell,i+2}+3\overline{\bm{u}}_{\ell,i+1}-3\overline{\bm{u}}_{\ell,i}+\overline{\bm{u}}_{\ell,i-1}}{4},

(4.9c)

[\![\bm{u}^{h}_{\ell}]\!]_{i-\frac{1}{2}}=\frac{\overline{\bm{u}}_{\ell,i+1}-3\overline{\bm{u}}_{\ell,i}+3\overline{\bm{u}}_{\ell,i-1}-\overline{\bm{u}}_{\ell,i-2}}{4}.

Combining (
4.9
), the mixed spherical harmonics DG method for the 1-D geometry setting can be cast as follows.

Problem 4.1
.

Find
\bm{u}^{h}\in\bm{V}^{h}:=\bm{V}^{h,1}_{0}\times\displaystyle\prod_{\ell=1}^{N}\bm{V}^{h,0}_{\ell}
such that

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{M}\Bigg\{\frac{1}{h}\Big(\bm{B}\{\!\!\{\bm{u}^{h}\}\!\!\}_{i+\frac{1}{2}}-\frac{1}{2}\bm{D}[\![\bm{u}^{h}]\!]_{i+\frac{1}{2}}\Big)-\frac{1}{h}\Big(\bm{B}\{\!\!\{\bm{u}^{h}\}\!\!\}_{i-\frac{1}{2}}+\frac{1}{2}\bm{D}[\![\bm{u}^{h}]\!]_{i-\frac{1}{2}}\Big)

(4.10a)

\displaystyle+\varepsilon\sigma_{\mathrm{a}}\overline{\bm{u}}^{h}_{i}+\left(\frac{\sigma_{\mathrm{t}}}{\varepsilon}-\varepsilon\sigma_{\mathrm{a}}\right)\bm{R}\overline{\bm{u}}^{h}_{i}\Bigg\}=\sum_{i=1}^{M}\varepsilon\overline{\bm{f}}_{i},

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{M}\Bigg\{\frac{3}{h}\Big(\bm{B}\{\!\!\{\bm{u}^{h}\}\!\!\}_{i+\frac{1}{2}}-\frac{1}{2}\bm{D}[\![\bm{u}^{h}]\!]_{i+\frac{1}{2}}\Big)_{0}+\frac{3}{h}\Big(\bm{B}\{\!\!\{\bm{u}^{h}\}\!\!\}_{i-\frac{1}{2}}+\frac{1}{2}\bm{D}[\![\bm{u}^{h}]\!]_{i-\frac{1}{2}}\Big)_{0}

(4.10b)

\displaystyle-\frac{6}{h}(\bm{B}\overline{\bm{u}}^{h}_{i})_{0}+\varepsilon\sigma_{\mathrm{a}}(\widetilde{\bm{u}}^{h}_{i})_{0}+\left(\frac{\sigma_{\mathrm{t}}}{\varepsilon}-\varepsilon\sigma_{\mathrm{a}}\right)\bm{R}(\widetilde{\bm{u}}^{h}_{i})_{0}\Bigg\}=\sum_{i=1}^{M}\varepsilon\big(\widetilde{\bm{f}}_{i}\big)_{0},

where
\{\!\!\{\bm{u}^{h}\}\!\!\}_{i+\frac{1}{2}}
and
[\![\bm{u}^{h}]\!]_{i\pm\frac{1}{2}}
are computed through (
4.9
).

In the proposed hybrid DG/FV method, solutions in each cell are still represented by at least linear polynomials for the
\ell=0
moment and constants for higher
\ell>0
moments. However, the values at each element interface for computing the upwind numerical fluxes of higher moments are determined by Fromm’s method.
Therefore, it can be considered as a further generalization of the DG method with heterogeneous polynomial spaces (
Problem

2.1
) and be recast as follows.

Problem 4.2
.

Find
\bm{u}^{\varepsilon,h}\in\bm{V}^{h,k_{0}}_{0}\times\bm{V}^{h,0}_{1}\times\cdots\times\bm{V}^{h,0}_{N}
such that for all
\bm{v}^{h}\in\bm{V}^{h,k_{0}}_{0}\times\bm{V}^{h,0}_{1}\times\cdots\times\bm{V}^{h,0}_{N}
,
k_{0}\geq 1
,

(4.11)

\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}^{h}}\bigg\{\left(\mathbf{n}^{K}\cdot\overrightarrow{\bm{A}\bm{u}^{\varepsilon,h}},\bm{v}^{h-}\right)_{\partial K}-\sum_{i=1}^{3}(\bm{A}^{i}\bm{u}^{\varepsilon,h},\partial_{i}\bm{v}^{h})_{K}+\left(\bm{Q}\bm{u}^{\varepsilon,h},\bm{v}^{h}\right)_{K}-\varepsilon(\bm{f},\bm{v}^{h})_{K}\bigg\}=0,

where the numerical trace
\mathbf{n}^{K}\cdot\overrightarrow{\bm{A}\bm{u}^{\varepsilon,h}}
is defined by the formula (
2.30
), with the boundary values of each element (used to evaluate jumps and averages)
\bm{u}^{\varepsilon,h}|_{\partial K}
computed by Fromm’s method.

6.
Concluding remarks

In this paper, we have performed an asymptotic analysis on a spherical harmonics DG discretization for the radiative transport equation to show that uniform convergence with respect to the scaling parameter
\varepsilon
can be achieved using piece-wise linear (or higher order) elements only for the degree zero moment while using constant elements to approximate higher moments. Based on this observation, we propose a new DG method with heterogeneous polynomial spaces for different moments. This results in a method that still captures the diffusion limit when
\varepsilon\to 0
, but uses fewer degrees of freedom in physical space. However, the accuracy of the method is reduced when
\varepsilon\sim 1
.

To overcome the reduction of uniform accuracy in the heterogeneous DG method, we propose a hybrid discontinuous Galerkin/finite volume scheme in which FV reconstructions are used for the higher moments.
The new method is asymptotic preserving, and it maintains the convergence rate of the conventional DG method, while still using fewer degrees of freedom. Some numerical experiments, including the comparison with other techniques, are carried out to test the performance of the scheme and show the effectiveness of the method.

Appendix A
P_{N}
equations for reduced geometries model

In this section, we connect the general
P_{N}
equations (
2.16
) to the reduced geometry equations in
Section

5
. We focus on the steady-state equations, but the time-dependent versions simulated in
Section

5
can be derived in a similar fashion.

Recall the components of
\bm{\omega}
in (
2.7
) expressed in terms of the polar angle
\theta
and azimuthal angle
\varphi
. The normalized, complex-valued spherical harmonics are

(A.1)

Y_{\ell}^{\kappa}(\bm{\omega})=\sqrt{\frac{2\ell+1}{4\pi}}\frac{(\ell-\kappa)!}{(\ell+\kappa)!}P_{\ell}^{\kappa}(\mu)e^{i\kappa\varphi},

where
\mu=\cos\theta
and the associated Legendre functions are given by

(A.2)

P_{\ell}^{\kappa}(\mu)=\begin{cases}(-1)^{\kappa}(1-\mu^{2})^{m/2}\frac{d^{(\kappa)}}{d\mu^{(\kappa)}}P_{\ell}(\mu),&\kappa\geq 0,\\
(-1)^{|\kappa|}\frac{(\ell+\kappa)!}{(\ell-\kappa)!}P_{\ell}^{|\kappa|},&\kappa<0,\end{cases}

and
P_{\ell}\colon[-1,1]\to\mathbb{R}
is the degree
\ell
Legendre polynomial, normalized so that
\int_{-1}^{1}P_{\ell}P_{\ell^{\prime}}d\mu=\frac{2}{2\ell+1}\delta_{\ell,\ell^{\prime}}
. The normalized, real-valued spherical harmonics are

(A.3)

m_{\ell}^{\kappa}(\bm{\omega})=\begin{cases}\sqrt{2}(-1)^{\kappa}\operatorname{Im}[Y_{\ell}^{|\kappa|}],&\kappa<0,\\
Y_{\ell}^{0},&\kappa=0,\\
\sqrt{2}(-1)^{\kappa}\operatorname{Re}[Y_{\ell}^{|\kappa|}],&\kappa>0.\end{cases}

A.1.
One-dimensional slab geometry

Recall the slab geometry RTE in (
2.8
). The equation assumes that
u
and
f
are independent of
x
,
y
and
\varphi
. If
u_{\mathrm{PN}}
is also assumed to be independent of
x
,
y
and
\varphi
, then for any
\kappa\neq 0
,

(A.4)

u_{\ell}^{\kappa}=\int_{-1}^{1}u_{\mathrm{PN}}(z,\mu)\left(\int_{0}^{2\pi}m_{\ell}^{\kappa}(\bm{\omega})d\varphi\right)d\mu=0.

Thus the only non-zero moments of
u
are with
\kappa=0
, i.e., with respect to
Y_{\ell}^{0}(\bm{\omega})=\sqrt{\frac{2\ell+1}{4\pi}}P_{\ell}(\mu)
.
A common convention
[
27
, Appendix D]
for slab geometry is to express the
P_{N}
equations in terms of

(A.5)

v_{\ell}=\frac{1}{2}\int_{-1}^{1}u_{\mathrm{PN}}P_{\ell}(\mu)\,\mathrm{d}\mu=\frac{1}{\sqrt{4\pi}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\ell+1}}u_{\ell}^{0},

so that

(A.6)

u_{\mathrm{PN}}=\sum_{\ell=0}^{N}(2\ell+1)v_{\ell}P_{\ell}.

Setting this expansion into (
2.8
) and integrating the result against
\frac{1}{2}P_{\ell}
over
\mu\in[-1,1]
give the following equation for
\bm{v}=\begin{bmatrix}v_{0}&\cdots&v_{N}\end{bmatrix}^{\mathsf{T}}
:

(A.7)

\bm{B}\partial_{z}\bm{v}+\varepsilon\sigma_{\mathrm{a}}\bm{v}+\left(\frac{\sigma_{\mathrm{t}}}{\varepsilon}-\varepsilon\sigma_{\mathrm{a}}\right){\bm{R}}\bm{v}=\varepsilon{\bm{f}},

where
f_{\ell}=\frac{1}{2}\int_{-1}^{1}fP_{\ell}(\mu)\,\mathrm{d}\mu
,
R_{\ell}=\delta_{\ell,0}
, and for
0\leq\ell,\ell^{\prime}\leq N
, the matrix
\bm{B}
has elements

(A.8)

\bm{B}_{\ell,\ell^{\prime}}=\frac{2\ell^{\prime}+1}{2}\int_{-1}^{1}P_{\ell}(\mu)P_{\ell^{\prime}}(\mu)d\mu=\frac{\ell+1}{2\ell+1}\delta_{\ell+1,\ell^{\prime}}+\frac{\ell}{2\ell+1}\delta_{\ell-1,\ell^{\prime}}.

The matrix
\bm{B}
is the flux matrix that appears in (
5.1
) when
N=1
and in (
5.3
) when
N=3
.

A.2.
2-D plane-parallel model

We summarize here the approach used in
[
5
]
to derive the
P_{N}
equation for the 2-D plane-parallel model.
Let us assume that
\sigma_{\mathrm{t}}
,
\sigma_{\mathrm{a}}
, and
f
do not depend on
y
, are the functions only of
x
,
z
,
\theta
, and
\varphi
, and are even in
\varphi-\pi
. Furthermore, the solution
u
is independent of
y
. Then obviously, the solution is a function only of
x
,
z
,
\theta
, and
\varphi
. Set
\mu=\cos\theta
, and the time-dependent scaled radiative transfer equation can be written in the following form

(A.9)

\sqrt{1-\mu^{2}}\cos\varphi\frac{\partial u}{\partial x}+\mu\frac{\partial u}{\partial z}+\frac{\sigma_{\mathrm{t}}}{\varepsilon}u=\left(\frac{\sigma_{\mathrm{t}}}{\varepsilon}-\varepsilon\sigma_{\mathrm{a}}\right)\left\langle u\right\rangle+\varepsilon f,

where
\mu=\cos\theta\in[0,1]
,
\varphi\in[0,2\pi)
,
u=u(x,z,\mu,\varphi)
, and
f=f(x,z,\mu,\varphi)
.
It is easy to show that if
u(x,z,\varphi,\mu)
is a solution of (
A.9
), then so too is
u(x,z,2\pi-\varphi,\mu)
.
Hence
u
is also an even function of
\varphi-\mathrm{\pi}
.

In lieu of real-valued spherical harmonics, we employ the complex-valued normalized spherical harmonics
Y_{\ell}^{\kappa}
of degree
\ell
and order
\kappa
for
-\ell\leq\kappa\leq\ell
and
0\leq\ell\leq N
.
We have the following recursive relations

(A.10a)

\displaystyle\mu Y_{\ell}^{k}

\displaystyle=A_{\ell}^{k}Y_{\ell+1}^{k}+B_{\ell}^{k}Y_{\ell-1}^{k},

(A.10b)

\displaystyle\sqrt{1-\mu^{2}}\cos\varphi Y_{\ell}^{k}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(-C_{\ell}^{k}Y_{\ell+1}^{k+1}+D_{\ell}^{k}Y_{\ell-1}^{k+1}+E_{\ell}^{k}Y_{\ell+1}^{k-1}-F_{\ell}^{k}Y_{\ell-1}^{k-1}\right),

with

(A.11)

\displaystyle A_{\ell}^{k}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{(\ell-k+1)(\ell+k+1)}{(2\ell+3)(2\ell+1)}},

\displaystyle\qquad B_{\ell}^{k}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{(\ell-k)(\ell+k)}{(2\ell+1)(2\ell-1)}},

(A.12)

\displaystyle C_{\ell}^{k}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{(\ell+k+1)(\ell+k+2)}{(2\ell+3)(2\ell+1)}},

\displaystyle D_{\ell}^{k}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{(\ell-k)(\ell-k-1)}{(2\ell+1)(2\ell-1)}},

(A.13)

\displaystyle E_{\ell}^{k}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{(\ell-k+1)(\ell-k+2)}{(2\ell+3)(2\ell+1)}},

\displaystyle F_{\ell}^{k}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{(\ell+k)(\ell+k-1)}{(2\ell+1)(2\ell-1)}}.

Similar to the procedure of obtaining the one-dimensional
P_{N}
equation (
A.7
), we multiply (
A.9
) by the complex-valued normalized spherical harmonics
Y_{\ell}^{\kappa}
and employ the recursive relations (
A.10
) to obtain

(A.14)

\frac{1}{2}\partial_{x}\left(-C_{\ell-1}^{\kappa-1}u_{\ell-1}^{\kappa-1}+D_{\ell+1}^{\kappa-1}u_{\ell+1}^{\kappa-1}+E_{\ell-1}^{\kappa+1}u_{\ell-1}^{\kappa+1}-F_{\ell+1}^{\kappa+1}u_{\ell+1}^{\kappa+1}\right)\\
+\partial_{z}\left(A_{\ell-1}^{\kappa}u_{\ell-1}^{\kappa}+B_{\ell+1}^{\kappa}u_{\ell+1}^{\kappa}\right)+\varepsilon\sigma_{\mathrm{a}}u_{\ell}^{\kappa}+\left(\frac{\sigma_{\mathrm{t}}}{\varepsilon}-\varepsilon\sigma_{\mathrm{a}}\right)(1-\delta_{\ell 0}\delta_{\kappa 0})u_{\ell}^{\kappa}=\varepsilon f_{\ell}^{\kappa},

for
0\leq\ell\leq N
and
-\ell\leq\kappa\leq\ell
with
f_{\ell}^{\kappa}=4\mathrm{\pi}\left\langle fY_{\ell}^{\kappa}\right\rangle
.
Note that the real and imaginary parts of the above equation are decoupled. Furthermore, since
f
is an even function of
\varphi-\mathrm{\pi}
, we have
\left\langle fP_{\ell}^{k}(\mu)\sin(k(\varphi-\mathrm{\pi}))\right\rangle=0
. Therefore,
f_{\ell}^{\kappa}
is real, and we only need to solve for the real parts of the equation (
A.14
).

We denote
\bm{u}
as a vector of spherical harmonics moments of
u
grouped in blocks of constant
\kappa

(A.15)

\bm{u}=\begin{bmatrix}\left(\bm{u}^{-N}\right)^{\mathsf{T}}&\left(\bm{u}^{-N+1}\right)^{\mathsf{T}}&\cdots&\left(\bm{u}^{-1}\right)^{\mathsf{T}}&\left(\bm{u}^{0}\right)^{\mathsf{T}}&\left(\bm{u}^{1}\right)^{\mathsf{T}}&\cdots&\left(\bm{u}^{N}\right)^{\mathsf{T}}\end{bmatrix}^{\mathsf{T}}

with
\bm{u}^{\kappa}=\begin{bmatrix}u_{|\kappa|}^{\kappa}&u_{|\kappa|+1}^{\kappa}&\cdots&u_{N}^{\kappa}\end{bmatrix}^{\mathsf{T}}
.
Then the matrix form of the 2D
P_{N}
equation (
A.14
) can be written as

(A.16)

\bm{A}_{x}\partial_{x}\bm{u}+\bm{A}_{z}\partial_{z}\bm{u}+\varepsilon\sigma_{\mathrm{a}}\bm{u}+\left(\frac{\sigma_{\mathrm{t}}}{\varepsilon}-\varepsilon\sigma_{\mathrm{a}}\right)\bm{R}\bm{u}=\varepsilon\bm{f},

which has the same format as (
2.16
) if we set
\bm{A}_{y}=0
.

The number of unknowns in (
A.16
) can be further reduced due to the special structure of matrix
\bm{A}_{x}
and
\bm{R}
.
First, if
\bm{u}

is solution of (
A.16

(A.17)

\bm{u}^{\prime}=\begin{bmatrix}\left(\bm{u}^{N}\right)^{\mathsf{T}}&\cdots&\left(\bm{u}^{1}\right)^{\mathsf{T}}&\left(\bm{u}^{0}\right)^{\mathsf{T}}&\left(\bm{u}^{-1}\right)^{\mathsf{T}}&\cdots&\left(\bm{u}^{-N}\right)^{\mathsf{T}}\end{bmatrix}^{\mathsf{T}}

is also a solution. Therefore, by the uniqueness

(A.18)

\bm{u}^{\kappa}=\bm{u}^{-\kappa},\quad\kappa=0,1,\cdots,N.

In particular, since
u_{\ell}^{-1}=u_{\ell}^{1}
,
C_{\ell}^{-1}=E_{\ell}^{1}
, and
D_{\ell}^{-1}=F_{\ell}^{1}

u_{\ell}^{-1}
terms from (

) for the

(A.19)

\partial_{x}\left(E_{\ell-1}^{1}u_{\ell-1}^{1}-F_{\ell+1}^{1}u_{\ell+1}^{1}\right)\\

This equation, together with the equations for
\kappa>0

(A.20)

\frac{1}{2}\partial_{x}\left(-C_{\ell-1}^{\kappa-1}u_{\ell-1}^{\kappa-1}+D_{\ell+1}^{\kappa-1}u_{\ell+1}^{\kappa-1}+E_{\ell-1}^{\kappa+1}u_{\ell-1}^{\kappa+1}-F_{\ell+1}^{\kappa+1}u_{\ell+1}^{\kappa+1}\right)\\
+\partial_{z}\left(A_{\ell-1}^{\kappa}u_{\ell-1}^{\kappa}+B_{\ell+1}^{\kappa}u_{\ell+1}^{\kappa}\right)+\varepsilon\sigma_{\mathrm{a}}u_{\ell}^{\kappa}+\left(\frac{\sigma_{\mathrm{t}}}{\varepsilon}-\varepsilon\sigma_{\mathrm{a}}\right)u_{\ell}^{\kappa}=\varepsilon f_{\ell}^{\kappa},\;\kappa>0

decouples the unknowns

from the rest and can be solved independently.

\displaystyle\{\!\!\{\bm{u}^{h}_{0}\}\!\!\}_{i+\frac{1}{2},j}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(\overline{\bm{u}}_{0,i+1,j}-\widehat{\bm{u}}_{0,i+1,j}+\widecheck{\bm{u}}_{0,i+1,j}\eta_{j}-\widetilde{\bm{u}}_{0,i+1,j}\eta_{j}\right)

\displaystyle{}\hskip 36.135pt+\frac{1}{2}\left(\overline{\bm{u}}_{0,i,j}+\widehat{\bm{u}}_{0,i,j}+\widecheck{\bm{u}}_{0,i,j}\eta_{j}+\widetilde{\bm{u}}_{0,i,j}\eta_{j}\right),

(B.7d)

\displaystyle[\![\bm{u}^{h}_{0}]\!]_{i,j+\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle=\overline{\bm{u}}_{0,i,j+1}+\widehat{\bm{u}}_{0,i,j+1}\xi_{i}-\widecheck{\bm{u}}_{0,i,j+1}-\widetilde{\bm{u}}_{0,i,j+1}\xi_{i}

\displaystyle{}\hskip 36.135pt-\left(\overline{\bm{u}}_{0,i,j}+\widehat{\bm{u}}_{0,i,j}\xi_{i}+\widecheck{\bm{u}}_{0,i,j}+\widetilde{\bm{u}}_{0,i,j}\xi_{i}\right),

(B.7e)

\displaystyle[\![\bm{u}^{h}]\!]_{0,i,j-\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle=-\overline{\bm{u}}_{0,i,j}-\widehat{\bm{u}}_{0,i,j}\xi_{i}+\widecheck{\bm{u}}_{0,i,j}+\widetilde{\bm{u}}_{0,i,j}\xi_{i}

\displaystyle{}\hskip 36.135pt+\left(\overline{\bm{u}}_{0,i,j-1}+\widehat{\bm{u}}_{0,i,j-1}\xi_{i}+\widecheck{\bm{u}}_{0,i,j-1}+\widetilde{\bm{u}}_{0,i,j-1}\xi_{i}\right),

(B.7f)

\displaystyle\{\!\!\{\bm{u}^{h}_{0}\}\!\!\}_{i,j+\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}(\overline{\bm{u}}_{0,i,j+1}+\widehat{\bm{u}}_{0,i,j+1}\xi_{i}-\widecheck{\bm{u}}_{0,i,j+1}-\widetilde{\bm{u}}_{0,i,j+1}\xi_{i}\big)

\displaystyle{}\hskip 36.135pt+\frac{1}{2}\left(\overline{\bm{u}}_{0,i,j}+\widehat{\bm{u}}_{0,i,j}\xi_{i}+\widecheck{\bm{u}}_{0,i,j}+\widetilde{\bm{u}}_{0,i,j}\xi_{i}\right).

For
\ell>0
, we only know the cell average value of
\bm{u}_{\ell}^{h}
. As in the slab geometry case, we employ Fromm’s method to approximate slopes with center differences to find edge values and then apply upwind fluxes at cell interfaces. Let us assume a uniform mesh size
h
. Applying Fromm’s method to
\bm{u}_{\ell}
on each cell gives

(B.8)

\bm{u}^{h}_{\ell}=\overline{\bm{u}}_{\ell,i,j}+\frac{\overline{\bm{u}}_{\ell,i+1,j}-\overline{\bm{u}}_{\ell,i-1,j}}{4}\xi_{i}+\frac{\overline{\bm{u}}_{\ell,i,j+1}-\overline{\bm{u}}_{\ell,i,j-1}}{4}\eta_{j}.

Therefore the edge values for
\ell>0
are

(B.9a)

\displaystyle\bm{u}^{-}_{\ell,i+\frac{1}{2},j}

\displaystyle=\overline{\bm{u}}_{\ell,i,j}+\frac{\overline{\bm{u}}_{\ell,i+1,j}-\overline{\bm{u}}_{\ell,i-1,j}}{4}+\frac{\overline{\bm{u}}_{i,j+1}-\overline{\bm{u}}_{\ell,i,j-1}}{4}\eta_{j},

(B.9b)

\displaystyle\bm{u}^{+}_{\ell,i+\frac{1}{2},j}

\displaystyle=\overline{\bm{u}}_{\ell,i+1,j}-\frac{\overline{\bm{u}}_{\ell,i+2,j}-\overline{\bm{u}}_{\ell,i,j}}{4}+\frac{\overline{\bm{u}}_{\ell,i+1,j+1}-\overline{\bm{u}}_{\ell,i+1,j-1}}{4}\eta_{j},

(B.9c)

\displaystyle\bm{u}^{-}_{\ell,i,j+\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle=\overline{\bm{u}}_{\ell,i,j}+\frac{\overline{\bm{u}}_{\ell,i+1,j}-\overline{\bm{u}}_{\ell,i-1,j}}{4}\xi_{i}+\frac{\overline{\bm{u}}_{\ell,i,j+1}-\overline{\bm{u}}_{\ell,i,j-1}}{4},

(B.9d)

\displaystyle\bm{u}^{+}_{\ell,i,j+\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle=\overline{\bm{u}}_{\ell,i,j+1}+\frac{\overline{\bm{u}}_{\ell,i+1,j+1}-\overline{\bm{u}}_{\ell,i-1,j+1}}{4}\xi_{i}-\frac{\overline{\bm{u}}_{\ell,i,j+2}-\overline{\bm{u}}_{\ell,i,j}}{4},

and the average and jump values for
\ell>0
are

\displaystyle[\![\bm{u}^{h}_{\ell}]\!]_{i+\frac{1}{2},j}

\displaystyle=\frac{-\overline{\bm{u}}_{\ell,i+2,j}+3\overline{\bm{u}}_{i+1,j}-3\overline{\bm{u}}_{i,j}+\overline{\bm{u}}_{\ell,i-1,j}}{4}

(B.10a)

\displaystyle{}\qquad+\frac{\overline{\bm{u}}_{\ell,i+1,j+1}-\overline{\bm{u}}_{\ell,i,j+1}-\overline{\bm{u}}_{\ell,i+1,j-1}+\overline{\bm{u}}_{\ell,i,j-1}}{4}\eta_{j},

\displaystyle[\![\bm{u}^{h}_{\ell}]\!]_{i-\frac{1}{2},j}

\displaystyle=\frac{\overline{\bm{u}}_{\ell,i+1,j}-3\overline{\bm{u}}_{\ell,i,j}+3\overline{\bm{u}}_{\ell,i-1,j}-\overline{\bm{u}}_{\ell,i-2,j}}{4}

(B.10b)

\displaystyle{}\qquad-\frac{\overline{\bm{u}}_{\ell,i,j+1}-\overline{\bm{u}}_{\ell,i-1,j+1}-\overline{\bm{u}}_{\ell,i,j-1}+\overline{\bm{u}}_{\ell,i-1,j-1}}{4}\eta_{j},

\displaystyle\{\!\!\{\bm{u}^{h}_{\ell}\}\!\!\}_{i+\frac{1}{2},j}

\displaystyle=\frac{-\overline{\bm{u}}_{\ell,i+2,j}+5\overline{\bm{u}}_{\ell,i+1,j}+5\overline{\bm{u}}_{\ell,i,j}-\overline{\bm{u}}_{\ell,i-1,j}}{8}

(B.10c)

\displaystyle{}\qquad+\frac{\overline{\bm{u}}_{\ell,i+1,j+1}+\overline{\bm{u}}_{\ell,i,j+1}-\overline{\bm{u}}_{\ell,i+1,j-1}-\overline{\bm{u}}_{\ell,i,j-1}}{8}\eta_{j},

\displaystyle[\![\bm{u}^{h}_{\ell}]\!]_{i,j+\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{-\overline{\bm{u}}_{\ell,i,j+2}+3\overline{\bm{u}}_{\ell,i,j+1}-3\overline{\bm{u}}_{\ell,i,j}+\overline{\bm{u}}_{\ell,i,j-1}}{4}

(B.10d)

\displaystyle{}\qquad+\frac{\overline{\bm{u}}_{\ell,i+1,j+1}-\overline{\bm{u}}_{\ell,i+1,j}-\overline{\bm{u}}_{i-1,j+1}+\overline{\bm{u}}_{\ell,i-1,j}}{4}\xi_{i},

\displaystyle[\![\bm{u}^{h}_{\ell}]\!]_{i,j-\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\overline{\bm{u}}_{\ell,i,j+1}-3\overline{\bm{u}}_{\ell,i,j}+3\overline{\bm{u}}_{\ell,i,j-1}-\overline{\bm{u}}_{\ell,i,j-2}}{4}

(B.10e)

\displaystyle{}\qquad-\frac{\overline{\bm{u}}_{i+1,j}-\overline{\bm{u}}_{\ell,i+1,j-1}-\overline{\bm{u}}_{\ell,i-1,j}+\overline{\bm{u}}_{\ell,i-1,j-1}}{4}\xi_{i},

\displaystyle\{\!\!\{\bm{u}^{h}_{\ell}\}\!\!\}_{i,j+\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{-\overline{\bm{u}}_{\ell,i,j+2}+5\overline{\bm{u}}_{\ell,i,j+1}+5\overline{\bm{u}}_{\ell,i,j}-\overline{\bm{u}}_{\ell,i,j-1}}{8}

(B.10f)

\displaystyle{}\qquad+\frac{\overline{\bm{u}}_{\ell,i+1,j+1}+\overline{\bm{u}}_{\ell,i+1,j}-\overline{\bm{u}}_{\ell,i-1,j+1}-\overline{\bm{u}}_{\ell,i-1,j}}{8}\xi_{i}.
