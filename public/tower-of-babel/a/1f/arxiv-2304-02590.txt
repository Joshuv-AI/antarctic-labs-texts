The Stable Matching Lattice under Changed Preferences, and Associated Algorithms
Rohith Reddy Gangam, Tung Mai, Nitya Raju, Vijay V. Vazirani

Abstract
[ MV18 ] introduced a fundamental new algorithmic question on stable matching, namely finding a matching that is stable under two “nearby” instances, where “nearby” meant that in going from instance A A to B B , only one agent changes its preference list. By first establishing a sequence of structural results on the lattices of A A and B B , [ MV18 ] and [ GMRV22 ] settled all algorithmic questions related to this case. The current paper essentially settles the general case. Assume that instance B B is obtained from A A , both on n n workers and n n firms, via changes in the preferences of p p workers and q q firms. If so, we will denote the change by ( p , q ) (p,q) . Thus [ MV18 , GMRV22 ] settled the case ( 0 , 1 ) (0,1) , since they adopt the convention that one firm changes its preferences. Let ℳ A \mathcal{M}_{A} and ℳ B \mathcal{M}_{B} be the sets of stable matchings of instances A A and B B , and let ℒ A \mathcal{L}_{A} and ℒ B \mathcal{L}_{B} be their lattices. Our results are: 1. For ( 0 , n ) (0,n) , ℳ A ∩ ℳ B \mathcal{M}_{A}\cap\mathcal{M}_{B} is a sublattice of ℒ A \mathcal{L}_{A} and of ℒ B \mathcal{L}_{B} . We can efficiently obtain the worker-optimal and firm-optimal stable matchings in ℳ A ∩ ℳ B \mathcal{M}_{A}\cap\mathcal{M}_{B} . We also obtain the associated partial order, as promised by Birkhoff’s Representation Theorem, and use it to enumerate these matchings with polynomial delay. 2. For ( 1 , n ) (1,n) , the only missing results are the partial order and enumeration. 3. We give an example with ( 2 , 2 ) (2,2) for which ℳ A ∩ ℳ B \mathcal{M}_{A}\cap\mathcal{M}_{B} fails to be a sublattice of ℒ A \mathcal{L}_{A} . In light of the fact that for ( n , n ) (n,n) , determining if ( ℳ A ∩ ℳ B ) = ∅ (\mathcal{M}_{A}\cap\mathcal{M}_{B})=\emptyset is NP-hard [ MO19 ] , a number of open questions arise; in particular, closing the gap between ( 2 , 2 ) (2,2) and ( n , n ) (n,n) .

Preliminaries

2.1
The stable matching problem and the lattice of stable matchings

A stable matching problem takes as input a set of
n
workers,
\mathcal{W}=\{w_{1},w_{2},\ldots,w_{n}\}
and a set of
n
firms,
\mathcal{F}=\{f_{1},f_{2},\ldots,f_{n}\}
, collectively referred to as agents; Each agent
a\in\cal W\cup\cal F
has a preference profile (
>_{a}
) that is a complete preference ranking (a strict total order) over the agents of the other type. The notation
w_{i}>_{f}w_{j}
indicates that firm
f
strictly prefers
w_{i}
to
w_{j}
in its preference list. A similar notation is used for worker preferences.

A matching
M
is a one-to-one correspondence between
\mathcal{W}
and
\mathcal{F}
. For each pair
(w,f)\in M
,
w
is called the partner of
f
in
M
(or
M
-partner) and vice versa. For a matching
M
, a pair
(w,f)\not\in M
is said to be a
blocking pair
if
they prefer each other to their partners, that is if
f>_{w}M(w)
and
w>_{f}M(f)
. A matching
M
is
stable
if it has no blocking pairs.

Let
M
and
M^{\prime}
be two stable matchings. We say that
M

dominates

M^{\prime}
, denoted

M\preceq M^{\prime}
, if every worker weakly prefers its partner in
M
to
M^{\prime}
. Define the relation
predecessor
as the transitive closure of dominates. For two stable matchings,
M_{1}
and
M_{2}
, stable matching
M
is a
common predecessor
of
M_{1}
and
M_{2}
if it is a predecessor of both
M_{1}
and
M_{2}
. Furthermore,
M
is a
lowest common predecessor
of
M_{1}
and
M_{2}
if it is a common predecessor
M_{1}
and
M_{2}
, and there is no other common predecessor
M^{\prime}
such that
M
is a predecessor of
M^{\prime}
. Analogously, one can define the notions of
successor
and
highest common successor
. This dominance partial order has the following property: For any two stable
M_{1}
and
M_{2}
, their lowest common predecessor is unique and their highest common successor is unique, i.e., the partial order is a
lattice
; the former is called the
meet
, denoted
M_{1}\wedge M_{2}
, and the latter is called the
join
, denoted
M_{1}\vee M_{2}
.

One can show that
M_{1}\wedge M_{2}
, is the matching that results when each worker chooses its more preferred partner from
M_{1}
and
M_{2}
; it is easy to show that this matching is also stable. Interestingly,
M_{1}\wedge M_{2}
is also the matching that results when each firm chooses its lesser preferred partner from
M_{1}
and
M_{2}
. Similarly,
M_{1}\vee M_{2}
is the matching that results when each worker(/firm) chooses its less(/more) preferred partner from
M_{1}
and
M_{2}
; this matching is also stable. Because of this interesting duality of the stable matching lattice, all the above relations can be equivalently defined in terms of firms, and the lattice properties will still hold true. Additionally, the lattice operations
join
and
meet
distribute, i.e.,
given three stable matchings
M,M^{\prime},M^{\prime\prime}
,

M\vee(M^{\prime}\wedge M^{\prime\prime})=(M\vee M^{\prime})\wedge(M\vee M^{\prime\prime})\ \ \mbox{and}\ \ M\wedge(M^{\prime}\vee M^{\prime\prime})=(M\wedge M^{\prime})\vee(M\wedge M^{\prime\prime}).

It is easy to see that the lattice must contain a matching,
M_{\top}
, that dominates all others and a matching
M_{\bot}
that is dominated by all others.
M_{\top}
is called the
worker-optimal stable matching
, since in it, each worker is matched to its most preferred firm among all stable matchings. This is also the
firm-pessimal stable matching
. Similarly,
M_{\bot}
is the
worker-pessimal
and
firm-optimal stable matching
. And since the number of matchings stable under any instance is finite, the stable matching lattice is always a
finite distributive lattice
.

2.2
Birkhoff’s Theorem, sublattices and compressions

Definition 1
.

A closed set of a poset is a set

S

S

It is easy to see that the family of closed sets (also called lower sets) of a partial order, say

Birkhoff’s theorem

Theorem 2
.

(Birkhoff
[

]

is isomorphic to

\Pi

A

such that for any two elements

\mathcal{L}

x,y\in\mathcal{L}_{m}
. A

is a subset of

that is a join and meet semi-sublattice of

.

The

of a poset is a directed graph with a vertex for each element in poset, and an edge from

and there is no

Let

Theorem 3
.

(
[
GMRV22

There is a one-to-one correspondence between the compressions of

The original Deferred Acceptance Algorithm

For the lattice of stable matchings, the partial order
\Pi
defined in Birkhoff’s Theorem has additional useful structural properties. First, its elements are rotations.
A
rotation
takes
r
matched worker-firm pairs in a fixed order, say

\{w_{0}f_{0},w_{1}f_{1},\ldots,w_{r-1}f_{r-1}\}
, and “cyclically” changes the matches of these
2r

agents. The number
r
, the
r
pairs, and the order among the pairs are so chosen that when a rotation is
applied to a stable matching containing all
r
pairs, the resulting matching is also stable;
moreover, there is no valid rotation on any subset of these
r
pairs, under
any ordering. Hence, a rotation can be viewed as a minimal
change to the current matching that results in a stable matching.

Any worker–firm pair,
(w,f)
,
belongs to at most one rotation. Consequently, the set
R
of rotations underlying
\Pi

satisfies
|R|
is
O(n^{2})
, and hence,
\Pi
is a succinct representation of
\mathcal{L}
; the
latter can be exponentially large.
\Pi
will be called the
rotation poset
for
\mathcal{L}
.

Second, the rotation poset helps traverse the lattice as follows.
For any closed set
S
of
\Pi
, the corresponding stable matching
M(S)
can be obtained
as follows: start from the worker-optimal matching in the lattice and apply
the rotations in set
S
, in any topological order consistent with
\Pi
. The resulting
matching will be
M(S)
. In particular, applying all rotations in
R
, starting from the worker-optimal
matching, leads to the firm-optimal matching.

The following process yields a rotation for a stable matching
M
. For a worker
w
let
s_{M}(w)
denote the first firm
f
on
w
’s list such that
f
strictly prefers
w
to its
M
-partner. Let
next_{M}(w)
denote the partner in
M
of firm
s_{M}(f)
. A
rotation

\rho

exposed
in
M
is an ordered list of pairs
\{w_{0}f_{0},w_{1}f_{1},\ldots,w_{r-1}f_{r-1}\}
such that for each
i
,
0\leq i\leq r-1
,
w_{i+1}
is
next_{M}(w_{i})
, where
i+1
is taken modulo
r
. In this paper, we assume that the subscript is taken modulo
r
whenever we mention a rotation. Notice that a rotation is cyclic and the sequence of pairs can be rotated.
M/\rho
is defined to be a matching in which each worker not in a pair of
\rho
stays matched to the same firm and each worker
w_{i}
in
\rho
is matched to
f_{i+1}=s_{M}(w_{i})
. It can be proven that
M/\rho
is also a stable matching. The transformation from
M
to
M/\rho
is called the
elimination
of
\rho
from
M
.

Lemma 8

(
[
GI89
]
, Theorem 2.5.4)
.

Every rotation appears exactly once in any sequence of elimination from
M_{0}
to
M_{z}
.

Let
\rho=\{w_{0}f_{0},w_{1}f_{1},\ldots,w_{r-1}f_{r-1}\}
be a rotation. For
0\leq i\leq r-1
, we say that
\rho

moves
w_{i}
from
f_{i}
to
f_{i+1}
, and
moves
f_{i}
from
w_{i}
to

f
is either
f_{i}
or is strictly between
f_{i}
and
f_{i+1}
in
w_{i}
’s list, then we say that
\rho

moves
w_{i}
below

\rho

moves
f_{i}
above

if
w
is
w_{i}
or between
w_{i}
and

in
f_{i}
’s list.

A.1
The rotation poset

A rotation
\rho^{\prime}
is said to
precede
another rotation
\rho

\rho^{\prime}\prec\rho

\rho^{\prime}
is eliminated in every sequence of eliminations from

to a stable matching in which

\rho^{\prime}

\rho^{\prime}

and

(

]

w

to
