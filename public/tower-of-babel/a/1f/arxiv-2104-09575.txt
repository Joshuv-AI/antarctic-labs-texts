Abstract
. Schrödinger operators with periodic (possibly complex-valued) potentials and discrete periodic operators (possibly with complex-valued entries) are considered, and in both cases the computational spectral problem is investigated: namely, under what conditions can a ‘one-size-fits-all’ algorithm for computing their spectra be devised? It is shown that for periodic banded matrices this can be done, as well as for Schrödinger operators with periodic potentials that are sufficiently smooth. In both cases implementable algorithms are provided, along with examples. For certain Schrödinger operators whose potentials may diverge at a single point (but are otherwise well-behaved) it is shown that there does not exist such an algorithm, though it is shown that the computation is possible if one allows for two successive limits.

1.
Introduction and Main Results

We study the computational spectral problem for periodic discrete operators, acting in
\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z})
, as well as Schrödinger operators with periodic potentials acting in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. We show that it is possible to compute their respective spectra as limits of finite-dimensional approximations. However, in the Schrödinger case this becomes
im
possible if the potential is allowed to be discontinuous at a
single
point (but otherwise it is smooth). More precisely, we prove:

Theorem 1.1

(Periodic banded matrices)
.

Let
B(\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z}))
denote the set of bounded operators on
\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z})
and let
\{e_{i}\}_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}
be a basis for
\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z})
. Let
\Omega^{\mathrm{per}}\subset B(\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z}))
be the class of banded, periodic operators with respect to the basis
\{e_{i}\}_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}
and
\Omega^{\mathrm{per}}_{N,b}
the subset of matrices with period
N
and bandwidth

. Then

(i)

there exists an algorithm that can compute the spectrum
\sigma(A)
of any
A\in\Omega^{\mathrm{per}}
as the limit of a sequence computable approximations;

(ii)

there exists an algorithm that can compute the spectrum
\sigma(A)
of any
A\in\Omega^{\mathrm{per}}_{N,b}
with guaranteed error bounds.

Theorem 1.2

(Schrödinger: good case)
.

For
d\in\mathbb{N}
define the class of potentials

\displaystyle\Omega^{\mathrm{Sch}}

\displaystyle:=\{V:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{C}\,|\,V\text{ is 1-periodic and }V|_{(0,1)^{d}}\in W^{1,p}((0,1)^{d})\text{ for some }p>d\},

and given

\displaystyle:=\{V\in\Omega^{\mathrm{Sch}}\,|\,\|V\|_{W^{1,p}((0,1)^{d})}\leq M\}.

(i)

there exists an algorithm that can compute the spectrum

(ii)

Theorem 1.3

(Schrödinger: bad case)
.

Let
x_{0}\in[0,1]
and let

\Omega^{\mathrm{Sch}}_{x_{0}}:=\left\{V:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}\,\middle|\,V\text{ is 1-periodic, }V(x_{0})=0\text{ and }V|_{[0,1]}\in L^{2}\big([0,1]\big)\cap C^{\infty}\big([0,1]\setminus\{x_{0}\}\big)\right\}.

Below, in Section
2

Informally, Theorems

and
1.2

1.1
) and pointwise evaluations of the potential (in Theorem

1.3
which implies that it is

to devise an algorithm that can compute the spectrum of

These statements are nontrivial. The existence of a ‘one-size-fits-all’ algorithm as in Theorems

and
1.2
is not obvious: while there are techniques for computing the spectrum of a given operator in each of the above cases – these are indeed well-studied problems – here we prove the existence of a single algorithm (which can be coded) that can handle any input from the given class, without any additional

information. Moreover, in the cases of
\Omega^{\mathrm{per}}_{N,b}
and
\Omega^{\mathrm{Sch}}_{p,M}
there are even guaranteed error bounds.
Conversely, the non-existence result of Theorem

is also not obvious: we prove that

of what operations are allowed, as long as the algorithm can only read a finite amount of information at each iteration, there will

be a potential for which the computation will fail.
In the spirit of the

Note that although the class of potentials

is still a regular point
for the differential equation
[
9

1.2.
Periodic Operators

Schrödinger equations with periodic potentials have been the subject of study since the earliest days of quantum mechanics, perhaps most famously
for the Bethe-Sommerfeld Conjecture
[
35
]
, which states that the number of gaps in the essential spectrum is finite in dimensions
\geq 2
. After
more than seventy years this conjecture was finally proved in complete generality for Schrödinger operators in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
by Parnovski
[
26
]
.
In dimensions 2 and 3 the conjecture had already been proved by Popov and Skriganov
[
27
]
and Skriganov
[
34
]
respectively, and in
dimension 4 by Helffer and Mohamed
[
21
]
.

Beyond these results in mathematical analysis, since the 1990s interest in periodic problems has grown rapidly in the
applied analysis and computational mathematics literature, partly driven by models of photonic crystals. These models are typically based on time-harmonic
Maxwell equations or upon second order elliptic equations with periodic coefficients. Figotin and Kuchment
[
15
,
16
,
17
]
give
particularly thorough analyses of some of these models, showing that already in these cases with piecewise constant coefficients the associated operators may
possess an arbitrarily large number of spectral gaps.

For a periodic problem with some particular coefficients, often the first question of interest is whether it has any spectral gaps at all. Numerical methods
may be used to obtain some preliminary evidence, and are almost always based on the Floquet-Bloch decomposition. The fact that the coefficients are
often only piecewise continuous requires substantial effort to be given to adaptive meshing, see Giani and Graham
[
18
]
, although the continuous
variation of the quasi-momentum over the Brillouin zone means that some of the effort can be recycled from one quasi-momentum to the next. Despite the substantial
computational cost, Floquet-Bloch techniques can sometimes be used to go beyond preliminary evidence, and have been combined with interval arithmetic to
obtain algorithms which yield computer-assisted proofs of existence of spectral gaps for a wide class of problems with coefficients expressed in terms
of elementary functions, see Hoang, Plum and Wieners
[
22
]
.
The literature for periodic problems which are not self-adjoint is much less extensive. For the ODE case, the work of Rofe-Beketov
[
30
]
is generally
the starting point for any research on this topic.

The discrete case encompasses numerous different directions of research. Typical examples are Toeplitz and Laurent operators
[
19
]
or Jacobi operators
[
36
]
. Another direction that has gained a great deal of attention in the last two decades is that of periodic discrete Schrödinger operators and generalizations thereof. A particular type known as
almost Mathieu operator
has been shown to exhibit rich spectral behavior (e.g. the spectrum can be a set of non-integer Hausdorff dimension, cf.
[
1
,
23
]
).
Beyond this, the literature pertaining to operators that are either not tri-diagonal or not self-adjoint is limited. The best starting point would be the book of Trefethen and Embree
[
37
]
.

2.
The Solvability Complexity Index Hierarchy

The Solvability Complexity Index (
\operatorname{SCI}
) and the
\operatorname{SCI}
Hierarchy provide a unified approach for understanding just how “difficult” it is to approximate infinite-dimensional problems (such as computing spectra) starting from finite-dimensional approximations.
We start by setting the scene with a concrete example before providing precise abstract definitions.

2.1.
Informal discussion & examples

Consider the set
\Omega=B(\ell^{2}(\mathbb{N}))
of all bounded operators on
\ell^{2}(\mathbb{N})
. Let
\{e_{i}\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
be the canonical basis. Then any element
A\in\Omega
is represented by an infinite matrix. Denote by
\Lambda
the set of all entries in this matrix. Then one could ask:

For any
A\in\Omega
, is it possible to compute its spectrum
\sigma(A)
as the limit of a sequence of computations
\Gamma_{n}
, where each
\Gamma_{n}
has access to only finitely many elements of
\Lambda
and can only perform finitely many arithmetic computations?

Needless to say, the whole point here is that the algorithms
\Gamma_{n}
are not tailored for this specific element
A
: they are meant to be able to handle
any
element
A\in\Omega
. The convergence of
\Gamma_{n}(A)
to
\sigma(A)
is made precise by realizing them as elements of the metric space
\mathcal{M}=(\mathrm{cl}(\mathbb{C}),d)
which comprises all closed subsets of
\mathbb{C}
endowed with an appropriate metric
d
(such as the Hausdorff metric).

In
[
20
]
, Hansen showed that it
is
possible to compute
\sigma(A)
for any
A\in\Omega
as above. However, rather than having algorithms
\Gamma_{n}
with a single index
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
three
indices were required, satisfying
\sigma(A)=\lim_{n_{3}\to\infty}\lim_{n_{2}\to\infty}\lim_{n_{1}\to\infty}\Gamma_{n_{1},n_{2},n_{3}}(A)
. The algorithms
\Gamma_{n_{1},n_{2},n_{3}}
are given explicitly, and can be implemented numerically (though this raises a philosophical question about what it means to take successive limits numerically). In
[
4
]
it was proved that this is optimal: this computation cannot be performed with fewer than
3
limits, and hence we say that this problem has an
\operatorname{SCI}
value of
3
.

The
\operatorname{SCI}
value strongly depends on
\Omega
: intuitively, if
\Omega
contains fewer elements, then devising a ‘one-size-fits-all’ algorithm should be easier. Indeed, if one considers
\Omega^{\mathrm{sa}}:=\{A\in\Omega\,|\,A\text{ is selfadjoint}\}
then the
\operatorname{SCI}
value reduces to
2
and for
\Omega^{\mathrm{cpt}}:=\{A\in\Omega\,|\,A\text{ is compact}\}
it further reduces to
1
.

The classification into
\operatorname{SCI}
values can be further refined into a classification that takes into account error bounds. This is the so-called
SCI Hierarchy
which we describe below.

2.2.
Definitions

We formalize the foregoing example with precise definitions:

Definition 2.1

(Computational problem)
.

A
computational problem
is a quadruple
(\Omega,\Lambda,\Xi,\mathcal{M})
, where

(i)

\Omega
is a set, called the
primary set
,

(ii)

\Lambda
is a set of complex-valued functions on
\Omega
, called the
evaluation set
,

(iii)

\mathcal{M}
is a metric space,

(iv)

\Xi:\Omega\to\mathcal{M}
is a map, called the
problem function
.

Remark 2.2
.

In this paper it is often clear what
\mathcal{M},\Lambda
and
\Xi
are, and the important element is the primary set
\Omega
. In this case we may abuse notation and refer to
\Omega
alone as the computational problem.

Definition 2.3

(Arithmetic algorithm)
.

Let
(\Omega,\Lambda,\Xi,\mathcal{M})
be a computational problem. An
arithmetic algorithm
is a map
\Gamma:\Omega\to\mathcal{M}
such that for each
T\in\Omega
there exists a finite subset
\Lambda_{\Gamma}(T)\subset\Lambda
such that

(i)

the action of
\Gamma
on
T
depends only on
\{f(T)\}_{f\in\Lambda_{\Gamma}(T)}
,

(ii)

for every
S\in\Omega
with
f(T)=f(S)
for all
f\in\Lambda_{\Gamma}(T)
one has
\Lambda_{\Gamma}(S)=\Lambda_{\Gamma}(T)
,

(iii)

the action of
\Gamma
on
T
consists of performing only finitely many arithmetic operations on
\{f(T)\}_{f\in\Lambda_{\Gamma}(T)}
.

Definition 2.4

(Tower of arithmetic algorithms)
.

Let
(\Omega,\Lambda,\Xi,\mathcal{M})
be a computational problem. A
tower of algorithms
of height
k
for
\Xi
is a family
\Gamma_{n_{1},n_{2},\dots,n_{k}}:\Omega\to\mathcal{M}
of arithmetic algorithms such that for all
T\in\Omega

\displaystyle\Xi(T)=\lim_{n_{k}\to\infty}\cdots\lim_{n_{1}\to\infty}\Gamma_{n_{1},n_{2},\dots,n_{k}}(T).

Definition 2.5

(SCI)
.

A computational problem
(\Omega,\Lambda,\Xi,\mathcal{M})
is said to have a
Solvability Complexity Index
(
\operatorname{SCI}
) of
k
if
k
is the smallest integer for which there exists a tower of algorithms of height
k
for
\Xi
.
If a computational problem has solvability complexity index
k
, we write

\displaystyle\operatorname{SCI}(\Omega,\Lambda,\Xi,\mathcal{M})=k.

If there exists a family
\{\Gamma_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of arithmetic algorithms and
N_{1}\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\Xi=\Gamma_{N_{1}}
then we define
\operatorname{SCI}(\Omega,\Lambda,\Xi,\mathcal{M})=0
.

Definition 2.6

(The SCI Hierarchy)
.

The
\operatorname{SCI}
Hierarchy
is a hierarchy
\{\Delta_{k}\}_{k\in{\mathbb{N}_{0}}}
of classes of computational problems
(\Omega,\Lambda,\Xi,\mathcal{M})
, where each
\Delta_{k}
is defined as the collection of all computational problems satisfying:

\displaystyle(\Omega,\Lambda,\Xi,\mathcal{M})\in\Delta_{0}\quad

\displaystyle\qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad\mathrm{SCI}(\Omega,\Lambda,\Xi,\mathcal{M})=0,

\displaystyle(\Omega,\Lambda,\Xi,\mathcal{M})\in\Delta_{k+1}

\displaystyle\qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad\mathrm{SCI}(\Omega,\Lambda,\Xi,\mathcal{M})\leq k,\qquad k\in\mathbb{N},

with the special class
\Delta_{1}
defined as the class of all computational problems in
\Delta_{2}
with a convergence rate:

(\Omega,\Lambda,\Xi,\mathcal{M})\in\Delta_{1}\qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad\exists\{\Gamma_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}},\,\exists\varepsilon_{n}\downarrow 0\quad\text{ s.t. }\quad\forall T\in\Omega,\ d(\Gamma_{n}(T),\Xi(T))\leq\varepsilon_{n}.

Hence we have that
\Delta_{0}\subset\Delta_{1}\subset\Delta_{2}\subset\cdots

When the metric space
\mathcal{M}
has certain ordering properties, one can define further classes that take into account convergence from below/above and associated error bounds. In order to not burden the reader with unnecessary definitions, we provide the definition that is relevant to the cases where
\mathcal{M}
is the space of closed (and bounded) subsets of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
together with the Attouch-Wets (Hausdorff) distance (definitions of which can be found in Appendix
A

[
4

(The SCI Hierarchy (Attouch-Wets/Hausdorff metric))
.

•

•

Remark 3.1
.

We note that the Hausdorff distance is only defined for non-empty sets, and it is finite only if the sets are bounded. Hence it is important to observe that for any

3.2

3.2.
Floquet-Bloch transform

Let

3.3

(3.6)

\displaystyle(\mathcal{U}_{\theta}x)_{n}:=(2\pi)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}x_{n+kN}e^{-\mathrm{i}\theta(k+\frac{n}{N})}.

We also introduce the symbol
\ell^{2}_{\text{per}}(N)

N

(y_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}

\|y\|_{\ell^{2}_{\text{per}}(N)}^{2}=\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}|y_{k}|^{2}
. Note that
\ell^{2}_{\text{per}}(N)
is canonically isomorphic to the Euclidean space
\mathbb{R}^{N}
.
The following lemma is easily proved by direct computation.

Lemma 3.4

(Properties of
\mathcal{U}_{\theta}

The map

(i)

\mathcal{U}_{\theta}x

\mathcal{U}_{\theta}:\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z})\to\ell^{2}_{\text{per}}(N)
;

(ii)

The map

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}:\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z})

\displaystyle\to\int_{[0,2\pi]}^{\oplus}\ell^{2}_{\mathrm{per}}(N)\,d\theta

\displaystyle x

\displaystyle\mapsto(\mathcal{U}_{\theta}x)_{\theta\in[0,2\pi]}

is unitary;

(iii)

The inverse
\mathcal{U}^{-1}
is given by

\displaystyle(\mathcal{U}^{-1}y)_{n}=(2\pi)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\int_{0}^{2\pi}y_{n}(\theta)e^{\mathrm{i}n\frac{\theta}{N}}\,d\theta.

Proof.

3.3.
Transform and Properties of
A\in\Omega^{\mathrm{per}}_{N,b}

The
N
-periodicity of
A\in\Omega^{\mathrm{per}}_{N,b}
along diagonals is equivalent to the identity

(3.7)

\displaystyle A_{m,n}=A_{m+kN,n+kN}\qquad\text{for all }m,n,k\in\mathbb{Z}.

Lemma 3.5
.

For any
A\in\Omega^{\mathrm{per}}_{N,b}
,
y\in\ell^{2}_{\mathrm{per}}(N)
and
\theta\in[0,2\pi]
, define
(A(\theta)y)_{n}=\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}e^{\mathrm{i}\theta\frac{j-n}{N}}A_{nj}y_{j}
. Then one has

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}A\mathcal{U}^{-1}=\int_{[0,2\pi]}^{\oplus}A(\theta)\,d\theta.

Proof.

Let
x\in\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z})
. We have

\displaystyle(\mathcal{U}_{\theta}Ax)_{n}

\displaystyle=(2\pi)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}(Ax)_{n+kN}e^{-\mathrm{i}\theta(k+\frac{n}{N})}

\displaystyle=(2\pi)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}A_{n+kN,j}x_{j}e^{-\mathrm{i}\theta(k+\frac{n}{N})}

\displaystyle=(2\pi)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}A_{n,j-kN}x_{j}e^{-\mathrm{i}\theta(k+\frac{n}{N})}

\displaystyle=(2\pi)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}A_{nj}x_{j+kN}e^{-\mathrm{i}\theta(k+\frac{n}{N})}

\displaystyle=(2\pi)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}\left(e^{-\mathrm{i}\theta\frac{n-j}{N}}A_{nj}\right)x_{j+kN}e^{-\mathrm{i}\theta(k+\frac{j}{N})}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}\left(e^{-\mathrm{i}\theta\frac{n-j}{N}}A_{nj}\right)(2\pi)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}x_{j+kN}e^{-\mathrm{i}\theta(k+\frac{j}{N})}

\displaystyle=(A(\theta)\mathcal{U}_{\theta}x)_{n},

where we have used the periodicity of
A
(see (
3.7
)) in the third line. The assertion now follows from the invertibility of
\mathcal{U}
.
∎

Remark 3.6
.

Observe that
(A(\theta)y)_{n}=(A(\theta)y)_{n+kN}
for any
k\in\mathbb{Z}
so that
A(\theta)y\in\ell^{2}_{\mathrm{per}}(N)
. Hence,
A(\theta)
is an operator
\ell^{2}_{\mathrm{per}}(N)\to\ell^{2}_{\mathrm{per}}(N)
, which can be expressed as an
N\times N
matrix. Note, however, that the numbers
e^{-\mathrm{i}\theta\frac{n-j}{N}}A_{nj}
are
not
the matrix elements of
A(\theta)
with respect to any basis. Indeed,
\ell^{2}_{\text{per}}(N)
is finite-dimensional, while
e^{-\mathrm{i}\theta\frac{n-j}{N}}A_{nj}
(
n,j\in\mathbb{Z}
) are infinitely many numbers.

As noted earlier, we have
\ell^{2}_{\text{per}}(N)\cong\mathbb{R}^{N}
. The identification can be made via the basis

\displaystyle e_{1}^{\text{per}}

\displaystyle=(\dots,0,\underbrace{1,0,\dots,0}_{N\text{ entries}},1,0,\dots)

\displaystyle e_{2}^{\text{per}}

\displaystyle=(\dots,0,0,\underbrace{1,0,\dots,0}_{N\text{ entries}},1\dots)

\displaystyle\quad\vdots

i.e.
(e_{n}^{\text{per}})_{j}=\delta_{(j\text{ mod }N),\,n}
for
n\in\{0,\dots,N-1\}
(Kronecker symbol). In this basis, the matrix elements of
A(\theta)
become

\displaystyle A(\theta)_{mn}

\displaystyle=\bigl\langle e_{m}^{\text{per}},A(\theta)e_{n}^{\text{per}}\bigr\rangle_{\ell^{2}_{\text{per}}(N)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\delta_{(k\text{ mod }N),\,m}\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}e^{\mathrm{i}\theta\frac{j-k}{N}}A_{kj}\delta_{(j\text{ mod }N),\,n}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\delta_{k,m}e^{\mathrm{i}\theta\frac{j-k}{N}}A_{kj}\delta_{(j\text{ mod }N),\,n}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}e^{\mathrm{i}\theta\frac{j-m}{N}}A_{mj}\delta_{(j\text{ mod }N),\,n}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j^{\prime}\in\mathbb{Z}}e^{\mathrm{i}\theta\frac{j^{\prime}N+n-m}{N}}A_{m,\,j^{\prime}N+n}

(3.8)

\displaystyle=e^{\mathrm{i}\theta\frac{n-m}{N}}\sum_{j^{\prime}\in\mathbb{Z}}e^{\mathrm{i}\theta j^{\prime}}A_{m,\,j^{\prime}N+n}.

Note that the sum in the last line is actually finite, because
A
is banded. Indeed, if the band width of
A
is less than the period, then the sum over
j^{\prime}
in (
3.8
) contains only one term.

Example 3.7
.

If
A
is a matrix with
N=1
, i.e.
A
is a Laurent operator, then formula (
3.8
) yields a scalar function of
\theta
given by

\displaystyle A(\theta)=\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}e^{\mathrm{i}\theta j}A_{0,j}.

Writing
z:=e^{\mathrm{i}\theta}
, we see that
A(\theta)=\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}z^{j}A_{0,j}
is given by the
symbol
of the Laurent operator. We thus recover the classical result that the spectrum of a Laurent operator is given by the image of the unit circle under its symbol (cf.
[
37
, Th. 7.1]
).

.

If

is tri-diagonal and

Next, we establish some elementary facts about the spectrum of a periodic operator.
By standard results about the Floquet-Bloch transform, we have

\displaystyle\sigma(A)=\bigcup_{\theta\in[0,2\pi]}\sigma(A(\theta))

for all
A\in\Omega^{\mathrm{per}}_{N,b}
. Thus, an algorithm may be devised by determining the zeros of the map
z\mapsto\det(A(\theta)-zI)
,
\theta\in[0,2\pi]
. To this end, note that by definition of the determinant, one has

\displaystyle\det(A(\theta)-zI)=\sum_{n=0}^{N}p_{n}(\theta)z^{n},

where the coefficient functions
p_{n}(\theta)
are polynomials in the matrix entries
A(\theta)_{mn}
and hence analytic and periodic in
\theta
.
Hence they are bounded:

\displaystyle\exists C>0\,:\,|p_{n}(\theta)|\leq C\qquad\forall\theta\in[0,2\pi],\,\,\forall n\in\{0,\dots,N\}.

Moreover,
p_{N}(\theta)\equiv 1
.

Lemma 3.9
.

Let
A\in\Omega^{\mathrm{per}}_{N,b}
and
R>0
. For any
z,w\in\mathbb{C}
with
|z|,|w|\leq R
and
\theta,\vartheta\in[0,2\pi]
one has

\begin{split}|\det(A(\theta)-zI)-\det(A(\vartheta)-wI)|&\leq\\
N^{\frac{N}{2}+2}\big((2b+1)\|A\|_{\infty}&+R\big)^{N}(2b+1)^{2}\|A\|_{\infty}(|z-w|+|\theta-\vartheta|),\end{split}

where we note that
\|A\|_{\infty}=\max\{|A_{ij}|\,|\,i,j\in\mathbb{Z}\}
can be computed in finitely many steps.

Proof.

Denote
B_{R}:=\{z\in\mathbb{C}\,|\,|z|\leq R\}
.
From the mean value theorem it follows that for any differentiable function
f:B_{R}\times[0,2\pi]\to\mathbb{C}
one has

\displaystyle|f(z,\theta)-f(w,\vartheta)|\leq\|\nabla f\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{R}\times[0,2\pi])}\big(|z-w|+|\theta-\vartheta|\big).

Hence to prove the claim it is enough to bound
\|\nabla f\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{R})\times[0,2\pi]}
for
f(z,\theta)=\det(A(\theta)-zI)
. This follows from the Jacobi formula: for any square matrix
M
one has

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\det(M)}{\partial M_{ij}}

\displaystyle=\operatorname{cof}(M)_{ij},

where
\operatorname{cof}(M)
denotes the cofactor matrix of
M
. Hence,

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial z}\det(A(\theta)-zI)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j=0}^{N-1}\operatorname{cof}(A(\theta)-zI)_{ij}(-\delta_{ij}),

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\det(A(\theta)-zI)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j=0}^{N-1}\operatorname{cof}(A(\theta)-zI)_{ij}\frac{\partial A(\theta)_{ij}}{\partial\theta}.

Using Hadamard’s inequality to bound the cofactor matrix, we obtain the bounds

\displaystyle\Big|\frac{\partial}{\partial z}\det(A(\theta)-zI)\Big|

\displaystyle\leq N^{\frac{N}{2}+1}\|A(\theta)-zI\|_{\infty}^{N}

(3.9)

\displaystyle\leq N^{\frac{N}{2}+1}\big((2b+1)\|A\|_{\infty}+R\big)^{N},

\displaystyle\Big|\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\det(A(\theta)-zI)\Big|

\displaystyle\leq N^{\frac{N}{2}+2}\|A(\theta)-zI\|_{\infty}^{N}\|\partial_{\theta}A(\theta)\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq N^{\frac{N}{2}+2}\big((2b+1)\|A\|_{\infty}+R\big)^{N}\|\partial_{\theta}A(\theta)\|_{\infty}

(3.10)

\displaystyle\leq N^{\frac{N}{2}+2}\big((2b+1)\|A\|_{\infty}+R\big)^{N}(2b+1)^{2}\|A\|_{\infty},

where the last two lines follow from the explicit formula (
3.8
). The bounds (
3.9
) and (
3.10
) imply

\displaystyle\max\{|\partial_{\theta}f|,|\partial_{z}f|\}

\displaystyle\leq N^{\frac{N}{2}+2}\big((2b+1)\|A\|_{\infty}+R\big)^{N}(2b+1)^{2}\|A\|_{\infty}

and the claim follows.
∎

3.4.
Proof of Theorem
1.1
(ii)

We can finally prove Theorem
1.1
(ii) which was restated equivalently as Theorem
3.3
. First, we define the family of algorithms
\{\Gamma_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
, where each of them maps
\Gamma_{n}:\Omega^{\mathrm{per}}_{N,b}\to\mathcal{M}
(we recall that
\mathcal{M}
is the space of all compact subsets of
\mathbb{C}
endowed with the Hausdorff metric).
It is easy to see that for any
A\in\Omega^{\mathrm{per}}_{N,b}
one has
\|A\|_{\ell^{2}\to\ell^{2}}\leq R_{A}:=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\sum_{k=-b}^{b}|A_{jk}|
(this follows from Young’s inequality), a quantity which can be computed in finitely many steps. Therefore, if we denote
B_{R_{A}}:=\{z\in\mathbb{C}\,|\,|z|\leq R_{A}\}
, the
a priori
inclusion
\sigma(A)\subset B_{R_{A}}
holds true for any
A\in\Omega^{\mathrm{per}}_{N,b}
.

Definition 3.10

(
N,b
-Periodic Matrix Algorithm)
.

Let
A\in\Omega^{\mathrm{per}}_{N,b}
and for
n\in\mathbb{N}
, let
\Theta_{n}=\big(\theta_{1}^{(n)},\dots,\theta_{n}^{(n)}\big)
be a linear spacing of
[0,2\pi]
and let
L_{n}:=\frac{1}{n}(\mathbb{Z}+\mathrm{i}\mathbb{Z})\cap B_{R_{A}}
be a finite lattice with spacing
n^{-1}
. Then we define

(3.11)

\displaystyle\Gamma_{n}(A):=\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}\left\{z\in L_{n}\,\middle|\,\big|\det\big(A(\theta_{i}^{(n)})-zI\big)\big|\leq n^{-\frac{1}{2}}\right\}.

Remark 3.11
.

We emphasize that (
3.11
) can be computed in finitely many arithmetic operations on the matrix elements of
A
. Indeed, computing the radius
R_{A}
consists of a finite sequence of multiplications and additions, as does the computation of each of the determinants
\det(A(\theta_{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle(n\scriptscriptstyle)})-z)
for
z
,
\theta_{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle(n\scriptscriptstyle)}
in the finite sets
L_{n}
,
\Theta_{n}
.

Proof of Theorem
3.3
(equiv. Theorem
1.1
(ii)).

The proof has two steps.

Step 1
:
\sigma(A)
is approximated from above by
\Gamma_{n}(A)
.

For any set
K\subset\mathbb{C}
we denote by
B_{\delta}(K)
the
\delta
-neighborhood of
K
.

Let
z\in\sigma(A)
. Then
|z|\leq\|A\|_{\ell^{2}\to\ell^{2}}\leq R_{A}
and there exists
\theta\in[0,2\pi]
such that
\det(A(\theta)-zI)=0
. Choose
z_{n}\in L_{n}
such that
|z-z_{n}|\leq n^{-1}
and
\theta_{n}\in\Theta_{n}
such that
|\theta-\theta_{n}|\leq n^{-1}
. Applying Lemma
3.9
we obtain the bound

\displaystyle|\det(A(\theta_{n})-z_{n}I)|

\displaystyle\leq N^{\frac{N}{2}+2}\big((2b+1)\|A\|_{\infty}+R_{A}\big)^{N}(2b+1)^{2}\|A\|_{\infty}(|z-z_{n}|+|\theta-\theta_{n}|)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2}{n}N^{\frac{N}{2}+2}\big((2b+1)\|A\|_{\infty}+R_{A}\big)^{N}(2b+1)^{2}\|A\|_{\infty}.

This inequality implies that
z_{n}\in\Gamma_{n}(A)
as soon as
2n^{-1}N^{\frac{N}{2}+2}\big((2b+1)\|A\|_{\infty}+R_{A}\big)^{N}(2b+1)^{2}\|A\|_{\infty}\leq n^{-\frac{1}{2}}
, or equivalently,

(3.12)

\displaystyle n>\left(2N^{\frac{N}{2}+2}\big((2b+1)\|A\|_{\infty}+R_{A}\big)^{N}(2b+1)^{2}\|A\|_{\infty}\right)^{2}.

Note that the right-hand side of (
3.12
) is computable in finitely many arithmetic operations if
N
and
b
are known
a priori
. Since
|z-z_{n}|\leq n^{-1}
by construction, this shows that
\sigma(A)\subset B_{\frac{1}{n}}(\Gamma_{n}(A))
for
n>(2N^{\frac{N}{2}+2}\big((2b+1)\|A\|_{\infty}+R_{A}\big)^{N}(2b+1)^{2}\|A\|_{\infty})^{2}
.

Step 2
:
\sigma(A)
is approximated from below by
\Gamma_{n}(A)
.

Next we prove that
\Gamma_{n}(A)\subset B_{\delta}(\sigma(A))
for
n
large enough. We first note that, since
\det(zI-A(\theta))
is a polynomial in
z
, it can be factored to take the form

(3.13)

\displaystyle\det(zI-A(\theta))=\prod_{i=1}^{N}(z-z_{i}(\theta)),

where
z_{i}(\theta)
are the zeros of
z\mapsto\det(zI-A(\theta))
(note that for a characteristic polynomial the coefficient of the leading order term is always 1). From (
3.13
) we obtain the bound

(3.14)

|\det(zI-A(\theta))|=\prod_{i=1}^{N}|z-z_{i}(\theta)|\geq\operatorname{dist}(z,\sigma(A))^{N}.

Let
z_{n}\in\Gamma_{n}(A)
be an arbitrary sequence. Then, by definition,
|\det(z_{n}I-A(\theta_{n}))|\leq n^{-\frac{1}{2}}
for some
\theta_{n}\in\Theta_{n}
. From (
3.14
) we conclude that

\displaystyle\operatorname{dist}(z_{n},\sigma(A))^{N}\leq|\det(z_{n}I-A(\theta_{n}))|\leq n^{-\frac{1}{2}}

and thus
z_{n}\in B_{n^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2N}}}}(\sigma(A))
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
. This concludes step 2.

Together, steps 1 and 2 imply that for any given
\delta>0
one has both
\Gamma_{n}(A)\subset B_{\delta}(\sigma(A))
and
\sigma(A)\subset B_{\delta}(\Gamma_{n}(A))
provided that

(3.15)

\displaystyle n>\max\left\{\delta^{-2N},\,\left(2N^{\frac{N}{2}+2}\big((2b+1)\|A\|_{\infty}+R_{A}\big)^{N}(2b+1)^{2}\|A\|_{\infty}\right)^{2}\right\}.

Since the right-hand side of (
3.15
) is computable from
N
,
b
and the matrix elements of
A
in finitely many arithmetic operations, we conclude that the computational problem is in
\Delta_{1}
.
∎

In this section we prove Theorem

H=-\Delta+V
with periodic potentials

. Again, this is done by defining an explicit algorithm. We shall consider three computational problems which only differ in their primary set
\Omega

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}\hfill\Omega&=\quad\Omega^{\mathrm{Sch}}\quad\text{or}\quad\Omega^{\mathrm{Sch}}_{p}\quad\text{or}\quad\Omega^{\mathrm{Sch}}_{p,M}\\[2.84526pt]

\Lambda&=\quad\{V\mapsto V(x)\,|\,x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\}\\[5.69054pt]
\Xi&:\quad\Omega\to\mathcal{M};\quad V\mapsto\sigma(-\Delta+V),\end{array}\right.

where
-\Delta+V
is meant to be defined on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
with domain
H^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, and

denotes the Attouch-Wets metric, which is a generalization of the Hausdorff metric for the case of sets which may be unbounded (see Appendix
A
for a brief discussion). Note that the spectrum
\sigma(-\Delta+V)
is always non-empty in this case, so taking this metric makes sense. For
p>d
and
M>0
, the primary sets are defined as follows:

\displaystyle\Omega^{\mathrm{Sch}}_{p}

\displaystyle:=\{V:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{C}\,|\,V\text{ is 1-periodic and }V|_{(0,1)^{d}}\in W^{1,p}((0,1)^{d})\},

\displaystyle\Omega^{\mathrm{Sch}}

\displaystyle:=\bigcup_{p>d}\Omega^{\mathrm{Sch}}_{p},

\displaystyle\Omega^{\mathrm{Sch}}_{p,M}

\displaystyle:=\{V\in\Omega^{\mathrm{Sch}}_{p}\,|\,\|V\|_{W^{1,p}((0,1)^{d})}\leq M\}.

Note that by Morrey’s inequality, every
V\in\Omega^{\mathrm{Sch}}_{p}
is continuous, and so the evaluation set
\Lambda
which comprises point evaluations of
V
, is well-defined. In the language of the Solvability Complexity Index, the two parts of Theorem
1.2
can be expressed as follows:

•

Part (i) amounts to proving that the computational problem for
\Omega^{\mathrm{Sch}}
has an
\operatorname{SCI}
value of
1
(or, equivalently, it belongs to
\Delta_{2}
).

•

Part (ii) amounts to showing that the computational problem for
\Omega^{\mathrm{Sch}}_{p,M}
belongs to
\Pi_{1}
, i.e. it can be approximated from above with explicit error bounds.

The proof of Theorem
1.2
is contained in Subsection
4.5
, and it relies on the following
weaker
theorem:

Theorem 4.1
.

The computational problem for
\Omega^{\mathrm{Sch}}_{p}
can be solved in one limit:
\operatorname{SCI}(\Omega^{\mathrm{Sch}}_{p})=1
(equivalently,
\Omega^{\mathrm{Sch}}_{p}\in\Delta_{2}
).

Note that this theorem is evidently weaker than Theorem
1.2
(i) as the class of potentials
\Omega^{\mathrm{Sch}}_{p}
considered here is a strict subset of the class
\Omega^{\mathrm{Sch}}=\bigcup_{p>d}\Omega^{\mathrm{Sch}}_{p}
considered in Theorem
1.2
(i). The proof is constructive, i.e. we provide an explicit algorithm that computes the spectrum of any given operator with
V\in\Omega^{\mathrm{Sch}}_{p}
.
Note that this problem is fundamentally different from the discrete problem (
3
) where we could directly access the matrix elements of the (discrete) operator. Instead, the evaluation set
\Lambda
gives access to the point values of the potential. Hence our task is to construct a sequence of algorithms
\{\Gamma_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
, such that each
\Gamma_{n}
computes its output from finitely many point evaluations of
V
using finitely many algebraic operations.

The proof of Theorem
4.1
is contained in Subsection
4.4
. Prior to that, Subsection
4.1
is dedicated to the Floquet-Bloch transform, in Subsection
4.2
we approximate the potential
V
and in Subsection
4.3
we define a provisional algorithm.

4.1.
Floquet-Bloch transform

The Floquet-Bloch transform for Schrödinger operators with periodic potentials is well-studied. The following lemma is a collection of results in
[
28
]
(below,
\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
denotes the Schwartz space of rapidly decaying functions).

Lemma 4.2

(
[
28
, Ch. XIII.16]
)
.

For
f\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and
\theta\in[0,2\pi]^{d}
, define the map

\displaystyle(\mathcal{U}_{\theta}f)(x)

\displaystyle:=(2\pi)^{-\frac{d}{2}}\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}f(x+n)e^{\mathrm{i}\theta\cdot(x+n)}.

Then the following hold.

(i)

\mathcal{U}_{\theta}
extends uniquely to a bounded operator on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
;

(ii)

For any
f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
,
\mathcal{U}_{\theta}f
is 1-periodic;

(iii)

The map

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}:L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})

\displaystyle\to\int_{[0,2\pi]^{d}}^{\oplus}L^{2}((0,1)^{d})\,d\theta

\displaystyle f

\displaystyle\mapsto(\mathcal{U}_{\theta}f)_{\theta\in[0,2\pi]^{d}}

is unitary;

(iv)

The inverse
\mathcal{U}^{-1}
is given by

\displaystyle(\mathcal{U}^{-1}g)(x+n)=(2\pi)^{-\frac{d}{2}}\int_{[0,2\pi]^{d}}g(x,\theta)e^{-\mathrm{i}n\theta}\,d\theta;

(v)

For
H=-\Delta+V
,
V\in\Omega_{p}
one has

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}H\mathcal{U}^{-1}

\displaystyle=\int_{[0,2\pi]^{d}}^{\oplus}H(\theta)\,d\theta,

where

(4.1)

\displaystyle H(\theta)

\displaystyle=-(\nabla+\mathrm{i}\theta)^{2}+V,

\displaystyle\operatorname{dom}(H(\theta))

\displaystyle=H^{2}_{\mathrm{per}}\big((0,1)^{d}\big)\qquad\forall\theta\in[0,2\pi]^{d}.

Moreover, the map
\theta\mapsto H(\theta)
is analytic and

(4.2)

\displaystyle\sigma(H)=\bigcup_{\theta\in[0,2\pi]^{d}}\sigma(H(\theta)).

The spectral identity (
4.2
) follows from the unitarity of
\mathcal{U}
and a straightforward calculation, noting that
\bigcup_{\theta\in[0,2\pi]^{d}}\sigma(H(\theta))
is closed by analyticity and periodicity in
\theta
.

4.2.
Estimating and Approximating the Potential

The critical step is to compute approximations to the spectrum
\sigma(-\Delta+V)
using only finitely many pointwise evaluations of
V
. This is done using the Floquet-Bloch transform in conjunction with the Birman-Schwinger principle. The approximate potential is defined in (
4.10
) and the critical error bound is stated in Lemma
4.7
.

4.2.1.
Birman-Schwinger principle

Let
p>d,\;V\in\Omega_{p}^{\mathrm{Sch}},\;\theta\in[0,2\pi]^{d}
and let
H(\theta)
be the corresponding Floquet-Bloch operator as in (
4.1
). Expanding the operator square,
H(\theta)
can be written as

\displaystyle H(\theta)

\displaystyle=-\Delta-2\mathrm{i}\theta\cdot\nabla+|\theta|^{2}+V.

Let us choose the following decomposition of
H(\theta)
. We define

(4.3a)

\displaystyle H_{0}

\displaystyle:=-\Delta+1,

\displaystyle\operatorname{dom}(H_{0})

\displaystyle=H^{2}_{\text{per}}\big((0,1)^{d}\big),

(4.3b)

\displaystyle B(\theta)

\displaystyle:=-2\mathrm{i}\theta\cdot\nabla+|\theta|^{2}-1+V,

\displaystyle\operatorname{dom}(B(\theta))

\displaystyle=H^{1}_{\text{per}}\big((0,1)^{d}\big).

Then clearly one has
H(\theta)=H_{0}+B(\theta)
.
The auxiliary constant 1, which is added in
H_{0}
and subtracted again in
B(\theta)
was chosen for convenience, so that
H_{0}
becomes a positive invertible operator. Note that
B(\theta)
is relatively compact with respect to
H_{0}
.
For
\lambda\notin\sigma(H_{0})
one has

\displaystyle\lambda-H_{0}-B(\theta)

\displaystyle=H_{0}^{\frac{1}{2}}\big(I-H_{0}^{-\frac{1}{2}}B(\theta)(\lambda-H_{0})^{-1}H_{0}^{\frac{1}{2}}\big)H_{0}^{-\frac{1}{2}}(\lambda-H_{0}),

where
I
denotes the identity operator on
L^{2}((0,1)^{d})
It follows that
\lambda-H_{0}-B(\theta)
is invertible if and only if
I-H_{0}^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}B(\theta)(\lambda-H_{0})^{-1}H_{0}^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}
is invertible, and in that case

\displaystyle(\lambda-H_{0}-B(\theta))^{-1}

\displaystyle=(\lambda-H_{0})^{-1}H_{0}^{\frac{1}{2}}\big(I-H_{0}^{-\frac{1}{2}}B(\theta)H_{0}^{\frac{1}{2}}(\lambda-H_{0})^{-1}\big)^{-1}H_{0}^{-\frac{1}{2}}.

This identity (sometimes called the Birman-Schwinger principle) implies that

\displaystyle\lambda\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\sigma(H_{0})\text{ is in }\sigma(H(\theta))\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad 1\in\sigma\big(H_{0}^{-\frac{1}{2}}B(\theta)H_{0}^{\frac{1}{2}}(\lambda-H_{0})^{-1}\big).

4.2.2.
Schatten class estimates

We now study the analytic operator valued function

\displaystyle K(\lambda,\theta):=H_{0}^{-\frac{1}{2}}B(\theta)H_{0}^{\frac{1}{2}}(\lambda-H_{0})^{-1}.

We choose a Fourier basis for
L^{2}((0,1)^{d})
, that is, we choose a numbering
\mathbb{N}\ni j\mapsto k_{j}\in 2\pi\mathbb{Z}^{d}
such that
|k_{j}|
is monotonically increasing with
j
and set

e_{j}:=e^{\mathrm{i}k_{j}\cdot x}.

We note that
e_{j}\in\operatorname{dom}(H(\theta))
for all
\theta
and
\|e_{j}\|_{L^{2}((0,1)^{d})}=1
for all
j\in\mathbb{N}
. In this basis, the operators
H_{0}^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}
,
H_{0}
and
\theta\cdot\nabla
are all diagonal and one has

(4.4)

\displaystyle H_{0}^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}

\displaystyle=\operatorname{diag}\big((1+|k_{j}|^{2})^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\big)

\displaystyle\lambda-H_{0}

\displaystyle=\operatorname{diag}\big(\lambda-1-|k_{j}|^{2}\big)

(4.5)

\displaystyle-2\mathrm{i}\theta\cdot\nabla

\displaystyle=\operatorname{diag}(2\,\theta\cdot k_{j}).

Therefore, we have

\displaystyle H_{0}^{-\frac{1}{2}}(-2\mathrm{i}\theta\cdot\nabla)H_{0}^{\frac{1}{2}}(\lambda-H_{0})^{-1}

\displaystyle=\operatorname{diag}\left(\frac{2\,\theta\cdot k_{j}}{\lambda-1-|k_{j}|^{2}}\right).

Now the following lemma is easily proved.

Lemma 4.3

(Schatten bound for
K
)
.

For every
s>d
the operator
K(\lambda,\theta)
belongs to the Schatten class
\mathcal{C}_{s}
and one has

\displaystyle\|K(\lambda,\theta)\|_{\mathcal{C}_{s}}

\displaystyle\leq\left(\frac{2}{\pi}|\theta|+\frac{2}{\pi}\bigl\||\theta|^{2}-1+V\bigr\|\right)C_{\lambda}\left(1-\frac{d}{s}\right)^{-\frac{1}{s}},

where
C_{\lambda}:=\sup_{j\in\mathbb{N}}\big|1-\frac{\lambda-1}{|k_{j}|^{2}}\big|^{-1}
and
\|\cdot\|
denotes the
L^{2}
operator norm.

Proof.

Let
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\sigma(H_{0})
and note that
C_{\lambda}<+\infty
by our choice of
\lambda
. Observe that simple geometric considerations lead to the bound

|k_{j}|\geq\pi j^{\frac{1}{d}}.

Then one has

\displaystyle\left|\frac{2\,\theta\cdot k_{j}}{\lambda-1-|k_{j}|^{2}}\right|

\displaystyle\leq 2\frac{|\theta||k_{j}|}{|\lambda-1-|k_{j}|^{2}|}

\displaystyle\leq 2C_{\lambda}|\theta||k_{j}|^{-1}

(4.6)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2}{\pi}C_{\lambda}|\theta|j^{-\frac{1}{d}}.

Hence the characteristic numbers of
H_{0}^{-\frac{1}{2}}(-2\mathrm{i}\theta\cdot\nabla)H_{0}^{\frac{1}{2}}(\lambda-H_{0})^{-1}
are bounded by
\frac{2}{\pi}C_{\lambda}|\theta|j^{-\frac{1}{d}}
and thus one has

\displaystyle H_{0}^{-\frac{1}{2}}(-2\mathrm{i}\theta\cdot\nabla)H_{0}^{\frac{1}{2}}(\lambda-H_{0})^{-1}\in\mathcal{C}_{s}\quad\text{ for any }\quad s>d

and

\displaystyle\bigl\|H_{0}^{-\frac{1}{2}}(-2\mathrm{i}\theta\cdot\nabla)H_{0}^{\frac{1}{2}}(\lambda-H_{0})^{-1}\bigr\|_{\mathcal{C}_{s}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2}{\pi}C_{\lambda}|\theta|\bigg(\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}j^{-\frac{s}{d}}\bigg)^{\frac{1}{s}}

(4.7)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2}{\pi}C_{\lambda}|\theta|\left(1-\frac{d}{s}\right)^{-\frac{1}{s}}.

Next we turn to the potential term in
K(\lambda,\theta)
, that is, the operator
H_{0}^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\big(|\theta|^{2}-1+V\big)H_{0}^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}(\lambda-H_{0})^{-1}
. This is easily treated by the ideal property of
\mathcal{C}_{s}
, since the operator
|\theta|^{2}-1+V
is bounded. Indeed, we have for every
s>d

\displaystyle\bigl\|H_{0}^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\big(|\theta|^{2}-1+V\big)H_{0}^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}(\lambda-H_{0})^{-1}\bigr\|_{\mathcal{C}_{s}}

\displaystyle\leq\bigl\|H_{0}^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\bigr\|\bigl\||\theta|^{2}-1+V\bigr\|\bigl\|H_{0}^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}(\lambda-H_{0})^{-1}\bigr\|_{\mathcal{C}_{s}}

(4.8)

\displaystyle\leq\bigl\||\theta|^{2}-1+V\bigr\|\frac{2}{\pi}C_{\lambda}\left(1-\frac{d}{s}\right)^{-\frac{1}{s}},

where the last line follows from a similar calculation to (
4.6
) and the fact that
\|H_{0}^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\|=1
(this follows from the matrix representation (
4.4
) and the fact that
k_{1}=0
).
∎

Lemma 4.4

(Lipschitz continuity of
K
)
.

For every
s>d
and
\lambda,\mu\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\sigma(H_{0})
,
\theta,\vartheta\in[0,2\pi]^{d}
one has

\displaystyle\|K(\lambda,\theta)-K(\mu,\vartheta)\|_{\mathcal{C}_{s}}

\displaystyle\leq 8d^{\frac{1}{2}}C_{\lambda}\left(1-\frac{d}{s}\right)^{-\frac{1}{s}}\big(|\theta-\vartheta|+c_{V}C_{\mu}|\lambda-\mu|\big),

where
C_{\lambda}
is defined as in Lemma
4.3
and
c_{V}=\frac{1}{2}+\pi d^{\frac{1}{2}}+(4\pi d^{\frac{1}{2}})^{-1}\|V-1\|_{\infty}
.

Proof.

First, note that

(4.9)

\displaystyle B(\theta)-B(\vartheta)=-2\mathrm{i}(\theta-\vartheta)\cdot\nabla+(\theta+\vartheta)\cdot(\theta-\vartheta).

Using (
4.9
) together with the resolvent identity for
(\lambda-H_{0})^{-1}
one obtains

\displaystyle K(\lambda,\theta)-K(\mu,\vartheta)

\displaystyle=H_{0}^{-\frac{1}{2}}(-2\mathrm{i}(\theta-\vartheta)\cdot\nabla+(\theta+\vartheta)\cdot(\theta-\vartheta))H_{0}^{\frac{1}{2}}(\lambda-H_{0})^{-1}

\displaystyle\hskip 113.81102pt+H_{0}^{-\frac{1}{2}}B(\vartheta)H_{0}^{\frac{1}{2}}(\lambda-\mu)(\lambda-H_{0})^{-1}(\mu-H_{0})^{-1}

\displaystyle=(\theta-\vartheta)\cdot(-2\mathrm{i}\nabla+\theta+\vartheta)(\lambda-H_{0})^{-1}+(\lambda-\mu)K(\lambda,\vartheta)(\mu-H_{0})^{-1}.

Taking norms in
\mathcal{C}_{s}
and using the estimates from the proof of Lemma
4.3
(and in particular (
4.7
)) we obtain the bound

\displaystyle\|K(\lambda,\theta)-K(\mu,\vartheta)\|_{\mathcal{C}_{s}}

\displaystyle\leq|\theta-\vartheta|\big\|(-2\mathrm{i}\nabla+\theta+\vartheta)(\lambda-H_{0})^{-1}\big\|_{\mathcal{C}_{s}}+|\lambda-\mu|\big\|K(\lambda,\vartheta)\big\|_{\mathcal{C}_{s}}\big\|(\mu-H_{0})^{-1}\big\|

\displaystyle\leq|\theta-\vartheta|\frac{2}{\pi}C_{\lambda}|\theta+\vartheta|\left(1-\frac{d}{s}\right)^{-\frac{1}{s}}+|\lambda-\mu|\big\|K(\lambda,\vartheta)\big\|_{\mathcal{C}_{s}}C_{\mu}.

Finally, applying Lemma
4.3
and using
|\theta+\vartheta|\leq 4\pi d^{\frac{1}{2}}
we obtain

\displaystyle\|K(\lambda,\theta)-K(\mu,\vartheta)\|_{\mathcal{C}_{s}}

\displaystyle\leq|\theta-\vartheta|8C_{\lambda}d^{\frac{1}{2}}\left(1-\frac{d}{s}\right)^{-\frac{1}{s}}+|\lambda-\mu|\left(4d^{\frac{1}{2}}+8\pi d+\frac{2}{\pi}\|V-1\|\right)C_{\lambda}C_{\mu}\left(1-\frac{d}{s}\right)^{-\frac{1}{s}}

\displaystyle\leq 8d^{\frac{1}{2}}C_{\lambda}\left(1-\frac{d}{s}\right)^{-\frac{1}{s}}\left(|\theta-\vartheta|+\left(\frac{1}{2}+\pi d^{\frac{1}{2}}+(4\pi d^{\frac{1}{2}})^{-1}\|V-1\|\right)C_{\mu}|\lambda-\mu|\right).

This concludes the proof.
∎

While Lemma
4.4
gives precise information about the dependence of the Lipschitz constant of
K
on all parameters, it will be useful for us to have a bound which is less precise but more explicit (and manifestly computable).

Corollary 4.5
.

Let
s>d
and
\delta,R>0
. Then for any
\theta,\vartheta\in[0,2\pi]^{d}
and
\lambda,\mu\in\mathbb{C}
such that
|\lambda-(1+|k_{j}|^{2})|,|\mu-(1+|k_{j}|^{2})|>\delta
for all
j
and
|\lambda|,|\mu|<R
one has

\displaystyle\|K(\lambda,\theta)-K(\mu,\vartheta)\|_{\mathcal{C}_{s}}

\displaystyle\leq\delta^{-2}48R^{2}\frac{sd}{s-d}\frac{3p-d}{p-d}\big(1+\|V\|_{W^{1,p}((0,1)^{d})}\big)\big(|\theta-\vartheta|+|\lambda-\mu|\big),

Proof.

This follows immediately from Lemma
4.4
via rather crude estimates, noting that
C_{\lambda}\leq R\delta^{-1}
and
\|V\|_{\infty}\leq\frac{3p-d}{p-d}\|V\|_{W^{1,p}((0,1)^{d})}
(cf.
[
8
, Ch. 9.3]
).
∎

4.2.3.
Approximation of the potential

Next we study the matrix representation of the potential
V
in the Fourier basis
\{e_{j}\}_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
and its approximations. First, we observe that in the Fourier basis
\{e_{j}\}_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
one has

\displaystyle\left\langle e_{j},Ve_{m}\right\rangle=\hat{V}_{k_{m}-k_{j}}

where
\hat{V}_{k}
denote the Fourier coefficients of
V
. Indeed, a direct calculation gives

\displaystyle\left\langle e_{j},Ve_{m}\right\rangle=\int_{(0,1)^{d}}\overline{e^{\mathrm{i}k_{j}\cdot x}}V(x)e^{\mathrm{i}k_{m}\cdot x}\,dx=\int_{(0,1)^{d}}V(x)e^{\mathrm{i}(k_{m}-k_{j})\cdot x}\,dx=\hat{V}_{k_{m}-k_{j}}.

Now, we want to build a computable, finite size approximation of the matrix
(V_{jm})=(\left\langle e_{j},Ve_{m}\right\rangle)
. We start by approximating the Fourier coefficients
\hat{V}_{k}
.

Lemma 4.6
.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
and define the lattice
I_{n}:=\{\frac{m}{n}\,|\,m=0,\dots,n-1\}^{d}\subset(0,1)^{d}
.
For every
f\in W^{1,p}((0,1)^{d})
,
p>d
, one has

\displaystyle\left|n^{-d}\sum_{\xi\in I_{n}}f(\xi)-\int_{(0,1)^{d}}f(x)\,dx\right|\leq\frac{2n^{-1+\nicefrac{{d}}{{p}}}}{1-\nicefrac{{d}}{{p}}}\|\nabla f\|_{L^{p}((0,1)^{d})}

Proof.

We write

\displaystyle\int_{(0,1)^{d}}f(x)\,dx=\sum_{\xi\in I_{n}}\int_{(0,\frac{1}{n})^{d}+\xi}f(x)\,dx.

Then, comparing the two sums term by term, we have

\displaystyle\left|\int_{(0,\frac{1}{n})^{d}+\xi}f(x)\,dx-n^{-d}f(\xi)\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\int_{(0,\frac{1}{n})^{d}+\xi}f(x)-f(\xi)\,dx\right|

\displaystyle\leq\int_{(0,\frac{1}{n})^{d}+\xi}|f(x)-f(\xi)|\,dx

\displaystyle\leq\int_{(0,\frac{1}{n})^{d}+\xi}\frac{2n^{-1+\nicefrac{{d}}{{p}}}}{1-\nicefrac{{d}}{{p}}}\|\nabla f\|_{L^{p}((0,1)^{d})}\,dx

\displaystyle=\frac{2n^{-1+\nicefrac{{d}}{{p}}}}{1-\nicefrac{{d}}{{p}}}\|\nabla f\|_{L^{p}((0,1)^{d})}\int_{(0,\frac{1}{n})^{d}+\xi}\,dx

\displaystyle=\frac{2n^{-1+\nicefrac{{d}}{{p}}}}{1-\nicefrac{{d}}{{p}}}\|\nabla f\|_{L^{p}((0,1)^{d})}n^{-d},

where Morrey’s inequality was used in the third line (cf. (28) in the proof of
[
8
, Th. 9.12]
). Summing these inequalities over
I_{n}
, we finally obtain

\displaystyle\left|n^{-d}\sum_{\xi\in I_{n}}f(\xi)-\int_{(0,1)^{d}}f(x)\,dx\right|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\xi\in I_{n}}\left|\int_{(0,\frac{1}{n})^{d}+\xi}f(x)\,dx-n^{-d}f(\xi)\right|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\xi\in I_{n}}\frac{2n^{-1+\nicefrac{{d}}{{p}}}}{1-\nicefrac{{d}}{{p}}}\|\nabla f\|_{L^{p}((0,1)^{d})}n^{-d}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2n^{-1+\nicefrac{{d}}{{p}}}}{1-\nicefrac{{d}}{{p}}}\|\nabla f\|_{L^{p}((0,1)^{d})}.

∎

Let us introduce the
approximate Fourier coefficients
for
k\in 2\pi\mathbb{Z}^{d}
and
n\in\mathbb{N}
,

(4.10)

\displaystyle\hat{V}_{k}^{\text{appr},n}:=n^{-d}\sum_{\xi\in I_{n}}V(\xi)e^{\mathrm{i}k\cdot\xi}.

Note that the
\hat{V}_{k}^{\text{appr},n}
can be computed in finitely many operations from the information provided in
\Lambda
.
Lemma
4.6
applied to the function
f(x)=V(x)e^{\mathrm{i}k\cdot x}
leads to the error estimate

\displaystyle\left|\hat{V}_{k}^{\text{appr},n}-\hat{V}_{k}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2n^{-1+\nicefrac{{d}}{{p}}}}{1-\nicefrac{{d}}{{p}}}\|\nabla(Ve^{\mathrm{i}k\cdot x})\|_{L^{p}((0,1)^{d})}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2n^{-1+\nicefrac{{d}}{{p}}}}{1-\nicefrac{{d}}{{p}}}\big(\|\nabla V\,e^{\mathrm{i}k\cdot x}\|_{L^{p}((0,1)^{d})}+\|V\nabla e^{\mathrm{i}k\cdot x}\|_{L^{p}((0,1)^{d})}\big)

\displaystyle=\frac{2n^{-1+\nicefrac{{d}}{{p}}}}{1-\nicefrac{{d}}{{p}}}\big(\|\nabla V\|_{L^{p}((0,1)^{d})}+|k|\,\|V\|_{L^{p}((0,1)^{d})}\big)

(4.11)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2n^{-1+\nicefrac{{d}}{{p}}}}{1-\nicefrac{{d}}{{p}}}(1+|k|)\|V\|_{W^{1,p}((0,1)^{d})}

Next, we define the approximate potential matrix

\displaystyle V^{\text{appr},n}:=\big(\hat{V}^{\text{appr},n}_{k_{m}-k_{j}}\big)_{m,j\in\mathbb{N}}.

We remark that the approximated potential matrix cannot be computed in finitely many algebraic operations from the point values of
V
, because it has infinitely many entries. This issue will be addressed next.

Lemma 4.7

(Main Error Bound)
.

For
N\in\mathbb{N}
let
\mathcal{H}_{N}=\mathrm{Span}\{e_{1},\dots,e_{N}\}
and let
P_{N}:L^{2}((0,1)^{d})\to\mathcal{H}_{N}
be the orthogonal projection. Moreover, define

\displaystyle K^{\mathrm{appr}}_{n}(\lambda,\theta):=H_{0}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\big(-2\mathrm{i}\theta\cdot\nabla+|\theta|^{2}-1+V^{\mathrm{appr},n}\big)H_{0}^{\frac{1}{2}}(\lambda-H_{0})^{-1}.

Then for every
s>d
one has

\displaystyle\big\|K(\lambda,\theta)-P_{N}K^{\mathrm{appr}}_{n}(\lambda,\theta)P_{N}\big\|_{\mathcal{C}_{s}}

\displaystyle\leq C_{\lambda}\bigg(C_{s,d}^{1}|\theta|N^{\frac{1}{s}-\frac{1}{d}}+C_{s,p,d}^{2}\frac{N^{1+\frac{1}{d}}}{n^{1-\frac{d}{p}}}\|V\|_{W^{1,p}((0,1)^{d})}

\displaystyle\hskip 85.35826pt+C_{s,d}^{3}N^{\frac{1}{s}-\frac{1}{d}}\Big(\big||\theta|^{2}-1\big|+\|V\|_{L^{\infty}((0,1)^{d})}\Big)\bigg),

where we recall that
C_{\lambda}=\sup_{j\in\mathbb{N}}\big|1-\frac{\lambda-1}{|k_{j}|^{2}}\big|^{-1}
and
C_{s,d}^{1},\,C_{s,p,d}^{2},\,C_{s,d}^{3}
are explicit constants independent of
n,N,\lambda,\theta
.

Proof.

Again, we denote by
\|\cdot\|
the
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
operator norm in this proof.
We first treat the
\theta\cdot\nabla
term. By equations (
4.4
) - (
4.5
) we have

\displaystyle H_{0}^{-\frac{1}{2}}(-2\mathrm{i}\theta\cdot\nabla)H_{0}^{\frac{1}{2}}(\lambda-H_{0})^{-1}-P_{N}H_{0}^{-\frac{1}{2}}(-2\mathrm{i}\theta\cdot\nabla)H_{0}^{\frac{1}{2}}(\lambda-H_{0})^{-1}P_{N}=\operatorname{diag}\left(\frac{2\,\theta\cdot k_{j}}{\lambda-1-|k_{j}|^{2}};\;j>N\right)

and by (
4.6
) we can estimate the above as

\displaystyle\left\|\operatorname{diag}\left(\frac{2\,\theta\cdot k_{j}}{\lambda-1-|k_{j}|^{2}};\;j>N\right)\right\|_{\mathcal{C}_{s}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2}{\pi}|\theta|C_{\lambda}\Bigg(\sum_{j=N+1}^{\infty}j^{-\frac{s}{d}}\Bigg)^{\frac{1}{s}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2}{\pi}|\theta|C_{\lambda}\left(\int_{N}^{\infty}t^{-\frac{s}{d}}\,dt\right)^{\frac{1}{s}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2}{\pi}|\theta|C_{\lambda}\left(\frac{N^{-\frac{s}{d}+1}}{\frac{s}{d}-1}\right)^{\frac{1}{s}}

(4.12)

\displaystyle=\frac{2|\theta|C_{\lambda}}{\pi(\frac{s}{d}-1)^{\frac{1}{s}}}N^{-\frac{1}{d}+\frac{1}{s}}.

To estimate the next term, we denote

Q_{z_{0}}:=\left\{z\in\mathbb{C}\,\middle|\,|\operatorname{Im}(z-z_{0})|,|\operatorname{Re}(z-z_{0})|\leq\frac{1}{2}\right\}
. Then we choose

(with

(4.23)

\displaystyle\tilde{\Gamma}_{N}^{z_{0}}(V):=\bigcup_{i=1}^{N}\left\{z\in L_{N}\,\middle|\,\big|\det\nolimits_{\lceil p\rceil}\big(I-P_{N}\tilde{K}^{\mathrm{appr}}_{n(N)}\big(z,\theta_{i}^{(N)}\big)P_{N}\big)\big|\leq N^{-(\frac{1}{2d}-\frac{1}{2p})}\right\},

where now

\displaystyle\tilde{K}^{\mathrm{appr}}_{n}(\lambda,\theta):=\tilde{H}_{0}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\big(-2\mathrm{i}\theta\cdot\nabla+|\theta|^{2}-2+V^{\mathrm{appr},n}\big)\tilde{H}_{0}^{\frac{1}{2}}(\lambda-\tilde{H}_{0})^{-1}.

From the proofs in Subsections

(Convergence of Second Provisional Algorithm)
.

Let

, where

).

Armed with the provisional algorithms (

) and (

.

Let

,

Combining Propositions
4.10

4.13

Proof of Theorem
4.1
.

Proof of part (i).

Remark 4.15
.

In view of Theorems
3.3
and
4.1
it is natural to ask whether one might have
\Omega_{p,M}^{\mathrm{Sch}}\in\Sigma_{1}
(and therefore
\Omega_{p,M}^{\mathrm{Sch}}\in\Delta_{1}
). This is indeed a nontrivial open problem. Recalling the proof of Theorem
3.3
(in particular (
3.14
)), the error bound for the approximation “from below” used the fact that
\det(zI-A(\theta))
is a polynomial. In the proof of Theorem
4.1
on the other hand, the function
\det_{\lceil p\rceil}(I-K(z,\theta))
, whose zeros must be approximated, is only known to be

. Obtaining

classification amounts to obtaining explicit upper bounds on the width of the zeros of this analytic function. These can not be deduced in any straightforward way from the values of the potential
V
.

5.
Sometimes Two Limits Are Necessary

In Section
2
we described a complicated construction called
towers of algorithms
, where more than one successive limit is required to correctly perform certain computations. In this section we exhibit this phenomenon first hand: we prove Theorem
1.3
which is rephrased in the language of
\operatorname{SCI}
as Theorem
5.1
below. This theorem shows that there exists a class of potentials (which are less smooth, yet can be evaluated at any point) for which there do
not
exist algorithms that can approximate the associated spectral problem in a single limit. That is,
\operatorname{SCI}>1
. We
are
able, though, to construct an arithmetic algorithm which converges by taking
two
successive limits. That is,
\operatorname{SCI}=2
. This class contains potentials which are allowed to have a singularity at a single point
x_{0}\in(0,1)
, but are otherwise smooth:

\displaystyle\Omega_{x_{0}}^{\mathrm{Sch}}

\displaystyle:=\left\{V:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}\,\middle|\,V\text{ is 1-periodic, }V(x_{0})=0\text{ and }V|_{[0,1]}\in L^{2}\big([0,1]\big)\cap C^{\infty}\big([0,1]\setminus\{x_{0}\}\big)\right\}.

We emphasize that
\Omega_{x_{0}}^{\mathrm{Sch}}
contains only real-valued functions and that the pointwise evaluation
V\mapsto V(x)
is well-defined for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
. Moreover, we remark that by
[
28
, Th. XIII.96]
the Schrödinger operator
-\Delta+V
is well-defined and selfadjoint with domain
H^{2}(\mathbb{R})
. We shall therefore consider the computational problem

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}\hfill\Omega&=\quad\Omega^{\mathrm{Sch}}_{x_{0}}\\[2.84526pt]
\mathcal{M}&=\quad\big(\{K\subset\mathbb{C}\,|\,K\text{ closed}\},\,d_{\mathrm{AW}}\big)\\[2.84526pt]
\Lambda&=\quad\{V\mapsto V(x)\,|\,x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\}\\[5.69054pt]
\Xi&:\quad\Omega\to\mathcal{M};\quad V\mapsto\sigma(-\partial_{x}^{2}+V),\end{array}\right.

and shall prove

Theorem 5.1
.

The computational problem (
5
) has
\operatorname{SCI}=2
(equivalently, it belongs to
\Delta_{3}\setminus\Delta_{2}
).

The proof of Theorem
5.1
has two parts. To prove
\operatorname{SCI}\leq 2
we construct an explicit tower of algorithms that computes the spectrum in two limits (cf. Definition
2.4
). The proof of
\operatorname{SCI}>1
is by contradiction. We assume the existence of a sequence
\{\Gamma_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
with
\Gamma_{n}(V)\to\sigma(-\partial_{x}^{2}+V)
for every
V\in\Omega_{x_{0}}^{\mathrm{Sch}}
and via a diagonal process construct a potential
V\in\Omega_{x_{0}}^{\mathrm{Sch}}
such that
\Gamma_{n}(V)\nrightarrow\sigma(-\partial_{x}^{2}+V)
, yielding the desired contradiction.

5.1.
Lemmas

We first collect some technical lemmas that will be necessary for the proof.

Lemma 5.2
.

Denote by
W(H)
the numerical range of an operator
H
. Let
V\in\Omega_{x_{0}}^{\mathrm{Sch}}
. There exists
\delta>0
such that
\inf W(-\partial_{x}^{2}+V)\geq-\frac{1}{4}
whenever
\|V\|_{L^{1}([0,1])}<\delta
.

Proof.

Choose a partition of unity
\{\chi_{i}\}_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}
such that
\operatorname{supp}(\chi_{i})\subset[i-1,i+1]
and
\sup_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}\|\chi_{i}\|_{W^{1,\infty}}<+\infty
. Then for any
\phi\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
we have

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}V(x)|\phi(x)|^{2}\,dx

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}V(x)\chi_{i}(x)|\phi(x)|^{2}\,dx

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}\int_{i-1}^{i+1}V(x)\chi_{i}(x)|\phi(x)|^{2}\,dx

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}\int_{-1}^{1}V(x)\chi_{i}(x-i)|\phi(x-i)|^{2}\,dx,

where the last line follows from periodicity of
V
. Moreover, we have

\displaystyle\left|\int_{-1}^{1}V(x)\chi_{i}(x-i)|\phi(x-i)|^{2}\,dx\right|

\displaystyle\leq\|V\|_{L^{1}([-1,1])}\big\|\chi_{i}(\cdot-i)|\phi(\cdot-i)|^{2}\big\|_{L^{\infty}([-1,1])}

\displaystyle\leq C\|V\|_{L^{1}([0,1])}\big\|\chi_{i}(\cdot-i)|\phi(\cdot-i)|^{2}\big\|_{W^{1,1}([-1,1])}

(5.5)

\displaystyle\leq C\|V\|_{L^{1}([0,1])}\|\chi_{i}\|_{W^{1,\infty}}\|\phi(\cdot-i)\|_{H^{1}([-1,1])}^{2},

where the second line follows from the Sobolev embedding
W^{1,1}\hookrightarrow L^{\infty}
(cf.
[
8
, Th. 8.8]
) and the third follows by the product rule and Hölder’s inequality. Summing (
5.5
) over
i
we obtain

\displaystyle\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}}V(x)|\phi(x)|^{2}\,dx\right|

\displaystyle\leq C\|V\|_{L^{1}([0,1])}\sum_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}\|\chi_{i}\|_{W^{1,\infty}}\|\phi(\cdot-i)\|_{H^{1}([-1,1])}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 2C\|V\|_{L^{1}([0,1])}\Big(\sup_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}\|\chi_{i}\|_{W^{1,\infty}}\Big)\|\phi\|_{H^{1}(\mathbb{R})}^{2}

(5.6)

\displaystyle\leq C^{\prime}\|V\|_{L^{1}([0,1])}\|\phi\|_{H^{1}(\mathbb{R})}^{2},

where
C^{\prime}=2C\sup_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}\|\chi_{i}\|_{W^{1,\infty}}
is finite by choice of the functions
\chi_{i}
.
Turning to the numerical range, we have for
\phi\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})

\displaystyle\langle(-\partial_{x}^{2}+V)\phi,\phi\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left(|\phi^{\prime}|^{2}+V|\phi|^{2}\right)dx

\displaystyle\geq\|\phi^{\prime}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}^{2}-C^{\prime}\|V\|_{L^{1}([0,1])}\|\phi\|_{H^{1}(\mathbb{R})}^{2}

\displaystyle=(1-C^{\prime}\|V\|_{L^{1}([0,1])})\|\phi^{\prime}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}^{2}-C^{\prime}\|V\|_{L^{1}([0,1])}\|\phi\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}^{2},

where the second line follows from (
5.6
). Finally, let
\delta:=(4C^{\prime})^{-1}
. Then if
\|V\|_{L^{1}([0,1])}<\delta
one has

\displaystyle\langle(-\partial_{x}^{2}+V)\phi,\phi\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}\geq\frac{3}{4}\|\phi^{\prime}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}^{2}-\frac{1}{4}\|\phi\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}^{2}\geq-\frac{1}{4}\|\phi\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}^{2}

and the proof is complete.
∎

Lemma 5.3
.

For
h>0
,
q_{1},q_{2}\in[0,1]
with
0<q_{1}<q_{2}<1
, define the periodic step function

\displaystyle V_{h,q_{1},q_{2}}(x)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{@{}l@{\quad}l@{}}-h&x\in[q_{1},q_{2}]\\
0&x\in([0,1]\setminus[q_{1},q_{2}]).\end{array}\right.

Then for every
\tilde{V}\in\Omega_{x_{0}}^{\mathrm{Sch}}
with
\tilde{V}\leq 0
one has

\displaystyle\inf W\big(-\partial_{x}^{2}+\tilde{V}+V_{h,q_{1},q_{2}}\big)\leq-(q_{2}-q_{1})h.

Proof.

Define the sequence of test functions

\displaystyle\phi_{n}(x)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{@{}l@{\quad}l@{}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2n}}&x\in[-n,n]\\
\text{linear to }0&x\in[-n-1,-n)\cup(n,n+1]\\
0&\textnormal{otherwise.}\end{array}\right.

We note that
\|\phi_{n}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}\to 1
as
n\to+\infty
.
Then if
\tilde{V}\in\Omega_{x_{0}}^{\mathrm{Sch}}
with
\tilde{V}\leq 0
we have

\displaystyle\langle(-\partial_{x}^{2}+V)\phi_{n},\phi_{n}\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}|\phi_{n}^{\prime}|^{2}\,dx+\int_{\mathbb{R}}(\tilde{V}+V_{h,q_{1},q_{2}})|\phi_{n}|^{2}\,dx

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}}|\phi_{n}^{\prime}|^{2}\,dx+\int_{\mathbb{R}}V_{h,q_{1},q_{2}}|\phi_{n}|^{2}\,dx

\displaystyle\leq\int_{-n-1}^{-n}\frac{1}{2n}\,dx+\int_{n}^{n+1}\frac{1}{2n}\,dx-\sum_{i=-n}^{n}\int_{[q_{1},q_{2}]+i}\frac{h}{2n}\,dx

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}-(q_{2}-q_{1})h.

The assertion follows by letting
n\to+\infty
.
∎

The next lemma is needed to construct the tower of algorithms that will compute the spectrum of elements in
\Omega^{\mathrm{Sch}}_{x_{0}}
.

Lemma 5.4
.

Let
V\in\Omega_{x_{0}}^{\mathrm{Sch}}
and for
n\in\mathbb{N}
let
\rho_{n}\in W^{1,\infty}(\mathbb{R})
be a periodic function such that

\displaystyle\rho_{n}(x)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{@{}l@{\quad}l@{}}0&x\in\bigcup_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}(k-\tfrac{1}{n},k+\tfrac{1}{n})\\
1&x\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\bigcup_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}(k-\tfrac{2}{n},k+\tfrac{2}{n}),\end{array}\right.

then one has
\sigma(-\partial_{x}^{2}+\rho_{n}V)\to\sigma(-\partial_{x}^{2}+V)
in Attouch-Wets distance.

Proof.

A Neumann series argument shows that

(5.9)

\displaystyle(-\partial_{x}^{2}+\rho_{n}V-z)^{-1}-(-\partial_{x}^{2}+V-z)^{-1}=\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\big((\rho_{n}-1)V(-\partial_{x}^{2}+V-z)^{-1}\big)^{m},

where
z\notin\mathbb{R}
and the right hand side is defined whenever
\|(\rho_{n}-1)V(-\partial_{x}^{2}+V-z)^{-1}\|_{L^{2}\to L^{2}}<1
. We will prove that the right hand side of (
5.9
) converges to 0 in the norm resolvent sense. To this end let
u\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
and compute

\displaystyle\left\|(\rho_{n}-1)V(-\partial_{x}^{2}+V-z)^{-1}u\right\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty}\left\|(\rho_{n}-1)V(-\partial_{x}^{2}+V-z)^{-1}u\right\|_{L^{2}([k,k+1])}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty}\left\|(\rho_{n}-1)V\right\|_{L^{2}([k,k+1])}^{2}\left\|(-\partial_{x}^{2}+V-z)^{-1}u\right\|_{L^{\infty}([k,k+1])}^{2}

\displaystyle=\left\|(\rho_{n}-1)V\right\|_{L^{2}([0,1])}^{2}\sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty}\left\|(-\partial_{x}^{2}+V-z)^{-1}u\right\|_{L^{\infty}([k,k+1])}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq C\left\|(\rho_{n}-1)V\right\|_{L^{2}([0,1])}^{2}\sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty}\left\|(-\partial_{x}^{2}+V-z)^{-1}u\right\|_{H^{1}([k,k+1])}^{2}

\displaystyle=C\left\|(\rho_{n}-1)V\right\|_{L^{2}([0,1])}^{2}\left\|(-\partial_{x}^{2}+V-z)^{-1}u\right\|_{H^{1}(\mathbb{R})}^{2}

(5.10)

\displaystyle=C\left\|(\rho_{n}-1)V\right\|_{L^{2}([0,1])}^{2}\left\|(-\partial_{x}^{2}+V-z)^{-1}\right\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})\to H^{1}(\mathbb{R})}^{2}\|u\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}^{2},

where Hölder’s inequality was used in the second line, periodicity of
(\rho_{n}-1)V
was used in the third line and the Sobolev embedding
H^{1}(\mathbb{R})\hookrightarrow L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
was used in the fouth line. Combining eqs. (
5.9
) and (
5.10
) we find that

\displaystyle\big\|(-\partial_{x}^{2}+\rho_{n}V-z)^{-1}-

\displaystyle(-\partial_{x}^{2}+V-z)^{-1}\big\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})\to L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}

\displaystyle\leq C\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\left\|(\rho_{n}-1)V\right\|_{L^{2}([0,1])}^{m}\left\|(-\partial_{x}^{2}+V-z)^{-1}\right\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})\to H^{1}(\mathbb{R})}^{m},

for all
n>N
large enough such that
\|(\rho_{n}-1)V\|_{L^{2}([0,1])}<\|(-\partial_{x}^{2}+V-z)^{-1}\|_{L^{2}\to H^{1}}^{-1}
. Since
V\in L^{2}([0,1])
we know that
\|(\rho_{n}-1)V\|_{L^{2}([0,1])}\to 0
, hence such
N
must exist. For
n>N
the geometric series gives

\displaystyle\big\|(-\partial_{x}^{2}+\rho_{n}V-z)^{-1}-(-\partial_{x}^{2}+V-z)^{-1}\big\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})\to L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}\leq C\frac{q_{n}}{1-q_{n}},

with
q_{n}=\|(\rho_{n}-1)V\|_{L^{2}([0,1])}\|(-\partial_{x}^{2}+V-z)^{-1}\|_{L^{2}\to H^{1}}
, which immediately implies norm resolvent convergence. Finally, an application of
[
29
, Thms. VIII.23-24]
yields the desired spectral convergence.
∎

5.2.
Proof of Theorem
5.1

We are now ready to prove Theorem
5.1
.

Step 1
: construction of an adversarial potential
V
.

Assume for contradiction that there exists a sequence of algorithms
\{\Gamma_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
such that for any
V\in\Omega_{x_{0}}^{\mathrm{Sch}}
,
\Gamma_{n}(V)\to\sigma(-\partial_{x}^{2}+V)
as
n\to+\infty
. We now describe a process that defines an “adversarial” potential
V
for which the sequence
\{\Gamma_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
will necessarily fail.

We begin with an example construction that illustrates how a potential can be obtained that “fools”
\Gamma_{n}
for a single
n
. This construction is then iterated below to obtain a potential whose spectrum is not approximated by the entire sequence
\{\Gamma_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
.
For the sake of definiteness we make the arbitrary choice
x_{0}:=\frac{1}{4}
, though the proof does not depend on this choice. Moreover, we note that by Hölder’s inequality one has
\|f\|_{L^{1}([0,1])}\leq\|f\|_{L^{2}([0,1])}
for all
f\in L^{2}([0,1])
.

With the notation from Lemma
5.3
, let
V_{1}
be the periodic square well potential
V_{h_{1},0,\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}
with
h_{1}>0
chosen such that
\inf W(-\partial_{x}^{2}+V_{1})\leq-1
. Because
-\partial_{x}^{2}+V
is selfadjoint, this implies that
\inf\sigma(-\partial_{x}^{2}+V_{1})\leq-1
. Our assumptions about
\Gamma_{n}
imply that there exists
n_{1}\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\inf\operatorname{Re}(\Gamma_{n}(V))<-\frac{1}{2}
for all
n\geq n_{1}
. By the definition of an algorithm (Definition
2.3
),
\Gamma_{n_{1}}(V)
depends only on finitely many elements of
\Lambda
, i.e. finitely many point values
V(x_{i})
,
i\in\{1,\dots,m_{n_{1}}\}
. We may assume without loss of generality that the sets
\{x_{1},\dots,x_{m_{n}}\}
are growing with
n
.

For later reference, let
\rho
be a smooth, compactly supported function on
\mathbb{R}
such that

\displaystyle\rho(0)

\displaystyle=1

\displaystyle\operatorname{supp}(\rho_{1})

\displaystyle\subset\bigl(-\tfrac{1}{4},\tfrac{1}{4}\bigr).

Next, define a new potential
\tilde{V_{1}}
by “thinning out”
V_{1}
around the points
x_{i}
. More concretely, let
\delta>0
(to be determined later) and define

The constructions outlined above define an iterative process that yields a sequence of smooth potentials
\{V_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
and
\{\tilde{V}_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
. We outline the details below. Fix
\eta>0
to be determined later and initialize

.

•

Let

•

Choose an interval

•

Choose

large enough such that

\Gamma_{n_{k}}(V_{k})
depends only on finitely many point values

•

Let

and define a “thinned out” potential

by

\displaystyle\tilde{V}_{k}(x):=\tilde{V}_{k-1}(x)+V_{h_{k},x_{0}-\varepsilon,x_{0}+\varepsilon}(x)\sum_{i=1}^{m_{n_{k}}}\rho\left(\frac{x}{\delta l_{k}}-x_{i}\right).

Choose

It follows that

and thus

•

Moreover, we have by construction

\displaystyle\|\tilde{V}_{k}\|_{L^{2}([0,1])}\leq\eta\sum_{j=0}^{k}2^{-j}

and there exists an interval

.

This process defines a sequence of potentials
\{\tilde{V}_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}

\displaystyle\liminf_{k\to+\infty}d_{\mathrm{AW}}\big(\Gamma_{n_{k}}(\tilde{V}_{k}),\sigma(-\partial_{x}^{2}+\tilde{V}_{k})\big)\geq\frac{1}{4}.

Next we show that the sequence
\tilde{V}_{k}
converges pointwise to a function

. By construction, for every

We conclude the proof of Theorem

Appendix A
The Hausdorff and Attouch-Wets distances

Since the Attouch-Wets distance is not well-known, we provide the basic definition here, and use the opportunity to remind the reader of the Hausdorff distance as well. For further information we refer to
[
3
]
.

Definition A.1

(Hausdorff distance for subsets of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
)
.

Let
A,B\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be two non-empty bounded sets. Their
Hausdorff distance
is

\displaystyle d_{\mathrm{H}}(A,B)

\displaystyle=\max\left\{\sup_{a\in A}\inf_{b\in B}|a-b|,\sup_{b\in B}\inf_{a\in A}|a-b|\right\}

\displaystyle=\sup_{p\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left|\inf_{a\in A}|a-p|-\inf_{b\in B}|b-p|\right|.

Definition A.2

(Attouch-Wets distance for subsets of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
)
.

Let
A,B\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be two non-empty (possibly unbounded) sets. Their
Attouch-Wets distance
is

\displaystyle d_{\mathrm{AW}}(A,B)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}2^{-n}\min\left\{1\,,\,\sup_{p\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\,|p|<n}\left|\inf_{a\in A}|a-p|-\inf_{b\in B}|b-p|\right|\right\}.

Note that if
A,B
are bounded, then
d_{\mathrm{AW}}
and
d_{\mathrm{H}}
are equivalent. Furthermore, it can be shown (cf.
[
3
, Ch. 3]
) that

d_{\mathrm{H}}(A_{n}\cap B,A\cap B)\to 0\text{ for all }B\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}\text{ compact }\;\Rightarrow\;d_{\mathrm{AW}}(A_{n},A)\to 0.
