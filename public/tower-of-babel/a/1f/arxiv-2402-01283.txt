Abstract
. In this note we show that the usual notion of fuzzy norm defined on a linear space is equivalent to that of quasiconcave function, in the sense that every fuzzy norm N : X × ℝ → [ 0 , 1 ] N:X\times\mathbb{R}\to[0,1] defined on a (real or complex) linear space X X is uniquely determined by a quasiconcave function f : X → [ 0 , 1 ] f:X\to[0,1] . We explore the minimum requirements that we need to impose to some quasiconcave function f : X → [ 0 , 1 ] f:X\to[0,1] in order to define a fuzzy norm N : X × ℝ → [ 0 , 1 ] N:X\times\mathbb{R}\to[0,1] . Later we use this equivalence to prove some properties of fuzzy norms, like a generalisation of the celebrated Decomposition Theorem.



1.
Introduction

Since the seminal paper
[
1
]
, where T. Bag and S.K. Samanta
introduced their definition of fuzzy normed linear space and proved that, under
some circumstances, a fuzzy norm can be seen as a collection of
usual

norms, a lot of work has been carried out in fuzzy analysis. Some results are
essentially adaptations of classic results in functional analysis; see, e.g.,

[
4
,
10
]
, while other deal with purely fuzzy
concepts; see
[
9
,
11
,
13
]
.

The goal in this note is to show that any fuzzy norm, in the sense of

[
1
]
,
N:X\times\mathbb{R}\to[0,1]
defined on a
(real or complex) linear space
X
is uniquely determined by a
quasiconcave function
f:X\to[0,1]
. It should be noted that the
relation between both concepts is not difficult to prove and that in
some sense it was noticed in
[
8
]
, but there has been no further
development, maybe due to the fact that
[
8
]
appeared one year
before
[
1
]
.
As this kind of functions (and specially their counterpart, the quasiconvex
functions) has been thoroughly studied throughout the years, this equivalence
may give rise to a great opportunity to begin taking advantage of tools developed
in nonconvex optimization and see what can be obtained in fuzzy analysis
– see
[
5
]
and, more precisely,
[
3
]
and the references therein.

Later, in Section
3
, we show that some results about the
continuity of quasiconcave functions lead to the equivalence between
fuzzy convergence of sequences and usual convergence of sequences in
finite-dimensional spaces. We finish this paper proving a more general
statement of the Decomposition Theorem that the reader may find
in
[
1
]
.

Definition 1.1
.

Let
X
be a non-empty set. A fuzzy set in
X
is a function

\mu:X\to[0,1]
.

Definition 1.2

(
[
1
]
)
.

Let
X
be a vector space over the field
\mathbb{K}=\mathbb{R},\mathbb{C}
. A fuzzy norm
on
X
is a fuzzy set
N
in
X\times[0,\infty)
satisfying:

(N1)

N(x,0)=0
, for all
x\in X
;

(N2)

[N(x,t)=1
, for all
t>0]
if and only if
x=0
;

(N3)

N(\lambda x,t)=N\left(x,\frac{t}{|\lambda|}\right)
,
for all
t\geq 0
,
x\in X
and
0\neq\lambda\in\mathbb{K}
;

(N4)

N(x+y,t+s)\geq\min\{N(x,t),N(y,s)\}
, for all
x,y\in X
and
t,s\geq 0
;

(N5)

For each
x\in X
,
\lim_{t\to\infty}N(x,t)=1
.

We will say that the pair
(X,N)
is a fuzzy normed linear space (briefly, FNLS).

Remark 1.3
.

When defining the concept of fuzzy norm, instead of defining
N:X\times\mathbb{R}\to[0,1]

and imposing that
N(x,t)=0
for every
t<0
, we have chosen
N:X\times[0,\infty)\to[0,1]
.
Of course, both definitions are equivalent.

Observe that for each
x\in X
, the function
N_{x}:[0,\infty)\rightarrow[0,1]
,

N_{x}(t)=N(x,t)
is non-decreasing. Indeed, consider
t<s
, applying (N2) and (N4) we have

N_{x}(s)=N(x,s)\geq\min\{N(0,s-t),N(x,t)\}=N(x,t)=N_{x}(t).

Example 1.4

(
[
4
]
)
.

Given a normed space
(X,\|\cdot\|)
, the function

N_{\|\cdot\|}:X\times[0,\infty)\rightarrow[0,1]

defined by

N_{\|\cdot\|}(x,t)=\dfrac{t}{t+\|x\|},\qquad N_{\|\cdot\|}(0,0)=0,

is a fuzzy norm on
X
and is called the
standard fuzzy norm.

Definition 1.5
.

Given a (complex or real) linear space
X
, a function
f:X\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is
said to be quasiconcave if for all
x,y\in X
and
0<\lambda<1
, the
following holds

f(\lambda x+(1-\lambda)y)\geq\min\{f(x),f(y)\}.

2.
The main result

Throughout this section,
X
will denote a vector space over the field

\mathbb{K}=\mathbb{R},\mathbb{C}
, and we will denote by
\mathcal{F}
the set of fuzzy norms on
X
.

Let
\mathcal{A}
be the set of quasiconcave functions
f:X\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
such that

(A1)

f(0)=1
and, if
f(tx)=1
for every
t
, then
x=0
;

(A2)

\lim_{t\to 0}f(tx)=1
for every
x
;

(A3)

f(\lambda x)=f(|\lambda|x)
for all
\lambda\in\mathbb{K},x\in X
.

Theorem 2.1

(Characterisation of fuzzy norms)
.

The map

\begin{array}[]{ccl}\mathcal{A}&\longrightarrow&\mathcal{F}\\
f&\longmapsto&N_{f}(x,t)=\begin{cases}f\left(\frac{x}{t}\right)&\text{if}\quad t\neq 0;\\
0&\text{if}\quad t=0;\\
\end{cases}\end{array}

is bijective.

Proof.

We will show that given
f\in\mathcal{A}
,
N_{f}
is a fuzzy norm on
X

–it is clear that if the map is well defined, then it is injective:

(N1)

N_{f}(x,0)=0
, for all
x\in X
by the definition of
N_{f}
.

(N2)

N_{f}(0,t)=f\left(\frac{0}{t}\right)=1
, for all
t>0
.
If
x\neq 0
, then (A1) implies that there exists
t\in\mathbb{R}
such that

N_{f}(x,t)=f\left(\frac{x}{t}\right)\neq 1
.

(N3)

N_{f}(\lambda x,t)=f\left(\frac{\lambda x}{t}\right)=f\left(\frac{|\lambda|x}{t}\right)=N_{f}\left(x,\frac{t}{|\lambda|}\right)
, for all
t\geq 0
,
x\in X
and
0\neq\lambda\in\mathbb{K}
.

(N4)

For every
x,y\in X
and
t,s\geq 0

\displaystyle N_{f}(x+y,t+s)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle f\left(\frac{x+y}{t+s}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle f\left(\frac{t}{t+s}\cdot\frac{x}{t}+\frac{s}{t+s}\cdot\frac{y}{s}\right)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(*)}}{{\geq}}

\displaystyle\min\left\{f\left(\frac{x}{t}\right),f\left(\frac{y}{s}\right)\right\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\min\{N_{f}(x,t),N_{f}(y,s)\};

where the inequality
\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(*)}}{{\geq}}
holds because
f
is quasiconcave.

(N5)

Given
x\in X
,
\lim_{t\to\infty}N_{f}(x,t)=\lim_{t\to\infty}f\left(\frac{x}{t}\right)=\lim_{t\to 0}f(tx)=1
.

Now we need to show that, given
N\in\mathcal{F}
, the function
f_{N}(x)=N(x,1)

belongs to
\mathcal{A}
–with this we have that the map is a surjection.

(A0)

f_{N}
is quasiconcave. Indeed, for every
x,y\in X,\lambda\in[0,1]
,
one has:

\displaystyle f_{N}(\lambda x+(1-\lambda)y)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle N(\lambda x+(1-\lambda)y,1)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\min\{N(\lambda x,\lambda),N((1-\lambda)y,1-\lambda)\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\min\{N(x,1),N(y,1)\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\min\{f_{N}(x),f_{N}(y)\}.

(A1)

f_{N}(0)=N(0,1)=1
and, if
x\neq 0,
then there exists
t
such that

f_{N}(x)=N(x,1)\neq 1
by (N2).

(A2)

\lim_{t\to 0}f_{N}(tx)=\lim_{t\to 0}N(tx,1)=\lim_{t\to\infty}N(x,t)=1
by (N5).

(A3)

f_{N}(\lambda x)=N(\lambda x,1)=N(|\lambda|x,1)=f_{N}(|\lambda|x)
by (N3).

So, both concepts are equivalent.
∎

3.
Applications

In this section we analyse some consequences of the equivalence of quasiconcave
functions and fuzzy norms. First we show that the only topology defined by
fuzzy norms in finite-dimensional spaces is the topology given by any norm. Later we give a generalisation of the Decomposition Theorem.

3.1.
Finite-dimensional spaces

In this subsection, we will show some results that improve the ones that
can be found in
[
12
, Section 3]
for the case of finite-dimensional
spaces. Namely, while in
[
12
, Section 3]
we can find results on
infinite-dimensional spaces, all of them depend on the Conditions (N6) and (N7).

(N6)

[\forall t>0,N(x,t)>0]
implies
x=0
.

(N7)

For any non-zero element
x
,
N(x,\,.\,)
is a continuous function on
\mathbb{R}
and
strictly increasing on
\{t:0<N(x,t)<1\}
.

We will use the following results, stated as Proposition 3.8 and Theorem 3.2
in
[
3
]
for quasiconvex functions. Please take into account that a
function
f
is quasiconvex if and only if
-f
is quasiconcave, so
in
[
3
]
, the inequality that appears in the next Proposition is reversed.

Proposition 3.1

(
[
3
]
)
.

Assume that
f:E\to\mathbb{R}
is quasiconcave,
a,b\in E
,
f(b)>f(a)
and
f
is
continuous at
b
. Then
f
is continuous at
a
whenever

t\mapsto f(a+t(a-b))
is continuous at
t=0
.

Theorem 3.2

(
[
3
]
)
.

Assume that
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
is quasiconcave. Then,
f
is continuous at
a

if and only if
t\mapsto f(a+tb)
is continuous at
t=0
for every
b\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

Definition 3.3

(
[
2
]
, p. 536)
.

For
\mathbb{K}=\mathbb{R}
or
\mathbb{C}
we will denote as
N_{\mathbb{K}}
the fuzzy norm in
\mathbb{K}
defined as

(1)

N_{\mathbb{K}}(x,t)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{c}0\text{ if }t\leq|x|\\
1\text{ if }t>|x|\end{array}\qquad\right.

Definition 3.4

(
[
2
]
, p. 524)
.

A mapping
T
from
(U,N_{1})
to
(V,N_{2})
is said to be fuzzy continuous at

x_{0}\in U
if for every
t>0,\alpha\in(0,1)
there exist
s>0,\beta\in(0,1)

such that

N_{1}(x-x_{0},s)>\beta\Rightarrow N_{2}(T(x)-T(x_{0}),t)>\alpha.

Definition 3.5

(
[
2
]
, p. 524)
.

A mapping
T
from
(U,N_{1})
to
(V,N_{2})
is said to be sequentially fuzzy

x_{0}\in U
if

(2)

\lim_{n\to\infty}N_{1}(x_{n}-x_{0},t)=1,\forall\,t>0\Rightarrow\lim_{n\to\infty}N_{2}(T(x_{n})-T(x_{0}),t)=1,\forall\,t>0.

Theorem 3.6

(
[
2
]
, Theorem 3.2)
.

Let
T:(U,N_{1})\to(V,N_{2})
be a mapping where
(U,N_{1})
and
(V,N_{2})
are FNLS.
Then T is fuzzy continuous iff it is sequentially fuzzy continuous.

With these definitions and results in mind, we can obtain the following.

Lemma 3.7
.

Let
(X,N)
be a finite-dimensional FNLS. Then,

N(\,\cdot\,,t_{0}):X\to\mathbb{R}
is continuous at
x=0
for every
t_{0}
.

Proof.

Consider the quasiconcave function
f:X\to[0,1]
associated to
N
.
Conditions (A2) and (A1) give

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to 0}N(tx,1)=\lim_{t\to 0}f(tx)=1=f(0)

for every
x
, so Theorem
3.2
implies that
f
is continuous at 0.
Equivalently, that
N(\,\cdot\,,1)
is. For other positive
t_{0}
we also have

\lim_{x\to 0}N(x,t_{0})=\lim_{x\to 0}f(x/t_{0})=1,

so
N(\,\cdot\,,t_{0})
is continuous at 0 for every
t_{0}>0
. When
t_{0}=0
the
statement is trivial, so it is continuous for every
t_{0}\geq 0.

∎

Proposition 3.8
.

Let
(X,N)
be a finite-dimensional FNLS and consider a sequence

(x_{n})_{n}\subset X
. One has

\lim_{n\to\infty}N(x_{n},t)=1,\,\forall\,t>0\ \Longleftrightarrow\ \lim_{n\to\infty}x_{n}=0.

Proof.

If we have
(x_{n})_{n}\subset X
such that

\lim_{n\to\infty}N(x_{n},t)=1,\,\forall\,t>0,
then
(x_{n})_{n}\to 0
with respect
to the only linear topology with which we can endow
X
. Indeed, suppose on the
contrary that
(x_{n})_{n}
does not converge to 0. Substituting
t
by 1,
we have
(f(x_{n}))_{n}\to 1
. Now we have two options; either
(x_{n})_{n}
is bounded,
in which case it has an accumulation point
y\in X
, or it is unbounded.

In the first case, passing if necessary to a subsequence, we may suppose that

(x_{n})_{n}\to y
. As
f
is quasiconcave, for every
\lambda\in[0,1]
one has

1=f(0)\geq\lim_{n\to\infty}f(\lambda x_{n})\geq\lim_{n\to\infty}f(x_{n})=1,

so for every
\lambda y
with
0\leq\lambda\leq 1
there is some sequence
that converges to
\lambda y
and whose image through
f
converges to 1.
As
t\in[0,\infty)\mapsto f(ty)\in[0,1]
is a nonincreasing function,
it is continuous almost everywhere, in particular it is continuous at some

\lambda\in(1/2,1)
. Then,
f(\lambda y)<1=f(0)
and Proposition
3.1

imply that
f
is continuous at
\lambda y
. This means that

f(\lambda y)=\lim_{n\to\infty}f(\lambda x_{n})=1
so, in any
case,
f(\lambda y)=1
. This implies that
f(\mu y)=1

whenever
0\leq\mu\leq 1/2<\lambda
. In terms of
N
, this means that

1=N(\mu y,1)=N(y,1/\mu)=N(y,t)
for every
t=1/\mu\geq 2
. Choose
s>0

such that
N(y,s)<1
, such
s
exists because of (N2). It is clear that

\lim_{n\to\infty}N(x_{n},t)=1,\,\forall\,t>0
is equivalent to

\lim_{n\to\infty}N(2x_{n}/s,2t/s)=1,\,\forall\,t>0
and to

\lim_{n\to\infty}N(2x_{n}/s,t)=1,\,\forall\,t>0
. Now, the previous argument
shows that
N(y,s)=N(2y/(2s),1)=1,
a contradiction with the choice of
s
.
This way, we obtain that
(x_{n})_{n}
must converge to 0.

As for the second case, suppose that
(x_{n})_{n}
has no accumulation point.
We only need to choose a bounded neighbourhood
B
of 0
and take into account that, for every
n
such that
x_{n}\not\in B
, there is

0<\lambda_{n}<1
such that
\lambda_{n}x_{n}\in B\setminus\left(\frac{1}{2}B\right)
.
As
f
is quasiconcave,
f(\lambda_{n}x_{n})\geq\min\{f(0),f(x_{n})\}=f(x_{n})

so we can substitute each
x_{n}
by
\lambda_{n}x_{n}
and apply the previous case.

So, whenever the fuzzy limit of a sequence is 0, the sequence converges and
its limit is 0.

To show that the other implication also holds we just need to observe that

\lim(x_{n})_{n}=0
implies
\lim(f(x_{n}))_{n}=1
because
f(0)=1
and, by the
previous Lemma,
f
is continuous at
0
. This gives
\lim N(x_{n},1)=1
and for
any
t_{0}>0
we can apply the same argument to
(f(x_{n}/t_{0}))_{n}
.
∎

Theorem 3.9
.

Let
(X,N)
be a finite-dimensional FNLS and
t_{0}\geq 0
. Then, the fuzzy
continuity of
N(\,\cdot\,,t_{0})
is equivalent to its continuity. In
particular, it is always continuous at 0 and, if
N(x_{0},t_{0})<1
, then it
is continuous at
x_{0}\neq 0
if and only if
t\mapsto N(t,\,\cdot\,)

is continuous at
t_{0}
.

Proof.

By Theorem
3.6
we know that
N(\,\cdot\,,t_{0})
is fuzzy continuous
if and only if it is sequentially fuzzy continuous. It is a well-known fact that
this also happens with continuity and sequential continuity in metric spaces,
so it suffices to show that sequential continuity and fuzzy sequential
continuity are equivalent, but this is immediate from Proposition
3.8
.

Now, applying Proposition
3.1
we obtain that
f(x)=N(x,1)
is fuzzy
continuous at
x_{0}
if and only if
t\mapsto f(tx_{0})
is continuous at
t=1
.
The general case for an arbitrary
t_{0}\in(0,\infty)
is analogous, so we are done.
∎

Remark 3.10
.

Observe that if we consider
(\mathbb{R},N_{\mathbb{R}})
and the associated quasiconcave function

f_{\mathbb{R}}(x)=N_{\mathbb{R}}(x,1)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{c}0\text{ when }1\leq|x|\\
1\text{ when }1>|x|\end{array}\right.

f_{\mathbb{R}}

\pm 1

at

On the other hand, if Condition (N7) holds then

is continuous

N(x_{0},t_{0})
whenever

, provided the space is finite-dimensional.

3.2.
Decomposition Theorem

After a thorough reading of
[
2
]
and
[
7
]
, we have
realized that the Condition (N6) in the Decomposition Theorem that appears as
Theorem 2.2 in
[
1
]
is unnecessarily restrictive. This can be
seen as a consequence of the intuition that quasiconcave functions give rather
than a consequence of any results on this kind of functions. Namely, it is
well-known that a real function
f:X\to\mathbb{R}
is quasiconcave
if and only if every
S(f,\lambda)=\{x\in X:f(x)\geq\lambda\}

is convex. As the function
f_{N}
always fulfils
f_{N}(x)=f_{N}(-x)
, these
epigraphs are also symmetric. The only that each
\operatorname{Epi}(f,\lambda)
needs to fulfil
in order to define a norm via its Minkowsky functional is that it is bounded
and absorbing and these properties are easily characterized by means of the
behaviour of
f(tx)
when
t\to 0
and
t\to\infty
. Summarizing, we have
the following result, that generalises Theorem 2.2 in
[
1
]
.

Theorem 3.11

(Decomposition Theorem,
[
1
]
)
.

Let
(X,N)
be an FNLS. Assume further that,

(N6
′
)

For every
x\in X
,
N(x,t)
converges to
0
when
t\to 0

and define for each
0<\alpha<1
the function

p_{\alpha}(x)=\inf\{t>0:N(x,t)>\alpha\}=\inf\{t>0:f_{N}(x/t)>\alpha\}.

Then
\mathcal{P}=\{p_{\alpha}:\alpha\in(0,1)\}
is an ascending family of norms
on
X
.

Proof.

For the first part of the proof we need to show that each
p_{\alpha}
fulfils
the three conditions of the definition of norm. Namely,

(i) It vanishes exactly at
x=0.

Since
N(0,t)=1
for every
t>0
, we have that
p_{\alpha}(0)=0
.
Conversely, if
p_{\alpha}(x)=0
then

x=0
because for every
x\neq 0
,
\lim_{t\to 0}N(x,t)=0
by (N6
′
).

(ii) It is positively homogeneous:

\displaystyle p_{\alpha}(\lambda x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\inf\{t>0:N(\lambda x,t)>\alpha\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\inf\left\{t>0:N\left(x,\dfrac{t}{|\lambda|}\right)>\alpha\right\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\inf\{|\lambda|t>0:N(x,t)>\alpha\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle|\lambda|\inf\{t>0:N(x,t)>\alpha\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle|\lambda|p_{\alpha}(x).

(iii) And fulfils the triangle inequality:

If
f(x/t)>\alpha
and
f(y/s)>\alpha
, then the definition of quasiconcave function
gives, for every
\lambda\in[0,1],
the inequality

f(\lambda x/t+(1-\lambda)y/s)>\alpha
. With
\lambda=t/(t+s)
we obtain

\alpha<f\left(\frac{t}{t+s}x/t+\frac{s}{t+s}y/s\right)=f((x+y)/(t+s)).

So,
p_{\alpha}(x)\leq t
and
p_{\alpha}(y)\leq s
imply
p_{\alpha}(x+y)\leq t+s.

To show that
\mathcal{P}=\{p_{\alpha}\}_{0<\alpha<1}
is an ascending family,
we just need to observe that, for
\alpha\geq\beta
one has

\displaystyle p_{\alpha}(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\inf\{t>0:N(x,t)>\alpha\}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\inf\{t>0:N(x,t)>\beta\}=p_{\beta}(x),

because
N(x,t)>\beta
implies
N(x,t)>\alpha
.
∎

Remark 3.12
.

The original statement of Theorem
3.11
includes as a hypothesis
that
N(x,\,\cdot\,)
must vanish eventually, i.e., that for every
x

there exists
t_{x}>0
such that
N(x,t)=0
for every
t\leq t_{x}
. It is written
in a different way,
but it is clear that our Condition (N6
′
) is much weaker
than (N6) and it does not hinder the proof.

3.3.
Concluding comments and remarks

We have seen that the easy observation labelled as Theorem
2.1
is
useful when it comes to analyse the continuity of fuzzy norms, and it will
probably be useful in other aspects of fuzzy functional analysis.

We think that it is worth pointing out that there is something that does
not feel right about the choice of
(\mathbb{K},N_{\mathbb{K}})
as the basic structure amongst
the fuzzy normed linear spaces, as in the definition of the dual FNLS that
the reader can find in
[
2
, Definition 5.1]
.
Of course,
(\mathbb{K},N_{\mathbb{K}})
can be seen as the most natural structure
of FNLS. Indeed, if for any fuzzy norm
N
we think
N(x,t)
as the truth
value of the statement “the norm of
x
is less than or equal to the real
number
t
” (see, e.g.,
[
6
]
), then
N_{\mathbb{K}}(x,t)
equals 1 if
|x|\leq t

and vanishes if
