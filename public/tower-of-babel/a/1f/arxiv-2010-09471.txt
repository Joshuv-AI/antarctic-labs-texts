Abstract
. In rendez-vous protocols an arbitrarily large number of indistinguishable finite-state agents interact in pairs. The cut-off problem asks if there exists a number B B such that all initial configurations of the protocol with at least B B agents in a given initial state can reach a final configuration with all agents in a given final state. In a recent paper [ HS20 ] , Horn and Sangnier proved that the cut-off problem is decidable (and at least as hard as the Petri net reachability problem) for protocols with a leader, and in 𝖤𝖷𝖯𝖲𝖯𝖠𝖢𝖤 \mathsf{EXPSPACE} for leaderless protocols. Further, for the special class of symmetric protocols they reduce these bounds to 𝖯𝖲𝖯𝖠𝖢𝖤 \mathsf{PSPACE} and 𝖭𝖯 \mathsf{NP} , respectively. The problem of lowering these upper bounds or finding matching lower bounds was left open. We show that the cut-off problem is 𝖯 \mathsf{P} -complete for leaderless protocols and in 𝖭𝖢 \mathsf{NC} for leaderless symmetric protocols. Further, we also consider a variant of the cut-off problem suggested in [ HS20 ] , which we call the bounded-loss cut-off problem and prove that this problem is 𝖯 \mathsf{P} -complete for leaderless protocols and 𝖭𝖫 \mathsf{NL} -complete for leaderless symmetric protocols. Finally, by reusing some of the techniques applied for the analysis of leaderless protocols, we show that the cut-off problem for symmetric protocols with a leader is 𝖭𝖯 \mathsf{NP} -complete, thereby improving upon all the elementary upper bounds of [ HS20 ] .

Introduction

Distributed systems are often designed for an unbounded number of participating agents.
Therefore, they are not just one system, but an infinite family of systems, one for each
number of agents. Parameterized verification addresses the problem of checking that all systems in
the family satisfy a given specification.

In many application areas, agents are indistinguishable. This is the case in computational biology, where cells or molecules have no identities; in some security applications, where the agents’ identities should stay private; or in applications where the identities can be abstracted away, like certain classes of multithreaded programs
[
GS92
,
AAD+06
,
SCWB08
,
BMWK09
,
KKW10
,
NB15
]
. Following
[
BMWK09
,
KKW10
]
, we use the term
replicated systems
for distributed systems with indistinguishable agents. Replicated systems include population protocols, broadcast protocols, threshold automata, and many other models
[
GS92
,
AAD+06
,
EFM99
,
DSTZ12
,
GKS+14
]
. They also arise after applying a
counter abstraction

[
PXZ02
,
BMWK09
]
. In finite-state replicated systems the global state of the system is determined by the function (usually called a
configuration
) that assigns to each state the number of agents that currently occupy it.
This feature makes many verification problems decidable
[
BJK+15
,
Esp16
]
.

Surprisingly, there is no a priori relation between the complexity of a parameterized verification question (i.e., whether a given property holds for all initial configurations, or, equivalently, whether its negation holds for some configuration), and the complexity of its corresponding single-instance question (whether the property holds for a fixed initial configuration). Consider replicated systems where agents interact in pairs
[
GS92
,
HS20
,
AAD+06
]
. The complexity of single-instance questions is very robust. Indeed, checking most properties, including all properties expressible in LTL and CTL, is
\mathsf{PSPACE}
-complete
[
Esp96
]
. On the contrary, the complexity of parameterized questions is very fragile, as exemplified by the following example. While the existence of a reachable configuration that populates a given state with
at least
one agent is in
\mathsf{P}
, and so well below
\mathsf{PSPACE}
, the existence of a reachable configuration that populates a given state with
exactly
one agent is as hard as the reachability problem for Petri nets, and so non-primitive recursive
[
Ler21
,
Las22
,
CO21
]
. This fragility makes the analysis of parameterized questions very interesting, but also much harder.

Work on parameterized verification has concentrated on whether every initial configuration satisfies
a given property (see e.g.
[
GS92
,
EFM99
,
BMWK09
,
KKW10
,
DSTZ12
]
). However, applications often lead to questions of the form “do all initial configurations
in a given set
satisfy the property?”, “do infinitely many initial configurations satisfy the property?”, or “do all but finitely many initial configurations satisfy the property?”. An example of the first kind is proving correctness of population protocols, where the specification requires that for a given partition
\mathcal{I}_{0}
,
\mathcal{I}_{1}
of the set of initial configurations, and a partition
Q_{0},Q_{1}
of the set of states, runs starting from
\mathcal{I}_{0}
eventually trap all agents within
Q_{0}
, and similarly for
\mathcal{I}_{1}
and
Q_{1}

[
EGLM17
]
. An example of the third kind is the existence of

; cut-off properties state the existence of an initial configuration such that for all larger initial configurations some given property holds
[
EK02
,
BJK+15
]
. A systematic study of the complexity of these questions is still out of reach, but first results are appearing. In particular, Horn and Sangnier have recently studied the complexity of the
cut-off problem
for parameterized rendez-vous networks
[
HS20
]
. The problem takes as input a network with one single initial state
\mathit{init}
and one single final state
\mathit{fin}

B
such that for every number of agents

is reachable from the initial configuration in which all agents are in state
\mathit{init}

Our results follow from two lemmas, called the Scaling and Insertion Lemmas, that

Rendez-vous Protocols and the Cut-off Problem.

Let
\Sigma

call the communication alphabet and we let
RV(\Sigma)=\{!a,?a:a\in\Sigma\}

!a
denotes that the message

is sent and

{defi}

A

(Q,\Sigma,\mathit{init},\mathit{fin},R)

The size

needed to encode
\mathcal{P}
using some standard encoding.
A configuration

is a multiset of states, where

should be interpreted as the number of agents in state
q

We use
\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{P})
to denote the set of all configurations of
\mathcal{P}

An initial (resp. final) configuration

is a configuration such that
C(q)=0

if

(resp.
C(q)=0
if
q\neq\mathit{fin}
). We use
C_{\mathit{init}}^{n}
(resp.

) to denote the initial (resp. final) configuration
such that
C_{\mathit{init}}^{n}(\mathit{init})=n
(resp.
C_{\mathit{fin}}^{n}(\mathit{fin})=n
).

The operational semantics of a rendez-vous protocol
\mathcal{P}
is given by
means of a transition system between the configurations of
\mathcal{P}
.
Suppose
a
is a message in
\Sigma
and
r=(p,!a,p^{\prime})
and
r^{\prime}=(q,?a,q^{\prime})
are two rules of
R
.
For any two configurations
C
and
C^{\prime}
, we say that
C\xRightarrow{r,r^{\prime}}C^{\prime}
if

C\geq\Lbag p,q\Rbag
and
C^{\prime}=C-\Lbag p,q\Rbag+\Lbag p^{\prime},q^{\prime}\Rbag
. Intuitively, the configuration

has agents at states
p
and
q
, and
the agent at state
p
sends the message

and moves to
p^{\prime}
and the agent at state
q
receives this
message and moves to
q^{\prime}
. We let
C\Rightarrow C^{\prime}
denote that there exist rules
r,r^{\prime}
for which
C\xRightarrow{r,r^{\prime}}C^{\prime}

is a transition from
C
to
C^{\prime}
.
As usual,
\xRightarrow{*}

denotes the reflexive and transitive closure of
\Rightarrow
. The
cut-off problem for rendez-vous protocols
, as stated in
[
HS20
]
, is then defined as the following decision problem.

Given:
A rendez-vous protocol
\mathcal{P}

Decide:
Does there exist
B\in\mathbb{N}
such that
C_{\mathit{init}}^{n}\xRightarrow{*}C_{\mathit{fin}}^{n}

If such a

\mathcal{P}
admits a cut-off and
that
B
is a cut-off for

.

{exa}

Let us consider the following protocol, which is taken from a slightly modified version of the family of protocols described in Figure 5 of
[

]
.

\mathit{init}
q_{1}
\mathit{fin}
!a
!b
!b
?a
?b

An example of a rendez-vous protocol

We can show that 4 is a cut-off for this protocol. Indeed, if

, then we have the run

C^{n}_{\mathit{init}}\Rightarrow{}\Lbag(n-2)\cdot\mathit{init}+2\cdot q_{1}\Rbag\Rightarrow{}\Lbag(n-3)\cdot\mathit{init}+q_{1}+2\cdot\mathit{fin}\Rbag\Rightarrow{}\Lbag(n-4)\cdot\mathit{init}+4\cdot\mathit{fin}\Rbag
. The first transition involves sending and receiving
the message
a
from the state
\mathit{init}
and the other two involve sending the message
b
from
q_{1}
and receiving it from

\mathit{init}
. Once we reach
\Lbag(n-4)\cdot\mathit{init}+4\cdot\mathit{fin}\Rbag

Further, we can show that no number strictly less than 4 can be a cut-off for this protocol. Indeed,
suppose

For a transition

Given a Petri net

Throughout the paper, we will repeatedly use the following property of Petri nets, called the

Suppose

Proof 2.1
.

By induction on the length of

and

2.3.
From rendez-vous protocols to Petri nets.

We now show that rendez-vous protocols can be seen as a special class of Petri nets.
Indeed, rendez-vous protocols can be thought of as Petri nets in which no tokens are created or destroyed during a run.

Let
\mathcal{P}=(Q,\Sigma,\mathit{init},\mathit{fin},R)
be a rendez-vous protocol.
Create a Petri net
\mathcal{N}_{\mathcal{P}}=(P,T,\mathit{Pre},\mathit{Post})
as follows. The set of places is
Q
.
For each message
a\in\Sigma
and for each pair of rules

r=(q,!a,s),\ r^{\prime}=(q^{\prime},?a,s^{\prime})\in R
, add a transition

t_{r,r^{\prime}}
to
\mathcal{N}_{\mathcal{P}}
and set

•

\mathit{Pre}[p,t_{r,r^{\prime}}]=0
for every

\mathit{Post}[p,t_{r,r^{\prime}}]=0
for every
p\notin\{s,s^{\prime}\}

•

If
q=q^{\prime}
then
\mathit{Pre}[q,t_{r,r^{\prime}}]=2
, otherwise

\mathit{Pre}[q,t_{r,r^{\prime}}]=\mathit{Pre}[q^{\prime},t_{r,r^{\prime}}]=1

•

If
s=s^{\prime}
then
\mathit{Post}[s,t_{r,r^{\prime}}]=2
, otherwise

\mathit{Post}[s,t_{r,r^{\prime}}]=\mathit{Post}[s^{\prime},t_{r,r^{\prime}}]=1

Intuitively, the transition
t_{r,r^{\prime}}
affects only the places in the set
\{q,q^{\prime},s,s^{\prime}\}
.
Firing
t_{r,r^{\prime}}
involves removing two tokens from
q
if
q=q^{\prime}
, and otherwise one token each from
q
and
q^{\prime}
.
Then, after removing these tokens, we put back two tokens in
s
if
s=s^{\prime}
, and otherwise we put one token each
in
s
and
s^{\prime}
.

Due to the way
\mathcal{N}_{\mathcal{P}}
is defined, we have that any configuration of the protocol
\mathcal{P}
is also
a marking of
\mathcal{N}_{\mathcal{P}}
, and vice versa. Further, we have the following proposition, whose proof immediately
follows from the definition of
\mathcal{N}_{\mathcal{P}}
.

Proposition 2
.

For any pair of configurations
C,C^{\prime}
and any pair of rules
r,r^{\prime}

we have that

C\xRightarrow{r,r^{\prime}}C^{\prime}
over the protocol
\mathcal{P}
if and only if

C\xrightarrow{t_{r,r^{\prime}}}C^{\prime}
over the Petri net
\mathcal{N}_{\mathcal{P}}
.
Consequently, it follows that
C\xRightarrow{*}C^{\prime}
over the protocol
\mathcal{P}
if and only if
C\xrightarrow{*}C^{\prime}
over the Petri net
\mathcal{N}_{\mathcal{P}}
.

We can now define
the cut-off problem for Petri nets
in the following manner.

Given :
A Petri net system
(\mathcal{N},M,M^{\prime})

Decide:
Does there exist
B\in\mathbb{N}

such that
n\cdot M\xrightarrow{*}n\cdot M^{\prime}
for every

?

If such a
B
exists, then we say that
(\mathcal{N},M,M^{\prime})
admits a cut-off and that
B
is a cut-off for
\mathcal{P}
.
By Proposition
2
, note that
B
is a cut-off for a protocol
\mathcal{P}
if and only if
B
is a cut-off for
the Petri net system
(\mathcal{N},\Lbag\mathit{init}\Rbag,\Lbag\mathit{fin}\Rbag)
. Hence, the cut-off problem for Petri nets
generalizes the cut-off problem for rendez-vous protocols.

3.
The cut-off problem for acyclic Petri nets

As a warm-up to the cut-off problem, we first show that the cut-off problem for
acyclic Petri nets
can be solved in polynomial time.
The reason for considering this special case first is that it illustrates one of the main
ideas of the general case in a very pure form.

Let us fix a Petri net system
(\mathcal{N},M,M^{\prime})
for the rest of this section,
where
\mathcal{N}=(P,T,Pre,Post)
is acyclic and
\mathcal{A}
is
its incidence matrix. It is well-known that in acyclic Petri nets the reachability relation is characterized by the marking equation.

{propC}

[
[
Mur89
, Theorem 16]
]
Let
(\mathcal{N},M,M^{\prime})
be an acyclic Petri net system. For every vector
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{N}^{T}
,

\mathbf{x}
is a solution of the marking equation if and only if there is a firing sequence
\sigma
such that

\overrightarrow{\sigma}=\mathbf{x}
and
M\xrightarrow{\sigma}M^{\prime}
. Consequently,
M\xrightarrow{*}M^{\prime}
if and only if the marking equation has a nonnegative integer solution.

This proposition shows that the reachability problem for acyclic Petri nets reduces to the feasibility problem (i.e., deciding the existence of a solution) for systems of linear Diophantine equations over the nonnegative integers. So the reachability problem for acyclic Petri nets is in
\mathsf{NP}
, and in fact both the reachability and the feasibility problems are
\mathsf{NP}
-complete
[
EN94
]
.

There are two ways to relax the conditions on the solution so as to make the feasibility problem solvable in polynomial time. Feasibility over the non-negative
rationals
and feasibility over all integers for systems of linear equations are both in
\mathsf{P}
. Indeed, the feasibility
problem over the non-negative rationals is simply an instance of the linear programming problem, which is in polynomial time.
Further, feasibility in
\mathbb{Z}
can be decided in polynomial time after computing the Smith or Hermite normal forms of the matrix in the marking equation (see e.g.
[
PZ89
]
), which can themselves be computed in polynomial time
[
KB79
]
. We now show that the cut-off problem for acyclic Petri net systems can be reduced to solving a polynomial number of instances of the linear programming problem
and the feasibility problem for systems of linear equations over integers.

3.1.
Characterizing acyclic systems with cut-offs

Horn and Sangnier proved in
[
HS20
]
a very useful characterization of cut-off admitting rendez-vous protocols: A rendez-vous protocol
\mathcal{P}
admits a cut-off if and only if
there exists
n\in\mathbb{N}
such that
C_{\mathit{init}}^{n}\xRightarrow{*}C_{\mathit{fin}}^{n}
and
C_{\mathit{init}}^{n+1}\xRightarrow{*}C_{\mathit{fin}}^{n+1}
. Their proof immediately generalizes to the case of Petri nets. Here, we refine their characterization and proof in a small, but important way, which will be helpful later on.

{lemC}

[
[
HS20
, Lemma 23]
]
A Petri net system
(\mathcal{N},M,M^{\prime})
(acyclic or not) admits a cut-off if and only if
there exists
n\in\mathbb{N}
and firing sequences
\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}
such that
n\cdot M\xrightarrow{\sigma}n\cdot M^{\prime}
,
(n+1)\cdot M\xrightarrow{\sigma^{\prime}}(n+1)\cdot M^{\prime}

and
{\llbracket\sigma^{\prime}\rrbracket}\subseteq{\llbracket\sigma\rrbracket}
. Moreover if such
n,\sigma
and
\sigma^{\prime}
exist,
then
n^{2}
is a cut-off for the system.

Proof 3.1
.

Suppose
(\mathcal{N},M,M^{\prime})
admits a cut-off.
Hence there exists
B\in\mathbb{N}
such that
for all
n\geq B
, there is a firing sequence
\sigma_{n}
satisfying
nM\xrightarrow{\sigma_{n}}nM^{\prime}
.
Let
T^{\prime}=\{t_{1},\dots,t_{k}\}
be the set
\bigcup_{n\geq B}{\llbracket\sigma_{n}\rrbracket}
.
For every transition
t_{i}
in
T^{\prime}
, let
n_{i}
be such that
\sigma_{n_{i}}
contains an occurrence of
t_{i}
. Further, let
n=\sum_{t_{i}\in T^{\prime}}n_{i}

and
\sigma=\sigma_{n_{1}}\sigma_{n_{2}}\dots\sigma_{n_{k}}
. Notice that, since
n_{i}M\xrightarrow{\sigma_{n_{i}}}n_{i}M^{\prime}
for each
i
, by the monotonicity property we have
nM\xrightarrow{\sigma}nM^{\prime}
. Since
\sigma
contain at least one occurrence of each transition in
T^{\prime}
, if we set
\sigma^{\prime}=\sigma_{n+1}
, the claim is satisfied.

Suppose there exists
n\in\mathbb{N}
and firing sequences
\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}
such that
n\cdot M\xrightarrow{\sigma}n\cdot M^{\prime}
,
(n+1)\cdot M\xrightarrow{\sigma^{\prime}}(n+1)\cdot M^{\prime}

and
{\llbracket\sigma^{\prime}\rrbracket}\subseteq{\llbracket\sigma\rrbracket}
. Let
s\geq n^{2}
. We can write
s
as
s=qn+r
for some
q\in\mathbb{N}
and some
r
such that
0\leq r\leq n-1
.
Since
s\geq n^{2}
, it follows that
q\geq n>r
. Hence, we can rewrite
s

s=r(n+1)+(q-r)n

. Hence,
n^{2}
is a cut-off for the system.

Theorem 5

Letting

(

3.2.
Polynomial time algorithm

We derive a polynomial time algorithm for the cut-off
problem from the characterization of Theorem
5
.
The first step is the following lemma. A very similar lemma is proved in
[
FH15
]
, but since the proof
is short we give it for the sake of completeness.

Lemma 6
.

If the marking equation is feasible over
\mathbb{Q}_{\geq 0}
, then one can compute in polynomial time a solution
\mathbf{u}
such that
for every solution
\mathbf{y}
,
{\llbracket\mathbf{y}\rrbracket}\subseteq{\llbracket\mathbf{u}\rrbracket}
.

Proof 3.3
.

If
\mathbf{y},\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{Q}_{\geq 0}^{T}
are solutions of the marking equation, then we have

M^{\prime}=M+\mathcal{A}((\mathbf{y}+\mathbf{z})/2)

and
{\llbracket\mathbf{y}\rrbracket}\cup{\llbracket\mathbf{z}\rrbracket}\subseteq{\llbracket(\mathbf{y}+\mathbf{z})/2\rrbracket}
. Hence if the marking
equation is feasible over
\mathbb{Q}_{\geq 0}
, then there is a solution
\mathbf{u}
such that for every solution
\mathbf{y}
,
{\llbracket\mathbf{y}\rrbracket}\subseteq{\llbracket\mathbf{u}\rrbracket}
.

To find such a solution in polynomial time we proceed as follows.
For every transition
t
we solve the linear program

M^{\prime}=M+\mathcal{A}\mathbf{v},\mathbf{v}\geq\mathbf{0},\mathbf{v}_{t}>0
.
(Recall that solving linear programs over the non-negative rationals
can be done in polynomial time). Let
\{t_{1},\ldots,t_{n}\}
be the set of transitions whose associated
linear programs are feasible over
\mathbb{Q}_{\geq 0}^{T}
,
and let
\{\mathbf{u}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{u}_{n}\}
be solutions to these programs.
Then
\mathbf{u}=1/n\cdot\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{u}_{i}

is a solution of the marking equation that satisfies the desired property.

We now have all the ingredients to give a polynomial time
algorithm.

Theorem 7
.

The cut-off problem for acyclic net systems can be solved in polynomial time.

Proof 3.4
.

First, we check that the marking equation
has a solution over the non-negative rationals.
If such a solution does not exist, by Theorem
5

the given net system does not admit a cut-off.

Suppose such a solution exists.
By Lemma
6
we can find a non-negative
rational solution
\mathbf{x}
with maximum support in polynomial time. Let
U
contain
all the transitions
t
such that
\mathbf{x}_{t}=0
.
We now check in polynomial time if the marking equation has a solution
\mathbf{y}
over

\mathbb{Z}^{T}
such that
\mathbf{y}_{t}=0

for every
t\in U
. By Theorem
5
such a solution exists
if and only if the net system admits a cut-off.

4.
The Scaling and Insertion lemmas

Similar to the case of acyclic net systems, we would like to provide
a characterization of net systems admitting a cut-off and then
use this characterization to derive a polynomial time algorithm.
Unfortunately, for general net systems there is no
characterization of reachability akin to Proposition
3
for
acyclic systems.
To this end, we prove two intermediate lemmas to help us come
up with a characterization for (general) net systems which admit a cut-off. We believe that these two lemmas could be of independent interest in their
own right. Further, the proofs of both the lemmas are provided so that
it will enable us later on to derive a bound on the cut-off for
net systems.

4.1.
The Scaling Lemma

The Scaling Lemma shows that, given a Petri net system
(\mathcal{N},M,M^{\prime})
, deciding whether
nM\xrightarrow{*}nM^{\prime}
holds for some
n\geq 1
can be done in polynomial time; moreover, if
nM\xrightarrow{*}nM^{\prime}
holds for some
n
, then it holds for some
n
which can be described by at most
(|\mathcal{N}|(\log\|{M}\|+\log\|{M^{\prime}}\|))^{O(1)}
bits. The name of the lemma is due to the fact that the firing sequence leading from
nM
to
nM^{\prime}
is obtained by
scaling up
a
continuous firing sequence
from
M
to
M^{\prime}
; the existence of such a continuous firing sequence can be decided in polynomial time due to a result by Fraca and Haddad
[
FH15
]
.

In the rest of the section we first recall continuous Petri nets and the characterization of
[
FH15
]
, and then present the Scaling Lemma. As mentioned in the introduction, this lemma is implicitly proved in
[
FH15
]
, but the bound on
n
is hidden in the details of the proof, and we make it explicit here.

4.1.1.
Reachability in continuous Petri nets.

Petri nets can be given a
continuous semantics
(see e.g.
[
AD98
,
RHS10
,
FH15
]
), in which
markings are continuous multisets; we call them
continuous markings
.
A continuous marking
M
enables a transition
t

with factor
\lambda\in(0,1]
if
M(p)\geq\lambda\cdot Pre[p,t]
for every place
p
; we also say that
M
enables
\lambda t
.
If
M
enables
\lambda t
, then
\lambda t
can fire or occur, leading to a new marking
M^{\prime}
given
by
M^{\prime}(p)=M(p)+\lambda\cdot\mathcal{A}[p,t]
for every
p\in P
. We denote this by
M\mathrel{\mathop{\vtop{\halign{\hfil#\hfil\cr\rightarrowfill\cr\hphantom{$\scriptstyle\mskip 8.0mu{\lambda t}\mskip 8.0mu$}\cr\vrule height=0.0pt,width=15.00002pt\cr$\scriptscriptstyle{\mathbb{Q}}$\cr}}}\limits^{\lambda t}}M^{\prime}
,
and say that
M^{\prime}
is reached from
M
by firing
\lambda t
. A
continuous firing sequence
is any sequence of the form
\sigma=\lambda_{1}t_{1},\lambda_{2}t_{2},\dots,\lambda_{k}t_{k}\in((0,1]\times T)^{*}
.
We let
M\mathrel{\mathop{\vtop{\halign{\hfil#\hfil\cr\rightarrowfill\cr\hphantom{$\scriptstyle\mskip 8.0mu{\sigma}\mskip 8.0mu$}\cr\vrule height=0.0pt,width=15.00002pt\cr$\scriptscriptstyle{\mathbb{Q}}$\cr}}}\limits^{\sigma}}M^{\prime}
denote that there exist continuous markings
M_{1},\dots,M_{k-1}

such that
M\mathrel{\mathop{\vtop{\halign{\hfil#\hfil\cr\rightarrowfill\cr\hphantom{$\scriptstyle\mskip 8.0mu{\lambda_{1}t_{1}}\mskip 8.0mu$}\cr\vrule height=0.0pt,width=15.00002pt\cr$\scriptscriptstyle{\mathbb{Q}}$\cr}}}\limits^{\lambda_{1}t_{1}}}M_{1}\mathrel{\mathop{\vtop{\halign{\hfil#\hfil\cr\rightarrowfill\cr\hphantom{$\scriptstyle\mskip 8.0mu{\lambda_{2}t_{2}}\mskip 8.0mu$}\cr\vrule height=0.0pt,width=15.00002pt\cr$\scriptscriptstyle{\mathbb{Q}}$\cr}}}\limits^{\lambda_{2}t_{2}}}M_{2}\cdots M_{k-1}\mathrel{\mathop{\vtop{\halign{\hfil#\hfil\cr\rightarrowfill\cr\hphantom{$\scriptstyle\mskip 8.0mu{\lambda_{k}t_{k}}\mskip 8.0mu$}\cr\vrule height=0.0pt,width=15.00002pt\cr$\scriptscriptstyle{\mathbb{Q}}$\cr}}}\limits^{\lambda_{k}t_{k}}}M^{\prime}
. We can then define
M\mathrel{\mathop{\vtop{\halign{\hfil#\hfil\cr\rightarrowfill\cr\hphantom{$\scriptstyle\mskip 8.0mu{*}\mskip 8.0mu$}\cr\vrule height=0.0pt,width=15.00002pt\cr$\scriptscriptstyle{\mathbb{Q}}$\cr}}}\limits^{*}}M^{\prime}
,
M\mathrel{\mathop{\vtop{\halign{\hfil#\hfil\cr\rightarrowfill\cr\hphantom{$\scriptstyle\mskip 8.0mu{\sigma}\mskip 8.0mu$}\cr\vrule height=0.0pt,width=15.00002pt\cr$\scriptscriptstyle{\mathbb{Q}}$\cr}}}\limits^{\sigma}}

and
\mathrel{\mathop{\vtop{\halign{\hfil#\hfil\cr\rightarrowfill\cr\hphantom{$\scriptstyle\mskip 8.0mu{\sigma}\mskip 8.0mu$}\cr\vrule height=0.0pt,width=15.00002pt\cr$\scriptscriptstyle{\mathbb{Q}}$\cr}}}\limits^{\sigma}}M
in a similar manner as before.

The
Parikh image
of
\sigma=\lambda_{1}t_{1},\lambda_{2}t_{2},\dots,\lambda_{k}t_{k}

is the vector
\overrightarrow{\sigma}\in\mathbb{Q}_{\geq 0}^{T}
where
\overrightarrow{\sigma}[t]=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\delta_{i,t}\lambda_{i}
,
where
\delta_{i,t}=1
if
t_{i}=t
and
0
otherwise.
The support of
\sigma
is the support of its Parikh image
\overrightarrow{\sigma}
.
If
M\mathrel{\mathop{\vtop{\halign{\hfil#\hfil\cr\rightarrowfill\cr\hphantom{$\scriptstyle\mskip 8.0mu{\sigma}\mskip 8.0mu$}\cr\vrule height=0.0pt,width=15.00002pt\cr$\scriptscriptstyle{\mathbb{Q}}$\cr}}}\limits^{\sigma}}M^{\prime}
then
\overrightarrow{\sigma}
is a solution of the marking equation over
\mathbb{Q}_{\geq 0}^{T}
, but the converse does not hold. In
[
FH15
]
, Fraca and Haddad strengthen this necessary condition to make it also sufficient, and use the resulting characterization to derive a polynomial time algorithm.

4.1.2.
Scaling.

(\mathcal{N},M,M^{\prime})
be a net system such that
M~\mathrel{\mathop{\vtop{\halign{\hfil#\hfil\cr\rightarrowfill\cr\hphantom{$\scriptstyle\mskip 8.0mu{\sigma}\mskip 8.0mu$}\cr\vrule height=0.0pt,width=15.33325pt\cr$\scriptscriptstyle{\mathbb{Q}}$\cr}}}\limits^{\sigma}}~M^{\prime}
for some
\sigma
.
Then there exist
n
and
m
which can be described by a polynomial number of bits in
|\mathcal{N}|(\log\|{M}\|+\log\|{M^{\prime}}\|)

such that

nM\xrightarrow{\sigma}nM^{\prime}
for some
\tau
with
{\llbracket\tau\rrbracket}={\llbracket\sigma\rrbracket}
and
\|{\overrightarrow{\tau}}\|\leq m
.

Proof 4.5
.

Suppose
M\xrightarrow[\mathbb{Q}]{\sigma}M^{\prime}
. Let
U
be the support of
\sigma
.
By
[
BFHH17
, Proposition 3.2]
, there is a formula
\phi(M,M^{\prime},\mathbf{v})
in the existential
theory of linear rational arithmetic Th
(\mathbb{Q},+,<)
, whose size is linear in the size of the net
\mathcal{N}
such that
\phi(M,M^{\prime},\mathbf{x})

is true if and only if there exists
\sigma^{\prime}
such that
\overrightarrow{\sigma^{\prime}}=\mathbf{x}

and
M\xrightarrow[\mathbb{Q}]{\sigma^{\prime}}M^{\prime}
. To this formula, let us add the constraints
\mathbf{v}_{t}>0\iff t\in U
and
let the resulting formula be
\xi
. Note that
\xi(M,M^{\prime},\mathbf{x})
is true if and only if there exists
\sigma^{\prime}

such that
\overrightarrow{\sigma^{\prime}}=\mathbf{x},{\llbracket\sigma^{\prime}\rrbracket}=U
and
M\xrightarrow[\mathbb{Q}]{\sigma^{\prime}}M^{\prime}
.

By
[
Son85
, Lemma 3.2]
if a formula in the existential theory of
linear rational arithmetic is satisfiable, then it
is satisfiable by a solution which can be described using
a polynomial number of bits in the size of the formula.
Hence, applying this result to the formula
\xi(M,M^{\prime},\mathbf{v})
, we get that there exists
\tau^{\prime}
such that
M\mathrel{\mathop{\vtop{\halign{\hfil#\hfil\cr\rightarrowfill\cr\hphantom{$\scriptstyle\mskip 8.0mu{\tau^{\prime}}\mskip 8.0mu$}\cr\vrule height=0.0pt,width=15.33325pt\cr$\scriptscriptstyle{\mathbb{Q}}$\cr}}}\limits^{\tau^{\prime}}}M^{\prime}
,

{\llbracket\tau^{\prime}\rrbracket}={\llbracket\sigma\rrbracket}
and the numerator and denominator of every entry of
\overrightarrow{\tau^{\prime}}
can be described using a polynomial number of bits in
|\mathcal{N}|(\log\|{M}\|+\log\|{M^{\prime}}\|)
. Notice that the smallest natural number
k
such that

k\cdot\overrightarrow{\tau^{\prime}}\in\mathbb{N}^{T}
is at most the
least common multiple of the denominators of all
the numbers in the vector
\overrightarrow{\tau^{\prime}}
.
Since the size of
{\llbracket\tau^{\prime}\rrbracket}
is at most the number of transitions
of
\mathcal{N}
, if we let
\mathit{w}
be the weight of the net
\mathcal{N}
, then it
is easy to verify that the quantities
(\mathit{w}+1)^{{\llbracket\tau^{\prime}\rrbracket}}
,
\|{\overrightarrow{\tau^{\prime}}}\|
and
k

can all be described using a polynomial number of bits in
|\mathcal{N}|(\log\|{M}\|+\log\|{M^{\prime}}\|)
.
Applying Lemma
8
now finishes the proof.

4.2.
The Insertion Lemma

In the acyclic case, the existence of a cut-off is roughly characterized by the existence of solutions to the marking equation
over
\mathbb{Q}_{\geq 0}^{T}
and
\mathbb{Z}^{T}
. Intuitively, in the general case we replace the existence of solutions over
\mathbb{Q}_{\geq 0}^{T}
by the conditions of the Scaling Lemma, and the existence of solutions over
\mathbb{Z}^{T}
by the Insertion Lemma:

Lemma 13

(
Insertion Lemma
)
.

Let
k\in\mathbb{N}
and let
M,M^{\prime},L,L^{\prime}
be markings of
\mathcal{N}
satisfying

M\xrightarrow{\sigma}M^{\prime}
for some
\sigma
and
L^{\prime}=L+\mathcal{A}\mathbf{y}
for some
\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{Z}^{T}
such that
{\llbracket\mathbf{y}\rrbracket}\subseteq{\llbracket\sigma\rrbracket}
. Then
\mu M+kL\xrightarrow{*}\mu M^{\prime}+kL^{\prime}
for

\mu=\alpha k+\alpha\|{\overrightarrow{\sigma}}\|n\mathit{w}+\|{\mathbf{y}}\|n\mathit{w}
, where
\alpha\in\mathbb{N}
is the smallest number such that
\alpha\mathbf{1}+\mathbf{y}\geq\mathbf{0}
,

\mathit{w}
is the weight of
\mathcal{N}
and
n
is the number of places in
{\rm Phys.~Rev.~E}{{\llbracket\sigma\rrbracket}}
.

Proof 4.6
.

We first provide an intuition behind the proof. In a first stage,
we asynchronously execute multiple “copies” of the firing sequence
\sigma
from
multiple “copies” of the marking
M
, until we reach a marking in which
all places of
{\rm Phys.~Rev.~E}{{\llbracket\sigma\rrbracket}}
contain a sufficiently large
number of tokens. At this point we temporarily interrupt the executions of the copies of
\sigma

to
insert

k
firing sequences each with Parikh mapping
\alpha\overrightarrow{\sigma}+\mathbf{y}
. The net effect of this sequence is to transfer
\alpha k
copies of
M
to
M^{\prime}
, leaving the other copies untouched, and exactly
k
copies of

L
to
L^{\prime}
. In the third stage, we resume the interrupted executions of the copies of
\sigma
,
which completes the transfer of the remaining copies of
M
to
M^{\prime}
. We now proceed to the formal proof.

Let
\mathbf{x}
be the
Parikh image of
\sigma
, i.e.,

\mathbf{x}=\overrightarrow{\sigma}
. Since
M\xrightarrow{\sigma}M^{\prime}
,
by the marking
equation we have
M^{\prime}=M+\mathcal{A}\mathbf{x}
.

First stage:

Let
\lambda_{x}=\|{x}\|
,
\lambda_{y}=\|{y}\|

and

\mu=\alpha k+\alpha\lambda_{x}n\mathit{w}+\lambda_{y}n\mathit{w}
.
Let
\sigma:=r_{1},r_{2},\dots,r_{j}

and let
M=:M_{0}\xrightarrow{r_{1}}M_{1}\xrightarrow{r_{2}}M_{2}\dots M_{j-1}\xrightarrow{r_{j}}M_{j}:=M^{\prime}
.
Notice that
for each place
p\in{\rm Phys.~Rev.~E}{{\llbracket\sigma\rrbracket}}
, there exists a marking

M_{i_{p}}\in\{M_{0},\dots,M_{j-1}\}
such that
M_{i_{p}}(p)>0
.

Since each of the markings in
\{M_{i_{p}}\}_{p\in{\rm Phys.~Rev.~E}{{\llbracket\sigma\rrbracket}}}

can be obtained from
M
by firing a (suitable) prefix of
\sigma
,
by the monotonicity property, it follows that starting from the marking

\mu M+~kL~=~\alpha kM+kL+(\alpha\lambda_{x}n\mathit{w}+\lambda_{y}n\mathit{w})M
,
we can reach the marking
L_{0}
given by
L_{0}

:=\alpha kM+kL+\sum_{p\in{\rm Phys.~Rev.~E}{{\llbracket\sigma\rrbracket}}}\ (\alpha\lambda_{x}\mathit{w}+\lambda_{y}\mathit{w})M_{i_{p}}
.
This completes our first stage.

Second stage - Insert:

Since
{\llbracket\mathbf{y}\rrbracket}\subseteq{\llbracket\sigma\rrbracket}
,
if
\mathbf{y}(t)\neq 0
then
\mathbf{x}(t)\neq 0
.
Since
\mathbf{x}(t)\geq 0
for every transition, by the definition of
\alpha
,
it now follows that
(\alpha\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{y})(t)\geq 0
for every transition

t
and
(\alpha\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{y})(t)>0
precisely for those
transitions in
{\llbracket\sigma\rrbracket}
.

Let
\xi
be any firing sequence such that
\overrightarrow{\xi}=\alpha\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{y}
and let
\mathtt{Inter}

:=\sum_{p\in{\rm Phys.~Rev.~E}{{\llbracket\sigma\rrbracket}}}\ (\alpha\lambda_{x}\mathit{w}+\lambda_{y}\mathit{w})M_{i_{p}}
.
Notice that for each place
p\in{\rm Phys.~Rev.~E}{{\llbracket\sigma\rrbracket}}
,

\mathtt{Inter}(p)\geq(\alpha\lambda_{x}+\lambda_{y})\mathit{w}\geq\|{(\alpha\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{y})}\|\cdot w
.
For each
1\leq i\leq k
, set
L_{i}
to be the marking
\alpha(k-i)M+\alpha iM^{\prime}+(k-i)L+iL^{\prime}+\mathtt{Inter}
.
By Proposition
11
and the marking equation, we have that for each
i\geq 0
,
L_{i}\xrightarrow{\xi}L_{i+1}
.
Hence, starting from
L_{0}
, we reach the marking
L_{k}=\alpha kM^{\prime}+kL^{\prime}+\sum_{p\in{\rm Phys.~Rev.~E}{{\llbracket\sigma\rrbracket}}}\ (\alpha\lambda_{x}\mathit{w}+\lambda_{y}\mathit{w})M_{i_{p}}
.
This completes our second stage.

Third stage:

Notice that
for each place
p\in{\rm Phys.~Rev.~E}{{\llbracket\sigma\rrbracket}}
, by construction of
M_{i_{p}}
, there is a firing sequence
which takes the marking
M_{i_{p}}
to the marking
M^{\prime}
.
By the monotonicity property, it then follows that there is a firing sequence
which takes the marking
L_{k}
to the marking

\alpha kM^{\prime}+kL^{\prime}+\sum_{p\in{\rm Phys.~Rev.~E}{{\llbracket\sigma\rrbracket}}}(\alpha\lambda_{x}\mathit{w}+\lambda_{y}\mathit{w})M^{\prime}=\mu M^{\prime}+kL^{\prime}
. This completes our third stage and also
completes the desired firing sequence from
\mu M+kL
to
\mu M^{\prime}+kL^{\prime}
.

5.
The cut-off problem for general Petri nets

Let
(\mathcal{N},M,M^{\prime})
be a net system with
\mathcal{N}=(P,T,Pre,Post)
, such that

\mathcal{A}
is its incidence matrix. As in Section
3
, we first characterize the
Petri net systems that admit a cut-off, and then provide a polynomial time algorithm to decide the existence of a cut-off for Petri nets (and hence also for rendez-vous protocols).

5.1.
Characterizing systems with cut-offs

We generalize the characterization of Theorem
5
for acyclic Petri net systems to general
ones.

Theorem 14
.

A Petri net system
(\mathcal{N},M,M^{\prime})
admits a cut-off if and only if there is a continuous firing sequence
\sigma
such that
M\mathrel{\mathop{\vtop{\halign{\hfil#\hfil\cr\rightarrowfill\cr\hphantom{$\scriptstyle\mskip 8.0mu{\sigma}\mskip 8.0mu$}\cr\vrule height=0.0pt,width=15.33325pt\cr$\scriptscriptstyle{\mathbb{Q}}$\cr}}}\limits^{\sigma}}M^{\prime}

and the marking equation has a solution
\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{Z}^{T}

such that
{\llbracket\mathbf{y}\rrbracket}\subseteq{\llbracket\sigma\rrbracket}
.

Proof 5.1
.

(
\Rightarrow
): Assume
(\mathcal{N},M,M^{\prime})
admits a cut-off. By Lemma
3.1
,
there exist
n\in\mathbb{N}
and firing sequences
\tau,\tau^{\prime}
such that

nM\xrightarrow{\tau}nM^{\prime}
,
(n+1)M\xrightarrow{\tau^{\prime}}(n+1)M^{\prime}

and
{\llbracket\tau^{\prime}\rrbracket}\subseteq{\llbracket\tau\rrbracket}
.

Let
\tau=t_{1}t_{2}\cdots t_{k}
and let
M_{0}:=nM\xrightarrow{t_{1}}M_{1}\xrightarrow{t_{2}}M_{2}\dots\xrightarrow{t_{k}}M_{k}:=nM^{\prime}
.
It is then easy to verify that that
M_{0}/n\xrightarrow{t_{1}/n}M_{1}/n\xrightarrow{t_{2}/n}M_{2}/n\dots\xrightarrow{t_{k}/n}M_{k}/n
.
This means that if we set
\sigma:=t_{1}/n,\ t_{2}/n,\ \ldots\ ,\ t_{k}/n
then
M\mathrel{\mathop{\vtop{\halign{\hfil#\hfil\cr\rightarrowfill\cr\hphantom{$\scriptstyle\mskip 8.0mu{\sigma}\mskip 8.0mu$}\cr\vrule height=0.0pt,width=15.33325pt\cr$\scriptscriptstyle{\mathbb{Q}}$\cr}}}\limits^{\sigma}}M^{\prime}
.
Further, by the marking equation we have
nM^{\prime}=nM+\mathcal{A}\overrightarrow{\tau}
and
(n+1)M^{\prime}=(n+1)M+\mathcal{A}\overrightarrow{\tau^{\prime}}
. Let
\mathbf{y}=\overrightarrow{\tau^{\prime}}-\overrightarrow{\tau}
. Then
\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{Z}^{T}
and

M^{\prime}=M+\mathcal{A}\mathbf{y}
. Since
{\llbracket\tau^{\prime}\rrbracket}\subseteq{\llbracket\tau\rrbracket}={\llbracket\sigma\rrbracket}
, we have
{\llbracket\mathbf{y}\rrbracket}\subseteq{\llbracket\sigma\rrbracket}
.

(
\Leftarrow
): Assume that there exists a continuous firing sequence
\sigma
and
a vector
\mathbf{y}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{Z}^{T}
such that
{\llbracket\mathbf{y}^{\prime}\rrbracket}\subseteq{\llbracket\sigma\rrbracket}
,

M\mathrel{\mathop{\vtop{\halign{\hfil#\hfil\cr\rightarrowfill\cr\hphantom{$\scriptstyle\mskip 8.0mu{\sigma}\mskip 8.0mu$}\cr\vrule height=0.0pt,width=15.33325pt\cr$\scriptscriptstyle{\mathbb{Q}}$\cr}}}\limits^{\sigma}}M^{\prime}
and
M^{\prime}=M+\mathcal{A}\mathbf{y}^{\prime}
. Let
s=|\mathcal{N}|(\log\|{M}\|+\log\|{M^{\prime}}\|)
.
We first claim that we can find a vector
\mathbf{y}
such that
{\llbracket\mathbf{y}\rrbracket}\subseteq{\llbracket\sigma\rrbracket}
,
M^{\prime}=M+\mathcal{A}\mathbf{y}
and

\mathbf{y}
can be described using a polynomial number of bits in
s
.
Indeed, let
t_{1},\dots,t_{k}
be the set of transitions not in
{\llbracket\sigma\rrbracket}
and
consider the system of equations given by
M^{\prime}=M+\mathcal{A}\mathbf{v},\ \mathbf{v}_{t_{1}}=0,\ \mathbf{v}_{t_{2}}=0,\ \dots,\ \mathbf{v}_{t_{k}}=0
.
We know that there is at least one integer solution to this system, namely
\mathbf{y}^{\prime}
.
It is well known that if a system of linear equations
over the integers is feasible, then there is a solution
which can be described using a number of bits which
is polynomial in the size of the input (see e.g.
[
KM78
]
).
Applying this result to our system of equations proves our claim.

Now, since
M\mathrel{\mathop{\vtop{\halign{\hfil#\hfil\cr\rightarrowfill\cr\hphantom{$\scriptstyle\mskip 8.0mu{\sigma}\mskip 8.0mu$}\cr\vrule height=0.0pt,width=15.33325pt\cr$\scriptscriptstyle{\mathbb{Q}}$\cr}}}\limits^{\sigma}}M^{\prime}
, by Lemma
12

there exists
n,m
(both of which can be described using a polynomial number of bits in
s
) and a firing sequence
\tau
such that

{\llbracket\tau\rrbracket}={\llbracket\sigma\rrbracket},\|{\overrightarrow{\tau}}\|\leq m
and
nM\xrightarrow{\tau}nM^{\prime}
.
Since
\mathbf{y}
can be described by a polynomial number of bits in
s
, by Lemma
13
,
there exists
\mu
(which can once again be described using a polynomial number of bits in
s
) such
that
\mu nM+M\xrightarrow{*}\mu nM^{\prime}+M^{\prime}
.
By Lemma
3.1
the system
(\mathcal{N},M,M^{\prime})
admits a cut-off which can be described by a polynomial number of bits in
s
.

Notice that we have actually proved that if a net system
admits a cut-off then it admits a cut-off which is expressible by a polynomial number of
bits in its size.
Since the cut-off problem for a rendez-vous protocol
\mathcal{P}

can be reduced to the cut-off problem for the Petri net system

(\mathcal{N}_{\mathcal{P}},\Lbag\mathit{init}\Rbag,\Lbag\mathit{fin}\Rbag)
,
it follows that:

Corollary 15
.

If the system
(\mathcal{N},M,M^{\prime})
admits a cut-off then
it admits a cut-off which is expressible by a polynomial number of bits
in
|\mathcal{N}|(\log\|{M}\|+\log\|{M^{\prime}}\|)
.
Hence, if a rendez-vous protocol
\mathcal{P}
admits a cut-off
then it admits a cut-off which is at most
2^{|\mathcal{P}|^{O(1)}}
.

It is already known from
[
HS20
, Section 6.3]
that there are protocols whose smallest cut-off is at least exponential in the size of the protocol.
Hence, the above corollary gives almost tight bounds on the smallest cut-off of a protocol.

{exa}

Let us consider the Petri net
\mathcal{N}_{\mathcal{P}}
from Figure
2
given in Example
2.3
. We have seen that 4 is the smallest cut-off for
the system
(\mathcal{N}_{\mathcal{P}},\mathit{init},\mathit{fin})
. We now show that the conditions of Theorem
14
are satisfied for this system. Note that
if we set
\sigma=t_{1}/8,\ t_{2}/4,\ t_{3}/2
then

\Lbag\mathit{init}\Rbag\mathrel{\mathop{\vtop{\halign{\hfil#\hfil\cr\rightarrowfill\cr\hphantom{$\scriptstyle\mskip 8.0mu{\sigma}\mskip 8.0mu$}\cr\vrule height=0.0pt,width=15.00002pt\cr$\scriptscriptstyle{\mathbb{Q}}$\cr}}}\limits^{\sigma}}\Lbag\mathit{fin}\Rbag
is a valid run of this net. Further, the marking equation for this system is:

\begin{array}[]{rclcl}0&=&1-2\mathbf{v}_{t_{1}}-\mathbf{v}_{t_{2}}-\mathbf{v}_{t_{3}}&&(\text{Equation for the place $\mathit{init}$})\\[5.69046pt]
0&=&0+2\mathbf{v}_{t_{1}}-\mathbf{v}_{t_{2}}&&(\text{Equation for the place $q_{1}$})\\[5.69046pt]
1&=&0+2\mathbf{v}_{t_{2}}+\mathbf{v}_{t_{3}}&&(\text{Equation for the place $f$})\\[5.69046pt]
\end{array}

Notice that
\mathbf{y}=(1,2,-3)
is a solution to the marking equation such that
{\llbracket\mathbf{y}\rrbracket}\subseteq{\llbracket\sigma\rrbracket}
. Hence, the conditions of Theorem
14

are satisfied.

5.2.
Polynomial time algorithm

We use the characterization given in the
previous section to provide a polynomial time algorithm for the cut-off
problem. The following lemma, which is very similar to Lemma
6
, was proved in
[
FH15
]
and enables us to find
a firing sequence between two markings with maximum support.

{lemC}

[
[
FH15
, Lemma 12 and Proposition 26]
]
Let
FS
be the set of all continuous firing sequences
\tau
such that
M\mathrel{\mathop{\vtop{\halign{\hfil#\hfil\cr\rightarrowfill\cr\hphantom{$\scriptstyle\mskip 8.0mu{\tau}\mskip 8.0mu$}\cr\vrule height=0.0pt,width=15.00002pt\cr$\scriptscriptstyle{\mathbb{Q}}$\cr}}}\limits^{\tau}}M^{\prime}
.
Then, there exists a sequence
\sigma\in FS
such that
{\llbracket\tau\rrbracket}\subseteq{\llbracket\sigma\rrbracket}
for every
\tau\in FS
.
Moreover, the support of such a sequence
\sigma
can be computed in polynomial time.

We now have all the ingredients to prove the existence of a polynomial time
algorithm.

Theorem 16
.

The cut-off problem for Petri nets can be solved in polynomial time.

Proof 5.2
.

The proof is exactly the same as the proof of Theorem
7
, except that instead of checking if the marking equation over
\mathbb{Q}_{\geq 0}

is feasible, we first check if
M
can reach
M^{\prime}
over the continuous semantics and if so, obtain the maximum support of all
the firing sequences
\tau
such that
M\mathrel{\mathop{\vtop{\halign{\hfil#\hfil\cr\rightarrowfill\cr\hphantom{$\scriptstyle\mskip 8.0mu{\tau}\mskip 8.0mu$}\cr\vrule height=0.0pt,width=15.33325pt\cr$\scriptscriptstyle{\mathbb{Q}}$\cr}}}\limits^{\tau}}M^{\prime}
and then use this maximum support to construct additional constraints for the marking equation over the integers.

5.3.
\mathsf{P}
-hardness

We now have the following lemma, which enables us to derive a
\mathsf{P}
-completeness result for the cut-off problem.

Lemma 17
.

The cut-off problem for rendez-vous protocols is
\mathsf{P}
-hard.

Proof 5.3
.

We give a logspace reduction from the
Circuit Value Problem (CVP)

which is known to be P-hard
[
Lad75
]
.

CVP is defined as follows: We are given a Boolean circuit
C
with
n

input variables
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}
and
m
gates
g_{1},\dots,g_{m}
.
We are also given an assignment
\mathit{v}
for the input variables and a gate
out
.
We have to check if the output of the gate
out
is 1,
when the input variables are assigned values according to the
assignment
\mathit{v}
.

We represent each binary gate
g
as a tuple
(\circ,s_{1},s_{2})
where

\circ\in\{\lor,\land\}
denotes the operation of
g

and
s_{1},s_{2}\in\{x_{1},\dots,x_{n},g_{1},\dots,g_{m}\}
are the inputs
to
g
. In a similar fashion each unary gate
g
is
represented as a tuple
(\lnot,s)
.

Let
\mathit{v}(x_{i})\in\{0,1\}
denote the value assigned to the variable
x_{i}
by the assignment
\mathit{v}
.
Similarly, let
\mathit{v}(g_{i})
denote the output of the
gate
g_{i}
when the input variables are assigned values according to the assignment
\mathit{v}
.
Hence, the problem is to determine if
v(out)
is 1.

The reduction

We now define a rendez-vous protocol as follows:
Our alphabet
\Sigma
will be
\{a,z\}\ \cup\ \{(g,b_{1},b_{2}):g\text{ is a binary gate and }b_{1},b_{2}\in\{0,1\}\}\ \cup\ \{(g,b):g\text{ is an unary gate and }\\
b\in\{0,1\}\}
.
We will have an initial state
\mathit{init}
,

2n
states
q_{x_{1}}^{0},q_{x_{1}}^{1},\dots,q_{x_{n}}^{0},q_{x_{n}}^{1}
and

2m
states
q_{g_{1}}^{0},q_{g_{1}}^{1},\dots,q_{g_{m}}^{0},q_{g_{m}}^{1}
.
The rules are defined as follows:

•

For each
1\leq i\leq n
, we have the rules
(\mathit{init},!a,q_{x_{i}}^{\mathit{v}(x_{i})})
and

(\mathit{init},?a,q_{x_{i}}^{\mathit{v}(x_{i})})
. These
two rules correspond to setting the value of
x_{i}
to
\mathit{v}(x_{i})
.

•

For each binary gate
g=(\circ,s_{1},s_{2})

and for each
b_{1},b_{2}\in\{0,1\}
, we have the rules

(q_{s_{1}}^{b_{1}},!(g,b_{1},b_{2}),q_{g}^{b_{1}\circ b_{2}})
and

(q_{s_{2}}^{b_{2}},?(g,b_{1},b_{2}),q_{g}^{b_{1}\circ b_{2}})
.
These rules say that if the output of
s_{1}
is
b_{1}

and the output of
s_{2}
is
b_{2}
, then the output of

g
is
b_{1}\circ b_{2}
.

•

For each unary gate
g=(\lnot,s)
and each
b\in\{0,1\}
, we have the rules

(q_{s}^{b},!(g,b),q_{g}^{\lnot b})
and
(q_{s}^{b},?(g,b),q_{g}^{\lnot b})
.
These rules guarantee that the output of
g
is the negation
of the output of
s
.

•

Finally we have the rule
(q_{out}^{1},!z,q_{out}^{1})
and also the rule
(q,?z,q_{out}^{1})
for
every state
q
. These rules guarantee that once a process
reaches the state
q_{out}^{1}
, it can make all the other
processes reach
q_{out}^{1}
as well.

We then set our final state
\mathit{fin}
to be
q_{out}^{1}
.
Notice that this construction can be performed using a logarithmic amount of space. Constructing the states and the letters of the alphabet can be accomplished by iterating over all
the input variables and the gates of the circuit which can be performed using a logarithmic amount of space on the work-tape. Constructing each rule requires a constant number of pointers to the input, which can also be
maintained by using a logarithmic amount of space. Hence, the entire reduction can be carried out by using only a logarithmic amount of space on the work-tape.

Some preliminary observations.

Before we proceed to the correctness of the construction, we set up some notation and make some preliminary observations.
For a state
q

and an initial configuration
C^{k}_{\mathit{init}}
, we say that
C^{k}_{\mathit{init}}
can
cover

q
if there
exists a run
C^{k}_{\mathit{init}}\xRightarrow{*}D
such that
D(q)>0
. We say that
q
is coverable if it can be covered
from some initial configuration
C^{k}_{\mathit{init}}
.

Remark 18
.

In our construction the final state
\mathit{fin}
is taken to be
q_{out}^{1}
. Notice that we have the rule
(\mathit{fin},!z,\mathit{fin})
in our protocol and also the rules

(q,?z,\mathit{fin})
for every state
q
. By using this collection of rules, it is easy to see that if
D
is a configuration such that
D(\mathit{fin})>0
and
|D|=k
,
then
D
can reach the configuration
C_{\mathit{fin}}^{k}
. This implies that
C^{k}_{\mathit{init}}
can cover
\mathit{fin}
if and only if
C^{k}_{\mathit{init}}
can reach

6.
The bounded-loss cut-off problem for rendez-vous protocols

In this section, we consider the following
bounded-loss cut-off problem for rendez-vous protocols
, which was suggested as a variant of the cut-off
problem in Section 7 of
[
HS20
]
.

Given:
A rendez-vous protocol
\mathcal{P}=(Q,\Sigma,\mathit{init},\mathit{fin},R)

Decide:
Is there
B\in\mathbb{N}
such that for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
, there is a configuration
D_{n}
with

C_{\mathit{init}}^{n}\xRightarrow{*}D_{n}
and
D_{n}(\mathit{fin})\geq n-B

If such a

exists, then we say that the protocol
\mathcal{P}
has the bounded-loss cut-off property and that

is a bounded-loss
cut-off for
\mathcal{P}
.

Intuitively, the cut-off problem asks if for all large enough population sizes

, we can move
n
agents from the
state
\mathit{init}
to the state
\mathit{fin}
. The bounded-loss cut-off problem asks if there is a bound
B
such that
for all population sizes
n
, we can move
n-B
agents from the state
\mathit{init}
to the state
\mathit{fin}
and leave the remaining
B

agents in any state of the protocol
\mathcal{P}
. Intuitively, we are allowed to “leave out” a bounded number of agents while moving everybody
else to the final state.

\mathcal{P}
from Figure

given in Example

\mathcal{P}

\mathit{fin}

\mathsf{P}
-complete.

We prove this by adapting the techniques developed for the cut-off problem. Similar to the cut-off problem, we first give
a characterization of protocols having the bounded-loss cut-off property and then use this characterization to give a polynomial-time

7.
The cut-off and bounded-loss cut-off problems

for symmetric rendez-vous protocols

In
[

, Horn and Sangnier introduced symmetric rendez-vous protocols, where sending and receiving a message at each state has the same effect, and showed that the cut-off problem for this class of protocols is in
\mathsf{NP}

\mathsf{NC}

We now formally define symmetric protocols.

{defi}

A rendez-vous protocol

Since

if and only if
(q,?a,q^{\prime})\in R
for a symmetric protocol
\mathcal{P}
,
in the following, we will simply denote rules of a symmetric protocol as a tuple in
Q\times\Sigma\times Q
, with the understanding that

(q,a,q^{\prime})
denotes that there are two rules
of the form
(q,!a,q^{\prime})
and
(q,?a,q^{\prime})
in the protocol.

{exa}

7.1.
Characterization of symmetric protocols admitting a cut-off.

Let us fix a symmetric protocol
\mathcal{P}=(Q,\Sigma,\mathit{init},\mathit{fin},R)
for the rest of this section.
Horn and Sangnier proved the following nice characterization of symmetric protocols that admit a cut-off.

{propC}

[
[
HS20
, Lemma 18]
]
The protocol

admits a cut-off if and only if
there exists an even number
e
and an odd number

such that
C^{e}_{\mathit{init}}
can reach
C^{e}_{\mathit{fin}}

and
C^{o}_{\mathit{init}}
can reach
C^{o}_{\mathit{fin}}
.

We will now translate this characterization into one that is more amenable to algorithmic analysis.
To begin with, we use the symmetric protocol
\mathcal{P}

to define a graph
\mathcal{G}_{\mathcal{P}}
whose
vertices are the states of
\mathcal{P}
and there is an edge between
q
and
q^{\prime}
in

if and only if there exists

. The following lemma is immediate from the definition of
\mathcal{P}
.

Lemma 26
.

There exists
k\in\mathbb{N}
such that

can reach

in

if and only if there is a path from
\mathit{init}
to
\mathit{fin}
in the graph

.

Proof 7.1
.

The left-to-right implication follows from the definition of
\mathcal{G}_{\mathcal{P}}
. For the other direction,
suppose there is a path
\mathit{init},q_{1},q_{2},\dots,q_{m-1},\mathit{fin}

in the graph
\mathcal{G}_{\mathcal{P}}
. Then notice that

\Lbag 2\cdot\mathit{init}\Rbag\Rightarrow\Lbag 2\cdot q_{1}\Rbag\Rightarrow\Lbag 2\cdot q_{2}\Rbag\Rightarrow\dots\Rightarrow\Lbag 2\cdot q_{m-1}\Rbag\Rightarrow\Lbag 2\cdot\mathit{fin}\Rbag
is
a valid run of
\mathcal{P}
.

Intuitively, the above lemma takes care of the “even” case in the characterization given in Proposition
7.1
.
To handle the “odd” case, we first need a couple of definitions.

A state
q
of
\mathcal{P}
will be called
good
if there is a path from
\mathit{init}
to
q
and a path from
q
to
\mathit{fin}
in the graph
\mathcal{G}_{\mathcal{P}}
.
A state which is not good is
bad
. Given the protocol
\mathcal{P}
, we consider the Petri net
\mathcal{N}_{\mathcal{P}}=(Q,T,Pre,Post)
with incidence matrix
\mathcal{A}=Post-Pre

that we constructed in Section

. Note that the set of places of
\mathcal{N}_{\mathcal{P}}
is the set of states of
\mathcal{P}
.
A transition
t
of
\mathcal{N}_{\mathcal{P}}
is called

if
{\rm Phys.~Rev.~E}{t}\cup t^{\overset{\bullet}{}}
contains a bad state.
We now have the following propositions about good states.

Proposition 27
.

If
q
is good, then
\Lbag 2\cdot\mathit{init}\Rbag\xrightarrow{*}\Lbag 2\cdot q\Rbag
and
\Lbag 2\cdot q\Rbag\xrightarrow{*}\Lbag 2\cdot\mathit{fin}\Rbag
in

\mathcal{N}_{\mathcal{P}}
.

Proof 7.2
.

Since

is good, there are paths
\mathit{init},p_{1},\dots,p_{n},q
and

it follows that
\Lbag 2\cdot\mathit{init}\Rbag\Rightarrow\Lbag 2\cdot p_{1}\Rbag\Rightarrow\dots\Rightarrow\Lbag 2\cdot q\Rbag

and
\Lbag 2\cdot q\Rbag\Rightarrow\Lbag 2\cdot q_{1}\Rbag\Rightarrow\dots\Rightarrow\Lbag 2\cdot\mathit{fin}\Rbag
are valid runs of
\mathcal{P}
.
By Proposition
2
, these two runs are also valid in the Petri net

.

The next proposition intuitively asserts that in any run from an initial configuration to a final configuration, only good states may occur.

Proposition 28
.

Suppose
C
is such that
C^{n}_{\mathit{init}}\xrightarrow{*}C
(resp.
C\xrightarrow{*}C^{n}_{\mathit{fin}}
) for some

\mathit{init}
to
q
(resp. from

to
\mathit{fin}

\mathcal{G}_{\mathcal{P}}
.

Proof 7.3
.

In both of these cases, we can prove the claim by induction on the length of the underlying run.

The following lemma now allows us to handle the “odd” case in the characterization of symmetric protocols that admit a cut-off.

Lemma 29
.

There exists
k\in\mathbb{N}
such that
C_{\mathit{init}}^{2k+1}\xrightarrow{*}C_{\mathit{fin}}^{2k+1}
if and only if the marking equation for
(\mathcal{N}_{\mathcal{P}},\Lbag\mathit{init}\Rbag,\Lbag\mathit{fin}\Rbag)
has a solution
\mathbf{x}
over the field
\mathbb{F}_{2}
such that
\mathbf{x}[t]=0
for every useless transition
t
.

Proof 7.4
.

We first provide an intuition behind the proof. The left-to-right implication is true because we can perform a
“modulo 2” operation on both sides of the marking equation.
For the other direction, we use an idea similar to the Insertion Lemma (Lemma
13
).
Let
\mathbf{x}
be a solution to the marking equation over
\mathbb{F}_{2}
such that
\mathbf{x}[t]=0
for every useless transition
t

Using Proposition
27
, we first
populate all the good states of

with enough agents
such that all the good states except
\mathit{init}
have an even number of agents.
Then, we fire exactly once all the transitions

such that
\mathbf{x}[t]=1

Since
\mathbf{x}
satisfies the marking equation over
\mathbb{F}_{2}

in the resulting configuration, the number of agents at each
bad state is 0 and the number of agents in each good state except

\mathit{fin}
is even. Hence, we can once again use Proposition
27

to conclude that we can move all the agents which are not at
\mathit{fin}

to the final state
\mathit{fin}
. We now proceed to the formal proof.

(\Rightarrow)
: Suppose there exists

and a firing sequence

(\Leftarrow)

We now claim that

7.2.
An
\mathsf{NC}
algorithm.

We now use Proposition
7.1
and Lemmas
26
and
29
to give an
\mathsf{NC}
algorithm for the
cut-off problem for symmetric protocols. Recall that
\mathsf{NC}
is the class of problems that can be solved by
parallel random access machines
(PRAMs) in polylogarithmic parallel time
with a polynomially many processors
[
Pap07
]
. Intuitively, a PRAM is a parallel computer with some number of random access machines
M_{1},M_{2},\dots,M_{k}
, also known as processors, and a central memory which can be accessed by any of these
processors. These processors have random access to any of the cells in the central memory and they can all read and write to these cells as dictated by their instruction set.
A single step of the PRAM consists of all of these processors executing a single step, with contradictory writes to the same cell avoided by adopting the convention that
the write of the processors with the smallest index prevails. A uniform family of polynomial-sized PRAMS is a set
\{P_{n}:n\in\mathbb{N}\}
such that each
P_{n}
only has a polynomial
number of processors in
n
and there is a logarithmic space Turing machine,
which when given input
1^{n}
can output
P_{n}
. A language
L
is said to be in
\mathsf{NC}
if there is a uniform family of polynomial-sized PRAMS
\{P_{n}:n\in\mathbb{N}\}
such that
given input
x
, the PRAM
P_{|x|}
correctly decides if
x\in L
and the number of steps
P_{|x|}
takes is polylogarithmic in the size of
|x|
. Similarly, we can
define the notion of a uniform family of polynomial-sized PRAMS computing a function, and hence a reduction between two languages.

From the definition of
\mathsf{NC}
, it follows that this class is closed under intersection of languages. Further, it also follows that if
L\in{\mathsf{NC}}
and there is a
\mathsf{NC}
-reduction
from another language
L^{\prime}
to
L
, then
L^{\prime}\in{\mathsf{NC}}
. With these two facts in mind, by Proposition
7.1
, to prove that the cut-off problem for symmetric protocols
is in
\mathsf{NC}
, it suffices to give two
\mathsf{NC}
algorithms: one to check if there is an even number
e
such that
C^{e}_{\mathit{init}}
can reach
C^{e}_{\mathit{fin}}
and another to check
if there is an odd number
o
such that
C^{o}_{\mathit{init}}
can reach
C^{o}_{\mathit{fin}}
.

Algorithm for the even case.

We now give an
\mathsf{NC}
algorithm to determine if there is an even number
e
such that
C^{e}_{\mathit{init}}
can reach
C^{e}_{\mathit{fin}}
.
To do this, we show that given the protocol
\mathcal{P}
as input, we can construct the graph
\mathcal{G}_{\mathcal{P}}
using an
\mathsf{NC}
algorithm.
By Lemma
26
, we have then reduced the problem to an instance of graph reachability, which is in
\mathsf{NC}

[
Pap07
]
. Since
\mathsf{NC}
is closed under
\mathsf{NC}
-reductions,
the required claim will then follow.

Let
\mathcal{P}=(Q,\Sigma,\mathit{init},\mathit{fin},R)
. We assume that
\mathcal{P}
is given as input in the central memory in some fixed encoding, i.e., the first
|Q|
cells contain the
encoding of the states, the next
|\Sigma|
cells contain the encoding of the alphabet and so on.
To construct
\mathcal{G}_{\mathcal{P}}
, we will have
n+n^{2}\cdot|\Sigma|
processors working in parallel, where
n=|Q|
.
The
i^{th}
processor in this collection will simply write down the encoding of the
i^{th}
state onto the memory.
Each of the other
n^{2}\cdot|\Sigma|
processors uniquely correspond to an element from
Q\times\Sigma\times Q
.
A processor responsible for the triple consisting of the
i^{th}
and the
j^{th}
states and the
k^{th}
letter
first checks if there is a rule in
R
for this triple by means of random access to the input. Depending on this check, it will then write down whether or not there is an edge in
\mathcal{G}_{\mathcal{P}}
corresponding
to the
i^{th}
and the
j^{th}
states.
Notice that we only have a polynomial number of processors and each of these processors only performs a polylogarithmic amount of work.
Hence, we have shown how to construct
\mathcal{G}_{\mathcal{P}}
using an
\mathsf{NC}
algorithm, which completes the proof of the claim.

Algorithm for the odd case.

We now give an
\mathsf{NC}
algorithm to determine if there is an odd number
o
such that
C^{o}_{\mathit{init}}
can reach
C^{o}_{\mathit{fin}}
.
First note that similar to the
\mathsf{NC}
algorithm which constructs
\mathcal{G}_{\mathcal{P}}
from
\mathcal{P}
, we can prove that there is an
\mathsf{NC}
algorithm
which constructs
\mathcal{N}_{\mathcal{P}}
from
\mathcal{P}
. So we can assume that we have access to both
\mathcal{G}_{\mathcal{P}}
and
\mathcal{N}_{\mathcal{P}}
for our
\mathsf{NC}
algorithm.
We now show that there is an
\mathsf{NC}
algorithm which allows us to identify the set of all bad states and useless transitions of
\mathcal{N}_{\mathcal{P}}
.

Let
n=|Q|
. Suppose the
\mathsf{NC}
algorithm to decide graph reachability on
n
vertices requires
f(n)
processors and takes
g(n)
(parallel) steps.
Then, to compute the set of bad states of
\mathcal{G}_{\mathcal{P}}
, we take
2nf(n)
processors. For each
i
, the
i^{th}
collection of
f(n)
processors will decide
if there is a path from
\mathit{init}
to the
i^{th}
state of
\mathcal{P}
in
\mathcal{G}_{\mathcal{P}}
and the
(n+i)^{th}
collection of
f(n)
processors will decide
if there is a path from the
i^{th}
state of
\mathcal{P}
to
\mathit{fin}
in
\mathcal{G}_{\mathcal{P}}
. Notice that each collection of
f(n)
processors can work in a parallel manner
and hence the overall (parallel) time of this algorithm will still be
g(n)
. Finally, we will have
n
processors, one for each state, which will wait
for the result from the processors responsible for graph reachability and based on this decide if the state corresponding to themselves is bad or not.

Once we have computed the set of bad states, computing the set of useless transitions is easy.
The shared memory will have one bit for each transition of
\mathcal{N}_{\mathcal{P}}
, initially set to 0.
For each transition
t
and each place
p
of
\mathcal{N}_{\mathcal{P}}
,
we will have one processor which will check if
p\in{\rm Phys.~Rev.~E}{t}\cup t^{\overset{\bullet}{}}
and also check if
p
is a bad state. If this is the case, then that process
changes the bit corresponding to
t
to 1 and otherwise, it does nothing.

Finally, we show that given
\mathcal{N}_{\mathcal{P}}
and its set of useless transitions, there is an
\mathsf{NC}
algorithm which constructs the marking
equation for
(\mathcal{N}_{\mathcal{P}},\Lbag\mathit{init}\Rbag,\Lbag\mathit{fin}\Rbag)
and additionally adds constraints specifying that the solution must be 0 on all useless transitions.
Composing this
\mathsf{NC}
algorithm, with the ones for constructing
\mathcal{N}_{\mathcal{P}}
and useless transitions, by Lemma
29
,
we have then reduced the problem to an instance of solving equations over the field
\mathbb{F}_{2}
, which is in
\mathsf{NC}

[
Mul87
]
and so the required
claim will then follow.

To construct the marking equation, we will have one processor responsible for each entry of the equation. We will have
n
processors for constructing the column corresponding
to the marking
\Lbag\mathit{init}\Rbag
and another
n
processors for the column corresponding to the marking
\Lbag\mathit{fin}\Rbag
. Further, for each transition
t
and each place
p
, we will have
one processor for outputting
\mathcal{A}[p,t]
. This processor will consult the memory and get the values of
Pre[p,t]
and
Post[p,t]
and will then compute

\mathcal{A}[p,t]
. Finally, we will have one processor for each transition
t
, which will check if
t
is a useless transition (by consulting the memory)
and then depending on this check, will write down a constraint specifying that the solution to the marking equation must have value 0 corresponding to the index
t
. This completes
the construction of the
\mathsf{NC}
algorithm and so we get,

Theorem 30
.

The cut-off problem for symmetric protocols is in
\mathsf{NC}
.

7.3.
The bounded-loss cut-off problem.

We can also consider the bounded-loss cut-off problem for symmetric protocols.
However, in the case of symmetric protocols, this problem becomes rather easy. More specifically, the bounded-loss cut-off problem is
\mathsf{NL}
-complete for symmetric protocols.

Indeed, for a symmetric protocol to have a bounded-loss cut-off, it is easy to see that there must be
a path from
\mathit{init}
to
\mathit{fin}
in the graph
\mathcal{G}_{\mathcal{P}}
. Moreover, this is also a sufficient condition, because if such a path exists,
then by Proposition
27
and the monotonicity property, for every
k\geq 1
,
\Lbag(2k-1)\cdot\mathit{init}\Rbag\xrightarrow{*}\Lbag 2k-2\cdot\mathit{fin}\Rbag+\Lbag\mathit{init}\Rbag
and

\Lbag 2k\cdot\mathit{init}\Rbag\xrightarrow{*}\Lbag 2k\cdot\mathit{fin}\Rbag
and so we can simply take the
bounded-loss cut-off to be
1
. Notice that we can compute
\mathcal{G}_{\mathcal{P}}
from
\mathcal{P}
by a reduction which uses a logarithmic amount of space.
This proves that the bounded-loss cut-off problem for symmetric protocols reduces to graph reachability which is known to be
\mathsf{NL}
-complete.

Moreover, given a graph reachability instance
(G,s,t)
, we can interpret it in a straightforward manner as the graph of a symmetric protocol
\mathcal{P}
whose communication alphabet is a single letter and where the initial state is
s
and the final state is
t
.
This then immediately implies that
\mathcal{P}
would have a bounded-loss cut-off if and only if
s
can reach
t
in the graph
G
,
which leads to
\mathsf{NL}
-hardness of the bounded-loss cut-off problem for symmetric protocols.

8.
The cut-off problem for symmetric rendez-vous protocols with a leader

In
[
HS20
]
, Horn and Sangnier proposed an extension to symmetric rendez-vous protocols by adding a special
agent called a leader. They defined the cut-off problem for that extension and showed that it is in
\mathsf{PSPACE}
.
We improve on their result and and prove that the problem is
\mathsf{NP}
-complete. We proceed by introducing this new model and
its cut-off problem.

{defi}

A symmetric rendez-vous protocol with a leader, or simply a
symmetric leader protocol
, is a pair of symmetric protocols

\mathcal{P}=(\mathcal{P}^{L},\mathcal{P}^{F})
where

\mathcal{P}^{L}=(Q^{L},\Sigma,\mathit{init}^{L},\mathit{fin}^{L},R^{L})
is the
leader protocol

and
\mathcal{P}^{F}=(Q^{F},\Sigma,\mathit{init}^{F},\mathit{fin}^{F},R^{F})
is the
follower protocol

such that
Q^{L}\cap Q^{F}=\emptyset
.

Characterization of symmetric leader protocols that admit a cut-off.

We use the Compatibility lemma to prove another characterization of symmetric leader protocols that
admit a cut-off, which will help us construct our final

Lemma 37
.

.

C_{\mathit{fin}}^{k}\geq\textbf{0}

\mathit{init}^{L}

in

Lemma 38
.

Given a symmetric leader protocol, deciding whether it
admits a cut-off can be done in
\mathsf{NP}
.

Proof 8.5
.

By Proposition
8
, it suffices
to decide if there exist an even number

Suppose we want to check that there exists

By Lemma

Therefore, at the end of this run,
we would have reached the configuration

\mathtt{Second}:=C^{2n+1}_{\mathit{fin}}+\sum_{1\leq i\leq m}\ \Lbag 2\cdot q_{i}\Rbag
.
Using Proposition
34
and the monotonicity property, from
\mathtt{Second}
we can reach
C^{2k+1}_{\mathit{fin}}
,
thereby completing the construction of the required run.

(
\Rightarrow
): Suppose there exists
k
such that there
is a run
\rho
from
C^{2k+1}_{\mathit{init}}
to
C^{2k+1}_{\mathit{fin}}
.
First, observe that for every
1\leq j\leq n
, by construction
of the protocol, the leader
has to visit exactly one of the states
\top_{j}
or
\bot_{j}
along this run.
To be more precise, there is exactly one state
s_{j}\in\{\top_{j},\bot_{j}\}

for which there exists a configuration
C_{j}
along this run such that
C_{j}(s_{j})>0
.
This suggests a natural assignment
T
for the variables of
\varphi
: Set

T(x_{j})
to true if
s_{j}=\top_{j}
and otherwise, set
T(x_{j})
to false.
The rest of the proof is devoted to proving that
T
is a
satisfying assignment of
\varphi
. To prove this,
we will actually show that for every
1\leq j\leq m
, there
is an occurrence of a leader rule corresponding to the message
c_{j}

at some point along the run
\rho
. By construction of our assignment
T

and the leader protocol, this would immediately imply that
T
is a satisfying assignment
for
\varphi
.

Let
\rho:=C^{2k+1}_{\mathit{init}}\xRightarrow{r_{1},r_{1}^{\prime}}C_{1}\xRightarrow{r_{2},r_{2}^{\prime}}C_{2}\dots\xRightarrow{r_{w},r_{w}^{\prime}}C^{2k+1}_{\mathit{fin}}
.
For every
0\leq j\leq n-1
, let
R^{j}_{\alpha}=\{(p_{j},\alpha,\top_{j}),(p_{j},\alpha,\bot_{j}),(\top_{j},\alpha,p_{j+1}),(\bot_{j},\alpha,p_{j+1})\}
. We claim that for every
j
,
there are exactly two occurrences of rules from
R^{j}_{\alpha}
in
\rho
. First, only at most two rules of
R^{j}_{\alpha}
can appear in
\rho
, since otherwise it would mean that
the leader visited both
\top_{j}
and
\bot_{j}
, which we have already established as a contradiction. Further, for the leader to move out
of the state
p_{j}
and go to
p_{n}
, by construction of the protocol, at least two of these rules must be fired.
It then follows that if we set
R_{\alpha}:=\bigcup\limits_{0\leq j\leq n-1}R^{j}_{\alpha}
, then there are exactly
2n
occurrences of rules from
R_{\alpha}
in
\rho
.

Let us now observe the parity of the number of occurrences of the rule
r:=(q_{0},\alpha,q_{m})
along
\rho
. Notice that for every
i
, the rule
r
can appear exactly once
in the pair
(r_{i},r_{i}^{\prime})
if and only if the other rule in the pair belongs to
R_{\alpha}
. Since the number of occurrences of rules from
R_{\alpha}
is exactly
2n
,
it follows that the number of occurrences of the rule
r
in
\rho
is even. Since the initial configuration has an odd number of follower agents
in
q_{0}
, it follows that the number of occurrences of the rule
r_{c_{1}}:=(q_{0},c_{1},q_{1})
is odd and hence non-zero. By definition of the run
\rho
and the
follower protocol, for every
i
, the number of times the rule
r_{c_{i}}:=(q_{i-1},c_{i},q_{i})
occurs in
\rho
must be equal to the number of
times the rule
r_{c_{i+1}}:=(q_{i},c_{i+1},q_{i+1})
occurs in
\rho
. It then follows that the all the rules in the set
\{r_{c_{i}}:1\leq i\leq m\}
occur an odd number of
times in
\rho
.

Let us fix some
1\leq j\leq m
and let us consider the rule
r_{c_{j}}
. Since it appears an odd number of times in
\rho
,
there must be an index
i
such that exactly one rule in the pair
(r_{i},r_{i}^{\prime})
is
r_{c_{j}}
. The only possibility for the other rule in that pair
is a leader rule of the form
(p,c_{j},p)
for some state
p
. Hence, we have shown that there is at least one occurrence of a leader rule
corresponding to the message
c_{j}
along the run
\rho
. Since
j
was an arbitrary number between 1 and
m
, the proof is complete.

9.
Conclusion

We have shown that the cut-off problem for Petri nets and rendez-vous protocols is
\mathsf{P}
-complete. For the special case of symmetric rendez-vous protocols we have proved
that the cut-off problem is in
\mathsf{NC}
for the leaderless case and is
\mathsf{NP}
-complete in the presence of a leader. Further, we have also studied the bounded-loss cut-off problem
and shown that it is
\mathsf{P}
-complete and
\mathsf{NL}
-complete for leaderless rendez-vous and leaderless symmetric protocols respectively. Many of these results follow from two lemmas,
the Scaling and Insertion lemmas, which we believe might be of independent interest. As future work, it might be worth studying other variants of the cut-off problem dealing
with different types of properties such as liveness specifications.
