Accelerated Shapley Value Approximation for Data Evaluation
Lauren Watson, Zeno Kujawa, Rayna Andreeva, Hao-Tsung Yang, Tariq Elahi, Rik Sarkar

Abstract
Data valuation has found various applications in machine learning, such as data filtering, efficient learning and incentives for data sharing. The most popular current approach to data valuation is the Shapley value. While popular for its various applications, Shapley value is computationally expensive even to approximate, as it requires repeated iterations of training models on different subsets of data. In this paper we show that the Shapley value of data points can be approximated more efficiently by leveraging the structural properties of machine learning problems. We derive convergence guarantees on the accuracy of the approximate Shapley value for different learning settings including Stochastic Gradient Descent with convex and non-convex loss functions. Our analysis suggests that in fact models trained on small subsets are more important in the context of data valuation. Based on this idea, we describe δ \delta -Shapley – a strategy of only using small subsets for the approximation. Experiments show that this approach preserves approximate value and rank of data, while achieving speedup of up to 9.9 ​ x 9.9x . In pre-trained networks the approach is found to bring more efficiency in terms of accurate evaluation using small subsets.



Introduction

Data valuation is a fundamental research problem in machine learning. With widespread use and monetization of personal data in AI and machine learning models, the value of data has become a concern in data related governance and economics, since policies, incentives and compensations depend on fair valuation
(
Posner and Weyl 2018
)
. In the model training process itself, valuations can be used to select useful data items
(
Pooladzandi, Davini, and
Mirzasoleiman 2022
)
, efficient active learning
(
Ghorbani, Zou, and Esteva 2022
)
, federated learning
(
Wang et al. 2020
)
or explainability
(
Koh and Liang 2017
)
.

The current popular approaches in data valuation
(
Ghorbani and Zou 2019
;
Jia et al. 2019
)
are based on the Shapley value. Shapley value
(
Shapley 1952
)
is a concept from cooperative game theory for fair measure of the contributions of players in a game. In data valuation, the Shapley value of each data point measures its contribution to the utility of a specific model. Utility can be measured based on the training or test set loss. The contribution of a data point to that utility is harder to quantify as it depends on the model as well as the rest of the data. Shapley value is a solution to this problem as it incorporates these complexities and is known to produce equitable valuations
(
Ghorbani and Zou 2019
)
.

The usefulness and wide applicability of Shapley values comes at the cost of computational time and resources. Computation of the Shapley value of a data point involves computing the weighted average of the point’s marginal contribution to all possible data subsets (called
coalitions
in game theory). The direct computation is naturally exponential time and the problem is computationally hard
(
Deng and Papadimitriou 1994
;
Nagamochi et al. 1997
)
. The approximation of Shapley value involves Monte Carlo sampling from all possible subsets and evaluating them by building models. While this is more efficient than exact computation, it still requires evaluating
\Omega(n)
coalitions
(
Maleki et al. 2014
)
, and each evaluation involves training and testing a model on a large dataset. In this paper, we show that the specific structure of machine learning problems implies that a more efficient approximation is possible for data valuation.

Our Contributions.
In this paper we present more efficient methods for approximating the Shapley values of data points for models trained in both convex and non-convex scenarios. These methods produce values close to the Monte Carlo sampling algorithm, but run several times faster. The key to the algorithmic speedup is to rely on smaller coalitions where model training is efficient, while foregoing larger coalitions and expensive model training. We also define a valuation called the
\delta-
Shapley that exclusively uses small coalitions and in practice performs as well as the Shapley value.
The main insight in our analysis is that the marginal contribution of a single data point is limited when the coalition is large. This property can be derived based on the stability properties of machine learning algorithms. The precise properties and results vary across learning scenarios.

Strongly convex loss functions are known to satisfy uniform stability
(
Bousquet and Elisseeff 2002
)
, where the difference in loss due to the presence or absence of a single data point can be bounded by
O(\frac{1}{n})
for datasets of size
n
. We show that this property implies that the effect of large coalitions on the Shapley value of a data point is limited, and can be approximated efficiently. In fact, to estimate the effect of all coalitions of size
k
, about
O(\frac{1}{k^{2}})
sample coalitions suffice to obtain an error bound of
a
with probability at most
b
.

When the model is trained via a randomized mechanism such as Stochastic Gradient Descent on non-convex loss, the strict stability bound no longer holds. In this case, a suitable property defined in
Hardt, Recht, and Singer 2016
which we call
Expected Uniform Stability
applies. We provide and discuss two different interpretations of Shapley value for randomized algorithms and derive
(a,b)
bounds for convex and non-convex losses.

Inspired by the approximation idea above, we define a new value function called the
\delta-
Shapley. This value is characterized by two observations: first, that larger coalitions have little impact on valuation, as seen in our analysis, and second, that tiny coalitions are likely to produce poorly trained models whose contributions are largely noise. Thus,
\delta-
Shapley uses coalitions from a small band of sizes in the small to medium range. Similar to Shapley and other values in the class,
\delta-
Shapley is a semi-value
(
Dubey, Neyman, and Weber 1981
)
.

Experiments show that
\delta-
Shapley values produce meaningful data valuations by accurately evaluating contributions of data points to test accuracy across multiple classification tasks.
\delta-
Shapley values are also highly correlated with Monte Carlo estimates of Shapley values (
\rho\in[0.84,0.98]
) for convex and non-convex classification tasks. We find that sampling mid-sized or small coalitions produces high correlations and that both methods significantly reduce computation time in comparison to the Monte Carlo approach, with a greater reduction in time when sampling from lower coalition sizes. Fine tuning pre-trained neural networks is a common approach to model development. We find that on pre-trained networks,
\delta-
Shapley is particularly effective, and can obtain accurate estimates even with small coalitions.

Technical Background

In this section, we review the formal definition of Shapley value, its use in data valuation, relevant concepts in machine learning and Stochastic Gradient Descent (SGD).

The Shapley value

Shapley value is defined in terms of a utility function
v
, where
v(S)
represents the utility generated by a set
S
of players. The marginal utility of a player
i
with respect to
S
is written as
v(S\cup i)-v(S)
. We will sometimes abbreviate it as
v_{i}(S)
. Also note that we use
S\cup i
to mean
S\cup\{i\}
for better readability.

Definition 1
.

The Shapley value
(
Shapley 1952
)
of player
i
in a set
D
of
n
players, written as
\varphi_{i}
, is given by

\varphi_{i}(v)=\frac{1}{n}\displaystyle\sum_{S\subseteq D\setminus\{i\}}{n-1\choose\left|S\right|}^{-1}(v(S\cup i)-v(S)).

(1)

The Shapley value is the unique single valued solution satisfying the following four natural axioms
(
Shapley 1952
)
:

•

Dummy (Null) Player
: if
v(S\cup i)=v(S)+c
for all
S\subseteq D\setminus\{i\}
and some
c\in\mathbb{R}
, then
\varphi_{i}=c
.

•

Symmetry
: if
v(S\cup i)=v(S\cup j)
for all
S\subseteq D\setminus\{i,j\}
, then
\varphi_{i}=\varphi_{j}
.

•

Linearity
: for two value functions
v_{1}
and
v_{2}
,
\varphi_{i}(a_{1}v_{1}+a_{2}v_{2})=a_{1}\varphi_{i}(v_{1})+a_{2}\varphi_{i}(v_{2})
for any
a_{1},a_{2}\in\mathbb{R}
.

•

Efficiency
: for every value function
v
,
\sum_{i=1}^{n}\varphi_{i}=v(D)
.

Data valuation with Shapley values.

In data valuation, each data point naturally takes the role of a player, and a subset of data points is equivalent to a coalition. The utility can be measured either in terms of loss or accuracy, with validation loss being the more popular utility
(
Ghorbani and Zou 2019
;
Jia et al. 2019
)
.
We assume there exists a test set
D_{e}
of data unseen during training, and the overall test loss for the output
w=A(S)
of algorithm
A
is:

\mathcal{L}(w)=\frac{1}{\left|D_{e}\right|}\sum_{z\in D_{e}}\ell(w,z)

where
\ell(w,z)
is the loss of the model
w
on the data point
z
. The utility
v
is then defined by
v(S)=-\mathcal{L}(w)

(
Ghorbani and Zou 2019
)
.
Thus with
w^{\prime}=A(S\cup{i})
and
w=A(S)
, the marginal contribution of
i
to
S
is:
v_{i}(S)=\mathcal{L}(w)-\mathcal{L}(w^{\prime})
.

Dependence on model and algorithms

Observe that the utility, marginal utility and therefore Shapley values are functions of the specific models found by the algorithm. While many different models can be computed on the same dataset, the data valuation question we consider here is not one of the general useability of a data point, but of how much a data point
i
has contributed to the specific model
M
.

Shapley Approximation via Monte Carlo Sampling

In each iteration of the Monte Carlo procedure
(
Castro, Gómez, and Tejada 2009
)
, a random permutation
\pi(D)
of the dataset
D
is sampled. The set
\text{Pre}^{i}(\pi(D))
of all predecessors of
i
forms the set
S
, and
v_{i}
is then computed as described above. An average of the sampled
v_{i}(S)
is taken to obtain an estimate of the Shapley Value of
i
. The process is repeated a suitable number of times
(
Ghorbani and Zou 2019
)
. When contributions of all data points need to be computed, a marginal contribution for all data points can be computed before the next permutation is sampled.

Machine learning

In machine learning, a training dataset
D
(drawn from a distribution
Z
) is used to find a model
w
within the model class
\mathcal{H}
. In common setups,
w
is usually a vector of real numbers representing the model parameters. The training process aims to find a model
w
that minimizes the training loss given by
L(w,D)=\frac{1}{\left|D\right|}\sum_{z\in D}\ell(w,z)
.

Stochastic Gradient Descent

To find a model with nearly minimum loss, or sufficiently low loss, a popular algorithm is Stochastic Gradient Descent (SGD), sometimes called the Stochastic Gradient Method (SGM). SGD operates by making incremental steps that locally reduce the loss. In each step
t
, SGD uses a random
z\in D
, computes the gradient of
\ell(w_{t},z)
and makes an incremental move in the direction of the gradient:

w_{t+1}=w_{t}-\alpha_{t}\nabla_{w}\ell(w_{t};z)

where
\alpha_{t}
is the learning rate at step
t
. In different variations of SGD, the choice of
z
may be made independently with replacement in each step, or the choice may be determined by cycling through different permutations of
D
. Each such cycle is called an epoch of SGD.

Convex and non-convex loss functions

Definition 2

(Convexity and Strong convexity)
.

A function
f:\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is convex if for all
u,v\in\Omega

f(u)\geq f(v)+\langle\nabla f(v),u-v\rangle
and
\lambda
-strongly convex for
\lambda>0
if for all
u,v\in\Omega

f(u)\geq f(v)+\langle\nabla f(v),u-v\rangle+\frac{\lambda}{2}\lVert u-v\rVert^{2}
.

Any local minimum of a convex function is also a global minimum. Additionally, a strongly convex functions has a unique minimum. Optimization in convex and strongly convex cases are considered easier as methods like SGD can converge to the global minimum. Non-convex losses are harder to optimize, as SGD may converge to a local minimum and there is no guarantee of approaching the global minimum. SGD with non-convex losses is an important current problem as many popular model classes including neural networks have non-convex losses and are trained using SGD or its variants. Loss functions are frequently assumed to have certain continuity properties for theoretical analysis:

Definition 3

(Lipschitzness)
.

A function
f:\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is L-Lipschitz if for all
u,v\in\Omega

||f(u)-f(v)||\leq L||u-v||
.

Definition 4

(Smoothness)
.

A function
f:\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is
\beta-
smooth if for all
u,v\in\Omega

||\nabla f(u)-\nabla f(v)||\leq\beta||u-v||
.

Next, we describe out general approach and analysis for various scenarios.

Approximation algorithm and Strongly Convex Optimization

To approximate the Shapley value of
i
, we divide all coalitions (subsets of
D
) into
n

layers
, where
S_{k}
is layer
k
consisting of all subsets of size
k
:
S_{k}=\{S\subseteq D:\left|S\right|=k\}
.

Definition 5

(Contribution at layer
k
)
.

The average marginal contribution of layer

is:

\phi_{i}^{k}={n-1\choose k}^{-1}\sum_{S\in S_{k}}v_{i}(S).

The Shapley value
\varphi_{i}
as in Definition

is then simply given by the average:
\varphi_{i}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\phi^{k}_{i}
. Thus, the main computational task of Shapley value can be viewed as finding the average marginal contributions
\phi^{k}_{i}
.

This computation is expensive when
{n-1\choose k}
is large. We can instead estimate
\phi^{k}_{i}
via sampling. Our strategy is to estimate each
\phi^{k}_{i}
independently. Algorithm
1
shows the idea.

Algorithm 1

\hat{\phi^{k}_{i}}
: Expected marginal contribution in layer
k
.

1:

Input: Data point

, Dataset
D
, coalition size
k
, utility function

2:

Output:
\hat{\phi^{k}_{i}}
: estimate of marginal contribution of
i
to coalitions of size
k

3:

Initialize
\phi^{k}_{i}=0

4:

Compute
m_{k}

\triangleright
The number of samples needed at layer
k

5:

repeat

m_{k}

times

6:

Draw
S\in{S}_{k}
at random

7:

\hat{\phi^{k}_{i}}\leftarrow\hat{\phi^{k}_{i}}+\frac{1}{m_{k}}(v(S\cup i)-v(S))

\triangleright
Update estimate
\hat{\phi^{k}_{i}}

8:

end

9:

return
\hat{\phi^{k}_{i}}

To obtain the Shapley value, the most natural method will be to compute all
\phi^{k}_{i}
’s and average them as above. Other methods for more efficient approximation are covered later.

The missing element in Algorithm
1
is the computation of sample size
m_{k}
. We discuss below the analysis of
m_{k}
for strongly convex loss functions.

Sample size for strongly convex optimization

In this section, we demonstrate the core idea of our approach in the case of strongly convex optimization. Strong convexity of the loss function holds for a large class of problems such as regularized regression, logistic regression and support vector machines. For our analysis, a strongly convex loss implies an important property of the learning algorithm called uniform stability, which is defined as a small change in the underlying dataset implying only a small change in the algorithm’s output.

Definition 6

(Neighboring Datasets)
.

Two datasets
S,S^{\prime}
are neighboring if
H(S,S^{\prime})\leq 1
, where
H(\cdot,\cdot)
represents the hamming distance.

Definition 7

(Uniform stability
(
Bousquet and Elisseeff 2002
)
)
.

A learning algorithm
A
has
\gamma
-uniform stability with respect to the loss function
\ell
if the following holds,
\forall z\in Z,\forall S\in Z^{n},\forall i\in\{1,...,n\}
,

\lVert\ell(A(S),z)-\ell(A(S^{\backslash i}),z)\rVert_{\infty}\leq\gamma.

Here
S^{\backslash i}=S\setminus\{i\}
, so
S,S^{\backslash i}
are neighboring datasets.

It can be shown that strong convexity implies a uniform stability of
\gamma=\frac{c}{n}
where
n=\left|S\right|
and
c
is constant independent of
S
. Thus, on a coalition of size
k
, strong convexity will imply a uniform stability of
\frac{c}{k}
. Note that a smaller value of uniform stability implies a more stable algorithm.

Theorem 1
.

(
Bousquet and Elisseeff 2002
)

Suppose we have learning function
A:Z^{n}\rightarrow\mathcal{H}
that finds
w\in\mathcal{H}
minimizing
L(w,D)=\frac{1}{\left|D\right|}\sum_{z\in D}\ell(w,z)
for
D\in Z^{n}
. Let
\mathcal{H}
be a reproducing kernel Hilbert space with kernel
\zeta
such that
\forall z\in D
we have
\zeta(z,z)\leq C
. Then if
\ell
is
L
-Lipschitz and
\lambda
strongly-convex,
A
satisfies uniform stability
\gamma\leq\frac{L^{2}C^{2}}{2\lambda n}
with respect to
\ell
.

This stability bound with inverse proportionality to data size can be used estimate
\phi^{k}_{i}
with small sample size by observing that
\gamma
-uniform stability directly bounds
|v_{i}(S)|
when the utility is given by the test loss
v(S)=-\mathcal{L}(A(S))
.

Theorem 2
.

Suppose we have utility function
v(S)=-\mathcal{L}(A(S))=-\frac{1}{\left|D_{e}\right|}\sum_{z\in D_{e}}\ell(w,z)
where
\ell
is
\lambda
strongly-convex and
L
-Lipschitz and
C
is defined as in Theorem
1
. Then,

m_{k}\geq\frac{L^{4}C^{4}}{8\lambda^{2}a^{2}k^{2}}\ln{\frac{2n}{b}}

(2)

in Algorithm
1
suffices for
(a,b/n)
-approximation of
\phi^{k}_{i}
and
(a,b)
-approximation of
\varphi_{i}
.

The key point of the theorem is that the impact of adding or removing any one point is small due to stability, and as a result, for a layer of large coalitions, a small sample suffices for high confidence estimates. The final result of approximating the Shapley value in Theorem
2
follows from a union bound applied to computing the average over all layers.

The specific result of
m_{k}\approx 1/k^{2}
implies that in the approximation process, a focus on layers with small coalitions will ensure a good approximation. In the following section, we show how this fundamental idea based on stability extends to more general scenarios including the use of Stochastic Gradient Descent.

Convex and Non-convex optimization with Stochastic Gradient Descent

As seen in Definition
1
, Shapley value depends on a well defined utility function
v
that determines the utility of any subset including the full set
D
of players. In data valuation, the utility function is the inverse loss of the model computed via an optimization algorithm. When the optimization is randomized – such as using SGD – the definition of Shapley value needs some reconsideration.

Suppose a model
M=\mathcal{A}(D)
computed via the randomized algorithm
\mathcal{A}
has been released and is widely used. What is the Shapley value of a data point
i
, in the sense of its contribution to the specific model
M
? The randomization in SGD implies inconsistency of Shapley values and marginal utilities. For example, on different runs of
SGD
, the output model
M
will be different, marginal utilities will be different, and consequently, Shapley values will be different. In these circumstances, there are two possible interpretations of the contribution of a data point.

Fixed subsequence interpretation

On any
S\subseteq D
the algorithm
\mathcal{A}
makes a sequence of random selections
\pi(S)\in\Pi(S)
, where
\Pi(S)
is the set of all possible random sequences of elements from
S
. For a fixed
\pi
, the consequent algorithm
\mathcal{A}_{\pi(S)}
operating on
S
is deterministic, in the sense that
\mathcal{A}_{\pi(S)}(S)
always produces the same output, and thus
v(S)=-\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{A}_{\pi(S)})
.
In the case of SGD, this interpretation corresponds to always selecting the same data points of coalition
S
in the same sequence. A special case of this fixed sequence interpretation is the case where
\pi(S)
is a subsequence of the global sequence
\pi(D)
that produces the model:
M=\mathcal{A}_{\pi(D)}(D)
. For coalition
S
,
\pi(S)
is the subsequence of
\pi(D)
consisting only of elements of
S
.

Expected utility interpretation

The other natural interpretation is in terms of expected utilities. That is,
v(S)=-\mathbb{E}_{\pi(S)\in\Pi(S)}[\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{A}_{\pi(S)})]
. In this case, the Shapley value
\varphi_{i}
not longer measures the contributions to the specific model
M
, but rather the expected contribution of
i
to the models in the range of
\mathcal{A}
based on the distribution implied by
\mathcal{A}
. We will see that approximating the expected value is also expensive, requiring many trials of SGD.

Sample complexity for Convex SGD

One of the challenges of estimating
\phi^{k}_{i}
for randomized algorithms is that the absolute Uniform Stability bound from Theorem
1
no longer applies. In this case, the relevant definition is Uniform Stability in Expectation, where the expectation is taken over the random sequences in the algorithm
\mathcal{A}
, that is
\Pi(S)
:

Definition 8

(Definition 2.1 in
(
Hardt, Recht, and Singer 2016
)
)
.

A randomized algorithm
\mathcal{A}
is uniformly stable in expectation if for all data sets
S,S^{\prime}\in Z^{n}
such that S and S’ are neighboring datasets, there exists an
\epsilon
such that

\sup_{z}\mathbb{E}_{\Pi(S)}\left[\ell(\mathcal{A}(S);z)-\ell(\mathcal{A}(S^{\prime});z)\right]\leq\epsilon,

(3)

where
\ell(\cdot,z)
is the loss function and
Z
is some space.

SGD can be shown to satisfy such a stability:

Theorem 3

(Theorem 3.7 in
(
Hardt, Recht, and Singer 2016
)
)
.

Assume that the loss function
\ell(\cdot;z)
is
\beta-
smooth, convex and
L-
Lipschitz for every
z

\alpha_{t}\leq 2/\beta

T

\epsilon\leq\frac{2L^{2}}{k}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\alpha_{t},

where
w=\mathcal{A}(S),w^{\prime}=\mathcal{A}(S^{\prime})
and

is the coalition size.

The results in this section assume a loss bounded within limits
\ell(w,z)\in[0,G]

(
Hardt, Recht, and Singer 2016
;
Kwon and Zou 2022
;
Wang and Jia 2023
)

Sample complexity for fixed subsequences

We consider the setup where
\pi
is a function that returns for every
S

\pi(S)
, and
\pi(S)
is a uniformly random choice from
\Pi(S)
. The stability in expectation concept can be applied to yield the following theorem:

Theorem 4
.

When using Algorithm

Since the absolute limit on stability does not hold (which is required for the Chernoff-Hoeffding bound), the proof of the theorem uses the Bernstein bound. The theorem assumes that the query item

Sample complexity for expected utility

Restricted Layer Selection and
\delta-
Shapley

In the previous section we saw that in multiple different kinds of learning scenarios, the expected stability of models trained on a dataset of size
k
is at most
O(1/k)
. As a result, the expected marginal utility of any data point is also bounded by
O(1/k)
in expectation. This observation suggests that on average it is the smaller coalition sizes that may be the main contributors to the Shapley value of data points.

On the other hand, when coalitions are very small, the stability is poorer and the noise from randomization of SGD is likely to have a greater effect. Further, it is generally known that in machine learning, small training datasets often produce noisy models, in the sense that the model may overfit to the training data, but generalise poorly to test data that is used to evaluate utility. This known fact suggests that marginal gains computed on small coalitions will be noisy, see the Appendix for experiments and further discussion.

These observations suggest that models in a certain
medium
size range are most likely to be effective – where the coalitions are large enough to generalise well, but not so large that the marginal utility disappears. Thus, we can define a define a different value to approximate
\varphi_{i}
that only evaluates a selection of layers:

Definition 9

(
\delta
-Shapley value)
.

The
\delta
-Shapley value of datapoint
i
in a training set
D
of size
n
with utility function
v
is given by

\varphi_{i}=\sum_{k=1}^{n}p_{k}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}S\subseteq D\setminus\{i\}\\
|S|=k-1\end{subarray}}v_{i}(S)

(11)

with

p_{k}=\begin{cases}0,&\text{if $k<B_{L}$ or $k>B_{U}$}.\\
\frac{1}{B_{U}-B_{L}+1},&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

(12)

where
B_{u}
represents the upper bound on layersize
k
being evaluated and
B_{L}
is the corresponding lower bound.

(

.

(

A value function

\varphi_{i}=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{w(k)}{n}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}S\subseteq D\setminus\{i\}\\
|S|=k-1\end{subarray}}v_{i}(S)

(13)

The Shapley value is the semi-value given by
w(k)={n-1\choose k-1}^{-1}

Experiments

The experiments outlined in this section empirically verify that Shapley values can be approximated via
\delta
-Shapley using relatively small coalitions. Specifically:

1.

Meaningful data valuations: they find relevant valuations of the dataset in the sense that they reliably identify points causing the greatest drop in test accuracy when removed from the training set.

2.

Accurate approximations of Monte Carlo Shapley values: they have high Spearman Rank correlation with the corresponding Monte Carlo Shapley values.

3.

Efficient: they are obtained using significantly less computation time (wall-clock time) in comparison to the Monte Carlo approach.

4.

On pretrained neural networks, even smaller coalitions suffice and computation is more efficient.

Related Work

The Shapley value was originally proposed in game theory to estimate the contribution of players in cooperative games
(
Shapley 1952
)
. In this general setting, Monte Carlo estimation is a popular Shapley value approximation method
(
Castro, Gómez, and Tejada 2009
;
Maleki et al. 2014
)
and several variance reduction techniques have been proposed that work by grouping together (or otherwise linking) similar permutations, coalitions or players
(
Burgess and Chapman 2021
;
Castro, Gómez et al. 2017
;
Illés and Kerényi 2019
)
. Recently, the Shapley value has been applied to attribution problems in Machine Learning including explainability
(
Lundberg and Lee 2017
)
, feature selection
(
Cohen, Dror, and Ruppin 2007
)
, federated learning
(
Wang et al. 2020
)
, ensemble pruning
(
Rozemberczki and Sarkar 2021
)
and multi-agent reinforcement learning
(
Li et al. 2021
)
.

Ghorbani and Zou 2019
introduced Shapley values for data valuation, which has seen many applications discussed earlier. Alternative data valuation methods include leave-one-out testing
(
Cook 1977
)
and influence function estimation
(
Sharchilev, Ustinovskiy et al. 2018
)
. Recently other valuation functions have been proposed such as the Beta-Shapley
(
Kwon and Zou 2022
)
and Banzhaf value
(
Wang and Jia 2023
)
which are semi-values with different weighting functions for the layers. These valuations are designed to improve ranking for use in machine learning and data selection. In comparison, our aim was to approximate the Shapley value itself which is important in applications such as valuation for fair compensation.

Conclusion

The important insight from this work is that small coalitions are good estimators of the data value due to the diminishing returns property of data value with set size. Similar properties and results may be relevant in other applications of Shapley value. An area that we believe requires further investigation is the randomness properties of SGD and consequent variations in value of data points. This question is also likely to have impact in other areas such as privacy.

A=\sum_{i=1}^{m}X_{i}-m\mathbb{E}\left[X\right]
, it holds that

Pr\left(\left|A\right|\geq ma\right)=Pr\left(A\leq-ma\right)+Pr\left(A\geq ma\right)

(35)

and

Pr\left(A\leq-ma\right)=Pr\left(-\left(\sum_{i=1}^{m}X_{i}-m\mathbb{E}\left[X\right]\right)\geq ma\right)

(36)

When we label each
Y_{i}:=-X_{i}
, then the conditions of Theorem
8
are fulfilled for
Y_{1},\dots,Y_{m}
and we see that:

\displaystyle Pr\left(m\mathbb{E}\left[X\right]-\sum_{i=1}^{m}X_{i}\geq ma\right)

(37a)

\displaystyle=Pr\left(\sum_{i=1}^{m}Y_{i}-m\mathbb{E}\left[Y\right]\geq ma\right)

(37b)

\displaystyle\leq\exp{\left(-\frac{ma^{2}}{2(\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\mathbb{E}\left[X_{i}^{2}\right]+Ma/3)}\right)}

(37c)

It therefore follows that

\displaystyle Pr\left(\left|\sum_{i=1}^{m}X_{i}-m\mathbb{E}\left[X\right]\right|\geq ma\right)\leq 2\exp{\left(-\frac{ma^{2}}{2(\hat{\sigma}^{2}+Ma/3)}\right)}

∎

We are now ready to prove the core result of the section on convex optimization, the sample size derivation for
m_{k}
. We remind ourselves by restating Theorem
4
.

Theorem 4
.

When using Algorithm
1
to estimate
\phi^{k}_{i}
for an SGD algorithm
\mathcal{A}_{\pi}
with a convex,
\beta-
smooth and
L-
Lipschitz loss function, then

m_{k}\geq\left(\frac{\frac{32T^{2}L^{4}}{\beta^{2}k^{2}}+\frac{8GTL^{2}}{k\beta}+4Ga/3}{a^{2}}\right)\ln{\frac{2n}{b}},

suffices for
\Pr(\left|\hat{\phi}^{k}_{i}-\phi^{k}_{i}\right|\geq a)\leq b/n
, and by union bound, it suffices to estimate the Shapley value:

Pr\left(\left|\hat{\varphi}_{i}-\varphi_{i}\right|\geq a\right)\leq b.

Proof.

Since each
\ell(\cdot;z)\in[0,G]
, we know that
\ell(w_{T};z)-\ell(w_{T}^{\prime};z)\leq 2G
. It follows by a direct application of Theorem
3
to Lemma
3
that

\mathbb{E}\left[(\mathcal{L}-\mathcal{L}^{\prime})^{2}\right]\leq\frac{4L^{4}}{k^{2}}\left(\sum_{t=1}^{T}\alpha_{t}\right)^{2}+G\frac{2L^{2}}{k}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\alpha_{t}.

(39)

Using the fact that Theorem
3
assumes
\alpha_{t}\leq 2/\beta
, we can finally bound

\mathbb{E}\left[(\mathcal{L}-\mathcal{L}^{\prime})^{2}\right]\leq T^{2}\frac{16L^{4}}{\beta^{2}k^{2}}+G\frac{4TL^{2}}{k\beta}

(40)

Because we have an upper bound on
\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\mathcal{L}-\mathcal{L}^{\prime}\right)^{2}\right]
given by Lemma
3
, we have an upper bound on
\hat{\sigma}^{2}=\frac{1}{m_{k}}\sum_{i=1}^{m_{k}}\mathbb{E}\left[X_{i}^{2}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\mathcal{L}-\mathcal{L}^{\prime}\right)^{2}\right]
due to the assumptions that the marginal contributions are independent and identically distributed. It therefore holds that
\hat{\sigma}^{2}\leq T^{2}\frac{16L^{4}}{\beta^{2}k^{2}}+G\frac{4TL^{2}}{k\beta}
:

\displaystyle 2\exp{\left(-\frac{m_{k}a^{2}}{2(\hat{\sigma}^{2}+2Ga/3)}\right)}

(41a)

\displaystyle\leq 2\exp{\left(-\frac{m_{k}a^{2}}{2(T^{2}\frac{16L^{4}}{\beta^{2}k^{2}}+G\frac{4TL^{2}}{k\beta}+2Ga/3)}\right)}.

(41b)

To see why the above holds, consider the function

Pr\left(-\frac{a}{b}\right)=\frac{1}{e^{\frac{a}{b}}}.

(42)

The larger
b
is, the smaller the exponent of
e
. For
b\leq c
, we thus have

e^{\frac{a}{b}}\geq e^{\frac{a}{c}}\Rightarrow\frac{1}{e^{\frac{a}{b}}}\leq\frac{1}{e^{\frac{a}{c}}}

(43)

As
\hat{\sigma}^{2}
is in the denominator of the left hand side of Inequality
64
, overestimating
\hat{\sigma}^{2}
by using
T^{2}\frac{16L^{4}}{\beta^{2}k^{2}}+G\frac{4TL^{2}}{k\beta}
to calculate the risk of deviation hence leads to the probability of a large deviation to be overestimated, which is desirable for our purpose. Using Theorem
8
, we can state

\displaystyle Pr\bigg(\left|\hat{\phi}^{k}_{i}-\phi^{k}_{i}\right|\geq\alpha\bigg)

(44a)

\displaystyle=Pr\bigg(\left|m_{k}\hat{\phi}^{k}_{i}-m_{k}\phi^{k}_{i}\right|\bigg)\geq m_{k}\alpha\bigg)

(44b)

\displaystyle\leq 2\exp{\bigg(-\frac{m_{k}\alpha^{2}/2}{\hat{\sigma}^{2}+2G\alpha/3}\bigg)}

(44c)

Since we want the probability of
\left|\hat{\varphi}-\varphi\right|\geq a
to be at most
b
, and we have
n
layers we are sampling, in each layer, we want a risk of
\frac{b}{n}
in each layer.

We now derive
m_{k}
as follows:

\displaystyle Pr\bigg(\left|\hat{\phi}^{k}_{i}-\phi^{k}_{i}\right|\geq\alpha\bigg)

(45a)

\displaystyle\leq 2\exp{\bigg(-\frac{m_{k}a^{2}}{2\hat{\sigma}^{2}+4Ga/3}\bigg)}\leq\frac{b}{n}\Rightarrow

(45b)

\displaystyle-\frac{m_{k}a^{2}}{2\hat{\sigma}^{2}+4Ga/3}\leq\ln{\frac{b}{2n}}\Rightarrow

(45c)

\displaystyle\frac{m_{k}a^{2}}{2\hat{\sigma}^{2}+4Ga/3}\geq\ln\frac{2n}{b}\Rightarrow

(45d)

\displaystyle m_{k}\geq\bigg(\frac{2\hat{\sigma}^{2}+4Ga/3}{a^{2}}\bigg)\ln\frac{2n}{b}

(45e)

Substituting
\hat{\sigma}^{2}=\frac{16T^{2}L^{4}}{\beta^{2}k^{2}}+\frac{4GTL^{2}}{k\beta}
yields the stated result.
∎

Proof of Theorem
5

Theorem 5
.

Suppose that, under the fixed subsequence interpretation, the number of samples required to compute
\hat{\phi}^{k}_{i}
such that
Pr\left(\left|\hat{\phi}^{k}_{i}-\phi^{k}_{i}\right|\geq\frac{a}{2}\right)\leq\frac{b}{2n}
using an algorithm satisfying expected uniform stability with constant
\epsilon
and loss function
\ell\in[0,G]
is
m_{k}
, then we can sample

h\geq\frac{8\left(\epsilon^{2}+\epsilon G+Ga/3\right)}{a^{2}}\ln{\frac{4nm_{k}}{b}}

(46)

permutations of each coalition
S\in\hat{S}_{k}
, where
\hat{S}_{k}
is the set of
m_{k}
coalitions sampled to compute
\hat{\phi}^{k}_{i}
such that we can compute estimate
\overline{\phi^{k}_{i}}
of
\hat{\phi}^{k}_{i}
satisfying
Pr\left(\left|\overline{\phi^{k}_{i}}-\phi^{k}_{i}\right|\geq a\right)\leq\frac{b}{n}

Proof.

The sample size follows from an application of Bernstein’s Inequality. For this purpose, we call
\hat{\mathbb{S}}
the set of
h
sampled permutations taken from
\mathbb{S}
and observe that

\hat{\sigma}^{2}=\frac{1}{h}\sum_{s\in\hat{\mathbb{S}}}\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{A}(s))-\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{A}(s^{\prime}))\right)^{2}\right]

(47)

It hence follows from Lemma
3
that

\hat{\sigma}^{2}\leq\epsilon^{2}+\epsilon G

(48)

We can substitute our values into Bernstein’s inequality to obtain:

\displaystyle Pr\left(\left|\hat{v}_{i}(S)-v_{i}(S)\right|\geq\frac{a}{2}\right)

(49a)

\displaystyle\leq 2\exp{\left(-\frac{ha^{2}}{8(\epsilon^{2}+\epsilon G+2Ga/6)}\right)}\leq\frac{b}{2m_{k}n}\Rightarrow

(49b)

\displaystyle\frac{ha^{2}}{8(\epsilon^{2}+\epsilon G+Ga/3)}\geq\ln{\frac{4nm_{k}}{b}}\Rightarrow

(49c)

\displaystyle h\geq\frac{8\left(\epsilon^{2}+\epsilon G+Ga/3\right)}{a^{2}}\ln{\frac{4nm_{k}}{b}}

(49d)

By the union bound, the probability that for at least one coalition
S
,
\left|\hat{v}_{i}(S)-v_{i}(S)\right|\geq\frac{a}{2}
holds is

Pr\left(\exists S\in\hat{S}:\left|\hat{v}_{i}(S)-v_{i}(S)\right|\geq\frac{a}{2}\right)\leq\sum_{j=1}^{m_{k}}\frac{b}{2nm_{k}}=\frac{b}{2n}

(50)

To see why our
(a,b)-
bound holds, we introduce
\overline{\phi^{k}_{i}}
defined as

\overline{\phi^{k}_{i}}=\frac{1}{m_{k}}\sum_{S\in\hat{S}_{k}}\hat{v}_{i}(S)

(51)

Remember that
\hat{\phi}^{k}_{i}=\frac{1}{m_{k}}\sum_{S\in\hat{S}_{k}}v_{i}(S)
, thus
\overline{\phi^{k}_{i}}-\hat{\phi}^{k}_{i}=\frac{1}{m_{k}}\sum_{S\in\hat{S}_{k}}\left(\hat{v}_{i}(S)-v_{i}(S)\right)
and

\displaystyle\left|\overline{\phi^{k}_{i}}-\hat{\phi}^{k}_{i}\right|=\left|\frac{1}{m_{k}}\sum_{S\in\hat{S}_{k}}\left(\hat{v}_{i}(S)-v_{i}(S)\right)\right|

(52a)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{m_{k}}\sum_{S\in\hat{S}_{k}}\left|\hat{v}_{i}(S)-v_{i}(S)\right|.

(52b)

Further, we observe that the event
\sum_{k=1}^{n}\left|\hat{v}_{i}(S)-v_{i}(S)\right|\geq m_{k}\frac{a}{2}
requires that there exists at least one
S
such that
\left|\hat{v}_{i}(S)-v_{i}(S)\right|\geq\frac{a}{2}
. We can therefore conclude that

\displaystyle Pr\left(\left|\overline{\phi^{k}_{i}}-\hat{\phi}^{k}_{i}\right|\geq\frac{a}{2}\right)

(53a)

\displaystyle\leq Pr\left(\frac{1}{m_{k}}\sum_{S\in\hat{S}_{k}}\left|\hat{v}_{i}(S)-v_{i}(S)\right|\geq\frac{a}{2}\right)

(53b)

\displaystyle=Pr\left(\sum_{S\in\hat{S}_{k}}\left|\hat{v}_{i}(S)-v_{i}(S)\right|\geq m_{k}\frac{a}{2}\right)\leq\frac{b}{2n}

(53c)

We note that
Pr\left(\left|\overline{\phi^{k}_{i}}-\phi^{k}_{i}\right|\geq a\right)
is equivalent to

Pr\left(\left(\left|\hat{\phi}^{k}_{i}-\phi^{k}_{i}\right|\geq\frac{a}{2}\right)\cup\left(\left|\overline{\phi^{k}_{i}}-\hat{\phi}^{k}_{i}\right|\geq\frac{a}{2}\right)\right)\leq\frac{b}{n}

(54)

which follows from the union bound.

Proof of Theorem
6

Theorem 6
.

Assume that
\ell(\cdot;z)\in[0,G]
is an L-Lipschitz and
\beta
-smooth loss function for
every z. Suppose that we run SGM for T steps with monotonically non-increasing step sizes
\alpha_{t}\leq c/t
. Then, for
w_{T}=A(S)
and
w_{T}^{\prime}=A(S^{\prime})
, where
S,S^{\prime}
are neighbouring datasets, we have that:

\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\ell(w_{T};z)-\ell(w_{T}^{\prime};z)\right|\right]\leq G^{\frac{\beta c}{\beta c+1}}(2cL^{2})^{\frac{1}{\beta c+1}}T^{\frac{\beta c}{\beta c+1}}\frac{1+\frac{1}{\beta c}}{k-1},

(55)

where
k
is the size of the dataset.

Proof.

It follows from the proof of Theorem 3.12 in
(
Hardt, Recht, and Singer 2016
)
that

\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\ell(w_{T};z)-\ell(w_{T}^{\prime};z)\right|\right]\leq\frac{t_{0}}{k}G+\frac{2L^{2}}{\beta(k-1)}\bigg(\frac{T}{t_{0}}\bigg)^{\beta c}=H_{sup}

(56)

To find the minimum value of the right hand side of the equation above, we need to find
t_{0}
such that:

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt_{0}}\frac{t_{0}}{k}G+\frac{2L^{2}}{\beta(k-1)}\bigg(\frac{T}{t_{0}}\bigg)^{\beta c}=0\Rightarrow

(57a)

\displaystyle\frac{G}{k}-\frac{2cL^{2}T^{\beta c}t_{0}^{-\beta_{c}-1}}{k-1}=0\Rightarrow

(57b)

\displaystyle t^{\beta c+1}=\frac{2kcL^{2}T^{\beta c}}{G(k-1)}\Rightarrow

(57c)

\displaystyle t_{0}=\sqrt[\beta c+1]{\frac{k}{k-1}}\sqrt[\beta c+1]{\frac{2cL^{2}T^{\beta c}}{G}}\approx\sqrt[\beta c+1]{\frac{2cL^{2}T^{\beta c}}{G}}

(57d)

substituting this expression back into the right hand side of Equation
56
and writing
q=\beta c
for convenience, we obtain:

\displaystyle H_{s}=\frac{\beta(k-1)t_{0}^{q+1}G+2kL^{2}T^{q}}{\beta k(k-1)t_{0}^{q}}\Rightarrow

(58a)

\displaystyle H_{s}=\frac{\beta(k-1)2cL^{2}T^{q}+2kL^{2}T^{q}}{\beta k(k-1)\bigg(\frac{2cL^{2}T^{q}}{G}\bigg)^{\frac{q}{q+1}}}

(58b)

\displaystyle=\frac{2cL^{2}T^{q}\bigg(\beta(k-1)+c^{-1}k\bigg)}{\beta k(k-1)\bigg(\frac{2cL^{2}}{G}\bigg)^{\frac{q}{q+1}}T^{\frac{q^{2}}{q+1}}}

(58c)

Simplifying further, we arrive at:

\displaystyle H_{s}=G^{\frac{q}{q+1}}\frac{(2cL^{2})^{\frac{1}{q+1}}T^{\frac{q}{q+1}}\bigg(\beta(k-1)+\frac{k}{c}\bigg)}{\beta k(k-1)}\Rightarrow

(59a)

\displaystyle\leq G^{\frac{q}{q+1}}(2cL^{2})^{\frac{1}{q+1}}T^{\frac{q}{q+1}}\frac{\beta k(1+\frac{1}{\beta c})}{\beta k(k-1)}=

(59b)

\displaystyle=G^{\frac{q}{q+1}}(2cL^{2})^{\frac{1}{q+1}}T^{\frac{q}{q+1}}\frac{1+\frac{1}{\beta c}}{k-1}

(59c)

∎

Further Experimental Details

Strongly convex experiments were completed using a 2.9 GHz Quad-Core Intel Core i7 processor. Non-convex experiments used NVIDIA RT2080Ti 11GB GPUs.
