On the BER vs. Bandwidth-Efficiency Trade-offs in Windowed OTSM Dispensing with Zero-Padding
Zeping Sui 1 , Hongming Zhang 2 , Hien Quoc Ngo 1 , Michail Matthaiou 1 and Lajos Hanzo 3

Abstract
An orthogonal time sequency multiplexing (OTSM) scheme using practical signaling functions is proposed under strong phase noise (PHN) scenarios. By utilizing the transform relationships between the delay-sequency (DS), time-frequency (TF) and time-domains, we first conceive the DS-domain input-output relationship of our OTSM system, where the conventional zero-padding is discarded to increase the spectral efficiency. Then, the unconditional pairwise error probability is derived, followed by deriving the bit error ratio (BER) upper bound in closed-form. Moreover, we compare the BER performance of our OTSM system based on several practical signaling functions. Our simulation results demonstrate that the upper bound derived accurately predicts the BER performance in the case of moderate to high signal-to-noise ratios (SNRs), while harnessing practical window functions is capable of attaining an attractive out-of-band emission (OOBE) vs. BER trade-off.



I
Introduction

Recently, the delay-Doppler (DD)-domain orthogonal time frequency space (OTFS) modulation scheme has emerged as an attractive waveform candidate for the 6G wireless networks since it is capable of handling high mobility scenarios
[
1
,
2
,
3

[
4
,
5
,
6
]
. The approximate orthogonalities in delay and Doppler domains can be achieved by invoking time and frequency periodic basis functions
[
7
]
. More importantly, since the sparse DD-domain representation of doubly-selective channels can be viewed as being quasi-static
[
8
,
9
,
10
]
, OTFS can attain a better BER performance than conventional orthogonal frequency division multiplexing (OFDM)
[
11
,
12
,
13
,
14
]
.

However, the complexity of the ISFFT/SFFT becomes excessive, particularly when the number of subcarriers and time-slots is high
[
12
,
15
,
16
]
. To this end, by mapping the symbols onto the DS-domain and harnessing the inverse Walsh-Hadamard transform (IWHT) to obtain the delay-time (DT) domain signal frame, OTSM has been proposed in
[
17
]
, where sequency denotes the number of zero-crossings per unit time. It was observed in
[
18
]
that OTSM is capable of attaining a similar BER performance to that of OTFS at a lower system complexity, since the IWHT reduces the number of multiplication operations
[
18
,
19
]
. Thaj
et al.
conceived a time-domain Gauss-Seidel detector for zero-padding-aided OTSM (ZP-OTSM) systems. Later in
[
19
]
, the input-output relationship of rectangular pulse-based non-ZP OTSM systems was derived, and a low-complexity vector approximate message passing detector was proposed. More recently, index modulation was combined with OTSM to obtain error performance gains
[
20
]
. Nonetheless, the above-mentioned OTSM schemes all utilize rectangular transmit windows, hence their sinc-shaped spectrum results in high OOBE
[
21
]
. Moreover, the system spectral efficiency suffers a lot due to the adoption of ZP. Furthermore, in realistic electronic circuit based scenarios, the jitter of the oscillator varies rapidly with the voltage
[
22
,
23
,
24
]
, resulting in severe PHN, which was ignored in the above mentioned OTSM related works.

To address the above issues, by dispensing with ZP, we conceive a practical smooth window-based band-limited OTSM system operating in the face of realistic PHN. The contributions of our paper are detailed as follows:

•

We first derive the DS-domain input-output relationship of the OTSM system based on the transform relationships of different domains, where both arbitrary window waveforms and the PHN effects are considered.

•

Then, based on the input-output relationship derived, the conditional pairwise error probability (CPEP) and the unconditional PEP (UPEP) of our practical OTSM systems are derived in the presence of PHN by exploiting the pairwise error events. Furthermore, we derive a closed-form BER upper bound of our OTSM systems, yielding an accurate prediction of the BER performance at high SNRs.

•

By exploiting several practical windows functions, we compare both the OOBE and the BER performance of low-density parity-check (LDPC)-coded OTSM systems by simulations. It is demonstrated that there is a trade-off between the BER and the OOBE.

II
System Model

II-A

Transmitter Description of Non-ZP OTSM Relying on Smooth Windowing

Let us consider an OTSM system having a bandwidth of
B=M\Delta f
and frame duration of
T_{f}=NT
, where
M
and
N
represent the number of bins along the delay- and sequency-domains, while
\Delta f
and
T=1/\Delta f
denote the frequency-domain (FD) sampling interval and the symbol duration, respectively. As illustrated in Fig.
1
, the LDPC-coded information bits are mapped onto
MN
symbols based on a
Q
-ary normalized constellation
\mathcal{A}=\{a_{1},\ldots,a_{Q}\}
, yielding a DS-domain OTSM frame
\boldsymbol{X}_{\text{DS}}\in\mathbb{C}^{M\times N}
. Therefore, the overall number of transmitted bits is
L=MN\log_{2}Q
. Consequently, upon exploiting the discrete Fourier transform (DFT) and IWHT along the delay- and sequency-domains, the elements of the TF-domain transmit frame matrix can be obtained as

\displaystyle X_{\text{TF}}(m,n)=\sum_{l=0}^{M-1}\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\frac{X_{\text{DS}}(l,k)}{\sqrt{MN}}\mathcal{W}_{N}(n,k)e^{-j2\pi\frac{ml}{M}},

(1)

for
n=0,\ldots,N-1
and
m=0,\ldots,M-1
. More specifically, we have
\mathcal{W}_{N}(n,k)=W(n,m/N+1/2N)/\sqrt{N}
, where
W(n,\xi)
is the Walsh function associated with
0\leq\xi\leq 1
. Therefore, (
1
) can be rewritten in the vectorial form of
\boldsymbol{X}_{\text{TF}}=\boldsymbol{\mathcal{F}}_{M}\boldsymbol{X}_{\text{DS}}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{W}}_{N}
, where
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{F}}_{M}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{W}}_{N}
denote the normalized
M
-point DFT and
N
-point WHT matrices, respectively. Then, by employing the Heisenberg transform, the transmitted time-domain (TD) signal can be expressed as
[
11
,
3
,
9
]

\displaystyle s(t)=\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\sum_{m=0}^{M-1}X_{\text{TF}}(m,n)g_{\text{tx}}(t-nT)e^{j2\pi m\Delta f(t-nT)},

(2)

where
g_{\text{tx}}(t)
represents the TD signaling function. Then, we sample
s(t)
at the interval of
1/(M\Delta f)
, hence (
2
) can be formulated in a vectorial form based on (
1
) as

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{s}=\text{vec}(\boldsymbol{G}_{\text{tx}}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{F}}_{M}^{H}\boldsymbol{X}_{\text{TF}})=(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{W}}_{N}\otimes\boldsymbol{G}_{\text{tx}})\boldsymbol{x},

(3)

where
\boldsymbol{G}_{\text{tx}}=\text{diag}\left[g_{\text{tx}}(0),g_{\text{tx}}(T/M),\ldots,g_{\text{tx}}((M-1)T/M)\right]
, and
\boldsymbol{x}=\text{vec}(\boldsymbol{X}_{\text{DS}})
. Moreover,
(\cdot)^{H}
,
\otimes
and
\text{vec}(\cdot)
denote the conjugate-transpose, Kronecker product and the vectorization operators, respectively.

II-B

PHN-contaminated Received Signals

Next, a time-varying Rayleigh fading channel having
P
paths is invoked, whose channel impulse response is formulated as

\displaystyle h(\tau,\nu)=\sum_{p=1}^{P}h_{p}\delta(\tau-\tau_{p})\delta(\nu-\nu_{p}),

(4)

where
h_{p}
,
\tau_{p}
and
\nu_{p}
denote the path gain, delay- and Doppler-shifts of the
p
th channel tap, respectively, while
\delta(\cdot)
represents the Dirac-delta function. Explicitly, upon considering a wideband system, we have
\tau_{p}=\frac{l_{p}}{M\Delta f}
and
\nu_{i}=\frac{\beta_{p}}{NT}
with
\beta_{p}=k_{p}+\kappa_{p}
. Moreover,
l_{p}
and
k_{p}
are the
p
th integer delay and Doppler indices, while
\kappa_{p}
denote the fractional Doppler component. Note that the independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) Gaussian variables
h_{p}
can be formulated as
h_{p}\sim\mathcal{CN}(0,1/P)
. Let us now consider the oscillator phase noise at the receiver. Then, the received TD signal can be expressed as

\displaystyle r(t)=e^{j\theta(t)}\int\int h(\tau,\nu)s(t-\tau)e^{j2\pi\nu(t-\tau)}d\tau d\nu+n(t),

(5)

where
e^{j\theta(t)}
and
n(t)\sim\mathcal{CN}(0,N_{0})
are the PHN at the receiver side and additive white Gaussian noise terms. By sampling (
5
) with an interval of
1/(M\Delta f)
, the
MN\times 1
-dimensional received signal vector can be formulated as

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{r}=\boldsymbol{\Theta}\boldsymbol{H}_{\text{T}}\boldsymbol{s}+\boldsymbol{n}_{\text{T}},

(6)

where
\boldsymbol{\Theta}=\text{diag}[e^{j\theta(0)},e^{j\theta(1)},\ldots,e^{j\theta(MN-1)}]
represents the PHN matrix,
\boldsymbol{n}_{\text{T}}
is the TD noise vector, and the TD channel matrix
\boldsymbol{H}_{\text{T}}
can be expressed as
\boldsymbol{H}_{\text{T}}=\sum_{p=1}^{P}h_{p}\boldsymbol{\Pi}^{\alpha_{p}}\boldsymbol{\Delta}^{\beta_{p}}
, where
\boldsymbol{\Pi}
denotes the permutation matrix, which is obtained by employing forward cyclic shift of each row of
\boldsymbol{I}_{MN}
, while
\boldsymbol{\Delta}=\text{diag}[z^{0},\ldots,z^{MN-1}]
with
z=e^{j\frac{2\pi}{MN}}
. Explicitly, the free-running oscillator can be modelled as a Wiener process, yielding white PHN samples as
[
22
,
23
]

\displaystyle\theta(q)=\theta(q-1)+\Delta_{\text{PHN}},

(7)

for
q=1,\ldots,MN-1
, where
\Delta_{\text{PHN}}
is the difference of white PHN samples with zero-mean and a variance of
\sigma^{2}
. Based on the Wigner transform
[
10
]
, the elements of the TF-domain symbol matrix
\boldsymbol{Y}_{\text{TF}}
can be formulated as

\displaystyle Y_{\text{TF}}(m,n)=\int r(t)g_{\text{rx}}^{*}(t-nT)e^{-j2\pi m\Delta f(t-nT)}dt,

(8)

where
g_{\text{rx}}(t)
denotes the receive window function, and
(\cdot)^{*}
is the conjugate operator. Therefore, (
8
) can be rewritten in a vectorial form as
\boldsymbol{Y}_{\text{TF}}=\boldsymbol{\mathcal{F}}_{M}\boldsymbol{G}_{\text{rx}}\boldsymbol{R}
, where
\boldsymbol{R}=\text{vec}^{-1}(\boldsymbol{r})
and
\boldsymbol{G}_{\text{rx}}=\text{diag}\left[g_{\text{rx}}(0),g_{\text{rx}}(T/M),\ldots,g_{\text{rx}}((M-1)T/M)\right]
. By leveraging the inverse transforms of (
1
), the elements of the DS-domain received symbol matrix can be expressed as

\displaystyle Y_{\text{DS}}(l,k)=\sum_{m=0}^{M-1}\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\frac{{{Y}_{\text{TF}}(m,n)}}{\sqrt{MN}}\mathcal{W}_{N}(k,n)e^{j2\pi\frac{ml}{M}},

(9)

for
l=0,\ldots,M-1
and
K=0,\ldots,N-1
. It can be readily shown that
\boldsymbol{Y}_{\text{DS}}=\boldsymbol{\mathcal{F}}_{M}^{H}\boldsymbol{Y}_{\text{TF}}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{W}}_{N}=\boldsymbol{\mathcal{F}}_{M}^{H}(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{F}}_{M}\boldsymbol{G}_{\text{rx}}\boldsymbol{R})\boldsymbol{\mathcal{W}}_{N}=\boldsymbol{G}_{\text{rx}}\boldsymbol{R}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{W}}_{N}
, and we have

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{y}=\text{vec}(\boldsymbol{Y}_{\text{DS}})=\text{vec}(\boldsymbol{G}_{\text{rx}}\boldsymbol{R}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{W}}_{N})=(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{W}}_{N}\otimes\boldsymbol{G}_{\text{rx}})\boldsymbol{r}.

(10)

By substituting (
3
) and (
6
) into (
10
), the DS-domain input-output relationship can be formulated as

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{y}

\displaystyle=(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{W}}_{N}\otimes\boldsymbol{G}_{\text{rx}})\boldsymbol{\Theta}\boldsymbol{H}_{\text{T}}(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{W}}_{N}\otimes\boldsymbol{G}_{\text{tx}})\boldsymbol{x}+(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{W}}_{N}\otimes\boldsymbol{G}_{\text{rx}})\boldsymbol{n}_{\text{T}}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{H}\boldsymbol{x}+\boldsymbol{n},

(11)

where
\boldsymbol{H}=(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{W}}_{N}\otimes\boldsymbol{G}_{\text{rx}})\boldsymbol{\Theta}\boldsymbol{H}_{\text{T}}(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{W}}_{N}\otimes\boldsymbol{G}_{\text{tx}})
is the DS-domain channel matrix, and we have
\boldsymbol{n}\sim\mathcal{CN}(\boldsymbol{0},N_{0}\boldsymbol{I}_{MN})
, leading to the SNR per symbol as
\gamma=1/N_{0}
.

III
Performance Analysis and Smooth Windows

III-A

Analysis of Error Performance

By collecting all the channel gain coefficients
h_{p}
in a vector, (
II-B
) can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{y}=\boldsymbol{\Sigma}(\boldsymbol{x})\boldsymbol{h}+\boldsymbol{{n}},

(12)

where we have
\boldsymbol{h}=[h_{1},\ldots,h_{P}]^{T}
, while the codeword matrix
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}(\boldsymbol{x})\in\mathbb{C}^{MN\times P}
can be formulated as

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Sigma}(\boldsymbol{x})=[\underbrace{\boldsymbol{\Upsilon}_{1}\boldsymbol{x}}_{MN\times 1}\quad\boldsymbol{\Upsilon}_{2}\boldsymbol{x}\quad\ldots\quad\boldsymbol{\Upsilon}_{P}\boldsymbol{x}],

(13)

where
\boldsymbol{\Upsilon}_{p}=(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{W}}_{N}\otimes\boldsymbol{G}_{\text{rx}})(\boldsymbol{\Theta}\boldsymbol{\Pi}^{\alpha_{p}}\boldsymbol{\Delta}^{\beta_{p}})(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{W}}_{N}\otimes\boldsymbol{G}_{\text{tx}})
, for
p=1,\ldots,P
. Here, we consider the pairwise error event
\{\boldsymbol{x}\rightarrow\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}\}
, where
\boldsymbol{x}
and
\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}
represent the transmitted and the received error symbol vectors, respectively, yielding the error symbol
\boldsymbol{e}=\boldsymbol{x}-\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}
. Therefore, the corresponding Euclidean distance is given by

\displaystyle\eta=||\boldsymbol{\Sigma}(\boldsymbol{e})\boldsymbol{h}||_{2}^{2}=\boldsymbol{h}^{H}\boldsymbol{\Xi}\boldsymbol{h},

(14)

where
||\cdot||_{2}
denotes the
\ell_{2}
-norm operator, and
\boldsymbol{\Xi}=\boldsymbol{\Sigma}(\boldsymbol{e})^{H}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}(\boldsymbol{e})
can be further expressed based on (
13
) and (
14
) as

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Xi}=\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{e}^{H}\boldsymbol{\Upsilon}_{1}^{H}\boldsymbol{\Upsilon}_{1}\boldsymbol{e}&\cdots&\boldsymbol{e}^{H}\boldsymbol{\Upsilon}_{1}^{H}\boldsymbol{\Upsilon}_{P}\boldsymbol{e}\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\boldsymbol{e}^{H}\boldsymbol{\Upsilon}_{P}^{H}\boldsymbol{\Upsilon}_{1}\boldsymbol{e}&\cdots&\boldsymbol{e}^{H}\boldsymbol{\Upsilon}_{P}^{H}\boldsymbol{\Upsilon}_{P}\boldsymbol{e}\end{bmatrix}.

(15)

In the case of exploiting the optimal maximum likelihood detector (MLD), the CPEP can be formulated based on the Chernoff bound as
[
25
,
19
]

\displaystyle P(\boldsymbol{x}\rightarrow\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}|\boldsymbol{h})\leq\frac{1}{2}\exp\left(-\frac{\eta}{4N_{0}}\right).

(16)

Since
\boldsymbol{\Xi}
is a positive semidefinite Hermitian matrix, its rank satisfies
1\leq r\leq P
. By sorting the eigenvalues in descending order, the eigenvalues and the corresponding eigenvectors can be expressed as
\left\{\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{r}\right\}
and
\left\{\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{r}\right\}
, respectively. Therefore, the CPEP upper bound can be modified as

\displaystyle P(\boldsymbol{x}\rightarrow\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}|\boldsymbol{h})\leq\frac{1}{2}\exp\left(-\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{r}\lambda_{i}|\tilde{h}_{i}|^{2}}{4N_{0}}\right),

(17)

where
\tilde{h}_{i}=\left\langle\boldsymbol{h},\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{i}\right\rangle
for
i=1,\ldots,r
, here
\left\langle\cdot,\cdot\right\rangle
denotes the inner product between two vectors. Furthermore, it can be shown that the independent complex-valued random variables
\{\tilde{h}_{1},\ldots,\tilde{h}_{r}\}
have a mean of
\varsigma_{i}=\left\langle\mathbb{E}[\boldsymbol{h}],\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{i}\right\rangle
and a variance of
1/(2P)
per real dimension, when
\mathbb{E}[\cdot]
represents the expectation operator. Therefore, based on the Ricean distribution properties,
|\tilde{h}_{i}|
are Ricean distributed variables with a Ricean factor of
\xi_{i}=|\varsigma_{i}|^{2}

[
26
]
, hence its probability density function can be written as

\displaystyle p(|\tilde{h}_{i}|)=2P|\tilde{h}_{i}|\exp(-P|\tilde{h}_{i}|^{2}-P\xi_{i})I_{0}(2P|\tilde{h}_{i}|\sqrt{\xi_{i}}),

(18)

where
I_{0}(\cdot)
denotes the zero-order modified Bessel function of the first kind. Based on (
17
) and (
18
), the UPEP can be formulated as
[
26
]

\displaystyle P(\boldsymbol{x}\rightarrow\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}})\leq\frac{1}{2}\prod_{i=1}^{r}\frac{1}{1+\frac{\lambda_{i}}{4PN_{0}}}\exp\left(-\frac{\frac{\xi_{i}\lambda_{i}}{4PN_{0}}}{1+\frac{\lambda_{i}}{4PN_{0}}}\right).

(19)

It can be observed that
|h_{i}|
follows the Rayleigh distribution in the case of
\xi_{i}=0
. Hence, in high SNR scenarios (
N_{0}\ll 1
), the UPEP of (
19
) can be rewritten as

\displaystyle P(\boldsymbol{x}\rightarrow\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}})\leq\frac{1}{2\prod_{i=1}^{r}\lambda_{i}}\left(\frac{1}{4PN_{0}}\right)^{-r}.

(20)

Finally, by invoking the union bounding technique, the BER of OTSM systems in the presence of practical windows and PHN can be approximated as

\displaystyle P_{\text{B}}\approx\frac{1}{L2^{L}}\sum_{\boldsymbol{x}}\sum_{\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}}P(\boldsymbol{x}\rightarrow\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}})d(\boldsymbol{x},\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}),

(21)

where
d(\boldsymbol{x},\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}})
is the number of different bits between
\boldsymbol{x}
and
\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}
. Consequently, the BER upper bound at high SNRs can be further reformulated based on (
20
) and (
21
) as

\displaystyle P_{\text{B}}\leq\frac{1}{L2^{L+1}}\sum_{\boldsymbol{x}}\sum_{\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}}\frac{1}{\prod_{i=1}^{r}\lambda_{i}}\left(\frac{1}{4PN_{0}}\right)^{-r}d(\boldsymbol{x},\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}).

(22)

III-B

Smooth Windows

The most commonly invoked practical windows are the rectangular, Hamming, Hanning, Blackman and Bartlett-Hann waveforms
[
27
,
28
,
29
]
. Given the time-duration of the window
T_{0}=(M-1)T/M
and the notation of

\displaystyle\Lambda(t/T)=\begin{cases}1,&\mbox{$|t|\leq T/2$},\\
0,&\text{otherwise,}\end{cases}

(23)

the window functions can be expressed as follows
[
28
]
:

Rectangular Window

\displaystyle g(t)=\Lambda\left(\frac{t-T_{0}/2}{T_{0}}\right).

(24)

Hamming Window

\displaystyle g(t)=\left(0.54-0.46\cos\frac{2\pi t}{T_{0}}\right)\Lambda\left(\frac{t-T_{0}/2}{T_{0}}\right).

(25)

Hanning Window

\displaystyle g(t)=\left(0.5-0.5\cos\frac{2\pi t}{T_{0}}\right)\Lambda\left(\frac{t-T_{0}/2}{T_{0}}\right).

(26)

Blackman Window

\displaystyle g(t)=\left(0.42-0.5\cos\frac{2\pi t}{T_{0}}+0.08\cos\frac{4\pi t}{T_{0}}\right)\Lambda\left(\frac{t-T_{0}/2}{T_{0}}\right).

(27)

Bartlett-Hann Window

\displaystyle g(t)=

\displaystyle\left(0.62-0.48\left|t^{\prime}\right|+0.38\cos 2\pi t^{\prime}\right)\Lambda\left(t^{\prime}\right),

(28)

where
t^{\prime}={(t-T_{0}/2)}/{T_{0}}
.

V
Conclusions

By considering the transforms between DS- and TF-domains, we have derived the DS-domain input-output relationship of the OTSM system, which invokes arbitrary windowing functions in the presence of strong PHN. Then, we have provided the error performance analysis of our practical OTSM systems. It was demonstrated that the BER upper bound derived becomes tight at moderate to high SNRs. Furthermore, the simulation results have also illustrated that our OTSM systems relying on practical windows can strike a trade-off between the OOBE and BER. Similar to
[
31
]
, we will exploit optimization algorithms to design optimal smooth windows to attain good BER and OOBE performance simultaneously in our future work.
