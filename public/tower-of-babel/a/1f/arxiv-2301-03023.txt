1.
Introduction and Statement of Results

In mathematical physics, resonances are the main spectral data in situations where eigenvalues are missing, which is often the case when the underlying geometry is not finite. In the last few decades, a great deal of research has been devoted to the mathematical study of resonances. We refer to
[
20
]
for a broad introduction to this subject.

In this paper, we are interested in “convex cocompact” hyperbolic surfaces, that is, infinite-area surfaces of constant negative curvature
-1
that can be decomposed into a compact surface
N
with geodesic boundary
1
1

1

The surface
N
is sometimes called the “Nielsen region”
and a finite number of funnel ends glued to
N
. Equivalently, a hyperbolic surface is said to be convex cocompact if it is geometrically finite and has no cusps.

The resonances of a hyperbolic surface
X
are defined as the poles of the meromorphic continuation of the resolvent

(1)

R_{X}(s)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}(\Delta_{X}-s(1-s))^{-1}\colon C_{c}^{\infty}(X)\to C^{\infty}(X),

where
\Delta_{X}
is the positive Laplacian on
X
. A proof of the meromorphic continuability of (
1
) to
s\in\mathbb{C}
was given by
[
9
]
. We also refer the reader to Borthwick’s book
[
1
]
which a good reference for the spectral theory of infinite-area hyperbolic surfaces, a subject that has not yet been fully explored.

Every hyperbolic surface
X
can be realized as a quotient
\Gamma\backslash\mathbb{H}^{2}
where
\mathbb{H}^{2}
is the hyperbolic plane and
\Gamma\subset\mathrm{PSL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
is a Fuchsian group (a discrete group of Möbius transformations). The limit set of
X
, denoted by
\Lambda
, is defined as the set of accumulation points of all orbits of the action of
\Gamma
on
\mathbb{H}^{2}
. The limit set is a Cantor-like fractal subset of
\partial\mathbb{H}^{2}\cong\mathbb{R}\cup\{\infty\}
whose Hausdorff dimension we denote by
\delta.

By a result of Patterson
[
15
]
,
X
has a simple resonance at
s=\delta
and all the remaining resonances (of which there are infinitely many by
[
9
]
) lie in half-plane
\mathrm{Re}(s)<\delta
. The multiplicity of a resonance
s_{0}
is defined as the rank of the residual operator (
1
) at
s=s_{0}.
From now on, we assume that
X
is convex cocompact and we denote by
\mathcal{R}_{X}
the set of resonances for
X
. We are interested in the resonance counting functions

M_{X}(\sigma,T)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\#\{s\in\mathcal{R}_{X}:\text{$\mathrm{Re}(s)\geqslant\sigma$ and $|\mathrm{Im}(s)-1|\leqslant T$}\}

and

N_{X}(\sigma,T)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\#\{s\in\mathcal{R}_{X}:\text{$\mathrm{Re}(s)\geqslant\sigma$ and $0\leqslant\mathrm{Im}(s)\leqslant T$}\},

where resonances are counted with multiplicities. These functions have been studied by a number of different authors. Using transfer operator techniques, Guillopé–Lin–Zworski
[
8
]
proved that for all
\sigma<\delta
we have, as
T\to\infty
,

(2)

M_{X}(\sigma,T)\ll T^{\delta}
and
N_{X}(\sigma,T)\ll T^{1+\delta}
.

(Note that the second bound follows by integrating the first one over
T
.) This is consistent with the “fractal Weyl conjecture”. According to this conjecture, there exists some
\sigma_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
such that for all
\sigma<\sigma_{0}
we have

N_{X}(\sigma,T)\asymp T^{\frac{1}{2}\dim\Omega(X)},

where
\dim\Omega(X)
is the dimension of the classical trapped set
\Omega(X)
of the geodesic flow on
X
, which in our setting is equal to
\dim\Omega(X)=2(1+\delta).
For more background on this conjecture we refer to
[
12
]
. For numerical evidence in support of it we refer to
[
1
, Chapter 16]
and
[
4
]
.

Naud
[
13
]
improved the bound in (
2
) in the range
\sigma>\delta/2
by showing that there exists a function
\tau(\sigma)
, where
\tau(\sigma)
is positive for
\sigma\in(\delta/2,\delta)
, such that

(3)

M_{X}(\sigma,T)\ll T^{\delta-\tau(\sigma)}
and
N_{X}(\sigma,T)\ll T^{1+\delta-\tau(\sigma)}
.

This result goes in the direction of a conjecture of Jakobson–Naud
[
10
]
, which states that the “essential spectral gap” of a hyperbolic surface
X
is equal to
\delta/2
. This is equivalent to the statement that for all
\sigma>\delta/2
we have
N_{X}(\sigma,T)=O(1)
, which seems out of reach given the current state of the art. The heuristic justification for this conjecture is an estimate for the spectral radius of the transfer operator associated the Schottky representation of
X
(see §
2.5
).

Using the fractal uncertainty principle, Dyatlov
[
4
]
proved that

(4)

M_{X}(\sigma,T)\ll_{\epsilon}T^{2(\delta-\sigma)+\epsilon}=T^{\delta-(2\sigma-\delta)+\epsilon}

and consequently

(5)

N_{X}(\sigma,T)\ll_{\epsilon}T^{1+2(\delta-\sigma)+\epsilon}=T^{1+\delta-(2\sigma-\delta)+\epsilon}

Once again, this improves upon (
2
) when
\sigma>\delta/2,
and makes Naud’s bound (
3
) explicit. The purpose of this paper is to further improve upon (
5
) by proving the following:

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
X
be a non-elementary, convex cocompact hyperbolic surface and let
\delta
be the dimension of its limit set. For every
\sigma>\delta/2
and every
\epsilon>0
we have

(6)

N_{X}(\sigma,T)\ll_{\epsilon}T^{1+\delta-\frac{3\delta+2}{2\delta+2}(2\sigma-\delta)+\epsilon}.

We are not able to improve upon the bound for
M_{X}(\sigma,T)
due to technical reasons discussed in §
1.1
below. Nevertheless, Theorem
1.1
shows that there are resonance-free boxes with arbitrarily large height in the strip

\left\{\delta-\frac{\delta^{2}}{6\delta+4}<\mathrm{Re}(s)<\delta\right\}.

More precisely, we have the following:

Corollary 1.2
.

Let
X
be as in Theorem
1.1
. For every
\lambda\in\left(0,\frac{\delta^{2}}{2\delta+2}\right)
and for every
\epsilon>0
sufficiently small there exists a density one subset
\mathcal{S}\subseteq\mathbb{N}
such that for all
n\in\mathcal{S}
the box

\left[\delta-\frac{\delta^{2}}{6\delta+4}+\frac{\delta+1}{3\delta+2}\lambda+\epsilon,\delta\right]+i[n,n+n^{\lambda}]

contains no resonances for
X
.

Proof.

Fix
\sigma\in[\frac{\delta}{2},\delta]
and
\epsilon>0
. By Theorem
1.1
the number of resonances inside the box
[T,2T]+i[\sigma,\delta]
is bounded by

\#\left([T,2T]+i[\sigma,\delta]\cap\mathcal{R}_{X}\right)\ll_{\epsilon}T^{1+\delta-\frac{3\delta+2}{2\delta+2}(2\sigma-\delta)+\epsilon}.

The interval
[T,2T]
contains roughly
T^{1-\lambda}
mutually disjoint intervals of the form
I_{n}=[n,n+n^{\lambda}]
with
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Hence, by the pigeonhole principle, for all but a tiny fraction of the intervals
I_{n}
, the number of resonances inside
I_{n}+i[\sigma,\delta]
is bounded by

(7)

\#\left(I_{n}+i[\sigma,\delta]\cap\mathcal{R}_{X}\right)\ll_{\epsilon}T^{\delta-\frac{3\delta+2}{2\delta+2}(2\sigma-\delta)+\lambda+\epsilon}.

A simple calculation shows that if we take

\sigma=\delta-\frac{\delta^{2}}{6\delta+4}+\frac{\delta+1}{3\delta+2}\lambda+\epsilon,

then the exponent in (
7
) drops below zero, and hence, for
T
sufficiently large, this implies that

\#\left(I_{n}+i[\sigma,\delta]\cap\mathcal{R}_{X}\right)=0,

completing the proof.
∎

1.1.
Outline of the proof

We now give an outline of the proof of Theorem
1.1
. Fix a non-elementary, convex cocompact hyperbolic surface
X
and let
\Lambda
be its limit set. We exploit the fact that resonances for
X
occur as zeros of the Fredholm determinant

f(s)=\det(1-A(s))

for a suitable family of holomorphic trace class operators
A(s)
with
s\in\mathbb{C}
acting on the functional space
H^{2}(\Omega)
, the Hilbert space of holomorphic
L^{2}
-functions on some neighbourhood
\Omega\subset\mathbb{C}
of
\Lambda
. The operators
A(s)
can be constructed explicitly after having fixed a Schottky group
\Gamma
such that
X\cong\Gamma\backslash\mathbb{H}^{2}
, see §
2
. We could take
A(s)
to be the standard transfer operator
\mathcal{L}_{s}
(see §
2.5
) or the “refined” transfer operator
\mathcal{L}_{\tau,s}
with some suitable resolution parameter
\tau>0
(see §
2.6
). In this paper we work with

A(s)=\mathcal{L}_{\tau_{0},\tau_{1},s}^{2}

for some suitable parameters
\tau_{0}>0
and
\tau_{1}>0
, where
\mathcal{L}_{\tau_{0},\tau_{1},s}
is the composed operator

\mathcal{L}_{\tau_{0},\tau_{1},s}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\mathcal{L}_{\tau_{0},s}\circ\mathcal{L}_{\tau_{1},s}.

The parameters
\tau_{0}
and
\tau_{1}
should be thought of as being small and will be chosen depending on
T
to optimize the estimates during the proof.

Following an idea of Guillopé–Lin–Zworski
[
8
]
, we introduce an additional (small) parameter
h>0
and we let
\mathcal{L}_{\tau_{0},\tau_{1},s}
act on the complex
h
-neighbourhood of the limit set

\Omega(h)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\Lambda+D_{\mathbb{C}}(0,h).

If
h,\tau_{0},\tau_{1}
are small enough, this gives a family of well-defined, trace class operators

\mathcal{L}_{\tau_{0},\tau_{1},s}\colon H^{2}(\Omega(h))\to H^{2}(\Omega(h)).

Finding an upper bound for
N_{X}(\sigma,T)
amounts to counting the zeros of the holomorphic function

f(s)=\det(1-\mathcal{L}_{\tau_{0},\tau_{1},s}^{2})

inside the rectangular box
[\sigma,\delta]+i[0,T]
. To that effect, we first observe that the log-modulus of
f(s)
is bounded by

\log|f(s)|\leqslant\|\mathcal{L}_{\tau_{0},\tau_{1},s}\|_{\mathrm{HS},h}^{2},

where
\|\cdot\|_{\mathrm{HS},h}
denotes the Hilbert–Schmidt norm of operators on
H^{2}(\Omega(h))
. Using a variant of Jensen’s formula we then deduce that
N_{X}(\sigma,T)
is bounded from above essentially by

\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}\|\mathcal{L}_{\tau_{0},\tau_{1},\sigma+it}\|_{\mathrm{HS},h}^{2}dt,

see Proposition
3.5
for a precise statement. A similar approach to count resonances can be found in Naud’s work
[
13
]
who used suitable powers
\mathcal{L}_{s}^{N}
of the standard transfer operator instead of
\mathcal{L}_{\tau_{0},\tau_{1},s}
.

To proceed, we note that elements of the free Schottky group
\Gamma
can be indexed by reduced words
a
in the alphabet
\mathcal{A}=\{1,\dots,2m\}
. We can write down an explicit formula for the Hilbert–Schmidt norm of
\mathcal{L}_{\tau_{0},\tau_{1},s}
in terms of the Bergman kernel of
H^{2}(\Omega(h))
. This formula is a sum over finitely many pairs of reduced words
(\textbf{a},\textbf{b})
in the alphabet
\mathcal{A}
. Using a separation lemma and choosing
\tau_{0}\approx h
this expression reduces to a sum over pairs of words of the form
(\textbf{a},\textbf{b})=(\textbf{c}\textbf{a}_{1},\textbf{c}\textbf{b}_{1})
with a large common prefix
c
, that is, only “near-diagonal” terms have a non-zero contribution. It turns out that by averaging these terms individually over
|\mathrm{Im}(s)|=t\in[0,T]
, we can exhibit some significant cancellation, allowing us to beat the estimate in (
5
). More precisely, we are led to consider averaged oscillatory integrals of the form

(8)

\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega_{b}(h)}e^{it\Phi_{\textbf{a},\textbf{b}}(z)}g_{\textbf{a},\textbf{b}}(z)\dvol(z)dt,

where
\Phi_{\textbf{a},\textbf{b}}(z)
is a so-called “phase function” and
g_{\textbf{a},\textbf{b}}(z)
is some function depending on the words
a
and
b
. When
\textbf{a}\neq\textbf{b}
we prove an upper bound for (
8
) that is significantly better than what one would get with the triangle inequality, see Proposition
3.4
. In order to prove this bound, it is crucial to control the derivatives of the phase
\Phi_{\textbf{a},\textbf{b}}(z)
.

We note that the same approach could, in principle, be used to estimate the function
M_{X}(\sigma,T)
, which is bounded roughly by
\|\mathcal{L}_{\tau_{0},\tau_{1},s}\|_{\mathrm{HS},h}^{2}
. Similarly as above, this leads to oscillatory integrals of the form

(9)

\int_{\Omega_{b}(h)}e^{it\Phi_{\textbf{a},\textbf{b}}(z)}g(z)\dvol(z).

To the author’s knowledge, all the non-trivial estimates for (
9
) involve derivatives of
g(z)
, which in our application are rather large as
h\to 0^{+}
, rendering these estimates unusable for our purposes. Fortunately, this issue does not arise in the averaged version (
8
).

The cancellation in (
8
) should reflect the existence of large resonance-free regions in the strip
\{\delta/2<\mathrm{Re}(s)\leq\delta\}
. Numerical evidence for such regions can be found in the appendix of
[
4
]
.

2.
Preliminaries

2.1.
Hyperbolic geometry

We start by reviewing some basic facts about hyperbolic geometry. We refer the reader to Borthwick’s book
[
1
]
for a comprehensive discussion. One of the standard models for the hyperbolic plane is the Poincaré half-plane

\mathbb{H}^{2}=\{x+iy\in\mathbb{C}\ :\ y>0\}

endowed with its standard metric of constant curvature
-1
,

ds^{2}=\frac{dx^{2}+dy^{2}}{y^{2}}.

The group of orientation-preserving isometries of
(\mathbb{H}^{2},ds)
is isomorphic to
\mathrm{PSL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
, which acts on the extended complex plane
\overline{\mathbb{C}}=\mathbb{C}\cup\{\infty\}
(and hence also on
\mathbb{H}^{2}
) by Möbius transformations

\gamma=\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{pmatrix}\in\mathrm{PSL}_{2}(\mathbb{R}),\quad z\in\overline{\mathbb{C}}\Longrightarrow\gamma(z)=\frac{az+b}{cz+d}.

•

“hyperbolic” if
|\tr(\gamma)|>2
, which implies that
\gamma
has two distinct fixed points on the boundary
\partial\mathbb{H}^{2}
,

•

“parabolic” if
|\tr(\gamma)|<2
, which implies that
\gamma
has precisely one fixed point on
\partial\mathbb{H}^{2}
, and

•

“elliptic” if
|\tr(\gamma)|=2
, which implies that
\gamma
has precisely one fixed point in the hyperbolic plane
\mathbb{H}^{2}
.

Discrete subgroups of
\mathrm{PSL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
are called “Fuchsian” groups. A Fuchsian group is “torsion-free” if it contains no elliptic elements. It is called “convex cocompact” if it is finitely generated and if it contains neither elliptic nor parabolic elements. This is equivalent to the “convex core” of
X=\Gamma\backslash\mathbb{H}^{2}
being compact.

2.2.
Selberg zeta function

Given a finitely generated Fuchsian group
\Gamma<\mathrm{PSL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
, the set of prime periodic geodesics on
X=\Gamma\backslash\mathbb{H}^{2}
is bijective to the set
[\Gamma]_{\mathsf{prim}}
of
\Gamma
-conjugacy classes of primitive hyperbolic elements in
\Gamma
. We denote by
\ell(\gamma)
the length of the geodesic corresponding to the conjugacy class
[\gamma]\in[\Gamma]_{\mathsf{prim}}
.

The “Selberg zeta function” is defined for
\mathrm{Re}(s)>\delta
by the infinite product

(10)

Z_{\Gamma}(s)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\prod_{k=0}^{\infty}\prod_{[\gamma]\in[\Gamma]_{\mathsf{prim}}}\left(1-e^{-(s+k)\ell(\gamma)}\right)

The Selberg zeta function has a meromorphic continuation to
s\in\mathbb{C}.
By Patterson–Perry
[
16
]
the zero set of
Z_{\Gamma}(s)
consists of the so-called “topological” zeros at
s=-k
for
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, and the set of resonances, repeated according to multiplicity. Therefore, any problem about resonances can be rephrased as a question about the distribution of the zeros of the Selberg zeta function.

By the product representation (
10
) and by uniqueness of analytic continuations, it follows that
Z_{\Gamma}(s)=Z_{\Gamma}(\overline{s})
for all
s\in\mathbb{C}
. Thus if
s_{0}
is a resonance for
X
, then so is its complex conjugate
\overline{s_{0}}
.

2.4.
Combinatorial notation for words

We will follow the combinatorial notation of Dyatlov–Zworski
[
5
]
for indexing elements in the free group
\Gamma
.

•

A word
a
in the alphabet
\mathcal{A}=\{1,\dots,2m\}
is a finite string
\textbf{a}=a_{1}\dots a_{n}
with
a_{1},\dots,a_{n}\in\mathcal{A}
. For technical reasons, we also introduce the empty word
\emptyset
, a string of length zero.

•

A word
\textbf{a}=a_{1}\dots a_{n}
is said to be “reduced” if
a_{j}\neq\overline{a_{j+1}}
for all
j=1,\dots,n-1
. For all
n\in\mathbb{N}
denote by
\mathcal{W}_{n}
the set of (reduced) words of length
n
:

\mathcal{W}_{n}=\left\{a_{1}\cdots a_{n}:\text{$a_{1},\dots,a_{n}\in\mathcal{A}$ s.t. $a_{j}\neq\overline{a_{j+1}}$ for all $j=1,\dots,n-1$}\right\}.

Moreover, put
\mathcal{W}_{0}=\{\emptyset\}
where
\emptyset
is the empty word.

•

Let
\mathcal{W}=\bigsqcup_{n\geqslant 0}\mathcal{W}_{n}
be the set of all reduced words and write
|\textbf{a}|=n
if
\textbf{a}\in\mathcal{W}_{n}
. In other words,
|\textbf{a}|
is the reduced word length of
a
. Given
m\in\mathbb{N}
let
\mathcal{W}_{\geqslant m}=\bigsqcup_{n\geqslant m}\mathcal{W}_{n}
the set of all reduced words whose length is at least
m
, and let
\mathcal{W}^{\circ}=\mathcal{W}_{\geqslant 1}
be the set of all non-empty reduced words.

•

Given a word
\textbf{a}=a_{1}\cdots a_{n}\in\mathcal{W}^{\circ}
write
\textbf{a}^{\prime}=a_{1}\cdots a_{n-1}\in\mathcal{W}
. Note that
\mathcal{W}
is a tree with root
\emptyset
and
\textbf{a}^{\prime}
is the parent of
\textbf{a}.

•

For
\textbf{a}=a_{1}\cdots a_{n}\in\mathcal{W}
and
\textbf{b}=b_{1}\cdots b_{m}\in\mathcal{W}
write
\textbf{a}\to\textbf{b}
if either
\textbf{a}=\emptyset
, or
\textbf{b}=\emptyset
, or
a_{n}\neq\overline{b_{1}}
. Note that in this case,
\textbf{a}\textbf{b}\in\mathcal{W}
, that is, if
\textbf{a}\to\textbf{b}
, then the concatenation
a
b
is also a reduced word.

•

Given a word
\textbf{a}=a_{1}\cdots a_{n}
let
\overline{\textbf{a}}=\overline{a_{1}}\cdots\overline{a_{n}}
be its “mirror” word.

•

Write
\textbf{a}\prec\textbf{b}
if
a
is a prefix
b
, that is, if
\textbf{b}=\textbf{ac}
for some
\textbf{c}\in\mathcal{W}
.

•

We have the one-to-one correspondence

\textbf{a}=a_{1}\cdots a_{n}\in\mathcal{W}\mapsto\gamma_{\textbf{a}}=\gamma_{a_{1}}\cdots\gamma_{a_{n}}\in\Gamma.

Moreover, we have
\gamma_{\textbf{ab}}=\gamma_{\textbf{a}}\gamma_{\textbf{b}}
,
\gamma_{\textbf{a}}^{-1}=\gamma_{\overline{\textbf{a}}}
, and
\gamma_{\textbf{a}}=\id
if and only if
\textbf{a}=\emptyset.

•

For
\textbf{a}=a_{1}\cdots a_{n}\in\mathcal{W}^{\circ}
we define the disk

D_{\textbf{a}}:=\gamma_{\textbf{a}^{\prime}}(D_{a_{n}}).

If
\textbf{a}\prec\textbf{b}
then
D_{\textbf{a}}\subset D_{\textbf{b}}
. On the other hand, if
\textbf{a}\nprec\textbf{b}
and
\textbf{b}\nprec\textbf{a}
then
D_{\textbf{a}}\cap D_{\textbf{b}}=\emptyset
. We define the interval

(11)

I_{\textbf{a}}:=D_{\textbf{a}}\cap\mathbb{R}

and we denote by
|I_{\textbf{a}}|
its length which is equal to the diameter of
D_{\textbf{a}}.

•

Denote

D=\bigsqcup_{a\in\mathcal{A}}D_{a}
and
I=\bigsqcup_{a\in\mathcal{A}}I_{a}
.

•

In the above notation, the limit set of
\Gamma
may be re-expressed as follows:

\Lambda=\bigcap_{n\geqslant 1}\bigsqcup_{\textbf{a}\in\mathcal{W}_{n}}I_{\textbf{a}}\subset\mathbb{R}.

2.5.
The standard transfer operator

Given any open set
\Omega\subset\mathbb{C}
let
H^{2}(\Omega)
be the “Bergman space” over
\Omega
, which is defined as the Hilbert space of square-integrable, holomorphic functions on
\Omega
:

(12)

H^{2}(\Omega)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\left\{\text{$f\colon\Omega\to\mathbb{C}$ holomorphic}\left|\;\left\|f\right\|<\infty\right.\right\},

with
L^{2}
-norm given by

\|f\|^{2}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\int_{\Omega}\|f(z)\|_{V}^{2}\dvol(z).

Here
\vol
denotes the Lebesgue measure on the complex plane.

Now consider the Bergman space
H^{2}(D)
over the union
D=\bigsqcup_{a\in\mathcal{A}}D_{a}
of the Schottky disks in §
2.3
. On this space, we define the holomorphic family of operators, parametrized by
s\in\mathbb{C}
,

(13)

\mathcal{L}_{s}\colon H^{2}(D)\to H^{2}(D)

by the formula

(14)

\mathcal{L}_{s}f(z)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a\in\mathcal{A}\\
a\to b\end{subarray}}^{2m}\gamma_{a}^{\prime}(z)^{s}f(\gamma_{a}(z))\ \mathrm{if}\ z\in D_{b}.

The derivative satisfies
\gamma_{a}^{\prime}(z)>0
for all
z\in I_{b}
, so the complex power
\gamma_{a}^{\prime}(z)^{s}
is uniquely defined and holomorphic for
z\in D_{b}
and
s\in\mathbb{C}.
More concretely, we define

\gamma_{a}^{\prime}(z)^{s}=\exp(s\mathbb{L}(\gamma_{a}^{\prime}(z))),

where

(15)

\mathbb{L}(z)=\log|z|+\mathrm{arg}(z),

with

\mathrm{arg}\colon\mathbb{C}\smallsetminus(-\infty,0]\to(-\pi,\pi)

being the principal value of the argument.

The operator in (
14
) is the well-known “transfer operator” associated to
\Gamma
which can be found for instance in Borthwick’s book
[
1
, Chapter 15]
. The following result relates the transfer operator to the Selberg zeta function:

Proposition 2.1

(Fredholm determinant identity)
.

For every
s\in\mathbb{C}
the operator (
13
) is trace class and we have the identity

(16)

Z_{\Gamma}(s)=\det(1-\mathcal{L}_{s}).

Proposition
2.1
characterizes resonances in terms of 1-eigenfunctions of the transfer operator. Indeed, using standard properties of the Fredholm determinant, this proposition says that
s\in\mathbb{C}
is a resonance for
X=\Gamma\backslash\mathbb{H}^{2}
if and only if there exists a non-zero
f\in H^{2}(D)
such that
f=\mathcal{L}_{s}f.

2.6.
Partitions and refined transfer operators

In the present paper, we will work with
refined
transfer operators. These are generalizations of the standard transfer operator
\mathcal{L}_{s}
introduced by Dyatlov–Zworski
[
5
]
. Let us now recall their definition.

Given a finite subset
Z\subset\mathcal{W}
, put
Z^{\prime}=\{\textbf{a}^{\prime}:\textbf{a}\in Z\}
and
\overline{Z}=\{\overline{\textbf{a}}:\textbf{a}\in Z\},
and for
s\in\mathbb{C}
define the family of operators

(17)

\mathcal{L}_{Z,s}\colon H^{2}(D)\to H^{2}(D)

by the formula

(18)

\mathcal{L}_{Z,s}f(z)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\textbf{a}\in(\overline{Z})^{\prime}\\
\textbf{a}\to b\end{subarray}}\gamma_{a}^{\prime}(z)^{s}f(\gamma_{a}(z))\ \mathrm{if}\ z\in D_{b}.

Note that
\mathcal{L}_{Z,s}
reduces to the standard transfer operator
\mathcal{L}_{s}
if
Z
is taken to be
\mathcal{W}_{2}
, the set of reduced words of length two.

A finite set
Z\subset\mathcal{W}^{\circ}
is called a “partition” if there exists
N\in\mathbb{N}
such that for every reduced word
\textbf{a}\in\mathcal{W}
with
|\textbf{a}|\geqslant N
, there exists a unique
\textbf{b}\in Z
such that
\textbf{b}\prec\textbf{a}
. In terms of the limit set, a finite set
Z\in\mathcal{W}^{\circ}
is a partition if we have the disjoint union

\Lambda=\bigsqcup_{\textbf{b}\in Z}(I_{\textbf{b}}\cap\Lambda).

Trivial examples of partitions are the sets
\mathcal{W}_{n}
for
n\in\mathbb{N}
, the set of reduced words of length
n
, in which case we have
\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{W}_{n},s}=\mathcal{L}_{s}^{n-1}.

The fundamental fact about partitions is the following result of Dyatlov–Zworski
[
5
]
, which says that 1-eigenfunctions of
\mathcal{L}_{s}
must also be 1-eigenfunctions of
\mathcal{L}_{Z,s}
, provided
Z
is a partition.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
Z
be a finite subset of
\mathcal{W}_{\geqslant 2}=\bigsqcup_{n\geqslant 2}\mathcal{W}_{n}
. If
Z
is a partition then for every
f\in H^{2}(D)
the following holds true:

\mathcal{L}_{s}f=f\Longrightarrow\mathcal{L}_{Z,s}f=f.

Combining this with Proposition
2.1
and the remark thereafter, we obtain that for any given partition
Z\subset\mathcal{W}^{\circ}
, resonances for
X=\Gamma\backslash\mathbb{H}^{2}
are zeros of the Fredholm determinant
\det(1-\mathcal{L}_{Z,s}).
More generally, given a finite collection of partitions
Z_{1},Z_{2},\dots,Z_{k}\subset\mathcal{W}_{\geqslant 2}
, resonances for
X
occur as zeros of the function

(19)

f(s)=\det(1-\mathcal{L}_{Z_{1},s}\circ\cdots\circ\mathcal{L}_{Z_{k},s}).

The most important family of partitions is defined as follows: given a parameter
\tau>0
, called the “resolution parameter”, set

Z(\tau)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\{\textbf{a}\in\mathcal{W}^{\circ}:|I_{\textbf{a}}|\leqslant\tau<|I_{\textbf{a}^{\prime}}|\}.

The set
Z(\tau)
is a partition by virtue of the fact that the interval length
|I_{\textbf{a}}|
tends to zero as
|\textbf{a}|\to\infty
. This in turn follows from the definition of the intervals
I_{\textbf{a}}
in (
11
) and from the uniform contraction property in Lemma
2.3
below.

Finally, we define the
\tau
-refined transfer operator
by

(20)

\mathcal{L}_{\tau,s}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\mathcal{L}_{Z(\tau),s}.

We can write down the following formula for
\mathcal{L}_{\tau,s}
for every
f\in H^{2}(D)
and
b\in\mathcal{A}
:

(21)

\mathcal{L}_{\tau,s}f(z)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\textbf{a}\in Y(\tau)\\
\textbf{a}\to b\end{subarray}}\gamma_{a}^{\prime}(z)^{s}f(\gamma_{a}(z))\ \mathrm{if}\ z\in D_{b},

where

(22)

Y(\tau)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\overline{Z(\tau)}^{\prime}.

Note that the operator (
20
) is well-defined if and only if
Y(\tau)\subset W^{\circ}
, or equivalently,
Z(\tau)\subset W_{\geqslant 2}
. This condition is satisfied if the resolution parameter
\tau>0
is small enough.

The main reason for using this special family of operators is that we can control the size of
Y(\tau)
size as well as the size of the derivatives
\gamma_{\textbf{a}}
with
\textbf{a}\in Y(\tau)
, see Lemma
2.4
below. This is what enables us to obtain explicit exponents in Theorem
1.1
.

2.7.
Bounds for derivatives

Now we record some basic and well-known estimates for the derivatives of elements of
\Gamma
. Following Magee–Naud
[
14
]
, we use the following notation: for every
a\in\mathcal{A}
we pick a point
o_{a}\in D_{a}
and for any
\textbf{a}\in\mathcal{W}^{\circ}
we set

o_{\textbf{a}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}o_{a}

where
a\in\mathcal{A}
is chosen such that
\textbf{a}\to a
.

Throughout the rest of this paper these points will remain fixed.

Moreover, put

\Upsilon_{\textbf{a}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}|\gamma_{\textbf{a}}^{\prime}(o_{\textbf{a}})|.

The following basic estimates are due to Naud
[
13
]
and Magee–Naud
[
14
]
:

Lemma 2.3

(Basic distortion estimates)
.

The following estimates hold true with implied constants depending only on
\Gamma
:

(i)

Uniform contraction: There are constants
0<\theta_{1}<\theta_{2}<1
and
C>0
such that for all
b\in\mathcal{A}
and for all
\textbf{a}\in\mathcal{W}
with
\textbf{a}\to b
and
z\in D_{b}
we have

C^{-1}\theta_{1}^{|\textbf{a}|}\leqslant|\gamma_{\textbf{a}}^{\prime}(z)|\leqslant C\theta_{2}^{|\textbf{a}|}.

(ii)

Bounded distortion 1: For all
b\in\mathcal{A}
and for all
\textbf{a}\in\mathcal{W}
with
\textbf{a}\to b
and
z_{1},z_{2}\in D_{b}
we have

C^{-1}\leqslant\frac{|\gamma_{\textbf{a}}^{\prime}(z_{1})|}{|\gamma_{\textbf{a}}^{\prime}(z_{2})|}\leqslant C.

(iii)

Bounded distortion 2: There exists a constant
C>0
such that for all
b_{1},b_{2}\in\mathcal{A}
, all
z_{1}\in D_{b_{1}}
and all
z_{2}\in D_{b_{2}}
, and all
\textbf{a}\in\mathcal{W}^{\circ}
with
\textbf{a}\to b_{1},b_{2}
we have

\left|\frac{\gamma^{\prime}_{\textbf{a}}(z_{1})}{\gamma^{\prime}_{\textbf{a}}(z_{2})}\right|\leqslant C.

(iv)

For all
b\in\mathcal{A}
and
z\in D_{b}
with
\textbf{a}\to b
we have
|\gamma^{\prime}_{\textbf{a}}(z)|\asymp\Upsilon_{\textbf{a}}
.

(v)

For all
\textbf{a}\in\mathcal{W}^{\circ}
we have
\Upsilon_{\textbf{a}}\asymp\Upsilon_{\textbf{a}}
.

(vi)

For all
\textbf{a}\in\mathcal{W}^{\circ}
we have
\Upsilon_{\overline{\textbf{a}}}\asymp\Upsilon_{\textbf{a}}
.

(vii)

For all
\textbf{a},\textbf{b}\in\mathcal{W}^{\circ}
with
\textbf{a}\to\textbf{b}
we have
\Upsilon_{\textbf{a}\textbf{b}}\asymp\Upsilon_{\textbf{a}}\Upsilon_{\textbf{b}}.

The following following estimates concerning
Z(\tau)
and
Y(\tau)
are also crucial:

Lemma 2.4

(Estimates for
Z(\tau)
and
Y(\tau)
)
.

For all
\tau>0
small enough the following estimates hold true with implied constants depending only on
\Gamma
:

(i)

For all
\textbf{a}\in Z(\tau)
we have
\Upsilon_{\textbf{a}}\asymp\tau.

(ii)

For all
\textbf{a}\in Y(\tau)
we have
\Upsilon_{\textbf{a}}\asymp\tau.

(iii)

|Y(\tau)|\asymp|Z(\tau)|\asymp\tau^{-\delta}.

The estimates for
Z(\tau)
in Lemma
2.4
can be found in
[
2
]
. Lemma
2.4
can also be deduced from the definitions of the sets
Z(\tau)
and
Y(\tau)
and Lemma
2.3
above.

2.8.
Refined function spaces

In this paper, we adopt the approach of Guillopé–Lin-Zworski
[
8
]
, which we present here. Roughly speaking, the idea is to let the transfer operators act on “refined” function spaces. To define these spaces, let
h>0
be a (small) parameter and consider the
complex
h
-neighbourhood
of
\Lambda
:

\Omega(h)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\Lambda+D_{\mathbb{C}}(0,h),\quad D_{\mathbb{C}}(0,h)=\{z\in\mathbb{C}:|z|<h\}.

Note that for all
h>0
small enough we have
\Omega(h)\subset D.
For each
b\in\mathcal{A}
put

\Omega_{b}(h)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\Omega(h)\cap D_{b}.

Given a partition
Z\subset\mathcal{W}_{\geqslant 2}
, we let the generalized transfer operator
\mathcal{L}_{\tau,s}
act on the functional space
H^{2}(\Omega(h))
. This gives a family of operators

(23)

\mathcal{L}_{Z,s}\colon H^{2}(\Omega(h))\to H^{2}(\Omega(h)),

defined for all functions
f\in H^{2}(\Omega(h))
by the formula

(24)

\mathcal{L}_{Z,s}f(z)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\textbf{a}\in\overline{Z}^{\prime}\\
\textbf{a}\to b\end{subarray}}\gamma^{\prime}_{\textbf{a}}(z)^{s}f(\gamma_{\textbf{a}}(z))\quad\text{if $z\in\Omega_{b}(h)$}.

By the definition of
\Omega(h)
we have

z\in\Omega(h)\Longrightarrow|\mathrm{Im}(z)|\leqslant h.

Using the definition of the complex powers in (
15
), this implies that for all
b\in\mathcal{A}
and
\textbf{a}\in\mathcal{W}^{\circ}
with
\textbf{a}\to b
we have for all
s=\sigma+it
:

(25)

z\in\Omega_{b}(h)\Longrightarrow|\gamma_{\textbf{a}}^{\prime}(z)^{s}|\ll_{\sigma}\Upsilon_{\textbf{a}}^{\sigma}e^{Ch|t|}.

Here the constant
C>0
depends only on
\Gamma
while the implied constant depends only on
\Gamma
and
\sigma.
It is clear that in order to optimize this bound one may choose
h\approx|t|^{-1}
so that
e^{Ch|t|}=O(1)
. In other words,
h
can be chosen to eliminate exponential terms in
|\mathrm{Im}(s)|
. This is the main reason for working with
H^{2}(\Omega(h))
.

It remains to show that (
23
) is well-defined. The next lemma shows that the operator (
23
) is well-defined and trace class, provided the parameter
h>0
is chosen small enough.

Lemma 2.5

(Contraction in
\Omega(h)
)
.

There exist constants
h_{0}>0
and
\eta\in(0,1)
, depending only on
\Gamma
, such that for all
h\in(0,h_{0})
,
b\in\mathcal{A}
and
\textbf{a}\in\mathcal{W}^{\circ}
with
\mathbf{a}\to b
,

\gamma_{\textbf{a}}(\Omega_{b}(h))\subset\Omega(\eta^{|\textbf{a}|}h).

In particular,

\mathrm{dist}(\gamma_{\textbf{a}}(z),\partial\Omega(h))>(1-\eta^{|\textbf{a}|})h\geqslant(1-\eta)h.

Proof.

Note that
\Omega(h)
and
\Omega_{b}(h)
may be re-expressed as

\Omega(h)=\{z\in\mathbb{C}:\text{there exists some $p\in\Lambda$ such that $|z-p|<h$}\},

and

\Omega_{b}(h)=\{z\in\mathbb{C}:\text{there exists some $p\in\Lambda\cap D_{b}$ such that $|z-p|<h$}\}.

Let
z\in\Omega_{b}(h)
. Then, by definition, there exists some
p\in\Lambda\cap D_{b}
such that
|z-p|\leqslant h
. Now let
a\in\mathcal{A}
be such that
a\to b
. By the uniform contraction property in Lemma
2.3
there exists
\eta\in(0,1)
such that

|\gamma_{a}(z)-\gamma_{a}(p)|\leqslant\eta|z-p|\leqslant\eta h.

Applying this estimate repeatedly, it follows that for all reduced words
\textbf{a}\in\mathcal{W}^{\circ}
with
\textbf{a}\to b
we have

(26)

|\gamma_{\textbf{a}}(z)-\gamma_{\textbf{a}}(p)|\leqslant\eta^{|\textbf{a}|}h.

But by
\Gamma
-invariance of
\Lambda
it follows that
\gamma_{\textbf{a}}(p)\in\Lambda
, so we obtain from (
26
) that
\gamma_{\textbf{a}}(z)\in\Omega(\eta^{|\textbf{a}|}h),
as claimed.
∎

We remark that for any finite collection of partitions
Z_{1},Z_{2},\dots,Z_{k}\subset\mathcal{W}_{\geqslant 2}
, Lemma
2.5
shows that for
h>0
small enough, the operator

\mathcal{L}_{Z_{1},s}\circ\cdots\circ\mathcal{L}_{Z_{k},s}\colon H^{2}(\Omega(h))\to H^{2}(\Omega(h))

is well-defined and trace-class. Moreover, the Fredholm determinant

(27)

\det(1-\mathcal{L}_{Z_{1},s}\circ\cdots\circ\mathcal{L}_{Z_{k},s})

is independent of the choice of the parameter
h
. Indeed, carefully applying the Lefschetz fixed point formula (see
[
1
, Lemma 15.9]
) shows that the traces of powers of
\mathcal{L}_{Z_{1},s}\circ\cdots\circ\mathcal{L}_{Z_{k},s}
(and hence (
27
)) are independent of
h.

Also crucial is the following upper bound for the Lebesgue measure of
\Omega(h)
.

Lemma 2.6

(Volume bound)
.

There exists a constant
C>0
such that for all
h>0
sufficiently small we have

\mathrm{vol}(\Omega(h))\leqslant Ch^{2-\delta}

Proof.

Consider the real
h
-neighbourhood of the limit set
\Lambda(h)=\Lambda+[-h,h]
. Let
I_{l}(h)
with
l=1,\dots,N(h)
denote the connected components of
\Lambda(h)
. It is well-known from
[
8
]
(see also
[
1
, Lemma 15.14]
) that there exists some constant
C=C(\Gamma)>0
such that for all
h>0
small enough the following hold true:

•

the diameter of each interval
I_{l}(h)
is less than
Ch
, and

•

the number of connected components
N(h)
is bounded by
N(h)\leqslant Ch^{-\delta}.

Now note that

\Omega(h)\subseteq\bigsqcup_{l=1}^{N(h)}\left(I_{l}(h)+i[-h,h]\right),

whence

\mathrm{vol}(\Omega(h))\leqslant\sum_{l=1}^{N(h)}\mathrm{vol}(I_{l}(h)+i[-h,h])=h\sum_{l=1}^{N(h)}|I_{l}(h)|,

where
|I_{l}(h)|
is the diameter of
I_{l}(h)
. From the previously mentioned facts it follows that the right hand side is at most
O(h^{2-\delta})
, as claimed.
∎

2.9.
Properties of Bergman kernels

We now recall some basic properties of the Bergman kernels which are crucial in the proof of Theorem
1.1
. We refer the reader to
[
11
]
for a more in-depth account of the material given here.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{C}
be a non-empty bounded (not necessarily connected) open set and let
H^{2}(\Omega)
be the Bergman space over
\Omega
. Since
H^{2}(\Omega)
is a closed subspace of
L^{2}(\Omega)
, it is a separable Hilbert space. Let
(\varphi_{n})
be a orthonormal basis of
H^{2}
. Given any function
f\in H^{2}(\Omega)
, we can expand it as

f=\sum_{n}c_{n}(f)\varphi_{n},\quad c_{n}(f)=\int_{\Omega}f(w)\overline{\varphi_{n}(w)}\dvol(w),

where the sum converges absolutely and uniformly on compact sets with respect to the
L^{2}
-norm. By the Riesz representation theorem there exists some unique
\overline{B_{\Omega}(z,\cdot)}\in H^{2}(\Omega)
, called the “Bergman reproducing kernel”, such that

(28)

f(z)=\int_{\Omega}B(z,w)f(w)\dvol(w).

For any fixed
z\in\Omega
, we can expand
\overline{B(z,w)}
as follows:

(29)

\overline{B(z,w)}=\sum_{n}c_{n}\varphi_{n}(w),

where

(30)

c_{n}=\int_{\Omega}\overline{B(z,w)}\,\overline{\varphi_{n}(w)}\dvol(w).

Inserting (
30
) and (
29
) into (
28
) and using the uniqueness of the Bergman kernel reveals that

(31)

B(z,w)=\sum_{n}\varphi_{n}(z)\overline{\varphi_{n}(w)},

where the series is uniformly convergent on compact subsets of
\Omega\times\Omega.

Lemma 2.7

(Basic properties of the Bergman kernel)
.

The following holds true:

(i)

If
z
and
w
lie in different components of
\Omega
then
B_{\Omega}(z,w)=0.

(ii)

For all
z,w
we have
|B_{\Omega}(z,w)|^{2}\leqslant B_{\Omega}(z,z)B_{\Omega}(w,w).

(iii)

Variational Formula:
B_{\Omega}(z,z)=\sup\{|f(z)|^{2}:f\in H^{2}(\Omega),\|f\|=1\}.

(iv)

Comparison Formula: If
z\in\Omega_{1}\subset\Omega_{2}
then
B_{\Omega_{2}}(z,z)\leqslant B_{\Omega_{1}}(z,z).

(v)

For the disk
D(z_{0},r)=\{|z-z_{0}|<r\}
we have the explicit formula

B_{D(z_{0},r)}(z,w)=\frac{2r^{2}}{\pi\left(r^{2}-(z-z_{0})(\overline{w}-\overline{w}_{0})\right)^{2}}.

Proof.

Properties (
i
)–(
iv
) are easy to deduce from the definition of the Bergman kernel, from the formula (
31
), and the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality. To prove (
v
), we use (
31
) with the following explicit orthonormal basis for
H^{2}(D(z_{0},r))
:

\varphi_{n}(z)=\sqrt{\frac{n+1}{\pi r^{2}}}\left(\frac{z-z_{0}}{r}\right)^{n},\quad n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}.

∎

Lemma 2.8

(Upper bound for the Bergman kernel)
.

For all
z\in\Omega
set

\mathrm{dist}(z,\partial\Omega)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\inf_{z^{\prime}\in\partial\Omega}|z-z^{\prime}|.

Then for all
z,w\in\Omega
we have

|B_{\Omega}(z,w)|\leqslant\frac{1}{\pi\mathrm{dist}(z,\partial\Omega)\mathrm{dist}(w,\partial\Omega)}.

Proof.

Note that
D(z,r_{z})\subset\Omega
and
D(w,r_{w})\subset\Omega
for any
r_{z}<\mathrm{dist}(z,\partial\Omega)
and
r_{w}<\mathrm{dist}(w,\partial\Omega)
, so we can use Parts (
ii
), (
iv
), and (
v
) of Lemma
2.7
to obtain

|B_{\Omega}(z,w)|^{2}\leqslant B_{\Omega}(z,z)B_{\Omega}(w,w)\leqslant B_{D(z,r_{z})}(z,z)B_{D(w,r_{w})}(w,w)\leqslant\frac{1}{\pi r_{z}^{2}}\cdot\frac{1}{\pi r_{w}^{2}}.

This gives

|B_{\Omega}(z,w)|\leqslant\frac{1}{\pi r_{z}r_{w}}.

Letting
r_{z}\nearrow\mathrm{dist}(z,\partial\Omega)
and
r_{w}\nearrow\mathrm{dist}(w,\partial\Omega)
finishes the proof.
∎

3.
Proof of Main Theorem

This chapter is devoted to the proof of Theorem
1.1
and is divided into several sections. Recall that
X=\Gamma\backslash\mathbb{H}^{2}
is a convex co-compact hyperbolic quotient, that we fix a Schottky representation for
\Gamma
as in §
2.3
, and that we use the notations introduced in §
2
.

3.1.
A separation lemma

In this section, we prove the following “separation” lemma which is an adaptation of a result of Jakobson–Naud
[
10
, Lemma 4.4]
. This result is a consequence of the total discontinuity of the limit set
\Lambda.

Lemma 3.1

(Separation Lemma)
.

There exist constants
c>0
and
h_{0}>0
, depending only on
\Gamma
, such that for all
h\in(0,h_{0})
and
\tau\in(0,ch)
the following holds true: for all
b\in\mathcal{A}
,
\textbf{a},\textbf{b}\in Y(\tau)
with
\textbf{a},\textbf{b}\to b
, and
z_{1},z_{2}\in D_{b}
we have

\gamma_{\textbf{a}}(z_{1})
and
\gamma_{\textbf{b}}(z_{2})
belong to the same connected component of
\Omega(h)\Longrightarrow\textbf{a}=\textbf{b}
.

Proof.

Fix
b\in\mathcal{A}
,
\textbf{a},\textbf{b}\in Y(\tau)
with
\textbf{a},\textbf{b}\to b
, and
z_{1},z_{2}\in D_{b}
. Suppose that
\textbf{a}\neq\textbf{b}
and that
\gamma_{\textbf{a}}(z_{1})
and
\gamma_{\textbf{b}}(z_{2})
lie in the same component of
\Omega(h).
Suppose further that
\tau<ch
. We seek to obtain a contradiction for some sufficiently small
c=c(\Gamma)>0
. By
[
1
, Lemma 15.14]
the diameter of each connected component of
\Omega(h)
is less that
Ch
, so we get

(32)

|\gamma_{\textbf{a}}(z_{1})-\gamma_{\textbf{b}}(z_{2})|\leqslant Ch.

If the first letter of
a
and
b
are different, then
\gamma_{\textbf{a}}(z_{1})
and
\gamma_{\textbf{b}}(z_{2})
lie in different Schottky disks. This implies that

|\gamma_{\textbf{a}}(z_{1})-\gamma_{\textbf{b}}(z_{2})|\geqslant K,

where
K>0
denotes the minimal distance between two Schottky disks, so we get a contradiction for
h
small enough. Thus we may assume that
a
and
b
have a common prefix, so we write
\textbf{a}=\textbf{c}\textbf{a}_{1}
and
\textbf{b}=\textbf{c}\textbf{b}_{1}
for some non-empty word
\textbf{c}\in\mathcal{W}^{\circ}
. We assume that
|\textbf{c}|
is maximal with this property, that is, we assume that the first letters of
\textbf{a}_{1}
and
\textbf{b}_{1}
are different, in which case

(33)

|\gamma_{\textbf{a}_{1}}(z_{1})-\gamma_{\textbf{b}_{1}}(z_{2})|\geqslant K.

Now we use the following identity which in valid for all
\gamma\in\mathrm{PSL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
and all
w_{1},w_{2}\in\mathbb{C}
:

|\gamma(w_{1})-\gamma(w_{2})|^{2}=|\gamma^{\prime}(w_{1})||\gamma^{\prime}(w_{2})||w_{1}-w_{2}|^{2}.

Applying this to
\gamma=\gamma_{\textbf{c}}
,
w_{1}=\gamma_{\textbf{a}_{1}}(z)
, and
w_{2}=\gamma_{\textbf{a}_{1}}(z)
yields

\displaystyle|\gamma_{\textbf{a}}(z_{1})-\gamma_{\textbf{b}}(z_{2})|^{2}

\displaystyle=|\gamma_{\textbf{c}}(\gamma_{\textbf{a}_{1}}(z))-\gamma_{\textbf{c}}(\gamma_{\textbf{b}_{1}}(z))|^{2}

\displaystyle=|\gamma_{\textbf{c}}^{\prime}(\gamma_{\textbf{a}_{1}}(z))||\gamma_{\textbf{c}}^{\prime}(\gamma_{\textbf{b}_{1}}(z))||\gamma_{\textbf{a}_{1}}(z)-\gamma_{\textbf{b}_{1}}(z)|^{2},

or equivalently,

(34)

|\gamma_{\textbf{a}}(z_{1})-\gamma_{\textbf{b}}(z_{2})|=|\gamma_{\textbf{c}}^{\prime}(\gamma_{\textbf{a}_{1}}(z))|^{1/2}|\gamma_{\textbf{c}}^{\prime}(\gamma_{\textbf{b}_{1}}(z))|^{1/2}|\gamma_{\textbf{a}_{1}}(z)-\gamma_{\textbf{b}_{1}}(z)|.

Note that
\gamma_{\textbf{a}_{1}}^{\prime}(z_{1})
is bounded from above by some constant that depends solely on
\Gamma.
Hence, using the chain rule and the fact that
\textbf{a}\in Y(\tau)
, it follows from Lemma
2.4
that

\gamma_{\textbf{c}}^{\prime}(\gamma_{\textbf{a}_{1}}(z_{1}))=\frac{\gamma_{\textbf{a}}^{\prime}(z_{1})}{\gamma_{\textbf{a}_{1}}^{\prime}(z_{1})}\geqslant c_{0}\tau

for some constant
c_{0}=c_{0}(\Gamma)>0
. Similarly, we obtain

\gamma_{\textbf{c}}^{\prime}(\gamma_{\textbf{b}_{1}}(z_{2}))\geqslant c_{0}\tau.

Inserting this into (
34
) and using (
33
) yields

|\gamma_{\textbf{a}}(z_{1})-\gamma_{\textbf{b}}(z_{2})|\geqslant c_{0}\tau|\gamma_{\textbf{a}_{1}}(z_{1})-\gamma_{\textbf{b}_{1}}(z_{2})|\geqslant c_{0}\tau K.

When combined with (
32
), this forces
c_{0}K\tau\leqslant Ch,
or equivalently
h\geqslant\frac{c_{0}K}{C}\tau.
We obtain the desired contradiction by taking
c=\frac{c_{0}K}{2C}
.
∎

3.2.
The phase and its derivative

Given words
\textbf{a},\textbf{b}\in\mathcal{W}^{\circ}
and
z\in D
, define the “phase” by

(35)

\Phi_{\textbf{a},\textbf{b}}(z)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\mathbb{L}(\gamma_{\textbf{a}}^{\prime}(z))-\mathbb{L}(\gamma_{\textbf{b}}^{\prime}(z)),

where
\mathbb{L}
is the complex logarithm defined in (
15
), so that for all
s=\sigma+it
,

\gamma_{\textbf{a}}^{\prime}(z)^{s}\overline{\gamma_{\textbf{b}}^{\prime}(z)^{s}}=\gamma_{\textbf{a}}^{\prime}(z)^{\sigma}\overline{\gamma_{\textbf{b}}^{\prime}(z)}^{\sigma}e^{it\Phi_{\textbf{a},\textbf{b}}(z)}.

Moreover, define the quantity

(36)

\mathcal{D}_{\textbf{a},\textbf{b}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\left(\frac{\Upsilon_{\textbf{a}}\Upsilon_{\textbf{b}}}{\Upsilon_{\textbf{a}\overline{\textbf{b}}}}\right)^{1/2}.

It turns out that the derivatives of
\Phi_{\textbf{a},\textbf{b}}(z)
are controlled by
\mathcal{D}_{\textbf{a},\textbf{b}}
.

Proposition 3.2

(Phase derivatives)
.

For all
b\in\mathcal{A}
and
\textbf{a},\textbf{b}\in\mathcal{W}^{\circ}
with
\textbf{a},\textbf{b}\to b
and
\textbf{a}\neq\textbf{b}
the following estimates hold true with implied constants depending only on
\Gamma
:

(i)

For all
z\in D_{b}
we have

|\Phi_{\textbf{a},\textbf{b}}^{\prime}(z)|\asymp\mathcal{D}_{\textbf{a},\textbf{b}}.

(ii)

There exists some
C=C(\Gamma)>0
such that for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
the
n
-th derivative satisfies

|\Phi_{\textbf{a},\textbf{b}}^{(n)}(z)|\leqslant n!C^{n}\mathcal{D}_{\textbf{a},\textbf{b}}.

(iii)

There exists some
r_{0}=r_{0}(\Gamma)>0
such that for any two points
z,z^{\prime}\in D_{b}
with
|z^{\prime}-z|<r_{0}
we have

|\Phi_{\textbf{a},\textbf{b}}(z)-\Phi_{\textbf{a},\textbf{b}}(z^{\prime})|\gg\mathcal{D}_{\textbf{a},\textbf{b}}|z^{\prime}-z|.

We need the following lemma:

Lemma 3.3
.

For every
\textbf{a}\in\mathcal{W}^{\circ}
write
\gamma_{\textbf{a}}=\begin{pmatrix}a_{\textbf{a}}&b_{\textbf{a}}\\
c_{\textbf{a}}&d_{\textbf{a}}\end{pmatrix}\in\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
. Then we have

|c_{\textbf{a}}|\asymp\Upsilon_{\textbf{a}}^{-1/2}

with implied constants depending only on
\Gamma.

Proof.

For all
b\in\mathcal{A}
,
\textbf{a}\in\mathcal{W}^{\circ}
with
\mathbf{a}\to b
, and
z\in D_{b}
we have

\gamma^{\prime}_{\textbf{a}}(z)=\frac{1}{(c_{\textbf{a}}z+d_{\textbf{a}})^{2}}=\frac{1}{c_{\textbf{a}}^{2}(z-x_{\textbf{a}})^{2}},

where
x_{\textbf{a}}=-d_{\textbf{a}}/c_{\textbf{a}}.
Write
\textbf{a}=a_{1}\cdots a_{n}
and note that
\mathbf{a}\to b
is equivalent to
\overline{a}_{n}\neq b
. Moreover, observe that

x_{\textbf{a}}=\gamma_{\textbf{a}}^{-1}(\infty)=\gamma_{\overline{\textbf{a}}}(\infty)\in D_{\overline{a}_{n}},

which implies that for all
z\in D_{b}
the quantity
|z-x_{\textbf{a}}|
is bounded from below and from above by some positive constants depending only on
\Gamma
. Therefore, we have

|\gamma^{\prime}_{\textbf{a}}(z)|\asymp\frac{1}{c_{\textbf{a}}^{2}}.

Combining this with Part (
iv
) of Lemma
2.3
, which asserts that
\Upsilon_{\textbf{a}}\asymp|\gamma^{\prime}_{\textbf{a}}(z)|
, proves the claim.
∎

Proof of Proposition
3.2
.

Throughout this proof fix
b\in\mathcal{A}
,
z\in D_{b}
, and
\textbf{a},\textbf{b}\in\mathcal{W}^{\circ}
such that
\textbf{a},\textbf{b}\to b
and
\textbf{a}\neq\textbf{b}
. A simple calculation shows that

(37)

\Phi_{\textbf{a},\textbf{b}}^{\prime}(z)=2\left(\frac{c_{\textbf{a}}}{c_{\textbf{a}}z+d_{\textbf{a}}}-\frac{c_{\textbf{b}}}{c_{\textbf{b}}z+d_{\textbf{b}}}\right)=2\frac{c_{\textbf{a}}d_{\textbf{b}}-c_{\textbf{b}}d_{\textbf{a}}}{(c_{\textbf{a}}z+d_{\textbf{a}})(c_{\textbf{b}}z+d_{\textbf{b}})},

which implies

|\Phi_{\textbf{a},\textbf{b}}^{\prime}(z)|=2|c_{\textbf{a}}d_{\textbf{b}}-c_{\textbf{b}}d_{\textbf{a}}|\cdot|\gamma_{\textbf{a}}^{\prime}(z)|^{1/2}|\gamma_{\textbf{b}}^{\prime}(z)|^{1/2}.

Combining this with Part (
iv
) of Lemma
2.3
gives

(38)

|\Phi_{\textbf{a},\textbf{b}}^{\prime}(z)|\asymp|c_{\textbf{a}}d_{\textbf{b}}-c_{\textbf{b}}d_{\textbf{a}}|\cdot\Upsilon_{\textbf{a}}^{1/2}\Upsilon_{\textbf{b}}^{1/2}

with implied constants depending only on
\Gamma.
Now note that

\gamma_{\textbf{a}\overline{\textbf{b}}}=\gamma_{\textbf{a}}\gamma_{\textbf{b}}^{-1}=\begin{pmatrix}a_{\textbf{a}}&b_{\textbf{a}}\\
c_{\textbf{a}}&d_{\textbf{a}}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}d_{\textbf{b}}&-b_{\textbf{b}}\\
-c_{\textbf{b}}&a_{\textbf{b}}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\ast&\ast\\
c_{\textbf{a}}d_{\textbf{b}}-c_{\textbf{b}}d_{\textbf{a}}&\ast\end{pmatrix},

so we can apply Lemma
3.3
to obtain

|c_{\textbf{a}}d_{\textbf{b}}-c_{\textbf{b}}d_{\textbf{a}}|=|c_{\textbf{a}\overline{\textbf{b}}}|\asymp\frac{1}{\Upsilon_{\textbf{a}\overline{\textbf{b}}}^{1/2}},

which when inserted into (
38
) leads to

|\Phi_{\textbf{a},\textbf{b}}^{\prime}(z)|\asymp\frac{\Upsilon_{\textbf{a}}^{1/2}\Upsilon_{\textbf{b}}^{1/2}}{\Upsilon_{\textbf{a}\overline{\textbf{b}}}^{1/2}}=\mathcal{D}_{\textbf{a},\textbf{b}},

proving Part (
i
).

Using (
37
) we calculate the
n
-th derivative of
\Phi_{\textbf{a},\textbf{b}}
:

(39)

\Phi_{\textbf{a},\textbf{b}}^{(n)}(z)=2(n-1)!\left(\frac{c_{\textbf{a}}^{n}}{(c_{\textbf{a}}z+d_{\textbf{a}})^{n}}-\frac{c_{\textbf{b}}^{n}}{(c_{\textbf{b}}z+d_{\textbf{b}})^{n}}\right).

Writing

(40)

\frac{c_{\textbf{a}}}{c_{\textbf{a}}z+d_{\textbf{a}}}=\frac{1}{z-x_{\textbf{a}}}

with
x_{\textbf{a}}=\gamma_{\textbf{a}}^{-1}(\infty)=\gamma_{\overline{\textbf{a}}}(\infty)
and arguing as in the proof of Lemma
3.3
, we deduce that (
40
) is bounded from above by some constant
C>0
depending only on
\Gamma
. Clearly, the same holds true for
\textbf{b}.
Now note that for all
u,v\in\mathbb{C}
with
|u|,|v|\leqslant C
we have the bound
|u^{n}-v^{n}|\leqslant nC^{n}|u-v|.
Applying this to (
39
), we obtain

\displaystyle|\Phi_{\textbf{a},\textbf{b}}^{(n)}(z)|

\displaystyle\leqslant 2(n-1)!\left|\frac{c_{\textbf{a}}^{n}}{(c_{\textbf{a}}z+d_{\textbf{a}})^{n}}-\frac{c_{\textbf{b}}^{n}}{(c_{\textbf{b}}z+d_{\textbf{b}})^{n}}\right|

\displaystyle\leqslant 2n!C^{n}\left|\frac{c_{\textbf{a}}}{c_{\textbf{a}}z+d_{\textbf{a}}}-\frac{c_{\textbf{b}}}{c_{\textbf{b}}z+d_{\textbf{b}}}\right|

\displaystyle=n!C^{n}|\Phi_{\textbf{a},\textbf{b}}^{\prime}(z)|.

Thus we obtain from Part (
i
) (possibly with a larger constant
C
)

|\Phi_{\textbf{a},\textbf{b}}^{(n)}(z)|\leqslant C^{n}n!\mathcal{D}_{\textbf{a},\textbf{b}},

proving Part (
ii
).

Now we prove Part (
iii
). Fix points
z,z^{\prime}\in D_{b}
and Taylor-expand
\Phi_{\textbf{a},\textbf{b}}(z^{\prime})
around
z
to obtain

(41)

\Phi_{\textbf{a},\textbf{b}}(z^{\prime})=\Phi_{\textbf{a},\textbf{b}}(z)+\Phi_{\textbf{a},\textbf{b}}^{\prime}(z)(z^{\prime}-z)+\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}\frac{\Phi_{\textbf{a},\textbf{b}}^{(n)}(z)}{n!}(z^{\prime}-z)^{n}.

Using the triangle inequality as well as Parts (
i
) and (
ii
) gives

\displaystyle|\Phi_{\textbf{a},\textbf{b}}(z^{\prime})-\Phi_{\textbf{a},\textbf{b}}(z)|

\displaystyle\geqslant|\Phi_{\textbf{a},\textbf{b}}^{\prime}(z)||z^{\prime}-z|-\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}\frac{|\Phi_{\textbf{a},\textbf{b}}^{(n)}(z)|}{n!}|z^{\prime}-z|^{n}

\displaystyle\geqslant C_{0}\mathcal{D}_{\textbf{a},\textbf{b}}|z^{\prime}-z|-\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}C^{n}\mathcal{D}_{\textbf{a},\textbf{b}}|z^{\prime}-z|^{n}.

Thus, if
|z^{\prime}-z|<r_{0}
with some
r_{0}=r_{0}(C,C_{0})>0
small enough, we obtain

\displaystyle|\Phi_{\textbf{a},\textbf{b}}(z^{\prime})-\Phi_{\textbf{a},\textbf{b}}(z)|

\displaystyle\geqslant C_{0}\mathcal{D}_{\textbf{a},\textbf{b}}|z^{\prime}-z|-\frac{Cr_{0}}{1-Cr_{0}}\mathcal{D}_{\textbf{a},\textbf{b}}|z^{\prime}-z|

\displaystyle\geqslant\left(C_{0}-\frac{Cr_{0}}{1-Cr_{0}}\right)\mathcal{D}_{\textbf{a},\textbf{b}}|z^{\prime}-z|

\displaystyle\geqslant\frac{C_{0}}{2}\mathcal{D}_{\textbf{a},\textbf{b}}|z^{\prime}-z|,

as desired.
∎

3.3.
Averaged oscillatory integrals

Recall from §
2
the following notations:

•

D=\bigsqcup_{a\in\mathcal{A}}D_{a}
is the union of all the Schottky disks
D_{a}
, and

•

for
h>0
and
a\in\mathcal{A}
we define
\Omega(h)=\Lambda+D_{\mathbb{C}}(0,h)
and
\Omega_{a}(h)=\Omega(h)\cap D_{a}
.

In our proof of Theorem
1.1
we are led to consider oscillatory integrals of the form

\int_{\Omega_{b}(h)}e^{it\Phi_{\textbf{a},\textbf{b}}(z)}f_{\textbf{a},\textbf{b}}(z)\dvol(z),

where
\Phi_{\textbf{a},\textbf{b}}
is the phase given by (
35
). The aim of this section is to prove the following key estimate:

Proposition 3.4

(Key bound for averaged oscillatory integrals)
.

For all
b\in\mathcal{A}
,
\textbf{a},\textbf{b}\in\mathcal{W}^{\circ}
with
\textbf{a},\textbf{b}\to b
and
\textbf{a}\neq\textbf{b}
, for all
T\gg 1
large, for all
h>0
small enough, for all measurable functions
f\colon D\to\mathbb{C}
, and for all
\epsilon>0
we have

\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}\left|\int_{\Omega_{b}(h)}e^{it\Phi_{\textbf{a},\textbf{b}}(z)}f(z)\dvol(z)\right|dt\ll_{\epsilon}h^{1-\frac{\delta}{2}}\mathcal{D}_{\textbf{a},\textbf{b}}^{-1}T^{-1+\epsilon}e^{ChT}\|f\|_{\infty,D_{b}},

where
\|f\|_{\infty,D_{b}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\sup_{z\in D_{b}}|f(z)|
and
\mathcal{D}_{\textbf{a},\textbf{b}}
is the quantity in (
36
). The implied constant and
C>0
depend only on
\Gamma.

Proof.

For the rest of this proof fix
h>0
small enough so that
\Omega(h)\subset D
, fix
b\in\mathcal{A}
and
\textbf{a},\textbf{b}\in\mathcal{W}^{\circ}
with
\textbf{a},\textbf{b}\to b
and
\textbf{a}\neq\textbf{b}
, and write

I_{T}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}\left|\int_{\Omega_{b}(h)}e^{it\Phi_{\textbf{a},\textbf{b}}(z)}f(z)\dvol(z)\right|dt.

Let
r_{0}>0
be as in Part (
iii
) of Proposition
3.2
. Clearly,
D
can be covered by finitely many euclidean disks of radius
r_{1}=r_{0}/2
. Let
B_{1},\dots B_{m}
be the sub-collection of those disks covering
\Omega_{b}(h)=\Omega(h)\cap D_{b}
. Set
\Omega_{b}^{i}(h)=\Omega_{b}(h)\cap B_{i}
for all
i=1,\dots,m
. By construction, for any pair of points
z,z^{\prime}\in\Omega_{b}^{i}(h)
, we have
|z^{\prime}-z|<r_{0}.
Hence, by Part (
iii
) of Proposition
3.2
, we have for all
z,z^{\prime}\in\Omega_{b}^{i}(h)
:

(42)

|\Phi_{\textbf{a},\textbf{b}}(z)-\Phi_{\textbf{a},\textbf{b}}(z^{\prime})|\gg\mathcal{D}_{\textbf{a},\textbf{b}}|z^{\prime}-z|.

We will use this bound further below. Here and throughout this proof, implied constants are allowed to depend solely on
\Gamma
.

For all
i=1,\dots,m
write

I_{T,i}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}\left|\int_{\Omega_{b}^{i}(h)}e^{it\Phi_{\textbf{a},\textbf{b}}(z)}f(z)\dvol(z)\right|dt.

Clearly, by the triangle inequality we have

I_{T}\leqslant I_{T,i}+\cdots+I_{T,m},

so it suffices to prove the bound

I_{T,i}\ll_{\epsilon}h^{1-\frac{\delta}{2}}\mathcal{D}_{\textbf{a},\textbf{b}}^{-1}T^{-1+\epsilon}e^{ChT}\|f\|_{\infty,D_{b}}

for each
I_{T,i}
individually. For the rest of this proof fix some index
i\in\{1,\dots,m\}.

Next, let
\psi\in C_{c}(\mathbb{R})
be a non-negative, smooth function, supported in
[-2,2]
with
\psi=1
in
[-1,1]
, and define

\psi_{T}(x)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\psi\left(\frac{x}{T}\right).

Clearly, we have

I_{T,i}\leqslant\frac{1}{T}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\psi_{T}(t)\left|\int_{\Omega_{b}^{i}(h)}e^{it\Phi_{\textbf{a},\textbf{b}}(z)}f(z)\dvol(z)\right|dt.

Applying the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality gives

\displaystyle I_{T}^{2}

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{1}{T^{2}}\left(\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\psi_{T}(t)dt\right)\left(\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\psi_{T}(t)\left|\int_{\Omega_{b}^{i}(h)}e^{it\Phi_{\textbf{a},\textbf{b}}(z)}f(z)\dvol(z)\right|^{2}dt\right)

\displaystyle\ll\frac{1}{T}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\psi_{T}(t)\left|\int_{\Omega_{b}^{i}(h)}e^{it\Phi_{\textbf{a},\textbf{b}}(z)}f(z)\dvol(z)\right|^{2}dt

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{T}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\psi_{T}(t)\int_{\Omega_{b}^{i}(h)}\int_{\Omega_{b}^{i}(h)}e^{it(\Phi_{\textbf{a},\textbf{b}}(z_{1})-\Phi_{\textbf{a},\textbf{b}}(\overline{z_{2}}))}f(z_{1})\overline{f(z_{2})}\dvol(z_{1})\dvol(z_{2}).

For last equality we opened the squared brackets and we used that

\overline{\Phi_{\textbf{a},\textbf{b}}(z)}=\Phi_{\textbf{a},\textbf{b}}(\overline{z}).

Using Fubini’s theorem (which is justified because the support of
\psi_{T}
is compact) we can interchange integrals to obtain

I_{T,i}^{2}\ll\frac{1}{T}\int_{\Omega_{b}^{i}(h)}\int_{\Omega_{b}^{i}(h)}\widehat{\psi}_{T}\left(\Phi_{\textbf{a},\textbf{b}}(\overline{z_{2}})-\Phi_{\textbf{a},\textbf{b}}(z_{1})\right)f(z_{1})\overline{f(z_{2})}\dvol(z_{1})\dvol(z_{2}),

where the Fourier transform is defined for all
\varphi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
as usual by

\widehat{\varphi}(z)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\varphi(t)e^{-itz}dt.

Using basic properties of the Fourier transform we find that
\widehat{\psi}_{T}(z)=T\widehat{\psi}(Tz),
whence

I_{T,i}^{2}\ll\int_{\Omega_{b}(h)}\int_{\Omega_{b}(h)}\widehat{\psi}\left(T\left(\Phi_{\textbf{a},\textbf{b}}(\overline{z_{2}})-\Phi_{\textbf{a},\textbf{b}}(z_{1})\right)\right)f(z_{1})\overline{f(z_{2})}\dvol(z_{1})\dvol(z_{2}).

Since
\psi
is smooth and supported in
[-2,2]
we have that for all
A>0
,

|\widehat{\psi}(z)|\ll_{A}\frac{e^{2|\mathrm{Im}(z)|}}{(1+|z|)^{A}}.

Therefore,

\displaystyle I_{T,i}^{2}

\displaystyle\ll_{A}\left\|f\right\|_{\infty,D_{b}}^{2}\cdot\int_{\Omega_{b}^{i}(h)}\int_{\Omega_{b}^{i}(h)}\frac{e^{2T|\mathrm{Im}\left(\Phi_{\textbf{a},\textbf{b}}(\overline{z_{2}})-\Phi_{\textbf{a},\textbf{b}}(z_{1})\right)|}}{\left(1+T\left|\Phi_{\textbf{a},\textbf{b}}(\overline{z_{2}})-\Phi_{\textbf{a},\textbf{b}}(z_{1})\right|\right)^{A}}\dvol(z_{1})\dvol(z_{2})

\displaystyle\ll_{A}e^{2ChT}\left\|f\right\|_{\infty,D_{b}}^{2}\cdot\int_{\Omega_{b}^{i}(h)}\int_{\Omega_{b}^{i}(h)}\frac{1}{\left(1+T\left|\Phi_{\textbf{a},\textbf{b}}(\overline{z_{2}})-\Phi_{\textbf{a},\textbf{b}}(z_{1})\right|\right)^{A}}\dvol(z_{1})\dvol(z_{2}).

For the second inequality we used that for all
z\in\Omega_{b}(h)
,

|e^{it\Phi_{\textbf{a},\textbf{b}}(z)}|=|\gamma_{\textbf{a}}^{\prime}(z)^{it}\gamma_{\textbf{b}}^{\prime}(z)^{-it}|\ll e^{Ch|t|}.

To proceed, divide the double integral above as follows:

\int_{\Omega_{b}^{i}(h)}\int_{\Omega_{b}^{i}(h)}=\int_{A^{r}}+\int_{B^{r}},

where

A^{r}=\{(z_{1},z_{2})\in\Omega_{b}^{i}(h)\times\Omega_{b}^{i}(h):|z_{1}-\overline{z_{2}}|>r\}

and

B^{r}=\{(z_{1},z_{2})\in\Omega_{b}^{i}(h)\times\Omega_{b}^{i}(h):|z_{1}-\overline{z_{2}}|\leqslant r\}.

Now take
r=\mathcal{D}_{\textbf{a},\textbf{b}}^{-1}T^{-1+\epsilon}
for some
\epsilon>0
. Invoking the estimate in (
42
), we obtain that for all
(z_{1},z_{2})\in A^{r}
:

\left|\Phi_{\textbf{a},\textbf{b}}(\overline{z_{2}})-\Phi_{\textbf{a},\textbf{b}}(z_{1})\right|\gg\mathcal{D}_{\textbf{a},\textbf{b}}r\gg T^{-1+\epsilon}.

It follows that

\displaystyle\int_{A^{r}}\frac{1}{\left(1+T\left|\Phi_{\textbf{a},\textbf{b}}(\overline{z_{2}})-\Phi_{\textbf{a},\textbf{b}}(z_{1})\right|\right)^{A}}\dvol(z_{1})\dvol(z_{2})

\displaystyle\ll\int_{A^{r}}\frac{1}{\left(1+T^{\epsilon}\right)^{A}}\dvol(z_{1})\dvol(z_{2})

\displaystyle\ll T^{-\epsilon A}\vol(\Omega(h))^{2},

where in the last inequality we simply used the fact that
A^{r}\subseteq\Omega(h)\times\Omega(h).
Using the volume bound from Lemma
2.6
we conclude that the integral over
A^{r}
is bounded from above by

(43)

\ll_{A}T^{-\epsilon A}h^{4-2\delta}.

The integral over
B^{r}
can be bounded as follows:

\displaystyle\int_{B^{r}}\frac{1}{\left(1+T\left|\phi(\overline{z_{2}})-\phi(z_{1})\right|\right)^{A}}\dvol(z_{1})\dvol(z_{2})

\displaystyle\leqslant\int_{B^{r}}1\dvol(z_{1})\dvol(z_{2})

\displaystyle\leqslant\int_{\Omega_{b}(h)}\int_{D(z_{1},r)}1\dvol(z_{2})\dvol(z_{1})

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega_{b}(h)}\vol(D(z_{1},r))\dvol(z_{1})

\displaystyle\ll\int_{\Omega_{b}(h)}r^{2}\dvol(z_{2}).

By the volume estimate and by our choice of
r
, the integral over
B_{r}
is less than

(44)

\vol(\Omega(h))r^{2}\ll h^{2-\delta}\mathcal{D}_{\textbf{a},\textbf{b}}^{-2}T^{-2+2\epsilon}.

Putting together the estimates (
43
) and (
44
), we obtain

I_{T,i}^{2}\ll_{A}\left(h^{4-\delta}T^{-\epsilon A}+h^{2-\delta}\mathcal{D}_{\textbf{a},\textbf{b}}^{-2}T^{-2+2\epsilon}\right)e^{2ChT}\left\|f\right\|_{\infty,D_{b}}^{2}.

Finally, for any fixed
\epsilon>0
we can choose
A>0
so large that the first term on the right gets absorbed by the second one, giving

I_{T,i}^{2}\ll_{\epsilon}h^{2-\delta}\mathcal{D}_{\textbf{a},\textbf{b}}^{-2}T^{-2+2\epsilon}e^{2ChT}\left\|f\right\|_{\infty,D_{b}}^{2},

as desired.
∎

3.4.
Applying Jensen’s Formula

Given
h>0
and resolution parameters
\tau_{0}>0
and
\tau_{1}>0
, define the composite operator

(45)

\mathcal{L}_{\tau_{0},\tau_{1},s}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\mathcal{L}_{\tau_{0},s}\circ\mathcal{L}_{\tau_{1},s}\colon H^{2}(\Omega(h))\to H^{2}(\Omega(h)),

where
\mathcal{L}_{\tau,s}
is the
\tau
-refined transfer operator from §
2.6
. Recall that (
45
) is well-defined, provided
h,\tau_{0},\tau_{1}
are small enough.

Given a trace-class operator
A\colon H\to H
on a separable Hilbert space
H
, the Hilbert–Schmidt norm is defined by

\|A\|_{\mathrm{HS}}^{2}=\mathrm{tr}\left(A^{\ast}A\right),

where
A^{\ast}
denotes the adjoint of
A
. The next result relates the resonance counting function
N_{X}(\sigma,T)
to the Hilbert–Schmidt norm of (
45
):

Proposition 3.5

(Key resonance counting bound)
.

There exist constants
K_{0}=K_{0}(\Gamma)>0
,
T_{0}=T_{0}(\Gamma)>0
, and
\epsilon_{0}=\epsilon_{0}(\Gamma)>0
such that for all
T>T_{0}
,
K>K_{0}
, and
h,\tau_{0},\tau_{1}\in(0,\epsilon_{0})
the following holds true:

N_{X}(\sigma,T)\ll K\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}\sigma-\frac{\alpha}{K}\leqslant\mathrm{Re}(s)\leqslant\beta K\\
|\mathrm{Im}(s)|\leqslant\beta K\end{subarray}}\left(\int_{0}^{T}\|\mathcal{L}_{\tau_{0},\tau_{1},s+it}\|_{\mathrm{HS},h}^{2}dt\right)+(C\tau_{0}\tau_{1})^{K}T,

where
\|\cdot\|_{\mathrm{HS},h}
denotes the Hilbert–Schmidt norm of operators
H^{2}(\Omega(h))\to H^{2}(\Omega(h)).
The implied constants as well as the constants
\alpha,\beta,C>0
depend solely on
\Gamma.

Proposition
3.5
is a consequence of the following variant of Jensen’s formula that we specifically tailored to our purposes:

Lemma 3.6

(Adaptation of Jensen’s Formula)
.

Let
f
be an entire function and let

N_{f}(\sigma,T)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\#\{s\in\mathbb{C}:\text{$f(s)=0$, $\sigma\leqslant\mathrm{Re}(s)\leqslant\delta$, $0\leqslant\mathrm{Im}(s)\leqslant T$}\}.

Then, for all
K>0
sufficiently large and for all
\sigma\in\mathbb{R}
with
\sigma<\delta
, we have

N_{f}(\sigma,T)\ll K\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}\sigma-\frac{\alpha}{K}\leqslant\mathrm{Re}(s)\leqslant\beta K\\
|\mathrm{Im}(s)|\leqslant\beta K\end{subarray}}\left(\int_{0}^{T}\log|f(s+it)|dt\right)-\int_{0}^{T}\log|f(\delta+K+it)|dt.

The implied constant as well as
\alpha>0
and
\beta>0
are independent of
f
,
\sigma
and
K
.

Proof.

Fix some
t\in\mathbb{R}
and consider the pair of concentric disks
D_{i}=D_{\mathbb{C}}(s_{0},r_{i})
with
i\in\{1,2\}
centered at the point
s_{0}=\sigma_{0}+it
and with radii
r_{2}>r_{1}>0
. Assume that
\sigma_{0},r_{1},r_{2}
are chosen in such a way that

(46)

\{s\in\mathbb{C}:\text{$\sigma\leqslant\mathrm{Re}(s)\leqslant\delta$, $|\mathrm{Im}(s)-t|\leqslant 1$}\}\subset\overline{D_{1}}\subset D_{2}.

Define

M_{f}(\sigma,t)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\#\{s\in\mathbb{C}:\text{$f(s)=0$, $\sigma\leqslant\mathrm{Re}(s)\leqslant\delta$, $|\mathrm{Im}(s)-t|\leqslant 1$}\}

Now we apply Jensen’s formula from complex analysis. We refer to Titchmarsh’s classical book
[
19
]
for a proof of Jensen’s formula and its relatives. We obtain

(47)

M_{f}(\sigma,t)\leqslant\frac{1}{\log(r_{2}/r_{1})}\int_{0}^{1}\log|f(\sigma_{0}+r_{2}e^{2\pi i\theta}+it)|d\theta-\log|f(\sigma_{0}+it)|.

By integrating this estimate, we obtain

\displaystyle N_{f}(\sigma,T)

\displaystyle\leqslant\int_{0}^{T}M_{f}(\sigma,t)dt

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{1}{\log(r_{2}/r_{1})}\int_{0}^{1}\left(\int_{0}^{T}\log|f(\sigma_{0}+r_{2}e^{2\pi i\theta}+it)|dt\right)d\theta-\int_{0}^{T}\log|f(\sigma_{0}+it)|dt.

Let us now choose appropriate parameters
\sigma_{0},r_{1},r_{2}.
For
K>1
, we put

\text{$\sigma_{0}=\delta+K$, $r_{1}=\sqrt{(\sigma_{0}-\sigma)^{2}+1}$ and $r_{2}=r_{1}+1/K$}.

One can verify that this choice guarantees that the inclusions in (
46
) hold true. Furthermore, for
K
large, the following estimates hold true with absolute implied constants:

(i)

r_{1}\asymp r_{2}\asymp\sigma_{0}-\sigma\asymp K,

(ii)

\sqrt{1+\frac{1}{(\sigma_{0}-\sigma)^{2}}}=1+O(\frac{1}{K^{2}}),

(iii)

r_{1}=\sqrt{(\sigma_{0}-\sigma)^{2}+1}=(\sigma_{0}-\sigma)\sqrt{1+\frac{1}{(\sigma_{0}-\sigma)^{2}}}=(\sigma_{0}-\sigma)+O(\frac{1}{K}),
and

(iv)

r_{2}=\sigma_{0}-\sigma+O(\frac{1}{K}).

These estimates imply that for all
s=\sigma_{0}+r_{2}e^{2\pi i\theta}
with
\theta\in[0,1]
we have

\mathrm{Re}(s)\geqslant\sigma_{0}-r_{2}\geqslant\sigma-O(\frac{1}{K})
and
|\mathrm{Im}(s)|\leqslant\sigma_{0}+r_{2}=O(K)
.

Thus, we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\log(r_{2}/r_{1})}\int_{0}^{1}\left(\int_{0}^{T}\log|f(\sigma_{0}+r_{2}e^{2\pi i\theta}+it)|dt\right)d\theta

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\ll K\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}\sigma-O(\frac{1}{K})\leqslant\mathrm{Re}(s)\leqslant O(K)\\
|\mathrm{Im}(s)|\leqslant O(K)\end{subarray}}\left(\int_{0}^{T}\log|f(s+it)|dt\right),

where all implied constants are absolute. The proof of complete.
∎

We also need the following pointwise estimate:

Lemma 3.7

(Pointwise estimate)
.

For all sufficiently small resolution parameters
\tau_{0}>0
and
\tau_{1}>0
, and for all
s=\sigma+it\in\mathbb{C}
with
\sigma>\delta
the Fredholm determinant of
\mathcal{L}_{\tau_{0},\tau_{1},s}^{2}
satisfies

-\log|\det\left(1-\mathcal{L}_{\tau_{0},\tau_{1},s}^{2}\right)|\leqslant\frac{(C\tau_{0}\tau_{1})^{2(\sigma-\delta)}}{1-(C\tau_{0}\tau_{1})^{2(\sigma-\delta)}},

where
C>0
depends only on
\Gamma.

Proof.

We will adapt the argument of Magee–Naud
[
14
]
. Given a trace class operator
A\colon H\to H
acting on a separable Hilbert space
H
with
\|A\|_{H}<1
, we have the absolutely convergent series expansion

(48)

\det(1-A)=\exp\left(-\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n}\mathrm{tr}(A^{n})\right),

see for instance
[
6
]
. In particular,

(49)

-\log|\det(1-A)|\leqslant\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{k}|\mathrm{tr}(A^{n})|.

Specializing this to
A=\mathcal{L}_{\tau_{0},\tau_{1},s}^{2}
with
\sigma=\mathrm{Re}(s)>\delta,
we get

(50)

-\log|\det(1-\mathcal{L}_{\tau_{0},\tau_{1},s}^{2})|\leqslant\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{k}|\mathrm{tr}(\mathcal{L}_{\tau_{0},\tau_{1},s}^{2n})|.

Therefore, proving the lemma is reduced to finding a suitable upper bound for the trace of
\mathcal{L}_{\tau_{0},\tau_{1},s}^{2n}
. Using the formula (
21
) we may write

(51)

\mathcal{L}_{\tau_{0},\tau_{1},s}f(z)=\sum_{\textbf{b}\in S_{b}}\gamma^{\prime}_{\textbf{b}}(z)^{s}f(\gamma_{\textbf{b}}(z))\quad\text{for}\quad z\in\Omega_{b}(h),

where for each
b\in\mathcal{A}
we put

S_{b}=\{\textbf{a}_{0}\textbf{a}_{1}\in Y(\tau_{0})\times Y(\tau_{1}):\textbf{a}_{0}\to\textbf{a}_{1}\to b\}.

Here
\textbf{a}_{0}\textbf{a}_{1}\in Y(\tau_{0})\times Y(\tau_{1})
means that for
i\in\{0,1\}
the sub-word
\textbf{a}_{i}
belongs to
Y(\tau_{i})
. Defining
S=S_{1}\sqcup\cdots\sqcup S_{b}
and carefully applying the Lefschetz fixed point formula (see
[
1
, Lemma 15.9]
), we get for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
:

\mathrm{tr}(\mathcal{L}_{\tau_{0},\tau_{1},s}^{n})=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\textbf{b}_{n}\to\textbf{b}_{1}\to\dots\to\textbf{b}_{n}\\
\textbf{b}_{1},\dots,\textbf{b}_{n}\in S\end{subarray}}\frac{\gamma^{\prime}_{\textbf{b}_{1}\textbf{b}_{2}\cdots\textbf{b}_{n}}(x_{\textbf{b}_{1}\textbf{b}_{2}\cdots\textbf{b}_{n}})^{s}}{1-\gamma^{\prime}_{\textbf{b}_{1}\textbf{b}_{2}\cdots\textbf{b}_{n}}(x_{\textbf{b}_{1}\textbf{b}_{2}\cdots\textbf{b}_{n}})},

where
x_{\textbf{b}_{1}\textbf{b}_{2}\cdots\textbf{b}_{n}}
is the unique attracting fixed point of
\gamma_{\textbf{b}_{1}\textbf{b}_{2}\cdots\textbf{b}_{n}}
. Note that
x_{\textbf{b}_{1}\textbf{b}_{2}\cdots\textbf{b}_{n}}\in D_{b}
where
b
is the first letter of
\textbf{b}_{1}
and the last letter of
\textbf{b}_{n}
. Thus, applying Part (
vii
) of Lemma
2.3

(n-1)
times, we obtain for some
C=C(\Gamma)>0
,

\gamma^{\prime}_{\textbf{b}_{1}\textbf{b}_{2}\cdots\textbf{b}_{n}}(x_{\textbf{b}_{1}\textbf{b}_{2}\cdots\textbf{b}_{n}})\ll\Upsilon_{\textbf{b}_{1}\textbf{b}_{2}\cdots\textbf{b}_{n}}\leqslant C^{n}\Upsilon_{\textbf{b}_{1}}\cdots\Upsilon_{\textbf{b}_{n}}.

By the definition of
S
it follows that for all
\textbf{b}\in S
,

\Upsilon_{\textbf{b}}\ll\tau_{0}\tau_{1}.

Thus (increasing
C
if necessary) we have

\gamma^{\prime}_{\textbf{b}_{1}\textbf{b}_{2}\cdots\textbf{b}_{n}}(x_{\textbf{b}_{1}\textbf{b}_{2}\cdots\textbf{b}_{n}})\leqslant(C\tau_{0}\tau_{1})^{n}.

Now if
C\tau_{0}\tau_{1}<\frac{1}{2}
then

\frac{\gamma^{\prime}_{\textbf{b}_{1}\textbf{b}_{2}\cdots\textbf{b}_{n}}(x_{\textbf{b}_{1}\textbf{b}_{2}\cdots\textbf{b}_{n}})^{\sigma}}{1-\gamma^{\prime}_{\textbf{b}_{1}\textbf{b}_{2}\cdots\textbf{b}_{n}}(x_{\textbf{b}_{1}\textbf{b}_{2}\cdots\textbf{b}_{n}})}\ll\gamma^{\prime}_{\textbf{b}_{1}\textbf{b}_{2}\cdots\textbf{b}_{n}}(x_{\textbf{b}_{1}\textbf{b}_{2}\cdots\textbf{b}_{n}})^{\sigma}\ll(C\tau_{0}\tau_{1})^{\sigma n},

and therefore,

\mathrm{tr}(\mathcal{L}_{\tau_{0},\tau_{1},s}^{n})\ll|S|^{n}(C\tau_{0}\tau_{1})^{\sigma n}.

By Lemma
2.4
the size of
S
is bounded from above by

(52)

|S|\ll|Y(\tau_{0})||Y(\tau_{1})|\ll(\tau_{0}\tau_{1})^{-\delta},

which when inserted above yields (possibly with a larger constant
C
)

\mathrm{tr}(\mathcal{L}_{\tau_{0},\tau_{1},s}^{n})\ll(C\tau_{0}\tau_{1})^{n(\sigma-\delta)}.

Returning to (
50
), taking
\tau_{0}
and
\tau_{1}
small, and using a geometric series summation, we obtain

\displaystyle-\log|\det(1-\mathcal{L}_{\tau_{0},\tau_{1},s}^{2})|

\displaystyle\ll\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}|\mathrm{tr}(\mathcal{L}_{\tau_{0},\tau_{1},s}^{2n})|

\displaystyle\ll\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}(C\tau_{0}\tau_{1})^{2n(\sigma-\delta)}

\displaystyle=\frac{(C\tau_{0}\tau_{1})^{2(\sigma-\delta)}}{1-(C\tau_{0}\tau_{1})^{2(\sigma-\delta)}},

completing the proof.
∎

We are now ready to finish the proof of Proposition
3.5
.

Proof of Proposition
3.5
.

By Lemma
2.2
and the remarks thereafter, resonances for
X
occur as zeros of

f(s)=\det(1-\mathcal{L}_{\tau_{0},\tau_{1},s}^{2}).

By Lemma
3.6
and Lemma
3.7
, we have for all
K
sufficiently large and for all
\tau_{0}>0
and
\tau_{1}>0
sufficiently small:

(53)

N_{X}(\sigma,T)\ll K\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}\sigma-\frac{\alpha}{K}\leqslant\mathrm{Re}(s)\leqslant\beta K\\
|\mathrm{Im}(s)|\leqslant\beta K\end{subarray}}\left(\int_{0}^{T}|\det(1-\mathcal{L}_{\tau_{0},\tau_{1},s+it}^{2})|dt\right)+(C\tau_{0}\tau_{1})^{2K}T.

It remains to show that

|\det(1-\mathcal{L}_{\tau_{0},\tau_{1},s}^{2})|\leqslant\|\mathcal{L}_{\tau_{0},\tau_{1},s}\|_{\mathrm{HS},h}^{2},

where
\|\cdot\|_{\mathrm{HS},h}
denotes the Hilbert–Schmidt norm of operators
H^{2}(\Omega(h))\to H^{2}(\Omega(h)).
To that effect, let us recall some basic facts on trace class operators and Fredholm determinants, referring the reader to
[
6
,
7
,
18
]
for details. Weyl’s estimate on Fredholm determinants states that for every trace-class operator
A\colon H\to H
on a separable Hilbert space
H
we have

(54)

\log|\det\left(1-A\right)|\leqslant\|A\|_{\mathrm{tr}},

where
\|\cdot\|_{\mathrm{tr}}
denotes the trace norm. Moreover, for any two Hilbert–Schmidt operators
A_{1},A_{2}\colon H\to H
we have the inequality

(55)

\|A_{1}A_{2}\|_{\mathrm{tr}}\leqslant\|A_{1}\|_{\mathrm{HS}}\|A_{2}\|_{\mathrm{HS}}.

Now recall that if
h,\tau_{0},\tau_{1}
are small enough, then

\mathcal{L}_{\tau_{0},\tau_{1},s}\colon H^{2}(\Omega(h))\to H^{2}(\Omega(h))

is a well-defined defined family of trace class operators, in which case we can apply the above facts to
A=A_{1}=A_{2}=\mathcal{L}_{\tau_{0},\tau_{1},s}
and
H=H^{2}(\Omega(h))
to obtain

|\det(1-\mathcal{L}_{\tau_{0},\tau_{1},s}^{2})|\leqslant\|\mathcal{L}_{\tau_{0},\tau_{1},s}^{2}\|_{\mathrm{tr}}\leqslant\|\mathcal{L}_{\tau_{0},\tau_{1},s}\|_{\mathrm{HS},h}^{2}

as desired.
∎

3.5.
Hilbert–Schmidt norm

The goal of this section is to prove the following explicit formula for the Hilbert–Schmidt norm of
\mathcal{L}_{\tau_{0},\tau_{1},s}
:

Proposition 3.8

(Hilbert–Schmidt norm of
\mathcal{L}_{\tau_{0},\tau_{1},s}
)
.

For all sufficiently small parameters
h,\tau_{0},\tau_{1}>0
the Hilbert–Schmidt norm of

\mathcal{L}_{\tau_{0},\tau_{1},s}=\mathcal{L}_{\tau_{0},s}\circ\mathcal{L}_{\tau_{1},s}\colon H^{2}(\Omega(h))\to H^{2}(\Omega(h))

is given by

\|\mathcal{L}_{\tau_{0},\tau_{1},s}\|_{\mathrm{HS},h}^{2}=\sum_{b\in\mathcal{A}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\textbf{a}=\textbf{a}_{0}\textbf{a}_{1}\in Y(\tau_{0})\times Y(\tau_{1})\\
\textbf{b}=\textbf{b}_{0}\textbf{b}_{1}\in Y(\tau_{0})\times Y(\tau_{1})\\
\textbf{a}_{0}\to\textbf{a}_{1}\to b\\
\textbf{b}_{0}\to\textbf{b}_{1}\to b\end{subarray}}\int_{\Omega_{b}(h)}\gamma^{\prime}_{\textbf{a}}(z)^{s}\overline{\gamma^{\prime}_{\textbf{b}}(z)^{s}}B_{\Omega(h)}(\gamma_{\textbf{a}}(z),\gamma_{\textbf{b}}(z))\dvol(z).

Here, we write
\textbf{a}=\textbf{a}_{0}\textbf{a}_{1}\in S_{0}\times S_{1}
to mean that for
i\in\{0,1\}
the sub-word
\textbf{a}_{i}
belongs to
S_{i}
. Moreover,
B_{\Omega(h)}(\cdot,\cdot)
is the Bergman kernel of
H^{2}(\Omega(h))
.

Proof.

Proofs of formulas for the Hilbert–Schmidt norm for similar operators can be found in
[
14
, Lemma 4.7]
and in
[
17
, Proposition 5.5]
. We will give an alternative but essentially equivalent argument. First, we write down the formula for
\mathcal{L}_{\tau_{0},\tau_{1},s}
using (
21
):

(56)

\mathcal{L}_{\tau_{0},\tau_{1},s}f(z)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\textbf{a}=\textbf{a}_{0}\textbf{a}_{1}\in Y(\tau_{0})\times Y(\tau_{1})\\
\textbf{a}_{0}\to\textbf{a}_{1}\to b\end{subarray}}\gamma^{\prime}_{\textbf{a}}(z)^{s}f(\gamma_{\textbf{a}}(z))\quad\text{for}\quad z\in\Omega_{b}(h).

For notational convenience we rewrite this as

(57)

\mathcal{L}_{\tau_{0},\tau_{1},s}f(z)=\sum_{\textbf{a}\in S_{b}}\gamma^{\prime}_{\textbf{a}}(z)^{s}f(\gamma_{\textbf{a}}(z))\quad\text{for}\quad z\in\Omega_{b}(h),

where for each
b\in\mathcal{A}
we put

S_{b}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\{\textbf{a}=\textbf{a}_{0}\textbf{a}_{1}\in Y(\tau_{0})\times Y(\tau_{1}):\textbf{a}_{0}\to\textbf{a}_{1}\to b\}.

Recall from §
2.9
that the Bergman kernel
B_{h}(z,w)
is defined by the property

\int_{\Omega(h)}B_{\Omega(h)}(z,w)f(w)\dvol(w)=f(z)

for all
f\in H^{2}(D)
and
z\in D
. Thus the operator
\mathcal{L}_{\tau_{0},\tau_{1},s}
is a kernel integral operator

\mathcal{L}_{\tau_{0},\tau_{1},s}f(z)=\int_{\Omega(h)}K(z,w)f(w)\dvol(w)

whose kernel is given by the formula

K(z,w)=\sum_{\textbf{a}\in S_{b}}\gamma^{\prime}_{\textbf{a}}(z)^{s}B_{\Omega(h)}(\gamma_{\textbf{a}}(z),w)\quad\text{for}\quad z\in\Omega_{b}(h).

The Hilbert–Schmidt norm can now be computed as follows:

\displaystyle\|

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\tau_{0},\tau_{1},s}\|_{\mathrm{HS}}^{2}=\int_{\Omega(h)}\int_{\Omega(h)}|K(z,w)|^{2}\dvol(w)\dvol(z)

\displaystyle=\sum_{b\in\mathcal{A}}\int_{\Omega_{b}(h)}\int_{\Omega(h)}|K(z,w)|^{2}\dvol(w)\dvol(z)

\displaystyle=\sum_{b\in\mathcal{A}}\int_{\Omega_{b}(h)}\int_{\Omega(h)}\sum_{(\textbf{a},\textbf{b})\in S_{b}\times S_{b}}\gamma^{\prime}_{\textbf{a}}(z)^{s}\overline{\gamma^{\prime}_{\textbf{b}}(z)^{s}}B_{\Omega(h)}(\gamma_{\textbf{a}}(z),w)\overline{B_{\Omega(h)}(\gamma_{\textbf{b}}(z),w)}\dvol(w)\dvol(z)

Using the defining property of the Bergman kernel we obtain the relation

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega(h)}B_{\Omega(h)}(\gamma_{\textbf{a}}(z),w)\overline{B_{\Omega(h)}(\gamma_{\textbf{b}}(z),w)}\dvol(w)

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega(h)}B_{\Omega(h)}(\gamma_{\textbf{a}}(z),w)B_{\Omega(h)}(w,\gamma_{\textbf{b}}(z))\dvol(w)

\displaystyle=B_{\Omega(h)}(\gamma_{\textbf{a}}(z),\gamma_{\textbf{b}}(z)),

which when inserted into the last line above completes the proof.
∎

3.6.
Finishing the proof of the main theorem

Now we finish the proof of Theorem
1.1
. It follows directly from Proposition
3.5
and the next estimate:

Proposition 3.9

(Main technical estimate)
.

For all
T\gg 1
large,
\sigma\in[0,\delta]
and
s\in\mathbb{C}
with
\mathrm{Re}(s)\geqslant\sigma
and
|\mathrm{Im}(s)|\leqslant T
there exist positive parameters
h,\tau_{0},\tau_{1}
such that for all
\epsilon>0
:

\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}\|\mathcal{L}_{\tau_{0},\tau_{1},s+it}\|_{\mathrm{HS},h}^{2}dt\ll_{\epsilon}T^{\delta-\frac{3\delta+2}{2\delta+2}(2\sigma-\delta)+\epsilon}.

Furthermore, this is achieved by choosing
h=T^{-1}
and resolution parameters
\tau_{0}
and
\tau_{1}
satisfying
\tau_{0}\asymp T^{-1}
and
\tau_{1}\asymp T^{-\frac{\delta}{2\delta+2}}
with implied constants depending only on
\Gamma
.

We now deduce Theorem
1.1
from this estimate. By Proposition
3.5
, we have for all
K
large enough

N_{X}(\sigma,T)\ll K\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}\sigma-\frac{\alpha}{K}\leqslant\mathrm{Re}(s)\leqslant\beta K\\
|\mathrm{Im}(s)|\leqslant\beta K\end{subarray}}\left(\int_{0}^{T}\|\mathcal{L}_{\tau_{0},\tau_{1},s+it}\|_{\mathrm{HS},h}^{2}dt\right)+(C\tau_{0}\tau_{1})^{K}T.

Combining this with Proposition
3.9
with parameters
h=T^{-1}
,
\tau_{0}\asymp T^{-1},\tau_{1}\asymp T^{-\frac{\delta}{2\delta+2}}
as in the statement, we obtain

\displaystyle N_{X}(\sigma,T)

\displaystyle\ll_{\epsilon}KT^{1+\delta-\frac{3\delta+2}{2\delta+2}(2\sigma-\delta)+O(\frac{1}{K})+\epsilon}+T^{1-AK},

where
A>0
is independent of
T.
It remains to choose
K
suitably, which may be done by taking
K=\log T
. Clearly, with this choice, the second term
T^{1-AK}
gets absorbed by the first one, and
KT^{O(\frac{1}{K})}\ll_{\epsilon}T^{\epsilon}
. This concludes the proof of Theorem
1.1
assuming Proposition
3.9
. The proof of the latter occupies the rest of this section.

Proof of Proposition
3.9
.

Let
h,\tau_{0},\tau_{1}
be sufficiently small positive parameters such that the operator

(58)

\mathcal{L}_{\tau_{0},\tau_{1},s}\colon H^{2}(\Omega(h))\to H^{2}(\Omega(h))

is well-defined. Furthermore, assume
T\gg 1
to be large and take
h=T^{-1}
. The parameters
\tau_{0}
and
\tau_{1}
will be chosen in the course of the proof.

Using Proposition
3.8
we can write down the following formula for the Hilbert–Schmidt norm of (
58
):

\|\mathcal{L}_{\tau_{0},\tau_{1},s}\|_{\mathrm{HS},h}^{2}=\sum_{b\in\mathcal{A}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\textbf{a}=\textbf{a}_{0}\textbf{a}_{1}\in Y(\tau_{0})\times Y(\tau_{1})\\
\textbf{b}=\textbf{b}_{0}\textbf{b}_{1}\in Y(\tau_{0})\times Y(\tau_{1})\\
\textbf{a}_{0}\to\textbf{a}_{1}\to b\\
\textbf{b}_{0}\to\textbf{b}_{1}\to b\end{subarray}}\int_{\Omega_{b}(h)}\gamma^{\prime}_{\textbf{a}}(z)^{s}\overline{\gamma^{\prime}_{\textbf{b}}(z)^{s}}B_{\Omega(h)}(\gamma_{\textbf{a}}(z),\gamma_{\textbf{b}}(z))\dvol(z).

Now we invoke the separation lemma (Lemma
3.1
). Fix
b\in\mathcal{A}
, a point
z\in D_{b}
, and words
\textbf{a}=\textbf{a}_{0}\textbf{a}_{1},\textbf{b}=\textbf{b}_{0}\textbf{b}_{1}\in Y(\tau_{0})\times Y(\tau_{1})
such that
\textbf{a}_{0}\to\textbf{a}_{1}\to b
and
\textbf{b}_{0}\to\textbf{b}_{1}\to b.
If we take
\tau_{0}=ch
with some sufficiently small
c>0
, then
\textbf{a}_{0}\neq\textbf{b}_{0}
implies that
\gamma_{\textbf{a}}(z)
and
\gamma_{\textbf{b}}(z)
lie in different connected components of
\Omega(h)
. Thus, taking
\tau_{0}=ch
and recalling that
B_{\Omega(h)}(z,w)=0
if
z
and
w
are in different connected components, the above sum reduces to summands with
\textbf{a}_{0}=\textbf{b}_{0}
:

\|\mathcal{L}_{\tau_{0},\tau_{1},s}\|_{\mathrm{HS},h}^{2}=\sum_{b\in\mathcal{A}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\textbf{a}=\textbf{a}_{0}\textbf{a}_{1}\in Y(\tau_{0})\times Y(\tau_{1})\\
\textbf{b}=\textbf{b}_{0}\textbf{b}_{1}\in Y(\tau_{0})\times Y(\tau_{1})\\
\textbf{a}_{0}\to\textbf{a}_{1}\to b\\
\textbf{b}_{0}\to\textbf{b}_{1}\to b\\
\textbf{a}_{0}=\textbf{b}_{0}\end{subarray}}\int_{\Omega_{b}(h)}\gamma^{\prime}_{\textbf{a}}(z)^{s}\overline{\gamma^{\prime}_{\textbf{b}}(z)^{s}}B_{\Omega(h)}(\gamma_{\textbf{a}}(z),\gamma_{\textbf{b}}(z))\dvol(z).

For ease of notation, we re-express this as

(59)

\|\mathcal{L}_{\tau_{0},\tau_{1},s}\|_{\mathrm{HS},h}^{2}=\sum_{b\in\mathcal{A}}\sum_{(\textbf{a},\textbf{b})\in Q_{b}}\int_{\Omega_{b}(h)}\gamma^{\prime}_{\textbf{a}}(z)^{s}\overline{\gamma^{\prime}_{\textbf{b}}(z)^{s}}B_{\Omega(h)}(\gamma_{\textbf{a}}(z),\gamma_{\textbf{b}}(z))\dvol(z),

where for each
b\in\mathcal{A}
the set
Q_{b}
consists of all pairs of words
(\textbf{a},\textbf{b})
satisfying

•

\textbf{a}=\textbf{c}\textbf{a}_{1}
and
\textbf{b}=\textbf{c}\textbf{b}_{1}
where
\textbf{c}\in Y(\tau_{0})
and
\textbf{a}_{1},\textbf{b}_{1}\in Y(\tau_{1})
, and

•

c\to\textbf{a}_{1},\textbf{b}_{1}\to b.

Next we decompose each
Q_{b}
as

Q_{b}=Q_{b}^{(1)}\sqcup Q_{b}^{(2)}

where

Q_{b}^{(1)}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\{(\textbf{a},\textbf{a})\in Q_{b}\},

is the diagonal subset in
Q_{b}
, and
Q_{b}^{(2)}
is the set of pairs
(\textbf{a},\textbf{b})
where
\textbf{a}=\textbf{c}\textbf{a}_{1}
and
\textbf{b}=\textbf{c}\textbf{b}_{1}
satisfy the above conditions and
\textbf{a}_{1}\neq\textbf{b}_{1}
.

We decompose the right hand side of (
59
) accordingly:

\|\mathcal{L}_{\tau_{0},\tau_{1},s}\|_{\mathrm{HS},h}^{2}=\Sigma^{(1)}(s)+\Sigma^{(2)}(s),

where

\displaystyle\Sigma^{(1)}(s)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\sum_{b\in\mathcal{A}}\sum_{(\textbf{a},\textbf{a})\in Q_{b}^{(1)}}\int_{\Omega_{b}(h)}\gamma^{\prime}_{\textbf{a}}(z)^{s}\overline{\gamma^{\prime}_{\textbf{a}}(z)^{s}}B_{\Omega(h)}(\gamma_{\textbf{a}}(z),\gamma_{\textbf{a}}(z))\dvol(z)

\displaystyle=\sum_{b\in\mathcal{A}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\textbf{a}=\textbf{c}\textbf{a}_{1}\in Y_{0}\times Y_{1}\\
\textbf{c}\to\textbf{a}_{1}\to b\end{subarray}}\int_{\Omega_{b}(h)}\gamma^{\prime}_{\textbf{a}}(z)^{s}\overline{\gamma^{\prime}_{\textbf{a}}(z)^{s}}B_{\Omega(h)}(\gamma_{\textbf{a}}(z),\gamma_{\textbf{a}}(z))\dvol(z)

and

\Sigma^{(2)}(s)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\sum_{b\in\mathcal{A}}\sum_{(\textbf{a},\textbf{b})\in Q_{b}^{(2)}}\int_{\Omega_{b}(h)}\gamma^{\prime}_{\textbf{a}}(z)^{s}\overline{\gamma^{\prime}_{\textbf{b}}(z)^{s}}B_{\Omega(h)}(\gamma_{\textbf{a}}(z),\gamma_{\textbf{b}}(z))\dvol(z).

To proceed we need to recall a few estimates for the terms appearing above. By the contraction property (Lemma
2.5
) we have for all
(\textbf{a},\textbf{b})\in Q_{b}
and
z\in D_{b}
:

\mathrm{dist}(\gamma_{\textbf{a}}(z),\partial\Omega(h))\gg h

and

\mathrm{dist}(\gamma_{\textbf{b}}(z),\partial\Omega(h))\gg h.

Combining this with Lemma
2.8
gives

B_{\Omega(h)}(\gamma_{\textbf{a}}(z),\gamma_{\textbf{b}}(z))\leqslant\frac{1}{\pi\mathrm{dist}(\gamma_{\textbf{a}}(z),\partial\Omega(h))\mathrm{dist}(\gamma_{\textbf{b}}(z),\partial\Omega(h))}\ll h^{-2}.

Let
\sigma\in[0,\delta]
and let
s\in\mathbb{C}
with
\mathrm{Re}(s)\geqslant\sigma
and
|\mathrm{Im}(s)|\leqslant K
. By (
25
) there exists
C=C(\Gamma)>0
such that for all
t\in(0,T)
, and for all
b\in\mathcal{A}
and
(\textbf{a},\textbf{b})\in Q_{b}
:

|\gamma^{\prime}_{\textbf{a}}(z)^{s+it}|\ll\Upsilon_{\textbf{a}}^{\sigma}e^{Ch(K+t)}\ll\tau_{0}^{\sigma}\tau_{1}^{\sigma}e^{Ch(K+T)}.

Recalling further that
K=O(T)
and
h=T^{-1}
, this gives

|\gamma^{\prime}_{\textbf{a}}(z)^{s+it}|\ll\tau_{0}^{\sigma}\tau_{1}^{\sigma}.

Similarly, we have

|\gamma^{\prime}_{\textbf{b}}(z)^{s+it}|\ll\tau_{0}^{\sigma}\tau_{1}^{\sigma}.

Furthermore, by Lemma
2.4
the cardinality of the set
Y(\tau)
is bounded by

|Y(\tau)|\asymp\tau^{-\delta},

and by Lemma
2.6
the volume of
\Omega(h)
is bounded by

\vol(\Omega(h))\ll h^{2-\delta}.

Using the triangle inequality and the above estimates, the diagonal term
\Sigma^{(1)}
may be bounded for all
t\in[0,T]
as follows:

\displaystyle\Sigma^{(1)}(s+it)

\displaystyle\ll\sum_{b\in\mathcal{A}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\textbf{a}=\textbf{c}\textbf{a}_{1}\in Y_{0}\times Y_{1}\\
\textbf{c}\to\textbf{a}_{1}\to b\end{subarray}}\int_{\Omega_{b}(h)}|\gamma^{\prime}_{\textbf{a}}(z)^{s}|^{2}|B_{\Omega(h)}(\gamma_{\textbf{a}}(z),\gamma_{\textbf{a}}(z))|\dvol(z)

\displaystyle\ll\sum_{b\in\mathcal{A}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\textbf{a}=\textbf{c}\textbf{a}_{1}\in Y_{0}\times Y_{1}\\
\textbf{c}\to\textbf{a}_{1}\to b\end{subarray}}\tau_{0}^{2\sigma}\tau_{1}^{2\sigma}\vol(\Omega(h))h^{-2}

\displaystyle\ll|Y(\tau_{0})||Y(\tau_{1})|\tau_{0}^{2\sigma}\tau_{1}^{2\sigma}\vol(\Omega(h))h^{-2}

\displaystyle\ll\tau_{0}^{-\delta+2\sigma}\tau_{1}^{-\delta+2\sigma}\vol(\Omega(h))h^{-2}

\displaystyle\ll\tau_{0}^{-\delta+2\sigma}\tau_{1}^{-\delta+2\sigma}h^{-\delta}.

Recalling that
\tau_{0}\asymp h
and
h=T^{-1}
gives

\Sigma^{(1)}(s+it)\ll T^{2\delta-2\sigma}\tau_{1}^{-\delta+2\sigma}

for all
t\in[0,T]
, and consequently

(60)

\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}\Sigma^{(1)}(s+it)dt\ll T^{2\delta-2\sigma}\tau_{1}^{-\delta+2\sigma}.

In order to bound the non-diagonal term
\Sigma^{(2)}(s+it)
, we appeal to the oscillatory integral estimate in Proposition
3.4
. First observe that

\gamma^{\prime}_{\textbf{a}}(z)^{s+it}\overline{\gamma^{\prime}_{\textbf{b}}(z)^{s+it}}=\gamma^{\prime}_{\textbf{a}}(z)^{s}\overline{\gamma^{\prime}_{\textbf{b}}(z)^{s}}e^{it\Phi_{\textbf{a},\textbf{b}}(z)},

where
\Phi_{\textbf{a},\textbf{b}}
is the phase defined in (
35
), so

\Sigma^{(2)}(s+it)=\sum_{b\in\mathcal{A}}\sum_{(\textbf{a},\textbf{b})\in Q_{b}^{(2)}}\int_{\Omega_{b}(h)}\gamma^{\prime}_{\textbf{a}}(z)^{s}\overline{\gamma^{\prime}_{\textbf{b}}(z)^{s}}e^{it\Phi_{\textbf{a},\textbf{b}}(z)}B_{\Omega(h)}(\gamma_{\textbf{a}}(z),\gamma_{\textbf{b}}(z))\dvol(z).

Recall that
Q_{b}^{(2)}
contains all pairs
(\textbf{a},\textbf{b})
of words
\textbf{a}=\textbf{c}\textbf{a}_{1}
and
\textbf{b}=\textbf{c}\textbf{b}_{1}
such that

•

\textbf{c}\in Z(\tau_{0})
and
\textbf{a}_{1},\textbf{b}_{1}\in Y(\tau_{1})
,

•

\textbf{a}_{1}\neq\textbf{b}_{1}
, and

•

\textbf{c}\to\textbf{a}_{1},\textbf{b}_{1}\to b
.

In particular, we have
\textbf{a}\overline{\textbf{b}}=\textbf{c}\textbf{a}_{1}\overline{\textbf{b}}_{1}\overline{\textbf{c}}
and

\textbf{c}\to\textbf{a}_{1}\overline{\textbf{b}}_{1}\to\overline{\textbf{c}}.

Therefore, applying Parts (
vi
) and (
vii
) of Lemma
2.3
gives

•

\Upsilon_{\textbf{a}\overline{\textbf{b}}}=\Upsilon_{\textbf{c}\textbf{a}_{1}\overline{\textbf{b}}_{1}\overline{\textbf{c}}}\asymp\Upsilon_{\textbf{c}}\Upsilon_{\textbf{a}_{1}\overline{\textbf{b}}_{1}}\Upsilon_{\overline{\textbf{c}}}\ll\Upsilon_{\textbf{c}}^{2},

•

\Upsilon_{\textbf{a}}\asymp\Upsilon_{\textbf{c}}\Upsilon_{\textbf{a}_{1}},
and

•

\Upsilon_{\textbf{b}}\asymp\Upsilon_{\textbf{c}}\Upsilon_{\textbf{b}_{1}}.

This implies that

\mathcal{D}_{\textbf{a},\textbf{b}}=\left(\frac{\Upsilon_{\textbf{a}}\Upsilon_{\textbf{b}}}{\Upsilon_{\textbf{a}\overline{\textbf{b}}}}\right)^{1/2}\gg\left(\frac{\Upsilon_{\textbf{c}}^{2}\Upsilon_{\textbf{a}_{1}}\Upsilon_{\textbf{b}_{1}}}{\Upsilon_{\textbf{c}}^{2}}\right)^{1/2}=\Upsilon_{\textbf{a}_{1}}^{1/2}\Upsilon_{\textbf{b}_{1}}^{1/2}\gg\tau_{1}.

Thus, by Proposition
3.4
and the previous estimates, we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}

\displaystyle\Sigma^{(2)}(s+it)dt

\displaystyle\ll\sum_{b\in\mathcal{A}}\sum_{(\textbf{a},\textbf{b})\in Q_{b}^{(2)}}\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}\left|\int_{\Omega_{b}(h)}\gamma^{\prime}_{\textbf{a}}(z)^{s}\overline{\gamma^{\prime}_{\textbf{b}}(z)^{s}}e^{it\Phi_{\textbf{a},\textbf{b}}(z)}B_{\Omega(h)}(\gamma_{\textbf{a}}(z),\gamma_{\textbf{b}}(z))\dvol(z)\right|dt

\displaystyle\ll_{\epsilon}\sum_{b\in\mathcal{A}}\sum_{(\textbf{a},\textbf{b})\in Q_{b}^{(2)}}\mathcal{D}_{\textbf{a},\textbf{b}}^{-1}T^{-1+\epsilon}h^{1-\delta/2}\left\|\gamma^{\prime}_{\textbf{a}}(\cdot)^{s}\overline{\gamma^{\prime}_{\textbf{b}}(\cdot)^{s}}B_{\Omega(h)}(\gamma_{\textbf{a}}(\cdot),\gamma_{\textbf{b}}(\cdot))\right\|_{\infty,D_{b}}

\displaystyle\ll\sum_{(\textbf{a},\textbf{b})\in Q_{b}^{(2)}}\tau_{0}^{2\sigma}\tau_{1}^{2\sigma}\mathcal{D}_{\textbf{a},\textbf{b}}^{-1}T^{-1+\epsilon}h^{-1-\delta/2}

\displaystyle\ll|Q_{b}^{(2)}|\cdot\tau_{0}^{2\sigma}\tau_{1}^{2\sigma}\tau_{1}^{-1}T^{-1+\epsilon}h^{-1-\delta/2}.

The size of
Q_{b}^{(2)}
is bounded by

|Q_{b}^{(2)}|\ll|Y(\tau_{0})|\cdot|Y(\tau_{1})|^{2}\ll\tau_{0}^{-\delta}\tau_{1}^{-2\delta}.

Inserting this into the previous line and using
\tau_{0}\asymp h
and
h=T^{-1}
gives

(61)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}\Sigma^{(2)}(s+it)dt

\displaystyle\ll_{\epsilon}\tau_{0}^{-\delta+2\sigma}\tau_{1}^{-2\delta+2\sigma}\tau_{1}^{-1}T^{-1+\epsilon}h^{-1-\delta/2}

(62)

\displaystyle\ll T^{2\delta-2\sigma+\epsilon}\tau_{1}^{-2\delta+2\sigma}\cdot\tau_{1}^{-1}T^{-\delta/2}.

Putting everything together yields

\displaystyle\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}\|\mathcal{L}_{\tau_{0},\tau_{1},s+it}\|_{\mathrm{HS},h}^{2}dt

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}\Sigma^{(1)}(s+it)dt+\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}\Sigma^{(2)}(s+it)dt

\displaystyle\ll\eqref{final1}+\eqref{final2}

\displaystyle\ll_{\epsilon}T^{2\delta-2\sigma}\tau_{1}^{-\delta+2\sigma}+T^{2\delta-2\sigma+\epsilon}\tau_{1}^{-2\delta+2\sigma}\cdot\tau_{1}^{-1}T^{-\delta/2}

\displaystyle=T^{2\delta-2\sigma+\epsilon}\tau_{1}^{-2\delta+2\sigma}\cdot\left(\tau_{1}^{\delta}+\tau_{1}^{-1}T^{-\delta/2}\right).

It remains to choose
\tau_{1}
optimally. This may be done by taking
\tau_{1}=T^{-\frac{\delta}{2\delta+2}}
, whence

\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}\|\mathcal{L}_{\tau_{0},\tau_{1},s+it}\|_{\mathrm{HS},h}^{2}dt\ll T^{2\delta-2\sigma-\frac{\delta}{2\delta+2}(2\sigma-\delta)}=T^{\delta-\frac{3\delta+2}{2\delta+2}(2\sigma-\delta)}.

This concludes the proof of Proposition
3.9
.
