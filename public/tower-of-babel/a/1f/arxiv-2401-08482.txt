Rate and Bifurcation Induced Transitions in Asymptotically Slow-Fast Systems
S. Jelbart

Abstract
This work provides a geometric approach to the study of bifurcation and rate induced transitions in a class of non-autonomous systems referred to herein as asymptotically slow-fast systems , which may be viewed as ‘intermediate’ between the (smaller resp. larger) classes of asymptotically autonomous and non-autonomous systems. After showing that the relevant systems can be viewed as singular perturbations of a limiting system with a discontinuity in time, we develop an analytical framework for their analysis based on geometric blow-up techniques. We then provide sufficient conditions for the occurrence of bifurcation and rate induced transitions in low dimensions, as well as sufficient conditions for ‘tracking’ in arbitrary (finite) dimensions, i.e. the persistence of an attracting and normally hyperbolic manifold through the transitionary regime. The proofs rely on geometric blow-up, a variant of the Melnikov method which applies on non-compact domains, and general invariant manifold theory. The formalism is applicable in arbitrary (finite) dimensions, and for systems with forward and backward attractors characterised by non-trivial (i.e. non-constant) dependence on time. The results are demonstrated for low dimensional applications.

Introduction

Bifurcation and rate induced transitions in non-autonomous dynamical systems have received a lot of attention in recent years, due in part to the direct relevance of these systems to important social and economic problems like climate change; see e.g.
[
5
,
35
,
36
,
44
,
52
]
and the many references therein. Significant progress has been made for
asymptotically autonomous systems
of the form

x^{\prime}=f(x,\gamma(\mu t),\sigma),\qquad x\in\mathbb{R}^{n},

(1)

where the dash denotes differentiation with respect to time
t
, the function
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}\times\Lambda\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
is smooth,
\sigma\in\Lambda\subset\mathbb{R}^{p}
is a vector of parameters,
\mu>0
is the so-called

and
\gamma:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is a (typically sigmoidal)
C^{1}
ramp (a.k.a. compactification or regularisation) function satisfying

\lim_{s\to\pm\infty}\gamma(s)=\begin{cases}\gamma_{-},&s\to-\infty,\\
\gamma_{+},&s\to\infty,\end{cases}\qquad\lim_{s\to\pm\infty}\gamma^{\prime}(s)=0,

where
\gamma_{\pm}
are constants.
System (
1

t\to-\infty
and
t\to\infty
, namely the systems

x^{\prime}=f(x,\gamma_{-},\sigma),\qquad x^{\prime}=f(x,\gamma_{+},\sigma),

respectively. This feature is crucial for the application of classical dynamical systems methods, e.g. center manifold theory and regular perturbation approaches, which can be applied at infinity after a suitable compactification of time.

\lim_{t\to\pm\infty}g(t)=\pm 1
.
In this context, we refer to
[

5
,

for further details and important examples of bifurcation and rate induced transitions in 1-dimensional systems,
[

for an example of rate induced transitions between non-equilibrium states of the past and future equations,

\sigma

Recently, the authors in
[

,

,

have considered critical transitions in scalar non-autonomous systems of the form

x^{\prime}=f(x,\gamma(\mu t),\sigma,t)

(2)

which, in contrast to the asymptotically autonomous system (
1
), feature past and future limits which depend non-trivially on time as
t\to\pm\infty
(note the additional dependence on
t
in the fourth argument). The additional generality is important for applications which exhibit non-trivial time-dependence before and/or after the ‘tipping event’. More generally, these works show that a wide variety
of

[
2

and the many references therein for background. Detailed analytical treatment of problems within this class remains a highly non-trivial task, and current analyses in this setting have, to the best of the authors knowledge, only been undertaken for scalar equations with additional specific structure. This is due, in part, to the fact that powerful dynamical systems methods that have been developed for autonomous systems cannot in general be applied here,
in contrast to asymptotically autonomous systems (
1
) described above.

Thus, there is a tension between analytical tractability and sufficient generality. A primary aim of this work, is to demonstrate that a compromise which balances tractability with generality can be found by considering an ‘intermediate’ class of non-autonomous systems in the general form

x^{\prime}=f(x,\gamma(\mu t),\sigma,\varepsilon t,\varepsilon),

(3)

where
0<\varepsilon\ll 1
is a small perturbation parameter. System (
3
) defines a class of dynamical systems which is strictly larger than the class of asymptotically non-autonomous systems (
1
), but strictly smaller than the class of non-autonomous systems (
2
). We shall hereafter refer to systems of the form (
3
) as
asymptotically slow-fast systems
, since it can be shown that the forward and backward limits in time are described by slow-fast systems.
2
2

2

This includes but is not limited to the class of slow-fast asymptotically autonomous systems of the form
x^{\prime}=f(x,\gamma(\mu t),\sigma,\varepsilon)
, for which the dynamics in the inner ‘transitionary regime’ is itself slow-fast as
\varepsilon\to 0
; see e.g.
[

for a recent application within this class.
The time-scale separation is a consequence of the fact that the ‘transition time-scale’

\gamma(\mu t)

\gamma(\mu t)\sim\gamma_{-}

\gamma(\mu t)\sim\gamma_{+}

) we can both (i) incorporate non-trivial time-dependence as
t\to\infty
, and (ii) use established and powerful theory for slow-fast systems

,
23
,

,
50
,
55

as a rigorous and geometrically informative approach to understanding the past and future dynamics. In this context, we also refer to
[
51
]
for a detailed exposition on the use of
geometric singular perturbation theory (GSPT)
to study slowly non-autonomous systems without a ramp (i.e. in systems (
1
) with no
\gamma(\mu t)
-dependence), and to
[
31
,
32
]
for more on the applicability of GSPT in the study of (bifurcation and noise induced) critical transitions.

The primary aim of this work is to make the first steps in the development of a geometric framework for the study of asymptotically slow-fast systems (
3
). An important feature of these systems is that they can be shown to converge to systems that are
piecewise-smooth (PWS)
in time as
\varepsilon\to 0
. In essence, the time-scale associated with the transition from
\gamma(\mu t)\sim\gamma_{-}
to
\gamma(\mu t)\sim\gamma_{+}
becomes instantaneous as
\varepsilon\to 0
, and we are left with distinct slow-fast systems on either side of the transition which correspond to the backward and forward limits in time. Of course, this is a very singular limit. We then use the
geometric blow-up method
in order to resolve the loss of smoothness as
\varepsilon\to 0
. This is based on an approach which has been developed in recent years for the study of both regularised PWS systems and smooth systems which converge to PWS systems, see e.g.
[
7
,
19
,
20
,
21
,
25
,
26
,
27
,
37
]
. Here it
is
worthy to note that the blow-up transformation is used to resolve a loss of smoothness, and not
a
loss of hyperbolicity as in the well-known references
[
12
,
28
,
29
,
30
,
46
,
47
]
. In the context of asymptotically slow-fast systems (
1
), we shall show that the geometric blow-up naturally incorporates a compactification of the transitionary regime which is similar to that developed in
[
6
,
53
,
54
]
, as well as providing a geometric way of connecting or ‘matching’ to the outer dynamics of the limiting systems in forward and backward time.

We shall use this approach in order to state sufficient conditions for the transversal intersection of 1-dimensional
locally
invariant manifolds which, for scalar systems (
1
), correspond to either bifurcation or rate induced transitions.
Typically, these 1-dimensional manifolds are obtained as
slow manifolds
in the outer regimes, and subsequently extended into the inner regime. After blow-up, the problem of identifying intersections of these manifolds becomes very similar to the problem of identifying
canard solutions
in classical slow-fast systems
[
8
,
12
,
28
,
30
,
46
,
49
]
(where canards arise due to the intersection of attracting and repelling slow manifolds)
, except in the context of systems with a PWS singular limit. A clear analogy to the well-known scenario involving heteroclinic connections in 1-dimensional asymptotically autonomous systems (
1
) appears (see e.g.
[
4
,
34
]
), insofar as intersections correspond to the transversal breaking of heteroclinic solutions identified in a nearby (but auxiliary) system which is defined on the blow-up surface. Although we are able to state the result for arbitrary dimensions, ‘tipping’ usually only occurs if the aforementioned heteroclinic connection is codimension-1, i.e. if it forms a separatrix. In addition to geometric blow-up, our primary tool for the proof is the variant of Melnikov theory which was developed for problems on non-compact domains in
[
48
]
; see also
[
29
]
for an earlier formulations in low dimensions which is surprisingly relevant in our setting.

Finally, we provide sufficient conditions for ‘tracking’ or, more precisely, the persistence of an attracting normally hyperbolic manifold through the transitionary regime. It is worthy to note that although the problem is slow-fast on either side of the transitionary regime, Fenichel theory cannot be applied in a neighbourhood of the transition itself due to the loss of smoothness there as
\varepsilon\to 0
. For this reason, we appeal to geometric blow-up techniques together with the more the general invariant manifold theory used in
[
42
]
(this is the formulation that we use, however see
[
17
,
45
]
for earlier work and additional references).

2
Setup and assumptions

We consider ODE systems of the form

x^{\prime}=\frac{dx}{dt}=f\left(x,\gamma(\mu t),\varepsilon t,\sigma,\varepsilon\right),

(4)

where
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}\times\Lambda\times{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}[0,\varepsilon_{0})}}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
is
C^{k}
-smooth (
k
will be assumed to be sufficiently large throughout),
\mu\in\Theta\subset\mathbb{R}
is a positive ‘rate parameter’,
\sigma\in\Lambda\subset\mathbb{R}
is a bifurcation parameter,
\Lambda
and
\Theta
are bounded open intervals and
\varepsilon\in[0,\varepsilon_{0})
is a small time-scale parameter. Generalisations to the multi-parameter case with
\sigma\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
where
p>1
are straightforward but omitted for simplicity. The function
\gamma:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is a bounded and
C^{k}
function which satisfies

\gamma(z)\to\begin{cases}1^{-}&\text{as }z\to\infty,\\
0^{+}&\text{as }z\to-\infty,\end{cases}

(5)

with

\gamma(-z^{-1})=O(z^{l}),\qquad\gamma(z^{-1})=1+O(z^{l}),

(6)

as
z\to 0^{+}
for some positive integer
l
. We take a dynamical systems approach and rewrite (
4
) as an autonomous system in
n+1
variables by defining

s:=\varepsilon t,\qquad 0<\varepsilon\ll 1,

(7)

and considering the system

\begin{split}x^{\prime}&=f\left(x,\gamma\left(\mu s/\varepsilon\right),s,\sigma,\varepsilon\right),\\
s^{\prime}&=\varepsilon,\end{split}

(8)

with
\varepsilon\in(0,\varepsilon_{0})
.
The key observation at this point is that system (
8
) is a slow-fast system with a PWS singular limit. Specifically, taking
\varepsilon\to 0
leads to

\begin{pmatrix}x^{\prime}\\
s^{\prime}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{cases}\begin{pmatrix}f_{-}(x,s,\sigma)\\
0\end{pmatrix}&\quad\text{for}\quad s<0,\\
\begin{pmatrix}f_{+}(x,s,\sigma)\\
0\end{pmatrix}&\quad\text{for}\quad s>0,\end{cases}

(9)

where we defined
f_{-}(x,s,\sigma):=f(x,0,s,\sigma,0)
and
f_{+}(x,s,\sigma):=f(x,1,s,\sigma,0)
. For a generic right-hand side, system (
9
) is discontinuous along a codimension-1 (
n
-dimensional)
switching manifold

\Sigma:=\left\{(x,0):x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\right\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n+1}.

Notice that
s\in\mathbb{R}
is a parameter for the limiting PWS system (
9
). Generically, if the 1-parameter families of vector fields defined by
x^{\prime}=f_{\pm}(x,s,\sigma)
have isolated equilibria in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, then system (
9
) can be expected to posses a union of 1-dimensional
critical manifolds
in
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
. We shall introduce a separate notation for critical manifolds on
\{s<0\}
and
\{s>0\}
, namely

\begin{split}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{C}^{-}}}&:=\left\{(x,s)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}_{-}:f_{-}(x,s,\sigma)=0\right\},\\
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{C}^{+}}}&:=\left\{(x,s)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}:f_{+}(x,s,\sigma)=0\right\},\end{split}

(10)

respectively, where
\mathbb{R}_{+}:=[0,\infty)
and
\mathbb{R}_{-}:=(-\infty,0]
(note that we include their extension up to
s=0
). An example of one possible limiting situation is sketched in Figure
1
.

Remark 2.1
.

The asymptotics in (
6
) imply that

x^{\prime}=f(x,\gamma(\mu s/\varepsilon),s,\sigma,\varepsilon)=\begin{cases}f_{-}(x,s,\sigma)+O(\varepsilon)&\text{as }\varepsilon\to 0\text{ on }\{s<0\},\\
f_{+}(x,s,\sigma)+O(\varepsilon)&\text{as }\varepsilon\to 0\text{ on }\{s>0\},\end{cases}

(11)

which leads to

\begin{pmatrix}x^{\prime}\\
s^{\prime}\end{pmatrix}\sim\begin{cases}\begin{pmatrix}f_{-}(x,s,\sigma)+{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}O(\varepsilon)}},\\
\varepsilon\end{pmatrix},&s<0,\\
\begin{pmatrix}f_{+}(x,s,\sigma)+{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}O(\varepsilon)}},\\
\varepsilon\end{pmatrix},&s>0,\end{cases}

as
\varepsilon\to 0
. This representation highlights the fact that the asymptotic dynamics on either side of
\Sigma
are slow-fast, which motivates the asymptotically slow-fast terminology.

The preceding observations show that the limit as
\varepsilon\to 0
is singular in two ways: Firstly, the equations on
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}\setminus\Sigma
are slow-fast. Secondly, the non-smooth limit indicates that an abrupt transition occurs as solutions traverse an
\varepsilon
-dependent neighbourhood of
\Sigma
. This transition is condensed into
\Sigma
in the limit
\varepsilon\to 0
. Although it appears as a loss of smoothness along
\Sigma
when
\varepsilon\to 0
, it is worthy to note that this highly singular feature is actually a natural consequence of the time-scale separation which is inherent in equations (
4
) and (
8
). Specifically, it follows from the assumption that the transition occurs on a time-scale
t
which is fast relative to the variation which occurs with time-scale
\varepsilon t
(cf. the second and third arguments of
f
in equation (
4
)). This leads to three distinct regimes:

•

A
left-outer regime
bounded within
\{s<0\}
;

•

An
inner/transitionary regime

\{s=O(\varepsilon)\}
;

•

A
right-outer regime
bounded within
\{s>0\}
.

Since
s^{\prime}>0
for all
\varepsilon>0
, solutions with initial conditions in the left-outer regime traverse the inner transitionary regime before passing into the right-outer regime.

Remark 2.2
.

Generically, the critical manifolds
\mathcal{C}^{\pm}
are 1-dimensional. Higher dimensional critical manifolds can be expected in applications if the vector fields
f_{\pm}
are themselves slow-fast. For example, if
x=(x_{1},x_{2})\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}}\times\mathbb{R}^{n_{2}}
and

f_{-}(x,s,\sigma)=\begin{pmatrix}h(x_{1},x_{2},s,\sigma)\\

for some smooth function

, then
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{C}^{-}}}
is defined by the condition
h(x_{1},x_{2},s,\sigma)=0
and is, therefore,
(n_{2}+1)
-dimensional. In this work we focus on the generic situation of 1-dimensional critical manifolds
\mathcal{C}^{\pm}
, but we expect the methods and formalism to extend naturally to the higher dimensional setting.

We now introduce an assumption on the existence and stability of the critical manifolds
\mathcal{C}^{\pm}
close to
\Sigma
. It can be viewed as a kind of regularity or genericity condition.

Assumption 1
.

The intersections
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{C}^{\pm}}}\cap\Sigma
are non-empty for all
\sigma\in\Lambda
. In particular, we have

p_{-}:(x_{-},0)\in{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{C}^{-}}}\cap\Sigma,\qquad p_{+}:(x_{+},0)\in{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{C}^{+}}}\cap\Sigma,

where
x_{\pm}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Moreover, the eigenvalues of the

matrices

and

.

Remark 2.3
.

For many problems it is sufficient to verify Assumption
1
for a specific
\sigma
and subsequently choose
\Lambda
small enough to ensure that it holds for all
\sigma\in\Lambda
.

Given Assumption

, a direct application of the implicit function theorem shows that
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{C}^{-}}}
(
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{C}^{+}}}

(

) which intersects

(

and two smooth (parameter-dependent) functions

\mathcal{S}^{-}=\left\{(h^{-}(s,\sigma),s):s\in[-\rho,0]\right\},\qquad\mathcal{S}^{+}=\left\{(h^{+}(s,\sigma),s):s\in[0,\rho]\right\}.

(12)

For
\rho>0
sufficiently small, Assumption
1
guarantees that
\mathcal{S}^{-}\setminus\Sigma
(
\mathcal{S}^{+}\setminus\Sigma
) is normally hyperbolic, with the same ‘stability type’ for all

(

). The reduced flow on both
\mathcal{S}^{\pm}
(and on all other branches of
\mathcal{C}^{\pm}
) can be expressed in the

-coordinate chart; it has the simple form

\dot{s}=1,

where the dot denotes differentiation with respect to the slow time
\tau=\varepsilon t=s
. Thus in particular, the reduced flow on
\mathcal{S}^{-}
approaches
\Sigma
from the left, while the reduced flow on
\mathcal{S}^{+}
is oriented away from
\Sigma
.

Remark 2.4
.

Fenichel theory applies to compact normally hyperbolic submanifolds of
\mathcal{C}^{\pm}
which are bounded away from
\Sigma
, but it cannot be applied in a neighbourhood of
\Sigma
because of the loss of smoothness there as

.

Remark 2.5
.

If
\mathcal{S}^{-}
(
\mathcal{S}^{+}
) are normally hyperbolic and attracting for all

(

), then the nearby Fenichel slow manifold constitutes a pullback (pushforward) attractor of system (

One of the following assertions is true:

(I)

The blow-up analysis in Section

3
Main results

We are now in a position to state and describe our main results. We present (i) results on bifurcation and rate induced transitions, and (ii) sufficient conditions for normally hyperbolic connections from
s<0
to
s>0
(in which case no such transition can occur). We begin with the former.

3.1
Bifurcation and rate induced transitions

Our main results on bifurcation and rate induced transitions will be formulated as sufficient conditions for the intersection of distinguished locally invariant manifolds which perturb from
\mathcal{S}^{\pm}
under variation in
\mu
or
\sigma
. We begin with a simple existence statement for these manifolds, deferring the detailed statement, which is formulated in the blown-up space, to Section
4
.

Lemma 3.1
.

Consider system (
8
) under Assumption
1
. There is an
\varepsilon_{0}>0
such that for all
\varepsilon\in(0,\varepsilon_{0})
, there exists 1-dimensional locally invariant manifolds
\mathcal{S}^{\pm}_{\varepsilon}
such that
\mathcal{S}^{-}_{\varepsilon}|_{s\in[-\rho,0)}\to\mathcal{S}^{-}
and
\mathcal{S}^{+}_{\varepsilon}|_{s\in(0,\rho]}\to\mathcal{S}^{+}
in the
C^{k}
metric as
\varepsilon\to 0
.

The existence and properties of the manifolds
\mathcal{S}^{\pm}_{\varepsilon}
can be shown directly in the blown-up space using center manifold theory; we refer to Lemmas
4.3
and
4.5
for a more detailed formulation and proof.
In the following, we say that a bifurcation (rate) induced transition occurs if there exists a critical value
\sigma=\sigma_{c}
(
\mu=\mu_{c}
) such that the asymptotics of the forward extension of
\mathcal{S}^{-}_{\varepsilon}

6
Application to scalar asymptotically slow-fast equations

In the following we demonstrate our results in the context of scalar asymptotically slow-fast equations of the form

x^{\prime}=\gamma(\mu t)F_{+}(x)+\left(1-\gamma(\mu t)\right)F_{-}(x)+g(\gamma(\mu t),\varepsilon t,\sigma,\varepsilon),

7
Summary and outlook

The increased attention to the study of bifurcation and rate induced tipping in non-autonomous dynamical systems may be attributed to prevalence of ‘tipping phenomena’ in important complex systems including the Earth’s climate system
[
5
,
35
,
36
,
44
,
52
]
, and detailed mathematical approaches to the study of bifurcation and rate induced transitions have been undertaken in the context of asymptotically autonomous systems of the general form (
1
). A major analytical advantage of restricting to asymptotically autonomous systems is the availability of classical dynamical systems methods,
following a suitable compactification procedure which may be applied to an extended autonomous system

[
6
,
53
,
54
]
. Unfortunately, despite significant analytical advantages, these approaches cannot account for non-trivial dependence on time as
t\to\pm\infty
, which may be restrictive in applications. In contrast, the class of ‘truly non-autonomous’ systems of the form (
2
) is much larger, but systems within this class can be extremely challenging to analyse, with existing works showing that detailed analytical treatment can be incredibly intricate even for scalar equations with a particular structure
[
9
,
10
,
11
,
38
,
39
,
40
]
.

In this work, we developed and applied a mathematical framework for the study of asymptotically slow-fast systems in the general form (
3
). These systems form a class which is strictly larger than the class of asymptotically autonomous systems (
1
), but strictly smaller than the class of non-autonomous systems (
2
). In Section
2
, we showed that the past and future subsystems of these systems are slow-fast, with 1-dimensional critical manifolds
\mathcal{C}^{\pm}
. In addition to the slow-fast structure in the ‘outer regimes’ where
|s|>0
, the limit as
\varepsilon\to 0
leads to the loss of smoothness as the entire inner/transitionary regime for which
s=O(\varepsilon)
is condensed into a codimension-1 switching manifold
\Sigma=\{s=0\}
. In Section
4
, we showed that the loss of smoothness along
\Sigma
can be resolved via geometric blow-up. A primary objective of this work has been to show that the blow-up method provides a natural geometric approach to the study of asymptotically slow-fast systems. In particular, it provides a rigorous and geometric way of connecting the limiting dynamics on the ‘compactified’ inner regime, which is governed by an asymptotically autonomous system that is constrained to the surface of the blow-up cylinder, to the limiting slow-fast systems which govern the dynamics in the outer regimes with
s<0
and
s>0
. It is also worthy to note that the blow-up procedure itself does not rely on any of the Assumptions
1
-
3
.

In order to demonstrate the utility of the formalism, we used it to prove two main results, both of which were stated in Section
3
. The first of these, Theorem
3.4
, provides sufficient conditions for canard-type intersection of forward and backward extensions of 1-dimensional slow manifolds
\mathcal{S}^{-}_{\varepsilon}
and
\mathcal{S}^{+}_{\varepsilon}
, for locally unique parameter values (the rate parameter
\mu
in the case of Assertions 1-2, and the bifurcation parameter
\sigma
in the case of Assertions 3-4). For scalar asymptotically slow-fast equations these intersections form separatrices, and the fact that they break regularly under parameter variation means that we have identified a mechanism for rate and bifurcation induced transitions. Importantly, Theorem
3.4
also provides a means for
calculating
the critical values, at least to leading order in
\varepsilon
. In the case of Assertions 1 and 3, a critical value
\mu_{c}
or
\sigma_{c}
corresponds to a simple zero of the Melnikov function (
15
). In the case of Assertion 2 or 4 a critical value
\mu_{c}
or
\sigma_{c}
corresponds to the existence of a particular solution of the inner limiting problem (
13
) which satisfies Assumption
2
(II) or Assumption
2
(III) respectively. Our second main result is Theorem
3.7
, which provides sufficient conditions for the persistence of an attracting normally hyperbolic manifold through the transitionary regime. In essence, this result provides conditions under which the extension of an attracting slow manifold in the left-outer regime can be ‘connected’ to an attracting slow manifold in the right-outer regime, while maintaining uniform attractivity in the transitionary regime. The blow-up analysis in Section
4
played a crucial role in the proof of both Theorems
3.4
and
3.7
, which appeared in Sections
5.1
and
5.2
respectively. In addition to the blow-up analysis, we relied quite heavily on the variant of Melnikov theory developed for the study of heteroclinic-like intersections of invariant manifolds on non-compact domains in
[
48
]
to prove Theorem
3.4
, and general invariant manifold theory, in particular
the
formulation in
[
42
]
, to prove Theorem
3.7
. As with the blow-up approach, these are well-developed methods which can be applied in rather general contexts.

Finally, we applied our results to examples of scalar asymptotically slow-fast systems in Section
6
. We used Theorem
3.4
to identify a bifurcation and rate induced transitions, and Theorem
3.7
in order to verify and describe the persistence of an attracting and normally hyperbolic locally invariant manifold through the inner transitionary regime.

The primary aim of this work has been to lay the foundations for a rigorous and geometric approach to the study of asymptotically slow-fast systems. Based on this foundation, we anticipate a lot of open problems and possibilities for future research. At this point, for example, we have not yet treated any applications (with the exception of the simple examples in Section
6
). We have also restricted our analysis to spatially homogeneous and deterministic systems, which rules out the possibility of spatially and noise induced transitions. The question of whether the formalism developed herein can be extended to the study of asymptotically slow-fast SDE’s and asymptotically slow-fast PDE’s is entirely open, albeit non-trivial, due in part to the fact that the geometric blow-up method needs further development in these areas (see however
[
3
]
and
[
13
,
14
,
18
,
22
,
56
]
for recent progress in the context of asymptotically autonomous systems and PDEs respectively). At present, these and other open problems remain for future work.
