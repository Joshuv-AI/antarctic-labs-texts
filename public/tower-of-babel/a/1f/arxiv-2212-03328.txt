\left\{-1,1\right\}^{n}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}

\sum_{i=1}^{n}|x_{i}-y_{i}|=2

A hyperplane dissects an edge if it intersects the edge, but does not contain any of its endpoints. In such a case we also say that the edge crosses the hyperplane. One may relax the notion to allow the hyperplane to include at most one of the endpoints – our result will carry over and incur a multiplicative constant degradation.

H(n)\leq\lceil\frac{5n}{6}\rceil
. On the lower bound front, each hyperplane can dissect at most
\lceil n/2\rceil\cdot\binom{n}{\lceil n/2\rceil}

In this paper we prove the following.

1.1
Proof overview

The lower bound
H(n)\geq\Omega(\sqrt{n})
proceeds by showing each hyperplane dissects at most
O(1/\sqrt{n})
of the available edges in
Q_{n}
.

Example of middle layers.

In fact, once we surpass
O(\sqrt{n})
hyperplanes, the planes can dissect almost all edges. To see this, partition all edges of the hypercube into
n+1
layers, the
k
’th of which connects vertices of Hamming weight
k
to those with weight
k+1
. Each such layer is dissected by a hyperplane orthogonal to the vector
(1,\ldots,1)
. Most edges in the hypercube are contained in the
O(\sqrt{n})
middle layers, thus a uniformly random edge is likely to cross some hyperplane in a fixed set of
O(\sqrt{n})
planes.

Question
.

Is there a (natural) distribution over edges which is likely to
evade
(i.e. not cross) a set of
m
hyperplanes?

In our example, the answer is positive – choose a uniformly random layer in
Q_{n}
(out of
n
), and draw a random edge
E
within that layer. What about a general configuration of planes?

Setting.

Assume
m
hyperplanes are given, with the
\ell
’th hyperplane specified by

H_{\ell}=\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\,\middle|\,\langle{v_{\ell}},{x}\rangle=t_{\ell}\right\},

(1)

with
v_{\ell}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
having
\left\|{v_{\ell}}\right\|_{2}=1
and
t_{\ell}\in\mathbb{R}
.

Middle layers revisited.

We present a different view for why in the example of the middle layers, the random edge
E
is likely to evade a small set of hyperplanes having
v_{\ell}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}(1,\ldots,1)
.
When projecting the edge
E
on
v_{\ell}
, we produce an interval of length
2/\sqrt{n}
that is located anywhere on
(-\sqrt{n},\sqrt{n})\cdot v_{\ell}
equally likely. Meanwhile each layer
H_{\ell}
projected on
v_{\ell}
is a single point. Hence
\Omega(n)
hyperplanes with
v_{\ell}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}(1,\ldots,1)
are required in order to dissect all edges.

The general case.

In general, projecting an edge on
v_{\ell}
results in an interval of length
\leq 2\left\|{v_{\ell}}\right\|_{\infty}
parallel to
v_{\ell}
. Assuming
\left\|{v_{\ell}}\right\|_{\infty}=\widetilde{O}(1/\sqrt{n})
(which holds for a uniformly random vector
v_{\ell}
with norm
1
), we have that each edge corresponds to an interval of length
\widetilde{O}(1/\sqrt{n})
on
v_{\ell}
, while
H_{\ell}
is projected to a point. Hence, if we are able to produce a random edge whose projection on any
v_{\ell}
has a significant variance – it will likely evade every
H_{\ell}
.

The distribution.

Keeping the assumption
\left\|{v_{\ell}}\right\|_{\infty}=\widetilde{O}(1/\sqrt{n})
for the
m
hyperplanes, let

P=\sum_{\ell=1}^{m}\alpha_{\ell}\sqrt{\frac{n}{m}}v_{\ell},

with
\alpha_{\ell}\sim U([-1,1])
independently. Note that each coordinate
P_{i}
has
\be[P_{i}]=0
and
\var[P_{i}]=(n/3m)\sum_{\ell=1}^{m}v_{\ell i}^{2}=\widetilde{O}(1)
. Hence we (informally) imagine
P
as a vertex in
\left\{-1,1\right\}^{n}
. Moreover, the standard deviation of the (norm of the) projection of
P
on
v_{\ell}
is at least
\Omega(\sqrt{n/m})
.
Considering a uniformly random edge incident to
P
, the probability that the projection of this edge on
v_{\ell}
(which has length
\widetilde{O}(1/\sqrt{n})
) crosses the projection of
H_{\ell}
on
v_{\ell}
(which is a single point), is about
(1/\sqrt{n})/\sqrt{n/m}=\sqrt{m}/n
. Since there are
m
hyperplanes, and we wish the edge to evade all of them, it is sufficient that
m\cdot\sqrt{m}/n\ll 1
, or in other words,
m\ll n^{2/3}
.

Getting rid of the assumption.

Although abandoning the assumption
\left\|{v_{\ell}}\right\|_{\infty}=\widetilde{O}(1/\sqrt{n})
introduces a few technicalities in the proof, it does not severely affect the general approach.
We get
v_{\ell}
decomposed into different groups of coordinates, such that
v_{\ell}
is roughly constant on each group. We call this process a ‘binary decomposition’. Then, we slightly alter the definition of
P
and the rest of the proof generalizes smoothly.

Organization.

In Sections
2.1
-
2.2
we define a distribution over edges. Sections
2.3
-
2.4
introduce linear forms and some of their anti-concentration properties. Finally, Section
2.5
shows the distribution evades the hyperplanes. Appendix
A
contains proofs of standard facts that are used in the paper.

2.1
A random bounded vector correlating with all
v_{\ell}

The proof overview in Section
1.1
assumes that
\left\|{v_{\ell}}\right\|_{\infty}
is small. The following definition is useful in order to deal with general vectors
v_{\ell}
.

Definition 2.1

(Vector Binary Decomposition)
.

Let
v\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. For an integer
j
, let
K(j)
be the set of coordinates of
v
whose absolute value is in the range
(2^{-j-1},2^{-j}]
, that is

K(j)=\left\{k\in[n]\,\middle|\,2^{-j-1}<|v_{k}|\leq 2^{-j}\right\}.

Let
J
be these
j
’s for which
K(j)
is nonempty. The Binary decomposition of
v
is

v=\sum_{j\in J}v^{(j)},

where
v^{(j)}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
aggregates all coordinates
K(j)
, that is

v^{(j)}_{k}=v_{k}\cdot\boldsymbol{1}\left\{k\in K(j)\right\}.

Definition 2.2

(The Random Bias)
.

Let
v_{1},\ldots,v_{m}
be vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
having
\left\|{v_{\ell}}\right\|_{2}=1
for all
\ell
.
Let
v_{\ell}=\sum_{j\in J_{\ell}}v_{\ell}^{(j)}
be the Binary decomposition of
v_{\ell}
, according to Definition
2.1
.
Define the random variable

P\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\frac{1}{10\sqrt{m\log(n)}}\sum_{\ell=1}^{m}\sum_{j\in J_{\ell}}\alpha_{\ell j}2^{j}v_{\ell}^{(j)}

with
\alpha_{\ell j}\sim U([-1,1])
uniformly and independently distributed in the interval
[-1,1]
.

We will use
P
in order to produce a product distribution over
\left\{-1,1\right\}^{n}
which has mean
P
. For the sake of this distribution not to be too biased, we note that with high probability
\|{P}\|_{\infty}\leq 1/2
; the simple proof appears in Appendix
A
.

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
P
be distributed as in Definition
2.2
, then

\Pr[\|{P}\|_{\infty}>1/2]\leq 2/n,

where the randomness is taken over the distribution of
\alpha_{\ell j}
’s.

2.2
Hyperplane-evasive edges

Definition 2.4
.

Given a vector
p\in[-1,1]^{n}
, we define a probability distribution
\mu_{p}
on vectors
z\in\left\{-1,1\right\}^{n}
, by letting
z_{i}
be independent random variables with

\Pr[z_{i}=1]=\frac{1+p_{i}}{2},\qquad\Pr[z_{i}=-1]=\frac{1-p_{i}}{2}.

We now define a distribution on edges of
Q_{n}
which are likely to evade every
H_{\ell}
.

Definition 2.5
.

Let
H_{i},v_{i},t_{i}
be as in Section
1.1
,
and let
P
be a random variable which is distributed according to Definition
2.2
, conditioned on that
\|{P}\|_{\infty}\leq 1/2
.

Let
U
be a random vertex of
Q_{n}
drawn from
\mu_{P}
, and let
k\sim[n]
be uniformly distributed. We define
(U,k)
as the edge which is incident to
U
and is parallel to the
k
’th axis.

Our main technical result is the following proposition, which states that the probability of the edge
(U,k)
to cross any of the hyperplanes
H_{i}
is small.

Proposition 2.6
.

Let
(U,k)
be distributed as in Definition
2.5
. Then for all
\ell
,

\Pr[(U,k)\text{ crosses }H_{\ell}]\leq O\left(\sqrt{m}\log(n)^{2}/n\right).

(2)

Corollary 2.7

(Restatement of Theorem
1.1
)
.

H(n)\geq\Omega(n^{2/3}/\log(n)^{4/3}).

Proof.

Suppose the hyperplanes
H_{1},\ldots,H_{m}
from (
1
) dissect all edges of
Q_{n}
. Since
(U,k)
is a distribution over edges of
Q_{n}
, we have
\Pr[\exists i\colon(U,k)\text{ crosses }H_{i}]=1
.
However, by union bound and Proposition
2.6
,

\Pr[\exists i\colon(U,k)\text{ crosses }H_{i}]\leq\sum_{i=1}^{m}\Pr[(U,k)\text{ crosses }H_{i}]\leq O\left(m^{3/2}\log(n)^{2}/n\right).

This yields
m\geq\Omega(n^{2/3}/\log(n)^{4/3})
.
∎

We prove Proposition
2.6
using anti-concentration techniques. In order to phrase our result in a probabilistic setting, we use the following claim.

Claim 2.8
.

In order for an edge
(u,k)
to cross the hyperplane
H=\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\,\middle|\,\langle{v},{x}\rangle=t\right\}
we must have

|\langle{v},{u}\rangle-t|<2|v_{k}|.

(3)

Proof.

Let
u^{\prime}
be the vertex connected to
u
by
(u,k)
. That is,
u^{\prime}
is either
u+2e_{k}
or
u-2e_{k}
. In order for the edge
(u,k)
to cross
\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\,\middle|\,\langle{v},{x}\rangle=t\right\}
we must have that
\langle{v},{u}\rangle-t
and
\langle{v},{u^{\prime}}\rangle-t
have different signs (and are nonzero). Since
\langle{v},{u^{\prime}}\rangle=\langle{v},{u\pm 2e_{k}}\rangle=\langle{v},{u}\rangle\pm 2v_{k}
, the edge
(u,k)
may cross
H
only if (
3
) holds.
∎

Anti-concentration of linear forms

Definition 2.11

(Lévy Concentration function)
.

Let
X
be a random variable. It’s

Q(\alpha,X)\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}\Pr[|X-t|<\alpha].

We need a basic property of concentration functions.

Claim 2.12
.

Every random variable
X
and integer
k\geq 1

Q(k\alpha,X)\leq kQ(\alpha,X)

Proof.

Follows by (almost) covering an interval of length

Next, we need a simple Littlewood-Offord-type result regarding the anti-concentration of a biased linear form

Lemma 2.13
.

There is a universal constant

.
Suppose that

Lemma 2.14
.

Let

\|{p}\|_{\infty}\leq 1/2
.
Suppose

has a binary decomposition

v=\sum_{j\in J}v^{(j)},

with the corresponding partition of the nonzero coordinates as
\bigcup_{j\in J}K(j)
.
Let
\alpha>0

1.

Let

be the universal constant from Lemma

, then

Q(\alpha,X_{v,p})\leq 2^{-r}.

(5)

2.

For all

(6)

Proof.

The proof of (

) is by induction on

Let
L=\lceil 4C^{2}\rceil
and

be the

. Denote

Claim 2.15
.

There are at least

.

To prove Claim

, observe that

. Including

, there are at most

, we have

, thus establishing Claim

\hfill\blacksquare

Given a decomposition

we have
X_{v,p}=X_{u,p}+X_{w,p}
. We set

u\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\sum_{j\in J\colon j<j_{1}}v^{(j)},\qquad w\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\sum_{j\in J\colon j\geq j_{1}}v^{(j)}.

Since
u
and

We use the induction hypothesis (

We immediately complete the inductive proof of (

Q(\alpha,X_{v,p})\leq\frac{1}{2}Q(2\alpha^{\prime},X_{u,p})

In order to prove Claim

\Pr[|X_{v,p}-t|<\alpha]\leq\frac{1}{2}\Pr[|X_{u,p}-t|<2\alpha^{\prime}].

Indeed, note that by definition of

(7)

). It remains to verify (
11
) and (
12
).

Proving (
11
).

Recall
E
is the union of the two events
E_{1}
and
E_{2}
. Under
E_{1}
we actually have the stronger

\Pr\left[|\langle{v},{x}\rangle-t|<2|v_{k}|\,\middle|\,E_{1}\right]=0.

(15)

Indeed, if
E_{1}
happens then

\displaystyle\left|\langle{v},{x}\rangle-t\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\langle{v-v^{(i)}},{x}\rangle+\langle{v^{(i)}},{x}\rangle-t\right|

\displaystyle\geq\left|\langle{v-v^{(i)}},{x}\rangle-t\right|-\left|\langle{v^{(i)}},{x}\rangle\right|

\displaystyle\geq 3\|{v^{(i)}}\|_{1}-\|{v^{(i)}}\|_{1}

\displaystyle=2\|{v^{(i)}}\|_{1}\geq 2|v_{1k}|,

giving (
15
). We now prove (
11
) with
E_{2}
instead of
E
. If
E_{2}
happens, then
|\langle{v},{x}\rangle-t|<2|v_{k}|
means

\displaystyle\left|\langle{v^{(i)}},{P}\rangle-\langle{v^{(i)}},{x}\rangle\right|

\displaystyle\geq\left|\langle{v^{(i)}},{P}\rangle+\langle{v-v^{(i)}},{x}\rangle-t\right|-|\langle{v},{x}\rangle-t|

\displaystyle>\sigma\|{v^{(i)}}\|_{2}-2|v_{k}|\geq(\sigma-2)\|{v^{(i)}}\|_{2},

by the triangle inequality.
Using again the fact that under conditioning of the value of
P
, the
K(i)
coordinates of
x
are independent of
E_{2}
, together with Hoeffding’s inequality, we deduce

\displaystyle\Pr\left[|\langle{v},{x}\rangle-t|<2|v_{k}|\,\middle|\,E_{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\be_{P}\left[\Pr\left[|\langle{v},{x}\rangle-t|<2|v_{k}|\,\middle|\,E_{2},P\right]\right]

\displaystyle=\be_{P}\left[\Pr\left[\left|\langle{v^{(i)}},{P}\rangle-\langle{v^{(i)}},{x}\rangle\right|>(\sigma-2)\|{v^{(i)}}\|_{2}\,\middle|\,P\right]\right]

\displaystyle\leq 2\exp(-(\sigma-2)^{2}/2)=2/n^{2}.

This concludes the proof of (
11
).

Proving (
12
).
Unlike the rest of the proof, we henceforth assume that
P
has the exact same distribution as in Definition (
2.2
), and we are explicit about the conditioning on
\|{P}\|_{\infty}\leq 1/2
.

Let
F^{\prime}
be the event that
\{\|{P}\|_{\infty}\leq 1/2\}
and let
F
be the event that
\{\|{P}\|_{\infty,[n]\setminus K(i)}\leq 1/2\}
, meaning that all coordinates of
P
except those in
K(i)
are less than
1/2
in absolute value.

In (
12
) we seek to bound
\Pr\left[\neg{E}\,\middle|\,F^{\prime}\right]
. However, it is more convenient to bound
\Pr\left[\neg{E}\,\middle|\,F\right]
instead. There is not much loss in doing so, in virtue of Lemma
2.3
and that
F^{\prime}\subseteq F
:

\Pr\left[\neg{E}\,\middle|\,F^{\prime}\right]=\frac{\Pr\left[\neg{E}\wedge F^{\prime}\right]}{\Pr[F^{\prime}]}\leq\frac{\Pr\left[\neg{E}\wedge F\right]}{(1-2/n)\Pr[F]}\leq O(\Pr\left[\neg{E}\,\middle|\,F\right]).

Thus (
12
) is reduced to

\Pr\left[\neg{E}\,\middle|\,F\right]\leq O\left(2^{-r}\sigma\sqrt{\frac{m\log(n)}{|K({i})|}}\right)

(16)

The estimate (
16
) follows by that
E=E_{1}\cup E_{2}
hence
\Pr\left[\neg{E}\,\middle|\,F\right]=\Pr\left[\neg{E_{1}}\,\middle|\,F\right]\Pr\left[\neg{E_{2}}\,\middle|\,F,\neg{E_{1}}\right]
, and

\Pr\left[\neg{E_{1}}\,\middle|\,F\right]\leq O(2^{-r}),

(17)

\Pr\left[\neg{E_{2}}\,\middle|\,F,\neg{E_{1}}\right]\leq O\left(\sigma\sqrt{\frac{m\log(n)}{|K({i})|}}\right).

(18)

To confirm (
17
) we use Lemma
2.14
. Denote
\alpha\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=3\|{v^{(i)}}\|_{1}
and note
\alpha\leq 3n\cdot 2^{-i}
. Recalling the definition of
r
, we see that there are at least
2(r-1)\log(n)
elements
j\in J
with
2^{-j-1}\geq\alpha
. Hence (
6
) reads as
Q(\alpha,X_{u,P})\leq O(2^{1-r})
with
u=v-v^{(i)}
, thus implying (
17
).

In order to prove (
18
) we recall that by definition
2.2

\begin{gathered}P=\frac{1}{10\sqrt{\log(n)}}\sum_{\ell=1}^{m}\sum_{j\in J_{\ell}}\alpha_{\ell j}\frac{2^{j}}{\sqrt{m}}\bar{v}_{\ell}^{(j)},\\
(\neg{E_{2}})=\left\{|\langle{v-v^{(i)}},{x}\rangle+\langle{v^{(i)}},{P}\rangle-t|<\sigma\|{v^{(i)}}\|_{2}\right\},\end{gathered}

with
\alpha_{\ell j}\sim U([-1,1])
. Roughly speaking, we show that the random variable
\langle{v^{(i)}},{P}\rangle
has a significant variance, and hence
\neg{E_{2}}
is a rare event.

Fix (that is, condition on) any value of
\alpha_{\ell j}
whenever
(\ell,j)\neq(1,i)
(recall
v=\bar{v}_{1}
) and on all coordinates of
x
that are outside
K(i)
. Observe that the occurrence of the events
E_{1}
and
F
is determined by this fixing. However, since
\alpha_{1i}
is independent of all other
\alpha_{\ell j}
, and it affects only the
K(i)
coordinates of
P
(hence is independent also of
F
and
E_{1}
),
\alpha_{1i}
is still uniformly distributed in
[-1,1]
. Hence, the following is a variable which is uniformly distributed in some real interval

\langle{v^{(i)}},{P}\rangle=\alpha_{1i}\frac{2^{i}}{10\sqrt{m\log(n)}}\langle{v^{(i)}},{v^{(i)}}\rangle+\rho,

(19)

with
\rho\in\mathbb{R}
a constant depending on the fixing. Note that by definition of binary decomposition,
v^{(i)}
has
K(i)
nonzero coordinates, all having absolute values in the range
(2^{-i-1},2^{-i}]
. Hence
\|{v^{(i)}}\|_{2}^{2}=\Theta(|K(i)|4^{-i})
. Overall, the probability that
\neg{E_{2}}
happens – which means that
\langle{v^{(i)}},{P}\rangle
is only
\sigma\|{v^{(i)}}\|_{2}
away from the constant
(t-\langle{v-v^{(i)}},{x}\rangle)
– is bounded by the anti-concentration of the uniform variable (
19
):

\Pr\left[\neg{E_{2}}\,\middle|\,F,\neg{E_{1}}\right]\leq\frac{\sigma\|{v^{(i)}}\|_{2}}{\frac{2^{i}}{10\sqrt{m\log(n)}}\langle{v^{(i)}},{v^{(i)}}\rangle}=\frac{10\sigma\sqrt{m\log(n)}}{2^{i}\|{v^{(i)}}\|_{2}}=\frac{10\sigma\sqrt{m}{\log(n)}}{\sqrt{|K(i)|}},

yielding (
18
) and concluding the proof.
∎

Appendix A
Proofs of standard facts

Proof of Lemma
2.3
.

Consider
P_{i}
, it is a random variable equal to

\frac{1}{10\sqrt{m\log(n)}}\sum_{\ell=1}^{m}\alpha_{\ell}v_{\ell i}^{\prime}

with
v_{\ell i}^{\prime}\in(1/2,1]
being
v_{\ell i}
multiplied by an integral power of 2, and
\alpha_{\ell}\sim U([-1,1])
.
Note that each
\alpha_{\ell}
may be implemented as
\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}2^{-j}x_{j}
with
x_{j}\sim\left\{-1,1\right\}
uniformly and independently.
If we hence aggregate an infinite vector
u=(2^{-j}v_{\ell i}^{\prime})_{\ell=1,j=1}^{m,\infty}
, then
P_{i}
has the distribution of
\frac{1}{10\sqrt{m\log(n)}}\langle{u},{x}\rangle
with all
x_{i}\sim\left\{-1,1\right\}
independently and uniformly distributed. Note also that
\|{u}\|_{2}=\sum_{\ell=1}^{m}v_{\ell i}^{\prime 2}/3\leq m/3
. Therefore, Claim
2.10
implies

\Pr_{\alpha}[|P_{i}|\geq 1/2]=\Pr_{x}[|\langle{u},{x}\rangle|\geq 5\sqrt{m\log(n)}]\leq\Pr_{x}[|\langle{u},{x}\rangle|\geq 8\|{u}\|_{2}\sqrt{\log(n)}]\leq 2/n^{32}.

Union bound implies that
\|{P}\|_{\infty}\leq 1/2
except for probability
2/n^{31}
.
∎

Proof of Lemma
2.13
.

Case
p=\underline{0}
.
In this case
X_{v,p}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}v_{i}
with the
x_{i}
’s uniformly and independently distributed in
\left\{-1,1\right\}
. Without loss of generality we may assume
v_{i}\geq 0
for all
i
. For any
t\in\mathbb{R}
the set of
x
’s for which
|X_{v,p}-t|<\alpha
corresponds to an anti-chain in every subcube of
\left\{-1,1\right\}^{n}
, generated by the
[a]
coordinates. Sperner’s theorem
[
7
]
hence implies
\Pr[|X_{v,p}-t|<\alpha]\leq\binom{a}{\lfloor a/2\rceil}/2^{a}=O(1/\sqrt{a})
, yielding
Q(\alpha,X_{v,p})\leq O(1/\sqrt{a})
.

Case
\|{p}\|_{\infty}\leq 1/2
.

We present
X_{v,p}
as a mixture of random variables
X_{v^{\prime},p^{\prime}}
with
p^{\prime}=\underline{0}
, and with
v^{\prime}
usually having
\Omega(a)
coordinates
\geq a
, and the previous case of the proof.

Indeed, choose
v^{\prime}
to be
v
with each coordinate
i
zeroed out independently with probability
|p_{i}|
. If
v_{i}^{\prime}
was zeroed out, choose
\rho_{i}
to be
v_{i}\cdot\frac{p_{i}}{|p_{i}|}
, otherwise (or if
p_{i}=0
) –
\rho_{i}=0
. We note that if
x\sim\mu_{p}
and
x^{\prime}\sim\mu_{\underline{0}}
, then
v_{i}\cdot x_{i}
can be realized as
\rho_{i}+v_{i}^{\prime}\cdot x^{\prime}_{i}
– the excess probability associated with one of the outcomes of
x_{i}\in\left\{-1,1\right\}
is simulated through
\rho_{i}
.

We conclude that the distribution of
X_{v,p}
can be sampled by first drawing
v^{\prime},\rho
and then sampling
\sum_{i}v_{i}^{\prime}\cdot x^{\prime}_{i}+\sum_{i}\rho_{i}
. In particular,

Q(\alpha,X_{v,p})\leq\be[Q(\alpha,X_{v^{\prime},\underline{0}})]\leq\be[\min(O(1/\sqrt{a^{\prime}}),1)],

(20)

with
a^{\prime}
the number of coordinates
k
with
|v^{\prime}_{k}|\geq\alpha
. By definition of
v^{\prime}
,
a^{\prime}
is distributed as a sum of
a
Bernoulli random variables, the
i
’th of which having success probability
1-|p_{i}|
. Since
|p_{i}|\leq 1/2
, we have
\be[a^{\prime}]\geq a/2
. Moreover, the distribution of
a^{\prime}
is quite concentrated around
\be[a^{\prime}]
, with Chebyshev’s inequality implying that
\Pr[a^{\prime}<a/4]\leq 4/a
. This with (
20
) give

Q(\alpha,X_{v,p})\leq O(\be[\min(1/\sqrt{a^{\prime}},1)])\leq O(1/\sqrt{a/4}+4/a)=O(1/\sqrt{a}).

∎
