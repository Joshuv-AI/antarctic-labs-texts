Abstract
. Ramanujan’s notebooks contain many elegant identities and one of the celebrated identities is a formula for ζ ⁡ ( 2 ​ k + 1 ) \zeta(2k+1) . In 1972, Grosswald gave an extension of the Ramanujan’s formula for ζ ⁡ ( 2 ​ k + 1 ) \zeta(2k+1) , which contains a polynomial of degree 2 ​ k + 2 2k+2 . This polynomial is now well-known as the Ramanujan polynomial R 2 ​ k + 1 ​ ( z ) R_{2k+1}(z) , first studied by Gun, Murty, and Rath. Around the same time, Murty, Smith and Wang proved that all the non-real zeros of R 2 ​ k + 1 ​ ( z ) R_{2k+1}(z) lie on the unit circle. Recently, Chourasiya, Jamal, and the first author found a new polynomial while obtaining a Ramanujan-type formula for Dirichlet L L -functions and named it as Ramanujan-type polynomial R 2 ​ k + 1 , p ​ ( z ) R_{2k+1,p}(z) . In the same paper, they conjectured that all the non-real zeros of R 2 ​ k + 1 , p ​ ( z ) R_{2k+1,p}(z) lie on the circle | z | = 1 / p |z|=1/p . The main goal of this paper is to present a proof of this conjecture.



1.
Introduction

In the Second Notebook [33, p. 173, Ch. 14, Entry 21(i)] as well as in the Lost Notebook [34, p. 319, Entry (28)], Ramanujan mentioned the following remarkable identity for odd zeta values. Considering
\alpha>0
and
\beta=\frac{\pi^{2}}{\alpha}
and non-zero integer
k
, he proved that

\displaystyle G_{k}(\alpha)=(-1)^{k}G_{k}(\beta)-2^{2k}\sum_{j=0}^{k+1}(-1)^{j-1}\frac{B_{2j}}{(2j)!}\frac{B_{2k+2-2j}}{(2k+2-2j)!}\alpha^{k+1-j}\beta^{j},

(1.1)

where

G_{k}(x)=x^{-k}\left\{\frac{1}{2}\zeta(2k+1)+\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{n^{-2k-1}}{e^{2xn}-1}\right\}.

In 1972, Emil Grosswald
[
17
]
gave a notable extension of Ramanujan’s formula (
1.1
).
Let us recall the divisor function
\sigma_{z}(m)=\sum_{d|m}d^{z}
for any
z\in\mathbb{C}
. For
\xi\in\mathbb{H}
,
k\in\mathbb{Z}-\{0\}
, we define

\mathfrak{F}_{k}(\xi):=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\sigma_{-k}(n)e^{2{\pi}in\xi}.

Then we have

\displaystyle\mathfrak{F}_{2k+1}(\xi)-\xi^{2k}\mathfrak{F}_{2k+1}\left(-\frac{1}{\xi}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\zeta(2k+1)(\xi^{2k}-1)

\displaystyle+\frac{(2{\pi}i)^{2k+1}}{2\xi}\sum_{j=0}^{k+1}\xi^{2k+2-2j}\frac{B_{2j}}{(2j)!}\frac{B_{2k+2-2j}}{(2k+2-2j)!}.

(1.2)

Substituting
\xi=i\beta/\pi,

\alpha\beta={\pi}^{2},
with
\alpha,\beta>0,
the identity (
1
) becomes same as the Ramanujan’s identity (
1.1
) for
\zeta(2k+1).
Later, Gun, Murty, and Rath
[
18
]
studied the finite sum involving Bernoulli numbers present on the right hand side of (
1
) and named it as the
Ramanujan polynomial
. Thus, for
k\in\mathbb{N}
and
z\in\mathbb{C}
, Ramanujan polynomials are defined as follows:

R_{2k+1}(z):=\sum_{j=0}^{k+1}z^{2k+2-2j}\frac{B_{2j}}{(2j)!}\frac{B_{2k+2-2j}}{(2k+2-2j)!}.

(1.3)

One can easily verify that Ramanujan polynomials are reciprocal or self-inverse. In 2011, Murty, Smith, and Wang
[
20
]
proved that all the non-real roots of
R_{2k+1}(z)
lie on the unit circle
|z|=1
. In 2013, Lalin and Rogers
[
22
]
observed similar behaviour for a few variant of Ramanujan polynomials involving Bernoulli numbers and Euler numbers. One of the polynomials they
[
22
, Theorem 2.2]
have studied is the following:

\displaystyle R_{2k+1,2}(z)=\sum_{j=1}^{k}(2^{2j}-1)(2^{2k+2-2j}-1)\frac{B_{2j}B_{2k+2-2j}}{{(2j)!(2k+2-2j)!}}z^{2k+2-2j}.

(1.4)

Interestingly, an existence of the above polynomial can be seen from a Ramanujan-type formula studied by Berndt
[
4
, Theorem 4.7]
and Malurkar
[
19
]
.
Recently, Chourasiya, Jamal, and the first author
[
11
]
found the following Grosswald-type formula for the Dirichlet
L
-function
L(s,\chi)
.
Let
p
be a prime number and
\chi_{1}
be the principal Dirichlet character modulo
p
. For any
k\in\mathbb{N}
and
z\in\mathbb{H}
, we define

\displaystyle\mathfrak{F}_{k,\chi_{1}}(z):=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}a_{n}\sigma_{-k,\chi_{1}}(n)\exp(2\pi inz),\quad\text{with}\quad a_{n}=\begin{cases}1,&\text{if}\,\,\gcd(n,p)=1,\\
1-p,&\text{if}\,\,\gcd(n,p)=p.\end{cases}

Then we have

\displaystyle\left(pz\right)^{2k}

\displaystyle\mathfrak{F}_{2k+1,\chi_{1}}\left(-\frac{1}{p^{2}z}\right)-\mathfrak{F}_{2k+1,\chi_{1}}(z)=\frac{p-1}{2}L(2k+1,\chi_{1})\left\{\left(pz\right)^{2k}-1\right\}

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\frac{(2\pi i)^{2k+1}}{2z\,p^{2k+2}}\sum_{j=1}^{k}{(p^{2j}-1)(p^{2k+2-2j}-1)\frac{B_{2j}}{(2j)!}\frac{B_{2k+2-2j}}{(2k+2-2j)!}}(pz)^{2k+2-2j}.

(1.5)

The above identity motivated them to define Ramanujan-type polynomials as

R_{2k+1,p}(z):=\sum_{j=1}^{k}(p^{2j}-1)(p^{2k+2-2j}-1)\frac{B_{2j}B_{2k+2-2j}}{{(2j)!(2k+2-2j)!}}(pz)^{2k+2-2j},

(1.6)

where
k\in\mathbb{N}
and
p
is a prime number.
In the same paper, they
[
11
, Conjecture 2.9]
have stated the below conjecture about the location of non-real roots of
R_{2k+1,p}(z)
.

Conjecture 1.1
.

Let
k\in\mathbb{N}
and
p
be any prime number, the only real root of Ramanujan-type polynomial
R_{2k+1,p}(z)
is
z=0
of multiplicity
2
. Moreover, the non-real roots are simple and lie on the circle
|z|=\frac{1}{p}
.

At the end of their paper
[
11
, p. 748]
, they also mentioned that the above conjecture might be true for any nature number
n\geq 2
instead of any prime number
p
.
The main goal of this article is to prove this conjecture. Our main result is the following.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
k
and
n\geq 2
be two fixed natural numbers. Then the following Ramanujan-type polynomial

R_{2k+1,n}(z):=\sum_{j=1}^{k}(n^{2j}-1)(n^{2k+2-2j}-1)\frac{B_{2j}B_{2k+2-2j}}{{(2j)!(2k+2-2j)!}}(nz)^{2k+2-2j},

(1.7)

has only one real root at
z=0
of multiplicity
2
. Again, the non-real roots are simple and lie on the circle
|z|=\frac{1}{n}
.

In the next section, we collect a few well-known results about the location of zeros of reciprocal polynomials.

2.
Known results on reciprocal polynomials

Let
p(x)\in\mathbb{R}[x]
be a non-zero polynomial of degree
n
with

p(x)=a_{0}+a_{1}x+a_{2}x^{2}+\ldots+a_{n}x^{n}.

We say that
p(x)
is a reciprocal or self-inverse polynomial if and only if

x^{n}p\left(\frac{1}{x}\right)=a_{n}+a_{n-1}x+a_{n-2}x^{2}+\ldots+a_{0}x^{n}=p(x).

That implies that the coefficients of real reciprocal polynomials satisfy

a_{k}=a_{n-k},~\forall~k=0,1,\cdots,n.

Theorem 2.1

(Lakatos
[
21
]
)
.

If a reciprocal polynomial

p(x)=\sum_{i=0}^{k}a_{i}x^{i}

of degree
k
with real coefficients satisfy the condition

|a_{k}|\geq\sum_{i=0}^{k}|a_{i}-a_{k}|,

(2.1)

then the polynomial
p(x)
certainly bears all its zeros on the unit circle.
If the inequality (
2.1
) is strict, then the multiplicity of all the zeros of
p(x)
is one.

Later, the above theorem has been modified by many mathematicians, thus the condition (
2.1
) for reciprocal polynomials has many improvements. Schinzel
[
23
]
gave one generalization of Theorem
2.1
for any reciprocal polynomial over
\mathbb{C}
.

Theorem 2.2

(Schinzel
[
23
]
)
.

Let
p(z)
be a reciprocal polynomial, say,

p(z)=\sum_{j=0}^{k}a_{j}z^{j}

of degree
k
with
a_{j}\in\mathbb{C},\forall j
. If the coefficients of
p(z)
satisfy the condition

|a_{k}|\geq\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}c,d\in\mathbb{C}\\
{|d|=1}\end{subarray}}\sum_{j=0}^{k}|ca_{j}-d^{k-j}a_{k}|,

(2.2)

then all the zeros of
p(z)
lie on
|z|=1
. If the inequality (
2.2
) is strict, then all the zeros of
p(x)
must be simple.

Next, we collect a few results related to Bernoulli numbers. The following bounds hold for Bernoulli numbers.

Lemma 2.3
.

[
1
, p. 805]

For
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{2(2n)!}{(2\pi)^{2n}}<|B_{2n}|<\frac{2(2n)!}{(2\pi)^{2n}(1-2^{1-2n})}.

(2.3)

We are now going to mention one useful identity about the convolution of Bernoulli polynomials.

Lemma 2.4
.

[
12
, p. 31, eq. (3.2)]
.
For
m\in\mathbb{N}
, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{m}{{m}\choose{j}}B_{j}(a)B_{m-j}(b)=m(a+b+1)B_{m-1}(a+b)-(m-1)B_{m}(a+b).

3.
Proof of Theorem
1.2

Before proving our main theorem, we need following lemmas.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
k\geq 1
and
n\geq 2
be two fixed natural numbers.
Let
f_{n}(x)
be a function
f_{n}:[0,~2k-2]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
defined as

\displaystyle f_{n}(x):=(n^{x+2}-1)(n^{2k-x}-1).

(3.1)

The function
f_{n}(x)
has global maxima at
x=k-1
and
its minima at
x=0,2k-2
, with the same minimum value
(n^{2}-1)(n^{2k}-1)
at both of the end points.

For fixed two natural numbers
n\geq 2
and
k\geq 1
, it is given that

f_{n}(x):=(n^{x+2}-1)(n^{2k-x}-1).

Differentiating with respect to
x
, one can see that

f_{n}^{\prime}(x)=-n^{x+2}\log(n)+n^{2k-x}\log(n)\Rightarrow f_{n}^{\prime\prime}(x)=-\left(n^{x+2}+n^{2k-x}\right)\log^{2}(n).

Further, we can easily check that
f_{n}^{\prime\prime}(x)
is always negative in the given domain, so all the extrema points obtained by equating
f^{\prime}_{n}(x)
to zero will correspond to the local maximum values. Now we find extrema points of the function
f_{n}(x)
by equating
f^{\prime}_{n}(x)
to zero, that is,

f_{n}^{\prime}(x)=0\Rightarrow n^{x+2}=n^{2k-x}\Rightarrow x=k-1.

Thus,
x=k-1
is the only local maxima point in the interval
[0,2k-2]
. Hence it must global maxima as well. Since the function is continuously differentiable, so we conclude that
x=2k-2~\text{and}~x=0
are the only points of minima. Moreover, we have

f_{n}(0)=f_{n}(2k-2)=(n^{2}-1)(n^{2k}-1).

Hence the result follows.
∎

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
k\geq 1
and
n\geq 2
be two fixed natural numbers. The function
g_{n}:[0,2k-2]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
defined as

g_{n}(x):=\frac{(n^{x+2}-1)}{(2^{x+2}-1)}\frac{(n^{2k-x}-1)}{(2^{2k-x}-1)},

(3.2)

has an upper bound
\frac{(n^{k+1}-1)^{2}}{3(2^{2k}-1)}.

We can write
g_{n}(x)
in terms of
f_{n}(x)
as follows:

g_{n}(x)=\frac{f_{n}(x)}{f_{2}(x)}.

Therefore, the ratio of maximum value of
f_{n}(x)
and the minimum value of
f_{2}(x)
will provide us an upper bound for
g_{n}(x).

From Lemma
3.1
, we know that the maxima of
f_{n}(x)
is attained at
x=k-1
and the corresponding maximum value is,

\displaystyle f_{n}(k-1)=(n^{k+1}-1)^{2}.

(3.3)

Also the minimum value of
f_{n}(x)
is attained at
x=0
and in particular for
n=2
the minimum value will be,

\displaystyle f_{2}(0)=(2^{2}-1)(2^{2k}-1)=3(2^{2k}-1).

(3.4)

Hence, using (
3.3
) and (
3.4
), it follows that

g_{n}(x)\leq\frac{(n^{k+1}-1)^{2}}{3(2^{2k}-1)}.

This completes the proof.
∎Next, we state a result which will be crucial for the proof of our main theorem.

Proposition 3.3
.

Let
R_{2k+1,n}(z)
be the Ramanujan-type polynomial defined as in (
1.7
) and
A_{j}
be the
(2k-2-2j)^{th}

|A_{k-1}|\geq\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}c,d\in\mathbb{C}\\
{|d|=1}\end{subarray}}\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}|cA_{j}-dA_{k-1}|.

By appropriate shifting, from the definition (
1.7
) of

(3.5)

Replacing
z
by
\frac{z}{n}
, we have

\displaystyle R_{2k+1,n}\left(\frac{z}{n}\right)

\displaystyle=z^{2}\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}(n^{2j+2}-1)(n^{2k-2j}-1)\frac{B_{2j+2}B_{2k-2j}}{{(2j+2)!(2k-2j)!}}z^{2k-2-2j}

\displaystyle=z^{2}H(z),

(3.6)

where
H(z)

\displaystyle H(z):=\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}(n^{2j+2}-1)(n^{2k-2j}-1)\frac{B_{2j+2}B_{2k-2j}}{{(2j+2)!(2k-2j)!}}z^{2k-2-2j}.

(3.7)

It is given that the coefficient of

in (

\displaystyle A_{j}=(n^{2j+2}-1)(n^{2k-2j}-1)\frac{B_{2j+2}B_{2k-2j}}{{(2j+2)!(2k-2j)!}}.

(3.8)

Now we show that the polynomial

\displaystyle|A_{k-1}|\geq\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}c,d\in\mathbb{C}\\

(3.9)

In particular, considering

Using the lower bound for Bernoulli numbers i.e., Lemma

\displaystyle|A_{j}|

Similarly, using the upper bound for

\displaystyle|A_{k-1}|

Note that the sign of

c
should satisfy the below inequality:

\displaystyle\frac{3(1+k)(2^{2k}-1)(2k+2)!|A_{k-1}|}{(2k+1)(n^{k+1}-1)^{2}(2^{2k+2}-1)|B_{2k+2}|}>c.

(3.25)

Moreover, using the lower bound (
3.11
) of
|A_{k-1}|
and upper bound (
2.3
) for
|B_{2k+2}|
, one can show that

\displaystyle\frac{3(1+k)(2^{2k}-1)(2k+2)!|A_{k-1}|}{(2k+1)(n^{k+1}-1)^{2}(2^{2k+2}-1)|B_{2k+2}|}>c_{n,k}

where

\displaystyle c_{n,k}:=\frac{(1+k)(n^{2k}-1)(n^{2}-1)(2^{2k}-1)(2^{2}-1)(2^{2k+1}-1)}{2^{2k}(2k+1)(n^{k+1}-1)^{2}(2^{2k+2}-1)}.

Note the constant
c_{n,k}
is a positive constant. Thus, for any positive
c
such that
c<c_{n,k}
will satisfy the inequality (
3.25
) and hence the equation (
3
).
This proves the inequality (
3.10
) for the polynomial
H(z)
and consequently the result follows.
∎

Now we are ready for proving the main theorem.

We are interested to show that all non-real roots of
R_{2k+1,n}(z)
lie on the circle
z=1/n
, which is equivalent to show that all non-real roots of
R_{2k+1,n}\left(\frac{z}{n}\right)
lie on the unit circle
|z|=1
. From the equation (
3
), we know that

\displaystyle R_{2k+1,n}\left(\frac{z}{n}\right)

\displaystyle=z^{2}\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}(n^{2j+2}-1)(n^{2k-2j}-1)\frac{B_{2j+2}B_{2k-2j}}{{(2j+2)!(2k-2j)!}}z^{2k-2-2j}

\displaystyle=z^{2}H(z),

(3.26)

where
H(z)
is defined as

\displaystyle H(z)=\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}A_{j}z^{2k-2-2j},

and

\displaystyle A_{j}=(n^{2j+2}-1)(n^{2k-2j}-1)\frac{B_{2j+2}B_{2k-2j}}{{(2j+2)!(2k-2j)!}}.

We can easily check that
A_{j}
and
A_{k-1-j}
are same, which indicates that the polynomial
R_{2k+1,n}\left(\frac{z}{n}\right)
is reciprocal and so
R_{2k+1,n}(z)
.
Now from (
3
), we can easily see that
R_{2k+1,n}\left(\frac{z}{n}\right)
has degree
2k
containing only even powers of
z
with
z^{2}
being the least power of
z
. Therefore,
R_{2k+1,n}\left(\frac{z}{n}\right)
has a real zero at
z=0
of multiplicity
2
. Utilizing Proposition
3.3
, we can see that
H(z)
satisfies Theorem
2.2
, so we can conclude that
H(z)
has all its roots on the unit circle. Moreover, as we have proved that the inequality (
3
) is strict so all the zeros of
H(z)
are simple. Thus, the polynomial
R_{2k+1,n}\left(\frac{z}{n}\right)
has all the zeros on the unit circle except
z=0
.
Next, we show that there is no real root except zero.
Let us suppose that the polynomial
R_{2k+1,n}\left(\frac{z}{n}\right)
has a real root except
z=0
, then it must be on the unit circle. Consequently, the possibility of the non-trivial real root will be either
z=1~\text{or}~z=-1
or both. So, putting
z=1
in (
3.7
), we get

\displaystyle H(1)=0,

\displaystyle\Rightarrow

\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}(n^{2j+2}-1)(n^{2k-2j}-1)\frac{B_{2j+2}B_{2k-2j}}{{(2j+2)!(2k-2j)!}}=0,

\displaystyle\Rightarrow

\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}A_{j}=0.

(3.27)

We have already seen that the sign of
A_{j}
is
(-1)^{k+1}
for all
j
. Note that all even Bernoulli numbers are never zero. Therefore, depending on the parity of
k
the sum
\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}A_{j}
is either positive or negative. This is a contradiction to (
3
). This completes the proof that
z=1
is not a root of
H(z)
. Again, as
H(z)
is an even function, so by the same argument we can conclude that
z=-1
is not a root of
H(z)
as well. Hence, all roots of
H(z)
are complex and simple, and lie on the unit circle. This completes the proof.
∎

4.
Concluding Remarks

Motivated from the Ramanujan’s formula (
1.1
) as well as Grosswald’s identity (
1
), Gun, Murty, and Rath
[
18
]
defined Ramanujan polynomial as

R_{2k+1}(z)=\sum_{j=0}^{k+1}z^{2k+2-2j}\frac{B_{2j}}{(2j)!}\frac{B_{2k+2-2j}}{(2k+2-2j)!}.

(4.1)

Around the same time, Murty, Smith, and Wang
[
20
]
showed that all the non-real roots of
R_{2k+1}(z)
lie on the unit circle. In this paper, we studied the following Ramanujan-type polynomial:

R_{2k+1,n}(z):=\sum_{j=1}^{k}(n^{2j}-1)(n^{2k+2-2j}-1)\frac{B_{2j}B_{2k+2-2j}}{{(2j)!(2k+2-2j)!}}(nz)^{2k+2-2j},

(4.2)

We showed that it has only one real root is at
z=0
of multiplicity
2
. Moreover, the non-real roots of
R_{2k+1,n}(z)
are simple and all of them lie on the circle
|z|=\frac{1}{n}
, which says that all the non-real roots of
R_{2k+1,n}\left(\frac{z}{n}\right)
lie on the unit circle.
Ramanujan’s formula (
1.1
) has been generalized by many mathematicians, over the years. Recently, Dixit and Gupta
[
13
, Theorem 2.1]
found an interesting analogue of Ramanujan’s formula (
1.1
) for squares of odd zeta values and in the process they encountered the following finite sum involving Bernoulli numbers. For any
k\in\mathbb{N}
,

\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{k+1}(-1)^{j}\frac{B_{2j}^{2}}{{(2j)!}^{2}}\frac{B_{2k+2-2j}^{2}}{{(2k+2-2j)!}^{2}}\alpha^{2j}\beta^{2k+2-2j}.

(4.3)

Very recently, Banerjee and Sahani
[
3
, Theorem 1.1]
generalized the work of Dixit and Gupta and found a formula for higher power of odd zeta values. While obtaining this generalization, they found the following finite sum involving higher powers of Bernoulli numbers. For any two fixed natural numbers
k
and
\ell
,

\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{k+1}(-1)^{j}\frac{B_{2j}^{\ell}}{{(2j)!}^{\ell}}\frac{B_{2k+2-2j}^{\ell}}{{(2k+2-2j)!}^{\ell}}\alpha^{\ell j}\beta^{\ell(k+1-j)}.

(4.4)

It is interesting to note that, in both of the equations (
4.3
) and (
4.4
),
\alpha
and
\beta
are positive and satisfy
\alpha\beta=\pi^{2}
, which is exactly the same condition also required in Ramanujan’s formula (
1.1
). This observation motivated us to define a one-variable generalization of Ramanujan polynomial.
Let
k
and
\ell
be two fixed natural numbers, then we define

\displaystyle R_{2k+1}^{(\ell)}(z):=\sum_{j=0}^{k+1}(-1)^{(\ell+1)j}\frac{B_{2j}^{\ell}}{{(2j)!}^{\ell}}\frac{B_{2k+2-2j}^{\ell}}{{(2k+2-2j)!}^{\ell}}z^{2\ell j}.

(4.5)

For
\ell=1
, this polynomial coincides with the Ramanujan polynomial (
4.1
). For simplicity, replace
z^{2\ell}
by
Z
to write

\displaystyle R_{2k+1}^{(\ell)}(Z):=\sum_{j=0}^{k+1}(-1)^{(\ell+1)j}\frac{B_{2j}^{\ell}}{{(2j)!}^{\ell}}\frac{B_{2k+2-2j}^{\ell}}{{(2k+2-2j)!}^{\ell}}Z^{j}.

(4.6)

We can easily verify that this is a reciprocal polynomial. Based on numerical evidences checked in Mathematica software, we conjecture the following statement.

Conjecture 4.1
.

All the non-real roots of
R_{2k+1}^{(\ell)}(Z)
are simple and lie on the unit circle
|Z|=1
.

Ramanujan’s formula for
\zeta(2k+1)
has been generalized by many mathematicians for different
L
-functions. For examples, readers are encouraged to see
[
2
,
5
,
6
,
7
,
9
,
10
,
14
,
15
,
16
]
and references therein. Interestingly, all of these generalizations consist of a finite sum involving Bernolli numbers or Euler numbers or some special values of
L
-functions.
This suggests that we can always find a generalized Ramanujan polynomial
from any analogue of Ramanujan’s formula for
\zeta(2k+1)
and that might be interest of study.
In this spirit, Berndt and Straub studied a character analogue of Ramanujan polynomial and gave a more general conjecture
[
8
, Conjecture 7.3]
, which is different from our Conjecture
4.1
.
