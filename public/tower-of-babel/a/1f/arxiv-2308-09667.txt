On Lifting Integrality Gaps to SSEH Hardness for Globally Constrained CSPs
Suprovat Ghoshal, Euiwoong Lee

Abstract
A μ \mu -constrained Boolean Max-CSP ( ψ ) (\psi) instance is a Boolean Max-CSP instance on predicate ψ : { 0 , 1 } r → { 0 , 1 } \psi:\{0,1\}^{r}\to\{0,1\} where the objective is to find a labeling of relative weight exactly μ \mu that maximizes the fraction of satisfied constraints. In this work, we study the approximability of constrained Boolean Max-CSPs via SDP hierarchies by relating the integrality gap of Max-CSP ( ψ ) (\psi) to its μ \mu -dependent approximation curve. Formally, assuming the Small-Set Expansion Hypothesis, we show that it is 𝖭𝖯 \mathsf{NP} -hard to approximate μ \mu -constrained instances of Max-CSP ( ψ \psi ) up to factor 𝖦𝖺𝗉 ℓ , μ ​ ( ψ ) / log ⁡ ( 1 / μ ) 2 {\sf Gap}_{\ell,\mu}(\psi)/\log(1/\mu)^{2} (ignoring factors depending on r r ) for any ℓ ≥ ℓ ⁡ ( μ , r ) \ell\geq\ell(\mu,r) . Here, 𝖦𝖺𝗉 ℓ , μ ​ ( ψ ) {\sf Gap}_{\ell,\mu}(\psi) is the optimal integrality gap of ℓ \ell -round Lasserre relaxation for μ \mu -constrained Max-CSP ( ψ \psi ) instances. Our results are derived by combining the framework of Raghavendra [STOC 2008] along with more recent advances in rounding Lasserre relaxations and reductions from the Small-Set Expansion (SSE) problem. A crucial component of our reduction is a novel way of composing generic bias-dependent dictatorship tests with SSE, which could be of independent interest.

Introduction

Maximum Constraint Satisfaction Problems (
Max-CSP
s) are some of the most commonly studied optimization problems in theoretical computer science. An instance

of a
r
-ary Boolean

r
-ary constraint (hyper)graph

, a constraint set
\{\Pi_{e}\}_{e}
, where for every edge

defines the set of “accepting” assignments to the edge. The overall objective of the
Max-CSP
problem is to find a Boolean labeling of the vertices that maximizes the fraction of satisfied constraints. The generality of the definition allows it to express a wide array of combinatorial optimization problems such as
Max-Cut
,
Max-SAT
,
Max-
3
-Lin
,
Label Cover
, among many others as special cases; each of these problems are of fundamental interest on their own, and have their own dedicated line of works that explore its various aspects (see
[
MM17
]
and references therein for a comprehensive overview of such results).

An extensively studied question in the context of each of these problems, and for
Max-CSP
s in general, is that of proving tight bounds for efficiently achievable approximation guarantees. While in the beginning, individual classes of CSPs seem to require their own problem-specific techniques for algorithms and hardness, over a steady sequence of works, the shape of a more unified framework for understanding CSPs began to emerge; upper bounds were facilitated using advances in understanding the power of semidefinite programming
[
GW94
,
KMS98
,
CMM06
]
, whereas lower bounds were being facilitated by dictatorship test-based gadget reductions
[
Hås01
,
KKMO07
]

[
Rag08
]
, which connected the two directions by showing that the limitations of SDP based algorithms are fundamentally connected to the best efficiently achievable approximation factors. Specifically,
[
Rag08
]
showed that assuming the Unique Games Conjecture (UGC)
[
Kho02
]
, for every
Max-CSP
there exists a canonical SDP relaxation whose integrality gap matches the best possible hardness for the problem under UGC. The soundness analysis of its reduction automatically yielded the optimal rounding schemes for the SDP relaxation, thus further strengthening the connection between SDPs and UGC-based hardness reductions. Following
[
Rag08
]
, there have been several subsequent works which establish analogous connections for the settings of graph partitioning problems
[
MNRS08
]
, Ordering CSPs
[
GHM+11
]
, Strict CSPs
[
KMTV11
]
, and Tree CSPs
[
CM22
]
.

Despite the ubiquity of
[
Rag08
]
’s framework, there are still several natural classes of optimization problems for which an analogous unified theory of optimal algorithms and hardness has remained elusive. A particularly well-studied class of such problems are CSPs with global cardinality constraints. Formally, given a predicate
\psi:\{0,1\}^{r}\to\{0,1\}
, a
\mu
-constrained
Max-CSP
(\psi)
instance is a Boolean
Max-CSP
where the edge constraints are identified by
\psi
, and the objective is to find an assignment of relative weight
exactly

\mu

Densest-
k
-Subgraph
,
Max-Bisection
,
Max
k
-Coverage
,
Small-Set Expansion

While there is extensive literature that study algorithms and hardness for these problems, these results often employ problem-specific techniques, and as such, a general unified framework of these problems, as in
[
Rag08
]
, is far from being realized. In particular, in the quest to develop such a theory for globally constrained CSPs, one is faced with following immediate challenges:

Question 1
:
What is a natural algorithmic framework that is amenable towards deriving optimal approximation guarantees for globally constrained CSPs?

Question 2
:
Is there a way to lift lower bounds for the class of optimal algorithms to (possibly conditional)
\mathsf{NP}
-hardness lower bounds?

Towards answering the above, it is useful to understand why the techniques of
[
Rag08
]
don’t immediately apply to the setting of globally constrained CSPs. The key observation here is that in the setting of Max-CSPs, one can usually reduce the task of solving the CSP instance to that of solving a distribution over constant sized ‘local’ instances. This viewpoint is especially useful in the context of both algorithmic and hardness frameworks. In particular, analyses of SDP based approximation algorithms often view the CSP as a distribution over constraints, and derive the approximation guarantee by reducing the task to that of analyzing the performance of the algorithm on individual constraints. On other hand, for establishing hardness results, a usual approach is to first construct a toy instance (aka a dictatorship test) that models the conceptual difficulty of the problem, and then one can create a large hard instance of the same by embedding copies of the smaller instance along the edges of a hard CSP such as Unique Games. However, back in the setting of globally constrained Max-CSPs, these local techniques often fail to capture the additional complexity introduced due to the ‘global’ constraint(s), and yield bounds that are far from optimal.

Fortunately, it turns out that there are more recent advances in the theory of approximation which can provide us with candidate guesses for the answers to these questions. Firstly, there is promising evidence which suggests that algorithmic frameworks based on higher-order SDP hierarchies (such as the
Sum-of-Squares
(SoS) hierarchy) are better suited for addressing local and global constraints simultaneously. In particular, the work of Raghavendra and Tan
[
RT12
]
proposed a systematic SoS-based framework for solving constrained CSPs, variations of which have been used to establish near-optimal approximation guarantees for several problems such as
Max/Min-Bisection
,
Balanced Separator

[
RT12
,
ABG16
]
,
Max-
k
-VC
,
CC Max-Cut

[
AS19
]
. For the second question, a candidate staging ground for proving hardness results for globally constrained Boolean Max-CSPs is the
Small Set Expansion Hypothesis (SSEH)
. Introduced in the work of Raghavendra and Steurer
[
RS09
]
, the Small-Set Expansion problem has been useful in establishing tight bounds for several natural problems such as
Balanced Separator
,
Min-Bisection

[
RST12

,

Min-
k

[
Man18
]
,
Densest-

In this work, we make progress towards bridging the gap between upper and lower bounds for globally constrained CSPs. In particular, we extend the techniques of

At this point, while it may appear that one has all the necessary ingredients for proving Theorem

Composition with SSE
. Unfortunately, it turns out that one of the simplest steps in

Variance Blow-up in Soundness Analysis
. A more subtle issue lies in the analysis of
[

Handling the above issues are the key contributions of this work, we expand on these issues and our techniques for handling them in the remaining sections.

2.3
Our Approach: Weak Coupling of
z
Variables

Our approach towards addressing the above is that we devise a way of sampling the

variables in a

such that the correlation structure of the

have the desired mixing properties (Figure

Towards stating our idea more formally, let us begin by defining the collection of distributions
(\mathcal{D}^{R}_{e})_{e\in E_{\rm gap}}

Lemma 2.4
(Informal version of Lemma

Remark 2.5

The key difference between the distribution employed by the test in this work and those of

On the other hand, the independence of

2.5
Handling the Variance Blow-up

We conclude our discussion by briefly describing our approach to handling the

-blow-up in relative weight guarantee in
[

]
’s soundness analysis. Roughly, in their analysis, the
2^{O(R)}
-blow up results from trying to relate the covariance of the rounded values to covariance of the corresponding local variables. The following proposition states their bound from this step formally.

Proposition 2.6
(Implicit in
[
RT12
]
)

Let
F:\mathbbm{R}^{R}\to[0,1]
be an
R
-variate function. Let
(X_{i},X_{j})
be a pair of jointly distributed
\{0,1\}
-valued random variables, and let
(g_{i},g_{j})
be a pair of jointly distributed
R
-dimensional Gaussian random variables such that
(g_{i}(\ell),g_{j}(\ell))
matches the covariance structure of
(X_{i},X_{j})
for every
\ell\in[R]
. Then,

|{\mathsf{E}}F(g_{i})F(g_{j})-{\mathsf{E}}F(g_{i}){\mathsf{E}}F(g_{j})|\leq 2^{O(R)}|{\rm Cov}(X_{i},X_{j})|.

Their analysis needs to use the above bound since the local-distributions used to construct their test can only guarantee a bound on the average covariance. This eventually results in an additional
2^{O(R)}
multiplicative blow-up in the variance of the weight of the rounded solution, which in turn shows up as the multiplicative error term in the relative weight.

Our Fix
. Towards addressing this, our first observation is that since
F
is
[0,1]
-valued, one can directly bound the covariance of the rounded values using the correlation of the Gaussian vector variables i.e., using elementary Hermite analysis one can show that

|{\mathsf{E}}F(g_{i})F(g_{j})-{\mathsf{E}}F(g_{i}){\mathsf{E}}F(g_{j})|\leq|{\rm Corr}(X_{i},X_{j})|,

where
{\rm Corr}(X_{i},X_{j})
is the correlation
15
15

15

Formally,
{\rm Corr}(X_{i},X_{j})=\frac{{\mathsf{E}}\left[X_{i}X_{j}\right]-\mu_{i}\mu_{j}}{\sqrt{\mu_{i}(1-\mu_{i})}\sqrt{\mu_{j}(1-\mu_{j})}}
, where
\mu_{i}={\mathsf{E}}[X_{i}],\mu_{j}={\mathsf{E}}[X_{j}]
.
between
X_{i}
and
X_{j}
under the local distribution. Hence, if we started with a local distribution with small average-correlation, then we would be able to get a
R
-independent bound on the variance of the rounded weight. However, note that in general the correlation of a pair of random variable is incomparable to its covariance, and hence a bound on the average covariance is insufficient to exploit this observation. To get around this issue, we use the observation that random conditionings of
smoothened
Lasserre solutions directly yield local distributions with the stronger property of small average correlation. Combining this with the above observation yields the improved bound on the variance of the rounded solution. We refer the readers to Sections

. Then observe that the set of functions is convex. Furthermore, fixing for any

for some choice of

. To that end, fix such a choice of

, and let

, and

. Then,

\displaystyle{\mathsf{E}}_{g\sim N(0,1)^{r}}{\mathsf{E}}_{g_{1},\ldots,g_{r}\underset{\rho}{\sim}g}\left[\prod_{j\in[r]}f_{j}(g_{j})\right]

\displaystyle=\Pr_{\begin{subarray}{c}g\sim N(0,1)^{r}\\
g_{1},\ldots,g_{r}\underset{\rho}{\sim}g\end{subarray}}\left[\mathop{~\forall}^{r}_{j=1}~g_{j}\in A_{j}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\Pr_{\begin{subarray}{c}g\sim N(0,1)\\
g_{1},\ldots,g_{r}\underset{\rho}{\sim}g\end{subarray}}\left[\mathop{~\forall}^{r}_{j=1}~g_{j}\leq\Phi^{-1}(\mu_{j})\right],

where the last inequality follows from applying Theorem

.

The Lasserre (aka the Sum-of-Squares) hierarchy is a sequence of strengthenings of a basic SDP relaxation. The
\ell

and

are consistent on the support of the intersection

. In particular, this allows us to define pseudo-variables

which are not necessarily jointly distributed but are locally consistent i.e., for every subset
S
of size at most
\ell

is distributed as
\theta_{S}
. Finally, it ensures that
\ell^{th}
-order pseudo-covariance matrix is PSD. We describe the

6
Completeness

Suppose
G=(V,E)
is a YES instance. Then from Definition
3.9
, there exists a subset
S\subseteq V
satisfying
{\sf Vol}(S)=\delta

\phi_{G}(S)\leq\varepsilon
. We begin by defining a map
i^{*}:V^{R}\times\{\bot,\top\}^{R}\to[R]
using this choice of
S
, which will be used to identify the dictator labeling. For every
(A,z)\in V^{R}\times\{\bot,\top\}^{R}
, define the set

\Pi(A,z):=\{i\in[R]~|~(A(i),z(i))\in S\times\{\top\}\}.

Then we use the following process to construct the map
i^{*}
:

I

Firstly, consider the choices of
(A,z)\in V^{R}\times\{\bot,\top\}^{R}_{\beta}
satisfying
|\Pi(A,z)|=1
. For any such
(A,z)
, we let
i^{*}(A,z)=i
where
i
is the
unique
element contained in
\Pi(A,z)
. Note that by construction, we have
i^{*}(\pi(A,z))=\pi(i^{*}(A,z))
, for every permutation
\pi
, for every choice of
(A,z)
satisfying
|\Pi(A,z)|=1
.

II

For the remaining choices of
(A,z)
(i.e.,
|\Pi(A,z)|\neq 1
), we assign
i^{*}
using the following process:

–

While there exists
(A,z)
such
i^{*}(A,z)
is unassigned, do the next step.

–

Pick any
(A,z)
such that
i^{*}(A,z)
is unassigned, and assign
i^{*}(A,z)=1
. Furthermore, assign
i^{*}(\pi(A,z))=\pi(1)
for every non-identity permutation
\pi
.

In other words, we fix
i^{*}(A,z)
for a given unassigned
(A,z)

(A,z)
. Note that this construction ensures that
i^{*}(\pi(A,z))=\pi(i^{*}(A,z))
holds for every permutation
\pi
, for all the pairs assigned in II.

Then for every
(A,z)\in V^{R}\times\{\bot,\top\}^{R}
, we assign the dictator function
f_{A,z}:=\chi_{i^{*}(A,z)}
i.e.,

f_{A,z}(x)=x(i^{*}(A,z))\qquad\qquad~\forall~x\in\{0,1\}^{R}

Finally, the full assignment for the instance
f:V^{R}\times\{0,1\}^{R}\times\{\bot,\top\}^{R}\to\{0,1\}
is defined as
f(A,x,z):=f_{A,z}(x)=x(i^{*}(A,z))
, for every
(A,x,z)
.

Observation 6.1

By construction, the above assignment satisfies the property
f(\pi(A,x,z))=f(A,x,z)
for every permutation
\pi
and every choice of
(A,x,z)
.

We introduce some additional notation used in the soundness analysis. Throughout the proof, we will be dealing with functions defined over various probability spaces. We will always use
\Omega

4

\{0,1\}\times\{\bot,\top\}

\{0,1\}_{\mu_{i}}\otimes\{\bot,\top\}_{\beta}

\kappa\in(0,1)

•

\mathcal{T}^{(\beta)}_{\kappa}
is noise operator on

•

For any

•

For any

We extend the above notation and use

Remark 7.1
(Definition of

We point out that in the above convention,

is not the
(1-\eta)
-correlated Bonami-Beckner operator in the space

. The

).

Finally, we will use

\{0,1\}_{\mu_{i}}
and
\{0,1\}_{\beta}
. Consequently, for every
j\in[R]
we have that

{\sf Inf}_{x_{i}(j)}\left[h^{\prime}_{i}\right]=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}S,T\subseteq[R]\\
j\in S\end{subarray}}\widehat{h^{\prime}_{i}}(S,T)^{2}\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \textnormal{and}\ \ \ \ \ \ \ {\sf Inf}_{z_{i}(j)}\left[h^{\prime}_{i}\right]=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}S,T\subseteq[R]\\
j\in T\end{subarray}}\widehat{h^{\prime}_{i}}(S,T)^{2}.

Next, we have the following claim which shows that if
h_{i}
has small influences in the
R
-dimensional probability space
\Omega^{R}_{i}
, then
h^{\prime}_{i}
has small influences in the
2R
-dimensional space
\{0,1\}^{R}_{\mu_{i}}\otimes\{\bot,\top\}^{R}_{\beta}
.

Claim 8.3

The following holds for any fixed
i\in[r]
. For every
j\in[R]
, we have

\max\left\{{\sf Inf}_{x_{i}(j)}[h^{\prime}_{i}],{\sf Inf}_{z_{i}(j)}[h^{\prime}_{i}]\right\}\leq{\sf Inf}^{(\Omega_{i})}_{j}[h_{i}],

where recall that
{\sf Inf}^{(\Omega_{i})}_{j}[h_{i}]
is the influence of
(x_{i},z_{i})(j)
in
h_{i}
measured in the
R
-dimensional space
\Omega^{R}_{i}
i.e.,

{\sf Inf}^{(\Omega_{i})}_{j}[h_{i}]={\mathsf{E}}_{(x_{i}(j^{\prime}),z_{i}(j^{\prime}))_{j^{\prime}\neq j}}\left[{\sf Var}_{(x_{i}(j),z_{i}(j))}\left[f(x_{i},z_{i})~\Big|~(x_{i}(j^{\prime}),z_{i}(j^{\prime}))_{j^{\prime}\neq j}\right]\right].

Fix an
i\in[r]
, and let
h_{i}\in L_{2}(\Omega^{R}_{i})
and
h^{\prime}_{i}\in L_{2}(\{0,1\}^{R}_{\mu_{i}}\otimes\{\bot,\top\}^{R}_{\beta})
be as above. Now, since
\Omega_{i}:=\{0,1\}_{\mu_{i}}\otimes\{\bot,\top\}_{\beta}
, we can use the Fourier characters of
\{0,1\}_{\mu_{i}}
and
\{\bot,\top\}_{\beta}
to construct a Fourier basis for
\Omega_{i}
. Let us denote the Fourier characters corresponding to the probability space
\Omega_{i}
as
\{\overline{\phi}_{\alpha}:\Omega_{i}\to\mathbbm{R}\}_{\alpha\in\{0,1\}^{2}}
and define them as:

\overline{\phi}_{0,0}\equiv 1,\qquad\overline{\phi}_{1,0}(x,z)=\phi^{(\mu_{i})}(x),\qquad\overline{\phi}_{0,1}(x,z)=\phi^{(\beta)}(z),\qquad\overline{\phi}_{1,1}(x,z)=\phi^{(\mu_{i})}(x)\phi^{(\beta)}(z).

As we will soon see, it is convenient for us to index the Fourier characters of
\Omega_{i}
using elements of
\{0,1\}^{2}
instead of
\mathbbm{Z}_{\leq 3}
. Note that since
h_{i}\in L_{2}(\Omega^{R}_{i})
, we can use the above basis to write the Fourier expansion of
h_{i}
as:

h_{i}(x,z)=\sum_{\alpha\in(\{0,1\}^{2})^{R}}\widehat{h_{i}}(\alpha)\prod_{j\in[R]}\overline{\phi}_{\alpha(j)}\Big((x,z)(j)\Big).

(40)

Now consider the following one-to-one correspondence between
(\{0,1\}^{2})^{R}
and
2^{[R]}\times 2^{[R]}
. Given any
\alpha\in(\{0,1\}^{2})^{R}
, we can uniquely identify sets
S_{\alpha}
and
T_{\alpha}
where

S_{\alpha}=\left\{j\in[R]~\Big|~\alpha(j)\in\{(1,0),(1,1)\}\right\}\ \ \ \ \ \ \textnormal{and}\ \ \ \ \ \ T_{\alpha}=\left\{j\in[R]~\Big|~\alpha(j)\in\{(0,1),(1,1)\}\right\}.

Similarly, given
S,T\subseteq[R]
, there exists a unique
\alpha\in(\{0,1\}^{2})^{R}
such that
(S_{\alpha},T_{\alpha})=(S,T)
. Using this one-to-one correspondence along with the definition of
\overline{\phi}_{\alpha}
-characters, we re-index the summation and the summands in (
40
):

\displaystyle\sum_{\alpha\in(\{0,1\}^{2})^{R}}\widehat{h_{i}}(\alpha)\prod_{j\in[R]}\overline{\phi}_{\alpha(j)}\Big((x,z)(j)\Big)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\alpha\in(\{0,1\}^{2})^{R}}\widehat{h_{i}}(\alpha)\prod_{j:\alpha(j)\in\{(1,0),(1,1)\}}\phi^{(\mu_{i})}(x(j))\prod_{j^{\prime}:\alpha(j^{\prime})\in\{(0,1),(1,1)\}}\phi^{(\beta)}(z(j^{\prime}))

\displaystyle=\sum_{\alpha\in(\{0,1\}^{2})^{R}}\widehat{h_{i}}(\alpha)\prod_{j\in S_{\alpha}}\phi^{(\mu_{i})}(x(j))\prod_{j^{\prime}\in T_{\alpha}}\phi^{(\beta)}(z(j^{\prime})).

(41)

But then (
41
) expresses
h_{i}=h^{\prime}_{i}
as a multilinear polynomial in the Fourier basis corresponding to the
2R
-variate space
\{0,1\}^{R}_{\mu_{i}}\otimes\{\bot,\top\}^{R}_{\beta}
and hence it follows that (
41
) must be the Fourier expansion of
h^{\prime}_{i}
in the
2R
-dimensional space (as described in Observation
8.2
). In particular, using the uniqueness of Fourier expansion, we must have that
\widehat{h_{i}}(\alpha)=\widehat{h^{\prime}_{i}}(S_{\alpha},T_{\alpha})
for every
\alpha\in\{0,1\}^{2}
.

Using the above equivalence of the Fourier coefficients from the expansions of
h_{i}
and
h^{\prime}_{i}
, we can infer that

{\sf Inf}^{(\Omega_{i})}_{j}\big[h_{i}\big]=\sum_{\alpha:\alpha(j)\neq(0,0)}\widehat{h_{i}}(\alpha)^{2}=\sum_{j\in S\cup T}\widehat{h^{\prime}_{i}}(S,T)^{2}.

Hence, using the above along with Observation
8.2
we can conclude that

{\sf Inf}_{x_{i}(j)}\big[h^{\prime}_{i}\big]=\sum_{S\ni j}\sum_{T\subseteq[R]}\widehat{h^{\prime}_{i}}(S,T)^{2}\leq\sum_{j\in S\cup T}\widehat{h^{\prime}_{i}}(S,T)^{2}={\sf Inf}^{(\Omega_{i})}_{j}\big[h_{i}\big],

and similarly,

{\sf Inf}_{z_{i}(j)}\big[h^{\prime}_{i}\big]=\sum_{S\subseteq[R]}\sum_{T\ni j}\widehat{h^{\prime}_{i}}(S,T)^{2}\leq\sum_{j\in S\cup T}\widehat{h^{\prime}_{i}}(S,T)^{2}={\sf Inf}^{(\Omega_{i})}_{j}\big[h_{i}\big],

which completes the proof.

\square

\displaystyle=\sum_{S,T\subseteq[R]}\widehat{h^{\prime}_{i}}(S,T)\prod_{j\in S}\phi^{(\mu_{i})}(x_{i}(j))\prod_{j^{\prime}\in T}\phi^{(\beta)}(z_{i}(j^{\prime}))

•

Finally, let

Now using the above notation, we can rewrite the term we wish to upper bound in the lemma:

\displaystyle{\mathsf{E}}_{(x_{i},z_{i})_{i\in[r]}\sim\mathcal{D}^{R}_{e}}\left[\prod_{i\in[r]}h_{i}(x_{i},z_{i})\right]

\displaystyle={\mathsf{E}}_{(x_{i},z_{i})_{i\in[r]}\sim\mathcal{D}^{R}_{e}}\left[\prod_{i\in[r]}h^{\prime}_{i}(x_{i},z_{i})\right]

\displaystyle={\mathsf{E}}_{\mathcal{W}}\left[\prod_{i\in[r]}Q_{i}(\mathcal{W})\right]

\displaystyle={\mathsf{E}}_{\mathcal{W}}\left[\Psi(Q(\mathcal{W}))\right].

1.

First, we construct a Gaussian ensemble sequence

2.

Next, we will use the invariance principle to show that

3.

We will then use the Exchangeable Gaussians Theorem (Theorem

4.

Finally, we will again use the invariance principle to move the analysis back into the Boolean space (Lemma

Step 1: Gaussian Ensemble Sequence Construction.

We begin by making a couple of observations about the first and second moments of the variables in

Claim 8.4

For every
j\in[R]
and

Claim 8.5

For every

\displaystyle{\mathsf{E}}\left[\mathcal{W}^{z}_{j}[i]\mathcal{W}^{z}_{j}[i^{\prime}]\right]

\displaystyle={\mathsf{E}}_{(z_{i})_{i\in[r]}}\left[\phi^{(\beta)}(z_{i}(j))\phi^{(\beta)}(z_{i^{\prime}}(j))\right]

Step 2: Invariance Application Step

In this section, we use the Invariance Principle to move the analysis to the Gaussian space, as stated in the following lemma.

Lemma 8.9

Let
\mathcal{W},\mathcal{G}

{\mathsf{E}}_{\mathcal{W}}\left[\Psi(Q(\mathcal{W}))\right]\leq{\mathsf{E}}_{\mathcal{G}}\left[\Psi(Q(\mathcal{G}))\right]+C_{r}\sqrt{r}\tau^{O(\eta\kappa/r^{2})},

Our first step here is to verify that the ensemble sequences

,

C_{1}

From Claim

{\mathcal{W}}
and

C_{2}

Due to the

C_{3}

From the setting of the lemma, for every

and

, we have that

\displaystyle{\sf Inf}_{\mathcal{W}^{x}_{j}}\left[Q_{i}\right]

\displaystyle={\mathsf{E}}_{\mathcal{W}^{x}_{j^{\prime}\neq j},\mathcal{W}^{z}}\left[{\rm Var}_{\mathcal{W}^{x}_{j}}\left[Q_{i}(\mathcal{W})\Big|(\mathcal{W}^{x}_{j^{\prime}})_{j^{\prime}\neq j},\mathcal{W}^{z}\right]\right]

\displaystyle\overset{1}{=}{\mathsf{E}}_{(x_{i}(j^{\prime}))_{j^{\prime}\neq j},z_{i}}\left[{\rm Var}_{x_{i}(j)}\left[h^{\prime}_{i}(x_{i},z_{i})\Big|(x_{i}(j^{\prime}))_{j^{\prime}\neq j},z_{i}\right]\right]

\displaystyle={\sf Inf}_{x_{i}(j)}\left[h^{\prime}_{i}\right]

\displaystyle\overset{2}{\leq}{\sf Inf}^{(\Omega_{i})}_{j}\left[h_{i}\right]\leq\tau,

where in step
1
we again use the observation that for every
\ell\in[R]
, the only variable in
\mathcal{W}^{x}_{\ell}
which appears in
Q_{i}(\mathcal{W})
is
\mathcal{W}^{x}_{\ell}[i]=\phi^{(\mu_{i})}(x_{i}(\ell))
(and similarly for
\mathcal{W}^{z}_{\ell}
), step
2
uses Claim
8.3
, and the final inequality follows from the assumption on
h_{i}
in the setting of the lemma. Using identical arguments, we also have that
{\sf Inf}_{\mathcal{W}^{z}_{j}}\left[Q_{i}\right]\leq\tau
for every
i\in[r]
and
j\in[R]
.

C_{4}

Furthermore, for every
i\in[r]
, from the setting of the lemma we have
{\rm Var}[Q^{>d}_{i}]={\rm Var}[h^{>d}_{i}]\leq(1-\eta)^{d}
for every
d\geq\Omega(\log(1/\tau)/\log(1/\gamma^{r}\eta^{r}\beta^{r}))
.

Then the conditions
C_{1}
-
C_{4}
from above show that the ensemble sequences
\mathcal{W},\mathcal{G}
along the
r
-dimensional vector polynomial
Q(\mathcal{W})
meet the conditions required to invoke Theorem
3.5
. Therefore, using Theorem
3.5
along with the fact that
\Psi(\cdot)
is
\sqrt{r}
-lipschitz, we can upper bound (
8.1
) as:

\displaystyle{\mathsf{E}}_{\mathcal{W}}\left[\Psi(Q(\mathcal{W}))\right]

\displaystyle\leq{\mathsf{E}}_{\mathcal{G}}\left[\Psi(Q(\mathcal{G}))\right]+C_{r}\sqrt{r}\tau^{\frac{C\eta\mu\beta}{(r\log(1/\beta\eta\gamma))}}

\displaystyle\leq{\mathsf{E}}_{\mathcal{G}}\left[\Psi(Q(\mathcal{G}))\right]+C_{r}\sqrt{r}\tau^{O(\eta\kappa/r^{2})},

where the last step uses
\kappa=\beta/\log(1/\gamma)
.

\square

8.4
Step 3: Decoupling the
\mathcal{G}^{z}
variables

In this section, we show that we can move from
\{\mathcal{G}^{z}_{j}\}_{j}
to an ensemble of
completely
independent Gaussians (while retaining the correlation structure of
\{\mathcal{G}^{x}_{j}\}_{j})
, at the cost of multiplicative and (negligible) additive factors, as stated in the following lemma.

Lemma 8.10

Let
\mathcal{G}
be as above, and let
\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}:=(\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}^{x}_{1},\ldots,\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}^{x}_{R},\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}^{z}_{1},\ldots,\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}^{z}_{R})
be another independent sequence of Gaussian ensembles defined as follows. Let
\tilde{g}_{z,1},\ldots,\tilde{g}_{z,r}\sim N(0_{R},I_{R})
be
r
independent
R
-dimensional Gaussian vector variables. For every
j\in[R]
, define

\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}^{x}_{j}:=\mathcal{G}^{x}_{j},\ \ \ \ \ \ \textnormal{and}\ \ \ \ \ \ \widetilde{\mathcal{G}}^{z}_{j}[i]:=\begin{cases}1&\mbox{ if }i=0,\\
\tilde{g}_{z,i}(j)&\mbox{ if }i\in[r].\end{cases}

(47)

Then we have that

{\mathsf{E}}_{\mathcal{G}}\left[\Psi(Q(\mathcal{G}))\right]\leq 2^{r}{\mathsf{E}}_{\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}}\left[\Psi(Q(\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}))\right]+\mu^{r}.

Before, we prove the above lemma, we need to setup some notation and observations. Denote
\mathcal{G}=(\mathcal{G}^{x},\mathcal{G}^{z})
where
\mathcal{G}^{x}:=(\mathcal{G}^{x}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{G}^{x}_{R})
, and similarly
\mathcal{G}^{z}:=(\mathcal{G}^{z}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{G}^{z}_{R})
. Then, note that by construction
\mathcal{G}^{x}
and
\mathcal{G}^{z}
satisfy the following properties:

(a)

The ensemble sequences
\mathcal{G}^{x}
and
\mathcal{G}^{z}
are independent w.r.t. each other, and therefore for any fixing of
\mathcal{G}^{x}
, the distribution of
\mathcal{G}^{z}
is unchanged.

(b)

By construction, the (vector) random variables
g_{z,1},\ldots,g_{z,r}
from
\mathcal{G}^{z}
are independent
\rho
-correlated copies of a Gaussian vector
g
(Observation
8.7
).

Also observe that by definition, for every
i\in[r]
, we have that
\Pi_{[0,1]}(Q_{i}(\mathcal{G}))
is actually just a function of the Gaussian random vectors
g_{x,i}
and
g_{z,i}
– this can be formalized as follows. For every
i\in[r]
, we define the function
P_{i}:\mathbbm{R}^{R}\times\mathbbm{R}^{R}\to[0,1]
as:

P_{i}(a,b)=\Pi_{[0,1]}\left(\sum_{S,T\subseteq[R]}\widehat{h^{\prime}_{i}}(S,T)\prod_{j\in S}a(j)\prod_{j^{\prime}\in T}b(j^{\prime})\right).

Then note that by definition of
Q_{i}
(from (
42
)) and
\mathcal{G}
(from (
45
)), we have that the following identity:

P_{i}(g_{x,i},g_{z,i})=\Pi_{[0,1]}\left(\sum_{S,T\subseteq[R]}\widehat{h^{\prime}_{i}}(S,T)\prod_{j\in S}\mathcal{G}^{x}_{j}[i]\prod_{j^{\prime}\in T}\mathcal{G}^{z}_{j^{\prime}}[i]\right)=\Pi_{[0,1]}(Q_{i}(\mathcal{G})).

Next, for any fixing of
g_{x,i}
, let
P^{(i)}_{g_{x,i}}:\mathbbm{R}^{R}\to[0,1]
denote the restriction of
P_{i}
to the realization of
g_{x,i}
i.e.,
P^{(i)}_{g_{x,i}}(b)=P_{i}(g_{x,i},b)
. Using these notations and the above observations, we can rewrite:

\displaystyle{\mathsf{E}}_{\mathcal{G}}\left[\Psi(Q(\mathcal{G}))\right]={\mathsf{E}}_{\mathcal{G}}\left[\prod_{i\in[r]}\Pi_{[0,1]}\left(Q_{i}(\mathcal{G})\right)\right]

\displaystyle={\mathsf{E}}_{\mathcal{G}^{x}}{\mathsf{E}}_{\mathcal{G}^{z}}\left[\prod_{i\in[r]}P_{i}(g_{x,i},g_{z,i})\right]

\displaystyle={\mathsf{E}}_{\mathcal{G}^{x}}{\mathsf{E}}_{\mathcal{G}^{z}}\left[\prod_{i\in[r]}P^{(i)}_{g_{x,i}}(g_{z,i})\right]

\displaystyle={\mathsf{E}}_{\mathcal{G}^{x}}{\mathsf{E}}_{g\sim N(0,I_{R})}{\mathsf{E}}_{g_{z,1},\ldots,g_{z,r}\underset{\rho}{\sim}g}\left[\prod_{i\in[r]}P^{(i)}_{g_{x,i}}(g_{z,i})\right],

(48)

where the second step uses item
(a)
and the last step uses item
(b)
. Now we state and prove the following key lemma which bounds the inner expectation over
\mathcal{G}^{z}
for each fixing of
\mathcal{G}^{x}
.

Lemma 8.11

Fix a realization of
\mathcal{G}^{x}
, and let
g_{x,1},\ldots,g_{x,r}
be the vector-variables corresponding to
\mathcal{G}^{x}
. Furthermore, for any
i\in[r]
, let
P^{(i)}_{g_{x,i}}
be the restriction of
P_{i}
to
g_{x,i}
as defined above. Then,

{\mathsf{E}}_{g\sim N(0_{R},I_{R})}{\mathsf{E}}_{g_{z,1},\ldots,g_{z,r}\underset{\rho}{\sim}g}\left[\prod_{i\in[r]}P^{(i)}_{g_{x,i}}(g_{z,i})\right]\leq 2^{r}\prod_{i\in[r]}{\mathsf{E}}_{\mathcal{G}^{z}}\left[P^{(i)}_{g_{x,i}}(g_{z,i})\right]+\mu^{r}.

We first observe that the vector random variables
g_{z,1},\ldots,g_{z,r}
satisfy the following properties:

•

For every
i\in[r]
,
g_{z,i}
is marginally distributed as
N(0_{R},I_{R})
(Observation
8.7
).

•

Using (
46
), and that
(g_{z,i}(\ell))_{i\in[r]}
are independent across
\ell\in[R]
, for every
i\neq i^{\prime}
we have

{\rm Cov}(g_{x,i},g_{x,i^{\prime}})={\mathsf{E}}\left[g_{x,i}\left(g_{x,i^{\prime}}\right)^{\top}\right]=\rho^{2}I_{R}.

Furthermore,
P^{(1)}_{g_{x,1}},\ldots,P^{(r)}_{g_{x,r}}
are all
[0,1]
-valued functions. Hence using the Exchangeable Gaussians Theorem (Corollary
3.8
), we get that

{\mathsf{E}}_{g\sim N(0_{R},I_{R})}{\mathsf{E}}_{g_{z,1},\ldots,g_{z,r}\underset{\rho}{\sim}g}\left[\prod_{i\in[r]}P^{(i)}_{g_{x,i}}(g_{z,i})\right]\leq\Lambda_{\rho}\Big(\delta_{1},\ldots,\delta_{r}\Big),

(49)

where
\delta_{i}:={\mathsf{E}}_{\mathcal{G}^{z}}P^{(i)}_{g_{x,i}}(g_{z,i})
for every
i\in[r]
. We now proceed to further upper bound the above RHS. Let
\delta_{\ell}
be the minimum among
\delta_{1},\ldots,\delta_{r}
. We consider two cases depending on the value of
\delta_{\ell}
.

Case (i)
Suppose
\delta_{\ell}\leq\mu^{r}
. Then,

\displaystyle\Lambda_{\rho}\Big(\delta_{1},\ldots,\delta_{r}\Big)

\displaystyle=\Pr_{g\sim N(0,1)}\Pr_{g_{1},\ldots,g_{r}\underset{\rho}{\sim}g}\left[~\forall j\in[r],g_{j}\leq\Phi^{-1}(\delta_{j})\right]

\displaystyle\leq\Pr_{g\sim N(0,1)}\Pr_{g_{\ell}\underset{\rho}{\sim}g}\left[g_{\ell}\leq\Phi^{-1}(\delta_{\ell})\right]

\displaystyle=\delta_{\ell}\leq\mu^{r}.

Case (ii)
Suppose
\delta_{\ell}>\mu^{r}
. Then our choice of
\rho
implies that

\rho=\frac{1}{4r^{2}\log(1/\mu)}=\frac{1}{4r\log(1/\mu^{r})}\leq\frac{1}{4r\log(1/\delta_{\ell})}.

Then, using Lemma
8.1
we can bound,

\Lambda_{\rho}(\delta_{1},\ldots,\delta_{r})\leq 2^{r}\prod_{i\in[r]}\delta_{i}.

Combining (
49
) with the bounds from the two cases we get that,

\displaystyle{\mathsf{E}}_{g\sim N(0,I_{R})}{\mathsf{E}}_{g_{z,1},\ldots,g_{z,r}\underset{\rho}{\sim}g}\left[\prod_{i\in[r]}P^{(i)}_{g_{x,i}}(g_{z,i})\right]

\displaystyle\leq 2^{r}\prod_{i\in[r]}\delta_{i}+\mu^{r}

\displaystyle=2^{r}\prod_{i\in[r]}{\mathsf{E}}_{\mathcal{G}^{z}}\left[P^{(i)}_{g_{x,i}}(g_{z,i})\right]+\mu^{r},

which completes the proof of the lemma.

\square

Using the above lemma, we can now prove Lemma
8.10

Recall that using the computation from (
48
) we have that

{\mathsf{E}}_{\mathcal{G}}\left[\prod_{i\in[r]}\Pi_{[0,1]}\left(Q_{i}(\mathcal{G})\right)\right]={\mathsf{E}}_{\mathcal{G}^{x}}{\mathsf{E}}_{g\sim N(0,I_{R})}{\mathsf{E}}_{g_{z,1},\ldots,g_{z,r}\underset{\rho}{\sim}g}\left[\prod_{i\in[r]}P^{(i)}_{g_{x,i}}(g_{z,i})\right].

We continue with bounding the RHS from above. Since
\mathcal{G}^{x}
and
\mathcal{G}^{z}
are independent, we can apply Lemma
8.11
to the inner expectation for each fixing of
\mathcal{G}^{x}
and get that:

\displaystyle{\mathsf{E}}_{\mathcal{G}^{x}}{\mathsf{E}}_{g\sim N(0,I_{R})}{\mathsf{E}}_{g_{z,1},\ldots,g_{z,r}\underset{\rho}{\sim}g}\left[\prod_{i\in[r]}P^{(i)}_{g_{x,i}}(g_{z,i})\right]

\displaystyle\leq 2^{r}{\mathsf{E}}_{\mathcal{G}^{x}}\left[\prod_{i\in[r]}{\mathsf{E}}_{\mathcal{G}^{z}}P^{(i)}_{g_{x,i}}(g_{z,i})\right]+\mu^{r}

\displaystyle=2^{r}{\mathsf{E}}_{\mathcal{G}^{x}}\left[\prod_{i\in[r]}{\mathsf{E}}_{\mathcal{G}^{z}}P_{i}(g_{x,i},g_{z,i})\right]+\mu^{r}

\displaystyle=2^{r}{\mathsf{E}}_{\mathcal{G}^{x}}{\mathsf{E}}_{\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}^{z}}\left[\prod_{i\in[r]}P_{i}(g_{x,i},\tilde{g}_{z,i})\right]+\mu^{r}

\displaystyle=2^{r}{\mathsf{E}}_{\mathcal{G}^{x}}{\mathsf{E}}_{\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}^{z}}\left[\prod_{i\in[r]}\Pi_{[0,1]}\left(Q_{i}(\mathcal{G}^{x},\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}^{z})\right)\right]+\mu^{r}

\displaystyle=2^{r}{\mathsf{E}}_{\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}}\left[\prod_{i\in[r]}\Pi_{[0,1]}(Q_{i}(\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}))\right]+\mu^{r}

\displaystyle=2^{r}{\mathsf{E}}_{\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}}\left[\Psi(Q(\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}))\right]+\mu^{r},

which concludes the proof.

\square

SSEH Decoding Lemmas

Influence Decoding in SSEH
. The following lemma says that if there exists a family of functions defined on the noisy graph
G^{\otimes R}_{\eta}
such that a constant fraction of the functions have influential coordinates, then it can be used to decode a small non-expanding set in
G
.

Lemma 9.1
(Lemma 10.1
[
GL22b
]
,
[
RST12
]
)

Let
(\Omega^{R},\gamma^{R})
be a product probability space. Let
\{f_{A}\}_{A\in V^{R}}
be a set of functions such that
f_{A}:\Omega^{R}\to[0,1]
. Furthermore, suppose the class of functions are permutation respecting i.e., for every
A\in V^{R}
,
\omega\in\Omega^{R}
and permutation
\pi:[R]\to[R]
we have
f_{\pi(A)}(\pi(\omega))=f_{A}(\omega)
. For every
A\in V^{R}
, define the averaged functions
g_{A}:={\mathsf{E}}_{B\sim G^{\otimes R}_{\eta}(A)}f_{B}
. Then if
G
is a NO instance of
(\varepsilon,\delta,M)
-
SmallSetExpansion
, then

\Pr_{A\sim V^{R}}\left[\max_{i\in[R]}{\sf Inf}_{i}\left[\mathcal{T}^{(\Omega)}_{1-\eta}g_{A}\right]>\tau\right]\leq\frac{\nu^{2}}{8r}.

The proof of the above follows using the following lemma from
[
RST12
]
.

Lemma 9.2
(
[
RST12
]
)

There exists a constant
\eta_{0}\in(0,1)
such that the following holds for any
\eta\in(0,\eta_{0}]
and
R
large enough. Suppose
(A,B)
be a distribution over pairs of vertices defined according to the following process: let
A\sim V^{R}
and
B\sim G^{\otimes R}_{\eta}(A)
. Now, suppose there exists a function
F:V^{R}\to[R]
such that it satisfies

\Pr_{(A,B)}\Pr_{\pi_{A},\pi_{B}\sim\mathbbm{S}_{R}}\left[\pi^{-1}_{A}(F(\pi_{A}(A)))=\pi^{-1}_{A}(F(\pi_{B}(B)))\right]\geq\zeta.

(50)

Then there exists a subset
S\subseteq V
such that
{\sf vol}(S)\in\left[\frac{\zeta}{16R},\frac{3}{\eta R}\right]
satisfying
\phi_{S}(G)\leq 1-\zeta
.

Using the above, we can prove Lemma
9.1
.

Let
\{f_{A}\}_{A\in V^{R}}
be the collection of functions in the setting of the lemma. For every
A\in V^{R}
, we introduce two sets
L_{A,1},L_{A,2}\subseteq[R]
which are defined as follows.

L_{A,1}:=\left\{j\in[R]~\Big|~{\sf Inf}_{j}\left[\mathcal{T}_{1-\eta}f_{A}\right]\geq\frac{\tau}{2}\right\}\ \ \ \ \ \ \textnormal{ and }\ \ \ \ \ \ L_{A,2}:=\left\{j\in[R]~\Big|~{\sf Inf}_{j}\left[\mathcal{T}_{1-\eta}g_{A}\right]\geq\tau\right\}

We can verify that
|L_{A,1}|,|L_{A,2}|\leq 2/\eta\tau
(for e.g.,
[
KKMO07
]
). Let
V^{\prime}\subset V^{R}
be the subset of vertices for which

\max_{j\in[R]}{\sf Inf}^{(\Omega)}_{j}\left[\mathcal{T}_{1-\eta}g_{A}\right]\geq\tau,

and for every such choice of
A\in V^{\prime}
, let
j_{A}
be fixed element of
L_{A,2}
. Note that for any such choice of
A\in V^{\prime}
we have

\tau\leq{\sf Inf}^{(\Omega)}_{j_{A}}\left[\mathcal{T}_{1-\eta}g_{A}\right]={\sf Inf}^{(\Omega)}_{j_{A}}\left[\mathcal{T}_{1-\eta}{\mathsf{E}}_{B\sim G^{\otimes R}_{1-\eta}(A)}f_{B}\right]\leq{\mathsf{E}}_{B\sim G^{\otimes R}_{1-\eta}(A)}{\sf Inf}^{(\Omega)}_{j_{A}}\left[\mathcal{T}_{1-\eta}f_{B}\right],

where the inequality follows using the convexity of influences. Therefore, for any such choices of
A
, for at least
\tau/2
fraction of choices of
B\sim G^{\otimes R}_{\eta}(A)
, we have
{\sf Inf}^{(\Omega)}_{j_{A}}\left[\mathcal{T}_{1-\eta}f_{B}\right]\geq\tau/2
; call this set of vertices
V(A)
. Now consider the following distribution over functions
F:V^{R}\to[R]
: for every
A\in V^{R}
, do the following independently.

•

W.p.
1/2
, if
L_{A,1}\neq\emptyset
, assign
F(A)\sim L_{A,1}
u.a.r., otherwise assign
F(A)
arbitrarily.

•

W.p.
1/2
, if
L_{A,2}\neq\emptyset
, assign
F(A)\sim L_{A,2}
u.a.r., otherwise assign
F(A)
arbitrarily.

Now let us analyze the probability of the event in (
50
) w.r.t above distribution over assignments. To that end, observe that for
A\sim V^{R}
, with probability at least
\nu^{2}/8r
, we have
A\in V^{\prime}
. Fixing such a choice of
A\in V^{\prime}
, note that for

,
B\in V(A)
with probability at least
\tau/2
. Fixing such a choice of
(A,B)

\{j_{A}\}\in L_{A,2}\cap L_{B,1}

\{f_{A^{\prime}}\}

are permutation respecting, for any pair of permutations

\pi_{A},\pi_{B}

, and hence
\pi^{-1}_{A}(F(\pi_{A}(A)))=\pi^{-1}_{B}(F(\pi_{B}(B)))
. Therefore, there exists a choice of

There exists a
\mu^{3/4}
-approximation algorithm for
\mu
-constrained instances of Max-CSP
(\psi)
.

Towards proving the above lemma, we shall need the following useful observation.

Observation C.2

Suppose
H=(V,E)
is a
{\sf D}k{\sf S}
instance with optimal value at least
c
. Then there exists an efficient algorithm that returns an assignment with value at least
c^{3}
.

The algorithm is the following: solve the basic SDP for
{\sf D}k{\sf S}
on
H
, and then do independent rounding from the SDP solution. If the optimal value of the instance is
c
, then the optimal SDP value is also at least
c
. By averaging, at least
c/2
edges would then have SDP value at least
c/2
. Fix any such edge
e\in E
. Then, denoting the local distributions corresponding to the SDP solution as
\theta
, we have

\Pr_{\theta}\Big[X_{i}=1\Big],\Pr_{\theta}\left[X_{j}=1\right]\geq\Pr_{\theta}\left[X_{i}=X_{j}=1\right]\geq c/2.

Therefore, any such edge will be induced by the sampled set with probability at least
c^{2}/4
, which implies that the expected number of edges induced by the rounded solution will be at least
c^{3}/8
. Using Chernoff bound, the weight of the rounded set will be
\approx\mu
w.h.p, from which the claim follows.

\square

Now we prove the above lemma.

Let
H=(V,E,w)
be the Max-CSP(
\psi
) instance. Denote
c:={\sf Opt}_{\mu}(H)
. We consider two cases:

Case(i)
Suppose
c\leq\mu^{1/4}
. Note that, since the weights are bounded by
1/|V|^{-1/100}
, randomly setting
\mu
-fraction of variables to
1
will yield a feasible labeling that satisfies
\mu/2
-fraction of constraints using the assignment
(1,0,0)
w.h.p, and hence this will yield a
\mu/\mu^{1/4}=\mu^{3/4}
-approximation.

Case (ii)
Suppose
c>\mu^{1/4}
, and let
\sigma^{*}
be the corresponding optimal labeling. Now suppose
\sigma^{*}
satisfies at least
c/2
-fraction of constraints using
(1,0,0)
assignment. Then solving the LP relaxation corresponding to the accepting string
(1,0,0)
and rounding it (similar to Max-
k
-Coverage) yields a
\mu
-weight assignment that satisfies at least
(1-1/e)c/2
-fraction of constraints.

Otherwise, if
\sigma^{*}
satisfies at least
c/2
-constraints using the
(0,1,1)
assignment, then we can do the following. Let
\tilde{H}=(V,\tilde{E})
be the graph where for every edge
e=(i,j,k)\in E
, we introduce an edge
(j,k)
of weight
w(e)
. Then note that there exists a set of weight
\mu
that induces at least
c/2
weight of edges in
\tilde{H}
. Then using the algorithm from Observation
C.2
, we can find a set of weight
\mu
that induces at least
c^{3}/8
-weight of edges in
\tilde{H}
– denote the corresponding labeling as
\sigma
. Note that
\sigma
is not guaranteed to be a good labeling for
H
as for some of the edges in
\tilde{H}
induced by set indicated by the labeling, the first vertex from the corresponding constraint in
H
might also be included in the set i.e., the assignment to the corresponding edge ends up being
(1,1,1)
instead of
(0,1,1)
. To fix this, we construct another labeling
\sigma^{\prime}
from
\sigma
by setting a random
\mu/2
-fraction of ones to zeros, and
\mu/2
-fraction of zeros to ones. It is easy to see that this is also a feasible labeling that will now satisfy at least
\Omega(c^{3})
-constraints in
\tilde{H}
, thus yielding a
\Omega(c^{2})=\Omega(\sqrt{\mu})
approximation.

\square
