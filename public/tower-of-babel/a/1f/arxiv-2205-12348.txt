Central Limit Theorem for Euclidean Minimal Spanning Acycles
Primoz Skraba, D. Yogeshwaran

Abstract
. We investigate asymptotics for the minimal spanning acycles of the (Alpha)-Delaunay complex on a stationary Poisson process on ℝ d , d ≥ 2 \mathbb{R}^{d},d\geq 2 . Minimal spanning acycles are topological (or higher-dimensional) generalization of minimal spanning trees. We establish a central limit theorem for total weight of the minimal spanning acycle on a Poisson-Delaunay complex. Our approach also allows us to establish central limit theorems for sum of birth times and lifetimes in the persistent diagram of the Delaunay complex. The key to our proof is in showing the so-called weak stabilization of minimal spanning acycles which proceeds by establishing suitable chain maps and uses matroidal properties of minimal spanning acycles. In contrast to the proof of weak-stabilization for Euclidean minimal spanning trees via percolation-theoretic estimates, our weak-stabilization proof is algebraic in nature and provides an alternative proof even in the case of minimal spanning trees.

1.
Introduction

Let
\mathcal{P}_{n}=\{X_{1},\ldots,X_{N_{n}}\}
be the restriction of a stationary Poisson point process
\mathcal{P}
with intensity
\lambda>0
to the window
W_{n}:=\left[-\frac{n^{1/d}}{2},\frac{n^{1/d}}{2}\right]^{d}
. Equivalently,
N_{n}
is a Poisson(
\lambda n
) random variable and
X_{i}
’s are i.i.d. uniform points in
W_{n}
independent of
N_{n}
. Consider the complete graph on
\mathcal{P}_{n}
with edge-weights given by the Euclidean distance between the points. A classical problem at the intersection of combinatorial optimization and geometric probability is to investigate the total edge-lengths (or total weighted edge-length) of the minimal spanning tree on this weighted graph. Strong laws for this statistic and many such Euclidean optimization functionals were proven using subadditive ergodic theory methods; see
Steele 1997
,
Yukich 1998
. For the case of sum of power-weighted edge-lengths, i.e., edge-lengths raised to
d
-th power, a strong law was also proven using local weak convergence ideas; see
Aldous and Steele 1992
. The central limit theorem for the same was remarked to be a difficult problem in
[
1
]
. It was solved independently by
Alexander 1996
and
Kesten and Lee 1996
. The former used an approach proposed by
Ramey 1983
in
d=2
while the latter used a martingale-difference central limit theorem which worked in all dimensions and for sum of weighted edge-lengths as well. Both proofs, however, use certain percolation-theoretic estimates. The aim of this article is to prove a central limit theorem (CLT) for a topological generalization of minimal spanning trees called minimal spanning acycles on weighted Poisson-Delaunay complexes. The main challenge in this extension is the lack of percolation-theoretic analogue in higher dimensions. Strong laws for statistics of minimal spanning acycles follow from the results of
[
14
]
where sub-additive ergodic theory methods are used.

Delaunay Complex.

Let
{\mathcal{X}}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a locally-finite point-set with points in general position. Recall that general position means that no three points are colinear and no
d+2
points lie on a common sphere, i.e., are cocircular. We define the
Voronoi cell
of

as

\mathrm{Vor}_{{\mathcal{X}}}(x):=\{y:|y-x|\leq|y-x^{\prime}|,\forall x^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{X}}\},

i.e., the set of points in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
whose closest point in
{\mathcal{X}}
is
x
. Each
\mathrm{Vor}_{{\mathcal{X}}}(x)
is a convex polyhedron and the collection
\{\mathrm{Vor}_{{\mathcal{X}}}(x)\}_{x\in{\mathcal{X}}}
forms a partition of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with disjoint interiors known as the
Voronoi diagram or tessellation
. See Figure
1
for an example.

Definition 1.1

(Delaunay Complex)
.

Let
{\mathcal{X}}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a locally-finite point-set with points in general position. The Delaunay complex on
{\mathcal{X}}
, denoted as
\mathcal{D}({\mathcal{X}})
is the following simplicial complex:

\mathcal{D}({\mathcal{X}}):=\cup_{k=0}^{\infty}\{[x_{0},\ldots,x_{k}]:\cap_{i=0}^{k}\mathrm{Vor}_{{\mathcal{X}}}(x_{i})\neq\emptyset\}.

The element
[x_{0},\ldots,x_{k}]
is called a
k
-face of the Delaunay complex and the collection of
k
-faces of the Delaunay complex is denoted by
\mathcal{F}_{k}({\mathcal{X}})
. Generic faces of
\mathcal{D}({\mathcal{X}})
is denoted by
\sigma,\tau
et al. The Delaunay graph on
{\mathcal{X}}
is the graph with vertex set
{\mathcal{X}}
and edge-set given by
1
-faces.
We define the weight function on the Delaunay complex as follows :

w([x_{0},\ldots,x_{k}])=\inf\{s:\cap_{i=0}^{k}B_{s}(x_{i})\cap\mathrm{Vor}_{{\mathcal{X}}}(x_{i})\neq\emptyset\}

where
B_{s}(x_{i})
denotes the ball of radius
s
centered on
x_{i}
.

It is a standard fact that if points are in general position, the Delanauy complex and the Voronoi diagram are dual to one another and that each simplex in the Delaunay complex corresponds to an empty circumsphere containing its vertices, with the weight being the radius of this sphere. The above weighted (simplicial) complex is also known as the Alpha-Delaunay complex. A closely related weight function

w^{\prime}([x_{0},\ldots,x_{k}])=\inf\{s:\cap_{i=0}^{k}B_{s}(x_{i})\neq\emptyset\},\,[x_{0},\ldots,x_{k}]\in\mathcal{D}({\mathcal{X}})

gives rise to the Delaunay-Čech complex; see
Bauer and Edelsbrunner 2017
. Our results apply to Delaunay-Čech complex as well but we restrict to Alpha-Delaunay complex for convenience, referring to it simply as the Delaunay complex.

The dimension of the Delaunay complex is the dimension of the largest face, which under the general position assumption, is the dimension of the ambient space -
d
.

-spanning acycle to be empty set. A

-spanning acycle

Given a spanning acycle (or any collection of faces), one can define the weight of the spanning acycle to be the sum of weights of faces in the spanning acycle i.e., if

Immediately, one can define

\mathbb{R}^{3}

Poisson-Delaunay Complexes.

The object of interest to us are MSAs on the Poisson-Delaunay complex. We take
{\mathcal{X}}=\mathcal{P}
, the stationary Poisson point process of intensity
\lambda>0
and consider the Delaunay complex
\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{P})
. More specifically, we set
\mathcal{P}_{n}:=\mathcal{P}\cap W_{n}
, the restriction of the stationary Poisson process to the window
W_{n}=[\frac{-n^{1/d}}{2},\frac{n^{1/d}}{2}]
and investigate the asymptotic behaviour for
k
-minimal spanning acycles on the sequence of Delaunay complexes
\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{P}_{n})
. Since the face-weights for a fixed dimension

are a.s. distinct, we have that
\MSA(\mathcal{P}_{n}):=\MSA(\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{P}_{n}))
is a.s. unique by
Skraba et al. 2020
. The statistic of interest for us is
the sum of face-weights
defined as

(1)

\mathbf{M}^{\phi}_{k}(\mathcal{P}_{n})=\mathbf{M}^{\phi}_{k}(\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{P}_{n})):=\sum_{\sigma\in\MSA(\mathcal{P}_{n})}\phi(w(\sigma)),

where
\phi:\mathbb{R}_{+}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
is a strictly increasing function. We denote
\mathbf{M}^{\phi}_{k}
by
\mathbf{M}_{k}
when
\phi
is identity. From the relation between persistent homology and minimal spanning acycles (see Theorem
2.2
), we know that
\mathbf{M}_{k}(\mathcal{P}_{n})
is the sum of death times in the persistence diagram of
\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{P}_{n})
. With this connection, it is natural also to study sum of birth times as well a sum of lifetimes in a persistence diagram. We define them as

(2)

\displaystyle\mathbf{B}^{\phi}_{k}(\mathcal{P}_{n})

\displaystyle:=\mathbf{B}^{\phi}_{k}(\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{P}_{n}))=\sum_{\sigma\in\mathcal{F}_{k}(\mathcal{P}_{n})\setminus MSA(\mathcal{P}_{n})}\phi(w(\sigma))

(3)

\displaystyle\mathbf{L}_{k}(\mathcal{P}_{n})

\displaystyle:=\mathbf{L}_{k}(\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{P}_{n}))=\mathbf{M}_{k}(\mathcal{P}_{n})-\mathbf{B}_{k-1}(\mathcal{P}_{n}).

Note that trivially
B_{0}(\mathcal{P}_{n})=0
as
\mathcal{F}_{0}(\mathcal{P}_{n})=\mathcal{P}_{n},\MSA_{0}(\mathcal{P}_{n})=\emptyset
and
w(v)=0
for all
v\in\mathcal{P}_{n}
. Thus,
\mathbf{L}_{1}(\mathcal{P}_{n})=\mathbf{M}_{1}(\mathcal{P}_{n})
(the total edge-length of minimal spanning tree).

Strong laws for
\mathbf{B}^{\phi}_{k}(\mathcal{P}_{n})
and
\mathbf{L}_{k}(\mathcal{P}_{n})
can be deduced from strong laws for
\mathbf{M}^{\phi}_{k}(\mathcal{P}_{n})
and
\mathbf{F}^{\phi}_{k}(\mathcal{P}_{n})
. Strong laws and central limit theorem for
\mathbf{F}^{\phi}_{k}(\mathcal{P}_{n}):=\sum_{\sigma\in\mathcal{F}_{k}(\mathcal{P}_{n})}\phi(w(\sigma))
can be deduced from
Penrose and Yukich 2002
and
Penrose and Yukich 2001
. Strong laws for
\mathbf{M}^{\phi}_{k}(\mathcal{P}_{n})
follow from
Divol and Polonik 2019
. Hence, we focus on central limit theorems for
\mathbf{B}^{\phi}_{k}(\mathcal{P}_{n})
,
\mathbf{M}^{\phi}_{k}(\mathcal{P}_{n})
and
\mathbf{L}_{k}(\mathcal{P}_{n})
.

Weak Stabilization and central limit theorem.

Our first main result is to show a weak stabilization result for sum of death times, birth times and lifetimes of persistent diagrams of weighted Poisson-Delaunay complexes.

be sequence of boxes (i.e.,
A_{n}=W_{m}+x

) and convergence of sets.

.

Let

and

be defined as above and

. All three statistics -
\mathbf{M}^{\phi}_{k}(\mathcal{P}_{n}),\mathbf{B}^{\phi}_{k}(\mathcal{P}_{n})
, and

- satisfy weak stabilization as in (

(4)

D_{\textbf{0}}\mathbf{M}^{\phi}_{k}(\mathcal{P}_{n}):=\mathbf{M}^{\phi}_{k}(\mathcal{P}_{n}\cup\{0\})-\mathbf{M}^{\phi}_{k}(\mathcal{P}_{n})\to D_{\infty}(\mathbf{M}^{\phi}_{k}),\,\,\mbox{a.s.},\quad

Kesten and Lee 1996
introduced the the notion of weak stabilization to prove a central limit theorem for Euclidean minimal spanning trees and this was abstracted to the above form of weak stabilization for general Poisson functionals in

Theorem 1.3
.

Let

be a strictly increasing function such that
\phi(t)\leq Ct^{p},t\geq 0
for some

. Then, there exist constants

.

For
k=1
, since
\mathbf{M}^{\phi}_{1}
is the
\phi
-weighted sum of edges on a minimal spanning tree, weak stabilization and central limit theorem follow from the results of
Kesten and Lee 1996
(see also
Lee 1997
,
Lee 1999
and
Penrose and Yukich 2003
), but these proofs use percolation properties of the the Poisson-Boolean model
\cup_{X\in\mathcal{P}}B_{r}(X),r\geq 0
. With the study of homological percolation still in a nascent stage (see
Skraba and Bobrowski 2020
), proof of weak stabilization in higher-dimensions necessitates a different approach. Our proof relies mainly upon local-finiteness of the weighted Delaunay graph, existence of certain homomorphisms between chain complexes and also implicitly that minimal spanning acycles can be constructed via greedy algorithms. We discuss the proof in more detail at the end of this section. Also to follow our proof ideas better, we give a proof overview in the case of minimal spanning tree in Section
3.1
where we consider
\mathcal{D}\cap\mathcal{P}_{n}
instead of
\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{P}_{n})
. The difference between
\mathcal{D}\cap\mathcal{P}_{n}
and
\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{P}_{n})
lies in the boundary effects. While this doesn’t significantly affect the percolation theoretic approach, our approach necessitates the use of suitable chain homomorphisms to overcome the boundary effects. In the case of
\mathcal{D}\cap\mathcal{P}_{n}
, one can use combinatorial arguments to deduce stabilization of the MSA, but for
\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{P}_{n})
, simplices may disappear with increasing window size. So rather than rely on monotoncity, we resort to the existence of certain chain maps (Section
3.4
), which requires more algebraic arguments (see Section
3.3
).

The main advantage of our approach is that it requires no change when going from minimal spanning trees to minimal spanning acycles. We use the randomness of the point process in controlling the stabilization of
\mathcal{D}
. This can be extended to fairly general point processes, see
Błaszczyszyn et al. 2019
and the discussion therein. However, the central limit theorem which relies heavily on Theorem
2.3
, does require the Poisson point process assumption.

We now comment on the use of weak stabilization in random topology and connections to our result. Weak stabilization has been a crucial tool to prove central limit theorems for topological statistics in recent years. This is mainly because other stronger notions of stabilization are insufficient to handle long-range dependence of topological statistics especially in the presence of percolation in the Poisson-Boolean model. In
Yogeshwaran et al. 2017
, weak stabilization was shown for Betti numbers and later this was extended to persistent Betti numbers in
Hiraoka et al. 2018
,
Krebs and Polonik 2019
. The statistics of minimal spanning acycle have more global dependence than Betti numbers and persistent Betti numbers and hence the methods do not naturally extend to minimal spanning acycles. The relation between Betti numbers and total lifetime sum (
\mathbf{L}_{k}
) can be exploited to prove weak stabilization of
\mathbf{L}_{k}
as in the case of random cubical complexes in
Hiraoka and Tsunoda 2018
. But this does not extend to the weight of a minimal spanning acycle (equivalently death times in the persistence diagram). In all these works, weak stabilization was shown en-route to the proof of central limit theorem for these statistics. Proposition
1.2
and Theorem
1.3
significantly extend these results by proving stabilization not only for lifetime sums but also more generally for weight of a minimal spanning acycle and its complement. Recently strong stabilization has been shown for persistent Betti numbers in
[
26
]
but it is unclear whether this can be extended to minimal spanning acycles.

We now mention some possible extensions and related results in non-geometric or mean-field models. Quantifying the convergence in (
4
) via percolation-theoretic estimates for the Poisson-Boolean model has been used to prove normal approximation results for Euclidean minimal spanning trees in
Chatterjee and Sen 2017
,
Lachièze-Rey et al. 2020
. Though one would expect this to hold for minimal spanning acycles, a proof is far from obvious at this stage. The results of
[
26
]
may be considered a first step towards such a quantification and in also extending central limit theorems in
[
43
,
21
]
to inhomogeneous Poisson processes and Binomial point processes.

In a different direction, central limit theorem for minimal spanning trees on randomly weighted complete graphs was proven in
Janson 1995
. Strong laws for lifetime sums have been shown for simplicial complex versions of randomly weighted complete graphs in
Hino and Kanazawa 2019
using the relation between Betti numbers and lifetime sums. A central limit theorem still evades us in these models and again the stabilization-based approach of
Cao 2021
offers promise.

Discussion of proofs:

We end the introduction with a detailed sketch of our proofs. As is obvious from the above discussions, the key result is Proposition
1.2
and then applying Theorem
2.3
, we obtain the central limit theorem - Theorem
1.3
. The crucial step towards the proof of Proposition
1.2
is in understanding stabilization when
A_{n}=W_{n}
i.e., a sequence of centred boxes. Proposition
3.14
proves stabilization in this case and we shall outline here this proof alone.

One of the technical difficulties in proving stabilization along windows
W_{n}
(i.e., Proposition
3.14
) lies in overcoming the boundary effects in the Delaunay complex. More precisely, it is easier to show weak stabilization for
\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{P})\cap W_{n}
but what is required is weak stabilization of
\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{P}_{n})
. The former sequence of complexes satisfy monotonicity in
n
but the latter do not. We overcome this via by passing to the nerve of covers induced by the Voronoi diagrams and using chain maps between
\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{P}_{n})
and
\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{P}_{m})
for
n<m
.

The starting point of our proof strategy is to decompose addition of the origin into a.s. a finite number of changes in the complex locally around the origin. By showing that the effect of these elementary changes stabilizes for Delaunay complexes, we show that the effect of adding the origin stabilizes. More precisely, denoting by
\mathcal{D}
the Delaunay complex on
\mathcal{P}
and
\mathring{\mathcal{D}}
the Delaunay complex on
\mathcal{P}\cup\{\textbf{0}\}
, we show the following statements (either a.s. or pointwise):

(1)

The removal and addition of simplices to go from
\mathcal{D}
to
\mathring{\mathcal{D}}
happens in
W_{N}
for some
N
(random but a.s. finite); Proposition
3.12
.

(2)

Thus for
m>N
,
\mathbf{M}^{\phi}_{k}(\mathcal{D}(P_{m}\cup\{\textbf{0}\}))-\mathbf{M}^{\phi}_{k}(\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{P}_{m}))
can be expressed as sum of finite differences of the form
\mathbf{M}^{\phi}_{k}(\mathcal{D}^{\prime}_{m})-\mathbf{M}^{\phi}_{k}(\mathcal{D}_{m})
such that
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}_{m},\mathcal{D}_{m}
are obtained from
\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{P}_{m})
or
\mathcal{D}(P_{m}\cup\{\textbf{0}\})
by the removal of finitely many simplices and furthermore
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}_{m}
and
\mathcal{D}_{m}
differ by at most one simplex.

(3)

Though there is no inclusion between
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}_{m},\mathcal{D}^{\prime}_{n}
for
n>m
(resp.
\mathcal{D}_{m},\mathcal{D}_{n}
), we show existence of appropriate chain maps between
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}_{m}
and
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}_{n}

(resp. between
\mathcal{D}_{m}
and
\mathcal{D}_{n}
); see Section
3.4
.

(4)

Then, using a series of lemmas (Section
3.3
) that analyse stabilization under chain maps when one simplex is added or removed, we show in Proposition
3.14
that
\mathbf{M}^{\phi}(\mathcal{D}^{\prime}_{m})-\mathbf{M}^{\phi}(\mathcal{D}_{m})
converges as
m\to\infty
.

(5)

To show that the limiting variance is non-degenerate, we show that limit of
\mathcal{D}(P_{m}\cup\{\textbf{0}\})-\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{P}_{m})
is a non-zero random variable by constructing some specific configurations for which the limiting random variable is strictly greater than zero with positive probability; see Section
3.7
.

For ease of understanding the main points of the above proof strategy and for the benefit of the readers interested in the case of minimal spanning trees, we give a proof overview for the special case of
k=1
and where
\phi
is identity in Section
3.1
.

Also, as mentioned below Definition
1.1
, our proof also works for Delaunay-Čech complex. All the above steps follow straightforwardly for the Delaunay-Čech case except that of variance lower bound proof. The latter uses specific constructions which must be suitably modified (see Remark

).

2.1.
Weighted complexes

We introduce weighted (simplicial) complexes and the associated filtration. A
(simplicial) complex

\mathcal{K}
on a vertex set
V
is a collection of subsets of
\mathcal{K}
such that if
\sigma\in\mathcal{K}
and
\tau\subset\sigma
then
\tau\in\mathcal{K}.
We shall assume that
V\subset\mathcal{K}
. Elements of
\mathcal{K}
are called
faces or simplices
and faces of cardinality
(k+1)
are called as
k
-dimensional faces or simplices. We represent a
k
-dimensional face
\tau
by
[v_{0},\ldots,v_{k}]
where
v_{i}\in V
,
i=0,\ldots,k
. A
weighted complex
is a complex equipped with a weight function
w:\mathcal{K}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
. We shall assume that all our weight functions on a simplicial complex are
monotone
i.e., for all
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
, the preimage
w^{-1}(-\infty,\alpha]
is a simplicial complex. The sub-complex of
\mathcal{K}
consisting of only
k
-faces and lower is called
the
k
-skeleton

2.3.
Relation to persistent homology

We shall now relate minimal spanning acycles to persistent homology. This is important in understanding the implications of our results to persistent homology
and also this shall yield us the important notion of negative and positive faces that will characterize minimal spanning acycles.

Persistent homology
is an algebraic invariant which keep track of changes of homology in

for a weighted complex
\mathcal{K}
.
[

9

10

\mathrm{H}^{s,t}_{k}(\mathcal{K})=\frac{Z_{k}(\mathcal{K}(s))}{Z_{k}(\mathcal{K}(t))\cap B_{k}(\mathcal{K}(t))},\quad s\leq t.

The ranks of

called as

[

]
or alternatively as a multi-set of points in called

[

. For our purposes, it suffices to understand that

(

2.4.
Weak stabilization and central limit theorem for Poisson functionals

We now introduce Poisson processes formally and state the central limit theorem for statistics of Poisson processes. We refer the reader to
[
28
,
3
]
for more details on point processes and in particular, Poisson point processes.

Let
\mathcal{N}
be the set of all locally-finite point sets (or equivalently simple Radon counting measures) in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. By a locally-finite point set
{\mathcal{X}}
, we mean a point set
{\mathcal{X}}
such that
{\mathcal{X}}(B):=|{\mathcal{X}}\cap B|<\infty
for all bounded Borel subsets
B
. We shall also view
{\mathcal{X}}
as a counting measure
\sum_{x\in{\mathcal{X}}}\delta_{x}
and
{\mathcal{X}}
is said to be a simple counting measure if
{\mathcal{X}}(\{x\})\in\{0,1\}
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. The associated
\sigma
-algebra (called the
evaluation
\sigma
-algebra
) is the smallest
\sigma
-algebra such that
{\mathcal{X}}\mapsto{\mathcal{X}}(B)
is measurable for all Borel subsets
B\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Here, we have used
|\cdot|
to denote cardinality and later we shall also use it to denote Lebesgue measure.

By
a point process
, we mean a random element of the space
\mathcal{N}
i.e., a measurable mapping from a probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
to
\mathcal{N}
equipped with the evaluation
\sigma
-algebra. A point process
\mathcal{P}
is said to be a
stationary Poisson point process on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with intensity
\lambda
if it satisfies the following two properties:

(1)

For every Borel subset
B\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
\mathcal{P}(B)
is a Poisson random variable with mean
\lambda|B|
.

(2)

For every
m\in\mathbb{N}
and pairwise disjoint Borel subsets
B_{1},\ldots,B_{m}
of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the random variables
\mathcal{P}(B_{1}),\ldots,\mathcal{P}(B_{m})
are independent (
complete independence
).

Given a bounded Borel subset
B
of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the restriction of Poisson point process to
B
can be realized as follows:

\mathcal{P}\cap B\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\{X_{1},\ldots,X_{N_{B}}\},

where
N_{B}
is a Poisson random variable with mean
\lambda|B|
and
X_{1},X_{2},\ldots
are i.i.d. random vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with uniform distribution in
B
and independent of
N_{B}
. We note that
\mathcal{P}
is a locally-finite point set with points in general position.

We recall a general central limit theorem for functionals of Poisson point processes due to
[
42
]
.
Recall that
W_{n}=\left[-\frac{n^{1/d}}{2},\frac{n^{1/d}}{2}\right]^{d}
, let
\mathfrak{A}
(
=\mathfrak{A}(\{W_{n}\})
) be the collection of all subsets

of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that
A=W_{n}+x
for some
W_{n}
in the sequence and some point
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. For any
\mathfrak{A}
-valued sequence
A_{n},n\geq 1
, we say that
A_{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}

\bigcup_{n\geq 1}\bigcap_{m\geq n}A_{m}\ =\ \mathbb{R}^{d}
.

Theorem 2.3
.

(
[
42
, Theorem 3.1]
)

Let
H
be a real-valued functional defined for all finite subsets of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and satisfying the following four conditions:

(i)

Translation invariance:

H({\mathcal{X}}+y)=H({\mathcal{X}})
for all finite subsets
{\mathcal{X}}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

(ii)

Weak stabilization:

H
is said to be weakly stabilizing if there exists a random variable
D_{\infty}(H)
such that for any
\mathfrak{A}
-valued sequence
A_{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the following holds.

(5)

\displaystyle(D_{\textbf{0}}H)(\mathcal{P}\cap A_{n})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle a.s.}}{{\rightarrow}}D_{\infty}(H),\,\mbox{as $n\to\infty$.}

(iii)

Poisson bounded moments:

\sup_{A\in\mathfrak{A};\,0\in A}\mathbb{E}\!\left\{\left[(D_{\textbf{0}}H)(\mathcal{P}\cap A)\right]^{4}\right\}<\infty
.

Then, there exists a constant
\sigma^{2}\geq 0
, such that, as

n^{-1}\mathsf{VAR}\!\left(H(\mathcal{P}\cap W_{n})\right)\to\sigma^{2},\qquad n^{-1/2}\left[H(\mathcal{P}\cap W_{n})-\mathbb{E}\!\left\{H(\mathcal{P}\cap W_{n})\right\}\right]\ {\Rightarrow}\ N(0,\sigma^{2}),

where
\Rightarrow
denotes convergence in distribution and

stands for normal random variable with mean

and variance
\sigma^{2}
.

If
D_{\infty}(H)
is a non-zero random variable (i.e.,
\mathbb{P}(D_{\infty}(H)\neq 0)>0

.

The last statement on variance lower bound follows from
[
7

[

3.1.
Proof sketch in the case of

To better illustrate the proof strategy sketched at the end of Section
1

and

is identity. In this case
\mathbf{M}(\mathcal{P}_{n})
is simply the total weight of the minimal spanning tree formed on
\mathcal{P}_{n}
. Though accounting for boundary effects is an important part of our proof, we shall explain the proof steps for minimal spanning tree ignoring the boundary effects. It makes it easier to understand the crux of our proof.

Let
G:=G({\mathcal{X}})
be the Delaunay graph on a locally-finite point set
{\mathcal{X}}
and recall that the edge-weights
w
is given by the distance between the points. Consider also the Delaunay graph
G^{\prime}:=G({\mathcal{X}}\cup\{\textbf{0}\})
. Let
W_{n}=[-\frac{n^{1/d}}{2},\frac{n^{1/d}}{2}]^{d},n\geq 0
be the sequence of windows increasing to
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
G_{n},G^{\prime}_{n}
be the restriction of graphs
G,G^{\prime}
to
W_{n}
. Further, let
\MST_{n}:=\MST(G_{n})
,
\MST^{\prime}_{n}:=\MST(G^{\prime}_{n})
be the corresponding MSTs and
w(\MST_{n}),w(\MST^{\prime}_{n})
be the total weight of the respective MSTs. Recall that for an edge
e
, we denote the subgraph of
G_{n}
consisting of edges whose weight is smaller than that of
e
by
G_{n}(e^{-})
.

(1)

Suppose that
G_{n}^{\prime}=G_{n}-\{e_{1},\ldots,e_{k}\}+\{e^{\prime}_{1},\ldots,e^{\prime}_{l}\}
for all
n\geq n_{0}
and assume that the edges are all in
W_{n_{0}}
.

(2)

The add-one cost for MST upon adding a point to the point process can be decomposed as a finite sum of add-one cost (or its negative) of MST upon adding an edge i.e.,

\displaystyle w(\MST^{\prime}_{n})-w(\MST_{n})=\sum_{i=1}^{k}w\big(\MST(G_{n}-\{e_{1},\ldots,e_{i}\})\big)-w\big(\MST(G_{n}-\{e_{1},\ldots,e_{i-1}\})\big)

\displaystyle+\sum_{i=1}^{l}w\big(\MST(G_{n}-\{e_{1},\ldots,e_{k}\}+\{e^{\prime}_{1},\ldots,e^{\prime}_{i}\})\big)-w\big(\MST(G_{n}-\{e_{1},\ldots,e_{k}\}+\{e^{\prime}_{1},\ldots,e^{\prime}_{i-1}\})\big).

(3)

Thus, we will show convergence of add-one cost for
\MST_{n}
’s of a suitable sequence of graphs under addition of a single edge and because of the above identity, this suffices to show convergence of
w(\MST^{\prime}_{n})-w(\MST_{n})
. Hence we may assume that
G^{\prime}_{n}=G_{n}\cup\{e_{0}\}
for
n
large enough, say
n\geq n_{0}
.

(4)

When an edge
e_{0}
is added to the graph, the MST can increase at most by one and the symmetric difference of
\MST_{n},\MST^{\prime}_{n}
is at most two for all
n
.

(5)

Suppose
e_{0}
is positive for
G_{n}
for some
n\geq n_{0}
, i.e., it creates a cycle in the graph for some
G_{n}(e_{0}^{-})
, then it must also create a cycle for any larger graph
G_{m}(e_{0}^{-})
for
m>n
. In this case, the edge does not contribute to the difference of
\MST_{m}
,
\MST_{m}^{\prime}
for
m>n
and so
\MST_{m}=\MST_{m}^{\prime}
for
m>n
.

(6)

If
e_{0}
is negative for all
n\geq n_{0}
i.e., it reduces the number of components when added for all
G_{n}(e_{0}^{-})
, then the following three case can occur

(a)

If
\MST_{m}^{\prime}=\MST_{m}+e_{0}
for all
m\geq n_{0}
(i.e.,
e_{0}
is not part of any cycle in
\MST_{m}
for the entire sequence) then again stabilization follows easily.

(b)

If for some graph
G_{n}
,
\MST_{n}\setminus\MST^{\prime}_{n}=e_{n}
(for some edge
e_{n}\in G_{n}
) then for any larger graph
G_{m}
,
\MST_{m}\setminus\MST^{\prime}_{m}=e_{m}
where
w(e_{m})\leq w(e_{n})
. In other words, adding
e_{0}
creates a cycle in
\MST_{n}
such that there is a larger edge
e_{n}\in\MST_{n}
and
\MST^{\prime}_{n}=\MST_{n}-e_{n}+e_{0}.
Then, the same holds for
\MST_{m}
as well with an edge
e_{m}
(
=e_{n}
possibly) removed from
\MST_{m}
. Further, the edge
w(e_{m})
has to be at most
w(e_{n})
else
e_{n}
itself would have been removed from
\MST_{m}
as well.

(c)

Thus
w(\MST(G_{n}+e_{0}))-w(\MST(G_{n}))=w(e_{0})-w(e_{n})
for all
n\geq n_{0}
with
w(e_{n})
being a decreasing sequence and hence the add-one cost converges.

Formally, we must consider minimal spanning trees on
G({\mathcal{X}}_{n})
and
G({\mathcal{X}}_{n}\cup\{\textbf{0}\})
. The difference between

and
G_{n}
is that the latter has monotonicity in

while the former does not. As discussed at the end of Section

, this is true for Delaunay complexes and we overcome this issue by using suitable chain maps between

and

for

3.3.

\mathcal{K}

\mathcal{K}^{\prime}

f:\bigoplus_{k}C_{k}(\mathcal{K})\rightarrow\bigoplus_{k}C_{k}(\mathcal{K}^{\prime})

We observe that even if

We remind the reader that: (1)

\mathrm{dim}(\sigma)=k

3.4.
Existence of chain maps between Delaunay complexes

In order to use the results of the previous section, we need existence of chain maps between Delaunay complexes built on two finite point sets
{\mathcal{X}}\subseteq{\mathcal{Y}}
such that they satisfy the assumptions in Assumption
3.3
. We assume
{\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}}
are
generic
in the sense that there are no co-linear or cocircular points. We first recall a few standard definitions and facts which are used in this section.

Definition 3.6
.

Given an open cover
\mathcal{U}:=\{\mathcal{U}_{i}\}_{i\in\mathcal{I}}
of a closed subset
U\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the nerve
\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{U})
is the set of finite subsets of
\mathcal{I}
defined as follows. A finite set
I\subseteq\mathcal{I}
belongs to
\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{U})
if and only if the intersection of the
\mathcal{U}_{i}
whose indices are in
I
, is non-empty, i.e.

\mathcal{U}_{I}=\bigcap\limits_{i\in I}\mathcal{U}_{i}\neq\emptyset

If
I
belongs to
\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{U})
, then so do all of its subsets making
\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{U})
an abstract simplicial complex.
If a cover satisfies the condition that all finite non-empty intersections are contractible, then it is said to be a
good cover
.

If an open cover is good, the corresponding nerve captures the same topological information combinatorially. This is formalized in the Nerve Theorem; see
[
5
, Theorem 10.7]
for example.

Theorem 3.7
.

If
\mathcal{U}
is a good open cover of a closed subset
U\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, then
U
is homotopy equivalent to the nerve
\mathcal{N}(U)
.

We show that the sequence of simplex deletions and additions which occur when adding a point to
{\mathcal{X}}
can be realized as a sequence of topological spaces with the Voronoi cells inducing a good cover of each space. This allows us to pass back and forth from the combinatorial description of the Delaunay complex to the geometric realization of the union of balls around point in
{\mathcal{X}}
via the Nerve Theorem. This allows us to relate the Delaunay complexes on point sets
{\mathcal{X}}\subset{\mathcal{Y}}
, by defining a chain map which meets the requirements of Assumption
3.3
. We remark more on the necessity for considering such a sequence of complexes at the end of this subsection.

First, we use the fact the Delaunay complex is the nerve of the cover induced by the Voronoi diagram of a generic point set
\mathcal{X}
. Define the
\varepsilon
-offset of the Voronoi cell of a point
x\in\mathcal{X}
as

\mathrm{Vor}^{\varepsilon}_{\mathcal{X}}(x)=\{p\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\exists y\in\mathrm{Vor}_{\mathcal{X}}(x)\,\mbox{such that}\,|p-y|<\varepsilon\}

We now give a straightforward result whose proof will be sketched for completeness.

Lemma 3.8
.

For
\varepsilon>0
small enough, the cover induced by

\mathrm{Vor}^{\varepsilon}_{\mathcal{X}}(x)\cap B_{r}(x)\qquad\forall x\in{\mathcal{X}}

form a good cover of the
\bigcup_{x\in{\mathcal{X}}}B_{r}(x)
for all
r
. We call this cover the Voronoi-induced cover of
{\mathcal{X}}
.

Proof.

As
{\mathcal{X}}
is finite, we can choose
\varepsilon>0
small enough such that the intersections of the
\varepsilon
-offsets of the Voronoi cells are in one-to-one correspondence with the adjacencies of the Voronoi cells.
The Voronoi cells (and their
\varepsilon
-offsets) are convex, hence they form a good cover of the full space. Additionally, the balls centered on the points are also convex, hence all intersections between balls and cells are either empty or convex, and hence contractible, implying the result.
∎

Note that the choice of
\varepsilon
depends on the point set and can be arbitrarily small. We simply require that for any finite generic point set (without multiplicity), an
\varepsilon>0
exists.
Assume that
{\mathcal{X}}\subset{\mathcal{Y}}
are generic finite point sets as above. First, we define

{\mathcal{X}}^{\prime}={\mathcal{X}}\cup\{\textbf{0}\},\qquad\qquad{\mathcal{Y}}^{\prime}={\mathcal{Y}}\cup\{\textbf{0}\},

and let
\mathcal{K}=\mathcal{D}({\mathcal{X}})
,
\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{D}({\mathcal{Y}})
the Delaunay complex of
{\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}}
respectively. Similarly let
\mathcal{K}^{\prime}
and
\mathcal{L}^{\prime}
be the Delaunay complexes on
{\mathcal{X}}^{\prime}
and
{\mathcal{Y}}^{\prime}
respectively. Observe that
\mathcal{K},\mathcal{K}^{\prime},\mathcal{L},\mathcal{L}^{\prime}
are complexes that correspond to Voronoi-induced covers on
{\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{X}}^{\prime},{\mathcal{Y}},{\mathcal{Y}}^{\prime}
respectively.

We assume that

(8)

\mathcal{K}\triangle\mathcal{K}^{\prime}=\mathcal{L}\triangle\mathcal{L}^{\prime}=\{\sigma_{0},\sigma_{1},\ldots,\sigma_{k}\}.

We can choose
\varepsilon
small enough, but dependent on the point sets
{\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}}
, so that the nerve of this cover is combinatorially equivalent to the Delaunay complex. This follows from the finiteness of the point sets.

There exists an ordering of the the simplices in (

) such that

\mathcal{K}^{i}=\begin{cases}\mathcal{K}^{i-1}-\sigma_{i}&0<i\leq l_{1}\\
\mathcal{K}^{i-1}\cup\sigma_{i}&l_{1}<i\leq l\end{cases}\quad;\quad\mathcal{L}^{i}=\begin{cases}\mathcal{L}^{i-1}-\sigma_{i}&0<i\leq l_{1}\\
\mathcal{L}^{i-1}\cup\sigma_{i}&l_{1}<i\leq l\end{cases}

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{K}^{0}\supseteq\mathcal{K}^{1}\supseteq\ldots\supseteq\mathcal{K}^{l_{1}}=\mathcal{K}\cap\mathcal{K}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathcal{K}^{l_{1}+1}\subseteq\ldots\subseteq\mathcal{K}^{l}=\mathcal{K}^{\prime},

(9)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{L}^{0}\supseteq\mathcal{L}^{1}\supseteq\ldots\supseteq\mathcal{L}^{l_{1}}=\mathcal{L}\cap\mathcal{L}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathcal{L}^{l_{1}+1}\subseteq\ldots\subseteq\mathcal{L}^{l}=\mathcal{L}^{\prime}.

In proving the main result of this section, we recount the following result

Theorem 3.9
.

(

4
, Theorem 5.10]

Let

Proof.

Define

\displaystyle X_{r}

\displaystyle=\bigcup\limits_{p\in{\mathcal{X}}}B_{r}(p)\cap\mathrm{Vor}_{\mathcal{X}}(p)

\displaystyle Y_{r}

\displaystyle=\bigcup\limits_{q\in{\mathcal{Y}}}B_{r}(p)\cap\mathrm{Vor}_{\mathcal{Y}}(q)

\displaystyle X^{i}(r)

Remark 3.11
.

At first glance, the intermediate step of the spaces may seem unnecessary as one could pass to the Čech complex rather than the topological spaces. However, in this case inserting and removing simplices is complicated by the fact that we need to insert and remove the corresponding cofaces in the Čech complex, which no longer corresponds to the cover by balls of a given radius complicating the analysis. By constructing the sequence of spaces, we are able to define the appropriate maps for all intermediate steps which allows us to use the results of the previous section.

\displaystyle:=\min\{\min\{|X|:X\in\mathcal{P}\cap\Lambda^{+}_{i}\cap B_{d_{i}(A)}(\textbf{0})\},d_{i}(A)\},1\leq i\leq m,

and so

. Thus, arguing as in the proof of Proposition

Now, we shall derive tail bounds for

and using that, derive suitable bounds for

. Suppose that for some

,

for some

. Then there is a

such that

and also

for some

. Here

but not on

, it holds that

and the required bound as well.

To bound
