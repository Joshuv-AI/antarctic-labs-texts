Quasi-Cyclic Stern Proof of Knowledge
Loïc Bidoux, Philippe Gaborit, Mukul Kulkarni, Nicolas Sendrier

Abstract
The ongoing NIST standardization process has shown that Proof of Knowledge (PoK) based signatures have become an important type of possible post-quantum signatures. Regarding code-based cryptography, the main original approach for PoK based signatures is the Stern protocol which allows to prove the knowledge of a small weight vector solving a given instance of the Syndrome Decoding ( 𝖲𝖣 \mathsf{SD} ) problem over 𝔽 2 \mathbb{F}_{2} . It features a soundness error equal to 2 / 3 2/3 . This protocol was improved a few years later by Véron who proposed a variation of the scheme based on the General Syndrome Decoding ( 𝖦𝖲𝖣 \mathsf{GSD} ) problem which leads to better results in term of communication. A few years later, the AGS protocol introduced a variation of the Véron protocol based on Quasi-Cyclic (QC) matrices. The AGS protocol permits to obtain an asymptotic soundness error of 1 / 2 1/2 and an improvement in term of communications. In the present paper, we introduce the Quasi-Cyclic Stern PoK which constitutes an adaptation of the AGS scheme in a 𝖲𝖣 \mathsf{SD} context, as well as several new optimizations for code-based PoK. Our main optimization on the size of the signature can’t be applied to 𝖦𝖲𝖣 \mathsf{GSD} based protocols such as AGS and therefore motivated the design of our new protocol. In addition, we also provide a special soundness proof that is compatible with the use of the Fiat-Shamir transform for 5-round protocols. This approach is valid for our protocol but also for the AGS protocol which was lacking such a proof. We compare our results with existing signatures including the recent code-based signatures based on PoK leveraging the MPC in the head paradigm. In practice, our new protocol is as fast as AGS while reducing its associated signature length by 20 % 20\% . As a consequence, it constitutes an interesting trade-off between signature length and execution time for the design of a code-based signature relying only on the difficulty of the 𝖲𝖣 \mathsf{SD} problem.

Two approaches have been used in the literature namely signatures from the hash-and-sign paradigm and signatures based on identification.
In the first category, a construction was proposed in 2001

The recent Wave construction

In the second category, two constructions have been proposed in the past few years namely LESS

The first PoK for the

In 1997, Véron showed that using the general decoding problem (

The

Both protocols require 3 rounds to be executed and feature a soundness error equal to 2/3.
In 2011, the CVE
[
20

and AGS
[
1
]
PoK respectively improved the Stern and Véron protocols by lowering their soundness error to 1/2 (asymptotically close to 1/2 for AGS) using 5 rounds of execution.
The CVE protocol is based on the
\mathsf{SD}
problem over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
while the AGS protocol relies on the
\mathsf{QCGSD}
problem namely the
\mathsf{GSD}

An issue with respect to the zero-knowledge property of

[
15

Some of these protocols have also been adapted in the rank metric setting, see
[
22

31
,
13

Recently, several proposals have used the MPC in the head paradigm in order to achieve a negligible soundness error of
1/N
for some parameter
N

The GPS
[
32
]
construction achieves such a small soundness error by relying on the SD problem over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
while the FJR
[
29
]
and BGKM
[
17
]
proposals rely on the SD problem over
\mathbb{F}_{2}

However, these constructions induce a performance overhead with respect to previous approaches.

Thanks to these new results, the research problem associated to these protocols has shifted from minimizing the signature size to finding the best trade-off between expected performances and signature size.
Amongst existing constructions, AGS features the smallest expected performance cost while FJR is the best approach to get small signature sizes.
In this paper, we propose a new PoK that has the same cost as AGS while featuring a signature size that is
20\%
smaller.
As such, our new protocol provides a new interesting trade-off for the design of signatures based on PoK for the
\mathsf{SD}

2.1
Coding Theory and Cryptography

We start by defining linear codes and quasi-cyclic codes.
Next, we describe the syndrome decoding (
\mathsf{SD}
) and quasi-cyclic syndrome decoding (
\mathsf{QCSD}
) problems which are two difficult problems commonly used in code-based cryptography.
The
\mathsf{SD}
problem has been proven NP-complete in
[
14
]
.

Definition 1
(Binary Linear Code)

Let
n
and
k
be positive integers such that
k<n
. A binary linear
\mathcal{C}
code (denoted
[n,k]
) is a
k
-dimensional subspace of
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
.

\mathcal{C}
can be represented in two equivalent ways: by a generator matrix
\mathbf{G}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{k\times n}
such that
\mathcal{C}=\{\mathbf{mG}~|~\mathbf{m}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{k}\}
or by a parity-check matrix
\mathbf{H}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{(n-k)\times n}
such that
\mathcal{C}=\{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}~|~\mathbf{H}\mathbf{x}^{\top}=0\}
.

Definition 2
(Systematic Quasi-Cyclic Code)

A systematic quasi-cyclic code of index
\ell
and rate
1/\ell
is a

[n=\ell k,k]
code with an
(\ell-1)k\times\ell k=(n-k)\times n
parity check matrix of the form:

\mathbf{H}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{I}_{k}&0&\cdots&0&\mathbf{A}_{0}\\
0&\mathbf{I}_{k}&&&\mathbf{A}_{1}\\
&&\ddots&&\vdots\\
0&&\cdots&\mathbf{I}_{k}&\mathbf{A}_{\ell-2}\end{bmatrix}

where
\mathbf{A}_{0},\ldots,\mathbf{A}_{\ell-2}
are circulant
k\times k
matrices.

Definition 3
(
\mathsf{SD}
problem)

Given positive integers
n
,
k
,
w
, a random parity-check matrix
\mathbf{H}\overset{\;\$}{\longleftarrow}\mathbb{F}_{2}^{(n-k)\times n}
, and a syndrome
\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n-k}
the syndrome decoding problem
\mathsf{SD}(n,k,w)
asks to find
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
, such that
\mathbf{Hx}^{\top}=\mathbf{y}^{\top}
and
w_{H}\big(\mathbf{x}\big)=w
.

Definition 4
(
\mathsf{QCSD}
problem)

Given positive integers
n,k,w
, with
n=\ell k
for some
\ell
, a random parity-check matrix of a quasi-cyclic code
\mathbf{H}\overset{\;\$}{\longleftarrow}\mathcal{QC}(\mathbb{F}_{2}^{(n-k)\times n})
, and a syndrome
\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{(n-k)}
, the syndrome decoding problem
\mathsf{QCSD}(n,k,w)
asks to find
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
, such that
\mathbf{Hx}^{\top}=\mathbf{y}^{\top}
and
w_{H}\big(\mathbf{x}\big)=w
.

The quasi-cyclic codes we will consider hereafter will
always have index
\ell=2
.

2.2
Signatures from Zero-Knowledge Proof of Knowledge

We start by introducing commitment schemes as they are a building block used to construct proofs of knowledge.
We require such schemes to be computationally hiding and computationally biding.
The former property ensures that efficient adversaries can’t distinguish between two commitments generated from different messages.
The latter property ensures that efficient adversaries can’t change their committed messages after the commit step.

Definition 5
(Commitment Scheme)

A commitment scheme is a tuple of algorithms
\mathsf{COM}=(\mathsf{Commit},\mathsf{Open})
such that
\mathsf{Commit}(m)
returns a commitment
c
for the message
m
and
\mathsf{Open}(c,m)
returns either
1
(
\mathsf{accept}
) or
0
(
\mathsf{reject}
). Note that, in general commitment schemes
also take some randomness
r
as input to the
\mathsf{Commit}
and

\mathsf{Open}
algorithms, we assume that this is implicit in the
subsequent definitions and discussion.
A commitment scheme is said to be correct if:

\mathsf{Pr}\left[b=1\ \middle|\begin{array}[]{l}c\leftarrow\mathsf{Commit}(m),~b\leftarrow\mathsf{Open}(c,m)\end{array}\right]=1.

Definition 6
(Computationally Hiding)

Let
(m_{0},m_{1})
be a pair of messages, the advantage of an adversary
\mathcal{A}
against the commitment hiding experiment be defined as:

\mathsf{Adv}^{\mathsf{hiding}}_{\mathcal{A}}(1^{\lambda})=\Bigg|\,\mathsf{Pr}\left[\begin{array}[]{l}b=b^{\prime}\\
\end{array}\ \middle|~\\
\begin{array}[]{l}b\overset{\;\$}{\longleftarrow}\{0,1\},~c\longleftarrow\mathsf{Commit}(m_{b})\\
b^{\prime}\longleftarrow\mathcal{A}.\mathsf{guess}(c)\end{array}\right]-\frac{1}{2}\,\Bigg|.

A commitment scheme
\mathsf{COM}
is computationally hiding if for all probabilistic polynomial time adversaries
\mathcal{A}
and every pair of messages
(m_{0},m_{1})
,
\mathsf{Adv}^{\mathsf{hiding}}_{\mathcal{A}}(1^{\lambda})
is negligible in
\lambda
.

Definition 7
(Computationally Binding)

Let the advantage of an adversary
\mathcal{A}
against the commitment binding experiment be defined as:

\mathsf{Adv}^{\mathsf{binding}}_{\mathcal{A}}(1^{\lambda})=\mathsf{Pr}\left[\begin{array}[]{l}m_{0}\neq m_{1}\\
1\longleftarrow\mathsf{Open}(c,m_{0})\\
1\longleftarrow\mathsf{Open}(c,m_{1})\\
\end{array}\ \middle|~\\
\begin{array}[]{l}(c,m_{0},m_{1})\longleftarrow\mathcal{A}.\mathsf{choose}(1^{\lambda})\end{array}\right].

A commitment scheme
\mathsf{COM}
is computationally binding if for all probabilistic polynomial time adversaries
\mathcal{A}
,
\mathsf{Adv}^{\mathsf{binding}}_{\mathcal{A}}(1^{\lambda})
is negligible in
\lambda
.

We now describe proofs of knowledge along with their soundness and zero-knowledge properties.
Informally, a zero-knowledge proof of knowledge allows a prover
\mathsf{P}
to prove the knowledge of a secret to a verifier
\mathsf{V}
without revealing anything about it.
An interactive proof system is a protocol between two parties
(
\mathsf{P}
and
\mathsf{V}
) used to establish the validity of some statement

x
by proving the existence of a witness
w
such that
R(x,w)=1

for some public relation
R
(
i.e.

x\in L
for some language

L\in\mathsf{NP}
). A PoK system
additionally proves that
\mathsf{P}
actually
knows
a valid
witness
w
(as opposed to its existence earlier). A PoK system is
(1)
complete
if proof corresponding to a valid statement (
x\in L)

is always accepted by the honest verifier,
(2)
sound
if malicious prover cannot prove a false statement,
(3)
special sound
if repeated interaction with malicious prover (with a fixed false statement) allows efficient recovery of a valid witness, and
(4)
zero-knowledge
if the verifier does not learn any information
about the witness
w
(beyond its existence) after interacting with the prover.

Definition 8
(Proof of Knowledge)

A
(2n+1)
-rounds proof of knowledge
\mathsf{PoK}=(\mathsf{Setup},\allowbreak\mathsf{Keygen},\allowbreak\mathsf{P}_{1},\allowbreak\cdots,\allowbreak\mathsf{P}_{n+1},\allowbreak\mathsf{V}_{1},\allowbreak\cdots,\allowbreak\mathsf{V}_{n+1})
is an interactive protocol between a prover
\mathsf{P}
and a verifier
\mathsf{V}
.
We denote by
\big\langle\mathsf{P}(\mathsf{param},\allowbreak\mathsf{\vphantom{p}sk},\allowbreak\mathsf{\vphantom{p}pk}),\allowbreak\mathsf{V}(\mathsf{param},\allowbreak\mathsf{\vphantom{p}pk})\big\rangle
the transcript of a proof of knowledge between a prover
\mathsf{P}
and a verifier
\mathsf{V}
.

A proof of knowledge is correct if for all
\mathsf{param}
returned by
\mathsf{Setup}(1^{\lambda})
and all
(\mathsf{\vphantom{p}sk},\mathsf{\vphantom{p}pk})
returned by
\mathsf{Keygen}(\mathsf{param})
:

\mathsf{Pr}\left[\begin{array}[]{l}\textsf{accept}\leftarrow\big\langle\mathsf{P}(\mathsf{param},\mathsf{\vphantom{p}sk},\mathsf{\vphantom{p}pk}),\\
\mathsf{V}(\mathsf{param},\mathsf{\vphantom{p}pk})\big\rangle\end{array}\ \middle|\\
\begin{array}[]{l}\mathsf{param}\leftarrow\mathsf{Setup}(1^{\lambda})\\
(\mathsf{\vphantom{p}sk},\mathsf{\vphantom{p}pk})\leftarrow\mathsf{Keygen}(\mathsf{param})\end{array}\right]=1.

Definition 9
(Soundness)

A proof of knowledge is sound with soundness error
\epsilon
if for all
\mathsf{param}
returned by
\mathsf{Setup}(1^{\lambda})
, all
(\mathsf{\vphantom{p}sk},\mathsf{\vphantom{p}pk})
returned by
\mathsf{Keygen}(\mathsf{param})
and all probabilistic polynomial time
\mathsf{PPT}
malicious prover

\mathsf{Pr}\left[\begin{array}[]{l}\textsf{accept}\leftarrow\big\langle\tilde{\mathsf{P}}(\mathsf{param},\mathsf{\vphantom{p}pk}),\\
\mathsf{V}(\mathsf{param},\mathsf{\vphantom{p}pk})\big\rangle\end{array}\ \middle|\begin{array}[]{l}\mathsf{param}\leftarrow\mathsf{Setup}(1^{\lambda})\\
(\mathsf{\vphantom{p}sk},\mathsf{\vphantom{p}pk})\leftarrow\mathsf{Keygen}(\mathsf{param})\end{array}\right]\leq\epsilon+\mathsf{negl}(\lambda).

Definition 10
(Honest-Verifier Zero-Knowledge)

A proof of knowledge satisfies the honest-verifier zero-knowledge (HZVK) property if there exists a

simulator

that given as input

with input

Fiat and Shamir
[

The key observation was, if the verifier’s challenges are

(this is also called public-coin),
then one can emulate the verifier’s behavior using a hash function modeled as a random oracle.
Thus, one can transform an interactive proof of knowledge into a (non-interactive) signature.

(Theorem 3,
[
9
]
(Informal, rephrased))

special-sound interactive proof system is knowledge sound.

This means that special-sound, HVZK interactive proof systems can be

2.3
Stern Protocol

In this section, we present the initial Stern protocol
[
42
]
and discuss informally its correctness, soundness and zero-knowledge properties.
Given a public key
\mathsf{\vphantom{p}pk}=(\mathbf{H},\mathbf{y}^{\top})
, the Stern protocol permits to prove the knowledge of
\mathsf{\vphantom{p}sk}=(\mathbf{x})
such that
\mathbf{y}^{\top}=\mathbf{H}\mathbf{x}^{\top}
and
w_{H}\big(\mathbf{x}\big)=w
.
As such, the Stern protocol constitutes a proof of knowledge of a solution to an
\mathsf{SD}
problem instance.
The idea of the protocol is to prove the knowledge of
\mathbf{x}
using
\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{u}
for some random value
\mathbf{u}
and to prove that
w_{H}\big(\mathbf{x}\big)=w
using
\pi[\mathbf{x}]
for some random permutation
\pi
.

The protocol (see Figure
1
) is a 3-rounds construction in which the prover
\mathsf{P}
starts by generating three commitments
c_{1},c_{2},c_{3}
related to
\pi,\mathbf{x}
and
\mathbf{u}
.
Then, the verifier
\mathsf{V}
computes a challenge
\textsc{Ch}\overset{\;\$}{\longleftarrow}\{0,1,2\}
and the prover
\mathsf{P}
outputs a response
Rsp
.
During the verification step, the verifier
\mathsf{V}
recomputes two out of three commitments (depending on the value of the challenge
Ch
) using
Rsp
and either
accept
or
reject
the proof.
The correctness of the proof follows straightforwardly from its description.

Regarding the soundness of the proof, one can see that a malicious prover
\tilde{P}
can always cheat in two out of three cases.
Indeed, by choosing
\mathbf{z}
such that
\mathbf{H}\mathbf{z}=\mathbf{H}\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{y}
(no constraint on the weight of
\mathbf{z}
) and computing
c_{3}=\textsf{Commit}(\pi[\mathbf{z}])
and
\textsc{Rsp}=(\pi,\mathbf{z})
in the case
\textsc{Ch}=1
, the malicious prover
\tilde{\mathsf{P}}
will be accepted for both
\textsc{Ch}=0
and
\textsc{Ch}=1
.
Similarly, by choosing
\tilde{\mathbf{x}}
such that
w_{H}\big(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}\big)=w
and computing
c_{3}=\textsf{Commit}(\pi[\tilde{\mathbf{x}}+\mathbf{u}])
and
\textsc{Rsp}=(\pi[\tilde{\mathbf{x}}],\pi[\mathbf{u}])
in the case
\textsc{Ch}=2
, the malicious prover
\tilde{\mathsf{P}}
will be accepted for both
\textsc{Ch}=0
and
\textsc{Ch}=2
.
Finally, by choosing
\tilde{\mathbf{x}}
such that
w_{H}\big(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}\big)=w
and computing
c_{1}=\textsf{Commit}(\mathbf{H}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}+\mathbf{u})-\mathbf{y})
and
c_{3}=\textsf{Commit}(\pi[\tilde{\mathbf{x}}+\mathbf{u}])
as well as
\textsc{Rsp}=(\pi,\tilde{\mathbf{x}}+\mathbf{u})
in the case
\textsc{Ch}=1
and
\textsc{Rsp}=(\pi[\tilde{\mathbf{x}}],\pi[\mathbf{u}])
in the case
\textsc{Ch}=2
, the malicious prover
\tilde{\mathsf{P}}
will be accepted for both
\textsc{Ch}=1
and
\textsc{Ch}=2
.
In addition, if a malicious prover is able to cheat in the three cases, then one can build a knowledge extractor solving an instance of the
\mathsf{SD}
problem from its response.
This explains why this protocol has a soundness error equal to
2/3
.
In practice, one need to execute the protocol
\delta
times to achieve a negligible soundness error.
This is not depicted in Figures
1
and
2
which serve an illustrative purpose nonetheless these
\delta
iterations are depicted in Figure
3
which is used to describe a complete instantiation of our new construction.

Regarding the zero-knowledge property of the proof, one can see that if
\mathsf{COM}
is a hiding commitment scheme, then

3.1
Quasi-Cyclic Stern Protocol

Our new protocol (see Figure
2
) is a ZK PoK for the Quasi-Cyclic Syndrome Decoding (
\mathsf{QCSD}
) problem.
Given inputs
(\mathbf{H},\mathbf{y})\overset{\;\$}{\longleftarrow}\mathcal{QC}(\mathbb{F}_{2}^{k\times 2k})\times\mathbb{F}_{2}^{k}
, it allows a prover to convince a verifier that he knows
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{2k}
such that
\mathbf{H}\mathbf{x}^{\top}=\mathbf{y}^{\top}
and
w_{H}\big(\mathbf{x}\big)=w
without revealing anything on it.
Let
\mathbf{a}=(a_{0},\cdots,a_{k-1})\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{k}
, we define the
\textbf{rot}()
operator as
\textbf{rot}_{r}(\mathbf{a}):=(a_{k-r+1},\cdots,a_{k-r})
.
For
\mathbf{b}=(\mathbf{b}_{1},\mathbf{b}_{2})\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{2k}
, we slightly abuse notations and define
\textbf{rot}_{r}(\mathbf{b}):=(\textbf{rot}_{r}(\mathbf{b}_{1}),\textbf{rot}_{r}(\mathbf{b}_{2}))
.
As we are considering QC matrices, one can see that
\mathbf{y}^{\top}=\mathbf{H}\mathbf{x}^{\top}\Leftrightarrow\textbf{rot}_{r}(\mathbf{y})^{\top}=\mathbf{H}\,\textbf{rot}_{r}(\mathbf{x})^{\top}

hence one can prove the knowledge of the secret
\mathbf{x}
associated to the public value
\mathbf{y}
using any of
k
different equations.
This property allows to introduce a new kind of challenge that permits to reduce the soundness error to
1/2
asymptotically.

3.2
Existing Improvements and New Optimizations

Reducing the number of commitments

[
1
]
.

We recall that code-based proofs of knowledge generally feature a soundness error of
2/3
or
1/2
therefore one need to perform
\delta
iterations of these protocols to get a negligible soundness error.
Using Figure
2
as an illustrative example, the idea is to compress the commitments over all the iterations so that only two initial commitments
\textsc{Cmt}_{1}=(c_{1,1}||c_{1,2}||\cdots||c_{\delta,1}||c_{\delta,2})
and
\textsc{Cmt}_{2}=(c_{1,3}||\cdots||c_{\delta,3})
need to be sent.
As the verifier is only able to reconstruct 2 out of 3 commitments himself, the prover must send him the missing commitment at the end of each iteration.
Overall, this reduces the number of commitments to be sent from
3\delta
to
2+\delta
.

Small weight vector compression

[
1
]
.

The prover must reveal a permutation of a small weight vector
\pi[\mathbf{x}_{r}]
to answer some challenges.
Leveraging the small weight of
\mathbf{x}_{r}
, one can compress
\pi[\mathbf{x}_{r}]
before sending it thus reducing the cost of sending small weight vectors from
n
to approximately
n/2
.

Mitigation of an attack against 5-rounds protocols

[
15
]
.

The attack against 5-rounds PoK from
[
36
]
is relevant for our construction.
The key idea of this attack is to split the attacker work in two steps by first trying to guess the first challenge for several repetitions and then guess the second challenge for the remaining repetitions.
One has to increase the number of iterations
\delta
of the underlying PoK to ensure that the resulting signature remains secure, which increases its size.
One way to mitigate this attack is to consider
s
instances of the
\mathsf{SD}
problem within the keypair namely using
\mathsf{\vphantom{p}sk}=(\mathbf{x}^{i})_{i\in[1,s]}
and
\mathsf{\vphantom{p}pk}=(\mathbf{H},(\mathbf{y}^{i})^{\top}=\mathbf{H}(\mathbf{x}^{i})^{\top})_{i\in[1,s]}
.
Doing so, the first challenge space size is increased from
k
to
s\times k
which makes the attack less efficient so that
\delta
won’t need to be increased as much as initially thought.
In practice, this introduces a trade-off between key size and signature size.

Additional vector compression from seeds.

Starting from the initial Stern proposal, all constructions suggest to use seeds to reduce communication costs.
Using Figure
2
for illustrative purposes, one can use a seed
\theta
to compute
\pi
and then substitute
\pi
by
\theta
in the prover’s response.
We now introduce an additional vector compression that is only applicable to
\mathsf{SD}
based protocols.
One can go one step further and use a seed
\xi
to generate a random value
\mathbf{v}\overset{\;\$,\xi}{\longleftarrow}\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
and then compute the value
\mathbf{u}=\pi^{-1}[\mathbf{v}]
.
When the prover is required to send
\pi[\mathbf{u}]
, he now needs to send
\mathbf{v}
which can be substituted by
\xi
.
Instead of sending a vector of size
n
, the prover only sends a seed which greatly reduces the communication cost.
We now explain why this optimization can’t be applied to the AGS protocol.
Under the
\mathsf{GSD}
representation, the prover need to send
\pi[\mathbf{uG}]
rather than
\pi[\mathbf{u}]
whenever
\textsc{Ch}_{2}=1
.
But the quantity
\pi[\mathbf{uG}]
cannot be replaced by a seed
generating it as
\mathbf{uG}
is a codeword hence the optimization can not be applied.

Seed and commitment compression.

Using the previous optimization, one can see that the prover sends one seed during each iteration either
\theta
from which
\pi
can be recomputed or
\xi
from which
\pi[\mathbf{u}]
can be recomputed.
Let us consider two consecutive iterations of the protocol, the prover will have to send one of the following tuple of seeds:
(\theta_{1},\theta_{2}),(\theta_{1},\xi_{2}),(\xi_{1},\theta_{2}),(\xi_{1},\xi_{2})
.
If master seeds
\theta
(respectively
\xi
) are used to generate
\theta_{1}
and
\theta_{2}
(respectively
\xi_{1}
and
\xi_{2}
), then the prover will have to send one of the following values:
\theta,(\theta_{1},\xi_{2}),(\xi_{1},\theta_{2}),\xi
.
By using such a technique, one reduces the average communication cost associated to seeds by
25
%.
This optimization can be seen as a variation of the seed compression optimization from
[
37
]
in which the (unique) binary tree used is replaced by several binary trees of depth 1.
Similarly, one can also group commitments using binary trees of depth 1 (from bottom to top contrarily to the previous case) which reduces the cost associated to commitments from
(2+\delta)\cdot|\mathsf{com}|
to
(2+0.75\delta)\cdot|\mathsf{com}|
.

of messages corresponding to

implies that the extractor knows the prover’s response
for both challenge bits
b=0
and
b=1
. We next show how this information is used to solve the
\mathsf{DiffSD}(n,k,w,\alpha)
problem.
Let
((s_{1},r_{1}),\cdots,(s_{\alpha},r_{\alpha}))
be the challenges for which the adversary
\mathcal{A}
is able to produce accepting transcripts for both the cases
b=0
and
b=1
.
Let
d^{\,r_{j},b}=(d^{\,r_{j},b}_{1},d^{\,r_{j},b}_{2},d^{\,r_{j},b}_{3})_{j\in[1,\alpha],b\in\{0,1\}}
denote the responses of the adversary
\mathcal{A}
to these challenges, one can build a knowledge extractor
\mathsf{Ext}
as follows:

\got@maxcolwd

\displaystyle\vphantom{\rule[1.93748pt]{0.0pt}{0.0pt}}\text{1. Let }\bar{\theta}^{j}=d^{\,r_{j},0}_{1},\text{ compute }\bar{d}^{\,r_{j},0}_{1}\overset{\;\$,\bar{\theta}^{j}}{\longleftarrow}S_{n}\text{ for all }j\in[1,\alpha]

\displaystyle\text{2. Let }\bar{\xi}^{j}=d^{\,r_{j},1}_{1},\text{ compute }\bar{d}^{\,r_{j},1}_{1}\overset{\;\$,\bar{\xi}^{j}}{\longleftarrow}\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}\text{ for all }j\in[1,\alpha]

\displaystyle\text{3. Compute }\mathbf{c}_{1}=\bar{d}^{\,r_{1},1}_{1}=\cdots=\bar{d}^{\,r_{\alpha},1}_{1}

\displaystyle\text{4. Compute }\pi=\bar{d}^{\,r_{1},0}_{1}=\cdots=\bar{d}^{\,r_{\alpha},0}_{1}

\displaystyle\text{5. Compute }\mathbf{c}_{2}=\mathbf{H}d^{\,r_{1},0}_{2}-\textbf{rot}_{r_{1}}(\mathbf{y})=\cdots=\mathbf{H}d^{\,r_{\alpha},0}_{2}-\textbf{rot}_{r_{\alpha}}(\mathbf{y})

\displaystyle\text{6. Compute }\mathbf{c}_{3}=\mathbf{H}(\pi^{-1}[\mathbf{c}_{1}])-\mathbf{c}_{2}

\displaystyle\text{7. Compute }\mathbf{z}_{j}=\pi^{-1}[d^{\,r_{j},1}_{2}]\text{ for all }j\in[1,\alpha]

\displaystyle\text{8. Output }(\mathbf{c}_{3},\mathbf{z}_{1},\cdots,\mathbf{z}_{\alpha})

We now prove that
(\mathbf{c}_{3},\mathbf{z}_{1},\cdots,\mathbf{z}_{\alpha})
is a solution to the
\mathsf{DiffSD}(n,k,w,\alpha)
problem.
If
\mathsf{COM}
is a binding commitment scheme, one can compute
\mathbf{c}_{1}
as all the
\bar{d}^{\,r_{j},1}_{1}
values are equals due to commitment
c_{2}
.
Likewise, one can compute
\pi
and
\mathbf{c}_{2}
as all the values
\bar{d}^{\,r_{j},0}_{1}
and
\mathbf{H}d^{\,r_{j},0}_{2}-\textbf{rot}_{r_{j}}(\mathbf{y})
are equals due to commitment
c_{1}
.
Using the same argument for commitment
c_{3}
, one has
\pi[d^{\,r_{j},0}_{2}]=\mathbf{c}_{1}+d^{\,r_{j},1}_{2}
hence
d^{\,r_{j},0}_{2}=\pi^{-1}[\mathbf{c}_{1}]+\pi^{-1}[d^{\,r_{j},1}_{2}]
for all
j\in[1,\alpha]
.
From commitment
c_{1}
, one deduces that
\mathbf{c}_{2}=\mathbf{H}(\pi^{-1}[\mathbf{c}_{1}])+\mathbf{H}(\pi^{-1}[d^{\,r_{j},1}_{2}])-\textbf{rot}_{r_{j}}(\mathbf{y})
for all
j\in[1,\alpha]
.
Using quasi-cyclic codes, one gets
\textbf{rot}_{r_{j}}(\mathbf{y})=\mathbf{H}\,\textbf{rot}_{r_{j}}(\mathbf{x})
which gives
\mathbf{H}\,\textbf{rot}_{r_{j}}(\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{c}_{3}+\mathbf{H}\mathbf{z}_{j}
for all
j\in[1,\alpha]
.
In addition, one have
w_{H}\big(\mathbf{z}_{j}\big)=w
as
w_{H}\big(d^{\,r_{j},1}_{2}\big)=w
for all
j\in[1,\alpha]
.
Therefore
(\mathbf{c}_{3},\mathbf{z}_{1},\cdots,\mathbf{z}_{\alpha})
is a solution to the
\mathsf{DiffSD}(n,k,w,\alpha)
problem.
Using Theorem
3.2
completes the proof as there exists a reduction from
\mathsf{QCSD}(n,k,w)
to
\mathsf{DiffSD}(n,k,w,\alpha)
for an appropriate choice of
\alpha
.

Theorem 3.4
(Honest Verifier Zero-Knowledge)

If
\mathsf{COM}
is a hiding commitment scheme, then the PoK depicted in Figure
3
satisfies the Honest-Verifier Zero-Knowledge property.

Proof

We build a
\mathsf{PPT}
simulator
\mathsf{Sim}
that generates the view of an honest verifier with access to the public key
\mathsf{\vphantom{p}pk}=(\phi_{2},\mathbf{y}^{1},\cdots,\mathbf{y}^{s})
only:

\got@maxcolwd

\displaystyle\vphantom{\rule[1.93748pt]{0.0pt}{0.0pt}}\text{1. }\mathsf{Sim}\text{ samples }G_{1}=((s_{1},r_{1}),\cdots,(s_{\delta},r_{\delta}))\overset{\;\$}{\longleftarrow}([0,s-1]\times[0,k-1])^{\delta}\text{ and computes}

\displaystyle\mathmakebox{}\mathbf{for}\ i\in[1\;..\;\delta]\ \mathbf{do}

\displaystyle\mathmakebox{}\mathmakebox{}\theta_{i}\overset{\;\$}{\longleftarrow}\{0,1\}^{|\mathsf{seed}|},~\pi_{i}\overset{\;\$,\theta_{i}}{\longleftarrow}S_{n},~\xi_{i}\overset{\;\$}{\longleftarrow}\{0,1\}^{|\mathsf{seed}|},~\mathbf{v}_{i}\overset{\;\$,\xi_{i}}{\longleftarrow}\mathbb{F}_{2}^{2k},~\mathbf{u}_{i}=\pi_{i}^{-1}[\mathbf{v}_{i}]

\displaystyle\mathmakebox{}\mathmakebox{}\mathbf{H}\overset{\;\$,\phi_{2}}{\longleftarrow}\mathcal{QC}(\mathbb{F}_{2}^{k\times 2k}),~c_{i,1}=\textsf{Commit}(\pi_{i}\,||\,\mathbf{H}\mathbf{u}_{i}),~c_{i,2}=\textsf{Commit}(\pi_{i}[\mathbf{u}_{i}])

\displaystyle\text{2. }\mathsf{Sim}\text{ sends }\textsc{Cmt}_{1}=\textsf{Commit}(c_{1,1}||c_{1,2}||\cdots||c_{\delta,1}||c_{\delta,2})\text{ to }\mathcal{A}

\displaystyle\text{3. }\mathcal{A}\text{ answers }\textsc{Ch}_{1}=((s_{1},r_{1}),\cdots,(s_{\delta},r_{\delta}))\text{ to }\mathsf{Sim}

\displaystyle\text{4. }\text{If }G_{1}\neq\textsc{Ch}_{1}\text{, rewind to step 1}

\displaystyle\text{5. }\mathsf{Sim}\text{ samples }G_{2}=(b_{1},\cdots,b_{\delta})^{\delta}\overset{\;\$}{\longleftarrow}\{0,1\}^{\delta}\text{ and computes}

\displaystyle\mathmakebox{}\mathbf{for}\ i\in[1\;..\;\delta]\ \mathbf{do}

\displaystyle\mathmakebox{}\mathmakebox{}\mathbf{if}\ b_{i}=0\ \mathbf{then}\ \text{ compute }\mathbf{\tilde{x}}^{s_{i}}_{r_{i}}\text{ such that }\mathbf{H}\mathbf{\tilde{x}}^{s_{i}}_{r_{i}}=\textbf{rot}_{r_{i}}(\mathbf{y}^{s_{i}})\text{ (no weight constraint) }

\displaystyle\mathmakebox{}\mathmakebox{}\mathbf{if}\ b_{i}=1\ \mathbf{then}\ \text{ compute }\mathbf{\tilde{x}}^{s_{i}}_{r_{i}}\overset{\;\$}{\longleftarrow}\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}\text{ such that }w_{H}\big(\mathbf{\tilde{x}}^{s_{i}}_{r_{i}}\big)=w

\displaystyle\mathmakebox{}\mathmakebox{}c_{i,3}=\textsf{Commit}(\pi_{i}[\mathbf{u}_{i}+\mathbf{\tilde{x}}^{s_{i}}_{r_{i}}])

\displaystyle\text{6. }\mathsf{Sim}\text{ sends }\textsc{Cmt}_{2}=\textsf{Commit}(c_{1,3}||\cdots||c_{\delta,3})\text{ to }\mathcal{A}.

\displaystyle\text{7. }\mathcal{A}\text{ answers }\textsc{Ch}_{2}=(b_{1},\cdots,b_{\delta})\text{ to }\mathsf{Sim}.

\displaystyle\text{8. }\text{If }G_{2}\neq\textsc{Ch}_{2}\text{, rewind to step 5}

\displaystyle\text{9. }\mathsf{Sim}\text{ computes }

\displaystyle\mathmakebox{}\mathbf{for}\ i\in[1\;..\;\delta]\ \mathbf{do}

\displaystyle\mathmakebox{}\mathmakebox{}\mathbf{if}\ b_{i}=0\ \mathbf{then}\ ~d_{i}=\big(\theta_{i},\,\mathbf{u}_{i}+\mathbf{\tilde{x}}^{s_{i}}_{r_{i}},\,c_{i,2}\big)

\displaystyle\mathmakebox{}\mathmakebox{}\mathbf{if}\ b_{i}=1\ \mathbf{then}\ ~d_{i}=\big(\xi_{i},\,\pi_{i}[\mathbf{\tilde{x}}^{s_{i}}_{r_{i}}],\,c_{i,1}\big)

\displaystyle\text{10. }\mathsf{Sim}\text{ sends }\textsc{Rsp}=\mathsf{Compress}(d_{1},\cdots,d_{\delta})\text{ to }\mathcal{A}.

The view generated by
\mathsf{Sim}
is
(\textsc{Cmt}_{1},\textsc{Ch}_{1},\textsc{Cmt}_{2},\textsc{Ch}_{2},\textsc{Rsp})
.
If
\mathsf{COM}
is an hiding commitment scheme, then
\textsc{Cmt}_{1}
and
\textsc{Cmt}_{2}
are indistinguishable in the simulation and during the real execution.
In the honest verifier setting,
\textsc{Ch}_{1}
and
\textsc{Ch}_{2}
are generated similarly in the simulation and in the real execution.
Thus, one only needs to check
Rsp

The simulator
\mathsf{Sim}
behaves exactly as an honest prover
\mathsf{P}
except with respect to the values
(\mathbf{u}_{i}+\mathbf{\tilde{x}}^{s_{i}}_{r_{i}})
and
\pi_{i}[\mathbf{\tilde{x}}^{s_{i}}_{r_{i}}]

One can see that
(\mathbf{u}_{i}+\mathbf{x}^{s_{i}}_{r_{i}})

\mathbf{u}_{i}
is sampled uniformly at random from
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
.

\pi_{i}[\mathbf{x}^{s_{i}}_{r_{i}}]
and

\mathbf{\tilde{x}}^{s_{i}}_{r_{i}}

\mathbf{x}^{s_{i}}
and

Parameters and Resulting Sizes

The protocol described in Figure
3
can be turned into
a signature using the Fiat-Shamir transform
[
30
,
39
,
25
,
34
,
9
]
. Hereafter, we discuss
the choice of our parameters and compare our protocol with existing schemes.

Decoding attack.
We consider the BJMM generic decoder
[
12
]

with estimates from
[
33
]
. The parameters

(n,k,w)
are chosen such that decoding
w
errors in a binary
quasi-cyclic
[n,k]
code costs at least
2^{\lambda}
. The attacker
has access to
N=sk
syndromes (
k
rotations of
s
public
keys) and is successful by decoding only one of them. As shown in

[
41
]
, this multiple targets attack reduces the complexity by
a factor at most
\sqrt{N}
.

Soundness error.
Following
[
1
]
, for given
(n,k,w)
,
solving
\mathsf{DiffSD}(n,k,w,\alpha)
provides a solution to
\mathsf{QCSD}(n,k,w)

with probability
1-\varepsilon(\alpha)
where

\varepsilon(\alpha)\approx{n\choose w}^{\alpha-1}/2^{(n-k)(\alpha-2)}
.
The soundness error for one iteration cannot exceed

\rho^{*}=\frac{sk+\alpha^{*}-1}{2sk}
where
\alpha^{*}
is the largest
integer such that
\varepsilon(\alpha^{*})\leq 2^{-\lambda}
. The
soundness error for
\delta
iterations is
(\rho^{*})^{\delta}
and it
is lower than
2^{-\lambda}
if

\delta\geq\frac{-\lambda}{\log_{2}\rho^{*}}
.

Attack against 5-rounds protocols.
The attack of

[
36
]
can be used against our protocol. For
\delta
iterations
of the protocol, the attacker will find the value of
\tau^{*}
(the number
of second challenges to guess) which minimizes the attack cost

P^{-1}+2^{\delta-\tau^{*}}
where

P=\sum_{\tau\geq\tau^{*}}{\delta\choose\tau}\left(\frac{1}{sk}\right)^{\tau}\left(\frac{sk-1}{sk}\right)^{\delta-\tau}
. The
choice of
\delta
must be such that this cost is
\geq 2^{\lambda}
.

Signature length and scalability.
The signature
consists of the outputs of
\mathsf{P}_{1},\mathsf{P}_{2},\mathsf{P}_{3}
namely two
commitments and a seed along with all the
d_{i}
(see
Figure
3
). Each response
d_{i}
consists of a
seed, a commitment, and a word of
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
with no particular structure
if
b_{i}=0
and of weight
w
if
b_{i}=1
. The seeds and commitments are taken of
length
\lambda
and
2\lambda
respectively. The
words of weight
w
can be compressed to
n-k
bits. Finally the
seeds and commitments can be structured pairwise as explained in
Section
3.2
, allowing to save one seed and one commitment every
4 iterations on average. For codes of rate
1/2
(
k=n/2
) the average length of
the signature is
|\sigma|=5\lambda+\delta(0.75n+2.25\lambda)
. Both

\delta
and
n
will grow linearly with the security parameters

\lambda
and thus the signature length grows as
\lambda^{2}
, roughly
we have here
|\sigma|\approx 11\lambda^{2}
.

Comparison with other schemes.

Outside of code-based cryptography, there exist many other signatures based on the Fiat-Shamir transform.
Some of them were submitted to the NIST standardization process
[
21
,
23
]
while other have been published recently
[
16
]
.
All these schemes reduce to a given difficult problem like for instance the MQ or PKP problems.
For 128 bits of security, depending on these different post-quantum ZK signature schemes, the size of the signature may vary (also depending on chosen trade-offs) between 12kB for
[
16
]

[

.

5
Generalization to Additional Metrics

The protocol described in this paper can be generalized to other type of weight through the use of Full Domain Linear Isometries such as Hamming weight (classical or large) over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
or rank weight.
We define a Full Domain Linear Isometry (FDLI) as a set of linear isometries which has the property that given a random element
f
of this set, the image by
f
of a random word
\mathbf{x}
of weight
w
is a random word
\mathbf{y}
of weight
w
.
Hereafter, we give some examples:

\bullet

Hamming weight and words over
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
(the case of this paper): consider the set of permutations
S_{n}
;

\bullet

Hamming weight and words over
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
: consider the set of monomial matrices (a permutation matrix times a diagonal matrix with non null elements of
\mathbb{F}_{q}
on its diagonal). It can work for classical Hamming weight but also for more recent large weight
[
19
]
;

\bullet

Rank weight and vectors over
\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}^{n}
: consider the function (originally defined in
[
31
]
) which associate to a word
\mathbf{x}
of
\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}^{n}
a
m\times n
matrix
\mathbf{X}
by writing elements of
\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}
as vectors of
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{m}
through a basis
\beta
of
\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}
over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
.
Then multiply
\mathbf{X}
by two random invertible matrices
\mathbf{P}
and
\mathbf{Q}
over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
of respective sizes
m\times m
and
n\times n
such that
\mathbf{X}\rightarrow\mathbf{PXQ}
.
Finally, rewrite
\mathbf{PXQ}
as an element of
\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}^{n}
through the basis
\beta
.

\mathbf{P}
permits to associate a support (a vector space) of weight
w
to any other support of weight
w
and the matrix
\mathbf{Q}
permits to associate words with the same support.

When considering such FDLI functions the protocol described in this paper can directly be adapted for a given weight, simply by changing the weight and replacing the function
\pi
in the protocol by a random element of a FDLI set.
The proof are straightforward and are the same than in the binary Hamming case described in this paper; we omit them in this short version of the paper.
The notion of linearity and isometry permits to prove the correctness and the soundness of the adapted protocol while the full domain property permits to prove the zero-knowledge property.
Interestingly enough, there was an adaptation of the Stern protocol in
[
22
]
where the author had kept permutations but in a rank metric context.
In this case, the support of the secret vector was not modified and led to a (unseen at that time) break of zero-knowledge and ultimately a break of the whole protocol as described in
[
31
]
.
