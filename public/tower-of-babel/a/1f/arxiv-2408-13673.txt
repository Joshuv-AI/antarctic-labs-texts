Takahiro Tanaka, Yuko Urakawa

Abstract
The δ ​ N \delta N formalism has been the major computational tool to study the superhorizon evolution of the scalar type perturbation sourced by scalar fields. Recently, this formalism was generalized to compute an arbitrary scalar, vector, and tensor type perturbations, including the gravitational waves (GWs), sourced by an arbitrary bosonic fields. In this paper, we explain how to use the generalized δ ​ N \delta N formalism (the g δ ​ N \delta N formalism), considering a model with U(1) gauge fields as a concrete example. Several new findings on this model and prospects on future gravitational wave experiments are also discussed, including the condition for the two linear polarizations of GWs to have different amplitudes. This paper provides a detailed explanation of our previous paper published in Physical Review Letters. We also discuss the Weinberg’s adiabatic mode for an anisotropic background, showing a qualitative difference from the one for the FLRW background.

Introduction

Since the first detection of gravitational waves (GWs) by LIGO in 2015

Subsequent detections by LIGO, along with its partners VIRGO and KAGRA, have cataloged numerous events, including black hole mergers and neutron star mergers. These observations have tested Einstein’s theory of general relativity under extreme conditions and provided new insights on the origin of black holes and their impacts on the cosmological history. 2.1.1
Basic conditions

The g
\delta N
formalism computes the evolution of the large scale fluctuations at the leading order of the gradient expansion
[
5
,
7
]
, which is an expansion scheme with respect to the spatial gradient at each given time. This provides a useful tool to address the long wavelength evolution of the fluctuations in a cosmological setup (see also Ref.
[
6
]
). The gradient expansion starts with smoothing the small scale fluctuations. As a consequence of the smoothing, operating the spatial gradient gives rise to the suppression by a small parameter
\epsilon
, which is usually characterized by the spatial variation of
\varphi^{a}
within each causally connected. At the leading order of the gradient expansion,
\epsilon
is simply sent to 0.

The g
\delta N
formalism generically applies to a theory which satisfies

\{\varphi^{a}(t,\,\hbox{\boldmath{$x$}})\}

\displaystyle x^{i}\to\tilde{x}^{i}(t,\,x^{i}).

(1)

. Taking variation of the corresponding action gives local field equations for the coarse-grained fields.

We call a constraint which generates a gauge symmetry a gauge constraint, expressing it as
{\cal G}
. Gauge constraints can be derived by taking derivative with respect to Lagrange multipliers associated with the gauge symmetry. The variation of the action for
{\cal L}(t,x^{i})

\{\varphi^{a}\}
which remain after all the non-gauge constraints are solved and the gauge conditions that remain even in the homogeneous universe are employed.

Under these two conditions, the separate universe evolution

\displaystyle\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\varphi^{a}(t,\,\hbox{\boldmath{$x$}})=\varphi^{a}(t;\{\varphi^{a^{\prime}}(t_{*})=\varphi^{a^{\prime}}(t_{*},\,\hbox{\boldmath{$x$}})\}^{\prime})\,,

where

2.1.2
Noether charge

In the g
\delta N
formalism, the Noether charge of a global transformation,

\displaystyle x^{i}\to\tilde{x}^{i}=x^{i}+{M^{i}}_{j}\,x^{j}\,,

(3)

9

Here, we focus on the traceless part of

Then, the invariance of the action under Eq. (

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\int d^{3}\hbox{\boldmath{$x$}}\,{Q^{i}}_{j\hbox{\boldmath{\scriptsize$x$}}}=0\,,

(4)

is the Noether charge density given by

\displaystyle{Q^{i}}_{j\hbox{\boldmath{\scriptsize$x$}}}\equiv\frac{1}{M_{\rm pl}^{2}}\left(\left[-2{\pi^{i}}_{j}+\sum_{\alpha}\pi^{\alpha}_{\rm mat}\frac{\partial\delta_{M}\varphi_{\rm mat}^{\alpha}}{\partial{M^{j}}_{i}}\right]^{\rm TL}\right)\,.

(5)

Here,

and

and

are conjugate momenta defined as

\displaystyle\pi^{ij}\equiv\frac{\partial(N\sqrt{g}{\cal L})}{\partial\dot{\gamma}_{ij}}\,,\qquad\pi^{\alpha}_{\rm mat}\equiv\frac{\partial(N\!\sqrt{g}{\cal L})}{\partial\dot{\varphi}_{\rm mat}^{\alpha}}\,,

(6)

with
{\cal L}
being the total Lagrangian density, and

denotes the change of the fields under Eq. (
3

and a vector field

transform the transformation (

\displaystyle\delta_{M}\phi=\delta_{M}A_{0}=0\,,\qquad\delta_{M}A_{i}=-{M^{k}}_{i}A_{k}\,,

(7)

where higher order terms of
{M^{i}}_{j}
are ignored, considering infinitesimally small

2.2

\delta N
formalism

In this subsection, we derive the basic formulae in g
\delta N
formalism, which gives the mapping of the fluctuations between the horizon crossing,
i.e.
,

\displaystyle ds^{2}=-N^{2}dt^{2}+g_{ij}(dx^{i}+N^{i}dt)(dx^{j}+N^{j}dt)\,,

(9)

\displaystyle g_{ij}=e^{2\psi}\,\gamma_{ij}\,,

(10)

where
\gamma_{ij}
satisfies
\det[\gamma]=1
. Using
\psi
, with which the determinant of
g_{ij}
is given by
g=e^{6\psi}

\star

\displaystyle N_{i}(t,\,\hbox{\boldmath{$x$}})=0\,,

(11)

as a spatial gauge condition.

In
N_{i}=0

K
and the shear
{A^{i}}_{j}
, given by the trace part and traceless part of the extrinsic curvature, read

\displaystyle K=\frac{3}{N}\dot{\psi}\,,

(12)

\displaystyle{A^{i}}_{j}=\frac{1}{2N}\left(\delta^{i}_{k}\delta^{l}_{j}-\frac{1}{3}\delta^{i}_{j}\delta^{l}_{k}\right)\gamma^{km}\dot{\gamma}_{ml}=\frac{1}{2N}\gamma^{im}\dot{\gamma}_{mj}=-\frac{1}{2N}\dot{\gamma}^{im}\gamma_{mj}\,.

(13)

12

, we obtain the time evolution of

\displaystyle\psi(t_{f},\,\hbox{\boldmath{$x$}})-\psi(t_{*},\,\hbox{\boldmath{$x$}})=\frac{1}{3}\int^{t_{f}}_{t_{*}}dtN(t,\,\hbox{\boldmath{$x$}})K(t,\,\hbox{\boldmath{$x$}})\,.

\psi
and the longitudinal part of
\gamma_{ij}
(see Eq. (
78

, it was shown that the separate universe evolution

\displaystyle\delta\psi(t_{*},\,\hbox{\boldmath{$x$}})=0\,,

\displaystyle\Theta_{\alpha}(-\hat{\hbox{\boldmath{$k$}}})=\pi-\Theta_{\alpha}(\hat{\hbox{\boldmath{$k$}}}),\qquad\Psi_{\alpha}(-\hat{\hbox{\boldmath{$k$}}})=-\Psi_{\alpha}(\hat{\hbox{\boldmath{$k$}}})\,.

In what follows, for a notational brevity, we sometimes drop the argument
(\hat{\hbox{\boldmath{$k$}}})
of the polarization bases,
\Theta_{\alpha}
, and
\Psi_{\alpha}

By using

\displaystyle\gamma^{(\lambda_{\rm gw})}(t_{f},\,\hbox{\boldmath{$k$}})\equiv e_{ij}^{(\lambda_{\rm gw})}(\hat{\hbox{\boldmath{$k$}}})\,\delta\gamma_{ij}(t_{f},\,\hbox{\boldmath{$k$}})\,.

(77)

At

\displaystyle\zeta(t_{f},\,\hbox{\boldmath{$k$}})=\delta\psi(t_{f},\,\hbox{\boldmath{$k$}})-\frac{1}{4}\hat{k}_{i}\hat{k}_{j}\delta\gamma_{ij}(t_{f},\,\hbox{\boldmath{$k$}})\,.

(78)

3.2.2
Circular polarization bases

One may want to use circular polarization bases. Using
e_{i}^{(\lambda)}
, the circular polarization bases are given by

\displaystyle e^{(\pm_{c})}_{i}(\hat{\hbox{\boldmath{$k$}}})\equiv\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(e_{i}^{(1)}(\hat{\hbox{\boldmath{$k$}}})\mp ie_{i}^{(2)}(\hat{\hbox{\boldmath{$k$}}}))\,.

(79)

Using
e^{(\pm_{c})}_{i}
, one can construct a symmetric and traceless rank
n\,(=2,3,\,\cdots)
tensor. In particular,
n=2
gives the bases of the gravitational waves in the circular polarization bases as

\displaystyle e^{(\pm_{c})}_{ij}(\hat{\hbox{\boldmath{$k$}}})\equiv e^{(\pm_{c})}_{i}(\hat{\hbox{\boldmath{$k$}}})e^{(\pm_{c})}_{j}(\hat{\hbox{\boldmath{$k$}}})\,,

(80)

which satisfy

\displaystyle e^{(\pm_{c})}_{ij}(\hat{\hbox{\boldmath{$k$}}})e^{(\pm_{c})}_{ij}(\hat{\hbox{\boldmath{$k$}}})=0\,,\qquad e^{(\pm_{c})}_{ij}(\hat{\hbox{\boldmath{$k$}}})e^{(\mp_{c})}_{ij}(\hat{\hbox{\boldmath{$k$}}})=e^{(\pm_{c})}_{ij}(\hat{\hbox{\boldmath{$k$}}})e^{(\pm_{c})*}_{ij}(\hat{\hbox{\boldmath{$k$}}})=1\,,

(81)

where the upper asterisk denotes the complex conjugate. Using Eq. (
75
), we find

\displaystyle e^{(\pm_{c})}_{i}(\hat{\hbox{\boldmath{$k$}}})=e^{(\pm_{c})*}_{i}(-\hat{\hbox{\boldmath{$k$}}})=e^{(\mp_{c})}_{i}(-\hat{\hbox{\boldmath{$k$}}})\,.

(82)

As usual, we can define the circular polarization modes at
t=t_{f}
as

\displaystyle\gamma^{(\pm_{c})}(t_{f},\,\hbox{\boldmath{$k$}})\equiv e_{ij}^{(\mp_{c})}(\hat{\hbox{\boldmath{$k$}}})\delta\gamma_{ij}(t_{f},\,\hbox{\boldmath{$k$}})\,.

(83)

The GWs in the two porlarization bases are related as

\displaystyle\gamma^{(\pm_{c})}(t_{f},\,\hbox{\boldmath{$k$}})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\gamma^{(+)}(t_{f},\,\hbox{\boldmath{$k$}})\pm i\gamma^{(\times)}(t_{f},\,\hbox{\boldmath{$k$}})),

(84)

where we have used
e_{ij}^{(\pm_{c})}=(e_{ij}^{(+)}\mp ie_{ij}^{(\times)})/\sqrt{2}
. Using Eq. (
84
), we find that their power spectrums are related as

\displaystyle\langle\gamma^{(\pm_{c})}(t_{f},\,\hbox{\boldmath{$k$}})\gamma^{(\pm_{c})}(t_{f},\,\hbox{\boldmath{$p$}})\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\!\left(\langle\gamma^{(+)}(t_{f},\,\hbox{\boldmath{$k$}})\gamma^{(+)}(t_{f},\,\hbox{\boldmath{$p$}})\rangle-\langle\gamma^{(\times)}(t_{f},\,\hbox{\boldmath{$k$}})\gamma^{(\times)}(t_{f},\,\hbox{\boldmath{$p$}})\rangle\right)

(85)

\displaystyle\quad\pm\frac{i}{2}\!\left(\langle\gamma^{(+)}(t_{f},\,\hbox{\boldmath{$k$}})\gamma^{(\times)}(t_{f},\,\hbox{\boldmath{$p$}})\rangle+\langle\gamma^{(\times)}(t_{f},\,\hbox{\boldmath{$k$}})\gamma^{(+)}(t_{f},\,\hbox{\boldmath{$p$}})\rangle\right)\!\!.

(86)

When the cross-correlation between the
+
and
\times
modes does not vanish, the two circular polarization modes have in general different amplitudes.

3.2.3
Parity and circular polarization

When all the fluctuations at
t=t_{*}
are parity invariant, satisfying Eq. (
64
), the circular polarization modes of the GWs at
t=t_{f}
satisfy

\displaystyle\langle\gamma^{(\lambda_{1})}(t_{f},\,\hbox{\boldmath{$k$}}_{1})\cdots\gamma^{(\lambda_{n})}(t_{f},\,\hbox{\boldmath{$k$}}_{n})\rangle=\langle\gamma^{(-\lambda_{1})}(t_{f},\,-\hbox{\boldmath{$k$}}_{1})\cdots\gamma^{(-\lambda_{n})}(t_{f},\,-\hbox{\boldmath{$k$}}_{n})\rangle

(87)

for
\lambda_{1},\,\cdots,\,\lambda_{n}=\pm_{c}
, where we have used Eqs. (
66
) and (
82
). specially for the power spectrum with
n=2
, we obtain

\displaystyle\langle\gamma^{(\lambda_{1})}(t_{f},\,\hbox{\boldmath{$k$}})\gamma^{(\lambda_{2})}(t_{f},\,-\hbox{\boldmath{$k$}})\rangle=\langle\gamma^{(-\lambda_{2})}(t_{f},\,\hbox{\boldmath{$k$}})\gamma^{(-\lambda_{1})}(t_{f},\,-\hbox{\boldmath{$k$}})\rangle\,,

(88)

where we have used Eq. (
87
) and the commutation relation. We have also taken into account the momentum conservation. Therefore, as a direct consequence of the parity invariance, the auto power spectrums for the two polarization modes agree with each other.

Meanwhile, even if the correlators at
t=t_{*}
are all parity invariant, satisfying Eq. (
64
), the background contributions of the gauge fields may result in

\displaystyle\langle\gamma^{(\lambda_{1})}(t_{f},\,\hbox{\boldmath{$k$}}_{1})\cdots\gamma^{(\lambda_{n})}(t_{f},\,\hbox{\boldmath{$k$}}_{n})\rangle\neq\langle\gamma^{(-\lambda_{1})}(t_{f},\,\hbox{\boldmath{$k$}}_{1})\cdots\gamma^{(-\lambda_{n})}(t_{f},\,\hbox{\boldmath{$k$}}_{n})\rangle

(89)

for
n\geq 3
, while the parity is still conserved at
t=t_{f}
. In this sense, checking the validity of Eq. (
87
) becomes a more direct test of the parity violation. The non-Gaussian spectrums for the
+
mode and the
-
mode can have the different amplitudes without violating the parity symmetry. Similarly, the parity invariance at
t=t_{*}
leads to

\displaystyle\langle\zeta(t_{f},\,\hbox{\boldmath{$k$}})\gamma^{(\lambda)}(t_{f},\,-\hbox{\boldmath{$k$}})\rangle=\langle\zeta(t_{f},\,-\hbox{\boldmath{$k$}})\gamma^{(-\lambda)}(t_{f},\,\hbox{\boldmath{$k$}})\rangle\,,

(90)

while this is not necessarily equal to
\langle\zeta(t_{f},\,\hbox{\boldmath{$k$}})\gamma^{(-\lambda)}(t_{f},\,-\hbox{\boldmath{$k$}})\rangle
. The discussion in Sec.
3.2.3
applies generically, when the separate universe evolution
(\star)
holds and the fluctuations are parity invariant at
t=t_{*}
, satisfying Eq. (
64
).

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4}\hat{k}_{i}\hat{k}_{j}\delta\gamma_{ij}(t_{f},\,\hbox{\boldmath{$k$}})

\displaystyle=-\sum_{\alpha=1}^{D^{\prime}}\hat{k}_{i}\bar{\hat{\pi}}^{i}_{(\alpha)}\hat{k}_{j}\delta\hat{\pi}^{j}_{(\alpha)}(\hbox{\boldmath{$k$}})\bar{\hat{{\cal I}}}_{(\alpha)f}-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\alpha=1}^{D^{\prime}}\left(\cos^{2}\Theta_{\alpha}-\frac{1}{3}\right)\delta\hat{{\cal I}}_{(\alpha)f}(\hbox{\boldmath{$k$}})\,,

(122)

where the fluctuation of the spatial metric, which only gives the subdominant contributions, is again ignored. Since
\hat{\pi}^{i}_{(\alpha)}
is given by
\dot{A}_{i(\alpha)*}
as in Eq. (
55

in the first term can be written as

\displaystyle\hat{k}_{j}\delta\hat{\pi}^{j}_{(\alpha)}(\hbox{\boldmath{$k$}})=-\hat{k}_{i}\bar{\hat{\pi}}^{i}_{(\alpha)}\bar{\hat{\pi}}^{j}_{(\alpha)}\frac{\delta\dot{A}_{j(\alpha)*}}{\dot{\bar{A}}_{(\alpha)*}}\,,

(123)

where we have used
k_{i}\delta\pi^{i}_{(\alpha)}=0
. Meanwhile, the fluctuation of the
e
-folding number depends significantly on the detail of the models. Once the power spectrums at

are given, one can compute the power spectrums of the adiabatic curvature perturbation and GWs, using the formulae derived in this section.

4
Power spectrums of GWs

In the previous section, the mapping between
t=t_{*}
and
t=t_{f}
was derived only by imposing the assumption (
50
). In what follows, we consider the case where the contributions of the gauge fields to the background evolution had been negligibly small until
t=t_{*}
, which allows to ignore the cross-correlation among the two polarization modes of GWs, the gauge fields, and the scalar fields at
t=t_{*}
. To be precise, since we have chosen the background spatial metric as
\bar{\gamma}_{ij}(t_{f})=\delta_{ij}
, we cannot simultaneously set
\bar{\gamma}_{ij}(t_{*})=\delta_{ij}
in the presence of the background shear, while the corrections due to this become higher orders with respect to
\hat{\cal I}_{(\alpha)f}
. This difference can be taken into account by performing the corresponding coordinate transformation after computing the quantum correlations in another set of coordinates with
\bar{\gamma}_{ij}(t_{*})=\delta_{ij}
. These higher order corrections regarding
\hat{\cal I}_{(\alpha)f}
are ignored in this paper. We also ignore
\delta\pi_{I*}(\hbox{\boldmath{$k$}})
, imposing the slow-roll approximation at
t=t_{*}
.

We express the power spectrums of the scalar fields and (the conjugate momenta of) the gauge fields at
t=t_{*}
as

\displaystyle\langle\delta\phi^{I}_{*}(\hbox{\boldmath{$k$}})\delta\phi^{J}_{*}(\hbox{\boldmath{$p$}})\rangle=P^{IJ}_{*}(k)\delta(\hbox{\boldmath{$k$}}+\hbox{\boldmath{$p$}})\,,

(124)

\displaystyle\langle e_{i}^{(\lambda)}(\hat{\hbox{\boldmath{$k$}}})\delta\dot{A}_{i(\alpha)}(\hbox{\boldmath{$k$}})e_{j}^{(\lambda^{\prime})}(\hat{\hbox{\boldmath{$p$}}})\delta\dot{A}_{j(\alpha)}(\hbox{\boldmath{$p$}})\rangle=\delta^{\lambda\lambda^{\prime}}\delta_{\alpha\alpha^{\prime}}P_{(\alpha)*}^{\lambda}(k)\,\delta(\hbox{\boldmath{$k$}}+\hbox{\boldmath{$p$}})\,,

(125)

where
P_{(\alpha)*}^{\lambda}(k)
denotes the power spectrums of
\delta\dot{A}_{i(\alpha)}
in the linear polarization bases with
\lambda=1,\,2
.

4.1.1
Power spectrums

Using the formulae derived in this paper, we compute the power spectrums of GWs. In the main text, we consider the case with

(132)

\displaystyle g_{t(\phi;\alpha\beta)}\equiv\left(\frac{\partial\bar{\hat{{\cal I}}}_{(\alpha)f}}{\partial\bar{\phi}^{I}_{*}}\right)_{\!\!\dot{A}_{(\alpha)*}}\left(\frac{\partial\bar{\hat{{\cal I}}}_{(\beta)f}}{\partial\bar{\phi}^{J}_{*}}\right)_{\!\!\dot{A}_{(\alpha)*}}P^{IJ}_{*}(k)\,k^{3}\left(\frac{M_{\rm pl}}{H_{*}}\right)^{2}\,.

\displaystyle\langle\gamma^{(+)}(t_{f},\,\hbox{\boldmath{$k$}})\gamma^{(\times)}(t_{f},\,\hbox{\boldmath{$p$}})\rangle=\delta(\hbox{\boldmath{$k$}}+\hbox{\boldmath{$p$}})\frac{2}{k^{3}}\left(\frac{H_{*}}{M_{\rm pl}}\right)^{2}\left(\,\sum_{\alpha=1}^{D^{\prime}}g_{t(\alpha)}^{\rm cr}(\hbox{\boldmath{$k$}})\sin^{2}\Theta_{\alpha}(\hat{\hbox{\boldmath{$k$}}})+\Delta^{\rm cr}_{\phi}(\hbox{\boldmath{$k$}})\right)

\displaystyle g_{t(\alpha)}^{\rm cr}(\hbox{\boldmath{$k$}})=-g_{t(\alpha)}\sin 2\Psi_{\alpha}(\hat{\hbox{\boldmath{$k$}}})\left(1+\frac{{\cal D}\bar{\hat{{\cal I}}}_{(\alpha)f}}{\bar{\hat{{\cal I}}}_{(\alpha)f}}\sin^{2}\Theta_{\alpha}(\hat{\hbox{\boldmath{$k$}}})+\left(\frac{{\cal D}\bar{\hat{{\cal I}}}_{(\alpha)f}}{\bar{\hat{{\cal I}}}_{(\alpha)f}}\right)^{\!\!2}\hskip-2.0pt\sin^{4}\Theta_{\alpha}(\hat{\hbox{\boldmath{$k$}}})\cos^{2}2\Psi_{\alpha}(\hat{\hbox{\boldmath{$k$}}})\right),

in any choice of coordinates.

\gamma^{(+)}
and

4.1.2
Order estimation

For a concrete evaluation, in what follows, let us compute the mode function of the gauge fields, ignoring he metric perturbations and the mixing with the scalar fields until
t=t_{*}
. Then, the mode functions of
\delta A_{i(\alpha)}
in the subhorizon limit is given by

\displaystyle v^{(\alpha)}(t,\,k)=\frac{1}{f_{(\alpha)}(t)}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2k}}e^{-ik\eta(t)}\,,

(137)

for the two polarization modes in the linear polarization bases, where
\eta
denotes the corresponding conformal time. Choosing

\displaystyle\frac{P_{(\alpha)*}(k)}{\dot{\bar{A}}_{(\alpha)*}^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2k}\left(1+\left(\frac{\dot{\bar{f}}_{(\alpha)*}}{H_{*}\bar{f}_{(\alpha)*}}\right)^{2}\right)\left(\frac{H_{*}}{\bar{f}_{(\alpha)*}\dot{\bar{A}}_{(\alpha)*}}\right)^{2}

(138)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{12}\left(1+\left(\frac{\dot{\bar{f}}_{(\alpha)*}}{H_{*}\bar{f}_{(\alpha)*}}\right)^{2}\right)\frac{1}{k^{3}}\frac{\bar{\rho}_{*}}{\bar{\rho}_{A(\alpha)*}}\left(\frac{H_{*}}{M_{\rm pl}}\right)^{2}\,.

(139)

Using this expression, we obtain

, defined in Eq. (
132

\displaystyle g_{t(\alpha)}=\frac{\bar{\rho}_{*}}{3\bar{\rho}_{A(\alpha)*}}\bar{\hat{{\cal I}}}_{(\alpha)f}^{2}\left(1+\left(\frac{\dot{\bar{f}}_{(\alpha)*}}{H_{*}\bar{f}_{(\alpha)*}}\right)^{\!\!2}\right)\,.

(140)

To validate the perturbation,

\displaystyle 1\gg\frac{\bar{\rho}_{*}}{\bar{\rho}_{A(\alpha)*}}\left(\frac{H_{*}}{M_{\rm pl}}\right)^{2}\simeq\frac{H_{*}^{4}}{\bar{\rho}_{A(\alpha)*}}

(141)

needs to be fulfilled.

When
{\cal D}\bar{\hat{{\cal I}}}_{(\alpha)f}
does not vanish, the amplitudes of the two linear polarization modes become different (see also footnote
2
). The information of each model is encoded through
\hat{\cal I}_{(\alpha)f}
and the time variation of
f_{(\alpha)}
, which can be determined just by solving the background evolution. The smaller the background energy fraction of the gauge field
(\alpha)
becomes, the more the fluctuation of the corresponding gauge field generates GWs. This is because the amplitude of the fluctuation of the gauge field,

becomes larger as
|\dot{\bar{A}}_{(\alpha)}|
or
\bar{\rho}_{A(\alpha)}/\bar{\rho}
decreases. When we roughly estimate

and

\displaystyle\frac{g_{t(\alpha)}}{g_{t(\phi;\alpha\beta)}}\sim c_{s}\frac{f_{(\alpha)}}{f_{(\alpha),\phi}}\frac{f_{(\beta)}}{f_{(\beta),\phi}}\frac{\bar{\rho}_{*}}{\bar{\rho}_{A(\alpha)*}}\,.

(142)

Here, the different scalar fields are not distinguished just to provide an order estimation.

5
Conclusion

In this paper, we have shown that the superhorizon evolution of the primordial curvature perturbation and the primordial GWs can be easily computed by using the g
\delta N
formalism, applying this formalism to an inflation model with U(1) gauge field. The g
\delta N
formalism is useful also to develop an intuitive understanding of the non-linear evolution of long wavelength perturbations. The list of the new findings in this paper (and partly in our previous papers
[
21
,
22
]
) is

•

When the backreaction of the gauge fields on the evolution of the scalar fields become non-negligible, the two linear polarization modes of GWs,
\gamma^{+}
and
\gamma^{\times}
, evolve differently in the superhorizon limit.

•

When there exist more than one gauge field,
i.e.

D^{\prime}\geq 2
, there is no choice of the polarization bases for which the cross-correlations
\langle\gamma^{+}\gamma^{\times}\rangle
and
\langle\zeta\gamma^{\times}\rangle
vanish.

•

The spectrums of
\zeta
,
\gamma^{+}
, and
\gamma^{\times}
depend also on the azimuthal direction
(\Psi_{\alpha})
for
D^{\prime}\geq 2
.

•

The number of the Weinberg’s adiabatic mode in
\delta\gamma_{ij}
is 4 for
D^{\prime}=1
and 5 for
D^{\prime}\geq 2
(See Appendix
A.1
.).

•

The tensor (local-type) non-Gaussianities sourced by the gauge field(s)
f_{\rm NL}^{\gamma,A}
can be much larger than
{\cal O}(1)
, while keeping the adiabatic perturbation consistent with observations.

As was also pointed out in Ref.
[
72
]
, when only subdominant scalar fields directly interact with the gauge fields, the power spectrum of GWs can acquire O(1) statistical anisotropy, while keeping the adiabatic perturbation consistent with the present observations. As was commented around Eq. (
90
), even with the parity symmetry unbroken,
\langle\zeta(t_{f},\,\hbox{\boldmath{$k$}})\gamma^{(+_{c})}(t_{f},\,\hbox{\boldmath{$p$}})\rangle

\langle\zeta(t_{f},\,\hbox{\boldmath{$k$}})\gamma^{(-_{c})}(t_{f},\,\hbox{\boldmath{$p$}})\rangle
.

Since the g
\delta N
formalism generically applies to a model with the locality and the spatial diffeomorphism invariance, various applications are possible. An interesting application may be considering a non-abelian gauge field or a
