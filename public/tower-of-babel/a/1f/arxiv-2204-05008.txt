Abstract
. We introduce the notion of cover time to dynamical systems. This quantifies the rate at which orbits become dense in the state space and can be viewed as a global, rather than the more standard local, notion of recurrence for a system. Using transfer operator tools for systems with holes and inducing techniques, we obtain an asymptotic formula for the expected cover time in terms of the decay rate of the measure of the ball of minimum measure. We apply this to a wide class of uniformly hyperbolic and non-uniformly hyperbolic interval maps, including the Gauss map and Manneville-Pomeau maps.

Theorem 2.1
.

Assume
(f,\mu)
satisfies
(a1)
-
(a6)
and (
2.8
)–(
2.11
). There exist
0<c<C<\infty

such that for all
\delta>0
,

c\delta^{-s_{f}}\leqslant\mathbb{E}_{\mu}(\tau_{\delta})\leqslant C\delta^{-s_{f}}\log(1/\delta).

Moreover, if the system is Gibbs Markov and
f
has at least 2 full branches then we also have the sharp lower bound:

c\delta^{-s_{f}}\log(1/\delta)\leqslant\mathbb{E}_{\mu}(\tau_{\delta})\leqslant C\delta^{-s_{f}}\log(1/\delta).

The assumption that
f
has at least 2 full branches allows us to find, for all
\delta>0
sufficiently small, sufficiently many
\delta
-balls centred at a point in
\Lambda
which have measure close to
M_{\mu}(\delta)
and are ‘far’ from each other in the sense that there is a good lower bound on the time in between consecutive visits to these balls. This is enough to provide a sharp lower bound, however we expect that there are weaker assumptions which would yield the same conclusion.

In the case where there is a sharp lower bound, one may wonder whether the limit

\lim_{\delta\to 0}\frac{\mathbb{E}(\tau_{\delta})}{\delta^{-s_{f}}\log(1/\delta)}

exists. In the probability theory literature on cover times of random walks, the existence of the analogous limit has been established for some specific examples, such as for the expected cover time of a disk by a random walk in
\mathbb{Z}^{2}

[
DPRZ
]
and the expected cover time of the binary tree by a simple random walk
[
Al3
]
.

We also consider more general measures
\mu
which may not satisfy (
2.11
). Suppose
\dim_{\textup{M}}\mu<\infty
. For each
\delta>0
we denote

(2.12)

\mathrm{Err}(\delta):=\left|\dim_{\textup{M}}\mu-\frac{\log\left(M_{\mu}(\delta))\right)}{\log\delta}\right|.

Clearly
\dim_{\textup{M}}\mu<\infty
implies
\lim_{\delta\to 0}\mathrm{Err}(\delta)=0
. Therefore in the case that
\dim_{\textup{M}}\mu<\infty
, one can think of
\delta^{-\dim_{\textup{M}}\mu\pm\mathrm{Err}(\delta)}
as being upper and lower bounds on the measure of the ball of minimum measure at scale
\delta
.

Theorem 2.2
.

Assume
(f,\mu)
satisfies
(a1)
-
(a6)
and (
2.8
)–(
2.10
). There exist
0<c<C<\infty
and
\varepsilon>0
such that such that for all
\delta>0
,

(2.13)

\frac{c}{M_{\mu}(\delta/\varepsilon)}\leqslant\mathbb{E}_{\mu}(\tau_{\delta})\leqslant\frac{C}{M_{\mu}(\varepsilon\delta)}\log(1/\delta).

In particular if
\dim_{\textup{M}}\mu<\infty
then

(2.14)

c\delta^{-\dim_{\textup{M}}\mu+\mathrm{Err}(\delta/\varepsilon)}\leqslant\mathbb{E}_{\mu}(\tau_{\delta})\leqslant C\delta^{-\dim_{\textup{M}}\mu-\mathrm{Err}(\varepsilon\delta)}\log(1/\delta).

Note that
\varepsilon=\frac{t}{2T}
where
t,T
are given by (U)(a) below. We note that if the measure
\mu
is doubling (for example, if
f
is a finite branch Markov map and
\mu
is Gibbs), then the dependence on
\varepsilon
can be removed from the estimates. For example in (
2.13
) the denominators appearing in the bounds could be replaced by
M_{\mu}(\delta)
(the constants coming from the doubling property would be absorbed in the constants
c
and
C
). The upper bound in (
2.14
) is analogous to the upper bound in Theorem
2.1
. The reason that
\mathrm{Err}(\delta)
does not appear in Theorem
2.1
is because for a general measure
\mu
, the measure of the ball of minimum measure at scale
\delta
may take some time to resemble the asymptotic limit
O(\delta^{\dim_{\textup{M}}\mu})
, whereas if (
2.11
) holds then this can already be seen at large scales
\delta
.

In order to obtain a sharper lower bound in Theorem
2.2
, roughly speaking we would require an estimate on the number of balls of measure
O(\delta^{\dim_{\textup{M}}\mu+\mathrm{Err}(\delta/\varepsilon)})
seen at scale
\delta
. This is straightforward in the case (
2.11
) holds, since all balls of comparable diameter have comparable measure, which ensures a sharp lower bound in Theorem
2.1
. However we note that it is not always true that we have exponentially many balls of measure
O(\delta^{\dim_{\textup{M}}\mu+\mathrm{Err}(\delta/\varepsilon)})
at scale
\delta
(meaning the system can cover faster than
\log(1/\delta)
times the reciprocal of the measure of the ball of minimum measure at scale
\delta
) see for instance
[
JM
, Theorem 1.1(2)]
. In the other hand, in §
7
we provide two examples of slowly covering systems: systems for which

and provide estimates on the asymptotic growth of their expected cover times.

Lemma 2.5

Proof.

For each

\displaystyle\frac{m(f^{-j}(U)\cap Z)}{m(Z)}

(2.15)

\displaystyle\frac{m(Z\setminus\mathring{U}^{n_{1}})}{m(Z)}

\displaystyle m(\mathring{f}_{U}^{n_{1}}(Z))=m(f^{n_{1}}(Z))-m(f^{n_{1}}(Z\setminus\mathring{U}^{n_{1}}))

\displaystyle\geqslant m(f^{n_{1}}(Z))-\frac{m(Z\setminus\mathring{U}^{n_{1}})}{m(Z)}(1+C_{d})\geqslant c_{m}-\tilde{\beta}>0

∎

denotes the

and

-
(a4)

\sum_{Z\in{\mathcal{Z}}^{n}}e^{S_{n}\phi}<\infty

2.7.
Transfer operators for open systems.

Here we use spectral properties of appropriate transfer operators which will later give us information on hitting time statistics. This type of approach has been used previously, for example in
[
D
]
,
[
KL2
]
and
[
BDT
]
, but we have to make some adaptations, principally to Proposition
2.8
below in order to get uniform estimates for our setting. Lemma
2.9
and Corollary
2.10
are then essentially standard, but we include the short proofs for completeness.

Definition 2.7

(Transfer operators for open systems)
.

For any hole
U
let
\mathring{\mathcal{L}}_{U}:{\mathcal{B}}\to{\mathcal{B}}
denote the transfer operator with a hole at
U
, given by

\mathring{\mathcal{L}}_{U}(\psi):=\sum_{y\in\mathring{f}_{U}^{-1}x}\psi(y)e^{\phi(y)}=\mathcal{L}({\mathbbm{1}}_{I\setminus U}\psi).

Note that the iterates of
\mathring{\mathcal{L}}_{U}
are given by

(2.16)

\mathring{\mathcal{L}}_{U}^{n}\psi(x)=\sum_{y\in\mathring{f}_{U}^{-n}x}\psi(y)e^{S_{n}\phi(y)}=\mathcal{L}^{n}(\psi{\mathbbm{1}}_{\mathring{U}^{n-1}}).

By conformality of
m
,

(2.17)

\int\mathring{\mathcal{L}}_{U}^{n}\psi dm=\int\mathcal{L}^{n}(\psi{\mathbbm{1}}_{\mathring{U}^{n-1}})dm=\int_{\mathring{U}^{n-1}}\psi dm.

By (U) we have the following set of uniform Lasota-Yorke inequalities for the family of operators
\{\mathring{\mathcal{L}}_{U}\}_{U\in{\mathcal{U}}(\delta_{0})}
.

Proposition 2.8
.

There exist
C_{0}>0
and
0<\alpha<1
such that for any
\varphi\in{\mathcal{B}}
,
U\in{\mathcal{U}}(\delta_{0})
and
n\geqslant 0
,

\lVert\mathring{\mathcal{L}}_{U}^{n}\varphi\rVert\leqslant C_{0}\alpha^{n}\lVert\varphi\rVert+C_{0}\int_{\mathring{U}^{n-1}}|\varphi|dm.

The above is also true when
\mathring{\mathcal{L}}_{U}
is replaced by
\mathcal{L}
and
\mathring{U}^{n-1}
is replaced by
\Lambda
.

Proof.

Throughout the proof we’ll assume
\Lambda=I
, the proof in the more general case is similar. We begin by noting that our holes
U
are closed, meaning that each
\mathring{U}^{n}
is relatively open in
I
, which is in contrast to much of the literature where the hole is taken to be open and
\mathring{U}^{n}
is closed. For each
J\in{\mathcal{Z}}^{n}
there is a unique choice of disjoint intervals
\{(x_{1},x_{2}),\ldots,(x_{k_{J}-1},x_{k_{J}})\}
such that

J\cap\mathring{U}^{n-1}=\bigcup_{i=1}^{k_{J}/2}(x_{2i-1},x_{2i}).

In particular note that
x_{1}
is the left hand end point of
J
and
x_{k_{J}}
is the right hand end point of
J
.

Given
\psi\in{\mathcal{B}}
,

|\mathring{\mathcal{L}}_{U}^{n}\psi|_{BV}\leqslant\sum_{J\in{\mathcal{Z}}^{n}}|\psi e^{S_{n}\phi}|_{BV,J}+\psi(x_{1})e^{S_{n}\phi(x_{1})}+\cdots+\psi(x_{k_{J}})e^{S_{n}\phi(x_{k_{J}})}.

Note that for all
x\in\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{k_{J}}\}
,

\psi(x)e^{S_{n}\phi(x)}\leqslant\frac{1}{m(J\cap\mathring{U}^{n-1})}\int_{m(J\cap\mathring{U}^{n-1})}|\psi|e^{S_{n}\phi}dm+|\psi e^{S_{n}\phi}|_{BV,J}

yielding

(2.18)

\displaystyle|\mathring{\mathcal{L}}_{U}^{n}\psi|_{BV}\leqslant\sum_{J\in{\mathcal{Z}}^{n}}(k_{J}+1)|\psi e^{S_{n}\phi}|_{BV,J}+\frac{k_{J}}{m(J\cap\mathring{U}^{n-1})}\int_{J\cap\mathring{U}^{n-1}}|\psi|e^{S_{n}\phi}dm.

Next, note that

\displaystyle|\psi e^{S_{n}\phi}|_{BV,J}

\displaystyle\leqslant\sup_{J}\psi|e^{S_{n}\phi}|_{BV,J}+\sup_{J}e^{S_{n}\phi}|\psi|_{BV,J}

(2.19)

\displaystyle\leqslant\sup_{J}\psi C_{d}\sup_{J}e^{S_{n}\phi}+\sup_{J}e^{S_{n}\phi}|\psi|_{BV,J}

\displaystyle\leqslant\sup_{J}e^{S_{n}\phi}\left(|\psi|_{BV,J}(C_{d}+1)+\frac{C_{d}}{m(J\cap\mathring{U}^{n-1})}\int_{J\cap\mathring{U}^{n-1}}|\psi|dm\right)

where in the second line we have used Lemma
2.6
(b) and in the last line we have bounded
\sup_{J}\psi\leqslant\frac{1}{m(J\cap\mathring{U}^{n-1})}\int_{J\cap\mathring{U}^{n-1}}|\psi|dm+|\psi|_{BV,J}.

Combining with (
2.18
) we obtain

\displaystyle|\mathring{\mathcal{L}}_{U}^{n}\psi|_{BV}

(2.20)

\displaystyle\leqslant\sum_{J\in{\mathcal{Z}}^{n}}(k_{J}+1)\sup_{J}e^{S_{n}\phi}|\psi|_{BV,J}(C_{d}+1)+((k_{J}+1)\sup_{J}e^{S_{n}\phi}C_{d}+k_{J})\frac{1}{m(J\cap\mathring{U}^{n-1})}\int_{J\cap\mathring{U}^{n-1}}|\psi|e^{S_{n}\phi}dm.

By conformality and
(a1)
, for all
x\in J\cap\mathring{U}^{n-1}
,

(2.21)

e^{S_{n}\phi(x)}\frac{m(f^{n}(J\cap\mathring{U}^{n-1}))}{m(J\cap\mathring{U}^{n-1})}\leqslant 1+C_{d}.

Also, note that
k_{J}\leqslant 2n+4
, since
J\cap\mathring{U}^{n-1}
contains at most
n+1
holes corresponding to
U,\ldots,f^{-n}(U)
. Combining these with (
2.20
) we obtain

\displaystyle|\mathring{\mathcal{L}}_{U}^{n}\psi|_{BV}

\displaystyle\leqslant(2n+5)\sup_{I}e^{S_{n}\phi}(C_{d}+1)|\psi|_{BV}

(2.22)

\displaystyle\;\;\;+\sum_{J\in{\mathcal{Z}}^{n}}((2n+5)\sup_{I}e^{S_{n}\phi}C_{d}+2n+4)(1+C_{d})\frac{1}{m(f^{n}(J\cap\mathring{U}^{n-1}))}\int_{J\cap\mathring{U}^{n-1}}|\psi|dm

(2.23)

\displaystyle\leqslant(2n+5)\sup_{I}e^{S_{n}\phi}(C_{d}+1)|\psi|_{BV}+((2n+5)\sup_{I}e^{S_{n}\phi}C_{d}+2n+4)(1+C_{d})\frac{1}{c_{0}}\int_{\mathring{U}^{n-1}}|\psi|dm

where we have used Lemma
2.5
in the final line.

Applying (
2.23
) with
n=n_{1}
we see that for
\tilde{\alpha}=(2n_{1}+5)\sup_{I}e^{S_{n_{1}}\phi}(C_{d}+1)|<1
we have

|\mathring{\mathcal{L}}_{U}^{n_{1}}\psi|_{BV}\leqslant\tilde{\alpha}|\psi|_{BV}+((2n_{1}+5)\sup_{I}e^{S_{n_{1}}\phi}C_{d}+2n_{1}+4)(1+C_{d})\frac{1}{c_{0}}\int_{\mathring{U}^{n_{1}-1}}|\psi|dm.

We can then iterate this to complete the proof with
\alpha=\tilde{\alpha}^{1/n_{1}}
.
∎

By Proposition
2.8
, the compactness of the unit ball of
{\mathcal{B}}
in
L^{1}(m)
and the conformality of
m
that the spectral radius of
\mathring{\mathcal{L}}_{U}
acting on
{\mathcal{B}}
is at most 1 and its essential spectral radius is bounded by

The following lemma allows us to estimate

Lemma 6.4

There exists

Proof.

By (

\sup_{1\leq n\leq N_{1}}\sup_{x\in Y}|(f^{n})^{\prime}(x)|\leqslant C

\frac{\delta}{CN_{1}(N_{1}+1)}

\frac{\delta}{2CN_{1}(N_{1}+1)}

Denote

and note that for each

\mbox{\rm diam}(f^{n}(J))\leqslant C\mbox{\rm diam}(J)=\frac{\delta}{N_{1}(N_{1}+1)}\leqslant\frac{\delta}{2}
. Moreover, by definition of

,

(6.4)

I\setminus Y\subseteq\bigcup_{U\in A}U

Fix

and let

belongs to. Note that each

except possibly the following sets:

(This is because any set in

We are almost ready to estimate
\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{Y}}(\tau_{\delta})
in terms of
\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{Y}}({\mathcal{T}}_{\delta})

Lemma 6.5
.

Suppose

. Then

\tau^{Y}_{\delta}(x)>\frac{u}{\mu(Y)}\Rightarrow{\mathcal{T}}_{\delta}(x)>\frac{u}{1+\varepsilon\mu(Y)}

and

{\mathcal{T}}_{\delta}(x)>\frac{u}{1-\varepsilon\mu(Y)}\Rightarrow\tau^{Y}_{\delta}(x)>\frac{u}{\mu(Y)}.

Proof.

Note that for any
x\in Y
,
R_{{\mathcal{T}}_{\delta}(x)}(x)=\tau_{\delta}^{Y}(x)
.

From our definitions

\frac{R_{{\mathcal{T}}_{\delta}(x)}(x)}{{\mathcal{T}}_{\delta}(x)}=\frac{\tau^{Y}_{\delta}(x)}{{\mathcal{T}}_{\delta}(x)}=\frac{1}{\mu(Y)}+s

where
|s|<\varepsilon
since

. Therefore if
\tau^{Y}_{\delta}(x)>\frac{u}{\mu(Y)}
then

{\mathcal{T}}_{\delta}(x)=\frac{\tau^{Y}_{\delta}(x)}{1/\mu(Y)+s}>\frac{u/\mu(Y)}{1/\mu(Y)+\varepsilon}=\frac{u}{1+\varepsilon\mu(Y)}

which proves the first part. On the other hand if
{\mathcal{T}}_{\delta}(x)>\frac{u}{1-\varepsilon\mu(Y)}
then

\tau^{Y}_{\delta}(x)=\frac{{\mathcal{T}}_{\delta}(x)}{\mu(Y)}+s{\mathcal{T}}_{\delta}(x)>\frac{u}{1-\varepsilon\mu(Y)}(1/\mu(Y)-\varepsilon)=\frac{u}{\mu(Y)}

which proves the second part.
∎

The following proposition allows us to estimate
\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{Y}}(\tau_{\delta})
in terms of
\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{Y}}({\mathcal{T}}_{\delta})

Proposition 6.6
.

Assume that
\sum_{u=1}^{\infty}\mu_{Y}(A_{u})<\infty
. There exist constants

such that for all
\delta>0
,

c\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{Y}}({\mathcal{T}}_{\delta})\leqslant\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{Y}}(\tau_{\delta})\leqslant C\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{Y}}({\mathcal{T}}_{\kappa\delta}).

Proof.

Since
\mu_{Y}
is supported on
Y
, by Lemma
6.4

\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{Y}}(\tau_{\delta}^{Y})\leqslant\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{Y}}(\tau_{\delta})\leqslant\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{Y}}(\tau_{\kappa\delta}^{Y})

so it is enough to obtain lower and upper bounds on
\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{Y}}(\tau_{\delta}^{Y})
in terms of
\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{Y}}({\mathcal{T}}_{\delta})
. To this end

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{Y}}(\tau_{\delta}^{Y})

\displaystyle=\sum_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}\mu_{Y}(\tau_{\delta}^{Y}\geqslant n)=\sum_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}\left(\mu_{Y}(\tau_{\delta}^{Y}\geqslant n\wedge A_{n\mu(Y)}^{c})+\mu_{Y}(\tau_{\delta}^{Y}\geqslant n\wedge A_{n\mu(Y)}\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant\sum_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}\mu_{Y}\left({\mathcal{T}}_{\delta}\geqslant\frac{n\mu(Y)}{1+\varepsilon\mu(Y)}\right)+\sum_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}\mu(A_{n\mu(Y)})

\displaystyle=\sum_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}\mu_{Y}\left(\frac{{\mathcal{T}}_{\delta}(1+\varepsilon\mu(Y))}{\mu(Y)}\geqslant n\right)+\sum_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}\mu(A_{n\mu(Y)})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{Y}}\left(\frac{{\mathcal{T}}_{\delta}(1+\varepsilon\mu(Y))}{\mu(Y)}\right)+\sum_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}\mu_{Y}(A_{n\mu(Y)})

\displaystyle=\frac{1+\varepsilon\mu(Y)}{\mu(Y)}\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{Y}}({\mathcal{T}}_{\delta})+\sum_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}\mu_{Y}(A_{n\mu(Y)})

where to get the inequality we have used Lemma
6.5

, it is just a constant which is independent of

Similarly for the lower bound,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{Y}}(\tau_{\delta}^{Y})

\displaystyle\geqslant\sum_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}\mu_{Y}(\tau_{\delta}^{Y}\geqslant n\wedge A_{n\mu(Y)}^{c})\geqslant\sum_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}\mu_{Y}\left({\mathcal{T}}_{\delta}\geqslant\frac{n\mu(Y)}{1-\varepsilon\mu(Y)}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{Y}}\left(\frac{{\mathcal{T}}_{\delta}(1-\varepsilon\mu(Y))}{\mu(Y)}\right)=\left(\frac{1-\varepsilon\mu(Y)}{\mu(Y)}\right)\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{Y}}({\mathcal{T}}_{\delta})

6.5
.
∎

7.4.

\Pi([w_{1}ab^{n_{3}}])\in{\mathcal{V}}_{\delta}^{\prime}
(the bounds on the derivative
Df_{w_{1}}^{-1}
cannot hold). However, Proposition
2.4
(b) may not hold for
{\mathcal{V}}_{\delta}^{\prime\prime}
since
\sup_{x}|Df_{w_{0}\ldots w_{n}}^{-1}(x)|\leqslant\delta<\sup_{x}|Df_{w_{0}\ldots w_{n-1}}^{-1}(x)|
does not exclude the possibility that
\sup_{x}|Df_{w_{1}\ldots w_{n}}^{-1}(x)|\leqslant\delta<\sup_{x}|Df_{w_{1}\ldots w_{n-1}}^{-1}(x)|
. However, if we let
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
be sufficiently large that

\frac{\inf_{x}\min\{Df_{a}^{-1}(x),Df_{b}^{-1}(x)\}}{\left(\sup_{x}\min\{Df_{a}^{-1}(x),Df_{b}^{-1}(x)\}\right)^{k}}<1

noting that this is possible since
f
is uniformly expanding, then whenever
\sup_{x}|Df_{w_{0}\ldots w_{n}}^{-1}(x)|\leqslant\delta<\sup_{x}|Df_{w_{0}\ldots w_{n-1}}^{-1}(x)|
we have
\sup_{x}|Df_{w_{k}\ldots w_{n}}^{-1}(x)|>\delta
. In particular
w_{\ell}\ldots w_{n}ab^{n_{3}}\notin{\mathcal{V}}_{\delta}^{\prime\prime}
for any
\ell\geqslant k
. Therefore, we can find
{\mathcal{V}}_{\delta}\subset{\mathcal{V}}_{\delta}^{\prime\prime}
, where
\#{\mathcal{V}}_{\delta}\geqslant\frac{1}{k}\#{\mathcal{V}}_{\delta}^{\prime\prime}
, so that Proposition
2.4
(b) holds for
{\mathcal{V}}_{\delta}
. Moreover, the remaining parts of Proposition
2.4
also hold for
{\mathcal{V}}_{\delta}
.
