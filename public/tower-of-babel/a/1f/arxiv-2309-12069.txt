Optimal non-gaussian Dvoretzky-Milman embeddings
Daniel Bartl, Shahar Mendelson

Abstract
We construct the first non-gaussian ensemble that yields the optimal estimate in the Dvoretzky-Milman Theorem: the ensemble exhibits almost Euclidean sections in arbitrary normed spaces of the same dimension as the gaussian embedding—despite being very far from gaussian (in fact, it happens to be heavy-tailed).



1
Introduction

The Dvoretzky-Milman Theorem is one of most remarkable results in Asymptotic Geometric Analysis. Dvoretzky proved in
[
8
]
that
\ell_{2}
is
finitely represented
in any infinite dimensional normed space: that any infinite dimensional normed space has subspaces of arbitrarily high (finite) dimension that are almost Euclidean in the
Banach-Mazur sense
(for the definition of that distance see, e.g.,
[
2
]
). Dvoretzky’s proof was finite dimensional: he showed that there is
d=d(n,\varepsilon)
that tends to infinity with
n
for any fixed
\varepsilon>0
, such that every
n
-dimensional normed space has a subspace of dimension
d
that is
1+\varepsilon
close to Euclidean.
As it happens, his estimate on
d(n,\varepsilon)
was suboptimal, and was dramatically improved by Milman in his seminal work
[
19
]
.

Milman showed that any finite dimensional normed space
(\mathbb{R}^{n},\|\cdot\|)
has a
critical dimension

d^{\ast}
, and a typical subspace of dimension
c(\varepsilon)d^{\ast}
—with respect to the Haar measure on the Grassmann manifold of that dimension—is almost Euclidean. Moreover, every normed space is isometric to a space for which
d^{\ast}\geq c_{1}\log n
.

The idea behind Milman’s proof is based on the fact that Lipschitz functions are almost constant on the sphere.
More accurately and in the gaussian version of the theorem, set
d\leq d^{\ast}
and let
G_{1},\dots,G_{d}
be independent copies of the standard gaussian random vector
G
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
One can show that a typical realization of the function
x\mapsto\|\sum_{i=1}^{d}x_{i}G_{i}\|
is almost constant on
S^{d-1}
.
As a result, there is
\Lambda>0
such that for every
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

(1-\varepsilon)\|x\|_{2}\leq\Lambda^{-1}\left\|\sum_{i=1}^{d}x_{i}G_{i}\right\|\leq(1+\varepsilon)\|x\|_{2}.

(1.1)

Equation
1.1
follows from two features of the gaussian vector: firstly,
rotation invariance
, in particular that for any
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
\mathbb{E}\|\sum_{i=1}^{d}x_{i}G_{i}\|=\|x\|_{2}\cdot\mathbb{E}\|G\|
; and secondly, that each random variable
\|\sum_{i=1}^{d}x_{i}G_{i}\|

concentrates sharply
around its mean.

Since Milman’s proof—some 50 years ago—the only random ensembles known to exhibit Euclidean subspaces of the optimal (critical) dimension in arbitrary normed spaces were the gaussian ensemble
x\to\sum_{i=1}^{d}x_{i}G_{i}
; and a
d
-dimensional subspace selected according to the Haar measure on the right Grassmann manifold.
Those were the only examples of random ensembles that satisfied both rotation invariance and suitable concentration.
Our goal in what follows is to construct more general ensembles (in fact, ensembles that can be heavy-tailed) that exhibit Euclidean subspaces of the optimal dimension in arbitrary normed spaces.

To formulate Milman’s result, consider a normed space
F=(\mathbb{R}^{n},\|\cdot\|)
and denote its unit ball by
K
.
Thus,
K
is a
convex body
: it is a convex, compact, centrally-symmetric subset of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with a nonempty interior.
Denote by
\left\langle\cdot,\cdot\right\rangle
the standard Euclidean inner product, and set

K^{\circ}=\{y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\left\langle x,y\right\rangle\leq 1\ {\rm for\ every\ }x\in K\}

to be the polar body of
K
.
Consider the standard gaussian random vector
G
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and define the critical dimension of
K
by

d^{\ast}(K)=\left(\frac{\mathbb{E}\|G\|}{\sup_{t\in K^{\circ}}\|t\|_{2}}\right)^{2}.

Milman’s result (in its gaussian version) is the following.

Theorem 1.1
.

For every
\varepsilon>0
there is a constant
c_{1}
depending on
\varepsilon
and absolute constants
c_{2},c_{3}
such that the following holds.
Let
d\leq c_{1}(\varepsilon)d^{\ast}(K)
and set
G_{1},\dots,G_{d}
to be independent copies of
G
.
If
A=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\left\langle e_{i},\cdot\right\rangle G_{i}\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
then with probability at least
1-2\exp(-c_{2}\varepsilon^{2}d^{\ast}(K))
,

\displaystyle(1-\varepsilon)\mathbb{E}\|G\|\cdot\left(K\cap A\mathbb{R}^{d}\right)\subset AB_{2}^{d}\subset(1+\varepsilon)\mathbb{E}\|G\|\cdot\left(K\cap A\mathbb{R}^{d}\right).

Moreover, for any convex body
K
there is
T\in GL_{n}
such that
d^{\ast}(TK)\geq c_{3}\log n
.

Remark 1.2
.

Clearly, for any
T\in GL_{n}
the normed spaces whose unit balls are
K
and
TK
are isometric. Hence, for the purpose of finding almost Euclidean sections of
K
we may and do assume without loss of generality that
d^{\ast}(K)\geq c\log n
.

The proof of Theorem
1.1
with the optimal
c_{1}(\varepsilon)\sim\varepsilon^{2}
is due to Gordon
[
9
]
(see also Schechtman’s proof
[
23
]
).
A simpler argument that follows Milman’s original proof can be found in
[
21
]
, leading to the slightly suboptimal estimate of
c_{1}(\varepsilon)\sim\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{\log(1/\varepsilon)}
.
A detailed survey on the Dvoretzky-Milman Theorem can be found in
[
2
]
.

The key features of the gaussian ensemble
A
that lead to the proof of Theorem
1.1
, namely, “gaussian concentration” and rotation invariance are rather special.
At least one of the two fails when it comes to other natural choices of random vectors.
For example, if
A
is generated by the Bernoulli random vector
(\varepsilon_{i})_{i=1}^{n}
—uniformly distributed in
\{-1,1\}^{n}
—, and
\|\cdot\|=\|\cdot\|_{\infty}
, then
x\mapsto\mathbb{E}\|Ax\|
is not even equivalent to a constant on
S^{d-1}
. The reason for the gap between
\inf_{x\in S^{d-1}}\|Ax\|_{\infty}
and
\sup_{x\in S^{d-1}}\|Ax\|_{\infty}
is straightforward: if
x=e_{i}
,
\|Ax\|_{\infty}=\|(\varepsilon_{1},...,\varepsilon_{n})\|_{\infty}=1
, but if
x
is in a ‘diagonal direction’,
\|Ax\|_{\infty}
is close to
\|G\|_{\infty}
, and for a typical realization the latter behaves like
\sqrt{\log n}
(see, e.g.,
[
15
]
for a detailed proof).
Also, it is standard to verify that
\|Ax\|
does not exhibit gaussian concentration around its mean for every fixed
x\in S^{d-1}
(see
[
12
]
).

Given the difficulty in finding ensembles that satisfy the two features, it is natural to ask whether the gaussian ensemble is really the only
optimal Dvoretzky-Milman ensemble
.
That is, are there other families of random operators that exhibit,
for an arbitrary normed space
and with nontrivial probability, the same behaviour as the gaussian operator: exposing Euclidean sections of the space’s critical dimension.
There are examples of optimal ensembles for restricted classes of normed spaces (e.g. spaces with cotype 2, see
[
18
]
), but none of them preforms well in an
arbitrary
normed space.

The
key
to our construction of general Dvoretzky-Milman ensembles is factoring through an intermediate space: first use the random operator
\Gamma\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
(with
m=\mathrm{poly}(d)
) that has iid rows
(X_{i})_{i=1}^{m}
generated by a random vector
X
,

\Gamma=\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left\langle X_{i},\cdot\right\rangle e_{i};

and then apply the random operator

D=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left\langle e_{i},\cdot\right\rangle Z_{i}\colon\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\mathbb{R}^{n},

where
(Z_{i})_{i=1}^{m}
are independent copies of a random vector
Z
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
that has iid coordinates; in particular,
D
has iid entries.
We will always assume that
(X_{i})_{i=1}^{m}
and
(Z_{i})_{i=1}^{m}
are independent.

As a preliminary test case to this idea’s validity, let
Z
be the standard gaussian random vector in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
Clearly
D\Gamma
has a chance of being an optimal Dvoretzky-Milman ensemble only if
\Gamma
is an almost isometric Euclidean embedding of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
in
\mathbb{R}^{m}
; otherwise, the embedding would fail even if
\|\cdot\|
were the Euclidean norm. At the same time, even if
\Gamma
is an isometry,
D\Gamma
need not be an optimal Dvoretzky-Milman ensemble for an arbitrary subgaussian

has iid rows distributed according to a random vector that is rotation invariant, and is an almost isometric embedding of

Before we list the assumptions that we require and specify the choices of
d
and

, let us recall some well-known definitions and facts.

Definition 1.3
.

A centred random vector
X
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
satisfies
L_{p}-L_{2}
norm equivalence with constant constant
L
if for any
u\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

\displaystyle\|\left\langle X,u\right\rangle\|_{L_{p}}\leq L\|\left\langle X,u\right\rangle\|_{L_{2}}.

(1.2)

Note that if, in addition,
X
is isotropic (that is,
X
is centred and its covariance is the identity), then (
1.2
) is equivalent to
\sup_{u\in S^{d-1}}\|\left\langle X,u\right\rangle\|_{L_{p}}\leq L
.

Next, given
X_{1},...,X_{m}
that are independent, selected according to the isotropic random vector
X
, set

\rho_{d,m}=\sup_{u\in S^{d-1}}\left|\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left\langle X_{i},u\right\rangle^{2}-1\right|.

Observe that the singular values of the random matrix
\Gamma=m^{-1/2}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left\langle X_{i},\cdot\right\rangle e_{i}
satisfy that

1-\rho_{d,m}\leq\lambda_{\rm{min}}^{2}(\Gamma)\leq\lambda_{\rm{max}}^{2}(\Gamma)\leq 1+\rho_{d,m}.

The behaviour of
\rho_{d,m}
is well-understood in rather general situations (see, e.g.,
[

,
11
,
17
,
25
]
).
It turns out that under minimal assumptions on
X
and with high probability, the random matrix
\Gamma
satisfies the
quantitative Bai-Yin asymptotics
(see
[
4
]
), namely that
\rho_{d,m}\leq c(d/m)^{1/2}
for an absolute constant
c
.

The assumptions we require on the random vectors
X
and
Z
are as follows:

(A1)

X
is an isotropic, rotation invariant random vector in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
that satisfies
L_{4}-L_{2}
norm equivalence with constant
L\geq 1
.

Moreover, with probability at least
1-\eta
,
\rho_{d,m}\leq B(d/m)^{2/\alpha}
for some constants
B\geq 1
and
\alpha\geq 4
.

(A2)

Z
is an isotropic, symmetric,
L
-subgaussian random vector in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
that has iid coordinates.

A key component in our construction relies on the recent results of
[
5
]
. In a nutshell, the random set
\Gamma S^{d-1}
has a rather specific structure: the coordinates of each vector
(\left\langle X_{i},u\right\rangle)_{i=1}^{m}
concentrate around a well-determined set of values, endowed by the probability distribution of
X
. More accurately, denote by
a^{\sharp}
the monotone non-decreasing rearrangement of a vector
a\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
and set, for
1\leq s\leq m
,

H_{s,m}=\sup_{u\in S^{d-1}}\sup_{|I|\leq s}\left(\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i\in I}\left\langle X_{i},u\right\rangle^{2}\right)^{1/2}.

Let
F_{\left\langle X,u\right\rangle}^{-1}
be the (right-)inverse distribution function of
\left\langle X,u\right\rangle
and put, for
i=1,\dots,m
,

\lambda_{i}^{u}=m\int_{(\frac{i-1}{m},\frac{i}{m}]}F_{\left\langle X,u\right\rangle}^{-1}(p)\,dp.

Proposition 1.4

(
[
5
]
)
.

Then there are constants
c_{1},c_{2},c_{3}
that depend on
L,B,\alpha
such that the following holds.
Suppose that Assumption (A1) is satisfied with the values
L,B,\alpha
.
Then, for every
d\geq 1
and
m\geq c_{1}d
, with probability at least
1-\eta-\exp(-d)
,

\sup_{u\in S^{d-1}}\left(\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left|\left\langle X_{i},u\right\rangle^{\sharp}-\lambda_{i}^{u}\right|^{2}\right)^{1/2}\leq c_{2}\left(\frac{d}{m}\right)^{1/\alpha}\log\left(\frac{m}{d}\right),

and for every
1\leq s\leq m
,

H_{s,m}\leq c_{3}\left(\left(\frac{s}{m}\right)^{1/4}+\left(\frac{d}{m}\right)^{1/\alpha}\log\left(\frac{m}{d}\right)\right).

Denote by
\Omega(\mathbb{X})
the event in which both the assertion of Proposition
1.4
holds and
\rho_{d,m}\leq B(d/m)^{2/\alpha}
.

Note that if
X
is rotation invariant then
\lambda^{u}=\lambda^{v}
for every
u,v\in S^{d-1}
. Thus, Proposition
1.4
implies that after replacing the vectors in
\Gamma S^{d-1}
by their monotone non-decreasing ordering, the resulting set has a small Euclidean diameter.
This is the key feature that ‘generates’ sufficient invariance.
As a result there is no need to assume that the random vector
Z
is rotation invariant.

We are now ready to formulate our main result.
Recall that
\Gamma\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
is the random operator whose rows are
(X_{i})_{i=1}^{m}
and
D\colon\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
is the random operator whose columns are
(Z_{i})_{i=1}^{m}
.
And, assume (as we may—see Remark

) that
d^{\ast}(K)\geq C_{0}\log n

.

Theorem 1.5
.

There are constants
c_{1}\leq\frac{1}{2}
,
c_{2},\dots,c_{8}
that depend on
L,B,\alpha,C_{0}
such that the following holds.
Suppose that Assumptions (A1) and (A2) are satisfied with the values
L,B,\alpha
.
Let
\varepsilon\leq c_{1}
,
n\geq c_{2}
,

m=c_{3}(d^{\ast}(K))^{\alpha}\quad\text{and}\quad d\leq c_{4}\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{\log(1/\varepsilon)}d^{\ast}(K).

Then there is some
\Lambda\in[c_{5}\mathbb{E}\|G\|,c_{6}\mathbb{E}\|G\|]
such that for any realization
(X_{i})_{i=1}^{m}\in\Omega(\mathbb{X})
, with
\mathbb{P}_{Z}
-probability at least
1-2\exp(-c_{7}\varepsilon^{2}d^{\ast}(K))
,

\sup_{u\in S^{d-1}}\left|\frac{\|D\Gamma u\|}{\Lambda}-1\right|\leq c_{8}\varepsilon.

Remark 1.6
.

The assumption that
X
satisfies
L_{4}-L_{2}
norm equivalence is necessary: by
[

]
, if
X
has iid coordinates distributed according to a mean zero, variance

tend to infinity while keeping their ratio constant.
Hence, without the norm equivalence assumption

is not well-behaved and
\Gamma
cannot even be an isomorphic embedding of
\ell_{2}^{d}
in
\ell_{2}^{m}
.
At the same time, if
\Gamma
satisfies the quantitative Bai-Yin asymptotics (and as noted previously, that is the case under minimal assumptions on

\alpha=4
.

Remark 1.7
.

The idea of factoring through a higher dimensional space was used in
[
15
]
to prove the existence of

non-gaussian Dvoretzky-Milman ensembles.
The argument was based on establishing lower bounds on the expected suprema of certain Bernoulli processes.
Such bounds are possible only up to a multiplicative constant, and thus cannot be used to prove that an ensemble yields an almost isometric embedding — regardless of the choice of
X
.

The rest of the article is devoted to the proof of Theorem

.
Before we dive into technical details, let us give an example of such an ensemble, for a heavy-tailed choice of
X
and

— the Bernoulli vector.

Example 1.8
.

Let
K
be the unit ball of
(\mathbb{R}^{n},\|\cdot\|)

.
Let
W
be distributed uniformly in

and consider a symmetric random variable

\mathbb{E}v^{2}=1
and

(but potentially

Setting

.
By

Remark 1.9
.

Let us note that the set of Euclidean subspaces generated by our operator

significantly differs from those generated by the standard approach.
Have, for example, the setting in Example

.
We claim that the Haar measure of all

is zero.

Indeed, each subspace we generate is

Since there are

) that a

is contained in a

is 0.
By rotation invariance, we may assume that the

Moreover,

But

, and almost surely all coordinate of the standard gaussian are nonzero.

exhibits ‘gaussian’ concentration around its mean (a behaviour that does not hold for an arbitrary

).

Preliminaries and a reduction step

We start with a word about
notation
.

is the Euclidean norm and
\left\langle\cdot,\cdot\right\rangle
is the standard inner product—though in what follows we do not specify the (finite) dimension of the underlying space.
Throughout,

are absolute constants whose values may change from line to line.
If a constant

for absolute constants

, that is denoted by

.
The cardinality of finite sets is denoted by

.
For two independent random vectors

; i.e., if

.

(2.1)

Recall that

4.1
Highlights of the proof of Theorem
4.1

It is worth stressing once again that the proof of Theorem
4.1
does not simply follow from gaussian concentration even though
Z
is subgaussian.
In fact, it was recently shown that gaussian-like concentration in a subgaussian setup is false in general, see
[
12
]
. As we explain in what follows, what saves the day is the particular structure of the vectors
(\left\langle X_{i},u\right\rangle)_{i=1}^{m}
.

The idea is to write for
u\in W
,

\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left\langle X_{i},u\right\rangle Z_{i}=\clubsuit_{u}+\diamondsuit_{u}+\heartsuit_{u},

where
\|\clubsuit_{u}\|
and
\|\diamondsuit_{u}\|
are “small”, while
\|\heartsuit_{u}\|
concentrates sharply around its mean.
The vectors
\clubsuit_{u}
are obtained by truncating the vectors
(\left\langle X_{i},u\right\rangle)_{i=1}^{m}
, and
\diamondsuit_{u}
is obtained from a truncation of
(\|Z\|_{i})_{i=1}^{m}
.

The wanted decomposition is achieved in three steps. Recall that
K
is the unit ball of the norm
\|\cdot\|
and
K^{\circ}
its polar body; in particular
\|\cdot\|=\sup_{t\in K^{\circ}}\langle\cdot,t\rangle
, and for every
I\subset\{1,\dots,m\}
,

\left\|\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}\sum_{i\in I}\left\langle X_{i},u\right\rangle Z_{i}\right\|=\sup_{t\in K^{\circ}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}\sum_{i\in I}\left\langle X_{i},u\right\rangle\left\langle Z_{i},t\right\rangle.

Step 1 —
\clubsuit_{u}
:

Removing the large coordinates of
(\left\langle X_{i},u\right\rangle)_{i=1}^{m}
.

Let
I_{u,s}\subset\{1,\dots,m\}
be the set of indices corresponding to the
s
largest coordinates of
(|\left\langle X_{i},u\right\rangle|)_{i=1}^{m}
,
and put

\clubsuit_{u}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}\sum_{i\in I_{u,s}}\left\langle X_{i},u\right\rangle Z_{i}.

By the assumption on
(X_{i})_{i=1}^{m}
, the term
\|(\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}\left\langle X_{i},u\right\rangle)_{i\in I_{u,s}}\|_{2}\leq H_{s,m}
is small.
We will show that as a result,
\|\clubsuit_{u}\|
must be small as well.

Following Step 1, one is left with
\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}\sum_{i\in I_{u,s}^{c}}\left\langle X_{i},u\right\rangle Z_{i}
to deal with. Set

\xi_{u}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}\max_{i\in I_{u,s}^{c}}|\left\langle X_{i},u\right\rangle|,

and note that

\xi_{u}\leq\frac{H_{s,m}}{\sqrt{s}}.

Thus, a bound on
H_{s,m}
leads to a uniform bound on
\xi_{u}
, a fact that will prove to be useful in Step 2 and Step 3.

Step 2 —
\diamondsuit_{u}
:

Truncation of
\|Z_{i}\|
.

Fix
r
to be named in what follows.
For a well-chosen function
\phi(r)
, denote by
J_{u,r}\subset\{1,\dots,m\}
the (random!) set of indices
i
such that
\|\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}\left\langle X_{i},u\right\rangle Z_{i}\|\geq\xi_{u}\phi(r)
.
We will show that with high probability with respect to
Z
, the sets
I_{u,s}^{c}\cap J_{u,r}
consist of at most
r
elements.

Put

\diamondsuit_{u}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}\sum_{i\in I_{u,s}^{c}\cap J_{u,r}}\left\langle X_{i},u\right\rangle Z_{i}.

The choice of
\diamondsuit_{u}
is useful in two important aspects:

(1)

Control in
\ell_{\infty}
:
For every
i\in I_{u,s}^{c}\cap J_{u,r}
, we have
\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}|\left\langle X_{i},u\right\rangle|\leq\xi_{u}
.

(2)

“Short support”:
since
|I_{u,s}^{c}\cap J_{u,r}|\leq r
,
\diamondsuit_{u}
is the sum of a few terms of the form
\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}\left\langle X_{i},u\right\rangle Z_{i}
.

It turns out that
(1)
and
(2)
are enough to ensure that
\|\diamondsuit_{u}\|
is sufficiently small.

Step 3 —
\heartsuit_{u}
:

Concentration.

Finally, one has to show that the norm of

\heartsuit_{u}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}\sum_{i\in I_{u,s}^{c}\cap J_{u,r}^{c}}\left\langle X_{i},u\right\rangle Z_{i}

concentrates sharply around its mean.
Note that here there is sufficient control on

\max_{i\in I_{u,s}^{c}\cap J_{u,r}^{c}}\|\left\langle X_{i},u\right\rangle Z_{i}\|:

thanks to the choice of
I_{u,s}
and
J_{u,r}
, both
|\left\langle X_{i},u\right\rangle|
and
\|Z_{i}\|
are well bounded for
i\in I_{u,s}^{c}\cap J_{u,r}^{c}
.

That will be enough to show that
\|\heartsuit_{u}\|
(as a function of
(Z_{i})_{i=1}^{m}
) concentrates around
\mathbb{E}_{Z}\|\heartsuit_{u}\|
. Moreover, thanks to the estimates in Step 1 and Step 2,
\mathbb{E}_{Z}\|\heartsuit_{u}\|
is close to
\Lambda=\mathbb{E}_{Z}\Psi(u)
.

Step 1 —
\clubsuit_{u}
:

Let
s
be specified in what follows, set
(Z_{i})_{i=1}^{m}\in\Omega_{s}(\mathbb{Z})
and recall that
I_{u,s}
is the set of indices corresponding to the
s
largest coordinates of
(|\left\langle X_{i},u\right\rangle|)_{i=1}^{m}
.
By the Cauchy-Schwartz inequality,

\displaystyle\|\clubsuit_{u}\|=

\displaystyle\sup_{t\in K^{\circ}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}\sum_{i\in I_{u,s}}\left\langle X_{i},u\right\rangle\left\langle Z_{i},t\right\rangle

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left(\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i\in I_{u,s}}\left\langle X_{i},u\right\rangle^{2}\right)^{1/2}\cdot\sup_{t\in K^{\circ}}\left(\sum_{i\in I_{u,s}}\left\langle Z_{i},t\right\rangle^{2}\right)^{1/2}.

Since
|I_{u,s}|=s
, it follows from the choice of
s
and the definitions of
H_{s,m}
and
\Omega_{s}(\mathbb{Z})
that

\|\clubsuit_{u}\|\leq H_{s,m}\cdot C_{0}L\left(\mathbb{E}\|G\|+{\cal R}(K^{\circ})\sqrt{s\log(em/s)}\right).

Moreover, since

d^{*}(K)=\left(\frac{\mathbb{E}\|G\|}{{\cal R}(K^{\circ})}\right)^{2},

it is evident that for suitable constants
c_{1}
and
c_{1}^{\prime}
the following holds:

If

H_{s,m}\leq c_{1}\varepsilon\qquad{\rm and}\qquad s\log(em/s)\leq c_{1}^{\prime}d^{*}(K),

(4.9)

then for
(Z_{i})_{i=1}^{m}\in\Omega_{s}(\mathbb{Z})
,

\sup_{u\in B_{2}^{d}}\|\clubsuit_{u}\|\leq\varepsilon\mathbb{E}\|G\|.

Using (
4.5
) and an identical argument, the conditions in (
4.9
) also imply that

\sup_{u\in B_{2}^{d}}\mathbb{E}_{Z}\|\clubsuit_{u}\|\leq\varepsilon\mathbb{E}\|G\|.

(4.10)

Step 2 —
\diamondsuit_{u}
:

Let
r
be named in what follows and set
(Z_{i})_{i=1}^{m}\in\Omega_{r}(\mathbb{Z})
.
Recall that

\xi_{u}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}\max_{i\in I_{u,s}^{c}}|\left\langle X_{i},u\right\rangle|

and thus
\xi_{u}\leq H_{s,m}/\sqrt{s}
.
Consider the function
\phi
defined in (
4.8
) and set

J_{u,r}=\left\{j\in\{1,\dots,m\}:\left\|\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{m}}\left\langle X_{j},u\right\rangle Z_{j}\right\|\geq\xi_{u}\phi(r)\right\}.

Clearly
|\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}\left\langle X_{i},u\right\rangle|\leq\xi_{u}
for every
i\in I_{u,s}^{c}
; hence,

\displaystyle I_{u,s}^{c}\cap J_{u,r}\subset\left\{j\in\{1,\dots,m\}:\|Z_{j}\|\geq\phi(r)\right\}.

(4.11)

Moreover, by the definition of
\Omega_{r}(\mathbb{Z})
,
|\{j:\|Z_{j}\|\geq\phi(r)\}|\leq r
.
Therefore,

\displaystyle\|\diamondsuit_{u}\|

\displaystyle=\sup_{t\in K^{\circ}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}\sum_{i\in I_{u,s}^{c}\cap J_{u,r}}\left\langle X_{i},u\right\rangle\left\langle Z_{i},t\right\rangle

\displaystyle\leq\xi_{u}\cdot\sup_{t\in K^{\circ}}\max_{|J|=r}\sum_{j\in J}|\left\langle Z_{j},t\right\rangle|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{H_{s,m}}{\sqrt{s}}\cdot\sqrt{r}\sup_{t\in K^{\circ}}\max_{|J|=r}\left(\sum_{j\in J}\left\langle Z_{j},t\right\rangle^{2}\right)^{1/2},

where the last inequality is evident by comparing the
\ell_{1}^{r}
and
\ell_{2}^{r}
norms.
Since
(Z_{i})_{i=1}^{m}\in\Omega_{r}(\mathbb{Z})
,

\|\diamondsuit_{u}\|\leq H_{s,m}\cdot\sqrt{\frac{r}{s}}C_{0}L\left(\mathbb{E}\|G\|+{\cal R}(K^{\circ})\sqrt{r\log\left(em/r\right)}\right),

and in particular, for suitable constants
c_{2}
and
c_{2}^{\prime}
, we have the following:

If

H_{s,m}\sqrt{\frac{r}{s}}\leq c_{2}\varepsilon\ \ {\rm and}\ \ r\log\left(em/r\right)\leq c_{2}^{\prime}d^{*}(K),

(4.12)

then

\sup_{u\in B_{2}^{d}}\|\diamondsuit_{u}\|\leq\varepsilon\mathbb{E}\|G\|.

Next, let us estimate
\mathbb{E}_{Z}\|\diamondsuit_{u}\|
.

Lemma 4.3
.

If (
4.12
) holds, then
\sup_{u\in B_{2}^{d}}\mathbb{E}_{Z}\|\diamondsuit_{u}\|\leq\varepsilon\mathbb{E}\|G\|
.

Proof.

Fix
u\in B_{2}^{d}
and set
Q=|\{i\in I_{u,s}^{c}:\|Z_{i}\|\geq\phi(r)\}|
. For
q\geq 1
, let

S(q)=\left(\sup_{t\in K^{\circ}}\max_{|J|\leq q}\sum_{j\in J}\left\langle Z_{j},t\right\rangle^{2}\right)^{1/2},

with the convention that
\max\emptyset=0
.
Therefore, by (
4.11
),

\displaystyle\|\diamondsuit_{u}\|

\displaystyle\leq\max_{i\in I_{u,s}^{c}}\left|\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}\left\langle X_{i},u\right\rangle\right|\cdot\sup_{t\in K^{\circ}}\sum_{j\in I_{u,s}^{c}\cap J_{u,r}}|\left\langle Z_{j},t\right\rangle|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{H_{s,m}}{\sqrt{s}}\cdot\sqrt{Q}S(Q).

Since
H_{s,m}
is independent of
(Z_{i})_{i=1}^{m}
, all that is left is to show that
\mathbb{E}_{Z}\sqrt{Q}S(Q)\leq c\sqrt{r}\mathbb{E}\|G\|
for a suitable constant
c
.
And indeed, observe that

\displaystyle\sqrt{Q}S(Q)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbbm{1}_{[0,2r]}(Q)\sqrt{2r}S(2r)+\sum_{r<2^{s}<m}\mathbbm{1}_{(2^{s},2^{s+1}]}(Q)\sqrt{2^{s+1}}S(2^{s+1})

\displaystyle=(\ast)+(\ast\ast).

By (
4.5
), for
1\leq q\leq m
,

\left(\mathbb{E}_{Z}S^{2}(q)\right)^{1/2}\leq C_{0}L\left(\mathbb{E}\|G\|+{\cal R}(K^{\circ})\sqrt{q\log\left(em/q\right)}\right),

and for
r
as in (
4.12
) and the choice of
d^{\ast}(K)
,
\mathbb{E}_{Z}(\ast)\leq c_{1}\sqrt{r}\mathbb{E}\|G\|
.

To estimate
\mathbb{E}_{Z}(\ast\ast)
, apply the Cauchy-Schwartz inequality to each term
\mathbb{E}_{Z}\mathbbm{1}_{(2^{s},2^{s+1}]}(Q)S(2^{s+1})
:

\mathbb{E}_{Z}(\ast\ast)\leq\sum_{r<2^{s}<m}\sqrt{\mathbb{P}(Q\geq 2^{s})}\cdot\sqrt{2^{s+1}}C_{0}L\left(\mathbb{E}\|G\|+{\cal R}(K^{\circ})\sqrt{2^{s+1}\log\left(em/2^{s+1}\right)}\right).

Finally, just as in the proof of the third part of Lemma
4.2
,
\mathbb{P}(Q\geq 2^{s})\leq 2\exp(-c_{2}2^{s})
for
2^{s}\geq 2r
; hence, by comparing the sum to a geometric progression,
\mathbb{E}_{Z}(\ast\ast)\leq c_{3}\sqrt{r}\mathbb{E}\|G\|
, as claimed.
∎

Step 3 —
\heartsuit_{u}
:

By definition of
J_{u,r}
,

\displaystyle\heartsuit_{u}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}\sum_{i\in I_{u,s}^{c}\cap J^{c}_{u,r}}\left\langle X_{i},u\right\rangle Z_{i}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}\sum_{i\in I_{u,s}^{c}}\left\langle X_{i},u\right\rangle\mathbbm{1}_{\left\{\left\|\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}\left\langle X_{i},u\right\rangle Z_{i}\right\|\leq\xi_{u}\phi(r)\right\}}Z_{i}.

In particular, if we set

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{u}

\displaystyle=\left\{f_{t}(\cdot)=\left\langle t,\cdot\right\rangle\mathbbm{1}_{\{\|\cdot\|\leq\xi_{u}\phi(r)\}}:t\in K^{\circ}\right\}\text{ and}

\displaystyle Y_{i}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}\left\langle X_{i},u\right\rangle Z_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{n},

then

\|\heartsuit_{u}\|=\sup_{t\in K^{\circ}}\sum_{i\in I_{u,s}^{c}}f_{t}(Y_{i}).

(4.13)

Remark 4.4
.

Note that the sets
I_{u,s}
depend on
(X_{i})_{i=1}^{m}
but not on
(Z_{i})_{i=1}^{m}
.

Let us show that conditionally on
(X_{i})_{i=1}^{m}
, each random variable
\|\heartsuit_{u}\|
concentrates around its mean.
Equation (
4.13
) means that
\|\heartsuit_{u}\|
is a supremum of an empirical process and one may invoke the following version of Talagrand’s concentration inequality for bounded empirical processes due to Klein and Rio
[
13
]
.

Theorem 4.5
.

Let
Y_{1},\dots,Y_{k}
be independent, set
\mathcal{F}
to be a class of functions into
[-a,a]
such that
\mathbb{E}f(Y_{i})=0
for every
i\in\{1,\dots,k\}
and
f\in\mathcal{F}
.
Let
\sigma^{2}=\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\mathbb{E}f^{2}(Y_{i})
and consider

U=\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\sum_{i=1}^{k}f(Y_{i}).

Then for
x>0
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(|U-\mathbb{E}U|\leq x\right)\geq 1-2\exp\left(-\frac{-x^{2}}{2(\sigma^{2}+2a\mathbb{E}U)+3ax}\right).

(4.14)

In the case that interests us,
k=|I_{u,s}^{c}|=m-s
and
\mathcal{F}=\mathcal{F}_{u}
.
We start by verifying the assumptions of Theorem
4.5
for the empirical process from (
4.13
).

Lemma 4.6
.

The set
\mathcal{F}_{u}
satisfies the assumptions in Theorem
4.5
with
a=\frac{H_{s,m}}{\sqrt{s}}\phi(r)
.
Moreover,
\sigma^{2}\leq 4\mathcal{R}^{2}(K^{\circ})
and
\mathbb{E}U\leq cL\mathbb{E}\|G\|
for an absolute constant
c
.

Proof.

First note that
\mathbb{E}f(Y_{i})=0
for every
f=f_{t}\in\mathcal{F}_{u}
and
1\leq i\leq k
.
Indeed, since
\|t\|=\|-t\|
and
Y_{i}
is symmetric,

\mathbb{E}f_{t}(Y_{i})=\mathbb{E}_{Z}\left\langle t,Y_{i}\right\rangle\mathbbm{1}_{\{\|Y_{i}\|\leq\xi_{u}\phi(r)\}}=0.

Turning to the estimate on
a
, let
f=f_{t}
for
t\in K^{\circ}
.
Then for every
y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,

\displaystyle|f_{t}(y)|

\displaystyle=|\left\langle t,y\right\rangle|\ \mathbbm{1}_{\{\|y\|\leq\xi_{u}\phi(r)\}}

\displaystyle\leq\|y\|\mathbbm{1}_{\{\|y\|\leq\xi_{u}\phi(r)\}}\leq\xi_{u}\phi(r)\leq\phi(r)\frac{H_{s,m}}{\sqrt{s}}

by the (uniform) upper estimate on
\xi_{u}
.

As for
\sigma^{2}
, recall that
\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left\langle X_{i},u\right\rangle^{2}\leq 2
and that
Z
is isotropic.
Hence

\displaystyle\sigma^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{t\in K^{\circ}}\sum_{i\in I_{u,s}^{c}}\frac{1}{m}\left\langle X_{i},u\right\rangle^{2}\mathbb{E}\left\langle Z_{i},t\right\rangle^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 2\sup_{t\in K^{\circ}}\|t\|_{2}^{2}=2{\cal R}^{2}(K^{\circ}).

Finally, since
(Z_{i})_{i=1}^{m}
and
(\varepsilon_{i}Z_{i})_{i=1}^{m}
have the same distribution by the symmetry of
Z
, a standard Bernoulli contraction argument (see, e.g.,
[
14
, Theorem 4.4]
) applied conditionally on
(X_{i})_{i=1}^{m}
and
(Z_{i})_{i=1}^{m}
shows that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}U

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{Z}\mathbb{E}_{\varepsilon}\sup_{t\in K^{\circ}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}\sum_{i\in I_{u,s}^{c}}\left\langle X_{i},u\right\rangle\mathbbm{1}_{\{\|Y_{i}\|\leq\xi_{u}\phi(r)\}}\varepsilon_{i}\left\langle Z_{i},t\right\rangle

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}_{Z}\left\|\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}\sum_{i\in I_{u,s}^{c}}\left\langle X_{i},u\right\rangle Z_{i}\right\|=(\ast).

Since
(Z_{i})_{i=1}^{m}
are independent and
L
-subgaussian, gaussian domination and the assumption that
\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left\langle X_{i},u\right\rangle^{2}\leq 2
imply that
(\ast)\leq cL\mathbb{E}\|G\|
for an absolute constant
c
.
∎

The next step is to control the denominator in the probability estimate in (
4.14
).
Consider
0<x<\mathbb{E}\|G\|
, and note that by Lemma
4.6
,

2(\sigma^{2}+2a\mathbb{E}U)+3ax\leq c_{3}\left({\cal R}^{2}(K^{\circ})+\frac{H_{s,m}}{\sqrt{s}}\phi(r)\mathbb{E}\|G\|\right).

It follows from the definition of
\phi
that if

m\leq c_{4}r\exp(d^{*}(K))

then
\phi(r)\leq c_{5}\mathbb{E}\|G\|
, and therefore

2(\sigma^{2}+2a\mathbb{E}U)+3ax\leq c_{6}\left({\cal R}^{2}(K^{\circ})+\frac{H_{s,m}}{\sqrt{s}}(\mathbb{E}\|G\|)^{2}\right).

Setting
x=\varepsilon\mathbb{E}\|G\|
and since
U=\|\heartsuit_{u}\|
(see (
4.13
)), it follows from Theorem
4.5
that for suitable constants
c_{7}
,
c_{7}^{\prime}
and
c_{7}^{\prime\prime}
we have that:

If

\frac{H_{s,m}}{\sqrt{s}}\leq\frac{c_{7}}{d^{\ast}(K)}\quad\text{and}\quad m\leq c_{7}^{\prime}r\exp(d^{*}(K)),

(4.15)

then for every
u\in B_{2}^{d}
, with
\mathbb{P}_{Z}
-probability at least
1-2\exp(-c_{7}^{\prime\prime}\varepsilon^{2}d^{*}(K))
,

\left|\|\heartsuit_{u}\|-\mathbb{E}_{Z}\|\heartsuit_{u}\|\right|\leq\varepsilon\mathbb{E}\|G\|.

Remark 4.7
.

It is worth stressing that in contrast to Step 1 and Step 2, the probability estimate here does not hold uniformly for
u\in B_{2}^{d}
, but only for a single (arbitrary)
u
.

Proof of Theorem
4.1

First, let us show that for well-chosen
s,r,m
, and
d
, all the requirements collected in (
4.9
), (
4.12
), and (
4.15
) are indeed satisfied.
Let
c_{1},\dots,c_{4}
be suitable constants that turn out to depend only on
L
,
C_{0}
, and
C_{1}
.
Set

s=c_{1}\frac{d^{*}(K)}{\log\left(em/d^{*}(K)\right)}\ \ \ {\rm and}\ \ \ r=c_{2}\min\{\varepsilon^{2}d^{*}(K),s\},

and let
m\in[c_{3}d^{*}(K),c_{4}\exp(d^{\ast}(K))]
.
Then (
4.9
) and (
4.12
) are satisfied for suitable choices of
c_{1},c_{2}
.
Moreover, as noted previously, one may assume without loss of generality that
\varepsilon^{2}d^{\ast}(K)\geq 1
—otherwise the claim in Theorem
4.1
is trivially true; thus,
m
satisfies (
4.15
) for suitable choice of
c_{4}
.

Next, collecting the requirements on
H_{s,m}
from (
4.9
), (
4.12
), and (
4.15
), one has to verify that

\displaystyle H_{s,m}\leq c_{5}\min\left\{\varepsilon,\varepsilon\sqrt{\frac{s}{r}},\frac{\sqrt{s}}{d^{\ast}(K)}\right\}.

(4.16)

To that end, recall that
r\leq c_{2}s
and that
\varepsilon\geq 1/\sqrt{d^{\ast}(K)}
; in particular, the third term in (
4.16
) is the dominant one, and (
4.16
) is equivalent to having
H_{s,m}\leq c_{6}\sqrt{s}/d^{\ast}(K)
, which was assumed to hold.

Therefore, if

(Z_{i})_{i=1}^{m}\in\Omega_{s}(\mathbb{Z})\cap\Omega_{r}(\mathbb{Z}),

then

\max\left\{\|\clubsuit_{u}\|,\|\diamondsuit_{u}\|,\mathbb{E}_{Z}\|\clubsuit_{u}\|,\mathbb{E}_{Z}\|\diamondsuit_{u}\|\right\}\leq\varepsilon\mathbb{E}\|G\|.

To estimate the probability of the event
\Omega_{s}(\mathbb{Z})\cap\Omega_{r}(\mathbb{Z})
, note that
s\log(em/s)\geq c_{7}d^{\ast}(K)
by the choice of
s
and since
m\geq c_{3}d^{\ast}(K)
.
In particular, as
\varepsilon\leq 1
, we have that
s\log(em/s)\geq c_{7}\varepsilon^{2}d^{\ast}(K)
.
Repeating the argument for
r
, it is evident from Lemma
4.2
that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{Z}\left(\Omega_{r}(\mathbb{Z})\cap\Omega_{s}(\mathbb{Z})\right)

\displaystyle\geq 1-2\exp\left(-C_{2}s\log\left(\frac{em}{s}\right)\right)-2\exp\left(-C_{2}r\log\left(\frac{em}{r}\right)\right)

\displaystyle\geq 1-2\exp(-C_{3}\varepsilon^{2}d^{\ast}(K))

for constants
C_{2}
and
C_{3}
that depend on
c_{7}
and on the absolute constants
C_{0}^{\prime}
and
C_{1}^{\prime}
from Lemma
4.2
.

Finally, one has to show that
\|\heartsuit_{u}\|
concentrates around its mean, and that the required concentration holds uniformly in
u\in W
.
As was noted previously, for any
u\in W
, with
\mathbb{P}_{Z}
-probability at least
1-2\exp(-c_{8}\varepsilon^{2}d^{*}(K))

\left|\ \|\heartsuit_{u}\|-\mathbb{E}_{Z}\|\heartsuit_{u}\|\ \right|\leq\varepsilon\mathbb{E}\|G\|,

and
\log|W|\leq d\log(5/\varepsilon)
.
If

d\leq c_{9}\frac{\varepsilon^{2}d^{*}(K)}{\log(5/\varepsilon)}

for a suitable constant
c_{9}
, then by the union bound, with
\mathbb{P}_{Z}
-probability at least
1-2\exp(-c_{10}\varepsilon^{2}d^{*}(K))
,

\max_{u\in W}\left|\ \|\heartsuit_{u}\|-\mathbb{E}_{Z}\|\heartsuit_{u}\|\ \right|\leq\varepsilon\mathbb{E}\|G\|.

(4.17)

Taking the intersection of
\Omega_{r}(\mathbb{Z})\cap\Omega_{s}(\mathbb{Z})
and the high-probability event on which (
4.17
) holds completes the proof.
∎

5

A lower bound on the conditional expectation—proof of (
2.7
)

Let us turn to verifying (
2.7
) from Lemma
2.1
.
Once again, recall that
Z
is a symmetric, isotropic,
L
-subgaussian random vector in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
that has iid coordinates.

Theorem 5.1
.

Let
\delta,\eta>0
.
There are constants
c_{1}
and
c_{2}
that depend on
\delta
and
\eta
such that the following holds.
Let
m\geq c_{1}\log n
and fix
v\in S^{d-1}
.
If

\displaystyle|\{i\in\{1,\dots,m\}:|\left\langle X_{i},v\right\rangle|\geq\eta\}|\geq\delta m,

(5.1)

then

\mathbb{E}_{Z}\Psi(v)\geq c_{2}\mathbb{E}\|G\|.

Remark 5.2
.

A version of Theorem
5.1
was established in
[
15
]
under different conditions (most notably that
m\geq cn
instead of
m\geq c\log n
and without the assumption that the coordinates of
Z
are iid).
The idea was to find a lower bound on the Bernoulli processes

\mathbb{E}_{\varepsilon}\sup_{t\in K^{\circ}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\varepsilon_{i}\left\langle X_{i},v\right\rangle\left\langle Z_{i},t\right\rangle,

conditioned on a typical realization of
(X_{i})_{i=1}^{m}
and
(Z_{i})_{i=1}^{m}
, and the proof relied on the construction of an approximation of
K^{\circ}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
consisting of
\exp(cm)
points.
Unfortunately, in the worst case it forced
m
to be proportional to
n
.
Since the aim here is for
m
to be as small as possible—specifically a low-degree polynomial function of the critical dimension
d^{\ast}(K)
—a different argument is called for.

Note that the condition
m\geq c_{1}\log n
in Theorem
5.1
is satisfied in the setting of Theorem
1.5
.
Indeed,
m
is proportional to
(d^{\ast}(K))^{\alpha}
for
\alpha\geq 4
(see (
2.2
)),
d^{\ast}(K)\geq C_{0}\log n
for an absolute constant
C_{0}
, and
n\geq C(L,\alpha)
for a constant
C
that we are free to choose as large as needed.

We will verify in Lemma
5.3
that there are constants
\delta,\eta>0
depending only on
L
such that (
5.1
) is satisfied.

Finally, by gaussian domination, if
\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left\langle X_{i},v\right\rangle^{2}\leq 2
then
\mathbb{E}_{Z}\Psi(v)\leq C_{2}L\mathbb{E}\|G\|
for an absolute constant
C_{2}
.
Thus, in the setting of Theorem
1.5
,

\mathbb{E}_{Z}\Psi(v)\in[C_{3}\mathbb{E}\|G\|,C_{4}\mathbb{E}\|G\|]

for constants
C_{3}
and
C_{4}
that depend only on
L
.

Recall that for
1\leq i\leq m
,
\lambda_{i}^{v}=m\int_{(\frac{i-1}{m},\frac{i}{m}]}F_{\left\langle X,v\right\rangle}^{-1}(p)\,dp
.

Lemma 5.3
.

Let
X
satisfy
L_{4}-L_{2}
norm equivalence with constant
L
.
There are constants
\delta,\eta>0
that depend only on
L
such that the following holds.
If
m\geq 2/\delta
and

\displaystyle\left(\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left|\left\langle X_{i},v\right\rangle^{\sharp}-\lambda_{i}^{v}\right|^{2}\right)^{1/2}\leq\eta\sqrt{\delta},

(5.2)

then

|\{i\in\{1,\dots,m\}:|\left\langle X_{i},v\right\rangle|\geq\eta\}|\geq\delta m.

Note that (
5.2
) holds for
(X_{i})_{i=1}^{m}\in\Omega(\mathbb{X})
.
Indeed, by (
2.3
), the left hand side in (
5.2
) is smaller than
2\varepsilon
, and we may assume that
\varepsilon\leq C
for an arbitrarily small constant
C=C(L)
, e.g., for
C=\eta\sqrt{\delta}
.

Proof.

By the
L_{4}-L_{2}
norm equivalence and the Paley-Zygmund inequality (see, e.g.,
[
7
, Corollary 3.3.2]
), there are constants
\eta
and
\delta
that depend only on
L
such that

\mu^{v}\left(\left(-2\eta,2\eta\right)^{c}\right)=\mathbb{P}\left(|\left\langle X,v\right\rangle|\geq 2\eta\right)\geq 4\delta.

In particular
\mathbb{P}(\left\langle X,v\right\rangle\geq 2\eta)\geq 2\delta
or
\mathbb{P}(\left\langle X,v\right\rangle\leq-2\eta)\geq 2\delta
, and we focus without loss of generality on the latter case.
Then clearly

\lambda^{v}_{i}\leq-2\eta\quad\text{for }i=1,\dots,2\delta m.

It is straightforward to verify that if
\left\langle X_{\delta m},v\right\rangle^{\sharp}>-\eta
, then (
5.2
) cannot be true.
In particular, (
5.2
) implies that
|\{i:\left\langle X_{i},v\right\rangle\leq-\eta\}|\geq\delta m
and (
5.1
) holds.
∎

Proof of Theorem
5.1

The proof of Theorem
5.1
is based on two facts.
To formulate them, let
(x^{\ast}_{i})_{i=1}^{m}
be the non-increasing rearrangement of
(|x_{i}|)_{i=1}^{m}
.

Lemma 5.4
.

Let
w
be a symmetric random variable, set
(w_{i})_{i=1}^{n}
to be independent copies of
w
and put
W=(w_{i})_{i=1}^{n}
.
Let
\varepsilon=(\varepsilon_{i})_{i=1}^{n}
be the Bernoulli vector and set
\pi
to be the uniform distribution on the permutations of
\{1,\dots,n\}
.
If
W
,
\varepsilon
and
\pi
are independent, then

\mathbb{E}_{W}\sup_{t\in K^{\circ}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}t_{i}=\mathbb{E}_{W,\pi,\varepsilon}\sup_{t\in K^{\circ}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}w^{\ast}_{i}\varepsilon_{\pi(i)}t_{\pi(i)}.

Proof.

The key is the (rather obvious) observation that
(w_{1},\dots,w_{n})
has the same distribution as
(\varepsilon_{1}w^{\ast}_{\pi(1)},\dots,\varepsilon_{n}w^{\ast}_{\pi(n)})
.
Therefore,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{W}\sup_{t\in K^{\circ}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}t_{i}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{W,\pi,\varepsilon}\sup_{t\in K^{\circ}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\varepsilon_{i}w^{\ast}_{\pi(i)}t_{i}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{W,\pi,\varepsilon}\sup_{t\in K^{\circ}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}w^{\ast}_{i}\varepsilon_{\pi(i)}t_{\pi(i)},

where the last equality holds because
\pi^{-1}
and
\pi
have the same distribution.
∎

Lemma 5.5
.

Let
\delta>0
.
There are constants
c_{1},\dots,c_{4}
that depend only on
\delta
and
L
such that the following holds.
Let
m\geq c_{1}\log n
, consider
I\subset\{1,\dots,m\}
,
|I|\geq\delta m
and set for
j=1,\dots,n
,

\displaystyle Y_{j}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}\sum_{i\in I}\left\langle Z_{i},e_{j}\right\rangle.

(5.3)

Then for any
1\leq k\leq n
,
\mathbb{E}|Y_{k}|\geq c_{2}
.
Also, for any
1\leq k\leq c_{3}n
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}Y^{\ast}_{k}\geq c_{4}\sqrt{\log\left(\frac{n}{k}\right)}.

(5.4)

The proof of Lemma
5.5
relies on the an estimate due to Montgomery-Smith
[
20
]
.
To formulate it, denote by
\lfloor u\rfloor
the largest integer that is smaller than
u
.

Lemma 5.6
.

There are absolute constants
c_{1},c_{2}
and
c_{3}
such that the following holds.
Let
\varepsilon=(\varepsilon_{i})_{i=1}^{m}
be the Bernoulli vector and set
0<u\leq m-2
.
Then, for every
x\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
, with probability at least
c_{1}\exp(-c_{2}u)
,

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{m}x_{i}\varepsilon_{i}\geq c_{3}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{\lfloor u\rfloor}x^{\ast}_{i}+\sqrt{u}\left(\sum_{i=\lfloor u\rfloor+1}^{m}(x^{\ast}_{i})^{2}\right)^{1/2}\right).

(5.5)

Proof of Lemma
5.5
.

We only present a proof of (
5.4
).
The proof that
\min_{1\leq k\leq n}\mathbb{E}|Y_{k}|\geq c
follows a similar (but simpler) path.

Since
Z
has iid coordinates, the random variables
(Y_{j})_{j=1}^{n}
are independent and each
Y_{j}
has the same distribution as
Y=Y_{1}
.
It is straightforward to verify that if
u
satisfies that
\mathbb{P}(|Y|\geq u)\geq c_{1}k/n
, then
\mathbb{P}(Y^{\ast}_{k}\geq u)\geq 1/2
as long as
c_{1}
is a well-chosen absolute constant; in particular,
\mathbb{E}Y^{\ast}_{k}\geq u/2
.
The rest of the proof is devoted to establishing that
u
of the order
\sqrt{\log\frac{n}{k}}
is a valid choice.

Since
Z
is
L
-subgaussian and isotropic, by the Paley–Zygmund inequality, there are constants
c_{2}
and
c_{3}
depending only on
L
such that
\mathbb{P}(|\left\langle Z,e_{1}\right\rangle|\geq c_{2})\geq 2c_{3}
.
Let
\varepsilon=(\varepsilon_{i})_{i=1}^{m}
be the Bernoulli vector, independent of
(Z_{i})_{i=1}^{m}
.
Setting

I^{\prime}=\{i\in I:|\left\langle Z_{i},e_{1}\right\rangle|\geq c_{2}\},

then clearly
\mathbb{P}\left(|I^{\prime}|\geq c_{3}|I|\right)\geq 1/2
.
By the contraction inequality for Bernoulli processes and the symmetry of
Z
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(|Y|\geq u)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{Z}\mathbb{P}_{\varepsilon}\left(\left|\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}\sum_{i\in I}\varepsilon_{i}\left\langle Z_{i},e_{1}\right\rangle\right|\geq u\right)

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{E}_{Z}\mathbbm{1}_{\{|I^{\prime}|\geq c_{3}|I|\}}\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{P}_{\varepsilon}\left(\left|\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}\sum_{i\in I^{\prime}}c_{2}\varepsilon_{i}\right|\geq u\right).

Fix a realization
(Z_{i})_{i=1}^{n}
satisfying
|I^{\prime}|\geq c_{3}|I|
.
It suffices to show that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\varepsilon}\left(\left|\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}\sum_{i\in I^{\prime}}c_{2}\varepsilon_{i}\right|\geq c_{4}\sqrt{\log\left(\frac{n}{k}\right)}\right)\geq\frac{4c_{1}k}{n}

(5.6)

for a suitable constant
c_{4}
.
To that end, by Lemma
5.6
there are absolute constants
c_{5},c_{6},c_{7}
such that for
0\leq u^{2}\leq|I^{\prime}|-2
, with probability at least
c_{5}\exp(-c_{6}u^{2})
,

\displaystyle\left|\sum_{i\in I^{\prime}}\frac{c_{2}}{\sqrt{m}}\varepsilon_{i}\right|\geq c_{7}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{\lfloor u^{2}\rfloor}\frac{c_{2}}{\sqrt{m}}+u\left(\sum_{i=\lfloor u^{2}\rfloor+1}^{|I^{\prime}|}\frac{c_{2}^{2}}{m}\right)^{1/2}\right)=A(u).

Let
u=c_{8}\sqrt{\log\frac{n}{k}}
for a (small) constant
c_{8}=c_{8}(\delta)
and observe that for that choice of
u
,
A(u)\geq c_{9}(\delta)u
.
Indeed, as was assumed,

|I^{\prime}|\geq c_{3}|I|\geq c_{3}\delta m\geq c_{3}\delta\log n;

in particular
|I^{\prime}|-(\lfloor u^{2}\rfloor+1)\geq\frac{c_{3}\delta m}{2}
and thus
A(u)\geq c_{7}u(\sum_{i=\lfloor u^{2}\rfloor+1}^{|I^{\prime}|}\frac{c_{2}^{2}}{m})^{1/2}\geq c_{9}(\delta)u
.
Therefore

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\varepsilon}\left(\left|\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}\sum_{i\in I^{\prime}}c_{2}\varepsilon_{i}\right|\geq c_{9}\cdot u\right)

\displaystyle\geq c_{5}\exp\left(-c_{6}c_{8}^{2}\log\left(\frac{n}{k}\right)\right),

(5.7)

and if
c_{8}
is sufficiently small and
k\leq c_{10}n
, then the right hand side in (
5.7
) is at least
c_{1}\frac{k}{n}
.
This completes the proof.
∎

Proof of Theorem
5.1
.

Set

I=\{i\in\{1,\dots,m\}:|\left\langle X_{i},v\right\rangle|\geq\eta\}

and thus
|I|\geq\delta m
.
Let
\varepsilon=(\varepsilon_{i})_{i=1}^{m}
be the Bernoulli vector, independent of
(Z_{i})_{i=1}^{m}
.
By the symmetry of
Z
and the contraction principle for Bernoulli processes,

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathbb{E}_{Z}\sup_{t\in K^{\circ}}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}\left\langle X_{i},v\right\rangle\left\langle Z_{i},t\right\rangle&=\mathbb{E}_{Z}\mathbb{E}_{\varepsilon}\sup_{t\in K^{\circ}}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\varepsilon_{i}\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}\left\langle X_{i},v\right\rangle\left\langle Z_{i},t\right\rangle\\
&\geq\mathbb{E}_{Z}\mathbb{E}_{\varepsilon}\sup_{t\in K^{\circ}}\sum_{i\in I}\varepsilon_{i}\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}\eta\left\langle Z_{i},t\right\rangle.\end{split}

(5.8)

Also, for every
t\in K^{\circ}
, using the symmetry of
Z
once again,

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in I}\varepsilon_{i}\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}\left\langle Z_{i},t\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\in I}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}\varepsilon_{i}\left\langle Z_{i},e_{j}\right\rangle t_{j}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left(\sum_{i\in I}\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}\varepsilon_{i}\left\langle Z_{i},e_{j}\right\rangle\right)t_{j}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}Y_{j}t_{j}.

In particular, it follows from (
5.8
) that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{Z}\sup_{t\in K^{\circ}}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left\langle X_{i},v\right\rangle\left\langle Z_{i},t\right\rangle\geq\eta\mathbb{E}_{Z}\sup_{t\in K^{\circ}}\sum_{j=1}^{n}Y_{j}t_{j}.

(5.9)

To complete the proof one has to “replace”
(Y_{j})_{j=1}^{m}
by independent gaussian random variables
(g_{j})_{j=1}^{m}
. To that end, observe that by Lemma
5.5
there is a constant
c_{1}=c_{1}(L,\delta)
such that for every
1\leq k\leq n
,
\mathbb{E}|Y_{k}|\geq c_{1}
.
And, by Lemma
5.4
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{G}\sup_{t\in K^{\circ}}\sum_{j=1}^{m}g_{j}t_{j}=\mathbb{E}_{G,\pi,\varepsilon}\sup_{t\in K^{\circ}}\sum_{j=1}^{n}g_{j}^{\ast}\varepsilon_{\pi(j)}t_{\pi(j)}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{G}\mathbb{E}_{\varepsilon,\pi}\sup_{t\in K^{\circ}}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left(\frac{g_{j}^{\ast}}{\mathbb{E}_{Y}Y^{\ast}_{j}+c_{1}}\right)(\mathbb{E}_{Y}Y^{\ast}_{j}+c_{1})\varepsilon_{\pi(j)}t_{\pi(j)}

\displaystyle\leq\left(\mathbb{E}_{G}\max_{1\leq j\leq n}\frac{g_{j}^{\ast}}{\mathbb{E}_{Y}Y^{\ast}_{j}+c_{1}}\right)\cdot\left(\mathbb{E}_{\varepsilon,\pi}\sup_{t\in K^{\circ}}\sum_{j=1}^{n}(\mathbb{E}_{Y}Y^{\ast}_{j}+c_{1})\varepsilon_{\pi(j)}t_{\pi(j)}\right)=(\ast)\cdot(\ast\ast),

where the last inequality follows from the contraction principle for Bernoulli processes (applied conditionally with respect to
G
).

All that remains is to show that
(\ast)\leq c(c_{1},L,\delta)
and that
(\ast\ast)\leq 2\mathbb{E}_{Y}\sup_{t\in K^{\circ}}\sum_{j=1}^{n}Y_{j}t_{j}
.

To estimate the first term in
(\ast\ast)
, note that by Jensen’s inequality followed by Lemma
5.4
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\varepsilon,\pi}\sup_{t\in K^{\circ}}\sum_{j=1}^{n}(\mathbb{E}_{Y}Y^{\ast}_{j})\varepsilon_{\pi(j)}t_{\pi(j)}

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}_{\varepsilon,\pi,Y}\sup_{t\in K^{\circ}}\sum_{j=1}^{n}Y^{\ast}_{j}\varepsilon_{\pi(j)}t_{\pi(j)}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{Y}\sup_{t\in K^{\circ}}\sum_{j=1}^{n}Y_{j}t_{j}.

As for the second term in
(\ast\ast)
, invoking Lemma
5.5
,
\mathbb{E}_{Y}|Y_{j}|\geq c_{1}
for
1\leq j\leq n
.
Hence, by contraction

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\varepsilon,\pi}\sup_{t\in K^{\circ}}\sum_{j=1}^{n}c_{1}\varepsilon_{\pi(j)}t_{\pi(j)}

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}_{\varepsilon,\pi}\sup_{t\in K^{\circ}}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}_{Y}|Y_{\pi(j)}|\varepsilon_{\pi(j)}t_{\pi(j)}

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}_{Y}\sup_{t\in K^{\circ}}\sum_{j=1}^{n}Y_{j}t_{j},

as required.

To control
(\ast)
, a standard binomial estimate shows that there are absolute constants
c_{2}
and
c_{3}
such that for
u\geq c_{3}
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(g^{\ast}_{j}\geq u\sqrt{\log(en/j)}\right)\leq 2\exp\left(-c_{4}u^{2}j\log(en/j)\right).

(5.10)

By Lemma
5.5
there is
c_{5}=c_{5}(L,\delta)
such that
\mathbb{E}_{Y}Y^{\ast}_{j}+c_{1}\geq c_{5}\sqrt{\log(en/j)}
for every
1\leq j\leq n
.
Thus, (
5.10
) and the union bound imply that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{G}\left(\max_{1\leq j\leq n}\frac{g_{j}^{\ast}}{\mathbb{E}_{Y}Y^{\ast}_{j}+c_{1}}\geq\frac{u}{c_{5}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j=1}^{n}2\exp\left(-c_{4}u^{2}j\log\left(\frac{en}{j}\right)\right)

\displaystyle\leq 2\exp\left(-c_{6}u^{2}\right),

and the claim follows from tail-integration.
∎
