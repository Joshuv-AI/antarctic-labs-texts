Abstract
. We propose and analyze a general goal-oriented adaptive strategy for approximating quantities of interest (QoIs) associated with solutions to linear elliptic partial differential equations with random inputs. The QoIs are represented by bounded linear or continuously Gâteaux differentiable nonlinear goal functionals, and the approximations are computed using the sparse grid stochastic collocation finite element method (SC-FEM). The proposed adaptive strategy relies on novel reliable a posteriori estimates of the errors in approximating QoIs. One of the key features of our error estimation approach is the introduction of a correction term into the approximation of QoIs in order to compensate for the lack of (global) Galerkin orthogonality in the SC-FEM setting. Computational results generated using the proposed adaptive algorithm are presented in the paper for representative elliptic problems with affine and nonaffine parametric coefficient dependence and for a range of linear and nonlinear goal functionals.

1.
Introduction

Partial differential equations (PDEs) with uncertain inputs are ubiquitous in the mathematical modelling of real-life phenomena and in predictive numerical simulations.
Practical simulation scenarios often target certain quantities of interest (QoIs)—specific (and usually localized) features of the solution to a PDE,
e.g., pointwise values of the solution or its fluxes across parts of the computational domain boundary.
In these scenarios, QoIs are represented by functionals of the solution to the underlying PDE with parametric uncertainty.

High-fidelity numerical solutions to parametric PDEs are infeasible to compute in many realistic applications.
Conversely, so-called
surrogate approximations
that are functions of the (stochastic) parameters are not only effective
in approximating the input-output map but can also be used to estimate a wide range of QoIs.
However, surrogate approximations introduce numerical errors that need to be accounted for when estimating QoIs.
In this work we address the reliable control of errors in computing QoIs from surrogate approximations of solutions to parameteric PDEs
and systematic approaches to reduce those errors via adaptive algorithms.
In this context, the aspects of so-called goal-oriented a posteriori error estimation and error control were studied
in
[
12
]
for generic surrogate approximations of solutions to parametric PDEs,
in
[
1
]
for stochastic collocation approximations, and
in
[
31
,
13
,
7
,
8
]

for surrogate approximations obtained via stochastic Galerkin finite element methods.

Goal-oriented adaptivity has also been studied in the context of Monte Carlo methods (see, for example
[
38
,
4
]
), although this class of methods typically only reveals information related to statistical moments of the solution to a given parametric PDE problem.
By contrast, methods which produce surrogate approximations yield explicit information about the solution’s dependence on individual random parameters, and furthermore, provide the opportunity to evaluate a larger class of QoIs.

In this paper, we focus on one particular type of
surrogate approximations—sparse
grid stochastic collocation finite element approximations
that combine sparse grid interpolation in the parameter domain with finite element discretization in the physical (spatial) domain.
In
[
1
]
, the authors design a goal-oriented adaptive algorithm for the stochastic collocation finite element method (SC-FEM) to approximate
linear
QoIs
derived from the solution to the advection-diffusion problem with uncertain diffusion coefficient, velocity field,
and source term
.
In that work, the errors in the corresponding linear functionals are
approximated
by sampling the finite element error estimates at collocation points,
and the associated error indicators are used to guide the adaptive algorithm.
In the present paper, we aim
to approximate
QoIs represented by
linear and nonlinear
goal functionals
by applying the SC-FEM to the underlying model PDE problem with uncertain inputs.
We build upon the a posteriori error analysis in
[
11
,
9
]

and use the duality technique in the spirit of
[
34
,
25
]
to derive,
for the first time to the best of our knowledge,
reliable a posteriori estimates
for the error in approximating QoIs within the SC-FEM framework.
The obtained error estimates are written in terms of spatial and parametric error indicators (for the primal and dual solutions)
that guide refinements of finite element approximations and sparse grids in the proposed goal-oriented adaptive algorithm.

There are two distinctive features in our goal-oriented error estimation approach.
Firstly, we introduce a ‘correction term’ into the approximation of the QoI whose
role is to compensate for the lack of (global) Galerkin orthogonality in the SC-FEM setting.
Our numerical experiments show that the correction term has a stabilizing effect on the decay of errors in the goal functional,
so that these errors decay with approximately the same rate as (computable) goal-oriented error estimates.
Secondly, we develop a systematic approach to the error estimation for a large class of goal functionals,
including bounded linear and continuously Gâteaux differentiable nonlinear functionals.

The paper is organized as follows.
In section
2
, we introduce the model problem and recast it in a weak form using
samplewise and combined spatial-parametric formulations.
Section
3
recalls the main ideas of the sparse grid stochastic collocation,
its combination with finite element approximations, as well as the a posteriori error estimation strategy for SC-FEM as developed in
[
11
]
.
In section
4
, we consider a special case of bounded linear goal functionals,
for which we develop a goal-oriented error estimation strategy and design the associated adaptive algorithm.
Then, in section
5
, we show how this error estimation strategy extends to a larger class of (possibly nonlinear) goal functionals
and propose the corresponding modifications to the previously designed adaptive algorithm.
Computational results generated using the proposed adaptive procedure
for representative elliptic problems with affine and nonaffine parametric coefficient dependence and for a range
of linear and nonlinear goal functionals are discussed in section
6
.

2.
Problem formulation

Let
D\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
be a bounded Lipschitz domain with polygonal boundary
\partial D
.
Let
\Gamma:=\Gamma_{1}\times\Gamma_{2}\ldots\times\Gamma_{M}
denote the parameter domain in
\mathbb{R}^{M}
,
where
M\in\mathbb{N}
and each
\Gamma_{m}
(
m=1,\ldots,M
) is a bounded interval in
\mathbb{R}
.
For simplicity of the presentation, and without loss of generality,
we assume that
\Gamma_{1}=\Gamma_{2}=\ldots=\Gamma_{M}=[-1,1]
.
We introduce a probability measure
\pi(\bm{y}):=\prod_{m=1}^{M}\pi_{m}(y_{m})
on
(\Gamma,\mathcal{B}(\Gamma))
;
here,
\pi_{m}
denotes a Borel probability measure on
\Gamma_{m}
(
m=1,\ldots,M
) and

\mathcal{B}(\Gamma)
is the Borel
\sigma
-algebra on
\Gamma
.

We consider the following parametric model problem:
find
u:\overline{D}\times\Gamma\to\mathbb{R}
satisfying

(1)

\displaystyle-\nabla\cdot(a(x,\bm{y})\nabla u(x,\bm{y}))

\displaystyle=f(x),\quad

\displaystyle x\in D,

\displaystyle u(x,\bm{y})

\displaystyle=0,\quad

\displaystyle x\in\partial D

\pi
-almost everywhere on
\Gamma
(i.e., almost surely).
Here, the function
f\in H^{-1}(D)
represents a deterministic forcing term,
and the coefficient
a(x,\bm{y})
is a random field on
(\Gamma,\mathcal{B}(\Gamma),\pi)
over
L^{\infty}(D)

that is bounded away from zero and infinity, i.e.,
there exist constants
a_{\min},\,a_{\max}
such that

(2)

0<a_{\min}\leqslant\underset{x\in D}{\text{ess inf }}a(x,\bm{y})\leqslant\underset{x\in D}{\text{ess sup }}a(x,\bm{y})\leqslant a_{\max}<\infty\quad\pi\text{-a.e. on }\Gamma.

The parametric problem (
1
) can be understood in a weak sense
by viewing the solution as a map
u:\Gamma\to H_{0}^{1}(D)=:\mathbb{X}
.
For
\pi
-almost all
\bar{\bm{y}}\in\Gamma
,
this yields the following
samplewise
weak formulation: find
u_{\bar{\bm{y}}}(x):=u(x,\bar{\bm{y}})\in\mathbb{X}
such that

(3)

B_{\bar{\bm{y}}}(u_{\bar{\bm{y}}},v)=F(v)\quad\forall\,v\in\mathbb{X},

where

(4)

\displaystyle B_{\bar{\bm{y}}}(u,v):=\int_{D}a(x,\bar{\bm{y}})\nabla u(x)\cdot\nabla v(x)\mathrm{d}x,\quad F(v):=\int_{D}f(x)v(x)\mathrm{d}x.

The assumptions on
a
and
f
guarantee the well-posedness of (
3
) by the Lax–Milgram lemma,
and the parametric problem (
1
)
admits a unique weak solution
u
in the Bochner space
L_{\pi}^{p}(\Gamma;\mathbb{X})
for any
p\in[1,\infty]
;
see
[
2
, Lemma 1.1]
for details.
The variational formulations as in (
3
) are utilized in the context of sampling methods
(e.g., Monte Carlo or stochastic collocation) where they are discretized using, e.g., the finite element method.
On the other hand, one can exploit the fact that
u:\Gamma\to\mathbb{X}
is an element
of the Bochner space
L_{\pi}^{2}(\Gamma;\mathbb{X})
yielding the following
combined spatial-parametric
weak formulation:
find
u\in\mathscr{V}:=L_{\pi}^{2}(\Gamma;\mathbb{X})
such that

(5)

\mathscr{B}(u,v)=\mathscr{F}(v)\quad\forall\,v\in\mathscr{V},

where

\mathscr{B}(u,v):=\int_{\Gamma}\int_{D}a(x,\bm{y})\nabla u(x,\bm{y})\cdot\nabla v(x,\bm{y})\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}\pi(\bm{y}),\quad\mathscr{F}(v):=\int_{\Gamma}\int_{D}f(x)v(x,\bm{y})\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}\pi(\bm{y}).

We note that the bilinear form
\mathscr{B}
induces the energy norm defined by
|\!|\!|\,v\,|\!|\!|{}:=\big(\mathscr{B}(v,v)\big)^{1/2}
, and
the following norm equivalence holds due to assumption (
2
):

(6)

\displaystyle a_{\min}^{1/2}\,\|v\|\leq|\!|\!|\,v\,|\!|\!|{}\leq a_{\max}^{1/2}\,\|v\|\quad\forall\,v\in\mathscr{V},

where
\|\cdot\|
denotes the standard norm in the Bochner space
\mathscr{V}=L_{\pi}^{2}(\Gamma;\mathbb{X})
.
The well-posedness of (
5
) follows again by the Lax–Milgram lemma.
The variational formulation in (
5
) is the starting point for approximating
the parametric solution to (
1
) using the stochastic Galerkin FEM.

In this work, rather than approximating the solution
u\in\mathscr{V}
,
we aim at the numerical approximation of the functional value
\mathscr{Q}(u)
,
where
\mathscr{Q}:\mathscr{V}\to\mathbb{R}
is a continuous goal functional.
Specifically, given a continuous functional
Q:\mathbb{X}\to\mathbb{R}
, we introduce the quantity of interest

Q(u(\cdot,\bm{y}))
for
\pi
-almost all
\bm{y}\in\Gamma
and consider the goal functional
\mathscr{Q}
defined by

(7)

\mathscr{Q}(v):=\int_{\Gamma}Q(v(\cdot,\bm{y}))\,\mathrm{d}\pi(\bm{y})\quad\text{for all $v\in\mathscr{V}$}.

Thus,
\mathscr{Q}(u)
is the mean value of the quantity of interest
Q(u)
derived from the solution
u
to problem (
1
).
In practice,
Q(u)
can represent a range of linear and nonlinear quantities of interest, for example,
the average of the solution, or the average of the associated convection term
(u,u)\cdot\nabla u
,
across a spatial subdomain, or a pointwise estimate.
In particular, through nonlinear
Q
, the goal functional
\mathscr{Q}
can represent second and further moments
of such quantities of interest as the average of the solution, or its flux, over a subdomain.

3.
Stochastic collocation finite element method

Our goal-oriented adaptive algorithm for approximating
\mathscr{Q}(u)
will employ
the sparse grid stochastic collocation finite element method. In this section, we recall the main ideas of SC-FEM, including the construction of the underlying approximation spaces
and the associated a posteriori error analysis.
For the latter, we follow the approach (and the notation) presented in
[
11
,
9
]
.

3.1.
Spatial discretization and mesh refinement

Let
\mathcal{T}_{\bullet}
be a mesh on the spatial domain
D
(i.e., a conforming triangulation of
D
into
compact non-degenerate triangles
T
), and let
\mathcal{N}_{\bullet}
denote the set of vertices of
\mathcal{T}_{\bullet}
.
For the numerical solution of (
3
),
we employ the space
\mathbb{X}_{\bullet}
of continuous piecewise linear functions,

\mathbb{X}_{\bullet}:=\mathcal{S}^{1}_{0}(\mathcal{T}_{\bullet}):=\{v\in\mathbb{X}:v|_{T}\text{ is affine for all }T\in\mathcal{T}_{\bullet}\}\subset\mathbb{X}=H^{1}_{0}(D).

The standard basis of
\mathbb{X}_{\bullet}
is given by

\{\varphi_{\bullet,\xi}:\xi\in\mathcal{N}_{\bullet}\setminus\partial D\}
, where

\varphi_{\bullet,\xi}
denotes the hat function associated with the vertex
\xi\in\mathcal{N}_{\bullet}
.

For mesh refinement, we employ newest vertex bisection (NVB); see, e.g.,
[
37
,
28
]
.
We assume that any mesh
\mathcal{T}_{\bullet}
employed for the spatial discretization
is obtained by (uniform or local) NVB refinement(s) of a given (coarse) initial mesh
\mathcal{T}_{0}
.
The finite element space associated with
\mathcal{T}_{0}
is denoted by
\mathbb{X}_{0}:=\mathcal{S}^{1}_{0}(\mathcal{T}_{0})
.

Let
\widehat{\mathcal{T}}_{\bullet}
be the mesh obtained by uniform refinement of
\mathcal{T}_{\bullet}

(i.e., all elements of
\mathcal{T}_{\bullet}
are refined by three bisections).
Then,
\widehat{\mathcal{N}}_{\bullet}
denotes the set of vertices of
\widehat{\mathcal{T}}_{\bullet}
,
and
\mathcal{N}_{\bullet}^{+}:=(\widehat{\mathcal{N}}_{\bullet}\setminus\mathcal{N}_{\bullet})\setminus\partial D

is the set of new interior vertices created by this refinement of
\mathcal{T}_{\bullet}
.
The finite element space associated with
\widehat{\mathcal{T}}_{\bullet}
is denoted as

\widehat{\mathbb{X}}_{\bullet}:=\mathcal{S}^{1}_{0}(\widehat{\mathcal{T}}_{\bullet})
,
and

\{\widehat{\varphi}_{\bullet,\xi}:\xi\in\widehat{\mathcal{N}}_{\bullet}\setminus\partial D\}
is the corresponding basis of hat functions.
Let
\mathbb{Y}_{\bullet}
be the approximation space associated with the set
\mathcal{N}_{\bullet}^{+}
of newly introduced nodes (edge midpoints), i.e.,

\mathbb{Y}_{\bullet}:=\hull\{\widehat{\varphi}_{\bullet,\xi}:\xi\in\mathcal{N}_{\bullet}^{+}\}.

Then, the following decomposition holds:

\widehat{\mathbb{X}}_{\bullet}=\mathbb{X}_{\bullet}\oplus\mathbb{Y}_{\bullet}
.

For a set of marked nodes
\mathcal{M}_{\bullet}\subseteq\mathcal{N}_{\bullet}^{+}
,
we introduce a routine
\refine(\mathcal{T}_{\bullet},\mathcal{M}_{\bullet})

that generates a mesh
\mathcal{T}_{\circ}
—the coarsest NVB refinement of
\mathcal{T}_{\bullet}

such that
\mathcal{M}_{\bullet}\subset\mathcal{N}_{\circ}
, i.e., all marked nodes are vertices of
\mathcal{T}_{\circ}
.

For a fixed point
\bar{\bm{y}}\in\Gamma
,
we denote by
u_{\bullet\bar{\bm{y}}}\in\mathbb{X}_{\bullet}
the Galerkin finite element approximation satisfying

(8)

\displaystyle B_{\bar{\bm{y}}}(u_{\bullet\bar{\bm{y}}},v)=F(v)\quad\forall v\in\mathbb{X}_{\bullet}.

The enhanced Galerkin approximation satisfying (
8
)
for all
v\in\widehat{\mathbb{X}}_{\bullet}
is denoted by
\widehat{u}_{\bullet\bar{\bm{y}}}\in\widehat{\mathbb{X}}_{\bullet}
.

3.2.
Sparse grid collocation and parametric enrichment

In the context of the numerical solution of high-dimensional parametric problems,
the state-of-the-art stochastic collocation methods employ the nodes of
sparse grids
as collocation points

\bar{\bm{y}}
in the parameter domain
\Gamma
.

In the sparse grid SC-FEM, the parametric approximation is associated with
a monotone (or, downward-closed) finite set
\Lambda_{\bullet}\subset\mathbb{N}^{M}
of multi-indices;

specifically,

\Lambda_{\bullet}\,{=}\,\{\bm{\nu}\,{=}\,(\nu_{1},\ldots,\nu_{M}):\nu_{m}\in\mathbb{N},\,m=1,\ldots,M\}
is such
that
\#\Lambda_{\bullet}<\infty
and

\bm{\nu}\in\Lambda_{\bullet}\Longrightarrow\bm{\nu}-\bm{\varepsilon}_{m}\in\Lambda_{\bullet}\quad\forall\,m=1,\ldots,M\text{ such that }\nu_{m}>1,

where
(\bm{\varepsilon}_{m})_{i}=\delta_{mi}
for all
i=1,\ldots,M
.

Each component
\nu_{m}
(
m=1,\ldots,M
) of the multi-index
\bm{\nu}\in\Lambda_{\bullet}
corresponds to
a set
\mathcal{Y}_{m}^{\kappa(\nu_{m})}
of
\kappa(\nu_{m})
points along the
m
th coordinate axis in
\mathbb{R}^{M}
,
where
\kappa:\mathbb{N}_{0}\to\mathbb{N}_{0}
is a strictly increasing function satisfying
\kappa(0)=0
,
\kappa(1)=1
.
Crucially,
for each
m=1,2,\dots,M
, the
sets
\mathcal{Y}_{m}^{\kappa(\nu_{m})}
come from the same family of
nested sets

of 1D nodes on
[-1,1]
;
examples of such node sets include Leja points and Clenshaw–Curtis quadrature points
(one has
\kappa(i)=i
for Leja points and
\kappa(i)=2^{i-1}+1
,
i>1
for Clenshaw–Curtis nodes with the doubling rule).
Then, the associated sparse grid
\mathcal{Y}_{\bullet}=\mathcal{Y}_{\Lambda_{\bullet}}

of collocation points on
\Gamma
is given by

\mathcal{Y}_{\Lambda_{\bullet}}:=\bigcup_{\bm{\nu}\in\Lambda_{\bullet}}\mathcal{Y}^{\,(\bm{\nu})}=\bigcup_{\bm{\nu}\in\Lambda_{\bullet}}\,\mathcal{Y}_{1}^{\kappa(\nu_{1})}\times\mathcal{Y}_{2}^{\kappa(\nu_{2})}\times\ldots\times\mathcal{Y}_{M}^{\kappa(\nu_{M})}.

Given a sparse grid
\mathcal{Y}_{\bullet}
of collocation points in
\Gamma
,
the SC-FEM approximation of the solution
u
to parametric problem (
1
) is constructed as
the Lagrange interpolant:

(9)

u_{\bullet}^{\rm SC}(x,\bm{y}):=\sum\limits_{\bar{\bm{y}}\in\mathcal{Y}_{\bullet}}u_{\bullet\bar{\bm{y}}}(x)L_{\bullet\bar{\bm{y}}}(\bm{y}),

where
\{L_{\bullet\bar{\bm{y}}}(\bm{y}),\bar{\bm{y}}\in\mathcal{Y}_{\bullet}\}
is a set of
multivariable Lagrange basis functions constructed for the set of collocation points
\mathcal{Y}_{\bullet}
and satisfying

L_{\bullet\bar{\bm{y}}}(\bar{\bm{y}}^{\prime})=\delta_{\bar{\bm{y}}\bar{\bm{y}}^{\prime}}
for any
\bar{\bm{y}},\,\bar{\bm{y}}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Y}_{\bullet}
.

The total number of degrees of freedom in the SC-FEM approximation
u_{\bullet}^{\rm SC}
defined by (
9
) is given by

\big(\dim(\mathbb{X}_{\bullet})\big)\times\big(\#\mathcal{Y}_{\bullet}\big)
.

The enhancement of the parametric component of the SC-FEM approximation given by (
9
)
is effected by enriching the index set
\Lambda_{\bullet}
.
To that end, we first define the
margin
of
\Lambda_{\bullet}
as

{\rm K}({\Lambda_{\bullet}}):=\big\{\bm{\nu}\in\mathbb{N}^{M}\setminus\Lambda_{\bullet}:\bm{\nu}-\bm{\varepsilon}_{m}\in\Lambda_{\bullet}\text{ for some }m\in\{1,\dots,M\}\big\}.

We enrich the index set
\Lambda_{\bullet}
by adding multi-indices from a subset of
{\rm K}(\Lambda_{\bullet})

that is called the
reduced margin
:

{\rm R}({\Lambda_{\bullet}}):=\big\{\bm{\nu}\in{\rm K}(\Lambda_{\bullet}):\bm{\nu}-\bm{\varepsilon}_{m}\in\Lambda_{\bullet}\text{ for all }m=1,\dots,M\text{ such that }\nu_{m}>1\big\}.

Any such enrichment of
\Lambda_{\bullet}

corresponds to adding some collocation points from the set
\widehat{\mathcal{Y}}_{\bullet}\setminus\mathcal{Y}_{\bullet}
,
where
\widehat{\mathcal{Y}}_{\bullet}:=\mathcal{Y}_{\Lambda_{\bullet}\cup{\rm R}(\Lambda_{\bullet})}
.

It is important to note that if
\Lambda_{\bullet}
is monotone,
then for any subset
\Upsilon\subseteq{\rm R}(\Lambda_{\bullet})
, the index
set
\Lambda_{\bullet}\cup\Upsilon
is also monotone.

Note that the SC-FEM solution considered in the present work
follows the so-called
single-level
construction that employs the same finite element space
\mathbb{X}_{\bullet}

for all collocation points
\bar{\bm{y}}\in\mathcal{Y}_{\bullet}
(cf.
[
2
,
33
,
27
,
11
]
).
This is in contrast to
multilevel
SC-FEM approximations that allow

\mathbb{X}_{\bullet\bar{\bm{y}}}\not=\mathbb{X}_{\bullet\bar{\bm{y}}^{\prime}}
for
\bar{\bm{y}}\not=\bar{\bm{y}}^{\prime}
; see, e.g.,
[
30
,
23
,
9
]
.
Our choice of the single-level SC-FEM in this paper is motivated by the results of numerical experiments in
[
11
,
9
]
.
These results have indicated that the single-level version is likely
to be more efficient when the same adaptively refined finite element mesh can adequately resolve solution features
for a range of individually sampled problems, which is often the case for the model parametric problem (
1
).

3.3.
A posteriori error estimation

In this section, we briefly recall the a posteriori error estimation strategy developed in
[
11
]

as well as the associated error indicators that steer the adaptive refinement of SC-FEM approximations;
we refer to
[
11
, section 4]
for full details.
We recall that
\|\cdot\|
denotes the norm in
\mathscr{V}=L^{2}_{\pi}(\Gamma,\mathbb{X})
with
\mathbb{X}=H_{0}^{1}(D)
;
we also define
\|{\cdot}\|_{\mathbb{X}}:=\|\nabla\cdot\|_{L^{2}(D)}
.

A key ingredient of the error estimation strategy developed in
[
11
]
is an

enriched
SC-FEM approximation, denoted by
\widehat{u}_{\bullet}^{\rm\,SC}
, that is generated using spatial and parametric
enhancements of the SC-FEM approximation
u_{\bullet}^{\rm SC}
.
The
spatial
enhancement of
u_{\bullet}^{\rm SC}
is performed by uniform refinement of the underlying mesh,
whereas the
parametric
enhancement is done by adding the full reduced margin
{\rm R}({\Lambda_{\bullet}})
to the current index set
\Lambda_{\bullet}
.
The use of these enhanced approximations allows one to independently control the spatial and parametric
contributions to the overall discretization error
u-u_{\bullet}^{\rm SC}
.
In particular, under the saturation assumption that
\widehat{u}_{\bullet}^{\rm\,SC}
reduces the discretization error, i.e.,

(10)

\|u-\widehat{u}_{\bullet}^{\rm\,SC}\|\leq q_{\rm sat}\,\|u-u_{\bullet}^{\rm SC}\|

with a saturation constant
q_{\rm sat}\in(0,1)
independent of the discretization parameters,
the error estimate in the SC-FEM approximation
u_{\bullet}^{\rm SC}

is given by the sum of spatial and parametric contributions as follows (see equations (22)–(24) and (33) in
[
11
]
):

(11)

\|u-u_{\bullet}^{\rm SC}\|\leq\big(1-q_{\rm sat}\big)^{-1}\,\|\widehat{u}_{\bullet}^{\rm\,SC}-u_{\bullet}^{\rm SC}\|\leq\big(1-q_{\rm sat}\big)^{-1}\,\big(\mu_{\bullet}+\tau_{\bullet}\big).

Here,
\mu_{\bullet}=\mu_{\bullet}[u]
and
\tau_{\bullet}=\tau_{\bullet}[u]
are, respectively, the spatial and parametric error estimates defined by

(12)

\displaystyle\mu_{\bullet}=\mu_{\bullet}[u]

\displaystyle:=\bigg\|\sum\limits_{\bar{\bm{y}}\in\mathcal{Y}_{\bullet}}(\widehat{u}_{\bullet\bar{\bm{y}}}-u_{\bullet\bar{\bm{y}}})\,L_{\bullet\bar{\bm{y}}}\bigg\|

and

(13)

\displaystyle\tau_{\bullet}=\tau_{\bullet}[u]

\displaystyle:=\bigg\|\sum\limits_{\bar{\bm{y}}^{\prime}\in\widehat{\mathcal{Y}}_{\bullet}\setminus\mathcal{Y}_{\bullet}}\big(u_{\bullet\bar{\bm{y}}^{\prime}}-u_{\bullet}^{\rm SC}(\cdot,\bar{\bm{y}}^{\prime})\big)\widehat{L}_{\bullet\bar{\bm{y}}^{\prime}}\bigg\|,

where

\widehat{L}_{\bullet\bar{\bm{y}}^{\prime}}(\bm{y})
denotes the multivariable Lagrange basis function associated with the point
\bar{\bm{y}}^{\prime}\in\widehat{\mathcal{Y}}_{\bullet}
and satisfying

\widehat{L}_{\bullet\bar{\bm{y}}^{\prime}}(\bar{\bm{y}}^{\prime\prime})=\delta_{\bar{\bm{y}}^{\prime}\bar{\bm{y}}^{\prime\prime}}
for any
\bar{\bm{y}}^{\prime},\,\bar{\bm{y}}^{\prime\prime}\in\widehat{\mathcal{Y}}_{\bullet}
.

Remark 1
.

While the saturation assumption can be empirically justified when numerical approximations exhibit some asymptotic convergence behavior,
rigorous theoretical proofs exist either for deterministic problems with constant coefficients
(see, e.g.,
[
16
,
14
]
) or in the context of stochastic Galerkin FEM for parametric PDEs (see
[
3
]
). In the context of SC-FEM, however,
the proof of (
10
) remains an open problem.

Let us now turn to local error indicators that are associated with
the error estimates in (
12
) and (
13
).
For each
\bar{\bm{y}}\in\mathcal{Y}_{\bullet}
, we define the
spatial
two-level error indicators
associated with interior edge midpoints
(recall that
\mathbb{Y}_{\bullet}:=\hull\{\widehat{\varphi}_{\bullet,\xi}:\xi\in\mathcal{N}_{\bullet}^{+}\}
):

(14)

\mu_{\bullet\bar{\bm{y}}}(\xi):=\frac{\big|F(\widehat{\varphi}_{\bullet,\xi})-B_{\bar{\bm{y}}}(u_{\bullet\bar{\bm{y}}},\widehat{\varphi}_{\bullet,\xi})\big|}{\|\widehat{\varphi}_{\bullet,\xi}\|_{\mathbb{X}}}\quad\text{for all }\xi\in\mathcal{N}_{\bullet}^{+}.

We combine these
local
indicators to define the
spatial
error indicator for each
\bar{\bm{y}}\in\mathcal{Y}_{\bullet}
:

(15)

\mu^{2}_{\bullet\bar{\bm{y}}}:=\sum\limits_{\xi\in\mathcal{N}_{\bullet}^{+}}\mu^{2}_{\bullet\bar{\bm{y}}}(\xi).

Next, for each
\bm{\nu}\in{\rm R}({\Lambda_{\bullet}})
, the
parametric
error indicator is defined as follows:

(16)

\displaystyle\widetilde{\tau}_{\bullet\bm{\nu}}=\widetilde{\tau}_{\bullet\bm{\nu}}[u]:=\sum\limits_{\bar{\bm{y}}^{\prime}\in\widetilde{\mathcal{Y}}_{\bullet\bm{\nu}}}\big\|u_{\bullet\bar{\bm{y}}^{\prime}}-u_{\bullet}^{\rm SC}(\cdot,\bar{\bm{y}}^{\prime})\big\|_{\mathbb{X}}\,\|\widehat{L}_{\bullet\bar{\bm{y}}^{\prime}}\|_{L_{\pi}^{2}(\Gamma)},

where

\widetilde{\mathcal{Y}}_{\bullet\bm{\nu}}:=\mathcal{Y}_{\Lambda_{\bullet}\cup\{\bm{\nu}\}}\setminus\mathcal{Y}_{\Lambda_{\bullet}}\subset\widehat{\mathcal{Y}}_{\bullet}\setminus\mathcal{Y}_{\bullet}

are the collocation points ‘generated’ by the multi-index
\bm{\nu}\in{\rm R}({\Lambda_{\bullet}})
.

We refer to
[
9
, section 3]
for a discussion of computational costs
associated with computing the error estimates
\mu_{\bullet}
and
\tau_{\bullet}
given by (
12
)
and (
13
).
The key point is that in the adaptive algorithm, the computation of these error estimates is only required to give
a reliable criterion for termination of the adaptive process.
In contrast to the error estimates
\mu_{\bullet}
and
\tau_{\bullet}
,
the error indicators
\mu_{\bullet\bar{\bm{y}}}
and
\widetilde{\tau}_{\bullet\bm{\nu}}
are cheaper to compute
and the following inequalities hold (see equation (31) and Remark 3 in
[
11
]
):

(17)

\mu_{\bullet}\lesssim\bar{\mu}_{\bullet}:=\sum\limits_{\bar{\bm{y}}\in\mathcal{Y}_{\bullet}}\mu_{\bullet\bar{\bm{y}}}\,\|L_{\bullet\bar{\bm{y}}}\|_{L^{2}_{\pi}(\Gamma)}\quad\text{and}\quad\tau_{\bullet}\leq\bar{\tau}_{\bullet}:=\sum\limits_{\bm{\nu}\in{\rm R}(\Lambda_{\bullet})}\widetilde{\tau}_{\bullet\bm{\nu}}.

This motivates the use of the
error indicators
(
14
) and (
16
) in the marking strategy within the adaptive algorithm.

4.
Goal-oriented adaptive SC-FEM with linear goal functionals

In this section, we assume that the quantity of interest
Q(u(\cdot,\bm{y}))
is obtained using a bounded linear functional
Q:\mathbb{X}\to\mathbb{R}
,
i.e.,
Q\in\mathbb{X}^{*}
.
Consequently, the goal functional
\mathscr{Q}:\mathscr{V}\to\mathbb{R}
defined in (
7
) is also linear and bounded, i.e.,
\mathscr{Q}\in\mathscr{V}^{*}
.
Focusing on this special case, we develop a goal-oriented error estimation strategy in the context of SC-FEM
and present the associated adaptive algorithm.
Subsequently, in section
5
, we will extend this error estimation strategy and adaptive algorithm
to a wider class of (possibly nonlinear) goal functionals.

4.1.
Dual formulations and the goal-oriented error estimate

We use the standard approach to goal-oriented error estimation (see, e.g.,
[
18
,
6
,
25
]
)
but adapt it to the setting of SC-FEM.
To that end, we introduce two
dual formulations
: one associated with the functional
Q\in\mathbb{X}^{*}=H^{-1}(D)

and the other associated with the functional
\mathscr{Q}\in\mathscr{V}^{*}
given by (
7
).
For
\pi
-almost all
\bar{\bm{y}}\in\Gamma
, the first dual formulation corresponds to the

samplewise
primal formulation (
3
) and reads as:
find
z_{\bar{\bm{y}}}(x):=z(x,\bar{\bm{y}})\in\mathbb{X}
such that

(18)

B_{\bar{\bm{y}}}(v,z_{\bar{\bm{y}}})=Q(v)\quad\forall\,v\in\mathbb{X}.

The second dual formulation corresponds to the
combined
primal formulation (
5
)
and reads as:
find
z\in\mathscr{V}
satisfying

(19)

\mathscr{B}(v,z)=\mathscr{Q}(v)\quad\forall v\in\mathscr{V}.

Both dual formulations are well-posed due to the Lax–Milgram lemma.

In the same way as we did for the primal formulation,
we discretize (
18
) at each collocation point
\bar{\bm{y}}\in\mathcal{Y}_{\bullet}
by the Galerkin FEM and
build the Lagrange interpolant out of the resulting Galerkin approximations.
Specifically, for each
\bar{\bm{y}}\in\mathcal{Y}_{\bullet}
, we denote by
z_{\bullet\bar{\bm{y}}}\in\mathbb{X}_{\bullet}
the Galerkin finite element approximation satisfying

(20)

\displaystyle B_{\bar{\bm{y}}}(v,z_{\bullet\bar{\bm{y}}})=Q(v)\quad\forall v\in\mathbb{X}_{\bullet}

and define

(21)

z_{\bullet}^{\rm SC}(x,\bm{y}):=\sum\limits_{\bar{\bm{y}}\in\mathcal{Y}_{\bullet}}z_{\bullet\bar{\bm{y}}}(x)L_{\bullet\bar{\bm{y}}}(\bm{y}).

To estimate the error
\|z-z_{\bullet}^{\rm SC}\|
, we use the error estimation strategy described in §
3.3
.
In particular, we define the error estimates

(22)

\displaystyle\eta_{\bullet}:=\mu_{\bullet}[z],\quad\sigma_{\bullet}:=\tau_{\bullet}[z]

and the parametric error indicator

(23)

\displaystyle\widetilde{\sigma}_{\bullet\bm{\nu}}:=\widetilde{\tau}_{\bullet\bm{\nu}}[z]\quad\text{ for each $\bm{\nu}\in{\rm R}({\Lambda_{\bullet}})$},

where
\mu_{\bullet}[\cdot]
,
\tau_{\bullet}[\cdot]
, and
\widetilde{\tau}_{\bullet\bm{\nu}}[\cdot]
are given by (
12
),
(
13
), and (
16
), respectively.
Also, similarly to (
14
) and (
15
),
for each
\bar{\bm{y}}\in\mathcal{Y}_{\bullet}
, we define the spatial two-level error indicators

(24)

\eta_{\bullet\bar{\bm{y}}}(\xi):=\frac{\big|Q(\widehat{\varphi}_{\bullet,\xi})-B_{\bar{\bm{y}}}(\widehat{\varphi}_{\bullet,\xi},z_{\bullet\bar{\bm{y}}})\big|}{\|\widehat{\varphi}_{\bullet,\xi}\|_{\mathbb{X}}}\quad\text{for all }\xi\in\mathcal{N}_{\bullet}^{+}

and

(25)

\eta^{2}_{\bullet\bar{\bm{y}}}:=\sum\limits_{\xi\in\mathcal{N}_{\bullet}^{+}}\eta^{2}_{\bullet\bar{\bm{y}}}(\xi).

Similarly to (
11
) and (
17
), the following estimates hold:

(26)

\|z-z_{\bullet}^{\rm SC}\|\leq\big(1-q_{\rm sat}\big)^{-1}\,\big(\eta_{\bullet}+\sigma_{\bullet}\big)

as well as

(27)

\eta_{\bullet}\lesssim\bar{\eta}_{\bullet}:=\sum\limits_{\bar{\bm{y}}\in\mathcal{Y}_{\bullet}}\eta_{\bullet\bar{\bm{y}}}\,\|L_{\bullet\bar{\bm{y}}}\|_{L^{2}_{\pi}(\Gamma)}\quad\text{and}\quad\sigma_{\bullet}\leq\bar{\sigma}_{\bullet}:=\sum\limits_{\bm{\nu}\in{\rm R}(\Lambda_{\bullet})}\widetilde{\sigma}_{\bullet\bm{\nu}}.

Now, recall that our aim is to approximate
\mathscr{Q}(u)
, where

\mathscr{Q}\in\mathscr{V}^{*}
is the goal functional given by (
7
) and

u
is the solution to the parametric problem (
1
).
Exploiting the linearity of
\mathscr{Q}
and using (
19
), we have

\displaystyle\mathscr{Q}(u)-\mathscr{Q}(u_{\bullet}^{\rm SC})

\displaystyle=\mathscr{Q}(u-u_{\bullet}^{\rm SC})=\mathscr{B}(u-u_{\bullet}^{\rm SC},z)

(28)

\displaystyle=\mathscr{B}(u-u_{\bullet}^{\rm SC},z-z_{\bullet}^{\rm SC})+\mathscr{B}(u-u_{\bullet}^{\rm SC},z_{\bullet}^{\rm SC}).

At this point, the standard approach (e.g., for Galerkin FEM approximations) would exploit the Galerkin orthogonality property
and the Cauchy–Schwartz inequality to derive the upper bound for the error in the goal functions as the product of energy errors, i.e.,
|\!|\!|\,u-u_{\bullet}^{\rm SC}\,|\!|\!|{}\,|\!|\!|\,z-z_{\bullet}^{\rm SC}\,|\!|\!|{}
.
In the setting of stochastic collocation FEM,
\mathscr{B}(u-u_{\bullet}^{\rm SC},z_{\bullet}^{\rm SC})\not=0
.
On the other hand, this quantity is computable:

\mathscr{B}(u-u_{\bullet}^{\rm SC},z_{\bullet}^{\rm SC})=\mathscr{B}(u,z_{\bullet}^{\rm SC})-\mathscr{B}(u_{\bullet}^{\rm SC},z_{\bullet}^{\rm SC})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:weak:primal}}}{{=}}\mathscr{F}(z_{\bullet}^{\rm SC})-\mathscr{B}(u_{\bullet}^{\rm SC},z_{\bullet}^{\rm SC}).

Therefore, we can use this quantity as a ‘correction term’
to define the approximation of
\mathscr{Q}(u)
as follows:

(29)

\displaystyle\mathscr{Q}(u)\,{\approx}\,\widetilde{\mathscr{Q}}(u_{\bullet}^{\rm SC},z_{\bullet}^{\rm SC})\,{:=}\,\mathscr{Q}(u_{\bullet}^{\rm SC})+\mathscr{B}(u-u_{\bullet}^{\rm SC},z_{\bullet}^{\rm SC})\,{=}\,\mathscr{Q}(u_{\bullet}^{\rm SC})+\mathscr{F}(z_{\bullet}^{\rm SC})-\mathscr{B}(u_{\bullet}^{\rm SC},z_{\bullet}^{\rm SC}).

Hence, applying the Cauchy–Schwartz inequality, we obtain from (
4.1
):

\displaystyle\big|\mathscr{Q}(u)-\widetilde{\mathscr{Q}}(u_{\bullet}^{\rm SC},z_{\bullet}^{\rm SC})\big|=\big|\mathscr{B}(u-u_{\bullet}^{\rm SC},z-z_{\bullet}^{\rm SC})\big|\leq\big|\!\big|\!\big|\,u-u_{\bullet}^{\rm SC}\,\big|\!\big|\!\big|{}\,\big|\!\big|\!\big|\,z-z_{\bullet}^{\rm SC}\,\big|\!\big|\!\big|{}.

Now, using the norm equivalence (
6
) and the a posteriori error estimates (
11
)
and (
26
) for the primal and dual SC-FEM approximations, we derive the final error estimate
for the approximation of
\mathscr{Q}(u)
by
\widetilde{\mathscr{Q}}(u_{\bullet}^{\rm SC},z_{\bullet}^{\rm SC})
:

(30)

\displaystyle\big|\mathscr{Q}(u)-\widetilde{\mathscr{Q}}(u_{\bullet}^{\rm SC},z_{\bullet}^{\rm SC})\big|\lesssim\big\|u-u_{\bullet}^{\rm SC}\big\|\,\big\|z-z_{\bullet}^{\rm SC}\big\|\lesssim\big(\mu_{\bullet}+\tau_{\bullet}\big)\,\big(\eta_{\bullet}+\sigma_{\bullet}\big),

where

\mu_{\bullet}=\mu_{\bullet}[u]
,
\tau_{\bullet}=\tau_{\bullet}[u]
,

\eta_{\bullet}=\mu_{\bullet}[z]
,
\sigma_{\bullet}=\tau_{\bullet}[z]
,
with
\mu_{\bullet}[\cdot]
and
\tau_{\bullet}[\cdot]
given by (
12
) and
(
13
), respectively, and
the hidden constant only depends on
a_{\max}
and the saturation constants
q_{\rm sat}
(for the primal and dual solutions).

We emphasize that in contrast to the standard goal-oriented approach in the context of Galerkin methods,
where
\mathscr{Q}(u)
is typically approximated by evaluating the goal functional on the
primal
Galerkin solution
u_{\bullet}
,
i.e.,
\mathscr{Q}(u)\approx\mathscr{Q}(u_{\bullet})
, the approximation of
\mathscr{Q}(u)
in the SC-FEM setting is best done using

\widetilde{\mathscr{Q}}(u_{\bullet}^{\rm SC},z_{\bullet}^{\rm SC})
, which depends on both
primal and dual
SC-FEM approximations; cf. (
29
).
We also note that, as iterations progress, it is expected that
u_{\bullet}^{\rm SC}
converges to
u
and, therefore,
the ‘correction term’ given by
\mathscr{B}(u-u_{\bullet}^{\rm SC},z_{\bullet}^{\rm SC})
converges to zero.

4.2.
Adaptive algorithm

In this section, we present a goal-oriented adaptive algorithm that employs SC-FEM approximations
of the primal and dual solutions satisfying (
5
) and (
19
), respectively.
It originates from the adaptive sparse grid algorithm proposed in
[
24
]
.

The refinement of the underlying finite element mesh and the enrichment of the set of collocation points
are steered by, respectively, the spatial error indicators
\mu_{\bullet\bar{\bm{y}}}
,
\eta_{\bullet\bar{\bm{y}}}
and
the parametric indicators
\widetilde{\sigma}_{\bullet\bm{\nu}}
,
\widetilde{\tau}_{\bullet\bm{\nu}}

discussed in §
3.3
and §
4.1
.
The calculation of the error estimates
\mu_{\bullet}
,
\tau_{\bullet}
,
\eta_{\bullet}
,
\sigma_{\bullet}

in (
12
), (
13
), (
22
) needs to be done periodically,
since the product error estimate in (
30
) is only required for reliable termination of the adaptive process
according to the given tolerance.
In practice, the approximation
\widetilde{\mathscr{Q}}(u_{\bullet}^{\rm SC},z_{\bullet}^{\rm SC})
of
\mathscr{Q}(u)
only needs to be computed once,
when the error tolerance is reached (see step (vii) in Algorithm
2
below).
However, in the numerical experiments presented in section
6
,
we compute
\widetilde{\mathscr{Q}}(u_{\bullet}^{\rm SC},z_{\bullet}^{\rm SC})
at each iteration to illustrate the decay of the error in approximating
\mathscr{Q}(u)
.

Algorithm 2
.

Input:

\Lambda_{0}=\{\bm{1}\}
;
\mathcal{T}_{0}
; marking criterion.

Set the iteration counter
\ell:=0
, the output counter
k
, and the error tolerance
tol
.

(i)

Compute the primal and dual Galerkin approximations

\big\{u_{\ell\bar{\bm{y}}}\in\mathbb{X}_{\ell}:\bar{\bm{y}}\in\mathcal{Y}_{\Lambda_{\ell}\cup{\rm R}(\Lambda_{\ell})}\big\}
and

\big\{z_{\ell\bar{\bm{y}}}\in\mathbb{X}_{\ell}:\bar{\bm{y}}\in\mathcal{Y}_{\Lambda_{\ell}\cup{\rm R}(\Lambda_{\ell})}\big\}

by solving (
8
) and (
20
), respectively.

(ii)

Compute local spatial error indicators

\big\{\mu_{\ell\bar{\bm{y}}}(\xi):\bar{\bm{y}}\in\mathcal{Y}_{\ell},\ \xi\in\mathcal{N}_{\ell}^{+}\big\}
and

\big\{\eta_{\ell\bar{\bm{y}}}(\xi):\bar{\bm{y}}\in\mathcal{Y}_{\ell},\ \xi\in\mathcal{N}_{\ell}^{+}\big\}

given by (
14
) and (
24
), respectively,
as well as their global counterparts

\big\{\mu_{\ell\bar{\bm{y}}}:\bar{\bm{y}}\in\mathcal{Y}_{\ell}\big\}
and

\big\{\eta_{\ell\bar{\bm{y}}}:\bar{\bm{y}}\in\mathcal{Y}_{\ell}\big\}

given by (
15
) and (
25
), respectively.

(iii)

Compute parametric error indicators

\big\{\widetilde{\tau}_{\ell\bm{\nu}}:\bm{\nu}\in{\rm R}(\Lambda_{\ell})\big\}
and

\big\{\widetilde{\sigma}_{\ell\bm{\nu}}:\bm{\nu}\in{\rm R}(\Lambda_{\ell})\big\}

given by (
16
) and (
23
), respectively.

(iv)

Use a marking criterion (e.g., Algorithm
3
below) to determine

\mathcal{M}_{\ell}\subseteq\mathcal{N}_{\ell}^{+}
and

\Upsilon_{\ell}\subseteq{\rm R}(\Lambda_{\ell})
.

(v)

Set
\mathcal{T}_{\ell+1}:=\refine(\mathcal{T}_{\ell},\mathcal{M}_{\ell})
.

(vi)

Set
\Lambda_{\ell+1}:=\Lambda_{\ell}\cup\Upsilon_{\ell}
,

\mathcal{Y}_{\ell+1}:=\mathcal{Y}_{\Lambda_{\ell+1}}
.

(vii)

If
\ell=jk,\ j\in\mathbb{N}
, compute the error estimates

\mu_{\ell}
,
\eta_{\ell}
,
\tau_{\ell}
,
\sigma_{\ell}
given by (
12
), (
13
), and (
22
).
If
(\mu_{\ell}+\tau_{\ell})\,(\eta_{\ell}+\sigma_{\ell})<{\tt tol}
, then
compute
\widetilde{\mathscr{Q}}(u_{\ell}^{\rm SC},z_{\ell}^{\rm SC})
using (
29
) and exit;
here, the SC-FEM approximations
u_{\ell}^{\rm SC}
and
z_{\ell}^{\rm SC}
are
computed via (
9
) and (
21
), respectively, from the Galerkin approximations

\big\{u_{{\ell}\bar{\bm{y}}},\,z_{{\ell}\bar{\bm{y}}}\in\mathbb{X}_{\ell}:\bar{\bm{y}}\in\mathcal{Y}_{\ell}\big\}
.

(viii)

Increase the counter
\ell\mapsto\ell+1
and goto
(i)
.

Output:
For some specific
\ell_{*}=jk\in\mathbb{N}
,
the algorithm returns the approximation
\widetilde{\mathscr{Q}}(u_{\ell_{*}}^{\rm SC},z_{\ell_{*}}^{\rm SC})
of the goal functional
\mathscr{Q}(u)

together with a corresponding error estimate
(\mu_{\ell_{*}}+\tau_{\ell_{*}})\,(\eta_{\ell_{*}}+\sigma_{\ell_{*}})
.

A marking strategy needs to be specified for step (iv) of Algorithm
2
.
The marking strategy serves two purposes.
Firstly, it identifies the refinement type (spatial versus parametric) and secondly,
it generates the corresponding marking sets.
In the context of goal-oriented adaptivity, both these tasks are complicated by the fact that there are two sets of spatial indicators
and two sets of parametric indicators, with each set corresponding to either primal or dual approximations.
To decide on the refinement type at each iteration, we use the
cumulative error indicators

\bar{\mu}_{\ell},\,\bar{\eta}_{\ell},\,\bar{\tau}_{\ell},\,\bar{\sigma}_{\ell}

defined in (
17
) and (
27
)
(recall that these error indicators are cheaper to compute than the error estimates
\mu_{\ell},\,\eta_{\ell},\,\tau_{\ell},\,\sigma_{\ell}
).
The refinement type is determined by
the following quantity:

(31)

\max\big\{\bar{\mu}_{\ell}^{2}+\bar{\eta}_{\ell}^{2},\,\bar{\tau}_{\ell}^{2}+\bar{\sigma}_{\ell}^{2}\big\}.

For example, if
\bar{\mu}_{\ell}^{2}+\bar{\eta}_{\ell}^{2}\geq\bar{\tau}_{\ell}^{2}+\bar{\sigma}_{\ell}^{2}
,
then the algorithm performs
spatial
refinement.
This approach to choosing the refinement type is motivated by the following bound on the product estimate
for the error in the goal functional (cf. (
30
)):

\displaystyle\big(\mu_{\ell}+\tau_{\ell}\big)\big(\eta_{\ell}+\sigma_{\ell}\big)

\displaystyle\leq\big(\bar{\mu}_{\ell}+\bar{\tau}_{\ell}\big)\big(\bar{\eta}_{\ell}+\bar{\sigma}_{\ell}\big)

(32)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\Big[\big(\bar{\mu}_{\ell}+\bar{\tau}_{\ell}\big)^{2}+\big(\bar{\eta}_{\ell}+\bar{\sigma}_{\ell}\big)^{2}\Big]\leq\big(\bar{\mu}_{\ell}^{2}+\bar{\eta}_{\ell}^{2}\big)+\big(\bar{\tau}_{\ell}^{2}+\bar{\sigma}_{\ell}^{2}\big).

Thus, the proposed approach identifies a dominant source of errors and selects the corresponding refinement type.
There are alternative ways to determine the refinement type from the above four cumulative error indicators.
For example, one can use

\max\{\bar{\mu}_{\ell},\,\bar{\tau}_{\ell},\,\bar{\eta}_{\ell},\,\bar{\sigma}_{\ell}\}

or
\max\{\bar{\mu}_{\ell}\bar{\eta}_{\ell},\,\bar{\tau}_{\ell}\bar{\sigma}_{\ell}\}
.

Our next objective is to determine a suitable marking set that corresponds to the identified refinement type.
Depending on whether spatial or parametric refinement is selected,
this marking set consists of either interior edge midpoints of the current spatial mesh,
or multi-indices from the current reduced margin.
First, we perform Dörfler marking on each of the sets of error indicators associated with primal and dual approximations.
Having obtained two marking sets of the required refinement type, we then follow
the strategy from
[
32
]
to select a single marking set out of the two—the one that has minimum cardinality.
This final marking set will feed into the refinement process in steps (v) or (vi) of Algorithm
2
.
Alternative approaches to combining two marking sets into one final set were proposed and analyzed in
[
5
,
22
]

in the deterministic setting.
The associated marking strategies lead to the same convergence rate as the ‘minimum cardinality’ strategy of
[
32
]
,
but require less adaptive iterations to reach a prescribed accuracy;
see the experiments in
[
22
]
for some deterministic test problems.

The above ideas are summarized in the following algorithm.

Algorithm 3
.

Input:

error indicators

\{\mu_{\ell\bar{\bm{y}}}(\xi):\bar{\bm{y}}\in\mathcal{Y}_{\ell},\,\xi\in\mathcal{N}_{\ell}^{+}\}
,

\{\mu_{\ell\bar{\bm{y}}}:\bar{\bm{y}}\in\mathcal{Y}_{\ell}\}
,

\{\widetilde{\tau}_{\ell\bm{\nu}}:\bm{\nu}\in{\rm R}({\Lambda_{\ell})}\}

and

\{\eta_{\ell\bar{\bm{y}}}(\xi):\bar{\bm{y}}\in\mathcal{Y}_{\ell},\,\xi\in\mathcal{N}_{\ell}^{+}\}
,

\{\eta_{\ell\bar{\bm{y}}}:\bar{\bm{y}}\in\mathcal{Y}_{\ell}\}
,

\{\widetilde{\sigma}_{\ell\bm{\nu}}:\bm{\nu}\in{\rm R}({\Lambda_{\ell})}\}
;
marking parameters
0<\theta_{\mathbb{X}},\theta_{\mathcal{Y}}\leq 1
.

\bullet

Calculate the cumulative error indicators

\bar{\mu}_{\ell},\,\bar{\tau}_{\ell},\,\bar{\eta}_{\ell},\,\bar{\sigma}_{\ell}

given by (
17
) and (
27
).

\bullet

If
\bar{\mu}_{\ell}^{2}+\bar{\eta}_{\ell}^{2}\geq\bar{\tau}_{\ell}^{2}+\bar{\sigma}_{\ell}^{2},

then proceed as follows:

\circ

set
\Upsilon_{\ell}:=\emptyset
;

\circ

for each
\bar{\bm{y}}\in\mathcal{Y}_{\ell}
,
determine
\mathcal{M}^{(\mu)}_{\ell\bar{\bm{y}}}\subseteq\mathcal{N}_{\ell}^{+}
and
\mathcal{M}^{(\eta)}_{\ell\bar{\bm{y}}}\subseteq\mathcal{N}_{\ell}^{+}
such that

(33)

\theta_{\mathbb{X}}\,\sum_{\bar{\bm{y}}\in\mathcal{Y}_{\ell}}\sum_{\xi\in\mathcal{N}_{\ell}^{+}}\mu_{\ell\bar{\bm{y}}}(\xi)\|L_{\ell\bar{\bm{y}}}\|_{L^{2}_{\pi}(\Gamma)}\leq\sum_{\bar{\bm{y}}\in\mathcal{Y}_{\ell}}\sum_{\xi\in\mathcal{M}^{(\mu)}_{\ell\bar{\bm{y}}}}\mu_{\ell\bar{\bm{y}}}(\xi)\|L_{\ell\bar{\bm{y}}}\|_{L^{2}_{\pi}(\Gamma)},

(34)

\displaystyle\theta_{\mathbb{X}}\,\sum_{\bar{\bm{y}}\in\mathcal{Y}_{\ell}}\sum_{\xi\in\mathcal{N}_{\ell}^{+}}\eta_{\ell\bar{\bm{y}}}(\xi)\|L_{\ell\bar{\bm{y}}}\|_{L^{2}_{\pi}(\Gamma)}\leq\sum_{\bar{\bm{y}}\in\mathcal{Y}_{\ell}}\sum_{\xi\in\mathcal{M}^{(\eta)}_{\ell\bar{\bm{y}}}}\eta_{\ell\bar{\bm{y}}}(\xi)\|L_{\ell\bar{\bm{y}}}\|_{L^{2}_{\pi}(\Gamma)}

with cumulative cardinalities
\sum_{\bar{\bm{y}}\in\mathcal{Y}_{\ell}}\#\mathcal{M}^{(\mu)}_{\ell\bar{\bm{y}}}
and

\sum_{\bar{\bm{y}}\in\mathcal{Y}_{\ell}}\#\mathcal{M}^{(\eta)}_{\ell\bar{\bm{y}}}
that are minimized
over all the sets that satisfy (
33
) and (
34
), respectively;

\circ

set
\mathcal{M}^{(\mu)}_{\ell}:=\bigcup_{\bar{\bm{y}}\in\mathcal{Y}_{\ell}}\mathcal{M}^{(\mu)}_{\ell\bar{\bm{y}}}
and

\mathcal{M}^{(\eta)}_{\ell}:=\bigcup_{\bar{\bm{y}}\in\mathcal{Y}_{\ell}}\mathcal{M}^{(\eta)}_{\ell\bar{\bm{y}}}
;

\circ

if
\#\mathcal{M}^{(\mu)}_{\ell}\,{\leq}\,\#\mathcal{M}^{(\eta)}_{\ell}
, then choose
\mathcal{M}_{\ell}:=\mathcal{M}^{(\mu)}_{\ell}
;
otherwise, choose
\mathcal{M}_{\ell}:=\mathcal{M}^{(\eta)}_{\ell}
.

\bullet

Otherwise, i.e., if
\bar{\mu}_{\ell}^{2}+\bar{\eta}_{\ell}^{2}<\bar{\tau}_{\ell}^{2}+\bar{\sigma}_{\ell}^{2}
,
proceed as follows:

\circ

set
\mathcal{M}_{\ell}:=\emptyset
;

\circ

determine
\Upsilon^{(\tau)}_{\ell}\subseteq{\rm R}(\Lambda_{\ell})
and
\Upsilon^{(\sigma)}_{\ell}\subseteq{\rm R}(\Lambda_{\ell})

of minimal cardinality such that

\theta_{\mathcal{Y}}\,\sum_{\bm{\nu}\in{\rm R}(\Lambda_{\ell})}\widetilde{\tau}_{\ell\bm{\nu}}\leq\sum_{\bm{\nu}\in\Upsilon^{(\tau)}_{\ell}}\widetilde{\tau}_{\ell\bm{\nu}}\quad\text{and}\quad\theta_{\mathcal{Y}}\,\sum_{\bm{\nu}\in{\rm R}(\Lambda_{\ell})}\widetilde{\sigma}_{\ell\bm{\nu}}\leq\sum_{\bm{\nu}\in\Upsilon^{(\sigma)}_{\ell}}\widetilde{\sigma}_{\ell\bm{\nu}};

\circ

if

, then choose

;

.

Output:

\mathcal{M}_{\ell}\subseteq\mathcal{N}_{\ell}^{+}
and

\Upsilon_{\ell}\subseteq{\rm R}(\Lambda_{\ell})
.

4.3.
Computation of
\widetilde{\mathscr{Q}}(u_{\bullet}^{\rm SC},z_{\bullet}^{\rm SC})

An important detail in Algorithm
2
that we need to address is
the computation of
\widetilde{\mathscr{Q}}(u_{\bullet}^{\rm SC},z_{\bullet}^{\rm SC})
—the approximation of the functional value
\mathscr{Q}(u)
.
Recalling the definition of
\widetilde{\mathscr{Q}}(u_{\bullet}^{\rm SC},z_{\bullet}^{\rm SC})
in (
29
),
let us first address the computation of
\mathscr{Q}(u_{\bullet}^{\rm SC})
and
\mathscr{F}(z_{\bullet}^{\rm SC})
.
In fact, it is easy to see that
\mathscr{Q}(u_{\bullet}^{\rm SC})=\mathscr{F}(z_{\bullet}^{\rm SC})
.
Indeed,

\displaystyle\mathscr{Q}(u_{\bullet}^{\rm SC})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:goal}}}{{=}}\int_{\Gamma}Q(u_{\bullet}^{\rm SC}(\cdot,\bm{y}))\mathrm{d}\pi(\bm{y})

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:scfem:sol}}}{{=}}\int_{\Gamma}Q\bigg(\sum_{\bar{\bm{y}}\in\mathcal{Y}_{\bullet}}u_{\bullet\bar{\bm{y}}}L_{\bullet\bar{\bm{y}}}(\bm{y})\bigg)\mathrm{d}\pi(\bm{y})

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\phantom{\eqref{eq:scfem:sol}}}}{{=}}\sum_{\bar{\bm{y}}\in\mathcal{Y}_{\bullet}}Q(u_{\bullet\bar{\bm{y}}})\int_{\Gamma}L_{\bullet\bar{\bm{y}}}(\bm{y})\mathrm{d}\pi(\bm{y})

and a similar calculation shows that

\displaystyle\mathscr{F}(z_{\bullet}^{\rm SC})=\int_{\Gamma}\sum_{\bar{\bm{y}}\in\mathcal{Y}_{\bullet}}F(z_{\bullet\bar{\bm{y}}})L_{\bullet\bar{\bm{y}}}(\bm{y})\mathrm{d}\pi(\bm{y})=\sum_{\bar{\bm{y}}\in\mathcal{Y}_{\bullet}}F(z_{\bullet\bar{\bm{y}}})\int_{\Gamma}L_{\bullet\bar{\bm{y}}}(\bm{y})\mathrm{d}\pi(\bm{y}).

Now, observing that

Q(u_{\bullet\bar{\bm{y}}})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:sample:fem:dual}}}{{=}}B_{\bar{\bm{y}}}(u_{\bullet\bar{\bm{y}}},z_{\bullet\bar{\bm{y}}})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:sample:fem}}}{{=}}F(z_{\bullet\bar{\bm{y}}})\quad\forall\,\bar{\bm{y}}\in\mathcal{Y}_{\bullet},

we conclude that

(35)

\displaystyle\mathscr{Q}(u_{\bullet}^{\rm SC})=\sum_{\bar{\bm{y}}\in Y_{\bullet}}B_{\bar{\bm{y}}}(u_{\bullet\bar{\bm{y}}},z_{\bullet\bar{\bm{y}}})\int_{\Gamma}L_{\bullet\bar{\bm{y}}}(\bm{y})\mathrm{d}\pi(\bm{y})=\mathscr{F}(z_{\bullet}^{\rm SC})

and therefore, the approximation
\widetilde{\mathscr{Q}}(u_{\bullet}^{\rm SC},z_{\bullet}^{\rm SC})
of the goal functional
\mathscr{Q}(u)
can be calculated using
a simplified formula

(36)

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathscr{Q}}(u_{\bullet}^{\rm SC},z_{\bullet}^{\rm SC})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:goal:approx}}}{{=}}\mathscr{Q}(u_{\bullet}^{\rm SC})+\mathscr{F}(z_{\bullet}^{\rm SC})-\mathscr{B}(u_{\bullet}^{\rm SC},z_{\bullet}^{\rm SC})=2\mathscr{Q}(u_{\bullet}^{\rm SC})-\mathscr{B}(u_{\bullet}^{\rm SC},z_{\bullet}^{\rm SC}).

Here,
\mathscr{Q}(u_{\bullet}^{\rm SC})
is easily evaluated using the representation in (
35
):
while the spatial contributions (i.e., the bilinear forms) are evaluated in a standard way
(using the finite element coefficient vectors and the stiffness matrices associated with samples of the diffusion coefficient),
the integrals of
L_{\bullet\bar{\bm{y}}}
are calculated by representing these polynomial functions
in terms of products of 1D Lagrange basis functions using the combination technique

(see, e.g.,
[
39
]
and
[
27
, section 3]
)

and by using precomputed values for the integrals of 1D Lagrange basis functions
for a given type of collocation points (e.g., Leja or Clenshaw–Curtis points).

The computation of the term
\mathscr{B}(u_{\bullet}^{\rm SC},z_{\bullet}^{\rm SC})
in (
36
) is more involved.
Using the representations of
u_{\bullet}^{\rm SC}
,
z_{\bullet}^{\rm SC}
in (
9
), (
21
), we have

\displaystyle\mathscr{B}(u_{\bullet}^{\rm SC},z_{\bullet}^{\rm SC})

\displaystyle=\int_{\Gamma}\int_{D}a(x,\bm{y})\nabla u_{\bullet}^{\rm SC}(x,\bm{y})\cdot\nabla z_{\bullet}^{\rm SC}(x,\bm{y})\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}\pi(\bm{y})

(37)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\bar{\bm{y}},\bar{\bm{y}}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Y}_{\bullet}}\int_{\Gamma}\int_{D}a(x,\bm{y})\nabla u_{\bullet\bar{\bm{y}}}(x)\cdot\nabla z_{\bullet\bar{\bm{y}}^{\prime}}(x)L_{\bullet\bar{\bm{y}}}(\bm{y})L_{\bullet\bar{\bm{y}}^{\prime}}(\bm{y})\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}\pi(\bm{y}).

Now, similarly to the calculation of
\mathscr{Q}(u_{\bullet}^{\rm SC})
in (
35
), our aim is to represent each term in the above sum
as a product of easily computable spatial and parametric integrals.
Doing this exactly is only possible in special cases.
For example, if the diffusion coefficient
a(x,\bm{y})
has an affine expansion in terms of parameters, i.e.,

a(x,\bm{y})=a_{0}(x)+\sum_{m=1}^{M}y_{m}a_{m}(x),

we obtain (here, we set
y_{0}:=1
)

\displaystyle\mathscr{B}(u_{\bullet}^{\rm SC},z_{\bullet}^{\rm SC})\,{=}\,\sum_{\bm{y},\bm{y}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Y}_{\bullet}}\sum_{m=0}^{M}\bigg(\int_{D}a_{m}(x)\nabla u_{\bullet\bar{\bm{y}}}(x)\cdot\nabla z_{\bullet\bar{\bm{y}}^{\prime}}(x)\mathrm{d}x\bigg)\bigg(\int_{\Gamma}y_{m}L_{\bullet\bar{\bm{y}}}(\bm{y})L_{\bullet\bar{\bm{y}}^{\prime}}(\bm{y})\mathrm{d}\pi(\bm{y})\bigg).

The resulting spatial integrals are then evaluated in a standard way (as explained above for the calculation of
\mathscr{Q}(u_{\bullet}^{\rm SC})
), and
the parametric integrals are again calculated from precomputed values of the corresponding integrals of polynomials in 1D.

In principle, such exact representations of
\mathscr{B}(u_{\bullet}^{\rm SC},z_{\bullet}^{\rm SC})
in terms of computable spatial and parametric integrals
are possible for any other
polynomial type
expansion of the diffusion coefficient, e.g., for the quadratic expansion

a(x,\bm{y})=\big(a_{0}(x)+\sum_{m=1}^{M}y_{m}a_{m}(x)\big)^{2}
.
In practice, however, calculating these integrals becomes computationally expensive very quickly.
Hence, even for nonaffine polynomial type expansions, it may be prudent to use the quadrature-based approach
that is presented next.

For a general (nonaffine) diffusion coefficient
a(x,\bm{y})
, we use a sparse grid-based quadrature rule to approximate
the integrals over
\Gamma
in (
4.3
).
First, we note that using the quadrature based on the current set of collocation points
\mathcal{Y}_{\bullet}

makes our use of the ‘correction term’
\mathscr{F}(z_{\bullet}^{\rm SC})-\mathscr{B}(u_{\bullet}^{\rm SC},z_{\bullet}^{\rm SC})
redundant.
Indeed, the approximation of integrals over
\Gamma
in (
4.3
) using the sparse grid
\mathcal{Y}_{\bullet}

will result in the approximation of
\mathscr{B}(u_{\bullet}^{\rm SC},z_{\bullet}^{\rm SC})
by
\mathscr{F}(z_{\bullet}^{\rm SC})
.
Therefore, one has to use an enriched set of collocation points, e.g., we can use the set
\widehat{\mathcal{Y}}_{\bullet}=\mathcal{Y}_{\Lambda_{\bullet}\cup{\rm R}(\Lambda_{\bullet})}
.
Denoting by
\omega_{\hat{\bm{y}}}
,
\hat{\bm{y}}\in\widehat{\mathcal{Y}}_{\bullet}
, the associated quadrature weights,
we approximate the bilinear form
\mathscr{B}(u_{\bullet}^{\rm SC},z_{\bullet}^{\rm SC})
given by (
4.3
) as follows:

\displaystyle\mathscr{B}(u_{\bullet}^{\rm SC},z_{\bullet}^{\rm SC})

\displaystyle\approx\sum_{\hat{\bm{y}}\in\widehat{\mathcal{Y}}_{\bullet}}\omega_{\hat{\bm{y}}}\sum_{\bar{\bm{y}},\bar{\bm{y}}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Y}_{\bullet}}\bigg[\int_{D}a(x,\hat{\bm{y}})\nabla u_{\bullet\bar{\bm{y}}}(x)\cdot\nabla z_{\bullet\bar{\bm{y}}^{\prime}}(x)\mathrm{d}x\bigg]\,L_{\bullet\bar{\bm{y}}}(\hat{\bm{y}})L_{\bullet\bar{\bm{y}}^{\prime}}(\hat{\bm{y}})

\displaystyle=\bigg(\sum_{\hat{\bm{y}}\in\mathcal{Y}_{\bullet}}+\sum_{\hat{\bm{y}}\in\widehat{\mathcal{Y}}_{\bullet}\setminus\mathcal{Y}_{\bullet}}\bigg)\,\omega_{\hat{\bm{y}}}\sum_{\bar{\bm{y}},\bar{\bm{y}}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Y}_{\bullet}}B_{\hat{\bm{y}}}(u_{\bullet\bar{\bm{y}}},z_{\bullet\bar{\bm{y}}^{\prime}})\,L_{\bullet\bar{\bm{y}}}(\hat{\bm{y}})L_{\bullet\bar{\bm{y}}^{\prime}}(\hat{\bm{y}}),

where we used the notation of the samplewise bilinear form (cf. (
4
)).
Hence, recalling that
L_{\bullet\bar{\bm{y}}}(\hat{\bm{y}})=\delta_{\bar{\bm{y}}\hat{\bm{y}}}
and

L_{\bullet\bar{\bm{y}}^{\prime}}(\hat{\bm{y}})=\delta_{\bar{\bm{y}}^{\prime}\hat{\bm{y}}}
for any
\bar{\bm{y}},\,\bar{\bm{y}}^{\prime},\,\hat{\bm{y}}\in\mathcal{Y}_{\bullet}
,
we obtain the following formula for (approximate) calculation of the bilinear form
\mathscr{B}(u_{\bullet}^{\rm SC},z_{\bullet}^{\rm SC})
:

\displaystyle\mathscr{B}(u_{\bullet}^{\rm SC},z_{\bullet}^{\rm SC})

\displaystyle\approx\sum_{\hat{\bm{y}}\in\widehat{\mathcal{Y}}_{\bullet}}\omega_{\hat{\bm{y}}}\,\widetilde{B}_{\hat{\bm{y}}}(u_{\bullet}^{\rm SC},z_{\bullet}^{\rm SC}),

where

\displaystyle\widetilde{B}_{\hat{\bm{y}}}(u_{\bullet}^{\rm SC},z_{\bullet}^{\rm SC}):=\begin{cases}B_{\hat{\bm{y}}}(u_{\bullet\hat{\bm{y}}},z_{\bullet\hat{\bm{y}}})&\quad\text{if $\hat{\bm{y}}\in\mathcal{Y}_{\bullet}$},\\[4.0pt]
\displaystyle\sum_{\bar{\bm{y}},\bar{\bm{y}}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Y}_{\bullet}}B_{\hat{\bm{y}}}(u_{\bullet\bar{\bm{y}}},z_{\bullet\bar{\bm{y}}^{\prime}})\,L_{\bullet\bar{\bm{y}}}(\hat{\bm{y}})L_{\bullet\bar{\bm{y}}^{\prime}}(\hat{\bm{y}})&\quad\text{if $\hat{\bm{y}}\in\widehat{\mathcal{Y}}_{\bullet}\setminus\mathcal{Y}_{\bullet}$}.\end{cases}

5.
Goal-oriented adaptive SC-FEM with nonlinear goal functionals

In this section, we aim to extend the error estimation strategy and adaptive algorithm presented in section
4
to a larger class of (possibly nonlinear) goal functionals.
Specifically, let the quantity of interest
Q(u(\cdot,\bm{y}))
be obtained using a functional
Q:\mathbb{X}\to\mathbb{R}
and assume that
Q\in C^{1}
,
i.e.,
Q
is Gâteaux differentiable and its Gâteaux derivative
Q^{\prime}:\mathbb{X}\to\mathbb{X}^{*}
is continuous.
As before, the goal functional
\mathscr{Q}:\mathscr{V}\to\mathbb{R}
is defined by (
7
).
Thus,
\mathscr{Q}
is also Gâteaux differentiable and its Gâteaux derivative
\mathscr{Q}^{\prime}:\mathscr{V}\to\mathscr{V}^{*}
is continuous.
We will denote by
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{D}
the duality pairing between
\mathbb{X}
and its dual
\mathbb{X}^{*}
;
similarly,
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{D\times\Gamma}
denotes the duality pairing between
\mathscr{V}
and
\mathscr{V}^{*}
.

5.1.
Dual problems and the dual SC-FEM approximation

Following the same approach as in §
4.1
and using the ideas from
[
8
]
,
let us first formulate dual problems that facilitate the goal-oriented error estimation.

Recall that for
\pi
-almost all
\bar{\bm{y}}\in\Gamma
,
u_{\bar{\bm{y}}}\in\mathbb{X}
denotes the samplewise solution satisfying (
3
),
and
u_{\bullet\bar{\bm{y}}}\in\mathbb{X}_{\bullet}
denotes its Galerkin finite element approximation satisfying (
8
).
According to the fundamental theorem of calculus there holds:

Q(u_{\bar{\bm{y}}})-Q(u_{\bullet\bar{\bm{y}}})=\int_{0}^{1}\langle Q^{\prime}(u_{\bullet\bar{\bm{y}}}+t(u_{\bar{\bm{y}}}-u_{\bullet\bar{\bm{y}}})),u_{\bar{\bm{y}}}-u_{\bullet\bar{\bm{y}}}\rangle_{D}\,\mathrm{d}t=:\langle Q^{*}_{u}(u_{\bullet\bar{\bm{y}}}),u_{\bar{\bm{y}}}-u_{\bullet\bar{\bm{y}}}\rangle_{D}.

Since formally speaking
\|Q^{*}_{u}(u_{\bullet\bar{\bm{y}}})-Q^{\prime}(u_{\bullet\bar{\bm{y}}})\|_{\mathbb{X}^{*}}\xrightarrow{}0
as

u_{\bullet\bar{\bm{y}}}\to u_{\bar{\bm{y}}}
, we consider the following
samplewise
dual formulation for
\pi
-almost all
\bar{\bm{y}}\in\Gamma

(cf. (
18
)):
find
z_{\bar{\bm{y}}}(x):=z(x,\bar{\bm{y}})\in\mathbb{X}
satisfying

(38)

\displaystyle B_{\bar{\bm{y}}}(v,z_{\bar{\bm{y}}})=\langle Q^{\prime}(u_{\bullet\bar{\bm{y}}}),v\rangle_{D}\quad\forall v\in\mathbb{X}.

This formulation and the definition of the goal functional
\mathscr{Q}:\mathscr{V}\to\mathbb{R}
in (
7
) yield the
following
combined
dual formulation (cf. (
19
)):
find
z\in\mathscr{V}
such that

(39)

\mathscr{B}(v,z)=\langle\mathscr{Q}^{\prime}(u),v\rangle_{D\times\Gamma}\quad\text{for all}\;v\in\mathscr{V}.

The dual formulations in (
38
) and (
39
) are well-posed due to the Lax–Milgram lemma.

In the same way as we did for the
samplewise
dual formulation in §
4.1
,
we discretize (
38
) at each collocation point
\bar{\bm{y}}\in\mathcal{Y}_{\bullet}
by the Galerkin FEM and
build the Lagrange interpolant out of the resulting Galerkin approximations.
Specifically, for each
\bar{\bm{y}}\in\mathcal{Y}_{\bullet}
, we denote by
z_{\bullet\bar{\bm{y}}}\in\mathbb{X}_{\bullet}
the Galerkin finite element approximation satisfying

(40)

\displaystyle B_{\bar{\bm{y}}}(v,z_{\bullet\bar{\bm{y}}})=\langle Q^{\prime}(u_{\bullet\bar{\bm{y}}}),v\rangle_{D}\quad\forall v\in\mathbb{X}_{\bullet}.

Given the set
\big\{z_{\bullet\bar{\bm{y}}}(x):\bar{\bm{y}}\in\mathcal{Y}_{\bullet}\big\}
,
we construct the dual SC-FEM approximation
z_{\bullet}^{\rm SC}
as in (
21
).

Now we can define the approximation of
\mathscr{Q}(u)
in the general case considered here.
In fact, we will use the same definition as in the case of linear goal functionals (cf. (
29
)):

\mathscr{Q}(u)\approx\widetilde{\mathscr{Q}}(u_{\bullet}^{\rm SC},z_{\bullet}^{\rm SC}):=\mathscr{Q}(u_{\bullet}^{\rm SC})+\mathscr{B}(u-u_{\bullet}^{\rm SC},z_{\bullet}^{\rm SC}).

5.2.
Goal-oriented error estimation

In order to estimate the approximation error
\big|\mathscr{Q}(u)-\widetilde{\mathscr{Q}}(u_{\bullet}^{\rm SC},z_{\bullet}^{\rm SC})\big|
,
we assume that there exists a constant
C_{\text{goal}}\geq 0
such that

(41)

|\langle\mathscr{Q}^{\prime}(v)-\mathscr{Q}^{\prime}(w),z\rangle_{D\times\Gamma}|\leq C_{\text{goal}}\,\big|\!\big|\!\big|\,v-w\,\big|\!\big|\!\big|{}\big|\!\big|\!\big|\,z\,\big|\!\big|\!\big|{}\quad\text{for all }v,w,z\in\mathscr{V}.

Furthermore, analogously to (
10
), we assume that there exists a constant
q_{\rm sat}\in(0,1)
independent of discretization parameters such that

(42)

\|z-\widehat{z}_{\bullet}^{\rm\,SC}\|\leq q_{\rm sat}\|z-z_{\bullet}^{\rm SC}\|,

where

z\in\mathscr{V}
solves (
39
),
the dual SC-FEM approximation
z_{\bullet}^{\rm SC}
is defined as described in §
5.1
above, and

\widehat{z}_{\bullet}^{\rm\,SC}
is the enhanced approximation of
z_{\bullet}^{\rm SC}
constructed as described in §
3.3
.
Then, similarly to (
11
) and (
26
), we obtain

(43)

\|z-z_{\bullet}^{\rm SC}\|\leq\big(1-q_{\rm sat}\big)^{-1}\,\big(\eta_{\bullet}+\sigma_{\bullet}\big),

where
\eta_{\bullet}
and
\sigma_{\bullet}
are defined by (
22
)
(e.g.,
\eta_{\bullet}=\mu_{\bullet}[z]
, that is
\eta_{\bullet}
is given by the right-hand side of (
12
)
with
u
replaced by
z
;
in particular,
\eta_{\bullet}
employs the enhanced samplewise Galerkin approximations
\widehat{z}_{\bullet\bar{\bm{y}}}

that satisfy (
40
) with
\mathbb{X}_{\bullet}
replaced by
\widehat{\mathbb{X}}_{\bullet}
).

We are now ready to prove the goal-oriented a posteriori error estimate.

Theorem 4
.

Suppose the saturation assumptions (
10
) and (
42
) hold for the
primal solution
u
and for the dual solution
z
, respectively.
In addition, assume that the goal functional
\mathscr{Q}
satisfies (
41
).
Then, the following error estimate holds:

(44)

|\mathscr{Q}(u)-\widetilde{\mathscr{Q}}(u_{\bullet}^{\rm SC},z_{\bullet}^{\rm SC})|\lesssim(\mu_{\bullet}+\tau_{\bullet})(\mu_{\bullet}+\tau_{\bullet}+\eta_{\bullet}+\sigma_{\bullet}),

where
\mu_{\bullet},\;\tau_{\bullet},\;\eta_{\bullet},\;\sigma_{\bullet}

are defined in (
12
), (
13
), (
22
) and
the hidden constant depends only on

a_{\max}>0
in (
2
),
C_{\rm goal}\geq 0
in (
41
),
and
q_{\rm sat}\in[0,1)
in (
10
), (
42
).

Proof.

The fundamental theorem of calculus implies that

\displaystyle\mathscr{Q}(u)

\displaystyle\,{-}\,\mathscr{Q}(u_{\bullet}^{\rm SC})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\phantom{\eqref{eq:weak:dual:nl}}}}{{=}}\int_{0}^{1}\langle\mathscr{Q}^{\prime}(u_{\bullet}^{\rm SC}+t(u-u_{\bullet}^{\rm SC})),u-u_{\bullet}^{\rm SC}\rangle_{D\times\Gamma}\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\phantom{\eqref{eq:weak:dual:nl}}}}{{=}}\int_{0}^{1}\langle\mathscr{Q}^{\prime}(u_{\bullet}^{\rm SC}+t(u-u_{\bullet}^{\rm SC}))-\mathscr{Q}^{\prime}(u),u-u_{\bullet}^{\rm SC}\rangle_{D\times\Gamma}\,\mathrm{d}t\,+\,\langle\mathscr{Q}^{\prime}(u),u-u_{\bullet}^{\rm SC}\rangle_{D\times\Gamma}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:weak:dual:nl}}}{{=}}\int_{0}^{1}\langle\mathscr{Q}^{\prime}(u_{\bullet}^{\rm SC}+t(u-u_{\bullet}^{\rm SC}))-\mathscr{Q}^{\prime}(u),u-u_{\bullet}^{\rm SC}\rangle_{D\times\Gamma}\,\mathrm{d}t\,+\,\mathscr{B}(u-u_{\bullet}^{\rm SC},z).

Therefore,

\displaystyle\mathscr{Q}(u)-\widetilde{\mathscr{Q}}(u_{\bullet}^{\rm SC},z_{\bullet}^{\rm SC})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:goal:approx}}}{{=}}\mathscr{Q}(u)-\mathscr{Q}(u_{\bullet}^{\rm SC})-\mathscr{B}(u-u_{\bullet}^{\rm SC},z_{\bullet}^{\rm SC})

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\langle\mathscr{Q}^{\prime}(u_{\bullet}^{\rm SC}+t(u-u_{\bullet}^{\rm SC}))-\mathscr{Q}^{\prime}(u),u-u_{\bullet}^{\rm SC}\rangle_{D\times\Gamma}\,\mathrm{d}t\,+\,\mathscr{B}(u-u_{\bullet}^{\rm SC},z-z_{\bullet}^{\rm SC}).

Using inequality (
41
), we obtain

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\langle\mathscr{Q}^{\prime}(u_{\bullet}^{\rm SC}+t(u-

\displaystyle u_{\bullet}^{\rm SC}))-\mathscr{Q}^{\prime}(u),u-u_{\bullet}^{\rm SC}\rangle_{D\times\Gamma}\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\leq C_{\text{goal}}\int_{0}^{1}(1-t)\,\big|\!\big|\!\big|\,u-u_{\bullet}^{\rm SC}\,\big|\!\big|\!\big|{}^{2}\;\mathrm{d}t=\frac{1}{2}C_{\text{goal}}\big|\!\big|\!\big|\,u-u_{\bullet}^{\rm SC}\,\big|\!\big|\!\big|{}^{2}

and, hence, we derive the following estimate of the error in the goal functional:

(45)

|\mathscr{Q}(u)-\widetilde{\mathscr{Q}}(u_{\bullet}^{\rm SC},z_{\bullet}^{\rm SC})|\leq\frac{1}{2}C_{\text{goal}}\,\big|\!\big|\!\big|\,u-u_{\bullet}^{\rm SC}\,\big|\!\big|\!\big|{}^{2}+\big|\!\big|\!\big|\,u-u_{\bullet}^{\rm SC}\,\big|\!\big|\!\big|{}\big|\!\big|\!\big|\,z-z_{\bullet}^{\rm SC}\,\big|\!\big|\!\big|{}.

Finally, using the norm equivalence (
6
) and
the a posteriori error estimates (
11
) and (
43
),
we conclude from (
45
) that

|\mathscr{Q}(u)-\widetilde{\mathscr{Q}}(u_{\bullet}^{\rm SC},z_{\bullet}^{\rm SC})|\lesssim(\mu_{\bullet}+\tau_{\bullet})^{2}+(\mu_{\bullet}+\tau_{\bullet})(\eta_{\bullet}+\sigma_{\bullet}).

This implies the required error estimate (
44
).
∎

Remark 5
.

Assumption (
41
)
holds true at least for linear and quadratic goal functionals.
Indeed, if
\mathscr{Q}\in\mathscr{V}^{*}
, then
\langle\mathscr{Q}^{\prime}(w),v\rangle_{D\times\Gamma}=g(v)
for all
v,\,w\in\mathscr{V}
and for some
g\in\mathscr{V}^{*}
.
Consequently, in this case,
the dual problem in (
39
) reduces to the dual formulation in (
19
). Moreover, inequality (
41
) is satisfied with
C_{\text{goal}}=0
, and
the error estimates (
44
) and (
45
)
reduce to the estimates (
30
) obtained in the case of linear goal functionals.
Furthermore, for the quadratic goal functional
\mathscr{Q}(u)=b(u,u)
, where
b:\mathscr{V}\times\mathscr{V}\to\mathbb{R}
is a
continuous bilinear form, we have

\langle\mathscr{Q}^{\prime}(v)-\mathscr{Q}^{\prime}(w),z\rangle_{D\times\Gamma}=b(v-w,z)+b(z,v-w)\quad\forall\,v,\,w,\,z\in\mathscr{V}.

Therefore, inequality (
41
) is valid in this case with
C_{\text{goal}}
depending
only on the continuity constant for
b(\cdot,\cdot)
.

5.3.
Modifications to the adaptive algorithm

In the light of the developments in §
5.1
and §
5.2
,
we need to replace the stopping criterion in step (vii) of Algorithm
2

(according to Theorem
4
, the new stopping criterion is given by

(\mu_{\ell}+\tau_{\ell})\,(\mu_{\ell}+\tau_{\ell}+\eta_{\ell}+\sigma_{\ell})<{\tt tol}
)
and we need to make two further modifications to Algorithm
2
.
Firstly, the samplewise dual Galerkin approximations

\big\{z_{\ell\bar{\bm{y}}}\in\mathbb{X}_{\ell}:\bar{\bm{y}}\in\mathcal{Y}_{\Lambda_{\ell}\cup{\rm R}(\Lambda_{\ell})}\big\}

are now computed by solving (
40
)
instead of (
20
); see step (i) of Algorithm
2
.
As a consequence, the corresponding local spatial error indicators are defined as follows (cf. (
24
)):

(46)

\eta_{\ell\bar{\bm{y}}}(\xi):=\frac{\big|\langle Q^{\prime}(u_{\ell\bar{\bm{y}}}),\widehat{\varphi}_{\ell,\xi}\rangle_{D}-B_{\bar{\bm{y}}}(\widehat{\varphi}_{\ell,\xi},z_{\ell\bar{\bm{y}}})\big|}{\|\widehat{\varphi}_{\ell,\xi}\|_{\mathbb{X}}}\quad\forall\,\xi\in\mathcal{N}_{\ell}^{+},\ \ \forall\,\bar{\bm{y}}\in\mathcal{Y}_{\ell}.

We also emphasize the fact that in the case of nonlinear goal functionals,
the right-hand side of the samplewise discrete dual problem (
40
)
depends on a sample of the primal Galerkin solution.
Therefore, unlike in the linear case, the samplewise primal and dual discrete formulations (
8
)
and (
40
) are not independent of each other.
Consequently, the samplewise Galerkin approximations
u_{\ell\bar{\bm{y}}}
and
z_{\ell\bar{\bm{y}}}
for each
\bar{\bm{y}}\in\mathcal{Y}_{\ell}

need to be computed sequentially: first the primal approximation
u_{\ell\bar{\bm{y}}}
satisfying (
8
)
and then the dual approximation
z_{\ell\bar{\bm{y}}}
satisfying (
40
).

The second modification to Algorithm
2
concerns the marking criterion.
In view of the error estimate in (
44
), the adaptive algorithm should employ a modified marking criterion
that ensures a reduction of either the primal error estimate
(\mu_{\ell}+\tau_{\ell})

or the combined primal-dual error estimate
(\mu_{\ell}+\tau_{\ell}+\eta_{\ell}+\sigma_{\ell})
.
However, for identifying the refinement type (spatial versus parametric),
we can still use the quantity in (
31
) as in the case of linear goal functionals.
This is now motivated by the following inequality (cf. (
4.2
)):

\displaystyle\big(\mu_{\ell}+\tau_{\ell}\big)\big(\mu_{\ell}+\tau_{\ell}+\eta_{\ell}+\sigma_{\ell}\big)

\displaystyle\leq\big(\bar{\mu}_{\ell}+\bar{\tau}_{\ell}\big)^{2}+\big(\bar{\mu}_{\ell}+\bar{\tau}_{\ell}\big)\big(\bar{\eta}_{\ell}+\bar{\sigma}_{\ell}\big)

\displaystyle\leq\big(3\bar{\mu}_{\ell}^{2}+\bar{\eta}_{\ell}^{2}\big)+\big(3\bar{\tau}_{\ell}^{2}+\bar{\sigma}_{\ell}^{2}\big)\leq 3\big[\big(\bar{\mu}_{\ell}^{2}+\bar{\eta}_{\ell}^{2}\big)+\big(\bar{\tau}_{\ell}^{2}+\bar{\sigma}_{\ell}^{2}\big)\big].

The following algorithm specifies the required marking criterion to be used in step (iv)
of Algorithm
2
in the case of the nonlinear goal functional
\mathscr{Q}
.

Algorithm 6
.

Input:

error indicators

\{\mu_{\ell\bar{\bm{y}}}(\xi):\bar{\bm{y}}\in\mathcal{Y}_{\ell},\,\xi\in\mathcal{N}_{\ell}^{+}\}
,

\{\mu_{\ell\bar{\bm{y}}}:\bar{\bm{y}}\in\mathcal{Y}_{\ell}\}
,

\{\widetilde{\tau}_{\ell\bm{\nu}}:\bm{\nu}\in{\rm R}({\Lambda_{\ell})}\}

and

\{\eta_{\ell\bar{\bm{y}}}(\xi):\bar{\bm{y}}\in\mathcal{Y}_{\ell},\,\xi\in\mathcal{N}_{\ell}^{+}\}
,

\{\eta_{\ell\bar{\bm{y}}}:\bar{\bm{y}}\in\mathcal{Y}_{\ell}\}
,

\{\widetilde{\sigma}_{\ell\bm{\nu}}:\bm{\nu}\in{\rm R}({\Lambda_{\ell})}\}
;
marking parameters
0<\theta_{\mathbb{X}},\theta_{\mathcal{Y}}\leq 1
.

\bullet

Calculate the cumulative error indicators

\bar{\mu}_{\ell},\,\bar{\tau}_{\ell},\,\bar{\eta}_{\ell},\,\bar{\sigma}_{\ell}

given by (
17
) and (
27
).

\bullet

If
\bar{\mu}_{\ell}^{2}+\bar{\eta}_{\ell}^{2}\geq\bar{\tau}_{\ell}^{2}+\bar{\sigma}_{\ell}^{2}
,
then proceed as follows:

\circ

set
\Upsilon_{\ell}:=\emptyset
;

\circ

for each
\bar{\bm{y}}\in\mathcal{Y}_{\ell}
,
determine
\mathcal{M}^{(1)}_{\ell\bar{\bm{y}}}\subseteq\mathcal{N}_{\ell}^{+}
and
\mathcal{M}^{(2)}_{\ell\bar{\bm{y}}}\subseteq\mathcal{N}_{\ell}^{+}
such that

(47)

\theta_{\mathbb{X}}\,\sum_{\bar{\bm{y}}\in\mathcal{Y}_{\ell}}\sum_{\xi\in\mathcal{N}_{\ell}^{+}}\mu_{\ell\bar{\bm{y}}}(\xi)\|L_{\ell\bar{\bm{y}}}\|_{L^{2}_{\pi}(\Gamma)}\leq\sum_{\bar{\bm{y}}\in\mathcal{Y}_{\ell}}\sum_{\xi\in\mathcal{M}^{(1)}_{\ell\bar{\bm{y}}}}\mu_{\ell\bar{\bm{y}}}(\xi)\|L_{\ell\bar{\bm{y}}}\|_{L^{2}_{\pi}(\Gamma)},

\displaystyle\theta_{\mathbb{X}}\,\sum_{\bar{\bm{y}}\in\mathcal{Y}_{\ell}}\sum_{\xi\in\mathcal{N}_{\ell}^{+}}(\mu_{\ell\bar{\bm{y}}}(\xi)+\eta_{\ell\bar{\bm{y}}}(\xi))\|L_{\ell\bar{\bm{y}}}\|_{L^{2}_{\pi}(\Gamma)}

(48)

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\leq\sum_{\bar{\bm{y}}\in\mathcal{Y}_{\ell}}\sum_{\xi\in\mathcal{M}^{(2)}_{\ell\bar{\bm{y}}}}(\mu_{\ell\bar{\bm{y}}}(\xi)+\eta_{\ell\bar{\bm{y}}}(\xi))\|L_{\ell\bar{\bm{y}}}\|_{L^{2}_{\pi}(\Gamma)}

with cumulative cardinalities

7.
Concluding remarks

Surrogate approximations of solutions to PDEs with uncertain inputs play an important role in reliable uncertainty quantification.
They can be cheaply evaluated at any point in the parameter domain and are used to estimate a wide range of QoIs,
thus, providing an effective alternative to Monte Carlo simulations.
While being effective in approximating the input-output map and QoIs, surrogate approximations introduce numerical errors that
need to be estimated and controlled in the course of simulations.
In this work, we have proved reliable a posteriori estimates for the errors associated with computing QoIs in the SC-FEM context.
Furthermore, we have designed a goal-oriented adaptive algorithm for reducing these errors.

The key features of our algorithm are its foundations in rigorous a posteriori analysis and its use of

adaptively refined
(in contrast to
a priori chosen
) approximation components.

The error analysis and the proposed adaptive algorithm can easily be extended to more general elliptic PDEs with random inputs
represented using a finite number of bounded random parameters;
all that is required is the well-posedness of the corresponding
samplewise weak formulation and the norm equivalence (cf. (
6
))
in appropriate function spaces.

The numerical experiments presented in this paper and more extensive experiments included in
[
35
]

demonstrate the robustness of our error estimation strategy and the effectiveness of the adaptive algorithm.
In particular, our conclusions hold for PDE problems with affine or nonaffine parametrizations of uncertainty in PDE inputs
and for a range of QoIs represented by linear or nonlinear goal functionals.

Adaptive algorithms for the sparse grid-based SC-FEM are particularly effective when combined with
dimensionality reduction techniques (see, e.g.,
[
21
,
29
]
)
and/or dimension adaptivity

(see, e.g.,
[
36
,
15
,
19
,
20
,
26
]
)
.

These techniques become critical when tackling PDE problems
with a large or countably infinite number of unbounded random parameters
(e.g., PDEs with inputs represented by lognormal random fields) that are not covered in this work.

The application of these techniques
in such a setting and
within the goal-oriented adaptive framework proposed in our work is
currently being investigated and will be the subject of a future publication.
