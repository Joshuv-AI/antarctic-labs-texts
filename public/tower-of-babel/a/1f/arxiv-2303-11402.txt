Phase transition in percolation games on rooted Galton-Watson trees
Sayar Karmakar, Moumanti Podder, Souvik Roy, Soumyarup Sadhukhan

Abstract
. We study the bond percolation game and the site percolation game on the rooted Galton-Watson tree T χ T_{\chi} with offspring distribution χ \chi . We obtain the probabilities of win, loss and draw for each player in terms of the fixed points of functions that involve the probability generating function G G of χ \chi , and the parameters p p and q q . Here, p p is the probability with which each edge (respectively vertex) of T χ T_{\chi} is labeled a trap in the bond (respectively site) percolation game, and q q is the probability with which each edge (respectively vertex) of T χ T_{\chi} is labeled a target in the bond (respectively site) percolation game. We obtain a necessary and sufficient condition for the probability of draw to be 0 0 in each game, and we examine how this condition simplifies to yield very precise phase transition results when χ \chi is Binomial ( d , π ) (d,\pi) , Poisson ( λ ) (\lambda) , or Negative Binomial ( r , π ) (r,\pi) , or when χ \chi is supported on { 0 , d } \{0,d\} for some d ∈ ℕ d\in\mathbb{N} , d ⩾ 2 d\geqslant 2 . It is fascinating to note that, while all other specific classes of offspring distributions we consider in this paper exhibit phase transition phenomena as the parameter-pair ( p , q ) (p,q) varies, the probability that the bond percolation game results in a draw remains 0 0 for all values of ( p , q ) (p,q) when χ \chi is Geometric ( π ) (\pi) , for all 0 < π ⩽ 1 0<\pi\leqslant 1 . By establishing a connection between these games and certain finite state probabilistic tree automata on rooted d d -regular trees, we obtain a precise description of the regime (in terms of p p , q q and d d ) in which these automata exhibit ergodicity or weak spatial mixing .

Introduction

1.1.
Overview of the paper

The work accomplished in this paper is the result of our curiosity regarding
percolation games
on rooted trees, both deterministic and random. Whereas
percolation
itself has been thoroughly researched for decades by both mathematicians and physicists (see, for instance,
[
11
]
,
[
18
]
and
[
29
]
), the notion of percolation games – more specifically,

or
site percolation games
– to the best of our knowledge, was introduced in
[
20
]
. In this paper, we address both site percolation games and
bond percolation games
. The latter is analogous, in some sense, to the former, but with the edges of the graph under consideration replacing the vertices in their roles in the game (see §
1.2
for a detailed description of each game).

In this paper, each of these games is played, and the probabilities of their various outcomes analyzed, on a
rooted Galton-Watson
(henceforth abbreviated as GW) tree
T_{\chi}
, with root
\phi
and offspring distribution
\chi
. We impose no assumption on
\chi
whatsoever, except that the probability of the event that
\phi
has no child is strictly less than
1
. We assign, to each edge (respectively, each vertex) of
T_{\chi}
, independently, a label that reads
trap
with probability
p
,
target
with probability
q
, and
safe
with probability
1-p-q
, where
0<p+q<1
, following which two players take turns to move a token along the edges of
T_{\chi}
(always moving
away
from the root). A player wins the bond percolation game (respectively, the site percolation game) if she is able to move the token
along
an edge (respectively,
to
a vertex) that has been labeleed a target, or force her opponent to move the token along an edge (respectively, to a vertex) that has been labeled a trap. We deduce a necessary and sufficient condition for the probability of draw to be
0
in each of these games when they are played on
T_{\chi}
for
any

\chi
, following which we consider specific classes of probability distributions in place of
\chi
. This allows us to gain further insight into the
regimes
, in terms of the underlying parameters, in which neither of the games results in a draw with positive probability.

The two primary sources of inspiration for this paper are
[
20
]
and
[
19
]
. In the former, each site
(x,y)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}
of the
2
-dimensional square lattice is assigned, independently, a label that reads
trap
with probability
p
,
target
with probability
q
and
open
with probability
1-p-q
, where

and

are pre-fixed parameters such that

. Two players take turns to make moves, where a move involves relocating a token from its current position, say the site
(x,y)
, to one of the sites
(x+1,y)
and
(x,y+1)
. A player wins if she either succeeds in moving the token to a site that has been labeled a target, or forces her opponent to move the token to a site that has been labeled a trap. The game continues for as long as the token stays on open sites, and this may happen indefinitely, thereby leading to a draw. In
[
20
]
, it is shown, exploiting the connection between this site percolation game and a suitably defined
probabilistic cellular automaton
(henceforth abbreviated as PCA), and using the technique of

or

In
[
19
]
,
normal
,
misère
and

T_{\chi}
. In each of these games, two players take turns to slide a token along the edges of
T_{\chi}
, always moving
from
the parent vertex
to
the child vertex. A player loses the normal game if she is unable to make a move (which happens when the token is stagnated at a leaf vertex of
T_{\chi}
). A player loses the misère game if her opponent is unable to make a move. In the escape game, the player titled

wins if

of the two players is unable to make a move, else her opponent, titled

, wins. The probabilities of win and loss (and draw, in case of the normal and misère games) for each player are derived in terms of the fixed points of certain functions that involve the
probability generating function

\chi

1.2.
A formal introduction to the games

In this paper, we study the site percolation game (inspired by the version studied in
[
20
]
, as described above) and the bond percolation game on the rooted GW tree
T_{\chi}
with offspring distribution
\chi
. We assume that
\chi
is supported on
\mathbb{N}_{0}
(which ensures that
\chi
takes only finite, non-negative integer values), and that
0\leqslant\chi(0)<1
(here,
\chi(m)
denotes the probability that a random variable, that follows the distribution
\chi
, takes the value
m
, for any
m\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
). To begin with, we recall for the reader that
T_{\chi}
is generated as follows. The root
\phi
is allowed to have a random number
X
of children according to the law
\chi
. If
X
takes the value
0
, the process stops and
T_{\chi}
comprises only the root
\phi
. Otherwise, conditioned on
X=m
for any
m\in\mathbb{N}
that belongs to the support of
\chi
, we name the children of the root
u_{1},u_{2},\ldots,u_{m}
. We let
u_{i}
have
X_{i}
children for
1\leqslant i\leqslant m
, where
X_{1},X_{2},\ldots,X_{m}
are independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.)
\chi
. We continue thus, and the rooted random tree thus generated is denoted by
T_{\chi}
. It has a positive probability of surviving, i.e. of being infinite, if and only if the expectation of
\chi
is strictly greater than
1
. For an overview of the GW branching process and its many applications, we refer the reader to
[
3
]
.

We visualize
T_{\chi}
as a directed graph, as follows: given any edge
\{u,v\}
of
T_{\chi}
, connecting vertices
u
and
v
where
u
is the parent of
v
, we let
(u,v)
denote the directed edge that travels
from

u

towards

v
. Next, we choose and fix a pair
(p,q)
of parameters from the set

(1.1)

I=\left\{(p,q)\in[0,1]^{2}:0<p+q<1\right\}.

Each game involves a token, and two players that we henceforth refer to as
A
and
B
. In each game,
A
and
B
take turns to make moves, where a move involves relocating the token along a directed edge of
T_{\chi}
(i.e. if the token is currently situated at some vertex
u
of
T_{\chi}
, the player whose turn it is to move next is permitted to move the token to any child of
u
, if such a child exists).

(i)

In case of the bond percolation game, each edge of
T_{\chi}
is assigned, independently, a label that reads
trap
with probability
p
,
target
with probability
q
, and
safe
with the remaining probability
1-p-q
. A player wins this game if she is able to move the token along a directed edge that has been labeled a target, or force her opponent to move the token along a directed edge that has been labeled a trap, or confine her opponent to a leaf vertex (from where her opponent cannot move the token any further).

(ii)

In case of the site percolation game, each vertex of
T_{\chi}
is assigned, independently, a label that reads trap with probability
p
, target with probability
q
, and safe with the remaining probability
1-p-q
. A player wins this game if she is able to move the token to a vertex that has been labeled a target, or force her opponent to move the token to a vertex that has been labeled a trap or that is a leaf vertex that has been labeled safe.

Once a realization of
T_{\chi}
, followed by a realization of the random assignment of trap / target / safe labels (be it to the edges of this realization of
T_{\chi}
or to the vertices), has been generated, it is revealed in its entirety to both
A
and
B
– this is what makes each of these games a
perfect information
game. A game that continues for eternity is said to result in a draw. In case of the bond percolation game, this happens if the token traverses
only
along edges that have been labeled safe, whereas in case of the site percolation game, this happens if the token
only ever
visits vertices that have been labeled safe. We assume both players to play
optimally
, i.e. if the game is destined to end in a finite number of rounds, then the player who wins tries to wrap up the game as quickly as possible, whereas the player who loses tries to prolong its duration as much as possible.

1.3.
The main results of this paper

For each of the two games described above, we investigate the probabilities of the various possible outcomes, i.e. win for player
A
, win for player
B
, and draw for both the players. Of particular interest is the phenomenon of
phase transition
pertaining to the probability of draw: in certain
regimes
, i.e. for certain values of the parameter-pair
(p,q)
(that depend on the offspring distribution
\chi
under consideration), the probability of the event that the game results in a draw is
0
, while for other regimes, this is strictly positive. We find a necessary and sufficient condition for the probability of draw to be
0
, then examine what this condition reduces to when we consider specific classes of probability distributions in place of the offspring distribution
\chi
.

In what follows, we let
G
denote the probability generating function of
\chi
, i.e.
G(x)=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}x^{m}\chi(m)
for all
x\in[0,1]
. We define the function
g_{p,q}:[0,1]\rightarrow[0,1]
as

(1.2)

\displaystyle g_{p,q}(x)=(1-q)-(1-p-q)G(x),\text{ for }x\in[0,1].

Henceforth, given any function
f:S\rightarrow S
, where
S
is a subset of the real line
\mathbb{R}
, we let
f^{(n)}
denote the
n
-fold composition of
f
with itself, for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
n\geqslant 2
. In what follows, we consider the fixed points of the function
g_{p,q}^{(2)}
in the interval
[0,1]
. We note that, since
0\leqslant g_{p,q}^{(2)}(x)\leqslant 1
for each
x\in[0,1]
, hence
g_{p,q}^{(2)}(x)-x
is non-negative at
x=0
and non-positive at
x=1
, guaranteeing the existence of at least one fixed point in
[0,1]
by the intermediate value theorem (since
g_{p,q}^{(2)}
is a continuous function). Yet another way to argue that at least one fixed point of
g_{p,q}^{(2)}
exists in
[0,1]
is to employ Brouwer’s fixed-point theorem, which states that every continuous function from a convex compact subset of a Euclidean space to itself has a fixed point.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let

denote the probability of the event that the bond percolation game starting with the token at the root

, where
g_{p,q}
is as defined in (
1.2
),

(ii)

\ell^{\prime}=(1-p-q)\ell
equals
(1-q)-g_{p,q}(w^{\prime})
, and

(iii)

and the probability that this game results in a draw is
0
if and only if the function

has a
unique
fixed point in
[0,1]
.

Theorem 1.2

Let

It is evident, from (

Theorem 1.3
.

When played on

When played on

When

Our final main result deviates a little in flavour from the theorems stated above, and concerns itself with the average or expected duration of the bond percolation game on

Suppose the probability of the event that the bond percolation game, played on

It is undeniable that a tremendous motivation for studying percolation games arises from the classically studied topic of percolation in physics. It is the presence of players who play

d
-regular tree
T_{d}
, independently, is marked

(this corresponds to the label that reads trap in our set-up) with probability
p

(this corresponds to the label that reads safe in our set-up) with probability

, then for all
p<1-d^{-1}
, there exists an open path (i.e. a path that comprises
only
open edges) from the root
\phi
of
T_{d}
to infinity, i.e.
percolation happens
. However, the existence of such a path is
not
enough to guarantee that the bond percolation game, endowed with the parameter-pair
(p,q)=(p,0)
(i.e. no edge is labeled a target) and played on
T_{d}
, will have a strictly positive probability of resulting in a draw. This is why, as can be deduced from Remark
4.3
by setting
q=0
, we require
p<1-(d+1)^{d-1}d^{-d}
for our game to have a strictly positive probability of draw, where we note that

and that of

(likewise, between

\widetilde{L}_{n}
, and between

and

) – for

, the game that begins with the token at

n

, the game that begins with the token at
v
lasts for at most

We let
\widetilde{w}_{n}
,
\widetilde{w}
,
\widetilde{\ell}_{n}
and
\widetilde{\ell}

\phi
of
T_{\chi}
belonging to
\widetilde{W}_{n}
,
\widetilde{W}

For

, the root

\widetilde{W}_{n}
if and only if either
\phi
is itself a trap, or it is safe
and
has at least one child that belongs to
\widetilde{L}_{n-1}
. Consequently,

(3.1)

\displaystyle\widetilde{w}_{n}

\displaystyle=p+(1-p-q)\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\left\{1-\left(1-\widetilde{\ell}_{n-1}\right)^{m}\right\}\chi(m)=(1-q)-(1-p-q)G\left(1-\widetilde{\ell}_{n-1}\right).

The root
\phi
belongs to
\widetilde{L}_{n}
if and only if either
\phi
itself is a target, or it is safe
and
each of its children is in
W_{n-1}
, leading to

(3.2)

\displaystyle\widetilde{\ell}_{n}

\displaystyle=q+(1-p-q)\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\widetilde{w}_{n-1}^{m}\chi(m)=q+(1-p-q)G\left(\widetilde{w}_{n-1}\right).

Combining (
3.1
) and (
3.2
), and using (
1.2
), we obtain:
\widetilde{w}_{n+1}=g_{p,q}^{(2)}\left(\widetilde{w}_{n-1}\right)
, for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Arguing as in §
2.2
, we conclude that
\widetilde{w}=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\widetilde{w}_{n}
is the smallest positive fixed point of
g_{p,q}^{(2)}
, and (
3.2
)) yields
\widetilde{\ell}=q+(1-p-q)G(\widetilde{w})
. This completes the proofs of parts (
i
) and (
ii
) of Theorem
1.2
.

The probability of draw is
0
if and only if
\widetilde{w}+\widetilde{\ell}=1
, which is equivalent to
\widetilde{w}=g_{p,q}\left(\widetilde{w}\right)
. By Lemma
2.2
, we conclude that this is possible if and only if
g_{p,q}^{(2)}
has a unique fixed point in
[0,1]
, completing the proof of part (
iii
) of Theorem
1.2
. This, in turn, tells us that the set of values of the parameter-pair
(p,q)
for which the probability of draw in the bond percolation game is
0
is precisely the set of values of
(p,q)
for which the probability of draw in the site percolation game is
0
, when both are played on the rooted GW tree
T_{\chi}
.

3.1.
A different method for analyzing the site percolation game

The second approach for analyzing the site percolation game played on
T_{\chi}
relies on an intimate connection that this game bears with the bond percolation game played on
T_{\chi}
. We denote by
(T,\omega)
a realization
T
of
T_{\chi}
in which
\omega((u,v))
denotes the label (trap, target or safe) assigned to
(u,v)
for every directed edge
(u,v)
of
T
. Given a
(T,\omega)
, we define a (deterministic) assignment
\widetilde{\omega}
of trap / target / safe labels to the vertices of
T
, as follows: the root
\phi
of
T
is labeled safe under
\widetilde{\omega}
, and given any vertex
v
of
T
that is not the root
\phi
, letting
u
be the parent of
v
,

(i)

v
is labeled a trap under
\widetilde{\omega}
if the directed edge
(u,v)
has been labeled a trap under
\omega
,

(ii)

v
is labeled a target under
\widetilde{\omega}
if the directed edge
(u,v)
has been labeled a target under
\omega
,

(iii)

v
is labeled safe under
\widetilde{\omega}
if the directed edge
(u,v)
has been labeled safe under
\omega
.

The way we have constructed
\widetilde{\omega}
allows us to conclude that
\phi
belongs to
\widetilde{W}
under
\widetilde{\omega}
if and only if it belongs to
W
under
\omega
, and
\phi
belongs to
\widetilde{L}
under
\widetilde{\omega}
if and only if it belongs to
L
under
\omega
.

From above, we conclude that the event
\phi\in\widetilde{W}
(in the site percolation game) has the same probability as the union of the following two disjoint events:

(i)

\phi
has been labeled a trap, which happens with probability
p
,

(ii)

\phi
is safe and
\phi\in W
(in the bond percolation game), which happens with probability
(1-p-q)w
,

thus yielding
\widetilde{w}=p+(1-p-q)w=w^{\prime}
(where
w^{\prime}
is the transformation of
w
as defined in the statement of Theorem
1.1
). Likewise, the event
\phi\in\widetilde{L}
(in the site percolation game) has the same probability as the union of the following two disjoint event:

(i)

\phi
has been labeled a target, which happens with probability
q
,

(ii)

\phi
is safe and
\phi\in L
(in the bond percolation game), which happens with probability
(1-p-q)\ell
,

thus yielding
\widetilde{\ell}=q+(1-p-q)\ell=q+\ell^{\prime}=1-g_{p,q}(w^{\prime})=q+(1-p-q)G(\widetilde{w})
, from Theorem
1.1
and using (
1.2
) and the fact, established above, that
\widetilde{w}=w^{\prime}
. Finally, the event that
\phi\in\widetilde{D}
(in the site percolation game) is the same as the event that
\phi
is safe and
\phi\in D
(in the bond percolation game), thus implying that the probability of draw in the site percolation game is
0
if and only if the probability of draw in the bond percolation game is
0
. This completes the proofs of all parts of Theorem
1.2
.

4.
When
\chi
belongs to one of several specified classes of distributions

4.1.
A general outline for the proof techniques adopted in the subsequent subsections

In the sections that follow, we focus on the probability of draw in the bond percolation game played on
T_{\chi}
, where
\chi
is specified to be one of the most commonly studied probability distributions supported on
\mathbb{N}_{0}
. In each case, a set of rather
ad hoc
computations are necessary, but broadly speaking, the underlying argument remains the same, and this is what we outline in §
4.1
.

To begin with, we compute the first and second derivatives of the function
g_{p,q}^{(2)}
, with
g_{p,q}
as defined in (
1.2
) (with the probability generating function
G
now being that of the
\chi
under consideration), and in each case, we find that there exists some unique
x_{p,q}>0
such that

(i)

g_{p,q}^{(2)}
is strictly convex, and equivalently, its derivative is strictly increasing, for
0\leqslant x<x_{p,q}
,

(ii)

g_{p,q}^{(2)}
is strictly concave, and equivalently, its derivative is strictly decreasing, for
x>x_{p,q}
.

The maximum of the derivative of
g_{p,q}^{(2)}(x)
, for
x>0
, is thus attained at
x=x_{p,q}
. It is important to note here (and it will be emphasized in the subsequent sections as well) that the probability generating function
G
of
\chi
may be defined on a domain
\mathcal{D}\subset\mathbb{R}^{+}
that is possibly larger than the interval
[0,1]
(i.e.
[0,1]\subset\mathcal{D}
), but
x_{p,q}
will always be contained inside
\mathcal{D}
. We can thus write

(4.1)

\max_{x\in D}\frac{d}{dx}g_{p,q}^{(2)}(x)=\frac{d}{dx}g_{p,q}^{(2)}(x)\Big|_{x=x_{p,q}}.

Next, we deduce the condition (involving
p
,
q
and the underlying parameters of the
\chi
under consideration) that is equivalent to

(4.2)

\frac{d}{dx}g_{p,q}^{(2)}(x)\big|_{x=x_{p,q}}\leqslant 1.

Note that when (
4.2
) holds, the derivative of
g_{p,q}^{(2)}
, and hence the slope of the curve
y=g_{p,q}^{(2)}(x)
, is bounded above by
1
throughout
\mathcal{D}
, and hence throughout
[0,1]
. This gives rise to the following lemma:

Lemma 4.1
.

As long as (
4.2
) holds, the function
g_{p,q}^{(2)}
has a unique fixed point in
[0,1]
.

Proof.

As established via (
2.6
) and (
2.7
), the curve
y=g_{p,q}^{(2)}(x)
lies above
y=x
at
x=0
. Since
w^{\prime}
is the smallest positive fixed point of
g_{p,q}^{(2)}
, from (
i
) of Theorem
1.1
, it must be the case that the curve
y=g_{p,q}^{(2)}(x)
travels from
above

y=x
to
beneath

y=x
at
x=w^{\prime}
. If there exists yet another fixed point
\beta\in(0,1)
of
g_{p,q}^{(2)}
, then
w^{\prime}<\beta
. Let us assume that there exists no fixed point of
g_{p,q}^{(2)}
in the sub-interval
(w^{\prime},\beta)
. This means that the curve
y=g_{p,q}^{(2)}(x)
must travel from
beneath

y=x
to
above

y=x
at
x=\beta
, and therefore, the slope of
y=g_{p,q}^{(2)}(x)
must exceed the slope of
y=x
at
x=\beta
. But the slope of
y=x
equals
1
, and by (
4.2
), the slope of
y=g_{p,q}^{(2)}(x)
is bounded above by
1
. This leads to a contradiction, implying that the fixed point
\beta
cannot exist in this case.
∎

The next major step in the argument that is common to all the subsequent sections is showing that
w^{\prime}\leqslant x_{p,q}
for
all
values of
(p,q)
in
I
, where
I
is as defined in (
1.1
). Recalling from (
2.6
) and (
2.7
) that the curve
y=g_{p,q}^{(2)}(x)
lies above
y=x
at
x=0
, and from (
i
) of Theorem
1.1
that
x=w^{\prime}
is the smallest positive value of
x
at which
y=g_{p,q}^{(2)}(x)
intersects
y=x
, it suffices to show, via the intermediate value theorem (which can be applied here because
g_{p,q}^{(2)}
is continuous), that
y=g_{p,q}^{(2)}(x)
lies
beneath
or
on

y=x
at
x=x_{p,q}
, i.e. that

(4.3)

g_{p,q}^{(2)}(x_{p,q})\leqslant x_{p,q}\text{ for all }(p,q)\in I.

The final step is to show that

(4.4)

x_{p,q}<\alpha_{p,q}\text{ whenever \eqref{max_derivative_GW_leq_1} does \emph{not} hold},

where we recall, from §
2.3
, that
\alpha_{p,q}
is the unique fixed point of
g_{p,q}
in
[0,1]
. From (
1.2
), it is evident that
g_{p,q}
is strictly decreasing on
[0,1]
, and the curve
y=g_{p,q}(x)
lies above the line
y=x
at
x=0
. Since
x=\alpha_{p,q}
is the first and only value of
x
(in
[0,1]
) where the curve
y=g_{p,q}(x)
intersects
y=x
, if we can show that

(4.5)

g_{p,q}(x_{p,q})>x_{p,q}\text{ whenever \eqref{max_derivative_GW_leq_1} does not hold},

then it would go to show that (
4.4
) is true.

Immediately, from (
4.3
) and (
4.4
), we conclude that
g_{p,q}^{(2)}
does
not
have a unique fixed point when (
4.2
) does not hold. This, along with Lemma
4.1
and part (
iii
) of Theorem
1.1
, allows us to conclude that

Theorem 4.2
.

The probability of draw in the bond percolation game played on
T_{\chi}
is
0
if and only if (
4.2
) holds, where
\chi
belongs to the list of probability distributions considered in the rest of this paper.

4.2.
The bond percolation game played on the Binomial GW tree

Let
\chi
be the Binomial
(d,\pi)
distribution, so that
\chi(i)={d\choose i}\pi^{i}(1-\pi)^{d-i}
for all
0\leqslant i\leqslant d
, for some
0<\pi\leqslant 1
and some
d\in\mathbb{N}
with
d\geqslant 2
. The corresponding generating function is
G(x)=\left\{\pi x+(1-\pi)\right\}^{d}
(note that this is well-defined for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
, i.e. in the context of our discussion in §
4.1
, we have
\mathcal{D}=\mathbb{R}^{+}
in this case). The function
g_{p,q}
now becomes

(4.6)

g_{p,q}(x)=(1-q)-(1-p-q)\left\{\pi x+(1-\pi)\right\}^{d},

so that

\displaystyle g^{\prime}_{p,q}(x)

\displaystyle=-(1-p-q)\pi d\left\{\pi x+(1-\pi)\right\}^{d-1}.

This, in turn, yields

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dx}g_{p,q}^{(2)}(x)

\displaystyle=g^{\prime}_{p,q}\left(g_{p,q}(x)\right)g^{\prime}_{p,q}(x)=(1-p-q)^{2}\pi^{2}d^{2}\left[\pi g_{p,q}(x)+(1-\pi)\right]^{d-1}\left\{\pi x+(1-\pi)\right\}^{d-1}

\displaystyle=(1-p-q)^{2}\pi^{2}d^{2}\left[\pi(1-q)-\pi(1-p-q)\left\{\pi x+(1-\pi)\right\}^{d}+(1-\pi)\right]^{d-1}\left\{\pi x+(1-\pi)\right\}^{d-1}

\displaystyle=(1-p-q)^{2}\pi^{2}d^{2}\left[\left\{1-\pi q-\pi(1-p-q)\left\{\pi x+(1-\pi)\right\}^{d}\right\}\left\{\pi x+(1-\pi)\right\}\right]^{d-1}

\displaystyle=(1-p-q)^{2}\pi^{2}d^{2}\left[(1-\pi q)\left\{\pi x+(1-\pi)\right\}-\pi(1-p-q)\left\{\pi x+(1-\pi)\right\}^{d+1}\right]^{d-1}.

Defining the function

f_{1}(x)=(1-\pi q)\left\{\pi x+(1-\pi)\right\}-\pi(1-p-q)\left\{\pi x+(1-\pi)\right\}^{d+1},

we see that

f^{\prime}_{1}(x)=\pi(1-\pi q)-(d+1)\pi^{2}(1-p-q)\left\{\pi x+(1-\pi)\right\}^{d},

which is strictly positive if and only if

\displaystyle(d+1)\pi^{2}(1-p-q)\left\{\pi x+(1-\pi)\right\}^{d}<\pi(1-\pi q)\Longleftrightarrow\left\{\pi x+(1-\pi)\right\}<\left(\frac{1-\pi q}{(d+1)\pi(1-p-q)}\right)^{\frac{1}{d}}

(4.7)

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow x<x_{p,q},\text{ where }x_{p,q}=\frac{1}{\pi}\left(\frac{1-\pi q}{(d+1)\pi(1-p-q)}\right)^{\frac{1}{d}}+1-\frac{1}{\pi}.

Following the reasoning outlined in §
4.1
, we know that the maximum of the derivative of
g_{p,q}^{(2)}(x)
, over
x\in[0,1]
(in fact, over
x\in\mathcal{D}=\mathbb{R}^{+}
), is attained at
x=x_{p,q}
, and this maximum value equals

\displaystyle(1-p-q)^{2}\pi^{2}d^{2}\left[1-\pi q-\pi(1-p-q)\left(\frac{1-\pi q}{(d+1)\pi(1-p-q)}\right)\right]^{d-1}\left(\frac{1-\pi q}{(d+1)\pi(1-p-q)}\right)^{\frac{d-1}{d}}

\displaystyle=(1-p-q)^{2}\pi^{2}d^{2}\left[1-\pi q-\frac{1-\pi q}{d+1}\right]^{d-1}\left(\frac{1-\pi q}{(d+1)\pi(1-p-q)}\right)^{\frac{d-1}{d}}

\displaystyle=(1-p-q)^{2}\pi^{2}d^{2}\cdot\frac{d^{d-1}(1-\pi q)^{d-1}}{(d+1)^{d-1}}\left(\frac{1-\pi q}{(d+1)\pi(1-p-q)}\right)^{\frac{d-1}{d}}

\displaystyle=(1-p-q)^{\frac{d+1}{d}}\pi^{\frac{d+1}{d}}d^{d+1}(1-\pi q)^{\frac{(d-1)(d+1)}{d}}(d+1)^{-\frac{(d-1)(d+1)}{d}}

\displaystyle=\left\{(1-p-q)^{\frac{1}{d}}\pi^{\frac{1}{d}}d(1-\pi q)^{\frac{d-1}{d}}(d+1)^{-\frac{d-1}{d}}\right\}^{d+1}.

The expression above is bounded above by
1
if and only if we have

(4.8)

\displaystyle(1-p-q)^{\frac{1}{d}}\pi^{\frac{1}{d}}d(1-\pi q)^{\frac{d-1}{d}}(d+1)^{-\frac{d-1}{d}}\leqslant 1\Longleftrightarrow(1-p-q)\pi(1-\pi q)^{d-1}\leqslant\frac{(d+1)^{d-1}}{d^{d}},

which is what the inequality in (
4.2
), mentioned in §
4.1
, boils down to when
\chi
is Binomial
(d,\pi)
. As argued in §
4.1
, the function
g_{p,q}^{(2)}
has a unique fixed point whenever (
4.8
) holds, and consequently, by part (
iii
) of Theorem
1.1
, the probability of draw in the bond percolation game is
0
in this regime.

As outlined in §
4.1
, our next task is to show that
w^{\prime}\leqslant x_{p,q}
, which we accomplish by showing that (
4.3
) holds, i.e. that
g_{p,q}^{(2)}(x_{p,q})\leqslant x_{p,q}
for all
(p,q)\in I
, with
I
as defined in (
1.1
). From (
4.6
) and (
4.7
), we see that

\displaystyle g_{p,q}(x_{p,q})

\displaystyle=(1-q)-(1-p-q)\left\{\pi x_{p,q}+(1-\pi)\right\}^{d}

(4.9)

\displaystyle=(1-q)-(1-p-q)\left(\frac{1-\pi q}{(d+1)\pi(1-p-q)}\right)=\frac{(d+1)\pi-1-d\pi q}{(d+1)\pi},

which leads to

\displaystyle\pi g_{p,q}(x_{p,q})+(1-\pi)

\displaystyle=\pi\cdot\frac{(d+1)\pi-1-d\pi q}{(d+1)\pi}+(1-\pi)=\frac{d(1-\pi q)}{d+1}.

Therefore, the condition
g_{p,q}^{(2)}(x_{p,q})\leqslant x_{p,q}
becomes equivalent to

\displaystyle(1-q)-(1-p-q)\left(\frac{d(1-\pi q)}{d+1}\right)^{d}\leqslant\frac{1}{\pi}\left(\frac{1-\pi q}{(d+1)\pi(1-p-q)}\right)^{\frac{1}{d}}+1-\frac{1}{\pi}

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow

\displaystyle(1-\pi q)-\pi(1-p-q)\left(\frac{d(1-\pi q)}{d+1}\right)^{d}\leqslant\left(\frac{1-\pi q}{(d+1)\pi(1-p-q)}\right)^{\frac{1}{d}}

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow

\displaystyle 1\leqslant\frac{\pi(1-p-q)d^{d}(1-\pi q)^{d-1}}{(d+1)^{d}}+\frac{(1-\pi q)^{-\frac{d-1}{d}}}{(d+1)^{\frac{1}{d}}\pi^{\frac{1}{d}}(1-p-q)^{\frac{1}{d}}}

(4.10)

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow

\displaystyle 1\leqslant\frac{\pi(1-p-q)d^{d}x^{-d}}{(d+1)^{d}}+\frac{x}{(d+1)^{\frac{1}{d}}\pi^{\frac{1}{d}}(1-p-q)^{\frac{1}{d}}},\text{ where }x=(1-\pi q)^{-\frac{d-1}{d}}.

Defining the function
f_{2}(x)=\pi(1-p-q)d^{d}x^{-d}(d+1)^{-d}+x(d+1)^{-\frac{1}{d}}\pi^{-\frac{1}{d}}(1-p-q)^{-\frac{1}{d}}
, we see that

\displaystyle f^{\prime}_{2}(x)

\displaystyle=-d\pi(1-p-q)d^{d}x^{-d-1}(d+1)^{-d}+(d+1)^{-\frac{1}{d}}\pi^{-\frac{1}{d}}(1-p-q)^{-\frac{1}{d}}

\displaystyle=-\pi(1-p-q)d^{d+1}x^{-d-1}(d+1)^{-d}+(d+1)^{-\frac{1}{d}}\pi^{-\frac{1}{d}}(1-p-q)^{-\frac{1}{d}},

and this is strictly positive if and only if

\displaystyle(d+1)^{-\frac{1}{d}}\pi^{-\frac{1}{d}}(1-p-q)^{-\frac{1}{d}}>\pi(1-p-q)d^{d+1}x^{-d-1}(d+1)^{-d}

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow

\displaystyle x^{d+1}>\pi^{\frac{d+1}{d}}(1-p-q)^{\frac{d+1}{d}}d^{d+1}(d+1)^{-\frac{(d-1)(d+1)}{d}}

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow

\displaystyle x>\pi^{\frac{1}{d}}(1-p-q)^{\frac{1}{d}}d(d+1)^{-\frac{d-1}{d}}.

This goes to show that the function
f_{2}
is strictly decreasing on
0\leqslant x<\pi^{\frac{1}{d}}(1-p-q)^{\frac{1}{d}}d(d+1)^{-\frac{d-1}{d}}
and strictly increasing on
x>\pi^{\frac{1}{d}}(1-p-q)^{\frac{1}{d}}d(d+1)^{-\frac{d-1}{d}}
, and its minimum is thus attained at
x=\pi^{\frac{1}{d}}(1-p-q)^{\frac{1}{d}}d(d+1)^{-\frac{d-1}{d}}
. This minimum value is given by

\displaystyle\pi(1-p-q)d^{d}\left\{\pi^{\frac{1}{d}}(1-p-q)^{\frac{1}{d}}d(d+1)^{-\frac{d-1}{d}}\right\}^{-d}(d+1)^{-d}+\left\{\pi^{\frac{1}{d}}(1-p-q)^{\frac{1}{d}}d(d+1)^{-\frac{d-1}{d}}\right\}

\displaystyle(d+1)^{-\frac{1}{d}}\pi^{-\frac{1}{d}}(1-p-q)^{-\frac{1}{d}}=\frac{1}{d+1}+\frac{d}{d+1}=1,

thus establishing that (
4.10
) does indeed hold for all values of
(p,q)\in I
.

The last step left, keeping to the argument outlined in §
4.1
, is to establish (
4.4
), i.e. to show that
x_{p,q}<\alpha_{p,q}
whenever (
4.8
) does not hold, and we prove this by showing that (
4.5
) holds, i.e. that
g_{p,q}(x_{p,q})>x_{p,q}
whenever (
4.8
) does not hold. From (
4.9
) and (
4.7
), the inequality
g_{p,q}(x_{p,q})>x_{p,q}
becomes equivalent to

\displaystyle 1-\frac{1}{(d+1)\pi}-\frac{dq}{d+1}>\frac{1}{\pi}\left(\frac{1-\pi q}{(d+1)\pi(1-p-q)}\right)^{\frac{1}{d}}+1-\frac{1}{\pi}

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow

\displaystyle-1-dq\pi>(d+1)\left(\frac{1-\pi q}{(d+1)\pi(1-p-q)}\right)^{\frac{1}{d}}-(d+1)

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow

\displaystyle d(1-\pi q)>(1-\pi q)^{\frac{1}{d}}(d+1)^{\frac{d-1}{d}}\pi^{-\frac{1}{d}}(1-p-q)^{-\frac{1}{d}}

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow

\displaystyle\pi^{\frac{1}{d}}(1-p-q)^{\frac{1}{d}}(1-\pi q)^{\frac{d-1}{d}}>d^{-1}(d+1)^{\frac{d-1}{d}},

which, upon raising both sides to the
d
-th power, becomes precisely the negation of (
4.8
). Therefore, we indeed have
g_{p,q}(x_{p,q})>x_{p,q}
whenever (
4.8
) fails, thus completing the last step of the argument outlined in §
4.1
for the case where
\chi
is Binomial
(d,\pi)
. This completes the proof of Theorem
1.3
.

Remark 4.3
.

Setting
\pi=1
in the above set-up, we obtain a special sub-case of the Binomial
(d,\pi)
distribution: where
\chi
becomes the Dirac probability measure at
d
. In other words,
T_{\chi}
is now replaced by the deterministic, rooted
d
-regular tree
T_{d}
, in which each vertex has precisely
d
children. The above proof now reveals that the probability of draw in the bond percolation game played on
T_{d}
is
0
if and only if

(1-p-q)(1-q)^{d-1}\leqslant\frac{(d+1)^{d-1}}{d^{d}}.

4.3.
The bond percolation game on the Poisson GW tree

Let the offspring distribution
\chi
of
T_{\chi}
be Poisson
(\lambda)
, so that the probability generating function now becomes
G(x)=e^{\lambda(x-1)}
(once again, the domain
\mathcal{D}
on which this is defined, referring to the notation set in §
4.1
, is the entire
\mathbb{R}^{+}
). Note that
G^{\prime}(x)=\lambda G(x)
and
G^{\prime\prime}(x)=\lambda^{2}G(x)
in this case. The function
g_{p,q}
now becomes

g_{p,q}(x)=(1-q)-(1-p-q)e^{\lambda(x-1)},

and the function
g_{p,q}^{(2)}
becomes

\displaystyle g_{p,q}^{(2)}(x)

\displaystyle=(1-q)-(1-p-q)\exp\left\{\lambda\left[(1-q)-(1-p-q)e^{\lambda(x-1)}-1\right]\right\}

\displaystyle=(1-q)-(1-p-q)\exp\left\{-\lambda q-\lambda(1-p-q)e^{\lambda(x-1)}\right\}.

Differentiating the expression above, we obtain

(4.11)

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dx}g_{p,q}^{(2)}(x)

\displaystyle=(1-p-q)^{2}\lambda^{2}\exp\left\{\lambda(x-1)-\lambda q-\lambda(1-p-q)e^{\lambda(x-1)}\right\}.

We note that the derivative

\displaystyle e^{\lambda(x-1)}<\lambda^{-1}(1-p-q)^{-1}\Longleftrightarrow x<x_{p,q}\text{ where }x_{p,q}=1-\frac{\log\lambda}{\lambda}-\frac{\log(1-p-q)}{\lambda}.

As argued in §

, and this maximum value equals, substituting

\displaystyle(1-p-q)^{2}\lambda^{2}\exp\left\{\lambda(x_{p,q}-1)-\lambda q-\lambda(1-p-q)e^{\lambda(x_{p,q}-1)}\right\}=(1-p-q)\lambda e^{-\lambda q-1}.

As argued in §

, the probability of draw in the bond percolation game played on

, with
\chi
Poisson

As the next step of the argument outlined in §

4.4.

\displaystyle g_{p,q}(x)

\displaystyle=(1-q)-(1-p-q)\left(\frac{\pi}{1-(1-\pi)x}\right)^{r}.

\displaystyle g^{\prime}_{p,q}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{-r(1-p-q)(1-\pi)\pi^{r}}{\left\{1-(1-\pi)x\right\}^{r+1}},

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dx}g_{p,q}^{(2)}(x)=g^{\prime}_{p,q}\left(g_{p,q}(x)\right)g^{\prime}_{p,q}(x)=\frac{-r(1-p-q)(1-\pi)\pi^{r}}{\left\{1-(1-\pi)g_{p,q}(x)\right\}^{r+1}}\cdot\frac{-r(1-p-q)(1-\pi)\pi^{r}}{\left\{1-(1-\pi)x\right\}^{r+1}}

From the argument outlined in §

, whenever the inequality in (

As the next step of the argument outlined in §

, we show that

(p,q)\in I
, for
I
as defined in (
1.1
).

The final step in the argument outlined in §
4.1
is proving (
4.4
), i.e. showing that
x_{p,q}<\alpha_{p,q}
whenever (
4.22
) does not hold, which is accomplished by proving (
4.5
), i.e. by showing that
g_{p,q}(x_{p,q})>x_{p,q}
holds whenever (
4.22
) does not. This inequality is equivalent to
(using (
4.21
))

\displaystyle\pi(1-q)+p(1-\pi)-(1-p-q)\pi x_{p,q}^{d}>x_{p,q}

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow

\displaystyle\pi(1-q)+p(1-\pi)-(1-p-q)\pi\cdot\frac{\pi(1-q)+p(1-\pi)}{(1-p-q)\pi(d+1)}>\left(\frac{\pi(1-q)+p(1-\pi)}{(1-p-q)\pi(d+1)}\right)^{\frac{1}{d}}

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow

\displaystyle\frac{d\left\{\pi(1-q)+p(1-\pi)\right\}}{d+1}>\left(\frac{\pi(1-q)+p(1-\pi)}{(1-p-q)\pi(d+1)}\right)^{\frac{1}{d}}

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow

\displaystyle\left\{\pi(1-q)+p(1-\pi)\right\}^{\frac{d-1}{d}}(1-p-q)^{\frac{1}{d}}\pi^{\frac{1}{d}}>(d+1)^{\frac{d-1}{d}}d^{-1}

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow

\displaystyle\left\{\pi(1-q)+p(1-\pi)\right\}^{d-1}(1-p-q)\pi>\frac{(d+1)^{d-1}}{d^{d}},

which is precisely what the negation of (
4.22
) is. This completes the argument outlined in §
4.1
for the case where
\chi
is supported on
\{0,d\}
, and establishes Theorem
1.6
.

5.
Ergodicity of a suitably defined probabilistic tree automaton

This section is dedicated to exploring the connection between the bond percolation game played on
T_{d}
(recall that
T_{d}
is the rooted
d
-regular tree in which each vertex has precisely
d
children, and it is obtained as a special case of
T_{\chi}
in §
4.2
by setting
\pi=1
in the Binomial
(d,\pi)
offspring distribution) and a probabilistic tree automaton (PTA) whose stochastic update rules represent the recurrence relations arising out of the game. We use the already established Theorem
1.3
to describe the regime in which this PTA is ergodic. Before proceeding further, we include here a brief introduction to what PTAs (defined on
T_{d}
) are, and what it means to say that such a PTA is ergodic.

5.1.
A formal introduction to probabilistic tree automata

A
finite state probabilistic tree automaton
(PTA)
A
, defined on
T_{d}
, comprises the following components:

(i)

a
finite
set
\mathcal{S}
of
states
or
colours
that we refer to as its
alphabet
,

(ii)

a
stochastic update rule
, encompassed by a
|\mathcal{S}|^{d}\times|\mathcal{S}|
-dimensional stochastic matrix
\vartheta_{A}
, so that if a vertex
u
in
T_{d}
has children named
u_{1},u_{2},\ldots,u_{d}
, and
u_{i}
has state
a_{i}\in\mathcal{S}
for
1\leqslant i\leqslant d
, the probability that the state of
u
is
b\in\mathcal{S}
is given by the entry
\vartheta_{A}((a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{d}),b)
of
\vartheta_{A}
.

Let
L_{n}
denote the set of all vertices of
T_{d}
that are in generation
n
for
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, where the root
\phi
is assumed to be in generation
0
(i.e.
L_{0}=\{\phi\}
). Conditioned on the states of all vertices in generation
L_{n+1}
, the (random) states assigned to the vertices in
L_{n}
under the application of
A
are independent.

A
configuration
on
L_{n}
refers to an assignment
\sigma:L_{n}\rightarrow\mathcal{S}
of states to the vertices of
L_{n}
, for each
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
. It is evident that once we fix a configuration on
L_{n}
, iterative applications of
A
are only capable of determining / affecting the (random) states of the vertices in
L_{i}
for
0\leqslant i\leqslant n-1
. In fact, it will frequently be the case that we shall fix
n
, focus on the
level-
n
truncated tree

T_{d}^{n}
that is the induced subgraph of
T_{d}
on the subset of vertices spanning generations
L_{0},L_{1},\ldots,L_{n}
, carry out our analysis and
then
let
n\rightarrow\infty
. In such situations, a configuration on
L_{n}
, which is the last generation to be included in
T_{d}^{n}
, will be, quite appropriately, referred to as a
boundary configuration
.

Given a boundary configuration
\sigma:L_{n}\rightarrow\mathcal{S}
and a vertex
u
that is situated in generation
i
for some
0\leqslant i\leqslant n-1
, we denote by
A(\sigma,u)
the random variable indicating the state of
u
obtained via iterative applications of
A
, starting from
\sigma
. Formally speaking, if
u
is in
L_{n-1}
and its children are named
u_{1},u_{2},\ldots,u_{d}
, then
A(\sigma,u)
is the random variable with the following probability distribution:

\Prob[A(\sigma,u)=b]=\vartheta_{A}((\sigma(u_{1}),\sigma(u_{2}),\ldots,\sigma(u_{d})),b),\text{ for each }b\in\mathcal{S},

where
\sigma(u_{i})
indicates the state that
\sigma
assigns to the vertex
u_{i}
(which is in
L_{n}
) for each
1\leqslant i\leqslant d
. For any
0\leqslant i\leqslant n-2
, having defined
A(\sigma,w)
for every vertex
w
that lies in
L_{j}
for all
i<j\leqslant n-1
, we define
A(\sigma,v)
, for every
v\in L_{i}
, as follows. Suppose
v
has children
v_{1},v_{2},\ldots,v_{d}
. Then

(5.1)

\displaystyle\Prob\left[A(\sigma,v)=b\big|A(\sigma,v_{1})=a_{1},A(\sigma,v_{2})=a_{2},\ldots,A(\sigma,v_{d})=a_{d}\right]=\vartheta_{A}((a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{d}),b),

for all
b,a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{d}\in\mathcal{S}
. Given a (deterministic) boundary configuration
\sigma
on
L_{n}
for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
, and
u
a vertex in
L_{i}
for any
0\leqslant i\leqslant n-1
, we let
\mu_{A}(\sigma,u)
denote the law of the random variable
A(\sigma,u)
. When
u
equals the root
\phi
of
T_{d}
, we abbreviate
\mu_{A}(\sigma,\phi)
as simply
\mu_{A}(\sigma)
.

The notations introduced in the previous paragraph can be extended as follows when, instead of considering a deterministic boundary configuration
\sigma
on
L_{n}
, we consider a
random
boundary configuration
\boldsymbol{\sigma}
on
L_{n}
, with law
\nu
supported on
\mathcal{S}^{d^{n}}
. For any
0\leqslant i\leqslant n-1
and any vertex
u
that lies in
L_{i}
, we let
A(\boldsymbol{\sigma},u)
denote the (random) state of
u
obtained by starting from
\boldsymbol{\sigma}
on
L_{n}
and iteratively applying
A
up the generations of
T_{d}^{n}
. We let
\mu_{A}(\nu,u)
denote the law of
A(\boldsymbol{\sigma},u)
. As above, we abbreviate
\mu_{A}(\nu,\phi)
as
\mu_{A}(\nu)
.

Recall that, given a measurable space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F})
and two probability distributions
\mu_{1}
and
\mu_{2}
defined on it, the
total variation distance
between
\mu_{1}
and
\mu_{2}
is defined as

\left|\left|\mu_{1}-\mu_{2}\right|\right|_{\tv}=\sup_{S\in\mathcal{F}}\left|\mu_{1}(S)-\mu_{2}(S)\right|.

When
\Omega
is countable and
\mathcal{F}
is its power set, the total variation metric is related to the
L^{1}
metric via the following relation:

\left|\left|\mu_{1}-\mu_{2}\right|\right|_{\tv}=\frac{1}{2}\left|\left|\mu_{1}-\mu_{2}\right|\right|_{L^{1}}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\omega\in\Omega}\left|\mu_{1}(\{\omega\})-\mu_{2}(\{\omega\})\right|.

Moreover, we have (see, for instance, Proposition 4.7 of
[
24
]
)

(5.2)

\left|\left|\mu_{1}-\mu_{2}\right|\right|_{\tv}=\inf\left\{\Prob[X_{1}\neq X_{2}]:(X_{1},X_{2})\text{ is a coupling of }\mu_{1}\text{ and }\mu_{2}\right\},

where by a coupling
(X_{1},X_{2})
of
\mu_{1}
and
\mu_{2}
we mean a pair of random variables,
X_{1}
and
X_{2}
, defined on the same probability space (with a specified joint distribution), such that
X_{1}
follows the law
\mu_{1}
and
X_{2}
follows the law
\mu_{2}
.

Definition 5.1
.

We call a PTA
A

ergodic
if

(5.3)

\limsup_{n\rightarrow\infty}\sup_{\sigma,\tau:L_{n}\rightarrow\mathcal{S}}\left|\left|\mu_{A}(\sigma)-\mu_{A}(\tau)\right|\right|_{\tv}=0,

where
\mu_{A}(\sigma)
and
\mu_{A}(\tau)
, as defined above, indicate the laws of the random variables
A(\sigma,\phi)
and
A(\tau,\phi)
respectively.

A definition this instrumental deserves some discussions about what truly its implications are. Consider
any
two boundary configurations
\sigma
and
\tau
on
L_{n}
. What the probability distributions
\mu_{A}(\sigma)
and
\mu_{A}(\tau)
of the random variables
A(\sigma,\phi)
and
A(\tau,\phi)
capture are the “effects” of the boundary configurations
\sigma
and
\tau
, respectively, on the state of the root
\phi
of
T_{d}
, under iterative applications of the PTA
A
. Consequently, what the total variation distance
\left|\left|\mu_{A}(\sigma)-\mu_{A}(\tau)\right|\right|_{\tv}
captures is the difference between these effects, i.e. how differently the probability distribution of the state of
\phi
is impacted because of starting from the boundary configuration
\sigma
on
L_{n}
as opposed to starting from the boundary configuration
\tau
on
L_{n}
. When (
5.3
) holds, it is an indication that the effect
any
boundary configuration assigned to
L_{n}
has on the state of
\phi

dwindles
or
decays
as
n
approaches
\infty
. It is as if, when
n
is sufficiently large, the PTA
A
, viewed as a stochastic operator that acts on the states of the vertices of
L_{i}
for all
1\leqslant i\leqslant n
, tends to nearly
forget
the “initial” boundary configuration on
L_{n}
.

5.2.
The PTA defined via the game’s recurrence relations

Recall the sets
W
,
L
and
D
introduced in §
2
, and the recurrence relations described in §
2.1
. It is evident, applying the same argument as adopted in §
2.1
, that for any vertex
v\in T_{d}
with children named
v_{1},\ldots,v_{d}
, we have
v\in W
if and only if either the edge
(v,v_{k})
is a target for some
1\leqslant k\leqslant d
, or there exists some
1\leqslant\ell\leqslant d
such that
v_{\ell}\in L
and the edge
(v,v_{\ell})
is safe. Likewise,
v\in D
if and only if no
(v,v_{\ell})
is a target,
(v,v_{\ell})
is a trap for every
v_{\ell}
that belongs to
L
, and there exists at least one
v_{k}\in D
with
(v,v_{k})
safe. In all other cases,
v\in L
.

The recurrence relations mentioned above can be summarized in the form of a PTA
B_{p,q}
, as follows. The associated alphabet is
\mathcal{S}=\{W,L,D\}
, and the stochastic update rule is captured by the
3^{d}\times 3
-dimensional stochastic matrix
\vartheta_{B_{p,q}}
whose elements are given by

(5.4)

\vartheta_{B_{p,q}}((a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{d}),b)=\begin{cases}1-p^{i}(1-q)^{d-i}&\text{when }b=W,\\
p^{i}(1-q)^{d-i}-p^{i+j}(1-q)^{d-i-j}&\text{when }b=D,\\
p^{i+j}(1-q)^{d-i-j}&\text{when }b=L,\end{cases}

for all
a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{d}\in\mathcal{S}
such that
i=\left|\left\{1\leqslant\ell\leqslant d:a_{\ell}=L\right\}\right|
and
j=\left|\left\{1\leqslant\ell\leqslant d:a_{\ell}=D\right\}\right|
.

5.3.
How the probability of draw ties in with the ergodicity of this PTA

Theorem 5.2
.

The probability of the event that the bond percolation game with parameter-pair
(p,q)
results in a draw is
0
if and only if
B_{p,q}
is ergodic.

Proof.

We let
\boldsymbol{\omega}
denote the random assignment of trap / target / safe labels to the edges of
T_{d}
. Given a vertex
u
of
T_{d}
, we let
\boldsymbol{\omega}(u)
denote the restriction of
\boldsymbol{\omega}
to the edges of
T_{d}(u)
, where
T_{d}(u)
denotes the sub-tree of
T_{d}
induced on the set of vertices comprising
u
and all its descendants. Let
X(u)
denote the (random) state (in
\mathcal{S}=\{W,L,D\}
) of
u
obtained as a result of the bond percolation game played using the random assignment
\boldsymbol{\omega}
and with the token placed at
u
at the start of the game. We abbreviate
X(\phi)
by
X
, and let
\mu
denote the law of
X
. Note, crucially, that
X(u)
is actually a function of
\boldsymbol{\omega}(u)
, and its law is also
\mu
.

From above, it is evident that if we assign i.i.d.
\mu
random states to the vertices of
L_{n}
, then the law of the random state of
\phi
obtained via iterative applications of
B_{p,q}
is again
\mu
. Formally, letting
\nu^{(n)}
denote the probability distribution

\nu^{(n)}\left[\left(a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{d^{n}}\right)\right]=\prod_{i=1}^{d^{n}}\mu[a_{i}],\text{ for all }a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{d^{n}}\in\mathcal{S},

we conclude that the law
\mu_{B_{p,q}}(\nu^{(n)})
is the same as
\mu
.

Let us denote by
\delta_{L}^{n}
the boundary configuration on
L_{n}
that assigns the state
L
to each vertex of
L_{n}
. From (
5.4
), it is evident that
\mu_{B_{p,q}}\left(\delta_{L}^{n}\right)[D]=0
for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
(in fact, this assertion is true for
any
boundary configuration on
L_{n}
that assigns the state
D
to
none
of the vertices in
L_{n}
). This fact, along with (
5.3
), implies that when
B_{p,q}
is ergodic,

\displaystyle\limsup_{n\rightarrow\infty}\mu_{B_{p,q}}(\nu^{(n)})[D]=\limsup_{n\rightarrow\infty}\left|\mu_{B_{p,q}}(\nu^{(n)})[D]-\mu_{B_{p,q}}\left(\delta_{L}^{n}\right)[D]\right|\leqslant\limsup_{n\rightarrow\infty}\left|\left|\mu_{B_{p,q}}(\nu^{(n)})-\mu_{B_{p,q}}\left(\delta_{L}^{n}\right)\right|\right|_{\tv}=0,

so that
\mu[D]=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\mu_{B_{p,q}}(\nu^{(n)})[D]=0
when
B_{p,q}
is ergodic. This shows that when
B_{p,q}
is ergodic, the probability that the root
\phi
has state
D
under
\boldsymbol{\omega}
is
0
, so that the bond percolation game starting with
\phi
as the initial vertex
almost never
results in a draw.

We now assume that the bond percolation game beginning at
\phi
has probability
0
of resulting in a draw. Let
\Omega
denote the set of all realizations of
\boldsymbol{\omega}
for which the game starting at
\phi
does not lead to a draw. By our hypothesis, the set
\{\text{trap},\text{target},\text{safe}\}^{E(T_{d})}\setminus\Omega
, where
E(T_{d})
denotes the set of all edges of
T_{d}
, is of measure
0
. Furthermore, our hypothesis guarantees the existence of a random variable
N
, taking values in
\mathbb{N}
and
finite almost surely
, such that the game terminates in less than
N
rounds. In fact, for each realization
\omega
of
\boldsymbol{\omega}
that belongs to
\Omega
, the corresponding value
N(\omega)
of
N
is finite.

Let
u
be a vertex in
T_{d}
, with children
u_{1},\ldots,u_{d}
, and let
\omega(u,u_{i})
, for
1\leqslant i\leqslant d
, denote the deterministic trap / target / safe labels assigned to the edges
(u,u_{i})
, for
1\leqslant i\leqslant d
, under
\omega
. We also fix a deterministic assignment of states
\sigma:\{u_{1},\ldots,u_{d}\}\rightarrow\mathcal{S}
. The state of
u
is then decided as follows:

(i)

If there exists some
1\leqslant i\leqslant d
with
\omega(u,u_{i})
safe and
\sigma(u_{i})=L
, then
u
is in
W
.

(ii)

If the above does not happen, but there exists some
1\leqslant j\leqslant d
with
\omega(u,u_{j})
safe and
\sigma(u_{j})=D
, then
u
is in
D
.

(iii)

In all other cases,
u
is in
L
.

Fix
any

\omega
in
\Omega
and
any
deterministic boundary configuration
\sigma:L_{N(\omega)}\rightarrow\mathcal{S}
on
L_{N(\omega)}
. Starting from
\sigma
, we use
\omega
and the rules described above to decide the state
B_{p,q}(\sigma,u)(\omega)
(recall this notation from §
5.1
) of the root
\phi
– however, since the fate of the game that begins from
\phi
is decided in
less
than
N(\omega)
rounds,
\sigma
has no effect whatsoever on this state. Using the notation introduced at the beginning of this proof, we conclude that
B_{p,q}(\sigma,u)(\omega)=X(\omega)
(where
X(\omega)
is dictated by
\omega
alone), irrespective of our choice of
\sigma
. Letting
S_{n}
, for every

, denote the set of all
\omega\in\Omega
for which

, we have

\displaystyle B_{p,q}(\sigma,\phi)(\omega)=X(\omega)\text{ for every boundary configuration }\sigma\text{ on }L_{n},\text{ for each }\omega\in S_{n}

\displaystyle\implies B_{p,q}(\sigma,\phi)\mathbf{1}_{S_{n}}=X\mathbf{1}_{S_{n}}\text{ for every boundary configuration }\sigma\text{ on }L_{n}

(5.5)

\displaystyle\implies\Prob\left[B_{p,q}(\sigma,\phi)\neq X\right]\leqslant 1-\Prob[S_{n}]=\Prob[N>n],\text{ for every boundary configuration }\sigma\text{ on }L_{n}.

Consequently, for
any

and

, we have

(5.6)

\displaystyle\Prob\left[B_{p,q}(\sigma,\phi)\neq B_{p,q}(\tau,\phi)\right]\leqslant\Prob\left[B_{p,q}(\sigma,\phi)\neq X\right]+\Prob\left[B_{p,q}(\tau,\phi)\neq X\right]\leqslant 2\Prob[N>n].

From (

) and the fact that

is finite almost surely, we conclude that

(5.7)

\displaystyle\limsup_{n\rightarrow\infty}\sup_{\sigma,\tau:L_{n}\rightarrow\mathcal{S}}\Prob\left[B_{p,q}(\sigma,\phi)\neq B_{p,q}(\tau,\phi)\right]\leqslant\limsup_{n\rightarrow\infty}2\Prob[N>n]=0,

so that from (

), (

) and (

), we conclude that

is indeed ergodic, as desired.
∎

Theorem
5.2
, along with Remark

, tells us that the PTA

is ergodic if and only if

.

6.
Condition that guarantees that the expected duration of the bond percolation game is finite

While the probability of draw being
0
implies that the duration of the bond percolation game (played on
T_{\chi}
, for
any
offspring distribution
\chi
), henceforth denoted by the random variable
\mathcal{T}
, is finite almost surely, it does not guarantee that its expected value, i.e.
\E[\mathcal{T}]
, is finite as well. Here, we provide a sufficient condition for
\E[\mathcal{T}]
to be finite.

To begin with, we assume that we are in a regime where the probability of draw is
0
. From parts (
i
) and (
iii
) of Theorem
1.1
, this means that
w^{\prime}=p+(1-p-q)w
is the unique fixed point of the function
g_{p,q}^{(2)}
in
[0,1]
. Recall, from §
2
, the sets
W_{n}
,
L_{n}
and
D_{n}
, the probabilities
w_{n}
and
\ell_{n}
, the transformed variables
w^{\prime}_{n}=p+(1-p-q)w_{n}
and
\ell^{\prime}_{n}=(1-p-q)\ell_{n}
, and the recurrence relations described in §
2.1
. We can write

\displaystyle\E[\mathcal{T}]

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\Prob[\mathcal{T}\geqslant n+1]=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\Prob[\phi\in D_{n}]

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\left\{1-\Prob[\phi\in W_{n}]-\Prob[\phi\in L_{n}]\right\}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\left\{1-w_{n}-\ell_{n}\right\}

\displaystyle=1+\frac{1}{1-p-q}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\left\{1-p-q-(1-p-q)w_{n}-(1-p-q)\ell_{n}\right\}

\displaystyle=1+\frac{1}{1-p-q}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\left\{1-q-w^{\prime}_{n}-\ell^{\prime}_{n}\right\}=1+\frac{1}{1-p-q}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\left\{w^{\prime}+\ell^{\prime}-w^{\prime}_{n}-\ell^{\prime}_{n}\right\}

(6.1)

\displaystyle=1+\frac{1}{1-p-q}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\left\{w^{\prime}-w^{\prime}_{n}\right\}+\frac{1}{1-p-q}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\left\{\ell^{\prime}-\ell^{\prime}_{n}\right\}.

Using the recurrence relation (
2.4
), and the mean value theorem, we have

(6.2)

\displaystyle w^{\prime}-w^{\prime}_{n+2}

\displaystyle=g_{p,q}^{(2)}(w^{\prime})-g_{p,q}^{(2)}\left(w^{\prime}_{n}\right)=\frac{d}{dx}g_{p,q}^{(2)}(x)\Big|_{x=\xi_{n}}\left(w^{\prime}-w^{\prime}_{n}\right),

where
\xi_{n}
is some quantity with
w^{\prime}_{n}<\xi_{n}<w^{\prime}
. Likewise, using (
2.3
), the definition of
g_{p,q}
from (
1.2
), and part (
ii
) of Theorem
1.1
, we have

(6.3)

\displaystyle\ell^{\prime}-\ell^{\prime}_{n+2}

\displaystyle=g_{p,q}(w^{\prime}_{n+1})-g_{p,q}(w^{\prime})=g^{\prime}_{p,q}\left(\zeta_{n+1}\right)\left(w^{\prime}_{n+1}-w^{\prime}\right)=\left|g^{\prime}_{p,q}\left(\zeta_{n+1}\right)\right|\left(w^{\prime}-w^{\prime}_{n+1}\right),

for some quantity
\zeta_{n+1}
, with
w^{\prime}_{n+1}<\zeta_{n+1}<w^{\prime}
, whose existence is guaranteed by the mean value theorem. Note that, under the assumption that the probability of draw is
0
, we have
w^{\prime}=\alpha_{p,q}
, where
\alpha_{p,q}
is the unique fixed point of
g_{p,q}
in
[0,1]
.

In §
2.1
, we showed that
w^{\prime}
is the smallest positive fixed point of
g_{p,q}^{(2)}
, and in (
2.6
) and (
2.7
), we showed that the curve
y=g_{p,q}^{(2)}(x)
lies above the line
y=x
at
x=0
. Consequently, the curve
y=g_{p,q}^{(2)}(x)
must travel from
above
the line
y=x
to
beneath
the line
y=x
at

. Therefore, the slope of

must be bounded above by the slope of
y=x
, which is
1
, at

. In other words,
\frac{d}{dx}g_{p,q}^{(2)}(x)\Big|_{x=w^{\prime}}\leqslant 1
.

We now assume that

(6.4)

\frac{d}{dx}g_{p,q}^{(2)}(x)\Big|_{x=w^{\prime}}<1\text{ and }\left|\frac{d}{dx}g_{p,q}(x)\Big|_{x=w^{\prime}}\right|<1.

Let
\frac{d}{dx}g_{p,q}^{(2)}(x)\Big|_{x=w^{\prime}}=\alpha
and
\left|\frac{d}{dx}g_{p,q}(x)\Big|_{x=w^{\prime}}\right|=\beta
, so that under the assumption made in (
6.4
),
\alpha<1
and
\beta<1
. Let us choose and fix
0<\epsilon<\min\{1-\alpha,1-\beta\}
. From (
2.1
), we know that
w^{\prime}_{n}\uparrow w^{\prime}
as
n\rightarrow\infty
. By the continuity of both the functions
\frac{d}{dx}g_{p,q}^{(2)}(x)
and
\left|\frac{d}{dx}g_{p,q}(x)\right|
in
x
, we know that there exists some
N\in\mathbb{N}
such that

\frac{d}{dx}g_{p,q}^{(2)}(x)<\alpha+\epsilon\text{ and }\left|\frac{d}{dx}g_{p,q}(x)\right|<\beta+\epsilon\text{ for all }w^{\prime}_{n}\leqslant x\leqslant w^{\prime},\text{ for all }n\geqslant N.

This, in turn, implies that

(6.5)

\frac{d}{dx}g_{p,q}^{(2)}(x)\Big|_{x=\xi_{n}}<\alpha+\epsilon<1\text{ and }\left|g^{\prime}_{p,q}\left(\zeta_{n+1}\right)\right|<\beta+\epsilon<1\text{ for all }n\geqslant N.

From (
6.2
), (
6.3
) and (
6.5
), we see that the terms corresponding to
n\geqslant N
in the infinite series of (
6.1
) decay exponentially fast, thereby allowing the series to converge. This completes the proof of the fact that under (
6.4
), the expected duration of the bond percolation game is finite. This also completes the proof of Theorem
1.7
.
