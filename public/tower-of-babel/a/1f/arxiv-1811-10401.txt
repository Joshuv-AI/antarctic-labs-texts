Kleene Algebra with Observations
Tobias Kappé, Paul Brunet, Jurriaan Rot, Alexandra Silva, Jana Wagemaker, Fabio Zanasi

Abstract
Kleene algebra with tests ( KAT ) is an algebraic framework for reasoning about the control flow of sequential programs. Generalising KAT to reason about concurrent programs is not straightforward, because axioms native to KAT in conjunction with expected axioms for concurrency lead to an anomalous equation. In this paper, we propose Kleene algebra with observations ( KAO ), a variant of KAT, as an alternative foundation for extending KAT to a concurrent setting. We characterise the free model of KAO, and establish a decision procedure w.r.t. its equational theory.

Introduction

The axioms of
Kleene algebra
(
KA
)
[
22
,
9
]
correspond well to program composition
[
14
]
, making them a valuable tool for studying equivalences between programs from an algebraic perspective.
An extension of Kleene algebra known as
Kleene algebra with tests
(
KAT
)
[
24
]
adds primitives for conditional branching, and is particularly useful when proving validity of program transformations, such as optimisations applied by a compiler
[
28
,
41
]
.

As a matter of fact, KAT is sufficiently abstract to express not only program behaviour, but also program specifications; consequently, its laws can be used to compare programs to specifications
[
25
,
1
]
.
What makes this connection especially powerful is that KA (resp. KAT) is known to be
sound
and
complete
with respect to a language model
[
5
,
30
,
23
,
29
]
, meaning that an equation is valid in any KA (resp. KAT) precisely when it holds in the corresponding language model.
Practical algorithms for deciding language equivalence
[
17
,
6
,
34
]
enable checking equations in KA or KAT, and hence automated verification becomes feasible
[
10
]
.

More recently, Kleene algebra has been extended with a parallel composition operator, yielding
concurrent Kleene algebra
(
CKA
)
[
15
,
13
,
16
]
.
Crucially, CKA includes the
exchange law
, which encodes
interleaving
, i.e., the (partial) sequentialisation of threads.
Like its predecessors, CKA can be applied to verify (concurrent) programs by reasoning about equivalences
[
16
]
.
The equational theory of CKA has also been characterised in terms of a language-like semantics
[
32
,
21
]
, where equivalence is known to be decidable
[
7
]
.

Since both KAT and CKA are conservative extensions of KA, this prompted Jipsen and Moshier
[
19
]
to study a marriage between the two, dubbed
concurrent Kleene algebra with tests
(
CKAT
).
The aim of CKAT is to extend CKA with Boolean guards, and thus arrive at a new algebraic perspective on verification of concurrent programs with conditional branching.

The starting point of this paper is the realisation that CKAT is not a suitable model of concurrent programs.
This is because for any test
p
and CKAT-term
e
, one can prove
p\cdot e\cdot\overline{p}\mathrel{\equiv_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{CKAT}}}0
, an equation that appears to have no reasonable interpretation for programs.
The derivation goes as follows:

0\mathrel{\leqq_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{KAT}}}p\cdot e\cdot\overline{p}\mathrel{\leqq_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{CKA}}}e\parallel(p\cdot\overline{p})\mathrel{\equiv_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{KAT}}}e\parallel 0\mathrel{\equiv_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{CKA}}}0\ .

As we shall see, this is possible because of the interplay between the exchange law and the fact that KAT identifies conjunction of tests with their sequential composition.
For sequential programs, this identification is perfectly reasonable.
In the context of concurrency with interleaving, however, actions from another thread may be scheduled in between two sequentially composed tests, whereas the conjunction of tests executes atomically.
Indeed, an action scheduled between the tests might very well change the result of the second test.

It thus appears that, to reason algebraically about programs with both tests and concurrency, one needs a perspective on conditional branching where the conjunction of two tests is not necessarily the same as executing one test after the other.
The remit of this paper is to propose an alternative to KAT, which we call
Kleene algebra with observations
(
KAO
), that makes exactly this distinction.
We claim that, because of this change, KAO is more amenable to a sensible extension with primitives for concurrency.
Establishing the meta-theory of KAO turns out to be a technically demanding task.
We therefore devote this paper to such foundations, and leave development of concurrent KAO to follow-up work.

Concretely, we characterise the equational theory of KAO in terms of a language model (
\cref
section:completeness).
Furthermore, we show that we can decide equality of these languages (and hence the equational theory of KAO) by deciding language equivalence of non-deterministic finite automata (
\cref
section:decision-procedure).
Both proofs show a clear separation of concerns: their kernel is idiomatic to KAO, and some well-known results from KA complete the argument.

For space reasons, detailed proofs are only included in the full version of this paper
[
20
]
; here, we sketch the main insights needed to prove the core propositions and theorems.

3
The problem with CKAT

Our motivation for adjusting the axioms of KAT is based on the fact that conjunction and sequential composition are distinct when interleaving is involved, because sequential composition leaves a “gap” between tests that might be used by another thread, possibly changing the outcome of the second test.
We now formalise this, by detailing how combining KAT and CKA to obtain CKAT (as in
[
19
]
) leads to the absurd equation
p\cdot e\cdot\overline{p}\mathrel{\equiv_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{CKAT}}}0
.

Kleene algebra with tests

To obtain KAT, we enrich rational terms with propositions; concretely, the set of
guarded rational terms
over
\Sigma
and
\Omega
, denoted
{GR}
, is generated by

e,f::=0\;\;|\;\;1\;\;|\;\;a\in\Sigma\;\;|\;\;p\in P\;\;|\;\;e+f\;\;|\;\;e\cdot f\;\;|\;\;e^{\star}\ .

We define
\mathrel{\equiv_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{KAT}}}
as the smallest congruence on
{GR}
which contains
\equiv_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{BA}}
and obeys the axioms of
\mathrel{\equiv_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{KA}}}
(e.g.,
e+e\mathrel{\equiv_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{KAT}}}e
).
Furthermore,
\mathrel{\equiv_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{KAT}}}
should relate constants and operators on propositional subterms: for all
p,q\in P
it holds that
1
1

1

This is a slightly contrived definition of KAT; one usually presents the constants and operators using the same symbols
[
24
]
.
To contrast KAO and KAT it is helpful to make this identification explicit.

\bot\mathrel{\equiv_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{KAT}}}0\top\mathrel{\equiv_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{KAT}}}1p\vee q\mathrel{\equiv_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{KAT}}}p+qp\wedge q\mathrel{\equiv_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{KAT}}}p\cdot q\ .

Guarded rational terms relate to programs by viewing actions as statements and propositions as assertions.
The last axiom is therefore not strange: if we assert
\mathtt{x=1}
followed by
\mathtt{y=1}
then surely, if
\mathtt{x}
and
\mathtt{y}
remain the same, this is equivalent to asserting
\mathtt{x=1\wedge y=1}
.

Concurrent Kleene algebra

To obtain CKA, we add a parallel composition operator to KA.
Concretely, the set of
series-rational terms

[
33
]
over
\Sigma
, denoted
{SR}
, is generated by

e,f::=0\;\;|\;\;1\;\;|\;\;a\in\Sigma\;\;|\;\;e+f\;\;|\;\;e\cdot f\;\;|\;\;e\parallel f\;\;|\;\;e^{\star}\ .

We define
\mathrel{\equiv_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{CKA}}}
as the smallest congruence on
{SR}
which obeys the axioms that generate
\mathrel{\equiv_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{KA}}}
, as well as the following for all
e,f,g,h\in{SR}
:

e\parallel f\mathrel{\equiv_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{CKA}}}f\parallel ee\parallel 1\mathrel{\equiv_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{CKA}}}ee\parallel 0\mathrel{\equiv_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{CKA}}}0(e+f)\parallel g\mathrel{\equiv_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{CKA}}}e\parallel g+f\parallel g\\
e\parallel(f\parallel g)\mathrel{\equiv_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{CKA}}}(e\parallel f)\parallel g(e\parallel f)\cdot(g\parallel h)\mathrel{\leqq_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{CKA}}}(e\cdot g)\parallel(f\cdot h)\ ,

in which
e\mathrel{\leqq_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{CKA}}}f
is an abbreviation for
e+f\mathrel{\equiv_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{CKA}}}f
.

Here,
0
annihilates parallel composition because
0
corresponds to the program without valid traces; hence, running

in parallel with
0
also cannot yield any traces.
Distributivity of
\parallel
over

witnesses that a choice within a thread can also be made outside that thread.

The last axiom, called the
exchange law

[
15
]
, encodes interleaving.
Intuitively, it says that when programs
e\cdot g
and
f\cdot h
run in parallel, their behaviour includes running their heads in parallel (i.e.,
e\parallel f
) followed by their tails (i.e.,
g\parallel h
).
Taken to its extreme, the exchange law says that the behaviour of a program includes its complete linearisations.

Concurrent Kleene algebra with tests

To define CKAT
[
19
]
, we choose
guarded series-rational terms
over
\Sigma
and
\Omega
, denoted
{GSR}
, as those generated by the grammar

e,f::=0\;\;|\;\;1\;\;|\;\;a\in\Sigma\;\;|\;\;p\in P\;\;|\;\;e+f\;\;|\;\;e\cdot f\;\;|\;\;e\parallel f\;\;|\;\;e^{\star}\ .

Now,
\mathrel{\equiv_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{CKAT}}}
is the least congruence on
{GSR}
obeying the axioms of
\mathrel{\equiv_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{KAT}}}
and
\mathrel{\equiv_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{CKA}}}
.

If we view guarded series-rational terms as representations of programs, and use
\mathrel{\equiv_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{CKAT}}}
to reason about them, then we should expect to obtain sensible statements about programs, since the axioms that underpin CKAT seem reasonable in that context.

To test this hypothesis, consider the guarded series-rational term
p\cdot e\cdot\overline{p}
, which represents a program that first performs an assertion
p
, then runs a program described by
e
, and asserts the negation of
p
.
There are choices of
p
and
e
that should make
p\cdot e\cdot\overline{p}
describe a program with valid behaviour; for instance,
p
could assert that
\mathtt{x=1}
, and
e
could be the program
\mathtt{x\leftarrow 0}
.
Hence, by our hypothesis,
p\cdot e\cdot\overline{p}
should not, in general, be equivalent to
0
, the program without any valid behaviour.
Unfortunately, the opposite is true.

{ant}

Let
e\in{GSR}
and
p\in P
; now
p\cdot e\cdot\overline{p}\mathrel{\equiv_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{CKAT}}}0
.

Proof 3.1
.

By the axiom that
1
is the unit of
\parallel

p\cdot e\cdot\overline{p}\mathrel{\equiv_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{CKAT}}}(p\parallel 1)\cdot(1\parallel e)\cdot\overline{p}\mathrel{\leqq_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{CKAT}}}((p\cdot 1)\parallel(1\cdot e))\cdot\overline{p}\ .

By the same reasoning, and the axiom that
1
is the unit of
\cdot
, we have that

((p\cdot 1)\parallel(1\cdot e))\cdot\overline{p}\mathrel{\equiv_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{CKAT}}}(p\parallel e)\cdot(\overline{p}\parallel 1)\mathrel{\leqq_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{CKAT}}}(p\cdot\overline{p})\parallel(e\cdot 1)\ .

Applying the unit, and using the identification of
\wedge
and
\cdot

(p\cdot\overline{p})\parallel(e\cdot 1)\mathrel{\equiv_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{CKAT}}}(p\cdot\overline{p})\parallel e\mathrel{\equiv_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{CKAT}}}(p\wedge\overline{p})\parallel e\mathrel{\equiv_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{CKAT}}}\bot\parallel e\ .

Since
\bot

0
are identified, and
0

\bot\parallel e\mathrel{\equiv_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{CKAT}}}0\parallel e\mathrel{\equiv_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{CKAT}}}0\ .

By the above, we have
p\cdot e\cdot\overline{p}\mathrel{\leqq_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{CKAT}}}0
.
Since
0\mathrel{\leqq_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{CKAT}}}p\cdot e\cdot\overline{p}
, the claim follows.

Another way of contextualising the above is to consider that propositional Hoare logic can be encoded in KAT
[
26
]
.
Concretely, a propositional Hoare triple
\{p\}S\{q\}
is valid if
p\cdot e_{S}\cdot\overline{q}\mathrel{\equiv_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{KAT}}}0
, where
e_{S}
is a straightforward encoding of
S
.
It stands to reason that sequential programs should similarly encode in CKAT.
However, if we apply that line of reasoning to the above, then
any
Hoare triple of the form
\{p\}S\{p\}
is valid, which would mean that
any property is an invariant of any program
.

4
Kleene algebra with observations

We now propose KAO as an alternative way of embedding Boolean guards in rational terms.
This approach should prevent
\cref
antinomy:problem, and thus make KAO more suitable for an extension with concurrency.
We start by motivating how we adapt KAT, before proceeding with a language model and a generalisation of partial derivatives to KAO.

4.1
From tests to observations

The root of the problem in
\cref
antinomy:problem is the axiom
p\cdot q\mathrel{\equiv_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{KAT}}}p\wedge q
, telling us that
(p\cdot\overline{p})\parallel e\mathrel{\equiv_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{CKAT}}}(p\wedge\overline{p})\parallel e
.
Interpreted as programs, these are different: in one,
e
can be interleaved between
p
and
\overline{p}
, which the other does not allow.

To fix this problem, we propose a new perspective on Boolean guards.
Rather than considering a guard a
test
, which in KAT entails an assertion valid from the last action to the next, we consider a guard an
observation
, i.e., an assertion valid at that particular point in the execution of the program.
This weakens the connection between conjunction and concatenation: if a program observes
p
followed by
q
, there is no guarantee that
p
and
q
can be observed simultaneously.
Hence, we drop the equivalence between conjunction and concatenation of tests.
We call this system
weak Kleene algebra with observations
; like KAT, its axioms are based on the axioms of Boolean algebra and Kleene algebra, as follows.

Definition 4.2

(
\mathsf{WKAO}
axioms)
.

We define
\mathrel{\equiv_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{WKAO}}}
as the smallest congruence on
{GR}
that contains
\equiv_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{BA}}
and also obeys the axioms of
\mathrel{\equiv_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{KA}}}
.
Furthermore,
\bot\mathrel{\equiv_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{WKAO}}}0
and for all
p,q\in P
it holds that
p\vee q\mathrel{\equiv_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{WKAO}}}p+q
.

Since conjunction and concatenation no longer coincide, we have also dropped the axiom that relates their units.
This can be justified from our shift in perspective:
\top
, i.e., the observation that always succeeds, is an action that leaves some record in the behaviour of the program, whereas
1
, i.e., the program that does nothing, has no such obligation.
2
2

2

We refer to
[
20
, Appendix E]
for further algebraic details on the consequences of identifying these units.

As hinted before, it may happen that when a program observes
p
followed by
q
, both these assertions are true simultaneously, i.e., their conjunction could have been observed; hence, the behaviour of observing
p

and

q
should be contained in the behaviour of observing
p

and then

q
.
For instance, consider the pseudo-programs
S=\mathtt{assert}\ p\wedge q;\ e
and
S^{\prime}=\mathtt{assert}\ p;\ \mathtt{assert}\ q;\ e
.
Here,
S
asserts that
p
and
q
hold at the same time before proceeding with
e
, while
S^{\prime}
first asserts
p
, and then
q
.
The behaviour of
S
should be included in that of
S^{\prime}
: if
p
and
q
are simultaneously observable, then the first two observations might take place simultaneously.
Encoding this in an additional axiom leads to KAO proper.

Definition 4.3

(
\mathsf{KAO}
axioms)
.

We define
\mathrel{\equiv_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{KAO}}}
as the smallest congruence on
{GR}
that contains
\mathrel{\equiv_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{WKAO}}}
, and furthermore satisfies the
contraction law
: for all
p,q\in P
it holds that
p\wedge q\mathrel{\leqq_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{KAO}}}p\cdot q
— where
e\mathrel{\leqq_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{KAO}}}f
is a shorthand for
e+f\mathrel{\equiv_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{KAO}}}f
.

We briefly return to our example programs.
If we encode
S
as
(p\wedge q)\cdot e
and
S^{\prime}
as
p\cdot q\cdot e
, then the behaviour of
S
is contained in that of
S^{\prime}
, because
(p\wedge q)\cdot e\mathrel{\leqq_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{KAO}}}p\cdot q\cdot e
.

Remark 4.4
.

In the presence of the other axioms,
p\wedge q\mathrel{\leqq_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{KAO}}}p\cdot q
is equivalent to
p\mathrel{\leqq_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{KAO}}}p\cdot p
.
Indeed, the second inequality may be inferred from the first, since
p\mathrel{\equiv_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{KAO}}}p\wedge p
.
The converse implication is obtained as follows:
p\wedge q\mathrel{\leqq_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{KAO}}}\left(p\wedge q\right)\cdot\left(p\wedge q\right)\mathrel{\leqq_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{KAO}}}p\cdot q
, using that
p\wedge q\mathrel{\leqq_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{KAO}}}p,q
.
While the contraction law is more convenient to compare terms, the axiom
p\mathrel{\leqq_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{KAO}}}p\cdot p
will serve implicitly as the basis for the contraction relation
\mathrel{\preceq}
defined in the next section.

4.2
A language model

The Kleene algebra variants encountered thus far have models based on rational languages, which characterise the equivalences derivable using the axioms.
To find a model for guarded rational terms which corresponds to KAO, we start with a model of weak KAO:

Definition 4.5

(
\mathsf{WKAO}
semantics)
.

Let
\Gamma=\and\cup\Sigma
.
Languages over
\Gamma
are called
observation languages
.
We define
\llbracket-\rrbracket_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{WKAO}}:{GR}\to 2^{\Gamma^{\star}}
as follows:

\displaystyle\llbracket 0\rrbracket_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{WKAO}}

\displaystyle=\emptyset

\displaystyle\llbracket a\rrbracket_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{WKAO}}

\displaystyle=\{a\}

\displaystyle\llbracket e+f\rrbracket_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{WKAO}}

\displaystyle=\llbracket e\rrbracket_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{WKAO}}\cup\llbracket f\rrbracket_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{WKAO}}

\displaystyle\llbracket e^{\star}\rrbracket_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{WKAO}}

\displaystyle=\llbracket e\rrbracket_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{WKAO}}^{\star}

\displaystyle\llbracket 1\rrbracket_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{WKAO}}

\displaystyle=\{\epsilon\}

\displaystyle\llbracket p\rrbracket_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{WKAO}}

\displaystyle=\llbracket p\rrbracket_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{BA}}

\displaystyle\llbracket e\cdot f\rrbracket_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{WKAO}}

\displaystyle=\llbracket e\rrbracket_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{WKAO}}\cdot\llbracket f\rrbracket_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{WKAO}}\ ,

where it is understood that
\llbracket p\rrbracket_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{BA}}\subseteq\and\subseteq\Gamma\subseteq\Gamma^{\star}
.

Observation languages are a model for guarded rational terms w.r.t.
\mathrel{\equiv_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{WKAO}}}
:

Lemma 3

(
\mathsf{WKAO}
soundness)
.

Let
e,f\in{GR}
.
Now, if
e\mathrel{\equiv_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{WKAO}}}f
, then
\llbracket e\rrbracket_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{WKAO}}=\llbracket f\rrbracket_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{WKAO}}
.

More work is necessary to get a model of guarded rational terms w.r.t.
\mathrel{\equiv_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{KAO}}}
.
In particular,
\llbracket-\rrbracket_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{WKAO}}
does not preserve the contraction law: if
o_{1},o_{2}\in\Omega
, then

\llbracket o_{1}\wedge o_{2}\rrbracket_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{WKAO}}=\{\alpha\in\and:o_{1},o_{2}\in\alpha\}\llbracket o_{1}\cdot o_{2}\rrbracket_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{WKAO}}=\{\alpha\beta\in\and\cdot\and:o_{1}\in\alpha,o_{2}\in\beta\}\ ,

meaning that
\llbracket o_{1}\wedge o_{2}\rrbracket_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{WKAO}}\not\subseteq\llbracket o_{1}\cdot o_{2}\rrbracket_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{WKAO}}
, despite
o_{1}\wedge o_{2}\mathrel{\leqq_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{KAO}}}o_{1}\cdot o_{2}
.
3
3

3

Incidentally, this shows that the contraction law is independent of the axioms that build
\mathrel{\equiv_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{WKAO}}}
, i.e., that those axioms are not sufficient to prove the contraction law.

To obtain a sound model, we need the counterpart of the contraction law on the level of observation languages, which works out as follows

Definition 4.6

(Contraction)
.

We define
\mathrel{\preceq}
as the smallest relation on
\Gamma^{\star}
s.t.

\frac{w,x\in\Gamma^{\star}\quad\alpha\in\and}{w\alpha{}x\mathrel{\preceq}w\alpha\alpha{}x}\ .

When
L\subseteq\Gamma^{\star}
, we write
L{\downarrow}
for the
\mathrel{\preceq}
-closure of
L
, that is to say, the smallest observation language such that
L\subseteq L{\downarrow}
, and if
w\mathrel{\preceq}x
with
x\in L{\downarrow}
, then
w\in L{\downarrow}
.

Thus,
L{\downarrow}
contains all words obtained from words in
L
by contracting any number of repeated atoms (but not actions) into one.
Applying this closure to the semantics encodes the intuition that repeated observations may also correspond to just one step in the execution.

With these tools, we can define a model of KAO as follows.

Definition 4.7

(
\mathsf{KAO}
semantics)
.

We define
\llbracket-\rrbracket_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{KAO}}:{GR}\to 2^{\Gamma^{\star}}
by
\llbracket e\rrbracket_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{KAO}}=\llbracket e\rrbracket_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{WKAO}}{\downarrow}
.

Alternatively, we can describe
\llbracket-\rrbracket_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{KAO}}
compositionally, using the following.

Lemma 4
.

Let
e,f\in{GR}
.
The following hold:
(i)
\llbracket e+f\rrbracket_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{KAO}}=\llbracket e\rrbracket_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{KAO}}\cup\llbracket f\rrbracket_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{KAO}}
, and
(ii)
\llbracket e\cdot f\rrbracket_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{KAO}}=(\llbracket e\rrbracket_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{KAO}}\cdot\llbracket f\rrbracket_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{KAO}}){\downarrow}
, and
(iii)
\llbracket e^{\star}\rrbracket_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{KAO}}=\llbracket e\rrbracket_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{KAO}}^{\star}{\downarrow}
.

It is now straightforward to check that
\llbracket-\rrbracket_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{KAO}}
preserves the axioms of
\mathrel{\equiv_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{KAO}}}
.

Lemma 5

(
\mathsf{KAO}
soundness)
.

Let
e,f\in{GR}
.
Now, if
e\mathrel{\equiv_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{KAO}}}f
, then
\llbracket e\rrbracket_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{KAO}}=\llbracket f\rrbracket_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{KAO}}
.

4.3
Partial derivatives

Derivatives

[
8
]
are a powerful tool in Kleene algebra variants.
In the context of programs, we can think of derivatives as an operational semantics; they tell us whether the term represents a program that can halt immediately (given by the
termination map
), as well as the program that remains to be executed once an action is performed (given by the
continuation map
).

Derivatives are typically compatible with the congruences used to reason about terms; what’s more, they are closely connected to finite automata
[
8
]
.
This makes them useful for reasoning about language models
[
26
,
6
,
34
]
.
We therefore introduce a form of derivatives of guarded rational terms that works well with
\mathrel{\equiv_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{KAO}}}
.
Let us start by giving the termination map, which is close to the termination map of rational terms compatible with
\mathrel{\equiv_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{KA}}}
.

Definition 4.8

(Termination)
.

We define
\epsilon:{GR}\to 2
inductively, as follows:

\displaystyle\epsilon(0)

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\epsilon(a)

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\epsilon(e+f)

\displaystyle=\max\{\epsilon(e),\epsilon(f)\}

\displaystyle\epsilon(e^{\star})

\displaystyle=1

\displaystyle\epsilon(1)

\displaystyle=1

\displaystyle\epsilon(p)

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\epsilon(e\cdot f)

\displaystyle=\min\{\epsilon(e),\epsilon(f)\}\ .

To check that
\epsilon
characterises guarded rational terms that can terminate, we record the following lemma, which says that
\epsilon(e)=1
precisely when
\llbracket e\rrbracket_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{KAO}}
includes the empty string.

Lemma 6
.

Let
e\in{GR}
.
Now
\epsilon(e)\mathrel{\leqq_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{KAO}}}e
, and
\epsilon(e)=1
if and only if
\epsilon\in\llbracket e\rrbracket_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{KAO}}
.

Next we define the continuation map, or more specifically,
partial continuation map

[
2
]
: given
\mathfrak{a}\in\Gamma
, this function gives a
set
of terms representing possible continuations of the program after performing
\mathfrak{a}
.
We start with the continuation map for actions.

Definition 4.9

(
\Sigma
-continuation)
.

We define
\delta:{GR}\times\Sigma\to 2^{{GR}}
inductively

\displaystyle\delta(0,a)

\displaystyle=\delta(1,a)=\emptyset

\displaystyle\delta(e+f,a)

\displaystyle=\delta(e,a)\cup\delta(f,a)

\displaystyle\delta(a,a^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\{1:a=a^{\prime}\}

\displaystyle\delta(e\cdot f,a)

\displaystyle=\{e^{\prime}\cdot f:e^{\prime}\in\delta(e,a)\}\cup\Delta(e,f,a)

\displaystyle\delta(p,a)

\displaystyle=\emptyset

\displaystyle\delta(e^{\star},a)

\displaystyle=\{e^{\prime}\cdot e^{\star}:e^{\prime}\in\delta(e,a)\}\ ,

where
\Delta(e,f,a)=\delta(f,a)
when
\epsilon(e)=1
, and
\emptyset
otherwise.

Finally, we give the continuation map for observations, which is similar to the one for actions, except that on sequential composition it is subtly different.

Definition 4.10

(
\and
-continuation)
.

We define
\zeta:{GR}\times\and\to 2^{{GR}}
inductively

\displaystyle\zeta(0,\alpha)

\displaystyle=\delta(1,\alpha)=\emptyset

\displaystyle\zeta(e+f,\alpha)

\displaystyle=\zeta(e,\alpha)\cup\zeta(f,\alpha)

\displaystyle\zeta(a,\alpha)

\displaystyle=\emptyset

\displaystyle\zeta(e\cdot f,\alpha)

\displaystyle=\{e^{\prime}\cdot f:e^{\prime}\in\zeta(e,\alpha)\}\cup Z(e,f,\alpha)

\displaystyle\zeta(p,\alpha)

\displaystyle=\{1:\pi_{\alpha}\leqq_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{BA}}p\}

\displaystyle\zeta(e^{\star},\alpha)

\displaystyle=\{e^{\prime}\cdot e^{\star}:e^{\prime}\in\zeta(e,\alpha)\}\ ,

where
Z(e,f,\alpha)=\zeta(f,\alpha)
when
\epsilon(e)=1
or
\epsilon(e^{\prime})=1
for some
e^{\prime}\in\zeta(e,\alpha)
, and
\emptyset
otherwise.

For instance, let
p,q\in P
.
If
\alpha\in\llbracket p\rrbracket_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{BA}}\cap\llbracket q\rrbracket_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{BA}}
, then we can calculate that

\zeta(p\cdot q,\alpha)=\{p^{\prime}\cdot q:p^{\prime}\in\zeta(p,\alpha)\}\cup Z(p,q,\alpha)=\{1\cdot q\}\cup\{1\}\ ,

which is to say that the program can either continue in
1\cdot q
, where it has to make an observation validating
q
, or it can choose to re-use the observation
\alpha
to validate
q
, continuing in
Z(p,q,\alpha)=\zeta(q,\alpha)=\{1\}
, because
1\in\zeta(p,\alpha)
.
This notion that
\zeta
can apply an observation more than once to validate multiple assertions in a row can be formalised.

Lemma 7
.

Let
e\in{GR}
,
\alpha\in\and
, and
e^{\prime}\in\zeta(e,\alpha)
.
Now
\zeta(e^{\prime},\alpha)\subseteq\zeta(e,\alpha)
.

To check that
\delta
and
\zeta
indeed output continuations of the term after the action or assertion has been performed, we should check that they are compatible with
\mathrel{\equiv_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{KAO}}}
.

Lemma 8
.

Let
e\in{GR}
.
For all
a\in\Sigma
and
e^{\prime}\in\delta(e,a)
, we have
a\cdot e^{\prime}\mathrel{\leqq_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{KAO}}}e
.
Furthermore, for all
\alpha\in\and
and
e^{\prime}\in\zeta(e,\alpha)
, we have
\pi_{\alpha}\cdot e^{\prime}\mathrel{\leqq_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{KAO}}}e
.

We also need the
reach
of a guarded rational term, which is meant to describe the set of terms that can be obtained by repeatedly applying continuation maps.

Definition 4.11

(Reach of a term)
.

For
e\in{GR}
, we define
\rho:{GR}\to 2^{{GR}}
inductively:

\displaystyle\rho(0)

\displaystyle=\emptyset

\displaystyle\rho(a)

\displaystyle=\{1,a\}

\displaystyle\rho(e+f)

\displaystyle=\rho(e)\cup\rho(f)

\displaystyle\rho(1)

\displaystyle=\{1\}

\displaystyle\rho(p)

\displaystyle=\{1,p\}

\displaystyle\rho(e\cdot f)

\displaystyle=\{e^{\prime}\cdot f:e^{\prime}\in\rho(e)\}\cup\rho(f)

\displaystyle\rho(e^{\star})

\displaystyle=\{1\}\cup\{e^{\prime}\cdot e^{\star}:e^{\prime}\in\rho(e)\}\ .

Indeed,
\rho(e)
truly contains all terms reachable from
e
by means of
\delta
and
\zeta
:

Lemma 9
.

Let
e\in{GR}
.
If
a\in\Sigma
, then
\delta(e,a)\subseteq\rho(e)
; if
e^{\prime}\in\rho(e)
, then
\delta(e^{\prime},a)\subseteq\rho(e)
.
Furthermore, if
\alpha\in\and
, then
\zeta(e,\alpha)\subseteq\rho(e)
; if
e^{\prime}\in\rho(e)
, then
\zeta(e^{\prime},\alpha)\subseteq\rho(e)
.

It is not hard to see that for all
e\in{GR}
, we have that
\rho(e)
is finite.
Note that
\rho(e)
does not, in general, contain
e
itself.
On the other hand,
\rho(e)
does contain a set of terms that is sufficient to reconstruct
e
, up to
\mathrel{\equiv_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{KAO}}}
.
We describe these as follows.

Definition 4.12

(Initial factors)
.

For
e\in{GR}
, we define
\iota:{GR}\to 2^{{GR}}
inductively:

\displaystyle\iota(0)

\displaystyle=\emptyset

\displaystyle\iota(a)

\displaystyle=\{a\}

\displaystyle\iota(e+f)

\displaystyle=\iota(e)\cup\iota(f)

\displaystyle\iota(1)

\displaystyle=\{1\}

\displaystyle\iota(p)

\displaystyle=\{p\}

\displaystyle\iota(e\cdot f)

\displaystyle=\{e^{\prime}\cdot f:e^{\prime}\in\iota(e)\}

\displaystyle\iota(e^{\star})

\displaystyle=\{1\}\cup\{e^{\prime}\cdot e^{\star}:e^{\prime}\in\iota(e)\}\ .

Now, to reconstruct
e
from
\iota(e)
, all we have to do is sum its elements.

Lemma 10
.

Let
e\in{GR}
.
Then
e\mathrel{\equiv_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{KAO}}}\sum_{e^{\prime}\in\iota(e)}e^{\prime}
.

5
Completeness

We now set out to show that
\llbracket-\rrbracket_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{KAO}}
characterises
\mathrel{\equiv_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{KAO}}}
.
Since soundness of
\llbracket-\rrbracket_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{KAO}}
w.r.t.
\mathrel{\equiv_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{KAO}}}
was already shown in
\cref
lemma:soundness, it remains to prove completeness, i.e., if
e
and
f
are interpreted equally by
\llbracket-\rrbracket_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{KAO}}
, then they can be proven equivalent via
\mathrel{\equiv_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{KAO}}}
.
To this end, we first identify a subset of guarded rational terms for which a completeness result can be shown by relying on the completeness result for KA.
To describe these terms, we need the following.

Definition 5.13

(Atomic and guarded terms)
.

Let
\Pi
denote the set
\{\pi_{\alpha}:\alpha\in\and\}
.
The set of
atomic guarded rational terms
, denoted
{AGR}
, is the subset of
{GR}
generated by the grammar

e,f::=0\;\;|\;\;1\;\;|\;\;a\in\Sigma\;\;|\;\;\pi_{\alpha}\in\Pi\;\;|\;\;e+f\;\;|\;\;e\cdot f\;\;|\;\;e^{\star}\ .

Furthermore, if
e\in{GR}
such that
\llbracket e\rrbracket_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{KAO}}=\llbracket e\rrbracket_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{WKAO}}
, we say that
e
is
closed
.

Completeness of
\mathrel{\equiv_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{KAO}}}
with respect to
\llbracket-\rrbracket_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{KAO}}
can then be established for atomic and closed guarded rational terms as follows.

Proposition 11
.

Let
e,f\in{AGR}
be closed, and
\llbracket e\rrbracket_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{KAO}}=\llbracket f\rrbracket_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{KAO}}
; then
e\mathrel{\equiv_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{KAO}}}f
.

Proof 5.14

(Sketch)
.

By noting that the semantics of an atomic and closed term coincide with the KA-semantics (over an extended alphabet); the result then follows by appealing to completeness of KA, and the fact that the axioms of KA are contained in those for KAO.

To show that
\llbracket-\rrbracket_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{KAO}}
characterises
\mathrel{\equiv_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{KAO}}}
for
all
guarded rational terms, it suffices to find for every
e\in{GR}
a closed
\hat{e}\in{AGR}
with
e\mathrel{\equiv_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{KAO}}}\hat{e}
.
After all, if we have such a transformation, then
\llbracket e\rrbracket_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{KAO}}=\llbracket f\rrbracket_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{KAO}}
implies
\llbracket\hat{e}\rrbracket_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{KAO}}=\llbracket\hat{f}\rrbracket_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{KAO}}
, which implies
\hat{e}\mathrel{\equiv_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{KAO}}}\hat{f}
, and hence
e\mathrel{\equiv_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{KAO}}}f
.

In the remainder of this section, we describe how to obtain a closed and atomic guarded rational term from a guarded rational term
e
.
This transformation works by first “disassembling”
e
using partial derivatives; on an intuitive level, this is akin to creating a (non-deterministic finite) automaton that accepts the observation language described by
e
.
Next, we “reassemble” from this representation an atomic term
\hat{e}
, equivalent to
e
; this is analogous to constructing a rational expression from the automaton.
To show that
\hat{e}
is closed, we leverage
\cref
lemma:double-derivatives, essentially arguing that the automaton obtained from
e
encodes
\mathrel{\preceq}
.

We extend the notation for vectors and matrices over rational terms to guarded rational terms.
We can then represent a guarded rational term by a matrix and a vector, as follows.

Definition 5.15
.

For
e\in{GR}
, define the
\rho(e)
-vector
x_{e}
and
\rho(e)
-matrix
M_{e}
by

x_{e}(e^{\prime})=\epsilon(e^{\prime})M_{e}(e^{\prime},e^{\prime\prime})=\sum_{e^{\prime\prime}\in\delta(e^{\prime},a)}a+\sum_{e^{\prime\prime}\in\zeta(e^{\prime},\alpha)}\pi_{\alpha}\ .

Note that, in the above,
\delta(e^{\prime},a)
and
\zeta(e^{\prime},\alpha)
are finite by
\cref
lemma:reach-contains-derivatives.

Kozen’s argument that matrices over rational terms satisfy the axioms of Kleene algebra
[
23
]
generalises to guarded rational terms.
This leads to a straightforward generalisation of
\cref
lemma:ka-matrix-fixpoint.
For the sake of brevity, we use
\mathsf{T}
to stand in for
\mathsf{WKAO}
or
\mathsf{KAO}
.

Lemma 12
.

Let
M
be a
Q
-matrix.
Using the entries of
M
and applying the operators of Kleene algebra, we can construct a
Q
-matrix
M^{\star}
, which has the following property.
Let
y
be any
Q
-vector; now
M^{\star}\cdot y
is the least (w.r.t.
\mathrel{\leqq_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}}}
)
Q
-vector
x
such that
M\cdot x+y\mathrel{\leqq_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}}}x
.

We can now use the above to reassemble a term from
M_{e}
and
x_{e}
as follows.

Definition 5.16

(Transformation of terms)
.

Let
e\in{GR}
.
We write
s_{e}
for the
\rho(e)
-vector given by
M_{e}^{\star}\cdot x_{e}
, and
\hat{e}
for the guarded rational term given by
\sum_{e^{\prime}\in\iota(e)}s_{e}(e^{\prime})
.

Since
\hat{e}
is constructed from
M_{e}
and
s_{e}
, and these are built using atomic guarded rational terms,
\hat{e}
is atomic.
We carry on to show that

Decision procedure

We now design a procedure that takes terms
e,f\in{GR}
and decides whether
\llbracket e\rrbracket_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{KAO}}=\llbracket f\rrbracket_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{KAO}}

\cref

To this end, we reduce to the problem of language equivalence between NFAs over guarded rational terms, defined using partial derivatives.
This, in turn, can be efficiently decided using bisimulation techniques.

First, observe that
{GR}

{thrm}

[Decision procedure]
For all

if and only if there exists

such that
(\iota(e),\iota(f))\in R
and

is a bisimulation up to congruence for the syntactic automaton restricted to
\rho(e)\cup\rho(f)
.

Proof 6.25

(Sketch)
.

By
\cref
theorem:bisim, such an
R
exists precisely when
\bigcup_{e^{\prime}\in\iota(e)}\llbracket e^{\prime}\rrbracket_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{KAO}}=\bigcup_{f^{\prime}\in\iota(f)}\llbracket f^{\prime}\rrbracket_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{KAO}}
; by
\cref

Example 6.26
.

We know that for all
a,b\in\Omega
we have that
a\wedge b\not\mathrel{\equiv_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{KAO}}}a\cdot b
.
This also follows from our decision procedure, which we argue by showing that any attempt to construct a bisimulation up to congruence
R
containing
(\{a\wedge b\},\{a\cdot b\})
fails.

We start with
R=\{(\{a\wedge b\},\{a\cdot b\})\}
, and note that
\bar{\epsilon}(\{a\wedge b\})=0=\bar{\epsilon}(\{a\cdot b\})
.
We now take a derivative w.r.t.
\alpha\in\and
such that
\pi_{\alpha}\leqq_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{BA}}a\wedge b
.
To grow
R
into a bisimulation up to congruence, all derivatives should be checked and possibly added to
R
; this specific choice, however, will lead to a counterexample.
We have that
\bar{\theta}(\{a\wedge b\},\alpha)=\{1\}
and
\bar{\theta}(\{a\cdot b\},\alpha)=\{1\cdot b,1\}
.
We add
(\{1\},\{1\cdot b,1\})
to
R
, noting that
\bar{\epsilon}(\{1\})=1=\bar{\epsilon}(\{1\cdot b,1\})
, and continue with the next derivative w.r.t.
\beta\in\and
such that
\pi_{\beta}\leqq_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{BA}}b
.
We get
\bar{\theta}(\{1\},\beta)=\emptyset
and
\bar{\theta}(\{1\cdot b,1\},\beta)=\{1\}
.
We now have
(\emptyset,\{1\})\in R
, but
\bar{\epsilon}(\emptyset)\neq\bar{\epsilon}(\{1\})
.
Thus, we cannot construct a bisimulation up to congruence
R
such that
(\{a\wedge b\},\{a\cdot b\})\in R
.

.

For
\mathsf{KAO}

Hence, for these
\mathsf{KAO}

[

]
.

Related work

This work fits in the larger tradition of Kleene algebra as a presentation of the “laws of programming”, the latter having been studied by Hoare and collaborators
[
14
,
13
]
.
More precisely, our efforts can be grouped with recent efforts to extend Kleene algebra with concurrency
[
15
,
13
,
32
,
21
]
, and thence with Boolean guards
[
19
]
.

We proved that
\mathrel{\equiv_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{KAO}}}
is sound and complete w.r.t.
\llbracket-\rrbracket_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{KAO}}
, based on the existing completeness proof for KA.
By re-using completeness results for a simpler algebra, the proof shows a clear separation of concerns between the “old” algebra being extended and the new layer of axioms placed on top.
This strategy pops up quite often in some form
[
29
,
1
,
32
,
21
]
.
We note that unlike
[
29
,
1
]
, our transformation does not proceed by induction on the term, but leverages the least fixpoints computable in every Kleene algebra.
Further, the combination of KA with additional hypotheses presented in
[
27
]
might yield another route to completeness.

The use of linear systems to study automata was pioneered by Conway
[
9
]
and Backhouse
[
3
]
.
Kozen’s completeness proof expanded on this by generalising Kleene algebra to matrices
[
23
]
.
The connection between linear systems, derivatives and completeness was studied by Kozen
[
26
]
and Jacobs
[
18
]
.
To keep our presentation simple, we give our proof of completeness in elementary terms; a proof in terms of coalgebra
[
38
]
may yet be possible.

Using bisimulation to decide language equivalence in a deterministic finite automaton originates from Hopcroft and Karp
[
17
]
.
This technique has many generalisations
[
36
]
.

8

Conclusions and further work

Kleene algebra with observations (KAO) is an algebraic framework that adds Boolean guards to Kleene algebra in such a way that a sensible extension with concurrency is still possible, in contrast with Kleene algebra with tests (KAT).
Indeed, the laws of KAO prevent the problem presented by
\cref
antinomy:problem.
The axiomatisation of KAO, as well as the decision procedure for equivalence, give an alternative foundation for combining KAO with concurrent Kleene algebra to arrive at a new equational calculus of concurrent programs.

The most obvious direction of further work is to define
concurrent Kleene algebra with observations
(
CKAO
) as the amalgamation of axioms of KAO and CKA, in pursuit of a characterisation of its equational theory and an analogous decidability result.
We conjecture that languages of
sp-pomsets

[
12
,
11
,
33
]
over actions and atoms, closed under some suitable relation, form the free model; a proof would likely build on
[
31
]
and re-use techniques from
[
21
]
.
While the equational theory of CKAO might be decidable, we are less optimistic about a feasible algorithm; deciding the equational theory of CKA is already
expspace
-complete
[
7
]
.

Another avenue of future research would be to create a programming language using KAO (or, possibly, CKAO) by instantiating actions and observations, and adding axioms that encode the intention of those primitives.
NetKAT
[
1
,
10
,
41
]
, a language for describing and reasoning about network behaviour is an excellent example of such an endeavour based on KAT.
Our long-term hope is that CKAO will function as a foundation for a “concurrent” version of NetKAT aimed at describing and reasoning about networks with concurrency.

Finally, we note that the decision procedure for KAO based on bisimulation up to congruence leaves room for optimisation.
Besides adapting the work on symbolic algorithms for bisimulation-based algorithms in KAT
[
34
]
, the transitivity property witnessed in
\cref
lemma:double-derivatives seems like it could sometimes allow a bisimulation-based algorithm to decide early.

Tests versus observations

We now investigate the relationships between models of
\mathsf{KAT}
and models of
\mathsf{KAO}
. To make this discussion more articulate, we note that both types of models are instances of a more general class of models: Kleene algebras
K
containing a subset
B\subseteq K
where
B
is a Boolean algebra.
For the purpose of this discussion, we assume a priori two (and only these two) correspondences between the two signatures
\langle K,0,1,+,\cdot,\star\rangle
and
\langle B,\bot,\top,\vee,\wedge,\overline{\cdot}\rangle
, namely:

(i)

that the natural order on

is compatible with the natural order on
K
, that is, for all

such that

, it also holds that
a+b=b
.

(ii)

the contraction law, that is, for all

it holds that
a\wedge b\leq a\cdot b
.

According to the laws of Boolean algebra,
\wedge
is a greatest lower bound (GLB) for elements of
B
.
In most semantics, one would like to interpret conjunction as an intersection, hence as a GLB in
K
(and not just in
B
).
Symbolically,

\forall x\in K,\ \forall a,b\in B,\ x\leq a\text{ and }x\leq b\implies x\leq a\wedge b\ .

(
Glb
)

Any
\mathsf{KAT}
satisfies (
Glb
), because for
x\in K
and
a,b\in B
with
x\leq a,b
, we have

x=x\cdot 1=x\cdot\left(b+\overline{b}\right)=x\cdot b+x\cdot\overline{b}\leq a\cdot b+b\cdot\overline{b}=a\cdot b+0=a\wedge b\ .

In most models of
\mathsf{KAT}
, including the language model and relational model, the Boolean sub-algebra is an ideal of the full algebra, i.e., it is downward-closed in the following sense:

\forall x\in K,\ \forall a\in B,\ x\leq a\implies x\in B\ .

(
Ideal
)

This is also the case in the language model of
\mathsf{KAO}
. For both algebras there are models that do not satisfy this property, but it is our impression that these are not the most useful.

Proposition 26
.

If (
Ideal
) holds, then so does (
Glb
).

Proof E.52
.

Let
x\in K
and
a,b\in B
such that
x\leq a,b
.
Since
a\in B
, we have
x\in B
by (
Ideal
).
Therefore, we have
x=x\wedge x\leq a\wedge b
.

Proposition 27
.

If (
Ideal
) holds, then
K
is a model of
\mathsf{KAO}
.

Proof E.53
.

First, we show that
a+b=a\vee b
.
Since
a+b\leqq_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{BA}}\top+\top\mathrel{\equiv_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{KA}}}\top
, we know that
a+b\in B
.
We also know that
a,b\mathrel{\leqq_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{KA}}}a+b
and that
a,b\leqq_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{BA}}a\vee b
, so we get:

a\vee b\mathrel{\leqq_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{KA}}}\left(a+b\right)\vee\left(a+b\right)\equiv_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{BA}}a+b\leqq_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{BA}}\left(a\vee b\right)+\left(a\vee b\right)\mathrel{\equiv_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{KA}}}a\vee b
.

Now, since
0\mathrel{\leqq_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{KA}}}\top
,
0
is also an element of
B
, and therefore we can conclude:

0\equiv_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{BA}}0\vee\bot=0+\bot\mathrel{\equiv_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{KA}}}\bot\ .

Consider now the following law, which holds trivially in any
\mathsf{KAT}
:

1=\top\ .

(
Units
)

Since
\mathsf{KAO}
distinguishes
\cdot
from
\wedge
, we distinguished their units as well.
This turns out to be important: identifying the units leads to identifying the products in a large class of models.

Proposition 28
.

Suppose (
Glb
) and (
Units
) hold.
Then for
a,b\in B
we have
a\cdot b=a\wedge b
.

Proof E.54
.

Since we have assumed the contraction law, we need to check
a\cdot b\leq a\wedge b
only.
Thanks to (
Glb
) this inequality reduces to checking that
a\cdot b\leq a
and
a\cdot b\leq b
.
Both of these hold, as for instance
a\cdot b\leq a\cdot\top=a\cdot 1=a
.

Proposition 29
.

Suppose (
Ideal
) holds.
Now
K
is a
\mathsf{KAT}
iff (
Units
) holds.

Proof E.55
.

The direct implication being straightforward, it remains to show the converse.
By
\cref
proposition:ideal-kao, we know that
K
is a model of
\mathsf{KAO}
, so it suffices to check that
\wedge
and
\cdot
coincide on
B
.
Thanks to
\cref
proposition:ideal-glb, we know that (
Glb
) holds, so we may conclude using
\cref
proposition:glb-unit-prods.

This concludes our comparison between models of
\mathsf{KAT}
and models of
\mathsf{KAO}
.
