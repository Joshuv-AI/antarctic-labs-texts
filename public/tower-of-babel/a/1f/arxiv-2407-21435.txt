Transient anisotropic kernel for probabilistic learning on manifolds Journal: ArXiv
Christian Soize, Roger Ghanem

Abstract
PLoM (Probabilistic Learning on Manifolds) is a method introduced in 2016 for handling small training datasets by projecting an Itô equation from a stochastic dissipative Hamiltonian dynamical system, acting as the MCMC generator, for which the KDE-estimated probability measure with the training dataset is the invariant measure. PLoM performs a projection on a reduced-order vector basis related to the training dataset, using the diffusion maps (DMAPS) basis constructed with a time-independent isotropic kernel. In this paper, we propose a new ISDE projection vector basis built from a transient anisotropic kernel, providing an alternative to the DMAPS basis to improve statistical surrogates for stochastic manifolds with heterogeneous data. The construction ensures that for times near the initial time, the DMAPS basis coincides with the transient basis. For larger times, the differences between the two bases are characterized by the angle of their spanned vector subspaces. The optimal instant yielding the optimal transient basis is determined using an estimation of mutual information from Information Theory, which is normalized by the entropy estimation to account for the effects of the number of realizations used in the estimations. Consequently, this new vector basis better represents statistical dependencies in the learned probability measure for any dimension. Three applications with varying levels of statistical complexity and data heterogeneity validate the proposed theory, showing that the transient anisotropic kernel improves the learned probability measure.

1.3
Methodology proposed and organization of the paper

Starting with a training dataset of
n_{d}
realizations of
\nu
random variables, we consider the probability flow from each of these
n_{d}
realizations towards the
\nu
-dimensional sampling probability distribution of the training dataset. We construct the associated coupled Fokker-Planck (FKP) equations with each of the realizations as initial condition. These describe the evolution of the transition probability measures over the graph described by the training dataset, that transport each independent realization, viewed as a concentrated measure at the initial time, into the joint probability measure, consisting of the common stationary probability measure (also called the steady-state solution) of the FKP equations. This evolution describes a trajectory along which transition probabilities are consistent with both the training dataset and its postulated joint probability density function (that we approximate using a Gaussian Kernel Density Estimate (KDE)). Each of these probabilities provides a distinct geometric characterization of the training dataset, with its own plausible model of statistical dependence, resulting in a transient anisotropic kernel from which we construct a time evolving PLoM. We then set criteria for selecting among these PLoM models, which is tantamount to identifying the most appropriate
statistical dependence structure for the learned dataset, along the flow characterized by the FKP equations. The following
description of the paper’s organization provides a coherent summary of the proposed methodology.

In Section

(centered and with an identity covariance matrix) and define the associated probability measure

Section

is the invariant measure, which is the steady-state solution. We then introduce a formal formulation of the eigenvalue problem of the FPK operator and the nonstationary solution of the Fokker-Planck equation with a deterministic initial condition.

Section

4.4
Finite approximation of operator

and of its eigenvalue problem

The Hilbert-Schmidt operator

We must thus construct a finite approximation of

Since

[

74
,
75
]
for Fokker-Planck equation and
[
76
]
for fractional Fokker-Planck equation) or such methods based on shape-morphing modes for solving the Fokker-Planck equation as proposed in
[

. Another classical method consists in introducing a finite family of functions in

, generating a finite dimension subspace of

, and in performing the projection of

on this finite subspace. Such an approach is not really adapted to operator

for which a large number of eigenvalues and associated eigenfunctions have to be computed.
It should be noted that a related problem, but distinct from the one addressed, is that of the numerical method for Schrödinger operator and the associated equation (see for instance
[
78
]
for solving the Schrödinger Equation,
[
79
]
for the solution of the Schrödinger equation by spectral methods,
[

]
for numerically solving the time-dependent Schrödinger equation, and
[

for the numerical solution of the Schrödinger equation using finite-difference method).
Nevertheless, such approaches are not well adapted to the objective of the actual developments, which has been detailed in Section

. We then propose to use a statistical sampling of

equipped with the probability measure

Using Eqs. (

Calculating

2\,\|{\bm{b}}({\bm{y}})\|\,\leq\,\frac{1}{\xi({\bm{y}})\,n_{d}\,\hat{s}^{2}}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{d}}\frac{\hat{s}}{s}\,\|{\bm{\eta}}^{j}\|\,\exp\left\{-\frac{1}{2\hat{s}^{2}}\|\frac{\hat{s}}{s}{\bm{\eta}}^{j}-{\bm{y}}\|^{2}\right\}+\frac{1}{\hat{s}^{2}}\,\|{\bm{y}}\|\,.

Since

The second inequality in Eq. (
5.10

\|{\bm{b}}({\bm{y}})-{\bm{b}}({\bm{y}}^{\prime})\|\,\,\leq\,\frac{1}{2\hat{s}^{2}}(\,\|{\bm{f}}({\bm{y}})-{\bm{f}}({\bm{y}}^{\prime})\|\,+\,\|{\bm{y}}-{\bm{y}}^{\prime}\|\,)\,,

in which

We must have
\|{\bm{f}}({\bm{y}})-{\bm{f}}({\bm{y}}^{\prime})\|\,\leq c\,\|{\bm{y}}-{\bm{y}}^{\prime}\|

\|\,[\nabla{\bm{f}}({\bm{y}})]\,\|_{F}\,\leq\,\tilde{c}<+\infty
, in which the
(\nu\times\nu)
-real matrix
[\nabla{\bm{f}}({\bm{y}})]

[\nabla{\bm{f}}({\bm{y}})]=\beta({\bm{y}})^{-1}\,[\nabla{\bm{a}}({\bm{y}})]-\beta({\bm{y}})^{-2}\,{\bm{a}}({\bm{y}})\otimes\nabla\beta({\bm{y}})

(c) We will admit that, for all

5.3

(i) Time sampling

(ii) ISDE discretization
.
Assuming that

\displaystyle\,[{\bm{Y}}_{\mu-1}]+\frac{\delta t}{2}\,[L([{\bm{Y}}_{\mu-1}])]+\sqrt{\delta t}\,[{\bm{\Gamma}}_{\mu}]\quad,\quad\mu\geq 1\,,

\displaystyle[{\bm{Y}}_{0}]=

\displaystyle\,[\eta_{d}]\,,\,a.s.\,,

at

, and where

\{[{\bm{\Gamma}}_{\mu}]_{kj}\,;k=1,\ldots,\nu\,;j=1,\ldots,n_{d}\,;\mu\geq 1\}

(iii) Convergence of the time-discrete approximation

For each

in

, Proposition
5
can be applied to the stochastic process

[{\bm{Y}}(t)]

approaches

tends to

[
87
]
, Page 323).

Lemma 4
(Strong convergence)

Under Proposition
5

(5.19)

where
[{\bm{Y}}_{\mu_{T}}]

5.17
) and (

is small enough

We can employ a criterion based on weak convergence, which is related to the covariance matrix

{\mathbb{R}}^{\nu}
-valued random variable

Let
\sigma_{{\bm{Y}}_{\mu_{T}}}^{2}

[{\bm{Y}}_{\mu_{T}}]

\sigma_{{\bm{Y}}_{\mu_{T}}}^{2}=E\{\,\|\,[{\bm{Y}}_{\mu_{T}}]-E\{[{\bm{Y}}_{\mu_{T}}]\}\,\|_{F}^{2}\}=\sum_{j=1}^{n_{d}}{\hbox{{\Ppcarac tr}}}[C_{{\bm{Y}}^{j}_{\mu_{T}}}]\,.

The criterion can then be based on the following properties,

\lim_{\delta t\rightarrow 0}\,\,|\,\sigma_{{\bm{Y}}_{\mu_{T}}}-\sigma_{{\bm{Y}}}(T)\,|\,=\,0\,.

(5.23)

We will detailed this criterion in paragraph (vi).

\{Y_{r}(t),t\geq 0\}
is a second-order Gaussian stochastic process, which is explicitely defined by

Y_{r}(t)=x\,e^{-t/2}+\int_{0}^{\,t}e^{-(t-\tau)/2}\,dW_{r}(\tau)\,.

(6.3)

A simple calculation shows that the mean value
m_{r}(t)=E\{Y_{r}(t)\}
and the standard deviation

\sigma_{r}(t)=(E\{(Y_{r}(t)-m_{r}(t))^{2}\})^{1/2}
of the random variable
Y_{r}(t)
for fixed
t>0
, are written as

m_{r}(t)=x\,e^{-t/2}\quad,\quad\sigma_{r}(t)=\sqrt{1-e^{-t}}\,.

(6.4)

For all
t>0
, as
\{Y_{r}(t)\,|\,Y_{r}(0)=x\}
is a Gaussian random variable, the transition probability density function is

\rho_{r}(y,t\,|\,x,0)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}\,\sigma_{r}(t)}\exp\left\{-\frac{1}{2\sigma_{r}(t)^{2}}(y-m_{r}(t))^{2}\right\}\,.

(6.5)

Note that Eqs. (
6.4
) and (
6.5
) show that, we effectively have

\lim_{t\rightarrow 0_{+}}\rho_{r}(y,t\,|\,x,0)\,dy=\delta_{0}(y-x)
(see Eq. (
5.6
)) and

\lim_{t\rightarrow+\infty}\rho_{r}(y,t\,|\,x,0)=p_{H_{r}}(y)
(see Eq. (
5.8
)).
It can be deduced that the kernel
k_{r,t}(y,x)
on
{\mathbb{R}}\times{\mathbb{R}}
, defined by Eq. (
4.1
) is written, for
t>0
, as

k_{r,t}(y,x)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-e^{-t}}}\exp\left\{-\frac{1}{2}\frac{e^{-t}}{(1-e^{-t})}(y^{2}+x^{2}-2e^{t/2}y\,x)^{2}\right\}\,.

(6.6)

Let
\{h_{\alpha}(y)\,,\alpha\in{\mathbb{N}}\}
be the Hermite polynomials and
\{h_{\alpha}(y)/\sqrt{\alpha!}\,,\alpha\in{\mathbb{N}}\}
the Hilbert basis in
L^{2}({\mathbb{R}};p_{H_{r}})
,

\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{h_{\alpha}(y)}{\sqrt{\alpha!}}\,\frac{h_{\beta}(y)}{\sqrt{\beta!}}\,p_{H_{r}}(y)\,dy=\delta_{\alpha\beta}\quad,\quad h_{0}(y)=1\quad,\quad h_{1}(y)=y\quad,\quad h_{\alpha+1}(y)=y\,h_{\alpha}(y)-\frac{d}{dy}h_{\alpha}(y)\,.

Let
\{{\mathbb{h}}_{\alpha}(y)\,,\alpha\in{\mathbb{N}}\}
be the polynomials defined by

h_{\alpha}(y)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2^{\alpha}}}\,{\mathbb{h}}_{\alpha}(\frac{y}{\sqrt{2}})\quad,\quad{\mathbb{h}}_{\alpha}(y)=\sqrt{2^{\alpha}}\,h_{\alpha}(\sqrt{2}\,y)\,.

We have the formula
[
89
]

\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-4a^{2}}}\exp\left\{\frac{4a}{1-4a^{2}}(\hat{x}\,\hat{y}-a\hat{x}^{2}-a\hat{y}^{2})\right\}=\sum_{\alpha\in{\mathbb{N}}}\frac{a^{\alpha}}{\alpha!}\,{\mathbb{h}}_{\alpha}(\hat{x})\,{\mathbb{h}}_{\alpha}(\hat{y})\,.

Taking
x=\sqrt{2}\,\hat{x}
,
y=\sqrt{2}\,\hat{y}
, and
a=\frac{1}{2}e^{-t/2}<1/2
for
t>0
, Eq. (
6.6
) can be rewritten as

k_{r,t}(y,x)=\sum_{\alpha\in{\mathbb{N}}}e^{-\lambda_{r,\alpha}t}\psi_{r,\alpha}(y)\,\psi_{r,\alpha}(x)\,,

(6.7)

\lambda_{r,\alpha}=\frac{\alpha}{2}\quad,\quad\psi_{r,\alpha}(y)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2^{\alpha}\,\alpha!}}\,{\mathbb{h}}_{\alpha}(\frac{y}{\sqrt{2}})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha!}}\,h_{\alpha}(y)\,,

(6.8)

and thus, for
\alpha
and
\beta
in
{\mathbb{N}}
,

\int_{\mathbb{R}}\psi_{r,\alpha}(y)\,\psi_{r,\,\beta}(y)\,p_{H_{r}}(y)\,dy=\delta_{\alpha,\,\beta}\,.

(6.9)

Comparing Eqs. (

are

\lambda_{r,\alpha}=\frac{\alpha}{2}\quad,\quad\alpha\in{\mathbb{N}}\,.

(6.10)

7.3
Criteria for comparing the reduced-order transient basis with the reduced-order DMAPS basis

The PLoM algorithm is based on the use of the RODB (see
A
). With such an RODB based on the isotropic kernel, PLoM has proven generally efficient, even in extremely difficult cases, as demonstrated in numerous publications since 2016. This efficacy will also be evident through the applications presented in Section
8
. However, for cases involving very heterogeneous data in the training dataset, the learned statistical dependence between the components of the random vector
{\bm{H}}
(through the learned probability measure for
{\bm{H}}
) can
a priori
be improved using ROTB
({n\Delta t})
for given
n
. To assess a possible improvement with this reduced-order transient basis compared to the reduced-order DMAPS basis, quantitative criteria are necessary. In this section, we introduce criteria for comparing the two vector bases, and then, in Section
7.4
, we will present a methodology for identifying the instance,
{n\Delta t}
, that maximizes the selection criteria of the ROTB
({n\Delta t})
.

(i) The first criterion will be the angle between the two vector subspaces generated by the two vector bases, ROTB
({n\Delta t})
and RODB. If this angle is close to zero then the two bases coincide. This must be the case when we choose the instant
n=1
for the ROTB
({n\Delta t})
(See Proposition
7
).

(ii) The second criterion is the concentration of the learned probability measure in relation to the concentration of the probability measure of the training dataset. It has been proven that the PLoM algorithm, which uses the RODB, was designed to preserve the concentration of the learned probability measure from a small training dataset. For this, in
[
27
,
29
]
, we introduced the indicator
d^{2}
, linked to the mean-square convergence, which we will recall and use. We also introduce a second concentration criterion, KL, based on the Kullback-Leibler divergence
[
90
,
91
]
between the learned probability measure and the probability measure of the training dataset.

(iii) The third criterion is mutual information
[
92
,
93
]
, which is defined as the relative entropy introduced by Kullback and Leibler
[
91
]
. This will be used to quantify the level of statistical dependencies among the components of the centered random vector
{\bm{H}}
, whose covariance matrix is the identity matrix. Such mutual information will be estimated for the learned probability measure generated by PLoM, comparing the RODB and the ROTB
({n\Delta t})
as a function of
n
.

(iv) The last criterion is the Entropy from Information Theory
[
94
,
93
,
95
]
, introduced by Shannon
[
96
]
. The estimation of this entropy is a function of the number of realizations used for estimating the probability measure. This property will be used in Section
7.4
to normalize the estimation of the mutual information.

7.3.1
Angle between the subspaces spanned by ROTB
({n\Delta t})
and RODB

Let
n
be fixed in
{\mathcal{N}}
. It is assumed that
\text{rank}([g_{\hbox{{\pppppcarac DM}}}])=\text{rank}([g({n\Delta t})])=m_{\hbox{{\ppppcarac opt}}}
in which the two matrices are defined by Eqs. (
7.13
) and (
7.16
). In addition, it is assumed that the null space of the matrix
[g({n\Delta t})]^{T}\,[g_{\hbox{{\pppppcarac DM}}}]
is
\{0\}
.
Let
V({n\Delta t})=\text{span}\{{\bm{g}}^{1}({n\Delta t}),\ldots,{\bm{g}}^{m_{\hbox{{\pppppcarac opt}}}}({n\Delta t})\}
be the
m_{\hbox{{\ppppcarac opt}}}
-dimension subspace of
{\mathbb{R}}^{n_{d}}
(spanned by the ROTB
({n\Delta t})
).
Let
V_{\hbox{{\pppppcarac DM}}}=\text{span}\{{\bm{g}}^{1}_{\hbox{{\pppppcarac DM}}},\ldots,{\bm{g}}_{\hbox{{\pppppcarac DM}}}^{m_{\hbox{{\pppppcarac opt}}}}\}
be the
m_{\hbox{{\ppppcarac opt}}}
-dimension subspace of
{\mathbb{R}}^{n_{d}}
(spanned by the RODB).
For
\beta\in\{1,\ldots m_{\hbox{{\ppppcarac opt}}}\}
, let
\hat{g}_{\hbox{{\pppppcarac DM}}}^{\,\beta}=g_{\hbox{{\pppppcarac DM}}}^{\,\beta}/\|g_{\hbox{{\pppppcarac DM}}}^{\,\beta}\|
be the normalized vector
g_{\hbox{{\pppppcarac DM}}}^{\,\beta}
and let
\hat{g}^{\,\beta}({n\Delta t})=g^{\,\beta}({n\Delta t})/\|g^{\,\beta}({n\Delta t})\|
be the normalized vector
g^{\,\beta}({n\Delta t})

Let
[\hat{g}_{\hbox{{\pppppcarac DM}}}]=[\hat{g}^{1}_{\hbox{{\pppppcarac DM}}}\ldots\hat{g}_{\hbox{{\pppppcarac DM}}}^{m_{\hbox{{\pppppcarac opt}}}}]

The angle

between the subspaces

7.4
Identification methodology for the instant maximizing the selection criterion of the ROTB
({n\Delta t})

In this section, all quantities denoted with a hat (such as
\hat{I}({\bm{H}})
) represent the estimated values of the corresponding quantities without a hat (such as
I({\bm{H}})
), using the realizations as explained in
B
.
Let
n_{d}
be the number of points in the training dataset and
n_{\hbox{{\ppppcarac ar}}}=n_{d}\times n_{\hbox{{\pppppcarac MCH}}}\gg n_{d}
be the number of learned realizations with PLoM (see
A.3
), either with the RODB or with the ROTB
({n\Delta t})
.

(i) Defining the subset
{\mathscr{C}}_{N}
of
{\mathcal{N}}
containing the admissible values of
n
.
Let
N
be the largest value of
n
for which the ROTB
({n\Delta t})
, represented by matrix
[g({n\Delta t})]
, is computed. This value
N
being fixed, the set
{\mathcal{N}}=\{1,\ldots,N\}
defined by Eq. (
5.24
) is fixed.
The subset
{\mathscr{C}}_{N}
of the admissible values of
n
is then defined by

{\mathscr{C}}_{N}=\{n\in{\mathcal{N}}\,,\,\frac{1}{\nu}\,\hat{d}_{\hbox{{\pppppcarac TB}}}^{\,2}(m_{\hbox{{\ppppcarac opt}}};{n\Delta t})\leq\tau_{c}\ll 1\}\,,

(7.29)

where
\hat{d}_{\hbox{{\pppppcarac TB}}}^{\,2}(m_{\hbox{{\ppppcarac opt}}};{n\Delta t})
is defined by Eq. (
7.21
) and where
0<\tau_{c}\ll 1
is fixed sufficiently small with respect to
1
in order to preserve the concentration of the learned probability measure (see Section
7.3
-(i) and
A.4
). It should be noted that the maximum value of
d_{\hbox{{\pppppcarac DB}}}^{\,2}(n_{d})
is
2
(corresponding to a measure concentration completely lost, which can be obtained with classical MCMC algorithms). The PLoM algorithm allows for preserving the concentration of the learned probability measure
[
27
]
yielding values of the order
0.01
to
0.1
through all the performed applications. For defining the concentration criterion in Eq. (
7.29
), we have normalized with respect to dimension
\nu
.

(ii) Characterizing a better ROTB
({n\Delta t})
compared to RODB
.
From Proposition
7
, in particular from Eq. (
7.7
), it can be deduced that, for
n=1
, we have
\hat{I}({\bm{H}}_{\hbox{{\pppppcarac TB}}}\,;1\times\Delta t)\simeq\hat{I}({\bm{H}}_{\hbox{{\pppppcarac DB}}})
for
\Delta t
sufficiently small (which is the considered case).
Let us assume that
\hat{I}({\bm{H}})<\hat{I}({\bm{H}}_{\hbox{{\pppppcarac DM}}})
. For
n
fixed in
{\mathscr{C}}_{N}
, we will say that the ROTB
({n\Delta t})
, represented by matrix
[g({n\Delta t})]
, is better than the RODB, represented by matrix
[g_{\hbox{{\pppppcarac DM}}}]
, if

\hat{I}({\bm{H}})\leq\hat{I}({\bm{H}}_{\hbox{{\pppppcarac TB}}}\,;{n\Delta t})<\hat{I}({\bm{H}}_{\hbox{{\pppppcarac DM}}})\,.

(7.30)

(iii) Determining the optimal value
n_{\hbox{{\ppppcarac opt}}}
of
n
in
{\mathscr{C}}_{N}
.
The underlying idea is to select a reduced-order transient basis that gives a learned probability measure whose mutual information is as close as possible to the mutual information of the probability measure
p_{\bm{H}}({\bm{\eta}}),d{\bm{\eta}}
.
Based on paragraph (ii) above, the optimal transient basis
[g(n_{\hbox{{\ppppcarac opt}}})]
among the set of possible
transient bases
\{[g({n\Delta t})],n\in{\mathcal{N}}\}
is obtained for
n=n_{\hbox{{\ppppcarac opt}}}
such that

n_{\hbox{{\ppppcarac opt}}}=\arg\min_{n\,\in{\mathscr{C}}_{N}}\hat{I}({\bm{H}}_{\hbox{{\pppppcarac TB}}}\,;{n\Delta t})\,.

(7.31)

(iv) Defining the normalized estimate of the mutual information
.
As explained in
B.3
, if
n_{\rm{samp}}
is the number of realizations (either
n_{d}
or
n_{\hbox{{\ppppcarac ar}}}
), the entropy estimation asymptotically decreases as
-log(n_{\rm{samp}})
when
n_{\rm{samp}}
increases.
For comparing the estimate of the mutual information for
{\bm{H}}
, which uses
n_{d}
realizations, with the one of
{\bm{H}}_{\hbox{{\pppppcarac DB}}}
or
{\bm{H}}_{\hbox{{\pppppcarac TB}}}({n\Delta t})
, which uses
n_{\hbox{{\ppppcarac ar}}}\gg n_{d}
learned realizations, we must normalize the estimated mutual information with respect to the number of realizations. Taking into account the relationship between the mutual information and the entropy estimates (see
B.3
), we chose to normalize the estimated mutual information by dividing by the function
\chi+\log(n_{\rm{samp}})
(with
n_{\rm{samp}}=n_{d}
or
n_{\rm{samp}}=n_{\hbox{{\ppppcarac ar}}}
), where
\chi
is a real number that must be identified and which must be such that
\chi+log(n_{d})>0
. Since
n_{\hbox{{\ppppcarac ar}}}>n_{d}
this condition implies that
\chi+log(n_{\hbox{{\ppppcarac ar}}})>0
is automatically verified.
For the
{\mathbb{R}}^{\nu}
-valued random variables
{\bm{H}}
,
{\bm{H}}_{\hbox{{\pppppcarac DB}}}
, and
{\bm{H}}_{\hbox{{\pppppcarac TB}}}({n\Delta t})
, the normalized estimated mutual information are then defined by

\hat{I}_{\hbox{{\pppppcarac norm}}}({\bm{H}})=\frac{\hat{I}({\bm{H}})}{\chi+log(n_{d})}\quad,\quad\hat{I}_{\hbox{{\pppppcarac norm}}}({\bm{H}}_{\hbox{{\pppppcarac DB}}})=\frac{\hat{I}({\bm{H}}_{\hbox{{\pppppcarac DB}}})}{\chi+log(n_{\hbox{{\ppppcarac ar}}})}\quad,\quad\hat{I}_{\hbox{{\pppppcarac norm}}}({\bm{H}}_{\hbox{{\pppppcarac TB}}}\,;{n\Delta t})=\frac{\hat{I}({\bm{H}}_{\hbox{{\pppppcarac TB}}}\,;{n\Delta t})}{\chi+log(n_{\hbox{{\ppppcarac ar}}})}\,.

(7.32)

in which
\hat{I}({\bm{H}})
,
\hat{I}({\bm{H}}_{\hbox{{\pppppcarac DB}}})
, and
\hat{I}({\bm{H}}_{\hbox{{\pppppcarac TB}}}\,;{n\Delta t})
are the estimates (computed with Eq. (
B.6
)) of the mutual information
I({\bm{H}})
,
I({\bm{H}}_{\hbox{{\pppppcarac DB}}})
, and
I({\bm{H}}_{\hbox{{\pppppcarac TB}}}\,;{n\Delta t})
defined by Eqs. (
7.24
) to (
7.26
).
Based on paragraph (iii) above, constant
\chi
is defined as follows. Let
\chi_{\hbox{{\ppppcarac opt}}}
be the solution in
\chi
of the equation,

\frac{\hat{I}({\bm{H}})}{\chi+log(n_{d})}=\frac{\hat{I}({\bm{H}}_{\hbox{{\pppppcarac TB}}}\,;n_{\hbox{{\ppppcarac opt}}}\,\Delta t)}{\chi+log(n_{\hbox{{\ppppcarac ar}}})}\,.

(7.33)

If
\chi_{\hbox{{\ppppcarac opt}}}+\log(n_{d})>0
, then
\chi_{\hbox{{\ppppcarac opt}}}
is the desired value of
\chi
.
It should be noted that the optimal value
n_{\hbox{{\ppppcarac opt}}}
is calculated with the estimation of the non-normalized mutual information (see Eq. (
7.31
). Normalization is only introduced for the purpose of comparing the value of this criterion for
{\bm{H}}
and
{\bm{H}}_{\hbox{{\pppppcarac TB}}}
.

(v) Identification of the instant maximizing the selection criterion of the ROTB
({n\Delta t})
.
Let
n_{\hbox{{\ppppcarac opt}}}
be defined by Eq. (
7.31
), and let
\chi_{\hbox{{\ppppcarac opt}}}
be identified by solving Eq. (
7.33
). Then, we have

\hat{I}_{\hbox{{\pppppcarac norm}}}({\bm{H}})=\hat{I}_{\hbox{{\pppppcarac norm}}}({\bm{H}}_{\hbox{{\pppppcarac TB}}}\,;n_{\hbox{{\ppppcarac opt}}}\Delta t)\leq\hat{I}_{\hbox{{\pppppcarac norm}}}({\bm{H}}_{\hbox{{\pppppcarac TB}}}\,;{n\Delta t})\quad,\quad\forall n\in{\mathscr{C}}_{N}\,.

(7.34)

We can then conclude that the ROTB
(n_{\hbox{{\ppppcarac opt}}}\Delta t)
, represented by matrix
[g(n_{\hbox{{\ppppcarac opt}}}\Delta t)]
, is better than the RODB, represented by matrix
[g_{\hbox{{\pppppcarac DM}}}]
. The proof is straightforward.

8.1
Preamble

We will present three applications, each with its own specificities. However, as the generation of the training dataset related to the random vector

{\bm{H}}
with values in
{\mathbb{R}}^{\nu}

Application 1

{\bm{H}}
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{9}
(

{\mathbb{R}}^{9}

Application 2
. For the second application (
Appli 2

n_{d}

{\bm{H}}
with values in
{\mathbb{R}}^{8}

3

6

8

12

13

17

{\mathbb{R}}^{8}

Application 3
. The third application (
Appli 3
) results from a statistical treatment of an experimental database containing photon measurements in the ATLAS detector at CERN. This dataset was obtained by loading the file ’pid22_E262144_eta_20_25_voxalisation.csv’ from the free access CERN Open Data Portal. The PCA step of PLoM has been performed, and an extraction of
45

{\mathbb{R}}^{45}

{\bm{H}}

8.2
Additional convergence analysis conducted for the three applications

For each of the three applications, we will provide detailed analyses of the calculation of the optimal value of the instant

, which allows for the selection of the best reduced-order transient basis (ROTB). We will also present the convergence results of the PLoM algorithms under the normalization constraints (see

(i) Convergence with respect to the value of

(ii) Convergence with respect to the size of

\Delta t
is already small enough to achieve good accuracy with the Euler scheme used to integrate the ISDE. We found that

is a good value.

(iii) Convergence with respect to
n_{\hbox{{\pppppcarac MC}}}
. This analysis was performed by examining the convergence of the angle
\gamma({n\Delta t})
for
n\in{\mathcal{N}}
. We observed that a large value of

9
Conclusion

In this paper, we have presented the theoretical elements of constructing a time-dependent anisotropic kernel, which allows us to create a data projection basis for PLoM. This basis serves as an alternative to the DMAPS basis built using a time-independent isotropic kernel used by PLoM. We have demonstrated that an optimal time can be determined to obtain an optimal transient basis, best respecting the statistical dependence between the components for the learned joint probability measure.

The proposed theory has been developed to improve PLoM in cases of highly heterogeneous data. The improvement of the learned joint probability measure is quantified by estimating an objective criterion from information theory, namely the mutual information, which we have normalized relative to the number of realizations using entropy.

This theory is consistent in the sense that, for a time close to the initial time, the DMAPS basis constructed with the time-independent isotropic kernel coincides with the transient basis constructed with the time-dependent anisotropic kernel. Thus, we can characterize the difference between the two bases by the angle of the vector subspaces they generate.

The theory is illustrated through three applications with decreasing levels of data heterogeneity. The three applications confirm that PLoM with the DMAPS basis (time-independent isotropic kernel) always results in learning that preserves the concentration of the measure, unlike the classic MCMC approach. The applications show that it is possible to improve the learned joint probability measure with the transient anisotropic kernel, which a priori allows for better estimates of conditional statistics.

Overview of the probabilistic learning on manifolds (PLoM) algorithm and its parameterization

The PLoM approach
[
1
,
27
,
29
]
, which has specifically been developed for small
data (as opposed to big data) starts from a training dataset
{\mathcal{D}}_{d}
made up of a relatively small number

n_{d}
of points. It is assumed that
{\mathcal{D}}_{d}
is generated with an underlying stochastic manifold related
to a
{\mathbb{R}}^{n_{x}}
-valued random variable
{\bm{X}}=({\bm{Q}},{\bm{W}})
, defined on a probability space
(\Theta,{\mathcal{T}},{\mathcal{P}})
,
in which
{\bm{Q}}
is the quantity of interest that is a
{\mathbb{R}}^{n_{q}}
-random variable, where
{\bm{W}}
is the
control parameter that is a
{\mathbb{R}}^{n_{w}}
-random variable, and where
n_{x}=n_{q}+n_{w}
. Another

{\mathbb{R}}^{n_{u}}
- valued random variable
{\bm{U}}
defined on
(\Theta,{\mathcal{T}},{\mathcal{P}})
is also be considered,
which is an uncontrolled parameter and/or a noise. Random variable
{\bm{Q}}
is assumed to be
written as
{\bm{Q}}={\bm{f}}({\bm{U}},{\bm{W}})
in which the measurable mapping
{\bm{f}}
is not explicitly known.
The joint probability distribution
P_{{\bm{W}},{\bm{U}}}(d{\bm{w}},d{\bm{u}})
of
{\bm{W}}
and
{\bm{U}}
is assumed to be given.
The non-Gaussian probability measure
P_{\bm{X}}({\bm{x}})=P_{{\bm{Q}},{\bm{W}}}(d{\bm{q}},d{\bm{w}})
of
{\bm{X}}=({\bm{Q}},{\bm{W}})

is concentrated in a region of
{\mathbb{R}}^{n_{x}}
for which the only available information is the cloud of
the points of training dataset
{\mathcal{D}}_{d}
. The PLoM method makes it possible to generate the learned dataset

{\mathcal{D}}_{\hbox{{\ppppcarac ar}}}
for
{\bm{X}}
whose
n_{\hbox{{\ppppcarac ar}}}\gg n_{d}
points (learned realizations) are generated by the
non-Gaussian probability measure that is estimated using the training dataset. The concentration of
the probability measure is preserved thanks to the use of a diffusion-maps basis that allows to
enrich the available information from the training dataset.
The training dataset
{\mathcal{D}}_{d}
is made up of the
n_{d}
independent realizations

{\bm{x}}_{d}^{j}=({\bm{q}}_{d}^{j},{\bm{w}}_{d}^{j})
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{n_{x}}={\mathbb{R}}^{n_{q}}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{n_{w}}
for
j\in\{1,\ldots,n_{d}\}

of random variable
{\bm{X}}=({\bm{Q}},{\bm{W}})
. The PLoM method allows for generating the learned dataset

{\mathcal{D}}_{\hbox{{\ppppcarac ar}}}
made up of
n_{\hbox{{\ppppcarac ar}}}\gg n_{d}
learned realizations
\{{\bm{x}}_{\hbox{{\ppppcarac ar}}}^{\ell},\ell=1,\ldots,n_{\hbox{{\ppppcarac ar}}}\}

of random vector
{\bm{X}}
. As soon as the learned dataset has been constructed, the learned realizations
for
{\bm{Q}}
and
{\bm{W}}
can be extracted as
({\bm{q}}_{\hbox{{\ppppcarac ar}}}^{\ell},{\bm{w}}_{\hbox{{\ppppcarac ar}}}^{\ell})={\bm{x}}_{\hbox{{\ppppcarac ar}}}^{\ell}
for
\ell=1,\ldots,n_{\hbox{{\ppppcarac ar}}}
.
Using the learned dataset
{\mathcal{D}}_{\hbox{{\ppppcarac ar}}}
, PLoM allows for carrying out any conditional statistics such as
{\bm{w}}\mapsto E\{{\bm{\xi}}({\bm{Q}})|{\bm{W}}={\bm{w}}\}
from
{\mathscr{C}}_{w}
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{n_{\xi}}
, in which
{\bm{\xi}}
is a given measurable mapping from
{\mathbb{R}}^{n_{q}}
into
{\mathbb{R}}^{n_{\xi}}
, that is to say to construct statistical surrogate models (metamodels) in a probabilistic framework.

n_{d}
independent realizations

[x_{d}]=[{\bm{x}}_{d}^{1}\ldots{\bm{x}}_{d}^{n_{d}}]

n_{d}

{\bm{X}}
. Using the PCA of

In this Appendix, the PLoM reduced-order basis will be represented by the matrix
[g_{\hbox{{\ppppcarac opt}}}]\in{\mathbb{M}}_{n_{d},m_{\hbox{{\pppppcarac opt}}}}
. Depending on the context of its use, this matrix will either be

A.3

[{\bm{H}}]

[{\bm{Z}}]
is a
{\mathbb{M}}_{\nu,m_{\hbox{{\pppppcarac opt}}}}

[{\bm{Z}}]
belongs to the class of Hamiltonian Monte Carlo methods, is explicitly described in
[
1
]
, and is mathematically detailed in Theorem 6.3 of
[
27
]
. This generator allows for computing
n_{\hbox{{\pppppcarac MCH}}}
realizations

[{\bm{H}}_{\hbox{{\ppppcarac ar}}}]

(A.7)

A.6
Constraints on the second-order moments of the components of
{\bm{H}}_{\hbox{{\ppppcarac ar}}}

In general, the mean value of
{\bm{H}}_{\hbox{{\ppppcarac ar}}}
estimated using the
n_{\hbox{{\ppppcarac ar}}}
learned realizations

\{{\bm{\eta}}_{\hbox{{\ppppcarac ar}}}^{\ell^{\prime}},\ell^{\prime}=1,\ldots,n_{\hbox{{\ppppcarac ar}}}\}
, is sufficiently close to zero.
Likewise, the estimate of the covariance matrix of
{\bm{H}}_{\hbox{{\ppppcarac ar}}}
, which must be the identity matrix, is
sufficiently close to a diagonal matrix. However, sometimes the
diagonal entries of the estimated covariance matrix can be lower than
1
.
Normalization can be recovered by imposing constraints

E\{(H_{{\hbox{{\ppppcarac ar}}},k})^{2}\}=1\,\,,\,\,k=1,\ldots,\nu\,,

(A.9)

in the algorithm presented in
A.5
.
For that, we use the method and the iterative algorithm presented in
[
29
]
(that is based on Sections 5.5 and 5.6 of
[
18
]
). The constraints are imposed by using the Kullback-Leibler minimum cross-entropy principle. The resulting optimization problem is formulated using a Lagrange multiplier
{\bm{\lambda}}=(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{\nu})
associated with the constraints. The optimal solution of the Lagrange multiplier is computed using an efficient iterative algorithm. At each iteration, the MCMC generator detailed in
A.5
is used.
The constraints are rewritten as

E\{{\bm{h}}({\bm{H}}_{\hbox{{\ppppcarac ar}}})\}={\bm{b}}\,,

(A.10)

in which the function
{\bm{h}}=(h_{1},\ldots,h_{\nu})
and the vector
{\bm{b}}=(b_{1},\ldots,b_{\nu})
are such that

h_{k}({\bm{H}}_{\hbox{{\ppppcarac ar}}})=(H_{{\hbox{{\ppppcarac ar}}},k})^{2}
and
b_{k}=1
for
k
in
\{1,\ldots,\nu\}
.
To take into account the constraints in the algorithm presented in
A.5
, Eq. (
A.8
) is replaced by the following one,

[L_{\bm{\lambda}}([u])]_{kj}=\frac{1}{p({\bm{u}}^{j})}\,\{{\bm{\nabla}}_{\!\!{\bm{u}}^{j}}\,p({\bm{u}}^{j})\}_{k}-2\,\lambda_{k}u_{k}^{j}\,.

(A.11)

It should be noted that Eqs. (
A.9
) to (

) can be straightforwardly extended to the case in which the constraint defined by
Eq. (
A.9
) is replaced by the full second-order moment constraints
E\{{\bm{H}}_{\hbox{{\ppppcarac ar}}}\}={\hbox{\bf 0}}_{\nu}
and
E\{{\bm{H}}_{\hbox{{\ppppcarac ar}}}\otimes{\bm{H}}_{\hbox{{\ppppcarac ar}}}\}=[I_{\nu}]
, that is to say,

E\{H_{{\hbox{{\ppppcarac ar}}},k}\}=0\,,\,E\{(H_{{\hbox{{\ppppcarac ar}}},k})^{2}\}=1\,\,,\,\,k=1,\ldots,\nu\quad,\quad E\{H_{{\hbox{{\ppppcarac ar}}},k}H_{{\hbox{{\ppppcarac ar}}},k^{\prime}}\}=0\,\,,\,\,1\leq k<k^{\prime}\leq\nu\,.

(A.12)

The iteration algorithm for computing
{\bm{\lambda}}^{i+1}
as a function of
{\bm{\lambda}}^{i}
is the following,

\displaystyle{\bm{\lambda}}^{i+1}

\displaystyle={\bm{\lambda}}^{i}-\alpha_{i}[\Gamma^{\prime\prime}({\bm{\lambda}}^{i})]^{-1}\,{\bm{\Gamma}}^{\prime}({\bm{\lambda}}^{i})\quad,\quad i\geq 0\,,

\displaystyle{\bm{\lambda}}^{0}

\displaystyle={\hbox{\bf 0}}_{\nu}\,,

in which
{\bm{\Gamma}}^{\prime}({\bm{\lambda}}^{i})={\bm{b}}-E\{{\bm{h}}({\bm{H}}_{{\bm{\lambda}}^{i}})\}
and

[\Gamma^{\prime\prime}({\bm{\lambda}}^{i})]=[\hbox{{\Ppcarac cov}}\{{\bm{h}}({\bm{H}}_{{\bm{\lambda}}^{i}})\}]
(the covariance matrix), and where
\alpha_{i}
is a relaxation function (less than
1
) that is introduced for controlling the convergence as a function of iteration number
i
.
For given
i_{2}\geq 2

\beta_{1}
and
\beta_{2}
such that

The convergence of the iteration algorithm is controlled by the error function

defined by

\hbox{{\Ppcarac err}}(i)=\|{\bm{b}}-E\{{\bm{h}}({\bm{H}}_{{\bm{\lambda}}^{i}})\}\|/\|{\bm{b}}\|\,.

(A.13)

At each iteration

Appendix B
Estimation of the Kullback-Leibler divergence, the mutual information, and the entropy from a set of realizations

The definition Kullback-Leibler divergence, the mutual information, and the entropy can be found in

[
93
,
94
,
96
]
. The estimation of these quantities from a set of independent realizations is carried out using the Gaussian kernel density estimation (GKDE) method
[
63
,
33
,
64
]
.

For
\nu>1
, let
{\bm{X}}=(X_{1},\ldots,X_{\nu})
and
{\bm{Y}}=(Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{\nu})
be
{\mathbb{R}}^{\nu}
-valued random variables defined on the probability space
(\Theta,{\mathcal{T}},{\mathcal{P}})
, whose probability measures are
P_{\bm{X}}(d{\bm{x}})=p_{\bm{X}}({\bm{x}})\,d{\bm{x}}
and
P_{\bm{Y}}(d{\bm{y}})=p_{\bm{Y}}({\bm{y}})\,d{\bm{y}}
,
in which the probability density functions
p_{\bm{X}}
and
p_{\bm{Y}}
are assumed to be strictly positive. Let
\{{\bm{x}}^{\ell},\ell=1,\ldots,N_{x}\}
be
N_{x}
independent realizations of
{\bm{X}}
and let
\{{\bm{y}}^{j},j=1,\ldots,N_{y}\}
be
N_{y}
independent realizations of
{\bm{Y}}
.
For
k=1,\ldots,\nu
, let
\sigma_{X_{k}}
and
\sigma_{Y_{k}}
be the standard deviation of
X_{k}
and
Y_{k}
that are estimated (empirical estimator) with the independent realizations.
Finally, we introduce the Silverman bandwidth for the Gaussian KDE estimation of
p_{\bm{X}}
and
p_{\bm{Y}}
,

s_{x}=\left\{\frac{4}{N_{x}(2+\nu)}\right\}^{1/(\nu+4)}\quad,\quad s_{y}=\left\{\frac{4}{N_{y}(2+\nu)}\right\}^{1/(\nu+4)}\,.

(B.1)

B.1
Estimation of the Kullback-Leibler divergence from a set of realizations

The Kullback-Leibler divergence (or the relative entropy) between
p_{\bm{X}}
and
p_{\bm{Y}}
is defined by

D(p_{\bm{X}}\|p_{\bm{Y}})=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{\nu}}p_{\bm{X}}({\bm{x}})\,\log\left(\frac{p_{\bm{X}}({\bm{x}})}{p_{\bm{Y}}({\bm{x}})}\right)\,d{\bm{x}}=E\left\{\log\left(\frac{p_{\bm{X}}({\bm{X}})}{p_{\bm{Y}}({\bm{X}})}\right)\right\}\,.

(B.2)

The GKDE,
\hat{D}(p_{\bm{X}}\|p_{\bm{Y}})
, of
D(p_{\bm{X}}\|p_{\bm{Y}})
yields the formula,

\displaystyle\hat{D}(p_{\bm{X}}\,\|\,p_{\bm{Y}})=

\displaystyle\nu\log\left(\frac{s_{y}}{s_{x}}\right)+\log\left(\frac{N_{y}}{N_{x}}\right)+\log\left(\frac{\sigma_{Y_{1}}\times\ldots\times\sigma_{Y_{\nu}}}{\sigma_{X_{1}}\times\ldots\times\sigma_{X_{\nu}}}\right)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{N_{x}}\sum_{\ell^{\prime}=1}^{N_{x}}\log\left\{\frac{\sum_{\ell=1}^{N_{x}}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2s_{x}^{2}}\sum_{k=1}^{\nu}(\frac{x_{k}^{\ell^{\prime}}-x_{k}^{\ell}}{\sigma_{X_{k}}})^{2}\right)}{\sum_{j=1}^{N_{y}}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2s_{y}^{2}}\sum_{k=1}^{\nu}(\frac{x_{k}^{\ell^{\prime}}-y_{k}^{j}}{\sigma_{Y_{k}}})^{2}\right)}\right\}\,.

(B.3)

B.2
Estimation of the mutual information from a set of realizations

The mutual information
I({\bm{X}})
of
{\bm{X}}
allows to quantify the level of statistical dependencies of the components
X_{1},\ldots,X_{\nu}
of
{\bm{X}}=(X_{1},\ldots,X_{\nu})
. Let
p_{X_{k}}
be the pdf of real-valued random variable
X_{k}
,

p_{X_{k}}(x_{k})=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{\nu-1}}p_{\bm{X}}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k-1},x_{k},x_{k+1},\ldots,x_{\nu})\,dx_{1}\ldots dx_{k-1}\,dx_{k+1}\ldots\,dx_{\nu}\,.

(B.4)

The mutual information
I({\bm{X}})
is defined by

I({\bm{X}})=D(p_{\bm{X}}\,\|\,\otimes_{k=1}^{\nu}p_{X_{k}})=E\left\{\log\left(\frac{p_{\bm{X}}({\bm{X}})}{p_{X_{1}}(X_{1})\times\ldots\times p_{X_{\nu}}(X_{\nu})}\right)\right\}\,.

(B.5)

Eq. (
B.5
) shows that, if the components
X_{1},\ldots,X_{\nu}
are statistically independent, then
I({\bm{X}})=0
.
The GKDE,
\hat{I}({\bm{X}})
of
I({\bm{X}})
yields the formula,

\hat{I}({\bm{X}})=\frac{1}{N_{x}}\sum_{\ell^{\prime}=1}^{N_{x}}\log\left\{\frac{\frac{1}{N_{x}}\sum_{\ell=1}^{N_{x}}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2s_{x}^{2}}\sum_{k=1}^{\nu}(\frac{x_{k}^{\ell^{\prime}}-x_{k}^{\ell}}{\sigma_{X_{k}}})^{2}\right)}{\prod_{k=1}^{\nu}\left(\frac{1}{N_{x}}\sum_{\ell^{\prime\prime}=1}^{N_{x}}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2s_{x}^{2}}(\frac{x_{k}^{\ell^{\prime}}-x_{k}^{\ell^{\prime\prime}}}{\sigma_{X_{k}}})^{2}\right)\right)}\right\}\,.

(B.6)

B.3
Estimation of the entropy from a set of realizations

The entropy related to
p_{\bm{X}}
is defined by

S_{\bm{X}}=-\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{\nu}}p_{\bm{X}}({\bm{x}})\,\log p_{\bm{X}}({\bm{x}})\,d{\bm{x}}=-E\left\{\log p_{\bm{X}}({\bm{X}})\right\}\,.

(B.7)

The GKDE,
\hat{S}_{\bm{X}}
, of
S_{\bm{X}}
yields the formula,

\displaystyle\hat{S}_{\bm{X}}=

\displaystyle\nu\log(s_{x}\!\!\sqrt{2\pi}\,)+\log(\sigma_{X_{1}}\times\ldots\times\sigma_{X_{\nu}})

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{N_{x}}\sum_{\ell^{\prime}=1}^{N_{x}}\log\left\{\frac{1}{N_{x}}\sum_{\ell=1}^{N_{x}}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2s_{x}^{2}}\sum_{k=1}^{\nu}(\frac{x_{k}^{\ell^{\prime}}-x_{k}^{\ell}}{\sigma_{X_{k}}})^{2}\right)\right\}\,.

(B.8)

Since
J_{S}(N_{x})=\nu\log(s_{x}\!\!\sqrt{2\pi}\,)
is asymptotically for

, the entropy decreases when
