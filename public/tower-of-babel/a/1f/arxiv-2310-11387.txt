Transitive generalized toggle groups containing a cycle
Jonathan S. Bloom

Abstract
In [ 9 ] Striker generalized Cameron and Fon-Der-Flaass’s notion of a toggle group. In this paper we begin the study of transitive generalized toggle groups that contain a cycle. We first show that if such a group has degree n n and contains a transposition or a 3-cycle then the group contains A n A_{n} . Using the result about transpositions, we then prove that a transitive generalized toggle group that contains a short cycle must be primitive. Employing a result of Jones [ 8 ] , which relies on the classification of the finite simple groups, we conclude that any transitive generalized toggle group of degree n n that contains a cycle with at least 3 fixed points must also contain A n A_{n} . Finally, we look at imprimitive generalized toggle groups containing a long cycle and show that they decompose into a direct product of primitive generalized toggle groups each containing a long cycle.



1
Introduction

The study of toggle groups dates back to the 1995 work
[
1
]
of Cameron and Fon-Der-Flaass on order ideals of posets. To provide a simpler proof of a result from
[
4
]
, Cameron and Fon-Der-Flaass associated to each element
p
of a finite poset
P
a permutation
s_{p}
of the set
J(P)
of order ideals of
P
. For each
I\in J(P)
,
s_{p}(I)
was defined to be
I\triangle\left\{p\right\}
if
I\triangle\left\{p\right\}\in J(P)
, and
s_{p}(I)=I
otherwise. After using the
s_{p}
’s to achieve their proof, they went on to study the subgroup
G(P)=\langle s_{p}\ |\ p\in P\rangle
of the symmetric group
S_{J(P)}
. They proved
[
1
, Theorem 4]
that if the Hasse diagram of
P
is connected then
G(P)
contains the alternating group on
J(P)
, while if
P
is the disjoint union of posets
P_{1}
and
P_{2}
then
G(P)\cong G(P_{1})\times G(P_{2})
.

In
[
10
]
Striker and Williams used the
s_{p}
’s in studying what they called “promotion” and “rowmotion” on order ideals. They called the
s_{p}
’s
toggles
and
G(P)
the
toggle group
. Several other papers soon followed
[
3
,
5
,
6
]
that used analogues of the
s_{p}
’s in different contexts. In
[
9
]
Striker noted that the definition of the
s_{p}
’s only relied on the order ideals of
P
being subsets of
P
, and generalized the definition of the toggle group.

Definition 1.1
.

Let
E
be a finite set, which we refer to as the
ground set
. Let
{\cal L}
be a set of subsets of
E
. For each
e\in E
, define the
toggle

\tau_{e}:{\cal L}\rightarrow{\cal L}
by letting
\tau_{e}(X)=X\triangle\left\{e\right\}
if
X\triangle\left\{e\right\}\in{\cal L}
and
\tau_{e}(X)=X
otherwise. The
generalized toggle group

T({\cal L})
is the subgroup of the symmetric group
S_{{\cal L}}
generated by
\left\{\tau_{e}\,|\,e\in E\right\}
.

Striker initiated the study of
T({\cal L})
for a number of combinatorially interesting choices of
{\cal L}
. In particular, she obtained analogs of
[
1
, Theorem 4]
, for several examples where
{\cal L}
is a set of subsets of a finite poset or graph.

In what follows, we head in a somewhat different direction. There is a long history of results about primitive permutation groups that contain a nontrivial cycle, dating back to the work of Jordan in the 1870’s. We will prove some structure results for all transitive generalized toggle groups that contain a nontrivial cycle. In Section 3 we will show that if
T({\cal L})
is transitive with degree
n
and contains a transposition then
T({\cal L})
must be the full symmetric group
S_{n}
, and if
T({\cal L})
is transitive with degree
n
and contains a 3-cycle then
T({\cal L})
must contain the alternating group
A_{n}
. These results are analogues of standard results
[
2
, Theorem 3.3A]
for primitive permutation groups, but we will obtain them for transitive generalized toggle groups without assuming primitivity. Using the result for transpositions, we will then show that if
T({\cal L})
is transitive with degree
n
and contains a “short cycle” (i.e., a cycle of length at most
n-1
), then
T({\cal L})
is primitive. Using
[
8
, Corollary 1.3]
(a result that relies on the classification of the finite simple groups) it will then follow that if
T({\cal L})
contains a cycle with at least three fixed points then
T({\cal L})
contains
A_{n}
.

In Section 4 we will show that if
T({\cal L})
is imprimitive and contains a nontrivial cycle (necessarily a “long cycle” of length
n
) then
T({\cal L})
is isomorphic to a direct product of primitive groups
T({\cal L}_{1})\times\cdots\times T({\cal L}_{k})
where each
T({\cal L}_{i})
has degree at least two and contains a long cycle whose length is the degree of
T({\cal L}_{i})
.

In obtaining the results of Section 4 we will use what Striker
[
9
]
calls the “toggle-disjoint Cartesian product” of sets
{\cal L}_{1}
and
{\cal L}_{2}
.

Definition 1.2
.

If
E_{1}
and
E_{2}
are disjoint ground sets and
{\cal L}_{i}\subseteq 2^{E_{i}}
for
i=1,2
then we say
{\cal L}
is the toggle-disjoint Cartesian product of
{\cal L}_{1}
and
{\cal L}_{2}
and write
{\cal L}={\cal L}_{1}\otimes{\cal L}_{2}
if
{\cal L}=\left\{X_{1}\cup X_{2}\,|\,X_{i}\in{\cal L}_{i}\right\}
.

Striker makes this definition under an assumption weaker than
E_{1}\cap E_{2}=\emptyset
, but we will only use it when
E_{1}\cap E_{2}=\emptyset
. It is straightforward to check
[
9
, Theorem 2.18]
that if
{\cal L}
is the toggle-disjoint Cartesian product of
{\cal L}_{1}
and
{\cal L}_{2}
then
T({\cal L})\cong T({\cal L}_{1})\times T({\cal L}_{2})
.

Conventions
.

It may happen that for some elements
e\in E
,
\tau_{e}
is the identity element of the symmetric group on
{\cal L}
. Since we will always be assuming that
T({\cal L})
is transitive in what follows, this can only happen if
e
is in all or none of the sets in
{\cal L}
. We will always remove such
e
’s from
E
. Doing this has no effect on
T({\cal L})
.

To simplify terminology we shall refer to generalized toggle groups simply as toggle groups.

2
Block systems in toggle groups

Recall that if a group
G
acts on a set
X
then we say a subset
{\cal B}\subseteq X
is a
block
for
G
if, for all
g\in G
, either
g({\cal B})={\cal B}
or
g({\cal B})
is disjoint from
{\cal B}
. If
G
acts transitively on
X
and
{\cal B}
is a block for
G
then the sets
\left\{g({\cal B})\,|\,g\in G\right\}
form a partition of
X
. We call such a partition a
block system
for
G
. Clearly, the blocks in a given block system must all have the same size. If there exists a block system for which this size is strictly between 1 and
|X|
then the group action is said to be
imprimitive
, and otherwise the action is said to be
primitive
. A good source for background information on these ideas is
[
2
, Chapter 1]
.

The purpose of this section is to explore how toggles behave on a block system. In particular, the lemmas established in this section describe a rich structure in the context of imprimitive toggle groups. We then exploit this structure in the following sections.

Recall that, as in the introduction,
E
is our finite ground set,
{\cal L}
is a collection of subsets of
E
and
T({\cal L})
is the corresponding toggle group. We assume throughout that
T({\cal L})

acts transitively on
{\cal L}
, and we fix a system of blocks for
T({\cal L})
. We often denote individual blocks by
{\cal B}
or
{\cal C}
.

Definition 2.1
.

We say that
g\in T({\cal L})
is
type 2
if
g({\cal B})={\cal B}
for all blocks
{\cal B}
, and that
g
is
type 1
otherwise.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
\tau_{y}
be a type 1 toggle in
T({\cal L})
.

i)

If
\tau_{y}({\cal B})={\cal B}
for some block
{\cal B}
then
\tau_{y}
is the identity on this block.

ii)

\tau_{y}
commutes with all type 2 toggles in
T({\cal L})
.

iii)

For any type 2 toggle
\tau
and blocks
{\cal B},{\cal C}
, the cycle structure of
\tau|_{{\cal B}}
is the same as the cycle structure of
\tau|_{{\cal C}}
.

Proof.

We prove i) by contradiction. Suppose that for some
A\in{\cal B}
we have
A,A\triangle\left\{y\right\}\in{\cal B}
. By definition of
\tau_{y}
there exists another block
{\cal C}\neq{\cal B}
. By transitivity there exist toggles
\tau_{z_{1}},\ldots,\tau_{z_{k}}
such that
\tau_{z_{k}}\cdots\tau_{z_{1}}({\cal B})={\cal C}
. Let
{\cal B}_{0}={\cal B}
and
{\cal B}_{i}=\tau_{z_{i}}({\cal B}_{i-1})
. By omitting some
\tau_{i}
’s we can assume that
{\cal B}_{i}\neq{\cal B}_{i-1}
. Then
A\triangle\left\{z_{1}\right\},A\triangle\left\{y,z_{1}\right\}\in{\cal B}_{1}
and hence
\tau_{y}(A\triangle\left\{z_{1}\right\})=A\triangle\left\{z_{1},y\right\}\in{\cal B}_{1}
. So
\tau_{y}({\cal B}_{1})={\cal B}_{1}
. By iterating this argument we can show that
\tau_{y}({\cal C})={\cal C}
. Since
{\cal C}
was an arbitrary block distinct from
{\cal B}
, it follows that
\tau_{y}
is type 2, a contradiction.

To prove ii) let
\tau_{x}
be a type 2 toggle and fix a block
{\cal B}
. If
\tau_{y}({\cal B})={\cal B}
then by i) it is clear that
\tau_{x}
and
\tau_{y}
commute at all elements of
{\cal B}
. So we may assume that
\tau_{y}({\cal B})={\cal C}
where
{\cal B}\neq{\cal C}
. Fix
A\in{\cal B}
so that
A\triangle\left\{y\right\}=\tau_{y}(A)\in{\cal C}
. If
\tau_{x}(A)=A
then
A\triangle\left\{x\right\}\not\in{\cal B}
(since
A\triangle\left\{x\right\}\not\in{\cal L}
) and hence
A\triangle\left\{x,y\right\}\notin{\cal C}
. So

\tau_{y}\tau_{x}(A)=\tau_{y}(A)=A\triangle\left\{y\right\}=\tau_{x}(A\triangle\left\{y\right\})=\tau_{x}\tau_{y}(A),

where the third equality follows from the fact that
\tau_{x}
is type 2 so that
\tau_{x}(A\triangle\left\{y\right\})
must be in
{\cal C}
. So if
\tau_{x}(A)=A
then
\tau_{x}
and
\tau_{y}
commute at
A
. If
\tau_{x}(A)=A\triangle\left\{x\right\}
then
A\triangle\left\{x\right\}\in{\cal B}
and therefore
\tau_{y}\tau_{x}(A)=A\triangle\left\{x,y\right\}=\tau_{x}\tau_{y}(A)
.

To prove iii) consider a type 2 toggle
\tau
and blocks
{\cal B},{\cal C}
. As in the proof of i) it suffices by transitivity to prove the claim in the case where
\tau_{y}({\cal B})={\cal C}
. If we write
\tau
as the product of its block restrictions we have

\tau=\cdots\tau|_{\cal B}\cdots\tau|_{\cal C}\cdots.

Conjugating by
\tau_{y}
gives

\tau_{y}\tau\tau_{y}=\cdots(\tau_{y}\tau|_{\cal B}\tau_{y})\cdots(\tau_{y}\tau|_{\cal C}\tau_{y})\cdots

and we note that

\tau_{y}\tau|_{{\cal C}}\tau_{y}
is a permutation of
{\cal B}
. Consequently

\tau_{y}\tau|_{{\cal C}}\tau_{y}=(\tau_{y}\tau\tau_{y})|_{{\cal B}}=\tau|_{\cal B},

where the last equality follows by part ii) and the fact that
\tau_{y}
is type 1. Since conjugation preserves cycle structure the result is now clear.
∎

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
\rho\in T({\cal L})
be a product of type 2 toggles. If
\rho
fixes all elements, pointwise, in some block
{\cal B}
then
\rho=1
.

Proof.

If
{\cal B}
is the only block we are done. Otherwise, transitivity implies that there must be some type 1 toggle
\tau
such that
\tau({\cal B})={\cal C}
with
{\cal B}\neq{\cal C}
. It follows by Lemma
2.2
that
\tau
and
\rho
commute. Fix
C\in{\cal C}
and let
B=\tau(C)\in{\cal B}
. Since
\tau
is an involution we have

\rho(C)=\rho\tau(B)=\tau\rho(B)=\tau(B)=C.

Hence
\rho
fixes all elements in
{\cal C}
. As in the proof of the first part of the preceding lemma, iterating this argument implies that
\rho=1
.
∎

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
\sigma\in T({\cal L})
be a product of type 1 toggles. For each block
{\cal B}
there exists some set
X\subseteq E
such that for each
A\in{\cal B}
we have

\sigma(A)=A\triangle X.

In particular, if
\sigma({\cal B})={\cal B}
then
\sigma|_{{\cal B}}
is either the identity or an involution with no fixed points.

Proof.

Let
\sigma=\tau_{x_{k}}\cdots\tau_{x_{1}}
where each
\tau_{x_{i}}
is a type 1 toggle. Set
{\cal B}_{0}={\cal B}
and let
{\cal B}_{i}=\tau_{x_{i}}({\cal B}_{i-1})
. By the first part of Lemma
2.2
we may assume
{\cal B}_{i}\neq{\cal B}_{i-1}
. It then follows that

{\cal B}_{i}=\left\{A\triangle\left\{x_{i}\right\}\,|\,A\in{\cal B}_{i-1}\right\}.

Defining
X
to be the set of all the
x_{i}
that appear an odd number of times in
x_{1},\ldots,x_{k}
it further follows that
\sigma(A)=A\triangle X
for every
A\in{\cal B}
. This proves our first claim. The second
claim follows since
\sigma
is now seen to act by symmetric difference with a fixed set
X
, on all of
{\cal B}
.
∎

Lemma 2.5
.

Let
X
and
Y
be disjoint subsets of
E
and define
\sigma=\tau_{x_{1}}\cdots\tau_{x_{m}}
and
\rho=\tau_{y_{1}}\cdots\tau_{y_{\ell}}
where
x_{i}\in X,\ y_{j}\in Y
. For all
A\in{\cal L}
we have
\sigma(A)=\rho(A)
if and only if
\sigma(A)=A=\rho(A)
.

Proof.

The reverse direction is clear. Now assume
\sigma(A)=\rho(A)
. For some
S\subseteq X
and
T\subseteq Y
we have

A\triangle S=\sigma(A)=\rho(A)=A\triangle T.

(Note that the subsets
S
and
T
may be dependent on
A
.) As
X
and
Y
are disjoint this can only occur if
S=T=\emptyset
, and the forward direction follows.
∎

Lemma 2.6
.

Let
E_{1}=\left\{x\in E\,|\,\tau_{x}\text{ is type 1}\right\}
,
E_{2}=\left\{y\in E\,|\,\tau_{y}\text{ is type 2}\right\}
and set

{\cal L}_{i}=\left\{A\cap E_{i}\,|\,A\in{\cal L}\right\}
.

Suppose
T({\cal L})
is such that any product
\sigma
of type 1 toggles has the property that if
\sigma({\cal B})={\cal B}
, for some block
{\cal B}
, then
\sigma(A)=A
for all
A\in{\cal B}
. Then
{\cal L}={\cal L}_{1}\otimes{\cal L}_{2}
where
|{\cal L}_{1}|
is the number of blocks and
|{\cal L}_{2}|
is the size of each block.

Proof.

Fix
A\in{\cal L}
and define
{\cal O}(A)
to be the orbit containing
A
under the group generated by all type 1 toggles. As
T({\cal L})
is transitive it follows that for any two blocks there is a product of type 1 toggles that maps one to the other. As a result
{\cal O}(A)
contains at least one set from each block.
On the other hand if
B,B^{\prime}\in{\cal O}(A)
are in the same block it follows by our assumption about type 1 toggles that
B=B^{\prime}
. So
{\cal O}(A)
contains exactly one set from each block. Consequently for a fixed block
{\cal B}
we have the disjoint union

{\cal L}=\bigcup_{A\in{\cal B}}{\cal O}(A).

We call the sets
{\cal O}(A)

layers
.

If
A\neq B
are elements of the same block
{\cal B}
then by transitivity, the second part of Lemma
2.2
and the fact that
{\cal O}(A)
contains exactly one set from each block, it follows that for some product
\rho
of type 2 toggles we have
\rho(A)=B
. From this we see that

A\cap E_{1}=B\cap E_{1}\text{ and }A\cap E_{2}\neq B\cap E_{2}.

On the other hand, if
A\neq C
are elements in the same layer then

A\cap E_{1}\neq C\cap E_{1}\text{ and }A\cap E_{2}=C\cap E_{2}.

So all the sets in
{\cal O}(A)
have different intersections with
E_{1}
and every set
D\in{\cal L}
has the same intersection with
E_{1}
as some set in
{\cal O}(A)
since
D
is in the same block as some set in
{\cal O}(A)
. Thus
|{\cal L}_{1}|=|{\cal O}(A)|
is the number of blocks
{\cal B}_{i}
, since
{\cal O}(A)
contains exactly one set from each block. Since all sets in
{\cal O}(A)
have the same intersection with
E_{2}
we have

{\cal O}(A)={\cal L}_{1}\otimes\{A\cap E_{2}\}.

So we have

{\cal L}=\bigcup_{A\in{\cal B}}{\cal O}(A)=\bigcup_{A\in{\cal B}}{\cal L}_{1}\otimes\{A\cap E_{2}\}={\cal L}_{1}\otimes{\cal L}_{2},

since every set in
{\cal L}
is in the same layer as some set in
{\cal B}
, hence has the same intersection with
E_{2}
.
The last equality further shows that
|{\cal L}_{2}|
is the size of each block as claimed.
∎

Lemma 2.7
.

Let
T({\cal L})
be imprimitive with a system of blocks of imprimitivity
{\cal B}_{i}
. If the block size is odd, then
{\cal L}
is a toggle-disjoint Cartesian product of sets each of which has at least two elements.

Proof.

To apply Lemma
2.6
, suppose
\sigma
is a product of type 1 toggles such that
\sigma({\cal B}_{i})={\cal B}_{i}
for some
i
. By Lemma
2.4
it follows that
\sigma|_{{\cal B}_{i}}
is either an involution with no fixed points or the identity. Since the blocks
{\cal B}_{i}
have odd size we see that
\sigma|_{{\cal B}_{i}}
is the identity. Lemma
2.6
completes the proof.
∎

Lemma 2.8
.

Let
T({\cal L})
be imprimitive with a system of blocks of imprimitivity
{\cal B}_{i}
. Assume there exists some
\sigma
, a product of type 1 toggles, such that
\sigma({\cal B}_{i})={\cal B}_{i}
and
\sigma|_{{\cal B}_{i}}\neq 1
for some
{\cal B}_{i}
. Then
\tau|_{{\cal B}_{j}}
is an even permutation for all type 2 toggles
\tau
and all blocks
{\cal B}_{j}
.

Consequently, if for some
{\cal B}_{i}
there exists a type 2 toggle
\tau
such that
\tau|_{{\cal B}_{i}}
is odd, then
{\cal L}
is a toggle-disjoint Cartesian product of two sets each of which has at least two elements.

Proof.

Let
{\cal B}_{i}
and
\sigma
be as stated. As
\sigma|_{{\cal B}_{i}}\neq 1
, Lemma
2.4
implies that
\sigma|_{{\cal B}_{i}}
is an involution with no fixed points. Define a partition on
{\cal B}_{i}
so that elements
A
and
B
are in the same class if and only if
\sigma(A)=B
. Denote the classes in this partition by
{\cal C}_{j}
and observe that
|{\cal C}_{j}|=2
. As
\sigma
is a product of type 1 toggles, it follows from Lemma
2.2
that any type 2 toggle commutes with
\sigma
. Consequently, the
{\cal C}_{j}
form a block system for the restriction to
{\cal B}_{i}
of the group
G
generated by all the type 2 toggles. By Lemma
2.5
we see that any type 2 toggle
\tau
restricted to
{\cal B}_{i}
can either have
\tau({\cal C}_{j})={\cal C}_{k}
with
j\neq k
or
\tau|_{{\cal C}_{j}}=1
. As each
C_{j}
has size 2 this implies that the restriction to
{\cal B}_{i}
of any type 2 toggle is an even permutation. By the third part of Lemma
2.2
, this proves our first claim.

The proof of our second claim is now immediate by Lemma
2.6
.
∎

3
Transitive toggle groups containing short cycles

Throughout this section we continue to assume that
T({\cal L})
is transitive. The
degree
of
T({\cal L})
is the number of elements in the set
{\cal L}
.

The first two theorems of this section state that if
T({\cal L})
has degree
n
and contains a transposition then
T({\cal L})
is
S_{n}
, and if
T({\cal L})
has degree
n
and contains a 3-cycle then
T({\cal L})
contains
A_{n}
. These two theorems echo well-known results of Jordan for primitive permutation groups (see
[
2
, Theorem 3.3A]
or
[
7
, Theorem 8.17, Corollary 8.19]
). Interestingly, in the toggle-group case we do not need to assume the groups are primitive but instead we obtain that as a consequence.

Theorem 3.1
.

Assume that
T({\cal L})
has degree
n
and contains a transposition. Then
T({\cal L})\cong S_{n}
.

Proof.

We define an equivalence relation
\sim
on
{\cal L}
by letting
A\sim B
if and only if either
A=B
or else
(A,B)\in T({\cal L})
. Since conjugating
(A,B)
by
(B,C)
yields
(A,C)
, it is easy to check that
\sim
is an equivalence relation. Likewise, conjugating
(A,B)
by any
g\in T({\cal L})
yields
(g(A),g(B))
, so the equivalence classes of
\sim
constitute a system of blocks for
T({\cal L})
. For the remainder of the proof we fix this system of blocks and denote the blocks by
{\cal B}_{1},\ldots,{\cal B}_{m}
.

We prove the theorem by showing that
m=1
(which implies that
T({\cal L})
contains all transpositions). By assumption we have
(A,B)\in T({\cal L})
for some
A,B\in{\cal L}
, and we can suppose without loss of generality that
A,B\in{\cal B}_{1}
. By the second part of Lemma
2.2
we can express

(A,B)=\underbrace{\tau_{y_{1}}\cdots\tau_{y_{k}}}_{\sigma}\underbrace{\tau_{x_{1}}\cdots\tau_{x_{\ell}}}_{\rho}

where the
\tau_{y_{j}}
are type 1 and the
\tau_{x_{i}}
are type 2. By squaring both sides we obtain

1=\sigma^{2}\rho^{2}.

As
\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{m}\}\cap\{y_{1},\ldots,y_{\ell}\}=\emptyset
, Lemma
2.5
implies that
\sigma^{2}=\rho^{2}=1
.

Assume for a contradiction that
m>1
, and let
C\in{\cal B}_{2}
. As
(A,B)=\sigma\rho
we must have
\sigma\rho(C)=C
. As
\sigma^{2}=1
it follows that
\rho(C)=\sigma(C)
. By Lemma
2.5
then
\rho(C)=C=\sigma(C)
for all
C\in{\cal B}_{2}
. So
\rho
, a product of type 2 toggles, fixes
{\cal B}_{2}
pointwise. Hence by Lemma
2.3
,
\rho=1
and
\sigma=(A,B)
. Now if each block has even size then it follows from the first part of Lemma
2.2
that each
\tau_{y_{i}}
is an even permutation, contradicting
\sigma=(A,B)
. So each block has odd size. As
\sigma({\cal B}_{1})={\cal B}_{1}
it follows by the second claim in Lemma
2.4
that
\sigma|_{{\cal B}_{1}}=1
, contradicting the fact that
\sigma=(A,B)
. We conclude that
m=1
as needed.
∎

Theorem 3.2
.

Assume
T({\cal L})
has degree
n
and contains a 3-cycle. Then
A_{n}\leq T({\cal L})
.

Proof.

We define an equivalence relation
\sim
on
{\cal L}
by letting
A\sim B
if and only if either
A=B
or else
(A,B,C)\in T({\cal L})
for some
C
. Using the fact that
(A,B,C)^{2}=(B,A,C)
, it is easy to check, as in the preceding proof, that
\sim
is an equivalence relation, and that the equivalence classes of
\sim
constitute a system of blocks for
T({\cal L})
. We again denote the blocks by
{\cal B}_{1},\ldots,{\cal B}_{m}
.

To prove the theorem it suffices to show that
T({\cal L})
must contain all 3-cycles or, equivalently, that
m=1
. To see that this suffices, suppose
m=1
and let
A,B,C
be distinct elements of
{\cal L}
. We claim that
(A,B,C)\in T({\cal L})
. Since
m=1
we have
A\sim B
, so
(A,B,D)\in T({\cal L})
for some
D\neq A,B
. If
D=C
we are done. Otherwise,
D\neq C
and since
m=1
we must have a 3-cycle
(D,C,E)\in T({\cal L})
. If
E\neq A,B
we are done by conjugating
(A,B,D)
by
(D,C,E)
. If
E=A
then we have
(A,B,C)=(D,C,A)(A,B,D)\in T({\cal L})
. If
E=B
we have
(A,B,C)=(A,B,D)(D,C,B)^{2}\in T({\cal L})
.

We now show that
m=1
. By assumption we have a 3-cycle
(A,B,C)
in
T({\cal L})
, and without loss of generality we can suppose that
A,B\in{\cal B}_{1}
and therefore
C\in{\cal B}_{1}
since
(A,B,C)
maps
A
and
B
into the same block. By Lemma
2.2
we can write

(A,B,C)=\underbrace{\tau_{y_{1}}\cdots\tau_{y_{k}}}_{\sigma}\underbrace{\tau_{x_{1}}\cdots\tau_{x_{\ell}}}_{\rho}

where the
\tau_{y_{j}}
are type 1 and the
\tau_{x_{i}}
are type 2.

For a contradiction assume
m>1
and let
D\in{\cal B}_{i}
where
i\neq 1
. As
(A,B,C)=\sigma\rho
we have
\sigma\rho(D)=D
and hence
\rho(D)=\sigma^{-1}(D)
. By Lemma
2.5
we conclude that
\rho(D)=D
. As
D
is an arbitrary element of
{\cal B}_{i}
then
\rho
is the identity on
{\cal B}_{i}
. By Lemma
2.3
it follows that
\rho=1
. So
\sigma=(A,B,C)
. But then Lemma
2.4
forces
\sigma^{2}=1
, a contradiction. We conclude that
m=1
as needed.
∎

In addition to the results that motivated Theorem
3.1
and Theorem
3.2
, Jordan also proved that if a primitive permutation group
G
of degree
n
contains a cycle of prime length
p\leq n-3
then
A_{n}\leq G
(see
[
2
, Theorem 3.3E]
or
[
7
, Theorem 8.23]
).
Much more recently, Jones, in
[
8
, Corollary 1.3]
, removed the assumption that the length of the cycle be prime in Jordan’s result. To obtain an analogue of Jones’ result for toggle groups (as opposed to primitive permutation groups), we will combine Jones’ result with the following.

Theorem 3.3
.

Suppose
T({\cal L})
has degree
n
and contains a nontrivial cycle
\gamma
of length
\leq n-1
. Then
T({\cal L})
is primitive.

Proof.

Suppose not, and take a system of blocks of imprimitivity for
T({\cal L})
. Note that if two points of
{\cal L}
are in the same block then so are their images under
\gamma
, so if one point moved by
\gamma
is in the same block as a fixed point of
\gamma
then all points moved by
\gamma
are in that block.

We first consider the case when all points moved by
\gamma
are in a block
{\cal B}
that contains a fixed point of
\gamma
. We know there are blocks other than
{\cal B}
, and each of these other blocks consists entirely of fixed points of
\gamma
. Let
{\cal C}
be any one of these other blocks. If we write
\gamma=\sigma\rho
with
\sigma
a product of type 1 toggles and
\rho
a product of type 2 toggles, then we can argue as we have before that
\rho
must fix every point of
{\cal C}
, and therefore
\rho=1
by Lemma
2.3
. Therefore
\gamma=\sigma
, and
\gamma
maps
{\cal B}
to itself. By Lemma
2.4
,
\gamma^{2}=1
, so
\gamma
is a transposition. By Theorem
3.1
,
T({\cal L})
is
S_{n}
, so since
T({\cal L})
has degree
n
,
T({\cal L})
is primitive.

We now consider the case when no points moved by
\gamma
are in the same block as any fixed point of
\gamma
. We first show that in this case each block has even size. If not, then there is a block
{\cal B}
of odd size
|{\cal B}|>1
consisting of points moved by
\gamma
, and there is a block
{\cal C}
consisting of fixed points of
\gamma
(since
\gamma
has length
\leq n-1
). As in the first case, we write
\gamma=\sigma\rho
and use the existence of the block
{\cal C}
to show that
\rho=1
and
\gamma=\sigma
. If we choose
A\neq B
in
{\cal B}
then there exists a positive integer
m
such that
\gamma^{m}(A)=B
, and therefore
\gamma^{m}
maps
{\cal B}
to
{\cal B}
. Since
\gamma^{m}=\sigma^{m}
, a product of type 1 toggles, Lemma
2.4
implies that
\gamma^{m}|_{{\cal B}_{i}}
is either an involution with no fixed points or the identity. Since the blocks have odd size we see that
\sigma|_{{\cal B}_{i}}
is the identity. But this is impossible, since
\gamma^{m}(A)=B
where
A\neq B
.

So we know the blocks have even size. Since the elements moved by
\gamma
comprise a set of blocks,
\gamma
has even length, hence is an odd permutation. But the fact that the blocks have even size also implies, by the first part of Lemma
2.2
, that every type 1 toggle is an even permutation. Since
\gamma=\sigma
is a product of type 1 toggles,
\gamma
is an even permutation. This contradiction concludes the proof.

∎

Corollary 3.4
.

If
T({\cal L})
has degree
n
and contains a nontrivial cycle of length
\leq n-3
then
A_{n}\leq T({\cal L})
.

Proof.

By Theorem
3.3
,
T({\cal L})
is primitive, so the corollary follows from
[
8
, Corollary 1.3]
, which states, among other things, that if
G
is a primitive permutation group of degree
n
that contains a nontrivial cycle with at least three fixed points, then
A_{n}\leq G
.

∎

Corollary
3.4
provides an alternate proof of Theorem
3.2
if we check separately the cases
n\leq 5
. But Corollary
3.4
relies on
[
8
, Corollary 1.3]
, which depends on the classification of the finite simple groups.

4
Imprimitive toggle groups containing long cycles

We have shown that a toggle group that contains a short cycle must be primitive. Therefore, if an imprimitive toggle group contains a cycle that cycle can only be a long cycle. In this section we study imprimitive toggle groups that contain a long cycle.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
E_{1},E_{2}
be disjoint ground sets and let
{\cal L}_{i}\subseteq 2^{E_{i}}
be such that
|{\cal L}_{i}|\geq 2
for
i=1,2
. Set
\ell=|{\cal L}_{1}|
and
m=|{\cal L}_{2}|
and
{\cal L}={\cal L}_{1}\otimes{\cal L}_{2}
. Then
T({\cal L})
contains a long cycle if and only if
(\ell,m)=1
and both
T({\cal L}_{1})
and
T({\cal L}_{2})
contain long cycles.

Proof.

Let
{\cal L}_{1}=\{A_{1},\ldots,A_{\ell}\}
and
{\cal L}_{2}=\{B_{1},\ldots,B_{m}\}
. Consider the block system given by the blocks

{\cal C}_{i}=\left\{A_{i}\cup B_{j}\,|\,B_{j}\in{\cal L}_{2}\right\},

so that the type 1 toggles are precisely
\tau_{x}
for
x\in E_{1}
and the type 2 toggles are precisely
\tau_{y}
for
y\in E_{2}
. (Recall that, by the convention we adopted at the outset, no
\tau_{x}
or
\tau_{y}
is the identity.) Note that
|{\cal C}_{i}|\geq 2
for all
{\cal C}_{i}
.

Suppose
T({\cal L})
contains a long cycle
\gamma
. Fix a block
{\cal C}
. Since
|{\cal C}|\geq 2
and
\gamma
is a long cycle, there exists a smallest positive integer
a
such that
\gamma^{a}
maps a point of
{\cal C}
into
{\cal C}
. Choose
X\in{\cal C}
such that
\gamma^{a}(X)\in{\cal C}
. By our choice of
a
, it follows that
X,\gamma(X),\gamma^{2}(X),\ldots,\gamma^{(a-1)}(X)
must all be in distinct blocks and so
a\leq\ell
. Further observe that
\gamma^{ka}(X)\in{\cal C}
for all
k
. If we write
\ell m=ka+r
, with
0\leq r<a
, then
\gamma^{r}(\gamma^{ka}(X))=X\in{\cal C}
since
\gamma
is an
\ell m
-cycle. Since
0\leq r<a
we must have
r=0
by our choice of
a
. So
a|\ell m
. Again since
\gamma
is an
\ell m
-cycle, we see that
\gamma^{a}(X),\gamma^{2a}(X)\ldots,\gamma^{\ell m}(X)
are distinct and in
{\cal C}
. As
|{\cal C}|=m
have
\ell m/a\leq m
, so
\ell\leq a
and therefore
\ell=a
and we have

\gamma^{\ell}=\gamma_{1}\cdots\gamma_{\ell},

where
\gamma_{i}
is a long cycle on block
{\cal C}_{i}
.

Now, using Lemma
2.2
, write
\gamma=\sigma\rho
where
\sigma
is a product of type 1 toggles and
\rho
is a product of type 2 toggles. Since
\sigma
and
\rho
commute we have
\gamma^{\ell}=\sigma^{\ell}\rho^{\ell}
, and since
\gamma^{\ell}
and
\rho^{\ell}
are type 2 permutations so is
\sigma^{\ell}
. Since
\sigma^{\ell}
is a product of type 1 toggles and
{\cal L}
is a toggle-disjoint Cartesian product it follows that
\sigma^{\ell}=1
. So

\gamma_{1}\cdots\gamma_{\ell}=\rho^{\ell}.

Restricting our attention to a fixed block
{\cal C}_{i}
we see that
\rho^{\ell}|_{{\cal C}_{i}}=\gamma_{i}
. Since
\gamma_{i}
is a long cycle on this block it follows that
\rho|_{{\cal C}_{i}}
must be a long cycle on
{\cal C}_{i}
and that
(m,\ell)=1
. It is now immediate that
T({\cal L}_{2})
must contain a long cycle as well. By repeating this argument with the block system

{\cal C}_{i}^{\prime}=\left\{B_{i}\cup A_{j}\,|\,A_{j}\in{\cal L}_{1}\right\}

we conclude that
T({\cal L}_{1})
must also have a long cycle as claimed.

Let us now consider the converse. Suppose
(m,\ell)=1
and
T({\cal L}_{1})
and
T({\cal L}_{2})
have long cycles
\gamma
and
\delta
, respectively. Note that
\gamma
and
\delta
have orders
\ell
and
m
, respectively. Label the sets in
{\cal L}
by
(i,j)
for
1\leq i\leq\ell,\ 1\leq j\leq m
and observe that
T({\cal L})
contains permutations

\gamma^{\prime}(i,j):=(\gamma(i),j)

and

\delta^{\prime}(i,j):=(i,\delta(j))

with orders
\ell
and
m
, respectively. The element
\gamma^{\prime}\sigma^{\prime}
is a long cycle in
T({\cal L})
, because
\gamma^{\prime}\sigma^{\prime}(1,1),\ldots,(\gamma^{\prime}\sigma^{\prime})^{\ell m}(1,1)
gives us all the elements of
{\cal L}
.

∎

Theorem 4.2
.

Assume
T({\cal L})
is imprimitive and contains a long cycle. Then
{\cal L}={\cal L}_{1}\otimes{\cal L}_{2}
for some
{\cal L}_{1}
and
{\cal L}_{2}
such that
|{\cal L}_{1}|\geq 2,\ |{\cal L}_{2}|\geq 2,\ (|{\cal L}_{1}|,|{\cal L}_{2}|)=1
, and each of
T({\cal L}_{1})
and
T({\cal L}_{2})
contains a long cycle.

Proof.

As
T({\cal L})
is imprimitive, let
{\cal B}_{1},\ldots,{\cal B}_{\ell}
be a system of blocks of imprimitivity. Set
|{\cal B}_{i}|=m
. If
m
is odd, then the result follows from Lemma
2.7
and Lemma
4.1
.

Now assume
m
is even. If we replace the blocks
{\cal C}_{i}
in the proof of Lemma
4.1
by the
{\cal B}_{i}
’s and define
a
as we did before, we obtain

\gamma^{\ell}=\gamma_{1}\cdots\gamma_{\ell},

where
\gamma_{i}
is a long cycle on block
{\cal B}_{i}
. We write
\gamma=\sigma\rho
as before and have

\gamma_{1}\cdots\gamma_{\ell}=\gamma^{\ell}=\sigma^{\ell}\rho^{\ell}.

Since
\gamma^{\ell}
and
\rho^{\ell}
are type 2 permutations, so is
\sigma^{\ell}
.

We claim that
\sigma^{\ell}=1
. For a contradiction, assume otherwise. Lemma
2.8
then implies that every type 2 toggle is even. Since
m
is even we also have (using the first part of Lemma
2.2
) that every type 1 toggle is even. Therefore since
\gamma
is product of toggles it must be even, but
\gamma
is an
\ell m
-cycle which is odd because
m
is even. We conclude that
\sigma^{\ell}=1
as claimed.

Since
\sigma^{\ell}=1
we see that
\rho^{\ell}=\gamma^{\ell}
which is the long cycle
\gamma_{i}
on each block
{\cal B}_{i}
. So
\rho
must be a long cycle on each block
{\cal B}_{i}
. As each block has even size
\rho|_{{\cal B}_{i}}
must be odd. Since
\rho
is a product of type 2 toggles we must have, for each
{\cal B}_{i}
, some type 2 toggle
\tau
such that
\tau|_{{\cal B}_{i}}
is odd.
Lemma
2.8
and Lemma
4.1
now complete the proof.
∎

Corollary 4.3
.

Assume
T({\cal L})
is imprimitive and contains a long cycle. Then

T({\cal L})\cong T({\cal L}_{1})\times\cdots\times T({\cal L}_{k}),

where each
T({\cal L}_{i})
is primitive and contains a long cycle, their degrees are larger than 1 and pairwise coprime, and
|{\cal L}|=|{\cal L}_{1}|\cdots|{\cal L}_{k}|
.

Proof.

Apply Theorem
4.2
repeatedly.

∎

Corollary 4.4
.

If
T({\cal L})
is transitive, has prime-power degree, and contains a long cycle, then
T({\cal L})
is primitive.

Proof.

This is immediate from Corollary
4.3
.

∎

Corollary
4.4
expresses a special property of toggle groups. For example, the dihedral group of order eight, in its natural action on a set of order four, contains a 4-cycle but is not primitive by
[
2
, Theorem 4.2A(vi)]
, since it has nontrivial center and is nonabelian.

In view of Theorem
3.3
, Corollary
3.4
and Corollary
4.3
it would be very interesting to have an answer to the following question: Which primitive toggle groups of degree
n
contain a cycle of length at least
n-2
but do not contain
A_{n}
? A list of all the primitive permutation groups that contain a cycle of length at least
n-2
but do not contain
A_{n}
is given in
[
8
, Theorem 1.2]
. The first groups on the list are the subgroups of the affine group
AGL_{1}(p)
that contain a cyclic subgroup of order
p
, and we can show that these are not toggle groups. But at this point we do not know the status of the other groups on the list.
