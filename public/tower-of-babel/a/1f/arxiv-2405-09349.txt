Optimal constants of smoothing estimates for the 3D Dirac equation Thanks: The second author was supported by Japan Society for the Promotion of Science (JSPS) KAKENHI Grant Number JP23KJ1939.
Makoto Ikoma, Soichiro Suzuki

Abstract
. Recently, Ikoma ( 2022 ) considered optimal constants and extremisers for the 2 2 -dimensional Dirac equation using the spherical harmonics decomposition. Though its argument is valid in any dimensions d ≥ 2 d\geq 2 , the case d ≥ 3 d\geq 3 remains open since it leads us to too complicated calculation: determining all eigenvalues and eigenvectors of infinite dimensional matrices. In this paper, we give optimal constants and extremisers of smoothing estimates for the 3 3 -dimensional Dirac equation. In order to prove this, we construct a certain orthonormal basis of spherical harmonics. With respect to this basis, infinite dimensional matrices actually become block diagonal and so that eigenvalues and eigenvectors can be easily found. As applications, we obtain the equivalence of the smoothing estimate for the Schrödinger equation and the Dirac equation, and improve a result by Ben-Artzi and Umeda ( 2021 ).

Introduction

The Kato–Yajima smoothing estimates are one of the fundamental results in study of dispersive equations such as Schrödinger equations and Dirac equations, which were firstly observed by
Kato and Yajima [10]
, and have been studied by numerous researchers.
At first, we consider the following Schrödinger-type equation:

(1.1)

\begin{cases}i\partial_{t}u(x,t)=\phi(\lvert D\rvert)u(x,t),&(x,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R},\\
u(x,0)=f(x),&x\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\end{cases}

where
\phi(\lvert D\rvert)
denotes the Fourier multiplier operator whose symbol is
\phi(\lvert\makebox[4.30554pt]{{$\cdot$}}\rvert)
, that is,

(1.2)

\mathcal{F}\phi(\lvert D\rvert)f=\phi(\lvert\xi\rvert)\widehat{f}(\xi).

The function
\phi
is called a dispersion relation.
For example, (
1.1
) becomes the free Schrödinger equation if
\phi(r)=r^{2}
and the relativistic Schrödinger equation if
\phi(r)=\sqrt{r^{2}+m^{2}}
, respectively.
The (global) smoothing estimate of the Schrödinger-type equation is expressed as

(1.3)

\int_{t\in\mathbb{R}}\int_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\lvert\psi(\lvert D\rvert)e^{-it\phi(\lvert D\rvert)}f(x)\rvert^{2}w(\lvert x\rvert)\,dx\,dt\leq C\lVert f\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}.

Here functions
w
and
\psi
are called spatial weight and smoothing function, respectively.
We write
C_{d}(w,\psi,\phi)
for the optimal constant for the inequality (
1.3
), in other words,

(1.4)

C_{d}(w,\psi,\phi)\coloneqq\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\\
\lVert f\rVert_{2}=1\end{subarray}}\int_{t\in\mathbb{R}}\int_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\lvert\psi(\lvert D\rvert)e^{-it\phi(\lvert D\rvert)}f(x)\rvert^{2}w(\lvert x\rvert)\,dx\,dt.

Since we are interested in explicit constants, here we clarify that the Fourier transform in this paper is defined by

(1.5)

\hat{f}(\xi)\coloneqq\int_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}f(x)e^{-ix\cdot\xi}\,dx.

In this case, the Plancherel theorem states that

(1.6)

\lVert\hat{f}\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}^{2}=(2\pi)^{d}\lVert f\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}^{2}.

Bez, Saito, and Sugimoto [6]
established the following abstract result for the Schrödinger-type equation:

Theorem 1.1

(
[
6
, Theorem 1.1]
)
.

Let
d\geq 2
.
Assume that
(w,\psi,\phi)
is sufficiently nice (see Assumption
1.2
below).
Then we have

(1.7)

(2\pi)^{d-1}C_{d}(w,\psi,\phi)=\lambda^{*}\coloneqq\sup_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\sup_{r>0}\lambda_{k}(r),

where

(1.8)

\lambda_{k}(r)=\lvert\mathbb{S}^{d-2}\rvert\frac{r^{d-1}\psi(r)^{2}}{\phi^{\prime}(r)}\int_{-1}^{1}F_{w}(r^{2}(1-t))p_{d,k}(t)(1-t^{2})^{\frac{d-3}{2}}\,dt.

Here
F_{w}\colon(0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
is the function satisfying

(1.9)

F_{w}(\lvert\xi\rvert^{2}/2)=\widehat{w(\lvert\makebox[4.30554pt]{{$\cdot$}}\rvert)}(\xi)=\int_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}w(\lvert x\rvert)e^{-ix\cdot\xi}\,dx,

and
p_{d,k}
is the Legendre polynomial of degree
k
in
d
dimensions, which may be defined in a number of ways, for example, via the Rodrigues formula,

(1.10)

(1-t^{2})^{\frac{d-3}{2}}p_{d,k}(t)=(-1)^{k}\frac{\Gamma(\frac{d-1}{2})}{2^{k}\Gamma(k+\frac{d-1}{2})}\frac{d^{k}}{dt^{k}}(1-t^{2})^{k+\frac{d-3}{2}}.

Furthermore, extremisers exist if and only if there exists
k\in\mathbb{N}
such that the Lebesgue measure of

(1.11)

\{\,r>0:\lambda_{k}(r)=\lambda^{*}\,\}

is non-zero.

Assumption 1.2
.

Throughout the paper, we assume that
(w,\psi,\phi)
satisfies the following conditions.

•

The spatial weight
w\colon(0,\infty)\to[0,\infty)
satisfies
w(\lvert\makebox[4.30554pt]{{$\cdot$}}\rvert)\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
in the sense that

(1.12)

\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\ni\varphi\mapsto(w(\lvert\makebox[4.30554pt]{{$\cdot$}}\rvert),\varphi)\coloneqq\int_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}w(\lvert x\rvert)\varphi(x)\,dx

is a tempered distribution. In addition, the Fourier transform of
w(\lvert\makebox[4.30554pt]{{$\cdot$}}\rvert)
is regular, which means that there exists
F_{w}\colon(0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
satisfying

(1.13)

\int_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}w(\lvert x\rvert)\widehat{\varphi}(x)\,dx=\int_{\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}F_{w}(\lvert\xi\rvert^{2}/2)\varphi(\xi)\,d\xi

holds for any
\varphi\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.
Furthermore,

(1.14)

(0,\infty)\ni r\mapsto\int_{-1}^{1}F_{w}(r^{2}(1-t))p_{d,k}(t)(1-t^{2})^{\frac{d-3}{2}}\,dt

is continuous on
(0,\infty)
for each
k\in\mathbb{N}
.

•

The smoothing function
\psi\colon(0,\infty)\to[0,\infty)
is continuous.

•

The dispersion relation
\phi\colon(0,\infty)\to(0,\infty)
is continuously differentiable and satisfies
\phi^{\prime}(r)>0
for any
r>0
.

See (
A
), (
B
), (
C
) for typical examples for
(w,\psi,\phi)
.
We note that
\lambda_{k}\colon(0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
defined by (
1.8
) is continuous under the assumption above.
Furthermore, it is known that
\lambda_{k}
is actually non-negative (see
[
6
]
for details).
Now notice that if
(w,\psi,\phi)
satisfies the assumption above, then
(w,\sqrt{r}\psi/\sqrt{\phi^{\prime}},r^{2})
also does.
Hence, by the definition of
\{\lambda_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
, Theorem
1.1
immediately implies that

(1.15)

C_{d}(w,\psi,\phi)=2C_{d}(w,\sqrt{r}\psi/\sqrt{\phi^{\prime}},r^{2})

holds.
In particular, we have

(1.16)

C_{d}(w,\psi,(r^{2}+m^{2})^{1/2})=2C_{d}(w,(r^{2}+m^{2})^{1/4}\psi,r^{2}),

this means that smoothing estimates and their optimal constants for relativistic Schrödinger equations can be reduced to those for Schrödinger equations.
Hereinafter, we write
\phi_{m}(r)\coloneqq(r^{2}+m^{2})^{1/2}
for simplicity.

Now we are going to discuss the free Dirac equation. Let
d\geq 1
and write
N\coloneqq 2^{\lfloor(d+1)/2\rfloor}
.
The
d
-dimensional free Dirac equation with mass
m\geq 0
is given by

(1.17)

\begin{cases}i\partial_{t}u(x,t)=H_{m}u(x,t),&(x,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R},\\
u(x,0)=f(x),&x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.\end{cases}

Here
u
and
f
are
\mathbb{C}^{N}
-valued, and
the Dirac operator
H_{m}
is defined by

(1.18)

H_{m}\coloneqq\alpha\cdot D+m\beta=\sum_{j=1}^{d}\alpha_{j}D_{j}+m\beta,

where
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\ldots,\alpha_{d}
,
\alpha_{d+1}=\beta

are
N\times N
Hermitian matrices satisfying the anti-commutation relation
\alpha_{j}\alpha_{k}+\alpha_{k}\alpha_{j}=2\delta_{jk}I_{N}
.
Note that we have
H_{m}^{2}=(-\Delta+m^{2})I_{N}
.
In this sense, the Dirac operator
H_{m}
is similar to the relativistic Schrödinger operator
(-\Delta+m^{2})^{1/2}
.
Hence, it is natural to conjecture that the following smoothing estimate for the Dirac equation,

(1.19)

\int_{t\in\mathbb{R}}\int_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\lvert\psi(\lvert D\rvert)e^{-itH_{m}}f(x)\rvert^{2}w(\lvert x\rvert)\,dx\,dt\leq C\lVert f\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathbb{C}^{N})},

is also similar to that for the relativistic Schrödinger equation.
Now let
\widetilde{C}_{d}(w,\psi,m)
be the optimal constant of (
1.19
),
and let
\widetilde{C}_{d,\textup{rad.}}(w,\psi,m)
be that with radial initial data, those are,

(1.20)

\displaystyle\widetilde{C}_{d}(w,\psi,m)\coloneqq\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathbb{C}^{N})\\
\lVert f\rVert_{2}=1\end{subarray}}\int_{t\in\mathbb{R}}\int_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\lvert\psi(\lvert D\rvert)e^{-itH_{m}}f(x)\rvert^{2}w(\lvert x\rvert)\,dx\,dt,

(1.21)

\displaystyle\widetilde{C}_{d,\textup{rad.}}(w,\psi,m)\coloneqq\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathbb{C}^{N})\\
\lVert f\rVert_{2}=1,f\text{:radial}\end{subarray}}\int_{t\in\mathbb{R}}\int_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\lvert\psi(\lvert D\rvert)e^{-itH_{m}}f(x)\rvert^{2}w(\lvert x\rvert)\,dx\,dt.

Let

Then we have

Remark
.

Ben-Artzi, Ruzhansky, and Sugimoto [4]
established smoothing estimates and obtained optimal constants in more abstract setting.
Let
\mathcal{H}
be a Hilbert space,
{H}
be a self-adjoint operator on
\mathcal{H}
whose spectrum
\sigma({H})
is purely absolutely continuous, and
\{E_{H}(r)\}_{r\in\mathbb{R}}
denotes the spectral family of
{H}
.
Consider the following abstract Schrödinger equation:

(1.31)

\begin{cases}i\partial_{t}u(t)=\phi({H})u(t),&t\in\mathbb{R},\\
u(0)=f,&f\in\mathcal{H}.\end{cases}

Ben-Artzi et al. [4, Theorem 3.8]
states that the optimal constant of the smoothing estimate

(1.32)

\int_{t\in\mathbb{R}}\lVert\psi({H})e^{-it\phi({H})}f\rVert_{\mathcal{X}^{*}}^{2}\,dt\leq C\lVert f\rVert_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}

is given by

(1.33)

C=2\pi\sup_{r\in\sigma({H})}\frac{(\psi(r))^{2}}{\lvert\phi^{\prime}(r)\rvert}\lVert A_{{H}}(r)\rVert_{\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{X}^{*}}^{2},

where:

•

\psi
and
\phi
are sufficiently nice real-valued functions (see
Ben-Artzi et al. [4, Assumption 3.2]
for details).

•

\mathcal{X}\subset\mathcal{H}
is a dense subspace with a stronger norm
\lVert\makebox[4.30554pt]{{$\cdot$}}\rVert_{\mathcal{X}}
and so that
\mathcal{H}\subset\mathcal{X}^{*}
via the canonical embedding

(1.34)

\mathcal{H}\ni f\mapsto(\varphi\in\mathcal{X}\mapsto\langle f,\varphi\rangle_{\mathcal{H}})\in\mathcal{X}^{*}.

•

A_{{H}}(r)\colon\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{X}^{*}
is the spectral derivative

(1.35)

\langle A_{{H}}(r)f,g\rangle_{\mathcal{X}^{*},\mathcal{X}}=\frac{d}{dr}\langle E_{H}(r)f,g\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}.

In particular, for a spatial weight satisfying
0<w\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, letting

(1.36)

\mathcal{H}=L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}),\quad\mathcal{X}=L^{2}_{1/w}(\mathbb{R}^{d}),\quad{H}=\lvert\nabla\rvert

and using
\mathcal{X}^{*}\cong L^{2}_{w}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, we obtain

(1.37)

C_{d}(w,\psi,\phi)=2\pi\sup_{r\geq 0}\frac{(\psi(r))^{2}}{\lvert\phi^{\prime}(r)\rvert}\lVert A_{\lvert\nabla\rvert}(r)\rVert_{L^{2}_{1/w}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\to L^{2}_{w}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}^{2}.

Similarly,
letting

(1.38)

\mathcal{H}=L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathbb{C}^{N}),\quad\mathcal{X}=L^{2}_{1/w}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathbb{C}^{N}),\quad{H}=H_{m}

gives us

(1.39)

\widetilde{C}_{d}(w,\psi,m)=2\pi\sup_{\lvert r\rvert\geq m}(\psi(\sqrt{r^{2}-m^{2}}))^{2}\lVert A_{H_{m}}(r)\rVert_{L^{2}_{1/w}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathbb{C}^{N})\to L^{2}_{w}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathbb{C}^{N})}^{2}.

However, this method is inapplicable for unbounded weights such as
w(x)=\lvert x\rvert^{-s}
unlike Theorems
1.1
and
1.5
, since
L^{2}_{1/w}\not\subset L^{2}
.

Combining Theorems
1.1
,
1.3
,
1.5
, and the trivial inequality

(1.40)

\frac{1}{2}\max\{\lambda_{k}(r),\lambda_{k+1}(r)\}\leq\widetilde{\lambda}_{k}(r)\leq\max\{\lambda_{k}(r),\lambda_{k+1}(r)\},

we obtain the equivalence of smoothing estimates for the Schrödinger, relativistic Schrödinger, and Dirac equations when
d=2,3
.

\displaystyle\widetilde{C}_{d}((1+r^{2})^{-s/2},\phi_{m}(r)^{-1/2}r^{1/2},m)

\displaystyle{\geq}\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d-1}}\sup_{r>0}\widetilde{\lambda}_{k}(r)

(5.17)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2(2\pi)^{d-1}}\limsup_{r\to\infty}\left(\lambda_{0}(r)+\lambda_{1}(r)+\frac{m^{2}}{r^{2}+m^{2}}(\lambda_{0}(r)-\lambda_{1}(r))\right),

where
\lambda_{k}
is that associated with
((1+r^{2})^{-s/2},\phi_{m}(r)^{-1/2}r^{1/2},\phi_{m})
.
Hence, it is enough to show that

(5.18)

\frac{1}{2(2\pi)^{d-1}}\limsup_{r\to\infty}\left(\lambda_{0}(r)+\lambda_{1}(r)+\frac{m^{2}}{r^{2}+m^{2}}(\lambda_{0}(r)-\lambda_{1}(r))\right)=\pi^{1/2}\frac{\Gamma((s-1)/2)}{\Gamma(s)}.

This follows from the fact that

(5.19)

\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d-1}}\lim_{r\to\infty}\lambda_{k}(r)=\pi^{1/2}\frac{\Gamma((s-1)/2)}{\Gamma(s)}

holds for each
k\in\mathbb{N}
.
See
[
5
, Theorem 2.2]
and
[
6
, (1.11)]
for details.
