From resolvents to generalized equations and quasi-variational inequalities: existence and differentiability
Gerd Wachsmuth

Abstract
We consider a generalized equation governed by a strongly monotone and Lipschitz single-valued mapping and a maximally monotone set-valued mapping in a Hilbert space. We are interested in the sensitivity of solutions w.r.t. perturbations of both mappings. We demonstrate that the directional differentiability of the solution map can be verified by using the directional differentiability of the single-valued operator and of the resolvent of the set-valued mapping. The result is applied to quasi-generalized equations in which we have an additional dependence of the solution within the set-valued part of the equation.

Introduction

We consider the (local) solution mapping
S\colon U\to Y
,
u\mapsto y
, of the generalized equation

0\in A(y,u)+B(y,u).

(1)

Here,
Y
is a (real) Hilbert space,

U
is a (real) Banach space,

A\colon Y\times U\to Y^{\star}
is (locally) strongly monotone and Lipschitz w.r.t. its first argument
and
the set-valued map
B\colon Y\times U\rightrightarrows Y^{\star}
is assumed to be maximally monotone w.r.t. its first argument.

The equation (
1
) can be used to model many real-world phenomena.
In the case where
B(\cdot,u)
coincides with the subdifferential
\partial j_{u}

of a proper, lower-semicontinuous and convex function
j_{u}\colon Y\to(-\infty,\infty]
,
(
1
) is a variational inequality (VI) of the second kind.
If further,
j_{u}
is the indicator function
\delta_{C_{u}}\colon Y\to\{0,\infty\}

of a non-empty, closed and convex set
C_{u}\subset Y
,
it is a VI of the first kind
and
\partial\delta_{C_{u}}=\mathcal{N}_{C_{u}}
is the normal cone of
C_{u}
.

We show by some simple arguments
that
the existence of solutions and the directional differentiability of
S

follow
from the properties of
A
and of the resolvent
J_{B}
of
B

and from the directional differentiability of
A
and
J_{B}
, respectively.
Here,
J_{B}\colon Y\times U\to Y
,
(q,u)\mapsto y
,
is the solution map of

0\in R(y-q)+B(y,u)

(2)

where
R\colon Y\to Y^{\star}
is the Riesz map of the Hilbert space
Y
.
Similarly, we treat the
equation

0\in A(y,u)+B(y-\Phi(y,u),u),

(3)

where
\Phi\colon Y\times U\to Y

is assumed to have a small Lipschitz constant w.r.t. the first variable.
Note that (
3
)
is an inclusion which generalizes
the setting of so-called quasi-variational inequalities (QVIs).

Let us put our work in perspective.
The existence of solutions to (
1
)
is well understood,
we refer to, e.g.,

[
5
, Section 23.4]
.
The first contribution
which studies differentiability of problems similar to (
1
)
is
[
22
]
,
in which it is shown that the projection onto a closed, convex set
C

is directionally differentiable on
C
itself.
In
[
16
,
13
]

it is proved that the projection is directionally differentiable everywhere,
if
C
is assumed to be polyhedric,
see also
[
19
]
.
Note that the projection onto
C
is the solution mapping
of (
1
) with

A(y,u)=R(y-u)

and
B(y,u)=\partial\delta_{C}(y)=\mathcal{N}_{C}(y)
.
The case of non-linear
A
was treated in
[
15
,
14
]
.
Later,
theory for the differentiability of
J_{B}
with
B=\partial j

was set up in
[
11
]
.
It was shown that the differentiability of
J_{\partial j}

is equivalent to the
so-called twice epi-differentiability of
j
,
see also
[
9
]
.
Finally,

[
1
, Theorem 1]

studies (
1
)
with a real parameter
u\geq 0
.
However, since we are mainly interested in directional differentiability,
this is not a restriction.

Contributions corresponding to the QVI case
(
3
)
are rather new
and are currently restricted to the special case

\text{Find }y\in K(y)\quad\text{s.t.}\quad\langle A(y)-u,v-y\rangle\geq 0\quad\forall v\in K(y)

with
K(y)=K-\Phi(y)
for some polyhedric set
K\subset Y
.
Important parameters for the study of this problem are
the constant
\mu_{A}
of strong monotonicity of
A

and the Lipschitz constants
L_{A}
and
L_{\Phi}

of
A
and
\Phi
, respectively.
The first contribution in this direction is

[
2
, Theorem 1]
.
Therein,
the authors showed directional differentiability
into non-negative directions
under monotonicity assumptions on
A

and

L_{\Phi}<\mu_{A}/(\mu_{A}+L_{A})
.
Afterwards,

[
20
, Theorem 5.5]

proved the directional differentiability into arbitrary directions
and the smallness assumption on
\Phi
was relaxed to

L_{\Phi}<\mu_{A}/L_{A}

(and an even weaker inequality suffices if
A
is the derivative of a convex function).
However, this result needs
that
\Phi
is Fréchet differentiable
(or, at least Bouligand differentiable).
Later,

[
3
, Theorem 3.2]

showed
that it is sufficient to have a directionally differentiable
\Phi

at the price of having again the stricter requirement

L_{\Phi}<\mu_{A}/(\mu_{A}+L_{A})
.
A different approach, which is based on concavity properties
and which is not restricted to the special case above,
is given in
[
10
]
.

The main contributions of our paper are the following.

(i)

Theorem

3.14
shows that the solution map of (
1
)
is directionally differentiable.
This result is very similar to

[
1
, Theorem 1]
.
However, our assumptions are localized around a solution
and we require the directional differentiability of the resolvent
J_{B}

instead of the proto-differentiability of
B
.
This results in a much easier proof.

(ii)

Lemma

3.20

shows that the directional differentiability of
J_{B}

is equivalent to
B
being proto-differentiable
with a maximally monotone proto-derivative.
Thus, the differentiability assumptions on
B

in
Theorem

3.14

and

[
1
, Theorem 1]

coincide.

(iii)

In
Section

4

we show that the approaches
of

[
2
, Theorem 1]

and

[
20
, Theorem 5.5]

can be generalized to deal with the solution map of (
3
).
Moreover,
we only need directional differentiability of the data functions

A
,
\Phi
and
J_{B}

as in

[
2
]

and only the weaker requirements on
L_{\Phi}

from

[
20
]
.

The paper is structured as follows.
In
Section

2
we fix some notation
and state
Theorem

2.2

concerning convex functions
with a strongly monotone and Lipschitz continuous derivative.

Section

3
contains the differentiability result for (
1
),
whereas

Section

4
is concerned with (
3
).
Some applications are presented in
Section

5
.

2
Notation

In this paper, all linear spaces are over the field of real numbers.
For a Banach space
U
,

\lVert\,\cdot\,\rVert\colon U\to[0,\infty)

and

\langle\,\cdot\,,\,\cdot\,\rangle\colon U^{\star}\times U\to\mathbb{R}

denote the norm and the duality product, respectively.
In a Hilbert space
Y
,

(\,\cdot\,,\,\cdot\,)\colon Y\times Y\to\mathbb{R}

is the inner product.
The spaces will be clear from the context
and will not be indicated by a subscript.

Let
U
and
Y
be a Banach space
and a Hilbert space, respectively.
We denote by
R\colon Y\to Y^{\star}

the Riesz map of
Y
.
For
\varepsilon>0
and
y^{*}\in Y
,

B_{\varepsilon}(y^{*})
(
U_{\varepsilon}(y^{*})
) denotes the closed (open) ball
in
Y

with center
y^{*}
and radius
\varepsilon
, respectively.

If
B\colon Y\times U\rightrightarrows Y^{\star}

and

\mathcal{U}\subset U

are given
such that

B(\cdot,u)\colon Y\rightrightarrows Y^{\star}

is maximally monotone
for all
u\in\mathcal{U}
,
we say that

B
is a parametrized maximally monotone operator
and
we define its resolvent

J_{B}\colon Y\times\mathcal{U}\to Y

via

J_{B}(\cdot,u):=J_{B(\cdot,u)}
,
i.e.,
for
(q,u)\in Y\times\mathcal{U}

the point

y=J_{B}(q,u)=(R+B(\cdot,u))^{-1}(Rq)

is the unique solution of

0\in R(y-q)+B(y,u).

For a closed, convex subset
K\subset Y
,
we denote by
T_{K}\colon Y\rightrightarrows Y
and
N_{K}\colon Y\rightrightarrows Y^{\star}

the tangent-cone and normal-cone map.
Moreover,
by

K^{\circ}:=\{\mu\in Y^{\star}\nonscript\;|\nonscript\;\mathopen{}\langle\mu,v\rangle\leq 0\;\forall v\in K\},\qquad\mu^{\perp}:=\{v\in Y\nonscript\;|\nonscript\;\mathopen{}\langle\mu,v\rangle=0\}

we
denote the polar cone of
K

and
the annihilator of
\mu\in Y^{\star}
,
respectively.

We need a characterization of convex functions
defined on subsets of
Y

with a Lipschitz continuous derivative.

Theorem 2.1
.

Let
\mathcal{Y}\subset Y
be nonempty, open and convex
and
L_{f}\in(0,\infty)
.
Further,
the function
f\colon\mathcal{Y}\to\mathbb{R}

is assumed to be convex and continuous.
Then, the following assertions
are equivalent.

(i)

f
is Gâteaux differentiable on
\mathcal{Y}
and
f^{\prime}\colon\mathcal{Y}\to Y^{\star}
is
L_{f}
-Lipschitz continuous on
\mathcal{Y}
.

(ii)

f
is Gâteaux differentiable on
\mathcal{Y}
and
f^{\prime}\colon\mathcal{Y}\to Y^{\star}
is
1/L_{f}
-cocoercive,
i.e.,

\langle f^{\prime}(y_{2})-f^{\prime}(y_{1}),y_{2}-y_{1}\rangle\geq\frac{1}{L_{f}}\lVert f^{\prime}(y_{2})-f^{\prime}(y_{1})\rVert^{2}\qquad\forall y_{1},y_{2}\in\mathcal{Y}.

(iii)

The map
\frac{L_{f}}{2}\lVert\cdot\rVert^{2}-f

is convex on
\mathcal{Y}
.

For the proof, we refer to
[
18
, Theorem 3.1]
.

Next, we give
an important
inequality
for convex functions.
This inequality is well-known
in the finite-dimensional case if the convex function is defined on the entire space,
see, e.g.,

[
17
, Theorem 2.1.12]

or

[
8
, Lemma 3.10]
.
The infinite dimensional version (on the entire space) was given in

[
20
, Lemma 3.4]
.
By using
Theorem

2.1
,
we can adopt the proof to the situation at hand.

Theorem 2.2
.

Let
\mathcal{Y}\subset Y
be nonempty, open and convex.
Further,
let
f\colon\mathcal{Y}\to\mathbb{R}

be convex, continuous and Gâteaux differentiable
such that

f^{\prime}\colon\mathcal{Y}\to Y^{\star}
is strongly monotone with constant
\mu_{f}\in(0,\infty)

and Lipschitz continuous with constant
L_{f}\in(0,\infty)
.
Then,

\big\langle f^{\prime}(y_{2})-f^{\prime}(y_{1}),y_{2}-y_{1}\big\rangle\geq\frac{\mu_{f}L_{f}}{\mu_{f}+L_{f}}\lVert y_{2}-y_{1}\rVert^{2}+\frac{1}{\mu_{f}+L_{f}}\big\lVert f^{\prime}(y_{2})-f^{\prime}(y_{1})\big\rVert^{2}

for all
y_{1},y_{2}\in\mathcal{Y}
.

Proof 2.3
.

We define
g:=f-\frac{\mu_{f}}{2}\lVert\cdot\rVert^{2}
.
Thus, we have
g^{\prime}(y)=f^{\prime}(y)-\mu_{f}Ry
.
The strong monotonicity of
f^{\prime}
implies that
g
is convex.
From
Theorem

2.1

we infer that

\frac{L_{f}}{2}\lVert\cdot\rVert^{2}-f=\frac{L_{f}-\mu_{f}}{2}\lVert\cdot\rVert^{2}-g

is convex.
Applying
Theorem

2.1
again
shows that
g^{\prime}
is
1/(L_{f}-\mu_{f})
-cocoercive,
i.e.,
for arbitrary
y_{1},y_{2}\in\mathcal{Y}

we have

\displaystyle(L_{f}-\mu_{f})\left(\langle f^{\prime}(y_{2})-f^{\prime}(y_{1}),y_{2}-y_{1}\rangle-\mu_{f}\lVert y_{2}-y_{1}\rVert^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad=(L_{f}-\mu_{f})\langle g^{\prime}(y_{2})-g^{\prime}(y_{1}),y_{2}-y_{1}\rangle

\displaystyle\qquad\geq\lVert g^{\prime}(y_{2})-g^{\prime}(y_{1})\rVert^{2}=\lVert f^{\prime}(y_{2})-f^{\prime}(y_{1})\rVert^{2}-2\mu_{f}\langle f^{\prime}(y_{2})-f^{\prime}(y_{1}),y_{2}-y_{1}\rangle+\mu_{f}^{2}\lVert y_{2}-y_{1}\rVert^{2}.

Rearranging terms yields
the claim.

3
Generalized equations

We consider the solution mapping of the generalized equation (
1
).
We show that solutions
are locally stable and directionally differentiable under suitable assumptions.

3.1
Local solvability

We set up the standing assumptions
which allow to prove
that (
1
)
is uniquely solvable around
a given reference solution
(y^{*},u^{*})
.

Assumption 3.1

(Standing assumptions)
.

Let
(y^{*},u^{*})\in Y\times U

be
given
and let
\mathcal{U}\subset U
be a neighborhood of
u^{*}
.

(i)

For all
u\in\mathcal{U}
,

A(\cdot,u)
is locally strongly monotone and Lipschitz
in a neighborhood of
y^{*}
, uniformly in
u\in\mathcal{U}
.
To be precise, there exist constants
\varepsilon,\mu_{A},L_{A}>0

such that
for all
y_{1},y_{2}\in B_{\varepsilon}(y^{*})

and for all
u\in\mathcal{U}

we have

\displaystyle\langle A(y_{2},u)-A(y_{1},u),y_{2}-y_{1}\rangle

\displaystyle\geq\mu_{A}\lVert y_{2}-y_{1}\rVert^{2},

\displaystyle\lVert A(y_{2},u)-A(y_{1},u)\rVert

\displaystyle\leq L_{A}\lVert y_{2}-y_{1}\rVert.

(ii)

B(\cdot,u)\colon Y\rightrightarrows Y^{\star}
is maximally monotone
for all
u\in\mathcal{U}
.

Here and in the sequel,
it is sufficient that
A
is defined only on
B_{\varepsilon}(y^{*})\times\mathcal{U}

and that
B
is defined on
Y\times\mathcal{U}
.

We check that the GE
has a unique solution in a neighborhood of
y^{*}

for certain “small” perturbations
u
of
u^{*}
.

Theorem 3.2
.

Let
3.1
be satisfied
by a solution
(y^{*},u^{*})
of (
1
).
We select
\rho\in(0,2\mu_{A}/L_{A}^{2})
,

r\in(0,\varepsilon]

and set
c:=\sqrt{1-2\rho\mu_{A}+\rho^{2}L_{A}^{2}}\in(0,1)
.
Suppose that
\zeta\in Y^{\star}

and
u\in\mathcal{U}

satisfy

\lVert J_{\rho B}(q_{\rho}^{*},u)-J_{\rho B}(q_{\rho}^{*},u^{*})\rVert+\rho\lVert A(y^{*},u)-A(y^{*},u^{*})\rVert+\rho\lVert\zeta\rVert\leq(1-c)r,

(4)

where

q_{\rho}^{*}=y^{*}-\rho R^{-1}A(y^{*},u^{*})
.
Then,
there exist unique solutions
y,\tilde{y}

of

0\in\zeta+A(y,u)+B(y,u),\quad y\in B_{\varepsilon}(y^{*})

(5)

and

0\in A(\tilde{y},u)+B(\tilde{y},u),\quad\tilde{y}\in B_{\varepsilon}(y^{*}).

(6)

These solutions satisfy

y,\tilde{y}\in B_{r}(y^{*})

and

\lVert y-\tilde{y}\rVert\leq\frac{\rho}{1-c}\lVert\zeta\rVert
.

Assumption (
4
) ensures that (
5
) and (
6
)
are small perturbations of (
1
) with
u=u^{*}
.
Later, we will see that continuity assumptions on
A
and on (the resolvent of)
B

can be used to verify (
4
), see
Lemma

3.7
.

Proof 3.3
.

The proof is inspired by the proof of
[
7
, Theorem 5.6]

which treats the special case of a variational inequality.

We define
T\colon Y\to Y
,
y\mapsto z

as the solution operator of

0\in R(z-(y-\rho R^{-1}A(y,u)-\rho R^{-1}\zeta))+\rho B(z,u),

i.e.,

T(y)=J_{\rho B}(y-\rho R^{-1}A(y,u)-\rho R^{-1}\zeta,u).

In order to apply the Banach fixed-point theorem,
we check that
the mapping

Q\colon B_{\varepsilon}(y^{*})\to Y
,
x\mapsto x-\rho R^{-1}A(x,u)

is a contraction.
Indeed,

\displaystyle\lVert x-\rho R^{-1}A(x,u)-y+\rho R^{-1}A(y,u)\rVert^{2}

\displaystyle=\lVert x-y\rVert^{2}+\lVert\rho R^{-1}(A(x,u)-A(y,u))\rVert^{2}

\displaystyle\qquad-2\rho\langle A(x,u)-A(y,u),x-y\rangle

\displaystyle\leq\left(1-2\rho\mu_{A}+\rho^{2}L_{A}^{2}\right)\lVert x-y\rVert^{2}

holds for all
x,y\in B_{\varepsilon}(y^{*})

due to
3.1
(i)
.
Hence,
Q
is Lipschitz with constant
c\in(0,1)
.
Since
J_{\rho B}(\cdot,u)

is Lipschitz with constant
1
,

T\colon B_{\varepsilon}(y^{*})\to Y
is a contraction.

It remains to check that
T
maps
B_{r}(y^{*})

onto itself.
To this end, we denote the three terms
on the left-hand side of (
4
)
by
\kappa_{1}
,
\kappa_{2}
and
\kappa_{3}
, respectively.
By using
y^{*}=J_{\rho B}(q_{\rho}^{*},u^{*})
,
we have for an arbitrary
y\in B_{r}(y^{*})

\displaystyle\lVert T(y)-y^{*}\rVert

\displaystyle=\lVert J_{\rho B}(Q(y)-\rho R^{-1}\zeta,u)-J_{\rho B}(q_{\rho}^{*},u^{*})\rVert

\displaystyle\leq\lVert J_{\rho B}(Q(y)-\rho R^{-1}\zeta,u)-J_{\rho B}(q_{\rho}^{*},u)\rVert+\lVert J_{\rho B}(q_{\rho}^{*},u)-J_{\rho B}(q_{\rho}^{*},u^{*})\rVert

\displaystyle\leq\lVert y-\rho R^{-1}A(y,u)-\rho R^{-1}\zeta-(y^{*}-\rho R^{-1}A(y^{*},u^{*}))\rVert+\kappa_{1}

\displaystyle\leq\lVert y-\rho R^{-1}A(y,u)-(y^{*}-\rho R^{-1}A(y^{*},u))\rVert+\rho\lVert A(y^{*},u)-A(y^{*},u^{*})\rVert+\rho\lVert\zeta\rVert+\kappa_{1}

\displaystyle=\lVert Q(y)-Q(y^{*})\rVert+\kappa_{1}+\kappa_{2}+\kappa_{3}\leq r.

Hence, we have shown that
T\colon B_{r}(y^{*})\to B_{r}(y^{*})

is a contraction.

The Banach fixed-point theorem yields a unique fixed point
y\in B_{r}(y^{*})

and this is a solution of (
5
).
Similarly, the solvability of (
6
)
is obtained by using the same arguments with
\zeta=0
.
By repeating the same argument with
r=\varepsilon
,
we establish the uniqueness of solutions in
B_{\varepsilon}(y^{*})
.

For the final estimate,
we note

\displaystyle\tilde{y}

\displaystyle=J_{\rho B}(\tilde{y}-\rho R^{-1}A(\tilde{y},u),u),

\displaystyle y

\displaystyle=J_{\rho B}(y-\rho R^{-1}A(y,u)-\rho R^{-1}\zeta,u).

Thus,

\lVert y-\tilde{y}\rVert\leq\lVert Q(y)-Q(\tilde{y})\rVert+\rho\lVert\zeta\rVert\leq c\lVert y-\tilde{y}\rVert+\rho\lVert\zeta\rVert

and this gives
the desired estimate.

The second term on the left-hand side of (
4
)
becomes small if we assume some continuity of
A

and if
u
is close to
u^{*}
.
In order to control the first term,
we use a famous result by Attouch.

Lemma 3.4

(
[
4
, Proposition 3.60]
)
.

Let
(B_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence of maximal monotone operators on
Y

and let
B_{0}\colon Y\rightrightarrows Y^{\star}
be maximally monotone.
Then, the following are equivalent.

(i)

There exists
\rho_{0}>0
such that for all
y\in Y
we have
J_{\rho_{0}B_{n}}(y)\to J_{\rho_{0}B_{0}}(y)
.

(ii)

For all
\rho>0
and all
y\in Y
we have
J_{\rho B_{n}}(y)\to J_{\rho B_{0}}(y)
.

Corollary 3.5
.

Suppose that for every
v\in Y
, the mapping
J_{B}(v,\cdot)\colon\mathcal{U}\to Y

is continuous at
u^{*}
.
Then, for all
\rho>0
and
v\in Y
,

J_{\rho B}(v,\cdot)\colon\mathcal{U}\to Y

is continuous at
u^{*}
.

Proof 3.6
.

We have to show that
J_{\rho B}(v,u_{n})\to J_{\rho B}(v,u^{*})

for all sequences

(u_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subset\mathcal{U}
with
u_{n}\to u^{*}
.
This follows directly from
Lemma

3.4

by setting
B_{n}:=B(\cdot,u_{n})
and
B_{0}:=B(\cdot,u^{*})
.

Hence, if

3.1

and the assumption of
Corollary

3.5

are satisfied
and if
A(y^{*},\cdot)\colon\mathcal{U}\to Y
is continuous at
u^{*}
,
the estimate (
4
) holds if
\zeta
is small enough
and if
u
is sufficiently close to
u^{*}
.
For later reference, we also give a directional version of this statement.

Lemma 3.7
.

Let
3.1
be satisfied
by a solution
(y^{*},u^{*})
of (
1
)
and choose
\rho,r,c
as in
Theorem

3.2
.
Further, let
h\in U
be arbitrary.
We assume that

t\mapsto A(y^{*},u^{*}+th)\in Y^{\star}

and

t\mapsto J_{B}(v,u^{*}+th)\in Y

are right-continuous at
t=0
for all
v\in Y
.
Then, the estimate (
4
)
is satisfied by
u=u^{*}+th

and
\zeta\in Y^{\star}

if
t>0
and
\lVert\zeta\rVert

are small enough.

3.2
Directional differentiability

In order to prove directional differentiability of the solution mapping,
we need some differentiability assumptions concerning the
mappings
A
and
B
.

Assumption 3.8

(Differentiability assumptions)
.

In addition to
3.1
,
we suppose the following.

(i)

A
is directionally differentiable at
(y^{*},u^{*})
.

(ii)

J_{B}
is directionally differentiable at
(q^{*},u^{*})

with
q^{*}=y^{*}-R^{-1}A(y^{*},u^{*})
.

Interestingly,
the next result shows that

3.8
(ii)

already implies
that the directional derivative of
J_{B}

is again a resolvent of a parametrized maximally monotone operator.
In the setting that
B
is a subdifferential and independent of
u
,
this result follows from
[
11
, Theorems 3.9, 4.3]
.

Lemma 3.9
.

Let
3.8
be satisfied
by a solution
(y^{*},u^{*})
of (
1
).
We further set

\xi^{*}:=-A(y^{*},u^{*})\in B(y^{*},u^{*})
.
Then,
the operator

DB(y^{*},u^{*}\mathbin{|}\xi^{*})\colon Y\times U\rightrightarrows Y^{\star}
,

DB(y^{*},u^{*}\mathbin{|}\xi^{*})(\delta,h):=\{R(k-\delta)\nonscript\;|\nonscript\;\mathopen{}k\in Y,\delta=J_{B}^{\prime}(q^{*},u^{*};k,h)\}\qquad\forall(\delta,h)\in Y\times U,

(7)

is
a
parametrized maximally monotone
operator
and we have

J_{B}^{\prime}(q^{*},u^{*};\cdot)=J_{DB(y^{*},u^{*}\mathbin{|}\xi^{*})},

(8)

i.e.,
\delta=J_{B}^{\prime}(q^{*},u^{*};k,h)=J_{DB(y^{*},u^{*}\mathbin{|}\xi^{*})}(k,h)

if and only if
\delta
solves

0\in R(\delta-k)+DB(y^{*},u^{*}\mathbin{|}\xi^{*})(\delta,h).

We have chosen such a complicated name
for the linearization of
B
,
since it will turn out that
this linearization coincides with the so-called
proto-derivative of
B
,
see
Section

3.3
.

Proof 3.10
.

Let us check the monotonicity w.r.t. the parameter
\delta
.
For fixed
h\in U
and arbitrary
k_{1},k_{2}\in Y
, we have

\displaystyle\left\lVert\frac{J_{B}(q^{*}+tk_{1},u^{*}+th)-J_{B}(x)}{t}-\frac{J_{B}(q^{*}+tk_{2},u^{*}+th)-J_{B}(x)}{t}\right\rVert^{2}

\displaystyle\qquad=\frac{1}{t^{2}}\lVert J_{B}(q^{*}+tk_{1},u^{*}+th)-J_{B}(q^{*}+tk_{2},u^{*}+th)\rVert^{2}

\displaystyle\qquad\leq\frac{1}{t^{2}}(J_{B}(q^{*}+tk_{1},u^{*}+th)-J_{B}(q^{*}+tk_{2},u^{*}+th),t\,(k_{1}-k_{2}))

\displaystyle\qquad=\left(\frac{J_{B}(q^{*}+tk_{1},u^{*}+th)-J_{B}(x)}{t}-\frac{J_{B}(q^{*}+tk_{2},u^{*}+th)-J_{B}(x)}{t},k_{1}-k_{2}\right).

Here,
the inequality follows
from the fact that resolvents
of maximally monotone operators
are firmly non-expansive.
By taking the limit

t\searrow 0
, we find

\lVert J_{B}^{\prime}(q^{*},u^{*};k_{1},h)-J_{B}^{\prime}(q^{*},u^{*};k_{2},h)\rVert^{2}\leq(J_{B}^{\prime}(q^{*},u^{*};k_{1},h)-J_{B}^{\prime}(q^{*},u^{*};k_{2},h),k_{1}-k_{2}).

Hence,

\left(J_{B}^{\prime}(q^{*},u^{*};k_{1},h)-J_{B}^{\prime}(q^{*},u^{*};k_{2},h),k_{1}-J_{B}^{\prime}(q^{*},u^{*};k_{1},h)-k_{2}+J_{B}^{\prime}(q^{*},u^{*};k_{2},h)\right)\geq 0,

i.e.,

\left\langle R(k_{1}-\delta_{1})-R(k_{2}-\delta_{2}),\delta_{1}-\delta_{2}\right\rangle\geq 0,

where
\delta_{i}:=J_{B}^{\prime}(q^{*},u^{*};k_{i},h)
.
This shows monotonicity of
DB(y^{*},u^{*}\mathbin{|}\xi^{*})(\cdot,h)
.

Minty’s theorem, see
[
5
, Theorem 21.1]
,
implies that the operator
DB(y^{*},u^{*}\mathbin{|}\xi^{*})(\cdot,h)

is maximally monotone
if and only if

R+DB(y^{*},u^{*}\mathbin{|}\xi^{*})(\cdot,h)

is surjective.
This, however,
is obvious:
for an arbitrary
\zeta\in Y^{\star}
,
we can set
\delta=J_{B}^{\prime}(q^{*},u^{*};R^{-1}\zeta,h)

and have

\zeta\in R\delta+DB(y^{*},u^{*}\mathbin{|}\xi^{*})(\delta,h)
.

Finally, (
8
) follows from a straightforward calculation.

Remark 3.11
.

Minimal changes to the proof
show
that
the assertion of
Lemma

3.9

remains true if we only assume that
J_{B}
is weakly directionally differentiable
at
(q^{*},u^{*})
,
i.e., if

\frac{J_{B}(q^{*}+tk,u^{*}+th)-J(q^{*},u^{*})}{t}\rightharpoonup J_{B}^{\prime}(q^{*},u^{*};k,h)\qquad\text{in $Y$ as $t\searrow 0$}

for all
(k,h)\in Y\times U
.

Next, we apply
Lemma

3.9

to the normal cone mapping of a polyhedric set.

Proposition 3.12
.

Suppose that
K\subset Y
is polyhedric
and
B(\cdot,u):=N_{K}
is the normal cone mapping to
K

(independent of
u
).
Then,
3.8
(ii)
is satisfied
and we have

DB(y^{*}\mathbin{|}\xi^{*})(\delta)=N_{\mathcal{K}}(\delta)=\begin{cases}\mathcal{K}^{\circ}\cap\delta^{\perp}&\text{if }\delta\in\mathcal{K},\\
\emptyset&\text{if }\delta\not\in\mathcal{K},\end{cases}

where
\mathcal{K}=T_{K}(y^{*})\cap(\xi^{*})^{\perp}

denotes the critical cone.
That is,
DB(y^{*}\mathbin{|}\xi^{*})

is the normal cone mapping to the critical cone
\mathcal{K}
.
Here, we have suppressed the arguments
u^{*}
and
h
.

Proof 3.13
.

From
[
16
,
13
]

we get the directional differentiability
of
J_{B}=\operatorname{Proj}_{K}

and

\operatorname{Proj}_{K}^{\prime}(q^{*};k)=\operatorname{Proj}_{\mathcal{K}}(k).

Using (
7
),
we have

\displaystyle DB(y^{*}\mathbin{|}\xi^{*})(\delta)

\displaystyle:=\{R(k-\delta)\nonscript\;|\nonscript\;\mathopen{}k\in Y,\delta=\operatorname{Proj}_{\mathcal{K}}(k)\}

\displaystyle\phantom{:}=\{R(k-\delta)\nonscript\;|\nonscript\;\mathopen{}k\in Y,\delta\in\mathcal{K},R(k-\delta)\in\mathcal{K}^{\circ},\langle R(k-\delta),\delta\rangle=0\}

and the claim follows.

Theorem 3.14
.

Suppose that
3.8
is satisfied
by a solution
(y^{*},u^{*})
of (
1
).
We denote by
S
the local solution mapping of (
1
),
cf.
Theorems

3.2
and
3.7
.
Then,
S
is directionally differentiable at
u^{*}
.
For

h\in U
,
the derivative

\delta=S^{\prime}(u^{*};h)

is the unique solution of

0\in A^{\prime}(y^{*},u^{*};\delta,h)+DB(y^{*},u^{*}\mathbin{|}\xi^{*})(\delta,h),

(9)

where
\xi^{*}=-A(y^{*},u^{*})
.

Proof 3.15
.

The proof is inspired by
[
14
]
.
Since
A^{\prime}(y^{*},u^{*};\cdot,h)\colon Y\to Y^{\star}

is again strongly monotone and Lipschitz,
and since the operator
DB(y^{*},u^{*}\mathbin{|}\xi^{*})(\cdot,h)\colon Y\rightrightarrows Y^{\star}

is maximally monotone by
Lemma

3.9
,
it is clear that the linearized equation
possesses a unique solution
\delta\in Y

for an arbitrary
h\in U
.
It remains to check that
\delta

is the directional derivative of
S
.
We set

\displaystyle q^{*}

\displaystyle:=y^{*}-R^{-1}A(y^{*},u^{*}),

\displaystyle k

\displaystyle:=\delta-R^{-1}A^{\prime}(y^{*},u^{*};\delta,h).

This implies
y^{*}=J_{B}(q^{*},u^{*})

and

\delta=J_{B}^{\prime}(q^{*},u^{*};k,h)
,
cf. (
8
).

Next, we take an arbitrary sequence
(t_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subset(0,\infty)
with
t_{n}\searrow 0

and define

y_{n}:=J_{B}(q^{*}+t_{n}k,u^{*}+t_{n}h),\qquad\text{i.e.,}\quad 0\in R(y_{n}-q^{*}-t_{n}k)+B(y_{n},u^{*}+t_{n}h)

for
n
large enough.
Then,

3.8
(ii)

implies

\frac{y_{n}-y^{*}}{t_{n}}\to J_{B}^{\prime}(q^{*},u^{*};k,h)=\delta.

Now, we define

\zeta_{n}:=\frac{R(y_{n}-q^{*}-t_{n}k)-A(y_{n},u^{*}+t_{n}h)}{t_{n}}.

By using the definition of
q^{*}

we get

\displaystyle\zeta_{n}

\displaystyle=\frac{R(y_{n}-y^{*}-t_{n}k)+A(y^{*},u^{*})-A(y_{n},u^{*}+t_{n}h)}{t_{n}}

\displaystyle=R\Big(\frac{y_{n}-y^{*}}{t_{n}}-k\Big)+\frac{A(y^{*},u^{*})-A(y^{*}+t_{n}\delta,u^{*}+t_{n}h)}{t_{n}}+\frac{A(y^{*}+t_{n}\delta,u^{*}+t_{n}h)-A(y_{n},u^{*}+t_{n}h)}{t_{n}}.

Due to
3.1
(i)
,
the last addend can be bounded by
L_{A}\lVert(y_{n}-y^{*})/t_{n}-\delta\rVert\to 0
.
Thus,
the directional differentiability of
A
implies

\zeta_{n}\to R(\delta-k)-A^{\prime}(y^{*},u^{*},\delta,h)=0
.
Next,
we note that the definition of
\zeta_{n}
yields

0\in t_{n}\zeta_{n}+A(y_{n},u^{*}+t_{n}h)+B(y_{n},u^{*}+t_{n}h).

Due to
Lemma

3.7
,
we can apply
Theorem

3.2

for
n
large enough.
This yields the existence
of a unique solution
\tilde{y}_{n}\in B_{\varepsilon}(y^{*})

of

0\in A(\tilde{y}_{n},u^{*}+t_{n}h)+B(\tilde{y}_{n},u^{*}+t_{n}h)

and this solution satisfies
\lVert y_{n}-\tilde{y}_{n}\rVert\leq C\lVert t_{n}\zeta_{n}\rVert
.
Note that
\tilde{y}_{n}=S(u^{*}+t_{n}h)
.
Hence,

\frac{S(u^{*}+t_{n}h)-S(u^{*})}{t_{n}}=\frac{\tilde{y}_{n}-y_{n}}{t_{n}}+\frac{y_{n}-y^{*}}{t_{n}}\to 0+\delta.

This shows the claim.

We mention that a similar result
has been given in

[
1
, Theorem 1]
.
Therein, global assumptions on
A
are used,
i.e.,

3.1
(i)

is required to hold for
\varepsilon=\infty
.
Moreover,
this contribution
uses the concept of proto-differentiability,
which is not utilized in our proof.
In particular, instead of
3.8
(ii)
,

[
1
]

requires that
B
is proto-differentiable
with a maximally monotone proto-derivative.

In our opinion,
it is often easier and more natural
to study the differentiability properties of
J_{B}

instead of checking whether
B
is proto-differentiable.
Indeed,
in the case that
B
is the normal cone mapping
of a polyhedric set
C\subset Y
,
the directional differentiability
of
J_{B}=\operatorname{Proj}_{C}

was already shown in
[
16
,
13
]
,
whereas the proto-differentiability
of
B
was proved later in

[
11
, Example 4.6]
,
see also

[
14
]
.
Moreover,
the former proofs are rather elementary,
whereas the latter proof
utilizes
[
11
, Theorem 3.9]

which is based on Attouch’s theorem
linking the Mosco-convergence of convex functions
with the graphical convergence of their subdifferentials.

Remark 3.16
.

We comment on some extensions and limitations of
Theorem

3.14
.

(i)

The strong monotonicity of
A
can be replaced
by requiring
that the linearized equation (
9
)
possesses solutions for all
h\in U

and
by assuming that
the assertion of
Theorem

3.2

holds.

(ii)

It is not possible to adapt the proof to the situation of
Remark

3.11
.
Indeed, if we only assume weak directional differentiability of
J_{B}
,
we only get
(y_{n}-y^{*})/t_{n}\rightharpoonup\delta

and, thus,
only
\zeta_{n}\rightharpoonup 0

(if
A
is Bouligand differentiable).
This, however, is not enough
to obtain
\lVert y_{n}-\tilde{y}_{n}\rVert=\mathpzc{o}(t_{n})

in the last step of the proof.

(iii)

Another approach for proving
Theorem

3.14

is to directly consider
\tilde{y}_{n}:=S(u^{*}+t_{n}h)
.
Due to the Lipschitz continuity of
S
,
one gets
(\tilde{y}_{n}-y^{*})/t_{n}\rightharpoonup\delta
along a subsequence
for some
\delta\in Y
.
The next step would be to perform a Taylor expansion of

A(\tilde{y}_{n},u^{*}+t_{n}h)-A(y^{*},u^{*})
,
but this needs stronger differentiability assumptions for
A
,
e.g., Bouligand/Fréchet differentiability w.r.t. its first argument,
cf.
[
9
, Remark 2.3(iii), Theorem 2.14]
.
In the above proof, this is avoided via the construction of
y_{n}
.

Finally,
we mention that the directional differentiability of

J_{\rho_{0}B}
for some
\rho_{0}>0

implies the directional differentiability
for all
\rho>0
.

Corollary 3.17
.

Let
B\colon Y\times\mathcal{U}\rightrightarrows Y^{\star}
as in

3.1
(ii)

be given and fix
(q^{*},u^{*})\in Y\times\mathcal{U}
.
With
y^{*}=J_{B}(q^{*},u^{*})
,
the following are equivalent.

(i)

There exists
\rho_{0}>0
such that
the resolvent

J_{\rho_{0}B}
is directionally
differentiable at
(y^{*}+\rho_{0}(q^{*}-y^{*}),u^{*})
.

(ii)

For all
\rho>0
,

J_{\rho B}
is directionally
differentiable at
(y^{*}+\rho(q^{*}-y^{*}),u^{*})
.

Proof 3.18
.

This follows from
Theorem

3.14
,
since
J_{\rho B}\colon(x,u)\mapsto y

is the solution map of

0\in\frac{\rho_{0}}{\rho}R(y-x)+\rho_{0}B(y,u)

and

y^{*}=J_{\rho B}(y^{*}+\rho(q^{*}-y^{*}),u^{*})
.

Similarly,
an application of
Theorem

3.14
shows
that the directional differentiability of the resolvent
J_{B}

is actually independent of the Riesz isomorphism
R
of
Y

and, thus, independent of the inner product in
Y
.

3.3
Relation to proto-differentiability

The purpose of this section is to shed some light
on the relation of directional differentiability of the resolvent
J_{B}

and
the proto-differentiability of
B
.

We first fix the notion of proto-differentiability
of the parametrized set-valued map
B
.

Definition 3.19
.

Let
(y^{*},u^{*})\in Y\times U
be given,
such that
3.1
(ii)

is satisfied.
For some
\xi^{*}\in B(y^{*},u^{*})

and
(\delta,h)\in Y\times U
,
we define

\Delta_{t}B(y^{*},u^{*}\mathbin{|}\xi^{*})(\delta,h):=\frac{B(y^{*}+t\delta,u^{*}+th)-\xi^{*}}{t}

(10)

for
t>0
small enough.
We say that

B
is proto-differentiable at
(y^{*},u^{*})

relative to
\xi^{*}\in B(y^{*},u^{*})

if the graph of

\Delta_{t}B(y^{*},u^{*}\mathbin{|}\xi^{*})(\cdot,h)\colon Y\rightrightarrows Y^{\star}

converges
as
t\searrow 0

in the sense of Painlevé–Kuratowski,
see
[
1
, Definition 2]
,
for all
h\in U
.
In this case,
we define its proto-derivative

DB(y^{*},u^{*}\mathbin{|}\xi^{*})\colon Y\times U\rightrightarrows Y^{\star}

via

\operatorname{graph}DB(y^{*},u^{*}\mathbin{|}\xi^{*})(\cdot,h):=\operatorname{graph~lim}_{t\searrow 0}\Delta_{t}B(y^{*},u^{*}\mathbin{|}\xi^{*})(\cdot,h)

for all
h\in U
.

Although the operator

\Delta_{t}B(y^{*},u^{*}\mathbin{|}\xi^{*})(\cdot,h)

defined in (
10
)
is maximally monotone,
cf.
[
1
, Lemma 1]
,
its graphical limit

DB(y^{*},u^{*}\mathbin{|}\xi^{*})(\cdot,h)

might
fail to be maximally monotone,
even if it exists,
see

[
21
, Theorem 2]
.

Lemma 3.20
.

Let
(y^{*},u^{*})\in Y\times U
be given,
such that
3.1
(ii)

is satisfied.
Further, let
q^{*}\in Y

be given such that
y^{*}=J_{B}(q^{*},u^{*})

and we set

\xi^{*}:=R(q^{*}-y^{*})\in B(y^{*},u^{*})
.
Then, the following are equivalent.

(i)

The mapping
J_{B}

is directionally differentiable at
(q^{*},u^{*})
.

(ii)

The mapping
B
is proto-differentiable
at
(y^{*},u^{*})
relative to
\xi^{*}

and
its proto-derivative

DB(y^{*},u^{*}\mathbin{|}\xi^{*})(\cdot,h)

is maximally monotone for all
h\in U
.

Proof 3.21
.

By defining

A(y,u):=R(y-q^{*})

it can be easily checked that
(y^{*},u^{*})
is a solution of (
1
)
and that
3.8
is satisfied.

In order to apply the results from
[
1
]
,
we fix
h\in U
and
t_{0}>0
such that
u^{*}+th\in\mathcal{U}
for all
t\in[0,t_{0})
.
We define

\tilde{B}_{h}(t,y):=B(y,u^{*}+th)\qquad\forall t\in[0,t_{0}),y\in Y.

Now, it can be checked that

\Delta_{t}B(y^{*},u^{*}\mathrel{|}\xi^{*})(\cdot,h)

coincides with

\Delta_{t}\tilde{B}_{h}(y^{*}\mathrel{|}\xi^{*})(\cdot)

as defined in
[
1
, (9)]
.
Thus,

B
is proto-differentiable at
(y^{*},u^{*})

relative to
\xi^{*}

if and only if

\tilde{B}_{h}
is proto-differentiable at
y^{*}

relative to
\xi^{*}

for all
h\in U

and we have the formula

DB(y^{*},u^{*}\mathbin{|}\xi^{*})(\cdot,h)=D\tilde{B}_{h}(y^{*}\mathbin{|}\xi^{*})(\cdot)

for the corresponding proto-derivatives.

“
\Rightarrow
”:
Due to
J_{B}(q,u^{*}+th)=J_{\tilde{B}_{h}}(t,q)
,
we get that
J_{\tilde{B}_{h}}

is directionally differentiable.
Further,
J_{\tilde{B}_{h}}(t,q)\in Y

is Lipschitz continuous w.r.t.
q\in Y

and, thus,
we get the semi-differentiability
(see
[
1
, Definition 1]
)
of

J_{\tilde{B}_{h}}

at

q^{*}
.
Now, we can apply

[
1
, Remark 5]

to obtain that

J_{\tilde{B}_{h}}
is proto-differentiable
at
q^{*}

relative to
y^{*}=J_{\tilde{B}_{h}}(0,q^{*})
.
Now,

[
1
, Lemma 2]

yields that

\tilde{B}_{h}

is proto-differentiable
at

y^{*}

relative to

\xi^{*}
.
As explained above,
this yields the desired proto-differentiability of
B
.
Moreover,
from these arguments, we can distill the formula

J_{B}^{\prime}(q^{*},u^{*};\cdot,h)=J_{\tilde{B}_{h}}^{\prime}(q^{*};\cdot)=DJ_{\tilde{B}_{h}}(q^{*}\mathbin{|}y^{*})=(R+D\tilde{B}_{h}(y^{*}\mathbin{|}\xi^{*}))^{-1}=(R+DB(y^{*},u^{*}\mathbin{|}\xi^{*})(\cdot,h))^{-1}.

Since a maximal monotone mapping
is uniquely determined
by its resolvent,
this shows that

DB(y^{*},u^{*}\mathbin{|}\xi^{*})

coincides with the mapping
defined in
Lemma

3.9

and, therefore, is maximally monotone.

“
\Leftarrow
”:
Using similar arguments as above,
this follows from

[
1
, Theorem 2]
.

In particular,
the normal cone mapping to a polyhedric set
is proto-differentiable
and the proto-derivative is given
as in
Proposition

3.12
.
As already mentioned, this result is known from

[
11
,
14
]
.

4
Quasi-generalized equations

In this section,
we treat the generalization

0\in A(y,u)+B(y-\Phi(y,u),u)

(
3
)

of (
1
).
Here,
\Phi\colon Y\times U\to Y
is an additional mapping.
We will follow two approaches
to investigate the solution mapping of (
3
).
In the first approach,
we reformulate (
3
) in the form (
1
)
by introducing a new variable
z=y-\Phi(y,u)
.
This idea was successfully
used in
[
20
]

to prove the directional differentiability
of QVIs.
The second approach,
which was pioneered in
[
2
]
,
uses a iteration approach,
i.e., it builds a sequence
(y_{u,n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
,
in which
y_{u,n}
solves

0\in A(y_{u,n},u)+B(y_{u,n}-\Phi(y_{u,n-1},u),u).

We shall see that both approaches will use a similar set of assumptions.

Assumption 4.1
.

We assume that
(y^{*},u^{*})\in Y\times U
is a solution of (
3
)
such that the operators
A
and
B
satisfy
3.1
.
In addition,

\Phi\colon Y\times U\to Y
is continuous at
(y^{*},u^{*})

and

\Phi(\cdot,u)
is locally a uniform contraction,
i.e., there exists a Lipschitz constant
L_{\Phi}\in[0,1)

such that

\lVert\Phi(y_{2},u)-\Phi(y_{1},u)\rVert\leq L_{\Phi}\lVert y_{2}-y_{1}\rVert

for all
y_{1},y_{2}\in B_{\varepsilon}(y^{*})
and for all
u\in\mathcal{U}
.

Assumption 4.2
.

In addition to
4.1
, we require

(a)

L_{\Phi}<\gamma_{A}^{-1}
,
or

(b)

L_{\Phi}<2\sqrt{\gamma_{A}}/(1+\gamma_{A})

and
there exists a function
g\colon U_{\varepsilon}(y^{*})\times\mathcal{U}\to\mathbb{R}

such that

A
is the Fréchet derivative of
g

w.r.t. the first variable,

where
\gamma_{A}=L_{A}/\mu_{A}\geq 1

is the local condition number of
A
.

Assumption 4.3
.

In addition to
4.1
, we assume
that

(i)

A
is directionally differentiable at
(y^{*},u^{*})
.

(ii)

J_{B}
is directionally differentiable at
(q^{*}-\phi^{*},u^{*})

with

q^{*}=y^{*}-R^{-1}A(y^{*},u^{*})

and

\phi^{*}=\Phi(y^{*},u^{*})
.

(iii)

\Phi
is directionally differentiable at
(y^{*},u^{*})
.

As a preparation,
we state a consequence of
4.2
.

Lemma 4.4
.

Let
4.2
be satisfied.
Then, there exist constants
C>0

and
\tilde{c}\in(0,1)
,
such that for all

u\in\mathcal{U}
and

y_{1},y_{2},z_{1},z_{2}\in B_{\varepsilon}(y^{*})

we have

\langle A(z_{2},u)-A(z_{1},u),z_{2}-\Phi(y_{2},u)-z_{1}+\Phi(y_{1},u)\rangle\geq C\left(\lVert z_{2}-z_{1}\rVert^{2}-\tilde{c}^{2}\lVert y_{2}-y_{1}\rVert^{2}\right).

Proof 4.5
.

We denote the left-hand side of the inequality by
M\in\mathbb{R}
.

In case that
4.2
(a)

holds,
we have

\displaystyle M\geq\mu_{A}\lVert z_{2}-z_{1}\rVert^{2}-L_{A}L_{\Phi}\lVert z_{2}-z_{1}\rVert\lVert y_{2}-y_{1}\rVert\geq\frac{\mu_{A}}{2}\lVert z_{2}-z_{1}\rVert^{2}-\frac{L_{A}^{2}L_{\Phi}^{2}}{2\mu_{A}}\lVert y_{2}-y_{1}\rVert^{2},

i.e., we can use
C=\mu_{A}/2

and
\tilde{c}=L_{A}L_{\Phi}/\mu_{A}=\gamma_{A}L_{\Phi}<1
.

Under

4.2
(b)
, we
first consider
z_{1},z_{2}\in U_{\varepsilon}(y^{*})
.

Theorem

2.2
yields

\displaystyle M

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\mu_{A}L_{A}}{\mu_{A}+L_{A}}\lVert z_{2}-z_{1}\rVert^{2}+\frac{1}{\mu_{A}+L_{A}}\lVert A(z_{2},u)-A(z_{1},u)\rVert^{2}-L_{\Phi}\lVert A(z_{2},u)-A(z_{1},u)\rVert\lVert y_{2}-y_{1}\rVert

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\mu_{A}L_{A}}{\mu_{A}+L_{A}}\lVert z_{2}-z_{1}\rVert^{2}-\frac{(\mu_{A}+L_{A})L_{\Phi}^{2}}{4}\lVert y_{2}-y_{1}\rVert^{2}.

Here, we have used Young’s inequality.
Since everything is continuous w.r.t.
z_{1},z_{2}\in Y
,
the inequality carries over to
z_{1},z_{2}\in B_{\varepsilon}(y^{*})
.
Now,
we can choose

C=\mu_{A}L_{A}/(\mu_{A}+L_{A})

and

\tilde{c}=(\mu_{A}+L_{A})L_{\Phi}/(2\sqrt{\mu_{A}L_{A}})=(1+\gamma_{A})L_{\Phi}/(2\sqrt{\gamma_{A}})<1

0\in A^{\prime}(y^{*},u^{*};\delta_{n},h)+DB(y^{*}-\phi^{*},u^{*}\mathbin{|}\xi^{*})(\delta_{n}-\Phi^{\prime}(y^{*},u^{*};\delta_{n-1},h),h).

(20)

We argue by induction over
n
.
The base case
n=0
is clear since
y_{t,0}=y^{*}=y_{0}
.
Assume that the assertion holds for
n-1
.
We abbreviate

\phi_{t,n}:=\Phi(y_{t,n},u^{*}+th)
.
Using the operators from (
15
),
we can recast the equation for
y_{t,n}
as

0\in\mathcal{A}(y_{t,n},(\phi_{t,n-1},u^{*}+th))+\mathcal{B}(y_{t,n},(\phi_{t,n-1},u^{*}+th)).

Next, we apply
Lemma

4.19
for
t>0
small enough (depending on
n
)
with
\phi:=\phi^{*}+t\psi_{t,n}
,
\psi_{t,n}=\Phi^{\prime}(y^{*},u^{*};\delta_{n-1},h)

to obtain a solution

\tilde{y}_{t,n}\in B_{\varepsilon}(y^{*})
of

0\in A(\tilde{y}_{t,n},u^{*}+th)+B(\tilde{y}_{t,n}-\phi^{*}-t\Phi^{\prime}(y^{*},u^{*};\delta_{n-1},h),u^{*}+th)

or, equivalently,

0\in\mathcal{A}(\tilde{y}_{t,n},(\phi^{*}+t\psi_{t,n},u^{*}+th))+\mathcal{B}(\tilde{y}_{t,n},(\phi^{*}+t\psi_{t,n},u^{*}+th)).

Now, we are in position to apply
Theorem

3.14

and this yields

(\tilde{y}_{t,n}-y^{*})/t\to\delta_{n}

in
Y
as
t\searrow 0
,
where

\delta_{n}\in Y
solves

0\in\mathcal{A}^{\prime}(y^{*},(\phi^{*},u^{*});\delta_{n},(\psi_{t,n},h))+D\mathcal{B}(y^{*},(\phi^{*},u^{*});\delta_{n},(\psi_{t,n},h)).

Using
Lemma

3.9
and (
16
),
we can relate
DB
and
D\mathcal{B}
as follows

\displaystyle D\mathcal{B}(y^{*},(\phi^{*},u^{*})\mathbin{|}\xi^{*})(\delta,(\psi,h))

\displaystyle\qquad=\big\{R(k-\delta)\nonscript\;\big|\nonscript\;\mathopen{}k\in Y,\delta=J_{\mathcal{B}}^{\prime}(q^{*},(\phi^{*},u^{*});k,(\psi,h))\big\}

\displaystyle\qquad=\big\{R(k-(\delta-\psi))\nonscript\;\big|\nonscript\;\mathopen{}k\in Y,\delta-\psi=J_{B}^{\prime}(q^{*}-\phi^{*},u^{*};k-\psi,h)\big\}

\displaystyle\qquad=DB(y^{*}-\phi^{*},u^{*}\mathbin{|}\xi^{*})(\delta-\psi,h).

This results in the equation (
20
).
By using the equations satisfied by
y_{t,n}

and
\tilde{y}_{t,n}
,
we get

\displaystyle\langle A(\tilde{y}_{t,n},u^{*}+th)-A(y_{t,n},u^{*}+th),\tilde{y}_{t,n}-y_{t,n}\rangle

\displaystyle\quad\leq\langle A(\tilde{y}_{t,n},u^{*}+th)-A(y_{t,n},u^{*}+th),\phi^{*}+t\Phi^{\prime}(y^{*},u^{*};\delta_{n-1},h)-\Phi(y_{t,n-1},u^{*}+th)\rangle.

Consequently,

\frac{1}{t}\lVert\tilde{y}_{t,n}-y_{t,n}\rVert\leq\frac{L_{A}}{\mu_{A}t}\lVert\phi^{*}+t\Phi^{\prime}(y^{*},u^{*};\delta_{n-1},h)-\Phi(y_{t,n-1},u^{*}+th)\rVert\to 0\quad\text{as }t\searrow 0.

In combination with
(\tilde{y}_{t,n}-y^{*})/t\to\delta_{n}
,
this yields the first convergence in (
19
).
The second convergence in (
19
) follows
since
\Phi
is directionally differentiable and Lipschitz w.r.t. its first argument.
Consequently,
(
19
) holds for all
n\geq 0
.

Thus, we have shown

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{y_{t,n}-y^{*}}{t}=\frac{y_{t}-y^{*}}{t}\quad\text{and}\quad\lim_{t\searrow 0}\frac{y_{t,n}-y^{*}}{t}=\delta_{n},

(21)

where both limits exist (strongly) in
Y
.
Moreover,

\left\lVert\frac{y_{t,n}-y^{*}}{t}-\frac{y_{t}-y^{*}}{t}\right\rVert=\left\lVert\frac{y_{t,n}-y_{t}}{t}\right\rVert\leq C\tilde{c}^{n},

cf. (
18
).
This shows that the limit
n\to\infty
in (
21
) is uniform in
t\in(0,t_{0}]
.
Hence, the classical theorem on the existence and equality of iterated limits
ensures

\delta:=\lim_{t\searrow 0}\frac{y_{t}-y^{*}}{t}=\lim_{t\searrow 0}\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{y_{t,n}-y^{*}}{t}=\lim_{n\to\infty}\lim_{t\searrow 0}\frac{y_{t,n}-y^{*}}{t}=\lim_{n\to\infty}\delta_{n}\quad\text{in }Y.

Finally, passing to the limit
n\to\infty
in (
20
)
yields
the equation for
\delta
.

Note that

4.2
is only used in
Lemma

4.23
.
It can be replaced by requiring that
the map

T_{u}\colon B_{\lambda}(y^{*})\to B_{\varepsilon}(y^{*})

is a contraction uniformly in
u\in\mathcal{U}
.

It is quite interesting to see that the approaches from

Sections

4.1
and
4.2

use the same assumptions
and,
actually,

Theorems

4.17
and
4.27

coincide.
If we look a little bit more carefully,
we see that in
Section

4.1
,

Lemma

4.4

is only applied in the special case
y_{i}=z_{i}
,
i=1,2
,
see
Lemmas

4.6
and
4.11
,
whereas
Section

4.2

requires the application in the general case

y_{i}\neq z_{i}
,
i=1,2
,
see
Lemma

4.23
.

Altogether,
it seems to be possible to craft special situations
in which only one of the approaches of

Sections

4.1
and
4.2

is applicable.
However,
we think that
(up to some exceptional boundary cases)
the ranges of applicability
of both approaches coincide.

5
Applications

5.1
Optimization with a parameter-dependent sparsity functional

As a first application,
we
consider
the minimization problem

\text{Minimize}\quad F(y)+G(y,u)\quad\text{w.r.t.\ }y\in Y

(
P(u)
)

with a parameter
u\in U
.
Here,

(\Omega,\Sigma,\mu)

is a measure space
and

Y=U=L^{2}(\mu)
.
Moreover,

F\colon Y\to\mathbb{R}

is a given functional
and

G\colon Y\times U\to\mathbb{R}

is defined via

G(y,u):=\int_{\Omega}\lvert uy\rvert\,\mathrm{d}\mu.

Thus,
(

P

(
u
)

)
models, e.g., optimal control problems
which include a sparsity functional
and we are interested
in the sensitivity of the solution
y

w.r.t. the distributed sparsity parameter
u
.

Suppose that
u^{*}\in U
is fixed
and
y^{*}\in Y
is a local minimizer
of
(
P(u^{*})
)
.
We assume that
F

is Fréchet differentiable
in
B_{\varepsilon}(y^{*})
for some
\varepsilon>0
,
such that its derivative
F^{\prime}

is (uniformly) strongly monotone and Lipschitz continuous
on
B_{\varepsilon}(y^{*})
,
see
3.1
(i)
.

As it is usually done,
we identify the dual space of
Y=L^{2}(\mu)

with itself.

Due to the convexity of
F
,
a point
y\in Y
with
\lVert y-y^{*}\rVert<\varepsilon

is a local minimizer of (

P

(
u
)

)
with
u\in U

if and only if

0\in F^{\prime}(y)+\partial_{y}G(y,u),

where

\partial_{y}G(y,u)=\left\{g\in L^{2}(\Omega)\nonscript\;\middle|\nonscript\;\mathopen{}G(v,u)\geq G(y,u)+\int_{\Omega}g(v-y)\,\mathrm{d}\mu\quad\forall v\in Y\right\}

is the subdifferential of
G
w.r.t.
y
.
It is clear that
3.1

is satisfied with the setting

A(y,u):=F^{\prime}(y),\qquad B(y,u):=\partial_{y}G(y,u).

In order to apply the results from
Section

3
,
we have to study the properties of the resolvent
J_{\rho B}
,
\rho>0
.
It is clear that

J_{\rho B}(q,u)=\operatorname{prox}_{\rho G(\cdot,u)}(q)=\argmin_{v\in Y}\int_{\Omega}\frac{1}{2}(v-q)^{2}+\rho\lvert uv\rvert\,\mathrm{d}\mu.

Now, a pointwise discussion shows that the resolvent
can be computed pointwise and is given by a soft-shrinkage with parameter
\rho\lvert u\rvert
,
i.e.,

J_{\rho B}(q,u)(x)=\operatorname{shrink}_{\rho\lvert u(x)\rvert}(q(x)):=\max(\lvert q(x)\rvert-\rho\lvert u(x)\rvert,0)\operatorname{sign}(q(x)).

Now, since

\mathbb{R}^{2}\ni(q,u)\mapsto\operatorname{shrink}_{\rho\lvert u\rvert}(q)=\max(\lvert q\rvert-\rho\lvert u\rvert,0)\operatorname{sign}(q)\in\mathbb{R}

is Lipschitz continuous and directionally differentiable,
it is easy to check that also
the associated Nemytskii operator

J_{\rho B}\colon Y\times U\to Y

is Lipschitz continuous and directionally differentiable.
If, additionally,

F^{\prime}\colon Y\to Y

is directionally differentiable,
we are in position to apply
Theorem

3.14

to obtain the directional differentiability
of the (local) solution mapping of (

P

(
u
)

).
Using
Lemma

3.9
,
it is also possible to characterize the directional derivative.

5.2
Quasi-linear QVIs

We demonstrate
the applicability of our results to
a QVI governed by a quasi-linear operator.
To this end,
let

\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be open and bounded
and
with

Y=H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)

and

U=L^{2}(\Omega)

we define the
quasi-linear operator

A\colon Y\times U\to Y^{\star}

via

A(y,u):=-\operatorname{div}g(\nabla y,u)+f(u),\quad\text{i.e.}\quad\langle A(y,u),v\rangle=\int_{\Omega}g(\nabla y,u)\nabla v+f(u)v\,\mathrm{d}x,

where

g\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}

is (uniformly) strongly monotone
w.r.t. its first argument;
and Lipschitz continuous and differentiable on
\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}
.
Moreover,

f\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is differentiable and Lipschitz continuous.
These conditions imply that
A
satisfies
3.1

globally on
Y
.
Moreover,
using the dominated convergence theorem,
we can check that
A
is directionally differentiable
with

\langle A^{\prime}(y,u;\delta,h),v\rangle=\int_{\Omega}\left(g^{\prime}_{y}(\nabla y,u)\nabla\delta+g^{\prime}_{u}(\nabla y,u)h\right)\nabla v+f^{\prime}(u)hv\,\mathrm{d}x,

see also
[
12
, Theorem 8]
.

To define the operator
B
,
let

K\subset Y

be given
such that
\operatorname{Proj}_{K}
is directionally differentiable,
e.g., we could choose a polyhedric
K
.
We set

B(\cdot):=N_{K}(\cdot)
,
where
N_{K}
is the normal cone mapping of
K
.
Note that
B
is independent of the variable
u
.

With this setting,
3.1
and
3.8
are satisfied.
Next, we choose
\Phi\colon Y\times U\to Y

such that
there exists a Lipschitz constant
L_{\Phi}\in[0,1)

with

\lVert\Phi(y_{2},u)-\Phi(y_{1},u)\rVert\leq L_{\Phi}\lVert y_{2}-y_{1}\rVert

for all
y_{1},y_{2}\in Y
and all
u\in U
.
Further, we suppose that
\Phi
is directionally differentiable
and that
4.2
concerning the smallness of
L_{\Phi}
is satisfied.

Since our assumptions on
A
,
B
and
\Phi
are global,
we obtain that for all
u\in U
,
there exists a unique solution
y\in Y

of
the QVI

0\in A(y,u)+B(y-\Phi(y,u)),

cf.
[
20
, Section 3]
.
Since all the assumptions from
Section

4

are satisfied,
our differentiability theorems
imply
that the mapping
y\mapsto u

is directionally differentiable.
For a fixed parameter
u^{*}\in U

we denote the solution by
y^{*}\in Y
.
Then,
the directional derivative
\delta\in Y

in the direction
h\in U

is given by the solution of

0\in A^{\prime}(y^{*},u^{*};\delta,h)+DB(y^{*}-\Phi(y^{*},u^{*})\mathbin{|}\xi^{*})(\delta-\Phi^{\prime}(y^{*},u^{*};\delta,h)),

where

\xi^{*}=-A(y^{*},u^{*})
.
We mention that in the particular case that the set
K
is polyhedric,
the set-valued mapping

DB(y^{*}-\Phi(y^{*},u^{*})\mathbin{|}\xi^{*})

coincides with the normal cone mapping of the critical cone

\mathcal{K}=T_{K}(y^{*})\cap(\xi^{*})^{\perp}
,
see
Proposition

3.12
.

It is also clear that the above assumptions and arguments
can be localized if we already have a solution
y^{*}

of the QVI
corresponding to the parameter
u^{*}
.
