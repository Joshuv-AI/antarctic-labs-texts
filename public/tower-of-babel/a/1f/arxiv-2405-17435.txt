Data Authorisation and Validation in Autonomous Vehicles: A Critical Review
Reem Alhabib, Poonam Yadav

Abstract
Autonomous systems are becoming increasingly prevalent in new vehicles. Due to their environmental friendliness and their remarkable capability to significantly enhance road safety, these vehicles have gained widespread recognition and acceptance in recent years. Automated Driving Systems (ADS) are intricate systems that incorporate a multitude of sensors and actuators to interact with the environment autonomously, pervasively, and interactively. Consequently, numerous studies are currently underway to keep abreast of these rapid developments. This paper aims to provide a comprehensive overview of recent advancements in ADS technologies. It provides in-depth insights into the detailed information about how data and information flow in the distributed system, including autonomous vehicles and other various supporting services and entities. Data validation and system requirements are emphasised, such as security, privacy, scalability, and data ownership, in accordance with regulatory standards. Finally, several current research directions in the AVs field will be discussed.



I
Introduction

An Automated Driving System (ADS) is an integrated system within vehicles that utilises various in-vehicle technologies and sensors to navigate autonomously from a starting point to a predefined destination. Due to its significant contribution to safety and overall economic growth, leading car companies have heavily invested in several Autonomous Vehicle (AV) technologies that are able to improve safety, efficiency, and cost savings. The technology is evolving through inter-industry alliances and technological contributions across the globe.

Famous technological firms and car manufacturers have recently invested billions of dollars in this major transformation of the automobile industry; several sources estimate that the self-driving car market share will take between 15 and 20 years to reach 25 percent globally
[
1
]
. In line with this trend and industrial development, governments worldwide are providing guidance that allows and encourages on-road Autonomous Vehicle (AV) trials. For example, the UK published a code of practice (2019) that specifies certain on-road trials
[
2

. Subsequently, in early 2022, the government changed the Highway Code to ensure the first self-driving vehicles are introduced safely on the roads

AVs have various control units that design a system to complete all driving tasks without human intervention. Cameras, GPS, and other sensors are connected to exchange data to facilitate independent driving decisions
[
4
]
. Due to dynamic road scenarios, data comes from external roadsides and other vehicles to the on-board sensors, which need to make continuous control decisions. In general, making automated driving systems trustworthy mainly centres around this halo of data. Thus, for the safety of the whole environment, this data must be preserved securely and not accessible to tampering. This is one of the most critical challenges due to the various networks and communications in this environment. In addition, in case of an accident, this data becomes significant to reconstruct the event for analysis and investigation.

This paper aims to provide an understanding of data/information flow in autonomous vehicles and their supporting services and infrastructure. Most current surveys investigate the ADS as a whole and explore the ADS techniques from the system level; with a study conducted on ADS that focuses on the data perspective. This detailed information provides a holistic view of the autonomous vehicle’s ecosystem, which helps design efficient and secure data storing and validation solutions. In this survey, the aim to explore several critical data aspects of Autonomous Vehicles (AV) by answering the following fundamental questions:

Since the invention of the automobile, there has been continuous innovation towards autonomous vehicles (AVs). Figure

Driving assistance systems (DAS) at all levels have been and continue to be the most critical component in AVs. The first generation of DAS utilised sensors that assessed a car’s internal condition. In the 1980s, Traction Control Systems (TCS) and Anti-lock Breaking Systems (ABS) were utilised for dynamic vehicle stabilization, as the early era of DAS. Following this, Electronic Stability Control (ESC) was introduced in 1995 for the same function. Additionally, in the early 1990s, sensors that were practically classified as exteroceptive were presented as the second generation. The most recent generation of DAS, also known as Advanced Driver Assist Systems (ADAS), has been developed to prevent collisions.

Autonomous refers to systems that are able to change their behavior in response to unanticipated events during operation
[
5
]
. The National Highway Traffic Safety Administration (NHTSA)
[
4
]
defines autonomous vehicles as "those vehicles in which at least aspects of a safety-critical control function (e.g., steering, throttle, or braking) occur without direct driver input". Vehicles, however, that provide safety warnings to drivers (e.g., forward crash signs) but do not perform a control function are not considered automated.

In September 2018, the NHTSA performed an extensive literature review of all the generic AV system features to identify the attributes that define the operational design domain (ODD). The comprehensive review resulted in 24 ADS features, specifically describing functionality and proposed timelines for commercial deployment across the different Society of Automotive Engineers (SAE) International levels of driving automation. Accordingly, the SAE’s six-level taxonomy has become the widespread industry standard
[
6
]
:

Level 1: Driver Assistance requires the driver to remain in control of the driving tasks, with the exception of certain driving modes where assistance tools may take over.

Level 2: "Partial Driving Automation" refers to a driving mode in which the vehicle system takes over certain driving functions, such as acceleration, while the driver remains responsible for monitoring the driving situation.

Level 3: Conditional Driving Automation. While the vehicle system is responsible for some driving modes and monitoring the entire environment, at this level, the driver must be prepared to take control at all times and be responsive to alerts.

Level 4: High Driving Automation. While the vehicle system is designed to perform all driving tasks for specific dynamic driving tasks, the driver may take control.

Level 5: "Full driving automation" refers to a driving mode where the vehicle system undertakes all driving tasks in all conditions, thereby relieving the driver of any responsibility to monitor the environment or take control of the vehicle.

Sensors Types

AV systems, while having some variations, all consist of two primary types of sensors based on their operational principle: Exteroceptive and Proprioceptive. Exteroceptive sensors are external state sensors that are utilised to perceive the environment, such as calculating the distance to objects or light intensity from the surroundings of the system. Examples of these sensors include cameras, radio detection and ranging (RADAR), etc. Proprioceptive sensors, on the other hand, are internal state sensors that capture the dynamic state and measure the internal values of a dynamic system. Examples of this type of sensor include Global Positioning Systems (GPS), encoders, accelerometers, etc.
[

,

Sensor Fusion

The advantage of this operation is to combine the data originating from different sources to complete each other’s functions in order to provide an improved outcome in some specific criteria and data aspects for decision tasks

VI
Conclusion and Future work

In light of the emergence of the 4.0 industry, self-driving cars are considered at the forefront of this revolution. On one side, it provides numerous advantages regarding the environment and road safety. However, very few people would still have enough trust to use those vehicles.
This massive amount of data is both extremely important and dangerous,
making it extremely delicate to use at this stage.
On the one hand, it can be used to extract a wealth of valuable information in order to improve the transportation system and increase safety. Comparatively, if not properly preserved, it may cost us our lives. Beginning with remote-control door locks, hackers may load firmware without authorisation into major ECUs and progress to sensor-based manipulation of cooperative adaptive cruise control (CACC). In addition, user data could be collected specific way, as the GM collects location data and radio listening routines from its users
[
115
]
. Due to the open nature of the used networks, safety, security, and privacy become vital issues

The variety of functions will continue to grow leading to an increase in new vulnerabilities. Adopting Cloud technology provides various applications and techniques; however, with more concerns regarding security and privacy. Besides the vast research studies presented in the field of autonomous systems of security and privacy, ADS are still not completely robust and secure.

The AV’s sensors are able to capture massive amounts of Real-time data in milliseconds and apply a deep learning algorithm to recognise other objects on the road and transport passengers safely. The problem of scale, though, becomes a challenge with this big data, as it is important to determine how the data will be effectively and safely managed, validated, and shared with the other subjects in the environment. In addition, once it is transferred, validation, and mining of useful information become another challenge.
The literature shows that in case of an accident, there are no data collection and analysis protocols where the investigators and regulators are able to access and analyse ADS data. Current EDRs, even the modern version, lack essential data in the investigation and analysis stage. Several parameters regarding to the system performance and the diver’s behaviour during the accident are not the only important data.

These vehicles have integrated hardware and software that can provide much more than the data captured by event data recorders. For example, cameras, alongside lidars raw and fusion data can add a lot of clarification of the environment at the time of the accident. Furthermore, the communication data with other vehicles and RDU, in addition to their sensors’ data could support the accident evidence in the investigator’s view. In addition, data such as updates, fault alerts, periodic maintenance, and insurance are all significant data to obtain before the process of analysis and investigation by regulators and analysts.

It is not only desirable, but also necessary, to make this data available, secure, and accessible.
