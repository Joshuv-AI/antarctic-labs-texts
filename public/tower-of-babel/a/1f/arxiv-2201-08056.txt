Multivariable evolution in final state parton shower algorithms Preprint: DESY-22-009
Zoltán Nagy, Davison E. Soper

Abstract
One can use more than one scale variable to specify the family of surfaces in the space of parton splitting parameters that define the evolution of a parton shower. Considering e + ​ e − e^{+}e^{-} annihilation, we use two variables, with shower evolution following a special path in this two dimensional space. In addition, we treat in a special way the part of the splitting function that has a soft emission singularity but no collinear singularity. This leads to certain advantages compared to the usual shower formulation with only one scale variable.



I
Introduction

In a parton shower event generator, one can view the parton state as evolving according to an operator based renormalization group equation. Starting with a state with just a few partons, the shower evolves as a scale
\mu_{\textsc{s}}
changes from a large value
\mu_{\textsc{h}}
characteristic of the hard scattering state at the start of the shower to a low value
\mu_{\rm f}
on the order of
1\ \mathrm{GeV}
. As the shower evolves, more and more partons are emitted. The function of the shower scale
\mu_{\textsc{s}}
is to divide possible parton splittings into
resolvable
splittings, with scales
\mu>\mu_{\textsc{s}}
, and
unresolvable
splittings, with scales
\mu<\mu_{\textsc{s}}
. There is substantial freedom to choose exactly what this means. The space of possible splittings is divided into the resolvable and unresolvable regions by a surface labelled by
\mu_{\textsc{s}}
. Many different choices are possible for defining this surface. For instance, one can use a measure of the transverse momentum in the splitting to define the surface or one can use a measure of the virtuality in the splitting.

In this paper, we explore the possibility of using more than one variable to define a family of surfaces. Instead of one
\mu_{\textsc{s}}
, we use
\vec{\mu}=(\mu_{1},\mu_{2},\dots)
. Then evolution means moving from large values of the component scales
\mu_{n}
to small values along a path
\vec{\mu}(t)
with
0<t<\infty
. Defining this path is then part of defining the shower algorithm.

There is an additional freedom available when multiple scales are involved. It may be possible to divide the shower splitting functions into separate terms such that one of the terms is not sensitive to one of the scales in the sense that no singularity is encountered when this scale approaches zero. When this happens, we can modify the definition of the unresolved region for this term in a way that makes this term exactly independent of this scale. This redefinition can simplify the shower evolution.

In this paper, we explore the additional freedom obtained by using two scales instead of one.

This general concept works for proton-proton,
e^{\pm}
-proton, and
e^{+}e^{-}
collisions. The simplest case is
e^{+}e^{-}
collisions, so we consider
e^{+}e^{-}
collisions in this paper, reserving cases involving incoming hadrons for future work.

II
General structure of unresolved regions

This paper generally concerns the definition of the unresolved region in the space of parton momenta in a parton shower. We will concentrate in the following sections on a single emission in a first order shower, but we begin with a discussion of the general case of a shower algorithm at an arbitrary order of perturbation theory. We use the general framework presented in Ref.
NSAllOrder
. This general framework allows for substantial freedom in choosing the functions that define a particular parton shower algorithm. We have developed particular realizations of these choices for a first order shower
NSI
. It remains an open problem to realize these choices for a parton shower with splitting functions beyond order
\alpha_{\mathrm{s}}

e^{+}e^{-}
collisions, but in this paper we restrict our analysis to
e^{+}e^{-}
annihilation so as to present the methods that we have in mind in the simplest possible context.

The final factor in Eq. (

\bm{V}_{\mathrm{L}}

The idea behind this is that a measurement using an infrared safe measurement algorithm (such as a jet algorithm) cannot distinguish between a single parton and a set partons, some of which are carry very small momenta and the others of which carry momenta that are very nearly collinear. One can then say that the difference between these two states is unresolvable. The parton shower version of an unresolvable region incorporates this idea without referring to any specific infrared safe observable. In designing a shower algorithm, there is then great freedom in choosing the unresolved region. We let the boundary of the unresolved region depend on one or more scale parameters
\mu_{\textsc{s},i}
such that increasing any of the
\mu_{\textsc{s},i}
makes the unresolved region larger and decreasing
\mu_{\textsc{s},i}

\{\{\hat{p}\}_{m+n_{\textsc{r}}},\{\ell\}_{n_{\textsc{v}}}\}
is in the unresolved region and equals 0 otherwise. The complement of the unresolved region is the resolved region, colored yellow in Fig.

Unresolved region with one scale

We now consider the unresolved region for a splitting in a first order shower in the standard case that there is a single shower scale
\mu_{\textsc{s}}
.

The operator
\mathcal{D}^{[1,0]}(\mu_{\textsc{s}})
contains an integration over splitting variables
(y,z,\phi)
with
(y,z)
integrated over the unresolved region defined by the scale
\mu_{\textsc{s}}
. The shower splitting operator is given by the first order version of Eq. (
12

\mathcal{S}^{[1,0]}(\mu_{\textsc{s}})=\frac{d}{d\mu_{\textsc{s}}}\,\mathcal{D}^{[1,0]}(\mu_{\textsc{s}})\;.

(34)

Integrating between a scale
\mu_{\textsc{s},1}
and a slightly smaller scale
\mu_{\textsc{s},2}
gives the exponent in Eq. (

\int_{\mu_{\textsc{s},2}}^{\mu_{\textsc{s},1}}\!d\mu_{\textsc{s}}\ \mathcal{S}^{[1,0]}(\mu_{\textsc{s}})=\mathcal{D}^{[1,0]}(\mu_{\textsc{s},1})-\mathcal{D}^{[1,0]}(\mu_{\textsc{s},2})\;.

(35)

Thus we integrate over the unresolved region for the larger scale omitting the unresolved region for the smaller scale.

VII
Evolution with two scales

The singular operator
\mathcal{D}(\vec{\mu})
has a perturbative expansion (
19
). The shower generator
\mathcal{S}_{j}(\vec{\mu})
is defined in Eq. (
12
). The index
j\in\{\mathrm{E},\mathrm{C}\}
includes two scale choices. The shower generator has a perturbative expansion

\mathcal{S}_{j}(\vec{\mu})=\frac{\alpha_{\mathrm{s}}}{2\pi}\,\mathcal{S}_{j}^{(1)}(\vec{\mu})+\mathcal{O}(\alpha_{\mathrm{s}}^{2})\;.

(49)

From Eq. (
12
), the first order contribution is

\mathcal{S}_{j}^{(1)}(\vec{\mu})=\frac{\partial}{\partial\mu_{j}}\,\mathcal{D}^{(1)}(\vec{\mu})\;.

(50)

In a first order shower, we truncate the expansion of
\mathcal{S}_{j}(\vec{\mu})
at first order,

\mathcal{S}_{j}(\vec{\mu})=\frac{\alpha_{\mathrm{s}}}{2\pi}\,\mathcal{S}_{j}^{(1)}(\vec{\mu})\;.

(51)

To obtain the shower evolution operator
\mathcal{U}(t_{2},t_{1})
following the chosen path
\vec{\mu}(t)
, we solve the differential equation Eq. (
13
). In general, this gives us the group multiplication property

\mathcal{U}(t_{2},t_{1})=\mathcal{U}(t_{2},\tau)\,\mathcal{U}(\tau,t_{1})\;.

(52)

Our path has two segments,
0<t<1
and
1<t<\infty
. This gives

\mathcal{U}(\infty,0)=\mathcal{U}(\infty,1)\,\mathcal{U}(1,0)\;.

(53)

We have divided
\mathcal{D}^{(1)}(\vec{\mu})
into a part
\mathcal{D}^{(1)}_{\mathrm{sc}}(\vec{\mu})
with both soft and collinear singularities and a part
\mathcal{D}^{(1)}_{\mathrm{soft}}(\vec{\mu})
with only soft singularities, as in Eq. (
33
). We recall that only
\mu_{\textsc{e}}
changes on the first segment of the path and this change affects only
\mathcal{D}^{(1)}_{\mathrm{soft}}(\vec{\mu})
. We also recall that only
\mu_{\textsc{c}}
changes on the second segment of the path and this change affects only
\mathcal{D}^{(1)}_{\mathrm{sc}}(\vec{\mu})
. Then

\mathcal{U}(1,0)=\mathbb{T}\exp\!\left\{-\int_{0}^{1}\!dt\,\frac{d\mu_{\textsc{e}}(t)}{dt}\,\mathcal{S}_{\textsc{e}}^{\mathrm{soft}}(\vec{\mu}(t))\right\}

(54)

and

\mathcal{U}(\infty,1)=\mathbb{T}\exp\!\left\{-\int_{1}^{\infty}\!dt\,\frac{d\mu_{\textsc{c}}(t)}{dt}\,\mathcal{S}_{\textsc{c}}^{\mathrm{sc}}(\vec{\mu}(t))\right\}.

(55)

In this formulation the parton shower, the result depends on what we choose for
\mathcal{D}^{(1)}_{\mathrm{soft}}(\vec{\mu})
and
\mathcal{D}^{(1)}_{\mathrm{sc}}(\vec{\mu})
. We can choose
\mathcal{D}^{(1)}_{\mathrm{soft}}(\vec{\mu})=0
. Then
\mathcal{D}^{(1)}_{\mathrm{sc}}(\vec{\mu})
is all of
\mathcal{D}^{(1)}(\vec{\mu})
. When
\mu_{\textsc{c}}=\mu_{\angle}
this gives us a simple angular ordered shower, as in Eq. (
44
) and Fig.
8
. The only difference is conceptual. First,
\mathcal{D}^{(1)}_{\mathrm{sc}}(\vec{\mu})
and its inverse
[\mathcal{D}^{(1)}_{\mathrm{sc}}(\vec{\mu})]^{-1}
are well defined at the hard scale. This is important because
[\mathcal{D}^{(1)}_{\mathrm{sc}}(\vec{\mu})]^{-1}
plays the role of removing infrared singularities from the hard scattering cross section calculated at next-to-leading order
NSAllOrder
. Second, with the two scale formulation, we could have eliminated the
m_{\perp}^{2}
cut. Then it would have been natural to choose a nonzero endpoint
\mu_{E,\mathrm{f}}^{2}\sim 1\ \mathrm{GeV}^{2}
for the evolution in
\mu_{\textsc{e}}
in the first segment of the path. This would leave us with no naked singularity in a natural way.

There are a number of nonzero choices we could make for
\mathcal{D}^{(1)}_{\mathrm{soft}}(\vec{\mu})
, letting
\mathcal{D}^{(1)}_{\mathrm{sc}}(\vec{\mu})=\mathcal{D}^{(1)}(\vec{\mu})-\mathcal{D}^{(1)}_{\mathrm{soft}}(\vec{\mu})
. One possibility is to define
\mathcal{D}^{(1)}_{\mathrm{sc}}(\vec{\mu})
so that, although it has a soft
\times
collinear double singularity, it has only a minimal wide-angle soft singularity. Whatever choice we make, the evolution
\mathcal{U}(1,0)
, using
\mathcal{D}^{(1)}_{\mathrm{soft}}(\vec{\mu})
, comes first, followed by evolution
\mathcal{U}(\infty,1)
, using
\mathcal{D}^{(1)}_{\mathrm{sc}}(\vec{\mu})
with an ordering prescription such as angular ordering,
\Lambda
ordering, or
k_{\mathrm{T}}
ordering.

This two scale formulation of a parton shower is reminiscent of soft-collinear effective theory (SCET). Suppose that we want to measure an observable that is nonzero when there are at least
N
hard jets. We start with a hard scattering that produces
N
hard jets. With a cut on
(N-1)
jettiness
Njettiness
,
\tau_{N-1}>\tau_{\mathrm{min}}
, we ensure that the hard partons constitute
N
jets and not
N-1
jets. With this as the hard state, the operator
\mathcal{S}_{\textsc{e}}^{\mathrm{soft}}(\vec{\mu}(t))
in
\mathcal{U}(1,0)
produces soft wide-angle radiation from the
N
hard jets, analogously to the soft factor in SCET. In the second segment of the shower evolution,
\mathcal{U}(\infty,1)
can add more soft radiation. However, if
\mathcal{S}_{\textsc{c}}^{\mathrm{sc}}(\vec{\mu}(t))
(where
\mathrm{C}=\angle,\Lambda,\perp
or some other choice) is defined to have only minimal wide-angle soft singularities, it is the first segment, involving
\mathcal{S}_{\textsc{e}}^{\mathrm{soft}}(\vec{\mu}(t))
that will dominate the soft radiation between the jets. Then the second evolution segment acts as the collinear factor in a SCET analysis and fills in the collinear radiation for each jet.

VIII
Improved color with two scales

In this section, we describe how one might use the choices available when using the two scales,
\mu_{\mathrm{C}}
and
\mu_{\mathrm{E}}
, to improve the treatment of color in the shower in a practical way.

First, we provide some background on color in parton showers. The most widely used parton shower event generators
Herwig
;
Pythia
;
Sherpa
use the leading color (LC) approximation, which captures just the leading term in an expansion in powers of
1/N_{\mathrm{c}}^{2}
, where
N_{\mathrm{c}}=3
is the number of colors. Here one simply supplies a color factor
C_{\mathrm{F}}=(N_{\mathrm{c}}^{2}-1)/(2N_{\mathrm{c}})
or
C_{\mathrm{A}}=N_{\mathrm{c}}
for emission of a gluon from a quark or gluon line, respectively, or else a factor
T_{\mathrm{R}}=1/2
for a gluon splitting to
q+\bar{q}
. To go beyond the LC approximation one needs to treat the color carried by quarks and gluons as fully quantum mechanical variables.

Throughout this paper, we have described color as fully quantum mechanical using a vector space for parton color with basis vectors
\big|{\{c,c^{\prime}\}_{m}}\big)

NSI
. The basis vector
\big|{\{c,c^{\prime}\}_{m}}\big)
represents a color density matrix
\big|{\{c\}_{m}}\big\rangle\big\langle{\{c^{\prime}\}_{m}}\big|
, where
\big|{\{c\}_{m}}\big\rangle
is a basis vector for the space of quantum color states for
m
partons. (
Deductor
uses the trace basis, but other choices are possible.) This description, with a somewhat different notation, is used in the recent papers
AngelesMartinez:2018cfz
;
Forshaw:2019ver
;
Forshaw:2020wrq
;
DeAngelis:2020rvq
;
Holguin:2020joq
;
Hoche:2020pxj
;
Platzer:2020lbr
to study color in parton shower evolution, accounting approximately for both real emission graphs and virtual exchange graphs. Other papers have used the color density matrix, but for the description of just real emissions
PlatzerSjodahl2012
;
PlatzerSjodahlThoren
;
Isaacson:2018zdi
. Ref.
HamiltonShowerSum
has worked to improve the treatment of color in parton showers without tying the description to the color density matrix.

One can express the evolution equations for a first order dipole shower so that it evolves with full color
NSI
. However, some approximation is needed for a shower realized in computer code. The
Deductor
shower uses what we call the LC+ approximation

for color
NScolor
. This is an improvement over the LC approximation. The splitting operators with this approximation,
\mathcal{S}^{\mathrm{LC+}}_{j}(\vec{\mu})
, are, however, still approximate in color, leaving a difference

\Delta\mathcal{S}_{j}(\vec{\mu})=\mathcal{S}_{j}(\vec{\mu})-\mathcal{S}^{\mathrm{LC+}}_{j}(\vec{\mu})\;.

(56)

Simply using
\mathcal{S}^{\mathrm{LC+}}_{j}
would give us an uncontrolled approximation since we would not know the size of corrections from
\Delta\mathcal{S}_{j}(\vec{\mu})
.
Deductor
allows a systematically improvable approximation: the user can compute corrections proportional to powers
[\Delta\mathcal{S}_{j}]^{N}
of
\Delta\mathcal{S}_{j}
(with a single scale
\mu_{\mathrm{s}}

NSNewColor
;
NSColoriPi
;
GapColor
. Any power

It would certainly be desirable to have particular choice of
\mathcal{S}_{\textsc{e}}^{\mathrm{soft}}(\vec{\mu}(t))
that results in making the inclusion of
\Delta\mathcal{S}_{j}(\vec{\mu})
computationally simpler. With this in mind, we note that the LC+ approximation has an important property. At each splitting, the leading soft
\times

. That is,
\Delta\mathcal{S}_{j}(\vec{\mu})
has no collinear singularity. Thus we can set

\begin{split}\mathcal{S}_{j}^{\mathrm{sc}}(\vec{\mu}(t))={}&\mathcal{S}_{j}^{\mathrm{LC+}}(\vec{\mu}(t))\;,\\
\mathcal{S}_{j}^{\mathrm{soft}}(\vec{\mu}(t))={}&\Delta\mathcal{S}_{j}(\vec{\mu}(t))\;.\end{split}

(57)

With this choice,
\mathcal{U}(\infty,1)
in Eq. (
53
) is exact in color and the corrections to the LC+ approximation appear in the factor
\mathcal{U}(1,0)
. This is significant for two reasons. First, the corrections to the LC+ approximation appear in one place, rather than appearing throughout the shower, interleaved with LC+ splittings, as in Refs.
NSNewColor
;
GapColor
;
NSColoriPi

\mathcal{U}(1,0)

For
\mathcal{U}(1,0)
, we can expand Eq. (
54
) in powers of
\Delta\mathcal{S}

\begin{split}\mathcal{U}(1,0)={}&1-\int_{0}^{1}\!dt\,\frac{d\mu_{\textsc{e}}(t)}{dt}\,\Delta\mathcal{S}_{\textsc{e}}(\vec{\mu}(t))\\
&+\int_{0}^{1}\!dt_{2}\,\frac{d\mu_{\textsc{e}}(t_{2})}{dt_{2}}\int_{0}^{t_{2}}\!dt_{1}\,\frac{d\mu_{\textsc{e}}(t_{1})}{dt_{1}}\\
&\quad\times\Delta\mathcal{S}_{\textsc{e}}(\vec{\mu}(t_{2}))\,\Delta\mathcal{S}_{\textsc{e}}(\vec{\mu}(t_{1}))\\
&+\cdots\;,\end{split}

(58)

keeping terms up to order

is chosen by the user. A more elaborate treatment is possible, but, given the simplicity of the hard scattering state to which

is applied, this very simple treatment should suffice.

If we start with the simplest process in

(59)

\mathcal{U}(\infty,0)\big|{\{p,f,c,c^{\prime}\}_{2}}\big)=\mathcal{U}(\infty,1)\big|{\{p,f,c,c^{\prime}\}_{2}}\big)\;.

(60)

We can write this in more detail. We choose the evolution scale in
\mathcal{U}(\infty,1)
as
\mu_{\textsc{c}}=\mu_{\angle}
,
\mu_{\Lambda}
, or
\mu_{\perp}

\mu_{\textsc{e}}=0

\begin{split}\mathcal{U}(\infty,0)\big|{\{p,f,c,c^{\prime}\}_{2}}\big)={}&\mathbb{T}\exp\!\left\{\int_{0}^{Q}\!d\mu_{\textsc{c}}\ \mathcal{S}^{\mathrm{LC+}}(\mu_{\textsc{c}})\right\}\\
&\times\big|{\{p,f,c,c^{\prime}\}_{2}}\big)\;.\end{split}

(61)

It is remarkable that the LC+ approximation for color gives the exact answer in this case. However, one should be careful about what “exact” means. A first order parton shower does not represent full QCD exactly. Two different choices for the choice of shower scale scheme will give two different parton shower algorithms. When we work within a framework that encompasses parton showers at any perturbative order
NSAllOrder
, we see that the first of two algorithms can, in principle, be mapped into the second by adding order
\alpha_{\mathrm{s}}^{2}
and higher order terms to the splitting functions of the second. With both splitting functions truncated at order
\alpha_{\mathrm{s}}
, the two algorithms give different results. The difference is a measure of the uncertainty inherent in using a first order shower.

Thus it is indeed remarkable that the LC+ approximation for color is exact in this case, but the meaning of this statement is that differences from the LC+ approximation in the one scale treatment can be absorbed into terms in the shower splitting functions that are higher order in
\alpha_{\mathrm{s}}
in the two scale treatment.

We emphasize that
e^{+}e^{-}
annihilation with
e^{+}e^{-}\to q\bar{q}
as the hard process is a special case. A hard scattering process with
m

m>2

58

\mathcal{U}(1,0)

XI
Summary and outlook

In a parton shower, the state of many partons evolves as partons split with increasing “shower time”
t
. For a first order shower, one parton can split into two partons as dictated by three splitting variables such as
(k_{\mathrm{T}},z,\phi)
. We have taken the view that for any
t
there is a resolvable region and an unresolvable region in the space of parton splitting variables. As
t
increases, more splittings become resolvable, so that there is a probability for a newly resolvable splitting to occur. The surface that divides the two regions can be parameterized by variables
\vec{\mu}
, with functions
\vec{\mu}(t)
specifying the progression of boundary surfaces.

One could have any number of parameters
\mu_{n}
to describe a surface in the space of parton splitting variables. Parton shower algorithms typically use one. In this paper, we use two parameters. We choose the first to be a parameter
\mu_{\textsc{e}}
that provides a cut on the energy of an emitted parton. We choose the second parameter to be
\mu_{\textsc{c}}
, which could be any of
\mu_{\perp}
,
\mu_{\Lambda}
, or
\mu_{\angle}
. In single variable evolution, this would correspond, respectively, to
k_{\mathrm{T}}
,
\Lambda
, or angular ordering, as described in Sec.
V
.

The probability for parton splitting in a first order shower is determined (using Eq. (
74
)) by splitting operators
\bm{D}_{l}
, where
l
is the index of the parton that splits and where
\bm{D}_{l}
is an operator on the color space of the partons and a function of the parton momenta and flavors. These operators can be decomposed into two terms

\bm{D}_{l}=\bm{D}_{l}^{\mathrm{sc}}+\bm{D}_{l}^{\mathrm{soft}}\;,

(67)

where
\bm{D}_{l}^{\mathrm{soft}}
is singular for soft emissions but is not singular for collinear emissions (or for soft
\times
collinear emissions). We have adopted an especially useful way to do this by defining

\bm{D}_{l}^{\mathrm{sc}}=\bm{D}_{l}^{\mathrm{LC+}}\;.

(68)

In
\bm{D}_{l}^{\mathrm{LC+}}
, we approximate
\bm{D}_{l}
using the LC+ approximation
NScolor
for color. Then the second term is

\bm{D}_{l}^{\mathrm{soft}}=\bm{D}_{l}-\bm{D}_{l}^{\mathrm{LC+}}\;.

(69)

As we see in Appendix
A
, the LC+ approximation is exact in the limit of collinear emissions
NScolor
. That is,
\bm{D}_{l}-\bm{D}_{l}^{\mathrm{LC+}}
has no collinear singularity.

This decomposition is important because we can define different treatments of the unresolvable region for the two contributions to
\bm{D}_{l}
. For splittings derived from
\bm{D}_{l}^{\mathrm{LC+}}
, there are three cuts used to determine when a splitting is unresolved. First, we impose a fixed infrared cutoff by defining a splitting to be unresolved whenever
k_{\mathrm{T}}^{2}<m_{\perp}^{2}
, where
m_{\perp}^{2}
is of order
1\ \mathrm{GeV}^{2}
. Second, there is a cut that depends on the energy scale
\mu_{\textsc{e}}^{2}
: a splitting is unresolved whenever
4z(1-z)<\mu_{\textsc{e}}^{2}/Q^{2}
. Third, there is a cut that depends on
\mu_{\textsc{c}}^{2}
. If
\mathrm{C}={\perp}
, a splitting is unresolved whenever
k_{\mathrm{T}}^{2}<\mu_{\perp}^{2}
. If
\mathrm{C}=\Lambda
, a splitting is unresolved whenever
\Lambda^{2}<\mu_{\Lambda}^{2}
, where
\Lambda^{2}
is the default ordering variable in
Deductor
and is proportional to the virtuality in the splitting. If
\mathrm{C}=\angle
, a splitting is unresolved whenever
\vartheta\,Q^{2}<\mu_{\angle}^{2}
where
\vartheta=[1-\cos(\theta)]/2
and
\theta
is the angle between the daughter parton momenta in the rest frame of
Q
.

We treat splittings derived from
\bm{D}_{l}^{\mathrm{soft}}
differently. The soft singularity is controlled by the cut
4z(1-z)<\mu_{\textsc{e}}^{2}/Q^{2}
. Since for these splittings there is no collinear singularity, we can omit the cut based on
\mu_{\textsc{c}}^{2}
.

The final ingredient in the formulation presented in this paper is the choice of a path
(\mu_{\textsc{e}}(t),\mu_{\textsc{c}}(t))
. The path we choose is has two segments, as shown in Fig.
12
. In the first segment, with
0<t<1
,
\mu_{\textsc{c}}^{2}
is fixed at
Q^{2}
and
\mu_{\textsc{e}}
decreases from
Q^{2}
to 0. In the second segment, with
1<t<\infty
,
\mu_{\textsc{e}}^{2}
is fixed at
0
and
\mu_{\textsc{c}}^{2}
decreases from
Q^{2}
to 0. In the first path segment, there is no unresolved region available for
\bm{D}_{l}^{\mathrm{LC+}}
because of the cut imposed by
\mu_{\textsc{c}}^{2}
. However, this cut does not apply for
\bm{D}_{l}^{\mathrm{soft}}
, so there is a contribution from
\bm{D}_{l}^{\mathrm{soft}}
. In the second part,
\bm{D}_{l}^{\mathrm{LC+}}
contributes, but the unresolved region does not change for
\bm{D}_{l}^{\mathrm{soft}}
, so
\bm{D}_{l}^{\mathrm{soft}}
does not contribute. This gives Eq. (
53
) for the complete evolution:

\mathcal{U}(\infty,0)=\mathcal{U}(\infty,1)\,\mathcal{U}(1,0)\;.

(70)

The second factor here,
\mathcal{U}(\infty,1)
, is a complete shower using the LC+ approximation for color and either
k_{\mathrm{T}}
,
\Lambda
, or angular ordering. The first factor provides an evolution in parton energy using the soft operator
\bm{D}_{l}^{\mathrm{soft}}
.

In the case of angular ordering, the formulation presented here provides a way to understand an angular ordered shower in which the only cutoff on soft emissions is provided by the fixed cutoff
k_{\mathrm{T}}^{2}>m_{\perp}^{2}
. If we were to set
m_{\perp}^{2}
to zero, we would have a naked singularity in the resolved region. With a fixed value of
m_{\perp}^{2}
, we do not find infinities in the results, but we can find large logarithms,
\log(Q^{2}/m_{\perp}^{2})
, that are not summed by a renormalization group equation. In the two scale treatment, the large logarithms are absorbed into
\mathcal{U}(1,0)
.

The LC+ shower provided by
\mathcal{U}(\infty,1)
is corrected by the operator
\mathcal{U}(1,0)
that is built from
\bm{D}_{l}^{\mathrm{soft}}
, Eq. (
69
). The splitting operator
\bm{D}_{l}^{\mathrm{soft}}
has a complicated color structure, making numerical calculations based on this operator difficult. However, this operator tends to be numerically small because it starts with a factor
1/N_{\mathrm{c}}^{2}\sim 1/10
and because it lacks a collinear singularity. Thus one can attack the numerical evaluation by expanding
\mathcal{U}(1,0)
in powers of
\bm{D}_{l}^{\mathrm{soft}}
. We have done this in Ref.
NSNewColor
, with splittings according to
\bm{D}_{l}^{\mathrm{soft}}
interleaved with LC+ evolution. The numerical evidence suggests that an expansion in powers of
\bm{D}_{l}^{\mathrm{soft}}
is adequate. With the shower formulation presented in this paper, the needed calculations are simpler because
\mathcal{U}(1,0)
is applied to the initial hard scattering state, denoted by
\big|{\rho_{\textsc{h}}}\big)
, which has few partons. The needed calculations are also simpler because the splittings from
\bm{D}_{l}^{\mathrm{soft}}
do not need to be interleaved with LC+ evolution, which we found to be complicated and computationally expensive.

In the case of
e^{+}e^{-}
annihilation with a color singlet
q\bar{q}
state
\big|{\rho_{\textsc{h}}}\big)
to start the shower, the calculations are, in fact, trivial. Because the space of
q\bar{q}\mathrm{g}
color states is just one dimensional,
\bm{D}_{l}^{\mathrm{soft}}
applied to
\big|{\rho_{\textsc{h}}}\big)
vanishes. Thus
\mathcal{U}(1,0)\big|{\rho_{\textsc{h}}}\big)=\big|{\rho_{\textsc{h}}}\big)
and no numerical calculation is needed.

Application of the formulation of this paper to hadron-hadron collisions is left to future work. Here, we note that for the Drell-Yan process at the Born level, the initial state with a color singlet
q\bar{q}
is like a
q\bar{q}
final state in
e^{+}e^{-}
annihilation, so that
\mathcal{U}(1,0)\big|{\rho_{\textsc{h}}}\big)=\big|{\rho_{\textsc{h}}}\big)
However, for jet production in hadron-hadron collisions,
\mathcal{U}(1,0)\big|{\rho_{\textsc{h}}}\big)\neq\big|{\rho_{\textsc{h}}}\big)
. Then a perturbative expansion of
\mathcal{U}(1,0)
will be needed. However, this expansion should be much simpler than when powers of
\bm{D}_{l}^{\mathrm{soft}}
are interleaved with the LC+ shower in the style of Ref.
NSNewColor
.

Finally, we offer the speculation that using multiple scales may prove useful in developing a parton shower algorithm with splitting functions defined at order
\alpha_{\mathrm{s}}^{2}
instead of just
\alpha_{\mathrm{s}}
. At order
\alpha_{\mathrm{s}}^{2}
, one can have two real emissions, one real emission together with a virtual exchange, or two virtual exchanges. For the case of two real emissions, both can be soft, one can be soft and one collinear with an existing parton, two can be collinear to two existing partons, or two can be collinear with one existing parton. The resulting singular surfaces are much more complicated than they are in a first order shower. It may well be useful to employ different scale parameters to describe an unresolved region that includes all of the singularities.

In Fig.
16
, we illustrated the application of the methods of this paper to the two jet cross section with the Cambridge algorithm in
e^{+}e^{-}
annihilation. This is quite simple since the contribution from the first component of the two component path is just the unit operator when one starts with just a
q\bar{q}
state. A surprising (at least to us) outcome was that with angular ordering for the second component of the path, the results were quite different than with
\Lambda
ordering or
k_{\mathrm{T}}
ordering for the second component.

Although the question of why this is lies outside of the main topic of this paper, we investigate in this appendix whether leaving everything the same in the
Deductor
code used for this paper and simply changing from
\Lambda
or
k_{\mathrm{T}}
ordering to angular ordering might change the accuracy with which the shower sums large logarithms.

For this purpose, we consider the thrust distribution, which we had previously investigated
NSThrustSum
(although not with angular ordering). The thrust,
T
, distribution is strongly peaked at small
1-T
. It contains a factor
1/(1-T)
and large logarithms of
(1-T)
. To investigate these logarithms, one takes the Laplace transform
\tilde{g}(\nu)
of the
(1-T)
distribution, with Laplace transform variable
\nu
. For large
\nu
, this function contains contributions proportional to
a_{s}^{n}\log^{k}(\nu)
with
k\leq 2n
. In QCD,
\tilde{g}(\nu)
exponentiates in the sense that
\log[\tilde{g}(\nu)]
contains contributions proportional to
a_{s}^{n}\log^{k}(\nu)
with
k\leq n+1
. The terms with
k=n+1
are the leading-log (LL) terms and the terms with
k=n
are the next-to-leading-log (NLL) terms. These terms are calculated analytically in Ref.
thrustsum
. Ref.
NSThrustSum
provides both analytical and numerical methods for investigating whether a parton shower reproduces those terms. In this appendix, we use one of the numerical methods. We calculate certain quantities
\langle I^{[J]}_{n}(\nu)\rangle
that are based on operating
J
times with the shower splitting operator and calculating its contribution at order
\alpha_{\mathrm{s}}^{n}
to
\log[\tilde{g}(\nu)]
minus what
\log[\tilde{g}(\nu)]
should be according to the analytic result.

We calculate
\langle I^{[2]}_{2}(\nu)\rangle
for the
Deductor
splitting functions with exact SU(3) color. In Fig.
18
, we show the results with
\Lambda
ordering and
k_{\mathrm{T}}
ordering.

This is an order

\log^{2}(\nu)

\nu
. Thus, for NLL accuracy, the curves representing

\log(\nu)
, as indeed they are.
