String attractors of some simple-Parry automatic sequences
France Gheeraert [0000-0002-8970-173X], Giuseppe Romana [0000-0002-3489-0684], Manon Stipulanti [0000-0002-2805-2465]

Abstract
Firstly studied by Kempa and Prezza in 2018 as the cement of text compression algorithms, string attractors have become a compelling object of theoretical research within the community of combinatorics on words. In this context, they have been studied for several families of finite and infinite words. In this paper, we obtain string attractors of prefixes of particular infinite words generalizing k k -bonacci words (including the famous Fibonacci word) and related to simple Parry numbers. In fact, our description involves the numeration systems classically derived from the considered morphisms. This extends our previous work published in the international conference WORDS 2023.

Introduction

Introduced in the field of data compression by Kempa and Prezza
Kempa and Prezza 2018
in 2018, the concept of
string attractor
can be conceptualized as follows: within a finite word, it is a set of positions that enables to catch all distinct factors.
Since then, questions related to string attractors have drawn the attention of many researchers from various scientific fields.
From the point of view of the theory of algorithmic complexity, the problem of finding a smallest string attractor is NP-hard
Kempa and Prezza 2018
.
In parallel, string attractors also have applications in combinatorial pattern matching
Christiansen et al. 2021
.
In order to understand the best way to measure data compressibility by exploiting repetitiveness in strings, measures have recently been introduced in relation to string attractors
Kociumaka et al. 2023
.

Quickly after, combinatorics-on-words researchers have quite naturally seized the notion and made a systematic topic of research out of it.
String attractors have been studied in the context of some (families of) sequences: automatic sequences
Schaeffer and
Shallit 2022
with some focus on the ubiquitous Thue–Morse word
Kutsukake et al. 2020
;
Schaeffer and
Shallit 2022
;
Dolce 2023
and the period-doubling word
Schaeffer and
Shallit 2022
, the famous Sturmian words
Mantaci et al. 2021
;
Restivo et al. 2022
;
Dvořáková 2024
and their extension known as episturmian words
Dvořáková 2024
, the Tribonacci word
Schaeffer and
Shallit 2022
and more generally the
k
-bonacci words
Cassaigne et al. 2024
, some binary generalized pseudostandard sequences
Dvořáková and
Hendrychová 2023
, and bi-infinite words
Béaur et al. 2024
.
Besides analyzing important families of words, another classical topic in combinatorics on words is the study of complexity functions for infinite words, such as the distinguished factor complexity function and the so-called abelian
Coven and Hedlund 1973
and binomial
Rigo and Salimov 2015
complexity functions.
A first complexity function based on string attractors was introduced in
Schaeffer and
Shallit 2022
and considered for automatic sequences and linearly recurrent infinite words.
In addition to further studying such a complexity function, the authors of
Restivo et al. 2022
;
Cassaigne et al. 2024
introduce and examine two other string attractor-based complexity functions.

Historically, the bond between string attractors and numeration systems was observed for the first time in
Cassaigne et al. 2024
.
There, the authors consider generalizations of the Fibonacci word to larger alphabets (on
k
letters, the corresponding word is called the
k
-bonacci word
) and show that some of their strings attractors rely on the well-known
k
-bonacci numbers.
These infinite words are
morphic
, i.e., they are obtained as images, under codings, of fixed points of morphisms.
Consequently, we currently believe that the link between string attractors and numeration systems can be adapted to other morphic sequences and we therefore raise the following general question:

Question.
Given a morphic sequence
\mathbf{z}
, does there exist a numeration system
\mathcal{S}
such that
\mathbf{z}
is
\mathcal{S}
-automatic and (minimal) string attractors of the prefixes of
\mathbf{z}
are easily described using
\mathcal{S}
?

In this paper, to support this question, we study a particular family of morphic words.
More precisely, given parameters in the shape of a length-
k
word
c=c_{0}\cdots c_{k-1}\in\mathbb{N}^{k}
, we define the morphism
\mu_{c}
such that
\mu_{c}(i)=0^{c_{i}}\cdot(i+1)
for all
i\in\{0,\ldots,k-2\}
and
\mu_{c}(k-1)=0^{c_{k-1}}
.
When it exists, we then look at the fixed point of
\mu_{c}
.
From a combinatorics-on-words point of view, this family is interesting as it generalizes the
k
-bonacci morphisms.
However, their main interest stems from numeration systems.
Given a simple Parry number
\beta
(a real number for which the
\beta
-expansion of
1
is of the form
u0^{\omega}
for some finite word
u
), Fabre
Fabre 1995
associated with it a morphism
\mu
which is strongly related to the automaton corresponding to the base-
\beta
numeration system (for real numbers), or alternatively to the greedy numeration system associated with the sequence
(|\mu^{n}(0)|)_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
(for integers)
Bertrand-Mathis 1989
. This morphism can also be used to understand the base-
\beta
integers
Fabre 1995
;
Thurston 2022
.
It turns out that
\mu
corresponds to
\mu_{c}
where
c
is the
\beta
-representation of 1. Note that, due to a result of Parry
Parry 1960
, not every
c
corresponds to a simple Parry number.
Combinatorially speaking, the fixed points of these particular morphisms have been studied for instance in
Burdík et al. 1998
;
Frougny et al. 2004
;
Ambrož et al. 2006
;
Turek 2015
.

Furthermore, the techniques used so far to obtain string attractors of infinite words do not apply to the words we consider.
Indeed, on the one hand, they are not necessarily episturmian, so we cannot use the approach from
Dvořáková 2024
.
On the other hand, for some parameter
c\in\mathbb{N}^{k}
, the corresponding numeration system is not
addable
, meaning that the addition within the numeration system is not recognizable by a finite automaton.
For example, this is the case of
c=3203

Frougny 1997
.
As a consequence, we cannot follow the methods from
Schaeffer and
Shallit 2022
; in particular, we study words outside the framework needed to use the software
Walnut

Shallit 2023
.

Under some conditions on the parameters, we show that the prefixes of the fixed point admit string attractors strongly related to the associated numeration system. Moreover, they are of size at most one more than the alphabet size, and are therefore nearly optimal. Finally, we provide an infinite family of words for which these string attractors have minimal size.

This work extends and completes some of the results presented in our first exploratory paper on this topic
Gheeraert et al. 2023
. In this version, we emphasize the link with numeration systems and simple Parry numbers. We also provide new results, and as a consequence, have restructured and simplified most of the proofs.

This paper is organized as follows.
In Section
2
, we first recall some classical notions of combinatorics on words.
In particular, we introduce the family of words that we will focus on.
We then show how these words are related to numeration systems in Section
3
, and we study some of their properties such as greediness.
Section
4
is devoted to the construction of string attractors of the words.
More precisely, we give the precise conditions under which every prefix admits a string attractor included in a particular subset, and we exhibit such a string attractor when the conditions are met.
Then in Section
5
, we analyze these conditions and give alternative formulations in terms of the numeration system and of the parameters
c_{0},\dots,c_{k-1}
.
Finally, in Section
6
, we discuss the optimality of the string attractors that we obtained and describe an infinite family of words for which they are optimal.

2.1
Words

We start with the bare minimum on words and introduce some notations.

Let
A
be an alphabet either finite or infinite (for instance, we will consider words over the set of non-negative integers
\mathbb{N}
).
The length of a word is its number of letters and will be denoted with vertical bars
|\cdot|
.
We let
\varepsilon
denote the empty word, and
A^{*}
denote the set of finite words over
A
.
For any integer
n\geq 0
, we let
A^{n}
be the set of length-
n
words over
A
.
If
w=xyz
for some
x,y,z\in A^{*}
, then
x
is a
prefix
,
y
is a
factor
, and
z
is a
suffix
of
w
.
A factor of a word is
proper
if it is not equal to the initial word.
A word

is a
fractional power
of a non-empty word
w
if there exist
\ell\geq 0
and
x
a prefix of
w
such that
v=w^{\ell}x
.
Infinite words are written in bold and we start indexing them at
0
.
We use classical notations of intervals to denote portions of words.
For a non-empty word
u\in A^{*}
, we let
u^{\omega}
denote the concatenation of infinitely many copies of
u
, that is,
u^{\omega}=uuu\cdots
.

Let
\leq
be a total order on
A
. The
lexicographic order
on
A^{*}
induced by
\leq
is defined as follows: for
x,y\in A^{*}
, we say that
x
is
lexicographically smaller than

y
, and we write
x<y
, if either
x
is a proper prefix of
y
, or
x=zax^{\prime}
and
y=zby^{\prime}
for some letters
a,b
with
a<b
.
We write
x\leq y
if
x
is lexicographically smaller than or equal to
y
.
The
genealogical order
, also known as

, on
A^{*}
induced by
\leq
is defined as follows: for
x,y\in A^{*}
, we say that
x
is
genealogically smaller than

y
, and we write
x<_{\text{gen}}y
, if either
|x|<|y|
, or
|x|=|y|
and
x=zax^{\prime}
and
y=zby^{\prime}
for some letters
a,b
with
a<b
.
We write again

if
x
is genealogically smaller than or equal to
y
.

A non-empty word

is

if
w=u^{n}
for
u\in A^{*}\setminus\{\varepsilon\}
implies
n=1
.
Two words are
conjugates
if they are cyclic permutation of each other.

A word is

if it is primitive and lexicographically minimal among its conjugates for some given order.
Defined in the 50’s, Lyndon words are not only classical in combinatorics on words but also of utmost importance.
See
Lothaire 1997
for a presentation.
A celebrated result in combinatorics on words is that London words form a so-called
complete factorization of the free monoid
.

Theorem 1

(Chen-Fox-Lyndon
Chen et al. 1958
)
.

For every non-empty word
w\in A^{*}
, there exists a unique factorization
(\ell_{1},\ldots,\ell_{n})
of
w
into Lyndon words over
A
such that
\ell_{1}\geq\ell_{2}\geq\cdots\geq\ell_{n}
.

Several variations of Lyndon words have been considered lately: generalized Lyndon
Reutenauer 2005/07
, anti-Lyndon
Gewurz and Merola 2012
, inverse Lyndon
Bonizzoni et al. 2018
, and Nyldon
Charlier et al. 2019

In this text, we will use the second.

Definition 1
.

Let
(A,\leq)
be a totally ordered alphabet.
We let
\leq_{-}
denote the
inverse order
on
A
, i.e.,
b<_{-}a
if and only if
a<b
for all

.
We also let
\mathbin{{\leq}_{-}}
denote the

which is the lexicographic order induced by
\leq_{-}
.
A word is

if it is Lyndon with respect to the inverse lexicographic order.

Otherwise stated, a word is anti-Lyndon if it is primitive and lexicographically maximal among its conjugates.

Let

with

, so

Proposition 4

For all

u_{n}=\begin{cases}\left(\displaystyle\prod_{i=0}^{n-1}u_{n-i-1}^{c_{i}}\right)\cdot n,&\text{if }0\leq n\leq k-1;\\

U_{n}=\begin{cases}1+\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}c_{i}U_{n-i-1},&\text{if }0\leq n\leq k-1;\\

(1)

c=c_{0}\cdots c_{k-1}\geq 0^{k}\text{ with }c_{0},c_{k-1}\geq 1.

3
Link with numeration systems

In this section, specific definitions will be recalled.
For the reader unfamiliar with the theory of numeration systems, we refer to
(
Berthé and Rigo 2010
, Chapters 2 and 3)
for an introduction and some advanced concepts.

A
numeration system
(for natural numbers) can be defined as a triple
\mathcal{S}=(A,\rep_{\mathcal{S}},L)
, where
A
is an alphabet and
\rep_{\mathcal{S}}\colon\mathbb{N}\to A^{*}
is an injective function such that
L=\rep_{\mathcal{S}}(\mathbb{N})
.
The map
\rep_{\mathcal{S}}
is called the
representation function
and
L
is the
numeration language
.
If
\rep_{\mathcal{S}}(n)=w
for some integer
n\geq 0
and some word
w\in A^{*}
, we say that
w
is the
representation (in
\mathcal{S}
)
of
n
and we define the
valuation (in
\mathcal{S}
)
of
w
by
\val_{\mathcal{S}}(w)=n
.
Note that, when the context is clear, we omit the subscript
\mathcal{S}
in
\rep
and
\val
.

Any given prolongable morphism naturally gives rise to a numeration system that we will call the
associated Dumont-Thomas numeration system

Dumont and Thomas 1989
. These are based on particular factorizations of the prefixes of the fixed point.
We only give here the definition in the particular case of the morphisms studied in this paper but the interested reader can find the general case in the original paper
Dumont and Thomas 1989
.

Proposition 6

(Dumont-Thomas
Dumont and Thomas 1989
)
.

Let
c
satisfy (
WH
).
For all
n\geq 1
, there exist unique integers
N,\ell_{0},\ldots,\ell_{N}\geq 0
such that
\ell_{0}\geq 1
,

\mathbf{u}[0,n)=u_{N}^{\ell_{0}}\cdots u_{0}^{\ell_{N}}
, and this factorization verifies the following:
u_{N+1}
is not a prefix of
\mathbf{u}[0,n)
and, for all
0\leq i\leq N
,
u_{N}^{\ell_{0}}\cdots u_{N-i+1}^{\ell_{i-1}}u_{N-i}^{\ell_{i}+1}
is not a prefix of
\mathbf{u}[0,n)
.

Recall that a numeration system based on a suitable sequence of integers
(U_{n})_{n\geq 0}
is called
greedy
when, at each step of the decomposition of any integer, the largest possible term of the sequence
(U_{n})_{n\geq 0}
is chosen; formally, we use the Euclidean algorithm in a greedy way.
As the conditions on the factorization in Proposition
6
resemble that of greedy representations in numeration systems, we will refer to it as being

For a given

where the integers

verify the conditions of Proposition

for

.

Lemma 14

4
String attractors of the prefixes

We now turn to the concept of string attractors in relation to the fixed points of the morphisms
\mu_{c}
,
c\geq 0^{k}

A

is a set

of

.

Example 19

We would like to stress the following crucial point: in this paper, the letters of infinite words are indexed starting from

while the positions in a string attractor are counted starting at

This could be seen as confusing, but we use the same notation as the original paper on string attractors

Where ambiguity may occur, we explicitly declare how finite words are indexed.

Algorithm 30

(Duval

)

Let

be an ordered set and let

Using the notation of the paragraph preceding Algorithm

We want to compute the next Lyndon word in the Lyndon factorization of a word, knowing that of some of its prefixes.

•

w^{\ell}vw=\mathbf{d}^{\star}[0,(\ell+1)|w|+|v|)\leq\mathbf{a}[0,(\ell+1)|w|+|v|)=w^{\ell}vuv.

Since

is anti-Lyndon, the only possibility is to have

and

is an integer power of

.
This proves Item
2c
.

We show that

By Item

of Lemma

, we have

.
If the previous inequality is strict, then the conclusion is direct by Item

of Lemma

.
Otherwise, by Item

of Lemma

is an integer power of

, which is enough.

Finally, the Assertions

and

This ends the proof.
∎

6
Optimality of the string attractors

So far we were interested in obtaining a precise description (related to a specific numeration system) of a string attractor for each prefix of the infinite word of interest.
In this section, we rather focus on the size of attractors and therefore recall the following concept from
Schaeffer and
Shallit 2022
;
Restivo et al. 2022
.
Given an infinite word
\mathbf{x}
and any integer
n\geq 1
, we let
s_{\mathbf{x}}(n)
denote the size of a smallest string attractor for the length-
n
prefix of
\mathbf{x}
. The function
s_{\mathbf{x}}\colon n\mapsto s_{\mathbf{x}}(n)
is called the
string attractor profile function
of
\mathbf{x}
.
As a consequence of Theorem
23
, we obtain the following.

Corollary 37
.

Let
c
satisfy (
WH
) and assume that one of the assertions of Proposition
34
holds.

1.

For all
n\geq 0
and all
m\in[{P_{n}},{Q_{n}}]
,
s_{\mathbf{u}}(m)=\card(\Gamma_{n})
.

2.

For all large enough
m
,
k\leq s_{\mathbf{u}}(m)\leq k+1
.

Proof.

Using a simple induction, one can check that the positions in
\Gamma_{n}
all correspond to different letters in
\mathbf{u}
. As any string attractor must contain at least one position per letter, we deduce the first claim from Theorem
23
.
The second part similarly follows from Theorem
23
since, for all large enough
n
,
\Gamma_{n}
is of size
k
, and the intervals
[U_{n},{Q_{n}}]
cover
\mathbb{N}\setminus\{0\}
by Proposition
34
.
∎

For some parameters
c
, the intervals
[{P_{n}},{Q_{n}}]
also cover
\mathbb{N}\setminus\{0\}
, implying that a string attractor of minimal size can always be given by some
\Gamma_{n}
. This is for example the case of
c=211
. We characterize these parameters
c
in the following result.
Recall the definition of the sequence
({P_{n}})_{n\geq 0}
given in Equation (
2
):

{P_{n}}=\begin{cases}U_{n},&\text{if }0\leq n\leq k-1;\\
U_{n}+U_{n-k+1}-U_{n-k}-1,&\text{if }n\geq k.\end{cases}

Proposition 38
.

Let
c
satisfy (
WH
) and let
w
denote the longest anti-Lyndon prefix of
c_{0}\cdots c_{k-2}
.
Moreover assume that one of the assertions of Proposition
34
holds. The inequality
{P_{n}}-1\leq{Q_{n-1}}
holds for all
n
if and only if the following conditions are satisfied:

1.

we have
c_{k-1}=1
;

2.

the word
c_{0}\cdots c_{k-2}
is an integer power of
w
.

Proof.

Observe that, for all
n\leq k-1
, Equation (
1
) gives

{P_{n}}-1=U_{n}-1=c_{0}U_{n-1}+\dots+c_{n}U_{0}.

On the other hand, Lemma
33
implies that
c_{0}\cdots c_{k-2}=\mathbf{a}[0,k-2]
, so

{Q_{n-1}}=\mathbf{a}_{0}U_{n-1}+\dots+\mathbf{a}_{n}U_{0}={P_{n}}-1

by Proposition
31
. This shows that the inequality of the statement always holds for all
n\leq k-1
. Let us now show that it is also satisfied for
n\geq k
if and only if the two conditions of the statement are satisfied.

For
n\geq k
, we have

\displaystyle{P_{n}}-1

\displaystyle=U_{n}+U_{n-k+1}-U_{n-k}-2

\displaystyle=c_{0}U_{n-1}+\dots+c_{k-2}U_{n-k+1}+(c_{k-1}-1)U_{n-k}+U_{n-k+1}-2,

where the second equality follows from Equation (
1
), and

\displaystyle{Q_{n-1}}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{a}_{0}U_{n-1}+\dots+\mathbf{a}_{k-2}U_{n-k+1}+\mathbf{a}_{k-1}U_{n-k}+\dots+\mathbf{a}_{n-1}U_{0}.

Therefore, by Lemma
33
,

{P_{n}}-1\leq{Q_{n-1}}\iff(c_{k-1}-1)U_{n-k}+U_{n-k+1}-2\leq\mathbf{a}_{k-1}U_{n-k}+\dots+\mathbf{a}_{n-1}U_{0}.

(8)

Now, if the two conditions of the statement are fulfilled, i.e., if
c_{k-1}=1
and
c_{0}\cdots c_{k-2}
is an integer power of
w
, then
\mathbf{a}_{k-1+\ell}=\mathbf{a}_{\ell}
for all
\ell\geq 0
, so

{P_{n}}-1\leq{Q_{n-1}}\iff U_{n-k+1}-2\leq{Q_{n-k}}.

Since
U_{n-k+1}-2\leq{P_{n-k+1}}-1
, we can then conclude by induction that the inequality of the statement is satisfied for all
n\geq 0
.

Let us now prove the converse by a detailed case-analysis, and assume first by contradiction that
c_{k-1}\neq 1
. Since
c_{k-1}\geq 1
, this in fact means that
c_{k-1}\geq 2
. For
n=k
, the right-hand side of (
8
) becomes

(c_{k-1}-1)U_{0}+U_{1}-2\leq\mathbf{a}_{k-1}U_{0},

which gives, since
U_{0}=1
and
U_{1}=c_{0}+1
,

c_{0}\leq c_{k-1}-2+c_{0}\leq\mathbf{a}_{k-1}.

Since
\mathbf{a}
is the infinite concatenation of an anti-Lyndon word, we have
\mathbf{a}_{k-1}\leq\mathbf{a}_{0}=c_{0}
. Therefore, we deduce that
\mathbf{a}_{k-1}=c_{0}
and
c_{k-1}=2
.
Now, if
n=k+1
, the right-hand side of (
8
) becomes

(c_{k-1}-1)U_{1}+U_{2}-2\leq\mathbf{a}_{k-1}U_{1}+\mathbf{a}_{k}U_{0}\iff c_{0}+1+(U_{2}-c_{0}U_{1})-2\leq\mathbf{a}_{k}.

This leads us to consider two cases depending on
k
.
If
k=2
, we get
U_{2}-c_{0}U_{1}=c_{1}=2
so
c_{0}+1+2-2\leq\mathbf{a}_{2}
, but as
\mathbf{a}_{2}\leq\mathbf{a}_{0}=c_{0}
, this is impossible.
If
k\geq 3
, then
U_{2}-c_{0}U_{1}=c_{1}+1
, however since
\mathbf{a}_{k-1}\mathbf{a}_{k}\leq\mathbf{a}_{0}\mathbf{a}_{1}=c_{0}c_{1}
and
\mathbf{a}_{k-1}=c_{0}
as established above, we must have
c_{0}+1+c_{1}+1-2\leq\mathbf{a}_{k}\leq c_{1}
which is also impossible.

This shows that, if
{P_{n}}-1\leq{Q_{n-1}}
for all
n\geq 0
, then
c_{k-1}=1
. Assume now by contradiction that
c_{0}\cdots c_{k-2}
is not an integer power of
w
. Therefore, by Lemma
33
, there exist
\ell\geq 1
and a proper non-empty prefix
x
of
w
such that such that
c_{0}\cdots c_{k-2}=w^{\ell}x
. Let us denote
w=xy
for some (non-empty) word

For
n=k+|w|
, the right-hand side of (

\displaystyle U_{|w|+1}-2

\displaystyle\leq\mathbf{a}_{k-1}U_{|w|}+\dots+\mathbf{a}_{k-1+|w|}U_{0}

\displaystyle\iff c_{0}U_{|w|}+\dots+(c_{|w|}-1)U_{0}

\displaystyle\leq\mathbf{a}_{k-1}U_{|w|}+\dots+\mathbf{a}_{k-1+|w|}U_{0},

(9)

where we used Equation (

(recall that
|w|+1\leq\ell|w|+|x|=k-1
).
Notice that
\mathbf{a}[k-1,+\infty)=yw^{\omega}
, so
\mathbf{a}[k-1,k-1+|w|]=yxy_{0}
. On the other hand,
c_{0}\cdots c_{|w|-1}(c_{|w|}-1)=w(w_{0}-1)
(recall that

).
As

is anti-Lyndon, we have

w(w_{0}-1)>yxy_{0}
. To obtain a contradiction, let us go back to numeration systems.

For all

which contradicts Inequality (

∎

Let
