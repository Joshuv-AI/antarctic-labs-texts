Extensions to the Proximal Distance Method of Constrained Optimization
Alfonso Landeros alanderos@ucla.edu, Oscar Hernan Madrid Padilla oscar.madrid@stat.ucla.edu, Hua Zhou huazhou@ucla.edu, Kenneth Lange klange@ucla.edu

Abstract
The current paper studies the problem of minimizing a loss f ⁡ ( 𝒙 ) f(\boldsymbol{x}) subject to constraints of the form 𝑫 ​ 𝒙 ∈ S \boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{x}\in S , where S S is a closed set, convex or not, and 𝑫 \boldsymbol{D} is a matrix that fuses the parameters. Fusion constraints can capture smoothness, sparsity, or more general constraint patterns. To tackle this generic class of problems, we combine the Beltrami-Courant penalty method of optimization with the proximal distance principle. The latter is driven by minimization of penalized objectives f ⁡ ( 𝒙 ) + ρ 2 ​ dist ​ ( 𝑫 ​ 𝒙 , S ) 2 f(\boldsymbol{x})+\frac{\rho}{2}\text{dist}(\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{x},S)^{2} involving large tuning constants ρ \rho and the squared Euclidean distance of 𝑫 ​ 𝒙 \boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{x} from S S . The next iterate 𝒙 n + 1 \boldsymbol{x}_{n+1} of the corresponding proximal distance algorithm is constructed from the current iterate 𝒙 n \boldsymbol{x}_{n} by minimizing the majorizing surrogate function f ⁡ ( 𝒙 ) + ρ 2 ​ ‖ 𝑫 ​ 𝒙 − 𝒫 S ​ ( 𝑫 ​ 𝒙 n ) ‖ 2 f(\boldsymbol{x})+\frac{\rho}{2}\|\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{x}-\mathcal{P}_{S}(\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{x}_{n})\|^{2} . For fixed ρ \rho and a subanalytic loss f ⁡ ( 𝒙 ) f(\boldsymbol{x}) and a subanalytic constraint set S S , we prove convergence to a stationary point. Under stronger assumptions, we provide convergence rates and demonstrate linear local convergence. We also construct a steepest descent (SD) variant to avoid costly linear system solves. To benchmark our algorithms, we compare their results to those delivered by the alternating direction method of multipliers (ADMM). Our extensive numerical tests include problems on metric projection, convex regression, convex clustering, total variation image denoising, and projection of a matrix to a good condition number. These experiments demonstrate the superior speed and acceptable accuracy of our steepest variant on high-dimensional problems. Julia code to replicate all of our experiments can be found at https://github.com/alanderos91/ProximalDistanceAlgorithms.jl

1
Introduction

The generic problem of minimizing a continuous function
f(\boldsymbol{x})
over a closed set
S
of
\mathbb{R}^{p}
can be attacked by a combination of the penalty method and distance majorization. The classical penalty method seeks the solution of a penalized version
h_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{x})=f(\boldsymbol{x})+\rho q(\boldsymbol{x})
of
f(\boldsymbol{x})
, where the penalty
q(\boldsymbol{x})
is nonnegative and 0 precisely when
\boldsymbol{x}\in S
. If one follows the solution vector
\boldsymbol{x}_{\rho}
as
\rho
tends to
\infty
, then in the limit one recovers the constrained solution
(
Beltrami 1970
;
Courant 1943
)
. The function

\displaystyle q(\boldsymbol{x})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\mathop{\rm dist}\nolimits(\boldsymbol{x},S)^{2}\mathop{\;\>}\nolimits=\mathop{\;\>}\nolimits\frac{1}{2}\min_{\boldsymbol{y}\in S}\|\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{y}\|^{2}

is one of the most fruitful penalties in this setting. Our previous research for solving this penalized minimization problem has focused on an MM (majorization-minimization) algorithm based on distance majorization
(
Chi et al. 2014
;
Keys et al. 2019
)
. In distance majorization one constructs the surrogate function

\displaystyle g_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{x}\mid\boldsymbol{x}_{n})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle f(\boldsymbol{x})+\frac{\rho}{2}\|\boldsymbol{x}-\mathcal{P}(\boldsymbol{x}_{n})\|^{2}

using the Euclidean projection
\mathcal{P}(\boldsymbol{x}_{n})
of the current iterate
\boldsymbol{x}_{n}
onto
S
. The minimum of the surrogate occurs at the proximal point

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{x}_{n+1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathop{\rm prox}\nolimits_{\rho^{-1}f}[\mathcal{P}(\boldsymbol{x}_{n})].

(1)

According to the MM principle, this choice of
\boldsymbol{x}_{n+1}
decreases
g_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{x}\mid\boldsymbol{x}_{n})
and hence the objective
h_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{x})
as well.
As we note in our previous
JMLR
paper
(
Keys et al. 2019
)
, the update (
1
) reduces to the classical proximal gradient method when
S
is convex
(
Parikh 2014
)
.

We have named this iterative scheme the proximal distance algorithm
(
Keys et al. 2019
;
Lange 2016
)
. It enjoys several virtues. First, it allows one to exploit the extensive body of results on proximal maps and projections. Second, it does not demand that the constraint set
S
be convex. If
S
is merely closed, then the map
\mathcal{P}(\boldsymbol{x})
may be multivalued, and one must choose a representative element from the projection
\mathcal{P}(\boldsymbol{x}_{n})
. Third, the algorithm does not require the objective function
f(\boldsymbol{x})
to be differentiable. Fourth, the algorithm dispenses with the chore of choosing a step length. Fifth, if sparsity is desirable, then the sparsity level can be directly specified rather than implicitly determined by the tuning parameter of the lasso or other penalty.

Traditional penalty methods have been criticized for their numerical instability. This hazard is mitigated in the proximal distance algorithm by its reliance on proximal maps, which usually are highly accurate. The major defect of the proximal distance algorithm is slow convergence. This can be ameliorated by Nesterov acceleration
(
Nesterov 2013
)
. There is also the question of how fast one should send
\rho
to
\infty
. Although no optimal schedule is known, simple numerical experiments usually yield a good choice. Finally, soft constraints can be achieved by stopping the steady increase of
\rho
at a finite value.

1.1
Proposed Framework

This simple version of distance majorization can be generalized in various ways. For instance, it can be expanded to multiple constraint sets. In practice, at most two constraint sets usually suffice. Another generalization is to replace the constraint
\boldsymbol{x}\in S
by the constraint
\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{x}\in S
, where
\boldsymbol{D}
is a compatible matrix. Again, the original case
\boldsymbol{D}=\boldsymbol{I}
is allowed. By analogy with the fused lasso of
Tibshirani et al. 2005
, we will call the matrix
\boldsymbol{D}
a fusion matrix. This paper is devoted to the study of the general problem of minimizing a differentiable function
f(\boldsymbol{x})
subject to
r
fused constraints
\boldsymbol{D}_{i}\boldsymbol{x}\in S_{i}
. We will approach this problem by extending the proximal distance method. For a fixed penalty constant
\rho
, the objective function and its MM surrogate now become

\displaystyle h_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{x})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle f(\boldsymbol{x})+\frac{\rho}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{r}\mathop{\rm dist}\nolimits(\boldsymbol{D}_{i}\boldsymbol{x},S_{i})^{2}

\displaystyle g_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{x}\mid\boldsymbol{x}_{n})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle f(\boldsymbol{x})+\frac{\rho}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{r}\|\boldsymbol{D}_{i}\boldsymbol{x}-\mathcal{P}_{i}(\boldsymbol{D}_{i}\boldsymbol{x}_{n})\|^{2},

where
\mathcal{P}_{i}(\boldsymbol{y})
denotes the projection of
\boldsymbol{y}
onto
S_{i}
. Any or all of the fusion matrices
\boldsymbol{D}_{i}
can be the identity
\boldsymbol{I}
.

Fortunately, we can simplify the problem by defining
S
to be the Cartesian product
\prod_{i=1}^{r}S_{i}
and
\boldsymbol{D}
to be the stacked matrix

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{D}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{D}_{1}\\
\vdots\\
\boldsymbol{D}_{r}\end{pmatrix}.

Our objective and surrogate then revert to the less complicated forms

\displaystyle h_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{x})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle f(\boldsymbol{x})+\frac{\rho}{2}\mathop{\rm dist}\nolimits(\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{x},S)^{2}

(2)

\displaystyle g_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{x}\mid\boldsymbol{x}_{n})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle f(\boldsymbol{x})+\frac{\rho}{2}\|\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{x}-\mathcal{P}(\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{x}_{n})\|^{2},

(3)

where
\mathcal{P}(\boldsymbol{x})
is the Cartesian product of the projections
\mathcal{P}_{i}(\boldsymbol{x})
. Note that all closed sets
S_{i}
with simple projections, including sparsity sets, are fair game.

1.2
Contributions

In the framework described above, we summarize the contributions of the current paper.

(a)

Section
2
describes different solution algorithms for minimizing the penalized loss
h_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{x})
. Our first algorithm is based on Newton’s method applied to the surrogate
g_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{x}\mid\boldsymbol{x}_{n})
. For some important problems, Newton’s method reduces to least squares. Our second method is a steepest descent algorithm on
g_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{x}\mid\boldsymbol{x}_{n})
tailored to high dimensional problems.

(b)

For a sufficiently large
\rho
, we show that when
f(\boldsymbol{x})
and
S
are convex and
f(\boldsymbol{x})
possesses a unique minimum point
\boldsymbol{y}\in\boldsymbol{D}^{-1}(S)
, the penalized loss
h_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{x})
attains its minimum value. This is the content of Proposition
3.1
. Similarly, Proposition
3.2
shows that the surrogate
g_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{x}\mid\boldsymbol{x}_{n})
also attains its minimum.

(c)

If in addition
f(\boldsymbol{x})
is differentiable, then Proposition
3.3
demonstrates that the MM iterates
\boldsymbol{x}_{n}
for minimizing
h_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{x})
satisfy

\displaystyle h_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{x}_{n})-h_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{z}_{\rho})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle O\left(\frac{\rho}{n}\right),

where
\boldsymbol{z}_{\rho}
minimizes
h_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{x})
. If
f(\boldsymbol{x})
is also
L
-smooth and
\mu
-strongly convex, then Proposition
3.4
shows that
\boldsymbol{z}_{\rho}
is unique and the iterates
\boldsymbol{x}_{n}
converge to
\boldsymbol{z}_{\rho}
at a linear rate.

(d)

More generally, Proposition
4.2
shows
that the iterates
\boldsymbol{x}_{n}
of a generic MM algorithm for minimizing a coercive subanalytic function
h(\boldsymbol{x})
with a good surrogate converge to a stationary point. Our objectives and their surrogates fall into this category.

(e)

Finally, we discuss a competing alternating direction method of multipliers (ADMM) algorithm and note its constituent updates. Our extensive numerical experiments compare the
two proximal distance algorithms to ADMM. We find that proximal distance algorithms are competitive with and often superior to ADMM in terms of accuracy and running time.

2
Different Solution Algorithms

Unless
f(\boldsymbol{x})
is a convex quadratic, exact minimization of the surrogate
g_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{x}\mid\boldsymbol{x}_{n})
is likely infeasible.
As we have already mentioned, to reduce the objective
h_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{x})
in (
2
), it suffices to reduce the surrogate (
3
). For the latter task, we recommend Newton’s method on small and intermediate-sized problems and steepest descent on large problems. The exact nature of these generic methods are problem dependent.
The following section provides a high-level overview of each strategy and we defer details on our later numerical experiments to the appendices.

2.1
Newton’s Method and Least Squares

Unfortunately, the proximal operator
\mathop{\rm prox}\nolimits_{\rho^{-1}f}(\boldsymbol{y})
is no longer relevant in calculating the MM update
\boldsymbol{x}_{n+1}
. When
f(\boldsymbol{x})
is smooth, Newton’s method for the surrogate
g_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{x}\mid\boldsymbol{x}_{n})
employs the update

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{x}_{n+1}

\displaystyle\!=\!

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{x}_{n}-\Big[\boldsymbol{H}_{n}+\rho\boldsymbol{D}^{t}\boldsymbol{D}\Big]^{-1}\Big\{\nabla f(\boldsymbol{x}_{n})+\rho\boldsymbol{D}^{t}[\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{x}_{n}-\mathcal{P}(\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{x}_{n})]\Big\},

where
\boldsymbol{H}_{n}=d^{2}f(\boldsymbol{x}_{n})
is the Hessian. To enforce the descent property, it is often prudent to substitute a positive definite approximation
\boldsymbol{H}_{n}
for
d^{2}f(\boldsymbol{x}_{n})
. In statistical applications, the expected information matrix is a natural substitute. It is also crucial to retain as much curvature information on
f(\boldsymbol{x})
as possible. Newton’s method has two drawbacks. First, it is necessary to compute and store
d^{2}f(\boldsymbol{x}_{n})
. This is mitigated in statistical applications by the substitution just mentioned. Second, there is the necessity of solving a large linear system. Fortunately, the matrix
\boldsymbol{H}_{n}+\rho\boldsymbol{D}^{t}\boldsymbol{D}
is often well-conditioned, for example, when
\boldsymbol{D}
has full column rank and
\boldsymbol{D}^{t}\boldsymbol{D}
is positive definite. The method of conjugate gradients can be called on to solve the linear system in this ideal circumstance.

To reduce the condition number of the matrix
\boldsymbol{H}_{n}+\rho\boldsymbol{D}^{t}\boldsymbol{D}
even further, one can sometimes rephrase the Newton step as iteratively reweighted least squares. For instance, in a generalized linear model, the gradient
\nabla f(\boldsymbol{x})
and the expected information
\boldsymbol{H}
can be written as

\displaystyle\nabla f(\boldsymbol{x})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\boldsymbol{Z}^{t}\boldsymbol{W}^{1/2}\boldsymbol{r}\quad\text{and}\quad\boldsymbol{H}\mathop{\;\>}\nolimits=\mathop{\;\>}\nolimits\boldsymbol{Z}^{t}\boldsymbol{W}\boldsymbol{Z},

where
\boldsymbol{r}
is a vector of standardized residuals,
\boldsymbol{Z}
is a design matrix, and
\boldsymbol{W}
is a diagonal matrix of case weights
(
Lange 2010
;
Nelder and Wedderburn 1972
)
. The Newton step is now equivalent to minimizing the least squares criterion

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{x}^{*}\boldsymbol{H}_{n}\boldsymbol{x}-\nabla f(\boldsymbol{x}_{n})^{*}\boldsymbol{x}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\|\boldsymbol{W}_{n}^{1/2}\boldsymbol{Z}\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{W}_{n}^{-1/2}\nabla f(\boldsymbol{x}_{n})\|^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\|\begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{W}_{n}^{1/2}\boldsymbol{Z}\\
\sqrt{\rho}\boldsymbol{D}\end{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{x}-\begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{W}_{n}^{1/2}\boldsymbol{Z}\boldsymbol{x}_{n}+\boldsymbol{r}_{n}\\
\sqrt{\rho}\mathcal{P}(\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{x}_{n})\end{pmatrix}\right\|^{2}.

In this context a version of the conjugate gradient algorithm adapted to least squares is attractive. The algorithms LSQR
(
Paige and Saunders 1982
)
and LSMR
(
Fong and Saunders 2011
)
perform well when the design is sparse or ill conditioning is an issue.

2.2
Proximal Distance by Steepest Descent

In high-dimensional optimization problems, gradient descent is typically employed to avoid matrix inversion. Determination of an appropriate step length is now a primary concern. In the presence of fusion constraints
\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{x}\in S
and a convex quadratic loss
f(\boldsymbol{x})=\frac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{x}^{t}\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}+\boldsymbol{b}^{t}\boldsymbol{x}
, the gradient of the proximal distance objective at
\boldsymbol{x}_{n}
amounts to

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{v}_{n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{A}_{n}\boldsymbol{x}+\boldsymbol{b}+\rho\boldsymbol{D}^{t}[\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{x}_{n}-\mathcal{P}(\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{x}_{n})].

For the steepest descent update
\boldsymbol{x}_{n+1}=\boldsymbol{x}_{n}-t_{n}\boldsymbol{v}_{n}
, one can show that the optimal step length is

\displaystyle t_{n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\|\boldsymbol{v}_{n}\|^{2}}{\boldsymbol{v}_{n}^{t}\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{v}_{n}+\rho\|\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{v}_{n}\|^{2}}.

This update obeys the descent property and avoids matrix inversion. One can also substitute a local convex quadratic approximation around
\boldsymbol{x}_{n}
for
f(\boldsymbol{x})
. If the approximation majorizes
f(\boldsymbol{x})
, then the descent property is preserved. In the failure of majorization, the safeguard of step halving is trivial to implement.

In addition to Nesterov acceleration, gradient descent can be accelerated by the subspace MM technique
(
Chouzenoux et al. 2010
)
. Let
\boldsymbol{G}_{n}
be the matrix with
k
columns determined by the
k
most current gradients of the objective
h_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{x})
, including
\nabla h_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{x}_{n})
. Generalizing our previous assumption, suppose
f(\boldsymbol{x})
has a quadratic surrogate with Hessian
\boldsymbol{H}_{n}
at
\boldsymbol{x}_{n}
. Overall we get the quadratic surrogate

\displaystyle q_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{x}\mid\boldsymbol{x}_{n})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle g_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{x}_{n}\mid\boldsymbol{x}_{n})+\nabla g_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{x}_{n}\mid\boldsymbol{x}_{n})^{t}(\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{x}_{n})

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}(\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{x}_{n})^{t}(\boldsymbol{H}_{n}+\rho\boldsymbol{D}^{t}\boldsymbol{D})(\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{x}_{n})

of
g_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{x}\mid\boldsymbol{x}_{n})
. We now seek the best linear perturbation
\boldsymbol{x}_{n}+\boldsymbol{G}_{n}\boldsymbol{\beta}
of
\boldsymbol{x}_{n}
by minimizing
q_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{x}_{n}+\boldsymbol{G}_{n}\boldsymbol{\beta}\mid\boldsymbol{x}_{n})
with respect to the coefficient vector
\boldsymbol{\beta}
. To achieve this end, we solve the stationary equation

\displaystyle{\bf 0}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{G}_{n}^{t}\nabla g_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{x}_{n}\mid\boldsymbol{x}_{n})+\boldsymbol{G}_{n}^{t}(\boldsymbol{H}_{n}+\rho\boldsymbol{D}^{t}\boldsymbol{D})\boldsymbol{G}_{n}\boldsymbol{\beta}

and find
\boldsymbol{\beta}=-[\boldsymbol{G}_{n}^{t}(\boldsymbol{H}_{n}+\rho\boldsymbol{D}^{t}\boldsymbol{D})\boldsymbol{G}_{n}]^{-1}\boldsymbol{G}_{n}^{t}\nabla g_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{x}_{n}\mid\boldsymbol{x}_{n})
, where the gradient is

\displaystyle\nabla g_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{x}_{n}\mid\boldsymbol{x}_{n})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\nabla h_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{x}_{n})\mathop{\;\>}\nolimits=\mathop{\;\>}\nolimits\nabla f(\boldsymbol{x}_{n})+\rho\boldsymbol{D}^{t}[\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{x}_{n}-\mathcal{P}(\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{x}_{n})].

The indicated matrix inverse is just
k\times k
.

2.3
ADMM

ADMM (alternating direction method of multipliers) is a natural competitor to the proximal distance algorithms just described
(
Hong et al. 2016
)
. ADMM is designed to minimize functions of the form
f(\boldsymbol{x})+g(\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{x})
subject to
\boldsymbol{x}\in C
, where
C
is closed and convex. Splitting variables leads to the revised objective
f(\boldsymbol{x})+g(\boldsymbol{y})
subject to
\boldsymbol{x}\in C
and
\boldsymbol{y}=\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{x}
. ADMM invokes the augmented Lagrangian

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{\mu}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y},\boldsymbol{\lambda})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle f(\boldsymbol{x})+g(\boldsymbol{y})+\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{t}(\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{y})+\frac{\mu}{2}\|\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{y}\|^{2}

with Lagrange multiplier
\boldsymbol{\lambda}
and step length
\mu>0
. At iteration
n+1
of ADMM one calculates successively

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{x}_{n+1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathop{\rm argmin}\nolimits_{\boldsymbol{x}\in C}\Big[f(\boldsymbol{x})+\frac{\mu}{2}\|\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{y}_{n}+\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{n}\|^{2}\Big]

(4)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{y}_{n+1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathop{\rm argmin}\nolimits_{\boldsymbol{y}}\Big[g(\boldsymbol{y})+\frac{\mu}{2}\|\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{x}_{n+1}-\boldsymbol{y}+\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{n}\|^{2}\Big]

(5)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{n+1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{n}+\mu(\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{x}_{n+1}-\boldsymbol{y}_{n+1}).

(6)

Update (
4
) succumbs to Newton’s
method when
f(\boldsymbol{x})
is smooth and
C=\mathbb{R}^{p}
, and update (
5
)
succumbs to a proximal map of
g(\boldsymbol{y})
.
Update (
6
) of the Lagrange multiplier
\boldsymbol{\lambda}
amounts to steepest ascent on the dual function. A standard extension to the scheme in (
4
) through (
6
) is to vary the step length
\mu
by considering the magnitude of residuals
(
Boyd et al. 2011
)
.
For example, letting
\boldsymbol{r}_{n}=\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{y}
and
\boldsymbol{s}_{n}=\mu\boldsymbol{D}^{t}(\boldsymbol{y}_{n-1}-\boldsymbol{y}_{n})
denote primal and dual residuals at iteration
n
, we make use of the heuristic

\displaystyle\mu_{n+1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\begin{cases}2~\mu_{n},&\text{if}~\|\boldsymbol{r}_{n}\|/\|\boldsymbol{s}_{n}\|>10\\
\mu_{n}/2,&\text{if}~\|\boldsymbol{r}_{n}\|/\|\boldsymbol{s}_{n}\|<10\\
\mu_{n},&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

which (a) keeps the primal and dual residuals within an order of magnitude of each other, (b) makes ADMM less sensitive to the choice of step length, and (c) improves convergence.

Our problem conforms to the ADMM paradigm when
S
is equal to the Cartesian product
\prod_{i=1}^{r}S_{i}
and

g(\boldsymbol{y})=\frac{\rho}{2}\mathop{\rm dist}\nolimits(\boldsymbol{y},S)^{2}
. Fortunately, the
\boldsymbol{y}
update (
5
) reduces to a simple formula
(
Bauschke and Combettes 2017
)
. To derive this formula, note that the proximal map
\boldsymbol{y}=\mathop{\rm prox}\nolimits_{\alpha g}(\boldsymbol{z})
satisfies the stationary condition

\displaystyle{\bf 0}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{y}-\boldsymbol{z}+\alpha[\boldsymbol{y}-\mathcal{P}(\boldsymbol{y})]

for any
\boldsymbol{z}
, including
\boldsymbol{z}=\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{x}_{n+1}+\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{n}
, and any
\alpha
, including
\alpha=\rho/\mu
. Since the projection map
\mathcal{P}(\boldsymbol{y})
has the constant value
\mathcal{P}(\boldsymbol{z})
on the line segment
[\boldsymbol{z},\mathcal{P}(\boldsymbol{z})]
, the value

\displaystyle\mathop{\rm prox}\nolimits_{\alpha g}(\boldsymbol{z})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\alpha}{1+\alpha}\mathcal{P}(\boldsymbol{z})+\frac{1}{1+\alpha}\boldsymbol{z}

satisfies the stationary condition. Because the explicit update (
5
) for
\boldsymbol{y}
decreases the Lagrangian even when
S
is nonconvex, we will employ it generally.

The
\boldsymbol{x}
update (
4
) is given by the proximal map
\mathop{\rm prox}\nolimits_{\mu^{-1}f}(\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{n}-\boldsymbol{y}_{n})
when
S=\mathbb{R}^{p}
and
\boldsymbol{D}=\boldsymbol{I}
. Otherwise, the update of
\boldsymbol{x}
is more problematic. Assuming
f(\boldsymbol{x})
is smooth and
S=\mathbb{R}^{p}
, Newton’s method gives the approximate update

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{x}_{n+1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{x}_{n}-\Big[d^{2}f(\boldsymbol{x}_{n})+\mu\boldsymbol{D}^{t}\boldsymbol{D}\Big]^{-1}\Big[\nabla f(\boldsymbol{x}_{n})+\mu\boldsymbol{D}^{t}(\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{x}_{n}-\boldsymbol{y}_{n}+\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{n})\Big].

Our earlier suggestion of replacing
d^{2}f(\boldsymbol{x}_{n})
by a positive definite approximation also applies here. Let us emphasize that ADMM eliminates the need for distance majorization. Although distance majorization is convenient, it is not necessarily a tight majorization. Thus, one can hope to see gains in rates of convergence. Balanced against this positive is the fact that ADMM is often slow to converge to high accuracy.

2.4
Proximal Distance Iteration

We conclude this section by describing proximal distance algorithms in pseudocode. As our theoretical results will soon illustrate, the choice of penalty parameter
\rho
is tied to the convergence rate of any proximal distance algorithm. Unfortunately, a large value for
\rho
is necessary for the iterates to converge to the constraint set
S
.
We ameliorate this issue by slowly sending
\rho\to\infty
according to annealing schedules from the family of geometric progressions
\rho(t)=r^{t-1}
with
t\geq 1
. Here we parameterize the family by an initial value
\rho=1
and a multiplier
r>1
. Thus, our methods approximate solutions to
\min f(\boldsymbol{x})
subject to
\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{x}\in S
by solving a sequence of increasingly penalized subproblems,
\min_{\boldsymbol{x}}h_{\rho(t)}(\boldsymbol{x})
.
In practice we can only solve a finite number of subproblems so we prescribe the following convergence criteria

\displaystyle\|\nabla h_{\rho(t)}(\boldsymbol{x}_{n})\|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\delta_{h},

(7)

\displaystyle\mathop{\rm dist}\nolimits(\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{x}_{t},S)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\delta_{d},\text{ or }

(8)

\displaystyle|\mathop{\rm dist}\nolimits(\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{x}_{t},S)-\mathop{\rm dist}\nolimits(\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{x}_{t-1},S)|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\delta_{q}[\mathop{\rm dist}\nolimits(\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{x}_{t-1},S)+1]

(9)

Condition (
7
) is a guarantee that a solution estimate
\boldsymbol{x}_{n}
is close to a stationary point after
n
inner iterations for the fixed value of
\rho=\rho(t)
. In conditions (
8
) and
(
9
), the vector
\boldsymbol{x}_{t}
denotes the
\delta_{h}
-optimal solution estimate once condition (
7
) is satisfied for a particular subproblem along the annealing path.
Condition (
8
) is a guarantee that solutions along the annealing path adhere to the fusion constraints at level
\delta_{d}
.
In general, condition (
8
) can only be satisfied for large values of
\rho(t)
. Finally, condition (
9
) is used to terminate the annealing process if the relative progress made in decreasing the distance penalty becomes too small as measured by
\delta_{q}
. Algorithm
1
summarizes the flow of proximal distance iteration, which uses Nesterov acceleration in inner iterations. Warm starts in solving subsequent subproblem are implicit in our formulation.

Algorithm 1

Proximal Distance Iteration

1:

Set tolerances
\delta_{h},\delta_{d},\delta_{q}
, and annealing schedule
\{\rho(t)\}_{t\geq 1}
.

2:

Set maximum outer iterations
i_{\mathrm{outer}}
and maximum inner iterations
i_{\mathrm{inner}}
.

3:

Set threshold for Nesterov acceleration,
i_{\mathrm{Nesterov}}
.

4:

Initialize
\boldsymbol{x}
and
q_{0}\leftarrow\mathop{\rm dist}\nolimits(\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{x},S)
.

5:

Initialize counter for Nesterov acceleration
i\leftarrow 1
.

6:

Convergence Analysis: Convex Case

4
Convergence Analysis: General Case

We now depart the comfortable confines of convexity. Let us first review the notion of a Fréchet subdifferential
(
Kruger 2003
)
. If
h(\boldsymbol{x})
is a function mapping
\mathbb{R}^{p}
into
\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
, then its Fréchet subdifferential at
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathop{\rm dom}\nolimits f
is defined as

\displaystyle\partial^{F}h(\boldsymbol{x})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Big\{\boldsymbol{v}:\liminf_{\boldsymbol{y}\to\boldsymbol{x}}\frac{h(\boldsymbol{y})-h(\boldsymbol{x})-\boldsymbol{v}^{t}(\boldsymbol{y}-\boldsymbol{x})}{\|\boldsymbol{y}-\boldsymbol{x}\|}\geq 0\Big\}.

The set
\partial^{F}h(\boldsymbol{x})
is closed, convex, and possibly empty. If
h(\boldsymbol{x})
is convex, then
\partial^{F}h(\boldsymbol{x})
reduces to its convex subdifferential. If
h(\boldsymbol{x})
is differentiable, then
\partial^{F}h(\boldsymbol{x})
reduces to its ordinary differential. At a local minimum
\boldsymbol{x}
, Fermat’s rule
{\bf 0}\in\partial^{F}h(\boldsymbol{x})
holds. For a locally Lipschitz and directionally differentiable function, the Fréchet subdifferential becomes

\displaystyle\partial^{F}h(\boldsymbol{x})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Big\{\boldsymbol{v}:d_{\boldsymbol{u}}h(\boldsymbol{x})\geq\boldsymbol{v}^{t}\boldsymbol{u}\;\text{for all directions}\;\boldsymbol{u}\Big\}.

Here
d_{\boldsymbol{u}}h(\boldsymbol{x})
is the directional derivative of
h(\boldsymbol{x})
at
\boldsymbol{x}
in the direction
\boldsymbol{u}
. This result makes it clear that at a critical point, all directional derivatives are flat or point uphill.

We will also need some notions from algebraic geometry
(
Bochnak et al. 2013
)
.
For simplicity we focus on the class of semialgebraic functions and the corresponding class of semialgebraic subsets of
\mathbb{R}^{p}
. The latter is the smallest class that:

(a)

contains all sets of the form

\{\boldsymbol{x}:q(\boldsymbol{x})>0\}
for a polynomial
q(\boldsymbol{x})
in
p
variables,

(b)

is closed under the formation of finite unions, finite intersections, set complementation, and Cartesian products.

A function
a:\mathbb{R}^{p}\mapsto\mathbb{R}^{r}
is said to be semialgebraic if its graph is a semialgebraic set of
\mathbb{R}^{p+r}
. The class of real-valued semialgebraic functions contains all polynomials
p(\boldsymbol{x})
and all
0
/
1
indicators of algebraic sets. It is closed under the formation of sums and products and therefore constitutes a commutative ring with identity. The class is also closed under the formation of absolute values, reciprocals when
a(\boldsymbol{x})\neq 0
,
n
th roots when
a(\boldsymbol{x})\geq 0
, and maxima
\max\{a(\boldsymbol{x}),b(\boldsymbol{x})\}
and minima
\min\{a(\boldsymbol{x}),b(\boldsymbol{x})\}
. Finally, the composition of two semialgebraic functions is semialgebraic.

For our purposes it is crucial that the Euclidean distance
\mathop{\rm dist}\nolimits(\boldsymbol{x},S)
to a semialgebraic set
S
is a semialgebraic function. In view of the closure properties of such functions, the function
\frac{\rho}{2}\mathop{\rm dist}\nolimits(\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{x},S)^{2}
is also semialgebraic. Sets such as the nonnegative orthant
\mathbb{R}^{p}_{+}
and the unit sphere
S^{p-1}
are semialgebraic. The next proposition buttresses several of our numerical examples.

Proposition 4.1
.

The order statistics of a finite set
\{f_{i}(\boldsymbol{x})\}_{i=1}^{n}
of semialgebraic functions are semialgebraic. Hence, sparsity sets are semialgebraic.

The next proposition is an elaboration and expansion of known results
(
Attouch et al. 2010
;
Bolte et al. 2007
;
Cui et al. 2018
;
Kang et al. 2015
;
Le Thi et al. 2018
)
and was featured in our previous paper
(
Keys et al. 2019
)
.

Proposition 4.2
.

In an MM algorithm suppose the objective
h(\boldsymbol{x})
is coercive, continuous, and subanalytic and all surrogates
g(\boldsymbol{x}\mid\boldsymbol{x}_{n})
are continuous,
\mu
-strongly convex, and satisfy the Lipschitz condition

\displaystyle\|\nabla g(\boldsymbol{a}\mid\boldsymbol{x}_{n})-\nabla g(\boldsymbol{b}\mid\boldsymbol{x}_{n})\|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle L\|\boldsymbol{a}-\boldsymbol{b}\|

on the compact set
\{\boldsymbol{x}:h(\boldsymbol{x})\leq h(\boldsymbol{x}_{0})\}
. Then the MM iterates

\boldsymbol{x}_{n+1}=\mathop{\rm argmin}\nolimits_{\boldsymbol{x}}g(\boldsymbol{x}\mid\boldsymbol{x}_{n})
converge to a stationary point.

Proposition
4.2
applies to proximal distance algorithms under the right hypotheses. Note that semialgebraic sets and functions are automatically subanalytic.
Before stating a precise result, let us clarify the nature of the Fréchet subdifferential in the current setting. This entity is determined by the identity

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\mathop{\rm dist}\nolimits(\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{x},S)^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\min_{\boldsymbol{z}\in S}\|\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{z}\|^{2},

Danskin’s theorem yields the directional derivative

\displaystyle d_{\boldsymbol{v}}\frac{1}{2}\mathop{\rm dist}\nolimits(\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{x},S)^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\min_{\boldsymbol{z}\in S(\boldsymbol{x})}(\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{z})^{t}\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{v},

where
S(\boldsymbol{x})
is the solution set where the minimum is attained. The Frechet differential

\displaystyle\partial^{F}h_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{x})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\nabla f(\boldsymbol{x})+\rho\{\boldsymbol{u}:(\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{z})^{t}\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{v}\geq\boldsymbol{u}^{t}\boldsymbol{v},\,\boldsymbol{z}\in S(\boldsymbol{x})\;\text{and all}\;\boldsymbol{v}\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\nabla f(\boldsymbol{x})+\rho\{\boldsymbol{u}:\boldsymbol{u}=\boldsymbol{D}^{t}(\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{z}),\,\boldsymbol{z}\in S(\boldsymbol{x})\}

holds owing to Corollary 1.12.2 and Proposition 1.17 of
(
Kruger 2003
)
since
\mathop{\rm dist}\nolimits(\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{x},S)^{2}
is locally Lipshitz. The latter fact follows from the identity
a^{2}-b^{2}=(a+b)(a-b)
with
a=\mathop{\rm dist}\nolimits(\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{y},S)^{2}
and
b=\mathop{\rm dist}\nolimits(\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{x},S)^{2}
, given that
\mathop{\rm dist}\nolimits(\boldsymbol{w},S)
is Lipschitz and bounded on bounded sets.

In any event, a stationary point
\boldsymbol{x}
satisfies
\boldsymbol{0}=\nabla f(\boldsymbol{x})+\rho\boldsymbol{D}^{t}(\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{z})
for all
\boldsymbol{z}\in S(\boldsymbol{x})
. As we expect, the stationary condition is necessary for
\boldsymbol{x}
to furnish a global minimum. Indeed, if it fails, take
\boldsymbol{z}\in S(\boldsymbol{x})
with surrogate satisfying
\nabla g_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{x}\mid\boldsymbol{x})\neq\boldsymbol{0}
. Then the negative gradient
-\nabla g_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{x}\mid\boldsymbol{x})
is a descent direction for
g_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{x}\mid\boldsymbol{x})
, which majorizes
h_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{x})
. Hence,
-\nabla g_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{x}\mid\boldsymbol{x})
is also a descent direction for
h_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{x})
. This conclusion is inconsistent with
\boldsymbol{x}
being a local minimum of the objective.

The next proposition proves convergence for a wide class of fused models.

Proposition 4.3
.

Suppose in our proximal distance setting that
\rho
is sufficiently large, the closed constraint sets
S_{i}
and the loss
f(\boldsymbol{x})
are semialgebraic, and
f(\boldsymbol{x})
is differentiable with a locally Lipschitz gradient. Under the coercive assumption made in Proposition
3.2
, the proximal distance iterates
\boldsymbol{x}_{n}
converge to a stationary point of the objective
h_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{x})
.

For sparsity constrained problems, one can establish a linear rate of convergence under the right hypotheses.

Proposition 4.4
.

Suppose in our proximal distance setting that
\rho
is sufficiently large, the constraint set is a sparsity
set
S_{k}
, and the loss
f(\boldsymbol{x})
is semialgebraic, strongly convex, and possesses a Lipschitz gradient. Then the proximal distance iterates
\boldsymbol{x}_{n}
converge linearly to a stationary point
\boldsymbol{x}_{\infty}
provided
\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{x}_{\infty}
has
k
unambiguous largest components in magnitude. When the rows of
\boldsymbol{D}
are unique, the complementary set of points
\boldsymbol{x}
where
\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{x}
has
k
ambiguous largest components in magnitude has Lebesgue measure
0
.

5
Numerical Examples

This section considers five concrete examples of constrained optimization amenable to distance majorization with fusion constraints, with
\boldsymbol{D}
denoting the fusion matrix in each problem. In each case, the loss function is both strongly convex and differentiable. The specific examples that we consider are the metric projection problem, convex regression, convex clustering, image denoising with a total variation penalty, and projection of a matrix to one with a better condition number. Each example is notable for the large number of fusion constraints and projections to convex constraint sets, except in convex clustering.
In convex clustering we encounter a sparsity constraint set.
Quadratic loss models feature prominently in our examples.
Interested readers may consult our previous work for nonconvex examples with
\boldsymbol{D}=\boldsymbol{I}

(
Keys et al. 2019
;
Xu et al. 2017
)
.

5.1
Mathematical Descriptions

Here we provide the mathematical details for each example.

5.1.1
Metric Projection

Solutions to the metric projection problem restore transitivity to noisy distance data for the
m
nodes of a graph
(
Brickell et al. 2008
;
Sra et al. 2005
)
. The data are encoded in an
m\times m
dissimilarity matrix
\boldsymbol{Y}=(y_{ij})
with nonnegative weights in the matrix
\boldsymbol{W}=(w_{ij})
. The metric projection
problem requires finding the symmetric semi-metric
\boldsymbol{X}=(x_{ij})
minimizing

\displaystyle f(\boldsymbol{X})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i>j}w_{ij}(x_{ij}-y_{ij})^{2}

subject to all
\binom{m}{2}
nonnegativity constraints
x_{ij}\geq 0
and all
3\binom{m}{3}
triangle inequality constraints
x_{ij}-x_{ik}-x_{kj}\leq 0
. The diagonal entries of
\boldsymbol{Y}
,
\boldsymbol{W}
, and
\boldsymbol{X}
are zero by definition. The fusion matrix
\boldsymbol{D}
has
\binom{m}{2}+3\binom{m}{3}
rows, and the projected value of
\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{X}
must fall in the set
S
of symmetric matrices satisfying all pertinent constraints.

One can simplify the required projection by stacking the nonredundant entries along each successive column of
\boldsymbol{X}
to create a vector
\boldsymbol{x}
with
\binom{m}{2}
entries. This captures the lower triangle of
\boldsymbol{X}
. The sparse matrix
\boldsymbol{D}
is correspondingly redefined to be
[\binom{m}{2}+3\binom{m}{3}]\times\binom{m}{2}
. These maneuvers simplfy constraints to
\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{x}\geq{\bf 0}
, and projection involves sending each entry
u
of
\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{x}
to
\max\{0,u\}
.
Putting everything together, the objective to minimize is

\displaystyle h_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{x})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\|\boldsymbol{W}^{1/2}(\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{y})\|^{2}_{2}+\frac{\rho}{2}\mathop{\rm dist}\nolimits(\boldsymbol{T}\boldsymbol{x},\mathbb{R}_{+}^{p_{1}})^{2}+\frac{\rho}{2}\mathop{\rm dist}\nolimits(\boldsymbol{x},\mathbb{R}_{+}^{p_{2}})^{2},

where
\boldsymbol{D}
consists of blocks
\boldsymbol{T}
and
\boldsymbol{I}_{p_{2}}
and
p_{1}
and
p_{2}
count the number of triangle inequality and nonnegativity constraints, respectively. The linear system
(\boldsymbol{I}+\rho\boldsymbol{D}^{t}\boldsymbol{D})\boldsymbol{x}=\boldsymbol{b}
appears in both the MM and ADMM updates for
\boldsymbol{x}_{n}
. Application of the Woodbury and Sherman-Morrison formulas yield an exact solution to the linear system and allow one to forgo iterative methods.
The interested reader may consult Appendix
B
for further details.

5.1.2
Convex Regression

Convex regression is a nonparametric method for estimating a regression function under shape constraints. Given
m
responses
y_{i}
and corresponding predictors
\boldsymbol{x}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the goal is to find the convex function
\psi(\boldsymbol{x})
minimizing the sum of squares
\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{m}[y_{i}-\psi(\boldsymbol{x}_{i})]^{2}
. Asymptotic and finite sample properties of this convex estimator have been described in detail by
Seijo and Sen 2011
. The convex regression program can be restated as the finite dimensional problem of finding the value
\theta_{i}
and subgradient

\boldsymbol{\xi}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
of
\psi(\boldsymbol{x})
at each sample point
(y_{i},\boldsymbol{x}_{i})
. Convexity imposes the supporting hyperplane constraint
\theta_{j}+\boldsymbol{\xi}_{j}^{t}(\boldsymbol{x}_{i}-\boldsymbol{x}_{j})\leq\theta_{i}
for each pair
i\neq j
. Thus, the problem becomes one of minimizing
\frac{1}{2}\|\boldsymbol{y}-\boldsymbol{\theta}\|^{2}

subject to these
m(m-1)
inequality constraints. In the proximal distance framework, we must minimize

\displaystyle h_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\Xi})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\|\boldsymbol{y}-\boldsymbol{\theta}\|^{2}+\frac{\rho}{2}\mathop{\rm dist}\nolimits(\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{\theta}+\boldsymbol{B}\boldsymbol{\Xi},\mathbb{R}_{-}^{m(m-1)})^{2},

where
\boldsymbol{D}=[\boldsymbol{A}~\boldsymbol{B}]
encodes the required fusion matrix. The reader may consult Appendix
C
for a description of each algorithm map.

5.1.3
Convex Clustering

Convex clustering of
m
samples based on
d
features can be formulated in terms of the regularized objective

\displaystyle F_{\gamma}(\boldsymbol{U})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\|\boldsymbol{u}_{i}-\boldsymbol{x}_{i}\|^{2}+\gamma\sum_{i>j}w_{ij}\|\boldsymbol{u}_{i}-\boldsymbol{u}_{j}\|,

based on columns
\boldsymbol{x}_{i}
and
\boldsymbol{u}_{i}
of
\boldsymbol{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times m}
and
\boldsymbol{U}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times m}
, respectively.
Here each
\boldsymbol{x}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a sample feature vector and the corresponding
\boldsymbol{u}_{i}
represents its centroid assignment.
The predetermined weights
w_{ij}
have a graphical interpretation under which similar samples have positive edge weights
w_{ij}
and distant samples have
0
edge weights. The edge weights are chosen by the user to guide the clustering process. In general, minimization of
F_{\gamma}(\boldsymbol{U})
separates over the connected components of the graph. To allow all sample points to coalesce into a single cluster, we assume that the underlying graph is connected. The regularization parameter
\gamma>0
tunes the number of clusters in a nonlinear fashion and potentially captures hierarchical information. Previous work establishes that the solution path
\boldsymbol{U}(\gamma)
varies continuously with respect to
\gamma

(
Chi and Lange 2015
)
. Unfortunately, there is no explicit way to determine the number of clusters entailed by a particular value of
\gamma
.

Alternatively, we can attack the problem using sparsity and distance majorization.
Consider the penalized objective

\displaystyle h_{\rho,k}(\boldsymbol{U})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\|\boldsymbol{U}-\boldsymbol{X}\|_{F}^{2}+\frac{\rho}{2}\mathop{\rm dist}\nolimits(\boldsymbol{U}\boldsymbol{D},S_{k})^{2}.

The fusion matrix
\boldsymbol{D}
has
\binom{m}{2}
columns
w_{ij}(\boldsymbol{e}_{i}-\boldsymbol{e}_{j})
and serves to map the centroid matrix
\boldsymbol{U}
to a
d\times\binom{m}{2}
matrix
\boldsymbol{V}
encoding the weighted differences

w_{ij}(\boldsymbol{u}_{i}-\boldsymbol{u}_{j})
. The members of the sparsity set
S_{k}
are
d\times\binom{m}{2}
matrices with at most
k
non-zero columns. Projection of
\boldsymbol{U}\boldsymbol{D}
onto the closed set
S_{k}
forces some centroid assignments to coalesce, and is straightforward to implement by sorting the Euclidean lengths of the columns of
\boldsymbol{U}\boldsymbol{D}
and sending to
\bf 0
all but the
k
most dominant columns. Ties are broken arbitrarily.

Our sparsity-based method trades the continuous penalty parameter
\gamma>0
in the previous formulation for an integer sparsity index
k\in\{0,1,2,\ldots,\binom{m}{2}\}
. For example with
k=0
, all differences
\boldsymbol{u}_{i}-\boldsymbol{u}_{j}
are coerced to
\bf 0
, and all sample points cluster together. The other extreme
k=\binom{m}{2}
assigns each point to its own cluster. The size of the matrices
\boldsymbol{D}
and
\boldsymbol{U}\boldsymbol{D}
can be reduced by
discarding column pairs corresponding to
0
weights.
Appendix
D
describes the projection onto sparsity sets and provides further details.

5.1.4
Total Variation Image Denoising

To approximate an image
\boldsymbol{U}
from a noisy input
\boldsymbol{W}
matrix,
Rudin et al. 1992

regularize a loss function
f(\boldsymbol{U})
by a total variation (TV) penalty. After discretizing the problem, the least squares loss leads to the objective

\displaystyle F_{\gamma}(\boldsymbol{U})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i,j}(U_{i,j}-W_{i,j})^{2}+\gamma\sum_{i,j}\sqrt{(U_{i+1,j}-U_{i,j})^{2}+(U_{i,j+1}-U_{i,j})^{2}},

where
\boldsymbol{U},\boldsymbol{W}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times p}
are rectangular monochromatic images and
\gamma
controls the strength of regularization. The anisotropic norm

\displaystyle\mathrm{TV}_{1}(\boldsymbol{U})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i,j}|U_{i+1,j}-U_{i,j}|+|U_{i,j+1}-U_{i,j}|\mathop{\;\>}\nolimits=\mathop{\;\>}\nolimits\|\boldsymbol{D}_{m}\boldsymbol{U}\|_{1}+\|\boldsymbol{U}\boldsymbol{D}_{p}^{t}\|_{1}

is often preferred because it induces sparsity in the differences. Here
\boldsymbol{D}_{p}
is the forward difference operator on
p
data points. Stacking the columns of
\boldsymbol{U}
into a vector
\boldsymbol{u}=\mathop{\rm vec}\nolimits(\boldsymbol{U})

allows one to identify a fusion matrix
\boldsymbol{D}
and write
\mathrm{TV}_{1}(\boldsymbol{U})
compactly as
\mathrm{TV}_{1}(\boldsymbol{u})=\|\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{u}\|_{1}
. In this context we reformulate the denoising problem
as minimizing
f(\boldsymbol{u})
subject to the set constraint
\|\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{u}\|_{1}\leq\gamma
. This revised formulation directly quantifies the quality of a solution in terms of its total variation and brings into play fast pivot-based algorithms for projecting onto multiples of the
\ell_{1}
unit ball
(
Condat 2016
)
.
Appendix
E
provides descriptions of each algorithm.

5.1.5
Projection of a Matrix to a Good Condition Number

Consider an
m\times p
matrix
\boldsymbol{M}
with
m\geq p
and full singular value decomposition
\boldsymbol{M}=\boldsymbol{U}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\boldsymbol{V}^{t}
. The condition number of
\boldsymbol{M}
is the ratio
\sigma_{\rm\max}/\sigma_{\rm min}
of the largest to the smallest singular value of
\boldsymbol{M}
. We denote the diagonal of
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}
as
\boldsymbol{\sigma}
. Owing to the von Neumann-Fan inequality, the closest matrix
\boldsymbol{N}
to
\boldsymbol{M}
in the Frobenius norm has the singular value decomposition
\boldsymbol{N}=\boldsymbol{U}\boldsymbol{X}\boldsymbol{V}^{t}
, where the diagonal
\boldsymbol{x}
of
\boldsymbol{X}
satisfies inequalities pertinent to a decent condition number
(
Borwein and Lewis 2010
)
. Suppose
c\geq 1
is the maximum condition number. Then every pair
(x_{i},x_{j})
satisfies
x_{i}-cx_{j}\leq 0
. Note that
x_{i}-cx_{i}>0
if and only if
x_{i}<0
. Thus, nonnegativity of the entries of
\boldsymbol{x}
is enforced. The proximal distance approach to the condition number projection problem invokes the objective and majorization

\displaystyle h_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{x})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\|\boldsymbol{\sigma}-\boldsymbol{x}\|^{2}+\frac{\rho}{2}\sum_{(i,j)}\mathop{\rm dist}\nolimits(x_{i}-cx_{j},\mathbb{R}_{-})^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\|\boldsymbol{\sigma}-\boldsymbol{x}\|^{2}+\frac{\rho}{2}\sum_{(i,j)}(x_{i}-cx_{j})_{+}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\|\boldsymbol{\sigma}-\boldsymbol{x}\|^{2}+\frac{\rho}{2}\sum_{(i,j)}(x_{i}-cx_{j}-q_{nij})^{2}

at iteration
n
, where
q_{nij}=\min\{x_{ni}-cx_{nj},0\}
. We can write the majorization more concisely as

\displaystyle h_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{x})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\|\boldsymbol{A}_{\rho}\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{r}_{n}\|^{2},\quad\boldsymbol{A}_{\rho}\mathop{\;\>}\nolimits=\mathop{\;\>}\nolimits\begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{I}_{p}\\
\sqrt{\rho}\boldsymbol{D}\end{pmatrix},\quad\boldsymbol{r}_{n}\mathop{\;\>}\nolimits=\mathop{\;\>}\nolimits\begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{\sigma}\\
\sqrt{\rho}\mathop{\rm vec}\nolimits{\boldsymbol{Q}_{n}}\end{pmatrix},

where
\mathop{\rm vec}\nolimits{\boldsymbol{Q}_{n}}
stacks the columns of
\boldsymbol{Q}_{n}=(q_{nij})
and the
p^{2}\times p
fusion matrix
\boldsymbol{D}
satisfies
(\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{x})_{k}=x_{i}-cx_{j}
for each component
k
. The minimum of the surrogate occurs at the point
\boldsymbol{x}_{n+1}=(\boldsymbol{A}_{\rho}^{t}\boldsymbol{A}_{\rho})^{-1}\boldsymbol{A}_{\rho}^{t}\boldsymbol{r}_{n}
.
This linear system can be solved exactly. Appendix
F
provides additional details.

Search Heuristic

Because the number of clusters is usually unknown, we implement the search heuristic outlined in Algorithm

. The idea behind the heuristic is to gradually coerce clustering without exploring the full range of the hyperparameter

6
Discussion

We now recapitulate the main findings of our numerical experiments. Tables
2
through
6
show a consistent pattern of superior speed by the steepest descent (SD) version of the proximal distance algorithm. This is hardly surprising since unlike ADMM and MM, SD avoids solving a linear system at each iteration. SD’s speed advantage tends to persist even when the linear system can be solved exactly. The condition number example summarized in Table
6
is an exception to this rule. Here the MM updates leverage a very simple matrix inverse. MM is usually faster than ADMM. We attribute MM’s performance edge to the extra matrix-vector multiplications involving the fusion matrix
\boldsymbol{D}
required by ADMM. In fairness, ADMM closes the speed gap and matches MM on convex regression.

The choice of annealing schedule can strongly impact the quality of solutions. Intuitively, driving the gradient norm
\|\nabla h_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{x})\|
to nearly 0 for a given
\rho
keeps the proximal distance methods on the correct annealing path and yields better approximations. Provided the penalized objective is sufficiently smooth, one expects the solution
\boldsymbol{x}_{\rho}\approx\mathop{\rm argmin}\nolimits h_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{x})
to be close to the solution
\boldsymbol{x}_{\rho^{\prime}}\approx\mathop{\rm argmin}\nolimits h_{\rho^{\prime}}(\boldsymbol{x})
when the ratio
\rho^{\prime}/\rho>1
is not too large. Thus, choosing a conservative
\delta_{h}
for the convergence criterion
\|\nabla h_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{x})\|\leq\delta_{h}
may guard against a poorly specified annealing schedule.
Quantifying sensitivity of intermediate solutions
\boldsymbol{x}_{\rho}
with respect to
\rho
is key in avoiding an increase in inner iterations per subproblem; for example, as observed in Figure
1
. Given the success of our practical annealing recommendation to overcome the unfortunate coefficients in Propositions
3.3
and
3.4
, this topic merits further consideration in future work.

In practice, it is sometimes unnecessary to impose strict convergence criteria on the proximal distance iterates.
It is apparent that the convergence criteria on convex clustering and image denoising are quite lax compared to choices in other examples, specifically in terms of
\delta_{h}
.
Figure
1
suggests that most of the work in metric projection involves driving the distance penalty downhill rather than in fitting the loss. Surpisingly, Table
4
shows that our strict distance criterion
\delta_{d}=10^{-5}
in clustering is achieved. This implies
\mathop{\rm dist}\nolimits(\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{x},S)^{2}\leq 10^{-10}
on the selected solutions with
\rho\leq 10^{8}
, yet we only required
\|\nabla h_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{x})\|\leq 10^{-2}
on each subproblem. Indeed, not every problem may benefit from precise solution estimates. The image processing example underscores this point as we are able to recover denoised images with the choices
\delta_{h}=\delta_{d}=10^{-1}
. Problems where patterns or structure in solutions are of primary interest may stand to benefit from relaxed convergence criteria.

Our proximal distance method, as described in Algorithm
1
, enjoys several advantages. First, fusion constraints fit naturally in the proximal distance framework. Second, proximal distances enjoy the descent property. Third, there is a nearly optimal step size for gradient descent when second-order information is available on the loss. Fourth, proximal distance algorithms are competitive if not superior to ADMM on many problems. Fifth, proximal distance algorithms like iterative hard thresholding rely on set projection and are therefore helpful in dealing with hard sparsity constraints. The main disadvantages of the proximal distance methods are (a) the overall slow convergence due to the loss of curvature information on the distance penalty and (b) the need for a reasonable annealing schedule. In practice, a little experimentation can yield a reasonable schedule for an entire class of problems. Many competing methods are only capable of dealing with soft constraints imposed by the lasso and other convex penalties. To their detriment, soft constraints often entail severe parameter shrinkage and lead to an excess of false positives in model selection.

Throughout this manuscript we have stressed the flexibility of the proximal distance framework in dealing with a wide range of constraints as a major strength. From our point of view, proximal distance iteration adequately approximates feasible, locally optimal solutions to constrained optimization problems for well behaved constraint sets, for instance convex sets or semialgebraic sets. Combinatorially complex constraints or erratic loss functions can cause difficulties for the proximal distance methods. The quadratic distance penalty
\mathop{\rm dist}\nolimits(\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{x},S)^{2}
is usually not an issue, and projection onto the constraint should be fast. Poor loss functions may either lack second derivatives or may possess a prohibitively expensive and potentially ill-conditioned Hessian
d^{2}f(\boldsymbol{x})
. In this setting techniques such as coordinate descent and regularized and quasi-Newton methods are viable alternatives for minimizing the surrogate
g_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{x}\mid\boldsymbol{x}_{n})
generated by distance majorization. In any event, it is crucial to design a surrogate that renders each subproblem along the annealing path easy to solve. This may entail applying additional majorizations in
f(\boldsymbol{x})
. Balanced against this possibility is the sacrifice of curvature information with each additional majorization.

We readily acknowledge that other algorithms may perform better than MM and proximal distance algorithms on specific problems. The triangle fixing algorithm for metric projection is a case in point
(
Brickell et al. 2008
)
, as are the numerous denoising algorithms based on the
\ell_{1}
norm. This objection obscures the generic utility of the proximal distance principle. ADMM can certainly be beat on many specific problems, but nobody seriously suggests that it be rejected across the board. Optimization, particularly constrained optimization, is a fragmented subject, with no clear winner across problem domains. Generic methods serve as workhorses, benchmarks, and backstops.

As an aside, let us briefly note that ADMM can be motivated by the MM principle, which is the same idea driving proximal distance algorithms. The optimal pair
(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})
and
\boldsymbol{\lambda}
furnishes a stationary point of the Lagrangian. Because the Lagrangian is linear in
\boldsymbol{\lambda}
, its maximum for fixed
(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})
is
\infty
. To correct this defect, one can add a viscosity minorization to the Lagrangian. This produces the modified Lagrangian

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mu}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y},\boldsymbol{\lambda})

\displaystyle\!\!=\!\!

\displaystyle f(\boldsymbol{x})+g(\boldsymbol{y})+\mu\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{t}(\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{y})+\frac{\mu}{2}\|\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{y}\|^{2}-\frac{\alpha}{2}\|\boldsymbol{\lambda}-\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{n}\|^{2}.

The penalty term has no impact on the
\boldsymbol{x}
and
\boldsymbol{y}
updates. However, the MM update for
\boldsymbol{\lambda}
is determined by the stationary condition

\displaystyle{\bf 0}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mu(\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{x}_{n+1}-\boldsymbol{y}_{n+1})-\alpha(\boldsymbol{\lambda}-\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{n}),

so that

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{n+1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{n}+\frac{\mu}{\alpha}(\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{x}_{n+1}-\boldsymbol{y}_{n+1}).

The choice
\alpha=1
gives the standard ADMM update. Thus, the ADMM algorithm alternates decreasing and increasing the Lagrangian in a search for the saddlepoint represented by the optimal trio
(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y},\boldsymbol{\lambda})
.

In closing we would like to draw the reader’s attention to some generalizations of the MM principle and connections to other well-studied algorithm classes. For instance, a linear fusion constraint
\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{x}\in S
can in principle by replaced by a nonlinear fusion constraint
M(\boldsymbol{x})\in S
. The objective and majorizer are then

\displaystyle h_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{x})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle f(\boldsymbol{x})+\frac{\rho}{2}\mathop{\rm dist}\nolimits[M(\boldsymbol{x}),S]^{2}

\displaystyle g(\boldsymbol{x}\mid\boldsymbol{x}_{n})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle f(\boldsymbol{x})+\frac{\rho}{2}\|M(\boldsymbol{x})-\mathcal{P}_{S}[M(\boldsymbol{x}_{n})]\|^{2}.

The objective has gradient
\boldsymbol{g}=\nabla f(\boldsymbol{x})+\rho dM(\boldsymbol{x})^{t}\{M(\boldsymbol{x})-\mathcal{P}_{S}[M(\boldsymbol{x})]\}
. The second differential of the majorizer is approximately
d^{2}f(\boldsymbol{x})+\rho dM(\boldsymbol{x})^{t}dM(\boldsymbol{x})
for
M(\boldsymbol{x})
close to
\mathcal{P}_{S}[M(\boldsymbol{x})]
. Thus, gradient descent can be implemented with step size

\displaystyle\gamma

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\|\boldsymbol{g}_{n}\|^{2}}{\boldsymbol{g}_{n}^{t}d^{2}f(\boldsymbol{x}_{n})\boldsymbol{g}_{n}+\rho\|dM(\boldsymbol{x}_{n})\boldsymbol{g}_{n}\|^{2}},

assuming the denominator is positive.

Algebraic penalties such as
\|g(\boldsymbol{x})\|^{2}
reduce to distance penalties with constraint set
\{\bf 0\}
. The corresponding projection operator sends any vector
\boldsymbol{y}
to
\bf 0
so that the algebraic penalty
\|g(\boldsymbol{x})\|^{2}=\mathop{\rm dist}\nolimits[g(\boldsymbol{x}),\{\bf 0\}]^{2}
. This observation is pertinent to constrained least squares with
g(\boldsymbol{x})=\boldsymbol{d}-\boldsymbol{C}\boldsymbol{x}

(
Golub and Van Loan 1996
)
. The proximal distance surrogate can be expressed as

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\|\boldsymbol{y}-\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}\|^{2}+\frac{\rho}{2}\|\boldsymbol{d}-\boldsymbol{C}\boldsymbol{x}\|^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left\|\begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{y}\\
\sqrt{\rho}\boldsymbol{d}\end{pmatrix}-\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{A}\\
\sqrt{\rho}\boldsymbol{C}\end{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{x}\right\|^{2}

and minimized by standard least squares algorithms. No annealing is necessary. Inequality constraints
g(\boldsymbol{x})\leq{\bf 0}
behave somewhat differently. The proximal distance majorization

\mathop{\rm dist}\nolimits[g(\boldsymbol{x}),\mathbb{R}^{m}_{-}]^{2}\leq\|g(\boldsymbol{x})-\mathcal{P}_{\mathbb{R}^{m}_{-}}[g(\boldsymbol{x}_{n})]\|^{2}
is not the same as the Beltrami quadratic penalty
g(\boldsymbol{x})_{+}^{2}

(
Beltrami 1970
)
. However, the standard majorization
(
Lange 2016
)

\displaystyle g(\boldsymbol{x})_{+}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\|g(\boldsymbol{x})-\mathcal{P}_{\mathbb{R}^{m}_{-}}[g(\boldsymbol{x}_{n})]\|^{2}

brings them back into alignment.

and Section

for the convex and noncovex cases, respectively.

7.1
Proposition
3.1

Proof.

Without loss of generality we can translate the coordinates so that
\boldsymbol{y}={\bf 0}
. Let
B
be the unit sphere
\{\boldsymbol{x}:\|\boldsymbol{x}\|=1\}
. Our first aim is to show that
h_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{x})>f({\bf 0})
throughout
B
. Consider the set
B\cap T
, which is possibly empty. On this set the infimum
b
of
f(\boldsymbol{x})
is attained, so
b>f({\bf 0})
by assumption. The set
B\setminus T
will be divided into two regions,
a narrow zone adjacent to
T
and the remainder. Now let us show that there exists a
\delta>0
such that
h_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{x})\geq f(\boldsymbol{x})\geq f({\bf 0})+\delta
for all
\boldsymbol{x}\in B
with
\mathop{\rm dist}\nolimits(\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{x},S)\leq\delta
. If this is not so, then there exists a sequence
\boldsymbol{x}_{n}\in B
with
f(\boldsymbol{x}_{n})<f({\bf 0})+\frac{1}{k}
and
\mathop{\rm dist}\nolimits(\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{x}_{n},S)\leq\frac{1}{k}
. By compactness, some subsequence of
\boldsymbol{x}_{n}
converges to
\boldsymbol{z}\in B\cap T
with
f(\boldsymbol{z})\leq f({\bf 0})
, contradicting the uniqueness of
\boldsymbol{y}
. Finally, let
a=\min_{\boldsymbol{x}\in B}f(\boldsymbol{x})
. To deal with the remaining region take
\rho
large enough so that
a+\frac{\rho}{2}\delta^{2}>f({\bf 0})
. For such
\rho
,
h_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{x})>f({\bf 0})
everywhere on
B
. It follows that on the unit ball
\{\boldsymbol{x}:\|\boldsymbol{x}\|\leq 1\}
,
h_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{x})
is minimized at an interior point. Because
h_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{x})
is convex, a local minimum is necessarily a global minimum.

To show that the objective
h_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{x})
is coercive, it suffices to show that it is coercive along every ray
\{t\boldsymbol{v}:t\geq 0,\;\|\boldsymbol{v}\|=1\}

(
Lange 2016
)
. The convex function
r(t)=h_{\rho}(t\boldsymbol{v})
satisfies
r(t)\geq r(1)+r_{+}^{\prime}(1)(t-1)
. Because
r(0)<r(1)
, the point
1
is on the upward slope of
r(t)
, and the one-sided derivative
r_{+}^{\prime}(1)>0
. Coerciveness follows from this observation.
∎

7.2
Proposition
3.2

Proof.

The first assertion follows from the bound
g_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{x}\mid\boldsymbol{x}_{n})\geq h_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{x})
. To prove the second assertion, we note that it suffices to prove the existence of some constant
\rho>0
such that the matrix
\boldsymbol{A}+\rho\boldsymbol{D}^{t}\boldsymbol{D}
is positive definite
(
Debreu 1952
)
. If no choice of
\rho
renders
\boldsymbol{A}+\rho\boldsymbol{D}^{t}\boldsymbol{D}
positive definite, then there is a sequence of unit vectors
\boldsymbol{u}_{m}
and a sequence of scalars
\rho_{m}
tending to
\infty
such that

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{u}_{m}^{t}\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{u}_{m}+\rho_{m}\boldsymbol{u}_{m}^{t}\boldsymbol{D}^{t}\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{u}_{m}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 0.

(11)

By passing to a subsequence if needed, we may assume that the sequence

\boldsymbol{u}_{m}
converges to a unit vector
\boldsymbol{u}
. On the one hand, because
\boldsymbol{D}^{t}\boldsymbol{D}
is positive semidefinite, inequality (
11
) compels the conclusions
\boldsymbol{u}_{m}^{t}\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{u}_{m}\leq 0
, which must carry over to the limit. On the other hand, dividing inequality (
11
) by
\rho_{m}
and taking limits imply
\boldsymbol{u}^{t}\boldsymbol{D}^{t}\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{u}\leq 0
and therefore
\|\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{u}\|=0
. Because the limit vector
\boldsymbol{u}
violates the condition
\boldsymbol{u}^{t}\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{u}>0
, the required
\rho>0
exists.
∎

7.3
Proposition
3.3

Proof.

Systematic decrease of the iterate values
h_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{x}_{n})
is a consequence of the MM principle. The existence of

\boldsymbol{z}_{\rho}
follows from Proposition
3.1
. To prove the stated bound, first observe that the function

g_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{x}\mid\boldsymbol{x}_{n})-\frac{\rho}{2}\|\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{x}\|^{2}
is convex, being the sum of the convex function
f(\boldsymbol{x})
and a linear function. Because

\nabla g_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{x}_{n+1}\mid\boldsymbol{x}_{n})^{t}(\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{x}_{n+1})\geq\bf 0
for any
\boldsymbol{x}
in
S
, the supporting hyperplane inequality implies that

\displaystyle g_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{x}\mid\boldsymbol{x}_{n})-\frac{\rho}{2}\|\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{x}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle g_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{x}_{n+1}\mid\boldsymbol{x}_{n})-\frac{\rho}{2}\|\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{x}_{n+1}\|^{2}

\displaystyle-\rho\boldsymbol{x}_{n+1}^{t}\boldsymbol{D}^{t}\boldsymbol{D}(\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{x}_{n+1}),

or equivalently

\displaystyle g_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{x}\mid\boldsymbol{x}_{n})

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle g_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{x}_{n+1}\mid\boldsymbol{x}_{n})+\frac{\rho}{2}\|\boldsymbol{D}(\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{x}_{n+1})\|^{2}.

(12)

Now note that the difference

\displaystyle d(\boldsymbol{x}\mid\boldsymbol{y})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\|\boldsymbol{x}-\mathcal{P}(\boldsymbol{y})\|^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\|\boldsymbol{x}-\mathcal{P}(\boldsymbol{x})\|^{2}

has gradient

\displaystyle\nabla d(\boldsymbol{x}\mid\boldsymbol{y})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}(\boldsymbol{x})-\mathcal{P}(\boldsymbol{y}).

Because
\mathcal{P}(\boldsymbol{x})
is non-expansive, the gradient
\nabla d(\boldsymbol{x}\mid\boldsymbol{y})
is Lipschitz with constant
1
.
The tangency conditions
d(\boldsymbol{y}\mid\boldsymbol{y})=0
and
\nabla d(\boldsymbol{y}\mid\boldsymbol{y})={\bf 0}
therefore yield

\displaystyle d(\boldsymbol{x}\mid\boldsymbol{y})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle d(\boldsymbol{y}\mid\boldsymbol{y})+\nabla d(\boldsymbol{y}\mid\boldsymbol{y})^{t}(\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{y})+\frac{1}{2}\|\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{y}\|^{2}

(13)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\|\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{y}\|^{2}

for all
\boldsymbol{x}
.
At a minimum
\boldsymbol{z}_{\rho}
of
h_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{x})
, combining inequalities (
12
) and (
13
) gives

\displaystyle h_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{x}_{n+1})+\frac{\rho}{2}\|\boldsymbol{D}(\boldsymbol{z}_{\rho}-\boldsymbol{x}_{n+1})\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle g_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{x}_{n+1}\mid\boldsymbol{x}_{n})+\frac{\rho}{2}\|\boldsymbol{D}(\boldsymbol{z}_{\rho}-\boldsymbol{x}_{n+1})\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle g_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{z}_{\rho}\mid\boldsymbol{x}_{n})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle h_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{z}_{\rho})-\frac{\rho}{2}\|\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{z}_{\rho}-\mathcal{P}(\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{z}_{\rho})\|^{2}+\frac{\rho}{2}\|\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{z}_{\rho}-\mathcal{P}(\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{x}_{n})\|^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle h_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{z}_{\rho})+\rho d(\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{z}_{\rho}\mid\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{x}_{n})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle h_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{z}_{\rho})+\frac{\rho}{2}\|\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{z}_{\rho}-\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{x}_{n}\|^{2}.

Adding the result

\displaystyle h_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{x}_{n+1})-h_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{z}_{\rho})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{\rho}{2}\Big[\|\boldsymbol{D}(\boldsymbol{z}_{\rho}-\boldsymbol{x}_{n})\|^{2}-\|\boldsymbol{D}(\boldsymbol{z}_{\rho}-\boldsymbol{x}_{n+1})\|^{2}\Big]

over
n
and invoking the descent property
h_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{x}_{n+1})\leq h_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{x}_{n})
, telescoping produces the desired error bound

\displaystyle h_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{x}_{n+1})-h_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{z}_{\rho})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{\rho}{2(n+1)}\Big[\|\boldsymbol{D}(\boldsymbol{z}_{\rho}-\boldsymbol{x}_{0})\|^{2}-\|\boldsymbol{D}(\boldsymbol{z}_{\rho}-\boldsymbol{x}_{n+1})\|^{2}\Big]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{\rho}{2(n+1)}\|\boldsymbol{D}(\boldsymbol{z}_{\rho}-\boldsymbol{x}_{0})\|^{2}.

This is precisely the asserted bound.
∎

7.4
Proposition
3.4

Proof.

The existence and uniqueness of
\boldsymbol{z}_{\rho}
are obvious. The remainder of the proof hinges on the facts that
h_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{x})
is
\mu
-strongly convex and the surrogate
g_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{x}\mid\boldsymbol{w})
is
L+\rho\|\boldsymbol{D}\|^{2}
-smooth for all
\boldsymbol{w}
. The latter assertion follows from

\displaystyle\nabla g_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{x}\mid\boldsymbol{w})-\nabla g_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{y}\mid\boldsymbol{w})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\nabla f(\boldsymbol{x})-\nabla f(\boldsymbol{y})+\rho\boldsymbol{D}^{t}\boldsymbol{D}(\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{y}).

These facts together with
\nabla g_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{z}_{\rho}\mid\boldsymbol{z}_{\rho})=\nabla h_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{z}_{\rho})={\bf 0}
imply

\displaystyle h_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{x})-h_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{z}_{\rho})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle g_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{x}\mid\boldsymbol{z}_{\rho})-g_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{z}_{\rho}\mid\boldsymbol{z}_{\rho})

(14)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\nabla g_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{z}_{\rho}\mid\boldsymbol{z}_{\rho})^{t}(\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{z}_{\rho})+\frac{L+\rho\|\boldsymbol{D}\|^{2}}{2}\|\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{z}_{\rho}\|^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{L+\rho\|\boldsymbol{D}\|^{2}}{2}\|\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{z}_{\rho}\|^{2}.

The strong convexity condition

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle h_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{z}_{\rho})-h_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{x})\mathop{\;\>}\nolimits\geq\mathop{\;\>}\nolimits\nabla h_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{x})^{t}(\boldsymbol{z}_{\rho}-\boldsymbol{x})+\frac{\mu}{2}\|\boldsymbol{z}_{\rho}-\boldsymbol{x}\|^{2}

entails

\displaystyle\|\nabla h_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{x})\|\cdot\|\boldsymbol{z}_{\rho}-\boldsymbol{x}\|

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle-\nabla h_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{x})^{t}(\boldsymbol{z}_{\rho}-\boldsymbol{x})\mathop{\;\>}\nolimits\geq\mathop{\;\>}\nolimits\frac{\mu}{2}\|\boldsymbol{z}_{\rho}-\boldsymbol{x}\|^{2}.

It follows that
\|\nabla h_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{x})\|\geq\frac{\mu}{2}\|\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{z}_{\rho}\|
.
This last inequality and inequality (
14
) produce the
Polyak-Łojasiewicz bound

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\|\nabla h_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{x})\|^{2}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\frac{\mu^{2}}{2(L+\rho\|\boldsymbol{D}\|^{2})}[h_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{x})-h_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{z}_{\rho})].

Taking
c=L+\rho\|\boldsymbol{D}\|^{2}
and

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{x}=\boldsymbol{x}_{n}-c^{-1}\nabla g_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{x}_{n}\mid\boldsymbol{x}_{n})\mathop{\;\>}\nolimits=\mathop{\;\>}\nolimits\boldsymbol{x}_{n}-c^{-1}\nabla h_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{x}_{n}),

the Polyak-Łojasiewicz bound gives

\displaystyle h_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{x}_{n+1})-h_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{x}_{n})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle g_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{x}_{n+1}\mid\boldsymbol{x}_{n})-g_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{x}_{n}\mid\boldsymbol{x}_{n})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle g_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{x}\mid\boldsymbol{x}_{n})-g_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{x}_{n}\mid\boldsymbol{x}_{n})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle-c^{-1}\nabla g_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{x}_{n}\mid\boldsymbol{x}_{n})^{t}\nabla h_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{x}_{n})+\frac{c}{2}\|c^{-1}\nabla h_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{x}_{n})\|^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2c}\|\nabla h_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{x}_{n})\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle-\frac{\mu^{2}}{2c^{2}}[h_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{x}_{n})-h_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{z}_{\rho})].

Rearranging this inequality yields

\displaystyle h_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{x}_{n+1})-h_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{z}_{\rho})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\Big[1-\frac{\mu^{2}}{2c^{2}}\Big][h_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{x}_{n})-h_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{z}_{\rho}))],

Appendix A
Convergence Properties of ADMM

To avail ourselves of the known results, we define three functions

\displaystyle H_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle f(\boldsymbol{x})+\frac{\rho}{2}\mathop{\rm dist}\nolimits(\boldsymbol{y},S)^{2}

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y},\boldsymbol{\lambda})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle H_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})+\lambda^{t}(\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{y})

\displaystyle q(\boldsymbol{\lambda})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\min_{\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}}\mathcal{L}_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y},\boldsymbol{\lambda}),

the second and third being the Lagrangian and dual function. This notation leads to following result; see
Beck 2017
for an accessible proof.

Proposition A.1
.

Suppose that
S
is closed and convex and that the loss
f(\boldsymbol{x})
is proper, closed, and convex with
domain whose relative interior is nonempty. Also assume the dual function
q(\boldsymbol{\lambda})
achieves its maximum value. If the objective
f(\boldsymbol{x})+\frac{\rho}{2}\|\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{x}\|^{2}+\boldsymbol{a}^{t}\boldsymbol{x}
achieves its minimum value for all
\boldsymbol{a}\neq{\bf 0}
, then the ADMM running averages

\displaystyle\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}_{n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\boldsymbol{x}_{k}\;\;\text{and}\;\;\bar{\boldsymbol{y}}_{n}\mathop{\;\>}\nolimits=\mathop{\;\>}\nolimits\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\boldsymbol{y}_{k}

satisfy

\displaystyle|H_{\rho}(\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}_{n},\bar{\boldsymbol{y}}_{n})-h_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{x}_{\rho})|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle O\Big(\frac{\rho}{n}\Big)

\displaystyle\|D\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}_{n}-\boldsymbol{y}_{n}\|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle O\Big(\frac{1}{k}\Big).

Note that Proposition
3.2
furnishes a sufficient condition under which the functions
f(\boldsymbol{x})+\frac{\rho}{2}\|\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{x}\|^{2}+\boldsymbol{a}^{t}\boldsymbol{x}
achieve their minima. Linear convergence holds under stronger assumptions.

Proposition A.2
.

Suppose that
S
is closed and convex, that the loss
f(\boldsymbol{x})
is
L
-smooth and
\mu
-strongly convex,
and that the map determined by
\boldsymbol{D}
is onto. Then the ADMM iterates converge at a linear rate.

Giselsson and Boyd 2016
proved Proposition
A.2
by operator methods. A range of convergence rates is
specified there.

Appendix B
Additional Details for Metric Projection Example

Given a
n\times n
dissimilarity matrix
\boldsymbol{C}=(c_{ij})
with non-negative weights
w_{ij}
, our goal is to find a semi-metric
\boldsymbol{X}=(x_{ij})
.
We start by denoting
\mathrm{trivec}
an operation that maps a symmetric matrix
\boldsymbol{X}
to a vector
\boldsymbol{x}
,
\boldsymbol{x}=\mathrm{trivec}(\boldsymbol{X})
(Figure
3
). Then we write the metric projection objective as

h_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{x})=\frac{1}{2}\|\boldsymbol{W}^{1/2}(\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{c})\|^{2}_{2}+\frac{\rho}{2}\mathop{\rm dist}\nolimits(\boldsymbol{T}\boldsymbol{x},\mathbb{R}_{+}^{m_{1}})^{2}+\frac{\rho}{2}\mathop{\rm dist}\nolimits(\boldsymbol{x},\mathbb{R}_{+}^{m_{2}})^{2},

where
\boldsymbol{c}=\mathrm{trivec}(\boldsymbol{C})
.

Figure 3:

Example of a symmetric matrix
\boldsymbol{X}
and its minimal representation
\boldsymbol{x}=\mathrm{trivec}(\boldsymbol{X})
.

Here
\boldsymbol{T}
encodes triangle inequalities and the
m_{i}
count the number of contraints of each type.
The usual distance majorization furnishes a surrogate

\displaystyle g_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{x}\mid\boldsymbol{x}_{n})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\|\boldsymbol{W}^{1/2}(\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{c})\|^{2}_{2}+\frac{\rho}{2}\|\boldsymbol{T}\boldsymbol{x}-\mathcal{P}(\boldsymbol{T}\boldsymbol{x}_{n},\mathbb{R}_{+}^{m_{1}})\|^{2}_{2}+\frac{\rho}{2}\lVert{\boldsymbol{x}-\mathcal{P}(\boldsymbol{x}_{n},\mathbb{R}_{+}^{m_{2}})}\rVert^{2}_{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\|\boldsymbol{W}^{1/2}(\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{c})\|^{2}_{2}+\frac{\rho}{2}\|\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{x}-\mathcal{P}(\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{x}_{n})\|_{2}^{2}.

The notation
\mathcal{P}(\cdot,S)
denotes projection onto a set
S
.
The fusion matrix
\boldsymbol{D}=[\boldsymbol{T};\boldsymbol{I}]
stacks the two operators; the joint projection operates in a block-wise fashion.

B.1
MM

We rewrite the surrogate explicitly as a least squares problem minimizing
\|\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{b}_{n}\|^{2}_{2}
:

\boldsymbol{x}_{n+1}=\underset{\boldsymbol{x}}{\mathop{\rm argmin}\nolimits}\frac{1}{2}\left\|\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{W}^{1/2}\\
\sqrt{\rho}\boldsymbol{D}\end{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{x}-\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{c}\\
\sqrt{\rho}\mathcal{P}(\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{x}_{n})\end{bmatrix}\right\|_{2}^{2},

where
\boldsymbol{c}\equiv\boldsymbol{y}
from the main text.
Updating the RHS
\boldsymbol{b}_{n}
in the linear system reduces to evaluating the projection and copy operations.
It is worth noting that triangle fixing algorithms that solve the metric nearness problem operate in the same fashion, except they work one triangle a time.
That is, each iteration solves
\binom{n}{3}
least squares problems compared to 1 in this formulation.
A conjugate gradient type of algorithm solves the normal equations directly using
\boldsymbol{A}^{t}\boldsymbol{A}
, whereas LSQR type methods use only
\boldsymbol{A}
and
\boldsymbol{A}^{t}
.

B.2
Steepest Descent

The updates
\boldsymbol{x}_{n+1}=\boldsymbol{x}_{n}-t_{n}\nabla h_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{x}_{n})
admit an exact solution for the line search parameter
t_{n}
.
Recall the generic formula from the main text:

t_{n}=\frac{\|\boldsymbol{v}_{n}\|^{2}}{\boldsymbol{v}_{n}^{t}\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{v}_{n}+\rho\|\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{v}_{n}\|^{2}}.

Identifying
\boldsymbol{v}_{n}
with
\nabla h_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{x}_{n})
we have

\displaystyle\nabla h_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{x}_{n})

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{W}(\boldsymbol{x}_{n}-\boldsymbol{c})+\rho\boldsymbol{D}^{t}[\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{x}_{n}-\mathcal{P}(\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{x}_{n})]

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{W}(\boldsymbol{x}_{n}-\boldsymbol{c})+\rho(\boldsymbol{I}+\boldsymbol{T}^{t}\boldsymbol{T})\boldsymbol{x}_{n}-\rho[\boldsymbol{T}^{t}\mathcal{P}(\boldsymbol{T}\boldsymbol{x}_{n},\mathbb{R}_{+}^{m_{1}})+\mathcal{P}(\boldsymbol{x}_{n},\mathbb{R}_{+}^{m_{1}})],

\displaystyle t_{n}

\displaystyle=\frac{\|\boldsymbol{v}_{n}\|^{2}}{\|\boldsymbol{W}^{1/2}\boldsymbol{v}_{n}\|^{2}+\rho\|\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{v}_{n}\|^{2}}.

B.3
ADMM

Taking
\boldsymbol{y}
as the dual variable and
\boldsymbol{\lambda}
as scaled multipliers, the updates for each ADMM block are

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{x}_{n+1}

\displaystyle=\underset{\boldsymbol{x}}{\mathop{\rm argmin}\nolimits}\left\|\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{W}^{1/2}\\
\sqrt{\mu}\boldsymbol{D}\end{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{x}-\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{c}\\
\sqrt{\mu}(\boldsymbol{y}_{n}-\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{n}))\end{bmatrix}\right\|_{2}^{2},

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{y}_{n+1}

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha}{1+\alpha}\mathcal{P}(\boldsymbol{z}_{n})+\frac{1}{1+\alpha}\boldsymbol{z}_{n};\qquad\boldsymbol{z}_{n}=\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{x}_{n+1}+\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{n},~\alpha=\rho/\mu.

Finally, the Multipliers follow the standard update.

Proposition 1
.

The matrix

has

Proof.

\binom{m}{3}

Now fix the orientation of the
3

The number of constraints is therefore

Proposition 2
.

Each column of
\boldsymbol{T}
has
3(m-2)
nonzero entries.

Proof.

In view of the previous result, the entries

encode whether edge
j
participates in constraint
i

We proceed by induction on the number of nodes
m

The base case

Note that a triangle encodes
3

Now consider a complete graph on
m

Without loss of generality, consider the collection of
3

Adding a node to the graph yields

This action also creates one new triangle for each existing edge.
Thus, edge

∎

Proposition 3
.

Each column of
\boldsymbol{T}
has
m-2

+1

-1

Proof.

Interpret the inequality

The statement is equivalent to counting

\displaystyle a(N)

\displaystyle=\text{number of times $x_{ij}$ appears in position 1, and},

\displaystyle b(N)

\displaystyle=\text{number of times $x_{ij}$ appears in position 2 or 3},

In view of the previous proposition, it is enough to prove

Note that

Proposition 4
.

The matrix
\boldsymbol{T}
has full column rank.

Proof.

It is enough to show that

is full rank.
The first two propositions imply

To compute the off-diagonal entries, fix a triangle and note that two edges
i
and

There are three possibilities for a given constraint

∎

Using the Woodbury formula, the inverse of

\displaystyle(\boldsymbol{T}^{t}\boldsymbol{T})^{-1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left[(3m-4)\boldsymbol{I}_{\binom{m}{2}}-\boldsymbol{M}\boldsymbol{M}^{t}\right]^{-1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(3m-4)^{-1}\boldsymbol{I}_{\binom{m}{2}}-(3m-4)^{-2}\boldsymbol{M}[-\boldsymbol{I}_{m}+(3m-4)^{-1}\boldsymbol{M}^{t}\boldsymbol{M}]^{-1}\boldsymbol{M}^{t}

\displaystyle(3m-4)^{-1}\boldsymbol{I}_{\binom{m}{2}}-(3m-4)^{-1}\boldsymbol{M}[-(2m-2)\boldsymbol{I}_{m}+\boldsymbol{1}_{m}\boldsymbol{1}_{m}^{t}]^{-1}\boldsymbol{M}^{t}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(3m-4)^{-1}\boldsymbol{I}_{\binom{m}{2}}-(3m-4)^{-1}\boldsymbol{M}[-(2m-2)^{-1}\boldsymbol{I}_{m}-(2m-2)^{-1}(m-2)^{-1}\boldsymbol{1}_{m}\boldsymbol{1}_{m}^{t}]\boldsymbol{M}^{t}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{3m-4}\boldsymbol{I}_{\binom{m}{2}}+\frac{2}{(3m-4)(m-1)(m-2)}\boldsymbol{1}_{\binom{m}{2}}\boldsymbol{1}_{\binom{m}{2}}^{t}+\frac{1}{2(3m-4)(m-1)}\boldsymbol{M}\boldsymbol{M}^{t}.

Solving linear system
\boldsymbol{T}^{t}\boldsymbol{T}
invokes two matrix vector multiplications involving the incidence matrix
\boldsymbol{M}

corresponds to taking pairwise sums of the components of a vector
\boldsymbol{v}

\end{split}\qquad\begin{split}a&=[3(m-1)\rho+1]^{-1}\\

\|\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{b}_{n}\|^{2}_{2}
:

\boldsymbol{u}_{n+1}=\underset{\boldsymbol{u}}{\mathop{\rm argmin}\nolimits}\frac{1}{2}\left\|\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{I}\\
\sqrt{\rho}\boldsymbol{D}\end{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{u}-\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{x}\\
\sqrt{\rho}\mathcal{P}_{S_{k}}(\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{u}_{n})\end{bmatrix}\right\|_{2}^{2}.

D.2
Steepest Descent

The updates
\boldsymbol{u}_{n+1}=\boldsymbol{u}_{n}-t_{n}\nabla h_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{u}_{n})
admit an exact solution for the line search parameter
t_{n}
.
Taking
\boldsymbol{q}_{n}=\nabla h_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{u}_{n})
as the gradient we have

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{q}_{n}

\displaystyle=(\boldsymbol{u}_{n}-\boldsymbol{x})+\rho\boldsymbol{D}^{t}[\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{u}_{n}-\mathcal{P}_{S_{k}}(\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{u}_{n})],

\displaystyle t_{n}

\displaystyle=\frac{\|\boldsymbol{q}_{n}\|^{2}}{\|\boldsymbol{q}_{n}\|^{2}+\rho\|\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{q}_{n}\|^{2}}.

Note that blocks in
[\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{u}_{n}-\mathcal{P}_{S_{k}}(\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{u}_{n})]_{\ell}
are equal to
\boldsymbol{0}
whenever the projection of block
[\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{u}_{n}]_{\ell}
is non-zero.

D.3
ADMM

Take
\boldsymbol{y}
as the dual variable and
\boldsymbol{\lambda}
as scaled multipliers.
Minimizing the
\boldsymbol{u}
block involves solving a single linear system:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{u}_{n+1}

\displaystyle=\underset{\boldsymbol{u}}{\mathop{\rm argmin}\nolimits}\frac{1}{2}\left\|\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{I}\\
\sqrt{\mu}\boldsymbol{D}\end{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{u}-\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{x}\\
\sqrt{\mu}(\boldsymbol{y}_{n}-\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{n})\end{bmatrix}\right\|_{2}^{2},

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{y}_{n+1}

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha}{1+\alpha}\mathcal{P}_{S_{k}}(\boldsymbol{z}_{n})+\frac{1}{1+\alpha}\boldsymbol{z}_{n};\qquad\boldsymbol{z}_{n}=\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{u}_{n+1}+\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{n},~\alpha=\rho/\mu.

Multipliers follow the standard update.

D.4
Blockwise Sparse Projection

The projection
\mathcal{P}_{S_{k}}
maps a matrix to a sparse representation with
k
non-zero columns (or blocks in the case of the vectorized version).
In the context of clustering, imposing sparsity permits a maximum of
k
violations in consensus of centroid assignments,
\boldsymbol{u}_{i}=\boldsymbol{u}_{j}
.
Letting
\Delta_{\ell}\equiv\Delta_{ij}=\|\boldsymbol{u}_{i}-\boldsymbol{u}_{j}\|
denote pairwise distances and
M=\binom{m}{2}
denote the number of unique pairwise distances, we define the projection along blocks
\boldsymbol{v}_{\ell}=\boldsymbol{u}_{i}-\boldsymbol{u}_{j}
for each pair as

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{S_{k}}(\boldsymbol{v}_{\ell})=\begin{cases}\boldsymbol{v}_{\ell},&\text{if}~\Delta_{\ell}\in\{\Delta_{(M)},\Delta_{(M-1)},\ldots\Delta_{(M-k+1)}\}\\
\boldsymbol{0},&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Here the notation
x_{(i)}
represents the
i
-th element in an ascending list.
Concretely, the magnitude of a difference
\boldsymbol{v}_{\ell}
must be within the top
k
distances.
An alternative, helpful definition is based on the smallest distances

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{S_{k}}(\boldsymbol{v}_{\ell})=\begin{cases}\boldsymbol{0},&\text{if}~\Delta_{k}\in\{\Delta_{(1)},\Delta_{(2)},\ldots\Delta_{(k)}\}\\
\boldsymbol{v}_{\ell},&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

Thus, it is enough to find a pivot
\Delta_{(M-k+1)}
or
\Delta_{(k)}
that splits the list into the top
k
elements.
Because the hyperparameter
k
has a finite range in
\{0,1,2,\ldots,\binom{m}{2}\}
one can exploit symmetry to reduce the best/average computational complexity in a search procedure.
We implement this projection using a partial sorting algorithm based on quicksort, and note that it is set-valued in general.

Appendix E
Additional Details for Image Denoising Example

Here we restate the total variation denoising problem to take advantage of proximal operators in the proximal distance framework.
We minimize the penalized objective

\displaystyle h_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{U})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\|\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{w}\|_{F}^{2}+\frac{\rho}{2}\mathop{\rm dist}\nolimits(\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{u},S_{\gamma})^{2},

where
\boldsymbol{w}=\mathop{\rm vec}\nolimits(\boldsymbol{W})
is a noisy input image and
S_{\gamma}
is the
\ell_{1}
ball with radius
\gamma
. Thus,
\gamma
may be interpreted as the target total variation of the reconstructed image.
Distance majorization yields the surrogate

g_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{u}\mid\boldsymbol{u}_{n})=\frac{1}{2}\|\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{w}\|_{2}^{2}+\frac{\rho}{2}\|\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{u}-\mathcal{P}_{\gamma}(\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{u}_{n})\|^{2}.

Here
\mathcal{P}_{\gamma}(\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{u})
enforces sparsity in all derivatives through projection onto the
\ell_{1}
ball with radius
\gamma
.
Because
\boldsymbol{D}
is ill-conditioned, we append an additional row with zeros everywhere except the last entry; that is,
\boldsymbol{D}=[\boldsymbol{D}_{n},\boldsymbol{D}_{p},\boldsymbol{e}_{p}]
with
\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
.
In this case, the sparse projection applies to all but the last component of
\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{u}
.

E.1
MM

Rewrite the surrogate explicitly as a least squares problem:

\boldsymbol{u}_{n+1}=\underset{\boldsymbol{x}}{\mathop{\rm argmin}\nolimits}\frac{1}{2}\left\|\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{I}\\
\sqrt{\rho}\boldsymbol{D}\end{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{u}-\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{w}\\
\sqrt{\rho}\mathcal{P}_{\gamma}(\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{u}_{n})\end{bmatrix}\right\|_{2}^{2}.

E.2
Steepest Descent

The updates
\boldsymbol{u}_{n+1}=\boldsymbol{u}_{n}-t_{n}\nabla h_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{u}_{n})
admit an exact solution for the line search parameter
t_{n}
.
Taking
\boldsymbol{q}_{n}=\nabla h_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{u}_{n})
as the gradient we have

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{q}_{n}

\displaystyle=(\boldsymbol{u}_{n}-\boldsymbol{w})+\rho\boldsymbol{D}^{t}[\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{u}_{n}-\mathcal{P}_{\gamma}(\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{u}_{n})],

\displaystyle t_{n}

\displaystyle=\frac{\|\boldsymbol{q}_{n}\|^{2}}{\|\boldsymbol{q}_{n}\|^{2}+\rho\|\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{q}_{n}\|^{2}}.

E.3
ADMM

We denote by
\boldsymbol{y}
the dual variable and
\boldsymbol{\lambda}
the scaled multipliers.
Minimizing the
\boldsymbol{u}
block involves solving a single linear system:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{u}_{n+1}

\displaystyle=\underset{\boldsymbol{x}}{\mathop{\rm argmin}\nolimits}\frac{1}{2}\left\|\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{I}\\
\sqrt{\mu}\boldsymbol{D}\end{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{u}-\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{w}\\
\sqrt{\mu}(\boldsymbol{y}_{n}-\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{n})\end{bmatrix}\right\|_{2}^{2},

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{y}_{n+1}

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha}{1+\alpha}\mathcal{P}_{\gamma}(\boldsymbol{z}_{n})+\frac{1}{1+\alpha}\boldsymbol{z}_{n};\qquad\boldsymbol{z}_{n}=\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{u}_{n+1}+\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{n},~\alpha=\rho/\mu.

Multipliers follow the standard update.

Appendix F
Additional Details for Condition Number Example

Given a matrix
\boldsymbol{M}=\boldsymbol{U}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\boldsymbol{V}^{-1}
with singular values
\sigma_{1}\geq\sigma_{2}\geq\ldots\geq\sigma_{p}
, we seek a new matrix
\boldsymbol{N}=\boldsymbol{U}\boldsymbol{X}\boldsymbol{V}^{-1}
such that
\mathrm{cond}(\boldsymbol{B})=x_{1}/x_{p}\leq c
.
We minimize the penalized objective

\displaystyle h_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{x})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\|\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{\sigma}\|^{2}+\frac{\rho}{2}\mathop{\rm dist}\nolimits(\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{x},\mathbb{R}^{p^{2}}_{-})^{2},

as suggested by the Von Neumann-Fan inequality.
The fusion matrix
\boldsymbol{D}=\boldsymbol{C}+\boldsymbol{S}
encodes the constraints
x_{i}-cx_{j}\leq 0
.
Distance majorization yields the surrogate

g_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{x}\mid\boldsymbol{x}_{n})=\frac{1}{2}\|\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{w}\|_{2}^{2}+\frac{\rho}{2}\|\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{x}-\mathcal{P}_{-}(\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{x}_{n})\|^{2}.

To be specific, the matrix
\boldsymbol{C}=-c{\bf 1}_{p}\otimes\boldsymbol{I}_{p}
scales the
p\times p
identity matrix by
-c
and stacks it
p
times. Similarly, the matrix
\boldsymbol{S}=\boldsymbol{I}_{p}\otimes{\bf 1}_{p}
stacks
p
matrices of dimension
p\times p
. Each of these stacked matrices has
(p-1)

{\bf 0}_{p}
columns and one shifted
{\bf 1}_{p}
column. For example, for
p=2

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{S}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
1&0\end{pmatrix}\\
\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}\end{bmatrix}.

F.1
MM

Rewrite the surrogate explicitly a least squares problem minimizing
\|\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{b}_{n}\|^{2}_{2}
:

\boldsymbol{x}_{n+1}=\underset{\boldsymbol{x}}{\mathop{\rm argmin}\nolimits}\frac{1}{2}\left\|\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{I}\\
\sqrt{\rho}\boldsymbol{D}\end{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{x}-\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{\sigma}\\
\sqrt{\rho}\mathcal{P}(\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{x}_{n})\end{bmatrix}\right\|_{2}^{2}.

Applying the matrix inverse from before yields an explicit formula (with
a
and
b
defined as before):

\boldsymbol{x}_{n+1}=\frac{1}{a}\left[\boldsymbol{z}_{n}-\frac{\boldsymbol{1}^{t}\boldsymbol{z}_{n}}{p-(a/b)}\boldsymbol{1}\right];\qquad\boldsymbol{z}_{n}=\boldsymbol{\sigma}+\rho\boldsymbol{D}^{t}\mathcal{P}(\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{x}_{n}),~a=1+\rho p(c^{2}+1),~b=2\rho c.

F.2
Steepest Descent

The updates
\boldsymbol{x}_{n+1}=\boldsymbol{x}_{n}-t_{n}\nabla h_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{x}_{n})
admit an exact solution for the line search parameter
t_{n}
.
Taking
\boldsymbol{q}_{n}=\nabla h_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{x}_{n})
as the gradient we have

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{q}_{n}

\displaystyle=(\boldsymbol{x}_{n}-\boldsymbol{u})+\rho\boldsymbol{D}^{t}[\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{x}_{n}-\mathcal{P}_{\nu}(\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{x}_{n})],

\displaystyle t_{n}

\displaystyle=\frac{\|\boldsymbol{q}_{n}\|^{2}}{\|\boldsymbol{q}_{n}\|^{2}+\rho\|\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{q}_{n}\|^{2}}.

F.3
ADMM

Take
\boldsymbol{y}
as the dual variable and
\boldsymbol{\lambda}
as scaled multipliers.
The formula for the MM algorithm applies in updating
\boldsymbol{x}_{n}
, except we replace
\rho
with
\mu
and
\mathcal{P}(\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{x}_{n})
with
\boldsymbol{y}_{n}-\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{n}
:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{x}_{n+1}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{a}\left[\boldsymbol{z}^{1}_{n}-\frac{\boldsymbol{1}^{t}\boldsymbol{z}^{1}_{n}}{p-(a/b)}\boldsymbol{1}\right];\qquad\boldsymbol{z}_{n}^{1}=\boldsymbol{\sigma}+\mu\boldsymbol{D}^{t}(\boldsymbol{y}_{n}-\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{n}),~a=1+\mu p(c^{2}+1),~b=2\mu c

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{y}_{n+1}

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha}{1+\alpha}\mathcal{P}(\boldsymbol{z}^{2}_{n})+\frac{1}{1+\alpha}\boldsymbol{z}^{2}_{n};\qquad\boldsymbol{z}^{2}_{n}=\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{x}_{n+1}+\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{n},~\alpha=\rho/\mu.

Multipliers follow the standard update.

F.4
Explicit Matrix Inverse

Both ADMM and MM reduce to solving a linear system.
Fortunately, the Hessian for
h_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{x})
reduces to a Householder-like matrix.
First we note that it is trivial to multiply either
\boldsymbol{C}^{t}
or
\boldsymbol{S}^{t}
by a
p^{2}
-vector. The more interesting problem is calculating
(\boldsymbol{A}_{\rho}^{t}\boldsymbol{A}_{\rho})^{-1}
, where
\nabla h_{\rho}^{2}=\boldsymbol{A}_{\rho}^{t}\boldsymbol{A}_{\rho}=\boldsymbol{I}_{p}+\rho\boldsymbol{D}^{t}\boldsymbol{D}
. The reader can check the identities

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{C}^{t}\boldsymbol{C}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle c^{2}p\boldsymbol{I}_{p}\;\;\text{and}\;\;\boldsymbol{C}^{t}\boldsymbol{S}\mathop{\;\>}\nolimits=\mathop{\;\>}\nolimits-c{\bf 1}_{p}{\bf 1}_{p}^{t},

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{S}^{t}\boldsymbol{C}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-c{\bf 1}_{p}{\bf 1}_{p}^{t}\;\;\text{and}\;\;\boldsymbol{S}^{t}\boldsymbol{S}\mathop{\;\>}\nolimits=\mathop{\;\>}\nolimits p\boldsymbol{I}_{p}.

It follows that
\boldsymbol{A}_{\rho}^{t}\boldsymbol{A}_{\rho}=(1+\rho p(c^{2}+1))\boldsymbol{I}_{p}-2c\rho{\bf 1}_{p}{\bf 1}_{p}^{t}
.
Applying the Sherman-Morrison formula to results in

\displaystyle[\boldsymbol{I}_{p}+\rho\boldsymbol{D}^{t}\boldsymbol{D}]^{-1}

\displaystyle=[a\boldsymbol{I}_{p}-b\boldsymbol{1}_{p}\boldsymbol{1}_{p}^{t}]^{-1}

\displaystyle=-b^{-1}\left[-(b/a)\boldsymbol{I}_{p}-\frac{(b/a)^{2}\boldsymbol{1}_{p}\boldsymbol{1}_{p}^{t}}{1-(a/b)\boldsymbol{1}_{p}^{t}\boldsymbol{1}_{p}}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{a}\left[\boldsymbol{I}_{p}-\frac{\boldsymbol{1}_{p}\boldsymbol{1}_{p}^{t}}{p-a/b}\right],

where
a=1+\rho p(c^{2}+1)
and
b=2\rho c
.
These simplifications make the exact proximal distance updates easy to compute.

Appendix G
Choice of Linear Solver

Updating parameters using MM or ADMM requires solving large-scale linear systems of the form
(\boldsymbol{I}+c_{t}\boldsymbol{D}^{t}\boldsymbol{D})\boldsymbol{x}=\boldsymbol{b}
.
Here
c_{t}
is a scalar that depends on the outer iteration number
t
, in general, and the matrix on the LHS is square, symmetric, and often reasonably well-conditioned.
Standard factorization methods like Cholesky and spectral decompositions cannot be applied without efficient update rules based on
c_{t}
.
Instead, we turn to iterative methods, specifically conjugate gradients (CG) and LSQR, and use a linear map approach to adequately address sparsity, structure, and computational efficiency in matrix-vector multiplication.
Tables
7
and
8
summarize performance metrics for MM and ADMM using both iterative linear solvers on instances of the convex regression problem.
Times are averages taken over 3 replicates with standard deviations in parentheses, and iteration counts reflect the total number of inner iterations with outer counts in parentheses.
We find no appreciable difference between CG and LSQR except on timing, and therefore favor CG in all our benchmarks.
