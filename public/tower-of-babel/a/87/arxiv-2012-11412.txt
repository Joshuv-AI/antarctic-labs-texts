I
Introduction

MOND
Milgrom 1983
is a theory of dynamics that strives to supplant Newtonian dynamics and general relativity. Its main motivation is to account for the dynamics of galactic systems and the Universe at large without the ‘dark’ constituents that are required in the standard dynamics. Extensive reviews of MOND can be found in references

Famaey and McGaugh 2012
,
Milgrom 2014
, and
Milgrom 2019
.

MOND introduces a new constant,
a_{0}
, with the dimensions of acceleration. It reduces to standard dynamics when all accelerations in the system at hand are much higher than
a_{0}
. In the opposite limit – the deep-MOND limit (DML) – when all relevant accelerations are much below
a_{0}
, dynamics become space-time scale invariant
Milgrom 2009
.

Given a theory, it is helpful to construct heuristic models of systems thought to be governed by the theory, either as approximate descriptions of actual systems, or as testbeds for studying various aspects of the dynamical theory at hand.
In the case of MOND, the relevant systems are stellar and galactic systems – such as star clusters, disc and spheroidal galaxies, clusters of galaxies, etc.

Examples of heuristic models for disc galaxies are the Kuzmin and the Mestel discs (e.g., Ref.
Binney and Tremaine 2008
).

In standard dynamics, heuristic models for spheroidal astronomical objects have also been in use for many years. Notable among them are the Lane-Emden spheres (described, e.g., in Refs.
Binney and Tremaine 2008
;
Kippenhahn Weigert and Weiss 2012
), which are self-gravitating spheres made of matter with isotropic velocity distribution that satisfies a polytropic equation of state (EoS) – a relation between pressure,
P
, and density,
\rho
, of the form

P=\mathcal{K}\rho^{\gamma}.

(1)

Examples of polytropes in astrophysics are zero-temperature white dwarfs and neutron stars, where
\gamma
can vary between that of a relativistic gas (
\gamma=4/3
) to that of nonrelativistic gas (
\gamma=5/3
) (e.g., Ref.
Kippenhahn Weigert and Weiss 2012
).

As stellar systems, such polytropes arise as spheres with isotropic velocity dispersions, with a phase-space distribution function,
f
, that depends on velocity and position only through the energy,
f({\bf r},{\bf v})=f(E)
, where
E={\bf v}^{2}/2+\phi
(
\phi
is the gravitational potential), and, furthermore
f(E)\propto(-E)^{n-3/2}
for bound constituents (and 0 for unbound values). This gives a polytrope with
\gamma=1+1/n

Binney and Tremaine 2008
. For such stellar systems, Ref.
Binney and Tremaine 2008
shows that one must have
n>1/2
(
\gamma<3
), but
n\leq 1/2
is allowed in general, and I shall consider all values of
n
between 0 and
\infty
(inclusive).

In the context of MOND, the first class of heuristic, spherical models to be discussed – ‘isothermal spheres’ – were described in detail in Ref.
Milgrom 1984
. These are self-gravitating spheres with a radius-independent, but possibly anisotropic velocity distribution. Their isotropic version is a subclass of polytropic spheres, with
\gamma=1
.

More general MOND polytropes (with a polytropic EoS, but obeying MOND dynamics) have been considered as models for astrophysical systems.
For example, Sanders, in Ref.
Sanders 2000
, considered MOND polytropic spheres as models of elliptical galaxies. Beyond the polytropic EoS, Sanders allowed anisotropic velocity distributions, with radius dependent degree of anisotropy.
He considered correlations between global properties of these models – such as mass, size, and velocity dispersions (without discussing the structure of spheres). He showed that the larger variety afforded by anisotropic polytropes – beyond that of MOND isothermal spheres – is needed to make these spheres close models of observed elliptical galaxies. For example, he showed that near-isothermal polytropes – those with
\gamma
values near 1 – better account for the so-called ‘fundamental plane’ of ellipticals – an observed correlation involving global properties.

Later on, Refs.
Ibata et al. 2011
and
Sanders 2012
debated the adequacy of MOND polytropes for describing the structure and dynamics of one specific globular cluster, NGC 2419.
1
1

1

Reference
Sanders 2012
even allowed for a radius-dependent polytropic index.

Both the ‘high-surface-density’ ellipticals considered in Ref.
Sanders 2000
, and typical globular clusters (including NGC 2419), are characterized by high accelerations (
g\gg a_{0}
) in their main body, and dip into the DML only at their outskirts. Their description in MOND thus requires accounting for the full gamut of accelerations between the Newtonian and the DML extremes.

But here I describe and discuss mainly the more limited subclass of deep-MOND polytropes (DMPs), isotropic and anisotropic. While this class of models is more limited in scope, it affords concentrating in more detail on the properties of such spheres.
And to boot, it does already capture many of the idiosyncracies of MOND that are also shared by the wider classes. Some of these characteristics, on the other hand, are specific to the DML and its scale invariance.

DMPs can serve as heuristic models for actual DML astrophysical systems – such as dwarf spheroidal galaxies and galaxy groups – if not in their details, at least in the general scaling relations that these models obey, and which I discuss.
For example – as I discuss in some detail here – they show the strong correlation between the central-surface-densities of baryons and of the putative dark matter halo, pointed to and considered for galaxies in Refs.
Milgrom 2009a
;
Lelli et al. 2016
;
Milgrom 2016
.

They are also used here, with several examples, to demonstrate that the DML
M-\sigma
relation is not unique, but may depend on the particular choice of
\sigma
measure.

II
Deep-MOND polytropic equation

‘Modified-gravity’ formulations of MOND – such as the ‘aquadratic Lagrangian’ formulation
Bekenstein and Milgrom 1984
, and the ‘quasi-linear’ formulation
Milgrom 2010
– predict that in spherical systems, such as I discuss here, the acceleration,
g
, at radius
r
, is given by

g=\nu(g_{\scriptscriptstyle N}/a_{0})g_{\scriptscriptstyle N},

(2)

where
g_{\scriptscriptstyle N}=M(r)G/r^{2}
is the Newtonian acceleration,
and
\nu
is the MOND interpolating function having the following behavior: In the high-acceleration, Newtonian regime,
g_{\scriptscriptstyle N}\gg a_{0}
,
\nu(q\gg 1)\approx 1
, and in the DML, we have
\nu(q\ll 1)\approx q^{-1/2}
. This functional dependence of
g
on
g_{\scriptscriptstyle N}
coincides with the original formulation of MOND in Ref.
Milgrom 1983
.

The hydrostatic-equilibrium equation, which determines the structure of self-gravitating spheres with isotropic velocity distribution, is

\rho g=-\frac{dP}{dr}.

(3)

In the DML we can thus write

\rho\left[\frac{\mathcal{A}_{0}M(r)}{r^{2}}\right]^{1/2}=-\frac{dP}{dr},

(4)

where
M(r)=4\pi\int_{0}^{r}\rho(\bar{r})\bar{r}^{2}~d\bar{r}
, is the accumulated mass, and
\mathcal{A}_{0}\equiv a_{0}G
is the ‘second MOND constant’, and is the only combination of
a_{0}
and
G
that can appear in DML equations, as a result of the scale invariance of this limit
Milgrom 2009
.

Consider self-gravitating spheres whose constituents satisfy a polytropic EoS, and which have an isotropic velocity dispersion,

\sigma^{2}\equiv 3P/\rho=3\mathcal{K}\rho^{1/n},

(5)

where

n\equiv\frac{1}{\gamma-1},

(6)

is used alternately with
\gamma
.

Substituting the EoS in eq. (
4
), we get the integro-differential equation for the density run of DML polytropes (for regions where
\rho\not=0
)
2

2

. For
\gamma\not=1
,

\left[\int_{0}^{r}\rho(\bar{r})\bar{r}^{2}~d\bar{r}\right]^{1/2}=-(n+1)Sr\frac{d\rho^{1/n}}{dr},

(7)

where

S\equiv\frac{\mathcal{K}}{\sqrt{4\pi\mathcal{A}_{0}}}.

(8)

For the special case
\gamma=1
(
n=\infty
), the equation of hydrostatic-equilibrium is

\left[\int_{0}^{r}\rho(\bar{r})\bar{r}^{2}~d\bar{r}\right]^{1/2}=-S\frac{d\ln\rho}{d\ln r}.

(9)

[This can also be obtained by taking the limit
n\rightarrow\infty
of eq. (

\rho^{1/n}=1+n^{-1}\ln\rho+O(n^{-2})

Equation (

\gamma\not=1

\int_{0}^{r}\rho(\bar{r})\bar{r}^{2}~d\bar{r}=-(n+1)S_{\scriptscriptstyle N}r^{2}\frac{d\rho^{1/n}}{dr},

II.1
Dimensional analysis and dimensionless forms

The coefficient
S
in eq. (
8
) has dimensions
[S]=[\ell^{3/n}][M^{1/2-1/n}]
.
We would like to absorb it by defining length and mass units and express all quantities in these units.

Two special values of
\gamma
suggest themselves. For
\gamma=1
(
n=\infty
),
S
has dimensions of
[M^{1/2}]
, and
\mathcal{K}
has dimensions of velocity squared. This is the isothermal-sphere case – discussed in detail in Ref.
Milgrom 1984
– where the constant velocity dispersion
(
\sigma^{4}\propto\mathcal{K}^{2}
) determines the total mass
M=(81/4)(\mathcal{K}^{2}/\mathcal{A}_{0}
) independent of the size. Scaling up the size of any such system, gives another sphere with the same velocities and total mass, but scaled density.

The other special case is
n=2
, where the dimensions of
S
are
[\ell^{3/2}]
. The polytropic coefficient
then defines an absolute size scale for the system
\ell\equiv S^{2/3}
. Systems with
n=2
and a given
\mathcal{K}
have all the same size, but different masses (and thus densities).
In terms of the dimensionless radius
w\equiv r/9^{1/3}\ell
we write eq. (
7
) for this case as

\left[\int_{0}^{w}\rho(\bar{w})\bar{w}^{2}~d\bar{w}\right]^{1/2}=-w\frac{d\rho^{1/2}}{dw}.

(12)

Clearly, if
\rho(w)
is a solution, so is
a\rho(w)
for any
a
.

For other values of
n
, we can choose a length unit, and
S
then defines mass and density scales,
with which we construct the dimensionless form of the equations. The values of
n
and
\mathcal{K}

define a Mass-radius relation.

As somewhat of an aside, note that,
in principle, MOND involves two dimensionful constants,
G
and
a_{0}
, which together with
\mathcal{K}
can be used to define absolute, ‘natural’ scales of length, mass, and density. For example, the relation
\hat{M}G/\ell=a_{0}
could be used together with
S=\ell^{3/n}\hat{M}^{1/2-1/n}
to define the scales
\hat{M}
and
\ell
(and from them construct a density scale).

This procedure is, however, neither useful nor desirable for systems in the present DML context, which is the limit
a_{0}\rightarrow\infty
,
G\rightarrow 0
, where it is ‘illegal’ to use
G
and/or
a_{0}
separately. Only the combination
\mathcal{A}_{0}=Ga_{0}
can be used. Viewed differently, the inutility of such a procedure can be seen by noting that DML systems have typical sizes that are much larger
(infinitely in the limit) than their MOND radius defined as
r_{\scriptscriptstyle M}\equiv MG/a_{0}
. So it is not instructive to measure lengths in units of the MOND radius.

So, we proceed by defining some arbitrary length unit
\ell
and define a density unit
\hat{\rho}

such that

\ell^{3}\hat{\rho}^{1-2/n}=(n+1)^{2}S^{2}.

(13)

Then, defining
\zeta\equiv\rho/\hat{\rho}
and
y\equiv r/\ell
, we write eq. (
7
) as the deep-MOND, dimensionless, polytropic integro-differential equation for
\zeta(y)

m^{1/2}(y)\equiv\left[\int_{0}^{y}\zeta\bar{y}^{2}d\bar{y}\right]^{1/2}=-y\frac{d\zeta^{1/n}}{dy},

(14)

where
m(y)
is the dimensionless accumulated mass.
As in the Newtonian limit, it is useful to work with the dependent variable
\theta=\zeta^{1/n}
, in terms of which

m^{1/2}(y)\equiv\left[\int_{0}^{y}\theta^{n}\bar{y}^{2}d\bar{y}\right]^{1/2}=-y\theta^{\prime}.

(15)

Or, converted into a 2nd order differential equation, we have

\theta^{n}(y)=y^{-2}[(y\theta^{\prime})^{2}]^{\prime}=2\theta^{\prime}\theta^{\prime\prime}+\frac{2}{y}(\theta^{\prime})^{2}.

(16)

The Newtonian analogue, dimensionless Lane-Emden equation reads

\theta^{n}(z)=-z^{-2}(z^{2}\theta^{\prime})^{\prime}=-\theta^{\prime\prime}-\frac{2}{z}\theta^{\prime}.

(17)

The dimensionless variables are defined differently, and the equation is of a very different nature (e.g., the right-hand side of the latter is linear in
\theta
).

We see from eq. (
15
) that hydrostatic-equilibrium with the EoS dictates that inside the sphere, where
\zeta>0
, the quantity
\theta\equiv\zeta^{1/n}
satisfies
\theta^{\prime}=-m^{1/2}/y
, which is the dimensionless, DML acceleration (
-m/y^{2}
being the dimensionless Newtonian acceleration). Thus, inside the sphere (but not outside)
\theta=-\phi
, where
\phi
is the dimensionless DML gravitational potential, with its free additive constant chosen such that
\phi
vanishes where the density does, which in our case is at the edge of the sphere (see below).

Equation (
16
) requires specifying two boundary conditions.
We shall see below that we need to have
\theta^{\prime}(0)=0
. The value
\theta(0)
can take up arbitrary positive values, but the solutions for different
\theta(0)
values are simply related to each other. Equation (
16
) is invariant to
\theta\rightarrow\lambda\theta
and
y\rightarrow\lambda^{(2-n)/3}y
. So the solution for one value of
\theta(0)
generates those for all other values.
I thus set
\theta(0)=1
hereafter.

Once the solution
\theta(y)
is found for a given
n
and
\theta(0)=1
, call it
\theta_{\scriptscriptstyle(n)}(y)
, the solution for any value of
\theta(0)
is

\theta(y)=\lambda\theta_{\scriptscriptstyle(n)}[\lambda^{-(2-n)/3}y];~~~~~\lambda=\theta(0).

(18)

Putting together all the reductions we made, one finds that for given
n
and
\mathcal{K}
, the solution
\theta_{\scriptscriptstyle(n)}(y)
fans into a one-parameter family of solutions. The parameter spanning this family can be the unit length
\ell
chosen arbitrarily, or the unit density
\hat{\rho}
.

One useful choice of
\hat{\rho}
is the central density
\rho_{0}\equiv\rho(0)
, in terms of which the general solution is

\rho(r)=\rho_{0}\theta_{\scriptscriptstyle(n)}^{n}[\rho_{0}^{(n-2)/3n}(n+1)^{-2/3}S^{-2/3}r].

(19)

We shall also use below an alternative independent variable defined by

x=\frac{2}{\sqrt{27}}y^{3/2}.

(20)

Then, eq. (
16
) becomes

\theta^{n}(x)=\frac{1}{2x}[(x\theta^{\prime})^{2}]^{\prime}=x\theta^{\prime}\theta^{\prime\prime}+(\theta^{\prime})^{2},

(21)

where here the derivatives are with respect to
x
.

In the Newtonian case, the dimensions of
S_{\scriptscriptstyle N}
are
[\ell^{3/n-1}][M^{1-1/n}]
. One special value of
n
is

n=3
(
\gamma=4/3
), where, in analogy with the DML
n=\infty
, isothermal case, the mass is determined by the value of
\mathcal{K}
. This value of
n
occurs, e.g., in the EoS of a relativistic degenerate fermion gas, where the special value of the mass determines the maximal mass of a white dwarf.

The other special value is
n=1
(
\gamma=2
), where the dimensions of
S_{\scriptscriptstyle N}
are
[\ell^{2}]
, resulting in a size of the polytrope that – in analogy with the DML
n=2
case – is determined by
\mathcal{K}
.

II.2
Loss of scale invariance

One of the basic tenets of MOND is that the DML is scale invariant
Milgrom 2009
, in the sense that the equations describing self gravitating systems in the DML are invariant to space-time scaling of the system’s degrees of freedom,
(r,t)\rightarrow\lambda(r,t)
. Since we are dealing here with time independent systems, scale invariance implies invariance to scaling of all lengths,
r\rightarrow\lambda r
. Such invariance means that scaling all lengths in a solution of the theory produces another solution.

It may thus appear puzzling that for the general case,
\gamma\not=1
, our eq. (
7
), which is meant to describe self-gravitating systems, is not scale invariant: Under scaling (under which
\rho\rightarrow\lambda^{-3}\rho
) the left-hand side is invariant, but the right-hand side is scaled by
\lambda^{-3/n}
.

The scale invariance of DML gravity is encapsulated in the fact that the only dimensionful constant that appears in it is
\mathcal{A}_{0}
, whose dimensions,
[\mathcal{A}_{0}]=[\ell^{4}][t^{-4}][M^{-1}]
, are invariant to scaling
of length and time units
. However, here, by imposing the polytropic EoS, we introduce another dimensionful constant,

\mathcal{K}
, whose dimensions are
[\mathcal{K}]=[\ell^{2+3/n}][t^{-2}][M^{-1/n}]
, and whose value (for
n<\infty
) does change under scaling of the time-length units.
So, if a given sphere is a solution for a certain
\mathcal{K}
, the scaled sphere will be a solution, not for the same
\mathcal{K}
, but for
\mathcal{K}^{\prime}=\lambda^{3/n}\mathcal{K}
.

When the EoS results from microscopic considerations, as in the EoS of a degenerate fermion gas, the problem is no more one of pure gravity. The microscopic physics breaks down the scale invariance: It introduces dimensionful constants that are not invariant to unit scaling (in contract with
\mathcal{A}_{0}
). The constant
\mathcal{K}
is then a ‘constant of nature’.

However, what is the underlying cause for scale-invariance breakdown in the application to galactic systems, which are purely gravitational? The cause is hidden in the processes that supposedly drive such self gravitating systems to become approximate polytropes. We do not know what such processes are, and so we do not know why they should cause the polytropic coefficient to depend on system size, as described above, thus apparently breaking scale invariance. But this need not worry us further.

III.4
Behavior at the edge

I now show that for
\gamma>1
(
n<\infty
), DMPs must have a finite edge where the density drops to
0
, and beyond which we discard the solution.
The right-hand side of eq. (
15
) is
-d\theta/d\ln{y}
.
As long as
\theta
is still positive (i.e. within the sphere) it equals
-\phi
; so to balance gravity,
\theta
has to be a decreasing function of
y
(thus of
\ln{y}
). To have the region with
\theta>0
extend to infinity, would mean that
\theta(\ln{y})
is a positive, ever-decreasing function. Such a function must have its derivative tend to 0 at infinity. But,
-d\theta/d\ln{y}\rightarrow 0
at infinity is inconsistent with the left-hand side of eq. (
15
) having to be nondecreasing (and positive). Thus the region where
\theta>0
cannot extend to infinity, and
\theta
must vanish at a finite radius.

This contrasts with the behavior of Newtonian polytropes, which for
n<5
have a finite radius, for
n=5
have an infinite extent, but a finite total mass, and have a diverging mass for
n>5

Kippenhahn Weigert and Weiss 2012
. The fact that DMPs all have finite masses, and a finite radius for all finite
n
, is due to the stronger MOND gravity.

If
y_{\scriptscriptstyle o}
is the radius of the edge [where
\theta(y_{\scriptscriptstyle o})=0
], and
m_{t}\equiv m(y_{\scriptscriptstyle o})
is the total mass,
then the dominant behavior just interior to the edge is

\theta(y)\approx-m_{t}^{1/2}\ln\left(\frac{y}{y_{\scriptscriptstyle o}}\right).

(28)

This is because
\ln(y)
annihilates the right-hand side of eq. (
16
), and
\theta^{n}
on the left-hand side is balanced by higher order terms in
\theta
.

This logarithmic behavior can be understood as follows: The enclosed mass converges to
m_{t}
at radii below
y_{\scriptscriptstyle o}
; for larger
n
values it does so much below
y_{\scriptscriptstyle o}
(see Fig.
3
). Once this happens, the DML potential becomes that outside a spherical mass
m_{t}
:
\phi=m_{t}^{1/2}\ln(y)
,
up to an additive constant, which is fixed by our definition
\theta(y_{\scriptscriptstyle o})=0
(remember that inside
y_{\scriptscriptstyle o}
,
\theta=-\phi
).
Figure
7
shows directly, for some
n
values, how far below
y_{\scriptscriptstyle o}
the logarithmic behavior of eq. (
28
) is a good approximation.

Our definition
\theta\equiv\zeta^{1/n}
implies that
\theta=0
outside the edge radius. That
\phi=-\theta
inside the sphere follows from the EoS and the dynamics. But, of course,
\phi
does not vanish outside the sphere where it is still given by

\phi=m_{t}^{1/2}\ln(y/y_{\scriptscriptstyle o}).

(29)

The next order correction to
\theta
below the edge gives

\theta(y)\approx-m_{t}^{1/2}\ln\left(\frac{y}{y_{\scriptscriptstyle o}}\right)-b\left(1-\frac{y}{y_{\scriptscriptstyle o}}\right)^{n+2},

(30)

where

b=\frac{m_{t}^{(n-1)/2}y_{\scriptscriptstyle o}^{3}}{2(n+1)(n+2)}.

(31)

Numerical solutions for several
n
values are compared with expression (
30
) in Fig.

.

Integrating the density in eq. (

IV.1.1
Universal
M-\langle\sigma^{2}\rangle
relation

We saw above that the with some choices of the characteristic velocity dispersion of the polytrope, there exists a triple relation between total mass,
M
,
n
, and
\sigma
; the ratio
M/\sigma^{4}
is not universal. However, all DMPs – more generally, all solutions of the DML hydrostatic-equilibrium equation, with any EoS – do satisfy a universal relation between
M
and the global, mass-averaged, velocity dispersion.

M\mathcal{A}_{0}=\frac{9}{4}\langle\sigma^{2}\rangle^{2},

(37)

where

\langle\sigma^{2}\rangle\equiv M^{-1}\int 4\pi\rho(r)\sigma^{2}(r)r^{2}~dr=\frac{12\pi}{M}\int P(r)r^{2}~dr.

(38)

This holds despite the apparent loss of scale invariance discussed above (but scale invariance of the DML does underlie this relation).
Ref.
Gerhard and Spergel 1992
showed this for the first time for spherical systems. Reference
Milgrom 1994
showed that it holds for arbitrary self-gravitating DML systems in the ‘aquadratic Lagrangian’ theory of Ref.
Bekenstein and Milgrom 1984
, and Ref.
Milgrom 2014a
showed it to be a general result of modified-gravity DML.

In the special case of self-gravitating ‘gas spheres’, this follows from the general DML hydrostatic-equilibrium eq. (
4
),
noting that

\int P(r)r^{2}~dr=-\frac{1}{3}\int r^{3}P^{\prime}(r)=\frac{1}{3}\mathcal{A}_{0}^{1/2}\int r^{2}\rho(r)M^{1/2}(r)

=\frac{1}{12\pi}\mathcal{A}_{0}^{1/2}\int M^{\prime}(r)M^{1/2}(r)=\frac{1}{18\pi}\mathcal{A}_{0}^{1/2}\int[M^{3/2}]^{\prime}(r)

=\frac{1}{18\pi}\mathcal{A}_{0}^{1/2}M^{3/2}.

(39)

Putting this together with eq. (
38
) gives eq. (
37
).

IV.2
Relations involving the size

We can also use some measure of the system size as a parameter. This radius will be some function of
n
because it depends on
\theta_{\scriptscriptstyle(n)}
multiplied by
our length unit

\ell=\rho_{0}^{(2-n)/3n}(n+1)^{2/3}S^{2/3}.

(40)

For example, we see in Fig.
6
that approximating
\zeta
by the first two terms in the expansion (
25
)
\zeta(y)\approx[1-(2/\sqrt{27})y^{3/2}]^{n}
gives a good approximation up to radii of order
y_{h}
– the radius where
\rho
drops to half of its central value. So, we can use this approximation to estimate
y_{h}
as

y_{h}\approx\frac{3}{4^{1/3}}(1-2^{-1/n})^{2/3}.

(41)

For
n\gg 1
this gives to lowest order in
n^{-1}

y_{h}\approx\frac{3\ln^{2/3}(2)}{4^{1/3}}n^{-2/3}.

(42)

Since this indicates that
y_{h}^{3/2}
decreases as
n^{-1}
, we deduce that approximation (
42
) does not improve with increasing
n
, since at
y_{h}
, higher order terms in the expansion (
25
) do not decrease with
n
.

Approximation (
42
) tells us, in conjunction with eq. (
40
), that in general,
r_{h}=\ell y_{h}
depends on
n
,
\mathcal{K}
and
\rho_{0}
.

We can check approximation (
42
) by examining the limit of
r_{h}
for
n\rightarrow\infty

Milgrom 1984
for DML isothermal spheres.
In this limit, the above expression for
r_{h}
gives

r_{h}\rightarrow\frac{3\ln^{2/3}(2)}{4^{1/3}}\rho_{0}^{-1/3}S^{2/3}.

(43)

But, in this limit,
S\rightarrow(M/81\pi)^{1/2}
; so

r_{h}\rightarrow\frac{3\ln^{2/3}(2)}{(324\pi)^{1/3}}\left(\frac{M}{\rho_{0}}\right)^{1/3}=0.234\left(\frac{M}{\rho_{0}}\right)^{1/3}.

(44)

From the results of Ref.
Milgrom 1984
I get for DML isothermal spheres the exact result

r_{h}=\frac{(2^{1/3}-1)^{2/3}3^{5/3}}{(324\pi)^{1/3}}\left(\frac{M}{\rho_{0}}\right)^{1/3}=0.253\left(\frac{M}{\rho_{0}}\right)^{1/3}.

(45)

So approximation (
42
) remains good for any
n
.

IV.3
The ‘central-surface-densities relation’

Reference
Milgrom 2009a
pointed out that MOND predicts a correlation between the central-surface-density of an isolated system, and the same quantity calculated for the ‘dynamical’ mass distribution. The former quantity is the column density of ‘baryons’ – the true density,
\rho({\bf r})
– along some symmetry axis going through (an assumed) symmetry axis of the system – such as the symmetry axis of a disc, or any diameter for a spherical system. The second quantity is defined as follows: We first determine the potential field,
\phi({\bf r})
, of the system, given
\rho
(in our case, using MOND). We then determine the mass distribution,
\rho_{\scriptscriptstyle D}({\bf r})
, that would give rise to this potential in Newtonian dynamics:

\rho_{\scriptscriptstyle D}\equiv\frac{1}{4\pi G}\Delta\phi.

(46)

A dark-matter adherent would interpret
\rho_{\scriptscriptstyle D}
as the total, ‘dynamical’ density of baryons plus dark matter. But in MOND,
\rho_{\scriptscriptstyle D}-\rho
is a fictitious, ‘phantom’ density.

Since by MOND, baryons determine the full dynamics,
\rho_{\scriptscriptstyle D}({\bf r})
is calculable from
\rho({\bf r})
(in standard dynamics, where dark matter and baryons are separate entities, any values of the two are acceptable as long as
\rho_{\scriptscriptstyle D}\geq\rho
).
As corollaries of this general rule, we can derive specific ‘laws’, or correlations, between properties of
\rho({\bf r})
and
\rho_{\scriptscriptstyle D}({\bf r})
.

Here, I discuss the predicted correlation between the quantities

\Sigma_{B}\equiv\int\rho~dr~~{\rm and}~~~\Sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle D}\equiv\int\rho_{\scriptscriptstyle D}~dr,

(47)

where the integral is along some symmetry axis.
Note that
\Sigma G
is a measure of a gravitational acceleration. So it is useful to refer it to
a_{0}
by defining the ‘MOND surface density’

\Sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle M}\equiv\frac{a_{0}}{2\pi G}.

(48)

MOND predicts a strong correlation between
\Sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle B}
and
\Sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle D}
for the full range of accelerations.

For disc galaxies, Ref.
Lelli et al. 2016
found such a relation between the two attributes evaluated along the symmetry axis. Reference
Milgrom 2016
then showed that modified-gravity formulations of MOND predict an exact, universal such relation that applies to all mass distributions in the disc

\Sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle D}=\Sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle M}\mathcal{S}(\Sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle B}/\Sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle M}),

(49)

and showed how
\mathcal{S}
is determined from the MOND interpolating function, in very good agreement with the observations.

However, as discussed in Ref.
Milgrom 2009a
, spherical systems do not obey such a universal relation. While MOND still predicts a correlation, the exact relation depends on the mass distribution in the sphere. Here, I use the gamut of DMPs to see how tight such a MOND relation is among them.

For all types of systems, MOND predicts that for
\Sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle B}\gg\Sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle M}
,
\Sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle D}\approx\Sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle B}
, namely, very little central ‘phantom matter’ for high-surface-density galaxies.

The DML is reached when
\Sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle B}\ll\Sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle M}
.
4
4

4

a_{0}
and
G
appear in
\Sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle M}
not through their product
\mathcal{A}_{0}
. But, there is a
G
appearing in the definition of
\Sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle D}
in terms of the accelerations; so in the end only
\mathcal{A}_{0}
appears in our DML result.
In this limit MOND predicts an approximate correlation of the form

\Sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle D}\propto(\Sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle B}\Sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle M})^{1/2}\gg\Sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle B}
; so, it is useful to define

\eta\equiv\frac{\Sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle D}}{(\Sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle B}\Sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle M})^{1/2}},

(50)

and study how
\eta
varies among systems.

For the above-mentioned relation for disc galaxies,
\eta=2
is universal. For spheres, Ref.
Milgrom 2009a
estimated
\eta\sim 2.5

For spheres, in general, we have

\Sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle D}\equiv 2\int_{\scriptscriptstyle 0}^{\scriptscriptstyle\infty}\rho_{\scriptscriptstyle D}dr\equiv-\frac{1}{2\pi G}\int_{\scriptscriptstyle 0}^{\scriptscriptstyle\infty}\vec{\nabla}\cdot\vec{g}dr=

-\frac{1}{2\pi G}\int_{\scriptscriptstyle 0}^{\scriptscriptstyle\infty}(g^{\prime}+2g/r)dr=\frac{1}{\pi G}\int_{\scriptscriptstyle 0}^{\scriptscriptstyle\infty}|g|dr/r,

(51)

where the first term is dropped assuming that the acceleration vanishes at the center as well as at infinity.

For a general spherical mass distribution

\eta=\pi^{-1/2}\frac{\int_{\scriptscriptstyle 0}^{\scriptscriptstyle\infty}M^{1/2}(r)r^{-2}dr}{[\int_{\scriptscriptstyle 0}^{\scriptscriptstyle\infty}\rho(r)dr]^{1/2}}.

(52)

Using the dimensionless variables for our polytropes

\eta=2\frac{\int_{\scriptscriptstyle 0}^{\scriptscriptstyle\infty}m^{1/2}(y)y^{-2}dy}{[\int_{\scriptscriptstyle 0}^{\scriptscriptstyle\infty}\zeta(y)dy]^{1/2}}.

(53)

This tells us that
\eta
is independent of the polytropic coefficient
\mathcal{K}
, but that it can depend on
n
.

V
Anisotropic-velocity polytropes

The class of DMPs with isotropic velocity distribution I discussed above can be enlarged in different ways. There are the spherical polytropes with the full acceleration range from the Newtonian to the DML. One may want to allow for anisotropic velocity dispersions, and one can further generalize by allowing one or more of the EoS parameters (
\mathcal{K}
,
n
, or the anisotropy ratio) to be radius-dependent.
While such generalizations afford larger flexibility, they are also less amenable to the general considerations that underlie our treatment here. For example, allowing
r
dependence of a parameter introduces a preferred distance scale, which complicates the discussion.

Unlike gas spheres such as stars, where the velocity dispersions are isotropic due to short relaxation times, in stellar systems, such as dwarf spheroidal galaxies, the velocity distributions are known not to be isotropic.
In this Section I thus discuss the more general class of anisotropic polytropes, but with a constant ratio between the tangential and the radial velocity dispersions.

In such systems, the velocity distribution at a point at radius
r
is characterized by two velocity dispersions,
\sigma_{r}
in the radial direction, and
\sigma_{t}
in any tangential direction. The three-dimensional dispersion is
\sigma=\sqrt{\sigma_{r}^{2}+2\sigma_{t}^{2}}
, and the anisotropy ratio is defined as
\beta=1-\sigma_{t}^{2}/\sigma_{r}^{2}
. The phase-space distribution function of these systems depends on the position and velocity vectors through the energy,
E
, and the angular momentum,
L
, as
f(E,L)\propto L^{-\beta}(-E)^{n-3/2}
.

Defining the pressure
P=\rho\sigma_{r}^{2}
, the DML hydrostatic-equilibrium equation (
4
) is modified to read

\rho\left[\frac{\mathcal{A}_{0}M(r)}{r^{2}}\right]^{1/2}=-\frac{dP}{dr}-\frac{2\beta P}{r}=-r^{-2\beta}\frac{d(r^{2\beta}P)}{dr}.

(54)

These spheres all satisfy the DML,
M-\sigma
relation (
37
); it is a general result of modified-gravity DML, but can also easily be shown following the same derivation as in Sec.
IV.1.1
,
mutatis mutandis
.

Using the same units of length and density as in the
\beta=0
case,
eq. (
54
) is written in the dimensionless form

[m(y)]^{1/2}\equiv\left[\int_{0}^{y}\theta^{n}\bar{y}^{2}d\bar{y}\right]^{1/2}=-y\theta^{\prime}-\xi\theta=-y^{1-\xi}(y^{\xi}\theta)^{\prime},

(55)

where

\xi=2\beta/(n+1).

(56)

It is useful to work with the dependent variable

\kappa\equiv y^{\xi}\theta
, in terms of which we have

[m(y)]^{1/2}=\left[\int_{0}^{y}\kappa^{n}\bar{y}^{\eta}d\bar{y}\right]^{1/2}=-y^{1-\xi}\kappa^{\prime}=-\xi\frac{d\kappa}{d(y^{\xi})},

(57)

where

\eta\equiv 2-n\xi=2(1-\beta/\gamma).

(58)

Since
\beta\leq 1
, we have
\eta\geq 0
for any
n\geq 0
.
This gives the 2nd order differential equation

\kappa^{n}(y)=y^{-\eta}[(y^{1-\xi}\kappa^{\prime})^{2}]^{\prime}.

(59)

The units of length and density are related, as before, by eq. (
13
), in which
n
and
\mathcal{K}
appear, but not
\beta
.

Note that for
\beta\not=0
,
\theta
is no longer
-\phi
, as its derivative is not the MOND acceleration. For this reason, it is possible for
\theta
(and thus
\rho
) to increase with radius, because it is not
-\theta^{\prime}
that balances gravity, but
-y^{-\xi}\kappa^{\prime}
that does; so
\kappa
has to decrease, instead.

V.1
Behavior at the origin

We see from eq. (
55
) that for
m(y)
to vanish at the origin, we cannot have a constant density near the origin. Rather, we have to have
\kappa
constant there, or

\theta(y)\propto y^{-\xi},

(60)

or a density
\rho\propto r^{-2\beta n/(n+1)}=r^{-2\beta/\gamma}
.
As in the isotropic case, a solution with any choice of the normalization – for example that with
\kappa(0)=1
[
\theta(y)=y^{-\xi}
] and
m(y)=y^{3-\xi}/(3-\xi)
, near the origin – generates those for all normalizations.
In solving the problem numerically, and presenting the results below, I use eq. (
57
), with the boundary conditions
m(0)=0
and
\kappa(0)=1
.

V.2
Behavior near the edge

The same arguments that led us to deduce that DMPs with
\beta=0
have a finite edge, apply also for
\beta\not=0
(for
\gamma\not=1
);
the right-hand side of eq. (
57
) can be written as
-\xi d\kappa/d(y^{\xi})
. So as before,
\kappa
positive that decreases all the way to
\infty
implies that the right-hand side tends to zero at infinity, inconsistent with the behavior of the left-hand side.

We see from eq.(
55
) that, since
m(y)
goes to
m_{t}=m(y_{\scriptscriptstyle o})
at
y_{\scriptscriptstyle o}
, the behavior of
\theta
just below
y_{\scriptscriptstyle o}
has to be

\theta\approx m_{t}^{1/2}[(y/y_{\scriptscriptstyle o})^{-\xi}-1]/\xi.

(61)

[This generalizes expression (
28
) to which it tends for
\beta\rightarrow 0
.)

The DML gravitational potential, just below the edge, and everywhere outside it, is still given by eq. (
29
), irrespective of the value of
\beta
.

V.3
Analytic solutions

As in the isotropic case, analytic solutions are known for
n=\infty
and
n=0
.
The former is the ‘isothermal’ case, for which the analytic solution is given in Ref.
Milgrom 1984

and reads

\rho(r)=ar^{-2\beta}[1+br^{(3-2\beta)/2}]^{-3},

(62)

where
a
is an arbitrary positive constant, and

is determined from

,
\beta
, and

, so that the total mass,

, and the three-dimensional velocity dispersion,

, satisfy the universal DML relation

. It is also the only

value for which the DMP does not have a finite radius.

For
n=0
, eq. (

) can be easily solved with the boundary conditions
\kappa(0)
constant, and
\kappa^{\prime}(0)=0
:

\kappa=a-\omega y^{3/2+\xi};~~~~~\omega\equiv 2/\sqrt{3}(3+2\xi),

(63)

where

is any positive constant. Thus

\theta=ay^{-\xi}-\omega y^{3/2}.

(64)

The edge, where
t(y_{\scriptscriptstyle o})=0
occurs at

y_{\scriptscriptstyle o}=(a/\omega)^{2/(3+2\xi)},

(65)

so we can also write

\theta=ay_{\scriptscriptstyle o}^{-\xi}[(y/y_{\scriptscriptstyle o})^{-\xi}-(y/y_{\scriptscriptstyle o})^{3/2}].

(66)

To demonstrate some of the variety afforded by allowing anisotropy, I show some results of numerical solutions using eq. (

The central-surface-densities relation

In Fig.
16
, I show the ratio of central surface densities,
\eta
, defined in eq. (

), as a function of
n

\beta=-0.5,~-0.3,~-0.1,~0,~0.1,~0.2
. Also shown are the limiting values of
\eta
for ‘isothermal spheres’ (
n=\infty
), calculated analytically from the density law (
62
), and found to be

\eta_{\scriptscriptstyle\infty}=-\frac{4b^{3/2}\Gamma(-2b)\Gamma(2b)}{\sqrt{\Gamma(4b)\Gamma(2-4b)}}=\frac{\sqrt{4\pi b}}{\sin{(2\pi b)}}\left[\frac{\sin{\pi(1-4b)}}{1-4b}\right]^{1/2},

where
b=(3-2\beta)^{-1}
, and
\Gamma
is the Gamma function. This result holds only for
\beta<1/2
. For
\beta\geq 1/2
, both
\Sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle B}
and
\Sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle D}
diverge (see below).
For example, for
\beta=-1/2
,
b=1/4
and
\eta_{\scriptscriptstyle\infty}=\pi
.

n=0

Another limiting case is
\beta\rightarrow-\infty
for large
n
.
This
\beta
limit corresponds to purely tangential, i.e., circular, orbits of the constituents. Since, for high
n
, the velocities become
r
-independent, the constituents must lie in a single, thin spherical shell. For this case one easily derives
\eta=2
. This checks, as the limit of expression (
67
) for
b\rightarrow 0
is 2.

Note that
\beta>0
implies a diverging density at the origin. Such models may still be relevant if we modify the small region around the origin. However, such models may give formally diverging values of
\Sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle D}
and
\Sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle B}
. Near the origin we have
\rho\propto r^{-\delta}
, where
\delta=2\beta n/(n+1)
. The MOND acceleration behaves there as
g\propto r^{1-\delta}
. Thus, for
\delta\geq 1
, both
\Sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle B}
and
\Sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle D}
diverge. For
\delta=1
, they both diverge logarithmically, and so
\eta
diverges as
\sqrt{\ln{r}}
at the center; e.g., expression (
67
) diverges for
\beta=1/2
.
For
\delta>1
,
\Sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle D}
diverges as
r^{(1-\delta)/2}
, and
\Sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle B}
as
r^{1-\delta}
; so the expression for
\eta
is still formally finite, but it involves a ratio of diverging quantities. Expression (
67
) for
\eta_{\scriptscriptstyle\infty}
is applicable only for
\beta<1/2
.

A class of spheres unrelated to polytropes are those with a power-law density distribution,
\rho(r)\propto r^{k}
within some finite radius and zero outside. Such spheres have
\eta=2\sqrt{(k+3)/(k+1)}
, which span values from
\eta=2\sqrt{3}
for
k=0
(homogeneous sphere – as above), to
\eta=2
for
k=\infty
, which, again, describes an infinitely thin, hollow, spherical shell. Such thin shells seem to have the lowest
\eta
value for a spherical distribution.

We see that while
\eta
does depend on
n
and
\beta
, the polytropes with the given range of
\beta
and all
n
values – and also other spherical systems – still satisfy a rather narrow DML central-surface-densities relation with
\eta
varying only by a factor of about 2, between
\eta=2
and
\eta=4
.

VI
Discussion

I have discussed a limited but indicative class of heuristic DML models of self-gravitating spheres, with well-defined distribution functions. These are polytropic spheres, with either isotropic velocity distributions, which I discuss in more detail, or polytropes with a constant velocity anisotropy, treated more succinctly.

One possible enlargement of the class could involve treatment of systems that are not fully in the DML, but the accelerations in which extend from the Newtonian to the DML regime. This was done in Ref.
Milgrom 1984
for only the ‘isothermal’ (
n=\infty
) case. These would be more relevant to ‘high-surface-density’ systems, such as massive elliptical galaxies and globular clusters.

One important thing that we learn about such, more general, MONDian spheres, already from the present study, is that they are all of finite radius (for
\gamma>1
).
As long as the acceleration stays above
a_{0}
, the sphere behaves as a Lane-Emden one. But then, if the corresponding Lane-Emden sphere is extended, the acceleration must, at large enough radii, enter and remain in the DML. Then our arguments here can be carried to show that the density must drop to 0 at a finite radius.

For
n<5
, Newtonian polytropes with
MG/r^{2}_{\scriptscriptstyle out}\gg a_{0}
are Newtonian everywhere.
