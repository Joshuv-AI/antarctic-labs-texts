A Numerical System For Nested Spaces - Defining An Intuitive, Universal Coordinate System For Self-Similarity
Petal Belle Mokryn

Abstract
In this paper I introduce a simple, universal numerical system for describing all self-similar nested spaces. For a nested space made of N > 1 N>1 smaller copies of itself, the location of all points can be specified using the double shift space { 0 , 1 , … , N − 1 } ℤ \{0,1,...,N-1\}^{\mathbb{Z}} . Connectivity rules are established to fully define the topology of the nested space, and immediate consequences are investigated using the numerical system defined. I introduce the numerical system and demonstrate how it unifies previous existing works under a single, simple framework, to argue for its adoption as standard in discussions of nested fractals.

Introduction

Ever since the 70’s, there has been a prevalent interest in self-similarity and fractal spaces, with many famous examples such as the Cantor set and the Sierpinski triangle, Julia sets, and more.

Mathematicians and physicists alike have been drawn to fractal mathematics like moths to a flame, enticed by the plethora of new concepts hanging like fruit ripe for the picking, that may be key to understanding many systems.

There are applications of fractal mathematics in Lattice Quantum Gravity

, condensed matter physics

]
, stochastics
[

]
, economics

]

One of the simplest kinds of fractals, and thus the most studied, are the
nested fractals
. Roughly speaking, that means fractals made of some finite
N>1
number of smaller copies of themselves. Examples include the Cantor Set, Sierpinski Gasket, Sierpinski Carpet, the Snowflake fractal, and more.

In this paper, I define a rigorous yet highly general notion of ”
N
-parts nested spaces”, capturing much of the existing literature on the topic under a single, general framework. I demonstrate that all nested spaces can be simply and effectively described using the double shift space
\{0,1,...,N-1\}^{\mathbb{Z}}
equipped with a suitable topology. I define an equivalence relation I call ”connectivity rules”, which defines a sort of arithmetic on
\{0,1,...,N-1\}^{\mathbb{Z}}
, and fully details the topological structure of nested spaces.

The numerical system introduced in this paper is a simple, effective way to describe nested spaces, which generalizes all previous notations and constructions, and gives an easy and effective form of notation, reminiscent of positive real numbers.

Background Formulation

2.1
Defining Nested Spaces

Nesting, in this paper, is a topological property. First I will define which sets
can
be nested sets/spaces. Using those definitions, I will then define the
N
-parts nested cell-structure topology
. Finally, nested spaces and nested sets will be defined as sets that can be nested, endowed with either the
N
-parts nested cell-structure topology or with the appropriate compact subspace topology of it, respectively.

2.1.1
Definition 1: Nested Sets.

Let
S
be a non-empty set. We say
S
can be a
N
-parts nested set
, if there exist
N>1
distinct, non-trivial, non-identity functions
F_{0},...,F_{N-1}:S\to S
, such that each
F_{i}
is defined on all of
S
, and the following equation holds:

S=\underset{i=0,...,N-1}{\bigcup}F_{i}\left[S\right]

(1)

The set
S
, if endowed with and compact under the
nested cell-structure topology
defined later in this paper, will then be called a
N
-parts nested set, and the functions
F_{i}
will be called its nesting relations. Note that very few restrictions are placed on what kind of functions the nesting relations actually are, as this paper aims to be as general as possible.

The following equation holds trivially:

\forall i=0,...,N-1:F_{i}\left[S\right]=F_{i}\left[\underset{j=0,...,N-1}{\bigcup}F_{j}S\right]=\underset{j=0,...,N-1}{\bigcup}\left(F_{i}\circ F_{j}\right)\left[S\right]

(2)

2.1.2
Definition 2: Nested Spaces.

We say a set
X
can be a
N
-parts nested space
if the following conditions are satisfied:

•

X
is a countable nested union
X=\underset{n\in\mathcal{A}}{\bigcup}X_{n}
, where
\mathcal{A}\subseteq\mathbb{N}
, and
k>0\in\mathcal{A}\Rightarrow k-1\in\mathcal{A}
.

•

Each

can be a
N
-parts nested set, each with its own nesting relations
\left\{F_{n;i}\right\}_{i=0}^{N-1}

•

The following relation holds:
\forall n,n+1\in\mathcal{A}:X_{n}=F_{n+1;0}\left[X_{n+1}\right]

•

For all
n,n+1\in\mathcal{A},i\in\{0,...,N-1\}:
, the following diagram commutes:

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces X_{n+1}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces F_{n+1;i}[X_{n+1}]}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces X_{n}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces X_{n}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces F_{n+1;0}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces F_{n+1;i}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces F_{n+1;0}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces F_{n;i}}

(3)

If
\left|\mathbb{A}\right|<\infty
, then a nested space is also a nested set. It’s trivial to show that every nested set is a nested space, but not every nested space is a nested set.

2.1.3
Defining Connectivity Rules

Plugging eq.
2
into eq.
1
and iterating, we get the following relation:

S=\lim\limits_{M\to\infty}\underset{i_{0},...,i_{M-1}=0,...,N-1}{\bigcup}F_{i_{0}...i_{M-1}}\left[S\right]

(4)

Where
F_{i_{0}...i_{M-1}}\left[S\right]=\left(F_{i_{0}}\circ...\circ F_{i_{M-1}}\right)\left[S\right]
are a generalization to all possible nesting relations of the ”

[

]
. In this paper I call them ”base-

words of length

”, to be specific. By virtue of the nesting relations, and the fact that set limits of the form
\lim\limits_{k\to\infty}A_{k},\forall k:A_{k+1}\subset A_{k}
always converge, there exist objects of the form

F_{\omega}=\lim\limits_{M\to\infty}F_{i_{1}...i_{M}}\left[S\right]

(5)

And they serve, as I will prove later, the topological role of ”points” under the nested cell-structure topology.

Now that we have this concept of ”points”, we can thus define the ”connectivity rules” of
S
as relations of the form

F_{\omega_{1}}=F_{\omega_{2}}\,,\,\omega_{1}\neq\omega_{2}

(6)

Where
F_{\omega_{1}},F_{\omega_{2}}
are the topological points introduced in eq.
5
. We will be proving theorems about the connectivity rules and their consequences later in this paper, after introducing a much more convenient notation system to discuss nested spaces with.

The

-Parts Nested Cell-Structure Topology

Given a set

Note that all objects

We can now define another surjective mapping,

2.3
Arithmetic Operations on
T_{N}

Lastly, we need to consider arithmetic operations on

The first operation we define is multiplication by integer powers of

\text{Sc}:T_{N}\times\mathbb{Z}\to T_{N}:\;\text{Sc}(a,k)=N^{k}\cdot a

We’ll simply mark the scaling operation using ”

Consequences

3.1
Properties of Nested Spaces and Connectivity Rules

1.

Given a connectivity rule
a\stackrel{{\scriptstyle x}}{{\sim}}b
, where
a,b\in T_{N}

Such connectivity rules are to be recognized as equivalent to each other, so we define the set of connectivity rules of a

-parts nested space modulo this equivalence.
We define the set of connectivity rules of a nested space

C_{X}=\nicefrac{{\left(\nicefrac{{\left\{\{a,b\}|a,b\in T_{N},a\stackrel{{\scriptstyle x}}{{\sim}}b\right\}}}{{\left\{\{p,p\}|p\in T_{N}\right\}}}\right)}}{{\cdot N^{k}}}

(12)

2.

Given a

-parts nested space

, such that

3.2
Example Nested Space: The Positive Real Number Line

The positive real number line can be defined in a way similar to how (Strichartz 2006)
[
21
]
defined the unit interval, but more straightforward:

We define the positive real number line as a 2-parts nested space charted by
T_{2}

0.\overline{1}\sim 1.\overline{0}
. The arithmetic operation of addition then trivially follows from this connectivity rule used together with scaling and imposition.

In fact, the positive real number line and its arithmetic can also be defined as a
N
-parts nested space of

together with the connectivity rule
\forall i=0,...,N-2:i.\overline{(N-1)}\sim(i+1).\overline{0}

As a matter of fact, for

Furthermore, by restricting the number of digits either to the left or to the right of the decimal dot on

or the natural numbers, respectively.

3.3
Example Nested Space: The Extended Sierpinski Triangle

The infinitely extended Sierpinski triangle with infinite detail, like those shown by Fukushima

]

]

There are two other popular constructions in literature of the Sierpinski triangle:
