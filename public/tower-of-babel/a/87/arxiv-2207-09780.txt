Feynman Integral Relations from GKZ Hypergeometric Systems
*

Abstract
We study Feynman integrals in the framework of Gel’fand-Kapranov-Zelevinsky (GKZ) hypergeometric systems. The latter defines a class of functions wherein Feynman integrals arise as special cases, for any number of loops and kinematic scales. Utilizing the GKZ system and its relation to D D -module theory, we propose a novel method for obtaining differential equations for master integrals. This note is based on the longer manuscript [ 1 ] .

2
GKZ hypergeometric systems

A GKZ hypergeometric function is defined as the solution to a particular system of PDEs, generally of higher order.
These PDEs are fixed by an integer matrix
A
and a complex vector
\beta
.
For FIs, the
A
matrix is determined by the set of propagators, while the
\beta
vector depends on the propagator powers and spacetime dimension of the integral.

Let us write the GKZ system of PDEs.
Consider
N
integer vectors
a_{1},\ldots,a_{N}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n+1}
, each of length
n+1
.
We collect these vectors into the columns of an
(n+1)\times N
matrix

\displaystyle A=\big(a_{1}\ \ldots\ a_{N}\big)\,.

(1)

The (left) kernel of
A
is defined as the set of vectors annihilated by
A
:

\text{Ker}(A)=\big\{u=(u_{1},\ldots,u_{N})\in\mathbb{Z}^{N}\ |\ A\cdot u=\mathbf{0}\big\}\,.

Moreover, fix
n+1
complex parameters
\beta=(\beta_{0},\ldots,\beta_{n})\in\mathbb{C}^{n+1}
and let
z=(z_{1},\ldots,z_{N})\in\mathbb{C}^{N}
be
N
complex variables.
A
GKZ hypergeometric function

f_{\beta}(z)
is defined by satisfying the
GKZ system

\displaystyle E_{j}\bullet f_{\beta}(z)

\displaystyle=0\,,\quad j=1,\ldots,n+1

(2)

\displaystyle\Box_{u}\bullet f_{\beta}(z)

\displaystyle=0\,,\quad\forall u\in\text{Ker}(A)\,,

(3)

where the differential operators
E_{j}
and
\Box_{u}
are given by

\displaystyle E_{j}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}a_{i,j}\,z_{i}\,\frac{\partial}{\partial z_{i}}\ -\ \beta_{j-1}\quad,\quad\Box_{u}=\prod_{u_{i}>0}\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial z_{i}}\right)^{u_{i}}\ -\ \prod_{u_{i}<0}\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial z_{i}}\right)^{-u_{i}}\,,

(4)

and
a_{i,j}
denotes the
j
th component of the column vector
a_{i}
.

2.1
Euler integrals

GKZ hypergeometric functions enjoy an
Euler integral
representation

2
2

2

It is possible to generalize to the case of several polynomial factors in the integrand, though we shall not require it here.

\displaystyle f_{\beta}(z)=\int_{\mathcal{C}}g(z;x)^{\beta_{0}}\,x_{1}^{-\beta_{1}}\cdots x_{n}^{-\beta_{n}}\frac{\text{d}x}{x}\quad,\quad\frac{\text{d}x}{x}:=\frac{\text{d}x_{1}}{x_{1}}\wedge\cdots\wedge\frac{\text{d}x_{n}}{x_{n}}\,,

(5)

where
\mathcal{C}
is an integration contour and
g=g(z;x)
is a Laurent polynomial in integration variables
x
with monomial coefficients
z
:

\displaystyle g(z;x)=\sum_{i=1}^{N}z_{i}\,x^{\alpha_{i}}\quad,\quad x^{\alpha_{i}}:=x_{1}^{\alpha_{i,1}}\cdots x_{n}^{\alpha_{i,n}}\quad,\quad\alpha_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}\,.

(6)

Note that we have
n
integration variables
x=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
and
N
monomials in
g
.

The column vectors
a_{1},\ldots,a_{N}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n+1}
of the
A
matrix (
1
) are related to the exponent vectors
\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{N}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
of
g
via

a_{i}=(1\ \alpha_{i})^{T}
.

Example.

Euler integral.

Consider the
(2+1)\times 4
matrix

\begin{array}[]{c c c c c c}&1&1&1&1\\
A\ =&1&0&2&1&x_{1}\\
&0&1&0&1&x_{2}\\
&z_{1}&z_{2}&z_{3}&z_{4}\end{array}\,.\vskip-22.76228pt

(7)

It defines the Euler integral

\displaystyle f_{\beta}(z)=\int_{\mathcal{C}}g(z;x)^{\beta_{0}}x_{1}^{-\beta_{1}}x_{2}^{-\beta_{2}}\frac{\text{d}x}{x}\quad,\quad g(z;x)=z_{1}x_{1}+z_{2}x_{2}+z_{3}x_{1}^{2}+z_{4}x_{1}x_{2}

(8)

in terms of
n=2
integration variables
x=(x_{1},x_{2})
and
N=4
monomial coefficients
z=(z_{1},z_{2},z_{3},z_{4})
.

Let us also write the GKZ system of PDEs satisfied by
f_{\beta}(z)
.
The
A
matrix in question leads to the following
E_{j}
operators:

\displaystyle E_{1}

\displaystyle=1\cdot\theta_{1}\ +\ 1\cdot\theta_{2}\ +\ 1\cdot\theta_{3}\ +\ 1\cdot\theta_{4}\ -\ \beta_{0}

(9a)

\displaystyle E_{2}

\displaystyle=1\cdot\theta_{1}\ +\ 0\cdot\theta_{2}\ +\ 2\cdot\theta_{3}\ +\ 1\cdot\theta_{4}\ -\ \beta_{1}

(9b)

\displaystyle E_{3}

\displaystyle=0\cdot\theta_{1}\ +\ 1\cdot\theta_{2}\ +\ 0\cdot\theta_{3}\ +\ 1\cdot\theta_{4}\ -\ \beta_{2}\,,

(9c)

where we defined the Euler operators
\theta_{i}:=z_{i}\frac{\partial}{\partial z_{i}}
.
To write the
\Box_{u}
operators, we first calculate that

\text{Ker}(A)=\text{span}\{u\}\ ,\ u=\big(1,-1,-1,1\big)^{T}\,.

We therefore have a single
\Box_{u}
operator given by

\Box_{u}=\partial_{1}\partial_{4}-\partial_{2}\partial_{3},

where we defined
\partial_{i}:=\frac{\partial}{\partial z_{i}}
.

2.2
Generalized Feynman integrals

Having defined the GKZ hypergeometric system and its associated Euler integral, we are now well equipped to make the connection to FIs.
We begin by defining a
generalized FI
(GFI) as

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}(d_{0};\nu):=c(d_{0};\nu)\,f_{\beta}(z)

(10)

involving a special choice for the
\beta
vector:

\displaystyle\beta

\displaystyle=(\epsilon,-\epsilon\delta,\ldots,-\epsilon\delta)\ -\ (d_{0}/2,\nu_{1},\ldots,\nu_{n})

(11)

\displaystyle d_{0}

\displaystyle\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}\ ,\ \nu=(\nu_{1},\ldots,\nu_{n})\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}\ ,\ 0<\epsilon,\delta\ll 1\ .

The constant
c(d_{0};\nu)
is given by a ratio of
\Gamma
-functions (see section 5 of
[
1
]
).
Now, consider the following identifications:

•

Integration contour of
f_{\beta}(z)
:
\mathcal{C}=(0,\infty)^{n}

•

\delta
appearing in (
11
):
\delta\to 0

•

z
variables of
f_{\beta}(z)
:
z_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}\cup\{m_{i}^{2},p_{i}^{2},p_{i}\cdot p_{j}\}
.

The
m_{i}
denote masses and
p_{i}
are external momenta.
Under these identifications, the GFI reduces to the
Lee-Pomeransky representation
(LPr)
[
35
]
of an
L
-loop FI in
d=d_{0}-2\epsilon
spacetime dimensions, with propagator powers
\nu=(\nu_{1},\ldots,\nu_{n})
.
In the LPr, the polynomial
g
in the integrand takes the form

g=\mathcal{U}+\mathcal{F},

where
\mathcal{U},\mathcal{F}
are the first and second Symanzik polynomials.

The key difference between the LPr and its corresponding GFI has to do with the status of the monomial coefficients in
g
.
For the GFI, each monomial coefficient
z_{i}
is regarded as an independent variable.
For the LPr, the
z_{i}
may not be independent.
At the end of a calculation involving GFIs, we may use the identifications itemized above to match with the LPr.

Example.

Bubble integral.

To illustrate the difference between the LPr and its associated GFI, let us give the example of a one-loop bubble diagram with one internal mass.
The propagators are
D_{1}=-\ell^{2}+m^{2}
and
D_{2}=-(\ell+p)^{2}
, where
\ell
is the integration momentum,
m
is the mass, and
p
is the external momentum.

The LPr is proportional to

\displaystyle\int_{(0,\infty)^{2}}\big(x_{1}+x_{2}+m^{2}x_{1}^{2}+\big(m^{2}-p^{2}\big)x_{1}x_{2}\big)^{\epsilon-d_{0}/2}x_{1}^{\nu_{1}}\,x_{2}^{\nu_{2}}\,\frac{\text{d}x}{x}\,,

(12)

with
\nu_{i}
denoting the exponent of propagator
D_{i}
in momentum space representation.

The corresponding GFI is proportional to

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{C}}\big(z_{1}x_{1}+z_{2}x_{2}+z_{3}x_{1}^{2}+z_{4}x_{1}x_{2}\big)^{\epsilon-d_{0}/2}x_{1}^{\nu_{1}+\epsilon\delta}\,x_{2}^{\nu_{2}+\epsilon\delta}\,\frac{\text{d}x}{x}\,.

(13)

Note that the GKZ system for (
13
) was presented in the example of section
2.1
.

3
Pfaffian systems

Using the GKZ framework, let us outline how to obtain the system of first-order PDEs obeyed by a basis of master integrals.
In other words, given a basis
\vec{\mathcal{I}}
and kinematic variables
z=(z_{1},\ldots,z_{N})
, we seek the PDEs

\displaystyle\partial_{i}\vec{\mathcal{I}}=P_{i}\cdot\vec{\mathcal{I}}\,,\quad i=1,\ldots,N\,,

(14)

where the matrices
P_{i}=P_{i}(z)
contain rational functions in
z
and satisfy the integrability conditions

\partial_{i}P_{j}-\partial_{j}P_{i}=[P_{i},P_{j}]\,.

We dub (
14
) the
Pfaffian system
for
\vec{\mathcal{I}}
and the
P_{i}
are called
Pfaffian matrices
.

3.1
From integrals to operators

We propose to obtain the Pfaffian system using tools from

-module theory
[
20
,
21

.
In this setting, we begin by representing GFIs as partial differential operators w.r.t. the

This means that our computations will be performed inside a rational Weyl algebra

3

We use the notation
\partial^{k}:=\partial_{1}^{k_{1}}\cdots\partial_{N}^{k_{N}}

k\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}^{N}

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{N}

\displaystyle=\Big\{\sum_{k\in K}h_{k}(z)\partial^{k}\ \Big|\ K\subset\mathbb{Z}_{>0}\text{ is finite}\ ,\ h_{k}(z)\text{ is rational in }z\Big\}

(15)

\displaystyle[z_{i},z_{j}]

\displaystyle=[\partial_{i},\partial_{j}]=0\ ,\ [\partial_{i},z_{j}]=\delta_{ij}\,.

(16)

It can be shown

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}\bullet\mathcal{I}(0;0)

(17)

Using this fact, we can let
\mathcal{D}

represent

The existence of

3.2
Macaulay matrices

Our algorithm for computing Pfaffian systems begins by choosing a special operator basis, namely that of
standard monomials
:
\mathcal{D}_{i}=\text{Std}_{i}

4
4

4

There is a fast algorithm for finding the standard monomials of GKZ systems
[
37
]
.

It is a monomial basis, i.e.
\text{Std}_{i}=\partial^{k_{i}}
for some
k_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}^{N}

1

Consider the Pfaffian system in the standard monomial basis:
\partial_{i}\,\text{Std}=P_{i}\cdot\text{Std}
.
We may split the LHS into two terms,

\displaystyle\partial_{i}\,\text{Std}=C_{\text{Ext},i}\cdot\text{Ext}\ +\ C_{\text{Std},i}\cdot\text{Std}\,,

(19)

with
external monomials

Ext
defined as those monomials in the vector
\partial_{i}\,\text{Std}
not already contained in
Std
.

C_{\text{Ext},i}
and

are sparse matrices consisting of
1

0

Choose an integer

\text{Der}_{d}:=\{\partial^{k}\,|\,k_{1}+\ldots+k_{N}\leq d\}

Moreover, let
\text{Mons}_{d}
be the set of all monomials in
\partial_{i}
appearing in the set

\big\{\partial^{k}\,E_{j}\,,\,\partial^{k}\,\Box_{u}\big\}
,
for all
\partial^{k}\in\text{Der}_{d}

and

The
Macaulay matrix
of degree

\displaystyle\big\{\partial^{k}\,E_{j}\,,\,\partial^{k}\,\Box_{u}\big\}_{\forall j,k,u}=M_{d}\cdot\text{Mons}_{d}\,.

(20)

On the LHS, we employ the Weyl algebra (
16
) to commute all the derivatives to the right, thereby exposing the vector
\text{Mons}_{d}
and its coefficient matrix
M_{d}
.
The identity (
19

M_{d}=(M_{\text{Ext}}\,|\,M_{\text{Std}})
, where the columns of
M_{\text{Ext}}
are labeled by the monomials in
Ext
, and similarly for
M_{\text{Std}}
.

The heart of our algorithm for computing Pfaffian matrices is then the following.
We first solve for an unknown matrix
C
in

5
5

5

The integer

\displaystyle C_{\text{Ext},i}-C\cdot M_{\text{Ext}}=0\,,

(21)

whereafter

\displaystyle C_{\text{Std},i}-C\cdot M_{\text{Std}}=P_{i}\,,

(22)

thereby yielding the Pfaffian matrix.
Note that the matrices

4
Conclusion

We have considered FIs in the framework of GKZ hypergeometric systems.
Utilizing the Macaulay matrix, built from the GKZ system, we presented a method for the calculation of Pfaffian systems, i.e. the set of first-order PDEs satisfied by master integrals.
In the future, it would be interesting to apply these tools to study stringy canonical forms
[
40
]
, which are also integrals of the GKZ type.
