Zachary Greenberg, Dani Kaufman, Haoran Li, Christian K. Zickert

Abstract
. We use Goncharov’s coproduct of multiple polylogarithms to define a Lie coalgebra over an arbitrary field. It is generated by symbols subject to inductively defined relations, which we think of as functional relations for multiple polylogarithms. In particular, we have inversion relations and shuffle relations. We relate our definition to Goncharov’s Bloch groups, and to the concrete model for ℒ ​ ( F ) ≤ 4 \mathcal{L}(F)_{\leq 4} by Goncharov and Rudenko.



1.
Introduction

1.1.
The motivic Lie coalgebra

For a field
F
, one expects the existence of a graded Lie coalgebra
\mathcal{L}(F)
such that the weight
n
part of its Chevalley-Eilenberg complex
\wedge^{*}(\mathcal{L}(F))

computes the motivic cohomology groups
H^{i}_{\mathcal{M}}(F,\mathbb{Z}(n))
(see e.g.
[
Gon94
]
). The existence of this so-called
motivic Lie coalgebra
is known for number fields
[
Gon05
]
. It is desirable to have a concrete description. Goncharov
[
Gon94
]
conjectures that
\wedge^{*}(\mathcal{L}(F))_{n}
is rationally quasi-isomorphic to the Bloch complex
\Gamma(F,n)
defined in
[
Gon95
]
. This complex has the form

(1)

\textstyle{\mathcal{B}_{n}(F)\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\delta}
\textstyle{\cdots\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\delta}
\textstyle{\mathcal{B}_{n-k}(F)\otimes\wedge^{k}(F^{*})\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\delta}
\textstyle{\cdots\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\delta}
\textstyle{\mathcal{B}_{2}(F)\otimes\wedge^{n-2}(F^{*})\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\delta}
\textstyle{\wedge^{n}(F^{*}),}

with each group
\mathcal{B}_{k}(F)
being generated by symbols
\{x\}_{k}
with
x\in F\cup\{\infty\}
subject to relations that may be thought of as polylogarithm relations. The rightmost
\delta
takes
\{x\}_{2}\otimes a
to
x\wedge(1-x)\wedge a
, and the others take
\{x\}_{k}\otimes a
to
\{x\}_{k-1}\otimes x\wedge a
. Goncharov conjectures that
\mathcal{L}(F)_{n}=\mathcal{B}_{n}(F)
for
n\leq 3
, but for
n=4
,
\mathcal{L}(F)_{4}
is larger. An explicit model for
\wedge^{*}(\mathcal{L}(F))_{n\leq 4}
is given in Goncharov-Rudenko
[
GR18
]
(see Section
6
for a brief summary).

1.2.
Multiple polylogarithms and Goncharov’s coproduct

The classical polylogarithms
\Li_{n}(x)
have multivariable generalizations
\Li_{n_{1},\dots,n_{d}}(x_{1},\dots,x_{d})
called
multiple polylogarithms
. Their properties are extensively studied by Goncharov
[
Gon01
,
Gon05
]
. He shows that one may view
\Li_{n_{1},\dots,n_{d}}(x_{1},\dots,x_{d})
and
\log(x)
as elements
\Li^{\mathscr{C}}_{n_{1},\dots,n_{d}}(x_{1},\dots,x_{d})
and
\log^{\mathscr{C}}(x)
of a certain Hopf algebra (the Hopf algebra of framed rational Hodge structures
[
Gon05
, appendix]
). In fact, they generate a Hopf subalgebra of this Hopf algebra, and Goncharov gives an explicit formula for the coproduct.
It is best described using the generating series

(2)

\displaystyle\Li^{\mathscr{C}}(x_{1},\dots,x_{d}|t_{1},\dots,t_{d})=

\displaystyle\sum_{n_{i}\geq 1}\Li^{\mathscr{C}}_{n_{1},\dots,n_{d}}(x_{1},\dots,x_{d})t_{1}^{n_{1}-1}\cdots t_{d}^{n_{d}-1},

\displaystyle x^{t}=

\displaystyle\sum_{n\geq 0}\frac{1}{n!}\log^{\mathscr{C}}(x)^{n}t^{n}.

The coproduct of a generating series is defined termwise (fixing the
t_{i}
).

Theorem 1.1

(
[
Gon05
, Prop. 6.1]
)
.

The coproduct
\Delta
of
\Li^{\mathscr{C}}(x_{1},\dots,x_{d}|t_{1},\dots,t_{d})
is given by

(3)

\displaystyle\sum\Li^{\mathscr{C}}(x_{i_{1}\to i_{2}},\dots,x_{i_{k}\to i_{k+1}}|

\displaystyle t_{j_{1}},\dots t_{j_{k}})\bigotimes

\displaystyle\prod_{\alpha=0}^{k}(-1)^{j_{\alpha}-i_{\alpha}}x_{i_{\alpha}\to i_{\alpha+1}}^{t_{j_{\alpha}}}\Big(

\displaystyle\Li^{\mathscr{C}}(x_{j_{\alpha}-1}^{-1},x_{j_{\alpha}-2}^{-1},\dots,x_{i_{\alpha}}^{-1}|t_{j_{\alpha}}-t_{j_{\alpha}-1},\dots,t_{j_{\alpha}}-t_{i_{\alpha}})

\displaystyle\cdot\Li^{\mathscr{C}}(x_{j_{\alpha}+1},x_{j_{\alpha}+2},\dots,x_{i_{\alpha+1}-1}|t_{j_{\alpha}+1}-t_{j_{\alpha}},\dots t_{i_{\alpha+1}-1}-t_{j_{\alpha}})\Big)

The sum is over all
k=0,\dots,d
, and all sequences
\{i_{\alpha}\}_{\alpha=0}^{k+1}
and
\{j_{\alpha}\}_{\alpha=0}^{k}
with

(4)

i_{\alpha}\leq j_{\alpha}<i_{\alpha+1},\qquad j_{0}=i_{0}=0,\quad i_{k+1}=d+1,

and by definition we have
x_{i\to j}=\prod_{r=i}^{j-1}x_{r}
(note that this convension differs from Goncharov’s, but works better in this paper) and
\Li^{\mathscr{C}}(\emptyset|\emptyset)=1
.

Example 1.2
.

When
d=2
, the
\{i_{\alpha}\}
and
\{j_{\alpha}\}
satisfying (
4
) are:

(5)

(\overset{i_{0}}{0},\overset{j_{0}}{0}|\overset{i_{1}}{1},\overset{j_{1}}{1}|\overset{i_{2}}{2},\overset{j_{2}}{2}|\overset{i_{3}}{3}),\quad(\overset{i_{0}}{0},\overset{j_{0}}{0}|\overset{i_{1}}{1},\overset{j_{1}}{1}|\overset{i_{2}}{3}),\quad(\overset{i_{0}}{0},\overset{j_{0}}{0}|\overset{i_{1}}{1},\overset{j_{1}}{2}|\overset{i_{2}}{3}),\quad(\overset{i_{0}}{0},\overset{j_{0}}{0}|\overset{i_{1}}{2},\overset{j_{1}}{2}|\overset{i_{2}}{3}),\quad(\overset{i_{0}}{0},\overset{j_{0}}{0}|\overset{i_{1}}{3}),

and it follows that

(6)

\displaystyle\Delta\Li^{\mathscr{C}}(x_{1},x_{2}|t_{1},t_{2})=\Li^{\mathscr{C}}(x_{1},x_{2}|t_{1},t_{2})\otimes x_{1}^{t_{1}}x_{2}^{t_{2}}+\Li^{\mathscr{C}}(x_{1}x_{2}|t_{1})\otimes(x_{1}x_{2})^{t_{1}}\Li^{\mathscr{C}}(x_{2}|t_{2}-t_{1})

\displaystyle-\Li^{\mathscr{C}}(x_{1}x_{2}|t_{2})\otimes(x_{1}x_{2})^{t_{2}}\Li^{\mathscr{C}}(x_{1}^{-1}|t_{2}-t_{1})+\Li^{\mathscr{C}}(x_{2}|t_{2})\otimes\Li^{\mathscr{C}}(x_{1}|t_{1})x_{2}^{t_{2}}+1\otimes\Li^{\mathscr{C}}(x_{1},x_{2}|t_{1},t_{2}).

One can then compute
\Delta\Li^{\mathscr{C}}_{n_{1},n_{2}}(x_{1},x_{2})
as the coefficient of
t_{1}^{n_{1}-1}t_{2}^{n_{2}-1}
of the right-hand side of (
6
).

Given a graded Hopf algebra
H
, we have a Lie coalgebra
L=\frac{H_{>0}}{H_{>0}H_{>0}}
with cobracket induced by the coproduct. We are thus interested in the quotient by products. The following elementary corollary of Theorem
1.1
is our main motivation.

Corollary 1.3
.

Modulo products and constants,
\Delta\Li^{\mathscr{C}}(x_{1},\dots,x_{d}|t_{1},\dots,t_{d})
can be written as

(7)

\displaystyle\Li^{\mathscr{C}}(\mathbf{x}_{1,\dots,d}|\mathbf{t}_{1,\dots,d})\otimes\sum_{p=1}^{d}t_{p}\log^{\mathscr{C}}(x_{p})+

\displaystyle\sum_{p=2}^{d}\Li^{\mathscr{C}}(\mathbf{x}_{p,\dots,d}|\mathbf{t}_{p,\dots,d})\otimes\Li^{\mathscr{C}}(\mathbf{x}_{1,\dots,p-1}|\mathbf{t}_{1,\dots,p-1})+

\displaystyle\sum_{1\leq p<q\leq d}\Li^{\mathscr{C}}(\mathbf{x}_{1,\dots,p\to q,\dots,d}|\mathbf{t}_{1,\dots,p,q+1,\dots,d})\otimes\Li^{\mathscr{C}}(\mathbf{x}_{p+1,\dots,q}|\mathbf{t}_{p+1,\dots,q}-t_{p})+

\displaystyle\sum_{1\leq p<q\leq d}(-1)^{q-p}\Li^{\mathscr{C}}(\mathbf{x}_{1,\dots,p\to q,\dots,d}|\mathbf{t}_{1,\dots,p-1,q,\dots,d})\otimes\Li^{\mathscr{C}}(x_{q-1}^{-1},x_{q-2}^{-1},\dots,x_{p}^{-1}|t_{q}-\mathbf{t}_{q-1,\dots,p})

Where
\mathbf{x}_{1,\dots,p\to q,\dots,d}
is shorthand for
(x_{1},\dots,x_{p-1},\prod_{r=p}^{q}x_{r},x_{q+1},\dots,x_{d})
and dots indicate that indices increase (or decrease) by 1. We stress that the product is from
p
to
q
, not to
q-1
.

Proof.

One easily checks that the only sequences
\{i_{\alpha}\}
and
\{j_{\alpha}\}
for which the corresponding term in (
3
) does not involve products are

(8)

\displaystyle(\overset{i_{0}}{0},\overset{j_{0}}{0}|\overset{i_{1}}{1},\overset{j_{1}}{1}|\overset{i_{2}}{2},\overset{j_{2}}{2}|\cdots|\overset{i_{d}}{d},\overset{j_{d}}{d}|,\overset{i_{d+1}}{d+1})

\displaystyle(\overset{i_{0}}{0},\overset{j_{0}}{0}|\overset{i_{1}}{p},\overset{j_{1}}{p}|\overset{i_{2}}{p+1},\overset{j_{2}}{p+1}|\cdots|\overset{i_{k}}{d},\overset{j_{k}}{d}|,\overset{i_{k+1}}{d+1})

\displaystyle(\overset{i_{0}}{0},\overset{j_{0}}{0}|\overset{i_{1}}{1},\overset{j_{1}}{1}|\cdots|\overset{i_{p}}{p},\overset{j_{p}}{p}|\overset{i_{p+1}}{q+1},\overset{j_{p+1}}{q+1}|\cdots|\overset{i_{k}}{d},\overset{j_{k}}{d}|,\overset{i_{k+1}}{d+1})

\displaystyle(\overset{i_{0}}{0},\overset{j_{0}}{0}|\overset{i_{1}}{1},\overset{j_{1}}{1}|\cdots|\overset{i_{p}}{p},\overset{j_{p}}{q}|\overset{i_{p+1}}{q+1},\overset{j_{p+1}}{q+1}|\cdots|\overset{i_{k}}{d},\overset{j_{k}}{d}|,\overset{i_{k+1}}{d+1}),

and that the corresponding terms are exactly those given in (
7
).
∎

1.3.
Real single valued polylogarithms

One has single valued variants
\mathcal{\mathcal{L}}_{n}(x)
of the classical polylogarithms (see e.g.
[
DB12
,
Zag91
]
)

(9)

\mathcal{L}_{n}(z)=\begin{matrix}\Real\text{if $n$ is odd}\\
\Imag\text{if $n$ is even}\end{matrix}\left(\sum_{r=0}^{n-1}\frac{2^{r}B_{r}}{r!}\Li_{n-r}(z)(\log|z|)^{r}\right).

The following result justifies thinking about the relations in
\mathcal{B}_{n}(F)
as polylogarithm relations:

Theorem 1.4

(
[
Zag91
, Prop. 3]
)
.

For any element
\alpha=\sum n_{i}{f_{i}(t)}\in\mathcal{B}_{n}(\mathbb{C}(t))
with
\delta(\alpha)=0
in
\mathcal{B}_{n-1}(\mathbb{C}(t))\otimes\mathbb{C}(t)^{*}
we have

(10)

\sum n_{i}\mathcal{L}_{n}(f_{i}(t))=\textnormal{constant}.

There are also single valued analogues
\mathcal{L}_{n_{1},\dots,n_{d}}
of the multiple polylogarithms
\Li_{n_{1},\dots,n_{d}}
. There is no closed formula, but they can be computed from a variation matrix
[
Zha02
]
. We shall here only need that
\mathcal{L}_{n_{1},\dots,n_{d}}
is defined on

(11)

S_{d}(\mathbb{C})=\Big\{(x_{1},\dots,x_{d})\in(\mathbb{C}^{*})^{d}\bigm|\prod_{r=i}^{j}x_{r}\neq 1\text{ for all }i\leq j\in\{1,\dots,d\}\Big\}

and that

(12)

\lim_{x_{i}\to 0}\mathcal{L}_{n_{1},\dots,n_{d}}(x_{1},\dots,x_{d})=0\qquad\text{for all $i$}.

The functions
\mathcal{L}_{n}(x)
also satisfy that
\lim_{x\to\infty}\mathcal{L}_{n}(x)=0
for
n>1
, but in higher depth the limit as
x_{i}
tends to
\infty
is no longer
0
.

1.4.
Structure of the paper

In Section
2
we define a purely symbolic coalgebra
\mathbb{L}^{\symb}(F)
with no relations. We have
\mathbb{L}_{1}^{\symb}(F)=F^{*}
, and
\mathbb{L}^{\symb}_{>1}
is generated by symbols
[x_{1},\dots,x_{d}]_{n_{1},\dots,n_{d}}
with
(x_{1},\dots,x_{d})\in S_{d}(F)
, where
d
and
n_{1},\dots,n_{d}
are positive integers. Thinking of a symbol as a polylogarithm we define the cobracket as in Corollary
1.3
and show directly that
\delta^{2}=0
. In Section
3
we inductively define a group of relations
R_{n}(F)
and define

(13)

\mathbb{L}_{n}(F)=\mathbb{L}_{n}^{\symb}(F)/R_{n}(F).

The definition mimics Goncharov’s definition of relations in
\mathcal{B}_{n}(F)
, and the proof that the cobracket takes relations to 0 in
\wedge^{2}(\mathbb{L}(F))
follows Goncharov as well. Section
3.1
gives some basic examples of relations, and Section
3.2
discusses the problem of defining symbols when
(x_{1},\dots,x_{d})\notin S_{d}(F)
. For example,
[1,1]_{1,1}
is not well defined.
In Section
3.3
we speculate that
\mathbb{L}(F)
is the motivic Lie coalgebra
\mathcal{L}(F)
and conjecture a generalization of Theorem
1.4
, which justifies thinking of
R_{n}(F)
as polylogarithm relations.
Section
4
discusses the
inversion relations
, which are inspired by Goncharov’s inversion relations for multiple polylogarithms. In particular, we show that in
\mathbb{L}(F)
one can express each symbol
[x_{d}^{-1},\dots,x_{1}^{-1}]_{n_{d},\dots,n_{1}}
in terms of symbols involving only non-inverted
x_{i}
. Section
4.1
shows that one can use this to define an alternative cobracket on
\mathbb{L}^{\symb}(F)
without inverted
x_{i}
. The relations are the same. The alternative one may in fact be more natural (see e.g. Remark
5.3
).
Section
5
briefly discusses the shuffle product relations showing that at least in low depth a shuffle product is 0 in
\mathbb{L}(F)
. Finally, Section
6
relates our work to that of Goncharov and Rudenko, who gave a concrete model for
\mathcal{L}_{n\leq 4}(F)
.

Remark 1.5
.

Although our work is heavily inspired by the work of Goncharov, it does not require any of Goncharov’s results, except for motivation.

2.
A purely symbolic Lie coalgebra

For a field
F
, and a positive integer
d
, let

(14)

S_{d}(F)=\Big\{(x_{1},\dots,x_{d})\in(F^{*})^{d}\bigm|\prod_{r=i}^{j}x_{r}\neq 1\text{ for all }i\leq j\in\{1,\dots,d\}\Big\}.

We wish to define a graded Lie coalgebra

(15)

\mathbb{L}^{\symb}(F)=\bigoplus_{n=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{L}_{n}^{\symb}(F).

We first define
\mathbb{L}_{1}^{\symb}(F)=F^{*}
,
which we shall identify with the abelian group generated by symbols
[x]_{1}
with
x\in F\setminus\{0,1\}
and
[x]_{0}
for
x\in F\setminus\{0,1\}
subject to the relations

(16)

[x]_{1}=-[1-x]_{0},\qquad[xy]_{0}=[x]_{0}+[y]_{0}.

For
n>1
, define
\mathbb{L}_{n}^{\symb}(F)
to be generated by symbols
[x_{1},\dots,x_{d}]_{n_{1},\dots,n_{d}}
with

(17)

(x_{1},\dots,x_{d})\in S_{d}(F),\qquad d,n_{1},\dots,n_{d}\in\mathbb{Z}_{+},\qquad n_{1}+\dots+n_{d}=n.

We refer to
n_{1}+\dots+n_{d}
as the
weight
and
d
as the
depth
of a symbol, but stress that
[x]_{0}
is in weight 1, not 0. We think of
[x_{1},\dots,x_{d}]_{n_{1},\dots,n_{d}}
as representing
\Li_{n_{1},\dots,n_{d}}(x_{1},\dots,x_{d})
and
[x]_{0}
as representing
\log(x)
.

2.1.
The cobracket on
\mathbb{L}^{\symb}(F)

As in (
2
) we define

(18)

[x_{1},\dots,x_{d}|t_{1},\dots,t_{d}]=\sum_{n_{i}\geq 1}[x_{1},\dots,x_{d}]_{n_{1},\dots,n_{d}}t_{1}^{n_{1}-1}\cdots t_{d}^{n_{d}-1},

and define
\delta\colon\mathbb{L}^{\symb}(F)\to\wedge^{2}(\mathbb{L}^{\symb}(F))
to be zero in weight 1, and otherwise given by (
7
). It is convenient to write
\delta=\delta_{1}+\delta_{2}+\delta_{3}+\delta_{4}
, where for
X=[\mathbf{x}_{1,\dots,d}|\mathbf{t}_{1,\dots,d}]

(19)

\displaystyle\delta_{1}(X)

\displaystyle=X\wedge\left(\sum_{p=1}^{d}t_{p}[x_{p}]_{0}\right)

\displaystyle\delta_{2}(X)

\displaystyle=\sum_{p=2}^{d}[\mathbf{x}_{p,\dots,d}|\mathbf{t}_{p,\dots,d}]\wedge[\mathbf{x}_{1,\dots,p-1}|\mathbf{t}_{1,\dots,p-1}]

\displaystyle\delta_{3}(X)

\displaystyle=\sum_{1\leq p<q\leq d}[\mathbf{x}_{1,\dots,p\to q,\dots,d}|\mathbf{t}_{1,\dots,p,q+1,\dots,d}]\wedge[\mathbf{x}_{p+1,\dots,q}|\mathbf{t}_{p+1,\dots,q}-t_{p}]

\displaystyle\delta_{4}(X)

\displaystyle=\sum_{1\leq p<q\leq d}(-1)^{q-p}[\mathbf{x}_{1,\dots,p\to q,\dots,d}|\mathbf{t}_{1,\dots,p-1,q,\dots,d}]\wedge[x_{q-1}^{-1},\dots,x_{p}^{-1}|t_{q}-\mathbf{t}_{q-1,\dots,p}].

Example 2.1
.

In depth 1, we have
\delta[x|t]=[x|t]\wedge t[x]_{0}
, from which it follows that
\delta[x]_{n}=[x]_{n-1}\wedge[x]_{0}
for
n>1
. Note the case
\delta[x]_{2}=[x]_{1}\wedge[x]_{0}=x\wedge(1-x)\in\wedge^{2}(F^{*})
. We thus recover the boundary map in the Bloch complex.

Example 2.2
.

In depth 2,
\delta[x_{1},x_{2}|t_{1},t_{2}]
equals (compare with (
6
))

(20)

\displaystyle\delta[x_{1},x_{2}|t_{1},t_{2}]=[x_{1},x_{2}|t_{1},t_{2}]\wedge(t_{1}[x_{1}]_{0}+t_{2}[x_{2}]_{0})+[x_{2}|t_{2}]\wedge[x_{1}|t_{1}]+

\displaystyle{[x_{1}x_{2}|t_{1}]\wedge[x_{2}|t_{2}-t_{1}]}-[x_{1}x_{2}|t_{2}]\wedge[x_{1}^{-1}|t_{2}-t_{1}].

In particular,
\delta[x_{1},x_{2}]_{r,s}
is given by

(21)

\displaystyle[x_{1},x_{2}]_{r-1,s}\wedge[x_{1}]_{0}+[x_{1},x_{2}]_{r,s-1}\wedge[x_{2}]_{0}+[x_{2}]_{s}\wedge[x_{1}]_{r}

\displaystyle+\sum_{i=1}^{r}(-1)^{r-i}\binom{r+s-1-i}{s-1}[x_{1}x_{2}]_{i}\wedge[x_{2}]_{r+s-i}

\displaystyle+\sum_{i=1}^{s}(-1)^{r}\binom{r+s-1-i}{r-1}[x_{1}x_{2}]_{i}\wedge[x_{1}^{-1}]_{r+s-i}.

In the case where
r
or
s
is 1, the symbols
[x_{1},x_{2}]_{0,s}
and
[x_{1},x_{2}]_{r,0}
are interpreted as 0.

Remark 2.3
.

In our definition of
\wedge^{2}(\mathbb{L}^{\symb}(F))
,
x\wedge x
is identically 0, not 2-torsion.

Theorem 2.4
.

The cobracket above makes
\mathbb{L}^{\symb}(F)
into a Lie coalgebra.

Proof.

We must show that
\delta^{2}=0
. To do this, it is enough to show that

(22)

\begin{gathered}\delta_{1}^{2}=0,\qquad\delta_{2}^{2}=0,\qquad\delta_{1}\delta_{i}+\delta_{i}\delta_{1}=0,\\
\delta_{3}^{2}+\delta_{2}\delta_{3}+\delta_{3}\delta_{2}=0,\qquad\delta_{2}\delta_{4}+\delta_{4}\delta_{2}+\delta_{4}^{2}+\delta_{3}\delta_{4}+\delta_{4}\delta_{3}=0.\end{gathered}

The proof that
\delta_{1}^{2}=0
is elementary. The remaining equalities are all straightforward, so for brevity, we prove only that
\delta_{2}^{2}=0
, and that
\delta_{1}\delta_{4}+\delta_{4}\delta_{1}=0
. Firstly,
\delta_{2}^{2}([\mathbf{x}_{1,\dots,d}|\mathbf{t}_{1,\dots,d}])
is given by

(23)

\displaystyle\sum_{2\leq p\leq d}\delta_{2}[\mathbf{x}_{p,\dots,d}|\mathbf{t}_{p,\dots,d}]\wedge[\mathbf{x}_{1,\dots,p-1}|\mathbf{t}_{1,\dots,p-1}]-\sum_{2\leq p\leq d}[\mathbf{x}_{p,\dots,d}|\mathbf{t}_{p,\dots,d}]\wedge\delta_{2}[\mathbf{x}_{1,\dots,p-1}|\mathbf{t}_{1,\dots,p-1}]

\displaystyle=\sum_{2\leq p\leq d}\sum_{p+1\leq r\leq d}[\mathbf{x}_{r,\dots,d}|\mathbf{t}_{r,\dots,d}]\wedge[\mathbf{x}_{p,\dots,r-1}|\mathbf{t}_{p,\dots,r-1}]\wedge[\mathbf{x}_{1,\dots,p-1}|\mathbf{t}_{1,\dots,p-1}]

\displaystyle-\sum_{2\leq p\leq d}\sum_{2\leq r\leq p-1}[\mathbf{x}_{p,\dots,d}|\mathbf{t}_{p,\dots,d}]\wedge[\mathbf{x}_{r,\dots,p-1}|\mathbf{t}_{r,\dots,p-1}]\wedge[\mathbf{x}_{1,\dots,r-1}|\mathbf{t}_{1,\dots,r-1}]=0.

Similarly,
\delta_{1}\delta_{4}([\mathbf{x}_{1,\dots,d}|\mathbf{t}_{1,\dots,d}])
equals

(24)

\displaystyle\sum_{1\leq p<q\leq d}(-1)^{q-p}\delta_{1}[\mathbf{x}_{1,\dots,p\to q,\dots,d}|\mathbf{t}_{1,\dots,p-1,q,\dots,d}]\wedge[x_{q-1}^{-1},\dots,x_{p}^{-1}|t_{q}-\mathbf{t}_{q-1,\dots,p}]

\displaystyle-(-1)^{q-p}[\mathbf{x}_{1,\dots,p\to q,\dots,d}|\mathbf{t}_{1,\dots,p-1,q,\dots,d}]\wedge\delta_{1}[x_{q-1}^{-1},\dots,x_{p}^{-1}|t_{q}-\mathbf{t}_{q-1,\dots,p}],

which equals

(25)

\displaystyle=\sum_{1\leq p<q\leq d}(-1)^{q-p}[\mathbf{x}_{1,\dots,p\to q,\dots,d}|\mathbf{t}_{1,\dots,p-1,q,\dots,d}]\wedge

\displaystyle\left(\sum_{1\leq r\leq p-1}t_{r}[x_{r}]_{0}+t_{q}[x_{p}\cdots x_{q}]_{0}+\sum_{q+1\leq r\leq d}t_{r}[x_{r}]_{0}\right)\wedge[x_{q-1}^{-1},\dots,x_{p}^{-1}|t_{q}-\mathbf{t}_{q-1,\dots,p}]

\displaystyle-(-1)^{q-p}[\mathbf{x}_{1,\dots,p\to q,\dots,d}|\mathbf{t}_{1,\dots,p-1,q,\dots,d}]\wedge[x_{q-1}^{-1},\dots,x_{p}^{-1}|t_{q}-\mathbf{t}_{q-1,\dots,p}]\wedge\left(\sum_{p\leq r\leq q-1}(t_{q}-t_{r})[x_{r}^{-1}]_{0}\right)

\displaystyle=-\sum_{1\leq p<q\leq d}(-1)^{q-p}[\mathbf{x}_{1,\dots,p\to q,\dots,d}|\mathbf{t}_{1,\dots,p-1,q,\dots,d}]\wedge[x_{q-1}^{-1},\dots,x_{p}^{-1}|t_{q}-\mathbf{t}_{q-1,\dots,p}]\wedge\left(\sum_{1\leq r\leq d}t_{r}[x_{r}]_{0}\right).

Finally,

(26)

\delta_{4}\delta_{1}([\mathbf{x}_{1,\dots,d}|\mathbf{t}_{1,\dots,d}])=\delta_{4}[\mathbf{x}_{1,\dots,d}|\mathbf{t}_{1,\dots,d}]\wedge\left(\sum_{p=1}^{d}t_{p}[x_{p}]_{0}\right)=\\
=\sum_{1\leq p<q\leq d}(-1)^{q-p}[\mathbf{x}_{1,\dots,p\to q,\dots d}|\mathbf{t}_{1,\dots,p-1,q,\dots d}]\wedge[x_{q-1}^{-1},\dots,x_{p}^{-1}|t_{q}-\mathbf{t}_{q-1,\dots,p}]\wedge\left(\sum_{p=1}^{d}t_{p}[x_{p}]_{0}\right),

and it follows that
\delta_{1}\delta_{4}+\delta_{4}\delta_{1}=0
.
∎

3.
The relations

We now define groups
R_{n}(F)
of relations in
\mathbb{L}_{n}^{\symb}(F)
. We can then define

(27)

\mathbb{L}_{n}(F)=\mathbb{L}_{n}^{\symb}(F)/R_{n}(F).

The definition is inductive starting with the definition of
R_{1}(F)
to be the trivial group, so that
\mathbb{L}_{1}(F)=F^{*}
. Suppose
n>1
and that
\mathbb{L}_{k}(K)
has been defined for all fields
K
and all
k<n
. Then

(28)

\wedge^{2}(\mathbb{L}(K))_{n}=\bigoplus_{k+l=n}\mathbb{L}_{k}(K)\wedge\mathbb{L}_{l}(K)

is also well defined for all
K
. Let

(29)

\mathcal{A}_{n}(K)=\Ker\Big(\delta\colon\mathbb{L}_{n}^{\symb}(K)\to\wedge^{2}(\mathbb{L}(K))_{n}\Big)

Mimicking Goncharov’s definition of relations in the Bloch complexes
[
Gon95
, p. 221]
we wish to define
R_{n}(F)\subset\mathbb{L}_{n}^{\symb}(F)
to be generated by elements
\alpha(p)-\alpha(q)
, where
p
and
q
are points on a connected (geometrically irreducible) smooth curve
X
over
F
with function field
F(X)
and
\alpha
is an element in
\mathcal{A}_{n}(F(X))
. The only problem with this is our requirement that all tuples be well defined (allowing for
0
and
\infty
; see Section
2.2
).

Each element
\alpha\in\mathbb{L}^{\symb}(F(X))
is a linear combination of terms
[f_{1},\dots,f_{d}]_{n_{1},\dots,n_{d}}
, which we refer to as
terms of
\alpha
.

Definition 3.1
.

We say that
\alpha\in\mathbb{L}^{\symb}(F(X))
is
well defined
at
x\in X
if all products
\prod_{r=i}^{j}f_{r}(x)
are defined and distinct from 1, for each term
[f_{1},\dots,f_{d}]_{n_{1},\dots,n_{d}}
of
\alpha
.

By induction, we see that if
\alpha\in\mathcal{A}_{n}(F(X))
, we can rearrange the terms
\delta(\alpha)
as a linear combination of terms
(\beta(q)-\beta(q^{\prime}))\otimes y
, where
Y
is a smooth connected curve over
F(X)
,
q
and
q^{\prime}
are points in
Y
,
\beta\in\mathcal{A}_{k}(F(X)(Y))
and
y\in\mathbb{L}^{\symb}_{n-k}(F(X))
.

Definition 3.2
.

All
\alpha\in\mathcal{A}_{2}
are
proper
. For
n>2
,
\alpha\in\mathcal{A}_{n}(F(X))
is
proper
if whenever
\alpha
is well defined at
x\in X
, there is an arrangement of
\delta(\alpha)
as above with
\beta(q)
,
\beta(q^{\prime})
, and
y
all well defined at
x
(no cancelation of undefined terms) with
\beta
proper.

Definition 3.3
.

The group
R_{n}(F)
is generated by elements of the form
\alpha(p)-\alpha(q)
, where
\alpha\in\mathcal{A}_{n}(F(X))
is proper and well defined at
p,q\in X
.

We now prove that
\mathbb{L}(F)
is also a Lie coalgebra. To do this we must prove that the cobracket respects the relations. The proof is similar to
[
Gon95
, Lemma 1.16]
.

Theorem 3.4
.

The map
\delta\colon\mathbb{L}_{n}^{\symb}(F)\to\wedge^{2}(\mathbb{L}(F))_{n}
takes
R_{n}(F)
to 0.

Proof.

It is enough to show that for any proper
\alpha\in\mathcal{A}_{n}(F(X))
, the element
\delta(\alpha(p))
is zero in
\wedge^{2}(\mathbb{L}(F))_{n}
for all
p\in X
where
\alpha(p)
is defined. Fix such
X
,
\alpha
, and
p
and write
\delta(\alpha)
in
\wedge^{2}(\mathbb{L}^{\symb}(F(X)))
as a linear combination of terms
(\beta(q)-\beta(q^{\prime}))\wedge y
as above. It follows that
\delta(\alpha(p))
is a linear combination of elements of the form
(\beta(q)(p)-\beta(q^{\prime})(p))\otimes y(p)
, and the result follows by showing that
\beta(q)(p)-\beta(q^{\prime})(p)
is in
R_{k}(F)
. Let
\beta_{p}
be the element in
\mathbb{L}_{k}^{\symb}(F(Y(p)))
obtained from
\beta
by restriction to the fiber
Y(p)
over
p
. Since
\beta
is in
\mathcal{A}_{k}(F(X)(Y)))
, an induction argument shows that
\beta_{p}
is in
\mathcal{A}_{k}(F(Y(p))
and is proper. Note that
q
and
q^{\prime}
can be regarded as maps
X\to Y
, so restriction to
p\in X
determines points
q_{p}
and
q^{\prime}_{p}
in
Y(p)
. We then have

(30)

\beta(q)(p)-\beta(q^{\prime})(p)=\beta_{p}(q_{p})-\beta_{p}(q_{p})\in R_{k}(F).

This concludes the proof.
∎

3.1.
Basic examples of relations

In the following examples we repeatedly use the fact that for
\alpha\in\mathbb{L}^{\symb}(F(x))
,
\delta(\alpha)=0\in\wedge^{2}(\mathbb{L}(F(x)))
implies that
\alpha(x)
is constant in
\mathbb{L}(F)
.

Example 3.5
.

Let
\alpha_{n}=2([x]_{n}+(-1)^{n}[x^{-1}]_{n})\in\mathbb{L}^{\symb}_{n}(F)
. By induction,
\alpha_{n}\in\mathcal{A}_{n}(F(x))
when
n\geq 2
, so
\alpha_{n}(x)
is constant in
\mathbb{L}_{n}(F)
. Hence,
\alpha_{n}(x)=\alpha_{n}(0)=2[0]_{n}+2(-1)^{n}[\infty]_{n}=0
. Note, however, that
\alpha_{1}(x)=-2[x]_{0}
.

Example 3.6
.

One easily shows that
\alpha=[x,y]_{1,1}+[x]_{2}-[\frac{x(1-y)}{1-xy}]_{2}
is in
\mathcal{A}_{2}(F(x,y))
. Considering the specialization at
(x,y)=(0,y)
it follows that
[x,y]_{1,1}+[x]_{2}-[\frac{x(1-y)}{1-xy}]_{2}=0\in\mathbb{L}_{2}(F)
whenever all terms are defined. This means that every element in
\mathbb{L}_{2}(F)
can be expressed using only terms in depth 1.

Example 3.7

(Five term relation)
.

Similarly, we obtain that

(31)

[x]_{2}+[y]_{2}-[xy]_{2}-[\frac{y(1-x)}{1-xy}]_{2}-[\frac{x(1-y)}{1-xy}]_{2}=0\in\mathbb{L}_{2}(F).

Proposition 3.8
.

Modulo 2-torsion every element in
\mathbb{L}_{3}(F)
can be written in terms of depth 1 symbols.

Proof.

A simple computation shows that the elements

(32)

[x,y]_{2,1}+[x]_{3}+[\frac{1-y}{1-xy}]_{3}+[xy]_{3}+[-\frac{xy}{1-xy}]_{3}-[1-y]_{3}-[\frac{x(1-y)}{1-xy}]_{3}

and

(33)

[x,y,z]_{1,1,1}-[-\frac{y}{1-y}]_{3}+[\frac{1-x}{1-xyz}]_{3}-[xy]_{3}+[\frac{xy(1-z)}{1-xyz}]_{3}\\
-[1-x]_{3}+[-\frac{y(1-x)}{1-y}]_{3}+[-\frac{y(1-z)}{1-y}]_{3}-[-\frac{y(1-x)(1-z)}{(1-xyz)(1-y)}]_{3}

have vanishing cobracket modulo 2-torsion. Specializing at
(0,y)
and
(x,0,z)
, respectively, proves the result for
[x,y]_{2,1}
and
[x,y,z]_{1,1,1}
. Finally,
[x,y]_{1,2}+[y,x]_{2,1}+[xy]_{3}
also has vanishing cobracket, concluding the proof.
∎

4.
Inversion relations

In the following we ignore 2-torsion. It is well known (see e.g.
[
Lew91
]
) that the classical polylogarithms satisfy the
inversion relation

(40)

\Li_{n}(x)+(-1)^{n}\Li_{n}(x^{-1})=-\frac{(2\pi i)^{n}}{n!}B_{n}\left(\frac{\log x}{2\pi i}\right),

where
B_{n}(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom{n}{k}B_{n-k}x^{k}
are the Bernoulli polynomials. Thinking of a symbol
[x]_{n}
as
\Li_{n}(x)
modulo products and powers of
\pi i
, we would thus expect
[x]_{n}+(-1)^{n}[x^{-1}]_{n}
to be zero in
\mathbb{L}_{n}(F)
. This was proved in Example
3.5
.

Goncharov
[
Gon01
, Sec. 2.6]
extended the classical inversion formula to multiple polylogarithms. For example, one has

(41)

\displaystyle\Li_{n_{1},n_{2}}(x_{1},x_{2})+(-1)^{n_{1}+n_{2}}\Li_{n_{2},n_{1}}(x_{2}^{-1},x_{1}^{-1})+(-1)^{n_{1}}\Li_{n_{1}}(x_{1}^{-1})\Li_{n_{2}}(x_{2})

\displaystyle+\sum_{p+q=n_{1}}\frac{(2\pi i)^{p}}{p!}(-1)^{q}\binom{q+n_{2}-1}{n_{2}-1}B_{p}\left(\frac{\log(x_{1}x_{2})}{2\pi i}\right)\Li_{q+n_{2}}(x_{2})

\displaystyle+\sum_{p+q=n_{2}}\frac{(2\pi i)^{p}}{p!}(-1)^{n_{1}}\binom{n_{1}+q-1}{n_{1}-1}B_{p}\left(\frac{\log(x_{1}x_{2})}{2\pi i}\right)\Li_{n_{1}+q}(x_{1}^{-1})=0,

which suggests that we should have

(42)

[x_{1},x_{2}]_{n_{1},n_{2}}+(-1)^{n_{1}+n_{2}}[x_{2}^{-1},x_{1}^{-1}]_{n_{2},n_{1}}\\
+(-1)^{n_{1}}\binom{n_{1}+n_{2}-1}{n_{2}-1}[x_{2}]_{n_{1}+n_{2}}+(-1)^{n_{1}}\binom{n_{1}+n_{2}-1}{n_{1}-1}[x_{1}^{-1}]_{n_{1}+n_{2}}=0\in\mathbb{L}_{n_{1}+n_{2}}(F).

More generally, Goncharov’s work suggests that
[x_{d}^{-1},\dots,x_{1}^{-1}]_{n_{d},\dots,n_{1}}
should be expressible using terms where no
x_{i}
is inverted. Inspired by Goncharov’s work we make the following definition.

Definition 4.1
.

The
inversion map
is defined inductively on power series by the formula

(43)

\inv([\mathbf{x}_{1,\dots,d}|\mathbf{t}_{1,\dots,d}])=-(-1)^{d}[\mathbf{x}_{1,\dots,d}|\mathbf{t}_{1,\dots,d}]+\frac{(-1)^{d}}{t_{1}}[\mathbf{x}_{2,\dots,d}|\mathbf{t}_{2,\dots,d}]\\
-\frac{(-1)^{d}}{t_{1}}[\mathbf{x}_{2,\dots,d}|\mathbf{t}_{2,\dots,d}-t_{1}]-\frac{1}{t_{d}}\inv([\mathbf{x}_{1,\dots,d-1}|\mathbf{t}_{1,\dots,d-1}])+\frac{1}{t_{d}}\inv([\mathbf{x}_{1,\dots,d-1}|\mathbf{t}_{1,\dots,d-1}-t_{d}]).

The induction starts with the formula
\inv[x|t]=[x|t]+[x]_{0}
.

We wish to prove the following:

Theorem 4.2
.

We have
[\mathbf{x}^{-1}_{d,\dots,1}|-\mathbf{t}_{d,\dots,_{1}}]=\inv[\mathbf{x}_{1,\dots,d}|\mathbf{t}_{1,\dots,d}]\in\mathbb{L}(F)
. In particular,

(44)

(-1)^{d}(-1)^{n_{1}+\dots+n_{d}}[x_{d}^{-1},\dots,x_{1}^{-1}]_{n_{d},\dots,n_{1}}=\inv[x_{1},\dots,x_{d}]_{n_{1},\dots,n_{d}}.

Example 4.3
.

For
d=1
, we have
(-1)^{n+1}[x^{-1}]_{n}=[x]_{n}
for
n>1
and
[x^{-1}]_{1}=[x]_{1}+[x]_{0}

3.5

Example 4.4
.

For
d=2
,
\inv[x_{1},x_{2}|t_{1},t_{2}]
equals

(45)

-[x_{1},x_{2}|t_{1},t_{2}]+\frac{1}{t_{1}}[x_{2}|t_{2}]-\frac{1}{t_{1}}[x_{2}|t_{2}-t_{1}]-\frac{1}{t_{2}}\inv[x_{1}|t_{1}]+\frac{1}{t_{2}}\inv[x_{1}|t_{1}-t_{2}].

The coefficient
t_{1}^{n_{1}-1}t_{2}^{n_{2}-1}
of the equation in Theorem
4.2
, gives (
42
).

Recall that the proof that

used that the symbol

.

If some of the

\infty

[x_{1},\dots,x_{d}]_{n_{1},\dots,n_{d}}=(-1)^{d}(-1)^{n_{1}+\dots+n_{d}}\inv([x_{d}^{-1},\dots,x_{1}^{-1}]_{n_{1},\dots,n_{d}})

For example we have

(47)

[x_{1},\infty]_{n_{1},n_{2}}=(-1)^{n_{2}}\binom{n_{1}+n_{2}}{n_{1}-1}[x_{1}]_{n_{1}+n_{2}},\quad[\infty,x_{2}]_{n_{1},n_{2}}=-(-1)^{n_{1}}\binom{n_{1}+n_{2}}{n_{2}-1}[x_{2}]_{n_{1}+n_{2}}

Remark 4.6
.

In weight 1, symbols

Proof of Theorem
4.2
.

The structure of the proof is similar to that of the depth 1 inversion relation in Example

[x]_{n}+(-1)^{n}[x^{-1}]_{n}=0

(48)

X^{-1}=[\mathbf{x}^{-1}_{d,\dots,1}|-\mathbf{t}_{d,\dots,1}].

We must prove that

4.5

\delta(X^{-1})_{n}=\delta(\inv(X))_{n}

(those that involve inverses

\mathbb{L}^{\symb}(F)
(or

) by assigning values to the

(49)

\displaystyle A=[\mathbf{x}_{2,\dots,d}|\mathbf{t}_{2,\dots,d}]

\displaystyle D=[\mathbf{x}_{1,\dots,d-1}|\mathbf{t}_{1,\dots,d-1}-t_{d}].

\mathbb{L}_{n}(F)

Using the shorthands

(53)

\displaystyle Y_{r,s}=[\mathbf{x}_{r,\dots,s}|\mathbf{t}_{r,\dots,s}]

\displaystyle,\quad Y_{r,s;u}=[\mathbf{x}_{r,\dots,s}|\mathbf{t}_{r,\dots,s}-t_{u}]

\displaystyle\delta(X^{-1})+(-1)^{d}\delta(X)=

\displaystyle\left(X+(-1)^{d}X^{-1}\right)\wedge\sum_{p=1}^{d}t_{p}[x_{p}]_{0}+

\displaystyle\sum_{2\leq p\leq d}(-1)^{p}\left(Y_{p,d}+(-1)^{d-p+1}{Y^{-1}_{p,d}}\right)\wedge{Y^{-1}_{1,p-1}}+

\displaystyle\sum_{2\leq p\leq d}Y_{p,d}\wedge\left(Y_{1,p-1}+(-1)^{p-1}{Y^{-1}_{1,p-1}}\right)+

For any regular term

B_{W}
,
C_{W}
, and
D_{W}
as in (
50
). If
W
has depth
d_{W}
the definitions of
\INV
and
\inv
imply that

(55)

\INV\big(W^{-1}+(-1)^{d_{W}}W\big)=\INV\Big(\frac{(-1)^{d_{W}}}{t_{1}}A_{W}-\frac{(-1)^{d_{W}}}{t_{1}}B_{W}-\frac{1}{t_{d_{W}}}C_{W}^{-1}+\frac{1}{t_{d_{W}}}D_{W}\Big).

Plugging this into
54
it is now straightforward to match up the terms with those on the righthand side of (
51
). This concludes the proof.
∎

Remark 4.7
.

Even if we don’t allow any symbols with
x_{i}=\infty
we still expect Theorem
4.2
to hold, but only up to torsion. For example, one can show that
6([x]_{2}+[x^{-1}]_{2})=0\in\mathbb{L}_{2}(F)
.

4.1.
A coalgebra without inverted terms

As an aside, we now show that if we change
\delta
by replacing each term
[x_{q-1}^{-1},\dots,x_{p}^{-1}|t_{q}-\mathbf{t}_{p,\dots,q-1}]
by
\inv[\mathbf{x}_{p,\dots,q-1}|\mathbf{t}_{p,\dots,q-1}-t_{q}]
, we obtain another cobracket
\delta^{\prime}
on
\mathbb{L}^{\symb}(F)
. Moreover, the groups
R_{n}(F)
defined using
\delta
or
\delta^{\prime}
are the same. All that remains is to show that
\delta^{\prime 2}=0
. As in the proof of Theorem
4.2
the argument is purely symbolic, and makes use of the operation
\INV
.

Lemma 4.8
.

For a regular term
X
we have

(56)

\INV\delta(X)=\delta^{\prime}(X),\qquad\INV\delta(X^{-1})=\delta^{\prime}(\inv(X)).

Proof.

The first equality follows immediately from the definition of
\delta^{\prime}
. The second holds in depth 1, and the general case follows by induction from (
51
).
∎

Theorem 4.9
.

\delta^{\prime}
is a cobracket on
\mathbb{L}^{\symb}(F)
, i.e.
\delta^{\prime 2}=0
.

Proof.

Let’s write
\delta(X)
in the form
\sum_{i}X^{(i)}_{1}\wedge X^{(i)}_{2}+\sum_{j}X^{(j)}_{3}\wedge{X^{(j)}_{4}}^{-1}
. By Lemma
4.8
we have

(57)

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\INV\delta^{2}(X)=\INV\delta\left(\sum_{i}X^{(i)}_{1}\wedge X^{(i)}_{2}+\sum_{j}X^{(j)}_{3}\wedge{X^{(j)}_{4}}^{-1}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}\INV\delta X^{(i)}_{1}\wedge X^{(i)}_{2}-X^{(i)}_{1}\wedge\INV\delta X^{(i)}_{2}+\sum_{j}\INV\delta X^{(j)}_{3}\wedge\inv X^{(j)}_{4}-X^{(j)}_{3}\wedge\INV\delta{X^{(j)}_{4}}^{-1}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}\delta^{\prime}X^{(i)}_{1}\wedge X^{(i)}_{2}-X^{(i)}_{1}\wedge\delta^{\prime}X^{(i)}_{2}+\sum_{j}\delta^{\prime}X^{(j)}_{3}\wedge\inv X^{(j)}_{4}-X^{(j)}_{3}\wedge\delta^{\prime}\inv X^{(j)}_{4}

\displaystyle=\delta^{\prime}\left(\sum_{i}X^{(i)}_{1}\wedge X^{(i)}_{2}+\sum_{j}X^{(j)}_{3}\wedge\inv X^{(j)}_{4}\right)=\delta^{\prime 2}(X).

This proves the result.
∎

5.
Shuffle relations

Since the multiple polylogarithms are iterated integrals they satisfy shuffle relations (see e.g.
[
Gon01
, Sec. 2.5]
). For example, one has

(58)

\Li_{n_{1}}(x_{1})\Li_{n_{2}}(x_{2})=\Li_{n_{1},n_{2}}(x_{1},x_{2})+\Li_{n_{2},n_{1}}(x_{2},x_{1})+\Li_{n_{1}+n_{2}}(x_{1}x_{2}).

This motivates us to define
shuffle products

(59)

[x_{1}]_{n_{1}}[x_{2}]_{n_{2}}=[x_{1},x_{2}]_{n_{1},n_{2}}+[x_{2},x_{1}]_{n_{2},n_{1}}+[x_{1}x_{2}]_{n_{1}+n_{2}}\in\mathbb{L}_{n_{1}+n_{2}}(F).

In terms of power series this is equivalent to

(60)

[x_{1}|t_{1}][x_{2}|t_{2}]=[x_{1},x_{2}|t_{1},t_{2}]+[x_{2},x_{1}|t_{2}|t_{1}]+\frac{1}{t_{1}-t_{2}}([x_{1}x_{2}|t_{1}]-[x_{1}x_{2}|t_{2}]).

Similarly, we define a shuffle product

(61)

[x_{1},x_{2}|t_{1},t_{2}][x_{3}|t_{3}]=[x_{1},x_{2},x_{3}|t_{1},t_{2},t_{3}]+[x_{1},x_{3},x_{2}|t_{1},t_{3},t_{2}]+[x_{3},x_{1},x_{2}|t_{3},t_{1},t_{2}]\\
+\frac{1}{t_{1}-t_{3}}\big([x_{1}x_{3},x_{2}|t_{1},t_{2}]-[x_{1}x_{3},x_{2}|t_{3},t_{2}]\big)+\frac{1}{t_{2}-t_{3}}\big([x_{1},x_{2}x_{3}|t_{1},t_{2}]-[x_{1},x_{2}x_{3}|t_{1},t_{3}]\big)

corresponding to the shuffle relations for
\Li_{n_{1},n_{2}}(x_{1},x_{2})\Li_{n_{3}}(x_{3})
.

Theorem 5.1
.

The shuffle products
[x_{1}|t_{1}][x_{2}|t_{2}]
and
[x_{1},x_{2}|t_{1},t_{2}][x_{3},t_{3}]
are zero in
\mathbb{L}(F)
.

Proof.

A simple computation shows that

(62)

\delta([x_{1}|t_{1}][x_{2}|t_{2}])=([x_{1}|t_{1}][x_{2}|t_{2}])\wedge(t_{1}[x_{1}]_{0}+t_{2}[x_{2}]_{0})\in\wedge^{2}(\mathbb{L}(F)).

Assuming by induction that
([x_{1}|t_{1}][x_{2}|t_{2}])_{n-1}=0
it follows that
([x_{1}|t_{1}][x_{2}|t_{2}])_{n}
is constant. Setting
x_{1}=x_{2}=0
shows that the constant is 0. Another straightforward computation shows that

(63)

\delta([x_{1},x_{2}|t_{1},t_{2}][x_{3}|t_{3}])=([x_{1},x_{2}|t_{1},t_{2}][x_{3}|t_{3}])\wedge\sum_{i=1}^{3}t_{i}[x_{i}]_{0}-[x_{1}|t_{1}]\wedge([x_{2}|t_{2}][x_{3}|t_{3}])\\
+[x_{2}|t_{2}]\wedge([x_{1}|t_{1}][x_{3}|t_{3}])+([x_{1}x_{2}|t_{1}][x_{3}|t_{3}])\wedge[x_{2}|t_{2}-t_{1}]-([x_{1}x_{2}|t_{2}][x_{3}|t_{3}])\wedge([x_{1}|t_{1}-t_{2}]+[x_{1}]_{0})\\
+[x_{1}x_{2}x_{3}|t_{1}]\wedge([x_{2}|t_{2}-t_{1}][x_{3}|t_{3}-t_{1}])-[x_{1}x_{2}x_{3}|t_{2}]\wedge([x_{1}|t_{1}-t_{2}][x_{3}|t_{3}-t_{2}]),

so the same argument as above shows that
[x_{1},x_{2}|t_{1},t_{2}][x_{3},t_{3}]=0
as well.
∎

Remark 5.2
.

We can similarly define shuffle products in arbitrary depth. We conjecture that they are all zero in
\mathbb{L}(F)
. Proving this would require showing that the cobracket of a shuffle relation is given in terms of shuffle relations in lower weight and depth. This appears to be the case.

Remark 5.3
.

It seems worth noting that if one replaces
\delta
by
\delta^{\prime}
, (
62
) and (
63
) hold not just in
\wedge^{2}(\mathbb{L}(F))
, but also
\wedge^{2}(\mathbb{L}^{\symb}(F))
(and not just modulo 2-torsion).

6.
The Goncharov–Rudenko Lie coalgebra

Goncharov and Rudenko
[
GR18
]
also considered the problem of explicitly constructing the motivic Lie-coalgebra. They give an explicit construction in weight 4 and less. We denote their coalgebra
\mathbb{L}^{GR}_{\leq 4}(F)
. They define
\mathbb{L}^{GR}_{1}(F)=F^{*}
,
\mathbb{L}^{GR}_{2}(F)=\mathcal{B}_{2}(F)
, and define (for
n=3,4
)
\mathbb{L}^{GR}_{n}(F)
to be the group generated by symbols
\{x\}_{n}
and
\{x,y\}_{n-1,1}
subject to certain relations, most notably a relation
Q_{n}
. The relationship with their work and ours is given in Theorem
6.1
below. Its proof is straightforward, but long, so we omit it. The main point is to show that
Q_{n}
maps to an element in
R_{n}(F)
. We stress that their symbol
\{x,y\}_{n-1,1}
does not correspond to our symbol
[x,y]_{n-1,1}
. We find our symbol more natural since it directly relates to the multiple polylogarithm (their symbol is related to motivic correlators).

Theorem 6.1
.

There is a surjective map
\mathbb{L}^{GR}_{\leq 4}(F)_{\mathbb{Q}}\to\mathbb{L}_{\leq 4}(F)_{\mathbb{Q}}
taking
\{x\}_{n}
to
[x]_{n}
,
\{x,y\}_{2,1}
to
-[x/y,y]_{2,1}-[x]_{3}-[y]_{3}
and
\{x,y\}_{3,1}
to
-[x/y,y]_{3,1}-[x]_{4}+[y]_{4}
. The map preserves the cobracket.

Remark 6.2
.

We suspect that the map is an isomorphism, but we do not know if all of our relations can be expressed in terms of the
Q_{n}
relations.
