Optimal Sequential Detection of Signals with Unknown Appearance and Disappearance Points in Time Thanks: The work was supported in part by the grant 18-19-00452 from the Russian Science Foundation at the Moscow Institute of Physics and Technology.
Alexander G. Tartakovsky, Nikita R. Berenkov, Alexei E. Kolessa, Igor V. Nikiforov

Abstract
The paper addresses a sequential changepoint detection problem, assuming that the duration of change may be finite and unknown. This problem is of importance for many applications, e.g., for signal and image processing where signals appear and disappear at unknown points in time or space. In contrast to the conventional optimality criterion in quickest change detection that requires minimization of the expected delay to detection for a given average run length to a false alarm, we focus on a reliable maximin change detection criterion of maximizing the minimal probability of detection in a given time (or space) window for a given local maximal probability of false alarm in the prescribed window. We show that the optimal detection procedure is a modified CUSUM procedure. We then compare operating characteristics of this optimal procedure with popular in engineering the Finite Moving Average (FMA) detection algorithm and the ordinary CUSUM procedure using Monte Carlo simulations, which show that typically the later algorithms have almost the same performance as the optimal one. At the same time, the FMA procedure has a substantial advantage – independence to the intensity of the signal, which is usually unknown. Finally, the FMA algorithm is applied to detecting faint streaks of satellites in optical images.

II
The Stochastic Model and Optimality Criteria

Suppose there is a sequence of independent observations
\{Y_{n}\}_{n\geqslant 1}
, observed sequentially in time subject to a change at an unknown time
\nu\in\{0,1,2,\dots\}
, which lasts till the time
\nu+N

so that
Y_{1},\dots,Y_{\nu}
and
Y_{N+\nu+1},Y_{N+\nu+2},\dots
are generated by one stochastic model and
Y_{\nu+1},Y_{\nu+2},\dots,Y_{\nu+N}
by another model.
Throughout the paper,
\nu
is treated as unknown and nonrandom, and
N

can be either unknown deterministic (as in Section
VI
) or random (as in Section
III
). See also Remark
2
.
The joint density
p({\mathbf{Y}}_{1}^{n}|{\mathsf{H}}_{\nu,N})
of the vector

{\mathbf{Y}}_{1}^{n}=(Y_{1},\dots,Y_{n})
observed up to time
n
under the hypothesis
{\mathsf{H}}_{\nu,N}
that the change happens at the time
\nu
and ends at
N
is of the form

\begin{split}&p({\mathbf{Y}}_{1}^{n}|{\mathsf{H}}_{\nu,N})=p({\mathbf{Y}}_{1}^{n}|{\mathsf{H}}_{\infty})=\prod_{t=1}^{n}g(Y_{t})\\
&~~\text{for}~~\nu\geqslant n,\\
&p({\mathbf{Y}}_{1}^{n}|{\mathsf{H}}_{\nu,N})=\prod_{t=1}^{\nu}g(Y_{t})\times\prod_{t=\nu+1}^{n}f(Y_{t})\\
&~~\text{for}~~\nu<n\leqslant\nu+N,\\
&p({\mathbf{Y}}_{1}^{n}|{\mathsf{H}}_{\nu,N})=\prod_{t=1}^{\nu}g(Y_{t})\times\prod_{t=\nu+1}^{\nu+N}f(Y_{t})\\
&\times\prod_{t=\nu+N+1}^{n}g(Y_{t})~~\text{for}~~n>\nu+N,\end{split}

(1)

where
g(Y_{t})
and
f(Y_{t})
are pre- and post-change densities, respectively. The event
\{\nu=\infty\}
and the
corresponding hypothesis
{\mathsf{H}}_{\infty}:\nu=\infty
mean that there never is a change. Notice that the model (
1
) implies that
Y_{\nu+1}
is the first post-change observation
under hypothesis
{\mathsf{H}}_{\nu,N}
.

A sequential changepoint detection procedure
T
is a stopping time associated with the time of alarm on change.

Conventional quickest detection optimality criteria require minimizing the average delay to detection for a given false alarm rate at an
infinite time horizon (assuming
N=\infty
) and do not consider a probability of detection of a change in a given fixed time interval
[
28
,
29
,
15
,
20
,
35
]
.
Often, however, practitioners are interested in such probabilities under a given false alarm rate even if the change lasts infinitely
long.
1
1

1

In practice, this means that the length of a change is substantially larger than an average detection delay.
Besides, in many applications, the length of a change
N
is finite, e.g., in
problems of detecting transient
changes with known and unknown durations
[
7
,
8
,
24
,
30
,
31
]
.
Then stopping outside of the interval
(\nu,\nu+N]
of a given duration
N
may not be quite appropriate. For example, in the context of safety-critical systems, serious degradation of the system
security occurs when the transient change is detected with a delay greater than a required time-to-alert. Therefore, the probability of detection of a change within a given fixed time interval should be used instead
of the average delay to detection. In such cases, it is reasonable to find detection rules that maximize the probability of
detection in a certain fixed time interval
(\nu,\nu+M],M\leqslant N
, and to consider the following optimality criterion:

Find a rule
T_{\mathrm{opt}}\in{\mathbb{C}}(m,\alpha)
such that for every
0<\alpha<1
and some
m\geqslant 1

\begin{split}&\inf_{\nu\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}}\operatornamewithlimits{ess\,inf}\,{\mathsf{P}}_{\nu}(T_{{\rm opt}}\leqslant\nu+M|{\mathbf{Y}}_{1}^{\nu},T_{{\rm opt}}>\nu)\\
&=\sup_{T\in{\mathbb{C}}(m,\alpha)}\inf_{\nu\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}}\operatornamewithlimits{ess\,inf}\,{\mathsf{P}}_{\nu}(T\leqslant\nu+M|{\mathbf{Y}}_{1}^{\nu},T>\nu),\end{split}

(2)

where

{\mathbb{C}}(m,\alpha)=\left\{T:\sup_{\ell\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}}{\mathsf{P}}_{\infty}(T\leqslant\ell+m|T>\ell)\leqslant\alpha\right\}

is the class of detection procedures for which the local maximal probability of false alarm

\mathsf{LPFA}_{m}(T)=\sup_{\ell\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}}{\mathsf{P}}_{\infty}(T\leqslant\ell+m|T>\ell)

in a time interval of a fixed length
m\geqslant 1
does not exceed a predefined level
\alpha\in(0,1)
.
2
2

2

In general,
m
and
M
are different.

Hereafter
{\mathsf{P}}_{\nu}
denotes the probability under which the change occurs at
\nu\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
and
{\mathsf{P}}_{\infty}
when the change never happens;
\mathbb{Z}_{+}=\{0,1,2,\dots\}
states for the set of
nonnegative integers. Also,
\operatornamewithlimits{ess\,inf}
and
\operatornamewithlimits{ess\,sup}
denote essential infimum and essential supremum, respectively.

Solving the optimization problem (
2
) for any fixed
M
(except for
M=1
) is very difficult (see
[
17
,
21
,
33
]

for some optimal properties established for the case
M=1
). Even in an asymptotic setting as
\alpha\to 0
this problem is still open.
Assume now that
M
,
M\leqslant N
is finite and random with the given distribution
\pi_{i}=\Pr(M=i)
,
i=1,2,\dots
. In particular, this assumption is reasonable when
M=N
and the unknown duration of a change
N
is a nuisance parameter, i.e., the fact of change disappearance does not have to be detected. The results also valid when the change is persistent, i.e.,
N=\infty
,
but still, the goal is to maximize the probability of detection in a time window of a random size
M
.

Introduce the probability measure

\bar{{\mathsf{P}}}_{\nu}({\mathcal{I}}\times{\mathcal{A}})=\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{I}}}\pi_{i}{\mathsf{P}}_{\nu}({\mathcal{A}}|M=i).

Then the probability of detection is re-written as

\begin{split}&\bar{{\mathsf{P}}}_{\nu}(T\leqslant\nu+M|{\mathbf{Y}}_{1}^{\nu},T>\nu)\\
&=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\pi_{i}{\mathsf{P}}_{\nu}(T\leqslant\nu+i|{\mathbf{Y}}_{1}^{\nu},T>\nu,M=i),\end{split}

(3)

and the optimality criterion (
2
) gets modified as

\begin{split}&\inf_{\nu\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}}\operatornamewithlimits{ess\,inf}\,\bar{{\mathsf{P}}}_{\nu}(T_{{\rm opt}}\leqslant\nu+M|{\mathbf{Y}}_{1}^{\nu},T_{{\rm opt}}>\nu)\\
&=\sup_{T\in{\mathbb{C}}(m,\alpha)}\inf_{\nu\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}}\operatornamewithlimits{ess\,inf}\,\bar{{\mathsf{P}}}_{\nu}(T\leqslant\nu+M|{\mathbf{Y}}_{1}^{\nu},T>\nu).\end{split}

(4)

In the next section, we provide a solution to this problem for the geometric distribution
\pi_{i}
.

III
An Optimal Detection Procedure

Let
\pi_{i}
be the geometric distribution
\mathrm{Geom}(\varrho)
with the parameter
\varrho\in(0,1)
:

\pi_{i}=\varrho(1-\varrho)^{i-1},\quad i=1,2,\dots.

Let
\Lambda_{n}=f(Y_{n})/g(Y_{n})
(
n=1,2,\dots
) be the likelihood ratio and introduce the statistic
V_{\varrho}(n)
by the recursion

V_{\varrho}(n)=\max\left\{1,V_{\varrho}(n-1)\right\}\Lambda_{n}(1-\varrho),\quad n\geqslant 1

(5)

with the initial condition
V_{\varrho}(0)=1
as well as the associated stopping time

T_{\varrho}(B)=\inf\left\{n\geqslant 1:V_{\varrho}(n)\geqslant B\right\},\quad B>0.

(6)

This kind of statistic first appears in the paper by Poor
[
22
]
who considered the exponential delay function in the minimax “quickest” detection problem. Also, his results will be used in the
proof of Theorem
1
.

Let
{\mathsf{E}}_{\nu}
and
{\mathsf{E}}_{\infty}
denote expectations under probability measures
{\mathsf{P}}_{\nu}
and
{\mathsf{P}}_{\infty}
, respectively, where
{\mathsf{P}}_{\nu}
corresponds to
model (
1
) with an assumed value of the change point
\nu
.

The following theorem, whose proof is given in the Appendix, establishes the structure of the optimal detection procedure.

Theorem 1
.

Let observations
\{Y_{n}\}_{n\geqslant 1}
be independent with a density
g(x)
pre-change and with a density
f(x)
post-change. Suppose the distribution of the window size
M
is

\mathrm{Geom}(\varrho)
. Further, assume that the
{\mathsf{P}}_{\infty}
-distribution of the likelihood ratio
\Lambda_{1}=f(Y_{1})/g(Y_{1})
is continuous and that
{\mathsf{P}}_{\infty}\{\Lambda_{1}>(1-\varrho)^{-1}\}=1
. Then the change detection rule
T_{\varrho}(B)
defined in
(
6
) with the statistic
V_{\varrho}(n)
given by the recursion (
5
)
and with threshold
B=B_{m,\alpha}
that satisfies

\sup_{\ell\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}}{\mathsf{P}}_{\infty}\left\{T_{\varrho}(B)\leqslant\ell+m|T_{\varrho}>\ell\right\}=\alpha

(7)

is maximin optimal in the problem (
4
) for all
0<\alpha<1
.

Note that statistic
V_{\varrho}(n)
is the maximal weighted likelihood ratio

V_{\varrho}(n)=\max_{1\leqslant k\leqslant n}\left[\prod_{j=k}^{n}(1-\varrho)\Lambda_{j}\right],

so the optimal rule (
6
) is nothing but a modified CUSUM rule with an additional factor
1-\varrho
.
3
3

3

In the standard CUSUM procedure
\varrho=0
.

If the distribution
{\mathsf{P}}_{\infty}(\Lambda_{1}\leqslant y)
is not continuous the assertion of Theorem
1
holds for a randomized procedure with a randomization on the boundary
B
.

Remark 1
.

As follows from the proof, the detection algorithm (
5
)–(
6
) is also optimal in the class of procedures subject to the constraint on the Average Run Length to False Alarm (ARL2FA)
{\mathsf{E}}_{\infty}[T]\geqslant\gamma
since class
{\mathbb{C}}(m,\alpha)
is more stringent than
{\mathbb{C}}_{\gamma}=\{T:{\mathsf{E}}_{\infty}[T]\geqslant\gamma\}
for some appropriately selected
\gamma=\gamma(m,\alpha)
(see Lemma
1
in the Appendix). However, ARL2FA makes sense only if the
{\mathsf{P}}_{\infty}
-distribution of stopping times of detection procedures is geometric or close to geometric – asymptotically exponential. Asymptotic exponentiality property holds for many detection procedures with Markov detection statistics
[
19
]
. However, we do not know whether this is correct for the FMA procedure considered below. If the no-change distribution of stopping times is not close to geometric, then a large value of ARL2FA does not guarantee a small value of the maximal local false alarm probability
\mathsf{LPFA}_{m}(T)
, which is usually a necessary property in practice. A detailed discussion of this issue may be found in
[
37
]
.

Remark 2
.

The assertions of Theorem
1
hold in two cases: (a) when the window size is equal to the unknown change duration,
M=N\sim\mathrm{Geom}(\varrho)
, and (b) when the change is persistent,
N=\infty
, and
M\sim\mathrm{Geom}(\varrho)
. However, the latter case has perhaps only theoretical rather than practical significance.

Remark 3
.

The time index
n
in all previous formulas can be replaced by the argument of the vector-valued function of position
(x_{i},y_{j})
,
as it is done in Section
VI
for the problem of detecting objects in two-dimensional images.

IV
Alternative Detection Procedures

In this and the next section, we set
M=N
, i.e., we assume that the window size
M
is equal to the signal duration
N
.

While detection procedure
T_{\varrho}
given by (
5
)–(
6
) is strictly optimal, it requires a strong assumption on the geometric distribution of the signal duration
N
.
A more practical approach is to use the procedures in a fixed sliding window with size
L
. In papers
[
7
,
8
]
,
a window-limited CUSUM procedure

\overline{T}=\inf\left\{n\geq L:\max\limits_{1\leq k\leq L}\left[\sum_{t=n-k+1}^{n}\lambda_{t}-A(k)\right]\geq 0\right\},

(8)

where
\lambda_{t}=\log\Lambda_{t}
is the log-likelihood ratio, has been proposed for this purpose. It was shown that
the Finite Moving Average procedure given by the stopping time

T_{\mbox{\rm\tiny FMA}}(a)=\inf\left\{n\geqslant L:\sum_{t=n-L+1}^{n}\lambda_{t}\geqslant a\right\}

(9)

and the window-limited CUSUM procedure
\overline{T}
with a specific (optimal) variable threshold
A(k)
, minimizing
the worst-case missed detection probability, have the same asymptotic performance as the maximal probability of false alarm
\alpha\to 0
.
Window-limited procedures were also considered by Lai in
[
13
]
and shown to be asymptotically optimal in the quickest change detection problem with persistent changes for minimizing average detection delay for i.i.d. and non-i.i.d stochastic models.

Another reasonable method is a simple CUSUM procedure. It is easy to show that maximizing the likelihood ratio over the unknown points of change appearance and disappearance (
\nu
and
N
, respectively) leads to Page’s CUSUM statistic:

\begin{split}&V(n)=\max_{\nu\geqslant 0}\max_{N\geqslant 1}\frac{p({\mathbf{Y}}_{1}^{n}|{\mathsf{H}}_{\nu,N})}{p({\mathbf{Y}}_{1}^{n}|{\mathsf{H}}_{\infty})}\\
&=\max\left\{1,V(n-1)\right\}\Lambda_{n},\quad n\geqslant 1.\end{split}

(10)

Hence, define the CUSUM procedure by the stopping time:

T_{\mbox{\rm\tiny CS}}(C)=\inf\left\{n\geqslant 1:V(n)\geqslant C\right\},\quad C>0.

Remark 4
.

If the LLR
\lambda_{t}
is a monotone function of the statistic
S_{t}
, then the FMA procedure can be written as

T_{\mbox{\rm\tiny FMA}}(\tilde{a})=\inf\left\{n\geqslant L:\sum_{t=n-L+1}^{n}S_{t}\geqslant\tilde{a}\right\}.

(11)

If the post-change distribution depends on an unknown parameter
\theta
, then
\lambda_{t}=\lambda_{t}(\theta)
and the optimal modified CUSUM
T_{\varrho}(B)
as well as the ordinary CUSUM
T_{\mbox{\rm\tiny CS}}(C)

depend on
\theta
. Therefore, they are sensitive to the mismatch of the true value and assumed values of
\theta
, while the FMA structure does not depend on
\theta
.
We expect that the FMA procedure maximizes (approximately)
the probability of detection
\mathsf{PD}_{\theta}
uniformly for all parameter values.

In the next section, we compare the performance of detection procedures
T_{\mbox{\rm\tiny FMA}}(\tilde{a})
and
T_{\mbox{\rm\tiny CS}}(C)
with the optimal one.

VI
Application to Detection of Streaks of Space Objects

Extracting streaks of faint space objects with unknown orbits from digital frames, captured with ground-based telescopes, is an important problem for Space Informatics.
A variety of methods is employed using intra-frame as well as inter-frame data processing (see, e.g.,
[
3
]
,
[
9
]
,
[
11
]
,
[
12
]
,
[
14
]
).
In this section, the FMA procedure, described in Section
IV
, is applied to the detection of a satellite streak with a low signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) in the digital frame (see Fig.
1
).

Fig. 1:
Digital frame with a low-contrast streak (SNR
\approx 1
). Red rectangle marks the streak position.

We use a two-stage approach. As our problem is similar to the changepoint detection problem (since the distribution of observations changes abruptly when the streak starts and ends), at the first stage, we use the FMA procedure (
9
), which will be re-written for the 2-D space case. Since FMA detects streaks with a delay (i.e., we can only localize streaks), at the next stage, we use maximum likelihood estimation to calculate the streak position more accurately.

When operating with real frames situation is aggravated by the presence of stars and background that produce strong discrete clutter. In this case, special preprocessing for clutter removal have to be implemented (see, e.g.,
[
34
]
). For our problem, we consider that the input frame is free from clutter and contains only one streak and Gaussian noise after preprocessing, independent from pixel to pixel.

To be specific, let the satellite have a linear and uniform motion in the frame. The satellite streak is given by the vector
{\mathbf{X}}=(x_{0},y_{0},x_{1},y_{1})
, where
(x_{0},y_{0})
corresponds to the start point and
(x_{1},y_{1})
corresponds to the endpoint.

Hence, consider the following model of the observation
Y_{i,j}
in pixel
(i,j)
of the 2-D frame
[
10
]
:

Y_{i,j}=AS_{i,j}({\mathbf{X}})+\epsilon_{i,j},

(12)

where
A
is a signal intensity from the object,

\{S_{i,j}({\mathbf{X}})\}
are values of the model profile of the streak that are calculated beforehand assuming point spread function (PSF) is Gaussian with a certain effective width (see Fig.
2
); and

\epsilon_{i,j}
is Gaussian noise after preprocessing with zero mean and known (estimated empirically) local variance
\sigma^{2}
. Thus, the observation
Y_{i,j}
has normal (“pre-change”) distribution
g(Y_{i,j})={\mathcal{N}}(0,\sigma^{2})

when the streak does not “cover” pixel
(i,j)
and normal (“post-change”) distribution
f(Y_{i,j})={\mathcal{N}}(AS_{i,j}({\mathbf{X}}),\sigma^{2})
when the streak “covers” the pixel
(i,j)
.

The problem is to detect the streak with the maximal probability of detection as well as to find the most accurate estimate
\hat{{\mathbf{X}}}=(\hat{x_{0}},\hat{y_{0}},\hat{x_{1}},\hat{y_{1}})
of the streak position or
to make a decision that streak does not exist in the frame.

Remark 5
.

It is worth noting that the problem of extraction of objects’ steaks can be solved non-sequentially as a fixed sample size joint hypothesis testing and estimation problem, using, e.g.,
the generalized likelihood ratio hypothesis (GLR) test. However, the GLR test requires testing too many hypotheses since neither direction nor position of streaks is known. As a result, the GLR test is usually
very time-consuming. The computational complexity of the proposed changepoint detection (with further estimation) algorithm is quite low. This is an mportant advantage over the GLR test.

VI-B

Stage 2. Accurate Estimation of the Streak Position

After localizing the streak in a certain area (denote it by
\Pi_{1}
), we use maximum likelihood estimates
\hat{A}
and
\hat{{\mathbf{X}}}
of the streak parameters
{\mathbf{X}},A
calculated from

(\hat{{\mathbf{X}}},\hat{A})=\mathop{\rm arg\,min}\limits_{{\mathbf{X}},A}\sum_{(i,j)\in\Pi_{1}}[Y_{i,j}-AS_{i,j}({\mathbf{X}})]^{2},

where minimization is under constraints
(x_{0},y_{0})\in\Pi_{1}
,
(x_{1},y_{1})\in\Pi_{1},~~A>0
.
It can be easily seen that

\hat{{\mathbf{X}}}=\mathop{\rm arg\,min}\limits_{{\mathbf{X}}}\sum_{(i,j)\in\Pi_{1}}\left[Y_{i,j}-\frac{\displaystyle\sum_{(i,j)\in\Pi_{1}}Y_{i,j}S_{i,j}({\mathbf{X}})}{\displaystyle\sum_{(i,j)\in\Pi_{1}}S_{i,j}({\mathbf{X}})^{2}}S_{i,j}({\mathbf{X}})\right]^{2}.

This maximum likelihood estimate
\hat{{\mathbf{X}}}
of the streak position yields the final solution to our problem.

to
0.9
(see Fig.

It is seen that the accuracy is high even for the SNR =1.

VII
Conclusion

We have found a strictly optimal solution to the problem of detecting signals with unknown moments of appearance and disappearance when it is required to maximize the probability of detection in the
window of random size, distributed according to geometric distribution, in the class of detection procedures with the given maximal probability of a false alarm in a prescribed
finite window. This solution is obtained using the optimal stopping theory, and the optimal procedure is a modified CUSUM. The results of Monte Carlo simulations for the Gaussian example
show that the operating characteristics of the optimal procedure are typically close to that of the more practical
FMA procedure. As a result, we propose to use the latter procedure in an important for Space Informatics problem – for
intra-frame detection of faint satellite streaks with unknown orbits in optical images.
