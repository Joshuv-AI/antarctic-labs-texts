Properties of Graphs Specified by a Regular Language
Volker Diekert, Henning Fernau, Petra Wolf

Abstract
Traditionally, graph algorithms get a single graph as input, and then they should decide if this graph satisfies a certain property Φ \Phi . What happens if this question is modified in a way that we get a possibly infinite family of graphs as an input, and the question is if there is a graph satisfying Φ \Phi in the family? We approach this question by using formal languages for specifying families of graphs, in particular by regular sets of words. We show that certain graph properties can be decided by studying the syntactic monoid of the specification language L L if a certain torsion condition is satisfied. This condition holds trivially if L L is regular. More specifically, we use a natural binary encoding of finite graphs over a binary alphabet Σ \Sigma , and we define a regular set 𝔾 ⊆ Σ ∗ \mathbb{G}\subseteq\Sigma^{*} such that every nonempty word w ∈ 𝔾 w\in\mathbb{G} defines a finite and nonempty graph. Also, graph properties can then be syntactically defined as languages over Σ \Sigma . Then, we ask whether the automaton 𝒜 \mathcal{A} specifies some graph satisfying a certain property Φ \Phi . Our structural results show that we can answer this question for all “typical” graph properties. In order to show our results, we split L L into a finite union of subsets and every subset of this union defines in a natural way a single finite graph F F where some edges and vertices are marked. The marked graph in turn defines an infinite graph F ∞ F^{\infty} and therefore the family of finite subgraphs of F ∞ F^{\infty} where F F appears as an induced subgraph. This yields a geometric description of all graphs specified by L L based on splitting L L into finitely many pieces; then using the notion of graph retraction, we obtain an easily understandable description of the graphs in each piece.

The paper is about families of finite graphs specified by a regular language, and their properties.
When dealing with algorithms, a graph is often specified by its adjacency matrix or by its induced edge-list together with the number of isolated vertices, if there are any. In either representation, a graph comes with a linear order on the vertices and the edges are directed. Moreover, an adjacency matrix ignores multiple edges, but self-loops may occur.

1.1
A sketch of our approach and our results

Let us try to give a high-level explanation of the underlying geometric idea how to approach
\rho(L)
.
Remotely, it is like understanding the geometry of a topological manifold using the fact that it locally resembles an Euclidean space. For example, it is possible to realize a torus (which is a compact
2
-dimensional surface)
as a unit square where opposite edges are identified. Every point has on open neighborhood which looks like
\mathbb{R}^{2}
and from that one easily derive that the so-called
fundamental group
(which is a global property) is the group
\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{Z}
. Therefore we cannot transform a torus neither into a sphere nor into a soup tureen with two or more handles.

In our case, we deal with purely combinatorial objects. Nevertheless, we wish to understand the set of graphs
\rho(L)
by constructing a finite subset of graphs together with an “open neighborhood” around these graphs such that
\rho(L)
is covered by that construction. Thus, if we want to check whether a certain property
\Phi
is satisfied by some graph in
\rho(L)
, then it is enough that we are able to check that locally. The key idea is to cut first

L
into pieces using the algebraic property that a regular language

has a finite syntactic monoid

is a finite union of congruence classes; and we obtain an important saturation property:

to be all words in the same congruence class of

\rho([w])
plays the role of an
open neighborhood around the graph
\rho(w)
. Inside each
\rho([w])
, we define finitely many “smallest” graphs. Thereby, we find
a finite set of finite graphs

such that the collection of these finitely many graphs still has the entire information about
\rho(L)
. In order to reveal that information, we construct for each
F\in\mathcal{F}
a (possibly infinite) graph

. The graph

contains

as an induced subgraph; and

comes
with a graph morphism onto the graph

.
The structure of that infinite graph is fully explicit and actually easy to understand. For example, it might happen that

consists of a single edge between two endpoints and

is the complete infinite bipartite graph

Our result shows that, for every regular language

if and only if for some

,

appears as a finite subgraph of

containing

.

Our geometric approach to
\rho(L)
has two steps. First, we use the algebraic notion of syntactic monoid. The second step is a graph theoretical definition of
F^{\infty}
.
The outcome of following this road map is Corollary
22

It tells us that (with respect to our positive and negative decidable results) it is enough to consider only four different classes
\mathcal{C}_{1}\subset\cdots\subset\mathcal{C}_{4}
of graphs

.

1.

\rho(L)\in\mathcal{C}_{1}
if and only if the set
\rho(L)
is finite.

2.

\rho(L)\in\mathcal{C}_{2}
implies that
\rho(L)
has bounded tree-width.

3.

\rho(L)\in\mathcal{C}_{3}
implies that every connected finite bipartite graph appears as a connected component of some
G\in\rho(L)
.

4.

\rho(L)\in\mathcal{C}_{4}
implies that every connected finite graph appears as a connected component of some

.

Moreover, if

is regular, then we can compute

such that

.
Caveat:
it may happen that

is in

.

Since the syntactic monoid of a regular language is finite, we find some

The second class

Languages

For the other two classes, the picture changes drastically: the First-Order theory

1.2
Encoding of graphs and related work

Our encoding of graphs by using words over a binary alphabet
is quite natural but obviously not unique.
For instance, one could use larger alphabets, say, a unique letter per vertex, in writing down vertex or edge lists.
As we use a code to write down vertex and edge names, we could interpret our encoding also as using a larger alphabet.
However, in the context of the questions that we discuss in this paper, this would lead to automata over infinite alphabets,
and we wanted to avoid discussing these here.

The bit complexity of
\gamma(G)
(encoding an edge-graph
G
with
n
vertices and
m
edges) is
\mathcal{O}(n\cdot m)
and hence as good as traditional incidence matrices. More compact representations seem to lead to encodings that are not fit to be tested by finite automata and are hence avoided.

With the idea of using larger alphabets, still completely different encodings are possible. For instance, Kitaev and Seif introduced in
[
13
]
a representation of directed acyclic graphs by associating vertices to sets of letters of a word. This is also interesting for our discussions, as
[
13

[
3

In

As an application, Kuske shows in

(|w|_{a})_{a\in\Gamma}
and the Parikh-mapping is the canonical homomorphism from the free monoid
\Gamma^{*}
to the free commutative monoid
\mathbb{N}^{\Gamma}
. As usual,
\mathbb{N}^{\Gamma}
is partially ordered such that

\displaystyle u\leq v\iff\forall z\in\Gamma:\;u(z)\leq v(z).

Subsets

which can be written as

L=q+\sum_{i\in I}\mathbb{N}p_{i}
are called
linear
and a finite union of linear sets is called
semi-linear
. Use the following classical results.

Proposition 1
.

(a)

The complement of a semi-linear set
L\subseteq\mathbb{N}^{\Gamma}
is effectively semi-linear. Hence, the family on semi-linear sets is an effective Boolean algebra, see
[
10
]
.

(b)

The Parikh-image of a context-free language is effectively semi-linear, see
[
20
]
.

A subset
S\subseteq\mathbb{N}^{\Gamma}
is called
positively downward-closed
if first
v(z)\geq 1
for all
v\in S
,
z\in\Gamma
and second,
u\leq v\in S
and
u(z)\geq 1
for all

imply
u\in S
. The complement of a positively downward-closed set
S\subseteq\mathbb{N}^{\Gamma}
is
upward-closed
, i. e.,
u\geq v\in S
implies
u\in S
.
An upward-closed
set
S
is determined by its set
\min(S)
of minimal elements. Dickson’s Lemma says that the set

\min(S)
is finite for all
S\subseteq\mathbb{N}^{\Gamma}
. Hence, every
upward-closed subset is semi-linear. It follows by Proposition
1
(
a
) that every
positively downward-closed set
S\subseteq\mathbb{N}^{\Gamma}
is semi-linear, too.
This observation is crucial for proving Theorem
11
.

2.3
Graphs

All graphs are assumed to be (at most) countable, given as a pair

G=(V,E)
where
E\subseteq V\times V
. An
undirected graph
is the special case where
E={E}^{-1}
, so that
E
describes the adjacency relation. If
G=(V,E)
is a directed graph, then
G
also defines the
undirected graph

(V,E\cup{E}^{-1})
;
and it defines the
undirected graph without self-loops

(V,(E\cup{E}^{-1})\setminus\mathrm{id}_{V})
. A graph without isolated vertices is called an
edge-graph
. Hence, the set of edges determines an edge-graph.
If
G^{\prime}=(V^{\prime},E^{\prime})
and
G=(V,E)

are graphs such that
V^{\prime}\subseteq V
and
E^{\prime}\subseteq E
, then

G^{\prime}
is a
subgraph
of graph
G
and we denote this fact by
G^{\prime}\leq G
. If
U\subseteq V
is any subset, then
G[U]=(U,E\cap U\times U)
denotes the
induced subgraph
of
U
in
G
.
A subset
U
is called
independent
if
G[U]
is without any edge.

A
graph morphism

\varphi:(V^{\prime},E^{\prime})\to(V,E)
is given by a mapping

\varphi:V^{\prime}\to V
such that

(u,v)\in E^{\prime}
implies
(\varphi(u),\varphi(v))\in E
. If
(V^{\prime},E^{\prime})
and
(V,E)
are undirected graphs without self-loops,
then
\varphi:(V^{\prime},E^{\prime})\to(V,E)
is a graph morphism as soon as
(\varphi(u),\varphi(v))\in E\cup\mathrm{id}_{V}
.
We say that
\varphi
is a
projection
if

\varphi
is surjective on vertices and edges, i.e.,
\varphi(V^{\prime})=V
and
\varphi(E^{\prime})=E
.
We consider graphs up to isomorphism, only. Hence, writing
G=G^{\prime}
means that graphs
G
and
G^{\prime}
are isomorphic.
According to the following Section
2.4
, a graph
F=(V,E)
is a
retract
of a graph
F^{\prime}=(V^{\prime},E^{\prime})
if there are morphisms
\varphi:F^{\prime}\to F
and
\gamma:F\to F^{\prime}
such that

\varphi\gamma
is the identity on vertices and edges of
(V,E)
.
Hence,
F
appears in
F^{\prime}
as the induced subgraph
F^{\prime}[\gamma(V)]
.
Another way to say this is that
F
is an induced subgraph of
F^{\prime}
and there is a morphism
\varphi:F^{\prime}\to F
which is the identity on
F
.

We consider several special graphs (and graph properties) in our paper. By a
star
, we denote a graph
(V,E)
such that
there exists a vertex
z\in V
with the property
E=\left\{\,\mathinner{(z,s)}\vphantom{s\in V\setminus\{z\}}\;\left|\;\vphantom{(z,s)}\mathinner{s\in V\setminus\{z\}}\right.\,\right\}
. Thus, a star has a
center

z
and the directed edges are the outgoing
rays
of the star. We also use this notion to refer to an undirected connected graph where all but possibly one vertex have degree one.
Let
G=(V,E)
be an undirected graph.
G
is called a clique if all possible edges (apart from self-loops) are contained in
E
.

G
is called bipartite if
V
can be partitioned into
V_{1}
and
V_{2}
such that there are no edges between vertices of the same class of the partition. A bipartite graph is called complete if no further edges can be added without violating bipartiteness.
By
K_{n}
we denote a clique with
n
vertices, and
K_{n,m}
denotes the complete bipartite graph with
n
vertices in one of the classes and
m
vertices in the other one. A vertex set is
independent
if its vertices are pairwise non-adjacent. A vertex set
C
is a
vertex cover
if for each edge, at least one endpoint belongs to
C
.

Some of our results revolve
tree-width
. The notation is due to Robertson and Seymour and was one of the cornerstones
in their famous graph minor project. A formal definition of tree-width is, for example, in
[
8
]
and in many other modern textbook on graph theory or graph algorithms.

In our paper, every word
w\in\Sigma^{*}
represents a directed finite graph
\rho(w)=(V(w),E(w))
together with a linear order on vertices as follows.

\displaystyle V(w)

\displaystyle=\left\{\,\mathinner{ab^{m}a\in ab^{+}a}\vphantom{ab^{m}a\leq w}\;\left|\;\vphantom{ab^{m}a\in ab^{+}a}\mathinner{ab^{m}a\leq w}\right.\,\right\}

\displaystyle E(w)

\displaystyle=\left\{\,\mathinner{(ab^{m}a,ab^{n}a)\in ab^{+}a\times ab^{+}a}\vphantom{ab^{m}aaab^{n}a\leq w}\;\left|\;\vphantom{(ab^{m}a,ab^{n}a)\in ab^{+}a\times ab^{+}a}\mathinner{ab^{m}aaab^{n}a\leq w}\right.\,\right\}

The empty word represents the empty graph: there are no vertices and no edges. We extend
\rho
to
2^{\Sigma^{*}}
by
\rho(L)=\{\rho(w)\mid w\in L\}
.
Vice versa, if
G=(V,E)
denotes a finite graph with a linear order on its vertices, then, for
1\leq i,j\in\mathbb{N}
, the
i
-th vertex is represented by the factor
ab^{i}a
, and an edge from the
i
-th vertex to the
j
-th vertex is represented
by the factor
ab^{i}aaab^{j}a
. Thus, vertices are encoded
by elements in the set
\mathbb{V}=\left\{\,\mathinner{ab^{i}a}\vphantom{1\leq i\in\mathbb{N}}\;\left|\;\vphantom{ab^{i}a}\mathinner{1\leq i\in\mathbb{N}}\right.\,\right\}
and
edges are encoded
by elements is the set
\mathbb{E}=\left\{\,\mathinner{ab^{i}aaab^{j}a}\vphantom{1\leq i,j\in\mathbb{N}}\;\left|\;\vphantom{ab^{i}aaab^{j}a}\mathinner{1\leq i,j\in\mathbb{N}}\right.\,\right\}
.
Note that
\mathbb{V}\cap\mathbb{E}=\emptyset
and
\mathbb{V}\cup\mathbb{E}
is an infinite regular code. Using these conventions, the regular set
\mathbb{G}=(\mathbb{V}\cup\mathbb{E})^{*}
as well as its subset
\mathbb{E}^{*}\mathbb{V}^{*}
represents all finite graphs.
The same property holds for the complement
\Sigma^{*}\setminus\mathbb{G}
: it
represents all finite graphs, too. Indeed,
\mathbb{G}\cap a\mathbb{G}=\emptyset
but
\rho(\mathbb{G})=\rho(a\mathbb{G})
, since the words
w\in\mathbb{G}
and
aw\notin\mathbb{G}
share the same set of factors from
\mathbb{V}\cup\mathbb{E}
.
In contrast to
\mathbb{G}
, infinitely many nonempty words in
\Sigma^{*}\setminus\mathbb{G}
represent the empty graph, for example all words without any
b
or with at most one
a
.
The set
\mathbb{E}^{*}
represents all edge-graphs, i. e., all graphs without isolated vertices.
Every nonempty finite graph has infinitely many representations in
\mathbb{G}
.
For example, there are uncountably many subsets
L\subseteq(aba)^{+}\subseteq\mathbb{V}^{+}
and each
\rho(L)
represents nothing but the one-point graph without self-loop.
In order to choose a unique (and minimal) representation for
a finite graph
G=(V,E)
, we choose the minimal word
\gamma(G)=u_{1}\cdots u_{m}v_{1}\cdots v_{n}\in\mathbb{G}
in the short-lex ordering
on
\Sigma^{*}
such that
\rho\gamma(G)=G
,
u_{k}\in\mathbb{E}

for
1\leq k\leq m
and
v_{\ell}\in\mathbb{V}

for
1\leq\ell\leq n
. Each
u_{k}
is of the form

ab^{i}aaab^{j}a
representing an edge and each
v_{\ell}
is of the form

ab^{i}a
representing an isolated vertex. We call
\gamma(G)
the
short-lex representation
of
G
.
Since
\gamma(G)
is minimal w.r.t.
\mathrel{\leq_{\mathrm{slex}}}
, we have
m=\left|\mathinner{E}\right|
and
n
is the number of isolated vertices. For a graph without isolated vertices, i. e., an edge-graph, this means that it is given by its edge list.
The set of all
\gamma\rho(\mathbb{G})
is context-sensitive but not context-free.
The
uvwxy
-Theorem (i. e., the context-free pumping lemma) does not hold for
\gamma\rho(\mathbb{G})
. For instance, all edge-graphs in
\gamma\rho(\mathbb{G})
with
n
vertices must be represented by vertex names
ab^{i}a
with
1\leq i\leq n
, admitting no ‘holes’ in this vertex name interval, as otherwise there would be a smaller short-lex representation of some graph. Such a property is not maintained by pumping.

2.4
Retractions and retracts

Let
\rho:X\to Y
and
\gamma:Y\to X
be mappings between sets. (This holds more general for mappings which are morphisms in some category.)
If
\rho(\gamma(y))=y
for all
y\in Y
, then
\rho
is called a
retraction
and
Y
is called a
retract
of
X
with
section

\gamma
. We also say that

{\rho}^{-1}(y)
is the
fiber
of
y\in Y
.
For example, if
\rho:X\to Y
is a homomorphism of groups
X
and
Y
and
H=\ker(\rho)
is the kernel, then
\rho
is a retraction
if and only if
X
is a semi-direct product of
H
by
Y
.
Another example comes from formal languages: let
X
be the set of deterministic finite automata (DFAs) where every state is reachable. Then the minimization process defines a retraction to the set of minimal DFAs.

Later in Section
4.5
, we define a
marked graph

as a triple
(V_{F},E_{F},\mu)
, where
(V_{F},E_{F})
is a finite graph and

\mu\subseteq V_{F}\cup E_{F}
is the set of marked vertices and edges.
Then,
(V_{F},E_{F},\mu)\mapsto(V_{F},E_{F})
defines a retraction by letting
\gamma(V_{F},E_{F})=(V_{F},E_{F},\emptyset)
.

Let
\mathcal{G}
be the set of finite graphs,
\rho:\mathbb{G}\to\mathcal{G}
be the
representation of graphs by words, and
\gamma:\mathcal{G}\to\mathbb{G}
the encoding of a graph by its short-lex normal form. Then
\rho
is a retraction. Retractions are a main tool to understand
\rho(L)
if

L
is regular or more general, if
L
satisfies the
b
-torsion property, as defined in the next section.

3
The
b
-torsion property

We are interested in properties of graphs which are specified
by languages
L\subseteq\mathbb{G}
. If
L
can be arbitrary, then
we can specify uncountably many families of graphs. So, we cannot expect any interesting and general positive (decidability) results.
As a minimal request, we restrict our attention to
subsets
L\subseteq\mathbb{G}
where membership for
\rho(L)
is decidable.
As a matter of fact, membership for
\rho(L)
might be decidable
although membership for
L
is undecidable.
As we will see in Corollary
12
, the following definition yields a sufficient condition that membership for
\rho(L)
becomes decidable.

Definition 2
.

Let
\Gamma
be a finite alphabet containing the letter
b
. A subset
L\subseteq\Gamma^{*}
satisfies
the
(b,t,p)
-torsion property
if we have:

b^{t}\equiv_{L}b^{t+p}.
It satisfies the
b
-torsion property
if there are
t,p\in\mathbb{N}
with
p\geq 1
such that
L
satisfies
the
(b,t,p)
-torsion property.

Every regular language
L\subseteq\Gamma^{*}
satisfies
the
b
-torsion property because the syntactic monoid
M_{L}
is finite.
The
b
-torsion property is a strong restriction if
L
is not regular. For example,
K=\left\{\,\mathinner{a^{n}b^{n}}\vphantom{n\in\mathbb{N}}\;\left|\;\vphantom{a^{n}b^{n}}\mathinner{n\in\mathbb{N}}\right.\,\right\}
does not have that property since
b^{k}\equiv_{L}b^{m}\iff k=m
.
The context-free language

L=\{wa\overleftarrow{w}\mid w\in\{aba,ab^{2}a\}^{+}\}
is not regular, but it satisfies the
b
-torsion property for
t=3
and
p=1
.
In this case,
\rho(L)
is a not very interesting set of a few small graphs. The next example shows that there are (non-regular) context-free languages satisfying a
(b,t,p)
-torsion property where
\rho(L)
is infinite.

Example 3
.

Let
C
be a nonempty finite alphabet and
K\subseteq C^{*}
be context-free.
Let
h:C^{*}\to\mathbb{E}^{*}
be a homomorphism. That is,
h
is defined
by words
h(c)\in(ab^{+}aaab^{+}a)^{*}
for
c\in C
. Suppose that
h(K)
is infinite.
Still, the set
\rho(h(K))
is finite. Indeed, let
t=\max\left\{i\in\mathbb{N}\,\middle|\,\exists c\in C:\,ab^{i}a\leq h(c)\right\}
, then graphs in
\rho(h(K))
have at most
t
vertices.
Let us make the language
h(K)

larger by closing
h(K)
under rewriting rules
b\to b^{1+p}
.
Context-free languages are closed under adding context-free rewriting rules. Therefore,
h(K)
is context-free, too. We obtain
a new context-free language
L
with
h(K)\subseteq L
and where
L
satisfies the
(b,t,p)
-torsion property. We claim that
\rho(h(L))
is a very rich and an infinite family of graphs (in contrast to the finite set
\rho(h(K))
).

We content ourselves to consider the case
p=1
. For
p=1
it is rather easy to see that every non-empty finite edge-graph appears in
\rho(h(L))
: we have
\rho(h(L))=\rho(\mathbb{E}^{*})
.
Let
G=(V_{G},E_{G})\in\rho(\mathbb{E}^{*})
and
m=|E_{G}|
be the number of edges.
Since
h(K)
is infinite, there is some edge

e=ab^{i}aaab^{j}a
with
1\leq i,j\leq t
such that
e
appears in some
w_{f}\in h(L)
at least
m
times as a factor.
Then, thanks to
p=1
, we have

w=(ab^{t}aaab^{t}a)^{\ell}\in L
, where
m\leq\ell
and
\ell
is very large.
The graph
\rho(w)
is a one-point graph with a self-loop.
Let us come back the graph
G
. Without restriction, we have

V_{G}\subseteq\{t,\ldots,\ell\}
.
Now, for each
(u,v)\in E_{G}
, one after the other,
we replace one factor
ab^{t}aaab^{t}a
in
w
by the factor

ab^{u}aaab^{v}a
. This changes the word
w
, but the new word
w
still belongs to
L
, again thanks to
p=1
. By creating, if necessary, several copies of the same edge, the procedure yields a word

w_{G}
such that
w_{G}\in L
and
\rho(w_{G})=G
.
∎

As soon as all cyclic submonoids of
M_{L}
are finite,
L
satisfies the
b
-torsion property for all letters
b\in\Gamma
. For example, consider the Word Problem of any free Burnside group
\mathcal{B}(2,p)
. All of them satisfy the
b
-torsion property. Almost
all of the groups
\mathcal{B}(2,p)
are infinite and therefore the corresponding Word Problems are
not regular. If it is not regular, then the Word Problem of
\mathcal{B}(2,p)
is even not context-free, since a periodic group cannot have any non-trivial free group of finite index by
[
19
]
.

For the rest of the paper, if

satisfies the

-torsion property, then
t,p\in\mathbb{N}
, standing for
threshold
and
period
, denote those natural numbers such that the cyclic submonoid generated by the letter
b
in the syntactic monoid
M_{L}
is isomorphic to
C_{t,p}
. That is, we have
t,p\in\mathbb{N}
with

, where

is minimal such that

\displaystyle\left\{\,\mathinner{[b^{n}]}\vphantom{n\in\mathbb{N}}\;\left|\;\vphantom{[b^{n}]}\mathinner{n\in\mathbb{N}}\right.\,\right\}=\left\{\,\mathinner{[b^{c}]}\vphantom{0\leq c\leq t+p-1}\;\left|\;\vphantom{[b^{c}]}\mathinner{0\leq c\leq t+p-1}\right.\,\right\}\,.

(1)

Moreover, we assume that
L
is specified such that on input

, we can compute the value

with

. This assumption is satisfied if

For

, we have

[ab^{c}a]=a[b^{c}]a
and

.

Let

Let

Lemma 6

Let

\displaystyle w\in L\iff\widehat{w}\subseteq L\iff\mathrm{rf}(w)\in L\,.

4
Main results

The main results of the paper are: (1) for
L\subseteq\mathbb{G}
satisfying the
b
-torsion property (see Section
3
), there is a regular language
R\subseteq\mathbb{G}
with
\rho(L)=\rho(R)
and (2)
for a context-free language satisfying the
b
-torsion property (e.g., any regular language)
R\subseteq\mathbb{G}
, we have
an effective ‘geometric description’ of the graphs in
\rho(R)
. From these representations, we can deduce our classification and (un)decidability results as already mentioned in the introduction. The (un)decidability results are detailed in the next section.

This geometric description is obtained as follows. Using the fact that
R
is regular (or context-free satisfying the
(b,t,p)
-torsion property), in a first step, we find effectively a semi-linear description of
\rho(R)
. In a second step, we compute a finite set of finite graphs. Each member
F
in that finite family is a retraction of some possibly infinite graph
F^{\infty}
.
The description of each
G\in\rho(L)
is given by selecting some

F
and the cardinality of every fiber. The precise meaning will become clear later. As a consequence of the description, we are able to show various decidability results.

4.1
Examples

The following examples serve as an introduction to a more general situation we will face later.

Example 7
.

In the following, we let
R\subseteq\mathbb{G}
and

such that

.

1.

Let

and

for

in

.
Therefore,

is a single star with at least one ray and at most

is finite, then

is infinite if and only if there is some

such that

.

One can show that

.

2.

If

is set of stars with center

is finite we are done.
Otherwise, let

be infinite. Then, for each

, there is a star in

with more than

there is some

and a word

which has more than

with

. If

is large enough, then each of these factors

where we have that

. This yields a word

. Now,

is very long as

is very large. Hence, we can factorize the word

such that first,

contains one of these factors

and second,

contains almost all stars, i.e., all stars but finitely many that are missing. ∎

The

-torsion property is trivially satisfied if

, but it might be exponentially better.
This type of data compression is important and well-known

]

4.2
Introducing new alphabets

After seeing a couple of examples, we introduce certain subsets of
\mathbb{G}
as alphabets to express subsets of graphs. This
prepares the geometric viewpoint to view a graph as a point in
the
d
-dimensional space
\mathbb{N}^{d}
if the size of the chosen alphabet is
d
. If
L
is regular, then the dimension depends on
L
and it can be quite large, but it is computable by using the reduced form of words in

. Below, all this will be explained in details.

Let
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
. Depending on
\ell
, we define
two finite and disjoint sets that we consider as alphabets:

\displaystyle A_{\ell}

\displaystyle=\left\{\,\mathinner{ab^{m}aaab^{n}a}\vphantom{1\leq m,n\leq\ell}\;\left|\;\vphantom{ab^{m}aaab^{n}a}\mathinner{1\leq m,n\leq\ell}\right.\,\right\}\subseteq\mathbb{E},

(2)

\displaystyle B_{\ell}

\displaystyle=\left\{\,\mathinner{ab^{m}a}\vphantom{1\leq m\leq\ell}\;\left|\;\vphantom{ab^{m}a}\mathinner{1\leq m\leq\ell}\right.\,\right\}\subseteq\mathbb{V}.

(3)

Note that
A_{\ell}=B_{\ell}aB_{\ell}
.
By

, we denote the union of

and
B_{\ell}
, which is also a finite alphabet with a linear order between letters given by
the following definition:

x\leq_{\ell}y\iff xy\in A_{\ell}B_{\ell}\vee(xy\in(A_{\ell}A_{\ell}\cup B_{\ell}B_{\ell})\wedge x\mathrel{\leq_{\mathrm{slex}}}y).

(4)

We have
A_{\ell}\cap B_{\ell}=\emptyset
and
\mathbb{N}^{C_{\ell}}
has dimension

d=\ell(\ell+1)
.
Actually,
C_{\ell}
is a code, i. e.,

c_{1}\cdots c_{m}=d_{1}\cdots d_{n}\in\Sigma^{*}
with
c_{i},d_{j}\in C_{\ell}
implies

m=n
and
c_{i}=d_{i}
for all
1\leq i\leq m
. Hence,
\leq_{\ell}
is well-defined by Equation (
4
).

Definition 9
.

Let
L\subseteq\mathbb{G}
satisfy the
b
-torsion property. Then we define
an alphabet
C_{L}\subseteq C_{\ell}
where
\ell
is large enough such that for every

its reduced form
\mathrm{rf}(w)
can be written as a word in
C_{\ell}^{*}
. To make the definition unique, we choose
C_{L}
to be the smallest

C_{L}\subseteq\bigcup_{\ell\in\mathbb{N}}C_{\ell}
which has this property.

If the context to
L
is clear, then we drop the index
L
and
we denote by
C
any subset of some
C_{\ell}
such that

. This flexibility is useful if we wish to introduce new vertices or new edges and we need “fresh” names in
C
for them. In order to distinguish the factor ordering
and the linear ordering defined by Equation (

), we denote the latter by
\leq_{C}
.

The linear order

on

defines a corresponding

.
Moreover,
if

with

and
u,v\in\Sigma^{*}
, then
u,v\in C^{*}
. The analogue for

does not hold, in general.
For example,
aba\in B
and
abaaaba\in C
, but

.
As

is a code, the inclusion

yields an embedding

. Let
w\in C^{*}
and
G=\rho(w)
.
If

, then
the minimal element in
{h}^{-1}_{C}(L)
w.r.t. the short-lex ordering for words in

is a word in

w.r.t. the ordering

.

4.3
The power of
b
-torsion

Recall that for any subset
P\subseteq\Sigma^{*}
and
c\in\Sigma^{*}
we denote by
\pi_{P}(v)\in\mathbb{N}^{P}
its Parikh image as defined in Section
2.2
.
The following lemma shows a crucial “downward-closure-property” used in Theorem
11
.

Lemma 10
.

Let
L
,
C
, and
\mathrm{rf}
as in Definition
4
and in Definition
9
.
Let
v\in C^{*}
and
w\in L
such that
\pi_{C}(v)\leq\pi_{C}(\mathrm{rf}(w))
. If
\pi_{\{z\}}(v)\geq 1
for all
z\in C
, then we have
\rho(v)\in\rho(L)
.

Proof.

If
\pi_{\{z\}}(v)=\pi_{\{z\}}(\mathrm{rf}(w))
for all
z\in C
, then

\rho(v)=\rho(w)
and therefore
\rho(v)\in\rho(L)
because
\rho(w)\in\rho(L)
thanks to
w\in L
. Thus, we may assume that

1\leq\pi_{\{z\}}(v)<\pi_{\{z\}}(\mathrm{rf}(w))
for some
z\in C
.
Thus, without restriction we have
\mathrm{rf}(w)=uzu^{\prime}zu^{\prime\prime}
with
z\in C
and
u,u^{\prime},u^{\prime\prime}\in C^{*}
and
\pi_{C}(v)\leq\pi_{C}(\mathrm{rf}(w^{\prime}))
where

w^{\prime}=uu^{\prime}zu^{\prime\prime}
. We have
\mathrm{rf}(w^{\prime})=w^{\prime}
by Definition
4
. Moreover, a repetition of some
z\in C
does not change the specified graph.
Let
\widehat{w}^{\prime}
denote the saturation of
w^{\prime}
. By Lemma
6
,
we have
\rho(\widehat{w}^{\prime})\subseteq\rho(L)
. Define
L^{\prime}=L\cup\widehat{w}^{\prime}
. Then we have

w^{\prime}\in L^{\prime}
and
\rho(L)=\rho(L^{\prime})
; moreover,
L^{\prime}
satisfies the same
(b,t,p)
-torsion property as
L
does. We can work with the same
C
, too.
Since
w^{\prime}
is shorter than
w
, we conclude that by induction on the length of
w
that
\rho(v)\in\rho(L^{\prime})=\rho(L)
. ∎

Theorem 11
.

Let
L\subseteq\mathbb{G}
be any language satisfying the
b
-torsion property. Then there is a regular
set
R\subseteq\mathbb{G}
such that

\rho(L)=\rho(R)\,.

Proof.

The proof relies on Dickson’s Lemma. We have
\rho(L)=\bigcup\left\{\rho(\widehat{v})\,\middle|\,v\in L\right\}
by Lemma
6
.
The set
\rho(\mathrm{rf}(L))
is finite, as
L
satisfies the
b
-torsion property. Thus, there is a finite
subset
K\subseteq L
such that
\rho(\mathrm{rf}(L))=\bigcup\left\{\rho(v)\,\middle|\,v\in K\right\}
. Let
C\subseteq\mathbb{E}\cup\mathbb{V}
be the finite subset such that

C=\left\{z\in\mathbb{E}\cup\mathbb{V}\,\middle|\,\exists v\in K:\pi_{\mathbb{E}\cup\mathbb{V}}(v)(z)\geq 1\right\}\,.

Splitting
L
into disjoint subsets and replacing
C
by various subsets of
C
we may assume without restriction that for all
w\in L
, the corresponding
C
contains (after the split) exactly all letters that are factors of
\mathrm{rf}(w)
and moreover, for all
v\in C^{*}
, we have

z\leq\mathrm{rf}(w)\iff\pi_{C}(w)(z)\geq 1
.
After this modification, there are no vectors in

\pi_{C}(\mathrm{rf}(L))
with zero-entries. The crucial observation is stated in
Lemma
10
. The lemma tells us that we do not change
\rho(L)
if we augment

L
by all words
v\in C^{*}
where there is some
w\in L

such that
1\leq\pi_{C}(v)(z)\leq\pi_{C}(\mathrm{rf}(w))(z)
for all
z\in C
.
Therefore, we may assume without restriction that
\pi_{C}(\mathrm{rf}(L))

is positively downward-closed according to Section
2.2
.
We also explained in Section
2.2
that Dickson’s Lemma implies that
\pi_{C}(\mathrm{rf}(L))
is semi-linear.
By Parikh’s Theorem, see Proposition
1
(
b
), there is a regular set
R^{\prime}\subseteq C^{*}
of words such that

\pi_{C}(R^{\prime})=\pi_{C}(\mathrm{rf}(L))
.
The class of regular sets is closed under regular substitutions.
The inclusion
C\subseteq(\mathbb{V}\cup\mathbb{E})^{*}
defines a canonical homomorphism
h:C^{*}\to(\mathbb{V}\cup\mathbb{E})^{*}
.
Hence, if we substitute in
R^{\prime}
every letter
z
by
[h(z)]
, then
we obtain
\rho(R)=\rho(L)
, where

R=\bigcup\left\{\widehat{w}\,\middle|\,w\in R^{\prime}\right\}
.
∎

Corollary 12
.

Let
L\subseteq\mathbb{G}
satisfy the
b
-torsion property. Then, given a finite graph
G=(V_{G},E_{G})
as an input,
it is decidable whether
G\in\rho(L)
.

The contents of Corollary
12
inspired the title of this subsection.

Proof.

By Theorem
11
, we may replace
L
by some regular set
R
where
\rho(R)=\rho(L)
.
In particular, we can calculate the threshold
t
and the period
p
such that
R
satisfies the
(b,t,p)
-torsion property.
The set
\rho(\mathrm{rf}(R))
is finite and effectively computable.
For every
F\in\rho(\mathrm{rf}(R))
, we compute the
short-lex normal-form
\gamma(F)
as defined above.
We obtain a finite set
W
of words containing all those
\gamma(F)
. Thus, the set
\widehat{W}=\bigcup\left\{\widehat{w}\,\middle|\,w\in W\right\}
is effectively regular and it holds that
\rho(\widehat{W})=\rho(R)
.
Let
m=\max\left\{|aua|\,\middle|\,u\in\Sigma^{*}\wedge\exists w\in W:aua\leq w\right\}
. Let

. Hence,

if and only if there is word

of length at most

such that

.
∎

Corollary 13
.

Let
L\subseteq\mathbb{G}
be context-free satisfying the
(b,t,p)
-torsion property. Then, we can effectively calculate a regular set
R\subseteq\mathbb{G}
such that

\rho(R)=\rho(L)
.

Proof.

Let
C=\left\{z\in\mathbb{V}\cup\mathbb{E}\,\middle|\,\forall m\in\mathbb{N}:ab^{m}a\leq z\implies m\leq t+p-1\right\}
. The
inclusion of
C\subseteq(\mathbb{V}\cup\mathbb{E})^{*}
defines
a homomorphism
h
from the free monoid
C^{*}
to
(\mathbb{V}\cup\mathbb{E})^{*}
.
Hence,
L^{\prime}={h}^{-1}(L)
is effectively context-free. Therefore,

\pi_{C}(L^{\prime})=\pi_{C}({h}^{-1}(L))
is effectively semi-linear.
The semi-linear set can be represented by a regular language

.
As in the proof of Theorem

, we obtain

as the image of
R^{\prime}
under the regular substitution which replaces every letter

by
[h(z)]
.
∎

Let
R\subseteq\mathbb{G}
be regular. It is well-known that there might be a much more concise representation by some context-free language
K\subseteq\mathbb{G}
such that
\pi_{C}(K)=\pi_{C}(R)
and hence
\rho(K)=\rho(R)
. Therefore, we might describe graph families even more concisely using context-free grammars than using NFAs.

Switching the alphabet and Parikh images

\displaystyle q_{j}+\sum_{i\in I_{j}}\mathbb{N}p_{i}=\left(q_{j}+\sum_{i\in I_{j}\setminus\{1\}}\mathbb{N}p_{i}\right)\cup\left(q_{j}+p_{1}+\sum_{i\in I_{j}}\mathbb{N}p_{i}\right)\,.

\pi(L)=q+\sum_{i\in I}\mathbb{N}p_{i}

.

4.5
Marked graphs

In the next steps, we define for

a finite family
of finite graphs
\mathcal{F}_{\beta}

is just a singleton of a marked graph. The family

still depends on

than indices

for defining

.
Then, for each

, we define a possibly infinite graph

, using the notion of a marked graph.

Definition 15
.

For
z\in C
let
\alpha^{\prime}(z)=\alpha(z)
if
\alpha(z)<\infty
and
\alpha^{\prime}(z)=1
, otherwise.
We let

is as in (

Since

is a single word, the set

is a finite set of finite graphs.
We are now defining the crucial notion of a marked graph,
where some vertices and edges are marked.

Definition 16
.

A

is a tuple
F=(V_{F},E_{F},\mu)
, where
(V_{F},E_{F})
is a finite graph and
\mu\subseteq V_{F}\cup E_{F}
denotes the set of marked vertices and edges.
Isolated vertices may appear, but if an isolated vertex is marked, then there is exactly one isolated vertex.
We also require that whenever an edge
(u,v)
is marked, then at least one of its endpoints is marked, too.
A
marked edge-graph
is a marked graph without isolated vertices.

In a first step we let the word
w_{\alpha}
and the set
R_{\alpha}
as defined above in Definition
15
. Performing another splitting, we can write

as a finite union of sets

R_{\alpha,k}
such that each
R_{\alpha,k}
defines exactly one graph
(V_{\alpha,k},E_{\alpha,k})
. By making, if necessary,
the alphabet
C
as well as the threshold

larger (but keeping the period

), we assume that for each
(\alpha,k)
, there is some
\beta:C\to\mathbb{N}_{\infty}
such that

\rho(\prod_{z\in C}z^{\beta(z)})=(V_{\alpha,k},E_{\alpha,k})

(9)

where
\beta(z)\in\{0,1,\infty\}
with the requirements that first,

\beta(z)=\infty\implies[z]\neq\{z\}
and second,
if

denotes an edge with

, then

\beta(z)=\infty\iff([u]\neq\{u\}\vee[v]\neq\{v\})
.
In order to simplify the notation, we concentrate on one

(\alpha,k)
and we denote
(V_{\alpha,k},E_{\alpha,k})
as
(V_{F^{\prime}},E_{F^{\prime}})
.

We are ready to define the marking for

.
We mark exactly those vertices and edges

where

where

are marked.
In the final step, if an isolated vertex is marked, then remove all isolated marked vertices

we were interested in.

We denote by

this family of marked graphs which was defined through the function

Let us switch to a more abstract viewpoint.
We let

be any finite family of marked graphs. For each

, we define a possibly infinite graph

A

is a
decidable

\Phi\subseteq\mathbb{G}
. For a finite graph
G
, we write

if the short-lex representation
\gamma(G)
belongs to
\Phi
.
8
8

8

Recall that

\rho(w)
is realized as a graph with a natural linear order on the vertices: we have
ab^{c}a\leq ab^{d}a\iff c\leq d
.

Given a word
w\in\mathbb{G}
, we can compute
\gamma\rho(w)
. Hence, we can assume without restriction that
\Phi
is saturated:
{\rho}^{-1}(\rho(\Phi))=\Phi.

To simplify our presentation, we focus on properties of undirected finite graphs (without self-loops).
This can be achieved by making the set
\Phi
larger
such that
\Phi
has the following desired property:
If
u\in\mathbb{G}
represents the graph
\rho(u)=(V,E)
and
\Phi
speaks about undirected graphs (resp. undirected graphs without self-loops) then
\rho(u)\models\Phi\iff(V,E\cup{E}^{-1})\models\Phi

(resp.
\rho(u)\models\Phi\iff(V,(E\cup{E}^{-1})\setminus\mathrm{id}_{V})\models\Phi
).

We are interested in the following (uniform) satisfiability problem
\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Sat}(\mathcal{G}_{F},\Phi)
.

•

Input: A marked graph
F
and a graph property
\Phi\subseteq\mathbb{G}
.

•

Question: “
\exists G\in\mathcal{G}_{F}:G\models\Phi
?”

Throughout this section,
F
denotes a marked graph and

denotes the family of graphs defined in Definition

Sometimes, it will be crucial in the following that

\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Sat}_{F}(\cdot)
or
\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Sat}_{\Phi}(\cdot)

For various well-studied graph properties, the satisfiability problem is always decidable. This includes problems where

\Phi
states that a graph is planar (resp. is closed under graph-minors, resp. perfect,
k

Theorem 24
.

Let either
\mathcal{G}_{F}
be finite (i. e., a singleton) or
\Phi
be any graph property which is closed under taking induced subgraphs (or both).
Then,
\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Sat}(\mathcal{G}_{F},\Phi)
is decidable.

Proof.

Since
F
is an induced subgraph for every
G\in\mathcal{G}_{F}
, it is enough to check whether
F\models\Phi
. This is possible, because
\Phi
is decidable by definition.
∎

In many cases, graph properties are expressible either in monadic second-order logic (MSO for short) or in first-order logic (FO for short). MSO is a rich and versatile class to define graph properties. Moreover, we allow quantification over both, sets of vertices and sets of edges. Since

defines graphs with a linear order, we can express in MSO, for example, that the

We use the following well-known results
as a black box.
First, given an MSO-sentence
\Phi
and

\Phi
, see, e. g.,
[
4
,
5
,
23
]
.
As a second black box, we use Trakhtenbrot’s Theorem
[

]

\Phi
.

Trakhtenbrot’s Theorem also holds in the following smaller family

\Phi

The family

Let

Let

According to Theorem

contains a graph

As
