An application of a generalization of Artin’s primitive root conjecture in the theory of monoid rings
R.​ C.​ Daileda

Abstract
Using techniques of algebraic and analytic number theory, we resolve a question on monoid rings posed by Kulosman, et. al., under the assumption of the Generalized Riemann Hypothesis (GRH). Specifically, we show that under an appropriate GRH, for any (rational) prime p p the set E ⁡ ( p ) = { q ​ prime | X q − 1 ​ factors in ​ 𝔽 p ​ [ X ; M ] } E(p)=\{q\text{ prime}\,|\,X^{q}-1\text{ factors in }\mathbb{F}_{p}[X;M]\} , where M = ⟨ 2 , 3 ⟩ = ℕ 0 ∖ { 1 } M=\langle 2,3\rangle=\mathbb{N}_{0}\setminus\{1\} , contains a subset with positive natural density. In particular E ⁡ ( p ) ≠ ∅ E(p)\neq\varnothing . This proves that M M is not a so-called “Matsuda monoid” of any positive type. For p = 2 , 3 p=2,3 this was observed by Kulosman, who provided factorizations of X 7 − 1 X^{7}-1 and X 11 − 1 X^{11}-1 in 𝔽 2 ​ [ X ; M ] \mathbb{F}_{2}[X;M] and 𝔽 3 ​ [ X ; M ] \mathbb{F}_{3}[X;M] , respectively. Our results explain and reproduce both of these factorizations, as well.

Let
\Gamma
be a commutative monoid, written additively. We say that
\Gamma
is
torison-free
if for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
and every
\alpha,\beta\in\Gamma
, the equation

implies that
\alpha=\beta
. If
\Gamma
is torsion-free,
\alpha\in\Gamma
and

, the equation
nx=\alpha
has at most one solution in
\Gamma
. If
px=\alpha
has
no
solution in
\Gamma
for every prime

, the authors of
[
1
]
say that
\alpha
has
height

(0,0,\ldots)
. If this is the case and

is an integer, choose any prime
p
dividing

. Then
nx=p(kx)
for some
k\in\mathbb{N}
. Since
px=\alpha
has no solution in
\Gamma
, this implies that
nx=\alpha
doesn’t either. We conclude that
\alpha
has height
(0,0,\ldots)
if and only if for every
n\geq 2
the equation
nx=\alpha
has no solution in
\Gamma
. We prefer to term such an
\alpha
to be
indivisible
. One advantage of this formulation is that it has a simple positive expression:
\alpha\in\Gamma
is indivisible if and only if
nx=\alpha
with
n\in\mathbb{N}
and

and
x=\alpha
.

Given an additive monoid
\Gamma

denote the

of all polynomials in

\Gamma
and coefficients in

(for a more careful definition and other essential properties of monoid rings, see
[
5

X^{\pi}-1\in F[X;\Gamma]
. If
\pi=n\alpha

and
\alpha\in\Gamma
, note that we have the factorization

X^{\pi}-1=(X^{\alpha}-1)\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}X^{j\alpha}.

(1)

If
\Gamma
is torsion-free and

(
\alpha+\beta=\alpha+\gamma

\beta=\gamma
),

implies that neither factor on the right-hand side of (

, which shows that

is reducible (not a unit and not irreducible) in

. Equivalently, if

is irreducible in

, then

must be indivisible. We are interested in conditions under which the converse holds. Specifically, we say that a commutative, torsion-free, cancellative monoid

provided that for every field

and every indivisible

is irreducible in

. A somewhat weaker condition is that this condition hold for all fields

of a given characteristic

, and in this case we say that
\Gamma
is a

.

Matsuda
[
14
]
showed that every torsion free abelian group is a Matsuda monoid. And according to
[

, Theorem 4.1]
, every submonoid of
\mathbb{Q}_{0}^{+}
is a Matsuda monoid of type 0. In positive characteristic, however, much less is known. If

M=\langle 2,3\rangle=\mathbb{N}_{0}\setminus\{1\}

is the submonoid of
\mathbb{N}_{0}
generated by
2
and

, the indivisible elements of

are precisely the prime numbers.

a Matsuda monoid of type 2 or 3. This was observed in

]
: one has

X^{7}-1=(X^{3}+X^{2}+1)(X^{4}+X^{3}+X^{2}+1)

\mathbb{F}_{3}[X;M]
. Although easily verified, these factorizations appear somewhat mysterious. As we will see in Section

For an arbitrary positive (prime) characteristic

Theorem 1
.

Let

, where

\prod_{\ell\text{ prime}}\left(1-\frac{1}{\ell(\ell-1)}\right).

The techniques used to study

and

consisting of those members of

for which

has index 2 in

. Specifically, we will prove:

Theorem 2

Let

be an odd rational prime, and assume that for every odd squarefree

Taken together these results imply:

Corollary 1

Reversal and Traces

Fix a prime
p
. It will be convenient to view
\mathbb{F}_{p}[X;M]
as a subring of
\mathbb{F}_{p}[X]
. Given
g\in\mathbb{F}_{p}[X]
we write

g(X)=\sum_{j=0}^{m}a_{j}X^{j}

with
m=\deg g
, so that
a_{m}\neq 0
. Define the
reversal operator

R:\mathbb{F}_{p}[X]\to\mathbb{F}_{p}[X]
by

R(g)(X)=\sum_{j=0}^{m}a_{m-j}X^{j}.

Note that
\deg(R(g))=\deg g
if and only if
X\nmid g
, and in this case
R^{2}(g)=g
. We also have

R(g)(X)=X^{\deg g}g\left(\frac{1}{X}\right),

which implies that
R
is multiplicative.

If
m=\deg g\geq 1
, we define the
trace
of
g
to be

\operatorname{Tr}(g)=-\frac{a_{m-1}}{a_{m}}.

The reader can easily verify that for any nonconstant
g,h\in\mathbb{F}_{p}[X]
and any
a\in\mathbb{F}_{p}^{\times}
one has:

\displaystyle\operatorname{Tr}(ag)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Tr}(g);

(2)

\displaystyle\operatorname{Tr}(gh)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Tr}(g)+\operatorname{Tr}(h).

(3)

Properties (
2
) and (
3
) suggest that we define
\operatorname{Tr}(a)=0
for any
a\in\mathbb{F}_{p}^{\times}
, but we prefer to leave the trace of a constant polynomial undefined. The field theoretic trace is connected to the polynomial trace
\operatorname{Tr}(g)
as follows. If
g
is irreducible in
\mathbb{F}_{p}[X]
and
\alpha
is any root of
g
in an extension of
\mathbb{F}_{p}
, then

\operatorname{Tr}(g)=\operatorname{Tr}_{\mathbb{F}_{p}[\alpha]/\mathbb{F}_{p}}(\alpha).

(4)

If
\deg g=f
, then
\mathbb{F}_{p}[\alpha]/\mathbb{F}_{p}
is a Galois extension of degree
f
whose Galois group is generated by the
Frobenius automorphism

\alpha\mapsto\alpha^{p}
. By (
4
) we therefore have

\operatorname{Tr}(g)=T_{p,f}(\alpha),

(5)

where

T_{p,f}(X)=\sum_{j=0}^{f-1}X^{p^{j}}\in\mathbb{F}_{p}[X].

We will say that
g\in\mathbb{F}_{p}[X]
is
traceless
if
\operatorname{Tr}(g)=0
. By definition a traceless polynomial must have positive degree. Let

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{1}

\displaystyle=\{g\in\mathbb{F}_{p}[X;M]\,:\,g\text{ is nonconstant and }X\nmid g\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{2}

\displaystyle=\{g\in\mathbb{F}_{p}[X]:g\text{ is traceless and }X\nmid g\}.

It is easy to see that
R:\mathcal{F}_{1}\to\mathcal{F}_{2}
is an involution. For any prime
q
we have

R(X^{q}-1)=1-X^{q}=-(X^{q}-1).

Because
X\nmid X^{q}-1
and
R
is multiplicative, it follows that
X^{q}-1
factors in
\mathcal{F}_{1}
if and only if it factors in
\mathcal{F}_{2}
. This proves:

Lemma 1
.

For any primes
p
and
q
,
X^{q}-1
is reducible in
\mathbb{F}_{p}[X;M]
if and only if it factors as the product of traceless polynomials in
\mathbb{F}_{p}[X]
.

Because
X^{p}-1=(X-1)^{p}
in
\mathbb{F}_{p}[X]
, the nontrivial (monic) factors of
X^{p}-1
in
\mathbb{F}_{p}[X]
all have the form
(X-1)^{k}
for some
1\leq k<p
. Since (
3
) implies
\operatorname{Tr}((X-1)^{k})=k\operatorname{Tr}(X-1)=k
, we find that
X^{p}-1

is not
the product of traceless polynomials in
\mathbb{F}_{p}[X]
. Therefore
X^{p}-1
is irreducible in
\mathbb{F}_{p}[X;M]
by Lemma
1
, so that
p\not\in E(p)
. Similarly,
2\not\in E(p)
for any
p
. Hence, when trying to determine whether or not
q\in E(p)
, we may assume that
q
is odd and different from
p
.

Let

\Phi_{q}(X)=\sum_{j=0}^{q-1}X^{j}

denote the
q
th cyclotomic polynomial, which can be considered as a member of
\mathbb{F}_{p}[X]
for any
p
. Suppose
\Phi_{q}=gh
in
\mathbb{F}_{p}[X]
with
g
traceless. Multiplying by
X-1
we obtain
X^{q}-1=g\widetilde{h}
. Because
X^{q}-1
and
g
are both traceless, so is
\widetilde{h}
by (
3
). Applying Lemma 1 we conclude that if
\Phi_{q}
has a traceless factor in
\mathbb{F}_{q}[X]
, then
X^{q}-1
factors nontrivially in
\mathbb{F}_{q}[X;M]
. Conversely, if
X^{q}-1
is reducible in
\mathbb{F}_{p}[X;M]
, then by Lemma
1
we can write
X^{q}-1=gh
with
g,h\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[X]
traceless. Because
X-1
is prime in
\mathbb{F}_{p}[X]
and divides
X^{q}-1
, without loss of generality we have
h=(X-1)\tilde{h}
for some
\tilde{h}\in\mathbb{F}_{p}[X]
. Cancelling the common factor of
X-1
we obtain
\Phi_{q}=g\tilde{h}
with
g
traceless. This proves:

Lemma 2
.

For any primes
p\neq q
,
X^{q}-1
is reducible in
\mathbb{F}_{p}[X;M]
if and only if
\Phi_{q}
has a traceless factor in
\mathbb{F}_{p}[X]
.

Write
\Phi_{q}=g_{1}\cdots g_{r}
with
g_{j}\in\mathbb{F}_{p}[X]
irreducible. In order for
\Phi_{q}
to have a traceless factor in
\mathbb{F}_{p}[X]
, it is necessary and sufficient that there exist a nonempty
J\subset\{1,\ldots,r\}
so that
\prod_{j\in J}g_{j}
is traceless. It will therefore be useful to recall a few basic facts about the irreducible factorization of
\Phi_{q}
in
\mathbb{F}_{p}[X]
. For
q\neq p
, let
f(p,q)
denote the order of the subgroup
\langle p\rangle
generated by the image of
p
in
(\mathbb{Z}/q\mathbb{Z})^{\times}
. Then it is well known that
\Phi_{q}
has exactly
r(p,q)=\frac{q-1}{f}=[(\mathbb{Z}/q\mathbb{Z})^{\times}:\langle p\rangle]
distinct irreducible factors in
\mathbb{F}_{p}[X]
, each of multiplicity 1 and degree
f
. It follows that if
\zeta
is a root of any
g_{j}
in an extension of
\mathbb{F}_{p}
, then
[\mathbb{F}_{p}[\zeta]:\mathbb{F}_{p}]=f
. The extension
\mathbb{F}_{p}[\zeta]/\mathbb{F}_{p}
is necessarily Galois and its Galois group is generated by the
Frobenius automorphism

\alpha\mapsto\alpha^{p}
. By equation (
5
) we therefore have

\operatorname{Tr}(g_{j})=T_{p,f}(\zeta).

(6)

The Densities of
E(2)
and
E(3)

Because the fields
\mathbb{F}_{2}
and
\mathbb{F}_{3}
are particularly small, we can give an alternate characterization of the sets
E(2)
and
E(3)
which will make it possible to compute their GRH-conditional densities using Artin’s (original) primitive root conjecture and work of Hooley
[
9
]
. For
a\in\mathbb{N}
let
\mathcal{S}(a)
denote the set of primes for which
a
is a primitive root. We will prove:

Lemma 3
.

We have

E(2)=\mathcal{P}-\mathcal{S}(2)\,\,\,\text{ and }\,\,\,E(3)=\mathcal{P}-\mathcal{S}(3).

In other words,
E(2)
consists of those primes for which 2
fails
to be a primitive root, and likewise for
E(3)
. According to Artin’s primitive root conjecture, for every prime
p\not\equiv 1\pmod{4}
one expects to have
d(\mathcal{S}(p))=A
, where

A=\prod_{\ell\text{ prime}}\left(1-\frac{1}{\ell(\ell-1)}\right)=0.3739\ldots

is
Artin’s constant
. This would mean that

d(E(2))=d(E(3))=1-A=0.6260\ldots,

(7)

a conclusion which is in close agreement with the computationally obtained results

\frac{\#E(2)\cap[1,10^{6}]}{\#\mathcal{P}\cap[1,10^{6}]}=0.6262\ldots\,\,\,\text{ and }\,\,\,\frac{\#E(3)\cap[1,10^{6}]}{\#\mathcal{P}\cap[1,10^{6}]}=0.6255\ldots

Hooley
[
9
]
showed that when
a\neq\pm 1,\square
, Artin’s conjecture for
\mathcal{S}(a)
is true, provided one assumes that the zeta functions of certain Kummerian extensions of
\mathbb{Q}
obey GRH. A careful reading of
[
9
]
shows that when
a=p
, it is sufficient to assume GRH only for the zeta functions of the fields
\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt[n]{1},\sqrt[n]{p})
, where
n\in\mathbb{N}
is squarefree. It is now a simple matter to deduce Theorem

.

Proof of Theorem
1
.

In view of Lemma
3
and the remarks above, it suffices only to observe that
A<1
.
∎

To prove Lemma
3
, we begin with a simple observation. If
q
is prime and
\Phi_{q}=gh
with
g,h\in\mathbb{F}_{2}[X]
of positive degree, then without loss of generality
g
must be traceless. This is because we have

1=-1=\operatorname{Tr}(\Phi_{q})=\operatorname{Tr}(g)+\operatorname{Tr}(h)

by (
3
), and we are working in
\mathbb{F}_{2}
. This means that
\Phi_{q}
has a traceless factor in
\mathbb{F}_{2}[X]
if and only if
\Phi_{q}
is reducible in
\mathbb{F}_{2}[X]
. Since the number of irreducible factors of
\Phi_{q}
is precisely
\frac{q-1}{f}
, the latter occurs if and only if

. That is,
\Phi_{q}
is reducible in
\mathbb{F}_{2}[X]
if and only if
2
is

q
. Thus, by Lemma

,

E(2)=\mathcal{P}-\mathcal{S}(2).

In order to handle
E(3)
we introduce another lemma.

Lemma 4
.

Let
p\neq q
be primes and let
a\in\mathbb{F}_{p}
. Then every irreducible factor of
\Phi_{q}
in
\mathbb{F}_{p}[X]
has trace equal to

if and only if
\Phi_{q}
is irreducible in
\mathbb{F}_{p}[X]
and

.

Proof.

Since
\operatorname{Tr}(\Phi_{q})=-1
, just the “only if” implication requires proof. As above, write
\Phi_{q}=g_{1}\cdots g_{r}
with each

irreducible of degree

and

. Suppose that
\operatorname{Tr}(g_{j})=a
for all

. If
\zeta\neq 1
is a primitive

\mathbb{F}_{p}
, then
\Phi_{q}(\zeta)=0
, which implies
g_{j}(\zeta)=0
for some

. Then by (

where
\overline{T_{p,f}}\in\mathbb{F}_{p}[X]
is the polynomial obtained by replacing each exponent in

with its least positive residue modulo

. Therefore,

is a root of

, and hence

\overline{T_{p,f}}-a

\mathbb{F}_{p}[X]
. Because

was arbitrary and the

\Phi_{q}

\overline{T_{p,f}}-a

. But

Comparing the monomials that appear on each side of this equality, we see that the powers of

. That is,

and

is irreducible in

. This completes the proof.

Because the second factor is traceless, we multiply both sides by
X-1
, absorb it into the first factor and obtain

X^{7}-1=(X^{4}+X^{2}+X+1)(X^{3}+X+1).

Finally, reversing coefficients (there’s no need to “negate” since
1=-1
in
\mathbb{F}_{2}
) we have

X^{7}-1=(X^{3}+X^{2}+1)(X^{4}+X^{3}+X^{2}+1)

(9)

in
\mathbb{F}_{2}[X;M]
, which is precisely the factorization appearing in
[
1
]
. Since
f(2,7)=3
and
r(2,7)=2
, the factors in (
9
) are irreducible in
\mathbb{F}_{2}[X]
. Because only one of these factors is traceless, it follows that (
9
) is the
only
factorization of
X^{7}-1
in
\mathbb{F}_{2}[X;M]
(up to the order in which the factors are written). This demystifies the appearance of (
9
) in
[
1
]
: 7 is simply the smallest prime for which 2 is not a primitive root, and there is only one traceless factorization of
X^{7}-1
in
\mathbb{F}_{2}[X]
.

When
p=3
and
q=11
we have
f=5
and
r=2
. Factoring
\Phi_{11}
over
\mathbb{F}_{3}
yields

\Phi_{11}(X)=(X^{5}+X^{4}-X^{3}+X^{2}-1)(X^{5}-X^{3}+X^{2}-X-1).

The second factor is again traceless, so we multiply by
X-1
and absorb it into the first factor:

X^{11}-1=(X^{6}+X^{4}-X^{3}-X^{2}-X+1)(X^{5}-X^{3}+X^{2}-X-1)

in
\mathbb{F}_{3}[X]
. Reversing coefficients and negating we have

X^{11}-1=(X^{6}-X^{5}-X^{4}-X^{3}+X^{2}+1)(X^{5}+X^{4}-X^{3}+X^{2}-1)

in
\mathbb{F}_{3}[X;M]
. As in the preceding example, 11 is the smallest prime for which 3 fails to be a primitive root, and this is essentially the only factorization of
X^{11}-1
in
\mathbb{F}_{3}[X;M]
possible, which provides an explanation for its appearance in
[
1
]
.

The smallest
q
for which none of the irreducible factors of
\Phi_{q}
in
\mathbb{F}_{3}[X]
is traceless is
q=67
. Here
f=22
,
r=3
and
\Phi_{67}=g_{1}g_{2}g_{3}
in
\mathbb{F}_{3}[X]
with
\operatorname{Tr}(g_{1})=\operatorname{Tr}(g_{2})=-1
and
\operatorname{Tr}(g_{3})=1
(the interested reader can easily compute
g_{1},g_{2}
and
g_{3}
explicitly with the help of her favorite computer algebra software). Following the proof of Lemma
3
, we see that
g_{2}g_{3}
is a traceless factor of
\Phi_{67}
over
\mathbb{F}_{3}
. Multiplying by
(X-1)
, reversing coefficients and negating, we see that

X^{67}-1=((X-1)R(g_{1}))R(g_{2}g_{3})

(10)

is a factorization of
X^{67}-1
in
\mathbb{F}_{3}[X;M]
. Because
X-1
,
g_{1}
,
g_{2}
and
g_{3}
are irreducible in
\mathbb{F}_{3}[X]
but not traceless, (
10
) is a factorization into irreducibles in
\mathbb{F}_{3}[X;M]
. Pairing
g_{1}
with
g_{3}
instead yields
different
irreducible factorization, which incidentally proves that
\mathbb{F}_{3}[X;M]
is
not
a unique factorization domain.

4
Number Fields

When
p>3
, the condition
f>1
is
not
sufficient to guarantee membership in
E(p)
. For instance,
f(5,11)=5
so that
r(5,11)=2
, but neither of the
2
irreducible factors of
\Phi_{11}
in
\mathbb{F}_{5}[X]
is traceless. So
11\not\in E(5)
by Lemma
2
. We therefore partition
E(p)
according to the value of the index
r
. For
k\in\mathbb{N}
set

E_{k}(p)=\{q\text{ prime}\,|\,q\in E(p)\text{ and }r(p,q)=k\}.

When
r(p,q)=2
, notice that
q\in E(p)
if and only if
\Phi_{q}
has a traceless
irreducible
factor in
\mathbb{F}_{p}[X]
. Regardless of the value of the index, according to (
6
) the latter statement holds if and only if there is a
q
th root of unity
\zeta\neq 1
in an extension of
\mathbb{F}_{p}
which satisfies
T_{p,f}(\zeta)=0
. Because the nontrivial
q
th roots of unity are precisely the roots of
\Phi_{q}
, this can happen if and only if
T_{p,f}
and
\Phi_{q}
have a nontrivial (positive degree) common factor in
\mathbb{F}_{p}[X]
. Together with Lemma
2
this proves:

Lemma 5
.

For any primes
p\neq q
,
\Phi_{q}
has a traceless irreducible factor in
\mathbb{F}_{p}[X]
if and only if
\operatorname{gcd}(\Phi_{q},T_{p,f})\in\mathbb{F}_{p}[X]
is nontrivial. In this case
X^{q}-1
is reducible in
\mathbb{F}_{p}[X;M]
.

Let
G_{p,q}=\operatorname{gcd}(\Phi_{q},T_{p,f})\in\mathbb{F}_{p}[X]
. We now see that

E_{2}(p)=\{q\text{ prime}\,|\,r(p,q)=2\text{ and }\deg G_{p,q}>0\}.

When
p
is odd and
r(p,q)\geq 3
, it is
not
generally the case that
q\in E(p)
implies
\deg G_{p,q}>0
, as our example with
p=3
and
q=67
demonstrates. It is interesting to note that when
p=2
, however, we have:

Corollary 2
.

Let
q
be an odd prime. Then
X^{q}-1
is reducible in
\mathbb{F}_{2}[X;M]
if and only if
\gcd(\Phi_{q},T_{2,f})\in\mathbb{F}_{2}[X]
is nontrivial.

Proof.

Because of Lemma
5
, it suffices to prove the “only if” implication. Suppose
X^{q}-1
is reducible in
\mathbb{F}_{2}[X;M]
. Then
\Phi_{q}
is reducible in
\mathbb{F}_{2}[X]
by Lemma
2
, and Lemma
4
implies that
\operatorname{Tr}(g)\neq 1
for some irreducible factor
g\in\mathbb{F}_{2}[X]
of
\Phi_{q}
. Because the only other possible value for the trace is 0, the conclusion now follows from Lemma
5
.
∎

Our goal now is to find an alternate characterization of the condition
\deg G_{p,q}>0
in terms of algebraic number theory. We assume throughout that
p
is odd. Although we have already considered the case
p=3
, there is no disadvantage to including it here. The condition that
\Phi_{q}
and
T_{p,f}
have a nontrivial common factor over
\mathbb{F}_{p}
is equivalent to the statement that
T_{p,f}
fails to be a unit in the quotient ring
\mathbb{F}_{p}[X]/(\Phi_{q})
. Let
\zeta_{q}\in\mathbb{C}
denote a primitive
q
th root of unity and set
K_{q}=\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{q})
. Let
\mathcal{O}_{q}=\mathbb{Z}[\zeta_{q}]
denote the ring of integers in
K_{q}
. Because
\Phi_{q}
is the minimal polynomial of
\zeta_{q}
over
\mathbb{Q}
, we have natural isomorphisms

\mathbb{F}_{p}[X]/(\Phi_{q})\cong\left((\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z})[X]\right)/(\Phi_{q})\cong\mathbb{Z}[X]/(p,\Phi_{q})\cong\left(\mathbb{Z}[X]/(\Phi_{q})\right)/(p)\cong\mathcal{O}_{q}/(p),

under which (the coset of)
X
corresponds to (the coset of)
\zeta_{q}
. We immediately find that
T_{p,f}
is not a unit in
\mathbb{F}_{p}[X]/(\Phi_{q})
if and only if
\alpha_{q}=T_{p,f}(\zeta_{q})
is divisible by one of the primes of
\mathcal{O}_{q}
lying over
p
. This occurs if and only if
p
divides the (ideal) norm
\mathcal{N}_{q}(\alpha_{q}\mathcal{O}_{q})=\left|\mathcal{O}_{q}/\alpha_{q}\mathcal{O}_{q}\right|
.

We can go a bit further if we introduce some Galois theory. The extension
K_{q}/\mathbb{Q}
is Galois with group isomorphic to
(\mathbb{Z}/q\mathbb{Z})^{\times}
. Explicitly, an element
a\in(\mathbb{Z}/q\mathbb{Z})^{\times}
corresponds to the automorphism induced by
\zeta_{q}\mapsto\zeta_{q}^{a}
. If
a_{1},\ldots,a_{r}
are coset representatives for
\langle p\rangle
in
(\mathbb{Z}/q\mathbb{Z})^{\times}
, where
r=\frac{q-1}{f}
, then the
T_{p,f}(\zeta_{q}^{a_{i}})
are the distinct conjugates of
\alpha_{q}
over
\mathbb{Q}
. It follows that

m_{q}(X)=\prod_{i=1}^{r}(X-T_{p,f}(\zeta_{q}^{a_{i}}))\in\mathbb{Z}[X]

is the minimal polynomial for
\alpha_{q}
over
\mathbb{Q}
(the coefficients are integral because
\alpha_{q}
is an algebraic integer). Up to a sign, the constant coefficient of
m_{q}(X)
is equal to the
(algebraic) norm

N_{q}(\alpha_{q})=N_{\mathbb{Q}(\alpha_{q})/\mathbb{Q}}(\alpha_{q})=\prod_{i=1}^{r}T_{p,f}(\zeta_{q}^{a_{i}}).

Because
[K_{q}:\mathbb{Q}(\alpha_{q})]=\frac{q-1}{r}=f
, the two notions of norm are related by the equation

\mathcal{N}_{q}(\alpha_{q}\mathcal{O}_{q})=N_{q}(\alpha_{q})^{f}.

This means that
p
divides
\mathcal{N}_{q}(\alpha_{q}\mathcal{O}_{q})
if and only if
p
divides
N_{q}(\alpha_{q})
. The preceding paragraph and Lemma
5
now yield:

Lemma 6
.

For any odd primes
p\neq q
,
\deg G_{p,q}>0
if and only if
p|N_{q}(\alpha_{q})
.

Lemmas
2
,
5
and
6
yield

E_{2}(p)=\{q\text{ prime}\,|\,r(p,q)=2\text{ and }p|N_{q}(\alpha_{q})\}.

If
q\in E_{2}(p)
, then
\alpha_{q}
has a single distinct Galois conjugate
\beta_{q}
and

\alpha_{q}+\beta_{q}=\sum_{a\in(\mathbb{Z}/q\mathbb{Z})^{\times}}\zeta_{q}^{a}=-1,

(11)

since
\Phi_{q}(\zeta_{q})=0
. Equation (
11
) has two important consequences. Because
\mathcal{O}_{q}=\mathbb{Z}[\zeta_{q}]
and
\mathbb{Q}(\alpha_{q})=K_{q}^{\langle p\rangle}
, it is not hard to show that the ring of integers in
\mathbb{Q}(\alpha_{q})=\mathbb{Q}(\alpha_{q},\beta_{q})
is
\mathbb{Z}[\alpha_{q},\beta_{q}]=\mathbb{Z}[\alpha_{q}]
, by (
11
). This means that
\operatorname{disc}(\mathbb{Q}(\alpha_{q}))=\operatorname{disc}(\alpha_{q})
. Furthermore, the minimal polynomial for
\alpha_{q}
over
\mathbb{Q}
is

m_{q}(X)=(X-\alpha_{q})(X-\beta_{q})=X^{2}+X+N_{q}(\alpha_{q}),

which means that

\operatorname{disc}(\alpha_{q})=\operatorname{disc}(X^{2}+X+N_{q}(\alpha_{q}))=1-4N_{q}(\alpha_{q}).

For odd
q
, the unique quadratic subfield of
K_{q}
is known to be
\mathbb{Q}\left(\sqrt{q^{\ast}}\right)
, where
q^{\ast}=(-1)^{\frac{q-1}{2}}q
. Since
\mathbb{Q}(\alpha_{q})
also has degree 2 over
\mathbb{Q}
, we must have
\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{q^{\ast}})=\mathbb{Q}(\alpha_{q})
. In particular

q^{\ast}=\operatorname{disc}\mathbb{Q}\left(\sqrt{q^{\ast}}\right)=\operatorname{disc}\mathbb{Q}(\alpha_{q})=\operatorname{disc}(\alpha_{q})=1-4N_{q}(\alpha_{q}).

Therefore
p|N_{q}(\alpha_{q})
if and only if
q^{\ast}\equiv 1\pmod{4p}
. Since
p
is odd this congruence is equivalent to the system
q^{\ast}\equiv 1\pmod{4}
and
q^{\ast}\equiv 1\pmod{p}
, by the Chinese remainder theorem. The condition
q^{\ast}\equiv 1\pmod{4}
is automatic, while
q^{\ast}\equiv 1\pmod{p}
is equivalent to

q\equiv\pm 1\!\!\!\pmod{4p}.

(12)

We have therefore proven:

Lemma 7
.

For any odd prime
p
,

E_{2}(p)=\left\{q\text{ prime}\,\Big|\,q\equiv\pm 1\!\!\!\pmod{4p},f=\frac{q-1}{2}\right\}.

The condition
\frac{q-1}{f}=2
implies that
\left(\frac{p}{q}\right)=1
, and one can use quadratic reciprocity to show that this implies
q\equiv\pm 1\pmod{4p}
when
p=2,3
. The expression for
E_{2}(p)
given in Lemma
7
is therefore valid for
all
primes
p
.

5
E_{2}(p)
and a generalized Artin conjecture

We continue to assume that
p
is odd. The set
E_{2}(p)
falls into a wider class of sets of primes whose densities were investigated by Lenstra in
[
12
]
. Such sets arose in the work of several authors
[
3
,
20
,
21
,
22
,
25
]
in the 1970s, in connection with certain properties of euclidean rings in global fields. Although these sets of primes (places) can be constructed in an arbitrary global field
K
, for our purposes it is sufficient to simply take
K=\mathbb{Q}
.

Given a prime number
q
and an integer
n
, let
\nu_{q}^{\times}:\mathbb{Q}\to\mathbb{Z}
denote the
q
-adic valuation (with
\nu_{q}(0)=\infty
), and let

R_{q}=\{a\in\mathbb{Q}\,|\,\nu_{q}(a)\geq 0\}\,\,\,\text{ and }\,\,\,R_{q}^{\times}=\{a\in\mathbb{Q}\,|\,\nu_{q}(a)=0\}

be the valuation ring and unit group at
q
, respectively. There is a natural surjection
\psi:R_{q}^{\times}\to(\mathbb{Z}/q\mathbb{Z})^{\times}
obtained by composing reduction modulo
qR_{q}
with the isomorphism
R_{q}/qR_{q}\cong\mathbb{Z}/q\mathbb{Z}
. For a subgroup
W\subseteq R_{q}^{\times}
, we let
r_{q}(W)
denote the index of
\psi(W)
in
(\mathbb{Z}/q\mathbb{Z})^{\times}
.

If
F
is a finite Galois extension of
\mathbb{Q}
,
C\subseteq G(F/\mathbb{Q})
is a union of conjugacy classes,
W\subset\mathbb{Q}^{\times}
is a finitely generated subgroup with positive rank, and
k
is a positive integer, let
\mathcal{S}(F,C,W,k)
denote the set of primes
q
so that

\displaystyle\left(\frac{F/\mathbb{Q}}{q}\right)\subseteq C,\,\,\,\,W\subseteq R_{q}^{\times},\,\,\,\,\text{and}\,\,\,\,r_{q}(W)|k,

where
\left(\frac{F/\mathbb{Q}}{q}\right)
is the Frobenius symbol. The density of
\mathcal{S}(F,C,W,k)
is the subject of the following conjecture, which is a combination of
[
12
, Conjecture (2.3)]
and
[
12
, (2.15)]
.

Conjecture 1

(Lenstra)
.

Under the hypotheses stated above, the natural density of
\mathcal{S}(F,C,W,k)
exists and is equal to

\delta(F,C,W,k)=\sum_{n}\frac{\mu(n)c(n)}{[F\cdot L_{n}:\mathbb{Q}]},

(13)

where for squarefree
n
,
L_{n}=\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{q(n)},W^{1/q(n)})
with
q(n)=\prod_{\ell|n}\ell^{\nu_{\ell}(k)+1}
, and
c(n)=\#(C\cap G(F/F\cap L_{n}))
.

Because
\mathcal{S}(a)=\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{Q},\{1\},\langle a\rangle,1)
, Conjecture
1
subsumes Artin’s primitive root conjecture, and is therefore commonly referred to as a
generalized Artin conjecture
. Various other generalizations exist; see for example
[
2
,
4
,
19
,
23
,
24
]
. We remark that because Lenstra
[
12
]
works with sets of primes in an arbitrary global field, his results are all actually stated in terms of
Dirichlet
density. However, if we are only working with primes in a number field, then we are free to use natural density in place of Dirichlet density (see
[
12
, p. 203]
).

Set
F=\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{4p})
. Identify
G(F/\mathbb{Q})
with
(\mathbb{Z}/4p\mathbb{Z})^{\times}
in the usual manner, mapping
a\in(\mathbb{Z}/q\mathbb{Z})^{\times}
to the automorphism
\sigma_{a}
defined by the rule
\zeta_{4p}\mapsto\zeta_{4p}^{a}
. We claim that

E_{2}(p)=\mathcal{S}(F,\{\pm 1\},\langle p\rangle,2).

(14)

Since
\left(\frac{F/\mathbb{Q}}{q}\right)=\{q\}
and
r_{q}(\langle p\rangle)=\frac{q-1}{f}
, the only question is why membership in
\mathcal{S}(F,\{\pm 1\},\langle p\rangle,2)
guarantees that
\frac{q-1}{f}=2
. To that end, let
q\in\mathcal{S}(F,\{\pm 1\},\langle p\rangle,2)
. Since
p
is odd this implies
q^{\ast}\equiv 1\pmod{p}
, and quadratic reciprocity then gives

\left(\frac{p}{q}\right)=\left(\frac{q^{\ast}}{p}\right)=1.

We conclude that
\langle p\rangle
is a subgroup of the group of squares in
(\mathbb{Z}/q\mathbb{Z})^{\times}
. Because the squares have index 2 in
(\mathbb{Z}/q\mathbb{Z})^{\times}
, this implies
\frac{q-1}{f}
is divisible by 2. But we also have that
r_{q}(\langle p\rangle)
divides 2. Since
r_{q}(\langle p\rangle)=\frac{q-1}{f}
, we are finished.

Let
a(p)=\delta(\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{4p}),\{\pm 1\},\langle p\rangle,2)
. We then have:

Lemma 8
.

If Conjecture
1
is true, then for any odd prime the set
E_{2}(p)
has natural density
a(p)
.

We will return to the truth of Conjecture
1
following the next section.

6
Nonvanishing of
a(p)

Note that Conjecture
1
only asserts that the density of
\mathcal{S}(F,C,W,k)

exists
. Whether or not
\delta(F,C,W,k)
is
nonzero
must be addressed separately. The following result allows us to answer this question without explicitly computing
\delta(F,C,W,k)
.

Lemma 9

(
[
12
, Theorem (4.1)]
)
.

Let
h
be the product of the primes
\ell
so that
W\subseteq(\mathbb{Q}^{\times})^{q(\ell)}
. Then
\delta(F,C,W,k)\neq 0
if and only if there is a
\sigma\in G(F(\zeta_{h})/\mathbb{Q})
so that

\displaystyle\sigma|F\in C,

(15)

\displaystyle\sigma|L_{\ell}\neq\operatorname{id}\text{ whenever }L_{\ell}\subset F(\zeta_{h}).

(16)

Because it is a potential source of confusion, we point out that the variable
p
appearing in the statement of the original
[
12
, Theorem (4.1)]
represents the characteristic of the global field in question, which in our case is simply 0. It is not “our”
p
.

Lemma 10
.

For any odd prime
p
one has
a(p)\neq 0
.

Proof.

Taking
F=\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{4p})
,
C=\{\pm 1\}
,
W=\langle p\rangle
and
k=2
in Lemma
9
, we have
h=1
, since
p
is not an
m
th power in
\mathbb{Q}
for any
m\geq 2
. So
F(\zeta_{h})=F
and from (
15
) and (
16
) we find that
a(p)\neq 0
if and only if there is a
\sigma\in\{\pm 1\}
so that

\sigma|L_{\ell}\neq\operatorname{id}\text{ whenever }L_{\ell}\subseteq F.

(17)

However,
L_{\ell}
contains the subextension
\mathbb{Q}(p^{1/\ell})
, which fails to be Galois over
\mathbb{Q}
. Since
F/\mathbb{Q}
is abelian, this implies
L_{\ell}
is
never
contained in
F
. So (
17
) is vacuously satisfied for any
\sigma\in G(F/\mathbb{Q})
, and hence
a(p)\neq 0
.
∎

We can also show that
a(p)
is nonzero directly, by expressing it as the product of a certain nonzero rational number and Artin’s constant
A
. In
[
24
]
Wagstaff obtained similar expressions for the densities of the sets

\mathcal{S}(a,t)=\{q\text{ prime}\,|\,\langle a\rangle\text{ has index }t\text{ in }(\mathbb{Z}/q\mathbb{Z})^{\times}\},

to which Conjecture
1
also applies (see
[
12
, p. 216]
). When
a>0
or
a\neq\pm\square
, Wagstaff was also able to give an Euler product expansion for
d(\mathcal{S}(a,t))
. Later Moree
[
17
]
extended Wagstaff’s results to obtain Euler products for every
d(\mathcal{S}(a,t))
. However
E_{2}(p)\neq\mathcal{S}(p,2)
when
p\geq 5
, since in
\mathcal{S}(p,2)
one has no control over the residue classes of its members. So the results of Moree and Wagstaff can’t help us compute
a(p)
. Instead, we find a closed form expression for the series (
13
) defining
a(p)=\delta(\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{4p}),\{\pm 1\},\langle p\rangle,2)
.

Lemma 11
.

For any odd prime
p
,

a(p)=\frac{3(2p-1)}{4(p^{2}-p-1)}A,

(18)

where
A
is Artin’s constant. In particular
a(p)\neq 0
.

Proof.

With
F=\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{4p})
,
C=\{\pm 1\}
,
W=\langle p\rangle
and
k=2
in Section 6, we have

L_{n}=\begin{cases}\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{n},p^{1/n})&\text{ if }2\nmid n,\\
\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{2n},p^{1/2n})&\text{ otherwise,}\end{cases}

so that

c(n)=\begin{cases}2&\text{ if }F\cap L_{n}\subseteq\mathbb{R},\\
1&\text{ otherwise}\end{cases}=\begin{cases}2&\text{ if }(2p,n)=1,\\
1&\text{ otherwise.}\end{cases}

Taking advantage of
[
11
, Theorem VI.9.4]
we also find that

[F\cdot L_{n}:\mathbb{Q}]=\begin{cases}2n\varphi(n)(p-1)&(p,n)=1,\\[2.0pt]
2n\varphi(n)&p|n\text{ and }2\nmid n,\\[2.0pt]
4n\varphi(n)&2p|n.\end{cases}

It then follows from (
13
) that

a(p)=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{p|n\atop 2\nmid n}\frac{\mu(n)}{n\varphi(n)}+\frac{1}{4}\sum_{2p|n}\frac{\mu(n)}{n\varphi(n)}+\frac{1}{2(p-1)}\sum_{p\nmid n\atop 2|n}\frac{\mu(n)}{n\varphi(n)}+\frac{1}{p-1}\sum_{(2p,n)=1}\frac{\mu(n)}{n\varphi(n)}.

Because
\mu
is multiplicative,

\sum_{(2p,n)=1}\frac{\mu(n)}{n\varphi(n)}=\prod_{\ell\text{ prime}\atop\ell\neq 2,p}\left(1-\frac{1}{\ell(\ell-1)}\right)=2\frac{p(p-1)}{p^{2}-p-1}A.

Therefore

\frac{1}{2}\sum_{p|n\atop 2\nmid n}\frac{\mu(n)}{n\varphi(n)}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{(2p,m)=1}\frac{\mu(mp)}{m\,p\,\varphi(mp)}=-\frac{1}{p(p-1)}\frac{p(p-1)}{p^{2}-p-1}A=-\frac{1}{p^{2}-p-1}A.

Likewise

\frac{1}{4}\sum_{2p|n}\frac{\mu(n)}{n\varphi(n)}=\frac{1}{4}\sum_{(2p,m)=1}\frac{\mu(2pm)}{2pm\varphi(2pm)}=\frac{1}{4p(p-1)}\frac{p(p-1)}{p^{2}-p-1}A=\frac{1}{4(p^{2}-p-1)}A.

Finally,

\frac{1}{2(p-1)}\sum_{p\nmid n\atop 2|n}\frac{\mu(n)}{n\varphi(n)}=\frac{1}{2(p-1)}\sum_{(2p,m)=1}\frac{\mu(2m)}{2m\varphi(2m)}=-\frac{1}{2(p-1)}\frac{p(p-1)}{p^{2}-p-1}A=-\frac{p}{2(p^{2}-p-1)}A.

Formula (
18
) follows immedately.
∎

Proof of Theorem
2
.

According to
[
12
, Theorem (3.1)]
, the generalized Artin Conjecture
1
is true under the assumption of the generalized Riemann hypothesis for the Dedekind zeta functions of the number fields
L_{n}
. And in this case Lemma
11
implies that for any odd prime
p
one has

d(E_{2}(p))=a(p)=\frac{3(2p-1)}{4(p^{2}-p-1)}A\neq 0.

∎

For
p>3
, determining membership in
E(p)
in general appears to be somewhat challenging. The condition
\deg\gcd(\Phi_{q},T_{p,q})>0
(remember that the GCD is being computed over
\mathbb{F}_{p}
) is easy enough to test with a computer algebra system (once one recognizes that
\gcd(\Phi_{q},T_{p,q})=\gcd(\Phi_{q},\overline{T_{p,q}})
). But as we have seen, this will only detect those
q\in E(p)
for which
\Phi_{q}
has a traceless
irreducible
factor, and these
q
comprise a proper subset
E^{\prime}(p)
of the set
E(p)
in which we are we are interested. It would be interesting to know the precise extent to which
E^{\prime}(p)
and
E(p)
differ.

To that end, for
r\geq 2
let

I_{r}(p)=\{q\text{ prime }\,|\,[(\mathbb{Z}/q\mathbb{Z})^{\times}:\langle p\rangle]=r\},

so that
E_{r}(p)\subseteq I_{r}(p)
. A simple combinatorial argument shows that for any fixed
p
,
I_{r}(p)=E_{r}(p)
for all sufficiently large
r
. So the set

F(p)=\{r\geq 2\,|\,E_{r}(p)\subsetneq I_{r}(p)\}

is finite and by our results contains
r=2
for
p\geq 5
. Since

E(p)=\left(\bigcup_{r\in F(p)}E_{r}(p)\right)\cup\left(\bigcup_{r\not\in F(p)}I_{r}(p)\right),

it would therefore be useful to determine
F(p)
completely, and this seems necessary in order to achieve a more thorough understanding of
E(p)
for
p\geq 5
.

For example, one can show (using techniques generalizing those in Section
4
) that

I_{3}(5)=E_{3}(5),

proving that
3\not\in F(5)
, and computational evidence suggests that
r\not\in F(5)
for all
r\geq 3
. This would mean that
F(5)=\{2\}
and hence

E(5)=E_{2}(5)\cup\bigcup_{r\geq 3}I_{r}(5),

making computation of the density of
E(5)
amenable to the generalized Artin Conjecture
1
. Our computations have also shown that
3\in F(7)
, and also suggest that
r\not\in F(7)
for
r\geq 4
. This yields the analogous conjectural decomposition

E(7)=E_{2}(7)\cup E_{3}(7)\cup\bigcup_{r\geq 4}I_{4}(7),

which can be used to compute a GRH-conditional value for
d(E(7))
as well, provided one can also find a “nice” description of
E_{3}(7)
, along the lines of that given for
E_{2}(p)
in Lemma
7
. Determining the set
F(p)
for
p\geq 5
, and describing the sets
E_{r}(p)
for
r\in F(p)
, are indeed the subjects of our continuing research.
