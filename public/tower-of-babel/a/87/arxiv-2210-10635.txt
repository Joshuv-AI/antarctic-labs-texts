Nonlinear effects in Thomas precession due to the interplay of Lorentz contraction and Thomas–Wigner rotation
Antonio Di Lorenzo

Abstract
It is demonstrated that the 3–vector 𝑺 \bm{S} currently associated to the spin in an inertial frame does not contract, but rather dilates, in the direction of the velocity. The correct vector 𝑻 \bm{T} is individuated. The equation of motion for the two vectors is shown to contain two terms, a common linear rotation, identified with Thomas precession, and also a nonlinear rotation depending on the direction of the spin itself.



Introduction

Often, particles carry with themselves properties, e.g. charge, mass, magnetic moment, electric dipole moment.
While the motion of a particle
O^{\prime}
is described in a simpler form in an inertial reference frame, the evolution of physical quantities transported along
O^{\prime}
appears simpler when described in the comoving frame, even though it is an accelerated frame. Think, for instance, of a gyroscope attached to a car, which may accelerate, make a curve, brake, etc. Meanwhile, the car is being carried by Earth, which is spinning and rotating around the Sun, which in its turn is rotating around the center of the Galaxy. Even though the interior of the car is not an inertial reference frame, it is much easier to describe the motion of the gyroscope in such noninertial frame, after introducing noninertial forces that one attributes to gravitational fields, rather than describe its motion relative to the fixed stars. However, if from the car you look out of the window, all bodies not being transported along with you — trees, mountains, the Sun, the planets, the stars in the night sky — will appear to follow complicated, inexplicable motions, and there are no masses around compatible with the noninertial effects that you believed to be gravitational. If you want to simplify the description of the outside universe you should eventually switch to an inertial reference frame.
The equations relating the change of the angular momentum of the gyroscope with the applied forces and torques should then be written back in an inertial frame, in which the movement of the origin of the gyroscope can be explained causally, i.e. all accelerations can be associated to other bodies, which are usually limited to nearby bodies due to the short range of all forces except gravity.

In a given reference frame, physical quantities are described by scalars, three–dimensional vectors, and
3\times 3
matrices.
The special theory of relativity, despite its name, allows to describe the equations of physics in an absolute form, by making use of abstract mathematical objects in Minkowski space, which encompasses space and time. Such objects — scalars, four–dimensional vectors, more generally tensors — describe absolute quantities. One can then deduce the physical quantities in any reference frame as representations of these absolute objects.

In our example, the angular momentum of the gyroscope, in the reference frame inside the car, is represented as a 3–vector
\bm{S}^{\prime}
,
which is
a representation
\{0,S^{\prime 1},S^{\prime 2},S^{\prime 3}\}
of the abstract 4–vector
\underline{S}
that is being transported along the car. In another reference frame, the same 4–vector is represented differently, as
\{S^{0},S^{1},S^{2},S^{3}\}
. Here, the first component is the time component.

With reference to Fig.

\mathchoice{\hbox to0.0pt{{$\underline{{\hbox to5.36667pt{}}}$\hss}}}{\hbox to0.0pt{{$\underline{{\hbox to5.36667pt{}}}$\hss}}}{\hbox to0.0pt{{$\underline{{\hbox to4.23447pt{}}}$\hss}}}{\hbox to0.0pt{{$\underline{{\hbox to3.6278pt{}}}$\hss}}}S_{t}
transported along a worldline
O^{\prime}
in Minkowski space, normally to the world line, so that it is represented as a purely spatial vector, a 3–vector, in the comoving frame
\mathfrak{S}^{\prime}
, how is it represented, as a 3–vector,
in an inertial frame
\mathfrak{S}
? what are the equations of motion for the new representation, supposing the equations in the comoving frame to be known?
These questions are particularly relevant in atomic physics, since an electron orbiting an atom carries an intrinsic magnetic moment.

Our first result is that the relativity of simultaneity implies that the spin, as a 3–vector in the lab frame,
is not the one which has appeared so far in the literature, consisting in the spatial part
\bm{S}
of the 4–vector representation
\{S^{0},\bm{S}\}
. Indeed,
\bm{S}
does not undergo Lorentz contraction in the direction of motion,
as a properly defined 3–vector should. On the contrary, it dilates in the direction of motion.

The correct 3–vector to describe the spin in the lab frame, instead,
is

\bm{T}=\bm{S}-S^{0}\bm{v}/c^{2}
. Because the spin is normal to the world line of the particle, in an inertial frame,
S^{0}=\bm{v}{\mathchoice{\cdot}{\cdot}{\cdot}{\cdot}}\bm{S}
,
while in a general frame
S^{0}=(\bm{v}-\bm{\Omega}\times\bm{r}){\mathchoice{\cdot}{\cdot}{\cdot}{\cdot}}\bm{S}/[(1-\bm{g}{\mathchoice{\cdot}{\cdot}{\cdot}{\cdot}}\bm{r})^{2}-(\bm{\Omega}\times\bm{r})^{2}+(\bm{\Omega}\times\bm{r}){\mathchoice{\cdot}{\cdot}{\cdot}{\cdot}}\bm{v}]
,
with
\bm{g}
the equivalent gravitational field,
\bm{\Omega}
the gravi–magnetic Coriolis field, and
\bm{r}
the position of the spin.
The combinations
\theta=1-\bm{g}{\mathchoice{\cdot}{\cdot}{\cdot}{\cdot}}\bm{r}
and
\bm{w}=\bm{\Omega}\times\bm{r}
are to be identified with the scalar and the vector potential of the gravitational field.

The previous literature uses
\bm{S}
as the spin,
and it is common to indicate as
\bm{V}
the spatial components of a 4–vector
\vec{V}
, hence we are keeping the symbol
\bm{S}
for the conventional spin for ease of comparison. We shall also write formulas for both the spin definitions.
We shall see later on that, irrespectively of the procedure used, the linear rotation, seen in an inertial reference frame,
of the 3–vectors
\bm{S}
and
\bm{T}
is the same.

Our second result is that both vectors
\bm{S}
and
\bm{T}
, in the lab frame, obey a differential equation which contains a linear rotation term and also a nonlinear term. The linear term, which we identify with Thomas precession, differs from the accepted value. We explain that the difference is due to the fact that the accepted value of Thomas precession does not describe the rotation of the spin in the lab frame, but rather is the rate of the Coriolis rotation of an auxiliary frame which is purely rotating relative to the lab.

Detailed results

Let us provide more details.
According to a naive approach, which takes into account only the relativistic transformation of the electromagnetic field, the spin–orbit term in the Hamiltonian for the electron should be about twice as
large as the one observed.
A full relativistic treatment, however, reproduces the correct spin–orbit coupling, as shown by Thomas
[
1
,
2
]
.
The additional precession, due exclusively to relativistic effects for an accelerated frame, has been calculated in various ways, and there are some discrepancies in the literature. The common value accepted for the precession is
[
2
,
3
]

\bm{\omega}^{\mathrm{Th}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle?}}{{=}}-\frac{\gamma-1}{v^{2}}\bm{v}\bm{\times}\bm{a}=-\frac{\gamma^{2}}{(\gamma+1)c^{2}}\bm{v}\bm{\times}\bm{a},

(1)

with
\bm{v}
and
\bm{a}
the Newtonian 3–velocity and 3–acceleration,
c
the speed of light,
and
\gamma=(1-v^{2}/c^{2})^{-1/2}
the Lorentz factor.
There are several other expressions for Thomas precession in the literature, some even with opposite signs, due mainly to some different interpretation or downright misinterpretation
[
4
,
5
]
.
While the work of Malykin
[
4
]
reports exhaustively and critically the values that have been proposed, it ends up agreeing on the value as proposed by Thomas
[
2
]
.
In the following, we shall show that the accepted factor is incorrect, in the sense that Eq. (
1
)
does not describe the precession of the spin as seen in an inertial frame, e.g. the lab frame:
because of Lorentz contraction, the spin of an accelerated particle makes a complicated motion, which is not a simple precession.
Instead, we prove that Eq. (
1
) describes rather the rotation, relative to the lab frame, of the axes of a rotating frame (whose origin coincides with that of the lab frame).
The relative rotation rate is thus a Coriolis rotation.
This fact has been occasionally pointed out in the literature,
[
6
,
7
,
8
]
.

We show that, in the lab frame, the motion of the direction of the spin

\frac{d}{dt}\bm{\hat{S}}_{t}=[\bm{\omega}^{\mathrm{Th}}(t)+\bm{\omega}^{\mathrm{nl}}(t,\bm{\hat{S}}_{t})]\times\bm{\hat{S}}_{t}

(2)

contains a linear kinematic rotation,

\bm{\omega}^{\mathrm{Th}}=-\frac{\gamma^{2}}{2c^{2}}\bm{v}\bm{\times}\bm{a},

(3)

but also an additional nonlinear precession, which, for a planar motion, is

\bm{\omega}^{\mathrm{nl}}=\frac{\gamma^{2}}{c^{2}}\left[\bm{v}{\mathchoice{\cdot}{\cdot}{\cdot}{\cdot}}\bm{a}\,\bm{\hat{v}}{\mathchoice{\cdot}{\cdot}{\cdot}{\cdot}}\bm{\hat{S}}\,\bm{\hat{n}}{\mathchoice{\cdot}{\cdot}{\cdot}{\cdot}}\bm{\hat{S}}+\frac{1}{2}\kappa v^{3}\left(\bm{\hat{n}}{\mathchoice{\cdot}{\cdot}{\cdot}{\cdot}}\bm{\hat{S}}\,\bm{\hat{n}}{\mathchoice{\cdot}{\cdot}{\cdot}{\cdot}}\bm{\hat{S}}-\bm{\hat{v}}{\mathchoice{\cdot}{\cdot}{\cdot}{\cdot}}\bm{\hat{S}}\,\bm{\hat{v}}{\mathchoice{\cdot}{\cdot}{\cdot}{\cdot}}\bm{\hat{S}}\right)\right]\bm{\hat{b}}.

(4)

Here
\kappa
is the curvature,
\bm{\hat{v}}
the tangent,
\bm{\hat{n}}
the normal, and
\bm{\hat{b}}=\bm{\hat{v}}\bm{\times}\bm{\hat{n}}
the binormal of the trajectory described in the lab frame.
The nonlinearity is a consequence of splitting the motion of the spin in an equation for its norm and an equation for its direction, as detailed in the Methods section. The overall equation for the spin vector, however, is linear, and it is but the Bargmann–Michel–Telegdi equation
[
9
]
represented in the lab frame.
The presence of a nonlinearity was found out by Stepanov
[
8
]
in a particular case, and also by Kholmetskii and Yarman
[
10
]
.

Furthermore, we shall prove that for a uniform circular motion, for which the linear precession rate
(
3
) is constant, the average contribution of the nonlinear term (
4
), when added to the linear term yields Eq. (
1
). This apparently fortuitous occurrence may be at the origin of the unquestioned standing of Eq. (
1
).

Our Eq. (
3
) has a simple geometrical interpretation for planar motions:
the angle of linear precession during a time
T
is equal to the area swiped
by the celerity
\gamma\bm{v}
divided by
c^{2}
. The corresponding lowest order limit, with the factor
\gamma=1
, was mentioned by Borel
[
11
,
12
]
already in 1913.
Thus, apparently, Borel had derived at least an approximate expression for Thomas precession, using merely geometrical considerations.

In solving the problem of Thomas precession, we used a coordinate–free approach to the problem, which allows us to identify an skew–symmetric tensor
\mathchoice{\hbox to0.0pt{{$\underline{{\hbox to5.6667pt{}}}$\hss}}}{\hbox to0.0pt{{$\underline{{\hbox to5.6667pt{}}}$\hss}}}{\hbox to0.0pt{{$\underline{{\hbox to4.47224pt{}}}$\hss}}}{\hbox to0.0pt{{$\underline{{\hbox to3.73895pt{}}}$\hss}}}\mathchoice{\hbox to0.0pt{{$\underline{{\hbox to5.6667pt{}}}$\hss}}}{\hbox to0.0pt{{$\underline{{\hbox to5.6667pt{}}}$\hss}}}{\hbox to0.0pt{{$\underline{{\hbox to4.47224pt{}}}$\hss}}}{\hbox to0.0pt{{$\underline{{\hbox to3.73895pt{}}}$\hss}}}\mathfrak{B}
, the generator of Fermi–Walker transport, depending only on the intrinsic geometry of the worldline of the accelerated particle, which describes a kinematic rotation in Minkowski space. The coordinate–free approach is extremely valuable, in that it is robust against error, and in that it provides a manifestly invariant picture, in the spirit of Minkowski
[
13
]
and of the reference book by Misner, Thorne, and Wheeler
[
14
]
.
Often we read that the equations of physics must be covariant. Actually, covariance implies already the choice of a representation, where the physical quantities are represented by lists with upper or lower indexes, which transform contravariantly or covariantly. However vectors and tensors are invariant objects, only their representations are contravariant or covariant.

In the lab frame, the motion of the spin
is dictated by

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\bm{S}_{t}=\ \frac{q}{m}\biggl\{

\displaystyle\frac{g_{\mathrm{L}}}{2\gamma_{t}}\left[\bm{S}_{t}\bm{\times}\bm{B}_{t}+\bm{S}_{t}{\mathchoice{\cdot}{\cdot}{\cdot}{\cdot}}\bm{v}_{t}\,\bm{E}_{t}\right]-\left(\frac{g_{\mathrm{L}}}{2}-1\right)\bm{S}_{t}{\mathchoice{\cdot}{\cdot}{\cdot}{\cdot}}\bm{G}_{t}\,\bm{v}_{t}\biggr\}\ ,

(5a)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\bm{T}_{t}=\ \frac{q}{m}\biggl\{

\displaystyle\frac{g_{\mathrm{L}}}{2\gamma_{t}}\left[\bm{T}_{t}\bm{\times}\bm{B}_{t}-\bm{T}_{t}{\mathchoice{\cdot}{\cdot}{\cdot}{\cdot}}\bm{E}_{t}\,\bm{v}_{t}\right]+\left(\frac{g_{\mathrm{L}}}{2}-1\right)\bm{T}_{t}{\mathchoice{\cdot}{\cdot}{\cdot}{\cdot}}\bm{v}_{t}\,\bm{G}_{t}\biggr\}\ .

(5b)

Equation (
5a
) is the spatial part of the Bargmann–Michel–Telegdi equation represented in the lab frame.
Here, the term not multiplied by

g_{\mathrm{L}}
,
(q/m)\bm{S}_{t}{\mathchoice{\cdot}{\cdot}{\cdot}{\cdot}}\bm{G}_{t}\,\bm{v}_{t}
, identifies the kinematic contribution due to the spin being accelerated;
the other terms are due to the direct coupling with the electromagnetic field.
The 3–vector
\bm{G}=\gamma\left[\bm{E}-\bm{E}{\mathchoice{\cdot}{\cdot}{\cdot}{\cdot}}\bm{v}\,\frac{\bm{v}}{c^{2}}+\bm{v}\bm{\times}\bm{B}\right]
is the simultaneity–corrected Lorentz force per unit charge.

Spin with no magnetic moment

We shall consider
\lambda=0
, so that the spin of the particle does not couple to the field causing the motion, and thus
only the kinematic term contributes to its time evolution.
Then, the terms
X^{\prime},Y^{\prime},Z^{\prime}

\bm{S}

Indeed, according to the decomposition outlined in the Methods section, the linear part of the angular velocity is

\bm{\omega}^{\mathrm{Th}}=\omega^{\mathrm{Th}}\bm{\hat{b}}

\omega^{\mathrm{Th}}=-(\gamma^{2}-1)\omega/2

\bm{\omega}^{\mathrm{nl},\pm}=\omega^{\mathrm{nl},\pm}\bm{\hat{b}}

\displaystyle{\omega}^{\mathrm{nl},\pm}=

\displaystyle\ \pm\frac{\gamma^{2}}{2}[\bm{a}_{t}{\mathchoice{\cdot}{\cdot}{\cdot}{\cdot}}\bm{\hat{P}}^{\pm}_{t}\,\bm{\hat{P}}^{\pm}_{t}\bm{\times}\bm{v}_{t}+\bm{v}_{t}{\mathchoice{\cdot}{\cdot}{\cdot}{\cdot}}\bm{\hat{P}}^{\pm}_{t}\,\bm{\hat{P}}^{\pm}_{t}\bm{\times}\bm{a}_{t}]{\mathchoice{\cdot}{\cdot}{\cdot}{\cdot}}\bm{\hat{b}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ \pm\frac{\gamma^{2}-1}{2}\frac{(\gamma^{\pm 2}+1)\cos^{2}(\gamma\omega t-\phi)-1}{(\gamma^{\pm 2}-1)\cos^{2}(\gamma\omega t-\phi)+1}\omega,

(12)

with
\tan(\phi)=Y^{\prime}/X^{\prime}

\bm{\hat{P}}^{\pm}
the unit vector of the in–plane component of the spin.
Thus
\bm{\omega}^{\mathrm{nl}}

.
The average value over a period is

\overline{\omega}^{\mathrm{nl},\pm}=\frac{(\gamma-1)^{2}}{2}\omega\ ,

(13)

thus it contributes a higher order correction, with respect to the linear term, in the non–relativistic limit.
Remarkably, the average contribution is the same for both the conventional and the simultaneity–corrected spin.

When this average correction is added to the linear part, we get, for a lucky coincidence, the accepted value of Thomas precession,

\overline{\bm{\omega}}_{\mathrm{tot}}=-\frac{1}{2}\left[\gamma^{2}-1-(\gamma-1)^{2}\right]\omega\bm{\hat{b}}=-(\gamma-1)\omega\bm{\hat{b}}.

(14)

Thus, we can say that the accepted result for Thomas precession holds, as an average, for uniform circular orbits, when adding the constant linear and the average nonlinear contribution to the precession.

Spin with magnetic moment in a cyclotron

In the lab frame, let
\bm{B}=B\bm{\hat{b}}
a uniform magnetic field and let the electric field
\bm{E}=\bm{0}

Let the lab frame the frame in which the particle is making a uniform circular motion (and not a helix) with cyclotron frequency

Contrary to the previous case, we do not put

Discussion

In conclusion, the accepted value for the Thomas precession stems from confusing it with the rate of Thomas–Wigner rotation, which applies in a rotating frame possibly convenient but of no physical significance.

Instead, we wish to know the equations of motion of the spin either in the comoving frame the axes of which undergo FW transport, or
in the lab frame. In the former frame, the equation takes its simplest form, Eq. (
43
), in the latter frame either
Eq. (
44a
) or (
44b
) holds, depending if one is using the conventional definition of spin
\bm{S}

or the simultaneity–corrected one
\bm{T}
.

The reason behind the complicated motion in the lab frame is that a Lorentz boost introduces a rotation: indeed, if a vector
\bm{S}^{\prime}

stays constant in the rest frame of the particle, when going to the lab frame one has to apply a Thomas–Wigner rotation and a boost.
The boost changes the component of
\bm{S}
along the velocity, thus
the vector will undergo an additional rotation. This rotation is nonlinear, as it depends on the orientation of
\bm{S}^{\prime}
relative to the velocity. In Section C of the Supplemental Materials we show in detail how the contribution of the Thomas–Wigner rotation and the contribution of Lorentz contraction get twined.

In a uniform circular motion, the effect of the Lorentz contraction averages out, because the Lorentz contraction factor is constant and because the spin, over time, points in all directions. This exceptional case, however, has been mistaken for the norm, since the uniform circular motion is the simplest curvilinear accelerated motion, and hence is the go–to case when treating Thomas precession.

While in the lab frame the spin does not undergo a simple precession, we have shown how to extract univocally from the equation of motion a linear rotation term, which we identify with the Thomas precession, and a nonlinear rotation term, due to the interplay of Lorentz contraction and Thomas–Wigner rotation.

In perspective, it is interesting to consider the expansion of the present results to the general relativistic case, along the lines of Ref.
[
23
]
, where the Thomas precession combines with de Sitter and Lense–Thirring precession.

We shall refer to it as the simultaneity-corrected procedure.
Two other important vector fields normal to the worldline of a particle are its 4–acceleration and the

\{A^{0},\bm{A}\}

q\bm{G}=m\bm{A}^{\mathrm{sim}}

In the case of spin, we opt for the simultaneity–corrected procedure because the intrinsic magnetic moment of the electron,
in the Gilbert model, might be due to two equal and opposite
magnetic monopoles separated by a small distance. (Precisely, if we take into account Dirac’s quantization and take the monopoles to be elementary, the separation explaining the observed magnetic dipole moment is the classical radius of the electron
\delta r=e^{2}/(mc^{2})

If instead the magnetic moment is due to the rotation of a charge, as in the Ampère model, consider the following:
a distributed charge is making a circular motion around its center of mass in a given frame.
In a frame where the center of mass moves with speed
\bm{v}
normal to the plane of circular motion, the charges will make
a helical trajectory, while their angular speed is reduced by a factor
\gamma
due to the dilation of time, and the magnetic moment is reduced correspondingly.

The magnetic moment, therefore, decreases by a factor
1/\gamma
in the direction of motion, irrespective of the underlying model.

A further argument in favour of using the simultaneity–corrected spin
\bm{T}
is the following. The magnetic dipole moment
\bm{M}

is obtained by the integration over space of the magnetic dipole density
\bm{m}
. The latter is a 3–vector, which, together with the electric dipole density
\bm{p}

d=(\bm{p},\bm{m}):=\begin{pmatrix}0&\bm{p}^{\mathrm{tr}}\\
\bm{p}&\bm{m}\cdot\bm{\mathcal{J}}\end{pmatrix}

d^{\alpha}_{\,\beta}

The electric and magnetic dipole density 4–vectors are obtained by

\bm{m}&-\bm{p}\cdot\bm{\mathcal{J}}\end{pmatrix}
by contraction with the 4–velocity,
p^{\alpha}=d^{\alpha}_{\,\beta}U^{\beta}
,
m^{\alpha}=*d^{\alpha}_{\,\beta}U^{\beta}
,
hence they are automatically normal to the 4–velocity.
In the rest frame of the electron
\bm{p}^{\prime}=0

Therefore, in the lab frame, recalling that the Lorentz transformation from the lab frame to the electron rest frame includes the Thomas–Wigner rotation,
L_{t}=R_{t}B[\bm{v}_{t}]

\bm{m}=\mathcal{R}_{t}^{\mathrm{tr}}\bm{m}^{\prime}-(\gamma_{t}-1)\bm{\hat{v}}_{t}\bm{\times}(\bm{\hat{v}}_{t}\bm{\times}\mathcal{R}_{t}^{\mathrm{tr}}\bm{m}^{\prime})

\mathcal{R}_{t}^{\mathrm{tr}}\bm{m}^{\prime}

\bm{\hat{v}}_{t}{\mathchoice{\cdot}{\cdot}{\cdot}{\cdot}}\bm{m}=\bm{\hat{v}}_{t}{\mathchoice{\cdot}{\cdot}{\cdot}{\cdot}}(\mathcal{R}_{t}^{\mathrm{tr}}\bm{m}^{\prime})

\gamma

\bm{M}=\int dV\bm{m}=\int\frac{dV^{\prime}}{\gamma_{t}}[\mathcal{R}_{t}^{\mathrm{tr}}\bm{m}^{\prime}+(\gamma_{t}-1)\bm{\hat{v}}_{t}\bm{\times}(\bm{\hat{v}}_{t}\bm{\times}\mathcal{R}_{t}^{\mathrm{tr}}\bm{m}^{\prime})]=\frac{\mathcal{R}_{t}^{\mathrm{tr}}\bm{M}^{\prime}}{\gamma_{t}}+\frac{\gamma_{t}-1}{\gamma_{t}}\bm{\hat{v}}_{t}\bm{\times}(\bm{\hat{v}}_{t}\bm{\times}\mathcal{R}_{t}^{\mathrm{tr}}\bm{M}^{\prime}).

(28)

Thus, the total dipole magnetic moment contracts by a factor
\gamma

In the rest frame of the electron
\bm{M}^{\prime}=\lambda\bm{S}^{\prime}

\lambda=g_{\mathrm{L}}e/(2m)

e=-|e|
being the signed charge of the electron.
In order for the same proportionality to apply in the lab frame,
we must have
\bm{M}=\lambda\bm{T}

As a test of which definition is relevant, consider a beam
of electrons at relativistic speed.
It is possible to polarize the electrons longitudinally, so that their spin is parallel to their velocity and their magnetic moment is antiparallel to it.
If the beam enters a region of magnetic field

If the spin, and hence the magnetic moment, were increased by a factor

For instance, in a 1GeV linear accelerator, electrons can reach a factor

Fermi–Walker transport

Fermi–Walker (FW) transport
[
15
,
16
]
has a crucial role in the following.
We illustrate it right away.

Consider a particle, having a velocity
\bm{v}_{t_{0}}
, relative to an inertial frame

\mathfrak{S}
, at time
t_{0}
in the inertial frame. Let us pick an inertial reference frame
\mathfrak{S}^{\prime}_{t_{0}}
where the particle is at rest at time
t_{0}
, and let
t^{\prime}_{0}
the proper time.
At an infinitesimally later time
\delta t^{\prime}
in

\mathfrak{S}^{\prime}_{t_{0}}
, the velocity of the particle changes by
\delta\bm{v}^{\prime}=\bm{a}^{\prime}_{t^{\prime}_{0}}\delta t^{\prime}
, with
\bm{a}^{\prime}_{t^{\prime}_{0}}

the proper acceleration.
Now, there are infinitely many different inertial reference frames where the particle is at rest at time
t^{\prime}_{0}+\delta t^{\prime}
, which can be obtained by applying an infinitesimal Lorentz boost followed by an arbitrary rotation of the spatial axes. A Lorentz boost does not rotate the spatial axes normal to the velocity, and it rotates the time axis and the spatial axis parallel to the velocity in their common plane.
It is therefore the simplest Lorentz transformation.
(Lorentz boosts are the transformations often presented in introductory texts on special relativity as the Lorentz transformations
tout court
. Actually, they form a subset, which is not a subgroup,
of Lorentz transformations: in special relativity, the 3D space is a hyperplane normal to the worldline of
the origin of a reference frame. Two reference frames in relative motion will thus have different 3D spaces.
These spaces, however, will share a common plane. Lorentz boosts are transformations between reference frames in which two of the spatial axes, usually the
Y
and
Z
axes, belong to the common plane and coincide between the two frames.)

Thus, let us consider, among these infinitely many frames, the one which is obtained by a pure infinitesimal boost with velocity
\bm{\delta v}^{\prime}
. Let us repeat the procedure at another infinitesimal time
\delta t^{\prime\prime}
, etc. The sequence of infinitesimal boosts defines FW transport. Since the boosts do not form a subgroup, their product is a general Lorentz transformation, which can be written
as the product of a 3D rotation and a boost.

In particular, if the spatial axes of the reference frame comoving with the particle are FW transported, they individuate a special accelerated reference frame, the FW frame, which is non–rotating.
This means that, if we spin three gyroscopes attached to the particle, then accelerate the particle without applying any torque to the gyroscopes, the axes of these will be FW transported. While the gyroscopes do not rotate in the sense that a simple Lorentz boost is applied from an instantaneous comoving frame to the next, when viewing them from the initial reference frame
\mathfrak{S}
, they will make a complicated motion in which we shall individuate a linear precession term, the Thomas precession.
It is erroneous, however, to state that the gyroscope will just appear as precessing in the lab frame.
On one hand, Lorentz contraction will affect the gyroscopes, on the other hand, due to the relativity of simultaneity, the angles among them will appear to change in time in the lab frame, as shown in Fig.
5

The Lorentz transformation from the lab frame to the FW frame

R_{\mathrm{TW}}(t)B[\bm{v}_{t}]

In the decomposition

In the decomposition
L_{\mathrm{FW}}=B[\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{TW}}(t)\bm{v}_{t}]R_{\mathrm{TW}}(t)
, the rotation is applied
before the Lorentz boost, hence it represents the rotation of the axes of a frame whose origin is at rest in the lab frame, and whose axes are rotating relative to the lab frame.
The rotation rate, as measured in the lab frame, is the skew–symmetric matrix
\Omega
defined by

\frac{d}{dt}\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{TW}}(t)=\Omega(t)\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{TW}}(t)
.
The two rotation rates satisfy
\Omega(t)=\Omega^{\prime}(\tau(t))/\gamma(t)
.

The relation between the proper time
t^{\prime}
of the accelerated particle
O^{\prime}
and the time
t
of the lab is given, up to arbitrary choices of initial time,
by the well known time dilation formula
dt^{\prime}/dt=\gamma^{-1}_{t}
, where
\gamma_{t}=[1-v^{2}_{t}]^{-1/2}
is the Lorentz contraction factor.
For a given motion of
O^{\prime}
, one finds, after integrating,
t^{\prime}=\tau(t)
, and upon inversion
t=\tau^{-1}(t^{\prime})
.
Relativistic invariant formulas are independent of the reference frame, hence they are expressed in terms of proper time.
We will use the time dilation formula and pass to the inertial time
t
when representing the formulas in the lab frame, as we did in the preceding paragraph.
Sometimes, we will write equations depending on
t^{\prime}
on one side and on
t
on the other side. In these cases, for brevity,
we will sometimes omit to write
t=\tau^{-1}(t^{\prime})
or
t^{\prime}=\tau(t)
.

The 3–vector corresponding to the skew–symmetric matrix
\Omega^{\prime}_{t^{\prime}}=\bm{\omega}^{\prime*}_{t^{\prime}}{\mathchoice{\cdot}{\cdot}{\cdot}{\cdot}}\bm{\mathcal{J}}
, with
\bm{\mathcal{J}}
the three
(3\times 3)
generators of rotations in 3D, is

\bm{\omega}^{\prime*}_{t^{\prime}}=\frac{\gamma_{t^{\prime}}}{\gamma_{t^{\prime}}+1}\bm{v}^{\prime*}_{t^{\prime}}\bm{\times}\bm{a}^{\prime*}_{t^{\prime}}=-\frac{\gamma^{3}_{t}}{\gamma_{t}+1}\bm{v}_{t}\bm{\times}\bm{a}_{t},

(31)

where
\bm{v}^{\prime*}_{t^{\prime}}
is the velocity of the lab frame relative to the boosted frame,
\gamma_{t^{\prime}}
is the corresponding Lorentz factor, and

\bm{a}^{\prime*}_{t^{\prime}}
is the proper acceleration of the boosted frame.
The second equality specifies the rotation in terms of quantities measured in the lab frame.

The vector associated to the skew–symmetric matrix
\Omega_{t}
is

\bm{\omega}_{t}=-\frac{\gamma^{2}_{t}}{\gamma_{t}+1}\bm{v}_{t}\bm{\times}\bm{a}_{t}.

(32)

We recognize in Eq. (
32
) the accepted value for the Thomas precession: it is not, therefore, an additional rotation of the spin in the lab frame, but rather the rotation of an auxiliary purely rotating reference frame.

By contrast, the formulation of the preceding subsection in terms of

of 3–vectors

\displaystyle\ -\mathcal{M}^{\mathrm{tr}}(t){\mathchoice{\cdot}{\cdot}{\cdot}{\cdot}}\bm{T}_{t}\ .

(44b)

where the matrix
\mathcal{M}
is

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}(t)

\displaystyle:=-\left[\mathbbm{1}_{3}+\gamma^{2}_{t}\bm{v}_{t}\bm{v}^{\mathrm{tr}}_{t}\right]{\mathchoice{\cdot}{\cdot}{\cdot}{\cdot}}\left[\lambda\gamma^{-1}_{t}\left(\bm{B}_{t}-\bm{v}_{t}{\bm{\times}}\bm{E}_{t}\right){\mathchoice{\cdot}{\cdot}{\cdot}{\cdot}}\bm{\mathcal{J}}-\bm{v}_{t}\bm{a}^{\mathrm{tr}}_{t}\right],

(45)

The time component satisfies identically
S^{0}(t)=\bm{v}_{t}{\mathchoice{\cdot}{\cdot}{\cdot}{\cdot}}\bm{S}_{t}
.
The terms on the rhs multiplied by
\lambda
are the direct terms, the remaining term
\left[\mathbbm{1}_{3}+\gamma^{2}_{t}\bm{v}_{t}\bm{v}^{\mathrm{tr}}_{t}\right]{\mathchoice{\cdot}{\cdot}{\cdot}{\cdot}}\bm{v}_{t}\bm{a}^{\mathrm{tr}}_{t}=\gamma^{2}_{t}\bm{v}_{t}\bm{a}^{\mathrm{tr}}_{t}
for
\bm{S}
, or
-\gamma^{2}_{t}\bm{a}_{t}\bm{v}^{\mathrm{tr}}_{t}
for
\bm{T}
,
is the kinematic term.

Separation of norm and direction in the motion of a vector

Here we discuss the linear homogeneous equation for an
N
–dimensional vector

{\partial_{t}\bm{P}(t)}=M(t)\bm{P}(t)\ ,

(46)

with
M(t)
a given one–parameter family of matrices.

We are indicating with a bold symbol a column vector.
A row vector will be indicated as a transpose, e.g.
\bm{P}^{\mathrm{tr}}
.
The row–column product is implied by juxtaposition of the symbols. Thus,
\bm{a}^{\mathrm{tr}}\bm{b}
is the scalar
\bm{a}{\mathchoice{\cdot}{\cdot}{\cdot}{\cdot}}\bm{b}
, while
\bm{a}\bm{b}^{\mathrm{tr}}
is a matrix;
M\bm{P}
is a column vector,
\bm{P}^{\mathrm{tr}}M
a row vector,
and
\bm{P}^{\mathrm{tr}}M\bm{P}
is a scalar.

If
M(t)
is skew–symmetric, the norm of
\bm{P}(t)
is constant, thus the equation
describes a rotation.
However, how to proceed if
M(t)
is not skew–symmetric? Can we still individuate a rotation term?

First, we shall drop from the problem any component which is possibly conserved and does not enter the equations for the other components.
This happens if
M(t)
has some rows and the corresponding columns identically equal to zero. Thus, if
M
can be written
in the block form
(\begin{smallmatrix}M_{0}(t)&0\\
0&0\end{smallmatrix})
, we shall consider only the block
M_{0}
and the corresponding components of

\bm{P}
, since the remaining components are constant.

More generally, if
M(t)
has a set of
\mu
common, constant in
t
, left and right eigenvectors
\bm{e}_{m}
with

0
eigenvalue, we change coordinates so that the last
\mu
axes coincide with
\bm{e}_{m}
. Then, since
\bm{P}(t)\cdot\bm{e}_{m}=const
, we consider
only the remaining components of
\bm{P}
, and the corresponding non–zero block of
M
.

Next, we separate
M(t)
, or its relevant part
M_{0}(t)
,
in a symmetric and an skew–symmetric part,
M(t)=\overline{M}(t)+M_{\mathrm{a}}(t)
.
The skew–symmetric part gives automatically a contribution perpendicular to
\bm{P}
, hence it provides a rotation.
The symmetric part, however, also provides a rotation, albeit a nonlinear one, as we shall see in the following.

We write the vector as
\bm{P}(t)=P(t)\bm{\hat{P}}(t)
, and we separate the equations
for the norm
P(t)
and for the direction
\bm{\hat{P}}(t)
. By construction, the equation for the direction can be only a rotation.
By multiplying Eq. (
46
) with
\bm{P}(t)
, we get the equation for the norm, which involves only the symmetric part of
M
,

P(t)\frac{\partial{P}(t)}{\partial t}=P^{2}(t)\,\bm{\hat{P}}^{\mathrm{tr}}(t)\overline{M}(t)\bm{\hat{P}}(t)\ .

(47)

The direction instead satisfies

\frac{\partial\bm{\hat{P}}(t)}{\partial t}=[\overline{M}(t)-\bm{\hat{P}}^{\mathrm{tr}}(t)\overline{M}(t)\bm{\hat{P}}(t)]\bm{\hat{P}}(t)+M_{\mathrm{a}}(t)\bm{\hat{P}}(t)\ .

(48)

The first term in the rhs can be written

[\overline{M}(t)-\bm{\hat{P}}^{\mathrm{tr}}(t)\overline{M}(t)\bm{\hat{P}}(t)]\bm{\hat{P}}(t)=A_{\mathrm{nl}}(t,\hat{\bm{P}}(t))\bm{\hat{P}}(t)\ ,

(49)

where we defined the skew–symmetric matrix

A_{\mathrm{nl}}(t,\hat{\bm{P}}):=[\overline{M}(t),\bm{\hat{P}}\bm{\hat{P}}^{\mathrm{tr}}]\ ,

(50)

with
[\ ,\ ]
the commutator.
The skew–symmetric matrix
A_{\mathrm{nl}}(t,\hat{\bm{P}})

hence corresponds to another rotation, which is nonlinear, as it depends on the direction
\bm{\hat{P}}
itself.

From Eq. (
47
), the square norm can be written after possibly solving for the direction,

P^{2}(t)=P^{2}(0)\,\exp{[\,2\!\int_{0}^{t}dt\,\bm{\hat{P}}^{\mathrm{tr}}(t)\overline{M}(t)\bm{\hat{P}}(t)]}.

(51)

Three dimensional case

In particular, in three dimensions, to any skew–symmetric matrix it is associated, through the Hodge dual operation, an axial vector which can be identified as an angular velocity. The linear angular velocity is

\bm{\omega}^{\mathrm{l}}(t){\mathchoice{\cdot}{\cdot}{\cdot}{\cdot}}\bm{\mathcal{J}}=M_{\mathrm{a}}(t)\ ,

(52)

with
\bm{\mathcal{J}}
the
3\times 3
angular momentum generators, which form a basis for skew–symmetric matrices.
The nonlinear angular velocity satisfies

\bm{\omega}^{\mathrm{nl}}(t,\bm{\hat{P}}){\mathchoice{\cdot}{\cdot}{\cdot}{\cdot}}\bm{\mathcal{J}}=A_{\mathrm{nl}}(t,\hat{\bm{P}}).

(53)

Explicitly,

\bm{\omega}^{\mathrm{nl}}(t,\bm{\hat{P}})=\bm{\hat{P}}\bm{\times}\left(\overline{M}(t)\bm{\hat{P}}\right)\ .

(54)

Finally, in three dimensions, the equations for the direction can be separated as

\frac{\partial\bm{\hat{P}}(t)}{\partial t}=[\bm{\omega}^{\mathrm{l}}(t)+\bm{\omega}^{\mathrm{nl}}(t,\bm{\hat{P}}(t))]\bm{\times}\bm{\hat{P}}(t).

(55)

Two–dimensional case

Suppose that the
Z
component of the vector is conserved, and that it does not enter in the equation for the other two components.
This case is relevant because often times the trajectory is planar, and the off-plane component of the spin is always conserved.
Let
M_{\textup{II}}
the
2\times 2
nonzero block of
M
.
Let
\bm{P}_{\parallel}=\{\bm{P}_{\textup{II}},0\}
the component of
\bm{P}
on the
XY
plane with
\bm{P}_{\textup{II}}
the 2–component vector consisting in the first two components of
\bm{P}
.
We have an effective two–dimensional equation

\partial_{t}\bm{P}_{\textup{II}}(t)=M_{\textup{II}}(t)\bm{P}_{\textup{II}}(t).

(56)

We may proceed as in the general case, and apply Eq. (
50
) to
\bm{P}_{\textup{II}}(t)
.
Since there is only one skew–symmetric matrix in 2D, up to a constant,
A_{Z}=(\begin{smallmatrix}0&-1\\
1&0\end{smallmatrix})
, the commutator yields

A_{\mathrm{nl}}(t,\hat{\bm{P}}_{\parallel}):=[\overline{M}_{\textup{II}}(t),\bm{\hat{P}}_{\textup{II}}\bm{\hat{P}}_{\textup{II}}^{\mathrm{tr}}]=\omega^{\mathrm{nl}}(t,\bm{\hat{P}}_{\parallel}(t))A_{Z},

(57)

where
\omega^{\mathrm{nl}}(t,\bm{\hat{P}}_{\parallel})=\bm{\hat{k}}{\mathchoice{\cdot}{\cdot}{\cdot}{\cdot}}[\bm{\hat{P}}_{\parallel}(t)\bm{\times}(\overline{M}(t)\bm{\hat{P}}_{\parallel}(t))]
,
while
\bm{\hat{P}}_{\parallel}(t)
is the unit vector of the component of
\bm{P}
parallel to the
XY
plane, i.e. if
\bm{P}=\{X,Y,Z\}
,

\bm{\hat{P}}_{\parallel}=\{X,Y,0\}/\sqrt{X^{2}+Y^{2}}
.
Better, in invariant form
|\bm{P}_{\parallel}|=|\bm{P}\bm{\times}\bm{\hat{k}}|
,

\bm{\hat{P}}_{\parallel}=(\bm{P}-\bm{\hat{k}}{\mathchoice{\cdot}{\cdot}{\cdot}{\cdot}}\bm{P}\,\bm{\hat{k}})/|\bm{P}_{\parallel}|
.

The nonlinear rotation rate, as a vector in 3D, is thus

\bm{\omega}^{\mathrm{nl}}(t,\bm{\hat{P}})=\frac{\{\bm{\hat{P}}\bm{\times}[\overline{M}(t)\bm{\hat{P}}]\}{\mathchoice{\cdot}{\cdot}{\cdot}{\cdot}}\bm{\hat{k}}\,\bm{\hat{k}}}{|\bm{\hat{P}}\times\bm{\hat{k}}|^{2}}

(58)

Had we not eliminated from the equations the constant component
Z
, and had we instead applied the result for the 3D case, we would get in–plane terms in
\bm{\omega}^{\mathrm{nl}}
, which would change
the
Z
component of the unit vector
\bm{\hat{P}}
in such a way as to compensate the change in the norm
P
.

Application to the spin precession

In our case, in absence of external fields, the equation for the simultaneity–corrected spin in an inertial frame is

\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\bm{T}(t)=-\gamma^{2}(t)\bm{a}(t)\bm{v}(t){\mathchoice{\cdot}{\cdot}{\cdot}{\cdot}}\bm{T}(t),

(59)

hence the matrix is

M(t)=-\gamma^{2}(t)\bm{a}(t)\bm{v}^{\mathrm{tr}}(t).

(60)

The symmetric part is

\overline{M}(t)=-\frac{1}{2}\gamma^{2}(t)\frac{d}{dt}\bm{v}(t)\bm{v}^{\mathrm{tr}}(t)\ .

(61)

Therefore, the nonlinear part of the precession (since a spin represents a rotation, the rotation of a spin is called a precession),

\displaystyle\bm{\omega}^{\mathrm{nl}}(t,\bm{\hat{T}})=

\displaystyle\ -\frac{\gamma^{2}(t)}{2}\bm{\hat{T}}\bm{\times}\left\{\left[\frac{d}{dt}\bm{v}(t)\bm{v}^{\mathrm{tr}}(t)\right]\bm{\hat{T}}\right\}\ .

(62)

However, if the motion is planar, the component of the spin normal to the plane of motion in the lab frame is conserved. Thus, we should use the 2D result, which yields a nonlinear rotation rate

\displaystyle\bm{\omega}^{\mathrm{nl}}(t,\bm{\hat{T}})=

\displaystyle\ -\frac{\gamma^{2}(t)}{2}\left(\bm{\hat{T}}_{\parallel}\bm{\times}\left\{\left[\frac{d}{dt}\bm{v}(t)\bm{v}^{\mathrm{tr}}(t)\right]\bm{\hat{T}}_{\parallel}\right\}\right){\mathchoice{\cdot}{\cdot}{\cdot}{\cdot}}\bm{\hat{k}}\,\bm{\hat{k}}\ .

(63)

The skew–symmetric term is

M_{\mathrm{a}}=-\frac{1}{2}\gamma^{2}(t)[\bm{a}(t)\bm{v}^{\mathrm{tr}}(t)-\bm{v}(t)\bm{a}^{\mathrm{tr}}(t)]\ ,

(64)

corresponding to the Thomas precession

\bm{\omega}^{\mathrm{Th}}(t)=-\frac{\gamma^{2}(t)}{2}\bm{v}(t)\bm{\times}\bm{a}(t)\ .

(65)

For the conventional spin
\bm{S}
the same linear term holds, while the non–linear term has the opposite sign.

A.1
Notation

We will use an underscore for abstract vectors in Minkowski space, while an arrow will indicate a contravariant representation of
the vector in some reference frame, which we define below. Greek labels range in
\{0,1,2,3\}
, while Latin labels range in
\{1,2,3\}
.
The convention of summing over repeated indexes is used, so that
u^{j}v_{j}=\sum_{j=1}^{3}u^{j}v_{j}
,
u^{\alpha}v_{\alpha}=\sum_{\alpha=0}^{3}u^{\alpha}v_{\alpha}
.
However, as soon as possible, we shall replace sums over components with dot products, for ease of readability.
The signature of the metric is chosen as
{-}{+}{+}{+}
.

Observers are represented by worldlines in Minkowski spacetime, usually indicated by a letter
O
, primed or not. The worldline can be parametrized with its natural parameter, the proper time
\tau_{O}
or
t
for short.
A reference frame
\mathfrak{S}
consists in a worldline
O
, along with a set of 4 vectors,
\mathchoice{\hbox to0.0pt{{$\underline{{\hbox to3.72502pt{}}}$\hss}}}{\hbox to0.0pt{{$\underline{{\hbox to3.72502pt{}}}$\hss}}}{\hbox to0.0pt{{$\underline{{\hbox to3.0353pt{}}}$\hss}}}{\hbox to0.0pt{{$\underline{{\hbox to2.70807pt{}}}$\hss}}}e_{(\alpha)}(t)
, such that the first one

is the unit tangent vector to the worldline (
\mathchoice{\hbox to0.0pt{{$\underline{{\hbox to3.72502pt{}}}$\hss}}}{\hbox to0.0pt{{$\underline{{\hbox to3.72502pt{}}}$\hss}}}{\hbox to0.0pt{{$\underline{{\hbox to3.0353pt{}}}$\hss}}}{\hbox to0.0pt{{$\underline{{\hbox to2.70807pt{}}}$\hss}}}e_{(0)}(t){\mathchoice{\cdot}{\cdot}{\cdot}{\cdot}}\mathchoice{\hbox to0.0pt{{$\underline{{\hbox to3.72502pt{}}}$\hss}}}{\hbox to0.0pt{{$\underline{{\hbox to3.72502pt{}}}$\hss}}}{\hbox to0.0pt{{$\underline{{\hbox to3.0353pt{}}}$\hss}}}{\hbox to0.0pt{{$\underline{{\hbox to2.70807pt{}}}$\hss}}}e_{(0)}(t)=-1

g_{\alpha\beta}:=\mathchoice{\hbox to0.0pt{{$\underline{{\hbox to3.72502pt{}}}$\hss}}}{\hbox to0.0pt{{$\underline{{\hbox to3.72502pt{}}}$\hss}}}{\hbox to0.0pt{{$\underline{{\hbox to3.0353pt{}}}$\hss}}}{\hbox to0.0pt{{$\underline{{\hbox to2.70807pt{}}}$\hss}}}e_{(\alpha)}(t){\mathchoice{\cdot}{\cdot}{\cdot}{\cdot}}\mathchoice{\hbox to0.0pt{{$\underline{{\hbox to3.72502pt{}}}$\hss}}}{\hbox to0.0pt{{$\underline{{\hbox to3.72502pt{}}}$\hss}}}{\hbox to0.0pt{{$\underline{{\hbox to3.0353pt{}}}$\hss}}}{\hbox to0.0pt{{$\underline{{\hbox to2.70807pt{}}}$\hss}}}e_{(\beta)}(t)

.

\mathchoice{\hbox to0.0pt{{$\underline{{\hbox to3.72502pt{}}}$\hss}}}{\hbox to0.0pt{{$\underline{{\hbox to3.72502pt{}}}$\hss}}}{\hbox to0.0pt{{$\underline{{\hbox to3.0353pt{}}}$\hss}}}{\hbox to0.0pt{{$\underline{{\hbox to2.70807pt{}}}$\hss}}}e^{(0)}=-\mathchoice{\hbox to0.0pt{{$\underline{{\hbox to3.72502pt{}}}$\hss}}}{\hbox to0.0pt{{$\underline{{\hbox to3.72502pt{}}}$\hss}}}{\hbox to0.0pt{{$\underline{{\hbox to3.0353pt{}}}$\hss}}}{\hbox to0.0pt{{$\underline{{\hbox to2.70807pt{}}}$\hss}}}e_{(0)}
.

.

\mathchoice{\hbox to0.0pt{{$\underline{{\hbox to3.72502pt{}}}$\hss}}}{\hbox to0.0pt{{$\underline{{\hbox to3.72502pt{}}}$\hss}}}{\hbox to0.0pt{{$\underline{{\hbox to3.0353pt{}}}$\hss}}}{\hbox to0.0pt{{$\underline{{\hbox to2.70807pt{}}}$\hss}}}e_{(0)}

\mathchoice{\hbox to0.0pt{{$\underline{{\hbox to3.72502pt{}}}$\hss}}}{\hbox to0.0pt{{$\underline{{\hbox to3.72502pt{}}}$\hss}}}{\hbox to0.0pt{{$\underline{{\hbox to3.0353pt{}}}$\hss}}}{\hbox to0.0pt{{$\underline{{\hbox to2.70807pt{}}}$\hss}}}e_{(j)}
, on the other hand, is fundamental, not an arbitrary choice.

A.2
Contravariant and covariant vector representations

In a given basis, the components of a vector transported along the same worldline,
\mathchoice{\hbox to0.0pt{{$\underline{{\hbox to6.44446pt{}}}$\hss}}}{\hbox to0.0pt{{$\underline{{\hbox to6.44446pt{}}}$\hss}}}{\hbox to0.0pt{{$\underline{{\hbox to5.1556pt{}}}$\hss}}}{\hbox to0.0pt{{$\underline{{\hbox to4.4223pt{}}}$\hss}}}V(t)=V^{\alpha}(t)\mathchoice{\hbox to0.0pt{{$\underline{{\hbox to3.72502pt{}}}$\hss}}}{\hbox to0.0pt{{$\underline{{\hbox to3.72502pt{}}}$\hss}}}{\hbox to0.0pt{{$\underline{{\hbox to3.0353pt{}}}$\hss}}}{\hbox to0.0pt{{$\underline{{\hbox to2.70807pt{}}}$\hss}}}e_{(\alpha)}(t)

V^{\alpha}(t)=\mathchoice{\hbox to0.0pt{{$\underline{{\hbox to6.44446pt{}}}$\hss}}}{\hbox to0.0pt{{$\underline{{\hbox to6.44446pt{}}}$\hss}}}{\hbox to0.0pt{{$\underline{{\hbox to5.1556pt{}}}$\hss}}}{\hbox to0.0pt{{$\underline{{\hbox to4.4223pt{}}}$\hss}}}V(t){\mathchoice{\cdot}{\cdot}{\cdot}{\cdot}}\mathchoice{\hbox to0.0pt{{$\underline{{\hbox to3.72502pt{}}}$\hss}}}{\hbox to0.0pt{{$\underline{{\hbox to3.72502pt{}}}$\hss}}}{\hbox to0.0pt{{$\underline{{\hbox to3.0353pt{}}}$\hss}}}{\hbox to0.0pt{{$\underline{{\hbox to2.70807pt{}}}$\hss}}}e^{(\alpha)}(t)
.
These components, collected in a column vector, will be denoted by the symbol
\vec{V}
, which is the representation of
\mathchoice{\hbox to0.0pt{{$\underline{{\hbox to6.44446pt{}}}$\hss}}}{\hbox to0.0pt{{$\underline{{\hbox to6.44446pt{}}}$\hss}}}{\hbox to0.0pt{{$\underline{{\hbox to5.1556pt{}}}$\hss}}}{\hbox to0.0pt{{$\underline{{\hbox to4.4223pt{}}}$\hss}}}V
in the reference frame defined by the basis
\mathchoice{\hbox to0.0pt{{$\underline{{\hbox to3.72502pt{}}}$\hss}}}{\hbox to0.0pt{{$\underline{{\hbox to3.72502pt{}}}$\hss}}}{\hbox to0.0pt{{$\underline{{\hbox to3.0353pt{}}}$\hss}}}{\hbox to0.0pt{{$\underline{{\hbox to2.70807pt{}}}$\hss}}}e_{(\alpha)}
. For increased readability, a column vector will be

\{V^{0},V^{1},V^{2},V^{3}\}
. One may want to write the components in the reciprocal basis, which will be denoted by a lower index,

V_{\alpha}:=\mathchoice{\hbox to0.0pt{{$\underline{{\hbox to6.44446pt{}}}$\hss}}}{\hbox to0.0pt{{$\underline{{\hbox to6.44446pt{}}}$\hss}}}{\hbox to0.0pt{{$\underline{{\hbox to5.1556pt{}}}$\hss}}}{\hbox to0.0pt{{$\underline{{\hbox to4.4223pt{}}}$\hss}}}V{\mathchoice{\cdot}{\cdot}{\cdot}{\cdot}}\mathchoice{\hbox to0.0pt{{$\underline{{\hbox to3.72502pt{}}}$\hss}}}{\hbox to0.0pt{{$\underline{{\hbox to3.72502pt{}}}$\hss}}}{\hbox to0.0pt{{$\underline{{\hbox to3.0353pt{}}}$\hss}}}{\hbox to0.0pt{{$\underline{{\hbox to2.70807pt{}}}$\hss}}}e_{(\alpha)}

,

. Given the metric representation

, while the spatial components are

.

In a given representation, we shall indicate column 3–vectors by boldface letters,
\bm{v},\bm{a}
, while the row vectors will be denoted by a boldface symbol with a conjugation,

.
In particular, for the Minkowski metric, since

We will also use the notation

matrix having in the

Vectors per se are invariant objects. Their components however, transform with the basis. The vector representations

and

are called contravariant and covariant vectors, resp. A stricter term would be

.

The basis in its own representation is simply the canonical basis:

A.3
Classification of reference frames

Inertial reference frames have a straight worldline
O
, which makes

. A frame with constant
\mathchoice{\hbox to0.0pt{{$\underline{{\hbox to3.72502pt{}}}$\hss}}}{\hbox to0.0pt{{$\underline{{\hbox to3.72502pt{}}}$\hss}}}{\hbox to0.0pt{{$\underline{{\hbox to3.0353pt{}}}$\hss}}}{\hbox to0.0pt{{$\underline{{\hbox to2.70807pt{}}}$\hss}}}e_{(0)}
but time-varying

A.5.1
Interpretation of the polar decompositions

The decomposition of the transformation between the frame
\mathfrak{S}
and
\mathfrak{S}^{\prime}
,
L=R^{\prime}B
,
means that the boost
B
gives the transformation from the frame
\mathfrak{S}
to a frame
\mathfrak{S}^{\prime*}
moving
with velocity
\bm{v}
relative to
\mathfrak{S}
and hence at rest in
\mathfrak{S}^{\prime}
, and relative to which
\mathfrak{S}
moves with velocity
\bm{v}^{\prime*}=-\bm{v}
.
The rotation
R^{\prime}
represents a rotation in the boosted frame, hence the prime. The matrix
R^{\prime}
transforms the representation of a 4–vector in

\mathfrak{S}^{\prime*}
into its representation in
\mathfrak{S}^{\prime}
, i.e. it describes how the axes of

\mathfrak{S}^{\prime*}
, obtained by a simple boost from
\mathfrak{S}
, are rotated relative to to the axes of the frame
\mathfrak{S}^{\prime}
.

The alternative decomposition
L=BR
has a different meaning. First one applies a rotation
R
in the frame
\mathfrak{S}
, thus representing
a 4–vector in a frame
\mathfrak{S}^{*}
at rest relative to
\mathfrak{S}
, but with the axes rotated.
Then one applies a boost in this rotated frame, yielding the representation of the 4–vector in the final frame

\mathfrak{S}^{\prime}
.
The inverse matrix
R^{-1}
thus describes the rotation of
\mathfrak{S}^{*}
relative to
\mathfrak{S}
.
The fact that the frames are connected by a boost means that, since
\bm{v}^{*}=\mathcal{R}\bm{v}
is the velocity of
\mathfrak{S}^{\prime}
relative to
\mathfrak{S}^{*}
, the velocity of
\mathfrak{S}^{*}
, and hence also of

\mathfrak{S}
, relative to
\mathfrak{S}^{\prime}
is

\bm{v}^{\prime}=-\bm{v}^{*}
.
For this reason, we denoted the first column of the Lorentz transformation (
70
) with the sign opposite to the first row:
the column
\bm{V}^{\prime}=\gamma^{\prime}\bm{v}^{\prime}
yields directly the velocity of the original frame relative to the final frame.

Formally, the rotation matrices are the same
R=R^{\prime}
. However, they represent different operations,
hence we used a prime to distinguish them.

A.5.2
Minimal rotation

While the rotation
\mathcal{R}
takes
\bm{v}
to
\bm{v}^{*}

\mathcal{R}
is not minimal, i.e. it is not a rotation about the axis

In Minkowski space a rotation is no longer characterized by an axis and an angle, but rather by a plane and an angle. It seems appropriate to adapt
to this characteristic when describing 3D rotations as well.
In this case, by specifying any two
unit vectors connected through

\bm{\hat{m}}=\bm{\hat{n}}

\tfrac{0}{0}

The latter case means that
\bm{\hat{n}}
tends to
-\bm{\hat{m}}

Without loss of generality, let us say that

Then taking the limit
\epsilon\to 0
, yields

\mathcal{R}\to\mathbbm{1}-2\bm{\hat{k}}\bm{\hat{k}}^{\mathrm{tr}}-2\bm{\hat{m}}\bm{\hat{m}}^{\mathrm{tr}}\ ,

(82)

which is a proper rotation of an angle
\pi
about the axis orthogonal to
\bm{\hat{m}}
and
\bm{\hat{k}}
.

A.6
Thomas–Wigner’s rotation and Stapp’s formula

We apply the result about polar decomposition (
76
) to the combination of two boosts,

L=B[\bm{u^{\prime}}]B[\bm{u}].

(83)

We are using a prime on the second velocity because when applied to a vector representation
\vec{C}
, the first boost will transform a vector from its representation in a frame
\mathfrak{S}
to its representation
\vec{C}^{\prime}
in a new frame
\mathfrak{S}^{\prime}
, moving with velocity
\bm{u}
relative to
\mathfrak{S}
.
The second boost will represent
\vec{C}^{\prime}
in another frame
\mathfrak{S}^{\prime\prime}
, moving with velocity
\bm{u}^{\prime}
relative to
\mathfrak{S}^{\prime}
.
The product of two boosts, as described in my manuscript
[
26
]
, individuates a special subset of Lorentz transformation, called planar transformations, which preserve the spatial axis orthogonal to the velocities.

It is convenient to work not with Newtonian velocities, but with the celerities
\bm{U}=\gamma_{u}\bm{u}
,
\bm{U}^{\prime}=\gamma_{u^{\prime}}\bm{u}^{\prime}
.
(It is not uncommon to read that
\gamma\bm{v}
is the proper velocity. However, a proper quantity is one measured in a frame where a particle is momentarily at rest. Thus the proper velocity is always zero. We would like to use the term relativistic velocity for
\gamma\bm{v}
, but it is easily confused with relativistic speed meaning a speed close to that of light.
To avoid this misnomer, we refer to
\gamma\bm{v}
with the other attested name in the literature, celerity.)
Then

\displaystyle L=

\displaystyle\ \begin{pmatrix}\gamma_{u^{\prime}}&-\bm{U}^{\prime\mathrm{tr}}\\
-\bm{U}^{\prime}&\mathbbm{1}_{3}+\frac{\bm{U}^{\prime}\bm{U}^{\prime\mathrm{tr}}}{\gamma_{u^{\prime}}+1}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\gamma_{u}&-\bm{U}^{\mathrm{tr}}\\
-\bm{U}&\mathbbm{1}_{3}+\frac{\bm{U}\bm{U}^{\mathrm{tr}}}{\gamma_{u}+1}\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ \begin{pmatrix}\gamma_{u^{\prime}}\gamma_{u}+\bm{U}^{\prime}{\mathchoice{\cdot}{\cdot}{\cdot}{\cdot}}\bm{U}&-\bm{V}^{\mathrm{tr}}\\
+\bm{V}^{\prime\prime}&\mathbbm{1}_{3}+\frac{\bm{U}^{\prime}\bm{U}^{\prime\mathrm{tr}}}{\gamma_{u^{\prime}}+1}+\frac{\bm{U}\bm{U}^{\mathrm{tr}}}{\gamma_{u}+1}+[1+\frac{\bm{U}^{\prime}{\mathchoice{\cdot}{\cdot}{\cdot}{\cdot}}\bm{U}}{(\gamma_{u^{\prime}}+1)(\gamma_{u}+1)}]\bm{U}^{\prime}\bm{U}^{\mathrm{tr}}\end{pmatrix}.

(84)

Here, we put

\displaystyle\bm{V}=

\displaystyle\ \bm{U^{\prime}}+[\gamma_{u^{\prime}}+\frac{\bm{U}^{\prime}{\mathchoice{\cdot}{\cdot}{\cdot}{\cdot}}\bm{U}}{\gamma_{u}+1}]\bm{U},

(85a)

\displaystyle\bm{V}^{\prime\prime}=

\displaystyle\ -\bm{U}-[\gamma_{u}+\frac{\bm{U}^{\prime}{\mathchoice{\cdot}{\cdot}{\cdot}{\cdot}}\bm{U}}{\gamma_{u^{\prime}}+1}]\bm{U}^{\prime}.

(85b)

The vector
\bm{V}
is but the celerity
\bm{U}^{\prime}
in the initial reference frame
\mathfrak{S}
, i.e. the celerity of
\mathfrak{S}^{\prime\prime}
relative to

\mathfrak{S}
. Conversely,
\bm{V}^{\prime\prime}
is the celerity of
\mathfrak{S}
relative to
\mathfrak{S}^{\prime\prime}
.

It is impossible to have
\bm{V}=\bm{V}^{\prime\prime}\neq\bm{0}
.
Indeed, imposing
\bm{V}=\bm{V}^{\prime\prime}
in Eqs. (
85
) we find

\frac{(\gamma_{u}+1)(\gamma_{u^{\prime}}+1)+\bm{U}{\mathchoice{\cdot}{\cdot}{\cdot}{\cdot}}\bm{U}^{\prime}}{\gamma_{u^{\prime}}+1}\bm{U}^{\prime}=-\frac{(\gamma_{u}+1)(\gamma_{u^{\prime}}+1)+\bm{U}{\mathchoice{\cdot}{\cdot}{\cdot}{\cdot}}\bm{U}^{\prime}}{\gamma_{u}+1}\bm{U}.

(86)

Since
(\gamma_{u}+1)(\gamma_{u^{\prime}}+1)+\bm{U}{\mathchoice{\cdot}{\cdot}{\cdot}{\cdot}}\bm{U}^{\prime}\geq\sqrt{(\gamma_{u}+1)(\gamma_{u^{\prime}}+1)}[\sqrt{(\gamma_{u}+1)(\gamma_{u^{\prime}}+1)}-\sqrt{(\gamma_{u}-1)(\gamma_{u^{\prime}}-1)}]>0
, it follows that Eq. (
86
) can hold only if
\bm{U}=-\bm{U}^{\prime}
, which implies
\bm{V}=\bm{V}^{\prime\prime}=\bm{0}
.

The common Lorentz factor of
\bm{V}
and
\bm{V}^{\prime\prime}
is

\gamma_{v}=\gamma_{u^{\prime}}\gamma_{u}+\bm{U}^{\prime}{\mathchoice{\cdot}{\cdot}{\cdot}{\cdot}}\bm{U}.

(87)

We shall use this relation to replace
\bm{U}^{\prime}{\mathchoice{\cdot}{\cdot}{\cdot}{\cdot}}\bm{U}
in terms of the Lorentz factors.
In particular

\displaystyle\bm{V}=

\displaystyle\ \bm{U^{\prime}}+\frac{\gamma_{u^{\prime}}+\gamma_{v}}{\gamma_{u}+1}\bm{U},

(88a)

\displaystyle\bm{V}^{\prime\prime}=

\displaystyle\ -\bm{U}-\frac{\gamma_{u}+\gamma_{v}}{\gamma_{u^{\prime}}+1}\bm{U}^{\prime}.

(88b)

After replacing
\bm{U}
and
\bm{U}^{\prime}
with
\bm{V}
and
\bm{V}^{\prime\prime}
, we have

\displaystyle L=

\displaystyle\ \begin{pmatrix}\gamma_{v}&-\bm{V}^{\mathrm{tr}}\\
\bm{V}^{\prime\prime}&\mathbbm{1}_{3}-\frac{(\gamma_{u}+1)(\gamma_{u^{\prime}}+1)}{(\gamma_{v}-1)(\gamma_{u}+\gamma_{u^{\prime}}+\gamma_{v}+1)^{2}}[\bm{V}-\bm{V}^{\prime\prime}][\bm{V}^{\mathrm{tr}}-\bm{V}^{\prime\prime\mathrm{tr}}]-\frac{\bm{V}^{\prime\prime}\bm{V}^{\mathrm{tr}}}{\gamma_{v}-1}\end{pmatrix}.

(89)

From the polar decomposition

L=R{\mathchoice{\cdot}{\cdot}{\cdot}{\cdot}}B[\bm{v}]=\begin{pmatrix}\gamma_{v}&-\bm{V}^{\mathrm{tr}}\\
-\mathcal{R}{\mathchoice{\cdot}{\cdot}{\cdot}{\cdot}}\bm{V}&\mathcal{R}{\mathchoice{\cdot}{\cdot}{\cdot}{\cdot}}(\mathbbm{1}_{3}+\frac{\bm{V}\bm{V}^{\mathrm{tr}}}{\gamma_{v}+1})\end{pmatrix},

(90)

we have, by comparison with Eq. (
89
),

\mathcal{R}=\mathbbm{1}_{3}-\frac{(\gamma_{u}+1)(\gamma_{u^{\prime}}+1)(\gamma_{v}+1)}{(\gamma_{u}+\gamma_{v}+\gamma_{u^{\prime}}+1)^{2}}[\bm{\hat{v}}-\bm{\hat{v}}^{\prime\prime}][\bm{\hat{v}}^{\mathrm{tr}}-\bm{\hat{v}}^{\prime\prime\mathrm{tr}}]-2\bm{\hat{v}}^{\prime\prime}\bm{\hat{v}}^{\mathrm{tr}}\ ,

(91)

where we divided
\bm{V}
and
\bm{V}^{\prime\prime}
by their common norm
\sqrt{\gamma_{v}^{2}-1}
, introducing the corresponding
unit vectors
\bm{\hat{v}}
and
\bm{\hat{v}}^{\prime\prime}
.

The rotation matrix
\mathcal{R}
is built combining only the identity and direct products of the vectors
\bm{V}
and
\bm{V}^{\prime\prime}
with themselves and with each other, hence, when applied to a vector
\bm{\hat{b}}
orthogonal to both, it returns the same vector. Thus,
\mathcal{R}
must describe a rotation around the axis orthogonal to both
\bm{V}
and
\bm{V}^{\prime\prime}
, unless the velocities are antiparallel, in which case the rotation is the identity.

In other words, if a Lorentz transformation
L
is the product of two boosts, the rotation
\mathcal{R}
defined in Eq. (
79
) is the minimal rotation
taking the velocity
\bm{v}
of the frame
\mathfrak{S}^{\prime\prime}
relative to
\mathfrak{S}
to the velocity
-\bm{v}^{\prime\prime}
, with

\bm{v}^{\prime\prime}
the velocity of the frame
\mathfrak{S}
relative to
\mathfrak{S}^{\prime\prime}
.
Using the definition of the previous subsection
A.5.2
, the rotation is
\mathcal{R}(\bm{\hat{v}},-\bm{\hat{v}}^{\prime\prime})
.
As we have established that it is impossible that
\bm{V}=\bm{V}^{\prime\prime}\neq\bm{0}
,
\mathcal{R}
is never indeterminate of the form
\tfrac{0}{0}
.

Since we have already deduced the axis (or better, the plane) of the rotation, in order to fully characterize the latter we need only provide the angle of rotation
\Theta
.
This is most easily achieved noting that since
\bm{V}^{\prime\prime}=-\mathcal{R}{\mathchoice{\cdot}{\cdot}{\cdot}{\cdot}}\bm{V}
,
applying
\mathcal{R}
in Eq. (
91
) to
\bm{v}
, and imposing that it results in
-\bm{v}^{\prime\prime}
, one finds

1-\frac{(\gamma_{u}+1)(\gamma_{u^{\prime}}+1)(\gamma_{v}+1)}{(\gamma_{u}+\gamma_{u^{\prime}}+\gamma_{v}+1)^{2}}(1-\bm{\hat{v}}{\mathchoice{\cdot}{\cdot}{\cdot}{\cdot}}\bm{\hat{v}}^{\prime\prime})=0\ .

(92)

Thus, the angle
\Theta
of the rotation
\mathcal{R}
and the supplementary angle
\Phi
between the velocity

\bm{v}
of the frame
\mathfrak{S}^{\prime\prime}
relative to
\mathfrak{S}
and the
velocity
\bm{v}^{\prime\prime}
of the frame
\mathfrak{S}
relative to
\mathfrak{S}^{\prime\prime}
satisfy

1+\cos\Theta=1-\cos\Phi=\frac{(\gamma_{u}+\gamma_{u^{\prime}}+\gamma_{v}+1)^{2}}{(\gamma_{u}+1)(\gamma_{u^{\prime}}+1)(\gamma_{v}+1)}\ .

(93)

This formula lacks determining the sense of the rotation, which we provide below.
If we choose the orientation of the rotation axis
\bm{\hat{b}}
to be such that
\bm{V},-\bm{V}^{\prime\prime}=\mathcal{R}\bm{V},\bm{\hat{b}}
form a right-handed triad,

\displaystyle\sin\Theta=

\displaystyle-\frac{(\bm{V}\bm{\times}\bm{V}^{\prime\prime}){\mathchoice{\cdot}{\cdot}{\cdot}{\cdot}}\bm{\hat{b}}}{|\bm{V}^{\prime\prime}||\bm{V}|}=-\frac{(\bm{V}\bm{\times}\bm{V}^{\prime\prime}){\mathchoice{\cdot}{\cdot}{\cdot}{\cdot}}\bm{\hat{b}}}{|\bm{V}|^{2}}=\left[\frac{\gamma_{u^{\prime}}+\gamma_{v}}{\gamma_{u}+1}\frac{\gamma_{u}+\gamma_{v}}{\gamma_{u^{\prime}}+1}-1\right]\frac{(\bm{U}\bm{\times}\bm{U}^{\prime}){\mathchoice{\cdot}{\cdot}{\cdot}{\cdot}}\bm{\hat{b}}}{|\bm{V}|^{2}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ (\gamma_{v}-1)\frac{\gamma_{u}+\gamma_{v}+\gamma_{u^{\prime}}+1}{(\gamma_{u}+1)(\gamma_{u^{\prime}}+1)}\frac{(\bm{U}\bm{\times}\bm{V}){\mathchoice{\cdot}{\cdot}{\cdot}{\cdot}}\bm{\hat{b}}}{{\gamma_{v}^{2}-1}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ \frac{\gamma_{u}+\gamma_{v}+\gamma_{u^{\prime}}+1}{(\gamma_{u}+1)(\gamma_{v}+1)(\gamma_{u^{\prime}}+1)}|\bm{U}||\bm{V}|\sin\phi.

(94)

This is Stapp’s formula
[
28
]
.
Here we used
\bm{U}\bm{\times}\bm{U}^{\prime}=\bm{U}\bm{\times}\bm{V}
, and defined
\phi
as the smaller angle between
\bm{U}
and
\bm{V}
. It is a quantity measurable in

\mathfrak{S}
.
We may express
\gamma_{u^{\prime}}
as a function of quantities observable in the same frame
\mathfrak{S}
, using

\gamma_{u^{\prime}}=\gamma_{u}\gamma_{v}-|\bm{U}||\bm{V}|\cos{\phi}.

(95)

Finally, we find that the angle of the rotation satisfies
[
29
]

\tan{\frac{\Theta}{2}}=\frac{\sin{\Theta}}{1+\cos{\Theta}}=\frac{|\bm{U}||\bm{V}|\sin\phi}{(1+\gamma_{u})(1+\gamma_{v})-|\bm{U}||\bm{V}|\cos{\phi}},

(96)

or better, in terms of hyperbolic functions, recalling that
|\bm{U}|=\sinh{\theta_{u}}
,
\gamma_{u}=\cosh{\theta_{u}}
, etc., with
\theta
the relativistic rapidity,

\tan{\frac{\Theta}{2}}=\frac{\sin\phi}{\coth{\frac{\theta_{u}}{2}}\coth{\frac{\theta_{v}}{2}}-\cos{\phi}}.

(97)

This equation allows to express the rotation in terms of quantities measured in
\mathfrak{S}
.
It is a formula that may be useful whenever there is an actual reference frame
\mathfrak{S}^{\prime}
, the velocity
\bm{u}
of which
\mathfrak{S}
will be able to measure, and it is known that the transformation from
\mathfrak{S}^{\prime}
and
\mathfrak{S}^{\prime\prime}
is a pure boost.

A.6.1
Infinitesimal Thomas–Wigner rotation

A particularly interesting case, applicable to the problem under consideration of an accelerated reference frame,
is when the second velocity
\bm{U}^{\prime}\to\bm{\delta U}^{\prime}
is infinitesimal.
Then the second boost takes the representation of a vector
\vec{C}^{\prime}

to the representation
\vec{C}^{\prime\prime}
in an inertial frame infinitesimally close, the axes of which have been FW transported from the axes of

\mathfrak{S}^{\prime}
, which is but the reference frame that, in the inertial frame
\mathfrak{S}
, is obtained from
\mathfrak{S}^{\prime}
by the velocity change

\bm{v}=\bm{u}+\bm{a}\delta t
(in terms of Newtonian 3–velocities and acceleration).
To first order in
\delta t
,

\gamma_{\delta u^{\prime}}\simeq 1,\qquad\gamma_{v}\simeq\gamma_{u}+\gamma_{u}^{3}\bm{a}(t){\mathchoice{\cdot}{\cdot}{\cdot}{\cdot}}\bm{u}(t)\delta t,

(98)

thus the relativistic velocities appearing in the combined Lorentz transformation satisfy

\displaystyle\bm{V}=

\displaystyle\ \bm{U}+\bm{\delta U}\simeq\bm{U}+\bm{\delta U}^{\prime}+\frac{\gamma_{u}^{3}\bm{a}(t){\mathchoice{\cdot}{\cdot}{\cdot}{\cdot}}\bm{u}(t)\delta t}{\gamma_{u}+1}\bm{U},

(99a)

\displaystyle\bm{V}^{\prime\prime}\simeq

\displaystyle\ -\bm{U}-\gamma_{u}\bm{\delta U}^{\prime},

(99b)

while the Newtonian velocities satisfy

\displaystyle\bm{v}=\ \bm{u}+\bm{\delta u}\simeq

\displaystyle\ \bm{u}+\bm{\delta u}^{\prime}-\frac{\gamma_{u}^{2}\bm{a}(t){\mathchoice{\cdot}{\cdot}{\cdot}{\cdot}}\bm{u}(t)\delta t}{\gamma_{u}+1}\bm{u},

(100a)

\displaystyle\bm{v}^{\prime\prime}\simeq

\displaystyle\ -\bm{u}-\bm{\delta u}^{\prime}-\bm{a}(t){\mathchoice{\cdot}{\cdot}{\cdot}{\cdot}}\bm{u}(t)\delta t\bm{u},

(100b)

and the angle of rotation in Eq. (
97
) becomes

\delta\Theta\simeq(\gamma_{u}-1)\delta\phi,

(101)

with
\delta\phi
the infinitesimal angle between
\bm{u}
and
\bm{v}=\bm{u}+\bm{a}\delta t
.
The axis of the rotation is orthogonal to
\bm{u}
and
\bm{u}+\bm{a}\delta t
, hence it is in the direction
\bm{u}\bm{\times}\bm{a}
.
In terms of boosts and rotation matrices, we have:

B[\bm{\delta v^{\prime}}]B[\bm{u}]=R[\bm{\delta\Theta}]B[\bm{u+\delta u}],

(102)

which we may rewrite as

R^{-1}[\bm{\delta\Theta}]B[\bm{\delta v^{\prime}}]=B[\bm{u+\delta u}]B[-\bm{u}],

(103)

Thus, we have rederived Thomas’s correct (up to a sign) statement
[
2
]
that a boost
B[-\bm{u}]
followed by
B[\bm{u+\delta u}]
is equivalent to an infinitesimal boost
B[\bm{\delta v^{\prime}}]

followed by an infinitesimal rotation
\bm{\delta\Theta}=-\frac{\gamma_{u}-1}{u^{2}}\bm{u}\bm{\times}\bm{\delta u}
.

Appendix B
The meaning of Thomas–Wigner rotation revealed through commutative diagrams

The pitfalls that lead to the error in Thomas precession could be avoided by using commutative diagrams for Lorentz transformations as a visual aid.
It is well known that a Lorentz transformation from an inertial frame
\mathfrak{S}
to an inertial frame
\mathfrak{S}^{\prime}
moving with velocity
\bm{v}
relative to

\mathfrak{S}
can be decomposed as the product of a boost and a rotation in two ways:
L=RB[\bm{v}]
and
L=B[-\bm{v}^{\prime}]R
, with
\bm{v}^{\prime}
the velocity of
\mathfrak{S}
relative to
\mathfrak{S}^{\prime}
.
We apply these decompositions to the series of Lorentz transformations from the lab frame to the comoving FW transported frames
\mathfrak{S}^{\prime}_{t}

the origins of which coincide with the position of an accelerated particle. Some care must be taken because dynamical quantities measured in the accelerated
frame do not coincide with corresponding quantities measured in the instantaneous rest frame
\mathfrak{S}^{\prime}_{t}
, since they involve incremental ratios
of quantities measured in two different inertial frames
\mathfrak{S}^{\prime}_{t}
and
\mathfrak{S}^{\prime}_{t+\delta t}
.

In the diagrams, boosts are indicated by dashed straight lines, rotations by dotted curved lines, and
general Lorentz transformations by full curved lines. Two additional frames appear: a comoving frame, the axes of which rotate relative to the comoving FW
frame, which we will call the comoving boosted frame
\mathfrak{S}^{\prime*}
; and a frame with the origin at rest in the lab, but rotating axes, which we will refer to as the rotating lab frame
\mathfrak{S}^{*}
.
In the literature, the distinction between the FW frame and the rotating frame has been seldom recognized, with a few exceptions, as for instance the excellent book by Garg
[
7
]
.

. Thus, when stating the velocity of an inertial frame relative to an accelerated frame, one must specify which point of the inertial frame is being considered.
The rotation of the axes of
\mathfrak{S}^{\prime}_{t}
relative to the axes of
\mathfrak{S}^{\prime*}_{t}
is, instead

\bm{\omega}^{\prime*}_{t^{\prime}}=\frac{\gamma_{t}}{\gamma_{t}+1}\bm{v}^{\prime*}_{t}\bm{\times}\bm{a}^{\prime*}_{t}.

(107)

In terms of quantities measured in the lab frame

\bm{\omega}^{\prime*}_{t^{\prime}}=-\frac{\gamma^{3}_{t}}{\gamma_{t}+1}\bm{v}_{t}\bm{\times}\bm{a}_{t}.

(108)

Equation (
108
) is the source of a common misunderstanding on the Thomas–Wigner rotation:
the rate on the lhs is observed in the boosted comoving frame of the particle, but the rhs is expressed as
a function of quantities measured in the lab frame.
This means merely that an inertial observer can predict what the accelerated observer will be measuring.
However, a common mistake is to consider the lhs as if it was a quantity observed in the lab frame, and hence
consider the rate in the lab frame,
\bm{\omega}^{\prime*}_{t}=\bm{\delta\Phi}/dt=-\frac{\gamma^{2}_{t}}{\gamma_{t}+1}\bm{v}_{t}\bm{\times}\bm{a}_{t}
. This is the equation for Thomas precession, as most commonly
derived.

Finally, we have a quantity which is measured in the lab frame, given by Eq. (

The rotation (
111
) guarantees that the Lorentz transformation from the rotating frame to the FW accelerated frame is a Lorentz boost.

In summary, the Thomas–Wigner rotation
describes the instantaneous rotation of a frame relative to another.
It does not describe the rotation of a vector transported by an accelerated particle as seen in the lab frame,
nor an additional Coriolis rotation seen by the accelerated particle.
Rather, it describes the Coriolis rotation observed either in a rotating frame with the origin at rest relative to the lab frame, or in a frame comoving with the particle but the axes
of which are not FW transported.
Therefore, the Thomas–Wigner rotation rate while strictly related to the Thomas precession — defined as the rotation observed in the lab frame of the spin of a particle due
only to relativistic kinematics effects — does not coincide with it,
and thus can not possibly account for the energy levels of an atom at rest in the lab frame.

Appendix C
The interplay of Thomas–Wigner rotation and Lorentz contraction in the spin dynamics

First of all, we establish a simple fact: pure Lorentz boosts imply a nonlinear rotation.
Indeed, let us consider first a vector
\bm{P}
associated to a particle initially at rest in the lab frame.
If the particle is boosted by a velocity
\bm{\Delta v}
, the vector

\bm{P}
will contract in the direction of the velocity, as illustrated in
the figure
9
. Therefore, the vector
\bm{P}
will rotate by an angle
\Delta\theta
, depending on its orientation relative to the velocity boost.

Figure 9
:
A 3D vector which is represented as the green arrow in a reference frame, is represented by the red arrow in a new reference frame boosted with velocity
\bm{v}
(black arrow) with respect to the former frame.
In general, the red vector will be rotated relative to the green vector, because of Lorentz contraction.
Here, we chose a Lorentz factor
\gamma=2
for illustrative purposes.

The angle
\Delta\theta
is zero when
\bm{P}
is normal, parallel, or antiparallel to the direction of the boost, and it reaches a maximum when the angle
\alpha
between
\bm{P}
and
\bm{\Delta v}
satisfies

\cos(2\alpha)=\frac{\gamma_{\Delta v}-1}{\gamma_{\Delta v}+1}
, which yields
\alpha\to 0,\pi
in the ultra–relativistic limit, and
\alpha\simeq\pi/4
in the non–relativistic limit (Fig.
10
).

Figure 10
:
The angle of rotation
\Delta\theta
of a vector
\bm{P}
due to Lorentz contraction as a function of the angle
\alpha
that the vector makes with the velocity of the boost, for several values of
\gamma
.

This rotation combines with the rotation of the vector due to external influences, and also with the Thomas–Wigner rotation.
Precisely, we have shown that the Lorentz transformation from the lab frame to the FW rest frame of the particle is
L_{\mathrm{FW}}(t)=R_{\mathrm{TW}}(t)B[\bm{v}_{t}]
.
Hence, since in the rest frame the spin is represented as the 4–vector:

\{0,\bm{S}^{\prime}\}
, the corresponding representation in the lab frame is

\begin{pmatrix}S^{0}(t)\\
\bm{S}_{t}\end{pmatrix}=B[-\bm{v}_{t}]R^{\mathrm{tr}}_{\mathrm{TW}}(t)\begin{pmatrix}0\\
\bm{S}^{\prime}_{t}\end{pmatrix},

(112)

where we used the fact that the inverse of a Lorentz boost
B[\bm{v}]
is

B^{-1}[\bm{v}]=B[-\bm{v}]
, and the inverse of a rotation is

R^{-1}=R^{\mathrm{tr}}
.

The relevant spatial part is

\bm{S}_{t}=\mathcal{R}^{\mathrm{tr}}_{\mathrm{TW}}(t)\bm{S}^{\prime}_{t}+(\gamma_{t}-1)\bm{\hat{v}}_{t}\bm{\hat{v}}_{t}^{\mathrm{tr}}\mathcal{R}^{\mathrm{tr}}_{\mathrm{TW}}(t)\bm{S}^{\prime}_{t}.

(113)

Here, we used Eqs. (
74

The second term on the rhs describes the dilation of the vector
\bm{S}
along the velocity axis.

For the simultaneity–corrected vector
\bm{T}_{t}
, instead

\bm{T}_{t}=\mathcal{R}^{\mathrm{tr}}_{\mathrm{TW}}(t)\bm{S}^{\prime}_{t}+(\gamma^{-1}_{t}-1)\bm{\hat{v}}_{t}\bm{\hat{v}}_{t}^{\mathrm{tr}}\mathcal{R}^{\mathrm{tr}}_{\mathrm{TW}}(t)\bm{S}^{\prime}_{t}.

(114)

The second term on the rhs describes the contraction of the vector
\bm{T}

Let us assume temporarily that we could neglect the effects of Lorentz contraction/dilation.
Then

\bm{S}_{t}\simeq\bm{T}_{t}\simeq\mathcal{R}^{\mathrm{tr}}_{\mathrm{TW}}(t)\bm{S}^{\prime}_{t}.

(115)

In the comoving FW frame, the spin
\bm{S}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\left[\Omega_{\mathrm{L}}(t)+\Omega_{\mathrm{TW}}(t)\right]\bm{S}_{t}=\left[\bm{\omega}_{\mathrm{L}}(t)+\bm{\omega}_{\mathrm{TW}}(t)\right]\times\bm{S}_{t}.
