Adaptive Time Series Forecasting with Markovian Variance Switching
Baptiste Abélès, Joseph de Vilmarest, Olivier Wintemberger

Abstract
Adaptive time series forecasting is essential for prediction under regime changes. Several classical methods assume linear Gaussian state space model (LGSSM) with variances constant in time. However, there are many real-world processes that cannot be captured by such models. We consider a state-space model with Markov switching variances. Such dynamical systems are usually intractable because of their computational complexity increasing exponentially with time; Variational Bayes (VB) techniques have been applied to this problem. In this paper, we propose a new way of estimating variances based on online learning theory; we adapt expert aggregation methods to learn the variances over time. We apply the proposed method to synthetic data and to the problem of electricity load forecasting. We show that this method is robust to misspecification and outperforms traditional expert aggregation.

Introduction

Time series forecasting is a fundamental issue. A few examples are weather forecasts for farmers, sales and inventory forecasting in retail, price and cost forecasting for industrials.
In this paper, a special motivation is electricity load forecasting, a crucial task for grid operators as the production must balance the consumption in real time
[
4
,
15
]
.

State-space models have been widely employed to model the temporal behavior of data. In particular, linear Gaussian state-space models (LGSSMs) yield tractable posterior distributions obtained by the Kalman filter (KF)
[
19
]
. Many situations are filled with non linearity both in dynamics and observation equation. A vast literature has been devoted to deal with non-linear dynamical systems, e.g. the extended Kalman filter (EKF)
[
10
]
which essentially consists to linearize the model using Taylor first-order expansion, and the unscentend Kalman filter (UKF)
[
30
]
which is radically different and consists to apply the unscented transformation to both prediction and filtering step.

Although the inference in LGSSMs is known
[
19
]
, the Kalman filter crucially depends on the variances of the observation and state noises. The choice of these variances may be seen as the parametrization of a second-order gradient descent algorithm, once we remark the equivalence of the Kalman recursions and gradient updates
[
26
,
8
]
. The most widely-employed setting of these variances is time-invariant
[
11
,
10
,
6
]
, and the natural choice of these variances is maximum-likelihood. However, constant variances mean a smooth evolution of the environment, while in some cases there are changes of regime. To tackle that problem, various methods have been introduced, often under the paradigm adaptive Kalman filter
[
22
,
16
,
17
]
.

Alternatively the switching Kalman filter (SKF)
[
24
]
considers several linear dynamic models and try to combine them with a switching mechanism (such as a Markov chain), selecting over time one among several regimes. This latter approach is of a particular interest in many real-world problems that cannot be approximated with a single regime, and where multiple behaviors are needed instead. For instance, time series forecasting during a period covering stable as well as unstable moments motivates switches between different regimes. Our application to electricity load forecasting is one of these, as we include very unstable periods such as lockdowns due to the coronavirus in 2020, during which it was best to switch to a more reactive method
[
25
]
.

The complexity of these hybrid dynamical systems,
i.e
. systems that combine discrete and continuous valued latent variables, increases exponentially with time. State-of-the art approaches propose to learn a switching linear dynamical system using Variational Bayesian (VB) techniques
[
2
]
. However, VB approaches have some limits under model misspecification
[
31
]
.

In this paper, we propose to consider the SKF as an aggregation of experts, where each expert corresponds to a different regime. We apply online learning techniques to track the best expert as in
[
14
,
5
]
. After introducing various methods in Section
2
, we define our algorithm in Section
3
, and we provide a regret bound in expectation. Then, in Section
4
, we conduct various experiments on synthetic data, as well as a real data set, forecasting the French national electricity load. We study well-specified and misspecified experiments and we show that this new framework is robust to misspecification and outperforms Kalman aggregation as in
[
1
]
.

Background

Notations

Given probability measures
p
and
q
with a common dominating measure
\mu
, we denote the Kullback-Leibler divergence as
D(p||q):=\int p\log(p/q)d\mu
. For any positive integer
t
we denote
[t]:=\{1,...,t\}
and
\mathcal{F}_{t}:=\sigma(\boldsymbol{x}_{1},y_{1},...,\boldsymbol{x}_{t},y_{t},\boldsymbol{x}_{t+1})
is the sigma algebra generated by the pair
(\boldsymbol{x}_{i},y_{i})_{i=1}^{t}
and
\boldsymbol{x}_{t+1}
. For some
\mu,\sigma\in\mathbb{R}
, the notation
\mathcal{N}(.|\mu,\sigma)
refers to as the normal distribution with mean
\mu
and variance
\sigma^{2}
(we keep the same notation if
\boldsymbol{\mu}
is a vector and
\Sigma
is the covariance matrix associated). We denote
\Delta(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)
as the diagonal matrix in
\mathbb{R}^{3\times 3}
with coefficients
\alpha,\beta,\gamma
.

Let
y_{t}\in\mathbb{R}
be the variable of interest,
\boldsymbol{x}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
a vector of explainable variables and
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
the vector of latent variables at time
t
. The classical LGSSM writes :

\displaystyle y_{t}=\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t}^{T}\boldsymbol{x}_{t}+\epsilon_{t},\quad\text{where }\epsilon_{t}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{t}^{2}),

(1)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t+1}=\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t}+\nu_{t},\quad\text{where }\nu_{t}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,Q_{t}),

(2)

Our goal is to estimate jointly the state and variances knowing the past that we denote
\boldsymbol{\hat{\theta}}_{t|t-1}=\mathbb{E}[\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t}|\mathcal{F}_{t-1}]
and
P_{t|t-1}=\mathbb{E}[(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t}-\boldsymbol{\hat{\theta}}_{t|t-1})(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t}-\boldsymbol{\hat{\theta}}_{t|t-1})^{T}|\mathcal{F}_{t-1}]
. The well-known KF gives the exact updates if
\sigma_{t}^{2}
and
Q_{t}
are known :

Theorem 1
.

(Kalman Filter)

\displaystyle P_{t|t}=P_{t|t-1}-\frac{P_{t|t-1}\boldsymbol{x}_{t}\boldsymbol{x}_{t}^{T}P_{t|t-1}}{\boldsymbol{x}_{t}^{T}P_{t|t-1}\boldsymbol{x}_{t}+\sigma_{t}^{2}},

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\hat{\theta}}_{t|t}=\boldsymbol{\hat{\theta}}_{t|t-1}-\frac{P_{t|t}}{\sigma_{t}^{2}}\left(\boldsymbol{x}_{t}(\boldsymbol{\hat{\theta}}_{t|t-1}^{T}\boldsymbol{x}_{t}-y_{t})\right),

\displaystyle P_{t+1|t}=P_{t|t}+Q_{t},

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\hat{\theta}}_{t+1|t}=\boldsymbol{\hat{\theta}}_{t|t}.

Usual choice in the literature is to assume that the variances are time-invariant
[
10
,
6
]
and apply the Expectation-Maximisation (EM) algorithm. A second paradigm which has frequently been called Adaptive Kalman Filter (AKF)
[
22
]
consists in estimating variances over time and applying the Kalman recursive updates using such adaptive variances.

A more recent approach consists in finding the complete posterior of both state and variances
[
9
]
.
The authors assume that
Q_{t},\sigma_{t}^{2}
are random variables and try to estimate the joint distribution of the state and the variances in a dynamical way. They assume a model on the dynamics of the variances; then, starting from a prior on
(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{1},\sigma_{1}^{2},Q^{(1)})
, they propose to recursively estimate the posterior distribution of
(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t},\sigma_{t}^{2},Q_{t})
.
However there is no natural parametric class of distributions on the joint distribution such that the posterior remains in the class considered. To handle this issue, they apply the Variational Bayesian (VB) approach
[
27
]
; it consists in approximating the posterior with the best factorized form in the sense of the minimal Kullback-Leibler divergence.
We believe that the limits of such methods lie on too much degree of freedom and we aim to constrain the model.

To reduce the degree of freedom of the problem, one can assume the existence of a hidden Markov chain
(Z_{t})\in[K]
parameterizing the dynamics of the classical LGSSM. At each time
t
,
\sigma_{t}^{2}=\sigma_{Z_{t}}^{2},Q_{t}=Q_{Z_{t}}
.
This model writes :

\displaystyle y_{t}=\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t}^{T}\boldsymbol{x}_{t}+\epsilon_{t},\quad\text{where }\epsilon_{t}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{Z_{t}}^{2}),

(3)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t+1}=\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t}+\nu_{t},\quad\text{where }\nu_{t}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,Q_{Z_{t}}),

(4)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(Z_{t+1}=k)=\sum_{i=1}^{K}M_{ik}\mathbb{P}(Z_{t}=i),

(5)

where
M_{ik}=\mathbb{P}(Z_{t+1}=k|Z_{t}=i)
for
i,k\in[K]
is an entry of the transition matrix
\boldsymbol{M}\in\mathbb{R}^{K\times K}
.

However, the complexity of the posterior distribution of such dynamical systems grows exponentially in time, making it intractable. Indeed the posterior writes :

\displaystyle p(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{1:t},Z_{1:t}|\mathcal{F}_{t})

\displaystyle\propto\pi(Z_{1})\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{1}|\boldsymbol{\hat{\theta}}_{1},P_{1})\prod_{s=1}^{t}\mathcal{N}(y_{s}|\boldsymbol{\theta}_{s}^{T}\boldsymbol{x}_{s},\sigma_{Z_{s}}^{2})

\displaystyle\prod_{s=1}^{t-1}\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{s+1}|\boldsymbol{\theta}_{s},Q_{Z_{s}})M_{Z_{s+1},Z_{s}},

where
\pi(Z_{1})
denotes the prior law on
Z_{1}
.
Several approaches, referred to as
Generalized Pseudo Bayesian algorithm

[
24
]
, approximate this exponential mixture of Gaussians with a smaller mixture of Gaussians.
Recent works use VB techniques to approximate the posterior
[
2
]
. The main idea is to look for a tractable approximation of the posterior. If we denote
p_{\mathcal{F}_{t}}
the posterior we look for
(q_{Z}^{*},q_{\theta}^{*})\in\mathcal{D}_{t}
such that :

(q_{Z}^{*},q_{\theta}^{*})\in\arg\min_{(q_{Z},q_{\theta})\in\mathcal{D}_{t}}D(q_{Z}\times q_{\theta}\ ||\ p_{\mathcal{F}_{t}}),

where
\mathcal{D}_{t}
is the parametric class considered. We provide the derivation and results of this method that performs poorly in our experiments in supplementary materials. An important limitation of this method
is the lack of robustness to misspecification
[
31
]
.

To overcome this problem we propose a new approach to adaptive variance estimation relying on expert aggregation. Aggregation of experts is a well-established framework in machine learning
[
5
,
29
]
. In particular, it yields excellent results in the context of electricity load forecasting
[
1
]
. Our work follows this classical setting, we consider a learner,
K
experts and a loss function
\ell:\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}\times\mathcal{Y}\to\mathbb{R}
where
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}
is the space of experts predictions and
\mathcal{Y}
is the space of observation. At each time
t
, each expert
k\in[K]
predicts
\boldsymbol{e}_{k,t}\in\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}
. The learner picks a distribution
\boldsymbol{p}_{t}
over the set of experts’ predictions, predicts
\boldsymbol{e}_{t}=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\boldsymbol{p}_{k,t}\boldsymbol{e}_{k,t}\in\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}
and observes his loss
\ell_{t}:=\ell(\boldsymbol{e}_{t},y_{t})
. The learner has also access to the loss of each expert at time
t
, that we denote
\ell_{k,t}:=\ell(\boldsymbol{e}_{k,t},y_{t})
. The goal of the learner is to minimize his cumulative loss on sequence of length
T
. A first way to measure his performance is to compare his cumulative loss with other expert during the sequence.

Definition 1
.

We define the regret with respect to the expert
k
for a sequence of length
T
as :

R_{T}(k)=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\ell_{t}-\ell_{k,t}

2.1
Exponential-Weight-Average (EWA) algorithm for Kalman Filter

To better understand our framework, we present the famous EWA to show how a learner can optimize his strategy to control his regret.

We make a small remark here that is we will essentially focus on the variance of the state Equation
4
. Indeed Kalman Filter gives exactly the same update for the pair
(\sigma^{2},Q)
and
(1,Q^{*})
with
Q^{*}=\frac{Q}{\sigma^{2}}
(refer to
[
3
]
).

Let’s consider the notations of the state space model and assume each expert is a KF derived with
Q_{t}^{*}=Q^{*(k)}
which starting from some
\boldsymbol{\hat{\theta}}_{0|0}
yields the prediction
\boldsymbol{\hat{\theta}}_{t|t-1}^{(k)}
of the state at time
t
, for
t\in[T]
. In this case, let’s identify the space of experts as the observation space ,
i.e

\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}=\mathcal{Y}
and each expert predicts
\boldsymbol{e}_{k,t}=\hat{y}_{k,t}
where
\hat{y}_{k,t}=\boldsymbol{\hat{\theta}}_{t|t-1}^{(k)T}\boldsymbol{x}_{t}
.
We consider the quadratic loss
\ell_{k,t}=(y_{t}-\hat{y}_{k,t})^{2}
.

select
\boldsymbol{p}_{t}

for

for

\boldsymbol{p}_{t+1}(k)=\frac{\boldsymbol{p}_{t}(k)e^{-\eta\ell_{k,t}}}{\sum_{j=1}^{K}\boldsymbol{p}_{t}(j)e^{-\eta\ell_{j,t}}}

A first important result is given by the following proposition.

Proposition 1
.

Assume that the loss function
\ell
is convex and takes value in
[0,1]
:

R_{T}^{*}\leq\frac{\log(K)}{\eta}+\frac{\eta T}{8}\,.

Refer to
[
5
]
, Theorem 2.2 for a proof.

Algorithm
1
belongs to the so-called class of
Static Expert algorithm
which tend to converge to the best expert. However in our case since we assume switches; we look for aggregation algorithms able to switch from one expert to the other.

2.2
Fixed-Share (FS) algorithm

For this reason we should consider a more refined measure of performance.

Definition 2
.

Let
k^{T}=(k_{1},...,k_{T})
be a sequence of experts we can define the regret with respect to this sequence as:

R_{T}(k^{T})=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\ell_{t}-\ell_{k_{t},t}

and

R_{T}^{*}=\sup_{k^{T}}R_{T}(k^{T})

A first and naïve approach would be to consider each sequence as a new expert and try to recover the best expert on the set of every possible sequence of expert using EWA algorithm starting from a prior distribution
p_{1}(k^{T})
over this set. However in practice we will face a problem of intractability since we need to store and update
O(K^{T})
weights to compare to
R_{T}(k^{T})
.
The problem of controlling the regret with respect to sequences of experts, known as tracking the best expert, was introduced by Herbster and Warmuth
[
14
]
, who proposed the simple FS algorithm with good regret guarantees.
A key fact highlighted in
[
28
]
(section 3.3) is that FS, can be interpreted as the EWA algorithm on sequences of experts under a markov prior.

Below we present a generalization of FS named as Markov-Hedge (MH) algorithm which starting from markov chain prior
\boldsymbol{p}_{1}(k_{1},...,k_{T})=\pi(k_{1})M_{k_{2}|k_{1}}...M_{k_{T}|k_{T-1}}
collapses to efficient algorithm with bounded regret
[
23
]
. For
t\in[T]
,
M_{k_{t}|k_{t-1}}
correspond to the entry of the transition matrix
\boldsymbol{M}\in\mathbb{R}^{K\times K}
of the markov chain.

Algorithm 2

Markov-Hedge Algorithm

Require:

\boldsymbol{M}=(M_{k_{t}|k_{t-1}}),\boldsymbol{p}_{1}=K^{-1}\mathbf{1}_{K}

for

t\in[T]

do

select
\boldsymbol{p}_{t}
, forecast
\hat{y}_{t}=\sum_{k=1}^{K}p_{k,t}\hat{y}_{k,t}

derive loss
\ell_{t}
and
\ell_{k,t}
for
k\in[K]

for
k\in[K]
,update:

\boldsymbol{p}_{t+1}(k)=\frac{\boldsymbol{p}_{t}(k)e^{-\eta\ell_{k,t}}}{\sum_{j=1}^{K}\boldsymbol{p}_{t}(j)e^{-\eta\ell_{j,t}}}

update:
\boldsymbol{p}_{t+1}=M\boldsymbol{p}_{t+1}

i.e

for
k\in[K]
:

\boldsymbol{p}_{t+1}(k)=\sum_{j=1}^{K}M_{k|j}\boldsymbol{p}_{t+1}(j)

end

for

We have the following proposition :

Proposition 2
.

Algorithm Markov-Hedge, with initial distribution
\boldsymbol{p}_{1}(k_{1},...,k_{T})=\pi(k_{1})M_{k_{2}|k_{1}}...M_{k_{T}|k_{T-1}}
and transition matrix
\boldsymbol{M}\in\mathbb{R}^{K\times K}
, guarantees the following regret bound. For
T\geq 1
and any sequence
k^{T}=(k_{1},...,k_{T})
:

R_{T}(k^{T})\leq\frac{1}{\eta}\log\frac{1}{\pi(k_{1})}+\frac{1}{\eta}\sum_{t=2}^{T}\log\frac{1}{M_{k_{t}|k_{t-1}}}+\frac{\eta T}{8}\,.

Methodology

Following the remark made in Subsection

we will first consider
\sigma_{t}^{2}=1
for
t\in[T]
.

3.1
Well-specified setting

As we saw in Section
2
what is usually done
[
1
]
is to perform an aggregation over the space of observations. This means that we consider our
K
experts as
K
Kalman Filters derived with constant variances
Q_{t}=Q^{(k)},\sigma_{t}^{2}=1
. The expert
k
predicts
\hat{y}_{k,t}
for all
t\in[T]
and updates his weights using Algorithm
2
. The main contribution of this paper is to apply the aggregation framework over the space of variances, as displayed by diagrams in Figure
1
.

Figure 1:
Aggregation of Kalman (left) versus Kalman with aggregation of variances (right).

The algorithm that we design is a Kalman filter that competes the best covariance matrix at each time
t
using expert aggregation on
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Q}}=\{Q^{(1)},...,Q^{(K)}\}
. We consider the space of experts
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}=\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Q}}
so that at time
t
, each expert
k
computes
P_{t|t-1}^{(k)},\boldsymbol{\hat{\theta}}_{t|t-1}^{(k)}
using equations of Theorem
1
and predicts:

\hat{y}_{k,t}=\boldsymbol{\hat{\theta}}_{t|t-1}^{(k)T}\boldsymbol{x}_{t}.

The learner observes the loss
\ell_{k,t}=(y_{t}-\hat{y}_{k,t})^{2}
of each expert and updates his weights as in Algorithm
2
. Then he selects
Q^{(k)}
with probability
\boldsymbol{p}_{t}(k)
to compute
P_{t+1|t},\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t+1|t}
given his previous predictions
P_{t|t-1},\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t|t-1}
and predicts:

\hat{y}_{t+1}=\boldsymbol{\hat{\theta}}_{t+1|t}^{T}\boldsymbol{x}_{t+1}.

This yields the randomized version of our algorithm, Kalman Filter Markov-Hedge (KFMH), detailed in Algorithm
3
.

Algorithm 3

Randomized KFMH

Require:

\boldsymbol{p}_{1}=K^{-1}\mathbf{1}_{K},\boldsymbol{M}=(M_{i,j})_{i,j=1}^{K}

for

do

and
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{t-1}

for

,
\boldsymbol{\hat{\theta}}_{t|t-1}^{(k)}

for

update:

\boldsymbol{p}_{t+1}(k)=\frac{\boldsymbol{p}_{t}(k)e^{-\eta\ell_{k,t}}}{\sum_{j=1}^{K}\boldsymbol{p}_{t}(j)e^{-\eta\ell_{j,t}}}

update:
\boldsymbol{p}_{t+1}=M\boldsymbol{p}_{t+1}

Q_{t}=Q^{(k)}
with proba

The main parameters of this algorithm are
\eta
and
M
. To reduce the complexity of the problem we will assume in our experience that
M
only depends of a parameter
\alpha
such that :

M_{\alpha}=\begin{pmatrix}1-\alpha&\frac{\alpha}{K-1}&\dots&\frac{\alpha}{K-1}\\
\frac{\alpha}{K-1}&\ddots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\vdots&\ddots&\ddots&\frac{\alpha}{K-1}\\
\frac{\alpha}{K-1}&\dots&\frac{\alpha}{K-1}&1-\alpha\end{pmatrix}

We can then calibrate
\eta
and
\alpha
by grid-search based on the best cumulative loss.

Proposition 3
.

Algorithm KFMH randomized, with transition matrix
M_{\alpha}
and fined tuning for
\eta
and
\alpha
, satisfies:

\mathbb{E}[R_{T}^{*}]\leq{\cal O}(\sqrt{ST\log T})\,,\qquad T\geq 1,

where
S
is the number of regime switching of the SKF competitor.

Proof.

Without loss of generality we consider that the random selection at step
t
following the distribution
{\bf p}_{t}
is independent of
{\cal F}_{t}
but included in
{\cal F}_{t+1}
.
The tower property yields the identity

\mathbb{E}\Big[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\ell_{t}\Big]=\mathbb{E}\Big[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{E}[\ell_{t}\mid{\cal F}_{t}]\Big]=\mathbb{E}\Big[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\sum_{k=1}^{K}p_{t}(k)\ell_{t,k}\Big]\,.

Rewriting the regret bound of Proposition
2
for the linearized loss and for the specific transition matrix
M_{\alpha}
, we obtain

\mathbb{E}[R_{T}]\leq\dfrac{S+1}{\eta}\log\dfrac{K}{\alpha}+\dfrac{T-S}{\eta}\log\Big(\dfrac{1}{1-\alpha}\Big)+\dfrac{\eta T}{8}\,.

The desired result follows after an optimisation of the regret bound in
\alpha
and
\eta
.
∎

3.2
Misspecified setting

In well-specified cases, the SKF competitor is optimal and has small cumulative quadratic losses, see e.g.
[
1
]
. However, in real-world applications one needs to be more robust to specification. We introduce a new setting where the prediction is provided by a Kalman filter acting on a state equation driven by the covariance matrix

\hat{Q}_{t}=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\boldsymbol{p}_{t}(k)Q^{(k)}\,.

The interpretation is to transfer the confidence on each experts computed by
\boldsymbol{p}_{t}
directly on the state Equation
4
. The gain is to design an estimation of the state variance and a forecaster that is a KF and not an aggregation. One also has to design each expert
k
in a different way than pure independent KF. Indeed, while in Algorithm
3
each expert was computing his prediction independently from the learner; now the expert starts from a prior knowledge
P_{t-2|t-2},\boldsymbol{\hat{\theta}}_{t-1|t-2}
of learner’s previous predictions
and derives new KF updates as follows:

\displaystyle P_{t-1|t-2}^{(k)}=P_{t-2|t-2}+Q^{(k)},

\displaystyle P_{t-1|t-1}^{(k)}=P_{t-1|t-2}^{(k)}-\frac{P_{t-1|t-2}^{(k)}\boldsymbol{x}_{t-1}\boldsymbol{x}_{t-1}^{T}P_{t-1|t-2}^{(k)}}{\boldsymbol{x}_{t-1}^{T}P_{t-1|t-2}^{(k)}\boldsymbol{x}_{t-1}+1},

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\hat{\theta}}_{t-1|t-1}^{(k)}=\boldsymbol{\hat{\theta}}_{t-1|t-2}-P_{t-1|t-1}^{(k)}\boldsymbol{x}_{t}(\boldsymbol{\hat{\theta}}_{t-1|t-2}^{T}\boldsymbol{x}_{t}-y_{t}),

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\hat{\theta}}_{t|t-1}^{(k)}=\boldsymbol{\hat{\theta}}_{t-1|t-1}^{(k)},

to predict:

\hat{y}_{k,t}=\boldsymbol{\hat{\theta}}_{t|t-1}^{T(k)}\boldsymbol{x}_{t}.

Then the learner observes the loss
\ell_{k,t}
of each expert, updates the weights and computes
\boldsymbol{\hat{\theta}}_{t+1|t},P_{t+1|t}
applying KF updates of Theorem
1
with
\hat{Q}_{t+1}
.

3.3
Choice of loss and optimization over
\sigma

In Section
2
we considered the square loss. However, standard methods optimizing time-invariant variances in state-space models optimize the likelihood as the natural objective function
[
10
,
6
]
. This leads us to consider the log-likelihood as loss function in our algorithm. Using the chain rule, the log-likelihood writes:

\displaystyle\log p(y_{1:T},\boldsymbol{x}_{1:T}|Q_{1:T-1},\sigma_{1:T}^{2})

\displaystyle\qquad=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\log p(y_{t}|\boldsymbol{x}_{t},y_{1:t-1},\boldsymbol{x}_{1:t-1},Q_{1:(t-1)},\sigma_{1:t}^{2})+c

\displaystyle\qquad=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\ell_{t}(\boldsymbol{\hat{\theta}}_{t|t-1},P_{t|t-1},\sigma_{t}^{2},y_{t})+c\,.

where
c
is a constant term and the loss function writes:

\displaystyle\ell_{t}(\boldsymbol{\hat{\theta}}_{t|t-1},P_{t|t-1},\sigma_{t}^{2},y_{t})

\displaystyle\quad=\log\mathcal{N}(y_{t}|\boldsymbol{\hat{\theta}}_{t|t-1}^{T}\boldsymbol{x}_{t},\sigma_{t}^{2}+\boldsymbol{x}_{t}^{T}P_{t|t-1}\boldsymbol{x}_{t})\,.

The dependence of this loss to
Q_{t-1}
is hidden in the estimated state distribution represented by
\boldsymbol{\hat{\theta}}_{t\mid t-1},P_{t\mid t-1}
.
The peculiarity of the log-likelihood is that it strongly depends of
\sigma_{t}^{2}
. This means that the assumption
\sigma_{t}^{2}=1
is too restrictive. In our experiments we observed that it did not perform well to apply the expert framework to both variances. We believe that it’s due to the fact that
Q_{t-1}
and
\sigma_{t}^{2}
are strongly related. Furthermore, estimating observation noise variance is an easier task compared to the estimation of the covariance matrix of a latent process. Therefore we directly apply a gradient descent algorithm, namely ADAM
[
20
]

We propose another improvement of Algorithm
4

[

\tau

This replacement of a window

\alpha\ll 1

\alpha=0.01

Formally, instead of starting from

\boldsymbol{\hat{\theta}}_{t-\tau-1|t-\tau-1},P_{t-\tau-1|t-\tau-1}

\boldsymbol{\hat{\theta}}_{t|t-1}^{(k)},P_{t\mid t-1}^{(k)}

where the dependency in
\tau

\boldsymbol{\hat{\theta}}_{t|t-1}^{(k)},P_{t|t-1}^{(k)}.

Conclusion

In this paper, we have studied LGSSMs under unknown variances. We relied on online learning to estimate the variances. We considered the paradigm of SKF and used aggregation of experts to infer the variance of the process noise; to approximate the observation noise variance we applied an online gradient descent algorithm. Our experiments show good performances not only in the favourable well-specified setting but also under misspecification on both synthetic and real data, illustrating the robustness of our method.

This paper opens different leads for future research. First, we specified on KF algorithms but we could apply our framework to other forecasting algorithms, involving the expert aggregation confidence weights at the right level, not necessarily on the predictions. Second, the choice of the diversity among the experts is crucial in aggregation
[
13
]
, and it is challenging to design different KF algorithms in an optimal way.

\displaystyle\rho_{s}(Z_{s})

\displaystyle\propto\mathcal{N}(y_{s}|\boldsymbol{\mu}_{s}^{T}\boldsymbol{x}_{s},\sigma_{Z_{s}}^{2})\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\text{Tr}\left(\frac{\boldsymbol{x}_{s}\boldsymbol{x}_{s}^{T}}{\sigma_{Z_{s}}^{2}}V_{s}\right)\right)

\displaystyle\gamma_{Z_{s}|Z_{s-1}}

\displaystyle\propto\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{s}|\boldsymbol{\mu}_{s-1},Q_{Z_{s-1}})\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\text{Tr}\left[Q_{Z_{s-1}}^{-1}(V_{s}+V_{s-1}-2W_{s-1})\right]\right)\tau_{Z_{s}|Z_{s-1}}

This last expression (
15
) is equivalent to a
Hidden Markov Model (HMM)
. We define the filtration
\mathcal{H}_{s}=\sigma(x_{1:s},y_{1:s},\mu_{1:s},V_{1:s},W_{1:s-1})
and
p^{\prime}
such that
q_{Z}(Z_{1:t})=p^{\prime}(Z_{1:t}|H_{t})
. Therefore, by computing the standard forward-backward algorithm for HMMs, one can easily obtain the forward
q_{Z}^{F}(Z_{s})=p^{\prime}(Z_{s}|\mathcal{H}_{s})\approx p(Z_{s}|\mathcal{F}_{s})
and backward
q_{Z}^{B}(Z_{s})=p^{\prime}(Z_{s}|\mathcal{H}_{s+1:t})\approx p(Z_{s}|\boldsymbol{x}_{s+1:t},y_{s+1:t})
and obtain the posterior approximation
q_{Z}(Z_{s})\approx p(Z_{s}|\mathcal{F}_{t})
:

q_{Z}(Z_{s})\propto q_{Z}^{F}(Z_{s})q_{Z}^{B}(Z_{s})

(16)

Proposition 7
.

The forward-backward recursions write for some
t>1
and
s\in[\![1,t]\!]
:

q_{Z}^{F}(Z_{s})\propto\rho_{s}(Z_{s})\odot(q_{Z}^{F}(Z_{s-1})\gamma_{Z_{s}|Z_{s-1}})

(17)

q_{Z}^{B}(Z_{s})\propto\gamma_{Z_{s+1}|Z_{s}}\left(q_{Z}^{B}(Z_{s+1})\odot\rho_{s+1}(Z_{s+1})\right)

(18)

Proof.

Starting from the joint distribution we have :

\displaystyle\alpha_{s}(Z_{s})

\displaystyle=p(Z_{s},x_{1:s},y_{1:s},\mu_{1:s},V_{1:s},W_{1:s-1})

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{K}p(Z_{s},Z_{s-1}=k,y_{1:s},x_{1:s},\mu_{1:s},V_{1:s},W_{1:s-1})\qquad\qquad\text{(marginalizing over $Z_{s-1}$)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{K}p(y_{s}|Z_{s},Z_{s-1}=k,y_{1:s-1},x_{1:s},\mu_{1:s},V_{1:s},W_{1:s-1})p(Z_{s},Z_{s-1}=k,y_{1:s-1},x_{1:s},\mu_{1:s},V_{1:s},W_{1:s-1})

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{K}p(y_{s}|Z_{s},Z_{s-1}=k,x_{s},\mu_{s},V_{s},W_{s-1})p(Z_{s}|Z_{s-1}=k)

\displaystyle p(x_{s}|Z_{s-1}=k,x_{1:s-1},y_{1:s-1},\mu_{1:s-1},V_{1:s-1},W_{1:s-2})p(Z_{s-1}=k,x_{1:s-1},y_{1:s-1},\mu_{1:s-1},V_{1:s-1},W_{1:s-2})

\displaystyle\propto\sum_{k=1}^{K}\rho_{s}(Z_{s})\gamma_{Z_{s}|Z_{s-1}=k}\alpha_{s-1}(Z_{s-1}=k)

\displaystyle\propto\rho_{s}(Z_{s})\odot\left(\alpha_{s-1}(Z_{s-1})\gamma_{Z_{s}|Z_{s-1}}\right)

\displaystyle\Longrightarrow q_{Z}^{F}(Z_{s})

\displaystyle=p(Z_{s}|x_{1:s},y_{1:s})

\displaystyle\propto\rho_{s}(Z_{s})\odot(q_{Z}^{F}(Z_{s-1})\gamma_{Z_{s}|Z_{s-1}})

\displaystyle q_{Z}^{B}(Z_{s})

\displaystyle=p(y_{s+1:t},x_{s+1:t}|Z_{s})

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{K}p(y_{s+1:t},x_{s+1:t},Z_{s+1}=k|Z_{s})\qquad\qquad\text{(marginalizing over $Z_{s+1}$)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{K}p(y_{s+1}|x_{s+1:t},y_{s+2:t},Z_{s+1}=k,Z_{s})p(x_{s+1:t},y_{s+2:t},Z_{s+1}=k,Z_{s})

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{K}p(y_{s+1}|x_{s+1},Z_{s+1}=k,Z_{s})p(Z_{s+1}=k|Z_{s},x_{s+1:t},y_{s+2:t})p(x_{s+1:t},y_{s+2:t},Z_{s+1}=k)

\displaystyle\propto\sum_{k=1}^{K}\rho_{s+1}(Z_{s+1}=k)q_{Z}^{B}(Z_{s+1}=k)\gamma_{Z_{s+1}=k|Z_{s}}\qquad\qquad\text{(\`{a} $p(x_{s+1}|.)$ pr\`{e}s)}

\displaystyle\propto\gamma_{Z_{s+1}|Z_{s}}(q_{Z}^{B}(Z_{s+1})\odot\rho_{s+1}(Z_{s+1}))

∎
