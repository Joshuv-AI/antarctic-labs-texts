Higher-form Symmetries under Weak Measurement
Kaixiang Su, Nayan Myerson-Jain, Chong Wang, Chao-Ming Jian, Cenke Xu

Abstract
We aim to address the following question: if we start with a quantum state with a spontaneously broken higher-form symmetry, what is the fate of the system under weak local quantum measurements? We demonstrate that under certain conditions, a phase transition can be driven by weak measurements, which suppresses the spontaneous breaking of the 1-form symmetry and weakens the 1-form symmetry charge fluctuation. We analyze the nature of the transitions employing the tool of duality, and we demonstrate that some of the transitions driven by weak measurement enjoy a line of fixed points with self-duality.

In this work, we consider systems with an

2d

Hence in this paper, we focus on another class of questions that are of potentially greater interest: we consider a system with a symmetry
G^{(n)}
, but we start with a state
|\Psi\rangle
with spontaneous symmetry breaking (SSB) of
G^{(n)}

If our system has only a 0-form symmetry, the answer to the question above is straightforward: A spontaneous breaking of a 0-form symmetry with true long-range order
cannot
be qualitatively altered by any local finite-strength weak measurement with or without post-selection; the SSB can only be destroyed with “strong” projective measurements, i.e.
p=1
. The physical picture of this conclusion is that there is a strong “proximity effect” from the bulk, which breaks the 0-form symmetry on any defect with one lower dimension. More formally, for a true long-range order that spontaneously breaks a 0-form symmetry, the state can be exemplified as a simple product state. For example, for an Ising system whose global
\mathbb{Z}^{(0)}_{2}
charge is
\prod_{j}X_{j}
, an Ising-ordered state has the following wave function at its fixed point:
|\Psi\rangle=\prod_{j}|Z_{j}=1\rangle
. Now on every site, we perform a weak measurement on operator
X_{j}
with probability
p
, namely, there is a probability
p
that the qubit on the site
j
is being measured for each
j
. The density matrix takes the following form when the measurement outcome
X_{j}=1
is selected

\displaystyle\rho=\otimes_{j}\rho_{j},\ \ \ \rho_{j}=(1-p)\frac{I+Z_{j}}{2}+p\frac{I+X_{j}}{2}.

(5)

Then as long as
p<1
, the expectation value of
Z_{j}
is always nonzero. And there is no intrinsic phase transition driven by the weak measurements as the system essentially reduces to a 0-dimensional problem. This conclusion holds
with or without
post-selecting the measurement outcomes.

We will show that higher-form symmetries lead to much more interesting consequences, as the SSB phase of the higher-form symmetry is
not
a simple product state in space, and an intrinsic phase transition can be driven by weak measurements.

IV
Summary and Discussions

In this work, we discuss the fate of higher-form symmetry under weak quantum measurements. In particular, we demonstrate that a pure state with SSB of higher-form symmetry can go through a phase transition driven by weak measurements. This starkly contrasts with the SSB state of a 0-form symmetry whose fate under weak measurements reduces to a
(0+1)d
problem and hence won’t have an intrinsic phase transition. Some of the phase transitions driven by weak measurement can be mapped to a mixed-dimensional QED, with a line of fixed points mapped to each other under self-duality.

There is another ingredient of the mixed dimensional QED that plays a nontrivial role in the duality, i.e., the topological
\Theta-
term in the bulk. In our current set-up, the effect of the
\Theta-
term likely cancels out at the temporal defects due to the trace of the density matrix. But the
\Theta-
term can make nontrivial contributions to quantities such as the strange correlator
You et al. 2014
, which involves temporal interfaces between two different states. It is worth exploring weak-measurement-driven transitions of strange correlators of systems with higher-form symmetries, which we expect to have an even richer structure of duality due to the nontrivial role of the
\Theta-
term.

In this work, we focused on SSB states of
continuous
higher-form symmetries. In another work
Su et al. 2024
, we will demonstrate that, if one starts with an SSB state at
d
-spatial dimension (
(d+1)D
space-time) with a
discrete
higher-form symmetry,
under different conditions various classical statistical mechanics models can be engineered through weak measurement.

Appendix A
Weak-measurement on
Z_{2}
gauge theory

Before discussing our measurement protocol on systems with U(1) gauge fields, we first discuss a simpler example of weak-measurement driven phase transition on the
Z_{2}
gauge theory. More examples of measurement-driven transitions on systems with discrete higher form symmetries (discrete gauge theories) are discussed in Ref.
Su et al. 2024
.
We take the
3d
toric code Hamiltonian as a starting point, which is defined on the
3d
cubic lattice

H_{\rm TC}=-\sum_{v}A_{v}-\sum_{p}B_{p}

(10)

where
v
denotes vertices on the cubic lattice and
p
denotes plaquettes on the cubic lattice. The local Hilbert space is made of qubits that reside on the links.
A_{v}
is then the product of
\sigma^{x}
operators connected to the vertex
v
;
B_{p}
is the product of
\sigma^{z}
operators along the edge of plaquette
p
.
\sigma^{x}_{ij}
is the analogue of the electric field of the
\mathrm{U}(1)
gauge field, and
\sigma^{z}_{ij}
is the analogue of
e^{\mathrm{i}a_{ij}}
of the lattice
\mathrm{U}(1)
gauge field.

The initial state is prepared to be the tensor product between the ground state of
H_{\rm TC}
and
\ket{0}_{a}
of the ancilla qubits.
We then evolve the system qubit and ancilla qubit via the following unitary operator

U=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{bmatrix}\cos t+\sigma^{z}\sin t,\ -\cos t+\sigma^{z}\sin t\\
\cos t-\sigma^{z}\sin t,\ \cos t+\sigma^{z}\sin t\end{bmatrix}

(11)

Here, the
2\times 2
matrix structure of
U
is associated with the 2-fold Hilbert space of the ancillary qubit.

When
t=0
,
U
takes the form

U_{0}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{bmatrix}1&-1\\
1&1\end{bmatrix}

(12)

The general
U(t)
can be implemented via the following procedure

U=e^{-\mathrm{i}tH}\cdot U_{0}

(13)

with

H=\mathrm{i}\sigma^{z}\sigma_{a}^{+}-\mathrm{i}\sigma^{z}\sigma_{a}^{-}

(14)

where
\sigma_{a}
denotes the Pauli operator acting on the ancilla qubit. We then measure the ancilla in its computational basis which gives us an ensemble of states labeled by measurement outcome
s
, a string that records the measurement outcomes on each link

\ket{\psi(s)}\propto\prod_{\rm link}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\cos t+s_{ij}\sin t\sigma^{z}_{ij})\ket{\psi}

(15)

To calculate expectation values of quantities of this ensemble, we need to evaluate

\bar{\expectationvalue{O}}=\sum_{s}P(s)\frac{\bra{\psi(s)}O\ket{\psi(s)}}{\innerproduct{\psi(s)}{\psi(s)}}

(16)

By Born’s rule (no bias), this is equivalent to

\tr\left(\prod_{\rm link}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\cos t+\sin t\sigma^{z}_{ij})\hat{\rho}_{0}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\cos t+\sin t\sigma^{z}_{ij})O+\prod_{\rm link}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\cos t-\sin t\sigma^{z}_{ij})\hat{\rho}_{0}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\cos t-\sin t\sigma^{z}_{ij})O\right)

(17)

Assuming
O
commute with
\sigma^{z}_{ij}
, the equation above is equivalent to

\prod_{\rm link}\tr(\hrho_0 O)

(18)

hence the measurements without post-selection does not change the expectation value of
O
.

We now consider a biased selection, meaning we collect all measurement outcomes but we give a higher weight to the outcome with
s_{ij}=+1
. Now the expectation of
O
becomes (again we consider quantity
O
that commutes with
\sigma^{z}_{ij}
)

\displaystyle\tr\left(\prod_{\rm link}(\frac{1}{2}+x)(\cos t+\sin t\sigma^{z}_{ij})(\cos t+\sin t\sigma^{z}_{ij})\hat{\rho}_{0}O+\prod_{\rm link}(\frac{1}{2}-x)(\cos t-\sin t\sigma^{z}_{ij})(\cos t-\sin t\sigma^{z}_{ij})\hat{\rho}_{0}O\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\tr\left(\prod_{\rm link}(1+(4x\cos t\sin t)\sigma^{z}_{ij})\hat{\rho}_{0}O\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\tr\left(\prod_{\rm link}(1+(\tanh\tilde{\beta})\sigma^{z}_{ij})\hat{\rho}_{0}O\right)

Here, we make the identification
4x\cos t\sin t=\tanh\tilde{\beta}
. We can reorganize the mixed density matrix into the following form:

\hat{\rho}\sim e^{-\frac{1}{2}I\left(\{\sigma^{z}_{ij}\}\right)}\hat{\rho}_{0}e^{-\frac{1}{2}I\left(\{\sigma^{z}_{ij}\}\right)},\ \ \ \tr\left(\hat{\rho}O\right)\sim\tr\left(\hat{\rho}_{0}e^{-I\left(\{\sigma^{z}_{ij}\}\right)}O\right)

(19)

We also note that
\hat{\rho}
can be expressed in a form compatible with what we have in the main text

\mathcal{E}_{{\bm{x}}}[\hat{\rho}_{0}]=(1-p)\hat{\rho}_{0}+p\left(\sum_{m}p_{m}\hat{P}_{m,{\bm{x}}}\hat{\rho}_{0}\hat{P}_{m,{\bm{x}}}\right).

(20)

if we make the identification

\displaystyle p=2\cos t\sin t,\ \ \ p_{+}=1+2x,\ p_{-}=1-2x

\displaystyle\hat{P}_{m,{\bm{x}}}=\frac{I+m\sigma^{z}_{ij}}{2}.

Here
m=\pm 1
, and
{\bm{x}}
corresponds to the link
<i,j>
on the lattice.

We can view
\tr(\hrho)
as a partition function of a
3d
classical Ising model from which one can extract
\tr(\hrho O)
. To see this, we note that the expectation of a string of Pauli-
\sigma^{z}_{ij}
operators with respect to
\hat{\rho}
is 1 when the string is closed and 0 when the string is open.
This is because the wave function of the
3d
toric code is a superposition of loop-configurations generated by the product of
B_{p}
, which only involves closed loops.
There are additional subtleties caused by logical operators, but there are only a finite number of them and will not affect the partition function in the thermal dynamic limit. Therefore, the partition function becomes

\sum_{\mathcal{C}}(\tanh\tilde{\beta})^{\absolutevalue{\mathcal{C}}}

(21)

where
\mathcal{C}
denotes closed loops formed by links of
3d
cubic lattice, and this partition function is exactly the high-temperature expansion of a
3d
classical Ising model.

Physically, the operator
\sigma^{z}_{ij}
enables the “hopping” of the charges of the
Z_{2}
gauge theory, hence at larger
\tilde{\beta}>\tilde{\beta}_{c}
(which can be tuned by choosing
x
and
t
), the partition function of the
3d
classical Ising model enters the “low temperature ordered” phase, i.e. the condensate of the electric charge of the
Z_{2}
gauge theory. It is important to note that this transition belongs to the
3d
Ising universality class, which is consistent with the picture described in the main text that weak measurement drives the electric charges to condense on the temporal slab (which is a
3d
space)
\tau=0
in the path-integral formalism (Fig.
2
).

In Ref.
Su et al. 2024
it is also shown that measurement without post-selection can drive a phase transition in the 2nd Renyi entropy of the
Z_{2}
gauge theory, which is consistent with the physical picture that weak-measurement can drive condensation of the bound state of gauge charges on the two temporal slabs in the path integral formalism (Fig.
2
).

Appendix B
Weak-measurement on the lattice model and its field theory description

In this section we explicitly formulate the weak-measurement driven phase transition discussed in the main text on a lattice model. We take the
3d
cubic lattice as an example. The local operators consist of rotors
\theta_{i}
defined on the sites, and
a_{ij}
defined on the links of the lattice, along with their canonical conjugates
n_{i}
and
E_{ij}

\displaystyle\commutator{E_{ij}}{e^{\ii a_{i'j;}}}=\delta_{ij,i^{\prime}j^{\prime}}e^{\mathrm{i}a_{ij}},\

\displaystyle\commutator{n_i}{e^{\ii\theta_{i'}}}=\delta_{i,i^{\prime}}e^{\mathrm{i}\theta_{i}}

(22)

Note that the rotors
\theta_{i}
and
a_{ij}
are periodically define,
i.e.

\theta_{i}=\theta_{i}+2\pi
,
a_{ij}=a_{ij}+2\pi
, while their conjugates
n_{i}
and
E_{ij}
take discrete integer eigenvalues.

Before weak measurement or decoherence, the pure quantum state
|\Psi\rangle
we start with is the ground state of the following lattice Hamiltonian

H=\frac{U}{2}\sum_{i}n_{i}^{2}-J\sum_{i,\mu}\cos(\theta_i-\theta_{i+\mu} + a_{i,i+\mu})+\frac{\tilde{U}}{2}\sum_{i,\mu}E_{i,i+\mu}^{2}-K\sum_{i,\mu}\cos(\vect{\nabla} \times\vect{a}).

(23)

The system is also subjected to a local “Gauss law” constraint

n_{i}-{\bm{\nabla}}\cdot{\bm{E}}=0.

(24)

One can verify that the local operator
n_{i}-{\bm{\nabla}}\cdot{\bm{E}}
commutes with the Hamiltonian.
The phase diagram of the lattice gauge theory is well-understood: we choose that
U/J
and
K/\tilde{U}
to be greater than a critical value, so that the system is in its Coulomb or photon phase, i.e. Physically this means that the rotor charge is gapped, and the gauge field
{\bm{a}}
is in its deconfined phase with gapless photon excitations. The structure of the spatial lattice is shown in fig.
2
.

Figure 2:
The upper left panel shows the protocol of our quantum operation with a biased selection process. This can be formulated as a defect insertion at a particular temporal slice in the Euclidean space-time path-integral (red line). The lower left panel shows the protocol that considers the 2nd Rényi entropy under decoherence, which becomes defect insertion at two temporal slices separated by
\beta
where
\beta\rightarrow\infty
. The right panel shows the spatial lattice structure. The red vertex stands for the Gauss law constraint operator, the blue plaquette stands for the plaquette term which controls pure gauge fluctuations, and the orange link is the coupling of the gauge field to the rotor matters.

B.1
Biased Selection

We now describe a quantum channel that couples the system operator
\exp(\ii(\theta_i - a_{ij} - \theta_j))
to an auxiliary qubit (one can consider more general ancilla degrees of freedom such as qudit or rotor but ancilla qubits suffice for our discussion).
The initial state is again prepared to be a tensor product between ground state of
H
and
\ket{0}_{a}
of all the ancilla qubits.
First, we have the qubit and
\theta_{i},a_{ij},\theta_{j}
undergo a collective unitary of the following form (in what follows we denote
\Theta_{ij}=\theta_{i}-a_{ij}-\theta_{j}
).

U=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{bmatrix}\cos t+e^{\mathrm{i}\Theta_{ij}}\sin t,\ -\cos t+e^{\mathrm{i}\Theta_{ij}}\sin t\\
\cos t-e^{-\mathrm{i}\Theta_{ij}}\sin t,\ \cos t+e^{-\mathrm{i}\Theta_{ij}}\sin t\end{bmatrix}

(25)

One can verify this operator is unitary. When
t=0
, U takes the simple form

U_{0}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{bmatrix}1&-1\\
1&1\end{bmatrix}

(26)

We can also rewrite
U
in the following form

U=e^{-\mathrm{i}tH}\cdot U_{0}

(27)

where

H=\mathrm{i}e^{\mathrm{i}\Theta_{ij}}\sigma^{+}-\mathrm{i}e^{-\mathrm{i}\Theta_{ij}}\sigma^{-}.

(28)

The density matrix we start with is

\hat{\rho}_{\rm total}=\hat{\rho}_{0}\otimes\ket{0}\bra{0}.

(29)

Evolution with
U
will generate an entangled state between the system and ancilla

\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\cos t+\sin te^{\mathrm{i}\Theta_{ij}})\ket{\psi}\ket{0}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\cos t-\sin te^{-\mathrm{i}\Theta_{ij}})\ket{\psi}\ket{1}

(30)

We then measure the ancilla in its computational basis which gives us an ensemble of states labeled by measurement outcome
s
, a string that records the measurement outcomes on each link

\ket{\psi(s)}\propto\prod_{\rm link}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\cos t+s_{ij}\sin te^{\mathrm{i}s_{ij}\Theta_{ij}})\ket{\psi}

(31)

To evaluate an average quantity of this ensemble, we need to investigate

\bar{\expectationvalue{O}}=\sum_{s}P(s)\frac{\bra{\psi(s)}O\ket{\psi(s)}}{\innerproduct{\psi(s)}{\psi(s)}}

(32)

By Born’s rule (no bias), this is equivalent to

\tr\left(\prod_{\rm link}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\cos t+\sin te^{\mathrm{i}\Theta_{ij}})\hat{\rho}_{0}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\cos t+\sin te^{-\mathrm{i}\Theta_{ij}})O+\prod_{\rm link}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\cos t-\sin te^{-\mathrm{i}\Theta_{ij}})\hat{\rho}_{0}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\cos t-\sin te^{\mathrm{i}\Theta_{ij}})O\right)

(33)

Assuming
O
commute with
e^{\mathrm{i}\Theta_{ij}}
, the equation above is equivalent to

\prod_{\rm link}\tr(\hrho_0 O)

(34)

hence the measurements without post-selection does not change the expectation value of
O
.

We now consider a biased selection, meaning we collect all measurement outcomes but we give a higher weight to the outcome with
s_{ij}=+1
. This is equivalent to

\displaystyle\tr\left(\prod_{\rm link}(\frac{1}{2}+x)(\cos t+\sin te^{-\mathrm{i}\Theta_{ij}})(\cos t+\sin te^{\mathrm{i}\Theta_{ij}})\hat{\rho}_{0}O+\prod_{\rm link}(\frac{1}{2}-x)(\cos t-\sin te^{\mathrm{i}\Theta_{ij}})(\cos t-\sin te^{-\mathrm{i}\Theta_{ij}})\hat{\rho}_{0}O\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\tr\left(\prod_{\rm link}(1+4x\cos t\sin t\cos\Theta_{ij})\hat{\rho}_{0}O\right)

We can again reorganize the mixed density matrix into the following form:

\hat{\rho}\sim e^{-\frac{1}{2}I\left(\{\Theta_{ij}\}\right)}\hat{\rho}_{0}e^{-\frac{1}{2}I\left(\{\Theta_{ij}\}\right)},\ \ \ \tr\left(\hat{\rho}O\right)\sim\tr\left(\hat{\rho}_{0}e^{-I\left(\{\Theta_{ij}\}\right)}O\right)

(35)

In the path integral language this is equivalent to increasing the weight for the configurations with
\Theta_{ij}=0
on a
3d
slice at
\tau=0
of the Euclidean space-time. By choosing
x
and
t
, we can see that the weight of configurations with
\Theta_{ij}=0
can be arbitrarily larger than the weight of configurations with
\Theta_{ij}=\pi
, i.e. the measurement followed by selection can strongly prefer
\theta_{i}
to order (charge condensation) at the temporal slab
\tau=0
, similar to the previous section on the
Z_{2}
gauge theory.

B.2
Field theory description

For a (classical) XY model defined on the lattice, the partition funciton reas

\displaystyle Z=\int_{0}^{2\pi}D\theta_{i}\exp\left(-I\left(\{\Theta_{ij}\}\right)\right),

(36)

where
\Theta_{ij}=\theta_{i}-\theta_{j}
, and
I\left(\Theta_{ij}\right)
is a periodic function of
\Theta_{ij}
. The simplest choice of
I(\{\Theta_{ij}\})
is a nearest neighbor XY model:

\displaystyle I(\{\Theta_{ij}\})=\sum_{\rm n.n.}-(J/T)\cos(\theta_i - \theta_j).

(37)

On a
3d
lattice, by tuning
J/T
there is a phase transition between a condensate of
e^{\mathrm{i}\theta_{i}}
(for
J>J_{c}
), and a disordered phase with
J<J_{c}
. The
3d
XY model is not soluble exactly, but its infrared physics can be studied reliably using field theory. In the field theory formalism, we need to use the “coarse-grained” complex field
\phi({\bm{x}})
rather than the lattice operator
\exp(\ii\theta_i)
. The partition function is now mapped to a field theory

\displaystyle Z\sim\int D\phi({\bm{x}})\exp\left(-\int d^{d}{\bm{x}}\ |{\bm{\nabla}}\phi|^{2}+r|\phi|^{2}+g|\phi|^{4}\right).

(38)

It is important to note that this field theory corresponds to a large class of lattice models with
\mathrm{U}(1)
variables. For example one can add further neighbor interactions in the partition function, the change of the microscopic parameters will change the UV values of the parameters in the field theory,
e.g.

r
and
g
. But the specific values of the field theory are not so important for the “universal” physics in the infrared we are concerned about, because the physics in the infrared limit is controlled by the fixed point values of these parameters under renormalization group flow, regardless of the values in the microscopic (UV) scale. Hence the physics described by the fixed points of the field theory is a very generic description of a large class of models with
\mathrm{U}(1)
variables.

If the original lattice model also involves gauge fields defined on the links, i.e.
\Theta_{ij}=\theta_{i}-\theta_{j}-a_{ij}
, with a lattice version of the Maxwell term of
a_{ij}
, the theory becomes an “Abelian Higgs” model and has attracted a lot of interests in the past (see for example Ref.
Callaway and Carson 1982
;
Fradkin and Shenker 1979
). The field theory of this model is

\displaystyle Z\sim\int D\phi({\bm{x}})D{\bm{a}}({\bm{x}})e^{-{\cal S}[{\bm{a}},\phi]},\ \ \ {\cal S}[{\bm{a}},\phi]=\int d^{d}x\ |({\bm{\nabla}}-\mathrm{i}{\bm{a}})\phi|^{2}+r|\phi|^{2}+g|\phi|^{4}+{\cal L}_{\rm Maxwell}

(39)

The pure state density matrix
\hat{\rho}_{0}
of a quantum system before weak-measurement or decoherence can be represented in a standard path-integral form. If we are only interested in the physics in the infrared limit, the density matrix can be represented in a field theory form in the continuum, without loss of generality.
In our case, the density matrix element between field configurations
|{\bm{a}}({\bm{x}})_{1},\phi({\bm{x}}_{1})\rangle
and
|{\bm{a}}({\bm{x}})_{2},\phi({\bm{x}})_{2}\rangle
reads

\displaystyle\langle{\bm{a}}({\bm{x}})_{2},\phi({\bm{x}})_{2}|\hat{\rho}_{0}|{\bm{a}}({\bm{x}})_{1},\phi({\bm{x}})_{1}\rangle=\int Da_{\mu}({\bm{x}},\tau)D\phi({\bm{x}},\tau)e^{-S_{d+1}[a_{\mu},\phi]},

(40)

(41)

\displaystyle S_{d+1}[a_{\mu},\phi]=\int d\tau d^{d}x\ |(\partial_{\mu}-\mathrm{i}a_{\mu})\phi|^{2}+r^{\prime}|\phi|^{2}+g^{\prime}|\phi|^{4}+\frac{1}{4e^{2}}(f_{\mu\nu})^{2},

(42)

where
f_{\mu\nu}=\partial_{\mu}a_{\nu}-\partial_{\nu}a_{\mu}
, and we may introduce
a_{0}
as a Lagrange multiplier to enforce the Gauss law constraint Eq.
24
. In the path-integral above, we also need the following boundary conditions

{\bm{a}}({\bm{x}},0)={\bm{a}}({\bm{x}})_{1}
,
{\bm{a}}({\bm{x}},\beta)={\bm{a}}({\bm{x}})_{2}
,

\phi({\bm{x}},0)=\phi({\bm{x}})_{1}
,
\phi({\bm{x}},\beta)=\phi({\bm{x}})_{2}
. In the bulk we need the system to be in the photon phase, hence we need
r^{\prime}>0
, i.e. the charged matter fields are gapped. For the physics in the infrared we can just focus on the last Maxwell term
\frac{1}{4e^{2}}(f_{\mu\nu})^{2}
which leads to gapless photons in the bulk.

After weak-measurement described in the previous section, the evaluation of an operator reduces to the following path integral problem:

\displaystyle\tr\left(\hat{\rho}O\right)\sim\int D\phi({\bm{x}},\tau)e^{-S[a_{\mu},\phi]+{\cal S}[{\bm{a}},\phi]_{\tau=0}}O_{\tau=0}.

(43)

Here in the path integral we need to identify
\tau=0
and
\tau=\beta
, due to the trace. The extra term in the action
{\cal S}[{\bm{a}},\phi]_{\tau=0}
is a coarse-grained version of
e^{-I(\{\Theta_{ij}\})}
in Eq.
35
, where
\Theta_{ij}=\theta_{i}-\theta_{j}-a_{ij}
on the lattice, and the form of
{\cal S}[{\bm{a}},\phi]_{\tau=0}
is given by Eq.
39
.

In the
d+1
dimensional space-time bulk, the charges are gapped out, and the low energy physics is controlled by the Maxwell term only. But since the Maxwell term is a free theory, its effects on the temporal boundary can be understood reliably. This was discussed in (for example) Ref.
Hsiao and Son 2017
, and the effect from the bulk will turn
{\cal S}[{\bm{a}},\phi]_{\tau=0}
in Eq.
39
to a nonlocal QED in Eq.4 of the main text.

As we mentioned in the main text, weak-measurement with or without post-selection can also drive transition int the 2nd Renyi entropy, which is mapped to a path-integral with interactions between the two temporal slabs
\tau=0,\beta
(Fig.
2
). In Ref.
Lee et al. 2023
;
Bao et al. 2023
;
Fan et al. 2023
;
Su et al. 2024
, it was shown that weak-measurement can drive bound states of gauge charges on the two temporal slabs to condense.
In our current problem, if we start with a state with an exact
\mathrm{U}(1)^{(1)}_{m}
symmetry, weak measurements may potentially also drive a condensation of the bound state of electric charges
\phi
on the
\tau=0
and
\tau=\beta
slabs. This bound state condensation breaks the doubled IR-
\mathrm{U}(1)_{e}^{(1)}
symmetry down to a diagonal IR-
\mathrm{U}(1)_{e}^{(1)}
symmetry, using the language developed in Ref.
Lee et al. 2022b
;
Lee et al. 2023
. This transition is described by the following effective action:

\displaystyle{\cal S}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int d^{3}x\ |({\bm{\nabla}}-\mathrm{i}{\bm{a}}_{1}-\mathrm{i}{\bm{a}}_{2})\phi|^{2}+r|\phi|^{2}+g|\phi|^{4}

(44)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2e^{2}}{\bm{f}}_{1}\cdot\frac{1}{|\partial|}{\bm{f}}_{1}+\frac{1}{2e^{2}}{\bm{f}}_{2}\cdot\frac{1}{|\partial|}{\bm{f}}_{2}.

(46)

{\bm{a}}_{1}
and
{\bm{a}}_{2}
are the gauge fields on the
\tau=0
and
\tau=\beta
interfaces respectively.
\phi
is effectively coupled to one gauge field
{\bm{a}}_{+}={\bm{a}}_{1}+{\bm{a}}_{2}
with a nonlocal action, and the transition belongs to the same universality class as Eq.4 in the main text. The 2nd Rényi entropy is the free energy for this transition, which should have a singularity at the transition.

In the condensate of
\phi
,
{\bm{a}}_{+}={\bm{a}}_{1}+{\bm{a}}_{2}
acquires a mass and suppresses the correlation of field
{\bm{B}}_{+}={\bm{B}}_{1}+{\bm{B}}_{2}
, i.e., a linear combination of fields on the two different temporal slabs. Hence we expect the following scaling:

\displaystyle\log\left(\tr\{\hat{\rho}\ e^{\mathrm{i}B_{a}({\bm{0}})}e^{-\mathrm{i}B_{b}({\bm{x}})}\hat{\rho}\ e^{\mathrm{i}B_{a}({\bm{0}})}e^{-\mathrm{i}B_{b}({\bm{x}})}\}\right)\sim\frac{1}{|{\bm{x}}|^{6}},

(47)

as this is the quantity that encodes the correlation of field
{\bm{B}}_{+}
.

Appendix C
Calculation with explicit form of density matrix

In this section we compute quantities under weak measurement using the explicit form of density matrix. Let us start with the Hamiltonian of the quantized electromagnetic field in the
3d
space:

\displaystyle H=\int d^{3}x\ \frac{e^{2}}{2}{\bm{E}}({\bm{x}})^{2}+\frac{1}{2e^{2}}{\bm{B}}(x)^{2}.

(48)

The
ground state wave functional of the EM field is

\displaystyle|\Psi\rangle\sim\int D{\bm{a}}({\bm{q}})\exp\left(-{\cal S}[{\bm{a}}({\bm{q}})]\right)|{\bm{a}}({\bm{q}})\rangle\text{ with }{\cal S}[{\bm{a}}]=\frac{1}{2e^{2}}\int\frac{d^{3}q}{(2\pi)^{3}}\frac{|{\bm{q}}\times{\bm{a}}({\bm{q}})|^{2}}{|{\bm{q}}|}.

(49)

The correlation function between
{\bm{B}}
fields in the ground
state reads

\displaystyle\tr\{\hat{\rho}_{0}{\bm{B}}({\bm{0}})\cdot{\bm{B}}({\bm{x}})\}\sim\int\frac{d^{3}q}{(2\pi)^{3}}e^{i{\bm{q}}\cdot{\bm{x}}}\epsilon^{aij}\epsilon^{amn}q_{i}q_{m}\langle a_{j}^{T}({\bm{q}})a_{n}^{T}(-{\bm{q}})\rangle

(50)

where
\langle a_{j}^{T}({\bm{q}})a_{n}^{T}(-{\bm{q}})\rangle
is the gauge-field propagator for the transverse modes of
{\bm{a}}
computed according to the “action”
2{\cal S}[{\bm{a}}]
. It is straight-forward to check that
\langle a_{j}^{T}({\bm{q}})a_{n}^{T}(-{\bm{q}})\rangle=\frac{e^{2}}{2}\frac{\delta^{jn}-q^{j}q^{n}/|{\bm{q}}|^{2}}{|{\bm{q}}|}
, hence

\displaystyle\tr\{\hat{\rho}_{0}{\bm{B}}({\bm{0}})\cdot{\bm{B}}({\bm{x}})\}\sim e^{2}\int\frac{d^{3}q}{(2\pi)^{3}}e^{i{\bm{q}}\cdot{\bm{x}}}|{\bm{q}}|\sim\frac{e^{2}}{|{\bm{x}}|^{4}}.

(51)

Suppose weak measurement drives electric charge to condense on the
(3+0)d
temporal defect which effectively amounts to turning on a mass term for the gauge-field in the evaluation above, the correlation between magnetic fields reads as Eq.
50
but with the propagator of the transverse components of the gauge-field computed according to the modified “action”
2{\cal S}[{\bm{a}}]+\frac{1}{2}m^{2}\int\frac{d^{3}q}{(2\pi)^{3}}|{\bm{a}}({\bm{q}})|^{2}
.
This new propagator – in the presence of a gauge-field mass – takes the form for the transverse components
\langle a_{j}^{T}({\bm{q}})a_{n}^{T}(-{\bm{q}})\rangle=\frac{\delta^{jn}-q^{j}q^{n}/|{\bm{q}}|^{2}}{m^{2}+2|{\bm{q}}|/e^{2}}
. Hence, at momentum much less than the scale set by the gauge-field mass

\displaystyle\tr\{\hat{\rho}{\bm{B}}({\bm{0}})\cdot{\bm{B}}({\bm{x}})\}

\displaystyle\sim

\displaystyle\int\frac{d^{3}q}{(2\pi)^{3}}e^{i{\bm{q}}\cdot{\bm{x}}}\frac{|{\bm{q}}|^{2}}{m^{2}+\frac{2}{e^{2}}|{\bm{q}}|}

(52)

\displaystyle\sim

\displaystyle\int\frac{d^{3}q}{(2\pi)^{3}}e^{i{\bm{q}}\cdot{\bm{x}}}|{\bm{q}}|^{2}\left(\frac{1}{m^{2}}-\frac{2|{\bm{q}}|}{e^{2}m^{4}}+O(|{\bm{q}}|^{2})\right).

(54)

Since the Fourier transformation of the quadratic piece
{\bm{q}}^{2}
only leads to local correlations, the leading term that contributes at long distance is
O(|{\bm{q}}|^{3})
and yields

\displaystyle\tr\{\hat{\rho}{\bm{B}}({\bm{0}})\cdot{\bm{B}}({\bm{x}})\}\sim\frac{1}{e^{2}m^{4}}\frac{1}{|{\bm{x}}|^{6}}.

(55)

These evaluations are consistent with what we expected from the boundary criticality analysis. The evaluation of the electric field is similar, but we need to
treat
{\bm{E}}
as
\delta/\delta{\bm{a}}({\bm{x}})
in the
evaluation with the wave functional. Then we shall see that the
extra mass term only causes some local correction to the
correlation between electric fields.

We now turn to the evaluation of the ’t Hooft loop. We begin with the calculation in the pure state with
\mathrm{U}(1)^{(1)}_{m}
SSB. We may define the eigenstates of the
{\bm{E(x)}}
field, which should obey the following relation

\innerproduct{\vect{a}}{\vect{E}}=e^{-\frac{\mathrm{i}}{2\pi}\int d^{3}x{\bm{a}}\cdot{\bm{E}}}

(56)

The ’t Hooft loop we would like to evaluate is

\expectationvalue{e^{\ii\int_\cC d\vect{l}\cdot\vect{b}}}=\expectationvalue{e^{\ii\int_\cA d\vect{\sigma}\cdot\vect{E}}}=\int D{\bm{E}}({\bm{x}})\text{ }\frac{\innerproduct{\Psi}{\vect{E}}e^{\mathrm{i}\int_{\cal A}d{\bm{\sigma}}\cdot{\bm{E}}}\innerproduct{\vect{E}}{\Psi}}{\innerproduct{\Psi}{\Psi}},

(57)

where
{\bm{\sigma}}
is a surface element on membrane
{\cal A}
. For simplicity, we choose
{\cal A}
to lie on the
z=0

XY
-plane. We can substitute in the ground-state wavefunction Eq.
49
to get

\displaystyle\expectationvalue{e^{\ii\int d\vect{\sigma}\cdot\vect{E}}}=\frac{\int D{\bm{a}}D{\bm{a^{\prime}}}D{\bm{E}}\text{ }e^{-\mathcal{S}[{\bm{a}}]-\mathcal{S}[{\bm{a^{\prime}}}]-\frac{\mathrm{i}}{2\pi}\int d^{3}x({\bm{a}}-{\bm{a^{\prime}}})\cdot{\bm{E}}+\mathrm{i}\int d^{3}x\delta(z)\theta(\sqrt{x^{2}+y^{2}}<R)E_{z}}}{\int D{\bm{a}}D{\bm{a^{\prime}}}D{\bm{E}}\text{ }e^{-\mathcal{S}[{\bm{a}}]-\mathcal{S}[{\bm{a^{\prime}}}]-\frac{\mathrm{i}}{2\pi}\int d^{3}x({\bm{a}}-{\bm{a^{\prime}}})\cdot{\bm{E}}}}

(58)

Integrating out field
{\bm{E}}
in the numerator gives

{\bm{a}}({\bm{x}})-{\bm{a^{\prime}}}({\bm{x}})=2\pi\delta(z)\theta(\sqrt{x^{2}+y^{2}}<R)\hat{e}_{z}=2\pi T({\bm{x}})\hat{e}_{z}=2\pi{\bm{T}}({\bm{x}})

(59)

while the integration of
{\bm{E}}
in the denominator simply imposes constraint
{\bm{a}}({\bm{x}})={\bm{a^{\prime}}}(x)
.
So the expectation value of the ’t Hooft loop of Eq.
57
reduces to

\frac{\int D{\bm{a}}\text{ }e^{-\mathcal{S}[{\bm{a}}]-\mathcal{S}[{\bm{a}}+2\pi{\bm{T}}]}}{\int D{\bm{a}}\text{ }e^{-2\mathcal{S}[{\bm{a}}]}}

(60)

with

\mathcal{S}[{\bm{a}}]+\mathcal{S}[{\bm{a}}+2\pi{\bm{T}}]=2\mathcal{S}[{\bm{a}}]+\frac{1}{2e^{2}}\int\frac{d^{3}q}{(2\pi)^{3}}\frac{({\bm{q}}\times 2\pi{\bm{T(q)}})\cdot({\bm{q}}\times{\bm{a(-q)}})+\text{c.c.}}{|{\bm{q}}|}+\frac{2}{e^{2}}\int\frac{d^{3}q}{(2\pi)^{3}}\frac{\pi^{2}|{\bm{q}}\times{\bm{T(q)}}|^{2}}{|{\bm{q}}|}

(61)

After shifting the gauge-field in the path integral by
{\bm{a}}({\bm{q}})\rightarrow{\bm{a}}({\bm{q}})-\pi{\bm{T}}({\bm{q}})
, we see that
{\bm{a}}({\bm{q}})
and
{\bm{T}}({\bm{q}})
decouple such that the expectation value is now merely

\langle e^{\mathrm{i}\int d{\bm{\sigma}}\cdot{\bm{E}}}\rangle=e^{-\frac{1}{e^{2}}\int\frac{d^{3}q}{(2\pi)^{3}}\frac{\pi^{2}(q_{y}^{2}+q_{x}^{2})T({\bm{q}})^{2}}{|{\bm{q}}|}}

(62)

The Fourier transform of
T
is

\displaystyle T(q)=\int_{0}^{R}drd\theta\text{ }re^{\mathrm{i}q_{2}r\cos\theta}=\frac{2\pi RJ_{1}(q_{2}R)}{q_{2}}

(63)

where
q_{2}=\sqrt{q_{x}^{2}+q_{y}^{2}}
is the magnitude of the in-plane component of the momentum, i.e. the Fourier distribution of
T
is constant in
q_{z}
.
J_{1}(x)
is the Bessel function of the first kind. We impose a cutoff
\Lambda
for
|{\bm{q}}|
so that the integral
62
is convergent. The integral in the exponential can be simplified to

\int d^{3}q\text{ }\frac{q_{2}^{2}T({\bm{q}})^{2}}{|{\bm{q}}|}=\int d^{3}q\text{ }(2\pi R)^{2}\frac{J_{1}^{2}(q_{2}R)}{|{\bm{q}}|}

(64)

A closed form expression is tedious, so we focus on the asymptotic behaviour of
J_{1}(q_{2}R)
which is

J_{1}(q_{2}R)\sim\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi q_{2}R}}\cos(q_2 R - \frac{3\pi}{4})+...

(65)

The large momentum integration would then roughly give

\displaystyle\int dq_{z}q_{2}dq_{2}\text{ }(2\pi R)^{2}\frac{2}{\pi q_{2}qR}\sim 8\pi R\int dq_{z}dq_{2}\text{ }\frac{1}{q}\sim 16\pi^{2}R\Lambda

One therefore get a perimeter law for the ’t Hooft loop as expected:

\expectationvalue{e^{\ii\int d\vect{\sigma}\cdot\vect{E}}}=e^{-\frac{2\pi\Lambda}{e^{2}}\mathcal{P}}.

(66)

When the electric charges condense on the
(3+0)d
temporal defect due to weak measurement, the evaluation of the ’t Hooft loop follows the same path integral on the
(3+0)d
as Eq.
60
, but with a modified effective action

\displaystyle{\cal S}=\frac{1}{2e^{2}}\int\frac{d^{3}q}{(2\pi)^{3}}\frac{|{\bm{q}}\times{\bm{a}}({\bm{q}})|^{2}}{|{\bm{q}}|}-m^{2}\int d^{3}x\sum_{i}\cos(\vect{a}_i)

(67)

In the limit of strong
m^{2}
(deep inside the Higgs phase) it suffices to take
{\bm{a}}(q)\sim\delta(q)
(analogous to the derivation of Ref.
Garratt et al. 2023
), which would make the second term in eq.
61
vanish. This leaves us with the third term which differs from the massless case (eq.
62
) by a factor of 2. In other words the ’t Hooft loop is now

\expectationvalue{e^{\ii\int d\vect{\sigma}\cdot\vect{E}}}=e^{-\frac{4\pi\Lambda}{e^{2}}\mathcal{P}}.

(68)

Hence the ’t Hooft loop still decays as a perimeter law as was expected from the argument in the main text, but with a larger factor, i.e. the electric charge condensation on the
(3+0)d
temporal defect induced by weak measurement still suppresses the SSB of the
\mathrm{U}(1)^{(1)}_{m}
symmetry.
