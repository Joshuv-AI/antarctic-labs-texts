1
Introduction.

1

Intrinsic volumes of convex subsets of a Euclidean space have been studied in convexity for a long time, see e.g. §4.1 of Schneider’s book
[
21
]
. In dimension 2 and 3 this notion goes back to Steiner (1819).
They can be defined using the Steiner formula as follows.
Let
\mathbb{R}^{n}
denote the Euclidean space. Let
B\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
denote the unit Euclidean ball. Let
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be an arbitrary convex compact subset. Set
K_{\varepsilon}
denotes
\varepsilon
-neighborhood of
K
.
The Steiner formula says that volume of
K_{\varepsilon}
is a polynomial of degree
n
in
\varepsilon\geq 0
:

\displaystyle vol(K_{\varepsilon})=\sum_{i=0}^{n}\varepsilon^{n-i}\kappa_{n-i}V_{i}(K),

(1.1)

where
\kappa_{n-i}
are normalizing constants (see
[
21
]
for an explicit expression). For
i=0,1,\dots,n
the coefficient
V_{i}(K)
is called the
i
th intrinsic volume of
K
. Note that
V_{n}(K)
equals the volume of
K
while
V_{0}(K)=1
.
Among important properties of intrinsic volumes of convex bodies let us mention their invariance under isometries of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, continuity in the Hausdorff metric, and the valuation property (i.e. they are finitely additive measures on convex compact sets); see
[
21
]
for details.
Intrinsic volumes are distinguished by the famous Hadwiger’s characterization theorem
[
16
]
saying that a functional on all convex compact subsets of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a linear combination of intrinsic volumes if and only if it is
invariant under Euclidean isometries, continuous in the Hausdorff metric, and is a valuation. Besides that, intrinsic volumes satisfy the Alexandrov-Fenchel inequality (see Ch. 7 in
[
21
]
).

2

In 1939 H. Weyl
[
22
]
has extended the definition of intrinsic volumes to smooth closed Riemannian manifolds. In this context intrinsic volumes are often called Lipschitz-Killing curvatures.
This notion is central for the present paper. The definition is based on a non-trivial result of him stated below.
However we will state it in greater generality
of manifolds with boundary or, still more generally, simplicial corners. Such an extension seems to be a folklore, and this generality seems to be more natural to us.

Let
(M^{n},g)
be a compact smooth Riemannian manifold, possibly with simplicial corners (e.g. with boundary). By the Nash imbedding theorem there exists a smooth isometric imbedding
\iota\colon M\hookrightarrow\mathbb{R}^{L}
into
a Euclidean space of sufficiently high dimension. The Euclidean volume of the
\varepsilon
-neighborhood of the image of
\iota
turns out to be polynomial for small
\varepsilon\geq 0
:

vol_{L}(\iota M)_{\varepsilon}=\varepsilon^{L-n}\sum_{i=0}^{n}c_{L,n,i}\varepsilon^{n-i}V_{i}(M),

where
c_{L,n,i}
are appropriately normalized coefficients independent of
M
and
\iota
. The Weyl theorem says that the coefficients
V_{i}(M)
are independent of the imbedding
\iota
. Weyl himself proved it for closed manifolds; he gave an explicit formula
(see below) in terms of the Riemann curvature tensor.
The case of boundary and corners seems to be known for a long time, but I do not have a classical reference. But this is a special case of Theorem 3.11 in a recent paper
[
15
]
. This definition is obviously consistent with the
definition for convex sets from the previous paragraph when convex sets have smooth boundary.

Note that
V_{n}(M^{n})
equals to the Riemannian volume of
M
,
V_{0}(M)
equals to the Euler characteristic of
M
by the generalized Gauss-Bonnet formula discussed below.
For a closed
M
one has
V_{i}(M^{n})=0
provided
n-i
is odd. Moreover for closed
M
the intrinsic volume
V_{n-2}(M)
is proportional
to the integral of the scalar curvature
\int_{M}Sc(x)dvol_{M}(x)
.

3

As we have mentioned, an important property of intrinsic volumes on convex sets is continuity in the Hausdorff metric. The convergence properties of intrinsic volumes of manifolds seem to be rare in literature.
The earliest known to me result in this direction is due to Cheeger, Müller, and Schrader
[
13
]
who proved a convergence of intrinsic volumes for polyhedral approximations of smooth Riemannian manifolds.

A more recent example is the work in progress by Lebedeva and Petrunin
[
19
]
where it is claimed that for a non-collapsing sequence
\{M_{i}^{n}\}
of closed Riemannian manifolds
with uniformly bounded below sectional curvature converging in the Gromov-Hausdorff sense to an Alexandrov space always there exists
\lim_{i\longrightarrow\infty}V_{n-2}(M^{n}_{i})
.

The author (based on discussions with A. Petrunin
[
20
]
) has formulated a few conjectures
[
6
]
on the behavior of intrinsic volumes under the Gromov-Hausdorff convergence
of Riemannian manifolds, and more generally Alexandrov spaces, with sectional curvature uniformly bounded below. The main result of the present paper verifies one of these conjectures in a very special
case. In fact in this case the assumption of uniform lower boundedness of sectional curvature is unnecessary.

4

Let us formulate the main result.

1.1 Definition
.

A smooth map
\pi\colon M\longrightarrow B
of two smooth Riemannian manifolds is called a Riemannian submersion if for any
o\in M
the differential

d\pi_{o}\colon T_{o}M\longrightarrow T_{\pi(o)}B

being restricted to the orthogonal complement of its kernel
(Ker(d\pi_{o}))^{\perp}
is an isometry.

Note that
Ker(d\pi_{o})
is the tangent space
T_{o}Z_{o}
of the fiber
Z_{o}:=\pi^{-1}\pi(o)
.

Let
\pi\colon M\longrightarrow B
be a Riemannian submersion. Let us change the original Riemannian metric
g
on
M
as follows. Fix any
o\in M
. We have the orthogonal with respect to
g
decomposition of the tangent space

T_{o}M=T_{o}Z_{o}\oplus(T_{o}Z_{o})^{\perp}.

For
\varepsilon>0
let us denote by
g(\varepsilon)
the metric which is equal to
\varepsilon g
on
T_{o}Z_{o}
,
g
on
(T_{o}Z_{o})^{\perp}
, and the two subspaces are still orthogonal to each other with respect to
g(\varepsilon)
. We denote by
M(\varepsilon)

the manifold
M
equipped with the metric
g(\varepsilon)
. The main result is

1.2 Theorem
.

Let
\pi\colon M\longrightarrow B
be a Riemannian submersion of smooth compact manifolds. Let us assume that
B
is connected. Let
Z
denote a fiber of
\pi
. Then for any
i\geq 0

\lim_{\varepsilon\longrightarrow+0}V_{i}(M(\varepsilon))=\chi(Z)V_{i}(B),

where
\chi(Z)
denotes the Euler characteristic of
Z
.

Just to compare with the conjectures
[
6
]
, we show in Proposition
5.1
that under the assumptions of Theorem
1.2
the manifolds
M(\varepsilon)
have uniform lower bound on sectional curvature
when
\varepsilon\longrightarrow+0
if and only if all the fibers of
\pi
have non-negative sectional curvature.

Reminder on Weyl’s and generalized Gauss-Bonnet formulas.

First let us remind the H. Weyl
[
22
]
formula for intrinsic volumes of a closed smooth Riemannian manifold
M^{n}
.
Let
R_{abcd}
denote the Riemann curvature tensor for a Riemannian metric
g_{ab}
.
Let us denote

R^{pq}_{rs}:=g^{pa}g^{qb}R_{abrs},

where
(g^{ab})
denotes the inverse matrix of
(g_{ab})
, and we use the summation convention over repeated indices.

H. Weyl
[
22
]
has shown that for a closed manifold
M^{n}
for odd
e
one has
V_{n-e}(M^{n})=0
, and for even
e
one has

\displaystyle V_{n-e}(M^{n})=(2\pi)^{-e/2}\int_{M}\sum_{[p,q]}sgn(p,q)R^{q_{1}q_{2}}_{p_{1}p_{2}}\dots R^{q_{e-1}q_{e}}_{p_{e-1}p_{e}}dvol_{M},

(2.1)

where
dvol_{M}
is the Riemannian volume form and the sum runs over all couplings
[p,q]
. Here the numbers
p_{1},\dots,p_{e}
are distinct numbers between 1 and
n
, and
q_{1},\dots,q_{e}
are any permutation of the
p_{i}
’s.
The sign
sgn(p,q)
is the sign of the permutation taking
p_{1},\dots,p_{e}
to
q_{1},\dots,q_{e}
.
The coupling
[p,q]
is a partition of pairs

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{c|c|c}(p_{1}p_{2})&(p_{3}p_{4})&\dots\\
(q_{1}q_{2})&(q_{3}q_{4})&\dots\end{array}.

The pairs
(p_{1}p_{2})
etc. of two distinct numbers is unordered; due to symmetries of the curvature tensor and the term
sgn(p,q)
the switch of the order of

p_{1}
and
p_{2}
does not change the summand. The coupling is also unchanged under any permutation of any of
e/2
columns of it.

In 1943 Allendoerfer and Weil
[
7
]
have shown that
V_{0}(M)
defined by the formula (
2.1
) (with
e=n
,
n
is even) coincides with the Euler characteristic
\chi(M)
thus obtaining a generalization of the Gauss-Bonnet formula.
Actually they got a more general result for compact manifolds with simplicial corners. In 1944 Chern
[
14
]
published a simpler proof of this fact for the case of closed manifolds.

A few technical lemmas.

Let
\pi\colon M\longrightarrow B
be a Riemannian submersion of compact manifolds. We fix a point
o\in M
. Define
o^{*}:=\pi(o)
. There exists a neighborhood
U
of
o^{*}
such that
\pi^{-1}(U)
is diffeomorphic to
Z\times U
and
under this identification
\pi
is the obvious projection
Z\times U\longrightarrow U
where
Z
is a fiber of
\pi
. In all local computations throughout this paper we will fix an arbitrary such a diffeomorphism.

We fix coordinates
\{x^{\alpha}\}
near
o^{*}
.
Let us denote
Z_{o^{*}}:=\pi^{-1}(o^{*})
. Let us fix coordinates
x^{i}
on
Z_{o^{*}}
near
o
. Throughout this paper we denote by Greek letters
\alpha,\beta,\gamma,\delta\dots
the coordinates on an open subset
U\subset B
, by Latin letters
i,j,k,l,m,n

coordinates on the fiber
Z_{o^{*}}
, and by Latin letters
p,q,r,s,t
coordinates of either kind on
Z\times U
.
Let
(g(\varepsilon))
the metric on
M
as in Theorem
1.2
.

3.1 Lemma
.

At any point
o\in M
we have

\displaystyle(g(\varepsilon))=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}\varepsilon\cdot(g_{ij})&\varepsilon\cdot(g_{i\alpha})\\
\varepsilon\cdot(g_{\alpha i})&g_{\alpha\beta}\end{array}\right].

Proof.
At any point
o\in M
we denote by
(\cdot,\cdot)
the scalar product on
T_{0}M
for the original metric
g
, and by
(\cdot,\cdot)_{\varepsilon}
for the metric
g(\varepsilon)
.

First it is obvious that
g(\varepsilon)_{ij}=\varepsilon\cdot g_{ij}
.
Next in the orthogonal complement to the tangent space at
o
to the fiber there exists a unique basis
\{\xi_{\alpha}\}

such that
d\pi(\xi_{\alpha})=\partial_{\alpha}
where
\partial_{\alpha}
are the obvious vector fields corresponding to chosen coordinate
x^{\alpha}
.
Clearly

\displaystyle g(\varepsilon)_{\alpha\beta}=(\partial_{\alpha},\partial_{\beta})_{\varepsilon}=(\xi_{\alpha},\xi_{\beta})_{\varepsilon}=(\xi_{\alpha},\xi_{\beta})=g_{\alpha\beta},

(3.2)

where the second equality follows from the Riemannian submersion property of
\pi
.

It remains to prove
g(\varepsilon)_{i\alpha}=\varepsilon g_{i\alpha}
. Let us denote by
(\tilde{g}^{ij})
the matrix inverse to
(g_{ij})
(note that both are matrices of size
N\times N
where
N
is the dimension of the fiber of
\pi
).
First let us express
\xi_{\alpha}
via
\partial_{p}
’s. Since
d\pi(\xi_{\alpha})=\partial_{\alpha}
we have

\xi_{\alpha}=\partial_{\alpha}+h^{i}_{\alpha}\partial_{i},

where
h^{i}_{\alpha}
are some coefficients. Then

\displaystyle 0=(\xi_{\alpha},\partial_{j})=(\partial_{\alpha},\partial_{j})+h^{i}_{\alpha}(\partial_{i},\partial_{j})=g_{\alpha j}+h^{i}_{\alpha}g_{ij}.

Consequently
h^{i}_{\alpha}=-g_{\alpha j}\tilde{g}^{ji}.

Hence

\displaystyle\xi_{\alpha}=\partial_{\alpha}-g_{\alpha j}\tilde{g}^{ji}\partial_{i}.

(3.3)

Using this we have

\displaystyle g(\varepsilon)_{i\alpha}=(\partial_{i},\partial_{\alpha})_{\varepsilon}=(\partial_{i},\xi_{\alpha}+g_{\alpha j}\tilde{g}^{jk}\partial_{k})_{\varepsilon}=g_{\alpha j}\tilde{g}^{jk}(\partial_{i},\partial_{k})_{\varepsilon}=

\displaystyle\varepsilon g_{\alpha j}\tilde{g}^{jk}g_{ik}=\varepsilon g_{\alpha i}=\varepsilon g_{i\alpha}.

Q.E.D.

3.2 Lemma
.

In the above notation the Riemannian metric on
B
at
o^{*}=\pi(o)
is equal to
(g_{\alpha\beta})
.

Proof
immediately follows from
3.2
. Q.E.D.

3.3 Lemma
.

Let us choose the normal coordinate system near
o^{*}=\pi(o)
, (in particular
\partial_{\gamma}g_{\alpha\beta}|_{o}=0
).
Then as
\varepsilon\longrightarrow+0
one has

\displaystyle\partial_{p}g_{qr}(\varepsilon)|_{o}=O(\varepsilon),

\displaystyle\Gamma_{pqr}(\varepsilon)|_{o}=O(\varepsilon).

Proof.
Obviously the second part follows from the first one since

\Gamma_{pqr}(\varepsilon)=\frac{1}{2}(\partial_{r}g_{pq}(\varepsilon)+\partial_{q}g_{pr}(\varepsilon)-\partial_{p}g_{qr}(\varepsilon)).

To prove the first part observe that if at least one of
q,r
is
i,j,...
then

g_{qr}(\varepsilon)=\varepsilon g_{qr}
, and the result follows in this case. In the remaining case
q=\alpha,r=\beta
we have

\partial_{p}g_{\alpha\beta}(\varepsilon)|_{o}=\partial_{p}g_{\alpha\beta}|_{o}.

By Lemma
3.2

g_{\alpha\beta}
is constant along the fibers of
\pi
. Hence
\partial_{i}g_{\alpha\beta}|_{o}=0
.
By the choice of normal coordinate system near
o^{*}
we have
\partial_{\gamma}g_{\alpha\beta}|_{o}=0
. Q.E.D.

3.4 Lemma
.

Let

\displaystyle G=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}A&B\\
C&D\end{array}\right]

be a block matrix with
A,D
invertible square matrices of not necessarily the same size. Then for
\varepsilon\longrightarrow 0
one has

\displaystyle\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}\varepsilon A&\varepsilon B\\
\varepsilon C&D\end{array}\right]^{-1}=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}\varepsilon^{-1}A^{-1}+O(1)&O(1)\\
O(1)&D^{-1}+O(\varepsilon)\end{array}\right]

while the left hand side is automatically invertible for small
\varepsilon>0
.

Proof.
We have

\displaystyle\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}\varepsilon A&\varepsilon B\\
\varepsilon C&D\end{array}\right]=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}A&0\\
0&D\end{array}\right]\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}\varepsilon I&\varepsilon X\\
\varepsilon Y&I\end{array}\right],

where
X=A^{-1}B
,
Y=D^{-1}C
. Hence it suffices to prove the lemma for
A=I,D=I
. It is easy to see that

\displaystyle\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}\varepsilon I&\varepsilon X\\
\varepsilon Y&I\end{array}\right]=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}I&\varepsilon X\\
0&I\end{array}\right]\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}\varepsilon(I-\varepsilon XY)&0\\
0&I\end{array}\right]\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}I&0\\
\varepsilon Y&I\end{array}\right].

Taking the inverse matrix and computing the inverse of each term we finally obtain

\displaystyle\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}\varepsilon I&\varepsilon X\\
\varepsilon Y&I\end{array}\right]^{-1}=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}\varepsilon^{-1}(I-\varepsilon XY)^{-1}&-(I-\varepsilon XY)^{-1}X\\
-Y(I-\varepsilon XY)^{-1}&I+\varepsilon Y(I-\varepsilon XY)^{-1}X\end{array}\right].

The lemma follows. Q.E.D.

At any point

one has

\displaystyle R_{\alpha\beta\gamma\delta}(\varepsilon)=O(1),

(3.9)

\displaystyle R_{ipqr}(\varepsilon)=O(\varepsilon).

(3.10)

3.6 Remark
.

The lemma says that if at least one of the indices is not Greek then
R(\varepsilon)=(\varepsilon)
, otherwise it is just bounded. Indeed this follows from the symmetries of the Riemann curvature tensor.

Proof.
Let us choose near
o^{*}=\pi(o)
normal coordinates. There is a known formula for the Riemann curvature tensor (see e.g. formula (92.1) in

18
]

\displaystyle 2R_{pqrs}(\varepsilon)=

(3.11)

\displaystyle\partial_{q}\partial_{r}g_{ps}(\varepsilon)+\partial_{p}\partial_{s}g_{qr}(\varepsilon)-\partial_{q}\partial_{s}g_{pr}(\varepsilon)-\partial_{p}\partial_{r}g_{qs}(\varepsilon)+2(\Gamma_{tqr}(\varepsilon)\Gamma^{t}_{ps}(\varepsilon)-\Gamma_{tqs}(\varepsilon)\Gamma^{t}_{pr}(\varepsilon)).

(3.12)

Let us show that each of the two
\Gamma\Gamma
summands is

. Let us estimate the first one, the second one is estimated similarly.
We have

\displaystyle\Gamma_{tqr}(\varepsilon)\Gamma^{t}_{ps}(\varepsilon)=\Gamma_{tqr}(\varepsilon)g^{tt^{\prime}}(\varepsilon)\Gamma_{t^{\prime}ps}(\varepsilon)=O(\varepsilon),

where we have used that

and by Lemmas

If at least one of

Proof of Theorem
1.2
.

Let us apply (
2.1
) to
(M,g(\varepsilon))=:M(\varepsilon)
. We denote by
N
the dimension of the fibers of
\pi
. We have

\displaystyle V_{n-e}(M(\varepsilon))=(2\pi)^{-e/2}\varepsilon^{N/2}\int_{M}\sum_{[p,q]}sgn(p,q)R^{q_{1}q_{2}}_{p_{1}p_{2}}(\varepsilon)\dots R^{q_{e-1}q_{e}}_{p_{e-1}p_{e}}(\varepsilon)dvol_{M},

(4.1)

where
dvol_{M}
is the Riemannian volume form corresponding to the original metric
g
(rather than
g(\varepsilon)
).
Indeed it is easy to see that for the metric
g(\varepsilon)
the volume form is equal to
dvol_{M(\varepsilon)}=\varepsilon^{N/2}dvol_{M}
.

4.1 Theorem
.

For a Riemannian submersion
\pi\colon M^{n}\longrightarrow B
of closed Riemannian manifolds

\lim_{\varepsilon\longrightarrow+0}V_{i}(M(\varepsilon))=\chi(Z)\cdot V_{i}(B),

where
Z
is a fiber of
\pi
.

Proof.
If
e
is odd then
V_{n-e}(M(\varepsilon))=0
for all
\varepsilon>0
. If
\dim B-n+e
is odd then
V_{n-e}(B)=0
, and the theorem follows when both
e
and
\dim B-n+e
are odd.
If
e
is odd and
\dim B-n+e
is even then the dimension
N=n-\dim B
of a fiber
Z
is odd and hence
\chi(Z)=0
. Thus the result follows in this case too.

Thus let us assume that
e
is even and consider
V_{n-e}(M(\varepsilon))
defined by (
4.1
). By Lemmas
3.5
and
3.4
we have uniformly on
M

\displaystyle R^{\alpha\beta}_{\gamma\delta}(\varepsilon)=O(1),

\displaystyle R^{\alpha\beta}_{ip}(\varepsilon)=O(\varepsilon),

\displaystyle R^{i\alpha}_{pq}(\varepsilon)=O(1),

\displaystyle R^{ij}_{pq}(\varepsilon)=O(\varepsilon^{-1}).

Let us consider a summand
R^{q_{1}q_{2}}_{p_{1}p_{2}}(\varepsilon)\dots R^{q_{e-1}q_{e}}_{p_{e-1}p_{e}}(\varepsilon)
in (
4.1
). Let us denote by

\tau
the number of terms of the form
R^{ij}_{pq}(\varepsilon)
, and by
\omega
the number of terms of the form
R^{\alpha\beta}_{ip}(\varepsilon)
. Then
the above formulas imply that uniformly on
M

R^{q_{1}q_{2}}_{p_{1}p_{2}}(\varepsilon)\dots R^{q_{e-1}q_{e}}_{p_{e-1}p_{e}}(\varepsilon)=O(\varepsilon^{-\tau+\omega}).

Since all
p_{1},\dots,p_{e}
are pairwise distinct we have
\tau\leq N/2
. Hence uniformly on
M

\varepsilon^{N/2}R^{q_{1}q_{2}}_{p_{1}p_{2}}(\varepsilon)\dots R^{q_{e-1}q_{e}}_{p_{e-1}p_{e}}(\varepsilon)=O(\varepsilon^{N/2-\tau+\omega}).

Thus the following claim holds.

4.2 Claim
.

The expression
\varepsilon^{N/2}R^{q_{1}q_{2}}_{p_{1}p_{2}}(\varepsilon)\dots R^{q_{e-1}q_{e}}_{p_{e-1}p_{e}}(\varepsilon)=O(\varepsilon^{N/2-\tau+\omega})

(1) is always uniformly bounded on
M
as
\varepsilon\longrightarrow+0
;

(2) vanishes in the limit when either
\tau<N/2
or
\omega>0
.

Hence it suffices to consider the summands with
\tau=N/2,\omega=0
simultaneously. In particular
N
must be even for such summands to exist.
Thus if
N
is odd then
\lim_{\varepsilon\longrightarrow+0}V_{n-e}(M(\varepsilon))=0
. At the same time
\chi(Z)=0
and the theorem follows for odd
N
.

Let us assume that
N
is even. Consider all the summands with
\tau=N/2
and
\omega=0
.
For any of them all the indices corresponding to coordinates of the fiber
Z
appear among upper indices of the terms of the form
R^{ij}_{pq}(\varepsilon)
, and
there are no more such indices to appear in terms of the form
R^{i\alpha}_{pq}(\varepsilon)
. In other words if
\tau=N/2
there are no terms of the form
R^{i\alpha}_{pq}(\varepsilon)
.
Hence the only terms which appear are
R^{\alpha\beta}_{\gamma\delta}(\varepsilon)
and
R^{ij}_{pq}(\varepsilon)
. Furthermore since all lower indices are a permutation of upper indices,
the total numbers of Greek upper and lower indices should be the same. Hence the term
R^{ij}_{pq}(\varepsilon)
should be
R^{ij}_{kl}(\varepsilon)
. Hence

\displaystyle(\star):=\lim_{\varepsilon\longrightarrow+0}\varepsilon^{N/2}\sum_{[p,q]}sgn(p,q)R^{q_{1}q_{2}}_{p_{1}p_{2}}(\varepsilon)\dots R^{q_{e-1}q_{e}}_{p_{e-1}p_{e}}(\varepsilon)=

\displaystyle\lim_{\varepsilon\longrightarrow+0}\varepsilon^{N/2}\left(\sum_{[\alpha,\beta]}sgn(\alpha,\beta)R^{\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}}_{\beta_{1}\beta_{2}}(\varepsilon)\dots R^{\alpha_{e-N-1}\alpha_{e-N}}_{\beta_{e-N-1}\beta_{e-N}}(\varepsilon)\right)\cdot

\displaystyle\left(\sum_{[i,j]}sgn(i,j)R^{i_{1}i_{2}}_{j_{1}j_{2}}(\varepsilon)\dots R^{i_{N-1}i_{N}}_{j_{N-1}j_{N}}(\varepsilon)\right).

We claim that this limit exists and

\displaystyle(\star)=\left(\sum_{[\alpha,\beta]}sgn(\alpha,\beta)\hat{R}^{\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}}_{\beta_{1}\beta_{2}}\dots\hat{R}^{\alpha_{e-N-1}\alpha_{e-N}}_{\beta_{e-N-1}\beta_{e-N}}\right)\cdot\left(\sum_{[i,j]}sgn(i,j)\tilde{R}^{i_{1}i_{2}}_{j_{1}j_{2}}\dots\tilde{R}^{i_{N-1}i_{N}}_{j_{N-1}j_{N}}\right)

(4.2)

where
\hat{R}^{\alpha\beta}_{\gamma\delta}
denotes the curvature tensor of
B

5
When
M(\varepsilon)
has uniform lower bound on sectional curvature?

5.1 Proposition
.

The sectional curvature of
M(\varepsilon)
stays uniformly bounded below as
\varepsilon\longrightarrow+0
if and only if the sectional curvature of all fibers of
\pi
is non-negative.

Proof.
Below we use the convention of lifting and lowering indices using the matrix
(g_{pq})
of size
\dim M
. For
p\neq q
let us denote by
K_{pq}(\varepsilon)
the Gauss curvature of
g(\varepsilon)
of the
(x^{p}x^{q})
-plane.
By the known formula

K_{pq}(\varepsilon)=\frac{2R_{pqpq}(\varepsilon)}{g_{pp}(\varepsilon)g_{qq}(\varepsilon)-g_{pq}^{2}(\varepsilon)}.

Case 1.
Assume
p=\alpha,q=\beta
. By Lemma
3.1

\displaystyle K_{\alpha\beta}(\varepsilon)=\frac{2R_{\alpha\beta\alpha\beta}(\varepsilon)}{g_{\alpha\alpha}g_{\beta\beta}-g_{\alpha\beta}^{2}}\overset{(\ref{E:curv2})}{=}\frac{O(1)}{g_{\alpha\alpha}g_{\beta\beta}-g_{\alpha\beta}^{2}}=O(1).

Case 2.
Assume
p=\alpha,q=i
. Similarly
by Lemma
3.1

\displaystyle K_{\alpha i}(\varepsilon)=\frac{2R_{\alpha i\alpha i}(\varepsilon)}{g_{\alpha\alpha}g_{ii}-g_{\alpha i}^{2}}\overset{(\ref{E:curv1})}{=}\frac{O(\varepsilon)}{\varepsilon g_{\alpha\alpha}g_{ii}-\varepsilon^{2}g_{\alpha i}^{2}}=O(1).

Case 3.
Assume
p=i,q=j
. Again

\displaystyle K_{ij}(\varepsilon)=\frac{2R_{ijij}(\varepsilon)}{\varepsilon^{2}(g_{ii}g_{jj}-g_{ij}^{2})}.

(5.1)

We have

\displaystyle 2R_{ijij}(\varepsilon)\overset{(\ref{E:RC-formula})}{=}

(5.2)

\displaystyle 2\partial_{i}\partial_{j}g_{ij}(\varepsilon)-\partial_{i}\partial_{i}g_{jj}(\varepsilon)-\partial_{j}\partial_{j}g_{ii}(\varepsilon)+2(\Gamma_{tij}(\varepsilon)\Gamma^{t}_{ij}(\varepsilon)-\Gamma_{tii}(\varepsilon)\Gamma^{t}_{jj}(\varepsilon)).

\displaystyle\Gamma_{tij}(\varepsilon)\Gamma_{sij}(\varepsilon)g^{st}(\varepsilon)-\Gamma_{tii}(\varepsilon)\Gamma_{sjj}(\varepsilon)g^{st}(\varepsilon).

Using Lemma
3.4

G=g(\varepsilon)

\displaystyle\Gamma_{kij}(\varepsilon)\Gamma_{lij}(\varepsilon)(\tilde{g}^{kl}(\varepsilon)+O(1))-\Gamma_{kii}(\varepsilon)\Gamma_{ljj}(\varepsilon)(\tilde{g}^{kl}(\varepsilon)+O(1)).

3.3

\displaystyle\varepsilon(\Gamma_{kij}\Gamma_{lij}\tilde{g}^{kl}-\Gamma_{kii}\Gamma_{ljj}\tilde{g}^{kl})+O(\varepsilon^{2}).

Substituting this back to (

\displaystyle 2R_{ijij}(\varepsilon)=

(5.5)

\displaystyle\varepsilon(2\partial_{i}\partial_{j}g_{ij}-\partial_{i}\partial_{i}g_{jj}-\partial_{j}\partial_{j}g_{ii})+\varepsilon(\Gamma_{kij}\Gamma_{lij}\tilde{g}^{kl}-\Gamma_{kii}\Gamma_{ljj}\tilde{g}^{kl})+O(\varepsilon^{2}).
