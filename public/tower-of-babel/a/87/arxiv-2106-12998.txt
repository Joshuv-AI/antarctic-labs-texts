Chapter 1
Stochastic Differential Equations and Partial Differential Equations

1.1
Brownian motion

The fundamental building block of the theory of stochastic differential
equations is a mathematical object called
Wiener process
,
or
Brownian motion
. This should not be confused with the physical
phenomenon of Brownian motion, describing for instance the erratic movements of
a small particle in a fluid, though the mathematical model has of course been
introduced as a simplified description of the physical process. There is a huge
literature on properties of Brownian motion. In what follows, we will focus on
only a few of these properties that will be important for links between
stochastic and partial differential equations.

1.1.1
Construction of Brownian motion

Heuristically, Brownian motion can be defined as a scaling limit of a random
walk. Let
\{X_{n}\}_{n\mathbin{\geqslant}0}
be a symmetric random walk on
\mathbb{Z}

variables, taking values
\pm 1

\frac{1}{2}

1.

X_{n}

\mathbb{E}[X_{n}]=0

1.1.2
Basic properties of Brownian motion

The following basic properties of Brownian motion follow more or less
immediately from Definition
1.1.1
.

1.

Markov property:
For any Borel set
A\subset\mathbb{R}
,

\mathbb{P}\Bigl\{W_{t+s}\in A\Bigm|W_{t}=x\Bigr\}=\intop\nolimits_{A}p(t+s,y|t,x)\dd\!y\;,

(1.1.12)

independently of
\{W_{u}\}_{u<t}
, with Gaussian transition probabilities

p(t+s,y|t,x)=\frac{\e^{-(y-x)^{2}/2s}}{\sqrt{2\pi s}}\;.

(1.1.13)

The proof follows directly from the decomposition

W_{t+s}=W_{t}+(W_{t+s}-W_{t})
, where the second term is independent of the
first one, with distribution
{\mathscr{N}}(0,s)
. In particular, one checks the
Chapman–Kolmogorov equation: For
t>u>s
,

p(t,y|s,x)=\intop\nolimits_{\mathbb{R}}p(t,y|u,z)p(u,z|s,x)\dd\!u\;.

(1.1.14)

2.

Differential property:
For all
t\mathbin{\geqslant}0
,

\{W_{t+s}-W_{t}\}_{s\mathbin{\geqslant}0}
is a standard Brownian motion,
independent of
\{W_{u}\}_{u<t}
.

3.

Scaling proprerty:
For all
c>0
,

\{cW_{t/c^{2}}\}_{s\mathbin{\geqslant}0}
is a standard Brownian motion.

4.

Symmetry:

\{-W_{t}\}_{t\mathbin{\geqslant}0}
is a standard Brownian motion.

5.

Gaussian process :
The Wiener process is Gaussian with zero
mean (meaning that its finite-dimensional joint distributions

\mathbb{P}\{W_{t_{1}}\mathbin{\leqslant}x_{1},\dots,W_{t_{n}}\mathbin{\leqslant}x_{n}\}
are centred normal),
and characterised by its covariance

\cov\{W_{t},W_{s}\}\equiv\mathbb{E}[W_{t}W_{s}]=s\wedge t

(1.1.15)

(where
s\wedge t
denotes the minimum of
s
and
t
).

Proof:
.

For
s<t
, we have

\mathbb{E}[W_{t}W_{s}]=\mathbb{E}[W_{s}(W_{s}+W_{t}-W_{s})]=\mathbb{E}[W_{s}^{2}]+\mathbb{E}[W_{s}(W_{t}-W_{s})]=s\;,

(1.1.16)

since the second term vanishes by the independent increments property.
∎

In fact, one can show that a centred Gaussian process whose covariance
satisfies (
1.1.15
) is a standard Wiener process.

One important consequence of the scaling and independent increments properties
is then the following.

{theorem}

[Non-differentiability of Brownian paths]

The paths
t\mapsto W_{t}(\omega)
are almost surely nowhere
Lipschitzian, and thus nowhere differentiable.

Proof:
.

Fix
C<\infty
and introduce, for
n\mathbin{\geqslant}1
, the event

A_{n}=\Biggl\{\omega\colon\exists s\in[0,1]\text{ s.t. }\lvert W_{t}(\omega)-W_{s}(\omega)\rvert\mathbin{\leqslant}C\lvert t-s\rvert\text{ if }\lvert t-s\rvert\mathbin{\leqslant}\frac{3}{n}\Biggr\}\;.

(1.1.17)

We have to show that
\mathbb{P}(A_{n})=0
for all
n
.
Observe that if
n
increases, the condition gets weaker, so that
A_{n}\subset A_{n+1}
. For
n\mathbin{\geqslant}3
and
1\mathbin{\leqslant}k\mathbin{\leqslant}n-2
, define

\displaystyle Y_{k,n}(\omega)

\displaystyle=\max_{j=0,1,2}\biggl\{\Bigl|W_{(k+j)/n}(\omega)-W_{(k+j-1)/n}(\omega)\Bigr|\biggr\}\;,

(1.1.18)

\displaystyle B_{n}

\displaystyle=\bigcupop\displaylimits_{k=1}^{n-2}\biggl\{\omega\colon Y_{k,n}(\omega)\mathbin{\leqslant}\frac{5C}{n}\biggr\}\;.

(1.1.19)

The triangular inequality implies
A_{n}\subset B_{n}
. Indeed,
let
\omega\in A_{n}
. If for instance
s=1
, then for
k=n-2
, one has

\bigl|W_{(n-3)/n}(\omega)-W_{(n-2)/n}(\omega)\bigr|\mathbin{\leqslant}\bigl|W_{(n-3)/n}(\omega)-W_{1}(\omega)\bigr|+\bigl|W_{1}(\omega)-W_{(n-2)/n}(\omega)\bigr|\mathbin{\leqslant}C\biggl(\frac{3}{n}+\frac{2}{n}\biggr)

(1.1.20)

and thus
\omega\in B_{n}
.
It follows from the independent increments and scaling properties that

\mathbb{P}(A_{n})\mathbin{\leqslant}\mathbb{P}(B_{n})\mathbin{\leqslant}n\mathbb{P}\Biggl(\lvert W_{1/n}\rvert\mathbin{\leqslant}\frac{5C}{n}\Biggr)^{3}=n\mathbb{P}\Biggl(\lvert W_{1}\rvert\mathbin{\leqslant}\frac{5C}{\sqrt{n}}\Biggr)^{3}\mathbin{\leqslant}n\Biggl(\frac{10C}{\sqrt{2\pi n}}\Biggr)^{3}\;.

(1.1.21)

Therefore
\mathbb{P}(A_{n})\to 0
for all
n\to\infty
. But since

\mathbb{P}(A_{n})\mathbin{\leqslant}\mathbb{P}(A_{n+1})
for all
n
, this implies
\mathbb{P}(A_{n})=0
for all

n
.
∎

{remark}

[Hölder regularity of Brownian paths]
Even though paths of Brownian motion are nowhere differentiable, one can show
that they do have a regularity that is better than continuity: namely, the
paths are almost surely (locally) Hölder continuous of exponent
\alpha
for
any
\alpha<\frac{1}{2}
. This can be shown by applying the Kolmogorov–Centsov
continuity criterion.

1.1.3
Brownian motion and heat equation

Observe that the Gaussian transition probabilities (
1.1.14
) of the Wiener
process are, up to a scaling, equal to the heat kernel. In particular,

p(t,x|0,0)
satisfies the heat equation

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}p(t,x|0,0)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\Delta p(t,x|0,0)

(1.1.22)

\displaystyle p(0,x|0,0)

\displaystyle=\delta(x)\;,

(1.1.23)

where we write
\Delta
for the second derivative with respect to
x
. This
reflects the fact that paths of Brownian motion have the same diffusive
behaviour as solutions of the heat equation.

Similarly, transition probabilities of
n

given by

p(t+s,y|t,x)=\frac{\e^{-\lVert y-x\rVert^{2}/2s}}{(2\pi s)^{n/2}}\;,

(1.1.24)

and satisfy therefore the
n

It is, however, important to realise that Brownian motion contains much more
information than the solutions of the heat equation, since it gives a
probability distribution on paths
t\mapsto W_{t}(\omega)
, rather than just a
collection of probability distributions for the
W_{t}(\omega)
with

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\Delta p^{\text{k}}(t,x)\qquad x\mathbin{\leqslant}H\;,

(1.1.40)

\displaystyle p(t,H)

\displaystyle=0\;,

(1.1.41)

that is, the heat equation with Dirichlet boundary conditions.

{exercise}

1.2
Ito calculus

While Brownian motion is a useful model with many interesting properties, one
may have to deal with more general processes, such as functions of Brownian
motion. The question then arises, what kinds of stochastic or
partial differential equations are associated with these processes.

{example}

[Brownian motion squared]
Consider Brownian motion squared. Since

\mathbb{P}\bigl\{W_{t}^{2}\mathbin{\leqslant}x\bigr\}=\mathbb{P}\bigl\{\lvert W_{t}\rvert\mathbin{\leqslant}\sqrt{x}\bigr\}=\Phi\Biggl(\sqrt{\frac{x}{t}}\Biggr)-\Phi\Biggl(-\sqrt{\frac{x}{t}}\Biggr)\;,

(1.2.1)

we obtain that the density of
W_{t}^{2}
is given by

2\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\Phi\Biggl(\sqrt{\frac{x}{t}}\Biggr)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi tx}}\e^{-x/(2t)}\;.

(1.2.2)

This function should solve some PDE, which, however,
is not straightforward to guess. In this section, we will develop methods that
will ultimately allow to determine associated PDEs quite easily.

1.2.1
Ito’s integral

The key notion of stochastic calculus is the Ito integral, which allows to give
a meaning to the quantity

\intop\nolimits_{0}^{t}f(s)\dd\!W_{s}

(1.2.3)

for suitable, possibly random functions
f
. Since the Wiener process is not
Lipschitzian, it does not have bounded variation, so that the above integral
cannot be defined as a Riemann–Stieltjes integral. There are nowadays several
(essentially equivalent) ways of defining the above integral, the oldest one
going back to Kiyoshi Ito.

Fix a Brownian motion
\{W_{t}\}_{t\mathbin{\geqslant}0}
. The random variables

\{W_{s}\}_{0\mathbin{\leqslant}s\mathbin{\leqslant}t}
define an increasing sequence
\sigma
-algebras

\{{\mathscr{F}}_{t}\}_{t\mathbin{\geqslant}0}
(called a
filtration
) that will play
a key role in what follows. In particular, we will use the notion of random
variables that are
measurable
with respect to a given
{\mathscr{F}}_{t}
.
Intuitively, these are exactly the random variables that depend only on the
behaviour of the Wiener process up to time
t
.

{definition}

[Ito integral of elementary functions]

Fix a time interval
[0,T]
.
A random function
\{e_{t}\}_{t\in[0,T]}
is called

simple
or
elementary
if there exists a partition

0=t_{0}<t_{1}<\dots<t_{N}=T
of
[0,T]
such that

e_{t}=\sumop\displaylimits_{k=1}^{N}e_{t_{k-1}}\mathbbm{1}_{[t_{k-1},t_{k})}(t)\;.

(1.2.4)

It is called
adapted
to the filtration
\{{\mathscr{F}}_{t}\}_{t\mathbin{\geqslant}0}
if each

e_{t_{k-1}}
is a random variable measurable with respect to

{\mathscr{F}}_{t_{k-1}}
.
For such an elementary function,
Ito’s integral
is defined as

\intop\nolimits_{0}^{t}e_{s}\dd\!W_{s}=\sumop\displaylimits_{k=1}^{m}e_{t_{k-1}}\bigl[W_{t_{k}}-W_{t_{k-1}}\bigr]+e_{t_{m}}\bigl[W_{t}-W_{t_{m}}\bigr]

(1.2.5)

for any
t\in[0,T]
, where
m
is such that
t\in[t_{m},t_{m+1})
.

One easily sees that this integral
is a linear functional of the integrand, and is additive with respect to time
intervals. Furthermore, since each increment
\bigl[W_{t_{k}}-W_{t_{k-1}}\bigr]
is
independent of
e_{t_{k-1}}
, the integral has zero expectation. The key
property is then the following.

{lemma}

[Ito isometry]If
\displaystyle\intop\nolimits_{0}^{t}\mathbb{E}[e_{s}^{2}]\dd\!s<\infty
, then

\mathbb{E}\Biggl[\Biggl(\intop\nolimits_{0}^{t}e_{s}\dd\!W_{s}\Biggr)^{2}\Biggr]=\intop\nolimits_{0}^{t}\mathbb{E}[e_{s}^{2}]\dd\!s\;.

(1.2.6)

Proof:
.

Set
t_{m+1}=t
. Then

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Biggl[\Biggl(\intop\nolimits_{0}^{t}e_{s}\dd\!W_{s}\Biggr)^{2}\Biggr]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\Biggl[\sumop\displaylimits_{k,l=1}^{m+1}e_{t_{k-1}}e_{t_{l-1}}\bigl(W_{t_{k}}-W_{t_{k-1}}\bigr)\bigl(W_{t_{l}}-W_{t_{l-1}}\bigr)\Biggr]

\displaystyle=\sumop\displaylimits_{k=1}^{m+1}\mathbb{E}\bigl[e_{t_{k-1}}^{2}\bigr]\underbrace{\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\bigl(W_{t_{k}}-W_{t_{k-1}}\bigr)^{2}\Bigr]}_{t_{k}-t_{k-1}}=\intop\nolimits_{0}^{t}\mathbb{E}[e_{s}^{2}]\dd\!s\;.

We have used the property of independent increments to eliminate the terms

k\neq l
from the double sum, and the fact that each
e_{s}

is measurable with respect to
{\mathscr{F}}_{s}
.
∎

Ito’s isometry is an isometry between the Hilbert space

L^{2}_{\text{ad}}([0,T]\times\Omega,\mathbb{P})
of adapted square-integrable processes
and the Hilbert space
L^{2}(\Omega,\mathbb{P})
of square-integrable random variables.
For a general square-integrable adapted process
(X_{t})_{t\mathbin{\geqslant}0}
, one can find
a sequence of elementary
e_{n}
such that

\lim_{n\to\infty}\intop\nolimits_{0}^{T}\mathbb{E}\bigl[\bigl(X_{s}-e^{(n)}_{s}\bigr)^{2}\bigr]\dd\!s=0\;.

(1.2.7)

The isometry (
1.2.6
) then shows that for any
t\in[0,T]
, the following
limit exists in

L^{2}(\mathbb{P})
:

\lim_{n\to\infty}\intop\nolimits_{0}^{t}e^{(n)}_{s}\dd\!W_{s}\bydef\intop\nolimits_{0}^{t}X_{s}\dd\!W_{s}\;.

(1.2.8)

This is by definition the Ito integral of
X_{s}
against
W_{s}
. This integral
has the same linearity and additivity properties as integrals of elementary
functions, and also satifies Ito’s isometry

\mathbb{E}\Biggl[\Biggl(\intop\nolimits_{0}^{t}X_{s}\dd\!W_{s}\Biggr)^{2}\Biggr]=\intop\nolimits_{0}^{t}\mathbb{E}[X_{s}^{2}]\dd\!s\;.

(1.2.9)

1.2.2
Ito’s formula

Ito’s formula gives a simple answer to a question we have been asking above,
namely what kind of differential relation governs functions of Brownian motion.
We start by a simple example, which however contains all the essential ideas of
the general case.

{example}

Let us show that

\intop\nolimits_{0}^{t}W_{s}\dd\!W_{s}=\frac{1}{2}W_{t}^{2}-\frac{t}{2}\;.

(1.2.10)

Define the sequence of elementary functions

e^{(n)}_{t}=W_{2^{-n}\lfloor 2^{n}t\rfloor}
. It is then sufficient to check that

\lim_{n\to\infty}\intop\nolimits_{0}^{t}e^{(n)}_{s}\dd\!W_{s}=\frac{1}{2}W_{t}^{2}-\frac{t}{2}\;.

(1.2.11)

Write
t_{k}=k2^{-n}
for
k\mathbin{\leqslant}m=\lfloor 2^{n}t\rfloor
and
t_{m+1}=t
.
The definition (
1.2.5
) implies

\displaystyle 2\intop\nolimits_{0}^{t}e^{(n)}_{s}\dd\!W_{s}

\displaystyle=2\sumop\displaylimits_{k=1}^{m+1}W_{t_{k-1}}\bigl(W_{t_{k}}-W_{t_{k-1}}\bigr)

\displaystyle=\sumop\displaylimits_{k=1}^{m+1}\Bigl[W_{t_{k}}^{2}-W_{t_{k-1}}^{2}-\bigl(W_{t_{k}}-W_{t_{k-1}}\bigr)^{2}\Bigr]

\displaystyle=W_{t}^{2}-\sumop\displaylimits_{k=1}^{m+1}\bigl(W_{t_{k}}-W_{t_{k-1}}\bigr)^{2}\;.

(1.2.12)

Consider now the random variable

M^{(n)}_{t}=\sumop\displaylimits_{k=1}^{m+1}\bigl(W_{t_{k}}-W_{t_{k-1}}\bigr)^{2}-t=\sumop\displaylimits_{k=1}^{m+1}\bigl[\bigl(W_{t_{k}}-W_{t_{k-1}}\bigr)^{2}-\bigl(t_{k}-t_{k-1}\bigr)\bigr]\;.

(1.2.13)

Since all terms of the sum are independent and have zero expectation, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\bigl[\bigl(M^{(n)}_{t}\bigr)^{2}\bigr]

\displaystyle=\sumop\displaylimits_{k=1}^{m+1}\mathbb{E}\bigl[\bigl[\bigl(W_{t_{k}}-W_{t_{k-1}}\bigr)^{2}-\bigl(t_{k}-t_{k-1}\bigr)\bigr]^{2}\bigr]

\displaystyle\mathbin{\leqslant}(m+1)\mathbb{E}\bigl[\bigl[\bigl(W_{t_{1}}-W_{t_{0}}\bigr)^{2}-\bigl(t_{1}-t_{0}\bigr)\bigr]^{2}\bigr]

\displaystyle\mathbin{\leqslant}\text{\it const}\,2^{n}\mathbb{E}\bigl[\bigl[\bigl(W_{2^{-n}}\bigr)^{2}-2^{-n}\bigr]^{2}\bigr]

\displaystyle=\text{\it const}\,2^{-n}\mathbb{E}\bigl[\bigl[\bigl(W_{1}\bigr)^{2}-1\bigr]^{2}\bigr]

\displaystyle\mathbin{\leqslant}\text{\it const}\,2^{-n}\;,

(1.2.14)

owing to the scaling property. Therefore,
M^{(n)}_{t}
converges to
zero in
L^{2}
, proving (
1.2.10
).

{remark}

Using more sophisticated tools from stochastic analysis, it is possible to
prove a stronger type of convergence. Indeed,
\smash{M^{(n)}_{t}}
is what is
known as a submartingale, for which Doob’s inequality yields

\mathbb{P}\biggl\{\sup_{0\mathbin{\leqslant}s\mathbin{\leqslant}t}\bigl(M^{(n)}_{s}\bigr)^{2}>n^{2}2^{-n}\biggr\}\mathbin{\leqslant}2^{n}n^{-2}\mathbb{E}\bigl[\bigl(M^{(n)}_{t}\bigr)^{2}\bigr]\mathbin{\leqslant}\text{\it const}\,n^{-2}\;.

(1.2.15)

The Borel–Cantelli lemma then shows that

\mathbb{P}\biggl\{\sup_{0\mathbin{\leqslant}s\mathbin{\leqslant}t}\bigl|M^{(n)}_{s}\bigr|<n2^{-n/2},n\to\infty\biggr\}=1\;,

(1.2.16)

proving almost sure convergence.

Consider now a stochastic integral of the form

X_{t}=X_{0}+\intop\nolimits_{0}^{t}f_{s}\dd\!s+\intop\nolimits_{0}^{t}g_{s}\dd\!W_{s}\;,\qquad t\in[0,T]

(1.2.17)

where
X_{0}
is a random variable independent of the Brownian motion, and
f

and
g
are two adapted processes satisfying

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\biggl\{\intop\nolimits_{0}^{T}\lvert f_{s}\rvert\dd\!s<\infty\biggr\}

\displaystyle=1

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\biggl\{\intop\nolimits_{0}^{T}g_{s}^{2}\dd\!s<\infty\biggr\}

\displaystyle=1\;.

(1.2.18)

The process (
1.2.17
) can also be written in differential form as

\dd\!X_{t}=f_{t}\dd\!t+g_{t}\dd\!W_{t}\;.

(1.2.19)

For instance, Relation (
1.2.10
) is equivalent to

\dd\!\,(W_{t}^{2})=\dd\!t+2W_{t}\dd\!W_{t}\;.

(1.2.20)

Ito’s formula allows to determine the effect of a change of
variables on the stochastic integral (
1.2.17
) in a general way.

{lemma}

[Ito’s formula]

Let
u:[0,\infty)\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R},(t,x)\mapsto u(t,x)
be
continuously differentiable with respect to
t
and twice
continuously differentiable with respect to
x
. Then the
stochastic process
Y_{t}=u(t,X_{t})
satisfies the equation

\displaystyle Y_{t}={}

\displaystyle Y_{0}+\intop\nolimits_{0}^{t}\frac{\partial u}{\partial t}(s,X_{s})\dd\!s+\intop\nolimits_{0}^{t}\frac{\partial u}{\partial x}(s,X_{s})f_{s}\dd\!s+\intop\nolimits_{0}^{t}\frac{\partial u}{\partial x}(s,X_{s})g_{s}\dd\!W_{s}

\displaystyle{}+\frac{1}{2}\intop\nolimits_{0}^{t}\frac{\partial^{2}u}{\partial x^{2}}(s,X_{s})g_{s}^{2}\dd\!s\;.

(1.2.21)

Proof:
.

It suffices to prove the result for elementary integrands, and by additivity of
the integrals, one can reduce the problem to the case of constant integrands.
In that case,
X_{t}=f_{0}t+g_{0}W_{t}
and
Y_{t}=u(t,f_{0}t+g_{0}W_{t})

can be expressed as functions of
(t,W_{t})
. It suffices thus to
consider the case
X_{t}=W_{t}
. Now for a partition

0=t_{0}<t_{1}<\dots<t_{n}=t
, one has

\displaystyle u(t,W_{t})-u(0,0)

\displaystyle=\sumop\displaylimits_{k=1}^{n}\bigl[u(t_{k},W_{t_{k}})-u(t_{k-1},W_{t_{k}})\bigr]+\bigl[u(t_{k-1},W_{t_{k}})-u(t_{k-1},W_{t_{k-1}})\bigr]

\displaystyle=\sumop\displaylimits_{k=1}^{n}\frac{\partial u}{\partial t}(t_{k-1},W_{t_{k}})(t_{k}-t_{k-1})+\frac{\partial u}{\partial x}(t_{k-1},W_{t_{k-1}})(W_{t_{k}}-W_{t_{k-1}})

\displaystyle\phantom{=}{}+\frac{1}{2}\frac{\partial^{2}u}{\partial x^{2}}(t_{k-1},W_{t_{k-1}})(W_{t_{k}}-W_{t_{k-1}})^{2}+{\scriptstyle\mathcal{O}}\Bigl(t_{k}-t_{k-1}\Bigr)+{\scriptstyle\mathcal{O}}\Bigl((W_{t_{k}}-W_{t_{k-1}})^{2}\Bigr)

\displaystyle=\intop\nolimits_{0}^{t}\frac{\partial u}{\partial t}(s,W_{s})\dd\!s+\intop\nolimits_{0}^{t}\frac{\partial u}{\partial x}(s,W_{s})\dd\!W_{s}+\frac{1}{2}\intop\nolimits_{0}^{t}\frac{\partial^{2}u}{\partial x^{2}}(s,W_{s})\dd\!s

\displaystyle\phantom{=}{}+\sumop\displaylimits_{k=1}^{n}\frac{1}{2}\frac{\partial^{2}u}{\partial x^{2}}(t_{k-1},W_{t_{k-1}})\bigl[(W_{t_{k}}-W_{t_{k-1}})^{2}-(t_{k}-t_{k-1})\bigr]+{\scriptstyle\mathcal{O}}(1)\;.

(1.2.22)

The sum can be dealt with as
M^{(n)}_{t}
in the above example when

t_{k}-t_{k-1}\to 0
, c.f. (
1.2.13
).
∎

{remark}

1.

Ito’s formula can be written in differential form as

\dd\!Y_{t}=\frac{\partial u}{\partial t}(t,X_{t})\dd\!t+\frac{\partial u}{\partial x}(t,X_{t})\bigl[f_{t}\dd\!t+g_{t}\dd\!W_{t}\bigr]+\frac{1}{2}\frac{\partial^{2}u}{\partial x^{2}}(t,X_{t})g_{t}^{2}\dd\!t\;.

(1.2.23)

2.

A mnemotechnic way to recover the formula is to write it in the form

\dd\!Y_{t}=\frac{\partial u}{\partial t}\dd\!t+\frac{\partial u}{\partial x}\dd\!X_{t}+\frac{1}{2}\frac{\partial^{2}u}{\partial x^{2}}\dd\!X_{t}^{2}\;,

(1.2.24)

where
\dd\!X_{t}^{2}
can be computed using the rules

\dd\!t^{2}=\dd\!t\dd\!W_{t}=0,\qquad\dd\!W_{t}^{2}=\dd\!t\;.

(1.2.25)

3.

The formula can be generalised to functions

u(t,X^{(1)}_{t},\dots,X^{(n)}_{t})
, depending on
n
processes defined
by
\dd\!X^{(i)}_{t}=f^{(i)}_{t}\dd\!t+g^{(i)}_{t}\dd\!W_{t}
, to

\dd\!Y_{t}=\frac{\partial u}{\partial t}\dd\!t+\sumop\displaylimits_{i}\frac{\partial u}{\partial x_{i}}\dd\!X^{(i)}_{t}+\frac{1}{2}\sumop\displaylimits_{i,j}\frac{\partial^{2}u}{\partial x_{i}\partial x_{j}}\dd\!X^{(i)}_{t}\dd\!X^{(j)}_{t}\;,

(1.2.26)

\dd\!X^{(i)}_{t}\dd\!X^{(j)}_{t}=g^{(i)}_{t}g^{(j)}_{t}\dd\!t
.

Therefore,
M_{t}=\exp\bigl\{\gamma W_{t}-\gamma^{2}\frac{t}{2}\bigr\}
solves the
equation

\dd\!M_{t}=\gamma M_{t}\dd\!W_{t}\;.

(1.2.28)

{exercise}

(1.2.29)

[Stratonovich integral]

Let
\{W_{t}\}_{t\in[0,T]}

Let
0=t_{0}<t_{1}<\dots<t_{N}=T
be a partition of

The Stratonovich integral of

\intop\nolimits_{0}^{T}e_{t}\circ\dd\!W_{t}=\sumop\displaylimits_{k=1}^{N}\frac{e_{t_{k}}+e_{t_{k-1}}}{2}\Delta W_{k}\qquad\text{where }\Delta W_{k}=W_{t_{k}}-W_{t_{k-1}}\;.

\intop\nolimits_{0}^{T}e^{(n)}_{t}\circ\dd\!W_{t}\;,

(1.2.33)

where
e^{(n)}
is a sequence of elementary functions
converging to
X_{t}
in

. Assume that this limit exists and is independent
of the sequence
e^{(n)}
.

Compute

\intop\nolimits_{0}^{T}W_{t}\circ\dd\!W_{t}\;.

(1.2.34)

1.2.3
Stochastic differential equations

A
stochastic differential equation
(SDE) is an equation of the form

\dd\!X_{t}=f(X_{t},t)\dd\!t+g(X_{t},t)\dd\!W_{t},

(1.2.38)

where
f,g:\mathbb{R}\times[0,T]\to\mathbb{R}
are deterministic measurable functions. A

strong solution
if this equation is by definition an adapted process
satisfying

X_{t}=X_{0}+\intop\nolimits_{0}^{t}f(X_{s},s)\dd\!s+\intop\nolimits_{0}^{t}g(X_{s},s)\dd\!W_{s}

(1.2.39)

almost surely for all
t\in[0,T]
, as well as the regularity conditions

\mathbb{P}\Biggl\{\intop\nolimits_{0}^{T}\lvert f(X_{s},s)\rvert\dd\!s<\infty\Biggr\}=\mathbb{P}\Biggl\{\intop\nolimits_{0}^{T}g(X_{s},s)^{2}\dd\!s<\infty\Biggr\}=1\;.

(1.2.40)

Here are two important examples of solvable SDEs.

{example}

[Linear SDE with additive noise]

Consider the linear SDE with additive noise

\dd\!X_{t}=a(t)X_{t}\dd\!t+\sigma(t)\dd\!W_{t}\;,

(1.2.41)

where
a
and
\sigma
are deterministic functions. In the particular case

\sigma\equiv 0
, the solution can be simply written

X_{t}=\e^{\alpha(t)}X_{0}\;,\qquad\alpha(t)=\intop\nolimits_{0}^{t}a(s)\,\dd\!s\;.

(1.2.42)

This suggest applying the method of variation of the constant, that is, looking
for a solution of the form

X_{t}=\e^{\alpha(t)}Y_{t}
. Ito’s formula applied to

Y_{t}=u(X_{t},t)=\e^{-\alpha(t)}X_{t}
gives us

\dd\!Y_{t}=-a(t)\e^{-\alpha(t)}X_{t}\,\dd\!t+\e^{-\alpha(t)}\,\dd\!X_{t}=\e^{-\alpha(t)}\sigma(t)\,\dd\!W_{t}\;,

(1.2.43)

so that integrating and using
Y_{0}=X_{0}
, one gets

Y_{t}=X_{0}+\intop\nolimits_{0}^{t}\e^{-\alpha(s)}\sigma(s)\,\dd\!W_{s}\;.

(1.2.44)

This finally gives the strong solution of equation (
1.2.41
)

X_{t}=X_{0}\e^{\alpha(t)}+\intop\nolimits_{0}^{t}\e^{\alpha(t)-\alpha(s)}\sigma(s)\,\dd\!W_{s}\;.

(1.2.45)

One checks that this process indeed solves (
1.2.39
) by applying Ito’s
formula once again.
Note in particular that if the initial condition
X_{0}
is
deterministic, then
X_{t}
follows a normal law, with expectation

\mathbb{E}[X_{t}]=X_{0}\e^{\alpha(t)}
and variance

\variance(X_{t})=\intop\nolimits_{0}^{t}\e^{2(\alpha(t)-\alpha(s))}\sigma(s)^{2}\,\dd\!s\;,

(1.2.46)

as a consequence of Ito’s isometry.

{example}

[Linear SDE with multiplicative noise]

Consider the linear SDE with multiplicative noise

\dd\!X_{t}=a(t)X_{t}\dd\!t+\sigma(t)X_{t}\dd\!W_{t}\;,

(1.2.47)

with again
a
and
\sigma
deterministic functions. We can then write

\frac{\dd\!X_{t}}{X_{t}}=a(t)\dd\!t+\sigma(t)\dd\!W_{t}\;.

(1.2.48)

Integrating the left-hand side, one should get
\log(X_{t})
,
but is this compatible with Ito calculus? To check this, set

Y_{t}=u(X_{t})=\log(X_{t})
. Then Ito’s formula gives

\displaystyle\dd\!Y_{t}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{X_{t}}\,\dd\!X_{t}-\frac{1}{2X_{t}^{2}}\,\dd\!X_{t}^{2}

\displaystyle=a(t)\dd\!t+\sigma(t)\dd\!W_{t}-\frac{1}{2}\sigma(t)^{2}\dd\!t\;.

(1.2.49)

Integrating and taking the exponential, one obtains the strong solution

X_{t}=X_{0}\exp\biggl\{\intop\nolimits_{0}^{t}\bigl[a(s)-\frac{1}{2}\sigma(s)^{2}\bigr]\dd\!s+\intop\nolimits_{0}^{t}\sigma(s)\dd\!W_{s}\biggr\}\;.

(1.2.50)

In particular, if
a\equiv 0
and
\sigma\equiv\gamma
, one recovers

X_{t}=X_{0}\exp\{\gamma W_{t}-\gamma^{2}t/2\}
, which is called the

geometric (or exponential) Brownian motion
.

We now state an existence and uniqueness result of solutions for a class of
SDEs.

{theorem}

[Existence and uniqueness of a strong solution]

Assume the functions
f
and
g
satisfy the following two conditions:

1.

Global Lipschitz condition:
there exists a constant
K

such that

\lvert f(x,t)-f(y,t)\rvert+\lvert g(x,t)-g(y,t)\rvert\mathbin{\leqslant}K\lvert x-y\rvert

(1.2.51)

for all
x,y\in\mathbb{R}
and
t\in[0,T]
.

2.

Bounded growth condition:
there exists a constant

L
such that

\lvert f(x,t)\rvert+\lvert g(x,t)\rvert\mathbin{\leqslant}L(1+\lvert x\rvert)

(1.2.52)

for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
and
t\in[0,T]
.

Then the SDE (
1.2.38
) admits, for any square-integrable initial condition

X_{0}
, a strong solution
\{X_{t}\}_{t\in[0,T]}
, which is almost surely
continuous. This solution is unique in the sense that if

\{X_{t}\}_{t\in[0,T]}

and
\{Y_{t}\}_{t\in[0,T]}
are two almost surely continuous solutions, then

\mathbb{P}\biggl\{\sup_{0\mathbin{\leqslant}t\mathbin{\leqslant}T}\lvert X_{t}-Y_{t}\rvert>0\biggr\}=0\;.

(1.2.53)

We will omit the details of the proof of this result, which is very similar to
corresponding proofs in the deterministic case. Uniqueness follows by
estimating the derivative of the expected
difference
\mathbb{E}\bigl[\lvert X_{t}-Y_{t}\rvert^{2}\bigr]
and applying
Gronwall’s lemma, while existence is obtained by applying a fixed-point
argument, or more precisely by showing that the sequence of functions

X^{(k+1)}_{t}=X_{0}+\intop\nolimits_{0}^{t}f(X^{(k)}_{s},s)\,\dd\!s+\intop\nolimits_{0}^{t}g(X^{(k)}_{s},s)\,\dd\!W_{s}

(1.2.54)

converges to a limit which solves the SDE.

{remark}

[Weaker conditions on drift and diffusion coefficients]
The conditions on
f
and
g
in the above result can be relaxed to the
following ones:

1.

Local Lipschitz condition:
For any compact

{\mathscr{K}}\in\mathbb{R}
, there exists a constant
K=K({\mathscr{K}})
such that

\lvert f(x,t)-f(y,t)\rvert+\lvert g(x,t)-g(y,t)\rvert\mathbin{\leqslant}K\lvert x-y\rvert

(1.2.55)

for all
x,y\in{\mathscr{K}}
and
t\in[0,T]
.

2.

Bounded growth condition:
There exists a constant

L
such that

xf(x,t)+g(x,t)^{2}\mathbin{\leqslant}L^{2}(1+x^{2})

(1.2.56)

for all
x,t
.

Indeed, one can show that under the local Lipschitz condition, any solution
path
X_{t}(\omega)
either exists up to time
T
, or leaves any compact
{\mathscr{K}}
at
a time
\tau(\omega)<T
. Therefore, there exists a random blow-up time
\tau
,
such that either
\tau(\omega)=+\infty
and then
X_{t}(\omega)
exists up to
time
T
, or
\tau(\omega)\mathbin{\leqslant}T
, and then
X_{t}(\omega)\to\pm\infty
as

t\to\tau(\omega)
.

Under the bounded growth condition, one shows that solution paths

X_{t}(\omega)
cannot blow up (because the drift term does not grow fast
enough, or pulls paths back towards the origin if
xf(x,t)
is
negative).

{exercise}

Solve the SDE

\dd\!X_{t}=-\frac{1}{2}X_{t}\dd\!t+\sqrt{1-X_{t}^{2}}\dd\!W_{t}\;,\qquad X_{0}=0

(1.2.57)

using the change of variables
Y=\Arcsin(X)
.

{exercise}

Fix
r,\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
. Solve the SDE

\dd\!Y_{t}=r\dd\!t+\alpha Y_{t}\dd\!W_{t}\;,\qquad Y_{0}=1

(1.2.58)

by using the “integrating factor”
F_{t}=\e^{-\alpha W_{t}+\frac{1}{2}\alpha^{2}t}
,
and considering
X_{t}=F_{t}Y_{t}
.

1.3
Diffusions

A
diffusion
is a stochastic process solving an SDE of the form

\dd\!X_{t}=f(X_{t})\dd\!t+g(X_{t})\dd\!W_{t}\;,

(1.3.1)

with a drift coefficient
f
(modelling a deterministic force), and a diffusion
coefficient
g
(modelling a random effect such as collisions with particles of
a fluid). When speaking of diffusions, we focus on the dependence of solutions
on the initial condition
X_{0}=x
, which is one of the main mechanisms
creating links between SDEs and PDEs.

{definition}

[Ito diffusion]
A
time-homogeneous Ito diffusion
is a stochastic process

\{X_{t}(\omega)\}_{t\mathbin{\geqslant}0}
satisfying an SDE of the form

\dd\!X_{t}=f(X_{t})\dd\!t+g(X_{t})\dd\!W_{t}\;,\qquad t\mathbin{\geqslant}s>0\;,\quad X_{s}=x\;,

(1.3.2)

where
W_{t}
is a standard Brownian motion of dimension
m
, and the

drift coefficient

f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
diffusion coefficient

g:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}
are such that the SDE (
1.3.2
) admits a unique
solution for all times.

We will denote the solution of (
1.3.2
)
X^{s,x}_{t}
.

1.3.1
The Markov property

Time homogeneity, that is, the fact that
f
and
g
do not depend on time,
has the following important consequence.

{lemma}

[Time homogeneity of the law]

The processes
\{X^{s,x}_{s+h}\}_{h\mathbin{\geqslant}0}
and
\{X^{0,x}_{h}\}_{h\mathbin{\geqslant}0}

have the same distribution.

Proof:
.

By definition,
X^{0,x}_{h}
satisfies the integral equation

X^{0,x}_{h}=x+\intop\nolimits_{0}^{h}f(X^{0,x}_{v})\dd\!v+\intop\nolimits_{0}^{h}g(X^{0,x}_{v})\dd\!W_{v}\;.

(1.3.3)

Furthermore,
X^{s,x}_{s+h}
satisfies the equation

\displaystyle X^{s,x}_{s+h}

\displaystyle=x+\intop\nolimits_{s}^{s+h}f(X^{s,x}_{u})\dd\!u+\intop\nolimits_{s}^{s+h}g(X^{s,x}_{u})\dd\!W_{u}

\displaystyle=x+\intop\nolimits_{0}^{h}f(X^{s,x}_{s+v})\dd\!v+\intop\nolimits_{0}^{h}g(X^{s,x}_{s+v})\dd\!\widetilde{W}_{v}

(1.3.4)

where we have used the change of variables
u=s+v
, and
\widetilde{W}_{v}=W_{s+v}-W_{s}
. By the differential property,
\widetilde{W}_{v}
is
a standard Brownian motion, so that by uniqueness of solutions of the
SDE (
1.3.2
), the integrals (
1.3.4
) and (
1.3.3
)
have the same distribution.
∎

We will denote
\mathbb{P}^{\mskip 1.5mux}
the probability measure on the

\sigma
-algebra generated by all random variables
X^{0,x}_{t}
,
t\mathbin{\geqslant}0
,

x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, defined by

{\mathbb{P}\rule{0.0pt}{9.0pt}}^{\mskip 1.5mux}\bigl\{X_{t_{1}}\in A_{1},\dots,X_{t_{k}}\in A_{k}\bigr\}=\mathbb{P}\bigl\{X^{0,x}_{t_{1}}\in A_{1},\dots,X^{0,x}_{t_{k}}\in A_{k}\bigr\}

(1.3.5)

for any choice of times
0\mathbin{\leqslant}t_{1}<t_{2}<\dots<t_{k}
and Borel sets

A_{1},\dots,A_{k}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Expectations with respect to
\mathbb{P}^{\mskip 1.5mux}
will be denoted
\mathbb{E}^{\mskip 1.5mux}
.

{theorem}

[Markov propery for Ito diffusions]

For any bounded measurable function
\varphi:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
,

\mathbb{E}^{\mskip 1.5mux}\bigl[\varphi(X_{t+h})\bigm|{\mathscr{F}}_{t}\bigr](\omega)={\mathbb{E}\rule{0.0pt}{9.0pt}}^{\mskip 1.5muX_{t}(\omega)}\bigl[\varphi(X_{h})\bigr]\;,

(1.3.6)

where the right-hand side denotes the function

\mathbb{E}^{\mskip 1.5muy}\!\left[\varphi(X_{h})\right]
evaluated at
y=X_{t}(\omega)
.

Proof:
.

Consider for
y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
s\mathbin{\geqslant}t
the function

F(y,t,s,\omega)=X^{t,y}_{s}(\omega)=y+\intop\nolimits_{t}^{s}f(X_{u}(\omega))\dd\!u+\intop\nolimits_{t}^{s}g(X_{u}(\omega))\dd\!W_{u}(\omega)\;.

(1.3.7)

Note that
F
is independent of
{\mathscr{F}}_{t}
. By uniqueness of solutions of
the SDE (
1.3.2
), we have

X_{s}(\omega)=F(X_{t}(\omega),t,s,\omega)\;.

(1.3.8)

Let
g(y,\omega)=\varphi\circ F(y,t,t+h,\omega)
. One can check that this
function is measurable.
Relation (
1.3.6
) is thus equivalent to

\mathbb{E}\bigl[g(X_{t},\omega)\bigm|{\mathscr{F}}_{t}\bigr]=\mathbb{E}\bigl[\varphi\circ F(y,0,h,\omega)\bigr]\Bigr|_{y=X_{t}(\omega)}^{\phantom{y=X_{t}(\omega)}}\;.

(1.3.9)

We have

\mathbb{E}\bigl[g(X_{t},\omega)\bigm|{\mathscr{F}}_{t}\bigr]=\mathbb{E}\bigl[g(y,\omega)\bigm|{\mathscr{F}}_{t}\bigr]\Bigr|_{y=X_{t}(\omega)}^{\phantom{y=X_{t}(\omega)}}\;.

(1.3.10)

Indeed, this relation is true for functions of the form

g(y,\omega)=\phi(y)\psi(\omega)
, since

\mathbb{E}\bigl[\phi(X_{t})\psi(\omega)\bigm|{\mathscr{F}}_{t}\bigr]=\phi(X_{t})\mathbb{E}\bigl[\psi(\omega)\bigm|{\mathscr{F}}_{t}\bigr]=\mathbb{E}\bigl[\phi(y)\psi(\omega)\bigm|{\mathscr{F}}_{t}\bigr]\Bigr|_{y=X_{t}(\omega)}^{\phantom{y=X_{t}(\omega)}}\;.

(1.3.11)

It can thus be extended to any bounded measurable function, by approximating it
by a sequence of linear combinations of functions as above. It follows from the
independence of
F
and
{\mathscr{F}}_{t}
that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\bigl[g(y,\omega)\bigm|{\mathscr{F}}_{t}\bigr]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\bigl[g(y,\omega)\bigr]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\bigl[\varphi\circ F(y,t,t+h,\omega)\bigr]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\bigl[\varphi\circ F(y,0,h,\omega)\bigr]\;,

(1.3.12)

where the last equality follows from Lemma
1.3.1
.
The result then follows by evaluating the last inequality at
y=X_{t}
.
∎

There exists an important generalisation of the Markov property to so-called
stopping times. We have already encountered such a time in André’s reflection
principle, see Proposition
1.1.3
, with the random
time

\tau=\inf\{t\mathbin{\geqslant}0\colon W_{t}\mathbin{\geqslant}H\}
. The general definition of a
stopping time is as follows.

{definition}

[Stopping time]

A
stopping time
is a random variable

\tau:\Omega\to[0,\infty]
such that
\{\tau<t\}\in{\mathscr{F}}_{t}
for all

t\mathbin{\geqslant}0
. For such a stopping time, the
pre-
\tau
sigma algebra is
defined by

{\mathscr{F}}_{\tau}=\bigl\{A\in{\mathscr{F}}\colon A\cap\{\tau\mathbin{\leqslant}t\}\in{\mathscr{F}}_{t}\;\forall t\mathbin{\geqslant}0\bigr\}

(1.3.13)

In what follows, it will be sufficient to know that first-exit times

\tau=\inf\{t>0\colon X_{t}\notin A\}

(1.3.14)

of an open or closed set
A
are stopping times. The pre-
\tau
sigma algebra
is in this case the set of all events that only depend on the behaviour of the
process as long as it stays in
A
.

The generalisation of the Markov property to stopping times reads as follows.

{theorem}

[Strong Markov property for Ito diffusions]

For any bounded, measurable function
\varphi:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
and almost
surely finite stopping time
\tau
,

\mathbb{E}^{\mskip 1.5mux}\bigl[\varphi(X_{\tau+h})\bigm|{\mathscr{F}}_{\tau}\bigr](\omega)={\mathbb{E}\rule{0.0pt}{9.0pt}}^{\mskip 1.5muX_{\tau}(\omega)}\bigl[\varphi(X_{h})\bigr]\;.

(1.3.15)

Proof:
.

The proof is a relatively direct adaptation of the previous proof. See for
instance
[
Øks03
, Theorem 7.2.4]
.
∎

1.3.2
Semigroups and generators

{definition}

[Markov semi-group]

To any bounded measurable function
\varphi:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
, one associates for all

t\mathbin{\geqslant}0
the function
P_{t}\varphi
defined by

(P_{t}\varphi)(x)={\mathbb{E}\rule{0.0pt}{9.0pt}}^{\mskip 1.5mux}\bigl[\varphi(X_{t})\bigr]\;.

(1.3.16)

The linear operator
P_{t}
is called the
Markov semi-group

of the diffusion.

For instance, if
\varphi(x)=\mathbbm{1}_{A}(x)
denotes the indicator function
of a Borel set
A\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
, one has

(P_{t}\mathbbm{1}_{A})(x)={\mathbb{P}\rule{0.0pt}{9.0pt}}^{\mskip 1.5mux}\bigl\{X_{t}\in A\bigr\}\;.

(1.3.17)

The name semi-group is justified by the following result.

{lemma}

[Semi-group property]
For any
t,h\mathbin{\geqslant}0
, one has

P_{h}\circ P_{t}=P_{t+h}\;.

(1.3.18)

Proof:
.

We have

\displaystyle(P_{h}\circ P_{t})(\varphi)(x)

\displaystyle=(P_{h}(P_{t}\varphi))(x)

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}\rule{0.0pt}{9.0pt}}^{\mskip 1.5mux}\bigl[(P_{t}\varphi)(X_{h})\bigr]

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}\rule{0.0pt}{9.0pt}}^{\mskip 1.5mux}\bigl[{\mathbb{E}\rule{0.0pt}{9.0pt}}^{\mskip 1.5muX_{h}}\bigl[\varphi(X_{t})\bigr]\bigr]

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}\rule{0.0pt}{9.0pt}}^{\mskip 1.5mux}\bigl[\mathbb{E}^{\mskip 1.5mux}\bigl[\varphi(X_{t+h})\bigm|{\mathscr{F}}_{t}\bigr]\bigr]

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}\rule{0.0pt}{9.0pt}}^{\mskip 1.5mux}\bigl[\varphi(X_{t+h})\bigr]

\displaystyle=(P_{t+h}\varphi)(x)\;,

(1.3.19)

where we have used the Markov property to go from the third to the fourth line.
∎

The following properties are easy to check:

1.

P_{t}
preserves constant functions:
P_{t}(c\mathbbm{1}_{\mathbb{R}^{n}})=c\mathbbm{1}_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}
;

2.

P_{t}
preserves non-negative functions:
\varphi(x)\mathbin{\geqslant}0\;\forall x\Rightarrow(P_{t}\varphi)(x)\mathbin{\geqslant}0\;\forall x
;

3.

P_{t}
is contracting (in the non-strict sense) in the
L^{\infty}
-norm:

\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\bigl|(P_{t}\varphi)(x)\bigr|=\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\bigl|{\mathbb{E}\rule{0.0pt}{9.0pt}}^{\mskip 1.5mux}\bigl[\varphi(X_{t})\bigr]\bigr|\mathbin{\leqslant}\sup_{y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\bigl|\varphi(y)\bigr|\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}{\mathbb{E}\rule{0.0pt}{9.0pt}}^{\mskip 1.5mux}\bigl[1\bigr]=\sup_{y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\bigl|\varphi(y)\bigr|\;.

(1.3.20)

The Markov semigroup is thus a positive, linear operator, which is bounded in

L^{\infty}
-norm (in fact, it has operator norm
1
).

The semi-group property implies that the behaviour of
P_{t}
on any interval

[0,\varepsilon]
, with
\varepsilon>0
arbitrarily small, determines its behaviour for any

t\mathbin{\geqslant}0
. It is thus natural to consider the derivative of
P_{t}
in
t=0
.

{definition}

[Infinitesimal generator of an Ito diffusion]

The
infinitesimal generator

{\mathscr{L}}
of an Ito diffusion is defined by its
action on test functions
\varphi
via

({\mathscr{L}}\varphi)(x)=\lim_{h\to 0_{+}}\frac{(P_{h}\varphi)(x)-\varphi(x)}{h}\;.

(1.3.21)

The domain of
{\mathscr{L}}
is by definition the set of functions
\varphi
for which
the limit (
1.3.21
) exists for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

{remark}

Formally, Relation (
1.3.21
) can be written

{\mathscr{L}}=\frac{\dd\!P_{t}}{\dd\!t}\Bigr|_{t=0}^{\phantom{t=0}}\;.

(1.3.22)

By the Markov property, this relation generalises to

\frac{\dd\!}{\dd\!t}P_{t}=\lim_{h\to 0_{+}}\frac{P_{t+h}-P_{t}}{h}=\lim_{h\to 0_{+}}\frac{P_{h}-\id}{h}P_{t}={\mathscr{L}}P_{t}\;,

(1.3.23)

and the semigroup can thus by formally written

P_{t}=\e^{t{\mathscr{L}}}\;.

(1.3.24)

{proposition}

The generator of the Ito diffusion (
1.3.2
) is the differential
operator

{\mathscr{L}}=\sumop\displaylimits_{i=1}^{n}f_{i}(x)\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{i}}+\frac{1}{2}\sumop\displaylimits_{i,j=1}^{n}(gg^{T})_{ij}(x)\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x_{i}\partial x_{j}}\;.

(1.3.25)

The domain of
{\mathscr{L}}
contains the set of twice continuously
differentiable functions of compact support.

Proof:
.

Consider the case
n=m=1
. Let
\varphi
be a twice continuously
differentiable function of compact support, and let
Y_{t}=\varphi(X_{t})
.
By Ito’s formula,

Y_{h}=\varphi(X_{0})+\intop\nolimits_{0}^{h}\varphi^{\prime}(X_{s})f(X_{s})\dd\!s+\intop\nolimits_{0}^{h}\varphi^{\prime}(X_{s})g(X_{s})\dd\!W_{s}+\frac{1}{2}\intop\nolimits_{0}^{h}\varphi^{\prime\prime}(X_{s})g(X_{s})^{2}\dd\!s\;.

(1.3.26)

Taking the expectation, as the expectation of the Ito integral vanishes, one
gets

{\mathbb{E}\rule{0.0pt}{9.0pt}}^{\mskip 1.5mux}\bigl[Y_{h}\bigr]=\varphi(x)+{\mathbb{E}\rule{0.0pt}{9.0pt}}^{\mskip 1.5mux}\biggl[\intop\nolimits_{0}^{h}\varphi^{\prime}(X_{s})f(X_{s})\dd\!s+\frac{1}{2}\intop\nolimits_{0}^{h}\varphi^{\prime\prime}(X_{s})g(X_{s})^{2}\dd\!s\biggr]\;,

(1.3.27)

so that

\frac{{\mathbb{E}\rule{0.0pt}{9.0pt}}^{\mskip 1.5mux}\bigl[\varphi(X_{h})\bigr]-\varphi(x)}{h}=\frac{1}{h}\intop\nolimits_{0}^{h}{\mathbb{E}\rule{0.0pt}{9.0pt}}^{\mskip 1.5mux}\bigl[\varphi^{\prime}(X_{s})f(X_{s})\bigr]\dd\!s+\frac{1}{2h}\intop\nolimits_{0}^{h}{\mathbb{E}\rule{0.0pt}{9.0pt}}^{\mskip 1.5mux}\bigl[\varphi^{\prime\prime}(X_{s})g(X_{s})^{2}\bigr]\dd\!s\;.

(1.3.28)

Taking the limit
h\to 0_{+}
, we get

({\mathscr{L}}\varphi)(x)=\varphi^{\prime}(x)f(x)+\frac{1}{2}\varphi^{\prime\prime}(x)g(x)^{2}\;.

(1.3.29)

The cases
n\mathbin{\geqslant}2
or
m\mathbin{\geqslant}2
are treated similarly, using the
multidimensional Ito formula.
∎

{example}

[Generator of Brownian motion]
Let
W_{t}
be an
m
-dimensional Brownian motion. This is a particular case of
diffusion, with
f=0
and
g={\mathchoice{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.5mul}{\rm 1\mskip-5.0mul}}
. Its generator is thus given by

{\mathscr{L}}=\frac{1}{2}\sumop\displaylimits_{i=1}^{m}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x_{i}^{2}}=\frac{1}{2}\Delta\;.

(1.3.30)

1.3.3
Dynkin’s formula

Dynkin’s formula is essentially a generalisation of the
expression (
1.3.27
) to stopping times. It will yield a first
important class of links between SDEs and PDEs.

{proposition}

[Dynkin’s formula]
Let
\{X_{t}\}_{t\mathbin{\geqslant}0}
be a diffusion with generator
{\mathscr{L}}
. Fix

x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, a stopping time
\tau
such that
\mathbb{E}^{\mskip 1.5mux}\!\left[\tau\right]<\infty
, and
a compactly supported, twice continuously differentiable
function
\varphi:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
. Then

{\mathbb{E}\rule{0.0pt}{9.0pt}}^{\mskip 1.5mux}\bigl[\varphi(X_{\tau})\bigr]=\varphi(x)+{\mathbb{E}\rule{0.0pt}{9.0pt}}^{\mskip 1.5mux}\Biggl[\intop\nolimits_{0}^{\tau}({\mathscr{L}}\varphi)(X_{s})\dd\!s\Biggr]\;.

(1.3.31)

Proof:
.

Consider the case
n=m=1
,
m
being the dimension of Brownian motion.
Proceeding as in the proof of Proposition
1.3.2
,
we obtain

{\mathbb{E}\rule{0.0pt}{9.0pt}}^{\mskip 1.5mux}\bigl[\varphi(X_{\tau})\bigr]=\varphi(x)+{\mathbb{E}\rule{0.0pt}{9.0pt}}^{\mskip 1.5mux}\Biggl[\intop\nolimits_{0}^{\tau}({\mathscr{L}}\varphi)(X_{s})\dd\!s\Biggr]+{\mathbb{E}\rule{0.0pt}{9.0pt}}^{\mskip 1.5mux}\Biggl[\intop\nolimits_{0}^{\tau}g(X_{s})\varphi^{\prime}(X_{s})\dd\!W_{s}\Biggr]\;.

(1.3.32)

It thus suffices to show that the expectation of the stochastic integral
vanishes. For any function
h
bounded by
M
and any
N\in\mathbb{N}
, one has

{\mathbb{E}\rule{0.0pt}{9.0pt}}^{\mskip 1.5mux}\Biggl[\intop\nolimits_{0}^{\tau\wedge N}h(X_{s})\dd\!W_{s}\Biggr]={\mathbb{E}\rule{0.0pt}{9.0pt}}^{\mskip 1.5mux}\Biggl[\intop\nolimits_{0}^{N}\mathbbm{1}_{\{s<\tau\}}h(X_{s})\dd\!W_{s}\Biggr]=0\;,

(1.3.33)

owing to the
{\mathscr{F}}_{s}
-measurability of
\mathbbm{1}_{\{s<\tau\}}
and
h(X_{s})
.
Moreover,

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}\rule{0.0pt}{9.0pt}}^{\mskip 1.5mux}\Biggl[\Biggl(\intop\nolimits_{0}^{\tau}h(X_{s})\dd\!W_{s}-\intop\nolimits_{0}^{\tau\wedge N}h(X_{s})\dd\!W_{s}\Biggr)^{2}\Biggr]

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}\rule{0.0pt}{9.0pt}}^{\mskip 1.5mux}\Biggl[\intop\nolimits_{\tau\wedge N}^{\tau}h(X_{s})^{2}\dd\!s\Biggr]

\displaystyle\mathbin{\leqslant}M^{2}{\mathbb{E}\rule{0.0pt}{9.0pt}}^{\mskip 1.5mux}\bigl[\tau-\tau\wedge N\bigr]\;,

(1.3.34)

which goes to
0
as
N\to\infty
, owing to the assumption

\mathbb{E}^{\mskip 1.5mux}\!\left[\tau\right]<\infty
, by Lebesgues’ dominated convergence theorem. One
can thus write

0=\lim_{N\to\infty}{\mathbb{E}\rule{0.0pt}{9.0pt}}^{\mskip 1.5mux}\Biggl[\intop\nolimits_{0}^{\tau\wedge N}h(X_{s})\dd\!W_{s}\Biggr]={\mathbb{E}\rule{0.0pt}{9.0pt}}^{\mskip 1.5mux}\Biggl[\intop\nolimits_{0}^{\tau}h(X_{s})\dd\!W_{s}\Biggr]\;,

(1.3.35)

which finishes the proof, after plugging (
1.3.35
)
into (
1.3.32
). The proof of the general case is analogous.
∎

Consider now the particular case where the stopping time
\tau
is the
first-exit time from an open bounded set
D\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Assume the boundary
value problem

\displaystyle({\mathscr{L}}u)(x)

\displaystyle=\theta(x)\qquad x\in D

\displaystyle u(x)

\displaystyle=\psi(x)\qquad x\in\partial D

(1.3.36)

admits a unique solution. This is the case if
D
,
\theta
and
\psi

are sufficiently regular. Replacing
\varphi
by
u
in Dynkin’s formula, we
get the relation

u(x)={\mathbb{E}\rule{0.0pt}{9.0pt}}^{\mskip 1.5mux}\biggl[\psi(X_{\tau})-\intop\nolimits_{0}^{\tau}\theta(X_{s})\dd\!s\biggr]\;.

(1.3.37)

For
\psi=0
and
\theta=-1
,
u(x)
is equal to the expectation of
\tau
,
starting form
x
. For
\theta=0
and
\psi
the indicator of a subset
A
of
the boundary
\partial D
,
u(x)
is the probability of leaving
D
though
A
.
Hence, if one can solve the problem (
1.3.36
), one obtains
information on the first-exit time and location from
D
. Conversely,
simulating the expression (
1.3.37
) by a Monte-Carlo method, one
gets a numerical approximation of the solution of the boundary value
problem (
1.3.36
).

{example}

[Mean exit time of Brownian motion from a ball]

Let
K=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\colon\lVert x\rVert<R\}
be the ball of radius
R
centred at
the origin. Given a point
x\in K
, let

\tau_{K}=\inf\{t>0\colon x+W_{t}\notin K\}

(1.3.38)

and let

\tau(N)=\tau_{K}\wedge N\;.

(1.3.39)

The function
\varphi(x)=\lVert x\rVert^{2}\mathbbm{1}_{\{\lVert x\rVert\mathbin{\leqslant}R\}}
is
compactly supported and satisfies
\Delta\varphi(x)=2n
for all
x\in K
. One
can furthermore extend it outsite
K
in a smooth and compactly supported way.
Plugging into Dynkin’s formula, one gets

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}\rule{0.0pt}{9.0pt}}^{\mskip 1.5mux}\bigl[\lVert x+W_{\tau(N)}\rVert^{2}\bigr]

\displaystyle=\lVert x\rVert^{2}+{\mathbb{E}\rule{0.0pt}{9.0pt}}^{\mskip 1.5mux}\biggl[\intop\nolimits_{0}^{\tau(N)}\frac{1}{2}\Delta\varphi(W_{s})\dd\!s\biggr]

\displaystyle=\lVert x\rVert^{2}+n{\mathbb{E}\rule{0.0pt}{9.0pt}}^{\mskip 1.5mux}\bigl[\tau(N)\bigr]\;.

(1.3.40)

Since
\lVert x+W_{\tau(N)}\rVert\mathbin{\leqslant}R
,
letting
N
go to infinity, one obtains by dominated convergence

{\mathbb{E}\rule{0.0pt}{9.0pt}}^{\mskip 1.5mux}\bigl[\tau_{K}\bigr]=\frac{R^{2}-\lVert x\rVert^{2}}{n}\;.

(1.3.41)

{example}

[Recurrence/transience of Brownian motion]

Let again
K=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\colon\lVert x\rVert<R\}
. We now consider
the case where
x\notin K
, and we want to determine if
Brownian motion starting in
x
hits
K
almost surely, in which case it is
called
recurrent
, or if it hits
K
with a probability
strictly less than
1
, in which case it is called
transient
.
As for random walks, the answer depends on the dimension
n
of space.

We define

\tau_{K}=\inf\{t>0\colon x+W_{t}\in K\}\;.

(1.3.42)

For
N\in\mathbb{N}
, let
A_{N}
be the ring

A_{N}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\colon R<\lVert x\rVert<2^{N}R\}\;,

(1.3.43)

and let
\tau
be the first-exit time of
x+W_{t}
from
A_{N}
. We thus have

\tau=\tau_{K}\wedge\tau^{\prime}\;,\qquad\tau^{\prime}=\inf\{t>0\colon\lVert x+W_{t}\rVert=2^{N}R\}\;.

(1.3.44)

Finally, let

p={\mathbb{P}\rule{0.0pt}{9.0pt}}^{\mskip 1.5mux}\bigl\{\tau_{K}<\tau^{\prime}\bigr\}={\mathbb{P}\rule{0.0pt}{9.0pt}}^{\mskip 1.5mux}\bigl\{\lVert x+W_{\tau}\rVert=R\bigr\}=1-{\mathbb{P}\rule{0.0pt}{9.0pt}}^{\mskip 1.5mux}\bigl\{\lVert x+W_{\tau}\rVert=2^{N}R\bigr\}\;.

(1.3.45)

The spherically symmetric solutions of
\Delta\varphi=0
are of the form

\varphi(x)=\begin{cases}\lvert x\rvert&\text{if $n=1$\;,}\\
-\log\lVert x\rVert&\text{if $n=2$\;,}\\
\lVert x\rVert^{2-n}&\text{if $n>2$\;.}\end{cases}

(1.3.46)

For such a
\varphi
, Dynkin’s formula yields

{\mathbb{E}\rule{0.0pt}{9.0pt}}^{\mskip 1.5mux}\bigl[\varphi(x+W_{\tau})\bigr]=\varphi(x)\;.

(1.3.47)

On the other hand,

{\mathbb{E}\rule{0.0pt}{9.0pt}}^{\mskip 1.5mux}\bigl[\varphi(x+W_{\tau})\bigr]=\varphi(R)p+\varphi(2^{N}R)(1-p)\;.

(1.3.48)

Solving with respect to
p
, one gets

p=\frac{\varphi(x)-\varphi(2^{N}R)}{\varphi(R)-\varphi(2^{N}R)}\;.

(1.3.49)

As

, one has

, so that

{\mathbb{P}\rule{0.0pt}{9.0pt}}^{\mskip 1.5mux}\bigl\{\tau_{K}<\infty\bigr\}=\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{\varphi(x)-\varphi(2^{N}R)}{\varphi(R)-\varphi(2^{N}R)}\;.

(1.3.50)

Consider now separately the cases
n=1
,
n=2
and
n>2
.

1.

For
n=1
, one has

{\mathbb{P}\rule{0.0pt}{9.0pt}}^{\mskip 1.5mux}\bigl\{\tau_{K}<\infty\bigr\}=\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{2^{N}R-\lvert x\rvert}{2^{N}R-R}=1\;,

(1.3.51)

showing that Brownian motion is recurrent in dimension
1
.

2.

For
n=2
, one has

{\mathbb{P}\rule{0.0pt}{9.0pt}}^{\mskip 1.5mux}\bigl\{\tau_{K}<\infty\bigr\}=\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{\log\lVert x\rVert+N\log 2-\log R}{N\log 2}=1\;,

(1.3.52)

showing that Brownian motion is also recurrent in dimension
2
.

3.

For

, one has

{\mathbb{P}\rule{0.0pt}{9.0pt}}^{\mskip 1.5mux}\bigl\{\tau_{K}<\infty\bigr\}=\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{(2^{N}R)^{2-n}+\lVert x\rVert^{2-n}}{(2^{N}R)^{2-n}+R^{2-n}}=\biggl(\frac{R}{\lVert x\rVert}\biggr)^{n-2}<1\;.

(1.3.53)

Brownian motion is thus transient in dimension

.

1.3.4
Kolmogorov’s equations

A second class of links between SDEs and PDEs is given by Kolmogorov’s
equations, which are initial value problems.

Observe that by taking the derivative of Dynkin’s formula with respect to
t
,
in the particuliar case
\tau=t
, one gets

\frac{\partial}{\partial t}(P_{t}\varphi)(x)=\frac{\partial}{\partial t}{\mathbb{E}\rule{0.0pt}{9.0pt}}^{\mskip 1.5mux}\bigl[\varphi(X_{t})\bigr]={\mathbb{E}\rule{0.0pt}{9.0pt}}^{\mskip 1.5mux}\bigl[({\mathscr{L}}\varphi)(X_{t})\bigr]=(P_{t}{\mathscr{L}}\varphi)(x)\;,

(1.3.58)

which can be written in compact form as

\frac{\dd\!}{\dd\!t}P_{t}=P_{t}{\mathscr{L}}\;.

(1.3.59)

We have seen in Remark
1.3.2
that one can also formally write

\frac{\dd\!}{\dd\!t}P_{t}={\mathscr{L}}P_{t}
. Therefore, the operators
{\mathscr{L}}
et
P_{t}
commute, at
least formally. The next theorem makes this observation rigorous.

{theorem}

[Backward Kolmogorov equation]
Let
\varphi:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
be a compactly supported, twice continuously
differentiable function.

1.

The function

u(t,x)=(P_{t}\varphi)(x)={\mathbb{E}\rule{0.0pt}{9.0pt}}^{\mskip 1.5mux}\bigl[\varphi(X_{t})\bigr]

(1.3.60)

satisfies the initial value problem

\displaystyle\frac{\partial u}{\partial t}(t,x)

\displaystyle=({\mathscr{L}}u)(t,x)\;,

\displaystyle t>0\;,\quad x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\;,

\displaystyle u(0,x)

\displaystyle=\varphi(x)\;,

\displaystyle x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\;.

(1.3.61)

2.

If
w(t,x)
is a bounded function, which is continuously differentiable
in
t
and twice continuously differentiable in
x
, satisfying
the initial value problem (
1.3.61
), then
w(t,x)=(P_{t}\varphi)(x)
.

Proof:
.

1.

One has
u(0,x)=(P_{0}\varphi)(x)=\varphi(x)
and

\displaystyle({\mathscr{L}}u)(t,x)

\displaystyle=\lim_{h\to 0_{+}}\frac{(P_{h}\circ P_{t}\varphi)(x)-(P_{t}\varphi)(x)}{h}

\displaystyle=\lim_{h\to 0_{+}}\frac{(P_{t+h}\varphi)(x)-(P_{t}\varphi)(x)}{h}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial}{\partial t}(P_{t}\varphi)(x)=\frac{\partial}{\partial t}u(t,x)\;.

(1.3.62)

2.

If
w(t,x)
satisfies (
1.3.61
), then one has

\widetilde{\mathscr{L}}w=0\qquad\text{where }\quad\widetilde{\mathscr{L}}w=-\frac{\partial w}{\partial t}+{\mathscr{L}}w\;.

(1.3.63)

Fix
(s,x)\in\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}
. The process
Y_{t}=(s-t,X^{0,x}_{t})
admits

\widetilde{\mathscr{L}}
as generator. Let

\tau_{R}=\inf\{t>0\colon\lVert X_{t}\rVert\mathbin{\geqslant}R\}\;.

(1.3.64)

Dynkin’s formula shows that

{\mathbb{E}\rule{0.0pt}{9.0pt}}^{\mskip 1.5mus,x}\bigl[w(Y_{t\wedge\tau_{R}})\bigr]=w(s,x)+{\mathbb{E}\rule{0.0pt}{9.0pt}}^{\mskip 1.5mus,x}\biggl[\intop\nolimits_{0}^{t\wedge\tau_{R}}(\widetilde{\mathscr{L}}w)(Y_{u})\dd\!u\biggr]=w(s,x)\;.

(1.3.65)

Letting
R
go to infinity, one obtains

w(s,x)={\mathbb{E}\rule{0.0pt}{9.0pt}}^{\mskip 1.5mus,x}\bigl[w(Y_{t})\bigr]\qquad\forall t\mathbin{\geqslant}0\;.

(1.3.66)

In particular, taking
t=s
, one has

w(s,x)={\mathbb{E}\rule{0.0pt}{9.0pt}}^{\mskip 1.5mus,x}\bigl[w(Y_{s})\bigr]=\mathbb{E}\bigl[w(0,X^{0,x}_{s})\bigr]=\mathbb{E}\bigl[\varphi(X^{0,x}_{s})\bigr]={\mathbb{E}\rule{0.0pt}{9.0pt}}^{\mskip 1.5mux}\bigl[\varphi(X_{s})\bigr]\;,

(1.3.67)

as claimed.

∎

Note that in the case of Brownian motion, which has generator

{\mathscr{L}}=\frac{1}{2}\Delta
, Kolmogorov’s backward equation (
1.3.61
) is nothing
but the heat equation.

Since Kolmogorov’s backward equation is linear, it is sufficient to solve it
for a complete family of initial conditions
\varphi
to determine its solution
for all initial conditions.
A first important case occurs when one knows all eigenfunctions and eigenvalues
of
{\mathscr{L}}
. Then the general solution can be decomposed on a basis of
eigenfunctions, with coefficients depending exponentially on time.

{example}

[Brownian motion]
Eigenfunctions of the generator
{\mathscr{L}}=\frac{1}{2}\Delta=\frac{1}{2}\frac{\dd\!^{2}}{\dd\!x^{2}}

of
one-dimensional Brownian motion are of the form
\e^{\icx kx}
. Decomposing the
solution on this basis of eigenfunctions amounts to solving the heat equation
by
Fourier transform. One knows that the solution can be written as

u(t,x)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\intop\nolimits_{\mathbb{R}}\e^{-k^{2}t/2}\hat{\varphi}(k)\e^{\icx kx}\dd\!k\;,

(1.3.68)

where
\hat{\varphi}(k)
is the Fourier transform of the
initial condition.

A second important case occurs when formally decomposing the initial condition
on a “basis” of Dirac distributions. In practice,
this amounts to using the notion of transition density.

{definition}

[Transition density]

The diffusion
\{X_{t}\}_{t}
is said to admit the
transition density

p_{t}(x,y)
, also written
p(y,t|x,0)
, if

{\mathbb{E}\rule{0.0pt}{9.0pt}}^{\mskip 1.5mux}\bigl[\varphi(X_{t})\bigr]=\intop\nolimits_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\varphi(y)p_{t}(x,y)\dd\!y

(1.3.69)

for all bounded measurable functions
\varphi:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
.

By linearity, if the transition density exists and is smooth, it satisfies
Kolmogorov’s backward equation (the generator
{\mathscr{L}}
acting on the variable

x
), with initial condition
p_{0}(x,y)=\delta(x-y)
.

{example}

[Brownian motion and heat kernel]
In the case of one-dimensional Brownian motion, we have seen
(c.f. (
1.1.13
)) that the transition density is given by the heat kernel

p(y,t|x,0)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi t}}\e^{-(x-y)^{2}/2t}\;.

(1.3.70)

This is also the value of the integral (
1.3.68
) with

\hat{\varphi}(k)=\e^{-\icx ky}/\sqrt{2\pi}
, which is indeed the
Fourier transform of
\varphi(x)=\delta(x-y)
.

The
adjoint
of the generator
{\mathscr{L}}
is by definition the linear operator

{\mathscr{L}}^{\dagger}
such that

\langle{\mathscr{L}}\phi,\psi\rangle=\langle\phi,{\mathscr{L}}^{\dagger}\psi\rangle

(1.3.71)

for any choice of twice continuously differentiable functions

\phi,\psi:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
, with
\phi
compactly supported, where

\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
denotes the usual inner product in
L^{2}
. Integrating

\langle{\mathscr{L}}\phi,\psi\rangle
by parts twice, one obtains

({\mathscr{L}}^{\dagger}\psi)(y)=\frac{1}{2}\sumop\displaylimits_{i,j=1}^{n}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial y_{i}\partial y_{j}}\bigl((gg^{T})_{ij}\psi\bigr)(y)-\sumop\displaylimits_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\partial}{\partial y_{i}}\bigl(f_{i}\psi\bigr)(y)\;.

(1.3.72)

{theorem}

[Forward Kolmogorov equation]
If
X_{t}
admits a smooth transition density
p_{t}(x,y)
, then it
satisfies the equation

\frac{\partial}{\partial t}p_{t}(x,y)={\mathscr{L}}^{\dagger}_{y}p_{t}(x,y)\;,

(1.3.73)

where the notation
{\mathscr{L}}^{\dagger}_{y}
means that
{\mathscr{L}}^{\dagger}
acts on the
variable
y
.

Proof:
.

Dynkin’s formula with
\tau=t
implies

\displaystyle\intop\nolimits_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\varphi(y)p_{t}(x,y)\dd\!y

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}\rule{0.0pt}{9.0pt}}^{\mskip 1.5mux}\bigl[\varphi(X_{t})\bigr]

\displaystyle=\varphi(x)+\intop\nolimits_{0}^{t}{\mathbb{E}\rule{0.0pt}{9.0pt}}^{\mskip 1.5mux}\bigl[({\mathscr{L}}\varphi)(X_{s})\bigr]\dd\!s

\displaystyle=\varphi(x)+\intop\nolimits_{0}^{t}\intop\nolimits_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}({\mathscr{L}}\varphi)(y)p_{s}(x,y)\dd\!y\;.

(1.3.74)

Taking the derivative with respect to time, and using (
1.3.71
), we get

\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\intop\nolimits_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\varphi(y)p_{t}(x,y)\dd\!y=\intop\nolimits_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}({\mathscr{L}}\varphi)(y)p_{t}(x,y)\dd\!y=\intop\nolimits_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\varphi(y)({\mathscr{L}}^{\dagger}_{y}p_{t})(x,y)\dd\!y\;,

(1.3.75)

which implies the result.
∎

Assume the distribution of
X_{0}
admits a density
\rho
with respect to
Lebesgue measure. Then
X_{t}
has the density

\rho(t,y)=(Q_{t}\rho)(y)\defby\intop\nolimits_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}p_{t}(x,y)\rho(x)\dd\!x\;.

(1.3.76)

Applying Kolmogorov’s forward equation (
1.3.73
), one
obtains the
Fokker–Planck equation

\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\rho(t,y)={\mathscr{L}}^{\dagger}_{y}\rho(t,y)\;,

(1.3.77)

which can also be formally written

\frac{\dd\!}{\dd\!t}Q_{t}={\mathscr{L}}^{\dagger}Q_{t}\;.

(1.3.78)

The adjoint generator
{\mathscr{L}}^{\dagger}
is thus the generator of the adjoint
semi-group
Q_{t}
.

{corollary}

If
\rho_{0}(y)
is the density of a probability measure satisfying

{\mathscr{L}}^{\dagger}\rho_{0}=0
, then
\rho_{0}
is a stationnary measure of the
diffusion. In other words, if the distribution of
X_{0}
admits the density

\rho_{0}
, then
X_{t}
admits the density
\rho_{0}
for all

.

1.3.5
The Feynman–Kac formula

So far, we have encountered elliptic boundary value problems of the form
{\mathscr{L}}u=\theta
, as well as parabolic evolution equations of the form
\partial_{t}u={\mathscr{L}}u
. The Feynman–Kac formula will show that one can also link properties of
diffusions with those of parabolic equations containing a term linear in
u
.
Adding a linear term to the generator can be interpreted as “killing” the diffusion at certain rate. The simplest case is that of a
constant rate. Let
\zeta
be a random variable of exponential distribution
with parameter
\lambda
, independent of
W_{t}
. Set

\widetilde{X}_{t}=\begin{cases}X_{t}&\text{if $t<\zeta$\;,}\\
\Delta&\text{if $t\mathbin{\geqslant}\zeta$\;,}\end{cases}

(1.3.80)

where
\Delta
is a “cemetery state” that has been added to

\mathbb{R}^{n}
. One checks that owing to the exponential distribution of
\zeta
,

\widetilde{X}_{t}
is a Markov process on
\mathbb{R}^{n}\cup\{\Delta\}
.
If
\varphi:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
is a bounded measurable test function, one has
(setting
\varphi(\Delta)=0
)

{\mathbb{E}\rule{0.0pt}{9.0pt}}^{\mskip 1.5mux}\bigl[\varphi(\widetilde{X}_{t})\bigr]={\mathbb{E}\rule{0.0pt}{9.0pt}}^{\mskip 1.5mux}\bigl[\varphi(X_{t})\mathbbm{1}_{\{t<\zeta\}}\bigr]=\mathbb{P}\{\zeta>t\}{\mathbb{E}\rule{0.0pt}{9.0pt}}^{\mskip 1.5mux}\bigl[\varphi(X_{t})\bigr]=\e^{-\lambda t}{\mathbb{E}\rule{0.0pt}{9.0pt}}^{\mskip 1.5mux}\bigl[\varphi(X_{t})\bigr]\;.

(1.3.81)

It follows that

\lim_{h\to 0}\frac{{\mathbb{E}\rule{0.0pt}{9.0pt}}^{\mskip 1.5mux}\bigl[\varphi(\widetilde{X}_{h})\bigr]-\varphi(x)}{h}=-\lambda\varphi(x)+({\mathscr{L}}\varphi)(x)\;,

(1.3.82)

which shows that the infinitesimal generator of
\widetilde{X}
is the
differential operator

\widetilde{\mathscr{L}}={\mathscr{L}}-\lambda\;.

(1.3.83)

More generally, if
q:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
is a continuous function bounded from below,
one can construct a random variable
\zeta
such that

{\mathbb{E}\rule{0.0pt}{9.0pt}}^{\mskip 1.5mux}\bigl[\varphi(\widetilde{X}_{t})\bigr]={\mathbb{E}\rule{0.0pt}{9.0pt}}^{\mskip 1.5mux}\biggl[\varphi(X_{t})\e^{-\intop\nolimits_{0}^{t}q(X_{s})\dd\!s}\biggr]\;.

(1.3.84)

In this case, the generator of
\widetilde{X}_{t}
is

\widetilde{\mathscr{L}}={\mathscr{L}}-q\;,

(1.3.85)

that is,
(\widetilde{\mathscr{L}}\varphi)(x)=({\mathscr{L}}\varphi)(x)-q(x)\varphi(x)
.

{theorem}

[Feynman–Kac formula]

Let
\varphi:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
be a compactly supported, twice continuously
differentiable function, and let

(1.3.86)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial v}{\partial t}(t,x)

\displaystyle=({\mathscr{L}}v)(t,x)-q(x)v(t,x)\;,\qquad\qquad

\displaystyle t>0\;,\quad x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\;,

\displaystyle v(0,x)

\displaystyle=\varphi(x)\;,

\displaystyle x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\;.\ignorespaces\ignorespaces

2.

If
w(t,x)
is continuously differentiable
in
t
and twice continuously differentiable in
x
, bounded for
x

in a compact set, and satisfies (

w(t,x)
is equal to the
right-hand side of (

Proof:

1.

Set

and

, and let

be given by (

\displaystyle\frac{1}{h}\Bigl[{\mathbb{E}\rule{0.0pt}{9.0pt}}^{\mskip 1.5mux}\bigl[v(t,X_{h})\bigr]-v(t,x)\Bigr]={}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{h}\Bigl[{\mathbb{E}\rule{0.0pt}{9.0pt}}^{\mskip 1.5mux}\Bigl[{\mathbb{E}\rule{0.0pt}{9.0pt}}^{\mskip 1.5muX_{h}}\bigl[Y_{t}Z_{t}\bigr]\Bigr]-{\mathbb{E}\rule{0.0pt}{9.0pt}}^{\mskip 1.5mux}\bigl[Y_{t}Z_{t}\bigr]\Bigr]

\displaystyle={}

Chapter 2
Invariant measures for SDEs

We consider in this chapter SDEs in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
of the form

\dd\!X_{t}=f(X_{t})\dd\!t+g(X_{t})\dd\!W_{t}\;,

(2.0.1)

where the drift coefficient
f
and the diffusion coefficient
g
are such that
there exists a unique strong solution, which is global in time. Then it is
natural to ask the following questions:

1.

Does the diffusion (
2.0.1
) admit an invariant probability
measure?

2.

If so, is this measure unique?

3.

If so, does any initial distribution converge to the invariant measure?

4.

If so, how fast does this convergence occur? For which distance? Can
one obtain explicit bounds on the speed of convergence?

Various methods have been derived to answer these questions, each one having
its advantages and drawbacks. Some methods are easier to use and provide, for
instance, convergence to an invariant distribution, but without any bound on
the speed of convergence, while others may provide sharp bounds, but are
limited to specific sets of initial distributions. In what follows, we are
going to present a few selected examples of these methods, which have been
chosen because they proved useful in particular applications. But one should
keep in mind that there exist many more approaches.

In what follows, it will by useful to employ the notation

P_{t}(x,A)=(P_{t}\mathbbm{1}_{A})(x)=\mathbb{P}^{\mskip 1.5mux}\{X_{t}\in A\}

(2.0.2)

for the Markov semigroup, where
A
is any Borel set in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Given a
probability measure
\mu
, we write

(\mu P_{t})(A)=\intop\nolimits_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\mu(\dd\!x)P_{t}(x,A)

(2.0.3)

instead of
(Q_{t}\mu)(A)
for the action of the adjoint semigroup, because it
is reminiscent of matrix multiplication used for Markov chains on finite sets.
A measure is invariant if it satisfies

(\mu P_{t})(A)=\mu(A)

(2.0.4)

for all
t\mathbin{\geqslant}0
and all Borel sets
A\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

{definition}

[Feller property]
A semigroup
(P_{t})_{t\mathbin{\geqslant}0}
is said to heave the
Feller property
if

P_{t}f
is bounded and continuous whenever
f
is bounded and continuous.

A useful standard result in our situation is then the following.

{proposition}

[Condition for Feller property]
Any diffusion
(X_{t})_{t\mathbin{\geqslant}0}
solving an SDE with globally Lipschitz
coefficients has the Feller property.

For a proof, see for instance
[
Øks03
, Lemma 8.1.4]
,
or
[
RY99
, Theorem IX.2.5]
. The global Lipschitz condition can often
be relaxed to a local condition by working with appropriate stopping times
(that is, by considering the diffusion up to its first exit from a sequence
of balls of growing radius).

{remark}

[Strong Feller property]
The semigroup
P_{t}
is said to have the
strong Feller property
if
P_{t}f

is bounded and continuous whenever
f
is bounded and measurable, but not
necessarily continuous. A sufficient condition for a diffusion to satisfy the
strong Feller property is the
ellipticity
condition on the drift
coefficient

\langle\xi,g(x)g(x)^{T}\xi\rangle\mathbin{\geqslant}c\lVert\xi^{2}\rVert\qquad\forall\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\;.

(2.0.5)

This condition can be relaxed to
hypoellipticity
(Hörmander
condition).

2.1
Existence of invariant probability measures

2.1.1
Some basic examples

Corollary
1.3.4
shows that the density
\rho
of an invariant
probability measure should satisfy
{\mathscr{L}}^{\dagger}\rho=0
, where
{\mathscr{L}}^{\dagger}

is the adjoint generator. Cases where this equation can be solved are rare, but
one important example is given by gradient SDEs.

{example}

[Gradient system]

Consider the SDE

\dd\!X_{t}=-\nabla V(X_{t})\dd\!t+\sqrt{2}\dd\!W_{t}\;,

(2.1.1)

where
V:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
is bounded below, and satisfies

\intop\nolimits_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\e^{-V(x)}\dd\!x<\infty\;.

(2.1.2)

The generator of the diffusion can be written in the two equivalent ways

{\mathscr{L}}=\Delta-\nabla V\cdot\nabla=\e^{V}\nabla\cdot\e^{-V}\nabla

(2.1.3)

(the factor
\sqrt{2}
in (
2.1.1
) avoids a factor
\frac{1}{2}
in
front of the Laplacian). Integrating by parts twice, we find

\langle f,{\mathscr{L}}g\rangle=-\intop\nolimits\e^{-V(x)}\nabla(f(x)\e^{V(x)})\cdot\nabla g(x)\dd\!x=\langle{\mathscr{L}}^{\dagger}f,g\rangle\;,

(2.1.4)

with the adjoint generator given by

{\mathscr{L}}^{\dagger}f=\nabla\cdot\bigl(\e^{-V}\nabla(\e^{V}f)\bigr)\;.

(2.1.5)

In view of Corollary
1.3.4
, this shows that

\rho(x)=\frac{1}{{\mathscr{Z}}}\e^{-V(x)}\;,\qquad{\mathscr{Z}}=\intop\nolimits\e^{-V(x)}\dd\!x

(2.1.6)

is the density of an invariant probability measure of the
diffusion (
2.1.1
), since it satisfies
{\mathscr{L}}^{\dagger}\rho=0
.

Next, we discuss two very simple examples for which existence or uniqueness of
an invariant probability measure fails.

{example}

[Brownian motion]
The transition density of Brownian motion in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a Gaussian of variance

t
, as we have seen for instance in Section
1.1.3
. Therefore, for
any fixed
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we have

\lim_{n\to\infty}p(t,x|0,0)=0\;,

(2.1.7)

which is not a normalisable measure. Therefore, Brownian motion in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
does
not admit an invariant probability measure (though it does admit invariant
measures, which are simply all multiples of Lebesgue measure).

{example}

[Non-irreducible SDE]
Consider the SDE in
\mathbb{R}^{2}

\displaystyle\dd\!X_{t}

\displaystyle=-X_{t}\dd\!t+\dd\!W_{t}

(2.1.8)

\displaystyle\dd\!Y_{t}

\displaystyle=(Y_{t}-Y_{t})^{3}\dd\!t\;.

(2.1.9)

The diffusion admits three extremal invariant measures, given by

\displaystyle\pi_{-}(\dd\!x,\dd\!y)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi}}\e^{-x^{2}}\dd\!x\,\delta_{-1}(\dd\!y)\;,

(2.1.10)

\displaystyle\pi_{0}(\dd\!x,\dd\!y)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi}}\e^{-x^{2}}\dd\!x\,\delta_{0}(\dd\!y)\;,

(2.1.11)

\displaystyle\pi_{+}(\dd\!x,\dd\!y)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi}}\e^{-x^{2}}\dd\!x\,\delta_{1}(\dd\!y)\;.

(2.1.12)

This is because the
x
- and
y
-components do not interact with each other,
and the process
X_{t}
is an Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process with Gaussian invariant
measure, while
Y_{t}
is deterministic, with three invariant points located at

\pm 1
and
0
. Note that in addition, any convex combination of
\pi_{-}
,

\pi_{0}
and
\pi_{+}
is also an invariant probability measure (
\pi_{-}
,

\pi_{0}
and
\pi_{+}
are called extremal because they do not admit a
nontrivial decomposition).

The two last examples illustrate a general fact about invariant
probability measures of Markov processes, namely that their existence requires
two properties to hold:

1.

There should be a mechanism preventing all the probability mass of
going to infinity. More precisely, a positive recurrence property is required
to hold, that is, the return time to some bounded set should have finite
expectation.

2.

There should be a mechanism making the diffusion irreducible, that is,
there should not be any non-trivial invariant sets.

These two conditions are analogous to those that one finds for Markov chains on
a countable space. The main difference is that discrete-time Markov chains
require an aperiodicity condition to hold in addition. However, this is
specific to discrete time, and no such condition is necessary as soon as
transition times between different points are sufficiently random.

2.1.2
The Krylov–Bogoliubov criterion

A general criterion for existence of invariant measures, going back to Krylov
and Bogoliubov, is based on the notion of ergodic averages (or Cesaro means).
Given an initial point
X_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, consider the family of measures

\biggl\{\frac{1}{T}\intop\nolimits_{0}^{T}P_{t}(X_{0},\cdot)\dd\!t\colon T\mathbin{\geqslant}1\biggr\}\;.

(2.1.13)

In a similar way as for Markov chains, if these ergodic averages converge to a
limiting probability measure, then this limit should be invariant. A
convergence criterion is given by thightness.

{definition}

[Tightness]
A family
\{\mu_{t}\}
of probability measures on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
is
tight
if for
any
\delta>0
, there exists a compact set
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that

\mu_{t}(K)\mathbin{\geqslant}1-\delta
for all
t
.

Then one has the following existence result, see for
instance
[
DPZ96
, Corollary 3.1.2]
.

{proposition}

[Existence of an invariant probability measure]
If the family of measures (
2.1.13
) is tight, then there exists an
invariant probability measure.

While this criterion may be used to obtain abstract existence results for
invariant probability measures, it is not so easy to apply because it requires
some a priori knowledge on the semi–group
P_{t}
. Therefore, in what follows,
we will rather discuss more practical criteria for analysing invariant measure.

2.2
The Lyapunov function approach by Meyn and Tweedie

We present here some results of the approach based on Lyapunov functions, as
developed in
[
MT93c
]
for continuous-time Markov processes.
This approach provides a relatively easy way of proving global existence of
solution, existence of an invariant measure, and some convergence results,
provided one can guess an appropriate Lyapunov function.

{definition}

[Norm-like function]
A function
V:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
is called
norm-like
if

\lim_{\lVert x\rVert\to\infty}V(x)=+\infty\;.

(2.2.1)

This means that the level sets
\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\colon V(x)\mathbin{\leqslant}h\}
are
precompact for any
h>0
.

In the case of ordinary differential equations, Lyapunov functions are
norm-like functions that decrease along orbits of the dynamical system, at
least when starting far away from the origin. The application to SDEs uses
quite similar ideas, where the time derivative along orbits is replaced by the
action of the generator. In the following, we will present several of these
results, without giving detailed proofs. A detailed proof of a quite
general existence and convergence result, due to Martin Hairer and Jonathan
Mattingly, will be discussed in Section
2.2.4
.

2.2.1
Non-explosion and Harris recurrence criteria

A first application of Lyapunov functions is a relatively easy criterion for
existence of global in time solutions.

{theorem}

[Non-explosion criterion]
Assume that there exist a norm-like function
V
and constants

such
that

\bigl({\mathscr{L}}V\bigr)(x)\mathbin{\leqslant}cV(x)+d

(2.2.2)

for all

. Then

1.

The SDE (
2.0.1
) admits global in time solutions for any
starting point
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

2.

There exists an almost surely finite random variable
D
such that

V(X_{t})\mathbin{\leqslant}D\e^{ct}\qquad\forall t\mathbin{\geqslant}0\;.

(2.2.3)

The random variable
D
satisfies the bound

{\mathbb{P}\rule{0.0pt}{9.0pt}}^{\mskip 1.5mux}\bigl\{D\mathbin{\geqslant}a\bigr\}\mathbin{\leqslant}\frac{V(x)}{a}\qquad\forall a>0\;,\quad\forall x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\;.

(2.2.4)

3.

The expectation
{\mathbb{E}\rule{0.0pt}{9.0pt}}^{\mskip 1.5mux}\bigl[V(X_{t})\bigr]
is finite for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}

and all
t\mathbin{\geqslant}0
, and satisfies

{\mathbb{E}\rule{0.0pt}{9.0pt}}^{\mskip 1.5mux}\bigl[V(X_{t})\bigr]\mathbin{\leqslant}\e^{ct}V(x)\;.

(2.2.5)

Sketch of proof:
.

Consider first the case
c=d=0
. Then Ito’s formula
(cf. Lemma
1.2.2
) yields

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}\rule{0.0pt}{9.0pt}}^{\mskip 1.5mux}\bigl[V(X_{t})\bigr]

\displaystyle=V(x)+{\mathbb{E}\rule{0.0pt}{9.0pt}}^{\mskip 1.5mux}\biggl[\intop\nolimits_{0}^{t}({\mathscr{L}}V)(X_{s})\dd\!s\biggr]

(2.2.6)

\displaystyle\mathbin{\leqslant}V(x)\;.

(2.2.7)

This proves (
2.2.5
), as well as global existence of the solution.
The bound (
2.2.4
) follows from a slightly more sophisticated
stochastic analysis argument, using the fact that
\e^{-ct}V(X_{s})
is a
supermartingale.

The case
c>0
and

follows in a similar way from the Feynman–Kac
formula (see Theorem
1.3.5
), while the other cases can be
reduced to already treated cases by changing the Lyapunov function.
∎

{remark}

[Stopping times]
To rule out the possibility of finite-time blow-up, the actual argument given
in
[
MT93c

uses a more careful computation based
on the process killed when leaving a large ball, whose radius is then sent to
infinity. This requires in particular using Dynkin’s formula instead of Ito’s
formula.

The following result gives a condition under which solutions remain bounded
almost surely (a property called
non-evanescence

in
[

), which is slightly stronger
than (
2.2.2
). For a proof,
see
[
MT93c

{theorem}

[Non-evanescence condition]

Assume there exist a compact set
C\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
, a constant

norm-like function

such that

\bigl({\mathscr{L}}V\bigr)(x)\mathbin{\leqslant}d\mathbbm{1}_{C}(x)

(2.2.8)

for all

\mathbb{P}\biggl\{\lim_{t\to\infty}\lVert X_{t}\rVert=\infty\biggr\}=0\;.

(2.2.9)

A discussed above, in order to obtain existence of an invariant measure, we will

Markov chain is said to be

cannot hold for continuous-space processes, since sets of measure

{definition}

[Harris recurrence]
The diffusion
(X_{t})_{t\mathbin{\geqslant}0}

(2.2.10)

Equivalently, the diffusion is Harris recurrent if there
exists a
\sigma
-finite measure
\nu

\nu(A)>0
, one has
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}

{\mathbb{P}\rule{0.0pt}{9.0pt}}^{\mskip 1.5mux}\bigl\{\eta_{A}=\infty\bigr\}=1\qquad\text{where $\eta_{A}=\intop\nolimits_{0}^{\infty}\mathbbm{1}_{X_{t}\in A}\dd\!t$\;.}

(2.2.11)

The equivalence of the two definitions is well-known for Markov chains on
countable spaces, and a proof in the general case can be found for instance
in
[
MT93a
, Theorem 1.1]
. The interest of this
definition is the following classical
result
[
ADR69
,
Get80
]
.

{proposition}

[Existence of an invariant measure]

If
(X_{t})_{t\mathbin{\geqslant}0}
is Harris recurrent, then it admits an (essentially
unique) invariant measure
\pi
.

One way of showing Harris recurrence is to use the (somewhat tricky)
notion of
petite sets
.

{definition}
[Petite set]
Let
a
be a probability distribution on
\mathbb{R}_{+}
, and define a Markov kernel

K_{a}
by

K_{a}(x,A)=\intop\nolimits_{0}^{\infty}P_{t}(x,A)a(\dd\!t)

(2.2.12)

for any Borel set
A\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
Let
\varphi_{a}
be a nontrivial measure on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Then a non-empty Borel set

C\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is called
\varphi_{a}
-petite
if
K_{a}(x,A)\mathbin{\geqslant}\varphi_{a}(A)
for all

x\in C
.

The intuition behind this definition is as follows. If we choose, say,
a=\delta_{1}
, then
K_{a}(x,A)=P_{1}(x,A)
. The condition
K_{a}(x,A)\mathbin{\geqslant}\varphi_{a}(A)

then requires that the probability of being in the set
A
at time
1
is
bounded below by a measure
\varphi_{a}(A)
independent of the starting point
x
in
the petite set (the measure
\varphi_{a}
need not be a probability mesure). This
would be quite restrictive, but the definition allows to replace
P_{1}(x,A)
by
an average of
P_{t}(x,A)
over all times
t\mathbin{\geqslant}0
, a much weaker requirement.
Petite sets allow us to give a first condition for a diffusion to be Harris
recurrent.

{theorem}

[Harris recurrence condition]

If all compact subsets of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
are petite and (
2.2.8
) holds for a
compact set
C\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
, a constant
d>0
, and a norm-like function
V
,
then the process
(X_{t})_{t\mathbin{\geqslant}0}
is Harris recurrent, and therefore admits an
essentially unique invariant measure
\pi
.

This result follows from Theorem
2.2.1
, combined
with
[
MT93b
, Theorem 5.1]
, which gives an analogous statement
in the discrete-time case.

2.2.2
Positive Harris recurrence and existence of an invariant
probability

Combining Theorem
2.2.1
with
Proposition
2.2.1
, we obtain a condition for the
existence of an invariant measure. This measure need not have finite mass,
preventing it from being normalisable to yield an invariant probability
measure. This motivates the following definition.

{definition}

[Positive Harris recurrence]
Let
(X_{t})_{t\mathbin{\geqslant}0}
be a Harris recurrent diffusion, with invariant measure

\pi
. If
\pi(\mathbb{R}^{n})<\infty
, then
(X_{t})_{t\mathbin{\geqslant}0}
is said to
be
positive Harris recurrent
.

{remark}

[Link with expected return times]
A diffusion satisfying
{\mathbb{E}\rule{0.0pt}{9.0pt}}^{\mskip 1.5mux}\bigl[\tau_{A}\bigr]<\infty
for any
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
any Borel set
A
such that
\mu(A)>0
for a
\sigma
-finite measure
\mu
is
positive Harris recurrent. Indeed, in this case,
\tau_{A}
is almost surely
finite, so that the chain is Harris recurrent. Furthermore, given
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and

A
with
\mu(A)>0
, the probability measure
\pi_{A}(x,\cdot)
given by

\pi_{A}(x,B)=\frac{1}{{\mathbb{E}\rule{0.0pt}{9.0pt}}^{\mskip 1.5mux}\bigl[\tau_{A}\bigr]}{\mathbb{E}\rule{0.0pt}{9.0pt}}^{\mskip 1.5mux}\Biggl[\intop\nolimits_{0}^{\tau_{A}}\mathbbm{1}_{X_{t}\in B}\dd\!t\Biggr]

(2.2.13)

for any Borel set
B\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
can easily be shown to be invariant. By
essential uniqueness, any invariant measure is thus normalisable.

A sufficient condition for the process
(X_{t})_{t\mathbin{\geqslant}0}
to be positive
Harris recurrent will thus automatically be a sufficient condition for the
existence of an invariant probability measure. A first such condition is
provided by the following result, which
is
[
MT93c
, Theorem 4.2]
.

{theorem}

[Positive Harris recurrence condition]
Assume there exist constants
c,d>0
, a function
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to[1,\infty)
, a
closed petite set
C\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and a positive function
V
such that

({\mathscr{L}}V)(x)\mathbin{\leqslant}-cf(x)+d\mathbbm{1}_{C}(x)

(2.2.14)

for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Assume furthermore that
V
is bounded on
C
. Then the
process
(X_{t})_{t\mathbin{\geqslant}0}
is positive Harris recurrent, and therefore admits an
invariant probability measure
\pi
. Furthermore,

\langle\pi,f\rangle:={\mathbb{E}\rule{0.0pt}{9.0pt}}^{\mskip 1.5mu\pi}\bigl[f\bigr]=\intop\nolimits_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}f(x)\pi(\dd\!x)<\infty\;.

(2.2.15)

While Condition (
2.2.14
) is usually easy to check for a given
f
and

V
, the requirement that
C
be petite may be harder to verify. Fortunately,
for Feller diffusions, there exists an alternative criterion for the existence
of an invariant measure, which avoids having to check that
C
is petite. The
following result is
[
MT93c
, Theorem 4.5]
.

{theorem}

[Existence of invariant probability measures for Feller
diffusions]
Assume that the diffusion
(X_{t})_{t\mathbin{\geqslant}0}
has the Feller property, and
that (
2.2.14
) holds for a compact
C\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Then the diffusion
admits an invariant probability measure
\pi
. Furthermore, any invariant
probability
\pi
satisfies
\langle\pi,f\rangle\mathbin{\leqslant}d/c
.

2.2.3
Convergence to the invariant probability measure

Once the existence of an invariant probability measure
\pi
is established,
the next natural question is whether the distribution of
(X_{t})_{t\mathbin{\geqslant}0}
will
converge to
\pi
, at least under some conditions on the law of
X_{0}
. There
are many choices of norms quantifying such a convergence, and results exist for
several of them. Here we consider the following weighted norm on signed
measures.

{definition}

[
f
-norm of a signed measure]
Let
\mu
be a signed measure on
(\mathbb{R}^{n},{\mathscr{B}})
, and let
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to[1,\infty)
be
a measurable function. Then we define the
f
-norm of
\mu
by

\lVert\mu\rVert_{f}=\sup_{g:\lvert g\rvert<f}\bigl|\langle\mu,g\rangle\bigr|\;,\qquad\langle\mu,g\rangle:={\mathbb{E}\rule{0.0pt}{9.0pt}}^{\mskip 1.5mu\mu}\bigl[g\bigr]=\intop\nolimits_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}g(x)\mu(\dd\!x)\;,

(2.2.16)

where the supremum runs over all measurable functions such that
\lvert g(x)\rvert\mathbin{\leqslant}f(x)
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

{definition}

[Exponential ergodicity]
Given a measurable function
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to[0,\infty)
, a diffusion process

(X_{t})_{t\mathbin{\geqslant}0}
admitting an invariant probability measure
\pi
is
called
f
-exponentially ergodic
if there exist
\beta>0
and a function

B:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
such that

\lVert P_{t}(x,\cdot)-\pi\rVert_{f}\mathbin{\leqslant}B(x)\e^{-\beta t}

(2.2.17)

for any
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
t\mathbin{\geqslant}0
.

The following result, which is
[
MT93c
, Theorem 6.1]
, provides
a condition on Lyapunov functions that guarantees exponential ergodicity.

{theorem}

[Condition for exponential ergodicity]
Assume there exist a norm-like function
V
, and constants
c>0
,
d\in\mathbb{R}
such
that the diffusion
(X_{t})_{t\mathbin{\geqslant}0}
satisfies the condition

({\mathscr{L}}V)(x)\mathbin{\leqslant}-cV(x)+d

(2.2.18)

for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Assume further that all compact
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
are petite
for some discrete-time Markov chain
(X_{n\Delta})_{n\mathbin{\geqslant}0}
. Then the
diffusion is exponentially ergodic. More precisely, there exist constants

\beta,b>0
such that

\lVert P_{t}(x,\cdot)-\pi\rVert_{1+V}\mathbin{\leqslant}b\bigl(1+V(x)\bigr)\e^{-\beta t}

(2.2.19)

for any
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
t\mathbin{\geqslant}0
.

2.2.4
A simplified proof by Hairer and Mattingly

We present here a convergence criterion from
[
HM11
]
, which
applies to discrete-time Markov processes. This is, however, not really a
restriction, because if
P_{t}
is the semi-group of a diffusion

(X_{t})_{t\mathbin{\geqslant}0}
, then for any
\delta>0
,
P_{\delta}
generates the embedded
discrete-time Markov chain
(X_{\delta n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
obtained by restricting

t
to integer multiples of
\delta
. An invariant measure
\pi
of the
diffusion is clearly also invariant for the discrete-time Markov chain, and it
is not difficult to convert a convergence result in discrete time to a
convergence result in continuous time. To avoid confusion, we will denote the
discrete semi-group by
{\mathscr{P}}
, and its actions on bounded measurable functions

f
and signed measures
\mu
by

\displaystyle\bigl({\mathscr{P}}f\bigr)(x)

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}\rule{0.0pt}{9.0pt}}^{\mskip 1.5mux}\bigl[f(X_{\delta})\bigr]:=\intop\nolimits_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}f(y){\mathscr{P}}(x,\dd\!y)\;,

(2.2.20)

\displaystyle\bigl(\mu{\mathscr{P}}\bigr)(A)

\displaystyle={\mathbb{P}\rule{0.0pt}{9.0pt}}^{\mskip 1.5mu\mu}\bigl\{X_{\delta}\in A\bigr\}:=\intop\nolimits_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}{\mathscr{P}}(x,A)\mu(\dd\!x)\;.

(2.2.21)

The convergence result of
[
HM11
]
requires two rather simple
conditions on
{\mathscr{P}}
. The first one is a discrete-time analogue
of (
2.2.18
), which guarantees that the Markov process does not escape
to infinity.

{assumption}

[Geometric drift condition]

There exist a function
V:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to[0,\infty)
and constants
d\mathbin{\geqslant}0
and

\gamma\in(0,1)
such that

\bigl({\mathscr{P}}V\bigr)(x)\mathbin{\leqslant}\gamma V(x)+d

(2.2.22)

for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

Note that
\gamma
is the discrete-time analogue of
\e^{-\delta c}
in
continuous time, so that (
2.2.22
) is indeed the discrete anlogue
of (
2.2.18
). The second condition is a form of irreducibility
condition, which is analogous to the conditions on sets being petite that we
encountered above, but simpler to verify. It is also similar to what is
known as
Doeblin condition
in the Markov chain literature.

{assumption}

[Minorisation condition]

Let
C=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\colon V(x)<R\}
for some
R>2d(1-\gamma)^{-1}
. Then
there exists
\alpha\in(0,1)
and a probability measure
\nu
such that

\inf_{x\in C}{\mathscr{P}}(x,A)\mathbin{\geqslant}\alpha\nu(A)

(2.2.23)

holds for all Borel sets
A\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

Under these two conditions, the main result of
[
HM11
]
is
the following statement, which provides both existence of a unique invariant
measure and convergence to this measure. Convergence takes place in the
weighted supremum norm defined by

\lVert f\rVert_{1+V}=\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\frac{\lvert f(x)\rvert}{1+V(x)}\;.

(2.2.24)

{theorem}

[Exponential ergodicity in discrete time]

If Assumptions
2.2.4
and
2.2.4
hold, then
{\mathscr{P}}

admits a unique invariant probability measure
\pi
. Furthermore, there exist
constants
M>0
and
\gamma\in(0,1)
such that

\lVert{\mathscr{P}}^{n}f-\langle\pi,f\rangle\rVert_{1+V}\mathbin{\leqslant}M\gamma^{n}\lVert f-\langle\pi,f\rangle\rVert_{1+V}

(2.2.25)

holds for all measurable functions
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
such that
\lVert f\rVert_{1+V}<\infty
.

Since the proof of this result is rather elementary (and also quite elegant),
we will provide the details of it in the remainder of this section. Our
presentation follows closely the article
[
HM11
]
. We first
recall the definition of the total variation distance between probability
measures.

{definition}

[Total variation distance]
Let
\mu
and
\nu
be two probability measures on a measure space
({\mathscr{X}},{\mathscr{F}})
.
The
total variation distance
between
\mu
and
\nu
is defined as

\|\mu-\nu\|_{\text{TV}}=2\sup\Bigl\{\lvert\mu(A)-\nu(A)\rvert\colon A\in{\mathscr{F}}\Bigr\}\;.

(2.2.26)

It is known that the total variation distance between
\mu
and
\nu
coincides
with the
L^{1}
-distance:

{lemma}

For any probability measures
\mu
and
\nu
on
({\mathscr{X}},{\mathscr{F}})
, one has

\|\mu-\nu\|_{\text{TV}}=\intop\nolimits_{{\mathscr{X}}}\lvert\mu-\nu\rvert(\dd\!x)\;.

(2.2.27)

Proof:
.

Consider the case where the measures have densities with respect to Lebesgue
measure, and let
B=\bigl\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\colon\mu(x)>\nu(x)\bigr\}
.
Note that since
\mu
and
\nu
are probability measures, we have

0\mathbin{\leqslant}\mu(B)-\nu(B)=1-\mu(B^{c})-\bigl[1-\nu(B^{c})\bigr]=\nu(B^{c})-\mu(B^{c})\;,

(2.2.28)

which implies

\intop\nolimits_{{\mathscr{X}}}\lvert\mu-\nu\rvert(\dd\!x)=(\mu-\nu)(B)+(\nu-\mu)(B^{c})=2(\mu-\nu)(B)\;.

(2.2.29)

For any Borel set
A\in{\mathscr{F}}
, we can write

\mu(A)-\nu(A)\mathbin{\leqslant}\intop\nolimits_{A\cap B}(\mu-\nu)(\dd\!x)\mathbin{\leqslant}\intop\nolimits_{B}(\mu-\nu)(\dd\!x)=\mu(B)-\nu(B)\;,

(2.2.30)

since
\mu-\nu
is negative on
A\cap B^{c}
, and positive on
A^{c}\cap B
. A
similar argument yields

\nu(A)-\mu(A)\mathbin{\leqslant}\intop\nolimits_{B^{c}}(\nu-\mu)(\dd\!x)=\nu(B^{c})-\mu(B^{c})=\mu(B)-\nu(B)\;.

(2.2.31)

We have thus shown the
\lvert\mu(A)-\nu(A)\rvert\mathbin{\leqslant}(\mu-\nu)(B)
, where
equality holds whenever
A=B
or
A=B^{c}
. The result thus follows
from (
2.2.29
).
∎

The main idea of the proof of Theorem
2.2.4
is to work with
a whole family of equivalent norms. Instead of just
\lVert f\rVert_{1+V}
, we
thus consider the norms
\lVert f\rVert_{1+\beta V}
where
\beta>0
is a scale
parameter. We also consider the dual metric on probability measures given by

\displaystyle\rho_{\beta}(\mu,\nu)

\displaystyle=\sup_{f:\lVert f\rVert_{1+\beta V}\mathbin{\leqslant}1}\intop\nolimits_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}f(x)(\mu-\nu)(\dd\!x)

(2.2.32)

\displaystyle=\sup_{f:\lVert f\rVert_{1+\beta V}\neq 0}\frac{1}{\lVert f\rVert_{1+\beta V}}\intop\nolimits_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}f(x)(\mu-\nu)(\dd\!x)\;,

(2.2.33)

which is in fact equivalent to the weighted total variation distance given by

\rho_{\beta}(\mu,\nu)=\intop\nolimits_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\bigl(1+\beta V(x)\bigr)\lvert\mu-\nu\rvert(\dd\!x)\;.

(2.2.34)

In particular, for
\beta=0
, we have
\rho_{0}(\mu,\nu)=\|\mu-\nu\|_{\text{TV}}
.
The supremum in (
2.2.33
) is attained for

f(x)=[1+\beta V(x)][\mathbbm{1}_{B}(x)-\mathbbm{1}_{B^{c}}(x)]
,
with
B
as in the proof of the above lemma.

The key result for the distance
\rho_{\beta}
is the following.

{proposition}

[Contraction estimate in
\rho_{\beta}
distance]

If Assumptions
2.2.4
and
2.2.4
hold, then there
exist
\bar{\alpha}\in(0,1)
and
\beta>0
such that

\rho_{\beta}(\mu{\mathscr{P}},\nu{\mathscr{P}})\mathbin{\leqslant}\bar{\alpha}\rho_{\beta}(\mu,\nu)

(2.2.35)

holds for all probability measures
\mu,\nu
on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. More precisely, for
any
\alpha_{0}\in(0,\alpha)
and any
\gamma_{0}\in(\gamma+2dR^{-1},1)
, one can
choose

\beta=\frac{\alpha_{0}}{d}\;,\qquad\bar{\alpha}=\Bigl(1-(\alpha-\alpha_{0})\Bigr)\vee\frac{2+R\beta\gamma_{0}}{2+R\beta}\;.

(2.2.36)

To prove this result, we introduce an alternative definition of
\rho_{\beta}
.
Consider first the function

d_{\beta}(x,y)=\begin{cases}0&\text{if $x=y$\;,}\\
2+\beta V(x)+\beta V(y)&\text{if $x\neq y$\;,}\end{cases}

(2.2.37)

which can easily be checked to be a metric on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. This metric induces the
Lipschitz seminorm

|\!|\!|f|\!|\!|_{\beta}=\sup_{x\neq y}\frac{\lvert f(x)-f(y)\rvert}{d_{\beta}(x,y)}\;,

(2.2.38)

and a dual metric on probability measures given by

\rho^{*}_{\beta}(\mu,\nu)=\sup_{f:|\!|\!|f|\!|\!|_{\beta}\mathbin{\leqslant}1}\intop\nolimits_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}f(x)(\mu-\nu)(\dd\!x)\;.

(2.2.39)

Note that the supremum is taken on a different set of functions
than in (
2.2.33
).

{lemma}

[Equivalence of norms]

We have

|\!|\!|f|\!|\!|_{\beta}=\inf_{c\in\mathbb{R}}\lVert f+c\rVert_{1+\beta V}\;.

(2.2.40)

In particular,
\rho^{*}_{\beta}=\rho_{\beta}
.

Proof:
.

We first note that since
\lvert f(x)\rvert\mathbin{\leqslant}\lVert f\rVert_{1+\beta V}(1+\beta V(x))

for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we have

\frac{\lvert f(x)-f(y)\rvert}{2+\beta V(x)+\beta V(y)}\mathbin{\leqslant}\frac{\lvert f(x)\rvert+\lvert f(y)\rvert}{2+\beta V(x)+\beta V(y)}\mathbin{\leqslant}\lVert f\rVert_{1+\beta V}

(2.2.41)

for all
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, so that
|\!|\!|f|\!|\!|_{\beta}\mathbin{\leqslant}\lVert f\rVert_{1+\beta V}
. It follows from the
definition (
2.2.38
) of
|\!|\!|f|\!|\!|_{\beta}
that
this seminorm is invariant under addition of a constant to
f
, so that

|\!|\!|f|\!|\!|_{\beta}\mathbin{\leqslant}\inf_{c\in\mathbb{R}}\;\lVert f+c\rVert_{1+\beta V}\;.

(2.2.42)

To prove the reverse inequality, it suffices by homogeneity of the norm to show
that if holds for
f
with
|\!|\!|f|\!|\!|_{\beta}=1
. We set

c^{*}=\inf_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\biggl(1+\beta V(x)-f(x)\biggr)\;.

(2.2.43)

For any
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we have

f(x)\mathbin{\leqslant}\lvert f(y)\rvert+\lvert f(x)-f(y)\rvert\mathbin{\leqslant}\lvert f(y)\rvert+2+\beta V(x)+\beta V(y)\;,

(2.2.44)

which implies

1+\beta V(x)-f(x)\mathbin{\geqslant}-1-\beta V(y)-\lvert f(y)\rvert\;.

(2.2.45)

Since
V(y)
is finite at one point at least,
c^{*}
is bounded
below, and hence
\lvert c^{*}\rvert<\infty
. Now we observe that on one hand,

f(x)+c^{*}\mathbin{\leqslant}f(x)+1+\beta V(x)-f(x)=1+\beta V(x)\;,

(2.2.46)

while on the other hand,

\displaystyle f(x)+c^{*}

\displaystyle=\inf_{y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\biggl(f(x)+1+\beta V(y)-f(y)\biggr)

(2.2.47)

\displaystyle\mathbin{\geqslant}\inf_{y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\biggl(1+\beta V(y)-|\!|\!|f|\!|\!|_{\beta}d_{\beta}(x,y)\biggr)

(2.2.48)

\displaystyle\mathbin{\geqslant}-1-\beta V(x)\;,

(2.2.49)

where we have used the fact that
|\!|\!|f|\!|\!|_{\beta}=1
.
Hence
\lvert f(x)+c^{*}\rvert\mathbin{\leqslant}1+\beta V(x)
, and thus

\inf_{c\in\mathbb{R}}\;\lVert f+c\rVert_{1+\beta V}\mathbin{\leqslant}\lVert f+c^{*}\rVert_{1+\beta V}\mathbin{\leqslant}1=|\!|\!|f|\!|\!|_{\beta}\;,

(2.2.50)

proving the reverse inequality. The equality of
\rho^{*}_{\beta}
and
\rho_{\beta}

follows from the fact that the unit balls
\{f\colon\lVert f\rVert_{1+\beta V}\}\mathbin{\leqslant}1
and
\{f\colon|\!|\!|f|\!|\!|_{\beta}\mathbin{\leqslant}1\}
only differ by additive
constants, and homogeneity of the norms.
∎

Proof of Proposition
2.2.4
.

We prove that under Assumptions
2.2.4

and
2.2.4
, one has

|\!|\!|{\mathscr{P}}f|\!|\!|_{\beta}\mathbin{\leqslant}\bar{\alpha}|\!|\!|f|\!|\!|_{\beta}\;.

(2.2.51)

Fix a test function
f
with
|\!|\!|f|\!|\!|_{\beta}\mathbin{\leqslant}1
. By
Lemma
2.2.4
we can assume, without loss of generality, that
one also has
\lVert f\rVert_{1+\beta V}\mathbin{\leqslant}1
. By homogeneity, it then suffices to
show that

\bigl|\bigl({\mathscr{P}}f\bigr)(x)-\bigl({\mathscr{P}}f\bigr)(y)\bigr|\mathbin{\leqslant}\bar{\alpha}d_{\beta}(x,y)\;.

(2.2.52)

Since the claim is true for
x=y
, we consider the case
x\neq y
. We treat
separately the cases
V(x)+V(y)\mathbin{\geqslant}R
and
V(x)+V(y)<R
.

•

If
V(x)+V(y)\mathbin{\geqslant}R
, we note that

\bigl|\bigl({\mathscr{P}}f\bigr)(x)\bigr|\mathbin{\leqslant}\lVert f\rVert_{1+\beta V}\intop\nolimits_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(1+\beta V(y)){\mathscr{P}}(x,\dd\!y)\mathbin{\leqslant}1+\beta\bigl({\mathscr{P}}V\bigr)(x)\;.

(2.2.53)

Therefore, the geometric drift condition (
2.2.22
) yields

\displaystyle\bigl|\bigl({\mathscr{P}}f\bigr)(x)-\bigl({\mathscr{P}}f\bigr)(y)\bigr|

\displaystyle\mathbin{\leqslant}2+\beta\bigl({\mathscr{P}}V\bigr)(x)+\beta\bigl({\mathscr{P}}V\bigr)(y)

(2.2.54)

\displaystyle\mathbin{\leqslant}2+\beta\gamma V(x)+\beta\gamma V(y)+2\beta d

(2.2.55)

\displaystyle\mathbin{\leqslant}2+\beta\gamma_{0}V(x)+\beta\gamma_{0}V(y)\;,

(2.2.56)

where we have set
\gamma_{0}=\gamma+2dR^{-1}
and used
V(x)+V(y)\mathbin{\geqslant}R
. We now set

\gamma_{1}=\frac{2+\beta R\gamma_{0}}{2+\beta R}\;.

(2.2.57)

One readily checks that
2(1-\gamma_{1})=\beta R(\gamma_{1}-\gamma_{0})\mathbin{\leqslant}\beta(\gamma_{1}-\gamma_{0})(V(x)+V(y))
, so that

\bigl|\bigl({\mathscr{P}}f\bigr)(x)-\bigl({\mathscr{P}}f\bigr)(y)\bigr|\mathbin{\leqslant}\gamma_{1}\Bigl(2+\beta V(x)+\beta V(y)\Bigr)=\gamma_{1}d_{\beta}(x,y)\;.

(2.2.58)

•

If
V(x)+V(y)<R
, then
x,y\in C
. We introduce the Markov kernel

\widetilde{\mathscr{P}}
defined by

\widetilde{\mathscr{P}}(x,A)=\frac{1}{1-\alpha}{\mathscr{P}}(x,A)-\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}\nu(A)\;.

(2.2.59)

Note that
\widetilde{\mathscr{P}}(x,\mathbb{R}^{n})=1
, while the minorisation
condition (
2.2.23
) implies that
\widetilde{\mathscr{P}}(x,A)
is always
positive, as required. Then we have

\bigl({\mathscr{P}}f\bigr)(x)=(1-\alpha)\bigl(\widetilde{\mathscr{P}}f\bigr)(x)+\alpha\intop\nolimits_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}f(y)\nu(\dd\!y)\;,

(2.2.60)

showing that

\displaystyle\bigl|\bigl({\mathscr{P}}f\bigr)(x)-\bigl({\mathscr{P}}f\bigr)(y)\bigr|

\displaystyle=(1-\alpha)\bigl|\bigl(\widetilde{\mathscr{P}}f\bigr)(x)-\bigl(\widetilde{\mathscr{P}}f\bigr)(y)\bigr|

(2.2.61)

\displaystyle\mathbin{\leqslant}(1-\alpha)\bigl[2+\beta\bigl(\widetilde{\mathscr{P}}V\bigr)(x)+\beta\bigl(\widetilde{\mathscr{P}}V\bigr)(y)\bigr]

(2.2.62)

\displaystyle\mathbin{\leqslant}2(1-\alpha)+\beta\bigl({\mathscr{P}}V\bigr)(x)+\beta\bigl({\mathscr{P}}V\bigr)(y)

(2.2.63)

\displaystyle\mathbin{\leqslant}2(1-\alpha)+\gamma\beta\bigl[V(x)+V(y)\bigr]+2\beta d\;.

(2.2.64)

Here, to obtain the second line, we have used a similar argument as
in (
2.2.53
), while the third line uses the fact that

\bigl(\widetilde{\mathscr{P}}V\bigr)(x)\mathbin{\leqslant}\frac{1}{1-\alpha}\bigl({\mathscr{P}}V\bigr)(x)

(2.2.65)

since
V
is non-negative. It follows that setting

\beta=\frac{\alpha_{0}}{d}\;,\qquad\gamma_{2}=\gamma\vee\Bigl(1-(\alpha-\alpha_{0})\Bigr)

(2.2.66)

for some
\alpha_{0}\in(0,\alpha)
, one obtains

\bigl|\bigl({\mathscr{P}}f\bigr)(x)-\bigl({\mathscr{P}}f\bigr)(y)\bigr|\mathbin{\leqslant}\gamma_{2}d_{\beta}(x,y)\;.

(2.2.67)

It follows from (
2.2.58
) and (
2.2.67
) that

\bigl|\bigl({\mathscr{P}}f\bigr)(x)-\bigl({\mathscr{P}}f\bigr)(y)\bigr|\mathbin{\leqslant}\bar{\alpha}d_{\beta}(x,y)\;,\qquad\bar{\alpha}=\gamma_{1}\vee\gamma_{2}\;.

(2.2.68)

Since
\gamma_{1}\mathbin{\geqslant}\gamma
, this implies (
2.2.51
).
The result then follows from the fact that
d_{\beta}
is the norm dual to

|\!|\!|\cdot|\!|\!|_{\beta}
. Indeed, we have

\displaystyle\rho^{*}_{\beta}(\mu{\mathscr{P}},\nu{\mathscr{P}})

\displaystyle=\sup_{f:|\!|\!|f|\!|\!|_{\beta}\neq 0}\frac{1}{|\!|\!|f|\!|\!|_{\beta}}\langle\mu{\mathscr{P}}-\nu{\mathscr{P}},f\rangle=\sup_{f:|\!|\!|f|\!|\!|_{\beta}\neq 0}\frac{1}{|\!|\!|f|\!|\!|_{\beta}}\langle\mu-\nu,{\mathscr{P}}f\rangle

(2.2.69)

\displaystyle\mathbin{\leqslant}\sup_{\tilde{f}:|\!|\!|\tilde{f}|\!|\!|_{\beta}\neq 0}\frac{\bar{\alpha}}{|\!|\!|\tilde{f}|\!|\!|_{\beta}}\langle\mu-\nu,\tilde{f}\rangle=\bar{\alpha}\rho^{*}_{\beta}(\mu,\nu)\;,

(2.2.70)

and we obtain the conclusion since
\rho_{\beta}=\rho^{*}_{\beta}
.
∎

To conclude the proof of Theorem
2.2.4
, it remains to
prove existence of the invariant measure, which can be done by a contraction
argument.

Proof of Theorem
2.2.4
.

We fix some

x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and define for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
the measure
\mu_{n}^{x}=\delta_{x}{\mathscr{P}}^{n}
.
Then by Proposition
2.2.4
, we have

\rho_{\beta}(\mu_{n+1}^{x},\mu_{n}^{x})\mathbin{\leqslant}\bar{\alpha}^{n}\rho_{\beta}(\mu_{1}^{x},\delta_{x})

(2.2.71)

for some
\bar{\alpha}\in(0,1)
and
\beta>0
. Therefore,
(\mu_{n}^{x})_{n}
is a
Cauchy sequence. It is known that the total variation distance is complete for
the space of measures with finite mass, implying that
\rho_{\beta}
is complete
for the space of probability measures integrating
V
. Therefore, there exists
a probability measure
\pi=\mu_{\infty}
such that

\rho_{\beta}(\mu_{n}^{x},\mu_{\infty})\to 0
as
n\to\infty
. This implies that

\mu_{n}^{x}
converges to
\mu_{\infty}
in total variation. Since
{\mathscr{P}}
is a
contraction in total variation, it follows that
\mu_{\infty}{\mathscr{P}}=\lim_{n\to\infty}\mu_{n}^{x}{\mathscr{P}}=\lim_{n\to\infty}\mu_{n+1}^{x}=\mu_{\infty}
.
In order to show (
2.2.25
), we observe that

\bigl({\mathscr{P}}^{n}f\bigr)(x)-\langle\pi,f\rangle=\langle\delta_{x}-\pi,{\mathscr{P}}^{n}f\rangle=\langle\delta_{x}{\mathscr{P}}^{n}-\pi,f\rangle=\langle\mu_{n}^{x}-\pi,f\rangle\;.

(2.2.72)

Therefore, by the definition (
2.2.33
) of
\rho_{\beta}
, we have for
any
\beta\mathbin{\geqslant}0
,

\lVert{\mathscr{P}}^{n}f-\langle\pi,f\rangle\rVert_{1+\beta V}=\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\frac{\lvert\langle\mu_{n}^{x}-\pi,f\rangle\rvert}{1+\beta V(x)}\mathbin{\leqslant}\lVert f\rVert_{1+\beta V}\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\frac{\rho_{\beta}(\mu_{n}^{x},\pi)}{1+\beta V(x)}\;.

(2.2.73)

Now it follows from (
2.2.71
) and a telescopic sum
argument that for
\beta
as in Proposition
2.2.4
, one
has
\rho_{\beta}(\mu_{n}^{x},\pi)\mathbin{\leqslant}M(x)\bar{\alpha}^{n}
, where
M(x)
is
proportional to

\rho_{\beta}(\mu_{1}^{x},\delta_{x})=\intop\nolimits_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\bigl(1+\beta V(y)\bigr)\lvert\mu_{1}^{x}-\delta_{x}\rvert(\dd\!y)\mathbin{\leqslant}\langle\mu_{1}^{x},1+\beta V\rangle+1+\beta V(x)\;.

(2.2.74)

Since
\langle\mu_{1}^{x},V\rangle=({\mathscr{P}}V)(x)\mathbin{\leqslant}\gamma V(x)+d
by the geometric
drift condition (
2.2.22
), the supremum
in (
2.2.73
) has order
\bar{\alpha}^{n}
, proving the result
for this particular
\beta
. However, all
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{1+\beta V}
-norms are
equivalent, so that the result holds in particular for
\beta=1
.
∎

2.3
Garrett Birkhoff’s approach

The aim of this section is to present a slightly different approach to
estimating the rate of convergence to an invariant probability distribution,
due to Garret Birkhoff
[
Bir57
]
. Compared to the approaches we have
discussed so far, it has the following advantages:

1.

The proof has a more transparent geometric interpretation, that helps
understand the minorisation condition (
2.2.23
) we have seen in
the last section.

2.

As Theorem
2.2.4
, the result provides explicit
bounds on the rate of convergence to the invariant probability.

3.

The result also works for submarkovian processes, that is, processes in
which the total probability decreases. In that case, it provides information on
the principal eigenvalue of the process, as well as on the spectral gap to the
next-to-leading eigenvalue.

As in the last subsection, the approach applies to discrete-time Markov chains.
For simplicity, we are going to assume that the transition kernel
{\mathscr{P}}
has a
density
p(x,y)
defined on
{\mathscr{X}}\times{\mathscr{X}}
for a domain
{\mathscr{X}}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
(or
possibly on a more general Banach space). The transition kernel thus acts on
bounded measurable functions
f
and on signed measures
\mu
according to

\displaystyle\bigl({\mathscr{P}}f\bigr)(x)

\displaystyle=\intop\nolimits_{{\mathscr{X}}}p(x,y)f(y)\dd\!y=\mathbb{E}^{\mskip 1.5mux}\!\left[f(X_{1})\right]\;,

(2.3.1)

\displaystyle\bigl(\mu{\mathscr{P}}\bigr)(\dd\!y)

\displaystyle=\intop\nolimits_{{\mathscr{X}}}\mu(\dd\!x)p(x,y)\dd\!y=\mathbb{P}^{\mskip 1.5mu\mu}\{X_{1}\in\dd\!y\}\;.

(2.3.2)

The main property that will guarantee convergence to an invariant probability
distribution (or to a so-called quasistationary distribution in the
submarkovian case) is the following. Note the similarity of the lower bound
with the minorisation condition (
2.2.23
).

{definition}

[Uniform positivity]

The transformation
{\mathscr{P}}
is called
uniformly positive
if there exist
strictly positive functions
s,m:{\mathscr{X}}\to(0,+\infty)
and a constant
L
such
that

s(x)m(y)\mathbin{\leqslant}p(x,y)\mathbin{\leqslant}Ls(x)m(y)\qquad\forall x,y\in{\mathscr{X}}\;.

(2.3.3)

The main results we are going to prove are as follows. The first one is an
existence result for an invariant probability measure, or for its equivalent if
the chain is submarkovian. It is thus a generalisation to integral operators of
the well-known Perron–Frobenius theorem, which was first obtained by
Jentzsch
[
Jen12
]
.

{theorem}

[Perron–Frobenius–Jentzsch theorem]

If
{\mathscr{P}}
is uniformly positive, there exist
\lambda_{0}>0
, a bounded
measurable function
h_{0}:{\mathscr{X}}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
, and a probability measure
\pi_{0}
on

{\mathscr{X}}
such that

\displaystyle\bigl({\mathscr{P}}h_{0}\bigr)(x)=\lambda_{0}h_{0}(x)\;,

(2.3.4)

\displaystyle\bigl(\pi_{0}{\mathscr{P}}\bigr)(A)=\lambda_{0}\pi_{0}(A)

(2.3.5)

for all
x\in{\mathscr{X}}
and all Borel sets
A\subset{\mathscr{X}}
. In particular, in the
Markovian case
{\mathscr{P}}(x,{\mathscr{X}})=1
for all
x\in{\mathscr{X}}
, one has
\lambda_{0}=1
and

h_{0}(x)=1
for all
x\in{\mathscr{X}}
.

The number
\lambda_{0}
is called the
principal eigenvalue
of the Markov
process, the function
h_{0}
is called the
principal eigenfunction
, while

\pi_{0}
is called the
quasistationary distribution
(in the submarkovian
case, when
\lambda_{0}<1
), and is equal to the stationary distribution in the
Markovian case, when
\lambda_{0}=1
.
The main interest of the uniform positivity condition is the following result
on speed of convergence towards the principal eigenfunction
h_{0}
.

{theorem}

[Spectral gap estimate]

If
{\mathscr{P}}
is uniformly positive, then for any bounded measurable
f:{\mathscr{X}}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
, there exist finite constants
M_{1}(f),M_{2}(f)
such that

\bigl|{\mathscr{P}}^{n}f(x)-\lambda_{0}^{n}M_{1}(f)h_{0}(x)\bigr|\mathbin{\leqslant}M_{2}(f)\lambda_{0}^{n}\Biggl(1-\frac{1}{L^{2}}\Biggr)^{n}h_{0}(x)

(2.3.6)

for all
x\in{\mathscr{X}}
. In particular,

M_{1}(f)=\frac{\langle\pi_{0},f\rangle}{\langle\pi_{0},h_{0}\rangle}\;,

(2.3.7)

which reduces in the Markovian case to
M_{1}(f)=\langle\pi_{0},f\rangle
.

Note that in the Markovian case
\lambda_{0}=1
, the
bound (
2.3.6
) is equivalent to the exponential
ergodicity result (
2.2.25
) of
Theorem
2.2.4
, but with an explicit value of the
contraction constant
\gamma
given by
1-L^{-2}
.

In what follows, we are going to provide a detailed proof of
Theorem
2.3
, starting with some simpler situations in order to
build the intuition.

2.3.1
Two-dimensional case

The simplest case occurs when
{\mathscr{X}}
is a discrete set of cardinality
2
. Then

{\mathscr{P}}
is a linear operator on
{\mathscr{E}}=\mathbb{R}^{2}
, that is, a
2\times 2
matrix

{\mathscr{P}}=\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{pmatrix}

(2.3.8)

with strictly positive entries. Therefore
{\mathscr{P}}
maps the cone

{\mathscr{E}}^{+}=\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}
strictly into itself. Iterating
{\mathscr{P}}
, the image of

{\mathscr{E}}^{+}
becomes thinner and thinner, and concentrates on the eigenvector of

{\mathscr{P}}
for the largest eigenvalue (Figure
2.1
). However, unless
the principal eigenvalue
\lambda_{0}
of
{\mathscr{P}}
is
1
, iterates of a vector in

{\mathscr{E}}^{+}
will not converge to an eigenvector: they will shrink to
0
if

\lambda_{0}<1
and diverge if
\lambda_{0}>1
.

To avoid this, one can identify all vectors
f,g
such that
f=\lambda g
for
some
\lambda>0
. In other words, this amounts to working on the projective
line. Iterates of a projective line in
{\mathscr{E}}^{+}
will converge to the eigenspace
associated with
\lambda_{0}
.

Birkhoff introduces Hilbert’s
projective metric
by defining, for

f=(f_{1},f_{2})
and
g=(g_{1},g_{2})\in{\mathscr{E}}^{+}

\theta(f,g)=\biggl|\log\biggl(\frac{f_{2}g_{1}}{f_{1}g_{2}}\biggr)\biggr|\;.

(2.3.9)

Note that this distance is infinite if
f
or
g
belongs to a coordinate axis;
in fact, it induces a hyperbolic geometry. Also note that by definition,

\theta(\lambda f,\mu g)=\theta(f,g)\qquad\forall\lambda,\mu>0\;.

(2.3.10)

{lemma}

[Projective operator norm of
{\mathscr{P}}

The operator norm of
{\mathscr{P}}
in the projective metric is given by

\sup_{f,g\in{\mathscr{E}}^{+}}\frac{\theta({\mathscr{P}}f,{\mathscr{P}}g)}{\theta(f,g)}=\tanh\Biggl(\frac{\Delta}{4}\Biggr)\;,

(2.3.11)

where
\Delta=\lvert\log(ad/bc)\rvert
is the diameter, in the projective norm, of

{\mathscr{P}}({\mathscr{E}}^{+})
.

Proof:
.

We may assume, without limitation of generality, that
ad>bc
(note that in
the case
ad=bc
, the cone
{\mathscr{E}}^{+}
is projected to a half line by
{\mathscr{P}}
, so
that the operator norm of
{\mathscr{P}}
is zero). Let
f=(1,x)
be a point in

{\mathscr{E}}^{+}
. Then

{\mathscr{P}}f=(a+bx,c+dx)=\lambda\bigl(1,\varphi(x)\bigr)\;,

(2.3.12)

where
\lambda=a+bx
, and
\varphi
denotes the homographic transformation

x\mapsto\varphi(x)=\frac{c+dx}{a+bx}\;.

(2.3.13)

The projective distance between two infinitesimally close points
f=(1,x)
and

(1,x+\dd\!x)
is given by
\dd\!\theta(x)=\lvert\log(x/(x+\dd\!x))\rvert=\lvert\dd\!x\rvert/x
.
Therefore, we obtain

\frac{\dd\!\theta(\varphi(x))}{\dd\!\theta(x)}=\frac{x\varphi^{\prime}(x)}{\varphi(x)}=\frac{x(ad-bc)}{(a+bx)(c+dx)}\;.

(2.3.14)

It is straightforward to check that this expression is maximal in
x=\sqrt{ac/(bd)}
, where it has value

\frac{ad-bc}{ad+bc+2\sqrt{abcd}}=\frac{\e^{\Delta}-1}{1+\e^{\Delta}+2\e^{\Delta/2}}=\tanh\Biggl(\frac{\Delta}{4}\Biggr)\;.

(2.3.15)

Since this value gives the smallest rate of contraction, the claim follows.

2.3.3
Integral transformations and Jentzsch’s theorem

We now return to the situation described at the beginning of this section, where

{\mathscr{X}}
is a Borel set of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and
{\mathscr{E}}
is the Banach space of continuous
functions
f:{\mathscr{X}}\to\mathbb{R}
, equipped with the supremum norm. Let
{\mathscr{E}}^{+}
denote the
cone of positive functions
f:{\mathscr{X}}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
. Consider the integral operator

{\mathscr{P}}:{\mathscr{E}}^{+}\to{\mathscr{E}}^{+}
defined by (
2.3.2
).

{proposition}

[Bound on the projective diameter]
If
{\mathscr{P}}
satisfies the uniform positivity
condition (
2.3.3
), then the diameter of
{\mathscr{P}}({\mathscr{E}}^{+})

satisfies

\Delta\mathbin{\leqslant}2\log L\;.

(2.3.22)

Proof:
.

Let
f,g\in{\mathscr{E}}^{+}
. Without limiting the generality, we may assume

\intop\nolimits_{{\mathscr{X}}}f(y)m(y)\dd\!y=\intop\nolimits_{{\mathscr{X}}}g(y)m(y)\dd\!y=1

(2.3.23)

This implies

s(x)\mathbin{\leqslant}({\mathscr{P}}f)(x)\;,\;({\mathscr{P}}g)(x)\mathbin{\leqslant}Ls(x)\qquad\forall x\in{\mathscr{X}}\;.

(2.3.24)

It follows that
({\mathscr{P}}f)-\frac{1}{L}({\mathscr{P}}g)\mathbin{\geqslant}0
and
({\mathscr{P}}g)-\frac{1}{L}({\mathscr{P}}f)\mathbin{\geqslant}0
. Thus
\alpha^{*},\beta^{*}
defined in (
2.3.17
) and (
2.3.18
) are
greater or equal than

, that is
1/\alpha^{*},1/\beta^{*}\mathbin{\leqslant}L

and the result follows from (
2.3.20
).
∎

Applying Theorem
2.3.2
, we recover Jentzsch’s
generalisation of the Perron–Frobenius theorem to integral
operators
[

{theorem}

[Perron–Frobenius theorem for integral operators]
If
{\mathscr{P}}
is uniformly positive, then there exists a strictly positive

h_{0}\in{\mathscr{E}}^{+}
and
\lambda_{0}>0
such that
{\mathscr{P}}h_{0}=\lambda_{0}h_{0}
. Moreover, for
any

f\in{\mathscr{E}}^{+}
, the sequence of lines spanned by
{\mathscr{P}}^{n}f
converges to the line
spanned by
h_{0}
.

{remark}

[Dual picture]

The dual map
{\mathscr{P}}^{*}
given by

\bigl({\mathscr{P}}^{*}v\bigr)(y):=\bigl(v{\mathscr{P}}\bigr)(y)=\intop\nolimits_{{\mathscr{X}}}v(x)p(x,y)\dd\!x

(2.3.25)

satisfies
\langle{\mathscr{P}}^{*}v,f\rangle=\langle x,{\mathscr{P}}f\rangle
, where
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle

denotes the usual inner product. Jentzsch’s theorem shows the existence of a
strictly positive function
p_{0}
such that
{\mathscr{P}}^{*}p_{0}=\lambda_{0}p_{0}

Large deviations for stochastic differential equations

The theory of large deviations is a very powerful tool to analyse sequences

(X_{n})_{n\mathbin{\geqslant}0}
or
(X_{\varepsilon})_{\varepsilon>0}
of random variables that obey some
scaling behaviour as
n\to\infty
or
\varepsilon\to 0
. Standard textbooks on that
theory are
[
DZ98
]
and
[
DS89
]
. In the particular case of scaled Brownian
motion

, Schilder’s theorem provides a large-deviation
principle, which can be transferred to SDEs using the so-called contraction
principle. This is the starting point of the theory developed by Freidlin and
Wentzell in the monograph
[
FW98
]
.

In this chapter, we will first provide a general introduction to
large-deviation principles, before specialising it to the case of SDEs, and
giving some applications to the exit problem of a diffusion from a domain.

3.1
Large-deviation principles

3.1.1
A simple example

Let
S_{n}
be the number of heads obtained when throwing a fair coin
n

Then

follows a binomial distribution with parameters
(n,\frac{1}{2})
, and

\frac{n}{2}

\widehat{S}_{n}=\frac{S_{n}-\frac{n}{2}}{\sqrt{\frac{n}{4}}}

(3.1.2)

converges in distribution to a standard normal law as

. This

is likely to belong to an interval of order

\frac{n}{2}

One may be interested in more precise estimates on the probability of

\mathbb{P}\Biggl\{\biggl|\frac{S_{n}}{n}-\frac{1}{2}\biggr|\mathbin{\geqslant}\delta\Biggr\}\mathbin{\leqslant}\frac{1}{\delta^{2}}\variance\biggl(\frac{S_{n}}{n}\biggr)=\frac{1}{4n\delta^{2}}\;.

C\subset S
one has

\limsup_{\varepsilon\to 0}\varepsilon\log\mu_{\varepsilon}(C)\mathbin{\leqslant}-\inf_{s\in C}{\mathscr{I}}(s)\;.

(3.1.17)

3.

For every open set
O\subset S
one has

\liminf_{\varepsilon\to 0}\varepsilon\log\mu_{\varepsilon}(O)\mathbin{\geqslant}-\inf_{s\in O}{\mathscr{I}}(s)\;.

(3.1.18)

Roughly speaking, the large-deviation principle says that for any
sufficiently nice set
\Gamma\subset S
, we have

\mu_{\varepsilon}(\Gamma)\simeq\e^{-\inf_{\Gamma}{\mathscr{I}}/\varepsilon}

(3.1.19)

in the sense of logarithmic equivalence.

{example}

[Cramér’s theorem as a large-deviation principle]
Setting
n=\lfloor\varepsilon^{-1}\rfloor
, Theorem
3.1.1
shows that

S_{n}=\sumop\displaylimits_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}
satisfies a large-deviation principle with rate
function

{\mathscr{I}}(s)=\Lambda^{*}(s)\;.

(3.1.20)

3.1.3
The contraction principle

The contraction principle is a powerful method to generate new large-deviation
principles from already known ones. The following version of this principle
follows directly from
[
DS89
, Lemma 2.1.4]
, see
also
[
HW15
, Lemma 3.3]
.

{lemma}

[Contraction principle]

Let
\{\mu_{\varepsilon}\}_{\varepsilon>0}
be a family of probability measures on a
separable metric space
(S,d)
, and let
{\mathscr{I}}:S\to[0,\infty]
satisfy the first
condition of Definition
3.1.2
.
Let
(S^{\prime},d^{\prime})
be another separable metric space, and let

\{{}_{\varepsilon}\colon\varepsilon\mathbin{\geqslant}0\}
be a family of maps from
S
to
S^{\prime}
which are
continuous on a neighbourhood of
\{s\in S\colon{\mathscr{I}}(s)<\infty\}
.
We assume that

1.

The probability measures
\{\mu_{\varepsilon}\}_{\varepsilon>0}
satisfy a
large-deviation principle on
S
with rate function
{\mathscr{I}}
.

2.

For every
c\in\mathbb{R}
, there exists a neighbourhood
O_{c}
of
\{s\in S\colon{\mathscr{I}}(s)\mathbin{\leqslant}c\}
such that the maps
ε
converge uniformly on

O_{c}
to
0
.

Then the image measures
\mu_{\varepsilon}\circ{}_{\varepsilon}^{-1}
satisfy a
large-deviation principle on
S^{\prime}
with rate function

{\mathscr{I}}^{\prime}(s^{\prime})=\inf\bigl\{{\mathscr{I}}(s)\colon s\in S,{}_{0}(s)=s^{\prime}\bigr\}\;,

(3.1.21)

with the convention that the infimum equals
+\infty
if the set is empty.

3.2
Large deviations for SDEs

3.2.1
Schilder’s theorem

We consider in this section scaled Brownian motion in
\mathbb{R}
or
\mathbb{R}^{n}
defined
for any
\varepsilon>0
by

W^{\varepsilon}_{t}=\sqrt{\varepsilon}W_{t}\;.

(3.2.1)

Our aim is to obtain a large-deviation principle on sample-paths of
W^{\varepsilon}_{t}
,
meaning that it should apply to the random functions
\{W^{\varepsilon}_{t}\}_{0\mathbin{\leqslant}t\mathbin{\leqslant}T}
for some fixed time horizon
T>0
. Let us first consider some
particular cases of subsets of sample paths.

{example}

[Brownian motion at a fixed time]

Fix a time
T>0
and an open set
A\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Since

\mathbb{P}\bigl\{W^{\varepsilon}_{T}\in A\bigr\}=\intop\nolimits_{A}\frac{1}{\sqrt{(2\pi\varepsilon T)^{n}}}\e^{-x^{2}/(2\varepsilon T)}\dd\!x\;,

(3.2.2)

applying the Laplace method shows that

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\varepsilon\log\mathbb{P}\bigl\{W^{\varepsilon}_{T}\in A\bigr\}=-\frac{1}{2}\inf_{x\in A}\frac{\lVert x\rVert^{2}}{T}\;.

(3.2.3)

{example}

[
1d
scaled Brownian motion staying below level
h
]

The reflection principle implies

\mathbb{P}\Biggl\{\sup_{0\mathbin{\leqslant}t\mathbin{\leqslant}T}W^{\varepsilon}_{t}>h\Biggr\}=2\mathbb{P}\bigl\{W^{\varepsilon}_{T}>h\bigr\}=2\mathbb{P}\Biggl\{W_{T}>\frac{h}{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}\Biggr\}=2\intop\nolimits_{h/\sqrt{\varepsilon}}^{\infty}\frac{\e^{-x^{2}/2}}{\sqrt{2\pi\varepsilon}}\dd\!x\;,

(3.2.4)

which yields

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\varepsilon\log\mathbb{P}\Biggl\{\sup_{0\mathbin{\leqslant}t\mathbin{\leqslant}T}W^{\varepsilon}_{t}>h\Biggr\}=-\frac{h^{2}}{2T}\;.

(3.2.5)

In both cases, we would like to write the right-hand side
of (
3.2.3
) and (
3.2.5
) as the infimum of
some rate function over all sample paths fulfilling the property defining the
event. It turns out that such a rate function is provided by the following
theorem.

{theorem}

[Schilder’s theorem]

Let
{\mathscr{C}}_{0}
be the space of continuous functions
\varphi:[0,T]\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that

\varphi(0)=0
. Scaled Brownian motion on
[0,T]
satisfies a large-deviation
principle on
{\mathscr{C}}_{0}
with rate function

{\mathscr{I}}_{[0,T]}(\varphi)=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\lVert\varphi\rVert_{H^{1}}^{2}=\frac{1}{2}\intop\nolimits_{0}^{T}\lVert\dot{\varphi}(s)\rVert^{2}\dd\!s&\text{if $\varphi\in H^{1}$ and $\varphi(0)=0$\;,}\\[14.0pt]
+\infty&\text{otherwise\;.}\end{cases}

(3.2.6)

Before giving a proof of this result, let us look at some of its consequences.
Let
\psi:[0,T]\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a path vanishing at times
0
and
T
. Then the
Gateaux derivative of the rate function in the direction
\psi
is given by

\frac{\dd\!}{\dd\!t}{\mathscr{I}}_{[0,T]}(\varphi+t\psi)\Bigr|_{t=0}=\intop\nolimits_{0}^{T}\langle\dot{\varphi}(s),\dot{\psi}(s)\rangle\dd\!s=-\intop\nolimits_{0}^{T}\langle\ddot{\varphi}(s),\psi(s)\rangle\dd\!s\;.

(3.2.7)

Stationary points of the rate function are thus straight lines of the form

\varphi(s)=sv
for some
v\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. This is nothing but the Euler–Lagrange
variational principle applied to the Lagrangian of a free particle. In
Example
3.2.1
, we can apply the large-deviation principle to the set
of paths
\varphi
such that
\varphi(T)\in A
. The infimum of the rate function is
achieved by the path

\varphi(s)=\frac{s}{T}x_{0}\;,\qquad x_{0}=\arginf_{x\in A}\lVert x\rVert\;,

(3.2.8)

and one has indeed
{\mathscr{I}}_{[0,T]}(\varphi)=\frac{1}{2T}\lVert x_{0}\rVert^{2}
. A similar
argument applies to Example
3.2.1
, with
\varphi(s)=\frac{s}{T}h
.

Let us now outline a proof of Schilder’s theorem. Its main ingredient is the
Cameron–Martin–Girsanov formula, which states that any drifted Brownian
motion is equivalent to standard Brownian motion under a change of measure.

{lemma}

[Cameron–Martin–Girsanov formula]
Let
(W_{t})_{t\mathbin{\geqslant}0}
be a standard
1
-dimensional Brownian motion on

(\Omega,{\mathscr{F}}_{t},\mathbb{P})
. Then for any
h\in L^{2}
, the process defined by

\widehat{W}_{t}=W_{t}-\intop\nolimits_{0}^{t}h(s)\dd\!s

(3.2.9)

is a standard Brownian motion on
(\Omega,{\mathscr{F}}_{t},\mathbb{Q})
, where
\mathbb{Q}
is defined via
its Radon–Nikodym derivative

\frac{\dd\!\mathbb{Q}}{\dd\!\mathbb{P}}\Biggr|_{{\mathscr{F}}_{t}}=\exp\Biggl\{\intop\nolimits_{0}^{t}h(s)\dd\!W_{s}-\frac{1}{2}\intop\nolimits_{0}^{t}h(s)^{2}\dd\!s\Biggr\}\;.

(3.2.10)

Sketch of proof:
.

For
h\in L^{2}
and
\gamma>0
, the processes

\displaystyle X_{t}

\displaystyle=\exp\Biggl\{\intop\nolimits_{0}^{t}h(s)\dd\!W_{s}-\frac{1}{2}\intop\nolimits_{0}^{t}h(s)^{2}\dd\!s\Biggr\}\;,

(3.2.11)

\displaystyle Y_{t}

\displaystyle=\exp\Biggl\{\intop\nolimits_{0}^{t}\bigl(\gamma+h(s)\bigr)\dd\!W_{s}-\frac{1}{2}\intop\nolimits_{0}^{t}\bigl(\gamma+h(s)\bigr)^{2}\dd\!s\Biggr\}=X_{t}\exp\Biggl\{\gamma\widehat{W}_{t}-\frac{1}{2}\gamma^{2}t\Biggr\}

(3.2.12)

are exponential martingales (similar to exponential Brownian motion) with
respect to
\mathbb{P}
. This means that
\mathbb{E}\bigl[X_{t}\bigm|{\mathscr{F}}_{s}\bigr]=X_{s}
and

\mathbb{E}\bigl[Y_{t}\bigm|{\mathscr{F}}_{s}\bigr]=Y_{s}
whenever
t>s
.
For any
{\mathscr{F}}_{s}
-measurable random variable
Z
, one has

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}\rule{0.0pt}{9.0pt}}_{\mskip 1.5mu\mathbb{Q}}\Bigl[Z\e^{\gamma(\widehat{W}_{t}-\widehat{W}_{s})}\Bigr]

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}\rule{0.0pt}{9.0pt}}_{\mskip 1.5mu\mathbb{P}}\Bigl[ZX_{t}\e^{\gamma(\widehat{W}_{t}-\widehat{W}_{s})}\Bigr]

(3.2.13)

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}\rule{0.0pt}{9.0pt}}_{\mskip 1.5mu\mathbb{P}}\Bigl[Z\e^{-\gamma\widehat{W}_{s}}Y_{t}\e^{\frac{1}{2}\gamma^{2}t}\Bigr]

(3.2.14)

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}\rule{0.0pt}{9.0pt}}_{\mskip 1.5mu\mathbb{P}}\Bigl[Z\e^{-\gamma\widehat{W}_{s}+\frac{1}{2}\gamma^{2}t}\mathbb{E}_{\mskip 1.5mu\mathbb{P}}\bigl[Y_{t}\bigm|{\mathscr{F}}_{s}\bigr]\Bigr]

(3.2.15)

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}\rule{0.0pt}{9.0pt}}_{\mskip 1.5mu\mathbb{P}}\Bigl[Z\e^{-\gamma\widehat{W}_{s}+\frac{1}{2}\gamma^{2}t}Y_{s}\Bigr]

(3.2.16)

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}\rule{0.0pt}{9.0pt}}_{\mskip 1.5mu\mathbb{P}}\Bigl[ZX_{s}\e^{\frac{1}{2}\gamma^{2}(t-s)}\Bigr]

(3.2.17)

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}\rule{0.0pt}{9.0pt}}_{\mskip 1.5mu\mathbb{Q}}\bigl[Z\bigr]\e^{\frac{1}{2}\gamma^{2}(t-s)}\;,

(3.2.18)

where the third line follows from the tower property of conditional
expectations, and the fourth line from the martingale property.
Since
\widehat{W}_{t}-\widehat{W}_{s}
is
\mathbb{Q}
-independent of
{\mathscr{F}}_{s}
, the
increments if
\widehat{W}_{t}
are independent. The above computation shows that
these increments are Gaussian of variance
t-s
, proving that indeed
\widehat{W}_{t}
is a Brownian motion with respect to the measure
\mathbb{Q}
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
3.2.1
.

Assume for simplicity we are in dimension
n=1
.
We start by proving the lower bound (
3.1.18
). We thus have to
prove that for any open set
O\subset{\mathscr{C}}_{0}
, one has

\liminf_{\varepsilon\to 0}\varepsilon\log\mathbb{P}\bigl\{W^{\varepsilon}\in O\bigr\}\mathbin{\geqslant}-\inf_{\varphi\in O}{\mathscr{I}}_{[0,T]}(\varphi)\;.

(3.2.19)

For this, it is in fact sufficient to prove that

\liminf_{\varepsilon\to 0}\varepsilon\log\mathbb{P}\bigl\{W^{\varepsilon}\in{\mathscr{B}}_{\delta}(\varphi)\bigr\}\mathbin{\geqslant}-{\mathscr{I}}_{[0,T]}(\varphi)

(3.2.20)

holds for all
\varphi\in{\mathscr{C}}_{0}
such that
{\mathscr{I}}_{[0,T]}(\varphi)<\infty
and all

\delta>0
, where
{\mathscr{B}}_{\delta}(\varphi)
denotes the ball of radius
\delta
in the
sup norm, centred in
\varphi
.

If we set
\widehat{W}_{t}=W_{t}-\frac{1}{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}\varphi_{t}
and define
\dd\!\mathbb{Q}

by (
3.2.10
), we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\bigl\{\lVert W^{\varepsilon}-\varphi\rVert_{\infty}<\delta\bigr\}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\biggl\{\lVert\widehat{W}\rVert_{\infty}<\frac{\delta}{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}\biggr\}=\intop\nolimits_{\widehat{W}\in{\mathscr{B}}_{\delta/\sqrt{\varepsilon}}(0)}\dd\!\mathbb{P}

(3.2.21)

\displaystyle=\intop\nolimits_{\widehat{W}\in{\mathscr{B}}_{\delta/\sqrt{\varepsilon}}(0)}\exp\biggl\{-\frac{1}{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}\intop\nolimits_{0}^{T}\dot{\varphi}_{s}\dd\!\widehat{W}_{s}+\frac{1}{2\varepsilon}\intop\nolimits_{0}^{T}\varphi_{s}^{2}\dd\!s\biggr\}\dd\!\mathbb{Q}

(3.2.22)

\displaystyle=\e^{-{\mathscr{I}}_{[0,T]}(\varphi)/\varepsilon}\intop\nolimits_{\widehat{W}\in{\mathscr{B}}_{\delta/\sqrt{\varepsilon}}(0)}\exp\biggl\{-\frac{1}{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}\intop\nolimits_{0}^{T}\dot{\varphi}_{s}\dd\!\widehat{W}_{s}\biggr\}\dd\!\mathbb{Q}\;.

(3.2.23)

Jensen’s inequality yields

\frac{1}{\mathbb{Q}\bigl\{\widehat{W}\in{\mathscr{B}}_{\delta/\sqrt{\varepsilon}}(0)\bigr\}}\intop\nolimits_{\widehat{W}\in{\mathscr{B}}_{\delta/\sqrt{\varepsilon}}(0)}\exp\biggl\{-\frac{1}{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}\intop\nolimits_{0}^{T}\dot{\varphi}_{s}\dd\!\widehat{W}_{s}\biggr\}\dd\!\mathbb{Q}\\
\mathbin{\geqslant}{}\exp\Biggl\{-\frac{1}{\sqrt{\varepsilon}\mathbb{Q}\bigl\{\widehat{W}\in{\mathscr{B}}_{\delta/\sqrt{\varepsilon}}(0)\bigr\}}\intop\nolimits_{\widehat{W}\in{\mathscr{B}}_{\delta/\sqrt{\varepsilon}}(0)}\intop\nolimits_{0}^{T}\dot{\varphi}(s)\dd\!\widehat{W}_{s}\dd\!\mathbb{Q}\Biggr\}\;.

(3.2.24)

It thus follows from the Cameron–Martin–Girsanov formula that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\bigl\{

\displaystyle\lVert W^{\varepsilon}-\varphi\rVert_{\infty}<\delta\bigr\}

(3.2.25)

\displaystyle\mathbin{\geqslant}\e^{-{\mathscr{I}}_{[0,T]}(\varphi)/\varepsilon}\mathbb{P}\bigl\{W\in{\mathscr{B}}_{\delta/\sqrt{\varepsilon}}(0)\bigr\}\exp\Biggl\{-\frac{1}{\sqrt{\varepsilon}\mathbb{P}\bigl\{W\in{\mathscr{B}}_{\delta/\sqrt{\varepsilon}}(0)\bigr\}}\intop\nolimits_{W\in{\mathscr{B}}_{\delta/\sqrt{\varepsilon}}(0)}\intop\nolimits_{0}^{T}\dot{\varphi}(s)\dd\!W_{s}\dd\!\mathbb{P}\Biggr\}

(3.2.26)

\displaystyle=\e^{-{\mathscr{I}}_{[0,T]}(\varphi)/\varepsilon}\mathbb{P}\bigl\{W\in{\mathscr{B}}_{\delta/\sqrt{\varepsilon}}(0)\bigr\}\;.

(3.2.27)

Noting that the probability in the last expression goes to
1
as
\varepsilon

decreases to
0
, we obtain indeed the required lower
bound (
3.2.20
).

Regarding the upper bound (
3.1.17
), we have to show that for
any closed set
C\subset{\mathscr{C}}_{0}
, one has

\limsup_{\varepsilon\to 0}\varepsilon\log\mathbb{P}\bigl\{W^{\varepsilon}\in O\bigr\}\mathbin{\geqslant}-\inf_{\varphi\in C}{\mathscr{I}}_{[0,T]}(\varphi)\;.

(3.2.28)

To do this, given
m\in\mathbb{N}
we introduce a polygonal approximation
W^{m,\varepsilon}

of the Brownian path
W^{\varepsilon}
, joining the
m+1
points
(0,0)
,
(T/m,W^{\varepsilon}_{T/m})
, …,
(T,W^{\varepsilon}_{T})
. For any
\delta>0
, one can find a set

C^{\delta}
whose boundary is at distance
\delta
from the boundary of
C
, and
such that

\mathbb{P}\bigl\{W^{\varepsilon}\in C\bigr\}\mathbin{\leqslant}\mathbb{P}\bigl\{W^{m,\varepsilon}\in C^{\delta}\bigr\}+\mathbb{P}\bigl\{\lVert W^{\varepsilon}-W^{m,\varepsilon}\rVert_{\infty}\mathbin{\geqslant}\delta\bigr\}\;.

(3.2.29)

We first show that the second term on the right-hand side is negligible.
Indeed, by the stationary increments property of Brownian motion, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\bigl\{\lVert W^{\varepsilon}-W^{m,\varepsilon}\rVert_{\infty}\mathbin{\geqslant}\delta\bigr\}

\displaystyle\mathbin{\leqslant}m\mathbb{P}\biggl\{\sup_{0\mathbin{\leqslant}s\mathbin{\leqslant}T/m}\lVert W^{\varepsilon}_{s}-W^{m,\varepsilon}_{s}\rVert_{\infty}\mathbin{\geqslant}\delta\biggr\}

(3.2.30)

\displaystyle\mathbin{\leqslant}m\mathbb{P}\biggl\{\sup_{0\mathbin{\leqslant}s\mathbin{\leqslant}T/m}\lVert W^{\varepsilon}_{s}\rVert\mathbin{\geqslant}\frac{\delta}{2}\biggr\}

(3.2.31)

\displaystyle=m\mathbb{P}\biggl\{\sup_{0\mathbin{\leqslant}s\mathbin{\leqslant}T/m}\lVert W_{s}\rVert\mathbin{\geqslant}\frac{\delta}{2\sqrt{\varepsilon}}\biggr\}

(3.2.32)

\displaystyle\mathbin{\leqslant}2m\e^{-m\delta^{2}/(8\varepsilon T)}\;,

(3.2.33)

where the last bound is a standard bound for Brownian motion, that can be
deduced from Doob’s submartingale inequality. It follows that for any

\delta>0
, one has

\limsup_{m\to\infty}\limsup_{\varepsilon\to 0}\mathbb{P}\bigl\{\lVert W^{\varepsilon}-W^{m,\varepsilon}\rVert_{\infty}\mathbin{\geqslant}\delta\bigr\}=-\infty\;.

(3.2.34)

We now deal with the first term on the right-hand side
of (
3.2.29
). Note that

\mathbb{P}\bigl\{W^{m,\varepsilon}\in C^{\delta}\bigr\}\mathbin{\leqslant}\mathbb{P}\biggl\{{\mathscr{I}}_{[0,T]}(W^{m,\varepsilon})\mathbin{\geqslant}\inf_{\varphi\in C^{\delta}}{\mathscr{I}}_{[0,T]}(\varphi)\biggr\}\;.

(3.2.35)

Since
W^{m,\varepsilon}
is a random polygon, we have

{\mathscr{I}}_{[0,T]}(W^{m,\varepsilon})=\frac{1}{2}\sumop\displaylimits_{k=1}^{m}\frac{T}{m}\Bigl\|\frac{m}{T}\bigl(W^{\varepsilon}_{kT/m}-W^{\varepsilon}_{(k-1)T/m}\bigr)\Bigr\|^{2}=\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\sumop\displaylimits_{k=1}^{m}\xi_{k}^{2}\;,

(3.2.36)

where the
\xi_{k}
are independent, with standard normal distribution.
Therefore, Markov’s inequality yields for any
\gamma<\frac{1}{2}
and

\ell\mathbin{\geqslant}0
the bound

\mathbb{P}\Bigl\{{\mathscr{I}}_{[0,T]}(W^{m,\varepsilon})\mathbin{\geqslant}\ell\Bigr\}\mathbin{\leqslant}\e^{-2\gamma\ell/\varepsilon}\Bigl(\mathbb{E}[\e^{\gamma\xi_{1}^{2}}]\Bigr)^{m}\mathbin{\leqslant}\frac{\e^{-2\gamma\ell/\varepsilon}}{(1-2\gamma)^{m/2}}\;.

(3.2.37)

Since
\gamma<\frac{1}{2}
is arbitrary, it follows that

\limsup_{m\to\infty}\,\limsup_{\varepsilon\to 0}\,\varepsilon\log\mathbb{P}\biggl\{{\mathscr{I}}_{[0,T]}(W^{m,\varepsilon})\mathbin{\geqslant}\inf_{\varphi\in C^{\delta}}{\mathscr{I}}_{[0,T]}(\varphi)\biggr\}\mathbin{\leqslant}-\inf_{\varphi\in C^{\delta}}{\mathscr{I}}_{[0,T]}(\varphi)\;,

(3.2.38)

and the result follows by taking the limit
\delta\searrow 0
.
∎

{remark}

The proof we have given here is essentially the one found
in
[
FW98
, Chapter 3, Theorem 2.2]
. There exist other proofs, however, for
instance a proof based on the Wiener isometry can be found
in
[
HW15
]
.

3.2.2
The Freidlin–Wentzell large-deviation principle

We consider from now on SDEs of the form

\dd\!X^{\varepsilon}_{t}=f(X^{\varepsilon}_{t})\dd\!t+\sqrt{\varepsilon}g(X^{\varepsilon}_{t})\dd\!W_{t}\;,

(3.2.39)

where
W_{t}
denotes
k
-dimensional Brownian motion,
X^{\varepsilon}_{t}
is

n
-dimensional,
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a drift coefficient, and

g:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n\times k}
is a matrix-valued diffusion coefficient. We assume
that
f
and
g
satisfy the Lipshitz and bounded-growth conditions seen in
Section
1.2.3
. Assume furthermore an ellipticity condition on the
diffusion matrix
D(x)=g(x)g(x)^{T}
, requiring that

\langle\xi,D(x)\xi\rangle\mathbin{\geqslant}c\lVert\xi^{2}\rVert\qquad\forall\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{d}

(3.2.40)

holds for some
c>0
. Note that this is only possible if
k\mathbin{\geqslant}n
, since
otherwise,
D(x)
cannot have full rank.

Recall that the solution of (
3.2.39
) with initial condition

X^{\varepsilon}_{0}=x_{0}
is defined as the fixed point of the integral equation

X^{\varepsilon}_{t}=x_{0}+\intop\nolimits_{0}^{t}f(X^{\varepsilon}_{s})\dd\!s+\intop\nolimits_{0}^{t}g(X^{\varepsilon}_{s})\dd\!W^{\varepsilon}_{s}\;,

(3.2.41)

where
W^{\varepsilon}_{s}=\sqrt{\varepsilon}W_{s}
denotes scaled Brownian motion. We view the
right-hand side as a map
\Psi
from realisations of

(W^{\varepsilon}_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
to sample paths
(X^{\varepsilon}_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
.

Consider first the case where
g
is the identity matrix. Then the contraction
principle, cf. Lemma
3.1.3
, suggests that
X^{\varepsilon}

satisfies a large-deviation principle with rate function

\displaystyle{\mathscr{J}}_{[0,T]}(\varphi)

\displaystyle=\inf\bigl\{{\mathscr{I}}_{[0,T]}(\psi)\colon\Psi(\psi)=\varphi\bigr\}

(3.2.42)

\displaystyle=\inf\Biggl\{\frac{1}{2}\intop\nolimits_{0}^{T}\lVert\dot{\psi}(s)\rVert^{2}\dd\!s\colon x_{0}+\intop\nolimits_{0}^{t}f(\varphi(s))\dd\!s+\intop\nolimits_{0}^{t}\dot{\psi}(s)\dd\!s=\varphi(t)\quad\forall t\in[0,T]\Biggr\}\;.

(3.2.43)

Taking the time-derivative of the condition suggests that one should take

\dot{\psi}(t)=\dot{\varphi}(t)-f(\varphi(t))
, yielding

{\mathscr{J}}_{[0,T]}(\varphi)=\frac{1}{2}\intop\nolimits_{0}^{T}\lVert\dot{\varphi}(s)-f(\varphi(s))\rVert^{2}\dd\!s\;.

(3.2.44)

Consider next the case where
f=0
, while
g
is a general matrix satisfying the
ellipticity condition (
3.2.40
). Then (
3.2.41
)
suggests choosing
\psi
in such a way that
g(\varphi)\dot{\psi}=\dot{\varphi}
.
However, since
g
is not a square matrix in general, some care is required
when solving the variational problem.

{lemma}

The minimum of
\lVert\dot{\psi}\rVert^{2}
under the constraint
g(\varphi)\dot{\psi}=\dot{\varphi}
is achieved for
\dot{\psi}=g(\varphi)^{T}D(\varphi)^{-1}\dot{\varphi}
, and has the
value
\dot{\varphi}^{T}D^{-1}\dot{\varphi}=\langle\dot{\varphi},D^{-1}\dot{\varphi}\rangle
.

Proof:
.

The problem amounts to minimising
p(y)=\lVert y\rVert^{2}
over
y\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
under the
constraint
q(y)=g(\varphi)y-\dot{\varphi}=0
. By the Lagrange multiplier theorem,
there exists a vector
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that

2y_{j}=\frac{\partial p}{\partial y_{j}}(y)=\sumop\displaylimits_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}\frac{\partial q_{i}}{\partial y_{j}}(y)=\sumop\displaylimits_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}g_{ij}(\varphi)\;,\qquad j\in\{1,\dots,k\}\;.

(3.2.45)

This amounts to setting
2y=g(\varphi)^{T}\lambda
, and the constraint
q(y)=0

yields
g(\varphi)g(\varphi)^{T}\lambda=2\dot{\varphi}
, or
\lambda=2D(\varphi)^{-1}\dot{\varphi}
.
Therefore,
y=g(\varphi)^{T}D(\varphi)^{-1}\dot{\varphi}
, and taking the norm gives the
result.
∎

It follows that for
f=0
, one has

{\mathscr{J}}_{[0,T]}(\varphi)=\frac{1}{2}\intop\nolimits_{0}^{T}\langle\dot{\varphi}(s),D(\varphi(s))^{-1}\dot{\varphi}(s)\rangle\dd\!s\;.

(3.2.46)

Combining these two special cases yields the following result. Its proof is
somewhat more complicated than a direct application of the contraction
principle, because of the continuity properties that need to be verified.
However, one can work with Euler approximations of the solutions, obtained by a
time-discretisation.

{theorem}

[Large-deviation principle for SDEs]

The processes
(X^{\varepsilon}_{t})_{\varepsilon>0}
satisfy a large-deviation principle with
rate function

{\mathscr{J}}_{[0,T]}(\varphi)=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\intop\nolimits_{0}^{T}\langle\dot{\varphi}(s)-f(\varphi(s)),D(\dot{\varphi})^{-1}\bigl(\dot{\varphi}(s)-f(\varphi(s))\bigr)\rangle\dd\!s&\text{if $\varphi\in H^{1}$ and $\varphi(0)=x_{0}$\;,}\\[14.0pt]
+\infty&\text{otherwise\;.}\end{cases}

(3.2.47)

{remark}

If
D(x)
is only positive semi-definite, the large-deviation principle remains
valid with

{\mathscr{J}}_{[0,T]}(\varphi)=\inf\Biggl\{{\mathscr{I}}(\varphi)\colon\varphi\in H^{1},\varphi(t)=x_{0}+\intop\nolimits_{0}^{t}f(\varphi(s))\dd\!s+\intop\nolimits_{0}^{t}D(\varphi(s))^{1/2}\dot{\varphi}(s)\dd\!s\quad\forall t\in[0,T]\Biggr\}\;.

(3.2.48)

{example}

[Interpretation of the rate function]
Fix a continuous path
\varphi_{0}:[0,T]\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and define for

Taking the limit

Let us also mention Varadhan’s lemma, which applies to general random variables
satisfying a large-deviation principle. The lemma allows to obtain results on
“tilted” processes, in which unlikely outcomes are made likely by
changing the probability measure.

{theorem}

[Varadhan’s lemma]

denote the set of continuous functions

be a continuous map. Assume the tail condition

(3.2.52)

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\varepsilon\log\intop\nolimits\e^{\phi(X^{\varepsilon})/\varepsilon}\dd\!\mathbb{P}=\sup_{\varphi\in{\mathscr{C}}}\Bigl[\phi(\varphi)-{\mathscr{J}}_{[0,T]}(\varphi)\Bigr]\;.

(3.2.53)

3.3
Application to the stochastic exit problem

One of the many applications of the large-deviation principle for SDEs is the
stochastic exit problem, that we have already encountered in
Section
1.3
. In particular, the large-deviation principle gives
information on the probability to leave a given set in finite time, on the
expected time required to leave the set, and on the most probable path the
process takes to do so. The approach is in fact quite similar to the geometric
optics limit of the wave equation, where waves are replaced by light rays moving
in straight lines. It can also be viewed as a version of Richard Feynman’s
path-integral approach to quantum mechanics.

3.3.1
The Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process

As a relatively simple warm-up example, let us consider an Ornstein–Uhlenbeck

3.3.2
Lagrangian and Hamiltonian formulations

The minimisation problems we encountered for Brownian motion and for the
Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process are in fact well known in analytical mechanics.
Indeed, the rate function is a Lagrangian action, of the form

{\mathscr{J}}_{[0,T]}(\varphi)=\intop\nolimits_{0}^{T}L(\varphi(s),\dot{\varphi}(s))\dd\!s\;,

(3.3.13)

where
L
is a Lagrangian given by

L(\varphi,\dot{\varphi})=\frac{1}{2}\langle\dot{\varphi}-f(\varphi),D(\dot{\varphi})^{-1}\bigl(\dot{\varphi}-f(\varphi)\bigr)\rangle\;.

(3.3.14)

Let us disregard for a moment the question of boundary conditions. The
stationary points of the action are known to satisfy the Euler–Lagrange
equations

\frac{\dd\!}{\dd\!t}\biggl(\frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot{\varphi}_{i}}\biggr)=\frac{\partial L}{\partial\varphi_{i}}\;,\qquad i=1,\dots,n\;.

(3.3.15)

{exercise}

Write the Euler–Lagrange equations corresponding to Brownian motion, and
those corresponding to the Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process. Check that their
solutions are indeed of the form discussed in the previous subsections.

It is often more convenient to use the Hamiltonian formalism. This is done by
introducing the conjugate momenta

\psi_{i}=\frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot{\varphi}_{i}}

(3.3.16)

and the Hamiltonian

H(\varphi,\psi)=\sumop\displaylimits_{i=1}^{n}\psi_{i}\dot{\varphi}_{i}-L(\varphi,\dot{\varphi})\;,

(3.3.17)

where
\dot{\varphi}
has to be expressed in terms of the
\psi_{i}
on the right-hand
side. The stationary points of the action are then solutions of the Hamilton
equations

\frac{\dd\!\varphi_{i}}{\dd\!t}=\frac{\partial H}{\partial\psi_{i}}\;,\qquad\frac{\dd\!\psi_{i}}{\dd\!t}=-\frac{\partial H}{\partial\varphi_{i}}\;.

(3.3.18)

For the Lagrangian (
3.3.14
), the Hamiltonian reads

H(\varphi,\psi)=\frac{1}{2}\langle\psi,D(\varphi)\psi\rangle+\langle\psi,f(\varphi)\rangle\;,

(3.3.19)

and the rate function takes the value

{\mathscr{J}}_{[0,T]}(\varphi,\psi)=\frac{1}{2}\intop\nolimits_{0}^{T}\langle\psi(s),D(\varphi(s))\psi(s)\rangle\dd\!s\;.

(3.3.20)

One should note that while the first term in the
Hamiltonian (
3.3.19
) can be interpreted as a kinetic energy,
the second term plays a different role from the usual potential energy in a
particle system.

The Hamilton equations (
3.3.18
) take the form

\frac{\dd\!\varphi}{\dd\!t}=D(\varphi)\psi+f(\varphi)\;,\qquad\frac{\dd\!\psi}{\dd\!t}=\frac{1}{2}\nabla_{\varphi}\langle\psi,D(\varphi)\psi\rangle-\nabla_{\varphi}\langle\psi,f(\varphi)\rangle\;.

(3.3.21)
