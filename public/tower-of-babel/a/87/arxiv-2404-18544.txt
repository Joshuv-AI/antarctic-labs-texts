Equivariant quantizations of the positive nilradical and covariant differential calculi
Marco Matassa

Abstract
. Consider a decomposition 𝔫 = 𝔫 1 ⊕ ⋯ ⊕ 𝔫 r \mathfrak{n}=\mathfrak{n}_{1}\oplus\cdots\oplus\mathfrak{n}_{r} of the positive nilradical of a complex semisimple Lie algebra of rank r r , where each 𝔫 k \mathfrak{n}_{k} is a module under an appropriate Levi factor. We show that this can be quantized as a finite-dimensional subspace 𝔫 k q = 𝔫 1 q ⊕ ⋯ ⊕ 𝔫 r q \mathfrak{n}^{q}_{k}=\mathfrak{n}^{q}_{1}\oplus\cdots\oplus\mathfrak{n}^{q}_{r} of the positive part of the quantized enveloping algebra, where each 𝔫 k q \mathfrak{n}^{q}_{k} is a module under the left adjoint action of a quantized Levi factor. Furthermore, we show that ℂ ⊕ 𝔫 q \mathbb{C}\oplus\mathfrak{n}^{q} is a left coideal, with the possible exception of components corresponding to some exceptional Lie algebras. Finally we use these quantizations to construct covariant first-order differential calculi on quantum flag manifolds, compatible in a certain sense with the decomposition above, which coincide with those introduced by Heckenberger-Kolb in the irreducible case.



1.
Introduction

1.1.
Background

Any Lie subalgebra
\mathfrak{k}\subset\mathfrak{g}
gives rise to a Hopf subalgebra
U(\mathfrak{k})\subset U(\mathfrak{g})
at the level of universal enveloping algebras. Conversely, any Hopf subalgebra of
U(\mathfrak{g})
is of this form by the Milnor-Moore theorem (in characteristic zero).
The situation is drastically different for the
quantized enveloping algebra

U_{q}(\mathfrak{g})
, which is a Hopf algebra deformation of
U(\mathfrak{g})
, where
\mathfrak{g}
is a complex semisimple Lie algebra.
In this case it is well-known that
U_{q}(\mathfrak{g})
admits fewer Hopf subalgebras than in the classical case, essentially only those corresponding to Dynkin subdiagrams.
Furthermore, even when a Hopf subalgebra
U_{q}(\mathfrak{k})\subset U_{q}(\mathfrak{g})
is available, there is no obvious counterpart for the underlying Lie subalgebra
\mathfrak{k}\subset\mathfrak{g}
.

The general philosophy to overcome this problem is that Hopf subalgebras should be replaced by
coideal subalgebras
, either one-sided or two-sided. These reduce to Hopf subalgebras in the case of
U(\mathfrak{g})
, due to cocommutativity, but are more general in the case of
U_{q}(\mathfrak{g})
.
For example, this point of view has been used to develop the theory of quantum symmetric spaces, culminating with the results of Letzter
[
Let99
,
Let02
]
, in which she constructs a family of one-sided coideals subalgebras
U_{q}(\mathfrak{g}^{\theta})
reducing in the classical limit to
U(\mathfrak{g}^{\theta})
, where
\mathfrak{g}^{\theta}
is the fixed-point subalgebra with respect to a maximally split involution
\theta
.
In a related direction, Heckenberger and Schneider
[
HeSc13
]
classify the right coideals subalgebras of the Borel part of
U_{q}(\mathfrak{g})
containing the Cartan part (we review this and related results later in the introduction).
The situation is less clear for a quantum analogue of a Lie subalgebra within
U_{q}(\mathfrak{g})
.
Despite various definitions having been proposed over the years, such as
[
Maj94
]
,
[
DeGo97
]
and
[
LySu98
]
, there is still no universally agreed-upon candidate.

In this paper we consider the problem of quantizing the positive nilradical
\mathfrak{n}
of a complex semisimple Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
of rank
r
, together with a fixed decomposition
\mathfrak{n}=\mathfrak{n}_{1}\oplus\cdots\oplus\mathfrak{n}_{r}
which we are going to discuss shortly. The goal is to produce a
finite-dimensional
subspace
\mathfrak{n}^{q}=\mathfrak{n}^{q}_{1}\oplus\cdots\oplus\mathfrak{n}^{q}_{r}
of
U_{q}(\mathfrak{n})
, the positive part of the quantized enveloping algebra, in such a way that
\mathbb{C}\oplus\mathfrak{n}^{q}
should be a coideal (we discuss the precise formulation later).
This should be an
equivariant
quantization, in the sense that each summand
\mathfrak{n}^{q}_{k}
should be a module under the left adjoint action of an appropriate quantized Levi factor, in parallel with the classical situation.
Similarly, the requirements on
\mathfrak{n}^{q}
being finite-dimensional and a coideal should be considered as replacement for the existence of a Lie algebra structure on
\mathfrak{n}
.

One important motivation for this construction comes from the theory of covariant differential calculi on quantum groups and homogeneous spaces, introduced by Woronowicz in
[
Wor89
]
.
As we are going to show, each quantization
\mathfrak{n}^{q}
gives rise to covariant (first-order) differential calculi on a class of quantum flag manifolds.
Furthermore, this assignment is compatible with the decomposition of
\mathfrak{n}^{q}
, in an appropriate sense to be discussed later.

1.2.
Setting

The classical setting we want to consider is as follows.
Fix a set
\Pi=\{\alpha_{1},\cdots,\alpha_{r}\}
of simple roots of
\mathfrak{g}
.
Then there is a one-to-one correspondence

\alpha_{i_{1}}<\cdots<\alpha_{i_{r}}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad S_{1}\subset\cdots\subset S_{r}

between total orders on the simple roots and increasing sequences of proper subsets of
\Pi
.
The increasing sequence is determined by removing the simple roots in the given order, while the total order is determined by the removal of the simple roots from the sequence.
More precisely, we have
S_{k}=S_{k+1}\backslash\{\alpha_{i_{k}}\}
for
k\in\{1,\cdots,r\}
and
S_{r+1}=\Pi
.
Denote by
\mathfrak{l}_{k}
the Levi factor corresponding to the subset
S_{k}\subset\Pi
as above, for any
k\in\{1,\cdots,r\}
.
Corresponding to any such choice, we have a decomposition of the positive nilradical
\mathfrak{n}\subset\mathfrak{g}
of the form

\mathfrak{n}=\mathfrak{n}_{1}\oplus\cdots\oplus\mathfrak{n}_{r}.

Here each summand
\mathfrak{n}_{k}
arises as the positive nilradical of a parabolic subalgebra corresponding to the inclusion
S_{k}\subset S_{k+1}
(for more details on this, see
Section

2.2
).
Furthermore, each summand
\mathfrak{n}_{k}
is a
\mathfrak{l}_{k}
-module and is
\mathbb{N}
-graded by the simple root
\alpha_{i_{k}}
.

On the quantum side, we consider the quantized enveloping algebra
U_{q}(\mathfrak{g})
, which is a Hopf algebra with generators
\{K_{i}^{\pm 1},E_{i},F_{i}\}_{i=1}^{r}
and suitable relations.
We write
U_{q}(\mathfrak{n})
for the positive part with generators
\{E_{i}\}_{i=1}^{r}
, while we write
U_{q}(\mathfrak{b})
for the non-negative (or Borel) part with generators
\{K_{i}^{\pm 1},E_{i}\}_{i=1}^{r}
.
We note that the latter is a Hopf subalgebra, while the former is not.
We can also find Hopf subalgebras
U_{q}(\mathfrak{l}_{k})\subset U_{q}(\mathfrak{g})
corresponding to each Levi factor
\mathfrak{l}_{k}
as above (since these arise from Dynkin subdiagrams of
\mathfrak{g}
).
Finally, writing
\triangleright
for the left adjoint action of
U_{q}(\mathfrak{g})
on itself, we consider the following problem.

Problem 1.1
.

Let
\mathfrak{g}
be a complex semisimple Lie algebra of rank
r
.
For any increasing sequence
S_{1}\subset\cdots\subset S_{r}
as above, find a finite-dimensional subspace

\mathfrak{n}^{q}=\mathfrak{n}^{q}_{1}\oplus\cdots\oplus\mathfrak{n}^{q}_{r}\subset U_{q}(\mathfrak{n})

such that each summand
\mathfrak{n}^{q}_{k}
satisfies the following properties:

•

\mathfrak{n}^{q}_{k}
is an
U_{q}(\mathfrak{l}_{k})
-module under
\triangleright
, corresponding to the
\mathfrak{l}_{k}
-module
\mathfrak{n}_{k}
,

•

each graded component
\mathfrak{n}^{q}_{k,n}
is contained in the
n
-fold product of
\mathfrak{n}^{q}_{k,1}
.

The first condition simply says that each
\mathfrak{n}^{q}_{k}
gives a quantization of
\mathfrak{n}_{k}
with its action by the Levi factor
\mathfrak{l}_{k}
.
On the other hand, the second condition should be considered as a weak replacement for the Lie algebra structure on
\mathfrak{n}_{k}
(this condition is going to be refined shortly).
We refer to any
\mathfrak{n}^{q}\subset U_{q}(\mathfrak{n})
as above as an
equivariant quantization
of
\mathfrak{n}
(with its fixed decomposition).
We observe that one could formulate a related problem by replacing
U_{q}(\mathfrak{n})
with
U_{q}(\mathfrak{b})
, the non-negative part of
U_{q}(\mathfrak{g})
quantizing the Borel subalgebra
\mathfrak{b}
.

As we are going to discuss, this problem is not too difficult to solve using general results on the representation theory of
U_{q}(\mathfrak{g})
.
On the other hand, not all such equivariant quantizations correspond to
\mathfrak{n}
upon specialization to the classical case.
To avoid this issue and to remedy the lack of uniqueness, we should impose further conditions on
\mathfrak{n}^{q}
.

We note that the classical property
\Delta(\mathfrak{n})\subset\mathfrak{n}\otimes 1+1\otimes\mathfrak{n}
inside
U(\mathfrak{n})
can be rephrased as saying that
\mathbb{C}\oplus\mathfrak{n}
is a left (and right)
U(\mathfrak{n})
-coideal.
Similarly, we require that
\mathbb{C}\oplus\mathfrak{n}^{q}
should be a coideal of some sorts. The precise problem we consider is as follows.

Problem 1.2
.

With notation as above, find an equivariant quantization
\mathfrak{n}^{q}=\mathfrak{n}^{q}_{1}\oplus\cdots\oplus\mathfrak{n}^{q}_{r}
such that each subspace
\mathbb{C}\oplus\mathfrak{n}^{q}_{k}
is a left
U_{q}(\mathfrak{b})
-coideal, that is

\Delta(\mathbb{C}\oplus\mathfrak{n}^{q}_{k})\subset U_{q}(\mathfrak{b})\otimes(\mathbb{C}\oplus\mathfrak{n}^{q}_{k}),\quad k\in\{1,\cdots,r\}.

We note that here the coideal condition is formulated in terms of
U_{q}(\mathfrak{b})
instead of
U_{q}(\mathfrak{n})
, in other words we must also include the Cartan part.
This is related to the fact that
U_{q}(\mathfrak{n})
is not a Hopf subalgebra, as mentioned above. More concretely, for its generators
E_{i}
we have the coproduct
\Delta(E_{i})=E_{i}\otimes 1+K_{i}\otimes E_{i}
(in our conventions).
This also motivates the choice of working with left coideals as opposed to right coideals.

Finally we describe a related setting that motivates the results of this paper.
Corresponding to any sequence
S_{1}\subset\cdots\subset S_{r}
, we have the generalized flag manifolds

G/P_{S_{1}}\to\cdots\to G/P_{S_{r}},

where the arrows denote appropriate quotient maps. We note that
G/P_{S_{1}}
corresponds to the full flag manifold
G/B
. This situation can quantized to give

\mathbb{C}_{q}[G/P_{S_{r}}]\subset\cdots\subset\mathbb{C}_{q}[G/P_{S_{1}}],

where each
quantum flag manifold

\mathbb{C}_{q}[G/P_{S_{k}}]
is defined as the subalgebra of the quantized coordinate ring
\mathbb{C}_{q}[G]
invariant under the quantized Levi factor
U_{q}(\mathfrak{l}_{k})
.

We would like to construct covariant (first-order) differential calculi over quantum homogeneous spaces of this type.
We note that covariance here refers to a quantum version of a group
G
acting on the differential forms of a homogeneous space
G/K
.
The results of
[
HeKo03
]
give a characterization of covariant first-order differential calculi over a certain class of quantum homogeneous spaces, which includes the quantum flag manifolds as above.
Such
n
-dimensional calculi over
\mathcal{B}
correspond to certain
(n+1)
-subspaces of
\mathcal{B}^{\circ}
, where the latter denotes the dual coalgebra.
As we are going to show, each equivariant quantization
\mathfrak{n}^{q}
has exactly the right properties to produce such first-order differential calculi.

1.3.
Results

Problem

1.1
can be solved by making use of some well-known facts on the representation theory of
U_{q}(\mathfrak{g})
, which lead to the following result.

Proposition 1.3
.

Let
\mathfrak{g}
be a complex semisimple Lie algebra of rank
r
.
Let
S_{1}\subset\cdots\subset S_{r}
be an increasing sequence of proper subsets of
\Pi
, with
\mathfrak{n}=\mathfrak{n}_{1}\oplus\cdots\oplus\mathfrak{n}_{r}
the corresponding decomposition of the positive nilradical.

Then there exists a finite-dimensional subspace

\mathfrak{n}^{q}=\mathfrak{n}^{q}_{1}\oplus\cdots\oplus\mathfrak{n}^{q}_{r}\subset U_{q}(\mathfrak{n})

such that each summand
\mathfrak{n}^{q}_{k}
satisfies the following properties:

•

\mathfrak{n}^{q}_{k}
is an
U_{q}(\mathfrak{l}_{k})
-module under
\triangleright
, corresponding to the
\mathfrak{l}_{k}
-module
\mathfrak{n}_{k}
,

•

each graded component
\mathfrak{n}^{q}_{k,n}
is contained in the
n
-fold product of
\mathfrak{n}^{q}_{k,1}
.

Furthermore, each summand
\mathfrak{n}^{q}_{k}
can be realized as a subspace of a twisted quantum Schubert cell as in
[
Zwi09
]
, which is a modification of the quantum Schubert cell introduced by De Concini, Kac and Procesi in
[
DCKP95
]
.
This implies that each degree-one component
\mathfrak{n}^{q}_{k,1}
consists of quantum root vectors (for some appropriate reduced decomposition).

The equivariant quantization
\mathfrak{n}^{q}=\mathfrak{n}^{q}_{1}\oplus\cdots\oplus\mathfrak{n}^{q}_{r}
from
Proposition

1.3
is not uniquely determined, in general. However this is notably the case when all the simple components are of classical type, that is corresponding to the series
A_{r}
,
B_{r}
,
C_{r}
and
D_{r}
.
Therefore extra conditions need to be imposed to obtain uniqueness and properly recover the positive nilradical
\mathfrak{n}
in the classical limit, as we are going to do now.

In
Problem

1.2
we impose the additional condition that
\mathbb{C}\oplus\mathfrak{n}^{q}
should be a a coideal. This problem appears to be harder to solve, and we are not able to do so in full generality here.
However we can solve it by making an additional assumption on
\mathfrak{g}
, namely that certain exceptional Lie algebras do not appear within its simple components.

Theorem 1.4
.

Let
\mathfrak{g}
be a complex semisimple Lie algebra of rank
r
, which does not contain components of type
F_{4}
,
E_{7}
,
E_{8}
.
Let
S_{1}\subset\cdots\subset S_{r}
be an increasing sequence of proper subsets of
\Pi
, with
\mathfrak{n}=\mathfrak{n}_{1}\oplus\cdots\oplus\mathfrak{n}_{r}
the corresponding decomposition of the positive nilradical.

Then there exists a unique finite-dimensional subspace

\mathfrak{n}^{q}=\mathfrak{n}^{q}_{1}\oplus\cdots\oplus\mathfrak{n}^{q}_{r}\subset U_{q}(\mathfrak{n})

such that each summand
\mathfrak{n}^{q}_{k}
satisfies the following properties:

•

\mathfrak{n}^{q}_{k}
is an
U_{q}(\mathfrak{l}_{k})
-module under
\triangleright
, corresponding to the
\mathfrak{l}_{k}
-module
\mathfrak{n}_{k}
,

•

each graded component
\mathfrak{n}^{q}_{k,n}
is contained in the
n
-fold product of
\mathfrak{n}^{q}_{k,1}
,

•

\mathbb{C}\oplus\mathfrak{n}^{q}_{k}
is a left
U_{q}(\mathfrak{b})
-coideal, that is
\Delta(\mathbb{C}\oplus\mathfrak{n}^{q}_{k})\subset U_{q}(\mathfrak{b})\otimes(\mathbb{C}\oplus\mathfrak{n}^{q}_{k})
.

The result we prove is slightly stronger than stated, namely we can quantize any graded component
\mathfrak{n}^{q}_{k,n}
up to
n=3
as left a
U_{q}(\mathfrak{b})
-coideal, including in the cases
F_{4},E_{7}
and
E_{8}
.
Hence it remains to check that this holds for components of higher degrees, which becomes more challenging.
We note that the most complicated case, corresponding to
E_{8}
, would require this verification up to degree six (which is the largest coefficient of a simple root in its highest root).
This provides some further evidence for the following conjecture.

Conjecture 1.5
.

The results of
Theorem

1.4
hold for any finite-dimensional complex semisimple
\mathfrak{g}
, without any restriction on the simple components.

Next we discuss, given an equivariant quantization
\mathfrak{n}^{q}
as above, how to construct covariant first-order differential calculi on quantum flag manifolds.
As previously mentioned, the results of
[
HeKo03
]
give a way to produce such finite-dimensional calculi over a quantum homogeneous space in terms of certain subspaces of its dual coalgebra.
Consider the case of a quantum flag manifold
\mathbb{C}_{q}[G/P_{S_{k}}]
, defined by invariance under the Hopf subalgebra
U_{q}(\mathfrak{l}_{k})
.
Then we obtain a differential calculus by producing a finite-dimensional subspace
T\subset U_{q}(\mathfrak{g})
which contains the unit, is a right
U_{q}(\mathfrak{g})
-coideal, and is invariant under the left adjoint action of
U_{q}(\mathfrak{l}_{k})
.

These are exactly the properties satisfied by
\mathbb{C}\oplus\mathfrak{n}^{q}
, except that it is a left coideal.
We can easily pass to right coideals by using the quantum analogue of the Chevalley involution, which is a Hopf algebra isomorphism
\omega:U_{q}(\mathfrak{g})\to U_{q}(\mathfrak{g})^{\mathrm{cop}}
, where the latter denotes
U_{q}(\mathfrak{g})
with opposite coproduct.
Furthermore we consider
U_{q}(\mathfrak{g})
as a Hopf
*
-algebra, with
*
-structure corresponding to the compact real form.
Then we have the following result.

Theorem 1.6
.

Under the same assumptions of
Theorem

1.4
, consider the unique equivariant quantization
\mathfrak{n}^{q}=\mathfrak{n}^{q}_{1}\oplus\cdots\oplus\mathfrak{n}^{q}_{r}
corresponding to
S_{1}\subset\cdots\subset S_{r}
. Write

\mathcal{T}_{-}=\mathbb{C}\oplus\omega(\mathfrak{n}^{q}),\quad\mathcal{T}_{+}=\mathbb{C}\oplus\omega(\mathfrak{n}^{q})^{*}.

Then we have the following:

(1)

\mathcal{T}_{-}
and
\mathcal{T}_{+}
give left-covariant first-order differential calculi over the quantum group
\mathbb{C}_{q}[G]
and any quantum flag manifold
\mathbb{C}_{q}[G/P_{S_{k}}]
with
k\in\{1,\cdots,r\}
,

(2)

if the quantum flag manifold
\mathbb{C}_{q}[G/P_{S_{k}}]
is irreducible, these calculi coincide with those introduced by Heckenberger and Kolb in
[
HeKo06
]
.

Here
\mathcal{T}_{-}
and
\mathcal{T}_{+}
should be thought of as corresponding to the holomorphic and anti-holomorphic parts of a differential calculus, since all flag manifolds are complex manifolds.
We recall that, in the case of a quantum
irreducible
flag manifold
\mathbb{C}_{q}[G/P_{S}]
, Heckenberger and Kolb construct in
[
HeKo06
]
a canonical differential calculus that shares many features of its classical counterpart, including this type of decomposition.
Here canonical refers to the fact there exist only two irreducible left-covariant first-order calculi on
\mathbb{C}_{q}[G/P_{S}]
, corresponding to the holomorphic and anti-holomorphic parts as above, and one considers their direct sum.
This should be contrasted with the general situation of a quantum homogeneous space, where the choice of a differential calculus appears to be highly non-canonical.
For the quantum full flag manifolds of type
A
, generalizations of the Heckenberger-Kolb calculi were recently constructed in
[
ÓBSo23
]
(where the the extension to higher-order forms is also studied).

We conclude this part with some remarks on covariant differential calculi on quantum groups and quantum homogeneous spaces, inspired by
Theorem

1.6
.
This result suggests that the choice of a differential calculus on
\mathbb{C}_{q}[G]
should be
adapted
to a sequence

\mathbb{C}_{q}[G/P_{S_{r}}]\subset\cdots\subset\mathbb{C}_{q}[G/P_{S_{1}}]

of quantum flag manifolds, corresponding to the increasing sequence
S_{1}\subset\cdots\subset S_{r}
of
\Pi
.
This in general will not be enough to single out a unique candidate for a differential calculus, since it should be possible to twist the previous construction by Cartan elements.
This would correspond to considering
Problem

1.1
and
Problem

1.2
within the Borel part
U_{q}(\mathfrak{b})
, as opposed to the positive part
U_{q}(\mathfrak{n})
.
However this difference should disappear at the level of quantum flag manifolds, since each quantized Levi factor
U_{q}(\mathfrak{l}_{k})
contains the Cartan part.
We expect that this could be leveraged to gain uniqueness in this setting.

1.4.
Existing literature

We now come back to the first part of the introduction, by giving a quick overview of some related results existing in the literature.

The problem of classifying (right) coideal
subalgebras
of
U_{q}(\mathfrak{b})
containing
U_{q}(\mathfrak{h})
has been studied extensively. This was first solved for
A_{r}
in
[
KhSa08
]
, then later on for
B_{r}
in
[
Kha11
]
and for
G_{2}
in
[
Pog09
]
, before a general classification was obtained by Heckenberger and Schneider in
[
HeSc13
]
.
The result of their classification is that such coideals are in one-to-one correspondence with elements of the Weyl group
W
by the map
w\mapsto U_{q}(w)U_{q}(\mathfrak{h})
, where
U_{q}(w)
denotes the quantum Schubert cell corresponding to
w
as above and
U_{q}(\mathfrak{h})
is the Cartan part.
Furthermore this map is order-preserving, where
W
is considered with the Duflo order and the coideal subalgebras are ordered by inclusion.
We should also mention
[
HeKo11
]
, where coideals of
U_{q}(\mathfrak{b})
satisfying a more general condition are also classified.

Comparing the results of our paper to those cited above, we see two main differences: 1) we are concerned with finite-dimensional coideals as opposed to coideal subalgebras, 2) we consider such coideals together with the action of appropriate quantized Levi factors.

Another result that should be mentioned in this context is the determination of the locally-finite part of
U_{q}(\mathfrak{g})
under the left adjoint action, due to Joseph and Letzter
[
JoLe92
]
.
They show that it consists of finite-dimensional blocks
B(\lambda)
, for each dominant weight
\lambda
, which are invariant under the left adjoint action of
U_{q}(\mathfrak{g})
.
Each block
B(\lambda)
is a left
U_{q}(\mathfrak{g})
-coideal but is not irreducible in general (it is isomorphic to
V(\lambda)^{*}\otimes V(\lambda)
).
It appears to be non-trivial to use the results of
[
JoLe92
]
for the problems considered in our paper, since we are interested in the locally-finite part of
U_{q}(\mathfrak{n})
under
U_{q}(\mathfrak{l}_{S})
, for various choices of
S\subset\Pi
.

2.
Classical preliminaries

In this section we recall some known results on parabolic subalgebras and decompositions in the Weyl group.
We also discuss in detail how to obtain the decomposition
\mathfrak{n}_{1}\oplus\cdots\oplus\mathfrak{n}_{r}
corresponding to
S_{1}\subset\cdots\subset S_{r}
mentioned in the introduction.

2.1.
Parabolic subalgebras

Let
\mathfrak{g}
be a complex semisimple Lie algebra.
A choice of Cartan subalgebra
\mathfrak{h}\subset\mathfrak{g}
determines a root system
\Phi\subseteq\mathfrak{h}^{*}
.
This can be decomposed into positive and negative roots as
\Phi=\Phi^{+}\sqcup\Phi^{-}
.
For any root
\alpha\in\Phi
we denote by
\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha}\subset\mathfrak{g}
the corresponding root space.
Write
\Pi=\{\alpha_{1},\cdots,\alpha_{r}\}
for the set of simple roots, so that
\mathfrak{g}
has rank
r
.

Any subset
S\subseteq\Pi
determines a
parabolic subalgebra

\mathfrak{p}_{S}
as follows. First set

\Phi(\mathfrak{l}_{S}):=\mathrm{span}(S)\cap\Phi,\quad\Phi(\mathfrak{n}_{S}):=\Phi^{+}\backslash\Phi(\mathfrak{l}_{S}).

In terms of these roots we define

\mathfrak{l}_{S}:=\mathfrak{h}\oplus\bigoplus_{\alpha\in\Phi(\mathfrak{l}_{S})}\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha},\quad\mathfrak{n}_{S}:=\bigoplus_{\alpha\in\Phi(\mathfrak{n}_{S})}\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha},\quad\mathfrak{p}_{S}:=\mathfrak{l}_{S}\oplus\mathfrak{n}_{S}.

Then
\mathfrak{l}_{S}
,
\mathfrak{n}_{S}
and
\mathfrak{p}_{S}
are Lie subalgebras of
\mathfrak{g}
.
We refer to
\mathfrak{l}_{S}
as the
Levi factor
and to
\mathfrak{n}_{S}
as the (positive)
nilradical
of the parabolic subalgebra
\mathfrak{p}_{S}
.

2.2.
Decompositions

Consider an increasing sequence

S_{1}\subset S_{2}\subset\cdots\subset S_{r-1}\subset S_{r}\subset\Pi

of proper subsets of the simple roots
\Pi
.
We have that each subset
S_{k}
has cardinality
k-1
, since
|\Pi|=r
.
For convenience we also write
S_{r+1}=\Pi
in the following.
Let us adopt the shorthand notation
\mathfrak{l}_{k}:=\mathfrak{l}_{S_{k}}
for the Levi factor corresponding to the subset
S_{k}\subseteq\Pi
.
Then we have an increasing sequence of reductive Lie algebras

(2.1)

\mathfrak{h}=\mathfrak{l}_{1}\subset\mathfrak{l}_{2}\subset\cdots\subset\mathfrak{l}_{r}\subset\mathfrak{l}_{r+1}=\mathfrak{g}.

Let
\mathfrak{g}_{k}:=[\mathfrak{l}_{S_{k}},\mathfrak{l}_{S_{k}}]
be the semisimple part of the Levi factor
\mathfrak{l}_{S_{k}}
.
In other words,
\mathfrak{g}_{k}\subseteq\mathfrak{g}
is the semisimple Lie algebra obtained from the Dynkin subdiagram corresponding to
S_{k}\subseteq\Pi
.
Then we have an increasing sequence of complex semisimple Lie algebras

(2.2)

\{0\}=\mathfrak{g}_{1}\subset\mathfrak{g}_{2}\subset\cdots\subset\mathfrak{g}_{r}\subset\mathfrak{g}_{r+1}=\mathfrak{g}.

Since the set
S_{k+1}
can be identified with the simple roots of
\mathfrak{g}_{k+1}
, we see that the inclusion
S_{k}\subset S_{k+1}
defines a parabolic subalgebra of
\mathfrak{g}_{k+1}
.
The semisimple part of its Levi factor is exactly
\mathfrak{g}_{k}
.
Then we denote by
\mathfrak{n}_{k}\subset\mathfrak{g}_{k+1}
the corresponding positive nilradical (note the shift by one in the notation).
We also observe that, in general, this is not the same as the positive nilradical corresponding the inclusion
S_{k}\subset\Pi
for the Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
.

Then, with notation as above, we have the decomposition

\mathfrak{n}=\mathfrak{n}_{1}\oplus\cdots\oplus\mathfrak{n}_{r}

corresponding to the sequence
S_{1}\subset\cdots\subset S_{r}
.
Each nilradical
\mathfrak{n}_{k}
is a
\mathfrak{l}_{k}
-module, so we have an equivariant decomposition with respect to these actions.

Example 2.1
.

To illustrate the setting above, consider
\mathfrak{g}=A_{3}=\mathfrak{sl}_{4}
with the usual numbering for the simple roots.
Consider the sequence of proper subsets given by

S_{1}=\emptyset,\quad S_{2}=\{\alpha_{1}\},\quad S_{3}=\{\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}\}.

Then the Lie subalgebras
\mathfrak{l}_{k}
and
\mathfrak{g}_{k}
are given by

\displaystyle\mathfrak{l}_{1}

\displaystyle=\mathfrak{h},\quad

\displaystyle\mathfrak{l}_{2}

\displaystyle=\mathfrak{h}\oplus\mathrm{span}\{e_{\alpha_{1}},f_{\alpha_{1}}\},\quad

\displaystyle\mathfrak{l}_{3}

\displaystyle=\mathfrak{h}\oplus\mathrm{span}\{e_{\alpha_{1}},e_{\alpha_{2}},e_{\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2}},f_{\alpha_{1}},f_{\alpha_{2}},f_{\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2}}\},

\displaystyle\mathfrak{g}_{1}

\displaystyle=\{0\},\quad

\displaystyle\mathfrak{g}_{2}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{span}\{h_{\alpha_{1}},e_{\alpha_{1}},f_{\alpha_{1}}\},\quad

\displaystyle\mathfrak{g}_{3}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{span}\{h_{\alpha_{1}},h_{\alpha_{2}},e_{\alpha_{1}},e_{\alpha_{2}},e_{\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2}},f_{\alpha_{1}},f_{\alpha_{2}},f_{\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2}}\}.

The corresponding decomposition
\mathfrak{n}=\mathfrak{n}_{1}\oplus\mathfrak{n}_{2}\oplus\mathfrak{n}_{3}
is given by

\mathfrak{n}_{1}=\mathrm{span}\{e_{\alpha_{1}}\},\quad\mathfrak{n}_{2}=\mathrm{span}\{e_{\alpha_{2}},e_{\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2}}\},\quad\mathfrak{n}_{3}=\mathrm{span}\{e_{\alpha_{3}},e_{\alpha_{2}+\alpha_{3}},e_{\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2}+\alpha_{3}}\}.

2.3.
Gradings

As observed in the introduction, an increasing sequence
S_{1}\subset\cdots\subset S_{r}
as above corresponds to a total order
\alpha_{i_{1}}<\cdots<\alpha_{i_{r}}
on the simple roots.
This is determined by the equality
S_{k}=S_{k+1}\backslash\{\alpha_{i_{k}}\}
for
k\in\{1,\cdots,r\}
, where we write
S_{r+1}=\Pi
.

Consider the corresponding decomposition
\mathfrak{n}=\mathfrak{n}_{1}\oplus\cdots\oplus\mathfrak{n}_{r}
.
Then each summand
\mathfrak{n}_{k}
is
\mathbb{N}
-graded by the simple root
\alpha_{i_{k}}
. Here a positive root
\alpha\in\Phi^{+}(\mathfrak{n}_{k})
has degree
n
if the simple root
\alpha_{i_{k}}
appears in
\alpha
with coefficient
n
.
We write this decomposition as

\mathfrak{n}_{k}=\bigoplus_{n=1}^{N_{k}}\mathfrak{n}_{k,n},

where
\mathfrak{n}_{k,n}
denotes the component of degree
n
.
We note that here
N_{k}
corresponds to the maximal coefficient of
\alpha_{i_{k}}
appearing in any root of
\mathfrak{n}_{k}
.

Furthermore, each
\mathfrak{n}_{k,n}
is an
\mathfrak{l}_{k}
-module with respect to the adjoint action, since the simple root
\alpha_{i_{k}}
does not appear in
\Phi(\mathfrak{l}_{k})
.
The following result gives more properties concerning these modules, which are going to be used in the quantum setting as well.

Proposition 2.2
.

The
\mathfrak{l}_{k}
-modules
\mathfrak{n}_{k,n}
satisfy the following properties:

(1)

Each
\mathfrak{n}_{k,n}
is a simple
\mathfrak{l}_{k}
-module.

(2)

For
n\leq N_{k}
, the simple component
\mathfrak{n}_{k,n}
occurs in
\mathfrak{n}_{k,1}^{\otimes n}
.

(3)

The tensor product
\mathfrak{n}_{k,m}\otimes\mathfrak{n}_{k,n}
has a multiplicity-free decomposition.

Proof.

(1) This follows by a general result of Kostant on root systems of Levi factors (of which we consider special cases here), see in particular
[
Kos10
, Theorem 1.9]
.

(2) Translating
[
Kos10
, Theorem 2.3]
into our notation, we have
[\mathfrak{n}_{k,a},\mathfrak{n}_{k,b}]=\mathfrak{n}_{k,a+b}
provided
a+b\leq N_{k}
. This fact easily implies the claim.

(3) This is a general result on the representation theory of semisimple Lie algebras.
Each component
V(\nu)
occurring in
V(\lambda)\otimes V(\mu)
is of the form
\nu=\lambda+\mu^{\prime}
, with
\mu^{\prime}
a weight of
V(\mu)
, and moreover for its multiplicity we have
m^{\nu}_{\lambda,\mu}\leq\dim V(\mu)_{\mu^{\prime}}
.
Then the result follows by observing that all weights of the modules
\mathfrak{n}_{k,n}
are one-dimensional, since they are roots.
∎

2.4.
Weyl group

Let
W
be the Weyl group of the complex semisimple Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
of rank
r
, generated by the simple reflections
s_{1},\cdots,s_{r}
.
We write
\ell
for the word-length function on
W
and denote by
w_{0}
be the longest word of
W
.

Let
w=s_{i_{1}}\cdots s_{i_{n}}
be a reduced decomposition of a Weyl group element. Then it is well-known that we obtain distinct positive roots of
\mathfrak{g}
by

\beta_{k}=s_{i_{1}}\cdots s_{i_{k-1}}(\alpha_{i_{k}}),\quad k=1,\cdots,n.

In particular, fixing a reduced decomposition
w_{0}=s_{i_{1}}\cdots s_{i_{N}}
of the longest word, we obtain an enumeration
\beta_{1},\cdots,\beta_{N}
of the positive roots of
\mathfrak{g}
.
This enumeration satisfies the following convexity property, see
[
Pap04
]
: if
i<j
and
\beta_{k}=\beta_{i}+\beta_{j}
then
i<k<j
.

Let
S\subseteq\Pi
be any subset of the simple roots of
\mathfrak{g}
.
This corresponds to a parabolic subalgebra
\mathfrak{p}_{S}=\mathfrak{l}_{S}\oplus\mathfrak{n}_{S}
.
Let
w_{0,S}
be the longest word for the Weyl group of the semisimple part of
\mathfrak{l}_{S}
.
Then we define the
parabolic element

w_{S}=w_{0,S}w_{0}
corresponding to
S
.

We recall some properties corresponding to these elements.

Lemma 2.3
.

Consider a parabolic element
w_{S}
as defined above. Then:

(1)

We have
w_{0}=w_{0,S}w_{S}
and
\ell(w_{0})=\ell(w_{0,S})+\ell(w_{S})
.

(2)

If we write
\ell(w_{0,S})=M
then

\beta_{1},\cdots,\beta_{M}\in\Phi^{+}(\mathfrak{l}_{S}),\quad\beta_{M+1},\cdots,\beta_{N}\in\Phi^{+}(\mathfrak{n}_{S}).

(3)

The positive roots
\Phi^{+}(\mathfrak{n}_{S})
are invariant under
w_{0,S}
.

(4)

The positive roots corresponding to
w_{S}
are exactly
\Phi^{+}(\mathfrak{n}_{S})
.

Proof.

(1) Clearly
w_{0}=w_{0,S}w_{S}
since
w_{0,S}
is involutive, being the longest word of a Weyl group.
For the statement on the length see for instance
[
Hum92
, Section 1.8]
.

(2) Clearly
\beta_{1},\cdots,\beta_{M}\in\Phi^{+}(\mathfrak{l}_{S})
since
w_{0,S}
is the longest word for the semisimple part of
\mathfrak{l}_{S}
.
The result follows since
w_{0}
gives an enumeration of the positive roots of
\mathfrak{g}
and we have the disjoint union
\Phi^{+}(\mathfrak{g})=\Phi^{+}(\mathfrak{l}_{S})\sqcup\Phi^{+}(\mathfrak{n}_{S})
.

(3) This follows from the fact that
\mathfrak{n}_{S}
is invariant under the adjoint action of
\mathfrak{l}_{S}
.

(4) Fix a reduced decomposition of
w_{S}
and denote by
\{\gamma_{k}\}_{k=1}^{N-M}
the corresponding positive roots.
Let
\{\beta_{k}\}_{k=1}^{N}
be the positive roots corresponding to
w_{0}=w_{0,S}w_{S}
as above.
We note that
\beta_{M+k}=w_{0,S}(\gamma_{k})
for
k=1,\cdots,N-M
.
We have
\beta_{M+1},\cdots,\beta_{N}\in\Phi^{+}(\mathfrak{n}_{S})
by (2).
Since
\Phi^{+}(\mathfrak{n}_{S})
is invariant under
w_{0,S}
by (3), the result follows.
∎

3.
Quantum preliminaries

In this section we briefly recall various results concerning the quantized enveloping algebra
U_{q}(\mathfrak{g})
, with the books
[
Jan96
]
and
[
BrGo02
]
as our main references.
In particular this includes the braid group action given by the Lusztig automorphisms
T_{i}
, the corresponding quantum root vectors, and the
q
-commutation relations among these.

3.1.
Quantized enveloping algebras

Let
\mathfrak{g}
be a complex semisimple Lie algebra of rank
r
.
The
quantized enveloping algebra

U_{q}(\mathfrak{g})
is the algebra with generators
\{K_{i}^{\pm 1},E_{i},F_{i}\}_{i=1}^{r}
and relations as in
[
Jan96
, Section 4.3]
or
[
BrGo02
, Section I.6.3]
.

It becomes a Hopf algebra by the following coproduct

\Delta(K_{i})=K_{i}\otimes K_{i},\quad\Delta(E_{i})=E_{i}\otimes 1+K_{i}\otimes E_{i},\quad\Delta(F_{i})=F_{i}\otimes K_{i}^{-1}+1\otimes F_{i}.

The corresponding antipode is

S(K_{i})=K_{i}^{-1},\quad S(E_{i})=-K_{i}^{-1}E_{i},\quad S(F_{i})=-F_{i}K_{i}.

As for any Hopf algebra, we have the left
adjoint action
of
U_{q}(\mathfrak{g})
on itself defined by

x\triangleright y:=x_{(1)}yS(x_{(2)}).

Here we use Sweedler’s notation
\Delta(x)=x_{(1)}\otimes x_{(2)}
for the coproduct.

We note that
U_{q}(\mathfrak{g})
can be defined over the field of fractions
\mathbb{C}(q)
of an indeterminate
q
, or over
\mathbb{C}
with
q\in\mathbb{C}
under some restrictions.
For the purpose of this paper the first version is slightly more convenient, but we can as well work with the second version under the assumption that
q
is transcendental.
In the last section we are also going to consider
*
-structures, in which case
q^{*}=q
in the first version and
q\in\mathbb{R}
in the second one.

Let
\mathfrak{l}_{S}\subseteq\mathfrak{g}
be the Levi subalgebra corresponding to a subset
S\subseteq\Pi
.
Then the
quantized Levi factor

U_{q}(\mathfrak{l}_{S})
can be defined analogously to
U_{q}(\mathfrak{g})
, with the difference that in this case there are some central elements in the Cartan part.
For our purposes, it is more convenient to define it as the Hopf subalgebra
U_{q}(\mathfrak{l}_{S})\subseteq U_{q}(\mathfrak{g})
generated by the elements
\{K_{i}^{\pm 1}\}_{i=1}^{r}\cup\{E_{i},F_{i}\}_{i\in S}
.
Clearly the left adjoint action restricts to an action of
U_{q}(\mathfrak{l}_{S})
on
U_{q}(\mathfrak{g})
.

The representation theory of
U_{q}(\mathfrak{g})
parallels the classical situation, since
the
U_{q}(\mathfrak{g})
-modules (of type 1) are in one-to-one correspondence with the
\mathfrak{g}
-modules.
More precisely, in both cases the (finite-dimensional) simple modules are labeled by their highest weights, and similarly when comparing
U_{q}(\mathfrak{l}_{S})
and
\mathfrak{l}_{S}
.
Furthermore, the tensor product of
U_{q}(\mathfrak{g})
-modules or
U_{q}(\mathfrak{l}_{S})
-modules decomposes into simple components as in the classical case.

Finally we recall that the category of finite-dimensional
U_{q}(\mathfrak{g})
-modules is braided.
The choice of braiding is not quite unique, and the one we consider can be characterized by

\hat{R}_{V,W}(v_{\mu}\otimes w_{\nu})=q^{(\mu,\nu)}w_{\nu}\otimes v_{\mu}+\sum_{i}w_{i}\otimes v_{i},

where for the additional terms
w_{i}
has weight higher than
w_{\nu}
and
v_{i}
has weight lower than
v_{\mu}
.
This particular choice is going to play a role later in
Section

7
.

3.2.
Braid group action

Let
B_{W}
be the braid group corresponding to the Weyl group
W
.
Concretely, it has the same relations as
W
except for the relations
s_{i}^{2}=1
for the simple reflections.
There is an action of
B_{W}
on
U_{q}(\mathfrak{g})
by algebra automorphisms
T_{i}
with
i\in\{1,\cdots,r\}
, usually called
Lusztig automorphisms
, see for instance
[
BrGo02
, Section I.6.7]
.

Given any reduced decomposition
w=s_{i_{1}}\cdots s_{i_{n}}\in W
we define

T_{w}=T_{i_{1}}\cdots T_{i_{n}}.

This algebra automorphism does not depend on the chosen reduced decomposition of
w
, since the
T_{i}
’s satisfy the braid relations.
If
X_{\mu}\in U_{q}(\mathfrak{g})
has weight
\mu
then
T_{w}(X_{\mu})
has weight
w(\mu)
.

There are many possible conventions for the Lusztig automorphisms
T_{i}
.
For definiteness, we adopt the conventions from
[
BrGo02
, Section I.6.7]
. In particular we have

(3.1)

T_{i}(K_{\alpha})=K_{s_{i}(\alpha)},\quad T_{i}(E_{i})=-F_{i}K_{i},\quad T_{i}(F_{i})=-K_{i}^{-1}E_{i}.

3.3.
Quantum root vectors

Fix a reduced decomposition
w_{0}=s_{a_{1}}\cdots s_{a_{N}}
of the longest word of the Weyl group of
\mathfrak{g}
.
Then the
quantum root vectors

E_{\beta_{i}}
are defined by

E_{\beta_{i}}:=T_{a_{1}}\cdots T_{a_{i-1}}(E_{a_{i}}),\quad i\in\{1,\cdots,N\}.

Note that they depend on the choice of reduced decomposition of
w_{0}
.
The quantum root vectors
F_{\beta_{i}}
corresponding to the negative roots are defined similarly.

The quantum root vectors can be used to construct a basis of
U_{q}(\mathfrak{n})
.
Let us write
\mathbf{M}=(m_{1},\cdots,m_{N})
for an
N
-tuple of non-negative integers. Then the elements

E^{\mathbf{M}}:=E_{\beta_{1}}^{(m_{1})}\cdots E_{\beta_{N}}^{(m_{N})},\quad m_{1},\cdots,m_{N}\geq 0,

give the corresponding basis, see for instance
[
BrGo02
, Section I.6.8]
.

The commutation relations between quantum root vectors are significantly more complicated than in the classical case.
The following general result can be found for instance in
[
BrGo02
, Section I.6.10]
, which we are going to use at various points later on.

Proposition 3.1
.

For
i<j
we have

E_{\beta_{i}}E_{\beta_{j}}-q^{(\beta_{i},\beta_{j})}E_{\beta_{j}}E_{\beta_{i}}=\sum_{k_{i+1},\cdots,k_{j-1}}c_{k_{i+1},\cdots,k_{j-1}}E_{\beta_{i+1}}^{k_{i+1}}\cdots E_{\beta_{j-1}}^{k_{j-1}},

where the coefficients
c_{k_{i+1},\cdots,k_{j-1}}
are certain Laurent polynomials in
q
.

We refer to this result as the
q
-commutation relations
for the quantum root vectors.
For any weight elements
X_{\mu},Y_{\nu}\in U_{q}(\mathfrak{g})
, we can define the
q-commutator
by

[X_{\mu},Y_{\nu}]_{q}:=X_{\mu}Y_{\nu}-q^{(\mu,\nu)}Y_{\nu}X_{\mu}.

Then the
q
-commutation relations express the
q
-commutator
[E_{\beta_{i}},E_{\beta_{j}}]_{q}
as a linear combination of elements
E_{\beta_{i+1}}^{k_{i+1}}\cdots E_{\beta_{j-1}}^{k_{j-1}}
ranging from
i+1
to
j-1
.

Remark 3.2
.

In the literature one finds various slightly different definitions of the Lusztig automorphisms. For instance, the
T_{i}
’s defined in
[
Jan96
, Section 8.14]
and
[
BrGo02
, Section I.6.7]
both satisfy the relations (
3.1
) but differ by signs for
T_{i}(E_{j})
and
T_{i}(F_{j})
when
i\neq j
.

More generally, consider two different choices
T_{i}
and
\tilde{T}_{i}
which are related in the following way: for any weight vector
X_{\mu}
of weight
\mu
, we have
\tilde{T}_{i}(X_{\mu})=t_{i,\mu}T_{i}(X_{\mu})
, where the coefficient
t_{i,\mu}
depends only of the weight of
X_{\mu}
.
Then, writing
E_{\beta_{k}}
and
\tilde{E}_{\beta_{k}}
for the quantum root vectors for these two choices, we find that they only differ by an overall factor.
This implies that the form of the commutation relations is the same in both cases, since the overall factor on the left-hand side can be absorbed into the coefficients on the right-hand side.

4.
Equivariant quantizations of the nilradical

With the proper preliminaries in place, we can now consider
Problem

1.1
as mentioned in the introduction.
We want to construct a quantum analogue of
\mathfrak{n}
inside
U_{q}(\mathfrak{n})
with a fixed decomposition
\mathfrak{n}=\mathfrak{n}_{1}\oplus\cdots\oplus\mathfrak{n}_{r}
, corresponding to an increasing sequence
S_{1}\subset\cdots\subset S_{r}
of
\Pi
.
We show that this can be obtained rather easily by representation-theoretic arguments.
The given construction is not unique in general, but it is notably so in the case of the classical series
A_{r}
,
B_{r}
,
C_{r}
and
D_{r}
.
Finally we discuss the classical situation in more detail, in order to motivate some of the requirements we are going to impose in later sections.

4.1.
Existence of quantizations

Consider an increasing sequence
S_{1}\subset\cdots\subset S_{r}
of proper subsets of
\Pi
and let
\alpha_{i_{1}}<\cdots<\alpha_{i_{r}}
be the corresponding total order.
Then, as discussed in
Section

2.2
, from this data we obtain a decomposition of the positive nilradical

\mathfrak{n}=\mathfrak{n}_{1}\oplus\cdots\oplus\mathfrak{n}_{r},

where each
\mathfrak{n}_{k}
is
\mathbb{N}
-graded by
\alpha_{i_{k}}
and a
\mathfrak{l}_{k}
-module.

We look for a quantization of this decomposition in the following sense.

Definition 4.1
.

An
equivariant quantization
of the positive nilradical
\mathfrak{n}
, together with a decomposition corresponding to
S_{1}\subset\cdots\subset S_{r}
, is a finite-dimensional subspace

\mathfrak{n}^{q}=\mathfrak{n}^{q}_{1}\oplus\cdots\oplus\mathfrak{n}^{q}_{r}\subset U_{q}(\mathfrak{n})

such that each summand
\mathfrak{n}^{q}_{k}
satisfies the following properties:

•

each
\mathfrak{n}^{q}_{k}
is a
U_{q}(\mathfrak{l}_{k})
-module under
\triangleright
, corresponding to the
\mathfrak{l}_{k}
-module
\mathfrak{n}_{k}
,

•

each graded component
\mathfrak{n}^{q}_{k,n}
is contained in the
n
-fold product of
\mathfrak{n}^{q}_{k,1}
.

We remark that the grading of each module
\mathfrak{n}^{q}_{k}
is with respect to the simple root
\alpha_{i_{k}}
, using the fact that
U_{q}(\mathfrak{n})
is graded by the root lattice.

Some comments on the definition.
In the first condition we make use of the fact that
U_{q}(\mathfrak{g})
-modules (of type 1) are in one-to-one correspondence with
\mathfrak{g}
-modules, and similarly for
U_{q}(\mathfrak{l}_{S})
and
\mathfrak{l}_{S}
.
The second condition is a weak replacement for the existence of a Lie algebra structure on
\mathfrak{n}_{k}
, which can be formulated in this way since
[\mathfrak{n}_{k,a},\mathfrak{n}_{k,b}]=\mathfrak{n}_{k,a+b}
by
Proposition

2.2
.

We begin by investigating the degree-one components of the decomposition.

Lemma 4.2
.

Suppose
\mathfrak{n}^{q}_{k,1}\subset U_{q}(\mathfrak{n})
is an
U_{q}(\mathfrak{l}_{k})
-module under the left adjoint action, corresponding to the
\mathfrak{l}_{k}
-module
\mathfrak{n}_{k,1}
.
Then we must have
\mathfrak{n}^{q}_{k,1}=U_{q}(\mathfrak{l}_{k})\triangleright E_{i_{k}}
.

Proof.

The degree-one component
\mathfrak{n}_{k,1}
is a simple
\mathfrak{l}_{k}
-module by
Proposition

2.2
.
It is easy to see that it is generated by the lowest weight vector
e_{i_{k}}
, where
\alpha_{i_{k}}
is the simple root with respect to which
\mathfrak{n}_{k}
is
\mathbb{N}
-graded.
Similarly, the simple
U_{q}(\mathfrak{l}_{k})
-module
\mathfrak{n}^{q}_{k,1}\subset U_{q}(\mathfrak{n})
must be generated by a lowest weight vector of weight
\alpha_{i_{k}}
. The only elements of
U_{q}(\mathfrak{n})
of weight
\alpha_{i_{k}}
are multiples of the generator
E_{i_{k}}
.
In our conventions we have

F_{i}\triangleright X=F_{i}XK_{i}-XF_{i}K_{i}=[F_{i},X]K_{i}.

This shows that
F_{i}\triangleright E_{i_{k}}=0
for any
F_{i}\in U_{q}(\mathfrak{l}_{k})
, since
\alpha_{i_{k}}
is not a root of the Levi factor
\mathfrak{l}_{k}
.
Therefore
E_{i_{k}}
is a lowest weight vector and generates a simple
U_{q}(\mathfrak{l}_{k})
-module corresponding to
\mathfrak{n}_{k,1}
.
Finally we note that
U_{q}(\mathfrak{l}_{k})\triangleright E_{i_{k}}\subset U_{q}(\mathfrak{n})
, since it suffices to take the positive part of
U_{q}(\mathfrak{l}_{k})
to generate this module, as
E_{i_{k}}
is a lowest weight vector.
∎

This shows that the degree-one components
\mathfrak{n}^{q}_{1,1},\cdots,\mathfrak{n}^{q}_{r,1}
exist and are uniquely determined.
They are going to be the building blocks for the next result.

Proposition 4.3
.

Let
\mathfrak{g}
be a complex semisimple Lie algebra of rank
r
.
Let
S_{1}\subset\cdots\subset S_{r}
be an increasing sequence of proper subsets of
\Pi
, with
\mathfrak{n}=\mathfrak{n}_{1}\oplus\cdots\oplus\mathfrak{n}_{r}
the corresponding decomposition of the positive nilradical.

Then there exists a finite-dimensional subspace

\mathfrak{n}^{q}=\mathfrak{n}^{q}_{1}\oplus\cdots\oplus\mathfrak{n}^{q}_{r}\subset U_{q}(\mathfrak{n})

such that each summand
\mathfrak{n}^{q}_{k}
satisfies the following properties:

•

\mathfrak{n}^{q}_{k}
is an
U_{q}(\mathfrak{l}_{k})
-module under
\triangleright
, corresponding to the
\mathfrak{l}_{k}
-module
\mathfrak{n}_{k}
,

•

each graded component
\mathfrak{n}^{q}_{k,n}
is contained in the
n
-fold product of
\mathfrak{n}^{q}_{k,1}
.

Proof.

In
Lemma

4.2
we proved that, for each
k\in\{1,\cdots,r\}
, the degree-one component
\mathfrak{n}^{q}_{k,1}\subset U_{q}(\mathfrak{n})
exists and is uniquely determined.
The components
\mathfrak{n}^{q}_{k,n}
of degree
n>1
can be constructed as follows (but not in a unique way, in general).

Consider the
n
-fold product
\mathfrak{n}^{q}_{k,1}\cdots\mathfrak{n}^{q}_{k,1}\subset U_{q}(\mathfrak{n})
.
This is an
U_{q}(\mathfrak{l}_{k})
-module and a quotient of
(\mathfrak{n}^{q}_{k,1})^{\otimes n}
by the multiplication map in
U_{q}(\mathfrak{n})
.
We know that the
\mathfrak{l}_{k}
-module
\mathfrak{n}_{k,n}
appears as a simple component of
\mathfrak{n}_{k,1}^{\otimes n}
by
Proposition

2.2
, hence the same is true for the corresponding
U_{q}(\mathfrak{l}_{k})
-module. Then we can pick a simple component corresponding to the
\mathfrak{l}_{k}
-module
\mathfrak{n}_{k,n}
within
(\mathfrak{n}^{q}_{k,1})^{\otimes n}
, and denote by
\mathfrak{n}^{q}_{k,n}
its image in
U_{q}(\mathfrak{n})
.
At least one such component is going to be non-zero under the multiplication map, by specialization at
q=1
.
∎

We have the following simple result concerning the uniqueness of this construction.

Corollary 4.4
.

Consider an equivariant quantization
\mathfrak{n}^{q}=\mathfrak{n}^{q}_{1}\oplus\cdots\oplus\mathfrak{n}^{q}_{r}
as above.

(1)

The components
\mathfrak{n}^{q}_{k,1}
and
\mathfrak{n}^{q}_{k,2}
are uniquely determined for
k\in\{1,\cdots,r\}
.

(2)

For the classical series
A_{r}
,
B_{r}
,
C_{r}
,
D_{r}
the quantization is uniquely determined.

Proof.

(1) We have already seen that each
\mathfrak{n}^{q}_{k,1}
is uniquely determined in
Lemma

4.2
.
For the component of degree two, observe that
\mathfrak{n}^{q}_{k,1}\otimes\mathfrak{n}^{q}_{k,1}
admits a multiplicity-free decomposition by
Proposition

2.2
, which implies that there is only one component of this type.

(2) It is easily checked that the classical series
A_{r}
,
B_{r}
,
C_{r}
,
D_{r}
have the property that their highest roots contain each simple root with coefficient at most two. This means that the
U_{q}(\mathfrak{l}_{k})
-module
\mathfrak{n}^{q}_{k}
has at most two components, for any
k\in\{1,\cdots,r\}
and for any sequence
S_{1}\subset\cdots\subset S_{r}
.
Then the uniqueness from (1) gives the result.
∎

This uniqueness does not extend to components of higher degree, as we discuss in the next subsection.
For this reason we need further conditions to obtain a reasonable analogue of
\mathfrak{n}=\mathfrak{n}_{1}\oplus\cdots\oplus\mathfrak{n}_{r}
for the exceptional Lie algebras, which we discuss later.

4.2.
Comments on the result

Here we want to illustrate some shortcomings of the construction given in the proof of
Proposition

4.3
, and motivate some of the developments that appear in the next sections.
We do this by specializing the construction to the classical case, that is for the universal enveloping algebra
U(\mathfrak{g})
corresponding to
\mathfrak{g}
.

Recall that
U(\mathfrak{g})
is a Hopf algebra with coproduct determined by

\Delta(x)=x\otimes 1+1\otimes x,\quad x\in\mathfrak{g}.

In particular, this is the case for any of the summands appearing in the decomposition of the positive nilradical
\mathfrak{n}=\mathfrak{n}_{1}\oplus\cdots\oplus\mathfrak{n}_{r}
, as well as for their graded components.

Focusing on a fixed summand
\mathfrak{n}_{k}
, we can try to follow the strategy of
Proposition

4.3
to recover the graded component
\mathfrak{n}_{k,n}
of degree
n
inside
U(\mathfrak{n})
.
In order to do this, we decompose the tensor product
\mathfrak{n}_{k,1}^{\otimes n}
into simple components as an
\mathfrak{l}_{k}
-module, then select one component isomorphic to
\mathfrak{n}_{k,n}
and map it to
U(\mathfrak{n})
using the multiplication map.
This procedure gives the expected result for
n=1
and
n=2
but not necessarily for
n\geq 3
, as we explain below.
Note that this is trivial for
n=1
, while for
n=2
it follows from the fact that
\mathfrak{n}_{k,1}\otimes\mathfrak{n}_{k,1}
has a multiplicity-free decomposition, as argued in
Corollary

4.4
.

First we discuss the case of the component of degree two more explicitly.
As already observed, the simple component
\tilde{\mathfrak{n}}_{k,2}\subset\mathfrak{n}_{k,1}\otimes\mathfrak{n}_{k,1}
is uniquely determined and antisymmetric.
Fixing a basis
\{x_{i}\}_{i}
of
\mathfrak{n}_{k,1}
, we have a basis
\{\tilde{y}_{i}\}_{i}
of
\tilde{\mathfrak{n}}_{k,2}
of the form
\tilde{y}_{i}=\sum_{a,b}c^{i}_{ab}x_{a}\otimes x_{b}
with
c^{i}_{ab}=-c^{i}_{ba}
.
Its image under the multiplication map
\mu
is then
y_{i}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j}c^{i}_{ab}[x_{a},x_{b}]
, which makes it clear that we have
\mu(\tilde{\mathfrak{n}}_{k,2})=\mathfrak{n}_{k,2}
and furthermore
\Delta(y_{i})=y_{i}\otimes 1+1\otimes y_{i}
.

Now consider the case of the component of degree three.
First we observe that there are at least two components isomorphic to
\mathfrak{n}_{k,3}
in the decomposition of
\mathfrak{n}_{k,1}^{\otimes 3}
. Indeed, denoting them by
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
, they are obtained from the following decompositions

C_{1}\subset\tilde{\mathfrak{n}}_{k,2}\otimes\mathfrak{n}_{k,1},\quad C_{2}\subset\mathfrak{n}_{k,1}\otimes\tilde{\mathfrak{n}}_{k,2}.

Both of these components are uniquely determined, as the two tensor products have multiplicity-free decompositions.
Then their images in
U(\mathfrak{n})
under the multiplication map are candidates for
\mathfrak{n}_{k,3}
, as in the construction of
Proposition

4.3
.

However neither of these actually correspond to the component
\mathfrak{n}_{k,3}\subset\mathfrak{n}
.
To see this, consider an element
\tilde{z}=\sum_{i,j}b_{ij}\tilde{y}_{i}\otimes x_{j}
of
C_{1}
and the corresponding element
z=\sum_{i,j}b_{ij}y_{i}x_{j}
in
U(\mathfrak{n})
.
Then its coproduct is given by
\Delta(z)=\sum_{i,j}b_{ij}\Delta(y_{i})\Delta(x_{j})
, which is not of the form
z\otimes 1+1\otimes z
. The situation is analogous for the component
C_{2}
.

On the other hand,
\mathfrak{n}_{k,3}
can be recovered from a subspace of
C_{1}\oplus C_{2}
as follows.
Denoting by
\tau
the flip map, we first observe that
C_{2}=\tau_{1}\tau_{2}(C_{1})
. Then we consider

C=(\mathrm{id}-\tau_{1}\tau_{2})(C_{1})\subset C_{1}\oplus C_{2}.

This means that any element of
C
has the general form
\tilde{z}=\sum_{i,j}b_{ij}(\tilde{y}_{i}\otimes x_{j}-x_{j}\otimes\tilde{y}_{i})
.
Then the corresponding element in
U(\mathfrak{n})
is given by
z=\sum_{i,j}b_{ij}[y_{i},x_{j}]
, which immediately shows that
\Delta(z)=z\otimes 1+1\otimes z
, as should be the case for the component
\mathfrak{n}_{k,3}
.

This illustrates that, in order to recover the classical situation, we should impose extra conditions when quantizing
\mathfrak{n}_{k,n}
for
n\geq 3
.
As an analogue of the condition
\Delta(z)=z\otimes 1+1\otimes z
for
z\in\mathfrak{n}_{k}
, we are going to require that
\mathbb{C}\oplus\mathfrak{n}^{q}_{k}
should be a left
U_{q}(\mathfrak{b})
-coideal.

5.
Adjoint action on Schubert cells

The goal of this section is to connect the equivariant quantizations from the previous section to the quantum Schubert cells
U_{q}(w)
introduced by De Concini, Kac and Procesi in
[
DCKP95
]
, corresponding to a Weyl group element
w
, as well as on their twisted versions
U^{\prime}_{q}(w_{S})
introduced by Zwicknagl in
[
Zwi09
]
, corresponding to a factorization of the type
w_{0}=w_{0,S}w_{S}
.
The advantage of the latter is that it is invariant under the left adjoint action of the quantized Levi factor, as shown in
[
Zwi09
, Theorem 5.21]
.

We give a short proof of this result, which tries to elucidate the roles of both
U_{q}(w_{S})
and
U^{\prime}_{q}(w_{S})
.
Then we discuss the connection with the equivariant quantizations of
\mathfrak{n}
.

5.1.
Quantum Schubert cells

For any Weyl group element
w\in W
, De Concini, Kac and Procesi define an algebra
U_{q}(w)\subseteq U_{q}(\mathfrak{n})
as follows.

Definition 5.1
.

Fix a reduced decomposition
w=s_{i_{1}}\cdots s_{i_{n}}
and let
E_{\beta_{1}},\cdots,E_{\beta_{n}}
be the corresponding quantum root vectors.
Then the
quantum Schubert cell

U_{q}(w)
is defined as the subalgebra of
U_{q}(\mathfrak{n})
generated by these quantum root vectors.

It can be shown that this algebra does not depend on the chosen reduced decomposition of
w
, see the proposition appearing in
[
DCKP95
, Section 2.2]
.

Now consider a factorization
w_{0}=w_{0,S}w_{S}
corresponding to a parabolic subalgebra defined by
S\subseteq\Pi
. Then in general
U_{q}(w_{S})
is not invariant under the left adjoint action of
U_{q}(\mathfrak{l}_{S})
, the quantized Levi factor.
This motivates the following modification of the Schubert cell
U_{q}(w_{S})
introduced by Zwicknagl, see
[
Zwi09
, Definition 5.4]
.

Definition 5.2
.

Consider the factorization
w_{0}=w_{0,S}w_{S}
with respect to
S\subseteq\Pi
.
Then the
twisted quantum Schubert cell

U^{\prime}_{q}(w_{S})
is defined by

U^{\prime}_{q}(w_{S}):=T_{w_{0,S}}(U_{q}(w_{S})).

Here
T_{w_{0,S}}
is the Lusztig automorphism corresponding to the longest word
w_{0,S}
for
S
.
It does not depend on the chosen reduced decomposition for this element.

This definition can be generalized to the relative setting of a Hopf algebra embedding
U_{q}(\mathfrak{k})\hookrightarrow U_{q}(\mathfrak{g})
, where
\mathfrak{k}
corresponds to a Dynkin subdiagram of
\mathfrak{g}
.
In this case the twisted quantum Schubert cell
U^{\prime}_{q}(w_{S})\subseteq U_{q}(\mathfrak{k})
can be transported to
U_{q}(\mathfrak{g})
in the obvious way.

5.2.
Invariance under the adjoint action

We are now going to show that the twisted quantum Schubert cell
U^{\prime}_{q}(w_{S})
is invariant under the left adjoint action of the quantized Levi factor
U_{q}(\mathfrak{l}_{S})
, a result originally proven in
[
Zwi09
, Theorem 5.21]
.

Recall that, given weight vectors
X_{\mu}
and
Y_{\nu}
of weight
\mu
and
\nu
respectively, their
q
-commutator is given by
[X_{\mu},Y_{\nu}]_{q}=X_{\mu}Y_{\nu}-q^{(\mu,\nu)}Y_{\nu}X_{\mu}
.
We begin by showing that the two algebras
U_{q}(w_{S})
and
U^{\prime}_{q}(w_{S})
are invariant, in an appropriate sense, under taking
q
-commutators with the generators of the positive part of the quantized Levi factor
U_{q}(\mathfrak{l}_{S})
.

Lemma 5.3
.

For any
i\in S
we have:

(1)

[U_{q}(w_{S}),E_{i}]_{q}\subseteq U_{q}(w_{S})
for the quantum Schubert cell,

(2)

[E_{i},U^{\prime}_{q}(w_{S})]_{q}\subseteq U^{\prime}_{q}(w_{S})
for the twisted quantum Schubert cell.

Proof.

(1) By definition the quantum Schubert cell
U_{q}(w_{S})
is generated by the quantum root vectors
\{E_{\xi_{j}}\}_{j=1}^{N}
corresponding to a reduced decomposition of
w_{S}
.
Consider a factorization
w_{0}=w_{S}w^{\prime}
and denote by
\{E_{\beta_{j}}\}_{j=1}^{M+N}
the corresponding quantum root roots. Then
E_{\beta_{j}}=E_{\xi_{j}}
for
j\in\{1,\cdots,N\}
, while the other quantum root vectors belong to the quantized Levi factor
U_{q}(\mathfrak{l}_{S})
.
It suffices to show that
[E_{\xi_{j}},E_{i}]_{q}\in U_{q}(w_{S})
for any
j\in\{1,\cdots,N\}
and
i\in S
, since the general claim follows from the identity

[X_{\lambda}Y_{\mu},Z_{\nu}]_{q}=X_{\lambda}[Y_{\mu},Z_{\nu}]_{q}+q^{(\mu,\nu)}[X_{\lambda},Z_{\nu}]_{q}Y_{\mu}.

We can choose the reduced decomposition for
w^{\prime}
in such a way that
\beta_{N+1}=\alpha_{i}
, that is
E_{\beta_{N+1}}=E_{i}
.
Note that this does not change the quantum quantum root vectors
\{E_{\xi_{j}}\}_{j=1}^{N}
.
Then for any
j\in\{1,\cdots,N\}
we consider the
q
-commutator
[E_{\xi_{j}},E_{i}]_{q}=[E_{\beta_{j}},E_{\beta_{N+1}}]_{q}
.
By the
q
-commutation relations from
Proposition

3.1
we get
[E_{\beta_{j}},E_{\beta_{N+1}}]_{q}=\sum_{\mathbf{M}}c_{\mathbf{M}}E^{\mathbf{M}}
, where the sum can be restricted to the tuples
(m_{1},\cdots,m_{M+N})
with
m_{k}=0
for
k\leq j
and
k\geq N+1
.
Since
E_{\beta_{k}}\in U_{q}(w_{S})
for
k\in\{j+1,\cdots,N\}
, we get the result.

(2) The proof is similar to (1). Choosing a reduced decomposition for
w_{0}=w_{0,S}w_{S}
, we have the quantum root vectors
\{E^{\prime}_{\gamma_{j}}\}_{j=1}^{M+N}
.
Then the generators
\{E^{\prime}_{\xi_{j}}\}_{j=1}^{N}
of
U^{\prime}_{q}(w_{S})
are given by
E^{\prime}_{\xi_{j}}=E^{\prime}_{\gamma_{M+j}}
for
j\in\{1,\cdots,N\}
, while the other quantum root vectors belong to the quantized Levi factor
U_{q}(\mathfrak{l}_{S})
.
It suffices to prove the claim for the generators, since

[X_{\lambda},Y_{\mu}Z_{\nu}]_{q}=[X_{\lambda},Y_{\mu}]_{q}Z_{\nu}+q^{(\lambda,\mu)}Y_{\mu}[X_{\lambda},Z_{\nu}]_{q}.

As above, we can choose the reduced decomposition for
w_{0,S}
in such a way that
E^{\prime}_{\gamma_{M}}=E_{i}
.
Then we consider the
q
-commutator
[E_{i},E^{\prime}_{\xi_{j}}]_{q}=[E^{\prime}_{\gamma_{M}},E^{\prime}_{\gamma_{M+j}}]_{q}
.
An argument using the
q
-commutation relations as in (1) gives the result.
∎

The previous result shows that there is not much difference between
U_{q}(w_{S})
and
U^{\prime}_{q}(w_{S})
from the point of view of the
q
-commutation relations.
However an important difference arises when we consider the left adjoint action, since we have the identity
E_{i}\triangleright X_{\mu}=[E_{i},X_{\mu}]_{q}
(in our conventions).
Then the previous
q
-commutation relations can be used to prove the following result originally due to Zwicknagl, see
[
Zwi09
, Theorem 5.21(b)]
.

Proposition 5.4
.

For any subset
S\subseteq\Pi
, the twisted quantum Schubert cell
U^{\prime}_{q}(w_{S})
is invariant under the left adjoint action of the quantized Levi factor
U_{q}(\mathfrak{l}_{S})
.

Proof.

Recall that the quantized Levi factor
U_{q}(\mathfrak{l}_{S})
is generated by the Cartan elements, together with
E_{i}
and
F_{i}
with
i\in S
.
It is clear that
U^{\prime}_{q}(w_{S})
is invariant under the action of the Cartan elements, since it is generated by weight vectors.
Next we note that, in our conventions for the coproduct, we have the identities

\begin{split}E_{i}\triangleright X_{\mu}&=E_{i}X_{\mu}-K_{i}X_{\mu}K_{i}^{-1}E_{i}=[E_{i},X_{\mu}]_{q},\\
F_{i}\triangleright X_{\mu}&=F_{i}X_{\mu}K_{i}-X_{\mu}F_{i}K_{i}=[F_{i},X_{\mu}]K_{i}.\end{split}

Here
X_{\mu}
is any weight vector.
We note that in the first case we have a
q
-commutator, while in the second case we have a regular commutator.
Then the first identity and
Lemma

5.3
imply that
E_{i}\triangleright U^{\prime}_{q}(w_{S})\subseteq U^{\prime}_{q}(w_{S})
for any
i\in S
.

Now consider
F_{i}\triangleright X_{\mu}=[F_{i},X_{\mu}]K_{i}
for
i\in S
.
Using the automorphism
T_{w_{0,S}}^{-1}
we get

T_{w_{0,S}}^{-1}(F_{i}\triangleright X_{\mu})=[T_{w_{0,S}}^{-1}(F_{i}),T_{w_{0,S}}^{-1}(X_{\mu})]T_{w_{0,S}}^{-1}(K_{i}).

We must have
w_{0,S}(\alpha_{i})=-\alpha_{j}
for some
j\in S
, since
w_{0,S}
is the longest word corresponding to
S
.
We rewrite this as
w_{0,S}(\alpha_{j})=-\alpha_{i}
, since
w_{0,S}
is involutive.
We can factorize
w_{0,S}=s_{i}w
with
\ell(w)=\ell(w_{0,S})-1
and
w(\alpha_{j})=\alpha_{i}
.
Then it follows from
[
Jan96
, Proposition 8.20]
that
T_{w}(E_{j})=E_{i}
.
Using this and (
3.1
) we compute

T_{w_{0,S}}(E_{j}K_{j})=T_{i}T_{w}(E_{j}K_{j})=T_{i}(E_{i}K_{i})=-F_{i}.

From this it follows that
T_{w_{0,S}}^{-1}(F_{i})=-E_{j}K_{j}
.
This lets us rewrite

T_{w_{0,S}}^{-1}(F_{i}\triangleright X_{\mu})=-[E_{j}K_{j},T_{w_{0,S}}^{-1}(X_{\mu})]K_{j}^{-1}.

Since
X_{\mu}
has weight
\mu
, the element
T_{w_{0,S}}^{-1}(X_{\mu})
has weight
w_{0,S}^{-1}(\mu)=w_{0,S}(\mu)
. Then

\begin{split}T_{w_{0,S}}^{-1}(F_{i}\triangleright X_{\mu})&=T_{w_{0,S}}^{-1}(X_{\mu})E_{j}-q^{(\alpha_{j},w_{0,S}(\mu))}E_{j}T_{w_{0,S}}^{-1}(X_{\mu})\\
&=[T_{w_{0,S}}^{-1}(X_{\mu}),E_{j}]_{q}.\end{split}

Therefore
F_{i}\triangleright X_{\mu}=T_{w_{0,S}}\left([T_{w_{0,S}}^{-1}(X_{\mu}),E_{j}]_{q}\right)
, where
w_{0,S}(\alpha_{i})=-\alpha_{j}
and
j\in S
.

Applying this identity to any element of
U^{\prime}_{q}(w_{S})
we get

F_{i}\triangleright U^{\prime}_{q}(w_{S})\subseteq T_{w_{0,S}}\left([T_{w_{0,S}}^{-1}(U^{\prime}_{q}(w_{S})),E_{j}]_{q}\right)=T_{w_{0,S}}\left([U_{q}(w_{S}),E_{j}]_{q}\right).

By
Lemma

5.3
we have
[U(w_{S}),E_{j}]_{q}\subseteq U(w_{S})
for any
j\in S
. Hence

F_{i}\triangleright U^{\prime}_{q}(w_{S})\subseteq T_{w_{0,S}}(U_{q}(w_{S}))=U^{\prime}_{q}(w_{S}),

which concludes the proof.
∎

Remark 5.5
.

The result remains valid if we replace the Lusztig automorphisms
T_{i}
with another choice
\tilde{T}_{i}
such that
\tilde{T}_{i}(X_{\mu})=t_{i,\mu}T_{i}(X_{\mu})
on weight vectors, as in
Remark

3.2
.

5.3.
Comparison

Now we discuss the connection between the twisted quantum Schubert cells and the equivariant quantizations
\mathfrak{n}^{q}=\mathfrak{n}^{q}_{1}\oplus\cdots\oplus\mathfrak{n}^{q}_{r}
as in
Proposition

4.3
.

First we observe that, for any increasing sequence
S_{1}\subset\cdots\subset S_{r}
of proper subsets of
\Pi
, we have a corresponding factorization of the longest word of
\mathfrak{g}
of the form

w_{0}=w_{1}\cdots w_{r},\quad\ell(w_{0})=\ell(w_{1})+\cdots+\ell(w_{r}).

To obtain it, start from the factorization
w_{0}=w_{0,S_{r}}w_{S_{r}}
with respect to
S_{r}\subset\Pi
and set
w_{r}=w_{S_{r}}
.
Then
w_{0,S_{r}}
is the longest word of the semisimple Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}_{r}\subset\mathfrak{g}
.
Proceeding in this way, we factorize
w_{0,S_{k+1}}=w_{0,S_{k}}w_{S_{k}}
with respect to
S_{k}\subset S_{k+1}
and set
w_{k}=w_{S_{k}}
.
This gives the factorization
w_{0}=w_{1}\cdots w_{r}
with respect to the sequence
S_{1}\subset\cdots\subset S_{r}
, where in the last step we use
w_{0,\emptyset}=1
.
We note that a factorization of this type for
w_{0}
is related to the notion of
nice decomposition
introduced by Littelmann, see
[
Lit98
, Section 4]
.

Note that the Weyl group element
w_{k}
occurs in the factorization of the longest word of
\mathfrak{g}_{k+1}
with respect to the inclusion
S_{k}\subset S_{k+1}
.
We have an embedding
U_{q}(\mathfrak{g}_{k+1})\hookrightarrow U_{q}(\mathfrak{g})
of
U_{q}(\mathfrak{l}_{k})
-modules obtained by mapping the generators in the obvious way.
Then the twisted quantum Schubert cell
U^{\prime}_{q}(w_{k})\subset U_{q}(\mathfrak{g}_{k+1})
can be transported to
U_{q}(\mathfrak{g})
via this embedding, and we denote it by the same symbol in the next result.

Proposition 5.6
.

Let
\mathfrak{n}^{q}=\mathfrak{n}^{q}_{1}\oplus\cdots\oplus\mathfrak{n}^{q}_{r}
be an equivariant quantization corresponding to
S_{1}\subset\cdots\subset S_{r}
. Then we have
\mathfrak{n}^{q}_{k}\subset U^{\prime}_{q}(w_{k})
for any
k\in\{1,\cdots,r\}
.
Moreover
\mathfrak{n}^{q}_{k,1}
coincides with the degree-one component of
U^{\prime}_{q}(w_{k})
with respect to the grading by
\alpha_{i_{k}}
.

Proof.

For any
k\in\{1,\cdots,r\}
we have
E_{i_{k}}\in U^{\prime}_{q}(w_{k})
.
Indeed, for the enumeration of the positive roots corresponding to
w_{0}=w_{0,S}w_{S}
, those of the Levi factor
\mathfrak{l}_{S}
occur before those of the positive nilradical
\mathfrak{n}_{S}
.
Recall that the twisted quantum Schubert cell
U^{\prime}_{q}(w_{k})
is invariant under the left adjoint action of
U_{q}(\mathfrak{l}_{k})
, by
Proposition

5.4
.
Then this action on
E_{i_{k}}
generates the degree-one component of
U^{\prime}_{q}(w_{k})
with respect to
\alpha_{i_{k}}
, since this is not a root of
\mathfrak{l}_{k}
.
By
Lemma

4.2
the degree-one component
\mathfrak{n}^{q}_{k,1}
is also generated by
E_{i_{k}}
under the left adjoint action of
U_{q}(\mathfrak{l}_{k})
, hence the two must coincide.
Finally, by construction any graded component
\mathfrak{n}^{q}_{k,n}
is contained in the
n
-fold product of
\mathfrak{n}^{q}_{k,1}
, hence it is contained in
U^{\prime}_{q}(w_{k})
.
∎

This result implies that each degree-one component
\mathfrak{n}^{q}_{k,1}
consists of quantum root vectors corresponding to some reduced decomposition of
w_{0}
, which makes its description more explicit.
We are going to make use of this fact later in
Proposition

9.7
.

6.
Quasitriangular Hopf algebras and transmutation

In this section we momentarily abandon the quantized enveloping algebra
U_{q}(\mathfrak{g})
to discuss some general results on quasitriangular Hopf algebras, which are going to be used in the next section.
The overall theme is the comparison of two left actions of a Hopf algebra
H
on the tensor product
H\otimes H
.
As we are going to discuss, in the quasitriangular case this comparison can be achieved by a form of transmutation, a notion introduced by Majid.
We provide some details for this result, as we could not find it in the literature in the form we require.
Finally we discuss a formula, which we refer to as the quantum shuffle product, for a certain type of products in the braided tensor product algebra
H\otimes_{\sigma}H
.

6.1.
Actions on the tensor product

Let
H
be a Hopf algebra and denote as before the left adjoint action of
H
on itself by
x\triangleright y=x_{(1)}yS(x_{(2)})
.
Then we have the tensor product action of
H
on
H\otimes H
, denoted by the same symbol, given by

x\triangleright(y\otimes z):=x_{(1)}\triangleright y\otimes x_{(2)}\triangleright z.

In general we have
x\triangleright(yy^{\prime}\otimes zz^{\prime})\neq x_{(1)}\triangleright(y\otimes z)\cdot x_{(2)}\triangleright(y^{\prime}\otimes z^{\prime})
, so that
H\otimes H
is not a
H
-module algebra with respect to
\triangleright
(but see
Proposition

6.2
for the quasitriangular case).
Furthermore let us consider the coproduct
\Delta(x\triangleright y)
. Then a quick computation shows that

\Delta(x\triangleright y)=x_{(1)}y_{(1)}S(x_{(3)})\otimes x_{(2)}\triangleright y_{(2)}.

In general this does not coincide with
x\triangleright\Delta(y)
, unless
H
is cocommutative.

On the other hand, we can introduce a second left action
\blacktriangleright
of
H
on
H\otimes H
, which reduces to the previous identity on the subspace
\Delta(H)\subseteq H\otimes H
, given by

x\blacktriangleright(y\otimes z):=x_{(1)}yS(x_{(3)})\otimes x_{(2)}\triangleright z.

Below we discuss some of its basic properties.

Proposition 6.1
.

The map
\blacktriangleright
satisfies the following properties:

(1)

It is a left action of
H
on
H\otimes H
.

(2)

It makes
H\otimes H
into a
H
-module algebra.

(3)

It satisfies
x\blacktriangleright\Delta(y)=\Delta(x\triangleright y)
.

Proof.

(1) Using the fact that
\triangleright
is a left action and
S
an anti-homomorphism, we compute

\begin{split}xx^{\prime}\blacktriangleright(y\otimes z)&=x_{(1)}x^{\prime}_{(1)}yS(x_{(3)}x^{\prime}_{(3)})\otimes x_{(2)}x^{\prime}_{(2)}\triangleright z\\
&=x_{(1)}x^{\prime}_{(1)}yS(x^{\prime}_{(3)})S(x_{(3)})\otimes x_{(2)}\triangleright x^{\prime}_{(2)}\triangleright z\\
&=x\blacktriangleright x^{\prime}\blacktriangleright(y\otimes z).\end{split}

(2) Using the module algebra property of
\triangleright
and
S(x_{(1)})x_{(2)}=\varepsilon(x)
we compute

\begin{split}x\blacktriangleright(yy^{\prime}\otimes zz^{\prime})&=x_{(1)}yy^{\prime}S(x_{(4)})\otimes(x_{(2)}\triangleright z)(x_{(3)}\triangleright z^{\prime})\\
&=x_{(1)}yS(x_{(3)})x_{(4)}y^{\prime}S(x_{(6)})\otimes(x_{(2)}\triangleright z)(x_{(5)}\triangleright z^{\prime})\\
&=x_{(1)}\blacktriangleright(y\otimes z)\cdot x_{(2)}\blacktriangleright(y^{\prime}\otimes z^{\prime}).\end{split}

(3) This is immediate from the definition of
\blacktriangleright
.
∎

We are interested in comparing the two actions
\triangleright
and
\blacktriangleright
, in some suitable sense.
This can be done in the case of a
quasitriangular
Hopf algebra, which we now review.

6.2.
Quasitriangular Hopf algebras

Let
H
be a quasitriangular Hopf algebra.
This means that
H
admits an
R-matrix
, which is an invertible element
\mathcal{R}\in H\otimes H
such that

\begin{gathered}\mathcal{R}\cdot\Delta(h)\cdot\mathcal{R}^{-1}=\Delta^{\mathrm{op}}(h),\quad h\in H,\\
(\Delta\otimes\mathrm{id})(\mathcal{R})=\mathcal{R}_{13}\cdot\mathcal{R}_{23},\quad(\mathrm{id}\otimes\Delta)(\mathcal{R})=\mathcal{R}_{13}\cdot\mathcal{R}_{12}.\end{gathered}

From these properties it follows that

\begin{gathered}(\varepsilon\otimes\mathrm{id})(\mathcal{R})=1,\quad(\mathrm{id}\otimes\varepsilon)(\mathcal{R})=1,\\
(S\otimes\mathrm{id})(\mathcal{R})=\mathcal{R}^{-1},\quad(\mathrm{id}\otimes S)(\mathcal{R}^{-1})=\mathcal{R}.\end{gathered}

Given an R-matrix
\mathcal{R}
as above, the corresponding
braiding
is defined to be
\hat{R}:=\tau\circ\mathcal{R}
, where
\tau
denotes the flip map.
The name is related to the fact that
\hat{R}
turns the category of finite-dimensional
H
-modules into a braided monoidal category.
We also denote this braiding by
\sigma
in the following, mostly for typographical reasons.

The following results is well-known, see for instance
[
Maj00
, Corollary 9.2.13]
(and note that the result in this reference should read
H
-modules as opposed to
H
-comodules).

Proposition 6.2
.

Let
H
be a quasitriangular Hopf algebra with braiding
\sigma
.
Let
A
and
B
be left
H
-module algebras.
Then the
braided tensor product

A\otimes_{\sigma}B
is the algebra given by
A\otimes B
as a vector space and with product

(a\otimes b)\cdot_{\sigma}(c\otimes d)=\mu_{1}\mu_{3}\sigma_{2}(a\otimes b\otimes c\otimes d).

It is a left
H
-module algebra with respect to the tensor product action
\triangleright
.

In this result
\mu:A\otimes A\to A
denotes the multiplication map on an algebra
A
and we use the standard leg-numbering notation, a convention we shall keep in the following.

6.3.
Transmutation

As discussed at the beginning of this section, the two left actions
\triangleright
and
\blacktriangleright
of
H
on
H\otimes H
do not coincide, in general.
To compare them we can use the theory of
transmutation
, due to Majid
[
Maj91
,
Maj93
]
.
This takes as input a quasitriangular Hopf algebra
H
and produces a braided Hopf algebra
\underline{H}
.
In particular we can compare the subspaces
\Delta(H)
and
\underline{\Delta}(H)
of
H\otimes H
, where
\underline{\Delta}
is the a modified coproduct of
\underline{H}
.
For more details on this see for instance
[
Maj00
, Theorem 7.4.2 and Example 9.4.9]
.

The setting we consider here is a bit different, since we want to work directly with
H\otimes H
rather than the subspaces described above.
As we could not find this result stated in this precise form anywhere, we provide some details of its proof.

Theorem 6.3
.

Let
H
be a quasitriangular Hopf algebra with universal R-matrix
\mathcal{R}=\sum_{i}a_{i}\otimes b_{i}
and braiding
\sigma
.
Define the map
\mathsf{T}:H\otimes H\to H\otimes H
by

\mathsf{T}(x\otimes y)=\sum_{i}xb_{i}\otimes a_{i}\triangleright y.

Then we have an isomorphism of
H
-module algebras
\mathsf{T}:(H\otimes_{\sigma}H,\triangleright)\to(H\otimes H,\blacktriangleright)
.

Proof.

Invertibility
. Using the properties of the R-matrix one easily checks that

\mathsf{T}^{-1}(x\otimes y)=\sum_{i}xS(b_{i})\otimes a_{i}\triangleright y.

Equivariance
. Using the definition of the left action
\blacktriangleright
we compute

\begin{split}z\blacktriangleright\mathsf{T}(x\otimes y)&=\sum_{i}z_{(1)}xb_{i}S(z_{(3)})\otimes z_{(2)}a_{i}\triangleright y\\
&=\sum_{i}z_{(1)}xb_{i}S(z_{(3)})\otimes z_{(2)}a_{i}S(z_{(4)})z_{(5)}\triangleright y.\end{split}

Using
\mathcal{R}_{21}\cdot\Delta^{\mathrm{op}}(x)=\Delta(x)\cdot\mathcal{R}_{21}
and
\Delta^{\mathrm{op}}(S(x))=S(x_{(1)})\otimes S(x_{(2)})
we get

\begin{split}z\blacktriangleright\mathsf{T}(x\otimes y)&=\sum_{i}z_{(1)}xS(z_{(4)})b_{i}\otimes z_{(2)}S(z_{(3)})a_{i}z_{(5)}\triangleright y\\
&=\sum_{i}z_{(1)}xS(z_{(2)})b_{i}\otimes a_{i}z_{(3)}\triangleright y\\
&=\sum_{i}(z_{(1)}\triangleright x)b_{i}\otimes a_{i}\triangleright z_{(2)}\triangleright y=\mathsf{T}(z\triangleright(x\otimes y)).\end{split}

Homomorphism
. The coproduct identities for
\mathcal{R}=\sum_{i}a_{i}\otimes b_{i}
can be written as

(6.1)

\begin{split}\sum_{i}a_{i(1)}\otimes a_{i(2)}\otimes b_{i}&=\sum_{i,j}a_{i}\otimes a_{j}\otimes b_{i}b_{j},\\
\sum_{i}a_{i}\otimes b_{i(1)}\otimes b_{i(2)}&=\sum_{i,j}a_{i}a_{j}\otimes b_{j}\otimes b_{i}.\end{split}

Using
xy=(x_{(1)}\triangleright y)x_{(2)}
and the second identity in (
6.1
), we compute

\begin{split}\mathsf{T}(x\otimes y)\cdot\mathsf{T}(x^{\prime}\otimes y^{\prime})&=\sum_{i,k}xb_{i}x^{\prime}b_{k}\otimes(a_{i}\triangleright y)(a_{k}\triangleright y^{\prime})\\
&=\sum_{i,k}x(b_{i(1)}\triangleright x^{\prime})b_{i(2)}b_{k}\otimes(a_{i}\triangleright y)(a_{k}\triangleright y^{\prime})\\
&=\sum_{i,j,k}x(b_{j}\triangleright x^{\prime})b_{i}b_{k}\otimes(a_{i}\triangleright a_{j}\triangleright y)(a_{k}\triangleright y^{\prime}).\end{split}

On the other hand, using the first identity in (
6.1
) we compute

\begin{split}\mathsf{T}(x\tilde{x}\otimes\tilde{y}y^{\prime})&=\sum_{i}x\tilde{x}b_{i}\otimes(a_{i(1)}\triangleright\tilde{y})(a_{i(2)}\triangleright y^{\prime})\\
&=\sum_{i,k}x\tilde{x}b_{i}b_{k}\otimes(a_{i}\triangleright\tilde{y})(a_{k}\triangleright y^{\prime}).\end{split}

Setting
\tilde{x}\otimes\tilde{y}=\sum_{j}b_{j}\triangleright x^{\prime}\otimes a_{j}\triangleright y
gives
\mathsf{T}(x\tilde{x}\otimes\tilde{y}y^{\prime})=\mathsf{T}(x\otimes y)\cdot\mathsf{T}(x^{\prime}\otimes y^{\prime})
.
Finally we note that since
\tilde{x}\otimes\tilde{y}=\sigma(x^{\prime}\otimes y)
we have
x\tilde{x}\otimes\tilde{y}y^{\prime}=(x\otimes y)\cdot_{\sigma}(x^{\prime}\otimes y^{\prime})
.
∎

Remark 6.4
.

The relation to Majid’s transmutation is described as follows.
If we restrict the inverse map
\mathsf{T}^{-1}
to the subspace
\Delta(H)\subseteq H\otimes H
, we obtain

\mathsf{T}^{-1}\circ\Delta(h)=\sum_{i}h_{(1)}S(b_{i})\otimes a_{i}\triangleright h_{(2)}.

Comparing with the braided coproduct
\underline{\Delta}
of
H
, as defined in
[
Maj00
, Theorem 7.4.2]
, we see that we have the relation
\mathsf{T}^{-1}\circ\Delta=\underline{\Delta}
between the two coproducts.

The previous result can be generalized to the relative setting, where
K
is a quasitriangular Hopf algebra and we have an injective Hopf algebra map
\iota:K\to H
.
Since we need this setting in the next section, we spell out some of the relevant details.

We can consider
K
as a Hopf subalgebra of
H
via the map
\iota
. As such, we get a left adjoint action of
K
on
H
as the restriction of the left adjoint action of
H
on itself. Similarly, we obtain the left actions
\triangleright
and
\blacktriangleright
of
K
on the tensor product
H\otimes H
.
Finally, transporting the R-matrix
\mathcal{R}_{K}
of
K
via the map
\iota
, we obtain the following.

Corollary 6.5
.

Let
K
be a quasitriangular Hopf algebra with universal R-matrix
\mathcal{R}_{K}
and braiding
\sigma_{K}
.
Let
H
be a Hopf algebra and
\iota:K\to H
and injective Hopf algebra map.
Write
(\iota\otimes\iota)(\mathcal{R}_{K})=\sum_{i}a_{i}\otimes b_{i}
. Define the map
\mathsf{T}_{K}:H\otimes H\to H\otimes H
by

\mathsf{T}_{K}(x\otimes y)=\sum_{i}xb_{i}\otimes a_{i}\triangleright y.

Then we have an isomorphism of
K
-module algebras
\mathsf{T}_{K}:(H\otimes_{\sigma_{K}}H,\triangleright)\to(H\otimes H,\blacktriangleright)
.

Proof.

Write
\mathcal{R}=(\iota\otimes\iota)(\mathcal{R}_{K})\in H\otimes H
. Since
\iota
is an injective Hopf algebra map we have that
\mathcal{R}
is invertible and satisfies the properties

\begin{gathered}(\Delta\otimes\mathrm{id})(\mathcal{R})=\mathcal{R}_{13}\cdot\mathcal{R}_{23},\quad(\mathrm{id}\otimes\Delta)(\mathcal{R})=\mathcal{R}_{13}\cdot\mathcal{R}_{12}.\end{gathered}

On the other hand we have
\mathcal{R}\cdot\Delta(k)\cdot\mathcal{R}^{-1}=\Delta^{\mathrm{op}}(k)
for any
k\in K
, where here we consider
K\subset H
via the inclusion
\iota
.
Then it is easy to check that the proof of
Theorem

6.3
remains valid in this setting, with the obvious exception of
K
-equivariance replacing
H
-equivariance.
∎

6.4.
Quantum shuffle product

We conclude this section by discussing an explicit formula for braided products in
H\otimes_{\sigma}H
of the form

\mathsf{P}^{(n)}(h_{1},\cdots,h_{n})=(h_{1}\otimes 1+1\otimes h_{1})\cdot_{\sigma}\cdots\cdot_{\sigma}(h_{n}\otimes 1+1\otimes h_{n}).

We should think of
\mathsf{P}^{(n)}(h_{1},\cdots,h_{n})
as the braided product of "primitive" elements in
H\otimes H
(more precisely, primitive with respect to the modified coproduct
\underline{\Delta}
).

We refer to the formula obtained below as the
quantum shuffle product
, for reasons soon to be apparent.
The result is known, see for instance
[
HeSc20
, Theorem 6.4.9]
, where it appears in the form of the comultiplication for a braided tensor algebra.
It is also essentially dual to the construction of the quantum shuffle Hopf algebra defined by Rosso in
[
Ros98
]
.
Nevertheless, we find it useful to give a short proof of this result below.

The set of
(i,j)
-shuffles
with
n=i+j
consists of permutations
s\in S_{n}
such that

s(1)<\cdots<s(i),\quad s(i+1)<\cdots<s(n).

We write
S_{i,j}
for the set of
(i,j)
-shuffles and note that it has cardinality
\binom{n}{i}
.
Below we actually consider the inverse shuffles, which are sometimes called
unshuffles
.
Given any reduced decomposition
s=s_{i_{1}}\cdots s_{i_{m}}
of
s\in S_{n}
, we define

\sigma_{s}:=\sigma_{i_{1}}\cdots\sigma_{i_{m}},

where we use the leg-numbering notation as usual.
This does not depend on the chosen reduced decomposition, since
\sigma
satisfies the braid equation.
Furthermore, consider the multiplication maps
\mu_{i,j}:H^{\otimes n}\to H^{i}\otimes H^{j}
for
n=i+j
, given by

\mu_{i,j}(h_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes h_{n})=h_{1}\cdots h_{i}\otimes h_{i+1}\cdots h_{n}.

We note the special cases
\mu_{0,n}(h_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes h_{n})=1\otimes h_{1}\cdots h_{n}
and
\mu_{n,0}(h_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes h_{n})=h_{1}\cdots h_{n}\otimes 1
.
Using this notation, we obtain the promised quantum shuffle product as follows.

Proposition 6.6
.

For any
n\geq 1
we have the formula

\mathsf{P}^{(n)}(h_{1},\cdots,h_{n})=\sum_{i+j=n}\mu_{i,j}\sum_{s^{-1}\in S_{i,j}}\sigma_{s}(h_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes h_{n}).

Proof.

First note that we can write

\mathsf{P}^{(n+1)}(h_{1},\cdots,h_{n+1})=\mathsf{P}^{(n)}(h_{1},\cdots,h_{n})\cdot_{\sigma}(h_{n+1}\otimes 1+1\otimes h_{n+1}).

Recall that
(a\otimes b)\cdot_{\sigma}(c\otimes d)=\mu_{1}\mu_{3}\sigma_{2}(a\otimes b\otimes c\otimes d)
by
Proposition

6.2
.
Using the compatibility between the multiplication, the braiding and the unit, we obtain

(a\otimes b)\cdot_{\sigma}(c\otimes 1)=\mu_{1}\sigma_{2}(a\otimes b\otimes c),\quad(a\otimes b)\cdot_{\sigma}(1\otimes d)=\mu_{2}(a\otimes b\otimes d).

This means that
\mathsf{P}^{(n+1)}=(\mu_{1}\sigma_{2}+\mu_{2})(\mathsf{P}^{(n)}\otimes\mathrm{id})
as maps on
H^{\times(n+1)}
.

The claimed formula holds for
n=1
, since
\mathsf{P}^{(1)}(h_{1})=(\mu_{1,0}+\mu_{0,1})(h_{1})
, so suppose the it holds for some generic
n
.
Let us note that
\mu_{i,j}=\mu_{1}^{i-1}\mu_{i+1}^{j-1}
, where this expression must be interpreted appropriately for the boundary cases
i=0
and
j=0
.
Let us also abbreviate
B_{i,j}=\sum_{s^{-1}\in S_{i,j}}\sigma_{s}
.
Then we are assuming that

\mathsf{P}^{(n)}=\sum_{i+j=n}\mu_{1}^{i-1}\mu_{i+1}^{j-1}B_{i,j}.

Using this assumption we compute

\begin{split}\mathsf{P}^{(n+1)}&=(\mu_{1}\sigma_{2}+\mu_{2})(\mathsf{P}^{(n)}\otimes\mathrm{id})\\
&=\sum_{i+j=n}\mu_{1}^{i}\sigma_{i+1}\mu_{i+1}^{j-1}(B_{i,j}\otimes\mathrm{id})+\sum_{i+j=n}\mu_{1}^{i-1}\mu_{i+1}^{j}(B_{i,j}\otimes\mathrm{id})\\
&=\sum_{i+j=n+1}\left(\mu_{1}^{i-1}\sigma_{i}\mu_{i}^{j-1}(B_{i-1,j}\otimes\mathrm{id})+\mu_{1}^{i-1}\mu_{i+1}^{j-1}(B_{i,j-1}\otimes\mathrm{id})\right).\end{split}

By induction one shows that
\sigma_{i}\mu_{i}^{m}=\mu_{i+1}^{m}\sigma_{i}\cdots\sigma_{i+m}
for any
m
.
This lets us rewrite

\mathsf{P}^{(n+1)}=\sum_{i+j=n+1}\mu_{1}^{i-1}\mu_{i+1}^{j-1}\left(\sigma_{i}\cdots\sigma_{n}(B_{i-1,j}\otimes\mathrm{id})+(B_{i,j-1}\otimes\mathrm{id})\right).

Then, as in the proof of
[
Ros98
, Proposition 6]
, we can use the identity

B_{i,j}=\sigma_{i}\cdots\sigma_{n}(B_{i-1,j}\otimes\mathrm{id})+(B_{i,j-1}\otimes\mathrm{id}).

This is obtained by observing that, for any shuffle
w\in S_{i,j}
with
i+j=n+1
, we have either
w(i)=n+1
or
w(n+1)=n+1
.
Using this identity gives the result.
∎

7.
Coideal property

We now come back to the equivariant quantizations
\mathfrak{n}^{q}=\mathfrak{n}^{q}_{1}\oplus\cdots\oplus\mathfrak{n}^{q}_{r}
associated to increasing sequences
S_{1}\subset\cdots\subset S_{r}
of proper subsets of
\Pi
.
These were shown to exist in
Proposition

4.3
, but are not guaranteed to be unique for a given sequence, since the construction depends on decomposing appropriate tensor products, where the simple components can appear with multiplicities larger than one.
Our main goal in this section is to study whether
\mathbb{C}\oplus\mathfrak{n}^{q}
is a left coideal, which as a byproduct allows us to get uniqueness in some cases.

The main tool for this analysis is the map
\mathsf{T}
introduced in the previous section, or rather its counterpart
\mathsf{T}_{S}
corresponding to a subset
S\subseteq\Pi
, as we explain below.
Together with the quantum shuffle product formula, this allows us to verify the coideal condition, with the possible exception of some cases corresponding to exceptional Lie algebras.

7.1.
General considerations

Let us consider an arbitrary subset
S\subseteq\Pi
of the simple roots and the corresponding quantized Levi factor
U_{q}(\mathfrak{l}_{S})
.
Then both
U_{q}(\mathfrak{l}_{S})
and
U_{q}(\mathfrak{g})
are quasitriangular Hopf algebras and we can apply the results of
Section

6
.

To be precise, in this case the universal R-matrix does not belong to the algebraic tensor product, but rather a completion of it. We do not dwell on this point, which only requires minor modifications and is well-understood in the literature.
Moreover, we mostly work in the situation where one of the leg of the tensor product is in the locally-finite part, in which case we have no issues regarding infinite sums for the R-matrix.

Proposition 7.1
.

Given any
S\subseteq\Pi
, denote by
\mathcal{R}_{S}=\sum_{i}a_{i}\otimes b_{i}
the universal R-matrix corresponding to
U_{q}(\mathfrak{l}_{S})\subseteq U_{q}(\mathfrak{g})
and by
\sigma_{S}
the corresponding braiding.
Define the map

\mathsf{T}_{S}:U_{q}(\mathfrak{g})\otimes U_{q}(\mathfrak{g})\to U_{q}(\mathfrak{g})\otimes U_{q}(\mathfrak{g}),\quad\mathsf{T}_{S}(x\otimes y)=\sum_{i}xb_{i}\otimes a_{i}\triangleright y.

Then we have an isomorphism of
U_{q}(\mathfrak{l}_{S})
-module algebras

\mathsf{T}_{S}:(U_{q}(\mathfrak{g})\otimes_{\sigma_{S}}U_{q}(\mathfrak{g}),\triangleright)\to(U_{q}(\mathfrak{g})\otimes U_{q}(\mathfrak{g}),\blacktriangleright).

Proof.

This is simply
Corollary

6.5
applied to the case
U_{q}(\mathfrak{l}_{S})\subseteq U_{q}(\mathfrak{g})
.
∎

Observe that in this result we have the braiding
\sigma_{S}=\tau\circ\mathcal{R}_{S}
corresponding to
U_{q}(\mathfrak{l}_{S})
.
For the rest of this section we use the shorter notation
\sigma=\sigma_{S}
, for fixed
S
.

Also note that the previous result is valid for any choice of R-matrix
\mathcal{R}_{S}
.
In the following we make a precise choice, which we write schematically as

\mathcal{R}_{S}=\kappa\circ\mathcal{Q}_{S}.

Here
\kappa(x_{\mu}\otimes y_{\nu}):=q^{(\mu,\nu)}x_{\mu}\otimes y_{\nu}
for weight vectors, while
\mathcal{Q}_{S}
is known as the
quasi R-matrix
.
The only properties we require of
\mathcal{Q}_{S}
is that it can be written as

\mathcal{Q}_{S}=\sum_{\beta\geq 0}\mathcal{Q}_{S,\beta},\quad\mathcal{Q}_{S,\beta}\in U_{q}(\mathfrak{l}_{S})_{-\beta}\otimes U_{q}(\mathfrak{l}_{S})_{\beta},

and moreover
\mathcal{Q}_{S,0}=1\otimes 1
.
See for instance the discussion in
[
Jan96
, Section 7]
, keeping in mind the general result that if
\mathcal{R}
is a universal R-matrix then so is
\mathcal{R}_{21}^{-1}
.
We also observe that for the corresponding braiding
\sigma=\tau\circ\mathcal{R}_{S}
we have

\sigma(x_{\mu}\otimes y_{\nu})=q^{(\mu,\nu)}y_{\nu}\otimes x_{\mu}+\sum_{i}y_{i}\otimes x_{i},

where each
x_{i}
has weight lower than
x_{\mu}
and each
y_{i}
has weight higher than
y_{\nu}
.

With the R-matrix
\mathcal{R}_{S}
as above, we have the following result.

Proposition 7.2
.

Let
V\subset U_{q}(\mathfrak{g})
be invariant under the left adjoint action of
U_{q}(\mathfrak{l}_{S})
.

(1)

The map
\mathsf{T}_{S}
preserves the subspace
U_{q}(\mathfrak{b})\otimes V
.

(2)

If
X_{\mu}\in V
is a lowest weight vector of weight
\mu
, then
\mathsf{T}_{S}(1\otimes X_{\mu})=K_{\mu}\otimes X_{\mu}
.

Proof.

(1) This follows from the formula
\mathsf{T}_{S}(x\otimes y)=\sum_{i}xb_{i}\otimes a_{i}\triangleright y
defining the map
\mathsf{T}_{S}
, since we have
\mathcal{Q}_{S}=\sum_{\beta\geq 0}\mathcal{Q}_{S,\beta}
and
\mathcal{Q}_{S,\beta}\in U_{q}(\mathfrak{l}_{S})_{-\beta}\otimes U_{q}(\mathfrak{l}_{S})_{\beta}
.

(2) Consider weight vectors
X_{\mu}
and
Y_{\nu}
, where
X_{\mu}
is a lowest weight vector with respect to
\triangleright
as above.
Recalling the given characterization of
\mathcal{Q}_{S}
, we have

\mathcal{Q}_{S}\triangleright(X_{\mu}\otimes Y_{\nu})=\mathcal{Q}_{S,0}\triangleright(X_{\mu}\otimes Y_{\nu})=X_{\mu}\otimes Y_{\nu}.

Then for the universal R-matrix we obtain

\mathcal{R}_{S}\triangleright(X_{\mu}\otimes Y_{\nu})=\kappa(X_{\mu}\otimes Y_{\nu})=q^{(\mu,\nu)}X_{\mu}\otimes Y_{\nu}=X_{\mu}\otimes K_{\mu}\triangleright Y_{\nu}.

From this identity it follows that
\mathsf{T}_{S}(1\otimes X_{\mu})=K_{\mu}\otimes X_{\mu}
.
∎

Remark 7.3
.

We remark that for the universal R-matrix
\mathcal{R}_{S}
we use
\kappa(x_{\mu}\otimes y_{\nu})=q^{(\mu,\nu)}x_{\mu}\otimes y_{\nu}
, where
(\cdot,\cdot)
is the inner product corresponding to the weight lattice of
\mathfrak{g}
(instead of the corresponding inner product for the semisimple part of
\mathfrak{l}_{S}
).
This is possible since the notions of weights for
\mathfrak{g}
and
\mathfrak{l}_{S}
coincide, as the latter contains the Cartan subalgebra
\mathfrak{h}
.
This choice guarantees that
\kappa(X_{\mu}\otimes Y_{\nu})=X_{\mu}\otimes K_{\mu}\triangleright Y_{\nu}
, which we used in the proof above.

We also observe that one can realize
\mathcal{R}_{S}
by first considering the universal R-matrix of
U_{q}(\mathfrak{g})
of a similar form and then projecting it down to the subspace
U_{q}(\mathfrak{l}_{S})\otimes U_{q}(\mathfrak{l}_{S})
.

The previous result implies the following properties.

Corollary 7.4
.

Let
V\subset U_{q}(\mathfrak{g})
be a simple
U_{q}(\mathfrak{l}_{S})
-module under the left adjoint action. Then

\mathsf{T}_{S}(V\otimes 1)=V\otimes 1,\quad\mathsf{T}_{S}(1\otimes V)\subset U_{q}(\mathfrak{l}_{S})^{\geq 0}\otimes V,

where
U_{q}(\mathfrak{l}_{S})^{\geq 0}=U_{q}(\mathfrak{l}_{S})\cap U_{q}(\mathfrak{b})
is the non-negative part of
U_{q}(\mathfrak{l}_{S})
.

Proof.

The first property is obvious, since
\mathsf{T}_{S}(X\otimes 1)=X\otimes 1
for any
X\in U_{q}(\mathfrak{g})
.
For the second property, let
X_{\mu}
be a lowest weight vector of
V
of weight
\mu
.
Then
\mathsf{T}_{S}(1\otimes X_{\mu})=K_{\mu}\otimes X_{\mu}
by the previous result.
Given any
Y\in U_{q}(\mathfrak{l}_{S})
we compute

\mathsf{T}_{S}(Y\triangleright(1\otimes X_{\mu}))=Y\blacktriangleright\mathsf{T}_{S}(1\otimes X_{\mu})=Y_{(1)}K_{\mu}S(Y_{(3)})\otimes Y_{(2)}\triangleright X_{\mu}.

Here we have used the equivariance of
\mathsf{T}_{S}
with respect to
U_{q}(\mathfrak{l}_{S})
and the definition of the left action
\blacktriangleright
.
Since
X_{\mu}
generates
V
as a
U_{q}(\mathfrak{l}_{S})^{\geq 0}
-module, we obtain

\mathsf{T}_{S}(1\otimes Y\triangleright X_{\mu})=\mathsf{T}_{S}(Y\triangleright(1\otimes X_{\mu}))\in U_{q}(\mathfrak{l}_{S})^{\geq 0}\otimes V.\qed

We are going to use these results to analyze the coproducts of elements in
\mathfrak{n}^{q}
.

7.2.
Degree one

For the rest of this section we consider a subset of the form
S=\Pi\backslash\{\alpha_{s}\}
.
Then, as in
Proposition

4.3
, we have a quantization
\mathfrak{n}^{q}_{S}
of the positive nilradical
\mathfrak{n}_{S}
, which is invariant under the left adjoint action of the quantized Levi factor
U_{q}(\mathfrak{l}_{S})
and
\mathbb{N}
-graded by
\alpha_{s}
.
Our goal is to show that we can choose
\mathfrak{n}^{q}_{S}
in such a way that
\mathbb{C}\oplus\mathfrak{n}^{q}_{S}
is a left
U_{q}(\mathfrak{b})
-coideal.
Recall that the components
\mathfrak{n}^{q}_{S,1}
and
\mathfrak{n}^{q}_{S,2}
of degrees one and two are uniquely determined by
Corollary

4.4
, so the only possible choices appear from degree
\geq 3
.

We begin by showing that
\mathbb{C}\oplus\mathfrak{n}^{q}_{S,1}
is a left coideal.
We note that this result could be proven by more elementary methods, but we state it in such a form that is well-suited to performing a similar analysis for the components of higher degree.

Proposition 7.5
.

For any
X\in\mathfrak{n}^{q}_{S,1}
we have

\Delta(X)=\mathsf{T}_{S}(X\otimes 1+1\otimes X).

This implies the property
\Delta(\mathfrak{n}^{q}_{S,1})\subseteq U_{q}(\mathfrak{b})\otimes(\mathbb{C}\oplus\mathfrak{n}^{q}_{S,1})
.

Proof.

Since
S=\Pi\backslash\{\alpha_{s}\}
, we have that
E_{s}
is a lowest weight vector for the left adjoint action of
U_{q}(\mathfrak{l}_{S})
, as in
Lemma

4.2
.
Then using
Proposition

7.2
we get

\Delta(E_{s})=E_{s}\otimes 1+K_{s}\otimes E_{s}=\mathsf{T}_{S}(E_{s}\otimes 1+1\otimes E_{s}).

This proves the first claim for the lowest weight vector
E_{s}
.

Next, recall that the coproduct satisfies the property
\Delta(x\triangleright y)=x\blacktriangleright\Delta(y)
for any
x,y\in U_{q}(\mathfrak{g})
.
Then for any
Y\in U_{q}(\mathfrak{l}_{S})
we obtain the identity

\Delta(Y\triangleright E_{s})=Y\blacktriangleright\Delta(E_{s})=Y\blacktriangleright\mathsf{T}_{S}(E_{s}\otimes 1+1\otimes E_{s}).

Similarly, the map
\mathsf{T}_{S}
satisfies the equivariance property
x\blacktriangleright\mathsf{T}_{S}(y\otimes z)=\mathsf{T}_{S}(x\triangleright(y\otimes z))
for any
x\in U_{q}(\mathfrak{l}_{S})
and
y,z\in U_{q}(\mathfrak{g})
.
Using this in the identity above, we get

\Delta(Y\triangleright E_{s})=\mathsf{T}_{S}(Y\triangleright(E_{s}\otimes 1+1\otimes E_{s}))=\mathsf{T}_{S}(Y\triangleright E_{s}\otimes 1+1\otimes Y\triangleright E_{s}).

Since
E_{s}
generates
\mathfrak{n}^{q}_{S,1}
as an
U_{q}(\mathfrak{l}_{S})
-module, this proves the first claim.
The second claim immediately follows from
Corollary

7.4
.
∎

This result shows that
\mathbb{C}\oplus\mathfrak{n}^{q}_{S,1}
is a left
U_{q}(\mathfrak{b})
-coideal.
We note that here we could replace
U_{q}(\mathfrak{b})
with
U_{q}(\mathfrak{l}_{S})^{\geq 0}
, but this is not going to be the case for the higher degrees.

Using the previous result we can also prove the following identity, which is going to be useful for studying the coideal property in general.

Lemma 7.6
.

Let
X_{1},\cdots,X_{n}\in\mathfrak{n}^{q}_{S,1}
. Then we have

\Delta(X_{1}\cdots X_{n})=\mathsf{T}_{S}\circ\mathsf{P}^{(n)}(X_{1},\cdots,X_{n}),

where the map
\mathsf{P}^{(n)}
is defined in
Proposition

6.6
.

Proof.

Clearly
\Delta(X_{1}\cdots X_{n})=\Delta(X_{1})\cdots\Delta(X_{n})
.
We have
\Delta(X)=\mathsf{T}_{S}(X\otimes 1+1\otimes X)
for any
X\in\mathfrak{n}^{q}_{S,1}
by
Proposition

7.5
.
From this we get

\Delta(X_{1}\cdots X_{n})=\mathsf{T}_{S}(X_{1}\otimes 1+1\otimes X_{1})\cdots\mathsf{T}_{S}(X_{n}\otimes 1+1\otimes X_{n}).

Now recall that
\mathsf{T}_{S}
is an algebra isomorphism from
U_{q}(\mathfrak{g})\otimes_{\sigma}U_{q}(\mathfrak{g})
to
U_{q}(\mathfrak{g})\otimes U_{q}(\mathfrak{g})
, where for the first algebra we have the product
\cdot_{\sigma}
which involves the braiding
\sigma=\sigma_{S}
corresponding to
S
.
This means that we can rewrite

\Delta(X_{1}\cdots X_{n})=\mathsf{T}_{S}\left((X_{1}\otimes 1+1\otimes X_{1})\cdot_{\sigma}\cdots\cdot_{\sigma}(X_{n}\otimes 1+1\otimes X_{n})\right).

Then the result follows from the definition of the map
\mathsf{P}^{(n)}
.
∎

7.3.
Degree two

Next we consider the degree-two component
\mathfrak{n}^{q}_{S,2}\subset\mathfrak{n}^{q}_{S,1}\cdot\mathfrak{n}^{q}_{S,1}
, which is uniquely determined as observed in
Corollary

4.4
.
In the following result we make crucial use of the previously introduced map
\mathsf{T}_{S}
, making the proof quite simple.

Proposition 7.7
.

We have

\Delta(\mathfrak{n}^{q}_{S,2})\subseteq U_{q}(\mathfrak{b})\otimes(\mathbb{C}\oplus\mathfrak{n}^{q}_{S,1}\oplus\mathfrak{n}^{q}_{S,2}).

Proof.

Let
\{X_{i}\}_{i}
be a basis of
\mathfrak{n}^{q}_{S,1}
.
Then we have
\Delta(X_{i}X_{j})=\mathsf{T}_{S}\circ\mathsf{P}^{(2)}(X_{i},X_{j})
by
Lemma

7.6
.
It easily follows from
Proposition

6.6
that

\mathsf{P}^{(2)}(X_{i},X_{j})=X_{i}X_{j}\otimes 1+(\mathrm{id}+\sigma)(X_{i}\otimes X_{j})+1\otimes X_{i}X_{j}.

Here we note that
(\mathrm{id}+\sigma)(X_{i}\otimes X_{j})\in\mathfrak{n}^{q}_{S,1}\otimes\mathfrak{n}^{q}_{S,1}\subset U_{q}(\mathfrak{b})\otimes\mathfrak{n}^{q}_{S,1}
. By
Proposition

7.2
we know that
\mathsf{T}_{S}
maps
U_{q}(\mathfrak{b})\otimes\mathfrak{n}^{q}_{S,1}
into itself, which gives

\mathsf{T}_{S}\circ(\mathrm{id}+\sigma)(X_{i}\otimes X_{j})\in U_{q}(\mathfrak{b})\otimes\mathfrak{n}^{q}_{S,1}.

Now consider any
X\in\mathfrak{n}^{q}_{S,2}
, which we can write in the form
X=\sum_{i,j}c_{ij}X_{i}X_{j}
. Then

\mathsf{T}_{S}(X\otimes 1)\in\mathfrak{n}^{q}_{S,2}\otimes\mathbb{C},\quad\mathsf{T}_{S}(1\otimes X)\in U_{q}(\mathfrak{b})\otimes\mathfrak{n}^{q}_{S,2},

by
Corollary

7.4
. This gives the result.
∎

In other words, the subspace
\mathbb{C}\oplus\mathfrak{n}^{q}_{S,1}\oplus\mathfrak{n}^{q}_{S,2}
is a left
U_{q}(\mathfrak{b})
-coideal.

7.4.
Degree three

The case of degree three is significantly more complicated.
One issue is that
\mathfrak{n}^{q}_{S,3}
is not uniquely determined in this case.
Indeed, we get at least two simple components of this type in
(\mathfrak{n}^{q}_{S,1})^{\otimes 3}
, arising from the decompositions of

\mathfrak{n}^{q}_{S,2}\otimes\mathfrak{n}^{q}_{S,1},\quad\mathfrak{n}^{q}_{S,1}\otimes\mathfrak{n}^{q}_{S,2}.

We begin by spelling out the product formula from
Proposition

6.6
in this case.

Lemma 7.8
.

For any
x_{1},x_{2},x_{3}\in U_{q}(\mathfrak{g})
we have

\begin{split}\mathsf{P}^{(3)}(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})&=x_{1}x_{2}x_{3}\otimes 1+1\otimes x_{1}x_{2}x_{3}\\
&+\mu_{1}(1+\sigma_{2}+\sigma_{2}\sigma_{1})(x_{1}\otimes x_{2}\otimes x_{3})\\
&+\mu_{2}(1+\sigma_{1}+\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2})(x_{1}\otimes x_{2}\otimes x_{3}).\end{split}

Proof.

The relevant shuffles
S_{i,j}
with
i+j=3
are

S_{0,3}=\{1\},\quad S_{1,2}=\{1,s_{1},s_{2}s_{1}\},\quad S_{2,1}=\{1,s_{2},s_{1}s_{2}\},\quad S_{3,0}=\{1\}.

Taking their inverses and inserting them into
\mathsf{P}^{(3)}
gives the result.
∎

We begin by giving an equivalent condition for
\mathfrak{n}^{q}_{S,3}
to give rise to a left coideal.

Lemma 7.9
.

Let
\tilde{\mathfrak{n}}^{q}_{S,3}\subset(\mathfrak{n}^{q}_{S,1})^{\otimes 3}
be an
U_{q}(\mathfrak{l}_{S})
-module corresponding to the
\mathfrak{l}_{S}
-module
\mathfrak{n}_{S,3}
.
Denote by
\mathfrak{n}^{q}_{S,3}
its image in
U_{q}(\mathfrak{n})
by the multiplication map.
Then we have

\Delta(\mathfrak{n}^{q}_{S,3})\subseteq U_{q}(\mathfrak{b})\otimes(\mathbb{C}\oplus\mathfrak{n}^{q}_{S,1}\oplus\mathfrak{n}^{q}_{S,2}\oplus\mathfrak{n}^{q}_{S,3}).

if and only if we have the property

\mu_{2}(1+\sigma_{1}+\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2})(\tilde{\mathfrak{n}}^{q}_{S,3})\subseteq\mathfrak{n}^{q}_{S,1}\otimes\mathfrak{n}^{q}_{S,2}.

Proof.

We denote by
\{X_{i}\}_{i}
a basis of
\mathfrak{n}^{q}_{S,1}
, as before.
Then by
Lemma

7.6
we have

\Delta(X_{i}X_{j}X_{k})=\mathsf{T}_{S}\circ\mathsf{P}^{(3)}(X_{i},X_{j},X_{k}),

where
\mathsf{P}^{(3)}
is given explicitly in
Lemma

7.8
.
We note that

\mu_{1}(1+\sigma_{2}+\sigma_{2}\sigma_{1})(X_{i}\otimes X_{j}\otimes X_{k})\in(\mathfrak{n}^{q}_{S,1})^{2}\otimes\mathfrak{n}^{q}_{S,1}\subset U_{q}(\mathfrak{b})\otimes\mathfrak{n}^{q}_{S,1}.

The same is true after applying
\mathsf{T}_{S}
, by
Proposition

7.2
.
Now consider any element
X=\sum_{i,j,k}c_{ijk}X_{i}X_{j}X_{k}\in\mathfrak{n}^{q}_{S,3}
.
Then by
Corollary

7.4
we get

\mathsf{T}_{S}(X\otimes 1)\in U_{q}(\mathfrak{b})\otimes\mathbb{C},\quad\mathsf{T}_{S}(1\otimes X)\in U_{q}(\mathfrak{b})\otimes\mathfrak{n}^{q}_{S,3}.

This accounts for three out of the four terms appearing in
Lemma

7.8
.

The only term left to consider is the one with the map
\mu_{2}(1+\sigma_{1}+\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2})
. We have

\mu_{2}(1+\sigma_{1}+\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2})(\tilde{\mathfrak{n}}^{q}_{S,3})\subseteq\mathfrak{n}^{q}_{S,1}\otimes(\mathfrak{n}^{q}_{S,1})^{2}\subset U_{q}(\mathfrak{b})\otimes(\mathfrak{n}^{q}_{S,1})^{2}.

Decompose
(\mathfrak{n}^{q}_{S,1})^{2}
into simple components, among which we have
\mathfrak{n}^{q}_{S,2}
with multiplicity one.
Let
V
be any such component and note that it must have degree two.
By
Proposition

7.2
we get
\mathsf{T}_{S}(U_{q}(\mathfrak{b})\otimes V)\subseteq U_{q}(\mathfrak{b})\otimes V
.
Since
\mathsf{T}_{S}
is invertible, we have

\mathsf{T}_{S}(U_{q}(\mathfrak{b})\otimes V)\subset U_{q}(\mathfrak{b})\otimes(\mathbb{C}\oplus\mathfrak{n}^{q}_{S,1}\oplus\mathfrak{n}^{q}_{S,2}\oplus\mathfrak{n}^{q}_{S,3})

if and only if
V=\mathfrak{n}^{q}_{S,2}
.
This gives the condition
\mu_{2}(1+\sigma_{1}+\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2})(\tilde{\mathfrak{n}}^{q}_{S,3})\subseteq\mathfrak{n}^{q}_{S,1}\otimes\mathfrak{n}^{q}_{S,2}.

∎

Hence the problem boils down to finding a simple component
\tilde{\mathfrak{n}}^{q}_{S,3}\subset(\mathfrak{n}^{q}_{S,1})^{\otimes 3}
, corresponding to the
\mathfrak{l}_{S}
-module
\mathfrak{n}_{S,3}
, such that the property from
Lemma

7.9
is satisfied.

As we discuss below, this is easy to achieve if
(\mathfrak{n}^{q}_{S,1})^{\otimes 3}
has exactly two components of this type. However, we do not know of any a priori reason why this should be the case.
Hence we have to resort to some tedious case-by-case checking in
Lemma

A.1
(which is perhaps to be expected, since this situation only arises for exceptional Lie algebras).

Proposition 7.10
.

There exists
\mathfrak{n}^{q}_{S,3}\subset U_{q}(\mathfrak{n})
such that we have

\Delta(\mathfrak{n}^{q}_{S,3})\subseteq U_{q}(\mathfrak{b})\otimes(\mathbb{C}\oplus\mathfrak{n}^{q}_{S,1}\oplus\mathfrak{n}^{q}_{S,2}\oplus\mathfrak{n}^{q}_{S,3}).

Moreover this choice is uniquely determined.

Proof.

To simplify the notation we write
\mathfrak{u}_{k}=\mathfrak{n}_{S,k}
for the classical
\mathfrak{l}_{S}
-modules and
\mathfrak{u}^{q}_{k}=\mathfrak{n}^{q}_{S,k}
for the
U_{q}(\mathfrak{l}_{S})
-modules in
U_{q}(\mathfrak{n})
(where
k=1,2,3
).
Let us also write
\mathfrak{c}^{q}_{3}
for the (abstract)
U_{q}(\mathfrak{l}_{S})
-module quantizing the classical
\mathfrak{l}_{S}
-module
\mathfrak{u}_{3}
for the decomposition under consideration.
Then we are looking for a (concrete)
U_{q}(\mathfrak{l}_{S})
-module
\mathfrak{u}^{q}_{3}\subset U_{q}(\mathfrak{n})
satisfying the condition in the claim.
We have already determined the (unique) components
\mathfrak{u}^{q}_{1}
and
\mathfrak{u}^{q}_{2}
of degrees one and two.
We also write
\tilde{\mathfrak{u}}^{q}_{2}\subset\mathfrak{u}^{q}_{1}\otimes\mathfrak{u}^{q}_{1}
for the unique simple component such that
\mu(\tilde{\mathfrak{u}}^{q}_{2})=\mathfrak{u}^{q}_{2}
.

Both tensor products
\tilde{\mathfrak{u}}^{q}_{2}\otimes\mathfrak{u}^{q}_{1}
and
\mathfrak{u}^{q}_{1}\otimes\tilde{\mathfrak{u}}^{q}_{2}
contain a unique simple component of type
\mathfrak{c}^{q}_{3}
, by the quantum analogue of
Proposition

2.2
(since both modules are weight multiplicity-free).
Hence we have at least two components of type
\mathfrak{c}^{q}_{3}
inside
(\mathfrak{u}^{q}_{1})^{\otimes 3}
, which we write as

C^{q}_{1}\subset\tilde{\mathfrak{u}}^{q}_{2}\otimes\mathfrak{u}^{q}_{1}\subset(\mathfrak{u}^{q}_{1})^{\otimes 3},\quad C^{q}_{2}\subset\mathfrak{u}^{q}_{1}\otimes\tilde{\mathfrak{u}}^{q}_{2}\subset(\mathfrak{u}^{q}_{1})^{\otimes 3}.

Denote the corresponding
U_{q}(\mathfrak{l}_{S})
-module maps by
\iota^{q}_{1}
and
\iota^{q}_{2}
, so that
C^{q}_{n}=\iota^{q}_{n}(\mathfrak{c}^{q}_{3})
for
n=1,2
.

By the quantum analogue of
Lemma

A.1
, there are exactly two components of type
\mathfrak{c}^{q}_{3}
inside
(\mathfrak{u}^{q}_{1})^{\otimes 3}
.
Hence any
U_{q}(\mathfrak{l}_{S})
-module map
\mathfrak{c}^{q}_{3}\to(\mathfrak{u}^{q}_{1})^{\otimes 3}
can be written as a linear combination of
\iota^{q}_{1}
and
\iota^{q}_{2}
. Equivalently, any
U_{q}(\mathfrak{l}_{S})
-module isomorphic to
\mathfrak{c}^{q}_{3}
in
(\mathfrak{u}^{q}_{1})^{\otimes 3}
is of the form

C^{q}_{c_{1},c_{2}}=\iota^{q}_{c_{1},c_{2}}(\mathfrak{c}^{q}_{3}),\quad\iota^{q}_{c_{1},c_{2}}=c_{1}\iota^{q}_{1}+c_{2}\iota^{q}_{2},

provided the map
\iota^{q}_{c_{1},c_{2}}
is non-zero (which is the case if either
c_{1}
or
c_{2}
is non-zero).

We claim that the image of
C^{q}_{c_{1},c_{2}}
in
U_{q}(\mathfrak{n})
is non-zero when
\iota^{q}_{c_{1},c_{2}}
is non-zero (for
q
an indeterminate or transcendental).
To see this we consider the specialization at
q=1
(for which we drop the decoration
q
everywhere). We can choose
\iota_{2}
and
\iota_{1}
such that
\iota_{2}=\tau_{1}\tau_{2}\iota_{1}
, where
\tau
denotes the flip map.
Then
C_{c_{1},c_{2}}\subset\mathfrak{u}_{1}^{\otimes 3}
is of the form

c_{1}\sum_{i}\tilde{y}_{i}\otimes x_{i}+c_{2}\sum_{i}x_{i}\otimes\tilde{y}_{i},

where
\sum_{i}\tilde{y}_{i}\otimes x_{i}\in C_{1}\subset\tilde{\mathfrak{u}}_{2}\otimes\mathfrak{u}_{1}
.
Writing
y_{i}=\mu(\tilde{y}_{i})
for the image of
\tilde{y}_{i}
in
U(\mathfrak{n})
, we see that the image
\mu_{1}^{2}(C_{c_{1},c_{2}})
of
C_{c_{1},c_{2}}
in
U(\mathfrak{n})
consists of elements of the form

c_{1}\sum_{i}y_{i}x_{i}+c_{2}\sum_{i}x_{i}y_{i}=(c_{1}+c_{2})\sum_{i}y_{i}x_{i}+c_{2}\sum_{i}[x_{i},y_{i}].

Recall that
[\mathfrak{u}_{1},\mathfrak{u}_{2}]=\mathfrak{u}_{3}
by
Proposition

2.2
.
Using this and the PBW theorem for
U(\mathfrak{n})
, it follows that the image of
C_{c_{1},c_{2}}
is non-zero, provided either
c_{1}
or
c_{2}
is non-zero.
Hence any
U_{q}(\mathfrak{l}_{S})
-module
\mathfrak{u}^{q}_{3}\subset(\mathfrak{u}^{q}_{1})^{3}
is of the form
\mathfrak{u}^{q}_{3}=\mu_{1}^{2}(C^{q}_{c_{1},c_{2}})
for some
c_{1}
and
c_{2}
.

Next, we investigate for which choice of coefficients
c_{1}
and
c_{2}
this gives rise to a left coideal.
By
Lemma

7.9
this is the case if and only if

\mu_{2}(1+\sigma_{1}+\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2})(C^{q}_{c_{1},c_{2}})\subset\mathfrak{u}^{q}_{1}\otimes\mathfrak{u}^{q}_{2}.

Note that
\mu_{2}(C^{q}_{2})\subset\mathfrak{u}^{q}_{1}\otimes\mathfrak{u}^{q}_{2}
by definition of
C^{q}_{2}
, and this is the only component of type
\mathfrak{c}^{q}_{3}
in
\mathfrak{u}^{q}_{1}\otimes\mathfrak{u}^{q}_{2}
.
Since
\mu_{2}(1+\sigma_{1}+\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2})
is a
U_{q}(\mathfrak{l}_{S})
-module map, the condition above becomes

(7.1)

\mu_{2}(1+\sigma_{1}+\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2})(C^{q}_{c_{1},c_{2}})\subseteq\mu_{2}(C^{q}_{2}).

We can study this condition in terms the maps
\iota^{q}_{1}
and
\iota^{q}_{2}
.
Let us write

(1+\sigma_{1}+\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2})\iota^{q}_{n}=t_{n,1}\iota^{q}_{1}+t_{n,2}\iota^{q}_{2}=\iota^{q}_{t_{n,1},t_{n,2}},\quad n=1,2.

Then
(1+\sigma_{1}+\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2})\iota^{q}_{c_{1},c_{2}}=\iota^{q}_{d_{1},d_{2}}
, where
d_{n}=c_{1}t_{1,n}+c_{2}t_{2,n}
for
n=1,2
.
In particular we have
(1+\sigma_{1}+\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2})\iota^{q}_{c_{1},c_{2}}(\mathfrak{c}^{q}_{3})=C^{q}_{d_{1},d_{2}}
.
As discussed above, the image of
C^{q}_{d_{1},d_{2}}
in
U_{q}(\mathfrak{n})
is non-zero if either
d_{1}
or
d_{2}
is non-zero.
In particular, it follows that the
U_{q}(\mathfrak{l}_{S})
-module maps
\mu_{2}\iota_{1}
and
\mu_{2}\iota_{2}
are linearly independent.
Then let us consider

\mu_{2}(1+\sigma_{1}+\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2})\iota_{c_{1},c_{2}}=d_{1}\mu_{2}\iota^{q}_{1}+d_{2}\mu_{2}\iota^{q}_{2}.

Since
\mu_{2}(C^{q}_{2})=\mu_{2}\iota_{2}(\mathfrak{c}^{q}_{3})
, we see that (
7.1
) is satisfied if and only if
d_{1}=c_{1}t_{1,1}+c_{2}t_{2,1}=0
.
We can find a solution with either
c_{1}
or
c_{2}
non-zero, which takes care of the existence of a
U_{q}(\mathfrak{l}_{S})
-module
\mathfrak{u}^{q}_{3}\subset U_{q}(\mathfrak{n})
satisfying the condition on the coproduct.
Hence the only thing left to show is uniqueness of
\mathfrak{u}^{q}_{3}
, for which we need some additional preparation.

The braiding
\sigma
acts on the simple component
\tilde{\mathfrak{u}}^{q}_{2}\subset\mathfrak{u}^{q}_{1}\otimes\mathfrak{u}^{q}_{1}
by a scalar, by Schur’s lemma.
Its value can be computed along the lines of
[
KlSc97
, Section 8.4, Corollary 23]
and takes the form
-q^{s}
for some rational number
s
.
Here the minus sign corresponding to the classical antisymmetry of the component (as in
Proposition

2.2
), while the exponent
s
can be computed explicitly in terms of Lie algebraic data (in our conventions we have
s=c(\lambda_{2})/2-c(\lambda_{1})
, where
\lambda_{1}
and
\lambda_{2}
are the highest weights of
\mathfrak{u}_{1}
and
\mathfrak{u}_{2}
, and
c(\lambda)=(\lambda,\lambda+2\rho)
is the classical value of the Casimir).
All we need to know here is that
s\neq 0
for the cases under consideration, as in
Lemma

A.1
(we omit the details of this verification).

Given this fact, it follows from the definition of
C^{q}_{1}
and
C^{q}_{2}
that we have

\sigma_{1}\iota^{q}_{1}=-q^{s}\iota^{q}_{1},\quad\sigma_{2}\iota^{q}_{2}=-q^{s}\iota^{q}_{2}.

Using the braid equation, it is not difficult to show that
\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}
maps
C^{q}_{1}
to
C^{q}_{2}
and
\sigma_{2}\sigma_{1}
maps
C^{q}_{2}
to
C^{q}_{1}
.
Then there are non-zero coefficients
a_{1}
and
a_{2}
such that

\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}\iota^{q}_{1}=a_{1}\iota^{q}_{2},\quad\sigma_{2}\sigma_{1}\iota^{q}_{2}=a_{2}\iota^{q}_{1}.

Finally let us write
\sigma_{1}\iota^{q}_{2}=b_{1}\iota^{q}_{1}+b_{2}\iota^{q}_{2}
for the decomposition of this
U_{q}(\mathfrak{l}_{S})
-module map.

Then, taking into account these relations, we find the identities

\begin{split}(1+\sigma_{1}+\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2})\iota^{q}_{1}&=(1-q^{s})\iota^{q}_{1}+a_{1}\iota^{q}_{2},\\
(1+\sigma_{1}+\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2})\iota^{q}_{2}&=(1-q^{s})b_{1}\iota^{q}_{1}+(1+(1-q^{s})b_{2})\iota^{q}_{2}.\end{split}

Using the previously introduced notation, this means that
t_{1,1}=1-q^{s}
and
t_{2,1}=(1-q^{s})b_{1}
.
Recall that we need to satisfy the condition
d_{1}=c_{1}t_{1,1}+c_{2}t_{2,1}=0
.
We see that this can be written as
(1-q^{s})(c_{1}+c_{2}b_{1})=0
, which is equivalent to
c_{1}+c_{2}b_{1}=0
.

If
b_{1}\neq 0
then both
c_{1}
and
c_{2}
must be non-zero (since we are excluding the case
c_{1}=c_{2}=0
). In this case
C^{q}_{c_{1},c_{2}}=C^{q}_{-b_{1},1}
is uniquely determined.
Suppose on the contrary that
b_{1}=0
, so that
\sigma_{1}\iota^{q}_{2}=b_{2}\iota^{q}_{2}
. Then we have
\sigma_{2}\sigma_{1}\iota^{q}_{2}=b_{2}\sigma_{2}\iota^{q}_{2}=-q^{s}b_{2}\iota^{q}_{2}
.
On the other hand, we have
\sigma_{2}\sigma_{1}\iota^{q}_{2}=a_{2}\iota_{1}
with
a_{2}\neq 0
. This would imply that
\iota^{q}_{1}
and
\iota^{q}_{2}
are linearly dependent, which is a contradiction (another possibility it to use specialization at
q=1
).
∎

7.5.
Main result

We now come back to the equivariant quantizations
\mathfrak{n}^{q}_{1}\oplus\cdots\oplus\mathfrak{n}^{q}_{r}
from
Proposition

4.3
to investigate the corresponding coideal property.
We recall that each summand
\mathfrak{n}^{q}_{k}
is
\mathbb{N}
-graded by some simple root
\alpha_{i_{k}}
.
In the next result we show that, if each
\mathfrak{n}^{q}_{k}
has at most three graded components, then it can be uniquely chosen in such a way that
\mathbb{C}\oplus\mathfrak{n}^{q}_{k}
is a left
U_{q}(\mathfrak{b})
-coideal.
This restriction can be reformulated in terms of the simple components appearing in the semisimple Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
, as we do below.

Theorem 7.11
.

Let
\mathfrak{g}
be a complex semisimple Lie algebra of rank
r
, which does not contain components of type
F_{4}
,
E_{7}
,
E_{8}
.
Let
S_{1}\subset\cdots\subset S_{r}
be an increasing sequence of proper subsets of
\Pi
, with
\mathfrak{n}=\mathfrak{n}_{1}\oplus\cdots\oplus\mathfrak{n}_{r}
the corresponding decomposition of the positive nilradical.

Then there exists a unique finite-dimensional subspace

\mathfrak{n}^{q}=\mathfrak{n}^{q}_{1}\oplus\cdots\oplus\mathfrak{n}^{q}_{r}\subset U_{q}(\mathfrak{n})

such that each summand
\mathfrak{n}^{q}_{k}
satisfies the following properties:

•

\mathfrak{n}^{q}_{k}
is an
U_{q}(\mathfrak{l}_{k})
-module under
\triangleright
, corresponding to the
\mathfrak{l}_{k}
-module
\mathfrak{n}_{k}
,

•

each graded component
\mathfrak{n}^{q}_{k,n}
is contained in the
n
-fold product of
\mathfrak{n}^{q}_{k,1}
,

•

\mathbb{C}\oplus\mathfrak{n}^{q}_{k}
is a left
U_{q}(\mathfrak{b})
-coideal, that is
\Delta(\mathbb{C}\oplus\mathfrak{n}^{q}_{k})\subset U_{q}(\mathfrak{b})\otimes(\mathbb{C}\oplus\mathfrak{n}^{q}_{k})
.

Proof.

We have already shown in
Proposition

4.3
that an equivariant quantization of
\mathfrak{n}=\mathfrak{n}_{1}\oplus\cdots\oplus\mathfrak{n}_{r}
exists (but is not unique, in general), that is the first two properties are satisfied.
For the coideal property we first recall that, as given in (
2.2
), the sequence
S_{1}\subset\cdots\subset S_{r}
determines a sequence of complex semisimple Lie algebras

\{0\}=\mathfrak{g}_{1}\subset\mathfrak{g}_{2}\subset\cdots\subset\mathfrak{g}_{r}\subset\mathfrak{g}_{r+1}=\mathfrak{g}.

We have that
\mathfrak{n}_{k}\subset\mathfrak{g}_{k+1}
is the positive nilradical corresponding to the inclusion
S_{k}\subset S_{k+1}
, more precisely
S_{k}=S_{k+1}\backslash\{\alpha_{i_{k}}\}
.
In other words, each summand
\mathfrak{n}_{k}
appears as the positive nilradical
\mathfrak{n}_{S}
of a semisimple Lie algebra corresponding to a proper subset
S
obtained by removing one simple root, which is the setting we considered above.

If
\mathfrak{g}
does not contain simple components of type
F_{4}
,
E_{7}
,
E_{8}
, then each summand
\mathfrak{n}_{k}
has graded components of degree at most three, as can be easily deduced by inspecting the highest roots of the various complex simple Lie algebras.
Then by combining
Proposition

7.5
,
Proposition

7.7
and
Proposition

7.10
, which correspond respectively to the cases of degree one, two and three, we deduce that
\mathfrak{n}^{q}_{k}
can be chosen in such a way that
\mathbb{C}\oplus\mathfrak{n}^{q}_{k}
is a left
U_{q}(\mathfrak{b})
-coideal.
This choice is necessarily unique in degrees one and two, while
Proposition

7.10
guarantees that this is the case in degree three as well.
∎

This result refines
Proposition

4.3
and gives a satisfactory quantization of the decomposition
\mathfrak{n}=\mathfrak{n}_{1}\oplus\cdots\oplus\mathfrak{n}_{r}
together with the action of the various Levi factors.
Indeed, under the conditions stated in the result, we have a unique quantization within
U_{q}(\mathfrak{n})
satisfying an analogue of the existence of a Lie algebra structure on each summand
\mathfrak{n}_{k}
.

We strongly suspect that it should be possible to remove the restriction that
\mathfrak{g}
should not contain simple components of type
F_{4}
,
E_{7}
and
E_{8}
.
Doing so would require the analysis of the graded components
\mathfrak{n}^{q}_{k,n}
up to degree
n=6
(but we note that the case of degree four would suffice for
F_{4}
and
E_{7}
).
In principle this analysis can performed following the strategy employed in the degree-three case, but the details get significantly harder.
In particular, it would be desirable to have a more conceptual understanding for the multiplicity of
\mathfrak{n}_{k,n}
inside the tensor product
\mathfrak{n}_{k,1}^{\otimes n}
, rather than resorting to explicit case-by-case checks as in
Lemma

A.1
.
In any case, this leads us to formulate the following conjecture.

Conjecture 7.12
.

The results of
Theorem

7.11
hold for any finite-dimensional complex semisimple Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
, without any restriction on the simple components.

As partial evidence beyond
Theorem

7.11
, we point out that for any semisimple Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
of rank
r
we have the following: for any
k\in\{1,\cdots,r\}
and
n\in\{1,2,3\}
, we have that
\mathbb{C}\oplus\mathfrak{n}^{q}_{k,1}\oplus\cdots\oplus\mathfrak{n}^{q}_{k,n}
is a left
U_{q}(\mathfrak{b})
-coideal.
Indeed, in the proof of
Proposition

7.10
there was no restriction on the semisimple Lie algebra being considered.

8.
The example of
G_{2}

In this section we illustrate the results obtained above in the case of the exceptional Lie algebra
G_{2}
.
In particular, we consider the decomposition
\mathfrak{n}=\mathfrak{n}_{1}\oplus\mathfrak{n}_{2}
such that
\mathfrak{n}_{2}
has graded components up to degree three, which is the most complicated case for the purpose of checking the coideal property.
This is well-suited to illustrating the complexity of the situation, while keeping computations manageable.
In fact, we are going to skip most of the relevant details, only giving indications for how they can be performed.

8.1.
Decomposition

We consider the simple Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}=G_{2}
.
We denote by
\alpha_{1}
the short root and by
\alpha_{2}
the long root, which means that

(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}^{\vee})=-1,\quad(\alpha_{2},\alpha_{1}^{\vee})=-3.

The corresponding positive roots are given by

\Phi^{+}=\{\alpha_{1},\ \alpha_{2},\ \alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2},\ 2\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2},\ 3\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2},\ 3\alpha_{1}+2\alpha_{2}\}.

We consider the decomposition
\mathfrak{n}=\mathfrak{n}_{1}\oplus\mathfrak{n}_{2}
with respect to the sequence
S_{1}\subset S_{2}
with
S_{2}=\{\alpha_{2}\}
.
The corresponding Levi factors
\mathfrak{l}_{1}
and
\mathfrak{l}_{2}
are given by

\mathfrak{l}_{1}=\mathfrak{h},\quad\mathfrak{l}_{2}=\mathfrak{h}\oplus\mathrm{span}\{e_{\alpha_{2}},f_{\alpha_{2}}\}.

We note that the semisimple part of
\mathfrak{l}_{2}
can be identified with
\mathfrak{sl}_{2}
.

For the positive nilradical we have
\mathfrak{n}_{1}=\mathbb{C}e_{\alpha_{2}}
and
\mathfrak{n}_{2}=\mathfrak{n}_{2,1}\oplus\mathfrak{n}_{2,2}\oplus\mathfrak{n}_{2,3}
, where the grading in this case is with respect to
\alpha_{1}
. These three components are given by

\mathfrak{n}_{2,1}=\mathrm{span}\{e_{\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2}},e_{\alpha_{1}}\},\quad\mathfrak{n}_{2,2}=\mathrm{span}\{e_{2\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2}}\},\quad\mathfrak{n}_{2,3}=\mathrm{span}\{e_{3\alpha_{1}+2\alpha_{2}},e_{3\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2}}\}.

These are all
\mathfrak{sl}_{2}
-modules by the adjoint action of the semisimple part of
\mathfrak{l}_{2}
. We note that the components of degrees one and three correspond to the fundamental module, while the component of degree two corresponds to the trivial module.

8.2.
Quantum root vectors

Our goal is to exhibit explicitly the equivariant quantization
\mathfrak{n}^{q}=\mathfrak{n}^{q}_{1}\oplus\mathfrak{n}^{q}_{2}
corresponding to the decomposition above.
We begin by introducing quantum root vectors that are adapted to this situation, as in
Proposition

5.6
.
This means that the longest word
w_{0}
should be factorized as
w_{0}=w_{0,S_{2}}w_{S_{2}}
where
S_{2}=\{\alpha_{2}\}
.
In other words,
w_{0}
should begin with the simple reflection
s_{2}
, giving the following enumeration.

Lemma 8.1
.

Consider
w_{0}=s_{2}s_{1}s_{2}s_{1}s_{2}s_{1}

\begin{gathered}\beta_{1}=\alpha_{2},\ \beta_{2}=\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2},\ \beta_{3}=3\alpha_{1}+2\alpha_{2},\\
\beta_{4}=2\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2},\ \beta_{5}=3\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2},\ \beta_{6}=\alpha_{1}.\end{gathered}

We define the quantum root vectors
E_{\beta_{1}},\cdots,E_{\beta_{6}}
with respect to

as above.
We also note that, with respect to this enumeration, the decomposition of
\mathfrak{n}
can be written as

\mathfrak{n}_{1,1}=\mathfrak{n}_{\beta_{1}},\quad\mathfrak{n}_{2,1}=\mathfrak{n}_{\beta_{2}}\oplus\mathfrak{n}_{\beta_{6}},\quad\mathfrak{n}_{2,2}=\mathfrak{n}_{\beta_{4}},\quad\mathfrak{n}_{2,3}=\mathfrak{n}_{\beta_{3}}\oplus\mathfrak{n}_{\beta_{5}}.

It follows from
Proposition

5.6
that the degree-one components are given by

\mathfrak{n}^{q}_{1,1}=\mathrm{span}\{E_{\beta_{1}}\},\quad\mathfrak{n}^{q}_{2,1}=\mathrm{span}\{E_{\beta_{2}},E_{\beta_{6}}\}.

So it remains to find the higher-degree components
\mathfrak{n}^{q}_{2,2}
and
\mathfrak{n}^{q}_{2,3}
.

Next, we are going to determine the
q
-commutators
[E_{\beta_{i}},E_{\beta_{j}}]_{q}=E_{\beta_{i}}E_{\beta_{j}}-q^{(\beta_{i},\beta_{j})}E_{\beta_{j}}E_{\beta_{i}}
in the case
i<j
.
We recall that, in our conventions for the coproduct, we have the identity
E_{i}\triangleright X=[E_{i},X]_{q}
for the generators of the quantized Levi factor, as in
Proposition

5.4
.

In the next result we use some standard notations related to quantized enveloping algebras, such as the
q
-numbers
[n]_{q}:=\frac{q^{n}-q^{-n}}{q-q^{-1}}
and the divided powers
E_{\beta}^{(n)}:=E_{\beta}^{n}/[n]_{q_{\beta}}!
.

Lemma 8.2
.

The non-zero
q
-commutators
[E_{\beta_{i}},E_{\beta_{j}}]_{q}
with
i<j
are given by

\begin{gathered}_{q}=(q^{6}-q^{4}-q^{2}+1)E_{\beta_{2}}^{(3)},\quad[E_{\beta_{1}},E_{\beta_{4}}]_{q}=(q^{3}-q^{-1})E_{\beta_{2}}^{(2)},\\
_{q}=-(q+q^{-1}-q^{-3})E_{\beta_{3}}+(q-q^{-1})E_{\beta_{2}}E_{\beta_{4}},\\
_{q}=E_{\beta_{2}},\quad[E_{\beta_{2}},E_{\beta_{4}}]_{q}=[3]_{q}E_{\beta_{3}},\quad[E_{\beta_{2}},E_{\beta_{5}}]_{q}=(q^{3}-q^{-1})E_{\beta_{4}}^{(2)},\\

_{q}=(q^{3}-q^{-1})E_{\beta_{4}}^{(2)},\quad[E_{\beta_{4}},E_{\beta_{6}}]_{q}=[3]_{q}E_{\beta_{5}}.{}\lx@close@alignment\end{gathered}

Proof.

These relations can be checked using the package
QuaGroup
available in GAP
[
GAP24
]
.
We warn the reader that the reduced decomposition
w_{0}=s_{2}s_{1}s_{2}s_{1}s_{2}s_{1}
is not the default one for this package, so that it should be specified to reproduce the results above.
∎

Using the
q
-commutators above, we see explicitly that
\mathfrak{n}^{q}_{2,1}=\mathrm{span}\{E_{\beta_{2}},E_{\beta_{6}}\}
is invariant under the left adjoint action of (the positive part of)

Proposition

5.4
.
On the other hand, we also see that
[E_{\beta_{1}},E_{\beta_{4}}]_{q}\neq 0
in the quantum setting, which means that
E_{\beta_{4}}
is not a highest weight vector for the left adjoint action of

. It follows that
E_{\beta_{4}}
is not going to be an element of
\mathfrak{n}^{q}_{2,2}
.
Similar remarks apply to
E_{\beta_{3}}
and
E_{\beta_{5}}
with respect to the degree-three component
\mathfrak{n}^{q}_{2,3}
.

8.3.
Braiding and coideal

Before discussing the higher-degree components
\mathfrak{n}^{q}_{2,2}
and
\mathfrak{n}^{q}_{2,3}
, we determine the braiding on the degree-one component
\mathfrak{n}^{q}_{2,1}=\mathrm{span}\{E_{\beta_{2}},E_{\beta_{6}}\}
.

Lemma 8.3
.

The braiding
\sigma:\mathfrak{n}^{q}_{2,1}\otimes\mathfrak{n}^{q}_{2,1}\to\mathfrak{n}^{q}_{2,1}\otimes\mathfrak{n}^{q}_{2,1}
is given by

\begin{split}\sigma(E_{\beta_{2}}\otimes E_{\beta_{2}})&=q^{2}E_{\beta_{2}}\otimes E_{\beta_{2}},\\
\sigma(E_{\beta_{2}}\otimes E_{\beta_{6}})&=q^{-1}E_{\beta_{6}}\otimes E_{\beta_{2}}+(q^{3}-q^{-3})q^{-1}E_{\beta_{2}}\otimes E_{\beta_{6}},\\
\sigma(E_{\beta_{6}}\otimes E_{\beta_{2}})&=q^{-1}E_{\beta_{2}}\otimes E_{\beta_{6}},\\
\sigma(E_{\beta_{6}}\otimes E_{\beta_{6}})&=q^{2}E_{\beta_{6}}\otimes E_{\beta_{6}}.\end{split}

Proof.

Follows from routine computations.
Note that in our conventions for the braiding
\sigma
we have
\sigma(E_{\beta_{i}}\otimes E_{\beta_{2}})=q^{(\beta_{i},\beta_{2})}E_{\beta_{2}}\otimes E_{\beta_{i}}
and
\sigma(E_{\beta_{6}}\otimes E_{\beta_{i}})=q^{(\beta_{6},\beta_{i})}E_{\beta_{i}}\otimes E_{\beta_{6}}
, since
E_{\beta_{2}}
and
E_{\beta_{6}}
are respectively the highest and lowest weight vectors.
∎

To determine the component
\mathfrak{n}^{q}_{2,2}
of degree two, we look for a simple component corresponding to the trivial module
\mathfrak{n}_{2,2}
inside the tensor product
\mathfrak{n}^{q}_{2,1}\otimes\mathfrak{n}^{q}_{2,1}
.
Also recall that this component is uniquely determined, as discussed in
Corollary

4.4
.

Lemma 8.4
.

The element

Y_{\beta_{4}}=E_{\beta_{2}}\otimes E_{\beta_{6}}-q^{3}E_{\beta_{6}}\otimes E_{\beta_{2}}

is invariant under the left adjoint action of
U_{q}(\mathfrak{l}_{2})
and satisfies
\sigma(Y_{\beta_{4}})=-q^{-4}Y_{\beta_{4}}
.

Next, we denote by
C^{q}_{1}
and
C^{q}_{2}
the two simple components corresponding to
\mathfrak{n}_{2,3}
inside the tensor product
(\mathfrak{n}^{q}_{2,1})^{\otimes 3}
, using the same notation as in the proof of
Proposition

7.10
.
Then it is immediate to check that we have the lowest weight vectors

L_{1}=Y_{\beta_{4}}\otimes E_{\beta_{6}}\in C^{q}_{1},\quad L_{2}=E_{\beta_{6}}\otimes Y_{\beta_{4}}\in C^{q}_{2}.

As discussed in
Proposition

7.10
, we have that
1+\sigma_{1}+\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}
maps
C^{q}_{1}\oplus C^{q}_{2}
into itself.
More precisely, we have the following identities.

Lemma 8.5
.

We have

\begin{split}(1+\sigma_{1}+\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2})(L_{1})&=(1-q^{-4})L_{1}+qL_{2},\\
(1+\sigma_{1}+\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2})(L_{2})&=(q^{-1}-q^{-5})L_{1}+(q^{2}+1-q^{-2})L_{2}.\end{split}

In particular, using these identities we obtain

(1+\sigma_{1}+\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2})(L_{1}-qL_{2})=-(q^{3}-q^{-1})L_{2}\in C^{q}_{2}.

Denote by
\mathfrak{n}^{q}_{2,3}
the
U_{q}(\mathfrak{l}_{2})
-module generated by the lowest weight vector
L_{1}-qL_{2}
.
Then
Proposition

7.10
guarantees that
\mathbb{C}\oplus\mathfrak{n}^{q}_{2}
is a left coideal.

8.4.
Results

We summarize the computations above in the next proposition.

Proposition 8.6
.

Define the elements

\displaystyle X_{\beta_{1}}

\displaystyle=E_{\beta_{1}},

\displaystyle X_{\beta_{2}}

\displaystyle=E_{\beta_{2}},

\displaystyle X_{\beta_{3}}

\displaystyle=E_{\beta_{1}}X_{\beta_{5}}-q^{-3}X_{\beta_{5}}E_{\beta_{1}},

\displaystyle X_{\beta_{5}}

X_{\beta_{5}}=L_{1}-qL_{2}

With respect to the chosen basis of

\begin{gathered}X_{\beta_{3}}=-q^{3}[3]_{q}!E_{\beta_{3}}+(q-q^{-1})q^{2}[2]_{q}E_{\beta_{2}}E_{\beta_{4}},\quad X_{\beta_{4}}=q^{4}[2]_{q}E_{\beta_{4}}-(q^{4}-1)E_{\beta_{2}}E_{\beta_{6}},\\

∎

We observe that they are all given as

9.
Covariant differential calculi

Consider the unique equivariant quantization
\mathfrak{n}^{q}=\mathfrak{n}^{q}_{1}\oplus\cdots\oplus\mathfrak{n}^{q}_{r}
corresponding to the sequence
S_{1}\subset\cdots\subset S_{r}
constructed in the previous sections.
We show how this construction can be used to produce covariant first-order differential calculi on the quantum flag manifolds
\mathbb{C}_{q}[G/P_{S_{k}}]
, in a manner compatible with the fixed sequence.
Moreover, we show that these calculi reduce to those introduced by Heckenberger-Kold in the irreducible case.

9.1.
Quantum homogeneous spaces

We briefly recall the class of quantum homogeneous spaces considered in
[
HeKo03
]
, although we are only concerned with the special case of quantum flag manifolds, which are defined with respect to Hopf subalgebras.

Let
U
be a Hopf algebra over
\mathbb{C}
with bijective antipode and
K\subset U
be a right coideal subalgebra.
Consider a tensor category
\mathcal{C}
of finite-dimensional left
U
-modules.
Let
\mathcal{A}=U^{\circ}_{\mathcal{C}}
denote the dual Hopf algebra generated by the matrix coefficients of all left
U
-modules in
\mathcal{C}
. Assume that
\mathcal{A}
separates the elements of
U
and that its antipode is bijective.

In this setting, we define a left coideal subalgebra
\mathcal{B}\subset\mathcal{A}
by

\mathcal{B}=\{b\in\mathcal{A}:b_{(1)}b_{(2)}(k)=\varepsilon(k)b,\ \forall k\in K\}.

We assume furthermore that
K
is
\mathcal{C}
-semisimple, which implies that
\mathcal{A}
is a faithfully flat
\mathcal{B}
-module. Then we call
\mathcal{B}
a
quantum homogeneous space
.

9.2.
Differential calculi

The notion of differential calculus on an algebra is fairly standard.
Here we are only concerned with the first-order part of such a calculus, as follows.

Definition 9.1
.

A
first-order differential calculus
(FODC) over an algebra
\mathcal{B}
is a
\mathcal{B}
-bimodule
\Gamma
together with a linear map
\mathrm{d}:\mathcal{B}\to\Gamma
such that
\Gamma=\mathrm{span}\{a\mathrm{d}bc:a,b,c\in\mathcal{B}\}
and
\mathrm{d}
satisfies the Leibnitz rule
\mathrm{d}(ab)=\mathrm{d}ab+a\mathrm{d}b
.

Suppose furthermore that
\mathcal{A}
is a Hopf algebra and that
\mathcal{B}
is a left
\mathcal{A}
-comodule algebra, where we write
\Delta_{\mathcal{B}}:\mathcal{B}\to\mathcal{A}\otimes\mathcal{B}
for the coaction.
In this setting we have the notion of covariant differential calculus, as defined by Woronowicz
[
Wor89
]
.

Definition 9.2
.

With notation as above, a FODC
\Gamma
over
\mathcal{B}
is
left-covariant
if
\Gamma
possesses the structure of a left
\mathcal{A}
-comodule
\Delta_{\Gamma}:\Gamma\to\mathcal{A}\otimes\Gamma
such that

\Delta_{\Gamma}(a\mathrm{d}bc)=\Delta_{\mathcal{B}}(a)\cdot(\mathrm{id}\otimes\mathrm{d})\Delta_{\mathcal{B}}(b)\cdot\Delta_{\mathcal{B}}(c).

In the classical setting this corresponds to
\mathcal{A}=\mathbb{C}[G]
and
\mathcal{B}=\mathbb{C}[G/K]
, with the coaction arising by dualizing the corresponding to the action of
G
on forms of
G/K
.

9.3.
Tangent spaces

By a result of Hermisson
[
Her02
]
, (finite-dimensional) left-covariant FODCs over
\mathcal{B}
are in one-to-one correspondence with certain right ideals of
\mathcal{B}
.
For various reasons, it is more convenient to work with the corresponding
quantum tangent spaces
, which are certain subspaces of the dual coalgebra
\mathcal{B}^{\circ}
, as discussed in
[
HeKo03
, Section 4]
.

Rather than spelling out their precise definition, we recall the following result.

Proposition 9.3

(
[
HeKo03
, Corollary 5]
)
.

Let
\mathcal{B}\subset\mathcal{A}
be as described above. Then there is a canonical one-to-one correspondence between
n
-dimensional left-covariant FODCs over
\mathcal{B}
and
(n+1)
-dimensional subspaces
T^{\varepsilon}\subset\mathcal{B}^{\circ}
such that

\varepsilon\in T^{\varepsilon},\quad\Delta(T^{\varepsilon})\subset T^{\varepsilon}\otimes\mathcal{B}^{\circ},\quad KT^{\varepsilon}\subset T^{\varepsilon}.

With respect to this correspondence, the quantum tangent space to a given FODC is the subspace of
T^{\varepsilon}
consisting of elements vanishing at the unit of
\mathcal{B}
.

Furthermore, this result shows that the classification of finite-dimensional left-covariant FODCs over
\mathcal{B}
requires the determination of the vector space
\{f\in\mathcal{B}^{\circ}:\dim Kf<\infty\}
, which is called the
locally-finite part
of the dual coalgebra
\mathcal{B}^{\circ}
in
[
HeKo03
]
.

In general it can be fairly complicated to work with the dual coalgebra
\mathcal{B}^{\circ}
.
It would be more convenient to work with subspaces of
U
satisfying analogous properties to
Proposition

9.3
, where we note that each
u\in U
can be considered as a linear functional on
\mathcal{B}
by
b\mapsto b(u)
.
We can do so at the cost of losing the one-to-one correspondence, so that different subspaces
T
and
T^{\prime}
of
U
might give rise to the same left-covariant FODC over
\mathcal{B}
.
Since this is not an issue for our purposes, we consider this setting in the following, as in the next lemma.

Lemma 9.4
.

With notation as above, suppose that
K
is a Hopf subalgebra of
U
.
Let
T\subset U
be a finite-dimensional subspace satisfying the properties

1\in T,\quad\Delta(T)\subset T\otimes U,\quad K\triangleright T\subset T.

Then mapping
T
to
\mathcal{B}^{\circ}
gives rise to a left-covariant FODC over
\mathcal{B}
.

Proof.

As described above, we can consider any
u\in U
as a linear functional on
\mathcal{B}
by
b\mapsto b(u)
.
In this way we obtain a map from
U
to the dual of
\mathcal{B}
, which is not injective in general.
It is a general fact that the image of this map is in
\mathcal{B}^{\circ}
, due to
U
being a coalgebra.

Under this map, the unit
1\in U
corresponds to the counit
\varepsilon
on
\mathcal{B}
.
Furthermore, writing
\tilde{T}
for the image of
T
in
\mathcal{B}^{\circ}
, the condition
\Delta(T)\subset T\otimes U
translates to
\Delta(\tilde{T})\subset\tilde{T}\otimes\mathcal{B}^{\circ}
.

For the last condition, consider
k\in K
,
u\in U
and
b\in\mathcal{B}
.
Using the identity
xy=(x_{(1)}\triangleright y)x_{(2)}
and the fact that
K
is a Hopf subalgebra, we compute

b(ku)=b((k_{(1)}\triangleright u)k_{(2)})=\varepsilon(k_{(2)})b(k_{(1)}\triangleright u)=b(k\triangleright u).

Here we used the fact that
\mathcal{B}
is invariant under
K
.
This shows that
ku=k\triangleright u
as elements of
\mathcal{B}^{\circ}
, which gives
K\tilde{T}\subset\tilde{T}
.
Then the result follows from
Proposition

9.3
.
∎

This formulation brings us in close contact with the corresponding properties satisfied by the equivariant quantizations of
\mathfrak{n}=\mathfrak{n}_{1}\oplus\cdots\oplus\mathfrak{n}_{r}
.

9.4.
Main result

In this section we discuss how to use our equivariant quantizations of
\mathfrak{n}
to produce covariant FODCs on
\mathbb{C}_{q}[G]
and certain quantum flag manifolds.
We consider the setting of
[
HeKo03
]
and compare our results with
[
HeKo06
]
, where the authors introduce canonical covariant differential calculi on quantum
irreducible
flag manifolds.

In order to do this we need to switch conventions, since these references work with right coideals and also use the opposite coproduct with respect to ours, that is

\Delta^{\mathrm{op}}(K_{i})=K_{i}\otimes K_{i},\quad\Delta^{\mathrm{op}}(E_{i})=E_{i}\otimes K_{i}+1\otimes E_{i},\quad\Delta^{\mathrm{op}}(F_{i})=F_{i}\otimes 1+K_{i}^{-1}\otimes F_{i}.

To switch to this setting, we consider the quantum analogue of the Chevalley involution, which is the Hopf algebra isomorphism
\omega:U_{q}(\mathfrak{g})\to U_{q}(\mathfrak{g})^{\mathrm{cop}}
given by

\omega(K_{i})=K_{i}^{-1},\quad\omega(E_{i})=F_{i},\quad\omega(F_{i})=E_{i}.

Here
U_{q}(\mathfrak{g})^{\mathrm{cop}}
denotes
U_{q}(\mathfrak{g})
with the opposite coproduct.
We also denote by
U_{q}(\mathfrak{n}_{-})
the quantization corresponding to the negative nilradical
\mathfrak{n}_{-}
, or more concretely the subalgebra of
U_{q}(\mathfrak{g})
generated by the elements
\{F_{i}\}_{i=1}^{r}
.

Lemma 9.5
.

The map
\omega:U_{q}(\mathfrak{g})\to U_{q}(\mathfrak{g})^{\mathrm{cop}}
sends left
U_{q}(\mathfrak{g})
-coideals of
U_{q}(\mathfrak{n})\subset U_{q}(\mathfrak{g})
to right
U_{q}(\mathfrak{g})
-coideals of
U_{q}(\mathfrak{n}_{-})\subset U_{q}(\mathfrak{g})^{\mathrm{cop}}
.

Proof.

It is clear that
\omega
sends left coideals to right coideals, since it is a coalgebra map.
Finally
\omega(U_{q}(\mathfrak{n}))=U_{q}(\mathfrak{n}_{-})
by definition of
\omega
.
∎

With these preparations in place, we can state our main result for this section.
We note that the restrictions on
\mathfrak{g}
are exactly those from
Theorem

7.11
, which guarantee that
\mathbb{C}\oplus\mathfrak{n}^{q}
is a left
U_{q}(\mathfrak{g})
-coideal (the general result would follow from
Conjecture

7.12
).

Theorem 9.6
.

Let
\mathfrak{g}
be a complex semisimple Lie algebra of rank
r
, which does not contain components of type
F_{4},E_{7},E_{8}
.
For any increasing sequence
S_{1}\subset\cdots\subset S_{r}
of proper subsets of
\Pi
, consider the equivariant quantization

\mathfrak{n}^{q}=\mathfrak{n}^{q}_{1}\oplus\cdots\oplus\mathfrak{n}^{q}_{r}

of the positive nilradical as in
Theorem

7.11
.

Write
\mathcal{T}_{-}=\mathbb{C}\oplus\omega(\mathfrak{n}^{q})
and
\mathcal{T}_{+}=\mathbb{C}\oplus\omega(\mathfrak{n}^{q})^{*}
. Then:

(1)

\mathcal{T}_{-}
and
\mathcal{T}_{+}
correspond to left-covariant FODCs on
\mathbb{C}_{q}[G]
.

(2)

They also give left-covariant FODCs on
\mathbb{C}_{q}[G/P_{S_{k}}]
for any
k\in\{1,\cdots,r\}
.

Proof.

(1) By
Theorem

7.11
the finite-dimensional subspace
\mathbb{C}\oplus\mathfrak{n}^{q}\subset U_{q}(\mathfrak{n})
is a left
U_{q}(\mathfrak{g})
-coideal.
Hence
\mathcal{T}_{-}=\mathbb{C}\oplus\omega(\mathfrak{n}^{q})\subset U_{q}(\mathfrak{n}_{-})
is a right
U_{q}(\mathfrak{g})
-coideal containing the unit.
By
Lemma

9.4
this gives a left-covariant FODC over
\mathbb{C}_{q}[G]=U_{q}(\mathfrak{g})^{\circ}
(with respect to the trivial Hopf subalgebra
K=\mathbb{C}
).
The situation is similar for
\mathcal{T}_{+}=\mathbb{C}\oplus\omega(\mathfrak{n}^{q})^{*}\subset U_{q}(\mathfrak{b})
, where in this case we use the fact that the coproduct is a
*
-homomorphism.

(2) We begin by recalling the relevant classical facts concerning the decomposition
\mathfrak{n}=\mathfrak{n}_{1}\oplus\cdots\oplus\mathfrak{n}_{r}
, as discussed in
Section

2.2
.
We write
\mathfrak{l}_{k}=\mathfrak{l}_{S_{k}}
for the Levi factor corresponding to the subset
S_{k}\subset\Pi
and recall that
\mathfrak{n}_{k-1}\subset\mathfrak{l}_{k}
.
Since
\mathfrak{l}_{1}\subset\cdots\subset\mathfrak{l}_{r}
, we find that:

•

for
j\geq k
, we have that
\mathfrak{n}_{j}
is a
\mathfrak{l}_{k}
-module,

•

for
j<k
, we have the inclusion
\mathfrak{n}_{j}\subset\mathfrak{l}_{k}
.

The situation is similar in the quantum setting.
We have
U_{q}(\mathfrak{l}_{1})\subset\cdots\subset U_{q}(\mathfrak{l}_{r})
, since the quantized Levi factor
U_{q}(\mathfrak{l}_{S})
is generated by the elements
\{K_{i}^{\pm}\}_{i=1}^{r}
and
\{E_{i},F_{i}\}_{i\in S}
.
Keeping in mind the construction of
\mathfrak{n}^{q}_{k}
from
Proposition

4.3
, we find that:

•

for
j\geq k
, we have that
\mathfrak{n}^{q}_{j}
is a
U_{q}(\mathfrak{l}_{k})
-module under
\triangleright
,

•

for
j<k
, we have the inclusion
\mathfrak{n}^{q}_{j}\subset U_{q}(\mathfrak{l}_{k})
.

Now consider the quantum flag manifold
\mathcal{B}=\mathbb{C}_{q}[G/P_{S_{k}}]
, which is defined as the subalgebra of
\mathcal{A}=\mathbb{C}_{q}[G]
invariant under the Hopf subalgebra
K=U_{q}(\mathfrak{l}_{k})
.
As observed above, for
j\geq k
we have
U_{q}(\mathfrak{l}_{k})\triangleright\mathfrak{n}^{q}_{j}\subseteq\mathfrak{n}^{q}_{j}
.
On the other hand, for
j<k
we have
\mathfrak{n}^{q}_{j}\equiv 0
as linear functionals on
\mathcal{B}
, since
\mathfrak{n}^{q}_{j}\subset U_{q}(\mathfrak{l}_{k})
and
\varepsilon(\mathfrak{n}^{q}_{j})=0
.
It follows that the image of
\mathbb{C}\oplus\mathfrak{n}^{q}
in
\mathcal{B}^{\circ}
is invariant under the left-adjoint action of
K=U_{q}(\mathfrak{l}_{k})
.
The same is true for
\mathcal{T}_{-}
, since
\omega
is an algebra map, and for
\mathcal{T}_{+}
, since
U_{q}(\mathfrak{l}_{k})
is a Hopf
*
-subalgebra (and we have
x\triangleright y^{*}=(S(x)^{*}\triangleright y)^{*}
).
Then
\mathcal{T}_{-}
and
\mathcal{T}_{+}
give rise to left-covariant FODCs on
\mathbb{C}_{q}[G/P_{S_{k}}]
by
Lemma

9.4
.
∎

Finally we want to compare this construction with the results of
[
HeKo06
]
, which we briefly summarize.
For any quantum
irreducible
flag manifold
\mathbb{C}_{q}[G/P_{S}]
, the authors construct two left-covariant (irreducible) FODCs
\Gamma_{\partial}
and
\Gamma_{\bar{\partial}}
, corresponding classically to the holomorphic and anti-holomorphic parts of the differential calculus over
G/P_{S}
.
We recall that irreducible flag manifolds arise from
cominuscule
parabolic subalgebras, which correspond to subsets
S=\Pi\backslash\{\alpha_{s}\}
with
\alpha_{s}
appearing with coefficient
one
in the highest root of
\mathfrak{g}
.

Hence we want to consider the case of
\mathfrak{g}
with an increasing sequence
S_{1}\subset\cdots\subset S_{r}
such that
S_{r}=\Pi\backslash\{\alpha_{i_{r}}\}
and
\alpha_{i_{r}}
appears with coefficient one in the highest root of
\mathfrak{g}
(let us also note in passing that
F_{4}
,
E_{7}
,
E_{8}
do not admit any irreducible flag manifolds).

Proposition 9.7
.

For any increasing sequence
S_{1}\subset\cdots S_{r}
as described above, consider the left-covariant FODCs
\mathcal{T}_{-}
and
\mathcal{T}_{+}
on
\mathbb{C}_{q}[G/P_{S_{r}}]
constructed in
Theorem

9.6
.
They coincide with the FODCs
\Gamma_{\partial}
and
\Gamma_{\bar{\partial}}
of Heckenberger-Kolb.

Proof.

Write
\mathcal{B}=\mathbb{C}_{q}[G/P_{S_{r}}]
and consider
\mathfrak{n}^{q}=\mathfrak{n}^{q}_{1}\oplus\cdots\oplus\mathfrak{n}^{q}_{r}
, corresponding to a sequence
S_{1}\subset\cdots\subset S_{r}
as described above.
We have
\mathfrak{n}^{q}_{1}\oplus\cdots\oplus\mathfrak{n}^{q}_{r-1}\subset U_{q}(\mathfrak{l}_{S_{r}})
, as already discussed in
Theorem

9.6
. These elements vanish when mapped to
\mathcal{B}^{\circ}
, since
\mathcal{B}
is defined by invariance under
U_{q}(\mathfrak{l}_{S_{r}})
. Hence
\mathfrak{n}^{q}|_{\mathcal{B}}=\mathfrak{n}^{q}_{r}|_{\mathcal{B}}
and the same is true for
\omega(\mathfrak{n}^{q})
.

In the cominuscule case all the radical roots contain
\alpha_{i_{r}}
with coefficient one, which means that the summand
\mathfrak{n}_{r}
has only a component of degree one.
For its quantum counterpart
\mathfrak{n}^{q}_{r}
, this means that is spanned by the quantum root vectors
\{E_{\xi_{1}},\cdots,E_{\xi_{n}}\}
corresponding to the radical roots, as in
Proposition

5.6
.
By applying
\omega
to
\mathfrak{n}^{q}_{r}
, we find that
\omega(\mathfrak{n}^{q}_{r})
is spanned by the quantum root vectors
\{F_{\xi_{1}},\cdots,F_{\xi_{n}}\}
.
To check this, we can use the identity
\omega(T_{i}(X_{\alpha}))=c_{i,\alpha}T_{i}(\omega(X_{\alpha}))
, which is valid for any weight element
X_{\alpha}\in U_{q}(\mathfrak{g})
(for some coefficient
c_{i,\alpha}
that depends only on
i
and
\alpha
).
By
[
HeKo06
, Proposition 3.3(v)]
, the quantum tangent space for the holomorphic part
\Gamma_{\partial}
of the FODC is
\mathrm{span}\{F_{\xi_{1}},\cdots,F_{\xi_{n}}\}
.

Next consider
\omega(\mathfrak{n}^{q}_{r})^{*}=\mathrm{span}\{F_{\xi_{1}}^{*},\cdots,F_{\xi_{n}}^{*}\}
.
The quantum tangent space for the anti-holomorphic part
\Gamma_{\bar{\partial}}
, which appears in
[
HeKo06
, Proposition 3.4(v)]
, is given instead as
\mathrm{span}\{E_{\xi_{1}},\cdots,E_{\xi_{n}}\}
.
The two vector spaces appear different at first sight, and it is certainly not true that
F_{\xi_{k}}^{*}
equals
E_{\xi_{k}}
in
U_{q}(\mathfrak{g})
. However the two should coincide as elements of
\mathcal{B}^{\circ}
, since in this case the Cartan part acts trivially.
Rather than proceeding this way, we can make use of
[
Mat19
, Theorem 4.2]
, where it is shown that the FODC
\Gamma=\Gamma_{\partial}\oplus\Gamma_{\bar{\partial}}
is a
*
-calculus over
\mathcal{B}
.
In this case the quantum tangent space must be invariant under
*
, see for instance
[
KlSc97
, Section 14.1.2, Proposition 6]
.
This gives the result.
∎

Hence the FODCs introduced here over the quantum flag manifolds
\mathbb{C}_{q}[G/P_{S_{k}}]
generalize those of Heckenberger-Kolb beyond the irreducible case.
The study of further properties of these calculi is left for future investigations.
We only briefly mention that they should have classical dimension, since by specialization at
q=1
the quantum tangent spaces considered here should reduce to the classical tangent spaces.
Finally, we should also mention that a similar analysis has recently appeared in
[
ÓBSo23
]
for the
A_{r}
series.
