Cauchy Pairs
Martin Himmel

Abstract
. The notion of pairable functions is introduced and some of its properties are developed. In this connection the famous Euler identity is interpreted as a property of certain pairable functions and finite cyclic groups.

1.
Introduction

In this note we start with a prelude on Euler´s reflection formula linking the Gamma function to trigonometric functions.
This allows us to reformulate Euler´s identity in terms of the Gamma function evaluated at certain affine functions summing to one.
We continue with a generalization of the classical Gamma function based on the original version due to Euler.
But the main topic of this paper is the notion of pairability of functions,
which in some sense is the the idea to translate or scale a given function under the condition that a certain functional equation is satisfied.

1.1.
Reflection formula for cosine function and a reformulation of Euler’s identity

The well-known reflection formula

\Gamma{\left(z\right)}\Gamma{\left(1-z\right)}=\frac{\pi}{\sin\left(\pi z\right)},\qquad z\in{\mathbb{C}}\setminus-{\mathbb{N}}_{0}

linking the Euler Gamma function and the sine function has a beautiful analogon for the cosine function.
Substituting here
z
by
\frac{1}{2}-z
, we get

\Gamma{\left(\frac{1}{2}-z\right)}\Gamma{\left(\frac{1}{2}+z\right)}=\frac{\pi}{\cos\left(\pi z\right)}.

Rearranging these equations for sine and cosine, respectively, gives us

\sin{z}=\frac{\pi}{\Gamma{\left(\frac{z}{\pi}\right)}\Gamma{\left(1-\frac{z}{\pi}\right)}},

and

\cos{z}=\frac{\pi}{\Gamma{\left(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{z}{\pi}\right)}\Gamma{\left(\frac{1}{2}+\frac{z}{\pi}\right)}},

and thus

\tan{z}=\frac{\Gamma{\left(\frac{z}{\pi}\right)}\Gamma{\left(1-\frac{z}{\pi}\right)}}{\Gamma{\left(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{z}{\pi}\right)}\Gamma{\left(\frac{1}{2}+\frac{z}{\pi}\right)}}

Hence, we may express the Euler identity
e^{iz}=\cos{z}+i\sin{z}
without explicitly using trigonometric functions by

e^{iz}=\frac{\pi}{\Gamma{\left(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{z}{\pi}\right)}\Gamma{\left(\frac{1}{2}+\frac{z}{\pi}\right)}}+i\frac{\pi}{\Gamma{\left(\frac{z}{\pi}\right)}\Gamma{\left(1-\frac{z}{\pi}\right)}}.

Substituting now
z
by
-z
gives us

e^{-iz}=\frac{\pi}{\Gamma{\left(\frac{1}{2}+\frac{z}{\pi}\right)}\Gamma{\left(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{z}{\pi}\right)}}+i\frac{\pi}{\Gamma{\left(\frac{-z}{\pi}\right)}\Gamma{\left(1+\frac{z}{\pi}\right)}}.

Multiplying these two equations with each other (
e^{iz}e^{-iz}=1=\ldots
), we obtain

1=\left(\frac{\pi}{\Gamma{\left(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{z}{\pi}\right)}\Gamma{\left(\frac{1}{2}+\frac{z}{\pi}\right)}}+i\frac{\pi}{\Gamma{\left(\frac{z}{\pi}\right)}\Gamma{\left(1-\frac{z}{\pi}\right)}}\right)\left(\frac{\pi}{\Gamma{\left(\frac{1}{2}+\frac{z}{\pi}\right)}\Gamma{\left(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{z}{\pi}\right)}}+i\frac{\pi}{\Gamma{\left(\frac{-z}{\pi}\right)}\Gamma{\left(1+\frac{z}{\pi}\right)}}\right).

Sorting now by real and immaginary part gives us the following two equations

1=\frac{\pi}{\Gamma{\left(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{z}{\pi}\right)}\Gamma{\left(\frac{1}{2}+\frac{z}{\pi}\right)}}\cdot\frac{\pi}{\Gamma{\left(\frac{1}{2}+\frac{z}{\pi}\right)}\Gamma{\left(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{z}{\pi}\right)}}-\frac{\pi}{\Gamma{\left(\frac{z}{\pi}\right)}\Gamma{\left(1-\frac{z}{\pi}\right)}}\cdot\frac{\pi}{\Gamma{\left(\frac{-z}{\pi}\right)}\Gamma{\left(1+\frac{z}{\pi}\right)}},

and

0=\frac{\pi}{\Gamma{\left(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{z}{\pi}\right)}\Gamma{\left(\frac{1}{2}+\frac{z}{\pi}\right)}}\cdot\frac{\pi}{\Gamma{\left(\frac{-z}{\pi}\right)}\Gamma{\left(1+\frac{z}{\pi}\right)}}+\frac{\pi}{\Gamma{\left(\frac{z}{\pi}\right)}\Gamma{\left(1-\frac{z}{\pi}\right)}}\cdot\frac{\pi}{\Gamma{\left(\frac{1}{2}+\frac{z}{\pi}\right)}\Gamma{\left(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{z}{\pi}\right)}}

On the other hand, the trigonometric version of the well-known Pythagorean theorem gives us

\frac{1}{\left(\Gamma\left(\frac{z}{\pi}\right)\Gamma\left(1-\frac{z}{\pi}\right)\right)^{2}}+\frac{1}{\left(\Gamma\left(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{z}{\pi}\right)\Gamma\left(\frac{1}{2}+\frac{z}{\pi}\right)\right)^{2}}=\frac{1}{\pi^{2}}.

2.
Euler Gamma function

The Euler Gamma function
\Gamma:(0,+\infty)\to(0,+\infty)
is usually defined by

\Gamma(x)=\int_{0}^{\infty}{e^{-t}t^{x-1}\,dt},\qquad x\in(0,+\infty).

An equivalent formulation,

\Gamma(x)=\int_{0}^{1}\left({-\log{t}}\right)^{x-1}\,dt,\qquad x\in(0,+\infty),

shows us a connection to the classical logarithm function, a regular solution to the Cauchy functional equation of logarithmic type.
This motivates us to introduce the following

Definition 1
.

Let
I\subset{\mathbb{R}}
be an interval and
\varphi:I\to(0,+\infty)
an integrable function.
We call

(2.1)

\Gamma_{\varphi}(x)=\int_{0}^{1}\left({\varphi{(t)}}\right)^{x-1}\,dt,\qquad x\in I,

the Gamma function of generator
\varphi
.

In this light, the Euler Gamma function equals the Gamma function of generator negative logarithm
1
1

1

For any
a>0
, the real logarithmic function
x\mapsto\log_{a}{x}
is negative for
x\in(0,a)
and positive for
x\in(a,+\infty)
. Thus, the Gamma integral is well-defined.
,
i.e.
\Gamma_{-\log}=\Gamma
.

2.1.
Gamma function of regular Cauchy generator

What kind of functions are obtained when
the generator
\varphi
in (
2.1
) is a regular solution to one of the other three Cauchy equations
is discussed now briefly.

When
\varphi
is a regular solution the Cauchy function equation of exponential type, i.e., if there is
a>0
such that
\varphi(t)=a^{t}
for all
t\in{\mathbb{R}}
,
we obtain

\Gamma_{\exp}(x)=\int_{0}^{1}\left({a^{t}}\right)^{x-1}\,dt,\qquad x\in{\mathbb{R}},

as the Gamma function of exponential generator.
Since the integration here can be performed explicity,
we have, for all
x\in{\mathbb{R}}
,
x\neq 1
,

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\exp}(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left.\frac{{\left(a^{t}\right)}^{x-1}}{(x-1)\log{a}}\right|_{0}^{1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{a^{x-1}-1}{(x-1)\log{a}},

and
\Gamma_{\exp}(1)=1
.

If
\varphi
is a regular solution the Cauchy functional equation of additive type, which means that there is
c\in{\mathbb{R}}
such that
\varphi(t)=ct
for all
t\in{\mathbb{R}}
,
we obtain, for all
x\in{\mathbb{R}}
,
x\neq 0
,

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\add}(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\left({c{t}}\right)^{x-1}\,dt,

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left.\frac{(ct)^{x}}{cx}\right|_{0}^{1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{c^{x-1}}{x},

as the Gamma function of additive generator.
For obvious reasons
c
must be positive here.

If
\varphi
is a regular solution to the Cauchy functional equation of logarithmic type, i.e.,
there is
c\in{\mathbb{R}}
such that
\varphi(t)=c\log{t}
for all
t\in(0,+\infty)
,
we obtain, for all
x\in{\mathbb{R}}\setminus(-{\mathbb{N}})
,

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\log}(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\left({c\log{t}}\right)^{x-1}\,dt,

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(-c)^{x-1}\Gamma(x),

as the Gamma function of logarithmic generator, and
c
is restricted to be negative.

If
\varphi
is a regular solution to the Cauchy functional equation of multiplicative type,
which means that there is
p\in{\mathbb{R}}
such that
\varphi(t)={t}^{p}
for all
t\in(0,+\infty)
,
we obtain, for all
x\in{\mathbb{R}}
,

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\pow}(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\left({{t}^{p}}\right)^{x-1}\,dt,

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left.\frac{t^{p(x-1)+1}}{p(x-1)+1}\right|_{0}^{1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{p(x-1)+1},

as the Gamma function of multiplicative generator, where
x\neq 1-\frac{1}{p}
for
p\neq 0
and
\Gamma_{\pow}\equiv 1
for
p=0
.

3.
Pairability

In this paper we mainly deal with the functional equation

(S)

f(x+y)=f(x)g(y)+f(y)g(x)

coming from the sine addition formula.
It is known that the pair of real trigonometric functions
(f,g)=(\sin,\cos)
satisfy equation (
S
) on
{\mathbb{R}}
.
Moreover, it holds
\cos{x}=\sin{\left(x+\frac{\pi}{2}\right)}
for all
x\in{\mathbb{R}}
.
This motivates us to deal with the question whether, for a given function
f
, there is some (possibly non-constant) period
T
such that

g(x)=f(x+T)
for all suitable
x
, and the pair
(f,g)
satisfies (
S
).
To make things more precise, we start with the following

Definition 2
.

Let
I\subset{\mathbb{R}}
be an interval closed under addition and
f,g:I\to{\mathbb{R}}
be functions.
The function
f
is called
pairable
with
g
with respect to the functional equation (
S
),
if there is some
T:I^{2}\to{\mathbb{R}}
, called period function, such that
g(x)=f(x+T)
for all
x\in I
,
and the pair
(f(\cdot),f(\cdot+T))
satisfies (
S
).
Moreover, if
f
satisfies in addition some Cauchy functional equation,
then
(f,g)
is called
Cauchy pair with respect to this functional equation.
If
(f,g)
is a Cauchy pair and
g
satisfies the same Cauchy functional euqation as
f
,
then
(f,g)
is called true Cauchy pair.

The careful reader will have noticed that in the latter definition to some degree the notion of domain is suppressed.
So, when introducing Cauchy pairs, it is not mentioned on what set the function
f
satisfies some given Cauchy functional equation.
The reason for this is twofold and we will clarify this later on ad hoc when dealing with concrete examples.

Obviously, the notion of pairability makes only sense for functional equations with at least two unknown functions,
as also the one

(C)

g(x+y)=g(x)g(y)-f(x)f(y)

coming from the cosine addition formula.

4.
Preliminaries

In this paper, functions often occure related to translation or scaling behavior.
It seems natural, especially during the calculations, to suppress sometimes the dependency on the variables.
For instance, the letter
T
denoting the period function may stand for a number or function of one or two variables,
and to make this more clear, we sometimes write
T=T(x,y)
or
T=T(x)
as common in engineering literature or when dealing with parametrized curves.
With the letter
{\mathbb{R}}^{*}
we denote the set of real numbers without zero.

4.1.
Cauchy pairs

In this section we prove some properties on Cauchy pairs
and start with the

4.2.
Additive Cauchy equation

Let
f:{\mathbb{R}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
be a regular solution to the additive Cauchy functional equation

(A)

f(x+y)=f(x)+f(y),

for all
x,y\in{\mathbb{R}}
.
Thus, there is
c\in{\mathbb{R}}
such that
f(x)=cx
for all
x\in{\mathbb{R}}
.
All Cauchy pairs with respect to the sine addition formula (
S
)
are determined in the following

Theorem 1
.

Let
f:{\mathbb{R}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
be a regular solution to the additive Cauchy functional equation (
A
) on
{\mathbb{R}}
,
namely there is
c\in{\mathbb{R}}
such that
f(x)=cx
for all
x\in{\mathbb{R}}
,
and
g:{\mathbb{R}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
be defined by
g(x)=f(x+T)
for all
x\in{\mathbb{R}}
.
Then
(f,g)
is a Cauchy pair for the sine addition functional equation (
S
) if, and only if,

T_{c}(x,y)=\frac{1}{c}-\frac{2xy}{x+y},

for all
x,y\in{\mathbb{R}}
.

Proof.

Take a function
f:{\mathbb{R}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
with
f(x)=cx
for some
c\in{\mathbb{R}}

and define
g:{\mathbb{R}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
by
g(x):=f(x+T)
for some
T:{\mathbb{R}}^{2}\to{\mathbb{R}}
.
Assume that the pair
(f,g)
satisfies (
S
).
Hence, for all
x,y\in{\mathbb{R}}
,

\displaystyle f(x+y)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle c(x+y)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle!}}{{=}}

\displaystyle f(x)g(y)+f(y)g(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle f(x)f(y+T)+f(y)f(x+T)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle cxc(y+T)+cyc(x+T)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle c^{2}\left(2xy+T(x+y)\right).

For
c=0
here clearly equality holds.
Therefore, we assume
c\neq 0
to devide both sides by it, yielding

(4.1)

x+y=c\left(2xy+T(x+y)\right).

Whence,

\frac{x+y}{c}-2xy=T(x+y).

If
y=-x
, we have
-2xy=0
, which holds iff
x=0
or
y=0
, so not for all
x,y\in{\mathbb{R}}
.
Otherwse, i.e., if
x+y\neq 0
,
the period function equals

T=T_{c}(x,y)=\frac{1}{c}-\frac{2xy}{x+y},

a non-zero constant minus the harmonic mean, as was claimed.
The conversion is easy to verify.

Interestingly,
T_{c}(x,x)=\frac{1}{c}-x
and
T_{1}(x,x)=1-x
, a term which appears naturally in the reflection formula of the Euler Gamma function.

Note also that from equation (
4.1
) we get

y\left(1-c(2x-T)\right)=x\left(cT-1\right),

which can be solved for
y
only if the bracket on the left hand side does not vanish,
thus if
x\neq\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{1}{c}+T\right)
.
(Since
T
depends on both
x
and
y
, this excludes more than one number!)
Assume that
T
is independent of, let’s say,
x
.
Then, since
T
is differentiable, its partial derivative with respect to this variable vanishes

\frac{\partial T}{\partial{x}}(x,y)=\frac{-2y^{2}}{(x+y)^{2}}=0,

hence
y=0
(observe that
T(x,0)=\frac{1}{c}
).
Since
T
is symmetric in both variables,
T
is indipendent of
y
only for
x=0
.
Thus,
T
is constant only at the origin
x=y=0
.
En passant we thus proved
that for regular solutions to the addtive Cauchy equation there is no Cauchy pair with respect to (
S
) having constant
period.
Since translations of a line through the origin is not additive any more,
there are no true Cauchy pairs of additive type with respect to (
S
).
Note also that the period function determined here vanishes exactly on the level sets of the harmonic mean
H
and
\lim_{c\to\infty}T_{c}=-H
.

What, on the other hand, happens, if the roles of
f
and
g
are reversed meaning that
g
is regular additive, the second function is defined by a certain translation of the first one, thus
f(x):=g(x+T)
for some period function
T
such that the sine functional equation holds true?
The answer is given in the following

Remark 1
.

Assume that
(g,f)
is a dual Cauchy pair of additive type with respect to the sine functional equation,
i.e.,
g(x)=cx
for some
c\in{\mathbb{R}}
,
f(x):=g(x+T)
.
By (
S
), we have, for all
x,y\in{\mathbb{R}}
,

\displaystyle f(x+y)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle g(x+y+T)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle c(x+y+T)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle f(x)g(y)+f(y)g(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle g(x+T)g(y)+g(y+T)g(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle c^{2}(xy+T(x+y)).

Without loss of generality we can assume
c\neq 0
.
Thus, for all
x,y\in{\mathbb{R}}
,

x+y+T=c(xy+T(x+y)),

and hence

T=\frac{x+y-cxy}{c(x+y)-1}

unless
y=\frac{1}{c}-x
where the period function is not defined.
It is natural to ask when the two period functions of Cauchy pair and dual Cauchy pair, respectively, are equal,
which in this case amounts to the algebraic equation

(x^{2}y+yx^{2})c(c-1)-c(x^{2}+y^{2}+4xy)+x+y=0.

Next all additive Cauchy pairs with respect to the cosine addition formula (
C
)
are determined.

Theorem 2
.

Let
g:{\mathbb{R}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
be a regular solution to the additive Cauchy functional equation (
A
) on
{\mathbb{R}}
,
namely there is
c\in{\mathbb{R}}
such that
g(x)=cx
for all
x\in{\mathbb{R}}
,
and
f:{\mathbb{R}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
be defined by
f(x)=g(x+T)
for all
x\in{\mathbb{R}}
.
Then
(g,f)
is a Cauchy pair with respect to the cosine addition functional equation (
C
) if, and only if,

T(x,y)=-\frac{x+y}{2}-\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{(x+y)\left[(x+y)-\frac{4}{c}\right]},

for all
x,y\in{\mathbb{R}}
.

Proof.

Take a function
g:{\mathbb{R}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
with
g(x)=cx
for some
c\in{\mathbb{R}}

and define
f:{\mathbb{R}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
by
f(x):=g(x+T)
for some
T:{\mathbb{R}}^{2}\to{\mathbb{R}}
.
Assume that the pair
(g,f)
satisfies (
C
).
Hence, for all
x,y\in{\mathbb{R}}
,

\displaystyle g(x+y)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle c(x+y)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle!}}{{=}}

\displaystyle g(x)g(y)-f(x)f(y)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle g(x)g(y)-g(x+T)g(y+T)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle cxcy-c(x+T)c(y+T)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-c^{2}\left(T^{2}+T(x+y)\right).

For
c=0
clearly equality holds.
Therefore, let us assume that
c\neq 0
. Hence,

x+y=-c\left(T^{2}+T(x+y)\right),

and thus

T^{2}+(x+y)T+\frac{x+y}{c}=0,

a quadratic equation in
T
with solution

\displaystyle T(x,y)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{x+y}{2}\pm\sqrt{\left(\frac{x+y}{2}\right)^{2}-\frac{x+y}{c}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{x+y}{2}\pm\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{(x+y)\left[(x+y)-\frac{4}{c}\right]}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{x+y}{2}\pm\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{x\left(x-\frac{4}{c}\right)+2xy+y\left(y-\frac{4}{c}\right)}.

The conversion is easy to verify.

Note that this period function vanishes iff
y=-x
, which obviously, unlike to the case before, is not the level set of a mean.
Moreover, the term under the square root is reflexive only at the two fixed points
x=0
and
x=\frac{8}{3}c
.

The special case where the sum of the variables is constant, say
x+y=d
, gives us the period

T_{c}(d):=T(x,d-x)=\frac{1}{2}\left(-d\pm\sqrt{d^{2}-4\frac{d}{c}}\right).

In the case
c=d
we have
T(c):=T(x,c-x)=\frac{1}{2}\left(-c\pm\sqrt{c^{2}-4}\right)
.
When the period function
c\mapsto T(c)
attains axtremas values, is of special interest.
Its derivative

T^{\prime}(c)=-\frac{1}{2}\pm\frac{c}{\sqrt{c^{2}-4}}

vanishes exactly for
c^{2}=-\frac{4}{3}
. The corresponding value of the period function is

\displaystyle T_{E}

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle T\left(\pm\frac{2i}{\sqrt{3}}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mp\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}i\pm\frac{2}{\sqrt{3}}i,

so
T_{E}=\pm\frac{\sqrt{3}}{3}i
.

4.3.
Exponential Cauchy equation

Let
f:{\mathbb{R}}\to(0,+\infty)
be a regular solution to the exponential Cauchy functional equation

(E)

f(x+y)=f(x)f(y),

for all
x,y\in{\mathbb{R}}
.
Thus, there is
a\in(0,+\infty)
such that
f(x)=a^{x}
for all
x\in{\mathbb{R}}
.
All Cauchy pairs with respect to the sine addition formula (
S
)
are determined in the following

Theorem 3
.

Let
f:{\mathbb{R}}\to(0,+\infty)
be a regular solution to the exponential Cauchy functional equation (
E
) on
{\mathbb{R}}
,
namely there is
a\in(0,+\infty)
such that
f(x)=a^{x}
for all
x\in{\mathbb{R}}
,
and
g:{\mathbb{R}}\to(0,+\infty)
be defined by
g(x)=f(x+T)
for all
x\in{\mathbb{R}}
.
Then
(f,g)
is a Cauchy pair for the sine addition functional equation (
S
) if, and only if,

T(x,y)=-\log_{a}{2},

for all
x,y\in{\mathbb{R}}
.
Moreover, there are no dual exponential Cauchy pairs of the form
(g,f)
for the sine functional equation.

Proof.

Take a function
f:{\mathbb{R}}\to(0,+\infty)
with
f(x)=a^{x}
for some fixed
a\in(0,+\infty)

and define
g:{\mathbb{R}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
by
g(x):=f(x+T)
for some
T:{\mathbb{R}}^{2}\to{\mathbb{R}}
.
Assume that the pair
(f,g)
satisfies (
S
).
Hence, for all
x,y\in{\mathbb{R}}
,

\displaystyle f(x+y)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle a^{x+y}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle!}}{{=}}

\displaystyle f(x)g(y)+f(y)g(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle f(x)f(y+T)+f(y)f(x+T)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle a^{x}a^{y+T}+a^{y}a^{x+T}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2a^{x+y+T},

thus
2a^{T}=1
and consequently
T=-\log_{a}{2}
.
To prove the second part, assume that
g(x)=a^{x}
is a regular exponential and
f
is defined by
f(x)=g(x+T)
for all
x\in{\mathbb{R}}
with some
T:{\mathbb{R}}^{2}\to{\mathbb{R}}
.
By (
S
), this means, for all
x,y\in{\mathbb{R}}
,

\displaystyle f(x+y)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle a^{x+y+T}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle!}}{{=}}

\displaystyle f(x)g(y)+f(y)g(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle g(x+T)g(y)+g(y+T)g(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle a^{x+T}a^{y}+a^{y+T}a^{x}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2a^{x+y+T},

whence,
a^{T}=0
, which is impossible since non-trivial exponentials have no zeros and
T
is supposed to be real-valued.

So we observed that there is exactly one constant period function for regular exponential Cauchy pairs,
but sadly no dual Cauchy pair of this type.

Next all regular exponential Cauchy pairs with respect to the cosine addition formula (

)
are dealt with.
Since
g
plays the role of the cosine function and

the one of sine in (

,

.

Let
g:{\mathbb{R}}\to(0,+\infty)
be a regular solution to the exponential Cauchy functional equation (
E
) on

,
namely there is
a\in(0,+\infty)

for all

,
and

be defined by

for all
x\in{\mathbb{R}}
.
Then there is no period such that
(g,f)
is a Cauchy pair for the cosine addition functional equation (
C
).
Moreover, if the roles of
f
and

4.5.
Sine and Cosine Representer

The sine and cosine additivity laws involve some degree of freedom in the sense that for a given function, one may calculate the other one.
To make things precise, we start with the following

Definition 3
.

Let

be an interval closed under addition and

Under some conditions, sine and cosine represenator are uniquely determined, which is formulated precisely in the followig two remarks.

Remark 2

(”Away from the zeros the sine representer is unique.”)
.

Let

be a function and

a sine representaor of

\displaystyle f(x+y)=f(x)g(y)+f(y)g(x),\qquad x,y\in I.

Setting

.
Consequently, the sine representor of

\displaystyle g(x)=\frac{f(2x)}{2f(x)},\qquad x\in I.

Thus, we may write the sine addition functional equations as

\displaystyle f(x+y)=f(x)\frac{f(2y)}{2f(y)}+f(y)\frac{f(2x)}{2f(x)},\qquad x,y\in I.

Remark 3

(”The cosine representer is unique.”)
.

Let
g:I\to{\mathbb{R}}
be a function and
f:I\to{\mathbb{R}}
a cosine representer of

, i.e.

\displaystyle g(x+y)=g(x)g(y)-f(x)f(y),\qquad x,y\in I.

Setting
x=y
, it follows

.
Consequently, the cosine representer of

is given by

\displaystyle f(x)=\pm\sqrt{\left(g(x)\right)^{2}-g(2x)},\qquad x\in I.

(The sign of the term under the square root is of special interest!)
We may write the cosine addition functional equations as

\displaystyle g(x+y)=g(x)g(y)-\sqrt{\left(g(x)\right)^{2}-g(2x)}\sqrt{\left(g(y)\right)^{2}-g(2y)},

for all
x,y\in I
such that
\left(\left(g(x)\right)^{2}-g(2x)\right)\left(\left(g(y)\right)^{2}-g(2y)\right)\geq 0
.

Note that the moral from the square bracket of the latter remark should rather be:
”The complex-valued cosine representer is unique.”
If the cosine representer

is assumed to be real-valued,
we need the condition
{\left(g(x)\right)^{2}-g(2x)}\geq 0
to hold on the interval

.

Since the quotient of two even functions or two odd functions is odd and sum and difference of even functions is even, we get the following

Remark 4
.

Let

be an even or an odd function defined on a real interval symmetric with respect to

Then its sine represenator

Moreover, if

is even, then so is its cosine representator

.

6.
Conclusion

The notion of pairability of functions opens a broad field of research
not limited to only functional equations. Of course, also other additivity laws may be the starting point for new and fruitful investigations.
We hope to have shown with this paper
that exploring the properties of functions having a (possibly non-constant) period induced by some additivity law or other suitable conditions
is indeed of great interest.
Up to now, we can not fully exclude that the devellopment of a certain duality theory based on the notion of pairability with respect to some additivity law
will give deep insights to some of the most difficult unsolved problems in mathematics.
