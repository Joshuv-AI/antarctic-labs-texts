Abstract
. We study conditions under which a given hypergraph is randomly robust Hamiltonian, which means that a random sparsification of the host graph contains a Hamilton cycle with high probability. Our main contribution provides nearly optimal results whenever the host graph is Hamilton connected in a locally robust sense, which translates to a typical induced subgraph of constant order containing Hamilton paths between any pair of suitable ends. The proofs are based on the recent breakthrough on Talagrand’s conjecture, which reduces the problem to specifying a distribution on the desired guest structure in the (deterministic) host structure. We find such a distribution via a new argument that reduces the problem to the case of perfect matchings in a higher uniformity. As applications, we obtain asymptotically optimal results for perfect tilings in graphs and hypergraphs both in the minimum degree and uniformly dense setting. We also prove random robustness for powers of cycles under asymptotically optimal minimum degrees and degree sequences. We solve the problem for loose and tight Hamilton cycles in hypergraphs under a range of asymptotic minimum degree conditions. This includes in particular k k -uniform tight Hamilton cycles under minimum d d -degree conditions for 1 ≤ k − d ≤ 3 1\leq k-d\leq 3 . In all cases, our bounds on the sparseness are essentially best-possible.



1.
Introduction

An old question in combinatorics is under which conditions a given graph
G
contains a particular (vertex) spanning substructure.
For instance, Dirac’s classic theorem
[
7
]
determines the optimal requirements on the minimum degree for a Hamilton cycle: when
G
has
n\geq 3
vertices it suffices that
\delta(G)\geq n/2
, and this is best possible.
Over the past decades the study of minimum degree conditions and other simple conditions that force vertex spanning substructures has developed into its own branch of combinatorics.
While our understanding of these phenomena has greatly expanded, many open questions remain.

A related field in combinatorics concerns the study spanning substructures in random graphs.
For instance, Pósa
[
41
]
and Korshunov
[
21
]
showed, independently, that the binomial random graph
\oG(n,p)
typically contains a Hamilton cycle provided that
p
is somewhat greater than
\log n/n
.

It is natural to ask whether these two branches can be combined.
The study of such
robustness problems
was initiated by Krivelevich, Lee and Sudakov
[
22
]
.
For an
n
-vertex graph
G
with
\delta(G)\geq n/2
and
p=\Omega(\log n/n)
, it was shown that the random sparsification
G_{p}
, obtained by keeping each edge of
G
independently with probability
p
, typically contains a Hamilton cycle.
We investigate similar robustness problems for Hamiltonicity in hypergraphs.

Our main result (
theorem

3.5
) offers a framework for embedding a range of guest structures into host structures under asymptotically optimal conditions.

Host structure.
Our results apply to classes of host (hyper)graphs that are hereditary in the sense of being approximately closed under taking random induced subgraphs of constant order.
This goes beyond the usually considered minimum degree conditions and includes for instance also classes of host graphs defined via degree (sequence) conditions and quasirandomness properties.

Guest structure.
Our results embed spanning guest (hyper)graphs that are in a general sense cyclical.
Basically, we can embed anything that can be expressed by ‘chaining up’ isomorphic copies of a fixed graph.
This includes perfect tilings, powers of cycles, hypergraph
\ell
-cycles and powers of hypergraph
\ell
-cycles.

Connectivity.
A central challenge of Dirac-type problems for hypergraphs concerns connectivity.
For instance, finding tight Hamilton cycles beyond codegree conditions becomes much harder, since the host graph may no longer be connected
[
42
]
.
To address this, we adopt a recently introduced setup for Hamiltonicity in dense graphs
[
30
]
.
This allows us to find spanning structures in host graphs that are not entirely connected.
In particular, we solve the robustness problem for
3
-uniform tight Hamilton cycles under vertex degree conditions, which serves as a benchmark for overcoming connectivity issues.

Proof techniques.
The proofs are based on the recent breakthrough on Talagrand’s conjecture
[
10
]
, which reduces the problem to specifying a ‘spread’ distribution on the desired guest structure in the (deterministic) host structure.
We achieve this via a new argument that reduces the problem to the case of perfect matchings in a higher uniformity.
Instead, we follow a recently introduced approach to study perfect tilings
[
27
]
and Hamiltonicity
[
30
]
in dense hypergraphs, which focuses on local substructures.

Applications

We present a series of applications that illustrate the use of our main result (
theorem

3.5
);
in particular, we essentially obtain all known results where the guest graph has a cyclic structure and the host graph robustly contains this guest structure.
To illustrate the strength of our theorem we also mention results that recently have been obtained by Kelly, Müyesser and Pokrovskiy
[
19
]
(
theorem

2.1
,
theorem

and

).
We remark that their work on (powers of) Hamilton cycles only applies to situations where the host (hyper)graph consists of a single connectivity component.
This is however only given in certain specific situations.
In contrast, our work takes into account connectivity and thus leads to many further new results (
theorems

2.4
,
2.2
,
2.6
,
2.7

), including the answer to a question formulated by Kelly, Müyesser and Pokrovskiy (

In many scenarios it is also in question whether the optimal probability

Another novelty of our result is that it covers both cases and which case actually applies can be easily read off from structural parameters of the guest graph.

We write

.
A

We write

for its

.
For two hypergraphs

, we write

.
For

For

.

We denote by

the

-graph

, that is, each potential edge is independently present with probability

.
For a
k
-graph

and

, let

be the

which arises from

by retaining each edge independently with probability

.
Thus,
G_{p}=\oG^{(k)}_{n,p}
when

is the complete
k
-graph on vertex set
[n]
.
We say that

satisfies

with probability tending to

The same formulation is used for random sparsifications

, where we implicitly assume that

can be specified for arbitrarily large values of

.

Perfect tilings

Our first set of outcomes concerns tilings, which present a generalisation of matchings.
Given
k
-graphs
F
and
G
, a
perfect
F
-tiling
of
G
is a set of copies of
F
in
G
(called
tiles
) such that every vertex of
G
is on exactly one copy. (Whenever we consider perfect
F
-tilings in a graph
G
, we tacitly assume that
v(F)
divides
v(G)
.)
We are interested in (asymptotic) Dirac-type results for perfect tilings.
It is convenient to express this in terms of ‘thresholds’.
For an
m
-vertex
k
-graph
F
, we define the
minimum
d
-degree threshold for perfect
F
-tilings
, denoted by
\th_{d}(\til_{F})
, as the infimum of
\delta\in[0,1]
such that for all
\mu>0
and
n
large enough,
every
n
-vertex
k
-graph
G
with
\delta_{d}(G)\geq(\delta+\mu)\binom{n-d}{k-d}
admits a perfect
F
-tiling.

The thresholds for
F
-tilings in
2
-graphs were determined by Kühn and Osthus
[
24
]
after preliminary work of many others.
(A more detailed trajectory of the problem can be found in the survey of Simonovits and Szemerédi
[
46
]
.)
For hypergraphs, fewer results are known (see
[
27
]
for a recent survey).
Our first application lifts these results to the random robust setting, even in those cases where the thresholds have not yet been determined.

For random graphs, Johansson, Kahn and Vu
[
15
]
investigated
F
-tilings in
\oG^{(k)}_{n,p}
.
Define
d_{1}(F)=e(F)/(v(F)-1)
as the
1
-density
of
F
, and say that a graph is
strictly
1
-balanced
if
d_{1}(F^{\prime})<d_{1}(F)
for all proper subgraphs
F^{\prime}
of
F
.
Most hypergraphs of interest are strictly
1
-balanced, such as cycles and complete graphs, and it is natural to restrict first the attention to these graphs as here the number of copies that contain a fixed vertex is well-behaved for all
p
.
Johansson, Kahn and Vu determined when
\oG^{(k)}_{n,p}
contains a perfect
F
-tiling for strictly
1
-balanced
k
-graphs
F
.

In the setting of robustness problems, it was recently shown
[
1
,
17
,
37
]
that for graphs
G
on
n
vertices with
\delta_{1}(G)\geq(1-1/r)n
,
the random sparsification
G_{p}
typically contains a perfect
K_{r}
-tiling, where
p
can be as low as we can guarantee that each vertex is contained in a copy of
K_{r}
.
We extend these results for asymptotically optimal degree conditions by replacing
K_{r}
with a strictly
1
-balanced
F
and also from graphs to hypergraphs.
This result was also obtained independently by Kelly, Müyesser and Pokrovskiy
[
19
]
.

Theorem 2.1

(Perfect tilings – minimum degree)
.

For all
1\leq d\leq k-1
,
\mu>0
and
1
-balanced
k
-graphs
F
, there is
C>0
with the following property.
Let
G
be an
n
-vertex
k
-graph with
\delta_{d}(G)\geq(\th_{d}(\til_{F})+\mu)\binom{n-d}{k-d}
.
Suppose
p\geq C(\log n)^{1/e(F)}n^{-1/d_{1}(F)}
and
n
is divisible by
v(F)
.
Then
G_{p}
contains a perfect
F
-tiling with high probability.

The bound on
p
is best-possible up to the leading constant.
This is because it coincides with the threshold where we can ensure that each vertex belongs to a copy of
F
, which is clearly a necessary condition to contain a perfect
F
-tiling.
Hence,
k
-graphs above the corresponding minimum degree threshold contain a perfect
F
-tiling in a similarly plentiful way as a perfect clique-tiling.

Beyond degree conditions

Our results are not restricted to host graphs from the setting of Dirac’s theorem.
In fact, we shall prove a generalisation of
theorem

2.1
that covers all hypergraph families which are approximately closed under taking typical induced subgraphs of constant order.
This includes for instance the setting of degree sequences and quasirandom graphs.
We illustrate this at the example of perfect tilings in uniformly dense hypergraphs.

For
\varepsilon,d>0
, we say that an
n
-vertex
k
-graph

is
uniformly
(\varepsilon,d)
-dense
if for all (not necessarily disjoint)

, we have

\displaystyle e_{G}(X_{1},\dots,X_{k})\geq d|X_{1}|\cdots|X_{k}|-\varepsilon n

where

is the number of tuples

with

for each

.
Let
\mathsf{\den}_{\varepsilon,\,d}
be the set of all
(\varepsilon,d)
-uniformly dense

-graphs.

Lenz and Mubayi
[
32
]
asked for which tiles
F
every large enough quasirandom hypergraph is guaranteed to contain a perfect

-tiling.
Since uniform density does not prevent isolated vertices, one has to add a mild degree condition for any chances of success.
Formally, let
\mathcal{F}({\til})
contain,
for every

-graphs

such that for every

, there is

such that for every large enough

with
\delta_{1}{(G)}\geq\mu\binom{n-1}{k-1}

Lenz and Mubayi
[

initiated the study of the class

More recently, Ding, Han, Sun, Wang and Zhou
[

provided a characterisation of all

We obtain the following robustness result in this setting.

Theorem 2.2

(Perfect tilings – uniform density)
.

For all

,

Let

Suppose

Powers of cycles

A graph
C
is
the
\ell
th power of a cycle
if there is a cyclical ordering of the vertices of
C
such that every set of
\ell+1
consecutive vertices forms a clique.
Moreover,
C\subseteq G
is
Hamilton
in a host graph
G
if it spans all its vertices.
We define the
minimum degree threshold for the
\ell
th power of a Hamilton cycle
, denoted (with hindsight) by
\th_{1}(\powham^{\ell})
,
as the infimum over all
\delta\in[0,1]
such that for all
\mu>0
and
n
large enough,
every
n
-vertex
2
-graph
G
with
\delta_{1}(G)\geq(\delta+\mu)n
contains the
\ell
th power of a Hamilton cycle.
It was shown by Komlós, Sárközy and Szemerédi
[
20
]
that
\th_{1}(\powham^{\ell})=1-1/(\ell+1)
for all
\ell\geq 1
, which generalises Dirac’s theorem.

As mentioned above,
\oG^{(2)}_{n,p}
contains a Hamilton cycle with high probability provided that
p
is somewhat greater than
\log n/n
.
For the
\ell
th power of a Hamilton cycle, similar results were derived by Kühn and Osthus
[
25
]
for
\ell\geq 3
from a result of Riordan
[
43
]
.
The remaining case

was recently resolved by Kahn, Narayanan and Park
[
16
]
. In combination, these results show that
\oG^{(2)}_{n,p}
contains the
\ell
th power of a Hamilton cycle for all

if
p=\Omega(n^{-1/\ell})
.
For more details, we refer the reader to the recent survey of Frieze
[
11
]
.

Here we give a common generalisation of these results.
The following result was also obtained independently by Kelly, Müyesser and Pokrovskiy
[
19
]
.

Theorem 2.3

(Powers of Hamilton cycles – minimum degree)
.

Let
k,\ell\geq 2
and
\mu>0
.
Suppose that
p=\omega(n^{-1/\ell})
.
Let
G
be an
n
-vertex
2
-graph with
\delta_{1}(G)\geq(\th_{1}(\powham^{\ell})+\mu)n
.
Then
G_{p}
contains the
\ell
th power of a Hamilton cycle with high probability.

Pósa
[
40
]
extended Dirac’s theorem by showing that a graph on
n\geq 3
vertices contains a Hamilton cycle provided that its degree sequence

satisfies

for all

.
This was recently generalised to powers of Hamilton cycles
[
29
,
48
]
.
Our next theorem gives a robustness version of this result.

Theorem 2.4

(Powers of Hamilton cycles – degree sequence)
.

Let
\ell\geq 2
,
\mu>0
,
and suppose that
p=\omega(n^{-1/\ell})
.
Let
G
be a graph with degree sequence
d_{1}\leq\dots\leq d_{n}
.
Suppose that
d_{i}>(\ell-1)n/(\ell+1)+i+\mu n
for every

.
Then
G_{p}
contains the
\ell
th power of a Hamilton cycle with high probability.

Tight cycles

We can study the
\ell
th power of a Hamilton cycle by considering an
(\ell+1)
-graph in which each edge plays the role of an
(\ell+1)
-clique.
This leads to the following notion of cycles.
A

in a
k
-graph

is an ordering of the vertices of
G
such that every
k
consecutive vertices form an edge.
We define the
minimum degree threshold for
k
-uniform tight Hamilton cycles
, denoted by
\th_{d}(\ham_{k})
, as the infimum over all
\delta\in[0,1]
such that for all
\mu>0
and large enough
n
, every
n
-vertex
k
-graph
G
with
\delta_{d}(G)\geq(\delta+\mu)\binom{n-d}{k-d}

It was shown by Rödl, Rucinski and Szemerédi
[
45
]
that

in (another) extension of Dirac’s theorem.
Reiher, Rödl, Ruciński, Schacht and Szemerédi
[
42
]
proved that
\th_{1}(\ham_{3})=5/9
after preliminary work of many others, which resolves the case of

when

.
Subsequently, this was generalised to

[
38
]
and finally, Polcyn, Reiher, Ruciński and Schülke
[

]
and, independently, Lang and Sanhueza-Matamala
[

.
Recently, it was proved by Lang, Schacht and Volec
[
31
]
that

for all

.

The threshold for tight Hamilton cycles in binomial random

[

, who showed that if

then

We prove the following approximately tight result, which was obtained independently by Kelly, Müyesser and Pokrovskiy
[

]
.

Theorem 2.5

(Tight Hamiltonicity I)
.

Let

In particular, we obtain the following result about

Theorem 2.6

.

Let

.

Let

Hamilton frameworks

There is a substantial difference in the nature of
theorem

2.5
on the one hand and
theorems

2.6
and
2.7
on the other hand, because unlike the former the latter require a careful analysis of the connectivity structure of hypergraphs.
Our main result, allows us to capture this difficulty in a concise way.
(We return to this discussion in
section

3
.)
To formalise this, we introduce the notion of
Hamilton frameworks
following the exposition of Lang and Sanhueza-Matamala
[
30
]
.

We begin with a discussion of three structural features that constitute Hamiltonicity: connectivity, space and aperiodicity.
Let
G
be a
k
-graph on
n
vertices.

Connectivity
.

We denote by
L(G)
the
line graph
of
G
, which is the
2
-graph on vertex set
E(G)
with an edge
ef
whenever
|e\cap f|=k-1
.
A subgraph of
G
is
tightly connected
if it has no isolated vertices and its edges induce a connected subgraph in
L(G)
.
Moreover, we refer to edge-maximal tightly connected subgraphs as
tight components
.

Space
.

A
fractional matching
is a function
\omega\colon E(G)\to[0,1]
such that
\sum_{e\colon v\in e}\omega(e)\leq 1
for every vertex
v\in V(G)
.
The
size
of a fractional matching
\omega
is
\sum_{e\in E(G)}\omega(e)
.
We say that
\omega
is
perfect
if its size is
n/k
.

Aperiodicity
.

A
homomorphism
from a
k
-graph
C
to
G
is a function
\phi\colon V(C)\to V(G)
that maps edges to edges.
We say that
W\subseteq G
is a
closed tight walk
if
W
is the image of homomorphism of a tight cycle
C
.
The
order
of
W
is the order of
C
.
We call
G

aperiodic
if it contains a closed walk whose order is congruent to
1
modulo
k
.

It is easy to see that any tight cycle is connected, contains a perfect fractional matching and (if its order is coprime to
k
) is aperiodic.
In other words, these three properties satisfied by any family
\mathcal{P}
of
k
-graphs whose members are Hamiltonian after deleting up to
k-1
vertices.
In addition to this, constructions show that such a family
\mathcal{P}
must also satisfy a fourth property, which ensures that these features are
consistent
between reasonable similar member of
\mathcal{P}
.
This motivates the following definition.

Definition 2.8

(Hamilton framework)
.

A family
\mathcal{P}
of
s
-vertex
k
-graphs has a
Hamilton framework

F
if for every
G\in\mathcal{P}
, there is an
s
-vertex subgraph
F(G)\subseteq G
such that

(1)

F(G)
is a tight component, (connectivity)

(2)

F(G)
has a perfect fractional matching, (space)

(3)

F(G)
contains a closed walk of order
1\bmod k
, and (aperiodicity)

(4)

F(G)\cup F(G^{\prime})
is tightly connected whenever
G,G^{\prime}\in\mathcal{G}
are obtained by deleting a distinct vertex from the same
(s+1)
-vertex
k
-graph. (consistency)

Thus, a Hamilton framework is a family of graphs which satisfy the four aforementioned necessary properties for Hamiltonicity.
It is not true that every member of a Hamilton framework is Hamiltonian, but, as we will see, a natural hardening of these properties does indeed imply Hamiltonicity.
The following definition connects Hamilton frameworks to degree conditions.
For a
k
-graph
G
and
t\geq k
, the
clique-graph

K_{t}(G)
has vertex set
V(G)
and a
t
-uniform edge
X
whenever
G[X]

Note that a

Motivation

Now let us turn to connected structures such as Hamilton cycles.
Our framework is stated in terms of non-uniform and directed hypergraphs.
This language has been adopted from a recent comprehensive study of Hamiltonicity in dense graphs

Before we come to the details, let us briefly explain why this is a suitable setup.

For given

This is no longer true for

The strategy to find a tight Hamilton cycle in

One then shows that

Outline of the proof

The proof of

Roughly speaking, this reduces the problem of finding a closed Hamilton

In other words, we reduce the problem to the setting of minimum degree conditions for perfect matchings, which explains the degree condition on

This sums up most of the proof ideas.

Given the assumptions of

Given the assumptions of

To obtain part

The following result was essentially proved in

Lemma 5.4

Lemma 6.1

(Spread Matching to Correct Distribution)
.

For all

the following holds.
Let

be positive integers.
Let

be an
n
-vertex

suppose that
\mu
is a
(Cn^{-s+1})
-spread distribution on the set of perfect matchings in

Let
I
be a
v

\probability[I\subseteq\partial_{2}M]\leq(2C)^{v}n^{c-v}

Proof.

A component
F
of
I
can appear as a subgraph of
\partial_{2}M
only if there exists an edge
e
of
M\subseteq P
with
V(F)\subseteq e
.
We first estimate the number of (not necessarily perfect) matchings

such that each

contains at least one component of
I
.
Suppose there are
r

N
that cover the
c

I

There are at most

ways to select the first component for each edge and then for each such choice at most further

.
If

refers to the total number of vertices of all components in

that are contained in

Therefore, there are at most
\sum_{r=1}^{c}\binom{c}{r}r^{c-r}n^{sr-v}

We compute

\displaystyle\probability[I\subseteq\partial_{2}M]

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{r=1}^{c}\sum_{N}\probability[N\subseteq M]\leq\sum_{r=1}^{c}\binom{c}{r}r^{c-r}n^{sr-v}\cdot(Cn^{-s+1})^{r}

\displaystyle\leq C^{v}n^{-v}\sum_{r=1}^{c}\binom{c}{r}r^{c-r}n^{r}\leq C^{v}n^{c-v}\cdot 2^{c}\leq(2C)^{v}n^{c-v},

(6.2)

\displaystyle c_{1}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j\in[c]}d_{j},

\displaystyle c_{2}^{\ast}

\displaystyle=|\{j\colon d_{j}=0\}|,\text{ and}

There are two main restrictions, which we call the ‘adjacency obstacle’ and the ‘cyclic obstacle’.

Suppose that

To illustrate the ‘cyclic obstruction’, consider

To capture all of these restrictions, we introduce two auxiliary graphs, which take care of the adjacency and cyclic obstructions respectively.
First, we use an auxiliary bipartite graph to record the connections between the minicomponents of

and

.

.

Given an adjacency-valid choice of

.
The edges are defined as follows.

, the minicomponents in

As explained before, if

We define the following event:

The next condition we need is that the ordering of the chains

Next, assuming that both

We clearly need that

-chains that connect each

.
Assuming

We define

, and also each minicomponent

By the previous discussion, we have that

To begin, we consider the simpler case of a single component

Given

of

We give a simple counting argument which bounds the number of valid partitions in this situation.

Claim 6.5
.

Suppose

is a component of
I
, and there are

-minicomponents in

.
Then there are at most

valid partitions for that set of minicomponents.

Proof of the claim.

We describe a greedy procedure which can generate all possible valid partitions and then estimate in how many ways it can be done.

We construct sets
P_{i}
, for each
1\leq i\leq m
, where initially all of them are empty.
Begin by picking an arbitrary minicomponent in

and adding it to

.
Next, if there are any
J
-minicomponents in
I_{1}
not yet allocated to a set of the partition, we proceed as follows.
Since

is connected, all
J
-minicomponents belong to the same component in the auxiliary graph

.
Hence, there exist
J
-minicomponents

such that

has already been allocated to a set

,

has not been allocated to any set, and

have a common neighbour

in

, which is a

-minicomponent in

.
Since the partition needs to be valid, if

are allocated to different sets in our partition then it would yield an edge in the graph

.
Since we can only have paths and cycles in

,
then this means that there are at most three possibilities to allocate

.
Thus, we allocate
X_{2}
, and iterate.
Once every minicomponent belongs to some set, the partition is found.

Clearly all valid partitions can be obtained in this way and there are at most

possible valid choices in our allocation.
∎

Now we consider ways to partition all minicomponents in
I_{1}
at once.
Given a valid choice of
R
, let
\kappa
be the number of components of the auxiliary graph

.
Recall that

corresponds precisely to those chains in

which house at least one minicomponent of

.
There are exactly

, so

holds.
If there is at least one minicomponent in

Given the set

of all minicomponents in

.
In the next claim, we bound

.

Claim 6.6
.

For any

, we have

.

Proof of the claim.

We note first that if there are no minicomponents in

, we have

, otherwise
P_{0}=0
.
From now on, we suppose

.
To bound

, we need to estimate how many valid choices for
R
yield exactly
N
components in
\Xi
.
Every valid choice for

can be obtained by specifying sets

such that

corresponds to the set of all minicomponents that belong to the

th path component in
\Xi
.
For all (non-floating) components

of

, all

-minicomponents in

belong to a single component in
\Xi
,
and hence we first assign the non-floating components

to some

such that no

is empty.
Let

be the number of

.
Afterwards allocate the

-minicomponents within one component.
Note that we overcount here by a factor of

, as any permutation among the

gives the same set of components.

We continue with the analysis of this procedure.
There are exactly
\binom{c^{\ast}_{1}-1}{N-1}\leq 2^{c_{1}^{\ast}}\leq 2^{v_{1}}

(g_{1},\ldots,g_{N})
; that is, where

.
Given the

(This can be done in

In total, the number of possibilities are

\displaystyle\binom{c^{\ast}_{1}-1}{N-1}\binom{c^{\ast}_{1}}{g_{1}}\binom{c^{\ast}_{1}-g_{1}}{g_{2}}\dots\binom{g_{N}}{{g_{N}}}

\displaystyle\leq 2^{v_{1}}\frac{c^{\ast}_{1}!}{\prod_{i=1}^{N}g_{i}!}.

Assuming we have groups

already defined, now we need to count how many ways there are to allocate the components of each

into a connected component of
\Xi
.

For each (non-floating) component

of

, we have to allocate the

-minicomponents in

to the different chains

.
This is a consecutive set among the chains

is the number of

-minicomponents in

.
Hence altogether for all

, there at most

such choices.

Now we focus on each component of

.
We have to appropriately glue together the different (linear) allocations of the

-minicomponents for all

.
Let

be the number of all
J
-minicomponents for

.
We start with one allocation and then for each following allocation, we have at most

(2d_{i})^{g_{i}-1}

.
Hence, given

, there are at most

For fixed reals

\sum_{i=1}^{N}\lambda_{i}=\lambda
and reals

with
\sum x_{i}\leq x
,
the product
\prod_{i=1}^{N}x_{i}^{\lambda_{i}}
is maximised when

.
Observe that
\sum_{i=1}^{N}(g_{i}-1)=c^{\ast}_{1}-N
and
\sum_{i=1}^{N}d_{i}\leq n
.
Hence

\displaystyle 3^{v_{1}}\cdot 2^{v_{1}}\cdot\prod_{i=1}^{N}d_{i}^{g_{i}-1}

\displaystyle\leq 6^{v_{1}}\frac{n^{c^{\ast}_{1}-N}}{(c^{\ast}_{1}-N)^{c^{\ast}_{1}-N}}\prod_{i\colon g_{i}\geq 1}(g_{i}-1)^{g_{i}-1}

\displaystyle\leq 7^{v_{1}}\frac{n^{c^{\ast}_{1}-N}}{(c^{\ast}_{1}-N)!}\prod_{i=1}^{N}g_{i}!.

Therefore, we conclude that
(recall the overcount by a factor of

)

\displaystyle P_{N}\leq\frac{1}{N!}\cdot 7^{v_{1}}\frac{c^{\ast}_{1}!}{\prod_{i=1}^{N}g_{i}!}\cdot\frac{n^{c^{\ast}_{1}-N}}{(c^{\ast}_{1}-N)!}\prod_{i=1}^{N}g_{i}!\leq\binom{c_{1}^{\ast}}{N}7^{v_{1}}n^{c^{\ast}_{1}-N}\leq 14^{v_{1}}n^{c^{\ast}_{1}-N}

as desired.
∎

Now, assuming a valid choice of

, let

be the number of vertices in

.
Recall that

refers to the number of components of

.
We can now bound the probability that

holds.

Claim 6.7
.

For all

, we have
\probability[\mathcal{E}_{\val},\beta=b,\kappa=N]\leq K^{\prime\prime 2v_{1}}n^{c^{\ast}_{1}-N+b-v_{1}}

The goal of this section is to prove

Before giving the proof, we briefly explain our strategy.

We connect our definitions with previous work via the notion of multihypergraphs.

9.
Conclusion

In this paper, we investigated the robustness of Hamiltonicity in hypergraphs under random sparsifications.
Our main result,
theorem

3.5
, states that Hamiltonicity is inherited to a random sparsification of a host graph
G
, provided that
G
robustly exhibits Hamilton connectedness at the local level.
As a consequence, we obtain (random) robustness versions of many classic theorems and several recent breakthroughs.
Importantly, our method works in settings (such as
d
-degree conditions for
d<k-1
) where the host graph is not necessarily connected, which was an obstacle in previous works.

Our work also implies counting versions of these results, which can be easily derived from the existence of spread distributions (see, for example
[
19
, Section 1.4.1]
for such a deduction).
For instance, this shows that there are at least
\exp(n\log n-O(n))
Hamilton
\ell
-cycles under the minimum
d
-degree conditions of
theorems

2.5
,
2.6
,
2.11
and
2.12
.
This recovers and extends the work of Glock, Gould, Joos, Kühn and Osthus
[
13
]
, Montgomery and Pavez-Signé
[
34
]
and Kelly, Müyesser and Pokrovskiy
[
19
]
.
For a more detailed discussion of the subject, we refer the reader to the work of Montgomery and Pavez-Signé
[
34
]
.

The applications in
section

2
illustrate the broad range of scenarios in which
theorem

3.5
can be used to establish robustness for Hamiltonicity.
The list of outcomes is by no means exhaustive and we believe that many further applications are possible.
For instance, it is not hard to deduce that Hamiltonicity is preserved in sparsifications of robust expanders
[
26
]
, loose cycles in quasirandom hypergraphs
[
33
]
or powers of tight cycles in hypergraphs
[
36
]
.
Our outcomes do not take advantage of the fact that
theorem

3.5
is formulated in terms of directed hypergraphs.
An illustrative application for this would be a robust version of the Dirac-type theorem for oriented graphs due to Keevash, Kühn and Osthus
[
18
]
, which can be derived from
theorem

3.5
in a similar way as the results of
section

Key to all of these applications is the notion of Hamilton connectedness.
Abstractly speaking, when it comes to using Hamiltonicity (in the broad sense of this paper) as an input condition, then Hamilton connectedness appears to be the ‘right’ form of it.
In related work
[
30
]

theorem

3.5
receive an immediate ‘upgrade’ to the random robustness setting.

Looking ahead, multiple open questions come to mind.
Our main result is formulated in terms of coarse thresholds.
We believe that this can be improved to semi-sharp thresholds as in the work of Kahn, Narayanan and Park

Beyond quantitative considerations, it would be quite interesting to know whether similar results are possible in other settings that related random graphs with deterministic conditions.

3.5

Or can we capture some of the progress in the field of random perturbations, initiated by Bohman, Frieze and Martin
