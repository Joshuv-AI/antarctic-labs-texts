Decompositions of q q -Matroids Using Cyclic Flats
Heide Gluesing-Luerssen, and Benjamin Jany

Abstract
We study the direct sum of q q -matroids by way of their cyclic flats. Using that the rank function of a q q -matroid is fully determined by the cyclic flats and their ranks, we show that the cyclic flats of the direct sum of two q q -matroids are exactly all the direct sums of the cyclic flats of the two summands. This simplifies the rank function of the direct sum significantly. A q q -matroid is called irreducible if it cannot be written as a (non-trivial) direct sum. We provide a characterization of irreducibility in terms of the cyclic flats and show that every q q -matroid can be decomposed into a direct sum of irreducible q q -matroids, which are unique up to equivalence.



Keywords:

q
-matroid, cyclic flats, direct sum, irreducibility, decomposition.

1
Introduction

A
q
-matroid is the
q
-analogue of a matroid. Its ground space is a finite-dimensional vector space over a finite field, and the rank function,
defined on the subspace lattice of that vector space, is the natural generalization of that of a matroid.

q
-Matroids were introduced formally in 2018 in
[
17
]
, but actually appeared already in
[
8
]

and
[
3
]
.
These combinatorial objects have gained considerable attention in the coding theory community because of their close relation to rank-metric codes.
Indeed, vector rank-metric codes give rise to
q
-matroids just like linear block codes induce matroids.
In the language of matroid theory the resulting (
q
-)matroids are simply the representable ones; see also
2.8
below.
Unsurprisingly, the induced (
q
-)matroids provide a lot of combinatorial information about the underlying codes.
For further details concerning rank-metric codes in relation to
q
-matroids we refer to
[
17
,
16
,
20
,
11
,
14
]
and the references therein.

Since their introduction in
[
17
]
, the theory of
q
-matroids has made considerable progress.
Most notably, an extensive catalogue of cryptomorphisms has been derived in
[
6
]
, and the notion of a direct sum of
q
-matroids has
been introduced in
[
7
]
.
While the cryptomorphisms form a (non-trivial) generalization of the corresponding ones for matroids, the same cannot be said about the direct sum.
None of the constructions of the direct sum known for matroids has a well-defined
q
-analogue.
The definition introduced in
[
7
]
is based on the rank function and makes use of the
q
-matroid union, a construction that is akin to associating a
matroid to a polymatroid (see
5.1
).
While this produces a
q
-matroid that has all the properties that one expects from a direct sum, it unfortunately does not appear to behave well
with respect to
q
-matroid structure: The rank function is somewhat unwieldy and there is no direct way of determining the
independent spaces (resp. circuits, bases, and flats) from the independent spaces (resp. circuits, bases, and flats) of the two summands.

Fortunately – as we will show in this paper – there is a
q
-matroid structure that does behave very well for the direct sum, namely the cyclic-flat structure.
A theory of cyclic flats has been first proposed in
[
1
]
.
For studying the direct sum of
q
-matroids, however, an alternative approach to cyclic flats is more beneficial.
It is presented in Sections
3
and
4
of this paper.
In those sections and with methods different from those in
[
1
]
, it is shown that the cyclic flats together with their rank values fully determine the entire
q
-matroid.
In
[
1
]
this is established by reconstructing the lattice of flats, which in turn leads to the
q
-matroid, while we determine the rank function of the entire
q
-matroid directly from the cyclic flats.
We will provide more details on the differences and similarities between
[
1
]
and this paper in
3.1
.
In short, thanks to the different approaches, the results appear in different order and with different proofs.
Clearly both approaches have their merits.
For the purpose of this paper however, our direct approach toward the rank function is better suited than the reconstruction of the lattice of flats – simply because flats do not behave well when taking direct sums of
q
-matroids.
In
[
1
, Sec. 3]
, the authors use their results to derive a cryptomorphism for
q
-matroids based on cyclic flats and their rank values.

Equipped with a sufficient theory on cyclic flats, we turn to the direct sum of
q
-matroids.
After recalling and deriving some basic properties in
Section

5
, we will
show in
Section

6
that the cyclic flats of a direct sum
{\mathcal{M}}_{1}\oplus{\mathcal{M}}_{2}
is the collection of the direct sums of all
cyclic flats of
{\mathcal{M}}_{1}
and
{\mathcal{M}}_{2}
.
As a consequence, the rank function of
{\mathcal{M}}_{1}\oplus{\mathcal{M}}_{2}
is fully determined by the cyclic flats of
{\mathcal{M}}_{1}
and
{\mathcal{M}}_{2}
along with their rank values.
This leads to a highly efficient formula of the rank function.
Even more, this result allows us to study decompositions of
q
-matroids into irreducible ones.
Naturally, a
q
-matroid is called irreducible if it cannot be written as a direct sum of
q
-matroids with
nonzero ground spaces. This can be nicely characterized in terms of the cyclic flats.
Furthermore, we show that every
q
-matroid can be written as a direct sum of irreducible ones, whose summands are unique up to equivalence.

We wish to point out that the use of cyclic flats for studying direct sums and decomposing
q
-matroids appears to be novel in the
sense that it does not have a blueprint in classical
matroid theory (as far as we know).
It is not surprising that such an approach does not exist for matroids because in that case each of the immediate structures
(rank, independent spaces, flats etc.) can be used to conveniently define the direct sum.
Even more, the notion of connectedness for matroids leads to an equivalence relation, which in turn
produces the decomposition of a matroid into its irreducible (i.e., connected) components.
No
q
-analogue of this equivalence relation that serves the same purpose has been found yet for
q
-matroids; see for instance
[
7
, Sec. 8]
.

2
Basic Notions of
q
-Matroids

In this section we collect some facts about
q
-matroids that will be crucial later on.

Definition 2.1
.

A
q
-matroid with ground space
E
is a pair
{\mathcal{M}}=(E,\rho)
, where
\rho:{\mathcal{L}}(E)\longrightarrow{\mathbb{N}}_{\geq 0}
is a map satisfying

(R1)

Dimension-Boundedness:
0\leq\rho(V)\leq\dim V
for all
V\in{\mathcal{L}}(E)
;

(R2)

Monotonicity:
V\leq W\Longrightarrow\rho(V)\leq\rho(W)
for all
V,W\in{\mathcal{L}}(E)
;

(R3)

Submodularity:
\rho(V+W)+\rho(V\cap W)\leq\rho(V)+\rho(W)
for all
V,W\in{\mathcal{L}}(E)
.

\rho(V)
is called the
rank of

V
and
\rho({\mathcal{M}}):=\rho(E)
the
rank
of the
q
-matroid.

Clearly,
\rho(V+\mbox{$\langle{x}\rangle$})\in\{\rho(V),\,\rho(V)+1\}
for all
V,\mbox{$\langle{x}\rangle$}\in{\mathcal{L}}(E)
. The following results will play a crucial role later on.

Proposition 2.2

(
[
17
, Prop. 6]
,
[
5
, Lem. 3.2]
)
.

Let
{\mathcal{M}}=(E,\rho)
be a
q
-matroid and
V,W\in{\mathcal{L}}(E)
.

(a)

Suppose
\rho(V+\mbox{$\langle{w}\rangle$})=\rho(V)
for all
w\in W
.
Then
\rho(V+W)=\rho(V)
.

(b)

Suppose
W\leq V
. Let
x\in E
be such that
\rho(W+\mbox{$\langle{x}\rangle$})=\rho(W)
. Then
\rho(V+\mbox{$\langle{x}\rangle$})=\rho(V)
.

Theorem 2.3

(
[
17
, Thm. 42]
)
.

Let
\langle{\cdot}\!\mid\!{\cdot}\rangle
be a non-degenerate symmetric bilinear form (NSBF) on
E
.
For
V\in{\mathcal{L}}(E)
define
V^{\perp}=\{w\in E\mid\mbox{$\langle{v}\!\mid\!{w}\rangle$}=0\text{ for all }v\in V\}
.
Let
{\mathcal{M}}=(E,\rho)
be a
q
-matroid and set

\rho^{*}(V)=\dim V+\rho(V^{\perp})-\rho(E).

(2.1)

Then
{\mathcal{M}}^{*}=(E,\rho^{*})
is a
q
-matroid, called the
dual
of
{\mathcal{M}}
with respect to the chosen NSBF.
Furthermore,
{\mathcal{M}}^{**}={\mathcal{M}}
, where
{\mathcal{M}}^{**}=({\mathcal{M}}^{*})^{*}
is the bidual.

The dual
q
-matroid depends on the choice of
\langle{\cdot}\!\mid\!{\cdot}\rangle
, but different NSBFs lead to equivalent dual

q
-matroids in the following sense
[
14
, Thm. 2.8]
.

Definition 2.4
.

Let
E_{i},\,i=1,2,
be
{\mathbb{F}}
-vector spaces of the same finite dimension and
{\mathcal{M}}_{i}=(E_{i},\rho_{i})
be
q
-matroids.
We call
{\mathcal{M}}_{1}
and
{\mathcal{M}}_{2}

equivalent
, denoted by
{\mathcal{M}}_{1}\approx{\mathcal{M}}_{2}
, if there exists an
{\mathbb{F}}
-isomorphism

\alpha:E_{1}\longrightarrow E_{2}
such that

\rho_{2}(\alpha(V))=\rho_{1}(V)
for all
V\in{\mathcal{L}}(E_{1})
.

Definition 2.5
.

Let
0\leq k\leq\dim E
. The
uniform
q
-matroid of rank
k
on
E
is defined as the
q
-matroid
{\mathcal{U}}_{k}(E)=(E,\rho)
, where
\rho(V)=\min\{k,\dim V\}

for all
V\in{\mathcal{L}}(E)
. Moreover,
{\mathcal{U}}_{0}(E)
is called the
trivial
q
-matroid
on
E
and
{\mathcal{U}}_{\dim E}(E)
is the
free
q
-matroid
on
E
.
We let
{\mathcal{U}}_{0,n}
and
{\mathcal{U}}_{n,n}
denote the trivial and free
q
-matroid on any
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}
-linear ground space of
dimension
n
(hence they are defined only up to equivalence).

Note that
{\mathcal{U}}_{k}(E)^{*}={\mathcal{U}}_{\dim E-k}(E)
for any NSBF on
E
.
In particular, the dual of the free
q
-matroid is the zero
q
-matroid and vice versa.

Definition 2.6

(
see for instance
[
14
, Def. 5.1 and Thm. 5.2]
)
.

Let
{\mathcal{M}}=(E,\rho)
be a
q
-matroid and
X\in{\mathcal{L}}(E)
.
The
restriction of
{\mathcal{M}}
to
X
and the
contraction of
X
from
{\mathcal{M}}

are defined as the
q
-matroids
{\mathcal{M}}|_{X}=(X,\hat{\rho})
with
\hat{\rho}(V)=\rho(V)
for all
V\leq X
and

{\mathcal{M}}/X=(E/X,\tilde{\rho})
with
\tilde{\rho}(V/X)=\rho(V+X)-\rho(X)
for all
V\in{\mathcal{L}}(E)
, respectively.

The following notions of
q
-matroids have been well studied; for details see
[
17
,
6
]
.
Let
{\mathcal{M}}=(E,\rho)
be a
q
-matroid. A subspace
I\in{\mathcal{L}}(E)
is called
independent
if
\rho(I)=\dim I
.
Every subspace of an independent space is independent and furthermore,

\rho(V)=\max\{\dim I\mid I\leq V,\,I\text{ independent}\}\ \text{ for all }\ V\in{\mathcal{L}}(E).

(2.2)

As a consequence, the collection of independent spaces fully determines the entire
q
-matroid
{\mathcal{M}}
.
If
V\in{\mathcal{L}}(E)
is not independent it is called
dependent
.
A
basis
of a subspace
V
is an independent space
I\leq V
satisfying
\rho(I)=\rho(V)
.
A dependent space all of whose proper subspaces are independent is a
circuit
.
A space is called
open
if it is a sum of circuits (by definition, the empty sum is the zero space).
Thus any open space
V\in{\mathcal{L}}(E)
is of the form

V=\sum_{\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{1}{C\leq V}{C\text{ circuit} }}\hskip-5.0ptC.

(2.3)

A space
F\in{\mathcal{L}}(E)
is a
flat
if it is maximal with respect to its rank value, that is,
\rho(F+\mbox{$\langle{x}\rangle$})>\rho(F)
for all
x\in E\setminus F
.
Clearly,
E
is a flat.
A flat
F
is a
hyperplane
if
F\neq E
and
E
is the only flat properly containing
F
.
We denote by
{\mathcal{I}}({\mathcal{M}})
the collection of all independent spaces of
{\mathcal{M}}
and similarly use

{\mathcal{D}}({\mathcal{M}}),\;{\mathcal{C}}({\mathcal{M}}),\;{\mathcal{O}}({\mathcal{M}}),\;{\mathcal{F}}({\mathcal{M}}),\;{\mathcal{H}}({\mathcal{M}})
for the collections of the other spaces.
Finally, we need the closure operator of
{\mathcal{M}}
which is defined as

{\rm cl}:{\mathcal{L}}(E)\longrightarrow{\mathcal{L}}(E),\quad V\longmapsto\hskip-5.0pt\sum_{\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{1}{x\in E}{\rho(V+\langle x\rangle)=\rho(V)}}\hskip-10.00002pt\mbox{$\langle{x}\rangle$}.

Clearly, a subspace

is a flat if and only if
{\rm cl}(V)=V
.
Thanks to

\rho({\rm cl}(V))=\rho(V)\text{ for all }V\in{\mathcal{L}}(E).

(2.4)

We now list some fundamental properties of
q
-matroids.
Part (a) and (b) are in
[
6
, Cor. 86, Cor. 79]
, and (c) is a consequence of (a) and (b) together with (
2.3
).
Part (d) is in
[
6
, Def. 10 and Thm. 44]

Theorem 2.7
.

Let
{\mathcal{M}}=(E,\rho)
be a

be its dual with respect to some NSBF

Let

. Then

(a)

V\in{\mathcal{O}}({\mathcal{M}})\Longleftrightarrow V^{\perp}\in{\mathcal{F}}({\mathcal{M}}^{*})
.

(b)

V\in{\mathcal{C}}({\mathcal{M}})\Longleftrightarrow V^{\perp}\in{\mathcal{H}}({\mathcal{M}}^{*})
.

(c)

If

, then
{\displaystyle V=\hskip-10.00002pt\bigcap_{\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{1}{F\leq H}{H\in\cH(\cM)}}\hskip-10.00002ptH}
.

(d)

F_{1},F_{2}\in{\mathcal{F}}({\mathcal{M}})\Longrightarrow F_{1}\cap F_{2}\in{\mathcal{F}}({\mathcal{M}})\
and
\ O_{1},O_{2}\in{\mathcal{O}}({\mathcal{M}})\Longrightarrow O_{1}+O_{2}\in{\mathcal{O}}({\mathcal{M}})

(e)

The closure operator satisfies for all

V\leq{\rm cl}(V),\quad V\leq W\Longrightarrow{\rm cl}(V)\leq{\rm cl}(W),\quad{\rm cl}({\rm cl}(V))={\rm cl}(V).

A particularly nice class of
q
-matroids are the representable ones.

Definition 2.8

(
[
17
, Sec. 5]
)
.

Consider a field extension
{\mathbb{F}}_{q^{m}}
of
{\mathbb{F}}={\mathbb{F}}_{q}
and let
G\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q^{m}}^{k\times n}
be a matrix of rank

.
Define the map

Then

satisfies (R1)–(R3) and thus defines a

-matroid
{\mathcal{M}}_{G}=({\mathbb{F}}^{n},\rho)
of rank

.

.

3
The Cyclic Core

We define the cyclic core of a subspace.
Our definition (
3.3
) is a vector-based
q
-analogue of the classical cyclic core in
[
10
, p. 395]
.
More precisely, our cyclic core consists of vectors in the ground space
E
(rather than subspaces) that behave in the desired way
with respect to hyperplanes, and it is not hard to see that the so defined set is indeed a subspace.
Making use of duality and the properties of the closure operator, we show that the cyclic core of a subspace
V
is the largest open space
contained in
V
. This also shows immediately that the cyclic-core operator is the dual of the closure operator (
3.7
) and idempotent
(
3.8
).
Subspaces that coincide with their cyclic core are called cyclic spaces, and it turns out that these are exactly the open spaces.

As mentioned in the introduction, a theory of the cyclic core was first proposed in
[
1
]
.
Here we develop an alternative approach, which is well suited for our study of the direct sum of
q
-matroids in subsequent sections.
We briefly describe the differences and similarities.

Remark 3.1
.

The main difference between
[
1
]
and our work is the starting point for developing the theory.
In
[
1
]
, the authors start with the definition of cyclic spaces.
This can easily be done without the need of a cyclic-core operator and is identical to what we call cyclic spaces.
Furthermore, the authors define two concepts that may be regarded as
q
-analogues of the classical cyclic core:

\text{Cyc}(V)
is defined as a collection of certain 1-dimensional subspaces (
[
1
, Def. 2.3]
) and the
cyclic-core operator
\text{cyc}(V)
is the sum of the cyclic subspaces contained in
V
(
[
1
, Def. 2.8]
).
The relation between these two concepts is clarified in
[
1
, Prop. 2.18]
, which then also shows that our cyclic-core operator
coincides with the one in
[
1
]
.
Due to the different routes taken, the authors in
[
1
, Sec. 2.1]
establish properties of cyclic spaces in order to study their cyclic-core operator, while we establish properties of our cyclic-core operator and obtain those for cyclic spaces as a consequence.
Thus, while the results look almost identical, they arise in different order and their proofs differ in each single case.

Notation 3.2
.

Throughout, let
\langle{\cdot}\!\mid\!{\cdot}\rangle
be a fixed NSBF on
E
, thus
V^{\perp}
denotes the orthogonal space of
V

with respect to
\langle{\cdot}\!\mid\!{\cdot}\rangle
.
Let
{\mathcal{M}}=(E,\rho)
be a
q
-matroid and
{\mathcal{M}}^{*}=(E,\rho^{*})
be its dual with respect to
\langle{\cdot}\!\mid\!{\cdot}\rangle

We denote by
{\rm cl}(\,\cdot\,)
and
{\rm cl}^{*}(\,\cdot\,)
the closure operators of
{\mathcal{M}}
and
{\mathcal{M}}^{*}
, respectively.

Definition 3.3
.

For
V\in{\mathcal{L}}(E)
we define the
cyclic core
of
V
as

{\rm cyc}(V)=\{x\in V\mid\rho(W)=\rho(V)\text{ for all $W\leq V$ such that $W+\mbox{$\langle{x}\rangle$}=V$}\}.

Note that every subspace
W
appearing in the definition has codimension at most
1
in
V
.
Clearly, if
V
is independent then
{\rm cyc}(V)=0
. The converse is true as well as we will see in
3.6
.

Proposition 3.4
.

Let
V\in{\mathcal{L}}(E)
. Then
{\rm cyc}(V)\in{\mathcal{L}}(E)
, i.e.,
{\rm cyc}(V)
is a subspace of
E
.

Proof.

Clearly
0\in{\rm cyc}(V)
and
{\rm cyc}(V)
is closed under scalar multiplication.
Let
x,y\in{\rm cyc}(V)
and let
W\leq V
be such that
W+\mbox{$\langle{x+y}\rangle$}=V
.
We want to show that
\rho(W)=\rho(V)
.
Without loss of generality let
W\neq V
. Then
\dim W=\dim V-1
and
x+y\not\in W
.
Thus we may assume that
x\not\in W
. Hence
W+\mbox{$\langle{x}\rangle$}=V
, and using that
x
is in
{\rm cyc}(V)
we conclude

\rho(W)=\rho(V)
.
∎

We will show below that
{\rm cyc}(V)
is the largest open space contained in
V
.
We will do so by making use of duality and the closure operator.

Lemma 3.5
.

Let
V\in{\mathcal{L}}(E)
and
C_{1},\ldots,C_{t}\in{\mathcal{C}}({\mathcal{M}})
be the circuits of
{\mathcal{M}}
contained in
V
. Then

{\rm cl}^{*}(V^{\perp})=\bigcap_{i=1}^{t}C_{i}^{\perp}.

Proof.

Since
{\rm cl}^{*}(V^{\perp})
is a flat in
{\mathcal{M}}^{*}
,
2.7
implies

{\rm cl}^{*}(V^{\perp})=\bigcap_{\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{1}{\cl^*(V^\perp)\leq H}{H\in\cH(\cM^*)}}\hskip-10.00002ptH\quad=\bigcap_{\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{1}{\cl^*(V^\perp)\leq C^\perp}{C\in\cC(\cM)}}\hskip-10.00002ptC^{\perp}\quad=\bigcap_{\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{1}{C\leq V}{C\in\cC(\cM)}}\hskip-5.0ptC^{\perp},

where the last step follows from the fact that
C^{\perp}
is a flat of
{\mathcal{M}}^{*}
, which implies the equivalences

{\rm cl}^{*}(V^{\perp})\leq C^{\perp}\Longleftrightarrow V^{\perp}\leq C^{\perp}\Longleftrightarrow C\leq V
.
∎

Now we obtain the following description of the cyclic core.
Using either the theory developed in
[
1
]
or ours, it is easy to verify that it agrees with the one defined in
[
1
, Def. 2.8]
.

Theorem 3.6
.

For
V\in{\mathcal{L}}(E)
we have

{\rm cyc}(V)=\sum_{\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{1}{C\leq V}{C\in\cC(\cM)}}\hskip-5.0ptC.

Thus
{\rm cyc}(V)
is an open space and in fact the largest open space contained in
V
.
As a consequence,
V
is independent if and only if
{\rm cyc}(V)=0
.

Proof.

Let
C_{1},\ldots,C_{t}\in{\mathcal{C}}({\mathcal{M}})
be the circuits of
{\mathcal{M}}
contained in
V
. We have to show that
{\rm cyc}(V)=\sum_{i=1}^{t}C_{i}
.
From
Lemma

3.5
we know that
{\rm cl}^{*}(V^{\perp})=\cap_{i=1}^{t}C_{i}^{\perp}
, and thus (
2.4
) implies

\rho^{*}(V^{\perp})=\rho^{*}\Big(\bigcap_{i=1}^{t}C_{i}^{\perp}\Big).

(3.1)

We now turn to the stated identity.

“
\subseteq
” Let
x\in{\rm cyc}(V)
. Let
W\leq V
be such that
\dim W=\dim V-1
and
W+\mbox{$\langle{x}\rangle$}=V
. Then
\rho(W)=\rho(V)
and thus

\rho^{*}(W^{\perp})=\dim W^{\perp}+\rho(W)-\rho(E)=\dim(V^{\perp})+1+\rho(V)-\rho(E)=\rho^{*}(V^{\perp})+1.

Thus
\rho^{*}(W^{\perp})>\rho^{*}\Big(\bigcap_{i=1}^{t}C_{i}^{\perp}\Big)
, which in turn implies that
W^{\perp}
is not a subspace of

\bigcap_{i=1}^{t}C_{i}^{\perp}
.
Hence
\sum_{i=1}^{t}C_{i}\not\leq W
.
Since this is true for every
W\in\mbox{$\text{Hyp}$}(V)
not containing
x
, we conclude that
x\in\sum_{i=1}^{t}C_{i}
.

“
\supseteq
” It suffices to show that each
C_{i}
is in
{\rm cyc}(V)
. Let
x\in C_{i}
.
We show that
\rho(W)=\rho(V)
for all
W\in\mbox{$\text{Hyp}$}(V)
such that
W+\mbox{$\langle{x}\rangle$}=V
.
Choose such a subspace
W
. Then clearly
x\not\in W
and thus
C_{i}\not\leq W
.
Using the containment
C_{i}\leq V
we obtain

{\rm cl}^{*}(V^{\perp})\leq{\rm cl}^{*}(C_{i}^{\perp})=C_{i}^{\perp}
and
{\rm cl}^{*}(W^{\perp})\not\leq C_{i}^{\perp}
.
This implies
{\rm cl}^{*}(V^{\perp})\lneq{\rm cl}^{*}(W^{\perp})
and thus

\rho^{*}(V^{\perp})=\rho^{*}({\rm cl}^{*}(V^{\perp}))<\rho^{*}({\rm cl}^{*}(W^{\perp}))=\rho^{*}(W^{\perp}).

Since
\dim V^{\perp}+1=\dim W^{\perp}
, submodularity of
\rho^{*}
yields
\rho^{*}(W^{\perp})\leq\rho^{*}(V^{\perp})+1
.
All of this leads to
\rho^{*}(W^{\perp})=\rho^{*}(V^{\perp})+1
.
Now we compute

\displaystyle\rho(V)

\displaystyle=\dim V+\rho^{*}(V^{\perp})-\rho^{*}(E)=\dim V+\rho^{*}(W^{\perp})-1-\rho^{*}(E)

\displaystyle=\dim W+\rho^{*}(W^{\perp})-\rho^{*}(E)=\rho(W),

as desired. All of this proves that
x\in{\rm cyc}(V)
and thus
\sum_{i=1}^{t}C_{i}\leq{\rm cyc}(V)
.
Finally,
{\rm cyc}(V)=0
if and only if
V
contains no circuits, which means that
V
is independent.
∎

The cyclic-core operator is dual to the closure operator in the following sense.
This also appears in
[
1
, Lem. 2.23]
.

Corollary 3.7
.

Let
V\in{\mathcal{L}}(E)
. Then
{\rm cyc}(V)^{\perp}={\rm cl}^{*}(V^{\perp})
.

Proof.

Let
C_{1},\ldots,C_{t}\in{\mathcal{C}}({\mathcal{M}})
be the circuits contained in
V
.
With the aid of
Lemma

3.5
and
3.6
we compute

{\rm cl}^{*}(V^{\perp})=\bigcap_{i=1}^{t}C_{i}^{\perp}=\big(\sum_{i=1}^{t}C_{i}\big)^{\perp}={\rm cyc}(V)^{\perp}
.
∎

The previous results imply that open spaces of
{\mathcal{M}}
form a lattice
({\mathcal{O}}({\mathcal{M}}),<,\wedge,\vee)
with
V\wedge W={\rm cyc}(V\cap W)
and

V\vee W=V+W
. It is the dual of the lattice of flats of
{\mathcal{M}}^{*}
; for the latter see
[

, Thm. 3.10 and 3.13]
.
We do not need this result in this paper.

The following is immediate with
3.6
or
3.7
; see also
[

, Thm. 2.10]
.

Corollary 3.8
.

Let
V,W\in{\mathcal{L}}(E)
. Then

(a)

V\leq W\Longrightarrow{\rm cyc}(V)\leq{\rm cyc}(W)
.

(b)

{\rm cyc}({\rm cyc}(V))={\rm cyc}(V)
.

In the context of the cyclic core, it is natural to cast the following definition.

Definition 3.9
.

A subspace
V\in{\mathcal{L}}(E)
is
cyclic
if
{\rm cyc}(V)=V
.

3.6
and
3.3
show that any
V\in{\mathcal{L}}(E)
satisfies

V\text{ is open }\Longleftrightarrow V\text{ is cyclic }\Longleftrightarrow\rho(W)=\rho(V)\text{ for all $W\in\mbox{$\text{Hyp}$}(V)$.}

(3.2)

We will use “open” and “cyclic” interchangeably and use the notation
{\mathcal{O}}({\mathcal{M}})
for the collection of cyclic spaces of
{\mathcal{M}}
.

Dualizing the identity
\rho(V)=\rho({\rm cl}(V))
provides us with part (a) of the next result; see also
[
1
, Lem. 2.16 and 2.17]
.

Proposition 3.10
.

Let
V\in{\mathcal{L}}(E)
.

(a)

\dim V-\rho(V)=\dim{\rm cyc}(V)-\rho({\rm cyc}(V))
.

(b)

Let
V={\rm cyc}(V)\oplus W
. Then
\rho(V)=\rho({\rm cyc}(V))+\dim W
and
\dim W=\rho(W)
, i.e.,
W
is independent.

Proof.

(a) follows from

\displaystyle\dim V^{\perp}+\rho(V)-\rho(E)

\displaystyle=\rho^{*}(V^{\perp})=\rho^{*}({\rm cl}^{*}(V^{\perp}))=\rho^{*}({\rm cyc}(V)^{\perp})

\displaystyle=\dim{\rm cyc}(V)^{\perp}+\rho({\rm cyc}(V))-\rho(E).

(b) The first part is a consequence of (a) because
\dim W=\dim V-\dim{\rm cyc}(V)
.
Now submodularity implies
\rho({\rm cyc}(V))+\dim W=\rho(V)\leq\rho({\rm cyc}(V))+\rho(W)
, and thus
\rho(W)=\dim W
.
∎

4
The Lattice of Cyclic Flats

We turn to the notion of cyclic flats, which are simply flats that are also cyclic spaces.
Thanks to the closure operator and cyclic-core operator, the collection of cyclic flats turns into a lattice.
The main result of this section states that the collection of cyclic flats along with their rank values uniquely determine the
q
-matroid.
This section is the relatively standard
q
-analogue of the theory of cyclic flats for matroids as can be found in
[
4
]
or

[
10
, Sec. 2.4]
.
The first two results appear also, with slightly different proofs, in
[
1
, Sec. 2.2]
.
Thereafter we take a different route than
[
1
]
by focusing on the rank function.

We continue with the setting from
3.2
.
Our first result states that the cyclic-core operator preserves flatness and the closure operator preserves cyclicity.
The corresponding fact for classical matroids is only mentioned in passing at
[
10
, Sec. 2.4]
.

Lemma 4.1
.

Recall the collections
{\mathcal{F}}({\mathcal{M}})
of flats and
{\mathcal{O}}({\mathcal{M}})
of open (i.e., cyclic) spaces of
{\mathcal{M}}
.

(a)

F\in{\mathcal{F}}({\mathcal{M}})\Longrightarrow{\rm cyc}(F)\in{\mathcal{F}}({\mathcal{M}})
.

(b)

F\in{\mathcal{O}}({\mathcal{M}})\Longrightarrow{\rm cl}(F)\in{\mathcal{O}}({\mathcal{M}})
.

As a consequence, every
V\in{\mathcal{L}}(E)
satisfies
{\rm cl}({\rm cyc}(V))\leq{\rm cyc}({\rm cl}(V))
and both spaces are elements of the intersection
{\mathcal{F}}({\mathcal{M}})\cap{\mathcal{O}}({\mathcal{M}})
.

The spaces
{\rm cl}({\rm cyc}(V))
and
{\rm cyc}({\rm cl}(V))
are in general not identical. See
4.3
below.

Proof.

(a) Let
x\in E\setminus{\rm cyc}(F)
. We have to show that
\rho({\rm cyc}(F)+\mbox{$\langle{x}\rangle$})>\rho({\rm cyc}(F))
.

i) If
x\not\in F
, then
\rho(F+\mbox{$\langle{x}\rangle$})=\rho(F)+1
, since
F
is a flat, and the desired inequality follows from
2.2
(b).

ii) Let
x\in F\setminus{\rm cyc}(F)
. The definition of
{\rm cyc}(F)
implies the existence of a space
\hat{W}\in\mbox{$\text{Hyp}$}(F)
such that

x\not\in\hat{W}
and
\rho(\hat{W}+\mbox{$\langle{x}\rangle$})=\rho(\hat{W})+1
. Thus,
\rho(F)=\rho(\hat{W})+1
.
Assume by contradiction that
\rho({\rm cyc}(F)+\mbox{$\langle{x}\rangle$})=\rho({\rm cyc}(F))
.
Then
2.2
(b) implies
\rho(W+\mbox{$\langle{x}\rangle$})=\rho(W)
for all subspaces
W
containing
{\rm cyc}(F)
.
Hence we conclude
{\rm cyc}(F)\not\leq\hat{W}
.
Thus there exists
y\in{\rm cyc}(F)\setminus\hat{W}
. Now
\hat{W}+\mbox{$\langle{y}\rangle$}=F
, and

\rho(\hat{W})=\rho(F)
since
y\in{\rm cyc}(F)
. This contradicts the above, and thus
\rho({\rm cyc}(F)+\mbox{$\langle{x}\rangle$})>\rho({\rm cyc}(F))
.

(b) Let
{\rm cyc}^{*}
be the cyclic-core operator of the dual
q
-matroid
{\mathcal{M}}^{*}
. With the aid of
2.7
(a),
3.7
and Part (a) we conclude

F\in{\mathcal{O}}({\mathcal{M}})\Rightarrow F^{\perp}\in{\mathcal{F}}({\mathcal{M}}^{*})\Rightarrow{\rm cyc}^{*}(F^{\perp})\in{\mathcal{F}}({\mathcal{M}}^{*})\Rightarrow{\rm cl}(F)^{\perp}\in{\mathcal{F}}({\mathcal{M}}^{*})\Rightarrow{\rm cl}(F)\in{\mathcal{O}}({\mathcal{M}}).

As for the consequence note that
{\rm cl}(V)
is a flat and thus (a) together with
3.8
implies that
{\rm cyc}({\rm cl}(V))
is a flat containing
{\rm cyc}(V)
.
Now the stated containment follows from
2.7
(e). The rest is clear.
∎

Using the cyclic-core operator and the closure operator we obtain a lattice consisting of the cyclic flats.
It also appears in
[
1
, Prop. 2.24]
and is the
q
-analogue of
[
9
, Prop. 3]
(which refers to
[
4
]
).

Corollary 4.2
.

Let
{\mathcal{Z}}({\mathcal{M}})={\mathcal{F}}({\mathcal{M}})\cap{\mathcal{O}}({\mathcal{M}})
, that is,
{\mathcal{Z}}({\mathcal{M}})
is the collection of cyclic flats of
{\mathcal{M}}
, or, alternatively, of open and closed spaces.
Then
({\mathcal{Z}}({\mathcal{M}}),\leq,\wedge,\vee)
is a lattice, where the meet and join are defined as

Z_{1}\wedge Z_{2}={\rm cyc}(Z_{1}\cap Z_{2})\ \text{ and }\ Z_{1}\vee Z_{2}={\rm cl}(Z_{1}+Z_{2})\ \text{ for all }Z_{1},Z_{2}\in{\mathcal{Z}}({\mathcal{M}}).

The rank values of the meet and join are given by

\rho(Z_{1}\wedge Z_{2})=\rho(Z_{1}\cap Z_{2})-\dim\big((Z_{1}\cap Z_{2})/Z_{1}\wedge Z_{2}\big)\ \text{ and }\ \rho(Z_{1}\vee Z_{2})=\rho(Z_{1}+Z_{2}).

As a consequence

\rho(Z_{1})+\rho(Z_{2})\geq\rho(Z_{1}\vee Z_{2})+\rho(Z_{1}\wedge Z_{2})+\dim\big((Z_{1}\cap Z_{2})/Z_{1}\wedge Z_{2}\big).

Proof.

By
2.7
(d)
Z_{1}\cap Z_{2}
is a flat and
Z_{1}+Z_{2}
is an open space. Hence
Z_{1}\wedge Z_{2}
and
Z_{1}\vee Z_{2}
are in
{\mathcal{Z}}({\mathcal{M}})
thanks to
Lemma

4.1
.
Next, if
V\in{\mathcal{Z}}({\mathcal{M}})
satisfies
V\leq Z_{i}
for
i=1,2
, then
V
is an open space in
Z_{1}\cap Z_{2}
and thus
V\leq{\rm cyc}(Z_{1}\cap Z_{2})
thanks to

3.6
.
Similarly, if
W\in{\mathcal{Z}}({\mathcal{M}})
satisfies
Z_{i}\leq W
for
i=1,2
, then
W
is a closed space containing
Z_{1}+Z_{2}
and thus
{\rm cl}(Z_{1}+Z_{2})\leq W
by

2.7
(e). Thus
({\mathcal{Z}}({\mathcal{M}}),\leq,\wedge,\vee)
is a lattice.
The rank value of
Z_{1}\vee Z_{2}
follows from (
2.4
), while the rank value of
Z_{1}\wedge Z_{2}
is a consequence of
3.10
(a).
The last inequality now follows from submodularity of
\rho
applied to
Z_{1}\cap Z_{2}
and
Z_{1}+Z_{2}
.
∎

Note that
{\mathcal{Z}}={\mathcal{Z}}({\mathcal{M}})
is not empty.
The least and greatest elements of the lattice
{\mathcal{Z}}
are given by
0_{{\mathcal{Z}}}={\rm cl}(0)
and
1_{{\mathcal{Z}}}={\rm cyc}(E)
and their rank values are

\rho(0_{{\mathcal{Z}}})=0\ \text{ and }\ \rho(1_{{\mathcal{Z}}})=\rho(E)-\dim(E/{\rm cyc}(E)),

where the second part follows from
3.10
.
In
4.7
below we will see that the lattice
{\mathcal{Z}}
is in general not semi-modular, and thus not graded.
But even in the case where
{\mathcal{Z}}
is graded, its height function does not agree, in general, with the rank function
\rho
.

Example 4.3

(see also
[
1
, Prop. 2.30]
)
.

Let
0<k<n=\dim E
and consider the uniform
q
-matroid
{\mathcal{U}}_{k}(E)
; see
2.5
.
The only flats other than
E
are the spaces of dimension at most
k-1
, and the only nonzero cyclic spaces are the spaces of
dimension at least
k+1
; see (
3.2
).
Thus
{\mathcal{Z}}:={\mathcal{Z}}({\mathcal{U}}_{k}(E))=\{0,E\}
.
Since this is true for every
k
, this shows that the collection
{\mathcal{Z}}({\mathcal{M}})
of a
q
-matroid
{\mathcal{M}}
does not uniquely determine
{\mathcal{M}}
.
Furthermore, since
\rho(E)=k
, we also see that the height function of the lattice
{\mathcal{Z}}

does not agree with the rank function of
{\mathcal{U}}_{k}(E)
(unless
k=1
).
Finally, for any
k
-dimensional subspace
V
we have
{\rm cl}({\rm cyc}(V))={\rm cl}(0)=0
, and

{\rm cyc}({\rm cl}(V))={\rm cyc}(E)=E
.
This shows that
{\rm cl}({\rm cyc}(V))
and
{\rm cyc}({\rm cl}(V))
do not agree.
For the trivial and the free
q
-matroid we have
{\mathcal{Z}}({\mathcal{U}}_{0}(E))=\{E\}
and
{\mathcal{Z}}({\mathcal{U}}_{n}(E))=\{0\}
.

Below we will show that we can reconstruct the entire
q
-matroid
{\mathcal{M}}
by way of the cyclic flats along with their rank values.
The following lemma will be needed. It is the
q
-analogue of
[
9
, Lem. 5]
and also appears in
[
1
, Lem. 2.23]
,
where it is proven with the aid of a characterization of cyclic spaces as inclusion-minimal spaces with respect to the nullity function.

Lemma 4.4
.

Let
V\in{\mathcal{L}}(E)
. Then
V\cap{\rm cl}({\rm cyc}(V))={\rm cyc}(V)
.

Proof.

“
\supseteq
” is clear. For the other containment let
x\in V\cap{\rm cl}({\rm cyc}(V))
. Then
\mbox{$\langle{x}\rangle$}+{\rm cyc}(V)\leq V
and

\rho(\mbox{$\langle{x}\rangle$}+{\rm cyc}(V))=\rho({\rm cyc}(V))
because
x
is in the closure of
{\rm cyc}(V)
.
Furthermore,

{\rm cyc}(V)={\rm cyc}({\rm cyc}(V))\leq{\rm cyc}(\mbox{$\langle{x}\rangle$}+{\rm cyc}(V))\leq{\rm cyc}(V)
, and thus
{\rm cyc}(V)={\rm cyc}(\mbox{$\langle{x}\rangle$}+{\rm cyc}(V))
.
With the aid of
3.10
(a) we compute

\displaystyle\dim{\rm cyc}(V)-\rho({\rm cyc}(V))

\displaystyle\leq\dim(\mbox{$\langle{x}\rangle$}+{\rm cyc}(V))-\rho({\rm cyc}(V))

\displaystyle=\dim(\mbox{$\langle{x}\rangle$}+{\rm cyc}(V))-\rho(\mbox{$\langle{x}\rangle$}+{\rm cyc}(V))

\displaystyle=\dim({\rm cyc}(\mbox{$\langle{x}\rangle$}+{\rm cyc}(V)))-\rho({\rm cyc}(\mbox{$\langle{x}\rangle$}+{\rm cyc}(V)))

\displaystyle=\dim{\rm cyc}(V)-\rho({\rm cyc}(V)).

Hence we have equality in the first step, and this means
x\in{\rm cyc}(V)
.
∎

Now we arrive at the following
q
-analogue of
[
4
, Lem. 3.1(i)]
characterizing independent spaces.
This characterization will be crucial because it
will allow us to derive the entire rank function of the
q
-matroid from the cyclic flats and their rank values.
This is different from the approach taken in
[
4
]
and in
[
1
]
for
q
-matroids, where
the entire (
q
-)matroid is reconstructed through the lattice of flats.

Theorem 4.5
.

Consider the collection
{\mathcal{Z}}({\mathcal{M}})
of cyclic flats of
{\mathcal{M}}
. Let
V\in{\mathcal{L}}(E)
. Then

V\text{ is independent}\Longleftrightarrow\dim(V\cap Z)\leq\rho(Z)\ \text{ for all }\ Z\in{\mathcal{Z}}({\mathcal{M}}).

Thus the cyclic flats together with their rank values fully determine the collection of independent spaces and thus the entire
q
-matroid
{\mathcal{M}}
.

Proof.

“
\Rightarrow
” If
V
is independent, then so is
V\cap Z
and thus
\dim(V\cap Z)=\rho(V\cap Z)\leq\rho(Z)
for any subspace
Z
.

“
\Leftarrow
” Let
V
be dependent and
Z={\rm cl}({\rm cyc}(V))
, which is in
{\mathcal{Z}}({\mathcal{M}})
.
Then
V\cap Z={\rm cyc}(V)
thanks to
Lemma

4.4
and
\rho(Z)=\rho({\rm cyc}(V))
by (
2.4
).
Now
3.10
(a) implies
\dim(V\cap Z)=\dim{\rm cyc}(V)=\rho(Z)+\dim V-\rho(V)>\rho(Z)
, where the last step follows from
the dependence of
V
.
This establishes the equivalence.
The last statement follows from the well-known fact that the independent spaces fully determine the
q
-matroid; see (
2.2
).
∎

The previous characterization of independent spaces lets us determine the rank function of the entire
q
-matroid from the cyclic
flats together with their rank values; see also
[
10
, Prop. 3]
for classical matroids.

Corollary 4.6
.

Let
{\mathcal{M}}=(E,\rho)
be a
q
-matroid and
{\mathcal{Z}}={\mathcal{Z}}({\mathcal{M}})
be its collection of cyclic flats.
Then

\rho(V)=\min_{Z\in{\mathcal{Z}}}\Big(\rho(Z)+\dim(V+Z)/Z\Big)\ \text{ for all }\ V\in{\mathcal{L}}(E).

Proof.

4.5
tells us that a space
I
is independent if and only if
\rho(Z)\geq\dim(I\cap Z)
for all
Z\in{\mathcal{Z}}
.
With the aid of the dimension formula for subspaces we may rewrite the inequality as
\dim I\leq\rho(Z)+\dim(I+Z)/Z
.
Now let
V\in{\mathcal{L}}(E)
.
Using (
2.2
) we obtain

\displaystyle\rho(V)

\displaystyle=\max\{\dim I\mid I\leq V,\ I\text{ independent}\}

\displaystyle=\max\{\dim I\mid I\leq V,\,\dim I\leq\rho(Z)+\dim(I+Z)/Z\text{ for all }Z\in{\mathcal{Z}}\}

\displaystyle\leq\max\{\dim I\mid I\leq V,\,\dim I\leq\rho(Z)+\dim(V+Z)/Z\text{ for all }Z\in{\mathcal{Z}}\}

\displaystyle\leq\min_{Z\in{\mathcal{Z}}}\Big(\rho(Z)+\dim(V+Z)/Z\Big).

For the converse consider
\hat{Z}={\rm cl}({\rm cyc}(V))
, which is in
{\mathcal{Z}}
.
With the aid of (
2.4
),
3.10
(a) and
Lemma

4.4
we arrive at

\displaystyle\min_{Z\in{\mathcal{Z}}}\Big(\rho(Z)+\dim(V+Z)/Z\Big)

\displaystyle\leq\rho(\hat{Z})+\dim(V+\hat{Z})/\hat{Z}

\displaystyle=\rho(\hat{Z})+\dim V-\dim(V\cap{\rm cl}({\rm cyc}(V))

\displaystyle=\rho({\rm cyc}(V))+\dim V-\dim{\rm cyc}(V)

\displaystyle=\rho(V).\qed

th standard basis vector).

Proposition 4.8
.

Let
{\mathcal{M}}=(E,\rho)
be a
q
-matroid with a single cyclic flat, say
{\mathcal{Z}}({\mathcal{M}})=\{\hat{Z}\}
. Thus
\hat{Z}={\rm cl}(0)={\rm cyc}(E)
and
\rho(\hat{Z})=0
.
Then for any
V\in{\mathcal{L}}(E)

V\in{\mathcal{F}}({\mathcal{M}})\Longleftrightarrow\hat{Z}\leq V\quad\text{ and }\quad V\in{\mathcal{O}}({\mathcal{M}})\Longleftrightarrow V\leq\hat{Z}.

In particular,
{\rm cl}(0)=E\Longleftrightarrow{\mathcal{M}}={\mathcal{U}}_{0}(E)\ \text{ and }\ {\rm cyc}(E)=0\Longleftrightarrow{\mathcal{M}}={\mathcal{U}}_{\dim E}(E)
.

Proof.

Note first that for any
V\in{\mathcal{L}}(E)
we have

{\rm cl}({\rm cyc}(V))={\rm cyc}({\rm cl}(V))=\hat{Z}.

(4.1)

Thus if
V
is a flat, then
\hat{Z}={\rm cyc}(V)\leq V
. Similarly, if
V
is cyclic, then
V\leq{\rm cl}(V)=\hat{Z}
.
It remains to consider the opposite implications.

1) If
V\leq\hat{Z}
, then
\rho(V)=0
and
V
is clearly cyclic.

2) Let now
\hat{Z}\leq V
. Then (
4.1
) along with
Lemma

4.4
implies
{\rm cyc}(V)=\hat{Z}
. Writing
V=V_{1}\oplus\hat{Z}
, we obtain from

3.10
(b)

\rho(V)=\rho(\hat{Z})+\dim V_{1}=\dim V_{1}.

Let now
x\in E\setminus V
. Then
\hat{Z}\leq V+\mbox{$\langle{x}\rangle$}
and we may write
V+\mbox{$\langle{x}\rangle$}=(V_{1}+\mbox{$\langle{x_{1}}\rangle$})\oplus\hat{Z}
for some
x_{1}\not\in V_{1}
.
But then the same reasoning as for
V
provides us with
\rho(V+\mbox{$\langle{x}\rangle$})=\dim(V_{1}+\mbox{$\langle{x_{1}}\rangle$})
, and hence
\rho(V+\mbox{$\langle{x}\rangle$})=\dim V_{1}+1=\rho(V)+1
.
Thus
V
is a flat.

The last two equivalences are clear.
∎

Later in
7.10
we will see that a
q
-matroid with a single cyclic flat is the direct sum of a trivial and a free
q
-matroid.

5
The Direct Sum of
q
-Matroids

In this section we turn to the direct sum of
q
-matroids.
The first definition of it has been given in
[
7
]
.
We will present a different, more concise, definition, which results in the same construction.
It will enable us to study its properties in more detail.
In particular, we will derive various ways to compute the rank function, the most efficient one being based on the cyclic flats of the components.
Moreover, we will show that the dual of a direct sum is the direct sum of the dual
q
-matroids.

We start with the union of two
q
-matroids, which is the
q
-analogue of the matroid union; see e.g.,
[
18
, Thm. 11.3.1]
.

Theorem 5.1

(
[
7
, Thm. 28]
)
.

Let
{\mathcal{M}}_{i}=(E,\rho_{i}),\,i=1,2,
be
q
-matroids on the same ground space
E
. For
V\in{\mathcal{L}}(E)
define

\rho(V)=\dim V+\min\{\rho_{1}(X)+\rho_{2}(X)-\dim X\mid X\leq V\}

(5.1)

Then
{\mathcal{M}}=(E,\rho)
is a
q
-matroid, called the
union
of
{\mathcal{M}}_{1}
and
{\mathcal{M}}_{2}
, and denoted by
{\mathcal{M}}={\mathcal{M}}_{1}\vee{\mathcal{M}}_{2}
.

Proof.

The fact that
\rho
is a rank function is in
[
7
, Thm. 29]
and also follows
from
[
13
, Thm. 3.6 and its proof]
.
The main argument in these proofs is that the map
\rho_{1}+\rho_{2}:{\mathcal{L}}(E)\longrightarrow{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
satisfies (R2) and (R3) from
2.1
.
Hence it gives rise to a
q
-polymatroid.
The minimization in (
5.1
) turns this map into a rank function of a
q
-matroid.
It mimics the analogous procedure that associates a matroid to a given polymatroid; see for instance
[
18
, Prop. 11.1.7]
.
∎

In order to define the direct sum of two
q
-matroids
{\mathcal{M}}_{i}=(E_{i},\rho_{i})
, we need, unsurprisingly, the direct sum
E=E_{1}\oplus E_{2}
of
{\mathbb{F}}
-vector spaces
E_{1}
and
E_{2}
.
For any such direct sum we denote by

\pi_{i}:E\longrightarrow E_{i}\ \text{ and }\ \iota_{i}:E_{i}\longrightarrow E

the corresponding projection and embedding.
We will identify a subspace
V_{i}\in{\mathcal{L}}(E_{i})
with its image
\iota_{i}(V_{i})
.

The construction of the direct sum consists of two steps:

(1) Add the ground space
E_{1}
of the
q
-matroid
{\mathcal{M}}_{1}=(E_{1},\rho_{1})
as a space of rank 0 to the
q
-matroid
{\mathcal{M}}_{2}=(E_{2},\rho_{2})
and vice versa.
This results in two
q
-matroids
{\mathcal{M}}^{\prime}_{1},\,{\mathcal{M}}^{\prime}_{2}
on the common ground space
E_{1}\oplus E_{2}
and with rank functions
\rho^{\prime}_{i}
.

(2) Take the union of
{\mathcal{M}}^{\prime}_{1}
and
{\mathcal{M}}^{\prime}_{2}
.

Theorem 5.2

(
[
7
, Sec. 7]
)
.

Let
{\mathcal{M}}_{i}=(E_{i},\rho_{i}),\,i=1,2,
be
q
-matroids and set
E=E_{1}\oplus E_{2}
.
Define
\rho^{\prime}_{i}:{\mathcal{L}}(E)\longrightarrow{\mathbb{N}}_{0},\ V\longmapsto\rho_{i}(\pi_{i}(V))
for
i=1,2
.
Then
{\mathcal{M}}^{\prime}_{i}=(E,\rho^{\prime}_{i})
is a
q
-matroid for
i=1,2
.
Set
{\mathcal{M}}={\mathcal{M}}^{\prime}_{1}\vee{\mathcal{M}}^{\prime}_{2}
, that is,
{\mathcal{M}}=(E,\rho)
, where

\rho(V)=\dim V+\min_{X\leq V}\big(\rho^{\prime}_{1}(X)+\rho^{\prime}_{2}(X)-\dim X\big)\ \text{ for }V\in{\mathcal{L}}(E).

(5.2)

Then
{\mathcal{M}}
is a
q
-matroid, called the
direct sum of
{\mathcal{M}}_{1}
and
{\mathcal{M}}_{2}
and denoted by
{\mathcal{M}}_{1}\oplus{\mathcal{M}}_{2}
.

Proof.

A short proof showing that
{\mathcal{M}}^{\prime}_{i}
is a
q
-matroid can be found in
[
12
, Thm. 5.2]
.
The rest follows from
5.1
.
∎

We clearly have
\rho^{\prime}_{i}(E_{j})=0
for
i\neq j
.
Even more, one can easily verify that
{\mathcal{M}}^{\prime}_{i}\approx{\mathcal{M}}_{i}\oplus{\mathcal{U}}_{0}(E_{j})
, where
{\mathcal{U}}_{0}(E_{j})
is the trivial
q
-matroid on the ground
space
E_{j}
. Thus
{\mathcal{M}}^{\prime}_{i}
is a special instance of the direct sum (called “adding a loop space” in
[
7
]
).
Some additional basic and to-be-expected properties of the direct sum will be presented below in
5.6
after deriving
more convenient expressions for the rank function.

Before doing so, we mention the following result, which implies that
{\mathcal{M}}_{1}\oplus{\mathcal{M}}_{2}
is the
q
-matroid on
E=E_{1}\oplus E_{2}
with the
“least amount of conditions” among all
q
-matroids on
E
whose restriction to
E_{i}
is isomorphic to
{\mathcal{M}}_{i}
for
i=1,2
.

Theorem 5.3

(
[
12
, Thm. 5.5]
)
.

Let
{\mathcal{M}}_{i}=(E_{i},\rho_{i}),\,i=1,2,
be
q
-matroids and
{\mathcal{M}}={\mathcal{M}}_{1}\oplus{\mathcal{M}}_{2}=(E,\rho)
be the direct sum.
Then
({\mathcal{M}},\iota_{1},\iota_{2})
is a coproduct of
{\mathcal{M}}_{1}
and
{\mathcal{M}}_{2}
in the category of
q
-matroids with linear weak maps as morphisms.
In other words, if
{\mathcal{N}}=(E,\tau)
is any
q
-matroid satisfying
\tau(V_{i})\leq\rho_{i}(V_{i})
for all
V\in{\mathcal{L}}(E_{i})
and
i=1,2
, then

\rho(V)\geq\tau(V)
for all
V\in{\mathcal{L}}(E)
.
As a consequence, the independent spaces satisfy
{\mathcal{I}}({\mathcal{N}})\subseteq{\mathcal{I}}({\mathcal{M}})
.

The definition of the rank function of
{\mathcal{M}}_{1}\oplus{\mathcal{M}}_{2}
in (
5.2
) becomes quickly very cumbersome as it requires computing
the minimum over all subspaces of
V
.
We now derive various more efficient ways of determining the rank values.
The most convenient one is given in
5.9
below, which only requires the cyclic flats of
{\mathcal{M}}_{1}
and
{\mathcal{M}}_{2}
.
We start with the following simple improvements.

Proposition 5.4
.

Consider the situation of
5.2
.
Define the sets

\displaystyle{\mathcal{X}}

\displaystyle=\{X\in{\mathcal{L}}(E)\mid\rho_{1}^{\prime}(X)+\rho_{2}^{\prime}(X)<\dim X\},

(5.3)

\displaystyle{\mathcal{T}}

\displaystyle=\{X_{1}\oplus X_{2}\mid X_{i}\in{\mathcal{L}}(E_{i})\},

\displaystyle{\mathcal{T}}(V)

\displaystyle=\{X_{1}\oplus X_{2}\mid X_{i}\leq\pi_{i}(V)\}\ \text{ for }\ V\in{\mathcal{L}}(E).

Then for any
V\in{\mathcal{L}}(E)

\displaystyle\rho(V)

\displaystyle=\dim V+\min_{X\in\{0\}\cup({\mathcal{X}}\cap{\mathcal{L}}(V))}\big(\rho^{\prime}_{1}(X)+\rho^{\prime}_{2}(X)-\dim X\big)

(5.4)

\displaystyle=\dim V+\min_{X\in{\mathcal{T}}}\big(\rho_{1}^{\prime}(X)+\rho_{2}^{\prime}(X)-\dim(X\cap V)\big)

(5.5)

\displaystyle=\dim V+\min_{X\in{\mathcal{T}}(V)}\big(\rho_{1}^{\prime}(X)+\rho_{2}^{\prime}(X)-\dim(X\cap V)\big).

(5.6)

As a consequence,
V
is independent in
{\mathcal{M}}_{1}\oplus{\mathcal{M}}_{2}
iff
{\mathcal{L}}(V)\cap{\mathcal{X}}=\emptyset
.

Proof.

1) Using
X=0
in (
5.2
) we obtain
\rho(V)\leq\dim V
(as it has to be).
Hence we only have to test the subspaces
X
of
V
that may lead to
\rho(V)<\dim V
. This is exactly the collection
{\mathcal{X}}
, and thus
(
5.4
) is established. This identity also implies the statement about the independent spaces.

2)
Let
M_{1}:=\min_{X\leq V}\big(\rho^{\prime}_{1}(X)+\rho^{\prime}_{2}(X)-\dim X\big)
and
M_{2}:=\min_{X\leq E}\big(\rho^{\prime}_{1}(X)+\rho^{\prime}_{2}(X)-\dim(X\cap V)\big)
.
Clearly
M_{2}\leq M_{1}
.
For the converse inequality let
\hat{X}\leq E
be such that

M_{2}=\rho^{\prime}_{1}(\hat{X})+\rho^{\prime}_{2}(\hat{X})-\dim(\hat{X}\cap V)
.
Set
\tilde{X}:=\hat{X}\cap V
.
Using monotonicity of the rank function we obtain

M_{1}\leq\rho^{\prime}_{1}(\tilde{X})+\rho^{\prime}_{2}(\tilde{X})-\dim\tilde{X}\leq\rho^{\prime}_{1}(\hat{X})+\rho^{\prime}_{2}(\hat{X})-\dim(\hat{X}\cap V)=M_{2}
.
Thus
M_{1}=M_{2}
and

\rho(V)=\dim V+\min_{X\leq E}\big(\rho_{1}^{\prime}(X)+\rho_{2}^{\prime}(X)-\dim(X\cap V)\big).

(5.7)

Actually, this expression for the rank function of the direct sum appears in
[
7
, Thm. 25]
; see also
[
7
, Rem. 26]
.
Now we are ready to prove (
5.5
).
Recall that
\rho^{\prime}_{i}(X)=\rho_{i}(\pi_{i}(X))
. Hence
\rho_{1}^{\prime}(X)+\rho_{2}^{\prime}(X)
only depends on the projections
\pi_{i}(X)
.
Since
X\leq\pi_{1}(X)\oplus\pi_{2}(X)=:X^{\prime}
for any subspace
X
, we have

\rho_{1}^{\prime}(X)+\rho_{2}^{\prime}(X)-\dim(X\cap V)\geq\rho_{1}^{\prime}(X^{\prime})+\rho_{2}^{\prime}(X^{\prime})-\dim(X^{\prime}\cap V)
.
This shows that it suffices to take the minimum in (
5.7
) over subspaces
X
satisfying
X=\pi_{1}(X)\oplus\pi_{2}(X)
.
But this is exactly the collection
{\mathcal{T}}
, and hence (
5.5
) is established.

3) Let
X=X_{1}\oplus X_{2}\in{\mathcal{T}}
. Then
X\cap V\subseteq Y_{1}\oplus Y_{2}
, where
Y_{i}=X_{i}\cap\pi_{i}(V)
.
Set
Y:=Y_{1}\oplus Y_{2}
. Then
Y\in{\mathcal{T}}(V)
and
X\cap V\subseteq Y\cap V
. Thus

\rho_{1}(Y_{1})+\rho_{2}(Y_{2})-\dim(Y\cap V)\leq\rho_{1}(X_{1})+\rho_{2}(X_{2})-\dim(X\cap V)
.
This shows that the minimum in (
5.5
) is attained by a subspace in
{\mathcal{T}}(V)
and (
5.6
) is proven.
∎

The above allows an immediate characterization of the circuits of the direct sum.
The result can already be found in
[
12
, Thm. 5.2]
.

Corollary 5.5
.

Consider the direct sum
{\mathcal{M}}_{1}\oplus{\mathcal{M}}_{2}
as in
5.2
and the set
{\mathcal{X}}
in (
5.3
).
Then the circuits of the direct sum are given by

{\mathcal{C}}({\mathcal{M}}_{1}\oplus{\mathcal{M}}_{2})=\{X\in{\mathcal{X}}\mid X\text{ is inclusion-minimal in }{\mathcal{X}}\}.

As to be expected, the direct sum behaves well with respect to restriction to the initial ground spaces
E_{i}
and the according contraction.
Recall Definitions
2.4
and
2.6
.

Theorem 5.6

(
[
7
, Thm. 47, Cor. 48]
)
.

Let
{\mathcal{M}}={\mathcal{M}}_{1}\oplus{\mathcal{M}}_{2}
be as in
5.2
.

(a)

For all
V_{i}\in{\mathcal{L}}(E_{i})
we have
\rho_{i}(V_{i})=\rho^{\prime}_{i}(V_{i})
and
\rho^{\prime}_{j}(V_{i})=0
for
j\neq i
.
Furthermore,
\rho(V_{1}\oplus V_{2})=\rho_{1}(V_{1})+\rho_{2}(V_{2})
.
Hence
{\mathcal{M}}_{i}\approx{\mathcal{M}}|_{E_{i}}\approx({\mathcal{M}}^{\prime}_{i})|_{E_{i}}
, and the isomorphism is provided by
\iota_{i}
.
Moreover, for
i\neq j
the
q
-matroid
({\mathcal{M}}^{\prime}_{i})|_{E_{j}}
is the zero
q
-matroid.

(b)

\rho({\mathcal{M}})=\rho_{1}({\mathcal{M}}_{1})+\rho_{2}({\mathcal{M}}_{2})
.

(c)

{\mathcal{M}}/E_{i}\approx{\mathcal{M}}_{j}
for
i\neq j
.

Proof.

(a) The first statement follows from
\rho_{i}^{\prime}(V_{i})=\rho_{i}(\pi_{i}(V_{i}))=\rho_{i}(V_{i})
.
Let now
V=V_{1}\oplus V_{2}
.
By (
5.6
) there exist
X=X_{1}\oplus X_{2}
for some
X_{i}\leq V_{i}
such that

\rho(V)=\dim V+\rho_{1}^{\prime}(X_{1})+\rho_{2}^{\prime}(X_{2})-\dim X
.
Write
V_{i}=X_{i}\oplus Z_{i}
for some
Z_{i}\leq V_{i}
.
Using the properties of the rank functions
\rho_{i}^{\prime}
we compute

\displaystyle\rho(V)

\displaystyle=\rho_{1}^{\prime}(X_{1})+\rho_{2}^{\prime}(X_{2})+\dim Z_{1}+\dim Z_{2}\geq\rho_{1}^{\prime}(X_{1})+\rho_{2}^{\prime}(X_{2})+\rho_{1}^{\prime}(Z_{1})+\rho_{2}^{\prime}(Z_{2})

\displaystyle\geq\rho_{1}^{\prime}(X_{1}\oplus Z_{1})+\rho_{2}^{\prime}(X_{2}\oplus Z_{2})=\rho_{1}^{\prime}(V_{1})+\rho_{2}^{\prime}(V_{2})

\displaystyle=\rho_{1}(V_{1})+\rho_{2}(V_{2})\geq\rho(V),

where the last step follows from (
5.6
) with
X=V
.
This establishes the desired identity.
The isomorphisms follow from
\rho(V_{i})=\rho_{i}(V_{i})=\rho^{\prime}_{i}(V_{i})
, and the rest is clear.

(b) is a consequence of (a) because
\rho({\mathcal{M}})=\rho(E)=\rho_{1}(E_{1})+\rho_{2}(E_{2})
.

(c) Without loss of generality let
i=1
.
Denote the rank function of
{\mathcal{M}}/E_{1}
by
\hat{\rho}_{1}
.
Let
V\in{\mathcal{L}}(E_{2})
. Then

\hat{\rho}_{1}(V/E_{1})=\rho(E_{1}\oplus V)-\rho(E_{1})=\rho_{1}(E_{1})+\rho_{2}(V)-\rho_{1}(E_{1})=\rho_{2}(V)
,
where the last identity follows from (a).
Hence
{\mathcal{M}}_{2}
and
{\mathcal{M}}/E_{1}
are equivalent via the isomorphism
\hat{\pi}:E_{2}\longrightarrow E/E_{1}
induced by the
canonical projection from
E
onto
E/E_{1}
.
∎

Now we obtain a very efficient way of computing the rank function of the direct sum which only requires the collections
of cyclic flats of the two summands. The following notation we be convenient for the rest of this paper.

Notation 5.7
.

Let
E=E_{1}\oplus E_{2}
and
{\mathcal{Y}}_{i}
be collections of subspaces of
E_{i}
for
i=1,2
. We define

{\mathcal{Y}}_{1}\oplus{\mathcal{Y}}_{2}=\{Y_{1}\oplus Y_{2}\mid Y_{i}\in{\mathcal{Y}}_{i}\}
.

Theorem 5.8
.

Let
{\mathcal{M}}_{i}=(E_{i},\rho_{i}),\,i=1,2,
be
q
-matroids and
let
{\mathcal{F}}_{i}={\mathcal{F}}({\mathcal{M}}_{i})
and
{\mathcal{Z}}_{i}={\mathcal{Z}}({\mathcal{M}}_{i})
, that is,
{\mathcal{F}}_{i}
(resp.
{\mathcal{Z}}_{i}
) are the collections of flats (resp. cyclic flats) of
{\mathcal{M}}_{i}
.
Let
\rho
be the rank function of
{\mathcal{M}}_{1}\oplus{\mathcal{M}}_{2}
. Then for all
V\in{\mathcal{L}}(E_{1}\oplus E_{2})
we have

\displaystyle\rho(V)

\displaystyle=\dim V+\min_{F_{1}\oplus F_{2}\in{\mathcal{F}}_{1}\oplus{\mathcal{F}}_{2}}\big(\rho_{1}(F_{1})+\rho_{2}(F_{2})-\dim((F_{1}\oplus F_{2})\cap V)\big)

(5.8)

\displaystyle=\dim V+\min_{Z_{1}\oplus Z_{2}\in{\mathcal{Z}}_{1}\oplus{\mathcal{Z}}_{2}}\big(\rho_{1}(Z_{1})+\rho_{2}(Z_{2})-\dim((Z_{1}\oplus Z_{2})\cap V)\big).

(5.9)

Proof.

1) We use (
5.5
).
Let
X=X_{1}\oplus X_{2}\in{\mathcal{T}}
.
Clearly,
\rho^{\prime}_{i}(X)=\rho_{i}(X_{i})
for
i=1,2
.
Suppose
X_{1}\not\in{\mathcal{F}}_{1}
. Then there exists
X_{1}^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{L}}(E_{1})
such that
X_{1}\lneq X_{1}^{\prime}
and

\rho_{1}(X_{1})=\rho_{1}(X_{1}^{\prime})
. Therefore
X\lneq X_{1}^{\prime}\oplus X_{2}=:X^{\prime}
and

\rho_{1}(X_{1}^{\prime})+\rho_{2}(X_{2})-\dim(X^{\prime}\cap V)\leq\rho_{1}(X_{1})+\rho_{2}(X_{2})-\dim(X\cap V)
.
The same argument applies to
X_{2}
, and this shows that the minimum in (
5.5
) is attained by a space in
{\mathcal{F}}_{1}\oplus{\mathcal{F}}_{2}
.
This establishes (
5.8
).

2) Let
V\in{\mathcal{L}}(E)
and set

\displaystyle M_{1}

\displaystyle=\min_{F_{1}\oplus F_{2}\in{\mathcal{F}}_{1}\oplus{\mathcal{F}}_{2}}\big(\rho_{1}(F_{1})+\rho_{2}(F_{2})-\dim((F_{1}\oplus F_{2})\cap V)\big),

\displaystyle M_{2}

\displaystyle=\min_{Z_{1}\oplus Z_{2}\in{\mathcal{Z}}_{1}\oplus{\mathcal{Z}}_{2}}\big(\rho_{1}(Z_{1})+\rho_{2}(Z_{2})-\dim((Z_{1}\oplus Z_{2})\cap V)\big).

Clearly
M_{1}\leq M_{2}
. For the converse let
\hat{F}:=\hat{F}_{1}\oplus\hat{F}_{2}\in{\mathcal{F}}_{1}\oplus{\mathcal{F}}_{2}
be such that

M_{1}=\rho_{1}(\hat{F}_{1})+\rho_{2}(\hat{F}_{2})-\dim(\hat{F}\cap V)
.
Set
Z_{i}={\rm cyc}_{i}(\hat{F}_{i})
, where
{\rm cyc}_{i}(\,\cdot\,)
is the cyclic core in the
q
-matroid
{\mathcal{M}}_{i}
.
Then
Z_{i}
is in
{\mathcal{Z}}_{i}
thanks to
Lemma

4.1
.
Moreover,
Z=Z_{1}\oplus Z_{2}\leq\hat{F}
and
\rho_{i}(\hat{F}_{i})=\rho_{i}(Z_{i})+\dim(\hat{F}_{i}/Z_{i})
by
3.10
.
Now we compute

\displaystyle M_{1}

\displaystyle=\rho_{1}(\hat{F}_{1})+\rho_{2}(\hat{F}_{2})-\dim(\hat{F}\cap V)

\displaystyle=\rho_{1}(Z_{1})+\dim(\hat{F}_{1}/Z_{1})+\rho_{2}(Z_{2})+\dim(\hat{F}_{2}/Z_{2})-\dim\hat{F}-\dim V+\dim(\hat{F}+V)

\displaystyle\geq\rho_{1}(Z_{1})+\rho_{2}(Z_{2})-\dim Z-\dim V+\dim(Z+V)

\displaystyle=\rho_{1}(Z_{1})+\rho_{2}(Z_{2})-\dim(Z\cap V)\geq M_{2}.

Hence
M_{1}=M_{2}
, which establishes (
5.9
).
∎

The identity (
5.9
) can be rewritten as the following convenient identity. Note its resemblance with
4.6
.
Using this identity we will prove in the next section that
{\mathcal{Z}}_{1}\oplus{\mathcal{Z}}_{2}
is the collection of cyclic flats of

, and thus

.

Corollary 5.9
.

In the situation of
5.8
we have

\rho(V)=\min_{Z\in{\mathcal{Z}}_{1}\oplus{\mathcal{Z}}_{2}}\big(\rho(Z)+\dim(V+Z)/Z\big)\ \text{ for all }\ V\in{\mathcal{L}}(E_{1}\oplus E_{2}).

Proof.

Let
Z=Z_{1}\oplus Z_{2}\in{\mathcal{Z}}_{1}\oplus{\mathcal{Z}}_{2}
. With the aid of

(a) we compute

\displaystyle\rho_{1}(Z_{1})+\rho_{2}(Z_{2})-\dim(Z\cap V)

\displaystyle=\rho(Z)-\dim Z-\dim V+\dim(Z+V)

\displaystyle=\rho(Z)+\dim(Z+V)/Z-\dim V,

and the result follows from (
5.9
).
∎

In the last part of this section we turn to the dual of the direct sum.
As shown next, it is the direct sum of the dual
q
-matroids if taken with respect to compatible NSBFs.
Defining duality with respect to lattice anti-isomorphisms, the result below appears also in
[
7
, Thm. 50]
.
We will comment on the relation in

.

Theorem 5.10
.

Let
{\mathcal{M}}_{i}=(E_{i},\rho_{i}),\,i=1,2,
be
q
-matroids. Set
E=E_{1}\oplus E_{2}
.
Choose NSBFs
\mbox{$\langle{\cdot}\!\mid\!{\cdot}\rangle$}_{i}
on
E_{i}
and set

\mbox{$\langle{v_{1}+v_{2}}\!\mid\!{w_{1}+w_{2}}\rangle$}=\mbox{$\langle{v_{1}}\!\mid\!{w_{1}}\rangle$}_{1}+\mbox{$\langle{v_{2}}\!\mid\!{w_{2}}\rangle$}_{2}\ \text{ for all }v_{i},w_{i}\in E_{i}
.
Then
\langle{\cdot}\!\mid\!{\cdot}\rangle
is an NSBF on
E
and

({\mathcal{M}}_{1}\oplus{\mathcal{M}}_{2})^{*}={\mathcal{M}}_{1}^{*}\oplus{\mathcal{M}}_{2}^{*},

where
{\mathcal{M}}_{i}^{*}
and
({\mathcal{M}}_{1}\oplus{\mathcal{M}}_{2})^{*}
are the dual
q
-matroids with respect to the given NSBFs.

Proof.

It is easy to see that
\langle{\cdot}\!\mid\!{\cdot}\rangle
is an NSBF on
E
.
We denote the corresponding orthogonal space of
V\leq E
by
V^{\perp}
, while
for
W\leq E_{i}
we use
W^{\perp(i)}
for the orthogonal of
W
in
E_{i}
with respect to
\mbox{$\langle{\cdot}\!\mid\!{\cdot}\rangle$}_{i}
.
By construction we have

(V_{1}\oplus V_{2})^{\perp}=V_{1}^{\perp(1)}\oplus V_{2}^{\perp(2)}\ \text{ for all }\ V_{i}\in{\mathcal{L}}(E_{i}).

Let
{\mathcal{M}}_{1}^{*}\oplus{\mathcal{M}}_{2}^{*}=(E,\tilde{\rho})
and
{\mathcal{M}}_{1}\oplus{\mathcal{M}}_{2}=(E,\rho)
.
Then
({\mathcal{M}}_{1}\oplus{\mathcal{M}}_{2})^{*}=(E,\rho^{*})
with
\rho^{*}
as in (
2.1
).
We have to show that
\rho^{*}(V)=\tilde{\rho}(V)
for all
V\in{\mathcal{L}}(E)
.
We will use (
5.5
) for the rank function
\rho
(
5.9
does not simplify the computations.).
Writing
X=X_{1}\oplus X_{2}
for
X\in{\mathcal{T}}
we have

\tilde{\rho}(V)=\dim V+\min_{X\in{\mathcal{T}}}\big(\rho_{1}^{*}(X_{1})+\rho_{2}^{*}(X_{2})-\dim(X\cap V)\big).

With the aid of
5.6
(a) we obtain for any
X=X_{1}\oplus X_{2}\in{\mathcal{T}}

\displaystyle\rho_{1}^{*}(X_{1})+\rho_{2}^{*}(X_{2})-\dim(X\cap V)

\displaystyle=\dim X_{1}-\rho_{1}(E_{1})+\rho_{1}(X_{1}^{\perp(1)})+\dim X_{2}-\rho_{2}(E_{2})+\rho_{2}(X_{2}^{\perp(2)})-\dim(X\cap V)

\displaystyle=\dim X-\rho(E)+\rho(X^{\perp})-\dim E+\dim(X\cap V)^{\perp}

\displaystyle=\dim X-\rho(E)+\rho(X^{\perp})-\dim E+\dim(X^{\perp}+V^{\perp})

\displaystyle=\dim X-\rho(E)+\rho(X^{\perp})-\dim E+\dim X^{\perp}+\dim V^{\perp}-\dim(X^{\perp}\cap V^{\perp})

\displaystyle=\dim V^{\perp}-\rho(E)+\rho(X^{\perp})-\dim(X^{\perp}\cap V^{\perp}).

Using
\{X^{\perp}\mid X\in{\mathcal{T}}\}={\mathcal{T}}
and again (
5.5
) we now arrive at

\displaystyle\tilde{\rho}(V)

\displaystyle=\dim E-\rho(E)+\min_{X\in{\mathcal{T}}}\big(\rho(X^{\perp})-\dim(X^{\perp}\cap V^{\perp})\big)

\displaystyle=\dim E-\rho(E)+\min_{X\in{\mathcal{T}}}\big(\rho(X)-\dim(X\cap V^{\perp})\big)

\displaystyle=\dim V-\rho(E)+\dim V^{\perp}+\min_{X\in{\mathcal{T}}}\big(\rho_{1}(X_{1})+\rho_{2}(X_{2})-\dim(X\cap V^{\perp})\big)

\displaystyle=\dim V-\rho(E)+\rho(V^{\perp})=\rho^{*}(V),

as desired.
∎

Remark 5.11
.

In
[
7
, Def. 6]
the authors define duality of
q
-matroids with respect to an involutory anti-isomorphism on the subspace lattice
{\mathcal{L}}(E)
.
Denoting such an anti-isomorphism by
\perp
, the definition of
{\mathcal{M}}^{*}
reads exactly as in
2.3
.
Since the orthogonal complement with respect to a chosen NSBF induces a lattice anti-isomorphism, the duality result in
[
7
, Thm. 50]
appears
to be more general than
5.10
.
However, as we now briefly discuss, the two results differ only by a semi-linear isomorphism on
E
(if
\dim E\geq 3
).
Indeed, choose an NSBF on
E
and denote the corresponding orthogonal space of
V\leq E
by

V^{\raisebox{0.60275pt}{\mbox{$\scriptstyle\perp\hskip-2.695pt\underline{\hskip 2.54802pt}$}}}\hskip 1.99997pt
.
Then
\tau:{\mathcal{L}}(E)\longrightarrow{\mathcal{L}}(E),\ V\longmapsto V^{\raisebox{0.60275pt}{\mbox{$\scriptstyle\perp\hskip-2.695pt\underline{\hskip 2.54802pt}$}}}\hskip 1.99997pt
is an anti-isomorphism on the lattice
{\mathcal{L}}(E)
.
Let now
\perp
be any anti-isomorphism on
{\mathcal{L}}(E)
. Then
\tau\circ\!\perp
is a lattice isomorphism and thanks to the Fundamental
Theorem of Projective Geometry (see for instance
[
2
, Ch. II.10]
or
[
19
, Thm. 1]
)
there exists a semi-linear isomorphism
f:E\longrightarrow E
such that
\tau(V^{\perp})=f(V)
for all
V\in{\mathcal{L}}(E)
.
In other words,

(V^{\perp})^{\raisebox{0.60275pt}{\mbox{$\scriptstyle\perp\hskip-2.695pt\underline{\hskip 2.54802pt}$}}}\hskip 1.99997pt=f(V)
or

V^{\perp}=f(V)^{\raisebox{0.60275pt}{\mbox{$\scriptstyle\perp\hskip-2.695pt\underline{\hskip 2.54802pt}$}}}\hskip 1.99997pt
for all
V\in{\mathcal{L}}(E)
.
This shows that the lattice anti-isomorphism
\perp
differs from the one induced by the chosen NSBF by the semi-linear isomorphism on
E
.
Denote the dual rank function of
{\mathcal{M}}=(E,\rho)
with respect to
\perp
and
\tau
by

\rho^{*(\perp)}
and
\rho^{*(\raisebox{0.60275pt}{\mbox{$\scriptstyle\perp\hskip-2.695pt\underline{\hskip 2.54802pt}$}})}
,
respectively.
Then

\rho^{*(\perp)}(V)=\rho^{*(\raisebox{0.60275pt}{\mbox{$\scriptstyle\perp\hskip-2.695pt\underline{\hskip 2.54802pt}$}})}(f(V))
and thus the two
dual
q
-matroids differ only by the semi-linear isomorphism
f
.
This shows that
[
7
, Thm. 50]
is a consequence
5.10
above, which has a significantly shorter and simpler proof.

6
The Cyclic Flats of the Direct Sum

In this short section we show that the cyclic flats of a direct sum
{\mathcal{M}}_{1}\oplus{\mathcal{M}}_{2}
is the collection of the direct sums of the cyclic flats of the two components
{\mathcal{M}}_{1}
and
{\mathcal{M}}_{2}
.
The following lemma is needed.

Lemma 6.1
.

(a)

Let
{\mathcal{M}}=(E,\rho)
be a
q
-matroid. Suppose
F\in{\mathcal{F}}({\mathcal{M}})
and
O\in{\mathcal{O}}({\mathcal{M}})
are such that
F\lneq O
. Then

0<\rho(O)-\rho(F)<\dim O-\dim F
.

(b)

Let
{\mathcal{M}}_{i}=(E_{i},\rho_{i}),\,i=1,2,
be
q
-matroids and
\rho
be the rank function of
{\mathcal{M}}_{1}\oplus{\mathcal{M}}_{2}
.
Let
F=F_{1}\oplus F_{2}
with
F_{i}\in{\mathcal{F}}({\mathcal{M}}_{i})
and
O=O_{1}\oplus O_{2}
with
O_{i}\in{\mathcal{O}}({\mathcal{M}}_{i})

be such that
F\lneq O
.
Then
0<\rho(O)-\rho(F)<\dim O-\dim F
.

Proof.

Since
F
is a flat, we clearly have
0<\rho(O)-\rho(F)
.
Furthermore, let
O=F\oplus T
for some
T\leq O
. Then (R1)–(R3) for
\rho
imply

\rho(O)\leq\rho(F)+\rho(T)\leq\rho(F)+\dim T=\rho(F)+\dim O-\dim F
.
We show that the first inequality is strict.
To do so, let
U\in\mbox{$\text{Hyp}$}(T)
.
Then
F\oplus U\in\mbox{$\text{Hyp}$}(O)
.
Thus cyclicity of
O
implies
\rho(O)=\rho(F\oplus U)\leq\rho(F)+\rho(U)<\rho(F)+\dim T
, as desired.

(b) By assumption
F_{i}\leq O_{i}
for
i=1,2
.
Without loss of generality we may assume
F_{1}\lneq O_{1}
.
With the aid of
5.6
(a) and Part (a) we compute

\displaystyle\rho(O)-\rho(F)

\displaystyle=\rho_{1}(O_{1})-\rho_{1}(F_{1})+\rho_{2}(O_{2})-\rho_{2}(F_{2})

\displaystyle<\dim O_{1}-\dim F_{1}+\dim O_{2}-\dim F_{2}=\dim O-\dim F.

The second expression also shows that
\rho(O)-\rho(F)>0
.
∎

Now we are ready for our main result.

Theorem 6.2
.

Let
{\mathcal{M}}_{i}=(E_{i},\rho_{i}),\,i=1,2,
be
q
-matroids and
{\mathcal{Z}}_{i}={\mathcal{Z}}({\mathcal{M}}_{i})
.
As in
5.7
let
{\mathcal{Z}}_{1}\oplus{\mathcal{Z}}_{2}=\{Z_{1}\oplus Z_{2}\mid Z_{i}\in{\mathcal{Z}}_{i}\}
.
Then

{\mathcal{Z}}({\mathcal{M}}_{1}\oplus{\mathcal{M}}_{2})={\mathcal{Z}}_{1}\oplus{\mathcal{Z}}_{2}.

Proof.

“
\supseteq
” Let
V\in{\mathcal{Z}}_{1}\oplus{\mathcal{Z}}_{2}
. Then
V=V_{1}\oplus V_{2}
for some
V_{i}\in{\mathcal{Z}}_{i}
.

a) We show that
V
is a flat in
{\mathcal{M}}_{1}\oplus{\mathcal{M}}_{2}
. Let
x\in E\setminus V
.
We need to show that
\rho(V+\mbox{$\langle{x}\rangle$})=\rho(V)+1
.
By
5.9
there exists
\hat{Z}=\hat{Z}_{1}\oplus\hat{Z}_{2}\in{\mathcal{Z}}_{1}\oplus{\mathcal{Z}}_{2}
such that

\rho(V+\mbox{$\langle{x}\rangle$})=\rho(\hat{Z})+\dim((V+\mbox{$\langle{x}\rangle$}+\hat{Z})/\hat{Z}).

If
\hat{Z}=V
, then this implies
\rho(V+\mbox{$\langle{x}\rangle$})=\rho(V)+1
, as desired.
Let now
\hat{Z}\neq V
and set
F:=V\cap\hat{Z}=(V_{1}\cap\hat{Z}_{1})\oplus(V_{2}\cap\hat{Z}_{2})
.
Since each
V_{i}\cap\hat{Z}_{i}
is a flat (see
2.7
),
F
is of the form
F=F_{1}\oplus F_{2}
, where
F_{i}\in{\mathcal{F}}({\mathcal{M}}_{i})
.
By assumption
V_{i}
are cyclic spaces, and thus we may apply
Lemma

6.1
(b) to
F\lneq V
.
This leads to

\rho(V)-\rho(\hat{Z})\leq\rho(V)-\rho(F)<\dim V-\dim(F)=\dim(V/F),

(6.1)

which in turn implies

\rho(V+\mbox{$\langle{x}\rangle$})=\rho(\hat{Z})+\dim((V+\mbox{$\langle{x}\rangle$}+\hat{Z})/\hat{Z})>\rho(V)-\dim(V/F)+\dim((V+\mbox{$\langle{x}\rangle$}+\hat{Z})/\hat{Z}).

Since
\dim(V/F)=\dim(V/(V\cap\hat{Z}))=\dim((V+\hat{Z})/\hat{Z})\leq\dim((V+\mbox{$\langle{x}\rangle$}+\hat{Z})/\hat{Z})
, we conclude that

\rho(V+\mbox{$\langle{x}\rangle$})>\rho(V)
, as desired.

b) We show that
V
is cyclic. Let
D\in\mbox{$\text{Hyp}$}(V)
. By
5.9
there exists
\hat{Z}\in{\mathcal{Z}}_{1}\oplus{\mathcal{Z}}_{2}
such that

\rho(D)=\rho(\hat{Z})+\dim((D+\hat{Z})/\hat{Z}).

If
\hat{Z}=V
, then this implies
\rho(D)=\rho(V)
, as desired. Thus let
\hat{Z}\neq V
.
As above, we set
F:=V\cap\hat{Z}
and apply
Lemma

6.1
(b) to
F\lneq V
.
Thus we have again (
6.1
) and compute

\displaystyle\rho(D)

\displaystyle=\rho(\hat{Z})+\dim((D+\hat{Z})/\hat{Z})

\displaystyle>\rho(V)-\dim(V/V\cap\hat{Z})+\dim(D/D\cap\hat{Z})=\rho(V)-1+\dim(V\cap\hat{Z})-\dim(D\cap\hat{Z})

\displaystyle\geq\rho(V)-1.

This shows
\rho(D)=\rho(V)
.

“
\subseteq
” Let
Z\in{\mathcal{Z}}({\mathcal{M}}_{1}\oplus{\mathcal{M}}_{2})
. Again, by
5.9
there exists
\hat{Z}\in{\mathcal{Z}}_{1}\oplus{\mathcal{Z}}_{2}
such that

\rho(Z)=\rho(\hat{Z})+\dim((Z+\hat{Z})/\hat{Z}).

(6.2)

Since
Z
is cyclic, every space
D\in\mbox{$\text{Hyp}$}(Z)
satisfies

\rho(D)=\rho(Z)=\rho(\hat{Z})+\dim((Z+\hat{Z})/\hat{Z})\geq\rho(\hat{Z})+\dim((D+\hat{Z})/\hat{Z})\geq\rho(D),

and hence
\dim((D+\hat{Z})/\hat{Z})=\dim((Z+\hat{Z})/\hat{Z})
. Since this is true for every
D\in\mbox{$\text{Hyp}$}(Z)
, we conclude
Z\leq\hat{Z}
.
Next,
Z
is a flat and thus every
x\in E\setminus Z
satisfies

\rho(Z)<\rho(Z+\mbox{$\langle{x}\rangle$})\leq\rho(\hat{Z})+\dim((Z+\mbox{$\langle{x}\rangle$}+\hat{Z})/\hat{Z}).

Together with (
6.2
) this implies
\dim(Z+\hat{Z})<\dim(Z+\mbox{$\langle{x}\rangle$}+\hat{Z})
, and thus
x\not\in\hat{Z}
.
Since this is true for every
x\in E\setminus Z
, we conclude that
\hat{Z}\leq Z
.
All of this shows that
Z=\hat{Z}
and thus
Z\in{\mathcal{Z}}_{1}\oplus{\mathcal{Z}}_{2}
.
This concludes the proof.
∎

6.2
in combination with
5.9
immediately implies
associativity of the direct sum operation (which is not obvious from the very definition of the direct sum).
The result will be crucial for the decomposition of
q
-matroids in the next section.

Corollary 6.3
.

Let
{\mathcal{M}}_{i},\,i=1,2,3
, be
q
-matroids. Then
({\mathcal{M}}_{1}\oplus{\mathcal{M}}_{2})\oplus{\mathcal{M}}_{3}={\mathcal{M}}_{1}\oplus({\mathcal{M}}_{2}\oplus{\mathcal{M}}_{3})
.

The analogous identity as in
6.2
is not true for the flats, independent spaces, circuits etc.

Remark 6.4
.

Let
{\mathcal{M}}_{i}=(E_{i},\rho_{i}),\,i=1,2,
and
{\mathcal{M}}={\mathcal{M}}_{1}\oplus{\mathcal{M}}_{2}
. Recall
5.7
and
denote by
{\mathcal{I}}(\,\cdot\,)
the collection of independent spaces of a
q
-matroid.
With the aid of
5.6
it is easy to see that

{\mathcal{I}}({\mathcal{M}}_{1})\oplus{\mathcal{I}}({\mathcal{M}}_{2})\subset{\mathcal{I}}({\mathcal{M}}),\ \;{\mathcal{F}}({\mathcal{M}}_{1})\oplus{\mathcal{F}}({\mathcal{M}}_{2})\subset{\mathcal{F}}({\mathcal{M}}),\ \;{\mathcal{O}}({\mathcal{M}}_{1})\oplus{\mathcal{O}}({\mathcal{M}}_{2})\subset{\mathcal{O}}({\mathcal{M}})
,
and
{\mathcal{C}}({\mathcal{M}}_{i})\subset{\mathcal{C}}({\mathcal{M}})
for
i=1,2
. In general equality does not hold in any of these cases.

We illustrate the discrepancies in the following example.

Example 6.5
.

Let
{\mathbb{F}}={\mathbb{F}}_{2}
and consider
{\mathbb{F}}_{2^{3}}
with primitive element
\omega
satisfying
\omega^{3}+\omega+1=0
.
Let

G_{1}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0&\omega^{3}\\
0&1&\omega\end{pmatrix},\ G_{2}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0&\omega^{3}&\omega\\
0&1&\omega^{4}&\omega^{2}\end{pmatrix},\ G=\begin{pmatrix}1&0&\omega^{3}&0&0&0&0\\
0&1&\omega&0&0&0&0\\
0&0&0&1&0&\omega^{3}&\omega\\
0&0&0&0&1&\omega^{4}&\omega^{2}\end{pmatrix}.

Note that
G
is the block diagonal matrix with diagonal blocks
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
.
Let
{\mathcal{M}}_{i}={\mathcal{M}}_{G_{i}}
and
{\mathcal{N}}={\mathcal{M}}_{G}
, i.e., they are the
q
-matroids represented by
G_{1},\,G_{2}
, and
G
, respectively (see
2.8
).
Furthermore, let
{\mathcal{M}}={\mathcal{M}}_{1}\oplus{\mathcal{M}}_{2}
.
Thus both
{\mathcal{M}}
and
{\mathcal{N}}
have ground space
{\mathbb{F}}^{7}
.
In the following table we list the number of flats, cyclic spaces, etc. of all these
q
-matroids.

\begin{array}[]{|c||c|c|c|c|c|c|c|}\hline\cr&\text{flats}&\text{cyclic spaces}&\text{cyclic flats}&\text{ind.\ spaces}&\text{dep.\ spaces}&\text{circuits}&\text{bases}\\
\hline\cr\hline\cr\rule[-4.26773pt]{0.0pt}{17.92537pt}{\mathcal{M}}_{1}&7&2&2&14&2&1&6\\
\hline\cr\rule[-4.26773pt]{0.0pt}{17.92537pt}{\mathcal{M}}_{2}&11&11&5&48&19&9&32\\
\hline\cr\rule[-4.26773pt]{0.0pt}{17.92537pt}{\mathcal{N}}&2201&124&40&24108&5104&73&9792\\
\hline\cr\rule[-4.26773pt]{0.0pt}{17.92537pt}{\mathcal{M}}&7541&412&10&24861&4351&355&10416\\
\hline\cr\end{array}

Note that the 10 cyclic flats of
{\mathcal{M}}
are consistent with
6.2
.
It is remarkable that
{\mathcal{M}}
has significantly more flats and cyclic spaces than
{\mathcal{N}}
, yet fewer cyclic flats.
Furthermore, one can verify that the cyclic flats of
{\mathcal{M}}
are also cyclic flats of
{\mathcal{N}}
.
We will prove this in generality in
6.6
below.
Finally, all independent spaces of
{\mathcal{N}}
are also independent spaces of
{\mathcal{M}}
.
This also follows from
6.6
.

Our last result in this section concerns representable
q
-matroids
{\mathcal{M}}_{1},\,{\mathcal{M}}_{2}
.
It is well known that the direct sum of such
q
-matroids is in general not representable; see
[
15
]
.
In particular, in contrast to the matroid case, the block diagonal matrix built from representing matrices
G_{1}
and
G_{2}

represents in general a
q
-matroid
{\mathcal{N}}
that is different from
{\mathcal{M}}_{1}\oplus{\mathcal{M}}_{2}
.
The following result shows that the cyclic flats of
{\mathcal{M}}_{1}\oplus{\mathcal{M}}_{2}
are also cyclic flats of
{\mathcal{N}}
.

Theorem 6.6
.

Let
{\mathbb{F}}_{q^{m}}
be a field extension of
{\mathbb{F}}={\mathbb{F}}_{q}
and
G_{i}\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q^{m}}^{a_{i}\times n_{i}},\,i=1,2,
be matrices of full row rank.
Set

G=\begin{pmatrix}G_{1}&0\\
0&G_{2}\end{pmatrix}\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q^{m}}^{(a_{1}+a_{2})\times(n_{1}+n_{2})}.

Denote by
{\mathcal{M}}_{i}=({\mathbb{F}}^{n_{i}},\rho_{i}),\,i=1,2,
and
{\mathcal{N}}=({\mathbb{F}}^{n_{1}+n_{2}},\hat{\rho})
the
q
-matroids represented by
G_{1},G_{2}
, and
G
, thus

\rho_{i}(\mbox{\rm rs}(Y))={\rm rk}\,(G_{i}Y\mbox{${}^{\sf T}$})
for
Y\in{\mathbb{F}}^{y\times n_{i}}
and
\hat{\rho}(\mbox{\rm rs}(Y))={\rm rk}\,(GY\mbox{${}^{\sf T}$})
for
Y\in{\mathbb{F}}^{y\times(n_{1}+n_{2})}
.

(a)

If
F\in{\mathcal{F}}({\mathcal{M}}_{1})\oplus{\mathcal{F}}({\mathcal{M}}_{2})
, then
F\in{\mathcal{F}}({\mathcal{N}})
.

(b)

If
O\in{\mathcal{O}}({\mathcal{M}}_{1})\oplus{\mathcal{O}}({\mathcal{M}}_{2})
, then
O\in{\mathcal{O}}({\mathcal{N}})
.

As a consequence,
{\mathcal{Z}}({\mathcal{M}}_{1}\oplus{\mathcal{M}}_{2})\subseteq{\mathcal{Z}}({\mathcal{N}})
and thus
{\mathcal{I}}({\mathcal{N}})\subseteq{\mathcal{I}}({\mathcal{M}}_{1}\oplus{\mathcal{M}}_{2})
.

Note that the very last statement about the independent spaces also follows from the coproduct property of
{\mathcal{M}}_{1}\oplus{\mathcal{M}}_{2}
, discussed in
5.3
.

Proof.

The first part of the consequence follows from
6.2
and the second part from
4.5
.
For the proof of (a) and (b) we will make use of the fact

\hat{\rho}(V_{1}\oplus V_{2})=\rho_{1}(V_{1})+\rho_{2}(V_{2})\ \text{ for all }V_{i}\in{\mathcal{L}}({\mathbb{F}}^{n_{i}}).

(6.3)

This follows directly from the block diagonal form of
G
and the fact that every space
V_{1}\oplus V_{2}
is the row space of a block diagonal matrix as well.

(a) Let
F\in{\mathcal{F}}({\mathcal{M}}_{1})\oplus{\mathcal{F}}({\mathcal{M}}_{2})
and
x\in{\mathbb{F}}^{n_{1}+n_{2}}\setminus F
.
We want to show that
\hat{\rho}(F+\mbox{$\langle{x}\rangle$})>\hat{\rho}(F)
.
By assumption,

F=\mbox{\rm rs}\begin{pmatrix}Y_{1}&0\\
0&Y_{2}\end{pmatrix}\ \text{ for some $Y_{i}\in{\mathbb{F}}^{y_{i}\times n_{i}}$ such that $\mbox{\rm rs}(Y_{i})\in{\mathcal{F}}({\mathcal{M}}_{i})$.}

Write
x=(x_{1}\mid x_{2})
with
x_{i}\in{\mathbb{F}}^{n_{i}}
. Without loss of generality let
x_{1}\not\in\mbox{\rm rs}(Y_{1})
.
Since
\mbox{\rm rs}(Y_{1})
is a flat in
{\mathcal{M}}_{1}
, this implies

{\rm rk}\,(G_{1}[Y_{1}^{\sf T},\,x_{1}^{\sf T}])=\rho_{1}(\mbox{\rm rs}(Y_{1})+\mbox{$\langle{x_{1}}\rangle$})>\rho_{1}(\mbox{\rm rs}(Y_{1}))={\rm rk}\,(G_{1}Y_{1}^{\sf T}).

Hence
G_{1}x_{1}^{\sf T}
is not in the column space of
G_{1}Y_{1}^{\sf T}
and we obtain

\displaystyle\hat{\rho}(F+\mbox{$\langle{x}\rangle$})={\rm rk}\,\hskip-3.00003pt\left[\begin{pmatrix}G_{1}\!\!&0\\
0\!\!&G_{2}\end{pmatrix}\!\!\!\begin{pmatrix}Y_{1}\!\!&0\\
0\!\!&Y_{2}\\
x_{1}\!\!&x_{2}\end{pmatrix}^{\hskip-3.27773pt\sf T}\,\right]\!\!={\rm rk}\,\hskip-3.00003pt\begin{pmatrix}G_{1}Y_{1}^{\sf T}\!\!\!\!&0\!\!\!&G_{1}x_{1}^{\sf T}\\
0\!\!\!\!&G_{2}Y_{2}^{\sf T}\!\!\!&G_{2}x_{2}^{\sf T}\end{pmatrix}>{\rm rk}\,\hskip-3.00003pt\begin{pmatrix}G_{1}Y_{1}^{\sf T}\!\!\!\!&0\\
0\!\!\!\!&G_{2}Y_{2}^{\sf T}\end{pmatrix}=\hat{\rho}(F).

Since
x
was arbitrary, this proves that
F
is a flat of
{\mathcal{N}}
.

(b) Let
O\in{\mathcal{O}}({\mathcal{M}}_{1})\oplus{\mathcal{O}}({\mathcal{M}}_{2})
and let
D\in\mbox{$\text{Hyp}$}(O)
. We want to show that
\hat{\rho}(D)=\hat{\rho}(O)
.
By assumption
O=O_{1}\oplus O_{2}
with
O_{i}\in{\mathcal{O}}({\mathcal{M}}_{i})
.
Recall the projections
\pi_{i}
from
{\mathbb{F}}^{n_{1}+n_{2}}
to
{\mathbb{F}}^{n_{i}}
and set
\hat{D}=\pi_{1}(D)\oplus\pi_{2}(D)
.
Then
D\leq\hat{D}\leq O
and since
\dim D=\dim O-1
, we have
\hat{D}=D
or
\hat{D}=O
.

(b1) If
\hat{D}=D
we may assume
\pi_{1}(D)=O_{1}
and
\pi_{2}(D)\in\mbox{$\text{Hyp}$}(O_{2})
.
Using cyclicity of
O_{2}
and (
6.3
) we arrive at

\hat{\rho}(D)=\hat{\rho}(\hat{D})=\rho_{1}(\pi_{1}(D))+\rho_{2}(\pi_{2}(D))=\rho_{1}(O_{1})+\rho_{2}(O_{2})=\hat{\rho}(O),

which is what we wanted.

(b2) Let
\hat{D}=O
. Set
\dim O=k+1
and thus
\dim D=k
. Write
D=\mbox{\rm rs}(M_{1}\mid M_{2})
, where
M_{i}\in{\mathbb{F}}^{k\times n_{i}}
. Then

\hat{D}=O=\mbox{\rm rs}\begin{pmatrix}M_{1}&0\\
0&M_{2}\end{pmatrix}.

Hence
O_{i}=\mbox{\rm rs}(M_{i})
. Let
{\rm rk}\,M_{1}=k_{1}
. Then
{\rm rk}\,M_{2}=k-k_{1}+1
.
Using elementary row operations we may assume that
(M_{1}\mid M_{2})
is of the form

\begin{pmatrix}m_{1}&m_{2}\\
M_{21}&0\\
0&M_{22}\end{pmatrix}\ \text{ for some }m_{i}\in{\mathbb{F}}^{n_{i}},\ M_{21}\in{\mathbb{F}}^{(k_{1}-1)\times n_{1}},\ M_{22}\in{\mathbb{F}}^{(k-k_{1})\times n_{2}}.

Since
{\rm rk}\,M_{2i}={\rm rk}\,M_{i}-1
it follows that
\mbox{\rm rs}(M_{2i})\in\mbox{$\text{Hyp}$}(\mbox{\rm rs}(M_{i}))
.
Using the cyclicity of
\mbox{\rm rs}(M_{i})
we conclude that
{\rm rk}\,(G_{i}M_{2i}^{\sf T})={\rm rk}\,(G_{i}M_{i}^{\sf T})={\rm rk}\,(G_{i}[m_{i}^{\sf T},M_{2i}^{\sf T}])
for
i=1,2
.
This means that
G_{i}m_{i}^{\sf T}
is in the column space of
G_{i}M_{2i}^{\sf T}
, and thus

\hat{\rho}(D)={\rm rk}\,\hskip-3.00003pt\begin{pmatrix}G_{1}m_{1}^{\sf T}&G_{1}M_{21}^{\sf T}&0\\
G_{2}m_{2}^{\sf T}&0&G_{2}M_{22}^{\sf T}\end{pmatrix}={\rm rk}\,\hskip-3.00003pt\begin{pmatrix}G_{1}M_{21}^{\sf T}&0\\
0&G_{2}M_{22}^{\sf T}\end{pmatrix}={\rm rk}\,\hskip-3.00003pt\begin{pmatrix}G_{1}M_{1}^{\sf T}&0\\
0&G_{2}M_{2}^{\sf T}\end{pmatrix}=\hat{\rho}(O).

All of this shows
\hat{\rho}(D)=\hat{\rho}(O)
for every
D\in\mbox{$\text{Hyp}$}(O)
and thus
O\in{\mathcal{O}}({\mathcal{N}})
.
This concludes the proof.
∎

7
Decomposition of
q
-Matroids into Irreducible Components

We introduce the notion of irreducibility for
q
-matroids and show that every
q
-matroid can be decomposed as a
direct sum of irreducible
q
-matroids, whose summands are unique up to equivalence.
Our main tool are cyclic flats, in particular
6.2
.
This makes our approach substantially different from classical matroid theory, where decompositions are usually based on connected components.
As to our knowledge there is no notion of connectedness for
q
-matroids that may be used for decompositions into direct sums; see also
[
7
, Sec. 8]
.

Throughout, let
{\mathcal{M}}=(E,\rho)
be a
q
-matroid.
In order to simplify the discussion of irreducibility and decompositions we start with the following simple fact concerning equivalence in the
sense of
2.4
. It can easily be checked with the definition in
5.2
.

Remark 7.1
.

Suppose
{\mathcal{M}}\approx\hat{{\mathcal{M}}}_{1}\oplus\hat{{\mathcal{M}}}_{2}
. Then there exists a decomposition
E=E_{1}\oplus E_{2}
and
q
-matroids
{\mathcal{M}}_{i}
such that

{\mathcal{M}}={\mathcal{M}}_{1}\oplus{\mathcal{M}}_{2},\quad{\mathcal{M}}_{i}={\mathcal{M}}|_{E_{i}},\quad{\mathcal{M}}_{i}\approx\hat{{\mathcal{M}}}_{i}.

As a consequence, we do not need to take equivalence into account when discussing decomposability into direct sums.

Definition 7.2
.

The
q
-matroid
{\mathcal{M}}
is called
reducible
if there exists
q
-matroids
{\mathcal{M}}_{1},\,{\mathcal{M}}_{2}
with nonzero ground spaces such that

{\mathcal{M}}={\mathcal{M}}_{1}\oplus{\mathcal{M}}_{2}
. Otherwise
{\mathcal{M}}
is called
irreducible
.

Clearly, a
q
-matroid on a 1-dimensional ground space is irreducible.
Furthermore, thanks to
5.10

{\mathcal{M}}
is irreducible if and only if
{\mathcal{M}}^{*}
is.
We collect some facts about the uniform
q
-matroids.

Example 7.3
.

(a)

The trivial and the free
q
-matroids
{\mathcal{U}}_{0,n}
and
{\mathcal{U}}_{n,n}
are irreducible if and only if
n=1
.
Indeed,
5.2
implies
{\mathcal{U}}_{0,n_{1}}\oplus{\mathcal{U}}_{0,n_{2}}={\mathcal{U}}_{0,n}
and likewise

{\mathcal{U}}_{n_{1},n_{1}}\oplus{\mathcal{U}}_{n_{2},n_{2}}={\mathcal{U}}_{n,n}
, where
n=n_{1}+n_{2}
.

(b)

For
0<k<n:=\dim E
the uniform
q
-matroid
{\mathcal{M}}:={\mathcal{U}}_{k}(E)
is irreducible.
To see this, note first that
{\mathcal{Z}}({\mathcal{M}})=\{0,E\}
thanks to
4.3
.
Suppose
{\mathcal{M}}={\mathcal{M}}_{1}\oplus{\mathcal{M}}_{2}
for some
q
-matroids
{\mathcal{M}}_{i}=(E_{i},\rho_{i})
.
Then the identity
{\mathcal{Z}}({\mathcal{M}})={\mathcal{Z}}({\mathcal{M}}_{1})\oplus{\mathcal{Z}}({\mathcal{M}}_{2})
implies that, without loss of generality,
{\mathcal{Z}}({\mathcal{M}}_{1})=\{Z_{1}\}
and

{\mathcal{Z}}({\mathcal{M}}_{2})=\{Z_{2},Z_{2}^{\prime}\}
.
Thus
0=Z_{1}\oplus Z_{2}
and
E=Z_{1}\oplus Z_{2}^{\prime}
, and therefore
Z_{1}=0,\,Z_{2}=0,\,Z_{2}^{\prime}=E
.
Since
E=E_{1}\oplus E_{2}
and
Z_{2}^{\prime}\leq E_{2}
, this leads to
E_{2}=E
and thus
E_{1}=0
.
Hence
{\mathcal{M}}_{1}
has a zero-dimensional ground space and
{\mathcal{M}}
is irreducible.

(c)

Conversely, if
{\mathcal{M}}=(E,\rho)
is such that
{\mathcal{Z}}({\mathcal{M}})=\{0,E\}
and
\rho(E)=k\in\{1,\ldots,n-1\}
, then
{\mathcal{M}}={\mathcal{U}}_{k}(E)
.
Indeed, suppose there exists a space
V
such that
\rho(V)<\min\{k,\dim V\}
.
Let
l=\dim V
be minimal subject to this condition. Then
\rho(V)=l-1
and
V
is cyclic.
Thus
{\rm cl}(V)=E
and
\rho(V)=\rho(E)=k
, contradicting the choice of
V
.
This shows
\rho(V)=\min\{k,\dim V\}
for all
V\in{\mathcal{L}}(E)
.

The goal of this section is (a) to provide a criterion for irreducibility and (b) to show that every
q
-matroid decomposes into a direct
sum of irreducible
q
-matroids, whose summands are unique up to ordering and equivalence.
The next lemma will be needed throughout.

Lemma 7.4
.

Let
\hat{Z}\in{\mathcal{Z}}({\mathcal{M}})
and consider the restriction
{\mathcal{M}}|_{\hat{Z}}
. Then

{\mathcal{Z}}({\mathcal{M}}|_{\hat{Z}})=\{Z\in{\mathcal{Z}}({\mathcal{M}})\mid Z\leq\hat{Z}\}.

Proof.

Set
{\mathcal{Z}}=\{Z\in{\mathcal{Z}}({\mathcal{M}})\mid Z\leq\hat{Z}\}
. Denote the rank function of
{\mathcal{M}}|_{\hat{Z}}
by
\hat{\rho}
.

“
\supseteq
” is obvious since
\hat{\rho}(V)=\rho(V)
for all
V\leq\hat{Z}
.

“
\subseteq
” Let
Z\in{\mathcal{Z}}({\mathcal{M}}|_{\hat{Z}})
.
Clearly
Z
is cyclic in
{\mathcal{M}}
because any
D\in\mbox{$\text{Hyp}$}(Z)
is a subspace of
\hat{Z}
and thus satisfies
\rho(D)=\hat{\rho}(D)=\hat{\rho}(Z)=\rho(Z)
.
To show that
Z
is a flat in
{\mathcal{M}}
, let
x\in E\setminus Z
.
If
x\in\hat{Z}\setminus Z
, then
\rho(Z+\mbox{$\langle{x}\rangle$})=\hat{\rho}(Z+\mbox{$\langle{x}\rangle$})>\hat{\rho}(Z)=\rho(Z)
.
If
x\in E\setminus\hat{Z}
, then
x\not\in{\rm cl}(Z)
because
{\rm cl}(Z)\leq{\rm cl}(\hat{Z})=\hat{Z}
(where
{\rm cl}(\,\cdot\,)
denotes the closure in
{\mathcal{M}}
).
Thus
\rho(Z+\mbox{$\langle{x}\rangle$})>\rho(Z)
.
All of this shows that
Z
is a flat in
{\mathcal{M}}
.
∎

Our first result shows that whenever
E
is not a cyclic flat of
{\mathcal{M}}
, then for any direct complement
E_{2}
of
{\rm cyc}(E)
in
E

we may split off the free
q
-matroid on
E_{2}
from
{\mathcal{M}}
.

Proposition 7.5
.

Let
E_{1}={\rm cyc}(E)
and choose
E_{2}\leq E
such that
E_{1}\oplus E_{2}=E
.
Consider the restrictions
{\mathcal{M}}_{i}={\mathcal{M}}|_{E_{i}}
for
i=1,2
. Then

(a)

{\mathcal{M}}_{2}
is the free
q
-matroid on
E_{2}
.

(b)

{\mathcal{Z}}({\mathcal{M}})={\mathcal{Z}}({\mathcal{M}}_{1})
.

(c)

{\mathcal{M}}={\mathcal{M}}_{1}\oplus{\mathcal{M}}_{2}
.

Proof.

(a)
3.10
(b) implies that
E_{2}
is independent in
{\mathcal{M}}
and thus in
{\mathcal{M}}_{2}
.
Since every subspace of an independent space is independent, the result follows.

(b) follows from
Lemma

7.4
together with the fact that
{\mathcal{Z}}({\mathcal{M}})
is a lattice with greatest element
{\rm cyc}(E)
.

(c) Part (a) and
4.3
tell us that
{\mathcal{Z}}({\mathcal{M}}_{2})=\{0\}
, and thus (b) and
6.2
imply

{\mathcal{Z}}({\mathcal{M}}_{1}\oplus{\mathcal{M}}_{2})={\mathcal{Z}}({\mathcal{M}}_{1})={\mathcal{Z}}({\mathcal{M}}).

Denote the rank function of
{\mathcal{M}}_{1}\oplus{\mathcal{M}}_{2}
by
\hat{\rho}
.
Then
5.6
(a) implies
\hat{\rho}(Z_{1})=\rho_{1}(Z_{1})=\rho(Z_{1})
for all
Z_{1}\in{\mathcal{Z}}({\mathcal{M}}_{1})
.
Hence the cyclic flats in
{\mathcal{Z}}({\mathcal{M}})
have the same rank value in the
q
-matroids
{\mathcal{M}}_{1}\oplus{\mathcal{M}}_{2}
and
{\mathcal{M}}
.
The result follows from
4.6
.
∎

Dually, we may split off the trivial
q
-matroid on
{\rm cl}(0)
from
{\mathcal{M}}
.

Proposition 7.6
.

Let
E_{1}={\rm cl}(0)
and choose
E_{2}\leq E
such that
E=E_{1}\oplus E_{2}
.
Consider the restrictions
{\mathcal{M}}_{i}={\mathcal{M}}|_{E_{i}}=(E_{i},\rho_{i})
for
i=1,2
and let
\pi_{i}:E\longrightarrow E_{i}
be the projections.

(a)

{\mathcal{M}}_{1}
is the trivial
q
-matroid on
E_{1}
.

(b)

\rho(V)=\rho_{2}(\pi_{2}(V))
for all
V\in{\mathcal{L}}(E)
.

(c)

{\mathcal{M}}={\mathcal{M}}_{1}\oplus{\mathcal{M}}_{2}
.

We call
{\rm cl}(0)
the
loop space
of
{\mathcal{M}}
. It consists of all vectors
x\in E
such that
\rho(\mbox{$\langle{x}\rangle$})=0
.

Proof.

(a) is clear and so is the very last part about the vectors in the loop space.

(b) Let
V\in{\mathcal{L}}(E)
.
Since
V\leq\pi_{1}(V)\oplus\pi_{2}(V)
we have
\rho(V)\leq\rho(\pi_{1}(V))+\rho(\pi_{2}(V))=\rho(\pi_{2}(V))=\rho_{2}(\pi_{2}(V))
.
For the converse inequality, let
y\in\pi_{2}(V)
. Then there exists
x\in E_{1}
such that
x+y\in V
.
As a consequence,
V+\mbox{$\langle{y}\rangle$}=V+\mbox{$\langle{x}\rangle$}
and
\rho(V)\leq\rho(V+\mbox{$\langle{x}\rangle$})\leq\rho(V)+\rho(\mbox{$\langle{x}\rangle$})=\rho(V)
.
Hence we have equality across and this shows that
\rho(V+\mbox{$\langle{y}\rangle$})=\rho(V)
for all
y\in\pi_{2}(V)
.
Now
2.2
(a) leads to
\rho(V+\pi_{2}(V))=\rho(V)
and thus
\rho(\pi_{2}(V))\leq\rho(V)
.

(c) Let
\hat{\rho}
be the rank function of
{\mathcal{M}}_{1}\oplus{\mathcal{M}}_{2}
.
Then

\displaystyle\hat{\rho}(V)

\displaystyle=\dim V+\min_{X\leq V}\big(\rho_{2}(\pi_{2}(X))-\dim X\big)=\dim V+\min_{X\leq V}\big(\rho(X)-\dim X\big)

\displaystyle=\dim V+\rho(V)-\dim V=\rho(V),

where the third step follows from the inequality
\dim A-\rho(A)\leq\dim B-\rho(B)
for any
A\leq B
(which is a simple consequence of submodularity).
∎

The following terminology will be convenient.

Definition 7.7
.

{\mathcal{M}}
is called
full
if
{\rm cl}(0)=0
and
{\rm cyc}(E)=E
.

Remark 7.8
.

The notion of a full matroid does not exist in classical matroid theory (as to our knowledge) because a matroid satisfying
{\rm cl}(0)=0
and
{\rm cyc}(E)=E

is simply called loopless and coloopless.
In the
q
-analogue, however, the dual
q
-matroid depends on the choice of a NSBF (see
2.3
) and therefore the notion of a coloop is not well-defined.
Indeed, a 1-dimensional subspace
\langle{x}\rangle
would be called a coloop of
{\mathcal{M}}
if it is a loop of
{\mathcal{M}}^{*}
, that is, if

\mbox{$\langle{x}\rangle$}\leq{\rm cl}^{*}(0)
.
By
3.7

{\rm cl}^{*}(0)={\rm cyc}(E)^{\perp}
and thus
{\rm cl}^{*}(0)\neq 0\Longleftrightarrow{\rm cyc}(E)\neq E
.
But if
{\rm cyc}(E)\neq E
one can find for any nonzero vector
x\in E
a NSBF such that
x\in{\rm cyc}(E)^{\perp}
, hence
\langle{x}\rangle
is a loop in the
dual
q
-matroid with respect to this NSBF.
For this reason we will not use the notion of coloops.

Now we can present a first step toward a decomposition of
{\mathcal{M}}
.

Theorem 7.9
.

Given
{\mathcal{M}}=(E,\rho)
. Set
l=\dim{\rm cl}(0)
and
f=\dim E-\dim{\rm cyc}(E)
.

(a)

{\mathcal{M}}
is the direct sum of a trivial, a free, and a full
q
-matroid.
Precisely, there exists a full
q
-matroid
{\mathcal{M}}^{\prime}
such that

{\mathcal{M}}\approx{\mathcal{U}}_{0,l}\oplus{\mathcal{U}}_{f,f}\oplus{\mathcal{M}}^{\prime}.

(b)

If
{\mathcal{M}}\approx{\mathcal{U}}_{0,a}\oplus{\mathcal{U}}_{b,b}\oplus{\mathcal{N}}
, where
{\mathcal{N}}
is full, then
a=l,\,b=f
and
{\mathcal{N}}\approx{\mathcal{M}}^{\prime}
.

We call
{\mathcal{U}}_{0,l},\,{\mathcal{U}}_{f,f}
, and
{\mathcal{M}}^{\prime}
the
trivial, free and full component of

{\mathcal{M}}
, respectively.

Proof.

Let
E={\rm cyc}(E)\oplus\Delta
and
{\rm cyc}(E)={\rm cl}(0)\oplus\Gamma
. Then
\dim\Delta=f
.

(a) By
7.5
we have
{\mathcal{M}}={\mathcal{U}}_{f}(\Delta)\oplus{\mathcal{M}}|_{{\rm cyc}(E)}
, and

7.6
implies
{\mathcal{M}}|_{{\rm cyc}(E)}={\mathcal{U}}_{0}({\rm cl}(0))\oplus{\mathcal{M}}|_{\Gamma}
.
This proves the stated decomposition of
{\mathcal{M}}
, and it remains to show that
{\mathcal{M}}^{\prime}:={\mathcal{M}}|_{\Gamma}
is full.
Again Propositions
7.5
and
7.6

give us
{\mathcal{Z}}({\mathcal{M}})={\mathcal{Z}}({\mathcal{M}}|_{{\rm cyc}(E)})=\{{\rm cl}(0)\oplus Z\mid Z\in{\mathcal{Z}}({\mathcal{M}}^{\prime})\}
.
Since
{\rm cl}(0)
and
{\rm cyc}(E)={\rm cl}(0)\oplus\Gamma
are the least and greatest element of the lattice
{\mathcal{Z}}({\mathcal{M}})
, we conclude that
0

and
\Gamma
are the least and greatest element of the lattice
{\mathcal{Z}}({\mathcal{M}}^{\prime})
.
Thus
{\mathcal{M}}^{\prime}
is full.

(b) Using
7.1
we have

{\mathcal{M}}={\mathcal{U}}_{0}(A)\oplus{\mathcal{U}}_{b}(B)\oplus{\mathcal{N}}

for some
A,B,N\leq E
such that
A\oplus B\oplus N=E,\,a=\dim A,\,b=\dim B
, and where
N
is the ground space of
{\mathcal{N}}
.
Moreover, by (a)

{\mathcal{M}}={\mathcal{U}}_{0}({\rm cl}(0))\oplus{\mathcal{U}}_{f}(\Delta)\oplus{\mathcal{M}}^{\prime}.

From
6.2
and
4.3
we obtain

{\mathcal{Z}}({\mathcal{M}})=\{A\}\oplus{\mathcal{Z}}({\mathcal{N}})
.
Since
{\mathcal{N}}
is full, the least element of the lattice
{\mathcal{Z}}({\mathcal{N}})
is
0
and thus
A
is the least element of
{\mathcal{Z}}({\mathcal{M}})
.
But the latter is
{\rm cl}(0)
and thus we arrive at
A={\rm cl}(0)
and
a=l
.
In the same way, the greatest element of
{\mathcal{Z}}({\mathcal{N}})
is
N
and thus
A\oplus N
is the greatest element of
{\mathcal{Z}}({\mathcal{M}})
.
Hence
A\oplus N={\rm cyc}(E)
and
a+\dim N=\dim{\rm cyc}(E)
, which implies
f=\dim E-\dim{\rm cyc}(E)=b
, as desired.
In order to show that
{\mathcal{N}}\approx{\mathcal{M}}^{\prime}
note that
{\rm cyc}(E)=A\oplus\Gamma=A\oplus N
and

{\mathcal{M}}|_{{\rm cyc}(E)}={\mathcal{U}}_{0}(A)\oplus{\mathcal{M}}^{\prime}={\mathcal{U}}_{0}(A)\oplus{\mathcal{N}}
(see
5.6
(a)).
Now
5.6
(c) yields
{\mathcal{N}}\approx({\mathcal{M}}|_{{\rm cyc}(E)})/A\approx{\mathcal{M}}^{\prime}
.
This concludes the proof.
∎

We have the following special case.

Corollary 7.10
.

|{\mathcal{Z}}({\mathcal{M}})|=1
if and only if
{\mathcal{M}}
is the direct sum of a trivial and a free
q
-matroid.
Precisely, let
l=\dim{\rm cl}(0)
and
f=\dim E-l
.
Then
{\mathcal{Z}}({\mathcal{M}})=\{{\rm cl}(0)\}\Longleftrightarrow{\mathcal{M}}\approx{\mathcal{U}}_{0,l}\oplus{\mathcal{U}}_{f,f}
.

Proof.

The backward direction follows immediately from
6.2
together with
|{\mathcal{Z}}({\mathcal{U}}_{0,l})|=|{\mathcal{Z}}({\mathcal{U}}_{f,f})|=1
for any
l,f
.
The forward direction is a consequence of
7.9
because the assumption implies
{\rm cl}(0)={\rm cyc}(E)
.
∎

In order to derive a criterion for irreducibility we need the following two lemmas.
For any subspace
V\in{\mathcal{L}}(E)
we use
{\mathcal{B}}(V)
for the
collection of bases of
V
, i.e.,
{\mathcal{B}}(V)=\{I\leq V\mid\dim I=\rho(I)=\rho(V)\}
.

Lemma 7.11
.

Suppose there exist flats
F_{1},\,F_{2}
of
{\mathcal{M}}
such that
F_{1}\cap F_{2}=0
and
\rho(F_{1}\oplus F_{2})=\rho(F_{1})+\rho(F_{2})
.

(a)

Let
B_{i}\in{\mathcal{B}}(F_{i})
. Then
B_{1}\oplus B_{2}\in{\mathcal{B}}(F_{1}\oplus F_{2})
.

(b)

Let
V_{i}\in{\mathcal{L}}(F_{i})
. Then
\rho(V_{1}\oplus V_{2})=\rho(V_{1})+\rho(V_{2})
.

Proof.

(a)
B_{i}\leq F_{i}={\rm cl}(F_{i})
together with
\rho(B_{i})=\rho(F_{i})
implies
{\rm cl}(B_{i})=F_{i}
.
Since
{\rm cl}(B_{i})\leq{\rm cl}(B_{1}\oplus B_{2})
, this leads to

B_{1}\oplus B_{2}\leq F_{1}\oplus F_{2}={\rm cl}(B_{1})\oplus{\rm cl}(B_{2})\leq{\rm cl}(B_{1}\oplus B_{2})
,
and thus
\rho(B_{1}\oplus B_{2})=\rho(F_{1}\oplus F_{2})
thanks to (
2.4
).
Now we have
\rho(B_{1}\oplus B_{2})=\rho(F_{1})+\rho(F_{2})=\dim B_{1}+\dim B_{2}=\dim(B_{1}\oplus B_{2})
, which shows that

B_{1}\oplus B_{2}
is a basis of
F_{1}\oplus F_{2}
.

(b) Let
B_{i}\in{\mathcal{B}}(V_{i})
.
Then
B_{i}
is an independent space in
F_{i}
and thus contained in a basis of
F_{i}
, say
B^{\prime}_{i}
(see
[
17
, Thm. 37]
).
Thanks to part (a)
B^{\prime}_{1}\oplus B^{\prime}_{2}
is independent and hence so is
B_{1}\oplus B_{2}
.
Putting everything together, we obtain

\rho(V_{1}\oplus V_{2})\leq\rho(V_{1})+\rho(V_{2})=\dim B_{1}+\dim B_{2}=\dim(B_{1}\oplus B_{2})=\rho(B_{1}\oplus B_{2})\leq\rho(V_{1}\oplus V_{2}).

which proves the stated identity.
∎

Lemma 7.12
.

Let
{\mathcal{M}}
be full. Suppose
{\mathcal{M}}={\mathcal{M}}_{1}\oplus{\mathcal{M}}_{2}
for some
q
-matroids
{\mathcal{M}}_{i}=(E_{i},\rho_{i})
.
Then
E_{i}\in{\mathcal{Z}}({\mathcal{M}})
and
{\mathcal{M}}_{1},\,{\mathcal{M}}_{2}
are full.

Proof.

Since
{\mathcal{M}}
is full,
E
is in
{\mathcal{Z}}({\mathcal{M}})={\mathcal{Z}}({\mathcal{M}}_{1})\oplus{\mathcal{Z}}({\mathcal{M}}_{2})
.
Thus there exist
Z_{i}\in{\mathcal{Z}}({\mathcal{M}}_{i})
such that
E=Z_{1}\oplus Z_{2}
. But then
E=E_{1}\oplus E_{2}
together with
Z_{i}\leq E_{i}

implies
Z_{i}=E_{i}
,
and we conclude that
E_{i}\in{\mathcal{Z}}({\mathcal{M}}_{i})
. In the same way,
0\in{\mathcal{Z}}({\mathcal{M}})
implies
0\in{\mathcal{Z}}({\mathcal{M}}_{i})
for
i=1,2
,
and hence
{\mathcal{M}}_{1}
and
{\mathcal{M}}_{2}
are full.
Finally,
E_{1}=E_{1}\oplus 0
is in
{\mathcal{Z}}({\mathcal{M}}_{1})\oplus{\mathcal{Z}}({\mathcal{M}}_{2})={\mathcal{Z}}({\mathcal{M}})
and similarly for
E_{2}
.
∎

Now we are ready for our first main result of this section, a characterization of irreducibility.

Theorem 7.13
.

Let
{\mathcal{M}}=(E,\rho)
and
\dim E\geq 2
. The following are equivalent.

(i)

{\mathcal{M}}
is irreducible.

(ii)

{\mathcal{M}}
is full and there exist no nonzero spaces
Z_{1},Z_{2}\in{\mathcal{Z}}({\mathcal{M}})
such that

Z_{1}\oplus Z_{2}=E,\quad\rho(Z_{1})+\rho(Z_{2})=\rho(E),\quad\text{ and }\ {\mathcal{Z}}_{1}\oplus{\mathcal{Z}}_{2}={\mathcal{Z}}({\mathcal{M}}),

(7.1)

where
{\mathcal{Z}}_{i}=\{Z\in{\mathcal{Z}}({\mathcal{M}})\mid Z\leq Z_{i}\}
.

Note that
{\mathcal{Z}}_{i}={\mathcal{Z}}({\mathcal{M}}|_{Z_{i}})
thanks to
Lemma

7.4
.

Proof.

“(i)
\Rightarrow
(ii)” Let
{\mathcal{M}}
be irreducible.
Then
7.3
(a) and
7.9
together with
\dim E\geq 2
imply that
{\mathcal{M}}
is full.
Suppose there do exist nonzero spaces
Z_{1},Z_{2}\in{\mathcal{Z}}({\mathcal{M}})
satisfying (
7.1
). We show that

{\mathcal{M}}={\mathcal{M}}|_{Z_{1}}\oplus{\mathcal{M}}|_{Z_{2}}.

Set
{\mathcal{Z}}={\mathcal{Z}}({\mathcal{M}}|_{Z_{1}})\oplus{\mathcal{Z}}({\mathcal{M}}|_{Z_{2}})
and denote the rank functions of
{\mathcal{M}}|_{Z_{1}}\oplus{\mathcal{M}}|_{Z_{2}}
and
{\mathcal{M}}|_{Z_{i}}
by
\rho^{\prime}
and
\rho_{i}
,
respectively.
With the aid of
5.8
and
Lemma

7.11
(b) we obtain for all
V\in{\mathcal{L}}(E)

\displaystyle\rho^{\prime}(V)

\displaystyle=\dim V+\min_{Y_{1}\oplus Y_{2}\in{\mathcal{Z}}}\Big(\rho_{1}(Y_{1})+\rho_{2}(Y_{2})-\dim((Y_{1}\oplus Y_{2})\cap V)\Big)

\displaystyle=\dim V+\min_{Y_{1}\oplus Y_{2}\in{\mathcal{Z}}}\Big(\rho(Y_{1})+\rho(Y_{2})-\dim((Y_{1}\oplus Y_{2})\cap V)\Big)

\displaystyle=\min_{Y_{1}\oplus Y_{2}\in{\mathcal{Z}}}\Big(\rho(Y_{1}\oplus Y_{2})+\dim((V+(Y_{1}\oplus Y_{2}))/(Y_{1}\oplus Y_{2}))\Big)

\displaystyle=\rho(V),
