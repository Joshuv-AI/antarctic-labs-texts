Bayesian Structured Mediation Analysis With Unobserved Confounders
Yuliang Xu, Shu Yang Jian Kang

Abstract
We explore methods to reduce the impact of unobserved confounders on the causal mediation analysis of high-dimensional mediators with spatially smooth structures, such as brain imaging data. The key approach is to incorporate the spatial latent subject-specific spatial confounding effects, which influence the structured mediators, as unobserved confounders in the outcome model, thereby potentially debiasing the mediation effects. We develop BAyesian Structured Mediation analysis with Unobserved confounders (BASMU) framework, and establish its model identifiability conditions. Theoretical analysis is conducted on the asymptotic bias of the Natural Indirect Effect (NIE) and the Natural Direct Effect (NDE) when the unobserved confounders are omitted in mediation analysis. For BASMU, we propose a two-stage estimation algorithm to mitigate the impact of these unobserved confounders on estimating the mediation effect. Extensive simulations demonstrate that BASMU substantially reduces the bias in various scenarios. We apply BASMU to the analysis of fMRI data in the Adolescent Brain Cognitive Development (ABCD) study, focusing on four brain regions previously reported to exhibit meaningful mediation effects. Compared with the existing image mediation analysis method, BASMU identifies two to four times more voxels that have significant mediation effects, with the NIE increased by 41%, and the NDE decreased by 26%.



Keywords:
Bayesian nonparametric, Brain image, Mediation analysis, Spatial structure, Unobserved confounders.

1
Introduction

High-dimensional mediation analysis is increasingly important in causal inference with complex data, especially as brain imaging and connectome datasets grow
(
Lindquist, 2012
;
Chen et al., 2022
)
. We propose a causal mediation framework that accounts for unobserved confounding in high-dimensional structured mediators, motivated by brain imaging data. Using fMRI data from the Adolescent Brain Cognitive Development (ABCD) study, we investigate how parental education affects children’s cognitive ability by identifying the neural pathways mediating this causal link.

Despite recent advances in imaging mediation analysis, the influence of unobserved confounders has largely been ignored. The no-unobserved-confounding assumption means that there exists no unobserved confounding variable that can impact both the outcome and the mediator. Existence of such unobserved confounders violates the sequential ignorability assumption proposed in
Imai et al. (2010)
, where the predictor (treatment) is fully randomized but the mediator is not. Many recent advances in the high-dimensional mediation analyses rely on the no-unobserved-confounding assumption
(
Xu et al., 2026
;
Lindquist, 2012
;
Nath et al., 2023
;
Song et al., 2021a
;
Jiang and Colditz, 2023
;
Wang et al., 2023
)
.

The no-unmeasured-confounding assumption is ubiquitous in causal mediation analysis and general causal inference. Existing methods to address bias from unobserved confounders fall into two main categories: sensitivity analysis and external information (negative control or instrumental variable).
The sensitivity analysis approach bounds the Natural Indirect and Direct Effects (NIE, NDE) given assumptions about the scale of unobserved confounding
(
Cinelli and Hazlett, 2020
;
Zhang and Ding, 2022
;
Imai et al., 2010
;
Tchetgen and Shpitser, 2012
;
Ding and Vanderweele, 2016
)
. Classical approaches include parametric sensitivity analysis under linear structural equation models with scalar mediators
(
Imai et al., 2010
)
and nonparametric extensions
(
Tchetgen and Shpitser, 2012
)
; see
Tchetgen and Shpitser (2012)
for detailed comparisons. Others provide sharp bounds for binary treatment/outcome
(
Ding and Vanderweele, 2016
)
or use partial
R^{2}
to quantify omitted-variable bias
(
Cinelli and Hazlett, 2020
;
Zhang and Ding, 2022
)
.
However, sensitivity analysis cannot directly identify hidden confounders or produce debiased mediation effects. With high-dimensional mediators, it further requires specifying infinitely many possible combinations of confounder effects across spatial locations, making this approach especially restrictive. Approaches using instrumental variables (IVs)
(
Angrist et al., 1996
;
Rudolph et al., 2024
)
or negative controls
(
Lipsitch et al., 2010
;
Miao et al., 2024
)
rely on stringent validity conditions: IVs must affect the exposure (or mediator) without directly influencing the outcome and be independent of all hidden confounders; negative controls must share the same confounding structure while having no causal effect. Such variables are rarely available, particularly with complex neuroimaging mediators.
Other directions, such as using shared structure between confounders and exposure
(
Guan et al., 2023
)
, lie outside the mediation framework.

Beyond bias from unobserved confounders, brain imaging mediation faces unique challenges: fMRI data are high-dimensional and spatially structured, with active mediation effects confined to small regions. The goal is to identify active mediation areas in the brain after accounting for unobserved confounding. Existing high-dimensional methods assume no unobserved confounding, and standard remedies (sensitivity analysis, instrumental variables, negative controls) do not adapt, leaving no principled way to adjust for spatially varying confounding.

To close this gap, we introduce BASMU (Bayesian Structured Mediation analysis with Unobserved confounders), a Bayesian framework that leverages spatial information to partially recover unidentifiable confounding effects. BASMU targets structured unobserved confounders with spatially smooth effects on the mediator, and directly estimates the subject-specific spatial confounding effect. Building on the classical linear structural equation model (LSEM) of
Baron and Kenny (1986)
, we extend it to include unobserved confounders in both mediator and outcome models. Under mild conditions, these confounders can be estimated from the mediator model and used to debias the outcome model. We provide theoretical results on the asymptotic bias when such confounders are omitted and propose several algorithms to estimate subject-specific spatial confounding effects. BASMU allows flexible priors for the confounder coefficients and consistently outperforms competing methods in simulations, while increasing detection of significant mediation effects in ABCD brain imaging data.

Section 2 introduces the structured mediation framework with unobserved confounders and its identifiability and bias analysis. Section 3 outlines the two-stage estimator, Section 4 compares BIMA and BASMU in simulations, Section 5 applies the method to ABCD data, and Section 6 concludes.

2
Bayesian Structured Mediation with Unobserved Confounders Framework

Structured mediators are multivariate mediators with latent correlation, such as 2D/3D fMRI images, time series, or network data. For subject
i
, denote the functional mediator as
M_{i}(s),s\in\mathcal{S}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
, where
\mathcal{S}
may be a spatial grid, time points, or a compact 3D brain domain. The mean function
\mathbb{E}\left\{M_{i}(s)\right\}
is assumed to vary smoothly, for example through low-rank spatial bases or autoregressive time dependence. A scalar mediator with no variation in
\mathcal{S}
cannot separate unobserved confounding from spatial-independent noise. In structured mediation analysis, ignoring unobserved confounders yields biased estimates. Unlike approaches relying on sensitivity analysis or external instruments, we estimate unobserved confounders directly by exploiting spatial structure. Motivated by the ABCD study of children’s brain fMRI, we examine how parental education influences children’s general cognitive ability through the mediation of brain development at voxel locations
s_{j}\in\mathcal{S}
.

For subject
i=1,\dots,n
and grid location
j=1,\dots,p
, let
M_{i}(s_{j})
be the image intensity,
X_{i}
the scalar exposure,
\mathbf{C}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{q}
the observed confounders, and
Y_{i}
the scalar-valued outcome. The domain
\mathcal{S}
is partitioned into disjoint pixels
\left\{\Delta s_{j}\right\}_{j=1}^{p}
of of equal Lebesgue measure
p^{-1}
with centers
s_{j}
. We abbreviate grid-based functional values as
\eta_{i}=\left\{\eta_{i}(s_{j})\right\}_{j=1}^{p}
, and similarly for
\alpha,\beta,\xi_{k}
. Standard notation includes
\mathcal{GP}(0,\kappa)
for a Gaussian Process with mean 0 and covariance
\kappa(\cdot,\cdot)
, and
|A|
be the cardinality of a set
A
(see Table
S1
).

\epsilon_{M,i}(\Delta s_{j})\overset{\scriptsize\mbox{ind}}{\sim}N(0,\sigma_{M}^{2}\lambda(\Delta s_{j}))
.

In the mediator model (
1
), we use
\alpha
to denote the impact of the treatment
X_{i}
on the image mediator
M_{i}
,
\xi_{k}
the functional-coefficient for the vector-valued confounders
C_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{q}
,
\eta_{i}
the subject-specific spatial confounding effect, and
\epsilon_{M,i}
the spatially-independent noise term. Model (
1
) is the structured mediator where the mean part of (
1
) follows a certain spatial pattern induced by Gaussian Process Kernels (see details for the GP priors in Section
\mathsection
3
and equation (
5
)).

In the outcome model (
2
), we use
\beta(s)
to denote the mediator
M_{i}(\Delta s_{j})
’s effect on the outcome
Y_{i}
. Here,
M_{i}(\Delta s)
denotes the integrated effect of the random function
M_{i}(s)
over a small voxel
\Delta s
. We use
\gamma,\zeta_{k}
to denote the effect of the exposure
X_{i}
and the observed confounder
C_{i,k}
, respectively. BASMU explicitly models unobserved confounders by exploiting spatial structure via the subject-specific spatial confounding effect
\eta_{i}(s)
, and uses the effect of
\eta_{i}(s)
on
Y_{i}
to represent the impact of unobserved confounders, denoted as
\nu(s)
.

The key intuition behind BASMU is that when an unobserved confounder influences both the mediator and the outcome, its effect on the mediator is unlikely to be homogeneous. Because the mediator itself exhibits spatial variation, the unobserved confounder must also leave a spatially varying footprint on the mediator. This latent structural effect can be captured by the subject-specific spatial confounding effect
\eta_{i}(s)
. By incorporating
\eta_{i}(s)
into the outcome model, BASMU captures the potential influence of unobserved confounders on the outcome, thereby reducing bias that arises when such confounding is ignored.

Consider the following example: if there exists unobserved confounder
\mathbf{Z}_{i}
of unknown dimension
m
, in the mediator model (
1
), there would be a term
\sum_{r=1}^{m}\xi_{r,z}(s_{j})Z_{r,i}
which plays the same role as
\sum_{k=1}^{q}\xi_{k}(s_{j})C_{i,k}
. Since
\mathbf{Z}_{i}
is unobservable, if
\xi_{m,z}(s_{j})
is a spatially-varying effect, we can replace the term
\sum_{r=1}^{m}\xi_{m,z}(s_{j})Z_{r,i}
by the subject-specific spatial confounding effect
\eta_{i}(s_{j})
, as long as they have the same spatially-varying structure so that
\eta_{i}(s_{j})
is separable from the independent noise
\epsilon_{M,i}(s_{j})
.

With the presence of unobserved confounders
\eta_{i}
as shown in the BASMU model (
1
) and (
2
), we follow the mediation assumption proposed in
VanderWeele and Vansteelandt (2014)
and make the following causal identification assumptions: for any
i
, endogenous
x
and
\mathbf{m}
,

\displaystyle\text{(i) }Y_{i,(x,\mathbf{m})}\perp X_{i}\mid\left\{\mathbf{C}_{i},\eta_{i}\right\},\ \text{(ii) }Y_{i,(x,\mathbf{m})}\perp\mathbf{M}_{i}\mid\{\mathbf{C}_{i},X_{i},\eta_{i}\},

\displaystyle\text{(iii) }\mathbf{M}_{i,(x)}\perp X_{i}\mid\left\{\mathbf{C}_{i},\eta_{i}\right\},\ \text{(iv) }Y_{i,(x,\mathbf{m})}\perp\mathbf{M}_{i,(x^{\prime})}\mid\left\{\mathbf{C}_{i},\eta_{i}\right\}.

(3)

The first three assumptions ensure that the confounders
C_{i},\eta_{i}
controls the outcome-treatment confounding, the outcome-mediator confounding, and the mediator-treatment confounding respectively. The fourth assumption ensures the outcome-mediator confounders are not affected by the underlying endogenous treatment
x
. For Assumption (iv) to hold, in the true data-generating mechanism,
\eta_{i}
is allowed to be a baseline variable, but not allowed to be a post-exposure variable affected by
X_{i}
, that also affect both the mediator
M_{i}(s)
and outcome
Y_{i}
, while not being part of the mediator of interest. We include a detailed discussion on the implications of Assumption (iv) in Supplementary Section
S10
.

The set of assumptions in (
3
) is the same as in
Ding and Vanderweele (2016)
, where a sensitivity analysis strategy is proposed for binary outcomes with binary exposures and scalar mediators. Here, we take advantage of the subject-specific spatial confounding effect
\eta_{i}
as the unobserved confounders.
Under SUTVA (Stable Unit Treatment Value Assumption) and causal identification assumptions in (
3
), following a similar argument in
Lindquist (2012)
, we can derive the NIE and NDE in the form of estimable parameters using the proposed structural equation models (
1
) and (
2
).

Conditional on
\left\{\mathbf{C}_{i},\eta_{i}\right\}
, the average treatment effect between
x
and
x^{\prime}
is

\mathbb{E}\left[Y_{i,\{x,\mathbf{M}_{i,(x)}\}}-Y_{i,\{x^{\prime},\mathbf{M}_{i,(x^{\prime})}\}}\right],

which can be decomposed into the NIE,

\mathbb{E}\left[Y_{i,\{x,\mathbf{M}_{i,(x)}\}}-Y_{i,\{x,\mathbf{M}_{i,(x^{\prime})}\}}\right]=\sum_{j=1}^{p}\beta(s_{j})\alpha(s_{j})\lambda(\Delta s_{j})(x-x^{\prime}),

and the NDE,

\mathbb{E}\left[Y_{i,\{x,\mathbf{M}_{i,(x^{\prime})}\}}-Y_{i,\{x^{\prime},\mathbf{M}_{i,(x^{\prime})}\}}\right]=\gamma(x-x^{\prime}).

Our primary interest is in estimating the spatially-varying NIE, defined as
\mathcal{E}(s)=\alpha(s)\beta(s)
, and the scalar-valued NIE is
\mathcal{E}=\sum_{j=1}^{p}\alpha(s_{j})\beta(s_{j})\lambda(\Delta s_{j})
.
For spatial mediation analysis, we are not only interested in the scalar-valued NIE
\mathcal{E}
, but also the spatial decomposition of NIE in
\mathcal{S}
where
\mathcal{E}(s)
is nonzero.

The joint identifiability of models (
1
) and (
2
) is non-trivial, especially with the introduction of
\eta_{i}
and

Define column vectors
\mathbf{X}=\left(X_{1},\dots,X_{n}\right)^{\mathrm{T}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}

\mathbf{C}=\left(\mathbf{C}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{C}_{n}\right)^{\mathrm{T}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times q}

Assumption 1
.

(i) Conditional on

\left\{\mathbf{X},\mathbf{C},\bm{\theta}_{\eta,1},\dots,\bm{\theta}_{\eta,L}\right\}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times(L+1+q)}

\mathcal{S}_{m}\subset\mathcal{S}
where

\left\{\mathbf{X},\mathbf{C},\left\{\bm{\eta}(s_{k})\right\}_{s_{k}\in\mathcal{S}_{m}}\right\}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times(m+1+q)}

(iv) The unobserved confounding effect

is either low-rank (i.e.
\nu(s)=\sum_{l=1}^{L}\theta_{\nu,l}\psi_{l}(s)

\bm{\nu}=(\nu(s_{1}),\dots,\nu(s_{p}))\in\left\{v\in\mathbb{R}^{p}:\|v\|_{0}=m\right\}

).

2.1
Structured Mediation When Omitting Unobserved Confounders

If we ignore the impact of unobserved spatial confounder on the outcome, the potentially biased outcome model can be defined as

\displaystyle Y_{i}=\sum_{j=1}^{p}\beta(s_{j})M_{i}(\Delta s_{j})+\gamma X_{i}+\sum_{k=1}^{q}\zeta_{k}C_{i,k}+\epsilon_{Y,i},\quad\epsilon_{Y,i}\overset{i.i.d}{\sim}N(0,\sigma^{2}_{Y}).

(4)

This outcome model (
4
), a simplified special case of our proposed BASMU model when
\nu(s)\equiv 0
for all
s\in\mathcal{S}
, is widely applied in many high-dimensional mediation analysis methods
(
Lindquist, 2012
;
Song et al., 2021a
;
Song et al., 2021b
;
Jiang and Colditz, 2023
;
Wang et al., 2023
;
Xu et al., 2026
)
. These methods often assume the high dimensional mediation effects have some low-dimensional latent structures; the difference is in the various low-dimensional space representations, including but not limited to: mapping through a known set of bases, such as the piecewise polynomials B-spline basis
(
Lindquist, 2012
)
, or the global Bernstein polynomial basis
(
Jiang and Colditz, 2023
)
, in the Bayesian alternative, through Gaussian Process (GP) priors
(
Xu et al., 2026
)
or vector-valued priors with a dense covariance matrix
(
Song et al., 2021a
)
, or imposing a conditional autoregressive correlation structure
(
Wang et al., 2023
)
. Using low-dimensional representations to approximate the high-dimensional parameter is an effective and ubiquitous approach in many high-dimensional data analysis problems. This makes our proposed BASMU framework even more valuable, because this idea can essentially be extended to any type of low-dimensional representations simply by changing the structure of
\eta_{i}
to the corresponding low-dimensional manifold.
As one example, Bayesian Image Mediation Analysis (BIMA)
(
Xu et al., 2026
)
has demonstrated the flexibility of using GP priors since the low-dimensional representation can be adjusted using different covariance kernels. We refer to model (
1
) and (
4
) as the Bayesian Image Mediation Analysis (BIMA) framework
(
Xu et al., 2026
)
, where the unobserved confounders are omitted.

2.2
Bayesian Bias Analysis With Omitted Unobserved Confounders

The bias analysis highlights the need for BASMU: spatial mediation models like BIMA are unbiased only without unobserved confounders, but in practice, bias is inevitable and depends on both confounder scale and spatial mediator distribution.
The posterior mean of
\alpha
in the mediator model (
1
) is a consistent estimator
(
Xu et al., 2026
)
. Hence the main focus is on the asymptotic bias of the posterior mean of
\beta
under (
4
) as a point estimator. We assign a GP prior on
\beta\sim\mathcal{GP}(0,\sigma_{\beta}^{2}\kappa)
. By Mercer’s theorem, for a given kernel
\kappa(s,s^{\prime})=\sum_{l=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{l}\psi_{l}(s)\psi_{l}(s^{\prime})
, we can represent
\beta(s)=\sum_{l=1}^{L}\theta_{\theta_{\beta},l}\psi_{l}(s)
,
L=o(n)
where
\theta_{\beta,l}\overset{\scriptsize\mbox{ind}}{\sim}N(0,\lambda_{l}\sigma_{\beta}^{2})
.

We use a superscript ‘0’ to denote a true parameter value, e.g.,
\theta^{0}

U^{0}:=\left(\bm{\eta}^{0}\right)^{\mathrm{T}}\nu^{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and given estimators
\widehat{\bm{\eta}}
and
\widehat{\nu}
, denote
\widehat{U}:=\left(\widehat{\bm{\eta}}\right)^{\mathrm{T}}\widehat{\nu}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}

4

For the structured mediator

,
\epsilon_{M,i}(\Delta s_{j})\overset{\scriptsize\mbox{ind}}{\sim}N(0,\sigma_{M}^{2}\lambda(\Delta s_{j}))
, denote
E_{i}(s):=\mathbb{E}\left\{M_{i}(s)\right\}

\theta_{E,i,l}=\int_{\mathcal{S}}E_{i}(s)\psi_{l}(s)\lambda(\text{d}s)

.

(i) For any

,

A
satisfy

0<c_{\min}<\lim\inf_{n\to\infty}\sigma_{\min}(A)/n\leq\lim\sup_{n\to\infty}\sigma_{\max}(A)/n\leq c_{\max}<\infty

with probability
1-\exp\left\{-c_{0}n\right\}
for some positive constant
c_{0},c_{\min},c_{\max}
. In addition, in the GP prior basis coefficients
\theta_{\beta,l}\overset{\scriptsize\mbox{ind}}{\sim}N(0,\sigma_{\beta}^{2}\lambda_{l})
, assume that
\lambda_{L}>c_{\lambda}n^{-1+a_{\lambda}}
for some positive constant
c_{\lambda},a_{\lambda}
. We can draw the following conclusions given Assumptions
2
and
3
:

(i) When
U^{0}=\bf 0
, i.e. no unobserved confounder, the asymptotic bias of
\widehat{\theta}_{\beta}^{B}
is 0.

(ii) Given Assumptions
2
and
3
, and assume that the true unobserved confounder term
\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(U_{i}^{0}\right)^{2}/n
is finite, then the bias of the posterior mean of
\theta_{\beta}
under BIMA model (
4
)
\text{bias}(\widehat{\theta}_{\beta}^{B})\overset{p}{\to}(H+\sigma_{M}^{2}I_{L})^{-1}h^{0}
, and the bias under the full model (
2
)
\text{bias}(\widehat{\theta}_{\beta}^{F})\overset{p}{\to}(H+\sigma_{M}^{2}I_{L})^{-1}\left(h^{0}-\widehat{h}\right)
.

Remark.
The result of Theorem
1
is conditional on the true values of
\gamma
and
\zeta_{k}
in (
2
) for simplicity of the analysis. A similar bias analysis result can be drawn on the NDE
\gamma
if we treat
\mathbf{X}
as one additional column in
\widetilde{M}
, and the bias of
\gamma
is the corresponding element in
\text{bias}(\widehat{\theta}_{\beta}^{B})
and
\text{bias}(\widehat{\theta}_{\beta}^{F})
.

Implications.
Theorem
1
(i) can be seen as a corollary of (ii). Based on Theorem
1
(ii), we can expect that: (a) the bias of BIMA depends on the scale of the unobserved term
h^{0}
, and is nonzero unless
h^{0}=0
; (b) the bias of BASMU depends on the estimation of
\widehat{\eta}
and
\widehat{\nu}
; (c) larger random noise
\sigma_{M}
in the mediator model may reduce the bias, because larger random noise makes the observed mediator
M_{i}(s)
less correlated with the subject-specific spatial confounding effect
\eta_{i}(s)
.

3
Two-stage Estimation

The most natural way to estimate models (
1
) and (
2
) is to use a fully Bayesian approach to update all parameters iteratively. This involves updating the subject-specific spatial confounding effects
\eta_{i}
jointly from both models (
1
) and (
2
) in every iteration. Due to the large parameter space of
\left\{\eta_{i}\right\}_{i=1}^{n}
to search from, the joint estimation approach usually takes very long to converge. For every iteration when
\left\{\eta_{i}\right\}_{i=1}^{n}
is updated, the new
\left\{\eta_{i}\right\}_{i=1}^{n}
can have a huge impact on the likelihood of (
2
), and all other parameters in (
2
) need longer iterations to converge to stable values, hence the joint estimation can make estimating the outcome model (
2
) very unstable. Because the posterior of
\left\{\eta_{i}\right\}_{i=1}^{n}
is mainly dominated by the mediator model (
1
), sampling
\left\{\eta_{i}\right\}_{i=1}^{n}
based solely on (
1
) can already give a consistent estimation (see remark of Assumption
1
), hence instead of the fully Bayesian joint estimation approach, we propose the two-stage estimation.

In the two-stage estimation, we compute the posterior of model (
1
) and (
2
) separately. First, we draw posterior samples based on model (
1
) based on the priors

\alpha\sim\mathcal{GP}(0,\sigma^{2}_{\alpha}\kappa),\xi_{k}\sim\mathcal{GP}(0,\sigma^{2}_{\xi}\kappa),\eta_{i}\sim\mathcal{GP}(0,\sigma^{2}_{\eta}\kappa),

(5)

and compute the posterior mean of
\eta_{i}
conditional only on model (
1
), denoted as
\widehat{\eta}_{i}
. Using
\widehat{\eta}_{i}
as part of the fixed design matrix in (
2
), draw MCMC samples from (
2
) conditioning on
\eta_{i}=\widehat{\eta}_{i}
, based on the following prior for
\nu
,

\displaystyle\nu(s)

\displaystyle=g(s)\delta(s),\quad g(s)\overset{\scriptsize\mbox{ind}}{\sim}N(0,\sigma_{\nu}^{2}),\quad\delta(s)\overset{\scriptsize\mbox{ind}}{\sim}\text{Ber}(1/2).

(6)

The two-stage estimator uses a flexible spatially independent prior on
\nu
with a sparsity-inducing variable
\delta
. Simulations show
\widehat{\eta}_{i}
estimates
\eta_{i}
well when
p\gg L
(i.e., the
\eta_{i}
kernel is smooth). Estimating
\nu
remains challenging if
\eta_{i}
is misspecified, but the flexible prior still greatly improves
\beta
estimation over BIMA. For faster computation, we apply SVD to
\left\{\eta_{i}\right\}_{i=1}^{n}
; the full algorithm is in Web Algorithm
1
.

We compare BIMA, which ignores unobserved confounders, with BASMU’s two-stage estimator in simulated examples. Though not fully Bayesian, it yields accurate point estimates and debiases
\beta
provided
\widehat{\eta}_{i}
is reasonably close to the truth. Supplementary Section
S7
provides additional simulation comparisons among different implementations of BASMU, including a fully joint update algorithm.

6
Conclusion and Discussion

In this work, based on the BIMA
(
Xu et al., 2026
)
, we propose the BASMU framework for structured mediators to account for the unobserved confounder effects. We utilize the subject-specific spatial confounding effects as the unobserved confounders and incorporate them into the outcome model. We provide rigorous proof for the theoretical analysis on the asymptotic bias of the outcome model, and the identifiability of the BASMU model. For the estimation step, due to the complexity of the BASMU model, we propose the two-stage estimation algorithm. While full Bayesian inference from joint estimation is challenging, our two-stage estimation method yields reasonably accurate point estimates for
\beta
and NIE, as evidenced by extensive simulation results. Alternative ways of the two-stage algorithm include bootstrap, or updating
\eta_{i}
and updating all other parameters until convergence and then iteratively updating
\eta_{i}
in loops until full convergence. We apply BASMU in the ABCD study and find the mediation effect takes a larger proportion after adjusting for the unobserved confounders. A discussion on the limitation of BASMU can be found in the Supplementary Materials Section
S10
.

Proof of Proposition
1

Proof.

Denote
\theta=\left(\alpha,\beta,\xi,\zeta,\eta,\nu,\sigma_{M},\sigma_{Y}\right)
as the collection of all parameters.
First of all, based on model (
1
) and the fact that for the intensity measure of
M_{i}(\Delta s_{j})
over a small voxel partition
\Delta s_{j}
follows Gaussian distribution
M_{i}(\Delta s_{j})=\mathbb{E}\left\{M_{i}(s_{j})\right\}\lambda(\Delta s_{j})+\epsilon_{M,i}(\Delta s_{j})
, the mean and variance function of
M_{i}(\Delta s_{j})
can be uniquely identified, hence
\sigma_{M}
and the mean function
\mathbb{E}\left\{M_{i}(s_{j})\mid\theta,X_{i},C_{i}\right\}
are both uniquely identifiable. We denote the mean function
\mathbb{E}\left\{M_{i}(s_{j})\mid\theta,X_{i},C_{i}\right\}
as

\displaystyle A_{i,j}(\alpha,\xi,\eta_{i})

\displaystyle=X_{i}\alpha(s_{j})+\sum_{k=1}^{q}C_{i,k}\xi_{k}(s_{j})+\eta_{i}(s_{j})

Similarly,
\sigma_{Y}
is also uniquely identifiable. In the derivation below, for simplicity we denote

\displaystyle B_{i}(\gamma,\zeta,\nu,\eta_{i})

\displaystyle=X_{i}\gamma+\sum_{k=1}^{q}C_{i,k}\zeta_{k}+\sum_{j=1}^{p}\nu(s_{j})\eta_{i}(s_{j})\lambda(\Delta s_{j})

Let
\mathbf{M}_{i}=\left\{M_{i}(\Delta s_{j})\right\}_{j=1}^{p}
.
Conditional on the covariates
X_{i},C_{i,k}
,
the joint distribution of
Y_{i},\mathbf{M}_{i}
can be expressed as

\displaystyle\pi\left(Y_{i},\mathbf{M}_{i}\mid X_{i},\left\{C_{i}\right\}_{k=1}^{m},\theta\right)=\pi\left(Y_{i}\mid\mathbf{M}_{i},X_{i},\left\{C_{i}\right\}_{k=1}^{m},\theta\right)\prod_{j=1}^{p}\pi\left(M_{i}(\Delta s_{j})\mid X_{i},\left\{C_{i}\right\}_{k=1}^{m},\theta\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma_{Y}^{2}}}\exp\left\{-\frac{1}{2\sigma_{Y}^{2}}\left(Y_{i}-\sum_{j=1}^{p}M_{i}(\Delta s_{j})\beta(s_{j})-B_{i}(\gamma,\zeta,\nu,\eta_{i})\right)^{2}\right\}

\displaystyle\times\prod_{j=1}^{p}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma_{M}^{2}\lambda(\Delta s_{j})}}\right)\times\exp\left\{-\frac{1}{2\sigma_{M}^{2}}\sum_{j=1}^{p}\frac{1}{\lambda(\Delta s_{j})}\left(M_{i}(\Delta s_{j})-A_{i,j}(\alpha,\xi,\eta_{i})\right)^{2}\right\}

Suppose that
Y_{i}
takes value
y_{i}
and
M_{i}(\Delta s_{j})
takes value
m_{i,j}
in the joint density function, note that
y_{i}
and
m_{i,j}
can be any real values. We can write the log joint density function over the
i
-th individual
i=1,\dots,n
as

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left\{\log\pi(y_{i},\left\{m_{i,j}\right\}_{j=1}^{p}\mid X_{i},\left\{C_{i,k}\right\}_{k=1}^{q},\theta)\right\}

\displaystyle\propto

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Bigg\{-\frac{1}{2\sigma_{Y}^{2}}y_{i}^{2}-\frac{1}{2\sigma_{Y}^{2}}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{p}m_{i,j}\beta(s_{j})\right)^{2}-\frac{1}{2\sigma_{Y}^{2}}B_{i}^{2}(\gamma,\zeta,\nu,\eta_{i})

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{\sigma_{Y}^{2}}y_{i}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{p}m_{i,j}\beta(s_{j})\right)+\frac{1}{\sigma_{Y}^{2}}B_{i}(\gamma,\zeta,\nu,\eta_{i})y_{i}+\frac{1}{\sigma_{Y}^{2}}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{p}m_{i,j}\beta(s_{j})\right)B_{i}(\gamma,\zeta,\nu,\eta_{i})

\displaystyle-\frac{p}{2\sigma_{M}^{2}}\sum_{j=1}^{p}\left(m_{i,j}\right)^{2}-\frac{p}{2\sigma_{M}^{2}}A_{i,j}^{2}(\alpha,\xi,\eta)+\frac{p}{2\sigma_{M}^{2}}\sum_{j=1}^{p}m_{i,j}A_{i,j}(\alpha,\xi,\eta_{i})\Bigg\}

This is a polynomial of
y_{i},m_{i,1},\dots,m_{i,p}
, and we only need to match the coefficients of the first-order, second-order, and interaction terms to identify the unique coefficients. Hence
\sigma_{Y}^{2}
can be uniquely determined by the quadratic term
\sum_{i=1}^{n}y_{i}^{2}
, similarly
\left\{\beta(s_{j})\right\}_{j=1}^{p}
uniquely determined by the interaction terms
\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{n}y_{i}m_{i,j}\right\}_{j=1}^{p}
, and
B_{i}(\gamma,\zeta,\nu,\eta_{i})
uniquely determined by the first-order term
y_{i}
. Given that
\left\{\beta(s_{j})\right\}_{j=1}^{p}
and
\sigma_{Y}^{2}
are uniquely identifiable, we can also uniquely determine
\sigma_{M}^{2}
from the coefficient of
\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{p}m_{i,j}^{2}
. Given the identified
\left\{\beta(s_{j})\right\}_{j=1}^{p}
,
\sigma_{Y}^{2}
and
\sigma_{M}^{2}
,
A_{i,j}(\alpha,\xi,\eta_{i})
is also identified from the coefficient of the first-order
m_{i,j}
.

Now we have shown the identifiability of
\sigma_{Y}^{2},\sigma_{M}^{2},\left\{\beta(s_{j})\right\}_{j=1}^{p}
,
A_{i,j}(\alpha,\xi,\eta_{i})
and
B_{i}(\gamma,\zeta,\nu,\eta_{i})
.

Next, we need to show that the rest of the parameters
\left(\alpha,\xi,\zeta,\eta,\nu\right)
can also be uniquely identified from
A_{i,j}(\alpha,\xi,\eta_{i})
and
B_{i}(\gamma,\zeta,\nu,\eta_{i})
. Given Assumption
1
.1-2, the identifiability of
\alpha,\xi,\eta
in
A_{i,j}(\alpha,\xi,\eta_{i})
directly follows from Proposition 1 in
Xu et al. (2026)
.

To show the identifiability of
\gamma,\zeta,\nu
in
B_{i}(\gamma,\zeta,\nu,\eta_{i})
, note that given
B_{i}(\gamma,\zeta,\nu,\eta_{i})
and
\eta_{i}
are identifiable for
i=1,\dots,n
, comparing
B_{i}(\gamma^{\prime},\zeta^{\prime},\nu^{\prime},\eta_{i})=B_{i}(\gamma,\zeta,\nu,\eta_{i}),i=1,\dots,n
to reach the identifiability of
\gamma,\zeta,\nu
is equivalent to solving a linear system (given that the design matrix is full rank, Assumption
1
.3 ) with
n
equations and
p+1+q
variables. Hence under the assumption (ii) where
\nu
is sparse,
\nu\in\Theta^{\text{SP}}
, when
n
is large enough, the number of nonzero elements in
\nu
will be smaller than
n-q-1
, hence
\gamma,\zeta,\nu
are all identifiable.

Under assumption (i),
\nu
is spatially-correlated and can be decomposed using
L
number of basis. Let
\nu(s)=\sum_{l=1}^{L}\theta_{\nu,l}\psi_{l}(s)
, and

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{S}}\nu(s)\eta_{i}(s)\lambda(\text{d}s)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{S}}\sum_{l=1}^{L}\theta_{\nu,l}\psi_{l}(s)\eta_{i}(s)\lambda(\text{d}s)=\sum_{l=1}^{L}\theta_{\nu,l}\theta_{\eta_{i},l}

Hence

B_{i}(\gamma,\zeta,\nu,\eta_{i})=X_{i}\gamma+\sum_{k=1}^{q}C_{i,k}\zeta_{k}+\sum_{l=1}^{L}\theta_{\nu,l}\theta_{\eta_{i},l}

Based on Assumption
1
.3, with the design matrix
B=\left(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{C}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{C}_{q},\bm{\theta}_{\eta,1},\dots,\bm{\theta}_{\eta,L}\right)\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times\left(1+L+q\right)}
. And
B_{i}(\gamma,\zeta,\nu,\eta_{i})-B_{i}(\gamma^{\prime},\zeta^{\prime},\nu^{\prime},\eta_{i})=0
for
i=1,\dots,n
can be written as

\displaystyle B\cdot\left\{\left(\gamma,\zeta,\theta_{\nu,1},\dots,\theta_{\nu,L}\right)^{\mathrm{T}}-\left(\gamma^{\prime},\zeta^{\prime},\theta^{\prime}_{\nu,1},\dots,\theta^{\prime}_{\nu,L}\right)^{\mathrm{T}}\right\}=\bf 0

By Assumption
1
.3,
\det(B)>0
, hence
\left(\gamma,\zeta,\theta_{\nu,1},\dots,\theta_{\nu,L}\right)=\left(\gamma^{\prime},\zeta^{\prime},\theta^{\prime}_{\nu,1},\dots,\theta^{\prime}_{\nu,L}\right)
, therefore
\gamma,\zeta,\nu
are also identifiable.
Similarly, if
\nu\in\Theta^{\text{SP}}
, the design matrix becomes

\left\{\mathbf{X},\mathbf{C}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{C}_{q},\left\{\bm{\eta}(s_{k})\right\}_{s_{k}\in\mathcal{S}_{m}}\right\}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times(m+1+q)}

where
\mathcal{S}_{m}=\left\{s:\nu(s)\neq 0\right\}
, and is also full rank by Assumption
1
.
∎

Proof of Theorem

Proof.

Throughout this proof, we use the notation

Using the decomposition on

\displaystyle Y_{i}=\sum_{l=1}^{L}\theta_{\beta,l}\tilde{M}_{i,l}+\gamma X_{i}+\sum_{k=1}^{q}\zeta_{k}C_{i,k}+\sum_{j=1}^{p}\eta_{i}(s_{j})\nu(s_{j})+\epsilon_{Y,i}

The posterior mean of

\displaystyle\text{Var}\left\{\theta_{\beta}|\sim\right\}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{1}{\sigma_{\beta}^{2}}D^{-1}+\frac{1}{\sigma_{Y}^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\tilde{M}_{i}\tilde{M}_{i}^{\mathrm{T}}\right)^{-1}

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left\{\theta_{\beta}|\sim\right\}

\displaystyle=\text{Var}\left\{\theta_{\beta}|\sim\right\}\left\{\frac{1}{\sigma_{Y}^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(Y_{i}-\gamma X_{i}-\sum_{k=1}^{q}\zeta_{k}C_{i,k}-\sum_{j=1}^{p}\eta_{i}(s_{j})\nu(s_{j})\right)\tilde{M}_{i}\right\}

To simplify these two bias expressions, we denote
\widetilde{M}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times L}
with each row being
\widetilde{M}_{i}^{\mathrm{T}}
. Let
A:=\widetilde{M}^{T}\widetilde{M}\in\mathbb{R}^{L\times L}

Denote the point estimator
\hat{\theta}_{\beta}^{F}=\mathbb{E}\left\{\theta_{\beta}|\sim\right\}

\theta^{0}

Conditional on the estimator
\hat{\eta}

\hat{\nu}

\displaystyle\text{bias}(\hat{\theta}_{\beta}^{F})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left(\hat{\theta}_{\beta}^{F}-\theta_{\beta}^{0}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{Y|\left\{X,C,M\right\}}\left(\hat{\theta}_{\beta}^{F}\right)-\theta_{\beta}^{0}

\displaystyle=\text{Var}\left\{\theta_{\beta}|\sim\right\}\frac{1}{\sigma_{Y}^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left\{\left(\theta_{\beta}^{0}\right)^{\mathrm{T}}\widetilde{M}_{i}+\left(\nu^{0}\right)^{\mathrm{T}}\eta_{i}^{0}-\left(\hat{\nu}\right)^{\mathrm{T}}\hat{\eta}_{i}\right\}\widetilde{M}_{i}-\theta_{\beta}^{0}

Similarly, if we denote the point estimator using BIMA model as
\hat{\theta}_{\beta}^{B}
,

\displaystyle\text{bias}(\hat{\theta}_{\beta}^{B})

\displaystyle=\text{Var}\left\{\theta_{\beta}|\sim\right\}\frac{1}{\sigma_{Y}^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left\{\left(\theta_{\beta}^{0}\right)^{\mathrm{T}}\widetilde{M}_{i}+\left(\nu^{0}\right)^{\mathrm{T}}\eta_{i}^{0}\right\}\widetilde{M}_{i}-\theta_{\beta}^{0}

Recall the notation
\widetilde{M}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times L}
and
A:=\widetilde{M}^{T}\widetilde{M}\in\mathbb{R}^{L\times L}

\text{bias}(\hat{\theta}_{\beta}^{F})
. By the singular value assumption of

, with probability
1-\exp\left\{-c_{0}n\right\}
,
A
is full rank. Conditioning on the event that

is full rank hence invertible, denote
\bm{\eta}^{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times p}
with the
i

\eta_{i}^{\mathrm{T}}
.

\displaystyle\text{bias}(\hat{\theta}_{\beta}^{B})

\displaystyle=\left[\frac{\sigma_{Y}^{2}}{\sigma_{\beta}^{2}}D^{-1}+A\right]^{-1}\left[A\theta_{\beta}^{0}+\left(\widetilde{M}\right)^{\mathrm{T}}\left(\bm{\eta}^{0}\right)^{\mathrm{T}}\nu^{0}\right]-\theta_{\beta}^{0}

\displaystyle=\left[\frac{\sigma_{Y}^{2}}{\sigma_{\beta}^{2}}D^{-1}A^{-1}+I_{L}\right]^{-1}\left[\theta_{\beta}^{0}+A^{-1}\left(\widetilde{M}\right)^{\mathrm{T}}\left(\bm{\eta}^{0}\right)^{\mathrm{T}}\nu^{0}\right]-\theta_{\beta}^{0}

\displaystyle\overset{(*)}{=}\left[I_{L}-\left(\tau^{2}DA+I_{L}\right)^{-1}\right]\left[\theta_{\beta}^{0}+A^{-1}\left(\widetilde{M}\right)^{\mathrm{T}}\left(\bm{\eta}^{0}\right)^{\mathrm{T}}\nu^{0}\right]-\theta_{\beta}^{0}

\displaystyle=A^{-1}\left(\widetilde{M}\right)^{\mathrm{T}}\left(\bm{\eta}^{0}\right)^{\mathrm{T}}\nu^{0}-\left(\tau^{2}DA+I_{L}\right)^{-1}\left[\theta_{\beta}^{0}-A^{-1}\left(\widetilde{M}\right)^{\mathrm{T}}\left(\bm{\eta}^{0}\right)^{\mathrm{T}}\nu^{0}\right]

Note that
(*)

(I+A)^{-1}=I-A(I+A)^{-1}=I-(A^{-1}+I)^{-1}
, and the notation
\tau^{-2}=\frac{\sigma_{Y}^{2}}{\sigma_{\beta}^{2}}
.

Proof of Part (i)

Now we can see that if
\left(\bm{\eta}^{0}\right)^{\mathrm{T}}\nu^{0}=\bm{0}
, i.e. when the unmeasured confounder effect does not exist, the bias of
\hat{\theta}_{\beta}^{F}
becomes

\displaystyle\text{bias}(\hat{\theta}_{\beta}^{B})

\displaystyle=-\left(\tau^{2}DA+I_{L}\right)^{-1}\theta_{\beta}^{0}

The range of
\text{bias}(\hat{\theta}_{\beta}^{B})
is controlled by the smallest and largest eigen-values of
(\tau^{2}DA+I_{L})^{-1}
, scaled up to a rotation of
\theta_{\beta}^{0}
. Note that
\sigma_{\min}(D)\sigma_{\min}(A)\leq\sigma_{\min}(DA)\leq\sigma_{\max}(DA)\leq\sigma_{\max}(D)\sigma_{\max}(A)
. With the assumption
\lambda_{L}>c_{\lambda}n^{-1+a_{\lambda}}
, use
h\gtrsim g
to denote the inequality
h>c_{g}g
up to a positive constant
c_{g}
that does not contain any rate of
n
. We can see that
\sigma_{\min}(D)\sigma_{\min}(A)\gtrsim n^{a_{\lambda}}\to\infty
as

, and
\sigma_{\max}(D)\sigma_{\max}(A)\lesssim n\to\infty
, hence
\text{bias}(\hat{\theta}_{\beta}^{B})\to\bm{0}
as
n\to\infty
.

Proof of Part (ii)

Similarly, when
\left(\bm{\eta}^{0}\right)^{\mathrm{T}}\nu^{0}\neq\bm{0}

\displaystyle\text{bias}(\hat{\theta}_{\beta}^{B})

\displaystyle=\left[\tau^{-2}D^{-1}+A\right]^{-1}\left(\widetilde{M}\right)^{\mathrm{T}}\left(\bm{\eta}^{0}\right)^{\mathrm{T}}\nu^{0}-\left(\tau^{2}DA+I_{L}\right)^{-1}\theta_{\beta}^{0}

\displaystyle\text{bias}(\hat{\theta}_{\beta}^{F})

\displaystyle=\left[\tau^{-2}D^{-1}+A\right]^{-1}\left(\widetilde{M}\right)^{\mathrm{T}}\left\{\left(\bm{\eta}^{0}\right)^{\mathrm{T}}\nu^{0}-\left(\hat{\bm{\eta}}\right)^{\mathrm{T}}\hat{\nu}\right\}-\left(\tau^{2}DA+I_{L}\right)^{-1}\theta_{\beta}^{0}

As we’ve shown that
\left(\tau^{2}DA+I_{L}\right)^{-1}\theta_{\beta}^{0}=o_{p}(1)
, we focus on the first term in both bias expressions.

For the image mediator
M_{i}
, the mediator model (
1
) assumes that
M_{i}(s_{j})=\mathbb{E}\left\{M_{i}(s_{j})\right\}+\sigma_{M}Z_{i,j}
, where
Z_{i,j}
are the independent standard normal variables. Under the orthonormal decomposition,
\widetilde{M}_{i,l}=\mu_{i,l}+\sigma_{M}Z_{i,l}
where the
Z_{i,l}
are still independent standard normal under orthonormal transformation, and
\mu_{i,l}
is a constant mean term that determines mean structure of
\widetilde{M}_{i,l}
.
Hence we can write
\widetilde{M}=\bm{\mu}+\sigma_{M}\mathbf{Z}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times L}
.

\displaystyle\left[\tau^{-2}D^{-1}+A\right]^{-1}\left(\widetilde{M}\right)^{\mathrm{T}}\left(\bm{\eta}^{0}\right)^{\mathrm{T}}\nu^{0}=

\displaystyle\left\{\frac{1}{n}\left[\tau^{-2}D^{-1}+\left(\bm{\mu}+\sigma_{M}\mathbf{Z}\right)^{\mathrm{T}}\left(\bm{\mu}+\sigma_{M}\mathbf{Z}\right)\right]\right\}^{-1}\left\{\frac{1}{n}\left(\bm{\mu}+\sigma_{M}\mathbf{Z}\right)^{\mathrm{T}}\left(\bm{\eta}^{0}\right)^{\mathrm{T}}\nu^{0}\right\}

The denominator can be broken down into 4 parts,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\left[\tau^{-2}D^{-1}+\left(\bm{\mu}+\sigma_{M}\mathbf{Z}\right)^{\mathrm{T}}\left(\bm{\mu}+\sigma_{M}\mathbf{Z}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\tau^{-2}D^{-1}+\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mu_{i}\mu_{i}^{\mathrm{T}}+\frac{\sigma_{M}^{2}}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}Z_{i}Z_{i}^{\mathrm{T}}+\frac{\sigma_{M}}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\mu_{i}Z_{i}^{\mathrm{T}}+Z_{i}\mu_{i}^{\mathrm{T}}\right)

The first term
\frac{1}{n}\tau^{-2}D^{-1}=o_{p}(1)
since
1/n/\lambda_{L}\lesssim n^{-a_{\lambda}}\to 0
.

By Assumption
2

\mu_{i,l}=\int_{\mathcal{S}}\left\{E_{i}(s)\psi_{l}(s)\right\}\lambda(\text{d}s)
. We denote
H=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mu_{i}\mu_{i}^{\mathrm{T}}\in\mathbb{R}^{L\times L}
, where the
(l,l^{\prime})
-th element in
H
is
H_{l,l^{\prime}}
, a finite constant introduced in Assumption
2
.

The third term has the limit
\frac{\sigma_{M}^{2}}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}Z_{i}Z_{i}^{\mathrm{T}}\overset{p}{\to}\sigma_{M}^{2}I_{L}
due to the i.i.d. normality of
\mathbf{Z}
. The last term is also
o_{p}(1)
, because the
(l,l^{\prime})
-th term is a normal variable with mean 0 and variance
\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\mu_{i,l}^{2}+\mu_{i,l^{\prime}}^{2}\right)
for
l\neq l^{\prime}
, and
\frac{4}{n^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mu_{i,l}^{2}
for

(H)_{l,l}=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\mu_{i,l}^{2}\right)

(H)_{l,l}/n\to 0
.

Hence the denominator is equivalent to
o_{p}(1)+H+\sigma_{M}^{2}I_{L}
.

To analyze the numerator and simplify the notations, we use

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\left(\bm{\mu}+\sigma_{M}\mathbf{Z}\right)^{\mathrm{T}}\left(\bm{\eta}^{0}\right)^{\mathrm{T}}\nu^{0}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left\{\mu_{i,l}+\sigma_{M}Z_{i,l}\right\}U_{i}^{0}\right)_{l=1}^{L}

Note that
\frac{\sigma_{M}}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}Z_{i,l}U_{i}^{0}\overset{p}{\to}\bf 0
for all

since

With the Assumption

is a finite vector in
\mathbb{R}^{L}
, we can draw the conclusion that
\text{bias}(\hat{\theta}_{\beta}^{B})\overset{p}{\to}(H+\sigma_{M}^{2}I_{L})^{-1}h^{0}

Similarly, if we define

\theta_{\beta}

The two-stage approach described in Algorithm
1
is not a fully Bayesian approach, and this can raise problems in the uncertainty quantification using the posterior samples. The simulations in the main manuscript have demonstrated the point estimation accuracy, and this section provides additional empirical validation on the variable selection accuracy using the posterior credible interval. The key criterion of interest is to use the 95% credible interval as the selection criterion for active spatial NIE
\mathcal{E}(s)
.

We implement three versions of the BASMU model, and compare them with BIMA to check the variable selection accuracy. The main version is Two-stage with the sparse
\nu
prior, the same prior used in the main manuscript (
6

\nu

\nu\sim\mathcal{GP}(0,\sigma_{\nu}^{2}\kappa)

5

\nu
prior.

Sensitivity analysis under different kernel specifications

In the main analysis, we use a region-specific Matérn kernel where the smoothness parameters differ for each region. As discussed in Section
2
, the latent structure of the subject-specific spatial confounding effect
\eta_{i}(s)
matters in the real data analysis. The kernel we used in the main analysis is chosen based on smoothness parameters that roughly match the empirical correlation, and slightly tuned to be more flexible. Since we don’t know the ground-truth latent structure of
\eta_{i}(s)

According to the Matérn kernel formulation in (
7
), the two smoothness parameters are
\tau,\rho

\tau,\rho
for each region by empirically matching the GP kernel with the sample covariance matrix. This kernel is referred to as the
base
kernel. Then, we try to set the smoothness parameters
\tau,\rho
the same across different regions, and we experiment with two such pairs,
(\tau=1,\rho=15)
and
(\tau=0.2,\rho=80)
. Lastly, we also try to vary the
base
kernel a little by keep
\tau
the same, and changing
\rho
to be
90\%
or
110\%
of the
base
values of
\rho
for each region. The
110\%\rho
based on the
base

To evaluate the performance of different kernels, we randomly split the whole data set into 50% training and 50% testing, and compute the test MSE for different kernels. The latent structure of
\eta_{i}(s)

\eta_{i}(s)

S10
Additional discussion on the limitation of BASMU

The limitation of this work is that the mediator has to be spatially smooth or satisfy certain pre-fixed correlation structures for the subject-specific spatial confounding effects to be estimated. In practice, unobserved confounders with more complex correlation structures may not be fully accounted for under the current BASMU framework.

As discussed in
VanderWeele et al. (2014)
, the
cross-world independence assumption
(assumption (iv) in (
3
)) requires that, in the true data generating mechanism, there is no post-exposure latent variables, affected by
X_{i}
, that also affect both the mediator
M_{i}(s)
and outcome
Y_{i}
, while not being part of the mediator of interest (i.e., no causal direction
X_{i}\to\eta_{i}
while
\eta_{i}\to M_{i},\eta_{i}\to Y_{i}
at the same time). This assumption is an untestable causal identification assumption, especially in the BASMU model, where
\eta_{i}
is an individual-level latent effect. In the real data analysis,
X_{i}
is the parental education level, and
\eta_{i}
is the spatial-varying individual effect coming from the mediator model, after regressing out
X_{i}

A few examples of such subject-specific spatial effects include: i) genetic polymorphisms influencing cortical maturation, since genetics are fixed at birth, clearly not caused by parental education; neurotoxic pollutant exposure (lead, air pollution), which is largely determined by residential environment, plausibly independent of parental education level;
baseline sleep or physical activity patterns, which reflect stable physiological and behavioral traits rather than parental behavior.

The focus of BASMU is to provide a way to reduce bias in the point estimation of scalar-valued NIE and NDE, and to select active mediation voxels using the criterion whether the Bayesian 95% credible intervals cover 0. The frequentist-style inference capability, such as the frequentist coverage probabilities of the point estimates for NIE and NDE in this Bayesian framework, would depend on the choice of the priors, especially priors for the variance parameters
\sigma_{\gamma}^{2},\sigma_{\beta}^{2},\sigma_{\nu}^{2}
. To achieve such frequentist coverage probabilities in finite samples, one needs to carefully set the priors to be adaptive to the sample size, which is beyond the scope of the current BASMU framework.
