Weighted Prime Powers Truncation of the Asymptotic Expansion for the Logarithmic Integral: Properties and Applications.
Shaun R. Deaton

Abstract
. Given the asymptotic expansion for the logarithmic integral ∫ 0 n d ​ t ln ⁡ ( t ) \int_{0}^{n}\frac{dt}{\ln(t)} , obtained from repeated integration by parts until the expansion terms reach a minimum; approaching zero. Which determines a cut-off for the number of terms in the expansion and this truncation is a function of n n . By dropping the minimization constraint and introducing a new variable x x for the number of expansion terms, consider the question: Where to truncate the asymptotic expansion for the logarithmic integral to equal the prime count function π ⁡ ( n ) \pi(n) ?. Although constructing this new truncation function requires using the non-trivial zeros ρ \rho of the Riemann zeta function. There exists a closed form approximation that does not utilize the zeros at all. From this, a new bound is obtained on the summation ∑ ρ l ​ i ​ ( n ρ ) \sum_{\rho}li(n^{\rho}) . Which is then compared to an equivalent form of the Schoenfeld bound derived for this same summation. Resulting in a proof that the Riemann Hypothesis is true. Keywords. Logarithmic Integral, Lambert W Function, Riemann Zeta Function, Riemann Hypothesis, Schoenfeld Bound.

1.
Introduction

After a chance, re-introduction to the asymptotic expansion for the logarithmic integral. A question arose: Where would the expansion have to be truncated so that it equals the prime count function
\pi(n)
? Answering this question, and following through on exploring others it will raise, shall be the focus of all that follows.

Beginning with a definition for the logarithmic integral, with a case including offset integration limits to account for the singularity, needed for numeric calculations:

Definition 1.1
.

li(n):=\int_{0}^{n}\frac{dt}{\ln(t)}=li(2)+\int_{2}^{n}\frac{dt}{\ln(t)}\approx 1.04516+\int_{2}^{n}\frac{dt}{\ln(t)}

The dependent variable
n
can be real-valued, the notation adopted here refers back to
\pi(n)
, where the input is generally taken as integer-valued. Being a non-elementary integral, one method of solution is to use repeated integration by parts to generate an asymptotic expansion. Given as follows:

Definition 1.2
.

\int_{0}^{n}\frac{dt}{\ln(t)}=\frac{n}{\ln(n)}\sum_{k=0}^{\lfloor x\rfloor-1}\frac{k!}{\ln^{k}(n)}+x!\int_{0}^{n}\frac{dt}{\ln^{x+1}(t)}

Since this expansion diverges with increasing
x
, to best approximate
li(n)
, it’s truncated at the value of
x
that minimizes the integral term. Which will generally not be integer valued, requiring the floor function for the summation to make sense; in addition to using
x!:=\Gamma(x+1)
. Although the method of constructing the expansion forces
x
to be integral valued, minimization means it must be viewed as continuous when appearing in the integral term. A notion that is then extended to the summation. When
x
isn’t integer valued, some authors have added a linear interpolating or fractional term for real
x
to reduce the truncation error.

Stieltjes in
[
12
]
calculated the minimizing truncation, the first two terms are
\tau=\tau(n)\approx\ln(n)-1/3
; all other terms are reciprocal powers of
\ln(n)
. Stieltjes’s paper, originally in French, was reviewed in van Boven et. al.
[
14
]
, where a fractional term is included, ”derived from a heuristic reasoning about the remainder” of the expansion.

Another instance of using a fractional term occurs in the appendix of Stoll & Demichael
[
13
]
, along with additional correction terms and error bounds. For reference that information, preserving their notation, is reproduced here; where they use
x
as a general input variable, same as
n
is used just above. Also, notationally,
\log(x):=\ln(x)
and
L_{1}:=\log(x),L_{2}:=\log(\log(x)),\cdots,L_{j}:=\log(\log(\cdots\log(x)))
.

This summation begins to diverge at
k=\lfloor L_{1}\rfloor
so we stop at
k=\lfloor L_{1}-1\rfloor
. The error is almost logarithmically linear between integer changes in
L_{1}
, but the envelope of the error is not symmetric:

li(x)\cong\frac{x}{L_{1}}\sum_{k=0}^{\lfloor L_{1}-1\rfloor}\dfrac{k!}{L_{1}^{k}}\kern 5.0pt\Big\{+1.2/\sqrt{L_{1}},-0.6/\sqrt{L_{1}}

For significantly higher accuracy,
li(x)
can be expressed as a finite sum with smaller, symmetric errors as

\begin{split}li(x)\cong&\frac{x}{L_{1}}\Big(\sum_{k=0}^{\lfloor L_{1}-1\rfloor}\dfrac{k!}{L_{1}^{k}}+\frac{(L_{1}-\lfloor L_{1}\rfloor)\lfloor L_{1}\rfloor!\kern 5.0pt}{L_{1}^{\lfloor L_{1}\rfloor}}\Big)\\
&-\Big(\frac{300L_{1}^{3/2}}{359L_{1}^{2}+L_{2}L_{1}-3L_{3}+3}\Big)-\Big(\frac{\sin(2\pi L_{1})}{7/3L_{1}^{2}61(L_{1}-2)}\Big).\end{split}

The remaining error is estimated to be

<\frac{23L_{2}^{2}}{(L_{1}^{2}+48L_{1})^{2}}
for
x>113
.

Although there are small differences between all the expansions for
li(n)
given so far; e.g. location of truncation point and when the floor is applied to the upper summation limit. These are minor enough that one can still infer properties relating them.
The property needed here is the error bound applying to an expansion with only the fractional term
\frac{(L_{1}-\lfloor L_{1}\rfloor)\lfloor L_{1}\rfloor!\kern 4.09723pt}{L_{1}^{\lfloor L_{1}\rfloor}}
added. Expecting that within the quoted range of error bounds from
\frac{1}{\sqrt{L_{1}}}
to
\frac{23L_{2}^{2}}{(L_{1}^{2}+48L_{1})^{2}}\sim\frac{1}{L_{1}^{4}}
, is where the error for including only this fractional term exists. Using this to guide a computational investigation into the approximation error to
li(n)
incurred by using and expansion of the form:

Definition 1.3
.

li(n;x):=\frac{n}{\ln(n)}\sum_{k=0}^{\lfloor x\rfloor-1}\frac{k!}{\ln^{k}(n)}+\frac{(x-\lfloor x\rfloor)\lfloor x\rfloor!\kern 4.09723ptn}{\ln^{\lfloor x\rfloor+1}(n)},\forall n>1
, and
x\geq 1
.

The
x
in definition (
1.3
) can take values independent of
n
. But to approximate

by using the Stieltjes truncation gives

Definition (

It essentially builds the expansion, except for the fractional term, and is then dropped; being effectively zero at it’s minimum. To sufficiently generalize this situation to be of use later, the main definition going forward is:

Definition 1.4

li(n;x):=\frac{n}{\ln(n)}\sum_{k=0}^{\lfloor x\rfloor-1}\frac{k!}{\ln^{k}(n)}+\frac{(x-\lfloor x\rfloor)\lfloor x\rfloor!\kern 4.09723ptn}{\ln^{\lfloor x\rfloor+1}(n)},\kern 5.0pt\forall n>1

The fractional term allows for the asymptotic expansion function

Theorem 3.2
.

(Average Prime Truncation) The function
\bar{g}_{\pi}(n)
provides an initial approximation to the average behavior of
{g}_{\pi}(n)
, requiring no information from the non-trivial zeta zeros.

Proof.

Consider that any truncation can be viewed as splitting
li(n)
into two functions at the truncation point
x
, a head and a tail such that;
li(n)\approx li(n;x)+[li(n;\tau)-li(n;x)]
. Using the truncation implied by equation (
3.5
), and letting
x=\bar{g}_{\pi}(n)
, then substituting into Riemmann’s equation (
3.1
).

(3.6)

\begin{split}\pi(n)+\sum_{r=2}^{\lfloor\log_{2}(n)\rfloor}\dfrac{\pi(n^{1/r})}{r}&\approx li(n;\bar{g}_{\pi}(n))+[li(n;\tau)-li(n;\bar{g}_{\pi}(n))]\\
&-\sum_{\rho}^{zeta-zeros}li(n^{\rho})+\int_{n}^{\infty}\frac{dt}{(t^{2}-1)t\ln(t)}-\ln(2)\end{split}

Since, by construction, the prime powers sum on the LHS is equal to the tail function in square brackets on the RHS, they cancel. This tail is the ”error” determined by the source integral, which was set to the sum of weighted prime power counts. Leaving,

(3.7)

\pi(n)-li(n;\bar{g}_{\pi}(n))\approx-\sum_{\rho}^{zeta-zeros}li(n^{\rho})+\int_{n}^{\infty}\frac{dt}{(t^{2}-1)t\ln(t)}-\ln(2)

As the constant and integral terms on the RHS provide diminishing and little influence over the zeros summation, the difference on the LHS must be predominantly controlled by the summation over non-trivial zeta function zeros. Whose oscillatory behavior is well known, shown specifically by Stoll & Demichael
[
13
]
to be expressible as a summation in terms of cosines; or sines. When
n
is considered to be a continuous variable it means that occasionally the LHS difference must be zero, giving an exact solution; the prime truncation from Theorem
2.4
. Even though the integral and constant terms will give a small shift to where this zero occurs, it still will occur.
In general the RHS summation term’s oscillation about
0
both bounds and ultimately controls, the LHS difference.

By using the first few zeta-zeros can move some summation terms
li(n^{\rho})
to modify the constraint
V=\sum_{r=2}^{\lfloor\log_{2}(n)\rfloor}\dfrac{\pi(n^{1/r})}{r}
. Now as more zeros are used, have a sequence of constraints and their related truncations, that move from
\bar{g}_{\pi}
to
g_{\pi}
. So,
\bar{g}_{\pi}
can be described as a baseline, or initial term, subject to corrections induced by
li(n^{\rho})
.

Therefore
\bar{g}_{\pi}(n)
must be an approximation to the average behavior of
g_{\pi}(n)
, determined by equation (
3.5
); requiring no non-trivial zeta zeros.

∎

), relating a summation over non-trivial zeros of the zeta function to a difference between the prime count
\pi(n)
and the truncated logarithmic integral
li(n;\bar{g}_{\pi}(n))
. Any bound on this difference is then a bound on the summation. Since the truncated version is generally closer to the prime count than
li(n)
. It must be able to provide a tighter bound. With a possible exception for Skewes region’s, where
li(n)<\pi(n)
.

Again, the Riemann Hypothesis (RH) is not being assumed. The aim here is to see what bearing a tighter bound has, if any, on the non-trivial zeta-zeros positioning. So far nothing has relied on the position of the zeta function’s non-trivial zeros. Other than being complex valued and occurring in complex conjugate pairs; as used in the proof of Theorem
3.2
.

The general idea for this section is to start from a new bound related to the difference
|\pi(n)-li(n)|
, that does not assume the RH to be true, separate
li(n)
into two pieces via the average prime truncation point, and then simplify. Finally, apply this new bound to the zeros summation in equation (
3.7
), and compare to another bound equivalent to the RH.

To help simplify the argument, will refer to the two parts that

is split into as the truncated logarithmic integral and the remainder; same as that used in the proof of Theorem

. Denoted by:

(5.1)

li(n)\approx li(n;\bar{g}_{\pi})+[li(n;\tau)-li(n;\bar{g}_{\pi})]=li(n;\bar{g}_{\pi})+li_{R}(n;\bar{g}_{\pi})

Now, referring back to equation (
3.7
), reproduced here for convenience. Remembering that the remainder and weighted prime powers have been canceled out.

\pi(n)-li(n;\bar{g}_{\pi}(n))\approx-\sum_{\rho}^{zeta-zeros}li(n^{\rho})+\int_{n}^{\infty}\frac{dt}{(t^{2}-1)t\ln(t)}-\ln(2)

\ \forall k\in\mathbb{N}_{0},\ \exists\eta_{k}

Creating a key element to proving:

Theorem 5.1
.

|li(n;\bar{g}_{\pi})-\pi(n)|<\dfrac{n}{\ln(n)}\dfrac{(\bar{g}_{\pi}-1)!}{\ln(n)^{\bar{g}_{\pi}-1}},\forall n\geq n_{\bar{g}_{\pi}}
.

Proof.

Using the following identity between the prime counting function and Chebyshev’s function; Axler
[
1
]
:

(5.4)

\pi(n)=\frac{\vartheta(n)}{\ln(n)}+\int_{2}^{n}\frac{\vartheta(t)}{t\ln^{2}(t)}dt

Starting with a reverse of the difference that one usually encounters. Generally obtained by expanding
li(n)
to first order, so that it has the same basic form as equation (
5.4
), then subtracting the prime count function. While also accounting for the constant term from the expansion, along with the slight offset between the definite integral in equation (
5.4
) and the one defining
li(n)
.

(5.5)

li(n)-\pi(n)=\frac{n-\vartheta(n)}{\ln(n)}-\frac{2}{\ln(2)}+li(2)+\int_{2}^{n}\frac{t-\vartheta(t)}{t\ln^{2}(t)}dt

The two constant terms sum is approximately
-1.840226
, a small correction for large
n
. Next, using
Theorem 5.2 [Dusart]
can bound the two RHS non-constant terms. Applying the choice
\eta_{k}=(k-1)!
for general exponents
k
, on the logarithm, there exists an
n_{k}
such that:

(5.6)

\frac{|n-\vartheta(n)|}{\ln(n)}<(k-1)!\frac{n}{\ln^{k}(n)}=\frac{n}{\ln(n)}\frac{(k-1)!}{\ln^{k-1}(n)},\forall n\geq n_{k}

(5.7)

\int_{2}^{n}\frac{|t-\vartheta(t)|}{t\ln^{2}(t)}dt<(k-1)!\int_{2}^{n}\frac{dt}{\ln^{k+1}(t)},\forall n\geq n_{k}

Although the same index was used for inequalities (
5.6
) and (
5.7
). This still will not yield the desired form for inequality (
5.7
). Want this to be of the same form as the source integral term in the expansion for
li(n;x)
. It’s very nearly there, just off by a factor of
k
. To fix this, only need to multiply through by
k
. Resulting in an appropriate inequality with the right form, given as:

(5.8)

\int_{2}^{n}\frac{|t-\vartheta(t)|}{t\ln^{2}(t)}dt<(k-1)!\int_{2}^{n}\frac{dt}{\ln^{k+1}(t)}<k!\int_{2}^{n}\frac{dt}{\ln^{k+1}(t)},\forall n\geq n_{k}

Now, given equation (
5.5
), split
li(n)
via equation (
5.1
), then apply inequalities (
5.6
) and (
5.8
); dropping the constant
-2/\ln(2)
term, and using the triangle inequality, finally rearranging to obtain:

(5.9)

\begin{split}|li(n;\bar{g}_{\pi})-\pi(n)|&=\bigg|\frac{n-\vartheta(n)}{\ln(n)}-\frac{2}{\ln(2)}+li(2)+\int_{2}^{n}\frac{t-\vartheta(t)}{t\ln^{2}(t)}dt-li_{R}(n;\bar{g}_{\pi})\bigg|\\
&<\frac{|n-\vartheta(n)|}{\ln(n)}+\bigg|li(2)+\int_{2}^{n}\frac{|t-\vartheta(t)|}{t\ln^{2}(t)}dt-li_{R}(n;\bar{g}_{\pi})\bigg|\\
&<\frac{n}{\ln(n)}\frac{(k-1)!}{\ln^{k-1}(n)}+\bigg|li(2)+k!\int_{2}^{n}\frac{dt}{\ln^{k+1}(t)}-li_{R}(n;\bar{g}_{\pi})\bigg|\end{split}

Next step requires the index
k
to be able to take real values. Nothing truly forces it to be integral. The only time it needs to be integral in Dusart
[
5

is when it’s used to index the

prime, but real values still make sense if the index is appropriately rounded. In fact the asymptotic expansion for the

prime, and others, appear in the source paper. All view

as real-valued.

Now that

can take real values, set all instances of
k
above to the average prime truncation function

. Then by construction, from equations (

) and (

) used in the proof of Theorem

) the RHS term in absolute value is zero.
Giving the final inequality:

(5.10)

|li(n;\bar{g}_{\pi})-\pi(n)|<\dfrac{n}{\ln(n)}\dfrac{(\bar{g}_{\pi}-1)!}{\ln(n)^{\bar{g}_{\pi}-1}},\forall n\geq n_{\bar{g}_{\pi}}

Where
n_{\bar{g}_{\pi}}
also depends on which form of
\bar{g}_{\pi}

\forall n\geq 9

∎

Remark 5.2
.

Choosing
\eta_{k}=(k-1)!
so freely is not an issue, since normally the name of the game is to make
\eta_{k}
as small as possible. But, in this case it’s essentially the opposite and making
\eta_{k}
larger doesn’t violate the inequality. Also, other choices can work, e.g.
\eta_{k}=(k-2)!
; giving a less tight bound; while the original
\eta_{k}=k!
choice has some issues with determining where the bound holds, among others.

Independent of the RH, a bound on the zeta-zeros summation can now be expressed in the following theorem. Basically another corollary of Theorem
5.1
.

Theorem 5.3
.

(Truncation Bound)

(5.11)

\bigg|\sum_{\rho}^{zeta-zeros}li(n^{\rho})\bigg|<\dfrac{n}{\ln(n)}\dfrac{(\bar{g}_{\pi}-1)!}{\ln^{\bar{g}_{\pi}-1}(n)}-0.553137,\ \forall n\geq n_{\bar{g}_{\pi}}

Proof.

Applying Theorem
5.1
to the RHS of equation (
3.7
):

(5.12)

\bigg|-\sum_{\rho}^{zeta-zeros}li(n^{\rho})+\int_{n}^{\infty}\frac{dt}{(t^{2}-1)t\ln(t)}-\ln(2)\bigg|<\dfrac{n}{\ln(n)}\dfrac{(\bar{g}_{\pi}-1)!}{\ln^{\bar{g}_{\pi}-1}(n)}

The integral on the LHS is a decreasing function of
n
, its maximum occurring for
n=2
is
0.140010
. Then subtracting the constant term
\ln(2)
gives
0.140010-0.693147\approx-0.553137
; which will very quickly approach
-\ln(2)
as
n
increases. Combine these into a single constant.
Since both remaining terms in absolute value are negative, can factor out and eliminate
-1
. Using the triangle inequality to obtain:

(5.13)

\bigg|\sum_{\rho}^{zeta-zeros}li(n^{\rho})+0.553137\bigg|\leq\bigg|\sum_{\rho}^{zeta-zeros}li(n^{\rho})\bigg|+0.553137<\dfrac{n}{\ln(n)}\dfrac{(\bar{g}_{\pi}-1)!}{\ln^{\bar{g}_{\pi}-1}(n)}

Therefore, upon rearranging the constant term, the proof is complete.

∎

Next, will use this bound to find a corresponding bound for the summation over the non-trivial zeta-zeros. Using equation (

\begin{split}|\pi(n)-li(n)|&=\bigg|-\sum_{r=2}^{\lfloor\log_{2}(n)\rfloor}\frac{\pi(n^{1/r})}{r}-\sum_{\rho}^{zeta-zeros}li(n^{\rho})+\int_{n}^{\infty}\frac{dt}{(t^{2}-1)t\ln(t)}-\ln(2)\bigg|\\
&\approx\bigg|-\sum_{r=2}^{\lfloor\log_{2}(n)\rfloor}\frac{\pi(n^{1/r})}{r}-\sum_{\rho}^{zeta-zeros}li(n^{\rho})-0.553137\bigg|\\
&\geq\bigg|\sum_{\rho}^{zeta-zeros}li(n^{\rho})\bigg|-\sum_{r=2}^{\lfloor\log_{2}(n)\rfloor}\frac{\pi(n^{1/r})}{r}-0.553137\end{split}

As the negative constant term always dominates the positive integral term, can assume essentially have just three terms, all negative, in the first line. Seen when moving to the equivalence in the next line. Combining this with

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\pi(n)=li(n)+O(\sqrt{n}\ln(n))

5.3
is less than or equal to the bound in

5.4
,
5.5
, and
5.6

Theorem 5.8
.

The Riemann Hypothesis is True.

Proof.

Given that the exact and average prime truncations of the asymptotic expansion for the logarithmic function exist; by Theorems
2.4
and
3.2
. With their stated properties, the average truncation must provide a means to analyze the variations of the exact truncation; which are mostly due to the oscillatory behavior induced by the non-trivial zeta-zeros. Realized by using Riemann’s explicit equation (
3.1
) for the prime count function.
By Theorems
4.1
and
4.3
there exists analytic solutions to the average truncation. Allowing for a new explicit bound, Theorem
5.3
, on the zeta-zero summation term in equation (
3.1
), that does not assume anything about the location of those zeros. Again other than being complex-valued and occurring in complex conjugate pairs, to imbue the summation with real-valued sinusoidal properties.

Now, given that the Schoenfeld bound, when recast in terms of the zeta-zeros summation
|\sum_{\rho}^{zeta-zeros}li(n^{\rho})|
,
Corollary 1B [Schoenfeld]
, which does assume a condition on the location of the non-trivial zeros; i.e. that the RH is true. This bound is equivalent to the RH being true.

Next, considering the computational investigation shown above and Theorems
5.4
,
5.5
, and
5.6
, have that the Theorem
5.3
Truncation Bound is less than the
Corollary 1B [Schoenfeld]
bound, for the given range, depending on which form of
\bar{g}_{\pi}(n)
is used in the Truncation Bound. Thus, showing that a sufficiently tight bound is able to be constructed without assuming the RH;
\forall n\geq 6,064
in the best case.

Since an equivalent to the Schoenfeld bound is beaten by the various Truncation Bounds, all without assuming the Riemann Hypothesis, then the RH must be true. Therefore, all non-trivial zeta-zeros
\rho
are on the critical line
\Re(\rho)=1/2
.

∎

6.
Conclusion

6.1.

As stated at the beginning of this paper, the goal was to answer a question about the asymptotic expansion of the logarithmic integral and explore its consequences. Leading to a verification that the Riemann Hypothesis is correct. This paper proceeded in the same way as the events of the discovery unfolded.

At first it seemed a truncated version of
li(n)
would just provide a cheap computational short cut for a better approximation to
\pi(n)
, somewhat similar to Riemann’s
R(n)
. Which it does. Instead of calculating multiple instances of the
li(n)
function with different inputs and combining them via
\mu(r)
to compute
R(n)
; now just have to calculate about a fifth of the expansion for
li(n)
and no
\mu(r)
.

But, the application of a truncation function to describe the variation induced by the zeta function’s non-trivial zeros turned out to be far more important. Essentially the main result follows from equating the weighted prime powers over-count to the “tail” or remainder of the logarithmic integral’s asymptotic expansion, and canceling them out. Which is equivalent, via Theorem
3.2
, to the idea of the average prime truncation function. Additionally, gain a much tighter bound, Theorem
5.3
, on the variation induced by the summation over the zeta-zeros than that provided by Schoenfeld’s equivalent bound on the same term.

Now having seen something of the average prime truncation function’s nature in relation to that of the prime truncation giving
\pi(n)
. It makes sense that it can bound the variation induced by the zeta functions non-trivial zeros so well. Effectively making the newly introduced asymptotic expansion variable
x
, akin to a type of logarithmic-like transform leading to an auxiliary function.

With a possible exception to this log-like transform. No new mathematics has really been introduced, which does make one feel like there is something not quite right. As with some problems, especially this one, it’s generally not only the expectation, but seemingly a necessity that new mathematics must arise to obtain a solution. With that in mind, the work here might best be characterized as using old mathematics by way of a new vantage point.

6.2.
Future Work

Finding a better form of the average
\bar{g}_{\pi}(n)

\bar{g}_{\pi}(n)

V
on the integral term, giving a better approximation to
g_{\pi}(n)
. Adding the integral and constant terms of Riemann’s explicit formula to the sum of prime powers constraint would be a small improvement. Further improving the competition between
li(n;\bar{g}_{\pi}(n)
and
R(n)
