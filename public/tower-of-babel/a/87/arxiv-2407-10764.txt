Generalization Bounds for Contextual Stochastic Optimization using Kernel Regression
Yijie Wang Grani A. Hanasusanto Chin Pang Ho

Abstract
In this paper, we consider contextual stochastic optimization using Nadaraya-Watson kernel regression, which is one of the most common approaches in nonparametric regression. Recent studies have explored the asymptotic convergence behavior of using Nadaraya-Watson kernel regression in contextual stochastic optimization; however, the performance guarantee under finite samples remains an open question. This paper derives a finite-sample generalization bound of the Nadaraya-Watson estimator with a spherical kernel under a generic loss function. Based on the generalization bound, we further establish a suboptimality bound for the solution of the Nadaraya-Watson approximation problem relative to the optimal solution. Finally, we derive the optimal kernel bandwidth and provide a sample complexity analysis of the Nadaraya-Watson approximation problem. Keywords: contextual stochastic optimization; Nadaraya-Watson estimator; finite-sample guarantee

Introduction

In the presence of uncertainty, decision makers can leverage contextual information such as weather conditions and macroeconomic indicators to refine the decision-making process
[
13
]
. In this paper, we consider a general stochastic optimization framework that incorporates contextual information into the decision-making process

\min_{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{X}}\;\left\{{\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}}[\ell(\boldsymbol{x},\tilde{\boldsymbol{\xi}})]\coloneqq{\mathbb{E}}[\ell(\boldsymbol{x},\tilde{\boldsymbol{\xi}})\,|\,\tilde{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}=\boldsymbol{\gamma}]\right\},

(1)

where the vector
\boldsymbol{x}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
represents the decision variables,
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{q}
denotes the uncertain parameters, and
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{p}
denotes contextual covariates. While the uncertain parameters
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\xi}}
and contextual covariates
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}
are both random, the decision-maker can observe the realization of
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}
before the decision-making stage. Hence, to exploit this side information, he solves a stochastic optimization problem with the conditional expectation of the random cost
\ell(\boldsymbol{x},\tilde{\boldsymbol{\xi}})
as an objective function, given the realization
\boldsymbol{\gamma}

Unfortunately, problem (
1

(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\xi}},\tilde{\boldsymbol{\gamma}})

1

[
11
,
17

\displaystyle\hat{\mathbb{E}}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}[\ell(\boldsymbol{x},\tilde{\boldsymbol{\xi}})]=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathcal{K}\left(\frac{\boldsymbol{\gamma}-\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{i}}{h}\right)\ell(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\xi}^{i})}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathcal{K}\left(\frac{\boldsymbol{\gamma}-\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{i}}{h}\right)},

(2)

where
\mathcal{K}
is a prescribed kernel function and
h>0
is the bandwidth parameter. In this paper, we consider the spherical kernel
[
16
]

\mathcal{K}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\mathbb{I}(\lVert\boldsymbol{\theta}\rVert\leq h).

(3)

Using the estimator (
2
), we arrive at the following Nadaraya-Watson approximation problem
[
7
]
to the contextual stochastic optimization problem (
1

\min_{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{X}}\;{\hat{\mathbb{E}}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}}[\ell(\boldsymbol{x},\tilde{\boldsymbol{\xi}})].

\mathcal{NW}

1.1
Related Work

The statistical properties and convergence behavior of Nadaraya-Watson estimations have been widely studied in recent years. In the nonparametric statistics literature, existing results on the Nadaraya-Watson estimator (when the decision is fixed) primarily focus on analyzing the mean square errors and the probabilistic convergence, but they lack finite-sample analysis
[
2
,
3
,
6
,
16
]
.

In the contextual stochastic optimization settings, Bertsimas and Kallus
[
4
]
show that the Nadaraya-Watson estimator converges to the true conditional expectation as the sample size grows, meaning that the approximation problem is asymptotically consistent. They further derive a performance guarantee by assuming
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\xi}}
or
\boldsymbol{x}
to be linear in
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}
. Bertsimas and Van Parys
[
5
]
derive an alternative bound for the bootstrap data that is obtained through resampling from the empirical distribution. For the specific single-item newsvendor problem, Ban and Rudin
[
1
]
employ the Nadaraya-Watson approximation with linear decision rule and derive a finite-sample performance guarantee for the solution. Unfortunately, the bound relies on the decision rule approximation and holds only for the specific one-dimensional newsvendor problem.
For generic problem settings and loss functions, Srivastava et al.
[
15
]
establish a generalization bound by leveraging techniques from large and moderate deviations theory. However, the derived generalization bound is asymptotic. Hence, the finite-sample behavior of the Nadaraya-Watson approximation problem remains an open question.

Finally, we remark that finite-sample bounds have been established for the
sample-average approximation
scheme in traditional
un
conditional stochastic optimization
[
10
,
14
]
. A recent work
[
8
]
derives generalization bounds in a non-data-driven setting where conditional samples of
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\xi}}
for any given
\boldsymbol{\gamma}
can be obtained. Our present work is motivated by the lack of results in
data-driven
contextual stochastic optimization settings, where we utilize kernel regression as a “conditional sample-average approximation.”

Finite Sample Generalization Bounds

In this section, we first derive generalization bounds on the approximation (

⁢
N
W

) for a fixed decision
\boldsymbol{x}
. We assume the following mild regularity conditions:

(
A1
)

The feasible set
\mathcal{X}
is bounded.

(
A2
)

The marginal density function
f(\boldsymbol{\gamma})
exists and is uniformly bounded from below by
\underline{f}
such that
0<\underline{f}\leq f(\boldsymbol{\gamma})
for all
\boldsymbol{\gamma}
in its support.

(
A3
)

The loss function
\ell(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\xi})
is
L_{x}
-Lipschitz continuous in
\boldsymbol{x}
takes value in the interval
[0,1]
. In addition, the conditional expectation
\mathbb{E}[\ell(\boldsymbol{x},\tilde{\boldsymbol{\xi}})\lvert\tilde{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}=\boldsymbol{\gamma}]
is
L_{\gamma}
-Lipschitz continuous in
\boldsymbol{\gamma}
.

The assumption about the feasible set in
(A1)
is typical in the literature
[
8
,
14
,
13
]
.
The assumption about the marginal density function in
(A2)
is merely to simplify the analysis, as it allows us to analytically bound the probability of
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\in\mathcal{B}_{r}
with a simple function of
r
, for any 2-norm ball
\mathcal{B}_{r}
with radius
r
.
The Lipschitz continuity assumptions in
(A3)
are standard mild regularity conditions to establish performance guarantees of kernel regression schemes
[
16
]
. The assumption that the loss function takes values in
[0,1]
follows immediately through scaling and translation if the loss function is bounded.
This boundedness assumption on the loss function is usual in practical contextual stochastic optimization problems, especially when the feasible set of the decision variable
\mathcal{X}
is bounded.
It is important to note that the convexity of the loss function is not required for our analysis.

Our main result establishes the suboptimality bound for the solution
\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}
to the Nadaraya-Watson approximation problem (

⁢
N
W

Theorem 1
.

Let
D=\sup_{\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}}\|\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}\|
be the diameter of
\mathcal{X}
. Under the bandwidth scaling

h(n)=\left(\frac{2L_{\gamma}^{2}nc\underline{f}}{p^{2}\log\left(\frac{2\mathcal{O}(1)(D/\tau)^{d}}{\delta}\right)}\right)^{\frac{-1}{p+2}}

the following suboptimality bound for the solution
\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}
of (

⁢
N
W

) holds with probability at least
1-\delta
:

\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}[\ell(\hat{\boldsymbol{x}},\tilde{\boldsymbol{\xi}})]\leq{\mathbb{E}}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}[\ell(\boldsymbol{x}^{\star},\tilde{\boldsymbol{\xi}})]+2L_{\gamma}^{\frac{p}{p+2}}\frac{p+2}{(4p^{p})^{\frac{1}{p+2}}}\left(\frac{2\log\left(\frac{2\mathcal{O}(1)(D/\tau)^{d}}{\delta}\right)}{nc\underline{f}}\right)^{\frac{1}{p+2}}+4L_{x}\tau.

Here,
\tau>0
is a free parameter that can be adjusted according to the desired approximation quality.

Before providing the proof of the above theorem, we first introduce its practical insights into the sample complexity of the Nadaraya-Watson problem.

Corollary 1
.

To obtain a suboptimality gap of
\epsilon
with probability at least
1-\delta
, it is sufficient that

n\geq\frac{2^{2p+3}L_{\gamma}^{p}(p+2)^{p+2}\log\left(\frac{2\mathcal{O}(1)(8DL_{x}/\epsilon)^{d}}{\delta}\right)}{p^{p}\epsilon^{p+2}c\underline{f}}.

Proof of Corollary
1
.

To obtain a suboptimality gap of
\epsilon
, we let
4L_{x}\tau=\epsilon/2
by setting
\tau=\frac{\epsilon}{8L_{x}}
, and we impose

\frac{\epsilon}{2}\geq 2L_{\gamma}^{\frac{p}{p+2}}\frac{p+2}{(4p^{p})^{\frac{1}{p+2}}}\left(\frac{2\log\left(\frac{2\mathcal{O}(1)(D/\tau)^{d}}{\delta}\right)}{nc\underline{f}}\right)^{\frac{1}{p+2}}.

Solving for the lower bound on
n
yields a sufficient number of samples. This completes the proof.
∎

The sample complexity of the Nadaraya-Watson approximation is
\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(1/\epsilon^{p+2})
, which grows exponentially in the dimension of the covariates. This suggests that our purely data-driven setting, where we do not impose stringent assumptions on the joint distribution of
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}
and
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\xi}}
, suffers from the
curse of dimensionality
. A more favorable sample complexity is achievable by using the traditional sample-average approximation in a non-data-driven setting, where conditional samples of
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\xi}}
can be collected for any given
\boldsymbol{\gamma}

[
8
]
. Alternatively, if
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\xi}}
can be decomposed into a sum of linear regression function in
\boldsymbol{\gamma}
and an independent random noise, then an improved sample complexity can be obtained using the
residuals-based sample-average approximation

[
9
]
.

The proof of Theorem
1
relies on the following result that establishes a high confidence bound on the errors of the Nadaraya-Watson estimate for any fixed
\boldsymbol{x}
. To our knowledge, the finite-sample generalization bound for kernel regression has not been derived in the literature on nonparametric statistics. Given that the Nadaraya-Watson estimator is biased, the proof of the proposition first decomposes the error into bias and mean-absolute deviation (MAD) terms, and then derives bounds for each term separately.

Proposition 1

(Finite-Sample Generalization Bound)
.

Consider the following Nadaraya-Watson estimator

\hat{\mathbb{E}}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}[\ell(\boldsymbol{x},\tilde{\boldsymbol{\xi}})]=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\ell(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\xi}^{i})\frac{\mathbb{I}(\lVert\boldsymbol{\gamma}-\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{i}\rVert\leq h)}{\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mathbb{I}(\lVert\boldsymbol{\gamma}-\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{j}\rVert\leq h)}&\displaystyle\textup{if}\;\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mathbb{I}(\lVert\boldsymbol{\gamma}-\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{j}\rVert\leq h)>0\\
0&\textup{otherwise.}\end{array}\right.

For any fixed
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{X}
,
\epsilon\in[0,1]
and
n\in\mathbb{Z}_{++}
, we have

\left|\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}[\ell(\boldsymbol{x},\tilde{\boldsymbol{\xi}})]-\hat{\mathbb{E}}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}[\ell(\boldsymbol{x},\tilde{\boldsymbol{\xi}})]\right|\leq L_{\gamma}h+\epsilon

(4)

with probability at least
1-2\exp\left(-nc\underline{f}h^{p}\epsilon^{2}/2\right)
, where
c=\frac{\pi^{p/2}}{\Gamma(p/2+1)}
is a constant that depends only on
p
.

Proof of
Proposition

1
.

We first define

\tilde{m}(\boldsymbol{\gamma})=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}[\ell(\boldsymbol{x},\tilde{\boldsymbol{\xi}})\lvert\tilde{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}=\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{i}]\frac{\mathbb{I}(\lVert\boldsymbol{\gamma}-\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{i}\rVert\leq h)}{\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mathbb{I}(\lVert\boldsymbol{\gamma}-\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{j}\rVert\leq h)}&\displaystyle\textup{if}\;\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mathbb{I}(\lVert\boldsymbol{\gamma}-\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{j}\rVert\leq h)>0,\\
0&\textup{otherwise.}\end{array}\right.

Then, by triangle inequality, we have

\displaystyle\left\lvert\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}[\ell(\boldsymbol{x},\tilde{\boldsymbol{\xi}})]-\hat{\mathbb{E}}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}[\ell(\boldsymbol{x},\tilde{\boldsymbol{\xi}})]\right\rvert\leq\underbrace{\lvert\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}[\ell(\boldsymbol{x},\tilde{\boldsymbol{\xi}})]-\tilde{m}(\boldsymbol{\gamma})\rvert}_{(a)}+\underbrace{\lvert\tilde{m}(\boldsymbol{\gamma})-\hat{\mathbb{E}}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}[\ell(\boldsymbol{x},\tilde{\boldsymbol{\xi}})]\rvert}_{(b)}.

(5)

Conditioning on the covariate samples
\{\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
, we observe that

\tilde{m}(\boldsymbol{\gamma})=\mathbb{E}\left[\hat{\mathbb{E}}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}[\ell(\boldsymbol{x},\tilde{\boldsymbol{\xi}})]\Big\lvert\{\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}\right].

Thus, the upper bound in (
5

\displaystyle=\bigg\lvert\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}[\ell(\boldsymbol{x},\tilde{\boldsymbol{\xi}})]-\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{i}}[\ell(\boldsymbol{x},\tilde{\boldsymbol{\xi}})]\right)\frac{\mathbb{I}(\lVert\boldsymbol{\gamma}-\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{i}\rVert\leq h)}{\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mathbb{I}(\lVert\boldsymbol{\gamma}-\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{j}\rVert\leq h)}\bigg\rvert+\mathbb{I}(\mathcal{E})

where the first inequality follows from the fact that

. In addition, we further have

\mathbb{P}(\tilde{\mathcal{E}})=\mathbb{P}({\displaystyle\cap}_{i=1}^{n}\{\lVert\boldsymbol{\gamma}-\tilde{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{i}\lVert>h\})=(1-p_{h})^{n},

(6)

where
p_{h}=\mathbb{P}(\lVert\boldsymbol{\gamma}-\tilde{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{i}\lVert\leq h)

Hence, we have

\displaystyle\lvert\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}[\ell(\boldsymbol{x},\tilde{\boldsymbol{\xi}})]-\tilde{m}(\boldsymbol{\gamma})\rvert\leq L_{\gamma}h

1-(1-p_{h})^{n}

Next, we obtain an upper bound on the term (b):

\displaystyle\displaystyle\lvert\tilde{m}(\boldsymbol{\gamma})-\hat{\mathbb{E}}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}[\ell(\boldsymbol{x},\tilde{\boldsymbol{\xi}})]\rvert=

\displaystyle\lvert\tilde{m}(\boldsymbol{\gamma})-\hat{\mathbb{E}}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}[\ell(\boldsymbol{x},\tilde{\boldsymbol{\xi}})]\rvert\cdot\mathbb{I}(\mathcal{E}^{c})+\lvert\tilde{m}(\boldsymbol{\gamma})-\hat{\mathbb{E}}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}[\ell(\boldsymbol{x},\tilde{\boldsymbol{\xi}})]\rvert\cdot\mathbb{I}(\mathcal{E})

\displaystyle\left\lvert\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\ell(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\xi}^{i})-\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{i}}[\ell(\boldsymbol{x},\tilde{\boldsymbol{\xi}})]\right)\frac{\mathbb{I}(\lVert\boldsymbol{\gamma}-\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{i}\rVert\leq h)}{\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mathbb{I}(\lVert\boldsymbol{\gamma}-\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{j}\rVert\leq h)}\right)\cdot\mathbb{I}(\mathcal{E}^{c})\right\rvert

From above, by the application of Hoeffding’s inequality, we get

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\lvert\tilde{m}(\boldsymbol{\gamma})-\hat{\mathbb{E}}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}[\ell(\boldsymbol{x},\tilde{\boldsymbol{\xi}})]\rvert>\epsilon\Big\lvert\{\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{i}\}_{i=1}^{n},\mathcal{E}^{c}\right)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\lvert\tilde{m}(\boldsymbol{\gamma})-\hat{\mathbb{E}}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}[\ell(\boldsymbol{x},\tilde{\boldsymbol{\xi}})]\rvert>\epsilon\right)

\displaystyle\leq 2\cdot\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(-2\epsilon^{2}\cdot\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mathbb{I}(\lVert\boldsymbol{\gamma}-\tilde{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{j}\rVert\leq h)\right)\mathbb{I}(\mathcal{E}^{c})\right]

(7)

\displaystyle=2\cdot\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(-2\epsilon^{2}\cdot\tilde{Z}\right)\mathbb{I}(\tilde{Z}>0)\right],

. The last equality holds because

Hence, we can compute the last expression in (

\displaystyle 2\cdot\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(-2\epsilon^{2}\cdot\tilde{Z}\right)\mathbb{I}(\tilde{Z}>0)\right]

\displaystyle=2\cdot\sum_{j=1}^{n}{n\choose j}(1-p_{h})^{n-j}p_{h}^{j}\exp\bigg(-2j\epsilon^{2}\bigg)

\displaystyle=2\cdot\sum_{j=1}^{n}{n\choose j}(1-p_{h})^{n-j}\left(p_{h}\exp\bigg(-2\epsilon^{2}\bigg)\right)^{j}

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\lvert\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}[\ell(\boldsymbol{x},\tilde{\boldsymbol{\xi}})]-\hat{\mathbb{E}}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}[\ell(\boldsymbol{x},\tilde{\boldsymbol{\xi}})]\rvert>Lh+\epsilon)

\displaystyle\;\lvert\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}[\ell(\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime},\tilde{\boldsymbol{\xi}})]-\hat{\mathbb{E}}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}[\ell(\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime},\tilde{\boldsymbol{\xi}})]\rvert\leq L_{\gamma}h+\epsilon\quad\forall\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}_{\tau}.

3
Concluding Remarks

We have presented the first finite-sample generalization bounds for contextual stochastic optimization via kernel regression. The derivation is based on a mean-MAD (mean absolute deviation) decomposition of the error, diverging from the traditional mean-variance decomposition commonly used in the statistics literature. Our findings offer practical guidance on selecting the optimal bandwidth parameter and determining the sufficient number of samples to achieve a desired suboptimality gap. Furthermore, our proof technique may find relevance in other contextual stochastic optimization settings that utilize a biased estimator of risk measures.

We remark that our result is conservative since we aimed for a simple analytical form comparable to existing bounds for sample-average approximation in stochastic programming. Improved results could be obtained by considering any valid intermediate bound in the proof.
As expected, due to the minimal assumptions made about the joint distribution, the sample complexity suffers from the curse of dimensionality in the covariates. Future research will focus on developing dimensionality reduction techniques and identifying specific distributional settings that enable improved sample complexity. Additionally, it will be interesting to extend the results to the multi-stage setting to address data-driven dynamic stochastic optimization problems.
