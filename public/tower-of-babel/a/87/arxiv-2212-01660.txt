Energy levels of graphene magnetic quantum dot in inhomogeneous gap
Fatima Belokda, Ahmed Jellal, El Houssine Atmani

Abstract
We investigate the energy levels of charge carriers confined in a magnetic quantum dot in graphene with an inhomogeneous gap through an electrical potential. We solve the eigenvalue equation for two regions. We explicitly determine the eigenspinors for both valleys K K , K ′ K^{\prime} and use the boundary condition at the quantum dot interface to obtain the energy levels. We show that the energy levels exhibit symmetric and asymmetric behavior under appropriate conditions of the physical parameters. It has been found that changing the energy levels by introducing an energy gap outside the quantum dot changes the electrical properties.

I
Introduction

Graphene is defined as a two-dimensional layer composed only of carbon atoms arranged in a honeycomb pattern
Novoslov1
.
The structure and the nature of the bonds between the atoms that compose it give graphene extraordinary and unique electronic properties
Novoselov2
;
Castro
.
The dispersion relation is linear such that the valence band and the conduction band touch at two points, K and K’, called Dirac points. At these points, the graphene electrons behave as massless Dirac fermions
Castro
;
Geim A
and are described by the Dirac equation
Castro
. In the framework of band theory, graphene is a zero-gap semiconductor. In general, the ultra-relativistic nature of graphene’s charge carriers has led researchers to question how they would respond to confinement
Gomes Peres 4
. It is precisely this particular property that prohibits the use of fabrication techniques. The question of confining Dirac fermions in graphene has led to many proposals. There are several theoretical methods for confining Dirac fermions in single-layer graphene
Rozhkov Giavaras
;
Chen
, such as inhomogeneous magnetic fields
Martino
;
Espinosa-Ortega19
, potential cylindrical symmetry
Chen
, spatial modulation of the Dirac gap
Giavaras Franco
, cutting the flake into small nanostructures
Zebrowski Wach
;
Thomson M R
, using the substrate to induce a band gap
Recher P
and so on.

Graphene quantum dots (QDs) are small disk-like pieces of graphene with a radius of
r_{0}
in which the electronic wave function is confined and exhibits quantum confinement effects, regardless of size. Therefore, graphene QDs have a non-zero band gap and are luminescent by excitation. This band gap is tunable by changing the size of the graphene QDs. Since its discovery, scientists have been trying to trap electrons in graphene-based QDs in anticipation of the wide field of novel applications of QDs in electronic devices
C. Berger
, valves
Espinosa-Ortega19
, photovoltaics
Bacon M
, qubits
Trauzettel B
, and gas detection
Sun H
. QDs made from nanostructures are very specific to the exact shape of the edge, which is difficult to control
Espinosa-Ortega19
.

Motivated by our previous work
Belokda
, we study the influence of two inhomogenous gaps on the energy spectrum of the graphene QDs. For this, we consider the confinement of charge carriers in a magnetic graphene quantum dot with the gap
\Delta_{1}
inside and the gap
\Delta_{2}
outside the dot. To obtain the eigenspinors, we solve the Dirac equation separately in each region of the system. By using the boundary condition at the interface of the quantum dot, we obtain an equation describing the energy levels as a function of the physical parameters. We numerically study the energy levels as a function of the radius of QDs, the magnetic field, the electrostatic potential, and two gaps
\Delta_{1}
,
\Delta_{2}
.

This paper is organized as follows. In Sec.
II
, we set up the mathematical tools needed to study the present system. In Sec.
III
, we determine the eigenspinors that describe the fermions in our theoretical model. Using the boundary condition, we derive a formula governing the energy levels as a function of the physical parameters in Sec.
IV
. We numerically analyze the energy levels in Sec.
V
. Finally, we conclude our results.

Model and theory

Let us consider the magnetic confinement of Dirac fermions in graphene subjected to an electrostatic potential and two inhomogeneous gaps.
We create a quantum dot with radius
r_{0}
in the presence of two different gaps, one inside the other

\Delta(r)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\Delta_{1},&r>r_{0}\\
\Delta_{2},&r<r_{0}\end{array}\right.

(1)

using the magnetic field along
z
-direction shown below

\vec{B}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}B\vec{e}_{z},&r<r_{0}\\
0,&r>r_{0}.\end{array}\right.

(2)

Such a system can be illustrated, as shown in Fig.

.

The following Hamiltonian can be used to describe the dynamics of carriers in the honycomb lattice of covalently bound carbon atoms in a single graphene

H=v_{F}(\vec{p}+e\vec{A})\cdot\vec{\sigma}+V(r)\mathbb{I}_{2}+\Delta{(r)}\sigma_{z}

(3)

where the Fermi velocity is
v_{F}\approx 10^{6}
m/s,
\vec{p}=(p_{x},p_{y})
is the two-dimensional momentum operator,
\vec{\sigma}
denotes the Pauli matrices,
\mathbb{I}_{2}
is the
2

\times

2
identity matrix, and
V
is the applied potential. The vector potential can be calculated using (
2
) to get

\vec{A}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\dfrac{B}{2r}\left(r^{2}-R^{2}\right)\vec{e_{z}},&r<r_{0}\\
0,&r>r_{0}.\end{array}\right.

(4)

In polar coordinates
(r,\theta)
, the Hamiltonian (
3
) reduces to the form

H=\begin{pmatrix}V_{+}&\pi^{+}\\
\pi^{-}&V_{-}\end{pmatrix}

(5)

and the momentum operators are introduced

\pi^{\pm}=-i\hbar\nu_{F}e^{\pm i\tau\theta}\left(\partial_{r}\pm\dfrac{\tau i}{r}\partial_{\theta}\right)

(6)

where we have defined

V_{\pm}=V\pm\Delta_{1}\pm\Delta_{2}.

(7)

We can now calculate the energy spectrum using the eigenvalues equation,
H\psi^{\tau}(r,\theta)=E\psi^{\tau}(r,\theta)
. Noting that the Hamiltonian commutes with the total angular momentum
J_{z}=L_{z}+\frac{\hbar}{2}\sigma_{z}
, the radial and angular components of the eigenspinors
\psi^{\tau}(r,\theta)
can be separated. As a result, we have

\psi^{\tau}{(r,\theta)}=e^{im\theta}\begin{pmatrix}\Phi_{A}^{\tau}(r)\\
ie^{-i\tau\theta}\Phi_{B}^{\tau}(r)\end{pmatrix}

(8)

where the angular-momentum quantum number is
m=0,\pm 1,\pm 2,\cdots
.
\Phi_{A}^{\tau}
and
\Phi_{B}^{\tau}
are wave functions that represent sublattices
A
and
B
, respectively. The parameter
\tau
has two values,
\pm 1
, which distinguishes the two valleys
K
and
K^{\prime}
.

III
Eigenspinors

Using the mathematical tools established above, we can now get the solutions to the energy spectrum. Indeed, the equations for the region 1 (
r>r_{0}
) are obtained

\displaystyle\left(\partial_{\rho}+\frac{m\tau}{\rho}\right)\Phi^{\tau}_{A}(\rho)=-\left((\varepsilon-v)+\delta_{1}\right)\Phi^{\tau}_{B}(\rho)

(9)

\displaystyle\left(\partial_{\rho}-\frac{m-\tau}{\rho}\right)\Phi^{\tau}_{B}(\rho)=\left((\varepsilon-v)-\delta_{1}\right)\Phi^{\tau}_{A}(\rho)

(10)

where the variable change
\rho=\frac{r}{r_{0}}
and
dimensionless units
\varepsilon=\frac{E}{E_{0}}
,
v=\frac{V}{E_{0}}
,
\delta_{1}=\frac{\Delta_{1}}{E_{0}}
are considered, such that

E_{0}=\frac{\hbar\nu_{F}}{r_{0}}
.
By injecting (
9
) into (
10
), we get a second order differential equation for
\Phi^{\tau}_{A}(\rho)

\left(\rho^{2}\partial^{2}_{\rho}+\rho\partial_{\rho}-m^{2}+\alpha^{2}_{1}\rho^{2}\right)\Phi^{\tau}_{A}(\rho)=0

(11)

showing the Bessel function of the first kind, regular at the origin,
as a solution

\Phi_{A}(\rho)=C^{\tau}_{1}J_{m}(\alpha_{1}\rho)

(12)

where the parameter
\alpha_{1}
is given by

\alpha_{1}=\sqrt{|(\varepsilon-v)^{2}-\delta_{1}^{2}|}

(13)

and
C^{\tau}_{1}
is a normalization constant. The second component of spinor can be derived from (
9
) as

\Phi_{B}(\rho)=-iC^{\tau}_{1}e^{-i\tau\theta}J_{m-\tau}(\alpha_{1}\rho).

(14)

Finally in region 1, the eigenspinors take the form

\psi^{\tau}_{1}{(\rho,\theta)}=C^{\tau}_{1}e^{im\theta}\begin{pmatrix}J_{m}(\alpha_{1}\rho)\\
-ie^{-i\tau\theta}J_{m-\tau}(\alpha_{1}\rho)\end{pmatrix}.

(15)

Remember that there is a magnetic field
B
in region 2 (
r<r_{0}
), which causes the momentum operators
\pi^{\pm}
to take the following forms

\pi^{\pm}=-i\hbar\nu_{F}e^{\pm i\tau\theta}\left(\partial_{r}\pm\dfrac{\tau i}{r}\partial_{\theta}\mp\frac{\tau eBr}{2\hbar}\right).

(16)

Using
H\psi^{\tau}_{2}=E\psi^{\tau}_{2}
to get

\displaystyle\left(\partial_{\rho}+\frac{m\tau}{\rho}+\tau\beta\rho\right)\Phi^{\tau}_{A}(\rho)=-\left((\varepsilon-v)+\delta_{2}\right)\Phi^{\tau}_{B}(\rho)

(17)

\displaystyle\left(\partial_{\rho}-\frac{m-\tau}{\rho}-\tau\beta\rho\right)\Phi^{\tau}_{B}(\rho)=\left((\varepsilon-v)-\delta_{2}\right)\Phi^{\tau}_{A}(\rho)

(18)

where
\beta=\frac{eBr_{0}^{2}}{2\hbar}
and
\delta_{2}={\frac{\delta_{2}r_{0}}{\hbar\nu_{F}}}
have been set. After substitution of
(
17
) into (
18
), one obtains

\left(\rho^{2}\partial^{2}_{\rho}+\rho\partial_{\rho}-m^{2}-2\beta(m-\tau)\rho^{2}+\alpha^{2}_{2}\rho^{2}-\beta^{2}\rho^{4}\right)\Phi^{\tau}_{A}(\rho)=0

(19)

and we have involved

\displaystyle\alpha_{2}=\sqrt{|(\varepsilon-v)^{2}-\delta_{2}^{2}|}.

(20)

To solve (
19
), let us take the first component of spinor as

\Phi_{A}(\rho)=\rho^{|m|}e^{-\frac{\beta\rho^{2}}{2}}\varphi(\beta\rho^{2})

(21)

and define a new variable
\eta=\beta\rho^{2}

to end up with the confluent hepergeometric ordinary differential equation

\left(\eta{\partial^{2}_{\eta}}+(b-\eta){\partial_{\eta}}-a\right)\varphi(\eta)=0

(22)

with the quantities

\displaystyle a=-\frac{(\varepsilon-v)^{2}-\delta^{2}_{2}}{4\beta}+\frac{m-\tau+|m|+1}{2}

(23)

\displaystyle b=1+|m|.

(24)

As a result, we obtain the solution

\Phi_{A}(\rho)=\rho^{|m|}e^{-\frac{\beta\rho^{2}}{2}}C^{\tau}_{2}M\left(a,b,\beta\rho^{2}\right)

(25)

where
M(a,b,\beta\rho^{2})
is the confluent hypergeometric function and
C^{\tau}_{2}
is a normalization constant. Thus, the second component can be extracted from (
17
) as

\Phi_{B}(\rho)=i\frac{\rho^{|m|}e^{-\frac{\beta\rho^{2}}{2}}e^{-i\tau\theta}}{(\varepsilon-v)+\delta_{2}}\left[\left(\frac{\tau m}{\rho}+\beta\tau\rho\right)M\left(a,b,\beta\rho^{2}\right)-aM\left(a+1,b+1,\beta\rho^{2}\right)\right].

(26)

When we combine everything, we get the eigenspinors in region 2

\psi^{\tau}_{2}(\rho,\theta)=C^{\tau}_{2}\rho^{|m|}e^{-\frac{\beta\rho^{2}}{2}}e^{im\theta}\begin{pmatrix}M\left(a,b,\beta\rho^{2}\right)\\
\frac{ie^{-i\tau\theta}}{(\varepsilon-v)+\delta_{2}}\left[\left(\frac{\tau m}{\rho}+\beta\tau\rho\right)M\left(a,b,\beta\rho^{2}\right)-aM\left(a+1,b+1,\beta\rho^{2}\right)\right]\end{pmatrix}.

(27)

IV
Energy levels

We will proceed by using the boundary condition at the interface,
r=r_{0}
, which is equivalent to
\rho=1
, because obtaining the energy level explicitly for the current system is difficult. In fact, the operation

\displaystyle\psi^{\tau}_{1}(1,\theta)=\psi^{\tau}_{2}(1,\theta)

(28)

produces the relations

\displaystyle C^{\tau}_{1}J_{m}(\alpha_{1})=C^{\tau}_{2}e^{-}\frac{\beta}{2}M(a,b,\beta)

(29)

\displaystyle C^{\tau}_{1}J_{m-\tau}(\alpha_{1})=-C^{\tau}_{2}\frac{e^{-\frac{\beta}{2}}}{(\varepsilon-v)+\delta_{2}}\left[({\tau m}+\tau\beta)M(a,b,\beta)-aM(a+1,b+1,\beta)\right].

(30)

It is convenient to write this set of equations in the matrix from

M^{\tau}\begin{pmatrix}C^{\tau}_{1}\\
C^{\tau}_{2}\end{pmatrix}=0

(31)

where we have defined

M^{\tau}=\begin{pmatrix}J_{m}(\alpha_{1})&e^{-\frac{\beta}{2}}M(a,b,\beta)\\
J_{m-\tau}(\alpha_{1})&-\frac{e^{-\frac{\beta}{2}}}{(\varepsilon-v)+\delta_{2}}[({\tau m}+\tau\beta)M(a,b,\beta)-aM(a+1,b+1,\beta)]\end{pmatrix}.

(32)

By requiring a null determinant,
\det M_{\tau}=0
, we can now determine the equation governing the energy level. This process yields

-J_{m}(\alpha_{1})\frac{e^{-\frac{\beta}{2}}}{(\varepsilon-v)+\delta_{2}}[({\tau m}+\tau\beta)M(a,b,\beta)-aM(a+1,b+1,\beta)]-J_{m-\tau}(\alpha_{1})e^{-\frac{\beta}{2}}M(a,b,\beta)=0.

(33)

Since this involves several physical parameters, a numerical analysis is required to underline the basic features of the energy levels. In fact, we will discuss the results shown in various plots of energy under appropriate conditions.

VI
Conclusion

In a graphene quantum dot with an inhomogeneous gap in the electrostatic potential and a perpendicular magnetic field, we have investigated the dynamics of charge carriers. We solved the Dirac equation to obtain the eigenspinors inside and outside the quantum dot. We were able to derive an equation describing the energy levels in accordance with the physical parameters that define our system by using the continuity condition at the quantum dot’s interface. The obtained energy levels are rich, which pushed us to underline their basic features.

Our numerical results are shown by studying the energy spectrum.
Indeed, the study of the spectrum’s dependence on the QD’s radius
r_{0}
revealed that the energy spectrum maintained the degeneracy of the asymmetry
E(m,\tau)\neq E(m,-\tau)
when the limit
r_{0}\longrightarrow 0
is satisfied.
The energy levels become linear as
r_{0}
increases, representing the symmetry
E(m,\tau)=E(m,-\tau)
. Furthermore, we see new and different energy levels occupying the region
-\Delta_{1}<E{<}\Delta_{1}
, and the number of these levels increases as the gap
\Delta_{1}
outside the QD is increased.
In terms of the energy spectrum’s dependence on the magnetic field
B
, we have seen that the spectra for
\pm\Delta_{1}<E<\pm\Delta_{2}
are linear even when
B
is close to zero. We observed the appearing energy levels as
\Delta_{1}
was increased, with the exception of the angular momentum
m\neq
0 and they satisfy the symmetry
E(m,\tau)=E(-m,{-\tau)}
This means that the transport properties of charge carriers at the center of the graphene magnetic QD are affected by
\Delta_{1}
.
Furthermore, the electrostatic potential
V
influenced the energy level to shift vertically from
V
. Finally, we have seen that the introduction of
\Delta_{1}
in each spectrum representation has broken the symmetry and resulted in new energy levels of the value energy
-\Delta_{1}<E<\Delta_{1}
.
