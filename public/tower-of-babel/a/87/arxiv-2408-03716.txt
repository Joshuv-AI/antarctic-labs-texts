Mechanics of poking a cyst
Shiheng Zhao, Pierre A. Haas

Abstract
Indentation tests are classical tools to determine material properties. For biological samples such as cysts of cells, however, the observed force-displacement relation cannot be expected to follow predictions for simple materials. Here, by solving the Pogorelov problem of a point force indenting an elastic shell for a purely nonlinear material, we discover that complex material behaviour can even give rise to new scaling exponents in this force-displacement relation. In finite-element simulations, we show that these exponents are surprisingly robust, persisting even for thick shells indented with a sphere. By scaling arguments, we generalise our results to pressurised and pre-stressed shells, uncovering additional new scaling exponents. We find these predicted scaling exponents in the force-displacement relation observed in cyst indentation experiments. Our results thus form the basis for inferring the mechanisms that set the mechanical properties of these biological materials.

Here, we therefore start by solving the nonlinear Pogorelov problem: A point force indents a purely nonlinearly elastic shell. Our asymptotic calculations for thin shells reveal new scaling exponents. In finite-element simulations, we verify these results and show that, strikingly, they also apply in the experimentally relevant setting of a thick shell indented with a sphere. We then develop scaling arguments that extend these results to pressurised and pre-stressed shells and predict additional new scaling exponents. We thus classify all possible diagrams of scaling exponents. We conclude by reanalysing experimental data on cyst indentation experiments, in which we discover signatures of these scaling exponents.

We conclude by reanalysing experimental data on cyst indentation experiments
[
11
]
in the light of our results. The observed force-displacement relation [Fig.

(a)
] reveals a scaling exponent
\beta
increasing from
1/2
to
2
as the indentation depth is increased [Fig.

]. Nonetheless, observing the exponent

that we have predicted above emphasises the importance of pre-stress for the mechanical behaviour of the cyst. Experiments at deeper indentation would be required to determine whether the observed exponent
\beta=2
, also seen in other recent experiments
[

, would indeed increase to the predicted exponent
\beta=3
. Nevertheless, observing strain-stiffening exponents
\beta>1
in this way signals a contribution of nonlinear elasticity to the mechanics of the cyst.

In summary, we have combined analytical calculations, scaling arguments, and finite-element simulations to predict new scaling exponents in the force-displacement relation of an elastic shell from nonlinear elasticity and pre-stress. We have found signatures of these exponents in cyst indentation experiments. Our results therefore form the basis for interpreting the mechanical behaviour of cysts and other biological materials, although further experimental work will be needed to test the scalings and transitions between scalings regimes predicted here. Future work, both experimental and theoretical, will also need to explore further the effect on these scaling exponents of viscoelasticity
[
8
,
9
]
or superelasticity
[
43
,
9
]
for slow indentation on timescales comparable to those over which the cortex rearranges.

Meanwhile, deriving these scaling exponents more microscopically, for example from coarse-grained vertex models of cysts
[
10
]
, remains an important theoretical challenge. More generally, the purely nonlinear elasticity introduced here adds to the burgeoning field that seeks new hyperelastic energy densities to describe soft biological materials
[
44
,
45
]
, opening up a host of mechanical questions: For example, how does the buckling of a shell under external pressure
[
46
,
47
,
48
,
42
]
change for such a “supersoft” material?

for all deformations, where
C_{20},C_{11},C_{02}
are material parameters, and
\mathcal{I}_{1},\mathcal{I}_{2}
are the principal invariants of the left Cauchy-Green tensor. To analyse this condition, we first express
\mathcal{I}_{1},\mathcal{I}_{2}
in terms of the principal stretches
\Lambda_{1},\Lambda_{2},\Lambda_{3}
. By incompressibility, assumed here,
\Lambda_{3}=1/\Lambda_{1}\Lambda_{2}
, so

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{1}=\Lambda_{1}^{2}+\Lambda_{2}^{2}+\frac{1}{\Lambda_{1}^{2}\Lambda_{2}^{2}},

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{2}=\Lambda_{1}^{2}\Lambda_{2}^{2}+\frac{1}{\Lambda_{1}^{2}}+\frac{1}{\Lambda_{2}^{2}},

(S2)

From the inequality of arithmetic and geometric means, we find

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{1}

\displaystyle\geqslant 3\sqrt[3]{\Lambda_{1}^{2}\Lambda_{2}^{2}\frac{1}{\Lambda_{1}^{2}\Lambda_{2}^{2}}}=3,

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{2}

\displaystyle\geqslant 3\sqrt[3]{\Lambda_{1}^{2}\Lambda_{2}^{2}\frac{1}{\Lambda_{1}^{2}}\frac{1}{\Lambda_{2}^{2}}}=3,

(S3)

with equality if and only if
\Lambda_{1}=\Lambda_{2}=1
. This proves that the ratio
R=(\mathcal{I}_{2}-3)/(\mathcal{I}_{1}-3)
is a positive, continuous function of
\Lambda_{1},\Lambda_{2}>0
, with a removable singularity at
\Lambda_{1}=\Lambda_{2}=1
. If
\Lambda_{1}=\Lambda_{2}=\Lambda\gg 1
, then
R\sim\Lambda^{2}/2\gg 1
. If
\Lambda_{1}=\Lambda^{-1/2}
and
\Lambda_{2}=\Lambda\gg 1
, then
R\sim 2/\Lambda\ll 1
. By continuity, this shows that
R
attains all positive real values for
\Lambda_{1},\Lambda_{2}>0
. This means that

\dfrac{W}{(\mathcal{I}_{1}-3)^{2}}=C_{02}R^{2}+C_{11}R+C_{20}\geqslant 0\quad\text{for all }R>0.

(S4)

If
C_{02}=0
, it is clear that condition
(
S4
)
holds if and only if
C_{11}\geqslant 0
and
C_{20}\geqslant 0
. If
C_{02}\neq 0
, considering the limits
R\gg 1
and
R\ll 1
respectively, it is clear that it holds only if
C_{02}\geqslant 0
and
C_{20}\geqslant 0
. Conversely, if
C_{20}\geqslant 0
and
C_{02}>0
, then the quadratic in
R
on the left-hand side of the inequality is non-negative for sufficiently small and sufficiently large
R>0
, so condition
(
S4
)
breaks down if and only if it has two positive real roots. The product of its roots is
C_{20}/C_{02}>0
, so if the roots are real, which is if and only if
\smash{C_{11}^{2}-4C_{20}C_{02}}\geqslant 0
, they are both positive if and only if their sum
-C_{11}/C_{02}
is positive, which is now if and only if
C_{11}<0
. Hence, if
C_{02}\neq 0
, condition
(
S4
)
holds if and only if
C_{02}>0
and
C_{20}\geqslant 0
and
\smash{C_{11}^{2}<4C_{20}C_{02}}
or
C_{11}>0
. Combining all of these conditions shows that the purely nonlinear strain energy function
(
S1
)
is non-negative if and only if

C_{20}\geqslant 0\quad\text{and}\quad C_{02}\geqslant 0\quad\text{and}\quad C_{11}\geqslant-2\sqrt{C_{20}C_{02}}.

(S5)

II.1
Calculation of the elastic deformation gradient

To derive the deformation gradient tensor that maps the initial (undeformed) configuration
\mathcal{V}
of the shell to its current deformed configuration
\tilde{\mathcal{V}}
, we review the geometry and kinematics of the axisymmetric shell.

We describe the undeformed configuration
\mathcal{V}
of the shell with reference to its midsurface
\mathcal{S}
. With respect to the basis
\{\boldsymbol{u_{r}},\boldsymbol{u_{\phi}},\boldsymbol{u_{z}}\}
of cylindrical coordinates, the position vector of a point on
\mathcal{S}
is

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\rho}(s,\phi)=r(s)\boldsymbol{u_{r}}(\phi)+z(s)\boldsymbol{u_{z}},

(S6)

where
s
is arclength along the curve generating the axisymmetric midsurface, and
\phi
is the azimuthal coordinate, so that
r(s)
and
z(s)
are the radial and vertical positions of a point on the midsurface [Fig.
S1
(a)
]. The tangent angle
\psi(s)
of
\mathcal{S}
is defined by

\displaystyle r^{\prime}(s)=\cos{\psi(s)},\quad z^{\prime}(s)=\sin{\psi(s)},

(S7)

in which dashes denote differentiation with respect to
s
. Thus

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{e_{s}}(s,\phi)=\cos{\psi(s)}\boldsymbol{u_{r}}(\phi)+\sin{\psi(s)}\boldsymbol{u_{z}},\quad\boldsymbol{e_{\phi}}(\phi)=\boldsymbol{u_{\phi}}(\phi)

(S8)

provide a basis of the tangent space of
\mathcal{S}
, from which we obtain a right-handed orthonormal basis
{\mathcal{B}=\{\boldsymbol{e_{s}},\boldsymbol{e_{\phi}},\boldsymbol{n}\}}
for
\mathcal{V}
by adjoining the normal to
\mathcal{S}
,

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{n}(s,\phi)=\cos{\psi(s)}\boldsymbol{u_{z}}-\sin{\psi(s)}\boldsymbol{u_{r}}(\phi).

(S9)

The curvatures of
\mathcal{S}
are found to be

\displaystyle\varkappa_{s}(s)=\psi^{\prime}(s),

\displaystyle\varkappa_{\phi}(s)=\dfrac{\sin{\psi(s)}}{r(s)}.

(S10)

This completes the description of the midsurface
\mathcal{S}
. The position of a point in
\mathcal{V}
is now

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{r}(s,\phi,\zeta)=\boldsymbol{\rho}(s,\phi)+\varepsilon\zeta\boldsymbol{n}(s,\phi),

(S11)

where
\zeta
is the transverse coordinate such that the midsurface is at
\zeta=0
and that, by the definition, the shell surfaces are at
\zeta=\pm h/2
. Since
\partial\boldsymbol{n}/\partial s=-\varkappa_{s}\boldsymbol{e_{s}}
and
\partial\boldsymbol{n}/\partial\phi=-\varkappa_{\phi}\boldsymbol{e_{\phi}}
, the tangent basis of
\mathcal{V}
is

\displaystyle\dfrac{\partial\boldsymbol{r}}{\partial s}=(1-\varepsilon\varkappa_{s}\zeta)\boldsymbol{e_{s}},\quad\dfrac{\partial\boldsymbol{r}}{\partial\phi}=r(1-\varepsilon\varkappa_{\phi}\zeta)\boldsymbol{e_{\phi}},\quad\dfrac{\partial\boldsymbol{r}}{\partial\zeta}=\varepsilon\boldsymbol{n}.

(S12)

The scale factors of the undeformed configuration are therefore

\displaystyle\chi_{s}=1-\varepsilon\varkappa_{s}\zeta,

\displaystyle\chi_{\phi}=r(1-\varepsilon\varkappa_{\phi}\zeta),

\displaystyle\chi_{\zeta}=\varepsilon.

(S13a)

and the volume element of the undeformed shell is

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}V=\chi_{s}\chi_{\phi}\chi_{\zeta}\,\mathrm{d}s\,\mathrm{d}\phi\,\mathrm{d}\zeta=\varepsilon(1-\varepsilon\varkappa_{s}\zeta)(1-\varepsilon\varkappa_{\phi}\zeta)\,r\,\mathrm{d}s\,\mathrm{d}\phi\,\mathrm{d}\zeta.

(S13b)

As the shell deforms into its deformed configuration
\tilde{\mathcal{V}}
, the undeformed midsurface
\mathcal{S}
maps to the deformed midsurface
\tilde{\mathcal{S}}
, which has position vector

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\tilde{\rho}}(s,\phi)=\tilde{r}(s)\boldsymbol{u_{r}}(\phi)+\tilde{z}(s)\boldsymbol{u_{z}},

(S14)

defining the positions
\tilde{r}(s),\tilde{z}(s)
of a point on
\tilde{\mathcal{S}}
[Fig.
S1
(c)
]. Let
\tilde{s}
denote the deformed arclength. The stretches of the deformed shell are thus

\displaystyle f_{s}(s)=\dfrac{\mathrm{d}\tilde{s}}{\mathrm{d}s},

\displaystyle f_{\phi}(s)=\dfrac{\tilde{r}(s)}{r(s)},

(S15)

and so the tangent angle
\tilde{\psi}(s)
of
\tilde{\mathcal{S}}
is defined by

\displaystyle\tilde{r}^{\prime}(s)=f_{s}\cos{\tilde{\psi}(s)},\quad\tilde{z}^{\prime}(s)=f_{s}\sin{\tilde{\psi}(s)}.

(S16)

As for the undeformed configuration, we introduce the tangent vectors

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\tilde{e}_{s}}(s,\phi)=\cos{\tilde{\psi}(s)}\boldsymbol{u_{r}}(\phi)+\sin{\tilde{\psi}(s)}\boldsymbol{u_{z}},\quad\boldsymbol{\tilde{e}_{\phi}}(\phi)=\boldsymbol{u_{\phi}}(\phi),

(S17)

and the normal vector

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\tilde{n}}(s,\phi)=\cos{\tilde{\psi}(s)}\boldsymbol{u_{z}}-\sin{\tilde{\psi}(s)}\boldsymbol{u_{r}}(\phi),

(S18)

They define a right-handed orthonormal basis
{\tilde{\mathcal{B}}=\{\boldsymbol{\tilde{e}_{s}},\boldsymbol{\tilde{e}_{\phi}},\boldsymbol{\tilde{n}}\}}
of
\tilde{\mathcal{V}}
. The curvatures of the deformed shell are

\displaystyle\kappa_{s}(s)=\dfrac{\tilde{\psi}^{\prime}(s)}{f_{s}(s)},

\displaystyle\kappa_{\phi}(s)=\dfrac{\sin{\tilde{\psi}(s)}}{\tilde{r}(s)}.

(S19)

Under the mapping from
\mathcal{V}
to
\tilde{\mathcal{V}}
, normals to
\mathcal{S}
will not in general map to normals to
\tilde{\mathcal{S}}
. We will therefore assume that a point in
\mathcal{V}
at a distance
\varepsilon\zeta
from
\mathcal{S}
is mapped to a point at a distance
\varepsilon\tilde{\zeta}
from and displaced by a distance
\varepsilon\tilde{\varsigma}
parallel to
\tilde{\mathcal{S}}
[Fig.
S1
(d)
]. The definition of the midsurfaces implies that
\tilde{\zeta}=\tilde{\varsigma}=0
if
\zeta=0
. A point in
\tilde{\mathcal{V}}
thus has position vector

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\tilde{r}}(s,\phi,\zeta)=\boldsymbol{\tilde{\rho}}(s,\phi)+\varepsilon\tilde{\zeta}(s,\zeta)\boldsymbol{\tilde{n}}(s,\phi)+\varepsilon\tilde{\varsigma}(s,\zeta)\boldsymbol{\tilde{e}_{s}}(s,\phi).

(S20)

We now compute

\displaystyle\dfrac{\partial\boldsymbol{\tilde{r}}}{\partial s}

\displaystyle=\left[\tilde{f}_{s}\left(1-\varepsilon\tilde{\kappa}_{s}\tilde{\zeta}\right)+\varepsilon\tilde{\varsigma}_{,s}\right]\boldsymbol{\tilde{e}_{s}}+\varepsilon\left(\tilde{\zeta}_{,s}+\tilde{f}_{s}\tilde{\kappa}_{s}\tilde{\varsigma}\right)\boldsymbol{\tilde{n}},

(S21a)

\displaystyle\dfrac{\partial\boldsymbol{\tilde{r}}}{\partial\phi}

\displaystyle=\left[\tilde{r}\left(1-\varepsilon\tilde{\kappa}_{\phi}\tilde{\zeta}\right)+\varepsilon\tilde{\varsigma}\cos{\tilde{\psi}}\right]\boldsymbol{\tilde{e}_{\phi}},

(S21b)

\displaystyle\dfrac{\partial\boldsymbol{\tilde{r}}}{\partial\zeta}

\displaystyle=\varepsilon\left(\tilde{\zeta}_{,\zeta}\boldsymbol{\tilde{n}}+\tilde{\varsigma}_{,\zeta}\boldsymbol{\tilde{e}_{s}}\right).

(S21c)

By definition
[
3
]
, the deformation gradient tensor is

\displaystyle\mathbfsfit{F}=\operatorname{Grad}{\boldsymbol{\tilde{r}}}=\dfrac{1}{\chi_{s}^{2}}\dfrac{\partial\boldsymbol{\tilde{r}}}{\partial s}\otimes\dfrac{\partial\boldsymbol{r}}{\partial s}+\dfrac{1}{\chi_{\phi}^{2}}\dfrac{\partial\boldsymbol{\tilde{r}}}{\partial\phi}\otimes\dfrac{\partial\boldsymbol{r}}{\partial\phi}+\dfrac{1}{\chi_{\zeta}^{2}}\dfrac{\partial\boldsymbol{\tilde{r}}}{\partial\zeta}\otimes\dfrac{\partial\boldsymbol{r}}{\partial\zeta},

(S22)

and we thus obtain, with respect to
\tilde{\mathcal{B}}\otimes\mathcal{B}
,

\displaystyle\mathbfsfit{F}

\displaystyle=\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}\dfrac{f_{s}\left(1\!-\!\varepsilon\kappa_{s}\tilde{\zeta}\right)\!+\!\varepsilon\tilde{\varsigma}_{,s}}{1-\varepsilon\varkappa_{s}\zeta}&0&\tilde{\varsigma}_{,\zeta}\\
0&\dfrac{f_{\phi}\left(1\!-\!\varepsilon\kappa_{\phi}\tilde{\zeta}\right)\!+\!\dfrac{\varepsilon\tilde{\varsigma}}{r}\cos{\tilde{\psi}}}{1-\varepsilon\varkappa_{\phi}\zeta}&0\\
\dfrac{\varepsilon\left(\tilde{\zeta}_{,s}+f_{s}\kappa_{s}\tilde{\varsigma}\right)}{1-\varepsilon\varkappa_{s}\zeta}&0&\tilde{\zeta}_{,\zeta}\end{array}\right).

II.2
Elastic energy and scaling assumptions

In this subsection, we derive the effective elastic energy for a “supersoft” shell by asymptotic expansion of three-dimensional elasticity. Let
\mathbfsfit{C}=\mathbfsfit{F}^{\top}\mathbfsfit{F}
denote the left Cauchy–Green tensor, and let
\mathcal{I}_{1}=\tr{\mathbfsfit{C}}
and
\mathcal{I}_{2}=\bigl(\mathcal{I}_{1}^{2}-\tr{\mathbfsfit{C}^{2}}\bigr)/2
be its invariants
[
3
,
4
]
. The elastic energy is

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}=\iiint_{\mathcal{V}}{W\,\mathrm{d}V},

(S26a)

where
W
is the purely nonlinear energy density

\displaystyle W=C_{20}(\mathcal{I}_{1}-3)^{2}+C_{11}(\mathcal{I}_{1}-3)(\mathcal{I}_{2}-3)+C_{02}(\mathcal{I}_{2}-3)^{2}.

(S26b)

In Eq.
(
S26a
)
, the integration is over the initial configuration
\mathcal{V}
of the shell, and
\mathrm{d}V
is the volume element given by Eq.
(
S13b
)
.

From its general expression
[
3
]
, the Cauchy stress tensor associated with this energy density is seen to be

\displaystyle\mathbfsfit{T}

\displaystyle=2\bigl[(\mathcal{I}_{1}-3)(2C_{\text{20}}+C_{\text{11}}\mathcal{I}_{1})+(\mathcal{I}_{2}-3)(C_{\text{11}}+2C_{\text{02}}\mathcal{I}_{1})\bigr]\mathbfsfit{B}

\displaystyle\quad-2\bigl[C_{\text{11}}(\mathcal{I}_{1}-3)+2C_{\text{02}}(\mathcal{I}_{2}-3)\bigr]\mathbfsfit{B}^{2}-p\mathbfsfit{I},

(S27)

in which
\mathbfsfit{I}
is the identity,
\mathbfsfit{B}=\mathbfsfit{F}\mathbfsfit{F}^{\top}
is the right Cauchy–Green tensor, and the pressure
p
is the Lagrange multiplier that imposes the condition
\det{\mathbfsfit{F}}=1
of incompressibility. The associated first Piola–Kirchhoff stress tensor
\mathbfsfit{P}=\mathbfsfit{T}\mathbfsfit{F}^{-\top}
satisfies the equilibrium equation
[
4
]

\displaystyle\text{Div}~\mathbfsfit{P}^{\top}=\boldsymbol{0},

(S28a)

where the divergence is with respect to the undeformed configuration. As pointed out in Ref.
[
2
]
, since
\mathcal{B},\tilde{\mathcal{B}}
are independent of
\zeta
, the equilibrium equation can separated into components perpendicular and parallel to the midsurface, viz.,

\displaystyle\frac{(\mathbfsfit{P}\boldsymbol{n})_{,\zeta}}{\varepsilon}+\boldsymbol{\nabla}\cdot\mathbfsfit{P}^{\top}=\boldsymbol{0},

(S28b)

where
\boldsymbol{\nabla}
denotes the gradient on
\mathcal{S}
. The force-free boundary conditions on the surfaces of the deformed shell are
\mathbfsfit{T}^{\boldsymbol{\pm}}\boldsymbol{\tilde{n}^{\pm}}=\boldsymbol{0}
, where
\boldsymbol{\tilde{n}^{\pm}}
are the normals to
\tilde{\zeta}=\tilde{h}^{\pm}
[Fig.
S1
(d)
], and
\mathbfsfit{T}^{\boldsymbol{\pm}}
are the values of the Cauchy stress there. These conditions are well known to be equivalent to
\mathbfsfit{P}^{\boldsymbol{\pm}}\boldsymbol{n^{\pm}}=\boldsymbol{0}

[
4
]
, where, since the undeformed shell is assumed to have constant thickness
h
,
\boldsymbol{n^{\pm}}=\boldsymbol{n}
are the normals to
\zeta=\pm h/2
, and
\mathbfsfit{P}^{\boldsymbol{\pm}}
are the values of the first Piola–Kirchhoff stress there.

Scaling assumptions

To derive the shell theory, we need to expand the elastic energy
(
S26a
)
in the asymptotic limit
\varepsilon\ll 1
. For this purpose, we need to make scaling assumptions for the stretches and curvatures of the deformed shell,

\displaystyle f_{s}=1+\varepsilon E_{s}+O\bigl(\varepsilon^{2}\bigr),\quad f_{\phi}=1+\varepsilon E_{\phi}+O\bigl(\varepsilon^{2}\bigr),

(S29)

and

\displaystyle\kappa_{s}=\varkappa_{s}+K_{s}+O(\varepsilon),\quad\kappa_{\phi}=\varkappa_{\phi}+K_{\phi}+O(\varepsilon).

(S30)

These relations define the shell strains
E_{s},E_{\phi}
and the curvature strain
K_{s},K_{\phi}
.

II.3
Asymptotic expansion

To obtain an asymptotic expansion of the elastic energy subject to the scalings assumptions above, we posit regular expansions

\displaystyle\tilde{\zeta}=\tilde{\zeta}_{(0)}+\varepsilon\tilde{\zeta}_{(1)}+O\bigl(\varepsilon^{2}\bigr),\quad\varsigma=\varsigma_{(0)}+O(\varepsilon),

(S31)

for the transverse and parallel displacements. We further expand

\displaystyle\mathbfsfit{P}

\displaystyle=\mathbfsfit{P}_{(0)}+\varepsilon\mathbfsfit{P}_{(1)}+\varepsilon^{2}\mathbfsfit{P}_{(2)}+\varepsilon^{3}\mathbfsfit{P}_{(3)}+O\bigl(\varepsilon^{4}\bigr),

(S32a)

\displaystyle p

\displaystyle=p_{(0)}+p_{(1)}\varepsilon+p_{(2)}\varepsilon^{2}+O(\varepsilon^{3}).

(S32b)

Asymptotic expansion of the incompressibility condition

We begin by expanding the incompressibility condition to leading-order using Eq.
(
II.1
)
to get

\displaystyle 1=\det{\mathbfsfit{F}}=\tilde{\zeta}_{(0),\zeta}+O(\varepsilon),

(S33)

Integrating this result using
\tilde{\zeta}_{(0)}=0
at
\zeta=0
, as required by the definition of the midsurface, yields
\smash{\tilde{\zeta}_{(0)}}\equiv\zeta
. We now expand the incompressibility condition further, finding

\displaystyle 0=\det{\mathbfsfit{F}}-1=\varepsilon\left(E_{s}+E_{\phi}-\zeta(K_{\phi}+K_{s})+\dfrac{\partial\tilde{\zeta}_{(1)}}{\partial\zeta}\right)+O\bigl(\varepsilon^{2}\bigr).

(S34)

This provides a differential equation for
\tilde{\zeta}_{(1)}
, the solution of which that satisfies
\tilde{\zeta}_{(1)}=0
at
\zeta=0
is

\displaystyle\tilde{\zeta}_{(1)}=\dfrac{K_{s}+K_{\phi}}{2}\zeta^{2}-(E_{s}+E_{\phi})\zeta.

(S35)

Asymptotic expansion of the constitutive relations

It turns out that we do not need to expand the deformation gradient explicitly beyond order
O(\varepsilon)
. Rather, it suffices to consider a formal expansion

\displaystyle\mathbfsfit{F}=\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}1+\varepsilon a_{(1)}+\varepsilon^{2}a_{(2)}+\varepsilon^{3}a_{(3)}+O\bigl(\varepsilon^{4}\bigr)&0&\varepsilon v_{(1)}+\varepsilon^{2}v_{(2)}+\varepsilon^{3}v_{(3)}+O\bigl(\varepsilon^{4}\bigr)\\
0&1+\varepsilon b_{(1)}+\varepsilon^{2}b_{(2)}+\varepsilon^{3}b_{(3)}+O\bigl(\varepsilon^{4}\bigr)&0\\
\varepsilon w_{(1)}+\varepsilon^{2}w_{(2)}+\varepsilon^{3}w_{(3)}+O\bigl(\varepsilon^{4}\bigr)&0&1+\varepsilon c_{(1)}+\varepsilon^{2}c_{(2)}+\varepsilon^{3}c_{(3)}+O\bigl(\varepsilon^{4}\bigr)\end{array}\right),

where, by comparison with Eq.
(
II.1
)
and using the previous results,

\displaystyle a_{(1)}=E_{s}-\zeta K_{s},\quad b_{(1)}=E_{\phi}-\zeta K_{\phi}.

(S39)

Expressions for
a_{(2)},a_{(3)},b_{(2)},b_{(3)},c_{(1)},c_{(2)},c_{(3)},v_{(1)},v_{(2)},w_{(1)},w_{(2)}
could of course also be obtained from Eq.
(
II.1
)
in terms of the expansions
(
S31
)
, but will not be needed. The incompressibility condition is now

\displaystyle 1=\det{\mathbfsfit{F}}

\displaystyle=1+\varepsilon\left(a_{(1)}+b_{(1)}+c_{(1)}\right)+\varepsilon^{2}\left(a_{(2)}+b_{(2)}+c_{(2)}+a_{(1)}b_{(1)}+b_{(1)}c_{(1)}+c_{(1)}a_{(1)}-v_{(1)}w_{(1)}\right)+O\bigl(\varepsilon^{3}\bigr).

(S40)

This yields, successively,

\displaystyle c_{(1)}

\displaystyle=-a_{(1)}-b_{(1)},

\displaystyle c_{(2)}

\displaystyle=a_{(1)}^{2}+a_{(1)}b_{(1)}+b_{(1)}^{2}-a_{(2)}-b_{(2)}+v_{(1)}w_{(1)}.

(S41)

Now, at leading order, Eq.
(
S28b
)
yields
(\mathbfsfit{P}_{(0)}\boldsymbol{n})_{,\zeta}=\boldsymbol{0}
, which shows that
\mathbfsfit{P}_{(0)}\boldsymbol{n}
is independent of
\zeta
. The boundary conditions require
\boldsymbol{0}=\mathbfsfit{P}^{\pm}\boldsymbol{n}=\mathbfsfit{P}_{(0)}\boldsymbol{n}+O(\varepsilon)
. This shows that
\mathbfsfit{P}_{(0)}\boldsymbol{n}\equiv\boldsymbol{0}
. Direct calculation using
Mathematica
(Wolfram, Inc.) reveals that this implies that
p_{(0)}=0
, and hence
\mathbfsfit{P}_{(0)}\equiv\mathbfsf{0}
. The equilibrium condition
(
S28b
)
now yields
(\mathbfsfit{P}_{(1)}\boldsymbol{n})_{,\zeta}=0
, and a similar argument leads to
p_{(1)}=0
and
\mathbfsfit{P}_{(1)}\equiv\mathbfsf{0}
. In a similar manner, we obtain
\mathbfsfit{P}_{(2)}\boldsymbol{n}\equiv\boldsymbol{0}
, whence, by direct calculation,

\displaystyle p_{(2)}=2(4C_{\text{02}}+3C_{\text{11}}+2C_{\text{20}})\bigl[4a_{(1)}^{2}+4a_{(1)}b_{(1)}+4b_{(1)}^{2}+(v_{(1)}+w_{(1)})^{2}\bigr],

(S42)

and thus
\mathbfsfit{P}_{(2)}\equiv\mathbfsf{0}
. In turn and in the now familiar fashion, we then obtain
\mathbfsfit{P}_{(3)}\boldsymbol{n}\equiv\boldsymbol{0}
. Now

0\\
O(1)\end{array}\right).

It is clear that the factor in square brackets only vanishes if all the terms in it vanish; in particular, its vanishing requires
v_{(1)}+w_{(1)}=0
. If the factor in square brackets does not vanish, then
\mathbfsfit{P}_{(3)}\boldsymbol{n}\equiv\boldsymbol{0}
still requires

, assuming that the nonlinear shear modulus

does not vanish (a possibility that we are not interested in). Hence

\displaystyle w_{(1)}=-v_{(1)}

(S46)

in either case. On computing the expansion of

from Eq.

II.1
)
and hence those of
\mathcal{I}_{1}=\tr{\mathbfsfit{C}}
and

, and simplifying using Eqs.
(

)
and

)
, we then obtain

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{1}

\displaystyle=3+\varepsilon\bigl[2\bigl(a_{(1)}+b_{(1)}+c_{(1)}\bigr)\bigr]+\varepsilon^{2}\left[a_{\smash{(1)}}^{2}+b_{\smash{(1)}}^{2}+c_{\smash{(1)}}^{2}+v_{\smash{(1)}}^{2}+w_{\smash{(1)}}^{2}+2\left(a_{(2)}+b_{(2)}+c_{(2)}\right)\right]+O\bigl(\varepsilon^{3}\bigr)

\displaystyle=3+\varepsilon^{2}\bigl[4\bigl(a_{\smash{(1)}}^{2}+a_{(1)}b_{(1)}+b_{\smash{(1)}}^{2}\bigr)\bigr]+O\bigl(\varepsilon^{3}\bigr)=\mathcal{I}_{2}+O\bigl(\varepsilon^{3}\bigr).

(S47a)

Hence, from Eqs.
(

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{1}-3

\displaystyle=4\varepsilon^{2}\left[(E_{s}-\zeta K_{s})(E_{\phi}-\zeta K_{\phi})+(E_{s}-\zeta K_{s})^{2}+(E_{\phi}-\zeta K_{\phi})^{2}\right]+O\bigl(\varepsilon^{3}\bigr)=\mathcal{I}_{2}-3.

(S47b)

This determines the leading-order term in the asymptotic expansion of the energy density in Eq.

)
, and thus allows us to derive the shell energy.

II.4
Derivation of the shell energy

Substituting Eq.
(
S47b
)
into Eq.

III
Calculation of the prefactor of the force-displacement relation

Just as Pogorelov extended the arguments that determine the scaling of the force with the indentation to a calculation of the corresponding prefactor
[
10
]
, we can extend the scaling arguments of the main text to calculate the prefactors. Following Pogorelov’s approach
[
10
]
, we describe the deformed shape of the shell by its displacements
u(s)
and
v(s)
, in the radial and axial directions of cylindrical polar coordinates respectively, from the isometric dimple shape [Fig.
S2
(a)
], where
s
is arclength along the isometric shape, with
s=0
corresponding to its ridge.

For a thin shell, the elastic energy is concentrated in the ridge of the dimple, where Pogorelov estimated
[
10
]

\displaystyle E_{\phi}\approx\dfrac{u}{\rho}\gg E_{s},

\displaystyle K_{s}\approx v^{\prime\prime}\gg K_{\phi},

(S51)

in which
\rho
is the radius of the dimple [Fig.
S2
(a)
]. The elastic energy of a “supersoft” dimpled shell is therefore, from Eqs.
(
S48
)
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}

\displaystyle\approx G_{2}\iint_{\mathcal{R}}{\left[16h\left(\dfrac{u}{\rho}\right)^{4}+4h^{3}\left(\dfrac{u}{\rho}\right)^{2}v^{\prime\prime 2}+\dfrac{h^{5}}{5}v^{\prime\prime 4}\right]\mathrm{d}S},

(S52)

where the integration region
\mathcal{R}
is the ridge of the dimple,
-s_{0}\leqslant s\leqslant s_{0}
, and the surface element is
\mathrm{d}S\approx 2\uppi\rho\,\mathrm{d}s
. Across the ridge, the tangent angle to the shell changes from
-\alpha
to
\alpha
, with
\rho=R\sin{\alpha}\approx R\alpha
. We introduce scaled variables

\displaystyle s

\displaystyle=\xi\overline{s},

\displaystyle u

\displaystyle=\xi\alpha^{2}\overline{u},

\displaystyle\dfrac{\mathrm{d}v}{\mathrm{d}s}=\alpha\overline{w}\;\Rightarrow\;\dfrac{\mathrm{d}^{2}v}{\mathrm{d}s^{2}}=\frac{\alpha}{\xi}\frac{\mathrm{d}\overline{w}}{\mathrm{d}\overline{s}},

(S53)

where we choose

\displaystyle\xi\equiv\frac{\alpha^{-1/2}h^{1/2}\rho^{1/2}}{80^{1/8}}.

(S54)

The energy of the shell becomes

\mathcal{E}=2a\int_{0}^{\overline{s}_{0}}{\left(\overline{u}^{4}+\overline{w}^{\prime 4}+\sqrt{5}\overline{u}^{2}\overline{w}^{\prime 2}\right)\mathrm{d}\overline{s}},

(S55)

where
\overline{s}_{0}=s_{0}/\xi
is the dimensionless arclength of the ridge and where

a=\frac{32\uppi G_{2}}{80^{5/8}}h^{7/2}\rho^{-1/2}\alpha^{11/2}\approx\frac{32\uppi G_{2}}{80^{5/8}}h^{7/2}e^{5/2}R^{-3},

(S56)

in terms of the indentation
{e=2(R-R\cos{\alpha})\approx R\alpha^{2}}
of the dimple, using
\alpha\approx(e/R)^{1/2}
,
\rho\approx(eR)^{1/2}
.

We start by defining the conditions for scaling transitions. Let
