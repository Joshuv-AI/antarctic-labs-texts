Quantum simulation of time-dependent Hamiltonians via commutator-free quasi-Magnus operators
Pablo A. M. Casares, Modjtaba Shokrian Zini, Juan Miguel Arrazola

Abstract
Hamiltonian simulation is arguably the most fundamental application of quantum computers. The Magnus operator is a popular method for time-dependent Hamiltonian simulation in computational mathematics, yet its usage requires the implementation of exponentials of commutators, which has previously made it unappealing for quantum computing. The development of commutator-free quasi-Magnus operators (CFQMs) circumvents this obstacle, at the expense of a lack of provable global numeric error bounds. In this work, we establish one such error bound for CFQM-based time-dependent quantum Hamiltonian simulation by carefully estimating the error of each step involved in their definition. This allows us to compare its cost with the alternatives, and show that CFQMs are often the most efficient product-formula technique available by more than an order of magnitude. As a result, we find that CFQMs may be particularly useful to simulate time-dependent Hamiltonians on early fault-tolerant quantum computers.

Introduction

One of the most fundamental applications of quantum computing is simulating the time evolution of a quantum system. However, the time-dependent version has received comparatively less attention, even though it plays a key role in important problems including quantum control
Nielsen et al. 2006
;
Pang and Jordan 2017
and the interaction of electronic systems with external fields
Alvermann et al. 2012
;
Auzinger et al. 2020
;
Auzinger et al. 2022
. It is also a core component of real-time electronic structure methods
Goings et al. 2018
;
Li et al. 2020
.

Quantum Hamiltonian simulation algorithms can be broadly grouped into two families of techniques: Linear Combination of Unitaries (LCU)
Childs and Wiebe 2012
and product formulas
Childs et al. 2021
. There have been proposals in each of these families for the simulation of time-dependent Hamiltonians. Notably, the Dyson series in the LCU category provides an efficient, and in some regimes state-of-the-art, interaction-picture technique for this purpose
Low and Wiebe 2018
;
Kieferová et al. 2019
;
Babbush et al. 2019
;
Su et al. 2021
. In the product formula category, prior arts include the Suzuki and continuous qDRIFT methods
Wiebe et al. 2010
;
Berry et al. 2020
.

Here, we study yet another approach, the Magnus operator
Magnus 1954
, which is an alternative to the explicit time-ordering of operators indicated by the Dyson series and Suzuki methods
Magnus 1954
;
Blanes et al. 2009
, and enjoys popularity in the field of quantum control
Ribeiro et al. 2017
;
Shapira et al. 2020
;
Le et al. 2022
. Recently, the Magnus operator has been adapted to its use under the LCU paradigm
An et al. 2022
;
Fang et al. 2024
. However, it has received less attention in its product formula form as a quantum algorithm, perhaps due to the need to implement exponentials of commutators
Childs and Wiebe 2013
;
Chen et al. 2022
;
Bosse et al. 2024
;
Casas and Escorihuela-Tomàs 2024
. Fortunately, the development over the past two decades of commutator-free quasi-Magnus operators (CFQMs) bypasses this necessity
Blanes and Moan 2006
;
Alvermann and Fehske 2011
;
Blanes et al. 2017
;
Blanes et al. 2009
. CFQMs are commutator-free product formulas that approximate the Magnus operator evolution to a desired order.

A key advantage of most quantum algorithms for electronic Hamiltonian simulation is their ability to efficiently achieve a desired target error
Childs and Su 2019
. In contrast, classical algorithms implementing Magnus and quasi-Magnus operators suffer from an error that is less tightly controlled because they often need to approximate the computation of each exponential, relying for instance on Krylov subspace or Lanczos methods
Bader et al. 2018
;
Iserles et al. 2018
; though there are exceptions
Celledoni and Iserles 2000
.

Conversely, classical algorithms have the advantage of being able to efficiently read, prepare, or copy any state in memory. As such, the known methods that bound the algorithmic error are to the best of our knowledge ‘a posteriori’ or adaptative, that is, they leverage knowledge of an approximate solution to compute the error incurred at each step
Wensch et al. 2004
;
Kormann et al. 2008
;
Kormann et al. 2011
;
Auzinger et al. 2019b
;
Auzinger et al. 2019a
;
Auzinger et al. 2021
. Unfortunately, this strategy is much less amenable to quantum computing, where preparing and reading out states is costly and requires recomputing the state preparation and time evolution up to the point of interest
Ikeda et al. 2024
;
Zhao et al. 2024
. Additionally, the convergence of CFQMs has been studied previously, including for unbounded operators, and partial results have been reported
Blanes et al. 2018
;
Blanes et al. 2020
;
Ikeda et al. 2023
;
Chen et al. 2023
.
However, as far as we are aware, no explicit ‘a priori’ error bounds were derived for CFQMs solving the time-dependent Schrödinger equation. This left unanswered the question of the largest time step that would guarantee a desired error when using them in a quantum algorithm.

Here, we fill this gap by enabling the use of CFQMs in quantum computing with the usual guarantees. Specifically, we bound the error incurred by using CFQMs to simulate bounded time-dependent Hamiltonians, whose Taylor series coefficients can also be bounded.
We compute the error incurred at each approximation used in the derivation of CFQMs, employing carefully chosen expressions and exploiting symmetries along the way.
The result is a set of quantum algorithms with similar asymptotic complexity as the Suzuki method
Wiebe et al. 2010
, but better constant factor overhead for at least some classes of Hamiltonians, including time-dependent Heisenberg models, see
fig.

7
.

The paper is structured as follows. First, we present a succinct derivation of CFQMs, which is instrumental for our error bounds. Then, in
section

3
we analyze each of the approximations involved in their development and provide error bounds for each. In
section

4
, we compare CFQMs with previous works and provide numerical estimations for the simulation error of time-dependent Heisenberg Hamiltonians. We conclude with a summary of the results and a discussion on future works, as well as the potential of this technique in the early fault-tolerant regime. Details of our analysis can be found in the appendices.
appendix

gives a more detailed comparison of CFQMs performance against other time-dependent Hamiltonian simulation methods.
appendix

contains the rigorous proofs,
appendix

evaluates specific extensions avoiding usage of Trotter product formulas for Hamiltonians composed of two self-commuting and fast-forwardable terms, and lastly,
appendix

D
discusses how CFQMs may be used in combination with multi-product formulas
Low et al. 2019
.

2.2
Commutator-free quasi-Magnus operators

We harness the machinery introduced above to define commutator-free operators that approximate the Magnus operator to the desired order. One may use the ansatz
Blanes and Moan 2006

\begin{split}\tilde{U}_{m}^{[2s]}(h)&:=\prod_{i=1}^{m}\exp\left(\sum_{g=0}^{s-1}y_{i,g}A^{(g)}(h)\right)=\exp(\tilde{\Omega}^{[2s]}(h))+O(h^{2s+1}),\end{split}

The formal analysis is simpler on the

\begin{split}U_{m}^{[2s]}(h)&:=\prod_{i=1}^{m}\exp\left(\sum_{j=1}^{s}x_{i,j}\alpha_{j}\right)=\exp(\Omega^{[2s]}(h))+O(h^{2s+1}).\end{split}

(16)

The parameters

represent the coefficients to be determined. We want to obtain a time-antisymmetric expression

Blanes and Moan 2006

As a consequence, for a given value of

\Theta(h^{p})
term in the Taylor expansion of
\exp(\Omega)
and
\tilde{U}_{m}^{[2s]}
, respectively. We will refer to them respectively as the Taylor series truncation error of the Magnus and quasi-Magnus operators.
Next, we present the main results and briefly comment on how to bound each error term.

3.1
Taylor remainder of the Magnus operator

The integer compositions
\mathcal{C}(p)
of
p\in\mathbb{N}
are a way of decomposing
p
into sums of positive integers, where order matters. For instance,
\mathcal{C}(3)=\{[3],[2,1],[1,2],[1,1,1]\}
, where
[2,1]
corresponds to
3=2+1
, and each element has a ‘dimension’ equal to its size, e.g.,
\dim([1,2])=2
. We denote by
\mathcal{C}^{z}(p)
the compositions of dimension
z
. Let us also uniformly bound the coefficients
a_{j}
of the Taylor series of
A(t)
by
c\geq\|a_{j}\|,\forall j
.

Proposition 1
.

Let
A(t)
be a bounded operator, with its Taylor coefficients bounded by
c
. Let
h
be the step size of the Magnus operator
\Omega
and

\exp(\Omega)_{p}
be the
\Theta(h^{p})
-term in the Taylor expansion of
\exp(\Omega)
. Then

\begin{split}&\sum_{p=2s+1}^{\infty}\|\exp(\Omega)_{p}\|\leq\sum_{p=2s+1}^{\infty}\left[\left(\frac{h}{2}\right)^{p}\sum_{\bm{k}\in\mathcal{C}(p)}\frac{1}{(\dim\bm{k})!}\prod_{l=1}^{\dim\bm{k}}\sum_{\bm{j}_{l}\in\mathcal{C}(k_{l})}\frac{(2c)^{\dim\bm{j}_{l}}}{\dim\bm{j}_{l}}\prod_{\ell_{l}=1}^{\dim\bm{j}_{l}}\frac{1}{j_{\ell_{l}}}\right].\end{split}

(23)

The proof can be found in
section

B.1
. The key step in bounding the norm of these Taylor expansion terms is to start from the expression of the Magnus operator given in Refs.
Mielnik and Plebański 1970
;
Moan and Oteo 2001
,

\displaystyle\Omega(t_{0},t_{0}+h)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{0}+h}dt_{n}\ldots\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{0}+h}dt_{1}L_{n}(t_{n},\ldots,t_{1})A(t_{n})\ldots A(t_{1}),

where

L_{n}(t_{n},\ldots,t_{1})=\frac{\Theta_{n}!(n-1-\Theta_{n})!}{n!}(-1)^{n-1-\Theta_{n}},

(24)

with
\Theta_{n}=\theta_{n-1,n-2}+\ldots+\theta_{2,1}
and
\theta_{b,a}
being the step function that is equal to
1
when
t_{b}>t_{a}
and
0
otherwise. Consequently,

|L_{n}(t_{n},\ldots,t_{1})|\leq\frac{(n-1)!}{n!}=\frac{1}{n}.

(25)

Then, we substitute the Taylor series of the Hamiltonian, and group terms by their
\Theta(h^{p})
order. This strategy allows us to use the submultiplicativity of the spectral norm, and generate triangle inequalities. Manipulating the expressions, we obtain the desired result.

3.2
Taylor remainder of the quasi-Magnus operator

Now we turn to the CFQM operator from
eq.

15
. We first need to define an extension of
x_{i,j}
for values
j>s
,

\overline{x}_{i,j}:=\sum_{g=0}^{s-1}y_{i,g}\frac{1-(-1)^{g+j}}{(g+j)2^{g+j}}.

(26)

This extension is needed as we want to account for all Taylor coefficients
a_{j}
in
eq.

5
, not just up to
j=s
.
Then, we uniformly bound
\|\overline{x}_{i,j}\alpha_{j}\|=\|\overline{x}_{i,j}a_{j-1}\|h^{j}\leq\bar{c}h^{j}
by the smallest possible
\bar{c}
.

Proposition 2
.

Let
h
and
\bar{c}
be defined as above. Let
\tilde{U}_{m}^{[2s]}
be the
2s
-order
m
-exponential commutator-free quasi-Magnus operator, expressed in terms of the univariate integrals
A^{(g)}(h)
in
eq.

15
.
Let
\tilde{U}_{m,p}^{[2s]}
be the
\Theta(h^{p})
-term in its Taylor expansion.
Then

\sum_{p=2s+1}\|\tilde{U}_{m,p}^{[2s]}\|\leq\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p=\\
2s+1\end{subarray}}^{\infty}h^{p}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\bm{w}\in\mathcal{C}(p)\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\bm{k}:\sum_{i=1}^{m}k_{i}\\
=\dim\bm{w}\end{subarray}}\frac{\bar{c}^{k_{1}}\cdots\bar{c}^{k_{m}}}{k_{1}!\cdots k_{m}!}.

(27)

The proof, in
section

B.2
, proceeds similarly to the previous result.

3.3
Gauss-Legendre quadrature error

Next, we estimate the error incurred by approximating the
A^{(g)}(h)
integrals in the CFQM with a Gauss-Legendre quadrature, with proof in
section

B.3
.

Proposition 3
.

Let
\tilde{U}_{m}^{[2s]}
represent the commutator-free Magnus operator, as a function of the univariate integrals and with coefficients
y^{(i)}_{j}
, see
eq.

15
. Let
\overline{U}_{m}^{[2s]}
be the operator where the integrals have been approximated via a Gauss-Legendre quadrature. Let
R_{s}(A)
denote the residual from approximating the integral
A
with quadrature of order
2s
.
Then,

\displaystyle\left\|\tilde{U}_{m}^{[2s]}-\overline{U}_{m}^{[2s]}\right\|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sum_{g=0}^{s-1}\left\|y_{i,g}R_{s}(A^{(g)}(h))\right\|

(28)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{h^{2s+1}(s!)^{4}}{(2s+1)((2s)!)^{3}}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sum_{g=0}^{s-1}\left(\frac{|y_{i,g}|}{h^{g}}\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{(j+2s)!}{j!}\frac{h^{j}}{2^{j}}\|a_{j+2n-g}\|\right).

(29)

To compute this error term, we apply the following well-known result for unitary operators

\displaystyle\|e^{X_{1}}\cdots e^{X_{m}}-e^{Y_{1}}\cdots e^{Y_{m}}\|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j=1}^{m}\|e^{X_{j}}-e^{Y_{j}}\|,

(30)

along with the following:

Corollary 1

(Corollary A.5 in
Childs et al. 2021
)
.

Given
\mathcal{H}
and
\mathcal{G}
two antihermitian continuous operator-valued functions defined on
\mathbb{R}
,

\begin{split}\left\|\mathcal{T}\exp\left(\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}d\tau\mathcal{H}(\tau)\right)-\mathcal{T}\exp\left(\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}d\tau\mathcal{G}(\tau)\right)\right\|\leq\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}d\tau\|\mathcal{H}(\tau)-\mathcal{G}(\tau)\|.\end{split}

(31)

We then compute the error from using quadrature to approximate each exponential
X_{i}=\sum_{g=0}^{s-1}y_{i,g}A^{(g)}(h)
in
\tilde{U}_{m}^{[2s]}
. The error from approximating the univariate integral
A^{(g)}(h)
with quadrature

\int_{a}^{b}f(x)dx=\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}f(c_{i})+R_{n}

(32)

can be bounded using the expression
(
Kahaner et al. 1989
, p. 146, Chapter 5)

R_{n}\left(\int_{a}^{b}f\right)=\frac{(b-a)^{2n+1}(n!)^{4}}{(2n+1)((2n)!)^{3}}f^{(2n)}(\xi),\qquad a<\xi<b.

(33)

These techniques suffice to compute
\left\|y_{i,g}R_{s}(A^{(g)}(h))\right\|
and prove the statement.

A key limitation of bound
eq.

28
is its convergence as
O(h^{s+2})
– instead of the desirable
O(h^{2s+1})
proven in
Blanes et al. 2020
– due to the presence of a factor of
h^{g}
in the denominator. We will see in our numerical experiments,
fig.

5
, that this error contribution is negligible. Yet, if we want to ensure the
O(h^{2s+1})
convergence, we could do so by increasing the number of quadrature points of the integrals to
\lceil\frac{3s}{2}\rceil
. This would not increase the number of exponentials to approximate via Trotter, but would modify the time-independent Hamiltonians in the exponents of
eq.

19
,
\sum_{k}z_{i,k}A_{k}h
.

3.4
Product formula error

The final error term is the product formula error
\|\overline{U}_{m}^{[2s]}-S_{2s}(\overline{U}_{m}^{[2s]})\|
, or in other words, the error due to splitting the exponentials in
\overline{U}_{m}^{[2s]}
into fast-forwardable exponentials. The implementation cost of such exponentials grows at most polylogarithmically in the simulation time, and optimized methods for their implementation are known
Motzoi et al. 2017
.
We refer to error bounds derived in the literature for Trotter-Suzuki product formulas, which can be directly applied to each operator
\exp\left(\sum_{k}z_{i,k}A_{k}h\right)
in
\overline{U}_{m}^{[2s]}

Childs et al. 2021
; and then combined with
eq.

30
for a full account of the errors incurred during the usage of CFQMs.

(35)

4.1
Time-dependent Heisenberg Hamiltonians

To compute the simulation error, we apply a Trotter-Suzuki product formula decomposition to each exponential in
\overline{U}_{m}^{[2s]}
, see in
eq.

19
. We compute the Trotter error of such approximation with the results in
Childs and Su 2019
. To do so, one starts by splitting each exponent
\sum_{k=1}^{s}z_{i,k}A_{k}h
into two fast-forwardable terms
-iBh
and
-iCh
, e.g., terms whose Hamiltonian simulation cost depends only logarithmically on the simulation time. For each
A_{k}
, we select
(
Childs et al. 2021
, Eqs 123,124)

\displaystyle H_{k,\text{odd}}=\frac{1}{4n}\sum_{l=1}^{\left\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\right\rfloor}\left(\vec{\sigma}_{2l-1}\vec{\sigma}_{2l}+\cos(\phi_{2l-1}+\omega_{2l-1}t_{k})\sigma_{2l-1}^{z}\right),

(36)

\displaystyle H_{k,\text{even}}=\frac{1}{4n}\sum_{l=1}^{\left\lceil\frac{n}{2}\right\rceil-1}\left(\vec{\sigma}_{2l}\vec{\sigma}_{2l+1}+\cos(\phi_{2l}+\omega_{2l}t_{k})\sigma_{2l}^{z}\right),

(37)

with
t_{k}=t_{0}+c_{k}\frac{h}{2}
. Thus, for each exponential index by
i
,
B:=\sum_{k=1}^{s}z_{i,k}H_{k,\text{odd}}
and
C:=\sum_{k=1}^{s}z_{i,k}H_{k,\text{even}}
.
Then, we define:

Definition 1

(Canonical product formula
Childs and Su 2019
)
.

Let
H=B+C
be a time-independent Hamiltonian, with
B
and
C
Hermitian operators. We say
S
is a canonical product formula when it has the form

S(t):=\left(e^{-it\xi_{\varsigma}C}e^{-it\beta_{\varsigma}B}\right)\ldots\left(e^{-it\xi_{1}C}e^{-it\beta_{1}B}\right).

(38)

The parameter
\varsigma
indicates the number of stages. Let
u
be an upper bound to the coefficients

\max\{|\xi_{1}|,\ldots,|\xi_{\varsigma}|,|\beta_{1}|,\ldots,|\beta_{\varsigma}|\}\leq u.

(39)

We say
S
has order
p
if
S(t)=e^{-iHt}+O(t^{p+1})
, and we will denote it as
S_{p}(t)
. We shall use the notation
(\varsigma,p,u)
-product formula to denote this canonical product formula. The
2s
-th order Trotter-Suzuki formula

is an
(\varsigma,p,u)
-formula with
\varsigma=2\cdot 5^{s-1}
,

and

.

The error of the Trotter formula for the unnormalized Heisenberg model scales as
(
Childs and Su 2019

\begin{split}\|S_{p}(t)-e^{-iHt}\|&\leq\sum_{k=1}^{\varsigma}n(2k-1)^{p}(ku)(2u)^{p}(2k-2)^{p}\frac{t^{p+1}}{(p+1)!}\\
&+\sum_{k=1}^{\varsigma}n(2k+1)^{p}(ku)(2u)^{p}(2k)^{p}\frac{t^{p+1}}{(p+1)!}.\end{split}

(40)

Since we are implementing a normalized Heisenberg model, we absorb the factor of
(4n)^{-1}
in

. We similarly include in

the prefactor
\sum_{k=1}^{s}|z_{i,k}|
for each of the

and substituting the remaining parameters of the

\begin{split}\left\|S_{2s}(h)-\exp\left(\sum_{k=1}^{s}z_{i,k}A_{k}h\right)\right\|&\leq\sum_{k=1}^{2\cdot 5^{s-1}}n(2k-1)^{2s}k(4k-4)^{2s}\frac{(Z_{i}h)^{2s+1}}{(2s+1)!}\\
&+\sum_{k=1}^{2\cdot 5^{s-1}}n(2k+1)^{2s}k(4k)^{2s}\frac{(Z_{i}h)^{2s+1}}{(2s+1)!}.\end{split}

(41)

This equation, in combination with
eq.

30

4.2
Comparison with the Suzuki method

There are three well-known broad techniques in quantum algorithms to simulate time-dependent Hamiltonians: the Suzuki method
Wiebe et al. 2010
, the continuous qDRIFT technique
Poulin et al. 2011
;
Berry et al. 2020
, and the Dyson series method
Kieferová et al. 2019
;
Low and Wiebe 2018
;
Berry et al. 2020
. The Suzuki method is an adaptation of Trotter-Suzuki product formulas for time-dependent Hamiltonians
Suzuki 1990
;
Suzuki 1991
;
Suzuki 1993
. The continuous qDRIFT is the generalization of the time-independent qDRIFT technique
Campbell 2019
, whose main disadvantage is the quadratic scaling with respect to the simulation time. Finally, the Dyson series method is a linear combination of unitaries approach that shares significant similarities with the Taylor series Hamiltonian simulation method, and similarly requires employing oblivious amplitude amplification
Berry et al. 2015
. Finally, there is always the option of approximating the time evolution using the Hamiltonian evaluated at the midpoint of each segment, which is called the exponential midpoint method and corresponds to the second-order Magnus operator
Gomez Pueyo et al. 2018
. We review and compare these techniques with CFQMs in
appendix

The most natural comparison of CFQMs is with other product formulas and specifically with the Suzuki technique of Ref.
Wiebe et al. 2010

(42)

5
Conclusion

In this paper, we have analyzed commutator-free quasi-Magnus operators (CFQMs) as a technique to implement time evolution under time-dependent Hamiltonians. The key contribution of this paper is providing a bound to the error of this method. This estimation allows us to find regimes where these operators outperform the alternative product formula techniques. Overall, we have found the split-operator CFQMs to exhibit a particularly performant behavior.

From a theoretical perspective, it would be desirable to find methods to systematically compute CFQM coefficients to arbitrary order
Blanes and Moan 2006
;
Alvermann and Fehske 2011
;
Blanes et al. 2017
;
Blanes 2018
. As far as we know, our work is the first attempt at finding the error bounds, which include error expressions that are quite different from those of other time-dependent simulation methods. Yet, as noted above our bound for the quadrature error is disappointing because it does not achieve
O(h^{2s+1})
convergence we know CFQMs achieve
Blanes et al. 2020
.
Further, it is likely that our error bounds are not tight, and further work may be able to improve them, either by deriving better bounds for each of the error terms, or via alternative constructions altogether. For instance, the error bounds we derive exhibit worse scaling than Trotter’s quasi-linear behavior in
n
for the Heisenberg Hamiltonian in
eq.

34

Childs and Su 2019
. It would be desirable to find out whether it is possible to find alternative
O(n^{1+o(1)})
bounds, or if it is possible to achieve commutator scaling and weak dependence on the derivatives shown in LCU-based Magnus techniques
Fang et al. 2024
. Explicit error bounds should be considered for cases where the Hamiltonian is unbounded too
Hochbruck and Lubich 2003
;
Singh 2018
;
Blanes et al. 2018
;
Blanes et al. 2020
;
An et al. 2021
, as well as for other CFQM constructions
Alvermann and Fehske 2011
;
Blanes et al. 2017
;
Blanes 2018
. The advanced techniques reported in
Blanes et al. 2018
;
Blanes et al. 2020
might be a good starting point for that endeavor.

A very useful next step would be the development of a CFQM operator tailored to periodic Hamiltonians, the counterpart to the Floquet evolution algorithm developed in the LCU paradigm
Mizuta and Fujii 2023
. While there exists a Floquet-Magnus operator, see
Casas et al. 2001
, its commutator-free approximations have not yet been developed.

Another field potentially deserving more attention is the application of CFQMs to quantum algorithms for the resolution of differential equations. The Magnus technique has been applied to study open systems dynamics
Blanes et al. 2017
, and generally integrate differential equations of the form
x^{\prime}=A(t)x

Blanes and Moan 2006
. This is in contrast to most of the quantum algorithm literature, which leverages linear algebra and finite method techniques
Berry 2014
;
Berry et al. 2017
;
Arrazola et al. 2019
;
Childs and Liu 2020
. However, since these dynamics may not be unitary, the techniques presented here will need to be developed further
Cao et al. 2023
. Additionally, CFQMs could become useful to implement interaction-picture algorithms that reduce the cost of the simulation when the Hamiltonian can be split into two components with different energy scales
Low and Wiebe 2018
;
Bosse et al. 2024
;
Sharma and Tran 2024
.

The area within quantum computing where these methods may be particularly useful is in the development of early fault-tolerant quantum algorithms, in particular for simulating spin models
Park et al. 2023
;
Childs et al. 2018
.
As we have seen in
section

4
, CFQMs, and particularly split-operator versions, seem particularly well suited to simulate time-dependent counterparts of spin systems.
As such, we believe they could become an important Hamiltonian simulation tool in the near future.

A.0.1
Exponential midpoint rule

The simplest method to simulate time-dependent Hamiltonians is known as the exponential midpoint rule. The idea is to divide the time simulation into small segments. Then, one approximates the time evolution by the imaginary exponential of the Hamiltonian evaluated at the midpoint of each segment. As the number of segments increases, the evolution converges. This method may be identified with the 2nd-order Magnus method, i.e.
s=1
. This is a special case however, in that the only error terms that contribute towards the overall bound are those due to truncating the Magnus Taylor series, using quadrature to approximate the exponential, and the subsequent Trotter product formula error.

A.0.2
Suzuki method

Instead of using a Trotter expansion of the Hamiltonian around the midpoint in each time segment, we can fit its time-ordered exponential.
This is the approach chosen by Refs.
Wiebe et al. 2010
;
Suzuki 1990
;
Suzuki 1991
;
Suzuki 1993
. Section IIB and Appendix A of
Childs et al. 2021
, and Appendix V of
Childs and Su 2019
also discuss the error of applying similar strategies. We do not discuss its cost here as we already did in the main text.

A.0.3
Continuous qDRIFT

Both the CFQM operators and the Suzuki method rely on Suzuki-Trotter product formulas. A well-known variation called qDRIFT uses randomization to reduce the cost in some time-independent instances
Campbell 2019
. There exists similarly a time-dependent version of this method, called continuous qDRIFT
(
Berry et al. 2020
, Theorem 7’)
. It inherits the same (dis)advantages and generalizes the prior Monte-Carlo-based method in Ref.
Poulin et al. 2011
. Its main drawback is its scaling with the simulation time. If the norm of the Hamiltonian does not vary significantly over time, this indicates an implicit quadratic dependence, worse than our and previously discussed methods.

A.0.4
Dyson series

Last but not least, we discuss the Dyson series approach of Refs.
Kieferová et al. 2019
;
Low and Wiebe 2018
. In contrast to the above algorithms, it relies on an LCU: one expands the Dyson series in a similar fashion to the Taylor series approach and then uses oblivious amplitude amplification to ensure the success probability does not decrease
Berry et al. 2015
. The key distinction from the Taylor series approach is that one has to coherently order the times at which the Hamiltonian is evaluated.
The cost scaling of this method is given in Ref.
(
Berry et al. 2020
, Theorem 10’)
,

O\left(L\|\alpha\|_{\infty,1}\frac{\log(L\|\alpha\|_{\infty,1}\epsilon^{-1})}{\log\log(L\|\alpha\|_{\infty,1}\epsilon^{-1})}\right)

(44)

calls to the oracle that computes the coefficients, and
\tilde{O}(L^{2}\|\alpha\|_{\infty,1}g_{c})
additional gates, where
g_{c}
is the cost of implementing each unitary in the linear combination. In the Heisenberg Hamiltonian from
eq.

34
, this translates into a complexity
\Omega(n)
in the number of spins, similar to the complexity achieved by CFQMs and the Suzuki technique. The dependence on the error is more favorable here, however, though product formulas may achieve similar logarithmic error scaling using multi-product formulas, which we cover in
appendix

D

Split-operator CFQMs

Appendix D
Multi-product formulas

One option to further optimize the cost of the Magnus simulation of time-dependent Hamiltonian is to replace the resulting product formula with a multi-product formula
Childs and Wiebe 2012
. Such multi-product formulas are a linear combination of powers of product formulas
U_{2s}\left(H;h\right)
, of the form

U_{2s,\vec{k}}(H;h)=\sum_{j=1}^{M}a_{j}U_{2s}^{k_{j}}\left(H;\frac{h}{k_{j}}\right)=e^{-ihH}+O(h^{2s^{\prime}+1}),

(106)

where the first argument indicates the Hamiltonian, and the second the segment lengths. For us,
U_{2s}\left(H;\frac{h}{k_{j}}\right)
would be the CFQM
S_{2s}\left(\overline{U}_{m}^{[2s]}\left(\frac{h}{k_{j}}\right)\right)
and
M
can be chosen to be equal to
s^{\prime}\geq s

Low et al. 2019
.
It is also possible to select the parameters
k_{j}
and amplitudes
a_{j}
such that
\|\vec{k}\|_{1}=O(s^{\prime 2}\log s^{\prime})
, while the success probability,
O(\|\vec{a}\|_{1}^{-1})
with
\|\vec{a}\|_{1}=O(\log s^{\prime})
, can be easily amplified to
O(1)
via oblivious amplitude amplification
Berry et al. 2014
;
Berry et al. 2015
;
Low et al. 2019
. Overall, this means that with a multiplicative overhead of
O(s^{\prime 2}\text{poly}\log s^{\prime})
over the cost of standard product formulas, multi-product variations exponentially reduce the error from
O(h^{2s+1})
to
O(h^{2s^{\prime}+1})
. In consequence, multi-product formulas achieve an exponential cost reduction (in terms of target accuracy) compared to the alternative of increasing the order of the product formula, where each 2-order increase multiplies the cost by 5,
Childs et al. 2021
.

The key requirement for the multi-product construction procedure in Ref.
Low et al. 2019
to work, is that we can Taylor expand the exact evolution as
Blanes et al. 1999
;
Blanes and Casas 2005
;
Chin 2010

\left[S_{2s}\left(\overline{U}_{m}^{[2s]}\left(\frac{h}{k_{j}}\right)\right)\right]^{k_{j}}=e^{\Omega(h)}+\frac{h^{2s+1}}{k_{j}^{2s}}\tilde{E}_{2s+1}(h)+\frac{h^{2s+3}}{k_{j}^{2s+2}}\tilde{E}_{2s+3}(h)+\ldots,

(107)

with
\tilde{E}_{2s+i}(h)
representing arbitrary error operators. A sufficient condition for this to happen is that

S_{2s}\left(\overline{U}_{m}^{[2s]}\left(h\right)\right)=e^{\Omega(h)+h^{2s+1}E_{2s+1}+h^{2s+3}E_{2s+3}+\ldots},

(108)

with
E_{2s+i}
again representing error operators, constant in
h
.
This requirement is equivalent to stating that only odd terms in
h
will be missing from the exact expression of
\Omega(h)
. It is clear that this same structure is followed in the truncation of the Magnus operator in
eqs.

6
,
7
and
8
: only odd terms appear in the expression, due to the time anti-symmetry that we enforced,
\Omega(t_{0},t_{0}+h)=-\Omega(t_{0}+h,t_{0})

Blanes et al. 2000
. In this case, the error operators will be given by the missing commutators of the derivatives of the Hamiltonian evaluated at
t_{\frac{1}{2}}
, the
a_{j}
operators.
Thus, we will argue that an operator fulfills the requirements to implement multi-product formulas whenever it exhibits the time anti-symmetric behavior.

Such time anti-symmetry is also enforced in the commutator-free quasi-Magnus operators thanks to the condition that
x_{m+1-i,j}=(-1)^{j+1}x_{i,j}
, see
eq.

17
. This condition will similarly carry over to the expression of
\tilde{U}_{m}^{[2s]}
. We can see this is the case due to two key facts. First,

\displaystyle\underset{\overset{\mkern 4.0mu\rotatebox{90.0}{$\,=$}}{}}{\sum_{j}x_{i,j}\alpha_{j}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{jg}y_{i,g}T_{g,j}^{(s)}\alpha_{j}=\sum_{g}y_{i,g}A^{(g)}(h)

(109)

\displaystyle\sum_{j}(-1)^{j+1}x_{m+1-i,j}\alpha_{j}

\displaystyle=\sum_{jg}y_{m+1-i,g}(-1)^{j+1}T^{(s)}_{g,j}\alpha_{j}=\sum_{g}y_{m+1-i,g}(-1)^{g}A^{(g)}(h).

(110)

Let us justify the last equality. Recall that

\displaystyle T^{(s)}_{g,j}=\frac{1-(-1)^{g+j}}{(g+j)2^{g+j}},

(111)

which will be different from
0
if and only if
g+j=1
mod
2
, or in other words,

mod
2

(-1)^{j+1}
with a

Blanes and Moan 2006

Finally, the quadrature operator will also ultimately respect the time anti-symmetry in (
17

104

A^{(k)}
with even

(

It is worth mentioning that the Suzuki formalism is also amenable to multi-product formulas

D.1
Computing the error of multi-product formulas

So far we have discussed why we can use multi-product formulas to decrease the error of CFQMs. Here we explain how to bound their error, thus being able to compute the step size
h
that will result in an equal or lower bound than the target error.
We follow a methodology similar to the one used in Theorem 2 in
Low et al. 2019
.

As a first step, we need to bound the error contributions of order greater or equal to
O(h^{2s^{\prime}+1})
in the product formula. Propositions
1
-
3
already display the adequate format for this: we only have to start the summatory from order
O(h^{2s^{\prime}+1})
instead of
O(h^{2s+1})
. In contrast, the error bound we used for the Trotter-Suzuki product formula step is not useful here. Instead, we use the result from Lemma F.2 in the supplementary material in
Childs et al. 2018
, which bounds the Taylor remainder of
\exp(\lambda)
of order
k
,
\mathcal{R}_{k}(\exp(\lambda))
, by

\|\mathcal{R}_{k}(\exp(\lambda))\|\leq\frac{|\lambda|^{k+1}}{(k+1)!}\exp(|\lambda|).

(112)

We can use this Lemma to compute the
O(h^{2s^{\prime}+1})
error of each exponential in
S_{2s}\left(\overline{U}_{m}^{[2s]}\left(h\right)\right)
, so in our case
\lambda=\sum_{k=1}^{s}z_{i,k}A_{k}h
. Then we leverage
eq.

30
, obtain the Trotter error of
O(h^{2s^{\prime}+1})
. Adding up all the errors we obtain the
O(h^{2s^{\prime}+1})
error for the CFQM.

The second step is converting such an error bound into an error bound for the multi-product formula. First, we use again
eq.

30
to bound the error of multiplying together
k_{j}
product formulas of time length
h/k_{j}
. Then, we take into account the final linear combination of unitaries, multiplying each term by its corresponding
|a_{j}|
, and adding them up. As stated above, the result is an upper bound to the multi-product formula error as a function of
h
,
a_{j}
and
k_{j}
. Then, we can maximize
h
subject to the error target we imposed.
