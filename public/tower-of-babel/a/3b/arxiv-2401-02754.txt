Structural completeness in quasivarieties
Paolo Aglianò, Alex Citkin

Abstract
In this paper we study various forms of (hereditary) structural completeness for quasivarieties of algebras, using mostly algebraic techniques. More specifically we study relative weakly projective algebras and the way they interact with structural completeness in quasivarieties. These ideas are then applied to the study of C C -structural completeness and C C -primitivity, through an algebraic generalization of Prucnal’s substitution. Finally we study in depth dual i-discriminator quasivarieties in which a particular instance of Prucnal’s substitution is used to prove that if each fundamental operation commutes with the i-discriminator, then it is primitive.



1
Introduction.

Research in admissibility of (finitary structural inference) rules in propositional logics is concerned with primarily two problems: given a propositional logic
\mathsf{L}
, does
\mathsf{L}
has admissible not derivable rules? And if it does, what are they (see, e.g.,
[
60
]
)? The logics in which all structural admissible rules are derivable are called
structurally complete
, and this notion was introduced in
[
54
]
. Since a structurally complete logic cannot have structurally complete extensions (e.g. Medvedev Logic (see
[
29
]
), it is natural to ask whether a logic and all its extensions are structurally complete; for such logics the term
hereditary structural completeness
was coined in
[
29
]
.
Thus, traditionally, the problem of structural completeness is formulated in the following way: given a propositional logic
\mathsf{L}
, is it structurally complete? And if it is, is it hereditarily structurally complete? And if it is not, which of its extensions are structurally complete?

The equivalent algebraic semantic of an algebraizable propositional logic is a quasivariety (see
[
17
]
): the formulas are translated into equations and the rules into quasiequations. In algebraic terms, given a quasivariety
\mathsf{Q}
, a quasiequation
\Phi
is admissible in
\mathsf{Q}
if
\mathsf{F}_{\mathsf{Q}}(\omega)\models\Phi
, and
\Phi
is derivable in
\mathsf{Q}
if
\mathsf{Q}\models\Phi
. Accordingly,
\mathsf{Q}
is
structurally complete
if
\mathsf{Q}
does not have admissible not derivable quasiequations, that is, if
\mathsf{Q}
is generated by
\mathsf{F}_{\mathsf{Q}}(\omega)
. And
\mathsf{Q}
is
hereditarily structurally complete
if
\mathsf{Q}
and all its subquasivarieties are structurally complete. Because hereditary structural completeness of
\mathsf{Q}
is equivalent to all subquasivarieties of
\mathsf{Q}
being equational (that is, every subquasivariety of
\mathsf{Q}
can be defined relative to
\mathsf{Q}
by a set of equations), hereditarily structurally complete quasivarieties also called deductive or
primitive
(see
[
13
]
), and we use the latter term. It follows that any question about structural completeness (and its variations, see
[
8
]
) of an algebraizable logic can be solved in purely algebraic terms. On the other hand, given the fact that structural completeness for quasivarieties does not make any reference to logic, the concept makes sense also for quasivarieties that are not quasivarieties of logic.

In
[
51
]
, Mints observed that any admissible in the intuitionistic propositional calculus (IPC) rule which does not contain either connectives
\lor
or
\to
is derivable in IPC. In other words, IPC is
\{\land,\lor,\neg\}
- and
\{\land,\to,\neg\}
-structurally complete. This observation leads to the following problem: given a set of formulas
F
in the language of logic
\mathsf{L}
, we say that
\mathsf{L}
is
structurally complete relative to

F
if every admissible rule whose premises and conclusion are in
F
, is derivable in
\mathsf{L}
. For instance, one can take
F
as a set of all formulas of IPC not containing disjunction and IPC will be structurally complete relative to such
F
. Moreover, we say that logic
\mathsf{L}

is p-structurally complete relative to

F
, if every admissible rule whose premises (not necessarily conclusions) are in
F
is derivable. For instance, IPC is p-structurally complete (and even hereditarily p-structurally complete) relative to set of all Harrop (or all anti-Harrop) formulas (see
[
58
]
). Another set of formulas relative to which IPC is p-complete was introduced in
[
30
]
.

This observation led the second author to introducing a notion of
C
-structural completeness (see
[
31
]
): if
C
is a subset of the set of connectives of a propositional logic
\mathsf{L}
,
\mathsf{L}
is
C
-structurally complete if every admissible rule containing only connectives from
C
is derivable in
\mathsf{L}
.
C
-structural completeness is very close but not the same as structural completeness of the
C
-fragment of
\mathsf{L}
. For instance, from Mints’ observation it follows that IPC is
\{\to,\neg\}
-structurally complete, while
\{\to,\neg\}
-fragment of IPC is not (see
[
28
,
31
]
for details). In some cases
C
-structural completeness can be extended to structural completeness; for instance, the proof from
[
51
]
holds for any axiomatic extension of IPC, thus it holds for the Dummett calculus LC = IPC +
((p\to q)\lor(q\to p))
and because
\lor
can be expressed in LC by a formula, LC (and every its extension) is structurally complete, and this is an alternative proof of hereditary structural completeness of LC first observed in
[
36
]
(the same argument is used to prove primitivity in
[
39
]
, Theorem 2.9).

In the algebraic setting, if
\mathsf{Q}
is a quasivariety and
C
is a subset of the set of basic operations, then
\mathsf{Q}
is
C
-structurally complete
if for every quasiequation
\Phi
containing operations only from
C
,
{\textbf{F}}_{\mathsf{Q}}(\omega)\models\Phi
entails
\mathsf{Q}\models\Phi
. In other words, if
\Phi
is refuted in some algebra from
\mathsf{Q}
, then
\Phi
must be refuted in
{\textbf{F}}_{\mathsf{Q}}(\omega)
. On the other hand, if
\mathsf{Q}^{C}
is a quasivariety generated by all
C
-reducts of algebras from
\mathsf{Q}
, then
\mathsf{Q}^{C}
is structurally complete if every quasiequation
\Phi
is refuted in
{\textbf{F}}_{\mathsf{Q}^{C}}(\omega)
as long as it is refuted in some algebra from
\mathsf{Q}^{C}
, or equivalently, in some
C
-reduct of an algebra from
\mathsf{Q}
. Let us observe that
C
-reduct of
{\textbf{F}}_{\mathsf{Q}}(\omega)
belongs to
\mathsf{Q}^{C}
and hence, structural completeness of
\mathsf{Q}^{C}
entails
C
-structural completeness of
\mathsf{Q}
, while the converse does not have to be true.

Admissibility and derivability of quasiequation
\Phi=\bigwedge_{i\in[1,n]}t_{i}\approx s_{i}\Rightarrow t\approx s
in a quasivariety
\mathsf{Q}
depend on properties of the algebra
A
finitely presented by relations
t_{i}\approx s_{i},i\in[1,n]
. Indeed,
\Phi
is derivable in
\mathsf{Q}
, that is
\mathsf{Q}\models\Phi
, if and only if the terms
t
and
s
represent the same element of
A
. And
\Phi
is admissible in
\mathsf{Q}
if and only if none of homomorphic images of
A
in which
t
and
s
represent distinct elements can be embeddable into
{\textbf{F}}_{\mathsf{Q}}(\omega)
. In particular, if
A
is subdirectly irreducible and
t
and
s
represent elements from the monolith of
A
,
\Phi
is admissible in Q if and only if
A
is not embeddable in
{\textbf{F}}_{\mathsf{Q}}(\omega)
; in this case,
\Phi
is a characteristic rule of
A
(cf.
[
29
]
).

The following simple sufficient condition is often used to demonstrate
C
-structural completeness: if
C
-reduct of every algebra finitely presented in
\mathsf{Q}
can be embedded in the
C
-reduct of
{\textbf{F}}_{\mathsf{Q}}(\omega)
, then
\mathsf{Q}
is
C
-structurally complete. Indeed, if a quasiequation
\Phi=\bigwedge_{i\in[1,n]}t_{i}\approx s_{i}\Rightarrow t\approx s
whose terms contain operations only from
C
is not valid in
\mathsf{Q}
, then there is an algebra
{\textbf{A}}\in\mathsf{Q}
and a valuation
v
in
A
such that
v(t_{i})=v(s_{i})
for all
i\in[1,n]
and
v(t)\neq v(s)
. Thus,
\Phi
can be refuted in algebra
B
finitely presented in
\mathsf{Q}
and defined by the relations
t_{i}\approx s_{i},i\in[1,n]
; and if
B
can be embedded in
{\textbf{F}}_{\mathsf{Q}}(\omega)
as
C
-subreduct, then
\Phi
can be refuted in
{\textbf{F}}_{\mathsf{Q}}(\omega)
as well.

As an example let us take
\mathsf{Q}
to be a quasivariety of Heyting algebras in the signature
\{\to,\land,\lor,\neg\}
and let
C=\{\to,\land\}
and
C^{\prime}=\{\land,\lor\}
and let us recall that every finitely presented in
\mathsf{Q}
algebra is (isomorphic to) a quotient algebra of some free algebra
{\textbf{F}}_{\mathsf{Q}}(n)
by a principal congruence. Let us also recall that for any Heyting algebra
A
and for every principal congruence
\varTheta
, each congruence class
a/\varTheta
contains a largest and a smallest element. It is a simple exercise to check that the set of all the largest elements of the
\varTheta
-classes is closed under
\to
and
\land
and hence it forms a
\{\to,\land\}
-subreduct of
A
, while the set of all smallest elements of the
\varTheta
-classes is closed under
\land
and
\lor
and hence it forms a
\{\land,\lor\}
-subreduct of
A
. Thus, the
C
- and
C^{\prime}
-reducts of any quotient algebra by a principal congruence can be embedded in a
C
- or
C^{\prime}
-reduct of the preimage. Therefore the
C
- and
C^{\prime}
-reducts of every finitely presented algebra can be embedded in the respective reducts of a free algebra, which means that
\mathsf{Q}
is
C
- and
C^{\prime}
-structurally complete.

The paper is structured in the following way: in Section
2
we give the necessary definitions and remind the basic facts about quasivarieties. In Section
3
we study relative weakly projective algebras and primitive quasivarieties. In particular we give a criterion of primitivity for tame quasivarieties, that is for quasivarieties in which every finitely generated algebra is finitely presented. In addition, we prove that every finite subdirectly irreducible algebra is projective in the quasivariety which it generates as long as it is weakly projective in it.

Section
4
is dedicated to studying C-structurally complete and C-primitive quasivarieties. We introduce the notion of u-presentable congruence and we prove that, for quasivariety
\mathsf{Q}
to be
C
-structurally complete, it is sufficient that every compact
\mathsf{Q}
-congruence of
\mathsf{F}_{\mathsf{Q}}(\omega)
is u-presentable. We also give an algebraic generalization of Prucnal’s substitution (see
[
55
]
).

In Section
5
we study in depth dual i-discriminator quasivarieties that are different from the varieties studied in Section
4
but to which one can apply techniques similar to Prucnal’s substitution. We prove several structure theorems, connecting them with the theory of if ideals in quasivarieties, and we show that every relatively congruence distributive dual i-discriminator variety in which every fundamental operation commutes with the i-discriminator is primitive.

In Section
6
we discuss some questions arising during our investigations into the matter of this paper.

2
Main definitions.

2.1
Class operators, quasivarieties an free algebras.

Let
\mathsf{K}
be a class of algebras; we denote by
{\mathbf{I}},{\mathbf{H}},{\mathbf{P}},{\mathbf{S}},{\mathbf{P}}_{u}
the class operators sending
\mathsf{K}
in the class of all isomorphic copies, homomorphic images, direct products, subalgebras and ultraproducts of members of
\mathsf{K}
. The operators can be composed in the obvious way; for instance
{\mathbf{S}}{\mathbf{P}}(\mathsf{K})
denotes all algebras that are embeddable in a direct product of members of
\mathsf{K}
; moreover there are relations among the classes resulting from applying operators in a specific orders, for instance
{\mathbf{P}}{\mathbf{S}}(\mathsf{K})\subseteq{\mathbf{S}}{\mathbf{P}}(\mathsf{K})
and
{\mathbf{H}}{\mathbf{S}}{\mathbf{P}}(\mathsf{K})
is the largest class we can obtain composing the operators. We will use all the known relations without further notice, but the reader can consult
[
53
]
or
[
21
]
for a textbook treatment. We only point out that if
\mathsf{K}
is a finite set of finite algebras, then
{\mathbf{P}}_{u}(\mathsf{K})=\mathsf{K}
.

If
\rho
is a type of algebras, an
equation
is a pair
p,q
of
\rho
-terms (i.e. elements of the absolutely free algebra
{\textbf{T}}_{\rho}(\omega)
) that we write suggestively as
p\approx q
; a
universal sentence
in
\rho
is a formal pair
(\Sigma,\Gamma)
that we write as
\Sigma\Rightarrow\Gamma
, where
\Sigma,\Gamma
are finite sets of equations; a universal sentence is a
quasiequation
if
|\Gamma|=1
. Clearly an equation is a quasiequation in which
\Sigma=\emptyset
.

Given any set of variables
X
, an assignment of
X
into an algebra
A
of type
\rho
is a function
h
mapping each variable
x\in X
to an element of
A
, that extends (uniquely) to a homomorphism (that we shall also call
h
) from the absolutely free algebra
{\textbf{T}}_{\rho}(\omega)
to
A
.
An algebra
A
satisfies an equation
p\approx q
with an assignment
h
(and we write
{\textbf{A}},h\models p\approx q
) if
h(p)=h(q)
in
A
. An equation
p\approx q
is
valid
in
A
(and we write
{\textbf{A}}\vDash p\approx q
) if for all assignments
h
in
A
,
{\textbf{A}},h\models p\approx q
; if
\Sigma
is a set of equations then

{\textbf{A}}\vDash\Sigma
if
{\textbf{A}}\vDash\sigma
for all
\sigma\in\Sigma
.
A universal sentence is
valid
in
A
(and we write
{\textbf{A}}\vDash\Sigma\Rightarrow\Delta
) if for all assignments
h
to
A
,
h(p)=h(q)
for all
p\approx q\in\Sigma
implies that there is an identity
s\approx t\in\Delta
with

h(s)=h(t)
; in other words a universal sentence can be understood as the formula
\forall\mathbf{x}(\bigwedge\Sigma\rightarrow\bigvee\Delta)
. An equation or a universal sentence is
valid
in a class
\mathsf{K}
if it is valid in all algebras in
\mathsf{K}
.

Let
\mathsf{K}
be any class of similar algebras:

(a)

if
\mathsf{K}={\mathbf{H}}{\mathbf{S}}{\mathbf{P}}\mathsf{K}
, then
\mathsf{K}
is a
variety
;

(b)

if
\mathsf{K}={\mathbf{I}}{\mathbf{S}}{\mathbf{P}}{\mathbf{P}}_{u}\mathsf{K}
, then
\mathsf{K}
is a
quasivariety
;

(c)

if
\mathsf{K}={\mathbf{I}}{\mathbf{S}}{\mathbf{P}}_{u}\mathsf{K}
, then
\mathsf{K}
is a
universal class
.

Of course we will use the standard abbreviations
{\mathbf{V}}
for
{\mathbf{H}}{\mathbf{S}}{\mathbf{P}}
and
{\mathbf{Q}}
for
{\mathbf{I}}{\mathbf{S}}{\mathbf{P}}{\mathbf{P}}_{u}
; from the properties of the operators
one can easily deduce that for any class
\mathsf{K}
,
{\mathbf{Q}}(\mathsf{K})\subseteq{\mathbf{V}}(\mathsf{K})
.

The following facts were essentially discovered by A. Tarski , J. Łòs and A. Lyndon in the pioneering phase of model theory; for proof of this and similar statements the reader can consult
[
27
]
.

Theorem 2.1
.

Let
\mathsf{K}
be any class of algebras. Then:

1.

\mathsf{K}
is a universal class if and only if it is the class of all algebras in which a given set of universal sentences is valid;

2.

\mathsf{K}
is a quasivariety if and only if it is the class of all algebras in which a given set of quasiequations is valid;

3.

\mathsf{K}
is a variety if and only if it is the class of all algebras in which a given set of equations is valid.

For the definition of free algebras in a class
\mathsf{K}
on a set
X
of generators, in symbols
{\textbf{F}}_{\mathsf{K}}(X)
, we refer to
[
21
]
; we merely observe that a class
\mathsf{K}
contains all the free algebras in
\mathsf{K}
if and only if
\mathsf{K}={\mathbf{I}}{\mathbf{S}}{\mathbf{P}}(\mathsf{K})
; therefore for any quasivariety
\mathsf{Q}
,
{\textbf{F}}_{\mathsf{Q}}(X)={\textbf{F}}_{{\mathbf{V}}(\mathsf{Q})}(X)
.

2.2
Congruences and subdirectly irreducible algebras.

A
congruence
on an algebra
A
is an equivalence relation that is compatible (in the usual sense) with all the fundamental operations of
A
. As the intersection of any family of congruences on
A
is again a congruence on
A
, the mapping sending
X\subseteq A^{2}
into
\vartheta_{\textbf{A}}(X)
(the smallest congruence of
A
containing
X
) is a closure operator on
A^{2}
that can be proved too by algebraic. Thus the congruences of
A
form an algebraic lattice which we denote by
\operatorname{Con}({\textbf{A}})
; hence there are always a largest and smallest congruence in
\operatorname{Con}({\textbf{A}})
which will be denoted by
1_{\textbf{A}}
and
0_{\textbf{A}}
. A variety
\mathsf{V}
is
congruence distributive
if all congruence lattices of algebras in
\mathsf{V}
are distributive.

Let
{\textbf{B}},({\textbf{A}}_{i})_{i\in I}
be algebras in the same signature; we say that
B

embeds
in
\prod_{i\in I}{\textbf{A}}_{i}
if
{\textbf{B}}\in{\mathbf{I}}{\mathbf{S}}(\prod_{i\in I}{\textbf{A}}_{i})
.
Let
p_{i}
be the
i
-th projection, or better, the composition of the embedding and the
i
-th projection, from
B
to
{\textbf{A}}_{i}
; the embedding is

subdirect
if for all
i\in I
,
p_{i}({\textbf{B}})={\textbf{A}}_{i}
and in this case we will write

{\textbf{B}}\leq_{sd}\prod_{i\in I}{\textbf{A}}_{i}.

An algebra
B
is
subdirectly irreducible
if it is nontrivial and for any subdirect embedding

{\textbf{B}}\leq_{sd}\prod_{i\in I}{\textbf{A}}_{i}

there is an
i\in I
such that
B
and
{\textbf{A}}_{i}
are isomorphic. An algebra
A
is
finitely subdirectly irreducible
if the same conclusion as above holds whenever
I
is finite.

Lemma 2.2
.

For an algebra
A
the following are equivalent:

1.

A
is subdirectly irreducible;

2.

\operatorname{Con}({\textbf{A}})
has exactly one minimal congruence
\mu_{\textbf{A}}>0_{\textbf{A}}
(which of course it is equivalent to saying that
0_{\textbf{A}}
is strictly meet irreducible in
\operatorname{Con}({\textbf{A}})
);

3.

there are
a,b\in A
such that
(a,b)\in\theta
for all
\theta\in\operatorname{Con}({\textbf{A}})
,
\theta>0_{\textbf{A}}
.

For any variety
\mathsf{V}
we denote by
\mathsf{V}_{fsi}
the class of finitely subdirectly irreducible members of
\mathsf{V}
; we have the classical results:

Theorem 2.3
.

1.

(Birkhoff
[
15
]
) Every algebra can be subdirectly embedded in a product of subdirectly irreducible algebras. So if
{\textbf{A}}\in\mathsf{V}
, then
A
can be subdirectly embedded in a product of members of

and then
{\mathbf{V}}(\mathsf{V}_{si})=\mathsf{V}
.

2.

(Jónsson’s Lemma
[
45
]
) Suppose that
\mathsf{K}
is a class of algebras such that
\mathsf{V}(\mathsf{K})=\mathsf{V}
is congruence distributive;
then
\mathsf{V}_{fsi}\subseteq{\mathbf{H}}{\mathbf{S}}{\mathbf{P}}_{u}(\mathsf{K})
.

2.3
Relative congruences

If
\mathsf{Q}
is a quasivariety and
{\textbf{A}}\in\mathsf{Q}
, a
\mathsf{Q}
-congruence
(of

; as the intersection of any family of
\mathsf{Q}
-congruences of

the smallest
\mathsf{Q}
-congruence of

.
If

we say that

(or briefly

for which

First we observe:

Lemma 2.6
.

Let
A
be an algebra, such that

is congruence distributive. Then

{\mathbf{Q}}({\textbf{A}})={\mathbf{V}}({\textbf{A}})
if and only if every subdirectly irreducible algebra in

is in

Proof.

Suppose first that
{\mathbf{Q}}({\textbf{A}})={\mathbf{V}}({\textbf{A}})
; since any subdirectly irreducible algebra

is

-irreducible by Theorem

{\textbf{B}}\in{\mathbf{I}}{\mathbf{S}}{\mathbf{P}}_{u}({\textbf{A}})

Conversely assume that every subdirectly irreducible algebra in

{\mathbf{I}}{\mathbf{S}}{\mathbf{P}}_{u}{\textbf{A}}
. Since
{\mathbf{V}}({\textbf{A}})

Next:

Proposition 2.7
.

Let
\mathsf{Q}
be a quasivariety,
{\textbf{A}}\in\mathsf{Q}
, and
{\textbf{B}}\leq{\textbf{A}}
. Then, there exists a maximal

-congruence

\displaystyle\theta|_{\textbf{B}}=0_{\textbf{B}}.

Moreover if

\mathsf{Q}
-irreducible, then

\mathsf{Q}

\mathsf{Q}
-primitive.

Proof.

We first show the equivalence between (1) and (2). Suppose that
\mathsf{Q}
is primitive and let
\mathsf{Q}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathsf{Q}
; if
\mathsf{Q}^{\prime\prime}\subseteq\mathsf{Q}^{\prime}
and
{\mathbf{H}}(\mathsf{Q}^{\prime\prime})={\mathbf{H}}(\mathsf{Q}^{\prime})
then

\mathsf{Q}^{\prime}={\mathbf{H}}(\mathsf{Q}^{\prime})\cap\mathsf{Q}={\mathbf{H}}(\mathsf{Q}^{\prime\prime})\cap\mathsf{Q}=\mathsf{Q}^{\prime\prime}

so
\mathsf{Q}^{\prime}
is structurally complete by Theorem
3.1
.

Conversely assume (2), let
\mathsf{Q}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathsf{Q}
and let
\mathsf{Q}^{\prime\prime}={\mathbf{H}}(\mathsf{Q}^{\prime})\cap\mathsf{Q}
(it is clearly a quasivariety); then
{\mathbf{H}}(\mathsf{Q}^{\prime\prime})={\mathbf{H}}(\mathsf{Q}^{\prime})
and thus
\mathsf{Q}^{\prime\prime}=\mathsf{Q}^{\prime}
, again using the characterization of Theorem
3.1
. So

\mathsf{Q}^{\prime}
is equational in
\mathsf{Q}
and
\mathsf{Q}
is primitive.

Assume (1) again, and let
{\textbf{A}},{\textbf{B}}\in\mathsf{Q}
with
A
subdirectly irreducible and
{\textbf{A}}\in{\mathbf{H}}({\textbf{B}})
. Since
\mathsf{Q}
is primitive we have

{\mathbf{Q}}({\textbf{B}})={\mathbf{H}}({\mathbf{Q}}({\textbf{B}}))\cap\mathsf{Q}

and hence
{\textbf{A}}\in{\mathbf{Q}}({\textbf{B}})
. Since
A
is subdirectly irreducible,
{\textbf{A}}\in{\mathbf{I}}{\mathbf{S}}{\mathbf{P}}_{u}({\textbf{B}})
by Theorem
2.5
and (3) holds.

Conversely, assume (3) and let
\mathsf{Q}^{\prime}
be a subquasivariety of
\mathsf{Q}
. Let
{\textbf{B}}\in{\mathbf{H}}(\mathsf{Q}^{\prime})\cap\mathsf{Q}
; then

B
is subdirectly embeddable in
\prod_{i\in I}{\textbf{A}}_{i}
where
{\textbf{A}}_{i}
is
\mathsf{Q}
-irreducible.
Now for any
i

{\textbf{A}}_{i}\in{\mathbf{H}}({\textbf{B}})
, so
{\textbf{A}}_{i}\in{\mathbf{I}}{\mathbf{S}}{\mathbf{P}}_{u}({\textbf{B}})
.
As
{\textbf{B}}\in{\mathbf{H}}(\mathsf{Q}^{\prime})
, there is a
{\textbf{C}}\in Q^{\prime}
with
{\textbf{B}}\in{\mathbf{H}}({\textbf{C}})
and thus
{\textbf{A}}_{i}\in{\mathbf{H}}{\mathbf{S}}{\mathbf{P}}_{u}({\textbf{C}})
.
So there is a
{\textbf{D}}_{i}\in{\mathbf{I}}{\mathbf{S}}{\mathbf{P}}_{u}({\textbf{C}})\subseteq\mathsf{Q}^{\prime}
with
{\textbf{A}}_{i}\in{\mathbf{H}}({\textbf{D}}_{i})
. So
{\textbf{A}}_{i}\in{\mathbf{I}}{\mathbf{S}}{\mathbf{P}}_{u}({\textbf{D}}_{i})\subseteq\mathsf{Q}^{\prime}

for all
i\in I
; this implies
{\textbf{B}}\in Q^{\prime}
,
\mathsf{Q}^{\prime}={\mathbf{H}}(\mathsf{Q}^{\prime})\cap\mathsf{Q}
and
\mathsf{Q}
is primitive.
∎

We observe that the equivalence of (1) and (3) entails Theorem 2.12 from
[
13
]
. If instead of a quasivariety we consider a variety we get a little improvement.

Corollary 3.7
.

For a variety
\mathsf{V}
the following are equivalent:

1.

\mathsf{V}
is primitive;

2.

every subquasivariety of
\mathsf{V}
is a variety;

3.

\mathsf{V}
is structurally complete and every proper subvariety of
\mathsf{V}
is primitive.

Proof.

Trivially (1) and (2) are equivalent and imply (3). Assume then (3) and let
\mathsf{Q}
be a subquasivariety of
\mathsf{V}
; if
\mathsf{Q}=\mathsf{V}
then it is a variety.
Otherwise
{\mathbf{H}}(\mathsf{Q})
must be a proper subvariety of
\mathsf{V}
, so it is primitive; hence
\mathsf{Q}
is a variety and (2) follows.
∎

We will see later that the hypothesis of structural completeness for
\mathsf{V}
cannot be removed: there are varieties such that every proper subvariety is primitive but fail to be structurally complete.
To get more information we need some definitions: let
A
be an algebra and
\mathsf{K}
a class of algebras of the same type as
A
. We say that:

\bullet

A
is
projective
in
\mathsf{K}
if for all
{\textbf{B}}\in\mathsf{K}
if
f:{\textbf{B}}\longrightarrow{\textbf{A}}
is a surjective epimorphism, then there is an embedding
g:{\textbf{A}}\longrightarrow{\textbf{B}}
with
gf=id_{\textbf{A}}
;

\bullet

A
is
weakly
\mathsf{K}
-projective
if for all
{\textbf{B}}\in\mathsf{K}
if
{\textbf{A}}\in{\mathbf{H}}({\textbf{B}})
, then
{\textbf{A}}\in{\mathbf{I}}{\mathbf{S}}({\textbf{B}})
.

It is clear that if
A
is projective in
\mathsf{K}
, then
A
is weakly
\mathsf{K}
-projective.

Lemma 3.8
.

Let
\mathsf{Q}
be a quasivariety. Then for
{\textbf{A}}\in\mathsf{Q}
the following are equivalent:

1.

A
is weakly
\mathsf{Q}
-projective;

2.

[\mathsf{Q}:{\textbf{A}}]
is closed under
{\mathbf{H}}
.

Proof.

Assume (1), and suppose that
{\textbf{B}}\in{\mathbf{H}}([\mathsf{Q}:{\textbf{A}}])
. If
{\textbf{A}}\in{\mathbf{I}}{\mathbf{S}}({\textbf{B}})
, then
{\textbf{A}}\in{\mathbf{S}}{\mathbf{H}}([\mathsf{Q}:{\textbf{A}}])\subseteq{\mathbf{H}}{\mathbf{S}}([\mathsf{Q}:{\textbf{A}}])
. Now
[\mathsf{Q}:{\textbf{A}}]\subseteq\mathsf{Q}
and
A
is weakly
\mathsf{Q}
-projective; so
{\textbf{A}}\in{\mathbf{S}}([\mathsf{Q}:{\textbf{A}}])
which is impossible. It follows that
{\textbf{A}}\notin{\mathbf{I}}{\mathbf{S}}({\textbf{B}})
and
{\textbf{B}}\in[\mathsf{Q}:{\textbf{A}}]
; thus
[\mathsf{Q}:{\textbf{A}}]
is closed under
{\mathbf{H}}
.
Assume now (2); we show that
A
is weakly
\mathsf{Q}
-projective. Suppose that
{\textbf{A}}\in{\mathbf{H}}({\textbf{B}})
for some
{\textbf{B}}\in\mathsf{Q}
; if
{\textbf{A}}\notin{\mathbf{I}}{\mathbf{S}}({\textbf{B}})
, then

{\textbf{B}}\in[\mathsf{Q}:{\textbf{A}}]
and, since
[\mathsf{Q}:{\textbf{A}}]
is closed under
{\mathbf{H}}
,
{\textbf{A}}\in[\mathsf{Q}:{\textbf{A}}]
, again a contradiction. Hence
{\textbf{A}}\in{\mathbf{I}}{\mathbf{S}}({\textbf{B}})
and
A
is weakly
\mathsf{Q}
-projective.
∎

Corollary 3.9
.

Let
\mathsf{Q}
be a quasivariety; if
A
is
\mathsf{Q}
-irreducible and
\mathsf{Q}
-finitely presented then the following are equivalent:

1.

A
is weakly
\mathsf{Q}
-projective;

2.

[\mathsf{Q}:{\textbf{A}}]
is
\mathsf{Q}
-equational;

3.

[\mathsf{Q}:{\textbf{A}}]
is a variety.

Proof.

If (1) holds then
[\mathsf{Q}:\mathsf{A}]
is a quasivariety by Lemma
2.13
and it is closed under
{\mathbf{H}}
by Lemma
3.8
; therefore it is a variety and (3) holds.
Clearly (3) implies (2), so let’s assume (2) i.e.
[\mathsf{Q}:{\textbf{A}}]={\mathbf{H}}([\mathsf{Q}:{\textbf{A}}])\cap\mathsf{Q}
. Let
{\textbf{B}}\in\mathsf{Q}
with
{\textbf{A}}\in{\mathbf{H}}({\textbf{B}})
; if
{\textbf{A}}\notin{\mathbf{S}}({\textbf{B}})
, then

{\textbf{B}}\in[\mathsf{Q}:{\textbf{A}}]
and thus
{\textbf{A}}\in{\mathbf{H}}([\mathsf{Q}:{\textbf{A}}])
. As
{\textbf{A}}\in\mathsf{Q}
we get
{\textbf{A}}\in[\mathsf{Q}:{\textbf{A}}]
, a contradiction; so
{\textbf{A}}\in{\mathbf{S}}({\textbf{B}})
and therefore it is weakly
\mathsf{Q}
-projective.
∎

Again Corollary
3.9
entails Lemma 5.1.23 from
[
41
]
;
also we get at once that if
\mathsf{Q}
is primitive, then every finitely
\mathsf{Q}
-presented
\mathsf{Q}
-irreducible algebra in
\mathsf{Q}
must be weakly
\mathsf{Q}
-projective.
The converse however does not seem to be true in general; it is however when we restrict to locally finite quasivarieties (Theorem
3.10
below).

A class
\mathsf{K}
of algebras is
tame
if every finitely generated algebra in
\mathsf{K}
is
\mathsf{K}
-finitely
presented. Note that the concept has content: any class
\mathsf{K}
of algebras of finite type which is
locally finite in the usual sense (i.e. every finitely generated algebra in
\mathsf{K}
is finite)
is tame since in that case the class of finite algebras, the class of finitely generated and the class of finitely presented algebras coincide.
Tame classes of algebras have been studied mainly in groups (better, in algebras in
which groups are interpretable): for instance nilpotent groups are tame,
so Abelian groups are tame (and it is an example of a tame non locally finite variety).

The next result is implicit in
[
41
]
(Section 5.1) for locally finite quasivarieties (for a self-contained proof the reader can look at
[
8
]
, Theorem 4.11); tameness
is weaker than local finiteness but the same proof goes through with minimal changes.

Theorem 3.10
.

If
\mathsf{Q}
is a tame quasivariety of finite type, then the following are equivalent.

1.

\mathsf{Q}
is primitive;

2.

for all finitely generated
{\textbf{A}}\in\mathsf{Q}
,
[\mathsf{Q}:{\textbf{A}}]
is equational relative to
\mathsf{Q}
;

3.

every finitely generated
\mathsf{Q}
-irreducible
{\textbf{A}}\in\mathsf{Q}
is weakly
\mathsf{Q}
-projective;

4.

every finitely generated
\mathsf{Q}
-irreducible
{\textbf{A}}\in\mathsf{Q}
is weakly
\mathsf{Q}_{fg}
-projective, where
\mathsf{Q}_{fg}
is the class of finitely generated algebras in
\mathsf{Q}
.

We observe that (4) implies (1) even in the absence of tameness; so we get:

Corollary 3.11
.

Let
\mathsf{Q}
be a quasivariety; if every finitely generated
\mathsf{Q}
-irreducible algebra is weakly
\mathsf{Q}
-projective (or even weakly
\mathsf{Q}_{fg}
-projective) then
\mathsf{Q}
is primitive.

From the fact that any locally finite variety is tame we get:

Corollary 3.12
.

Let
\mathsf{Q}
be a quasivariety such that every finite
{\textbf{A}}\in\mathsf{Q}
is weakly
\mathsf{Q}
-projective. Then every locally finite subquasivariety of
\mathsf{Q}
is primitive.

We have another characterization of weak
\mathsf{Q}
-projectivity that gives rise to an interesting class of examples.

Lemma 3.13
.

Let
\mathsf{K}
be any class of algebras. Then the following are equivalent:

1.

every algebra in
\mathsf{K}
is weakly
\mathsf{K}
-projective;

2.

for all
{\textbf{A}}\in\mathsf{K}
and for any
\theta\in\textrm{Con}_{\mathsf{K}}({\textbf{A}})
there is and endomorphism
f
of
A
with
\theta=\textrm{ker}(f)
.

Moreover every algebra in
\mathsf{K}
is projective in
\mathsf{K}
if and only if
f
in (2) can always taken to be idempotent.

Proof.

Assume (1) and let
{\textbf{B}}\in\mathsf{K}
and
\theta\in\textrm{Con}_{\mathsf{K}}({\textbf{B}})
; then
{\textbf{A}}={\textbf{B}}/\theta\in\mathsf{K}
and
{\textbf{A}}\in{\mathbf{H}}({\textbf{B}})
.
By (1),
{\textbf{A}}\in{\mathbf{I}}{\mathbf{S}}({\textbf{B}})
; if
f
is the canonical epimorphism and
g
is the embedding, then
gf
is and endomorphism of
A
whose kernel is equal to
\theta
. Moreover if
B
is projective, then
gf
is the identity and so it is idempotent.

Conversely assume (2) let
{\textbf{A}}\in\mathsf{K}
such that
{\textbf{A}}\in{\mathbf{H}}({\textbf{B}})
for some
{\textbf{B}}\in\mathsf{K}
. Then
{\textbf{A}}\cong{\textbf{B}}/\theta
for some
\theta\in\textrm{Con}_{\mathsf{K}}({\textbf{B}})
and there is an endomorphism
f
of
B
with
\textrm{ker}(f)=\theta
. If
f({\textbf{B}})={\textbf{C}}
then

{\textbf{A}}\cong{\textbf{B}}/\theta\cong{\textbf{B}}/\textrm{ker}(f)\cong{\textbf{C}}\leq{\textbf{B}}.

So
{\textbf{A}}\in{\mathbf{I}}{\mathbf{S}}({\textbf{B}})
and it is weakly
\mathsf{K}
-projective. If
f
is idempotent then for all
b\in B
,
(b,f(b))\in\textrm{ker}(f)
; if
g:{\textbf{B}}\longrightarrow{\textbf{A}}
is the canonical epimorphism then
g(b)=g(f(b))
. If
h:{\textbf{C}}\longrightarrow{\textbf{A}}
is the isomorphism, then
h
is a monomorphism from
A
to
B
. If
a\in A
and
b\in B
with
g(b)=a
then

g(h(a))=g(f(b))=g(b)=a.

We have just proved that
A
is a retract of
B
, so
A
is projective in
\mathsf{K}
.
∎

Since every locally finite variety is tame from Theorem
3.10
we get:

Corollary 3.14
.

For any variety
\mathsf{Q}
the following are equivalent:

1.

every finite algebra in
\mathsf{Q}
is weakly
\mathsf{Q}_{fin}
-projective, where
\mathsf{Q}_{fin}
is the class of finite algebras in
\mathsf{Q}
;

2.

every locally finite subquasivariety of
\mathsf{Q}
is primitive.

Finally we observe that many of the known examples above have the property that every
\mathsf{Q}
-finitely presented
\mathsf{Q}
-irreducible algebra is projective in
\mathsf{Q}
, which is an (apparently) stronger condition than the one requested by Theorem
3.10
. This is because it seems plausible that being projective in a quasivariety
\mathsf{Q}
is a strictly stronger concept that being weakly
\mathsf{Q}
-projective (and indeed it is, see Example
3.17
below). However:

Theorem 3.15
.

Let
A
be a finite subdirectly irreducible algebra; if
A
is weakly projective in
{\mathbf{Q}}({\textbf{A}})
, then it is projective in
{\mathbf{Q}}({\textbf{A}})
.

Proof.

Let
\mathsf{Q}={\mathbf{Q}}({\textbf{A}})
; since
A
is finite,
\mathsf{Q}
is locally finite. Let
F
be a finitely generated (hence finite) free algebra in
\mathsf{Q}
such that
{\textbf{A}}\in{\mathbf{H}}({\textbf{F}})
; since
A
is weakly projective,
A
is embeddable in
F
and without loss of generality we may assume that
{\textbf{A}}\leq{\textbf{F}}
.
Consider the set

V=\{\alpha\in\textrm{Con}_{\mathsf{Q}}({\textbf{F}}):\alpha\cap A^{2}=0_{\textbf{A}}\},

where we denote by
0_{{\textbf{A}}}
the minimal congruence of
A
.
It is easy to see that
V
is an inductive poset so we may apply Zorn’s Lemma to find a maximal congruence
\theta\in V
. Clearly
a\longmapsto a/\theta
is an embedding of
A
into
{\textbf{F}}/\theta
. We claim that
{\textbf{F}}/\theta
is
\mathsf{Q}
-irreducible and to prove so, since everything is finite, it is enough to show that
\theta
is meet irreducible in
\textrm{Con}_{\mathsf{Q}}({\textbf{F}})
;
so let
\alpha,\beta\in\textrm{Con}_{\mathsf{Q}}({\textbf{A}})
such that
\alpha\wedge\beta=\theta
. Then

0_{\textbf{A}}=\theta\cap A^{2}=(\alpha\wedge\beta)\cap A^{2}=(\alpha\cap A^{2})\wedge(\beta\cap A^{2});

But
A
is subdirectly irreducible, so
0_{A}
is meet irreducible in
\textrm{Con}({\textbf{A}})
; hence either
\alpha\cap A^{2}=0_{\textbf{A}}
or
\beta\cap A^{2}=0_{\textbf{A}}
, so either
\alpha\in V
or
\beta\in V
. Since
\theta
is maximal in
V
,
either
\alpha=\theta
or
\beta=\theta
, which proves that
{\textbf{F}}/\theta
is relative subdirectly irreducible. Therefore, by Theorem
2.5
(2),
{\textbf{F}}/\theta\in{\mathbf{I}}{\mathbf{S}}({\textbf{A}})
; since
{\textbf{F}}/\theta
and
A
are both finite and
each one is embeddable in the other, they are in fact isomorphic. Thus
{\textbf{A}}\leq{\textbf{F}}
, and there is a homomorphism from
F
onto
A
that maps each
a\in A
to itself. This shows that
A
is a retract of
F
, and therefore
A
is projective in
{\mathbf{Q}}({\textbf{A}})
.
∎

We close this section with several examples

Example 3.16
.

(Fragments of Heyting algebras)
An early example of an application of Lemma
3.13
is in the seminal papers
[
55
]
,
[
56
]
and
[
57
]
.
Remember that for any Hilbert algebra
A
and
a,b,c\in A

a\rightarrow(b\rightarrow c)=(a\rightarrow b)\rightarrow(a\rightarrow c).

Let now
A
be a finite Hilbert algebra,
\theta\in\operatorname{Con}({\textbf{A}})
and
F=1/\theta
the filter associated with

\theta
. Since
A
is finite, so is
F
and hence we may assume that
F=\{a_{1},\dots,a_{n}\}
; define

\varphi(x)=a_{1}\rightarrow(a_{2}\rightarrow\dots\rightarrow(a_{n}\rightarrow x)).

Then clearly
\varphi
is an endomorphism of
A
; moreover if
\varphi(b)=1
then

a_{1}\rightarrow(a_{2}\rightarrow\dots\rightarrow(a_{n}\rightarrow b))=1

and since
a_{1},\dots,a_{n}\in F
we get by modus ponens that
b\in F
. Hence
\textrm{Ker}(\varphi)\subseteq F
; on the other hand
\textrm{Ker}(\varphi)
is a filter
and for any
i\leq n

\displaystyle\varphi(a_{i})

\displaystyle=a_{1}\rightarrow(a_{2}\rightarrow\dots\rightarrow(a_{n}\rightarrow a_{i}))

\displaystyle\geq a_{1}\rightarrow(a_{2}\rightarrow\dots\rightarrow(a_{n-1}\rightarrow a_{i}))

\displaystyle\quad\vdots

\displaystyle\geq a_{1}\rightarrow(a_{2}\rightarrow\dots\rightarrow(a_{i}\rightarrow a_{i}))=1

so
a_{1}\in\textrm{Ker}(\varphi)
for
i\leq n
. Hence
F=\textrm{Ker}(\varphi)
and every finite Hilbert algebra is projective (since
\varphi
is clearly idempotent) in the class of finite Hilbert algebras.
It follows that any locally finite subvariety of Hilbert algebras is primitive; but Hilbert algebras themselves are
locally finite
[
35
]
so any variety of Hilbert algebras is primitive (and so are their prelinear versions, i.e. varieties of Gödel BCK-algebras)).
All this follows of course also from Prucnal’s results (
[
56
]
,
[
57
]
).

The
{\rightarrow,\wedge}
fragment (i.e. Brouwerian semilattices) is easier since
(\rightarrow,\wedge)
form a residuated pair. Let
A
be a Brouwerian semilattice, then
for all
a,b,c\in A

a\rightarrow(b\wedge c)=(a\rightarrow b)\wedge(a\rightarrow c).

But Brouwerian semilattices are locally finite
[
47
]
, so every the variety of Brouwerian semilattices is primitive (and so is its prelinear version, i.e. varieties of Gödel hoops
[
5
]
).

If we add the join (i.e. we have Brouwerian lattices), then we run into problems since we can no longer prove that the map we want to define is an endomorphism.
However in
[
33
]
the second author proved that there are two Brouwerian lattices, called
{\textbf{S}}_{1}
and
{\textbf{S}}_{2}
such that, for any variety
\mathsf{V}
of Brouwerian lattices

\bullet

if
{\textbf{S}}_{1}\notin\mathsf{V}
, then
\mathsf{V}
is locally finite;

\bullet

if
{\textbf{S}}_{1},{\textbf{S}}_{2}\notin\mathsf{V}
, then every finite Brouwerian lattice in
\mathsf{V}
is projective in
\mathsf{V}_{fin}
;

\bullet

if either
{\textbf{S}}_{1}\in\mathsf{V}
or
{\textbf{S}}_{2}\in\mathsf{V}
, then
\mathsf{V}
is not primitive.

It follows that a variety of Brouwerian lattices is primitive if and only if it does not contain
{\textbf{S}}_{1}
and
{\textbf{S}}_{2}
; moreover
all such varieties are locally finite.

Primitive subvarieties of Heyting algebras have been totally characterized in
[
32
]
.
Other applications of Lemma
3.13
to (fragments of) commutative residuated lattices can be found in
[
7
]
; a more general theory encompassing these results is in Section
4.2
below.∎

Example 3.17
.

The only examples we know of a algebras that are
\mathsf{Q}
-weakly projective but not projective appeared very recently.
A
modal algebra
is a Boolean algebra with a modal operator
\Box
, that we take as a fundamental unary operation, satisfying
\Box 1\approx 1
and
\Box(x\wedge y)\approx\Box x\wedge\Box y

[

A modal algebra is a

if it satisfies

in

V.V. Rybakov classified all the primitive varieties of K4-algebras. However very recently
[

Upon reading the thesis the first author of this paper suggested that this probably depended on the fact that these varieties contained a weakly projective algebra that is not projective. And J. Carr proved exactly that (private communication): all the varieties missed by Rybakov contain a weakly projective algebra that is not projective.∎

Example 3.19
.

(Quasivarieties of abelian groups and modules) Since abelian groups are tame we can deal only with finitely generated abelian groups; the Fundamental Theorem states that a finitely generated abelian group is isomorphic to the direct product (direct sum really, but since there are only finitely many summands it is the same) of a finite power of
\mathbb{Z}
and finitely many finite cyclic groups. This implies at once that:

\bullet

the variety of abelian groups is
{\mathbf{V}}(\mathbb{Z})
(since all finitely generated abelian groups belong to
{\mathbf{V}}(\mathbb{Z})
);

\bullet

a subquasivariety of abelian groups is proper if and only if it does not contain
\mathbb{Z}
;

\bullet

every proper subvariety is finitely generated by finitely many cyclic groups.

Let’s justify the last argument; if
\mathsf{V}
is locally finite let
F
be the 1-generated free algebra in
\mathsf{V}
; then
F
is finite and cyclic and every cyclic group in
\mathsf{V}
is a homomorphic image of
F
.
As
F
is finite there are only finitely many of them and, by the Fundamental Theorem, they generate all the finitely generated groups in
\mathsf{V}
. Thus
\mathsf{V}
is generated by them and thus it is finitely generated.
Hence every proper subvariety of the variety of abelian groups is locally finite.

Moreover any locally finite variety of abelian groups is primitive; in fact every finite subdirectly irreducible abelian group is cyclic of order
p^{n}
for some prime
p
.
If
\mathsf{V}
is a locally finite variety of abelian groups, let
{\textbf{A}}\in\mathsf{V}
and let
f:{\textbf{A}}\longrightarrow\mathbb{Z}_{p^{n}}
be a onto homomorphism; if
b
is generator of
\mathbb{Z}_{p^{n}}
then any
a\in f^{-1}(b)
generates in
A
a finite cyclic group
C
of which
\mathbb{Z}_{p^{n}}
is a homomorphic image. But it is well-known that homomorphic images and subalgebras of a cyclic group coincide so really
\mathbb{Z}_{p^{n}}\in{\mathbf{I}}{\mathbf{S}}({\textbf{C}})
. Hence
\mathbb{Z}_{p^{n}}\in{\mathbf{I}}{\mathbf{S}}({\textbf{B}})
and it is weakly projective in
\mathsf{V}
. By Theorem
3.10

\mathsf{V}
is primitive.

Now observe that
\mathbb{Z}
is
torsion-free
so it satisfies all the quasi equations

nx\approx 0\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad x\approx 0.

(
\varepsilon_{n}
)

Hence
{\mathbf{Q}}(\mathbb{Z})
satisfies the same quasiequations and it is a proper subquasivariety; but of course
{\mathbf{H}}({\mathbf{Q}}(\mathbb{Z}))
is the entire variety of abelian groups, and so the variety of abelian groups cannot be structurally complete. So abelian groups form a variety in which every proper subvariety is primitive but the variety fails to be structurally complete. To close the circle we observe that also
{\mathbf{Q}}(\mathbb{Z})
is primitive since it is an atom in the lattice of subquasivarieties of abelian groups. To prove that, let
\mathsf{Q}
be a proper subquasivariety of
\mathsf{Q}(\mathbb{Z})
; then
\mathbb{Z}\notin\mathsf{Q}
. If
\mathsf{Q}
is nontrivial then it contains finitely generated nontrivial algebras and each of them is a direct product of cyclic groups; since any factor of a direct product is also a subalgebra
\mathsf{Q}
must contain at least a finite cyclic group. But any finite cyclic group does not satisfy
\varepsilon_{n}
for some (really infinitely many)
n
; thus
\mathsf{Q}
must be trivial and
{\mathbf{Q}}(\mathbb{Z})
is an atom in the lattices of subquasivarieties. Therefore it is primitive.

Finally there are no other primitive subquasivarieties of the variety of abelian groups, other the ones we described. First observe that the only locally finite primitive subquasivarieties are the locally finite subvarieties.
If
\mathsf{Q}
is a primitive proper subquasivariety of abelian groups that is not locally finite, then
\mathbb{Z}\in\mathsf{Q}
so
{\mathbf{Q}}(\mathbb{Z})\subseteq\mathsf{Q}
; since
\mathsf{Q}
is primitive it is structurally complete and thus every proper subquasivariety of
\mathsf{Q}
must generate a proper subvariety of abelian groups (Theorem
3.1
) . But
{\mathbf{Q}}(\mathbb{Z})
generates the entire variety of abelian groups so it cannot be proper; hence
{\mathbf{Q}}(\mathbb{Z})=\mathsf{Q}
.

Now an abelian group is a module over
\mathbb{Z}
seen as a principal ideal domain and not surprising most of what we said has been generalized to modules over a PID
[
12
]
and (at some cost) even to modules
over a Dedekind domain
[
44
]
. In general, if
\mathsf{M}_{\textbf{R}}
is the variety of modules over a Dedekind domain
R
,
the primitive subquasivarieties of
\mathsf{M}_{\textbf{R}}
are exactly the locally finite varieties and
{\mathbf{Q}}({\textbf{R}})
(regarded as an
R
-module over itself).∎

4
More on structural completeness

4.1
C
-completeness

The concept of
C
-completeness has been introduced by the second author in
[
31
]
.
Let
A
be any set; a
clone
of operations on
A
is a set of operations on
A
that contains all the projections and it is closed under composition (whenever possible). As the intersection of any family of clones is still a clone, it makes sense to talk about clone generation (that is of course a closure operator). If
A
is any algebra, then the
term clone
of
A
, denoted by
\textrm{Clo}({\textbf{A}})
is the clone on
A
generated by all the fundamental operations.

Let now
\mathsf{Q}
be a quasivariety; then the terms in the language of
\mathsf{Q}
can be seen as operations on
{\textbf{F}}_{\mathsf{Q}}(\omega)
, and the set of all terms is just the clone of all derived operations on
{\textbf{F}}_{\mathsf{Q}}(\omega)
, i.e. the clone on
{\textbf{F}}_{\mathsf{Q}}(\omega)
generated by all the fundamental operations. We will refer to it as the
term clone
of
\mathsf{Q}
and we will denote it by
\textrm{Clo}(\mathsf{Q})
. Let
C
be a subclone of
\textrm{Clo}(\mathsf{Q})
; a
C
-quasiequation
is a quasiequation containing only operations from
C
. We say that
\mathsf{Q}
is
C
-structurally complete if for every
C
-quasiequation
\Phi
,
if
{\textbf{F}}_{\mathsf{Q}}(\omega)\vDash\Phi
, then
\mathsf{Q}\vDash\Phi
. A quasivariety is
C
-primitive if all its subquasivarieties are
C
-structurally complete.
Observe that if
C^{\prime}
is a subclone of
C
and
\mathsf{Q}
is
C
-structurally complete (
C
primitive), then
\mathsf{Q}
is
C^{\prime}
-structurally complete (
C^{\prime}
-primitive).
Observe also that if
T
is a set of generators for
C
, it is easy to check that
\mathsf{Q}
is
C
-structurally complete if and only if for every quasiequation
\Phi
containing only operations from
T
,

{\textbf{F}}_{\mathsf{Q}}(\omega)\vDash\Phi
entails
\mathsf{Q}\vDash\Phi
. Therefore if
T
is a set of terms that generates
C
we may talk about
T
-structural completeness and
T
-primitivity, meaning the corresponding concept for the clone generated by
T
. If
C
is the entire term clone of
\mathsf{Q}
then
C
-structural completeness is the usual structural completeness.

Let
\mathsf{Q}
be a quasivariety and let
C
a subclone of the term clone of
\mathsf{Q}
. Let
\mathsf{Q}^{C}
be the class of all
C
-subreducts of algebras in
\mathsf{Q}
; then it is easily seen that
\mathsf{Q}^{C}
is a quasivariety in which all the
C
-quasiequation holding in
\mathsf{Q}
are valid. So if
\mathsf{Q}^{C}
is structurally complete or primitive, then
\mathsf{Q}
is
C
-structurally complete or
C
-primitive. For instance if
\mathsf{H}
is the variety of Heyting algebras, then its
\{\wedge,\rightarrow\}
-subreducts form the variety of Brouwerian semilattices, that is (as we have already observed) primitive; thus
\mathsf{H}
is
\{\wedge,\rightarrow\}
-primitive.

The converse however fails to hold; the variety
\mathsf{H}
of Heyting algebras is
\{\rightarrow,\neg\}
-structurally complete
[
51
]
but the quasivariety of its
\{\rightarrow,\neg\}
-subreducts is not structurally complete
[
28
]
. The problem is that there is a
\{\rightarrow,\neg\}
-quasiequation that is valid in the in
{\textbf{F}}_{\mathsf{H}^{\{\rightarrow,\neg\}}}(\omega)
but it is not valid in
{\textbf{F}}_{\mathsf{H}}(\omega)
.

4.2
u-presentability and Prucnal terms

Let
A
be any algebra,
\theta\in\textrm{Con}({\textbf{A}})
and let
C
be a subclone of
\textrm{Clo}({\textbf{A}})
; by
{\textbf{A}}^{C}
we denote the algebra whose universe is
A
and whose fundamental operations are those in
C
. We say that
\theta
is
u-presentable
relative to
C
if there is a set
\Delta\subseteq\textrm{Con}({\textbf{A}})
such that

1.

\theta=\bigcup\Delta
;

2.

\Delta
is closed under finite joins;

3.

{\textbf{A}}^{C}/\delta\in{\mathbf{I}}{\mathbf{S}}{\mathbf{P}}_{u}({\textbf{A}}^{C})
for all
\delta\in\Delta
.

In this case
\Delta
is called a
u-presentation
of
\theta
relative to
C
.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
A
be an algebra,
\theta\in\operatorname{Con}({\textbf{A}})
and
C
a subclone of
\textrm{Clo}({\textbf{A}})
; then
the following are equivalent:

1.

\theta
is u-presentable relative to
C
;

2.

{\textbf{A}}^{C}/\theta\in{\mathbf{I}}{\mathbf{S}}{\mathbf{P}}_{u}({\textbf{A}}^{C})
.

Proof.

Obviously (2) implies (1). Let then
\mathsf{U}={\mathbf{I}}{\mathbf{S}}{\mathbf{P}}_{u}({\textbf{A}}^{C})
and let
\Delta
be a u-presentation of
\theta
relative to
C
. For any
\delta\in\Delta_{\theta}
, let
L_{\delta}\{\varepsilon\in\Delta:\delta\leq\varepsilon\}
. By the definition of u-presentability if
\delta,\delta^{\prime}\in\Delta
, then
\delta\vee\delta^{\prime}\in\Delta
, hence

\{L_{\delta}:\delta\in\Delta\}
has the finite intersection property. Therefore there exists an ultrafilter
U
on
\Delta
such that
L_{\delta}\in U
for all
\delta\in\Delta
.

Consider the map

f:a\longmapsto(a/\delta)_{\delta\in\Delta}/U;

this is clearly a homomorphism from
{\textbf{A}}^{C}
to
\prod_{d\in\Delta}{\textbf{A}}^{C}/\delta
. We claim that
\textrm{ker}(f)=\theta
. In fact if
(a/\delta)_{\delta\in\Delta}/U=(b/\delta)_{\delta\in\Delta}/U
, then
\{\delta:a/\delta=\beta/\delta\}\in U
.
If
(a,b)\notin\theta
then, as
\theta=\bigcup\Delta
,
(a,b)\notin\delta
for all
\delta\in J
; this implies that
\{\delta:a/\delta=\beta/\delta\}=\emptyset
which contradicts the fact that
U
is an ultrafilter.
Hence
(a,b)\in\theta
and
\theta=\textrm{ker}(f)
.

Conversely assume that
(a,b)\in\theta
; then
(a,b)\in\delta^{\prime}
for some
\delta^{\prime}\in\Delta
. It follows that
(a,b)\in\varepsilon
for all
\varepsilon\in L_{\delta^{\prime}}
and thus

L_{\delta^{\prime}}\subseteq\{\delta:a/\delta=b/\delta\}.

As
U
extends
\{L_{\delta}:\delta\in J\}
, the right hand side of the above inclusion belongs to
U
, which means that
(a/\delta)_{\delta\in J}/U=(b/\delta)_{\delta\in J}/U
as wished.
Therefore
{\textbf{A}}^{C}/\delta\in{\mathbf{I}}{\mathbf{S}}{\mathbf{P}}_{u}({\textbf{A}}^{C})
.
∎

Corollary 4.2
.

For any algebra
A
and
C\subseteq\textrm{Clo}({\textbf{A}})
the following are equivalent:

1.

every congruence of
A
is u-presentable relative to
C
;

2.

every compact congruence of
A
is
u
-presentable relative to
C
.

Proof.

(1) trivially implies (2). Let
\theta\in\operatorname{Con}({\textbf{A}})
and let
\Delta_{\theta}=\{\delta\in\operatorname{Con}({\textbf{A}}):\delta\leq\theta,\ \text{$\delta$ compact}\}
.
Then
\Delta_{\theta}
is closed under finite joins an
\bigcup\Delta_{\theta}=\theta
. Moreover by Theorem
4.1
,
{\textbf{A}}^{C}/\delta\in{\mathbf{I}}{\mathbf{S}}{\mathbf{P}}_{u}({\textbf{A}}^{C})
for all

\delta\in\Delta_{\theta}
. Therefore
\theta
is u-presentable relative to
C
.
∎

Now we can connect u-presentability and structural completeness, but first we need a lemma.

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
\mathsf{Q}
be a quasivariety and let

\Phi:=\bigwedge_{i=1}^{n}r_{i}(\mathbf{x})\approx s_{i}(\mathbf{x})\Rightarrow r(\mathbf{x})\approx s(\mathbf{x})

be a quasiequation. Then
\mathsf{Q}\vDash\Phi
if and only if
{\textbf{A}}\vDash\Phi
where
A
is an algebra in
\mathsf{Q}
finitely presented by the relations

r_{i}(\mathbf{x})\approx s_{i}(\mathbf{x})
,
i=1,\dots,n
.

Proof.

The left-to-right implication is trivial. Suppose that
{\textbf{A}}\vDash\Phi
and assume by way of contradiction
that there is
{\textbf{B}}\in\mathsf{Q}
with
{\textbf{B}}\not\vDash\Phi
.
Then there are
b_{1},\dots,b_{n}\in B
such that

r_{i}(b_{1},\dots,b_{n})=s_{i}(b_{1},\dots,b_{n})\quad\text{ for all $i$}

but
r(b_{1},\dots,b_{n})\neq s(b_{1},\dots,b_{n})
.

As
A
is finitely presented and generated by
a_{1},\dots,a_{n}
we can extend the
map
f:a_{i}\longmapsto b_{i}
to a homomorphism
f:{\textbf{A}}\longrightarrow{\textbf{B}}
. By assumption,
{\textbf{A}}\vDash\Phi
and thus
r(a_{1},\dots,a_{n})=s(a_{1},\dots,a_{n})
.
Therefore

\displaystyle r(b_{1},\dots,b_{n})

\displaystyle=r(f(a_{1}),\dots,f(a_{n}))

\displaystyle=f(r(a_{1},\dots,a_{n}))=f(s(a_{1},\dots,a_{n}))

\displaystyle=s(f(a_{1}),\dots,f(a_{n}))=s(b_{1},\dots,b_{n}).

So
{\textbf{B}}\vDash\Phi
, a clear contradiction.
∎

Corollary 4.4
.

Let
\mathsf{Q}
be a quasivariety and
\Phi
be a quasiequation. Then
\mathsf{Q}\vDash\Phi
if and only if for every compact
\mathsf{Q}
-congruence

\theta
of
{\textbf{F}}_{\mathsf{Q}}(\omega)
we have
{\textbf{F}}_{\mathsf{Q}}(\omega)/\theta\vDash\Phi
.

Theorem 4.5
.

Let
\mathsf{Q}
be a quasivariety,
{\textbf{A}}\in\mathsf{Q}
and
C
a clone of operations of
\mathsf{Q}
; if every compact congruence of
{\textbf{F}}_{\mathsf{Q}}(\omega)
is u-presentable relative to
C
, then
\mathsf{Q}
is
C
-structurally complete.

Proof.

Let
\Phi
be a
C
-quasiequation and suppose that
\mathsf{Q}\not\vDash\Phi
; then by Corollary
4.4
there exists a compact
\mathsf{Q}
-congruence
\theta
of
{\textbf{F}}_{\mathsf{Q}}(\omega)
such that
{\textbf{F}}_{\mathsf{Q}}(\omega)/\theta\not\vDash\Phi
. Since
\theta
is u-presentable relative to
C
,
{\textbf{F}}^{C}_{\mathsf{Q}}/\theta
embeds in an ultrapower of
{\textbf{F}}^{C}_{\mathsf{Q}}(\omega)
and so
{\textbf{F}}^{C}_{\mathsf{Q}}(\omega)\not\vDash\Phi
. As
\Phi
is a
C
-quasiequation
{\textbf{F}}_{\mathsf{Q}}(\omega)\not\vDash\Phi
,
hence the thesis holds.
∎

Given a quasivariety
\mathsf{Q}
and a countably generated algebra
{\textbf{A}}\in\mathsf{Q}
, it is well-known that there is an epimorphism
\varphi
from
\textrm{Clo}({\textbf{F}}_{\mathsf{Q}}(\omega))

\textrm{Clo}({\textbf{A}})

.

Corollary 4.6
.

Let
\mathsf{Q}
be a quasivariety,
{\textbf{A}}\in\mathsf{Q}
and
C
a clone of operations of
\mathsf{Q}
; if every compact
\mathsf{Q}
-congruence of every countably generated algebra in
\mathsf{Q}
is u-presentable with respect to

, then
\mathsf{Q}
is
C
-primitive.

Proof.

It is enough to observe that if
\mathsf{Q}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathsf{Q}
, then
{\textbf{F}}_{\mathsf{Q}^{\prime}}(\omega)
is a countably generated algebra in
\mathsf{Q}
.
∎

It is interesting to observe that if
C
is the entire term clone of
\mathsf{Q}
, then we obtain a new necessary and sufficient condition.

Theorem 4.7
.

A quasivariety
\mathsf{Q}
is structurally complete if and only if every completely meet irreducible congruence
\theta\in\textrm{Con}_{\mathsf{Q}}({\textbf{F}}_{\mathsf{Q}}(\omega))
is u-presentable.

Proof.

If
\mathsf{Q}
is structurally complete, then
\mathsf{Q}={\mathbf{Q}}({\textbf{F}}_{\mathsf{Q}}(\omega))
and therefore every
\mathsf{Q}
-irreducible algebra is in
{\mathbf{I}}{\mathbf{S}}{\mathbf{P}}_{u}({\textbf{F}}_{\mathsf{Q}}(\omega))
. It follows that every completely meet irreducible congruence of
{\textbf{F}}_{\mathsf{Q}}(\omega)
is u-presentable.

Conversely if (2) holds then, by Theorem
4.1
, if
\theta
is a completely meet irreducible congruence of
{\textbf{F}}_{\mathsf{Q}}(\omega)
then
{\textbf{F}}_{\mathsf{Q}}(\omega)/\theta\in{\mathbf{I}}{\mathbf{S}}{\mathbf{P}}_{u}({\textbf{F}}_{\mathsf{Q}}(\omega))
. Every
\mathsf{Q}
-irreducible algebra
A
is embeddable in an ultraproduct of finitely generated subalgebras and each one of them is a subdirect product of finitely generated
\mathsf{Q}
-irreducible algebras that are necessarily equal to
{\textbf{F}}_{\mathsf{Q}}(\omega)/\delta
for some completely meet irreducible congruence
of
{\textbf{F}}_{\mathsf{Q}}(\omega)
. Then each one of them is in
{\mathbf{I}}{\mathbf{S}}{\mathbf{P}}_{u}({\textbf{F}}_{\mathsf{Q}}(\omega))
and so does
A
, since it is
\mathsf{Q}
-irreducible. It follows that
\mathsf{Q}={\mathbf{Q}}({\textbf{F}}_{\mathsf{Q}}(\omega))
and hence
\mathsf{Q}
is structurally complete.
∎

Corollary 4.8
.

A quasivariety
\mathsf{Q}
is primitive if and only if for every countably generated
{\textbf{A}}\in\mathsf{Q}
every completely meet irreducible
\theta\in\textrm{Con}_{\mathsf{Q}}({\textbf{A}})
is u-presentable.

\mathsf{Q}

\displaystyle t_{n-1}(\overline{\mathbf{a}}^{n-1},\overline{\mathbf{b}}{}^{n-1},\overline{c})=t_{n-1}(\overline{\mathbf{a}}^{n-1},\overline{\mathbf{b}}{}^{n-1},\overline{d})\quad\text{if and only if}

\displaystyle t_{n-1}(\overline{a}^{n-1},\overline{b}^{n-1},\overline{d})d\quad\mathrel{\vartheta_{\textbf{A}}^{\mathsf{Q}}(a_{n},b_{n})}\quad t_{n-1}(\overline{a}^{n-1},\overline{b}^{n-1},d)\quad\text{if and only if}

\displaystyle t(a_{n},b_{n},t_{n-1}(\overline{a}^{n-1},\overline{b}^{n-1},c))=t(a_{n},b_{n},t_{n-1}(\overline{a}^{n-1},\overline{b}^{n-1},d))\quad\text{if and only if}

\displaystyle t_{n}({\mathbf{a}}^{n},{\mathbf{b}}^{n},c)=t_{n}({\mathbf{a}}^{n},{\mathbf{b}}^{n},d).

∎

We observe that Lemma
4.13
is a generalization of Theorem 2.6 in
[
18
]
.

Corollary 4.14
.

If a quasivariety
\mathsf{Q}
has a principal Prucnal term relative to
C
, then it has the Prucnal property relative to
C
.

Proof.

The terms
t_{n},n\geq 1
clearly satisfy the first condition for Prucnal terms, as iterated compositions of
t
. By Lemma
4.13
they also satisfy the second.
∎

4.3
The relative TD-term

Here we will consider a special principal Prucnal term.
Let
\mathsf{Q}
be a quasivariety; a
relative TD-term
for
\mathsf{Q}
is a ternary term
t(x,y,z)
such that

\bullet

\mathsf{Q}\vDash t(x,x,z)
;

\bullet

if
(c,d)\in\vartheta_{\textbf{A}}^{\mathsf{Q}}(a,b)
then
t(a,b,c)=t(a,b,d)
.

Let
\mathsf{Q}
be a quasivariety with a relative TD-term
t(x,y,z)
and
q
a
k
-term of
\mathsf{Q}
; we say that
q
commutes with
t
if for all
{\textbf{A}}\in\mathsf{Q}
and
a,b,c_{1},\dots,c_{k}\in A

t(a,b,q(c_{1},\dots,c_{k}))=q(t(a,b,c_{1}),\dots,t(a,b,c_{k})).

Theorem 4.15
.

If
t(x,y,z)
is a relative TD-term for
\mathsf{Q}
, then
t
is a principal Prucnal term relative to any clone
C
of operations that commute with

t(x,y,z)
.

Proof.

Let
{\textbf{A}}\in\mathsf{Q}
and let
a,b\in A
and consider the mapping

z\longmapsto t(a,b,z).

If
q\in C
and
c_{1},\dots,c_{k}\in A
, then

t(a,b,q(c_{1},\dots,c_{k}))=q(t(a,b,c_{1}),\dots,t(a,b,c_{k})),

so the mapping is an endomorphism of
{\textbf{A}}^{C}
.

Now if
(c,d)\in\vartheta_{\textbf{A}}^{\mathsf{Q}}(a,b)
then
t(a,b,c)=t(a,b,d)
by definition; conversely if
t(a,b,c)=t(a,b,d)
then

c=t(a,a,c)\mathrel{\vartheta_{\textbf{A}}^{\mathsf{Q}}(a,b)}t(a,b,c)=t(a,b,d)\mathrel{\vartheta_{\textbf{A}}^{\mathsf{Q}}(a,b)}t(a,a,d)=d.

∎

Corollary 4.16
.

Let
\mathsf{Q}
be a quasivariety with a relative TD-term
t(x,y,z)
. Then for all nontrivial
{\textbf{A}}\in\mathsf{Q}
,
A
has the Prucnal property for
A
, relative to any clone
C
of operations that commute with the relative TD-term.

Corollary 4.17
.

Let
\mathsf{Q}
be a quasivariety with a relative TD-term; then
\mathsf{Q}
is
C
-primitive for any clone
C
of terms that commute with the relative TD-term.

The notion of relative TD-term generalizes to quasivarieties the notion of TD-term in
[
18
]
and the results in this section
generalizes to quasivarieties the results in
[
31
]
. Some of the others results in
[
18
]
can be generalized to quasivarieties with a relative TD-term.

A quasivariety has
definable principal relative congruences
(DPRC) if there is a first order formula
\Phi(x,y,z,w)
in the language of of
\mathsf{Q}
such that for all
A
in
\mathsf{Q}

.

.

If
\mathsf{Q}

then

for all

The proofs are easily patterned after the corresponding proofs in

5
Other primitive quasivarieties

5.1
Congruence intersection terms

There are quasivarieties not necessarily having Prucnal terms to which some of the techniques in the previous sections can be applied (at a cost).
We will say that a quasivariety has the
relative principal intersection property
(RPIP) if there are quaternary terms
p,q
in the type of
\mathsf{Q}
such that for any
{\textbf{A}}\in\mathsf{Q}

\vartheta^{\mathsf{Q}}_{\textbf{A}}(a,b)\cap\vartheta^{\mathsf{Q}}(c,d)=\vartheta_{\textbf{A}}^{\mathsf{Q}}(p(a,b,c,d),q(a,b,c,d)).

The RPIP for varieties was introduced by K. Baker in
[
11
]
and studied extensively in
[
2
]
. We observe that if
\textrm{Con}_{\mathsf{Q}}({\textbf{A}})
is distributive, then (as any complete distributive lattice) it is
pseudocomplemented
: in particular if
{\textbf{A}}\in\mathsf{Q}
, then for any
\theta\in\textrm{Con}_{\mathsf{Q}}({\textbf{A}})
there is a
\theta^{*}\in\textrm{Con}_{\mathsf{Q}}({\textbf{A}})
such that

\theta^{*}=\bigvee\{\varphi\in\textrm{Con}_{\mathsf{Q}}({\textbf{A}}):\theta\wedge\varphi=0_{\textbf{A}}\}.

In particular, if
\textrm{MI}^{\mathsf{Q}}_{\textbf{A}}
is the set of meet irreducible elements in
\textrm{Con}_{\mathsf{Q}}({\textbf{A}})
and
a,b\in A
, then

(\vartheta_{\textbf{A}}^{\mathsf{Q}}(a,b))^{*}=\bigwedge\{\varphi\in\textrm{MI}_{\textbf{A}}^{\mathsf{Q}}:(a,b)\notin\varphi\}.

From now on we will denote by
\gamma_{\textbf{A}}^{\mathsf{Q}}(a,b)
the pseudocomplement of
\vartheta_{\textbf{A}}^{\mathsf{Q}}(a,b)
in
\textrm{Con}_{\mathsf{Q}}({\textbf{A}})
.

Theorem 5.1
.

For a quasivariety
\mathsf{Q}
the following are equivalent:

1.

\mathsf{Q}
has the RPIP;

2.

\mathsf{Q}
is relative congruence distributive and there are terms
p,q
such that for all
\mathsf{Q}
-irreducible
{\textbf{A}}\in\mathsf{Q}
and
a,b,c,d\in A

p(a,b,c,d)=q(a,b,c,d)\quad\text{if and only if}\quad\text{$a=b$ or $c=d$}.

3.

\mathsf{Q}
is relative congruence distributive and there are terms
p,q
such that for all
{\textbf{A}}\in\mathsf{Q}
and
a,b\in A

\gamma_{\textbf{A}}^{\mathsf{Q}}(a,b)=\{(c,d):p(a,b,c,d)=q(a,b,c,d)\}.

Proof.

Observe that any quasivariety with the RPIP is relatively congruence distributive, by Proposition 1.2 in
[
34
]
.
Let’s assume (1), i.e.
\mathsf{Q}
has the RPIP witnessed by
p,q
; let
{\textbf{A}}\in\mathsf{Q}
,
a,b\in A
and

\alpha=\{(c,d):p(a,b,c,d)=q(a,b,c,d)\}.

Then we have

\displaystyle(c,d)\in\alpha\quad\text{if and only if}

\displaystyle p(a,b,c,d)=q(a,b,c,d)\quad\text{if and only if}

\displaystyle\vartheta_{\textbf{A}}\mathsf{Q}(a,b)\cap\vartheta_{\textbf{A}}^{\mathsf{Q}}(c,d)=0_{\textbf{A}}\quad\text{if and only if}

\displaystyle\vartheta_{\textbf{A}}^{\mathsf{Q}}(c,d)\leq\gamma_{\textbf{A}}^{\mathsf{Q}}(a,b)\quad\text{if and only if}

\displaystyle(c,d)\in\gamma_{\textbf{A}}^{\mathsf{Q}}(a,b)

so
\alpha=\gamma_{\textbf{A}}^{\mathsf{Q}}(a,b)
and (1) implies (3).

Next assume (3) and let
A
be a
\mathsf{Q}
-irreducible algebra in
\mathsf{Q}
. Then

\gamma_{\textbf{A}}^{\mathsf{Q}}(a,b)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}1_{\textbf{A}},&\hbox{if $a=b$;}\\
0_{\textbf{A}},&\hbox{if $a\neq b$.}\end{array}\right.

Suppose that
p(a,b,c,d)=q(a,b,c,d)
with
a\neq b
; then
(c,d)\in\gamma_{\textbf{A}}^{\mathsf{Q}}(a,b)
and thus
c=d
.
Conversely, if
a=b
then
\gamma_{\textbf{A}}^{\mathsf{Q}}(a,b)=1_{\textbf{A}}
so
p(a,b,c,d)=q(a,b,c,d)
for all
c,d
; if
c=d
then
(c,d)\in\gamma_{\textbf{A}}^{\mathsf{Q}}(a,b)

and so again
p(a,b,c,d)=q(a,b,c,d)
. Therefore (3) implies (2).

Finally assume (2). We will make use of the following fact whose proof can be found in
[
34
]
: for any
\theta\in\textrm{Con}_{\mathsf{Q}}({\textbf{A}})
and
a,b\in A
,
(\theta\vee\theta^{\mathsf{Q}}_{\textbf{A}}(a,b))/\theta=\vartheta^{\mathsf{Q}}_{{\textbf{A}}/\theta}(a/\theta,b/\theta)
. Let then
a,b,c,d\in A
; we must prove that

\vartheta^{\mathsf{Q}}_{\textbf{A}}(a,b)\cap\vartheta^{\mathsf{Q}}(c,d)=\vartheta_{\textbf{A}}^{\mathsf{Q}}(p(a,b,c,d),q(a,b,c,d)).

Since
\textrm{Con}_{\mathsf{Q}}({\textbf{A}})
is an algebraic lattice, every element is a meet of completely meet irreducible elements. So to prove (1), it is enough to show that, if
\varphi
is completely meet irreducible in
\textrm{Con}_{\mathsf{Q}}({\textbf{A}})
then

\vartheta^{\mathsf{Q}}_{\textbf{A}}(a,b)\cap\vartheta^{\mathsf{Q}}(c,d)\leq\varphi\quad\text{if and only if}\quad\vartheta_{\textbf{A}}^{\mathsf{Q}}(p(a,b,c,d),q(a,b,c,d))\leq\varphi.

So suppose that
\vartheta^{\mathsf{Q}}_{\textbf{A}}(a,b)\cap\vartheta^{\mathsf{Q}}(c,d)\leq\varphi
; as
\varphi
is completely meet irreducible and
\textrm{Con}_{\mathsf{Q}}({\textbf{A}})
is distributive,
\varphi
is join prime, so either
\vartheta^{\mathsf{Q}}_{\textbf{A}}(a,b)\leq\varphi
or
\vartheta^{\mathsf{Q}}(c,d)\leq\varphi
. Then in
{\textbf{A}}/\varphi
we have

\displaystyle\vartheta^{\mathsf{Q}}_{{\textbf{A}}/\varphi}(a/\theta,b/\varphi)

\displaystyle\cap\vartheta^{\mathsf{Q}}_{{\textbf{A}}/\theta}(c/\varphi,d/\varphi)

\displaystyle=(\varphi\vee\theta^{\mathsf{Q}}_{\textbf{A}}(a,b))/\varphi\cap(\varphi\vee\theta^{\mathsf{Q}}_{\textbf{A}}(a,b))/\varphi

\displaystyle=\varphi/\varphi=0_{{\textbf{A}}/\varphi}.

Since
{\textbf{A}}/\varphi
is
\mathsf{Q}
-irreducible
p(a,b,c,d)/\varphi=q(a,b,c,d)/\varphi
which implies

\vartheta_{\textbf{A}}^{\mathsf{Q}}(p(a,b,c,d),q(a,b,c,d))\leq\varphi.

Conversely if
\vartheta_{\textbf{A}}^{\mathsf{Q}}(p(a,b,c,d),q(a,b,c,d))\leq\varphi
, then

\displaystyle\vartheta_{{\textbf{A}}/\varphi}^{\mathsf{Q}}(p(a,b,c,d)/

\displaystyle\varphi,q(a,b,c,d)/\varphi)

\displaystyle=(\varphi\vee\vartheta_{\textbf{A}}^{\mathsf{Q}}(p(a,b,c,d),q(a,b,c,d)))/\varphi=\varphi/\varphi=0_{{\textbf{A}}/\varphi}

so, since
{\textbf{A}}/\varphi
is
\mathsf{Q}
-irreducible, either
(a,b)\in\varphi
or
(c,d)\in\varphi
, which implies
\vartheta_{\textbf{A}}^{\mathsf{Q}}(a,b)\cap\vartheta_{\textbf{A}}^{\mathsf{Q}}(c,d)\leq\varphi
as wished.
So (3) implies (1) and the proof is finished.
∎

Let
\mathsf{Q}
be a quasivariety with the RPIP (and hence relative congruence distributive),
{\textbf{A}}\in\mathsf{Q}
and let
L
be the lattice of compact
\mathsf{Q}
-congruences of
A
plus eventually
1_{\textbf{A}}
. Then one can define a topology on
\textrm{MI}_{\textbf{A}}^{\mathsf{Q}}
which is equivalent to the Priestley duality on the lattice of ideals
L
(see
[
25
]
, Proposition 3.10). More precisely the topology
is defined by the subbase
\textrm{MI}_{\textbf{A}}^{\mathsf{Q}}(a,b)=\{\theta\in\textrm{MI}_{\textbf{A}}:(a,b)\notin\theta\}
, for
a,b\in A
. It follows that
\textrm{MI}_{\textbf{A}}^{\mathsf{Q}}
is compact in that topology which in turn implies (see Lemma 2.1 in
[
63
]
):

Lemma 5.2
.

Let
\mathsf{Q}
be a quasivariety with the RPIP and
{\textbf{A}}\in\mathsf{Q}
; then if
\bigwedge\textrm{MI}_{\textbf{A}}^{\mathsf{Q}}(a,b)\leq\gamma
, where
\gamma\in\textrm{MI}^{\mathsf{Q}}_{\textbf{A}}
, then there
exists a
\theta\in\textrm{MI}_{\textbf{A}}^{\mathsf{Q}}(a,b)
with
\theta\leq\gamma
.

Theorem 5.3
.

Let
\mathsf{Q}
be a quasivariety with the RPIP such that
every finitely
\mathsf{Q}
-irreducible algebra in
\mathsf{Q}
is
\mathsf{Q}
-simple. Then the following hold.

1.

For any
{\textbf{A}}\in\mathsf{Q}
and
a,b\in A
,
\vartheta_{\textbf{A}}^{\mathsf{Q}}(a,b)
and
\gamma_{\textbf{A}}^{\mathsf{Q}}(a,b)
are complements; therefore
the meet of two coprincipal
\mathsf{Q}
-congruences of
A
is coprincipal.

2.

If
\theta\in\textrm{Con}_{\mathsf{Q}}({\textbf{A}})
is such that
{\textbf{A}}/\theta
is
\mathsf{Q}
-simple, then

\bigcup\{\gamma_{\textbf{A}}^{\mathsf{Q}}(a,b):\gamma_{\textbf{A}}^{\mathsf{Q}}(a,b)\leq\theta\}=\theta.

Proof.

By hypothesis every element of
\textrm{MI}_{\textbf{A}}^{\mathsf{Q}}
is a maximal element in in
\textrm{Con}_{\mathsf{Q}}({\textbf{A}})
. Let
\gamma\geq\gamma_{\textbf{A}}^{\mathsf{Q}}(a,b)
with
\gamma\in\textrm{MI}_{\textbf{A}}^{\mathsf{Q}}
; then

\bigwedge\textrm{MI}_{\textbf{A}}^{\mathsf{Q}}(a,b)=\gamma_{\textbf{A}}^{\mathsf{Q}}(a,b)\leq\gamma

so by Lemma
5.2
there is a nontop
\theta\in\textrm{MI}_{\textbf{A}}^{\mathsf{Q}}(a,b)
with
\theta\leq\gamma
. By maximality of
\theta
we must have
\theta=\gamma
and thus
(a,b)\notin\gamma
.

Now suppose that
\vartheta_{\textbf{A}}^{\mathsf{Q}}(a,b)\vee\gamma_{\textbf{A}}^{\mathsf{Q}}(a,b)\neq 1_{\textbf{A}}
; then there must be a maximal congruence
\gamma
with
\vartheta_{\textbf{A}}^{\mathsf{Q}}(a,b)\vee\gamma_{\textbf{A}}^{\mathsf{Q}}(a,b)\leq\gamma
; as
\gamma\in\textrm{MI}_{\textbf{A}}^{\mathsf{Q}}
and

\gamma\geq\gamma_{\textbf{A}}(a,b)
we must have
(a,b)\notin\gamma
, a clear contradiction. So
\vartheta_{\textbf{A}}^{\mathsf{Q}}(a,b)\vee\gamma_{\textbf{A}}^{\mathsf{Q}}(a,b)=1_{\textbf{A}}
and the first part of the thesis follows.

That the meet of two coprincipal
\mathsf{Q}
-congruences of
A
is coprincipal comes straight from the properties of complemented elements in a distributive lattice and this concludes the proof of (1).

For (2) we will show that for all
\vartheta_{\textbf{A}}^{\mathsf{Q}}(a,b)\leq\theta
we have
\gamma_{\textbf{A}}^{\mathsf{Q}}(a,b)\leq\theta
. As

\bigcup\{\vartheta_{\textbf{A}}^{\mathsf{Q}}(a,b):\vartheta_{\textbf{A}}^{\mathsf{Q}}(a,b)\leq\theta\}=\theta
the conclusion will follow.

If
\vartheta_{\textbf{A}}(a,b)\leq\theta
then
\vartheta_{\textbf{A}}(a,b)\vee\gamma_{\textbf{A}}(a,b)=1_{\textbf{A}}
and, since
\theta
is covered by
1_{\textbf{A}}
there
exists
(c,d)\in\gamma_{\textbf{A}}(a,b)
with
(c,d)\notin\theta
.
Now
\vartheta_{\textbf{A}}(c,d)\leq\gamma_{\textbf{A}}(a,b)
so
\vartheta_{\textbf{A}}(a,b)\leq\gamma_{\textbf{A}}(c,d)
; moreover

\vartheta_{\textbf{A}}(c,d)\cap\gamma_{\textbf{A}}(c,d)=0_{\textbf{A}}\leq\theta
and, as
\theta
is meet prime (since it is meet irreducible and
\textrm{Con}_{\mathsf{Q}}A
is distributive),
we must have
\gamma{\textbf{A}}(c,d)\leq\theta
.
∎

5.2
Dual i-discriminator varieties

In the late 1990’s K. Baker and the second author collaborated for some time (
[
2
]
,
[
3
]
; while writing the second paper an interest arose about various kinds of dual discriminator functions and produced an unpublished (and very rough) manuscript
[
4
]
, that had a very limited circulation at the time (but some of the results have been rediscovered in
[
14
]
). In this section we improve on those results and we show how they connect to the problems we are considering.

The
dual i-discriminator function
on a set
A
is a ternary function
p(x,y,z)
such that for any
a,b,c\in A

\displaystyle a\neq b\quad\text{implies}\quad p(a,b,c)=c

\displaystyle p(a,a,b)=p(a,a,c)

\displaystyle p(a,a,a)=p(p(a,a,a),p(a,a,a),p(a,a,a)).

If we write
\pi(x)=p(x,x,x)
, this definition becomes:

\pi(\pi(a))=\pi(a)\quad\text{and}\quad p(a,b,c)=\begin{cases}\pi(a)&\text{if $a=b$}\\
c&\text{if $a\neq b$}\end{cases}.

In case
\pi(x)
is the identity, then the dual i-discriminator function is just the dual discriminator function described in
[
38
]
. We first observe the following easy fact:

Lemma 5.4
.

If
A
is an algebra and
p(x,y,z)
is a dual i-discriminator term for
A
, then
A
is simple.

Proof.

If
\theta\in\operatorname{Con}({\textbf{A}})
and there are
(a,b)\in\theta
with
a\neq b
, then for all
c,d\in A

c=p(a,b,c)\mathrel{\theta}p(a,a,c)=p(a,a,d)\mathrel{\theta}p(a,b,d)=d.

Therefore
\theta=1_{\textbf{A}}
and
A
is simple.
∎

A quasivariety
\mathsf{Q}
is a
dual i-discriminator
quasivariety if there is term
p(x,y,z)
that is the dual i-discriminator on every
\mathsf{Q}
-irreducible algebra in
\mathsf{Q}
. Observe that, by Lemma
5.4
, if
\mathsf{Q}
is a dual i-discriminator variety, then every
\mathsf{Q}
-irreducible algebra in
\mathsf{Q}
is simple; so
\mathsf{Q}
is semisimple in the absolute sense.

Dual i-discriminator varieties share many properties with dual discriminator varieties in the sense of
[
38
]
. However
there are dual i-discriminator quasivarieties that are not dual discriminator quasivarieties (see Example
5.19
below).
First we want to show that every dual i-discriminator quasivariety is relative congruence distributive. In order to do that first we show:

Theorem 5.5
.

Let
\mathsf{K}
be a class of similar algebras
with a term
p(x,y,z)
that is the dual i-discriminator on
each member of
\mathsf{K}
. Then
{\mathbf{V}}(\mathsf{K})
is congruence distributive and in
fact satisfies Jónsson’s condition

\Delta_{3}
.

Proof.

We must produce four ternary terms

t_{0},t_{1},t_{2},t_{3}
such that
t_{0}(x,y,z)\approx z
,
t_{3}(x,y,z)\approx z

and moreover

1.

t_{i}(x,y,x)\approx x
for
i=1,3
,

2.

t_{1}(x,x,z)\approx x
,

3.

t_{2}(x,x,z)\approx z
,

4.

t_{1}(x,z,z)\approx t_{2}(x,z,z)

all hold in
{\mathbf{V}}(\mathsf{K})
. Let

t_{1}(x,y,z)=p(p(x,y,z),y,x)\qquad\qquad t_{2}(x,y,z)=p(p(x,y,y),z,z).

Since conditions 1.–4. are equational, it is enough to show that
they hold for each member of
\mathsf{K}
.
Accordingly, let
{\textbf{A}}\in\mathsf{K}
and let

a,b,c\in A
. Then
t_{1}(a,b,a)=p(p(a,b,a),b,a)
. If
a\neq b
, then

p(p(a,b,a),b,a)=p(a,b,a)=a
.
If
a=b
then
p(p(a,b,a),b,a)=p(\pi(a),a,a)=a
, whether or not

\pi(a)=a
. An identical argument shows that
t_{2}(a,b,a)=a
, thus proving
1.

Next,
t_{1}(a,a,c)=p(p(a,a,c),a,a)=p(\pi(a),a,a)=a
as
above. On the other hand
t_{2}(a,a,c)=p(p(a,a,a),c,c)=p(\pi(a),c,c)
,
which is equal to
c
if
\pi(a)\neq c
, or to

\pi\pi(a)=\pi(a)=c
if
\pi(a)=c
. This proves 2. and 3.

Finally we have to show that
t_{1}(a,c,c)=t_{2}(a,c,c)
. If

a=c
, both sides reduce to
p(p(a,a,a),a,a)
. If

a\neq c
we have

\displaystyle t_{1}(a,c,c)=p(p(a,c,c),c,a)=p(c,c,a)=\pi(c)

\displaystyle t_{2}(a,c,c)=p(p(a,c,c),c,c)=p(c,c,c)=\pi(c).

This proves 4. and therefore
{\mathbf{V}}(\mathsf{K})
is congruence distributive.
∎

Next:

Lemma 5.6
.

Let
\mathsf{K}
be a class of similar algebras with
a term
p(x,y,z)
that is the dual i-discriminator on
each member of
\mathsf{K}
. Then
\mathsf{Q}={\mathbf{Q}}(\mathsf{K})
is a dual i-discriminator
variety with dual i-discriminator term
p(x,y,z)
. Moreover
p(x,y,z)
is
a dual i-discriminator term for any finitely
\mathsf{Q}
-irreducible algebra in
\mathsf{Q}
.

Proof.

Since the second claim clearly implies the first we will prove that one. By Theorem

2.5

all the finitely
\mathsf{Q}
-irreducible members of
\mathsf{Q}
lie in

{\mathbf{I}}{\mathbf{S}}{\mathbf{P}}_{u}(\mathsf{K})
. Since being a dual i-discriminator function
can be expressed by the universal sentence

\forall x,y,z[p(x,x,y)\approx p(x,x,z)]\wedge[\neg(x\approx y)\rightarrow p(x,y,z)\approx\pi(z)],

p(x,y,z)
is a dual i-discriminator on each member of
{\mathbf{I}}{\mathbf{S}}{\mathbf{P}}_{u}(\mathsf{K})
. This concludes the proof.
∎

Theorem 5.7
.

Every dual i-discriminator quasivariety is relative congruence distributive.

Proof.

Let
\mathsf{Q}
be a dual i-discriminator variety; then by Lemma
5.6

{\mathbf{V}}(\mathsf{Q})
is congruence distributive. Now in
[
37
]
(Corollary 2.4) the author showed
that a subquasivariety
\mathsf{R}
of a congruence distributive variety is relative congruence distributive if and only if any finitely
\mathsf{R}
-irreducible algebra in
\mathsf{R}
is finitely subdirectly irreducible in
the absolute sense.

But by Lemma
5.6
every finitely
\mathsf{Q}
-irreducible algebra has
p(x,y,z)
as a dual i-discriminator term, so it is simple; hence it is finitely subdirectly irreducible in the absolute sense.
Therefore
\mathsf{Q}
is relatively congruence distributive.
∎

Remark 5.8
.

Let
\mathsf{V}
be a dual i-discriminator variety with witness term
p(x,y,z)
. Which subquasivarieties of
\mathsf{V}
are dual i-discriminator
with the same term? In general not all of them; however let
\mathsf{V}_{fsi}
be the class of finitely subdirectly irreducible (i.e. simple) algebras in
\mathsf{V}
.
Then
{\mathbf{Q}}(\mathsf{K})
is a dual i-discriminator quasivariety with witness term

if and only if
\mathsf{K}\subseteq\mathsf{V}_{fsi}
, so apparently we have a recipe to construct many dual i-discriminator quasivarieties. However it is an immediate consequence of Proposition 2.5 in
[
37
]
that if
\mathsf{V}
is locally finite, then each of those subquasivarieties is really a variety.

Dual i-discriminator variety have a special kind of RPIP; we say that
a quasivariety
\mathsf{Q}
has the
relative ternary principal intersection property
(RTPIP for short) if there is a ternary term
p(x,y,z)
such that

\vartheta_{\textbf{A}}^{\mathsf{Q}}(a,b)\cap\vartheta_{\textbf{A}}^{\mathsf{Q}}(a,b)=\vartheta_{\textbf{A}}^{\mathsf{Q}}(p(a,b,c),p(a,b,d)).

Lemma 5.9
.

Every dual i-discriminator variety
\mathsf{Q}
has the RTPIP.

Proof.

Let
A
be a
\mathsf{Q}
-irreducible algebra in
\mathsf{Q}
and let
a,b,c,d\in A
.
Then
p(x,y,z)
is the dual i-discriminator function on
A
therefore

p(a,b,c)=p(a,b,d)\quad\text{if and only if}\quad\text{$a=b$ or $c=d$}.

As
\mathsf{Q}
is relatively congruence distributive, Theorem
5.1
yields the conclusion.
∎

The concept of having the RTPIP and being a dual i-discriminator quasivariety coincide for
finitely generated quasivarieties.

Theorem 5.10
.

A finitely generated quasivariety
has the RTPIP if and only if it is a dual i-discriminator
quasivariety.

Proof.

Any dual i-discriminator variety has the RTPIP by Lemma
5.9
.
Suppose
\mathsf{Q}
has the RTPIP, witness
p(x,y,z)
; then it is relative congruence distributive by Theorem
5.1
.

Moreover, since it is finitely generated, it has only finitely many
\mathsf{Q}
-irreducible algebras, all of them finite.
Let then
A
one of them and let
a,b\in A
with
a\neq b
; then by Theorem
5.1
the function

z\longmapsto t(a,b,z)
is one-to one on
A
and hence it is a permutation. The order of this permutation
divides
n!
, where
n
is the largest among the cardinalities of
\mathsf{Q}
-irreducible algebras and the
n
-th power
of this permutation is the identity.

Define inductively
a sequence of terms
p_{k}(x,y,z)
for
k=0,1,2,\dots,n-1
by

p_{0}(x,y,z)=z\qquad\qquad p_{k+1}(x,y,z)=p(x,y,p_{k}(x,y,z)).

Then each
t_{k}
for
1\leq k\leq n
witnesses the RTPIP for
\mathsf{V}
and moreover
t_{n}

has the property that in each
\mathsf{Q}
-irreducible member of
\mathsf{Q}
,

a\neq b
implies
t_{n}(a,b,c)=c
.
However, the unary term
\tau(x)=p_{n}(x,x,x)
is not necessarily idempotent,
so further modifications are needed.

For any
\mathsf{Q}
-irreducible algebra

{\textbf{A}}\in\mathsf{Q}
there exist

such that
\tau^{l_{\textbf{A}}+m_{\textbf{A}}}=\tau^{l_{\textbf{A}}}
on
A
. Let
L
be a positive integer
such that for any
\mathsf{Q}
-irreducible
{\textbf{A}}\in\mathsf{V}
,

L
is both a multiple of

and greater than

;
since there are only finitely many
\mathsf{Q}
-irreducible algebras in

\mathsf{Q}
we can take

to be the product of all

and

. Then on each
\mathsf{Q}
-irreducible member we have

, so the term

is idempotent.

Now we must construct a dual idempotent discriminator term of which
\pi(x)
is
the diagonal. Define terms

for
i=0,1,2,\dots,L
by

q_{0}(x,y,z)=z\qquad\qquad q_{k+1}(x,y,z)=p_{n}(q_{k}(x,y,x),q_{k}(x,y,y),q_{k}(x,y,z)).

Set

. Then

as desired, while

retains the properties of

.
Hence

\mathsf{Q}
.
∎

Corollary 5.11
.

Let
\mathsf{Q}
be a quasivariety with the

Let
{\textbf{A}}\leq_{sd}\prod_{i\in I}{\textbf{A}}_{i}

Lemma 5.13
.

If

By Lemma

5.4
Fixedpoint discriminator quasivarieties

In this section we will explore an important subclass of dual i-discriminator quasivarieties. A pointed quasivariety
\mathsf{Q}
(and from now on we will denote constant by
0
) is a

fixedpoint discriminator quasivariety
if there is a ternary term
d(x,y,z)
such that, for any
\mathsf{Q}
-irreducible algebra
{\textbf{A}}\in\mathsf{Q}
and for any

t(a,b,c)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}c,&\hbox{if $a=b$;}\\
0,&\hbox{if $a\neq b$.}\end{array}\right.

Theorem 5.18
.

For a pointed quasivariety
\mathsf{Q}
the following are equivalent:

1.

\mathsf{Q}
is a fixedpoint discriminator variety;

2.

\mathsf{Q}
is a dual i-discriminator variety with dual i-discriminator term
p(x,y,z)
satisfying

p(x,x,x)\approx 0
.

Proof.

Assume (1) and let
p(x,y,z)=d(0,d(x,y,z),z)
; then for any
{\textbf{A}}\in\mathsf{Q}_{ir}
and
a,b,c\in A

p(a,b,c)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}0,&\hbox{if $a=b$;}\\
c,&\hbox{if $a\neq b$.}\end{array}\right.

Then
p(a,a,a)=0
and (2) holds.

Conversely assume (2) and let
d(x,y,z)=p(z,p(x,y,z),z)
. Let
{\textbf{A}}\in\mathsf{Q}_{ir}

and
a,b,c\in A
; then if
a=b

d(a,a,c)=p(c,p(a,a,c),c)=p(c,0,c)=c

and if
a\neq b

d(a,b,c)=p(c,p(a,b,c),c)=p(c,c,c)=0.

Therefore
d(x,y,z)
is a fixedpoint discriminator for
\mathsf{Q}
.
∎

.

Finally we remark that there are quasivarieties that are dual i-discriminator, but do not have a relative TD-term
(for instance the variety of distributive lattices). So in this section we covered a different class of examples.

6
Conclusions and open problems

During our investigation several questions arose (some very general in character, some more specific to the theory) that does not seem to have an immediate answer. We collect them a in series of remarks.

Remark 6.1
.

(Higman’s theorem)
Every finitely generated algebra has a (possibly infinite) presentation; if
A
is finitely generated let
B
be the set of generators of
A
let
X=\{x_{b}:b\in B\}
and let
\Delta
be the set of equations in the language of
A
that are true in
A
with the substitution
x_{b}\longmapsto b
. Then if
{\textbf{A}}\in\mathsf{K}
,
{\textbf{A}}\cong{\textbf{F}}_{\mathsf{K}}(X)/\vartheta(\Delta)
. At this regard we quote the classical (and beautiful) result of G. Higman
[
43
]
:
a finitely generated group is embeddable in a finitely presented group if and only if
\Delta
is a recursively enumerable set
. The property in this form is probably tailored for groups; however investigating varieties of algebras for which a suitable analogue of Higman’s property holds might be worthwhile.∎

Remark 6.2
.

(Belkin’s lattices) In Example
3.19
we discussed primitivity in subquasivarieties of abelian groups and modules. This is linked to a more general investigation that we believe worthwhile; Vinogradov
[
64
]

described all subquasivarieties of abelian groups without investigating the lattice structure. On the other hand it is folklore that the lattice of subvarieties of
\mathsf{M}_{\textbf{R}}
for any module
R
is dually isomorphic to the lattice of ideals of
R
. Of course the lattice of subquasivarieties is way more complex in general and Belkin tackled the question in
[
12
]
; since Belkin’s thesis is in Russian and has not been translated, some words of explanation are necessary. If
I
is any set let
I^{*}=I\cup\{\infty\}
; the
Belkin lattice

\mathfrak{B}(I)
of
I
is the set of functions
f:I^{*}\longrightarrow\mathbb{N}^{*}
with the following properties

\bullet

f(\infty)\in\{0,\infty\}

\bullet

if
f(\infty)=0
, then
f(i)\neq\infty
for all
i\in I
and
f(i)=0
for all (except possibly a finite number)
i\in I
.

If we assume that
\{\infty\}
is the uppermost element of
\mathbb{N}^{*}
, then
\mathfrak{B}(I)
is partially ordered by the natural ordering

f\leq g\qquad\text{if and only if}\qquad f(i)\leq g(i)\ \text{for all $i\in I^{*}$}

and this is a (distributive) lattice ordering. Belkin showed that in case
R
is a PID, then the lattice of subquasivarieties of
\mathsf{M}_{\textbf{R}}
is isomorphic with the Belkin lattice of the set of prime ideals of
R
and his result has been extended to Dedekind domains in
[
44
]
; as abelian groups are
\mathbb{Z}
-modules this gives a description of the lattice of subquasivarieties of abelian groups. So there are two questions that come naturally:

\bullet

can we further generalize Belkin’s result? To do so it seems logical to look for some form of decomposition theorem for prime ideals; we do not know if we can do it but for instance the case in which
R
is a finite direct product of Dedekind domains might be promising;

\bullet

can we find instances of Belkin’s lattices in lattices of subquasivarieties of different structures? This of course depends on the structures but we believe that some cases might be worth investigating.

Remark 6.3
.

In Section
4.1
we observed that, if
C
is a subclone of the term clone of
\mathsf{Q}
, then
\mathsf{Q}
can be
C
-structurally complete while
\mathsf{Q}^{C}
is not structurally complete.
This happens because
\mathsf{Q}
and
\mathsf{Q}^{C}
might not have the same admissible quasiequations, the reason being that the set of available substitutions in
\mathsf{Q}
can be much richer than the one in
\mathsf{Q}^{C}
, making
harder for a rule to be admissible This problem clearly depends on the specific quasivariety we are dealing with; however we suspect that if we restrict to quasivarieties that are equivalent algebraic semantics of substructural logics, then more can be said.

Remark 6.4
.

We have shown that having the RTPIP and being relatively filtral are unrelated properties (Examples
4
and
5.17
).
An open question is whether any relatively filtral quasivariety with the RTPIP is a dual i-discriminator quasivariety.

Remark 6.5
.

Theorem
5.32
is a little bit unsatisfactory, in that we would like it to hold also for
C
-structural completeness (and/or
C
-primitivity). The
problem seems to be that the analogue of Theorem
4.7
does not seem to hold in the case of relative u-presentability. This deserves further investigation.
