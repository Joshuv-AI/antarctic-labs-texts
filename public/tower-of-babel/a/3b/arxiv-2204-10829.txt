Bayesian operator inference for data-driven reduced-order modeling Journal: Computer Methods in Applied Mechanics and Engineering
Mengwu Guo, Shane A. McQuarrie, Karen E. Willcox

Abstract
This work proposes a Bayesian inference method for the reduced-order modeling of time-dependent systems. Informed by the structure of the governing equations, the task of learning a reduced-order model from data is posed as a Bayesian inverse problem with Gaussian prior and likelihood. The resulting posterior distribution characterizes the operators defining the reduced-order model, hence the predictions subsequently issued by the reduced-order model are endowed with uncertainty. The statistical moments of these predictions are estimated via a Monte Carlo sampling of the posterior distribution. Since the reduced models are fast to solve, this sampling is computationally efficient. Furthermore, the proposed Bayesian framework provides a statistical interpretation of the regularization term that is present in the deterministic operator inference problem, and the empirical Bayes approach of maximum marginal likelihood suggests a selection algorithm for the regularization hyperparameters. The proposed method is demonstrated on two examples: the compressible Euler equations with noise-corrupted observations, and a single-injector combustion process.



1
Introduction

The objective of this work is to enable uncertainty quantification for data-driven reduced-order modeling. A reduced-order model (ROM) is a low-dimensional surrogate for a high-dimensional physical system, such as the many natural and engineering systems that are governed by partial differential equations. Ideally, the computational cost of a ROM should be reduced compared to that of a high-fidelity model of the original system, yet without compromising the solution accuracy. Model reduction strategies typically construct a ROM by identifying a predominant, low-dimensional subspace that well approximates the solution manifold, then representing the state dynamics in the subspace
[
4
,
43
]
. Classical reduced-order models are constructed by explicitly projecting high-fidelity operators onto the reduced subspace. Such models are called
intrusive
because the high-fidelity operators must be extracted from a numerical solution code. In contrast,
non-intrusive
data-driven ROMs learn low-dimensional representations of systems primarily from solution data, and do not require intrusive access to a high-fidelity simulator. Non-intrusive ROMs are advantageous for applications with well-established legacy codes that can be readily executed but not easily modified. On the other hand, the extensive use of data introduces uncertainties beyond those originating from the modeling decisions of low-dimensional approximation and makes it difficult to obtain classical
a posteriori
error estimates as in
[
21
]
. In particular, measurement noise in the data and misspecification of the model structure may impact the accuracy and robustness of non-intrusive ROMs. It is therefore crucial that validation efforts for data-driven ROMs quantify the errors and uncertainties associated with non-intrusive learning.

There are several data-driven methods for constructing ROMs, including dynamic mode decomposition
[
45
]
, operator inference to learn a ROM with polynomial structure inspired by known governing equations
[
15
,
40
]
, sparse regression to identify reduced-order latent dynamics
[
8
,
44
]
, manifold learning using deep auto-encoders
[
30
]
, data-driven approximation of time-integration schemes
[
56
]
, and Gaussian process modeling for parametric reduced basis methods
[
17
]
. This paper focuses on operator inference (OpInf), a non-intrusive, projection-based model reduction technique introduced in
[
40
]
. OpInf learns a ROM by inferring reduced-order operators through a deterministic linear regression. The regression requires solution data of the states and their corresponding time derivatives; if needed, the time derivatives are typically estimated from the states with finite differences. Importantly, numerical regularization is often required in the inference problem to address ill-conditioning, over-fitting, and/or model inadequacy
[
31
,
48
]
, but the regularization selection remains a subject of further investigation. Errors introduced by time derivative approximation, noise in the solution data, and/or numerical regularization have not been directly addressed in the literature, with the exception of
[
50
]
, which considers an active sampling strategy for overcoming noise pollution in the training data. Even without noise in the data, establishing error bounds or uncertainty guarantees in OpInf is challenging because the learning is driven by data, although
[
49
]
establishes probabilistic error estimates for linear OpInf ROMs in the case where the reduced-order operators can be ensured to be the same as those determined by explicit projection as in
[
39
]
.

The main contribution of this work is to establish a probabilistic formulation of OpInf, thereby facilitating uncertainty quantification for non-intrusive ROMs of time-dependent systems. Our strategy accounts for approximation errors and data noise, endows the resulting ROM with uncertainty bounds, and provides statistical insight into the numerical regularization. Specifically, the task of inferring reduced-order operators is posed as a Bayesian inversion problem
[
6
]
, which incorporates prior knowledge and observational information to update the probabilistic distribution of unknown system parameters. Bayesian approaches are especially useful in data-driven modeling and analysis
[
9
]
and, in particular, have recently shown promise in estimating the operators in differential equations, see, e.g.,
[
22
,
38
,
55
]
. In our case, multivariate normal distributions are adopted to define the prior and likelihood when Bayes’ rule is applied, yielding a Bayesian linear regression to estimate the reduced-order operators. Different from the deterministic OpInf workflow that gives point estimates only, the proposed Bayesian version derives posterior distributions of the learned reduced-order operators, whose uncertainties are subsequently propagated to solutions through time integration. In other words, endowing the reduced-order operators with probabilistic descriptions results in ROM predictions with quantifiable uncertainties. Additionally, our probabilistic treatment of the regression leads to a selection algorithm for the regularization hyperparameters.

2
Deterministic operator inference

We consider problems governed by partial differential equations, written after spatial discretization in the form

\displaystyle\frac{\textup{d}}{\textup{d}t}{\mathbf{q}}(t)={\mathbf{F}}({\mathbf{q}}(t),{\mathbf{u}}(t))\,,\quad\mathbf{q}(t_{0})=\mathbf{q}_{0}\,,\quad t\in[t_{0},t_{f}]\,,

(2.1)

in which
\mathbf{q}(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is the state vector collecting values of
n
degrees of freedom at time
t
,

\mathbf{u}(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
collects
m
input values at time
t
(e.g., encoding boundary conditions or time-dependent source terms),

\mathbf{F}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
maps state/input pairs to the state evolution,

t_{0}
and
t_{f}
are the initial and final time, respectively, and

\mathbf{q}_{0}
is the prescribed initial condition.
A projection-based ROM approximates the full-order system (
2.1
) in a subspace with reduced dimensionality
r\ll n
, spanned by a collection of
r
basis vectors
\mathbf{V}=[\mathbf{v}_{1}~\mathbf{v}_{2}~\cdots~\mathbf{v}_{r}]\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times r}
.
One widely used approach to construct the basis
\mathbf{V}
is the proper orthogonal decomposition (POD)
[
5
,
16
,
46
]
.
For a collection of known state snapshots
\mathbf{q}_{j}:=\mathbf{q}(t_{j})
and inputs
\mathbf{u}_{j}:=\mathbf{u}(t_{j})
,
j=0,1,\cdots,k-1
, at
k
time instances
t_{0}<t_{1}<\cdots<t_{k-1}
, organized as
\mathbf{Q}=[~\mathbf{q}_{0}~\mathbf{q}_{1}~\cdots~\mathbf{q}_{k-1}~]\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times k}
and
\mathbf{U}=[~\mathbf{u}_{0}~\mathbf{u}_{1}~\cdots~\mathbf{u}_{k-1}~]\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times k}
,
POD takes advantage of the singular value decomposition
\mathbf{Q}={\bf\it\Phi}{\bf\it\Xi}{\bf\it\Psi}^{\mathsf{T}}
by using the first
r
columns of
{\bf\it\Phi}
as the
r
-dimensional reduced basis, i.e.,
\mathbf{V}={\bf\it\Phi}_{:,1:r}
.
Projection-based methods, such as those based on POD, have the key advantage of producing ROMs that inherit structure from the full-order system (
2.1
)
[
16
]
.
This observation motivates us to consider, for a wide class of problems, ROMs with the following polynomial structure:

\frac{\textup{d}}{\textup{d}t}\hat{\mathbf{q}}(t)=\hat{\mathbf{A}}\hat{\mathbf{q}}(t)+\hat{\mathbf{H}}[\hat{\mathbf{q}}(t)\otimes\hat{\mathbf{q}}(t)]+\hat{\mathbf{B}}{\mathbf{u}}(t)+\hat{\mathbf{c}}\,,\quad\hat{\mathbf{q}}(t_{0})=\mathbf{V}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{q}_{0}\,,\quad t\in[t_{0},t_{f}]\,,

(2.2)

where

\hat{\mathbf{q}}(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{r}
is the reduced state,

\otimes
denotes the Kronecker product
[
51
]
, and

\hat{\mathbf{A}}\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times r}
,

\hat{\mathbf{H}}\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times r^{2}}
,

\hat{\mathbf{B}}\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times m}
, and

\hat{\mathbf{c}}\in\mathbb{R}^{r}
are reduced-order operators that together comprise a low-dimensional representation of
{\mathbf{F}}
.
The full- and reduced-order states are related by a linear approximation
\mathbf{q}(t)\approx\mathbf{V}\hat{\mathbf{q}}(t)
.

The data-driven
operator inference
(OpInf) method
[
40
]
employs the projection-based setting of (
2.2
), but utilizes a regression problem to find the reduced-order operators that best match the snapshot data in a minimum-residual sense.
For non-polynomial problems, training data may be pre-processed with variable transformations to induce an exact or approximate polynomial structure as needed
[
42
]
.
Such a non-intrusive approach avoids entailing intrusive queries and access to source code. The regression problem to infer the reduced-order operators is written as

\min_{\hat{\mathbf{A}},\hat{\mathbf{H}},\hat{\mathbf{B}},\hat{\mathbf{c}}}\left\{\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\left\|\hat{\mathbf{A}}\hat{\mathbf{q}}_{j}+\hat{\mathbf{H}}[\hat{\mathbf{q}}_{j}\otimes\hat{\mathbf{q}}_{j}]+\hat{\mathbf{B}}{\mathbf{u}}_{j}+\hat{\mathbf{c}}-\dot{\hat{\mathbf{q}}}_{j}\right\|_{2}^{2}+\mathcal{P}\left([~\hat{\mathbf{A}}~~\hat{\mathbf{H}}~~\hat{\mathbf{B}}~~\hat{\mathbf{c}}~]\right)\right\}\,,

(2.3)

in which the first term is a loss function defined by the residual of (
2.2
) at the
k
time instances
t_{0}<t_{1}<\cdots<t_{k-1}
, and the second term
\mathcal{P}
represents a regularization. Here the reduced-state dataset
\{\hat{\mathbf{q}}_{j}\}_{j=0}^{k-1}
is constructed by evaluating the projection of known snapshots onto the reduced basis
\mathbf{V}
, i.e.,
\hat{\mathbf{q}}_{j}=\mathbf{V}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{q}_{j}
, and the time-derivative data
\{\dot{\hat{\mathbf{q}}}_{j}\}_{j=0}^{k-1}
are then typically approximated using finite differences.
The regularization term
\mathcal{P}
penalizes the entries of the learned quantities, which is often necessary to induce stability in the resulting ROM
[
31
]
.
The training time instances are contained in
[t_{0},t_{k-1}]
, a subset of the full time interval of interest
[t_{0},t_{f}]
, and the reduced-order solution over
[t_{k},t_{f}]
will be predictive through the inferred ROM.

Remark 1:

The quadratic interaction
\hat{\mathbf{q}}\otimes\hat{\mathbf{q}}=\text{vec}(\hat{\mathbf{q}}\hat{\mathbf{q}}^{\mathsf{T}})\in\mathbb{R}^{r^{2}}

has only
r(r+1)/2<r^{2}
degrees of freedom due to symmetry, i.e., the upper-triangular elements of
\hat{\mathbf{q}}\hat{\mathbf{q}}^{\mathsf{T}}
completely prescribe
\hat{\mathbf{q}}\otimes\hat{\mathbf{q}}
.
Hence, in practice we may learn an
r\times r(r+1)/2
quadratic operator
\hat{\mathbf{H}}
as opposed to an
r\times r^{2}
quadratic operator.

The least-squares problem (
2.3
) can be rewritten in a matrix form and decoupled into
r
least-squares problems, one for each ordinary differential equation in the reduced system (
2.2
), i.e.,

\min_{\hat{\mathbf{O}}}\left\{\left\|\mathbf{D}\hat{\mathbf{O}}^{\mathsf{T}}-\mathbf{R}^{\mathsf{T}}\right\|_{F}^{2}+\mathcal{P}(\hat{\mathbf{O}})\right\}=\sum_{i=1}^{r}\min_{\hat{\mathbf{o}}_{i}}\left\{\left\|\mathbf{D}\hat{\mathbf{o}}_{i}-\mathbf{r}_{i}\right\|_{2}^{2}+\mathcal{P}_{i}(\hat{\mathbf{o}}_{i})\right\}\,,

(2.4)

where

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \hat{\mathbf{O}}&:=[~\hat{\mathbf{A}}~~\hat{\mathbf{H}}~~\hat{\mathbf{B}}~~\hat{\mathbf{c}}~]:=[~\hat{\mathbf{o}}_{1}~~\cdots~~\hat{\mathbf{o}}_{r}~]^{\mathsf{T}}\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times d(r,m)}\,,\\
\mathbf{D}&:=[~\hat{\mathbf{Q}}^{\mathsf{T}}~~(\hat{\mathbf{Q}}\odot\hat{\mathbf{Q}})^{\mathsf{T}}~~\mathbf{U}^{\mathsf{T}}~~\mathbf{1}_{k}~]\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times d(r,m)}\,,\\
\hat{\mathbf{Q}}&:=[~\hat{\mathbf{q}}_{0}~~\hat{\mathbf{q}}_{1}~~\cdots~~\hat{\mathbf{q}}_{k-1}~]\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times k}\,,\\
\mathbf{R}&:=[~\dot{\hat{\mathbf{q}}}_{0}~~\dot{\hat{\mathbf{q}}}_{1}~~\cdots~~\dot{\hat{\mathbf{q}}}_{k-1}~]:=[~\mathbf{r}_{1}~~\cdots~~\mathbf{r}_{r}~]^{\mathsf{T}}\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times k}\,,\quad\text{and}\\
\mathbf{U}&:=[~\mathbf{u}_{0}~~\mathbf{u}_{1}~~\cdots~~\mathbf{u}_{k-1}~]\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times k}\,,\end{aligned}

(2.5)

in which
d(r,m)=r+r(r+1)/2+m+1
,

\mathbf{1}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
is a length-
k
vector with all entries set to 1,

\odot
is the Khatri-Rao product (the column-wise Kronecker product
[
28
,
29
]
), and

\hat{\mathbf{o}}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d(r,m)}
and
\mathbf{r}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
are the
i
-th rows of
\hat{\mathbf{O}}
and
\mathbf{R}
, respectively.
Here an individual regularization
\mathcal{P}_{i}
is defined for each
\hat{\mathbf{o}}_{i}
, i.e.,
\mathcal{P}(\hat{\mathbf{O}})=\sum_{i=1}^{r}\mathcal{P}_{i}(\hat{\mathbf{o}}_{i})
. Thus the OpInf comprises of
r
independent regressions with respect to the unknown reduced-order operators
\hat{\mathbf{o}}_{i}
. Note that while (
2.4
) is a linear regression, the corresponding ROM (
2.2
) defined by
\hat{\mathbf{o}}_{i}
is a nonlinear dynamical system.

We usually have
k>d(r,m)
; in that case, and with
\mathcal{P}_{i}(\hat{\mathbf{o}}_{i})=0
, the point estimate
\tilde{\mathbf{o}}_{i}
of
\hat{\mathbf{o}}_{i}
through the least squares (
2.4
) can be yielded in an explicit form as

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathbf{o}}_{i}:=\mathbf{D}^{+}\mathbf{r}_{i}=(\mathbf{D}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{D})^{-1}\mathbf{D}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{r}_{i}\,,

(2.6)

\mathbf{D}^{+}
denoting the Moore–Penrose inverse of the matrix
\mathbf{D}
.
Here we assume that
\mathbf{D}
has full column rank; the cases with ill-conditioned
\mathbf{D}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{D}
will be discussed in Section
4
.

3
Bayesian operator inference

In this section, the regression for learning the reduced-order operators
\hat{\mathbf{O}}=[~\hat{\mathbf{A}}~~\hat{\mathbf{H}}~~\hat{\mathbf{B}}~~\hat{\mathbf{c}}~]
is posed as a Bayesian inference problem
[
6
,
9
]
. We define a probabilistic ROM through the resulting posterior distribution of
\hat{\mathbf{O}}
substituted into the form (
2.2
), which enables uncertainty estimates through Monte-Carlo sampling. We abbreviate the proposed
Bayesian operator inference
method as BayesOpInf.

3.1
Bayesian inference of reduced-order operators

The quadratic structure of the ROM (
2.2
) introduces a model misspecification error whenever the full-order system (
2.1
) includes non-quadratic dynamics. Additionally, the quality of the least-squares estimation (
2.4
) may suffer from approximation error in the estimated time derivatives of the training snapshots and/or noise in the snapshots themselves. To account for these uncertainties, we use Bayesian inference to formulate the estimators of the reduced-order operators
\hat{\mathbf{o}}_{i}
as probabilistic distributions,
i=1,\ldots,r
.

For the definition of a likelihood distribution, we model the residual error in the data-driven scheme (
2.4
) as independent Gaussian noise, i.e., we consider

\displaystyle\mathbf{r}_{i}=\mathbf{D}\hat{\mathbf{o}}_{i}+\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{i},\qquad p(\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{i})=\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{i}|\mathbf{0}_{k},\sigma_{i}^{2}\mathbf{I}_{k})\,,

(3.1)

in which
\mathbf{0}_{k}
is the
k
-dimensional zero vector and
\mathbf{I}_{k}
denotes the
k\times k
identity matrix. Here the noise
{\bf\it\epsilon}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
is a random vector consisting of the error terms in the observations at
k
time instances. All these noise measurements are assumed to follow independent normal distributions with zero mean and variance
\sigma_{i}^{2}
. Hence the likelihood distribution for the training data pair
(\mathbf{D},\mathbf{r}_{i})
is written as

\displaystyle p(\mathbf{r}_{i}|~\mathbf{D},\hat{\mathbf{o}}_{i},\sigma_{i}^{2})=\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{r}_{i}|~\mathbf{D}\hat{\mathbf{o}}_{i},\sigma_{i}^{2}\mathbf{I}_{k})\,.

(3.2)

The choice to model the entries of
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{i}
as independent random variables (i.e., assuming the noise is not correlated across time) is convenient but not a requirement. An alternative, kernel-based model of the residual error is provided in Appendix 1, which accounts for correlations over time and constructs a surrogate model of the ROM closure.

We also define the prior distribution of
\hat{\mathbf{o}}_{i}
as a multivariate normal distribution,

\displaystyle p(\hat{\mathbf{o}}_{i}|~\sigma_{i}^{2},{\bf\it\lambda}_{i})=\mathcal{N}(\hat{\mathbf{o}}_{i}|~{\bf\it\beta}_{i},\sigma_{i}^{2}\mathrm{diag}({\bf\it\lambda}_{i})^{-1})\,,

(3.3)

where
{\bf\it\beta}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d(r,m)}
is a pre-defined mean vector and
{\bf\it\lambda}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d(r,m)}
is a vector of positive numbers hyperparametrizing the prior variances of
\hat{\mathbf{o}}_{i}
. Note that the prior covariance is a diagonal matrix, implying that the prior does not impose correlations between the components of
\hat{\mathbf{o}}_{i}
.

Conditioning on the training data, Bayes’ rule gives the posterior distribution
p(\hat{\mathbf{o}}_{i}|~\mathbf{D},\mathbf{r}_{i},\sigma_{i}^{2},{\bf\it\lambda}_{i})
as follows:

\begin{split}p(\hat{\mathbf{o}}_{i}|~\mathbf{D},\mathbf{r}_{i},\sigma_{i}^{2},{\bf\it\lambda}_{i})&\propto p(\mathbf{r}_{i}|~\mathbf{D},\hat{\mathbf{o}}_{i},\sigma_{i}^{2})~p(\hat{\mathbf{o}}_{i}|~\sigma_{i}^{2},{\bf\it\lambda}_{i})\\
&=\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{r}_{i}|~\mathbf{D}\hat{\mathbf{o}}_{i},\sigma_{i}^{2}\mathbf{I}_{k})~\mathcal{N}(\hat{\mathbf{o}}_{i}|~{\bf\it\beta}_{i},\sigma_{i}^{2}\mathrm{diag}({\bf\it\lambda}_{i})^{-1})\\
&=\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{D}\hat{\mathbf{o}}_{i}|~\mathbf{r}_{i},\sigma_{i}^{2}\mathbf{I}_{k})~\mathcal{N}(\hat{\mathbf{o}}_{i}|~{\bf\it\beta}_{i},\sigma_{i}^{2}\mathrm{diag}({\bf\it\lambda}_{i})^{-1})\\
&=\mathcal{N}\left(\mathbf{r}_{i}|~\mathbf{D}{\bf\it\beta}_{i},\sigma_{i}^{2}\mathbf{D}\mathrm{diag}({\bf\it\lambda}_{i})^{-1}\mathbf{D}^{\mathsf{T}}+\sigma_{i}^{2}\mathbf{I}_{k}\right)~\mathcal{N}(\hat{\mathbf{o}}_{i}|~{\bf\it\mu}_{i},{\bf\it\Sigma}_{i})\,,\quad\text{i.e.,}\\
p(\hat{\mathbf{o}}_{i}|~\mathbf{D},\mathbf{r}_{i},\sigma_{i}^{2},{\bf\it\lambda}_{i})&=\mathcal{N}(\hat{\mathbf{o}}_{i}|~{\bf\it\mu}_{i},{\bf\it\Sigma}_{i})\,,\end{split}

(3.4)

in which

\begin{split}{\bf\it\Sigma}_{i}&={\bf\it\Sigma}_{i}(\mathbf{D},\sigma_{i}^{2},{\bf\it\lambda}_{i})=\sigma_{i}^{2}\left[\mathrm{diag}({\bf\it\lambda}_{i})+\mathbf{D}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{D}\right]^{-1},\\
{\bf\it\mu}_{i}&={\bf\it\mu}_{i}(\mathbf{r}_{i},\mathbf{D},{\bf\it\lambda}_{i})=\left[\mathrm{diag}({\bf\it\lambda}_{i})+\mathbf{D}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{D}\right]^{-1}\left(\mathrm{diag}({\bf\it\lambda}_{i}){\bf\it\beta}_{i}+\mathbf{D}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{r}_{i}\right)\\
&={\bf\it\beta}_{i}+\underbrace{\left[\mathrm{diag}({\bf\it\lambda}_{i})+\mathbf{D}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{D}\right]^{-1}\mathbf{D}^{\mathsf{T}}(\mathbf{r}_{i}-\mathbf{D}{\bf\it\beta}_{i})}_{\delta{\bf\it\mu}_{i}(\mathbf{r}_{i},\mathbf{D},{\bf\it\lambda}_{i})}\,,\end{split}

(3.5)

all consistent with the results of Bayesian linear regression
[
6
]
.

To marginalize
(\sigma_{i}^{2},{\bf\it\lambda}_{i})
in the distribution
p(\hat{\mathbf{o}}_{i}|~\mathbf{D},\mathbf{r}_{i},\sigma_{i}^{2},{\bf\it\lambda}_{i})
and obtain
p(\hat{\mathbf{o}}_{i}|~\mathbf{D},\mathbf{r}_{i})
as the inference of
\hat{\mathbf{o}}_{i}
, we adopt an empirical Bayes method—maximum log marginal likelihood
[
54
]
—to estimate a set of optimal values of
(\sigma_{i}^{2},{\bf\it\lambda}_{i})
. Mathematically, the maximization problem is written as

\begin{split}(\sigma^{*2}_{i},{\bf\it\lambda}_{i}^{*})=\underset{\sigma_{i}^{2},{\bf\it\lambda}_{i}}{\textrm{arg\,max}}&~\log~p(\mathbf{r}_{i}|~\mathbf{D},\sigma_{i}^{2},{\bf\it\lambda}_{i})\\
=\underset{\sigma_{i}^{2},{\bf\it\lambda}_{i}}{\textrm{arg\,max}}&~\log\int p(\mathbf{r}_{i}|~\mathbf{D},\hat{\mathbf{o}}_{i},\sigma_{i}^{2})~p(\hat{\mathbf{o}}_{i}|~\sigma_{i}^{2},{\bf\it\lambda}_{i})~\differential\hat{\mathbf{o}}_{i}\\
\overset{\text{from~\eqref{eq:post}}}{=}\underset{\sigma_{i}^{2},{\bf\it\lambda}_{i}}{\textrm{arg\,max}}&~\log~\mathcal{N}\left(\mathbf{r}_{i}|~\mathbf{D}{\bf\it\beta}_{i},\sigma_{i}^{2}\left(\mathbf{D}\mathrm{diag}({\bf\it\lambda}_{i})^{-1}\mathbf{D}^{\mathsf{T}}+\mathbf{I}_{k}\right)\right)\\
=\underset{\sigma_{i}^{2},{\bf\it\lambda}_{i}}{\textrm{arg\,max}}&~-\frac{1}{2\sigma_{i}^{2}}\|\mathbf{r}_{i}-\mathbf{D}{\bf\it\mu}_{i}(\mathbf{r}_{i},\mathbf{D},{\bf\it\lambda}_{i})\|_{2}^{2}-\frac{1}{2\sigma_{i}^{2}}\left\|\mathrm{diag}\left({\bf\it\lambda}_{i}\right)^{1/2}\delta{\bf\it\mu}_{i}(\mathbf{r}_{i},\mathbf{D},{\bf\it\lambda}_{i})\right\|_{2}^{2}\\
&~-\frac{1}{2}\log~\left|\mathrm{diag}({\bf\it\lambda}_{i})+\mathbf{D}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{D}\right|+\frac{1}{2}\log(\vb*{\lambda}_i)^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{1}_{d(r,m)}-\frac{k}{2}\log(\sigma_i^2)-\frac{k}{2}\log(2\pi)\,,\end{split}

(3.6)

where
|\cdot|
indicates the matrix determinant. Thus the final posterior distribution
p(\hat{\mathbf{o}}_{i}|~\mathbf{D},\mathbf{r}_{i})
is approximated as

p(\hat{\mathbf{o}}_{i}|~\mathbf{D},\mathbf{r}_{i})=p(\hat{\mathbf{o}}_{i}|~\mathbf{D},\mathbf{r}_{i},\sigma_{i}^{*2},{\bf\it\lambda}^{*}_{i})=\mathcal{N}\left(\hat{\mathbf{o}}_{i}|~{\bf\it\mu}_{i}(\mathbf{r}_{i},\mathbf{D},{\bf\it\lambda}^{*}_{i}),{\bf\it\Sigma}_{i}(\mathbf{D},\sigma_{i}^{*2},{\bf\it\lambda}^{*}_{i})\right)\,.

(3.7)

Furthermore, by taking partial derivative of the objective function in (
3.6
) with respect to
\sigma_{i}^{2}
and setting it to zero, we see that the maximum is achieved at

\sigma_{i}^{*2}=\frac{\|\mathbf{r}_{i}-\mathbf{D}{\bf\it\mu}_{i}\|_{2}^{2}+\left\|\mathrm{diag}\left({\bf\it\lambda}_{i}^{*}\right)^{1/2}\delta{\bf\it\mu}_{i}\right\|_{2}^{2}}{k}\,.

(3.8)

Remark 2:
A pragmatic choice is to set
{\bf\it\beta}_{i}=\mathbf{0}
and thus
\delta{\bf\it\mu}_{i}={\bf\it\mu}_{i}
. When we assume that the prior variance values are very large (
{\bf\it\lambda}_{i}\to\mathbf{0}
), i.e., the prior of
\hat{\mathbf{o}}_{i}
is uninformative, we have
{\bf\it\mu}_{i}=\tilde{\mathbf{o}}_{i}=\mathbf{D}^{+}\mathbf{r}_{i}
,
{\bf\it\Sigma}_{i}=\sigma_{i}^{*2}(\mathbf{D}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{D})^{-1}
, and
\sigma_{i}^{*2}=\|\mathbf{r}_{i}-\mathbf{D}\tilde{\mathbf{o}}_{i}\|_{2}^{2}/k
. In this case, the posterior mean vector of
\hat{\mathbf{o}}_{i}
coincides with the original least squares estimate
\tilde{\mathbf{o}}_{i}
as in deterministic OpInf, and
\sigma_{i}^{*2}
is the mean squared error of the linear regression.

Remark 3:
If the pre-defined prior mean vector of
\hat{\mathbf{o}}_{i}
is taken as its point estimate
\tilde{\mathbf{o}}_{i}
, i.e.,
{\bf\it\beta}_{i}=\tilde{\mathbf{o}}_{i}=\mathbf{D}^{+}\mathbf{r}_{i}
, then we have
\delta{\bf\it\mu}_{i}=\mathbf{0}
and
{\bf\it\mu}_{i}=\tilde{\mathbf{o}}_{i}
, as well as
\sigma_{i}^{*2}=\|\mathbf{r}_{i}-\mathbf{D}\tilde{\mathbf{o}}_{i}\|_{2}^{2}/k
. Moreover, by simplifying (
3.6
) the estimate of
{\bf\it\lambda}_{i}
is given by

\begin{split}{\bf\it\lambda}_{i}^{*}&=\underset{{\bf\it\lambda}_{i}}{\textrm{arg\,max}}~-\frac{1}{2}\log~|\mathrm{diag}({\bf\it\lambda}_{i})+\mathbf{D}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{D}|+\frac{1}{2}\log~|\mathrm{diag}({\bf\it\lambda}_{i})|\\
&=\underset{{\bf\it\lambda}_{i}}{\textrm{arg\,max}}~-\frac{1}{2}\log~\left|\mathbf{I}+\mathrm{diag}({\bf\it\lambda}_{i})^{-\frac{1}{2}}\mathbf{D}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{D}\mathrm{diag}({\bf\it\lambda}_{i})^{-\frac{1}{2}}\right|\,.\end{split}

(3.9)

Since
\mathrm{diag}({\bf\it\lambda}_{i})^{-\frac{1}{2}}\mathbf{D}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{D}\mathrm{diag}({\bf\it\lambda}_{i})^{-\frac{1}{2}}
is semi-positive definite,
\inf_{{\bf\it\lambda}_{i}}\left|\mathbf{I}+\mathrm{diag}({\bf\it\lambda}_{i})^{-\frac{1}{2}}\mathbf{D}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{D}\mathrm{diag}({\bf\it\lambda}_{i})^{-\frac{1}{2}}\right|=1
, which is achieved as
\mathrm{diag}({\bf\it\lambda}_{i})^{-1}\to\mathbf{0}
(
{\bf\it\lambda}_{i}\to\infty^{d(r,m)}
), i.e., when the prior of
\hat{\mathbf{o}}_{i}
is deterministic. In this case, the Bayesian formulation reduces to deterministic OpInf, indicating that the deterministic version essentially forms an optimal solution, provided that the Gram matrix
\mathbf{D}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{D}
is well-conditioned.

3.2
A probabilistic reduced-order model

Figure 1:
The BayesOpInf workflow. To derive the probabilistic ROM, infer the multivariate normal posterior distribution for the operator matrix

\hat{\mathbf{O}}

Because the OpInf regression naturally decouples along the rows of the reduced operator matrix

\hat{\mathbf{o}}_{i}

1\leq i\leq r

(3.10)

Given

) over

\hat{\mathbf{O}}

3.3
Equivalence to ridge regression

Recall the posterior mean vector of
\hat{\mathbf{o}}_{i}
given in (
3.5
). The correction term
\delta{\bf\it\mu}_{i}={\bf\it\mu}_{i}-{\bf\it\beta}_{i}
can be expressed alternatively as

\delta{\bf\it\mu}_{i}=\underset{{\bf\it\eta}\in\mathbb{R}^{d(r,m)}}{\textrm{arg\,min}}\left\{\|\mathbf{r}_{i}-\mathbf{D}({\bf\it\beta}_{i}+{\bf\it\eta})\|_{2}^{2}+\|\mathrm{diag}({\bf\it\lambda}_{i})^{\frac{1}{2}}{\bf\it\eta}\|_{2}^{2}\right\}\,,

(3.14)

implying that the posterior mean is the solution of a
ridge regression
problem, i.e., a linear least-squares problem with a Tikhonov regularization (in this case
\|\mathrm{diag}({\bf\it\lambda}_{i})^{\frac{1}{2}}{\bf\it\eta}\|_{2}^{2}
). The use of Tikhonov regularization in OpInf has been reported in
[
31
,
48
]
. Thus another source of modeling uncertainty is introduced to the ROM by the Tikhonov regularization defined with OpInf. Based on the equivalence between the posterior mean of the inferred operators and ridge regression, it can be claimed that the uncertainty introduced by Tikhonov regularization is quantified through the proposed Bayesian framework as well.

On the other hand, the selection of the regularization parameters
\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{r}
has been shown to be important in addressing ill-conditioning, over-fitting, or model inadequacy in OpInf
[
31
,
48
]
. The proposed Bayesian method in this work not only formulates a framework of uncertainty quantification, but also provides a new perspective for understanding the regularization selection process for deterministic OpInf. We address this further in Section
4
.

4
Regularization selection

Without regularization, the OpInf point estimate (
2.6
) is represented with the Moore–Penrose inverse of matrix
\mathbf{D}
and requires that
\mathbf{D}
has full column rank, i.e., the Gram matrix
\mathbf{D}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{D}
is invertible. In practice, however, the condition number of
\mathbf{D}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{D}
may be large, which can lead to a poor estimate of the reduced operators and compromise the stability of the corresponding ROM. In this case, making use of a proper regularization can be critical for stabilizing the OpInf scheme and improving the robustness of the ROM
[
31
,
48
]
.

As specified in Section
3.3
, the posterior mean of inferred operators is equivalent to an estimator through ridge regression. The penalty coefficients
{\bf\it\lambda}_{i}
in the Tikhonov regularization correspond to the prior variances of the operators
\hat{\mathbf{o}}_{i}
. As given in (
3.6
), the values of the hyperparameters
{\bf\it\lambda}_{i}
can be determined by an empirical Bayes method of maximizing marginal likelihood,

\begin{split}\mathcal{L}_{i}(\sigma_{i}^{2},{\bf\it\lambda}_{i})=&~\log~\mathcal{N}\left(\mathbf{r}_{i}|~\mathbf{D}{\bf\it\beta}_{i},\sigma_{i}^{2}\left(\mathbf{D}\mathrm{diag}({\bf\it\lambda}_{i})^{-1}\mathbf{D}^{\mathsf{T}}+\mathbf{I}_{k}\right)\right)\\
=&-\frac{1}{2\sigma_{i}^{2}}\|\mathbf{r}_{i}-\mathbf{D}{\bf\it\mu}_{i}\|_{2}^{2}-\frac{1}{2\sigma_{i}^{2}}\left\|\mathrm{diag}\left({\bf\it\lambda}_{i}\right)^{1/2}\delta{\bf\it\mu}_{i}\right\|_{2}^{2}\\
&-\frac{1}{2}\log~\left|\mathrm{diag}({\bf\it\lambda}_{i})+\mathbf{D}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{D}\right|+\frac{1}{2}\log(\vb*{\lambda}_i)^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{1}_{d(r,m)}-\frac{k}{2}\log(\sigma_i^2)-\frac{k}{2}\log(2\pi)\,.\end{split}

(4.1)

In this sense, the empirical method provides guidance for determining the penalty parameters
{\bf\it\lambda}_{i}
in regularized OpInf.

Taking partial derivatives of
\mathcal{L}_{i}
with respect to
\sigma_{i}^{2}
and all the entries of

which has already been given in (
3.8

\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}_{i}}{\partial\lambda_{i,j}}=0\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\lambda_{i,j}^{*}\left(\delta\mu_{i,j}^{2}+\Sigma_{i,jj}\right)=\sigma_{i}^{*2}\,,\quad 1\leq j\leq d(r,m)\,,

(4.2)

in which
\delta\mu_{i,j}
and
\Sigma_{i,jj}
are the
j

\delta{\bf\it\mu}_{i}
and the
j
-th diagonal entry of
{\bf\it\Sigma}_{i}

5.1
Noised Euler Equations

Consider the conservative one-dimensional compressible Euler equations for an ideal gas,

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\left[\rho\right]

\displaystyle=-\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\left[\rho u\right],

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\left[\rho u\right]

\displaystyle=-\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\left[\rho u^{2}+p\right],

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\left[\rho e\right]

\displaystyle=-\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\left[(\rho e+p)u\right],

(5.1)

where
u
is velocity [m/s],
\rho
is density [kg/m
3
],
p
is pressure [Pa],
\rho u
is specific momentum [kg/m
2
s], and
\rho e
is total energy [kgJ/m
3
].
The state variables are related via the ideal gas law
\rho e=\frac{p}{\gamma-1}+\frac{1}{2}\rho u^{2}
, where
\gamma=1.4
is the heat capacity ratio.
Our goal is to learn a reduced model of (
5.1
) from noisy observations of the conservative variables
(\rho,\rho u,\rho e)
and to quantify the uncertainties induced by the noise. The ideal gas law enables a change of variables
\tau:(\rho,\rho u,\rho e)\mapsto(u,p,1/\rho)
that transforms (
5.1
) into the purely quadratic system

\displaystyle\frac{\partial u}{\partial t}

\displaystyle=-u\frac{\partial u}{\partial x}-\zeta\frac{\partial p}{\partial x},

\displaystyle\frac{\partial p}{\partial t}

\displaystyle=-\gamma p\frac{\partial u}{\partial x}-u\frac{\partial p}{\partial x},

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\zeta}{\partial t}

\displaystyle=-u\frac{\partial\zeta}{\partial x}+\zeta\frac{\partial u}{\partial x},

(5.2)

where
\zeta=1/\rho
is the specific volume [m
3
/kg].
In the model (
5.2
), every term is the product of exactly two state variables
(u,p,\zeta)
or their spatial derivatives, hence for this problem we seek a reduced model with quadratic terms only:

\displaystyle\frac{\textup{d}}{\textup{d}t}\hat{\mathbf{q}}(t)=\hat{\mathbf{H}}[\hat{\mathbf{q}}(t)\otimes\hat{\mathbf{q}}(t)].

(5.3)

The OpInf operator matrix of (
2.5
) in this case is
\hat{\mathbf{O}}=\hat{\mathbf{H}}\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times d(r)}
, with column dimension
d(r)=r(r+1)/2
.

6
Concluding remarks

The proposed BayesOpInf method non-intrusively propagates uncertainties from training data noise and model form error to learned low-dimensional operators of a ROM. Specifically, a linear Bayesian inference driven by projected state data and Gaussian priors leads to Gaussian posterior distributions for the reduced operators. Because constructing the posterior distributions for the reduced operators and integrating particular realizations of the ROM are relatively inexpensive operations, we can efficiently equip the resulting ROM solutions with uncertainty bands through Monte Carlo sampling.

BayesOpInf is a natural generalization of the deterministic OpInf framework. Since the inference problem is generic and carried out only using the solution snapshots and their time-derivative data, BayesOpInf is a promising tool for more specialized applications of OpInf, including localized methods
[
14
]
, problems with detailed structure
[
3
]
, or parametric settings
[
32
]
. Furthermore, because the posterior mean of BayesOpInf is equivalent to Tikhonov-regularized OpInf, the approach is applicable for any Tikhonov-type regularization strategy. Hierarchical Bayes models may be a good candidate for formulating an implicit, non-Tikhonov regularization in the future. For now, we observe that the choice of the Tikhonov regularization remains an important issue for the stability of ROMs, but coupling the state-of-the-art error-based regularization selection of
[
31
]
with BayesOpInf leads to strong performance. Even with carefully chosen regularization, reduced models drawn from the posterior distribution may be unstable. Ensuring ROM stability—and, more generally, embedding desired model structure such as symmetry or spectral properties—through the posterior distribution remains an important topic for future work.

Our Bayesian approach has an inherent connection to Gaussian processes (GPs). A further discussion on this fact is provided in Appendix 1, wherein a GP surrogate model is used for the likelihood definition in the Bayesian inference. One open question is whether additional known information about the problem structure can be used to more strongly inform the Bayesian inference, for example by imposing a non-Gaussian distribution to model the reduced-order residual. Another potential extension of this work is utilizing the uncertainty indicators in the ROM solutions to define acquisition functions for active learning, for example adaptively selecting new training snapshots over time and parameter domains.

In summary, BayesOpInf quantifies the modeling uncertainties stemming from the data noise, the discretization errors of time derivatives, and the model misspecification error when the full-order model generating the snapshots does not match the assumed form of the ROM, as well as the uncertainties introduced by the use of Tikhonov regularization. As demonstrated in the numerical examples, BayesOpInf is effective in constructing accurate ROMs from noisy snapshot data and is capable of capturing complex physics while simultaneously providing meaningful credible intervals.

Appendix 1: Alternative definition of the likelihood

In (
3.1
), an independent Gaussian noise was considered for the modeling of residual errors. If one would like to take into account the correlation over time and/or construct a surrogate model of the ROM closure, a Gaussian process can be used to replace the noise term, i.e.,

\frac{\differential}{\differential t}\hat{q}_{i}(t)=\mathbf{d}(\hat{\mathbf{q}}(t),\mathbf{u}(t))^{\mathsf{T}}\hat{\mathbf{o}}_{i}+\epsilon_{i}(t),\quad\epsilon_{i}(t)\sim\mathcal{GP}(0,\sigma_{i}^{2}\kappa(t,t^{\prime}|{\bf\it\ell}_{i}))\,,

(A.1)

in which
\kappa(t,t^{\prime}|{\bf\it\ell}_{i})
is a given kernel function with hyperparameters
{\bf\it\ell}_{i}
. In this case the likelihood is alternatively defined as

p(\mathbf{r}_{i}|~\mathbf{D},\hat{\mathbf{o}}_{i},\sigma_{i}^{2},{\bf\it\ell})=\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{r}_{i}|~\mathbf{D}\hat{\mathbf{o}}_{i},\sigma_{i}^{2}\mathbf{K}_{i}({\bf\it\ell}_{i}))\,,

(A.2)

where the covariance matrix
\mathbf{K}_{i}({\bf\it\ell}_{i})\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times k}
is defined to be
\mathbf{K}_{i}({\bf\it\ell}_{i})=\kappa(\mathcal{T},\mathcal{T}|{\bf\it\ell}_{i})
and assumed to be positive definite. Here
\mathcal{T}
denotes the set of
k
time instances
\{t_{0},t_{1},\cdots,t_{k-1}\}
corresponding to the training data. Conditioning on the training data
(\mathbf{D},\mathbf{r}_{i})
, the posterior distribution
p(\hat{\mathbf{o}}_{i}|~\mathbf{D},\mathbf{r}_{i},\sigma_{i}^{2},{\bf\it\lambda}_{i},{\bf\it\ell}_{i})=\mathcal{N}(\hat{\mathbf{o}}_{i}|{\bf\it\mu}_{i},{\bf\it\Sigma}_{i})
thus has new formulations of mean vector and covariance matrix as follows:

\begin{split}{\bf\it\Sigma}_{i}&=\sigma_{i}^{2}\left[\mathrm{diag}({\bf\it\lambda}_{i})+\mathbf{D}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{K}^{-1}_{i}\mathbf{D}\right]^{-1}\\
{\bf\it\mu}_{i}&=\left[\mathrm{diag}({\bf\it\lambda}_{i})+\mathbf{D}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{K}^{-1}_{i}\mathbf{D}\right]^{-1}\left(\mathrm{diag}({\bf\it\lambda}_{i}){\bf\it\beta}_{i}+\mathbf{D}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{K}^{-1}_{i}\mathbf{r}_{i}\right)\\
&={\bf\it\beta}_{i}+\underbrace{\left[\mathrm{diag}({\bf\it\lambda}_{i})+\mathbf{D}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{K}^{-1}_{i}\mathbf{D}\right]^{-1}\mathbf{D}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{K}^{-1}_{i}(\mathbf{r}_{i}-\mathbf{D}{\bf\it\beta}_{i})}_{:=\delta{\bf\it\mu}_{i}}\,.\end{split}

(A.3)

Note that the correction term
\delta{\bf\it\mu}_{i}={\bf\it\mu}_{i}-{\bf\it\beta}_{i}
is then the solution to the following generalized least squares problem with a Tikhonov regularization

\delta{\bf\it\mu}_{i}=\underset{{\bf\it\eta}\in\mathbb{R}^{d(r,m)}}{\textrm{arg\,min}}\left\{\|\mathbf{r}_{i}-\mathbf{D}({\bf\it\beta}_{i}+{\bf\it\eta})\|_{\mathbf{K}^{-1}_{i}}^{2}+\|\mathrm{diag}({\bf\it\lambda}_{i})^{\frac{1}{2}}{\bf\it\eta}\|_{2}^{2}\right\}\,.

(A.4)

Here the norm
\|\cdot\|_{\mathbf{K}^{-1}_{i}}
is defined as
\|\mathbf{r}\|_{\mathbf{K}^{-1}_{i}}=\sqrt{\mathbf{r}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{K}^{-1}_{i}\mathbf{r}}
,
\mathbf{r}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
. In the meantime, the estimate of hyperparameters
(\sigma_{i}^{2},{\bf\it\lambda}_{i},{\bf\it\ell}_{i})
by maximizing marginal likelihood is reformulated as

\begin{split}(\sigma^{*2}_{i},{\bf\it\lambda}_{i}^{*},{\bf\it\ell}^{*}_{i})=\underset{\sigma_{i}^{2},{\bf\it\lambda}_{i},{\bf\it\ell}_{i}}{\textrm{arg\,max}}&~\log~p(\mathbf{r}_{i}|~\mathbf{D},\sigma_{i}^{2},{\bf\it\lambda}_{i},{\bf\it\ell}_{i})\\
=\underset{\sigma_{i}^{2},{\bf\it\lambda}_{i},{\bf\it\ell}_{i}}{\textrm{arg\,max}}&~\log\int p(\mathbf{r}_{i}|~\mathbf{D},\hat{\mathbf{o}}_{i},\sigma_{i}^{2},{\bf\it\ell}_{i})~p(\hat{\mathbf{o}}_{i}|~\sigma_{i}^{2},{\bf\it\lambda}_{i})~\differential\hat{\mathbf{o}}_{i}\\
=\underset{\sigma_{i}^{2},{\bf\it\lambda}_{i},{\bf\it\ell}_{i}}{\textrm{arg\,max}}&~\log~\mathcal{N}\left(\mathbf{r}_{i}|~\mathbf{D}{\bf\it\beta}_{i},\sigma_{i}^{2}\left(\mathbf{D}\mathrm{diag}({\bf\it\lambda}_{i})^{-1}\mathbf{D}^{\mathsf{T}}+\mathbf{K}_{i}({\bf\it\ell}_{i})\right)\right)\,.\end{split}

(A.5)

The model (
A.1
) is equivalent to a universal kriging with the following Gaussian process prior:

\frac{\differential}{\differential t}\hat{q}_{i}(t)\sim\mathcal{GP}\bigg(\mathbf{d}(\hat{\mathbf{q}}(t),\mathbf{u}(t))^{\mathsf{T}}{\bf\it\beta}_{i},\sigma_{i}^{2}\mathbf{d}(\hat{\mathbf{q}}(t),\mathbf{u}(t))^{\mathsf{T}}\mathrm{diag}({\bf\it\lambda}_{i})^{-1}\mathbf{d}(\hat{\mathbf{q}}(t^{\prime}),\mathbf{u}(t^{\prime}))+\sigma_{i}^{2}\kappa(t,t^{\prime}|{\bf\it\ell}_{i})\bigg)\,.

(A.6)

Accordingly, the posterior prediction of
\frac{\differential}{\differential t}\hat{q}_{i}(t)
is again a Gaussian process whose mean and covariance functions take the form of

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{\differential}{\differential t}\hat{q}_{i}(t)\middle|~\mathbf{D},\mathbf{r}_{i}\right]=&~\mathbf{d}(\hat{\mathbf{q}}(t),\mathbf{u}(t))^{\mathsf{T}}{\bf\it\mu}_{i}+\kappa(t,\mathcal{T}|{\bf\it\ell}_{i})\mathbf{K}^{-1}_{i}(\mathbf{r}_{i}-\mathbf{D}{\bf\it\mu}_{i})\,,\quad\text{and}\\
\mathbb{C}ov\left[\frac{\differential}{\differential t}\hat{q}_{i}(t),\frac{\differential}{\differential t}\hat{q}_{i}(t^{\prime})\middle|~\mathbf{D},\mathbf{r}_{i}\right]=&~\sigma_{i}^{2}\kappa(t,t^{\prime}|{\bf\it\ell}_{i})-\sigma_{i}^{2}\kappa(t,\mathcal{T}|{\bf\it\ell}_{i})\mathbf{K}^{-1}_{i}\kappa(\mathcal{T},t^{\prime}|{\bf\it\ell}_{i})\\
+\big[\mathbf{d}(\hat{\mathbf{q}}(t),\mathbf{u}(t))^{\mathsf{T}}&-\kappa(t,\mathcal{T}|{\bf\it\ell}_{i})\mathbf{K}_{i}^{-1}\mathbf{D}\big]{\bf\it\Sigma}_{i}\big[\mathbf{d}(\hat{\mathbf{q}}(t^{\prime}),\mathbf{u}(t^{\prime}))-\mathbf{D}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{K}_{i}^{-1}\kappa(\mathcal{T},t^{\prime}|{\bf\it\ell}_{i})\big]\,,\end{split}

(A.7)

respectively. Note that the second term in the mean function, i.e.,
\kappa(t,\mathcal{T}|{\bf\it\ell}_{i})\mathbf{K}^{-1}_{i}(\mathbf{r}_{i}-\mathbf{D}{\bf\it\mu}_{i})
, formulates a surrogate of the closure in the linear model (
2.2
).
