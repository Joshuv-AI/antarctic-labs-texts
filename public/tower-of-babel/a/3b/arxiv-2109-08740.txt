On intersections of symmetric determinantal varieties and theta characteristics of canonical curves
Avinash Kulkarni, Sameera Vemulapalli

Abstract
. From a block-diagonal ( n + 1 ) × ( m + 1 ) × ( m + 1 ) (n+1)\times(m+1)\times(m+1) tensor symmetric in the last two entries one obtains two varieties: an intersection of symmetric determinantal hypersurfaces X X in n n -dimensional projective space, and an intersection of quadrics ℭ \mathfrak{C} in m m -dimensional projective space. Under mild technical assumptions, we characterize the accidental singularities of X X in terms of ℭ \mathfrak{C} . We apply our methods to algebraic curves and show how to construct theta characteristics of certain canonical curves of genera 3, 4, and 5, generalizing a classical construction of Cayley.

14M12 (primary), 14H10 (secondary)

1.
Introduction

Let
k
be a field of characteristic not
2
or
3
. A tensor
\mathcal{A}\in k^{n+1}\otimes\Sym_{2}k^{m+1}
defines two important varieties. First, write
\mathcal{A}
as an
(n+1)
-tuple of symmetric
(m+1)\times(m+1)
matrices
(A_{0},\dots,A_{n})
with
A_{i}\in\Sym_{2}k^{m+1}
and define the determinantal variety

X\coloneqq Z(\det(x_{0}A_{0}+\dots+x_{n}A_{n}))\subseteq\mathbb{P}^{n}:=\Proj k[x_{0},\ldots,x_{n}].

We call a hypersurface with such a symmetric determinantal representation a
symmetroid
. The second variety we associate to
\mathcal{A}
is the intersection of
n+1
quadrics

\mathfrak{C}:=Z(\mathbf{y}^{T}A_{0}\mathbf{y},\ldots,\mathbf{y}^{T}A_{n}\mathbf{y})\subseteq\mathbb{P}^{m}:=\Proj k[y_{0},\ldots,y_{m}],

where
\mathbf{y}:=(y_{0},\ldots,y_{m})^{T}
.

More generally, a tensor
\mathcal{A}\in k^{n+1}\otimes(\bigoplus_{l=1}^{r}\Sym_{2}k^{d_{l}})
gives rise to varieties
X
and
\mathfrak{C}
as follows. First,
\mathcal{A}
can be thought of as a tuple of
r
tensors
(\mathcal{A}^{(1)},\ldots,\mathcal{A}^{(r)})
, with each
\mathcal{A}^{(l)}\in k^{n+1}\otimes\Sym_{2}k^{d_{l}}
. Each
\mathcal{A}^{(l)}
defines a symmetroid
X_{l}
as above, and we define
X:=X_{1}\cap\ldots\cap X_{r}
. Second, we may consider
\mathcal{A}
as an element of
k^{n+1}\otimes\Sym_{2}k^{d_{1}+d_{2}+\ldots+d_{r}}
and construct
\mathfrak{C}
as an intersection of
n+1
quadrics in
\mathbb{P}^{d_{1}+d_{2}+\ldots+d_{r}-1}
as before. We call
\mathfrak{C}
the
Cayley variety of
\mathcal{A}
. This article studies the connection between
\mathcal{A}
,
X
, and
\mathfrak{C}
.

This work has two major inspirations. First is the classical relationship between plane quartic curves and intersections of
3
quadrics in
\mathbb{P}^{3}
(i.e.
\mathcal{A}\in k^{3}\otimes\Sym_{2}k^{4}
), originally studied by Hesse
[
18
,
17
]
and Dixon
[
13
]
; this case serves as a motivating example throughout the paper. The eight points in
\mathbb{P}^{3}
are classically known as a
Cayley octad
, and the
28
secants between these points are in one-to-one correspondence with the bitangents of a plane quartic curve. This relation has been further studied by
[
16
,
22
]
. Secondly, orbit parametrizations of geometric objects often have applications to topics of interest in arithmetic geometry; see
[
2
,
3
,
4
,
5
]
for low rank rings and certain ideal classes,
[
28
]
for binary n-ic rings and certain ideal classes,
[
7
]
and
[
7
]
for K3 surfaces,
[
8
]
for genus 1 curves, and
[
6
]
for hyperelliptic curves.

The orbits of
k^{n+1}\otimes(\bigoplus_{l=1}^{r}\Sym_{2}k^{d_{l}})
under the group
\GL_{n+1}\times(\bigoplus_{l=1}^{r}\GL_{d_{l}})
simultaneously classifies two families of geometric objects: intersections of symmetroids in
\mathbb{P}^{n}
with prescribed line bundles and complete intersections of quadrics in
\mathbb{P}^{m}
. It is natural to ask what the general relationship between these objects is.

1.1.
Statement of results

A
block-diagonal tensor with
r
blocks
is a tensor
\mathcal{A}\in k^{n+1}\otimes(\bigoplus_{l=1}^{r}\Sym_{2}k^{d_{l}})
. In this article, we assume that the determinantal variety
X_{l}
associated to each block of
\mathcal{A}(\mathbf{x},\cdot,\cdot)
is reduced and irreducible, we have
d_{l}\geq 2
, and no block is uniformly zero. See Section
2.1
for more details.

Our first result describes the relationship between accidental singularities of
X
and singularities of
\mathfrak{C}
. For a hypersurface
X
, i.e.
r=1
, an
essential singularity
of
X=\det\mathcal{A}(\mathbf{x},\cdot,\cdot)
is a point
p
such that
\corank\mathcal{A}(p,\cdot,\cdot)\geq 2
. Generically, the only singularities of a determinantal hypersurface are its essential singularities
[
20
]
. It is possible for a determinantal hypersurface to have singularities that are not essential; these are called
accidental singularities
.

Similarly, an
essential singularity
of an intersection of symmetroids
X
defined by a block-diagonal tensor
\mathcal{A}
is a point
p\in X(\bar{k})
such that
\corank\mathcal{A}^{(l)}(p,\cdot,\cdot)\geq 2
for some block of
\mathcal{A}
, and an
accidental singularity
is a singular point of
X
that is not an essential singularity. A
coincident singularity
of
X
is an accidental singularity
x\in X
which is a singular point of two intersections of symmetroids
X^{\prime},X^{\prime\prime}\supset X
defined by two distinct strict subsets of the blocks of
\mathcal{A}
. For further details, see Section
4
. We denote by
X^{\mathrm{n.e.}}
the complement of the essential singularities in
X
.

Theorem 1.1.1
.

Let
\mathcal{A}\in k^{n+1}\otimes(\bigoplus_{l=1}^{r}\Sym_{2}k^{d_{l}})
be a block-diagonal tensor with
r
blocks, let
X
be the associated intersection of symmetroids, and let
\mathfrak{C}
be the Cayley variety of
\mathcal{A}
. If
X
is a complete intersection,
\sing X^{\mathrm{n.e.}}
has dimension
0
, and
X
does not have any coincident singularities, then the scheme

\{(x,y)\in X^{\mathrm{n.e.}}\times\mathbb{P}^{m}:\mathcal{A}(x,y,\cdot)=0,\ y\in\mathfrak{C}\}

is a finite cover of
\sing X^{\mathrm{n.e.}}
of degree
2^{r-1}
via projection on the first factor.

We apply Theorem
1.1.1
to obtain a generalization of the relation between the bitangents of a plane quartic curve and the secant lines of the Cayley octad. The tensor
\mathcal{A}
defines a map
\psi\colon\mathbb{P}^{m}\dashrightarrow\widehat{\mathbb{P}}^{n}
by

\psi(\mathbf{y}):=(\mathbf{y}^{T}A_{0}\mathbf{y},\ldots,\mathbf{y}^{T}A_{n}\mathbf{y}),

with base locus
\mathfrak{C}
. The map
\psi
contracts any secant line of
\mathfrak{C}
to a point
[H]\in\widehat{\mathbb{P}}^{n}
. When
\mathfrak{C}
is zero dimensional,
H
intersects
X
in a special way. Let the
block spaces
of
\mathcal{A}
be the linear subspaces of
\mathbb{P}^{d_{1}+\dots+d_{r}-1}
defined by the natural inclusions
k^{d_{l}}\lhook\joinrel\relbar\joinrel\rightarrow k^{d_{1}+\dots+d_{r}}
corresponding to the decomposition
\bigoplus_{l=1}^{r}\Sym_{2}k^{d_{l}}
.

Proposition 1.1.2
.

Let
\mathcal{A}\in k^{n+1}\otimes(\bigoplus_{l=1}^{r}\Sym_{2}k^{d_{l}})
be a block-diagonal tensor whose associated Cayley variety
\mathfrak{C}
is
0
-dimensional and let
X
be the associated complete intersection of symmetroids. Let
\ell
be a secant line of
\mathfrak{C}
which does not intersect the block spaces and let
[H]=\psi(\ell)
. If
X\cap H
contains no essential singularities or coincident singularities of
X
, then
\sing(X\cap H)
contains a subscheme of dimension
0
and degree
n
.

We apply Proposition
1.1.2
to four main cases: smooth plane quartics, generic quintic symmetroid surfaces, smooth canonical genus
4
curves whose canonical embedding is contained in a quadric cone, and smooth canonical genus
5
curves whose canonical embedding can be written as the intersection of three quadrics of rank
3
. The case of smooth plane quartics is well-known, and the case of generic quintic symmetroid surfaces was studied by Roth in
1930

[
25
]
. The latter two cases are novel to the knowledge of the authors. In the case of curves of genus
4
and
5
, we describe the moduli functor of genus
4
curves with a vanishing even theta characteristic (respectively, genus
5
curves with three vanishing even theta characteristics) using the symmetric determinantal representation. We also describe the relationship between the determinantal representations to certain odd theta characteristics. If
\mathcal{A}\in k^{4}\otimes(\Sym_{2}k^{2}\oplus\Sym_{2}k^{3})
or
\mathcal{A}\in k^{5}\otimes(\Sym_{2}k^{2})^{\oplus 3}
, we say that
\mathcal{A}
is
nondegenerate
if the associated intersection of symmetroids is a smooth curve (of genus
4
or
5
, respectively). As nondegeneracy is preserved by the natural group action, we may speak of nondegenerate orbits.

1.2.
Additional results for canonical curves of genus
4

A construction of Milne, later studied by Bruin and Sertöz, relates the bitangents of a genus
3
curve to the tritangents of a genus
4
curve
[
21
,
9
]
. We provide a summary of their construction in Section

in the case of canonical curves of genus
4
with a vanishing even theta characteristic (see Theorem
6.4.1
). In this case, our tensor descriptions simplify this construction significantly.

Choose a general
\mathcal{A}\in k^{4}\otimes(\Sym_{2}k^{2}\oplus\Sym_{2}k^{3})
and let
X
be the associated genus
4
curve. Let
\pi\colon\mathbb{P}^{4}\dashrightarrow\mathbb{P}^{2}
be the projection onto the last
3
coordinates. Then the image of the
112
secant lines of
\mathfrak{C}
is a set of
28
lines in
\mathbb{P}^{2}
. These
28
lines are precisely the secants through the eight points of
\pi(\mathfrak{C})
(see Section
6.4
).
The tensor
\mathcal{A}
defines a natural quadruple cover

\left\{\begin{array}[]{c}\text{The $112$ secant lines }\ell\text{ of }\mathfrak{C}\text{ defining}\\
\text{ odd theta characteristics of }X\end{array}\right\}\rightarrow\left\{\begin{array}[]{c}\text{The $28$ lines given by }\pi(\ell)\\
\text{for }\ell\text{ a secant line of }\mathfrak{C}\end{array}\right\}

which factors through a double cover

\left\{\begin{array}[]{c}\text{The $112$ secant lines }\ell\text{ of }\mathfrak{C}\text{ defining}\\
\text{ odd theta characteristics of }X\end{array}\right\}\rightarrow\left\{\begin{array}[]{c}\text{The $56$ odd theta characteristics represented}\\
\text{by the image of the secant lines of }\mathfrak{C}\text{ under }\psi\end{array}\right\}.

In this way, a line in
\mathbb{P}^{2}
corresponds to a pair of odd theta characteristics of

.

1.3.
Historical notes

Hesse studied the classical relationship between plane quartic curves and intersections of
3
quadrics in
\mathbb{P}^{3}
. Hesse’s result has since been generalized in two different directions. The first is the work of Roth
[
25
]
, who studied tritangent hyperplanes to a quintic symmetroid in terms of an intersection of four quadrics in
\mathbb{P}^{5}
. Secondly, the relationship between a net of quadrics in
\mathbb{P}^{n}
and its degeneracy locus in
\mathbb{P}^{2}
has been studied by Reid and Tyurin
[
24
,
27
]
. Reid proved that when
n
is even, the intermediate Jacobian of the intersection of three quadrics is the Prym variety of a double cover of the determinantal curve in
\mathbb{P}^{2}
defined by the determinantal representation. In both cases, the determinantal variety is necessarily a hypersurface. Determinantal varieties themselves have been well studied since the 19th century
[
15
]
.

2.
General notation and setup

We denote by
k
an arbitrary field of characteristic not
2
or
3
. Let
\bar{k}
denote an algebraic closure of
k
. A summary of the main notation for this article is given in Figures
1
and
2
.

Let
\mathcal{A}\in k^{n+1}\otimes\Sym_{2}k^{m+1}
be a tensor, symmetric in the last two entries. We view
\mathcal{A}
as an
(n+1)\times(m+1)\times(m+1)
array of elements of
k
. We denote the contraction of
\mathcal{A}
along a vector
v\in k^{n+1}
by
\mathcal{A}(v,\cdot,\cdot)
. More generally, we will contract along an element of
R^{n}
, where
R
is a
k
-algebra. Similarly, we will denote the contraction by an element
y\in R^{m+1}
by
\mathcal{A}(\cdot,y,\cdot)
or
\mathcal{A}(\cdot,\cdot,y)
, depending along which axis we contract. We may always view a tensor
\mathcal{A}\in k^{n+1}\otimes\Sym_{2}k^{m+1}
as a multilinear map
\mathcal{A}\colon(k^{n+1})^{\vee}\otimes(k^{m+1})^{\vee}\otimes(k^{m+1})^{\vee}\rightarrow k
symmetric in the last two entries, and vice-versa. The contraction by an element is simply evaluating this multilinear map in the appropriate entry. We refer to a
slice
of the tensor
\mathcal{A}
as the contraction along a standard basis vector; let
A_{i}
denote the
i
–th slice of
\mathcal{A}
.
Denote
\mathbb{P}^{n}:=\Proj k[x_{0},\ldots,x_{n}]
and
\mathbb{P}^{m}:=\Proj k[y_{0},\ldots,y_{m}]
. We will also denote
\mathbf{x}:=(x_{0},\ldots,x_{n})
and
\mathbf{y}:=(y_{0},\ldots,y_{m})
. The contractions
\mathcal{A}(\mathbf{x},\cdot,\cdot),\mathcal{A}(\cdot,\mathbf{y},\cdot)
are matrices of linear forms. If
x\in\mathbb{P}^{n}(k)
is a point, then the contraction

\mathcal{A}(x,\cdot,\cdot)

is a symmetric
(m+1)\times(m+1)
matrix with entries in
k
, well-defined up to scaling. In other words, one obtains the defining equation for a quadric in
\mathbb{P}^{m}
. We express this as a rational map

\begin{tabular}[]{cccc}\widehat{\psi}\colon&\mathbb{P}^n&\dashrightarrow&\mathbb{P}\!\operatorname{H}^0(\mathbb{P}^m, \mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}^m}(2))\\
&x&\mapsto&\mathcal{A}(x, \cdot, \cdot)\end{tabular}.

The map
\widehat{\psi}
is the dual of the map
\psi
which we now define. The tensor
\mathcal{A}
consists of
n+1
slices, which are symmetric
(m+1)\times(m+1)
matrices. Write
(q_{0},\ldots,q_{n})
for the quadrics defined by these
n+1
slices. The
q_{i}
define the rational map

\begin{tabular}[]{cccc}\psi\colon&\mathbb{P}^m&\dashrightarrow&\widehat{\mathbb{P}}^n\\
&y&\mapsto&\mathcal{A}(\cdot, y, y) = (q_0(y), \ldots, q_n(y))\end{tabular}.

Definition 2.0.1
.

The
Cayley variety

\mathfrak{C}
associated to
\mathcal{A}
is the scheme in
\mathbb{P}^{m}
given by

\{y\in\mathbb{P}^{m}:\mathcal{A}(x,y,y)=0,\ \text{for all }x\in\mathbb{P}^{n}\}.

The
automorphism group
of
\mathcal{A}\in k^{n+1}\otimes\Sym_{2}k^{m+1}
is the subgroup of
\GL_{n+1}\times\GL_{m+1}
defined by

\Aut(\mathcal{A}):=\{(g,h)\in\GL_{n+1}\times\GL_{m+1}:(g,h)\cdot\mathcal{A}=\mathcal{A}\}.

On each of the slices
A_{j}
, we have that
h
acts by
A_{j}\mapsto h^{T}A_{j}h
. In particular, the image of
\Aut(\mathcal{A})
in
\PGL_{n+1}
is a group of linear automorphisms of
\mathbb{P}^{n}
leaving
Z(\det\mathcal{A}(\mathbf{x},\cdot,\cdot))
invariant. The image of
\Aut(\mathcal{A})
in
\PGL_{m+1}
is a subgroup of linear automorphisms of
\mathbb{P}^{m}
leaving the Cayley variety invariant, which is also the group of linear automorphisms of
\mathbb{P}^{m}
such that
\psi\circ g=\psi
. If
\mathcal{A}
is generic and
\mathfrak{C}
is nonempty, then the image of
\Aut(\mathcal{A})
in
\PGL_{m+1}
is precisely the group of linear transformations preserving
\mathfrak{C}
.

We obtain a hypersurface in
\mathbb{P}^{n}
with a symmetric determinantal representation by
X:=Z(\det\mathcal{A}(\mathbf{x},\cdot,\cdot))
.
The tensor
\mathcal{A}
also determines natural rational maps. Here, we discuss these maps in the case that
X
is a hypersurface. The analogous maps for an intersection of symmetroid hypersurfaces are discussed in Section
2.1
. The tensor
\mathcal{A}
defines a rational map by

\begin{tabular}[]{cccc}\varphi\colon&X&\dashrightarrow&\mathbb{P}^m\\
&x&\mapsto&\ker\mathcal{A}(x, \cdot, \cdot)\end{tabular}.

Note that
\varphi
is well-defined for all
x\in X
such that
\corank\mathcal{A}(x,\cdot,\cdot)=1
. We refer to this map as the
kernel map
. If
A
is a non-zero symmetric matrix, then the singular locus of the quadric
Z(\mathbf{y}^{T}A\mathbf{y})\subseteq\mathbb{P}^{m}
is precisely
\mathbb{P}(\ker A)
. In other words, when
x
is a smooth point of
X
, we see that the kernel of
\mathcal{A}(x,\cdot,\cdot)
is 1-dimensional, i.e, the singular locus of the associated quadric hypersurface is a single point; in particular, we may view
\varphi
as sending a point
x\in X(\bar{k})
to the vertex of the cone
Z(\mathcal{A}(x,\mathbf{y},\mathbf{y}))\subset\mathbb{P}^{m}
.

For a hypersurface
X
, its dual variety is denoted
\widehat{X}
; it is the subvariety of

parameterizing the planes tangent to

\begin{tabular}[]{cccc}\theta_X\colon&X&\dashrightarrow&\widehat{\mathbb{P}}^n\\

The Gauss map is defined on the smooth subscheme of

. The dual variety

. The fundamental relationship between

2.1.
Block-diagonal tensors and intersections of symmetroids

Let
d_{1},\ldots,d_{r}\geq 2
be integers and set
m=(\sum d_{l})-1
. Observe that
k^{n+1}\otimes(\bigoplus_{l=1}^{r}\Sym_{2}k^{d_{l}})
embeds block-diagonally in
k^{n+1}\otimes\Sym_{2}k^{m+1}
(see Example
2.1.1
). For a tensor
\mathcal{A}\in k^{n+1}\otimes(\bigoplus_{l=1}^{r}\Sym_{2}k^{d_{l}})
, we may view
\mathcal{A}\colon k^{n+1}\otimes\Sym_{2}k^{m+1}\rightarrow k
as a multilinear map. The restrictions to each
k^{n+1}\otimes\Sym_{2}k^{d_{l}}
define
r
multilinear maps, i.e. tensors,
\mathcal{A}^{(1)},\ldots,\mathcal{A}^{(r)}
; these tensors are called the
blocks
of
\mathcal{A}
. The
size
of the block
\mathcal{A}^{(l)}
is
d_{l}
. A
block-diagonal tensor
with
r
blocks is a tensor
\mathcal{A}\in k^{n+1}\otimes(\bigoplus_{l=1}^{r}\Sym_{2}k^{d_{l}})
for some
d_{l}\geq 1
such that each
\det\mathcal{A}^{(l)}(\mathbf{x},\cdot,\cdot)
is reduced and irreducible. As a convention, we will always assume that the size of a block is at least
2
and we will never consider a tensor where one of the blocks is identically zero.

Every block-diagonal tensor
\mathcal{A}\in k^{n+1}\otimes(\bigoplus_{l=1}^{r}\Sym_{2}k^{d_{l}})
defines an intersection of
r
symmetroids
X:=X_{1}\cap\ldots\cap X_{r}
, where
X_{l}
is the symmetroid associated to the
l
-th block. Additionally,
Z(\det\mathcal{A}(\mathbf{x},\cdot,\cdot))\subseteq\mathbb{P}^{n}
is the reducible determinantal hypersurface
X_{1}\cup\ldots\cup X_{r}
. The
block spaces
of
\mathcal{A}
are the linear subspaces of
\mathbb{P}^{m}
defined by the natural inclusions
k^{d_{l}}\lhook\joinrel\relbar\joinrel\rightarrow k^{m+1}
corresponding to the decomposition
\bigoplus_{l=1}^{r}\Sym_{2}k^{d_{l}}
.

Example 2.1.1
.

Let
a,b,c,d,e,f,g,h,i\in k[x_{0},x_{1},x_{2},x_{3}]
be homogeneous linear forms. Define the tensors
\mathcal{A}^{(1)}
and
\mathcal{A}^{(2)}
by forming the two matrices

\mathcal{A}^{(1)}(\mathbf{x},\cdot,\cdot):=\begin{bmatrix}a&c\\
c&b\end{bmatrix},\quad\mathcal{A}^{(2)}(\mathbf{x},\cdot,\cdot):=\begin{bmatrix}d&g&h\\
g&e&i\\
h&i&f\end{bmatrix}.

Specifically, each matrix of linear forms defines a
4\times d\times d
tensor by declaring the
j
-th slice of
\mathcal{A}^{(l)}
to be the matrix of coefficients of
x_{j}
. The two tensors
\mathcal{A}^{(1)},\mathcal{A}^{(2)}
can be joined diagonally along the first axis to create the
4\times 5\times 5
tensor

\mathcal{A}(\mathbf{x},\cdot,\cdot):=\begin{bmatrix}a&c&0&0&0\\
c&b&0&0&0\\
0&0&d&g&h\\
0&0&g&e&i\\
0&0&h&i&f\end{bmatrix}.

The two determinantal varieties
X_{1}:=Z(\det\mathcal{A}^{(2)}(\mathbf{x},\cdot,\cdot))
and
X_{2}:=Z(\det\mathcal{A}^{(2)}(\mathbf{x},\cdot,\cdot))
generically intersect in a smooth canonical curve of genus
4
. The Cayley variety associated to
\mathcal{A}
is a set of
16
points in
\mathbb{P}^{4}
.

Every block-diagonal tensor with at least two blocks always has a non-trivial automorphism group.

Definition 2.1.2
.

Let

be a block-diagonal tensor with
r

The diagonal automorphisms of a tensor

\mathcal{A}

\DAut(\mathcal{A})
generated by
-I

\DAut(\mathcal{A})\rightarrow\PGL_{m+1}
.

For the purposes of studying the intersection of symmetroids
X:=X_{1}\cap\ldots\cap X_{r}

X
of each of the kernel maps
\varphi_{X_{1}},\ldots,\varphi_{X_{r}}
in tandem.

Definition 2.1.3
.

The

^d_1 - 1 ×…×

^d_r-1

2.2.
Arithmetically Cohen-Macaulay sheaves

It is well-understood that the linear determinantal representations of a hypersurface in projective space can be classified in terms of arithmetically Cohen-Macaulay (aCM) sheaves on the projective space; see
[
1
]
,
[
15

.
A coherent sheaf

on

is
arithmetically Cohen-Macaulay (aCM)
if

•

Each stalk

is a Cohen-Macaulay
\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}^{n},x}
-module, and

•

Example 3.0.2
.

A plane quartic with a symmetric determinantal representation

3.1.
The number of
k
-rational bitangents of a smooth plane quartic

Let
X
be a smooth plane quartic defined over
k
. A symmetric determinantal representation of
X
can be used to determine the number of
k
-rational bitangents of that smooth plane quartic. Specifically:

Theorem 3.1.1
.

Let
k
be an infinite field of characteristic not
2
or
3
. For any smooth non-hyperelliptic genus
3
curve
X
defined over
k
, the number of odd theta characteristics of
X
defined over
k
lies in
S:=\{28,16,10,8,6,4,3,2,1,0\}
. If
k
has normal separable extensions of degrees
1,\ldots,8
, then each of the numbers in
S
is the number of
k
-rational bitangents of some smooth plane quartic
X
defined over
k
.

The remainder of this section is devoted to the proof of this theorem. First, we note that if

\mathcal{A}\in k^{3}\otimes\Sym_{2}k^{4}
is a tensor such that
X=Z(\det\mathcal{A}(\mathbf{x},\cdot,\cdot))
is a smooth plane quartic, then for any
p_{1},p_{2}\in\mathfrak{C}
, the bitangent
\psi(\ell(p_{1},p_{2}))
is defined over
k
if and only if the set
\{p_{1},p_{2}\}
is defined over
k
. We will also use the following theorem that describes the even theta characteristics of
X
.

Theorem 3.1.2

(
[
15
]
, Section 6.3.2, Theorem 6.3.3)
.

Write the Cayley octad as
\mathfrak{C}=\{p_{1},\dots,p_{8}\}
. Let
\theta_{i,j}
be the odd theta characteristic given by
\psi(\ell(p_{i},p_{j}))
. Then for any
\{i,j,k,l\}\subset\{1,\dots,8\}
, the line bundle

\theta_{i,j}\otimes\theta_{i,k}\otimes\theta_{i,l}\otimes\omega_{C}^{\vee}

is an even theta characteristic. Moreover, this line bundle is independent of the ordering of
i
,
j
,
k
, and
l
. For
\{i^{\prime},j^{\prime},k^{\prime},l^{\prime}\}\cap\{i,j,k,l\}=\emptyset
, we have

\theta_{i,j}\otimes\theta_{i,k}\otimes\theta_{i,l}\otimes\omega_{C}^{\vee}\cong\theta_{i^{\prime},j^{\prime}}\otimes\theta_{i^{\prime},k^{\prime}}\otimes\theta_{i^{\prime},l^{\prime}}\otimes\omega_{C}^{\vee}.

Lemma 3.1.3
.

If
X
does not have an even theta characteristic defined over
k
, then
X
has at most
4
odd theta characteristics defined over
k
.

Proof.

Let
X
be a smooth plane quartic over
k
with at least
5
rational bitangents and let
\mathcal{A}\in\bar{k}^{3}\otimes\Sym_{2}\bar{k}^{4}
be a tensor such that
X=Z(\det\mathcal{A}(\mathbf{x},\cdot,\cdot))
. Let
\{p_{1},\ldots,p_{8}\}
be the geometric points of the Cayley octad defined by
\mathcal{A}
, and let
\vartheta_{i,j}
denote the odd theta characteristic given by
\psi(\ell(p_{i},p_{j}))
. Note that because
\mathcal{A}
is not necessarily defined over
k
, the Galois action on
\{p_{1},\ldots,p_{8}\}
does not naturally correspond to a Galois action on the odd theta characteristics of
X
; we are simply using the Cayley octad to label the bitangents.

Let
G=(V,E)
be the graph where
V=\{1,\dots,8\}
and an edge exists between vertices
i
and
j
if and only if
\vartheta_{i,j}
is defined over
k
. There are at least
5
edges of
G
, so either
3
edges meet at a vertex or there are
3
edges
a
,
b
,
c
such that
a
and
b
are adjacent,
a
and
c
are not adjacent, and
b
and
c
are not adjacent. We may assume up to relabelling that
a=(1,2),b=(1,3),c=(4,5)\in E
. Then by Theorem
3.1.2

\vartheta_{1,2,3,4}=\vartheta_{1,2}\otimes\vartheta_{1,3}\otimes\vartheta_{1,4}\otimes\omega_{X}^{\vee}\cong\vartheta_{4,1}\otimes\vartheta_{4,2}\otimes\vartheta_{4,3}\otimes\omega_{X}^{\vee}

and thus

\vartheta_{1,2}\otimes\vartheta_{1,3}\cong\vartheta_{4,2}\otimes\vartheta_{4,3}.

Because
\vartheta_{1,2}\otimes\vartheta_{1,3}
is defined over
k
, so is
\vartheta_{4,2}\otimes\vartheta_{4,3}
. Thus, as
\vartheta_{4,5}
is defined over
k
, the even theta characteristic
\vartheta_{2,3,4,5}=\vartheta_{4,2}\otimes\vartheta_{4,3}\otimes\vartheta_{4,5}\otimes\omega_{X}^{\vee}
is as well.
∎

Proof of Theorem
3.1.1
.

We first prove that the number of odd theta characteristics defined over
k
is one of the numbers in
S
. If there does not exist an even theta characteristic defined over
k
, then apply by Lemma
3.1.3
. Otherwise, choose an even theta characteristic defined over the ground field and a corresponding symmetric determinantal representation of
X
. Suppose the Cayley octad for this representation has
n_{1}
points that are
k
-rational and
n_{2}
pairs of quadratic conjugate points, with
n_{1}+2n_{2}\leq 8
.
Then
X
has
{\binom{n_{1}}{2}}+n_{2}
bitangents defined over
k
. Note
S=\{\binom{n_{1}}{2}+n_{2}:0\leq n_{1},n_{2}\text{ and }n_{1}+2n_{2}\leq 8\}
.

The second part of the claim follows from the result of
[
16
]
. Any Galois action on the bitangents of
X
acts via a subgroup of
\operatorname{Sp}(6,\mathbb{F}_{2})
. This group contains a unique subgroup

up to conjugacy of index

; such a subgroup is the stabilizer of some even theta characteristic of

. The result:

Theorem 3.1.4

(
[
16

)
.

Let
k
be an infinite field of characteristic not 2, let

be a normal and separable

be an injective group homomorphism.
Then there exists a nonsingular quartic curve

over

finishes the proof of Theorem

∎

4.
Singularities of intersections of symmetroids

In this section we explore the relationship between singularities of an intersection of symmetroids defined by a tensor
\mathcal{A}
and the Cayley variety associated to
\mathcal{A}
. First, we describe this relationship set-theoretically and later give a more precise relationship in terms of morphisms of schemes.

Definition 4.0.1
.

Let
\mathcal{A}
be a block-diagonal tensor with blocks
\mathcal{A}^{(1)},\ldots,\mathcal{A}^{(r)}
and let
X
be the associated intersection of symmetroids. A point
x\in X
is an
essential singularity
if
x
is a singular point and
\corank{A}^{(l)}(x,\cdot,\cdot)\geq 2
for some
l
. If
x\in X
is singular, but not an essential singularity, it is an
accidental singularity
. The complement of the essential singularities in
X
is denoted by
X^{\mathrm{n.e.}}
.

If
X
is a complete intersection defined by
\mathcal{A}
and
\corank{A}^{(l)}(x,\cdot,\cdot)\geq 2
for some point
x
and block
\mathcal{A}^{(l)}
, then
x
is a singular point of
X
.

Definition 4.0.2
.

We say that accidental singularity
x\in X
is an
inherited singularity
if it is a singularity of
X^{\prime}\supset X
, where
X^{\prime}
is an intersection of symmetroids defined using a strict subset of the blocks of
\mathcal{A}
. A
coincident singularity
of
X
is an accidental singularity
x\in X
which is a singular point of two intersections of symmetroids
X^{\prime},X^{\prime\prime}\supset X
defined by two distinct strict subsets of the blocks of
\mathcal{A}
.

The corank of the Jacobian matrix of
X
classifies the coincident singularities of
X
.

Lemma 4.0.3
.

Let
X
be a complete intersection of symmetroids, let
x\in X
, and let
J(x)
be the Jacobian matrix for
X
at
x
. Then
\corank J(x)>1
if and only if
x
is a coincident singularity.

Proof.

If
X:=Z(f_{1},\ldots,f_{r})
is an intersection of the
r
symmetroids
Z(f_{l})
, we may assume that
J(x)
is the Jacobian matrix associated to
f_{1},\ldots,f_{r}
.
Since
X
is a complete intersection of symmetroids, we may assume that the rows of the Jacobian matrix are given by the equations for these symmetroids.
Suppose
\corank J(x)>1
and choose
2
linearly independent vectors
v
and
w
in the left kernel of
J(x)
. Take a linear combination to force one of the entries of
v
and a different entry of
w
to be zero. Then
v
and
w
correspond to linear combinations of two distinct subsets of the rows of
J(x)
, which shows that there are two symmetroids
X^{\prime}
and
X^{\prime\prime}
containing
X
with an accidental singularity at
x
. The converse follows as well.
∎

Every essential singularity of
\mathcal{A}
is an inherited singularity. Theorem
4.0.4
characterizes when an intersection of symmetroids has accidental singularities. Given a singular point
q
of
\mathfrak{C}
and a chosen lift
q\in\operatorname{H}^{0}(\mathbb{P}^{n},\mathcal{E})
, denote by
q_{l}
its restrictions to
\operatorname{H}^{0}(\mathbb{P}^{n},\mathcal{E}_{l})
, for
1\leq l\leq r
.

Theorem 4.0.4
.

Let
\mathcal{A}\in k^{n+1}\otimes\Sym_{2}k^{m+1}
be a block-diagonal tensor over a field
k
and let
X
be the associated intersection of symmetroids. Let
q\in\operatorname{H}^{0}(\mathbb{P}^{n},\mathcal{E})
represent a singular point of the Cayley variety associated to
\mathcal{A}
, with restrictions
q_{l}
to the summands of
\mathcal{E}
. Then:

(a)

If
\mathfrak{C}
is a complete intersection of
n+1
quadrics, then there exists a
p\in k^{n+1}
such that
\mathcal{A}(p,q,\cdot)=0
if and only if
q
represents a singular point of
\mathfrak{C}
.

(b)

If
\mathcal{A}(p,q,\cdot)=0
and each
q_{l}
is non-zero, then
p
is a singular point of
X
. If
X
is a complete intersection and
p\in X(\bar{k})
is an accidental singularity of
X
, then there exists a
q=(q_{1},\ldots,q_{r})
representing a point of
\mathfrak{C}(\bar{k})
such that each
q_{l}=\varphi_{l}(p)
and
\mathcal{A}(p,q,\cdot)=0
.

(c)

If some
q_{l}=0
, then
(q_{i})_{i\neq l}
represents a point of the Cayley variety for the tensor
\mathcal{A}^{(\neg l)}
obtained by deleting the
l
-th block of
\mathcal{A}
. Conversely, if
q^{\prime}:=(q_{1}^{\prime},\ldots,q_{r-1}^{\prime})
is a point of the Cayley variety obtained by deleting the
l
-th block, then
q:=(q_{1}^{\prime},\ldots,0,\ldots,q_{r-1}^{\prime})
is a point of
\mathfrak{C}
, where the
0
vector is spliced into the
l
-th component. Finally,
\mathcal{A}(p,q,\cdot)=0
if and only if
\mathcal{A}^{(\neg l)}(p,q^{\prime},\cdot)=0
.

Proof.

Denote the blocks of
\mathcal{A}
by
\mathcal{A}^{(1)},\ldots,\mathcal{A}^{(r)}
. Let
A_{0},\ldots,A_{n}
denote the slices of
\mathcal{A}
corresponding to the standard basis vectors and let
Q_{0},\ldots,Q_{n}
be the associated quadratic forms.

If the Cayley variety
\mathfrak{C}
associated to
\mathcal{A}
has a singular point
q
, then by definition the Jacobian matrix for
Z(Q_{0},\ldots,Q_{n})
has a rank degeneracy. If
\mathfrak{C}
is a complete intersection, this is equivalent to the existence of a non-zero vector
p
such that

p\cdot\left(\frac{\partial Q_{i}}{\partial y_{j}}(q)\right)_{i,j}=0.

Identify the linear forms
\frac{\partial Q_{i}}{\partial y_{j}}
with their vector of coefficients. Thus, the equation above is equivalent to

0=2\cdot\mathcal{A}(p,q,\cdot),

which completes the proof of part (a).

Since
\mathcal{A}
is block-diagonal, the projections
q_{1},\ldots,q_{r}
to the
r
subspaces corresponding to the blocks yields the equations
0=2\cdot\mathcal{A}^{(l)}(p,q_{l},\cdot)
. As
q\in\mathfrak{C}
, we have that
\mathcal{A}^{(l)}(\cdot,q,q)=0
. From the block-diagonal structure we have that
\sum_{l=1}^{r}\mathcal{A}^{(l)}(\cdot,q_{l},q_{l})=\mathcal{A}(\cdot,q,q)=0
.

If each
q_{l}
is non-zero, then by definition of
X
, we see that the point in
\mathbb{P}^{n}
associated to
p
lies on
X
. If any
\mathcal{A}^{(l)}(p,\cdot,\cdot)
has corank greater than
1
, then
p
is a singular point of
Z(\det\mathcal{A}^{(l)}(\mathbf{x},\cdot,\cdot))
, so
X
is singular at
p
. Alternatively, each block has corank
1
at
p
, so by definition of the kernel map
q_{l}=\varphi_{l}(p)
. We now consider the Jacobian matrix for
X
at
p
, which is given by

\left(\frac{\partial\det\mathcal{A}^{(l)}(\mathbf{x},\cdot,\cdot)}{\partial x_{j}}(p)\right)_{l,j}.

Jacobi’s formula gives that
\frac{\partial\det\mathcal{A}^{(l)}(\mathbf{x},\cdot,\cdot)}{\partial x_{j}}(p)=\trace\left(\operatorname{adj}\mathcal{A}^{(l)}(p,\cdot,\cdot)\cdot A_{j}^{(l)}\right)
. Since each
\mathcal{A}_{j}^{(l)}
has corank
1
at
p
, we have
\operatorname{adj}\mathcal{A}_{j}^{(l)}(p,\cdot,\cdot)
is a scalar multiple of
q_{l}q_{l}^{T}
. But as
q\in\mathfrak{C}
we have

\sum_{l=1}^{r}\trace\left(q_{l}q_{l}^{T}\cdot A_{j}^{(l)}\right)=\sum_{l=1}^{r}q_{l}^{T}A_{j}^{(l)}q_{l}=q^{T}A_{j}q=0.

Thus, there is a nontrivial relation among the rows of the Jacobian matrix
J(p)
for
X
at
p
, so
X
is singular at
p
. Conversely, suppose that
p
is a singular point of
X
and each
\mathcal{A}^{(l)}(p,\cdot,\cdot)
has corank
1
. Since
X
is a complete intersection, the singularity at
p
ensures there is a solution to
0=v\cdot J(p)
. From the corank assumption, we define
q_{l}:=\varphi_{l}(p)\neq 0
. The Jacobi formula then shows that
q:=(\sqrt{v_{1}}\ q_{1},\ldots,\sqrt{v_{r}}\ q_{r})
satisfies
\mathcal{A}(\cdot,q,q)=0
. On the other hand,
\mathcal{A}(p,q,\cdot)=0
. Note that different choices of signs for the square roots of the
v_{l}
correspond to applying automorphisms of
\mathcal{A}
to
q
.

If some
q_{l}=0
, let
\mathcal{A}^{(\neg l)}
be the tensor obtained from
\mathcal{A}
by deleting the
l
-th block. Then
\sum_{l^{\prime}\neq l}\mathcal{A}^{(l^{\prime})}(\cdot,q_{l^{\prime}},q_{l^{\prime}})=0
, which by definition means
q^{\prime}:=(q_{l^{\prime}})_{l^{\prime}\neq l}
is a point on the Cayley variety
\mathfrak{C}_{\mathcal{A}^{(\neg l)}}
for
\mathcal{A}^{(\neg l)}
. Furthermore, we see that
\ker\mathcal{A}^{(\neg l)}(\cdot,q^{\prime},\cdot)
is non-trivial, so
q^{\prime}
is a point of
\mathfrak{C}_{\mathcal{A}^{(\neg l)}}

4.2.
Singular subschemes of intersections of symmetroids

In this subsection we prove the main theorem on accidental singularities of intersections of symmetroids. Given a block-diagonal tensor
\mathcal{A}\in k^{n+1}\otimes(\bigoplus_{l=1}^{r}\Sym_{2}k^{d_{l}})
, its intersection of symmetroids

already lets us determine the accidental singularities of

defined by bilinear conditions. The procedure of the proof is to replace global objects with sheaves, and then to work locally. We make essential use of the kernel sheaf on

Lemma 4.2.1

Let

5.
Fibres of

In this section, we comment on how one can study the geometry of hyperplane sections via the fibres of

Viewing

as a multilinear map, we can use the restriction map

via contraction. The determinantal variety defined by

allows us to study the fibre of

over

. We will say that the tensor

is the

of

to

, then

is the aCM sheaf on

.

Proposition 5.0.1
.

Let

as in the standard setup. Let

(a)

Let

(b)

(c)

Since

by definition, we see that parts (a) and (b) follow from the fact that

is defined by a matrix of forms in

5.1.
Secants of Cayley varieties of dimension zero

Here, we show how the Cayley variety associated to a tensor
\mathcal{A}
is related to interesting hyperplane sections of the intersection of symmetroids determined by
\mathcal{A}
. First, we look at a necessary and sufficient condition for a fibre of
\psi
to contain a line.

Lemma 5.1.1
.

Let
\mathfrak{C}
be the Cayley variety associated to a tensor
\mathcal{A}
. Let
\ell
be a secant line of
\mathfrak{C}
not contained in

. Then
\psi(\ell)
is a single point in
\widehat{\mathbb{P}}^{n}
. Conversely, if
\ell
is a line such that
\psi(\ell)
is a single point, then it is a secant line of the Cayley variety.

Proof.

We first consider the case where the two intersection points of
\ell
with the Cayley variety are distinct. Let
p,q\in k^{m+1}
be two vectors representing the intersection points of
\ell
with
\mathfrak{C}
. Then

\ell=\mathbb{P}\{ap+bq:a,b\in k\}.

For
(a:b)\in\mathbb{P}^{1}
we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}(\cdot,ap+bq,ap+bq)

\displaystyle=a^{2}\mathcal{A}(\cdot,p,p)+2ab\mathcal{A}(\cdot,p,q)+b^{2}\mathcal{A}(\cdot,q,q)

\displaystyle=2ab\mathcal{A}(\cdot,p,q)

which defines a constant linear form. We now consider the case when
\ell
is tangent to
\mathfrak{C}
. Let
p\in k^{m+1}
represent the point of tangency, and let
v\in k^{m+1}
be a vector such that
\ell=\mathbb{P}\{ap+bv:a,b\in k\}
. Since
\ell
is tangent to
\mathfrak{C}
at
p
, we have the relation

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}(\cdot,p+\epsilon v,p+\epsilon v)

\displaystyle\equiv 2\epsilon\mathcal{A}(\cdot,p,v)\equiv 0\pmod{\epsilon^{2}}

over the ring
k[\epsilon]/\langle\epsilon^{2}\rangle
. But then
\mathcal{A}(\cdot,p,v)=0
, so
\psi
is constant along
\ell
.

We now prove the converse claim. Let
\ell
be a line in
\mathbb{P}^{m}
not contained in
\mathfrak{C}
where
\psi(\ell)
is a single point. Up to linear transformations of
\widehat{\mathbb{P}}^{n}
we may assume that
\psi(\ell)=[1:0:\ldots:0]
. Thus, if
A_{0}
is the
0

\mathcal{A}
and

the corresponding quadric in
\mathbb{P}^{m}
, we see

is not contained in

. As

is a hypersurface of degree
2
, we have that
\ell
meets
Q_{0}
in two points (counting multiplicity). Observe that the intersection
\ell\cap\mathfrak{C}
is precisely where
\ell
meets
Q_{0}
. In particular, either
\ell
is tangent to
\mathfrak{C}
at a single point or
\ell\cap Q_{0}
consists of two distinct points of
\mathfrak{C}
.
∎

and the multiplicity of

in the intersection is why we need to use a genericity hypothesis in the case of a quintic symmetroid. For canonical curves, we can remove the genericity hypothesis.

Proposition 5.1.5
.

Let
\mathcal{A}\in k^{n+1}\otimes(\bigoplus_{l=1}^{r}\Sym_{2}k^{d_{l}})
be a block-diagonal tensor, let
X
be the associated intersection of symmetroids, and let
\mathfrak{C}
be the associated Cayley variety. Assume that

is a smooth complete intersection of dimension
1

\mathfrak{C}
is a complete intersection of dimension
0

(a)

X
is either a canonical genus
3
curve, a canonical genus
4
curve lying on a cone, or a canonical genus
5
curve given as the intersection of three quadric cones each of rank
3
.

(b)

Let
\ell\subseteq\mathbb{P}^{m}
be a secant line of
\mathfrak{C}
and let

. Then
H
is tangent to
X
at each point of

.

(c)

Write

of size

and

the corresponding quadric in

if and only if

, where

by the identity and on

(-1)

Proof.

The hypotheses imply that

. However, each

and

. The three cases identified in part (a) are the only ones possible.

By Proposition
4.1.1
, if

is some subscheme, then

\bigcap_{x\in S}\psi(\ker\mathcal{A}(x,\cdot,\cdot))

is the locus in

tangent to

along

Let

is a quadric in

\psi(\ell)\in\bigcap_{x\in(X\cap H)(\bar{k})}\psi(\ker\mathcal{A}(x,\cdot,\cdot))

Finally, let

be a secant of
\mathfrak{C}
such that

is tangent to

. Let

is smooth and hence

Remark 5.1.6
.

We can say something more general in the case of Proposition

Corollary 5.1.7
.

Let
X
denote the intersection of symmetroids defined by a tensor
\mathcal{A}
from one of the following cases:

(a)

\mathcal{A}\in k^{3}\otimes\Sym_{2}k^{4}
(and

(c)

\mathcal{A}\in k^{5}\otimes(\Sym_{2}k^{2})^{\oplus 3}

(d)

\mathcal{A}\in k^{4}\otimes\Sym_{2}k^{5}

Let
\ell
be a secant line of the Cayley variety of

Proof.

For a generic

, it suffices to show that

is reduced, of dimension

, and of degree
n
. In turn, it suffices to exhibit a particular example where this is the case. This is easily done.

In cases (a-c), we now show that the result from the generic case implies the result for any smooth

. We fix our attention to case (a), the others being similar. Up to linear transformations and base extension, we may assume that
\ell
is the secant between the two points

of the Cayley variety. Let
\mathcal{A}
be a tensor defining a smooth curve

. We may choose
\mathcal{B}
to be a generic tensor of the same format as

. We consider the pencil of tensors

over

, which defines a family of curves

and hyperplanes

. We obtain a proper flat family of intersections

, whose coordinate ring

is a finite flat (thus free)

-algebra. By genericity of

, the intersection of the generic fibre of

and the generic fibre of

is a pair of double points. Thus, by
[

we have that the multiplicities of the geometric points of the special fibre of

∎

In these cases, we are particularly interested in the number of hyperplanes arising from secants of the Cayley variety. For a block-diagonal tensor

with

Lemma 5.1.8
.

Let
\mathcal{A}\in k^{n+1}\otimes(\bigoplus_{l=1}^{r}\Sym_{2}k^{d_{l}})
be a block-diagonal tensor, let
X
be the associated intersection of symmetroids, and let
\mathfrak{C}
be the Cayley variety. Assume that
X
is a complete intersection,
\mathfrak{C}
is a complete
0
-dimensional intersection, and that
\sum_{l\in I}d_{l}\neq\frac{m+1}{2}
for any
I\subset\{1,\ldots,r\}
. Let
\ell,\ell^{\prime}
be two secants of
\mathfrak{C}
such that
\psi(\ell)=\psi(\ell^{\prime})
and let
[H]:=\psi(\ell)
. If
X\cap H
has no essential singularities or coincident accidental singularities,
\dim\sing(X\cap H)=0
, and
\sing(X\cap H)
contains at least two distinct geometric points, then there is some
\sigma\in\DAut(\mathcal{A})
such that
\ell^{\prime}=\sigma(\ell)
.

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{B}
be the restriction of
\mathcal{A}
to
H
. We see from Theorem
1.1.1
that

Z:=\{(x,y)\in(X\cap H)\times\mathbb{P}^{m}:\mathcal{B}(x,y,\cdot)=0,\mathcal{B}(\cdot,y,y)=0\}

is a finite cover of
\sing(X\cap H)
of degree
2^{r-1}
via the projection to
X
and that
\DAut(\mathcal{B})/\langle\pm I\rangle
act transitively on the fibres of

.

Let

represent
\ell,\ell^{\prime}
, and let
\mathcal{M}\colon k^{n}\otimes L\otimes k^{m+1}\rightarrow k
,
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}\colon k^{n}\otimes L^{\prime}\otimes k^{m+1}\rightarrow k
denote the restriction of the multilinear map
\mathcal{B}
to
L,L^{\prime}
(respectively).
As we have seen before, because
\ell
is contained in the Cayley variety for

, we have that the degeneracy loci

\{(x,y)\in(X\cap H)\times\ell:\mathcal{M}(x,y,\cdot)=0\},\quad\{(x,y)\in(X\cap H)\times\ell^{\prime}:\mathcal{M}^{\prime}(x,y,\cdot)=0\}

are both subschemes of
Z
.
Choose some distinct points

. Then we may find
q_{1},q_{2}\in\ell
, and
q_{1}^{\prime},q_{2}^{\prime}\in\ell^{\prime}
such that

\mathcal{M}(p_{j},q_{j},\cdot)=\mathcal{M}^{\prime}(p_{j},q_{j}^{\prime},\cdot)=0

for
j=1,2
.
Up to applying an automorphism of
\mathcal{B}
, we may assume that
q_{1}=q_{1}^{\prime}
. We also have that
q_{2}^{\prime}=\sigma(q_{2})
for some
\sigma\in\DAut(\mathcal{B})
.

If
r=1
, then
\DAut(\mathcal{B})=\langle\pm I\rangle
and so
\ell=\ell^{\prime}
. Otherwise, suppose for the sake of contradiction that
\sigma\not\in\langle\pm I\rangle
. We endow
k^{n+1},k^{m+1}
with coordinates and write

\mathcal{B}:=\begin{bmatrix}\mathcal{B}^{(1)}&0\\
0&\mathcal{B}^{(2)}\end{bmatrix},\qquad\sigma=\begin{bmatrix}I&0\\
0&-I\end{bmatrix},

where either
\mathcal{B}^{(1)}
or
\mathcal{B}^{(2)}
may be further decomposable. We may write
q_{2}=(q_{2}^{(1)},q_{2}^{(2)})
and
\sigma(q_{2})=(q_{2}^{(1)},-q_{2}^{(2)})
. By definition of
\mathcal{M},\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
we see

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}(\cdot,q_{1},q_{2})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{B}^{(1)}(\cdot,q_{1}^{(1)},q_{2}^{(1)})+\mathcal{B}^{(2)}(\cdot,q_{1}^{(2)},q_{2}^{(2)})=0

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}^{\prime}(\cdot,q_{1},q_{2})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{B}^{(1)}(\cdot,q_{1}^{(1)},q_{2}^{(1)})-\mathcal{B}^{(2)}(\cdot,q_{1}^{(2)},q_{2}^{(2)})=0.

Thus, both terms are
0
, and by symmetry of
\mathcal{B}
we have
\mathcal{B}^{(1)}(\cdot,q_{2}^{(1)},q_{1}^{(1)})=\mathcal{B}^{(2)}(\cdot,q_{2}^{(2)},q_{1}^{(2)})=0
.

If

is the eigenspace decomposition for

, we have by the condition of the block sizes that (without loss of generality)

. Additionally, by considering the projection

of

to

, we have that

defines a linear map
\widehat{\psi}_{\mathcal{B}^{(1)}}\colon k^{n}\rightarrow(\pi(L)\otimes V_{1})^{\vee}
. The image lies in a space of dimension

. Since

is of dimension

,

, so the kernel must have dimension at least

∎

For a general generic complete intersection Cayley variety of dimension
0
, we can expect a secant of the Cayley variety to define a hyperplane with exactly

singularities.

Lemma 5.1.9
.

Let
\mathbb{P}^{1}\times\mathbb{P}^{n-1}\rightarrow\mathbb{P}^{2n-1}
be the Segre embedding. Choose
d_{1},\dots,d_{r}\in\mathbb{Z}_{>1}
such that
d_{1}+\dots+d_{r}=n
. Write an element
M\in\mathbb{P}^{2n-1}
as the projectivization of a
2\times n
matrix

\begin{bmatrix}a_{1}&a_{2}&\dots&a_{n}\\
b_{1}&b_{2}&\dots&b_{n}\\
\end{bmatrix}.

Let
L
be the linear subspace of
\mathbb{P}^{2n-1}
defined by the equations

b_{1}=a_{2},\;b_{d_{1}+1}=a_{d_{1}+2},\;b_{d_{2}+1}=a_{d_{2}+2},\;\dots,\;a_{d_{r-1}+2}=b_{d_{r-1}+1}.

Then the intersection of
L
and the image of
\mathbb{P}^{1}\times\mathbb{P}^{n-1}
is a smooth variety of dimension
n-r
.

Proof.

It suffices to assume that

; thus we will show that the intersection of the hyperplane
a_{2}=b_{1}
with the Segre variety is a smooth variety of dimension
n-1
. As the Segre variety is irreducible and is not contained in the hyperplane
Z(a_{2}-b_{1})
; the intersection clearly has dimension
n-1
. If
n\leq 3
, a computation shows that the intersection of
L
and the image of
\mathbb{P}^{1}\times\mathbb{P}^{n-1}
is a smooth variety of dimension
n-1
. By induction on

it suffices to demonstrate the claim on the open set
U\coloneqq D(\prod_{i=3}^{n}a_{i}b_{i})
. Let
\sigma
denote the Segre embedding.

First, we we prove that the intersection

L\cap\im(\sigma)\cap D(\prod_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}b_{i})

is smooth. On
D(\prod_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}b_{i})
, the Segre variety is a complete intersection of the equations
\{a_{i}b_{i+1}-a_{i+1}b_{i}\}_{1\leq i<n}
. Let
M
be the
n\times 2n
matrix defined as follows. For
1\leq i<n
and
1\leq j\leq n
, let the entry in the
i
-th row and
j
-th column be
\frac{\partial(a_{i}b_{i+1}-a_{i+1}b_{i})}{\partial a_{j}}
. For
1\leq i<n
and
n<j\leq 2n
, let the entry in the
i
-th row and
j
-th column be
\frac{\partial(a_{i}b_{i+1}-a_{i+1}b_{i})}{\partial b_{j-n}}
. For
1\leq j\leq n
, let the entry in the
n
-th row and
j
-th column be
\frac{\partial(a_{2}-b_{1})}{\partial a_{j}}
, and for
n<j\leq 2n
, let the entry in the
n
-th row and
j
-th column be
\frac{\partial(a_{2}-b_{1})}{\partial b_{j-n}}
. Then notice that the determinant of

is invertible on

; thus the intersection

L\cap\im(\sigma)\cap D(\prod_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}b_{i})

Now consider the locus
Z(a_{1}a_{2}b_{1}b_{2})\cap U
. Note that for

, the variety

is a smooth complete intersection in

isomorphic to the Segre image of
\mathbb{P}^{1}\times\mathbb{P}^{n-3}
, given by the equations

(a_{3}b_{4}-a_{4}b_{3},\dots,a_{n-1}b_{n}-a_{n}b_{n-1},a_{1},b_{1},a_{2},b_{2}).

As the Segre image of
\mathbb{P}^{1}\times\mathbb{P}^{n-3}

∎

6.
Genus 4: sextic space curves and tritangents

In this section we consider tensors
\mathcal{A}\in k^{4}\otimes(\Sym_{2}k^{2}\oplus\Sym_{2}k^{3})
. The tensor
\mathcal{A}\in k^{4}\otimes\left(\Sym_{2}k^{2}\oplus\Sym_{2}k^{3}\right)
defines a quadric cone, denoted by

, and a symmetric determinantal cubic surface, denoted by

. The intersection of symmetroids generically defines a canonical genus
4

\mathbb{P}^{3}
. We study the relationship between the orbits of the action of
\GL_{4}(k)\times\GL_{2}(k)\times\GL_{3}(k)
on

and the moduli of canonical genus
4
curves with a vanishing even theta characteristc. Secondly, the Cayley variety associated to
\mathcal{A}
is generically a smooth intersection of
4
quadrics in
\mathbb{P}^{4}
and is therefore
16
points. We have
\DAut(\mathcal{A})=\langle-I,\sigma\rangle
, where
\sigma([y_{0}:y_{1}:y_{2}:y_{3}:y_{4}])=[y_{0}:y_{1}:-y_{2}:-y_{3}:-y_{4}]
. The connection between the tritangents of a genus
4

[
21
]
and its generalizations by Bruin-Sertöz
[
9

[

can be interpreted in terms of tensors.

Throughout this section we use a specific convention regarding notation. We use the notation
X_{2},X_{3}
and subscripts

accordingly so that the index reflects the degree of the corresponding hypersurface or block.

6.1.
Moduli

We say that the tensor
\mathcal{A}
is
nondegenerate
if the associated genus
4
curve is smooth. The vector space

has a natural

If

and
4.0.8
. Thus, by Theorem
4.0.4
we see that the Cayley variety associated to

is a smooth complete intersection of dimension
0
.

We consider genus
4
curves

with a vanishing even theta characteristic
\theta_{0}

\Jac(X)[2]_{\mathrm{odd}}:=\{[\theta-\theta_{0}]\in\Jac(X)[2]:\theta\text{ is an odd theta characteristic}\}.

Our main result of this subsection is:

Theorem 6.1.1
.

Let

be a scheme over

A vanishing even theta characteristic of

-module whose rank is even and greater than

6.5.
Tensorification of Recillas’ construction

For genus
4
curves with a vanishing even theta characteristic, we show how to realize Recillas’ construction in terms of tensors. If
Y
is a hyperelliptic genus
3
curve defined by
y^{2}=-F(x,z)
, we can represent an open subscheme of
\Sym^{4}Y
by a pair of polynomials
(a(x,z),b(x,z)-y)
such that
a,b
are homogeneous forms of degree
4
and
b^{2}+F\equiv 0\pmod{a}
. This is the Mumford representation of such a divisor
[
10
]
. Not every divisor in
\Sym^{4}Y
can be represented in this way; if
\pi\colon Y\rightarrow\mathbb{P}^{1}
is the canonical map, the collection of divisors which do not have a Mumford representation are of the form
D^{\prime}+\pi^{*}(p)
for some
D^{\prime}\in\Div^{2}Y_{k^{\mathrm{al}}}
and
p\in\mathbb{P}^{1}(k^{\mathrm{al}})
. We will say that a divisor in
\Sym^{4}Y
is
semireduced
if it admits a Mumford representation with
a,b
binary quartics. We will say that the pair of divisors
\{D_{1},D_{2}\}
of
Y
is
residual
if
D_{1}
is the image of
D_{2}
under the hyperelliptic involution.

Proposition 6.5.1
.

There is a bijection between
\GL_{2}\times\GL_{2}
-orbits of
\Sym^{4}k^{2}\otimes\Sym_{2}k^{2}
and hyperelliptic genus
3
curves
Y
with a residual pair of semireduced rational divisors
D_{1},D_{2}\in\Sym^{4}(Y)(k)
.

Proof.

If
Y
is defined by
y^{2}=-F(x,z)
and
(a,b-y)
is the Mumford representation of a divisor
D
in
\Sym^{4}(Y)(k)
, then
F=ac-b^{2}
for some binary quartic polynomial
c
. We obtain the determinantal representation
\begin{bmatrix}a&\pm b\\
\pm b&c\end{bmatrix}
. Note that the choice of sign is irrelevant, as the two determinantal representations are equivalent under
\{\id\}\times\GL_{2}
.

We see that the morphisms
|D_{1}|,|D_{2}|\colon Y\rightarrow\mathbb{P}^{1}
of degree
4
are explicitly represented by
(x,y,z)\mapsto(b\pm y:c)
. The natural
\PGL_{2}
action on the morphism
(b-y:c)\colon Y\rightarrow\mathbb{P}^{1}
is identified with the natural action of
\{\id\}\times\SL_{2}
on the tensor. The action of
\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\
0&-1\end{bmatrix}
on the tensor exchanges the two morphisms.
∎

Remark 6.5.2
.

Scaling the defining equation of a hyperellitpic curve by a constant does not change the orbit under
\GL_{2}(\bar{k})

With

gives a surjection

of degree

7.2.
Theta characteristics

We can identify
\mathbb{P}^{5}
with
\mathbb{P}(\operatorname{H}^{0}(X,\theta_{1})\oplus\operatorname{H}^{0}(X,\theta_{2})\oplus\operatorname{H}^{0}(X,\theta_{3}))

\DAut(\mathcal{A})
is of order
8

\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2},\sigma_{3}

\sigma_{l}
acts on
\operatorname{H}^{0}(X,\theta_{1})\oplus\operatorname{H}^{0}(X,\theta_{2})\oplus\operatorname{H}^{0}(X,\theta_{3})

-1
in the
l

The action on the
32
geometric points of
\mathfrak{C}
has
8
orbits, each of size
4
.
The action of
\DAut(\mathcal{A})
on the
496
secants of the Cayley variety has
136
orbits, giving
136
distinct images under the
\psi
map. Proposition
5.1.5
allows us to tabulate these images:

(1)

There are
24\times 2
secants of the form
\ell(p,\sigma_{l}(p))
for some
p\in\mathfrak{C}(\bar{k})
and
l\in\{1,2,3\}
.
The image under
\psi
of a secant of the form
\ell(p,\sigma_{l}(p))
gives rise to an effective representative of
\theta_{l}
. For each
\theta_{l}
, there are
8
distinct orbits of secants giving rise to an effective representative of
\theta_{l}
. Given any point
p\in\mathfrak{C}(\bar{k})
, the orbit
\{p,\sigma_{1}(p),\sigma_{2}(p),\sigma_{3}(p)\}

6

, the pair
\{\ell(p,\sigma_{1}(p)),\ell(\sigma_{2}(p),\sigma_{3}(p))\}

\theta_{1}
. Similarly, the other two orbits give rise to effective representatives of
\theta_{2}
and
\theta_{3}

(2)

112\times 4

112

7.3.
Weil pairing

In the case of genus
5
curves, the three distinguished vanishing even theta characteristics
\theta_{1},\theta_{2},\theta_{3}
define a distinguished
2
-torsion subgroup
\langle\theta_{2}-\theta_{1},\theta_{3}-\theta_{1}\rangle\subset J_{X}[2]
. Just like in the genus
4
case, the
112
odd theta characteristics obtained from this construction also have a special property with respect to the Weil pairing.

Proposition 7.3.1
.

Let
\mathcal{A}\in k^{5}\otimes(\Sym_{2}k^{2})^{\oplus 3}
be a nondegenerate tensor, let
\theta_{1},\theta_{2},\theta_{3}
denote the three associated vanishing even theta characteristics. Denote the Weil pairing on
\Jac(X)[2]
by
e_{2}
. Then the
112
distinct odd theta characteristics constructed from the secants of the Cayley variety of
\mathcal{A}
are precisely the odd theta characteristics
\theta
such that
e_{2}(\theta\otimes\theta_{l}^{\vee},T)=0
for all
T\in\{\theta_{i}\otimes\theta_{j}^{\vee}:1\leq i,j\leq 3\}
and for some (equivalently, all)
1\leq l\leq 3
.

Proof.

Let
W:=\{\theta_{i}\otimes\theta_{j}^{\vee}:1\leq i,j\leq 3\}
denote the distinguished
2
-torsion subgroup.
We give the argument to show that
e_{2}(\theta-\theta_{1},\theta_{2}-\theta_{1})=0
. Since
W
is an isotropic subspace of
e_{2}
, it follows whenceforth that
e_{2}(\theta-\theta_{1},T)=0
for all
T\in W
.
First, we establish that
h^{0}(\theta+\theta_{2}-\theta_{1})=1
. We consider the projections from
V:=\operatorname{H}^{0}(\theta_{1})\oplus\operatorname{H}^{0}(\theta_{2})\oplus\operatorname{H}^{0}(\theta_{3})
onto the various factors and denote these by
\pi_{i},\pi_{ij}
for
i,j=1,2,3
. If
\ell
is a secant line of
\mathfrak{C}
giving rise to an odd theta characteristic, then
\ell
surjects onto each
|\theta_{l}|\cong\mathbb{P}^{1}
for
l=1,2,3
, and additionally the projection restricted to
\ell
is a morphism of degree
1
. In particular, it follows that the image of
\ell
under
\pi_{12}\colon\mathbb{P}V\dashrightarrow|\theta_{1}|\times|\theta_{2}|
is a curve of bidegree
(1,1)
.

Let
[H]=\psi(\ell)
and let
\{x_{1},x_{2},x_{3},x_{4}\}=X\cap H
. We see that each quadric
Q_{x_{i}}
is rank
3
, so
\sing(Q_{x_{i}})
is a plane in
\mathbb{P}V
. Furthermore, each block of
\mathcal{A}
has size
2
, so each
\pi_{l}(\sing(Q_{x_{i}}))\subset|\theta_{l}|
is a point, so
\pi_{12}(\sing(Q_{x_{i}}))\subset|\theta_{1}|\times|\theta_{2}|
is also a point. Moreover, as
\sing(Q_{x_{i}})
meets
\ell
, the point
\pi_{12}(\sing(Q_{x_{i}}))
lies on the
(1,1)
curve from before.

Next, we have that
\theta+\theta_{2}-\theta_{1}\sim\theta_{1}+\theta_{2}-\theta
. We observe that the image of
X
under

(\varphi_{1},\varphi_{2})\colon X\rightarrow|\theta_{1}|\times|\theta_{2}|

must contain the
4
points
\pi_{12}(\sing(Q_{x_{i}}))
, so
\pi_{12}(\ell)
defines an effective section of
\theta_{1}+\theta_{2}-\theta
.

Let
\mathcal{Q}_{1}
denote the quadratic form associated to the even theta characteristic
\theta_{1}
.
We now use Mumford’s formula for the Weil pairing. Observe that if
T^{\prime}
is a non-trivial
2
-torsion class we have
h^{0}(X,T^{\prime})=0
, so

\displaystyle e_{2}(\theta-\theta_{1},\theta_{2}-\theta_{1})

\displaystyle\equiv\mathcal{Q}_{1}(\theta+\theta_{2}-2\theta_{1})-\mathcal{Q}_{1}(\theta-\theta_{1})-\mathcal{Q}_{1}(\theta_{2}-\theta_{1})

\displaystyle\equiv\mathcal{Q}_{1}(\theta+\theta_{2}-2\theta_{1})-\left(h^{0}(\theta-\theta_{1})+h^{0}(\theta)\right)-\left(h^{0}(\theta_{2}-\theta_{1})+h^{0}(\theta_{2})\right)

\displaystyle\equiv\mathcal{Q}_{1}(\theta+\theta_{2}-2\theta_{1})+1

\displaystyle\equiv h^{0}(\theta+\theta_{2}-2\theta_{1})+h^{0}(\theta+\theta_{2}-\theta_{1})+1\pmod{2}.

Next, since we have that
\mathcal{Q}_{1}(\theta-\theta_{1})=1
and
\mathcal{Q}_{1}(\theta_{2}-\theta_{1})=0
, the sum
\theta+\theta_{2}-2\theta_{1}
is not the trivial class. Finally, we see that
h^{0}(\theta+\theta_{2}-\theta_{1})=1
, and this concludes the proof.
∎

8.
Quintic symmetroid surfaces: tritangents

A general element
\mathcal{A}\in k^{4}\otimes\Sym_{2}k^{5}
defines a determinantal surface
X
in
\mathbb{P}^{3}
classically called a quintic symmetroid. The Cayley variety associated to
\mathcal{A}
is a complete intersection of
4
quadrics in
\mathbb{P}^{4}
, so generically defines a locus of
16
points and thus
\binom{16}{2}=120
secants. The singular locus of
X
consists of
20
nodal singularities and
\varphi(X)
is a smooth degree
10
surface in
\mathbb{P}^{4}
birational to
X
; it is the normalization of
X
.

It was shown in
[
25
]
that a generic quintic symmetroid surface has at least
120
tritangent planes. Roth constructs these
120
tritangent planes as we do, via the secants of the
16
points of the Cayley variety
[
25
, Section 4.2]
. In light of Proposition
5.1.2
, it is immediate why these secants correspond to tritangent planes, simplifying Roth’s proof. This particular set of
120
tritangents is distinguished by the determinantal representation. We say
H
is a
distinguished
tritangent of
\mathcal{A}
if the contact points
x_{1},x_{2},x_{3}
are smooth points of
X
and the three points
\varphi(x_{1})
,
\varphi(x_{2})
,
\varphi(x_{3})
are collinear.

Corollary 8.0.1
.

Let
\mathcal{A}\in k^{4}\otimes\Sym_{2}k^{5}
be a generic tensor. Then
there are
120
distinguished tritangent planes of
\mathcal{A}
; each of these is of the form
[H]=\psi(\ell)
, where
\ell
is one of the
120
secant lines of
\mathfrak{C}
.

Proof.

First consider
\ell
a secant line of
\mathfrak{C}
. Let
x_{1}
,
x_{2}
,
x_{3}
be the contact points of
H
with
X
. This follows from the fact that the incidence variety

\{(x,q)\in X\times\ell:\mathcal{A}(x,q,\cdot)=0\}

consists of
3
points; c.f. the proof of Proposition
5.1.2
. Thus,
\varphi(x_{1})
,
\varphi(x_{2})
, and
\varphi(x_{3})
all lie on
\ell
and so
H
is a distinguished tritangent. The tritangents defined by the secants of
\mathfrak{C}
are distinct by Lemma
5.1.8
.

Conversely, suppose
H
is a distinguished tritangent plane with contact points
x_{1}
,
x_{2}
,
x_{3}
. Let
\ell
be the line in
\mathbb{P}^{4}
containing
\varphi(x_{1}),\varphi(x_{2}),\varphi(x_{3})
. Since
\theta_{X}=\psi\circ\varphi
, we have that
\psi^{-1}([H])
contains
\varphi(x_{1}),\varphi(x_{2}),\varphi(x_{3})
. However, any quadric containing these three points contains
\ell
. Since
\psi^{-1}([H])
is defined by a complete intersection of three quadrics,
\ell\subset\psi^{-1}([H])
. The only lines contracted by
\psi
are secant lines of
\mathfrak{C}
.
∎
