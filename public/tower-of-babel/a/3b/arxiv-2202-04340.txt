Efficient Construction of Reversible Transducers from Regular Transducer Expressions
Luc Dartois, Paul Gastin, R. Govind, Shankara Narayanan Krishna

Abstract
The class of regular transformations has several equivalent characterizations such as functional MSO transductions, deterministic two-way transducers, streaming string transducers, as well as regular transducer expressions (RTE). For algorithmic applications, it is very common and useful to transform a specification, here, an RTE, to a machine, here, a transducer. In this paper, we give an efficient construction of a two-way reversible transducer (2RFT) equivalent to a given RTE. 2RFTs are a well behaved class of transducers which are deterministic and co-deterministic (hence allows evaluation in linear time w.r.t. the input word), and where composition has only polynomial complexity. We show that, for full RTE, the constructed 2RFT has size doubly exponential in the size of the expression, while, if the RTE does not use Hadamard product or chained-star, the constructed 2RFT has size exponential in the size of the RTE.

Related Work
. A paper which has looked at the evaluation of transducer expressions is
[
1
]
.
Here, the authors investigate the complexity of evaluation of a

]

\mathsf{lu}
-semantics. A
major difference between
[
1
]
and our paper is that
[
1
]
does not construct

difficulty in coming up with an automaton for the

-semantics. Instead,

[
1

To the best of our knowledge, our paper is the first one to efficiently construct a 2RFT

This 2RFT may be used to solve algorithmic problems on transformations

One such problem is indeed the evaluation of any number of input words
w

|w|
. Note that
[

]
also evaluates with

\mathsf{lu}
-semantics.
The consistent semantics is also more restrictive than our

Automata.

Let
\Sigma
be an
alphabet
, i.e., a finite set of letters.
A word
u
over
\Sigma
is a possibly empty sequence of letters.
The set of words is denoted
\Sigma^{*}
, with
\varepsilon
denoting the empty word. Given an alphabet
\Sigma

\Sigma_{\vdash\dashv}
the set
\Sigma\uplus\{\vdash,\dashv\}
, where
\vdash
and
\dashv
are two fresh symbols called the left and right endmarkers.
A
two-way finite state automaton
(2NFA) is a tuple
\mathcal{A}=(\Sigma,Q,q_{I},F,\Delta)
, where
\Sigma
is a finite alphabet,

Q
is a finite set of states partitioned into the set of forward states
Q^{+}
and the set of backward states
Q^{-}
,

q_{I}\in Q^{+}
is the initial state,

F\subseteq Q^{+}
is the set of final states,

\Delta\subseteq Q\times\Sigma_{\vdash\dashv}\times Q
is the state transition relation.
By convention,
q_{I}
and
q_{F}\in F
are the only forward states verifying
(q_{I},\vdash,q)\in\Delta

and
(q,\dashv,q_{F})\in\Delta
for some
q\in Q
.
However, for any backward state
p^{-}\in Q^{-}
,
\Delta
might contain transitions
(p^{-},\vdash,q)

and
(q,\dashv,p^{-})
, for some
q\in Q
.

Before defining the semantics of our two-way automata,
let us remark that we choose one of several equivalently expressive semantics of two-way. The particularity of the one we chose, which is the one in
[
6
]
, is that
(1)
the reading head is put between positions rather than on, and
(2)
the set of states is divided into
+
states and
-
states. The advantage of this semantics is that the sign of a state defines what position the head reads both before and after this state in a valid run.
A
+
state (resp.
-
state) reads the position to its right (resp. to its left) and the previous position read was on its left (resp. on its right).
Intuitively, in a transition
(p,a,q)
both states move the reading head half a position, either to the right for
+
states or to the left for
-
states. Hence if
p
and
q
are of different signs, the reading head does not move, but the position read will be different.

We now formally define the semantics.
A configuration

of
\mathcal{A}

such that

.
The configuration

If

, the input head currently reads the first letter of the suffix
u^{\prime}=a^{\prime}v^{\prime}
.
The successor of

after a transition

if

, or

.
Conversely, if

.
The successor of

after
(p,a,q)\in\Delta

if

, or
v.q.au^{\prime}
if
q\in Q^{-}
.
A

on a word

is a sequence of successive configurations

such that for every

The run

The language
\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{A}}

such that

admits an accepting run.
The automaton

is called

•

a
one-way finite state automaton
(1NFA) if the set

,

•

deterministic
(2DFA) if for all

, there is at most one

verifying
(p,a,q)\in\Delta
,

•

if for all

, there is at most one

verifying
(p,a,q)\in\Delta
and

•

reversible

Transducers.

A
two-way finite state transducer
(2NFT) is a tuple
\mathcal{T}=(\Sigma,\Gamma,Q,q_{I},F,\Delta,\mu)
, where
\Gamma
is a finite alphabet;

\mathcal{A}_{\mathcal{T}}=(\Sigma,Q,q_{I},F,\Delta)
is a 2NFA, called the
underlying automaton
of
\mathcal{T}
;
and
\mu:\Delta\rightarrow\Gamma^{*}
is the output function.
A run of
\mathcal{T}
is a run of its underlying automaton, and the language
\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{T}}
recognized by
\mathcal{T}

is the language
\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{A}_{\mathcal{T}}}\subseteq\Sigma^{*}
recognized by its underlying automaton.
Given a run
\varrho
of
\mathcal{T}
, we set
\mu(\varrho)\in\Gamma^{*}
as the concatenation of the images by
\mu
of the transitions of
\mathcal{T}

occurring along
\varrho
.
The transduction
\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{T}}\subseteq\Sigma^{*}\times\Gamma^{*}
defined by
\mathcal{T}
is the set of pairs
(u,v)

such that
u\in\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{T}}
and
\mu(\varrho)=v
for an accepting run
\varrho
of

\mathcal{A}_{\mathcal{T}}
on
{\vdash}u{\dashv}
.
Two transducers are called
equivalent
if they define the same transduction.
A transducer
\mathcal{T}
is respectively called one-way (1NFT), deterministic (2DFT),
co-deterministic or reversible (2RFT), if its underlying automaton has the corresponding
property.

Note that while a generic transducer defines a relation over words, a deterministic, co-deterministic or a reversible one defines a (partial) function from the input words to the output words since any input word has at most one accepting run, and hence at most one image. Extracting a maximal function from a relation is called a
uniformization
of a relation.
Formally, given a relation on words
R\subseteq\Sigma^{*}\times\Gamma^{*}
,
a
uniformization
of
R
is a function
f
such that:

•

\mathsf{dom}(f)=\mathsf{dom}(R)=\{u\in\Sigma^{*}\mid\exists v\in\Gamma^{*},(u,v)\in R\}

•

\forall u\in\mathsf{dom}(f),(u,f(u))\in R
.

Intuitively, a uniformization chooses, for each left component of
R
, a unique right component.
If
R
is already a function, then it is its only possible uniformization.

Reversible transducers can be composed easily, and the composition can be done with a single machine having a polynomial number of states.
Hence, when dealing with two-way machines, it is always beneficial to handle reversible machines.
To this end, we specialize results from
[
6
]
and
[
11
]
respectively.

Lemma 1
.

Let
\mathcal{T}
be a 1NFT with
n
states.
Then we can construct a reversible 2RFT
\mathcal{T}^{\prime}
such that
[\![\mathcal{T}^{\prime}]\!]
is a
uniformization of
[\![\mathcal{T}]\!]
and
\mathcal{T}^{\prime}
has at most
144n^{2}2^{2n}
states.

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{T}
be a 1NFT and
n
its number of states.
We write
\mathcal{T}^{\prime}
as the composition
D\circ C
where
C
is a co-deterministic one-way transducer and
D
is a deterministic one.
The co-deterministic transducer
C
is a classical powerset construction that computes and adds to the input the set of co-reachable states of
\mathcal{T}
.
Its number of states is at most
2^{n}
.
The set of states of the deterministic transducer
D
is the same as
\mathcal{T}
, and at each
step, if it is in a state
q
, it uses the information given by
C
to select a successor
of
q
that is also co-reachable.
It can be made deterministic by using an arbitrary global order on the set of states of
\mathcal{T}
. Its number of states is then
n
.

We conclude on the size of
\mathcal{T}^{\prime}
using two theorems from
[
6
]
, stating that
C
can be made into a reversible two-way
C^{\prime}
with
4m^{2}
states with

the number of states of
C
(Theorem 2) and that

can be made into a reversible
D^{\prime}
with
36n^{2}
states (Theorem 3).
Finally,
\mathcal{T}^{\prime}
is defined as the composition

, whose number of states is at most
36n^{2}\cdot 4(2^{n})^{2}=144n^{2}2^{2n}
.
∎

We will also need a more specific result for computing the complement of an automaton. We rely on Proposition 4 of

.

Lemma 2
.

Let

be a 1NFT with

Then we can compute a 2RFT

is the complement of

, and

has at most

The proof is straightforward. First, we transform

into a deterministic automaton

by doing a classical powerset construction.
Then
C
has

states. By inverting the accepting states, we obtain

which is deterministic and recognizes the complement of

.
We conclude by using Proposition 4 of
[

]
, which states that from a

by adding

The resulting automaton

3
Transducer expressions and their semantics

In this section, we formally define
RTE
, and then propose
the most natural relational semantics. Then we
define the unambiguous domain of a relation,
and propose
our global unambiguous semantics (called unambiguous semantics from here on)
as a restriction
of the relational semantics to the unambiguous domain. As already mentioned
in the introduction, this semantics refines the unambiguous semantics which has been proposed in earlier papers. Finally, we state the main results of the paper, and an overview of our results.

Regular transducer expressions (
RTE
s).
Let
\Sigma
be the input alphabet and
\Gamma
be the output alphabet.
For the combinator expressions, we use the following syntax:

h::={e}\triangleright{v}\mid h+h\mid h\cdot h\mid h\cdot_{r}h\mid h^{\star}\mid h_{r}^{\star}\mid h\odot h\mid[e,h]^{k\star}\mid[e,h]_{r}^{k\star}

where
e
is a regular expression over
\Sigma
,
v\in\Gamma^{\star}
and
k\geq 1
.

The semantics of the basic expression
{e}\triangleright{v}
is the partial function with
(constant) value
v
and domain
L(e)
, the regular language denoted by
e
. For
instance, the semantics of
{\varnothing}\triangleright{v}
is the partial function with empty
domain and the semantics of
{\Sigma^{\star}}\triangleright{v}
is the
total
constant
function with value
v
. We use
\bot
and
v
as macros to respectively
denote
{\varnothing}\triangleright{\varepsilon}
and
{\Sigma^{*}}\triangleright{v}
.

Since our goal is to construct “small” transducers from
RTE
s, we have to define
formally the
size
of expressions. We use the classical syntax for regular
expressions over
\Sigma
:

e::=\varnothing\mid\varepsilon\mid a\mid e+e\mid e\cdot e\mid e^{\star}

where
a\in\Sigma
.
We also define inductively the number of literals occurring in a regular expression
e
,
denoted by
\mathsf{nl}(e)
:
\mathsf{nl}(\varepsilon)=\mathsf{nl}(\varnothing)=0
,

\mathsf{nl}(a)=1
for
a\in\Sigma
,
\mathsf{nl}(e_{1}+e_{2})=\mathsf{nl}(e_{1}\cdot e_{2})=\mathsf{nl}(e_{1})+\mathsf{nl}(e_{2})
and
\mathsf{nl}(e_{1}^{\star})=\mathsf{nl}(e_{1})
.
Notice that we have
\mathsf{nl}(e)\leq|e|
for all regular expressions
e
, where
|e|
denotes the standard size of expressions. Actually, if

e
is not a single letter
a\in\Sigma
, we even have
1+\mathsf{nl}(e)\leq|e|
.

Now, we define the size
|h|
of a regular transducer expression
h
.
For the base case, we define
|{e}\triangleright{v}|=1+(1+\mathsf{nl}(e))+\max(1,|v|)
.
Note that when
v=\varepsilon
it still contributes 1 to the size of

{e}\triangleright{\varepsilon}
since it appears as a symbol.
Also, we have chosen that the regular expression
e
contributes to
1+\mathsf{nl}(e)
in
this size. This is because the number of states of the Glushkov automaton associated with

e
(which will be used in our construction) is
1+\mathsf{nl}(e)
. As discussed above,
unless
e
is a single letter from
\Sigma
, we have
1+\mathsf{nl}(e)\leq|e|
(and
otherwise
\mathsf{nl}(e)=1=|e|
).
For the inductive cases, we let
|f+g|=|f\cdot g|=|f\cdot_{r}g|=|f\odot g|=1+|f|+|g|
,
|f^{\star}|=|f_{r}^{\star}|=1+|f|
, and

|[e,f]^{k\star}|=|[e,f]_{r}^{k\star}|=1+\mathsf{nl}(e)+|f|+k+1
.

3.1
Relational semantics

In general, the semantics of a regular transducer expression
h
is a relation

[\![h]\!]^{R}\subseteq\Sigma^{\star}\times\Gamma^{\star}
. This is due to the fact that input
words may be parsed in several ways according to a given expression. For instance, when
applying a Cauchy product
h=f\cdot g
to an input word
w\in\Sigma^{\star}
, we split

w=uv
and we output the concatenation of
f
applied to
u
and
g
applied to
v
.
There might be several decompositions
w=uv
with
u\in\mathsf{dom}(f)
and
v\in\mathsf{dom}(g)
, in
which case, the parsing is ambiguous and
h
applied to
w
may result in several outputs.

We define inductively for an
RTE

h
, the domain
\mathsf{dom}(h)\subseteq\Sigma^{\star}
and the
relational semantics
[\![h]\!]^{R}\subseteq\Sigma^{\star}\times\Gamma^{\star}
. As usual, for

u\in\Sigma^{\star}
, we let
[\![h]\!]^{R}(u)=\{v\in\Gamma^{\star}\mid(u,v)\in[\![h]\!]^{R}\}
.

We also define simultaneously the
unambiguous domain

\mathsf{udom}(h)\subseteq\mathsf{dom}(h)

which is the set of words
w\in\mathsf{dom}(h)
such that parsing
w
according to
h
is
unambiguous. This is used in the next subsection to define a functional semantics.

•

h={e}\triangleright{v}
: As already discussed, we set

[\![{e}\triangleright{v}]\!]^{R}=\{(u,v)\mid u\in L(e)\}

and
\mathsf{udom}(h)=\mathsf{dom}(h)=L(e)
.

•

h=f+g
: We have
\mathsf{dom}(f+g)=\mathsf{dom}(f)\cup\mathsf{dom}(g)
,

[\![f+g]\!]^{R}=[\![f]\!]^{R}\cup[\![g]\!]^{R}

and
\mathsf{udom}(f+g)=(\mathsf{udom}(f)\setminus\mathsf{dom}(g))\cup(\mathsf{udom}(g)\setminus\mathsf{dom}(f))
.

•

h=f\cdot g
(Cauchy product): We have
\mathsf{dom}(f\cdot g)=\mathsf{dom}(f)\cdot\mathsf{dom}(g)
,
for
w\in\Sigma^{*}
we let
[\![f\cdot g]\!]^{R}(w)=\bigcup_{w=uv}[\![f]\!]^{R}(u)\cdot[\![g]\!]^{R}(v)

and a word
w
is in the unambiguous domain of
f\cdot g
if there is a

unique
factorization
w=uv
with
u\in\mathsf{dom}(f)
and
v\in\mathsf{dom}(g)
and moreover
this factorization satisfies
u\in\mathsf{udom}(f)
and
v\in\mathsf{udom}(g)
:

\mathsf{udom}(f\cdot g)=(\mathsf{udom}(f)\cdot\mathsf{udom}(g))\setminus\{uvw\mid v\neq\varepsilon\text{ and }u,uv\in\mathsf{dom}(f)\text{ and }vw,w\in\mathsf{dom}(g)\}
.

•

h=f\cdot_{r}g
(reverse Cauchy product): We have
\mathsf{dom}(f\cdot_{r}g)=\mathsf{dom}(f\cdot g)
,

\mathsf{udom}(f\cdot_{r}g)=\mathsf{udom}(f\cdot g)
, and for
w\in\Sigma^{*}
we let

[\![f\cdot_{r}g]\!]^{R}(w)=\bigcup_{w=uv}[\![g]\!]^{R}(v)\cdot[\![f]\!]^{R}(u)
.

•

h=f^{\star}
(Kleene star): We have
\mathsf{dom}(h)=\mathsf{dom}(f)^{\star}
, and for

w\in\Sigma^{*}
we let
[\![h]\!]^{R}(w)=\bigcup_{w=u_{1}\cdots u_{n}}[\![f]\!]^{R}(u_{1})\cdots[\![f]\!]^{R}(u_{n})
. Notice that if
[\![f]\!]^{R}
is

proper
, i.e., if
\varepsilon\notin\mathsf{dom}(f)
, then we may restrict
n
to at most

|w|
in the union above. Otherwise, the union will have infinitely many nonempty terms
and
[\![h]\!]^{R}(w)
may be an infinite language.
Finally,
\mathsf{udom}(h)
is the set of words
w\in\Sigma^{\star}
which have a unique
factorization
w=u_{1}\cdots u_{n}
with
n\geq 0
and
u_{i}\in\mathsf{dom}(f)
for all
1\leq i\leq n
, and moreover, for this factorization, we have
u_{i}\in\mathsf{udom}(f)
for all

1\leq i\leq n
. Notice that if
\varepsilon\in\mathsf{dom}(f)
, then
\mathsf{udom}(h)=\varnothing
.

•

h=f_{r}^{\star}
(reverse Kleene star): We have
\mathsf{dom}(f_{r}^{\star})=\mathsf{dom}(f^{\star})
,

\mathsf{udom}(f_{r}^{\star})=\mathsf{udom}(f^{\star})
, and for
w\in\Sigma^{*}
we let

[\![f_{r}^{\star}]\!]^{R}(w)=\bigcup_{w=u_{1}\cdots u_{n}}[\![f]\!]^{R}(u_{n})\cdots[\![f]\!]^{R}(u_{1})
.

•

h=f\odot g
(Hadamard product): We have
\mathsf{dom}(f\odot g)=\mathsf{dom}(f)\cap\mathsf{dom}(g)
,
for
w\in\Sigma^{*}
we let
[\![f\odot g]\!]^{R}(w)=[\![f]\!]^{R}(w)\cdot[\![g]\!]^{R}(w)

and
\mathsf{udom}(f\odot g)=\mathsf{udom}(f)\cap\mathsf{udom}(g)
.

•

h=[e,f]^{k\star}
(
k
-star): The domain of
h
is the set of words

w\in\Sigma^{\star}
which have a factorization
w=u_{1}\cdots u_{n}
satisfying
(
\dagger
)
n\geq 0
,
u_{i}\in L(e)
for
1\leq i\leq n
, and
u_{i+1}\cdots u_{i+k}\in\mathsf{dom}(f)
for
0\leq i\leq n-k
. For
w\in\Sigma^{*}
we let

[\![[e,f]^{k\star}]\!]^{R}(w)=\bigcup_{\begin{subarray}{c}w=u_{1}\cdots u_{n}\\
u_{1},\ldots,u_{n}\in L(e)\end{subarray}}\prod_{i=0}^{n-k}[\![f]\!]^{R}(u_{i+1}\cdots u_{i+k})\,.

Notice that a factorization
w=u_{1}\cdots u_{n}
with
n<k
and
u_{i}\in L(e)
for all

1\leq i\leq n
, automatically satisfies (
\dagger
). Hence,

\bigcup_{n<k}L(e)^{n}\subseteq\mathsf{dom}(h)
. Moreover, when
n<k
, the empty product in the
definition above evaluates to
\{\varepsilon\}
which is the unit for concatenation of
languages.
The
unambiguous
domain of
h
is the set of words
w\in\mathsf{dom}(h)
which have
a
unique
factorization
w=u_{1}\cdots u_{n}
satisfying (
\dagger
) and moreover,
for this factorization, we have
u_{i+1}\cdots u_{i+k}\in\mathsf{udom}(f)
for all
0\leq i\leq n-k
.

•

h=[e,f]_{r}^{k\star}
(reverse
k
-star): We have
\mathsf{dom}([e,f]_{r}^{k\star})=\mathsf{dom}([e,f]^{k\star})
,

\mathsf{udom}([e,f]_{r}^{k\star})=\mathsf{udom}([e,f]^{k\star})
, and for
w\in\Sigma^{*}
we let

[\![[e,f]_{r}^{k\star}]\!]^{R}(w)=\bigcup_{\begin{subarray}{c}w=u_{1}\cdots u_{n}\\
u_{1,\ldots,u_{n}\in L(e)}\end{subarray}}[\![f]\!]^{R}(u_{n-k+1}\cdots u_{n})\cdots[\![f]\!]^{R}(u_{1}\cdots u_{k})\,.

We show that the inductive definitions of
\mathsf{dom}(h)
and
\mathsf{udom}(h)
indeed give the

domain
of the relation
[\![h]\!]^{R}
and ensure functionality. The proof is an
easy structural induction.

Lemma 3
.

Let
h
be an
RTE
and
w\in\Sigma^{\star}
. Then,

1.

w\in\mathsf{dom}(h)
if and only if
[\![h]\!]^{R}(w)\neq\varnothing
.

2.

If
w\in\mathsf{udom}(h)
, then
[\![h]\!]^{R}(w)
is a singleton.

3.2
Functional semantics

Our goal is now to define
functions
with regular transducer expressions
h
. This
can be achieved by a restriction of the relational semantics
[\![h]\!]^{R}
to a suitable subset of the domain
\mathsf{dom}(h)
.

The first natural idea is to restrict to the set of input words
w
on
which
h
is functional. Formally, let
\mathsf{fdom}(h)=\{w\in\mathsf{dom}(h)\mid|[\![h]\!]^{R}(w)|=1\}
be
the functional domain of
h
. A functional semantics is obtained by restricting the
relational semantics
[\![h]\!]^{R}
to the functional domain
\mathsf{fdom}(h)
. The unacceptable
problem with this approach is that

3.4
Main results

The goal of the paper is to construct efficiently, a two-way reversible transducer
equivalent to a given
RTE
under the unambiguous semantics. Consider an
RTE

h
and some
word
w\in\mathsf{dom}(h)
. We first parse
w
by adding some marker symbols
(_{f},)_{f}
inside

w
, signifying the scope of the subexpressions
f
in
h
. If
w\notin\mathsf{udom}(h)
, then
there can be many ways of parsing
w
. We build a non-deterministic one-way transducer

\mathcal{P}_{h}
which produces all possible parsings of
w
. Figure
1
helps
to get an overview of the construction.
We check if
w
has at most one parsing
using a 2RFA
B^{\prime}
; and if so, apply the uniformized parser transducer
\mathcal{P}_{h}^{\prime}
on
w

to obtain the unique parsing of
w
.
\mathcal{P}_{h}^{U}
represents the sequential composition
of
B^{\prime}
and
\mathcal{P}_{h}^{\prime}
. Finally, the parsing of
w
,
\mathcal{P}_{h}^{U}(w)
is taken as input
by a 2RFT, the evaluator transducer
\mathcal{T}_{h}
, and produces the output of
w
according to

h
, making use of the markers.

Why not a 2-way machine for the parser?.

A natural question to ask is whether we can have a two-way transducer for
\mathcal{P}_{h}
or even directly construct a two-way machine that evaluates
h
.
We discuss some difficulties in this direction. Consider for instance
h=f\cdot g
. We could have a non-deterministic two-way transducer (2NFT) which guesses the
point where the scope of
\mathsf{dom}(f)
ends in the input
w
and where
\mathsf{dom}(g)
begins; if

w\notin\mathsf{udom}(h)
, it is unclear if in the backward sweep, the machine can go back to
this correct point so as to apply
f,g
. On another note, if we design a 2NFT which
first inserts a marker where the scope of
\mathsf{dom}(f)
ends and
\mathsf{dom}(g)
begins, and a
second 2NFT which processes this, we will require the composition of these two machines. It is
unclear how we can go about composition of two 2NFTs. Irrespective of these
difficulties, a 1NFT is easier to use anytime than a 2NFT, if one can construct one,
justifying our choice.

In Section
4
, we define the parsing relation and construct the
corresponding parser and evaluator for
\textsf{RTE}[\textsf{Rat}]
, and then extend to

\textsf{RTE}[\textsf{Rat},._{r},\star_{r}]
in Section
5
. For these fragments, both
the parser and the evaluator have size linear in the given expression. In
Section
6
, we extend the constructions to handle Hadamard product. There, we
show that the size of the parser for
h
is at most exponential in a new parameter, called
the
width
of
h
. Section
7
concludes by showing how to handle the

k
-star operators.

We define the
width
of an RTE

, denoted
\mathsf{width}({h})
, intuitively as the maximum number of times a position in
w
needs to be read to output
h(w)
:

\mathsf{width}({{e}\triangleright{v}})=1
,

\mathsf{width}({f+g})=\mathsf{width}({f\cdot g})=\mathsf{width}({f\cdot_{r}g})=\max(\mathsf{width}({f}),\mathsf{width}({g}))
,

\mathsf{width}({f^{\star}})=\mathsf{width}({f_{r}^{\star}})=\mathsf{width}({f})
,
\mathsf{width}({f\odot g})=\mathsf{width}({f})+\mathsf{width}({g})

and
\mathsf{width}({[e,f]^{k\star}})=\mathsf{width}({[e,f]_{r}^{k\star}})=2+k\times\mathsf{width}({f})
.
Note that, for
k
-star and reverse
k
-star, we define the width as

instead of
1+k\times\mathsf{width}({f})
simply to get a uniform expression
for the complexity bounds.

:
[\![h]\!]^{R}=[\![\mathcal{T}_{h}]\!]\circ P_{h}
. Let
\mathcal{T}^{U}_{h}
be the
2RFT obtained by the composition of
\mathcal{T}_{h}
and
\mathcal{P}^{U}_{h}
(which restricts the
parser to the unambiguous domain of
h
), i.e.,
\mathcal{T}^{U}_{h}=\mathcal{T}_{h}\circ\mathcal{P}^{U}_{h}
.
Then,
\mathcal{T}^{U}_{h}
computes the unambiguous semantics of
h
:
[\![h]\!]^{U}=[\![\mathcal{T}^{U}_{h}]\!]
.

Recall that the number of states of the evaluator is

\lVert{\mathcal{T}_{h}}\rVert=\mathcal{O}(|h|\cdot\mathsf{width}({h}))
. Then, as the composition of 2RFTs can be done
with polynomial blowup, we get
\mathcal{T}^{U}_{h}
whose number of states is

2^{\mathcal{O}(|h|^{2\cdot\mathsf{width}({h})})}
.
Note that, if
h
does not use Hadamard,
k
-star or reverse
k
-star, then both

\lVert{\mathcal{P}_{h}}\rVert
and
\lVert{\mathcal{T}_{h}}\rVert
are linear in
|h|
. As a consequence, for an RTE

h
belonging to this fragment, we get
\mathcal{T}^{U}_{h}
whose number of states is

2^{\mathcal{O}(|h|^{2})}
.

9
Conclusion

We conclude with some interesting avenues for future work. An immediate future work is to
adapt our parser-evaluator construction to work for
SDRTE
. We believe that this can be
done with some effort, preserving our complexity bounds. Note that in this paper, we have
already done the work to handle
[e,f]^{k\star}
even though 2-star was sufficient for

RTE
; it remains to ensure the aperiodicity of the parser-evaluator, preserving our
complexity bounds, to make our construction work for
SDRTE
. Another interesting question
is to see if our approach and construction can be made amenable for extended
RTE
, which
also allows function composition as an operator of the
RTE
syntax. Note that this does not
increase expressiveness, but is a useful shorthand.
Already in this paper, we have
considered some useful functions like duplicate and reverse along with the fragment

RTE
[
Rat
,
._{r},\star_{r}
]; allowing function composition makes the use of these shorthands
meaningful.
As we construct reversible machines, composing them is effective and hence having composition as the topmost operator is straight-forward. However, using composition as an operator would require the parsing of intermediate outputs which we leave as an open problem.
