Minimum ℓ 1 \ell_{1} -norm interpolators: Precise asymptotics and multiple descent
Yue Li, Yuting Wei

Abstract
An evolving line of machine learning works observe empirical evidence that suggests interpolating estimators — the ones that achieve zero training error — may not necessarily be harmful. This paper pursues theoretical understanding for an important type of interpolators: the minimum ℓ 1 \ell_{1} -norm interpolator, which is motivated by the observation that several learning algorithms favor low ℓ 1 \ell_{1} -norm solutions in the over-parameterized regime. Concretely, we consider the noisy sparse regression model under Gaussian design, focusing on linear sparsity and high-dimensional asymptotics (so that both the number of features and the sparsity level scale proportionally with the sample size). We observe, and provide rigorous theoretical justification for, a curious multi-descent phenomenon; that is, the generalization risk of the minimum ℓ 1 \ell_{1} -norm interpolator undergoes multiple (and possibly more than two) phases of descent and ascent as one increases the model capacity. This phenomenon stems from the special structure of the minimum ℓ 1 \ell_{1} -norm interpolator as well as the delicate interplay between the over-parameterized ratio and the sparsity, thus unveiling a fundamental distinction in geometry from the minimum ℓ 2 \ell_{2} -norm interpolator. Our finding is built upon an exact characterization of the risk behavior, which is governed by a system of two non-linear equations with two unknowns.



Keywords:

minimum norm interpolators, multiple descent, Lasso, sparse linear regression,
exact asymptotics, approximate message passing

1
Introduction

At the core of statistical learning lies the problem of understanding the generalization performance (e.g., out-of-sample errors) of the learning algorithms in use.
Conventional wisdom in statistics held that including too many covariates when training statistical models can hurt generalization (despite improving training accuracy), due to the undesired over-fit.
This leads to the classical conclusion that: proper regularization — through either adding certain penalty functions to the loss function or algorithmic self-regularization — seems to be critical in achieving the desired accuracy (e.g.,
Friedman et al., 2001
;
Wei et al., 2019
).
However, an evolving line of works in machine learning observes empirical evidence that suggests, to the surprise of many statisticians, over-parameterization is not necessarily harmful.
Indeed, many machine learning models (such as random forests or deep neural networks) are trained until the training error vanishes to zero — meaning that they are able to perfectly interpolate the data — while still generalizing well (e.g.,
Zhang et al., 2021
;
Wyner et al., 2017
;
Belkin et al., 2019
).
As a key observation to explain this phenomenon,
many models when trained by gradient type methods (e.g., gradient descent, stochastic gradient descent, AdaBoost) converge to certain minimum norm interpolators, which implicitly favor models with smaller model complexity.

These empirical mysteries inspire a recent flurry of activity towards understanding the generalization properties of various interpolators.
A dominant fraction of recent efforts, however, concentrated on studying certain minimum
\ell_{2}
-norm interpolators, primarily in the context of linear and/or kernel regression (see, e.g.,
Liang and Rakhlin, 2020
;
Mei and Montanari, 2019
;
Hastie et al., 2019
;
Belkin et al., 2020
;
Bartlett et al., 2020
;
Bartlett et al., 2021
and the references therein).
This was in part due to the existence of closed-form expressions for minimum
\ell_{2}
-norm interpolators,
which are particularly handy when determining the statistical risk.
In contrast, the theoretical underpinnings for minimum
\ell_{1}
-norm interpolators,
despite growing interest (e.g.,
Ju et al., 2020
;
Liang and Sur, 2020
;
Chinot et al., 2020
), remain highly inadequate and considerably more challenging to establish.
Given that multiple learning algorithms are known to favor low
\ell_{1}
-norm solutions in the over-parameterized regime (such as
Rosset et al., 2004
;
Gunasekar et al., 2018
),
understanding the statistical properties of the minimum
\ell_{1}
-norm interpolation plays a pivotal role in unveiling the trade-offs between over-parameterization and generalization, which we seek to explore in this paper.

1.1
Motivation: a multi-descent phenomenon

An intriguing empirical phenomenon called “
double descent
”
has recently emerged in the study of over-parameterized learning models

(
Neyshabur et al., 2014
;
Nakkiran et al., 2019
;
Belkin et al., 2018
;
Belkin et al., 2019
;
Belkin, 2021
)
.
Consider, for example, a risk curve that depicts how the generalization error varies as more parameters are added to the model.
Following the classical bias-variance trade-off U-shape curve before entering the interpolation (or over-parameterized) regime
(
Friedman et al., 2001
)
, the generalization error of various models descends again as one further increases the number of parameters beyond the interpolation limit. In addition, this double-descent phenomenon is also closely related to a curious observation — the non-monotonicy of risk as the model capacity grows — that has attracted much recent attention
(
Viering et al., 2019
)
.

Aimed at distilling insights that help explain this phenomenon,
a recent body of works studied the behavior of the minimum
\ell_{2}
-norm interpolator in the presence of a linear model,
which solidified the double-descent phenomenon for this interpolator (see, e.g.,
Mei and Montanari, 2019
;
Hastie et al., 2019
;
Bartlett et al., 2020
;
Belkin et al., 2020
and the references therein).
Moving beyond minimum
\ell_{2}
-norm interpolators,
empirical observations have been discussed regarding the minimum
\ell_{1}
-norm interpolator as well; for instance,
similar double descent was numerically observed in
Muthukumar et al., 2020
, with heuristic justification provided in
Mitra, 2019
based on statistical physics intuitions.
Our own numerical experiments uncover even more intriguing risk behavior of the minimum
\ell_{1}
-norm interpolator.
As illustrated in Figure

and Figure
2

multiple descent
” in certain parameter regimes; that is,
as the model complexity continues to grow, the out-of-sample risk of the minimum
\ell_{1}
-norm interpolator undergoes multiple phases of increase and decrease, and ultimately becomes non-increasing even as the over-parameterized ratio tends to infinity.
There is, however, lack of theoretical support that elucidates this empirical observation. It remains unclear how to interpret the striking distinction in the risk behavior between the minimum
\ell_{1}
-norm and the minimum
\ell_{2}
-norm interpolators.

1.2
Main results and insights

In this paper, we concentrate on linear models,
and investigate the generalization error (in terms of the out-of-sample squared error) of
the minimum
\ell_{1}
-norm interpolator — or equivalently,
the Lasso estimator with regularization parameter approaching zero.
We pursue a comprehensive understanding of such estimators in the
proportional growth
and
over-parameterized
regime, where the number of parameters
p
scales linearly with, but larger than, the number of samples
n
.
Recognize that Figure
1
and Figure
2
(whose difference only lies in how the sparsity levels are chosen) exhibit very similar behavior in the over-parameterized regime.
To streamline presentation and avoid repetition, we shall restrict our attention to the geometric properties of the risk curve in the setting of Figure
2
.
In what follows, we formulate the problem precisely, followed by a summary of our main results.

Models.

Setting the stage, imagine that we have gathered
n
i.i.d. noisy training data drawn from a linear model

\displaystyle y_{i}=\langle\bm{x}_{i},\bm{\theta}^{\star}\rangle+z_{i},\qquad 1\leq i\leq n,

(1)

where
\bm{\theta}^{\star}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}

\bm{x}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}

and the

In addition, we consider the linear sparsity regime, where (i) the unknown signal
\bm{\theta}^{\star}
is
(\epsilon\cdot p)

\epsilon>0
,
and (ii) all the
\epsilon\cdot p
non-zero entries have magnitudes proportional to some given quantity
M
(to be made precise momentarily in Section
2.1
).

Under the well-specified linear model, the generalization error (or out-of-sample risk) of any estimator

Main findings: the risk curve of the minimum
\ell_{1}
-norm interpolator.

When it comes to the over-parametrized regime where

y_{i}=\langle\bm{x}_{i},\bm{\theta}\rangle

Among all possible interpolators, the focal point of this paper is the

\ell_{1}
-norm intepolator

\displaystyle\widehat{\bm{\theta}}^{\mathsf{Int}}\coloneqq\arg\!\min_{\bm{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}}\|\bm{\theta}\|_{1}\qquad\text{subject to}\quad y_{i}=\langle\bm{x}_{i},\,\bm{\theta}\rangle,~~1\leq i\leq n.

In an attempt to understand its generalization behavior, we seek to pin down the exact asymptotics of the above risk metric.
Encouragingly, the large system limit of

Theorem 1

(Shape of the risk curve)
.

Suppose that

.
Assume i.i.d. Gaussian design, i.i.d. Gaussian noise, and linear sparsity
(to be made precise in Section

Then the generalization error (cf. (

-norm interpolator (

(a)

(b)

\mathsf{Risk}(\bm{0})

(c)

For any fixed signal-to-noise ratio (to be defined precisely in (

)), there exists a constant

In addition, for every given

Geometric implications and insights.

Theorem
1
reveals certain geometric properties of the risk curve of the minimum
\ell_{1}
-norm estimator in the over-parameterized regime (i.e.,
p>n
).
Let us take a moment to discuss the implications regarding how the risk changes in the over-parameterized ratio
p/n
.

•

Theorem
1
(a) identifies two
non-overlapping
regions within the over-parameterized regime that exhibit risk descent. Consequently, the total number of descent depends largely on the risk behavior in between these two regions.

•

Theorem
1
(c) indicates that the risk in between the above-mentioned two regions exhibits contrastingly different behavior depending on the sparsity ratio.

–

When the sparsity ratio is relatively small, the generalization error exhibits an intriguing “
decreasing – increasing – decreasing again
” pattern in the over-parameterized regime.
This taken together with what happens in the under-parameterized regime unveils a
“triple-descent”
behavior,
which matches the numerical findings in Figure
3
.
Interestingly, the minimum
\ell_{2}
-norm interpolator for such a model often enjoys a double-descent behavior rather than triple descent, thus uncovering a fundamental difference between the minimum
\ell_{1}
-norm and the minimum
\ell_{2}
-norm interpolation.

–

In stark contrast,
if the sparsity ratio is relatively large (in the sense that
\epsilon>\epsilon^{\star}
), the generalization error might actually decrease monotonically with
p/n
in the entire over-parameterized regime.
If this were true, then taking it together with the classical conclusion in the under-parameterized regime
would justify the double-descent behavior that has also been empirically observed in
Muthukumar et al., 2020
;
Mitra, 2019
.

•

In view of Theorem
1
(b), the minimum
\ell_{1}
-norm interpolator is essentially no better than a trivial estimator (i.e., the zero estimator) when the over-parameterized ratio
p/n
is overly large.

•

Finally, Theorem
1
(d) reveals that at any over-parameterized ratio, the generalization risk can be decreasing with
p/n
as long as the sparsity ratio is small enough.

1.3
A glimpse of our technical approach and novelty

Demonstrating the multi-descent phenomenon requires understanding the asymptotic risk of the interpolator of interest,
which can be achieved by analyzing an iterative algorithm called
Approximate Message Passing
(AMP),
originally proposed by
Donoho et al., 2009
in the context of compressed sensing.
Most relevant to our paper is the series of papers by
Bayati and Montanari, 2011a
;
Bayati and Montanari, 2011b

that determined the asymptotic Lasso risk with a fixed and
strictly
positive regularization.
In order to analyze the minimum
\ell_{1}
-norm interpolator,
the present work extends the AMP machinery to accommodate Lasso with the regularization parameter approaching zero,
which can be accomplished by running a sequence of AMP that changes the algorithm parameters in an epoch-based manner.
Noteworthily,
previous analyses relied on the observation that having positive regularization encourages sparse solutions and, in turn, induces certainty restricted strong convexity around the solution.
This, however, fails to capture our AMP dynamics due to the absence of positive regularization.
To remedy this issue, we develop a new type of structural properties that allows one to analyze AMP iterates with changing parameters.
As it turns out, the minimum
\ell_{1}
-norm solution coincides with the fixed-point of the new AMP updates, whose risk behavior can be characterized by a new system of two nonlinear equations with two unknowns.
Obtaining the exact characterization of the minimum
\ell_{1}
-norm is beyond what prior AMP theory has to offer.

With the risk characterization in place, everything boils down to analyzing the above-mentioned nonlinear systems of equations — in particular, how its solutions vary with the aspect ratio
\delta
.
This, however, is challenging to cope with, as there is no closed-form expression of the solution points.
While the prior work
Miolane and Montanari, 2018
studied the existence and uniqueness of the state evolution solutions,
it is unclear how the solution varies with
\delta
, particularly in the absence of strictly positive regularization.
All this is addressed in the present paper via careful analysis of the first- and second-order properties of the system of equations,
which constitutes much of our analysis. We expect our analysis idea to be useful to analyze other estimators such as more general M-estimators
(
Donoho and Montanari, 2016
)
or the SLOPE estimator
(
Su and Candes, 2016
)
.

1.4
Other related works

Multiple descent.

While the emergence of the multi-descent phenomenon in our setting is caused by the special structure of minimum
\ell_{1}
-norm interpolators as well as the interplay between the over-parameterized ratio and the sparsity level,
this phenomenon has also been observed in other settings for
\ell_{2}
-norm minimization — albeit of different nature compared to ours.
It is noteworthy that the presence of multiple descent can be caused by various other structures of the design matrix.
As concrete examples, this might arise in non-isotropic linear regression where the covariance of the design matrix possesses two eigenspaces of different variance
(
Nakkiran et al., 2020
)
; another possibility that leads to this phenomenon is to tweak the change points of the risk curve of the minimum
\ell_{2}
-norm solution by carefully adding new columns (features) to the design matrix (with either standard Gaussian or Gaussian mixtures distributions)
(
Chen et al., 2020
)
.
Additionally, this phenomenon might also stem from the regression kernel in use. For instance,
Adlam and Pennington, 2020
derived the high-dimensional asymptotics for the risk curve when using neural tangent kernels in a two-layer neural network;

Liang et al., 2020
studied the convergence properties of the minimum kernel-Hilbert norm interpolators under various scaling of
p=n^{\alpha}
,
\alpha\in(0,1)
, and suggested possible change points (from ascent to descent) at
\alpha=\frac{1}{l+1/2}
for every integer
l.

In addition,
d’Ascoli et al., 2020
empirically observed the multi-descent phenomenon under the random Fourier feature model.

Minimum
\ell_{1}
-norm solutions.

In the over-parametrized regime (
p>n
),
the minimum
\ell_{1}
-norm interpolator considered herein is closely related to the problem of Basis Pursuit (BP) in the compressed sensing literature (e.g.,
Chen et al., 2001
;
Wojtaszczyk, 2010
;
Candes and Tao, 2006
;
Donoho, 2006
;
Donoho et al., 2005
).
In particular, the algorithm (
4
) has been well-established paradigm for finding a sparse solution to a noiseless linear system.
When it comes to the proportional growth and linear sparsity regime in the noiseless case,

Donoho and Tanner, 2009
;
Amelunxen et al., 2014
characterized the exact phase transition boundary regarding the sample size in achieving perfect recovery. Moving to the noisy scenario,
Ju et al., 2020
considered the same estimator and exhibited a double-descent phenomenon when
p
is exponentially larger than
n
.

Chinot et al., 2020
studied the setting with
p
exceeding the order of
n\log^{1-\beta}(n)
for some constant
\beta\in(0,1)
, which did not focus on determining exact pre-constants and the double- or multi-descent phenomenon.
Another recent work
Liang and Sur, 2020

studied a drastically different problem — binary classification, and pinned down exact asymptotics of the minimum
\ell_{1}
-norm solution when the data are separable, which has intimate connection to AdaBoost.

Exact high-dimensional asymptotics.

The exact asymptotic framework adopted in this work is closely related to the risk characterization of the Lasso estimator (for positive
\lambda
) that has been obtained in prior literature.
In the proportional growth regime (so that
p
and
n
are comparable),
the Lasso risk under i.i.d. Gaussian designs has been determined by
Bayati and Montanari, 2011b
;
Stojnic, 2013
;
Oymak et al., 2013
.
In particular, the AMP machinery is a powerful tool for determining exact asymptotics in this regime, and we postpone further discussions to Section
3.1
.
The distributional characterization of the Lasso has been recently established by
Miolane and Montanari, 2018
under the i.i.d. Gaussian designs, and by
Celentano et al., 2020
;
Bellec and Zhang, 2019
under general correlated Gaussian designs, where the first two works were built upon the convex Gaussian min-max theorem.
Going beyond the
\ell_{1}
-penalty, the estimation risk of the robust regression estimators was pioneered by
El Karoui, 2013
;
El Karoui, 2018
;
Donoho and Montanari, 2016
and extensively studied by, e.g.,
Dobriban and Wager, 2018
;
Thrampoulidis et al., 2018
;
Hastie et al., 2019
;
Patil et al., 2021
.

Approximate message passing.

Inspired by statistical physics and information theory literature, AMP was first proposed as an efficient scheme to solve compressed sensing problems
(
Donoho et al., 2009
)
.

Bayati and Montanari, 2011a
;
Javanmard and Montanari, 2013
then rigorously proved that the dynamics of AMP can be accurately tracked by a simple small-dimensional recursive formula called the
state evolution
.
This state-evolution characterization made AMP amenable as a analysis device to describe the statistical behaviors for various problems, despite that AMP is an effective algorithm on its own.
The AMP algorithm and machinery has been successfully applied to a variety of problems beyond compressed sensing,
including but not limited to robust M-estimators
(
Donoho and Montanari, 2016
)
, SLOPE
(
Bu et al., 2020
)
,
low-rank matrix estimation and PCA
(
Rangan and Fletcher, 2012
;
Montanari and Venkataramanan, 2021
;
Fan, 2020
;
Zhong et al., 2021
)
, stochastic block models
(
Deshpande et al., 2015
)
, phase retrieval
(
Ma et al., 2018
)
, phase synchronization
(
Celentano et al., 2021
)
, and generalized linear models
(
Rangan, 2011
;
Sur et al., 2019
;
Sur and Candès, 2019
;
Barbier et al., 2019
)
.
See
Feng et al., 2021
for an accessible introduction of this machinery and its applications.
Moreover, a dominant fraction of the AMP works focused on high-dimensional asymptotics (so that the problem dimension tends to infinity first before the number of iterations), except for
Rush and Venkataramanan, 2018
that derived finite-sample guarantees allowing the number of iterations to grow up to
O(\log n/\log\log n)
.

such that

\displaystyle|\psi(x)-\psi(y)|\leq L(1+\|x\|_{2}+\|y\|_{2})\|x-y\|_{2}

(6)

holds for all
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
. Additionally, we shall often suppress a.s. in the notation
\overset{\mathrm{a.s.}}{=}
for almost sure convergence if it is clear from the context.

2
Risk characterization for the minimum
\ell_{1}
-norm interpolator

2.1
Modelling assumptions

For notational simplicity, we shall often adopt the vector and matrix notation as follows

\displaystyle\bm{z}\coloneqq[z_{i}]_{1\leq i\leq n}

\displaystyle\in\mathbb{R}^{n},\qquad\bm{X}\coloneqq[\bm{x}_{1},\cdots,\bm{x}_{n}]^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times p},

(7a)

\displaystyle\bm{y}\coloneqq[y_{i}]_{1\leq i\leq n}=\bm{X}\bm{\theta}^{\star}+\bm{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

(7b)

To formalize the problem setting, we first impose the following assumptions on the sampling process throughout the paper.

•

Gaussian design.
We study i.i.d. Gaussian design, where each design vector is independently drawn:

\bm{x}_{i}\overset{\text{i.i.d.}}{\sim}\mathcal{N}\bigg(0,\frac{1}{n}\textbf{I}_{p}\bigg),\qquad 1\leq i\leq n.

(8)

Here, the scaling factor
1/n
is introduced merely for normalization purpose.
This tractable model is widely adopted when studying the high-dimensional asymptotics of Lasso (e.g.
Bayati and Montanari, 2011b
;
Miolane and Montanari, 2018
;
Su et al., 2017
) and has been extended to other statistical learning problems
(e.g.,
Donoho and Montanari, 2016
;
El Karoui, 2013
;
Sur et al., 2019
;
Thrampoulidis et al., 2018
).
While Gaussian design is typically not satisfied in practice,
it allows for useful mathematical insights that might shed light on practical contexts.

•

Gaussian noise.
It is assumed that the noise components are independent and obey

\displaystyle\bm{z}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2}\textbf{I}_{n}).

(9)

Under the above Gaussian design and Gaussian noise model, the generalization error (
2
) of an estimator
\widehat{\bm{\theta}}
should be defined when
(\bm{x}_{\text{new}},y_{\text{new}})
is drawn from the same assumption, i.e.,
y_{\text{new}}=\langle\bm{x}_{\text{new}},\bm{\theta}^{\star}\rangle+z_{\text{new}}
with
\bm{x}_{\text{new}}\sim\mathcal{N}\big(0,\frac{1}{n}\textbf{I}_{p}\big)
and
z_{\text{new}}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2})
. This leads to

\displaystyle\mathsf{Risk}(\widehat{\bm{\theta}})\coloneqq\mathbb{E}\big[(\bm{x}_{\text{new}}^{\top}\widehat{\bm{\theta}}-y_{\text{new}})^{2}\big]=\mathbb{E}\big[\big(\bm{x}_{\text{new}}^{\top}(\widehat{\bm{\theta}}-\bm{\theta}^{\star})\big)^{2}\big]+\sigma^{2}=\frac{1}{n}\big\|\widehat{\bm{\theta}}-\bm{\theta}^{\star}\big\|_{2}^{2}+\sigma^{2}.

(10)

In addition, we shall make assumptions regarding how the ground truth is generated, as formalized below.

•

Linear sparsity.
Suppose that each coordinate of
\bm{\theta}^{\star}=[\theta^{\star}_{i}]_{1\leq i\leq p}
is identically and independently drawn as follows

\theta^{\star}_{i}\overset{\mathrm{i.i.d.}}{\sim}\epsilon\mathcal{P}_{M\sqrt{\delta}}+(1-\epsilon)\mathcal{P}_{0},

(11)

where
\mathcal{P}_{c}
denotes the Dirac measure at point
c\in\mathbb{R}
,
and
M>0
is some given quantity that determines the magnitude of a non-zero entry.
In words, each coordinate is non-zero (and with magnitude
M\sqrt{\delta}
) with probability
\epsilon
.
Here, the scaling factor
\sqrt{\delta}
is introduced solely for notational convenience,
which ensures that the
signal-to-noise-ratio
(SNR) obeys

\displaystyle\mathrm{SNR}\coloneqq\frac{\mathbb{E}\big[(\bm{x}^{\top}\bm{\theta}^{\star})^{2}\big]}{\sigma^{2}}=\frac{\epsilon M^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}.

(12)

When
\epsilon
is a fixed constant,
the number of non-zero coordinates concentrates around
\epsilon\cdot p
,
meaning that
\epsilon
determines the sparsity level of
\bm{\theta}^{\star}
.
Noteworthily, a model with linear sparsity lends itself well to high-dimensional applications with only moderate degrees of sparsity (for instance, in various problems in genomics, the relevant signals are observed to be spread out across a good fraction of the genome
(
Boyle et al., 2017
;
Tam et al., 2019
)
).

Remark
1
.

It is worth noting that the linear sparsity regime often precludes consistency results in both estimation and support recovery,
which is in stark contrast to the regime where the sparsity level is vanishingly small compared to the sample size
(
Bickel et al., 2009
;
Wainwright, 2009
;
Bühlmann and van de Geer, 2011
)
.
In fact, results featuring this regime often require an additional adjustment due to the effect of undersampling, as discussed in
El Karoui et al., 2013
.

2.2
Risk characterization

In order to depict the shape of the risk curve, we first characterize
the precise asymptotics of
\mathsf{Risk}(\widehat{\bm{\theta}}^{\mathsf{Int}};\delta)
with a fixed aspect ratio
0<\delta<1
.
As alluded to previously, this is accomplished by considering sequences of instances of increasing sizes, along which the minimum
\ell_{1}
-norm interpolator (cf. (
4
)) has a non-trivial limiting risk behavior.

Towards this end, we consider a more general distribution on
\bm{\theta}^{\star}
by assuming that

\displaystyle\text{The empirical distribution of }\bm{\theta}^{\star}\text{ converges weakly to a probability measure }P_{\Theta}.

(13)

The following theorem determines the exact asymptotics of
\mathsf{Risk}(\widehat{\bm{\theta}}^{\mathsf{Int}};\delta)
for any given
0<\delta<1
.

Theorem 2

(Risk of min
\ell_{1}
-norm interpolation)
.

Consider the linear model (
1
), and suppose that the assumptions (
8
), (
9
) and
(
13
) hold. Consider any given
0<\delta<1
.
If
\mathbb{E}[\Theta^{2}]<\infty
and
\mathbb{P}(\Theta\neq 0)>0
,
then the prediction risk of the minimum
\ell_{1}
-norm interpolator obeys

\displaystyle\lim_{\begin{subarray}{c}n/p=\delta\\
n,\,p\to\infty\end{subarray}}\mathsf{Risk}\big(\widehat{\bm{\theta}}^{\mathsf{Int}}\big)~\overset{\mathrm{a.s.}}{=}~{\tau^{\star}}^{2}.

(14)

Here,
({\tau^{\star}},{\alpha^{\star}})
stands for the unique solution to the following system of equations

\displaystyle\tau^{2}

\displaystyle=\sigma^{2}+\frac{1}{\delta}\mathbb{E}\left[\big(\eta(\Theta+\tau Z;\alpha\tau)-\Theta\big)^{2}\right],

(15a)

\displaystyle\delta

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\big(|\Theta+\tau Z|>\alpha\tau\big),

(15b)

where
\Theta\sim P_{\Theta}
, and
Z\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
and is independent of
\Theta
.

Remark
2
.

It is noteworthy that Theorem
2
is completely general regarding the distribution of
\bm{\theta}^{\star}
as long as its empirical distribution converges to a fixed measure; in particular, it does not require
\Theta
to follow the sparse distribution specified in the expression (
11

First, there exists a unique solution pair to the set of equations (
15

B.1
(see Section
B
for more details).
Experienced readers who are familiar with literature on Lasso shall immediately recognize the similarity between these equations and the ones used to determine the Lasso risk in the proportional regime
(
Bayati and Montanari, 2011b
)
. We shall elaborate a bit more on their connections and differences in Section
2.3
.

We now pause to interpret the above result.
The risk of the minimum
\ell_{1}
-norm interpolator — when the ratio
n/p
is held fixed — converges to a quantity
{\tau^{\star}}^{2}
, which is a function of
(\sigma,\delta,P_{\Theta})
and can be determined by solving a system of two nonlinear equations with two unknowns.
At a high level, the equation (
15a
) indicates that

{\tau^{\star}}>\sigma
, which can be viewed as variance inflation as a result of undersampling.
In addition,
{\tau^{\star}}
taken together with the other parameter
{\alpha^{\star}}
controls the sparsity level of
\widehat{\bm{\theta}}^{\mathsf{Int}}
. In fact, as can be seen from the equation (
15b
) and our analysis, we have

\displaystyle\lim_{\begin{subarray}{c}n/p=\delta\\
n,\,p\to\infty\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{p}\|\widehat{\bm{\theta}}^{\mathsf{Int}}\|_{0}~\overset{\mathrm{a.s.}}{=}~\mathbb{P}\big(|\Theta+{\tau^{\star}}Z|>{\alpha^{\star}}{\tau^{\star}}\big)=\delta,

which is essentially saying that the support size of
\widehat{\bm{\theta}}^{\mathsf{Int}}
converges to
n
in the limit.
The proof of Theorem
2
is established via analyzing
a sequence of
Approximate Message Passing
(AMP) updates with careful choices of parameters, such that the minimum
\ell_{1}
-norm solution is the fixed point of these updates.
The state evolution formula that characterizes the large
n
limit for each iterate is derived, and its large
t
limit corresponds to the risk of
the minimum
\ell_{1}
-norm solution.
The readers are referred to Section
3.1
for details.

Multi-descent phenomenon.

Having obtained an exact characterization of
\mathsf{Risk}(\widehat{\bm{\theta}}^{\mathsf{Int}})
in a general manner,
we are ready to specialize Theorem

to the ground-truth distribution (
11

and examine how
{\tau^{\star}}
changes as a function of the aspect ratio

Specifically, if denote
\nu\coloneqq M/\tau

\displaystyle 1

\displaystyle=\frac{\nu^{2}}{M^{2}}\sigma^{2}+\frac{\epsilon}{\delta}\mathbb{E}\left[\big(\eta(\sqrt{\delta}\nu+Z;\alpha)-\sqrt{\delta}\nu\big)^{2}\right]+\frac{1-\epsilon}{\delta}\mathbb{E}\left[\eta^{2}(Z;\alpha)\right]

(16a)

\displaystyle\delta

\displaystyle=\epsilon\mathbb{P}\big(|\nu\sqrt{\delta}+Z|>\alpha\big)+(1-\epsilon)\mathbb{P}(|Z|>\alpha)

(16b)

in the presence of the distribution (

From equation set (

In particular,
we can use (

To the best of our knowledge, this provides the first theoretical justification for the multiple-descent phenomenon associated with the minimum

-norm interpolator, and might shed light on understanding the behavior of other interpolators such as the M-estimators with a general family of objective functions.

Comparisons with ridgeless regression.

Hastie et al., 2019

By calculating the derivative of the right-hand side of (

one can easily demonstrate that the exact asymptotics of
\mathsf{Risk}(\widehat{\bm{\theta}}^{\mathsf{Int},\ell_{2}})
decays with

.

1/\delta
when

otherwise, if

Next, we single out a few key differences between their results and ours in Theorem

•

The current paper considers the case where the sparsity ratio of

The role of over-parametrization is studied when the minimum

In contrast,

Unfortunately, the minimum

2.3
Connections to the Lasso estimator

Apparently, the minimum
\ell_{1}
-norm interpolator (
4
) is closely related to the classical Lasso estimator studied extensively in high-dimensional statistics
(
Tibshirani, 1996
)
.
Given a positive regularization parameter
\lambda>0
,
the Lasso estimates the regression coefficients by solving the following optimization problem

\displaystyle\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{\lambda}\coloneqq\arg\!\min_{\bm{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}}\left\{\frac{1}{2}\left\|\bm{y}-\bm{X}\bm{\theta}\right\|_{2}^{2}+\lambda\left\|\bm{\theta}\right\|_{1}\right\}.

(18)

As a consequence, the minimum
\ell_{1}
-norm interpolator corresponds to the limit of
\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{\lambda}
when taking
\lambda
to zero.

Several prior works have attempted to characterize the exact asymptotics of the Lasso risk
\mathsf{Risk}(\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{\lambda})
in the proportional regime. Specifically, it has been proven that for any given
\lambda>0
,
\mathsf{Risk}(\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{\lambda})
converges to a non-trivial limit
{\tau^{\star}}(\lambda)
.
Here,

({\tau^{\star}}(\lambda),\alpha^{*}(\lambda))
represents the solution pair to the following set of nonlinear equations

\displaystyle\tau^{2}

\displaystyle=\sigma^{2}+\frac{1}{\delta}\mathbb{E}\left[\big(\eta(\Theta+\tau Z,\alpha\tau)-\Theta\big)^{2}\right],

(19a)

\displaystyle\lambda

\displaystyle=\alpha\tau\left(1-\frac{1}{\delta}\mathbb{P}\big(|\Theta+\tau Z|>\alpha\tau\big)\right),

(19b)

where
\Theta\sim P_{\Theta}
and
Z\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
are independent random variables.
The interested reader can consult
Bayati and Montanari, 2011b
;
Miolane and Montanari, 2018
;
Celentano et al., 2020
,
which determined the Lasso risk using either the AMP machinery or the convex Gaussian min-max theorem.

As can be easily seen, the system of equations (
19
) bears much resemblance to (
15
).
More precisely, by directly setting
\lambda
to
0
, the equation set (
19
) reduces to the one in (
15
). In other words, one has

\displaystyle\lim_{\lambda\to 0}\lim_{\begin{subarray}{c}n/p=\delta\\
n,p\to\infty\end{subarray}}\mathsf{Risk}(\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{\lambda})~\overset{\mathrm{a.s.}}{=}~\lim_{\lambda\to 0}[{\tau^{\star}}(\lambda)]^{2}={\tau^{\star}}^{2}.

(20)

where
{\tau^{\star}}
denotes the quantity in Theorem
2
, and
the last identity holds under certin continuity assumptions w.r.t. the equations (
19
).

Intuitively, Theorem
2
can be directly established if it is legitimate to switch the order of limits between
\lambda
and
p
on the left hand side of expression (
20
), given that the minimum
\ell_{1}
-norm interpolator is the limit of the Lasso by taking
\lambda
to zero.
However, formally establishing the validity of exchanging limits is quite challenging, since doing so normally requires the loss function being strongly convex (at least locally strongly convex around the solution point). Such a strong convexity property, however, is lacking in our problem structure when
\lambda
is taken to zero.
In fact, this presents a major roadblock to directly applying the established AMP theory for the Lasso estimator.

Fortunately, we can directly argue that exchanging the two limits leads to the same result, as formalized in the proposition below. The proof of this result is postponed to Section
A
.

Proposition 1

(The Lasso limit when
\lambda\rightarrow 0
)
.

In the setting of Theorem
2
,
the Lasso risk obeys the following asymptotically exact characterization:

1.

When
\delta<1
, the asymptotic Lasso risk converges to the risk of min
\ell_{1}
-norm interpolator (
4
):

\displaystyle\lim_{\lambda\rightarrow 0}\lim_{\begin{subarray}{c}n/p=\delta\\
n,p\to\infty\end{subarray}}\mathsf{Risk}(\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{\lambda})~\overset{\mathrm{a.s.}}{=}~{\tau^{\star}}^{2},

with
{\tau^{\star}}
being the solution to the system of equations (
15
).

2.

When
\delta>1
, the asymptotic Lasso risk converges to the risk of the ordinary least-square solution, namely,

\displaystyle\lim_{\lambda\rightarrow 0}\lim_{\begin{subarray}{c}n/p=\delta\\
n,p\to\infty\end{subarray}}\mathsf{Risk}(\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{\lambda})~\overset{\mathrm{a.s.}}{=}~\frac{\delta}{\delta-1}\sigma^{2}.

In words, the above result reveals that: while the connection between the set of equations (
19
) and the Lasso risk was previously only shown for a positive
\lambda
, such exact asymptotics continue be valid even in the limit when
\lambda
approaches zero.

3
Key analysis

This section presents the key ideas for proving our main results.
We start by presenting the proof strategy for Theorem
2
, which is built upon the recently developed approximate message passing machinery. It is then followed by the proof of Theorem
1
that characterizes the geometric properties of the risk curve.

3.1
Key analysis tool: approximate message passing

The major technical enabler for proving Theorem
2
lies in the recent development of an iterative algorithm called the
Approximate Message Passing
(AMP) algorithms. As mentioned previously,
Bayati and Montanari, 2011b
employed AMP to pin down the risk of the Lasso estimator with positive regularization. Motivated by this line of works, this paper resorts to the AMP technique as a proof device towards understanding the risk behavior of the minimum
\ell_{1}
-norm interpolators.

For our purpose, we need to generalize the original AMP updates (
Bayati and Montanari, 2011b
) — which were designed to solve a single Lasso problem in the large-system limit — to approximate a sequence of Lasso problems with changing (and converging) regularization parameters.
To better illustrate this idea, we shall first provide a brief review of how AMP is invoked to solve a single Lasso problem, followed by a generalization of this framework to accommodate the minimum
\ell_{1}
-norm interpolator.

3.1.1
AMP for the Lasso estimator

AMP updates for Lasso.

Recall that the soft-thresholding function is defined in expression (
5
) and
\left\langle\cdot\right\rangle
denotes the average of the coordinates for the target vector.
When initialized at
\bm{\theta}^{0}=\bm{0}
and
\bm{z}^{-1}=\bm{0}
,
the AMP algorithm proceeds recursively in the following fashion

\displaystyle\bm{\theta}^{t+1}

\displaystyle=\eta(\bm{X}^{\top}\bm{z}^{t}+\bm{\theta}^{t};\zeta_{t});

(21a)

\displaystyle\bm{z}^{t}

\displaystyle=\bm{y}-\bm{X}\bm{\theta}^{t}+\frac{1}{\delta}\bm{z}^{t-1}\left\langle\eta^{\prime}(\bm{X}^{\top}\bm{z}^{t-1}+\bm{\theta}^{t-1};\zeta_{t-1})\right\rangle.

(21b)

Here,
\{\zeta_{t}\}_{t=0}^{\infty}
is an appropriate sequence of scalars to be selected.
To approximate the Lasso solution with positive
\lambda>0
(defined in (
18
)),
Bayati and Montanari, 2011b
showed that it suffices to set

\displaystyle\zeta_{t}={\alpha^{\star}}(\lambda)\cdot\tau_{t}(\lambda),\quad\text{for all }t\geq 0,

(22)

where
{\alpha^{\star}}(\lambda)
is taken as the corresponding solution to the fixed-point equation (
19
)
and
\tau_{t}(\lambda)
shall be specified momentarily.
Given this choice of parameters,
Bayati and Montanari, 2011b
proved that the corresponding AMP update
\bm{\theta}^{t}
converges to the Lasso solution in the following sense: as long as
\mathbb{E}[\Theta^{2}]<\infty
and
\mathbb{P}(\Theta\neq 0)>0
, it holds that

\displaystyle\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\lim_{\begin{subarray}{c}n/p=\delta\\
n,p\to\infty\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{p}\|\bm{\theta}^{t}-\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{\lambda}\|_{2}^{2}~\overset{\mathrm{a.s.}}{=}~0.

(23)

We emphasize that this convergence result requires taking the limit of the model dimensions before taking the limit of the iteration steps; hence, it should be understood as high-dimensional asymptotics.
Equipped with this result, one is able to study the limiting performance of Lasso via the AMP iterations at each fixed step
t
; the latter is made possible by the state evolution characterization to be introduced below.

State evolution.

Consider the AMP procedure (
21
) with an arbitrary sequence of thresholds
\{\zeta_{t}\}>0
.
The state evolution sequence
\{\tau_{t}^{2}\}_{t=0}^{\infty}
is a one-dimensional iteration sequence, recursively defined for all
t\geq 0
as follows

\displaystyle\tau_{t+1}^{2}

\displaystyle=\mathsf{F}(\tau_{t}^{2},\zeta_{t})

(24a)

\displaystyle\text{where }~\mathsf{F}(\tau^{2},\zeta)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\sigma^{2}+\frac{1}{\delta}\mathbb{E}\left[\big[\eta(\Theta+\tau Z;\zeta)-\Theta\big]^{2}\right]

(24b)

with initialization
\tau_{0}^{2}=\sigma^{2}+\mathbb{E}[\Theta^{2}]/\delta
.
Here,
\Theta
is the distribution of the true signal, and
Z\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
is independent of
\Theta
.
The above sequence is known to characterize the limiting variance of the AMP recursion, as formalized by the following result.

Proposition 2

(Theorem 1.1,
Bayati and Montanari, 2011b
)
.

Consider the linear model (
1
) and i.i.d. Gaussian design.
If
\mathbb{E}[\Theta^{2}]<\infty
and
\mathbb{P}(\Theta\neq 0)>0
,
then for any positive sequence
\{\zeta_{t}\}
and any pseudo-Lipschitz function
\psi
, it holds that

\displaystyle\lim_{p\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{p}\sum_{i=1}^{p}\psi(\theta_{i}^{t+1},\theta^{\star}_{i})~\overset{\mathrm{a.s.}}{=}~\mathbb{E}\big[\psi\big(\eta(\Theta+\tau_{t}Z;\zeta_{t}),\Theta\big)\big],

where
Z\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
is independent of
\Theta
.

In words, this proposition asserts that the coordinates of
\bm{X}^{\top}\bm{z}^{t}+\bm{\theta}^{t}
have roughly the same distribution as
\Theta+\tau_{t}Z
.
Taking
\psi(x,y)=(x-y)^{2}
and combining this with the expression (
24b
) indicate that: the asymptotic risk of the AMP in the
t
-th iteration is characterized by
\tau_{t}^{2}
.
Indeed, the state evolution
\tau_{t}^{2}
quantifies how this asymptotic risk
evolves with the iteration count.
If we let the iteration number
t
tend to infinity, then
\tau_{t}^{2}
converges to a nonzero limit — i.e., the solution to the system of equations (
19
) — which is precisely the limiting risk for the Lasso estimator by virtue of the property (
23
).

3.1.2
AMP for the minimum
\ell_{1}
-norm interpolator

As discussed above, when AMP adopts the choice of
\zeta_{t}={\alpha^{\star}}(\lambda)\cdot\tau_{t}(\lambda)
, then in each iteration, it makes progress towards the Lasso solution in the presence of a positive regularization parameter
\lambda
.
Intuitively, one can run AMP in an epoch-based manner, and gradually reduce the value of
\lambda
by taking a vanishing sequence of
\left\{\lambda_{t}\right\}
and set
\zeta_{t}={\alpha^{\star}}(\lambda_{t})\cdot\tau_{t}(\lambda_{t})
. Heuristically, each epoch solves a Lasso problem approximately with parameter
\lambda_{t}
and, in the end, one can recover the minimum
\ell_{1}
-norm interpolator in the limit. Similar heuristics have been pointed out by
Donoho et al., 2010
without a rigorous argument.

It turns out this intuition can be solidified
as long as one selects the sequence
\left\{\lambda_{t}\right\}
appropriately.
Let us now describe our choice of the
\left\{\lambda_{t}\right\}
sequence, and use them to construct
\left\{\zeta_{t}\right\}
in the AMP updates.

Choice of
\{\zeta_{t}\}
in our setting.

Our first step is to construct a positive sequence of
\left\{\lambda_{t}\right\}
satisfying the following assumption:

Assumption 1
.

For every
t=1,2,\ldots
, define
\Lambda_{t}\coloneqq\sum_{s=1}^{t}\lambda_{s}
.
We assume that
\left\{\lambda_{t}\right\}_{t=1}^{\infty}
satisfies the following conditions:

•

\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\lambda_{t}=0
and
\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\lambda_{t}/\lambda_{t+1}=1
;

•

\sum_{j=t/2}^{t}\lambda_{j}\geq c\log t
for every constant
c
and sufficiently large
t
;

•

The following two sequences are summable for every constant
c
,

\displaystyle\sum_{t=1}^{+\infty}\exp\left\{-c\Lambda_{t}\right\}<\infty,\qquad\text{ and }~~~\sum_{t=1}^{+\infty}\sqrt{l_{t}}<\infty,

(25)

\displaystyle\text{where }~~l_{t}\coloneqq\sum_{s=1}^{t}\big|\lambda_{s}-\lambda_{s+1}\big|\exp\big(-c[\Lambda_{t}-\Lambda_{s}]\big).

In words, Assumption
1
requires that
\left\{\lambda_{t}\right\}
converges to
0
, but the convergence rate should be slow enough.
We shall provide an example of
\{\lambda_{t}\}
satisfying this assumption in Section
E.1
.
As will be made clear from the proof,
having
\lambda_{t}\rightarrow 0
guarantees that the solution to the system of equations converges to the minimum
\ell_{1}
-norm solution,
whereas other conditions ensure that the AMP iterates do not experience drastic changes in adjacent iterations, such that the state evolution formula still accurately describe the behavior of their limit. Specifically, under these other conditions, the difference between the support sets of consecutive iterates can be properly controlled.

With this choice of
\left\{\lambda_{t}\right\}
sequence, we define a series of nonlinear systems of equations with two unknowns, indexed by
t
as follows:

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \tau^{2}&=\mathsf{F}(\tau^{2},\alpha\tau),\\
\lambda_{t}&=\alpha\tau\left(1-\frac{1}{\delta}\mathbb{E}\big[\eta^{\prime}(\Theta+\tau Z;\alpha\tau)\big]\right),\end{aligned}

(26)

where the function
\mathsf{F}(\cdot,\cdot)
is specified in expression (
24b
). As usual,
Z
is a standard Gaussian random variable that is independent of
\Theta
.
Recognizing the existence and uniqueness property shown in Section
B
, we can guarantee that the equation set (
26
) yields a unique solution pair, which shall be denoted by
({\alpha^{\star}_{t}},{\tau^{\star}_{t}})
.
Further, we define the threshold
\zeta_{t}
for our AMP updates (
21
) as follows

\displaystyle\zeta_{t}\coloneqq{\alpha^{\star}_{t}}\cdot\tau_{t}\qquad\text{for all }t>0,

(27)

where
\zeta_{0}\coloneqq 1
and
\tau_{t}
corresponds to the state evolution formula provided in the expression (
24a
).
In view of the correspondence between the AMP updates and the Lasso estimator, iteration
t
of our AMP updates takes a step towards approximating the Lasso estimator with parameter
\lambda_{t}
.
As
\lambda_{t}
converges to zero, the iteration procedure has the minimum
\ell_{1}
-norm interpolator as a limiting point.
The informal intuition is illustrated in Figure
4
.

Figure 4:
Illustration of the AMP updates for the minimum
\ell_{1}
-norm interpolator. At each step,
\bm{\theta}^{t+1}
is computed as in the expression (
21a
), with
\zeta_{t}
chosen according to (
27
).
The plateau of the blue curve stands for all the interpolators that satisfy
\bm{y}=\bm{X}\bm{\theta}
, among which
\widehat{\bm{\theta}}^{\mathsf{Int}}
has the smallest
\ell_{1}
-norm.
The curves in green stand for the Lasso loss functions (with parameter
\lambda_{t}
changing with
t
) where AMP aims to move towards its minimizer in each
t
.

We are now ready to state our main result on the risk of the min
\ell_{1}
-norm solution.

Theorem 3
.

Consider the linear model (
1
) and i.i.d. Gaussian design.
If
\mathbb{E}[\Theta^{2}]<\infty
and
\mathbb{P}(\Theta\neq 0)>0
,
then for any pseudo-Lipschitz function
\psi
, one has

\lim_{p\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{p}\sum_{i=1}^{p}\psi(\widehat{\theta}^{\mathsf{Int}}_{i},\theta_{i}^{\star})~\overset{\mathrm{a.s.}}{=}~\mathbb{E}\big[\psi(\eta(\Theta+{\tau^{\star}}Z;{\alpha^{\star}}{\tau^{\star}}),\Theta)\big],

(28)

where
Z\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
is independent of
\Theta
.
Here,
({\alpha^{\star}},{\tau^{\star}})
is the solution to the following equations

\tau^{2}=\mathsf{F}(\tau^{2},\alpha\tau);\quad\frac{1}{\delta}\mathbb{E}\big[\eta^{\prime}(\Theta+\tau Z;\alpha\tau)\big]=1.

(29)

We now point out an immediate consequence of Theorem
3
. In view of the pseudo-Lipschitz property of the function
\psi(a,b)=(a-b)^{2}
, we can obtain Theorem
2
as a corollary, namely,
the limiting risk of the minimum
\ell_{1}
-norm solution obeys

\displaystyle\lim_{p\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{p}\big\|\widehat{\bm{\theta}}^{\mathsf{Int}}-\bm{\theta}^{\star}\big\|_{2}^{2}~\overset{\mathrm{a.s.}}{=}~{{\tau^{\star}}}^{2}.

(30)

This enables us to study the risk curve by examining the equations (
29
).

Proof ideas.

Before proceeding, let us highlight several key challenges and differences in this part of the proof in comparison to
Bayati and Montanari, 2011b
. The complete details are deferred to Section
C
.
As already mentioned, we look at a sequence of AMP updates, each targeting at solving a Lasso problem with a different regularization parameter
\lambda_{t}
that obeys
\lim_{t\to\infty}\lambda_{t}=0
.
In the fixed
\lambda
scenario, it is known that even if
p>n
, the loss function around the Lasso estimate enjoys certain restricted strongly convexity. This is, however, not the case for the minimum
\ell_{1}
-norm interpolator, whose support size equals
n
; this implies that the condition number of
\bm{X}^{\top}\bm{X}
(restricted to the support) might be very large.
Consequently, it calls for the development of a new structural property tailored to the minimum
\ell_{1}
-norm solution, as we shall detail in Lemma
C.2
.

Moreover, for each
\lambda_{t}
, the AMP iterations are contractive towards different fixed points (i.e., minimizers of different Lasso problems). One thus needs to investigate how the pesudo-state evolution point
\tau^{\star}_{t}
varies with the iteration number
t
.
In addition, to demonstrate that AMP converges to the new system of equations as specified in (
15
),
at a high level, we construct some distance measure between
\bm{\theta}^{t+1}
and
\bm{\theta}^{t}
so as to guarantee that

\displaystyle\mathsf{dist}(\bm{\theta}^{t+1},\bm{\theta}^{t})\leq\exp(-\lambda_{t})\cdot\mathsf{dist}(\bm{\theta}^{t},\bm{\theta}^{t-1})+c|\lambda_{t}-\lambda_{t+1}|.

(31)

In the case of a fixed
\lambda
, the above relation simplifies to

\mathsf{dist}(\bm{\theta}^{t+1},\bm{\theta}^{t})\leq\exp(-\lambda)\cdot\mathsf{dist}(\bm{\theta}^{t},\bm{\theta}^{t-1}),

which means that
\mathsf{dist}(\bm{\theta}^{t+1},\bm{\theta}^{t})
converges linearly and
\bm{\theta}^{t}
converges to the corresponding limit.
In contrast, the second term on the right-hand side of (
31
) reflects the price one needs to pay when
\lambda_{t}
varies across iterations.
Assumption
1
is imposed to help ensure that these
errors — albeit accumulated over time — stay bounded.

3.2
Analysis ingredients for the risk curve

Thus far, we have demonstrated that the risk curve of the minimum
\ell_{1}
-norm interpolator can be characterized by the solutions to the system of equations (
15
).
In order to analyze the shape of the risk curve and establish Theorem
1
,
this section takes a close look at the geometric properties of these solutions.
For ease of exposition, let us assume without loss of generality that
\sigma^{2}=1
throughout the proof; clearly, having a different value of
\sigma^{2}
does not change the shape of the curve as long as the SNR remains unchanged.

Roadmap of the proof.

We start by providing a roadmap of our proof.
To begin with, it is challenging to track the behavior of the solution
{\tau^{\star}}
directly in the original form of the equations (
15
);
in fact,
{\tau^{\star}}
blows up as
\delta\rightarrow 1
.
Hence, we find it more convenient to work with a new parameter
\nu\coloneqq M/\tau
(resp.
{\nu^{\star}}\coloneqq M/{\tau^{\star}}
) that leads to alternative versions of Theorem
1
and (
15
).
With this change of variables in place, it suffices to study how
{\nu^{\star}}
behaves as one varies
\delta
.
Towards this end, we first eliminate the parameter
\alpha
and express
\nu
purely as a function of
\delta
. We then proceed to analyze the derivative of
{\nu^{\star}}(\delta)
using the implicit function theorem, with a special focus on the sign of
{\nu^{\star}}^{\prime}(\delta)
when
\delta
is close to
0
or
1
, as well as when
\epsilon\rightarrow 0
. As we shall argue momentarily, these steps suffice in establishing Theorem
1
.

An equivalent formulation.

As mentioned above, let us denote
\nu\coloneqq M/\tau
, and define two functions of
(\nu,\delta,\alpha)
as follows

\displaystyle F_{1}(\nu,\delta,\alpha)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\epsilon\mathbb{P}\left(\big|\nu\sqrt{\delta}+Z\big|>\alpha\right)+(1-\epsilon)\mathbb{P}\left(\left|Z\right|>\alpha\right)-\delta,

(32a)

\displaystyle F_{2}(\nu,\delta,\alpha)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\frac{\nu^{2}}{M^{2}}-1+\frac{\epsilon}{\delta}\mathbb{E}\left[\big(\eta(\sqrt{\delta}\nu+Z;\alpha)-\sqrt{\delta}\nu\big)^{2}\right]+\frac{1-\epsilon}{\delta}\mathbb{E}\left[\eta^{2}(Z;\alpha)\right],

(32b)

where
Z\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
and is independent of
\Theta\sim P_{\Theta}
.
Under our assumptions on
\Theta
(cf. (
11
)),
solving the equations (
15a
) and (
15b
) can be accomplished by first finding the solutions
\big({\nu^{\star}}(\delta),{\alpha^{\star}}(\delta)\big)
to

\displaystyle\begin{cases}F_{1}(\nu,\delta,\alpha)=0,\\
F_{2}(\nu,\delta,\alpha)=0,\end{cases}

(33)

and then mapping
{\nu^{\star}}
back to
{\tau^{\star}}
.

3.2.1
Step 1: existence of the mapping
{\nu^{\star}}(\delta)

We now attempt to eliminate the variable
\alpha
in (
33
), and expressing
{\nu^{\star}}
as a function of
\delta
. For any
\delta\in(0,1)
and
\nu>0
, direct computation of the derivative of the function
F_{1}(\nu,\delta,\alpha)
yields

\displaystyle\nabla_{\alpha}F_{1}(\nu,\delta,\alpha)=-\epsilon\left[\phi(\alpha-\sqrt{\delta}\nu)+\phi(\alpha+\sqrt{\delta}\nu)\right]-2(1-\epsilon)\phi(\alpha)<0.

It is also straightforward to calculate the limiting values

\displaystyle\lim_{\alpha\rightarrow 0^{+}}F_{1}(\nu,\delta,\alpha)=1-\delta>0;\quad\lim_{\alpha\rightarrow+\infty}F_{1}(\nu,\delta,\alpha)=-\delta<0.

Based on the above observations, given any
\delta
and
\nu
, the function
F_{1}(\nu,\delta,\alpha)
is monotonically non-increasing in
\alpha
, and can take both positive and negative values within the interval
(0,\infty)
.
As a result, there exists a mapping from
(\nu,\delta)\to\alpha
that satisfies
F_{1}(\nu,\delta,\alpha)=0
; with an abuse of notation, we often denote this function as
\alpha(\nu,\delta)
.
Substitution into the function
F_{2}
allows us to define

F_{3}(\nu,\delta)\coloneqq F_{2}(\nu,\delta,\alpha(\nu,\delta)).

(34)

Here, the function
F_{3}
depends solely on the two parameters
(\nu,\delta).

Armed with the derivations above, solving (
33
) comes down to finding a solution to
F_{3}(\nu,\delta)=0
.

By construction of the function
F_{3}
, we know that the solutions to
F_{3}(\nu,\delta)=0
correspond to the solutions to the system of equations (
15
).
As we shall demonstrate in Proposition
B.1
, for every
0<\delta<1
, there exists a unique pair of
(\tau,\alpha)
satisfying (
15
); therefore,
F_{3}(\nu,\delta)=0
yields a unique solution for every
0<\delta<1
— which shall be denoted by
{{\nu^{\star}}}(\delta)
in the sequel.
Correspondingly, the solution pair for (
15
) shall be written as

({\tau^{\star}}(\delta),{\alpha^{\star}}(\delta))
where
{\alpha^{\star}}(\delta)\coloneqq\alpha({\tau^{\star}}(\delta),\delta)
.
We also note that since both
F_{1}
and
F_{2}
are smooth functions with bounded derivatives w.r.t. all parameters,
{{\nu^{\star}}}^{\prime}(\delta)
exists and is continuous.

3.2.2
Step 2: derivative of
{\nu^{\star}}(\delta)

With the mapping
{\nu^{\star}}(\delta)
in place, we can translate Theorem
1
into statements about
{{\nu^{\star}}}^{\prime}(\delta)
.
Before doing so, recall that the risk incurred by using
\bm{\theta}=\bm{0}
as the estimator satisfies

\displaystyle\mathsf{Risk}(\bm{0})=\mathbb{E}\left[(\langle\bm{x}_{i},\,\bm{\theta}^{\star}\rangle+z_{i})^{2}\right]=1+\frac{1}{n}\|\bm{\theta}^{\star}\|_{2}^{2}~\overset{\mathrm{a.s.}}{\longrightarrow}~1+\epsilon M^{2}\eqqcolon\tau_{0}^{2}.

(35)

Let us define the corresponding value of
\nu
as
\nu_{0}\coloneqq M/\tau_{0}
.
Formally, to prove the first two claims in Theorem
1
, it suffices to establish the following proposition.

Proposition 3
.

In the setting of Theorem
1
, for
\delta\in(0,1)
,
{\nu^{\star}}(\delta)
satisfies the following properties:

1.

\lim_{\delta\rightarrow 0^{+}}{\nu^{\star}}(\delta)=\nu_{0}
;

2.

There exist two constants
0<\delta_{1},\delta_{2}<1
such that when
0<\delta<\delta_{1}
and
\delta_{2}<\delta<1
,
{{\nu^{\star}}}^{\prime}(\delta)<0
.

Clearly, if Proposition
3
were valid,
then the first two claims in Theorem
1
would follow immediately by invoking the change of variables
\tau=M/\nu
.
Now we discuss how to establish this proposition.
For notational simplicity, we use
{\nu^{\star}}
and
{\alpha^{\star}}
to denote the unique solution to (
15a
) and (
15b
) for any
\delta\in(0,1)
, which should be understood as
{\nu^{\star}}(\delta)
and
{\alpha^{\star}}(\delta)
.
To begin with, recognizing the fact that
F_{1}({\nu^{\star}},\delta,{\alpha^{\star}})=0
,
the implicit function theorem implies that

\displaystyle\nabla_{\nu}\alpha({\nu^{\star}},\delta)=\left.-\frac{\nabla_{\nu}F_{1}(\nu,\delta,\alpha)}{\nabla_{\alpha}F_{1}(\nu,\delta,\alpha)}\right|_{({\nu^{\star}},\delta,{\alpha^{\star}})};\quad\nabla_{\delta}\alpha({\nu^{\star}},\delta)=\left.-\frac{\nabla_{\delta}F_{1}(\nu,\delta,\alpha)}{\nabla_{\alpha}F_{1}(\nu,\delta,\alpha)}\right|_{({\nu^{\star}},\delta,{\alpha^{\star}})}.

(36)

We are now ready to derive an explicit expression of
{\nu^{\star}}^{\prime}(\delta)
.
A little algebra leads to

\displaystyle{\nu^{\star}}^{\prime}(\delta)=\left.-\frac{\nabla_{\delta}F_{3}(\nu,\delta)}{\nabla_{\nu}F_{3}(\nu,\delta)}\right|_{({\nu^{\star}},\delta)}

\displaystyle=\left.-\frac{\nabla_{\delta}F_{2}(\nu,\delta,\alpha)+\nabla_{\delta}\alpha(\nu,\delta)\nabla_{\alpha}F_{2}(\nu,\delta,\alpha)}{\nabla_{\nu}F_{2}(\nu,\delta,\alpha)+\nabla_{\nu}\alpha(\nu,\delta)\nabla_{\alpha}F_{2}(\nu,\delta,\alpha)}\right|_{({\nu^{\star}},\delta,{\alpha^{\star}})}

\displaystyle=\left.-\frac{\nabla_{\delta}F_{2}\nabla_{\alpha}F_{1}-\nabla_{\alpha}F_{2}\nabla_{\delta}F_{1}}{\nabla_{\nu}F_{2}\nabla_{\alpha}F_{1}-\nabla_{\alpha}F_{2}\nabla_{\nu}F_{1}}\right|_{({\nu^{\star}},\delta,{\alpha^{\star}})},

(37)

where the second equality invokes the relation (
36
).
This expression plays a crucial role in our subsequent analysis in understanding how
{\tau^{\star}}
changes with
\delta
.

In order to establish Proposition
3
, we gather in the following two lemmas some key facts on the limiting behavior of
{\nu^{\star}}^{\prime}(\delta)
and
{\nu^{\star}}(\delta)
, when
\delta\rightarrow 0^{+}
and
\delta\rightarrow 1^{-}
, respectively.
All of these are stated with the assumptions of Theorem
1
imposed, with the proofs
deferred to Section
D.1
.

Lemma 1
.

When
\delta\rightarrow 0^{+}
, the numerator and denominator in (
3.2.2
) satisfy respectively the following properties

\displaystyle\nabla_{\delta}F_{2}\nabla_{\alpha}F_{1}-\nabla_{\alpha}F_{2}\nabla_{\delta}F_{1}\sim-2{{\alpha^{\star}}}^{-3},

(38a)

\displaystyle\nabla_{\nu}F_{2}\nabla_{\alpha}F_{1}-\nabla_{\alpha}F_{2}\nabla_{\nu}F_{1}\sim-4\nu_{0}^{-1}\phi({\alpha^{\star}}),

(38b)

where all the partial derivatives of
F_{1}
and
F_{2}
are evaluated at the point
({\nu^{\star}},\delta,{\alpha^{\star}})
.
Additionally, it holds that

\displaystyle\lim_{\delta\rightarrow 0^{+}}{\nu^{\star}}=\nu_{0}.

(39)

Lemma 2
.

When
\delta\rightarrow 1^{-}
, it satisfies that

\lim_{\delta\rightarrow 1^{-}}{{\nu^{\star}}}^{\prime}(\delta)=-\infty.

(40)

Note that the property (
39
) in Lemma
1
validates the first claim in Proposition
3
directly.
Therefore, to prove Proposition
3
,
we are only left with verifying the second claim in Proposition
3
.
In view of Lemma
1
, it is guaranteed that as
\delta\rightarrow 0^{+}
, both the denominator and the numerator in (
3.2.2
) are negative.
By continuity of
{\nu^{\star}}^{\prime}(\delta)
, there exists some
\delta_{1}>0
such that when
0<\delta<\delta_{1}
, one has
{\nu^{\star}}^{\prime}(\delta)<0
.
Finally, Lemma
2
immediately suggests that one can find
\delta_{2}>0
such that: when
\delta_{2}<\delta<1
, one has
{\nu^{\star}}^{\prime}(\delta)<0
.
Taking these properties collectively concludes the proof of Proposition
3
.

3.2.3
Step 3: limit behavior for the case with
\epsilon\rightarrow 0

Finally, let us move on to establishing the third and fourth claims of Theorem
1
. Specifically, fixing some
\delta>0
, we shall study how the risk limit
{\tau^{\star}}
behaves as
\epsilon
varies, particularly as it tends to zero.
Thus far, we have focused on the case when both
\epsilon
and
M
are regarded as fixed constants while the value of
\delta
varies;
in this case, the analyses were primarily performed w.r.t.
{\nu^{\star}}^{\prime}(\delta)
,
since studying
{\nu^{\star}}(\delta)
and
{\nu^{\star}}(\delta)/M
are equivalent when
M
is taken to be a fixed constant.
However, in the case when we fix SNR (namely,
\epsilon M^{2}
) as opposed to
M
, one has
M\rightarrow\infty
as
\epsilon\rightarrow 0
,
and hence studying
{\nu^{\star}}(\delta)
and studying
{\nu^{\star}}(\delta)/M
are no longer equivalent.
As a result, we need to analyze
{\nu^{\star}}(\delta)/M
directly, that is, to examine the behavior of
{\nu^{\star}}(\delta)/M
and
{\nu^{\star}}^{\prime}(\delta)/M
in a fixed-SNR regime as
\epsilon
approaches zero.

The readers shall also bear in mind that we always focus on the derivative of the risk of the model (
11
) with given
(M,\epsilon)
but varying
\delta
.
In other words, the function
{\nu^{\star}}(\delta):(0,1)\mapsto\mathbb{R}_{+}
is defined for any given
(M,\epsilon)
, and we do not associate the change of
(M,\epsilon)
and the change of
\delta
together.
To emphasize that we now work with a
fixed
ratio, we shall use
\delta_{0}
in place of
\delta
.
At this given ratio
\delta_{0}
, the quantities
{\alpha^{\star}}
and
{\nu^{\star}}
are treated as functions of
\epsilon
.

Proof for part (d) of Theorem
1
.

When
\epsilon\rightarrow 0
, we first make a key observation on
the behavior of
\frac{{\nu^{\star}}^{\prime}(\delta_{0})}{M}
, as summarized in the lemma below.

Lemma 3
.

In the setting of Theorem
1
, given any fixed SNR
=\epsilon M^{2}
and
\delta_{0}\in(0,1)
, the derivative
{\nu^{\star}}^{\prime}
(with respect to
\delta
) obeys

\displaystyle\lim_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0}\frac{{\nu^{\star}}^{\prime}(\delta_{0})}{M}>0.

(41)

The proof of Lemma
3
contains two main parts, whose
details are deferred to Section
D.4
.
First, letting
\alpha_{0}\coloneqq-\Phi^{-1}(\delta_{0}/2)
for this given
\delta_{0}
, we establish the following relation

\displaystyle{\alpha^{\star}}\rightarrow\alpha_{0};\quad\text{ and }~\frac{{\nu^{\star}}}{M}\rightarrow\sqrt{1-2\delta_{0}^{-1}[-\alpha_{0}\phi(\alpha_{0})+(\alpha_{0}^{2}+1)\Phi(-\alpha_{0})]},

(42)

as one takes
\epsilon\rightarrow 0
; the details can be found in Section
D.4
.
It is worth noting that both
{\alpha^{\star}}
and
\frac{{\nu^{\star}}}{M}
converge to fixed quantities that are determined only by
\delta_{0}
in this limit.

Equipped with these two limiting values, we proceed to consider the numerator and denominator of
\frac{{\nu^{\star}}^{\prime}}{M}
, with the assistance of
the expression (
3.2.2
).
In fact, one can pin down the limiting orders of these two parts as follows

\displaystyle\nabla_{\delta}F_{2}\nabla_{\alpha}F_{1}-\nabla_{\alpha}F_{2}\nabla_{\delta}F_{1}

\displaystyle~\rightarrow~2\delta_{0}^{-2}\phi(\alpha_{0})\left[-2\alpha_{0}\phi(\alpha_{0})+(\alpha_{0}^{2}+1)\delta_{0}\right]-2\delta_{0}^{-1}\left[2\phi(\alpha_{0})-\alpha_{0}\delta_{0}\right],

(43a)

and

\displaystyle M(\nabla_{\nu}F_{2}\nabla_{\alpha}F_{1}-\nabla_{\alpha}F_{2}\nabla_{\nu}F_{1})

\displaystyle~\rightarrow~-4\phi(\alpha_{0})\sqrt{1-2\delta_{0}^{-1}[-\alpha_{0}\phi(\alpha_{0})+(\alpha_{0}^{2}+1)\Phi(-\alpha_{0})]}.

(43b)

The details can be found in Step 2 in Section
D.4
.

Putting these together, we can conclude that

\displaystyle\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\frac{{\nu^{\star}}^{\prime}(\delta_{0})}{M}

\displaystyle=\frac{2\delta_{0}^{-2}\left[-2\alpha_{0}\phi^{2}(\alpha_{0})+(\alpha_{0}^{2}+1)\delta_{0}\phi(\alpha_{0})-2\delta_{0}\phi(\alpha_{0})+\alpha_{0}\delta_{0}^{2}\right]}{4\phi(\alpha_{0})\sqrt{1-2\delta_{0}^{-1}[-\alpha_{0}\phi(\alpha_{0})+(\alpha_{0}^{2}+1)\Phi(-\alpha_{0})]}}<0,

where the last inequality follows due to the fact that

\Phi(-\alpha_{0})=\delta_{0}/2
and the basic relation

\displaystyle\Phi(-\alpha_{0})\in\Big[\phi(\alpha_{0})\bigg(\frac{1}{\alpha_{0}}-\frac{1}{\alpha_{0}^{3}}\bigg),~\phi(\alpha_{0})\bigg(\frac{1}{\alpha_{0}}-\frac{1}{\alpha_{0}^{3}}+\frac{1}{\alpha_{0}^{5}}\bigg)\Big].

In summary, in view of Lemma
3
,
we can conclude that there exists
\epsilon^{\star}>0
, depending only on SNR and
\delta_{0}
, such that: when
\epsilon<\epsilon^{\star}
, one has
{\nu^{\star}}^{\prime}(\delta_{0})/M<0
.
Translating this back to
{\tau^{\star}}=M/{\nu^{\star}}
ensures the existence of an
\epsilon^{\star}
such that: when
\epsilon<\epsilon^{\star}
, one has
{\tau^{\star}}^{\prime}<0
.
We have thus completed the proof of Part (d) of Theorem
1
.

Proof of part (c) of Theorem
1
.

The idea for proving this result is to find
\delta\in(0,1)
such that the value of
{{\tau^{\star}}}^{2}(\delta)
is strictly below
\mathsf{Risk}(\bm{0})
.
Recognizing that
{{\tau^{\star}}}^{2}(\delta)
decays to
\mathsf{Risk}(\bm{0})
as
p/n
approaches infinity, there must exist an ascending regime for
{\tau^{\star}}
as a function of
p/n.

More concretely, let us view
{\nu^{\star}}/M
as a function of
\delta
within the interval
\delta\in(0,1)
.
Rewriting the relation (
42
) ensures that as
\epsilon\to 0
,
one has

\displaystyle\frac{1}{{\tau^{\star}}(\delta)}=\frac{{\nu^{\star}}(\delta)}{M}\rightarrow\sqrt{\frac{\alpha\phi(\alpha)-\alpha^{2}\Phi(-\alpha)}{\Phi(-\alpha)}}\eqqcolon H(\delta)\qquad\text{for }~\alpha\coloneqq-\Phi^{-1}(\delta/2).

It can be easily verified that the function
H(\cdot)
is a continuous and decreasing function of
\delta
on
(0,1)
.
Additionally, direct calculations yield

\displaystyle\lim_{\delta\rightarrow 1^{-}}H(\delta)=0;\qquad\lim_{\delta\rightarrow 0^{+}}H(\delta)=1.

(44)

As a result, the continuity of
H(\cdot)
guarantees that there exists
\delta_{\mathrm{SNR}}>0
such that

\displaystyle H(\delta)\coloneqq\lim_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0}\frac{{\nu^{\star}}(\delta)}{M}>\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+\mathrm{SNR}}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\mathsf{Risk}(\bm{0})}}\in(0,1),\qquad\text{for }~\delta<\delta_{\mathrm{SNR}},

where we recall
\mathrm{SNR}\coloneqq\epsilon M^{2}
.
In other words, recognizing the relation
{\tau^{\star}}(\delta)\coloneqq M/{\nu^{\star}}(\delta)
, we can show the existence of a regime for
\delta\in(0,1)
where the
\epsilon
-limit of
{\tau^{\star}}(\delta)
lies below
\sqrt{\mathsf{Risk}(\bm{0})}
.

In addition, for any given
(\epsilon,M)
, recall that the limiting value (as
\delta\rightarrow 0^{+}
) obeys

\displaystyle\lim_{\delta\rightarrow 0^{+}}\frac{{\nu^{\star}}(\delta)}{M}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+\mathrm{SNR}}}.

(45)

It further implies that for any fixed
\delta_{0}<\delta_{\mathrm{SNR}}
, one can find a corresponding
\epsilon_{0}
depending on
\delta_{0}
such that

\displaystyle{\tau^{\star}}(\delta_{0})<\lim_{\delta\to 0^{+}}{\tau^{\star}}(\delta)

holds for every
\epsilon\leq\epsilon_{0}
.
Consequently,
{\tau^{\star}}(\delta)
has an ascending phase w.r.t.
p/n.

Putting the above pieces together establishes the claimed result.

4
Numerical simulations and discussion

This section conducts numerical experiments to confirm the applicability of our results in finite samples and non-Gaussian designs.
Along the way, we shall also point out several directions worthy of future investigation.

Finite-sample behavior.

Although the theorems obtained in the paper are asymptotic in nature, our numerical experiments suggest that they are accurate descriptions of the risk behavior even when
p
and
n
are on the order of 10s or 100s.

Beyond Gaussian design.

Thus far, our risk characterization focuses on the idealistic case with i.i.d. Gaussian design matrices.
There is no shortage of practical scenarios where such distributional assumptions are violated.
To see whether our prediction continues to be valid beyond Gaussian design,
we carry out several empirical experiments concerning design matrices that are composed of i.i.d. non-Gaussian entries.
Figure
6
illustrates two cases where the entries are generated from the Bernoulli distributions and the
t
-distribution with parameter
3
, respectively.
Our theoretical risk characterization remains fairly accurate in these numerical experiments.
This is perhaps not unexpected, due to a
universality
phemoneon that has been justified in multiple other problems with i.i.d. random design (see, e.g.,
Bayati et al., 2015
;
Oymak and Tropp, 2018
;
Montanari and Nguyen, 2017
;
Chen and Lam, 2021
).
These predictions might, however, be completely off when the covariates are correlated,
meaning that the covariance structure of the design matrix plays a pivotal role in determining the shape of the risk curves.
Leveraging the current effort towards understanding Lasso under correlated designs
(
Celentano et al., 2020
)
, we conjecture that the risk of the interpolator is dictated by a more complicated nonlinear system of equations that reflects the covariance structure.
Given that the main message of this paper is to verify the existence of a multiple-descent phenomenon,
we leave these more general cases to future investigation.

APPENDIX

Appendix A
Proof of Proposition
1

In what follows, we intend to establish the two claims separately.

Case I:
n/p>1
.

In this part, we aim to prove that, for any
\delta>1
, the Lasso risk converges to the risk of the least-square estimator — denoted by
\widehat{\bm{\theta}}^{\mathsf{LS}}
— as
\lambda\rightarrow 0
.
To begin with, the risk of
\widehat{\bm{\theta}}^{\mathsf{LS}}
can be characterized using standard random matrix theory results; see, for example,
Hastie et al., 2019
.
As
p\rightarrow\infty
, one has

\displaystyle\mathsf{Risk}(\widehat{\bm{\theta}}^{\mathsf{LS}})=\sigma^{2}+\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{E}\big[\|\widehat{\bm{\theta}}^{\mathsf{LS}}-\bm{\theta}^{\star}\|_{2}^{2}\big]~{\rightarrow}~\sigma^{2}\frac{\delta}{\delta-1}.

(46)

In view of the KKT condition for the corresponding loss functions, we can see that the Lasso and the least-square estimator obey

\displaystyle\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{\lambda}-\bm{\theta}^{\star}=(\bm{X}^{\top}\bm{X})^{-1}(\bm{X}^{\top}\bm{z}-\lambda\bm{s})\qquad\text{and}\qquad\widehat{\bm{\theta}}^{\mathsf{LS}}-\bm{\theta}^{\star}=(\bm{X}^{\top}\bm{X})^{-1}\bm{X}^{\top}\bm{z}.

Here,
\bm{s}=[s_{j}]_{1\leq j\leq p}
denotes the sub-gradient of the
\ell_{1}
norm at point
\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{\lambda}
, which obeys
s_{j}\in\left[-1,1\right]
. Thus, the risk of the Lasso satisfies

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[\|\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{\lambda}-\bm{\theta}^{\star}\|_{2}^{2}\big]=\mathbb{E}\big[\|\widehat{\bm{\theta}}^{\mathsf{LS}}-\bm{\theta}^{\star}\|_{2}^{2}\big]-2\lambda\mathbb{E}\big[\langle(\bm{X}^{\top}\bm{X})^{-2}\bm{X}^{\top}\bm{z},\,\bm{s}\rangle\big]+\lambda^{2}\mathbb{E}\big[\|(\bm{X}^{\top}\bm{X})^{-1}\bm{s}\|_{2}^{2}\big],

which combined with the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality further leads to

\displaystyle\ \left|\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{E}[\|\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{\lambda}-\bm{\theta}^{\star}\|_{2}^{2}]-\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{E}[\|\widehat{\bm{\theta}}^{\mathsf{LS}}-\bm{\theta}^{\star}\|_{2}^{2}]\right|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\ 2\lambda\sqrt{\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|(\bm{X}^{\top}\bm{X})^{-1}\bm{X}^{\top}\bm{z}\right\|_{2}^{2}\right]}\sqrt{\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{E}\big[\|(\bm{X}^{\top}\bm{X})^{-1}\|_{2}^{2}\|\bm{s}\|_{2}^{2}\big]}+\lambda^{2}\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{E}\left[\|(\bm{X}^{\top}\bm{X})^{-1}\|_{2}^{2}\|\bm{s}\|_{2}^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\ 2\lambda\sqrt{\mathsf{Risk}(\widehat{\bm{\theta}}^{\mathsf{LS}})-\sigma^{2}}\sqrt{\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{\delta}\sigma_{\min}^{-4}(\bm{X})\right]}+\frac{\lambda^{2}}{\delta}\mathbb{E}\left[\sigma_{\min}^{-4}(\bm{X})\right].

(47)

Now it is sufficient to control the two terms on the right-hand side above, and show that both terms converge to
0
when
p\rightarrow\infty.

First, it has been shown in the proof of
Chen and Dongarra, 2005
that

\mathbb{P}\left(\sigma_{\min}(\bm{X})\leq\frac{\sqrt{n}}{x^{2}}\right)<\frac{n^{n-p+1}}{(n-p+1)!}\frac{1}{x^{n-p+1}}\leq\left(\frac{e}{x}\right)^{n-p+1}

for any
x>0
,
where the last inequality comes from the well-known Stirling inequality
\sqrt{2\pi}m^{m+\frac{1}{2}}e^{-m}\leq m!

(
Robbins, 1955
)
.
Consequently,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\sigma_{\min}^{-4}(\bm{X})\right]

\displaystyle\leq\bigg(\frac{2}{1-1/\sqrt{\delta}}\bigg)^{4}\mathbb{P}\left\{\sigma_{\min}^{-1}(\bm{X})\leq\frac{2}{1-1/\sqrt{\delta}}\right\}+{\displaystyle\int}_{\big(\frac{2}{1-1/\sqrt{\delta}}\big)^{4}}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}\left\{\sigma_{\min}^{-4}(\bm{X})>z\right\}\mathrm{d}z

\displaystyle\leq 2\bigg(\frac{2}{1-1/\sqrt{\delta}}\bigg)^{4}+{\displaystyle\int}_{\big(\frac{2}{1-1/\sqrt{\delta}}\big)^{4}}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}\left\{\sigma_{\min}(\bm{X})<\frac{1}{z^{1/4}}\right\}\mathrm{d}z

\displaystyle\leq 2\bigg(\frac{2}{1-1/\sqrt{\delta}}\bigg)^{4}+{\displaystyle\int}_{\big(\frac{2}{1-1/\sqrt{\delta}}\big)^{4}}^{\infty}\left(\frac{e}{z^{1/8}n^{1/4}}\right)^{n-p+1}\mathrm{d}z

\displaystyle\leq 4\bigg(\frac{2}{1-1/\sqrt{\delta}}\bigg)^{4}

for sufficiently large
n
.
In addition, by virtue of (
46
), it is guaranteed that

\displaystyle\sqrt{\mathsf{Risk}(\widehat{\bm{\theta}}^{\mathsf{LS}})-\sigma^{2}}\rightarrow\sqrt{\frac{\sigma^{2}}{\delta-1}}.

Substitution into (
47
) yields

\displaystyle\lim_{\lambda\rightarrow 0}\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{E}[\|\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{\lambda}-\bm{\theta}^{\star}\|_{2}^{2}]=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{E}\big[\|\widehat{\bm{\theta}}^{\mathsf{LS}}-\bm{\theta}^{\star}\|_{2}^{2}\big]

for any
0<\delta<1
that is strictly bounded away from
1
.

\displaystyle{\tau^{\star}}^{\prime}(\alpha)=\frac{\partial_{\alpha}\mathsf{F}(\tau^{2},\alpha\tau)}{2\tau-\partial_{\tau}\mathsf{F}(\tau^{2},\alpha\tau)}=-\frac{\tau^{\star}(\alpha)\left[\alpha E_{1}-E_{3}\right]}{(1+\alpha^{2})E_{1}+E_{2}-\alpha E_{3}-\delta}.

For
\alpha_{0}
with
\lambda(\alpha_{0})=0
, we know
E_{1}=\delta
, and then

\displaystyle{\tau^{\star}}^{\prime}(\alpha_{0})=-\frac{\tau^{\star}(\alpha_{0})\left[\alpha_{0}\delta-E_{3}\right]}{\alpha_{0}^{2}\delta+E_{2}-\alpha_{0}E_{3}}.

In terms of
\lambda^{\prime}(\alpha)
, one has

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\alpha}\mathbb{E}\left[\eta^{\prime}(\Theta+\tau_{\star}(\alpha)Z;\tau^{\star}(\alpha)\alpha)\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[(-1+\Xi\frac{{\tau^{\star}}^{\prime}(\alpha)}{\tau_{\star}(\alpha)})\phi(-\Xi-\alpha)+(-1-\Xi\frac{{\tau^{\star}}^{\prime}(\alpha)}{\tau_{\star}(\alpha)})\phi(\Xi-\alpha)\right]

\displaystyle=-\frac{\alpha\left[\alpha E_{1}E_{3}-E_{3}^{2}+E_{1}E_{2}\right]+E_{3}(E_{1}-\delta)}{(1+\alpha^{2})E_{1}+E_{2}-\alpha E_{3}-\delta}.

Finally, we are ready to calculate the derivative
\lambda^{\prime}(\alpha)
at
\alpha=\alpha_{0}
. By expression (
19
), and noticing that
\lambda(\alpha_{0})=0
and
E_{1}=\delta
in this case, we calculate

\displaystyle\lambda^{\prime}(\alpha_{0})

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha_{0}{\tau^{\star}}^{\prime}(\alpha_{0})+\tau^{\star}(\alpha_{0})}{\alpha_{0}\tau^{\star}(\alpha_{0})}\lambda(\alpha_{0})-\left.\frac{\alpha_{0}\tau^{\star}(\alpha_{0})}{\delta}\frac{d}{d\alpha}\mathbb{E}\left[\eta^{\prime}(\Theta+\tau^{\star}(\alpha)Z;\tau^{\star}(\alpha)\alpha)\right]\right|_{\alpha=\alpha_{0}}

(52)

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha_{0}^{2}\tau^{\star}(\alpha_{0})}{\delta}\cdot\frac{\delta\alpha_{0}E_{3}-E_{3}^{2}+\delta E_{2}}{\delta\alpha_{0}^{2}+E_{2}-\alpha_{0}E_{3}}.

Bounding the derivatives.

To establish the inequality (
49
), it is sufficient for us to control the following quantities

\displaystyle\delta\alpha_{0}E_{3}-E_{3}^{2}+\delta E_{2}~\text{ and }~\delta\alpha_{0}^{2}+E_{2}-\alpha_{0}E_{3},

respectively.
Let us first express the function
\mathsf{F}(\tau^{2},\tau\alpha)
in an explicit fashion.
For every fixed
\xi
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\eta(\xi+Z;\alpha)-\xi\right]^{2}=

\displaystyle(-\alpha-\xi)\phi(\alpha-\xi)+(\alpha^{2}+1)\Phi(-\alpha+\xi)+(-\alpha+\xi)\phi(\alpha+\xi)+(\alpha^{2}+1)\Phi(-\alpha-\xi)

\displaystyle+\xi^{2}\left[\Phi(\alpha-\xi)-\Phi(-\alpha-\xi)\right].

Taking expectation with respect to
\xi=\Theta/\tau^{\star}(\alpha)
on both sides, we arrive at

\displaystyle{\tau^{\star}(\alpha)}^{2}=\mathsf{F}({\tau^{\star}(\alpha)}^{2},\tau^{\star}(\alpha)\alpha)=\sigma^{2}+\frac{{\tau^{\star}(\alpha)}^{2}}{\delta}\left[(\alpha^{2}+1)E_{1}+E_{2}-\alpha E_{3}+E_{4}\right].

(53)

For
\alpha_{0}>\alpha_{\min}(\delta)>0
with
\lambda(\alpha_{0})=0
, one has
E_{1}=\delta
and
E_{4}\geq 0
.
As a result, the equation (
53
) leads to

\displaystyle\alpha_{0}^{2}\delta+E_{2}-\alpha E_{3}\leq-\sigma^{2}\delta{{\tau^{\star}}^{-2}(\alpha_{0})}<0.

(54)

Next we control the quantity
\delta\alpha_{0}E_{3}-E_{3}^{2}+\delta E_{2}
by looking at two cases separately.
Observing that
\alpha_{0}^{2}\delta+E_{2}-\alpha E_{3}\leq-\sigma^{2}\delta{{\tau^{\star}}^{-2}(\alpha_{0})}
,
E_{3}\geq 0
and
E_{2}\leq 0
, we know

\displaystyle\delta\alpha_{0}E_{3}-E_{3}^{2}+\delta E_{2}<E_{3}\left[-\frac{\sigma^{2}\delta}{{\tau^{\star}}^{2}(\alpha_{0})\alpha_{0}}-\frac{E_{2}}{\alpha_{0}}\right]+\delta E_{2}=-\frac{\sigma^{2}\delta}{{\tau^{\star}}^{2}(\alpha_{0})\alpha_{0}}E_{3}+\frac{\alpha_{0}\delta-E_{3}}{\alpha_{0}}E_{2}.

Then we have

\displaystyle\delta\alpha_{0}E_{3}-E_{3}^{2}+\delta E_{2}\leq\left\{\begin{aligned} &-\frac{\sigma^{2}\delta}{{\tau^{\star}}^{2}(\alpha_{0})\alpha_{0}}E_{3},&&\alpha_{0}\delta-E_{3}>0;\\
&\delta E_{2},&&\alpha_{0}\delta-E_{3}\leq 0.\end{aligned}\right.

Taking the above properties collectively with (
52
), we now move on to prove the conclusion in (
49
) for two cases respectively, namely,

\displaystyle\lambda^{\prime}(\alpha_{0})\left\{\begin{aligned} &\geq\frac{\sigma^{2}\alpha_{0}}{\tau^{\star}(\alpha_{0})}\frac{E_{3}}{|E_{2}|-\alpha_{0}\left[\delta\alpha_{0}-E_{3}\right]}\geq\frac{\sigma^{2}\alpha_{0}}{\tau^{\star}(\alpha_{0})}\frac{E_{3}}{|E_{2}|},&\alpha_{0}\delta-E_{3}>0;\\
&=\frac{\alpha_{0}^{2}\tau^{\star}(\alpha_{0})}{\delta}\frac{E_{3}\left[\alpha_{0}\delta-E_{3}\right]+\delta E_{2}}{\alpha_{0}\left[\alpha_{0}\delta-E_{3}\right]+E_{2}}\geq\frac{\alpha_{0}^{2}\tau^{\star}(\alpha_{0})}{\delta}\min\left\{\delta,\frac{E_{3}}{\alpha_{0}}\right\}=\min\left\{\alpha_{0}^{2}\tau^{\star}(\alpha_{0}),\frac{\alpha_{0}\tau^{\star}(\alpha_{0})E_{3}}{\delta}\right\},&\alpha_{0}\delta-E_{3}\leq 0.\end{aligned}\right.

(55)

For the upper bound of
\lambda^{\prime}(\alpha_{0})
, we can directly verify that

\displaystyle\delta\alpha_{0}E_{3}-E_{3}^{2}+\delta E_{2}\geq\delta E_{2}-4,

which combined with the expression (
54
) leads to

\displaystyle\lambda^{\prime}(\alpha_{0})\leq\frac{\alpha_{0}^{2}\tau^{\star}(\alpha_{0})}{\delta}\frac{\delta|E_{2}|+4}{\sigma^{2}\delta{\tau^{\star}}^{-2}(\alpha_{0})}.

(56)

From our assumption
\mathbb{E}[\Theta^{2}]<\infty
, we can find
M
, such that

\mathbb{E}[\Theta^{2}\textbf{1}\left\{|\Theta|\leq M\right\}]\geq\mathbb{E}[\Theta^{2}]/2.

Then we know that
\mathbb{P}(|\Theta|\leq M)\geq\mathbb{E}[\Theta^{2}/M^{2}\textbf{1}\left\{|\Theta|\leq M\right\}]\geq\mathbb{E}[\Theta^{2}]/(2M^{2})
. Combining with the definition of
E_{3}
yields

\displaystyle E_{3}\geq\mathbb{E}\left[\phi(|\Xi|-\alpha_{0})\right]\geq\mathbb{E}\left[\phi\left(\frac{|\Theta|}{{\tau^{\star}}(\alpha_{0})}+\alpha_{0}\right)\right]\geq\frac{\mathbb{E}[\Theta^{2}]}{2M^{2}}\phi\left(\frac{M}{{\tau^{\star}}(\alpha_{0})}+\alpha_{0}\right).

(57)

Also, it is easily seen that

\displaystyle|E_{2}|\leq\max_{x\in\mathbb{R}}\left\{x\phi(x-\alpha_{0})-x\phi(-x-\alpha_{0})\right\},

(58)

where the right-hand side is positive and bounded away from
0
whenever
\alpha_{0}
is positive. Therefore, the right-hand side of the expression (
55
) and the expression (
56
) are both bounded away from
0
and
\infty
for any fixed
\alpha_{0}\geq\alpha_{\min}
. Combining these two cases, we have proved the advertised inequality (

).

B.3
Proof of Proposition
B.3

From the proof of
Bayati and Montanari, 2011b
, we know that the function
\tau^{2}\mapsto\mathsf{F}(\tau^{2},\alpha\tau)
is concave for any
\alpha>0
and
\Theta
not equal to
0
. Therefore, we obtain

\displaystyle\Big|\frac{\tau_{t+1}^{2}-{\tau^{\star}_{t}}^{2}}{\tau_{t}^{2}-{\tau^{\star}_{t}}^{2}}\Big|\leq\frac{{\tau^{\star}_{t}}^{2}-\sigma^{2}}{{\tau^{\star}_{t}}^{2}-0}\leq 1-\frac{\sigma^{2}}{{\tau^{\star}_{\max}}^{2}}=:\eta,

as
\tau_{t+1}^{2}=\mathsf{F}(\tau^{2},\alpha\tau)
and
\sigma^{2}=\mathsf{F}(0,0).

Consequently, it leads to

\displaystyle\left|\tau_{t+1}^{2}-{\tau^{\star}_{t+1}}^{2}\right|\leq\eta\left|\tau_{t}^{2}-{\tau^{\star}_{t}}^{2}\right|+\left|{\tau^{\star}_{t}}^{2}-{\tau^{\star}_{t+1}}^{2}\right|.

Without loss of generality, assume
\left\{\lambda_{t}\right\}
decays with
t
. Invoking the above relation recursively, we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{\left|\tau_{t+1}^{2}-{\tau^{\star}_{t+1}}^{2}\right|}{\eta^{t+1}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\left|\tau_{1}^{2}-{\tau^{\star}_{1}}^{2}\right|}{\eta}+\sum_{s=1}^{t}\frac{\left|{\tau^{\star}_{s}}^{2}-{\tau^{\star}_{s+1}}^{2}\right|}{\eta^{s}}

(since
{\tau^{\star}}(\lambda)
is
L_{\tau}
-Lipschitz )

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\left|\tau_{1}^{2}-{\tau^{\star}_{1}}^{2}\right|}{\eta}+2\tau_{\max}^{\star}L_{\tau}\sum_{s=1}^{[t/2]}\frac{\left|{\lambda_{s}}-{\lambda_{s+1}}\right|}{\eta^{s}}+2\tau_{\max}^{\star}L_{\tau}\sum_{s=1+[t/2]}^{t}\frac{\left|{\lambda_{s}}-{\lambda_{s+1}}\right|}{\eta^{s}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\left|\tau_{1}^{2}-{\tau^{\star}_{1}}^{2}\right|}{\eta}+2\tau_{\max}^{\star}L_{\tau}\frac{1}{\eta^{[t/2]}}\sum_{s=1}^{[t/2]}[\lambda_{s}-\lambda_{s+1}]+2\tau_{\max}^{\star}L_{\tau}\frac{[\lambda_{[t/2]}-\lambda_{[t/2]+1}]}{1-\eta}\eta^{-t}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\left|\tau_{1}^{2}-{\tau^{\star}_{1}}^{2}\right|}{\eta}+2\tau_{\max}^{\star}L_{\tau}\lambda_{1}\frac{1}{\eta^{[t/2]}}+2\tau_{\max}^{\star}L_{\tau}\frac{[\lambda_{[t/2]}-\lambda_{[t/2]+1}]}{1-\eta}\eta^{-t}.

Re-arranging the above expression, we are left with

\displaystyle\left|\tau_{t}^{2}-{\tau^{\star}_{t}}^{2}\right|\leq\frac{\left|\tau_{1}^{2}-{\tau^{\star}_{1}}^{2}\right|}{\eta}\eta^{t}+2\tau_{\max}^{\star}L_{\tau}\lambda_{1}\eta^{[t/2]}+\frac{2\tau_{\max}^{\star}L_{\tau}}{1-\eta}[\lambda_{[t/2]}-\lambda_{[t/2]+1}]\rightarrow 0,\quad t\rightarrow\infty.

This completes the proof of Proposition
B.3
.

The proof of this lemma is adapted from BM-Lemma 3.2 with modifications tailored to the minimum

As mentioned previously, Proposition

and
{\tau^{\star}}

Consequently, to prove Lemma

61b

Let us first decompose the minimum
\ell_{1}
-norm interpolator
\widehat{\bm{\theta}}^{\mathsf{Int}}
into the projection onto the row space of
\bm{X}
, and the residual. Formally, we write

\displaystyle\widehat{\bm{\theta}}^{\mathsf{Int}}=\widehat{\bm{\theta}}^{\mathsf{Int}}_{\perp}+\widehat{\bm{\theta}}^{\mathsf{Int}}_{||},

where
\widehat{\bm{\theta}}^{\mathsf{Int}}_{||}=\bm{X}^{\top}(\bm{XX}^{\top})^{-1}\bm{X}\widehat{\bm{\theta}}^{\mathsf{Int}}

It is straightforward to verify that
\bm{X}\widehat{\bm{\theta}}^{\mathsf{Int}}_{||}=\bm{X}\widehat{\bm{\theta}}^{\mathsf{Int}}=\bm{y}

\widehat{\bm{\theta}}^{\mathsf{Int}}_{\perp}

(p-n)

\delta

c_{2}
depending only on
\delta
such that
\|\widehat{\bm{\theta}}^{\mathsf{Int}}_{||}\|_{2}^{2}\leq c_{2}\|\bm{X}\widehat{\bm{\theta}}^{\mathsf{Int}}_{||}\|_{2}^{2}

(see BM-Lemma F.2). Therefore, we arrive at

\displaystyle\lim_{p\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{p}\|\widehat{\bm{\theta}}^{\mathsf{Int}}\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=\lim_{p\rightarrow\infty}\left[\frac{1}{p}\|\widehat{\bm{\theta}}^{\mathsf{Int}}_{\perp}\|_{2}^{2}+\frac{1}{p}\|\widehat{\bm{\theta}}^{\mathsf{Int}}_{||}\|_{2}^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\lim_{p\rightarrow\infty}\left[c_{1}\frac{\|\widehat{\bm{\theta}}^{\mathsf{Int}}_{\perp}\|_{1}^{2}}{p^{2}}+\frac{\|\widehat{\bm{\theta}}^{\mathsf{Int}}_{||}\|_{2}^{2}}{p}\right]

\displaystyle(\text{by Cauchy-Schwarz})

\displaystyle\leq\lim_{p\rightarrow\infty}\left[2c_{1}\left(\frac{\|\widehat{\bm{\theta}}^{\mathsf{Int}}\|_{1}^{2}}{p^{2}}+\frac{\|\widehat{\bm{\theta}}^{\mathsf{Int}}_{||}\|_{2}^{2}}{p}\right)+\frac{\|\widehat{\bm{\theta}}^{\mathsf{Int}}_{||}\|_{2}^{2}}{p}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\lim_{p\rightarrow\infty}\left[2c_{1}\frac{\|\widehat{\bm{\theta}}^{\mathsf{Int}}\|_{1}^{2}}{p^{2}}+(2c_{1}+1)c_{2}\frac{\|\bm{y}\|_{2}^{2}}{p}\right],

(70)

where the last step uses
\bm{X}\widehat{\bm{\theta}}^{\mathsf{Int}}_{||}=\bm{X}\widehat{\bm{\theta}}^{\mathsf{Int}}=\bm{y}
.

\|\widehat{\bm{\theta}}^{\mathsf{Int}}\|_{1}/p
and
\|\bm{y}\|_{2}^{2}/p

First, by our model assumption,
y_{i}\overset{\mathrm{i.i.d.}}{\sim}\mathcal{N}(0,\|\bm{\theta}^{\star}\|_{2}^{2}/n+\sigma^{2})
; it immediately follows that
\lim_{p\rightarrow\infty}\|\bm{y}\|_{2}^{2}/p
is bounded.
In addition, we note that
\bm{a}=\bm{X}^{\top}(\bm{XX}^{\top})^{-1}\bm{y}
satisfies the condition
\bm{Xa}=\bm{y}
.
Since
\widehat{\bm{\theta}}^{\mathsf{Int}}
has the minimum
\ell_{1}
-norm over all linear interpolators, we can guarantee that

\displaystyle\|\widehat{\bm{\theta}}^{\mathsf{Int}}\|_{1}\leq\|\bm{X}^{\top}(\bm{XX}^{\top})^{-1}\bm{y}\|_{1}.

Finally, by virtue of BM-Lemma F.2, we obtain
\sigma_{\max}((\bm{XX}^{\top})^{-1})\leq c_{3}
for some

depending on
\delta

\displaystyle\left(\frac{\|\widehat{\bm{\theta}}^{\mathsf{Int}}\|_{1}}{p}\right)^{2}\leq\frac{1}{p}\|\bm{X}^{\top}(\bm{XX}^{\top})^{-1}\bm{y}\|_{2}^{2}\leq\frac{\bm{y}^{\top}(\bm{XX}^{\top})^{-1}\bm{y}}{p}\leq 2\frac{\|\bm{\theta}^{\star}\|_{2}^{2}}{p}+2c_{3}\frac{\|\bm{z}\|_{2}^{2}}{p}.

Combining this with the expression (

Proof of Lemma
C.7

In view of the proof for BM-Lemma 4.3 — a general result proved for any positive thresholding sequence

\displaystyle\lim_{p\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{p\lambda_{t}^{2}}\|\bm{z}^{t}-\bm{z}^{t-1}\|_{2}^{2}~\overset{\mathrm{a.s.}}{=}~\lim_{p\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{p\lambda_{t}^{2}}\|\bm{\theta}^{t}-\bm{\theta}^{t-1}\|_{2}^{2}.

It is thus sufficient to prove Lemma

As a consequence of the generalized state evolution formula (cf. Lemma

\displaystyle\lim_{p\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{p\lambda_{t}^{2}}\|\bm{\theta}^{t}-\bm{\theta}^{t-1}\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2(\zeta_{t}-\zeta_{t-1})^{2}}{\lambda_{t}^{2}}+\frac{2\mathbb{E}[(Z_{t}-Z_{t-1})^{2}]}{\lambda_{t}^{2}}.

•

The term
\mathbb{E}[(Z_{t}-Z_{t-1})^{2}]
can be controlled via Lemma
C.9
, where

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[(Z_{t}-Z_{t-1})^{2}]=\mathsf{R}_{t,t}-2\mathsf{R}_{t-1,t}+\mathsf{R}_{t-1,t-1}\leq c_{0}\exp\left\{-c\Lambda_{t}\right\}+4L\tau^{\star}_{\max}l_{t}.

1

1

\displaystyle\frac{2\mathbb{E}[(Z_{t}-Z_{t-1})^{2}]}{\lambda_{t}^{2}}\rightarrow 0.

•

It remains to show that the term

that
0\leq{{\alpha^{\star}}}^{\prime}(0)\leq C

\displaystyle\left|\frac{\zeta_{t}-\zeta_{t-1}}{\lambda_{t}}\right|\leq{\alpha^{\star}_{t}}\left|\frac{\tau_{t}-\tau_{t-1}}{\lambda_{t}}\right|+\tau_{t-1}\left|\frac{{\alpha^{\star}_{t}}-\alpha^{\star}_{t-1}}{\lambda_{t}-\lambda_{t-1}}\right|\frac{\lambda_{t}-\lambda_{t-1}}{\lambda_{t}}\rightarrow{\alpha^{\star}}\left|\frac{\tau_{t}-\tau_{t-1}}{\lambda_{t}}\right|+{\tau^{\star}}{{\alpha^{\star}}}^{\prime}(0)\frac{\lambda_{t}-\lambda_{t-1}}{\lambda_{t}}\rightarrow 0,

where the limit value

holds by virtue of Assumption

Putting all this together completes the proof.

C.4.4
Proof of Lemma
C.10

The main idea of this proof is to decompose

\mathsf{R}_{t,t+1}-{\tau^{\star}}^{2}
or
\mathsf{R}_{t,t}-{\tau^{\star}}^{2}

Without loss of generality, we assume

The proof of BM-Theorem 4.2 (which is a general result for any positive

\displaystyle\bm{h}^{t+1}=\bm{\theta}^{\star}-(\bm{X}^{\top}\bm{z}^{t}+\bm{\theta}^{t}),

\displaystyle\left|\mathsf{R}_{t_{1},t_{1}}-2\mathsf{R}_{t_{1},t_{2}}+\mathsf{R}_{t_{2},t_{2}}\right|=\mathbb{E}[(Z_{t_{1}}-Z_{t_{2}})^{2}]

77

The proof of Lemma
C.10

C.4.6
Proof of the three claims

In the sequel, we look at these three claims separately.

Proof of Claim 1

The iteration updates (
78a
) and (
78b
), along with the initial condition, yield that
y_{t+1,1}=y_{t,2}=\tau_{t}
,
\forall t\geq 1
. Taking the results in Proposition

collectively with
\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\tau_{t}={\tau^{\star}}
and
\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}{\tau^{\star}_{t}}={\tau^{\star}}

The second part of the claim is proved by induction; for the proof to go through, let us verify the initial condition and the induction step respectively.

•

For the initial condition, we can write

\displaystyle y_{1,1}+y_{1,2}-y_{1,3}=2\sigma^{2}+\frac{1}{\delta}\mathbb{E}\left\{\Theta\left(\Theta-\eta(\Theta+Z_{0};\zeta_{0})\right)\right\}.

It is easy to check that the function

is monotonically increasing for any
Z_{0}
and
\zeta_{0}
; therefore, the random variables
\Theta
and
\Theta-\eta(\Theta+Z_{0};\zeta_{0})
are positively correlated. In other words,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\delta}\mathbb{E}\left\{\Theta\left(\Theta-\eta(\Theta+Z_{0};\zeta_{0})\right)\right\}\geq 0\quad\Longrightarrow\quad y_{1,1}+y_{1,2}-y_{1,3}\geq 2\sigma^{2}.

•

Suppose
y_{t,3}<y_{t,1}+y_{t,2}-\sigma^{2}
holds for all steps up to
t
.
At step
t+1
, the iteration formula (
78
) directly leads to

\displaystyle y_{t+1,1}+y_{t+1,2}-y_{t+1,3}=2\sigma^{2}+\frac{2}{\delta}\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\eta(\Theta+Z_{t};{\alpha^{\star}_{t}}\sqrt{y_{t,2}})-\Theta\right)\left(\eta(\Theta+Z_{t-1};\alpha_{t-1}^{\star}\sqrt{y_{t,1}})-\Theta\right)\right],

where
(Z_{t},Z_{t-1})
are jointly Gaussian with
\mathbb{E}[Z_{t}^{2}]=y_{t,2}
,
\mathbb{E}[Z_{t-1}^{2}]=y_{t,1}
and
\mathbb{E}[(Z_{t}-Z_{t-1})^{2}]=y_{t,3}
.
By definition of
Z_{t}
and
Z_{t-1}
,
one has
\mathbb{E}[Z_{t}Z_{t-1}]=(y_{t,1}+y_{t,2}-y_{t,3})/2

which stays positive given the induction assumption.

Now since the mapping
x\mapsto\eta(x+\theta;\zeta)-\theta
is monotone in
x
, we can readily conclude that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\eta(\Theta+Z_{t};{\alpha^{\star}_{t}}\sqrt{y_{t,2}})-\Theta\right)\left(\eta(\Theta+Z_{t-1};\alpha_{t-1}^{\star}\sqrt{y_{t,1}})-\Theta\right)\right]\geq 0,

which further leads to

y_{t+1,1}+y_{t+1,2}-y_{t+1,3}>2\sigma^{2}
.
We have thus proved that
y_{t+1,3}<y_{t+1,1}+y_{t+1,2}-\sigma^{2}
at
t+1
.

Combining the initial condition with the induction argument, we conclude that
y_{t,3}<y_{t,1}+y_{t,2}-\sigma^{2}
for all

.

Proof of Claim 2(a)

To establish the second claim, with a slight abuse of notation, we define

\displaystyle G_{3}(y_{1},y_{2},y_{3};\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2})\coloneqq\frac{1}{\delta}\mathbb{E}\left\{\left[\eta(\Theta+Z_{1};\alpha_{1}\sqrt{y_{1}})-\eta(\Theta+Z_{2};\alpha_{2}\sqrt{y_{2}})\right]^{2}\right\},

(81)

where
(Z_{1},Z_{2})

\mathbb{E}[Z_{1}^{2}]=y_{1}
,
\mathbb{E}[Z_{2}^{2}]=y_{2}
and
\mathbb{E}[(Z_{1}-Z_{2})^{2}]=y_{3}

We remark that the dependence on
y_{3}
is only through the covariance between
Z_{1}
and
Z_{2}
.
From the definition of
\widetilde{y}_{t+1,3}
, we immediately have

\displaystyle\widetilde{y}_{t+1,3}=G_{3}({\tau^{\star}_{t}}^{2},{\tau^{\star}_{t}}^{2},\widetilde{y}_{t,3},{\alpha^{\star}_{t}},\alpha^{\star}_{t-1}).

It is straightforward to verify that
G_{3}
is continuous in all the parameters under our assumption that
\mathbb{E}[\Theta^{2}]<\infty
. The continuity of
G_{3}
, together with the fact that
{{\alpha^{\star}_{t}}}^{2}
converges,
implies that
\widetilde{y}_{t+1,3}-G_{3}({{\tau^{\star}_{t}}}^{2},{{\tau^{\star}_{t}}}^{2},\widetilde{y}_{t,3};{\alpha^{\star}_{t}},{\alpha^{\star}_{t}})\rightarrow 0
, for any fixed
t\geq 0
.

With this notation in mind, we are ready to prove that
\widetilde{y}_{t+1,3}<2(\tau_{t+1}^{\star})^{2}
for all sufficiently large
t
via an inductive argument.
First, suppose
\widetilde{y}_{i,3}<2(\tau_{i}^{\star})^{2}
holds for all steps between
t_{0}
up to
t
.
In view of relation
{{\tau^{\star}_{t}}}^{2}=\sigma^{2}+\frac{1}{\delta}\mathbb{E}\left\{\left[\eta(\Theta+Z_{t};{\alpha^{\star}_{t}}{\tau^{\star}_{t}})-\Theta\right]^{2}\right\}
, we arrive at

\displaystyle 2{{\tau^{\star}_{t}}}^{2}-G_{3}({{\tau^{\star}_{t}}}^{2},{{\tau^{\star}_{t}}}^{2},\widetilde{y}_{t,3};{\alpha^{\star}_{t}},{\alpha^{\star}_{t}})=2\sigma^{2}+\frac{2}{\delta}\mathbb{E}\left\{\left(\eta(\Theta+Z_{1};{\alpha^{\star}_{t}}{\tau^{\star}_{t}})-\Theta\right)\left(\eta(\Theta+Z_{2};{\alpha^{\star}_{t}}{\tau^{\star}_{t}})-\Theta\right)\right\},

where again
(Z_{1},Z_{2})
are jointly Gaussian with zero mean and
\mathbb{E}[Z_{1}^{2}]={\tau^{\star}_{t}}^{2}
,
\mathbb{E}[Z_{2}^{2}]={\tau^{\star}_{t}}^{2}
and
\mathbb{E}[Z_{1}Z_{2}]=2{{\tau^{\star}_{t}}}^{2}-\widetilde{y}_{t,3}
.
Under the induction assumption
\widetilde{y}_{t,3}\leq 2{{\tau^{\star}_{t}}}^{2}
, the two random variables
\eta(\Theta+Z_{1};{\alpha^{\star}_{t}}{\tau^{\star}_{t}})-\Theta
and
\eta(\Theta+Z_{2};{\alpha^{\star}_{t}}{\tau^{\star}_{t}})-\Theta
are positively or zero correlated. Then it is guaranteed that

\displaystyle\frac{2}{\delta}\mathbb{E}\left\{\left(\eta(\Theta+Z_{1};{\alpha^{\star}_{t}}{\tau^{\star}_{t}})-\Theta\right)\left(\eta(\Theta+Z_{2};{\alpha^{\star}_{t}}{\tau^{\star}_{t}})-\Theta\right)\right\}\geq 0\quad\Longrightarrow\quad 2{{\tau^{\star}_{t}}}^{2}-G_{3}({{\tau^{\star}_{t}}}^{2},{{\tau^{\star}_{t}}}^{2},\widetilde{y}_{t,3};{\alpha^{\star}_{t}},{\alpha^{\star}_{t}})\geq 2\sigma^{2}.

(82)

Now to prove the upper bound for
\widetilde{y}_{t+1,3}
, it is sufficient for us to control the difference between

G_{3}({{\tau^{\star}_{t}}}^{2},{{\tau^{\star}_{t}}}^{2},\widetilde{y}_{t,3};{\alpha^{\star}_{t}},{\alpha^{\star}_{t}})
and
\widetilde{y}_{t+1,3}
.
Towards this end, let us invoke the Lipschitz property for soft-thresholding function

\displaystyle\left|\eta(\Theta+Z_{1};{\alpha^{\star}_{t}}{\tau^{\star}_{t}})-\eta(\Theta+Z_{1};\alpha^{\star}_{t-1}{\tau^{\star}_{t}})\right|\leq{\tau^{\star}_{t}}|{\alpha^{\star}_{t}}-\alpha_{t-1}^{\star}|,

which leads to

\displaystyle|G_{3}({{\tau^{\star}_{t}}}^{2},{{\tau^{\star}_{t}}}^{2},\widetilde{y}_{t,3};{\alpha^{\star}_{t}},{\alpha^{\star}_{t}})-\widetilde{y}_{t+1,3}|

(83)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle|G_{3}({{\tau^{\star}_{t}}}^{2},{{\tau^{\star}_{t}}}^{2},\widetilde{y}_{t,3};{\alpha^{\star}_{t}},{\alpha^{\star}_{t}})-G_{3}({{\tau^{\star}_{t}}}^{2},{{\tau^{\star}_{t}}}^{2},\widetilde{y}_{t,3};\alpha_{t-1}^{\star},{\alpha^{\star}_{t}})|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\delta}\mathbb{E}\left|\left[\eta(\Theta+Z_{1};{\alpha^{\star}_{t}}{\tau^{\star}_{t}})-\eta(\Theta+Z_{1};\alpha^{\star}_{t-1}{\tau^{\star}_{t}})\right]\left[\eta(\Theta+Z_{1};{\alpha^{\star}_{t}}{\tau^{\star}_{t}})+\eta(\Theta+Z_{1};\alpha^{\star}_{t-1}{\tau^{\star}_{t}})-2\eta(\Theta+Z_{2};{\alpha^{\star}_{t}}{\tau^{\star}_{t}})\right]\right|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{{\tau^{\star}_{t}}|{\alpha^{\star}_{t}}-\alpha_{t-1}^{\star}|}{\delta}\mathbb{E}\left|\eta(\Theta+Z_{1};{\alpha^{\star}_{t}}{\tau^{\star}_{t}})+\eta(\Theta+Z_{1};\alpha^{\star}_{t-1}{\tau^{\star}_{t}})-2\eta(\Theta+Z_{2};{\alpha^{\star}_{t}}{\tau^{\star}_{t}})\right|

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\mathrm{i})}}{{\leq}}

\displaystyle\frac{{\tau^{\star}_{t}}|{\alpha^{\star}_{t}}-\alpha_{t-1}^{\star}|}{\delta}\cdot 4(\mathbb{E}[|\Theta|]+\tau^{\star}_{\max})\leq\frac{4(\mathbb{E}[|\Theta|]+\tau^{\star}_{\max})\tau^{\star}_{\max}}{\delta}|{\alpha^{\star}_{t}}-\alpha^{\star}_{t-1}|.

Here, the inequality
(\mathrm{i})
follows since

\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\eta(\Theta+Z_{i};{\alpha^{\star}_{t}}\tau^{\star}_{s})\right|\right]\leq\mathbb{E}\left[|\Theta+Z_{1}|\right]\leq\mathbb{E}[|\Theta|]+\tau^{\star}_{\max}
with
i\in\left\{1,2\right\}
and
s\in\left\{t,t-1\right\}
.

Combining the inequalities (
82
) and (
83
) yields

\displaystyle\widetilde{y}_{t+1,3}\leq 2{{\tau_{t+1}^{\star}}}^{2}+\frac{4(\mathbb{E}[|\Theta|]+\tau^{\star}_{\max})\tau^{\star}_{\max}}{\delta}|{\alpha^{\star}_{t}}-\alpha_{t-1}^{\star}|+2({\tau^{\star}_{t}}^{2}-{\tau^{\star}_{t+1}}^{2})-2\sigma^{2}.

As
t\rightarrow\infty
, we know from Proposition
B.3
that
|{\alpha^{\star}_{t}}-\alpha_{t-1}^{\star}|\rightarrow 0
, as well as
|{\tau^{\star}_{t}}^{2}-{\tau^{\star}_{t+1}}^{2}|\rightarrow 0
; as such, we can always find some
T_{\min}
determined by
\Theta
and
\sigma^{2}
, such that the
\frac{4(\mathbb{E}[|\Theta|]+\tau^{\star}_{\max})\tau^{\star}_{\max}}{\delta}|{\alpha^{\star}_{t}}-\alpha_{t-1}^{\star}|+2|{\tau^{\star}_{t}}^{2}-{\tau^{\star}_{t+1}}^{2}|-2\sigma^{2}<0
.
In this case, we conclude that
\widetilde{y}_{t+1,3}<2{{\tau_{t+1}^{\star}}}^{2}
, and then by induction,
\widetilde{y}_{t,3}<2{{\tau_{t+1}^{\star}}}^{2}
,
\forall t\geq t_{0}
.
This completes the proof of Claim 2(a).

Proof of Claim 2(b)

It turns out that the function
G_{3}({{\tau^{\star}_{t}}}^{2},{\tau_{t}^{\star}}^{2},\widetilde{y}_{t,3};{\alpha^{\star}_{t}},{\alpha^{\star}_{t}})
is the same as the
\mathsf{G}_{*}
function defined in BM-Pg 36.
We record here two key observations from BM-Pg 36 regarding this function.

•

The derivative of function
G_{3}
with respect to its third argument satisfies

\displaystyle\left.\frac{\partial}{\partial x}G_{3}({{\tau^{\star}_{t}}}^{2},{\tau_{t}^{\star}}^{2},x;{\alpha^{\star}_{t}},{\alpha^{\star}_{t}})\right|_{x=0}=\frac{1}{\delta}\mathbb{E}\left\{\eta^{\prime}(\Theta+{\tau^{\star}_{t}}Z;{\alpha^{\star}_{t}}{\tau^{\star}_{t}})\right\}=1-\frac{\lambda_{t}}{{\alpha^{\star}_{t}}{\tau^{\star}_{t}}};

(84)

•

The mapping
x\mapsto\frac{\partial}{\partial x}G_{3}({{\tau^{\star}_{t}}}^{2},{\tau_{t}^{\star}}^{2},x;{\alpha^{\star}_{t}},{\alpha^{\star}_{t}})
is decreasing in
[0,2{\tau^{\star}_{t}}^{2})
.

In addition, we claim that
G_{3}({{\tau^{\star}_{t}}}^{2},{\tau_{t}^{\star}}^{2},0;{\alpha^{\star}_{t}},{\alpha^{\star}_{t}})=0
. In order to see this, by construction in (
81
), we have

\displaystyle G_{3}({{\tau^{\star}_{t}}}^{2},{\tau^{\star}_{t}}^{2},0;{\alpha^{\star}_{t}},{\alpha^{\star}_{t}})=\frac{1}{\delta}\mathbb{E}\left\{\left[\eta(\Theta+Z_{1};{\alpha^{\star}_{t}}{\tau^{\star}_{t}})-\eta(\Theta+Z_{2};{\alpha^{\star}_{t}}{\tau^{\star}_{t}})\right]^{2}\right\},

where
\mathbb{E}[Z_{1}^{2}]=\mathbb{E}[Z_{2}^{2}]={\tau^{\star}_{t}}^{2}
and
\mathbb{E}[(Z_{1}-Z_{2})^{2}]=0
.
From this construction, we know
Z_{1}=Z_{2}
, almost surely; it follows that
\eta(\Theta+Z_{1};\alpha_{1}{\tau^{\star}_{t}})=\eta(\Theta+Z_{1};\alpha_{1}{\tau^{\star}_{t}})
, almost surely. We therefore obtain
G_{3}({{\tau^{\star}_{t}}}^{2},{\tau_{t}^{\star}}^{2},0;{\alpha^{\star}_{t}},{\alpha^{\star}_{t}})=0
.

Taking these observations collectively
guarantees that: under the condition
\widetilde{y}_{t,3}<2{\tau_{t}^{\star}}^{2}
, one has

\displaystyle G_{3}({{\tau^{\star}_{t}}}^{2},{\tau_{t}^{\star}}^{2},\widetilde{y}_{t,3};{\alpha^{\star}_{t}},{\alpha^{\star}_{t}})\leq(1-\lambda_{t}/{\alpha^{\star}_{t}}{\tau^{\star}_{t}})\widetilde{y}_{t,3}\leq(1-(\alpha_{\max}^{\star}\tau_{\max}^{\star})^{-1}\lambda_{t})\widetilde{y}_{t,3}.

In view of the inequality (
83
), we have

|G_{3}({{\tau^{\star}_{t}}}^{2},{{\tau^{\star}_{t}}}^{2},\widetilde{y}_{t,3};{\alpha^{\star}_{t}},{\alpha^{\star}_{t}})-\widetilde{y}_{t+1,3}|\leq C|{\alpha^{\star}_{t}}-\alpha_{t-1}^{\star}|,

where
C
is determined by
\Theta
and
\sigma^{2}
. Putting these pieces together leads to

\displaystyle\widetilde{y}_{t+1,3}\leq(1-(\alpha_{\max}^{\star}\tau_{\max}^{\star})^{-1}\lambda_{t})\widetilde{y}_{t,3}+C|\alpha_{t+1}^{\star}-{\alpha^{\star}_{t}}|.

(85)

Invoking the above relation recursively, we obtain, for
c_{1}\coloneqq(\alpha_{\max}^{\star}\tau_{\max}^{\star})^{-1}
, that

\displaystyle\widetilde{y}_{t+1,3}

\displaystyle\leq\prod_{i=0}^{t-t_{0}}(1-c_{1}\lambda_{t-i})\widetilde{y}_{t_{0}}+C\sum_{i=0}^{t-t_{0}}\prod_{j=1}^{i}(1-c_{1}\lambda_{t+1-j})|\alpha^{\star}_{t+1-i}-\alpha^{\star}_{t-i}|

\displaystyle\leq e^{-c_{1}\sum_{i=t_{0}}^{t}\lambda_{i}}\widetilde{y}_{t_{0}}+C\sum_{i=t_{0}}^{t}e^{-c_{1}\sum_{j=i+1}^{t}\lambda_{j}}|\alpha^{\star}_{i+1}-\alpha^{\star}_{i}|

\displaystyle=e^{-c_{1}\sum_{i=t_{0}}^{t}\lambda_{i}}\widetilde{y}_{t_{0}}+C\sum_{i=t_{0}}^{t/2}e^{-c_{1}\sum_{j=i+1}^{t}\lambda_{j}}|\alpha^{\star}_{i+1}-\alpha^{\star}_{i}|+C\sum_{i=t/2}^{t}e^{-c_{1}\sum_{j=i+1}^{t}\lambda_{j}}|\alpha^{\star}_{i+1}-\alpha^{\star}_{i}|.

(86)

Observing that
\widetilde{y}_{t_{0},3}<2{\tau^{\star}_{t_{0}}}^{2}
and the sequence

\sum_{i=t_{0}}^{t}\lambda_{i}
diverges to infinity, we know that the first term on the right-hand side of (
86
) converges to zero as
t
increases.
Moreover, as proved in Proposition
B.2
, the sequence
\left\{{\alpha^{\star}_{t}}\right\}
is monotone when
t
is sufficiently large, and we then see that
\sum_{t=t/2}^{+\infty}|\alpha_{t+1}^{\star}-{\alpha^{\star}_{t}}|
converges to zero.
In the meantime,
e^{-c_{1}\sum_{j=i+1}^{t}\lambda_{j}}\leq e^{-c_{1}\lambda_{t}}\leq e^{-c_{1}\max_{t}\lambda_{t}}
is always controlled by some constant.
As a result, the third term on the right-hand side of (
86
) also vanishes.
It remains to control the second term on the right-hand side of (
86
).
To this end, we make the observation that
|\alpha^{\star}_{i+1}-\alpha^{\star}_{i}|\leq 2\alpha_{\max}^{\star}
and

\displaystyle\sum_{i=t_{0}}^{t/2}e^{-c_{1}\sum_{j=i+1}^{t}\lambda_{j}}\leq te^{-c_{1}\sum_{j=t/2}^{t}\lambda_{j}}=e^{(\log t-c_{1}\sum_{j=t/2}^{t}\lambda_{j})}\to 0,

where the last inequality follows from Assumption
1
. Thus, the second term on the right-hand side of (
86
) also has a zero limit.
Taking these collectively, we establish the advertised result in Claim 2(b).

Proof of Claim 3.

To begin with, by some direct algebra, we can express the Jacobian matrix of mapping
\mathsf{G}^{t}
at
\bm{y}_{\star}=({\tau_{t}^{\star}}^{2},{\tau_{t}^{\star}}^{2},0)
by

\displaystyle J_{\mathsf{G}^{t}}(\bm{y}_{\star})=\begin{pmatrix}0&1&0\\
0&\left.\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\tau^{2}}\mathsf{F}(\tau^{2},{\alpha^{\star}_{t}}\tau)\right|_{{\tau_{t}^{\star}}^{2}}&0\\
\cdots&\cdots&\left.\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}y_{3}}\mathsf{G}_{3}^{t}({\tau_{t}^{\star}}^{2},{\tau_{t}^{\star}}^{2},y_{3})\right|_{y_{3}=0}\end{pmatrix}.

It is easily seen that the maximal eigenvalues of the above matrix satisfies the following relation

\displaystyle\sigma(J_{\mathsf{G}^{t}}(\bm{y}_{\star}))=\max\left\{\left.\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\tau^{2}}\mathsf{F}(\tau^{2},{\alpha^{\star}_{t}}\tau)\right|_{{\tau_{t}^{\star}}^{2}},\left.\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}y_{3}}\mathsf{G}_{3}^{t}({\tau_{t}^{\star}}^{2},{\tau_{t}^{\star}}^{2},y_{3})\right|_{y_{3}=0}\right\}.

(87)

To control
\sigma(J_{\mathsf{G}^{t}}(\bm{y}_{\star}))
, it is sufficient for us to bound each term respectively.
For the first term, from the proof of BM-Proposition 1.3, we know that the function
\tau^{2}\mapsto\mathsf{F}(\tau^{2},\alpha\tau)
is concave, for any
\alpha>0
and
\Theta
that is not identically
0
; therefore, we obtain

\displaystyle\left.\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\tau^{2}}\mathsf{F}(\tau^{2},{\alpha^{\star}_{t}}\tau)\right|_{{\tau_{t}^{\star}}^{2}}\leq\frac{\mathsf{F}({\tau^{\star}_{t}}^{2},{\alpha^{\star}_{t}}{\tau^{\star}_{t}})-\mathsf{F}(0,0)}{{\tau^{\star}_{t}}^{2}-0}=\frac{{\tau^{\star}_{t}}^{2}-\sigma^{2}}{{\tau^{\star}_{t}}^{2}}\leq 1-\frac{\sigma^{2}}{{\tau^{\star}_{\max}}^{2}}\coloneqq\eta.

For the second term, with the function
G_{3}
defined in expression (
81
), we write

\displaystyle\left.\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}y_{3}}\mathsf{G}_{3}^{t}({\tau_{t}^{\star}}^{2},{\tau_{t}^{\star}}^{2},y_{3})\right|_{y_{3}=0}

\displaystyle=\left.\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}x}G_{3}({\tau_{t}^{\star}}^{2},{\tau_{t}^{\star}}^{2},x;\alpha_{t-1}^{\star},{\alpha^{\star}_{t}})\right|_{x=0}

\displaystyle=\left.\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}x}\mathbb{E}\left[\eta(\Theta+a(x)Z+b(x)W;{\alpha^{\star}_{t}}{\tau_{t}^{\star}})\eta(\Theta+{\tau_{t}^{\star}}Z;\alpha_{t-1}^{\star}{\tau_{t}^{\star}})\right]\right|_{x=0}.

Here
Z
and
W
are independent standard Gaussian random variables and we denote

\displaystyle a(x)\coloneqq{\tau_{t}^{\star}}-x/(2{\tau_{t}^{\star}})\qquad\text{and }~b(x)\coloneqq\sqrt{x-x^{2}/(4{\tau_{t}^{\star}}^{2})}.

(88)

Further define

\displaystyle g(\Theta,Z,W;x)\coloneqq\eta(\Theta+a(x)Z+b(x)W;{\alpha^{\star}_{t}}{\tau_{t}^{\star}})\Big[\eta(\Theta+{\tau_{t}^{\star}}Z;\alpha_{t-1}^{\star}{\tau^{\star}})-\eta(\Theta+{\tau^{\star}}Z;{\alpha^{\star}_{t}}\tau^{\star}_{t})\Big].

(89)

With this notation in place, we can write

\displaystyle\left.\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}y_{3}}\mathsf{G}_{3}^{t}({\tau^{\star}_{t}}^{2},{\tau^{\star}_{t}}^{2},y_{3})\right|_{y_{3}=0}

\displaystyle=\left.\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}y_{3}}G_{3}({\tau^{\star}_{t}}^{2},{\tau^{\star}_{t}}^{2},x;{\alpha^{\star}_{t}},{\alpha^{\star}_{t}})\right|_{x=0}+\left.\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}x}\mathbb{E}\left\{g(\Theta,Z,W;x)\right\}\right|_{x=0}

(90)

\displaystyle=1-\frac{\lambda_{t}}{{\tau^{\star}_{t}}{\alpha^{\star}_{t}}}+\left.\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}x}\mathbb{E}\left\{g(\Theta,Z,W;x)\right\}\right|_{x=0},

where the last equality follows from the expression (
84
).
To control the spectral radius of the Jacobian, it suffices to control the last term on the right-hand side above.
We claim that it satisfies the following property:

\displaystyle\left.\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}x}\mathbb{E}\left\{g(\Theta,Z,W;x)\right\}\right|_{x=0}\leq 2|{\alpha^{\star}_{t}}-\alpha_{t-1}^{\star}|.

(91)

Taking this claim as given for the moment, we can translate the expression (
90
) into

\displaystyle\left.\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}y_{3}}\mathsf{G}_{3}^{t}({\tau^{\star}_{t}}^{2},{\tau^{\star}_{t}}^{2},y_{3})\right|_{y_{3}=0}\leq 1-\frac{\lambda_{t}}{\alpha^{\star}_{\max}\tau^{\star}_{\max}}+2|{\alpha^{\star}_{t}}-\alpha_{t-1}^{\star}|.

Combining the results of Proposition
B.2
and Assumption
1
gives

\displaystyle|{\alpha^{\star}_{t}}-\alpha_{t-1}^{\star}|\leq L_{\alpha}|\lambda_{t}-\lambda_{t-1}|\leq\frac{\lambda_{t}}{4\alpha^{\star}_{\max}\tau^{\star}_{\max}}

for sufficiently large
t
.
Thus we complete the proof of expression (
79
).
The only thing that is left is to establish the inequality (
91
), which shall be done as follows.

Proof of the inequality (
91
).

Throughout this part, we denote
a=a(x)
and
b=b(x)
for simplicity if there is no confusion.
Direct calculation of the derivative for the expression (
89
) gives

\displaystyle\left.\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}x}\mathbb{E}\left\{g(\Theta,Z,W;x)\right\}\right|_{x=0}=(\mathrm{I})+(\mathrm{II}),

where

\displaystyle(\mathrm{I})\coloneqq\left.\mathbb{E}\left\{-\frac{Z}{2{\tau^{\star}_{t}}}\left[\eta(\Theta+{\tau^{\star}_{t}}Z;\alpha_{t-1}^{\star}{\tau^{\star}_{t}})-\eta(\Theta+{\tau^{\star}_{t}}Z;{\alpha^{\star}_{t}}{\tau^{\star}_{t}})\right]\eta^{\prime}(\Theta+aZ+bW;{\alpha^{\star}_{t}}{\tau^{\star}_{t}})\right\}\right|_{x=0};

\displaystyle(\mathrm{II})\coloneqq\left.\mathbb{E}\left\{-\frac{W}{2b}\left[\eta(\Theta+{\tau^{\star}_{t}}Z;\alpha_{t-1}^{\star}{\tau^{\star}_{t}})-\eta(\Theta+{\tau^{\star}_{t}}Z;{\alpha^{\star}_{t}}{\tau^{\star}_{t}})\right]\eta^{\prime}(\Theta+aZ+bW;{\alpha^{\star}_{t}}{\tau^{\star}_{t}})\right\}\right|_{x=0}.

Next we shall control each term respectively.
For the first term, invoking the Lipschitz property yields
|\eta^{\prime}(x;\zeta)|\leq 1
and
|\eta(x;\zeta_{1})-\eta(x;\zeta_{2})|\leq|\zeta_{1}-\zeta_{2}|
, which further give

\displaystyle(\mathrm{I})\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\frac{Z}{2}\right|\cdot|\alpha_{t-1}^{\star}-{\alpha^{\star}_{t}}|\right]\leq|\alpha_{t-1}^{\star}-{\alpha^{\star}_{t}}|.

(93)

It remains to study the second term (II), for which the main difficulty lies in the fact that
b(x)\rightarrow 0
as

.
It is easy to see that the limit value of
(\mathrm{II})
is equal to the limiting value of
(\mathrm{IIa})+(\mathrm{IIb})
, where

\displaystyle(\mathrm{IIa})

\displaystyle\coloneqq-\frac{1}{2b}\mathbb{E}\left\{W\left[\eta(\Theta+{\tau^{\star}_{t}}Z;\alpha_{t-1}^{\star}{\tau^{\star}_{t}})-\eta(\Theta+{\tau^{\star}_{t}}Z;{\alpha^{\star}_{t}}{\tau^{\star}_{t}})\right]\textbf{1}(\Theta+aZ+bW\geq{\alpha^{\star}_{t}}{\tau^{\star}_{t}})\right\};

\displaystyle(\mathrm{IIb})

\displaystyle\coloneqq-\frac{1}{2b}\mathbb{E}\left\{W\left[\eta(\Theta+{\tau^{\star}_{t}}Z;\alpha_{t-1}^{\star}{\tau^{\star}_{t}})-\eta(\Theta+{\tau^{\star}_{t}}Z;{\alpha^{\star}_{t}}{\tau^{\star}_{t}})\right]\textbf{1}(\Theta+aZ+bW\leq-{\alpha^{\star}_{t}}{\tau^{\star}_{t}})\right\}.

The analysis of the two parts are quite similar, so we only only discuss the first part. Denote

\displaystyle\mu(x;Z,\Theta,W)\coloneqq\left[\eta(\Theta+{\tau^{\star}_{t}}Z;\alpha_{t-1}^{\star}{\tau^{\star}_{t}})-\eta(\Theta+{\tau^{\star}_{t}}Z;{\alpha^{\star}_{t}}{\tau^{\star}_{t}})\right]\textbf{1}(\Theta+a(x)Z+b(x)W\geq{\alpha^{\star}_{t}}{\tau^{\star}_{t}}),

(94)

To establish expression (
91
), it suffices to show that

\displaystyle\lim_{x\rightarrow 0}\left|\frac{1}{2b(x)}\mathbb{E}[W\mu(x;Z,\Theta,W)]\right|\leq\left|{\alpha^{\star}_{t}}-\alpha^{\star}_{t-1}\right|.

(95)

The remaining part of the current section is then devoted to the proof of (
95
).
Towards this, we make the key observation that

\mathbb{E}[\mu(x;Z,\Theta,W)\mid W=w]
is a Lipschitz function in
w.

In order to see this, first notice that the convoluted density of
p_{aZ}*p_{\Theta}
is bounded over all
a\in[{\tau^{\star}_{t}}/2,{\tau^{\star}_{t}}]
.
Indeed, we can calculate the convolution density by

\displaystyle(p_{aZ}*p_{\Theta})(z)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{1}{a\sqrt{2\pi}}\exp\left\{-\frac{(\Theta-z)^{2}}{2a^{2}}\right\}\mathrm{d}P_{\Theta}\leq\frac{1}{a\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}P_{\Theta}=\frac{1}{a\sqrt{2\pi}}\leq\frac{1}{{\tau^{\star}_{t}}},\quad\forall z\in\mathbb{R}.

(96)

Next, for any
x\in(0,{\tau^{\star}_{t}}^{2})
and

, direct calculations yield

\displaystyle\ \Big|\mathbb{E}[\mu(x;Z,\Theta,w_{1})]-\mathbb{E}[\mu(x;Z,\Theta,w_{2})]\Big|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ \Big|\mathbb{E}\big[(\eta(\Theta+{\tau^{\star}_{t}}Z;\alpha_{t-1}^{\star}{\tau^{\star}_{t}})-\eta(\Theta+{\tau^{\star}_{t}}Z;{\alpha^{\star}_{t}}{\tau^{\star}_{t}}))\cdot(\textbf{1}(\Theta+a(x)Z+b(x)w_{1}\geq{\alpha^{\star}_{t}}{\tau^{\star}_{t}})-\textbf{1}(\Theta+a(x)Z+b(x)w_{2}\geq{\alpha^{\star}_{t}}{\tau^{\star}_{t}})\big]\Big|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\ {\tau^{\star}_{t}}|{\alpha^{\star}_{t}}-\alpha^{\star}_{t-1}|\cdot\mathbb{P}\left\{{\alpha^{\star}_{t}}{\tau^{\star}_{t}}-b(x)w_{2}\leq\Theta+a(x)Z\leq{\alpha^{\star}_{t}}{\tau^{\star}_{t}}-b(x)w_{1}\right\}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\ b(x)|{\alpha^{\star}_{t}}-\alpha^{\star}_{t-1}||w_{1}-w_{2}|,

where the last inequality follows from the expression (
96
) and the fact that
a(x)\in[{\tau^{\star}_{t}}/2,{\tau^{\star}}]
when
0<x<{\tau^{\star}_{t}}^{2}
.

Now we proceed to control
\mathbb{E}[W\mu(x;Z,\Theta,W)].

First we can write

\displaystyle\left|\mathbb{E}\left[W\mu(x;Z,\Theta,W)-W\mu(x;Z,\Theta,0)\right]\right|=

\displaystyle\ \mathbb{E}\left\{W\mathbb{E}\left[\mu(x;Z,\Theta,W)-\mu(x;Z,\Theta,0)|W\right]\right\}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\ b(x)|{\alpha^{\star}_{t}}-\alpha^{\star}_{t-1}|\mathbb{E}[W^{2}]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ b(x)|{\alpha^{\star}_{t}}-\alpha^{\star}_{t-1}|.

Additionally, by symmetry, we know that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[W\mu(x;Z,\Theta,0)\right]=0,

since
W\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
is independent from
\mu(x;Z,\Theta,0)
.
Putting everything together, we conclude that

\displaystyle\left|\frac{1}{2b(x)}\mathbb{E}[W\mu(x;Z,\Theta,W)]\right|\leq\frac{1}{2}|{\alpha^{\star}_{t}}-\alpha^{\star}_{t-1}|,

thus concluding the proof of the inequality (
95
).
Similarly, one can control
(\mathrm{IIb})
.
Taking these collectively with (
93
),
we validated the inequality (
91
).

Putting these together completes the proof of Lemma

Finally, the proofs of auxiliary Lemma

D.1.1
Expressions of
F_{1}
and
F_{2}

Let us first express

and
F_{2}

as functions of the density and cumulative density functions of the standard Gaussian distribution (denoted as
\phi(\cdot)
and
\Phi(\cdot)
respectively).
For
F_{1}

F_{1}(\nu,\delta,\alpha)=\epsilon\left[\Phi(-\alpha+\sqrt{\delta}\nu)+\Phi(-\alpha-\sqrt{\delta}\nu)\right]+2(1-\epsilon)\Phi(-\alpha)-\delta.

(97)

When it comes to
F_{2}
, invoking the equality (
124a
) in Lemma
E.1
, we arrive at the following decomposition

\displaystyle F_{2}=F_{21}+\epsilon\delta^{-1}F_{22}+(1-\epsilon)\delta^{-1}F_{23},

(98)

where

\displaystyle F_{21}(\nu,\delta,\alpha)\coloneqq

\displaystyle\frac{\nu^{2}}{M^{2}}-1;

(99)

\displaystyle F_{22}(\nu,\delta,\alpha)\coloneqq

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}(\eta(\sqrt{\delta}\nu+z,\alpha)-\sqrt{\delta}\nu)^{2}\phi(z)\mathrm{d}z

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(\sqrt{\delta}\nu-\alpha)\phi(\alpha+\sqrt{\delta}\nu)+(-\sqrt{\delta}\nu-\alpha)\phi(\alpha-\sqrt{\delta}\nu)

\displaystyle+(\alpha^{2}+1-\delta\nu^{2})\left[\Phi(-\alpha-\sqrt{\delta}\nu)+\Phi(-\alpha+\sqrt{\delta}\nu)\right]+\delta\nu^{2};

\displaystyle F_{23}(\nu,\delta,\alpha)\coloneqq

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\eta^{2}(z,\alpha)\phi(z)\mathrm{d}z=2\left[-\alpha\phi(\alpha)+(\alpha^{2}+1)\Phi(-\alpha)\right].

Direct computation of the partial derivatives yield the following expressions.

Partial derivatives of
F_{1}
.

For
F_{1}
,
we have the following three partial derivatives with respect to
\alpha,\delta
and
\nu
respectively, where

\displaystyle\nabla_{\alpha}F_{1}(\nu,\delta,\alpha)

\displaystyle=-\epsilon\left[\phi(\alpha-\sqrt{\delta}\nu)+\phi(\alpha+\sqrt{\delta}\nu)\right]-2(1-\epsilon)\phi(\alpha);

(100)

\displaystyle\nabla_{\delta}F_{1}(\nu,\delta,\alpha)

\displaystyle=\epsilon\frac{\nu}{2\sqrt{\delta}}\left[\phi(\alpha-\sqrt{\delta}\nu)-\phi(\alpha+\sqrt{\delta}\nu)\right]-1;

\displaystyle\nabla_{\nu}F_{1}(\nu,\delta,\alpha)

\displaystyle=\epsilon\sqrt{\delta}\left[\phi(\alpha-\sqrt{\delta}\nu)-\phi(\alpha+\sqrt{\delta}\nu)\right].

Partial derivatives of
F_{2}
.

With respect to
\alpha
, from the decomposition (
98
), one obtains

\displaystyle\nabla_{\alpha}F_{2}=\epsilon\delta^{-1}\nabla_{\alpha}F_{22}+(1-\epsilon)\delta^{-1}\nabla_{\alpha}F_{23},

(101)

where

\displaystyle\nabla_{\alpha}F_{22}(\nu,\delta,\alpha)=

\displaystyle-2\left[\phi(\alpha+\sqrt{\delta}\nu)+\phi(\alpha-\sqrt{\delta}\nu)\right]+2\alpha\left[\Phi(-\alpha-\sqrt{\delta}\nu)+\Phi(-\alpha+\sqrt{\delta}\nu)\right];

\displaystyle\nabla_{\alpha}F_{23}(\nu,\delta,\alpha)=

\displaystyle-4\left[\phi(\alpha)-\alpha\Phi(-\alpha)\right].

With respect to
\delta
, a little algebra leads to

\displaystyle\nabla_{\delta}F_{2}(\nu,\delta,\alpha)

\displaystyle=-\delta^{-2}\left[\epsilon F_{22}+(1-\epsilon)F_{23}\right]+\epsilon\delta^{-1}\nabla_{\delta}F_{22}

\displaystyle=-\epsilon\delta^{-2}\left[F_{22}-\delta\nabla_{\delta}F_{22}\right]-(1-\epsilon)\delta^{-2}F_{23}.

(102)

We then further evaluate the right-hand side of the above equation. Recognizing that

\nabla_{\delta}F_{22}(\nu,\delta,\alpha)=\nu^{2}\left[\Phi(\alpha-\sqrt{\delta}\nu)-\Phi(-\alpha-\sqrt{\delta}\nu)\right],

we can guarantee that

\displaystyle F_{22}-\delta\nabla_{\delta}F_{22}=-(\alpha-\sqrt{\delta}\nu)\phi(\alpha+\sqrt{\delta}\nu)-(\alpha+\sqrt{\delta}\nu)\phi(\alpha-\sqrt{\delta}\nu)+(\alpha^{2}+1)\left[\Phi(-\alpha-\sqrt{\delta}\nu)+\Phi(-\alpha+\sqrt{\delta}\nu)\right].

Plugging this into the expression (
D.1.1
) yields the final expression of

Finally, with respect to
\nu

\displaystyle\nabla_{\nu}F_{2}(\nu,\delta,\alpha)=2M^{-2}\nu+\epsilon\delta^{-1}\nabla_{\nu}F_{22},

where

\displaystyle\nabla_{\nu}F_{22}(\nu,\delta,\alpha)=2\nu\delta\left[\Phi(\alpha-\sqrt{\delta}\nu)-\Phi(-\alpha-\sqrt{\delta}\nu)\right].

where all the partial derivatives of

and

are evaluated at the point

.

First note that relation (
39
) in Lemma
1
directly comes from Lemma

; Lemma
D.2
combined with a little algebra leads to relation (
38b
).
Therefore, to prove Lemma

Proof of relation (

By virtue of Lemma

Throughout this part, all the partial derivatives of

and

({\nu^{\star}},\delta,{\alpha^{\star}})
unless otherwise noted.

Second-order terms of
\nabla_{\alpha}F_{1}
and
\nabla_{\delta}F_{1}
.

Let us consider
\nabla_{\alpha}F_{1}
and
\nabla_{\delta}F_{1}
.
We first make the observation that

\displaystyle\frac{\nabla_{\alpha}F_{1}+2\phi({\alpha^{\star}})}{\phi({\alpha^{\star}})}

\displaystyle=-\epsilon\left[e^{-\delta{\nu^{\star}}^{2}/2}\left(e^{{\alpha^{\star}}{\sqrt{\delta}\nu^{\star}}}+e^{-{\alpha^{\star}}{\sqrt{\delta}\nu^{\star}}}\right)-2\right]

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\mathrm{i})}}{{\sim}}-\epsilon{\alpha^{\star}}^{2}\delta{\nu^{\star}}^{2}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\mathrm{ii})}}{{\sim}}-2\epsilon\nu_{0}^{2}{\alpha^{\star}}\phi({\alpha^{\star}}),

(106)

where

103

Regarding the relation

\displaystyle e^{{\alpha^{\star}}{\sqrt{\delta}\nu^{\star}}}+e^{-{\alpha^{\star}}{\sqrt{\delta}\nu^{\star}}}=2+\delta{\nu^{\star}}^{2}{\alpha^{\star}}^{2}+o(\delta{\nu^{\star}}^{2}{\alpha^{\star}}^{2}),\quad e^{-\delta{\nu^{\star}}^{2}/2}=1-\delta{\nu^{\star}}^{2}/2+o(\delta{\nu^{\star}}^{2}).

As already shown in Lemma

Here, recall that these limits are taken with respect to

Moreover, direct computation gives

\displaystyle\frac{\phi({\alpha^{\star}}-\sqrt{\delta}{\nu^{\star}})-\phi({\alpha^{\star}}+\sqrt{\delta}{\nu^{\star}})}{\sqrt{\delta}{\alpha^{\star}}{\nu^{\star}}\phi({\alpha^{\star}})}=\exp\left\{-\delta{\nu^{\star}}^{2}/2\right\}\frac{\exp\{\sqrt{\delta}{\alpha^{\star}}{\nu^{\star}}\}-\exp\{-\sqrt{\delta}{\alpha^{\star}}{\nu^{\star}}\}}{\sqrt{\delta}{\alpha^{\star}}{\nu^{\star}}}\sim 2,\quad\delta\rightarrow 0^{+},

Taking this collective with Lemma

,
one arrives at

\displaystyle\nabla_{\delta}F_{1}+1=\epsilon\frac{{\nu^{\star}}}{2\sqrt{\delta}}\left[\phi({\alpha^{\star}}-\sqrt{\delta}{\nu^{\star}})-\phi({\alpha^{\star}}+\sqrt{\delta}{\nu^{\star}})\right]\sim\epsilon\nu_{0}^{2}{\alpha^{\star}}\phi({\alpha^{\star}}).

(108)

Second-order term of
\nabla_{\delta}F_{2}

Moving on to the quantity
\nabla_{\delta}F_{2}

\frac{\nabla_{\delta}F_{2}+({\alpha^{\star}}\phi({\alpha^{\star}}))^{-1}}{({\alpha^{\star}}\phi({\alpha^{\star}}))^{-1}}=\underbrace{\frac{\nabla_{\delta}F_{2}+\delta^{-2}F_{23}}{({\alpha^{\star}}\phi({\alpha^{\star}}))^{-1}}}_{=:\Delta_{1}}-\underbrace{\frac{\delta^{-2}F_{23}-({\alpha^{\star}}\phi({\alpha^{\star}}))^{-1}}{({\alpha^{\star}}\phi({\alpha^{\star}}))^{-1}}}_{=:\Delta_{2}}.

•

Step 1: Bounding the term

Armed with the expressions of

•

\displaystyle=\lim_{{\alpha^{\star}}\rightarrow\infty}\frac{{\alpha^{\star}}^{5}\left[-{\alpha^{\star}}\phi({\alpha^{\star}})+({\alpha^{\star}}^{2}+1)\Phi(-{\alpha^{\star}})\right]-2{\alpha^{\star}}^{2}\phi({\alpha^{\star}})}{2\phi({\alpha^{\star}})}

\displaystyle=\lim_{{\alpha^{\star}}\rightarrow\infty}\frac{(2{\alpha^{\star}}^{2}-7{\alpha^{\star}}^{4})\phi({\alpha^{\star}})+(7{\alpha^{\star}}^{5}+5{\alpha^{\star}}^{3})\Phi(-{\alpha^{\star}})}{-2\phi({\alpha^{\star}})}+2

\displaystyle\epsilon\left[\Phi(-{\alpha^{\star}}+\sqrt{\delta}{\nu^{\star}})+\Phi(-{\alpha^{\star}}-\sqrt{\delta}{\nu^{\star}})\right]+2(1-\epsilon)\Phi(-{\alpha^{\star}})-\delta=0

In this section, we present the proofs of the four relations in Lemma

First claim in (

Recall that

Since the first two terms are non-negative, when

From the second expression, it can be immediately concluded that
\lim_{\delta\rightarrow 0^{+}}{\alpha^{\star}}=\infty
.

Second claim in (

).

In order to study the limiting behavior of
\sqrt{\delta}{\alpha^{\star}}
, we first make the observations that
0<{\nu^{\star}}\leq M
as
M/{\nu^{\star}}={\tau^{\star}}\geq 1
(see (

\displaystyle\Phi(-{\alpha^{\star}}-{\sqrt{\delta}\nu^{\star}})<\Phi(-{\alpha^{\star}})<\Phi(-{\alpha^{\star}}+{\sqrt{\delta}\nu^{\star}}).

Consequently,
F_{1}({\nu^{\star}},\delta,{\alpha^{\star}})
can be upper bounded as

\displaystyle 0=F_{1}({\nu^{\star}},\delta,{\alpha^{\star}})

\displaystyle=\epsilon\left[\Phi(-{\alpha^{\star}}+\sqrt{\delta}{\nu^{\star}})+\Phi(-{\alpha^{\star}}-\sqrt{\delta}{\nu^{\star}})\right]+2(1-\epsilon)\Phi(-{\alpha^{\star}})-\delta

\displaystyle\leq 2\Phi(-{\alpha^{\star}}+{\sqrt{\delta}\nu^{\star}})-\delta,

\displaystyle\Phi(-{\alpha^{\star}}+\sqrt{\delta}{\nu^{\star}})\geq\delta/2.

Apply Lemma
E.1

(\mathrm{ii})
follows from the observations that
{\alpha^{\star}}\rightarrow\infty
and
{\sqrt{\delta}\nu^{\star}}\leq M\sqrt{\delta}\rightarrow 0
as
\delta\rightarrow 0^{+}
.
It therefore reveals that
{\alpha^{\star}}^{2}\delta=o(1)
, due to the fact that
{\alpha^{\star}}\rightarrow\infty

Thus, we complete the proof of the second claim.

Third claim in (

With the limiting values of
{\alpha^{\star}}
and
{\alpha^{\star}}\sqrt{\delta}

To this end, we first recognize the following relation

\displaystyle\Phi(-{\alpha^{\star}}-{\sqrt{\delta}\nu^{\star}})\sim\Phi(-{\alpha^{\star}}+{\sqrt{\delta}\nu^{\star}})\sim\Phi(-{\alpha^{\star}})\sim\frac{\phi({\alpha^{\star}})}{{\alpha^{\star}}},

(116)

when

followed by the property

It immediately follows that

\displaystyle\lim_{\delta\rightarrow 0^{+}}\frac{\delta{\alpha^{\star}}}{\phi({\alpha^{\star}})}

\displaystyle=\lim_{\delta\rightarrow 0^{+}}\frac{\left[\epsilon\Phi(-{\alpha^{\star}}+{\sqrt{\delta}\nu^{\star}})+\epsilon\Phi(-{\alpha^{\star}}-{\sqrt{\delta}\nu^{\star}})+2(1-\epsilon)\Phi(-{\alpha^{\star}})\right]{\alpha^{\star}}}{\phi({\alpha^{\star}})}

\displaystyle=\epsilon+\epsilon+2(1-\epsilon)=2,

We thus finish the proof of the third claim.

).
In this case, when
S_{k}\leq t\leq S_{k+1}-1
, direct calculations yield

\displaystyle l_{t}=\sum_{s=1}^{t}|\lambda_{s}-\lambda_{s+1}|\exp\left\{-c[\Lambda_{t}-\Lambda_{s}]\right\}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{k}|\mu_{j}-\mu_{j+1}|\exp\left\{-c[\Lambda_{t}-\Lambda_{S_{j}}]\right\}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j=1}^{k}|\mu_{j}-\mu_{j+1}|\exp\left\{-ck^{3}+cj^{3}\right\}\exp\left\{-c(t-S_{k})\mu_{k}\right\}.

Further, it is easy to verify that
|\mu_{j}-\mu_{j+1}|\sim 1/(j\log^{2}j)
, and as a result,

\displaystyle\sqrt{l_{t}}\lesssim\exp\left\{-\frac{c\mu_{k}(t-S_{k})}{2}\right\}\exp\left\{-\frac{ck^{3}}{2}\right\}\sqrt{\sum_{j=1}^{k}\frac{1}{j\log^{2}j}\exp\left\{cj^{3}\right\}}\overset{\text{(}*)}{\lesssim}\exp\left\{-\frac{c\mu_{k}(t-S_{k})}{2}\right\}\frac{1}{k^{3/2}\log k},

where
\text{(}*)
follows from the fact that

\displaystyle\int_{1}^{k}\frac{1}{x\log^{2}x}\exp(cx^{3})\mathrm{d}x\lesssim\frac{1}{k^{3}\log^{2}k}\exp(ck^{3}).

Finally, we arrive at

\displaystyle\sum_{t=1}^{+\infty}\sqrt{l_{t}}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k=1}^{+\infty}\left[\sum_{t=S_{k}}^{S_{k+1}-1}\exp\left\{-\frac{c\mu_{k}(t-S_{k})}{2}\right\}\right]\frac{1}{k^{3/2}\log k}\lesssim\sum_{k=1}^{+\infty}\frac{1}{1-\exp(-c/2\mu_{k})}\frac{1}{k^{3/2}\log k}\lesssim\sum_{k=1}^{+\infty}\frac{1}{k^{3/2}}<\infty.

∎

E.2
Auxiliary lemma for Gaussian distributions

We collect some useful expressions about the standard Gaussian distribution, which shall be used multiple times in the proof of Section
D
.

Lemma E.1
.

The density function and cumulative density function
\phi(\cdot)
and
\Phi(\cdot)
of the standard Gaussian distribution obey the following relations:

\displaystyle\int_{b}^{\infty}(z-a)^{2}\phi(z)\mathrm{d}z=(b-2a)\phi(b)+(a^{2}+1)[1-\Phi(b)];

(124a)

\displaystyle\lim_{\alpha\rightarrow+\infty}\frac{\alpha\Phi(-\alpha)}{\phi(\alpha)}=1;

(124b)

\displaystyle\lim_{\alpha\rightarrow+\infty}\frac{\alpha^{2}\left[\phi(\alpha)-\alpha\Phi(-\alpha)\right]}{\phi(\alpha)}=1;

(124c)

\displaystyle\lim_{\alpha\rightarrow+\infty}\frac{\alpha^{3}\left[\alpha\phi(\alpha)-(\alpha^{2}+1)\Phi(-\alpha)\right]}{\phi(\alpha)}=-2.

(124d)

Proof of Lemma
E.1
.

To verify the first expression, direct calculations yield

\displaystyle\int_{b}^{\infty}(z-a)^{2}\phi(z)\mathrm{d}z

\displaystyle=\int_{b}^{\infty}z^{2}\phi(z)\mathrm{d}z-2a\int_{b}^{\infty}z\phi(z)\mathrm{d}z+a^{2}\int_{b}^{\infty}\phi(z)\mathrm{d}z

\displaystyle=-\int_{b}^{\infty}z\mathrm{d}\phi(z)+2a\int_{b}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\phi(z)+a^{2}\int_{b}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\Phi(z)

\displaystyle=-\left[-b\phi(b)-\int_{b}^{\infty}\phi(z)\mathrm{d}z\right]-2a\phi(b)+a^{2}\left[1-\Phi(b)\right]

\displaystyle=(b-2a)\phi(b)+(a^{2}+1)[1-\Phi(b)].

The last three equations can be verified similarly by use of L’Hôpital’s rule; as an illustration, we provide the proof of the last equation here.
By taking the derivatives of both the numerator and the denominator and using a little algebra, we obtain

\displaystyle\lim_{\alpha\rightarrow+\infty}\frac{\alpha^{3}\left[\alpha\phi(\alpha)-(\alpha^{2}+1)\Phi(-\alpha)\right]}{\phi(\alpha)}

\displaystyle=\lim_{\alpha\rightarrow+\infty}\frac{4\alpha^{3}\phi(\alpha)-\alpha^{5}\phi(\alpha)+(\alpha^{5}+\alpha^{3})\phi(\alpha)-(5\alpha^{4}+3\alpha^{2})\Phi(-\alpha)}{-\alpha\phi(\alpha)}

\displaystyle=\lim_{\alpha\rightarrow+\infty}\frac{-5\alpha^{2}\phi(\alpha)+(5\alpha^{3}+3\alpha)\Phi(-\alpha)}{\phi(\alpha)}

\displaystyle=3\lim_{\alpha\rightarrow+\infty}\frac{\alpha\Phi(-\alpha)}{\phi(\alpha)}-5\lim_{\alpha\rightarrow+\infty}\frac{\alpha^{2}\left[\phi(\alpha)-\alpha\Phi(-\alpha)\right]}{\phi(\alpha)}=-2,

which validates the relation (
124d
).
Thus we complete the proof.
∎
