Electrical Transport in the Hatsugai-Kohmoto Model
Daniele Guerci, Giorgio Sangiovanni, Andrew J. Millis, Michele Fabrizio

Abstract
We show that in models with the Hatsugai-Kohmoto type of interaction that is local in momentum space thus infinite-range in real space, Kubo formulas neither reproduce the correct thermodynamic susceptibilities, nor yield sensible transport coefficients. Using Kohn’s trick to differentiate between metals and insulators by threading a flux in a torus geometry, we uncover the striking property that Hatsugai-Kohmoto models with an interaction-induced gap in the spectrum sustain a current that grows as the linear size at any non-zero flux and which can be either diamagnetic or paramagnetic.



I
Introduction

In periodic systems, many-body electronic correlations induce insulating phases that can either be of genuine Mott character
Mott 1949
or accompanied by translational and/or time reversal symmetry breaking
Wigner 1934
. The underlying mechanism is the non-perturbative interplay between band energy gain, which
is maximised by electron delocalisation throughout the lattice, and Coulomb repulsion, which instead is minimised by electron localisation.
While the former prefers many-body wavefunctions
factorised in crystalline momentum
\mathbf{k}
, the latter favours instead factorisation in real space.
This competition escapes conventional independent-particle descriptions, making any theoretical approach to the interaction-driven transition from free electrons to Mott- or Wigner-like insulators intrinsically difficult.

As a consequence, the search for minimal models capturing key physical aspects of this transition has been considered essential ever since the earliest theoretical as well as experimental instances of Mottness. In this respect, the chief role has been for long played by the single-band Hubbard model
Hubbard 1963
, where the band energy is provided by inter-site hopping and the Coulomb repulsion replaced by an on-site one. This model, a lynchpin of the research on strongly correlated materials, eludes an exact solution except in one or infinite spatial dimensions despite its simplicity.
Hatsugai and Kohmoto
Hatsugai and Kohmoto 1992
introduced a variant of the Hubbard model which is exactly solvable and displays an interaction-driven gap-opening transition; this model has attracted considerable further attention in the context of the Mott transition
Hatsugai and Kohmoto 1992
;
Continentino and Coutinho-Filho 1994
;
Vitoriano et al. 2000
;
Yeo and Phillips 2019
and, more recently, superconductivity
Tarasewicz et al. 2009
;
Phillips et al. 2020
;
Li et al. 2022
and topological band structure effects
Mai et al. 2023
;
Wysokiński and Brzezicki 2023
;
Zhao et al. 2023
;
Jabłonowski et al. 2023
;
Setty et al. 2023
;
Skolimowski et al. 2024
.
Variations starting from the HK but introducing a coupling between high-symmetry momenta have been also proposed for the analysis of the Luttinger surface
Worm et al. 2023
.
Models with HK interactions allow analytically access not only to the thermodynamic properties of Mott phases
Hatsugai and Kohmoto 1992
;
Continentino and Coutinho-Filho 1994
;
Vitoriano et al. 2000
;
Yeo and Phillips 2019
, but also their single-particle Green’s functions
G(\epsilon,\mathbf{k})
, which in the gapped phase exhibit bands of Green function zeros
Gurarie 2011
;
Volovik 2012
;
Fabrizio 2023
;
Wagner et al. 2023a
;
Wagner et al. 2023b
a hard-to-compute non-perturbative effect that can remarkably be obtained by means of simple exact calculations with the HK interaction.
The peculiar feature of the HK interaction that makes the model integrable in any dimension and for generic number of bands is its locality in momentum space,
U_{\text{HK}}=\sum_{\mathbf{k}}U_{\text{HK}}(\mathbf{k})
, which corresponds to infinite range in real space
Setty 2020
.
Such a decisive modification of the spatial structure of the electron-electron repulsion calls for a closer examination of some physical properties of HK interacting models.

In this paper we focus on the fundamental case of long wavelength density-density and current-current response functions, exposing a fundamental non-physical aspect of models with HK type interactions and showing that the Mott phase of HK models in fact has a divergent long wavelength current response physically related to an infinite-ranged pair hopping implied by the HK form of the interaction and mathematically expressed by a nonanaliticity in the
\mathbf{q}\to 0
limit of response functions. The rest of this paper is organized as follows: in Section
II
we present general arguments highlighting the importance of the analyticity of the long-wavelength limit in the extraction of physical observables from Kubo-type linear response formulas. Section
III
defines the generalized HK models that we analyse and recalls their basic properties. Section
IV
applies to HK models a criterion introduced by Kohn in 1964
Kohn 1964
to distinguish metals from insulators, showing that the gapped phases of HK models are in fact metallic, with a non-vanishing Drude weight. Section
V
is a summary and conclusion.

II
Charge conservation versus continuity equation

We begin with some general remarks relating to the long wavelength limit of correlation functions of conserved quantities. We explicitly discuss quantities related to the particle density but the results can be straightforwardly generalized to any other conserved quantities, e.g. the total energy or (for models without spin-orbit interaction), the total spin.

Assume a many-body Hamiltonian
H
that commutes with the total number of electrons
N=\int d\mathbf{r}\,\rho(\mathbf{r})
with
\rho(\mathbf{r})
the particle density operator. We define the Fourier transform of
\rho(\mathbf{r})
as
\rho(\mathbf{q})=\int d\mathbf{r}\,\text{e}^{-i\mathbf{q}\cdot\mathbf{r}}\;\rho(\mathbf{r}),

thus

\displaystyle\Big[\rho(\mathbf{q}={\boldsymbol{0}})\,,\,H\Big]

\displaystyle=\Big[N\,,\,H\Big]=0\,.

(1)

If, as is in the case of conventional models with finite range Coulombic interactions,
\mathbf{q}\to 0
limit is smooth,

\displaystyle\lim_{\mathbf{q}\to{\boldsymbol{0}}}\,\Big[\rho(\mathbf{q})\,,\,H\Big]

\displaystyle=0\,,

(2)

then many important properties follow. For example, the static structure factor at zero temperature, which we define hereafter as

S(\mathbf{q})=\Big(\langle\Psi_{0}\mid\rho(\mathbf{q})\,\rho(-\mathbf{q})\mid\Psi_{0}\rangle-N^{2}\,\delta_{\mathbf{q}{\boldsymbol{0}}}\Big),

(3)

with
\mid\Psi_{0}\rangle
the ground state, obeys

\lim_{\mathbf{q}\to 0}S(\mathbf{q})=0.

(4)

Similarly, the retarded density-density response function at zero temperature is defined as

\chi(\omega,\mathbf{q})=-\frac{\displaystyle i}{\displaystyle V}\int_{0}^{\infty}\!\!\!\!dt\,\text{e}^{i\omega t}\,\langle\Psi_{0}\!\mid\!\Big[\rho(t,\mathbf{q}),\rho(-\mathbf{q})\Big]\!\mid\!\Psi_{0}\rangle\,,

(5)

with
V
the number of
\mathbf{k}
points within the first Brillouin zone, and
\omega
having an infinitesimal positive imaginary part. Smoothness in
\mathbf{q}
implies that

\displaystyle\chi(\omega,\mathbf{q}\to{\boldsymbol{0}})

\displaystyle=0\,,

\displaystyle\forall

\displaystyle\omega\not=0\,,

(6)

as well as

\displaystyle\frac{\displaystyle\partial n}{\displaystyle\partial\mu}

\displaystyle=-\lim_{\mathbf{q}\to{\boldsymbol{0}}}\,\chi(0,\mathbf{q})\,,

(7)

where
n=N/V
and
\partial n/\partial\mu
is the thermodynamic charge compressibility.
One particularly important consequence of the smoothness of the
\mathbf{q}\to 0
limit is

\displaystyle\Big[\rho(\mathbf{q})\,,\,H\Big]\equiv\mathbf{q}\cdot{\boldsymbol{J}}(\mathbf{q})\,,

(8)

with
{\boldsymbol{J}}(\mathbf{q}\to{\boldsymbol{0}})
non singular.
Eq. (
8
) is just the continuity equation, which allows the identification of the longitudinal component of the current operator
{\boldsymbol{J}}(\mathbf{q})
.
The smoothness of the
\mathbf{q}\to 0
limit ensures that at
\mathbf{q}=0
the current operator defined in this way is identical to the current operator defined from
\delta H/\delta\mathbf{k}
with the minimal coupling
\mathbf{k}\to\mathbf{k}-e{\boldsymbol{A}}/c
and
{\boldsymbol{A}}
the vector potential.

To summarize this section, one can define a meaningful current operator if any of equations (
3
), (
6
) and (
7
) is satisfied. If one insists in using (
8
) to find

{\boldsymbol{J}}(\mathbf{q})
even when they are violated, the outcome is a current operator that is singular for
\mathbf{q}\to{\boldsymbol{0}}
, whose physical consequences we here uncover
in models with HK interactions.

III
Models with Hatsugai-Kohmoto type of interactions

We consider
M
(counting both spin and orbital degrees of freedom) bands of interacting electrons with Hamiltonian

\displaystyle H

\displaystyle=H_{0}+U_{\text{HK}}=\sum_{\mathbf{k}}\,H(\mathbf{k})

(9)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathbf{k}}\,\Big(H_{0}(\mathbf{k})+U_{\text{HK}}(\mathbf{k})\Big)\,,

where

H_{0}(\mathbf{k})=\sum_{a,b=1\dots M}t^{ab}(\mathbf{k})\,c^{\dagger}_{a\mathbf{k}}\,c^{\phantom{\dagger}}_{b\mathbf{k}}\,,

(10)

is the non-interacting part, whereas

U_{\rm HK}(\mathbf{k})=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{a,b,c,d}U_{abcd}({\mathbf{k}})\,c^{\dagger}_{a\mathbf{k}}\,c^{\dagger}_{b\mathbf{k}}\,c^{\phantom{\dagger}}_{c\mathbf{k}}\,c^{\phantom{\dagger}}_{d\mathbf{k}}\,,

(11)

is a generic HK two-body interaction
Hatsugai and Kohmoto 1992
, local in momentum space. Here
c^{\dagger}_{a\mathbf{k}}
creates an electron into a state with wave function
\phi_{a\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{r})
and for later reference the density operator

\rho(\mathbf{q})=\sum_{\mathbf{k}ab}\,c^{\dagger}_{a\mathbf{k}}\,\rho_{a\mathbf{k},b\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q}}\,c^{\phantom{\dagger}}_{b\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q}},

(12)

with coefficients

\rho_{a\mathbf{k},b\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q}}=\int d\mathbf{r}\,\phi_{a\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{r})^{*}\,\text{e}^{-i\mathbf{q}\cdot\mathbf{r}}\;\phi_{b\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q}}(\mathbf{r})\underset{\mathbf{q}\to 0}{\to}\delta_{ab},

(13)

where the second line follows from the orthogonality of wave functions.
We observe that, in real space
U_{\text{HK}}
corresponds to

\displaystyle U_{\text{HK}}=\sum_{abcd}\frac{\displaystyle 1}{\displaystyle V}\,\int d\mathbf{R}\,d\mathbf{R}^{\prime}\,d\mathbf{r}\,d\mathbf{r}^{\prime}\,U_{abcd}(\mathbf{R}-\mathbf{R}^{\prime})\;\Psi_{a}^{\dagger}\left(\frac{\mathbf{R}}{2}+\mathbf{r}\right)\,\Psi_{b}^{\dagger}\left(\frac{\mathbf{R}}{2}-\mathbf{r}\right)\,\Psi^{\phantom{\dagger}}_{c}\left(\frac{\mathbf{R^{\prime}}}{2}-\mathbf{r}^{\prime}\right)\,\Psi^{\phantom{\dagger}}_{d}\left(\frac{\mathbf{R^{\prime}}}{2}+\mathbf{r}^{\prime}\right)\,,

(14)

where
\Psi^{\dagger}_{a}(\mathbf{r})=\sum_{\mathbf{k}}\,\phi_{a\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{r})\,c^{\dagger}_{a\mathbf{k}}
is the operator that creates an orbital-
a
electron at position
\mathbf{r}
. We thus see that the HK interaction includes pair hopping and orbital exchange terms with peculiar long-ranged structure. A weak
\mathbf{k}
dependence is normally assumed, so that the center of mass of a pair is preserved,
\mathbf{R}\simeq\mathbf{R^{\prime}}
, still the interaction is independent of the relative spacing of particles in a pair thus can move charge over arbitrarily large distances, for example annihilating a close pair (small relative separation
\mathbf{r}^{\prime}
) and creating a widely separated pair
(large
\mathbf{r}
).

Because
H(\mathbf{k})
conserves the number of electrons in each
\mathbf{k}
sector, we may label the eigenstates by crystal momentum
\mathbf{k}
,
n_{\mathbf{k}}=1,\dots,M
the number of particles. Within the sector with
n_{\mathbf{k}}
particles, we denote as
\mid\!\ell_{\mathbf{k}},\mathbf{k},n_{\mathbf{k}}\rangle
the eigenstates of
H(\mathbf{k})
with eigenvalues
E_{\ell_{\mathbf{k}}}(\mathbf{k},n_{\mathbf{k}})
in ascending order in
\ell_{\mathbf{k}}=0,\dots,\binom{2M}{m}-1
. A generic many-body eigenstate of
H
is therefore a product
state

\displaystyle\mid\{\ell_{\mathbf{k}},n_{\mathbf{k}}\}\rangle\equiv\prod_{\mathbf{k}}\,\mid\ell_{\mathbf{k}},\mathbf{k},n_{\mathbf{k}}\rangle\,,

with energy
\sum_{\mathbf{k}}\,E_{\ell_{\mathbf{k}}}(\mathbf{k},n_{\mathbf{k}})
and number of electrons

\sum_{\mathbf{k}}\,n_{\mathbf{k}}
.

Let us label the (possibly degenerate) ground state as

\displaystyle\mid\Psi_{0}\rangle

\displaystyle\equiv\prod_{\mathbf{k}}\,\mid\ell_{0\mathbf{k}},\mathbf{k},n_{\mathbf{k}}\rangle\,,

where at each
\mathbf{k}
the occupancy
n_{\mathbf{k}}
and the internal quantum number
\ell_{0\mathbf{k}}
are chosen to minimize
H(\mathbf{k})
.
It is very useful for subsequent discussion to define the natural orbitals
\alpha
with corresponding creation
c^{\dagger}_{\alpha\mathbf{k}}
and annihilation
c_{\alpha\mathbf{k}}
operators where
\alpha=1,\dots,M
in such a way that

\displaystyle\langle 0,\mathbf{k},n\mid c^{\dagger}_{\alpha\mathbf{k}}\,c^{\phantom{\dagger}}_{\beta\mathbf{k}}\mid 0,\mathbf{k},n\rangle

\displaystyle=\delta_{\alpha\beta}\,n_{\alpha}(\mathbf{k})\,,

(15)

with
\sum_{\alpha}\,n_{\alpha}(\mathbf{k})=n
, and assuming
n<M
. Correspondingly, we define

\displaystyle\langle\ell,\mathbf{k},n-1\mid c^{\phantom{\dagger}}_{\alpha\mathbf{k}}\mid 0,\mathbf{k},n\rangle

\displaystyle=\sqrt{n_{\alpha}(\mathbf{k})\;}\;C^{-}_{\ell\alpha}(\mathbf{k})\,,

(16)

\displaystyle\langle\ell,\mathbf{k},n+1\mid c^{\dagger}_{\alpha\mathbf{k}}\mid 0,\mathbf{k},n\rangle

\displaystyle=\sqrt{1-n_{\alpha}(\mathbf{k})\;}\;C^{+}_{\ell\alpha}(\mathbf{k})\,,

where, because of (
15
),

\sum_{\ell}\,C^{+}_{\ell\alpha}(\mathbf{k})^{*}\,C^{+}_{\ell\beta}(\mathbf{k})=\sum_{\ell}\,C^{-}_{\ell\alpha}(\mathbf{k})^{*}\,C^{-}_{\ell\beta}(\mathbf{k})=\delta_{\alpha\beta}\,,

(17)

and thus the coefficients
C^{\pm}_{\ell\alpha}(\mathbf{k})
are, by construction, of order one. In terms of the natural orbitals creation and annihilation operators, the
Fourier transform of the density reads

\displaystyle\rho(\mathbf{q})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathbf{k}\alpha\beta}\,c^{\dagger}_{\alpha\mathbf{k}}\;\rho_{\alpha\mathbf{k},\beta\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q}}\;c^{\phantom{\dagger}}_{\beta\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q}}\,,

(18)

where
\rho_{\alpha\mathbf{k},\beta\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q}}
is defined as in (
13
).
The static structure factor (
3
) for
\mathbf{q}\not={\boldsymbol{0}}
is therefore

\displaystyle S(\mathbf{q})

\displaystyle=\langle\Psi_{0}\mid\rho(\mathbf{q})\,\rho(-\mathbf{q})\mid\Psi_{0}\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathbf{k}\mathbf{p}}\,\sum_{\alpha\beta\gamma\delta}\,\rho_{\alpha\mathbf{k},\beta\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q}}\,\rho_{\delta\mathbf{p},\gamma\mathbf{p}+\mathbf{q}}^{*}

\displaystyle\langle\Psi_{0}\mid c^{\dagger}_{\alpha\mathbf{k}}\,c^{\phantom{\dagger}}_{\beta\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q}}\;c^{\dagger}_{\gamma\mathbf{p}+\mathbf{q}}\,c^{\phantom{\dagger}}_{\delta\mathbf{p}}\mid\Psi_{0}\rangle\,.

Since the ground state is factorised, we readily find through (
15
) and (
13
) that

\displaystyle S(\mathbf{q})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathbf{k}\alpha\beta}\,\big|\rho_{\alpha\mathbf{k},\beta\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q}}\big|^{2}\,n_{\alpha}(\mathbf{k})\,\big(1-n_{\beta}(\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q})\big)

\displaystyle\xrightarrow[\mathbf{q}\to{\boldsymbol{0}}]{}\,\sum_{\mathbf{k}\alpha}\,n_{\alpha}(\mathbf{k})\,\big(1-n_{\alpha}(\mathbf{k})\big)\,,

hence does not vanish for
\mathbf{q}\to{\boldsymbol{0}}
unless
n_{\alpha}(\mathbf{k})=0,1
,
which occurs, e.g., if the model is non interacting,
H^{\text{HK}}_{int}(\mathbf{k})=0
(recall that we assume zero temperature).

The density-density response function (
5
), using (
16
)
and defining the matrix elements

\displaystyle\text{P}_{\ell\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q},\ell^{\prime}\mathbf{k}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\alpha\beta}\,\rho_{\beta\mathbf{k},\alpha\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q}}^{*}\,C^{+}_{\ell\alpha}(\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q})\,C^{-}_{\ell^{\prime}\beta}(\mathbf{k})

(19)

\displaystyle\sqrt{\big(1-n_{\alpha}(\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q})\big)\,n_{\beta}(\mathbf{k})\;}\;,

reads

\displaystyle\chi(\omega,\mathbf{q})

\displaystyle=\frac{\displaystyle 1}{\displaystyle V}\!\sum_{\mathbf{k},\ell\ell^{\prime}}\!\Bigg\{\frac{\displaystyle\;\text{P}^{*}_{\ell\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q},\ell^{\prime}\mathbf{k}}\,\text{P}^{\phantom{\dagger}}_{\ell\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q},\ell^{\prime}\mathbf{k}}\;}{\displaystyle\omega-\epsilon_{\ell}(\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q},n+1)-\epsilon_{\ell^{\prime}}(\mathbf{k},n-1)}

(20)

\displaystyle-\frac{\displaystyle\;\text{P}^{*}_{\ell\mathbf{k},\ell^{\prime}\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q}}\,\text{P}^{\phantom{\dagger}}_{\ell\mathbf{k},\ell^{\prime}\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q}}\;}{\displaystyle\omega+\epsilon_{\ell}(\mathbf{k},n+1)+\epsilon_{\ell^{\prime}}(\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q},n-1)}\Bigg\}\,,

where

\displaystyle\epsilon_{\ell}(\mathbf{k},n\pm 1)=E_{\ell}(\mathbf{k},n\pm 1)-E_{0}(\mathbf{k},n)>0\,,

are the excitation energies. For
\mathbf{q}\to{\boldsymbol{0}}
,
\chi(\omega,\mathbf{q})
in (
20
)
becomes

\displaystyle\chi(\omega,\mathbf{q}\to{\boldsymbol{0}})

\displaystyle\to\frac{\displaystyle 1}{\displaystyle V}\sum_{\mathbf{k},\ell\ell^{\prime}}\,\Bigg\{\;\big|\text{P}^{\phantom{\dagger}}_{\ell\mathbf{k},\ell^{\prime}\mathbf{k}}\big|^{2}

\displaystyle\frac{\displaystyle\epsilon_{\ell}(\mathbf{k},n+1)+\epsilon_{\ell^{\prime}}(\mathbf{k},n-1)}{\displaystyle\;\omega^{2}-\big(\epsilon_{\ell}(\mathbf{k},n+1)+\epsilon_{\ell^{\prime}}(\mathbf{k},n-1)\big)^{2}\;}\Bigg\}\;,

with

\displaystyle\text{P}_{\ell\mathbf{k},\ell^{\prime}\mathbf{k}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\alpha}\,C^{+}_{\ell\alpha}(\mathbf{k})\,C^{-}_{\ell^{\prime}\alpha}(\mathbf{k})

(21)

\displaystyle\sqrt{\big(1-n_{\alpha}(\mathbf{k})\big)\,n_{\alpha}(\mathbf{k})\;}\;.

Therefore,
\chi(\omega,\mathbf{q}\to{\boldsymbol{0}})
is evidently non zero if
n_{\alpha}(\mathbf{k})\not=0,1
, see (
21
), in contrast to (
6
).
Suppose, as we do hereafter, that the model describes an insulator with a single-particle gap, thus vanishing compressibility
\partial n/\partial\mu
and

\epsilon_{\ell}(\mathbf{k},n\pm 1)>0
that remain finite in the thermodynamic limit.
However, looking at Eq. (
20
) we readily conclude that

\displaystyle 0=\frac{\displaystyle\partial n}{\displaystyle\partial\mu}\not=-\lim_{\mathbf{q}\to{\boldsymbol{0}}}\,\chi(0,\mathbf{q})\,.

It follows that, in presence of interaction,
\chi(0,\mathbf{q}\to{\boldsymbol{0}})
does not provide the correct charge compressibility.

This is directly tested on a two-orbital Hamiltonian

H=\sum_{\mathbf{k}}\big(H_{0}(\mathbf{k})+U_{\rm HK}(\mathbf{k})\big)
,
where
H_{0}(\mathbf{k})
is a non-interacting BHZ
Bernevig et al. 2006
;
Budich et al. 2013

\displaystyle U_{\text{HK}}(\mathbf{k})

\displaystyle=\frac{U}{2}\sum_{a=1,2}\,\big(n_{a\mathbf{k}}-1\big)^{2}+J\,{\boldsymbol{S}}_{\mathbf{k}1}\cdot{\boldsymbol{S}}_{\mathbf{k}2},

(22)

with

\mathbf{k}
, while

stabilises a non-magnetic Mott insulator at large

.
Specifically, defining the four component spinor

{\boldsymbol{c}}^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{k}}=\big(c^{\dagger}_{1\mathbf{k}\uparrow},c^{\dagger}_{2\mathbf{k}\uparrow},c^{\dagger}_{1\mathbf{k}\downarrow},c^{\dagger}_{2\mathbf{k}\downarrow}\big)
,

\displaystyle H_{0}(\mathbf{k})

\displaystyle={\boldsymbol{c}}^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{k}}\,\begin{pmatrix}\hat{h}_{\uparrow}(\mathbf{k})&0\\
0&\hat{h}_{\downarrow}(\mathbf{k})\end{pmatrix}\,{\boldsymbol{c}}^{\phantom{\dagger}}_{\mathbf{k}}\,,

(23)

where

\displaystyle\hat{h}_{\uparrow}(\mathbf{k})

\displaystyle=\big(M+t\cos k_{x}+t\cos k_{y}\big)\,\tau^{z}

\displaystyle+\lambda\,\big(\sin k_{x}\,\tau^{x}+\sin k_{y}\,\tau^{y}\big)\equiv{\boldsymbol{d}}_{\uparrow}(\mathbf{k})\cdot{\boldsymbol{\tau}}\,,

with

. At

the model describes a quantum spin-Hall insulator,
while

stabilises a Mott insulator that is essentially

inter-orbital singlets. The Mott transition is not unique but occurs

, until, above

The above results show explicitly that any Hamiltonian with a HK type of interaction yields non analytic properties at

III.1
Explicit expression of the interaction-induced current component
and its consequences

To be more precise, let us write the general expression of a Hatsugai-Kohmoto type of interaction, using for convenience the operators in the natural orbital basis,

\displaystyle U_{\text{HK}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\displaystyle 1}{\displaystyle 2}\,\sum_{\mathbf{k}}U_{\alpha\beta\gamma\delta}(\mathbf{k})\;c^{\dagger}_{\alpha\mathbf{k}}\,c^{\dagger}_{\beta\mathbf{k}}\,c^{\phantom{\dagger}}_{\gamma\mathbf{k}}\,c^{\phantom{\dagger}}_{\delta\mathbf{k}}\,,

(24)

where repeated indices are summed and

\displaystyle U_{\alpha\beta\gamma\delta}(\mathbf{k})

\displaystyle=-U_{\beta\alpha\gamma\delta}(\mathbf{k})=U_{\beta\alpha\delta\gamma}(\mathbf{k})=U_{\delta\gamma\beta\alpha}(\mathbf{k})^{*}\,.

Using the small
\mathbf{q}
limit of the density (
18
), i.e., approximating
the matrix element with
\delta_{\alpha\beta}
,
one readily finds that (
24
) yields, through (
8
),
a contribution
{\boldsymbol{J}}^{\parallel}_{\text{int}}(\mathbf{q})
to the longitudinal component of the current operator that reads

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{J}}^{\parallel}_{\text{int}}(\mathbf{q})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\displaystyle\mathbf{q}}{\displaystyle\;q^{2}\,}\sum_{\mathbf{k}}\bigg\{U_{\alpha\beta\gamma\delta}(\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q})\;c^{\dagger}_{\alpha\mathbf{k}}\,c^{\dagger}_{\beta\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q}}\,c^{\phantom{\dagger}}_{\gamma\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q}}\,c^{\phantom{\dagger}}_{\delta\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q}}

(25)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\;-U_{\alpha\beta\gamma\delta}(\mathbf{k})\;c^{\dagger}_{\alpha\mathbf{k}}\,c^{\dagger}_{\beta\mathbf{k}}\,c^{\phantom{\dagger}}_{\gamma\mathbf{k}}\,c^{\phantom{\dagger}}_{\delta\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q}}\bigg\}\,.

We observe that, as discussed in detail in Ref.
Setty et al. 2023
, see, for comparison, Ref.
Zhao et al. 2023
,

{\boldsymbol{J}}^{\parallel}_{\text{int}}(\mathbf{q})
does not vanish
for
\mathbf{q}\to{\boldsymbol{0}}
, as can be realised by computing the matrix elements between any two eigenstates of the Hamiltonian.
In fact,
{\boldsymbol{J}}^{\parallel}_{\text{int}}(\mathbf{q})
is singular for
\mathbf{q}\to{\boldsymbol{0}}
, as we anticipated.

This result has a striking consequence. Because of gauge invariance,

\displaystyle\frac{\displaystyle\omega^{2}}{\displaystyle q^{2}}\;\chi(\omega,\mathbf{q})=\Big(\chi_{\parallel}(\omega,\mathbf{q})+\text{N}(\mathbf{q})\Big)\,,

(26)

where
\chi_{\parallel}(\omega,\mathbf{q})
is the longitudinal component of the
current-current response function, and

\displaystyle\text{N}(\mathbf{q})

\displaystyle=\frac{\displaystyle 1}{\displaystyle Vq^{2}}\;\langle 0\mid\Big[\big[\rho(\mathbf{q}),H\big],\rho(-\mathbf{q})\Big]\mid 0\rangle\,,

is the diamagnetic contribution. The left hand side of
(
26
) vanishes for
\omega\to 0
, thus

\chi_{\parallel}(0,\mathbf{q})=-\text{N}(\mathbf{q})
, which is reassuringly consistent with gauge invariance. However, because of (
26
), the
longitudinal component of the uniform conductivity
\sigma_{\parallel}(\omega)

reads

\displaystyle\sigma_{\parallel}(\omega)

\displaystyle=\lim_{\mathbf{q}\to{\boldsymbol{0}}}\,\frac{\displaystyle ie^{2}}{\displaystyle\omega}\,\Big(\chi_{\parallel}(\omega,\mathbf{q})+\text{N}(\mathbf{q})\Big)

\displaystyle=\lim_{\mathbf{q}\to{\boldsymbol{0}}}\,\frac{\displaystyle ie^{2}}{\displaystyle\omega}\;\frac{\displaystyle\omega^{2}}{\displaystyle q^{2}}\;\chi(\omega,\mathbf{q})\,.

Hence, since
\chi(\omega,\mathbf{q}\to{\boldsymbol{0}})\not=0
,
\sigma_{\parallel}(\omega)
is singular despite the fact that the model is supposed to describe an insulator.
Such anomalous behaviour is evidently a consequence of the infinite-range Hatsugai-Kohmoto interaction and, in particular, of the long-distance pair-hopping processes that are allowed and contribute to the current.

The results of this and the previous section, which hold true also for densities and currents associated to other conserved quantities, show an unavoidable inconsistency in the calculation of thermodynamic susceptibilities and transport coefficients through low-frequency and long-wavelength linear response functions for models with the HK interaction.

IV
Kohn’s criterium applied to HK models

In 1964, Walter Kohn proposed a very elegant and enlightening argument
Kohn 1964
to discriminate between metals and insulators through their responsiveness to boundary conditions. He argued that metals
are sensitive to varying boundary conditions, whereas insulators are not.
Indeed, Kohn showed
Kohn 1964
that, upon twisting boundary conditions by an angle
\phi
in
one direction, the curvature of the total energy
E(\phi)
at
\phi=0

is proportional to the formal expression of the Drude weight
D
of a realistic Hamiltonian, as calculated by spectral decomposition. Specifically,

\displaystyle\lim_{L\to\infty}\,\frac{\displaystyle e^{2}}{\displaystyle L^{d-2}}\,\frac{\displaystyle\partial^{2}E(\phi)}{\displaystyle\partial\phi^{2}}_{\big|\phi=0}

\displaystyle=D\,,

(27)

where
L
is the system size and
d
the dimension. Strictly speaking, for the equivalence (
27
) to be valid, the evolution of the ground state energy with
\phi
has to be adiabatic. The necessary but not sufficient condition for this is that the thermodynamic limit
L\to\infty
is taken after calculating the second derivative
Scalapino et al. 1993
. Hereafter, we shall consider a system with a spectral gap to all excitations, which we believe implies that the necessary condition is also sufficient.

We may wonder whether Kohn’s distinguishing criterium holds true also in models with HK interactions, given the infinite range of the latter.

For that, we consider a model on a cubic lattice with linear size
L
and unit lattice spacing, which is folded into a three-dimensional torus.
The lattice potential
V(\mathbf{r})
as well as the potential

U(\mathbf{r})
in the HK interaction (
14
) are assumed periodic on the torus, e.g.,
U(\mathbf{r})=U(\mathbf{r}+L\,{\boldsymbol{e}}_{a})
where
{\boldsymbol{e}}_{a}
is the unit vector in the
a=x,y,z
direction. Therefore, also the many-body eigenfunctions of
the Hamiltonian
H
must have the same periodicity.

Next, we suppose that the torus is threaded by a flux
\phi_{a}
,
a=x,y,z
, which we parametrise through the vector
{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}=(\phi_{x},\phi_{y},\phi_{z})/L
, so that
the new Hamiltonian is
H({\boldsymbol{\kappa}})
. In realistic Hamiltonians
that have, e.g., a first quantization representation,
the flux can be formally
gauged away by a unitary transformation
U({\boldsymbol{\kappa}})=\text{e}^{-W({\boldsymbol{\kappa}})}
,

W({\boldsymbol{\kappa}})^{\dagger}=-W({\boldsymbol{\kappa}})
, which changes the originally periodic Fermi fields
\Psi_{\alpha}(\mathbf{r})=\Psi_{\alpha}(\mathbf{r}+L\,{\boldsymbol{e}}_{a})
into twisted ones,

\displaystyle U({\boldsymbol{\kappa}})^{\dagger}\,\Psi_{\alpha}(\mathbf{r})\,U({\boldsymbol{\kappa}})=\text{e}^{-i{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}\cdot\mathbf{r}}\;\Psi_{\alpha}(\mathbf{r})\,.

One finds that

\displaystyle W({\boldsymbol{\kappa}})

\displaystyle=i\,\int d\mathbf{r}\,{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}\cdot\mathbf{r}\,\rho(\mathbf{r})\,,

(28)

and translates by
{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}
the total momentum of the many-body system.
As emphasised by Kohn
Kohn 1964
, despite after the gauge transformation

H({\boldsymbol{\kappa}})\to H
, its spectrum does depend on
{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}
since it must be
calculated on a basis of many-body wavefunctions satisfying

\displaystyle\Psi(\mathbf{r}_{1},\mathbf{r}_{2},\dots,\mathbf{r}_{i}+L\,{\boldsymbol{e}}_{a},\dots\mathbf{r}_{N})

\displaystyle=\text{e}^{i\phi_{a}}\;\Psi(\mathbf{r}_{1},\mathbf{r}_{2},\dots,\mathbf{r}_{i},\dots\mathbf{r}_{N})\,,

which have not anymore the periodicity of the torus, unlike
H
does.

In the case of an Hamiltonian with a HK interaction, as in (
9
),
there is a further issue. Indeed, while the non-interacting Hamiltonian

H_{0}
has a first quantization representation,

\displaystyle H_{0}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\,\bigg(\frac{\displaystyle\mathbf{p}_{i}^{2}}{\displaystyle 2m}+V(\mathbf{r}_{i})\bigg)\,,

where
i=1,\dots,N
label the
N
electrons, so that
H_{0}({\boldsymbol{\kappa}})
is simply obtained by
\mathbf{p}_{i}\to\mathbf{p}_{i}+{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}
,

U_{\text{HK}}
does not and therefore it is not obvious what
U_{\text{HK}}({\boldsymbol{\kappa}})
is. For that, one can reasonably assume, as we do hereafter, that the
previous gauge argument can be extended even in HK Hamiltonians, thus that
U^{\dagger}({\boldsymbol{\kappa}})\,U_{\text{HK}}({\boldsymbol{\kappa}})\,U({\boldsymbol{\kappa}})=U_{\text{HK}}
,
which implies that

\displaystyle U_{\text{HK}}({\boldsymbol{\kappa}})=U({\boldsymbol{\kappa}})\,U_{\text{HK}}\,U^{\dagger}({\boldsymbol{\kappa}})

(29)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\alpha\beta\gamma\delta}\,\frac{\displaystyle 1}{\displaystyle L^{3}}\int\prod_{i=1}^{4}\,d\mathbf{r}_{i}\;U_{\alpha\beta\gamma\delta}(\mathbf{r}_{1}+\mathbf{r}_{2}-\mathbf{r}_{3}-\mathbf{r}_{4})

\displaystyle\text{e}^{-i{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}\cdot(\mathbf{r}_{1}+\mathbf{r}_{2}-\mathbf{r}_{3}-\mathbf{r}_{4})}\;\Psi^{\dagger}_{\alpha}(\mathbf{r}_{1})\,\Psi^{\dagger}_{\beta}(\mathbf{r}_{2})\,\Psi^{\phantom{\dagger}}_{\gamma}(\mathbf{r}_{3})\,\Psi^{\phantom{\dagger}}_{\alpha}(\mathbf{r}_{4})\,.

We observe that, unlike conventional two-body interaction terms,
(
29
) explicitly depends on
{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}
. In particular,
U_{\text{HK}}({\boldsymbol{\kappa}})
looses the nice property of
U_{\text{HK}}
of being diagonal in momentum.
Indeed, is we write, consistently with the torus geometry,

\displaystyle U_{\alpha\beta\gamma\delta}(\mathbf{r})

\displaystyle=\frac{\displaystyle 1}{\displaystyle L^{3}}\,\sum_{\mathbf{q}}\,U_{\alpha\beta\gamma\delta}(\mathbf{q})\,\text{e}^{i\mathbf{q}\cdot\mathbf{r}}\;,

\displaystyle\Psi^{\phantom{\dagger}}_{\alpha}(\mathbf{r})

\displaystyle=\frac{\displaystyle 1}{\displaystyle\;\sqrt{L^{3}\,}\;}\,\sum_{\mathbf{k}}\,\text{e}^{i\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{r}}\;c^{\phantom{\dagger}}_{\alpha\mathbf{k}}\,,

where
\mathbf{q}
and
\mathbf{k}
have components quantised in integer multiples
of
2\pi/L
, then

\displaystyle U_{\text{HK}}({\boldsymbol{\kappa}})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathbf{q}\,\mathbf{k}_{i}}U_{\alpha\beta\gamma\delta}(\mathbf{q})\,c^{\dagger}_{\alpha\mathbf{k}_{1}}\,c^{\dagger}_{\beta\mathbf{k}_{2}}\,c^{\phantom{\dagger}}_{\gamma\mathbf{k}_{3}}\,c^{\phantom{\dagger}}_{\delta\mathbf{k}_{4}}

(30)

\displaystyle I(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{k}_{1})\,I(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{k}_{2})\,I(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{k}_{3})^{*}\,I(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{k}_{4})^{*}\,,

where

\displaystyle I(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{k})

\displaystyle=\frac{\displaystyle 1}{\displaystyle L^{3}}\,\int d\mathbf{r}\,\text{e}^{i(\mathbf{q}-\mathbf{k}-{\boldsymbol{\kappa}})\cdot\mathbf{r}}\;,

is not equal to
\delta_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{q}}
unless
{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}=0
or in the thermodynamic
limit
L\to\infty
.

To proceed with Kohn’s argument, we need to evaluate the ground state energy

E_{0}({\boldsymbol{\kappa}})
of
H({\boldsymbol{\kappa}})
and calculate its curvature at
{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}=0
.
We assume that the Peierls substitution is valid for the non-interacting Hamiltonian (
10
)
Wissgott et al. 2012
;
Guerci et al. 2020
;
Dmytruk and Schiró 2021
;
Schüler et al. 2021
, so that

H_{0}(\mathbf{k},{\boldsymbol{\kappa}})=\sum_{\alpha,\beta=1\dots M}t^{\alpha\beta}(\mathbf{k}+{\boldsymbol{\kappa}})\,c^{\dagger}_{\alpha\mathbf{k}}\,c^{\phantom{\dagger}}_{\beta\mathbf{k}}\,,

(31)

which can be readily expanded up to second order in
{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}
. We further need
to expand (
30
) to the same order, which is equivalent to

U_{\text{HK}}({\boldsymbol{\kappa}})\simeq U_{\text{HK}}-\big[W({\boldsymbol{\kappa}}),U_{\text{HK}}\big]+\frac{\displaystyle 1}{\displaystyle 2}\Big[W({\boldsymbol{\kappa}}),\big[W({\boldsymbol{\kappa}}),U_{\text{HK}}\big]\Big]\,.

(32)

Since
\rho(\mathbf{r})
in (
28
) has the periodicity of the torus, it can be written as

\displaystyle\rho(\mathbf{r})

\displaystyle=\frac{\displaystyle 1}{\displaystyle L^{3}}\,\sum_{\mathbf{q}}\,\text{e}^{i\mathbf{q}\cdot\mathbf{r}}\;\rho(\mathbf{q})\,,

\displaystyle\mathbf{q}

\displaystyle=\frac{\displaystyle 2\pi}{\displaystyle L}\big(n_{x},n_{y},n_{z}\big)\,.

Therefore
W({\boldsymbol{\kappa}})
in (
28
) is also equivalent to

\displaystyle W({\boldsymbol{\kappa}})

\displaystyle=\sum_{a=x,y,z}\,\sum_{q_{a}\not=0}\,\frac{\displaystyle\kappa_{a}}{\displaystyle q_{a}}\;\rho\left(q_{a}\,{\boldsymbol{e}}_{a}\right)\,.

(33)

and can be used to evaluate (
32
). Equations
(
31
) and (
32
) allow calculating
the ground state energy up to second order in
{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}
.

As an example, we consider a simple one-band model, with nearest-neighbour hopping
-t
and HK interaction
U_{\rm HK}(\mathbf{k})=U(\mathbf{k})\,(n_{\mathbf{k}}-1)^{2}/2
, where

n_{\mathbf{k}}=n_{\mathbf{k}\uparrow}+n_{\mathbf{k}\downarrow}
. For
U(\mathbf{k})>4dt>0
, the model describes a Mott insulator where each momentum state is occupied by a single electron. This state
is degenerate, since each electron can have any spin. We decide to use the non-pure
density matrix

\displaystyle\rho=\prod_{\mathbf{k}}\,\frac{\displaystyle\;\mid\mathbf{k},\uparrow\rangle\langle\mathbf{k},\uparrow\mid+\mid\mathbf{k},\downarrow\rangle\langle\mathbf{k},\downarrow\mid\;}{\displaystyle 2}\;,

to perform the calculations, a choice commonly adopted in the literature.
Since each
\mathbf{k}
is singly-occupied, the ground state energy variation
at second order in
{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}

comes just from the interaction part (
32
) and, assuming the flux finite only along
x
, reads

\Delta E_{0}({\boldsymbol{\kappa}})\simeq E_{0}({\boldsymbol{\kappa}})-E_{0}({\boldsymbol{0}})=\sum_{q_{x}\not=0}\,\sum_{\mathbf{k}}\,\frac{\displaystyle\phi_{x}^{2}}{\displaystyle\;2L^{2}q_{x}^{2}\;}\;\frac{\displaystyle\;\Big(U(\mathbf{k})+U(\mathbf{k}+q_{x}{\boldsymbol{e}}_{x})\Big)\,\big(\epsilon_{\mathbf{k}}-\epsilon_{\mathbf{k}+q_{x}{\boldsymbol{e}}_{x}}\big)\;}{\displaystyle\;U(\mathbf{k})+U(\mathbf{k}+q_{x}{\boldsymbol{e}}_{x})+2\big(\epsilon_{\mathbf{k}}-\epsilon_{\mathbf{k}+q_{x}{\boldsymbol{e}}_{x}}\big)\;}\,.

(34)

Few comments are in order. First,
\Delta E({\boldsymbol{\kappa}})\not=0
despite the single-particle gap, and vanishes only for
U(\mathbf{k})\to\infty
.

Furthermore, the double sum, over
q_{x}
and
\mathbf{k}
, grows as
L^{d+1}
, which corresponds through
Eq. (
27
) to a Drude weight scaling as
L
, evidently a consequence of the infinite range of the HK interaction.

Finally, one easily realises that
\Delta E({\boldsymbol{\kappa}})<0
, which formally corresponds to a negative Drude weight linear in the system size, thus to a singular (orbital) paramagnetic response. In fact, we can rigorously prove that
\Delta E({\boldsymbol{\kappa}})<0
whenever the eigenstates of
H(\mathbf{k})
are simultaneously eigenstates of
H_{0}(\mathbf{k})
and of
U_{\text{HK}}(\mathbf{k})
, which indeed occurs in the
single-band model we analysed. In more general cases when
\big[H_{0}(\mathbf{k}),U_{\text{HK}}(\mathbf{k})\big]\not=0
, we cannot exclude that
\Delta E({\boldsymbol{\kappa}})>0
, thus a positive Drude weight still growing with the linear size of the system. However, irrespective of the sign of
\Delta E({\boldsymbol{\kappa}})
, we can definitely conclude that a model with HK interaction sustains a finite current at any finite flux, diamagnetic if
\Delta E({\boldsymbol{\kappa}})>0
and
paramagnetic otherwise, even if it has a gap in the single-particle spectrum. This, in light of Kohn’s argument, does not come as a surprise since the interaction has infinite range in real space and therefore also the insulator remains sensitive to the boundary conditions, as recently also discussed in Ref.
Skolimowski 2024
.

We conclude noticing that the knowledge of the ground-state wavefunction

\mid\Psi_{0}({\boldsymbol{\kappa}})\rangle
up to first order in
{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}
allows the direct evaluation
of the many-body Chern number
C
, for instance in
d=2

Resta 2005

\displaystyle C

\displaystyle=-\lim_{L\to\infty}\,\frac{\displaystyle 2\pi}{\displaystyle L^{2}}\,\text{Im}\,\langle\partial_{\kappa_{x}}\Psi_{0}({\boldsymbol{\kappa}})\mid\partial_{\kappa_{y}}\Psi_{0}({\boldsymbol{\kappa}})\rangle_{\big|{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}={\boldsymbol{0}}}\,,

which avoids using Kubo formulas.

V
Concluding remarks

Models with Hatsugai-Kohmoto interactions
Hatsugai and Kohmoto 1992
are often used to
discuss non-perturbative phenomena and represent, in particular, a very elegant and a convenient analytic proxy to Mottness.
In general, the latter is instead hard to access and exactly solvable only in specific limits.
However, in view of the infinite range of the HK interaction, one must be very cautious in adapting results valid
for realistic models to HK ones, and vice versa. Indeed, we have shown that
the common way of calculating thermodynamic susceptibilities and transport coefficients through
Kubo formulas is incorrect when the interaction is of Hatsugai-Kohmoto type.

Important considerations regard also the topological properties of HK-interacting insulators. On the one hand, it has been shown that for HK models, similarly to the case of Hubbard-Mott insulators
Blason and Fabrizio 2023
;
Lessnich et al. 2023
, quantum spin Hall conductivities can correctly be non-quantized and hence deviate from the values of the (interacting) topological invariant
Zhao et al. 2023
;
Setty et al. 2023
. On the other hand, the issues of transport originating from the infinite-range nature of the HK interaction discussed in this work should be taken into account. In particular, the unphysical (and infinite) value of the diagonal bulk conductivity notwithstanding the presence of a gap as well as the impossibility, due to the infinite range of the interaction, to introduce a notion of boundary are pathologies that in some cases may reduce the convenience of using HK models for studying interacting topology.
