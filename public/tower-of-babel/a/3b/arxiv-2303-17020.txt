Mesoscopic Spectral CLT for Block Correlated Random Matrices
Torben Krüger, Yuriy Nemish

Abstract
For random matrices with block correlation structure we show that the fluctuations of linear eigenvalue statistics are Gaussian on all mesoscopic scales with universal variance which coincides with that of the Gaussian unitary or Gaussian orthogonal ensemble, depending on the symmetry class of the model. The main tool used for determining this variance is a two-point version of the matrix-valued Dyson equation, that encodes the asymptotic behavior of the product of resolvents at different spectral parameters.



Keywords: Mesoscopic Central Limit Theorem, Hermitian random matrix, linear matrix pencil

AMS Subject Classification: 60B20, 15B52

Introduction

The eigenvalues of Hermitian random matrices form a strongly correlated point process on the real line. To gain insights into the underlying correlations we study linear statistics of this process.
More generally, for
N
real random variables
\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{N}
their linear statistics is
L_{N}(f):=\sum_{i=1}^{N}f(\lambda_{i})
for some test function
f
. If
\frac{1}{N}L_{N}(f)
converges as
N\to\infty
and becomes nonrandom, we say that the law of large numbers holds. Such results are well know not only in the case when
\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{n}
are independent, but also when they are the eigenvalues of an
N\times N
random matrix ensemble. In this case
\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N}L_{N}(f)=\int f(x)\rho(x)dx
, where
\rho
is the asymptotic spectral density.

The next important question to ask about
\frac{1}{N}L_{N}(f)
concerns the size and distribution of its fluctuations around the limit. In the case of independent random variables the central limit theorem (CLT) states that these fluctuations are Gaussian of order
N^{-1/2}
. The strong correlation between the eigenvalues of random matrices, however, typically reduces the fluctuation to the order
N^{-1}
, while the distribution remains Gaussian.
Such results were proved, e.g., for invariant ensembles
[
45
]
, Wigner matrices with non-Gaussian i.i.d. entries
[
46
,
8
]
, sample covariance matrices
[
9
]
, ensembles with external source
[
44
]
, and polynomials of several independent random matrices
[
14
]
.

The law of large numbers and CLT described above provide information about the eigenvalues on global scales
\eta=O(1)
. To resolve the eigenvalue distribution on mesoscopic scales
N^{-1}\ll\eta\leq 1
above the typical spacing distance, or even microscopic scales of order
\eta=O(N^{-1})
, the compactly supported test function
f
with
\int f(x)\,dx=1
is rescaled to capture an
\eta
-sized neighbourhood around
x_{0}
, i.e.,

f_{\eta}(x):=f\bigg(\frac{x-x_{0}}{\eta}\bigg)\,.

On mesoscopic scales the linear statistics for
f_{\eta}
still involves a large number of
O(N\eta)
eigenvalues and thus the law of large numbers now takes the form
\frac{1}{N\eta}L_{N}(f_{\eta})\to\rho(x_{0})
. Such local laws have been established for a large variety of random matrix models, including Wigner matrices
[
32
]
, deformed Wigner matrices
[
52
,
40
]
, matrices with variance profiles
[
1
]
and correlations
[
2
,
31
]
, invariant ensembles
[
16
]
, and polynomials in several random matrices
[
7
,
28
]
.

The fluctuation of linear eigenvalue statistics exhibits a striking universality phenomenon on mesoscopic scales. Not only its order and Gaussian distribution become universal, but also the corresponding variance, namely

L_{N}(f_{\eta})-\mathbb{E}L_{N}(f_{\eta})\to\mathcal{N}(0,v_{f})\,,\quad v_{f}=\frac{1}{2\pi^{2}\beta}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\bigg(\frac{f(x)-f(y)}{x-y}\bigg)^{2}dxdy\,,

(1.1)

is independent of
\eta
and
x_{0}
. The only signature of the underlying random matrix ensemble still present in these fluctuations is its symmetry class
\beta
, where
\beta=1
corresponds to real symmetric and
\beta=2
to complex Hermitian matrices.
Versions of the mesoscopic CLT (
1.1
) have been established for a wide class of random matrix models. These include the classical Gaussian ensembles
[
17
,
33
]
, Wigner matrices, for which the CLT has been shown covering an increasing range of mesoscopic regimes
[
18
,
39
,
55
]
, Wigner-type matrices
[
58
]
, invariant ensembles
[
13
,
49
]
, deformed Wigner matrices
[
53
]
, functions of Wigner matrices
[
24
]
, Dyson Brownian motion
[
26
,
43
]
, band matrices
[
27
]
, and free sums
[
11
]
.
The mesoscopic CLT is also used to establish fluctuations of individual eigenvalues
[
50
]
.
For non-Hermitian random matrices with independent entries and Coulomb gases in dimension two, mesoscopic CLTs have recently been proven in
[
22
]
and
[
12
]
, respectively.

While the local law has been established for Hermitian and non-Hermitian models with very general correlation structure among the entries of the underlying random matrix, the current proofs of mesoscopic CLTs either assume independent entries or isotropic randomness in the matrix, which is invariant under either the unitary or orthogonal group. In this work we establish the CLT (
1.1
) for linear matrix pencils in several random matrices, which do not satisfy either condition. Instead they belong to a class of random matrix ensembles with non-isotropic block correlations among their entries.

For matrices
\bm{X}_{0},\bm{X}_{1},\dots,\bm{X}_{d}\in\mathbb{C}^{N\times N}
a linear matrix pencil (LMP) is a linear combination of these matrices with matrix valued coefficients, i.e., a matrix in
\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{N\times N}
of the form

\bm{H}=\sum_{i=0}^{d}L_{i}\otimes\bm{X}_{i}\,.

(1.2)

These LMPs find applications in optimization
[
62
]
, systems engineering
[
59
]
, theoretical computer science
[
15
]
(see, e.g.,
[
47
,
34
]
and references therein for numerous other applications).

In this work we consider the case when
\bm{X}_{0}=\bm{I}_{N}
and
\bm{X}_{1},\dots,\bm{X}_{d}
are Wigner matrices.
These random LMPs are of particular interest in the study of the evolution of ecosystems
[
38
]
and neural networks
[
57
]
(see also
[
3
,
30
]
and references therein).
Moreover, such LMPs appear as linearizations of non-commutative polynomials
P(\bm{X}_{1},\dots,\bm{X}_{d})
in the Wigner matrices. We use the result of the current work and this fact in the companion paper
[
48
]
to establish the mesoscopic CLT for linear eigenvalue statistics of such polynomials.
The linearization technique also extends to non-commutative rational functions of random matrices. These are used, e.g., in the study of transport properties through disordered quantum systems, such as quantum dots
[
29
]
and, therefore, the corresponding spectral CLTs can be used to determine fluctuations of transport eigenvalues.

Linearization techniques have been used extensively in free probability
[
37
,
6
,
41
,
56
]
.
Various properties of the spectrum of the LMPs of free elements have been established, e.g. in
[
36
,
61
,
10
]
.
The study of the CLTs in the context of free probability uses the theory of second order freeness (see, e.g.,
[
25
]
).

The matrix coefficients
L_{i}
of the LMP
\bm{H}
encode the correlation structure of the entries and determine the spectral density via a nonlinear matrix equation, the Matrix Dyson Equation (MDE), whose solution
M(z)
is interpreted as the expectation value
\mathbb{E}[\bm{G}(z)]
of the resolvent
\bm{G}(z):=(\bm{H}-z)^{-1}
in the large
N
limit. Incorporating this non-trivial structure of the resolvent in the calculation of the fluctuations is one of the main novelties in this work and can be extended to other models with decaying correlations, such as the Kronecker random matrices in
[
5
]
or matrices with general decaying correlations in
[
2
,
31
]
. To keep the presentation simple, however, we do not pursue this direction. Instead we show that the mesoscopic CLT (
1.1
) with
N
replaced by
nN
and in the limit
N\to\infty
holds for matrices
\bm{H}
of the form (
1.2
), where the symmetry indicator
\beta\in\{1,2\}
depends on whether the Wigner matrices
\bm{X}_{1},\dots,\bm{X}_{d}
and their coefficient matrices
L_{i}
are real symmetric or complex Hermitian.
Our proof relies solely on resolvent methods and does not involve the application of Dyson Brownian motion.
This allows to obtain the CLT on
all
mesoscopic scales for models with large zero blocks of size
O(N)
.

2
Model and results

In this paper we study random matrix models having general block correlation structures with blocks drawn from random matrix ensembles with independent identically distributed (
i.i.d.
) entries.

The model.

Fix
d,n\in\mathbb{N}
.
Let
K_{0},L_{1},\ldots,L_{d}\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
be deterministic matrices, and let
\bm{X}_{1},\ldots,\bm{X}_{d}\in\mathbb{C}^{N\times N}
,
\bm{X}_{\alpha}=\big(x_{ij}^{(\alpha)}\big)_{i,j=1}^{N}
,
1\leq\alpha\leq d
, be independent
N\times N
random matrices with
i.i.d.
entries.
Consider the random matrix model
\bm{H}^{(\beta)}\in\mathbb{C}^{nN\times nN}=\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{N\times N}
of the form

\bm{H}^{(\beta)}=K_{0}\otimes\bm{I}_{N}+\sum_{\alpha=1}^{d}\Big(L_{\alpha}\otimes\bm{X}_{\alpha}+L_{\alpha}^{*}\otimes\bm{X}_{\alpha}^{*}\Big),

(2.1)

where
\beta\in\{1,2\}
denotes the symmetry class with
\beta=1
corresponding to the real-symmetric matrices and
\beta=2
to the (complex) Hermitian matrices. We distinguish these two classes in our assumptions as follows:

•

for
\beta=1
, the structure matrices
K_{0},L_{1},\ldots,L_{d}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
are deterministic real
n\times n
matrices, and
\bm{X}_{1},\ldots,\bm{X}_{d}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}
are independent
N\times N
real random matrices with
i.i.d.
entries satisfying

\mathbb{E}\big[x_{11}^{(\alpha)}\big]=0,\quad\mathbb{E}\big[\big(x_{11}^{(\alpha)}\big)^{2}\big]=\frac{1}{N}

(2.2)

for all
1\leq\alpha\leq d
;

•

for
\beta=2
, the structure matrices
K_{0},L_{1},\ldots,L_{d}\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
are deterministic complex
n\times n
matrices, and
\bm{X}_{1},\ldots,\bm{X}_{d}\in\mathbb{C}^{N\times N}
are independent
N\times N
complex random matrices with
i.i.d.
entries satisfying

\mathbb{E}\big[x_{11}^{(\alpha)}\big]=0,\quad\mathbb{E}\big[\big|x_{11}^{(\alpha)}\big|^{2}\big]=\frac{1}{N}

(2.3)

for all
1\leq\alpha\leq d
.

The dimension
N\in\mathbb{N}
of the second tensor factor is the large parameter that tends to infinity,
\bm{I}_{N}
is the
N\times N
identity matrix, and
\otimes
denotes the tensor (or Kronecker) product.

We additionally assume that the entries of
\bm{X}_{\alpha}
have bounded moments: for each
p\in\mathbb{N}
,
p\geq 3
, there exists
c_{p}>0
such that

\max_{1\leq\alpha\leq d}\mathbb{E}\Big[\big|\sqrt{N}x_{11}^{(\alpha)}\big|^{p}\Big]\leq c_{p},

(2.4)

and in the complex Hermitian case (
\beta=2
) we assume that
\Re x_{11}^{(\alpha)}
and
\Im x_{11}^{(\alpha)}
are independent and

\mathbb{E}\Big[\big(\Re x_{11}^{(\alpha)}\big)^{2}\Big]=\frac{1}{2N},\quad\mathbb{E}\Big[\big(\Im x_{11}^{(\alpha)}\big)^{2}\Big]=\frac{1}{2N}.

(2.5)

We call
d,n,K_{0},L_{1},\ldots,L_{d}
and
c_{3},c_{4},\ldots
the
model parameters
.

Notice, that if
L_{\alpha}=L_{\alpha}^{*}
for some
\alpha\in\mathbb{N}
, then this gives rise to the term of the form

L_{\alpha}\otimes\bm{X}_{\alpha}+L_{\alpha}^{*}\otimes\bm{X}_{\alpha}^{*}=\sqrt{2}L_{\alpha}\otimes\bigg(\frac{\bm{X}_{\alpha}+\bm{X}_{\alpha}^{*}}{\sqrt{2}}\bigg),

where
(\bm{X}_{\alpha}+\bm{X}_{\alpha}^{*})/\sqrt{2}
is a (real or complex) Wigner matrix.
Whenever the symmetry class is irrelevant, we will suppress the parameter
\beta
in the notation.

Preliminary results about Kronecker random matrices.

The model
\bm{H}
defined in (
2.1
) is a special case of the Kronecker random matrices, introduced in
[
5
]
to denote a model of the type (
2.1
) in which matrices
\{\bm{X}_{\alpha}\}
are assumed to be independent with independent but not necessarily identically distributed entries.
Below we collect several properties of
\bm{H}
that are direct consequences of the corresponding results for general Kronecker random matrices from
[
5
]
and
[
4
]
.

We start by introducing the
matrix Dyson equation
(
MDE
), which, among other, characterizes the large-
N
limit of the empirical spectral measure of
\bm{H}
.
In the case of the Kronecker random matrix
\bm{H}
defined in (
2.1
), the MDE takes the form

-\frac{1}{M(z)}=zI_{n}-K_{0}+\Gamma[M(z)]

(2.6)

with unknown
M(z)\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
,
z\in\mathbb{C}_{+}:=\{z\in\mathbb{C}\,:\,\Im z>0\}
,
I_{n}\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
the identity matrix, and the operator
\Gamma:\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}\to\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
given by

\Gamma[R]:=\sum_{\alpha=1}^{d}\Big(L_{\alpha}RL_{\alpha}^{*}+L_{\alpha}^{*}RL_{\alpha}\Big)

(2.7)

for any
R\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
.
We call
\Gamma
the
self-energy
operator.
Notice that the operator
\Gamma
maps the set of positive semidefinite matrices into itself.
Therefore, by
[
42
, Theorem 2.1]
, for any
z\in\mathbb{C}_{+}
there exists a unique solution
M=M(z)
to the matrix Dyson equation (
2.6
) with positive definite imaginary part
\Im M=\frac{1}{2\mathrm{i}\,}(M-M^{*})>\penalty\ 0
.
The function
M:\mathbb{C}_{+}\to\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
is a matrix-valued Herglotz function (see, e.g.,
[
35
, Section 5]
), depends analytically on
z
, and admits the representation

M(z)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{V(dx)}{x-z},

(2.8)

where
V(dx)
is a (positive semidefinite) matrix-valued measure on
\mathbb{R}
with compact support.
The Stieltjes transform representation (
2.8
) readily follows from
[
4
, Proposition 2.1]
by taking
\mathcal{A}=\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
and the self-energy operator
\Gamma
.
In the following we will often assume that
\Gamma
satisfies the following
L
-
flatness
property:

(A)

there exist
L\in\mathbb{N}
, a matrix
Z=(z_{kl})_{k,l=1}^{n}\in\{0,1\}^{n\times n}
, and a constant
C_{\mathrm{flat}}>0
such that
z_{kk}=1
for
1\leq k\leq n
, the matrix
Z^{L}
has all entries strictly positive, and for any positive semidefinite matrix
R=(r_{kl})_{k,l=1}^{n}\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}

\Gamma\big[R\,\big]\geq C_{\mathrm{flat}}\cdot\sum_{l=1}^{n}\Big(\sum_{k=1}^{n}z_{kl}r_{kk}\Big)E_{ll}.

(2.9)

The special case of the
L
-flatness with
L=1
(in the literature often simply called the
flatness property
) requires that all entries of
Z
are equal to
1
, in which case the relation (
2.9
) takes the form

\Gamma\big[R\,\big]\geq C_{\mathrm{flat}}\Tr\big(R\big)I_{n}.

(2.10)

The
L
-flatness property was first introduced in the context of the matrix Dyson equation in an early arXiv version of
[
2
]
.
We collect certain important properties of the MDE (
2.6
) with self-energy satisfying
(A)
in Proposition
A.1
.
If
\Gamma
satisfies
(A)
, then it follows from part (i) of Proposition
A.1
that
\|M(z)\|
is uniformly bounded on
\mathbb{C}_{+}
.
This implies (see, e.g.,
[
35
, Lemma 5.4 (vi) and Lemma 5.5 (i)]
) that the measure
V(dx)
in (
2.8
) is absolutely continuous with respect to the Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{R}
and its density is given by the inverse Stieltjes transform of
M(z)
, i.e.,

V(dx)=V(x)dx,\quad\mbox{where}\quad V(x):=\lim_{y\downarrow 0}\frac{1}{\pi}\Im M(x+\mathrm{i}\,y).

(2.11)

From part (iv) of Proposition
A.1
we know that
\lim_{y\downarrow 0}M(x+\mathrm{i}\,y)
exists for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
.
Using this we extend the function
M
beyond the set
\mathbb{C}_{+}
by setting

M(z):=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\big(M(\overline{z})\big)^{*},&z\in\mathbb{C}_{-}:=\{z\in\mathbb{C}\,:\,\Im z<0\},\\[10.0pt]
\lim_{y\downarrow 0}M(x+\mathrm{i}\,y),&z=x\in\mathbb{R}.\end{array}\right.

(2.12)

With the above definition,
M(z)
is continuous on
\mathbb{C}_{+}\cup\mathbb{R}
, and
\lim_{y\downarrow 0}(M(x+\mathrm{i}\,y)-M(x-\mathrm{i}\,y))=2\mathrm{i}\,\Im M(x)=2\pi\mathrm{i}\,V(x)
.

We define the empirical spectral measure of
\bm{H}
by

\mu_{\bm{H}}:=\frac{1}{nN}\sum_{i=1}^{nN}\delta_{\lambda_{i}},

where
\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{nN}\in\mathbb{R}
are the eigenvalues of
\bm{H}
counted with multiplicities, and
\delta_{\lambda}
denotes the Dirac measure at
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
.
Assume that the self-energy operator satisfies the
L
-flatness property
(A)
.
By specializing
[
5
, Theorem 2.7]
to
\bm{H}
and using (
2.11
) we obtain that, as
N\to\infty
, the empirical spectral measure
\mu_{\bm{H}}
converges weakly in probability to
\rho(x)dx
, where

\rho(x):=\frac{1}{n}\Tr\big(V(x)\big)=\lim_{y\to 0}\frac{1}{n\pi}\Tr\big(\Im M(x+\mathrm{i}\,y)\big).

(2.13)

We call the weak convergence
\mu_{\bm{H}}\Rightarrow\rho(x)dx
the
global law
for
\bm{H}
, and we call the function
\rho(x)
the
(self-consistent) density of states
for
\bm{H}
.
Notice that
\rho
depends only on the model parameters.

In this paper we determine the fluctuations of the linear spectral statistics of
\bm{H}^{(\beta)}
for
\beta\in\{1,2\}
on
mesoscopic
scales inside the bulk, i.e., for those
x\in\mathbb{R}
for which
0<\rho(x)<\infty
.
The following central limit theorem for linear spectral statistics is our main result.

Theorem 2.1
.

Let
\bm{H}=\bm{H}^{(\beta)}
be as in (
2.1
), and suppose that the corresponding self-energy operator
\Gamma
satisfies the
L
-flatness assumption
(A)
for some
L\in\mathbb{N}
.
Let
g\in\mathcal{C}^{2}_{c}(\mathbb{R})
be a twice continuously differentiable test function with compact support.
Then for any
\gamma\in(0,1)
and
E_{0}
satisfying
0<\rho(E_{0})<\infty
, the mesoscopic linear spectral statistic

\sum_{i=1}^{nN}\big(f_{N}(\lambda_{i})-\mathbb{E}[f_{N}(\lambda_{i})]\big)

(2.14)

with

f_{N}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R},\qquad f_{N}(x)=g\Big(N^{\gamma}(x-E_{0})\Big)

(2.15)

converges in distribution to a centered Gaussian random variable with variance

V[g]:=\frac{1}{2\beta\pi^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{(g(x)-g(y))^{2}}{(x-y)^{2}}dxdy.

(2.16)

Remark 2.2

(Comparison of
1
-flatness and
L
-flatness for
L>1
)
.

The condition (
2.10
) that defines the

-flatness does not allow any of the

to be constantly equal to

.

while

On the other hand, the

3
Complex Hermitian case

The proof of Theorem
2.1
presented in this section relies on the study of the characteristic function of the mesoscopic linear spectral statistic
\sum_{i}\big(f_{N}(\lambda_{i})-\mathbb{E}[f_{N}(\lambda_{i})]\big)=\Tr f_{N}(\bm{H})-\mathbb{E}\big[\Tr f_{N}(\bm{H})\big]
.
More precisely, we show that the characteristic function
\mathbb{E}\big[\exp\big\{\mathrm{i}\,t\big(\Tr f_{N}(\bm{H})-\mathbb{E}\big[\Tr f_{N}(\bm{H})\big]\big)\big\}\big]
converges to
\exp\{-t^{2}V[g]/2\}
, the characteristic function of a Gaussian random variable with variance (
2.16
).
This is achieved through the detailed analysis of the resolvent of
\bm{H}
, which can be related to the linear spectral statistics (
2.14
) via the Helffer-Sjöstrand formula.
This relation will be explained below in (
3.4
).
We start by introducing the necessary notation.

3.1
Notation and preliminary reductions

Denote by
\eta_{0}(N):=N^{-\gamma}
the (mesoscopic) scaling parameter.
We assume
\gamma\in(0,1)
, so that
N^{-1}\ll\eta_{0}(N)\ll 1
and
f_{N}(x)=g\big((x-E_{0})/\eta_{0}\big)
(defined in (
2.15
)) satisfies

\|f_{N}\|_{1}=\|g\|_{1}\eta_{0},\quad\|f_{N}^{\prime}\|_{1}=\|g^{\prime}\|_{1},\quad\|f_{N}^{\prime\prime}\|_{1}=\frac{\|g^{\prime\prime}\|_{1}}{\eta_{0}}.

(3.1)

We will suppress the
N
-dependence in
\eta_{0}
and
f
for brevity.

Since the mesoscopic test function
f
is localized around
E_{0}
, we can restrict our analysis to a small region around the support of
f
.
To this end, let
\sigma>0
be a sufficiently large constant satisfying
\mathrm{supp}(g)\subset[-\sigma,\sigma]
, which, in turn, implies

\mathrm{supp}(f)\subset\big[E_{0}-\delta,E_{0}+\delta\big]

with
\delta=\delta_{N}:=\sigma\eta_{0}
.
From the continuity of
\rho
and the fact that
0<\rho(E_{0})<\infty
, there exists
\theta\in(0,1)
such that

\theta\leq\rho(x)\leq\theta^{-1}\quad\mbox{for all}\quad x\in(E_{0}-2\delta,E_{0}+2\delta)

(3.2)

for
N
sufficiently large.
In the sequel, we will always assume that
N
is large enough for (
3.2
) to hold.

Denote by
\chi:\mathbb{R}\to[0,1]
a smooth cutoff function supported on
[-2\delta,2\delta]
and equal to
1
on
[-\delta,\delta]
, and define the almost analytic extension of
f
by

\tilde{f}(z):=(f(x)+\mathrm{i}\,yf^{\prime}(x))\chi(y)

(3.3)

with
z=x+iy
.
An integral representation formula, used in the Helffer-Sjöstrand functional calculus, expresses the value
f(\lambda)
for any
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
as

f(\lambda)=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{\mathbb{C}}\frac{\frac{\partial}{\partial\overline{z}}\tilde{f}(z)}{\lambda-z}d^{2}\!z=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{C}}\frac{\mathrm{i}\,yf^{\prime\prime}(x)\chi(y)+\mathrm{i}\,(f(x)+\mathrm{i}\,yf^{\prime}(x))\chi^{\prime}(y)}{\lambda-x-\mathrm{i}\,y}dxdy

with the standard Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{C}
denoted by
d^{2}\!z:=d\Re z\,d\Im z
.
Therefore, we rewrite the fluctuations of the linear spectral statistics (
2.14
) as

(1-\mathbb{E})\big[\Tr f(\bm{H})\big]=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{\mathbb{C}}\frac{\partial\tilde{f}(z)}{\partial\overline{z}}(1-\mathbb{E})\big[\Tr\bm{G}(z)\big]d^{2}\!z,

(3.4)

where

\bm{G}(z):=(\bm{H}-z\bm{I}_{nN})^{-1}\in\mathbb{C}^{nN\times nN}
is the resolvent of
\bm{H}
,
\bm{I}_{nN}
is the
nN\times nN
identity matrix and we used the shorthand notation
(1-\mathbb{E})[X]:=X-\mathbb{E}[X]
for any random variable
X
.
The above representation, with resolvent
\bm{G}(z)
appearing on the right-hand side of (
3.4
), allows to exploit the properties of the resolvents of Kronecker random matrices, in particular, the local law.

To state the local law for Kronecker random matrices, we use the following notation.
For any matrix
\bm{R}\in\mathbb{C}^{\,nN\times nN}
we denote its (left) matrix coefficients
\{R_{ij}\}_{1\leq i,j\leq N}
with respect to the standard basis of
\mathbb{C}^{N\times N}
via the identity

\bm{R}=\sum_{i,j=1}^{N}R_{ij}\otimes\bm{E}_{ij},\quad R_{ij}\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n},

where
\bm{E}_{ij}=\big(\delta_{ki}\delta_{jl}\big)_{k,l=1}^{N}\in\mathbb{C}^{N\times N}
,
\delta_{kl}
is the Kronecker delta, and
\{\bm{E}_{ij}\}_{1\leq i,j\leq N}
is the standard basis of
\mathbb{C}^{N\times N}
.
For example, the collection
\{G_{ij}(z)\}_{1\leq i,j\leq N}
gives the matrix coefficients of the resolvent
\bm{G}(z)\in\mathbb{C}^{\,nN\times nN}
through the identity

\bm{G}(z)=\sum_{i,j=1}^{N}G_{ij}(z)\otimes\bm{E}_{ij},\quad G_{ij}(z)\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}.

(3.5)

We also need the notion of
stochastic domination
.
For two sequences of nonnegative random variables
\bm{\Phi}:=(\Phi_{N})_{N\in\mathbb{N}}
and
\bm{\Psi}:=(\Psi_{N})_{N\in\mathbb{N}}
we say that
\bm{\Phi}
is
stochastically dominated
by
\bm{\Psi}
, denoted
\bm{\Phi}\prec\bm{\Psi}
, if for any
\varepsilon>0
small and
D\in\mathbb{N}
there exists
C_{\varepsilon,D}>0
such that

\mathbb{P}\Big[\Phi_{N}\geq N^{\varepsilon}\Psi_{N}\Big]\leq\frac{C_{\varepsilon,D}}{N^{D}}

(3.6)

holds for all
N\in\mathbb{N}
.
If
\bm{\Phi}
and
\bm{\Psi}
are deterministic,
\bm{\Phi}\prec\bm{\Psi}
means that for any
\varepsilon>0
there exists
C_{\varepsilon}>0
such that
\bm{\Phi}\leq C_{\varepsilon}N^{\varepsilon}\bm{\Psi}
.
Finally, if there exists
C>0
such that
\bm{\Phi}\leq C\bm{\Psi}
uniformly for all
N
, then we denote this as
\bm{\Phi}\lesssim\bm{\Psi}
.
We write
\bm{\Phi}\sim\bm{\Psi}
if
\bm{\Phi}\lesssim\bm{\Psi}
and
\bm{\Psi}\lesssim\bm{\Phi}
.

We now state a result that will be used repeatedly throughout this paper to analyze the right-hand side of (
3.4
).

Proposition 3.1

(Optimal bulk local law for
\bm{H}
)
.

Suppose that the self-energy operator
\Gamma
satisfies the
L
-flatness property
(A)
.
Then for any
\nu\in(0,1)
and
\theta\in(0,1)

\max_{1\leq i,j\leq N}\|G_{ij}(z)-\delta_{ij}M(z)\|\prec\frac{1}{\sqrt{N\Im z}}

(3.7)

and

\Big\|\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}G_{ii}(z)-M(z)\Big\|\prec\frac{1}{N\Im z}

(3.8)

uniformly on the set
\{z\,:\,\theta<\rho\,(\Re z)<\theta^{-1},\,\Im z\geq N^{-1+\nu}\}
, where
M(z)
is the solution to the MDE (
2.6
),
\rho
is the corresponding self-consistent density of states defined in (
2.13
), and
\|\cdot\|
denotes the matrix norm of
G_{ij}(z)\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
induced by the Euclidean norm on
\mathbb{C}^{n}
.

Proof.

The bounds (
3.7
) and (
3.8
) are an immediate consequence of
[
5
, Lemma B.1]
and Proposition
A.1
.
Indeed, it has been proven in
[
5
, Lemma B.1]
that (
3.7
) and (
3.8
) hold for all
z\in\mathbb{C}_{+}
such that
\Im z\geq N^{-1+\nu}
and

(i)

\|M(z)\|
is bounded, and

(ii)

the
stability operator
defined for any
R\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
by

R\mapsto R-M(z)\Gamma[R]M(z)

(3.9)

is invertible.

Parts (i) and (iii) of Proposition
A.1
establish the boundedness of
\|M(z)\|
(as well as
\|(M(z))^{-1}\|
) uniformly on
\mathbb{C}_{+}
, and the boundedness of the inverse of the stability operator (
3.9
) uniformly on the set
\{z\,:\,\theta<\rho\,(\Re z)<\theta^{-1},\,\Im z\geq 0\}
, which together imply (
3.7
) and (
3.8
).
∎

Notice that the constants
C_{\varepsilon,D}
in (
3.7
) and (
3.8
) that are hidden in the notion of stochastic domination depend on the model parameters and additionally on
\nu
,
\theta
.
We call (
3.7
) the
entry-wise local law
and (
3.8
) the
averaged local law
for
\bm{H}
.
Local law bounds (
3.7
) and (
3.8
) provide us with the necessary control of the resolvent
\bm{G}(z)
for the spectral parameters
z
very close to the real line, namely for
|\Im z|\geq N^{-1+\nu}
for arbitrarily small
\nu\in(0,1)
.

In order to establish the weak convergence of the random variable (
2.14
) to a centered Gaussian random variable with variance (
2.16
), we consider the characteristic function of
(1-\mathbb{E})[\Tr f(\bm{H})]
.
Define

e(t):=e^{\mathrm{i}\,t(1-\mathbb{E})[\Tr f(\bm{H})]}=e^{\frac{\mathrm{i}\,t}{\pi}\int_{\mathbb{C}}\frac{\partial\tilde{f}(z)}{\partial\overline{z}}(1-\mathbb{E})[\Tr\bm{G}(z)]d^{2}\!z},

(3.10)

where on the right-hand side we used the representation (
3.4
).
As the first step of the analysis of (
3.10
) we show that it is enough to consider the integral over a small neighborhood around
E_{0}
.
Moreover, removing a sufficiently narrow strip around the real line in the integral in (
3.10
) does not change the limiting value of
e(t)
and its expectation.
More precisely, denote

\Omega=\Omega_{N}:=\{z\in\mathbb{C}\,:\,|\Re z-E_{0}|<\delta,N^{-\tau}\eta_{0}<|\Im z|<2\delta\},

where
\tau\in\big(0,1\big)
is a sufficiently small constant.
For each statement in this and subsequent sections we will indicate how small
\tau
should be compared to
\gamma
.
The following holds.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
\gamma\in\big(0,1\big)
,
\tau\in\big(0,(1-\gamma)\big)
and

\mathfrak{e}(t):=e^{\frac{\mathrm{i}\,t}{\pi}\int_{\Omega}\frac{\partial\tilde{f}(z)}{\partial\overline{z}}(1-\mathbb{E})[\Tr\bm{G}(z)]d^{2}\!z}.

(3.11)

Then

\big|\mathbb{E}[e(t)]-\mathbb{E}[\mathfrak{e}(t)]\big|\prec|t|\|g^{\prime\prime}\|_{1}N^{-\tau}.

The proof of this lemma follows the lines of the similar result from
[
51
, Section 4.2]
with only minor changes, therefore, we omit it in the present work.
From Lemma
3.2
we see that
\mathbb{E}[\mathfrak{e}(t)]
and the characteristic function of the mesoscopic linear spectral statistic
\mathbb{E}[e(t)]
coincide in the limit
N\to\infty
.
In the remainder of this section we will study
\mathbb{E}[\mathfrak{e}(t)]
.
Notice that the local laws (
3.7
)-(
3.8
) hold for
|\Im z|\gg N^{-1}
, therefore, working with
\mathfrak{e}(t)
makes it possible to apply the local laws for
\bm{G}(z)
for all
z
in the domain of integration
\Omega
in (
3.11
).

3.2
Computing
\mathbb{E}[\mathfrak{e}(t)]
for
\beta=2

In this section we obtain an approximate equation for
\mathbb{E}[\mathfrak{e}(t)]
in the complex Hermitian case.
The derivation is based on direct algebraic computations, and the obtained equation will be further analyzed and refined in the subsequent sections.

The main tool that will be used throughout this section is the cumulant expansion formula.
Denote by
\bm{W}=\bm{H}-\mathbb{E}[\bm{H}]
the fluctuation matrix of
\bm{H}
from (
2.1
) with complex i.i.d. matrices
\bm{X}_{\alpha}
in the second tensor factor, i.e.

\bm{W}:=\sum_{\alpha=1}^{d}\bigg(L_{\alpha}\otimes\bm{X}_{\alpha}+L_{\alpha}^{*}\otimes\bm{X}_{\alpha}^{*}\bigg).

(3.12)

For any differentiable function
\mathscr{F}:\mathbb{C}^{nN\times nN}\to\mathbb{C}^{nN\times nN}
we define the directional derivative of
\mathscr{F}(\bm{W})
with respect to
\bm{W}=(w_{ij})_{i,j=1}^{nN}
in the direction
\bm{R}=(r_{ij})_{i,j=1}^{nN}\in\mathbb{C}^{nN\times nN}
as

\nabla_{\bm{R}}\mathscr{F}(\bm{W}):=\sum_{i,j=1}^{nN}\frac{\partial\mathscr{F}(\bm{W})}{\partial\,w_{ij}}\,r_{ij}.

(3.13)

We also define the partial trace operator
\mathrm{Id}_{n}\otimes\Tr_{N}:\mathbb{C}^{nN\times nN}\to\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
acting as
(\mathrm{Id}_{n}\otimes\Tr_{N})\big[A\otimes\bm{B}\big]=A\Tr\,(\bm{B})
for any
A\otimes\bm{B}\in\mathbb{C}^{nN\times nN}=\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{N\times N}
.
Then the following holds.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
\tau\in\big(0,\min\{\gamma,(1-\gamma)\}\big)
.
Denote
\mathscr{F}_{1}(\bm{W}):=\bm{G}(z)
and
\mathscr{F}_{2}(\bm{W}):=\bm{G}(z)\,\mathfrak{e}(t)
.
Then for
\star\in\{1,2\}
we have

\mathbb{E}\Big[\bm{W}\mathscr{F}_{\star}(\bm{W})\Big]=\mathbb{E}\Big[\widetilde{\bm{W}}\,\nabla_{\widetilde{\bm{W}}}\mathscr{F}_{\star}(\bm{W})\Big]+\mathcal{D}_{\star}(z),

(3.14)

where
\widetilde{\bm{W}}
is an independent copy of
\bm{W}
and the error terms
\mathcal{D}_{\star}(z)
are analytic in
z
and satisfy the bounds

\Big\|\mathcal{D}_{\star}(z)\Big\|_{\max}=O_{\prec}\bigg(\big(1+|t|^{3}\big)\frac{N^{5\tau/2}}{N\sqrt{\eta_{0}}}\bigg)

(3.15)

uniformly for
z\in\Omega
.

The proof of Lemma
3.3
relies on the cumulant expansion formula for real random variables (see, e.g.,
[
60
]
) and is presented in Appendix
B
.
Below we will see that applying formula (
3.14
) gives rise to the operator
\mathscr{S}:\mathbb{C}^{nN\times nN}\to\mathbb{C}^{nN\times nN}
acting as

\mathscr{S}[\bm{R}]:=\mathbb{E}\big[\bm{W}\bm{R}\,\bm{W}\big]

(3.16)

for any matrix
\bm{R}\in\mathbb{C}^{nN\times nN}
.
The operator
\mathscr{S}
can be written in terms of the self-energy operator
\Gamma
and the partial trace operator
\mathrm{Id}_{n}\otimes\Tr_{N}
.
Indeed, for any
\bm{R}=\sum_{i,j=1}^{N}R_{ij}\otimes\bm{E}_{ij}
with
R_{ij}\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
we have that

\displaystyle\mathscr{S}[\bm{R}]=\mathbb{E}\big[\bm{W}\bm{R}\,\bm{W}\big]=\Gamma\Big[\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}R_{jj}\Big]\otimes\bm{I}_{N}=\frac{1}{N}\Gamma\Big[(\mathrm{Id}_{n}\otimes\Tr_{N})\big[\bm{R}\big]\Big]\otimes\bm{I}_{N},

(3.17)

where

(\mathrm{Id}_{n}\otimes\Tr_{N})\big[\bm{R}\big]=\sum_{i,j=1}^{N}(\mathrm{Id}_{n}\otimes\Tr_{N})\big[R_{ij}\otimes\bm{E}_{ij}\big]=\sum_{j=1}^{N}R_{jj}.

It will be often convenient to decompose taking the trace on
\mathbb{C}^{nN\times nN}
into two steps, first taking the partial trace
(\mathrm{Id}_{n}\otimes\Tr_{N})
, and then applying the trace on the smaller space
\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
.

Denote
\bm{M}(z):=M(z)\otimes\bm{I}_{N}\in\mathbb{C}^{nN\times nN}
.
Then
\mathscr{S}[\bm{M}(z)]=\Gamma[M(z)]\otimes\bm{I}_{N}
, and thus for any
N\in\mathbb{N}
the function
\bm{M}(z)
satisfies the equation

-\frac{1}{\bm{M}(z)}=z\bm{I}_{nN}-\bm{K}_{0}+\mathscr{S}\big[\bm{M}(z)\big]

(3.18)

with
\bm{K}_{0}:=K_{0}\otimes\bm{I}_{N}
.
Equation (
3.18
) is a Dyson equation with a positivity preserving self-energy operator (
3.17
) that admits the solution
\bm{M}(z)=M(z)\otimes\bm{I}_{N}
for any
z\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathbb{R}
and
N\in\mathbb{N}
.

For any
r\in\mathbb{N}
and
T\in\mathbb{C}^{r\times r}
, denote by
\mathcal{C}_{T}
the operator acting on
\mathbb{C}^{\,r\times r}
as the multiplication by
T
from the left and from the right

\mathcal{C}_{T}\big[R\,\big]=TR\,T.

(3.19)

If
T
is invertible, then so is
\mathcal{C}_{T}
, and
\mathcal{C}_{T}^{-1}=\mathcal{C}_{T^{-1}}
.
For
z\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathbb{R}
and
N\in\mathbb{N}
, we define the operators

\mathscr{B}_{z}:=\mathcal{C}_{\bm{M}(z)}^{-1}-\mathscr{S},\qquad\mathcal{B}_{z}:=\mathcal{C}_{M(z)}^{-1}-\Gamma

(3.20)

acting on
\mathbb{C}^{nN\times nN}
and
\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
correspondingly.
Notice that the composition
\mathcal{C}_{M(z)}\mathcal{B}_{z}
gives the stability operator for the Dyson equation (
2.6
) introduced in (
3.9
).
Similarly, the composition
\mathcal{C}_{\bm{M}(z)}\mathscr{B}_{z}=\mathrm{Id}-\mathcal{C}_{\bm{M}(z)}\mathscr{S}
is the stability operator for the Dyson equation (
3.18
).
As mentioned in Section
3.1
, the inverse of the stability operator (
3.9
) is uniformly bounded in the operator norm on the set
\{z\,:\,\theta<\rho\,(\Re z)<\theta^{-1},\,\Im z\geq 0\}
for any
\theta\in(0,1)
.
This, together with the boundedness of
\|M(z)\|
and
\|M^{-1}(z)\|
established in part (i) of Proposition
A.1
and the extension of
M(z)
to
\mathbb{C}
defined in (
2.12
), implies that for any
\theta,\nu\in(0,1)
and any
C>0

\sup\{\|\mathcal{B}_{z}^{-1}\|\,:\,\theta<\rho\,(\Re z)<\theta^{-1},\,|z|\leq C\}\lesssim 1.

(3.21)

From (
3.17
) and (
3.20
) we have that for any
\bm{R}\in\mathbb{C}^{nN\times nN}

(\mathrm{Id}_{n}\otimes\Tr_{N})\,\mathscr{B}_{z}\big[\bm{R}\big]=\mathcal{B}_{z}\,(\mathrm{Id}_{n}\otimes\Tr_{N})\big[\bm{R}\big].

(3.22)

Now we define the adjoints of the operators
\mathscr{B}_{z}
and
\mathcal{B}_{z}
(introduced in (
3.9
)).
To this end, for any
r\in\mathbb{N}
we equip
\mathbb{C}^{r\times r}
with the scalar product

\langle S,T\rangle=\frac{1}{r}\Tr\big(S^{*}T\big)

(3.23)

for all
S,T\in\mathbb{C}^{r\times r}
.
We denote by
\mathcal{B}^{*}
and
\mathscr{B}^{*}
the adjoints of
\mathcal{B}
and
\mathscr{B}
with respect to the corresponding scalar products (
3.23
).
We also introduce the notation for the normalized trace functional
\langle T\rangle:=\langle I,T\rangle=\frac{1}{r}\Tr\big(T\big)
.

We proceed to the analysis of
\mathbb{E}[\mathfrak{e}(t)]
.

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
\gamma\in(0,1)
and
\tau\in\big(0,\min\{\gamma,(1-\gamma)\}/7\big)
. Then

\frac{d}{dt}\mathbb{E}[\mathfrak{e}(t)]=-\frac{t}{\pi^{2}}\int_{\Omega\times\Omega}\frac{\partial\tilde{f}(z)}{\partial\overline{z}}\frac{\partial\tilde{f}(\zeta)}{\partial\overline{\zeta}}\frac{\partial}{\partial\zeta}\mathbb{E}\Big[\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\Tr\Big(E_{ji}\,S_{ij}(z,\zeta)\Big)\mathfrak{e}(t)\Big]d^{2}\!\zeta d^{2}\!z+\mathcal{E}_{1}

(3.24)

where
|\mathcal{E}_{1}|\prec N^{-\tau}
and

S_{ij}(z,\zeta):=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k,l=1}^{N}G_{lk}(z)\frac{1}{M(z)}\mathcal{B}_{z}^{-1}[I_{n}]\,\Gamma[E_{ij}]G_{kl}(\zeta).

Proof.

We start by differentiating the function
\mathfrak{e}(t)
in (
3.11
) with respect to
t

\frac{d}{dt}\mathbb{E}[\mathfrak{e}(t)]=\frac{\mathrm{i}\,}{\pi}\int_{\Omega}\frac{\partial\tilde{f}(z)}{\partial\overline{z}}\mathbb{E}\big[\mathfrak{e}(t)(1-\mathbb{E})[\Tr\bm{G}(z)]\big]d^{2}\!z.

(3.25)

Recall that
\bm{G}(z)=(\bm{W}+\bm{K}_{0}-zI_{nN})^{-1}
with
\bm{K}_{0}=K_{0}\otimes\bm{I}_{N}
and
\bm{W}
defined in (
3.13
), which implies the following trivial identities

(z\bm{I}_{nN}-\bm{K}_{0})(1-\mathbb{E})[\bm{G}(z)]=(1-\mathbb{E})\big[(z\bm{I}_{nN}-\bm{K}_{0})\bm{G}(z)\big]=(1-\mathbb{E})\big[\bm{W}\bm{G}(z)\big].

(3.26)

The resolvent matrix
\bm{G}(z)
is an analytic function of
\bm{W}
satisfying
\|\bm{G}(z)\|\leq|\Im z|^{-1}
for all
z\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathbb{R}
.
Moreover, for any
\bm{R}\in\mathbb{C}^{nN\times nN}

\nabla_{\bm{R}}\,\bm{G}(z)=-\bm{G}(z)\bm{R}\,\bm{G}(z).

(3.27)

We recall that
\nabla_{\bm{R}}=\sum_{i,j}r_{ij}\partial_{ij}
denotes the directional derivative with respect to
\bm{W}
in the direction
\bm{R}=(r_{ij})
.
Therefore, by applying the cumulant expansion formula (
3.14
) with
\star=1

and taking separately the partial expectation with respect to
\widetilde{\bm{W}}
we get

\mathbb{E}[\bm{W}\bm{G}(z)]=-\mathbb{E}[\widetilde{\bm{W}}\bm{G}(z)\widetilde{\bm{W}}\bm{G}(z)]+\mathcal{D}_{1}(z)=-\mathbb{E}\big[\mathscr{S}[\bm{G}(z)]\,\bm{G}(z)\big]+\mathcal{D}_{1}(z),

(3.28)

where the operator
\mathscr{S}:\mathbb{C}^{nN\times nN}\to\mathbb{C}^{nN\times nN}
was defined in (
3.16
).
Similarly, using (
3.14
) for
\star=2
and (
3.27
) we have that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Big[\bm{W}\bm{G}(z)\mathfrak{e}(t)\Big]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\Big[\widetilde{\bm{W}}\nabla_{\widetilde{\bm{W}}}\big(\bm{G}(z)\mathfrak{e}(t)\big)\Big]+\mathcal{D}_{2}(z)

(3.29)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\Big[\widetilde{\bm{W}}\nabla_{\widetilde{\bm{W}}}\big(\bm{G}(z)\big)\mathfrak{e}(t)\Big]+\mathbb{E}\Big[\widetilde{\bm{W}}\bm{G}(z)\nabla_{\widetilde{\bm{W}}}\big(\mathfrak{e}(t)\big)\Big]+\mathcal{D}_{2}(z)

\displaystyle=-\mathbb{E}\Big[\mathscr{S}[\bm{G}(z)]\bm{G}(z)\mathfrak{e}(t)\Big]+\mathbb{E}\Big[\widetilde{\bm{W}}\bm{G}(z)\nabla_{\widetilde{\bm{W}}}\big(\mathfrak{e}(t)\big)\Big]+\mathcal{D}_{2}(z).

Combining (
3.28
) and (
3.29
) yields

\mathbb{E}\Big[\mathfrak{e}(t)\big(1-\mathbb{E}\big)\big[\bm{W}\bm{G}\big]\Big]=-\mathbb{E}\Big[\mathfrak{e}(t)\big(1-\mathbb{E}\big)\big[\mathscr{S}[\bm{G}]\,\bm{G}\big]\Big]+\mathbb{E}\Big[\widetilde{\bm{W}}\bm{G}\,\nabla_{\widetilde{\bm{W}}}\big(\mathfrak{e}(t)\big)\Big]+\mathcal{E}_{2},

(3.30)

where we suppressed the argument
z
for brevity, and defined the error matrix

(3.36)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Big[\widetilde{\bm{W}}\bm{G}(z)\nabla_{\widetilde{\bm{W}}}\big(\mathfrak{e}(t)\big)\Big]

After plugging (

(3.40)

With the Hilbert space structure on

\Tr\Big(\mathcal{B}_{z}^{-1}\big(\mathrm{Id}_{n}\otimes\Tr_{N}\big)\Big[\widetilde{\bm{W}}\bm{G}(z)\frac{1}{\bm{M}(z)}\Big]\Big)=\Tr\bigg(\Big(\big(\mathscr{B}_{z}^{\,-1}\big)^{*}\big[\bm{I}_{nN}\big]\Big)^{*}\,\widetilde{\bm{W}}\bm{G}(z)\frac{1}{\bm{M}(z)}\bigg),

where we used that

(3.41)

following from the definitions (

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\widetilde{\bm{W}}}\Big[\Tr\Big(\mathscr{B}_{z}^{-1}\Big[\widetilde{\bm{W}}\bm{G}(z)\frac{1}{\bm{M}(z)}\Big]\Big)\Tr\Big(\widetilde{\bm{W}}\bm{G}(\zeta)\Big)\Big]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\sum_{p,q=1}^{N}\Tr\Big((E_{ji}\otimes\bm{E}_{qp})\bm{G}(z)\frac{1}{\bm{M}(z)}\big((\mathscr{B}_{z}^{*})^{-1}[\bm{I}_{nN}]\big)^{*}\mathscr{S}[E_{ij}\otimes\bm{E}_{pq}]\bm{G}(\zeta)\Big),

(3.45)

In (

(see (

From (

\mathscr{S}[E_{ij}\otimes\bm{E}_{pq}]=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}0,&\mbox{ if }p\neq q,\\
N^{-1}\Gamma[E_{ij}]\otimes\bm{I}_{N},&\mbox{ if }p=q.\end{array}\right.

(3.46)

Combining (

\displaystyle h_{1}(z,\zeta)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\Big[\Tr\Big(\mathscr{B}_{z}^{-1}\Big[\widetilde{\bm{W}}\bm{G}(z)\frac{1}{\bm{M}(z)}\Big]\Big)\Tr\Big(\widetilde{\bm{W}}\bm{G}(\zeta)\Big)\mathfrak{e}(t)\Big]

(3.47)

Since

\big((\mathcal{B}_{z}^{*})^{-1}[I_{n}]\big)^{*}=\mathcal{B}_{z}^{-1}[I_{n}]

Together with (

|\mathcal{E}_{1}|\prec N^{-\tau}
, where

\|\mathcal{E}_{2}(z)\|_{\max}=O_{\prec}\big((1+|t|^{3})N^{5\tau/2}N^{-1}\eta_{0}^{-1/2}\big)

(3.48)

uniformly for

.
Next we use the local laws (

) and (
3.8
) to estimate the error term
\mathcal{E}_{4}
from (

) and
\mathcal{E}_{3}
from (

By (

\mathscr{S}[\bm{M}(z)-\bm{G}(z)]=\Gamma\Big[M(z)-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}G_{jj}(z)\Big]\otimes\bm{I}_{N}.

Therefore, we can rewrite the term appearing in the definition of

\mathscr{S}[\bm{M}(z)-\bm{G}(z)]\big(\bm{M}(z)-\bm{G}(z)\big)\\

\Big\|(\mathrm{Id}_{n}\otimes\Tr_{N})\Big[\mathscr{S}[\bm{M}(z)-\bm{G}(z)]\big(\bm{M}(z)-\bm{G}(z)\big)\Big]\Big\|\prec\frac{1}{N(\Im z)^{2}}

uniformly on

.

Using this, the bound on

.

\leq\big(\|g\|_{1}+\|g^{\prime}\|_{1}\big)\Big(\frac{N^{2\tau}}{N\eta_{0}}+N^{5\tau/2}\sqrt{\eta_{0}}\Big).

3.3
Equation for
S_{ij}(z,\zeta)

Our next step is to analyze the term

S_{ij}(z,\zeta):=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k,l=1}^{N}G_{lk}(z)\mathcal{B}_{z}^{-1}[I_{n}]M(z)\Gamma[E_{ij}]G_{kl}(\zeta)

appearing in (
3.24
).
It will be convenient to consider a more general quantity of the form

G^{B}(z,\zeta):=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k,l=1}^{N}G_{lk}(z)BG_{kl}(\zeta)

(3.54)

with an arbitrary fixed deterministic
B\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
.
We can then specialize
G^{B}(z,\zeta)
to
S_{ij}(z,\zeta)
by taking
B=\mathcal{B}_{z}^{-1}[I_{n}]M(z)\Gamma[E_{ij}]
.
We start by showing that
G^{B}(z,\zeta)
satisfies the following self-consistent equation with random error term.
The derivation is reminiscent of the proofs used in some earlier works on the mesoscopic CLT for random matrices with independent entries (e.g.,
[
54
, Lemma 4.3]
,
[
50
, Proposition 5.1 and Lemma 5.2]
or
[
58
, Theorem 3.2]
).

Lemma 3.5
.

Let
\gamma\in(0,1)
and
\tau\in(0,1-\gamma)
. Then for any
B\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}

G^{B}(z,\zeta)=M(z)BM(\zeta)+M(z)\Gamma\big[G^{B}(z,\zeta)\big]M(\zeta)+\mathcal{E}_{1}^{B}(z,\zeta)

(3.55)

with the error term
\mathcal{E}_{1}^{B}(z,\zeta)
being analytic on
\Omega\times\Omega
and satisfying

\mathbb{E}[\|\mathcal{E}_{1}^{B}(z,\zeta)\|]\prec\frac{1}{N^{1/2}(\min\{|\Im z|,|\Im\zeta|\})^{3/2}}.

(3.56)

uniformly for
(z,\zeta)\in\Omega\times\Omega
.

Proof.

Similarly as in (
3.5
), we define the (left) matrix coefficients
\{W_{ij}\}_{1\leq i,j\leq N}
via
\bm{W}=\sum_{i,j=1}^{N}W_{ij}\otimes\bm{E}_{ij}
with
W_{ij}\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
.
Directly from the definition (
3.12
) we get

W_{ij}=\sum_{\alpha=1}^{d}\bigg(L_{\alpha}\,x^{(\alpha)}_{ij}+L_{\alpha}^{*}\,\overline{x}^{\,(\alpha)}_{ji}\bigg),

(3.57)

where
x^{(\alpha)}_{ij}
are the entries of
\bm{X}_{\alpha}
.
For any
i\in\{1,\ldots,N\}
, we denote by
\bm{X}_{\alpha}^{(i)}
the random i.i.d. matrix
\bm{X}_{\alpha}
with the
i
-th row and
i
-th column removed.
To make the notation consistent and easier to follow, we index the rows and columns of
\bm{X}_{\alpha}^{(i)}
by
\{1,\ldots,N\}\setminus\{i\}
.
The resolvent of the model with removed
i
-th rows and columns in each
N\times N
block is

\bm{G}^{(i)}(z):=\bigg(K_{0}\otimes\bm{I}_{N-1}+\sum_{\alpha=1}^{d}\Big(L_{\alpha}\otimes\bm{X}_{\alpha}^{(i)}+L_{\alpha}^{*}\otimes\big(\bm{X}_{\alpha}^{(i)}\big)^{*}\Big)-z\bm{I}_{n(N-1)}\Big)^{-1},

and the corresponding (left) coefficient matrices are

\bm{G}^{(i)}(z)=\sum_{p,q\neq i}G^{(i)}_{pq}(z)\otimes\bm{E}_{pq}.

With this notation, the Schur complement formula yields

\displaystyle\frac{1}{G_{ii}(z)}=W_{ii}+K_{0}-z-\sum_{p,q\neq i}W_{ip}\,G^{(i)}_{pq}(z)\,W_{qi},

(3.58)

\displaystyle G_{ij}(z)=-G_{ii}(z)\sum_{p\neq i}W_{ip}\,G^{(i)}_{pj}(z)=-\sum_{p\neq j}G^{(j)}_{ip}(z)W_{pj}\,G_{jj}(z)

(3.59)

for all
1\leq i,j\leq N
,
i\neq j
.
Moreover, for
l\neq k
we have

\displaystyle G_{lk}=M\frac{1}{G_{ll}}G_{lk}+(G_{ll}-M)\frac{1}{M+(G_{ll}-M)}G_{lk},

(3.60)

where we dropped the spectral parameter
z
for brevity.
Denote
\eta:=|\Im z|
.
From the local law (
3.7
), the bounds
\|G_{ll}-M\|\prec(N\eta)^{-1/2}
and
\|G_{lk}\|\prec(N\eta)^{-1/2}
hold uniformly on
\Omega
.
The functions
\|M\|
and
\|M^{-1}\|
are uniformly bounded on
\Omega
(see Proposition
A.1
), which, in turn, implies that
\|M\|\sim 1
uniformly on
\Omega
.
After applying the local law (
3.7
) and the first equality in (
3.59
) to the first term in (
3.60
), we arrive at

G_{lk}=-M\sum_{p\neq l}W_{lp}G^{(l)}_{pk}+O_{\prec}\Big(\frac{1}{N\eta}\Big),

(3.61)

where for any
r\in\mathbb{N}
, any (random)
\phi>0
and (random)
r\times r
matrix
\Psi
we write
\Psi=O_{\prec}(\phi)
if
\|\Psi\|\prec\phi
.
Similarly, by putting
M
on the right-hand side in (
3.60
), we get that for
k\neq l

G_{kl}=-\sum_{p\neq l}G^{(l)}_{kp}W_{pl}M+O_{\prec}\Big(\frac{1}{N\eta}\Big).

(3.62)

By the local law (
3.7
) we also see that

M\frac{1}{G_{ll}}G_{lk}=O_{\prec}\Big(\frac{1}{\sqrt{N\eta}}\Big),\quad G_{kl}\frac{1}{G_{ll}}M=O_{\prec}\Big(\frac{1}{\sqrt{N\eta}}\Big)

(3.63)

holds for
l\neq k
.
Denoting
\widehat{\eta}:=\min\{|\Im z|,|\Im\zeta|\}
with
N^{-\gamma-\tau}\lesssim\widehat{\eta}\lesssim N^{-\gamma}
on
\Omega\times\Omega
, we conclude that for
l\neq k
the identity

\displaystyle G_{lk}(z)BG_{kl}(\zeta)

\displaystyle=M(z)\sum_{p\neq l}W_{lp}G^{(l)}_{pk}(z)B\sum_{q\neq l}G^{(l)}_{kq}(\zeta)W_{ql}M(\zeta)+O_{\prec}\Big(\frac{1}{(N\widehat{\eta})^{\,3/2}}\Big)

(3.64)

holds uniformly on
(z,\zeta)\in\Omega\times\Omega
.
By construction,
G^{(l)}_{pk}
and
G^{(l)}_{kq}
are independent of
W_{lp}
and
W_{ql}
.
After taking the partial expectation
\mathbb{E}_{l}
with respect to
\{W_{lp},W_{ql}\,:\,1\leq p,q\leq N\}
(denoted below by
\mathbb{E}_{l}
) we rewrite (
3.64
) as

\displaystyle G_{lk}(z)BG_{kl}(\zeta)

\displaystyle=M(z)\Gamma\Big[\frac{1}{N}\sum_{p\neq l}G^{(l)}_{pk}(z)BG^{(l)}_{kp}(\zeta)\Big]M(\zeta)

(3.65)

\displaystyle\qquad+M(z)(1-\mathbb{E}_{l})\Big[G_{lk}(z)BG_{kl}(\zeta)\Big]M(\zeta)+O_{\prec}\Big(\frac{1}{(N\widehat{\eta})^{\,3/2}}\Big).

(3.66)

In (
3.65
) the (linear) operator
\Gamma
appears as a result of the structure of
W_{ij}
(see (
3.57
)) after applying
\mathbb{E}_{l}
and using that for the complex i.i.d. matrices
\bm{X}_{\alpha}=(x_{pq}^{(\alpha)})_{1\leq p,q\leq N}

\mathbb{E}[x_{kp}^{(\alpha_{1})}x_{ql}^{\,(\alpha_{2})}]=0,\quad\mathbb{E}[x_{kp}^{(\alpha_{1})}\overline{x}_{ql}^{\,(\alpha_{2})}]=\delta_{\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}}\delta_{kq}\delta_{pl}\frac{1}{N}.

(3.67)

From the standard identity (a consequence of the Woodbury formula)

G_{pk}^{(l)}=G_{pk}-G_{pl}\frac{1}{G_{ll}}G_{lk}

(3.68)

holding for all
l\notin\{p,k\}
, together with (
3.7
), we deduce that

G_{pk}^{(l)}=G_{pk}+O_{\prec}\Big(\frac{1}{N\eta}\Big).

(3.69)

This implies that

G^{(l)}_{pk}(z)BG^{(l)}_{kp}(\zeta)=G_{pk}(z)BG_{kp}(\zeta)+\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}O_{\prec}\big((N\widehat{\eta})^{\,-1}\big),&p=k,\\
O_{\prec}\big((N\widehat{\eta})^{\,-3/2}\big),&p\neq k.\end{array}\right.

(3.70)

Recall that we are dealing with the case
k\neq l
, and
\widehat{\eta}\gg N^{-1}
for
(z,\zeta)\in\Omega\times\Omega
.
We replace the
G^{(l)}
entries in (
3.65
) using formula (
3.70
) to get

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}\sum_{p\neq l}G^{(l)}_{pk}(z)BG^{(l)}_{kp}(\zeta)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{p=1}^{N}G_{pk}(z)BG_{kp}(\zeta)+O_{\prec}\Big(\frac{1}{(N\widehat{\eta})^{\,3/2}}\Big).

The boundedness of
M
and
\Gamma
(as an operator acting on
\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
) implies that

\displaystyle G_{lk}(z)BG_{kl}(\zeta)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}M(z)\Gamma\Big[\sum_{p=1}^{N}G_{pk}(z)BG_{kp}(\zeta)\Big]M(\zeta)

\displaystyle\qquad+M(z)(1-\mathbb{E}_{l})\Big[G_{lk}(z)BG_{kl}(\zeta)\Big]M(\zeta)+O_{\prec}\Big(\frac{1}{(N\widehat{\eta})^{\,3/2}}\Big)

holds for
k\neq l
.
By the local law (
3.7
),
\sum_{p=1}^{N}G_{pk}(z)BG_{kp}(\zeta)=O_{\prec}(1+\widehat{\eta}^{\,-1})
for any
k\in\{1,\ldots,N\}
.
Therefore, summing the above equality over
N-1
indices
l
for
l\neq k
gives

\displaystyle\sum_{l:\,l\neq k}G_{lk}(z)BG_{kl}(\zeta)=M(z)\Gamma\Big[\sum_{p=1}^{N}G_{pk}(z)BG_{kp}(\zeta)\Big]M(\zeta)

(3.71)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad+M(z)\sum_{l:\,l\neq k}(1-\mathbb{E}_{l})\Big[G_{lk}(z)BG_{kl}(\zeta)\Big]M(\zeta)+O_{\prec}\Big(\frac{1}{N^{1/2}\widehat{\eta}^{\,3/2}}\Big)

where we used that
N^{-1}+(N\widehat{\eta})^{-1}\ll N^{-1/2}\widehat{\eta}^{\,-3/2}
for
(z,\zeta)\in\Omega\times\Omega
.
Finally, in order to obtain the first term on the right hand side of (
3.55
), we use the local law (
3.7
) and apply it to the diagonal terms
G_{kk}

G_{kk}(z)BG_{kk}(\zeta)=M(z)BM(\zeta)+O_{\prec}\Big(\frac{1}{\sqrt{N\widehat{\eta}}}\Big),

(3.72)

which together with (
3.71
) gives

\displaystyle\sum_{l=1}^{N}G_{lk}(z)BG_{kl}(\zeta)

\displaystyle=M(z)BM(\zeta)+M(z)\Gamma\Big[\sum_{p=1}^{N}G_{pk}(z)BG_{kp}(\zeta)\Big]M(\zeta)

\displaystyle\qquad+M(z)\sum_{l\neq k}(1-\mathbb{E}_{l})\Big[G_{lk}(z)BG_{kl}(\zeta)\Big]M(\zeta)+O_{\prec}\Big(\frac{1}{N^{1/2}\widehat{\eta}^{\,3/2}}\Big).

(3.73)

It is left to control

D_{k}:=\sum_{l\neq k}(1-\mathbb{E}_{l})\big[G_{lk}(z)BG_{kl}(\zeta)\big]
.
For this we use the decomposition

\displaystyle D_{k}D_{k}^{*}

\displaystyle=\sum_{l:\,l\neq k}(1-\mathbb{E}_{l})\Big[G_{lk}(z)BG_{kl}(\zeta)\Big]\bigg((1-\mathbb{E}_{l})\Big[G_{lk}(z)BG_{kl}(\zeta)\Big]\bigg)^{*}

(3.74)

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}l,j:\,l,j\neq k,\\
l\neq j\end{subarray}}(1-\mathbb{E}_{l})\Big[G_{lk}(z)BG_{kl}(\zeta)\Big]\bigg((1-\mathbb{E}_{j})\Big[G_{jk}(z)BG_{kj}(\zeta)\Big]\bigg)^{*}.

(3.75)

By (
3.7
) the first term is of order
O_{\prec}(N^{-1}\widehat{\eta}^{\,-2})
.
For the second term we use (
3.68
) to see

\displaystyle(1-\mathbb{E}_{l})\Big[G_{lk}(z)BG_{kl}(\zeta)\Big]\bigg((1-\mathbb{E}_{j})\Big[G_{jk}(z)BG_{kj}(\zeta)\Big]\bigg)^{*}

\displaystyle=(1-\mathbb{E}_{l})\Big[\Big(G_{lk}^{(j)}(z)+G_{lj}(z)\frac{1}{G_{jj}(z)}G_{jk}(z)\Big)B\Big(G_{kl}^{(j)}(\zeta)+G_{kj}(\zeta)\frac{1}{G_{jj}(\zeta)}G_{jl}(\zeta)\Big)\Big]

\displaystyle\quad\times\bigg((1-\mathbb{E}_{j})\Big[\Big(G_{jk}^{(l)}(z)+G_{jl}(z)\frac{1}{G_{ll}(z)}G_{lk}(z)\Big)B\Big(G_{kj}^{(l)}(\zeta)+G_{kl}(\zeta)\frac{1}{G_{ll}(\zeta)}G_{lj}(\zeta)\Big)\Big]\bigg)^{*}.

The expectation of the summands in (
3.75
) vanishes, i.e.,

\mathbb{E}\Big[(1-\mathbb{E}_{l})\big[G_{lk}^{(j)}(z)BG_{kl}^{(j)}(\zeta)\big](1-\mathbb{E}_{j})\big[G_{jk}^{(l)}(z)BG_{kj}^{(l)}(\zeta)\big]\Big]=0.

Therefore, the expectation of
D_{k}D_{k}^{*}
can be written as a sum of at most
O(N^{2})
non-zero terms, each containing a product of at least 6 off-diagonal coordinate matrices of
\bm{G}
or
\bm{G}^{(l)}
.
By the local law (
3.7
) each such product is of order
O_{\prec}((N\widehat{\eta})^{\,-3})
.
All the terms in (
3.74
) are also deterministically bounded by a sufficiently high power of
1/\widehat{\eta}
, which, in particular, means that
\|D_{k}D_{k}^{*}\|\leq N^{C}
for some
C>0
.
Combining this with the stochastic domination estimate
D_{k}D_{k}^{*}\prec N^{-1}\widehat{\eta}^{\,-3}
(defined in (
3.6
)), we obtain an estimate for the expectation
\mathbb{E}[D_{k}D_{k}^{*}]=O_{\prec}(N^{-1}\widehat{\eta}^{\,-3})
.
Notice, that
\mathbb{E}[\|D_{k}\|^{2}_{\mathrm{HS}}]=\mathbb{E}[\Tr(D_{k}D_{k}^{*})/n]=O_{\prec}(N^{-1}\widehat{\eta}^{\,-3})
, where
\|\cdot\|_{\mathrm{HS}}
is the Hilbert-Schmidt norm on
\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
induced by the scalar product (
3.23
).

The functions
M
,
G_{ij}
,
M^{-1}
,
G_{ii}^{-1}
used in the above proof are analytic on
\Omega
.
By collecting the error terms in (
3.73
) (
O_{\prec}(N^{-1/2}\widehat{\eta}^{\,-3/2})
and
D_{k}
), taking the average over
k\in\{1,\ldots,N\}
, denoting the resulting error by
\mathcal{E}_{1}^{B}(z,\zeta)
and using the equivalence of norms on
\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
, we obtain (
3.56
), which finishes the proof.
∎

3.4
Self-consistent equation for
G^{B}(z,\zeta)

In this section we study the limiting behavior of
G^{B}(z,\zeta)
introduced in (
3.54
) through the analysis of the self-consistent equation (
3.55
).
More precisely, for any
z,\zeta\in\Omega
and
B\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
we consider the equation

M^{B}(z,\zeta)=M(z)BM(\zeta)+M(z)\Gamma\big[M^{B}(z,\zeta)\big]M(\zeta)

(3.76)

for
M^{B}(z,\zeta)
.
For any
Q_{1},Q_{2}\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
we denote by
\mathcal{C}_{Q_{1},Q_{2}}
an operator on
\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
given by

\mathcal{C}_{Q_{1},Q_{2}}[R]:=Q_{1}RQ_{2}

(3.77)

for all
R\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
.
The operator
\mathcal{C}_{M(z),M(\zeta)}
is invertible, and
\mathcal{C}_{M(z),M(\zeta)}^{-1}=\mathcal{C}_{\frac{1}{M(z)},\frac{1}{M(\zeta)}}
.
With this notation, equation (
3.76
) reads

\mathcal{B}_{z,\zeta}\big[M^{B}(z,\zeta)\big]=B,

(3.78)

where we defined
\mathcal{B}_{z,\zeta}:=\mathcal{C}_{M(z),M(\zeta)}^{-1}-\Gamma
.

If the operator
\mathcal{B}_{z,\zeta}
is invertible, then
M^{B}(z,\zeta)
is uniquely determined from (
3.78
).
Here it is sufficient to restrict the parameters
z
and
\zeta
to a small neighborhood of
E_{0}
.
Therefore, we show that
\mathcal{B}_{z,\zeta}
is invertible in a sufficiently small region around the point
(E_{0},E_{0})\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}
.
The bound for
\|\mathcal{B}_{z,\zeta}^{-1}\|
depends on whether
z
and
\zeta
belong to the same half-plane or not.
If
z
and
\zeta
are in the same half-plane, then the
\mathcal{B}_{z,\zeta}^{-1}
is bounded around
(E_{0},E_{0})
, while for
z
and
\zeta
in different half-planes the main contribution to
\mathcal{B}_{z,\zeta}^{-1}
stems from its smallest isolated eigenvalue of
\mathcal{B}_{z,\zeta}
.
The latter is analyzed using analytic perturbation theory.
Similar analysis has also been performed to establish the mesoscopic spectral CLT for Wigner-type matrices in
[
58
]
.
Denote
\Omega^{+}:=\Omega\cap\mathbb{C}_{+}
and
\Omega^{-}:=\Omega\cap\mathbb{C}_{-}
.

Lemma 3.6

(Invertibility of
\mathcal{B}_{z,\zeta}
)
.

Let
\gamma\in(0,1)
and
\tau\in(0,\gamma/2)
.
Then there exists
C>0
such that the following holds

(i)

Uniformly for
(z,\zeta)\in\big(\Omega^{+}\times\Omega^{+}\big)\cup\big(\Omega^{-}\times\Omega^{-}\big)

\big\|\,\mathcal{B}_{z,\zeta}^{\,-1}\,\big\|\leq C;

(3.79)

(ii)

Uniformly for
(z,\zeta)\in\big(\Omega^{-}\times\Omega^{+}\big)\cup\big(\Omega^{+}\times\Omega^{-}\big)

\big\|\,\mathcal{B}_{z,\zeta}^{\,-1}\,\big\|\leq\frac{C}{|z-\zeta|},

(3.80)

and the operator
\mathcal{B}_{z,\zeta}^{\,-1}
admits the decomposition

\mathcal{B}_{z,\zeta}^{\,-1}=\vartheta\,\frac{2\mathrm{i}\,}{\langle\Im M(E_{0})\rangle}\frac{1}{z-\zeta}\Im M(E_{0})\langle\Im M(E_{0}),\,\cdot\,\rangle+\mathcal{J}_{z,\zeta},

(3.81)

where
\vartheta=1
if
(z,\zeta)\in\Omega^{+}\times\Omega^{-}
,
\vartheta=-1
if
(z,\zeta)\in\Omega^{-}\times\Omega^{+}
, and
\|\mathcal{J}_{z,\zeta}\|
is uniformly bounded for
(z,\zeta)\in\big(\Omega^{-}\times\Omega^{+}\big)\cup\big(\Omega^{+}\times\Omega^{-}\big)
.

Proof.

Consider first the case (i), and assume that both
z
and
\zeta
are in the upper half-plane, i.e.,
z,\zeta\in\Omega^{+}
.
The case
z,\zeta\in\Omega^{-}
follows analogously.
For
z,\zeta\in\Omega^{+}
we write

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{z,\zeta}=\mathcal{C}^{-1}_{M(z),M(\zeta)}-\Gamma=\mathcal{B}_{z}+\mathcal{C}^{-1}_{M(z),M(\zeta)}-\mathcal{C}^{-1}_{M(z),M(z)},

(3.82)

where
\mathcal{B}_{z}
was introduced in (
3.20
).
By the definition (
3.77
), we have that

\mathcal{C}^{-1}_{M(z),M(\zeta)}-\mathcal{C}^{-1}_{M(z),M(z)}=\mathcal{C}_{\frac{1}{M(z)},\frac{1}{M(\zeta)}-\frac{1}{M(z)}}=O(|z-\zeta|)

(3.83)

from the analyticity of
M(z)^{-1}
on
\mathbb{C}_{+}
.
Therefore, using the boundedness of
\mathcal{B}_{z}^{-1}
from (
3.21
), we get (
3.79
).

Now suppose that
z
and
\zeta
are in different half-planes, and consider first the case
z\in\mathbb{C}_{-}
and
\zeta\in\mathbb{C}_{+}
.
Denote
M_{0}:=\lim_{y\downarrow 0}M(E_{0}+\mathrm{i}\,y)
for brevity.
From part (ii) of Proposition
A.1
we have that
\Im M(z)\sim\rho(z)I_{n}
uniformly on
\mathbb{C}_{+}
, which together with
\rho(E_{0})\sim 1
implies

\Im M_{0}\sim I_{n}.

(3.84)

We see that
\lim_{y\to 0}M(E_{0}+\mathrm{i}\,y)-M(E_{0}-\mathrm{i}\,y)=2\mathrm{i}\,\Im M_{0}
is a non-zero matrix, and thus
\|\mathcal{C}_{\frac{1}{M(z)},\frac{1}{M(\zeta)}-\frac{1}{M(z)}}\|=O(1)
.
Therefore, the perturbation argument used in (
3.82
) and (
3.83
) to control
\mathcal{B}_{z,\zeta}
through comparison with
\mathcal{B}_{z}
and the analyticity of
M(z)
in the upper (lower) half-plane is not applicable anymore.

Instead, we consider the perturbation of
\mathcal{B}_{z,\zeta}
around the operator
\mathcal{B}_{E_{0},E_{0}}
given by

\mathcal{B}_{E_{0},E_{0}}:=\mathcal{C}_{M_{0}^{*},M_{0}}^{-1}-\Gamma.

For convenience, define the centered variables
w:=z-E_{0}\in\mathbb{C}_{-}
,
\xi:=\zeta-E_{0}\in\mathbb{C}_{+}
, so that

\mathcal{B}_{z,\zeta}=\mathcal{B}_{E_{0}+w,E_{0}+\xi}=\mathcal{B}_{E_{0},E_{0}}+\mathcal{C}_{M(E_{0}+w),M(E_{0}+\xi)}^{-1}-\mathcal{C}_{M_{0}^{*},M_{0}}^{-1}.

For
(z,\zeta)\in\Omega^{-}\times\Omega^{+}
the variables
w
and
\xi
remain in their corresponding half-planes, the perturbation
\mathcal{C}_{M(E_{0}+w),M(E_{0}+\xi)}^{-1}-\mathcal{C}_{(M(E_{0}))^{*},M(E_{0})}^{-1}
is an analytic function in
w
and
\xi
, and thus we can apply the analytic perturbation theory to control the invertibility of
\mathcal{B}_{z,\zeta}
on
\Omega^{-}\times\Omega^{+}
.
We start by collecting the necessary properties of the spectrum of
\mathcal{B}_{E_{0},E_{0}}
.

Firstly, by taking the imaginary part of the MDE (
2.6
) at
z=E_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
we find that

\mathcal{B}_{E_{0},E_{0}}\big[\Im M_{0}\big]=0,\quad\mathcal{B}_{E_{0},E_{0}}^{*}\big[\Im M_{0}\big]=0,

(3.85)

where we used that the adjoint operator to
\mathcal{B}_{E_{0},E_{0}}
with respect to the scalar product (
3.23
) is

\mathcal{B}_{E_{0},E_{0}}^{*}=\mathcal{C}_{M_{0},M_{0}^{*}}^{-1}-\Gamma.

This means that
\mathcal{B}_{E_{0},E_{0}}
is not invertible, and the kernels of both
\mathcal{B}_{E_{0},E_{0}}
and
\mathcal{B}_{E_{0},E_{0}}^{*}
contain the eigenvector
\Im M_{0}
.
We now show that

\mathrm{dim}\big(\mathrm{ker}\big(\mathcal{B}_{E_{0},E_{0}}\big)\big)=\mathrm{dim}\big(\mathrm{ker}\big(\mathcal{B}_{E_{0},E_{0}}^{*}\big)\big)=1.

(3.86)

To this end, we use the balanced polar decomposition of
M_{0}
from
[
4
, Eq. (3.1)]

M_{0}=Q^{*}UQ

(3.87)

with unitary
U\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
and invertible
Q\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
.
Notice that the decomposition (
3.87
) is well defined since
\Im M_{0}
is (strictly) positive definite as shown in (
3.84
).
The operator
\mathcal{B}_{E_{0},E_{0}}
can be written in terms of
U
and
Q
as

\mathcal{B}_{E_{0},E_{0}}=\mathcal{C}_{Q,Q^{*}}^{-1}\mathcal{C}_{U^{*},U}^{-1}\mathcal{C}_{Q^{*},Q}^{-1}-\Gamma=\mathcal{C}_{Q,Q^{*}}^{-1}\Big(\mathcal{C}_{U^{*},U}^{-1}-\mathcal{F}\Big)\mathcal{C}_{Q^{*},Q}^{-1},

(3.88)

where
\mathcal{F}:=\mathcal{C}_{Q,Q^{*}}\Gamma\,\mathcal{C}_{Q^{*},Q}
is a self-adjoint and positivity preserving operator.
Similarly, we can rewrite the MDE (
2.6
) in terms of
U
,
Q
and
\mathcal{F}
as

-\frac{1}{U}=E_{0}QQ^{*}-QK_{0}Q^{*}+\mathcal{F}\big[U\big].

Notice that by (
3.84
) and (
3.87
) we have that
\Im U\geq cI_{n}
for some
c>0
.
Since
U
is unitary, the above equation yields

\Im U=\mathcal{F}\big[\Im U\big].

(3.89)

Let
\|\mathcal{F}\|_{2}
denote the operator norm of
\mathcal{F}
induced by the Hilbert-Schmidt norm on
\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
with the scalar product (
3.23
).
It follows from (
3.89
) and the properties (
A.20
) and (
A.22
) of
\mathcal{F}
discussed in Appendix
A
that
\|\mathcal{F}\|_{2}=1
is a simple eigenvalue of
\mathcal{F}
, and the operator
\mathcal{F}
has a spectral gap

\mathrm{Spec}(\mathcal{F})\subset[-1+\kappa,1-\kappa]\cup\{1\}

(3.90)

for some
\kappa>0
sufficiently small.
Denote by
F:=\Im U/\|\Im U\|_{\mathrm{HS}}
the normalized eigenvector of
\mathcal{F}
corresponding to the eigenvalue
\|\mathcal{F}\|_{2}=1
.
Since
F
commutes with
U
and
U^{*}
, and
\mathcal{F}
is self-adjoint, we get that

\Big(\mathcal{C}_{U^{*},U}^{-1}-\mathcal{F}\Big)\big[F\big]=0,\qquad\Big(\mathcal{C}_{U^{*},U}^{-1}-\mathcal{F}\Big)^{*}\big[F\big]=0.

Moreover, if
Y\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
is such that
\langle Y,F\rangle=0
, then (
3.90
) and the fact that
\|\mathcal{F}\|_{2}=1
is a simple eigenvalue imply that

\Big\|\Big(\mathcal{C}_{U^{*},U}^{-1}-\mathcal{F}\Big)\big[Y\big]\Big\|_{\mathrm{HS}}\geq\big\|UYU^{*}\big\|_{\mathrm{HS}}-\big\|\mathcal{F}\big[Y\big]\big\|_{\mathrm{HS}}\geq\kappa\|Y\|_{\mathrm{HS}}.

We conclude that
\mathrm{dim}\big(\mathrm{ker}\big(\mathcal{C}_{U^{*},U}^{-1}-\mathcal{F}\big)\big)=1
, which together with (
3.88
) and the invertibility of
Q
implies (
3.86
).

From (
3.85
) and (
3.86
) we know that
\mathcal{B}_{E_{0},E_{0}}
has eigenvalue
0
with geometric multiplicity
1
, and the corresponding left and right eigenvectors coincide and are equal to
\Im M_{0}
.
If we assume that the algebraic multiplicity of the eigenvalue at
0
is greater than
1
, then the corresponding Jordan chain contains a generalized eigenvector
T\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
such that
\mathcal{B}_{E_{0},E_{0}}[T]=\Im M_{0}
.
This implies that

\langle\Im M_{0},\Im M_{0}\rangle=\langle\Im M_{0},\mathcal{B}_{E_{0},E_{0}}[T]\rangle=\langle\mathcal{B}_{E_{0},E_{0}}^{*}[\Im M_{0}],T\rangle=0,

which contradicts to
\|\Im M_{0}\|_{\mathrm{HS}}\geq\theta
.
We, therefore, conclude that the algebraic multiplicity of the eigenvalue
0
of
\mathcal{B}_{E_{0},E_{0}}
is also equal to
1
.

Since zero is a simple eigenvalue, we can apply the analytic perturbation theory of non-Hermitian operators to control the spectrum of
\mathcal{B}_{z,\zeta}
.
At the same time, the dimension of the space
\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
is a fixed model parameter independent of
N
.
Therefore, we can find a sufficiently small
\varepsilon>0
such that
\mathrm{Spec}(\mathcal{B}_{E_{0},E_{0}})\cap\{v\in\mathbb{C}\,:\,|v|<\varepsilon\}=\{0\}
and

\sup_{|v|=\varepsilon}\Big(\big\|\big(\mathcal{B}_{E_{0},E_{0}}-v\mathrm{Id}\big)^{-1}\big\|+\big\|\big(\mathcal{B}_{E_{0},E_{0}}^{*}-v\mathrm{Id}\big)^{-1}\big\|\Big)\lesssim 1.

Recall that the function
M(z)
defined in (
2.12
) has a jump at
z=E_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
.
In order to apply the analytic perturbation theory, we will restrict
M(z)
to the set
\mathbb{C}_{+}
or
\mathbb{C}_{-}
depending on whether
z\in\Omega^{+}
or
z\in\Omega^{-}
, and then use part (v) of Proposition
A.1
to extend it analytically to a neighborhood of
E_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
containing
\Omega
.
Thus, for sufficiently small
\varepsilon>0
we define
M_{+}:\mathbb{C}_{+}\cup\{z\,:\,|z-E_{0}|<\varepsilon\}\to\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
such that
M_{+}(z)=M(z)
on
\mathbb{C}_{+}
and
M_{+}
is analytic on
\mathbb{C}_{+}\cup\{z\,:\,|z-E_{0}|<\varepsilon\}
.
Similarly, we define
M_{-}:\mathbb{C}_{-}\cup\{z\,:\,|z-E_{0}|<\varepsilon\}\to\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
such that
M_{-}(z)=M(z)
on
\mathbb{C}_{-}
and
M_{-}
is analytic on
\mathbb{C}_{-}\cup\{z\,:\,|z-E_{0}|<\varepsilon\}
.
In particular, if we denote
M_{0}:=\lim_{y\downarrow 0}M(E_{0}+\mathrm{i}\,y)
, then
M_{+}(E_{0})=M_{0}
and
M_{-}(E_{0})=M_{0}^{*}
.

Consider the (analytic) perturbation of
\mathcal{B}_{E_{0},E_{0}}
by
\mathcal{D}_{w,\xi}
, where

\mathcal{D}_{w,\xi}:=\mathcal{C}_{M_{+}(E_{0}+w),M_{-}(E_{0}+\xi)}^{-1}-\mathcal{C}_{(M(E_{0}))^{*},M(E_{0})}^{-1},

so that
\mathcal{B}_{z,\zeta}=\mathcal{B}_{E_{0},E_{0}}+\mathcal{D}_{w,\xi}
.
Denote by
L:=\Im M_{0}/\|\Im M_{0}\|_{\mathrm{HS}}
the (left and right) normalized eigenvector of
\mathcal{B}_{E_{0},E_{0}}
corresponding to the eigenvalue
0
.
Then it follows from the analytic perturbation theory (see, e.g.,
[
4
, Lemma C.1]
) that
\mathcal{B}_{z,\zeta}
has a unique eigenvalue inside the disk
\{v\,:\,|v|\leq\varepsilon\}
, which we denote by
\lambda_{z,\zeta}
, that satisfies

\lambda_{z,\zeta}=\langle L,\mathcal{D}_{w,\xi}[L]\rangle+O(|w|^{2}+|\xi|^{2}).

(3.91)

In order to separate the leading term in (
3.91
), we calculate the derivative
(\partial_{w}\mathcal{D}_{w,\xi},\partial_{\xi}\mathcal{D}_{w,\xi})
at
(w,\xi)=(0,0)

\big(\partial_{w}\mathcal{D}_{w,\xi},\partial_{\xi}\mathcal{D}_{w,\xi}\big)\Big|_{(w,\xi)=(0,0)}=\Big(\mathcal{C}_{-\frac{1}{M_{0}^{*}}(M^{\prime}_{0})^{*}\frac{1}{M_{0}^{*}},\frac{1}{M_{0}}}\,,\,\mathcal{C}_{\frac{1}{M_{0}^{*}},-\frac{1}{M_{0}}M^{\prime}_{0}\frac{1}{M_{0}}}\Big)

with
M^{\prime}_{0}:=\lim_{y\downarrow 0}M^{\prime}(E_{0}+\mathrm{i}\,y)
.
The first order approximation of
\mathcal{D}_{w,\xi}
gives

\lambda_{z,\zeta}=-\overline{\alpha}\,w-\alpha\,\xi+O(|w|^{2}+|\xi|^{2}),

(3.92)

where

\alpha:=\Big\langle L,\frac{1}{M_{0}^{*}}L\frac{1}{M_{0}}M^{\prime}_{0}\frac{1}{M_{0}}\Big\rangle.

We now show that
\Re\alpha=0
and
\Im\alpha\geq c>0
for some
c>0
.
For brevity, the
0
subscript will be dropped, and we will write
M:=M_{0}
and
M^{\prime}:=M^{\prime}_{0}
.

Recall that
L=\Im M/\|\Im M\|_{\mathrm{HS}}
,
\Im M
is symmetric and positive definite, and thus
\alpha\|\Im M\|_{\mathrm{HS}}^{2}
is equal to
\Big\langle\big(\Im M\big)\frac{1}{M^{*}}\big(\Im M\big)\frac{1}{M}M^{\prime}\frac{1}{M}\Big\rangle
.
After differentiating the Dyson equation (
2.6
) at
z=E_{0}
, we obtain the relation

\frac{1}{M}M^{\prime}\frac{1}{M}=I_{n}+\Gamma[M^{\prime}].

Multiplying the above equation by
\Im M
on the left and taking the normalized trace yields

\displaystyle\Big\langle\Im M\frac{1}{M}M^{\prime}\frac{1}{M}\Big\rangle=\langle\Im M\rangle+\big\langle\Im M\,\Gamma[M^{\prime}]\big\rangle=\langle\Im M\rangle+\big\langle\Gamma[\Im M]M^{\prime}\big\rangle,

(3.93)

where in the second equality we used that
\Gamma
is self-adjoint with respect to the scalar product (
3.23
).
From the imaginary part of the Dyson equation we know that

\Gamma[\Im M]=\frac{1}{M^{*}}\Im M\frac{1}{M}.

(3.94)

Plugging the above identity into (
3.93
) gives

\displaystyle\Big\langle\Im M\frac{1}{M}M^{\prime}\frac{1}{M}\Big\rangle=\langle\Im M\rangle+\Big\langle\frac{1}{M^{*}}\Im M\frac{1}{M}M^{\prime}\Big\rangle=\langle\Im M\rangle+\Big\langle\Im M\frac{1}{M}M^{\prime}\frac{1}{M^{*}}\Big\rangle.

After moving the second term on the right-hand side to the left-hand side we get that

\displaystyle\langle\Im M\rangle

\displaystyle=\Big\langle\Im M\frac{1}{M}M^{\prime}\Big(\frac{1}{M}-\frac{1}{M^{*}}\Big)\Big\rangle

(3.95)

\displaystyle=-2\mathrm{i}\,\Big\langle\Im M\frac{1}{M}M^{\prime}\Big(\frac{1}{M}\Im M\frac{1}{M^{*}}\Big)\Big\rangle=-2\mathrm{i}\,\alpha\|\Im M\|^{2}_{\mathrm{HS}}.

Since
\langle\Im M\rangle\geq\theta
, we conclude that

\alpha=\frac{\mathrm{i}\,}{2}\frac{\langle\Im M\rangle}{\|\Im M\|^{2}_{\mathrm{HS}}},

and thus
\Re\alpha=0
and
\Im\alpha>0
.
In particular, (
3.92
) becomes

\lambda_{z,\zeta}=\frac{\mathrm{i}\,}{2}\frac{\langle\Im M\rangle}{\|\Im M\|^{2}_{\mathrm{HS}}}\,(w-\xi)+O(|w|^{2}+|\xi|^{2}).

(3.96)

The assumption
\tau<\gamma/2
ensures that
(|w|^{2}+|\xi|^{2})=o(|w-\xi|)
on
\Omega\times\Omega
, and thus (
3.96
) gives the leading term in the expansion of
\lambda_{z,\zeta}
.

Finally, using analytic functional calculus we can decompose
\mathcal{B}_{z,\zeta}^{-1}
on
\Omega^{-}\times\Omega^{+}
as

\mathcal{B}_{z,\zeta}^{-1}=\frac{1}{\lambda_{z,\zeta}}\mathcal{P}_{z,\zeta}+\mathcal{J}_{z,\zeta}^{(1)}

(3.97)

with

\mathcal{P}_{z,\zeta}:=-\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}\,}\int_{|v|=\varepsilon}\Big(\mathcal{B}_{z,\zeta}-v\cdot\mathrm{Id}\Big)^{-1}dv,\quad\mathcal{J}_{z,\zeta}^{(1)}:=-\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}\,}\int_{\Sigma}\frac{1}{v}\Big(\mathcal{B}_{z,\zeta}-v\cdot\mathrm{Id}\Big)^{-1}dv,

where
\Sigma
is a contour encircling the eigenvalues of
\mathcal{B}_{z,\zeta}
located away from the
\varepsilon
-disk around zero and not crossing the circle
\{|v|=\varepsilon\}
.
By analytic perturbation theory,
\mathcal{J}_{z,\zeta}^{(1)}
is bounded for all
(z,\zeta)\in\Omega^{-}\times\Omega^{+}
.
Moreover, by using (
3.96
) and perturbation theory for the eigenvectors (see, e.g.,
[
4
, Lemma C.1]
) we can rewrite the first term in (
3.97
) as

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\lambda_{z,\zeta}}\mathcal{P}_{z,\zeta}=-\frac{2\mathrm{i}\,}{\langle\Im M_{0}\rangle}\frac{\langle\Im M_{0},\,\cdot\,\rangle}{z-\zeta}\Im M_{0}+\mathcal{J}_{z,\zeta}^{(2)}

with
\mathcal{J}_{z,\zeta}^{(2)}
uniformly bounded on
\Omega^{-}\times\Omega^{+}
.
Setting
\mathcal{J}:=\mathcal{J}^{(1)}+\mathcal{J}^{(2)}
establishes (
3.80
) and (
3.81
) and finishes the proof of the lemma for
(z,\zeta)\in\Omega^{-}\times\Omega^{+}
.

In the case when
(z,\zeta)\in\Omega^{+}\times\Omega^{-}
the equation (
3.92
) becomes

\lambda_{z,\zeta}=-\alpha\,w-\overline{\alpha}\,\xi+O(|w|^{2}+|\xi|^{2}),

while the remaining part of the proof can be repeated line by line.
This completes the proof.
∎

It follows from the above lemma that for any
(z,\zeta)\in\Omega\times\Omega
the equation (
3.76
) has a unique solution given by

M^{B}(z,\zeta)=\mathcal{B}_{z,\zeta}^{-1}\big[B\big].

Combining the results of Lemmas
3.5
and
3.6
we find the following estimates for
G^{B}(z,\zeta)
that depend on whether
z
and
\zeta
belong to the same complex half-plane.

Corollary 3.7
.

Let
\gamma\in(0,1)
,
\tau\in(0,\min\{\gamma/2,1-\gamma\})
, and let
B\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
.
Denote
\widehat{\eta}:=\min\{|\Im z|,|\Im\zeta|\}
.

Uniformly for
(z,\zeta)\in\big(\Omega^{+}\times\Omega^{+}\big)\cup\big(\Omega^{-}\times\Omega^{-}\big)

\mathbb{E}\Big[\big\|G^{B}(z,\zeta)\big\|\Big]=O_{\prec}\bigg(\big\|B\big\|\Big(1+\frac{1}{N^{1/2}\widehat{\eta}^{\,3/2}}\Big)\bigg).

(3.98)

Uniformly for
(z,\zeta)\in\big(\Omega^{-}\times\Omega^{+}\big)\cup\big(\Omega^{+}\times\Omega^{-}\big)

G^{B}(z,\zeta)=\vartheta\,\frac{2\mathrm{i}\,\langle\Im M(E_{0}),\,B\,\rangle}{\langle\Im M(E_{0})\rangle}\frac{1}{z-\zeta}\Im M(E_{0})+\mathcal{E}^{B}(z,\zeta)

(3.99)

with

\mathbb{E}\Big[\big\|\mathcal{E}^{B}(z,\zeta)\big\|\Big]=O_{\prec}\bigg(\big\|B\big\|\Big(1+\frac{1}{N^{1/2}\widehat{\eta}^{\,5/2}}\Big)\bigg).

(3.100)

Proof.

After multiplying both sides of equation (
3.55
) by
M^{-1}(z)
from the left and
M^{-1}(\zeta)
from the right, and reordering the terms, we get that

\mathcal{B}_{z,\zeta}\big[G^{B}(z,\zeta)\big]=B+\frac{1}{M(z)}\mathcal{E}_{1}^{B}\frac{1}{M(\zeta)}.

Lemma
3.6
implies that the (linear) operator
\mathcal{B}_{z,\zeta}
is invertible for all
(z,\zeta)\in\Omega\times\Omega
with a uniformly bounded inverse, therefore,

G^{B}(z,\zeta)=\mathcal{B}_{z,\zeta}^{-1}\big[B\big]+\mathcal{B}_{z,\zeta}^{-1}\bigg[\frac{1}{M(z)}\mathcal{E}_{1}^{B}\frac{1}{M(\zeta)}\bigg]

(3.101)

for all
(z,\zeta)\in\Omega\times\Omega
.
Finally (
3.79
) and (
3.81
) give the decomposition and the bounds in (
3.98
) and (
3.99
).
∎

The bound in (
3.100
) is obtained by directly applying Lemmas
3.5
and
3.6
and serves as a preliminary estimate, which we will considerably improve in the next section.

3.5
Proof of Theorem
2.1
for
\beta=2

In this section we complete the proof of Theorem
2.1
for
\beta=2
.
For this we first need to improve the error term bound in (
3.100
).
Indeed, after substituting (
3.98
) and (
3.99
) into (
3.24
) and taking a derivative with respect to
\zeta
, the error term (
3.100
) gives a function of order
O_{\prec}(1+N^{-1/2}\widehat{\eta}^{\,-7/2})
on
\Omega\times\Omega
.
As we will see below in the proof of Theorem
2.1
, multiplying this function by
\frac{\partial\tilde{f}(z)}{\partial\overline{z}}\frac{\partial\tilde{f}(\zeta)}{\partial\overline{\zeta}}
and integrating over
\Omega\times\Omega
improves the estimate by
\widehat{\eta}^{\,2}
.
We end up with an overall bound
O_{\prec}(\widehat{\eta}^{\,2}+N^{-1/2}\widehat{\eta}^{\,-3/2})
, which is small only as long as
\widehat{\eta}\gg N^{-1/3}
.
Therefore, in the following lemma we obtain an improved estimate of
\mathcal{E}^{B}(z,\zeta)
from (
3.99
) it the case when
z
and
\zeta
belong to different complex half-planes.
The main idea in its proof consists of splitting the matrix
B
into a projection onto a co-dimension 1 subspace on which the operator
\mathcal{B}_{z,\zeta}
has a bounded inverse and its complement.
Consequently, both terms are treated separately. This approach has been first introduced in the context of Wigner matrices in
[
21
]
, and was later generalized to their multi-resolvents in
[
23
]
.

Lemma 3.8

(Improved estimate of
G^{B}(z,\zeta)
)
.

Let
\gamma\in(0,1)
,
\tau\in(0,\min\{\gamma/2,1-\gamma\})
, and let
B\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
.
Then uniformly for
(z,\zeta)\in\big(\Omega^{-}\times\Omega^{+}\big)\cup\big(\Omega^{+}\times\Omega^{-}\big)

G^{B}(z,\zeta)=\vartheta\,\frac{2\mathrm{i}\,}{z-\zeta}\frac{\langle\Im M(E_{0}),\,B\,\rangle}{\langle\Im M(E_{0})\rangle}\Im M(E_{0})+\mathcal{E}^{B}(z,\zeta)

(3.102)

with

\mathbb{E}\Big[\big\|\mathcal{E}^{B}(z,\zeta)\big\|\Big]=O_{\prec}\bigg(\big\|B\big\|\Big(1+\frac{1}{N\widehat{\eta}^{\,2}}+\frac{1}{N^{1/2}\widehat{\eta}^{\,3/2}}\Big)\bigg)

(3.103)

and
\vartheta=1
for
(z,\zeta)\in\Omega^{+}\times\Omega^{-}
and
\vartheta=-1
for
(z,\zeta)\in\Omega^{-}\times\Omega^{+}
.

Proof.

We split the proof into two steps by first considering the case
\langle\Im M(E_{0}),B\rangle=0
, and then establishing (
3.102
) for general
B\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
.
As before, we denote
M_{0}:=\lim_{y\downarrow 0}M(E_{0}+\mathrm{i}\,y)
for brevity.

Step 1.
Suppose that
\langle\Im M_{0},B\rangle=0
.
Denote by
\mathcal{Q}:\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}\to\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
the projection onto
\Im M_{0}
given by

\mathcal{Q}:=\frac{\langle\Im M_{0},\,\cdot\,\rangle}{\|\Im M_{0}\|^{2}_{\mathrm{HS}}}\Im M_{0},

(3.104)

and notice that from (
3.81
) we have that
(\mathrm{Id}-\mathcal{Q})\mathcal{B}_{z,\zeta}^{-1}=\mathcal{J}_{z,\zeta}
.
Therefore, (
3.101
), (
3.81
) and (
3.56
) imply that

G^{B}(z,\zeta)=\mathcal{J}_{z,\zeta}\bigg[B+\frac{1}{M(z)}\mathcal{E}_{1}^{B}\frac{1}{M(\zeta)}\bigg]+\mathcal{Q}\Big[G^{B}(z,\zeta)\Big],

(3.105)

where the first term, after taking norm and expectation, is of order
O_{\prec}(\|B\|(1+N^{-1/2}\widehat{\eta}^{-3/2}))
by(
3.56
).
In order to obtain an improved bound of the second term, we notice that

\mathcal{Q}\Big[G^{B}(z,\zeta)\Big]=\frac{1}{\|\Im M_{0}\|_{\mathrm{HS}}^{2}}\Big\langle\Im M_{0},G^{B}(z,\zeta)\Big\rangle\Im M_{0}.

Using the cyclicity of the trace together with the definitions (
3.54
) and (
3.23
) we get

\Big\langle\Im M_{0},G^{B}(z,\zeta)\Big\rangle=\Big\langle\Im M_{0},\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k,l=1}^{N}G_{lk}(z)BG_{kl}(\zeta)\Big\rangle=\Big\langle G^{\Im M_{0}}_{\overline{z},\overline{\zeta}},B\Big\rangle.

Applying (
3.101
) to
G^{\Im M_{0}}_{\overline{z},\overline{\zeta}}
gives

\displaystyle\Big\langle G^{\Im M_{0}}_{\overline{z},\overline{\zeta}},B\Big\rangle

\displaystyle=\Big\langle\mathcal{B}_{\overline{z},\overline{\zeta}}^{-1}\Big[\Im M_{0}+\frac{1}{M(\overline{z})}\mathcal{E}_{1}^{\Im M_{0}}\frac{1}{M(\overline{\zeta})}\Big],B\Big\rangle

\displaystyle=\Big\langle\Im M_{0}+\frac{1}{M(\overline{z})}\mathcal{E}_{1}^{\Im M_{0}}\frac{1}{M(\overline{\zeta})},\Big(\mathcal{B}_{\overline{z},\overline{\zeta}}^{-1}\Big)^{*}\big[B\big]\Big\rangle,

where
\mathbb{E}[\|\mathcal{E}_{1}^{\Im M_{0}}\|]=O_{\prec}(N^{-1/2}\widehat{\eta}^{\,-3/2})
due to (
3.56
).
Since
\big\langle\Im M_{0},B\big\rangle=0
, the decomposition (
3.81
) implies that
\Big(\mathcal{B}_{\overline{z},\overline{\zeta}}^{-1}\Big)^{*}\big[B\big]=\mathcal{J}_{\overline{z},\overline{\zeta}}^{*}[B]=O(\|B\|)
, and we conclude that

\mathcal{Q}\Big[G^{B}(z,\zeta)\Big]=O_{\prec}\Big(\|B\|\Big(1+\frac{1}{N^{1/2}\widehat{\eta}^{\,3/2}}\Big)\Big).

Combining this with the estimate of the first term in (
3.105
) we get that

\mathbb{E}\Big[\Big\|G^{B}(z,\zeta)\Big\|\Big]=O_{\prec}\bigg(\big\|B\big\|\Big(1+\frac{1}{N^{1/2}\widehat{\eta}^{\,3/2}}\Big)\bigg)

(3.106)

uniformly for
(z,\zeta)\in\big(\Omega^{-}\times\Omega^{+}\big)\cup\big(\Omega^{+}\times\Omega^{-}\big)
.

Step 2.
Consider now general
B\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
.
Denote

B^{\circ}:=B-\frac{\langle\Im M_{0},B\rangle}{\langle\Im M_{0}\rangle}I_{n}.

This definition is reminiscent of the definition of
B^{\circ}
from
[
20
]
and
[
19
]
, where it was used to control the 2-resolvent expression for hermitization of matrices with i.i.d. entries and generally deformed Wigner matrices correspondingly.
Then
\langle\Im M_{0},B^{\circ}\rangle=0
and

G^{B}(z,\zeta)=\frac{\langle\Im M_{0},B\rangle}{\langle\Im M_{0}\rangle}G^{I_{n}}(z,\zeta)+G^{B^{\circ}}(z,\zeta).

(3.107)

Using the resolvent identity we have

G^{I_{n}}(z,\zeta)=\big(\mathrm{Id}_{n}\otimes\frac{1}{N}\Tr_{N}\big)\Big(\bm{G}(z)\bm{G}(\zeta)\Big)=\frac{1}{z-\zeta}\big(\mathrm{Id}_{n}\otimes\frac{1}{N}\Tr_{N}\big)\Big(\bm{G}(z)-\bm{G}(\zeta)\Big).

After applying the local law (
3.8
) we get

G^{I_{n}}(z,\zeta)=\frac{1}{z-\zeta}\big(M(z)-M(\zeta)\big)+O_{\prec}\Big(\frac{1}{N\widehat{\eta}^{\,2}}\Big).

Finally, the analyticity of
M
implies that

G^{I_{n}}(z,\zeta)=\vartheta\frac{2\mathrm{i}\,}{z-\zeta}\Im M_{0}+O_{\prec}\Big(1+\frac{1}{N\widehat{\eta}^{\,2}}\Big).

Together with (
3.107
) and (
3.106
) this gives

G^{B}(z,\zeta)=\vartheta\frac{2\mathrm{i}\,}{z-\zeta}\frac{\langle\Im M_{0},B\rangle}{\langle\Im M_{0}\rangle}\Im M_{0}+O_{\prec}\Big(\|B\|\Big(1+\frac{1}{N\widehat{\eta}^{\,2}}+\frac{1}{N^{1/2}\widehat{\eta}^{\,3/2}}\Big)\Big).

This finishes the proof for general
B\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
.
∎

Lemma
4.1
improves the estimate of the error them in
G^{B}(z,\zeta)
for
(z,\zeta)\in\big(\Omega^{-}\times\Omega^{+}\big)\cup\big(\Omega^{+}\times\Omega^{-}\big)
by roughly
\widehat{\eta}
, which is crucial for establishing Theorem
2.1
for
all
mesoscopic scales.
We now proceed to the proof of the main result.

Proof of Theorem
2.1
for
\beta=2
.

We start by introducing additional notation.
Let
B_{ij}:\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
,
1\leq i,j\leq n
, be the collection of deterministic
n\times n
matrix-valued functions

B_{ij}(z)=\frac{1}{M(z)}\mathcal{B}_{z}^{-1}[I_{n}]\,\Gamma[E_{ij}].

Consider the integral

\mathcal{V}:=\frac{1}{\pi^{2}}\int\displaylimits_{\Omega\times\Omega}\frac{\partial\tilde{f}(z)}{\partial\overline{z}}\frac{\partial\tilde{f}(\zeta)}{\partial\overline{\zeta}}\frac{\partial}{\partial\zeta}\mathbb{E}\Big[\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\Tr\Big(E_{ji}\,G^{B_{ij}(z)}(z,\zeta)\Big)\mathfrak{e}(t)\Big]d^{2}\!zd^{2}\!\zeta.

(3.108)

This is the integral that appears in (
3.24
) with
S_{ij}(z,\zeta)=G^{B_{ij}(z)}(z,\zeta)
.
Using the analyticity of
M
at
E_{0}
and the boundedness of
\mathcal{B}_{E_{0}}^{-1}
, we write

B_{ij}(z)=B_{ij}(E_{0})+\widehat{B}_{ij}(z)

(3.109)

with

B_{ij}(E_{0}):=\frac{1}{M(E_{0})}\mathcal{B}_{E_{0}}^{-1}[I_{n}]\,\Gamma[E_{ij}]

and
\|\widehat{B}_{ij}(z)\|=O(|\Im z|)
.
In particular,
\|B_{ij}(z)\|\lesssim 1
uniformly on
\Omega
.

We split the domain of integration in (
3.108
) into four regions determined by the signs of
\Im z
and
\Im\zeta
, namely,

\Omega\times\Omega=\big(\Omega^{+}\times\Omega^{+}\big)\cup\big(\Omega^{+}\times\Omega^{-}\big)\cup\big(\Omega^{-}\times\Omega^{+}\big)\cup\big(\Omega^{-}\times\Omega^{-}\big),

(3.110)

and denote the integrals over the corresponding four regions by
\mathcal{V}^{\,(+,+)}
,
\mathcal{V}^{\,(+,-)}
,
\mathcal{V}^{\,(-,+)}
and
\mathcal{V}^{\,(-,-)}
, so that

\mathcal{V}=\mathcal{V}^{\,(+,+)}+\mathcal{V}^{\,(+,-)}+\mathcal{V}^{\,(-,+)}+\mathcal{V}^{\,(-,-)}.

(3.111)

Now we treat each term separately.
Consider first
\mathcal{V}^{\,(+,+)}
.
If we denote

h_{1}(z,\zeta):=\mathbb{E}\Big[\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\Tr\Big(E_{ji}\,G^{B_{ij}(z)}(z,\zeta)\Big)\mathfrak{e}(t)\Big],

then

\mathcal{V}^{(+,+)}=\frac{1}{\pi^{2}}\int\displaylimits_{\Omega^{+}\times\Omega^{+}}\frac{\partial\tilde{f}(z)}{\partial\overline{z}}\frac{\partial\tilde{f}(\zeta)}{\partial\overline{\zeta}}\frac{\partial}{\partial\zeta}\,h_{1}(z,\zeta)\,d^{2}\!zd^{2}\!\zeta\,.

(3.112)

The estimate (
3.98
) and the boundedness of
B_{ij}(z)
implies that
\mathbb{E}\big[\big\|G^{B_{ij}(z)}(z,\zeta)\big\|\big]\prec 1+N^{-1/2}\widehat{\eta}^{\,-3/2}
uniformly on
\Omega^{+}\times\Omega^{+}
.
Since
M^{-1}
is uniformly bounded on
\Omega
and
|\mathfrak{e}(t)|\leq 1
, we have that
h_{1}
is an analytic function on
\Omega^{+}\times\Omega^{+}
satisfying the bound

|h_{1}(z,\zeta)|\prec 1+\frac{1}{N^{1/2}\widehat{\eta}^{\,3/2}}.

(3.113)

The Cauchy integral formula applied to
\frac{\partial}{\partial\zeta}h_{1}(z,\zeta)
yields the bound

\Big|\frac{\partial}{\partial\zeta}h_{1}(z,\zeta)\Big|\prec\frac{1}{\widehat{\eta}}+\frac{1}{N^{1/2}\widehat{\eta}^{\,5/2}}

(3.114)

uniformly on
\Omega^{+}\times\Omega^{+}
.
By using Stokes’ theorem (
3.51
) (twice) and the definition of
\tilde{f}
in (
3.3
), we rewrite the integral in (
3.112
) as

\frac{1}{\pi^{2}}\int\displaylimits_{\Omega^{+}\times\Omega^{+}}\frac{\partial\tilde{f}(z)}{\partial\overline{z}}\frac{\partial\tilde{f}(\zeta)}{\partial\overline{\zeta}}\frac{\partial}{\partial\zeta}\,h_{1}(z,\zeta)\,d^{2}\!zd^{2}\!\zeta=\frac{1}{4\pi^{2}}\int\displaylimits_{\partial\Omega^{+}\times\partial\Omega^{+}}\tilde{f}(z)\tilde{f}(\zeta)\frac{\partial}{\partial\zeta}\,h_{1}(z,\zeta)\,dzd\zeta.

Since
\tilde{f}
vanishes everywhere on
\partial\Omega^{+}
except the part that intersects with the line
\Im z=N^{-\tau}\eta_{0}
, and
\frac{\partial}{\partial\zeta}h_{1}(z,\zeta)
satisfies the bounds (
3.114
), we estimate this integral as

\bigg|\int\displaylimits_{\partial\Omega^{+}\times\partial\Omega^{+}}\tilde{f}(z)\tilde{f}(\zeta)\frac{\partial}{\partial\zeta}\,h_{1}(z,\zeta)\,dzd\zeta\bigg|\\
\prec\int\displaylimits_{E_{0}-2\delta}^{E_{0}+2\delta}\int\displaylimits_{E_{0}-2\delta}^{E_{0}+2\delta}\big|\tilde{f}(x_{1}+\mathrm{i}\,N^{-\tau}\eta_{0})\tilde{f}(x_{2}+\mathrm{i}\,N^{-\tau}\eta_{0})\big|\Big(\frac{N^{\tau}}{\eta_{0}}+\frac{N^{5\tau/2}}{N^{1/2}\eta_{0}^{5/2}}\Big)\,dx_{1}dx_{2}.

(3.115)

From the definition of
\tilde{f}
in (
3.3
) close the real line we know that
\tilde{f}(x+\mathrm{i}\,N^{-\tau}\eta_{0})=f(x)+\mathrm{i}\,N^{-\tau}\eta_{0}f^{\prime}(x)
, and thus (
3.115
) can be further estimated as

\bigg|\int\displaylimits_{\partial\Omega^{+}\times\partial\Omega^{+}}\tilde{f}(z)\tilde{f}(\zeta)\frac{\partial}{\partial\zeta}\,h_{1}(z,\zeta)\,dzd\zeta\bigg|\\
\prec\big(\|f\|_{1}+N^{-\tau}\eta_{0}\|f^{\prime}\|_{1}\big)^{2}\Big(\frac{N^{\tau}}{\eta_{0}}+\frac{N^{5\tau/2}}{N^{1/2}\eta_{0}^{5/2}}\Big)\prec N^{-\tau}\eta_{0}+\frac{N^{5\tau/2}}{(N\eta_{0})^{1/2}},

(3.116)

where in the last step we used the norm bounds for
f
and
f^{\prime}
from (
3.1
).
In this case it is sufficient to have
\gamma\in(0,1)
and
\tau\in(0,\min\{(1-\gamma)/7,\gamma/2\})
.
Indeed, with this restriction on
\gamma
and
\tau
the last expression in (
3.116
) gives
N^{-\gamma-\tau}+N^{(-1+\gamma+5\tau)/2}\lesssim N^{-\tau}
, that ensures the convergence to zero as
N\to\infty
.
The same argument can be applied to estimate the integral over the set
\Omega^{-}\times\Omega^{-}
, giving

\big|\mathcal{V}^{(+,+)}\big|+\big|\mathcal{V}^{(-,-)}\big|\prec N^{-\tau}.

(3.117)

Consider now
\mathcal{V}^{(+,-)}
, the second term in (
3.111
)

\mathcal{V}^{(+,-)}=\frac{1}{\pi^{2}}\int\displaylimits_{\Omega^{+}\times\Omega^{-}}\frac{\partial\tilde{f}(z)}{\partial\overline{z}}\frac{\partial\tilde{f}(\zeta)}{\partial\overline{\zeta}}\frac{\partial}{\partial\zeta}\mathbb{E}\Big[\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\Tr\Big(E_{ji}\,G^{B_{ij}(z)}(z,\zeta)\Big)\mathfrak{e}(t)\Big]d^{2}\!zd^{2}\!\zeta.

(3.118)

Using the improved estimate (
3.102
) for
G^{B(z)}(z,\zeta)
from Lemma
3.8
and the approximation of
B_{ij}(z)
by
B_{ij}(E_{0})
on
\Omega\times\Omega
from (
3.109
), we rewrite the expectation in (
3.118
) as

\frac{1}{\pi^{2}}\mathbb{E}\Big[\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\Tr\Big(E_{ji}\,G^{B_{ij}(z)}(z,\zeta)\Big)\mathfrak{e}(t)\Big]\\
=\frac{\mathbb{E}\big[\mathfrak{e}(t)\big]}{\pi^{2}(z-\zeta)}\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\Tr\Big(E_{ji}\,\frac{2\mathrm{i}\,}{\langle\Im M(E_{0})\rangle}\Im M(E_{0})\langle\Im M(E_{0}),\,B_{ij}(E_{0})\,\rangle\Big)+h_{2}(z,\zeta),

(3.119)

where
h_{2}(z,\zeta)
is analytic on
\Omega^{+}\times\Omega^{-}
and satisfies
|h_{2}(z,\zeta)|\prec(N^{\tau}+N^{-1/2}\widehat{\eta}^{\,-3/2})
uniformly on
\Omega^{+}\times\Omega^{-}
.
The
N^{\tau}
term in the last estimate comes from the bound

\Big|\frac{B_{ij}(z)-B_{ij}(E_{0})}{z-\zeta}\Big|\prec N^{\tau},

(3.120)

holding on
\Omega^{+}\times\Omega^{-}
.
Repeating the computations in (
3.113
)-(
3.116
) we arrive at

\bigg|\int\displaylimits_{\Omega^{+}\times\Omega^{-}}\frac{\partial\tilde{f}(z)}{\partial\overline{z}}\frac{\partial\tilde{f}(\zeta)}{\partial\overline{\zeta}}\frac{\partial}{\partial\zeta}\,h_{2}(z,\zeta)\,d^{2}\!zd^{2}\!\zeta\bigg|\prec\eta_{0}+\frac{N^{5\tau/2}}{(N\eta_{0})^{1/2}}\lesssim N^{-\tau}

(3.121)

for
\gamma\in(0,1)
and
\tau\in(0,\min\{(1-\gamma)/7,\gamma/2\})
.

We now simplify the expression in the first term in (

).
Denote for brevity
M_{0}:=\lim_{y\downarrow 0}M(E_{0}+\mathrm{i}\,y)
and
M_{0}^{\prime}:=\lim_{y\downarrow 0}M^{\prime}(E_{0}+\mathrm{i}\,y)
.
Then the

-independent constant appearing in the first term of (

) is written as

\displaystyle\phi^{\,(+,-)}

\displaystyle:=\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\Tr\Big(E_{ji}\,\frac{2\mathrm{i}\,}{\langle\Im M_{0}\rangle}\Im M_{0}\Big\langle\Im M_{0},\,\frac{1}{M_{0}}\mathcal{B}_{E_{0}}^{-1}[I_{n}]\,\Gamma[E_{ij}]\,\Big\rangle\Big)

\displaystyle=\frac{2\mathrm{i}\,n}{\langle\Im M_{0}\rangle}\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\Big\langle\Im M_{0}\,\frac{1}{M_{0}}\mathcal{B}_{E_{0}}^{-1}[I_{n}]\,\Gamma[E_{ij}]\,\Big\rangle\Big\langle E_{ji}\,\Im M_{0}\Big\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{2\mathrm{i}\,}{\langle\Im M_{0}\rangle}\Big\langle\Gamma\Big[\Im M_{0}\,\frac{1}{M_{0}}\mathcal{B}_{E_{0}}^{-1}[I_{n}]\Big]\,\Im M_{0}\Big\rangle.

(3.123)

By differentiating the Dyson equation (
2.6
) at

and taking the conjugate-transpose on both sides, we get that
\mathcal{B}_{E_{0}}\big[M^{\prime}_{0}\big]=I_{n}
, so that

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{E_{0}}^{-1}[I_{n}]=M^{\prime}_{0}.

(3.124)

Plugging this into (

) yields

\displaystyle\Big\langle\Gamma\Big[\Im M_{0}\,\frac{1}{M_{0}}M^{\prime}_{0}\Big]\,\Im M_{0}\Big\rangle=\Big\langle\Im M_{0}\,\frac{1}{M_{0}}M^{\prime}_{0}\,\Gamma\big[\Im M_{0}\big]\Big\rangle

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad=\Big\langle\Im M_{0}\,\frac{1}{M_{0}}M^{\prime}_{0}\,\frac{1}{M_{0}}\Im M_{0}\frac{1}{(M_{0})^{*}}\Big\rangle=\frac{\mathrm{i}\,}{2}\big\langle\Im M_{0}\big\rangle,

(3.125)

where we used (
3.94
) in the second and (
3.95
) in the last step. For

from (

) we obtain

.
Now, using the analyticity of the function
(z-\zeta)^{-2}
on
\Omega^{+}\times\Omega^{-}
and Stokes’ theorem we get

\displaystyle\int\displaylimits_{\Omega^{+}\times\Omega^{-}}\frac{\partial\tilde{f}(z)}{\partial\overline{z}}\frac{\partial\tilde{f}(\zeta)}{\partial\overline{\zeta}}\frac{\partial}{\partial\zeta}\Big(\frac{\phi^{\,(+,-)}}{z-\zeta}\Big)\frac{d^{2}\!zd^{2}\!\zeta}{\pi^{2}}=\int\displaylimits_{\Omega^{+}\times\Omega^{-}}\frac{\partial\tilde{f}(z)}{\partial\overline{z}}\frac{\partial\tilde{f}(\zeta)}{\partial\overline{\zeta}}\frac{(-1)}{(z-\zeta)^{2}}\frac{d^{2}\!zd^{2}\!\zeta}{\pi^{2}}

(3.126)

\displaystyle=\int\displaylimits_{\Omega^{+}\times\Omega^{-}}\frac{\partial}{\partial\overline{z}}\frac{\partial}{\partial\overline{\zeta}}\frac{(\tilde{f}(z)-\tilde{f}(\zeta))^{2}}{(z-\zeta)^{2}}\frac{d^{2}\!zd^{2}\!\zeta}{2\pi^{2}}=-\frac{1}{8\pi^{2}}\int\displaylimits_{\partial\Omega^{+}\times\partial\Omega^{-}}\frac{(\tilde{f}(z)-\tilde{f}(\zeta))^{2}}{(z-\zeta)^{2}}dzd\zeta.

The function
\tilde{f}
vanishes everywhere on
\partial\Omega^{+}\times\partial\Omega^{-}
except the part that intersects with the lines
\Im z=N^{-\tau}\eta_{0}
and
\Im\zeta=N^{-\tau}\eta_{0}
, thus, the last integral in (
3.126

-\frac{1}{8\pi^{2}}\int_{\partial\Omega^{+}\times\partial\Omega^{-}}\frac{(\tilde{f}(z)-\tilde{f}(\zeta))^{2}}{(z-\zeta)^{2}}dzd\zeta\\
=\frac{1}{8\pi^{2}}\int\displaylimits_{E_{0}-2\delta}^{E_{0}+2\delta}\int\displaylimits_{E_{0}-2\delta}^{E_{0}+2\delta}\frac{(f(x)+\mathrm{i}\,N^{-\tau}\eta_{0}f^{\prime}(x)-f(y)+\mathrm{i}\,N^{-\tau}\eta_{0}f^{\prime}(y))^{2}}{(x-y+2\mathrm{i}\,N^{-\tau}\eta_{0})^{2}}dxdy,

(3.127)

where the change of the sign is due to the change in the orientation of the contour
\partial\Omega^{-}
.
Recall that
f(x)=g((x-E_{0})/\eta_{0})
.
By changing the variables
s=(x-E_{0})/\eta_{0}
,
t=(y-E_{0})/\eta_{0}
, the last integral becomes

\displaystyle\frac{1}{8\pi^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{(g(s)-g(t)+\mathrm{i}\,N^{-\tau}(g^{\prime}(s)+g^{\prime}(t))^{2}}{(s-t+2\mathrm{i}\,N^{-\tau})^{2}}dsdt.

Combining this with (
3.119
), (
3.121
), (
3.122
) and (
3.126
) we end up with the following expression for
\mathcal{V}^{(+,-)}
from (

\mathcal{V}^{(+,-)}=\frac{\mathbb{E}\big[\mathfrak{e}(t)\big]}{8\pi^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{(g(s)-g(t)+\mathrm{i}\,N^{-\tau}(g^{\prime}(s)+g^{\prime}(t))^{2}}{(s-t+2\mathrm{i}\,N^{-\tau})^{2}}dsdt+O_{\prec}(N^{-\tau}).

(3.128)

The last term left to evaluate is

\mathcal{V}^{(-,+)}=\frac{1}{\pi^{2}}\int\displaylimits_{\Omega^{-}\times\Omega^{+}}\frac{\partial\tilde{f}(z)}{\partial\overline{z}}\frac{\partial\tilde{f}(\zeta)}{\partial\overline{\zeta}}\frac{\partial}{\partial\zeta}\mathbb{E}\Big[\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\Tr\Big(E_{ji}\,G^{B_{ij}(z)}(z,\zeta)\Big)\mathfrak{e}(t)\Big]d^{2}\!zd^{2}\!\zeta.

Proceeding as in the analysis of
\mathcal{V}^{(+,-)}
in (
3.119
)-(
3.121
) we have that

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}^{(-,+)}=\frac{E\big[\mathfrak{e}(t)\big]}{\pi^{2}}\int_{\Omega^{-}\times\Omega^{+}}\frac{\partial\tilde{f}(z)}{\partial\overline{z}}\frac{\partial\tilde{f}(\zeta)}{\partial\overline{\zeta}}\frac{\partial}{\partial\zeta}\Big(\frac{\phi^{\,(-,+)}}{z-\zeta}\Big)d^{2}\!zd^{2}\!\zeta+O_{\prec}(N^{-\tau}),

where

\phi^{\,(-,+)}=-\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\Tr\Big(E_{ji}\,\frac{2\mathrm{i}\,}{\langle\Im M_{0}\rangle}\Im M_{0}\Big\langle\Im M_{0},\,\frac{1}{M_{0}^{*}}\big(\mathcal{B}_{E_{0}}^{-1}[I_{n}]\big)^{*}\Gamma[E_{ij}]\,\Big\rangle\Big).

(3.129)

The expression in (
3.129
) reflects the fact that
\vartheta=-1
in (
3.81
) and
\lim_{z\to E_{0}}M(z)=(M(E_{0}))^{*}
due to the analytic extension of

for

.
Then from (

) and (
3.125
) we obtain that

\Big\langle\Gamma\Big[\Im M_{0}\,\frac{1}{(M_{0})^{*}}(M^{\prime}_{0})^{*}\Big]\,\Im M_{0}\Big\rangle=-\frac{\mathrm{i}\,}{2}\big\langle\Im M_{0}\big\rangle.

This cancels the minus sign coming from
\vartheta
, and therefore
\phi^{\,(-,+)}=-1
and

\mathcal{V}^{(-,+)}=\frac{\mathbb{E}\big[\mathfrak{e}(t)\big]}{8\pi^{2}}\int\displaylimits_{\mathbb{R}}\int\displaylimits_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{(g(s)-g(t)+\mathrm{i}\,N^{-\tau}(g^{\prime}(s)+g^{\prime}(t))^{2}}{(s-t-2\mathrm{i}\,N^{-\tau})^{2}}dsdt+O_{\prec}(N^{-\tau}).

(3.130)

Combining Lemma
3.4
, (
3.111
), (
3.116
), (
3.117
), (
3.128
) and (
3.130
) gives

\frac{d}{dt}\mathbb{E}[\mathfrak{e}(t)]=-t\,V_{N}[g]\,\mathbb{E}[\mathfrak{e}(t)]+O_{\prec}\big(N^{-\tau}(1+|t|)\big),

(3.131)

where

V_{N}[g]:=\frac{1}{4\pi^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{\big(g(s)-g(t)+\mathrm{i}\,N^{-\tau}(g^{\prime}(s)+g^{\prime}(t)\big)^{2}}{\big(s-t+2\mathrm{i}\,N^{-\tau}\big)^{2}}dsdt.

Notice that

\bigg|\frac{\big(g(s)-g(t)+\mathrm{i}\,N^{-\tau}(g^{\prime}(s)+g^{\prime}(t)\big)^{2}}{\big(s-t+2\mathrm{i}\,N^{-\tau}\big)^{2}}\bigg|=\frac{|g(s)-g(t)|^{2}}{|s-t|^{2}+4N^{-2\tau}}+N^{-2\tau}\frac{|g^{\prime}(s)+g^{\prime}(t)|^{2}}{|s-t|^{2}+4N^{-2\tau}},

so that

\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}N^{-2\tau}\frac{|g^{\prime}(s)+g^{\prime}(t)|^{2}}{|s-t|^{2}+4N^{-2\tau}}dsdt\lesssim\|g^{\prime}\|_{2}^{2}

and

\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{|g(s)-g(t)|^{2}}{|s-t|^{2}+4N^{-2\tau}}dsdt\lesssim\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{(g(s)-g(t))^{2}}{(s-t)^{2}}dsdt<\infty,

and therefore the sequence
(V_{N}[g])_{N\in\mathbb{N}}
is bounded.
Now we complete the proof using a standard argument.
If we denote
\varphi_{N}(t):=\mathbb{E}[\mathfrak{e}(t)]
, then by (
3.131
) and the boundedness of
V_{N}[g]
, the sequences of functions
(\varphi_{N})
and
(\varphi_{N}^{\prime})
are uniformly bounded on
[-a,a]
for any
a>0
.
By Arzelà-Ascoli theorem, any subsequence of
(\varphi_{N})
has a subsequence that converges uniformly on
[-a,a]
to a function
\varphi
.
From the dominated convergence theorem,
V_{N}[g]
converges to
V[g]
as
N\to\infty
, and thus by integrating (
3.131
), taking the limit over the convergent subsequence and switching the limit and integration we find that
\varphi(t)=e^{-V[g]t^{2}/2}
.
Since
\varphi(t)
is the unique limit for all the converging subsequences, the entire sequence
(\varphi_{N})
converges to
\varphi
, and we have that for any
t\in\mathbb{R}

\lim_{N\to\infty}\mathbb{E}[\mathfrak{e}(t)]=e^{-\frac{t^{2}}{2}V[g]}.

Finally, by Lemma
3.2
, we have the convergence of the characteristic function

\lim_{N\to\infty}\mathbb{E}[e(t)]=e^{-\frac{t^{2}}{2}V[g]},

and we finish the proof of Theorem
2.1
for
\beta=2
by invoking Lévy’s continuity theorem.
∎

4.2
Computing

\beta=1

The derivation and analysis of the approximate equation for

in the case of the Kronecker model with real i.i.d. blocks requires several important modifications compared to the complex case.
These modifications are needed to take into account the differences in the correlation structures of real and complex Kronecker models.
In order to compare the two cases, consider the operator
\mathscr{S}^{(\beta)}:\mathbb{C}^{nN\times nN}\to\mathbb{C}^{nN\times nN}
given by

\mathscr{S}^{(\beta)}\big[\bm{R}\big]:=\mathbb{E}\Big[\bm{W}^{(\beta)}\bm{R}\,\bm{W}^{(\beta)}\Big]

for
\bm{R}\in\mathbb{C}^{nN\times nN}
and
\beta\in\{1,2\}
.
This operator appears naturally in the proof of Lemma
3.4
, where it takes the form (
3.17
)

\mathscr{S}^{(2)}[\bm{R}]=\mathscr{S}[\bm{R}]=\Gamma\Big[\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}R_{jj}\Big]\otimes\bm{I}_{N}.

On the other hand, in the case of real i.i.d. blocks we have

\displaystyle\mathscr{S}^{(1)}[\bm{R}]=\Gamma\Big[\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}R_{jj}\Big]\otimes\bm{I}_{N}+\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i,j=1}^{N}\widetilde{\Gamma}\big[R_{ji}\big]\otimes\bm{E}_{ij},

(4.1)

where the operator
\widetilde{\Gamma}:\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}\to\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
is given by

\widetilde{\Gamma}\big[R\big]:=\sum_{\alpha=1}^{d}\Big(L_{\alpha}RL_{\alpha}+L_{\alpha}^{\,t}RL_{\alpha}^{\,t}\Big).

Notice that in the case when
L_{\alpha}=L_{\alpha}^{\,t}
for all
\alpha\in\{1,\ldots,d\}
(i.e., when
\bm{H}^{(1)}
is constructed using real Wigner blocks) the operators
\Gamma
and
\widetilde{\Gamma}
coincide.
In the general case the operators
\Gamma
and
\widetilde{\Gamma}
are different.

Moreover, applying operator
\mathscr{S}^{(1)}
in the derivation of the equation for
\frac{d}{dt}\mathbb{E}[\mathfrak{e}(t)]
gives rise to two types of multiresolvent averages

G^{B}(z,\zeta):=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k,l=1}^{N}G_{lk}(z)BG_{kl}(\zeta),\quad\widetilde{G}^{B}(z,\zeta):=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k,l=1}^{N}G_{lk}(z)BG_{lk}(\zeta).

(4.2)

The details of how these terms emerge when differentiation
\mathbb{E}[\mathfrak{e}(t)]
with respect to

, are presented in Lemma
4.2
below.
The first quantity
G^{B}
from (
4.2
) was studied in Section
3.3
for
\beta=2
, and the results of Lemma
3.5
, Corollary
3.7
and Lemma
3.8
hold for

without any changes.
The second quantity

on the other hand requires a new proof which we present below.
Notice also, that if
n=1
(i.e.,
\bm{H}^{(1)}
is a real Wigner matrix), then

and thus the two quantities in (

) are the same.
Therefore, in the case of one real Wigner matrix the variance of the fluctuations of the linear spectral statistics follows almost immediately from the complex case using the fact that
\widetilde{\Gamma}=\Gamma
in (
4.1
) and

We start by proving the approximation of

and Lemma

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
\gamma\in(0,1)
,
\tau\in(0,\min\{\gamma/2,1-\gamma\})
, and let
B\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
.
Denote
\widehat{\eta}:=\min\{|\Im z|,|\Im\zeta|\}

Uniformly on
\big(\Omega^{+}\times\Omega^{+}\big)\cup\big(\Omega^{-}\times\Omega^{-}\big)

\mathbb{E}\Big[\big\|\widetilde{G}^{B}(z,\zeta)\big\|\Big]=O_{\prec}\bigg(\big\|B\big\|\Big(1+\frac{1}{N^{1/2}\widehat{\eta}^{\,3/2}}\Big)\bigg).

(4.3)

Uniformly on
\big(\Omega^{-}\times\Omega^{+}\big)\cup\big(\Omega^{+}\times\Omega^{-}\big)

\widetilde{G}^{B}(z,\zeta)=\vartheta\,\frac{2\mathrm{i}\,}{\langle\Im M(E_{0})\rangle}\frac{1}{z-\zeta}\Im M(E_{0})B^{\,t}\Im M(E_{0})+\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}^{B}(z,\zeta)

(4.4)

with

\mathbb{E}\Big[\big\|\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}^{B}(z,\zeta)\big\|\Big]=O_{\prec}\bigg(\big\|B\big\|\Big(1+\frac{1}{N\widehat{\eta}^{\,2}}+\frac{1}{N^{1/2}\widehat{\eta}^{\,3/2}}\Big)\bigg)

(4.5)

and
\vartheta=1
for
(z,\zeta)\in\Omega^{+}\times\Omega^{-}
and
\vartheta=-1
for
(z,\zeta)\in\Omega^{-}\times\Omega^{+}
.

Proof.

For convenience we view
n\times n
matrices as elements of the vector space
\mathbb{C}^{n}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{n}
with the two interpretations related by the isomorphism
\varphi:\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}\to\mathbb{C}^{n}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{n}
acting on the basis vectors
E_{ij}\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
as
\varphi(E_{ij})=e_{i}\otimes e_{j}
for
i,j\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
.
Then for any
A,B\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
the linear operator
\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}\ni R\mapsto A\,R\,B\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
corresponds to the linear operator
A\otimes B^{\,t}:\mathbb{C}^{n}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{n}\to\mathbb{C}^{n}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{n}
in the sense that

\varphi\big(ARB\big)=A\otimes B^{\,t}\,\varphi(R),

(4.6)

where as before
\otimes
denotes the tensor (Kronecker) product and
A\otimes B^{\,t}
is an
n^{2}\times n^{2}
matrix.
Using the above isomorphism we can write
\widetilde{G}^{B}(z,\zeta)
as

\varphi\Big(\widetilde{G}^{B}(z,\zeta)\Big)=\Big[\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k,l=1}^{N}G_{lk}(z)\otimes(G_{lk}(\zeta))^{t}\Big]\varphi(B).

(4.7)

Notice that for all
k,l\in\{1,\ldots,N\}
the symmetry of
\bm{W}
implies that
W_{lk}^{\,t}=W_{kl}
,
\big(G_{lk}(z)\big)^{t}=G_{kl}(z)
,
and thus
G_{lk}(z)\otimes(G_{lk}(\zeta))^{t}=G_{lk}(z)\otimes G_{kl}(\zeta)
.
Moreover, the solution of the Dyson equation (
2.6
) in the case of real symmetric matrices
L_{\alpha}
is also real symmetric,
M(z)=(M(z))^{t}
.

In the first part we derive an approximate equation for
\widetilde{G}^{B}(z,\zeta)
.
The proof is similar to the proof of Lemma
3.5
and relies on the general resolvent identities (
3.61
)-(
3.62
) and the local laws (
3.7
)-(
3.8
).
Fix
k\in\{1,\ldots,N\}
and recall that
\widehat{\eta}:=\min\{|\Im z|,|\Im\zeta|\}
.
From (
3.61
), (
3.62
) and (
3.63
) we have that for any
l\neq k
uniformly on
(z,\zeta)\in\Omega\times\Omega

\displaystyle G_{lk}(z)\otimes G_{kl}(\zeta)

\displaystyle=M(z)\sum_{p\neq l}W_{lp}G^{(l)}_{pk}(z)\otimes\sum_{q\neq l}G^{(l)}_{kq}(\zeta)W_{ql}M(\zeta)+O_{\prec}\Big(\frac{1}{(N\widehat{\eta})^{\,3/2}}\Big).

By taking the partial expectation
\mathbb{E}_{l}
we get

\displaystyle G_{lk}(z)\otimes G_{kl}(\zeta)=M(z)(1-\mathbb{E}_{l})\Big[G_{lk}(z)\otimes G_{kl}(\zeta)\Big]M(\zeta)+O_{\prec}\Big(\frac{1}{(N\widehat{\eta})^{\,3/2}}\Big)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+M(z)\frac{1}{N}\sum_{p\neq l}\sum_{\alpha=1}^{d}\Big[L_{\alpha}G^{(l)}_{pk}(z)\otimes G^{(l)}_{kp}(\zeta)L_{\alpha}^{\,t}+L_{\alpha}^{\,t}G^{(l)}_{pk}(z)\otimes G^{(l)}_{kp}(\zeta)L_{\alpha}\Big]M(\zeta).

Applying (
3.69
), (
3.72
) and summing the above equality over
l\in\{1,\ldots,N\}
yields

\displaystyle\sum_{l=1}^{N}G_{lk}(z)\otimes G_{kl}(\zeta)=M(z)\otimes M(\zeta)

(4.8)

\displaystyle\qquad+M(z)\sum_{p=1}^{N}\sum_{\alpha=1}^{d}\Big[L_{\alpha}G_{pk}(z)\otimes G_{kp}(\zeta)L_{\alpha}^{\,t}+L_{\alpha}^{\,t}G_{pk}(z)\otimes G_{kp}(\zeta)L_{\alpha}\Big]M(\zeta)

\displaystyle\qquad+M(z)\sum_{l:l\neq k}(1-\mathbb{E}_{l})\Big[G_{lk}(z)\otimes G_{kl}(\zeta)\Big]M(\zeta)+O_{\prec}\Big(\frac{1}{N^{1/2}\widehat{\eta}^{\,3/2}}\Big).

We multiply the left tensor factor in (
4.8

M^{-1}(z)
and the right tensor factor from the right by
M^{-1}(\zeta)
.
By averaging over
k\in\{1,\ldots,N\}
and repeating the proof in Lemma
3.5
we show that the error term

\widetilde{\mathcal{E}_{1}}(z,\zeta):=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k,l=1}^{N}\frac{1}{M(z)}G_{lk}(z)\otimes G_{kl}(\zeta)\frac{1}{M(\zeta)}\\
-I_{n}\otimes I_{n}-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k,p=1}^{N}\sum_{\alpha=1}^{d}\Big[L_{\alpha}G_{pk}(z)\otimes G_{kp}(\zeta)L_{\alpha}^{\,t}+L_{\alpha}^{\,t}G_{pk}(z)\otimes G_{kp}(\zeta)L_{\alpha}\Big]

(4.9)

is analytic on
\Omega\times\Omega
and satisfies the bound

\mathbb{E}[\|\widetilde{\mathcal{E}_{1}}(z,\zeta)\|]\prec\frac{1}{N^{1/2}\widehat{\eta}^{\,3/2}}.

(4.10)

Now we rewrite equation (

\widehat{\mathcal{B}}_{z,\zeta}\Big[\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k,l=1}^{N}G_{lk}(z)\otimes G_{kl}(\zeta)\Big]=I_{n}\otimes I_{n}+\widetilde{\mathcal{E}_{1}}(z,\zeta),

(4.11)

where the linear operator
\widehat{\mathcal{B}}_{z,\zeta}:\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}\to\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
is given by

\widehat{\mathcal{B}}_{z,\zeta}\big[A\otimes B\big]=\frac{1}{M(z)}A\otimes B\frac{1}{M(\zeta)}-\sum_{\alpha=1}^{d}\Big[L_{\alpha}A\otimes BL_{\alpha}^{\,t}+L_{\alpha}^{\,t}A\otimes BL_{\alpha}\Big]

.
To show the invertibility of
\widehat{\mathcal{B}}_{z,\zeta}
we define the linear operator
\Phi:\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}\to\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}

\Phi\big[E_{ij}\otimes E_{kl}\big]=E_{il}\otimes E_{kj}.

The operator

is an involution,
\Phi^{2}=\mathrm{Id}_{n}\otimes\mathrm{Id}_{n}
.
For any vectors

\Phi\big[ab^{\,t}\otimes cd^{\,t}\big]=ad^{\,t}\otimes cb^{\,t}
, which in composition with

gives

\displaystyle\Phi\widehat{\mathcal{B}}_{z,\zeta}\big[E_{ij}\otimes E_{kl}\big]

\displaystyle=\Phi\bigg[\frac{1}{M(z)}E_{ij}\otimes E_{kl}\frac{1}{M(\zeta)}-\sum_{\alpha=1}^{d}\Big[L_{\alpha}E_{ij}\otimes E_{kl}L_{\alpha}^{\,t}+L_{\alpha}^{\,t}E_{ij}\otimes E_{kl}L_{\alpha}\Big]\bigg]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{M(z)}E_{il}\frac{1}{M(\zeta)}\otimes E_{kj}-\sum_{\alpha=1}^{d}\Big[L_{\alpha}E_{il}L_{\alpha}^{\,t}\otimes E_{kj}+L_{\alpha}^{\,t}E_{il}L_{\alpha}\otimes E_{kj}\Big]

\displaystyle=\big(\mathcal{B}_{z,\zeta}\otimes\mathrm{Id}\big)\Phi\Big[E_{ij}\otimes E_{kl}\Big].

We see that
\widehat{\mathcal{B}}_{z,\zeta}=\Phi\big(\mathcal{B}_{z,\zeta}\otimes\mathrm{Id}\big)\Phi
, which means that the invertibility of
\widehat{\mathcal{B}}_{z,\zeta}
is equivalent to the invertibility of
\mathcal{B}_{z,\zeta}
.
By Lemma
3.6
, the operator
\widehat{\mathcal{B}}_{z,\zeta}
is invertible for
(z,\zeta)\in\Omega\times\Omega

\widehat{\mathcal{B}}_{z,\zeta}^{-1}=\Phi\,\big(\mathcal{B}_{z,\zeta}^{-1}\otimes\mathrm{Id}\big)\,\Phi.

(4.12)

Applying now
\widehat{\mathcal{B}}_{z,\zeta}^{-1}
on both sides of (

\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k,l=1}^{N}G_{lk}(z)\otimes G_{kl}(\zeta)=\Phi\big(\mathcal{B}_{z,\zeta}^{-1}\otimes\mathrm{Id}\big)\Phi\Big[I_{n}\otimes I_{n}\Big]+\Phi\big(\mathcal{B}_{z,\zeta}^{-1}\otimes\mathrm{Id}\big)\Phi\Big[\widetilde{\mathcal{E}_{1}}(z,\zeta)\Big].

(4.13)

After applying
\varphi^{-1}
in (
4.7
), the estimate (

given in (

The direct application of (

)-(

).
In order to resolve this problem, consider the operator
\widehat{\mathcal{Q}}:\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}\to\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
given by

\widehat{\mathcal{Q}}:=\Phi\,\big(\mathcal{Q}\otimes\mathrm{Id}_{n}\big)\,\Phi,

where

Since
\Phi

), introduced again the shorthand notations
M_{0}:=\lim_{y\downarrow 0}M(E_{0}+\mathrm{i}\,y)
and
M_{0}^{\prime}:=\lim_{y\downarrow 0}M^{\prime}(E_{0}+\mathrm{i}\,y)
, and used
\lim_{\Omega^{-}\ni z\to E_{0}}M(z)=M_{0}^{*}
and
\lim_{\Omega^{-}\ni z\to E_{0}}M^{\prime}(z)=(M_{0}^{\prime})^{*}
.
It has been shown in (
3.122
)-(
3.125
) that

\phi^{\,(+,-)}=\frac{2\mathrm{i}\,}{\langle\Im M_{0}\rangle}\Big\langle\Gamma\Big[\Im M_{0}\,\frac{1}{M_{0}}M^{\prime}_{0}\Big]\,\Im M_{0}\Big\rangle=-1.

(4.31)

Since
M(z)
and
M^{\prime}(z)
are symmetric and
\big(\widetilde{\Gamma}[R]\big)^{t}=\widetilde{\Gamma}[R^{t}]
for any
R\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\Tr\Big(E_{ji}\,\Im M_{0}\Big(\frac{1}{M_{0}}M^{\prime}_{0}\,\widetilde{\Gamma}[E_{ij}]\Big)^{t}\Im M_{0}\Big)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\Big\langle E_{ji}\Im M_{0}\widetilde{\Gamma}[E_{ji}]\Big(\frac{1}{M_{0}}M_{0}^{\prime}\Big)^{t}\Im M_{0}\Big\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\sum_{\alpha=1}^{d}\Big\langle E_{ji}\Im M_{0}\Big(L_{\alpha}E_{ji}\Big(\Im M_{0}\frac{1}{M_{0}}M_{0}^{\prime}L_{\alpha}^{t}\Big)^{t}+L_{\alpha}^{t}E_{ji}\Big(\Im M_{0}\frac{1}{M_{0}}M_{0}^{\prime}L_{\alpha}\Big)^{t}\,\Big)\Big\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\sum_{\alpha=1}^{d}\Big\langle E_{ii}\Im M_{0}L_{\alpha}E_{jj}\Im M_{0}\frac{1}{M_{0}}M_{0}^{\prime}L_{\alpha}^{t}\Big\rangle+\Big\langle E_{ii}\Im M_{0}L_{\alpha}^{t}E_{jj}\Im M_{0}\frac{1}{M_{0}}M_{0}^{\prime}L_{\alpha}\Big\rangle

\displaystyle=\Big\langle\Im M_{0}\,\Gamma\Big[\Im M_{0}\frac{1}{M_{0}}M_{0}^{\prime}\Big]\Big\rangle.

Plugging this into (
4.28
) and using (
4.31
) yields

\tilde{\phi}^{\,(+,-)}=\frac{2\mathrm{i}\,}{\langle\Im M_{0}\rangle}\Big\langle\Gamma\Big[\Im M_{0}\,\frac{1}{M_{0}}M^{\prime}_{0}\Big]\,\Im M_{0}\Big\rangle=-1.

By applying the same method we show that
\phi^{\,(-,+)}=\tilde{\phi}^{\,(-,+)}=-1
.
We conclude that

\mathcal{V}=-\frac{2}{\pi^{2}}\mathbb{E}\big[\mathfrak{e}(t)\big]\int\displaylimits_{(\Omega^{+}\times\Omega^{-})\cup(\Omega^{-}\times\Omega^{+})}\frac{\partial\tilde{f}(z)}{\partial\overline{z}}\frac{\partial\tilde{f}(\zeta)}{\partial\overline{\zeta}}\frac{\partial}{\partial\zeta}\frac{1}{z-\zeta}\,d^{2}\!\zeta d^{2}\!z+O\big(N^{-\tau}\big).

Finally, using (
3.127
), (
3.130
) and the same argument as at the end of Section
3.5
we obtain that in the real symmetric case

\lim_{N\to\infty}\mathbb{E}[e(t)]=e^{-\frac{t^{2}}{2}V[g]}
,
where

V[g]=\frac{1}{2\pi^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{(g(x)-g(y))^{2}}{(x-y)^{2}}dxdy.

This finishes the proof of Theorem
2.1
.

Appendix A

For the reader’s convenience, we present the full proof, adjusted to our current setup, and show how the modifications of the arguments from

Consider the Kronecker random matrix model
\bm{H}^{(\beta)}
,

, defined in (

Let

be the solution to the corresponding matrix Dyson equation (

Recall that by

Denote

Proposition A.1

(Properties of the solution to the MDE)
.

Suppose that the self-energy operator
\Gamma
satisfies the
L
-flatness property
(A)
for some

.
Then the following holds.

(i)

and

(ii)

(iii)

Linear stability
: The bound

\Big\|\big(\mathrm{Id}_{n}-\mathcal{C}_{M(z)}\Gamma\big)^{-1}\Big\|\lesssim 1+\frac{1}{(\rho(z)+\mathrm{dist}(z,\mathrm{supp}\,\rho))^{2}}

(iv)

Regularity of

and the density of states

The continuous extension of

.
Moreover, the density

is real analytic on the open set

.

(v)

Analytic continuation of

: For any

satisfying

can be analytically extended to a neighborhood of

in
\mathbb{C}
.

The hidden constants in

and

and the structure matrices

Proof.

We split the proof of Proposition

into four steps corresponding to the statements

,
(ii)
,
(iii)
and
(iv) - (v)
.
To make the presentation lighter, we will often suppress the

-dependence in the notation.

Proof of (i)
.
By taking the imaginary part of the Dyson equation (

) and multiplying it by
M^{*}
from the left and
M
from the right we get

\Im M=\Im zM^{*}M+M^{*}\Gamma[\Im M]M\geq\Gamma[\Im M]

(A.4)

for all

.
Since

is positive definite, the

-flatness (

\Im M\gtrsim\sum_{k,l=1}^{n}z_{kl}\big(\Im M\big)_{kk}M^{*}E_{ll}M\gtrsim\sum_{k=1}^{n}\big(\Im M\big)_{kk}M^{*}E_{kk}M,

where in the last step we used that

for

If in (
A.4

M
from the left and

from the right, then instead of (

By looking at the diagonal entries of (

The matrix

Proof of (ii)

Then applying the first inequality in (

)

Appendix B
Proof of Lemma
3.3

The crucial ingredient of the proof of Lemma
3.3
is the following cumulant expansion formula.
This formula is the complex analog of
[
31
, Proposition 3.2]
and follows immediately from the real case presented there.

Lemma B.1
.

Let
\bm{\xi}:=(\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{K})\in\mathbb{C}^{K}
be a complex random vector with finite moments up to order
R+1
for
R\in\mathbb{N}
, and let
\varphi:\mathbb{C}^{K}\to\mathbb{C}
be
R+1
times differentiable with bounded partial derivatives. Then

\mathbb{E}\Big[\xi_{1}\varphi(\bm{\xi})\Big]=\sum_{m=0}^{R-1}\frac{1}{m!}\mathbb{E}\Big[\big(\kappa^{\nabla}_{m+1}[\xi_{1},\bm{\xi}]\varphi\big)(\bm{\xi})\Big]+\frac{1}{R!}\mathbb{E}\bigg[\int_{0}^{1}\big(K^{\nabla}_{R+1,s}[\xi_{1},\bm{\xi}]\varphi\big)(s\bm{\xi})ds\bigg],

(B.1)

where for a complex random variable
\theta
we set
\kappa^{\nabla}_{1}[\theta,\bm{\xi}]\varphi:=\mathbb{E}[\theta]\varphi
,
K_{m+1,s}^{\nabla}
is a random differential operator defined for
m\geq 1
through

K_{m+1,s}^{\nabla}[\theta,\bm{\xi}]:=m!\int_{[0,1]^{m-1}}\nabla_{\bm{\xi}}(\mathbbm{1}_{s\leq t_{m-1}}-\mathbb{E})\nabla_{\bm{\xi}}(\mathbbm{1}_{t_{m-1}\leq t_{m-2}}-\mathbb{E})\cdots\nabla_{\bm{\xi}}(\mathbbm{1}_{t_{1}\leq 1}-\mathbb{E})\theta d\bm{t}

with
d\bm{t}:=dt_{1}\dots dt_{m-1}
,
\nabla_{\bm{\xi}}:=\sum_{i=1}^{K}(\xi_{i}\partial_{i}+\overline{\xi}_{i}\overline{\partial}_{i})
,
and where

\kappa^{\nabla}_{m+1}[\theta,\bm{\xi}]:=\mathbb{E}\bigg[\int_{0}^{1}K^{\nabla}_{m+1,s}[\theta,\bm{\xi}]ds\bigg]

is a non-random differential operator.

The derivation of the above lemma is identical to the real case treated in
[
31
, Proposition 3.2]
, thus omitted.
We now proceed to establishing Lemma
3.3
.

Proof of Lemma
3.3
.

For
\star\in\{1,2\}
we have

\mathbb{E}[\bm{W}\mathscr{F}_{\star}]=\sum_{i,j=1}^{N}\sum_{l=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}\Big[W_{il}F_{\star,lj}\Big]\otimes\bm{E}_{ij},

(B.2)

where
F_{1,lj}:=G_{lj}
and
F_{2,lj}:=G_{lj}\mathfrak{e}(t)
.
We apply Lemma
B.1
to two cases, namely
\varphi=\varphi(W_{il},W_{li}):=F_{1,lj}(W_{il},W_{li})
and
\varphi=\varphi(W_{il},W_{li}):=F_{2,lj}(W_{il},W_{li})
, where we keep the entries of
W_{ab}
with
(a,b)\in\{1,\dots,N\}^{2}\setminus\{(i,l),(l,i)\}
fixed, i.e., we take the partial expectation
\mathbb{E}_{il}
with respect to
W_{il}
first.
For
i=l
we interpret this as
\varphi=\varphi(W_{ii})
.

In order to accommodate the matrix setting of (
B.2
), where both
W_{il}
and
F_{\star,lj}(W_{il},W_{li})
are
n\times n
matrices, we denote by
\nabla^{ij}_{V}:=\sum_{\alpha,\beta=1}^{n}v_{\alpha\beta}\partial_{w_{ij}^{\alpha\beta}}
the directional derivative with respect to the
(i,j)
-th block
W_{ij}=(w_{ij}^{\alpha\beta})_{\alpha,\beta=1}^{n}
in the direction
V=(v_{\alpha\beta})_{\alpha,\beta=1}^{n}\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
.
The analyticity of
F_{\star,lj}(W_{il},W_{li})
for
\star\in\{1,2\}
implies that
\overline{\partial}_{w_{ik}^{\alpha\beta}}F_{\star,lj}=0
for all
1\leq i,k\leq N
and
1\leq\alpha,\beta\leq n
.

With this notation, the cumulant expansion (
B.1
) for
R=3
reads

\mathbb{E}\Big[W_{il}F_{\star,lj}\Big]=\mathbb{E}\bigg[\int_{0}^{1}\big(\nabla^{il}_{\widetilde{W}_{il}}+\nabla^{li}_{\widetilde{W}_{li}}\big)(\mathbbm{1}_{s\leq 1}-\mathbb{E}_{\widetilde{\bm{W}}})\widetilde{W}_{il}ds\,F_{\star,lj}(W_{il},W_{li})\\
+\sum_{\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2}\in\{il,li\}}\int_{[0,1]^{2}}\nabla^{\sigma_{1}}_{\widetilde{W}_{\sigma_{1}}}(\mathbbm{1}_{s\leq t_{1}}-\mathbb{E}_{\widetilde{\bm{W}}})\nabla^{\sigma_{2}}_{\widetilde{W}_{\sigma_{2}}}(\mathbbm{1}_{t_{1}\leq 1}-\mathbb{E}_{\widetilde{\bm{W}}})\widetilde{W}_{il}dt_{1}ds\,F_{\star,lj}(W_{il},W_{li})\\
+\sum_{\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2},\sigma_{3}\in\{il,li\}}\int_{0}^{1}K^{\nabla,(\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2},\sigma_{3})}_{4,s}[W_{il},(W_{\sigma_{1}},W_{\sigma_{2}},W_{\sigma_{3}})]F_{\star,lj}(sW_{il},sW_{li})ds\bigg],

(B.3)

where we introduced a random differential operator

K^{\nabla,\bm{\sigma}}_{4,s}[V_{0},\bm{V}]:=\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}\nabla_{V_{1}}^{\sigma_{1}}(\mathbbm{1}_{s\leq t_{2}}-\mathbb{E})\nabla_{V_{2}}^{\sigma_{2}}(\mathbbm{1}_{t_{2}\leq t_{1}}-\mathbb{E})\nabla_{V_{3}}^{\sigma_{3}}(\mathbbm{1}_{t_{1}\leq 1}-\mathbb{E})V_{0}dt_{1}dt_{2}

with random matrices
V_{0}
,
(V_{1},V_{2},V_{3})=:\bm{V}
and indices
\bm{\sigma}:=(\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2},\sigma_{3})
.
In the above formulas
\widetilde{\bm{W}}
is an independent copy of
\bm{W}
, and
\mathbb{E}_{\widetilde{\bm{W}}}
denotes the partial expectation with respect to
\widetilde{\bm{W}}
.
In (
B.3
) we also used that
W_{il}
is centered, which implies that the term corresponding to
m=0
in (
B.1
) vanishes.

We now treat each term on the right-hand side of (
B.3
) individually.
From the direct computation of the first term we see that for
\sigma_{1}\in\{il,li\}

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\bigg[\int_{0}^{1}\nabla^{\sigma_{1}}_{\widetilde{W}_{\sigma_{1}}}(\mathbbm{1}_{s\leq 1}-\mathbb{E}_{\widetilde{\bm{W}}})\widetilde{W}_{il}dsF_{\star,lj}(W_{il},W_{li})\bigg]=\mathbb{E}\bigg[\widetilde{W}_{il}\nabla^{\sigma_{1}}_{\widetilde{W}_{\sigma_{1}}}F_{\star,lj}(W_{il},W_{li})\bigg].

Taking the sum of the above expression for
\sigma_{1}\in\{il,li\}
and
l\in\{1,\ldots,N\}
, and using the independence of
\widetilde{W}_{il}
and
\widetilde{W}_{ab}
for
ab\notin\{il,li\}
gives
\mathbb{E}\big[\big(\widetilde{\bm{W}}\nabla_{\widetilde{\bm{W}}}\mathscr{F}_{\star}(\bm{W})\big)_{ij}\big]
.
Together with (
B.2
) we recover the first term in (
3.14
).

Similar computations for the second term give

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\bigg[\sum_{l=1}^{N}\sum_{\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2}\in\{il,li\}}\int_{[0,1]^{2}}\nabla^{\sigma_{1}}_{\widetilde{W}_{\sigma_{1}}}(\mathbbm{1}_{s\leq t_{1}}-\mathbb{E}_{\widetilde{\bm{W}}})\nabla^{\sigma_{2}}_{\widetilde{W}_{\sigma_{2}}}(\mathbbm{1}_{t_{1}\leq 1}-\mathbb{E}_{\widetilde{\bm{W}}})\widetilde{W}_{il}dt_{1}ds\,F_{\star,lj}(W_{il},W_{li})\bigg]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}\bigg[\sum_{l=1}^{N}\sum_{\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2}\in\{il,li\}}\widetilde{W}_{il}\nabla^{\sigma_{1}}_{\widetilde{W}_{\sigma_{1}}}\nabla^{\sigma_{2}}_{\widetilde{W}_{\sigma_{2}}}F_{\star,lj}(\bm{W})\bigg]=\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}\Big[\Big(\widetilde{\bm{W}}\big(\nabla_{\widetilde{\bm{W}}}\big)^{2}\mathscr{F}_{\star}(\bm{W})\Big)_{ij}\Big].

(B.4)

We now estimate the above expression when
F_{1,lj}(\bm{W})=G_{lj}(z)
, i.e.,
\mathscr{F}_{1}(\bm{W})=\bm{G}(z)
.
From the properties of the resolvent (
3.27
) we find that

\nabla^{\sigma_{1}}_{V_{1}}\dots\nabla_{V_{k}}^{\sigma_{k}}G_{lj}=(-1)^{k}\sum_{\tau\in S_{k}}G_{li_{\tau(1)}}V_{\tau(1)}G_{j_{\tau(2)}i_{\tau(2)}}\cdots G_{j_{\tau(k-1)}i_{\tau(k)}}V_{\tau(k)}G_{j_{\tau(k)}j}

(B.5)

for any
V_{1},\ldots,V_{k}\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
and double indices
\sigma_{p}=i_{p}j_{p}
.
Using the local law (
3.7
) together with (
3.57
), (
B.5
) and moment bounds (
2.4
) we obtain for
\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2}\in\{il,li\}
the estimates

\widetilde{W}_{il}\nabla^{\sigma_{1}}_{\widetilde{W}_{\sigma_{1}}}\nabla^{\sigma_{2}}_{\widetilde{W}_{\sigma_{2}}}G_{lj}(\bm{W})=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}O_{\prec}\Big(\frac{1}{N^{3/2}}\Big)\,,&\quad l=j\,,\\
O_{\prec}\bigg(\frac{1}{\sqrt{N|\Im z|}}\,\frac{1}{N^{3/2}}\bigg)\,,&\quad l\neq j\,.\end{array}\right.

(B.6)

uniformly on
z\in\Omega
.
When
z\in\Omega
the functions
G_{ij}(z)
are deterministically bounded by
N
, thus the stochastic domination bounds (
B.6
) also hold in expectation.
Therefore, if in (
B.4
) we take the sum with respect to
l\in\{1,\ldots,N\}
and use the estimate (
B.6
) in expectation, we obtain the bound

\bigg\|\mathbb{E}\Big[\frac{1}{2}\widetilde{\bm{W}}(\nabla_{\widetilde{\bm{W}}})^{2}\bm{G}\Big]\bigg\|_{\max}=O_{\prec}\Big(\frac{1}{N|\Im z|^{1/2}}\Big)=O_{\prec}\Big(\frac{N^{\tau/2}}{N\sqrt{\eta_{0}}}\Big).

(B.7)

To analyze the case
\mathscr{F}_{2}(\bm{W})=\bm{G}\mathfrak{e}(t)
,
F_{2,lj}(\bm{W})=G_{lj}(z)\mathfrak{e}(t)
, we derive convenient formulas for the repeated directional derivatives of
\mathfrak{e}(t)
, similar to (
B.5
).
For this, we define

\begin{split}\mathcal{I}([\sigma,V])&:=\sum_{\tau\in S_{k}}(-1)^{k}\frac{\mathrm{i}\,t}{\pi}\int\displaylimits_{\Omega}\frac{\partial\tilde{f}(\zeta)}{\partial\overline{\zeta}}\Tr\sum_{l=1}^{N}G_{li_{\tau(1)}}(\zeta)V_{\tau(1)}G_{j_{\tau(1)}i_{\tau(2)}}(\zeta)\cdots V_{\tau(k)}G_{j_{\tau(k)}l}(\zeta)d^{2}\!\zeta\\
&=\frac{(-1)^{k+1}}{k}\frac{\mathrm{i}\,t}{\pi}\sum_{\tau\in S_{k}}\int\displaylimits_{\Omega}\frac{\partial\tilde{f}(\zeta)}{\partial\overline{\zeta}}\frac{\partial}{\partial\zeta}\Tr G_{j_{\tau(k)}i_{\tau(1)}}(\zeta)V_{\tau(1)}\cdots G_{j_{\tau(k-1)}i_{\tau(k)}}(\zeta)V_{\tau(k)}d^{2}\!\zeta\end{split}

for a multiset
[\sigma,V]:=[(\sigma_{1},V_{1}),\dots,(\sigma_{k},V_{k})]
, where
\sigma_{p}=i_{p}j_{p}
are double indices, and
V_{1},\ldots,V_{k}\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
.
In particular, we have

\nabla^{\sigma_{0}}_{V_{0}}\mathcal{I}([{\sigma},{V}])=\mathcal{I}\big(\big[(\sigma_{0},V_{0}),(\sigma_{1},V_{1}),\ldots,(\sigma_{k},V_{k})\big]\big)\,.

Using this and
\nabla^{\sigma_{1}}_{V_{1}}\mathfrak{e}(t)=\mathfrak{e}(t)\mathcal{I}\big([(\sigma_{1},V_{1})]\big)
we see by induction that

\nabla^{\sigma_{1}}_{V_{1}}\dots\nabla^{\sigma_{k}}_{V_{k}}\mathfrak{e}(t)=\mathfrak{e}(t)\sum_{\pi\in\mathcal{P}([\sigma,V])}\mathcal{I}(\pi)\,,

(B.8)

where
\mathcal{P}([\sigma,V])=\mathcal{P}([(\sigma_{1},V_{1}),\dots,(\sigma_{k},V_{k})])
denotes all partitions of
[(\sigma_{1},V_{1}),\dots,(\sigma_{k},V_{k})]
, and for
\pi\in\mathcal{P}([\sigma,V])
we set

\mathcal{I}(\pi)=\prod_{\lambda\in\pi}\mathcal{I}(\lambda)\,.

(B.9)

From (
B.6
) and
|\mathfrak{e}(t)|\leq 1
we have that for all
i,j,l\in\{1,\ldots,N\}
and
\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2}\in\{il,li\}
the estimate

\mathfrak{e}(t)\widetilde{W}_{il}\nabla^{\sigma_{1}}_{\widetilde{W}_{\sigma_{1}}}\nabla^{\sigma_{2}}_{\widetilde{W}_{\sigma_{2}}}G_{lj}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}O_{\prec}\Big(\frac{1}{N^{3/2}}\Big)\,,&\quad l=j\,,\\
O_{\prec}\bigg(\frac{N^{\tau/2}}{\sqrt{N\eta_{0}}}\,\frac{1}{N^{3/2}}\bigg)\,,&\quad l\neq j\,,\end{array}\right.

(B.10)

holds uniformly on
z\in\Omega
.
For the mixed derivatives (
B.5
) and (
B.8
) yield

\widetilde{W}_{il}\Big(\nabla^{\sigma_{1}}_{\widetilde{W}_{\sigma_{1}}}G_{lj}\Big)\Big(\nabla^{\sigma_{2}}_{\widetilde{W}_{\sigma_{2}}}\mathfrak{e}(t)\Big)=-\widetilde{W}_{il}G_{li_{1}}\widetilde{W}_{i_{1}j_{1}}G_{j_{1}j}\,\mathfrak{e}(t)\,\mathcal{I}\big([(\sigma_{2},\widetilde{W}_{\sigma_{2}})]\big).

(B.11)

The local law (
3.7
) implies that for any
i,l\in\{1,\ldots,N\}
,
\sigma_{2}=i_{2}j_{2}\in\{il,li\}
, and
A\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}

\mathcal{I}\big([(\sigma_{2},A)]\big)=\frac{\mathrm{i}\,t}{\pi}\int_{\Omega}\frac{\partial\tilde{f}(\zeta)}{\partial\overline{\zeta}}\frac{\partial}{\partial\zeta}\Tr\Big[G_{j_{2}i_{2}}(\zeta)A\Big]d^{2}\!\zeta=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}O_{\prec}\Big(N^{\tau}|t|\|A\|\Big),&\,l=i,\\

(B.12)

where we applied Stokes’ theorem to rewrite the above expression using a contour integral as in (

Combining (
B.11
), (
B.12

3.7

\widetilde{W}_{il}\Big(\nabla^{\sigma_{1}}_{\widetilde{W}_{\sigma_{1}}}G_{lj}\Big)\Big(\nabla^{\sigma_{2}}_{\widetilde{W}_{\sigma_{2}}}\mathfrak{e}(t)\Big)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}O_{\prec}\bigg(\frac{N^{\tau}|t|}{N^{3/2}}\bigg)\,,&\quad l=i,\\
O_{\prec}\bigg(\frac{1}{N^{3/2}}\,\frac{N^{3\tau/2}|t|}{\sqrt{N\eta_{0}}}\bigg)\,,&\quad l\neq i\,.\end{array}\right.

(B.13)

Finally, using (
B.8
), the structure of
\mathcal{I}[\sigma,V]

\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2}\in\{il,li\}

\mathcal{I}\Big(\big[(\sigma_{1},\widetilde{W}_{\sigma_{1}}),(\sigma_{2},\widetilde{W}_{\sigma_{2}})\big]\Big)=O_{\prec}\bigg(\frac{N^{\tau}|t|}{N}\bigg).

Together with (
B.12
), (
B.8
) and (

)

\displaystyle\nabla^{\sigma_{1}}_{\widetilde{W}_{\sigma_{1}}}\nabla^{\sigma_{2}}_{\widetilde{W}_{\sigma_{2}}}\mathfrak{e}(t)=O_{\prec}\bigg(\frac{N^{2\tau}|t|^{2}}{N}+\frac{N^{3\tau}|t|^{2}}{N^{2}\eta_{0}}+\frac{N^{\tau}|t|}{N}\bigg)=O_{\prec}\bigg(\frac{N^{2\tau}(|t|+|t|^{2})}{N}\bigg)\,,

(B.14)

where we also used that

Now (

), (

\widetilde{W}_{il}G_{lj}\nabla^{\sigma_{1}}_{\widetilde{W}_{\sigma_{1}}}\nabla^{\sigma_{2}}_{\widetilde{W}_{\sigma_{2}}}\mathfrak{e}(t)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}O_{\prec}\bigg(\frac{N^{2\tau}(|t|+|t|^{2})}{N^{3/2}}\bigg)\,,&\quad l=j\,,\\
O_{\prec}\bigg(\frac{N^{2\tau}(|t|+|t|^{2})}{N^{3/2}}\,\frac{N^{\tau/2}}{\sqrt{N\eta_{0}}}\bigg)\,,&\quad l\neq j\,.\end{array}\right.

(B.15)

From the Leibniz rule, (
B.10

B.13
) and (
B.15

\displaystyle\sum_{l=1}^{N}\sum_{\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2}\in\{il,li\}}\widetilde{W}_{il}\nabla^{\sigma_{1}}_{\widetilde{W}_{\sigma_{1}}}\nabla^{\sigma_{2}}_{\widetilde{W}_{\sigma_{2}}}\Big(G_{lj}\mathfrak{e}(t)\Big)

\displaystyle=O_{\prec}\bigg(\frac{N^{2\tau}(1+|t|^{2})}{N^{3/2}}+\frac{N^{5\tau/2}(1+|t|^{2})}{N\sqrt{\eta_{0}}}\bigg)

Using the same argument as in the case

\bigg\|\mathbb{E}\Big[\frac{1}{2}\widetilde{\bm{W}}(\nabla_{\widetilde{\bm{W}}})^{2}\big(\bm{G}\mathfrak{e}(t)\big)\Big]\bigg\|_{\max}=O_{\prec}\bigg(\frac{N^{5\tau/2}(1+|t|^{2})}{N\sqrt{\eta_{0}}}\bigg)

uniformly for

It remains to estimate the last term in (

The local law bound

|\nabla^{\sigma_{1}}_{V_{1}}\dots\nabla^{\sigma_{k}}_{V_{k}}\mathfrak{e}(t)|\prec(1+|t|^{k})N^{k\tau}\prod_{i=1}^{k}\|V_{i}\|\,,\qquad\|\nabla^{\sigma_{1}}_{V_{1}}\dots\nabla_{V_{k}}^{\sigma_{k}}G_{ab}\|\prec\prod_{i=1}^{k}\|V_{i}\|.

\|K^{\nabla,(\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2},\sigma_{3})}_{4,s}[W_{il},(W_{\sigma_{1}},W_{\sigma_{2}},W_{\sigma_{3}})]F_{\star,lj}(sW_{il},sW_{li})\|=O_{\prec}\Big(\frac{(1+|t|^{3})N^{3\tau}}{N^{2}}\Big)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Big[\bm{W}\mathscr{F}_{\star}(\bm{W})\Big]=\mathbb{E}\Big[\widetilde{\bm{W}}\,\nabla_{\widetilde{\bm{W}}}\mathscr{F}_{\star}(\bm{W})\Big]+\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}\Big[\widetilde{\bm{W}}(\nabla_{\widetilde{\bm{W}}})^{2}\mathscr{F}_{\star}(\bm{W})\Big]+\mathcal{E}_{\star}(z),
