Abstract
. In this paper, we introduce a criterion for maximal operators associated with Fourier multipliers to be bounded on L p ​ ( ℝ d ) L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d}) for each p ∈ ( 1 , ∞ ) p\in(1,\infty) . Noteworthy examples satisfying the criterion are multipliers of the Mikhlin type or limited decay which are not necessarily radial. To do so, we make use of modified square function estimates and bilinear interpolation. For the bilinear interpolation we introduce a function space Σ 2 ​ ( ℬ ) \Sigma^{2}(\mathcal{B}) where ℬ \mathcal{B} denotes a Banach space of functions on ℝ d \mathbb{R}^{d} , which is a variant of weighted Sobolev spaces. In result, we obtain convergence results for fractional half-wave equations and surface averages as well as the L p L^{p} boundedness for the maximal operators.

Introduction

Maximal operators and Fourier multipliers are crucial objects in harmonic analysis
due to their importance in harmonic analysis itself
and vast applications in other areas of mathematics such as theory of partial differential equations.
Maximal operators of our particular interest are given in terms of Fourier multipliers.
One of the best examples of this kind could be the spherical maximal function

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{sph}(f)(x)

\displaystyle:=\sup_{t>0}\bigg|\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}f(x-ty)d\sigma_{sph}(y)\bigg|,

where
d\sigma_{sph}
denotes the spherical measure on
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
induced by the Lebesgue measure.
For
f\in\mathscr{S}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, we make use of the Fourier inversion formula to write

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{sph}(f)(x)

\displaystyle:=\sup_{t>0}\bigg|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{2\pi i\langle x,\xi\rangle}\widehat{d\sigma_{sph}}(t\xi)\widehat{f}(\xi)d\xi\bigg|,

which is the form of
\sup_{t>0}\big|\big(m(t\cdot)\widehat{f}(\cdot)\big)\check{\,\,}\big|
.
Note that
\widehat{d\sigma_{sph}}
is the Fourier transform of the surface-carried measure of
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}

and equals to
\frac{J_{(d-2)/2}(|\xi|)}{|\xi|^{(d-2)/2}}
up to constant, where
J_{\nu}(r)
is the Bessel function of order
\nu
.

\mathcal{M}_{sph}
is bounded on
L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
for
p>\frac{d}{d-1}
when
d\geq 3
due to E. M. Stein
[
19
,
21
]
and for
d=2
by J. Bourgain
[
3
]
.

In studies of
\mathcal{M}_{sph}
, what has been mainly concerned is the relation between the
L^{p}
boundedness and geometric aspects of
\mathcal{M}_{sph}
.
Generally speaking, geometric properties of
\widehat{d\sigma_{sph}}
are one of many examples
which allow maximal operators associated with the Fourier multipliers to be bounded on
L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.
Thus, one can naturally question that for which conditions on the Fourier multipliers
m

\mathcal{M}_{m}(f):=\sup_{t>0}\big|(m(t\cdot)\widehat{f}(\cdot))\check{\,}\big|
is bounded on
L^{p}
.

In
[
17
]
, Rubio de Francia showed mapping properties of
\mathcal{M}_{m}
when
m
satisfies the limited decay condition,

i.e
,

|\partial^{\gamma}m(\xi)|\leq C|\xi|^{-a}
for a given
a>0
,

which is a generalisation of geometric properties of
\widehat{d\sigma_{sph}}
.

[

To do so, we first modify square function estimates for

For the bilinear interpolation, we consider the square function as a bilinear operator for

To state main theorem, we recall notations for maximal Fourier multiplier operators.

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{m}(f)(x)

\displaystyle:=\sup_{t>0}\big|T_{m(t\cdot)}(f)(x)\big|,

\displaystyle T_{m}(f)(x)

\displaystyle:=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{2\pi ix\cdot\xi}m(\xi)\widehat{f}(\xi)d\xi.

We also make use of a function space
\Sigma^{2}(\mathcal{B})
whose norm is given by

\|f\|_{\Sigma^{2}(\mathcal{B})}:=\biggl(\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}\big\|f(2^{j}\cdot)\widehat{\psi}(\cdot)\big\|_{\mathcal{B}}^{2}\biggr)^{1/2},

where
\mbox{supp}(\widehat{\psi})

denotes a Banach space of functions on

Properties of

are introduced in Section

In most cases, we choose

We note that

is a space of tempered distributions

whose norm is given by

\|f\|_{B_{p,q}^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}:=\|S_{0}f\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}+\bigg(\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\big(2^{js}\|\psi_{j}\ast f\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\big)^{q}\bigg)^{1/q},\quad S_{0}f:=\sum_{j\leq 0}\psi_{j}\ast f,

where
\psi_{j}(\cdot)=2^{jd}\psi(2^{j}\cdot)
.
Then for

we let
B_{p,p}^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{d})=B_{p}^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, and

satisfies the embedding property

and

.
Now we introduce our main theorem.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
p\in(1,\infty)
.
If

\frac{1}{p_{0}}=|\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{2}|

\displaystyle\big\|\mathcal{M}_{m}(f)\big\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\lesssim\|m\|_{\Sigma^{2}(B_{p_{0}}^{s})}\|f\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}.

Note that

satisfies

Function spaces

Choose

Define

(2.1)

\displaystyle\widehat{\psi}_{j}=\widehat{\psi}(\cdot/2^{j})=\widehat{\phi}(\cdot/2^{j})-\widehat{\phi}(\cdot/2^{j-1})\quad\text{for}\quad j\in\mathbb{Z}.

Then we introduce function spaces appeared in our arguments.
Let
\mathcal{B}
be a Banach space continuously embedded in the space of distributions
\mathscr{D}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.
For

\displaystyle:=\{\{f_{j}\}_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}:f_{j}\in\mathcal{B},\,\,\text{and}\,\,\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}2^{q\theta}\|f_{j}\|_{\mathcal{B}}<\infty\}.

Note that
\Sigma_{\theta}^{q}(\mathcal{B})
is a Banach space where we additionally assume that

(2.2)

\displaystyle\|\widehat{\psi}f\|_{\mathcal{B}}\lesssim\|f\|_{\mathcal{B}}\quad\text{and}\quad\|f(2\,\cdot\,)\|_{\mathcal{B}}\simeq\|f\|_{\mathcal{B}}.

Let
S
and
R
be mappings given by

\displaystyle S(f)

\displaystyle=\{f(2^{j}\cdot)\widehat{\psi}(\cdot)\}_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}=:\{f_{j}\}_{j\in\mathbb{Z}},

\displaystyle R\bigl(\{f_{j}\}\bigr)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j}\big(\widehat{\psi}_{j-1}+\widehat{\psi}_{j}+\widehat{\psi}_{j+1}\big)(\cdot)f_{j}(2^{-j}\cdot).

)

and

, respectively, where

denotes a space of bounded linear maps from
A
to
B
.
Moreover, one can check that

is an identity map of

.
Hence by
[

, Theorem 1.2.4]

\Sigma_{\theta}^{q}(\mathcal{B})
is isomorphic to a closed subspace of

which is a reflexive Banach space when
\mathcal{B}
is reflexive.
If
\theta=0
, then we simply write
\Sigma^{q}(\mathcal{B})
.

Let
(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
be an interpolation couple of Banach spaces,

, and
\theta=(1-\delta)\theta_{0}+\delta\theta_{1}
.
Then we have from

\displaystyle[\Sigma_{\theta_{0}}^{q_{0}}(\mathcal{A}),\Sigma_{\theta_{1}}^{q_{1}}(\mathcal{B})]_{\delta}=\Sigma_{\theta}^{q}\bigl([\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B}]_{\delta}\bigr).

For instance, if we choose

\displaystyle[\Sigma^{2}(L_{s_{1}}^{2}),\Sigma^{2}(L_{s_{2}}^{2})]_{\delta}=\Sigma^{2}(L_{s}^{2}),\quad s=(1-\delta)s_{1}+\delta s_{2},\,\delta\in(0,1).

When

\displaystyle H_{\theta}^{p,\gamma}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\{0\})=\{f\in\mathscr{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{d}):\|f\|_{H_{\theta}^{p,\gamma}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\{0\})}^{p}:=\sum_{l=0}^{\gamma}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\big|D_{x}^{l}(f)(x)\big|^{p}|x|^{pl+\theta-d}dx<\infty\}.

2.2.
Fractional calculus

To study a maximal operator, classical square function argument makes use of the fundamental theorem of calculus with respect to
t>0
.
In doing so, one must take derivative with respect to
t
, which in turn yields loss of regularity of a symbol.
Such loss may be optimized if we use a fractional analogue of the fundamental theorem.

We define the Riemann-Liouville integrals and fractional derivatives of order
\alpha\in(0,1)
.
For all
t\in(0,\infty)

\displaystyle I_{0+}^{\alpha}f(t)

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{\Gamma(\alpha)}\int_{0}^{t}(t-s)^{\alpha-1}f(s)ds,

\displaystyle D_{0+}^{\alpha}F(t)

\displaystyle:=\frac{d}{dt}\big(I_{0+}^{1-\alpha}F\big)(t)

where
f
is a locally integrable function on
[0,\infty)
and
F
satisfies
I_{0+}^{1-\alpha}F
is absolutely continuous.
For further use of
I_{0+}^{\alpha}
and
D_{0+}^{\alpha}
, we introduce the following lemma which is crucial in the proof of Theorem
1.1
:

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
F\in C_{loc}\big([0,\infty)\big)\cap C^{0,\beta}_{loc}\big((0,\infty)\big)
for a fixed
\beta\in(0,1]
.
Then for any
\alpha\in(0,\beta)
,
D^{\alpha}_{0+}F
satisfies the following identity:

(2.6)

\displaystyle D^{\alpha}_{0+}F(t)=\frac{1}{\Gamma(1-\alpha)}\left(\frac{F(t)}{t^{\alpha}}+\int_{0}^{t}\frac{F(t)-F(s)}{(t-s)^{1+\alpha}}ds\right)\,.

Moreover, we have

(2.7)

\displaystyle F(t)=I_{0+}^{\alpha}D_{0+}^{\alpha}F(t)+\frac{t^{\alpha}}{\Gamma(\alpha)}F(0)\,.

Proof.

From the definition of
I^{1-\alpha}F(t)
, for
h>0
we have

\displaystyle\frac{I^{1-\alpha}F(t+h)-I^{1-\alpha}F(t)}{h}

\displaystyle=\,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{h}\int_{t}^{t+h}\big((t+h-s)^{-\alpha})\big(F(s)-F(t)\big)ds

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{h}\int_{0}^{t}\big((t+h-s)^{-\alpha}-(t-s)^{-\alpha})\big(F(s)-F(t)\big)ds

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{h}F(t)\Big(\int_{0}^{t+h}(t+h-s)^{-\alpha}-\int_{0}^{t}(t-s)^{-\alpha}ds\Big)

\displaystyle=:

\displaystyle I_{1}+I_{2}+I_{3}\,.

Define

A=\sup_{0\leq s\leq 2t}|F(s)|\quad\text{and}\quad B=\sup_{t/2\leq s\leq 2t}\frac{|F(t)-F(s)|}{|t-s|^{\beta}}.

For
0<h<t/2
, by change of variables, we have

\displaystyle|I_{1}|\leq\int_{0}^{1}(hr)^{-\alpha}\big|F(t+h(1-r))-F(t)\big|ds\leq h^{\beta-\alpha}B\int_{0}^{1}r^{-\alpha}(1-r)^{\beta}dr.

Therefore,
I_{1}\rightarrow 0
as
h\rightarrow 0
.
For
I_{2}
, we make use of the fundamental theorem of calculus to obtain

\displaystyle I_{2}=-\alpha\int_{0}^{t}\int_{0}^{1}(t-s+hr)^{-1-\alpha}dr\big(F(s)-F(t)\big)ds\,.

Note that

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\int_{0}^{1}(t-s+hr)^{-1-\alpha}\big|F(s)-F(t)\big|drds

\displaystyle\leq\,

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}(t-s)^{-1-\alpha}|F(s)-F(t)|ds

\displaystyle\lesssim\,

\displaystyle At^{-\alpha}+Bt^{\beta-\alpha}<\infty\,.

Thus by the dominated convergence theorem, it follows that

I_{2}\rightarrow-\alpha\int_{0}^{t}(t-s)^{-1-\alpha}\big(F(s)-F(t)\big)ds\quad\text{as}\quad h\to 0.

For
I_{3}
, by direct calculation we obtain

I_{3}=\frac{F(t)}{1-\alpha}\times\frac{(t+h)^{1-\alpha}-t^{1-\alpha}}{h}\rightarrow\frac{F(t)}{t^{\alpha}}\,.

For
\frac{I^{1-\alpha}F(t)-I^{1-\alpha}F(t-h)}{h}
and
h>0
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{I^{1-\alpha}F(t)-I^{1-\alpha}F(t-h)}{h}=\,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{h}\int_{t-h}^{t}\big((t-s)^{-\alpha})\big(F(s)-F(t-h)\big)ds

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{h}\int_{0}^{t-h}\big((t-s)^{-\alpha}-(t-h-s)^{-\alpha})\big(F(s)-F(t-h)\big)ds

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{h}F(t-h)\Big(\int_{0}^{t}(t-s)^{-\alpha}-\int_{0}^{t-h}(t-h-s)^{-\alpha}ds\Big).

Then by the same argument it follows that for
h>0

\frac{I^{1-\alpha}F(t)-I^{1-\alpha}F(t-h)}{h}\rightarrow\frac{F(t)}{t^{\alpha}}+\int_{0}^{t}\frac{F(t)-F(s)}{(t-s)^{1+\alpha}}ds\quad\text{as}\quad h\to 0\,.

Therefore (
2.6
) is proved.

From the assumption of
F
we obtain
D^{\alpha}_{0+}F(t)\in L^{1}_{loc}([0,\infty))
, and the equality (
2.7
) holds by
[
18
, Theorem 2.4]
.
The lemma is proved.
∎

Proof of Theorem

Let
\varepsilon\in(0,\frac{1}{6})
be sufficiently small and
s>d|\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{2}|+\frac{1}{2}+3\varepsilon
. Since
\Sigma^{2}(B^{s}_{p_{0}})\subset\Sigma^{2}(C^{0,\frac{1}{2}+3\varepsilon})
, we consider
m
as an element of
C_{loc}^{0,\frac{1}{2}+3\varepsilon}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\{0\})
and

\|m(2^{j}\cdot)\widehat{\psi}\|_{C^{0,1/2+3\varepsilon}}\rightarrow 0\quad\text{as}\quad j\rightarrow-\infty.

This implies that
m(\xi)\rightarrow 0
as

.
Thus we want to show that for such
m
,

\displaystyle\big\|\mathcal{M}_{m}(f)\big\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\lesssim\|m\|_{\Sigma^{2}(B_{p_{0}}^{s})}\|f\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}.

To do this we introduce a square function type estimate using fractional calculus.
From Lemma
2.1
we obtain that

m(t\xi)=\frac{1}{\Gamma(\frac{1}{2}-\varepsilon)}I^{\frac{1}{2}+\varepsilon}\big((\cdot)^{-\frac{1}{2}-\varepsilon}\widetilde{m}(\cdot\xi)\big)(t),

where

\widetilde{m}(t\xi):=m(t\xi)+(1/2+\varepsilon)\int_{0}^{1}\frac{m(t\xi)-m(st\xi)}{(1-s)^{\frac{3}{2}+\varepsilon}}ds\,.

Note that for fixed
\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
|\xi|\in(2^{j-1},2^{j+1})
for some
j\in\mathbb{Z}
, we have

\displaystyle|\widetilde{m}(\xi)|\lesssim\|m\|_{L^{\infty}}+\Big|\int_{0}^{1}\frac{m(\xi)-m(s\xi)}{(1-s)^{\frac{3}{2}+\varepsilon}}ds\Big|\lesssim\|m\|_{L^{\infty}}+\Big|\int_{1/2}^{1}\frac{m(\xi)-m(s\xi)}{(1-s)^{\frac{3}{2}+\varepsilon}}ds\Big|.

Note that
\|m\|_{\infty}\lesssim\|m\|_{\Sigma(L^{\infty})}\lesssim\|m\|_{\Sigma(C^{0,1/2+3\varepsilon})}
.
We also have

\displaystyle\Big|\int_{1/2}^{1}\frac{m(\xi)-m(s\xi)}{(1-s)^{\frac{3}{2}+\varepsilon}}ds\Big|

\displaystyle\lesssim|\xi|^{\frac{1}{2}+3\varepsilon}\sup_{1/2<s<1}\frac{|m(\xi)-m(s\xi)|}{|\xi-s\xi|^{\frac{1}{2}+3\varepsilon}}

\displaystyle\lesssim 2^{j(\frac{1}{2}+3\varepsilon)}\sum_{k=j-1}^{j+1}\big[m\widehat{\psi}_{k}\big]_{C^{0,1/2+3\varepsilon}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\sum_{k=j-1}^{j+1}\|m(2^{k}\cdot)\widehat{\psi}(\cdot)\|_{C^{0,1/2+3\varepsilon}}\lesssim\|m\|_{\Sigma^{2}(C^{0,1/2+3\varepsilon})}<\infty.

Since we choose arbitrary
\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
\widetilde{m}
is bounded.
By the boundedness of
\widetilde{m}
, we make use of Fubini’s theorem to obtain

T_{m(t\cdot)}f=\frac{1}{\Gamma(\frac{1}{2}-\varepsilon)}I^{\frac{1}{2}+\varepsilon}\big(t^{-\frac{1}{2}-\varepsilon}T_{\widetilde{m}(t\cdot)}f\big).

Thus it follows that

(3.1)

\displaystyle\big|T_{m(t\cdot)}f\big|^{2}

\displaystyle=\big|\frac{1}{\Gamma(\frac{1}{2}-\varepsilon)}I^{\frac{1}{2}+\varepsilon}\big(t^{-\frac{1}{2}-\varepsilon}T_{\widetilde{m}(t\cdot)}f\big)\big|^{2}

\displaystyle=\bigg|\frac{1}{\Gamma(\frac{1}{2}-\varepsilon)}\int_{0}^{t}(t-s)^{-\frac{1}{2}+\varepsilon}s^{-\frac{1}{2}-\varepsilon}T_{\widetilde{m}(s\cdot)}fds\bigg|^{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim\int_{0}^{t}(t-s)^{-1+2\varepsilon}s^{-2\varepsilon}ds\times\int_{0}^{t}\big|T_{\widetilde{m}(s\cdot)}f\big|^{2}\frac{ds}{s}.

t>0
on (

), we obtain

), we obtain

in price of
\widetilde{m}
.

m
for the

.

For

For the square function

\displaystyle\big\|G_{m}(f)\big\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\lesssim\sup_{\xi\neq 0}\Big(\int_{0}^{\infty}|m(t\xi)|^{2}\frac{dt}{t}\,\Big)^{1/2}\|f\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})},

\displaystyle\big\|G_{m}(f)\big\|_{BMO(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\lesssim\|m\|_{\Sigma^{2}(L_{s}^{2})}\|f\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}.

3.1
and

.

and

.
Then by Lemmas
3.1
,
3.2
,

\displaystyle\big\|G_{\widetilde{m}}(f)\big\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}

\displaystyle\big||x|^{N}m(x)-|x|^{N}m(y)\big|^{2}\lesssim\big||x|^{N}m(x)-|y|^{N}m(y)\big|^{2}+\big||x|^{N}-|y|^{N}\big|^{2}|m(y)|^{2},

\displaystyle\quad\lesssim I_{1,3}+I_{0},

where the last inequality holds due to (

).
Moreover, one can observe that

\displaystyle\lesssim\,

\displaystyle\big||x|^{N}m(x)\big|^{2}|\eta(2^{-k}y)-\eta(2^{-k}x)\big|^{2}+\big||x|^{N}m(x)\eta(2^{-k}x)-|y|^{N}m(y)\eta(2^{-k}y)\big|^{2},

which yields

\displaystyle I_{1,3}\lesssim I_{1,2}+I_{1}.

Since
I_{1,2}\lesssim I_{0}
and
I_{0}+I_{1}\simeq\|m\|_{\Sigma_{\theta}^{2}(L_{\alpha}^{2})}^{2}

(4.23)

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\Big(\int_{|y-x|<\frac{|x|}{2}}\frac{|m(x)-m(y)|^{2}}{|x-y|^{d+2\alpha}}dy\Big)|x|^{2\alpha+2\theta-d}dx\lesssim\|m\|_{\Sigma_{\theta}^{2}(L_{\alpha}^{2})}^{2}.

By (

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|m(x)|^{2}|x|^{2\theta-d}dx+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\Big(\int_{|y-x|<\frac{|x|}{2}}\frac{|m(x)-m(y)|^{2}}{|x-y|^{d+2\alpha}}dy\Big)|x|^{2\alpha+2\theta-d}dx\lesssim\|m\|_{\Sigma_{\theta}^{2}(L^{2}_{\alpha})}^{2}.

This proves the proposition.

,
s\in\big(d\bigl|\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{2}\bigr|+\frac{1}{2},\frac{\beta}{\alpha})

\displaystyle L_{s}^{p_{0}}=F_{p_{0},2}^{s}\subseteq F_{p_{0},p_{0}}^{s}=B_{p_{0},p_{0}}^{s}.

Thus for

Morevoer,
