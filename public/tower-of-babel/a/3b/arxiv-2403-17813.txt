Abstract
. A very first step to develop non-commutative algebraic geometry is the arithmetic of polynomials in non-commuting variables over a commutative field, that is, the study of elements in free associative algebras. This investigation is presented as a natural extension of the classical theory in one variable by using Leavitt algebras, which are localizations of free algebras with respect to the Gabriel topology defined by an ideal of codimension 1. In particular to any polynomial in n n non-commuting variables with non-zero constant term we associate a finite-dimensional module over the free algebra of rank n n , which turns out to be simple if and only if the polynomial is irreducible. This approach leads to new insights in the study of the factorization of polynomials into irreducible ones and other related topics, such as an algorithm to divide polynomials or to compute the greatest common divisor between them, or a description of similar polynomials. The case of polynomials with zero constant term reduces to an open question whether the intersection of nonunital subalgebras of codimension 1 in a free associative algebra is trivial, that is, 0.

1.
Introduction

The classical theory of polynomials in one variable over a commutative field
K
assigns to every irreducible polynomial its residue field, i.e., its finite-dimensional field extension of remainders by Euclidean division, and shows that every polynomial is uniquely a product of irreducible ones up to permutation and units. The
division algorithm to compute the greatest common divisor of polynomials leads to a complete picture of the lattice of the ideals of
K[x]
.
Moreover, the irreducible representations of
K[x]
are explicitly constructed from the irreducible polynomials in
K[x]
.

Analogous results for polynomials in several commutative variables are no longer true. For instance, the factor algebra of an irreducible polynomial in
K[x_{1},\cdots,x_{n}]
for
n\geq 2
is not a field extension of
K
and is never of finite dimension.

For
n\geq 2
polynomials in non-commuting variables are elements of the free associative algebra
K\langle x_{1},\cdots,x_{n}\rangle
. Factorization problems in
K\langle x_{1},\cdots,x_{n}\rangle
have been deeply studied by Cohn in
[
7
]
,
[
9
]
,
[
11
]
,
[
10
]
, where he showed that free algebras have a weak division algorithm, they are weak Bézout free ideal rings, and they are unique factorization rings, i.e any element is the product of irreducible ones in a unique way up to units and similarity. Roughly speaking, weaker versions of the relevant properties of
K[x]
to be a Euclidean domain, a principal ideal domain and a unique factorization domain hold for the free algebra
K\langle x_{1},\cdots,x_{n}\rangle
. Moreover, by considering the category of strictly cyclic modules or more generally of (specific) torsion modules over free ideal rings, Cohn succeeded to show a categorical simplicity of factor modules of the free algebra by irreducible polynomials. However, Cohn’s approach leaves open the problem to parametrize the irreducible representations of
K\langle x_{1},\cdots,x_{n}\rangle
by irreducible polynomials, as in the classical case of one variable.

The aim of our work is to present a theory of polynomials
which coincides with the classical one in case of one variable. The starting point is the Cohn’s unique normal form
[
8
]

\lambda=k+\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}\lambda_{x_{i}}\quad(k\in K;\,\lambda_{x_{i}}\in K\langle x_{1},\cdots,x_{n}\rangle)

of any polynomial
\lambda\in K\langle x_{1},\cdots,x_{n}\rangle
, where
\lambda_{x_{i}}=\lambda_{i}
is called the
cofactor
with respect to the
i
-th axis (i.e., variable)
x_{i}
. Hence taking cofactors behaves like taking generalized partial differentiation or truncated partial inverse along axes. One can iterate this process to obtain further cofactors of
\lambda
until getting nonzero constants.
For any polynomial
\lambda
in
K\langle x_{1},\cdots,x_{n}\rangle
with nonzero constant term we define an easily handled finite-dimensional vector space
V_{\lambda}
, spanned by all nonzero iterated cofactors of
\lambda
, which admits a natural left module structure over the free algebra. This new invariant becomes a good tool in the study of the polynomial
\lambda
itself, independent on
K\langle x_{1},\cdots,x_{n}\rangle
. For instance, the module
V_{\lambda}
helps to decide the irreducibility of
\lambda
, to compute its similar polynomials and its irreducible factors or indecomposable factors, i.e., primary decompositions, and to compute other invariants such as endomorphism rings, submodule lattices, etc. In particular,
V_{\lambda}
is simple if and only
\lambda
is irreducible. More generally, if
\lambda=\pi_{1}\cdots\pi_{l}
is a factorization of
\lambda
as a product of irreducible polynomials
\pi_{1},\cdots,\pi_{l}
, then
l
is the length of
V_{\lambda}
and
\{V_{\pi_{1}},\cdots,V_{\pi_{l}}\}
is the set of composition factors of
V_{\lambda}
. If
\gamma
is another polynomial with nonzero constant term, then
V_{\lambda}
and
V_{\gamma}
are isomorphic
K\langle x_{1},\cdots,x_{n}\rangle
-modules if and only if the
K\langle x_{1},\cdots,x_{n}\rangle
-modules
K\langle x_{1},\cdots,x_{n}\rangle/K\langle x_{1},\cdots,x_{n}\rangle\lambda
and
K\langle x_{1},\cdots,x_{n}\rangle/K\langle x_{1},\cdots,x_{n}\rangle\gamma
are such. Therefore
V_{\lambda}
encodes the structure of
\lambda
.

The module structure of
V_{\lambda}
is inspired by ideas coming from Leavitt algebras, which are universal with
respect to an isomorphism property between finite rank free modules
[
13
]
. This is not surprising, since free algebras and Leavitt algebras are tightly connected by the concept of localization (see Section
3
). This means that even if these two algebraic structures are deeply different in view of their ring properties (for instance Leavitt algebras are far from being domains), their module categories are closely related.
Moreover, any free algebra can be seen naturally in two ways as a subalgebra of a Leavitt algebra. This fact reflects the decisive role of Leavitt algebras in the investigation of both right and left factorizations of polynomials. By symmetry it is enough to study right factorizations. Since Leavitt algebras are one-sided Bézout rings, i.e. any finitely generated right (or left) ideal is principal, moving to this larger setting it is possible to define the
generalized greatest common divisor
, shortly, the
greatest common divisor
, for finitely
many polynomials
\lambda_{i}
in
K\langle x_{1},\cdots,x_{n}\rangle
: it is the uniquely determined, up to scalars, polynomial with nonzero constant term which is a generator of the left ideal of the extended Leavitt algebra generated by the
\lambda_{i}
’s.

In this manner
we succeed to have an algorithm computing the greatest common divisor of polynomials and to divide polynomials in
K\langle x_{1},\cdots,x_{n}\rangle
analogous to the Euclidean algorithm for
K[x]
. This will be a crucial tool for our theory. Another basic notion in our treatment is that of similarity, which is naturally inspired by the similarity of linear transformations. Namely, two elements are
similar
if the corresponding factors by the left (right) ideals generated by them are isomorphic (see
[
10
]
). We will show that two elements
\lambda
and
\gamma
with nonzero constant term are similar if and only if their associated finite-dimensional modules
V_{\lambda}
and
V_{\gamma}
are isomorphic.

The article is organized as follows.

In Section
2
we state the main result, Theorem
2.3
, that every irreducible polynomial with constant term in
n
non-commutative variables defines a simple finite-dimensional module over the free algebra of rank
n
, whence a finite-dimensional division algebra, opening several exciting related questions. Consequently, every polynomial with nonzero constant term factors uniquely into products of irreducible up to units and similarity, in the sense that if
\lambda=\delta_{1}\cdots\delta_{s}=\pi_{1}\cdots\pi_{t}
where the
\delta_{i}
’s and the
\pi_{j}
’s are irreducible, then
s=t
and there exists a permutation
\sigma
such that
\delta_{i}
is similar to
\pi_{\sigma(i)}
, even if
\lambda
is no longer equal to a permutated product
\prod\limits_{i=1}^{s}\pi_{\sigma(i)}
.
Since irreducibilty does not change under automorphisms, polynomials with zero constant term may become ones with nonzero constant after suitable substitutions. The feasibility of this trick is an open question whether or not
the intersection of nonunital subalgebras of codimension 1 is 0.

Section
3
develops shortly the theory of Leavitt algebras necessary for Theorem
2.3
and presents the interaction between Leavitt algebras and free algebras in terms of their module categories. The importance of Leavitt algebras is underlined by the fact that they contain the pair of free algebras
K\langle x_{1},\cdots,x_{n}\rangle
and
K\langle x^{*}_{1},\cdots,x^{*}_{n}\rangle
together with both the isomorphism and anti-isomorphism induced canonically by the assignment
x_{i}\mapsto x^{*}_{i}\,(i=1,\cdots,n)
.
This parallel reflects precisely both the left and the right factorizations of polynomials. Hence we deal mainly with the right factorizations of polynomials.

Section
4
is devoted to prove Theorem
2.3
with additional results and consequences on the arithmetic of polynomials in non-commutative variables.

Section
5
presents applications of Theorem
2.3
to companion structures of polynomials with nonzero constant term, like their similarity classes, atomic factorizations etc. Some important problems concerning irreducible polynomials and finite-dimensional non-commutative division algebras are stated.

Section
6
contains further problems, discusses primary decompositions of a polynomial as indecomposable decompositions of its module of remainders and shows that Theorem
2.3
remains true when the investigated polynomial is considered as an element of a free algebra of an arbitrary rank. Hence the study of an individual polynomial via the modules of remainders is independent of the free algebra which contains it.

2.
Atomic factorizations in free algebras

Throughout this work
K
denotes an arbitrary commutative field, rings are unital
K
-algebras, and modules are unitary whence
K
-spaces, too. When we discuss to
nonunital subalgebras
of a given
K
-algebra
A
, we mean multiplicatively closed subspaces without identity. A ring
A
is
projective-free
if projective modules are free. It is worth to note that being projective-free is left-right symmetric. Two elements
a,b\in A
are
similar
(over
A
) if the left
A
-modules
A/Aa,A/Ab
are isomorphic. The
codimension
of a subspace is the dimension of its direct complement. The
colength
of a submodule is the length of its factor module.

A non-zero element of a ring
A
is called

irreducible
, or
an atom
in Cohn’s terminology
[
7
]
, if it cannot be written as
a product of two nonzero non-invertible elements. This definition is clearly preserved by automorphisms. A factorization of an element as a product of irreducible ones is called an
atomic factorization
. Notice that in the non commutative setting irreducible and prime elements may be different. For example, the equality
\lambda=(x_{1}x_{2}x_{3}+x_{1}+x_{3})(1+x_{2}x_{1})=(1+x_{1}x_{2})(x_{3}x_{2}x_{1}+x_{1}+x_{3})
analyzed in
[
16
]
shows that the irreducible element
1+x_{1}x_{2}
divides
\lambda
but it divides neither
1+x_{2}x_{1}
nor
x_{1}x_{2}x_{3}+x_{1}+x_{3}
. Indeed the non-commutativity requires to take into account both the left and the right division in the definition of primeness. Moreover, although prime elements are trivially irreducible, their existence is not necessarily guaranteed. Conversely, even if irreducible elements are not necessary prime, their existence is ensured for instance by the existence of a degree function, as in the case of polynomials in non-commuting variables, where any non-invertible element admits at least one atomic factorization. Thus in general it makes more sense to look for atomic than prime factorizations.

A free
K
-algebra
\Lambda=\Lambda_{n}

via

If a free generating set

(2)

\End(V_{\pi})

Theorem

Remark 2.3
.

As we will see in Section

, the definition of the
\star
-action in
\Lambda=\Lambda_{n}
is inspired by the multiplication by the elements
x_{i}^{*}\,(i=1,\cdots,n)
in the associated Leavitt algebra
L(1,n)
. In case of
n=1
, so that
\Lambda_{1}=K[x]
, the extended Leavitt algebra is the algebra of Laurent polynomials
K[x,x^{-1}]
and
x^{*}
can be seen as
x^{-1}
. Assume
\lambda=1+k_{1}x+\cdots+k_{l}x^{l}
is a comonic polynomial in
K[x]
, so that
\lambda=1+x\lambda_{x}
and
1=-x\lambda_{x}+\lambda
. If

is any polynomial in

, then
x\star_{\lambda}g=-k\lambda_{x}+g_{x}=x^{-1}\cdot g-kx^{-1}\lambda
, and hence the
\star_{\lambda}
-action coincides with the multiplication by
x^{-1}
modulo
K[x,x^{-1}]\lambda
. Consequently, using the
\star_{\lambda}
-action on
K[x]
, one can see that
g
is a multiple of
\lambda
, i.e.
g\in K[x]\lambda
, if and only if
x^{|g|}\star_{\lambda}g=0
.
Moreover, denoting by

\gamma=x^{l}+k_{1}x^{l-1}+\cdots+k_{l}=x^{l}\lambda(x^{-1})

the reciprocal polynomial of
\lambda
, we get
K[x,x^{-1}]\lambda=K[x,x^{-1}]\gamma(x^{-1})
establishing a one-to-one correspondence between remainders modulo
\lambda
in
K[x]
and
\gamma(x^{-1})
in
K[x^{-1}]
, respectively.

V_{\lambda}
is clearly the
K
-subspace of

spanned by
\{1,x,\cdots,x^{l-1}\}
and it is clearly closed under the
\star_{\lambda}
-action induced by the rule
x\star_{\lambda}1=-\lambda_{x}
(so that, for instance,
x\star_{\lambda}x=1
). In particular,

is isomorphic to the
K[x^{-1}]
-module
K[x^{-1}]/K[x^{-1}]\gamma(x^{-1})
with respect to the
K
-algebra isomorphism
K[x]\rightarrow K[x^{-1}]:x\mapsto x^{-1}
.
So
V_{\lambda}=\Lambda\star_{\lambda}1

is a cyclic module and the annihilator of

is the ideal
K[x]\gamma
. Indeed, from
x\star_{\lambda}x=1
it follows that
k_{i}x^{l-i+1}\star_{\lambda}1=x^{l-1}\star_{\lambda}k_{i}x^{i-1}
and

\gamma\star_{\lambda}1=(x^{l}+k_{1}x^{l-1}+\cdots+k_{l})\star_{\lambda}1=x^{l}\star_{\lambda}1+x^{l-1}\star_{\lambda}\lambda_{x}=

=x^{l-1}\star_{\lambda}(-\lambda_{x}+\lambda_{x})=x^{l-1}\star_{\lambda}0=0.

Hence in general

is not isomorphic to

even when
\lambda
is an irreducible polynomial.

In case
\lambda
is irreducible, the ideal

is maximal, and hence
V_{\lambda}
is isomorphic to the irreducible representation
K[x]/K[x]\gamma
.
Therefore, in case of one variable, Theorem
2.3
provides a parameterization of the irreducible representations of

alternative to the classical one. Namely, one uses the reciprocical
\gamma
of an irreducible polynomial
\lambda
for this job.

To conclude Remark
2.3
notice that
V_{\lambda}
can be identified with the space of remainders by the Euclidean division by
\lambda
. In the classical approach using the factor algebra

, in order to compute the remainder of

by
\lambda
without carrying over the division, one has to substitute

by
(-k_{0}-k_{1}x-\cdots-k_{l-1}x^{l-1})
. Unfortunately this approach is not extendable to the case of several non-commutative variables. However, using the module
V_{\lambda}
and the
\star_{\lambda}
action, in order to find the remainder of

by

, we can compute recursively

until we get an element in

. As we will see in Section
4
where the proof of Theorem

is presented, this approach can be generalized for several non-commutative variables and is, in fact, the crucial idea of the proof.

From now on, let us assume

. Then Theorem
2.3
shows a relatively transparent and simple way assigning to every proper and comonic polynomial

a finite-dimensional
\Lambda
-module

whose endomorphism ring is a finite-dimensional algebra.

plays the role of ”remainders” in a generalized Euclidean division algorithm by the comonic polynomial
\lambda

at the division by
\lambda
, and then to compute the unique expression

with

, as we will see in Section

[
7
]
,
[
9
]
.

3.
The tool: Leavitt algebras

By definition (cf.
[
13
]
) the

module type
of a ring is either
IBN
, an abbreviation for
invariant basis number
, that is, an isomorphism between modules generated freely by
m
and
n
elements, respectively, implies
m=n
; or the first integral pair
(m,n)\,(1\leq m\leq n-1)
such that modules generated freely by
m
and
n
elements, respectively, are isomorphic. This definition implies immediately the following obvious but important property of rings without IBN.

Proposition 3.1
.

If free modules of rank
m
and
n\geq m+1
, respectively, are isomorphic, then any finitely generated module can be generated by at most
m
elements. In particular, a ring of module type
(1,n),\,\,n\geq 2
is a Bezout ring, i.e., every finitely generated one-sided ideal is principal.

For the existence of rings of arbitrary module type
(m,n)
, Leavitt invented the algebra
\mathbb{L}_{K}(m,n)
by inverting the matrix
(x_{ij})
over the free algebra
\Lambda=K\langle x_{ij}\rangle\,(i=1,\cdots,m;j=1,\cdots,n)

[
13
]
. Although
\mathbb{L}_{K}(1,n)
is now somewhat well-understood,
\mathbb{L}_{K}(m,n)\,(2\leq m\leq n-1)

is still a big puzzle.

We present below an elementary and transparent approach to
\mathbb{L}_{K}(1,n)
, shortly
\mathbb{L}
, by listing results in such an order that their proof is immediate. For the details we refer to
[
4
]
.

By definition
\mathbb{L}
is the localization of
\Lambda=K\langle x_{1},\cdots,x_{n}\rangle=\Lambda_{n}
inverting the row
(x_{1},\cdots,x_{n})
, i.e.,
\mathbb{L}
is generated by elements
x_{i}
and
x^{*}_{i}\,(i=1,\cdots,n)
subject to relations

(7)

\displaystyle\mathop{\sum}\limits_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}x^{*}_{i}=1\qquad\&\qquad x^{*}_{j}x_{i}=\begin{cases}1&\text{if}\qquad j=i,\\
0&\text{if}\qquad j\neq i\end{cases}.

The images of
x_{i},x^{*}_{i}
in
\mathbb{L}
are also denoted by
x_{i},x^{*}_{i}
themselves by convenience and by the fact that free algebras
\Lambda
and
\Lambda^{*}
are isomorphic canonically to the
K
-subalgebras
\bar{\Lambda}
and
{\bar{\Lambda}}^{*}
of
\mathbb{L}
generated by
\{x_{1},\cdots,x_{n}\}
and
\{x^{*}_{1},\cdots x^{*}_{n}\}
, respectively. This result is immediate by Theorem
3.3
from which almost all basic properties of
\mathbb{L}
can be read off easily. By putting

b^{*}=x^{*}_{i_{l}}\cdots x^{*}_{i_{1}}
for
b=x_{i_{1}}\cdots x_{i_{l}}\in\bf B
, every element
r\in\mathbb{L}
can be expressed (not necessarily uniquely) as a linear combination
\sum k_{i}b_{i}c^{*}_{i}\,(0\neq k_{i}\in K;b_{i},c_{i}\in\bf B)
. To verify Theorem
3.3
we need first

Lemma 3.2
.

For any
r\in\mathbb{L}
,
r\in({\bar{\Lambda}}\cap r\mathbb{L})\mathbb{L}
and
{\bar{\Lambda}}\cap r\mathbb{L}
is a finitely generated right ideal of
\bar{\Lambda}
. In particular, if
\mathfrak{R}
is a right ideal of
\mathbb{L}
and
R=\bar{\Lambda}\cap\mathfrak{R}
, then

\mathfrak{R}=R\mathbb{L}
. Moreover
R
is a finitely generated right ideal of
\bar{\Lambda}
if and only if
\mathfrak{R}
is principal. By symmetry,
\mathfrak{L}=\mathbb{L}(\bar{\Lambda}^{*}\cap\mathfrak{L})=\mathbb{L}L
holds for every left ideal
\mathfrak{L}
of
\mathbb{L}
and
L
is a finitely generated left ideal of
{\bar{\Lambda}^{*}}
if and only if
{\mathfrak{L}}
is principal.

Proof.

The equality in
\mathbb{L}

1=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}x^{*}_{i}=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}\left(\sum\limits_{j=1}^{n}x_{j}x^{*}_{j}\right)x^{*}_{i}=\cdots=\sum\limits_{b,c\in\bf B}^{|b|=|c|=l}bc^{*}\quad(l\in\mathbb{N})

and
r\cdot 1=r=1\cdot r
imply

(8)

\displaystyle r=\sum\limits_{b,c\in\bf B}^{|b|=|c|=l}(rb)c^{*}=\sum\limits_{b,c\in\bf B}^{|b|=|c|=l}b(c^{*}r)\quad(l\in\mathbb{N})

with
rb\in\bar{\Lambda}
and
c^{*}r\in{\bar{\Lambda}}^{*}
for almost all
l
. Consequently, if
r
is represented as a linear combination

r=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{l}b_{i}c^{*}_{i}
with the least number
l
of non-zero coefficients, setting
d=d_{r}=\max\{|c_{i}|\,|\,i=1,\cdots,l\}
, then for
m=d+1
one has
rb\in r\mathbb{L}\cap\Lambda
for all monomials
b
of degree
m
. Thus
r\in(r\mathbb{L}\cap\bar{\Lambda})\mathbb{L}
holds by (
8
) and hence
\mathfrak{R}=R\mathbb{L}
. Moreover,
r\mathbb{L}\cap\bar{\Lambda}
is trivially generated by the finite set
\{rb\,|\,\deg b=m\}
, hence the proof is complete by Proposition
3.1
.
∎

Remark 3.1
.

Recall that
I
resp.,
I^{*}
is the two-sided ideal of
\Lambda
resp.,
\Lambda^{*}
defined by the set
\{x_{1},\cdots,x_{n}\}
resp.,
\{x^{*}_{1},\cdots,x^{*}_{n}\}
of variables. The corresponding ideal topology

is a right (resp., left) Gabriel topology of
\Lambda
(resp.,
\Lambda^{*}
). By
[
15
, Lemma IX.1.6]
,
Q_{I}(\Lambda)=\underrightarrow{\rm lim}_{l\in\mathbb{N}}\Hom_{\Lambda}(I^{l},\Lambda_{\Lambda})

is a ring with respect to the multiplication induced by partial composition of maps. Another equivalent way is to identify

with the subring of the maximal ring of right quotients of
\Lambda
generated by
\Lambda
and
\Hom_{\Lambda}(I_{\Lambda},\Lambda)
.
By definition
Q_{I}(\Lambda)
is the ring of right quotients of
\Lambda
by the Gabriel topology
\mathfrak{T}
.

Theorem 3.3
.

\mathbb{L}
is naturally isomorphic to the ring
Q_{I}(\Lambda)
of right quotients of
\Lambda
by the ideal topology
{\mathfrak{T}}=\{I^{l}\,|\,l\in\mathbb{N}\}
hence it is a flat left module over
\Lambda
. By symmetry,
\mathbb{L}
is also isomorphic to the ring
Q_{I^{*}}(\Lambda^{*})
of left quotients of
\Lambda^{*}
by the ideal topology
{\mathfrak{T}}^{*}=\{(I^{*})^{l}\,|\,l\in\mathbb{N}\}
hence it is a flat right module over
\Lambda^{*}
.

Proof.

Since the
I^{l}_{\Lambda}
’s are freely generated by
n^{l}
monomials of degree
l
,
\mathfrak{T}
is a perfect Gabriel topology by
[
15
, Propositions VI.6.10, XI.3.3 and XI.3.4(d)]
. Hence

Q_{I}(\Lambda)={\underrightarrow{\rm lim}}_{l\in\mathbb{N}}\Hom_{\Lambda}(I^{l},\Lambda_{\Lambda})

is a
K
-algebra, called the
ring of right quotients
of
\Lambda
by
\mathfrak{T}
, as we already mentioned. For every monomial
b\in{\bf B}\,(|b|=l\geq 1)
of positive degree
l
let

b^{\star}
be the right
\Lambda
-module homomorphism

b^{\star}:I^{l}\rightarrow\Lambda_{\Lambda}
such that

c\in I^{l}\cap{\bf B}\mapsto b^{\star}(c)=\begin{cases}1&\text{if}\qquad c=b,\\
0&\text{if}\qquad c\neq b\end{cases}.

In view of the equalities

I^{l}=\bigoplus\limits_{b\in\bf B}^{|b|=l}b\Lambda\qquad\&\qquad\Hom_{\Lambda}(I^{l}_{\Lambda},\Lambda)=\bigoplus\limits_{b\in\bf B}^{|b|=l}{{}_{\Lambda}\Lambda}b^{\star}

and the fact that all

are free left

-modules of rank

one obtains that
{{}_{\Lambda}Q_{I}}(\Lambda)
is flat. For
b=b_{i_{1}}\cdots b_{i_{l}}

in

. Hence
\Lambda
and the
\,x^{\star}_{i}
’s generate
Q_{I}(\Lambda)
and the row
(x_{1},\cdots,x_{n})
is the inverse of the column
{}^{t}(x^{\star}_{1},\cdots,x^{\star}_{n})
. Therefore, from the definition of
\mathbb{L}
, there is a canonical map
\phi:\mathbb{L}\rightarrow Q_{I}(\Lambda)
induced by the identity map on
\Lambda
and the rule

for

. Consequently,
\phi
is surjective and restricts to the identity on
\Lambda
whence
\Lambda
is a subalgebra of
\mathbb{L}
.

For the injectivity of

, assume
\phi(r)=0
for some

. Then, by Lemma
3.2
,

implies

for some monomial

and so

is isomorphic to

.
∎

Interchanging the role of
x_{i}
and

one obtains

Corollary 3.4
.

The free algebra
\Lambda^{*}=K\langle x^{*}_{1},\cdots,x^{*}_{n}\rangle
is naturally isomorphic to the subalgebra

of
\mathbb{L}
. The rule

induces an involution of
\mathbb{L}
which restricts to the canonical anti-isomorphism from

to

.

Hence
\Lambda
and
\Lambda^{*}
are viewed naturally as
K
-subalgebras of
\mathbb{L}
. Moreover,
\Lambda^{*}
and

.

Since a nonunital subalgebra

of codimension 1 is both a two-sided ideal and hence a free nonunital algebra of rank

, as it is straightforward to verify, Theorem
3.3
provides a variable-free definition of
\mathbb{L}
and sheds a light on ”canonical” involutions of

and a close connection to the automorphism groups of free algebras.

Corollary 3.5
.

If

is a nonunital subalgebra of codimension 1, then

is a two-sided ideal of
\Lambda
and
\mathbb{L}\cong Q_{A}(\Lambda)
, the ring of right quotients with respect to the ideal topology

.

As a ring of partial maps, every element

is represented by a

-homomorphism

from the right ideal

and

and

a monomial contained in the range

as subalgebras of

Proposition 3.7
.

If

, then
\mathbb{L}
is a purely infinite simple

one has

for suitable elements

Assume

, let

and consider the elements

In the following corollary we list some basic properties of
\mathbb{L}
that can be deduced from Theorem
3.3
. In particular Claim (3) describes the open left ideals of
\mathbb{L}
.

Corollary 3.8
.

Let
\mathfrak{L}
a left ideal of
\mathbb{L}
and
L=\Lambda^{*}\cap\mathfrak{L}
. Then:

(1)

\mathfrak{L}=\mathbb{L}L
holds for every left ideal
\mathfrak{L}
of
\mathbb{L}
, so
L
is a finitely generated left ideal of
\Lambda^{*}
if and only if
\mathfrak{L}
is finitely generated.

(2)

The algebra
\mathbb{L}
is hereditary and projective-free.

(3)

A left ideal
N
of
\Lambda^{*}
satisfies
\mathbb{L}N=\mathbb{L}
iff
N
contains a power of
I^{*}
.

(4)

\mathfrak{L}
is finitely generated of finite colength if and only if
L
is of finite codimension.

Proof.

Claim
(1)
is given by Lemma
3.2
.

Claim (2) follows from the fact that
\mathfrak{L}=\mathbb{L}L=\mathbb{L}\otimes_{\Lambda^{*}}L
by the flatness of
\mathbb{L}_{\Lambda^{*}}
ensured in Theorem
3.3
. Thus we conclude since free algebras, being free ideal rings, are hereditary and projective-free.

If
N
contains
I^{l}
for a suitable
l\geq 0
, then from the identity
1=\sum\limits_{b,c\in\bf B}^{|b|=|c|=l}bc^{*}
we get
\mathbb{L}N=\mathbb{L}
. Conversely, if
1=\sum\limits_{i=1}r_{i}\lambda^{*}_{i}
for appropriate

and
\lambda^{*}_{i}\in N
, we can choose an integer

big enough such that
b^{*}r_{i}\in\Lambda^{*}
for any

with
|b|=l
and any

. So we get that

. This shows Claim (3).

Since
\mathbb{L}
is projective free, any principal left ideal is free and of infinite length. It follows easily that if
\mathfrak{L}
has finite colength, then it is essential. From the essentiality of
\mathfrak{L}
we get that also
L
is essential in
\Lambda^{*}
. By the result of Rosenmann and Rosset in
[

, we conclude that

has finite codimension since it is finitely generated and essential.
To conclude Claim

assume

has finite codimension. In particular, again by
[
14
, Theorem 3.3]
, it is finitely generated and hence
\mathfrak{L}
is finitely generated. Notice that if
\mathfrak{L}<\mathfrak{M}<\mathbb{L}
, then
L<\mathfrak{M}\cap\Lambda^{*}<\Lambda^{*}
, so
\mathfrak{L}
is of finite colength since

has finite colength.
∎

In the notation of the previous corollary, if
L
is an arbitrary maximal left ideal of

such that the associated primitive ideal

, then

. For,

. Consequently, one has

, which is impossible. Hence

by Corollary

is maximal, Corollary

(1) implies that
\mathbb{L}L

is a maximal left ideal of

.

different from

. In this way one obtains a nice large class of new simple modules over

with interesting endomorphism algebras. The subclass induced by maximal left ideals of

. Hence if

satisfies

\Lambda^{*}

.
Since

and ones of finite-dimensional non-trivial (i.e., not isomorphic to the trivial

Localization theory shows that the torsion modules defined by the Gabriel topology
{\mathfrak{T}}^{*}=\{(I^{*})^{l}\,|\,l\in\mathbb{N}\}
form a Serre localizing subcategory of
\Lambda^{*}
-Mod and
\mathbb{L}
-Mod is the quotient category of
\Lambda^{*}
-Mod by this Serre category. Hence Ara’s Proposition 3.3 and Theorem 5.1
[

are consequences of Theorem

and the Gabriel’s localization theory. However, one need not to use sledge hammer to crack nuts and to both readers’ benefit and the sake of the elementary self-containedness we present the special case of finitely presented modules of finite length.

Let
\mathfrak{FD}
be the full subcategory of finite-dimensional left
\Lambda^{*}
-modules. A finite-dimensional
\Lambda^{*}
-module is called a

module if none of its simple subfactors is isomorphic to the trivial module

, that is, its annihilator ideal is a product of maximal two-sided finite-codimensional ideals different from

. The full subcategory of
\Lambda^{*}
-Mod consisting of all Leavitt modules is called the

. A finite-dimensional module is called a

K
. Any

contains the largest submodule

which has all simple subfactors isomorphic to the trivial module
K
. Then
T(M/T(M))=0
holds clearly.
T(M)
is called the

or simply
torsion submodule
of

is a
(Leavitt) torsionfree module
. Leavitt modules are clearly torsionfree but torsionfree modules are not necessarily Leavitt modules since they may contain simple subfactors which are not contained in their socle and isomorphic to the simple trivial module
K
. The full subcategory
\mathfrak{LT}
of Leavitt-torsion modules is clearly a Serre subcategory of
\mathfrak{FD}
. If
M
is a Leavitt module of length

\mathbb{L}_{\Lambda^{*}}
implies that
{\mathfrak{M}}=\mathbb{L}\otimes_{\Lambda^{*}}M
is finitely presented and has the same length
l
. Namely, if

is a composition chain of
M
, then the flatness of
\mathbb{L}_{\Lambda^{*}}
implies that

\mathbb{L}\otimes_{\Lambda^{*}}M_{i}

(i=0,1,\cdots,l)
form a corresponding composition chain of
\mathfrak{M}
. Hence by comparing lengths and by the now routine use of the equality (
8
) together with similar reasoning for Corollary
3.8
, one can see that

is isomorphic to

\{1\otimes m\,|\,m\in M\}\subseteq\mathfrak{M}
as left modules over
\Lambda^{*}
. After this identification we have the lattice isomorphism between the submodule lattices of
\mathfrak{M}
and
M
induced by
N\subseteq M\mapsto{\mathfrak{N}}=\mathbb{L}\otimes_{\Lambda^{*}}N=\mathbb{L}N\mapsto{\mathfrak{N}}\cap M

\mathbb{L}\otimes f:{\mathfrak{M}}=\mathbb{L}\otimes_{\Lambda^{*}}M\rightarrow{\mathfrak{N}}=\mathbb{L}\otimes_{\Lambda^{*}}N

{\mathfrak{f}}:{\mathfrak{M}}\rightarrow\mathfrak{N}

of

-Mod.
On the other hand, if

is any finitely presented left

Lemma 4.1

Let

The following theorem is the key tool in proving Theorem

Theorem 4.9

Let

Proof.

Without loss of generality one can assume that

If

\lambda=1
. Hence one can assume
\mathfrak{L}\neq\mathbb{L}
. Since
\gamma
does not belong to
\mathbb{L}\delta

there exits a monomial

We conclude this section emphasizing how our approach of considering polynomials in
\Lambda
as elements of
\mathbb{L}
and studying their associated modules could be an effective tool for the study of the arithmetic of polynomials in noncommutating
variables. First of all, given any finitely many arbitrary polynomials
\lambda_{1},\cdots,\lambda_{l}\in K\langle x_{1},\cdots x_{n}\rangle
, we can define their
generalized greatest common divisor
, shortly
greatest common divisor
, as the polynomial with constant
\delta\in K\langle x_{1},\cdots,x_{n}\rangle
which is a generator of the left ideal
\sum\limits_{i=1}^{l}\mathbb{L}\lambda_{i}
. As an immediate consequence of Corollary
4.11
, this greatest common divisor
\delta
exists and is unique up to a scalar, even in the case that the ordinary least common multiple of the
\lambda_{i}
’s is
0
. It is important to note the following: while the greatest common divisor of a polynomial with constant is itself up to a scalar, they are different in the case of a polynomial without constant, since the greatest common divisor has constant by definition. For example, the equality
\mathbb{L}(x_{1}+x^{3}_{1}+x_{1}x^{2}_{2}+x_{2}+x_{2}x^{2}_{1}+x^{3}_{2})=\mathbb{L}(1+x^{2}_{1}+x^{2}_{2})
shows by the definition that the greatest common divisor of the polynomial
\lambda=x_{1}+x^{3}_{1}+x_{1}x^{2}_{2}+x_{2}+x_{2}x^{2}_{1}+x^{3}_{2}
is the comonic polynomial
1+x^{2}_{1}+x^{2}_{2}
. Theorem
4.9
offers also an algorithm to find
\lambda
. Because of its importance, we rephrase this fact together with Theorem
4.9
as

Theorem 4.16
.

Given
\lambda
and
\gamma
monic polynomials in
\Lambda
, then there exists a unique monic polynomial
\delta
in
\Lambda
such that
\delta
is a right divisor of
\lambda
and
\gamma
, and any other common right divisor is a right divisor of
\delta
.

For example, let
\lambda=1+x^{2}_{1}+x^{2}_{2}
. Then
V_{\lambda}=K+Kx_{1}+Kx_{2}
has dimension 3. It is easy to see that
V_{\lambda}
is a simple
\Lambda
-module with
x_{1}\star_{\lambda}1=-x_{1}
and
x_{2}\star_{\lambda}1=-x_{2}
. Hence
\lambda
is irreducible. Let
\gamma=1+x_{1}+x^{2}_{1}
. Then
x^{2}_{1}\star_{\lambda}\gamma=(x^{2}_{1}\star_{\lambda}1)+(x^{2}_{1}\star_{\lambda}x_{1})+(x^{2}_{1}\star_{\lambda}x^{2}_{1})=-1-x_{1}+1=-x_{1}\neq 0
implies that the greates common divisor of
\lambda
and
\gamma
is 1. If
\alpha=1+x^{2}_{1}+x_{2}+x_{2}x^{2}_{1}+x^{2}_{2}+x^{3}_{2}
, then one can check routinely that
b\star_{\lambda}\alpha=0
for all monomials
b
in
x_{1},x_{2}
of degree 3. Hence
\lambda
is the greatest common divisor of
\alpha,\lambda
. Actually,
\alpha=(1+x_{2})\lambda
holds.

Moreover one can solve the so-called ”membership problem” in
\Lambda
by a relatively simple and quick algorithm as an easy consequence of Theorem
4.8
. Namely, let
\gamma=1+\sum x_{i}\gamma_{i}=1+\underline{\gamma}
be a comonic polynomial in
\Lambda
and
\lambda=k_{\lambda}+\sum x_{i}\lambda_{i}
an arbitrary polynomial of degree
m
. Let
R_{\lambda}=\{\delta_{b}=b\star_{\gamma}\lambda\neq 0\ \,\,|b\in\bf B,\,|b|=|\lambda|\}\subseteq V_{\gamma}
. According to Theorem
4.8

\gamma
is a right factor of
\lambda
if and only if this set
R_{\lambda}
is empty. Hence
R_{\lambda}
behaves like a set of ”generalized remainders of
\lambda
at division on the left by
\gamma
. If
R_{\lambda}
is not empty, then

\mathbb{L}\gamma+\mathbb{L}\lambda=\mathbb{L}\gamma+\sum\limits_{\delta_{b}\in R_{\lambda}}\delta_{b}

and for
\delta_{\lambda}=\sum\limits_{\delta_{b}\in R_{\lambda}}b\delta_{b}
one can find algorithmically
\rho_{\lambda}\in\Lambda
such that
\lambda=\rho_{\lambda}\gamma+\delta_{\lambda}
.

Hence we have an expression
\lambda=\rho_{\lambda}\gamma+\delta_{\lambda}
as a final result of dividing
\lambda
by
\gamma
. We summarize these results in the following theorem.

Theorem 4.17
.

Let
\gamma\in\Lambda
be a comonic polynomial and
\lambda\in\Lambda
of degree
m
. Then
\lambda\in\Lambda\gamma
if and only if
b\star_{\gamma}\lambda=0
holds for every monomial
b\in\bf B
of degree
m
. Moreover, if
R_{\lambda}=\{\delta_{b}=b\star_{\gamma}\lambda\neq 0\,|\,|b|=m\}
, then there is a unique polynomial
\rho_{\lambda}\in\Lambda
satisfying
\lambda=\rho_{\lambda}\gamma+\delta_{\lambda}
where
\delta_{\lambda}=\sum\limits_{\delta_{b}\in R_{\lambda}}b\delta_{b}
. By symmetry, one has an algorithm to determine the right quotient
{}_{\lambda}\rho
and the set of remainders
{{}_{\lambda}}R=\{_{b}\delta=\lambda\star_{\gamma}b\neq 0\,|\,|b|=m\}
together with the expression
\lambda=\gamma({{}_{\lambda}\rho})+_{\lambda}\delta
where
{}_{\lambda}\delta=\sum\limits_{{}_{b}\delta\in{{}_{\lambda}}R}{{}_{b}\delta}b
.

Remarks 4.4
.

(1)

For the particular computation it is worth to note that it is not necessarily to compute all
b\star_{\gamma}\lambda
with
|b|=m=|\lambda|
. It is enough to take such monomials
b
of a minimal degree with
b\star_{\gamma}\lambda=0
or
b\star_{\gamma}\lambda\in V_{\gamma}
, respectively. The constraint
|b|=m
is required only to grant that
b\star_{\gamma}\lambda
is an element of
V_{\gamma}
when it is not zero.

(2)

Although the ”membership problem” is solved, the division algorithm is completely different from the case of polynomials in commutating variables despite of the fact that it looks formally almost the same. First the ”generalized remainder”
\delta_{\lambda}
by dividing
\lambda
by
\gamma
is, in general, not an element of
V_{\gamma}
. Secondly, the degree of
\delta_{\lambda}
can be arbitrary. In fact, we have no idea to describe the set
\{\delta_{\lambda}=\sum b\delta_{b}\,|\,\lambda\in\Lambda\}
. It is clear that every polynomial
\mu\in\Lambda
with
|\mu|\leq|\gamma|
can appear as a remainder at a division by
\gamma
if
\mu
is not a scalar multiple of
\gamma
in case of equal degree. For example, if
\gamma=1-x_{1}
, then
\mu=1-x_{2}\notin\Lambda\gamma
but its ”strict remainder” in our sense is
x^{*}_{1}\mu=x^{*}_{1}\equiv 1
mod
\mathbb{L}\gamma
. Even for
m=1-x^{l}_{1}x_{2}
we have
(x^{*}_{1})^{l}\mu=(x^{*}_{1})^{l}-x_{2}\equiv 1-x_{2}\equiv 1
mod
\mathbb{L}\gamma
. Therefore for the particular use it is reasonable to determine only the set
R_{\lambda}
.

(3)

To check the simplicity of
\mathbb{L}/\mathbb{L}\pi
where
\pi
has constant, one needs compute
\mathbb{L}\pi+\mathbb{L}\alpha
where
\alpha
runs over all nonzero elements of
V_{\pi}
. This is a definitely harder, exhaustive job than the memership problem. According to Theorem
4.9
it can be verified in finitely many steps for each element
\alpha\in V_{\pi}
. Hence the exercise can be carried out in finitely many steps when the base field
K
is finite. If
K
is infinite, then
V_{\pi}
has unfortunately infinitely many elements although it has a finite dimension. Consequently, finding right factors and irreducible factorizations of a polynomial is more difficult job.

Finally, by Theorem
2.3
we know that, given
\lambda\in\Lambda
, its irreducible decompositions are unique up to similarity. For some particular polynomials we can improve this result obtaining the uniqueness of the irreducible factorization in the classical sense.

Corollary 4.18
.

If
\lambda=\pi_{1}\pi_{2}\cdots\pi_{l}
is a product of polynomials
\pi_{i}
similar to an irreduclible polynomial
\pi
, then this factorization of
\lambda
is unique up to a scalar, that is, if
\lambda=\delta_{1}\cdots\delta_{l}
is another prime factorization of
\lambda
, then each
\delta_{j}
is a scalar multiple of
\pi_{j}
for
j=1,\cdots,l
.

Proof.

Since
V_{\lambda}
has the distributive submodule lattice by Corollary
4.12
,
one obtains immediately that
V_{\lambda}
and
\mathbb{L}/\mathbb{L}\lambda
are uniserial modules, that is, their submodules form a unique finite chain if all of their simple subfactors are isomorphic. We then conclude the proof recalling that if
\mathbb{L}\delta=\mathbb{L}\gamma
for
\gamma
and
\delta
in
\Lambda
with constant, then by Corollary
4.2
we get that

\delta
is a scalar multiple of
\gamma

∎

5.
Applications to factorizations of polynomials

In the next two sections we give some hints for future directions that can be explored.
As shown by results and techniques exposed in the previous sections, free algebras and Leavitt algebras are deeply interrelated. Facts in free algebras are used to
investigate Leavitt algebras and vice versa. However, there is sometime the need to treat them separately in their own language, to better understand their applications and their relevance in the setting we choose to focus on. For instance, from this point of view, results of Section

4
provide an algorithm to find factorizations of
\lambda\in\Lambda
starting from the right. By symmetry or by using the canonical
∗
involution of
\mathbb{L}
and the anti-isomorphism between
\Lambda
and
\Lambda^{*}
, they yield also a way to find factorizations of
\lambda
starting from the left.
Moreover, given a comonic polynomial
\lambda\in\Lambda
, instead of

\mathbb{L}/\mathbb{L}\lambda
and
\Lambda^{*}/L
one can use
V_{\lambda}
and the algorithm described in proof of Theorem
4.9
to decide irreducibility, similarity, factorization, and more generally, to do standard arithmetic of non-commutative polynomials. Since
\End_{\Lambda}(V_{\lambda})
is a finite-dimensional division
K
-algebra and
V_{\lambda}
has a quite easily handled
K
-basis it is interesting to closely study
\End_{\Lambda}(V_{\lambda})
. The first question arising is which non-algebraically closed field
K
has a comonic irreducible polynomial
\lambda\in\Lambda
such that
\End_{\Lambda}(V_{\lambda})
is non-commutative. The next one is to determine non-commutative finite-dimensional division
K
-algebras which are not representable as
\End_{\Lambda}(V_{\lambda})
of irreducible polynomials
\lambda
, and so on.

5.1.
Companions to a polynomial: similarity, remainders etc.

Polynomials in not necessarily commuting variables form perhaps the most classical and quite hard subject of algebra. By definition, a polynomial
\lambda
is dependent only on variables appearing in its expression. Hence it does not depend on the carrying free algebra
\Lambda
, which may have even infinitely many variables. From this point of view the finite-dimensional space
V_{\lambda}
seems to be a good tool, a companion data structure as a simple combinatorial invariant. It plays the role of the module of remainders and it is easy to describe. As we have already noticed, it can be used to determine whether
\lambda
is irreducible and then to find, at least theoretically, its factorizations together with other related structures like its endomorphism ring and similar polynomials. By definition, to find a polynomial
\gamma
similar to
\lambda
one needs first to compute the factor module
M{{}_{\lambda}}=\Lambda/\Lambda\lambda\cong\mathbb{L}/\mathbb{L}\lambda
, then find another generator
m
of
M_{\lambda}
such that
\ann_{\mathbb{L}}m=\{r\in\mathbb{L}\,|\,rm=0\in M_{\lambda}\}\neq\mathbb{L}\lambda
, and finally look for a polynomial
\gamma\in\Lambda
such that
\ann_{\mathbb{L}}m=\mathbb{L}\gamma
. Hence
\gamma
is similar to
\lambda
.

However, this direct approach with a few exception is not feasible. Fortunately, for a comonic polynomial
\lambda
one can use
V_{\lambda}
to provide a way to compute all polynomials similar to
\lambda
, that is, to determine the similarity class
\mathfrak{S}\lambda
of
\lambda
. Since
V_{\lambda}
is finite-dimensional, it is theoretically feasible to find all the generators of
V_{\lambda}
and then to any generator

the unique comonic polynomial
\gamma_{m}
similar to
\lambda
satisfying
\mathbb{L}\gamma_{m}=\mathbb{L}L_{m}
, where
L_{m}
is the image of
\ann_{\Lambda}m=\{\mu\in\Lambda\,\,|\,\,\mu\star m=0\}
under the canonical isomorphism from
\Lambda
onto
\Lambda^{*}
, provided that such a
\gamma_{m}
exists.
These results are presented in the next claim.

Proposition 5.1
.

Let
\lambda\in\Lambda
be a comonic polynomial. Then,
up to non-zero scalars and automorphisms of
V_{\lambda}
, every comonic polynomial
\gamma
similar to
\lambda
determines uniquely a generator
m_{\gamma}
of
V_{\lambda}
.

Proof.

By Corollary
4.15
, the
\Lambda
-modules
V_{\gamma}
and
V_{\lambda}
are isomorphic, whence
\gamma
determines uniquely a generator
m_{\gamma}
of
V_{\lambda}
up to automorphisms of
V_{\lambda}
, where the automorphism group of
V_{\lambda}
is isomorphic to that of the
\mathbb{L}
-module
{\mathbb{L}}/{\mathbb{L}\lambda}
.
∎

Remark 5.1
.

This assertion can be considered as a starting step describing similarity classes of polynomials. As the first trivial application one sees immediately that the similarity class of a linear polynomial with constant has exactly one element up to scalars. In view of
[
7
, 1st ed. Proposition 3.3.4]
similar polynomials provide a method to find different not permutated factorizations. In particular, until Proposition
5.1
there is no practical method finding similar polynomials. A thorough study of similar polynomials will be a subject of forthcoming works.

Example 5.2
.

In commutative algebra it is well-known that the maximal ideals of the polynomial algebra
A=K[x_{1},\cdots,x_{n}]
of codimension 1, are the ideals
\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}A(x_{i}-k_{i})
where
{\mathfrak{k}}=(k_{1},\cdots,k_{n})\in K^{n}
runs over the points in the
n
-dimensional affine space

\Lambda_{n}

-modules are clearly parameterized by the polynomials

for points
{\mathfrak{k}}=(k_{1},\cdots,k_{n})\in K^{n}
.

Example 5.3
.

Let
\lambda=1+x_{1}x_{2}\in\Lambda=\Lambda_{2}
. We compute the similarity class of
\lambda
. By definition the 2-dimensional
K
-space
V_{\lambda}=\{1,x_{2}\}
is a
\Lambda_{2}
-module with respect to

and
x_{2}\star 1=0
. One can see that

is irreducible and
\{1+kx_{2},\,x_{2}\,\,|\,\,k\in K\}
is the set of all generators of
V_{\lambda}
. Put

, then
\ann_{\Lambda}v
is a
\Lambda_{2}
-module freely generated by

whence

is freely generated by
\{x^{*}_{2},(x^{*}_{1})^{2},\,1+x^{*}_{2}x^{*}_{1}\}

we see that
\ann w

and so by symmetry

, that is,
\lambda
is similar to
1+x_{2}x_{1}
.

To describe the similarity class of
\lambda
, for any

let
v_{k}=1+kx_{2}

be an arbitrary generator of
V_{\lambda}
different from
1
and
x_{2}
. We intend to look for the unique comonic polynomial
\gamma_{k}\in\Lambda

is the image of
\ann v_{k}

\Lambda
onto
\Lambda^{*}

contains 1 and has

must have degree 2 in view of the definition of

has a

\gamma_{k}=1+x_{1}\gamma_{1}+x_{2}\gamma_{2}

and

from

Hence

\{x^{*}_{i}x^{*}_{j}\gamma\,\,|\,i,j=1,2\}\subseteq L_{k}

\{(x^{*}_{1})^{2}+l_{1}x^{*}_{1}+l_{1}l_{2},(x^{*}_{2})^{2}+m_{1}x^{*}_{2}+m_{2}k_{2},x^{*}_{1}x^{*}_{2}+m_{1}x^{*}_{1}+k_{1}m_{2},x^{*}_{2}x^{*}_{1}+l_{1}x^{*}_{2}+k_{2}l_{2}\}

.
By

5.2.
Factorizations of polynomials

Using Leavitt algebras to investigate polynomials requires that the studied polynomials must be with constant. In view of Lemma
3.10
, a polynomial
\lambda\in\Lambda
without constant induces a finite set
\{\lambda_{b}\}
of its cofactors, where the
\lambda_{b}
’s run over monomials of minimal degree in
\lambda
and they are with constant. So the
\lambda_{b}
’s admit a greatest common divisor
\delta
by Theorem
4.9
. Hence

\delta
divides
\lambda
if
\delta
is not a constant. In this way one can find all right factors of
\lambda
with constant and by symmetry left factors with constant, too. However, this argument does not work in case
\delta\in K
. One can avoid this restriction by an appropriate substitution. For example, one can pass from the polynomial
x^{2}_{1}x^{3}_{2}
to the polynomial
(x_{1}+1)^{2}(x_{2}+1)^{3}
. The feasibility of this trick depends on a problem whether there exists a polynomial
\lambda
with zero constant which remains with zero constant term at any possible substitution of variables.This opens the question whether the intersection of nonunital subalgebras of codimension 1 is zero as we have already pointed out.

In any case, using Leavitt algebra may help to find factors with constant of a polynomial
\lambda
without constant when
\mathbb{L}\lambda\neq\mathbb{L}
. For example, let

\lambda=x_{1}x_{2}x_{3}x_{2}x_{1}+x_{1}x_{2}x_{3}+x_{1}x_{2}x_{1}+x_{1}+x_{3}x_{2}x_{1}+x_{3}

be the polynomial considered by Volčič
[
16
]
. Then by Corollary
3.10

\mathbb{L}\lambda=\mathbb{L}\lambda_{1}+\mathbb{L}\lambda_{3}=\mathbb{L}\lambda_{3}
by
\lambda_{1}=\lambda_{3}+x_{2}x_{3}\lambda_{3}
, where
\lambda_{1}=x_{2}(x_{3}x_{2}x_{1}+x_{1}+x_{3})+1
and
\lambda_{3}=x_{2}x_{1}+1
. Consequently,
\lambda_{3}
is a right factor of
\lambda=(x_{1}x_{2}x_{3}+x_{1}+x_{3})\lambda_{3}
. On the other hand, one can use left factors of
\lambda
, that is, left factors of the image of
\lambda
with respect to the
\ast
-adding isomorphism to obtain another factorization of
\lambda=(x_{1}x_{2}+1)(x_{3}x_{2}x_{1}+x_{3}+x_{1})
. Iterating the argument of this example one obtains immediately together with Cohn’s characterization of similarity
[
7
, Proposition 3.3.4]
the following result on factorizations of polynomials without constant.

Proposition 5.2
.

Let
\lambda\in\Lambda
be a polynomial without constant and
\overline{\lambda}
the image of
\lambda
by the canonical isomorphism from
\Lambda
onto
\Lambda^{*}
. If
\mathbb{L}\lambda\neq\mathbb{L}\neq{\overline{\lambda}}\mathbb{L}
, then
\lambda
has two different factorizations
\lambda=\alpha\beta=\gamma\delta
such that

\alpha,\delta
have constant and
\beta,\gamma
have no constant.

Proof.

The assumption that
\mathbb{L}\lambda\neq\mathbb{L}
implies that
\lambda
has a factorization
\lambda=\gamma\delta
such that
\delta
has constant and
\gamma
has no constant. The condition
{\overline{\lambda}}\mathbb{L}\neq\mathbb{L}
implies by symmetry that
{\overline{\lambda}}={\overline{a}}{\overline{\beta}}
, that is,
\lambda
has a factorization
\lambda=\alpha\beta
where
\alpha
has constant but
\beta
has no constant.
∎

Therefore we have a way to produce polynomials having different factorizations. Just take a product
\lambda=\alpha\beta
such that
\alpha
is a product of irreducible polynomials with constant and
\beta
is a product of irreducible ones without constant. If
\mathbb{L}\lambda\neq\mathbb{L}
, then
\lambda
admits another factorization
\lambda=\gamma\delta
where
\delta
has constant. In view of the results
of Section
4
, it is a routine matter to check whether
\mathbb{L}\lambda=\mathbb{L}
holds. One can start with any polynomial
\lambda
without constant and then use its left or right cofactors to check the inequalities
\mathbb{L}\lambda\neq\mathbb{L}\neq{\overline{\lambda}}\mathbb{L}
where
\overline{\lambda}
is the image of
\lambda
by the canonical isomorphism in
\Lambda^{*}
. This argument can not be applied for polynomials
\lambda
without constant satisfying
\mathbb{L}\lambda=\mathbb{L}=\lambda\mathbb{L}
such that
\lambda
remains without constant under any variables substitution, even in the case when
\lambda
has a factorization
\lambda=\alpha\beta\gamma
where
\alpha
and
\gamma
are polynomials without constant and
\beta
is one with constant.

In order to find atomic factorizations of a comonic polynomial
\lambda\in\Lambda
, we focus first to a right comonic factor
\gamma\in\Lambda
of
\lambda
. This means exactly that there is a positive integer
l\in\mathbb{N}
such that
b\star_{\gamma}\lambda=0
for all monomials
b\in\bf B
of degree
l
. So this is a routine calculation to verify whether
\gamma
is a right factor of another comonic polynomial. To compute the polynomial
\delta
satisfying
\lambda=\delta\gamma
we use the equality for each index
i\in\{1,2,\cdots,n\}

x_{i}\star_{\gamma}\lambda=x_{i}\star_{\gamma}\delta\gamma=x_{i}\star_{\gamma}(\gamma+{\bar{\delta}}\gamma)=\delta_{i}\gamma.

Taking in account that
\delta=1+{\bar{\delta}}=1+\mathop{\sum}\limits_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}\delta_{i}=1+\mathop{\sum}\limits_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}\delta_{x_{i}}
the determination of
\delta
reduces to compute
now a not necessarily comonic polynomial
\delta_{1}
with constant satisfying
\delta_{i}\gamma=x_{i}\star_{\gamma}\lambda
where for each
i\in\{1,2,\cdots,n\}
the polynomial
x_{i}\star_{\gamma}\lambda
has lower degree. Thus in principle one can compute the left quotient
\delta
of
\lambda
at the right division by
\gamma
.

We are now in position to find atomic factorizations of
\lambda
. It is routine to compute
V_{\lambda}
. Then one can continue as for integers. Take an arbitrary comonic polynomial
\gamma\in V_{\lambda}
and verify if
\gamma
does divide
\lambda
by computing
b\star_{\gamma}\lambda
for all monomials
b\in\bf B
with
|b|=|\lambda|
. In the positive case one finds further a factorization
\lambda=\delta\gamma
, and the problem of factorization reduces to one of lower degree. In principle this can be carry out without difficulty in case of a finite basic field
K
. However, there is a better way with possibly less computations as follows.
By Corollary
4.14
, irreducible left factors of
\lambda
correspond to minimal submodules of
V_{\lambda}
. So one can look for a minimal submodule
S
of
V_{\lambda}
, then to a generator
m
of
S
and finally to an irreducible polynomial
\pi
determining
m
as in
5.1
. Next step is to check whether
\pi
divides
\lambda
from the left. To check this matter, we need to use
W_{\pi}
which is the simple finite-dimensional right
\Lambda
-module associated to
\pi
defined in Remark
2.2
, and verify whether
\lambda\star_{\pi}b=0
for all monomials
b\in\bf B
of degree
|\lambda|
. If
\pi
divides
\lambda
, then one can use the right symmetric version of the above described process to compute the right quotient
\lambda_{1}
of
\lambda
at the left division by
\pi
, i.e.,
\lambda=\pi\lambda_{1}
and continue in the same way for
\lambda_{1}
instead of
\lambda
. If
\pi
does not divide
\lambda
, then
look for the other generators of
S
, until we find a polynomial similar to
\pi
which divides
\lambda
. This method can be carried out step by step until an atomic factorization of
\lambda
is found. This is a method to find atomic factorizations of
\lambda
from the left hand side. To find right irreducible factors of
\lambda
, and then atomic factorizations of
\lambda
starting from the right hand side, one need first to compute
W_{\lambda}
, generators of the simple submodules of
W_{\lambda}
and then irreducible polynomials
\pi
representing them which become the right factors of
\lambda
. Of course the process can be very hard, complicated from a computational point of view. It is also worth to note that we have to use here both the left and right version of Theorem
4.9
to get all possible factorizations of
\lambda
.

6.
Final remarks and further open questions

In this last section we continue a discussion of an individual polynomial in noncommuting variables. One companion structure of a polynomial is a primary decomposition. A polynomial
\lambda
is called
primary
if the associated module
V_{\lambda}
is indecomposable. In contrast to the case of polynomials in commuting variables, primary polynomials in noncommuting variables have a great diversity. For example,
x_{1}x_{2}x_{3}
and its permutated polynomials
x_{i}x_{j}x_{k}
are all primary and none of them is similar to the other. Since
V_{\lambda}
is finite-dimensional, it is a direct sum of its indecomposable submodules and this decomposition is unique up to isomorphism by Krull-Schmidt theorem. A challenging problem is to find factorizations of a comonic polynomial
\lambda
into products of primary polynomials.

6.1.
Primary decomposition of a polynomial

For a comonic polynomial
\lambda\,(|\lambda|\geq 1)
the
K
-vector space
V_{\lambda}
is a finite-dimensional
\Lambda
-module. Hence it decomposes uniquely as a direct sum of indecomposable submodules
V_{j}
up to permutations. Indecomposable direct summands of
V_{\lambda}
are called
primary components
of
V_{\lambda}
. They are determined by right factors
\pi_{j}
of
\lambda
and can be considered as invariants of
\lambda
. However, we know almost nothing about them. For example we do not know whether
\lambda
is their product. Since
V_{\lambda}
has a distributive submodule lattice, Corollary
4.18
shows that, if
\lambda=\pi_{1}\pi_{2}\cdots\pi_{l}
is a product of polynomials
\pi_{i}
similar to the same irreducible polynomial
\pi
, then
V_{\lambda}
is uniserial and hence it has a unique primary component.

It is an interesting project to study primary decompositions closely. For instance, in contrast to the commutative case, there are polynomials
\lambda
such that
V_{\lambda}
is indecomposable and their simple subfactors are pairwise non-isomorphic. For example
x_{1}x_{2}
is a product of two non-similar irreducible factors and
\Lambda/\Lambda x_{1}x_{2}
is indecomposable. Substituting
x_{1}+1,x_{2}+1
for
x_{1}.x_{2}
, respectively, one gets
\lambda=(1+x_{1})(1+x_{2})=1+x_{1}(1+x_{2})+x_{2}
, a polynomial with constant. By definition
V_{\lambda}
is a
K
-space generated by
1,1+x_{2}
. It is now obvious by immediate calculation that
V_{\lambda}
is indecomposable of length 2 with endomorphism ring isomorphic to
K
.

Finally, one can use the distributivity of the submodule lattice of
V_{\lambda}
to show that not every finite-codimensional left ideal of
\Lambda^{*}
can be represented as
L_{\lambda}=\mathbb{L}\lambda\cap\Lambda^{*}
for some
\lambda\in\Lambda
, as we already saw in Subsection
5.1
. Namely, let
L_{1},L_{2}
be different finite-codimensional maximal left ideals of
\Lambda^{*}
such that their associated primitive ideal is not
I^{*}
and such that the factors
\Lambda^{*}/L_{i}\,(i=1,2)
are isomorphic. There are a great number of choices for such maximal left ideals, for example by
L_{\lambda}
where
\lambda
runs over similar irreducible polynomials with constant in
\Lambda
. Put
L=L_{1}\cap L_{2}
. Then
\Lambda^{*}/L=N
and hence
\mathfrak{N}=\mathbb{L}\otimes_{\Lambda^{*}}N
is a direct sum of two isomorphic simple modules. Therefore their submodule lattice is not distributive whence
L
can not be represented as
L_{\lambda}
for some
\lambda\in\Lambda
with constant.

In fact, there are even maximal two-sided ideals of
\Lambda^{*}
with division factor algebras which cannot be represented as
V_{\lambda}
for some comonic polynomial
\lambda\in\Lambda
. This claim is obvious for infinite-dimensional division factor algebras of
\Lambda^{*}
because all
V_{\lambda}
are finite-dimensional. Furthermore, if
K
is the field
\mathbb{R}
of the reals and
\mathbb{H}
is the field of the real quaternions given by the two-sided ideal
\langle{x_{1}^{*}}^{2}+1,\ {x_{2}^{*}}^{2}+1,\ x^{*}_{1}x^{*}_{2}+x^{*}_{2}x^{*}_{1}\rangle
, then
\mathbb{H}
cannot be represented as
V_{\lambda}
for any appropriate comonic polynomial
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}\langle x_{1},x_{2}\rangle
.

6.2.
Free algebra of arbitrary rank

It is clear that any element
\lambda
of a free algebra contains only finitely many variables. Hence its study does not essentially depend on the other variables. Therefore from now on it is more
natural to assume that
\lambda
is a polynomial in a free algebra
K\langle X\rangle
on an arbitrary possibly infinite variable set
X
. In this case,
V_{\lambda}
remains a finite-dimensional
\Lambda
-module but the equality
\mathbb{L}(X)\lambda=\mathbb{L}(X)(\Lambda^{*}\cap\mathbb{L}\lambda)
does not hold. Here
\mathbb{L}(X)
denote the Leavitt algebra by adding only a right inverse of the row matrix
X
, that is, it is generated by

\{x,x^{*}\,|\,x\in X\}
subject to

y^{*}x=\begin{cases}1&\text{if}\qquad x=y,\\
0&\text{if}\qquad x\neq y\end{cases}

for any two
x,y\in X
when
X
is infinite. For example, for infinite
X
the right ideal
\mathbb{L}(X)(1-x)\cap\Lambda^{*}=\Lambda^{*}(1-x^{*})+\sum\limits_{y\neq x}\Lambda^{*}y^{*}=L
is still maximal but
\mathbb{L}(X)(1-x)\neq\mathbb{L}L
. However,
\mathbb{L}(X)\lambda
is still a left ideal of colength
l
if
\lambda=\pi_{1}\cdots\pi_{l}
is a factorization of
\lambda
into
l
irreducible factors. To verify this result we need first an infinite version of Theorem
4.9
.

Theorem 6.1
.

Let
\Lambda=K\langle X\rangle
be the free algebra on an infinite set
X
and
\mathbb{L}=\mathbb{L}(X)
the corresponding Leavitt algebra. Then for any two polynomials
\delta,\gamma\in\Lambda
there is a comonic polynomial
\lambda\in\Lambda
with
\mathbb{L}\delta+\mathbb{L}\gamma=\mathbb{L}\lambda
. In particular, any submodule of the
\mathbb{L}
-module
{}_{\lambda}M=\mathbb{L}/\mathbb{L}\lambda\cong\Lambda/\Lambda\lambda
is cyclic and can be generated by some comonic
\gamma\in\Lambda
.

Proof.

From the definition one sees immediately that Lemma
3.10
remains true whence by a trivial induction one can assume without loss of generality that both
\delta,\lambda
are comonic. Moreover, one can define a
\star_{\lambda}
-action of
\Lambda
on itself for any comonic polynomial
\lambda\in\Lambda
in the same way as it was defined before Theorem
2.3
whence
\Lambda/\Lambda\lambda
becomes naturally a left
\mathbb{L}
-module isomorphic to
M_{\lambda}=\mathbb{L}/\mathbb{L}\lambda
. Therefore one has

x\star_{\lambda}\alpha=-k_{\alpha}k^{-1}_{\lambda}\lambda_{x}+\alpha_{x}
if
\lambda=k_{\lambda}+\sum\limits_{x\in X}x\lambda_{x},\,\,\alpha=k_{\alpha}+\sum\limits_{x\in X}x\alpha_{x},\,(0\neq k_{\lambda},k_{\alpha}\in K)
where
\beta_{x}
denotes the cofactor of
\beta\in\Lambda
with respect to
x\in X
. If we denote by
X_{\alpha}
the subset of variables appearing in
\alpha
, then
X_{\alpha}
is finite and
\sum\limits_{x\in X_{\alpha}}x^{*}
acts as the identity on
\alpha
mod
\mathbb{L}\lambda
, that is,

\sum\limits_{x\in X_{\alpha}}x(x\star_{\lambda}\alpha)\equiv\alpha\quad\mod\Lambda\lambda.

Consequently, if
b
runs over the finite set of all monomials in
x\in X_{\alpha}
of the degree
\alpha
, then at least one of these
b\star_{\lambda}\alpha
is a non-zero element of
V_{\lambda}
, hence of degree
\leq|\lambda|-1
. These observations show that almost all results of Sections
3
and
4
together with their proof remain true if they are formulated by using
\mathbb{L}\lambda,V_{\lambda}
and
M_{\lambda}(\cong\Lambda/\Lambda\lambda)
and not involving
L_{\lambda}=\Lambda^{*}\cap\mathbb{L}\lambda,\,N_{\lambda}=\Lambda^{*}/L_{\lambda}
. Thus we have

Corollary 6.2
.

Let
\lambda\in\Lambda=K\langle X\rangle\,(|X|=\infty,\,\,|\lambda|\geq 1)
comonic. Then
V_{\lambda}
is a finite-dimensional

\Lambda
-module with respect to the
\star_{\lambda}
-action which embeds essentially in
\Lambda/\Lambda\lambda\cong M_{\lambda}=\mathbb{L}/\mathbb{L}\lambda
. Both
V_{\lambda}
and the
\mathbb{L}
-module
M_{\lambda}
are simple with endomorphism ring isomorphic to
K
if
\lambda
is linear. For any
\alpha\in\Lambda
not contained in
\Lambda\lambda
there is a monomial
b
in variables of
\alpha
of degree
|\alpha|
with
0\neq b\star_{\lambda}\alpha\in V_{\lambda}
.

In view of Corollary
6.2
the proof of Theorem
4.9
works also in this case, and so Theorem
6.1
is verified.
∎

In view of Corollary
6.2
and Theorem
6.1
the same argument in the last part of Section
4
implies the more general form of Theorem
2.3
as follows.

Theorem 6.3
.

Let
\gamma,\lambda\in\Lambda=K\langle X\rangle\,
be comonic polynomials of positive degree where
X
has an arbitrary rank, together with finite-dimensional left
\Lambda
-modules
V_{\gamma},V_{\lambda}
with respect to the
\star
-action defined by
\gamma,\lambda
, respectively. Let
\mathbb{L}(X)
be the corresponding Leavitt algebra defined by

\Lambda
.

(1)

\lambda
is an irreducible polynomial if and only if
M_{\lambda}=\mathbb{L}(X)/\mathbb{L}(X)\lambda
and
V_{\lambda}
are simple modules over
\mathbb{L}(X)
and
\Lambda
, respectively.

(2)

If
\lambda=\pi_{1}\cdots\pi_{m}
is a factorization of
\lambda
into a product of irreducible polynomials, then
m
is the length of both
M_{\lambda}
and
{{}_{\Lambda}}V_{\lambda}
whence
m
is an invariant of
\lambda
.

(3)

V_{\lambda}\cong V_{\gamma}\Longleftrightarrow\Lambda/\Lambda\gamma\cong\Lambda/\Lambda\lambda\Longleftrightarrow M_{\gamma}\cong M_{\lambda}
, that is,
\gamma
and
\lambda
are similar over
\Lambda
iff they are such over
\mathbb{L}(X)
.
