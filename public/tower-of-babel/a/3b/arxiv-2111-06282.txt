Compositions and decompositions of binary relations
Ivan Chajda, Helmut Länger

Abstract
It is well-known that to every binary relation on a non-void set I I there can be assigned its incidence matrix, also in the case when I I is infinite. We show that a certain kind of “multiplication” of such incidence matrices corresponds to the composition of the corresponding relations. Using this fact we investigate the solvability of the equation R ∘ X = S R\circ X=S for given binary relations R R and S S on I I and derive an algorithm for solving this equation by using the connections between the corresponding incidence matrices. Moreover, we describe how one can obtain the incidence matrix of a product of binary relations from the incidence matrices of its factors.

Proof.

\displaystyle c_{kl}

\displaystyle=1,

\displaystyle(k,l)

\displaystyle\in R\circ S,

\displaystyle\text{there exists some }m\in I\text{ with }

\displaystyle(k,m)\in R\text{ and }(m,l)\in S,

\displaystyle\text{there exists some }m\in I\text{ with }

\displaystyle a_{km}=b_{ml}=1,

\displaystyle\max_{m\in I}a_{km}b_{ml}

\displaystyle=1,

\displaystyle d_{kl}

\displaystyle=1.

(ii)

Next we show how the incidence matrix of the Cartesian product of binary relations over different base sets can be derived from the incidence matrices of the corresponding factors. For this we introduce the following kind of product of relations over different base sets.

Let
(I_{k})_{k\in K}
be a family of sets, put
I:=\prod\limits_{k\in K}I_{k}
and for all
k\in K

R_{k}\subseteq I_{k}\times I_{k}

\prod_{k\in K}R_{k}:=\{\big((i_{k})_{k\in K},(j_{k})_{k\in K}\big)\in I\times I\mid(i_{k},j_{k})\in R_{k}\text{ for all }k\in K\}.

Theorem 5
.

Let
(I_{k})_{k\in K}
be a family of sets, and for all
k\in K
let
R_{k}\subseteq I_{k}\times I_{k}
and
M_{R_{k}}=[a_{i_{k}j_{k}}]
. Put
I:=\prod\limits_{k\in K}I_{k}
and
R:=\prod\limits_{k\in K}R_{k}
. Then
R\subseteq I\times I
. Let
M_{R}=[a_{ij}]
. Then

a_{(i_{k})_{k\in K}(j_{k})_{k\in K}}=\min_{k\in K}a_{i_{k}j_{k}}

for all
(i_{k})_{k\in K},(j_{k})_{k\in K}\in I
.

Proof.

Let
(l_{k})_{k\in K},(m_{k})_{k\in K}\in I
. Then the following are equivalent:

\displaystyle a_{(l_{k})_{k\in K}(m_{k})_{k\in K}}

\displaystyle=1,

\displaystyle\big((l_{k})_{k\in K},(m_{k})_{k\in K}\big)

\displaystyle\in R,

\displaystyle(l_{k},m_{k})

\displaystyle\in R_{k}\text{ for all }k\in K,

\displaystyle a_{l_{k}m_{k}}

\displaystyle=1\text{ for all }k\in K,

\displaystyle\min_{k\in K}a_{l_{k}m_{k}}

\displaystyle=1.

This shows

a_{(l_{k})_{k\in K}(m_{k})_{k\in K}}=\min_{k\in K}a_{l_{k}m_{k}}.

∎

Theorem 7

Let

,

(i)

has a solution if and only if

\text{for all }j,k,l\in I\text{ we have }b_{kj}=b_{lj}\text{ whenever }f(k)=f(l).

(ii)

Proof.

Let
X\subseteq I\times I
and
M_{X}=[x_{ij}]
.

(i)

Then the following are equivalent:

\displaystyle R\circ X

\displaystyle=S,

\displaystyle M_{R}\odot M_{X}

\displaystyle=M_{S},

\displaystyle\max_{k\in I}\delta_{k,f(i)}x_{kj}

\displaystyle=b_{ij}\text{ for all }i,j\in I,

\displaystyle x_{f(i),j}

\displaystyle=b_{ij}\text{ for all }i,j\in I.

(ii)

is bijective then the following are equivalent:

\displaystyle x_{f(i),j}

\displaystyle=b_{ij}\text{ for all }i,j\in I,

\displaystyle x_{ij}

\displaystyle=b_{f^{-1}(i),j}\text{ for all }i,j\in I.

We are going to show several cases in which the equation
R\circ X=S
is not solvable.

Lemma 9
.

Let
R,S\subseteq I\times I
,
M_{R}=[a_{ij}]
,
M_{S}=[b_{ij}]
and
k,l,m,n\in I
and assume that one of the following conditions holds:

(i)

a_{kj}=0
for all
j\in I
and there exists some
p\in I
with
b_{kp}\neq 0
,

(ii)

a_{kj}=a_{lj}
for all
j\in I
and there exists some
p\in I
with
b_{kp}\neq b_{lp}
,

(iii)

a_{kj}=\delta_{jl}
for all
j\in I
,
a_{nl}=b_{km}=1
and
b_{nm}=0
.

Then the equation
R\circ X=S
has no solution.

Proof.

Assume
X
to be a solution with
M_{X}=[x_{ij}]
. Then
M_{R}\odot M_{X}=M_{S}
. Now we have

(i)

b_{kp}=\max\limits_{r\in I}a_{kr}x_{rp}=0
, a contradiction.

(ii)

b_{kp}=\max\limits_{r\in I}a_{kr}x_{rp}=\max\limits_{r\in I}a_{lr}x_{rp}=b_{lp}
, a contradiction.

(iii)

x_{lm}=\max\limits_{j\in I}\delta_{jl}x_{jm}=\max\limits_{j\in I}a_{kj}x_{jm}=b_{km}=1
which implies

1=a_{nl}x_{lm}\leq\max_{j\in I}a_{nj}x_{jm}=b_{nm}=0,

a contradiction.

∎

In the following proposition we present a case where the equation

can be easily solved.

Proposition 10
.

Let
R,S\subseteq I\times I
with
R\subseteq S
and assume

Let

and

. If
(c,b)\in\Delta

(a,b)=(a,c)\in R\subseteq S
. If
(c,b)\in S\setminus R

(a,c)\in R\subseteq S
and

and hence
(a,b)\in S
according to the transitivity of

. This shows

\Rightarrow

f(k)\in I_{k}
with
\vec{a}_{f(k)}=\vec{e}_{k}
. Clearly,
f\colon I\rightarrow I
is injective and thus bijective and we obtain
\vec{a}_{j}=\vec{e}_{f^{-1}(j)}
for all
j\in I
, i.e.
a_{ij}=\delta_{i,f^{-1}(j)}=\delta_{j,f(i)}
for all
i,j\in I
.

(ii)
\Rightarrow
(i):

If
B=[b_{ij}]:=[\delta_{j,f^{-1}(i)}]
then

\displaystyle\max_{k\in I}a_{ik}b_{kj}

\displaystyle=\max_{k\in I}\delta_{k,f(i)}\delta_{j,f^{-1}(k)}=\delta_{f(i),f(j)}=\delta_{ij},

\displaystyle\max_{k\in I}b_{ik}a_{kj}

\displaystyle=\max_{k\in I}\delta_{k,f^{-1}(i)}\delta_{j,f(k)}=\delta_{f^{-1}(i),f^{-1}(j)}=\delta_{ij}

showing
A\odot B=B\odot A=E
.
∎

Note that condition (ii) means that every row and every column of
A
contains exactly one
1
and that the implication (ii)
\Rightarrow
(i) also holds for infinite
I
.

Remark 13
.

Let
R
and
S
be binary relations on a set
I
such that for the incidence matrix
M_{R}
of
R
there exists a bijection
f\colon I\rightarrow I
as described in Theorem
7
. Put
E:=[\delta_{ij}]\in\{0,1\}^{I\times I}
. It is easy to check that
A\odot E=E\odot A=A
for every
A\in\{0,1\}^{I\times I}
. From Proposition
12
we obtain
M_{R}^{-1}=[\delta_{j,f^{-1}(i)}]
. Now the following are equivalent:

\displaystyle R\circ X

\displaystyle=S,

\displaystyle M_{R}\odot M_{X}

\displaystyle=M_{S},

\displaystyle M_{X}

\displaystyle=M_{R}^{-1}\odot M_{S}

and hence
x_{ij}=\max\limits_{k\in I}\delta_{k,f^{-1}(i)}b_{kj}=b_{f^{-1}(i),j}
for all
i,j\in I
. Note that here we used associativity of
\odot
.

The next theorem characterizes solvability of the equation
R\circ X=S
and also characterizes the corresponding solutions. From this theorem we will derive an algorithm for computing all solutions.

Theorem 14
.

Let
R,S\subseteq I\times I
,
M_{R}=[a_{ij}]
and
M_{S}=[b_{ij}]
and put

\displaystyle A_{i}

\displaystyle:=\{j\in I\mid a_{ij}=1\},

\displaystyle B_{k}

\displaystyle:=\{i\in I\mid b_{ik}=0\},

\displaystyle C_{k}

\displaystyle:=\bigcup_{l\in B_{k}}A_{l}

for all
i,k\in I
.

(i)

The equation
R\circ X=S
is solvable if and only if
A_{i}\setminus C_{k}\neq\emptyset
for all
k\in I
and all
i\in I\setminus B_{k}
.

(ii)

X\subseteq I\times I
with
M_{X}=[x_{ij}]
is a solution of the equation
R\circ X=S
if and only if the following hold:

(a)

x_{jk}=0
for all
k\in I
and all
j\in C_{k}
,

(b)

for every
k\in I
and
i\in I\setminus B_{k}
there exists some
j\in A_{i}\setminus C_{k}
with
x_{jk}=1
.

Proof.

Let
X\subseteq I\times I
and
M_{X}=[x_{ij}]
. Then the following are equivalent:

\displaystyle R\circ X

\displaystyle=S,

\displaystyle\max_{j\in I}a_{ij}x_{jk}

\displaystyle=b_{ik}\text{ for all }i,k\in I,

\displaystyle\max_{j\in A_{i}}x_{jk}

\displaystyle=b_{ik}\text{ for all }i,k\in I.

For
k\in I
the following are equivalent:

\displaystyle\max_{j\in A_{i}}x_{jk}

\displaystyle=b_{ik}\text{ for all }i\in B_{k},

\displaystyle\max_{j\in A_{i}}x_{jk}

\displaystyle=0\text{ for all }i\in B_{k},

\displaystyle x_{jk}

\displaystyle=0\text{ for all }i\in B_{k}\text{ and all }j\in A_{i},

\displaystyle x_{jk}

\displaystyle=0\text{ for all }j\in\bigcup_{i\in B_{k}}A_{i},

\displaystyle x_{jk}

\displaystyle=0\text{ for all }j\in C_{k}.

For
k\in I
and
i\in I\setminus B_{k}
the following are equivalent:

\displaystyle\max_{j\in A_{i}}x_{jk}

\displaystyle=b_{ik},

\displaystyle\max_{j\in A_{i}}x_{jk}

\displaystyle=1,

\displaystyle\text{there exists some }j\in A_{i}\text{ with }x_{jk}

\displaystyle=1.

∎

As mentioned above we now derive an algorithm for computing all solutions of the equation
R\circ X=S
provided this equation is solvable and
I
is finite. This algorithm consists of the following three steps (let
A_{i}
,
B_{k}
and
C_{k}
be defined as in Theorem
14
):

(1)

Put
x_{jk}:=0
for all
k\in I
and all
j\in C_{k}
.

(2)

For all
k\in I
and
i\in I\setminus B_{k}
choose some
j\in A_{i}\setminus C_{k}
and put
x_{jk}:=1
.

(3)

Choose the remaining
x_{jk}\in\{0,1\}
arbitrarily.

This algorithm was already implicitly used in Example
4
, see Example
15
. In fact it is similar to the method for solving linear equations. In steps (1) and (2) the algorithm reduces the possibilities for choosing the elements of
M_{X}
whereas steps (2) and (3) determine the number of solutions.

The aforementioned algorithm will be demonstrated by the following example.

Example 15
.

Let us apply the algorithm to Example
4
. Hence we have

M_{R}=\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}0&1&0\\
1&0&0\\
0&1&1\end{array}\right),M_{S}=\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}1&1&0\\
0&0&0\\
1&1&0\end{array}\right).

We compute

\displaystyle A_{1}=\{2\},A_{2}=\{1\},A_{3}=\{2,3\},

\displaystyle B_{1}=\{2\},B_{2}=\{2\},B_{3}=\{1,2,3\},

\displaystyle C_{1}=\{1\},C_{2}=\{1\},C_{3}=\{1,2,3\},

\displaystyle I\setminus B_{1}=\{1,3\},I\setminus B_{2}=\{1,3\},I\setminus B_{3}=\emptyset,

\displaystyle A_{1}\setminus C_{1}=\{2\}\neq\emptyset,A_{3}\setminus C_{1}=\{2,3\}\neq\emptyset,A_{1}\setminus C_{2}=\{2\}\neq\emptyset,A_{3}\setminus C_{2}=\{2,3\}\neq\emptyset.

Hence the equation
R\circ X=S
is solvable and we obtain

\displaystyle x_{11}=x_{12}=x_{13}=x_{23}=x_{33}=0,

\displaystyle 1\in\{x_{21}\}\cap\{x_{21},x_{31}\}\cap\{x_{22}\}\cap\{x_{22},x_{32}\},

i.e.
x_{21}=x_{22}=1
and
x_{31},x_{32}\in\{0,1\}
. Thus we got all four solutions derived in Example
4
.

There arises the question what can be said concerning the equation
X\circ R=S
.

Remark 16
.

Since the equation
X\circ R=S
is dual to the equation
R\circ X=S
, the investigation of the first equation does not bring new insights in the problem.
