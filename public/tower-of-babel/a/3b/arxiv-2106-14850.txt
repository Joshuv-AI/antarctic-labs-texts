Theoretical and computational analysis of the thermal quasi-geostrophic model
D Crisan, DD Holm, E Luesink, PR Mensah, W Pan

Abstract
. This work involves theoretical and numerical analysis of the Thermal Quasi-Geostrophic (TQG) model of submesoscale geophysical fluid dynamics (GFD). Physically, the TQG model involves thermal geostrophic balance, in which the Rossby number, the Froude number and the stratification parameter are all of the same asymptotic order. The main analytical contribution of this paper is to construct local-in-time unique strong solutions for the TQG model. For this, we show that solutions of its regularized version α \alpha -TQG converge to solutions of TQG as its smoothing parameter α → 0 \alpha\rightarrow 0 and we obtain blowup criteria for the α \alpha -TQG model. The main contribution of the computational analysis is to verify the rate of convergence of α \alpha -TQG solutions to TQG solutions as α → 0 \alpha\rightarrow 0 for example simulations in appropriate GFD regimes.



1.
Introduction

1.1.
Purpose

The thermal quasi-geostrophic (TQG) equations comprise a mesoscale model of ocean dynamics in a solution regime near thermal geostrophic balance. In thermal geostrophic balance, three forces – the Coriolis, hydrostatic pressure gradient and buoyancy-gradient forces – sum to zero. Numerical simulations of the TQG equations show the onset of instability at high wavenumbers which creates small coherent structures which resemble submesoscale (1–20 km) features observed in satellite ocean color images as seen in figure

The submesoscale features persist and interact strongly with each other in Kelvin-Helmholtz roll-up dynamics, instead of simply cascading energy to higher wavenumbers.
This observation means that the instabilities which create these submesoscale features quickly regain stability without cascading them to ever smaller scales.
The present work aims to understand these features of the TQG solution dynamics, both analytically and numerically.

The contributions of this paper.

The main analytical contribution of this paper is the construction of a unique local strong solution of the TQG model. In particular, we show that there is a unique maximal solution of the TQG equation defined on a (possibly infinite) time interval
[0,T_{\mathrm{max}})
. The solution will be shown to exist in a suitable Sobolev space. More precisely, provided that the initial data resides in a chosen Sobolev space, the solution at time
t>0
will remain in this space as long as
t<T_{\mathrm{max}}
. Should
t<T_{\mathrm{max}}
be finite, then the solution will blow up in the chosen Sobolev norm.

As a second analytical contribution, we show that the TQG model depends continuously on the initial condition. This dependence only holds in a slightly weaker norm than the one corresponding to the space where the solution resides.
This is a useful property from a numerical perspective. It implies that initial small errors when simulating the TQG model will stay small at any subsequent time.

The third analytical contribution is to construct a regularized version of the TQG model, termed the
\alpha
-TQG model. This model is constructed in a similar manner as the
\alpha

[
11

12

23

The
\alpha

In addition, we show that the
\alpha

\alpha\rightarrow 0
in a norm that depends on two physical parameters, the vorticity and the gradient of buoyancy.
We also identify the rate of convergence as a function of the
\alpha

\alpha

\alpha>0

The blow-up phenomenon is important, particularly if it is observed in numerical simulations. Therefore, blow-up criteria (in other words, criteria required for the solution to blow up) are important. In this paper we state three characterizations of the blow-up time. The fourth analytical contribution of this paper is to show that blow-up occurs in the

The contribution of this paper from a numerical perspective is to describe our spatial and temporal discretisation methods for approximating TQG and

1.2.
Brief history of the TQG model

The history of the TQG model goes back about half a century, perhaps first elucidated by O’Brien and Reid
[
26
]
as sketched, in
[
6
]
. Briefly put, the TQG model generalises the classical QG equations by introducing horizontal gradients of buoyancy which alter the geostrophic balance to include the inhomogeneous thermal effects which influence buoyancy. Indeed, O’Brien and Reid
[
26
]
write that their work was inspired by observations that the passage of hurricanes could draw enough heat from the ocean to significantly lower the sea-surface temperature in the Gulf of Mexico. Since O’Brien and Reid
[
26
]
introduced their two-layer model, further developments of it have been applied to a variety of ocean processes, particularly to equatorial dynamics. For more details of the theoretical model developments, see Ripa
[
27
,
29
,
30
]
and for developments of applications in oceanography see
[
1
,
3
,
24
,
31
]
, as well as other citations in
[
6
]
. In particular, Ripa refers to the TQ models as inhomogeneous-layer (IL) models and his papers explain rational derivations of theories with increasing vertical structure IL1, IL2, etc. The TQG model analysed here and derived systematically from asymptotic expansions in small dimensionless parameters of the Hamilton’s principle for the rotating, stratified Euler equations in

is equivalent to Ripa’s model IL0QG

1.3.
A sketch of the derivation of the TQG model

We have explained that certain thermal effects in the mesoscale ocean have historically been modelled by the thermal quasi-geostrophic (TQG) equations. TQG is characterised by several dimensionless numbers arising from the dimensional parameters of planetary rotation, gravity and buoyancy. These are the familiar Rossby number, Froude number and stratification parameter. The Rossby number is the ratio of a typical horizontal velocity divided by the product of the rotation frequency and a typical horizontal length scale. The Froude number is the ratio of a typical horizontal velocity divided by the velocity of the fastest propagating gravity wave, which in turn is given by the square root of the gravity times the typical vertical length scale. The final dimensionless number is the stratification parameter, which specifies the typical size of the buoyancy stratification.

The regime in which the thermal quasi-geostrophic equations are derived is characterised by the thermal geostrophic balance. This balance arises because the Rossby number, the Froude number and the stratification parameter all have a similar amplitude. Preserving this three-fold balance requires simultaneously adapting the Froude number and the stratification parameter to match any change in Rossby number, for example, so that the dimensionless parameters will still have the same size. We consider the non-dissipative case, because of the large scales of mesoscale ocean dynamics. As mentioned earlier, the mesoscale dynamics has high wavenumber instabilities, which in principle can generate submesoscale effects. At smaller scales, viscous dissipation and thermal diffusivity will come into play as well. However, in what follows, dissipative effects will be neglected. The derivation of the TQG model involves a series of coordinated approximations to the rotating, stratified Euler model, as is illustrated in the diagram below.

starts with the Lagrangian for the rotating, stratified Euler equations at the top of the figure. After identifying the small dimensionless parameters in the Euler Lagrangian, the Lagrangians for the successive approximate models can be derived by inserting asymptotic expansions into the Euler Lagrangian. In the ocean, the buoyancy stratification is typically small. Hence, it makes sense to apply the Boussinesq approximation in the Lagrangian for the rotating, stratified Euler equations. This approximation yields the Lagrangian for the Euler–Boussinesq equations. At this point, one has a choice of two routes. The first route begins by making the hydrostatic approximation, which leads to the Lagrangian for the primitive equations. Then, upon vertically integrating the Lagrangian for the primitive equations, one finds the Lagrangian for the thermal rotating shallow water equations. The alternative route first vertically integrates the Lagrangian for the Euler–Boussinesq equations to obtain the Lagrangian for the thermal and rotating version of the Green–Naghdi equations. By subsequently making the hydrostatic approximation in the Lagrangian for the thermal rotating Green–Naghdi equations, the alternative route arrives at the Lagrangian for the thermal rotating shallow water equations.

The Lagrangian for the thermal rotating shallow water (TRSW) equations is the starting point in
[
17
]
for the derivation of the thermal quasi-geostrophic (TQG) equations. The typical values of the dimensionless numbers for mesoscale ocean problems lead to thermal geostrophic balance. This balance implies an algebraic expression for the balanced velocity field in terms of the horizontal gradients of the free surface elevation and the buoyancy. Upon expanding the TRSW Lagrangian around this balance and truncating, one obtains the Lagrangian for the thermal Lagrangian 1 (L1) model. The Lagrangian for the thermal L1 model is not hyperregular, though. Hence, the Legendre transformation is for thermal L1 is not invertible. Hence, no Hamiltonian description of this model is available via the Legendre transformation. Nonetheless, an application of the Euler-Poincaré theorem
[
18
]
to the thermal L1 Lagrangian yields the corresponding equations of motion. By identifying the leading order terms in the thermal L1 equations and truncating the asymptotic expansion, one finally obtains the TQG equations. However, the truncations of the asymptotic expansions in the thermal L1 equations also prevent the resulting TQG equations from possessing a Hamilton’s principle derivation. This feature is unlike the other models in figure
2
, which all arise from approximated Lagrangians in their corresponding action integrals for Hamilton’s principle. However, as it turns out, the thermal quasi-geostrophic equations do possess a Hamiltonian formulation in terms of a non-standard Lie-Poisson bracket. The Lie-Poisson bracket for the quasi-geostrophic equations can be obtained via a linear change of variables from the usual semi-direct product Lie-Poisson bracket for fluids. The resulting Hamiltonian formulation for the thermal quasi-geostrophic equations is important for the future application of stochastic advection by Lie transport (SALT), introduced in
[
15
]
, which requires either a Lagrangian, or a Hamiltonian interpretation of the equations of motion.

By applying the method of Stochastic Advection by Lie Transport (SALT) to the Hamiltonian formulation of the TQG equations, one obtains a stochastic version of them which preserves the infinite family of integral conserved quantities. More details on the stochastic version can be found in the conclusion section as well as in
[
17
]
. A discussion of the remaining models in Figure
2
can be found in
[
16
]
.

1.4.
TQG versus Rayleigh-Bénard convection with viscosity and thermal diffusivity

The TQG equations in (
1.6
)-(
1.8
) can be compared to the equations for Rayleigh-Bénard convection in a vertical plane, as remarked in
[
17
]
. Recent results of
[
9
]
show that bounds exist for the enstrophy and temperature gradient of solutions lying in the attractor for the planar Rayleigh-Bénard convection problem which are algebraic in the viscosity and thermal diffusivity. This result is a significant improvement over previously established estimates. This result also provides a motivation to study the TQG equations with viscous dissipation and thermal diffusivity. The equations for non-dissipative Rayleigh-Bénard convection in the vertical plane are given by

(1.10)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\omega+J(\psi,\omega)

\displaystyle=\alpha gT_{z},

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}T+J(\psi,T)

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle\omega

\displaystyle=\Delta\psi,

where
\omega
denotes the vorticity,
\psi
is the stream function,
T
is the temperature,
g
is gravity and
\alpha
is the thermal expansion coefficient. The Rayleigh-Bénard equations (
1.10
) have a long and illustrious history in mathematical analysis. The resemblance of the Rayleigh-Bénard equations to the TQG equations suggests that perhaps the TQG equations will also provide a fruitful challenge to mathematical analysis.

In TQG, the buoyancy plays the role of the temperature in the Rayleigh-Bénard equations. By rearranging the TQG equations (
1.8
) such that the buoyancy terms all appear on the right hand side, one has

(1.11)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\omega+J(\psi,\omega)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}J(\psi,b)+\frac{1}{2}J(\zeta-h,b),

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}b+J(\psi,b)

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle\omega

\displaystyle=\Delta\psi-\psi+f.

There are similarities and also several differences between the Rayleigh-Bénard equations (
1.10
) and the TQG equations (
1.11
). For example, the equation that relates the vorticity to the stream function is a Helmholtz equation for TQG and a Poisson equation for Rayleigh-Bénard. In addition, in Rayleigh-Bénard convection the forcing term on the right hand side of the vorticity equation depends only on the derivative of the temperature in the vertical direction. In TQG the forcing terms depends on the derivatives of the buoyancy in both directions. Thus, TQG arises as an interesting and challenging extension of the celebrated Rayleigh-Bénard problem.

Plan for the rest of the paper

We now give the plan for the rest of the paper.
We collect preliminary tools in Section
2

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}=W^{3,2}(\mathbb{T}^{2})\times W^{2,2}(\mathbb{T}^{2})

\displaystyle\|(b,\omega)\|_{\mathcal{M}}:=\|b\|_{3,2}+\|\omega\|_{2,2}.

2.3.
Main results

Our current goal is to construct a solution for the system of equations (
1.1
)–(
1.5
). To do this, we first make the following assumption on our set of data.

Assumption 2.4
.

Let
\mathbf{u}_{h}\in W^{3,2}_{\mathrm{div}}(\mathbb{T}^{2};\mathbb{R}^{2})
and
f\in W^{2,2}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
and assume that
(b_{0},\omega_{0})\in\mathcal{M}
.

Unless otherwise stated, Assumption
2.4
now holds throughout the rest of the paper.
We are now in a position to make precise, exactly what we mean by a solution.

Definition 2.5

(Local strong solution)
.

Let
T>0
be a constant. We call the triple
(b,\omega,T)

a
local strong solution
or simply, a
local solution
or a
solution
to the system (
1.1
)–(
1.5
) if the following holds.

•

The buoyancy
b
satisfies
b\in C([0,T];W^{3,2}(\mathbb{T}^{2}))

and the equation

\displaystyle b(t)

\displaystyle=b_{0}-\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{div}(b\mathbf{u})\,\,\mathrm{d}\tau,

holds for all
t\in[0,T]
;

•

the potential vorticity
\omega
satisfies
\omega\in C([0,T];W^{2,2}(\mathbb{T}^{2}))

and the equation

\displaystyle\omega(t)

\displaystyle=\omega_{0}-\int_{0}^{t}\Big[\mathrm{div}((\omega-b)\mathbf{u})+\mathrm{div}(b\mathbf{u}_{h})\Big]\,\,\mathrm{d}\tau

holds for all
t\in[0,T]
.

Remark 2.6
.

We remark that the regularity of the solution
(b,\omega,T)
and its data together with the integral equations immediately imply that
b
and
\omega
are differentiable in time.
Indeed, by using the fact that
W^{3,2}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
and
W^{2,2}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
are Banach algebras, we immediately deduce from the integral equations for
b
and
\omega
above that

\displaystyle b\in C^{1}([0,T];W^{2,2}(\mathbb{T}^{2})),\qquad\omega\in C^{1}([0,T];W^{1,2}(\mathbb{T}^{2})).

It also follow from the integral equations for the buoyancy and potential vorticity above that the initial conditions are
b(0,x)=b_{0}(x)
and
\omega(0,x)=\omega_{0}(x)
. The corresponding differential forms (
1.1
)–(
1.2
) are clearly immediate from the integral representations.

Remark 2.7
.

Since we are working on the torus, and the velocity fields are defined by (
1.4
)-(
1.5
), we have in
particular,
\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}\mathbf{u}_{h}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}=0
and
\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}\mathbf{u}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}=0
. From the latter, we get that
q
and
f
have zero averages. Consequently, we will assume that all
functions under consideration have zero averages.

Definition 2.8

(Maximal solution)
.

We call
(b,\omega,T_{\max})

a
maximal solution
to the system (
1.1
)–(
1.5
) if:

•

there exists an increasing sequence of time steps
(T_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
whose limit is
T_{\max}\in(0,\infty]
;

•

for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
, the triple
(b,\omega,T_{n})
is
a local strong solution to the system (
1.1
)–(
1.5
) with initial condition
(b_{0},\omega_{0})
;

•

if
T_{\max}<\infty
, then

(2.9)

\displaystyle\limsup_{T_{n}\rightarrow T_{\max}}\|(b,\omega)(T_{n})\|_{\mathcal{M}}^{2}=\infty.

We shall call
T_{\max}>0
the
maximal time
.

Remark 2.9
.

Condition (
2.9
) means that the solution breaks down at the limit point
T_{\max}
.

We are now in a position to state our first main result.

Theorem 2.10

(Existence of local solutions)
.

There exists a solution
(b,\omega,T)
of (
1.1
)–(
1.5
) under Assumption
2.4
.

Once we have constructed a local solution, we can show that this solution is continuously dependent on its initial state in a more general class of function space. This choice of class appears to be the strongest space in which the analysis may be performed. More details will follow in the sequel but first, we give the statement on the continuity property of strong solutions with respect to its data.

Theorem 2.11

(Continuity at low regularity)
.

Let
\mathbf{u}_{h}\in W^{3,2}_{\mathrm{div}}(\mathbb{T}^{2};\mathbb{R}^{2})
and
f\in W^{2,2}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
. Assume that
(b^{1},\omega^{1},T^{1})
and
(b^{2},\omega^{2},T^{2})
are solutions of (
1.1
)–(
1.5
) with initial conditions
(b_{0}^{1},\omega_{0}^{1})\in\mathcal{M}
and
(b_{0}^{2},\omega_{0}^{2})\in\mathcal{M}
respectively. Then there exists a constant

\displaystyle c=c\big(\|b_{0}^{1}\|_{3,2},\|\omega_{0}^{1}\|_{2,2},\|b_{0}^{2}\|_{3,2},\|\omega_{0}^{2}\|_{2,2},\|\mathbf{u}_{h}\|_{3,2},\|f\|_{2,2}\big)

such that

(2.10)

\displaystyle\|(b^{1}-b^{2})(t)\|_{2,2}^{2}+\|(\omega^{1}-\omega^{2})(t)\|_{1,2}^{2}\leq\exp(cT)\big(\|b_{0}^{1}-b_{0}^{2}\|_{2,2}^{2}+\|\omega_{0}^{1}-\omega_{0}^{2}\|_{1,2}^{2}\big)

holds for all
t\in[0,T]
where
T=\min\{T^{1},T^{2}\}
.

Remark 2.12
.

For the avoidance of doubt, we make clear that the final estimate for the differences in Theorem
2.11
above is stated in terms of a larger space
W^{2,2}(\mathbb{T}^{2})\times W^{1,2}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
with smaller norms than the space of existence
W^{3,2}(\mathbb{T}^{2})\times W^{2,2}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
. In order to obtain this bound however, in particular, we require the initial conditions of one of the solution to be bounded in the stronger space of existence, i.e, boundedness of
\|b_{0}^{1}\|_{3,2},\|\omega_{0}^{1}\|_{2,2}
rather than in the weaker space for which we obtain our final stability estimate. Furthermore, we also require the potential vorticity
\omega^{2}
(at all time of existence) of the second solution to be also bounded in the stronger space of existence
W^{2,2}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
. Explicitly, the only flexibility we have has to do with the second buoyancy
b^{2}
for which it appears that we are able to relax to live in the weaker space
W^{2,2}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
. Unfortunately however, because of the highly coupled nature of the system of equations under study, needing
\omega^{2}\in W^{2,2}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
for all times automatically requires that
b^{2}\in W^{3,2}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
for all times. Subsequently, this means that we require boundedness of the initial condition of the second solution in the stronger space of existence, i.e, boundedness of
\|b_{0}^{2}\|_{3,2},\|\omega_{0}^{2}\|_{2,2}
in addition to that of the first solution.
The requirement of needing both pair of initial conditions to live in a stronger space is in contrast to simpler looking models like the Euler equation where it suffices to require just having one initial condition to have stronger regularity. Unfortunately, we can not do better by our method of proof (which is to derive estimates for equations solved by the differences, i.e., the energy method) but we do not claim that other methods for deriving analogous estimates may not yield better result either.

As a consequence of Theorem
2.11
, the following statement about uniqueness of the strong solution is immediate.

(Uniqueness)
.

(b^{1},\omega^{1},T^{1})
and
(b^{2},\omega^{2},T^{2})
be two solutions of (

)–(
1.5
) under Assumption
2.4
.
Then
the difference

satisfies the equation

(2.11)

\displaystyle\|(b^{1}-b^{2})(t)\|_{2,2}^{2}+\|(\omega^{1}-\omega^{2})(t)\|_{1,2}^{2}=0

for all
t\in[0,T]
where
T=\min\{T^{1},T^{2}\}
.

Finally, we can show that a

of (

3.
Construction of strong solutions

In this section, we give a construction of a solution, in the sense of Definition
2.5
, to the thermal quasi-geostrophic system of equations (
1.1
)–(
1.5
). We will achieve this goal by rewriting our set of equations (
1.1
)–(
1.5
) in an abstract form and show that the resulting operator acting on the pair
(b,\omega)
satisfies the assumptions of Theorem
8.1
in the appendix. The details are as follows.
We consider

(3.1)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\binom{b}{\omega}+\mathcal{A}\begin{pmatrix}b\\
\omega\end{pmatrix}=0,\qquad t\geq 0,\qquad b\big|_{t=0}=b_{0},\qquad\omega\big|_{t=0}=\omega_{0}

where the operator

(3.2)

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}:=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla&0\\[3.00003pt]
(\mathbf{u}_{h}-\mathbf{u})\cdot\nabla&\mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla\end{bmatrix}

is defined such that

(3.3)

\displaystyle\mathbf{u}=\nabla^{\perp}\psi,\qquad\mathbf{u}_{h}=\frac{1}{2}\nabla^{\perp}h,\qquad\omega=(\Delta-1)\psi+f.

3.1.
Estimates for convective terms

In the following, we let
k\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
and recall that
W^{k,p}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
is a Banach algebra when
kp>2
. By using Sobolev embeddings and (
2.7
), we obtain the following estimates.

(1b)

Let
k\in\{0,1,2\}
. If
b\in W^{k+1,2}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
,
\omega\in W^{2,2}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
and
\mathbf{u}\in W^{3,2}_{\mathrm{div}}(\mathbb{T}^{2};\mathbb{R}^{2})
solves (
1.5
) for a given
f\in W^{2,2}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
and a given
\mathbf{u}_{h}\in W^{3,2}_{\mathrm{div}}(\mathbb{T}^{2};\mathbb{R}^{2})
, then

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla b\|_{k,2}\lesssim\|\mathbf{u}\|_{2,2}\|b\|_{k+1,2}\lesssim\big(1+\|\omega\|_{1,2}\big)\|b\|_{k+1,2}

and

\displaystyle\Big|\big\langle\mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla b\,,\,b\big\rangle_{k,2}\Big|\lesssim\|\mathbf{u}\|_{3,2}\|b\|_{k,2}^{2}\lesssim\big(1+\|\omega\|_{2,2}\big)\|b\|_{k,2}^{2},

\displaystyle\Big|\big\langle\mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla b\,,\,\omega\big\rangle_{k,2}\Big|\lesssim\|\mathbf{u}\|_{2,2}\|b\|_{k+1,2}\|\omega\|_{k,2}\lesssim\big(1+\|\omega\|_{1,2}\big)\|b\|_{k+1,2}\|\omega\|_{k,2},

\displaystyle\Big|\big\langle\mathbf{u}_{h}\cdot\nabla b\,,\,\omega\big\rangle_{k,2}\Big|\lesssim\|\mathbf{u}_{h}\|_{2,2}\|b\|_{k+1,2}\|\omega\|_{k,2}\lesssim\|b\|_{k+1,2}\|\omega\|_{k,2}.

(1b)

Let
k\geq 3
. If
b\in W^{k+1,2}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
,
\omega\in W^{k,2}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
and
\mathbf{u}\in W^{k+1,2}_{\mathrm{div}}(\mathbb{T}^{2};\mathbb{R}^{2})
solves (
1.5
) for a given
f\in W^{k,2}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
and a given
\mathbf{u}_{h}\in W^{k+1,2}_{\mathrm{div}}(\mathbb{T}^{2};\mathbb{R}^{2})
, then

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla b\|_{k,2}\lesssim\|\mathbf{u}\|_{2,2}\|b\|_{k+1,2}+\|\mathbf{u}\|_{k,2}\|b\|_{3,2}\lesssim\big(1+\|\omega\|_{k-1,2}\big)\|b\|_{k+1,2}

and

\displaystyle\Big|\big\langle\mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla b\,,\,b\big\rangle_{k,2}\Big|\lesssim\|\mathbf{u}\|_{3,2}\|b\|_{k,2}^{2}+\|\mathbf{u}\|_{k,2}\|b\|_{k,2}\|b\|_{3,2}\lesssim\big(1+\|\omega\|_{k-1,2}\big)\|b\|_{k,2}^{2},

\displaystyle\Big|\big\langle\mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla b\,,\,\omega\big\rangle_{k,2}\Big|\lesssim\big(\|\mathbf{u}\|_{2,2}\|b\|_{k+1,2}+\|\mathbf{u}\|_{k,2}\|b\|_{3,2}\big)\|\omega\|_{k,2}\lesssim\big(1+\|\omega\|_{k-1,2}\big)\|b\|_{k+1,2}\|\omega\|_{k,2},

\displaystyle\Big|\big\langle\mathbf{u}_{h}\cdot\nabla b\,,\,\omega\big\rangle_{k,2}\Big|\lesssim\big(\|\mathbf{u}_{h}\|_{2,2}\|b\|_{k+1,2}+\|\mathbf{u}_{h}\|_{k,2}\|b\|_{3,2}\big)\|\omega\|_{k,2}\lesssim\|b\|_{k+1,2}\|\omega\|_{k,2}.

(2a)

Let
k\in\{0,1,2\}
. If
\omega\in W^{k+1,2}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
and that
\mathbf{u}\in W^{3,2}_{\mathrm{div}}(\mathbb{T}^{2};\mathbb{R}^{2})
solves (
1.5
) for a given

, then

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla\omega\|_{k,2}\lesssim\|\mathbf{u}\|_{2,2}\|\omega\|_{k+1,2}\lesssim\big(1+\|\omega\|_{1,2}\big)\|\omega\|_{k+1,2}

and

\displaystyle\Big|\big\langle\mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla\omega\,,\,\omega\big\rangle_{k,2}\Big|\lesssim\|\mathbf{u}\|_{3,2}\|\omega\|_{k,2}^{2}\lesssim\big(1+\|\omega\|_{2,2}\big)\|\omega\|_{k,2}^{2}.

(2b)

Let
k\geq 3
. If
\omega\in W^{k+1,2}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
and
\mathbf{u}\in W^{k+1,2}_{\mathrm{div}}(\mathbb{T}^{2};\mathbb{R}^{2})
solves (
1.5
) for a given

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla\omega\|_{k,2}\lesssim\|\mathbf{u}\|_{2,2}\|\omega\|_{k+1,2}+\|\mathbf{u}\|_{k,2}\|\omega\|_{3,2}\lesssim\big(1+\|\omega\|_{k-1,2}\big)\|\omega\|_{k+1,2}

Our proof of a local strong solution to (

Proposition 3.1
.

Take Assumption
2.4
.
There exists a solution
(b,\omega,T)

(1)

the time

(2)

the pair

\displaystyle b\in C_{w}([0,T];W^{3,2}(\mathbb{T}^{2})),\qquad\omega\in C_{w}([0,T];W^{2,2}(\mathbb{T}^{2}));

\displaystyle\|(b,\omega)(t)\|_{\mathcal{M}}^{2}\leq\frac{\|(b_{0},\omega_{0})\|^{2}_{\mathcal{M}}+2c(1+\|(b_{0},\omega_{0})\|^{2}_{\mathcal{M}})\,t}{1-2c(1+\|(b_{0},\omega_{0})\|^{2}_{\mathcal{M}})\,t},\quad\quad t\in(0,T).

Proof.

Let us consider the following triplet
\{V,H,X\}
given as follows. Take
X:=W^{1,2}(\mathbb{T}^{2})\times L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{2})

and let

\displaystyle\big\langle(b,\omega),(b^{\prime},\omega^{\prime})\big\rangle_{H}=\langle b\,,\,b^{\prime}\rangle_{3,2}+\langle b\,,\,b^{\prime}\rangle_{2,2}+\langle\omega\,,\,\omega^{\prime}\rangle_{2,2}+\langle\omega\,,\,\omega^{\prime}\rangle_{1,2}.

We note that for
(b,\omega)\in\mathcal{M}
, we have that

(3.8)

\displaystyle\|(b,\omega)\|_{\mathcal{M}}\leq\|(b,\omega)\|_{H}\leq 2\|(b,\omega)\|_{\mathcal{M}}

and thus,
H
is equivalent to
\mathcal{M}
. Also, by virtue of the estimates shown in Section

, we can conclude that the operator
\mathcal{A}
is weakly continuous from

into
X
.

Next, we let
V
be the domain of an unbounded selfadjoint operator
S\geq 0
in
X
with
\mathrm{domain}(S)\subset H
and
\langle Su,v\rangle=\langle u,v\rangle_{H}
for
u\in\mathrm{domain}(S)
. More precisely,

S=\sum_{|\beta|\leq 3}(-\partial)^{\beta}\partial^{\beta}

subject to periodic boundary conditions. With this definition of
V
in hand, we can again conclude from the estimates in Section
3.1
that
\mathcal{A}
maps
V
into
H
.
Our goal now is to show that for any
(b,\omega)\in V
, there exists an increasing function

of

such that

(3.9)

\displaystyle\Big|\big\langle\mathcal{A}(b,\omega),(b,\omega)\big\rangle_{H}\Big|\leq\rho\big(\|(b,\omega)\|^{2}_{H}\big).

To achieve this goal, we further refine the following estimates from Section
3.1
. In particular, we recall that if
b\in W^{k+1,2}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
and
\omega\in W^{2,2}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
and that
\mathbf{u}\in W^{3,2}_{\mathrm{div}}(\mathbb{T}^{2};\mathbb{R}^{2})
solves (
1.5
) for a given
f\in W^{2,2}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
and a given
\mathbf{u}_{h}\in W^{3,2}_{\mathrm{div}}(\mathbb{T}^{2};\mathbb{R}^{2})
, then for
k\in\{0,1,2\}
,

(3.10)

\displaystyle

\displaystyle\Big|\big\langle\mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla b\,,\,b\big\rangle_{k,2}\Big|\lesssim\big(1+\|\omega\|_{2,2}\big)\|b\|_{k+1,2}^{2}\lesssim\big(1+\|(b,\omega)\|^{2}_{H}\big)\|b\|_{3,2}^{2},

\displaystyle\Big|\big\langle\mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla\omega\,,\,\omega\big\rangle_{k,2}\Big|\lesssim\big(1+\|\omega\|_{2,2}\big)\|\omega\|_{2,2}^{2}\lesssim\big(1+\|(b,\omega)\|^{2}_{H}\big)\|\omega\|_{2,2}^{2},

\displaystyle\Big|\big\langle\mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla b\,,\,\omega\big\rangle_{k,2}\Big|\lesssim\big(1+\|\omega\|_{2,2}\big)\|b\|_{k+1,2}\|\omega\|_{2,2}\lesssim\big(1+\|(b,\omega)\|^{2}_{H}\big)\big(\|b\|_{3,2}^{2}+\|\omega\|_{2,2}^{2}\big),

\displaystyle\Big|\big\langle\mathbf{u}_{h}\cdot\nabla b\,,\,\omega\big\rangle_{k,2}\Big|\lesssim\|b\|_{k+1,2}\|\omega\|_{2,2}\lesssim\big(\|b\|_{3,2}^{2}+\|\omega\|_{2,2}^{2}\big)\lesssim\big(1+\|(b,\omega)\|^{2}_{H}\big).

In the above estimates, we have used inequalities such as
x\lesssim 1+x^{2}
for
x\geq 0
and Young’s inequalities.
Furthermore, if
b\in W^{3,2}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
,
\omega\in W^{2,2}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
and
\mathbf{u}\in W^{3,2}_{\mathrm{div}}(\mathbb{T}^{2};\mathbb{R}^{2})
, we also have that

(3.11)

\displaystyle

\displaystyle\Big|\big\langle\mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla b\,,\,b\big\rangle_{3,2}\Big|\lesssim\big(1+\|\omega\|_{2,2}\big)\|b\|_{3,2}^{2}\lesssim\big(1+\|(b,\omega)\|^{2}_{H}\big)\|b\|_{3,2}^{2}.

We can therefore conclude that for

(and in particular, for any

),

\displaystyle\Big|\big\langle\mathcal{A}(b,\omega),(b,\omega)\big\rangle_{H}\Big|\lesssim\big(1+\|(b,\omega)\|^{2}_{H}\big)^{2}

with a constant depending only on

and

.
Since

is an increasing function of

, we can conclude from the result in the Appendix, Section

, that for a given
(b_{0},\omega_{0})\in H\equiv\mathcal{M}
, there exist a solution

of (
3.1
) of class

(3.13)

\displaystyle b\in C_{w}([0,T];W^{3,2}(\mathbb{T}^{2})),\qquad\omega\in C_{w}([0,T];W^{2,2}(\mathbb{T}^{2}))

satisfying the bound (recall (
3.8
))

(3.14)

\displaystyle\|(b,\omega)(t)\|_{\mathcal{M}}^{2}\leq\|(b,\omega)(t)\|_{H}^{2}\leq r(t),\quad\quad t\in(0,T)

∎

3.3.
Difference estimate

In the following, we let

)–(
1.5

\displaystyle\sup_{t\in[0,T)}\|b^{i}(t,\cdot)\|_{3,2}<\infty,\qquad\sup_{t\in[0,T)}\|\omega^{i}(t,\cdot)\|_{2,2}<\infty

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}b^{12}+\mathbf{u}^{12}\cdot\nabla b^{1}+\mathbf{u}^{2}\cdot\nabla b^{12}=0,

(3.23)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\omega^{12}+\mathbf{u}^{12}\cdot\nabla(\omega^{1}-b^{1})+\mathbf{u}^{2}\cdot\nabla(\omega^{12}-b^{12})=-\mathbf{u}_{h}\cdot\nabla b^{12},

where

(3.24)

\displaystyle\mathbf{u}^{12}=\nabla^{\perp}\psi^{12},\qquad\mathbf{u}_{h}=\frac{1}{2}\nabla^{\perp}h,\qquad\omega^{12}=(\Delta-1)\psi^{12}.

We now show the following stability and uniqueness results in the space
W^{2,2}(\mathbb{T}^{2})\times W^{1,2}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
which is larger than the space
W^{3,2}(\mathbb{T}^{2})\times W^{2,2}(\mathbb{T}^{2})

Proof of Theorem
2.11
and Corollary
2.13
.

If we apply
\partial^{\beta}

where

\displaystyle\|S_{1}\|_{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim\|\nabla\mathbf{u}^{12}\|_{4}\|b^{1}\|_{2,4}+\|\nabla b^{1}\|_{\infty}\|\mathbf{u}^{12}\|_{2,2}\lesssim\|\mathbf{u}^{12}\|_{2,2}\|b^{1}\|_{3,2},

(3.27)

\displaystyle\|S_{2}\|_{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim\|\nabla\mathbf{u}^{2}\|_{\infty}\|b^{12}\|_{2,2}+\|\nabla b^{12}\|_{4}\|\mathbf{u}^{2}\|_{2,4}\lesssim\|\mathbf{u}^{2}\|_{3,2}\|b^{12}\|_{2,2}.

Additionally, the following estimate for the

holds

\displaystyle\Big|\big\langle\mathbf{u}^{12}\cdot\nabla\partial^{\beta}b^{1}\,,\,\partial^{\beta}b^{12}\big\rangle\Big|\lesssim\|b^{1}\|_{3,2}\|\mathbf{u}^{12}\|_{\infty}\|b^{12}\|_{2,2}.

\displaystyle\frac{\,\mathrm{d}}{\,\mathrm{d}t}\|b^{12}\|_{2,2}^{2}

Next, we apply
\partial^{\beta}
to (

) with

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\partial^{\beta}\omega^{12}+\mathbf{u}^{2}\cdot\nabla\partial^{\beta}\omega^{12}=

\displaystyle-\mathbf{u}^{12}\cdot\nabla\partial^{\beta}(\omega^{1}-b^{1})+(\mathbf{u}^{2}-\mathbf{u}_{h})\cdot\nabla\partial^{\beta}b^{12}

\displaystyle+S_{3}+\ldots+S_{7}

where

\displaystyle S_{3}

\displaystyle:=\mathbf{u}^{2}\cdot\partial^{\beta}\nabla\omega^{12}-\partial^{\beta}(\mathbf{u}^{2}\cdot\nabla\omega^{12}),

\displaystyle S_{4}

\displaystyle:=\mathbf{u}^{12}\cdot\partial^{\beta}\nabla\omega^{1}-\partial^{\beta}(\mathbf{u}^{12}\cdot\nabla\omega^{1}),

\displaystyle S_{5}

\displaystyle:=-\mathbf{u}^{12}\cdot\partial^{\beta}\nabla b^{1}+\partial^{\beta}(\mathbf{u}^{12}\cdot\nabla b^{1}),

\displaystyle S_{6}

\displaystyle:=-\mathbf{u}^{2}\cdot\partial^{\beta}\nabla b^{12}+\partial^{\beta}(\mathbf{u}^{2}\cdot\nabla b^{12}),

Lemma 3.5
.

Take the assumptions of Proposition
3.1
to be true. Then

\displaystyle b\in C([0,T];W^{3,2}(\mathbb{T}^{2})),\qquad\omega\in C([0,T];W^{2,2}(\mathbb{T}^{2})).

Proof.

Firstly, we recall that
the solution we constructed in Proposition
3.1
is actually strongly continuous at time

, recall Remark
3.2
. Now consider a solution
(b,\omega,T_{0})
of (
1.1
)–(
1.2
) where

is fixed but arbitrary. Then by (

), this solution will satisfy the bound

(3.39)

\displaystyle\|(b,\omega)(T_{0})\|^{2}_{\mathcal{M}}\leq\frac{\|(b_{0},\omega_{0})\|^{2}_{\mathcal{M}}+2c(1+\|(b_{0},\omega_{0})\|^{2}_{\mathcal{M}})T_{0}}{1-2c(1+\|(b_{0},\omega_{0})\|^{2}_{\mathcal{M}})T_{0}}

c=c(\|\mathbf{u}_{h}\|_{3,2},\|f\|_{2,2})
. We can now construct a new solution

by taking

as an initial condition. A repetition of the argument leading to (

) means that this new pair

solving (

)–(
1.2
) will satisfy the inequality

(3.40)

\displaystyle\|(b^{1},\omega^{1})(t)\|^{2}_{\mathcal{M}}\leq\frac{\|(b,\omega)(T_{0})\|^{2}_{\mathcal{M}}+2c(1+\|(b,\omega)(T_{0})\|^{2}_{\mathcal{M}})t}{1-2c(1+\|(b,\omega)(T_{0})\|^{2}_{\mathcal{M}})t}

.

4.
Convergence of
\alpha
-TQG to TQG

In this section, for a given
\mathbf{u}_{h}\in W^{3,2}_{\mathrm{div}}(\mathbb{T}^{2};\mathbb{R}^{2})
and

serving as data for both the TQG (

)–(
1.5
) and the
\alpha
-TQG (
2.12
)–(
2.14
), we aim to show that any family
(b^{\alpha},\omega^{\alpha},T_{\max}^{\alpha})_{\alpha>0}
of unique maximal solutions to the
\alpha
-TQG model (
2.12
)–(
2.14
) converges strongly to the unique maximal solution
(b,\omega,T_{\max})
of the TQG model (
1.1
)–(
1.5
) as
\alpha\rightarrow 0
provided that both system share the same initial conditions and are defined on a common time interval
[0,T]
. Our main result is the following.

Proposition 4.1
.

Under Assumption
2.4
,
let
(b^{\alpha},\omega^{\alpha},T_{\max}^{\alpha})_{\alpha>0}
be a family of unique maximal solutions of (
2.12
)–(
2.14
) and let
(b,\omega,T_{\max})
be the unique maximal solution of (

)–(
1.5
). Then

(4.1)

\displaystyle\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\big(\|({b}^{\alpha}-b)(t)\|_{1,2}^{2}

\displaystyle+\|({\omega}^{\alpha}-\omega)(t)\|_{2}^{2}\big)\lesssim\alpha^{2}T\exp(cT)\big[1+\exp(cT)\big]

for any
T<\min\{T_{\max},T_{\max}^{\alpha}\}
and

(4.2)

\displaystyle\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\|({b}^{\alpha}-b)(t)\|_{1,2}\rightarrow 0,\quad\qquad\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\|({\omega}^{\alpha}-\omega)(t)\|_{2}\rightarrow 0

as
\alpha\rightarrow 0
.

We will now devote the entirety of this section to the proof of Proposition
4.1
.
In order to achieve this goal, we first introduce a unique maximal solution
(\overline{b}^{\alpha},\overline{\omega}^{\alpha},\overline{T}^{\alpha}_{\max})
with initial conditions

and
\omega_{0}\in W^{2,2}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
of the following “intermediate
\alpha
-TQG” equation given by

(4.3)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\overline{b}^{\alpha}+\overline{\mathbf{u}}^{\alpha}\cdot\nabla\overline{b}^{\alpha}=0,

(4.4)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\overline{\omega}^{\alpha}+\overline{\mathbf{u}}^{\alpha}\cdot\nabla(\overline{\omega}^{\alpha}-\overline{b}^{\alpha})=-\mathbf{u}_{h}\cdot\nabla\overline{b}^{\alpha},

where like (
2.15
),

(4.5)

\displaystyle\overline{\mathbf{u}}^{\alpha}=\nabla^{\perp}(1-\alpha\Delta)^{-1}(\Delta-1)^{-1}(\omega-f),\quad\mathbf{u}_{h}=\frac{1}{2}\nabla^{\perp}h,

and where
\omega
(and not
\omega^{\alpha}
) satisfies the original potential vorticity equation (
1.2
) strongly in the PDE sense.
The existence of a unique maximal solution to the above intermediate system, denoted by
\bf{i}\alpha
-TQG for short, follow from the proof of existence for the original TQG and it particular, for a uniform-in-
\alpha

(\mathbf{u}_{h},f,b_{0},\omega_{0})
, we have the following uniform-in-
\alpha
estimates

(4.6)

\displaystyle\sup_{\alpha>0}\sup_{t\in[0,\overline{T}^{\alpha}_{\max})}\|\overline{b}^{\alpha}\|_{3,2}\lesssim 1,\quad\sup_{\alpha>0}\sup_{t\in[0,\overline{T}^{\alpha}_{\max})}\|\overline{\omega}^{\alpha}\|_{2,2}\lesssim 1

for the maximal solution of (
4.3
)–(
4.5
). Indeed, exactly as in Theorem
2.15
, we also have the following result.

Lemma 4.2
.

Fix
\alpha>0
. There exist a unique maximal solution
(\overline{b}^{\alpha},\overline{\omega}^{\alpha},\overline{T}^{\alpha}_{\max})
of (
4.3
)–(
4.5
) under Assumption
2.4
. In particular, uniformly of
\alpha>0
, the inequality

(4.7)

\displaystyle\|(\overline{b}^{\alpha},\overline{\omega}^{\alpha})(t)\|_{\mathcal{M}}\lesssim 1

holds for
t<\overline{T}^{\alpha}_{\max}
.

As a next step, we show that any family
(\overline{b}^{\alpha},\overline{\omega}^{\alpha},\overline{T}^{\alpha}_{\max})_{\alpha>0}
of maximal solutions to the intermediate
\mathbf{i}\alpha
-TQG model (
4.3
)–(
4.5
) (rather than of the
\alpha
-TQG model (
2.12
)–(
2.14
)) converges strongly to the unique maximal solution of the TQG model (
1.1
)–(
1.5
) as
\alpha\rightarrow 0
on the time interval
[0,T]
where
T<\min\{\overline{T}^{\alpha}_{\max},T_{\max}\}
. In order to achieve this goal, we replicate the uniqueness argument in Section
3.3
by setting

b^{12}:=\overline{b}^{\alpha}-b
,
\omega^{12}:=\overline{\omega}^{\alpha}-\omega
and
\mathbf{u}^{12}:=\overline{\mathbf{u}}^{\alpha}-\mathbf{u}
so that
(b^{12},\mathbf{u}^{12},\omega^{12})
satisfies

(4.8)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}b^{12}+\mathbf{u}^{12}\cdot\nabla\overline{b}^{\alpha}+\mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla b^{12}=0,

(4.9)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\omega^{12}+\mathbf{u}^{12}\cdot\nabla(\overline{\omega}^{\alpha}-\overline{b}^{\alpha})+\mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla(\omega^{12}-b^{12})=-\mathbf{u}_{h}\cdot\nabla b^{12}.

Similar to the uniqueness argument, if we apply
\partial^{\beta}
to the equation for
b^{12}
above where now
|\beta|\leq 1
, we obtain

(4.10)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\partial^{\beta}b^{12}+\mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla\partial^{\beta}b^{12}=S_{1}+S_{2}-\mathbf{u}^{12}\cdot\nabla\partial^{\beta}\overline{b}^{\alpha},

where

\displaystyle S_{1}

\displaystyle:=\mathbf{u}^{12}\cdot\partial^{\beta}\nabla\overline{b}^{\alpha}-\partial^{\beta}(\mathbf{u}^{12}\cdot\nabla\overline{b}^{\alpha})=-\partial\mathbf{u}^{12}\cdot\nabla\overline{b}^{\alpha},

\displaystyle S_{2}

\displaystyle:=\mathbf{u}\cdot\partial^{\beta}\nabla b^{12}-\partial^{\beta}(\mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla b^{12})=-\partial\mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla b^{12}

are such that

(4.11)

\displaystyle\|S_{1}\|_{2}\lesssim\|\mathbf{u}^{12}\|_{1,2}\|\overline{b}^{\alpha}\|_{3,2},\qquad\qquad\|S_{2}\|_{2}\lesssim\|\mathbf{u}\|_{3,2}\|b^{12}\|_{1,2}.

Also,

(4.12)

\displaystyle\Big|\big\langle\mathbf{u}^{12}\cdot\nabla\partial^{\beta}\overline{b}^{\alpha}\,,\,\partial^{\beta}b^{12}\big\rangle\Big|\lesssim\|\mathbf{u}^{12}\|_{1,2}\|\overline{b}^{\alpha}\|_{3,2}\|b^{12}\|_{1,2}.

Collecting the information above yields the estimate

(4.13)

\displaystyle\frac{\,\mathrm{d}}{\,\mathrm{d}t}\|b^{12}\|_{1,2}^{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim\big(\|\mathbf{u}^{12}\|_{1,2}\|\overline{b}^{\alpha}\|_{3,2},+\|\mathbf{u}\|_{3,2}\|b^{12}\|_{1,2}\big)\|b^{12}\|_{1,2}.

For the equation for
\omega^{12}
, we aim to derive a square-integrable estimate in space. For this, we first note that

(4.14)

\displaystyle\Big|\big\langle\mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla\omega^{12}\,,\,\omega^{12}\big\rangle\Big|=0.

Next, we have that

(4.15)

\displaystyle\Big|\big\langle(\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{u}_{h})\cdot\nabla b^{12}\,,\,\omega^{12}\big\rangle\Big|\lesssim\|\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{u}_{h}\|_{\infty}\|b^{12}\|_{1,2}\|\omega^{12}\|_{2}.

Finally, we also have that

(4.16)

\displaystyle\Big|\big\langle\mathbf{u}^{12}\cdot\nabla(\overline{\omega}^{\alpha}-\overline{b}^{\alpha})\,,\,\omega^{12}\big\rangle\Big|

\displaystyle\lesssim\|\mathbf{u}^{12}\|_{4}\|\nabla(\overline{\omega}^{\alpha}-\overline{b}^{\alpha})\|_{4}\|\omega^{12}\|_{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim\|\mathbf{u}^{12}\|_{1,2}\|\overline{\omega}^{\alpha}-\overline{b}^{\alpha}\|_{2,2}\|\omega^{12}\|_{2}.

Therefore,

(4.17)

\displaystyle\frac{\,\mathrm{d}}{\,\mathrm{d}t}\|\omega^{12}\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim\big(\|\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{u}_{h}\|_{\infty}\|b^{12}\|_{1,2}+\|\mathbf{u}^{12}\|_{1,2}\|\overline{\omega}^{\alpha}-\overline{b}^{\alpha}\|_{2,2}\big)\|\omega^{12}\|_{2}.

Summing up with the estimate for
b^{12}
then yield

(4.18)

\displaystyle\frac{\,\mathrm{d}}{\,\mathrm{d}t}\big(\|b^{12}\|_{1,2}^{2}+\|\omega^{12}\|_{2}^{2}\big)

\displaystyle\lesssim\big(\|\mathbf{u}^{12}\|_{1,2}\|\overline{b}^{\alpha}\|_{3,2},+\|\mathbf{u}\|_{3,2}\|b^{12}\|_{1,2}\big)\|b^{12}\|_{1,2}

\displaystyle+\big(\|\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{u}_{h}\|_{\infty}\|b^{12}\|_{1,2}+\|\mathbf{u}^{12}\|_{1,2}\|\overline{\omega}^{\alpha}-\overline{b}^{\alpha}\|_{2,2}\big)\|\omega^{12}\|_{2}.

If we use (
2.7
) and (
4.7
), we obtain

(4.19)

\displaystyle\frac{\,\mathrm{d}}{\,\mathrm{d}t}\big(\|b^{12}\|_{1,2}^{2}+\|\omega^{12}\|_{2}^{2}\big)

\displaystyle\lesssim\big(\|\mathbf{u}^{12}\|_{1,2}+\|b^{12}\|_{1,2}\big)\|b^{12}\|_{1,2}

\displaystyle+\big(\|b^{12}\|_{1,2}+\|\mathbf{u}^{12}\|_{1,2}\big)\|\omega^{12}\|_{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim\|\mathbf{u}^{12}\|_{1,2}^{2}+\|b^{12}\|_{1,2}^{2}+\|\omega^{12}\|_{2}^{2}.

Now recall that the velocity fields for the
\alpha
-TQG, the
\bf{i}\alpha
-TQG, and the TQG are given by

(4.20)

\displaystyle{\mathbf{u}}^{\alpha}=\nabla^{\perp}(1-\alpha\Delta)^{-1}(\Delta-1)^{-1}(\omega^{\alpha}-f),

(4.21)

\displaystyle\overline{\mathbf{u}}^{\alpha}=\nabla^{\perp}(1-\alpha\Delta)^{-1}(\Delta-1)^{-1}(\omega-f),

(4.22)

\displaystyle\mathbf{u}=\nabla^{\perp}(\Delta-1)^{-1}(\omega-f)

respectively. As a result, in particular, the difference

\displaystyle\overline{\mathbf{u}}^{\alpha}-\mathbf{u}=\alpha\Delta\nabla^{\perp}(1-\alpha\Delta)^{-1}(\Delta-1)^{-1}(\omega-f)

enjoys two extra order of regularity. Furthermore, since each of these individual velocity fields satisfies the bound (
2.7
), it immediately follows that

(4.23)

\displaystyle\|\overline{\mathbf{u}}^{\alpha}-\mathbf{u}\|_{1,2}^{2}\lesssim\alpha^{2}\|\mathbf{u}\|_{3,2}^{2}\lesssim\alpha^{2}\|\omega-f\|_{2,2}^{2}\lesssim\alpha^{2}\big(\|\omega\|_{2,2}^{2}+\|f\|_{2,2}^{2}\big)\lesssim\alpha^{2}.

This means that for the comparison of the TQG and
\bf{i}\alpha
-TQG, we can conclude from (
4.19
) that

(4.24)

\displaystyle\frac{\,\mathrm{d}}{\,\mathrm{d}t}\big(\|\overline{b}^{\alpha}-b\|_{1,2}^{2}

\displaystyle+\|\overline{\omega}^{\alpha}-\omega\|_{2}^{2}\big)\lesssim\alpha^{2}+\|\overline{b}^{\alpha}-b\|_{1,2}^{2}+\|\overline{\omega}^{\alpha}-\omega\|_{2}^{2}

and by Grönwall’s lemma, (note that
b^{12}_{0}=0
and
\omega^{12}_{0}=0
)

(4.25)

\displaystyle\|(\overline{b}^{\alpha}-b)(t)\|_{1,2}^{2}

\displaystyle+\|(\overline{\omega}^{\alpha}-\omega)(t)\|_{2}^{2}\lesssim\alpha^{2}T\exp(cT),\qquad t\in[0,T].

The above gives the decay rate of the difference of the solution to the
\mathbf{i}\alpha
-TQG and TQG equations. Our next goal is to obtain a decay rate for the difference between the
\mathbf{i}\alpha
-TQG and the
\alpha
-TQG equations. Recall that they share the same initial data.
For this, we use the estimate

\displaystyle\|\overline{\mathbf{u}}^{\alpha}-{\mathbf{u}}^{\alpha}\|_{1,2}^{2}\lesssim\|\omega-\omega^{\alpha}\|_{2}^{2}\lesssim\|\overline{\omega}^{\alpha}-\omega\|_{2}^{2}+\|\overline{\omega}^{\alpha}-\omega^{\alpha}\|_{2}^{2}

so that by setting

b^{12}:=\overline{b}^{\alpha}-b^{\alpha}
,
\omega^{12}:=\overline{\omega}^{\alpha}-\omega^{\alpha}
and
\mathbf{u}^{12}:=\overline{\mathbf{u}}^{\alpha}-{\mathbf{u}}^{\alpha}
,
we obtain from (
4.19
),

(4.26)

\displaystyle\frac{\,\mathrm{d}}{\,\mathrm{d}t}\big(\|b^{12}\|_{1,2}^{2}+\|\omega^{12}\|_{2}^{2}\big)

\displaystyle\lesssim\|\mathbf{u}^{12}\|_{1,2}^{2}+\|b^{12}\|_{1,2}^{2}+\|\omega^{12}\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim\|\overline{\omega}^{\alpha}-\omega\|_{2}^{2}+\|b^{12}\|_{1,2}^{2}+\|\omega^{12}\|_{2}^{2}.

Since both systems share the same initial conditions, by Grönwall’s lemma and (
4.25
), it follows that

(4.27)

\displaystyle\|(\overline{b}^{\alpha}-b^{\alpha})(t)\|_{1,2}^{2}+\|(\overline{\omega}^{\alpha}-\omega^{\alpha})(t)\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\exp(cT)\int_{0}^{T}c\,\|(\overline{\omega}^{\alpha}-\omega)(t)\|_{2}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\lesssim\alpha^{2}T\big[\exp(cT)\big]^{2}.

By the triangle inequality, it follows from (
4.25
) and (
4.27
) that

(4.28)

\displaystyle\|({b}^{\alpha}-b)(t)\|_{1,2}^{2}

\displaystyle+\|({\omega}^{\alpha}-\omega)(t)\|_{2}^{2}\lesssim\alpha^{2}T\exp(cT)\big[1+\exp(cT)\big]

so that

\displaystyle\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\|({b}^{\alpha}-b)(t)\|_{1,2}\rightarrow 0,\quad\qquad\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\|({\omega}^{\alpha}-\omega)(t)\|_{2}\rightarrow 0

as
\alpha\rightarrow 0
. This ends the proof.

local-well-posedness-for-the-euler-equations

5.
Conditions for blowup of the
\alpha
-TQG model

In the following, we give Beale–Kato–Majda
[
2
]
type conditions under which we expect the strong solution of the
\alpha
-TQG to blowup.

Theorem 5.1
.

Fix
\alpha>0
. Under Assumption
2.4
, let
(b^{\alpha},\omega^{\alpha},T_{\max}^{\alpha})
be
a maximal solution of (
2.12
)–(
2.14
). If
T_{\max}^{\alpha}<\infty
, then

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T_{\max}^{\alpha}}\|\nabla b^{\alpha}\|_{\infty}\,\mathrm{d}t=\infty.

Proof.

First of all, fix
\alpha>0
and let
T_{\max}^{\alpha}>0
be the maximal time so that

(5.1)

\displaystyle\limsup_{T_{n}\rightarrow T_{\max}^{\alpha}}\|(b^{\alpha},\omega^{\alpha})(T_{n})\|_{\mathcal{M}}^{2}=\infty.

We now suppose that

(5.2)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T_{\max}^{\alpha}}\|\nabla b^{\alpha}\|_{\infty}\,\mathrm{d}t=K<\infty

and show that

(5.3)

\displaystyle\|(b^{\alpha},\omega^{\alpha})(t)\|_{\mathcal{M}}^{2}\lesssim 1,\qquad t<T_{\max}^{\alpha}

holds yielding a contradiction to (
5.1
).

Before we show the estimate (
5.3
), we first require preliminary estimates for
\|\nabla\mathbf{u}^{\alpha}\|_{\infty}
and
\|\omega^{\alpha}\|_{2}
. To obtain these estimates, we note that the Fourier multiplier
m^{\alpha}(\xi)
defined below satisfies the bound

(5.4)

\displaystyle m^{\alpha}(\xi):=\frac{(1+|\xi|^{2})|\xi|^{2}}{(1+\alpha|\xi|^{2})^{2}}\lesssim_{\alpha}1

for all fixed
\alpha>0
and in particular, the bound may be taken uniformly of all
\alpha\geq 1
. Due to (
5.4
) and the continuous embedding
W^{3,2}(\mathbb{T}^{2})\hookrightarrow W^{1,\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
, we can conclude that

(5.5)

\displaystyle\|\nabla\mathbf{u}^{\alpha}\|_{\infty}\lesssim\|\mathbf{u}^{\alpha}\|_{3,2}\lesssim_{\alpha}\|\omega^{\alpha}-f\|_{2}\lesssim\|\omega^{\alpha}-f\|_{2,2}.

On the other hand, if we test (
2.13
) with
\omega^{\alpha}
and
use (
2.7
) for
w=(1-\alpha\Delta)^{-1}(\omega^{\alpha}-f)
and
k=0
,
we obtain

(5.6)

\displaystyle\frac{\,\mathrm{d}}{\,\mathrm{d}t}\|\omega^{\alpha}\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim\Big(\|\mathbf{u}^{\alpha}\|_{2}\|\omega^{\alpha}\|_{2}+\|\mathbf{u}_{h}\|_{2}\|\omega^{\alpha}\|_{2}\Big)\|\nabla b^{\alpha}\|_{\infty}\lesssim\Big(1+\|\omega^{\alpha}\|_{2}^{2}\Big)\|\nabla b^{\alpha}\|_{\infty}

for a constant depending only on
\|\mathbf{u}_{h}\|_{2}
and
\|f\|_{2}
. It therefore follow from (
5.6
) and (
5.2
) that

(5.7)

\displaystyle\|\omega^{\alpha}(t)\|_{2}^{2}\lesssim\big(1+\|\omega_{0}\|_{2}^{2}\big)\exp(cK),\qquad t<T_{\max}^{\alpha}

which when combined with the first estimate in (
5.5
) yields

(5.8)

\displaystyle\|\nabla\mathbf{u}^{\alpha}(t)\|_{\infty}\lesssim_{\alpha}1+\big(1+\|\omega_{0}\|_{2}^{2}\big)\exp(cK)\lesssim_{\alpha}\big(1+\|\omega_{0}\|_{2}^{2}\big)\exp(cK),\qquad t<T_{\max}^{\alpha}.

Recall that
f\in W^{2,2}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
by assumption.

With these preliminary estimates in hand, we can proceed to derive estimates for
(b^{\alpha},\omega^{\alpha})
in the space
\mathcal{M}
of existence of a solution.
Since the space of smooth functions is dense in
\mathcal{M}
, it suffices to show our result for a smooth solution pair
(b^{\alpha},\omega^{\alpha})
.

To achieve our goal, we apply
\partial^{\beta}
to (
2.12
) for
|\beta|\leq 3
to obtain

(5.9)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\partial^{\beta}b^{\alpha}+\mathbf{u}^{\alpha}\cdot\nabla\partial^{\beta}b^{\alpha}=R_{1}

where

\displaystyle R_{1}:=\mathbf{u}^{\alpha}\cdot\partial^{\beta}\nabla b^{\alpha}-\partial^{\beta}(\mathbf{u}^{\alpha}\cdot\nabla b^{\alpha}).

Now since
\mathrm{div}\mathbf{u}^{\alpha}=0
, if we multiply (
5.9
) by
\partial^{\beta}b^{\alpha}
and sum over the multiindex
\beta
so that
|\beta|\leq 3
, we obtain

(5.10)

\displaystyle\frac{\,\mathrm{d}}{\,\mathrm{d}t}\|b^{\alpha}\|_{3,2}^{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim\Big(\|\nabla\mathbf{u}^{\alpha}\|_{\infty}\|b^{\alpha}\|_{3,2}+\|\nabla b^{\alpha}\|_{\infty}\|\mathbf{u}^{\alpha}\|_{3,2}\Big)\|b^{\alpha}\|_{3,2}

\displaystyle\lesssim(\|\nabla\mathbf{u}^{\alpha}\|_{\infty}+\|\nabla b^{\alpha}\|_{\infty})(1+\|(b^{\alpha},\omega^{\alpha})\|_{\mathcal{M}}^{2})

where we have used (
2.7
) for
w=(1-\alpha\Delta)^{-1}(\omega^{\alpha}-f)
and
k=2
.

Next, we find a bound for
\|\omega^{\alpha}\|^{2}_{2,2}
. For this, we apply
\partial^{\beta}
to (
2.13
) for
|\beta|\leq 2
and we obtain

(5.11)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\partial^{\beta}\omega^{\alpha}+\mathbf{u}^{\alpha}\cdot\nabla\partial^{\beta}(\omega^{\alpha}-b^{\alpha})+\mathbf{u}_{h}\cdot\nabla\partial^{\beta}b^{\alpha}=R_{2}+R_{3}+R_{4}

where

\displaystyle R_{2}

\displaystyle:=\mathbf{u}^{\alpha}\cdot\partial^{\beta}\nabla\omega^{\alpha}-\partial^{\beta}(\mathbf{u}^{\alpha}\cdot\nabla\omega^{\alpha}),

\displaystyle R_{3}

\displaystyle:=-\mathbf{u}^{\alpha}\cdot\partial^{\beta}\nabla b^{\alpha}+\partial^{\beta}(\mathbf{u}^{\alpha}\cdot\nabla b^{\alpha}),

\displaystyle R_{4}

\displaystyle:=\mathbf{u}_{h}\cdot\partial^{\beta}\nabla b^{\alpha}-\partial^{\beta}(\mathbf{u}_{h}\cdot\nabla b^{\alpha})

are such that

(5.12)

\displaystyle\big|\big\langle R_{2}\,,\,\partial^{\beta}\omega^{\alpha}\big\rangle\big|\lesssim\|R_{2}\|_{2}\|\omega^{\alpha}\|_{2,2}

\displaystyle\lesssim\Big(\|\nabla\mathbf{u}^{\alpha}\|_{\infty}\|\omega^{\alpha}\|_{2,2}+\|\omega^{\alpha}\|_{2,2}(1+\|\omega^{\alpha}\|_{2})\Big)\|\omega^{\alpha}\|_{2,2},

(5.13)

\displaystyle\big|\big\langle R_{3}\,,\,\partial^{\beta}\omega^{\alpha}\big\rangle\big|\lesssim\|R_{3}\|_{2}\|\omega^{\alpha}\|_{2,2}

\displaystyle\lesssim\Big(\|\nabla\mathbf{u}^{\alpha}\|_{\infty}\|b^{\alpha}\|_{3,2}+\|\nabla b^{\alpha}\|_{\infty}(1+\|\omega^{\alpha}\|_{2,2})\Big)\|\omega^{\alpha}\|_{2,2},

(5.14)

\displaystyle\big|\big\langle R_{4}\,,\,\partial^{\beta}\omega^{\alpha}\big\rangle\big|\lesssim\|R_{4}\|_{2}\|\omega^{\alpha}\|_{2,2}

\displaystyle\lesssim\Big(\|b^{\alpha}\|_{3,2}+\|\nabla b^{\alpha}\|_{\infty}\Big)\|\omega^{\alpha}\|_{2,2}

holds true. Recall that
\mathbf{u}_{h}\in W^{3,2}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
by assumption and note that we have used the continuous embedding
W^{3,2}(\mathbb{T}^{2})\hookrightarrow W^{2,4}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
and the second inequality in (
5.5
) in order to obtain the term
1+\|\omega^{\alpha}\|_{2}
for the estimates (
5.12
).
Next, by using
\mathrm{div}\mathbf{u}^{\alpha}=0
, we have the following identity

(5.15)

\displaystyle\big\langle(\mathbf{u}^{\alpha}\cdot\nabla\partial^{\beta}\omega^{\alpha})\,,\,\partial^{\beta}\omega^{\alpha}\big\rangle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}\mathrm{div}(\mathbf{u}^{\alpha}|\partial^{\beta}\omega^{\alpha}|^{2})\,\mathrm{d}x=0.

Additionally, the following estimates for
L^{2}
inner products holds true

(5.16)

\displaystyle\Big|\big\langle(\mathbf{u}^{\alpha}\cdot\nabla\partial^{\beta}b^{\alpha})\,,\,\partial^{\beta}\omega^{\alpha}\big\rangle\Big|

\displaystyle\lesssim\|\mathbf{u}^{\alpha}\|_{\infty}\|b^{\alpha}\|_{3,2}^{2}+\|\mathbf{u}^{\alpha}\|_{\infty}\|\omega^{\alpha}\|_{2,2}^{2},

(5.17)

\displaystyle\Big|\big\langle(\mathbf{u}_{h}\cdot\nabla\partial^{\beta}b^{\alpha})\,,\,\partial^{\beta}\omega^{\alpha}\big\rangle\Big|

\displaystyle\lesssim\|b^{\alpha}\|_{3,2}^{2}+\|\omega^{\alpha}\|_{2,2}^{2}.

since
\mathbf{u}_{h}\in W^{3,2}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
.
If we now collect the estimates above (keeping in mind that
f\in W^{2,2}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
and
\mathbf{u}_{h}\in W^{3,2}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
), we obtain by multiplying (
5.11
) by
\partial^{\beta}\omega^{\alpha}
with
|\beta|\leq 2
and then summing over
|\beta|\leq 2
, the following

(5.18)

\displaystyle\frac{\,\mathrm{d}}{\,\mathrm{d}t}\|\omega^{\alpha}\|_{2,2}^{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim\big(1+\|\mathbf{u}^{\alpha}\|_{1,\infty}+\|\nabla b^{\alpha}\|_{\infty}+\|\omega^{\alpha}\|_{2}\big)\big(1+\|(b^{\alpha},\omega^{\alpha})\|_{\mathcal{M}}^{2}\big).

Summing up (
5.10
) and (
5.18
) and using (
5.7
)–(
5.5
) yields

(5.19)

\displaystyle\frac{\,\mathrm{d}}{\,\mathrm{d}t}(1+\|(b^{\alpha},\omega^{\alpha})\|_{\mathcal{M}}^{2})

\displaystyle\lesssim_{\alpha}\Big(\big(1+\|\omega_{0}\|_{2}^{2}\big)\exp(cK)+\|\nabla b^{\alpha}\|_{\infty}\Big)(1+\|(b^{\alpha},\omega^{\alpha})\|_{\mathcal{M}}^{2})

so that by Grönwall’s lemma and (

(5.20)

\displaystyle\|(b^{\alpha},\omega^{\alpha})(t)\|_{\mathcal{M}}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\big[1+\|(b_{0},\omega_{0})\|_{\mathcal{M}}^{2}\big]\exp\Big(c(\alpha)T_{\max}\big(1+\|\omega_{0}\|_{2}^{2}\big)\exp(cK)+c(\alpha)K\Big)

\displaystyle\lesssim_{T_{\max}^{\alpha},K,f,\mathbf{u}_{h},\omega_{0},b_{0},\alpha}1

for all
t<{T_{\max}^{\alpha}}
contradicting (
5.1
).

Remark 5.2

(Global existence for constant-in-space buoyancy)
.

We note that for the
\alpha
-TQG model,
when the buoyancy is constant in space so that
\nabla b^{\alpha}=0
, then the equation for the buoyancy decouples from that of the potential vorticity. The potential vorticity equation then reduces to the
2
-dimensional Euler equation albeit a different Biot–Savart law. Nevertheless, by inspecting the proof of Theorem
5.1
above, one observes that the same global-in-time result applies. In particular, the residual estimate (
5.12
) still holds true and we obtain from this, a refine version of (
5.7
)-(
5.8
) where
\exp(cK)=1
. Recall from (
5.2
) that
K=0
when
\nabla b^{\alpha}=0
.

Remark 5.3

(Global existence for super diffusive buoyancy)
.

Fix
\alpha>0
. Note that by adding any super diffusive term
\Lambda^{\beta}b^{\alpha}
,
\beta\geq 1
to the right-hand side of (
2.12
) so that

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\|\nabla b^{\alpha}(s)\|_{\infty}\,\mathrm{d}s\lesssim\int_{0}^{t}\|\Lambda^{\beta/2}b^{\alpha}(s)\|_{2}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}s

holds for all
t<T_{\max}^{\alpha}
, then we obtain a global solution since in this case, we obtain the energy estimate

\displaystyle\|b^{\alpha}(t)\|_{2}^{2}+\int_{0}^{t}\|\Lambda^{\beta/2}b^{\alpha}(s)\|_{2}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}s\leq\|b_{0}\|_{2}\leq\|b_{0}\|_{3,2}

for all
t<T_{\max}^{\alpha}
.

Next, we also give an alternative to the blowup condition in Theorem
5.1
above.

Theorem 5.4
.

Fix
\alpha>0
. Under Assumption
2.4
, let
(b^{\alpha},\omega^{\alpha},T_{\max}^{\alpha})
be
a maximal solution of (
2.12
)–(
2.14
). If
T_{\max}^{\alpha}<\infty
, then

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T_{\max}^{\alpha}}\|\nabla\mathbf{u}^{\alpha}\|_{\infty}\,\mathrm{d}t=\infty.

Proof.

In analogy with the proof of Theorem
5.1
, we fix
\alpha>0
, let
T_{\max}^{\alpha}>0
be the maximal time so that

(5.21)

\displaystyle\limsup_{T_{n}\rightarrow T_{\max}^{\alpha}}\|(b^{\alpha},\omega^{\alpha})(T_{n})\|_{\mathcal{M}}^{2}=\infty.

We now suppose that

(5.22)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T_{\max}^{\alpha}}\|\nabla\mathbf{u}^{\alpha}\|_{\infty}\,\mathrm{d}t=K<\infty

and show that

(5.23)

\displaystyle\|(b^{\alpha},\omega^{\alpha})(t)\|_{\mathcal{M}}^{2}\lesssim 1,\qquad t<T_{\max}^{\alpha}

holds yielding a contradiction to (
5.21
).

To show this, we first fix
\alpha>0
. If we differentiate (
2.12
) in space, multiply the resulting equation by
p|\nabla b^{\alpha}|^{p-2}\nabla b^{\alpha}
where
p>1
is fixed and finite in this instant, and then integrate over
\mathbb{T}^{2}
, we obtain

(5.24)

\displaystyle\frac{\,\mathrm{d}}{\,\mathrm{d}t}\|\nabla b^{\alpha}\|_{p}^{p}\leq p\|\nabla\mathbf{u}^{\alpha}\|_{\infty}\|\nabla b^{\alpha}\|_{p}^{p}.

Rather than use Grönwall’s lemma at this point, we use the analogous separation of variable technique to obtain

\displaystyle\frac{1}{p}\ln\|\nabla b^{\alpha}(t)\|_{p}^{p}\leq\frac{1}{p}\ln\|\nabla b_{0}\|_{p}^{p}+\int_{0}^{t}\|\nabla\mathbf{u}^{\alpha}(s)\|_{\infty}\,\mathrm{d}s

so that

\displaystyle\|\nabla b^{\alpha}(t)\|_{p}\leq\|\nabla b_{0}\|_{p}\exp\bigg(\int_{0}^{t}\|\nabla\mathbf{u}^{\alpha}(s)\|_{\infty}\,\mathrm{d}s\bigg)

holds for all
t<T_{\max}^{\alpha}
uniformly in

. Since

, by Sobolev embedding and (

), we obtain

(5.25)

\displaystyle\|\nabla b^{\alpha}(t)\|_{\infty}\lesssim\|b_{0}\|_{3,2}\exp(K)

for all
t<T_{\max}^{\alpha}
so that

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T_{\max}^{\alpha}}\|\nabla b^{\alpha}(t)\|_{\infty}\,\mathrm{d}t\leq c\,T_{\max}^{\alpha}\|b_{0}\|_{3,2}\exp(K).

We can therefore deduce from the estimate (
5.6
) that

(5.26)

\displaystyle\|\omega^{\alpha}(t)\|_{2}^{2}\lesssim\big(1+\|\omega_{0}\|_{2}^{2}\big)\exp\big(cT_{\max}^{\alpha}\|b_{0}\|_{3,2}\exp(K)\big),\qquad t<T_{\max}^{\alpha}.

Summing up (
5.10
) and (
5.18
) and using (
5.25
)–(
5.36
) yields

(5.27)

\displaystyle\frac{\,\mathrm{d}}{\,\mathrm{d}t}(1+\|(b^{\alpha},\omega^{\alpha})\|_{\mathcal{M}}^{2})

\displaystyle\lesssim\big(1+\|\mathbf{u}^{\alpha}\|_{1,\infty}+\mathfrak{A}\big)(1+\|(b^{\alpha},\omega^{\alpha})\|_{\mathcal{M}}^{2})

where

\displaystyle\mathfrak{A}:=\|b_{0}\|_{3,2}\exp(K)+\big(1+\|\omega_{0}\|_{2}^{2}\big)\exp\big(cT_{\max}^{\alpha}\|b_{0}\|_{3,2}\exp(K)\big).

We can therefore conclude from Grönwall’s lemma and (
5.22
) that

(5.28)

\displaystyle\|(b^{\alpha},\omega^{\alpha})(t)\|_{\mathcal{M}}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\big[1+\|(b_{0},\omega_{0})\|_{\mathcal{M}}^{2}\big]\exp\Big(cT_{\max}^{\alpha}+cK+cT_{\max}^{\alpha}\mathfrak{A}\Big)

\displaystyle\lesssim_{T_{\max}^{\alpha},K,f,\mathbf{u}_{h},\omega_{0},b_{0}}1

for all
t<{T_{\max}^{\alpha}}
contradicting (
5.21
).
∎

In order the give the final blowup criterion,
let us now recall the following endpoint Sobolev inequality by Brézis and Gallouet
[
8
]
.

Lemma 5.5
.

If
f\in W^{2,2}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
then

\displaystyle\|f\|_{\infty}\lesssim(1+\|f\|_{1,2})\sqrt{\ln(\mathrm{e}+\|f\|_{2,2})}.

Remark 5.6
.

Note that the original statement in
[
8
]
restricted the size of
\|f\|_{1,2}
to being at most one. The current form for any size of
\|f\|_{1,2}
follows immediately as demonstrated in for example
[
33
,
10
]
.

With Lemma
5.5
in hand, we can now show the final blowup condition.

Theorem 5.7
.

Fix
\alpha>0
. Let
(b^{\alpha},\omega^{\alpha},T_{\max}^{\alpha})
be
a maximal solution of (
2.12
)–(
2.14
). If
T_{\max}^{\alpha}<\infty
, then

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T_{\max}^{\alpha}}\|\nabla b^{\alpha}\|_{1,2}\,\mathrm{d}t=\infty.

Proof.

Fix
\alpha>0
and let
T_{\max}^{\alpha}>0
be the maximal time so that

(5.29)

\displaystyle\limsup_{T_{n}\rightarrow T_{\max}^{\alpha}}\|(b^{\alpha},\omega^{\alpha})(T_{n})\|_{\mathcal{M}}^{2}=\infty.

We now suppose that

(5.30)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T_{\max}^{\alpha}}\|\nabla b^{\alpha}\|_{1,2}\,\mathrm{d}t=K<\infty

and show that

(5.31)

\displaystyle\|(b^{\alpha},\omega^{\alpha})(t)\|_{\mathcal{M}}^{2}\lesssim 1,\qquad t<T_{\max}^{\alpha}

holds and thereby yields a contradiction to (
5.29
).

To show this, we first fix
\alpha>0

and define

\displaystyle g(t):=\mathrm{e}+\|(b^{\alpha},\omega^{\alpha})(t)\|_{\mathcal{M}}^{2},\qquad t>0.

With this definition in hand, it follows from
estimates (
5.10
) and (
5.18
) that

(5.32)

\displaystyle\frac{\,\mathrm{d}}{\,\mathrm{d}t}g\lesssim\big(1+\|\mathbf{u}^{\alpha}\|_{1,\infty}+\|\omega^{\alpha}\|_{2}+\|\nabla b^{\alpha}\|_{\infty}\big)g.

By using Lemma
5.5
and the monotonic property of logarithms however, we can deduce that

(5.33)

\displaystyle\|\nabla b^{\alpha}\|_{\infty}\lesssim\big(1+\|\nabla b^{\alpha}\|_{1,2}\big)\ln\,g.

Next, we observe that the second estimate in (
5.5
) yields

(5.34)

\displaystyle 1+\|\mathbf{u}^{\alpha}\|_{1,\infty}+\|\omega^{\alpha}\|_{2}\lesssim_{\alpha}1+\|\omega^{\alpha}\|_{2}

for
f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
.
However, if we test (
2.13
) with
\omega^{\alpha}
,
we also obtain

(5.35)

\displaystyle\frac{\,\mathrm{d}}{\,\mathrm{d}t}\|\omega^{\alpha}\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim\Big(\|\mathbf{u}^{\alpha}\|_{\infty}\|\omega^{\alpha}\|_{2}+\|\mathbf{u}_{h}\|_{\infty}\|\omega^{\alpha}\|_{2}\Big)\|\nabla b^{\alpha}\|_{2}\lesssim\Big(1+\|\omega^{\alpha}\|_{2}^{2}\Big)\|\nabla b^{\alpha}\|_{1,2}

for a constant depending only on
\|\mathbf{u}_{h}\|_{\infty}
and
\|f\|_{2}
. It therefore follow from (
5.30
) that

(5.36)

\displaystyle\|\omega^{\alpha}(t)\|_{2}^{2}\lesssim\big(1+\|\omega_{0}\|_{2}^{2}\big)\exp(cK),\qquad t<T_{\max}^{\alpha}.

Using the fact that
\ln\,g\geq 1
, we can conclude from (
5.34
) and (
5.36
) that

(5.37)

\displaystyle 1+\|\mathbf{u}^{\alpha}\|_{1,\infty}+\|\omega^{\alpha}\|_{2}\lesssim_{\alpha}\big(1+\|\omega_{0}\|_{2}^{2}\big)\exp(cK)\ln\,g.

If we now combine this estimate with (
5.33
), we can conclude from (
5.32
) that

\displaystyle\frac{\,\mathrm{d}}{\,\mathrm{d}t}g\lesssim\big(1+\|\omega_{0}\|_{2}+\|\nabla b^{\alpha}\|_{1,2}\big)\exp(cK)\,g\ln\,g

so that

\displaystyle g(t)\leq g(0)^{\exp\int_{0}^{t}c(1+\|\omega_{0}\|_{2}+\|\nabla b^{\alpha}(s)\|_{1,2})\exp(cK)\,\mathrm{d}s}

holds for
t<T_{\max}^{\alpha}
. In particular, given (
5.30
), we have shown that

(5.38)

\displaystyle\|(b^{\alpha},\omega^{\alpha})(t)\|_{\mathcal{M}}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\big[\mathrm{e}+\|(b^{\alpha}_{0},\omega^{\alpha}_{0})\|_{\mathcal{M}}^{2}\big]^{\exp(c(T_{\max}^{\alpha}+K)\exp(cK))}

\displaystyle\lesssim_{T_{\max}^{\alpha},K,f,\mathbf{u}_{h},\omega_{0},b_{0}}1

for all
t<{T_{\max}^{\alpha}}
contradicting (
5.29
).
∎

6.
Numerical implementation

For numerical implementation, we choose to work in weaker spaces than what the wellposedness theorem dictates. Additionally we allow for boundary conditions in the numerical setup. We recognise these choices create gaps between the theory and the implementation.

6.1.
Discretisation methods for
\alpha
–TQG and TQG

In this subsection, we describe the finite element (FEM) spatial discretisation, and finite difference Runge-Kutta time stepping discretisation methods that are utilised for the
\alpha
-TQG system.
By setting
\alpha
to zero we obtain our numerical setup for the TQG system.

Consider a bounded domain
{\mathcal{D}}
. Let
\partial{\mathcal{D}}
denote the boundary. We impose Dirichlet boundary conditions

(6.1)

\psi^{\alpha}=0,\ \Delta\psi^{\alpha}=0,\qquad\text{on }\partial{\mathcal{D}}.

For
{\mathcal{D}}=\mathbb{T}^{2}
, our numerical setup does not change, in which case the boundary flux terms in the discretised equations are set to zero.

6.1.1.
The stream function equation

Let
H^{1}\left({\mathcal{D}}\right)
denote the Sobolev
W^{1,2}\left({\mathcal{D}}\right)

space and let
\left\|.\right\|_{\partial{\mathcal{D}}}
denote
the
L^{2}\left(\partial{\mathcal{D}}\right)
norm.
Define the space

(6.2)

W^{1}\left({\mathcal{D}}\right):=\left\{\nu\in H^{1}\left({\mathcal{D}}\right)\left|\left\|\nu\right\|_{\partial{\mathcal{D}}}=0\right.\right\}.

We express (
2.14
) as two inhomogeneous Helmholtz equations

(6.3)

\displaystyle\omega^{\alpha}-f

\displaystyle=(\Delta-1)\tilde{\psi}

(6.4)

\displaystyle-\tilde{\psi}

\displaystyle=(\alpha\Delta-1)\psi^{\alpha}.

We take
\tilde{\psi},\psi^{\alpha}\in W^{1}({\mathcal{D}})
.
Since the two equations are of the same form, let us first consider (
6.3
).
Using an arbitrary test function
\phi\in W^{1}({\mathcal{D}})
, we obtain the following weak form of (
6.3
),

(6.5)

\displaystyle\langle\nabla\tilde{\psi},\nabla\phi\rangle_{\mathcal{D}}+\langle\tilde{\psi},\phi\rangle_{\mathcal{D}}

\displaystyle=-\langle\omega^{\alpha}-f,\phi\rangle_{\mathcal{D}}.

Define the functionals

(6.6)

\displaystyle L_{\alpha}(v,\phi)

\displaystyle:=\alpha\langle\nabla v,\nabla\phi\rangle_{\mathcal{D}}+\langle v,\phi\rangle_{\mathcal{D}}

(6.7)

\displaystyle F_{\cdot}(\phi)

\displaystyle:=-\langle\cdot,\phi\rangle_{\mathcal{D}}

for
v,\phi\in W^{1}({\mathcal{D}})
, then (
6.5
) can be written as

(6.8)

L_{1}(\tilde{\psi},\phi)=F_{\omega^{\alpha}-f^{\alpha}}(\phi).

And similarly for (
6.4
), we have

(6.9)

L_{\alpha}(\psi^{\alpha},\phi)=F_{-\tilde{\psi}}(\phi).

We discretise equations (
6.8
) and (
6.9
) using a continuous
Galerkin (CG) discretisation scheme.

Let
\delta
be the discretisation parameter, and let
{\mathcal{D}}_{\delta}
denote a space filling triangulation of the domain, that consists of geometry-conforming nonoverlapping elements.
Define the approximation space

(6.10)

W_{\delta}^{k}({\mathcal{D}}):=\left\{\phi_{\delta}\in W^{1}\left({\mathcal{D}}\right)\ :\ \phi_{\delta}\in C\left({\mathcal{D}}\right),\left.\phi_{\delta}\right|_{K}\in\Pi^{k}\left(K\right)\text{ each }K\in{\mathcal{D}}_{\delta}\right\}.

in which
C({\mathcal{D}})
is the space of continuous functions on
{\mathcal{D}}
, and
\Pi^{k}\left(K\right)
denotes the space of polynomials of degree at most
k
on element
K\in{\mathcal{D}}_{\delta}
.

For (
6.8
), given
f_{\delta}\in W_{\delta}^{k}({\mathcal{D}})
and
\omega_{\delta}\in V_{\delta}^{k}({\mathcal{D}})
(see (
6.13
) for the definition of
V_{\delta}^{k}({\mathcal{D}})
),
our numerical approximation is the solution
\tilde{\psi}_{\delta}\in W_{\delta}^{k}({\mathcal{D}})
that satisfies

(6.11)

L_{1}(\tilde{\psi}_{\delta},\phi_{\delta})=F_{\omega_{\delta}-f_{\delta}}(\phi_{\delta})

for all test functions
\phi_{\delta}\in W_{\delta}^{k}({\mathcal{D}})
. Then, using
\tilde{\psi}_{\delta}
, our numerical approximation of
\psi^{\alpha}
is the solution
\psi_{\delta}^{\alpha}\in W_{\delta}^{k}({\mathcal{D}})
that satisfies

(6.12)

L_{\alpha}(\psi_{\delta}^{\alpha},\phi_{\delta})=F_{-\tilde{\psi}_{\delta}}(\phi_{\delta})

for all test functions
\phi_{\delta}\in W_{\delta}^{k}({\mathcal{D}})
.

For a detailed exposition of the numerical algorithms that solves the discretised problems (
6.11
) and (
6.12
) we point the reader to
[
13
,
7
]
.

6.1.2.
Hyperbolic equations

We choose to discretise the hyperbolic buoyancy (
2.12
) and potential vorticity (
2.13
) equations using a discontinuous Galerkin (DG) scheme. For a detailed exposition of DG methods, we refer the interested reader to
[
14
]
.

Define the DG approximation space, denoted by
V_{\delta}^{k}({\mathcal{D}})
, to be the element-wise polynomial space,

(6.13)

V_{\delta}^{k}({\mathcal{D}})=\left\{\left.v_{\delta}\in L^{2}({\mathcal{D}})\right|\forall K\in{\mathcal{D}}_{\delta},\ \exists\phi_{\delta}\in\Pi^{k}(K):\ \left.v_{\delta}\right|_{K}=\left.\phi_{\delta}\right|_{K}\right\}.

We look to approximate
b^{\alpha}
and
\omega^{\alpha}
in the space
V_{\delta}^{k}({\mathcal{D}})
. Essentially, this means our approximations of
b^{\alpha}
and
\omega^{\alpha}
are each an direct sum over the elements in
{\mathcal{D}}_{\delta}
. Additional constraints on the numerical fluxes across shared element boundaries are needed to ensure conservation properties and stability.
Further, note that
W^{k}_{\delta}({\mathcal{D}})\subset V^{k}_{\delta}({\mathcal{D}})
. This inclusion is also needed for ensuring numerical conservation laws, see Section
6.1.3
.

For the buoyancy equation (
2.12
), we obtain the following variational formulation

(6.14)

\displaystyle\langle\partial_{t}b^{\alpha},\nu_{\delta}\rangle_{K}

\displaystyle=\langle b^{\alpha}{\bf u}^{\alpha},\nabla\nu_{\delta}\rangle_{K}-\langle b^{\alpha}{\bf u}^{\alpha}\cdot{\hat{\mathbf{n}}},\nu_{\delta}\rangle_{\partial K},\quad K\in{\mathcal{D}}_{\delta}

where
\nu_{\delta}\in V^{k}_{\delta}({\mathcal{D}})
is any test function,
\partial K
denotes the boundary of
K
, and
{\hat{\mathbf{n}}}
denotes the unit normal vector to
\partial K
. Let
b^{\alpha}_{\delta}
be the approximation of
b^{\alpha}
in
V_{\delta}^{k}
, and let
\mathbf{u}^{\alpha}_{\delta}=\nabla^{\perp}\psi^{\alpha}_{\delta}
for
\psi^{\alpha}_{\delta}\in W^{k}_{\delta}
. Our discretised buoyancy equation over each element is given by

(6.15)

\langle\partial_{t}b^{\alpha}_{\delta},\nu_{\delta}\rangle_{K}=\langle b^{\alpha}_{\delta}{\bf u}^{\alpha}_{\delta},\nabla\nu_{\delta}\rangle_{K}-\langle b^{\alpha}_{\delta}{\bf u}^{\alpha}_{\delta}\cdot{\hat{\mathbf{n}}},\nu_{\delta}\rangle_{\partial K},\quad K\in{\mathcal{D}}_{\delta}.

Similarly, let
\omega^{\alpha}_{\delta}\in V_{\delta}^{k}({\mathcal{D}})
be the approximation of
\omega^{\alpha}
, and let
h_{\delta}\in W_{\delta}^{k}
. We obtain the following discretised variational formulation that corresponds to (
2.13
),

\displaystyle\langle\partial_{t}\omega^{\alpha}_{\delta},\nu_{\delta}\rangle_{K}

\displaystyle=\langle(\omega^{\alpha}_{\delta}-b^{\alpha}_{\delta}){\bf u}^{\alpha}_{\delta},\nabla\nu_{\delta}\rangle_{K}-\langle(\omega^{\alpha}_{\delta}-b^{\alpha}_{\delta}){\bf u}^{\alpha}_{\delta}\cdot{\hat{\mathbf{n}}},\nu_{\delta}\rangle_{\partial K}

(6.16)

\displaystyle\qquad-\frac{1}{2}\langle\nabla\cdot(b^{\alpha}_{\delta}\nabla^{\perp}h_{\delta}),\nu_{\delta}\rangle_{K},\qquad K\in{\mathcal{D}}_{\delta},

for test function
\nu_{\delta}\in V_{\delta}^{k}({\mathcal{D}})
.

At this point, we only have the discretised problem on single elements. To obtain the global approximation, we sum over all the elements in
{\mathcal{D}}_{\delta}
. In doing so, the
\partial K
terms in (
6.15
) and (
6.16
) must be treated carefully.
Let
\partial K_{\text{ext}}
denote the part of cell boundary that is contained in
\partial{\mathcal{D}}
. Let
\partial K_{\text{int}}
denote the part of the cell boundary that is contained in the interior of the domain
{\mathcal{D}}\backslash\partial{\mathcal{D}}
.
On
\partial K_{\text{ext}}
we simply impose the PDE boundary conditions. However, on
\partial K_{\text{int}}
we need to consider the contribution from each of the neighbouring elements.
By choice, the approximant
\psi^{\alpha}_{\delta}\in W_{\delta}^{k}
is continuous on
\partial K
. And since

(6.17)

\mathbf{u}^{\alpha}_{\delta}\cdot{\hat{\mathbf{n}}}=\nabla^{\perp}\psi^{\alpha}_{\delta}\cdot{\hat{\mathbf{n}}}=-\nabla\psi^{\alpha}_{\delta}\cdot\hat{\tau}=-\frac{\,\mathrm{d}\psi^{\alpha}_{\delta}}{\,\mathrm{d}\hat{\tau}},

where
\hat{\tau}
denotes the unit tangential vector to
\partial K
,
\mathbf{u}^{\alpha}_{\delta}\cdot{\hat{\mathbf{n}}}
is also continuous. This means
\mathbf{u}^{\alpha}_{\delta}\cdot{\hat{\mathbf{n}}}
in (
6.15
) (also in (
6.16
)) is single valued. However, due to the lack of global continuity constraint in the definition of
V_{k}^{\alpha}({\mathcal{D}})
,
b^{\alpha}_{\delta}
and
\omega^{\alpha}_{\delta}
are multi-valued on
\partial K_{\text{int}}
. Thus, as our approximation of
\omega^{\alpha}
and
b^{\alpha}
over the whole domain is the sum over
K\in{\mathcal{D}}_{\delta}
, we have to constrain the flux on the set
\left(\bigcup_{K\in{\mathcal{D}}_{\delta}}\partial K\right)\setminus\partial{\mathcal{D}}
. This is done using appropriately chosen numerical flux fields in the boundary terms of (
6.15
) and (
6.16
).

Let

\nu^{-}:=\lim_{\epsilon\uparrow 0}\nu({\bf x}+\epsilon{\hat{\mathbf{n}}})

and

\nu^{+}:=\lim_{\epsilon\downarrow 0}\nu({\bf x}+\epsilon{\hat{\mathbf{n}}})
,
for
{\bf x}\in\partial K
, be the inside and outside (with respect to a fixed element
K
) values respectively, of a function
\nu
on the boundary.
Let
\hat{f}
be a
numerical flux
function
that satisfies the following properties:

(i)

consistency

(6.18)

\hat{f}(\nu,\nu,\mathbf{u}_{\delta}\cdot{\hat{\mathbf{n}}})=\nu\mathbf{u}_{\delta}\cdot{\hat{\mathbf{n}}}

(ii)

conservative

(6.19)

\hat{f}(\nu^{+},\nu^{-},\mathbf{u}_{\delta}\cdot{\hat{\mathbf{n}}})=-\hat{f}(\nu^{-},\nu^{+},-\mathbf{u}_{\delta}\cdot{\hat{\mathbf{n}}})

(iii)

L^{2}
stable in the enstrophy norm with respect to the buoyancy equation, see
[
4
, Section 6]
.

With such an
\hat{f}
, we replace

b^{\alpha}_{\delta}\mathbf{u}^{\alpha}_{\delta}\cdot{\hat{\mathbf{n}}}
by the numerical flux
\hat{f}(b_{\delta}^{\alpha,+},b_{\delta}^{\alpha,-},{\bf u}^{\alpha}_{\delta}\cdot{\hat{\mathbf{n}}})
in (
6.15
).
Similarly, in (
6.16
), we replace
(\omega^{\alpha}_{\delta}-b^{\alpha}_{\delta})\mathbf{u}_{\delta}^{\alpha}\cdot{\hat{\mathbf{n}}}
by
\hat{f}((\omega^{\alpha}_{\delta}-b^{\alpha}_{\delta})^{+},(\omega^{\alpha}_{\delta}-b^{\alpha}_{\delta})^{-},\mathbf{u}_{\delta}^{\alpha}\cdot{\hat{\mathbf{n}}})
.

Remark 6.1
.

For a general nonlinear conservation law, one has to solve what is called the
Riemann problem
for the numerical flux, see
[
14
]
for details.
In our setup, we use the following local Lax-Friedrichs flux, which is an approximate Riemann solver,

(6.20)

\hat{f}(\nu^{+},\nu^{-},\mathbf{u}_{\delta}\cdot{\hat{\mathbf{n}}})=\mathbf{u}_{\delta}\cdot{\hat{\mathbf{n}}}\{\{\nu\}\}-\frac{|\mathbf{u}_{\delta}\cdot{\hat{\mathbf{n}}}|}{2}\llbracket\nu\rrbracket

where

(6.21)

\{\{\nu\}\}:=\frac{1}{2}(\nu^{-}+\nu^{+}),\qquad\llbracket\nu\rrbracket:={\hat{\mathbf{n}}}^{-}\nu^{-}+{\hat{\mathbf{n}}}^{+}\nu^{+}.

Finally, our goal is to find
b^{\alpha}_{\delta},\omega^{\alpha}_{\delta}\in V_{\delta}^{k}({\mathcal{D}})
such that for all
\nu_{\delta}\in V_{\delta}^{k}({\mathcal{D}})
we have

(6.22)

\displaystyle\sum_{K\in{\mathcal{D}}_{\delta}}\langle\partial_{t}b^{\alpha}_{\delta},\nu_{\delta}\rangle_{K}

\displaystyle=\sum_{K\in{\mathcal{D}}_{\delta}}\big\{\langle b^{\alpha}_{\delta}\nabla^{\perp}\psi^{\alpha}_{\delta},\nabla\nu_{\delta}\rangle_{K}-\langle\hat{f}_{b^{\alpha}}(b_{\delta}^{\alpha,+},b_{\delta}^{\alpha,-},\nabla^{\perp}\psi^{\alpha}_{\delta}\cdot{\hat{\mathbf{n}}}),\nu_{\delta}^{-}\rangle_{\partial K}\big\},

(6.23)

\displaystyle\sum_{K\in{\mathcal{D}}_{\delta}}\langle\partial_{t}\omega^{\alpha}_{\delta},\nu_{\delta}\rangle_{K}

\displaystyle=\sum_{K\in{\mathcal{D}}_{\delta}}\big\{\langle(\omega^{\alpha}_{\delta}-b^{\alpha}_{\delta})\nabla^{\perp}\psi^{\alpha}_{\delta},\nabla\nu_{\delta}\rangle_{K}-\frac{1}{2}\langle\nabla\cdot(b^{\alpha}_{\delta}\nabla^{\perp}h_{\delta}),\nu_{\delta}\rangle_{K}

\displaystyle\qquad-\langle\hat{f}_{\omega^{\alpha}}((\omega^{\alpha}_{\delta}-b^{\alpha}_{\delta})^{+},(\omega^{\alpha}_{\delta}-b^{\alpha}_{\delta})^{-},\nabla^{\perp}\psi_{\delta}^{\alpha}\cdot{\hat{\mathbf{n}}}),\nu_{\delta}^{-}\rangle_{\partial K}\big\}

with
\psi_{\delta}^{\alpha}\in W_{\delta}^{k}({\mathcal{D}})
being the numerical approximation to the stream function.

Remark 6.2
.

In (
6.22
) and (
6.23
) we do not explicitly distinguish
\partial K_{\text{ext}}
and
\partial K_{\text{int}}
because for the boundary conditions (
6.1
), the
\partial K_{\text{ext}}
terms vanish.

6.1.3.
Numerical conservation

Conservation properties of the TQG system was first shown in
[
17
]
. More specifically, the TQG system conserves energy, and an infinite family of quantities called casimirs. Proposition
6.3
below describes the conservation properties of the
\alpha
–TQG system. We note that the form of the conserved energy and casimirs are the same as that of the TQG system.
Although the result is stated for the system with boundary conditions, it is easy to show that the same result holds for
\mathbb{T}^{2}
.

Proposition 6.3

(
\alpha
–TQG conserved quantities)
.

On a bounded domain
{\mathcal{D}}
with boundary
\partial{\mathcal{D}}
, consider the
\alpha
-TQG system (
2.12
) – (
2.14
)
with boundary conditions

(6.24)

{\hat{\mathbf{n}}}\cdot\mathbf{u}^{\alpha}=0,\ {\hat{\mathbf{n}}}\times\nabla b^{\alpha}=0,\ \frac{\,\mathrm{d}}{\,\mathrm{d}t}\int_{\partial{\mathcal{D}}}\nabla\psi^{\alpha}\cdot{\hat{\mathbf{n}}}=0,\ \frac{\,\mathrm{d}}{\,\mathrm{d}t}\int_{\partial{\mathcal{D}}}\nabla\Delta\psi^{\alpha}\cdot{\hat{\mathbf{n}}}=0,\ {\hat{\mathbf{n}}}\times\nabla\Delta\psi^{\alpha}=0

We have

(6.25)

E^{\alpha}(t):=-\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathcal{D}}\{(\omega^{\alpha}-f)\psi^{\alpha}+{h}b^{\alpha}\}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x},

and

(6.26)

C^{\alpha}_{\Psi,\Phi}(t):=\int_{\mathcal{D}}\{\Psi(b^{\alpha})+\omega^{\alpha}\Phi(b^{\alpha})\}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x},\qquad\forall\Psi,\Phi\in C^{\infty}

are conserved, i.e.
\,\mathrm{d}_{t}E^{\alpha}(t)=0
and
\,\mathrm{d}_{t}C^{\alpha}_{\Psi,\Phi}(t)=0
.

Proof.

For the energy
E^{\alpha}(t)
, we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{\,\mathrm{d}}{\,\mathrm{d}t}E^{\alpha}(t)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\frac{\,\mathrm{d}}{\,\mathrm{d}t}\int_{\mathcal{D}}\{\psi^{\alpha}(\Delta-1)(1-\alpha\Delta)\psi^{\alpha}+{h}b^{\alpha}\}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{D}}\{-\psi^{\alpha}\ \partial_{t}\omega^{\alpha}-\frac{1}{2}h\partial_{t}b^{\alpha}\}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}.

Substitute
\partial_{t}\omega^{\alpha}
and
\partial_{t}b^{\alpha}
using (
2.12
) and (
2.13
),
the result then follows from direct calculations.

Similarly for the casimirs, we obtain the result by directly evaluating
\,\mathrm{d}_{t}C^{\alpha}_{\Psi,\Phi}(t)
.
∎

Remark 6.4
.

In (
6.24
), except for the integral Neumann boundary conditions, we effectively have Dirichlet boundary conditions for
\psi^{\alpha}
,
b^{\alpha}
and
\Delta\psi^{\alpha}
. The integral Neumann boundary conditions can be viewed as imposing the Kelvin theorem on the boundary – consider

(6.27)

\frac{\,\mathrm{d}}{\,\mathrm{d}t}\int_{\partial{\mathcal{D}}}\nabla(\psi^{\alpha}-\alpha\Delta\psi^{\alpha})\cdot{\hat{\mathbf{n}}}=0,

and apply the divergence theorem.

In our FEM discretisation, we impose only Dirichlet boundary conditions.
If the integral Neumann boundary conditions are not imposed, then for Proposition
6.3
to hold, we necessarily require
\psi^{\alpha}
and
\Delta\psi^{\alpha}
on
\partial{\mathcal{D}}
to be
zero
.

We now analyse the conservation properties of our spatially discretised
\alpha
-TQG system.
Let
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{H^{1}({\mathcal{D}})}
be the
H^{1}({\mathcal{D}})
inner product.
Define the numerical total energy

(6.28)

E^{\alpha}_{\delta}(t)=\frac{1}{2}\langle\psi^{\alpha}_{\delta},\tilde{\psi}_{\delta}\rangle_{H^{1}({\mathcal{D}})}-\frac{1}{2}\langle{h}_{\delta},b^{\alpha}_{\delta}\rangle_{\mathcal{D}},

in which
\psi_{\delta}^{\alpha},\tilde{\psi}_{\delta}\in W_{\delta}^{\alpha}({\mathcal{D}})
and
b_{\delta}^{\alpha}\in V_{\delta}^{k}({\mathcal{D}})
are the numerical approximations of
\psi^{\alpha}
,
\tilde{\psi}
and
b^{\alpha}
respectively.
Define the numerical casimir functional

(6.29)

C_{\delta}^{\alpha}(t;\Psi,\Phi):=\langle\Psi(b_{\delta}^{\alpha}),1\rangle_{\mathcal{D}}+\langle\omega_{\delta}^{\alpha}\Phi(b_{\delta}^{\alpha})\rangle_{\mathcal{D}},\quad\Psi,\Phi\in C^{\infty}.

Lemma 6.5
.

With boundary conditions (
6.1
)

(6.30)

\frac{\,\mathrm{d}}{\,\mathrm{d}t}E^{\alpha}_{\delta}(t)=0

for
\alpha=0
.

In other words, the semi-discrete discretisation conserves energy in the TQG case.

Proof.

We consider
\,\mathrm{d}/\,\mathrm{d}_{t}
evaluated at an arbitrary fixed value
t_{0}
.
First note that when
\alpha=0
, we have
\psi^{\alpha}_{\delta}=\tilde{\psi}_{\delta}
. Then, from (
6.6
) we have

(6.31)

\displaystyle 2E_{\delta}^{\alpha}(t)

\displaystyle=L_{1}(\psi_{\delta}^{\alpha},{\psi}^{\alpha}_{\delta})-\langle{h}_{\delta},b_{\delta}^{\alpha}\rangle_{\Omega}

For the first term in (
6.31
), following (
6.11
) we have

(6.32)

\displaystyle\left.L_{1}(\partial_{t}\psi_{\delta},\phi_{\delta})\right|_{t=t_{0}}=-\sum_{K\in\mathcal{{\mathcal{D}}}_{\delta}}\left.\langle\partial_{t}\omega^{\alpha}_{\delta},\phi_{\delta}\rangle_{K}\right|_{t=t_{0}},\quad\forall\phi_{\delta}\in W_{\delta}^{k}({\mathcal{D}}).

Thus, substituting in the discretised equation (
6.23
) for
\omega^{\alpha}
, we obtain

\displaystyle-L_{1}(\left.\partial_{t}\psi_{\delta},\phi_{\delta})\right|_{t=t_{0}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{K\in{\mathcal{D}}_{\delta}}\left[\big\{\langle(\omega^{\alpha}_{\delta}-b^{\alpha}_{\delta})\nabla^{\perp}\psi^{\alpha}_{\delta},\nabla\phi_{\delta}\rangle_{K}-\frac{1}{2}\langle\nabla\cdot(b^{\alpha}_{\delta}\nabla^{\perp}h_{\delta}),\phi_{\delta}\rangle_{K}\right.

(6.33)

\displaystyle\qquad-\left.\langle\hat{f}_{\omega^{\alpha}}((\omega^{\alpha}_{\delta}-b^{\alpha}_{\delta})^{+},(\omega^{\alpha}_{\delta}-b^{\alpha}_{\delta})^{-},\nabla^{\perp}\psi_{\delta}^{\alpha}\cdot{\hat{\mathbf{n}}}),\phi_{\delta}^{-}\rangle_{\partial K}\big\}\right]_{t=t_{0}},

in which
we can choose
\phi_{\delta}=[\psi^{\alpha}_{\delta}]_{t=t_{0}}
. This choice is consistent with the discretisation space
V_{\delta}^{k}({\mathcal{D}})
for
\omega^{\alpha}
, since
W_{\delta}^{k}({\mathcal{D}})\subset V_{\delta}^{k}({\mathcal{D}})
.

Similarly, for the second term in (
6.31
), using (
6.22
) we obtain

(6.34)

\left.\frac{\,\mathrm{d}}{\,\mathrm{d}t}\right|_{t=t_{0}}\langle b^{\alpha}_{\delta},\nu_{\delta}\rangle_{\mathcal{D}}=\sum_{K\in{\mathcal{D}}_{\delta}}\left[\big\{\langle b^{\alpha}_{\delta}\nabla^{\perp}\psi^{\alpha}_{\delta},\nabla\nu_{\delta}\rangle_{K}-\langle\hat{f}_{b^{\alpha}}(b_{\delta}^{\alpha,+},b_{\delta}^{\alpha,-},\nabla^{\perp}\psi^{\alpha}_{\delta}\cdot{\hat{\mathbf{n}}}),\nu_{\delta}^{-}\rangle_{\partial K}\big\}\right]_{t=t_{0}},

where we can choose
\nu_{\delta}={h}_{\delta}
.

Putting together (
6.31
), (
6.33
) and (
6.34
), and substituting in
{h}_{\delta}
and
[\psi^{\alpha}_{\delta}]_{t=t_{0}}
for the arbitrary choices, we obtain

\displaystyle 2\frac{\,\mathrm{d}}{\,\mathrm{d}t}\Big|_{t=t_{0}}E^{\alpha}_{\delta}(t)

\displaystyle=-\sum_{K\in{\mathcal{D}}_{\delta}}\left[2\Big\{\langle(\omega^{\alpha}_{\delta}-b^{\alpha}_{\delta})\underbrace{\nabla^{\perp}\psi^{\alpha}_{\delta},\nabla\psi^{\alpha}_{\delta}}_{=0}\rangle_{K}-\frac{1}{2}\langle\nabla\cdot(b^{\alpha}_{\delta}\nabla^{\perp}h_{\delta}),\psi^{\alpha}_{\delta}\rangle_{K}\right.

\displaystyle\qquad-\left.\langle\hat{f}_{\omega^{\alpha}}((\omega^{\alpha}_{\delta}-b^{\alpha}_{\delta})^{+},(\omega^{\alpha}_{\delta}-b^{\alpha}_{\delta})^{-},\nabla^{\perp}\psi_{\delta}^{\alpha}\cdot{\hat{\mathbf{n}}}),{\psi^{\alpha}_{\delta}}^{-}\rangle_{\partial K}\Big\}\right.

(6.35)

\displaystyle+\left.\Big\{\underbrace{\langle b^{\alpha}_{\delta}\nabla^{\perp}\psi^{\alpha}_{\delta},\nabla{h}_{\delta}\rangle_{K}}_{=-\langle b^{\alpha}_{\delta}\nabla^{\perp}{h}_{\delta},\nabla\psi^{\alpha}_{\delta}\rangle_{K}}-\langle\hat{f}_{b^{\alpha}}(b_{\delta}^{\alpha,+},b_{\delta}^{\alpha,-},\nabla^{\perp}\psi^{\alpha}_{\delta}\cdot{\hat{\mathbf{n}}}),{h}_{\delta}^{-}\rangle_{\partial K}\Big\}\right]_{t=t_{0}}

\displaystyle\frac{\,\mathrm{d}}{\,\mathrm{d}t}E^{\alpha}_{\delta}(t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{K\in\mathcal{{\mathcal{D}}}_{\delta}}\Big[-\langle\hat{f}_{\omega^{\alpha}}((\omega^{\alpha}_{\delta}-b^{\alpha}_{\delta})^{+},(\omega_{\delta}^{\alpha}-b^{\alpha}_{\delta})^{-},\nabla^{\perp}\psi^{\alpha}_{\delta}\cdot{\hat{\mathbf{n}}}),{\psi^{\alpha}_{\delta}}^{-}\rangle_{\partial K}

(6.36)

\displaystyle\qquad-\frac{1}{2}\langle\hat{f}_{b^{\alpha}}({b^{\alpha}_{\delta}}^{+},{b^{\alpha}_{\delta}}^{-},\nabla^{\perp}\psi^{\alpha}_{\delta}\cdot{\hat{\mathbf{n}}}),{h}_{\delta}^{-}\rangle_{\partial K}\Big]_{t=t_{0}}

Since
\psi_{\delta}
and
{h}_{\delta}
are continuous across element boundaries, we have
\psi_{\delta}^{+}=\psi_{\delta}^{-}
and
{h}_{\delta}^{+}={h}_{\delta}^{-}
.
Thus, if the numerical fluxes
\hat{f}_{\omega^{\alpha}}
and
\hat{f}_{b^{\alpha}}
satisfy the conservative property (
6.19
), the sum of flux terms is zero.

Remark 6.6
.

In order for our semi-discrete discretisation to conserve numerical energy when
\alpha>0
, more regularity is required for the approximation space for
\psi^{\alpha}_{\delta}
. Additionally, the current scheme coupled with the time stepping algorithm do not conserve casimirs. For future work, we look to explore casimir conserving schemes for the model.

6.1.4.
Time stepping

To discretise the time derivative, we use the strong stability preserving Runge-Kutta of order 3 (SSPRK3) scheme, see
[
14
]
.
Writing the finite element spatial discretisation formally as

(6.37)

\partial_{t}b^{\alpha}_{\delta}={\rm f}_{\delta}(b^{\alpha}_{\delta})

where
{\rm f}_{\delta}
is the discretisation operator that follows from (
6.22
), and

(6.38)

\partial_{t}\omega={\rm g}_{\delta}(\omega,b)

where
{\rm g}_{\delta}
is the discretisation operator that follows from (
6.23
).
Let
b^{\alpha,n}_{\delta}
and
\omega^{\alpha,n}_{\delta}
denote the approximation of
b^{\alpha}_{\delta}
and
\omega^{\alpha}_{\delta}
at time step
t_{n}
.
The SSPRK3
time discretisation is as follows

(6.39a)

\displaystyle b^{(1)}

\displaystyle=b^{\alpha,n}_{\delta}+\Delta t\ {\rm f}_{\delta}(b^{\alpha,n}_{\delta})

(6.39b)

\displaystyle\omega^{(1)}

\displaystyle=\omega^{\alpha,n}_{\delta}+\Delta t\ {\rm g}_{\delta}\left(\omega^{\alpha,n}_{\delta},b^{\alpha,n}_{\delta}\right)

(6.39c)

\displaystyle b^{\left(2\right)}

\displaystyle=\frac{3}{4}b^{\alpha,n}_{\delta}+\frac{1}{4}\left(b^{\left(1\right)}+\Delta t\ {\rm f}_{\delta}\left(b^{\left(1\right)}\right)\right)

(6.39d)

\displaystyle\omega^{\left(2\right)}

\displaystyle=\frac{3}{4}\omega^{\alpha,n}_{\delta}+\frac{1}{4}\left(\omega^{\left(1\right)}+\Delta t\ {\rm g}_{\delta}\left(\omega^{\left(1\right)},b^{(1)}\right)\right)

(6.39e)

\displaystyle b^{\alpha,n+1}_{\delta}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{3}b^{\alpha,n}_{\delta}+\frac{2}{3}\left(b^{\left(2\right)}+\Delta t\ {\rm f}_{\delta}\left(b^{\left(2\right)}\right)\right)

(6.39f)

\displaystyle\omega^{\alpha,n+1}_{\delta}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{3}\omega^{\alpha,n}_{\delta}+\frac{2}{3}\left(\omega^{\left(2\right)}+\Delta t\ {\rm g}_{\delta}\left(\omega^{\left(2\right)},b^{(2)}\right)\right)

where
\Delta t=t_{n+1}-t_{n}
each
n
.

6.2.
\alpha
-TQG linear thermal Rossby wave stability analysis

A dispersion relation for the TQG system was derived in
[
17
]
. There, the authors showed that the linear thermal Rossby waves of the TQG system possess high wavenumber instabilities. More specifically, the Doppler-shifted phase speed of these waves becomes complex at sufficiently high wavenumbers. However, the growth rate of the instability decreases to zero as
|{\bf k}|^{-1}
, in the limit that
|{\bf k}|\to\infty
. Consequently, the TQG dynamics is linearly well-posed. That is, the TQG solution depends continuously on initial conditions.
In this subsection, using the same equilibrium state as in
[
17
]
, we derive a dispersion relation for the thermal Rossby wave solutions of the linearised
\alpha
-TQG system. Again the Doppler-shifted phase speed of these waves becomes complex at sufficiently high wavenumbers. However, in this case, the growth rate of the instability decreases to zero as
|{\bf k}|^{-2}
, in the limit that
|{\bf k}|\to\infty
. In the limit that
\alpha
tends to zero, one recovers the TQG dispersion relation derived in
[
17
]
.

For the reader’s convenience, we repeat the equations solved by the pair of variables
(b^{\alpha},\omega^{\alpha})
in the
\alpha
-TQG system in (
2.12
)–(
2.13
), as formulated now in vorticity-streamfunction form with fluid velocity given by
{\mathbf{u}}^{\alpha}=\nabla^{\perp}\psi^{\alpha}
. This formulation is given by

(6.40)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}b^{\alpha}+J(\psi^{\alpha},b^{\alpha})

\displaystyle=0,

(6.41)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\omega^{\alpha}+J(\psi^{\alpha},\omega^{\alpha}-b^{\alpha})

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}J(h,b^{\alpha}),

(6.42)

\displaystyle\omega^{\alpha}

\displaystyle=(\Delta-1)(1-\alpha\Delta)\psi^{\alpha}+f.

Here, as before,
J(a,b)=\nabla^{\perp}a\cdot\nabla b=a_{x}b_{y}-b_{x}a_{y}
denotes the Jacobian of any smooth functions
a
and
b
defined on the
(x,y)
plane.

Equilibrium states of the
\alpha
-TQG system in the equations (
6.40
)-(
6.42
) satisfy
J(\psi^{\alpha}_{e},\omega^{\alpha}_{e})=J(\psi^{\alpha}_{e},b^{\alpha}_{e})=J(h,b^{\alpha}_{e})=0.

The equilibrium TQG state considered in
[
17
]
was given via the specification of the following
gradient fields

\begin{array}[]{c}\nabla\psi^{\alpha}_{e}=-U{\hat{\mathbf{y}}},\ \nabla\omega^{\alpha}_{e}=(U-\beta){\hat{\mathbf{y}}},\ \nabla f=-\beta{\hat{\mathbf{y}}}\\
\\
\nabla b^{\alpha}_{e}=-B{\hat{\mathbf{y}}},\ \nabla{h}=-H{\hat{\mathbf{y}}}\end{array}

where we have taken
f=1-\beta y
and the equilibrium parameters
U,\beta,B,H\in{\mathbb{R}}
are all constants.
Linearising the
\alpha
-TQG system around the steady state produces the
following evolution equations for the perturbations
\omega^{\prime}
,
b^{\prime}

and
{\psi}^{\prime}

(6.43a)

\displaystyle\omega_{t}^{\prime}+U\omega^{\prime}_{x}+(U+B-\beta){\psi}_{x}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=(U-H/2)b^{\prime}_{x}

(6.43b)

\displaystyle b^{\prime}_{t}+Ub_{x}^{\prime}-B{\psi}_{x}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=0

(6.43c)

\displaystyle\Big((1-\alpha\Delta)(1-\Delta)\Big){\psi}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=-\,\omega^{\prime}.

Since these equations are linear with constant coefficients, one has
the plane wave solutions

\omega^{\prime}=e^{i({\bf k}\cdot\mathbf{x}-\nu t)}\hat{\omega},\quad b^{\prime}=e^{i({\bf k}\cdot\mathbf{x}-\nu t)}\hat{b},\quad{\psi}^{\prime}=e^{i({\bf k}\cdot\mathbf{x}-\nu t)}\hat{{\psi}}.

Further, from (
6.43c
) we obtain

-\,\hat{\omega}=(|{\bf k}|^{2}+1)(\alpha|{\bf k}|^{2}+1)\hat{{\psi}}.

Substituting these solutions into the linearised equations we have

\displaystyle(\nu-kU)\hat{\omega}-k(U+B-\beta)\hat{{\psi}}

\displaystyle=-k(U-H/2)\hat{b}

\displaystyle\left(\nu-kU\right)\hat{b}

\displaystyle=-kB\hat{{\psi}}

where
k
is the first component of
{\bf k}
.
From the above we obtain a quadratic formula for the Doppler-shifted phase speed
C=C(\alpha):=\left(\nu(\alpha)-kU\right)/k

C^{2}(|{\bf k}|^{2}+1)(\alpha|{\bf k}|^{2}+1)+CX+Y=0.

where
X:=U+B-\beta

and
Y:=(U-H/2)B
. Thus, the dispersion relation for the thermal Rossby wave for TQG possesses two branches,
corresponding to two different phase velocities,

(6.44)

C(\alpha)=\frac{-X\pm\sqrt{X^{2}-4Y(|{\bf k}|^{2}+1)(\alpha|{\bf k}|^{2}+1)}}{2(|{\bf k}|^{2}+1)(\alpha|{\bf k}|^{2}+1)}.

Upon setting
\alpha=0
in (
6.44
), we recover the Doppler-shifted phase speed of thermal Rossby waves for the TQG system in
[
17
]
.

When
\alpha=0
,
B=0
, and
U=0
in (
6.44
), the remaining dispersion relation differs slightly from the dispersion relation for QG Rossby waves in non-dimensional variables. This is because the Casimirs in the Hamiltonian formulation of the TQG system differ from the those of standard QG.

As discussed in
[
17
]
for the TQG system, when
Y>0
, the Doppler shifted phase velocity in (
6.44
) for the linearised wave motion becomes complex at high wavenumber
|{\bf k}|\gg 1
; namely for
(|{\bf k}|^{2}+1)(\alpha|{\bf k}|^{2}+1)\geq X^{2}/(4Y)
, for both branches of the dispersion relation. However, the growth-rate of the instability found from the imaginary part of
C(\alpha)
in (
6.44
) decays to zero as
O(|{\bf k}|^{-2})
, for
|{\bf k}|\gg 1
. Indeed, the linear stability analysis leading to
C(\alpha)
predicts that the maximum growth rate occurs at a finite wavenumber
|{\bf k}|_{\max}
which depends on the value of
\alpha
, beyond which the growth rate of the linearised TQG wave amplitude falls rapidly to zero. One would expect that simulated solutions of
\alpha
-TQG would be most active at the length-scale corresponding to
|{\bf k}|_{\max}
, at which the linearised thermal Rossby waves are the most unstable. If this maximum activity length-scale is near the grid truncation size, then numerical truncation errors could cause additional numerical stability issues! We have experience such problems during our testing of the numerical algorithm for the TQG system. Even for equilibrium solution “sanity” check tests, we have found that unless the time step was taken to be incredibly small, high wavenumber truncation errors have caused our numerical solutions to eventually blow up.

Figure 3
.
An example of the growth-rate of linearised TQG waves as determined from the imaginary part of
C(\alpha)
in equation (
6.44
), plotted for different values of
\alpha
. We observe that increasing
\alpha
shifts the wavenumber at the maximum growth-rate to lower values.

The magnitude of
\alpha
controls the unstable growth rate of linearised TQG waves at asymptotically high wavenumbers. That is, the presence of
\alpha
regularises the wave activity at high wavenumbers, see Figure
3
. Thus, from the perspective of numerics, the
\alpha
regularisation is available to control numerical problems that may arise from the inherent model instability at high wavenumbers, without the need for additional dissipation terms in the equation.

7.
Conclusion and outlook

In conclusion, we have formulated the thermal quasi-geostrophic (TQG) model equations in the regime of approximations relevant to GFD and we have explored their analytical and numerical properties. In respect to the analytical properties, we have shown that the TQG model and its
\alpha
-regularized version, the
\alpha
-TQG model, admit unique local strong solutions that are stable in a larger space with weaker norm, that both models have a maximum time of existence, solutions of the latter model converge to solutions of the former model on any time interval in which a solution to the former lives, and we have also determined conditions under which the solution of the
\alpha
-TQG will blow up.

With respect to numerics, we described our discretisation methods for approximating TQG and
\alpha
-TQG solutions and showed that the FEM semi-discrete scheme conserves numerical energy in the TQG case.
Using example simulation results, we numerically verified that
\alpha
-TQG solutions converge to that of the TQG system and attains
the order
1
in
\alpha
convergence rate predicted by
Proposition
4.1
.
Additionally, we derived a dispersion relation for the linearised
\alpha
-TQG system and showed how
\alpha
-regularisation could be used to control the development of high wavenumber instabilites.
Given that we have convergence of
\alpha
-TQG solutions to TQG, the linear stability results can be viewed as generalisations of those shown in
[
17
]
for the TQG system.

We now end this section with some open problems.

•

Can one construct a global-in-time strong solution (or a global weak solution) of either the TQG model equations (
1.1
)–(
1.5
), or the
\alpha
-TQG model model equations (
2.12
)–(
2.14
)?

•

Can one give a Beale–Kato–Majda condition for the blowup of a strong solution to the TQG in terms of a single unknown? That is, is there a BKM condition in terms of either
b
or
\omega
(or
\mathbf{u}
) that does not require a combination of both variables?

•

As discussed in Section
1.3
, an application of the Stochastic Advection by Lie Transport (SALT) approach to the Hamiltonian formulation of the TQG equations results in a stochastic Hamiltonian formulation of TQG equations in (
1.6
)-(
1.8
). This stochastic version of TQG preserves an infinite family of integral conserved quantities, as is shown in
[
17
]
. The SALT version of TQG represents an outstanding challenge for uncertainty quantification and data assimilation which will surely spur us on to further investigations of these problems.

•

As also discussed in Section
1.3
, the parallels between TQG and the Rayleigh-Bénard equations should also attract our attention for the consideration of a SALT version of the deterministic Rayleigh-Bénard convection equations.
