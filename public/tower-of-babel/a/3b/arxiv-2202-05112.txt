Probabilistic learning inference of boundary value problem with uncertainties based on Kullback-Leibler divergence under implicit constraints Journal: ArXiv
C. Soize

Abstract
In a first part, we present a mathematical analysis of a general methodology of a probabilistic learning inference that allows for estimating a posterior probability model for a stochastic boundary value problem from a prior probability model. The given targets are statistical moments for which the underlying realizations are not available. Under these conditions, the Kullback-Leibler divergence minimum principle is used for estimating the posterior probability measure. A statistical surrogate model of the implicit mapping, which represents the constraints, is introduced. The MCMC generator and the necessary numerical elements are given to facilitate the implementation of the methodology in a parallel computing framework. In a second part, an application is presented to illustrate the proposed theory and is also, as such, a contribution to the three-dimensional stochastic homogenization of heterogeneous linear elastic media in the case of a non-separation of the microscale and macroscale. For the construction of the posterior probability measure by using the probabilistic learning inference, in addition to the constraints defined by given statistical moments of the random effective elasticity tensor, the second-order moment of the random normalized residue of the stochastic partial differential equation has been added as a constraint. This constraint guarantees that the algorithm seeks to bring the statistical moments closer to their targets while preserving a small residue.

Introduction

We consider a boundary value problem (BVP) with control parameters, for which the observations (quantities of interest) are defined by a given transformation of the solution of the BVP. The graph of the functional dependence between the parameters and the observations defines a manifold that is not explicitly described. We consider the statistical inverse problem consisting in identifying the parameters by giving targets for the observations. A prior probabilistic model of the control parameters is introduced what induces random observations. A training set, constituted of independent realizations belonging to the manifold, is constructed by solving the BVP. The high computational numerical cost to solve the stochastic BVP is a limitation requiring that only a small number of points can be computed for constructing the training set.

We consider a probabilistic learning inference for which the prior probability measure on the manifold is estimated with the points of the training set. The posterior probability model on the manifold is constructed with respect to some statistical moments related to the observations. These statistical moments are expressed with an implicit function
{\bm{h}}^{c}
for which an algebraic representation is not available. The underlying realizations that have been used for estimating the targets of the statistical moments (such as those coming from experiments) are unknown (and consequently, are not available).

Setting the problem and summarizing the methodology

In this paper, all the random variables are defined on a probability space
(\Theta,{\mathcal{T}},{\mathcal{P}})
in which
\Theta
is the sample set,
{\mathcal{T}}
is the
\sigma
-field of
\Theta
, and where
{\mathcal{P}}
is a probability measure on the measurable space
(\Theta,{\mathcal{T}})
. A "sample"
{\bm{X}}(\theta),\theta\in\Theta

{\bm{X}}
defined on

2.1
Framework of the developments presented in the paper

For instance, we consider a stochastic elliptic BVP on an open bounded domain
\Omega\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
with
d\geq 1
, whose partial differential equation (PDE) is written as
{\bm{\mathcal{N}}}({\bm{Y}},{\bm{G}},{\bm{W}})={\hbox{\bf 0}}\,\,a.s
. The unknown is the non-Gaussian vector-valued field
\{{\bm{Y}}({\bm{\xi}}),{\bm{\xi}}\in\Omega\}
defined on
(\Theta,{\mathcal{T}},{\mathcal{P}})
and which satisfies the boundary conditions.
The coefficients of the stochastic elliptic operator depend on a non-Gaussian second-order vector-valued random field
{\bm{G}}
defined on
(\Theta,{\mathcal{T}},{\mathcal{P}})
and on a random vector-valued control parameter
{\bm{W}}
also defined on
(\Theta,{\mathcal{T}},{\mathcal{P}})
. It is assumed that the weak formulation of this stochastic BVP admits a unique strong stochastic solution
{\bm{Y}}={\bm{f}}({\bm{G}},{\bm{W}})
, which is a second-order random field. The observation (quantity of interest) is, for instance, a second-order vector-valued random variable
{\bm{Q}}={\bm{\mathcal{O}}}({\bm{Y}},{\bm{G}},{\bm{W}})
in which
{\bm{\mathcal{O}}}
is a given measurable mapping.

The problem under consideration belongs to the class of the statistical inverse problems. A prior probability model of
\{{\bm{G}},{\bm{W}}\}
is given and we are interested in estimating a posterior model
\{{\bm{G}}^{c},{\bm{W}}^{c}\}
of
\{{\bm{G}},{\bm{W}}\}
in order that some statistical moments of the posterior observations
{\bm{Q}}^{c}={\bm{\mathcal{O}}}({\bm{Y}}^{c},{\bm{G}}^{c},{\bm{W}}^{c})
with
{\bm{Y}}^{c}={\bm{f}}({\bm{G}}^{c},{\bm{W}}^{c})
, are equal to some given targets
(the superscript "
c
" is introduced to designate the solution with the constraints, which corresponds to the posterior model). The statistical moments of
{\bm{Q}}^{c}
are globally written as

E\{{\bm{\mathcal{M}}}^{c}({\bm{Q}}^{c})\}={\bm{b}}^{c}
in which
{\bm{b}}^{c}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n_{c}}
is the target,
{\bm{q}}\mapsto{\bm{\mathcal{M}}}^{c}({\bm{q}})
is a given measurable mapping, and
E
is the mathematical expectation operator. As explained in Section
1
, the realizations that have allowed
{\bm{b}}^{c}
to be estimated are not available. Only
{\bm{b}}^{c}
is known (it can be the case when one or several components of
{\bm{b}}^{c}
have been estimated with experimental realizations that are not available). However, as we will see in Section
5
, a component of the vector of statistical moments can also be related to the random normalized residue of the PDE of the stochastic boundary value problem.

The

{\bm{q}}_{d}^{j}={\bm{\mathcal{O}}}({\bm{y}}_{d}^{j},{\bm{g}}_{d}^{j},{\bm{w}}_{d}^{j})

The training set is then made up of a small number

(ii) Statistical inverse problem
. The available information consists of the targets of statistical moments related to observation

(iii) Finite reduced-order representation
. The second-order random variable

, is assumed to be with values in a real Hilbert space

and its associated norm

for which the square of the associated norm is

Since the problem is in infinite dimension, in order to implement the probabilistic learning inference that we propose, we need to introduce a finite representation

]

can be represented using the truncated Karhunen-Loève expansion

]
of

{\bm{X}}^{(\nu)}=\underline{{\bm{x}}}+\sum_{\alpha=1}^{\nu}\sqrt{\kappa_{\alpha}}\,{\bm{\varphi}}^{\alpha}\,H_{\alpha}\,,

(2.1)

in which the eigenvalues of the covariance operator are

with

,
where the family of the eigenfunctions

is a Hilbertian basis of

, where

, and where

is a second-order, centered,

-valued random variable whose covariance matrix is the identity matrix

is not positive definite but only positive (its kernel is not reduced to zero) and therefore, there is a zero eigenvalue with a finite multiplicity, which is not taken into account in the truncated representation of

For

2.3
Formulation using the Kullback-Leibler divergence minimum principle

Taking into account Section
2.2
-(ii), we use the Kullback-Leibler divergence minimum principle
[
17
,
18
,
19
]
for estimating the posterior probability measure
P_{{\bm{H}}^{c}}(d{\bm{\eta}})=p_{{\bm{H}}^{c}}({\bm{\eta}})\,d{\bm{\eta}}
on
{\mathbb{R}}^{\nu}
of the
{\mathbb{R}}^{\nu}
-valued random variable

. This estimation of

is performed (1) using the prior probability measure

on
{\mathbb{R}}^{\nu}
in which

is estimated with the Gaussian KDE method using the points
\{{\bm{\eta}}_{d}^{1},\ldots,{\bm{\eta}}_{d}^{N_{d}}\}
of the training set

E\{{\bm{h}}^{c}({\bm{H}}^{c})\}={\bm{b}}^{c}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n_{c}}\,.

(2.2)

In Eq. (
2.2
),

is a function from
{\mathbb{R}}^{\nu}
into
{\mathbb{R}}^{n_{c}}
with

, such that

is a random variable equal to

that is expressed as a function of
{\bm{H}}^{c}
.
We recall that the Kullback-Leibler divergence between two probability measures

and

on
{\mathbb{R}}^{\nu}
is defined by

D(p,p_{\bm{H}})=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{\nu}}p({\bm{\eta}})\,\log(p({\bm{\eta}})/p_{\bm{H}}({\bm{\eta}}))\,d{\bm{\eta}}
,
and is such that

(that can be proven by applying the Jensen inequality
[
67
,
68
]
)
and

if and only if

. Note that

is not a distance because the symmetry property and the triangle inequality are not verified. It can easily be seen that the cross entropy

S(p,p_{\bm{H}})=-\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{\nu}}p({\bm{\eta}})\,\log(p_{\bm{H}}({\bm{\eta}}))\,d{\bm{\eta}}

are related to

by

S(p,p_{\bm{H}})=S(p)+D(p,p_{\bm{H}})

[

]
) that
(p,p_{\bm{H}})\mapsto D(p,p_{\bm{H}})
is a convex function in the pair
(p,p_{\bm{H}})
.
The probability density function
p_{{\bm{H}}^{c}}
on
{\mathbb{R}}^{\nu}
, which satisfies the constraint defined by Eq. (
2.2
) and which is closest to
p_{\bm{H}}
, is the solution of the optimization problem (see for instance
[
18
,
19
,
25
]
),

p_{{\bm{H}}^{c}}=\arg\,\min_{p\in{\mathcal{C}}_{{\hbox{{\pppppcarac ad}}},p}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{\nu}}p({\bm{\eta}})\,\log\left(\frac{p({\bm{\eta}})}{p_{\bm{H}}({\bm{\eta}})}\right)\,d{\bm{\eta}}\,,

(2.3)

in which the admissible set
{\mathcal{C}}_{{\hbox{{\ppppcarac ad}}},p}
is defined by

{\mathcal{C}}_{{\hbox{{\ppppcarac ad}}},p}=\left\{{\bm{\eta}}\mapsto p({\bm{\eta}}):{\mathbb{R}}^{\nu}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}^{+}\,,\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{\nu}}p({\bm{\eta}})\,d{\bm{\eta}}=1\,,\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{\nu}}\!{\bm{h}}^{c}({\bm{\eta}})\,p({\bm{\eta}})\,d{\bm{\eta}}={\bm{b}}^{c}\right\}\,.

(2.4)

It should be noted that, since we are interested in divergence (from
p_{\bm{H}}({\bm{\eta}})\,d{\bm{\eta}}
) of probability measure

p({\bm{\eta}})\,d{\bm{\eta}}
that satisfies the constraints expressed in
{\mathcal{C}}_{{\hbox{{\ppppcarac ad}}},p}
, we are in fact interested in the divergence and therefore, the symmetry condition is irrelevant in this case.

2.4
Methodology used for solving the optimization problem and MCMC generator

The constraints, which are defined in admissible set
{\mathcal{C}}_{{\hbox{{\ppppcarac ad}}},p}
, are taken into account by introducing the Lagrange multipliers
\lambda_{0}-1
with
\lambda_{0}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{+}
, which is associated with the normalization condition,
and
{\bm{\lambda}}\in{\mathcal{C}}_{{\hbox{{\ppppcarac ad}}},{\bm{\lambda}}}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{n_{c}}
, which is associated with the moments constraints. The admissible set

{\mathcal{C}}_{{\hbox{{\ppppcarac ad}}},{\bm{\lambda}}}
of
{\bm{\lambda}}
is a subset of
{\mathbb{R}}^{n_{c}}
, which is completely defined by Definition
2
in Section
3
.
The Lagrange multiplier
\lambda_{0}
is eliminated as a function of
{\bm{\lambda}}
. In Eq. (
2.3
), the posterior pdf
p_{{\bm{H}}^{c}}
is constructed as the limit of a sequence
\{p_{{\bm{H}}_{\bm{\lambda}}}\}_{\bm{\lambda}}
of probability density functions of a
{\mathbb{R}}^{\nu}
-valued random variable

{\bm{H}}_{\bm{\lambda}}=(H_{{\bm{\lambda}},1},\ldots,H_{{\bm{\lambda}},\nu})
that depends on
{\bm{\lambda}}
. The construction of
\{p_{{\bm{H}}_{\bm{\lambda}}}\}_{\bm{\lambda}}
requires to generate with a MCMC algorithm a constrained learned set
{\mathcal{D}}_{{\bm{H}}_{\bm{\lambda}}}=\{{\bm{\eta}}^{1}_{\bm{\lambda}},\ldots{\bm{\eta}}^{N}_{\bm{\lambda}}\}
constituted of
N\gg N_{d}
independent realizations
\{{\bm{\eta}}_{\bm{\lambda}}^{\ell},\ell=1,\ldots,N\}
of
{\bm{H}}_{\bm{\lambda}}
. When the convergence is reached with respect to
{\bm{\lambda}}
, the constrained learned set
{\mathcal{D}}_{{\bm{H}}^{c}}=\{{\bm{\eta}}_{c}^{1},\ldots,{\bm{\eta}}_{c}^{N}\}
is generated. This set is made up of
N
independent realizations
\{{\bm{\eta}}_{c}^{\ell},\ell=1,\ldots,N\}
of
{\bm{H}}^{c}
whose probability measure is
p_{{\bm{H}}^{c}}({\bm{\eta}})\,d{\bm{\eta}}
(subscript or superscript "
c
" is introduced to designate the quantities related to the constrained learned set (posterior model)). For
{\bm{\lambda}}
fixed in
{\mathcal{C}}_{{\hbox{{\ppppcarac ad}}},{\bm{\lambda}}}
,
{\mathcal{D}}_{{\bm{H}}_{\bm{\lambda}}}
is generated using a MCMC generator based on a nonlinear Itô stochastic differential equation (ISDE) associated with the nonlinear stochastic dissipative Hamiltonian dynamical system proposed in
[
69
]
and based on
[
70
]
. This MCMC generator allows for deleting the transient part to rapidly reach the stationary response associated with the invariant measure for which measure
p_{{\bm{H}}^{c}}({\bm{\eta}})\,d{\bm{\eta}}
is a marginal measure of this invariant measure. The ISDE is solved by using the Störmer-Verlet algorithm, which yields an efficient and accurate MCMC algorithm. This algorithm can then easily be parallelized for strongly decreasing the elapsed time on a multicore computer.

2.5
Formal construction of the optimal solution using the sequence
\{p_{{\bm{H}}_{\bm{\lambda}}}\}_{\bm{\lambda}}

Let us assumed that the optimization problem defined by Eq. (
2.3
) has almost one solution
p_{{\bm{H}}^{c}}
and that
p=p_{{\bm{H}}^{c}}
is a regular point of the continuously differentiable functional
p\mapsto\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{\nu}}{\bm{h}}^{c}({\bm{\eta}})\,p({\bm{\eta}})\,d{\bm{\eta}}-{\bm{b}}^{c}
. For
\lambda_{0}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{+}
and
{\bm{\lambda}}\in{\mathcal{C}}_{{\hbox{{\ppppcarac ad}}},{\bm{\lambda}}}
, we define the Lagrangian,

{\mathcal{L}}ag(p,\lambda_{0},{\bm{\lambda}})=\!\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{\nu}}p({\bm{\eta}})\,\log\left(\frac{p({\bm{\eta}})}{p_{\bm{H}}({\bm{\eta}})}\right)d{\bm{\eta}}+(\lambda_{0}-1)\,(\!\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{\nu}}\!p({\bm{\eta}})\,d{\bm{\eta}}-1)+\langle{\bm{\lambda}}\,,\!\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{\nu}}\!{\bm{h}}^{c}({\bm{\eta}})\,p({\bm{\eta}})\,d{\bm{\eta}}-{\bm{b}}^{c}\rangle\,.

For all
{\bm{\eta}}
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{\nu}
, the pdf of
{\bm{H}}
is written as

p_{\bm{H}}({\bm{\eta}})=c_{\nu}\,\zeta({\bm{\eta}})

in which the positive-valued function
\zeta
is integrable on
{\mathbb{R}}^{\nu}
, is such that
\hbox{supp}\,\zeta={\mathbb{R}}^{\nu}
, and where
the positive constant
c_{\nu}
is such that
c_{\nu}^{-1}=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{\nu}}\zeta({\bm{\eta}})\,d{\bm{\eta}}
. We define the sequence
\{p_{{\bm{H}}_{\bm{\lambda}}}\}_{\bm{\lambda}}
of pdf
{\bm{\eta}}\mapsto p_{{\bm{H}}_{\bm{\lambda}}}({\bm{\eta}}\,;{\bm{\lambda}})
on
{\mathbb{R}}^{\nu}
, indexed by
{\bm{\lambda}}
, such that
p_{{\bm{H}}_{\bm{\lambda}}}(.\,;{\bm{\lambda}})
is an extremum of functional
p\mapsto{\mathcal{L}}ag(p,\lambda_{0},{\bm{\lambda}})
. Using the calculus of variations yields

p_{{\bm{H}}_{\bm{\lambda}}}({\bm{\eta}}\,;{\bm{\lambda}})=c_{0}({\bm{\lambda}})\,\zeta({\bm{\eta}})\,\exp\{-\langle{\bm{\lambda}}\,,{\bm{h}}^{c}({\bm{\eta}})\rangle\}\quad,\quad\forall\,{\bm{\eta}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{\nu}\,,

(2.5)

in which
c_{0}({\bm{\lambda}})
is the constant of normalization that depends on
{\bm{\lambda}}
(note that
\lambda_{0}
is eliminated and we have
c_{0}({\bm{\lambda}})=c_{\nu}\,\exp\{-\lambda_{0}\}
). The existence of a unique solution requires that the constraints be algebraically independent in the following sense: given any bounded positive measure
P(d{\bm{\eta}})
on
{\mathbb{R}}^{\nu}
with support
{\mathbb{R}}^{\nu}
, there exists a bounded set
{\mathcal{B}}
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{\nu}
with

such that

\forall\,{\bm{v}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n_{c}}\,\,,\,\,\|\,{\bm{v}}\,\|\neq 0\,\,,\,\,\int_{\mathcal{B}}\langle{\bm{h}}^{c}({\bm{\eta}})\,,{\bm{v}}\rangle^{2}P(d{\bm{\eta}})>0\,.

(2.6)

Under the condition defined by Eq. (
2.6
), there exists (see
[
71
]
)

{\bm{\lambda}}^{\hbox{{\ppppcarac sol}}}
in
{\mathcal{C}}_{ad,{\bm{\lambda}}}
such that the functional
(p,\lambda_{0},{\bm{\lambda}})\mapsto{\mathcal{L}}ag(p,\lambda_{0},{\bm{\lambda}})

is stationary at point
p=p_{{\bm{H}}^{c}}
for
{\bm{\lambda}}={\bm{\lambda}}^{\hbox{{\ppppcarac sol}}}
and
\lambda_{0}=-\log(c_{0}({\bm{\lambda}}^{\hbox{{\ppppcarac sol}}})/c_{\nu})
.
Consequently,
p_{{\bm{H}}^{c}}=p_{{\bm{H}}_{\!{\bm{\lambda}}^{\,{\hbox{{\ppppppcarac sol}}}}}}(.\,;{\bm{\lambda}}^{\hbox{{\ppppcarac sol}}})
and Eq. (
2.5
) yield

p_{{\bm{H}}^{c}}({\bm{\eta}})=c_{0}({\bm{\lambda}}^{\hbox{{\ppppcarac sol}}})\,\zeta({\bm{\eta}})\,\exp\{-\langle{\bm{\lambda}}^{\hbox{{\ppppcarac sol}}},{\bm{h}}^{c}({\bm{\eta}})\rangle\}\quad,\quad\forall\,{\bm{\eta}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{\nu}\,.

(2.7)

Taking into account the introduced hypotheses,
p_{{\bm{H}}^{c}}
is the unique solution of the optimization problem defined by Eq. (
2.3
), in which
{\bm{\lambda}}^{\hbox{{\ppppcarac sol}}}
is the unique solution of a convex optimization problem that will be defined by Proposition
1
in Section
3
) and which will be, under required hypotheses, the solution
of the following nonlinear algebraic equation in
{\bm{\lambda}}
,

\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{\nu}}{\bm{h}}^{c}({\bm{\eta}})\,p_{{\bm{H}}_{\bm{\lambda}}}({\bm{\eta}}\,;{\bm{\lambda}})\,d{\bm{\eta}}={\bm{b}}^{c}
.

2.6
Implicit definition of function
{\bm{h}}^{c}
, resulting difficulties, and necessity to construct a statistical surrogate model

Using the methodology presented in Section
2.4
, the drift vector of the ISDE will involve the matrix
[\nabla_{\!{\bm{\eta}}}{\bm{h}}^{c}({\bm{\eta}})]\in{\mathbb{M}}_{\nu,n_{c}}
(the transpose of the Jacobian matrix of
{\bm{h}}^{c}
). As we have explained in Section
1
, an additional strong assumption used in this paper is that function
{\bm{\eta}}\mapsto{\bm{h}}^{c}({\bm{\eta}})
from
{\mathbb{R}}^{\nu}
into
{\mathbb{R}}^{n_{c}}
is not explicitly defined by an algebraic expression. It is assumed that we can only compute
{\bm{a}}^{\ell}={\bm{h}}^{c}({\bm{\eta}}^{\ell})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n_{c}}
for any point
{\bm{\eta}}^{\ell}
given in
{\mathbb{R}}^{\nu}

(for instance and as previously underlined, a component of
{\bm{h}}^{c}
can be related to the square of a norm of the random normalized residue of the stochastic PDE). The MCMC generator requires the evaluation of
[\nabla_{\!{\bm{\eta}}}{\bm{h}}^{c}({\bm{\eta}})]
for a large number of values of
{\bm{\eta}}
. Consequently, a statistical surrogate model
\hat{\bm{h}}^{N}
of
{\bm{h}}^{c}
is constructed and allows for deducing an algebraic representation
{\bm{\eta}}\mapsto[\nabla_{\!{\bm{\eta}}}\hat{\bm{h}}^{N}{\bm{\eta}})]
of function
{\bm{\eta}}\mapsto[\nabla_{\!{\bm{\eta}}}{\bm{h}}^{c}({\bm{\eta}})]
from
{\mathbb{R}}^{\nu}
into
{\mathbb{M}}_{\nu,n_{c}}
. Such statistical surrogate model is an approximation whose convergence with respect to
N
will be given by Proposition
2
in Section
3
.

is symmetric and positive definite almost surely. If there was a scale separation, then the statistical fluctuations of this tensor would be negligible.

Prior probability model of random field
{\mathbb{C}}

The prior probability model of

88

{\mathbb{R}}^{3}

{\mathbb{C}}({\bm{\xi}})={\mathbb{c}}({\bm{G}}({\bm{\xi}}),\underline{{\bm{z}}})\quad,\quad{\bm{\xi}}\in\Omega\,,

{\mathbb{R}}^{3}

5.5
Definition of the statistical moments and their targets

From Eq. (
5.11
), the
{\mathbb{M}}^{+}_{6}
-valued random variable
[{\mathbb{C}}^{\hbox{{\ppppcarac eff}}}]
is of second-order. We can then define its first two moments.

(i) The first statistical moment of interest is the mean value

[\,\underline{{\mathbb{C}}}^{\hbox{{\ppppcarac eff}}}]=E\{\,[{\mathbb{C}}^{\hbox{{\ppppcarac eff}}}]\,\}\in{\mathbb{M}}^{+}_{6}

of random matrix
[{\mathbb{C}}^{\hbox{{\ppppcarac eff}}}]
while its target counterpart is the given matrix

[\,\underline{{\mathbb{C}}}^{\hbox{{\ppppcarac exp}}}]\in{\mathbb{M}}^{+}_{6}
.
Let
\mu_{\hbox{{\ppppcarac exp}}}=\|\,[\,\underline{{\mathbb{C}}}^{\hbox{{\ppppcarac exp}}}]\,\|_{F}
be the Frobenius norm of
[\,\underline{{\mathbb{C}}}^{\hbox{{\ppppcarac exp}}}]
.
Introducing the subscript "
n
" to designate a normalization, we define the normalized quantities with respect to
\mu_{\hbox{{\ppppcarac exp}}}
as,

[{\mathbb{C}}^{\hbox{{\ppppcarac eff}}}_{n}]=\frac{1}{\mu_{\hbox{{\ppppcarac exp}}}}\,[{\mathbb{C}}^{\hbox{{\ppppcarac eff}}}]\quad,\quad[\,\underline{{\mathbb{C}}}^{\hbox{{\ppppcarac eff}}}_{\,n}]=\frac{1}{\mu_{\hbox{{\ppppcarac exp}}}}\,[\,\underline{{\mathbb{C}}}^{\hbox{{\ppppcarac eff}}}]\quad,\quad[\,\underline{{\mathbb{C}}}^{\hbox{{\ppppcarac exp}}}_{\,n}]=\frac{1}{\mu_{\hbox{{\ppppcarac exp}}}}\,[\,\underline{{\mathbb{C}}}^{\hbox{{\ppppcarac exp}}}]\,.

(5.12)

The corresponding constraint equation will then be written as

E\{\,[{\mathbb{C}}^{\hbox{{\ppppcarac eff}}}_{n}]\,\}=[\,\underline{{\mathbb{C}}}^{\hbox{{\ppppcarac exp}}}_{\,n}]\,.

(5.13)

(ii) The second statistical moment of interest is the coefficient of dispersion
\delta^{\,{\hbox{{\ppppcarac eff}}}}
of random matrix
[{\mathbb{C}}^{\hbox{{\ppppcarac eff}}}]
and its target counterpart
\delta^{\,{\hbox{{\ppppcarac exp}}}}
. Let
\Delta_{2}^{\hbox{{\ppppcarac eff}}}
be the positive-valued random variable defined by

\Delta_{2}^{\hbox{{\ppppcarac eff}}}=\frac{1}{\|\,[\,\underline{{\mathbb{C}}}^{\hbox{{\ppppcarac eff}}}]\,\|^{2}_{F}}\,\|\,[{\mathbb{C}}^{\hbox{{\ppppcarac eff}}}]-[\,\underline{{\mathbb{C}}}^{\hbox{{\ppppcarac eff}}}]\,\|^{2}_{F}\,.

(5.14)

Consequently,
\delta^{\,{\hbox{{\ppppcarac eff}}}}
that is defined by

\delta^{\,{\hbox{{\ppppcarac eff}}}}=\left(E\{\,\|\,[{\mathbb{C}}^{\hbox{{\ppppcarac eff}}}]-[\,\underline{{\mathbb{C}}}^{\hbox{{\ppppcarac eff}}}]\,\|^{2}_{F}/\|\,[\,\underline{{\mathbb{C}}}^{\hbox{{\ppppcarac eff}}}]\,\|^{2}_{F}\,\}\right)^{1/2}
can be rewritten as

\delta^{\,{\hbox{{\ppppcarac eff}}}}=\sqrt{E\{\Delta_{2}^{\hbox{{\ppppcarac eff}}}\}}\,.

(5.15)

Defining
\mu_{\hbox{{\ppppcarac eff}}}=\|\,[\,\underline{{\mathbb{C}}}^{\hbox{{\ppppcarac eff}}}]\,\|_{F}
, the constraint equation that is defined by

\delta^{\,{\hbox{{\ppppcarac eff}}}}=\delta^{\,{\hbox{{\ppppcarac exp}}}}\,,

(5.16)

can be rewritten, using Eqs. (
5.12
) to (
5.15

(5.17)

while Eq. (
5.14
) yields

\Delta_{2}^{\hbox{{\ppppcarac eff}}}=\left(\frac{\mu_{\hbox{{\ppppcarac exp}}}}{\mu_{\hbox{{\ppppcarac eff}}}}\right)^{2}\,\|\,[{\mathbb{C}}^{\hbox{{\ppppcarac eff}}}_{n}]-[\,\underline{{\mathbb{C}}}^{\hbox{{\ppppcarac eff}}}_{\,n}]\,\|^{2}_{F}\,\}\,.

(5.18)

It should be noted that, if

goes to zero, then the statistical fluctuations of

5.12
Discussion about the presented results

(i) The results obtained with the posterior model (see Tables
4
and
5
) show that the constrained learned set significantly improves the prior probability model used for generating the training set. The comparison of the posterior statistics with the targets are good.

(ii) As explained in Section
5.9
the residue constraint is taken into account for the generation of the constrained learned set, but does not intervene in the estimation of the optimal value
{\bm{\lambda}}^{\hbox{{\ppppcarac sol}}}
of
{\bm{\lambda}}
using the error function. Nevertheless and as expected, Fig.
5
and Table
4
show that the residue is controlled and stayed small with respect to the reference (the training) for the optimal solution.

(iii) The convergence of the sequence of MCMC generators with respect to the number of points generated in the constrained learned set is good as shown by Figs.
2
and
4
in accordance to Proposition
4
.

(iv) The dispersion of the target, measured by the value of
\delta^{\,{\hbox{{\ppppcarac exp}}}}
, is smaller than the one of the prior probability model (with which the training set has been constructed). So, it was expected that the posterior random effective elasticity matrix be less dispersed than the one exhibited by the prior probability model. The results confirm this point as it can be seen in Tables
4
and
5
and also in Figs.
3
and
6
.

(v) Comment (ii) is extended as follows. It can be seen that the target is "well" reached for the mean value of the random effective elasticity matrix (see Table
5
), while it is "less well" reached for its coefficient of dispersion computed using the maximum likelihood.
This can be explained by the fact that the second-order moment
E\{\rho_{c}^{2}\}
of the random normalized residue of the equation is kept small during the probabilistic learning of the posterior probability measure, what prevents the dispersion coefficient from reaching its target.
We have carried out numerical tests without imposing the constraint related to
E\{\rho_{c}^{2}\}
, which should stay close to 1. We have observed that the dispersion coefficient "reasonably" reached its target but that
E\{\rho_{c}^{2}\}
did not stay close to
1
but took on significant values greater than
1
. There is indeed a choice of objective between (1) correctly satisfying the constraint on the dispersion coefficient while degrading the value of
E\{\rho_{c}^{2}\}
or (2) preserving a small value of
E\{\rho_{c}^{2}\}
to the detriment of perfectly reaching the target for the dispersion coefficient. We have chosen to present the compromise consisting in taking into account the constraint on the residue during the probabilistic learning process, but the error function that we have chosen to identify the optimal value
{\bm{\lambda}}^{\hbox{{\ppppcarac sol}}}
of
{\bm{\lambda}}
does not take into account the constraint on the residue.

(vi) A last comment concerns the effects of no scale separation. As expected, for the three cases SC1, SC2, and SC3, Table
4
shows that the coefficient of dispersion is significant and increases with the spatial correlation lengths of the random apparent elasticity field at mesoscale, inducing statistical fluctuations of the effective elasticity tensor at macroscale. It should be noted that, even for the case SC1, for which homogenization in the plane of the plate (domain
\Omega
) is guaranteed (the correlation lengths
\underline{L}_{C1}
and
\underline{L}_{C2}
being much lower than
1
), this is not the case for the correlation length
\underline{L}_{C3}
that is equal to the thickness of the plate. Consequently, there is no homogenization at the macroscopic scale and the effective elasticity tensor remains random and is not deterministic.

6
Conclusions

In this paper, we have presented a general methodology to estimate a posterior probability model for a stochastic boundary value problem from a prior probability model. The given targets are statistical moments for which the underlying realizations are not available. Under these conditions, it has been proposed to use the Kullback-Leibler divergence minimum principle for estimating the posterior probability measure, given the prior probability measure and the constraints related to the targets of the statistical moments. We have proposed the construction of a statistical surrogate model of the implicit mapping that represents the constraints. The constrained learned set, which defines the posterior model, is constructed using only a training set constituted of a small number of points. A mathematical analysis of the proposed methodology has been presented. We have defined the required mathematical hypotheses, which have allowed us to prove the convergence of introduced approximations. We have also given all the necessary numerical elements, which facilitate the implementation of the methodology in a parallel computing framework.

The application presented to illustrate the proposed theory is also, as such, a contribution to the three-dimensional stochastic homogenization of heterogeneous linear elastic media in the case of a non-separation of the microscale and macroscale, that is to say, when there are significant statistical fluctuations in the effective elasticity tensor at macroscale. The prior stochastic model of the elasticity tensor field at the mesoscopic scale is an advanced model, recently proposed, which takes into account uncertainties on its spectral measure. In addition to the statistical moments of the random effective elasticity tensor, for the construction of the posterior probability measure by probabilistic learning inference, the second-order moment of the random normalized residue of the stochastic partial differential equation has been added as a constraint. This constraint guarantees that the algorithm seeks to bring the statistical moments closer to their targets while preserving a small residue. The results obtained are those which were expected and give a very good illustration of the theory developed for a non-trivial application.
