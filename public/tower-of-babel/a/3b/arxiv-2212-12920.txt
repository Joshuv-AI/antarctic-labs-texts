Quadrupole partial orders and triple- 𝒒 \bm{q} states on the face-centered cubic lattice
Kazumasa Hattori, Takayuki Ishitobi, Hirokazu Tsunetsugu

Abstract
We study Γ 3 \Gamma_{3} quadrupole orders in a face-centered cubic lattice. The Γ 3 \Gamma_{3} quadrupole moments under cubic symmetry possess a unique cubic invariant in their free energy in the uniform ( 𝒒 = 𝟎 \bm{q}=\bm{0} ) sector and the triple- 𝒒 \bm{q} sector for the X points 𝒒 = ( 2 ​ π , 0 , 0 ) , ( 0 , 2 ​ π , 0 ) \bm{q}=(2\pi,0,0),(0,2\pi,0) , and ( 0 , 0 , 2 ​ π ) (0,0,2\pi) . Competition between this cubic anisotropy and anisotropic quadrupole-quadrupole interactions causes a drastic impact on the phase diagram both in the ground state and at finite temperatures. We show details about the model construction and its properties, the phase diagram, and the mechanism of the various triple- 𝒒 \bm{q} quadrupole orders reported in our preceding letter [J. Phys. Soc. Jpn. 90 , 43701 (2021)]. By using a mean-field approach, we analyze a quadrupole exchange model that consists of a crystalline-electric field scheme with the ground-state Γ 3 \Gamma_{3} non-Kramers doublet and the excited singlet Γ 1 \Gamma_{1} state. We have found various triple- 𝒒 \bm{q} orders in the four-sublattice mean-field approximation. A few partially ordered phases are stabilized in a wide range of parameter space and they have a higher transition temperature than single- 𝒒 \bm{q} orders. With lowering temperature, there occur transitions from these partially ordered phases into further symmetry broken phases in which previously disordered sites acquire nonvanishing quadrupole moments. The identified phases in the mean-field approximation are further analyzed by a phenomenological Landau theory. This analysis reveals results qualitatively consistent with the mean-field results and also shows that the cubic invariant plays an important role for stabilizing the triple- 𝒒 \bm{q} states. The present mechanism for the triple- 𝒒 \bm{q} states also takes effect in systems with different types of quadrupoles, and we discuss its implications for recent experiments in a few f f - and d d -electron compounds.

III
Single-site property

For discussing possible
phases and their transitions in this system,
it is important to first understand single-site properties
under quadrupole molecular field

\bm{h}
=
(h_{u},h_{v})^{\rm T}
.
It turns out that the wavefunction changes its topological
character as the field strength grows,
and this change is reflected in the phase diagrams
of interacting quadrupoles.

A single-site Hamiltonian is given as

{\mathcal{H}}_{\rm mf}=-\bm{h}\cdot\bm{Q}+E_{1}|s\rangle\langle s|,

(21)

where the site label
\bm{r}
is omitted.
As for the external fields,
only those coupled to
\bm{Q}
are considered here,
since our concern in this paper is quadrupole orders.
Since multiplying a positive factor to
\mathcal{H}_{\rm mf}

does not change its ground state,
we normalize the Hamiltonian
such that
\bm{h}^{2}\!+\!E_{1}^{2}\mspace{-2mu}=\mspace{-2mu}1

and introduce the polar coordinates defined by

(h_{u},h_{v})=\sin\Theta(\cos\Phi,\sin\Phi),\ \ E_{1}=\cos\Theta.

(22)

The normalized Hamiltonian is now
represented as

\displaystyle\mspace{-6mu}{\mathcal{H}}_{\rm mf}={\mathcal{H}}_{\rm mf}(\Theta,\Phi)

\displaystyle\mspace{-6mu}=\left(\begin{array}[]{@{}r@{\hs{1pt}}r@{\hs{1pt}}r@{\hs{1pt}}r@{\hs{3pt}}r@{\hs{1pt}}r@{}}\cos\Theta\hskip 1.0&\hskip 1.0&-\alpha\sin\Theta\hskip 1.0&\cos\Phi\hskip 3.0&-\alpha\sin\Theta\hskip 1.0&\sin\Phi\\
-\alpha\sin\Theta\hskip 1.0&\cos\Phi\hskip 1.0&-\sin\Theta\hskip 1.0&\cos\Phi\hskip 3.0&\sin\Theta\hskip 1.0&\sin\Phi\\
-\alpha\sin\Theta\hskip 1.0&\sin\Phi\hskip 1.0&\sin\Theta\hskip 1.0&\sin\Phi\hskip 3.0&\sin\Theta\hskip 1.0&\cos\Phi\end{array}\right).\vskip-6.0pt

Our primary concern is about the case
of
0\mspace{-2mu}<\mspace{-2mu}\Theta\mspace{-2mu}\leq\mspace{-2mu}{\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}\pi
,
and there exists the symmetry

{\mathcal{H}}_{\rm mf}(\mbox{$\pi$$-$$\Theta$},\mbox{$\pi$$+$$\Phi$})\mspace{-2mu}=\mspace{-2mu}-{\mathcal{H}}_{\rm mf}(\Theta,\Phi)
.

It is important to understand the symmetry of this Hamiltonian
that is related to the cubic lattice structure.
Let
R
be one of the aforementioned two types of rotation operators.
Its application transforms
a wavefunction with an orthogonal matrix as

\psi^{\prime}\mspace{-2mu}=\mspace{-2mu}\mathcal{U}_{R}\psi
,
and thus the Hamiltonian is correspondingly transformed as

\mathcal{H}_{\rm mf}^{\prime}\mspace{-2mu}=\mspace{-2mu}\mathcal{U}_{R}\mathcal{H}_{\rm mf}\mathcal{U}_{R}^{\rm T}
.
The transformation matrices
for
R
=
R_{z}(\pm\pi/2)
and
R_{1}(\pm\omega)

are

\mathcal{U}_{R}\mspace{-2mu}=\mspace{-2mu}\mathcal{M}_{z}\mspace{-2mu}=\mspace{-2mu}\mathcal{M}(0)
and

\mathcal{U}(\pm\omega)
,
respectively, in terms of the notations
defined for general
\theta
by

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}(\theta)

\displaystyle=\left(\begin{array}[]{@{}c@{\hspace{4pt}}|@{\hspace{4pt}}c@{\hspace{5pt}}c@{}}1\hfil\hskip 4.0pt\vrule\hskip 4.0&0\hfil\hskip 5.0&0\\
\hline\cr 0\hfil\hskip 4.0pt\vrule\hskip 4.0&\phantom{-}\cos\theta\hfil\hskip 5.0&-\sin\theta\\
0\hfil\hskip 4.0pt\vrule\hskip 4.0&-\sin\theta\hfil\hskip 5.0&-\cos\theta\end{array}\right)=1\oplus\mu(\theta),\

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}(\theta)

\displaystyle=\left(\begin{array}[]{@{}c@{\hspace{4pt}}|@{\hspace{4pt}}c@{\hspace{5pt}}c@{}}1\hfil\hskip 4.0pt\vrule\hskip 4.0&0\hfil\hskip 5.0&0\\
\hline\cr 0\hfil\hskip 4.0pt\vrule\hskip 4.0&\cos\theta\hfil\hskip 5.0&-\sin\theta\\
0\hfil\hskip 4.0pt\vrule\hskip 4.0&\sin\theta\hfil\hskip 5.0&\phantom{-}\cos\theta\end{array}\right)=1\oplus U(\theta),

​​where
\mu(\theta)
and
U(\theta)
are
2\!\times\!2

orthogonal matrices operating in the
\Gamma_{3}
subspace.
Note that the
\Gamma_{1}
space is invariant for any
R
,
since it is singlet.

\mu(\theta)
is a mirror operator in the
\Gamma_{3}
multiplet.
The other symmetry operations of

\theta\mspace{-2mu}=\mspace{-2mu}\pm{\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}\pi
rotations can
be represented as

R_{x}(\theta)\mspace{-2mu}=\mspace{-2mu}R_{1}(\omega)R_{z}(\theta)R_{1}(-\omega)
and

R_{y}(\theta)\mspace{-2mu}=\mspace{-2mu}R_{1}(-\omega)R_{z}(\theta)R_{1}(\omega)
.
The corresponding mirrors are given as

\mathcal{M}_{x,y}\mspace{-2mu}=\mspace{-2mu}\mathcal{U}(\pm\omega)\mathcal{M}_{z}\mathcal{U}(\mp\omega)\mspace{-2mu}=\mspace{-2mu}\mathcal{M}(\pm\omega)
.
Note the relations

[\mathcal{U}(n\omega)]^{\rm T}\mspace{-2mu}=\mspace{-2mu}\mathcal{U}(\mbox{$-$$n\omega$})

and

\mathcal{M}(n\omega)\mathcal{M}(n^{\prime}\omega)\mspace{-2mu}=\mspace{-2mu}\mathcal{U}((\mbox{$n^{\prime}$$-$$n$})\omega)
.

Now that the transformation matrices are obtained, we can directly
show that the transformed Hamiltonian also has the form of

\mathcal{H}_{\rm mf}(\Theta,\Phi^{\prime})

and the new field direction
\Phi^{\prime}
is determined.
The result is summarized as follows:

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}\left(n\omega\right)\,\mathcal{H}_{\rm mf}(\Theta,\Phi)\,[\mathcal{U}\left(n\omega\right)]^{\rm T}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{H}_{\rm mf}\left(\Theta,\mbox{$\Phi$$+$$n\omega$}\right),

(27a)

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}\left(n\omega\right)\,\mathcal{H}_{\rm mf}(\Theta,\Phi)\,\mathcal{M}\left(n\omega\right)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{H}_{\rm mf}\left(\Theta,\mbox{$-$$\Phi$$-$$n\omega$}\right),

(27b)

for
n\mspace{-2mu}=\mspace{-2mu}0,\pm 1
.
Note that the transformations change
\Phi
alone.
These manifest the equivalence of
the following six field directions

\Phi^{\prime}=\pm(\Phi+n\omega),\quad(n=0,\pm 1).

(28)

To be more precise, the Hamiltonians for these
\Phi
’s
are related to each other through orthogonal
transformations.
For the three special directions
\Phi\mspace{-2mu}=\mspace{-2mu}n\omega
(
n\mspace{-2mu}=\mspace{-2mu}0,\pm 1
),

[\mathcal{M}\left(\Phi\right),\,\mathcal{H}_{\rm mf}(\Theta,\Phi)\mspace{2mu}]\mspace{-2mu}=\mspace{-2mu}0

and therefore the ground state wavefunction should be a simultaneous
eigenvector of
\mathcal{M}(\Phi)
.
Finally, in the limit
\Theta
\to
0,
the
\Gamma_{1}
component of
the ground state vanishes.
The Hamiltonian projected to
the remaining
\Gamma_{3}
subspace

\mathcal{P}\mathcal{H}_{\rm mf}\mathcal{P}

\mathcal{P}\mathcal{H}_{\rm mf}(0,\Phi)\mathcal{P}\mspace{-2mu}=\mspace{-2mu}U({\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}\Phi)[\mathcal{P}\mathcal{H}_{\rm mf}(0,0)\mathcal{P}][U({\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}\Phi)]^{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\rm T}

Let
\langle\bm{Q}\rangle_{0}(\Phi)

\Phi

For simplicity, we drop the

The relations (

\displaystyle\langle\bm{Q}\rangle_{0}\left(\mbox{$\Phi$$+$$n\omega$}\right)

\displaystyle=\bigl\langle[\mathcal{U}\left(n\omega\right)]^{\rm T}\bm{Q}\,\mathcal{U}\left(n\omega\right)\bigr\rangle_{0}(\Phi)

\displaystyle=U\left(n\omega\right)\langle\bm{Q}\rangle_{0}(\Phi),

(29a)

\displaystyle\langle\bm{Q}\rangle_{0}\left(\mbox{$2n\omega$$-$$\Phi$}\right)

\displaystyle=\bigl\langle\mathcal{M}\left(n\omega\right)\bm{Q}\,\mathcal{M}\left(n\omega\right)\bigr\rangle_{0}(\Phi)

\displaystyle=\mu\left(n\omega\right)\langle\bm{Q}\rangle_{0}(\Phi).

\mathcal{H}_{\rm mf}(\Theta,\Phi)

IV.1
Pseudospin-1/2 limit

In the limit of

For an isolated quadrupole, the response to the mean field
\bm{h}

has an elementary form

\langle\bm{Q}\rangle\mspace{-2mu}=\mspace{-2mu}\mathscr{N}(h/T)\,\chi_{0}(T)\,\bm{h}

Here,

\mathscr{N}(x)\mspace{-2mu}\equiv\mspace{-2mu}x^{-1}\tanh x

The latter can be neglected for

First,
let us consider the region of ferro order (

The eigenmode analysis

\mathsf{J}(\bm{k})

since the
K

\bm{k}_{0}\mspace{-2mu}=\mspace{-2mu}\bm{0}

in the

–

12|J|\chi_{0}(T_{c})\mspace{-2mu}=\mspace{-2mu}1

Its solution and the ground-state energy are

T_{c}=12|J|,\quad E(\mbox{$T$=0})=6J,\quad(\rm FQ).

(34)

\bm{q}_{*}

VI
Landau theory

In this section, we employ a phenomenological Landau analysis
and interpret the determined mean-field phase diagrams (Fig.
9
).
Our aim is to explain the stability of various ordered states
and describe their transitions based on a phenomenological theory.

The determined phase diagrams in Sec.
V
are quite complicated, and
their complete analysis is beyond the scope of the present paper.
Since the partially ordered
zyox
and
zoxy
states
are exotic states characteristic to the present model,
we set them as our main targets and investigate mainly
their stability mechanism and instability to other states.

In our phenomenological analysis,
we will construct the Landau free energy
in terms of
\bm{Q}_{\bm{k}_{0}}
and
\bm{Q}_{\bm{k}_{\ell}}
’s
and analyze various triple-
\bm{q}
orders in detail.
An important point is that the ordering vectors
\bm{q}_{*}
=
\bm{k}_{\ell}

allow cubic couplings of the three antiferro modes
\bm{Q}_{\bm{k}_{\ell}}
,
which play a crucial role in stabilizing several ordered states
in this system.
This analysis succeeds in explaining
most of the results of the microscopic mean field calculations
in Sec.
IV
.

VI.1
Landau-Ginzburg free energy expansion

Let us first introduce the local free energy
f_{\rm loc}(\bm{r})

with the quadrupole moment

\bm{\phi}(\bm{r})\mspace{-2mu}\equiv\mspace{-2mu}[(\phi_{u},\phi_{v})(\bm{r})]^{\rm T}

as

\displaystyle f_{\rm loc}(\bm{r})=

\displaystyle{{\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}}a_{0}(T)|\bm{\phi}(\bm{r})|^{2}-b\bigl[\phi_{u}^{3}(\bm{r})-3\phi_{u}(\bm{r})\phi_{v}^{2}(\bm{r})\bigr]

\displaystyle+c|\bm{\phi}(\bm{r})|^{4}+O(|\bm{\phi}(\bm{r})|^{5}).

(53)

The coefficients
a_{0}
,
b
, and
c
are all positive
constants depending on temperature,
which may be derived from the local CEF model
through a Legendre transformation
Hattori and Tsunetsugu 2014
.
It is customary to consider the temperature dependence
of
a_{0}(T)
alone and neglect the change
s
in
b
and
c
.
One should note that
a_{0}(T)
is the inverse
of local quadrupole susceptibility and decreases
monotonically towards zero with decreasing temperature.
An important characteristic of this system
is the presence of the
b
-term.
This third-order term exists
only for order parameters
with even parity under both time reversal
and space inversion operations,
and our quadrupole moments belong to this category.
Its explicit form in terms of
\phi_{u}
and
\phi_{v}

is independent of the details of the system,
and determined from the coefficient related to the reduction
of the triple product of the
\Gamma_{3}
representation
to the trivial one,

\Gamma_{3}\otimes\Gamma_{3}\otimes\Gamma_{3}\mspace{-2mu}\to\mspace{-2mu}\Gamma_{1}
.

Following the conventional procedure,
we add to Eq. (
53
) the exchange interaction energy,
which are given by
the mean-field approximation.
Thus the total free energy
density
\bar{f}=F/N

reads as

\displaystyle\bar{f}\equiv\!\!\frac{1}{N}\sum_{\bm{r}}\Bigl[f_{\rm loc}(\bm{r})+\sum_{j=1}^{6}\,\bm{\phi}(\bm{r}+\bm{\delta}_{j})\cdot\mathsf{J}_{\bm{\delta}_{j}}\,\bm{\phi}(\bm{r})\Bigr]\mspace{-10mu}

(54a)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{\bm{p}}\bm{\phi}(-\bm{p})\cdot\bigl[{\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}a_{0}+\mathsf{J}(\bm{p})\bigr]\bm{\phi}(\bm{p})

\displaystyle-\frac{b}{N^{3/2}}{\sum}^{\prime}\phi_{u}(\bm{p}_{3})\bigl[\phi_{u}(\bm{p}_{1})\phi_{u}(\bm{p}_{2})-3\phi_{v}(\bm{p}_{1})\phi_{v}(\bm{p}_{2})\bigr]

\displaystyle+\frac{c}{N^{2}}{\sum}^{\prime}\bigl[\bm{\phi}(\bm{p}_{1})\cdot\bm{\phi}(\bm{p}_{2})\bigr]\bigl[\bm{\phi}(\bm{p}_{3})\cdot\bm{\phi}(\bm{p}_{4})\bigr],\mspace{-10mu}\vskip-6.0pt

(54b)

where
\sum^{\prime}
denotes the sum over
\bm{p}_{i}
’s
under the constraint
\sum_{i}\bm{p}_{i}=
(some reciprocal
lattice vector
\bm{G}
).
The interaction matrix

\mathsf{J}_{\bm{\delta}}

is given by Eq. (
7
),
while its Fourier transform

\mathsf{J}(\bm{p})
is given by Eq. (
11b
).
We have neglected a constant energy,
and
N
is the number of the sites.
The terms for
\bm{G}\neq\bm{0}
are Umklapp processes,
and they will turn out to be important later.

Since our main concern is
various antiferro orders of quadrupole,
we split the total free energy as follows:

\bar{f}=f_{\rm X}+f_{\Gamma}+f_{\Gamma{\rm X}}+f_{\mathrm{other}},

(55)

where
f_{\rm X}
is the antiferro part,
i.e., contributions of
\bm{\phi}(\bm{k}_{\ell})
’s alone.

f_{\Gamma}
is the ferro part contributed by
\bm{\phi}(\bm{k}_{0})
,
and
f_{\Gamma{\rm X}}
is the coupling of
\bm{\phi}(\bm{k}_{\ell})
’s
with
\bm{\phi}(\bm{k}_{0})
.
We will show that this coupling modifies
and eventually destabilizes several antiferro orders.
Lastly,
f_{\mathrm{other}}
is the sum of
all the remaining parts.

VI.2
Free energy of the antiferro modes

In this section, we will rewrite the antiferro part
f_{\rm X}

into a convenient form for later analyses.
When
|K|\mspace{-2mu}+\mspace{-2mu}2J\mspace{-2mu}>\mspace{-2mu}0
,
the maximally negative eigenvalue of the matrix
\mathsf{J}(\bm{p})

is
\Lambda_{\rm min}\mspace{-2mu}=\mspace{-2mu}-2J-4|K|
and it is realized
at
\bm{p}\mspace{-2mu}=\mspace{-2mu}\bm{k}_{\ell}
’s.
Thus it is natural to expect a single-
\bm{q}
order
with the ordering vector located at one of
\bm{k}_{\ell}
’s.
The transition temperature of this order
T\mspace{-2mu}=\mspace{-2mu}T_{c}^{0}

is determined by the equation

{\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}a_{0}(T_{c}^{0})\mspace{-2mu}+\mspace{-2mu}\Lambda_{\rm min}\mspace{-2mu}=\mspace{-2mu}0
.
However, the situation is not so simple in this system,
since the free energy contains the third-order terms
including
\bm{\phi}(\bm{k}_{\ell})
’s.
The three wavevectors at the X points satisfy the relation

\bm{k}_{1}\mspace{-2mu}+\mspace{-2mu}\bm{k}_{2}\mspace{-2mu}+\mspace{-2mu}\bm{k}_{3}\mspace{-2mu}=\mspace{-2mu}(2\pi,2\pi,2\pi)\mspace{-2mu}=\mspace{-2mu}\bm{G}
, and thus
the corresponding third-order coupling is nonvanishing.
This may lead to a first-order transition to
a triple-
\bm{q}
state with a transition temperature

T_{c}\mspace{-2mu}>\mspace{-2mu}T_{c}^{0}
.

To analyze such triple-
\bm{q}
states,
we restrict the degrees of freedom
to those
\{\bm{\phi}(\bm{k}_{\ell})\}_{\ell=1,2,3}

and ignore other modes.
Notice that
\bm{\phi}(\bm{k}_{\ell})
’s are real,
since each
\bm{k}_{\ell}
is equivalent to
-\bm{k}_{\ell}
,
and proportional to
\sqrt{N}
in the antiferro ordered states.
We thus rescale them as

\bm{\psi}^{({\ell})}\mspace{-2mu}\equiv\mspace{-2mu}\bm{\phi}(\bm{k}_{\ell})/\sqrt{N}
.
The related part of the free energy density
reads as

\displaystyle f_{\rm X}=\sum_{\ell}\bm{\psi}^{({\ell})}\cdot\bigl[{\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}a_{0}(T)+\mathsf{J}(\bm{k}_{\ell})\bigr]\bm{\psi}^{({\ell})}

\displaystyle-6b\Bigl[\psi_{u}^{(1)}\psi_{u}^{(2)}\psi_{u}^{(3)}-\sum_{\ell}\psi_{u}^{({\ell})}\psi_{v}^{({\ell}+1)}\psi_{v}^{({\ell}+2)}\Bigr]+c\sum_{\ell}\,\bigl|\bm{\psi}^{({\ell})}\bigr|^{4}

\displaystyle+2c^{\prime}\sum_{{\ell}<{\ell}^{\prime}}\bigl|\bm{\psi}^{({\ell})}\bigr|^{2}\,\bigl|\bm{\psi}^{({\ell}^{\prime})}\bigr|^{2}+4c{{}^{\prime}\mspace{-2mu}^{\prime}}\sum_{{\ell}<{\ell}^{\prime}}\bigl[\bm{\psi}^{({\ell})}\mspace{-4mu}\cdot\bm{\psi}^{({\ell}^{\prime})}\bigr]^{2},

(56)

where one should understand that

\bm{\psi}^{({\ell}+3)}\mspace{-2mu}\equiv\mspace{-2mu}\bm{\psi}^{({\ell})}
.
Here, although the direct calculations provide

c^{\prime}\mspace{-2mu}=\mspace{-2mu}c{{}^{\prime}\mspace{-2mu}^{\prime}}\mspace{-2mu}=\mspace{-2mu}c
,
we regard these three as independent parameters,
since each term is separately invariant.
We rewrite Eq. (
56
) in terms of
the polar coordinates defined as

\bm{\psi}^{(\ell)}=(\psi^{(\ell)}_{u},\psi^{(\ell)}_{v})^{\rm T}\equiv X_{\ell}\hat{\bm{e}}(\theta_{\ell})
as in Eqs. (
49
) and (
50
).
We also define

\bm{X}\mspace{-2mu}\equiv\mspace{-2mu}(X_{1},X_{2},X_{3})
and

\bm{\theta}\mspace{-2mu}\equiv\mspace{-2mu}(\theta_{1},\theta_{2},\theta_{3})

for later use.
Substituting these into Eq. (
56
), we obtain

\displaystyle f_{\rm X}=

\displaystyle\bigl[{\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}a_{0}(T)-2J\bigr]R^{2}+cR^{4}-4K\sum_{\ell}X_{\ell}^{2}\cos\left(2\theta_{\ell}\mspace{-2mu}+\mspace{-2mu}\ell\omega\right)

\displaystyle-6bX_{1}X_{2}X_{3}\cos\bar{\theta}+2(c^{\prime}\mspace{-2mu}-\mspace{-2mu}c)\sum_{\ell<\ell^{\prime}}X_{\ell}^{2}X_{\ell^{\prime}}^{2}

\displaystyle+4c{{}^{\prime}\mspace{-2mu}^{\prime}}\sum_{\ell<\ell^{\prime}}X_{\ell}^{2}X_{\ell^{\prime}}^{2}\,\cos^{2}(\theta_{\ell}\mspace{-2mu}-\mspace{-2mu}\theta_{\ell^{\prime}}),

(57)

where

R^{2}\equiv X_{1}^{2}+X_{2}^{2}+X_{3}^{2},\quad\bar{\theta}\equiv\theta_{1}+\theta_{2}+\theta_{3}.

(58)

Further analysis depends on the sign of
the anisotropic interaction
K
.
In the following sections, we will discuss the two cases separately.

VII.1
Large
K

zyox

In the limit of
K\mspace{-2mu}\to\mspace{-2mu}\infty
,
the free energy
f_{\rm X}
has a symmetry inherited from the
K
-term.
The replacement

\theta_{\ell}\mspace{-2mu}\to\mspace{-2mu}\theta_{\ell}\mspace{-2mu}+\mspace{-2mu}\pi
for any
\ell

does not change the
K

and this results in an eightfold degeneracy of
f_{\rm X}
minimum:

\bm{\theta}\mspace{-2mu}=\mspace{-2mu}(\omega,-\omega,0)\mspace{-2mu}+\mspace{-2mu}(n_{1},n_{2},n_{3})\mspace{2mu}\pi
where
n_{j}\mspace{-2mu}=\mspace{-2mu}0,1
.
The third-order term favors four
out of these eight possibilities.
Since
b\mspace{-2mu}>\mspace{-2mu}0
, the favored ones are
those with
\sum_{j}n_{j}\mspace{-2mu}=\mspace{-2mu}(\mbox{even})
.

We continue the minimization procedure for
f_{\rm X}

now with respect to

X_{\ell}
’s.
This is a cumbersome but straightforward calculation,
and we show only its results.
There exist two types of solutions, and both are
controlled by the renormalized second-order
coupling

a_{\rm X}(T)\mspace{-2mu}\equiv\mspace{-2mu}{\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}a_{0}(T)\mspace{-2mu}-\mspace{-2mu}2J\mspace{-2mu}-\mspace{-2mu}4K.

(59)

One type is the solution that only one of

X_{\ell}
’s
is nonvanishing,
while

X_{1}\mspace{-2mu}=\mspace{-2mu}X_{2}\mspace{-2mu}=\mspace{-2mu}X_{3}

in the other type.
The former one is the single-
\bm{q}
order, and the latter one
is the
zyox

A solution for the single-
\bm{q}
order
(
\mspace{2mu}X_{\ell}\mspace{-2mu}=\mspace{-2mu}\delta_{\ell 3}R_{1\bm{q}}^{*}\mspace{2mu}
)
exists when
a_{\rm X}(T)\mspace{-2mu}<\mspace{-2mu}0
,
and it is represented as follows

R_{1\bm{q}}^{*}\equiv\left[\frac{-a_{\rm X}(T)}{2c}\right]^{1/2},\quad f_{\rm X}^{1\bm{q}}=-\frac{a_{\rm X}(T)^{2}}{4c}.

(60)

See Eq. (
99
) in Appendix
A
.

A
zyox
-type solution
(
\mspace{2mu}X_{1}\mspace{-2mu}=\mspace{-2mu}X_{2}\mspace{-2mu}=\mspace{-2mu}X_{3}\mspace{-2mu}=\mspace{-2mu}:R_{zyox}^{*}/\sqrt{3}\mspace{2mu}
)
appears as a local minimum,
and we denote its transition temperature by
T^{\star}
.
See Eq. (
100

effective free energy as a function of
R_{zyox}^{*}
.
We define
R^{\star}
as the
R_{zyox}^{*}
value
just below
T^{\star}
.
These values are determined as

R^{\star}\equiv\frac{\sqrt{3}b}{4(c\mspace{-2mu}+\mspace{-2mu}c_{1}/3)},\quad a_{\rm X}(T^{\star})=\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}bR^{\star}\equiv a^{\star}>0,

(61)

where

c_{1}\equiv 2(c^{\prime}\mspace{-2mu}-\mspace{-2mu}c)\mspace{-2mu}+\mspace{-2mu}c{{}^{\prime}\mspace{-2mu}^{\prime}}.

(62)

Then, the temperature dependence of important quantities
is written as

\displaystyle R_{zyox}^{*}

\displaystyle=R^{\star}\bigl[1+\sqrt{1-a_{\rm X}(T)/a^{\star}}\,\bigr],

(63)

\displaystyle f_{\rm X}^{zyox}

\displaystyle=(4\sqrt{3})^{-1}b{R^{\star}}^{3}\,\mathscr{F}_{2}\bigl(1-a_{\rm X}(T)/a^{\star}\mspace{2mu}\bigr),

(64)

where
\mathscr{F}_{2}(x)=4-3(1+x)^{2}-8x^{3/2}
.

Let us now compare the stability of these two ordered states
by calculating their free energies.
One should recall that
a_{\rm X}(T)
decreases monotonically
with lowering temperature.
Four temperatures characterize possible phase transitions:

T^{\times}<T_{1\bm{q}}^{c}<T_{zyox}^{c}<T^{\star}.

(65)

As defined before,
T^{\star}
is the temperature where
a solution of the
zyox
order appears.
However, since its free energy is still higher than
that for the disordered phase,
this order is not realized yet.
The
zyox
order is stable below
T_{zyox}^{c}

and this temperature is determined by the condition

a_{\rm X}(T_{zyox}^{c})\mspace{-2mu}=\mspace{-2mu}{\textstyle\frac{8}{9}}a^{\star}
.
With further lowering temperature,
the effective second-order coupling

a_{\rm X}(T)

vanishes eventually and this defines the “transition temperature”
of the single-
\bm{q}
order
T_{1\bm{q}}^{c}
.
Below this temperature, the single-
\bm{q}
ordered state is
more stable than the disordered state.
However, its free energy

f_{\rm X}^{1\bm{q}}

remains
higher than
f_{\rm X}^{zyox}
for a while,
and thus the single-
\bm{q}
order does not appear yet.
With further decreasing temperature,

f_{\rm X}^{zyox}(T)
may cross with
f_{\rm X}^{1\bm{q}}(T)
.
This determines the final characteristic temperature
T^{\times}
,
below which
the single-
\bm{q}
order is finally realized.
Equating the two free energies,

T^{\times}
is obtained
as a solution of the following equation

\displaystyle\frac{c_{1}}{8c}=x^{-2}\bigl[1-{\textstyle\frac{3}{2}}x+(1-x)^{3/2}\bigr]\Big|_{x=a(T^{\times})/a^{\star}}.

(66)

One should note that the transitions
at
T_{zyox}^{c}
and
T^{\times}

are both first order.

We have not examined a possibility of double-
\bm{q}
orders,
and there is a reason for that.
In the free energy density
f_{\rm X}
in Eq. (
57
),
the third-order term generally has a nonzero coupling

.
Thus, when two order parameters are nonvanishing, say

\bm{\theta}

\bm{q}

\bm{\psi}_{0}

Thus, the above analysis is satisfactory

zyox

and we need to include those corrections for

zyzx
and single-
\bm{q}

zyox

than the transition temperature

\bm{q}
state.
This is one of the main results in this paper.

Finally, let us demonstrate the partially ordered configuration of
quadrupoles in real space for the
zyox

Since

\bm{\psi}_{0}\mspace{-2mu}=\mspace{-2mu}\bm{0}

(67)

IX
Discussion

In this section, we
discuss the implications of the present theory for
the related materials
including PrMgNi
4
and 5

double perovskites.
We also briefly comment on further implications of
the multiple-
\bm{q}
physics of multipoles in other systems.
It should be noted that the microscopic mean-field results
in Sec.
V

localized model are supported
by the Landau analysis in Sec.
VI

This indicates that
the discussions about the triple-
\bm{q}

in this paper are also applicable to metallic systems.
One should understand that the ordering wavevectors
are determined with taking into account
the effects of the conduction electrons.

IX.1
\Gamma_{3}
quadrupole moments in real systems

The rare earth compound PrMgNi
4
has a structure in which
Pr
3+
ions form an fcc sublattice
Kusanose et al. 2019
,
and the CEF ground state of the Pr
3+
ion has been identified
as the non-Kramers doublet
\Gamma_{3}
.
This material is metallic but shows no indication
of the quadrupolar Kondo effects.
For discussing the phase transition in this material,
it is useful to compare it with the
results obtained for the localized model.
A detailed quantitative analysis needs more
elaborate calculations and it is one of our future studies.
The excited states of Pr
3+
ion are
the
\Gamma_{4}
triplet at 1.16 meV,
the
\Gamma_{1}
singlet at 2.78 meV,
and the
\Gamma_{5}
triplet at 11.6 meV,
and these excitation energies have been determined
by the inelastic neutron scattering experiments
Kusanose et al. 2022
.
Here, the excitation gap to
\Gamma_{1}
corresponds to the parameter

E_{1}
used in Eq. (
10
).
Since the other states
have no quadrupole matrix elements with the
ground states
\Gamma_{3}
,
we have not taken them into account.
The unidentified inelastic peaks at 2.5 and 5.9 meV
suggest that the cubic lattice symmetry is weakly broken.
However, thermodynamic experiments such as
specific heat and magnetization measurements have shown no
signature of phase transitions down to the temperature
\sim 0.1
K. This broken lattice symmetry is now considered as an extrinsic
effect of lattice imperfections or excess Mg atoms.
They mask the intrinsic quadrupole ordering discussed in this paper,
and thus further experimental studies using single crystals
are necessary to identify the type of quadrupole order
realized in this system. To explore exotic quadrupolar physics,
it is important to find other materials related to PrMgNi
4
.
Mg or Ni may be replaced by nearby elements in the periodic table
with similar chemical properties.

In a recent study, PrCdNi
4
was synthesized
and found to show a clear phase transition
Kusanose and Onimaru
.
The estimated entropy
S
at the transition temperature
T_{q}\simeq 1
K is
less than
\sim 0.5
ln 2, and
the ordering degrees of freedom have not been
identified. A broad peak at
T\simeq 5
K is reported in its specific heat above
T_{q}
.
Although this might be a Schottky peak due to the CEF excited states,
its origin remains unclear,
since
S(T\simeq 5~{\rm K})
is less than ln 2 and too small
to conclude that this anomaly
is due to the CEF excitations.
In this respect, it is interesting to apply the present theory
and explore a possibility of triple-
\bm{q}
ordered states above
T_{q}
.
We have no information on
the detailed bond dependence of the quadrupole exchange interactions,
except the point that
the total magnitude is about 1 K.
In addition to the nearest-neighbor interactions,
some further-neighbor ones may also be large and
have non-negligible effects.
For determining their values,
it is useful to observe the spin-orbital wave
in the ordered phase,
and such experiments are highly desirable.
We also expect
that further experimental studies
clarify the nature of the low temperature phase in PrCdNi
4
.

Other interesting materials related to the present theory
are the family of double-perovskites containing an fcc sublattice of
ions with 5
d^{1}
electron configuration
Erickson et al. 2007
;
de Vries et al. 2010
;
Lu et al. 2017
;
Gao et al. 2020
;
Hirai et al. 2020
;
Arima et al. 2022
.
A characteristic point is that
those ions have a quartet ground state with the effective
total angular momentum
J_{\rm eff}\mspace{-2mu}=\mspace{-2mu}{\textstyle\frac{3}{2}}

due to strong spin-orbit coupling in
5d
orbitals.
Various nontrivial orders have been proposed for this system

Chen et al. 2010
;
Churchill and Kee 2022
;
Svoboda et al. 2021
.
Under the cubic CEF,
this quartet can be regarded as a product state made of
a spin-
{\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}
doublet and an orbital
\Gamma_{3}
(
E_{g}
) doublet.
Thus, in the temperature range where their spins remain disordered,
we may expect that their orbital degrees of freedom are
described by the present theory.

One member of this family is Ba
2
MgReO
6
.
Hirai et al. studied it by synchrotron x-ray-diffraction measurement
and observed a phase transition
of the AFO
22
-type quadrupole order at
T_{q}\mspace{-2mu}=\mspace{-2mu}33
K above
the magnetic ordering temperature
T_{m}\mspace{-2mu}=\mspace{-2mu}18
K
Hirai et al. 2020
. They also found a ferro O
20
component below
T_{q}
,
and it has been explained by considering the electron-lattice anharmonic coupling or lattice anharmonicity

Hirai et al. 2020
;
Svoboda et al. 2021
;
Iwahara and Chibotaru 2022
.
We propose to apply the present theory to this system and explain a ferro component as an induced moment due to the third-order coupling.

From our point of view, it is worthwhile to examine
the effects of CEF excited states above
the
J_{\rm eff}={\textstyle\frac{3}{2}}

multiplet on the magnitude of the observed ferro orbital moments.
Important excited states are orbital singlet (spin doublet) states.
They realize a situation of the orbital degrees of freedom
similar to those studied in this paper,
where the third-order couplings of quadrupoles take effects
Kubo et al. 2023
.

IX.2
Other applications

We have demonstrated in this paper that
the triple-
\bm{q}
quadrupole orders emerge generically, if not always,
in the fcc lattice with nearest-neighbor interactions,
and that some of them are partially ordered states.
One of the main results is that the partial-order
state for
K\mspace{-2mu}>\mspace{-2mu}0

(named
zoxy
) has a higher transition temperature
than the single-
\bm{q}
quadrupole order.
This is a consequence of the cooperation of
the anisotropic interaction
K
and the third-order
b
-term
of the local potential in the free energy.
It is also important that the
b
term couples the modes
at all the three X points,
and this causes several triple-
\bm{q}
order patterns of quadrupoles.
The translation symmetry imposes the important matching condition

\bm{k}_{1}\mspace{-2mu}+\mspace{-2mu}\bm{k}_{2}\mspace{-2mu}+\mspace{-2mu}\bm{k}_{3}\mspace{-2mu}=\mspace{-2mu}\bm{G}
.
Some three-dimensional systems have a set of
high-symmetric
\bm{k}
-points in the Brillouin zone
satisfying this condition.
For example,
\bm{k}_{1}=(0,\pi,\pi)
,
\bm{k}_{2}=(\pi,0,\pi)

In two dimensions, this condition is easily satisfied particularly in
systems with a hexagonal symmetry,

Multipoles have such third-order couplings,

One can expect similar triple-

In the

xy

Nikolaev and Michel 1999

and this is similar to the

We also note that

For example, most natural candidates are the

This case was studied using the

(

Amara et al. 1995

Recently, two of the present authors

to this type of materials

It was motivated
by the experiments pointing out the importance of quadrupole

Yanagisawa et al. 2021

A triple-
\bm{q}

This suggests an interesting possibility of

When a leading instability occurs at an incommensurate

X
Summary

In this paper, we have studied
\Gamma_{3}
quadrupole orders
in an fcc lattice.
We have employed a four sublattice mean-field theory,
and shown the presence of various triple-
\bm{q}
states,
which include partially ordered states with disordered sites
at high temperatures.
We have discussed the stability of these states,
based on the phenomenological Landau theory
and shown that its results can well explain
those of the microscopic mean-field calculations.
The third-order coupling of quadrupoles in the free energy plays a
crucial role for stabilizing the triple-
\bm{q}
states
with the ordering vectors located at the zone boundary X points.
This unique mechanism for the triple-
\bm{q}
orders
is quite ubiquitous
in the systems with the time-reversal even parity
including electric multipoles,
and it also works in many other systems.
We believe that our work stimulates further theoretical studies
and experiments on exotic quadrupole or other multipole orders in future.

By repeating this procedure with varying the ray’s direction

Let us write down explicitly the above procedure.
Suppose a set of parameters
a
,
b
, and
c
’s is given, and consider
possible phase transitions upon lowering temperature.
The first step is the construction of the following
function:

\bar{g}_{u}(R)\equiv\bar{g}^{\mspace{2mu}34}(t_{\sharp},s_{\sharp})\Bigr|_{\bar{c}_{\mathrm{A}}=RC\cos u,\ \bar{c}_{\mathrm{B}}=RC\sin u},

(110)

where
the two dimensional vector

is parameterized by its modulus

and angle

.
Here,
(t_{\sharp},s_{\sharp})
denotes the minimum position
for the given value of
R
.
The total free energy density is then given as

\displaystyle\tilde{f}_{\rm X}=a(T)R^{2}+cR^{4}+bR^{3}\bar{g}_{u}(R).

(111)

Now, the Landau free energy functional has been minimized with respect to
all the degrees of freedom except for

Therefore, the minimization with respect to

A nontrivial solution is the one satisfying the

-2a(T)-4cR^{2}=3bR\bar{g}_{u}(R)+bR^{2}\frac{d\bar{g}_{u}(R)}{dR}=:\mathscr{Y}_{u}(R^{2}).

(112)

One can solve this graphically:

determines the minimum position
as
R\mspace{-2mu}=\mspace{-2mu}\sqrt{\rho_{\star}(T)}
.
If there are multiple crossing points of this kind,
the one with the lowest
\tilde{f}_{\rm X}
is the global minimum.
Then, the stable state at the temperature
T

is that
in Fig.
18

at the position

(\bar{c}_{\mathrm{A}},\bar{c}_{\mathrm{B}})\mspace{-2mu}=\mspace{-2mu}\sqrt{\rho_{\star}(T)}\mspace{2mu}C(\cos u,\sin u)
.
