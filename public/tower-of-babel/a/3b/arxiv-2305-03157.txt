Good Will Hunting’s Problem: Counting Homeomorphically Irreducible Trees
Ira M. Gessel ∗

Abstract
. In the film Good Will Hunting , the main character, a janitor at MIT named Will Hunting, attacks the problem of drawing all the homeomorphically irreducible trees with 10 vertices. Although the film suggests that this is a difficult problem, it is in fact quite easy. A much more interesting problem is counting homeomorphically irreducible trees with n n vertices for all n n , a feat accomplished by Harary and Prins in 1959. Here we give an exposition and simplification of Harary and Prins’s result, introducing some of the fundamental ideas of graphical enumeration.

1.
Introduction.

In the 1997 film
Good Will Hunting
, the main character, a young janitor at MIT named Will Hunting, astounds MIT Professor Lambeau by tackling the problem “Draw all the homeomorphically irreducible trees with
n=10
.” In other words, draw all trees on 10 vertices with no vertices of degree 2, where isomorphic trees are considered the same.

Like the other problems in the film, this one is at about the level of an undergraduate homework problem. Since there are only ten such trees (Will draws eight of them before he is interrupted), to solve the problem it is enough to find a systematic way of making sure that no trees have been omitted or repeated, and it’s not hard to do this by considering the possible degrees of the vertices, breaking up the problem into small cases that are easily dealt with individually. (See, for example, Horváth, Korándi and Szabó
[
7
]
.)

A much more interesting problem is that of counting homeomorphically irreducible trees with
n
vertices for
all

n
. Since we can’t compute infinitely many numbers, this requires some explanation.
Ideally we would like a simple explicit formula, such as Cayley’s formula
n^{n-2}
for the number of
labeled
trees on
n
vertices. But there is unfortunately no such simple formula for counting unlabeled trees.
As an alternative, we might be satisfied with an efficient algorithm for computing the numbers.
(See Wilf
[
17
]
for a discussion of what an answer is for a general enumeration problem; see also Pak
[
14
]
.)
But we can do better than this: we can find a functional equation for the generating function for homeomorphically irreducible trees that enables us to count them quite efficiently.

We will first count unlabeled trees with no restrictions on the degrees of the vertices. The enumeration of these trees is one of the fundamental results of graphical enumeration, and our account is an introduction to the topic. Once we understand how to count these trees, the modification needed for counting homeomorphically irreducible trees is easy.

Unrestricted trees were first counted by Cayley
[
1
,
2
]
and a simpler approach was given by Otter
[
13
]
.
We shall follow Otter’s approach, with some variations and simplifications.
We count trees in two steps. First we count
rooted
trees, using a recursive decomposition that yields a functional equation for their generating function. Rather than using Pólya’s theorem, as in some approaches, we use simple facts about enumeration of multisets to derive the functional equation. Then we reduce the enumeration of (unrooted) trees to that of rooted trees. To do this, Otter introduced the method of “dissimilarity characteristic theorems,” which relate the numbers of orbits of vertices and edges under the automorphism group of a tree. We use instead a “dissymmetry theorem,” introduced by Leroux
[
10
]
(see also Leroux and Miloudi
[
11
]
), which is conceptually simpler and generalizes more easily. (See, e.g., Gainer-Dewar and Gessel
[
4
]
.)

Homeomorphically irreducible trees were first counted by Harary and Prins in 1959
[
6
]
. An exposition of their work can also be found in the book of Harary and Palmer
[
5
, pp. 61–64]
, and another enumeration of homeomorphically irreducible trees, using the theory of combinatorial species, was given by Leroux and Miloudi
[
11
, Exemple 1]
.

When we apply this decomposition to unlabeled trees, the set of rooted subtrees becomes a

The reader may find it instructive to give a rigorous proof, using the definition of unlabeled trees in terms of isomorphisms, of the following intuitively obvious fact: There is a bijection from unlabeled rooted trees to pairs consisting of a single unlabeled vertex and a multiset of unlabeled rooted trees, and this bijection preserves the total number of vertices.

2

2

We assume that there are only finitely many elements of

To apply the decomposition of a rooted tree into a root and a multiset of rooted trees, we need to compute generating functions for multisets. Given a set

x^{s(a_{1})+s(a_{2})+\cdots+s(a_{k})}

We would like to find a formula for the generating function for the set

in terms of the generating function

for

Let

the generating function for multisets of

Lemma 3.1

The generating function for

Lemma 3.2

The generating function for all multisets of elements of

4.
Counting trees.

To count trees, we first count rooted trees. We then relate the enumeration of unrooted trees to that of rooted trees.

4.1.
Rooted trees.

Our decomposition of a rooted tree into a root together with a multiset of rooted trees gives the functional equation for the generating function
r(x)
for rooted trees, where the size of a rooted tree is the number of vertices:

Theorem 4.1
.

The generating function
r(x)
for rooted trees satisfies

(2)

r(x)=xh[r(x)]=x\exp\biggl(\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{r(x^{k})}{k}\biggr).

Proof.

The decomposition of Section
2

The result then follows from Lemma
3.2
, which gives the generating function for multisets of rooted trees in terms of
r(x)
.
∎

Let

Then

x\exp\biggl(\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{r(x^{k})}{k}\biggr).

It is easy to use this formula, inefficient though it is, with a computer algebra system to compute the numbers

If we want our computer to do less work, we can derive a more efficient formula.
Suppose two generating functions

4.2.
Unrooted trees.

To count unrooted trees, we find a formula that expresses the generating function for unrooted trees in terms of the generating function for rooted trees. We accomplish this through the
dissymmetry theorem
of Pierre Leroux
[
10
,
11
]
. (Otter
[
13
]
used a related, but somewhat different, result called the “dissimilarity characteristic theorem.”)

Leroux’s theorem relates unrooted trees to trees rooted at a vertex, trees rooted at an edge, and trees rooted at a vertex and incident edge.

Theorem 4.2

(The dissymmetry theorem)
.

Let
T
be a
(
labeled
)
tree. Let
T^{\bullet}
be the set of rootings of
T
at a vertex, let
T^{\raise 1.6pt\hbox{\kern 0.3pt{\vrule width=5.0pt,height=0.8pt}}}
be the set of rootings of
T
at an edge, and let
T^{\bullet\raise 1.6pt\hbox{\vrule width=4.0pt,height=0.8pt}}
be the set of rootings of
T
at a vertex and incident edge. Then there is a bijection
\phi_{T}
from

T^{\bullet}\cup T^{\raise 1.6pt\hbox{\kern 0.3pt{\vrule width=5.0pt,height=0.8pt}}}

to

\{T\}\cup T^{\bullet\raise 1.6pt\hbox{\vrule width=4.0pt,height=0.8pt}}

which is compatible with isomorphisms of trees; i.e., if
\alpha\colon T_{1}\to T_{2}
is an isomorphism of trees, and we extend
\alpha
in the natural way to rootings of
T_{1}
and
T_{2}
, then
\alpha\circ\phi_{T_{1}}=\phi_{T_{2}}\circ\alpha
.

Thus
\phi_{T}
gives a corresponding bijection for unlabeled trees.

Proof.

We start with the fact that every tree has either an edge or a vertex, called the
center
, which is fixed by every automorphism of the tree.
4
4

4

All that we really need is that every tree has either a vertex or an edge that is fixed by every automorphism of the tree; the center as we define it is a convenient way of constructing such an edge or vertex.

The center may obtained by successively removing every leaf (vertex of degree 1) and its incident edge until only a single vertex or edge remains.
(The center may also be characterized as the central vertex or edge on every longest path in the tree.)

Now suppose that
T^{w}
is a tree
T
rooted at a vertex or edge
w
. If
w
is the center of
T
then we define
\phi_{T}(T^{w})
to be
T
. Otherwise, there is a unique path from
w
to the center. Then
\phi_{T}(T^{w})
is
T
rooted at both
w
and the next vertex or edge on this path (which might be the center).

It is easy to describe the inverse of
\phi_{T}
:
\phi_{T}^{-1}(T)
is
T
rooted at its center, and
\phi_{T}^{-1}
of
T
rooted at a vertex
v

is
T
rooted at whichever of
v
and
e
is farther from the root.

Since the center of a tree is fixed by every automorphism of the tree, it follows that any isomorphism is fixed of trees takes centers to centers, thus paths to the center to paths to the center. Thus for any isomorphism
\alpha\colon T_{1}\to T_{2}
, we have
\alpha\circ\phi_{T_{1}}=\phi_{T_{2}}\circ\alpha
.
∎

Our main result on the enumeration of unrooted trees is a consequence of the dissymmetry theorem.

Theorem 4.3
.

Let
\mathscr{T}
be a class of trees closed under isomorphism. Let
t(x)
be the generating function for unlabeled
\mathscr{T}

t^{\bullet}(x)
,
t^{\raise 1.6pt\hbox{\kern 0.3pt{\vrule width=5.0pt,height=0.8pt}}}(x)
, and
t{{}^{\bullet\raise 1.6pt\hbox{\vrule width=4.0pt,height=0.8pt}}}(x)
be the generating functions for unlabeled
\mathscr{T}

t(x)=t^{\bullet}(x)+t^{\raise 1.6pt\hbox{\kern 0.3pt{\vrule width=5.0pt,height=0.8pt}}}(x)-t{{}^{\bullet\raise 1.6pt\hbox{\vrule width=4.0pt,height=0.8pt}}}(x).

Proof.

Theorem
4.2
implies that

t(x)+t{{}^{\bullet\raise 1.6pt\hbox{\vrule width=4.0pt,height=0.8pt}}}(x)=t^{\bullet}(x)+t^{\raise 1.6pt\hbox{\kern 0.3pt{\vrule width=5.0pt,height=0.8pt}}}(x).\qed

Applying Corollary
4.3

Theorem 4.4
.

The generating function
u(x)
for unrooted trees is given by

u(x)=r(x)-\frac{1}{2}\left(r(x)^{2}-r(x^{2})\right),

where

r(x)=x\exp\biggl(\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{r(x^{k})}{k}\biggr).

4.3

\mathscr{T}
to be the class of all trees. We need to compute

t^{\raise 1.6pt\hbox{\kern 0.3pt{\vrule width=5.0pt,height=0.8pt}}}(x)
and
t{{}^{\bullet\raise 1.6pt\hbox{\vrule width=4.0pt,height=0.8pt}}}(x)
.
We know that
t^{\bullet}(x)=r(x)
. If we remove the root edge
\{v,w\}
from a tree rooted at the edge
\{v,w\}
, we are left with a pair of trees. If we root these at
v
and
w

This bijection is compatible with isomorphism so it applies to unlabeled trees, and thus we have

t^{\raise 1.6pt\hbox{\kern 0.3pt{\vrule width=5.0pt,height=0.8pt}}}(x)=h_{2}[r(x)]=\frac{1}{2}\left(r(x)^{2}+r(x^{2})\right)

by Lemma

Similarly, if a tree is rooted at an edge
\{v,w\}

T_{1}
is rooted at

is rooted at
