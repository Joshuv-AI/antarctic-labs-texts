A matrix algebra approach to approximate Hessians
Warren Hare, Gabriel Jarry–Bolduc, Chayne Planiden

Abstract
This work presents a novel matrix-based method for constructing an approximation Hessian using only function evaluations. The method requires less computational power than interpolation-based methods and is easy to implement in matrix-based programming languages such as MATLAB. As only function evaluations are required, the method is suitable for use in derivative-free algorithms. For reasonably structured sample sets, the method is proven to create an order- 1 1 accurate approximation of the full Hessian. Under more specialized structures, the method is proved to yield order- 2 2 accuracy. The undetermined case, where the number of sample points is less than required for full interpolation, is studied and error bounds are developed for the resulting partial Hessians.

Hessian approximation; derivative-free optimization; order-
N
accuracy; generalized simplex gradient;generalized simplex Hessian; generalized centered simplex Hessian.

primary 65K10, 90C56.

Model-based DFO methods use numerical analysis techniques to approximate gradients and Hessians in a manner that has controllable error bounds. For example, a technique to approximate gradients is the simplex gradient. This method consists of taking a set of
n+1
sample points in
\operatorname{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
that are appropriately spaced, called a simplex, and using them to build a linear interpolation function that approximates the objective function locally, then calculating the gradient of the affine function. The simplex gradient has been shown to have a nicely bounded error, for functions whose gradients are locally Lipschitz continuous
[
10
]
.

One recent line of research explores methods of improving or generalizing the simplex gradient for its use in DFO

[

In a similar vein, researchers have also explored methods to approximate full Hessians or partial Hessians. In
[
21
]
, the authors outline an idea for a “simplex Hessian” that is constructed via
quadratic interpolation through
(n+1)(n+2)/2
well-poised sample
points. They further suggested that if only a portion of the Hessian
were desired (say the diagonal component), then fewer points could be used. These ideas were formalized in
[
16
]

[

In this work, we continue the development of these tools by introducing the
generalized simplex Hessian (GSH)
and the
generalized centered simplex Hessian (GCSH)

This paper can be viewed as an extension of the work related to simplex Hessians introduced in
[
17
,
21
]

f
. This formula provides several advantages. First, the formula provides an approach that is well-defined regardless of the number of sample points utilized. Indeed, as long as the matrices used to build the sample set of points are nonempty, the GSH and the GCSH are well-defined. In particular, the GSH provides an explicit formula to approximate the Hessian even when the quadratic interpolation function does not exist or is not unique. However, we note that when the matrices used in the computation of the GSH have a specific structure, the GSH is equivalent to a
forward-finite-difference approximation
of the Hessian.

The GSH provides an accurate approximation of the Hessian of an objective function
f:\operatorname{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\to\operatorname{\mathbb{R}}
under reasonable assumptions. The technique uses a point of interest
x^{0}
and sets of directions, stored in a set of matrices
\{S,T_{1},\ldots,T_{m}\}
whose columns are vectors that are added to the point of interest to build the sample points.
Defining
\Delta_{S}
as the radius of
S
and
\Delta_{T}
as the maximum radius of the
T_{i}
, we prove that if
S
and all
T_{i}
are full row rank and the Hessian
\nabla^{2}f
exists and is Lipschitz continuous, then the GSH is an accurate estimate of
\nabla^{2}f
to within a multiple of
\Delta_{u}:=\max\{\Delta_{S},\Delta_{T}\}
. In terms of order-
N
accuracy
[
24
]
, the GSH provides order-
1
accurate approximations of the full Hessian (see Definition
2.11
herein). Furthermore, the GCSH provides order-2 accurate approximations of the full Hessian.
Error bounds for the
underdetermined case
(see Definition
2.9
) are presented showing that the GSH and its centered version, the GCSH, are order-1 and order-2 accurate approximation of the appropriate partial Hessian, respectively.

The error bounds presented in this paper share similarities to those presented in
[
15
,
16
]
. In those papers, the authors provide error bounds for the Hessian of a fully quadratic model
[
15
, Theorem 3]
, the Hessian of a quadratic regression model
[
16
, Theorem 3.2]
, and the Hessian of a quadratic undetermined model
[
16

A second benefit of the GSH formula is that by employing a matrix structure in the definition, the Hessian approximation is extremely easy to implement in any matrix-based programming language. Moreover, after function evaluations are complete, the matrix structure requires less computational effort (in flops) than quadratic interpolation. Indeed, when

are square matrices, then the GSH requires order

Definition 2.1

(Poised for quadratic interpolation)
.

[
15
,
16
,
17

The set of distinct points

\displaystyle\alpha_{0}+\alpha^{\top}y^{i}+\frac{1}{2}(y^{i})^{\top}\mathbf{H}y^{i}=0,\quad i\in\{0,1,\dots,m\},

(1)

has a unique solution for

Therefore, the Hessian of the quadratic model defined through

is unique. Note that a set of sample points containing

1

is not poised for quadratic interpolation since the rank of the matrix associated to the system (
1
) is
5<6
. The set

\left\{\begin{bmatrix}0\\
0\end{bmatrix},\begin{bmatrix}1\\
0\end{bmatrix},\begin{bmatrix}0\\
1\end{bmatrix},\begin{bmatrix}-1\\
0\end{bmatrix},\begin{bmatrix}0\\
-1\end{bmatrix},\begin{bmatrix}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\\

) is 6. When the number of distinct sample points in

is fewer than

(see also

.

[

In the next definition, we introduce key notation used in the construction of the GSH. Within, we write a set of vectors in matrix form, by which we mean that each column of the matrix is a vector in the set.

Definition 2.3

(GSH notation)
.

Let
f:\operatorname{dom}f\subseteq\operatorname{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\to\operatorname{\mathbb{R}}
and let
x^{0}\in\operatorname{dom}f
be the
point of interest
. Let

\displaystyle S

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}s^{1}&s^{2}&\cdots&s^{m}\end{bmatrix}\in\operatorname{\mathbb{R}}^{n\times m}~~\mbox{and}

\displaystyle T_{i}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}t^{1}_{i}&t^{2}_{i}&\cdots&t^{k}_{i}\end{bmatrix}\in\operatorname{\mathbb{R}}^{n\times k_{i}},i\in\{1,\ldots,m\}

be sets of directions contained in
\operatorname{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
, written in matrix form. Define

T_{1:m}=\{T_{1},\ldots,T_{m}\}.

Assume that
x^{0}\oplus T_{i},x^{0}+s^{i},x^{0}+s^{i}\oplus T_{i}
are contained in
\operatorname{dom}f
for all
i~\in\{1,\ldots,m\}
. Define

\Delta_{S}=\max\limits_{i\,\in\{1,\ldots,m\}}\|s^{i}\|,\quad\Delta_{T_{i}}=\max\limits_{j\,\in\{1,\ldots,k_{i}\}}\|t^{j}_{i}\|,\quad\Delta_{T}=\max\limits_{i\in\{1,\ldots,m\}}\Delta_{T_{i}},

\displaystyle\widehat{S}=\frac{1}{\Delta_{S}}S,

(2)

and

\displaystyle\delta_{s}\left(x^{0};T_{i}\right)

\displaystyle=\left[\begin{array}[]{c}f(x^{0}+t^{1}_{i})-f(x^{0})\\
f(x^{0}+t^{2}_{i})-f(x^{0})\\
\vdots\\
f(x^{0}+t^{k}_{i})-f(x^{0})\end{array}\right]\in\operatorname{\mathbb{R}}^{k_{i}}.

In this paper, all matrices involved are assumed to have non-null rank. This ensures that the radius of the matrix is nonzero and hence, (
2
) is always well-defined.
Note that in the previous definition,
m\in\operatorname{\mathbb{N}}
and
k_{i}
can be any positive integer for all
i\in\{1,\dots,m\}
.

Recall that for nonsquare matrices, a generalization of the matrix inverse is the pseudoinverse. The most well-known type of matrix pseudoinverse is the Moore–Penrose pseudoinverse.

Definition 2.4

(Moore–Penrose pseudoinverse)
.

Let
A\in\operatorname{\mathbb{R}}^{n\times m}
. The
Moore–Penrose pseudoinverse
of
A
, denoted by
A^{\dagger}
, is the unique matrix in
\operatorname{\mathbb{R}}^{m\times n}
that satisfies the following four equations:

(i)

AA^{\dagger}A=A,

(ii)

A^{\dagger}AA^{\dagger}=A^{\dagger},

(iii)

(AA^{\dagger})^{\top}=AA^{\dagger},

(iv)

(A^{\dagger}A)^{\top}=A^{\dagger}A.

Note that given
A\in\operatorname{\mathbb{R}}^{n\times m},
there exists a unique Moore–Penrose pseudoinverse
A^{\dagger}\in\operatorname{\mathbb{R}}^{m\times n}.
The following two properties hold.

(i)

If
A
has full column rank
m
, then
A^{\dagger}
is a left-inverse of
A
, that is
A^{\dagger}A=\operatorname{Id}_{m}.
In this case,

A^{\dagger}=(A^{\top}A)^{-1}A^{\top}.

(3)

(ii)

If
A
has full row rank
n
, then
A^{\dagger}
is a right-inverse of
A
, that is
AA^{\dagger}=\operatorname{Id}_{n}.
In this case,
A^{\dagger}=A^{\top}(AA^{\top})^{-1}.

Before introducing the GSH, it is valuable to recall the definition of the generalized simplex gradient.

Definition 2.5

(Generalized simplex gradient)
.

[
25
, Definition 2]
Let
f:\operatorname{dom}f\subseteq\operatorname{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\to\operatorname{\mathbb{R}}
and let
x^{0}\in\operatorname{dom}f
be the point of interest. Let
S\in\operatorname{\mathbb{R}}^{n\times m}
with
x^{0}\oplus S\subset\operatorname{dom}f.
The
generalized simplex gradient
of
f
at
x^{0}
over
S
is denoted by
\nabla_{s}f(x^{0};S)
and defined by

\nabla_{s}f(x^{0};S)=(S^{\top})^{\dagger}\delta_{s}(x^{0};S).

The Moore–Penrose pseudoinverse allows for
S
to contain any finite number of columns, rather than being restricted to
m=n
. When
S\in\operatorname{\mathbb{R}}^{n\times n}
and
\operatorname{rank}S=n
, then the Moore–Penrose pseudoinverse of
S^{\top}
is the inverse of
S^{\top}
and we recover the definition of the simplex gradient
[
3
, Definition 9.5]
. Note that the generalized simplex gradient is a generalization of the familiar
forward-finite-difference approximation
of the gradient
[
12
, Section 4.1]
. Indeed, if
S=H\operatorname{Id}_{n},
where
H=\operatorname{Diag}\begin{bmatrix}h_{1}&h_{2}&\dots&h_{n}\end{bmatrix},h_{i}>0,
then we recover the forward-finite-difference approximation of the gradient.

We now introduce the two key definitions for this paper, the generalized simplex Hessian and the generalized centered simplex Hessian. It requires finite sets of directions and a point of interest at which the Hessian is approximated. Similarly to the generalized simplex gradient, the GSH involves the Moore–Penrose pseudoinverse and a difference matrix. In this case, the difference matrix consists of the difference between generalized simplex gradients.

For ease of notation, we use the definition
T_{1:m}=\{T_{1},\ldots,T_{m}\}
where convenient.

Definition 2.6

(Generalized simplex Hessian)
.

Let
f:\operatorname{dom}f\subseteq\operatorname{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\to\operatorname{\mathbb{R}}
and let
x^{0}\in\operatorname{dom}f
be the point of interest. Let
S=\begin{bmatrix}s^{1}&s^{2}&\cdots&s^{m}\end{bmatrix}\in\operatorname{\mathbb{R}}^{n\times m}
and
T_{i}\in\operatorname{\mathbb{R}}^{n\times k_{i}}
with
x^{0}\oplus T_{i},x^{0}\oplus S,x^{0}+s^{i}\oplus T_{i}
contained in
\operatorname{dom}f
for all
i\in\{1,\dots,m\}.
The
generalized simplex Hessian
of
f
at
x^{0}
over
S
and
T_{1:m}
is denoted by
\nabla^{2}_{s}f(x^{0};S,T_{1:m})
and defined by

\nabla^{2}_{s}f(x^{0};S,T_{1:m})=(S^{\top})^{\dagger}\delta^{2}_{s}(x^{0};S,T_{1:m}),

(4)

where

\delta^{2}_{s}(x^{0};S;T_{1:m})=\left[\begin{array}[]{c}(\nabla_{s}f(x^{0}+s^{1};T_{1})-\nabla_{s}f(x^{0};T_{1}))^{\top}\\
(\nabla_{s}f(x^{0}+s^{2};T_{2})-\nabla_{s}f(x^{0};T_{2}))^{\top}\\
\vdots\\
(\nabla_{s}f(x^{0}+s^{m};T_{m})-\nabla_{s}f(x^{0};T_{m}))^{\top}\end{array}\right]\in\operatorname{\mathbb{R}}^{m\times n}.

In the case
T_{1}=T_{2}=\cdots=T_{m}
, we use
\overline{T}:=T_{i}
to simplify notation and write
\nabla^{2}_{s}f(x^{0};S;\overline{T})
to emphasize the special case.

We remark that the
s
in
\nabla^{2}_{s}f,\delta_{s}
and
\delta^{2}_{s}
is for simplex. This becomes important as we now introduce the generalized centered simplex Hessian. The ‘square’ in
\delta^{2}_{s}
emphasizes the fact that we are taking differences of first-order objects (hence getting a second-order object).

Definition 2.7

(Generalized centered simplex Hessian)
.

Let
f:\operatorname{dom}f\subseteq\operatorname{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\to\operatorname{\mathbb{R}}
and let
x^{0}\in\operatorname{dom}f
be the point of interest. Let
S\in\operatorname{\mathbb{R}}^{n\times m}
and
T_{i}\in\operatorname{\mathbb{R}}^{n\times k_{i}}
with
x^{0}\oplus S\oplus T_{i},x^{0}\oplus(-S)\oplus(-T_{i}),x^{0}\oplus(\pm S),
and
x^{0}\oplus(\pm T_{i})
contained in
\operatorname{dom}f
for all
i\in\{1,\dots,m\}.
The generalized centered simplex Hessian of
f
at
x^{0}
over
S
and
T_{1:m}
is denoted by
\nabla^{2}_{c}f(x^{0};S,T_{1:m})
and defined by

\nabla^{2}_{c}f(x^{0};S,T_{1:m})=\frac{1}{2}\left(\nabla_{s}^{2}f(x^{0};S,T_{1:m})+\nabla_{s}^{2}f(x^{0};-S,-T_{1:m})\right).

(5)

Note that the
c
in
\nabla^{2}_{c}f
is for centered.

The GCSH can be viewed as a generalization of the centered-finite difference approximation of the Hessian, also called midpoint approximation
[
12
, Section 4.1]
. Similarly, The GSH can be viewed as a generalization of the forward-finite-difference approximation of the Hessian
[
12
, Section 4.1]
. Indeed, when
S=T_{1}=\dots=T_{m}=H\operatorname{Id}_{n},
where
H=\operatorname{Diag}\begin{bmatrix}h_{1}&h_{2}&\dots&h_{n}\end{bmatrix},h_{i}>0,
then the GSH (GCSH) is equivalent to a forward-finite-difference approximation (centered-finite-difference approximation) of the second-order derivatives. Moreover, the GSH can be viewed as the DFO analog of the Hessian approximation given by Taylor’s Theorem in the derivative-based context, which approximates the gradient by differences of true gradients (see
[
37
, Section 8.1]
). An advantage of the GSH (GCSH) over forward-finite-difference approximations (centered-finite-difference approximations) is that it is less restrictive. In particular, it is well-defined as long as the matrices
S
and
T_{1},\dots,T_{m}
are nonempty.

Remark 2.8
.

Consider the case where
S
and
T_{i}
are square matrices, i.e.,
m=n
and
k_{i}=n
for all
i\in\{1,\dots,m\}.
The computation of the GSH begins by the construction of
n+1
generalized simplex gradients. Setting aside function evaluations, computing a simplex gradient is dominated by computing a matrix inverse, which is
O(n^{3})
flops. Thus the construction of
\delta^{2}_{s}(x^{0};S;T_{1:m})
requires
O(n^{4})
flops. The inverse of
S
is computed (
O(n^{3})
flops) and multiplied by
\delta^{2}_{s}(x^{0};S;T_{1:m})
,
O(n^{3})
flops. Hence, setting aside function evaluations, computing the GSH requires
O(n^{4})
flops.

Conversely, setting aside function evaluations, quadratic interpolation requires
O(n^{6})
flops to compute.

Definition 2.9
.

Let
f:\operatorname{dom}f\subseteq\operatorname{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\to\operatorname{\mathbb{R}}
and let
x^{0}\in\operatorname{dom}f
be the point of interest. Let
S=\begin{bmatrix}s^{1}&s^{2}&\cdots&s^{m}\end{bmatrix}\in\operatorname{\mathbb{R}}^{n\times m}
and
T_{i}\in\operatorname{\mathbb{R}}^{n\times k_{i}}
with
x^{0}\oplus S\oplus T_{i},x^{0}\oplus(-S)\oplus(-T_{i}),x^{0}\oplus(\pm S),
and
x^{0}\oplus(\pm T_{i})
contained in
\operatorname{dom}f
for all
i\in\{1,\dots,m\}.
Assume that all matrices are non-null rank.
We define the following four cases to characterize the matrix
S\in\operatorname{\mathbb{R}}^{n\times m}
and the set of matrices
T_{1:m}:=\{T_{1},\dots,T_{m}\}.

•

Underdetermined: the GSH (GCSH) is said to be
S
-underdetermined if
S
is non-square and full column rank.
We say that it is

-underdetermined if all matrices in the set

are full column rank and at least one matrix is non-square.

•

Determined: the GSH (GCSH) is said to be
S
-determined if

\overline{T}
are full rank and
\operatorname{\mathcal{S}}(x^{0};S,\overline{T})
contains exactly
(n+1)(n+2)/2
distinct points.

This definition requires
S
and
\overline{T}
to have exactly
n
columns and to be full rank. This is the case where the GSH is
S
-determined and
\overline{T}
-determined. This implies that
S
and
\overline{T}
are of minimal size to ensure that the GSH is an order-1 accurate approximation of the full Hessian.

Note that a minimal poised set for the GCSH could be defined in a similar manner. Obviously, such a set must contain more than
(n+1)(n+2)/2.
An investigation of this topic is left as a future research direction.
We next show that it is possible to create a minimal poised set for the GSH.

Proposition 5.3
.

Let
x^{0}
be the point of interest. Let
S=\begin{bmatrix}s^{1}&s^{2}&\cdots&s^{n}\end{bmatrix}\in\operatorname{\mathbb{R}}^{n\times n}
. Define the set
U_{\ell}
for each index
\ell\in\{0,1,\ldots,n\}
as

U_{0}=S

and

U_{\ell}=\begin{bmatrix}s^{1}-s^{\ell}&s^{2}-s^{\ell}&\cdots&s^{\ell-1}-s^{\ell}&-s^{\ell}&s^{\ell+1}-s^{\ell}&\cdots&s^{n}-s^{\ell}\end{bmatrix}\in\operatorname{\mathbb{R}}^{n\times n},\ell\neq 0.

Then for each
\ell
,
|\operatorname{\mathcal{S}}(x^{0};S,U_{\ell})|\leq(n+1)(n+2)/2
. Moreover, if
S
has full rank, then
|\operatorname{\mathcal{S}}(x^{0};S,U_{\ell})|=(n+1)(n+2)/2
.

Proof.

Without loss of generality, let
x^{0}=\mathbf{0}
. First, suppose
\ell\in\{1,2,\dots,n\}.
For arbitrary function
f
, consider the matrix
\delta^{2}_{s}(x^{0};S,\overline{T})
where
\overline{T}=U_{\ell}.
The computation of
\nabla_{s}f(x^{0};U_{\ell})
evaluates
f
at the points

\begin{array}[]{l}~~~\{\mathbf{0},(s^{1}-s^{\ell}),\dots,(s^{\ell-1}-s^{\ell}),-s^{\ell},(s^{\ell+1}-s^{\ell}),\dots,(s^{n}-s^{\ell})\}\\
=\{\mathbf{0}\}\cup\{-s^{\ell}\}\cup\bigcup\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}i=1\\
i\neq\ell\end{subarray}}^{n}\left\{s^{i}-s^{\ell}\right\}.\end{array}

For
i\neq\ell
, the computation of
\nabla_{s}f(x^{0}+s^{i};U_{k})
evaluates
f
at the points

\begin{array}[]{l}~~~\{s^{i},s^{i}+(s^{1}-s^{\ell}),\dots,s^{i}+(s^{\ell-1}-s^{\ell}),s^{i}-s^{\ell},s^{i}+(s^{\ell+1}-s^{\ell}),\dots,s^{i}+(s^{n}-s^{\ell})\}\\
=\{s^{i}\}\cup\{s^{i}-s^{\ell}\}\cup\bigcup\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=1\\
j\neq\ell\end{subarray}}^{n}\{s^{i}+s^{j}-s^{\ell}\}.\end{array}

The computation of
\nabla_{s}f(x^{0}+s^{\ell};U_{\ell})
evaluates
f
at the points

\begin{array}[]{l}~~~\{s^{\ell},s^{\ell}+(s^{1}-s^{\ell}),\dots,s^{\ell}+(s^{\ell-1}-s^{\ell}),s^{\ell}-s^{\ell},s^{\ell}+(s^{\ell+1}-s^{\ell}),\dots,s^{\ell}+(s^{n}-s^{\ell})\}\\
=\{s^{\ell}\}\cup\{\mathbf{0}\}\cup\bigcup\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}i=1\\
i\neq\ell\end{subarray}}^{n}\{s^{i}\}=\{\mathbf{0}\}\cup S.\end{array}

Thus,
f
is evaluated at the points

\begin{array}[]{l}\tiny{\bigg(\{\mathbf{0}\}\cup\{-s^{\ell}\}\cup\bigcup\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}i=1\\
i\neq\ell\end{subarray}}^{n}\left\{s^{i}-s^{\ell}\right\}\bigg)\cup\bigg(\bigcup\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}i=1\\
i\neq\ell\end{subarray}}^{n}\{s^{i}\}\cup\bigcup\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}i=1\\
i\neq\ell\end{subarray}}^{n}\{s^{i}-s^{\ell}\}\cup\bigcup\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}i=1\\
i\neq\ell\end{subarray}}^{n}\bigcup\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}j\geq i\\
j\neq\ell\end{subarray}}\{s^{i}+s^{j}-s^{\ell}\}\bigg)\cup\bigg(\{\mathbf{0}\}\cup S\bigg)}\\

i\neq\ell\end{subarray}}^{n}\left\{s^{i}-s^{\ell}\right\}\cup\bigcup\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}i=1\\
i\neq\ell\end{subarray}}^{n}\bigcup\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}j\geq i\\
j\neq\ell\end{subarray}}\{s^{i}+s^{j}-s^{\ell}\}.\end{array}

(22)

This is at most
1+n+1+(n-1)+(n-1)(n-2)/2=(n+1)(n+2)/2
points.

Now, suppose
\ell=0.
Using a similar process to the above, we find that
f
is evaluated at the points

\begin{array}[]{l}\bigg(\{\mathbf{0}\}\cup\bigcup\limits_{i=1}^{n}\left\{s^{i}\right\}\bigg)~\cup~\bigg(\bigcup\limits_{i=1}^{n}\{2s^{i}\}\cup\bigcup\limits_{i=1}^{n}\bigcup\limits_{j>i}\{s^{i}+s^{j}\}\bigg).\end{array}

(23)

This is at most
(n+1)(n+2)/2
points.

Finally, if

is full rank, then the four sets in (

function evaluations.∎

Proposition 5.7
.

.

for

, assume that

1
) is the trivial solution.
Considering the point
\mathbf{0}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\alpha_{0}

\displaystyle=0.

Considering the points
s^{i}
for

and noting that for all

\displaystyle\overline{\alpha}^{\top}e^{i}+\frac{1}{2}(e^{i})^{\top}\widehat{\mathbf{H}}e^{i}

is symmetric.
Considering the points

\displaystyle-\overline{\alpha}^{\top}e^{n}-(e^{i})^{\top}\widehat{\mathbf{H}}e^{n}+\frac{1}{2}(e^{n})^{\top}\widehat{\mathbf{H}}e^{n}=0.

\displaystyle-(e^{i})^{\top}\widehat{\mathbf{H}}e^{n}=0\quad\mbox{for }~i\in\{1,2,\ldots,n-1\}.

\displaystyle\overline{\alpha}^{\top}e^{n}+\frac{1}{2}(e^{n})^{\top}\widehat{\mathbf{H}}e^{n}=0=\overline{\alpha}^{\top}e^{n}-\frac{1}{2}(e^{n})^{\top}\widehat{\mathbf{H}}e^{n},

6
Conclusion

We have presented the generalized simplex Hessian (GSH), a compact and simple formula for approximating the Hessian of a function
f
. The GSH is well-defined as long as the matrices
S
and
T_{i}
are nonempty. In particular, the matrices
S
and
T_{i}
do not need to be full row rank nor square to compute the GSH. We have also presented a centered version of the GSH called the generalized centered simplex Hessian (GCSH) and shown its relation to the GSH (Section
3
).

In Theorems
4.2
and
4.3
, we developed error bounds for the GSH and GCSH. Under some assumptions , the GSH is an order-1 accurate approximation of the partial Hessian
\operatorname{Proj}_{S,T_{1:m}}\nabla^{2}f(x^{0})
, and the GCSH is an order-2 accurate approximation of the partial Hessian
\operatorname{Proj}_{S,T_{1:m}}\nabla^{2}f(x^{0})
. Indeed, when all matrices of directions in the set
T_{1:m}
are equal, then we obtain error bounds that covers all possible 16 cases defined in Definition
2.9
. When all matrices
T_{i}
are not equal, then 12 out of 16 cases are covered. Future research directions include an investigation of the cases not covered in Theorems
4.2
and
4.3
: the four cases
S
-nondetermined \overdetermined and
T_{1:m}
-nondetermined\underdetermined.

We note that when a matrix is nondetermined or overdetermined, it is possible to remove columns (or rows) of the matrix to make it full rank. This may lead to error bounds for the nondetermined cases.

As the results in this paper are reported in terms of order-
N
accuracy, it is possible to apply the theory of DFO calculus developed in
[
13
]
directly. In
[
13
]
, approximation based product, quotient, and chain rules are developed. For each rule, it is shown that when an objective function can be decomposed into a product, quotient, or composition of simpler functions, then it is possible to construct approximate Hessians of the simpler functions and then use those to construct approximation Hessians of the objective function. Combining
[
13
]
with the research herein has lead to a model-based trust region algorithm using the calculus-based approximation techniques
[
26
]
.

In Section
5
, we showed that if a minimal poised set for the GSH is used, the number of distinct function evaluations required to compute the GSH is
(n+1)(n+2)/2.
We investigated the relationship between a minimal poised set for the GSH and poisedness for quadratic interpolation, proving that a minimal poised set for the GSH of the form
\operatorname{\mathcal{M}}(x^{0};S,U_{\ell})
is well-poised for quadratic interpolation, but that the converse does not necessarily hold. In this case, we know that
\nabla^{2}_{s}f(x^{0};S,\overline{T})
with
\overline{T}=U_{\ell}
is equal to the Hessian of the quadratic interpolation function
Q_{f}(x^{0};S,U_{\ell})
. We also developed explicit formulae for obtaining the parameters of the quadratic interpolation function of
f
over a minimal poised set for the GSH of the form
\operatorname{\mathcal{M}}(x^{0};S,U_{\ell})
.

Future research should explore if the sets
U_{0},U_{1},\dots,U_{n}
as defined in Proposition
5.3
are the only possible choices for
\overline{T}\in\operatorname{\mathbb{R}}^{n\times n}
such that
\operatorname{\mathcal{S}}(x^{0};S,\overline{T}),
where
S\in\operatorname{\mathbb{R}}^{n\times n}
is full rank, is a minimal poised set for GSH at
x^{0}
. It can be proven that it is indeed the case by using brute force in
\operatorname{\mathbb{R}}
and
\operatorname{\mathbb{R}}^{2},
but it is still unclear how to generalized this claim in an arbitrary dimension
n
.

While it is immediately clear that a minimal poised set for GSH can be used to construct a ‘small’ set for the GCSH (using Proposition
3.3
), it is not clear if such a set would be minimal. Future research will investigate the properties of a minimal poised set for the GCSH and the matrices of directions that can be used to minimize the number of sample points.

It is known that the diagonal entries of the Hessian may be obtained for free in terms of function evaluations when a centered simplex gradient is computed. Hence, a future research direction could be to verify if the main diagonal entries of
\nabla^{3}f(x^{0})
may be obtained for free when a GCSH is computed.

On a final note, MATLAB implementations of the GSH and the GCSH are available upon request.
