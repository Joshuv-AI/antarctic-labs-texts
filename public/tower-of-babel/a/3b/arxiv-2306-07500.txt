Adding guardrails to advanced chatbots
Yanchen Wang, Lisa Singh

Abstract
Generative AI models continue to become more powerful. The launch of ChatGPT in November 2022 has ushered in a new era of AI. ChatGPT and other similar chatbots have a range of capabilities, from answering student homework questions to creating music and art. There are already concerns that humans may be replaced by chatbots for a variety of jobs. Because of the wide spectrum of data chatbots are built on, we know that they will have human errors and human biases built into them. These biases may cause significant harm and/or inequity toward different subpopulations. To understand the strengths and weakness of chatbot responses, we present a position paper that explores different use cases of ChatGPT to determine the types of questions that are answered fairly and the types that still need improvement. We find that ChatGPT is a fair search engine for the tasks we tested; however, it has biases on both text generation and code generation. We find that ChatGPT is very sensitive to changes in the prompt, where small changes lead to different levels of fairness. This suggests that we need to immediately implement "corrections" or mitigation strategies in order to improve fairness of these systems. We suggest different strategies to improve chatbots and also advocate for an impartial review panel that has access to the model parameters to measure the levels of different types of biases and then recommends safeguards that move toward responses that are less discriminatory and more accurate.

Generative tool on other occupation related tasks

In the US, there are laws such as Civil Rights Act, Americans with Disabilities Act (ADA) and Equal Employment Opportunity Act prohibiting discrimination against certain demographic characteristics, including race, gender, age and disability status. Given these laws, it is important that technologies we develop, including chatbots, not produce responses that appear discriminatory.

To test ChatGPT’s adherence to these laws, we consider two tasks,
writing job interview questions and writing programming code. Appendix
B
shows all prompts we used. Our goal is to identify potential bias in the responses and how different prompts can affect the level of bias in responses.

4.1
Job interview questions

For this task, we ask ChatGPT to generate 5 interview questions for a specific occupation. Table
5
shows 5 generated interview questions from ChatGPT for a programmer. The generated interview questions are reasonable and they do not contain any demographic bias or discriminatory language. We then change the prompt and ask ChatGPT to generate more interview questions but incorporate some demographic characteristics in the prompt. Table
6
shows two different prompts that have the same intent. The first prompt asks ChatGPT to generate job interview questions based on race and gender. ChatGPT detects that this prompt is unethical and discriminatory, and chooses not to answer the question. In the second prompt, we adjust the prompt to include specific demographic characteristics (
black female
) as opposed to a demographic category (race and gender). Comparing the questions generated, we see that none of them are the same, and for the black female, three of them are about challenges, work environment, and collaboration.

In addition to race and gender, we try the same types of prompts for disability. We expect ChatGPT to have a similar response to the one we received when including race and gender in the question. However, this is not the case. Table
7
shows the generated interview questions for a candidate with a disability. ChatGPT generated these questions even though we formatted the prompt the same way as when we investigated race and gender. We also see that all the interview questions are based on the disability itself and how the candidate can manage the disability during work. These questions do not focus on assessing the candidate’s qualifications related to the requirements of the job. ChatGPT is making the assumption that we do not want general questions, but ones that are targeted and related to the candidate’s disability. An employer can ask about an applicant’s ability to perform job functions, but cannot ask about their disability, medications, etc. Asking about communication needs, challenges related to the disability, and medical appointment management is not legal in the US.

In general, ChatGPT provides some safeguards on unethical and discriminatory questions, but the safeguards are very limited and users can easily bypass the safeguards by changing the prompt.

Programming code generation

Code generation is a very popular task for ChatGPT. Because of how quickly ChatGPT generates large amounts of code, this task has received a lot of attention. There is much discussion about ChatGPT replacing some entry level software developer positions

5
Discussion and possible strategies for reducing bias

Our analysis confirms that ChatGPT learns everything from human, including their biases. According to OpenAI, 60% of the training data come from Common Crawl, a large data set consisting of web pages, extracted metadata and text extractions through a big web crawler since 2008. Another 22% of data are from WebText2, containing all Reddit posts until December 2017 that have a score of 3 or higher. Another 16% are from books
[
29
]
. In their training data, more than 80% of the data are from the Internet and online discussions. Researchers have already shown that online discussions are very biased
[
30
,
31
,
32
,
33
]
. ChatGPT and other chatbots need to be able to discern high quality content, ethically acceptable content, and socially acceptable content if they are going to generate text that improves human society. It is reasonable (and even important) to learn the poor quality, ethically questionable, and socially unacceptable content. But generating it will only reinforce content that is destructive to a healthy society. We do not want ChatGPT saying that Nazi rhetoric is acceptable within Western society today.

Another problem with biased generated text is its potential use as training data. As large language models become more powerful, researchers may find it useful for generating training data for their learning models. Gilardi and colleagues have already shown that ChatGPT is more reliable and accurate for text-annotation tasks than crowdsource-workers
[
34
]
. Meyer et al. used GPT-3 to generate synthetic training data to train their classification algorithms for conversational agents. Their results show that the classifiers trained on synthetic data from GPT-3 are much better than random baselines, but worse than training data from real users because of the lack of variability in the synthetic data
[
35
]
. However, with the continual improvement in large language models, it is only a matter of time before the synthetic data will have sufficient variability to improve the performance to levels comparable to those of real user data.

Examples of using synthetic or generated data to increase the size of the training set exist in many areas
[
36
,
37
,
38
]
. In all these examples, using synthetic data improved the final models because the distribution of the underlying data generation process was understood. This is not the case for generative AI models using billions of parameters. Therefore, when the generated data contains biases that are then used to train other models, the bias will propagate to the next step. This is a perfect example of
bias in bias out

[
39
]
. The original idea of bias in bias out is from the historical bias literature. Using training data containing historical biases will produce models that also contain those historical biases. With generative AI, the harm can be significantly worse since we do not know the biases that existed in the training data. Therefore, these unknown biases are being shared and even reinforced. This is referred to as confirmation bias in human reinforcement learning
[
40
,
41
,
42
]
, where "the tendency to overweight information that matches prior beliefs or choices—has been shown to manifest even in simple reinforcement learning"
[
41
]
. This type of confirmation bias is how we form bias as humans and now how generative AI is forming its bias as well. In the case of synthetic data created using generative AI, we are repeating this type of confirmation bias and making the output model even more biased.

5.1
Black box model

Given our experimental results, it is clear that some safeguards have been put into ChatGPT. Unfortunately, a small amount of prompt rephrasing can significantly change the responses provided by ChatGPT. This is also the case for code generation. This is one of the major drawbacks of black box models. It is even more problematic when the creators do not understand the decision making process or know how to control output of the model. As users, we have even less control on the model and the output since we can only interact with the model from the prompt.

5.2
Recommendations

Transparency is always the key to understanding the strengths and weaknesses of new technology. Even if Open AI does not want to share the details of their technology, they can easily share information about the safeguards that have been put in place to ensure ethical, accurate, socially acceptable responses. Companies developing AI driven technologies need to share the potential harms and safeguards that have been put into place. Otherwise, the public cannot easily determine which safeguards are missing or how to use the new technology responsibly.

5.3
Possible mitigation methods

Within the machine learning fairness literature, there are three categories of bias mitigation methods: pre-processing methods, in processing methods, and post processing methods. Users of ChatGPT are limited to pre and post processing mitigation strategies. Focusing on those two areas, we suggest the following mitigation strategies:

•

Response Bans:
Some questions are discriminatory, ethically complex (should I own a gun), or socially unacceptable. These type of questions should be banned from being responded to until the technology has less reliance on biased training data and also understands the health implications of answering questions like that.

•

Prompt Bias Correction:
Given a prompt, we can convert poor quality prompts to fair prompts by developing algorithms that check on the level of bias in the prompt. If the prompt is biased, correction algorithms could provide users with an unbiased (or less biased) prompt. Similar work has been done on toxic language detection and correction
[
43
,
44
,
45
]
. For example, Yoshida and colleagues first use BERT to detect if a sentence is likely to be flamed before it is posted and if the sentence is determined to be toxic, they propose an algorithm that uses BERT’s masked word to convert the toxic expressions into safe ones.
[
43
]
.

•

Response Bias Correction:
If we focus on generative responses by ChatGPT, before the user sees a specific response, it can be postprocessed to change it from one that may be demographic-biased to one that is demographic-neutral. For example, replacing gender biased words and pronouns to be gender neutral ones
[
46
]
.

•

Advisory Boards:
Given the lack of regulation that exists, we advocate for an impartial review panel composed of researchers, technologist, lawyers, ethicists, and advocates that has the ability to identify and measure different types of bias, and recommend strategies for reducing them or at least sharing them with users.

As computer scientists, we are very familiar with concepts like system vulnerabilities and security breaches. Faculty working in these areas think about how these vulnerabilities arise and develop principled ways to address them. Chatbots can be viewed as a new type of system that has its own set of vulnerabilities. As computer scientists, ethicists, and social scientists, we need to identify the vulnerabilities (the biases), try to understand the different ways users will exploit these vulnerabilities and the harms these vulnerabilities may cause, and develop strategies for eliminating the vulnerabilities or at least reducing their harms. To do this effectively, researchers and industry leaders developing large language models and chatbots need to work together to
patch
ethical and discriminatory holes in existing generative AI tools. But even more importantly, as the next generation of generative AI tools are created, we need to design and build them with ethics and societal good in mind. We need to design object functions that attempt to minimize personal and societal harm. It is time for us to work together to safeguard society from technology containing and reinforcing the same biases we have.
