Perturbative Steady States of Completely Positive Quantum Master Equations
Jae Sung Lee, Joonhyun Yeo

Abstract
The Lindblad form guarantees complete positivity of a Markovian quantum master equation (QME). However, its microscopic derivation for a quantum system weakly interacting with a thermal bath requires several approximations, which may result in inaccuracies in the QME. Recently, various Lindbladian QMEs were derived without resorting to the secular approximation from the Redfield equation which does not guarantee the complete positivity. Here, we explicitly calculate, in a perturbative manner, the equilibrium steady states of these Lindbladian QMEs. We compare the results with the steady state of the Redfield equation obtained from an analytic continuation method, which coincides with the so-called mean force Gibbs (MFG) state. The MFG state is obtained by integrating out the bath degrees of freedom for the Gibbs state of the total Hamiltonian. We explicitly show that the steady states of the Lindbladian QMEs are different from the MFG state. Our results indicate that manipulations of the Redfield equation needed to enforce complete positivity of a QME drives its steady state away from the MFG state. We also find that, in the high-temperature regime, both the steady states of the Lindbladian QMEs and MFG state reduce to the same Gibbs state of a system Hamiltonian under certain conditions.



I
Introduction

Many interesting fundamental and practical physical problems arise in open quantum systems
breuer
;
rivas
interacting with an environment acting as a thermal bath. One of the most widely used equations to study open quantum systems is the quantum master equation (QME), which describes the temporal evolution of the reduced density operator of the system. The reduced density operator is obtained by tracing over the variables describing the environment, for which an explicit description of dynamics is not usually feasible and often not of interest.

Various forms of QMEs can be derived depending on the underlying physical situation or assumptions. When a system is weakly coupled to the environment, the Born-Markov approximation can be applied
breuer
;
rivas
, from which the Redfield equation can be derived
redfield
. This master equation can also be obtained from the more formal Nakajima-Zwanzig time-convolutionless “projection operator perturbation expansion”
nakajima
;
zwanzig
via truncation at the second order of the interaction strength between the system and bath. However, the Redfield equation does not guarantee complete positivity of the density matrix, which may give rise to unphysical solutions. The most general form of the Markovian QME that preserves the positivity is known as the Lindblad equation
lindblad
;
gks
. A weak coupling limit
davies
;
spohn
, which amounts to the so-called secular approximation, is needed to obtain the QME in Lindblad form from the Redfield equation.

The secular approximation is usually valid in a quantum optics setting, where the energy level spacings are sufficiently large, but fails when there are nearly degenerate energy levels. Recently, many attempts have been made
ule
;
becker
;
trush
;
david
;
potts
;
becker2
to derive a completely positive QME in Lindblad form from the Redfield equation without resorting to secular approximation. These studies demonstrate that there is no specific way to derive a QME in Lindblad form: as noted in Ref.
ule
, there is a continuous family of Markovian QMEs within the same order of precision. This naturally raises the question of which QME is most appropriate to describe a given open quantum system, and the physical basis on which a given QME can be justified.

One way to examine the appropriateness of QMEs is to study their thermodynamic behavior
binder
, and especially their steady states. Here, we focus on the
equilibrium
steady state of QMEs when the system is coupled with a single thermal bath. It is well known
breuer
that the Lindblad equation obtained from secular approximation results in the Gibbs state of the system Hamiltonian as a steady state. However, if we accept that the total system, consisting of the system of interest and the bath, thermalizes, for example by satisfying the condition required for the eigenstate thermalization hypothesis
sred
;
deutsch
;
Huse
;
Rigol
, the steady state of the reduced density operator will be the so-called mean force Gibbs (MFG) state
mfg
;
cresser
;
subasi
;
lobejko
, which is obtained by tracing the Gibbs state of the total Hamiltonian over the bath variables
Muller
;
Liu
;
Garrison
. Therefore, the Lindblad equation is not consistent with the thermalization property because it neglects the effect of coupling to the bath. In comparison, the Redfield equation correctly takes the MFG state, expanded up to the second order of coupling between the system and bath, as its steady state
thingna
. The perturbative evaluation of the steady state of the Redfield equation has some subtleties, since only the second-order coherence (off-diagonal) components can be determined self-consistently
mori
;
fleming
;
geva
. In general, correction to the population (diagonal) components in the steady state requires fourth-order expansion of the Redfield equation
mori
;
fleming
;
geva
. However, the analytic continuation method, which has been
developed in Ref.
thingna
and applied to various situations in Ref.
thingna2
;
xu
, provides a
convenient
way to evaluate the second-order population within the Redfield equation; the result coincides with the second-order coherence and population of the MFG state
thingna
.

In this respect, the steady states of the completely positive QMEs mentioned above, which are obtained without resorting to secular approximation, are of great interest. It was recently noted
tupkary
that any modification of the Redfield equation to achieve complete positivity inevitably changes the perturbative steady state from the MFG state. In this paper, we explicitly evaluate the steady states of these Lindbladian QMEs perturbatively. We focus on two prominent examples: the universal Lindblad equation (ULE)
ule
;
nathan
and truncated Lindblad equation (TLE)
becker
. We use the analytic continuation method of Ref.
thingna
to evaluate the second-order population, as well as the coherence. We find that the Gibbs state of the system Hamiltonian is not a steady state, but the effect of coupling to the bath is included in the solution (unlike the case of the secular approximation). We explicitly compare the steady states of these QMEs with the MFG state. We find that although the steady states of these Lindbladian equations capture the effect of coupling to the bath, they differ from the MFG state. This can be regarded as a tradeoff for Lindbladian QMEs derived from the Redfield equation. To ensure that the coupling effect persists, as in the MFG state, complete positivity must be sacrificed and vice versa.

We also evaluate the steady states of QMEs in a high-temperature regime where the order of the inverse temperature is similar to the system-bath coupling strength. From this calculation, we find that the equilibrium steady states of various QMEs can become the Gibbs state of the system Hamiltonian up to the second order of coupling strength under certain conditions. Therefore, the steady state of the conventional Lindblad equation is also acceptable in this high-temperature regime, even when the system–bath coupling strength is weak but finite.

This paper is organized as follows. In Section II, we describe how to calculate the steady state of the Redfield equation and MFG state perturbatively. In Section III, we calculate the steady states of the ULE and TLE up to the second order of coupling strength. We conclude the paper in Section IV.

II
Redfield equation and its steady state

In this section we review the calculation of perturbative steady states of the Redfield equation.
We consider a quantum mechanical system interacting with the bath described by the Hamiltonians
H_{\rm S}

and
H_{\rm B}
, respectively. The total Hamiltonian is written as

\displaystyle H=H_{\rm S}+H_{\rm B}+H_{\rm I},

(1)

where the interaction between the system and the bath is governed by
H_{\rm I}
.
We assume that the initial state is given by the product of the system and bath
states as
\rho(0)\otimes\rho_{\rm B}
, where
\rho_{\rm B}
describes the
equilibrium state of the bath with the inverse temperature
\beta
.
The state of the system at time

is given by the reduced density operator (
\hbar=1
)

\displaystyle\rho(t)=\mathrm{Tr}_{\rm B}(e^{-iHt}\rho(0)\otimes\rho_{B}e^{iHt}),

(2)

where the partial trace over the bath is performed.

In general, a QME describes the time evolution of the
reduced density operator expressed as

\displaystyle\frac{d\rho(t)}{dt}=\mathcal{L}[\rho(t)].

(3)

Various forms of the superoperator
\mathcal{L}
can be derived especially in the regime where
the coupling between the system and the bath is weak.
The Redfield equation can either be obtained
by applying the Born-Markov approximation or by using the time-convolutionless projector operator formalism

;
rivas
.
In the interaction picture, denoted by tilde as
\tilde{\rho}(t)=e^{iH_{\rm S}t}\rho(t)e^{-iH_{\rm S}t}
and
\tilde{H}_{\rm I}(t)=e^{iH_{\rm S}t}H_{\rm I}e^{-iH_{\rm S}t}

\displaystyle\frac{d\tilde{\rho}(t)}{dt}=-\int_{0}^{t}ds\;\mathrm{Tr}_{\rm B}[\tilde{H}_{\rm I}(t),[\tilde{H}_{I}(t-s),\tilde{\rho}(t)\otimes\rho_{\rm B}]].

(4)

In some cases, a further approximation is made where the upper limit of the integral is taken to infinity. This is also referred to
as the Redfield equation in literature.

We consider the case where the interaction
Hamiltonian takes the form

\displaystyle H_{\rm I}=\epsilon\sum_{\alpha}A_{\alpha}\otimes B_{\alpha},

(5)

where
\epsilon
measures the strength of the coupling between the system and bath
and
A_{\alpha}
and
B_{\alpha}
are (hermitian) operators acting on the system and bath spaces, respectively.
It is convenient to use the expansion using the Bohr frequency as

\displaystyle A_{\alpha}(\omega)\equiv\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m,n\\
E_{n}-E_{m}=\omega\end{subarray}}\Pi_{m}A_{\alpha}\Pi_{n},

(6)

where
\Pi_{m}=|m\rangle\langle m|
is the projection operator onto the eigenstate

of
H_{S}

with eigenvalue
E_{m}
. Note that
A_{\alpha}^{\dagger}(\omega)=A_{\alpha}(-\omega)
.
Then we can rewrite Eq. (
4

\displaystyle\frac{d\tilde{\rho}(t)}{dt}=-\epsilon^{2}\sum_{\alpha,\beta}\sum_{\omega,\omega^{\prime}}

\displaystyle\Big(e^{i(\omega^{\prime}-\omega)t}\Gamma^{t}_{\alpha\beta}(\omega)[A^{\dagger}_{\alpha}(\omega^{\prime}),A_{\beta}(\omega)\tilde{\rho}(t)]

\displaystyle+\mathrm{h.c.}\Big),

(7)

where
\Gamma^{t}_{\alpha\beta}(\omega)
is defined as

\displaystyle\Gamma^{t}_{\alpha\beta}(\omega)\equiv\int_{0}^{t}ds\;e^{i\omega s}C_{\alpha\beta}(s).

(8)

Here, the bath correlation function
C_{\alpha\beta}(t)
is given by

\displaystyle C_{\alpha\beta}(t)\equiv\mathrm{Tr}_{B}[\tilde{B}_{\alpha}(t)B_{\beta}\rho_{B}]

(9)

with
\tilde{B}_{\alpha}(t)\equiv e^{iH_{B}t}B_{\alpha}e^{-iH_{B}t}
. Note that
C_{\alpha\beta}^{*}(t)=C_{\beta\alpha}(-t)
due to the time translational invariance.

In the Schrödinger picture, the superoperator in Eq. (
3
) for the Redfield equation takes the form,

\mathcal{L}_{\rm Red}=\mathcal{L}^{(0)}+\epsilon^{2}\mathcal{L}^{(2)}_{\rm Red}
, where

\mathcal{L}^{(0)}[\rho(t)]=-i[H_{S},\rho(t)]
and

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{(2)}_{\rm Red}[\rho(t)]=-\sum_{\alpha,\beta}\sum_{\omega,\omega^{\prime}}

\displaystyle\Big(\Gamma^{t}_{\alpha\beta}(\omega)[A_{\alpha}^{\dagger}(\omega^{\prime}),A_{\beta}(\omega)\rho(t)]

\displaystyle+\mathrm{h.c.}\Big),

(10)

or equivalently

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{(2)}_{\rm Red}[\rho(t)]=-\sum_{\alpha,\beta}\int_{0}^{t}ds\;

\displaystyle\Big(C_{\alpha\beta}(s)[A_{\alpha},\tilde{A}_{\beta}(-s)\rho(t)]

\displaystyle+\mathrm{h.c.}\Big),

(11)

with
\tilde{A}_{\alpha}(t)\equiv e^{iH_{S}t}A_{\alpha}e^{-iH_{S}t}
.

In order to find the steady state of the Redfield equation, we first decompose
Eq. (
10
) (after taking
t\to\infty
) into
\mathcal{L}^{(2)}_{\mathrm{Red}}=\mathcal{L}^{(S)}_{\mathrm{Red}}+\mathcal{L}^{(\gamma)}_{\mathrm{Red}}
, where

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{(S)}_{\mathrm{Red}}[\rho(t)]=

\displaystyle-i\sum_{\alpha,\beta}\sum_{\omega,\omega^{\prime}}S_{\alpha\beta}(\omega)\Big[-A_{\beta}(\omega)\rho(t)A^{\dagger}_{\alpha}(\omega^{\prime})

\displaystyle+A_{\beta}(\omega^{\prime})\rho(t)A^{\dagger}_{\alpha}(\omega)+A^{\dagger}_{\alpha}(\omega^{\prime})A_{\beta}(\omega)\rho(t)

\displaystyle-\rho(t)A^{\dagger}_{\alpha}(\omega)A_{\beta}(\omega^{\prime})\Big],~~{\rm and}

(12)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{(\gamma)}_{\mathrm{Red}}[\rho(t)]=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\alpha,\beta}\sum_{\omega,\omega^{\prime}}\gamma_{\alpha\beta}(\omega)\Big[A_{\beta}(\omega)\rho(t)A^{\dagger}_{\alpha}(\omega^{\prime})

\displaystyle+A_{\beta}(\omega^{\prime})\rho(t)A^{\dagger}_{\alpha}(\omega)-A^{\dagger}_{\alpha}(\omega^{\prime})A_{\beta}(\omega)\rho(t)

\displaystyle-\rho(t)A^{\dagger}_{\alpha}(\omega)A_{\beta}(\omega^{\prime})\Big].

(13)

Here we have introduced
\gamma_{\alpha\beta}(\omega)\equiv\Gamma^{\infty}_{\alpha\beta}(\omega)+\Gamma^{\infty\ast}_{\beta\alpha}(\omega)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dte^{i\omega t}C_{\alpha\beta}(t)=\gamma^{*}_{\beta\alpha}(\omega)
and

S_{\alpha\beta}(\omega)\equiv(\Gamma^{\infty}_{\alpha\beta}(\omega)-\Gamma^{\infty\ast}_{\beta\alpha}(\omega))/(2i)
. These two are related to each other as

\displaystyle S_{\alpha\beta}(\omega)=-\mathcal{P}\int^{\infty}_{-\infty}\frac{d\tilde{\omega}}{2\pi}\frac{1}{\tilde{\omega}}\gamma_{\alpha\beta}(\tilde{\omega}+\omega),

(14)

where
\mathcal{P}
denotes the Cauchy principal value.
As noted in Introduction, the system Gibbs state

\rho_{\mathrm{G}}=e^{-\beta H_{\mathrm{S}}}/Z_{\rm S}

of the system Hamiltonian with
Z_{\rm S}=\mathrm{Tr_{S}}e^{-\beta H_{\mathrm{S}}}
is not a steady state of the Redfield equation.
It is, however, interesting to note that we still have

\mathcal{L}^{(\gamma)}_{\mathrm{Red}}[\rho_{\rm G}]=0
. This can be proved by using

\rho_{\textrm{G}}A_{\alpha}(\omega)=e^{\beta\omega}A_{\alpha}(\omega)\rho_{\textrm{G}}
,
\rho_{\textrm{G}}A_{\alpha}^{\dagger}(\omega)=e^{-\beta\omega}A_{\alpha}^{\dagger}(\omega)\rho_{\textrm{G}}
, and the Kubo-Martin-Schwinger (KMS) condition

;
kms1

\gamma_{\alpha\delta}(-\omega)=e^{-\beta\omega}\gamma_{\delta\alpha}(\omega)

satisfied by the bath in equilibrium at temperature
\beta^{-1}
.
It is the particular form of
\mathcal{L}^{(S)}_{\mathrm{Red}}
that
prevents
\rho_{\rm G}
from being the steady state of the Redfield equation as we can easily see that

\mathcal{L}^{(S)}_{\mathrm{Red}}[\rho_{\rm G}]\neq 0
in general.

II.1
Perturbative steady-state solution of Redfield equation

One can calculate the steady of the Redfield equation in a perturbative way.
In this subsection, we review the method developed in
Ref.
thingna
for its calculation. In the next section, we will apply this method to
the completely positive master equations to calculate their perturbative steady states.
By regarding the Redfield superoperator as an
O(\epsilon^{2})
expansion of the full expression and by
expanding the steady state as

\rho^{\rm st}=\rho^{(0)}+\epsilon^{2}\rho^{(2)}+O(\epsilon^{4})
, we have steady-state equations,

\mathcal{L}^{(0)}[\rho^{(0)}]=0
and
\mathcal{L}^{(0)}[\rho^{(2)}]+\mathcal{L}^{(2)}_{\rm Red}[\rho^{(0)}]=0

\displaystyle\rho^{(0)}_{nm}=0,~~~~\mathrm{for}~~n\neq m,

(15)

\displaystyle-i\Delta_{nm}\rho^{(2)}_{nm}+\left(\mathcal{L}^{(2)}_{\rm Red}[\rho^{(0)}]\right)_{nm}=0,

The diagonal part of Eq. (

\displaystyle\left(\mathcal{L}^{(2)}_{\rm Red}[\rho^{(0)}]\right)_{nn}=0

determines

We can explicitly show from Eq. (

Therefore, the condition, Eq. (

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\left(\mathcal{L}^{(\gamma)}_{\mathrm{Red}}[\rho^{(0)}]\right)_{nn}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\alpha,\beta}\sum_{j}\left(\gamma_{\alpha\beta}(\Delta_{jn})\rho^{(0)}_{jj}-\gamma_{\beta\alpha}(\Delta_{nj})\rho^{(0)}_{nn}\right)(A_{\beta})_{nj}(A_{\alpha})_{jn}.

The off-diagonal part of Eq. (

\displaystyle\rho^{(2)}_{nm}=\frac{1}{i\Delta_{nm}}\left(\mathcal{L}^{(S)}_{\mathrm{Red}}[\rho^{(0)}]\right)_{nm},

where we have used the fact that

\displaystyle\rho^{(2)}_{nm}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\Delta_{nm}}\sum_{\alpha,\beta,j}\Big[\{S_{\beta\alpha}(\Delta_{jn})-S_{\beta\alpha}(\Delta_{jm})\}\rho^{(0)}_{jj}

\displaystyle+\left\{S_{\alpha\beta}(\Delta_{nj})\rho^{(0)}_{nn}-S_{\alpha\beta}(\Delta_{mj})\rho^{(0)}_{mm}\right\}\Big](A_{\alpha})_{nj}(A_{\beta})_{jm}.

Up to

, the diagonal part

\displaystyle\bar{\rho}^{(2)}_{nn}=\sum_{\alpha,\mu,j}

\displaystyle\Big[-S^{\prime}_{\mu\alpha}(\Delta_{jn})\rho^{(0)}_{jj}+S^{\prime}_{\alpha\mu}(\Delta_{nj})\rho^{(0)}_{nn}

Note that the zero frequency part of

The normalization

II.2
Mean Force Gibbs State

Since we expect that a quantum system coupled to a thermal bath will equilibriate, the steady state of
a QME must be described by the MFG state

\displaystyle\rho_{\rm mG}\equiv\frac{{\rm Tr}_{\rm B}(e^{-\beta H})}{{\rm Tr}_{\rm SB}(e^{-\beta H})}.

Now we consider both the weak coupling and high temperature regime, where we can

III
Steady states of Lindbladian equations derived from the Redfield equation

III.1
Lindblad equation from the secular approximation

When the system energy level spacing is large compared to
the inverse of the relaxation time scale, the secular approximation can be applied. It amounts to taking only the diagonal part (
\omega=\omega^{\prime}
)
in the double sums in Eqs. (
12
) and (
13
). After taking the upper limit of the integral in Eq. (

) to infinity,
we have the superoperator for the secular approximation as

\mathcal{L}_{\rm sec}=\mathcal{L}^{(0)}+\epsilon^{2}[\mathcal{L}^{(S)}_{\rm sec}+\mathcal{L}^{(\gamma)}_{\rm sec}]

It is straightforward to see that

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{(S)}_{\rm sec}[\rho(t)]=-i[H_{\rm LS},\rho(t)]

(27)

where

\displaystyle H_{\rm LS}=\sum_{\alpha,\beta}\sum_{\omega}S_{\alpha\beta}(\omega)A^{\dagger}_{\alpha}(\omega)A_{\beta}(\omega)

(28)

and

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{(\gamma)}_{\rm sec}[\rho(t)]=\sum_{\alpha,\beta}\sum_{\omega}

\displaystyle\gamma_{\alpha\beta}(\omega)\Big(A_{\beta}(\omega)\rho(t)A^{\dagger}_{\alpha}(\omega)

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\left\{A^{\dagger}_{\alpha}(\omega)A_{\beta}(\omega),\rho(t)\right\}\Big).

(29)

Since
\gamma_{\alpha\beta}(\omega)
is positive semidefinite, Eq. (

It is well known that
\rho_{\rm G}
is a steady state for this equation
breuer

We have already established that

Unlike the Redfield equation,
\mathcal{L}^{(S)}_{\rm sec}

\mathcal{L}^{(S)}_{\rm sec}[\rho_{\rm G}]=0

Although the Lindblad equation obtained from the secular approximation is of
O(\epsilon^{2})

it does not give
O(\epsilon^{2})
corrections to

in the steady state. Therefore, as far as the steady state is concerned,
the secular approximation is valid in the

limit

III.2
Steady state of the universal Lindblad equation

(32)

Here
g_{\alpha\beta}(\omega)
is a matrix square root of the Fourier transform
\gamma_{\alpha\beta}(\omega)

of the bath correlation function given as
g_convention

\displaystyle\gamma_{\alpha\beta}(\omega)=\sum_{\mu}g_{\alpha\mu}(\omega)g_{\mu\beta}(\omega).

(33)

Since
\gamma_{\alpha\beta}(\omega)
is positive semidefinite,
g_{\alpha\beta}(\omega)
is hermitian and also positive semidefinite.
Now the function
f_{\alpha\beta}(\omega,\omega^{\prime})
is given by

\displaystyle f_{\alpha\beta}(\omega,\omega^{\prime})\equiv-\mathcal{P}\sum_{\mu}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{d\tilde{\omega}}{2\pi}\frac{1}{\tilde{\omega}}g_{\alpha\mu}(\tilde{\omega}-\omega)g_{\mu\beta}(\tilde{\omega}+\omega^{\prime}).

(34)

A comparison of the above equations with Eqs. (
14
), (
27
), (
28
), and (
29
) for the secular approximation
reveals that the ULE roughly corresponds to taking
the square root of the Fourier transform of the bath correlation function as defined in Eq. (
33
) and to distributing it to the new jump operators.

We can check whether the system Gibbs state
\rho_{\textrm{G}}

is a steady state solution of the ULE. Using Eqs. (
30
) and (
32
), it is in fact straightforward to
verify that both
\mathcal{L}^{(a)}_{\rm ULE}[\rho_{\rm G}]
and
\mathcal{L}^{(b)}_{\rm ULE}[\rho_{\rm G}]
in general
do not vanish. The detailed calculations are in Appendix
B
.

We now apply the perturbation scheme used for the Redfield equation to the ULE to find its stationary state.
We again write the steady state as
\rho^{\rm st}=\rho^{(0)}+\epsilon^{2}\rho^{(2)}+O(\epsilon^{4})
.
Since
\mathcal{L}^{(0)}
takes the same form as the Redfield equation, the off-diagonal matrix elements of
\rho^{(0)}
vanish.
The diagonal
matrix element of
\rho^{(0)}
is determined by

\displaystyle\left(\mathcal{L}^{(2)}_{\rm ULE}[\rho^{(0)}]\right)_{nn}=0.

(35)

We can show that
\mathcal{L}^{(a)}_{\rm ULE}[\rho^{(0)}]_{nn}
identically vanishes (see Appendix
C

for details). Now the condition
(\mathcal{L}^{(b)}_{\rm ULE}[\rho^{(0)}])_{nn}=0
yields

\displaystyle\sum_{\alpha,\beta,\gamma}

\displaystyle\sum_{k}\big[g_{\alpha\beta}(\Delta_{kn})g_{\gamma\alpha}(\Delta_{kn})\rho^{(0)}_{kk}

\displaystyle-g_{\beta\alpha}(\Delta_{nk})g_{\alpha\gamma}(\Delta_{nk})\rho^{(0)}_{nn}\big](A_{\beta})_{nk}(A_{\gamma})_{kn}=0.

(36)

Note that the KMS condition for
\gamma_{\alpha\delta}(\omega)

translates into
g_{\alpha\delta}(-\omega)=e^{-\beta\omega/2}g_{\delta\alpha}(\omega)
. By applying this, we immediately find that

\rho^{(0)}=\rho_{\rm G}
satisfies the above condition. As in the Redfield equation, the zeroth order of the
steady state is again given by
\rho_{\rm G}
.

The off-diagonal elements of the
O(\epsilon^{2})
part of the steady state is obtained through the condition corresponding to Eq. (
16
) as

\displaystyle\rho^{(2)}_{mn}=\frac{1}{i\Delta_{nm}}\left(\mathcal{L}^{(2)}_{\mathrm{ULE}}[\rho^{(0)}]\right)_{nm}

(37)

for
n\neq m
. We have evaluated Eq. (
37
) for
n\neq m
in Appendix
C
as

\displaystyle\rho^{(2)}_{nm}=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{i\Delta_{nm}}\Bigg[-i\sum_{\alpha,\beta}\sum_{k}f_{\alpha\beta}(\Delta_{kn},\Delta_{mk})(A_{\alpha})_{nk}(A_{\beta})_{km}\left(\rho^{(0)}_{mm}-\rho^{(0)}_{nn}\right)

\displaystyle+\sum_{\alpha,\beta,\gamma}\sum_{k}(A_{\beta})_{nk}(A_{\gamma})_{km}\Big\{g_{\alpha\beta}(\Delta_{kn})g_{\gamma\alpha}(\Delta_{km})\rho^{(0)}_{kk}-\frac{1}{2}g_{\beta\alpha}(\Delta_{nk})g_{\alpha\gamma}(\Delta_{mk})\left(\rho^{(0)}_{mm}+\rho^{(0)}_{nn}\right)\Big\}\Bigg].

(38)

We note that this is manifestly different from the perturbative steady state, Eq. (
20
) of the Redfield equation. The latter
involves only the Lamb shift part for
\rho^{(2)}_{nm}
for
n\neq m
. On the other hand, for the ULE, the
O(\epsilon^{2})
part
of the steady state also depends on the dissipator. We therefore conclude that the steady state of the ULE
differs from both
\rho_{\rm G}
and
\rho_{\rm mG}
, which are the steady states of the
the QME with the secular approximation and the Redfield equation, respectively.

The diagonal part,
\rho^{(2)}_{nn}
can be obtained by the analytic continuation method used in Ref.
thingna
. We take
the limit
E_{m}\to E_{n}
or
\theta\to 0
, where
E_{m}=E_{n}-\theta
. We first look at the dissipator part in the second term on
the right hand side of Eq. (
38
). Using the KMS condition for
g_{\alpha\beta}(\omega)

and the fact that
\rho^{(0)}_{nn}=(\rho_{\rm G})_{nn}\sim e^{-\beta E_{n}}
, we can show that the
quantity inside the curly bracket is proportional to

\displaystyle e^{-\beta(E_{n}+E_{m})/2}-\frac{1}{2}(e^{-\beta E_{m}}+e^{-\beta E_{n}}).

(39)

In the limit
\theta\to 0
, this quantity is of
O(\theta^{2})
.
Therefore the second term on the right hand side of Eq. (
38
) does not contribute to

\rho^{(2)}_{nn}
. Taking the limit
\theta\to 0
in the first term, we have

\displaystyle\bar{\rho}^{(2)}_{nn}=-\beta\sum_{\alpha,\delta,k}f_{\alpha\delta}(\Delta_{kn},\Delta_{nk})(A_{\alpha})_{nk}(A_{\delta})_{kn}\rho^{(0)}_{nn}.

(40)

Unlike the off-diagonal counterparts, the diagonal part of
\rho^{(2)}
depends only on the Lamb shift-like term.
From Eqs. (
14
), (
33
), and (
34
), we note that
f_{\alpha\delta}(\Delta_{kn},\Delta_{nk})=S_{\alpha\delta}(\Delta_{nk})
. Therefore, Eq. (
40
) is equal to only one term among three terms in
the corresponding expression Eq. (
21
) for the Redfield equation.
As in the Redfield equation, the second-order correction to the population is given by

\rho^{(2)}_{nn}=\bar{\rho}^{(2)}_{nn}-\rho^{(0)}_{nn}\sum_{k}\bar{\rho}^{(2)}_{kk}
.

Now we discuss the behavior of the steady state of ULE in the weak coupling and the high temperature regime
as done in Sec.
II.2
.
We first consider the population term. In this regime, the orders of
\rho_{nn}^{(2)}
is
O(\beta f_{\alpha\beta}/Z_{\rm S})
from Eq. (
40
). Therefore, when
O(f_{\alpha\beta}/Z_{\rm S})<O(\beta^{-1})
, the population of the ULE steady state approaches that of the system Gibbs state. For the coherence term, the first summation part of the right hand side of Eq. (
38
) gives the order of
O(\beta f_{\alpha\beta}/Z_{\rm S})
. The order of the second summation part of the right hand side of Eq. (
38
) can be estimated as
O(\beta^{2}g_{\alpha\beta}g_{\gamma\alpha}/Z_{\rm S})
by using Eq. (
39
). Therefore, when
O(f_{\alpha\beta}/Z_{\rm S})<O(\beta^{-1})
and
O(g_{\alpha\beta}g_{\gamma\alpha}/Z_{\rm S})<O(\beta^{-2})
, the coherence of the ULE becomes negligible up to the order of
\epsilon^{2}
, which demonstrates that the steady state
of the ULE approaches the system Gibbs state
\rho_{\rm G}
in this high temperature regime.

III.3
Steady states of the truncated Lindblad equation

Recently, Becker, Wu, and Eckardt proposed a Lindbladian QME by truncating some part of the Redfield equation
becker
.
To be more specific, they decompose the Redfield equation into the Lindblad form and
identify the part that gives the negative contribution, which ruins the complete positivity, and
truncate it. They optimize this process by minimizing this negative contribution
becker
.
They consider the case that the interaction Hamiltonian is given by
H_{\rm I}=\epsilon A\otimes B
, where
A
is a dimensionless operator acting on a system and
B
is an operator with dimension of energy acting on a bath, and both are hermitian. Here we set for
A
to satisfy a
normalization condition

\sum_{nm}|A_{nm}|^{2}=1
. Their suggested QME is

\frac{d\rho}{dt}=-i[H_{\rm S}+H_{\rm LS},\rho]+\gamma\mathcal{D}_{\rm trc},

(45)

where
H_{\rm LS}
and
\mathcal{D}_{\rm trc}
are given by

\displaystyle H_{\rm LS}

\displaystyle=\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{2i}(A\mathbb{A}_{t}-\mathbb{A}_{t}^{\dagger}A),

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{\rm trc}

\displaystyle=L\rho L^{\dagger}-\frac{1}{2}\{L^{\dagger}L,\rho\}.

(46)

Here,

\displaystyle L

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\cos\varphi_{t}}}\left(\lambda_{t}^{+}A+\frac{1}{\lambda_{t}^{+}}\mathbb{A}_{t}\right),

\displaystyle\mathbb{A}_{t}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}d\tau C(\tau)e^{-iH_{\rm S}\tau}Ae^{iH_{\rm S}\tau}

with

and

. In this formulation, there exists freedom to choose
\lambda_{t}
and

to minimize the truncated part. Their optimized values are given as

\displaystyle-\frac{\delta_{kn}}{2}\sum_{j}\left[(G_{jl}^{t})^{*}-G_{jm}^{t}+(W_{jl}^{t})^{*}W_{jm}^{t}\right]A_{lj}A_{jm}

In this study, we calculate equilibrium steady states of the recently developed QMEs up to the second order in the coupling strength between a bath and a system of interest. The steady states we have studied in this paper are summarized in Table
1
. From this calculation, we explicitly show that the steady states of Lindbladian QMEs differ from the MFG state, which is the equilibrium state when the total system thermalizes. The steady state of the ULE in the leading order is shown to be the system Gibbs state
\rho_{\rm G}
, similar to the Redfield equation. For the ULE, we explicitly evaluate the next-order corrections for the population and coherence, which exhibit discrepancies from those of the MFG state. For the TLE, we find that the steady state differs from the MFG, even in the leading order. Thus, our result clearly demonstrates that any modification of the Redfield equation to achieve complete positivity of a QME can cause undesirable changes to the steady state. However, we also show that this discrepancy can be reduced in the high-temperature regime, where the steady states of the Lindbladian QMEs and MFG can reduce to the same Gibbs state of the system Hamiltonian under certain conditions. Therefore, the steady-state problem of the Lindblad QMEs can be mitigated by increasing the environmental temperature. Our results lead to another next important question, that is, how can we establish a QME with both complete positivity and a desirable steady state simultaneously for low or moderate temperatures? This requires further study. We anticipate that the recent study on the completely positive QME in zero temperature
david
can be a good starting point to achieve this goal.

Using the relations

\displaystyle=-i\sum_{\alpha,\beta}\sum_{\omega,\omega^{\prime}}f_{\alpha\beta}(-\omega,\omega^{\prime})\left(A^{\dagger}_{\alpha}(\omega)A_{\beta}(\omega)\rho_{\textrm{G}}-\rho_{\textrm{G}}A^{\dagger}_{\alpha}(\omega)A_{\beta}(\omega)\right)

\displaystyle=-i\sum_{\alpha,\beta}\sum_{\omega,\omega^{\prime}}f_{\alpha\beta}(-\omega,\omega^{\prime})\left(1-e^{-\beta(\omega-\omega^{\prime})}\right)A^{\dagger}_{\alpha}(\omega)A_{\beta}(\omega^{\prime})\rho_{\textrm{G}}.

(67)

For the dissipator part, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{\alpha}L_{\alpha}\rho_{\textrm{th}}L^{\dagger}_{\alpha}=

\displaystyle\sum_{\alpha,\beta,\gamma}\sum_{\omega,\omega^{\prime}}g_{\alpha\beta}(\omega)g^{*}_{\alpha\gamma}(\omega^{\prime})A_{\beta}(\omega)\rho_{\rm G}A^{\dagger}_{\gamma}(\omega^{\prime})

\displaystyle\sum_{\alpha,\beta,\gamma}\sum_{\omega,\omega^{\prime}}g_{\alpha\beta}(-\omega)g_{\gamma\alpha}(-\omega^{\prime})A^{\dagger}_{\beta}(\omega)A_{\gamma}(\omega^{\prime})\rho_{\rm G}e^{\beta\omega^{\prime}},

\displaystyle\left\{\sum_{\alpha}L_{\alpha}^{\dagger}L_{\alpha},\rho_{\textrm{th}}\right\}=

\displaystyle\sum_{\alpha,\beta,\gamma}\sum_{\omega,\omega^{\prime}}g^{*}_{\alpha\beta}(\omega)g_{\alpha\gamma}(\omega^{\prime})\left(A^{\dagger}_{\beta}(\omega)A_{\gamma}(\omega^{\prime})\rho_{\rm G}+\rho_{\rm G}A^{\dagger}_{\beta}(\omega)A_{\gamma}(\omega^{\prime})\right)

\displaystyle\sum_{\alpha,\beta,\gamma}\sum_{\omega,\omega^{\prime}}g_{\beta\alpha}(\omega)g_{\alpha\gamma}(\omega^{\prime})\left(1+e^{-\beta(\omega-\omega^{\prime})}\right)A^{\dagger}_{\beta}(\omega)A_{\gamma}(\omega^{\prime})\rho_{\rm G}.

It is clear that Eqs. (
