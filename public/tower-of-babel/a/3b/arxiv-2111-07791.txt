Abstract
In this paper we prove a weak form of the a ​ b ​ c abc Conjecture generalised to algebraic number fields. Given algebraic integers a , b , c a,\,b,\,c in a number field K K satisfying a + b = c a+b=c , we give an upper bound for the logarithm of the projective height H L ​ ( a , b , c ) H_{L}(a,\,b,\,c) in terms of norms of prime ideals dividing a ​ b ​ c ​ 𝒪 L abc\mathcal{O}_{L} , where L L is the Hilbert Class Field of K K . In many cases this allows us to give a bound in terms of the modified radical G := G ⁡ ( a , b , c ) G:=G(a,\,b,\,c) as given by Masser in [ 21 ] . Furthermore, by employing a recent result by Le Fourn, our estimates imply the upper bound log ⁡ H L ​ ( a , b , c ) < G 1 3 + 𝒞 ​ log ⁡ log ⁡ log ⁡ G log ⁡ log ⁡ G , \log H_{L}\left(a,\,b,\,c\right)<G^{\frac{1}{3}+\mathcal{C}\frac{\log\log\log G}{\log\log G}}, where 𝒞 \mathcal{C} is an effectively computable constant. First we discuss the effective Skolem-Mahler-Lech problem. The problem is, given a linear recurrence sequence, to decide whether said sequence contains zeroes.

We recall that a linear recurrence sequence is a sequence

The Skolem-Mahler-Lech Theorem is as follows.

Theorem 4

(Skolem-Mahler-Lech)

If a sequence of numbers satisfies a linear recurrence relation over a field of characteristic zero, the the zeroes of this sequence can be decomposed into the union of a finite set, and finitely many arithmetic progressions.

More comments on this theorem and a proof for sequences defined over the rationals is given in

Remarks

We note that there exists an algorithm to tell us if there are infinitely many zeroes, and if so to find the decomposition of these zeros into periodic sets guaranteed to exist by the Skolem–Mahler–Lech Theorem

Lemma 4
.

Let
F
be a number field with ring of integers
\mathcal{O}_{F}
. Apply a total ordering to the prime ideals, so that if
\emph{\text{Nm}}_{\mathbb{Q}}^{F}~\left(\mathfrak{x}\right)>\emph{\text{Nm}}_{\mathbb{Q}}^{F}\left(\mathfrak{y}\right)
, then
\mathfrak{x}\succ\mathfrak{y}
. Arbitrarily order ideals of the same norm. Then there is an effectively computable positive constant
\mathcal{C}_{15}
such that for every positive integer

\prod_{i=1}^{r}\frac{\emph{\text{Nm}}_{\mathbb{Q}}^{F}~\left(\mathfrak{p}_{i}\right)}{\log\emph{\text{Nm}}_{\mathbb{Q}}^{F}~\left(\mathfrak{p}_{i}\right)}>\left(\frac{r}{\mathcal{C}_{15}}\right)^{r}.

Proof.

Let
\pi_{F}(x)
denote the number of prime ideals in number field
F
of norm less than or equal to

. By the Landau Prime Ideal Theorem
[
17
]
, we know that
\pi_{F}(x)\sim\frac{x}{\log x}
. Partially order the prime ideals as in the statement of the Lemma. Then by Landau,

\pi_{F}\left(\text{Nm}_{\mathbb{Q}}^{F}\left(\mathfrak{p}_{j}\right)\right)\sim\frac{\text{Nm}_{\mathbb{Q}}^{F}\left(\mathfrak{p}_{j}\right)}{\log\left(\text{Nm}_{\mathbb{Q}}^{F}\left(\mathfrak{p}_{j}\right)\right)}.

Thus by Landau, there exists an effectively computable number
\mathcal{C}_{16}
such that

\frac{\text{Nm}_{\mathbb{Q}}^{F}\left(\mathfrak{p}_{j}\right)}{\log\left(\text{Nm}_{\mathbb{Q}}^{F}\left(\mathfrak{p}_{j}\right)\right)}>j/\mathcal{C}_{16}.

Thus, using the inequality

, we see that

\prod_{j=1}^{r}\frac{\text{Nm}_{\mathbb{Q}}^{F}\left(\mathfrak{p}_{j}\right)}{\log\left(\text{Nm}_{\mathbb{Q}}^{F}\left(\mathfrak{p}_{j}\right)\right)}>\frac{r!}{\mathcal{C}_{16}^{r}}\geq\left(\frac{r}{\mathcal{C}_{17}e}\right)^{r}\geq\left(\frac{r}{\mathcal{C}_{18}}\right)^{r},

which proves the claim in the lemma.

Lemma 5

(11)

Multiplying together (

Recall that there are

\displaystyle\alpha

Let

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{r+1}N_{i}\log\max\left(\left|e_{i}(u),\,1\right|\right)-\sum_{i=1}^{r+1}N_{i}\log\min\left(\left|e_{i}(u),\,1\right|\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{r+1}N_{i}\left|\varepsilon_{i}(u)\right|,

We now take advantage of some properties of

and

\max\left\{h\left(x\right),\,h\left(y\right)\right\}\leq\mathcal{C}_{118}\left(d,\,s\right)R_{S}\log^{+}\left(R_{S}\right)H,

where

is the
S
-regulator and

[

For any general set of places

\max\left\{h\left(x\right),\,h\left(y\right)\right\}\leq\mathcal{C}_{119}\left(d,\,s\right)P^{\prime}_{S}R_{S}\left(1+\frac{\log^{+}\left(R_{S}\right)}{\log^{+}\left(P^{\prime}_{S}\right)}\right)H,

7
Application to Effective Skolem-Mahler-Lech Problem

In this section we will use our main result to allow us to determine whether a linear recurrence sequence of degree three with no repeated roots of the characteristic polynomial has zeroes. As noted in the introduction, there exists an algorithm to determine whether there are periodic zeroes, so we are concerned with the case when there are only potentially finitely many zeroes.

7.1
Case Where all Terms are Coprime

Consider a linear recurrence sequence of the following form:

a_{n}=c_{1}a_{n-1}+c_{2}a_{n-2}+c_{3}a_{n-3},

where the values of
a_{0},\,a_{1}
and
a_{2}
are known. We form the characteristic polynomial of the sequence

x^{3}-c_{1}x^{2}-c_{2}x-c_{3}

and assume that this has distinct roots
r_{1},\,r_{2},\,r_{3}
. Let
K=\mathbb{Q}\left(r_{1},\,r_{2},\,r_{3}\right)
. We further assume the roots are pairwise coprime when considered as principal ideals of the ring of integers
\mathcal{O}_{K}
.

By our assumptions, we know that we can write

a_{n}=k_{1}r_{1}^{n}+k_{2}r_{2}^{n}+k_{3}r_{3}^{n}

where
k_{1},\,k_{2},\,k_{3}
are constants depending on
a_{0},\,a_{1}
and
a_{2}
. We further assume
k_{1},\,k_{2}
and
k_{3}
are coprime, but we will look at ways to try and deal with this when not coprime later.

For ease, we use the result obtained by using Le Fourn’s Lemma, that is the inequality given at (
123
). Assume there exists an
n
such that
a_{n}=0
. Explicitly,

0=k_{1}r_{1}^{n}+k_{2}r_{2}^{n}+k_{3}r_{3}^{n}.

We are in a position to use the result. Let
L=HCF(K)
and define
G
as above. Then,

\log H\left(k_{1}r_{1}^{n},\,k_{2}r_{2}^{n},\,k_{3}r_{3}^{n}\right)<G^{\frac{1}{3}+\mathcal{C}_{128}\frac{\log\log\log G}{\log\log G}}.

Without loss of generality, assume that

h\left(r_{1}\right)\leq h\left(r_{2}\right)\leq h\left(r_{3}\right).

Note that

H\left(k_{1}r_{1}^{n},\,k_{2}r_{2}^{n},\,k_{3}r_{3}^{n}\right)=H\left(\frac{k_{1}}{k_{3}}r_{1}^{n},\,\frac{k_{2}}{k_{3}}r_{2}^{n},\,r_{3}^{n}\right).

Further, by comparing definitions,

h\left(r_{3}^{n}\right)\leq\log H\left(\frac{k_{1}}{k_{3}}r_{1}^{n},\,\frac{k_{2}}{k_{3}}r_{2}^{n},\,r_{3}^{n}\right).

Moreover,
h\left(r_{3}^{n}\right)~=nh\left(r_{3}\right)

[
37
]
. Combining all this we obtain that

nh\left(r_{3}\right)<G^{\frac{1}{3}+\mathcal{C}_{128}\frac{\log\log\log G}{\log\log G}}.

It follows that

n<\frac{G^{\frac{1}{3}+\mathcal{C}_{128}\frac{\log\log\log G}{\log\log G}}}{h\left(r_{3}\right)},

giving an upper bound for
n
.

Explicitly, given a recurrence relation satisfying the given conditions, we first check whether there are any zeroes in arithmetic progressions. If so, we are done. If not, we apply the above method, which gives an upper bound for the maximal value of
n
such that
a_{n}=0
. We numerically check the values of
a_{x}
for
x
less than the obtained upper bound. This answers the question as to whether the recurrence sequence has a zero.

Example 1
.

Consider the linear recurrence sequence with
a_{0}=31,\,a_{1}=112,\,a_{2}=452
and

a_{n}=10a_{n-1}-31a_{n-2}+30a_{n-3}.

This sequence has characteristic polynomial

x^{3}-10x^{2}+31x-30,

with roots
2,\,3
and
5
. Thus,

a_{n}=k_{1}2^{n}+k_{2}3^{n}+k_{3}5^{n},

where
k_{1},\,k_{2}
and
k_{3}
are to be found. They are found to be
k_{1}=7,\,k_{2}=11
and
k_{3}=13
, so

a_{n}=7\cdot 2^{n}+11\cdot 3^{n}+13\cdot 5^{n}.

This means
G=2\cdot 3\cdots 5\cdot 7\cdot 11\cdot 13=30030
. The largest logarithmic height of the roots is
h\left(5\right)=\log 5
. It follows that if
a_{n}=0
, then

\displaystyle n

\displaystyle<\frac{30030^{\frac{1}{3}+\mathcal{C}_{128}\frac{\log\log\log 30030}{\log\log 30030}}}{\log 5}

\displaystyle<20\cdot 43^{\mathcal{C}_{130}}.

In principle,
\mathcal{C}_{128}
and
\mathcal{C}_{130}
can be computed following the proof given in this paper. This gives an upper bound for
n
.

In this example, once we derive
a_{n}=7\cdot 2^{n}+11\cdot 3^{n}+13\cdot 5^{n}
, it is clear there are no zeroes. With more complicated examples, it may not be so obvious.

7.2
Case Where Terms are Not Coprime

Assume we have a linear recurrence relation as above with characteristic polynomial
f(x)
with roots
r_{1},\,r_{2},\,r_{3}
. Assume there are constants
k_{1},\,k_{2},\,k_{3}
so

a_{n}=k_{1}r_{1}^{n}+k_{2}r_{2}^{n}+k_{3}r_{3}^{n}.

We assume nothing about coprimeness. If they are all coprime, we’re done as above. We thus assume
k_{1}r_{1}^{n},\,k_{2}r_{2}^{n},\,k_{3}r_{3}^{n}
are not coprime.

If there exists an
n
such that
a_{n}=0
, then the same prime ideal must divide all 3 terms. We can see this as if

0=k_{1}r_{1}^{n}+k_{2}r_{2}^{n}+k_{3}r_{3}^{n},

then

-k_{1}r_{1}^{n}=k_{2}r_{2}^{n}+k_{3}r_{3}^{n},

and it follows if a prime ideal divides two of these as ideals, it has to divide the third.

More rigorously,

\text{ord}_{\mathfrak{p}}\left(a+b\right)\geq\min\left\{\text{ord}_{\mathfrak{p}}\left(a\right),\,\text{ord}_{\mathfrak{p}}\left(b\right)\right\},

(124)

with equality when
\text{ord}_{\mathfrak{p}}\left(a\right)\neq\text{ord}_{\mathfrak{p}}\left(b\right)
. The claim directly follows from this. It also follows from this that at least two of the terms are divisible by the prime ideal to the same order.

We consider these as ideals of
\mathcal{O}_{K}
. Assume that
\mathfrak{q}
is a prime ideal of
\mathcal{O}_{K}
dividing all three terms, and that
\text{ord}_{\mathfrak{q}}\left(k_{1}r_{1}^{n}\right)=\text{ord}_{\mathfrak{q}}\left(k_{2}r_{2}^{n}\right)=l
. We write

\displaystyle k_{1}r_{1}^{n}\mathcal{O}_{K}

\displaystyle=\mathfrak{p}_{k_{1},\,1}^{e_{k_{1},\,1}}\cdots\mathfrak{p}_{k_{1},\,a}^{e_{k_{1},\,a}}\mathfrak{p}_{r_{1},\,1}^{n\cdot e_{r_{1},\,1}}\cdots\mathfrak{p}_{r_{1},\,b}^{n\cdot e_{r_{1},\,b}}\mathfrak{q}^{l}

\displaystyle k_{2}r_{2}^{n}\mathcal{O}_{K}

\displaystyle=\mathfrak{p}_{k_{2},\,1}^{e_{k_{2},\,1}}\cdots\mathfrak{p}_{k_{2},\,a}^{e_{k_{2},\,c}}\mathfrak{p}_{r_{2},\,1}^{n\cdot e_{r_{2},\,1}}\cdots\mathfrak{p}_{r_{2},\,b}^{n\cdot e_{r_{2},\,d}}\mathfrak{q}^{l}

\displaystyle k_{3}r_{3}^{n}\mathcal{O}_{K}

\displaystyle=\mathfrak{p}_{k_{3},\,1}^{e_{k_{3},\,1}}\cdots\mathfrak{p}_{k_{3},\,f}^{e_{k_{3},\,f}}\mathfrak{p}_{r_{3},\,1}^{n\cdot e_{r_{3},\,1}}\cdots\mathfrak{p}_{r_{3},\,b}^{n\cdot e_{r_{3},\,g}}\mathfrak{q}^{m},

where these ideals are prime ideals of
\mathcal{O}_{K}
.

Note, it may be the case that
l=m
. Also, if
\mathfrak{q}\mid r_{1}^{n}
, this implies that
l=an
for some
a
, but we will see this doesn’t matter for the argument. Finally, it may be that there is a further prime ideal that divides all three terms;p if so, we apply the following process iteratively on all prime ideals dividing all three terms.

We now move the the Hilbert Class Field
L
. All the ideals above are principal as ideals of
\mathcal{O}_{L}
, so we can write

\displaystyle k_{1}r_{1}^{n}

\displaystyle=u_{1}{p}_{k_{1},\,1}^{e_{k_{1},\,1}}\cdots{p}_{k_{1},\,a}^{e_{k_{1},\,a}}{p}_{r_{1},\,1}^{n\cdot e_{r_{1},\,1}}\cdots{p}_{r_{1},\,b}^{n\cdot e_{r_{1},\,b}}{q}^{l}

\displaystyle k_{2}r_{2}^{n}

\displaystyle=u_{2}{p}_{k_{2},\,1}^{e_{k_{2},\,1}}\cdots{p}_{k_{2},\,a}^{e_{k_{2},\,c}}{p}_{r_{2},\,1}^{n\cdot e_{r_{2},\,1}}\cdots{p}_{r_{2},\,b}^{n\cdot e_{r_{2},\,d}}{q}^{l}

\displaystyle k_{3}r_{3}^{n}

\displaystyle=u_{3}{p}_{k_{3},\,1}^{e_{k_{3},\,1}}\cdots{p}_{k_{3},\,f}^{e_{k_{3},\,f}}{p}_{r_{3},\,1}^{n\cdot e_{r_{3},\,1}}\cdots{p}_{r_{3},\,b}^{n\cdot e_{r_{3},\,g}}{q}^{m},

where the terms on the right hand side are all elements of
L
that generate the relevant principal ideals. Thus, we can now write

\displaystyle a_{n}=

\displaystyle u_{1}{p}_{k_{1},\,1}^{e_{k_{1},\,1}}\cdots{p}_{k_{1},\,a}^{e_{k_{1},\,a}}{p}_{r_{1},\,1}^{n\cdot e_{r_{1},\,1}}\cdots{p}_{r_{1},\,b}^{n\cdot e_{r_{1},\,b}}{q}^{l}

\displaystyle+u_{2}{p}_{k_{2},\,1}^{e_{k_{2},\,1}}\cdots{p}_{k_{2},\,a}^{e_{k_{2},\,c}}{p}_{r_{2},\,1}^{n\cdot e_{r_{2},\,1}}\cdots{p}_{r_{2},\,b}^{n\cdot e_{r_{2},\,d}}{q}^{l}

\displaystyle+u_{3}{p}_{k_{3},\,1}^{e_{k_{3},\,1}}\cdots{p}_{k_{3},\,f}^{e_{k_{3},\,f}}{p}_{r_{3},\,1}^{n\cdot e_{r_{3},\,1}}\cdots{p}_{r_{3},\,b}^{n\cdot e_{r_{3},\,g}}{q}^{m}.

By (

Smooth Solutions to the
abc
Conjecture

In this section we will prove Theorem
5
, as given in the introduction. We will first prove the following lemma.

Lemma 9
.

Let
\left(X,\,Y,\,Z\right)\in\mathbb{Z}^{3}
be a triple with smoothness
S\left(X,\,Y,\,Z\right)
and radical
G\left(X,\,Y,\,Z\right)
defined as above. Then

G\left(X,\,Y,\,Z\right)\leq e^{3S\left(X,\,Y,\,Z\right)}.

(125)

Proof.

From
[
30
]
, we know that for
n\geq 6
,

\frac{p_{n}}{n}<\log n+\log\log n,

where
p_{n}
denotes the n’th rational prime.

It follows that for
n\geq 6
,

p_{n}\leq 2n\log n.

Indeed, computationally we can check primes
2,\,3,\,5,\,7,\,11
and we find that for
n>2
the above inequality holds.

Further, by Rosser’s Theorem
[
29
]
,

p_{n}>n\log n.

Thus, it follows that the product of the first
k
primes satisfies the following inequality:

\displaystyle\prod_{i=1}^{k}p_{i}

\displaystyle\leq 2\cdot 3\cdot\prod_{i=3}^{k}2i\log i

\displaystyle=2\cdot 3\cdot 2^{k-2}\left(4\cdot 5\cdots k\right)\cdot\prod_{i=3}^{k}\log i

\displaystyle=2^{k-2}\cdot k!\cdot\prod_{i=3}^{k}\log i

\displaystyle\leq 2^{k}\cdot k^{k}\cdot\prod_{i=3}^{k}\log i.

(126)

We now take logs of each side of the inequality attaining

\displaystyle\log\left(\prod_{i=1}^{k}p_{i}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\log\left(2^{k}\cdot k^{k}\cdot\prod_{i=3}^{k}\log i\right)

\displaystyle=k\log 2+k\log k+\log\left(\prod_{i=3}^{k}\log i\right)

\displaystyle=k\log 2+k\log k+\sum_{i=3}^{k}\log\log i

\displaystyle\leq k\log 2+k\log k+k\log\log k

\displaystyle\leq k\log 2+k\log k+k\log k

\displaystyle\leq 3k\log k

\displaystyle\leq 3p_{k}

(127)

where the last line follows from Rosser’s Theorem.

It thus follows that for a triple of pairwise coprime integers satisfying
X+Y=Z
with smoothness
S\left(X,\,Y,\,Z\right)
,

\displaystyle G\left(X,\,Y,\,Z\right)

\displaystyle\leq\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}p\textrm{ prime}\\
p\leq S\left(X,\,Y,\,Z\right)\end{subarray}}p

\displaystyle\leq e^{3S\left(X,\,Y,\,Z\right)},

(128)

by the above inequality.
∎

We are now in a position to prove Theorem
5
.

Proof.

By Northcott’s Theorem, we can assume in the following that
H\left(X,\,Y,\,Z\right)>B
for any given bound
B
as we will only be excluding finitely many possible solutions
\left(X,\,Y,\,Z\right)
satisfying (
8
).

For ease of notation, write

T:=\log\log H\left(X,\,Y,\,Z\right).

(129)

By assumption, we have that

S\left(X,\,Y,\,Z\right)<T\frac{\log T}{\log\log T\phi\left(T\right)}.

(130)

We study triples
\left(X,\,Y,\,Z\right)
satisfying (
130
), and note that for a sufficiently large
H\left(X,\,Y,\,Z\right)
,

T\frac{\log T}{\log\log T\phi\left(T\right)}<\left(\log H\left(X,\,Y,\,Z\right)\right)^{\frac{1}{2}},

so we can apply Corollary 10. We note the choice of the exponent to be
\frac{1}{2}
is incidental; indeed any exponent less than
\frac{2}{3}
could have been chosen. Further, the Hilbert Class Field of
\mathbb{Q}
is itself
\mathbb{Q}

[
7
]
, so the radical
G
in this case is defined over
\mathbb{Q}
and coincides with the radical given in
[
16
]
.

By Corollary 10, we know that

\log H\left(X,\,Y,Z\right)<G^{\mathcal{C}_{89}\frac{\log\log\log G}{\log\log G}}.

From the upper bound for
G
given at (
125
) in Lemma
9
, we obtain from the above that

\displaystyle\log H\left(X,\,Y,\,Z\right)

\displaystyle<e^{3\mathcal{C}_{89}S\left(X,\,Y,\,Z\right)\frac{\log\log\log e^{3S\left(X,\,Y,\,Z\right)}}{\log\log e^{3S\left(X,\,Y,\,Z\right)}}}

\displaystyle=e^{\mathcal{C}_{131}S\left(X,\,Y,\,Z\right)\frac{\log\log 3S\left(X,\,Y,\,Z\right)}{\log 3S\left(X,\,Y,\,Z\right)}}.

(131)

It follows from the above that

\log\log H\left(X,\,Y,\,Z\right)=T<\mathcal{C}_{131}S\left(X,\,Y,\,Z\right)\frac{\log\log 3S\left(X,\,Y,\,Z\right)}{\log 3S\left(X,\,Y,\,Z\right)}.

(132)

We recall that by Northcott’s Theorem again, we can assume that
S\left(X,\,Y,\,Z\right)
can be larger than any given constant while only dropping finitely many solutions to
X+Y=Z
. Thus, only loosing finitely many solutions, for sufficiently large

it follows from (
132

T<\mathcal{C}_{132}S\left(X,\,Y,\,Z\right)\frac{\log\log S\left(X,\,Y,\,Z\right)}{\log S\left(X,\,Y,\,Z\right)}.

(133)

Taking logarithms on both side of inequality (

is large enough to give that

\displaystyle\log T

\displaystyle<\log\left(\mathcal{C}_{132}S\left(X,\,Y,\,Z\right)\frac{\log\log S\left(X,\,Y,\,Z\right)}{\log S\left(X,\,Y,\,Z\right)}\right)

\displaystyle=\log\mathcal{C}_{132}+\log S\left(X,\,Y,\,Z\right)+\log\log\log S\left(X,\,Y,\,Z\right)-\log\log S\left(X,\,Y,\,Z\right)

\displaystyle<2\log S\left(X,\,Y,\,Z\right).

(134)

For ease later, we divide the above by

\frac{1}{2}\log T<\log S\left(X,\,Y,\,Z\right).

(135)

For triples

\displaystyle=\log T+\log\log T-\log\log\log T-\log\phi\left(T\right)

(136)

We note that this also implies that for sufficiently large

,

\log\log S\left(X,\,Y,\,Z\right)<2\log\log T.

Substituting this and (
135
) into (
130
) we obtain that

\displaystyle S\left(X,\,Y,\,Z\right)

\displaystyle<T\frac{\log T}{\log\log T\phi\left(T\right)}

\displaystyle<T\frac{2\log S\left(X,\,Y,\,Z\right)}{\frac{1}{2}\log\log\left(S\left(X,\,Y,\,Z\right)\right)\phi\left(T\right)}

\displaystyle=4T\frac{\log S\left(X,\,Y,\,Z\right)}{\log\log\left(S\left(X,\,Y,\,Z\right)\right)\phi\left(T\right)}.

Rearranging the above we obtain that

\frac{1}{4}\frac{S\left(X,\,Y,\,Z\right)\log\log S\left(X,\,Y,\,Z\right)}{\log S\left(X,\,Y,\,Z\right)}\phi\left(T\right)<T

which we rewrite as

\displaystyle\frac{S\left(X,\,Y,\,Z\right)\log\log S\left(X,\,Y,\,Z\right)}{\log S\left(X,\,Y,\,Z\right)}\phi\left(T\right)<T<\mathcal{C}_{133}\frac{S\left(X,\,Y,\,Z\right)\log\log S\left(X,\,Y,\,Z\right)}{\log S\left(X,\,Y,\,Z\right)}.

Cancelling terms on both sides gives us that

\phi\left(T\right)<\mathcal{C}_{133}.

(141)

However,
