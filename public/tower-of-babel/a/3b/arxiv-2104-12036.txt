A class of dimension-free metrics for the convergence of empirical measures
Jiequn Han, Ruimeng Hu, Jihao Long

Abstract
This paper concerns the convergence of empirical measures in high dimensions. We propose a new class of probability metrics and show that under such metrics, the convergence is free of the curse of dimensionality (CoD). Such a feature is critical for high-dimensional analysis and stands in contrast to classical metrics ( e.g. , the Wasserstein metric). The proposed metrics fall into the category of integral probability metrics, for which we specify criteria of test function spaces to guarantee the property of being free of CoD. Examples of the selected test function spaces include the reproducing kernel Hilbert spaces, Barron space, and flow-induced function spaces. Three applications of the proposed metrics are presented: 1. The convergence of empirical measure in the case of random variables; 2. The convergence of n n -particle system to the solution to McKean-Vlasov stochastic differential equation; 3. The construction of an ε \varepsilon -Nash equilibrium for a homogeneous n n -player game by its mean-field limit. As a byproduct, we prove that, given a distribution close to the target distribution measured by our metric and a certain representation of the target distribution, we can generate a distribution close to the target one in terms of the Wasserstein metric and relative entropy. Overall, we show that the proposed class of metrics is a powerful tool to analyze the convergence of empirical measures in high dimensions without CoD.



Keywords:
Integral probability metrics, curse of dimensionality, empirical measure, McKean-Vlasov stochastic differential equation, mean-field games.

1
Introduction

The convergence of empirical measures plays a crucial role in analyzing the efficiency of mean-field theory or mean-field games (MFGs), which are fundamental tools to approximate finite-particle or finite-agent systems in the asymptotic of a very large population. Specifically, mean-field theory studies the behavior of a high-dimensional stochastic particle system by considering the effect of all other particles approximated by an average single effect. It plays a significant role in many fields, for instance, statistical physics
[
8
,
14
]
. MFGs were introduced independently by Lasry-Lions (
[
42
,
43
,
44
]
) and Huang-Malhamé-Caines (
[
35
,
36
]
).
MFGs study the decision-making problem of a continuum of agents, and are able to provide approximations to Nash equilibria of
n
-player games in which players interact through their empirical measure. For further background on MFGs, we refer to the books
[
12
,
13
]
and the references therein.

In stochastic analysis, there is a rich literature on the convergence analysis of
n
interacting-bodies/particle system to the corresponding limit (also known as the McKean-Vlasov system
[
46
,
47
]
). Recent developments in this field can be found in
[
41
,
40
,
37
]
. In general, the distance between an
n
-body empirical measure and its limit is of order
n^{-c/d}
, where
d
is the dimension of one body and
c
is a constant independent of
d
.
Such results have been established in various settings, from the simple case of
n
-independent samples drawn from a given distribution
[
21
,
27
,
61
]
, to complicated cases of the McKean-Vlasov system
[
20
]
and MGFs
[
11
]
. In many interesting applications (
e.g.
, the construction of
\varepsilon
-Nash equilibria
[
11
]
),
d
can be so large that the resulting convergence rate is extremely slow. This phenomenon is referred to as the curse of dimensionality (CoD), the main challenge in high-dimensional analysis and algorithms.

The analysis in
[
21
,
27
,
61
,
20
,
11
]
suggests that the CoD phenomenon is related to the usage of the Wasserstein metric and the type of interaction kernels that drive the interaction between bodies. In fact, it is well-known that the convergence of empirical measures under the Wasserstein metric presents the CoD
[
21
]
for any distribution that is absolutely continuous with respect to the Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

In this paper, we propose a new class of dimension-free metrics for the convergence analysis of mean-field problems. Specifically, we take the form of integral probability metrics (IPMs),

\mathrm{D}_{\Phi}(\mu,\mu^{\prime})=\sup_{f\in\Phi}\left|\int f\,\mathrm{d}\mu-f\,\mathrm{d}\mu^{\prime}\right|,

(1)

and impose a set of criteria for selecting the test function class
\Phi
to guarantee that the convergence rate under various settings is dimension-free. Our criteria mainly build on the function class’s empirical Rademacher complexity, allowing the test functions to be the reproducing kernel Hilbert spaces (RKHSs), the Barron function space, and flow-induced function spaces, just to name a few. The choice of RKHSs is closely related to the maximum mean discrepancy (MMD)
[
7
]
as a tool for statistical tests to check if two sets of observations are generated by the same distribution. Therein, for computational efficiency, the test function space is chosen as the unit ball of RKHSs.

Beating the CoD is also a central topic in the machine learning community.
One of the core problems in the high-dimensional analysis of machine learning models is identifying an appropriate function space equipped with an appropriate norm that can control the approximation and estimation errors of a particular machine learning model.
This perspective is closely related to the proposed probability metrics, and we should already point out to the readers that all proposed test function classes originate from machine learning models. Reproducing kernel Hilbert spaces are particularly important in statistical learning theory due to the representer theorem established in
[
38
]
. The
Barron space
[
23
]
was introduced for analyzing two-layer neural network models where optimal direct and inverse approximation theorems hold, as well as the
a priori
estimate
[
22
]
. The flow-induced function spaces
[
23
]
were introduced for analyzing residual neural networks
[
32
]
, which have wide applications in computer vision and scientific machine learning.

2
A Class of Integral Probability Metrics

To define a metric on the space of probability measures, one natural approach is to choose a suitable class
{\Phi}
of test functions on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and compare the difference of integrals.

Definition 2.1

(Integral Probability Metrics
[
49
]
)
.

Let
{\Phi}
be a class of measurable functions on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that

\sup_{f\in{\Phi}}|f(x)|\leq C(1+\|x\|),

(3)

for a constant
C>0
depending on
{\Phi}
.
Then for any
\mu,\mu^{\prime}\in\mathcal{P}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, the integral probability metric (IPM)
\mathrm{D}_{\Phi}
associated to
\Phi
is defined as:

\mathrm{D}_{\Phi}(\mu,\mu^{\prime})=\sup_{f\in{\Phi}}|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f\,\mathrm{d}(\mu-\mu^{\prime})|.

(4)

In
[
66
]
, these metrics are called
probability metrics with a
\zeta
-structure
. In this paper, following
[
49
]
, we will stick to the more intuitive terminology IPM. Many probability metrics are based on the comparison of integrals of certain functions, for instance,

•

the class of 1-Lipschitz functions, which leads to 1-Wasserstein metric
\mathcal{W}_{1}
;

•

all functions
\bm{1}_{[t,\infty)}
,
t\in\mathbb{R}
, which gives the Kolmogorov metric on
\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R})
;

•

all functions
\bm{1}_{B}
with
B
being a Borel set on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, which leads to the total variation metric (in fact the set of continuous functions is sufficient);

•

the unit ball of a reproducing kernel Hilbert space (RKHS), which yields the maximum mean discrepancy (MMD) defined in
[
7
]
.

We are interested in the metrics with “dimension-free” properties, for instance, that the empirical measure obtained from
n
independent samples from a given measure
\mu
approaches
\mu
with a convergence speed not depending on
d
explicitly (for a precise statement, see Theorem
2.7
(c)). In contrast to working with a particular class of functions (as in
\mathcal{W}_{1}
or MMD), we pose conditions on
{\Phi}
, presented in Assumption
2.2
, to fulfill our goal. Later in Section
3
, we shall discuss several classes of test functions, including RKHS, Barron space, and flow-induced function spaces, where Assumption
2.2
is satisfied.

Assumption 2.2

(function class)
.

The set
{\Phi}
satisfies the following properties:

(a)

If
\mu
is a signed measure on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f\,\mathrm{d}\mu=0,\;\forall f\in{\Phi}\Rightarrow\mu\equiv 0;

(5)

(b)

There exist two constants
A_{1}:=\sup_{f\in\Phi}\mathrm{Lip}(f)<+\infty
and
A_{2}:=\sup_{f\in\Phi}|f(0)|<+\infty
;

(c)

There exists a constant
A_{3}>0
, such that for any
\mathcal{X}=\{x^{1},\dots,x^{n}\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the empirical Rademacher complexity satisfies

\mathrm{Rad}_{n}({\Phi},\mathcal{X})\coloneqq\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{E}\sup_{f\in{\Phi}}|\sum_{i=1}^{n}\xi_{i}f(x^{i})|\leq\frac{A_{3}}{n}\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\|x^{i}\|^{2}+1)},

(6)

where
\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{n}
are i.i.d. random variables drawn from the Rademacher distribution, i.e.,
\mathbb{P}(\xi_{i}=1)=\mathbb{P}(\xi_{i}=-1)=\frac{1}{2}
.

Remark 2.3
.

Given any
\Phi
satisfying Assumption
2.2
, we can define another function class:

\Phi^{\prime}=\{f-f(0):f\in\Phi\}.

(7)

Then it is obvious that
\mathrm{D}_{\Phi}(\mu,\mu^{\prime})=\mathrm{D}_{\Phi^{\prime}}(\mu,\mu^{\prime})
for any
\mu,\mu^{\prime}\in\mathcal{P}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and
\Phi^{\prime}
satisfies that

(a’)

If
\mu
is a signed measure on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

\mu(\mathbb{R}^{d})=0,\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f\,\mathrm{d}\mu=0,\;\forall f\in{\Phi^{\prime}}\Rightarrow\mu\equiv 0;

(8)

(b’)

\sup_{f\in\Phi^{\prime}}\mathrm{Lip}(f)=A_{1}<+\infty
and
\sup_{f\in\Phi^{\prime}}|f(0)|=0
;

(c’)

For any
\mathcal{X}=\{x^{1},\dots,x^{n}\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{nd}
, the empirical Rademacher complexity satisfies

\mathrm{Rad}_{n}({\Phi^{\prime}},\mathcal{X})\coloneqq\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{E}\sup_{f\in{\Phi^{\prime}}}|\sum_{i=1}^{n}\xi_{i}f(x^{i})|\leq\frac{A_{3}^{\prime}}{n}\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\|x^{i}\|^{2}+1)},

(9)

where
\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{n}
are i.i.d. random variables drawn from the Rademacher distribution, and
A_{3}^{\prime}
satisfies
A_{3}^{\prime}=A_{2}+A_{3}
with
A_{2}
,
A_{3}
defined from the original class
\Phi
.

We can replace Assumption
2.2
by (a’), (b’), and (c’), and all properties of the IPM in this work still hold. We choose to use Assumption
2.2
which allows
f(0)\neq 0
, and introduce
A_{2}=\sup_{f\in\Phi}|f(0)|
, mainly because this is the case for most examples we discussed in Section
3
.

Remark 2.4
.

Assumption
2.2
(c) is crucial for overcoming the CoD in our case. It is stronger than the usual estimation of the Rademacher complexity that depends on
n^{-1/2}[\max_{1\leq i\leq n}\|x^{i}\|+1]
rather than
n^{-1/2}\sqrt{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\|x^{i}\|^{2}+1)}
. Rademacher complexity measures the richness of a function class with respect to a specific probability distribution. It is a powerful tool to bound the generalization error when learning the function in the class through empirical risk minimization.
It is also closely related to other concepts for measuring the richness of a function class, such as the covering number and fat-shattering dimension, which will be discussed below. In addition, by comparing Theorem
2.7
(b) below and
[
24
, Corollaries 3.2 and 3.4]
), we know that in high dimensions, the richness of any class of functions satisfying Assumption
2.2
is relatively small compared to the class of all
A_{1}
-Lipschitz functions. We refer to
[
4
,
56
]
for further information about the Rademacher complexity.

We recall below the definitions of the covering number and fat-shattering dimension and show how to estimate the Rademacher complexity by them. These estimations provide more criteria for checking if a function class satisfies Assumption
2.2
(c).

Definition 2.5

(covering number)
.

Given a function class
\Phi
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
\mathcal{X}=\{x^{1},\dots,x^{n}\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\epsilon>0
, a subset
\hat{\Phi}\subset\Phi
is a
\epsilon
-covering of
\Phi
if for any
f\in\Phi
, there exists
\hat{f}\in\hat{\Phi}
such that

\|f-\hat{f}\|_{L^{2}(\mathcal{X})}:=\sqrt{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}[f(x^{i})-\hat{f}(x^{i})]^{2}}\leq\epsilon.

(10)

The covering number of
\Phi
is the cardinality of the smallest
\epsilon
-covering of
\Phi
:

\mathcal{C}(\Phi,\epsilon,L^{2}(\mathcal{X}))=\min\{|\hat{\Phi}|:\hat{\Phi}\text{ is a $\epsilon$-covering of }\Phi\}.

(11)

Definition 2.6

(fat-shattering dimension)
.

Given a function class
\Phi
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
\mathbb{I}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\epsilon>0
. We say
\mathbb{I}
is
\epsilon
-shattered by
\Phi
if there exists
h:\mathbb{I}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
such that for any
\mathbb{J}\subset\mathbb{I}
, there exists
f\in\Phi
such that

f(x)\leq h(x),\forall x\in\mathbb{J},\,f(x)>h(x)+\epsilon,\forall x\in\mathbb{I}\backslash\mathbb{J}.

(12)

The fat-shattering dimension of
\Phi
is the largest cardinality of
\epsilon
-shattering:

\mathrm{vc}(\Phi,\epsilon)=\max\{|\mathbb{I}|:\mathbb{I}\text{ is $\epsilon$-shattered by }\Phi\}.

If
\Phi
is a
\{0,1\}
-value function class, the fat-shattering dimension coincides with the classic Vapnik-Chernoveniks (VC) dimension; see
[
45
]
for a detailed introduction of the VC dimension. We refer to
[
59
]
for a detailed introduction of the covering number, VC dimension, and fat-shattering dimension.

The following famous inequality by Dudley
[
52
]
gives an upper bound of the Rademacher complexity by the covering number:

\mathrm{Rad}_{n}(\Phi,\mathcal{X})\leq\inf_{\epsilon>0}\{4\epsilon+\frac{12}{\sqrt{n}}\int_{\epsilon}^{c}\sqrt{\log\mathcal{C}(\Phi,t,L^{2}(\mathcal{X}))}\,\mathrm{d}t\},

(13)

where
c=\sup_{f\in\Phi}\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n}f^{2}(x_{i})/n}
.
Let
p>0
. We will use the
O(\cdot)
notation to ignore the constant term which depends only on
p
. Now assume that the function class
\Phi
satisfies

\log\mathcal{C}(\Phi,t,L^{2}(\mathcal{X}))\leq O(\frac{c^{p}}{t^{p}}),

(14)

then one has that when
p>2
,

\mathrm{Rad}_{n}(\Phi,\mathcal{X})\leq O(\inf_{\epsilon>0}\{\epsilon+c^{\frac{p}{2}}n^{-\frac{1}{2}}\epsilon^{1-\frac{p}{2}}-cn^{-\frac{1}{2}}\})\leq O(cn^{-\frac{1}{p}});

(15)

when
p=2
,

\mathrm{Rad}_{n}(\Phi,\mathcal{X})\leq O(\inf_{\epsilon>0}\{\epsilon+cn^{-\frac{1}{2}}[\log c-\log\epsilon]\})\leq O(cn^{-\frac{1}{2}}+cn^{-\frac{1}{2}}[\log c-\log cn^{-\frac{1}{2}}])=O(cn^{-\frac{1}{2}}\log n);

(16)

and when
0<p<2
,

\mathrm{Rad}_{n}(\Phi,\mathcal{X})\leq O(\inf_{\epsilon>0}\{\epsilon+cn^{-\frac{1}{2}}-c^{\frac{p}{2}}n^{-\frac{1}{2}}\epsilon^{1-\frac{p}{2}}\})=O(cn^{-\frac{1}{2}}).

(17)

In summary,

\mathrm{Rad}_{n}(\Phi,\mathcal{X})\leq\begin{cases}O(cn^{-\frac{1}{p}}),&\text{ when }p>2\\
O(cn^{-\frac{1}{2}}\log n),&\text{ when }p=2\\
O(cn^{-\frac{1}{2}}),&\text{ when }0<p<2.\end{cases}

(18)

Noticing that if
\Phi
satisfies Assumption
2.2
(b), we have that

c=\sup_{f\in\Phi}\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{f^{2}(x_{i})}{n}}\leq\frac{\sqrt{2}\max\{A_{1},A_{2}\}}{\sqrt{n}}\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n}[\|x^{i}\|^{2}+1]}.

(19)

Therefore, when
p<2
, the function class
\Phi
satisfies Assumption
2.2
(c). When
p\geq 2
, the convergence rate of the Rademacher complexity in (
18
) is slower than the rate
n^{-\frac{1}{2}}
in Assumption
2.2
(c), but most results in this work still hold if we change the convergence rate to
n^{-\frac{1}{p}}
or
n^{-\frac{1}{2}}\log n
in these results.

The fat-shattering dimension can be used to bound the covering number and hence bound the Rademacher complexity. Assume that there exists
\xi:\mathbb{R}^{+}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{+}
and
\alpha>1
such that

\mathrm{vc}(\Phi,\epsilon)\leq\xi(\epsilon),\quad\xi(\alpha\epsilon)\leq\frac{\xi(\epsilon)}{8},

(20)

then for any
\mathcal{X}=\{x^{1},\dots,x^{n}\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, there exists
C>0
depending only on
\alpha
such that

\log\mathcal{C}(\Phi,\epsilon,L^{2}(\mathcal{X}))\leq C\xi(C\epsilon).

(21)

For further details, see
[
53
, Theorem 1.3]
and
[
59
, Corollary 7.48]
.

In our case, if
\xi(\epsilon)=\epsilon^{-p}
, one can obtain that
\log\mathcal{C}(\Phi,\epsilon,L^{2}(\mathcal{X}))=O(\epsilon^{-p})
and then use inequality (
18
) to bound the Rademacher complexity.

defined as follows

\mathcal{W}_{p}(\mu,\mu^{\prime})=\Bigl(\inf_{\gamma\in\Gamma(\mu,\mu^{\prime})}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}\|x-y\|^{p}\,\mathrm{d}\gamma(x,y)\Bigr)^{1/p},

(22)

where
\mu,\mu^{\prime}\in\mathcal{P}^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and
\Gamma(\mu,\mu^{\prime})
denotes the collection of all probability distributions on
\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
with marginals
\mu
and
\mu^{\prime}
on the first and second arguments, respectively. Note that Definition
2.1
with
\Phi=\{\text{all continuous 1-Lipschitz functions from }\mathbb{R}^{d}\text{ to }\mathbb{R}\}
admits the dual representation of (
22
) with
p=1
(
cf.

[
5
, Theorem 1.3]
). We also introduce the relative entropy or Kullback-Leibler divergence

\mathcal{H}(\mu|\mu^{\prime})\coloneqq\mathbb{E}^{\mu}\left(\log(\frac{\,\mathrm{d}\mu}{\,\mathrm{d}\mu^{\prime}})\right),

(23)

and the total variation distance

\|\mu-\mu^{\prime}\|=\sup_{A\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}:\text{Borel measurable set}}|\mu(A)-\mu^{\prime}(A)|.

(24)

2.1
Convergence Analysis for Random Variables

Theorem 2.7
.

Under Assumption
2.2
, we have:

(a)

\mathrm{D}_{\Phi}
is a metric on
\mathcal{P}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. In addition, if
\mu,\mu^{\prime}\in\mathcal{P}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
,

\mathrm{D}_{\Phi}(\mu,\mu^{\prime})\leq A_{1}\mathcal{W}_{1}(\mu,\mu^{\prime})\leq A_{1}\mathcal{W}_{2}(\mu,\mu^{\prime}).

(25)

(b)

Let
K
be a compact set in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
\{\mu_{i}\}_{i=1}^{\infty},\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
such that
\mu_{i}(K)=\mu(K)=1
for
i\geq 1
. Then the following statements are equivalent:

1.

\mu_{i}
converges to
\mu
in the weak sense;

2.

\lim_{i\rightarrow\infty}\mathrm{D}_{\Phi}(\mu_{i},\mu)=0
.

In other words,
\mathrm{D}_{\Phi}
metrizes the weak convergence of measures on compact sets.

(c)

Given
\mu\in\mathcal{P}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, let
X^{1},\dots,X^{n}
be i.i.d. random variables drawn from the distribution
\mu
and

\bar{\mu}^{n}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{X^{i}}

(26)

be the empirical measure of
X^{1},\ldots,X^{n}
. Then,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\mathrm{D}_{{\Phi}}(\mu,\bar{\mu}^{n})\leq\frac{2A_{3}}{\sqrt{n}}\sqrt{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}[\|x\|^{2}+1]\,\mathrm{d}\mu(x)},

(27)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{D}_{\Phi}^{2}(\mu,\bar{\mu}^{n})]\leq\frac{A_{1}^{2}}{n}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\|x\|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\mu(x)+\frac{4A_{3}^{2}}{n}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}[\|x\|^{2}+1]\,\mathrm{d}\mu(x).

(28)

(d)

If
\mu
satisfies the
\mathrm{T}_{1}
inequality (cf.
[
29
]
),

\mathcal{W}_{1}^{2}(\mu,\tilde{\mu})\leq 2\kappa^{2}\mathcal{H}(\tilde{\mu}|\mu)\quad\forall\tilde{\mu}\ll\mu,

(29)

(an equivalent condition of the
\mathrm{T}_{1}
inequality is that, there exists a constant
\delta>0
such that
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\exp(\delta\|x-y\|^{2})\,\mathrm{d}\mu(x)\,\mathrm{d}\mu(y)<+\infty
; see
[
18
, Theorem 2.3]
),
then,

\mathbb{P}\left(\mathrm{D}_{\Phi}(\mu,\bar{\mu}^{n})-\frac{2A_{3}}{\sqrt{n}}\sqrt{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}[\|x\|^{2}+1]\,\mathrm{d}\mu(x)}\geq a\right)\leq\exp\left(-\frac{na^{2}}{2A_{1}^{2}\kappa^{2}}\right).

(30)

Proof.

For claim (a), by definition
\mathrm{D}_{\Phi}
is symmetric and satisfies the triangle inequality. We only need to show that for any
\mu,\mu^{\prime}\in\mathcal{P}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
,
\mathrm{D}_{\Phi}(\mu,\mu^{\prime})=0
leads to
\mu=\mu^{\prime}
, which is ensured directly by Assumption
2.2
(a). In addition, from Assumption
2.2
(b), we deduce that
\mathrm{D}_{\Phi}(\mu_{1},\mu_{2})\leq A_{1}\mathcal{W}_{1}(\mu_{1},\mu_{2})
by using the definition of
\mathcal{W}_{1}
.
The relation between
\mathcal{W}_{1}
and
\mathcal{W}_{2}
is a classical result following from Jensen’s inequality.

For claim (b), we first prove
1\Rightarrow 2
. By Theorem 8.3.2 in
[
6
]
, we know that

\lim_{i\rightarrow\infty}\mathcal{W}_{1}(\mu_{i},\mu)=0.

(31)

Then noticing that
\mathrm{D}_{\Phi}(\mu_{i},\mu)\leq A_{1}\mathcal{W}_{1}(\mu_{i},\mu)
, we obtain the result. For the claim
2\Rightarrow 1
, we first notice that
\{\mu_{i}\}_{i=1}^{\infty}
is tight. Then by Theorem 8.6.2 in
[
6
]
, every subsequence of
\{\mu_{i}\}_{i=1}^{\infty}
has a weakly convergent subsequence, denoted by
\{\mu_{i_{k}}\}_{k=1}^{\infty}
. Let
\mu^{\prime}
be the limit of
\{\mu_{i_{k}}\}_{k=1}^{\infty}
. Then for any
f\in\Phi
,

\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{i_{k}}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f\,\mathrm{d}\mu^{\prime}.

(32)

Meanwhile, since
\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\mathrm{D}_{\Phi}(\mu_{i_{k}},\mu)=0
, we have

\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{i_{k}}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f\,\mathrm{d}\mu.

(33)

Hence, for any
f\in\Phi
,
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f\,\mathrm{d}\mu=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f\,\mathrm{d}\mu^{\prime}
, which means that
\mu\equiv\mu^{\prime}
. In other words, every subsequence of
\{\mu_{i}\}_{i=1}^{\infty}
has a weakly convergent subsequence to
\mu
. Since the weakly convergence can be metrized
[
6
, Theorem 8.3.2]
, we know that
\{\mu_{i}\}_{i=1}^{\infty}
weakly converge to
\mu
.

For claim (c), we first notice that

\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{D}_{\Phi}(\mu,\bar{\mu}^{n})]=\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{f\in{\Phi}}\Bigl|\sum_{i=1}^{n}[f(X^{i})-\mathbb{E}f(X^{i})]\Bigr|\right]\leq\frac{2}{n}\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{f\in{\Phi}}\Bigl|\sum_{i=1}^{n}\xi_{i}f(X^{i})\Bigr|\right],

(34)

where in the last step we use the Rademacher complexity to bound the largest gap between the expectation of a function and its empirical version (see,
e.g.
,
[
56
, Lemma 26.2]
).
Using Assumption
2.2
(c),
one deduces

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\mathrm{D}_{{\Phi}}(\mu,\bar{\mu}^{n})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2A_{3}}{n}\mathbb{E}\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\|X^{i}\|^{2}+1)}\leq\frac{2A_{3}}{n}\sqrt{\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\|X^{i}\|^{2}+1)\right]}=\frac{2A_{3}}{\sqrt{n}}\sqrt{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}[\|x\|^{2}+1]\,\mathrm{d}\mu(x)}.

(35)

Now let
Y^{1},\dots,Y^{n}
be i.i.d. random variables drawn from the distribution
\mu
and be independent of
X^{1},\dots,X^{n}
.
By the Efron-Stein-Steele inequality (
cf.

[
9
, Theorem 5]
)
that reads

\mathrm{Var}(Z)\leq\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}[Z-Z_{i}^{\prime}]^{2},

(36)

for some measurable function
g
of
n
variables,
Z=g(X^{1},\ldots,X^{n})
and
Z^{\prime}_{i}=g(X^{1},\ldots,Y^{i},\ldots,X^{n})
, and the uniform Lipschitz property of
f\in\Phi
, one has

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}(\mathrm{D}_{{\Phi}}(\mu,\bar{\mu}^{n}))

(37)

\displaystyle=~

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}\left(\frac{1}{n}\sup_{f\in{\Phi}}\Bigl|\sum_{i=1}^{n}[f(X^{i})-\mathbb{E}f(X^{i})]\Bigr|\right)

\displaystyle\leq~

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\frac{1}{n}\sup_{f\in{\Phi}}\Bigl|\sum_{j=1}^{n}[f(X^{j})-\mathbb{E}f(X^{j})]\Bigr|-\frac{1}{n}\sup_{f\in{\Phi}}\Bigl|\sum_{j=1,j\neq i}^{n}[f(X^{j})-\mathbb{E}f(X^{j})]+f(Y^{i})-\mathbb{E}f(X^{i})\Bigr|\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq~

\displaystyle\frac{A_{1}^{2}}{2n^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}\|X^{i}-Y^{i}\|^{2}=\frac{A_{1}^{2}}{n}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\|x\|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\mu(x).

(38)

Therefore, we have

\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{D}^{2}_{{\Phi}}(\mu,\bar{\mu}^{n})]=\mathrm{Var}(\mathrm{D}_{{\Phi}}(\mu,\bar{\mu}^{n}))+[\mathbb{E}\mathrm{D}_{{\Phi}}(\mu,\bar{\mu}^{n})]^{2}\leq\frac{A_{1}^{2}}{n}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\|x\|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\mu(x)+\frac{4A_{3}^{2}}{n}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}[\|x\|^{2}+1]\,\mathrm{d}\mu(x).

(39)

For claim (d), we need some established concentration inequalities.
Let
\mu^{\otimes n}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d\times n})
be the
n^{th}
times product of
\mu
and the distance between
\bm{x}=(x^{1},\dots,x^{n}),\bm{y}=(y^{1},\dots,y^{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}
be

\|\bm{x}-\bm{y}\|=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|x^{i}-y^{i}\|.

(40)

By Theorem 5.2 in
[
17
]
, for any
\tilde{\mu}\ll\mu^{\otimes n}
, we have

\mathcal{W}_{1}^{2}(\mu^{\otimes n},\tilde{\mu})\leq 2n\kappa^{2}\mathcal{H}(\tilde{\mu}|\mu^{\otimes n}).

(41)

Define

G(\bm{x})=\sup_{f\in\Phi}|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}f(x^{i})-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f\,\mathrm{d}\mu|.

(42)

Combining the fact that

|G(\bm{x})-G(\bm{y})|\leq\frac{A_{1}}{n}\|\bm{x}-\bm{y}\|,

(43)

for any
\bm{x}
and
\bm{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}
with Theorem 5.1 in
[
17
]
, we have

\mathbb{P}\left(\mathrm{D}_{\Phi}(\mu,\bar{\mu}^{n})-\mathbb{E}\mathrm{D}_{\Phi}(\mu,\bar{\mu}^{n})\geq a\right)=\mu^{\otimes n}\Bigl(G(\bm{x})-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}}G(\bm{x})\,\mathrm{d}\mu^{\otimes n}(\bm{x})\geq a\Bigr)\leq\exp\left(-\frac{na^{2}}{2A_{1}^{2}\kappa^{2}}\right).

(44)

Finally, we conclude our proof by the observation

\mathbb{E}\mathrm{D}_{\Phi}(\mu,\bar{\mu}^{n})\leq\frac{2A_{3}}{\sqrt{n}}\sqrt{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}[\|x\|^{2}+1]\,\mathrm{d}\mu(x)},

(45)

from inequality (
35
).
∎

The above theorem focuses on the convergence of
\bar{\mu}^{n}
to
\mu
in the sense of IPM satisfying Assumption
2.2
. It is well-known that the convergence of empirical measures under the Wasserstein metric faces the CoD
[
21
]
for any distribution that is absolutely continuous with respect to the Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. However, as shown in
[
63
,
64
]
, if the target distribution admits a specific representation, the bias potential model or the density model, we can use the empirical measure and the representation form to generate a new distribution that is close to the target distribution in the sense of the Wasserstein metric, total variation distance, or relative entropy. The following theorem, generalizing results in
[
63
,
64
]
, shows that given a bias potential model or a density model as the target distribution and a distribution close to the target measured by our proposed IPM, we can generate a distribution close to the target one in terms of the Wasserstein metric, total variation distance or relative entropy.

Theorem 2.8
.

Suppose
P\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
is a known base distribution and
\Phi
is a function class satisfying Assumption
2.2
.

(a)

Assume the target distribution
\mu\in\mathcal{P}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
satisfies the bias potential model - that is, there exists
V^{*}\in\Phi
such that

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{-V^{*}(x)}\,\mathrm{d}P(x)<+\infty,\,\frac{\,\mathrm{d}\mu}{\,\mathrm{d}P}=\frac{e^{-V^{*}}}{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{-V^{*}(x)}\,\mathrm{d}P(x)}.

(46)

Let
\nu\in\mathcal{P}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
be an accessible probability measure, define

\mathrm{Loss}_{1}(V)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}V(x)\,\mathrm{d}\nu(x)+\log\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{-V(x)}\,\mathrm{d}P(x)\right),

(47)

for
V\in\Phi
. Let
V^{\prime}\in\Phi
and
\mu^{\prime}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
satisfy

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{-V^{\prime}(x)}\,\mathrm{d}P(x)<+\infty,\,\frac{\,\mathrm{d}\mu^{\prime}}{\,\mathrm{d}P}=\frac{e^{-V^{\prime}}}{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{-V‘’}\,\mathrm{d}P(x)}.

(48)

Then
\inf_{V\in\Phi}\mathrm{Loss}_{1}(V)>-\infty
and

\mathcal{H}(\mu|\mu^{\prime})\leq 2\mathrm{D}_{\Phi}(\mu,\nu)+\mathrm{Loss}_{1}(V^{\prime})-\inf_{V\in\Phi}\mathrm{Loss}_{1}(V).

(49)

Assume additionally that
P
has a compact support

K=\max\{\|x-y\|,x,y\in\mathrm{supp}(P)\}<+\infty,

(50)

then

\mathcal{W}_{1}^{2}(\mu,\mu^{\prime})\leq\frac{K^{2}}{2}[2\mathrm{D}_{\Phi}(\mu,\nu)+\mathrm{Loss}_{1}(V^{\prime})-\inf_{V\in\Phi}\mathrm{Loss}_{1}(V)].

(51)

(b)

Assume the target distribution
\mu\in\mathcal{P}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
satisfies the density model - that is, there exists
q^{*}\in\Phi
such that

q^{*}=\frac{\,\mathrm{d}\mu}{\,\mathrm{d}P}.

Let
\nu\in\mathcal{P}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
be an accessible probability measure, define

\mathrm{Loss}_{2}(q)=\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f\,\mathrm{d}\nu-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}fq\,\mathrm{d}P|,

(52)

for
q\in\Phi
. Let
q^{\prime}\in\Phi
and
\mu^{\prime}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
satisfy
q^{\prime}=\frac{\,\mathrm{d}\mu^{\prime}}{\,\mathrm{d}P}
. Then

\inf_{q\in\Phi}\mathrm{Loss}_{2}(q)\leq\mathrm{D}_{\Phi}(\mu,\nu),

(53)

and

\|\mu-\mu^{\prime}\|_{TV}^{2}\leq 2[\mathrm{D}_{\Phi}(\mu,\nu)+\mathrm{Loss}_{2}(q^{\prime})].

(54)

Assume additionally that
P
has a compact support

K=\max\{\|x-y\|,x,y\in\mathrm{supp}(P)\}<+\infty,

(55)

then

\mathcal{W}_{1}^{2}(\mu,\mu^{\prime})\leq 2K^{2}[\mathrm{D}_{\Phi}(\mu,\nu)+\mathrm{Loss}_{2}(q^{\prime})].

(56)

Proof.

For the claim (a), first noticing that
\mathrm{Loss}_{1}(V)
is lower bounded for
V\in\Phi
using
|V(x)|\leq A_{1}\|x\|+A_{2}
, one know that

\displaystyle\inf_{V\in\Phi}\mathrm{Loss}_{1}(V)

\displaystyle\geq-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}[A_{1}\|x\|+A_{2}]\,\mathrm{d}\nu(x)+\log(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{-[A_{1}\|x\|+A_{2}]}\,\mathrm{d}P(x))

(57)

\displaystyle\geq-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}[A_{1}\|x\|+A_{2}]\,\mathrm{d}\nu(x)+\log(\int_{\|x\|\leq M}e^{-[A_{1}\|x\|+A_{2}]}\,\mathrm{d}P(x))

(58)

\displaystyle\geq-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}[A_{1}\|x\|+A_{2}]\,\mathrm{d}\nu(x)+\log(e^{-MA_{1}-A_{2}}P(\|x\|\leq M))

(59)

\displaystyle=-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}[A_{1}\|x\|+A_{2}]\,\mathrm{d}\nu(x)-(MA_{1}+A_{2})+\log(P(\|x\|\leq M))>-\infty,

(60)

where
M
is large enough such that
P(\|x\|\leq M)>0
.
It is then easy to compute

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}(\mu|\mu^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(V^{\prime}-V^{*})(x)\,\mathrm{d}\mu(x)-\log\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{-V^{*}(x)}\,\mathrm{d}P(x)\right)+\log\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{-V^{\prime}(x)}\,\mathrm{d}P(x)\right)

\displaystyle\leq 2\mathrm{D}_{\Phi}(\mu,\nu)+\mathrm{Loss}(V^{\prime})-\mathrm{Loss}(V^{*})\leq 2\mathrm{D}_{\Phi}(\mu,\nu)+\mathrm{Loss}(V^{\prime})-\inf_{V\in\Phi}\mathrm{Loss}(V).

(61)

To estimate the Wasserstein metric between
\mu
and
\mu^{\prime}
, first notice the Pinsker’s inequality
[
51
,
16
]
:

\|\mu-\mu^{\prime}\|_{TV}^{2}\leq\frac{1}{2}\mathcal{H}(\mu|\mu^{\prime}).

(62)

Recalling the definition of the 1-Wasserstein metric (
22
) and
\mathrm{supp}(\mu),\mathrm{supp}(\mu^{\prime})\in\mathrm{supp}(P)
, we have

\mathcal{W}_{1}(\mu,\mu^{\prime})=\inf_{\gamma\in\Gamma(\mu,\mu^{\prime})}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}\|x-y\|\,\mathrm{d}\gamma(x,y)\leq K\inf_{\gamma\in\Gamma(\mu,\mu^{\prime})}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathrm{1}_{x\neq y}\,\mathrm{d}\gamma(x,y)=K\|\mu-\mu^{\prime}\|_{TV},

(63)

which concludes the proof.
For the claim (b), first noticing that

\mathrm{D}_{\Phi}(\mu,\nu)=\mathrm{Loss}_{2}(q^{*})\geq\inf_{q\in\Phi}\mathrm{Loss}_{2}(q).

(64)

By the triangular inequality,

\mathrm{D}_{\Phi}(\mu,\mu^{\prime})\leq\mathrm{D}_{\Phi}(\mu,\nu)+\mathrm{D}_{\Phi}(\nu,\mu^{\prime})=\mathrm{D}_{\Phi}(\mu,\nu)+\mathrm{Loss}_{2}(q^{\prime}).

(65)

Noticing that

\mathrm{D}_{\Phi}(\mu,\mu^{\prime})=\sup_{f\in\Phi}|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f(q^{*}-q^{\prime})\,\mathrm{d}P|\geq\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|q^{*}-q^{\prime}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}P\geq\frac{1}{2}[\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|q^{*}-q|\,\mathrm{d}P]^{2}=\frac{1}{2}\|\mu-\mu^{\prime}\|_{TV}^{2},

(66)

as
\frac{1}{2}(q^{*}-q^{\prime})\in\Phi
. The rest of claim (b) follows (
63
).
∎

Theorem
2.8
shows that if one knows that the target distribution satisfies a bias potential model or density model and can access to a distribution which is close to the target distribution with respect to a IPM (usually the empirical distribution by Theorem
2.7
), one can then solve the optimization problem

\inf_{V\in\Phi}\mathrm{Loss}_{1}(V)\quad\text{or}\quad\inf_{q\in\Phi}\mathrm{Loss}_{2}(q),

(67)

and the resulting distribution is close to the target distribution with respect to the Wasserstein metric, total variation distance or relative entropy.
Besides the bias potential model and density model we study here, another important form of distribution representation is the generative adversarial network (GAN)
[
28
,
1
]
, which assumes
\mu=\phi\circ P
(this notation stands for the measure
P
push-forwarded by
\phi
) with a known base distribution
P\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and
\phi
lies in certain function classes. In practice, GAN has shown astonishing power in learning distribution
[
10
,
19
]
. However, how to establish similar theoretical results with respect to GAN is far from clear.

2.2
Convergence Analysis for Stochastic Processes

Theorems in the previous subsection focus on the measure
\mu
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. When dealing with empirical measures of a stochastic process such as discussing the convergence of the
n
-particle dynamics to the McKean-Vlasov system (see Section
4
for details),
i.e.
,
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(C([0,T];\mathbb{R}^{d}))
, we need to extend Theorem
2.7
to Theorem
2.11
below. The results are similar, except for an additional term
\phi(n)
coming from the regularity of the process. To this end, we introduce the following assumption.

Assumption 2.9

(modulus of continuity)
.

Given a probability measure
\mu
on
C([0,T];\mathbb{R}^{d})
, there exist constants
Q,\alpha,\beta>0
, such that for any
h>0
,

\int_{C([0,T];\mathbb{R}^{d})}\Delta(x,h)\,\mathrm{d}\mu(x)\leq Qh^{\alpha}\log^{\beta}\left(\frac{2T}{h}\right),

(68)

where
\Delta(f,h)
denotes the modulus of continuity of
f\in C([0,T];\mathbb{R}^{d})
:

\Delta(f,h)=\sup_{t,s\in[0,T],|t-s|\leq h}\|f(t)-f(s)\|.

(69)

Remark 2.10
.

The condition (
68
) is satisfied by many kinds of processes. For instance, see
[
26
, Theorem 1]
for results for Brownian motion and Itô diffusion processes with
\alpha=\beta=\frac{1}{2}
, under quite generic conditions on the drift and diffusion coefficients. Note that the constant
Q
derived therein may depend on the dimension
d
, while the result in our Theorem
2.13
does not.

Theorem 2.11
.

Under Assumptions
2.2
and
2.9
,

(a)

Let
\mu
be a probability distribution on
C([0,T];\mathbb{R}^{d})
such that

\int_{C([0,T];\mathbb{R}^{d})}\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}\|x_{t}\|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\mu(x)<+\infty.

(70)

Denote by
X^{1},\cdots,X^{n}
i.i.d. random processes drawn from
\mu
, and

\bar{\mu}_{t}^{n}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{X_{t}^{i}}

(71)

as the empirical measure of
X_{t}^{1},\dots,X_{t}^{n}
. Define
\mu_{t}=\mathcal{L}(X_{t}^{1})
, then

\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}\mathrm{D}_{\Phi}(\mu_{t},\bar{\mu}_{t}^{n})\right]\leq\phi(n),

(72)

where

\displaystyle\phi(n)=~

\displaystyle 2\left[\frac{A_{3}+8\max\{A_{1},A_{2}\}}{\sqrt{n}}+8\max\{A_{1},A_{2}\}\sqrt{\frac{\log(2^{2\alpha}n)}{2\alpha n}}\right]\left(\int_{C([0,T];\mathbb{R}^{d})}\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}\|x_{t}\|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\mu(x)+1\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle+2\frac{A_{1}QT^{\alpha}}{\sqrt{n}}\left(\frac{\log(2^{4\alpha}n)}{2\alpha}\right)^{\beta}

\displaystyle=~

\displaystyle O(n^{-\frac{1}{2}}(\log n)^{\max\{\beta,\frac{1}{2}\}}).

(73)

In addition,

\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}\mathrm{D}^{2}_{\Phi}(\mu_{t},\bar{\mu}_{t}^{n})\right]\leq\frac{A_{1}^{2}}{n}\int_{C([0,T];\mathbb{R}^{d})}\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}\|x_{t}\|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\mu(x)+\phi^{2}(n).

(74)

(b)

If
\mu
satisfies the
\mathrm{T}_{1}
inequality, that is,

\mathcal{W}_{1}^{2}(\mu,\tilde{\mu})\leq 2\kappa^{2}\mathcal{H}(\tilde{\mu}|\mu)\quad\forall\tilde{\mu}\ll\mu,

(75)

where
\mathcal{H}
is the relative entropy defined in Theorem
2.7
(d), then

\mathbb{P}\left(\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}\mathrm{D}_{\Phi}(\mu_{t},\bar{\mu}_{t}^{n})-\phi(n)\geq a\right)\leq\exp\left(-\frac{na^{2}}{2A_{1}^{2}\kappa^{2}}\right).

(76)

Remark 2.12
.

The logarithmatic term in
\phi(n)
can be removed for many kinds of function classes
\Phi
, in particular for the function classes discussed in Section
3
. We defer the proof of this claim to
B
.

Proof.

The proof can be reduced to establishing (
72
). The estimate of
\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}\mathrm{D}_{\Phi}^{2}(\mu_{t},\bar{\mu}_{t}^{n})\right]
and (b) can be indeed derived from (
72
), and by following the proof arguments of Theorem
2.7
. The class
\Phi
in Assumption
2.2
is invariant up to an additive constant and so without loss of generality,
\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}\mathrm{D}_{\Phi}(\mu_{t},\bar{\mu}_{t}^{n})\right]
can be reduced to

\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{E}\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\sup_{f\in{\Phi}}\Bigl|\sum_{i=1}^{n}\xi_{i}f(X_{t}^{i})\Bigr|,

(77)

where
\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{n}
are i.i.d. random variables drawn from the Rademacher distribution and are independent of the processes
X^{1},\dots,X^{n}
.

To this end, given any
y^{1},\dots,y^{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we define
F(\xi)
for
\xi=(\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{n})\in\{-1,1\}^{\otimes n}
by

F(\xi)\coloneqq\frac{1}{n}\sup_{f\in{\Phi}}\Bigl|\sum_{i=1}^{n}\xi_{i}f(y^{i})\Bigr|.

(78)

By Assumption
2.2
(c), we immediately have

\mathbb{E}F(\xi)\leq\frac{A_{3}}{n}\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\|y^{i}\|^{2}+1)}.

(79)

By definition, for any
\xi
and
\epsilon>0
, there exists a function
f^{\xi}\in{\Phi}
such that

F(\xi)\leq\frac{1}{n}\Bigl|\sum_{i=1}^{n}\xi_{i}f^{\xi}(y^{i})\Bigr|+\epsilon.

(80)

Note that, for any
1\leq j\leq n
, one has

\displaystyle D_{j}F(\xi)

\displaystyle\coloneqq F(\xi)-\min_{z\in\{0,1\}}F(\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{j-1},z,\xi_{j+1},\dots,\xi_{n})

\displaystyle\leq\epsilon+\frac{1}{n}\Bigl|\sum_{i=1}^{n}\xi_{i}f^{\xi}(y^{i})\Bigr|-\min_{z\in\{0,1\}}F(\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{j-1},z,\xi_{j+1},\dots,\xi_{n})

\displaystyle\leq\epsilon+\frac{1}{n}\Bigl|\sum_{i=1}^{n}\xi_{i}f^{\xi}(y^{i})\Bigr|-\min_{z\in\{0,1\}}\frac{1}{n}\Bigl|\sum_{i\neq j}\xi_{i}f^{\xi}(y^{i})+zf^{\xi}(y^{j})\Bigr|

\displaystyle\leq\epsilon+\frac{2}{n}|f^{\xi}(y^{j})|.

(81)

Taking the square and summing the above inequality over
j
and next taking the supremum over all
\xi
gives,

\sup_{\xi}\sum_{j=1}^{n}|D_{j}F(\xi)|^{2}\leq\sup_{\xi}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left(\epsilon+\frac{2}{n}|f^{\xi}(y^{j})|\right)^{2}\leq\sup_{f\in{\Phi}}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left(\epsilon+\frac{2}{n}|f(y^{j})|\right)^{2},

(82)

holding true for arbitrary
\epsilon
, which implies

\displaystyle\sup_{\xi}\sum_{j=1}^{n}|D_{j}F(\xi)|^{2}\leq\frac{4}{n^{2}}\sup_{f\in{\Phi}}\sum_{j=1}^{n}|f(y^{j})|^{2}\leq\frac{8}{n^{2}}\sum_{j=1}^{n}(A_{1}^{2}\|y^{j}\|^{2}+A_{2}^{2}).

(83)

Therefore, by the bounded difference inequality (
[
59
, Theorem 3.18]
), we obtain

\mathbb{P}(F(\xi)-\mathbb{E}F(\xi)\geq a)\leq\exp\left(-\frac{n^{2}a^{2}}{64\sum_{j=1}^{n}(A_{1}^{2}\|y^{j}\|^{2}+A_{2}^{2})}\right).

(84)

Given
n^{\prime}\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
,
x^{1},\dots,x^{n}\in C([0,T];\mathbb{R}^{d})
and
t_{1},\dots,t_{n^{\prime}}\in[0,T]
, we define the constant

M\coloneqq\sqrt{\max_{1\leq p\leq n^{\prime}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\|x^{i}_{t_{p}}\|^{2}+1)},

based on the deterministic values
\{x^{i}_{t_{p}}\}_{p=1}^{n^{\prime}}
, for
i=1,\ldots,n
.
By (
79
), we have

\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{n}\sup_{f\in{\Phi}}\Bigl|\sum_{i=1}^{n}\xi_{i}f(x_{t_{p}}^{i})\Bigr|\right]\leq\frac{A_{3}}{n}M,\;\forall\;p=1,\ldots,n^{\prime}.

(85)

Using the Boole’s inequality and (
84
)-(
85
), we have the following estimation of the tail probability, for any
a>0
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\frac{1}{n}\max_{1\leq p\leq n^{\prime}}\sup_{f\in{\Phi}}\Bigl|\sum_{i=1}^{n}\xi_{i}f(x_{t_{p}}^{i})\Bigr|-\frac{A_{3}}{n}M\geq\frac{8\sqrt{\log n^{\prime}}\max\{A_{1},A_{2}\}}{n}M+a\right)

(86)

\displaystyle\leq~

\displaystyle n^{\prime}\max_{1\leq p\leq n^{\prime}}\mathbb{P}\left(\frac{1}{n}\sup_{f\in{\Phi}}\Bigl|\sum_{i=1}^{n}\xi_{i}f(x_{t_{p}}^{i})\Bigr|-\frac{A_{3}}{n}M\geq\frac{8\sqrt{\log n^{\prime}}\max\{A_{1},A_{2}\}}{n}M+a\right)

\displaystyle\leq~

\displaystyle n^{\prime}\max_{1\leq p\leq n^{\prime}}\mathbb{P}\left(\frac{1}{n}\sup_{f\in{\Phi}}\Bigl|\sum_{i=1}^{n}\xi_{i}f(x_{t_{p}}^{i})\Bigr|-\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{n}\sup_{f\in{\Phi}}\Bigl|\sum_{i=1}^{n}\xi_{i}f(x_{t_{p}}^{i})\Bigr|\right]\geq\frac{8\sqrt{\log n^{\prime}}\max\{A_{1},A_{2}\}}{n}M+a\right)

\displaystyle\leq~

\displaystyle n^{\prime}\exp\left(-\frac{n^{2}[\frac{8\sqrt{\log n^{\prime}}\max\{A_{1},A_{2}\}}{n}M+a]^{2}}{64\max\{A_{1}^{2},A_{2}^{2}\}M^{2}}\right)

(87)

\displaystyle\leq~

\displaystyle n^{\prime}\exp(-\log(n^{\prime}))\exp\left(-\frac{n^{2}a^{2}}{64\max\{A_{1}^{2},A_{2}^{2}\}M^{2}}\right)

(88)

\displaystyle\leq~

\displaystyle\exp\left(-\frac{n^{2}a^{2}}{64\max\{A_{1}^{2},A_{2}^{2}\}M^{2}}\right).

(89)

Therefore,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{E}\max_{1\leq p\leq n^{\prime}}\sup_{f\in{\Phi}}\Bigl|\sum_{i=1}^{n}\xi_{i}f(x_{t_{p}}^{i})\Bigr|

(90)

\displaystyle\leq~

\displaystyle\frac{A_{3}}{n}M+\frac{8\sqrt{\log n^{\prime}}\max\{A_{1},A_{2}\}}{n}M+\int_{0}^{+\infty}\exp\left(-\frac{n^{2}a^{2}}{64\max\{A_{1}^{2},A_{2}^{2}\}M^{2}}\right)\,\mathrm{d}a

(91)

\displaystyle\leq~

\displaystyle\frac{A_{3}+8(\sqrt{\log n^{\prime}}+1)\max\{A_{1},A_{2}\}}{n}M

(92)

\displaystyle=~

\displaystyle\frac{A_{3}+8(\sqrt{\log n^{\prime}}+1)\max\{A_{1},A_{2}\}}{n}\sqrt{\max_{1\leq p\leq n^{\prime}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\|x^{i}_{t_{p}}\|^{2}+1)}.

(93)

The above estimate is for deterministic functions in
C([0,T];\mathbb{R}^{d})
evaluated at time points
t_{1},\ldots,t_{n^{\prime}}
. Applying it to i.i.d. continuous stochastic processes
\{X^{i}_{\cdot}\}_{i=1}^{n}
with the law
\mu
and independent of
\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{n}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{E}\max_{1\leq p\leq n^{\prime}}\sup_{f\in{\Phi}}\Bigl|\sum_{i=1}^{n}\xi_{i}f(X_{t_{p}}^{i})\Bigr|

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{E}\Bigl\{\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\max_{1\leq p\leq n^{\prime}}\sup_{f\in{\Phi}}\Bigl|\sum_{i=1}^{n}\xi_{i}f(X_{t_{p}}^{i})\Bigr|\,\big|\,X^{1}_{\cdot},\dots,X^{n}_{\cdot}\Bigr]\Bigr\}

(94)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{A_{3}+8(\sqrt{\log n^{\prime}}+1)\max\{A_{1},A_{2}\}}{n}\mathbb{E}\sqrt{\max_{1\leq p\leq n^{\prime}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\|X_{t_{p}}^{i}\|^{2}+1)}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{A_{3}+8(\sqrt{\log n^{\prime}}+1)\max\{A_{1},A_{2}\}}{\sqrt{n}}\left(\int_{C([0,T];\mathbb{R}^{d})}\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}\|x_{t}\|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\mu(x)+1\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

(95)

Using the above estimate and letting
t_{0},t_{1},\ldots,t_{n}
define a partition of
[0,T]
, that is,
t_{p}=\frac{(p-1)T}{n^{\prime}}
for
p=1,\dots,n^{\prime}
, by Assumption
2.9
, and with the notation
\Pi(t)=\lfloor\frac{n^{\prime}t}{T}\rfloor\frac{T}{n^{\prime}}
, one can deduce

\displaystyle\left|\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{E}\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\sup_{f\in{\Phi}}\Bigl|\sum_{i=1}^{n}\xi_{i}f(X_{t}^{i})\Bigr|-\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{E}\sup_{1\leq p\leq n^{\prime}}\sup_{f\in{\Phi}}\Bigl|\sum_{i=1}^{n}\xi_{i}f(X_{t_{p}}^{i})\Bigr|\right|

\displaystyle\leq~

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{E}\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\sup_{f\in{\Phi}}\Bigl|\sum_{i=1}^{n}\xi_{i}\left(f(X_{t}^{i})-f(X_{\Pi(t)}^{i})\right)\Bigr|

\displaystyle\leq~

\displaystyle A_{1}\mathbb{E}\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\|X_{t}^{1}-X_{\Pi(t)}^{1}\|\leq A_{1}Q(\frac{T}{n^{\prime}})^{\alpha}\log^{\beta}(2n^{\prime}).

(96)

Choosing

gives

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{E}\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\sup_{f\in{\Phi}}\Bigl|\sum_{i=1}^{n}\xi_{i}f(X_{t}^{i})\Bigr|

(97)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left[\frac{A_{3}+8\max\{A_{1},A_{2}\}}{\sqrt{n}}+8\max\{A_{1},A_{2}\}\sqrt{\frac{\log(2^{2\alpha}n)}{2\alpha n}}\right]\left(\int_{C([0,T];\mathbb{R}^{d})}\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}\|x_{t}\|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\mu(x)+1\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}

(98)

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{A_{1}QT^{\alpha}}{\sqrt{n}}\left(\frac{\log(2^{4\alpha}n)}{2\alpha}\right)^{\beta}.

(99)

Therefore we have proved (
72
). ∎

3.1
The Reproducing Kernel Hilbert Spaces (RKHSs)

RKHS has developed into an essential tool in many areas, especially statistics and machine learning
[
33
]
. We first recall its definition: a Hilbert space of functions
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
, is said to be an RKHS if all evaluation functionals are bounded and linear. A more intuitive definition is through the so-called reproducing kernel.

A symmetric function
k:\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is called a positive kernel function on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, if

\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}a_{i}a_{j}k(x^{i},x^{j})\geq 0

(107)

holds for any
n\in\mathbb{N},x^{1},\dots,x^{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and
a_{1},\dots,a_{n}\in\mathbb{R}
. We then define the inner product space

\{f(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\alpha_{i}k(x,x_{i}):m\in\mathbb{N}^{+},\{\alpha_{i}\}_{i=1}^{m}\subset\mathbb{R}^{m},\{x_{i}\}_{i=1}^{m}\subset\mathbb{R}^{dm}\}

(108)

with the inner product

\langle f,g\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{k}}=\sum_{i=1}^{m^{f}}\sum_{j=1}^{m^{g}}\alpha_{i}^{f}\alpha_{j}^{f}k(x_{i}^{f},x_{j}^{g}),\;\forall f(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{m^{f}}\alpha_{i}^{f}k(x,x_{i}^{f}),\;g(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{m^{g}}\alpha_{i}^{g}k(x,x_{i}^{g}).

(109)

The reproducing kernel Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}_{k}
is the completion of the inner product space with respect to
\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal{H}_{k}}=\sqrt{\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{k}}}
.
Moreover,
\mathcal{H}_{k}
satisfies the reproducing property:

f(x)=\langle f,k(x,\cdot)\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{k}},\forall x\in\mathbb{R}^{d},f\in\mathcal{H}_{k}.

(110)

In particular, the function
k
, called the reproducing kernel of
\mathcal{H}_{k}
, satisfies

k(x,y)=\langle k(x,\cdot),k(y,\cdot)\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{k}}.

(111)

We refer the interested readers to
[
2
]
for more properties of RKHSs. Theorem
3.1
below guarantees that RKHSs associated with Gaussian kernels, Laplacian kernels and neural tangent kernels satisfy Assumption
2.2
. We remark that the choice in Theorem
3.1
reproduces the maximum mean discrepancy in
[
7
]
.

Theorem 3.1
.

Assume the reproducing kernel
k(\cdot,\cdot)
satisfies:

(a)

There exist constants
K_{1},K_{2}>0
, such that
\forall x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
k(x,x)+k(y,y)-2k(x,y)\leq K_{1}^{2}\|x-y\|^{2}
and
K_{2}=\sqrt{k(0,0)}
;

(b)

If
\mu
is a signed measure on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}k(x,y)\,\mathrm{d}\mu(y)=0,\;\forall x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\Rightarrow\mu\equiv 0.

(112)

Then for any
\mu\in\mathcal{P}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
,
{\Phi}=\{f\in\mathcal{H}_{k},\|f\|_{\mathcal{H}_{k}}\leq 1\}
satisfies Assumption
2.2
with
A_{1}=K_{1}
,
A_{2}=K_{2}
and
A_{3}=\sqrt{2}\max\{K_{1},K_{2}\}
.

Proof.

By definition,
k(x,\cdot)\in\mathcal{H}_{k}
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Hence, Assumption
2.2
(a) is implied by item (b) above.
For Assumption
2.2
(b),
\forall f\in\mathcal{H}_{k}
such that
\|f\|_{\mathcal{H}_{k}}\leq 1
, we compute

\displaystyle|f(x)-f(y)|

\displaystyle=|\langle f,k(x,\cdot)-k(y,\cdot)\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{k}}|\leq\sqrt{\langle k(x,\cdot)-k(y,\cdot),k(x,\cdot)-k(y,\cdot)\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{k}}}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{k(x,x)+k(y,y)-2k(x,y)}\leq K_{1}\|x-y\|,

(113)

for any
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, which implies that the Lipschitz constant is
K_{1}
.

For Assumption
2.2
(c), we first derive an estimate for
k(x,x)
. To this end, let
n=2
,
x^{1}=x
,
x^{2}=0
in inequality (
107
), we have

a_{1}^{2}k(x,x)+a_{2}^{2}k(0,0)+2a_{1}a_{2}k(x,0)\geq 0,

(114)

for any
a_{1},a_{2}
, implying
|k(x,0)|\leq\sqrt{k(x,x)k(0,0)}
.
Therefore,

(\sqrt{k(x,x)}-\sqrt{k(0,0)})^{2}=k(x,x)+k(0,0)-2\sqrt{k(x,x)k(0,0)}\leq k(x,x)+k(0,0)-2k(x,0)\leq K_{1}^{2}\|x\|^{2},

(115)

and
k(x,x)\leq(\sqrt{k(0,0)}+K_{1}\|x\|)^{2}=(K_{2}+K_{1}\|x\|)^{2}\leq 2(K_{2}^{2}+K_{1}^{2}\|x\|^{2})
.
Now we estimate the Rademacher complexity of
{\Phi}=\{f\in\mathcal{H}_{k},\|f\|_{\mathcal{H}_{k}}\leq 1\}
:

\displaystyle\mathrm{Rad}_{n}({\Phi},\mathcal{X})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{E}\sup_{\|f\|_{\mathcal{H}_{k}}\leq 1}\Bigl|\sum_{i=1}^{n}\xi_{i}f(x^{i})\Bigr|=\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{E}\sup_{\|f\|_{\mathcal{H}_{k}}\leq 1}\Bigl|\sum_{i=1}^{n}\xi_{i}\langle f,k(x^{i},\cdot)\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{k}}\Bigr|

(116)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{E}\sup_{\|f\|_{\mathcal{H}_{k}}\leq 1}|\langle f,\sum_{i=1}^{n}\xi_{i}k(x^{i},\cdot)\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{k}}|\leq\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{E}\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\xi_{i}\xi_{j}k(x^{i},x^{j})}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{n}\sqrt{\mathbb{E}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\xi_{i}\xi_{j}k(x^{i},x^{j})}=\frac{1}{n}\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n}k(x^{i},x^{i})}\leq\frac{1}{n}\sqrt{2\sum_{i=1}^{n}(K_{1}^{2}\|x^{i}\|^{2}+K_{2}^{2})}

(117)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{A_{3}}{n}\sqrt{(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|x^{i}\|^{2}+1)}.

(118)

∎

3.2
The Barron Space

Barron space was firstly introduced in
[
23
,
22
]
, which is designed to analyze the approximation and generalization properties of two-layer neural networks. It can be considered as the continuum analog of two-layer neural networks. See
[
23
, Section 2.1]
for a detailed discussion on Barron space.

Definition 3.2
.

We say
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is a Barron function, if
f
admits the following representation:

f(x)=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d}}\sigma(\omega\cdot x+b)\,\mathrm{d}\rho(\omega,b),

(119)

where
\sigma(x)=\max\{x,0\}
is the ReLU function, and
\rho
is a finite signed measure on
\mathbb{S}^{d}=\{(\omega,b)\in\mathbb{R}^{d+1},\|\omega\|^{2}+|b|^{2}=1\}
with
\|\cdot\|
being the Euclidean norm. We will use the Barron space
\mathcal{B}
to denote the collection of all Barron functions and define a norm
\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal{B}}
on the Barron space as follows:

\|f\|_{\mathcal{B}}=\inf_{\rho}\|\rho\|_{TV},

(120)

where the infimum is taken over all
\rho
for which (
119
) holds for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and
\|\cdot\|_{TV}
is the total variation of
\rho
.

The following theorem reveals some useful properties of Barron space and shows that the unit ball of Barron space, denoted by
\mathcal{B}_{1}:=\{f\in\mathcal{B},\|f\|_{\mathcal{B}}\leq 1\}
, can serve as a good choice of the test function class
{\Phi}
. Although Theorem
3.3
is stated when the activation function
\sigma
is the ReLU function, all claims except (d) hold if
\sigma
is any 1-Lipschitz, nonlinear function and hence
\mathcal{B}_{1}
corresponding to such
\sigma
is also a good choice of the test function class
\Phi
. The proof of claim (e) for general activation functions can be found in
[
50
]
while other claims (a), (b), (c) and (f) follow the same proof presented below.

Theorem 3.3
.

(a)

The Barron space is a Banach space.

(b)

For any
f\in\mathcal{B}
, f is Lipschitz continuous with the Euclidean norm in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\mathrm{Lip}(f)\leq\|f\|_{\mathcal{B}}
.

(c)

Denote by
\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{S}^{d})
all probability measures on
\mathbb{S}^{d}
. For any
\pi\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{S}^{d})
, let
k_{\pi}(x,x^{\prime}):\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
be

k_{\pi}(x,x^{\prime})=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d}}\sigma(\omega\cdot x+b)\sigma(\omega\cdot x^{\prime}+b)\,\mathrm{d}\pi(\omega,b),

(121)

and
\mathcal{H}_{\pi}
be the RKHS associated with
k_{\pi}
. Then,

\mathcal{B}=\mathop{\cup}_{\pi\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{S}^{d})}\mathcal{H}_{\pi},

(122)

and

\|f\|_{\mathcal{B}}=\inf_{\pi\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{S}^{d})}\|f\|_{\mathcal{H}_{\pi}},

(123)

with
\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal{H}_{\pi}}
being the norm in
\mathcal{H}_{\pi}
.

(d)

Let
f
be a measurable function in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and

\gamma(f)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(1+\|\omega\|^{2})|\hat{f}(\omega)|\,\mathrm{d}\omega<+\infty,

(124)

where
\hat{f}(\omega)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f(x)e^{-i\omega\cdot x}\,\mathrm{d}x
is the Fourier transform of f. Then
f\in\mathcal{B}
and

\|f\|_{\mathcal{B}}\leq 4\gamma(f).

(125)

(e)

For any compact set
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the restriction of
\mathcal{B}
on
K
is dense in
C(K)
.

(f)

Let
\mathcal{X}=\{x^{1},\dots,x^{n}\}
, where
x^{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
{\Phi}=\mathcal{B}_{1}
. Then the empirical Rademacher complexity satisfies

\mathrm{Rad}_{n}({\Phi},\mathcal{X})\leq\frac{2}{n}\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\|x^{i}\|^{2}+1)},

(126)

and
{\Phi}=\mathcal{B}_{1}
satisfies Assumption
2.2
with
A_{1}=A_{2}=1
and
A_{3}=2
.

Remark 3.4
.

In
[
3
, Section 9]
, examples of functions with bounded
\gamma(f)
are provided (
e.g.
, Gaussian, positive definite functions, linear functions, radial functions and functions in fractional Sobolev spaces
H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
with
s>\frac{d}{2}+1
). By claim (d), they all belong to the Barron space.

Proof.

(a)

Let
\mathcal{M}
be the set of all signed measures on
\mathbb{S}^{d}
. With
\|\rho\|_{\mathcal{M}}:=\|\rho\|_{TV}
,
\mathcal{M}
is a Banach space. Let
\mathcal{N}=\{\rho\in\mathcal{M}:\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d}}\sigma(\omega\cdot x+b)\,\mathrm{d}\rho(\omega,b)=0,\;\forall x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\}
, then
\mathcal{N}
is a closed subspace of
\mathcal{M}
. It is evident that
\mathcal{B}\cong\mathcal{M}/\mathcal{N}
, thus we obtain the desired result
[
55

(b)

For any
(\omega,b)\in\mathbb{S}^{d}
,
\mathrm{Lip}(\sigma(\omega\cdot x+b))\leq 1
. Then our result follows from the subadditivity of
\mathrm{Lip}
.

(c)

See
[
23
, Theorem 3]
.

(d)

Our proof is similar to that in
[
39
, Theorem 6]
. By the property of Fourier transform, one has

f(x)=x\cdot\nabla f(0)+f(0)+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(e^{i\omega\cdot x}-i\omega\cdot x-1)\hat{f}(\omega)\,\mathrm{d}\omega.

(127)

For
|z|\leq c
, the following identity holds

-\int_{0}^{c}[\sigma(z-u)e^{iu}+\sigma(-z-u)e^{-iu}]\,\mathrm{d}u=e^{iz}-iz-1.

(128)

The above two equations can be found in
[
39

.
Now let
c=\|\omega\|
,
z=\omega\cdot x
,
\alpha(\omega)=\omega/\|\omega\|
and
u=\|\omega\|t
, we have:

-\|\omega\|^{2}\int_{0}^{1}\sqrt{1+t^{2}}\left[\sigma\left(\frac{\alpha(\omega)\cdot x-t}{\sqrt{1+t^{2}}}\right)e^{i\|\omega\|t}+\sigma\left(\frac{-\alpha(\omega)\cdot x-t}{\sqrt{1+t^{2}}}\right)e^{-i\|\omega\|t}\right]\,\mathrm{d}t=e^{i\omega\cdot x}-i\omega\cdot x-1.

(129)

In other words,

e^{i\omega\cdot x}-i\omega\cdot x-1=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d}}\sigma(\omega^{\prime}\cdot x+b)\,\mathrm{d}\rho_{\omega}(\omega^{\prime},b),

(130)

where

\rho_{\omega}=-\|\omega\|^{2}\int_{0}^{1}\sqrt{1+t^{2}}[e^{i\|\omega\|t}\delta_{(\frac{\alpha(\omega)}{\sqrt{1+t^{2}}},-\frac{t}{\sqrt{1+t^{2}}})}+e^{-i\|\omega\|t}\delta_{(-\frac{\alpha(\omega)}{\sqrt{1+t^{2}}},-\frac{t}{\sqrt{1+t^{2}}})}]\,\mathrm{d}t.

(131)

Hence,
e^{i\omega\cdot x}-i\omega\cdot x-1
is in the Barron space
\mathcal{B}
with
\|\rho_{\omega}\|_{TV}\leq 2\sqrt{2}\|\omega\|^{2}
. Recall that equation (

\displaystyle f(x)

\displaystyle=\|\nabla f(0)\|\left[\sigma\left(\frac{\nabla f(0)\cdot x}{\|\nabla f(0)\|}\right)+\sigma\left(-\frac{\nabla f(0)\cdot x}{\|\nabla f(0)\|}\right)\right]+f(0)\sigma(0\cdot x+1)

\displaystyle\qquad+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d}}\sigma(\omega^{\prime}\cdot x+b)\,\mathrm{d}\rho_{\omega}(\omega^{\prime},b)\hat{f}(\omega)\,\mathrm{d}\omega,

(132)

and one concludes

\displaystyle\|f\|_{\mathcal{B}}\leq 2\|\nabla f(0)\|+|f(0)|+2\sqrt{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\|\omega\|^{2}|\hat{f}(\omega)|\,\mathrm{d}\omega

(133)

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}[1+2\|\omega\|+2\sqrt{2}\|\omega\|^{2}]|\hat{f}(\omega)|\,\mathrm{d}\omega\leq 4\gamma(f).

(134)

(e)

For any
f\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, we know that
f
is a Schwartz function. Therefore,
\hat{f}
is also a Schwartz function and we have
\gamma(f)<+\infty

f\in\mathcal{B}
; see e.g.
[
58
, Section 6]
. Because the restriction of
C_{0}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
on
K
is dense in

, we easily conclude.

(f)

Our proof here is similar to the one in
[
23
, Theorem 6]
.

\displaystyle\mathrm{Rad}_{n}({\Phi},\mathcal{X})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{E}\sup_{f\in{\Phi}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\xi_{i}f(x^{i})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{E}\sup_{\|\rho\|_{TV}\leq 1}\Bigl|\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\xi_{i}\sigma(\omega\cdot x^{i}+b)\,\mathrm{d}\rho(\omega,b)\Bigr|

Remark 3.5
.

As the Barron space serves as the continuum analog of two-layer neural networks, the generalized Barron space or the Banach space associated with multi-layer networks introduced in
[
25
]
serves as the continuum analog of multi-layer neural networks. By the fact that the unit ball of the Barron space
\mathcal{B}_{1}
is a Polish space, one can define a signed Radon measure
\rho
on the Borel
\sigma

\mathcal{B}_{1}

\displaystyle f_{\rho}(x)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{B}_{1}}\sigma(g(x))\,\mathrm{d}\rho(g),

\displaystyle\|f\|_{\mathcal{B}^{2}}

\displaystyle=\inf\{\|\rho\|_{TV}:f=f_{\rho}\text{ on }\mathbb{R}^{d}\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}^{2}

\displaystyle=\{f\in C(\mathbb{R}^{d}),\|f\|_{\mathcal{B}^{2}}<+\infty\},

(137)

where the integral is in the sense of Bochner intergrals.
\mathcal{B}^{2}
can be interpreted as the Banach space associated with three-layer neural networks. We can then repeat this process and define
\mathcal{B}^{L}
for any

, which is associated with
(L+1)
-layer neural networks. Naturally we denote by
\mathcal{B}^{1}
the Barron space
\mathcal{B}
defined via (

).
It can be proven that the unit ball of the generalized Barron space is also a suitable test function class. For more technical issues and intuition about
\mathcal{B}^{L}

[
25

5
Application to Mean-Field Games

In this section, we shall show that, for a homogeneous
n
-player game, the strategy derived by its mean-field counterpart produces an
\varepsilon
-Nash equilibrium, where
\varepsilon
is free of the dimension of the state processes.

Following the setup in
[
11
]
, we consider a homogeneous
n
-player stochastic differential game

\,\mathrm{d}X_{t}^{i}=b(t,X_{t}^{i},\bar{\nu}_{t}^{n},\alpha_{t}^{i})\,\mathrm{d}t+\sigma(t,X_{t}^{i},\bar{\nu}_{t}^{n},\alpha^{i})\,\mathrm{d}W_{t}^{i},\quad 0\leq t\leq T,\quad i\in\mathcal{I}\equiv\{1,\ldots,n\},

(212)

where each player
i
controls her private state
X_{t}^{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
through an
\mathbb{R}^{k}\supseteq A
-valued action
\alpha_{t}^{i}
,
W_{t}^{i}=(W_{t}^{i})_{0\leq t\leq T}
are
m
-dimensional independent Brownian motions,
b
and
\sigma
are deterministic measurable functions,
(b,\sigma)
:
[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\times A\to(\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathbb{R}^{d\times m})
, and
\bar{\nu}_{t}^{n}
is the empirical measure of
(X_{t}^{1},\ldots,X_{t}^{n})
defined by

\bar{\nu}_{t}^{n}(\mathrm{d}x):=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{X_{t}^{i}}(\mathrm{d}x).

(213)

Each player aims to minimize the expected cost over the period
[0,T]
by taking her action
\alpha^{i}\in\mathbb{A}
:

J^{i}(\bm{\alpha}):=\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{T}f(t,X_{t}^{i},\bar{\nu}_{t}^{n},\alpha^{i}_{t})\,\mathrm{d}t+g(X_{T}^{i},\bar{\nu}_{T}^{n})\right],

(214)

where
\mathbb{A}
denotes the set of all admissible strategies:

\mathbb{A}=\left\{A\text{-valued progressively measurable processes }(\alpha_{t})_{0\leq t\leq T}:\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{T}\left|\alpha_{t}\right|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}t\right]<\infty\right\},

(215)

and
f
and
g
are deterministic measurable functions,
f:[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\times A\to\mathbb{R}
,
g:\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\to\mathbb{R}
. Since the players interact through their empirical measure
\bar{\nu}_{t}^{n}
, which depends on all players’ stragety
\bm{\alpha}=(\alpha^{1},\ldots,\alpha^{n})\in\mathbb{A}^{n}
, so does the cost functional for player
i
,
J^{i}(\bm{\alpha})
. Here
\mathbb{A}^{n}
is the product space of
n

\mathbb{A}
.

To solve such games, we are interested in the concept of Nash equilibrium. That is a tuple
\bm{\alpha}^{\ast}=(\alpha^{1,\ast},\ldots,\alpha^{n,\ast})\in\mathbb{A}^{n}
such that

\forall i\in\mathcal{I},\text{ and }\alpha^{i}\in\mathbb{A},\quad J^{i}(\bm{\alpha}^{\ast})\leq J^{i}(\alpha^{1,\ast},\ldots,\alpha^{i-1,\ast},\alpha^{i},\alpha^{i+1,\ast},\ldots,\alpha^{n,\ast}).

(216)

For homogeneous games with large
n
, if the system lacks tractability and needs to rely on numerical methods for Nash equilibrium, the conventional algorithms soon lose their efficiency, and one may resort to recently developed machine learning tools
[
34
,
30
,
31
]
. On the other hand, one could utilize its limiting mean-field strategy to approximate the Nash equilibrium. More precisely, one can first obtain the optimal control
\alpha
from the mean field games using the following steps:

(i)

Fixed a deterministic measure
\mu_{t}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
,
\forall t\in[0,T]
;

(ii)

Solve the standard stochastic control problem:

\displaystyle\inf_{\alpha\in\mathbb{A}}\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{T}f(t,X_{t},\mu_{t},\alpha_{t})\,\mathrm{d}t+g(X_{T},\mu_{T})\right]

(217)

\displaystyle\text{subject to: }\,\mathrm{d}X_{t}=b(t,X_{t},\mu_{t},\alpha_{t})\,\mathrm{d}t+\sigma(t,X_{t},\mu_{t},\alpha_{t})\,\mathrm{d}W_{t},\quad X_{0}=x_{0};

(218)

(iii)

Determine the flow of measures
\mu_{t}
such that
\forall t\in[0,T]
,
\mathcal{L}({X_{t}^{\ast,\mu_{t}}})=\mu_{t}
, where
X_{t}^{\ast,\mu_{t}}
denotes the state process associated to the optimal control given
\mu_{t}
in step (

Then one can construct an
\varepsilon
-Nash equilibrium from it if the optimal control
\alpha

6
Conclusion

A new class of metrics, in the form of integral probability metrics, is proposed in this paper to study the convergence of empirical measures in high-dimensional spaces. We generalize the standard definition of maximum mean discrepancy by imposing specific criteria for selecting the test function space to guarantee the property of being free of the CoD. Examples of test function spaces include reproducing kernel Hilbert spaces, Barron function space, and flow-induced function spaces. Under the proposed metrics, we can show the following three cases of convergence are dimension-free: 1. The convergence of empirical measure drawn from a given distribution; 2. The convergence of
n
-particle system to the solution to McKean-Vlasov stochastic equation; 3. The construction of an
\varepsilon
-Nash equilibrium for a homogeneous
n
-player game by its mean-field limit. We also generalize the results in
[
63
,
64
]
and show that, given a distribution close to the target distribution measured by the newly proposed metric and a certain representation of the target distribution, we can generate a distribution close to the target one in terms of the Wasserstein metric and relative entropy.

As future work, we shall deepen the study of our metric by investigating the mean-field limit of the
n
-player stochastic differential games in high dimensions, whose Nash equilibria can be given by the deep fictitious theory and algorithms
[
34
,
30
,
31
,
62
]
.
Besides, we are interested in developing a similar theory (
cf.
Theorem
2.8
) for models other than the bias potential type and density type, for instance, the generative adversarial network models, which are useful and important in the machine learning community. We also plan to apply Theorem
2.8
to the solution of the McKean-Vlasov SDE (
4
) to study whether one can construct a distribution based on the solution of the
n
-particle system (
4
), which is close to the distribution of the solution of McKean-Vlasov SDE (
4
) in the sense of the Wasserstein metric, total variation distance, or relative entropy. The technical difficulty therein is to analyze when the distribution of (
4
) satisfies a bias potential model or density model. Finally, it is of interest to apply this class of metrics to analyze the convergence rates in other problems, for example, McKean-Vlasov models, MFGs, and stochastic gradient descent for two-layer neural networks or multi-layer neural networks
[
15
,
48
,

]
.

Then by the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, one has

\displaystyle\left(\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\sup_{s,t\in[0,T],|s-t|\leq h}\|Y_{t}-Y_{s}\|^{2}\Bigr]\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k=1}^{+\infty}\left(\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\sup_{s,t\in[0,T],|s-t|\leq h}\|Y_{t}-Y_{s}\|^{2}\mathrm{1}_{k-1\leq S_{X}\leq k}\Bigr]\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}

(270)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k=1}^{+\infty}\left(\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\sup_{s,t\in[0,T],|s-t|\leq h}\|Y_{t}-Y_{s}\|^{4}\mathrm{1}_{S_{X}\leq k}\Bigr]\mathbb{P}\Bigl(S_{X}\geq k-1\Bigr)\right)^{\frac{1}{4}}

(271)

\displaystyle\leq C\sqrt{h\log\left(\frac{2T}{h}\right)}\sum_{k=1}^{+\infty}(k+1)\mathbb{P}^{\frac{1}{4}}(S_{X}\geq k-1).

(272)

Using claim (b) and Theorem 5.1 in
[
17
]
, we know that

\mathbb{P}(\sup_{0\leq u\leq T}\|X_{u}\|\geq n)\leq 2\exp\left(-\frac{n^{2}}{C(1+\|x_{0}\|^{2})}\right).

(273)

Therefore,

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{+\infty}(k+1)\mathbb{P}^{\frac{1}{4}}(S_{X}\geq k-1)

\displaystyle\leq C+C\sum_{k=1}^{+\infty}(k+2)\exp\left(-\frac{k^{2}}{C(1+\|x_{0}\|^{2})}\right)

(274)

\displaystyle\leq C\Bigl[1+\int_{0}^{+\infty}(a+2)\exp\left(-\frac{a^{2}}{C(1+\|x_{0}\|^{2})}\right)\,\mathrm{d}a\Bigr]\leq C(1+\|x_{0}\|),

(275)

which means

\left(\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\sup_{s,t\in[0,T],|s-t|\leq h}\|Y_{t}-Y_{s}\|^{2}\Bigr]\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\leq C(1+\|x_{0}\|)\sqrt{h\log\left(\frac{2T}{h}\right)}.

(276)

For general
\eta
, we use the above inequality to deduce

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\sup_{s,t\in[0,T],|s-t|\leq h}\|Y_{t}-Y_{s}\|^{2}\Bigr]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\left(\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\sup_{s,t\in[0,T],|s-t|\leq h}\|Y_{t}-Y_{s}\|^{2}\;\Bigl|\;X_{0}=x_{0}\Bigr]\right)

(277)

\displaystyle\leq C(1+\mathbb{E}\|\eta\|^{2})h\log\left(\frac{2T}{h}\right)\leq Ch\log\left(\frac{2T}{h}\right).

(278)

With inequality (
255
), we obtain the desired result.

Appendix B
Discussion on Theorem
2.11

We show in this section that, under a slightly stronger condition (
280
) compared to (
68
), the logarithm term in
\phi(n)
defined in Theorem
2.11
can be removed, for all the examples of the test function classes discussed in Section
3
.

Throughout this appendix, we assume
\mu
to be a distribution on
C([0,T];\mathbb{R}^{d})
such that

\int_{C([0,T];\mathbb{R}^{d})}\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}\|x_{t}\|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\mu(x)<+\infty,

(279)

and

\int_{C([0,T];\mathbb{R}^{d})}[\Delta(x,h)]^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\mu(x)\leq Qh^{\alpha}\log^{\beta}\left(\frac{2T}{h}\right),

(280)

for any
h>0
, where
Q,\alpha,\beta>0
are positive constants. Still, we will use
C
to denote a positive constant depending only on
\alpha
and
\beta
, which may vary from line to line.

The first result is established for the reproducing kernel Hilbert spaces (RKHSs).

Proposition B.1
.

Assume the kernel k satisfies the condition (a) in Theorem
3.1
and
\Phi
is the unit ball of
\mathcal{H}_{k}
, then

\mathbb{E}\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\mathrm{D}_{\Phi}(\mu_{t},\bar{\mu}_{t}^{n})\leq 2\sqrt{\frac{2}{n}\Big[K_{1}^{2}\int_{C([0,T];\mathbb{R}^{d})}\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}\|x_{t}\|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\mu(x)+K_{2}^{2}\Big]}+CK_{1}\sqrt{\frac{QT^{\alpha}}{n}}.

(281)

Proof.

Let
X^{1},\dots,X^{n}
be i.i.d. processes drawn from the distribution
\mu
. Following
[
56
, Lemma 26.2]
, we immediately have

\mathbb{E}\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\mathrm{D}_{\Phi}(\mu_{t},\bar{\mu}_{t}^{n})\leq\frac{2}{n}\mathbb{E}\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\sup_{\|f\|_{\mathcal{H}_{k}}\leq 1}\Big|\sum_{i=1}^{n}\xi_{i}f(X_{t}^{i})\Big|.

(282)

For a fixed integer
n^{\prime}\geq 2
and any
t_{p},s_{p}\in[0,T]
,
p=1,\dots,n^{\prime}
, we first compute

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{E}\max_{1\leq p\leq n^{\prime}}\sup_{\|f\|_{\mathcal{H}_{k}}\leq 1}\Big|\sum_{i=1}^{n}\xi_{i}[f(X^{i}_{t_{p}})-f(X^{i}_{s_{p}})]\Big|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{E}\max_{1\leq p\leq n^{\prime}}\sup_{\|f\|_{\mathcal{H}_{k}}\leq 1}\Big|\langle f,\sum_{i=1}^{n}\xi_{i}(k(X_{t_{p}}^{i},\cdot)-k(X_{s_{p}}^{i},\cdot)\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{k}}\Big|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{E}\max_{1\leq p\leq n^{\prime}}\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\xi_{i}\xi_{j}[k(X_{t_{p}}^{i},X_{t_{p}}^{j})+k(X_{s_{p}}^{i},X_{s_{p}}^{j})-k(X_{t_{p}}^{i},X_{s_{p}}^{j})-k(X_{s_{p}}^{i},X_{t_{p}}^{j})]}.

(283)

Let
S
be a nonnegative-definite matrix, then there exists a nonnegative-definite
n\times n
matrix
F
such that
S=F^{\operatorname{T}}F
. Let
\xi=(\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{n})^{\operatorname{T}}
, then there exists a universal constant
C>0
(
cf.

[
54
, Theorem 2.1]
and
[
60
, Example 2.5.8]
), such that for any
a\geq 0
,

\mathbb{P}(\sqrt{\xi^{\operatorname{T}}S\xi}-\sqrt{\mathrm{Trace}(S)}\geq a)=\mathbb{P}(\|F\xi\|-\|F\|_{\mathrm{HS}}\geq a)\leq 2\exp(-\frac{a^{2}}{C\mathrm{Trace}(S)}),

(284)

where
\|F\|_{\mathrm{HS}}=\sqrt{\mathrm{Trace}(F^{\operatorname{T}}F)}
.
Therefore, for any positive definite matrices
S_{1},\dots,S_{n^{\prime}}
,
[
60
, Exercise 2.5.10]
gives

\mathbb{E}\sup_{1\leq p\leq n^{\prime}}\sqrt{\xi^{\operatorname{T}}S_{p}\xi}\leq C\max_{1\leq p\leq n^{\prime}}\sqrt{\mathrm{Trace}(S_{p})\log n^{\prime}}.

(285)

Since for any
a_{1},a_{2},\dots,a_{n}\in\mathbb{R}
,

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}a_{i}a_{j}[k(X_{t_{p}}^{i},X_{t_{p}}^{j})+k(X_{s_{p}}^{i},X_{s_{p}}^{j})-k(X_{t_{p}}^{i},X_{s_{p}}^{j})-k(X_{s_{p}}^{i},X_{t_{p}}^{j})]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}[k(X_{t_{p}}^{i},\cdot)-k(X_{s_{p}}^{i},\cdot)],\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}[k(X_{t_{p}}^{i},\cdot)-k(X_{s_{p}}^{i},\cdot)]\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{k}}\geq 0,

(286)

using (
283
) and (
284
) with
(K_{p})_{i,j}=k(X_{t_{p}}^{i},X_{t_{p}}^{j})+k(X_{s_{p}}^{i},X_{s_{p}}^{j})-k(X_{t_{p}}^{i},X_{s_{p}}^{j})-k(X_{s_{p}}^{i},X_{t_{p}}^{j})
, we deduce

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{E}\max_{1\leq p\leq n^{\prime}}\sup_{\|f\|_{\mathcal{H}_{k}}\leq 1}\Big|\sum_{i=1}^{n}\xi_{i}[f(X^{i}_{t_{p}})-f(X^{i}_{s_{p}})]\Big|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C}{n}\sqrt{\log n^{\prime}}\mathbb{E}\max_{1\leq p\leq n^{\prime}}\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n}k(X_{t_{p}}^{i},X_{t_{p}}^{i})+k(X_{s_{p}}^{i},X_{s_{p}}^{i})-2k(X_{t_{p}}^{i},X_{s_{p}}^{i})}

\displaystyle\leq C\sqrt{\frac{\log n^{\prime}}{n}\mathbb{E}\max_{1\leq p\leq n^{\prime}}[k(X_{t_{p}}^{1},X_{t_{p}}^{1})+k(X_{s_{p}}^{1},X_{s_{p}}^{1})-2k(X_{t_{p}}^{1},X_{s_{p}}^{1})]}

\displaystyle\leq CK_{1}\sqrt{\frac{\log n^{\prime}}{n}\mathbb{E}\max_{1\leq p\leq n^{\prime}}\|X_{t_{p}}^{1}-X_{s_{p}}^{1}\|^{2}},

(287)

where
K_{1}
is the constant defined in Theorem
3.1
.

We now use the chaining method to estimate the Rademacher complexity. For any
t\in[0,T]
and integer
k
, we define

\Pi_{k}(t)=T2^{-k}\lfloor\frac{2^{k}t}{T}\rfloor.

(288)

We first compute

\displaystyle\Bigg|\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{E}\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\sup_{\|f\|_{\mathcal{H}_{k}}\leq 1}\Big|\sum_{i=1}^{n}\xi_{i}f(X_{t}^{i})\Big|-\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{E}\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\sup_{\|f\|_{\mathcal{H}_{k}}\leq 1}\Big|\sum_{i=1}^{n}\xi_{i}f(X_{\Pi(t)}^{i})\Big|\Bigg|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{E}\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\sup_{\|f\|_{\mathcal{H}_{k}}\leq 1}\Big|\sum_{i=1}^{n}\xi_{i}[f(X_{t}^{i})-f(X_{\Pi(t)}^{i})]\Big|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle K_{1}\mathbb{E}\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\|X_{t}^{1}-X_{\Pi(t)}^{1}\|\leq CK_{1}Q\left(\frac{T}{2^{k}}\right)^{\alpha/2}(k+1)^{\beta/2}\rightarrow 0,

(289)

as
k\rightarrow+\infty
. We have also derived in Theorem
3.1
that

\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{E}\sup_{\|f\|_{\mathcal{H}_{k}}\leq 1}|\sum_{i=1}^{n}\xi_{i}f(X_{0}^{i})|\leq\sqrt{\frac{2}{n}\Big[K_{1}^{2}\int_{C([0,T];\mathbb{R}^{d})}\|x_{0}\|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\mu(x)+K_{2}^{2}\Big]}.

(290)

Together with (
287
), we finally achieve

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{E}\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\sup_{\|f\|_{\mathcal{H}_{k}}\leq 1}\Big|\sum_{i=1}^{n}\xi_{i}f(X_{t}^{i})\Big|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{E}\sup_{\|f\|_{\mathcal{H}_{k}}\leq 1}\Big|\sum_{i=1}^{n}\xi_{i}f(X_{0}^{i})\Big|+\sum_{k=0}^{+\infty}\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{E}\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\sup_{\|f\|_{\mathcal{H}_{k}\leq 1}}\Big|\sum_{i=1}^{n}\xi_{i}[f(X_{\Pi_{k+1}(t)}^{i})-f(X_{\Pi_{k}(t)}^{i})]\Big|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sqrt{\frac{2}{n}\Big[K_{1}^{2}\int_{C([0,T];\mathbb{R}^{d})}\|x_{0}\|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\mu(x)+K_{2}^{2}\Big]}+CK_{1}\sum_{k=0}^{+\infty}\sqrt{\frac{\log(2^{k+1})}{n}\mathbb{E}\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\|X_{\Pi_{k+1}(t)}^{1}-X_{\Pi_{k}(t)}^{1}\|^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sqrt{\frac{2}{n}\Big[K_{1}^{2}\int_{C([0,T];\mathbb{R}^{d})}\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}\|x_{t}\|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\mu(x)+K_{2}^{2}\Big]}+CK_{1}\sum_{k=0}^{+\infty}\sqrt{\frac{k+1}{n}Q\left(\frac{T}{2^{k}}\right)^{\alpha}(k+1)^{\beta}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sqrt{\frac{2}{n}\Big[K_{1}^{2}\int_{C([0,T];\mathbb{R}^{d})}\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}\|x_{t}\|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\mu(x)+K_{2}^{2}\Big]}+CK_{1}\sqrt{\frac{QT^{\alpha}}{n}},

(291)

where we have used
\sum_{k=0}^{+\infty}\sqrt{\frac{(k+1)^{\beta+1}}{2^{\alpha k}}}<+\infty
.
∎
