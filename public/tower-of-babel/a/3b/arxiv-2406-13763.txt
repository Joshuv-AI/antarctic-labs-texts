Through the Theory of Mind’s Eye: Reading Minds with Multimodal Video Large Language Models
Zhawnen Chen, Tianchun Wang, Yizhou Wang, Michal Kosinski, Xiang Zhang, Yun Fu, Sheng Li

Abstract
Can large multimodal models have a human-like ability for emotional and social reasoning, and if so, how does it work? Recent research has discovered emergent theory-of-mind (ToM) reasoning capabilities in large language models (LLMs). LLMs can reason about people’s mental states by solving various text-based ToM tasks that ask questions about the actors’ ToM (e.g., human belief, desire, intention). However, human reasoning in the wild is often grounded in dynamic scenes across time. Thus, we consider videos a new medium for examining spatio-temporal ToM reasoning ability. Specifically, we ask explicit probing questions about videos with abundant social and emotional reasoning content. We develop a pipeline for multimodal LLM for ToM reasoning using video and text. We also enable explicit ToM reasoning by retrieving key frames for answering a ToM question, which reveals how multimodal LLMs reason about ToM.

2.1
Theory of Mind Reasoning

Human Theory-of-Mind Reasoning on Text

Traditional Theory of Mind (ToM) Reasoning tasks and assessments revolve around text. In addition to text, we engage in ToM reasoning when we watch movies or television. In fact, we sometimes do so explicitly when we discuss films or TV shows with one another, such as discussing character intentions or the morality of certain actions in dynamic scenes. Indeed, video-based ToM reasoning can serve as an important developmental tool.
Valkenburg et al. 1999
found that parents often use videos as a form of
instructive mediation
(also known as
active mediation

The children’s answers are accompanied by instructional feedback from the parents. Video-based instructive mediation is especially useful, considering that children can spend between 2.5 and 3 hours watching videos a day
(
Rideout & Robb 2019
)
. In the context of developmental psychology, the intervention for autism spectrum disorder (ASD) incorporates many ToM tasks in formal assessments

)

(
Jacoby et al. 2016

(

(

, which uses movie discussions to assist children with autism in grasping social contexts and understanding others’ points of view.

2.2
Computational ToM Reasoning

Computational Theory-of-Mind Reasoning on Text
Efforts on computational theory of mind encompass a diverse range of datasets aimed at enhancing AI’s understanding and prediction of human mental states and social interactions. The Theory of Mind Task dataset provides brief text stories paired with ToM-focused questions, facilitating the evaluation of AI’s ability to reason about mental states
(
Nematzadeh et al. 2018

(

Narrative descriptions of prevalent social scripts and cultural norms further contribute to this domain by providing context-specific social knowledge

Li et al. 2012

(
Sap et al. 2019

are specifically designed to help individuals with autism improve their interpersonal skills via better understanding and navigating social situations. The Story Commonsense and GLUCOSE datasets contain stories annotated with commonsense information, enabling models to grasp everyday logic and causality in narratives
(
Chen et al. 2019

Mostafazadeh et al. 2020

. Finally, the Neural Theory-of-Mind (TOMI) dataset focuses on assessing the ability of large language models to reason about mental states and social interactions, highlighting current limitations and future directions for research
(
Sap et al. 2022
)

These combined efforts are crucial for advancing the field of AI, making machines better at understanding and predicting human behavior and interactions.

Theory-of-Mind Reasoning with Large Language Models

Kosinski 2024
examined the performance of large language models (LLMs) on text-based false-belief tasks and unexpected transfer tasks and found that the latest model, ChatGPT-4, performs at a level comparable to that of seven-year-old children on these tasks. The study suggests that LLMs’ performance on these tasks is driven by a single factor, possibly an ability to detect false belief. The high correlation between LLMs’ performance on both types of tasks suggests that a single factor, such as the ability to detect false belief, is driving their performance rather than separate task-specific abilities.

Computational Theory-of-Mind Reasoning on Videos
Current research in theory-of-mind reasoning typically involves training a supervised machine learning model. The training data comprises (1) a video segment with related features or annotations, (2) a question in natural language, sometimes accompanied by multiple-choice answers, and (3) the accurate answer (s). The Social-IQ dataset, for example, features video snippets from real-life YouTube interactions like seminars and interviews, and poses multiple choice questions centered on social perceptions, intentions, mental states, and attitudes

Methods

In this section, we introduce the ToM Localization (ToMLoc) task and a pipeline to pre-train and evaluate baseline video LLMs models on ToMLoc.

3.1
The ToM Localization (ToMLoc) Task

Given a video
X_{V}
and a question
X_{Q}
, the ToM Localization (ToMLoc) task retrieves the most relevant input frame
X_{I}
to the user text query. Although both ToMLoc and the traditional video-text retrieval task learn the function
R_{\phi}(X_{V},X_{Q})\rightarrow X_{I}
, ToMLoc requires a global understanding of neurotypical ToM modeling and the ability to model the mental states of each character in a video over time. This additional challenge posed by temporal ToM reasoning stems from the base task, ToM-based video question answering (QA). As illustrated in Example
1
, ToM-rich questions need higher-order mental state modeling for each character for the duration of the video. The MASC video ToM assessment paused the ToM-rich video at 46 key moments as a means of ablation
Dziobek et al. 2006
. ToMLoc can be seen as a temporally ablative understanding of the ToM content in video.

3.2
Example Illustrating the ToM Video Frame Retrieval

We show an example of ToM video frame retrieval to draw a distinction from that of traditional video frame retrieval.

Example 1

Consider the scenario from the TinySocial dataset where Harry Potter is at the Weasley’s home. In this scene, the father begins to scold the boys, and the task is to predict the boys’ reaction. The question posed is: "How did the boys react when the father began to scold them?" The possible answers are: (A) The boys looked understandingly at each other and looked down in pretended embarrassment, (B) They got defensive, (C) They started crying and begging for forgiveness, and (D) They jumped up from the table and ran outside. The correct answer is (A).

To correctly answer the ToM-probing question in Example
1
, a video LLM first needs to understand the first-order actions and attributes such the boys’ looking at each other and looking down. It is also necessary to recognize facial expressions such as laughing and body language such as looking down indicating embarrassment. Furthermore, a seemingly trivial question belies a challenging two-hop ToM reasoning task. To arrive at "understandingly" and "pretended embarrassment," a model would need to understand the mental states of both Harry Potter and Ron Weaseley. In turn, the two boys had themselves understood the mental states of Arthur Weaseley, who is pretending to scold Ron for driving the flying car without permission. Without the key frame of the boys witnessing Molly hitting Arthur on the arm and causing him to change his question and demeanor, it would be impossible for humans and AI alike to answer this question.

This example illustrates the necessity of the ToM localization task to being able to answer ToM-rich questions about a video. In contrast, traditional video retrieval is primarily focused on the alignment between video and text modalities is not concerned with modeling human mental states.

To determine the most influential frame for answering this question, we apply our proposed frame relevance scoring method. Let
F=\{f_{1},f_{2},\ldots,f_{n}\}
be the sequence of frames in the video, and
Q=\{q_{1}\}
be the set of ToM questions pertaining to the scene. The relevance score function
R(f_{i},q_{1})
is defined to measure the alignment between the visual features in frame
f_{i}
and the mental state question
q_{1}
.

Definition 1
(Frame Relevance Score)

We define the relevance score function
R(f_{i},q_{1})
as:

R(f_{i},q_{1})=\text{SIM}(\phi(f_{i}),\psi(q_{1}))

where
\phi(f_{i})
represents the feature vector of frame
f_{i}
, and
\psi(q_{1})
represents the feature vector of the question
q_{1}
. The similarity measure,
\text{SIM}(\cdot,\cdot)
, can be computed using cosine similarity.

The subset of frames
F^{*}\subseteq F
that maximizes the relevance score for answering
q_{1}
is given by:

F^{*}=\arg\max_{F^{\prime}\subseteq F}\sum_{q_{1}\in Q}\max_{f_{i}\in F^{\prime}}R(f_{i},q_{1})

By optimizing this function, we identify the frames
F^{*}
that are crucial for understanding the mental states of the characters in the scene. This allows us to determine the boys’ reaction based on the visual and contextual cues present in the most relevant frames. The optimization process helps in pinpointing these critical moments, providing a robust basis for answering the ToM question accurately. The methodology demonstrates the effectiveness of our frame selection approach in capturing essential social and emotional dynamics in video-based ToM tasks.

3.3
ToMLoc Pre-training

In order to evaluate the spatio-temporal theory-of-mind understanding of video MLLM models, we need to first pre-train a linear projector before a foundational model to map a textual query to a specific segment within a video. Given a video and a textual query, the objective is to retrieve the frame most relevant to the query. The Theory of Mind localization task is designed to identify specific segments within a video based on a textual query. Essentially, given a video and a textual query, the aim is to determine the start and end frames relevant to the question. For this task to be effective, each query should ideally be associated with specific frame annotations, marking the beginning and end of the relevant segment.

3.4
Approximating Most Influential Frames with Median Frame

However, a significant challenge arises with the Social-IQ dataset, which lacks these vital timestamps. Owing to the absence of temporal annotations in the Social-IQ dataset and budget constraints precluding the acquisition of human labels, we propose an approximation task for most influential frame retrieval using median frames. We combine multiple videos into a single composite video, the entire duration of each individual video can serve as the ground-truth timestamp. For example, a new video
V_{123}

V_{1}
(e.g.,“Mrs. Doubtfire”),
V_{2}

V_{3}
(“Harry Potter”). The task then evolves into localizing a query to a specific video within this composite video. For example, a query regarding why a man says “I used to” would be localized to the segment from “Mrs. Doubtfire”.

Empirical Studies

4.1
Experimental Setup

We evaluated the Theory of Mind (ToM) reasoning capabilities of video-based large language models (LLMs) through a series of experiments. The experiments were designed to assess the models’ performance on frame localization and question answering (QA) tasks using the Social-IQ 2.0 dataset. We compared baseline models with our proposed Video Theory of Mind (VToM) pipeline.

Dataset for QA:

We used the Social-IQ 2.0 dataset for fine-tuning Video-ChatGPT. The dataset consists of 1,403 video clips, with a total of 8,076 questions and 32,304 answers (4 answers per question, 3 incorrect, 1 correct). Prior to fine-tuning, we performed preprocessing steps including normalization, resizing, and frame extraction at a uniform sampling rate of 100 frames per video.

5
Discussions

The experimental results provide insights into the Theory of Mind (ToM) capabilities of video-based large language models (LLMs). The significant improvement in frame localization and QA accuracy after fine-tuning on ToM-specific datasets (ToMLoc) highlights the importance of targeted training data.

5.1
Fine-tuning and Model Performance

The fine-tuning process substantially improved the models’ ability to understand and reason about complex actions and interactions within videos, crucial for ToM reasoning. However, challenges such as handling occlusions and varying lighting conditions remain. Future work should focus on augmenting the dataset with more diverse samples and exploring alternative model architectures to further enhance performance.

5.2
Limitations and Future Work

Our study highlights significant challenges in modeling and assessing computational ToM reasoning. One major limitation is the scarcity of high-quality, diverse video datasets, which constrains the models’ ability to generalize across different contexts and scenarios. Additionally, the current video datasets often lack high-quality human-annotated labels, making accurate training and evaluation challenging. Addressing these limitations is crucial for advancing the field of computational ToM reasoning. Future work should focus on creating and curating richer datasets and exploring alternative model architectures to improve performance and generalizability.

6
Discussions

Fine-tuning

The fine-tuning process significantly improved the model’s performance, particularly in recognizing complex actions and interactions within the videos. However, some challenges remained, such as handling occlusions and varying lighting conditions. Future work will focus on augmenting the dataset with more diverse samples and exploring alternative model architectures to further enhance performance.

Limitations

Our work is an initial study on the hypothesis that computational theory-of-mind reasoning can be effectively modeled and assessed. However, this area poses significant challenges, mirroring the difficulties in assessing theory of mind in humans. One major limitation is the scarcity of high-quality, diverse video datasets essential for training these models. The availability of such datasets is limited, which constrains the model’s ability to generalize across different contexts and scenarios. Furthermore, the current video datasets often lack high-quality human-annotated labels, making it challenging to train and evaluate the models accurately. These limitations highlight the need for more comprehensive and well-annotated datasets to advance the field of computational theory-of-mind reasoning.

7
Conclusion

Our work uncovers emergent theory-of-mind (ToM) reasoning in large multimodal models for video understanding. We introduced the Video Theory of Mind (VToM) architecture to model the evolution of mental states over time. By integrating textual and visual features from state-of-the-art video captioning models, our approach aims to enhance the ToM reasoning capabilities of these models. Through rigorous evaluation on datasets such as Social-IQ 2.0 and TVQA, we demonstrated the potential of our method in capturing complex mental state transitions within dynamic video contexts. However, our study also highlights significant challenges, including the scarcity of high-quality, diverse video datasets and the need for comprehensive human annotations. Addressing these limitations is crucial for further advancements in computational ToM reasoning. Future work should focus on creating and curating richer datasets and exploring alternative model architectures to improve performance and generalizability. In summary, this research provides a foundational step towards developing AI systems capable of human-like ToM reasoning, with implications for improving human-computer interactions and enhancing the social intelligence of AI agents.
