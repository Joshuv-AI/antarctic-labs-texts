1.
Introduction

Spectral properties of non-Hermitian random matrices can have different behaviors depending on their degree of sparsity. These properties are now well understood for dense matrices with iid entries; a well-known example is the Circular Law
[
11
]
, for which the optimal sparsity threshold is known
[
57
,
8
,
54
]
. However, when the matrices in question are very sparse, with a fixed number of non-zero entries on each row, including dependencies, the problem becomes different and more challenging. One of our goals in this paper is to understand these differences.

Sums-of-permutations

There are numerous ways to enforce sparsity in random matrices, and different ensembles are expected to behave differently. In this paper, we focus on
permutation matrices
, that is, matrices with exactly one non-zero entry on each row and each column; adding
d
independent permutation matrices chosen uniformly at random, we obtain a random matrix
A
with integer entries, whose row/column-sums are all exactly equal to
d
. Thus,
A
can be viewed as a typical matrix with fixed row sums and column sums. This model displays two important properties:

(i)

The structure of
A
is very constrained (fixed row/column sums), hence the entries of
A
are not independent.

(ii)

The rows and columns of
A
can be swapped while keeping the distribution of
A
invariant (invariance by permutations).

Random regular directed graphs

Sums of random permutations are of particular interest to random graph theory since they are a popular proxy for the (adjacency) matrix of
random regular digraphs
. A digraph is
regular
when each node has the same number of in-neighbors and out-neighbors; consequently, the matrix
A
defined above is the matrix of a
d
-regular digraph, possibly with multiple edges. It turns out that for fixed
d
, conditioning on
A
having no entry greater than
1
, the graph represented by
A
is nearly uniform among all the
d
-regular directed graphs (the two models are
contiguous

[
36
]
, and both are contiguous with the configuration model). Our analysis thus provides results on the eigenvalues of random regular digraphs, notably a new proof for the directed version of Friedman’s second eigenvalue theorem
[
26
,
10
]
which relates to important graph-theoretical notions such as graph expansion and random walks on digraphs
[
51
,
20
]
.

Spectral properties

In general, studying the eigendecomposition of non-Hermitian matrices can be challenging.
For an
n\times n
permutation matrix (
d=1
), the eigenvalues of
A
belong to the
n
-th roots of unity, and their multiplicity is given by the cycle decomposition theorem; if

denotes the number of cycles of length

(thus
c_{1}+2c_{2}+\dotsb+nc_{n}=n
), then the multiplicity of a root of unity

will be the sum of the

for which
\omega^{k}=1
.
This basic structure allows to study even non-uniform permutation matrices
[
34
,
9
]
.
However, already for
d=2
, this straightforward analysis breaks down because generically, the permutation matrices that we sum do not commute. In particular, a famous conjecture states that for fixed
d
as
n\to\infty
, the empirical spectral measure of

converges to the
oriented Kesten-McKay

[

]
.
In contrast, in the regime where

, one expects to recover the circular law and there are already several results in this direction
[

,
15
,
44
]
.

Our contributions

In this paper, we study asymptotics of the characteristic polynomial outside of the spectral support.
The results we obtain are analogous to

This problem has also been considered in the Hermitian case for Gaussian

In particular, the relationships between our results and the theory of multiplicative chaos are discussed in Section

Surprisingly, we obtain the same limiting functions as in

d\geq 2
.
We establish that

\frac{\mathrm{tr}(A^{k})-d^{k}}{d^{k/2}}\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{\mathrm{law}}L_{k}

in the sense of finite dimensional distributions for a sequence of independent random variables
(L_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
. This is the content of Theorem
3.2
thereafter, which is proved in Section
5
at page
5
.

(iii)

Suppose that
\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}}|L_{k}|r^{k}
converges for any
r<1
almost surely.
From (i) tightness and (ii) we conclude that the random analytic function

\bigg(\frac{\widehat{\chi}_{n}(z)}{z-1/\sqrt{d}}\bigg)\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{\mathrm{law}}\exp\bigg(\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\frac{(-1)^{k+1}}{k}L_{k}z^{k}\bigg)

in the topology of locally uniform convergence on
D_{1}
; cf. Section
7
.

Step (i) is somehow technical and we state it as a result. The outline of the proof and the key elements are in Section
4.1
at page
4.1
. We expect Proposition
3.1
to hold for larger

but this would require a different approach.

Proposition 3.1
.

For any sequence
d(n)\in\mathbb{N}
with
d=o(\sqrt{n})
, the sequence of random analytic function
(\widehat{\chi}_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is a tight sequence for the compact-open topology.

Step (ii) consists in a standard application of the trace method, without resorting to high traces. The main result is the following one. It is the same result as
[
21
]
(for different models) and it is reminiscent of the result for non-backtracking matrices of random regular graphs in
[
23
,
37
]
. The
d=1
case is well known
[
9
,
2
]
.

Theorem 3.2
.

(1)

Suppose that
d\in\mathbb{N}
is fixed and
\{\Lambda_{\ell}\}_{\ell\in\mathbb{N}}
be as in Definition
2.1
.
Then for every
k\in\mathbb{N}
,

(3.2)

\big(\mathrm{tr}(A),\dotsc,\mathrm{tr}(A^{k})\big)\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{\mathrm{law}}\bigg(\Lambda_{1},\dotsc,\sum_{\ell|k}\ell\Lambda_{\ell}\bigg).

(2)

Consider a sequence
d(n)\in\mathbb{N}
such that
d(n)\to\infty
and

d(n)=n^{o(1)}
as

. Then for every
k\in\mathbb{N}
,

(3.3)

\displaystyle\left(\frac{\mathrm{tr}(A)-d}{\sqrt{d}},\dotsc,\frac{\mathrm{tr}(A^{k})-d^{k}}{\sqrt{d^{k}}}\right)\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{\mathrm{law}}\left(N_{1},\sqrt{2}N_{2}+1,\dotsc,\sqrt{k}N_{k}+\mathbf{1}_{\{k\text{ is even}\}}\right).

The proof of Theorem
3.2
relies on the relationship between the random variables
\mathrm{tr}(A^{k})
and
k
-cycles on the (adjacency) digraph associated to the matrix

– note that

is generically non-Hermitan, and

is the degree of the regular digraph.
The argument proceeds by the moment method, estimating the probabilities to observe some given collection of cycles for large

. Our proof remains valid for

growing with

but very slowly (

; the approximation is expected to be true for faster rates for

, but would require more precise combinatorial arguments.

Remark 3.3
.

Theorem

yields the fluctuations of linear statistics
\sum_{i=1}^{n}[f(\lambda_{i})-\mathbb{E}f(\lambda_{i})]
for any (analytic) polynomial
f
, where
\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{n}
are eigenvalues of
A/\sqrt{d}
. To study the fluctuations of linear statistics for general smooth test function with the moment method, one needs to extend the results to polynomials in

and

. This approach was developed in
[

]
for the Ginibre ensemble based on the determinantal structure.

3.2.
Notational preliminaries

We never indicate that
d
and the random variables defined in terms of
A

depend on
n
=size(
A
).
We always consider limits as
n\to\infty
.

Sets.

For any
k\in\mathbb{N}
, denote
[k]=\{1,\dotsc,k\}
.
If
I\subset\mathbb{N}
is a finite set, we denote by
S_{I}
, the group of permutations of elements in
I
.
For
\sigma\in S_{I}
, we let
\epsilon(\sigma)
be the sign of this permutation.
If
I
is a finite set, we denote by
|I|
its cardinal (number of elements). Tuples are denoted by
boldface letters
.
If
I\subseteq\mathbb{N}
is a set, then

I^{k}=\{\mathbf{i}=(i_{1},\dots,i_{k}):i_{1},\dots,i_{k}\in I\}

for
k\in\mathbb{N}
.
For
k\in\mathbb{N}
, we denote

\mathcal{E}_{k}=\{\mathbf{i}\in[n]^{k}:i_{1},\dots,i_{k}\text{ are distinct}\}.

Matrices.
In the sequel
A=(A_{i,j})_{i,j\in[n]}
is a random matrix and we denote for any multi-index
\mathbf{i}\in[n]^{k}
,

A_{\mathbf{i}}:=A_{i_{1},i_{2}}\dotsb A_{i_{k-1},i_{k}}A_{i_{k},i_{1}}.

With this notation,

\mathrm{tr}(A^{k})=\sum_{\mathbf{i}\in[n]^{k}}A_{\mathbf{i}}.

For any subset
I\subset[n]
, we denote

A(I)=(A_{i,j})_{i,j\in I}
the corresponding submatrix.

Directed graphs.
The matrix
A
is an
n\times n
matrix with integer entries. It represents the adjacency matrix of a (weighted) digraph, on the vertex set
V=[n]
. The edge set is
E=\{(i,j)\in[n]\times[n]:A_{i,j}\geq 1\}
and the weights correspond to the entries of
A
. We insist on the fact that
G_{A}=(V,E)
is directed and possibly has simple loops.
We can interpret
\mathbf{i}\in[n]^{k}
as
cyclic paths
on the graph of
A
and
A_{\mathbf{i}}
as the
weight
of this path.
In addition, the paths
\mathbf{i}\in\mathcal{E}_{k}
correspond to simple loops.

Random permutation.

We define a random permutation matrix
P=(\mathbf{1}_{\pi(i)=j})_{i,j\in[n]}
, where
\pi
is a uniform random element of
S_{[n]}
.
We will use that under this law, for any
k\in[n]
, any
\mathbf{i},\mathbf{j}\in\mathcal{E}_{k}
,

\mathbb{P}[\pi(i_{1})=j_{1},\dotsc,\pi(i_{k})=j_{k}]=\mathbb{P}[P_{i_{1},j_{1}}=\cdots=P_{i_{k},j_{k}}=1]=\frac{(n-k)!}{n!}.

We will also make several use of the following bounds; for any
d\in\mathbb{N}
, if
\mathbf{k}\in\mathbb{N}^{d}
with
k_{1}+\cdots+k_{d}=k\leq n
, then

(3.4)

\prod_{\delta=1}^{d}\frac{(n-k_{\delta})!}{n!}\leq\frac{(n-k)!}{n!}.

4.
Tools for proving the tightness of
\{\chi_{n}\}

4.1.
A simple tightness criterion and proof for Proposition
3.1

By Montel’s theorem, a family of analytic functions
\{f_{n}\}
on a domain
D
is tight (for the compact-open topology) if for any compact
K\subset D
,

\big\{\|f_{n}\|_{L^{\infty}(K)}\big\}\text{ is tight.}

See, for example,
[
55
, Proposition 2.5]
.
In particular, by Markov’s inequality, it suffices to check that for some
\alpha>0

(4.1)

\displaystyle\sup_{n}\mathbb{E}\|f_{n}\|_{L^{\infty}(K)}^{\alpha}<\infty.

We will use this criterion to show that the characteristic polynomial of a random
n\times n
matrix
A
,

\big\{\chi_{n}=\det(I+\cdot A)\big\}

forms a tight sequence in a disk
D_{r}=\{|z|<r\}
.
Note that the random matrices
A
need not be defined on the same probability spaces for different
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Recall that for
z\in\mathbb{C}
,

\chi_{n}(z)=\sum_{k\leq n}z^{k}\Delta_{k}^{(n)},

where
\Delta_{k}
are called
secular coefficients
and are given by
\Delta_{0}=1
,

(4.2)

\displaystyle\Delta_{k}^{(n)}=\sum_{I\subseteq[n],|I|=k}\det(A(I)),\qquad k\in[n].

The key technical element for the proof of Proposition
3.1
is the following estimate on the secular coefficients.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
A=\sum_{q=1}^{d}P^{(q)}
where
P^{(q)}
are
d\in\mathbb{N}
independent random permutation matrices.
For any
n\geq 2
, it holds for all
0<r<1
and
1\leq d<\sqrt{\frac{n(1-r)}{r}}
,

(4.3)

\displaystyle\sum_{k\leq n}\frac{r^{k}}{d^{k+1}}\mathbb{E}|\Delta_{k}^{(n)}|^{2}\leq\frac{\frac{2}{d}+r}{(1-r-\frac{rd^{2}}{n})^{2}}.

The proof of this lemma is postponed to a subsequent section.

Proof of Proposition
3.1
given (
4.3
).

For any
\theta<1
,
r>0
and
\epsilon>0
, Jensen’s inequality yields

\bigg(\sum_{k=0}^{n}r^{k}\theta^{k}|\Delta_{k}^{(n)}|\bigg)^{1+\epsilon}\leq\frac{1}{(1-\theta)^{\epsilon}}\sum_{k=0}^{n}r^{k(1+\epsilon)}|\Delta_{k}^{(n)}|^{1+\epsilon}

and consequently,

\mathbb{E}\|\chi_{n}\|_{L^{\infty}(D_{r\theta})}^{1+\epsilon}\leq\mathbb{E}\bigg(\sum_{k=0}^{n}r^{k}\theta^{k}|\Delta_{k}^{(n)}|\bigg)^{1+\epsilon}\leq\frac{1}{(1-\theta)^{\epsilon}}\sum_{k=0}^{n}r^{k(1+\epsilon)}\mathbb{E}|\Delta_{k}^{(n)}|^{1+\epsilon}.

With the normalization (
2.1
), by scaling and choosing
\epsilon=1
,
it holds for
r>0
and
\theta<1
,

(4.4)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\|\widehat{\chi}_{n}\|_{L^{\infty}(D_{r\theta})}^{2}\leq\frac{1}{1-\theta}\sum_{k=0}^{n}\frac{r^{2k}}{d^{k+1}}\mathbb{E}|\Delta_{k}^{(n)}|^{2}.

Now, (
4.1
), (
4.4
), and (
4.3
) immediately imply Proposition
3.1
.

∎

4.2.
Exchangeability

By (
4.2
), it holds for any
k\in[n]

\mathbb{E}\Delta_{k}^{2}=\sum_{|I|,|J|=k}\mathbb{E}\det(A(I))\det(A(J)).

where the sum is over all subsets
I,J\subseteq[n]
.
Lemma
4.3
below allows to reduce this sum to subsets
(I,J)
which share at least
k-1
elements;

(4.5)

\mathbb{\mathbb{E}}\Delta_{k}^{2}=\sum_{|I|=k}\mathbb{E}\det(A(I))^{2}+\sum_{|I|=|J|=k,|I\cap J|=k-1}\mathbb{E}\det(A(I))\det(A(J)).

The Lemmas of this section apply to any exchangeable random matrix model, that is, if the following invariance property holds; for any given permutation
\pi\in S_{[n]}
,

(4.6)

\big(A_{i,\pi(j)}\big)_{i,j\in[n]}\overset{\rm law}{=}\big(A_{\pi(i),j}\big)_{i,j\in[n]}\overset{\rm law}{=}\big(A_{i,j}\big)_{i,j\in[n]}.

Lemma 4.2
.

For any
k\in[n]
,

\mathbb{E}\Delta_{k}^{(n)}=\mathbf{1}_{k=1}\mathbb{E}\mathrm{tr}A.

In particular, for the random matrix
A=\sum_{q=1}^{d}P^{(q)}
,
we have
\mathbb{E}\Delta_{1}^{(n)}=d
and
\mathbb{E}\Delta_{k}^{(n)}=0
for
k>1
, hence (
2.2
) holds.

Proof.

By (
4.2
), it holds for any
k\in[n]

\mathbb{E}\Delta_{k}=\sum_{|I|=k}\mathbb{E}\det(A(I)).

If the random matrix
A
satisfies (
4.6
), observe that by permuting two columns of
A
,
\mathbb{E}\det(A(I))=-\mathbb{E}\det(A(I))
, hence
\mathbb{E}\det(A(I))=0
for any
I\subset[n]
provided that
|I|\geq 2
.
This shows that
\mathbb{E}\Delta_{k}=0
for all
k\geq 2
and

\mathbb{E}\Delta_{1}=\sum_{i\in[n]}\mathbb{E}A_{i,i}=\mathbb{E}\mathrm{tr}A.\qed

Lemma 4.3
.

Assume
|I|=|J|=k
. If
|I\cap J|\leq k-2
, then
\mathbb{E}\det(A(I))\det(A(J))=0
.

Proof.

For ease of notation, we consider the case when
|I\cap J|=k-2
. Other cases follow in the same way.
Let us denote
\{x,y\}=I\setminus J
.
Given
\sigma\in S_{I},\tau\in S_{J}
, using the invariance in law (
4.6
) applied to
\pi=(xy)
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\prod_{i\in I,j\in J}A_{i,\sigma(i)}A_{j,\tau(j)}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\prod_{i\in I,j\in J}A_{i,\sigma^{\prime}(i)}A_{j,\tau(j)}\right]

where
\sigma^{\prime}=\sigma\circ\pi
. Note that we used that
\tau\circ\pi=\tau
since
x,y\notin J
. Since the map
\sigma\to\sigma^{\prime}=\sigma\circ\pi
is a bijection on
S_{I}
with the property that
\epsilon(\sigma^{\prime})=-\epsilon(\sigma)
, by summing over all
\sigma\in S_{I}
, this implies that for any fixed
\tau\in S_{J}
,

\displaystyle\sum_{\sigma\in S_{I}}\epsilon(\sigma)\epsilon(\tau)\mathbb{E}\left[\prod_{i\in I,j\in J}A_{i,\sigma(i)}A_{j,\tau(j)}\right]=0.

Summing over
\tau\in S_{J}
gives the desired result.
∎

From Lemma
4.3
, identity (
4.5
) holds.

5.
The general case: tightness and trace asymptotics

5.1.
Proving the tightness of the determinants in the
d=1
case

When
d=1
, the matrix
A
is indeed the permutation matrix of a uniform permutation of
[n]
and the analysis is easier to perform. We include this special case because it gives all the ideas for the general proof.

When
d=1
, the whole problem reduces to the study of random permutation matrix. In this case we can calculate the second moment of secular coefficients exactly, which gives a proof of Lemma
4.1
for the case
d=1
.

Lemma 5.1
.

For all
1\leq k\leq n-1
,

(5.1)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}|\Delta_{k}|^{2}=2,

and

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}|\Delta_{n}|^{2}=1.

Proof.

Let
\pi\in S_{n}
be a uniform random permutation and
A
be the permutation matrix of
\pi
.
By Lemma
4.3
, we only need to evaluate

\displaystyle\sum_{|I|=k}\mathbb{E}\det(A(I)^{2})+\sum_{|I|=|J|=k,|I\cap J|=k-1}\mathbb{E}\det A(I)A(J).

The summation in the second term is non-empty if and only if
k<n
. For the first term,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\det(A(I)^{2})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\sigma,\tau\in S(I)}\epsilon(\sigma)\epsilon(\tau)\mathbb{E}\prod_{i\in I}A_{i,\sigma(i)}A_{i,\tau(i)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\sigma,\tau}\epsilon(\sigma)\epsilon(\tau)\mathbb{P}\left(\pi(i)=\sigma(i)=\tau(i),\forall i\in I\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\sigma}\mathbb{P}\left(\pi(i)=\sigma(i),\forall i\in I\right)=\mathbb{P}(\pi:I\to I)=\frac{k!(n-k)!}{n!}=\frac{1}{\binom{n}{k}},

where
\pi:I\to I
is the event that
\pi
restricted on
I
is a permutation on
I
.
Hence

\displaystyle\sum_{|I|=k}\mathbb{E}\det(A(I)^{2})=1.

By taking
k=n
, it gives
\mathbb{E}[|\Delta_{n}|^{2}]=1
.

For the second term, with the assumption
|I|=|J|=k,|I\cap J|=k-1
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\det A(I)A(J)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\sigma\in S(I),\tau\in S(J)}\epsilon(\sigma)\epsilon(\tau)\mathbb{E}\prod_{i\in I}A_{i,\sigma(i)}\prod_{j\in J}A_{j,\tau(j)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\sigma\in S(I),\tau\in S(J)}\epsilon(\sigma)\epsilon(\tau)\mathbb{P}(\pi(i)=\sigma(i),i\in I,\pi(j)=\tau(j),j\in J)

Without loss of generality, we assume
I=\{1,\dots,k\},J=\{2,\dots,k+1\}
. To have a non-zero probability, we must have
\sigma(i)=\tau(i)
for all
i\in I\cap J
. This forces
\sigma,\tau
to be two permutations on
I\cap J
, and
\sigma(1)=1,\tau(k+1)=k+1
. Let

\Omega=\{(\sigma,\tau)\in S(I)\times S(J):\sigma(1)=1,\tau(k+1)=k+1,\sigma(k)=\tau(k),\forall k\in I\cap J\}.

For any
(\sigma,\tau)\in\Omega
, both permutations have the same cycle types, hence
\epsilon(\sigma)=\epsilon(\tau)
.
Therefore

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\det A(I)A(J)

\displaystyle=\sum_{(\sigma,\tau)\in\Omega}\mathbb{P}(\pi(i)=\sigma(i),i\in I,\pi(j)=\tau(j),j\in J)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}(\pi(1)=1,\pi(k+1)=k+1,\pi:\{2,\dots,k\}\to\{2,\dots,k\})

\displaystyle=\frac{(k-1)!(n-k-1)!}{n!}.

Since
\#\big\{I,J\subset[n]:|I|=|J|=k,|I\cap J|=k-1\big\}=\binom{n}{k}k(n-k)
, we conclude that

\sum_{|I|=|J|=k,|I\cap J|=k-1}\mathbb{E}\det A(I)A(J)=\binom{n}{k}\frac{k!(n-k)!}{n!}=1.

∎

5.2.
Proving the tightness of the sequence
(\chi_{n})
with the second moment argument

In the case of a sum of
d>1
permutations, proving the tightness of
(\chi_{n})
using the criteria given in Subsection
4.1
is more involved and necessitates a detailed technical analysis of the determinant expansion of
I-zA
. We recall that
A=\sum_{q=1}^{d}P^{(q)}
, where
P^{(q)},q\in[d]
are
n\times n
independent random permutation matrices.
The subsequent computations crucially rely on the exchangeability of
A
as in (
4.6
).

Lemma 5.2
.

For any
n\geq 2
, it holds for all
0<r<1
and
1\leq d<\sqrt{\frac{n(1-r)}{r}}
,

\sum_{k\geq 1}\frac{r^{k}}{d^{k}}\sum_{|I|=k}\mathbb{E}\det(A(I)^{2})\leq\frac{2}{1-r-\frac{rd^{2}}{n}}.

Proof.

First observe that

\sum_{|I|=k}\mathbb{E}\det(A(I)^{2})=\sum_{|I|=k}\sum_{\sigma,\tau\in S(I)}\epsilon(\sigma)\epsilon(\tau)\mathbb{E}\prod_{i\in I}A_{i,\sigma(i)}\prod_{j\in I}A_{j,\tau(j)}.

Then, by symmetry of the permutation model,

(5.2)

\displaystyle\sum_{|I|=k}\mathbb{E}\det(A(I)^{2})

\displaystyle=\binom{n}{k}\sum_{\sigma,\tau\in S_{k}}\epsilon(\sigma)\epsilon(\tau)\prod_{i\in[k]}\mathbb{E}A_{i,\sigma(i)}A_{i,\tau(i)}

\displaystyle=\binom{n}{k}\sum_{\sigma,\tau\in S_{k}}\epsilon(\sigma^{-1}\tau)\mathbb{E}\prod_{i\in[k]}A_{i,i}A_{i,\sigma^{-1}\tau(i)}

\displaystyle=\binom{n}{k}k!\sum_{\tau\in S_{k}}\epsilon(\tau)\mathbb{E}\prod_{i\in[k]}A_{i,i}A_{i,\tau(i)}

\displaystyle=\frac{n!}{(n-k)!}\sum_{q_{1},\dots,q_{k}\in[d]}\sum_{\ell_{1},\dots,\ell_{k}\in[d]}\sum_{\tau\in S_{k}}\epsilon(\tau)\mathbb{E}\prod_{i\in[k]}P_{ii}^{(q_{i})}P_{i\tau(i)}^{(\ell_{i})}.

where we used (
4.6
) to obtain (
5.2
).
Observe that for any fixed
q,\ell\in[d]^{k}
and all
i\in[k]
, if
q_{i}=\ell_{i}
then

P_{i,i}^{(q_{i})}P_{i,\tau(i)}^{(\ell_{i})}=P_{i,i}^{(q_{i})}\mathbf{1}\{\tau(i)=i\},

and using independence,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\prod_{i\in[k]}P_{ii}^{(r)}P_{i\tau(i)}^{(r)}

\displaystyle=\prod_{r\in[d]}\mathbb{E}\bigg[{\prod_{i:q_{i}=r}P_{ii}^{(r)}\prod_{i:\ell_{i}=r}P_{i\tau(i)}^{(r)}}\bigg]

\displaystyle=\prod_{r\in[d]}\mathbf{1}\big\{\tau(i)=i;\forall i\in\{q_{i}=\ell_{i}=r\}\big\}\mathbb{E}\bigg[{\prod_{i:q_{i}=r}P_{ii}^{(r)}\prod_{i\in K_{r}}P_{i\tau(i)}^{(r)}}\bigg]

where
K_{r}=K_{r}(\ell,q)=\{i:\ell_{i}=r,\ q_{i}\neq r\}
.
Let
K=K(\ell,q)=\{i:q_{i}\neq\ell_{i}\}=\bigcup_{r=1}^{d}K_{r}
(disjoint) and for
r\in[d]
,

\Theta_{r}(\tau)=\mathbb{E}\bigg[\prod_{i:q_{i}=r}{P_{ii}^{(r)}\prod_{i\in K_{r}}P^{(r)}_{i\tau(i)}}\bigg]\mathbf{1}\big\{\tau(i)\notin\{i:q_{i}=r\};\forall i\in K_{r}\big\}.

This implies that

(5.3)

\sum_{\tau\in S_{k}}\epsilon(\tau)\mathbb{E}\prod_{i\in[k]}P_{ii}^{(q_{i})}P_{i\tau(i)}^{(\ell_{i})}=\sum_{\tau\in S_{K}}\epsilon(\tau)\prod_{r\in[d]}\Theta_{r}(\tau)

where in the right hand side of (
5.3
), a sum over an empty set is considered 0.
By (
4.6
), observe that for any permutation
\sigma\in S_{K}
which fixes the subsets
\{K_{r}\}_{r\in[d]}
,

\prod_{r\in[d]}\Theta_{r}(\sigma\tau)=\prod_{r\in[d]}\Theta_{r}(\tau).

Let

\delta_{r}=\#K_{r}=\#\{i:\ell_{i}=r,q_{i}\not=\ell_{i}\}.

If
\delta_{r}\geq 2
for some
r\in[d]
, by a transposition, one has

\sum_{\tau\in S_{K}}\epsilon(\tau)\prod_{r\in[d]}\Theta_{r}(\tau)=0.

In particular, this shows that for any fixed
q,\ell\in[d]^{k}
,

\bigg|\sum_{\tau\in S_{K}}\epsilon(\tau)\prod_{r\in[d]}\Theta_{r}(\tau)\bigg|\leq\Delta!\prod_{r\in[d]}\mathbf{1}\big\{\delta_{r}\in\{0,1\}\big\}\frac{(n-\#\{i:q_{i}=r\}-\delta_{r})!}{n!},\qquad\Delta=\sum_{r\in[d]}\delta_{r}=\#K.

Observe that for any integers
k_{1}\geq 1,k_{2}\geq 0
,
\delta_{1},\delta_{2}\in\{0,1\}
,

(5.4)

\displaystyle\frac{(n-k_{1}-\delta_{1})!}{(n-k_{1}-k_{2})!}\leq\begin{cases}\frac{n!}{(n-k_{2}-\delta_{2})!}\frac{1}{n^{\delta_{1}+\delta_{2}}},&\delta_{1}+\delta_{2}\leq 1,\\
\frac{n!}{(n-k_{2}-\delta_{2})!}\frac{2}{n^{\delta_{1}+\delta_{2}}},&\delta_{1}=\delta_{2}=1.\end{cases}

Let
k_{r}(q)=\#\{i:q_{i}=r\}
. We have
k_{1}+\cdots+k_{r}=k
. By symmetry, we can assume
k_{1}\geq 1
, and by induction, we obtain

\displaystyle\prod_{r\in[d]}\frac{(n-k_{r}-\delta_{r})!}{n!}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2}{n^{\delta_{1}+\delta_{2}}}\frac{(n-k_{1}-k_{2})!}{n!}\prod_{r\geq 3}\frac{(n-k_{r}-\delta_{r})!}{n!}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{(n-k)!}{n!}\frac{2}{n^{\Delta}}

where
\Delta=\sum_{r\in[d]}\delta_{r}
.
We conclude that

\bigg|\sum_{\tau\in S_{K}}\epsilon(\tau)\prod_{r\in[d]}\Theta_{r}(\tau)\bigg|\leq\frac{2\Delta!(n-k)!}{n!n^{\Delta}}\prod_{r\in[d]}\mathbf{1}\big\{\delta_{r}\in\{0,1\}\big\},\qquad\Delta=\sum_{r\in[d]}\delta_{r}=\#K.

Going back to (
5.2
), using formula (
5.3
), this argument shows that

\displaystyle\sum_{|I|=k}\mathbb{E}\det(A(I)^{2})

\displaystyle\leq 2\sum_{q_{1},\dots,q_{k}\in[d]}\sum_{\ell_{1},\dots,\ell_{k}\in[d]}\frac{\Delta!}{n^{\Delta}}\prod_{r\in[d]}\mathbf{1}\big\{\delta_{r}\in\{0,1\}\big\}.

For
\ell\in[d]^{k}
, let us denote
k_{r}=k_{r}(\ell)=\#\{i:\ell_{i}=r\}
for
r\in[d]
.
Observe that given
k_{1},\dots,k_{r}
, there are
\binom{k}{k_{1},\dots,k_{d}}
many choices of
\ell\in[d]^{k}
, and for
\delta\in\{0,1\}^{d}
, there are at most
d^{\Delta}\prod_{r=1}^{d}k_{r}^{\delta_{r}}
configurations
q\in[d]^{k}
which contributes to the previous sum.
Hence,

\displaystyle\sum_{|I|=k}\mathbb{E}\det(A(I)^{2})

\displaystyle\leq 2\sum_{k_{1},\dots,k_{d}\geq 0}\binom{k}{k_{1},\cdots,k_{d}}\sum_{\delta_{1},\dots,\delta_{d}\in\{0,1\}}\prod_{r\in[d]}\bigg(\frac{k_{r}d^{2}}{n}\bigg)^{\delta_{r}}

\displaystyle=2\sum_{k_{1},\dots,k_{d}\geq 0}\binom{k}{k_{1},\cdots,k_{d}}\prod_{r\in[d]}\bigg(1+\frac{k_{r}d^{2}}{n}\bigg)

(5.5)

\displaystyle=2d^{k}\big(1+\tfrac{d}{n}\partial_{x}\big)^{d}x^{k}\big|_{x=1}.

The last identity follows from the fact that the second display equals (up to a factor 2)

\displaystyle\sum_{k_{1},\dots,k_{d}\geq 0}\binom{k}{k_{1},\cdots,k_{d}}\prod_{r\in[d]}\big(1+\epsilon\partial_{x}\big)x^{k_{r}}\big|_{x=1}

\displaystyle=\prod_{r\in[d]}\big(1+\epsilon\partial_{x_{r}}\big)\bigg(\sum_{k_{1},\dots,k_{d}\geq 0}\binom{k}{k_{1},\cdots,k_{d}}\prod_{r\in[d]}x_{r}^{k_{r}}\bigg)\bigg|_{{x_{1}=\dotsc=x_{d}=1}}

\displaystyle=\prod_{r\in[d]}\big(1+\epsilon\partial_{x_{r}}\big)\big(x_{1}+\cdots+{x_{d}}\big)^{k}\big|_{{x_{1}=\cdots=x_{d}=1}}

\displaystyle=\big(1+\epsilon\partial_{x}\big)^{d}x^{k}\big|_{x=d}

\displaystyle=d^{k}\big(1+\tfrac{\epsilon}{d}\partial_{x}\big)^{d}x^{k}\big|_{x=1}

by scaling, and taking
\epsilon=d^{2}/n
.
By summing over all
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, we conclude that for any
0<r<1
,

\displaystyle\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\frac{r^{k}}{d^{k}}\sum_{|I|=k}\mathbb{E}\det(A(I)^{2})

\displaystyle\leq 2\big(1+\tfrac{d}{n}\partial_{x}\big)^{d}\frac{1}{1-xr}\bigg|_{x=1}

\displaystyle=2\sum_{\ell\geq 0}\binom{d}{\ell}\frac{d^{\ell}}{n^{\ell}}\partial_{x}^{\ell}\frac{1}{1-xr}\bigg|_{x=1}

\displaystyle\leq 2\sum_{\ell=0}^{d}\frac{d!}{(d-\ell)!}\frac{(dr)^{\ell}}{n^{\ell}}\frac{1}{(1-r)^{\ell+1}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2}{1-r}\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}\left(\frac{d^{2}r}{n(1-r)}\right)^{\ell}\leq\frac{2}{1-r}\cdot\frac{1}{1-\frac{d^{2}r}{n(1-r)}}=\frac{2}{1-r(1+\frac{d^{2}}{n})}.

The previous sum converges if
\frac{d^{2}r}{n(1-r)}<1
.
This completes the proof.
∎

Lemma 5.3
.

For any
n\geq 2,
it holds for all
0<r<1
and
1\leq d<\sqrt{\frac{n(1-r)}{r}}
,

\sum_{k\geq 1}\frac{r^{k}}{d^{k+1}}\bigg|\sum_{|I|=|J|=k,|I\cap J|=k-1}\mathbb{E}\det A(I)A(J)\bigg|\leq\frac{r}{(1-r-\frac{rd^{2}}{n})^{2}}.

Proof.

Using the invariance (
4.6
) of the random matrix
A
, for
k\leq n-1
, we can rewrite

(5.6)

\displaystyle\sum_{|I|=|J|=k,|I\cap J|=k-1}\mathbb{E}\det A(I)A(J)

\displaystyle=\binom{n}{k}k(n-k)\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\tau\in S_{k}\otimes S_{1}\\
\sigma\in S_{1}\otimes S_{k}\end{subarray}}\epsilon(\sigma)\epsilon(\tau)\mathbb{E}\prod_{j=1}^{k}\prod_{i=2}^{k+1}A_{i,\sigma(i)}A_{j,\tau(j)}

where
S_{k}\otimes S_{1}=\{\tau\in S_{k+1}:\tau(k+1)=k+1\}.
and
S_{1}\otimes S_{k}=\{\sigma\in S_{k+1}:\sigma(1)=1\}.
Now (
5.6
) can be written as

\displaystyle=\binom{n}{k}k(n-k)\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\tau\in S_{k}\otimes S_{1}\\
\sigma\in S_{1}\otimes S_{k}\end{subarray}}\epsilon(\sigma)\epsilon(\tau)\sum_{\ell_{1},\dots,\ell_{k}}\sum_{q_{2},\dots,q_{k+1}}\mathbb{E}\prod_{i=2}^{k+1}P_{i,\sigma(i)}^{(q_{i})}\prod_{j=1}^{k}P_{j,\tau(j)}^{(\ell_{j})}

\displaystyle=\binom{n}{k}k(n-k)\sum_{\ell_{1},\dots,\ell_{k}}\sum_{q_{2},\dots,q_{k+1}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\tau\in S_{k}\otimes S_{1}\\
\sigma\in S_{1}\otimes S_{k}\end{subarray}}\epsilon(\sigma)\epsilon(\tau)\mathbf{1}_{\big\{\tau(j)=\sigma(j),\forall j\in[k]\setminus K\big\}}\prod_{r\in[d]}\mathbb{E}\bigg[\prod_{i\geq 2:q_{i}=r}P_{i,\sigma(i)}\prod_{j\in K:\ell_{j}=r}P_{j\tau(j)}\bigg],

where
K=K(\ell,q)=\{j\in[k]:q_{j}\neq\ell_{j}\}
.
By convention
1\in K
and by symmetry we may assume that
l_{1}=1
and multiply the sum with an extra factor
d
.
Moreover, by the argument following (
5.3
), we must have for all
r\in\{2,\dots,d\}
,

\delta_{r}=\#\{j\in K:\ell_{j}=r\}\in\{0,1\}

for otherwise the sum over all permutation
\tau\in S_{k}
yields 0 by a transposition. Similarly, we must have

\delta_{1}=\#\{j\in K:\ell_{j}=1\}=1

since
1\in K
.
Then, for any configuration
q,\ell\in[d]^{k}
with
\ell_{1}=1
, following the argument in (
5.4
), it holds for any
\sigma,\tau\in S_{k+1}

\displaystyle\prod_{r\in[d]}\mathbb{E}\bigg[\prod_{i\geq 2:q_{i}=r}P_{i,\sigma(i)}\prod_{j\in K:\ell_{j}=r}P_{j,\tau(j)}\bigg]

\displaystyle\leq\prod_{r\in[d]}\frac{(n-\#\{i\geq 2:q_{i}=r\}-\delta_{r})!}{n!}

\displaystyle=\frac{(n-k_{1}-1)!}{n!}\prod_{r\geq 2}\frac{(n-k_{r}-\delta_{r})!}{n!}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{(n-k-1)!}{n!\,n^{\Delta}}

where
\Delta=\sum_{r=2}^{d}\delta_{r}
and
k_{r}=k_{r}(q)=\#\{i\geq 2:q_{i}=r\}
form a partition of
k
for
r\in[d]
.
Moreover, for any
\Delta\leq d-1
and any given
\sigma\in S_{1}\otimes S_{k}
,

\#\big\{\tau\in S_{k}:\tau(j)=\sigma(j),\forall j\in[k]\setminus K\big\}\leq(\Delta+1)!\leq d^{\Delta},

so that this argument shows that

\displaystyle\binom{n}{k}k(n-k)\sum_{\ell_{1},\dots,\ell_{k}}\sum_{q_{2},\dots,q_{k+1}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\tau\in S_{k}\otimes S_{1}\\
\sigma\in S_{1}\otimes S_{k}\end{subarray}}\epsilon(\sigma)\epsilon(\tau)\mathbf{1}_{\big\{\tau(j)=\sigma(j),\forall j\in[k]\setminus K\big\}}\prod_{r\in[d]}\mathbb{E}\bigg[\prod_{i\geq 2:q_{i}=r}P_{i,\sigma(i)}\prod_{j\in K:\ell_{j}=r}P_{j\tau(j)}\bigg]

\displaystyle\leq dk\sum_{q_{2},\dots,q_{k+1}\in[d]}\sum_{l_{2},\dots,l_{k}\in[d]}\frac{d^{\Delta}}{n^{\Delta}}\mathbf{1}\big\{\delta_{1}=1\big\}\prod_{r=2}^{d}\mathbf{1}\big\{\delta_{r}\in\{0,1\}\big\}.

Let
k_{r}=\#\{i\geq 2:\ell_{i}=r\}
for
r\in[d]
. There are
\binom{k-1}{k_{1},\dots,k_{d}}
many choices of
\ell\in[d]^{k}
with
\ell_{1}=1
.
As in the proof of Lemma
5.2
, for a given
l\in[d]^{k}
with
\ell_{1}=1
, there are at most

d^{\Delta+1}\prod_{r=2}^{d}k_{r}^{\delta_{r}}

configurations
(q_{2},\cdots,q_{k+1})\in[d]^{k}
which contribute to the previous sum; this is because there are
d^{\Delta}\prod_{r=2}^{d}k_{r}^{\delta_{r}}
many choices for
(q_{2},\dots,q_{k})
and at most
d
choices for
q_{k+1}
.
Hence,

\displaystyle dk\sum_{q_{2},\dots,q_{k+1}\in[d]}\sum_{\ell_{2},\dots,\ell_{k}\in[d]}\frac{d^{\Delta}}{n^{\Delta}}\mathbf{1}\big\{\delta_{1}=1\big\}\prod_{r=2}^{d}\mathbf{1}\big\{\delta_{r}\in\{0,1\}\big\}

\displaystyle\leq d^{2}k\sum_{k_{1},\dots,k_{d}\geq 0}\binom{k-1}{k_{1},\dots,k_{d}}\sum_{\delta_{2},\dots,\delta_{d}\in\{0,1\}}\prod_{r=2}^{d}\left(\frac{k_{r}d^{2}}{n}\right)^{\delta_{r}}

\displaystyle\leq kd^{k+1}\big(1+\tfrac{d}{n}\partial_{x}\big)^{d}x^{k-1}\big|_{x=1}.

The last inequality follows as in the proof of Lemma
5.2
(replacing
k
by
k-1
).
Summing over all
k\in\mathbb{N}
, we conclude that for any
0<r<1
,

\displaystyle\sum_{k\geq 1}\frac{r^{k}}{d^{k+1}}\bigg|\sum_{|I|=|J|=k,|I\cap J|=k-1}\mathbb{E}\det A(I)A(J)\bigg|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k\geq 1}kr^{k}\big(1+\tfrac{d}{n}\partial_{x}\big)^{d}x^{k-1}\bigg|_{x=1}=\big(1+\tfrac{d}{n}\partial_{x}\big)^{d}\frac{r}{(1-rx)^{2}}\bigg|_{x=1}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell=0}^{d}\binom{d}{\ell}\frac{d^{\ell}}{n^{\ell}}\partial_{x}^{\ell}\frac{r}{(1-xr)^{2}}\bigg|_{x=1}

\displaystyle=r\sum_{\ell=0}^{d}\frac{d!}{(d-\ell)!\ell!}\frac{(rd)^{\ell}}{n^{\ell}}\frac{(\ell+1)!}{(1-r)^{\ell+2}}

(5.7)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{r}{(1-r)^{2}}\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}(\ell+1)\bigg(\frac{d^{2}r}{n(1-r)}\bigg)^{\ell}=\frac{r}{(1-r-\frac{rd^{2}}{n})^{2}}.

This completes the proof.
∎

Proof of Proposition
3.1
.

From Lemma
5.2
and Lemma
5.3
, together with (
4.5
), we obtain when
1\leq d\leq\sqrt{\frac{n(1-r)}{r}}
,

(5.8)

\displaystyle\sum_{k\leq n}\frac{r^{k}}{d^{k+1}}\mathbb{E}|\Delta_{k}^{(n)}|^{2}\leq\frac{\frac{2}{d}+r}{(1-r-\frac{rd^{2}}{n})^{2}}.

Then from (

5.3.
Asymptotics of traces of sums of
d
independent uniform permutations

We now turn to the identification of the limits of
\mathrm{trace}(A^{k})
stated in Theorem
3.2
. We will first need some probability estimates for the
d=1
case that will be used in the sequel.

5.4.
Probability estimates for the
d=1
case

Recall that in case
d=1
,
A=P=(\mathbf{1}_{\pi(i)=j})_{i,j\in[n]}
where
\pi
is a uniform random element of
S_{[n]}
.
We start by giving an estimate on the probability of generic events.
Recall that for
s\in\mathbb{N}
, we denote

\mathcal{E}_{s}=\{\mathbf{i}\in[n]^{s}:i_{1},\dots,i_{s}\text{ are distinct}\}.

Proposition 5.4
.

Let
r,s\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
with
r+s<n
and fix
\mathbf{i},\mathbf{j}\in\mathcal{E}_{s}
such that
(i_{1},j_{1}),\cdots,{(i_{s},j_{s})}
are distinct, and
\mathbf{k},\mathbf{q}\in[n]^{r}
. Assume

(5.9)

\big\{(k_{1},q_{1}),\cdots,(k_{r},q_{r})\big\}\cap\big\{(i_{1},j_{1}),\cdots,(i_{s},j_{s})\big\}=\emptyset.

Then there exists
0\leq\varepsilon\leqslant\frac{r}{n-s}
such that

(5.10)

\mathbb{P}\big(P_{i_{1},j_{1}}=\dotsc=P_{i_{s},j_{s}}=1,P_{k_{1},q_{1}}=\dotsc=P_{k_{r},q_{r}}=0\big)=(1-\varepsilon)\frac{(n-s)!}{n!}.

Otherwise, if
\mathbf{i}\notin\mathcal{E}_{s}
, or
\mathbf{j}\notin\mathcal{E}_{s}
, or the condition (
5.9
) fails, then the probability on the LHS of (
5.10
) equals
0
.

Proof.

Under the hypothesis (
5.9
), we have the upper-bound

\mathbb{P}\big(P_{i_{1},j_{1}}=\dotsc=P_{i_{s},j_{s}}=1,P_{k_{1},q_{1}}=\dotsc=P_{k_{r},q_{r}}=0\big)\leq\mathbb{P}\begin{pmatrix}\pi(i_{1})=j_{1},\dotsc,\pi(i_{s})=j_{s}\end{pmatrix}=\frac{(n-s)!}{n!}

valid for any
\mathbf{i},\mathbf{j}\in\mathcal{E}_{s}
.
For the lower-bound, we may assume that
k_{1},\dots,k_{r}\notin\mathbf{i}

and
q_{1},\dots,q_{r}\notin\mathbf{j}
. Otherwise, there are less constraints and the probability of the event in question is larger.
In this case, using the invariance (
4.6
) of the uniform measure on
S_{[n]}
, we can write

\mathbb{P}\begin{pmatrix}P_{i_{1},j_{1}}=\dotsc=P_{i_{s},j_{s}}=1\\
P_{k_{1},q_{1}}=\dotsc=P_{k_{r},q_{r}}=0\end{pmatrix}=\frac{(n-s)!}{n!}\mathbf{Q}\begin{pmatrix}\pi(1)\neq 1,\dotsc,\pi(r)\neq r\end{pmatrix}

where
\mathbf{Q}
is the uniform measure on
S_{[n-s]}
.
In particular, we have the simple lower-bound

\mathbf{Q}\begin{pmatrix}\pi(1)\neq 1,\dotsc,\pi(r)\neq r\end{pmatrix}\geq 1-r\mathbf{Q}\big(\pi(1)=1\big)=1-\frac{r}{n-s}.

This proves the claim.
∎

5.4.1.
Subgraph probability estimation

Given integers

0\leqslant k_{1},\dotsc,k_{r}\leqslant d
with
k=k_{1}+\dotsb+k_{r}
, define

\mathscr{T}_{\mathbf{k}}=\big\{T\in\{0,1\}^{d\times r}:{\textstyle\sum_{i=1}^{d}}T_{ij}=k_{j}\text{ for all }j\in[r]\big\}.

Observe that

(5.11)

|\mathscr{T}_{\mathbf{k}}|=\binom{d}{k_{1}}\cdots\binom{d}{k_{r}}\leq\frac{d^{k}}{k_{1}!\cdots k_{r}!}.

Proposition 5.5
.

Fix
\mathbf{i},\mathbf{j}\in[n]^{r}
for
r\in\mathbb{N}
such that

(i_{1},j_{1}),\cdots,(i_{r},j_{r})
are distinct.
Let
\mathbf{k}\in[d]^{r}
and set
k=k_{1}+\dotsb+k_{r}
. Then,

\mathbb{P}(A_{i_{1},j_{1}}=k_{1},\dotsc,A_{i_{r},j_{r}}=k_{r})\leqslant|\mathscr{T}_{\mathbf{k}}|\frac{(n-k)!}{n!}.

Proof.

Decomposing the event
\{A_{i_{1},j_{1}}=k_{1},\dotsc,A_{i_{r},j_{r}}=k_{r}\}
in terms of the permutations
P^{1},\dotsc,P^{d}
leads to

\mathbb{P}(A_{i_{1},j_{1}}=k_{1},\dotsc,A_{i_{r},j_{r}}=k_{r})=\sum_{T\in\mathscr{T}}\mathbb{P}\left(\begin{bmatrix}P^{1}_{i_{1},j_{1}}&\dots&P^{1}_{i_{r},j_{r}}\\
\vdots&&\vdots\\
P^{d}_{i_{1},j_{1}}&\dots&P^{d}_{i_{r},j_{r}}\end{bmatrix}=T\right).

Since
P^{1},\dotsc,P^{d}
are independent, we obtain

\mathbb{P}(A_{i_{1},j_{1}}=k_{1},\dotsc,A_{i_{r},j_{r}}=k_{r})=\sum_{T\in\mathscr{T}}\prod_{\delta=1}^{d}\mathbb{P}(P_{i_{1},j_{1}}=T_{\delta,1},\dotsc,P_{i_{r},j_{r}}=T_{\delta,r}).

Each term in this product falls under Proposition
5.4
– either it equals
0
or (
5.10
) holds.
Let
s_{\delta}=T_{\delta,1}+\dotsb+T_{\delta,r}
for
\delta\in[d]
,
this implies that there exists
0\leq\varepsilon_{\delta}\leqslant\frac{r}{n-r}
for
\delta\in[d]
so that

(5.12)

\mathbb{P}(A_{i_{1},j_{1}}=k_{1},\dotsc,A_{i_{r},j_{r}}=k_{r})\leq\sum_{T\in\mathscr{T}}\prod_{\delta=1}^{d}(1-\varepsilon_{\delta})\frac{(n-s_{\delta})!}{n!},\quad\text{ with equality if $\mathbf{i},\mathbf{j}\in\mathcal{E}_{r}$.}

Note that by definition of
\mathscr{T}
,
\sum_{\delta=1}^{d}s_{\delta}=\sum_{j=1}^{r}k_{j}=k
, this yields the upper-bound,

\mathbb{P}(A_{i_{1},j_{1}}=k_{1},\dotsc,A_{i_{r},j_{r}}=k_{r})\leq\sum_{T\in\mathscr{T}}\prod_{\delta=1}^{d}\frac{(n-s_{\delta})!}{n!}\leq|\mathscr{T}|\frac{(n-k)!}{n!}

where we used (
3.4
).
∎

Proposition 5.6
.

Fix
\mathbf{i},\mathbf{j}\in\mathcal{E}_{r}
for
r\leq n/2
. Let
\theta=d/n
.
There exists
|\varepsilon|\leq\max\left\{\frac{dr}{n-r},\frac{2r^{2}}{n}\right\}
such that

(5.13)

\mathbb{P}\big(A_{i_{1},j_{1}}=\cdots=A_{i_{r},j_{r}}=1\big)=(1+\varepsilon)\theta^{r}.

Proof.

Note that for
r\leq n/2
,

\displaystyle\frac{n^{r}(n-r)!}{n!}=\left(1+\frac{r-1}{n-r+1}\right)\cdots\left(1+\frac{1}{n-1}\right)\leq\left(1+\frac{r-1}{n-r+1}\right)^{r-1}\leq 1+\frac{2r^{2}}{n}.

We have the inequality for
r\leq n/2
,

(5.14)

\displaystyle\frac{(n-r)!}{n!}\leq\frac{1+2r^{2}/n}{n^{r}}.

By Proposition
5.5
and using the fact that
|\mathscr{T}_{\mathbf{k}}|=d^{r}
for
\mathbf{k}=(1,\dots,1)
, and (
5.14
), we get

\mathbb{P}\big(A_{i_{1},j_{1}}=\cdots=A_{i_{r},j_{r}}=1\big)\leq\left(1+\frac{2r^{2}}{n}\right)\theta^{r}.

For the lower bound, by formula (
5.12
) in case
\mathbf{i},\mathbf{j}\in\mathcal{E}_{r}
,

\mathbb{P}(A_{i_{1},j_{1}}=k_{1},\dotsc,A_{i_{r},j_{r}}=k_{r})\geq(1-\varepsilon)^{d}\sum_{T\in\mathscr{T}}\prod_{\delta=1}^{d}\frac{(n-s_{\delta})!}{n!}

with
\varepsilon=\frac{r}{n-r}
.
By convexity, when
r<n/2
,
(1-\varepsilon)^{d}\geq 1-d\epsilon
. Then, using that in this case
\sum_{\delta=1}^{d}s_{\delta}=\sum_{j=1}^{r}k_{j}=r
, this implies that

\mathbb{P}(A_{i_{1},j_{1}}=k_{1},\dotsc,A_{i_{r},j_{r}}=k_{r})\geq(1-d\varepsilon)|\mathscr{T}|n^{-r}.

This concludes the proof.
∎

As a direct consequence of Proposition
5.6
, we have the asymptotics of the probability of a simple cycle on the digraph of the random matrix
A
.
We now turn to bounding joint moments of the entries of the random matrix
A
.

Proposition 5.7
.

For a fixed constant
r\in\mathbb{N}
, fix
\bm{\alpha},\bm{\beta}\in\mathbb{N}^{r}
and let
\beta=\beta_{1}+\cdots+\beta_{r}
. Let
\theta=d/n
and assume that
d\leq\sqrt{n}
. Then, for any
\mathbf{i},\mathbf{j}\in[n]^{r}
,

\mathbb{E}\big[A_{i_{1},j_{1}}^{\alpha_{1}}\dotsb A_{i_{r},j_{r}}^{\alpha_{r}}\mathbf{1}_{\{A_{i_{\ell},j_{\ell}}\geq\beta_{\ell},\ell\in[r]\}}\big]=O_{\bm{\alpha},\bm{\beta}}(\theta^{\beta}).

Proof.

Without loss of generality, we may assume that
(i_{1},j_{1}),\cdots,(i_{r},j_{r})
are distinct.
By Proposition
5.5
and (
5.11
), we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[A_{i_{1},j_{1}}^{\alpha_{1}}\dotsb A_{i_{r},j_{r}}^{\alpha_{r}}\mathbf{1}_{\{A_{i_{\ell},j_{\ell}}\geq\beta_{\ell},\ell\in[r]\}}\big]

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathbf{k}\in[d]^{r},\mathbf{k}\geq\bm{\beta}}k_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}\cdots k_{r}^{\alpha_{r}}\mathbb{P}(A_{i_{1},j_{1}}=k_{1},\dotsc,A_{i_{r},j_{r}}=k_{r})

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\mathbf{k}\in[d]^{r},\mathbf{k}\geq\bm{\beta}}\frac{k_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}\cdots k_{r}^{\alpha_{r}}}{k_{1}!\cdots k_{r}!}d^{k}\frac{(n-k)!}{n!};\qquad k=k_{1}+\cdots+k_{r}.

Again with (
5.14
), we can bound
\frac{(n-k)!}{n!}\leq\frac{1+2k^{2}/n}{n^{k}}
for
k\leq n/2
.
Since
r
is fixed, this yields the estimate valid for
k\leq rd\leq n/2
, and
d\leq\sqrt{n}
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[A_{i_{1},j_{1}}^{\alpha_{1}}\dotsb A_{i_{r},j_{r}}^{\alpha_{r}}\mathbf{1}_{\{A_{i_{\ell},j_{\ell}}\geq\beta_{\ell},\ell\in[r]\}}\big]

\displaystyle\leq(1+2r^{2}d^{2}/n)\sum_{\mathbf{k}\geq\bm{\beta}}\prod_{j=1}^{r}\frac{k_{j}^{\alpha_{j}}}{k_{j}!}\theta^{k_{j}}\leq(1+2r^{2})\sum_{\mathbf{k}\geq\bm{\beta}}\prod_{j=1}^{r}\frac{k_{j}^{\alpha_{j}}}{k_{j}!}\theta^{k_{j}}.

Now, we can use the bound for
\theta\leq 1
,

\sum_{k\geq b}\frac{k^{a}}{k!}\theta^{k}\leq C_{a,b}\theta^{b},

to conclude the proof.
∎

5.4.2.
Reducing traces to cycle counts in the digraph of
A

Recall that

\mathrm{tr}(A^{k})=\sum_{\mathbf{i}\in[n]^{k}}A_{\mathbf{i}}.

In particular, by Proposition
5.7
applied with
\alpha_{1}+\cdots+\alpha_{r}=k
,
\beta_{1}=\cdots=\beta_{r}=1
,

\mathbb{E}\mathrm{tr}(A^{k})\leq C_{k}n^{k}\theta^{k}=C_{k}d^{k}.

In particular, in the regime where
d
is fixed, for any
k\in\mathbb{N}
, the non-negative random variables
\big(\mathrm{tr}(A^{k})\big)_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
are tight.

For every
\mathbf{i}=(i_{1},\dotsc,i_{k})\in[n]^{k}
, we can associate a digraph

\displaystyle V(\mathbf{i})=\{i_{1},\dotsc,i_{k}\}

\displaystyle v(\mathbf{i})=\#V(\mathbf{i})

\displaystyle E(\mathbf{i})=\{(i_{1},i_{2}),(i_{2},i_{3}),\dotsc,(i_{k-1},i_{k}),(i_{k},i_{1})\}

\displaystyle e(\mathbf{i})=\#E(\mathbf{i}).

One can interpret
v(\mathbf{i})
as the number of ‘vertices’ of
[n]
that are visited by
\mathbf{i}
, and
e(\mathbf{i})
as the number of distinct ‘edges’ of
\mathbf{i}
. The digraph
(V(\mathbf{i}),E(\mathbf{i}))
contains at least a loop, so necessarily
v(\mathbf{i})\leqslant e(\mathbf{i})\leq k
.
For integers
v\leqslant e\leqslant k
, we define

(5.15)

\mathscr{E}_{k}(v,e)=\{\mathbf{i}\in[n]^{k}:v(\mathbf{i})=v,e(\mathbf{i})=e\}.

Let us decompose

(5.16)

\mathrm{tr}(A^{k})=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{i}\in\mathscr{E}_{k}(v,e)\\
v=e\end{subarray}}\mathbf{1}_{A_{\mathbf{i}}=1}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{i}\in\mathscr{E}_{k}(v,e)\\
v=e\end{subarray}}A_{\mathbf{i}}\mathbf{1}_{A_{\mathbf{i}}>1}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{i}\in\mathscr{E}_{k}(v,e)\\
v<e\end{subarray}}A_{\mathbf{i}}=:T_{k}+R_{k}+S_{k}.

First, let us show that both

6.
Poisson analytic functions

Proof of Proposition
2.5
.

The function
Y_{d}
is centred and it is almost surely analytic in
D_{1}
since
\|Y_{d}\|^{2}_{L^{\infty}(D_{r})}<\infty
almost surely for any
r<1
.
Indeed, by Jensen’s inequality, it holds for
r<1
,

\mathbb{E}\|Y_{d}\|_{L^{\infty}(D_{r})}^{2}\leq\mathbb{E}\bigg(\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\frac{r^{k}}{kd^{k/2}}\bigg|\sum_{\ell|k}\ell\overline{\Lambda_{\ell}}\bigg|\bigg)^{2}\leq\frac{r}{1-r}\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\frac{r^{k}}{k^{2}d^{k}}\mathbb{E}\bigg|\sum_{\ell|k}\ell\overline{\Lambda_{\ell}}\bigg|^{2}.

By independence of
\{\Lambda_{\ell}\}_{\ell\in\mathbb{N}}
, it holds for
k\in\mathbb{N}
,

\frac{1}{k^{2}d^{k}}\mathbb{E}\bigg|\sum_{\ell|k}\ell\overline{\Lambda_{\ell}}\bigg|^{2}=\frac{1}{k^{2}d^{k}}\sum_{\ell|k}\ell^{2}\mathbb{E}\big|\overline{\Lambda_{\ell}}\big|^{2}=\frac{1}{k^{2}d^{k}}\sum_{\ell|k}\ell d^{\ell}\leq 1,

where we used that
\operatorname{Var}(\Lambda_{\ell})=d^{\ell}/\ell
for
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
. This shows that for any
r<1
,

(6.1)

\mathbb{E}\|Y_{d}\|_{L^{\infty}(D_{r})}^{2}\leq\frac{r^{2}}{(1-r)^{2}}.

Repeating this argument clearly leads to the same estimate for the random analytic function
X_{d}
. This proves that
Y_{d}
is well-defined. Then by rearranging the series, we can write

(6.2)

Y_{d}(z)=\sum_{\ell\in\mathbb{N}}\overline{\Lambda_{\ell}}\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\frac{z^{k\ell}}{kd^{k\ell/2}}=-\sum_{\ell\in\mathbb{N}}\overline{\Lambda_{\ell}}\log\big(1-(z/\sqrt{d})^{\ell}\big)

for the principal branch of
\log(1+z)
which is analytic for
z\in D_{1}
.
Recall that the Laplace transform of a random variable
\Lambda\sim\mathrm{Poisson}(\lambda)
satisfies

(6.3)

\mathbb{E}\exp(z(\Lambda-\mathbb{E}\Lambda))=\exp\big(\lambda(e^{z}-1-z)\big),\qquad z\in\mathbb{C}.

In particular, this can be used to give an alternative proof that the series (
6.2
) is (almost surely) absolutely convergent and to justify rearranging this sum.
Indeed, for any
x\in\mathbb{R}
we have
e^{|x|}\leqslant e^{x}+e^{-x}
, hence using (
6.3
) with
z=\pm t/\sqrt{\lambda}
, it holds for all
0\leq t\leq\sqrt{\lambda}
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\exp\big(t|\Lambda-\mathbb{E}\Lambda|/\sqrt{\lambda}\big)

\displaystyle\leq\exp\left(\lambda\left(e^{t/\sqrt{\lambda}}-1-t/\sqrt{\lambda}\right)\right)+\exp\left(\lambda\left(e^{-t/\sqrt{\lambda}}-1+t/\sqrt{\lambda}\right)\right)\leq 2\exp\big(t^{2}\big),

where in the last inequality, we use the fact that
e^{x}\leq 1+x+x^{2}
for
|x|\leq 1
.
For
d\geq 2
, by Markov’s inequality, we obtain the large deviation estimate,

\mathbb{P}\big[|\overline{\Lambda_{\ell}}|\geq\sqrt{d^{\ell}}\big]\leq\exp\big(-\ell/4\big).

Hence, almost surely,
|\overline{\Lambda_{\ell}}|\leq\sqrt{d^{\ell}}
for all
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
sufficiently large and (
6.2
) is absolutely convergent.

Remark 6.1
.

In the special case

, observe that

\mathbb{P}\big[\Lambda_{\ell}\geq 2\text{ infinitely often}\big]\leq\lim_{n\to\infty}\sum_{\ell\geq n}\bigg(\mathbb{P}\big[\Lambda_{\ell}\geq 2\big]\bigg)\leq\bigg(\lim_{n\to\infty}\sum_{\ell\geq n}\frac{1/2}{\ell^{2}}\bigg)=0.

In particular, the random analytic function

converges almost surely for

.
In contrast, for

, it is necessary to re-center the Poisson random coefficients to define a random analytic function in

.

Let us denote by
\mathrm{f}_{\ell}(z):=(1-z^{\ell})e^{z^{\ell}}
for

so that

is analytic in

(using the principal branch).
By independence of
\{\Lambda_{\ell}\}_{\ell\in\mathbb{N}}
and (

\mathbb{E}e^{-Y_{d}(z)}=\bigg(\prod_{\ell\in\mathbb{N}}\mathrm{f}_{\ell}(z/\sqrt{d})^{\frac{d^{\ell}}{\ell}}\bigg)^{-1}.

This proves (
2.5
).
Now, if we repeat the computation leading to (

), then for any

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\|\Upsilon_{d}\|_{L^{\infty}(D_{r})}^{2}=\mathbb{E}\bigg\|\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\frac{z^{k}}{kd^{k/2}}\sum_{\ell|k,\ell<k}\ell\overline{\Lambda_{\ell}}\bigg\|_{L^{\infty}(D_{r})}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{r}{d^{1/4}-r}\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\frac{r^{k}}{k^{2}d^{3k/4}}\mathbb{E}\bigg|\sum_{\ell|k,\ell<k}\ell\overline{\Lambda_{\ell}}\bigg|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{r/2}{d^{1/4}-r}\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\frac{r^{k}}{d^{k/4}}=\frac{r^{2}/2}{(d^{1/4}-r)^{2}},

where we used that

\sum_{\ell|k,\ell<k}\ell d^{\ell}\leq\frac{k^{2}}{2}d^{k/2}.

This shows that the random analytic function

converges almost surely in the disk
D_{d^{1/4}}

D_{1}
if the degree

.
On the other-hand, by independence of

(a_{k}^{(n)})_{k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}\to(a_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
converges in finite-dimensional distributions; for every
k\geq 0
,

(7.1)

\big(a_{0}^{(n)},\dotsc,a_{k}^{(n)}\big)\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{\mathrm{law}}\big(a_{0},\dotsc,a_{k}\big),

then
f=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}a_{k}z^{k}
is convergent almost surely in
D_{1}
and

f_{n}\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{\mathrm{law}}f.

We also need the following non-trivial observation.

Lemma 7.2
.

According to Definition
2.1
,

\mathbb{E}e^{-Y_{d}(z)}=\exp\bigg(\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\frac{z^{k}}{kd^{k/2}}\sum_{\ell|k,\ell<k}\ell\,\mathbb{E}\Lambda_{\ell}\bigg)

where the sum converges absolutely in
D_{1}
.

Proof.

Recall that
\mathrm{f}_{\ell}(z):=(1-z^{\ell})e^{z^{\ell}}
for
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
. These functions does not vanishes in
D_{1}
so
\log\mathrm{f}_{\ell}
is well-defined for the principal branch of
\log
and

-\log\mathrm{f}_{\ell}(z)=\sum_{j\geq 2}\frac{z^{j\ell}}{j};\qquad z\in D_{1}.

Then, since
\mathbb{E}\Lambda_{\ell}=d^{\ell}/\ell
for
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
, we compute

\displaystyle\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\frac{z^{k}}{kd^{k/2}}\sum_{\ell|k,\ell<k}\ell\,\mathbb{E}\Lambda_{\ell}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell\in\mathbb{N}}\frac{d^{\ell}}{\ell}\sum_{j\geq 2}\frac{z^{j\ell}}{jd^{j\ell/2}}

\displaystyle=-\sum_{\ell\in\mathbb{N}}\frac{d^{\ell}}{\ell}\log\mathrm{f}_{\ell}(z/\sqrt{d})

where all sums converge absolutely in
D_{1}
; e.g. using the bound

\big|\log\mathrm{f}_{\ell}(z)\big|\leq\frac{r^{2\ell}}{1-r^{\ell}}
valid for
z\in D_{r}
.
Taking
\exp
and using formula (

), this proves the claim.
∎

The characteristic polynomial of a
n\times n
random matrix
A
satisfies for
z\in\mathbb{C}
,

\chi_{n}(z)=\det(1-zA)=\sum_{k\leq n}z^{k}\Delta_{k}^{(n)}

where
\Delta_{0}^{(n)}=1
and for
k\geq 1
,

(7.2)

{\Delta_{k}^{(n)}}=p_{k}\big(-\mathrm{tr}(A),\cdots,-\mathrm{tr}(A^{k})\big)

where
p_{k}
is a (multivariate) polynomial of degree
k
independent of
n
.
These polynomials arise by (formally) identifying the power series

(7.3)

\exp\bigg(\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\frac{z^{k}}{k}x_{k}\bigg)=1+\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}}p_{k}(x_{1},\cdots,x_{k})z^{k},\qquad(x_{1},x_{2},\cdots)\in\mathbb{C}^{\infty}.

In particular, they have the following property for every
k\in\mathbb{N}
;

p_{k}\big(zx_{1},\cdots,z^{k}x_{k}\big)=z^{k}p_{k}\big(x_{1},\cdots,x_{k}\big)\qquad\text{for $z\in\mathbb{C}$}

and

\big|p_{k}(x_{1},\cdots,x_{k})\big|\leq p_{k}(|x_{1}|,\cdots,|x_{k}|).

Hence, if
\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\frac{|x_{k}|}{k}r^{k}<\infty
for
r>0
, then both sums in (
7.3
) are absolutely convergent for
z\in\overline{D_{r}}
.

The underlying idea is that for
d\in\mathbb{N}
fixed, by formula (
7.2
), Theorem
3.2
(1) and the continuous mapping theorem, for every
k\in\mathbb{N}

\big(\Delta_{1}^{(n)},\dotsc,\Delta_{k}^{(n)}\big)\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{\mathrm{law}}\big(P_{1},\dotsc,P_{k}\big)

where

P_{k}=p_{k}\bigg(-\Lambda_{1},\dotsc,-\sum_{\ell|k}\ell\Lambda_{\ell}\bigg).

Moreover, by Proposition
3.1
, the characteristic polynomial
\{\chi_{n}\}
is tight in
D_{1/\sqrt{d}}
. Thus, by Lemma
7.1
,

\chi_{n}(z)\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{\mathrm{law}}1+\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}}P_{k}z^{k}

locally uniformly for
z\in D_{1/\sqrt{d}}
.
However, we cannot directly identify the limit from this arguments since the series
\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\frac{z^{k}}{k}\sum_{\ell|k}\ell\Lambda_{\ell}
does not converge for

(cf. Remark
6.1
). Hence, we give a modified version of this argument which also applies in the regime where
d(n)\to\infty
.

Proof of Theorem
2.2
.

Instead of the characteristic polynomial, we consider the function

f_{n}(z)=\frac{\widehat{\chi}_{n}(z)}{z-1/\sqrt{d}}\mathbb{E}e^{-Y_{d}(z)},\qquad z\in D_{1},n\in\mathbb{N}.

By Lemma
7.2
and since the rescaled characteristic polynomial has a trivial root at

.
Moreover, by Lemma

,
\mathbb{E}\|\widehat{\chi}_{n}\|_{L^{\infty}(D_{e^{-t}})}\leq C_{t}
for

so that by Cauchy’s formula,

\mathbb{E}\|f_{n}\|_{L^{\infty}(D_{e^{-2t}})}\leq\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{\mathbb{E}\big|\widehat{\chi}_{n}(e^{-t+i\theta})\mathbb{E}e^{-Y_{d}(e^{-t+i\theta})}\big|}{|e^{-t}-1/\sqrt{d}||e^{-t}-e^{-2t}|}\frac{d\theta}{2\pi}

which is bounded uniformly for

. This shows that

is tight.
We can expand

f_{n}(z)=1+\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}}a_{k}^{(n)}z^{k};\qquad a_{k}^{(n)}=p_{k}\bigg(-\frac{\mathrm{tr}(A)-\mathbb{E}\Lambda_{1}}{\sqrt{d}},\dots,-\frac{\mathrm{tr}(A^{k})-\sum_{\ell|k}\ell\mathbb{E}\Lambda_{\ell}}{\sqrt{d^{k}}}\bigg).

Note that since

is analytic, the series for
f_{n}
is convergent on
D_{1}
. This follows from (
7.3
) and the facts that

\widehat{\chi}_{n}(z)=\exp\bigg(-\sum_{k\geq 1}\frac{z^{k}}{kd^{k/2}}\mathrm{tr}(A^{k})\bigg)/\sqrt{d}

(the sum converges at least for

since we have the crude bound

because the entries of

are bounded by

)
and according to Lemma

,

\frac{\mathbb{E}e^{-Y_{d}(z)}}{1-z\sqrt{d}}=\exp\bigg(\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\frac{z^{k}}{kd^{k/2}}\sum_{\ell|k}\ell\,\mathbb{E}\Lambda_{\ell}\bigg),\qquad z\in D_{1/\sqrt{d}}.

Hence, for a fixed

, by Theorem
3.2
(1) and the continuous mapping theorem, it holds for every

\big(a_{1}^{(n)},\dotsc,a_{k}^{(n)}\big)\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{\mathrm{law}}\big(a_{1},\dotsc,a_{k}\big),

where

a_{k}=d^{-k/2}p_{k}\bigg(-\overline{\Lambda_{1}},\dots,-\sum_{\ell|k}\ell\overline{\Lambda_{\ell}}\bigg).

By Lemma

f_{n}(z)=\frac{\widehat{\chi}_{n}(z)}{z-1/\sqrt{d}}\mathbb{E}e^{-Y_{d}(z)}\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{\mathrm{law}}f(z)=1+\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}}a_{k}z^{k}.

Moreover, from the proof of Proposition

, it holds almost surely

∎

Proof of Theorem

.

This is a variant of the previous argument. We consider the function

8.
Proof of spectral gaps

In the statement of Theorem
1.1
, we recalled the main models for random regular digraphs: (i) is the sum-of-permutation model, (ii) is the sum-of-permutation model conditioned on being simple and (iii) is the uniform directed regular graph.
It was proved in
[
36
]
and
[
47
]
that the two models (ii) and (iii) are
contiguous
, which means if a sequence of events happens asymptotically almost surely in model, it holds for the other model as well.
For fixed
d
, our Theorem
2.2
can be applied to show a spectral gap result for the three models.

The proof is slightly different than
[
12
, Theorem 1.1]
and
[
21
, Theorem 2.6]
since we directly rely on the Hurwitz theorem as in
[
55
]
. We identify multisets with integer-valued Radon measures and endow the space of multisets with the topology of
vague convergence, and the space of random multisets with the topology of weak convergence with respect to the topology of vague convergence.

Proposition 8.1

(Proposition 2.3 in
[
55
]
)
.

Let
f_{n}
be a sequence of random functions in
D_{r}
converging in law to a function
f
that is almost surely not identically zero. Let
\Phi_{n}
and
\Phi
be the random multisets of the zeros of
f_{n},f
in
D_{r}
, respectively. Then
\Phi_{n}
converges in law towards
\Phi
.

Proof of Theorem
1.1
.

We first prove the statement for case (i): the sum of random permutations. Let
f(z)
be defined by the formula on the right hand side of Theorem
2.2
, which has a simple zero at
d^{-1/2}
. By Proposition
8.1
, with probability tending to 1,
\widehat{\chi}_{n}(z)
has only one zero at
1/\sqrt{d}
, which implies
A/\sqrt{d}
has no eigenvalue outside
D_{1/r}
except
\sqrt{d}
. For any
\varepsilon>0
, take
\frac{1}{1+\varepsilon/\sqrt{d}}<r<1
, then as
n\to\infty
,

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}(|\lambda_{2}|>\sqrt{d}+\varepsilon)\leq\mathbf{P}\left(\frac{|\lambda_{2}|}{\sqrt{d}}>\frac{1}{r}\right)\to 0.

It was shown in
[
47
]
that the probability that the digraph associated to
A
is simple is bounded from below when
d
is fixed. Therefore
|\lambda_{2}|>\sqrt{d}
holds asymptotically almost surely for case (ii). By contiguity between model (ii) and model (iii), it holds for uniform random
d
regular digraphs as well.
∎

When
d(n)\to\infty
, there is no contiguity result in the literature between the two models (ii) and (iii), and we are not aware of any result to provide a constant lower bound on the probability that
A=\sum_{i=1}^{d(n)}P^{(i)}
being simple. So our Theorem
2.4
only gives a result for sums of random permutations.

Proof of Theorem
1.2
.

In Theorem
2.4
,
\frac{\widehat{\chi}_{n}(z)}{z-1/\sqrt{d}}
converges in law to the limiting function
\sqrt{1-z^{2}}e^{X_{\infty}(z)}
, which has no root inside
D_{r}
for any
r\in(0,1)
. From Proposition
8.1
, with high probability
\frac{\widehat{\chi}_{n}(z)}{z-1/\sqrt{d}}
has no roots in
D_{r}
for any
0<r<1
. Then
A/\sqrt{d(n)}
has no eigenvalue outside
D_{1/r}
except for
\sqrt{d(n)}
. Therefore (
1.1
) holds.
∎

Appendix A
Appendix: a Poisson CLT

Let
\Lambda\sim\mathrm{Poisson}(\lambda)
.
Recall that the Laplace transform of
\Lambda
is

\mathbb{E}\exp(z(\Lambda-\lambda))=\exp\big(\lambda(e^{z}-1-z)\big),\qquad z\in\mathbb{C}.

Let
\mu_{k}(\lambda)=\mathbb{E}(\Lambda-\lambda)^{k}
be the central moments of
\Lambda
for
k\in\mathbb{N}
. We have for
k\in\mathbb{N}

\mu_{k}(\lambda)/k!=[z^{k}]\exp\big(\lambda(e^{z}-1-z)\big).

In particular,
\mu_{1}=0
and for
k\in\mathbb{N}
,

(A.1)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\mu_{2k}(\lambda)=m_{k}\lambda^{k}+p_{k}(\lambda),&p_{k}\in\mathcal{P}_{k-1}\\
\mu_{2k+1}\in\mathcal{P}_{k}\end{cases}

where
m_{k}=\tfrac{(2k)!}{2^{k}k!}
, and
\mathcal{P}_{k}=\{\text{polynomials of degree }\leq k\}
.
From this fact, we deduce that for every
k\in\mathbb{N}
,

(A.2)

\frac{\mu_{k}(\lambda)}{\lambda^{k/2}}=\mathbb{E}\bigg(\frac{\Lambda-\lambda}{\sqrt{\lambda}}\bigg)^{k}\xrightarrow[\lambda\to\infty]{}\mathbb{E}N^{k}=\begin{cases}m_{k/2}&k\text{ even}\\
0&k\text{ odd}\end{cases},

where
N
is a standard Gaussian.
This is a CLT in the sense of moments for a Poisson random variable.

The goal of this appendix is to extend this results to a collection of asymptotically Poisson random variables with large parameters.
First recall that we can expand for
k\in\mathbb{N}
,

(A.3)

(x-\lambda)^{k}=\textstyle{\sum_{j=0}^{k}}(x)_{k-j}q_{k,j}(\lambda);\qquad q_{k,j}\in\mathcal{P}_{j}.

Then, according to Lemma
5.10
, we have

(A.4)

\mu_{k}(\lambda)=\sum_{j\leq k}q_{k,j}(\lambda)\mathbb{E}(\Lambda)_{k-j}=\sum_{j\leq k}q_{k,j}(\lambda)\lambda^{k-j}.

Remarkably, from (
A.1
), the RHS is a polynomial in
\lambda
of degree
\leq k/2
.

Lemma A.1
.

Fix
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
and for
j\in[\ell]
, let
(\lambda_{j,n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence in
\mathbb{R}_{+}
such that
\lambda_{j,n}\to\infty
as
n\to\infty
.
Let
(Q_{j,n})_{j\in[\ell],n\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence of random vectors such that its joint factorial moments satisfy for any
\mathbf{k}\in\mathbb{N}^{\ell}
,

(A.5)

\mathbb{E}\big[(Q_{1,n})_{k_{1}}\cdots(Q_{\ell,n})_{k_{\ell}}\big]=\lambda_{1,n}^{k_{1}}\cdots\lambda_{\ell,n}^{k_{\ell}}(1+\epsilon_{\mathbf{k},n})

where
\epsilon_{\mathbf{k},n}\in\mathbb{R}
and for
\mathbf{k}\in\mathbb{N}^{\ell}
,

(A.6)

\lambda_{1,n}^{k_{1}/2}\cdots\lambda_{\ell,n}^{k_{\ell}/2}\cdot\max_{\mathbf{j}\leq\mathbf{k}}|\epsilon_{\mathbf{j},n}|\to 0.

Then

\left(\frac{Q_{1,n}-\lambda_{1,n}}{\sqrt{\lambda_{1,n}}},\dots,\frac{Q_{\ell,n}-\lambda_{\ell,n}}{\sqrt{\lambda_{\ell,n}}}\right)\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{\mathrm{law}}\big(N_{1},\dots,N_{\ell}\big)

and in the sense of moments, where
(N_{i})_{i\in[\ell]}
are i.i.d. standard Gaussians.

Proof.

Let us denote
\mu_{\mathbf{k},n}=\mathbb{E}\big[(Q_{1,n}-\lambda_{1,n})^{k_{1}}\cdots(Q_{\ell,n}-\lambda_{\ell,n})^{k_{\ell}}\big]
for
\mathbf{k}\in\mathbb{N}^{\ell}
.
Using (
A.3
) and (
A.5
), we can write

\displaystyle\mu_{\mathbf{k},n}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathbf{j}\leq\mathbf{k}}\prod_{i\leq\ell}q_{k_{i},j_{i}}(\lambda_{i,n})\mathbb{E}\big[(Q_{1,n})_{k_{1}-j_{1}}\cdots(Q_{\ell,n})_{k_{\ell}-j_{\ell}}\big]

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathbf{j}\leq\mathbf{k}}\prod_{i\leq\ell}q_{k_{i},j_{i}}(\lambda_{i,n})\lambda_{i,n}^{k_{i}-j_{i}}(1+\epsilon_{\mathbf{k-j},n}).

Then, the conditions (
A.6
) imply that for any fixed
\mathbf{k}\in\mathbb{N}^{\ell}
,

\mu_{\mathbf{k},n}=\prod_{i\leq\ell}\bigg(\sum_{j\leq k_{i}}q_{k_{i},j}(\lambda_{i,n})\lambda_{i,n}^{k_{i}-j}\bigg)+\underset{n\to\infty}{o_{\mathbf{k}}}\big(\lambda_{1,n}^{k_{1}/2}\cdots\lambda_{\ell,n}^{k_{\ell}/2}\big).

Here we used that
q_{k,j}\in\mathcal{P}_{j}
.
Hence using the combinatorial identity (
A.4
),

\mu_{k,n}=\mu_{k_{1}}(\lambda_{1,n})\cdots\mu_{k_{\ell}}(\lambda_{\ell,n})+\underset{n\to\infty}{o_{\mathbf{k}}}\big(\lambda_{1,n}^{k_{1}/2}\cdots\lambda_{\ell,n}^{k_{\ell}/2}\big).

From (
A.2
), we conclude that for every
\mathbf{k}\in\mathbb{N}^{\ell}
,

\frac{\mathbb{E}\big[(Q_{1,n}-\lambda_{1,n})^{k_{1}}\cdots(Q_{\ell,n}-\lambda_{\ell,n})^{k_{\ell}}\big]}{\lambda_{1,n}^{k_{1}/2}\cdots\lambda_{\ell,n}^{k_{\ell}/2}}\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}\mathbb{E}\big[N_{1}^{k_{1}}\cdots N_{\ell}^{k_{\ell}}\big].

This completes the proof, since the law of
(N_{i})_{i\in[\ell]}
is characterized by its joint moments.
∎

Appendix B
Appendix: sums of non-uniform random permutations

In this paper, we studied random digraphs arising as sums of
n\times n
uniform permutations. A crucial technical argument in our analysis was that the rows of the matrix
A=P^{(1)}+\dotsb+P^{(d)}
are exchangeable; but this property is in fact very strong. It would not hold if the permutation matrices
P^{(i)}
were not uniformly distributed on
S_{n}
. This is typically what happens when they are skewed towards having more or fewer short cycles. For example, the Ewens distribution with parameter
\theta
is defined as follows; we say that a random permutation
\pi
is
\mathrm{Ewens}(\theta)
-distributed if

\forall\sigma\in S_{n},\qquad\mathbb{P}(\pi=\sigma)=\frac{\theta^{\mathrm{cyc}(\sigma)}}{\theta(\theta+1)\dotsb(\theta+n-1)},

where
\mathrm{cyc}(\sigma)
is the number of cycles in the cycle decomposition of
\sigma
. When
\theta=1
, the random permutation
\pi
is uniform; when
\theta>1
(respectively,
<1
), it is skewed towards having more cycles (respectively, less), resulting in a directed graph with a different local structure. Crucially, Ewens-distributed random permutations with
\theta\neq 1
are invariant by
conjugation
by any permutation matrix, but not invariant by multiplication by a permutation matrix.

We were able to identify the asymptotics of the traces of
A=P^{(1)}+\dotsb+P^{(d)}
is this model, with
\theta
fixed and not depending on
n
; indeed, Theorem
3.2
still holds, with the limiting random variables
\Lambda_{\ell}
being replaced with the following ones:

\Theta_{\ell}\sim\mathrm{Poisson}\left(\frac{d^{\ell}+d(\theta-1)}{\ell}\right).

A proof is available on demand; however, the proof of the tightness of the sequence
\det(I-zA_{n})
is still under exploration. We mention this fact, since the asymptotic behaviour of the spectrum of
A
in the Ewens case displays some unusual features; when
\theta
is sufficiently large (this might depend on
n
), the whole limiting shape of the eigenvalues seems not rotation-invariant, with a previously unseen pattern of eigenvector localization — see Figure
2
. For comparison, Figure
3
displays the eigenvalues of our model of sums-of-permutation matrices, together with an example of a real Ginibre spectrum.

We note that the continuous counterpart of the Ewens measure on permutations, is a generalization of the Haar measure on the unitary group, which is called the Hua-Pickrell measure
[
32
,
52
]
, also known as the circular Jacobi ensembles
[
13
]
. It is also an interesting question to study the limiting spectral distribution for sums of independent unitary matrices sampled from the Hua-Pickrell distribution beyond the Haar case
[
7
]
.
