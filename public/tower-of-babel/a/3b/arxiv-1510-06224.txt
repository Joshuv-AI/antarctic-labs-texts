Internally Calabi–Yau algebras and cluster-tilting objects
Matthew Pressland

Abstract
. We describe what it means for an algebra to be internally d d -Calabi–Yau with respect to an idempotent. This definition abstracts properties of endomorphism algebras of ( d − 1 ) (d-1) -cluster-tilting objects in certain stably ( d − 1 ) (d-1) -Calabi–Yau Frobenius categories, as observed by Keller–Reiten. We show that an internally d d -Calabi–Yau algebra satisfying mild additional assumptions can be realised as the endomorphism algebra of a ( d − 1 ) (d-1) -cluster-tilting object in a Frobenius category. Moreover, if the algebra satisfies a stronger ‘bimodule’ internally d d -Calabi–Yau condition, this Frobenius category is stably ( d − 1 ) (d-1) -Calabi–Yau. We pay special attention to frozen Jacobian algebras; in particular, we define a candidate bimodule resolution for such an algebra, and show that if this complex is indeed a resolution, then the frozen Jacobian algebra is bimodule internally 3 3 -Calabi–Yau with respect to its frozen idempotent. These results suggest a new method for constructing Frobenius categories modelling cluster algebras with frozen variables, by first constructing a suitable candidate for the endomorphism algebra of a cluster-tilting object in such a category, analogous to Amiot’s construction in the coefficient-free case.

1.
Introduction

Cluster categories, first introduced in special cases by Buan–Marsh–Reineke–Reiten–Todorov
[
10
]
and later generalised by Amiot
[
3
]
, are certain
\Hom
-finite
2
-Calabi–Yau triangulated categories that model the combinatorics of cluster algebras without frozen variables. In particular, a cluster category
\mathcal{C}
contains cluster-tilting objects, which are objects

satisfying

\add{T}=\{X\in\mathcal{C}:\Ext^{1}_{\mathcal{C}}(T,X)=0\}=\{X\in\mathcal{C}:\Ext^{1}_{\mathcal{C}}(X,T)=0\}.

Basic cluster-tilting objects, whose summands in any direct sum decomposition are pairwise non-isomorphic, model the clusters of the cluster algebra; from now on, any time we refer to a cluster-tilting object, we assume it to be basic.

The main reason that the cluster-tilting objects of
\mathcal{C}
can be said to model the clusters of a cluster algebra is that, as with a cluster, it is possible to pass from one cluster-tilting object to another by a process of mutation. For any indecomposable summand
T_{i}
of a cluster-tilting object

, there exists a unique indecomposable

, not isomorphic to
T_{i}
, such that
T/T_{i}\mathbin{\oplus}T_{i}^{\prime}
is a cluster-tilting object. Moreover,

can be computed by either of the exchange triangles

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces X_{i}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces T_{i}^{\prime}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces T_{i}[1],}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces T_{i}^{\prime}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces Y_{i}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces T_{i}}

For a more thorough introduction to the theory of cluster algebras and their categorification, we recommend Keller’s survey
[
33
]
.

The categorification of cluster algebras by cluster categories has proved to be very useful in studying their combinatorics, since the cluster category
\mathcal{C}
can be considered more globally than the associated cluster algebra
\mathscr{A}(Q,\mathbf{x})
. For example, to identify clusters or cluster variables of
\mathscr{A}(Q,\mathbf{x})
, one usually has to find a sequence of mutations from a known cluster, which is a highly computationally intensive procedure. By contrast, cluster-tilting or rigid objects of
\mathcal{C}
are characterised intrinsically.

For this reason, it would be extremely useful to be able to more readily categorify cluster algebras that do have frozen variables, particularly as most of the examples occurring in nature, such as those on the coordinate rings of partial flag varieties and their unipotent cells, as studied by Geiß–Leclerc–Schröer
[
18
]
, are of this type. The natural candidate for such a categorification is a stably
2
-Calabi–Yau Frobenius category, as we now describe.

A Frobenius category is an exact category with enough projective and injective objects, such that these two classes of objects coincide. If
\mathcal{E}
is a Frobenius category, then the stable category
\underline{\mathcal{E}}=\mathcal{E}/\catproj{\mathcal{E}}
is triangulated by a famous result of Happel
[
23
]
*§I.2. It is immediate from the definition that if
T\in\mathcal{E}
is cluster-tilting, then
\catproj{\mathcal{E}}\subset\add{T}
. In this case, we must have
\catproj{\mathcal{E}}=\add{P}
for some object
P=\bigoplus_{i=r+1}^{n}T_{i}
; the intention is that the objects
T_{i}
for
r<i\leq n
, which occur as summands of every cluster-tilting object of
\mathcal{E}
, will correspond to the frozen variables of a cluster algebra. Factoring out
\catproj{\mathcal{E}}
corresponds to setting these frozen variables to
1
in the cluster algebra, to recover a cluster algebra without frozen variables. Thus the stable category
\underline{\mathcal{E}}
should be a cluster category—in particular it should be
2
-Calabi–Yau.

Such categorifications have been described for certain cluster algebras relating to partial flag varieties, for example by Geiss–Leclerc–Schröer
[
18
]
, Jensen–King–Su
[
28
]
and Demonet–Iyama
[
14
]
, and we will recall some of these constructions in Section
3
. Nájera Chávez
[
35
]
has also categorified finite type cluster algebras with ‘universal’ coefficients. However, in all cases, the construction of the category depends on having at least a partial understanding of the overall structure of the cluster algebra. Thus the methods of these papers cannot be easily abstracted to produce categorifications for more general cluster algebras with frozen variables.

The main aim of this paper is to consider how one might be able to produce a categorification of a cluster algebra with frozen variables without understanding this global structure, instead starting only from the data of a single seed, which is how a cluster algebra is usually specified. This is analogous to Amiot’s construction of cluster categories in the case that there are no frozen variables; given a seed, one has to find a rigid potential on the quiver of the seed such that the resulting Jacobian algebra (see
Section

5
) is finite dimensional, and then Amiot provides a general recipe for constructing a categorification of the cluster algebra. Our construction in the case that there are frozen variables is similar, but requires more data satisfying more conditions. Given the seed of a cluster algebra with frozen variables, we take its quiver, and aim to add arrows between frozen variables and choose a potential such that the resulting frozen Jacobian algebra satisfies a number of conditions, most importantly that of being bimodule internally
3
-Calabi–Yau. If this can be achieved, the general machinery developed in this paper can take over to produce the desired Frobenius category, from which the frozen Jacobian algebra can be recovered as the endomorphism algebra of a cluster-tilting object.

For most of the paper, we will in fact work in a higher level of generality, and construct stably
d
-Calabi–Yau Frobenius categories admitting
d
-cluster-tilting objects (
3.2
); setting
d=2
recovers the definition of cluster-tilting given above.

If

\RHom_{A}(C\mathbin{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathbf{L}}}{{\otimes}}}_{A}N,M)=\RHom_{A}(N,\RHom_{A}(C,M))=0.

Thus
\RHom_{A}(\Omega_{A}\mathbin{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathbf{L}}}{{\otimes}}}_{A}N,M)\cong\RHom_{A}(N[-d],M)
. We obtain the desired result by taking
0
-th cohomology and applying
2.7
.
∎

Corollary 2.9
.

If
A
is bimodule internally
d
-Calabi–Yau with respect to
e
, then it is internally
d
-Calabi–Yau with respect to
e
, and moreover we have a functorial isomorphism

\mathrm{D}\Ext_{A}^{i}(M,N)=\Ext_{A}^{d-i}(N,M)

for any finite dimensional
M\in\fgmod{A/\langle e\rangle}
and any
N\in\Mod{A}
, which need not be perfect as
A
-modules.

Proof.

Since
\pdim_{{A}^{\varepsilon}}{A}\leq d
, there is an exact sequence

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces 0}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces P_{d}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\dotsb}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces P_{1}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces P_{0}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces A}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces 0}

of
A
-bimodules, in which each
P_{i}
is a projective
A
-bimodule. If
X
is any
A
-module, then
P_{i}\mathbin{\otimes}_{A}X
is a projective
A
-module, and so applying
-\mathbin{\otimes}_{A}X
to the above sequence gives a projective resolution

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces 0}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces P_{d}\mathbin{\otimes}_{A}X}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\dotsb}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces P_{1}\mathbin{\otimes}_{A}X}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces P_{0}\mathbin{\otimes}_{A}X}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces X}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces 0}

of
X
. It follows that
\gldim{A}\leq d
.

Now by
2.8
, if
N\in\Mod{A}
and
M\in\fgmod{A/\langle e\rangle}
is finite dimensional, we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{D}\Ext^{i}_{A}(M,N)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{D}\Hom_{\mathcal{D}{A}}(M,N[i])

\displaystyle=\Hom_{\mathcal{D}{A}}(N[i-d],M)

\displaystyle=\Ext^{d-i}_{A}(N,M).

In particular, this duality applies when
M
and
N
are perfect
A
-modules, so
A
is internally
d
-Calabi–Yau with respect to
e
.
∎

While
2.8
and
2.9
show that bimodule internally Calabi–Yau algebras have stronger properties than those required by the definition of internally Calabi–Yau, with the duality formula applying to a wider class of objects, we do not currently know of any examples of internally Calabi–Yau algebras which are not bimodule internally Calabi–Yau. It seems unlikely that the two classes of algebras coincide, but it is tempting to speculate based on work of Bocklandt
[
6
]
that if
A
is an internally Calabi–Yau algebra admitting a suitable grading, then it is also bimodule internally Calabi–Yau.

3.
Frobenius
m
-cluster categories

This section is devoted to describing a class of categories, which we term Frobenius
m
-cluster categories, providing us with a rich source of internally
(m+1)
-Calabi–Yau algebras. Indeed, certain categories in this class motivated
2.1
.

Recall that an exact category
\mathcal{E}
is Frobenius if it has enough projective objects and enough injective objects, and
\catproj{\mathcal{E}}=\catinj{\mathcal{E}}
. By a famous result of Happel
[
23
]
*§I.2, the stable category
\underline{\mathcal{E}}=\mathcal{E}/\catproj{\mathcal{E}}
is triangulated, with suspension given by the inverse syzygy
\Omega^{-1}
, taking an object to the cokernel of an injective hull.

Definition 3.1
.

Let
\mathcal{E}
be a Frobenius category, and let
m\geq 1
. We say that
\mathcal{E}
is
stably
m
-Calabi–Yau
if its stable category
\underline{\mathcal{E}}
is
m
-Calabi–Yau, meaning that
\underline{\mathcal{E}}
is
\Hom
-finite, and there is a functorial duality

\mathrm{D}\stabHom_{\mathcal{E}}(X,Y)=\stabHom_{\mathcal{E}}(Y,\Omega^{-m}X)

for all
X,Y\in\mathcal{E}
. Here
\stabHom_{\mathcal{E}}(X,Y)
denotes the space of morphisms
X
to
Y
in

A well-known result of Herzog

*Prop. 13.22, one can see that the Auslander–Reiten translation on

is stably
1
-Calabi–Yau. Let

is the unique (up to isomorphism) basic

-cluster-tilting object of

. By Auslander’s Theorem

\displaystyle\End_{R}(T)^{\mathrm{op}}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\sim}}{{\to}}\Pi(\widetilde{\Delta}),

\displaystyle\stabEnd_{R}(T)^{\mathrm{op}}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\sim}}{{\to}}\Pi(\Delta),

where

The algebra

.

4.
From internally

-Calabi–Yau algebras to
d
-cluster-tilting objects

3.4

*Thm. 2.3 on bimodule

-Calabi–Yau algebras. Since we will work only with Noetherian algebras, by

Theorem 4.1

(cf.
[
1
]
*Thm. 2.3)
.

Let

be a Noetherian algebra and let

be an idempotent such that

is a Frobenius category,

(ii)

\End_{\stabGP(B)}(eA)^{\mathrm{op}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\sim}}{{\to}}\underline{A}

Remark 4.2
.

While all of the conclusions of

Since the assumptions of

and

\GP(B^{\mathrm{op}})
is a Frobenius category in which

is a
(d-1)
-cluster-tilting object, and there are natural isomorphisms
\End_{B^{\mathrm{op}}}(Ae)^{\mathrm{op}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\sim}}{{\to}}A^{\mathrm{op}}
and
\End_{\stabGP(B^{\mathrm{op}})}(Ae)^{\mathrm{op}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\sim}}{{\to}}\underline{A}^{\mathrm{op}}

We emphasise two cases in which the assumptions of

may be made to appear one-sided. Firstly, as in

is a finite dimensional algebra then it is internally

-Calabi–Yau with respect to

is bimodule internally

-Calabi–Yau with respect to

) so is

are internally

-Calabi–Yau with respect to

We note that Amiot–Iyama–Reiten’s result
[

]
*Thm. 2.3 is a special case of our

The rest of the section is largely devoted to proving

,

,

and

be as in the assumptions of this theorem. We begin with the following straightforward observation.

Proposition 4.3
.

The algebra
B
is Noetherian.

Proof.

Any left ideal

of

is of the form

for a left ideal
\widetilde{I}=AI
of
A
. So any ascending chain of left ideals of

determines and is determined by such a chain of ideals of

, which stabilises as

is Noetherian. A similar argument shows that

is right Noetherian.
∎

Proposition 4.4

(cf.
[
1
]
*Lem. 2.6)
.

For any
X\in\fgmod{\underline{A}}
, we have

(i)

\Ext^{i}_{A}(X,A)=0
for

, and

(ii)

\Ext^{i}_{A}(X,Ae)=0
for any

.

Proof.

Both

and

are finitely generated projective

-modules, and so are in particular perfect. Since

, and therefore

, is finite dimensional,

is a finitely generated

-module, and thus perfect since

is Noetherian of finite global dimension. Now we can use the internal Calabi–Yau duality of

to deduce that

\Ext^{i}_{A}(X,A)=\mathrm{D}{\Ext^{d-i}_{A}(A,X)}=0

and

\Ext^{i}_{A}(X,Ae)=\mathrm{D}\Ext^{d-i}_{A}(Ae,X)=0

for

, since

and
Ae
are projective. Since

, we have

, and so

\Ext^{d}_{A}(X,Ae)=\mathrm{D}{\Hom_{A}(Ae,X)}=\mathrm{D}(eX)=0.\qed

The assumption of part (i) of

is slightly more restrictive than that of
[
1
]
*Lem. 2.6(a). This is necessary for the result to hold in our setting, since our

is only internally

-Calabi–Yau. However, this stronger assumption is satisfied whenever
[

]
*Lem. 2.6(a) is used in the proof of
[

]

The following results (

,

,

Proposition 4.5

(cf.
[
1
]
*Prop. 2.7)
.

We have isomorphisms

\Ext^{i}_{B}(eA,B)\cong\begin{cases}Ae,&i=0,\\
0,&i\neq 0\end{cases}

of

-modules, and isomorphisms

\Ext^{i}_{B}(eA,eA)\cong\begin{cases}A^{\mathrm{op}},&i=0,\\
0,&0<i<d-1,\end{cases}

of vector spaces, the isomorphism in case

being additionally an isomorphism of algebras.

Proof.

We can compute
\Ext^{i}_{B}(eA,B)
as the cohomology of

\RHom_{B}(eA,B)\cong\RHom_{B}(eA,\RHom_{A}(Ae,Ae))\cong\RHom_{A}(Ae\mathbin{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathbf{L}}}{{\otimes}}}_{B}eA,Ae),

and wish to show that this is isomorphic to the cohomology of
\RHom_{A}(A,Ae)
. To do this, we show that

\RHom_{A}(Ae\mathbin{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathbf{L}}}{{\otimes}}}_{B}eA,Ae)\cong\RHom_{A}(A,Ae).

Let
f
be the composition of the natural map

Ae\mathbin{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathbf{L}}}{{\otimes}}}_{B}eA\to\mathrm{H}^{0}(Ae\mathbin{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathbf{L}}}{{\otimes}}}_{B}eA)=Ae\mathbin{\otimes}_{B}eA

with the multiplication map
Ae\mathbin{\otimes}_{B}eA\to A
, and let
X
be the mapping cone of

, so we have a triangle

(1)

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces Ae\mathbin{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathbf{L}}}{{\otimes}}}_{B}eA}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces A}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces X}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces Ae\mathbin{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathbf{L}}}{{\otimes}}}_{B}eA[1]}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces f}

in
\mathcal{D}{A}
. The map
eA\mathbin{\otimes}_{A}f
is the natural isomorphism
B\mathbin{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathbf{L}}}{{\otimes}}}_{B}eA\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\sim}}{{\to}}eA
, so
eA\mathbin{\otimes}_{A}X=0
. It follows that

, and hence
\mathrm{H}^{i}(X)\in\fgmod{\underline{A}}
for all

. Thus, by
4.4
,
\Ext^{j}_{A}(\mathrm{H}^{i}(X),Ae)=0
for all

.

We can compute

via a hypercohomology spectral sequence
{}^{II}E^{ij}_{r}

[
40
]
*§5.7.9, see also Defn. 5.6.2, in which

{}^{II}E^{ij}_{2}=\Ext^{j}_{A}(\mathrm{H}^{i}(X),Ae)=0

as above. It follows that
\mathrm{H}^{k}(\RHom_{A}(X,Ae))=0
for all

, and so
\RHom_{A}(X,Ae)=0
. Now applying

to the triangle (

) yields the required isomorphism

\RHom_{A}(Ae\mathbin{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathbf{L}}}{{\otimes}}}_{B}eA,Ae)\cong\RHom_{A}(A,Ae)

in
\mathcal{D}{A\mathbin{\otimes}_{\mathbb{K}}B^{\mathrm{op}}}
, from which the first assertion follows by our initial calculations.

Similarly, we have isomorphisms

\RHom_{B}(eA,eA)\cong\RHom_{B}(eA,\RHom_{A}(Ae,A))\cong\RHom_{A}(Ae\mathbin{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathbf{L}}}{{\otimes}}}_{B}eA,A),

and so to obtain the second assertion we wish to show that

\RHom_{A}(Ae\mathbin{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathbf{L}}}{{\otimes}}}_{B}eA,A)\cong\RHom_{A}(A,A).

Both
Ae
and

are concentrated in degree

, so by triangle (

) we have
\mathrm{H}^{i}(Ae\mathbin{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathbf{L}}}{{\otimes}}}_{B}eA)=0
for

, and so
\mathrm{H}^{i}(X)=0
for

. Recalling that

, it follows from

that
\Ext^{j}_{A}(\mathrm{H}^{i}(X),A)=\Hom_{\mathcal{D}{A}}(X,A[i])=0
for

. By an analogous spectral sequence argument to above,
\mathrm{H}^{i}(\RHom_{A}(X,A))=0
for

.

From (

), we obtain the long exact sequence

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\cdots}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\Hom_{\mathcal{D}{A}}(X,A[i])}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\Hom_{\mathcal{D}{A}}(A,A[i])}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\Hom_{\mathcal{D}{A}}(Ae\mathbin{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathbf{L}}}{{\otimes}}}_{B}eA,A[i])}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\cdots}

As

for

as above, it follows from our initial calculations that

\Ext^{i}_{B}(eA,eA)\cong\Hom_{\mathcal{D}{A}}(Ae\mathbin{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathbf{L}}}{{\otimes}}}_{B}eA,A[i])\cong\Hom_{\mathcal{D}{A}}(A,A[i])\cong\begin{cases}A^{\mathrm{op}},&i=0,\\

as required.
∎

Lemma 4.6

(cf.
[

]
*Lem. 2.9)
.

For any

Since

,

has a projective presentation

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces 0}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\Hom_{B}(X,eA)}

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\Hom_{B}(P_{1},eA)}

of

-modules. Since

-module, the above sequence shows that

(cf.
[

]

in which each

Lemma 4.9

(cf.
[

]

.

If

The quasi-inverse dualities

.

(i)

We have already shown in

is Noetherian, so it remains to show that

. Given such an

of

denotes the complex obtained by applying

Since

\displaystyle=\Hom_{A}(\mathbf{P},\Hom_{B}(eA,B))\cong\Hom_{A}(\mathbf{P},Ae),

]
*§4.8.

\displaystyle=\End_{B}(eA)^{\mathrm{op}}/\langle\add{{}_{B}B}\rangle

where

-Calabi–Yau. We now show, using descriptions by Kalck–Yang

By

.

*Proof of Cor. 2.12, the functor

, which restricts to a triangle equivalence
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{fd}}(C)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\sim}}{{\to}}\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{fd},\underline{A}}(A)
. Thus for any

and

, we have functorial isomorphisms

the second coming from
2.8
, using the assumption that

is bimodule internally

-Calabi–Yau with respect to

. Thus

*§5.1, and so it follows from
[

-Calabi–Yau.

We complete the proof by showing that
\stabGP(B)
is equivalent to a full triangulated subcategory of
\per{C}/\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{fd}}(C)
, and so is also
(d-1)
-Calabi–Yau. Since
\gldim{A}\leq d
,

]
*Cor. 2.12a tells us that

provides a triangle equivalence between the idempotent completion of

, denoted by

\per{C}
. Moreover,
[

]
*Proof of Cor. 2.12 shows that
i_{*}=\RHom_{C}(C,-)
induces a triangle equivalence
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{fd}}(C)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\sim}}{{\to}}\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{fd},\underline{A}}(A)
, and that the codomain of this equivalence coincides with
\thick(\fgmod{\underline{A}})
. Since

, we see that

restricts to an equivalence
\thick(\fgmod{\underline{A}})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\sim}}{{\to}}\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{fd}}(C)

\frac{(\mathcal{D}^{b}{A}/\thick{Ae})^{\omega}}{q(\thick(\fgmod{\underline{A}}))}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\sim}}{{\to}}\frac{\per{C}}{\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{fd}}(C)},

where

by the above observations. We also have equivalences

\frac{\mathcal{D}^{b}{A}/\thick{Ae}}{q(\thick(\fgmod{\underline{A}}))}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\sim}}{{\to}}\mathcal{D}^{b}{B}/\per{B}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\sim}}{{\to}}\stabGP(B),

*Thm. 4.4.1. Since

with a full triangulated subcategory of the

-Calabi–Yau triangulated category

, from which it follows that

is itself

-Calabi–Yau.
∎

It was necessary in the proof of

-Calabi–Yau might hold only under the weaker assumptions of Theorem

Under the assumptions of

is in fact a Frobenius

-cluster category in the sense of Definition

-cluster-tilting object

is Noetherian, we can apply

Remark 4.11
.

As indicated in the introduction,
4.1
and
4.10
are motivated by the problem of constructing Frobenius categories modelling cluster algebras. Given the seed of a cluster algebra with frozen variables, we can look for an algebra
A
with the same quiver (up to the addition of arrows between frozen vertices), satisfying the conditions of
4.10
for
d=3
, and then apply this theorem to obtain the Frobenius category
\GP(B)
. Since constructing such an
A
can be very difficult, we wish to comment on the degree to which the conditions we are imposing are necessary.

Firstly, we consider it likely that the condition that
A
is Noetherian can be dropped, up to finding an appropriate replacement for the category
\GP(B)
. While
B
may not be Noetherian if
A
fails to be, it will still have injective dimension at most
d
on each side, so there should be a ‘good’ theory of Gorenstein projective modules over
B
. For our methods to work, we would need the analogues of
[
1
]
*Prop. 1.3 to hold in this setting. We would also hope for a more general version of the Iyama–Kalck–Wemyss–Yang equivalence stated here as
3.9
, without the Noetherianity assumption, which would then apply to arbitrary Frobenius
m
-cluster categories, and Buchweitz’s description of the stable category
[
11
]
*Thm. 4.4.1.

The other conditions are more essential; if
A=\End_{\mathcal{E}}(T)^{\mathrm{op}}
for
T
a cluster-tilting object in a Frobenius cluster category
\mathcal{E}
, and
e
is the idempotent given by projecting onto a maximal projective summand of
T
, then
A/\langle e\rangle
must be finite dimensional since
\underline{\mathcal{E}}
is Hom-finite, and
A
is internally
3
-Calabi–Yau with respect to
e
by
3.4
. On the other hand, it may not be necessary for
A
to be bimodule internally
3
-Calabi–Yau.

5.
A bimodule complex for frozen Jacobian algebras

Given a Frobenius cluster category
\mathcal{E}
and a cluster-tilting object
T\in\mathcal{E}
, it is often the case that
A=\End_{\mathcal{E}}(T)^{\mathrm{op}}
takes the form of a frozen Jacobian algebra (see
5.1
below). Indeed, this is the case for at least some cluster-tilting objects in the families of Frobenius cluster categories we described in
3.11
and
3.12
; see
[
9
]
*Thm. 6.6,
[
5
]
*Thm. 10.3. Thus these algebras, which also come with a preferred ‘frozen’ idempotent, are ideal candidates for constructing stably
2
-Calabi–Yau Frobenius categories via the methods of
4.1
and
4.10
. Moreover,
3
-Calabi–Yau properties of ordinary Jacobian algebras have been widely studied, for example by Bocklandt
[
6
]
and, in the context of dimer models on closed surfaces, by Broomhead
[
7
]
.

With this in mind, the main result of this section,
5.6
, shows that a frozen Jacobian algebra admitting a particular bimodule resolution (analogous to one defined by Ginzburg
[
20
]
*5.1.5) is bimodule internally
3
-Calabi–Yau with respect to its frozen idempotent.

Definition 5.1

(cf.
[
9
]
*Defn. 1.1,
[
15
]
*§2.1,
[
17
]
*§6.1)
.

An
ice quiver

(Q,F)
consists of a finite quiver
Q
without loops and a (not necessarily full) subquiver
F
of
Q
. Denote by
\widehat{\mathbb{K}Q}
the completion of the path algebra of

over
\mathbb{K}
with respect to the arrow ideal. A
potential

Q

is called

if it is a vertex or arrow of

, and
mutable
or
unfrozen
otherwise. For brevity, we write
Q_{0}^{\mathrm{m}}=Q_{0}\setminus F_{0}
and
Q_{1}^{\mathrm{m}}=Q_{1}\setminus F_{1}
for the sets of mutable vertices and unfrozen arrows respectively. For
\alpha\in Q_{1}
and
\alpha_{n}\dotsm\alpha_{1}
a cycle in

, write

\partial_{\alpha}{\alpha_{n}\dotsm\alpha_{1}}=\sum_{\alpha_{i}=\alpha}\alpha_{i-1}\dotsm\alpha_{1}\alpha_{n}\dotsm\alpha_{i+1}

\langle\partial_{\alpha}{W}:\alpha\in Q_{1}^{\mathrm{m}}\rangle
of
\widehat{\mathbb{K}Q}
is called the
Jacobian ideal
, and we may take its closure
\overline{\langle\partial_{\alpha}{W}:\alpha\in Q_{1}^{\mathrm{m}}\rangle}
since
\widehat{\mathbb{K}Q}
is a topological algebra. We define the
frozen Jacobian algebra
associated to
(Q,F,W)
by

\mathcal{J}(Q,F,W)=\widehat{\mathbb{K}Q}/\overline{\langle\partial_{\alpha}{W}:\alpha\in Q_{1}^{\mathrm{m}}\rangle}.

Write
A=\mathcal{J}(Q,F,W)
. The above presentation of
A
suggests a preferred idempotent
e=\sum_{v\in F_{0}}e_{v}
, which we call the
frozen idempotent
. We will call
B=eAe
the
boundary algebra
of
A
.

Remark 5.2

\bar{\mu}_{1}(x\mathbin{\otimes}\alpha\mathbin{\otimes}y)=x\mathbin{\otimes}\alpha y-x\alpha\mathbin{\otimes}y.

of

A\mathbin{\otimes}\mathbb{K}Q_{3}^{\mathrm{m}}\mathbin{\otimes}M=0
, and
\pdim_{A}M\leq 2
.

It follows from
5.6
that if
A
is a frozen Jacobian algebra with the property that
\mu_{0}\colon\mathbf{P}(A)\to A
is a quasi-isomorphism, then both
A
and
A^{\mathrm{op}}
are internally
3
-Calabi–Yau with respect to the frozen idempotent
e
. If additionally
A
is Noetherian and
A/\langle e\rangle
is finite dimensional, then we may apply
4.1
. We may also make some observations about the homological algebra of the boundary algebra
B
in this situation.

Proposition 5.8

(cf.
[
1
]
*Rem. 2.8)
.

Let
A
be a Noetherian frozen Jacobian algebra with frozen idempotent
e
such that
\mu_{0}\colon\mathbf{P}(A)\to A
is a quasi-isomorphism and
A/\langle e\rangle
is finite dimensional, and let
B=eAe
. Let
\Omega_{B}=\RHom_{{B}^{\varepsilon}}(B,{B}^{\varepsilon})
. Then
\Omega_{B}\cong e\Omega_{A}e
in
\mathcal{D}^{b}{{B}^{\varepsilon}}
.

Proof.

Write
P_{i}=A\mathbin{\otimes}\mathbb{K}Q_{i}\mathbin{\otimes}A
for
i=0,1
and
P_{i}=A\mathbin{\otimes}\mathbb{K}Q_{i}^{\mathrm{m}}\mathbin{\otimes}A
for
i=2,3
. By
5.6
,
A
and
A^{\mathrm{op}}
are internally
3
-Calabi–Yau with respect to
e
, so we have
\Ext^{i}_{B}(eA,B)=0=\Ext^{i}_{B^{\mathrm{op}}}(Ae,B)
for all
i>0
by
4.5
. Thus we may calculate

\displaystyle\RHom_{{B}^{\varepsilon}}(eA\mathbin{\otimes}_{\mathbb{K}}Ae,{B}^{\varepsilon})

\displaystyle=\RHom_{B}(eA,B)\mathbin{\otimes}_{\mathbb{K}}\RHom_{B^{\mathrm{op}}}(Ae,B)

\displaystyle=\Hom_{B}(eA,B)\mathbin{\otimes}_{\mathbb{K}}\Hom_{B^{\mathrm{op}}}(Ae,B)

\displaystyle=\Hom_{{B}^{\varepsilon}}(eA\mathbin{\otimes}_{\mathbb{K}}Ae,{B}^{\varepsilon}).

It follows that the terms
eP_{i}e
of the sequence
e\mathbf{P}(A)e\cong B
satisfy
\Ext^{i}_{{B}^{\varepsilon}}(eP_{i}e,{B}^{\varepsilon})=0
for
i>0
, and so

\RHom_{{B}^{\varepsilon}}(B,{B}^{\varepsilon})\cong\Hom_{{B}^{\varepsilon}}(e\mathbf{P}(A)e,{B}^{\varepsilon}).

By
4.1
(iii), the functor
eA\mathbin{\otimes}_{A}-\mathbin{\otimes}_{A}Ae\colon\catproj{{A}^{\varepsilon}}\to\fgmod{{B}^{\varepsilon}}
is fully faithful, and so

\Hom_{{B}^{\varepsilon}}(eP_{i}e,{B}^{\varepsilon})\cong\Hom_{{A}^{\varepsilon}}(P_{i},Ae\mathbin{\otimes}_{\mathbb{K}}eA)=e\Hom_{{A}^{\varepsilon}}(P_{i},{A}^{\varepsilon})e.

It follows that

\displaystyle\Omega_{B}

\displaystyle=\RHom_{{B}^{\varepsilon}}(B,{B}^{\varepsilon})\cong\Hom_{{B}^{\varepsilon}}(e\mathbf{P}(A)e,{B}^{\varepsilon})

\displaystyle=e\Hom_{{A}^{\varepsilon}}(\mathbf{P}(A),{B}^{\varepsilon})e\cong e\RHom_{{A}^{\varepsilon}}(A,{A}^{\varepsilon})e=e\Omega_{A}e.\qed

Proposition 5.9
.

With the notation and assumptions of
5.8
, for any
X\in\mathcal{D}^{b}{B}
we have

\Omega_{B}\mathbin{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathbf{L}}}{{\otimes}}}_{B}X\cong X[-3]

in the quotient category
\mathcal{D}^{b}{B}/\per{B}\simeq\stabGP(B)
.

Proof.

The proof of
5.6
constructs a map
A\to\Omega_{A}[3]
with mapping cone

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces C=A\mathbin{\otimes}\mathbb{K}F_{3}\mathbin{\otimes}A}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces A\mathbin{\otimes}(\mathbb{K}F_{2}\mathbin{\oplus}\mathbb{K}F_{1})\mathbin{\otimes}A}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces A\mathbin{\otimes}\mathbb{K}F_{0}\mathbin{\otimes}A.}

Since each
S
-bimodule
\mathbb{K}F_{i}
has the property that
e(\mathbb{K}F_{i})e=\mathbb{K}F_{i}
, we can instead write
C
as

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces Ae\mathbin{\otimes}\mathbb{K}F_{3}\mathbin{\otimes}eA}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces Ae\mathbin{\otimes}(\mathbb{K}F_{2}\mathbin{\oplus}\mathbb{K}F_{1})\mathbin{\otimes}eA}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces Ae\mathbin{\otimes}\mathbb{K}F_{0}\mathbin{\otimes}eA.}

Now applying the functor
eA\mathbin{\otimes}_{A}-\mathbin{\otimes}_{A}Ae
to the triangle
A\to\Omega_{A}[3]\to C\to A[1]
in
\per{{A}^{\varepsilon}}
yields the triangle

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces B}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces e\Omega_{A}e[3]}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces eCe}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces B[1]}

in
\mathcal{D}^{b}{{B}^{\varepsilon}}
. We have

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces eCe=B\mathbin{\otimes}\mathbb{K}F_{3}\mathbin{\otimes}B}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces B\mathbin{\otimes}(\mathbb{K}F_{2}\mathbin{\oplus}\mathbb{K}F_{1})\mathbin{\otimes}B}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces B\mathbin{\otimes}\mathbb{K}F_{0}\mathbin{\otimes}B\in\per{{B}^{\varepsilon}},}

and
e\Omega_{A}e\cong\Omega_{B}
by
5.8
. So applying
-\mathbin{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathbf{L}}}{{\otimes}}}_{B}X
to the above triangle yields the triangle

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces X}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\Omega_{B}\mathbin{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathbf{L}}}{{\otimes}}}_{B}X[3]}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces eCe\mathbin{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathbf{L}}}{{\otimes}}}_{B}X}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces X[1]}

in
\mathcal{D}^{b}{B}
. Since
eCe\in\per{{B}^{\varepsilon}}
, we have
eCe\mathbin{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathbf{L}}}{{\otimes}}}_{B}X\in\per{B}
, and so
\Omega_{B}\mathbin{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathbf{L}}}{{\otimes}}}_{B}X\cong X[-3]
in the quotient
\mathcal{D}^{b}{B}/\per{B}
, by the above triangle. The algebra
B
is Iwanaga–Gorenstein by
4.1
(a), so
\mathcal{D}^{b}{B}/\per{B}\simeq\stabGP(B)
by
[
11
]
*Thm. 4.4.1.
∎
