Enrichment of the nonconforming virtual element method with singular functions
E. Artioli, L. Mascotto

Abstract
We construct a nonconforming virtual element method (ncVEM) based on approximation spaces that are enriched with special singular functions. This enriched ncVEM is tailored for the approximation of solutions to elliptic problems, which have singularities due to the geometry of the domain. Differently from the traditional extended Galerkin method approach, based on the enrichment of local spaces with singular functions, no partition of unity is employed. Rather, the design of the method hinges upon the special structure of the nonconforming virtual element spaces. We discuss the theoretical analysis of the method and support it with several numerical experiments. We also present an orthonormalization procedure drastically trimming the ill-conditioning of the final system. AMS subject classification : 65N12, 65N15, 65N30 Keywords : virtual element method, extended Galerkin method, singular function, enrichment, optimal convergence, polygonal mesh

Introduction

The virtual element method (VEM) is a recent generalization of the finite element method (FEM) to very general polygonal/polyhedral meshes; see
[
12
]
and
[
5
]
,
as for the original references of conforming and nonconforming VEM for elliptic problems in primal formulation, respectively.
In this paper, we shall design a modification of the nonconforming VEM.
Differently from several other polytopal methods, virtual element spaces are designed to mimic properties of the solution to the problem under consideration.
This is very much in the spirit of Trefftz methods and renders the VEM extremely similar to the boundary element method-based FEM
[
42
]
.
Even on standard triangular and tetrahedral meshes, new elements can be constructed.

The design of special virtual element spaces mimicking the continuous problem has been exploited in various occasions.
Amongst them, we mention the approximation of solutions to
the Stokes equation
[
17
]
with divergence free spaces;
polyharmonic problems
[
25
,
1
]
with polyharmonic virtual element spaces;
problems with zero right-hand side tackled with the Trefftz VEM
[
34
]
;
elasticity problems
[
3
,
28
]
with symmetric stresses inserted in the virtual element spaces;
see also
[
11
,
2
,
43
]
.

In this paper, we construct special virtual element spaces for the approximation of solutions to elliptic problems, which have singularities due to the geometry of the domain.
Our approach falls within the broad family of extended Galerkin methods based on the enrichment of local spaces with singular functions, such as the extended finite element method (XFEM), see, e.g.,
[
37
,
36
]
,
and the generalized finite element method (GFEM); see, e.g.,
[
41
]
.
These methods work as follows. Consider an elliptic problem on a polygonal domain with smooth data.
The solution to this problem has an a priori known singular behaviour at the vertices of the domain; see, e.g.,
[
32
]
and the references therein.
For this reason, a standard FEM converges to the exact solution suboptimally.
In order to cope with this suboptimality, in the extended Galerkin methods, the approximation space is enriched with special singular functions.
A partition of unity is employed in order to patch the local approximation spaces seamlessly.
This enrichment permits to recover an optimal convergence rate of the error of the method; see also
[
35
]
.

In
[
18
]
, the extended finite element setting of
[
37
]
is translated into the virtual element one:
local spaces consisting of polynomials plus singular functions are patched with the aid of a virtual partition of unity, in the spirit of
[
38
]
.

Our approach is different and exploits the structure of virtual element spaces.
Instead of inserting the singular functions in the approximation spaces explicitly and patching the local spaces with a partition of unity, we proceed as follows.
The singular functions, which are typically added to the approximation spaces in the extended Galerkin methods, belong to the kernel of the differential operator appearing in the problem under consideration.
Such singular functions can be inserted into local virtual element spaces, using the fact that they are defined as solutions to local problems with data in the finite dimensional spaces.
By suitably tuning the boundary conditions in such local spaces, we include the singular functions implicitly.
Eventually, the local spaces are patched in a nonconforming fashion.

A first advantage of our approach resides in the flexibility of using polygonal meshes.
On the other hand, the analysis and the implementation of the method hinge upon a minor modification of what is done in the nonenriched nonconforming VEM; see, e.g.,
[
5
]
.
Furthermore, the structure of nonconforming spaces allows for the use of techniques suited to damp the ill-conditioning, which typically arises in the extended Galerkin methods.

The method presented in this paper can be extended to more general problems, such as linear and nonlinear elasticity problems.
The extension to the three dimension VEM is straightforward, thanks to the nonconforming structure of the space; see
[
23
]
.

The enriched virtual element method is based on two main ingredients: local stabilizations and projections onto bulk and face enriched polynomial spaces.
We develop the analysis of the method for arbitrary polynomial order.
We point put that the analysis of the stabilization of the method is still at an embryonic stage.
In fact, we are able to provide an explicit stabilization assuming that the method is enriched with functions that are not “too singular”;
for instance, we are still not able to provide an explicit stabilization for singular functions arising from, e.g., slit domains.
Moreover, we prove the lower stability bound under a strong assumption related to inverse estimates in enriched polynomial spaces.
Importantly, we provide practical stabilizations, which lead to optimal convergence rate in the numerical experiments.

Importantly, for the approximation of solutions to elliptic problems in primal formulation, we mention that there are two main families of the VEM: conforming and nonconforming VEMs.
For technical reasons, it appears that the latter family is more suited to the enrichment we are going to present.
This is a relevant fact, for the nonconforming VEM has strong links with the Hybrid-High order (HHO) methods and Hybridizable Discontinuous Galerkin method;
see, e.g.,
[
26
,
30
,
31
]
,
and
[
44
]
where the ideas underlying enriched non-conforming methods, like the ncVEM, have been adapted to the HHO setting.
Thus, the proposed enrichment goes beyond the scope of the nonconforming VEM and could be investigated in other settings as well.

We employ a standard notation for Sobolev spaces.
Given
D\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
a domain and
s~\in~\mathbb{N}
, we denote the standard Sobolev space of integer order
s
over
D
by
H^{s}(D)
.
The case

is special: the Sobolev space
H^{0}(D)
is the Lebesgue space
L^{2}(D)
.
We endow the Sobolev spaces with the standard inner products and seminorms
(\cdot,\cdot)_{s,D}
and
|\cdot|_{s,D}
,
and denote the Sobolev norm of order
s

\|\cdot\|_{s,D}^{2}:=\sum_{\ell=0}^{s}\|\cdot\|^{2}_{\ell,D}.

For
s=1
, it is convenient to write

a^{D}(\cdot,\cdot):=(\cdot,\cdot)_{1,D}.

Fractional Sobolev spaces can be defined in several ways. We use the definition of finiteness of the Aronszajin-Gagliardo-Slobodeckij norm; see, e.g.,
[
29
]
and the references therein.
In particular, for any sufficiently smooth
v
on
\partial D
, set

|v|_{\frac{1}{2},\partial D}^{2}:=\int_{\partial D}\int_{\partial D}\frac{|v(\xi)-v(\eta)|^{2}}{|\xi-\eta|^{2}}d\xi d\eta,\quad\quad\|v\|^{2}_{\frac{1}{2},\partial D}:=\|v\|^{2}_{0,\partial D}+|v|^{2}_{\frac{1}{2},\partial D}.

We define the fractional Sobolev space
H^{\frac{1}{2}}(\partial D)
as

H^{\frac{1}{2}}(\partial D):=\left\{v\in L^{2}(\partial D)\text{ such that }\|v\|_{\frac{1}{2},\partial D}\text{ is finite}\right\}.

Negative Sobolev spaces are defined via duality. In particular, we introduce
H^{-\frac{1}{2}}(\partial D)
as the dual space of
H^{\frac{1}{2}}(\partial D)
. This space and its norm read

H^{-\frac{1}{2}}(\partial D):=(H^{\frac{1}{2}}(\partial D))^{*},\quad\quad\|v\|_{-\frac{1}{2},\partial D}:=\sup_{\|w\|_{\frac{1}{2},\partial D}=1}(v,w)_{0,\partial D}.

3.2
Nonconforming enriched virtual element spaces

The core idea behind the design of the enriched VEM (EVEM) is that the singular functions appearing in the expansions (
9
) and (
10
) belong to the kernel of the Laplace operator.

Subdivide
\mathscr{T}_{n}
into three layers.
The first layer
\mathscr{T}_{n}^{1}
consists of the polygons abutting or containing the singular vertex
\mathbf{A}
, as well as polygons that are sufficiently close to
\mathbf{A}
: given
\widetilde{\gamma}>0

\mathscr{T}_{n}^{1}:=\left\{K\in\mathscr{T}_{n}\text{ such that }\text{dist}(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{x}_{K})\leq\widetilde{\gamma}\diam(\Omega)\right\}.

(15)

Further comments on this definition will be provided in Remark
6
below.

The second layer
\mathscr{T}_{n}^{2}
consists of the polygons sharing at least one edge with elements in the first layer, i.e.,

\mathscr{T}_{n}^{2}:=\left\{K\in\mathscr{T}_{n}\setminus\mathscr{T}_{n}^{1}\text{ such that there exists~$\widetilde{K}\in\mathscr{T}_{n}^{1}$ with }\text{card}(\mathscr{E}^{K}\cap\mathscr{E}^{\widetilde{K}})>0\right\}.

We set the third layer
\mathscr{T}_{n}^{3}
as the remainder of the elements in
\mathscr{T}_{n}
:

\mathscr{T}_{n}^{3}:=\left\{K\in\mathscr{T}_{n}\setminus(\mathscr{T}_{n}^{1}\cup\mathscr{T}_{n}^{2})\right\}.

(16)

We consider an analogous splitting for the set of edges
\mathscr{E}_{n}
. In particular, we subdivide
\mathscr{E}_{n}
into two layers of edges.
The first one,
\mathscr{E}_{n}^{1}
, is the set of all the edges belonging to the boundary of elements in
\mathscr{T}_{n}^{1}
:

\mathscr{E}_{n}^{1}=\left\{e\in\mathscr{E}_{n}\text{ such that there exists~$K\in\mathscr{T}_{n}^{1}$ with }e\subset\partial K\right\},

whereas second layer consists of the remainder of the edges, i.e.,

\mathscr{E}_{n}^{2}=\left\{e\in\mathscr{E}_{n}\setminus\mathscr{E}_{n}^{1}\right\}.

v\mathbf{n}_{K}&\text{if }e\in\mathscr{E}_{n}^{B}.\\
\end{cases}

(30)

\begin{split}H^{1,nc}_{0}(\mathscr{T}_{n},p):=&\left\{v\in H^{1}(\mathscr{T}_{n})\middle|\int_{e}\llbracket v\rrbracket\cdot\mathbf{n}_{e}\,\widetilde{m}_{\alpha}^{e}=0\quad\forall\widetilde{m}_{\alpha}^{e}\in\widetilde{\mathbb{P}}_{p-1}(e),\;\forall e\in\mathscr{E}_{n}\right\}.\\

3.3
The discrete bilinear form and right-hand side

Functions in the virtual element spaces are not available in closed form.
Thus, in order to design the numerical scheme for the approximation of solutions to (
11
),
we introduce a global bilinear form and right-hand side that are computable in terms of the degrees of freedom.
To this purpose, we generalize the construction in
[
5
]
to the enriched setting.

The discrete bilinear form.

Using the orthogonality property of the projector
\widetilde{\Pi}^{\nabla,K}_{p}
in (
26
), we apply Pythagoras’ theorem in Hilbert spaces and get

a^{K}(u,v)=a^{K}(\widetilde{\Pi}^{\nabla,K}_{p}u,\widetilde{\Pi}^{\nabla,K}_{p}v)+a^{K}((I-\widetilde{\Pi}^{\nabla,K}_{p})u,(I-\widetilde{\Pi}^{\nabla,K}_{p})v)\quad\forall u,\,v\in H^{1}(K).

The first term on the right-hand side is computable on
V_{n}(K)\times V_{n}(K)
, see (
27
), whereas the second one is not.
As standard in virtual elements
[
12
,
13
]
,
we introduce a symmetric bilinear form
S^{K}:\ker(\widetilde{\Pi}^{\nabla,K}_{p})\times\ker(\widetilde{\Pi}^{\nabla,K}_{p})\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
satisfying

c_{*}(K)|v_{n}|^{2}_{1,K}\leq S^{K}(v_{n},v_{n})\leq c^{*}(K)|v_{n}|^{2}_{1,K}\quad\quad\forall v_{n}\in\ker(\widetilde{\Pi}^{\nabla,K}_{p}),\quad\forall K\in\mathscr{T}_{n},

(32)

where
c_{*}(K)
and
c^{*}(K)
are two positive constants, possibly depending on the polynomial degree
p
, the geometric properties of
K
, and the singular function
\mathscr{S}_{\mathbf{A}}
.

Having this at hand, define the local discrete bilinear forms

a_{n}^{K}(u_{n},v_{n})=a^{K}(\widetilde{\Pi}^{\nabla,K}_{p}u_{n},\widetilde{\Pi}^{\nabla,K}_{p}v_{n})+S^{K}((I-\widetilde{\Pi}^{\nabla,K}_{p})u_{n},(I-\widetilde{\Pi}^{\nabla,K}_{p})v_{n})\quad\quad\forall u_{n},\,v_{n}\in V_{n}(K),

and the global discrete bilinear form

a_{n}(u_{n},v_{n})=\sum_{K\in\mathscr{T}_{n}}a_{n}^{K}(u_{n}{}_{|K},v_{n}{}_{|K})\quad\quad\forall u_{n},v_{n}\in V_{n}.

We postpone explicit choices and further considerations about the stabilization forms
S^{K}
to Section
4.6
below.

For all
K\in\mathscr{T}_{n}
, the local discrete bilinear forms
a_{n}^{K}(\cdot,\cdot)
are coercive and continuous with respect to the
H^{1}
seminorm,
with coercivity and continuity constants

\alpha_{*}(K)=\min(1,c_{*}(K)),\quad\quad\alpha^{*}(K)=\max(1,c^{*}(K)).

In other words, for all
K\in\mathscr{T}_{n}
, we have

\alpha_{*}(K)|v_{n}|^{2}_{1,K}\leq a_{n}^{K}(v_{n},v_{n})\leq\alpha^{*}(K)|v_{n}|^{2}_{1,K}\quad\quad\forall v_{n}\in V_{n}(K).

(33)

Moreover, the stabilization is symmetric and polynomially-enriched consistent: for all
K\in\mathscr{T}_{n}
,

a_{n}^{K}(q_{p},v_{n})=a^{K}(q_{p},v_{n})\quad\quad\forall q_{p}\in\widetilde{\mathbb{P}}_{p}(K),\quad\forall v_{n}\in V_{n}.

(34)

The discrete right-hand side.

Denote the number and the set of vertices of element
K
by
N_{V}^{K}
and
\{\nu_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N_{V}^{K}}
, recall the definition of the projector
\Pi^{0,K}_{p-2}
in (
29
),
and define

\langle f,v_{n}\rangle_{n}=\sum_{K\in\mathscr{T}_{n}}\langle f_{|K},v_{n}{}_{|K}\rangle_{n,K}:=\begin{cases}\sum_{K\in\mathscr{T}_{n}}\int_{K}f_{|K}\Pi^{0,K}_{p-2}v_{n}{}_{|K}&\text{if }p\geq 2\\
\sum_{K\in\mathscr{T}_{n}}\frac{1}{N_{V}^{K}}\int_{K}f_{|K}(\sum_{i=1}^{N_{V}}v_{n}(\nu_{i}))&\text{if }p=1.\\
\end{cases}

The method

The nonconforming enriched virtual element method for problem (

\begin{cases}\text{find }u_{n}\in V_{n}\text{ such that}\\
a_{n}(u_{n},v_{n})=\langle f,v_{n}\rangle_{n}\quad\quad\forall v_{n}\in V_{n}.\end{cases}

(35)

Method (

.
We devote Section

below to the analysis of (

),
whereas we provide the implementation details in Appendix

The role of the Heaviside function.

Our approach does not allow for the inclusion of the Heaviside in the test and trial spaces as done in the XFEM, GFEM, and XVEM.
In fact, the Laplacian of the Heaviside function is not a function, rendering the construction of
V_{n}(K)
in (
21

However, the flexibility of polygonal meshes renders the use of the Heaviside function practically useless.
In fact, elements cut into two by a crack can be re-meshed into distinct polygons,
whereas the setting in Section
3.2
allows for handling automatically elements with internal cracks;
see also
[
4
]
.

4.1

Here, we present the abstract error analysis result for method (

(37)

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
u
and
u_{n}
be the solutions to (
11
) and (
35
).
Recall that
\alpha_{*}(K)
and
\alpha^{*}(K)
are the stability constants in (
33
), and
\mathscr{N}_{n}
is defined in (
37
).
Then, the following a priori estimate is valid: for all
u_{\pi}
piecewise in
\widetilde{\mathbb{P}}_{p}(K)
and for all
u_{I}\in V_{n}
,

\begin{split}|u-u_{n}|_{1,\mathscr{T}_{n}}\leq&\max_{K\in\mathscr{T}_{n}}\alpha_{*}^{-1}(K)\left\{\sup_{v_{n}\in V_{n}}\frac{\langle f,v_{n}\rangle_{n}-(f,v_{n})_{0,\Omega}}{|v_{n}|_{1,\mathscr{T}_{n}}}+\sup_{v_{n}\in V_{n}}\frac{\mathscr{N}_{n}(u,v_{n})}{|v_{n}|_{1,\mathscr{T}_{n}}}\right.\\
&\quad\quad\left.+\left(1+\max_{K\in\mathscr{T}_{n}}\alpha^{*}(K)\right)\left(|u-u_{\pi}|_{1,\mathscr{T}_{n}}+|u-u_{I}|_{1,\mathscr{T}_{n}}\right)\right\}.\\
\end{split}

(38)

Proof.

The proof follows along the same lines as that in
[
5
, Theorem 4.1]
. For the sake of completeness, we carry out all the details.

For all
u_{I}\in V_{n}
, the triangle inequality entails, for
\delta_{n}:=u_{n}-u_{I}
,

|u-u_{n}|_{1,\mathscr{T}_{n}}\leq|u-u_{I}|_{1,\mathscr{T}_{n}}+|u_{I}-u_{n}|_{1,\mathscr{T}_{n}}=|u-u_{I}|_{1,\mathscr{T}_{n}}+|\delta_{n}|_{1,\mathscr{T}_{n}}.

(39)

We estimate the second term on the right-hand side: for all
u_{\pi}
piecewise in
\widetilde{\mathbb{P}}_{p}(K)
, apply (
33
) and (
34
) and get

\begin{split}|\delta_{n}|_{1,\mathscr{T}_{n}}^{2}&=\sum_{K\in\mathscr{T}_{n}}|\delta_{n}|^{2}_{1,K}\leq\sum_{K\in\mathscr{T}_{n}}\alpha_{*}^{-1}(K)a_{n}^{K}(\delta_{n},\delta_{n})\\
&\leq\max_{K\in\mathscr{T}_{n}}\alpha_{*}^{-1}(K)\sum_{K\in\mathscr{T}_{n}}\left\{a_{n}^{K}(u_{n},\delta_{n})-a_{n}^{K}(u_{I}-u_{\pi},\delta_{n})-a^{K}(u_{\pi}-u,\delta_{n})-a^{K}(u,\delta_{n})\right\}.\\
\end{split}

Observe that

\begin{split}\sum_{K\in\mathscr{T}_{n}}a^{K}(u,\delta_{n})&=\sum_{K\in\mathscr{T}_{n}}\left\{\int_{K}-\Delta u\,\delta_{n}+\int_{\partial K}\mathbf{n}_{K}\cdot\nabla u\,\delta_{n}\right\}\\
&=(f,\delta_{n})_{0,\Omega}+\sum_{e\in\mathscr{E}_{n}}\int_{e}\nabla u\cdot\llbracket\delta_{n}\rrbracket=:(f,\delta_{n})_{0,\Omega}+\mathscr{N}_{n}(u,\delta_{n}).\\
\end{split}

Deduce that

\begin{split}|\delta_{n}|^{2}_{1,\mathscr{T}_{n}}&\leq\max_{K\in\mathscr{T}_{n}}\alpha_{*}^{-1}(K)\Big\{\langle f,\delta_{n}\rangle_{n}-(f,\delta_{n})_{0,\Omega}-\mathscr{N}_{n}(u,\delta_{n})\\
&\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\left.+\max_{K\in\mathscr{T}_{n}}\alpha^{*}(K)|u_{I}-u_{\pi}|_{1,\mathscr{T}_{n}}|\delta_{n}|_{1,\mathscr{T}_{n}}+|u-u_{\pi}|_{1,\mathscr{T}_{n}}|\delta_{n}|_{1,\mathscr{T}_{n}}\right\},\\
\end{split}

which implies

\begin{split}|\delta_{n}|_{1,\mathscr{T}_{n}}&\leq\max_{K\in\mathscr{T}_{n}}\alpha_{*}^{-1}(K)\Big\{\frac{\langle f,\delta_{n}\rangle_{n}-(f,\delta_{n})_{0,\Omega}}{|\delta_{n}|_{1,\mathscr{T}_{n}}}-\frac{\mathscr{N}_{n}(u,\delta_{n})}{|\delta_{n}|_{1,\delta_{n}}}\\
&\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\left.+(1+\max_{K\in\mathscr{T}_{n}}\alpha^{*}(K))|u-u_{\pi}|_{1,\mathscr{T}_{n}}+\max_{K\in\mathscr{T}_{n}}\alpha^{*}(K)|u-u_{I}|_{1,\mathscr{T}_{n}}\right\}.\\
\end{split}

This, together with (
39
),
yields
the assertion.
∎

Here, we show how to estimate from above the term

Lemma 4.3
.

Proof.

The proof follows along the same lines as that of

For the sake of completeness, we provide some details.

We define

\begin{split}&\int_{K}(u-u_{I})q_{p-2}=0\quad\quad\forall q_{p-2}\in\mathbb{P}_{p-2}(K),\quad\forall K\in\mathscr{T}_{n},\\
&\int_{e}(u-u_{I})\widetilde{q}_{p-1}^{e}=0\quad\quad\forall\widetilde{q}_{p-1}^{e}\in\widetilde{\mathbb{P}}_{p-1}(e),\quad\forall e\in\mathscr{E}_{n}.\\

4.5

Lemma 4.6
.

Let
u
be the solution to problem (

),
u_{0}
be as in (

\mathscr{N}_{n}

Proof.

We prove the bound edge by edge. Without loss of generality, we assume that

and
e\in\mathscr{E}_{n}^{1}

Let

4.6
Stabilizations

Here, we exhibit explicit choices of the stabilization
S^{K}(\cdot,\cdot)
introduced in (

More precisely, we exhibit a theoretical stabilization, for which we are able to prove the bounds in (

assuming that

with
\varepsilon>0

To the aim, we shall assume the validity of an inverse estimate for enriched polynomials on the boundary; see inequality (

Eventually, we introduce a practical stabilization, which we shall widely employ in the numerical experiments in Section

A theoretical stabilization.

For every
K\in\mathscr{T}_{n}
, define

S^{K}_{T}(u_{n},v_{n})=h_{K}^{-2}(\Pi^{0,K}_{p-2}u_{n},\Pi^{0,K}_{p-2}v_{n})_{0,K}+h_{K}^{-1}\sum_{e\in\mathscr{E}^{K}}(\widetilde{\Pi}^{0,e}_{p-1}u_{n},\widetilde{\Pi}^{0,e}_{p-1}v_{n})_{0,e}\quad\forall u_{n},\,v_{n}\in V_{n}(K).

(48)

Recall that the projector
\Pi^{0,K}_{p-2}
is defined in (
29
), whereas the projector
\widetilde{\Pi}^{0,e}_{p-1}
is defined in (
28
).

In the proof of Proposition
4.7
below, we assume the validity of the following inverse estimate: for all
K\in\mathscr{T}_{n}
,

\|\mathbf{n}_{e}\cdot\nabla v_{n}\|_{0,\partial K}\lesssim h_{K}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\|\mathbf{n}_{e}\cdot\nabla v_{n}\|_{-\frac{1}{2},\partial K}\qquad\forall v_{n}\in V_{n}(K).

(49)

The inverse inequality (
49
) involves piecewise discontinuous enriched polynomials on the boundary of each element
K
.
Standard arguments imply such an inverse estimate, if only standard polynomial spaces are employed; yet, we are currently not able to provide a precise proof for the enriched case and postpone it to future investigations.
Notably, at the present stage, we are not able to claim that the hidden constant does not depend on the singular function.

Proposition 4.7
.

Let assumptions (
A0
)-(
A2
) be valid and
\mathscr{S}_{\mathbf{A}}\in H^{\frac{3}{2}+\varepsilon}(K)
with
\varepsilon>0
.
Assuming the validity of the inverse estimate (
49
),
the stabilization
S^{K}_{T}(\cdot,\cdot)
in
(
48
)
satisfies (
32
).

Proof.

First, we show the lower bound in (
32
).
For every
v_{n}\in\ker(\widetilde{\Pi}^{\nabla,K}_{p})
, using the definition of the local enriched spaces
V_{n}(K)
, we write

|v_{n}|^{2}_{1,K}=\int_{K}\nabla v_{n}\cdot\nabla v_{n}=\int_{K}-\Delta v_{n}\Pi^{0,K}_{p-2}v_{n}+\int_{\partial K}(\mathbf{n}_{K}\cdot\nabla v_{n})\,\widetilde{\Pi}^{0,e}_{p-1}v_{n}.

Use the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality to get

|v_{n}|_{1,K}\leq\|\Delta v_{n}\|_{0,K}\|\Pi^{0,K}_{p-2}v_{n}\|_{0,K}+\|\mathbf{n}_{K}\cdot\nabla v_{n}\|_{0,\partial K}\|\widetilde{\Pi}^{0,e}_{p-1}v_{n}\|_{0,\partial K}.

(50)

Recall that we assume the validity of (
49
).
Recall also that the following inverse inequality is valid; see
[
22
, Lemma 10]
and
[
14
, Theorem 2]
:

\|\Delta v_{n}\|_{0,K}\lesssim h_{K}^{-1}|v_{n}|_{1,K}.

(51)

To see (
51
), we provide some details, which we can be found in two references above:

\|\Delta v_{n}\|_{0,K}\lesssim h_{K}^{-1}\|\Delta v_{n}\|_{-1,K}:=h_{K}^{-1}\sup_{\Phi\in H^{1}_{0}(K)}\frac{(\Delta v_{n},\Phi)}{|\Phi|_{1,K}}=h_{K}^{-1}\sup_{\Phi\in H^{1}_{0}(K)}\frac{(\nabla v_{n},\nabla\Phi)_{0,K}}{|\Phi|_{1,K}}\leq h_{K}^{-1}|v_{n}|_{1,K},

where in the first inequality we used a standard polynomial inverse inequality on polygons,
whence the hidden constant in (
51
) depends on the order of accuracy
p
and on the shape of element
K
.

The Neumann trace inequality is valid as well:

\|\mathbf{n}_{K}\cdot\nabla v_{n}\|_{-\frac{1}{2},\partial K}\lesssim|v_{n}|_{1,K}+h_{K}\|\Delta v_{n}\|_{0,K}\overset{\eqref{inverse:VEM}}{\lesssim}|v_{n}|_{1,K}.

(52)

The Neumann trace inequality is valid not only for polynomials or functions in virtual element spaces, but for
H^{1}(K)
functions with Laplacian in
L^{2}(K)
; see, e.g.,
[
40
, Theorem A.33]
,
and the hidden constant depends on the shape of the element
K
.

Collecting (
50
), (
51
), and (
52
) leads to

|v_{n}|_{1,K}\lesssim h_{K}^{-1}\|\Pi^{0,K}_{p-2}v_{n}\|_{0,K}+h_{K}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\|\widetilde{\Pi}^{0,e}_{p-1}v_{n}\|_{0,\partial K},

which is the lower bound in (
32
).

Next, we show the upper bound in (
32
). We estimate from above the two terms on the right-hand side of the following identity:

S^{K}_{T}(v_{n},v_{n})=h_{K}^{-2}\|\Pi^{0,K}_{p-2}v_{n}\|^{2}_{0,K}+h_{K}^{-1}\|\widetilde{\Pi}^{0,e}_{p-1}v_{n}\|^{2}_{0,\partial K}.

As for the first term, we use the stability of orthogonal projections and the Poincaré-Wirtinger inequality
[
19
, equation (1.2)]
:

h_{K}^{-2}\|\Pi^{0,K}_{p-2}v_{n}\|^{2}_{0,K}\lesssim|v_{n}|^{2}_{1,K}.

As for the second term, use the stability of orthogonal projections, the trace inequality, the Poincaré-Wirtinger inequality
[
19
, equation (1.2)]
again, and assumption (
A2
):

h_{K}^{-1}\|\widetilde{\Pi}^{0,e}_{p-1}v_{n}\|_{0,\partial K}^{2}\lesssim|v_{n}|^{2}_{1,K}.

We can apply the Poincaré-Wirtinger inequality
[
19
, equation (1.2)]
, because
v_{n}
belongs to
\ker(\widetilde{\Pi}^{\nabla,K}_{p})
, whence
v_{n}
has zero average on
\partial K
.

This concludes the proof.
∎

A practical stabilization.

The stabilization
S^{K}_{T}(\cdot,\cdot)
introduced in (

) is computable in terms of the degrees of freedom (

) and (

).
Notwithstanding, it requires a certain amount of work to implement.

(53)

Originally, such a stabilization was introduced for the nonenriched 3D VEM in
[

]
, and its performance was analyzed in the 2D case in
[

]
.
To the best of our knowledge, such a stabilization is amongst the most robust from the numerical standpoint in the literature.
Roughly speaking, this stabilization keeps trace somehow of the true energy of the basis functions.

In Section

below, we perform the numerical experiments using the stabilization

9
). We discuss two generalizations.

On the one hand, we can associate more singular functions at one singular vertex/tip.
The extension from the unique singular function is rather straightforward: it suffices to add more singular functions to the local spaces
V_{n}(K)
, for all
K\in\mathscr{T}_{n}
close to
\mathbf{A}
.

On the other hand, we can consider more than one singular vertex/tip. In this case, we define the first layer
\mathscr{T}_{n}^{1}
as the set of all elements close to one or more singular vertices-tips.
Thus, the local spaces are enriched with special functions having singularities at different points.

The 3D case.

In 3D, the singularities arising from the geometry of the domain are different from those in the 2D case. In particular, vertex and edge singularities have to be dealt with.

The advantage of employing nonconforming virtual element spaces over conforming ones is that the definition of the local spaces is the same as in the two dimensional case:
fiven
\mathscr{T}_{n}
a polyhedral decomposition of the physical domain, for all
K\in\mathscr{T}_{n}
, we set

V_{n}(K)=\left\{v_{n}\in H^{1}(K)\mid\Delta v_{n}\in\mathbb{P}_{p-2}(K),\;\mathbf{n}\cdot\nabla v_{n}{}_{|F}\in\widetilde{\mathbb{P}}_{p-1}(F)\;\,\forall\,F\text{ face of }K\right\}.

Here,
\widetilde{\mathbb{P}}_{p-1}(K)
denotes the space of polynomials of degree at most
p-1
over a face
F
, possibly enriched of the normal derivative of the singular functions that we add to the local spaces.

The design of the method, as well as its analysis (with the exception of the stabilization bounds), follows exactly along the same lines as those of the two dimensional case.
Clearly, the big issue here is to find explicit corner and edge singularities, as well as to design proper quadrature formulas for singular functions over 2D faces.

Other differential operators.

It is possible to generalize our setting to PDEs with more general elliptic operators.

Let
\mathscr{L}
be an elliptic differential operator of the second order,
f
a smooth datum, and
\gamma_{1}
a trace operator.
Consider the following problem: find
u
such that

\begin{cases}\mathscr{L}u=f&\text{in }\Omega\\
\gamma_{1}u=0&\text{on }\partial\Omega.\\
\end{cases}

(55)

If
\mathscr{L}
is an elliptic operator, then the solution to problem (
55
) presents some singularities due to the geometry of the domain, and more specifically at corners, edges, tips of cracks, etc.
In case the singular behaviour is known explicitly, the solution can be decomposed as in (
9
) into a combination of a smooth and a singular part.

For simplicity, consider the case of a unique singular function
\mathscr{S}
. Such an
\mathscr{S}
belongs to the kernel of operator
\mathscr{L}
,
i.e.,
\mathscr{L}(\mathscr{S})=0
. Thus, on every element
K\in\mathscr{T}_{n}
sufficiently close to the singular corner, tip, edge etc., we define the local space

V_{n}(K):=\{v_{n}\in\text{H}\mid\mathscr{L}v_{n}\in[\mathbb{P}_{p_{1}}(K)]^{\ell_{1}},\,\gamma_{2}(v_{n})_{|e}\in[\mathbb{P}_{p_{2}}(e)]^{\ell_{2}}\oplus\gamma_{2}(S)\,\forall e\in\mathscr{E}^{K}\}\quad\quad\text{$\ell_{1}$, $\ell_{2}\in\mathbb{N}$}.

Here,
H
and
\gamma_{2}(\cdot)
denote a suitable Sobolev space and a suitable trace operator.

We postpone the generalizations presented in this section to future works.
In view of the ill-conditioning haunting extended methods, the above mentioned generalizations might be not straightforward
and require an orthonormalization procedure as illustrated in Section
5.3
and Appendix
B
.

Errors.

In order to measure the convergence of the method, we cannot use the exact relative error

\frac{|u-u_{n}|_{1,\mathscr{T}_{n}}}{|u|_{1,\Omega}}.

(56)

In fact,
u_{n}
is not known in closed form, but only through its degrees of freedom.
Therefore, we measure the decay of the following
computable
relative error: given
\widetilde{\Pi}_{p}^{\nabla}
defined piecewise as
\widetilde{\Pi}^{\nabla,K}_{p}
over
\mathscr{T}_{n}
,

\frac{|u-\widetilde{\Pi}_{p}^{\nabla}u_{n}|_{1,\mathscr{T}_{n}}}{|u|_{1,\Omega}}.

(57)

Errors (
56
) and (
57
) have the same convergence rate in terms of the mesh size
h
.
In order to see this, on the one hand, we use the stability of the
H^{1}
projection to get

|u-\widetilde{\Pi}^{\nabla}_{p}u_{n}|_{1,\mathscr{T}_{n}}\leq|u-\widetilde{\Pi}^{\nabla}_{p}u|_{1,\mathscr{T}_{n}}+|\widetilde{\Pi}^{\nabla}_{p}u-\widetilde{\Pi}^{\nabla}_{p}u_{n}|_{1,\mathscr{T}_{n}}\leq|u-\widetilde{\Pi}^{\nabla}_{p}u|_{1,\mathscr{T}_{n}}+|u-u_{n}|_{1,\mathscr{T}_{n}}.

The convergence of the first term on the right-hand side is provided by Lemma
4.2
.

On the other hand, we use Theorem
4.8
to get

\begin{split}|u-u_{n}|_{1,\mathscr{T}_{n}}\lesssim&\max_{K\in\mathscr{T}_{n}}\alpha_{*}^{-1}(K)\left\{\sup_{v_{n}\in V_{n}}\frac{\langle f,v_{n}\rangle_{n}-(f,v_{n})_{0,\Omega}}{|v_{n}|_{1,\mathscr{T}_{n}}}+\sup_{v_{n}\in V_{n}}\frac{\mathscr{N}_{n}(u,v_{n})}{|v_{n}|_{1,\mathscr{T}_{n}}}\right.\\
&\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\left.+\left(1+\max_{K\in\mathscr{T}_{n}}\alpha^{*}(K)\right)|u-\widetilde{\Pi}^{\nabla}_{p}u_{n}|_{1,\mathscr{T}_{n}}\right\}.\\
\end{split}

The convergence of the first two terms on the right-hand side is provided by Lemmas
4.5
and
4.6
.

5.1.4
On why the fully enriched scheme is more ill-conditioned than the partially enriched one

In Sections

The motivation behind the growth of the ill-conditioning for fine meshes is due to the behaviour of the singular enrichment function

On the elements that are close to the singular vertex
\mathbf{A}

On the other hand, on the elements that are far from
\mathbf{A}

becomes close to a constant function, especially for small elements.
In a sense, the basis functions of the virtual element space become close to be linearly dependent, as keeping on refining the mesh.

\mathbf{A}

6
Conclusions

We introduced a novel enriched nonconforming virtual element method for the approximation of solutions to the Laplace problem on polygonal domain.
This has been done in the spirit of the extended Galerkin methods, but with a novel twist.
The nonenriched virtual element spaces are endowed with special singular functions arising from asymptotic singular expansions at the corners of the domain.
We analyzed the method and presented several numerical results, including the high-order version of the method, which validate the theoretical predictions.
In Appendix
B
below, we discuss the implementation details. Importantly, both the theoretical and practical aspects are extensions of what is done in the nonenriched nonconforming virtual element methods.

In future works, we plan to investigate some generalizations of this approach:

•

the full analysis of a stabilization, which works regardless of the strength of the singularity;

•

a full analysis of the stabilizing term without resorting to inverse estimates in enriched polynomial spaces; this could be done analyzing errors as those in
[
44
]
,
i.e., stabilization dependent norms and errors involving the projected discrete solution;

•

multiple singularities;

•

the 3D version of the method;

•

enriched virtual elements for more general elliptic operators.

Notably, our approach seems to be applicable in several branches of computational mechanics including modeling of cracking phenomena, discontinuous media, and highly nonlinear complex materials behaviours.
