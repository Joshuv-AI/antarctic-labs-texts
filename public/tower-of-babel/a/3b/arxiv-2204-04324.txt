Approximate discounting-free policy evaluation from transient and recurrent states
Vektor Dewanto, Marcus Gallagher

Abstract
In order to distinguish policies that prescribe good from bad actions in transient states, we need to evaluate the so-called bias of a policy from transient states. However, we observe that most (if not all) works in approximate discounting-free policy evaluation thus far are developed for estimating the bias solely from recurrent states. We therefore propose a system of approximators for the bias (specifically, its relative value) from transient and recurrent states. Its key ingredient is a seminorm LSTD (least-squares temporal difference), for which we derive its minimizer expression that enables approximation by sampling required in model-free reinforcement learning. This seminorm LSTD also facilitates the formulation of a general unifying procedure for LSTD-based policy value approximators. Experimental results validate the effectiveness of our proposed method.



1
Introduction

Consider an environment where there are two types of states:
those that are visited infinitely many times by an agent, and
those that are not (even though the agent is modelled to operate up to infinity).
The members of the former group are called recurrent states, whereas
those of the latter are called transient states.
If all recurrent states form a single closed irreducible set, then we have
the so-called unichain Markov chain (MC).
It is closed in that once the agent is in any member of the set,
the agent cannot go outside to any non-member state.
It is irreducible because from any member of the set, the agent can
visit any other member.
In reinforcement learning (RL), such a unichain MC is induced by (at least)
one of the stationary policies of the Markov decision process (MDP) model,
for which we call such a model a unichain MDP.

The work in this paper is concerned with evaluating a stationary policy
\pi

from both transient and recurrent states in terms of
discounting-free evaluation functions.
Particularly, the policy value function of interest is the bias (denoted by
v_{b}
)
of
\pi
as follows,

v_{b}(\pi,s)\coloneqq\lim_{t_{\mathrm{max}}\to\infty}\mathbb{E}_{A_{t}\sim\pi(\cdot|s_{t}),S_{t+1}\sim p(\cdot|s_{t},a_{t})}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\sum_{t=0}^{t_{\mathrm{max}}-1}\Big(r(S_{t},A_{t})-v_{g}(\pi)\Big)\Big|S_{0}=s,\pi}}\right],\quad\forall s\in\mathcal{S},

(1)

where
S_{t}
and
A_{t}
are discrete state and action random variables on
the state set
\mathcal{S}
and action set
\mathcal{A}

of an infinite-horizon MDP with one-step state transition distribution
p
,
and reward function
r
.
Here,
v_{g}
denotes the gain (the average-reward) value function, which is
state-invariant whenever the induced MC is unichain.
Both bias and gain do not involve any discount factor (hence, they are said to
be discounting-free; cf. the discounted reward value function).

Evaluating the bias from both state types is essential for carrying out
further policy selection on gain-optimal policies that induce unichain MCs.
This is because the gain value function only concerns with the long-run rewards
(which are earned in recurrent states).
It ignores rewards earned at the outset in transient states, in which
gain-optimal policies therefore cannot distinguish “good” from “bad” actions.
In other words, they are suboptimal in transient states with respect to
the finest (the most selective) optimality criterion, i.e. the Blackwell optimality.

Despite the aforementioned importance, we observe that most works in RL are
designed for approximately evaluating a policy from recurrent states,
specifically for MDPs whose all induced MCs have only recurrent states.
The stationary state distribution is used for weighting the state-wise error terms
in the error function.
This applies to both discounted and discounting-free policy evaluation,
e.g.
(
liu_2021_tdgs
, Assumption 3)
,
(
dann_2014_petd
, Sec 2.4.2)
.
There are a few works that estimate the policy values from transient states.
However, they are applicable merely for MCs with a single recurrent state
whose reward is zero and known (thus no estimation is needed).
For instance,
bradtke_1996_lstd
proposed
a discounted-reward estimator for environments with
multiple transient states and a single 0-reward absorbing terminal state.
The error terms are weighted by the visitation probabilities of transient states
from the initial time until absorption, which is known to happen at
the last timestep of a trial (as the agent reaches the absorbing terminal state).

In this paper, we propose techniques that approximate the bias value
(of any stationary policy) from multiple transient and recurrent states
in model-free RL.
This requires value approximation from both state types,
instead of either one out of two types as the above-mentioned existing works.
Moreover, the state classification is unknown since
the agent does not know the state transition distribution
and does not attempt to estimate it.
This also implies that the agent does not know when it is absorbed into
the closed, irreducible recurrent state set (i.e. the absorption time).
If the state classification was known, the state set could be sliced
and two approximators could be built:
one for the recurrent states and one for the transient states;
taking advantage of the existing works.
However in that case, some additional work would still be needed for two reasons.
First is that all recurrent states cannot simply be separated from the state set
(for the sake of the transient state value approximator)
because there must be transitions from some transient states to recurrent states,
which may have non-zero rewards affecting transient state values.
Second is because those two individual bias approximators have different offsets
from the true bias (due to the nature of the error function that they minimize).
Therefore, their approximation results need to be calibrated before being used
simultaneously in a formula that involves bias values of
both transient and recurrent states.
More exposition about these two issues are provided later
in Secs
3
and
5
.

We present a system of approximators for the bias (relative) value.
Each approximator is based on least-squares temporal difference (LSTD).
In one extreme (where the computation cost is put aside), the system
instantiates stepwise LSTD approximators that use stepwise state distributions,
denoted as
p_{\pi}^{t}
, to weight the state-wise error terms.
Here,
p_{\pi}^{t}(s)\coloneqq\mathbb{E}_{S_{0}\sim\mathring{p}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mathrm{Pr}\{S_{t}=s|s_{0},\pi\}}}\right],\forall s\in\mathcal{S}
, which indicates the probability of visiting a state
s

in
t
timesteps when the agent begins at an initial state
S_{0}\sim\mathring{p}

then follows a policy
\pi
.
Such a system dismisses the need for state classification, but
poses at least three challenges, for which we contribute some solutions.

First
, the stepwise state distribution
p_{\pi}^{t}
may not have
the whole state set as its support.
For example, when the agent can only begin in transient states,

p_{\pi}^{t=0}
has zero probability for any recurrent state.
The same goes for transient states with respect to
p_{\pi}^{t}
after
the stationary state distribution is reached
(as there is no chance of visiting transient states
once the recurrent class is entered).
Consequently, the diagonal matrix derived from
p_{\pi}^{t}
may be
positive semidefinite (PSD).
This necessitates an LSTD approximator that involves a seminorm,
hence a generalized pseudoinverse.
The main difficulty comes from the fact that the reverse product law
does not apply to pseudoinverse.
We derive the optimal (minimizing) parameter of the seminorm LSTD, and
its sampling-based estimator in Sec
3
.

Second
, a system of
stepwise
approximators
requires at least one parameter per timestep.
This implies an infinite number of parameters whenever there is
an infinite number of timesteps (as in infinite-horizon MDPs).
To be practical, we propose a procedure that accommodates the specification of
the desired number of approximators (hence, the desired number of parameters in a system).
It determines a number of timestep neighborhoods according to
sampling-based estimated distances among stepwise state distributions.
For every neighborhood, it then applies a seminorm LSTD that weights
the state-wise error terms using the average state distributions on that neighborhood.
This procedure is general and unifies existing LSTD-based methods,
as explained in Sec
4
.

Third
, the approximators of the proposed system estimate the bias
only up to some offset; generally one unique offset for each approximator.
This is inherently due to the limitation of the bias error-function
that those approximators individually minimize.
In order to use the resulting approximations (of all approximators)
in a formula that jointly involves transient and recurrent state values,
we need to calibrate those offsets so that all approximations have
one common offset with respect to the true bias, which is time-invariant.
In Sec
5
, we describe such offset calibration along with
the pseudocode of the proposed system of seminorm LSTD approximators
for the bias (i.e. its relative value).

We provide experimental results in Sec
7
, which is
preceded by experimental setup in Sec
6
.
Finally, we conclude this work in Sec
8
, where
we also describe its limitations as well as some avenues for future research.
The next Sec
2
presents some necessary prerequisites
for this work.

Preliminaries

Given a stationary policy
\pi

its bias value

Since the policy and the value function type are fixed,

This

A parametric state-value approximator is parameterized by a parameter vector

.
Linear parameterization (which is the focus of this work) gives

\hat{v}(s,\boldsymbol{w})\coloneqq\boldsymbol{w}^{\intercal}\cdot\boldsymbol{f}(s)\approx v(s),\quad\forall s\in\mathcal{S},\forall\boldsymbol{w}\in\mathcal{W},\quad\text{equivalently,}\quad\hat{\boldsymbol{v}}(\boldsymbol{w})=\boldsymbol{F}\boldsymbol{w},

(2)

where

denotes the true (ground-truth) state value of
s
,

\boldsymbol{f}(s)\in\real{\dim(\boldsymbol{w})}
the state feature vector of
s
,
and
\boldsymbol{F}\in\real{|\mathcal{S}|\times\dim(\boldsymbol{w})}

the corresponding state feature matrix
(whose
s
-th row contains
\boldsymbol{f}^{\intercal}(s)
).
Here, the approximate state value vector
\hat{\boldsymbol{v}}\in\real{|\mathcal{S}|}

is obtained by stacking all scalar approximations

\hat{v}(s)\in\real{},\forall s\in\mathcal{S}
on top of each other.

One way to learn
\boldsymbol{w}
is by minimizing
the weighted mean squared projected Bellman error (MSPBE),
denoted as
\tilde{e}_{\mathbb{PB}}(\boldsymbol{w})
in (
4
).
This error function is derived based on the identity in
the average-reward Bellman equation, namely

v(s_{t})=r(s_{t})-g+\mathbb{E}_{p(\cdot|s_{t})}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[v(S_{t+1})}}\right],\quad\forall s_{t}\in\mathcal{S},\ \text{equivalently,}\quad\boldsymbol{v}=\boldsymbol{r}-\boldsymbol{g}+\boldsymbol{P}\boldsymbol{v}\eqqcolon\mathbb{B}[\boldsymbol{v}],

(3)

and some projection to obtain the representation of
\mathbb{B}[\boldsymbol{v}]

in the parameter space.
Here, the reward function
r(s_{t})=\mathbb{E}_{\pi}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[r(s_{t},A_{t})}}\right]

the reward vector
\boldsymbol{r}\in\real{|\mathcal{S}|}
,
the gain
g\coloneqq v_{g}(\pi)
corresponds to the gain vector

\boldsymbol{g}\coloneqq g\boldsymbol{1}\in\real{|\mathcal{S}|}

(using the vector
\boldsymbol{1}
, whose entries are all 1’s), and

\boldsymbol{P}\in\real{|\mathcal{S}|\times|\mathcal{S}|}
is
the one-step state transition stochastic matrix of an induced MC, whose

s_{t}
-th row represents a next-state conditional distribution

p_{\pi}(S_{t+1}|s_{t})=\sum_{a_{t}\in\mathcal{A}}\pi(a_{t}|s_{t})p(S_{t+1}|s_{t},a_{t})
.
The operator
\mathbb{B}:\real{|\mathcal{S}|}\mapsto\real{|\mathcal{S}|}

is termed as the Bellman policy-evaluation operator on

\boldsymbol{v}\in\real{|\mathcal{S}|}

The MSPBE is defined as follows,

\tilde{e}_{\mathbb{PB}}(\boldsymbol{w})\coloneqq\|\underbrace{\hat{\boldsymbol{v}}(\boldsymbol{w})-\tilde{\mathbb{P}}\mathbb{B}\hat{\boldsymbol{v}}(\boldsymbol{w})}_{\Delta_{\hat{\boldsymbol{v}}}}\|_{\tilde{\boldsymbol{p}}}^{2}=\Delta_{\hat{\boldsymbol{v}}}^{\!\!\intercal}\ \boldsymbol{D}_{\!\tilde{\boldsymbol{p}}}\ \Delta_{\hat{\boldsymbol{v}}}=\sum_{s\in\mathcal{S}}\tilde{p}(s)\ \Delta_{\hat{v}[s]}^{\!2},

(4)

where
\tilde{\boldsymbol{p}}\in\real{|\mathcal{S}|}
is a vector of probability values
of some state distribution
\tilde{p}(s)=\mathrm{Pr}\{S=s\},\forall s\in\mathcal{S}
,
and
\boldsymbol{D}_{\!\tilde{\boldsymbol{p}}}
is an
|\mathcal{S}|
-by-
|\mathcal{S}|

\tilde{\boldsymbol{p}}
along its diagonal.
Here,
\tilde{\mathbb{P}}
denotes a projection operator such that

\tilde{\mathbb{P}}\boldsymbol{v}=\boldsymbol{F}\boldsymbol{w}^{\diamond},\ \text{where}\ \boldsymbol{w}^{\diamond}=\argmin_{\boldsymbol{w}\in\mathcal{W}}\Big\{\|\boldsymbol{F}\boldsymbol{w}-\boldsymbol{v}\|_{\tilde{\boldsymbol{p}}}^{2}=\sum_{s\in\mathcal{S}}\tilde{p}(s)\ (\boldsymbol{f}^{\intercal}(s)\ \boldsymbol{w}-v(s))^{2}\Big\}.

(5)

At this stage, what is left to fully define

is

\tilde{p}
, whose probability values serve as weights

4

5

3
Seminorm LSTD approximators

In this section, we present an LSTD approximator that minimizes
\tilde{e}_{\mathbb{PB}}

(
4
), whose state distribution
\tilde{p}
does not necessarily
have the whole state set as its support,
i.e.
\tilde{p}(s)\geq 0,\forall s\in\mathcal{S}
.
This
\tilde{p}
induces a positive semidefinite (PSD) diagonal matrix
\boldsymbol{D}_{\!\tilde{\boldsymbol{p}}}
,
hence a seminorm
\tilde{e}_{\mathbb{PB}}
.
Consequently, minimizing
\tilde{e}_{\mathbb{PB}}
and deriving its projector
\tilde{\mathbb{P}}

(
5
) require solving
\boldsymbol{D}_{\!\tilde{\boldsymbol{p}}}
-seminorm LS problems.
We call the corresponding state-value approximator based on such
\tilde{e}_{\mathbb{PB}}

as
a seminorm LSTD
.

A seminorm LSTD is useful for unichain MCs with multiple transient and
recurrent states (and with certain reward structures).
For example, since each state type has different timing
(transient states are visited at the outset before absorption, whereas
recurrent states in the long-run),
a proper
\tilde{p}
is different for each type so that
the support of a proper type-specific
\tilde{p}
only contains a subset of
the state set; inducing a seminorm
\tilde{e}_{\mathbb{PB}}
.
It is proper in that it provides reasonable weighting for the state-wise error terms
in (
4
) and (
5
), and
that it enables state sampling in (
8
) and (
9
).
More importantly, a seminorm LSTD facilitates the derivation of
a general approximation procedure (see Sec
4
).

The main result of this Section is a sampling-enabler expression for the minimizer
of
\tilde{e}_{\mathbb{PB}}
of a seminorm LSTD.
It is presented in Thm
3.1
(Sec
3.2
).
For that, the preceding Sec
3.1
contains
the projection operator for the seminorm
\tilde{e}_{\mathbb{PB}}
and two necessary lemmas for the minimizer.

\displaystyle=\argmin_{\boldsymbol{w}\in\mathcal{W}}\Big\{\|\{\hat{\boldsymbol{v}}=\boldsymbol{F}\boldsymbol{w}\}-\boldsymbol{v}\|_{\boldsymbol{D}_{\!\tilde{\boldsymbol{p}}}}^{2}=\sum_{s\in\mathcal{S}}[\tilde{p}^{\frac{1}{2}}(s)]^{2}\ [\hat{v}(s;\boldsymbol{w})-v(s)]^{2}=\|\boldsymbol{D}_{\!\tilde{\boldsymbol{p}}}^{\!\frac{1}{2}}(\boldsymbol{F}\boldsymbol{w}-\boldsymbol{v})\|_{2}^{2}\Big\}.

Finding

\displaystyle\text{the $\boldsymbol{D}_{\!\tilde{\boldsymbol{p}}}$-seminorm LS solutions of}\quad\boldsymbol{F}\boldsymbol{w}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{v},\text{or equivalently,}

\displaystyle\text{the Euclidean-norm LS solutions of}\quad\boldsymbol{D}_{\!\tilde{\boldsymbol{p}}}^{\!\frac{1}{2}}\boldsymbol{F}\boldsymbol{w}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{D}_{\!\tilde{\boldsymbol{p}}}^{\!\frac{1}{2}}\boldsymbol{v}.

(13)

The latter has the following general form
(
ben_2003_ginv
, p106)
,

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{w}^{\diamond}

\displaystyle=[\boldsymbol{D}_{\!\tilde{\boldsymbol{p}}}^{\!\frac{1}{2}}\boldsymbol{F}]^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{D}_{\!\tilde{\boldsymbol{p}}}^{\!\frac{1}{2}}\boldsymbol{v}+[\boldsymbol{I}-(\boldsymbol{D}_{\!\tilde{\boldsymbol{p}}}^{\!\frac{1}{2}}\boldsymbol{F})^{\dagger}(\boldsymbol{D}_{\!\tilde{\boldsymbol{p}}}^{\!\frac{1}{2}}\boldsymbol{F})]\boldsymbol{c}

\displaystyle=\underbrace{[(\boldsymbol{D}_{\!\tilde{\boldsymbol{p}}}^{\!\frac{1}{2}}\boldsymbol{F})^{\intercal}\boldsymbol{D}_{\!\tilde{\boldsymbol{p}}}^{\!\frac{1}{2}}\boldsymbol{F}]^{\dagger}(\boldsymbol{D}_{\!\tilde{\boldsymbol{p}}}^{\!\frac{1}{2}}\boldsymbol{F})^{\intercal}\boldsymbol{D}_{\!\tilde{\boldsymbol{p}}}^{\!\frac{1}{2}}\boldsymbol{v}}_{[\boldsymbol{F}^{\intercal}\boldsymbol{D}_{\!\tilde{\boldsymbol{p}}}\boldsymbol{F}]^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{F}^{\intercal}\boldsymbol{D}_{\!\tilde{\boldsymbol{p}}}\boldsymbol{v}}+[\boldsymbol{I}-((\boldsymbol{D}_{\!\tilde{\boldsymbol{p}}}^{\!\frac{1}{2}}\boldsymbol{F})^{\intercal}\boldsymbol{D}_{\!\tilde{\boldsymbol{p}}}^{\!\frac{1}{2}}\boldsymbol{F})^{\dagger}((\boldsymbol{D}_{\!\tilde{\boldsymbol{p}}}^{\!\frac{1}{2}}\boldsymbol{F})^{\intercal}\boldsymbol{D}_{\!\tilde{\boldsymbol{p}}}^{\!\frac{1}{2}}\boldsymbol{F})]\boldsymbol{c},

(14)

for an arbitrary vector
\boldsymbol{c}\in\real{\dim(\boldsymbol{w})}
.
Since the gradient at
\boldsymbol{w}^{\diamond}
vanishes (a necessary condition for the minimizer),
it can be shown that
\boldsymbol{w}^{\diamond}
is also the solution of the normal equation of
(
13
) as in
(
campbell_2009_ginv
, Thm 2.1.2)
. That is,

\displaystyle\nabla\|\boldsymbol{F}\boldsymbol{w}-\boldsymbol{v}\|_{\boldsymbol{D}_{\!\tilde{\boldsymbol{p}}}}^{2}=2\boldsymbol{F}^{\intercal}\boldsymbol{D}_{\!\tilde{\boldsymbol{p}}}(\boldsymbol{F}\boldsymbol{w}^{\diamond}-\boldsymbol{v})

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{0}

(Whenever
\boldsymbol{w}=\boldsymbol{w}^{\diamond}
)

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow\boldsymbol{F}^{\intercal}\boldsymbol{D}_{\!\tilde{\boldsymbol{p}}}\boldsymbol{F}\boldsymbol{w}^{\diamond}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{F}^{\intercal}\boldsymbol{D}_{\!\tilde{\boldsymbol{p}}}\boldsymbol{v}

(cf. (
14
))

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow(\boldsymbol{D}_{\!\tilde{\boldsymbol{p}}}^{\!\frac{1}{2}}\boldsymbol{F})^{\intercal}\boldsymbol{D}_{\!\tilde{\boldsymbol{p}}}^{\!\frac{1}{2}}\boldsymbol{F}\boldsymbol{w}^{\diamond}

\displaystyle=(\boldsymbol{D}_{\!\tilde{\boldsymbol{p}}}^{\!\frac{1}{2}}\boldsymbol{F})^{\intercal}\boldsymbol{D}_{\!\tilde{\boldsymbol{p}}}^{\!\frac{1}{2}}\boldsymbol{v}.

(The normal equation of (
13
))

By setting
\boldsymbol{c}
to zero in (
14
),
we obtain one
\boldsymbol{D}_{\!\tilde{\boldsymbol{p}}}
-seminorm LS solution, denoted as
\tilde{\boldsymbol{w}}^{\diamond}
.
The projection then takes the form of

\tilde{\mathbb{P}}\boldsymbol{v}=\boldsymbol{F}\tilde{\boldsymbol{w}}^{\diamond}=\boldsymbol{F}\{[\boldsymbol{F}^{\intercal}\boldsymbol{D}_{\!\tilde{\boldsymbol{p}}}\boldsymbol{F}]^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{F}^{\intercal}\boldsymbol{D}_{\!\tilde{\boldsymbol{p}}}\boldsymbol{v}\}=\boldsymbol{F}\{\boldsymbol{Z}^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{F}^{\intercal}\boldsymbol{D}_{\!\tilde{\boldsymbol{p}}}\boldsymbol{v}\},\quad\text{hence,}\ \tilde{\mathbb{P}}=\boldsymbol{F}\boldsymbol{Z}^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{F}^{\intercal}\boldsymbol{D}_{\!\tilde{\boldsymbol{p}}}.

Note that this
\tilde{\boldsymbol{w}}^{\diamond}
is not
the minimal
\boldsymbol{D}_{\!\tilde{\boldsymbol{p}}}
-seminorm
\boldsymbol{D}_{\!\tilde{\boldsymbol{p}}}
-LS solution, i.e.
\tilde{\boldsymbol{w}}^{\diamond}\neq\argmin_{\boldsymbol{w}^{\diamond}}\|\boldsymbol{w}^{\diamond}\|_{\boldsymbol{D}_{\!\tilde{\boldsymbol{p}}}}
,
see
proszynski_1995_snls
.
This concludes the proof.
∎

The next Lemma
3.2
describes the relevant properties of
matrix
\boldsymbol{Z}
, which emerges during the foregoing derivation of
\tilde{\mathbb{P}}
.
This is essential because
\boldsymbol{Z}
and its pseudoinverse
\boldsymbol{Z}^{\dagger}

(along with its matrix square root) play an important role in the derivation of
the minimizer of
\tilde{e}_{\mathbb{PB}}
(see Thm
3.1
).

Lemma 3.2
.

These real matrices
\boldsymbol{Z}
,
\boldsymbol{Z}^{\dagger}
, and
\boldsymbol{Z}^{\nicefrac{{\dagger}}{{2}}}

are symmetric positive semidefinite (PSD).
Here,
\boldsymbol{Z}\coloneqq\boldsymbol{F}^{\intercal}\boldsymbol{D}_{\!\tilde{\boldsymbol{p}}}\boldsymbol{F}
, and

\boldsymbol{Z}^{\nicefrac{{\dagger}}{{2}}}\coloneqq(\boldsymbol{Z}^{\dagger})^{\frac{1}{2}}=\sqrt{\boldsymbol{Z}^{\dagger}}
,
which is the matrix square root of
\boldsymbol{Z}^{\dagger}
.

Proof.

First,
\boldsymbol{Z}
involves a PSD diagonal matrix

\boldsymbol{D}_{\!\tilde{\boldsymbol{p}}}=(\boldsymbol{D}_{\!\tilde{\boldsymbol{p}}}^{\!\frac{1}{2}})^{2}
with its unique matrix square root
\boldsymbol{D}_{\!\tilde{\boldsymbol{p}}}^{\!\frac{1}{2}}
.
Let
\boldsymbol{G}\coloneqq\boldsymbol{D}_{\!\tilde{\boldsymbol{p}}}^{\!\frac{1}{2}}\boldsymbol{F}
.
Expressing
\boldsymbol{Z}
as a Gram matrix gives

\boldsymbol{Z}^{\intercal}=(\boldsymbol{G}^{\intercal}\boldsymbol{G})^{\intercal}=\boldsymbol{G}^{\intercal}\boldsymbol{G}=\boldsymbol{Z}
,
which shows that
\boldsymbol{Z}
is symmetric.
Moreover,

\boldsymbol{u}^{\intercal}\boldsymbol{Z}\boldsymbol{u}=\boldsymbol{u}^{\intercal}\boldsymbol{F}^{\intercal}\boldsymbol{D}_{\!\tilde{\boldsymbol{p}}}^{\!\frac{1}{2}}\boldsymbol{D}_{\!\tilde{\boldsymbol{p}}}^{\!\frac{1}{2}}\boldsymbol{F}\boldsymbol{u}=(\boldsymbol{D}_{\!\tilde{\boldsymbol{p}}}^{\!\frac{1}{2}}\boldsymbol{F}\boldsymbol{u})^{\intercal}(\boldsymbol{D}_{\!\tilde{\boldsymbol{p}}}^{\!\frac{1}{2}}\boldsymbol{F}\boldsymbol{u})=\|\boldsymbol{D}_{\!\tilde{\boldsymbol{p}}}^{\!\frac{1}{2}}\boldsymbol{F}\boldsymbol{u}\|_{2}^{2}\geq 0,\quad\forall\boldsymbol{u}\in\real{\dim(\boldsymbol{f})}.

(15)

Hence,
\boldsymbol{Z}
is symmetric positive semidefinite (PSD).

Second, let the singular value decomposition (SVD) of
\boldsymbol{Z}
is
given by
\boldsymbol{M}\boldsymbol{D}_{\!\!\sigma}\boldsymbol{N}^{\intercal}
.
Then, we have

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{Z}\boldsymbol{Z}^{\intercal}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{M}\boldsymbol{D}_{\!\!\sigma}\boldsymbol{N}^{\intercal}\boldsymbol{N}\boldsymbol{D}_{\!\!\sigma}\boldsymbol{M}^{\intercal}=\boldsymbol{M}\boldsymbol{D}_{\!\!\sigma}^{2}\boldsymbol{M}^{\intercal},

(Since
\boldsymbol{N}
is orthogonal)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{Z}^{\intercal}\boldsymbol{Z}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{N}\boldsymbol{D}_{\!\!\sigma}\boldsymbol{M}^{\intercal}\boldsymbol{M}\boldsymbol{D}_{\!\!\sigma}\boldsymbol{N}^{\intercal}=\boldsymbol{N}\boldsymbol{D}_{\!\!\sigma}^{2}\boldsymbol{N}^{\intercal}.

(Since
\boldsymbol{M}
is orthogonal)

Because
\boldsymbol{Z}
is symmetric (hence, normal), we have

\boldsymbol{Z}\boldsymbol{Z}^{\intercal}=\boldsymbol{Z}^{\intercal}\boldsymbol{Z}=\boldsymbol{Z}^{2}
.
Thus,
\boldsymbol{M}=\boldsymbol{N}
whose columns are the orthogonal eigenvectors of
\boldsymbol{Z}^{2}
,
which are then normalized to become unit vectors in order to have
an orthogonal matrix
\boldsymbol{M}
.
By SVD, the singular value diagonal matrix
\boldsymbol{D}_{\!\!\sigma}
contains
the squared roots of eigenvalues of
\boldsymbol{Z}^{2}
.

Let
\mu
be the eigenvalue of
\boldsymbol{Z}
with eigenvector
\boldsymbol{u}
, then

\boldsymbol{Z}\boldsymbol{u}=\mu\boldsymbol{u},\quad\text{and}\quad\boldsymbol{Z}^{2}\boldsymbol{u}=\boldsymbol{Z}(\boldsymbol{Z}\boldsymbol{u})=\boldsymbol{Z}(\mu\boldsymbol{u})=\mu(\boldsymbol{Z}\boldsymbol{u})=\mu(\mu\boldsymbol{u})=\mu^{2}\boldsymbol{u},

which shows that
\boldsymbol{u}
is an eigenvector of
\boldsymbol{Z}^{2}

with the eigenvalue
\mu^{2}
.
This holds for all eigenvalues of
\boldsymbol{Z}
, which become the diagonal entries
of
\boldsymbol{D}_{\!\!\sigma}
(such eigenvalues are non-negative since
\boldsymbol{Z}
is PSD).
Moreover, because
\boldsymbol{Z}
is symmetric,
both
\boldsymbol{Z}
and
\boldsymbol{Z}^{2}
have the same set of orthogonal eigenvectors,
which becomes the columns of
\boldsymbol{M}
.
Thus, the eigen (spectral) decomposition (EigD) of
\boldsymbol{Z}
, namely

\boldsymbol{U}\boldsymbol{D}_{\!\!\mu}\boldsymbol{U}^{\intercal}
, is also a valid SVD.
Consequently,

\boldsymbol{Z}^{\dagger}=(\underbrace{\boldsymbol{M}\boldsymbol{D}_{\!\!\sigma}\boldsymbol{N}^{\intercal}}_{\text{SVD of $\boldsymbol{Z}$}})^{\dagger}=(\underbrace{\boldsymbol{U}\boldsymbol{D}_{\!\!\mu}\boldsymbol{U}^{\intercal}}_{\text{EigD of $\boldsymbol{Z}$}})^{\dagger}=\boldsymbol{U}\boldsymbol{D}_{\!\!\mu}^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{U}^{\intercal}=\boldsymbol{U}\boldsymbol{D}_{\!\!\mu}^{\nicefrac{{\dagger}}{{2}}}\boldsymbol{D}_{\!\!\mu}^{\nicefrac{{\dagger}}{{2}}}\boldsymbol{U}^{\intercal}=(\boldsymbol{D}_{\!\!\mu}^{\nicefrac{{\dagger}}{{2}}}\boldsymbol{U}^{\intercal})^{\intercal}(\boldsymbol{D}_{\!\!\mu}^{\nicefrac{{\dagger}}{{2}}}\boldsymbol{U}^{\intercal}),

(16)

where
\boldsymbol{D}_{\!\!\mu}^{\dagger}
is obtained by taking the reciprocal of non-zeroes entries
of
\boldsymbol{D}_{\!\!\mu}
.
Thus,
\boldsymbol{Z}^{\dagger}
can be expressed as a Gram matrix,
which is always PSD as shown before in (
15
).
Since
\boldsymbol{Z}^{\dagger}
is PSD, there exists exactly one (symmetric) PSD matrix

\boldsymbol{Z}^{\nicefrac{{\dagger}}{{2}}}
such that
\boldsymbol{Z}^{\dagger}=\boldsymbol{Z}^{\nicefrac{{\dagger}}{{2}}}\boldsymbol{Z}^{\nicefrac{{\dagger}}{{2}}}
.
From (
16
) above, we have

\boldsymbol{Z}^{\nicefrac{{\dagger}}{{2}}}=\boldsymbol{D}_{\!\!\mu}^{\nicefrac{{\dagger}}{{2}}}\boldsymbol{U}^{\intercal}
.
This concludes the proof, whose alternatives can be found in

lewis_1968_psd
, Corollary 3
;
harville_1997_mat
, Thm 20.5.3
.
∎

3.2
The sampling-enabler expression for the minimizer

The core component of a seminorm LSTD is the minimizer
\tilde{\boldsymbol{w}}^{*}

of its error
\tilde{e}_{\mathbb{PB}}
, which involves a PSD matrix
\boldsymbol{D}_{\!\tilde{\boldsymbol{p}}}
.
In particular for model-free RL, we need an expression of
\tilde{\boldsymbol{w}}^{*}

that enables sampling based approximation, akin to (
7
).
By utilizing Lemmas
3.1
and
3.2
from
the previous Sec
3.1
,
we are now ready to derive such a sampling-enabler expression.
It is stated in the following Thm
3.1
.

Theorem 3.1
.

One minimizer of the error
\tilde{e}_{\mathbb{PB}}(\boldsymbol{w})
in (
4
), which involves
a state distribution
\tilde{p}
with

\tilde{p}(s)\geq 0,\forall s\in\mathcal{S}

(hence,
\tilde{e}_{\mathbb{PB}}
is a seminorm with a PSD diagonal matrix
\boldsymbol{D}_{\!\tilde{\boldsymbol{p}}}
),
is given by

\tilde{\boldsymbol{w}}^{*}=(\boldsymbol{X}^{\intercal}\boldsymbol{Z}^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{X})^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{X}^{\intercal}\boldsymbol{Z}^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{y}=(\boldsymbol{Z}^{\nicefrac{{\dagger}}{{2}}}\boldsymbol{X})^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{Z}^{\nicefrac{{\dagger}}{{2}}}\boldsymbol{y},\qquad\text{where}

(17)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{X}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\mathbb{E}_{S\sim\tilde{p},S^{\prime}\sim p(\cdot|s)}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\boldsymbol{f}(S)\big(\boldsymbol{f}(S)-\boldsymbol{f}(S^{\prime})\big)^{\!\intercal}}}\right],

(with state feature
\boldsymbol{f}(s)
and one-step transition
p
)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{Z}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\mathbb{E}_{S\sim\tilde{p}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\boldsymbol{f}(S)\boldsymbol{f}(S)^{\intercal}}}\right],\quad\text{and}

(with state feature
\boldsymbol{f}(s)
as above)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{y}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\mathbb{E}_{S\sim\tilde{p}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\big(r(S)-g\big)\boldsymbol{f}(S)}}\right].

(with state reward
r(s)
, gain
g
and
\boldsymbol{f}(s)
as above)

Here,
p(\cdot|s)
denotes the next state conditional distribution
(i.e. the one-step state transition distribution given the current state
s
).
Note that we abuse the notations
\boldsymbol{X}
and
\boldsymbol{y}
, which are also used
in (
8
) and (
9
) but with
a different state distribution.

Pertaining to Thm
3.1
, we remark that the formula simplication
in (
17
) is crucial because
the resulting expression enables unbiased sampling-based estimation
for the minimizer
\tilde{\boldsymbol{w}}^{*}
in model-free RL.
This is possible because the last expression in (
17
)
involves only one factor of
\boldsymbol{X}
.
In contrast, the expression before simplification has three factors of
\boldsymbol{X}
,
hence it does not enable such unbiased estimation for
\tilde{\boldsymbol{w}}^{*}
.
The reason stems from the fact that
\boldsymbol{X}
depends on the next-state random variable
through
\mathbb{E}_{p(\cdot|s)}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\boldsymbol{f}(S^{\prime})}}\right]
, which
leads to a similar situation as described by
sutton_2018_irl
.
They explain that multiple independent samples of next states are required
to obtain an unbiased estimate of the product of multiple factors that
involve expectations of next states.
Such independent next-state samples are only available in deterministic transition
(where the next state is not random),
or in simulation where the agent can roll-back from any state to its previous state.
This sampling requirement cannot be accommodated in model-free RL settings since
the agent cannot roll-back to its previous state and
transitions are generally stochastic.

In addition, we also remark that the simplication in (
17
)
is carried out without introducing any error
(putting aside errors due to numerical computation).
In comparison, simplifying
\tilde{\boldsymbol{w}}^{*}
(to involve only one factor of
\boldsymbol{X}
)
through the reverse order law for the pseudoinverse is possible but with some errors
because the identity

(\boldsymbol{X}^{\intercal}\boldsymbol{Z}^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{X})^{\dagger}=\boldsymbol{X}^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{Z}\boldsymbol{X}^{\intercal\dagger}

requires strict conditions
(
hartwig_1986_rev
;
tian_2019_rev
)
.
Two example simplifications with errors are as follows,

•

by orthogonal approximation
\boldsymbol{X}_{\!\!\perp}\approx\boldsymbol{X}

(where
\boldsymbol{X}_{\!\!\perp}^{\dagger}=\boldsymbol{X}_{\!\!\perp}^{-1}=\boldsymbol{X}_{\!\!\perp}^{\intercal}
)
and the identity

(\boldsymbol{X}_{\!\!\perp}^{\intercal}\boldsymbol{Z}^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{X}_{\!\!\perp})^{\dagger}=\boldsymbol{X}_{\!\!\perp}^{\intercal}\boldsymbol{Z}\boldsymbol{X}_{\!\!\perp}

(
campbell_2009_ginv
, Theorem 1.2.1: 7)

such that

\tilde{\boldsymbol{w}}^{*}=(\boldsymbol{X}^{\intercal}\boldsymbol{Z}^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{X})^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{X}^{\intercal}\boldsymbol{Z}^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{y}\approx(\boldsymbol{X}_{\!\!\perp}^{\intercal}\boldsymbol{Z}^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{X}_{\!\!\perp})^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{X}_{\!\!\perp}^{\intercal}\boldsymbol{Z}^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{y}=\boldsymbol{X}_{\!\!\perp}^{\intercal}\boldsymbol{Z}\boldsymbol{Z}^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{y},

•

by nullifying the constant matrices
\boldsymbol{C}_{1}
and
\boldsymbol{C}_{2}

(in below expression) such that

\displaystyle\tilde{\boldsymbol{w}}^{*}

\displaystyle=(\boldsymbol{X}^{\intercal}\boldsymbol{Z}^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{X})^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{X}^{\intercal}\boldsymbol{Z}^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{y}=(\boldsymbol{X}^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{Z}\boldsymbol{X}^{\intercal\dagger}+\boldsymbol{C}_{\!1})\boldsymbol{X}^{\intercal}\boldsymbol{Z}^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{y}

\displaystyle\approx\boldsymbol{X}^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{Z}\boldsymbol{X}^{\intercal\dagger}\boldsymbol{X}^{\intercal}\boldsymbol{Z}^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{y}=\boldsymbol{X}^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{Z}(\boldsymbol{I}+\boldsymbol{C}_{\!2})\boldsymbol{Z}^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{y}\approx\boldsymbol{X}^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{Z}\boldsymbol{Z}^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{y}.

Finally, we present the proof for Thm
3.1
about
the minimizer
\tilde{\boldsymbol{w}}^{*}
below.

Proof.

(of Thm
3.1
)
The MSBPE error
\tilde{e}_{\mathbb{PB}}
in (
4
) can be expressed as follows,

\displaystyle\tilde{e}_{\mathbb{PB}}(\boldsymbol{w})

\displaystyle=\|\hat{\boldsymbol{v}}-\tilde{\mathbb{P}}\mathbb{B}\hat{\boldsymbol{v}}\|_{\boldsymbol{D}_{\!\tilde{\boldsymbol{p}}}}^{2}=\|\tilde{\mathbb{P}}\hat{\boldsymbol{v}}-\tilde{\mathbb{P}}\mathbb{B}\hat{\boldsymbol{v}}\|_{\boldsymbol{D}_{\!\tilde{\boldsymbol{p}}}}^{2}=\|\tilde{\mathbb{P}}[\hat{\boldsymbol{v}}-\mathbb{B}\hat{\boldsymbol{v}}]\|_{\boldsymbol{D}_{\!\tilde{\boldsymbol{p}}}}^{2}

(Recall
\hat{\boldsymbol{v}}
is representable)

\displaystyle=\{\tilde{\mathbb{P}}[\hat{\boldsymbol{v}}-\mathbb{B}\hat{\boldsymbol{v}}]\}^{\intercal}\boldsymbol{D}_{\!\tilde{\boldsymbol{p}}}\{\tilde{\mathbb{P}}[\hat{\boldsymbol{v}}-\mathbb{B}\hat{\boldsymbol{v}}]\}=[\hat{\boldsymbol{v}}-\mathbb{B}\hat{\boldsymbol{v}}]^{\intercal}\{\tilde{\mathbb{P}}^{\intercal}\boldsymbol{D}_{\!\tilde{\boldsymbol{p}}}\tilde{\mathbb{P}}\}[\hat{\boldsymbol{v}}-\mathbb{B}\hat{\boldsymbol{v}}]

\displaystyle=[\hat{\boldsymbol{v}}-\mathbb{B}\hat{\boldsymbol{v}}]^{\intercal}\{\boldsymbol{F}\boldsymbol{Z}^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{F}^{\intercal}\boldsymbol{D}_{\!\tilde{\boldsymbol{p}}}\}^{\intercal}\boldsymbol{D}_{\!\tilde{\boldsymbol{p}}}\{\boldsymbol{F}\boldsymbol{Z}^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{F}^{\intercal}\boldsymbol{D}_{\!\tilde{\boldsymbol{p}}}\}[\hat{\boldsymbol{v}}-\mathbb{B}\hat{\boldsymbol{v}}]

(Expand
\tilde{\mathbb{P}}
from Lemma
3.1
)

\displaystyle=[\hat{\boldsymbol{v}}-\mathbb{B}\hat{\boldsymbol{v}}]^{\intercal}\{\boldsymbol{D}_{\!\tilde{\boldsymbol{p}}}\boldsymbol{F}\boldsymbol{Z}^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{F}^{\intercal}\}\boldsymbol{D}_{\!\tilde{\boldsymbol{p}}}\{\boldsymbol{F}\boldsymbol{Z}^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{F}^{\intercal}\boldsymbol{D}_{\!\tilde{\boldsymbol{p}}}\}[\hat{\boldsymbol{v}}-\mathbb{B}\hat{\boldsymbol{v}}]

(
\boldsymbol{Z}^{\dagger}
is symmetric (Lemma
3.2

\displaystyle=\{\boldsymbol{F}^{\intercal}\boldsymbol{D}_{\!\tilde{\boldsymbol{p}}}[\hat{\boldsymbol{v}}-\mathbb{B}\hat{\boldsymbol{v}}]\}^{\intercal}\boldsymbol{Z}^{\dagger}(\boldsymbol{F}^{\intercal}\boldsymbol{D}_{\!\tilde{\boldsymbol{p}}}\boldsymbol{F})\boldsymbol{Z}^{\dagger}\{\boldsymbol{F}^{\intercal}\boldsymbol{D}_{\!\tilde{\boldsymbol{p}}}[\hat{\boldsymbol{v}}-\mathbb{B}\hat{\boldsymbol{v}}]\}

\displaystyle=\{\boldsymbol{F}^{\intercal}\boldsymbol{D}_{\!\tilde{\boldsymbol{p}}}[\hat{\boldsymbol{v}}-\mathbb{B}\hat{\boldsymbol{v}}]\}^{\intercal}\boldsymbol{Z}^{\dagger}\{\boldsymbol{F}^{\intercal}\boldsymbol{D}_{\!\tilde{\boldsymbol{p}}}[\hat{\boldsymbol{v}}-\mathbb{B}\hat{\boldsymbol{v}}]\}

(Apply the condition (ii) in Def
3.1
)

\displaystyle=\|\boldsymbol{F}^{\intercal}\boldsymbol{D}_{\!\tilde{\boldsymbol{p}}}[\hat{\boldsymbol{v}}-\mathbb{B}\hat{\boldsymbol{v}}]\|_{\boldsymbol{Z}^{\dagger}}^{2}

(
\boldsymbol{Z}^{\dagger}
is PSD (Lemma
3.2
),
hence
\boldsymbol{Z}^{\dagger}
-seminorm)

\displaystyle=\|\boldsymbol{F}^{\intercal}\boldsymbol{D}_{\!\tilde{\boldsymbol{p}}}[\boldsymbol{F}\boldsymbol{w}-\boldsymbol{P}\boldsymbol{F}\boldsymbol{w}-(\boldsymbol{r}-\boldsymbol{g})]\|_{\boldsymbol{Z}^{\dagger}}^{2}

(Expand

from (

)

\displaystyle=\|\boldsymbol{F}^{\intercal}\boldsymbol{D}_{\!\tilde{\boldsymbol{p}}}\boldsymbol{F}\boldsymbol{w}-\boldsymbol{F}^{\intercal}\boldsymbol{D}_{\!\tilde{\boldsymbol{p}}}\boldsymbol{P}\boldsymbol{F}\boldsymbol{w}-\boldsymbol{F}^{\intercal}\boldsymbol{D}_{\!\tilde{\boldsymbol{p}}}(\boldsymbol{r}-\boldsymbol{g})\|_{\boldsymbol{Z}^{\dagger}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\|\underbrace{\boldsymbol{F}^{\intercal}\boldsymbol{D}_{\!\tilde{\boldsymbol{p}}}(\boldsymbol{I}-\boldsymbol{P})\boldsymbol{F}}_{\boldsymbol{X}}\boldsymbol{w}-\underbrace{\boldsymbol{F}^{\intercal}\boldsymbol{D}_{\!\tilde{\boldsymbol{p}}}(\boldsymbol{r}-\boldsymbol{g})}_{\boldsymbol{y}}\|_{\boldsymbol{Z}^{\dagger}}^{2}=\|\boldsymbol{X}\boldsymbol{w}-\boldsymbol{y}\|_{\boldsymbol{Z}^{\dagger}}^{2}.

for the discounted-reward value function for recurrent MDPs.

Therefore, minimizing

\displaystyle\text{the $\boldsymbol{Z}^{\dagger}$-seminorm LS solutions of}\quad\boldsymbol{X}\boldsymbol{w}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{y},\quad\text{or equivalently,}

(18)

\displaystyle=(\boldsymbol{L}^{\!\intercal}\boldsymbol{L})^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{L}^{\!\intercal}\boldsymbol{Z}^{\nicefrac{{\dagger}}{{2}}}\boldsymbol{y}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{L}^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{Z}^{\nicefrac{{\dagger}}{{2}}}\boldsymbol{y}

\displaystyle=(\boldsymbol{Z}^{\nicefrac{{\dagger}}{{2}}}\boldsymbol{X})^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{Z}^{\nicefrac{{\dagger}}{{2}}}\boldsymbol{y},

(Expand
\boldsymbol{L}

Definition 4.1
.

A timestep neighborhood, denoted as
\mathcal{N}
, is an ordered set of
consecutive timesteps from an anchor timestep
t
to
(t+|\mathcal{N}|-1)
.
Every neighborhood
\mathcal{N}
has a unique anchor

(i.e. the earliest timestep in

Hence, the notation
\mathcal{N}_{t}
denotes a neighboorhod anchored at
t

The non-anchor member of
\mathcal{N}_{t}

Hence, every anchor has
(|\mathcal{N}_{t}|-1)
neighbors.

Definition 4.2
.

A state probability distribution of a neighborhood
\mathcal{N}_{t}
,
denoted as
\bar{p}_{t}
, is a lumpsum of stepwise state probabilities

from
\tau=t
to
(t+|\mathcal{N}|-1)
.
That is,

\bar{p}_{t}(s)\coloneqq\frac{1}{|\mathcal{N}_{t}|}\sum_{\tau=t}^{t+|\mathcal{N}_{t}|-1}p^{\tau}(s),\quad\text{with}\ p^{\tau}(s)=\mathbb{E}_{S_{0}\sim\mathring{p}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[p^{\tau}(s|s_{0})}}\right],\qquad\forall s\in\mathcal{S},

(19)

where

the probability of visiting the state
s

\tau

an initial state

.
This

is equivalent to the

\boldsymbol{P}^{\tau}

which is the one-step transition matrix
\boldsymbol{P}
raised to the power of
\tau

That is,

denotes the

The

of the stepwise conditional state distribution
p^{\tau}(\cdot|s_{0})

Definition 4.3
.

The support of a neighborhood

is defined as
the support of its state distribution
\bar{p}_{t}
,
denoted as

Definition 4.4
.

A general procedure for LSTD-based policy evaluation has three steps as follows.

1.

Specify a number of timestep neighborhoods (Def
4.1

2.

\bar{p}_{t}

\tau

\mathcal{N}_{t}
where
\tau

\mathcal{N}_{t}

The number of timestep neighborhoods (equivalently, the number of anchors or approximators)

This procedure forms a system of

seminorm LSTD-

4.2

One extreme of the general procedure (Def

First

Second
, the stepwise distribution may have a support smaller

By product, a system of stepwise LSTD-

between approximation accuracy and capacity

Third
, putting computation cost aside, stepwise treatment is

exploiting what the agent knows, that is the timestep

the corresponding state and reward samples at

This is crucial for unichain MDPs with multiple transient states
and multiple recurrent states.
In addition, the unichain category includes recurrent MDPs and

Because of this generality therefore,

We propose a resolution to the scalability issue of stepwise LSTD-
p^{t}

It accommodates the use-case of more than two but much less than many neighborhoods

This is at the cost of getting a reduced amount of the above-mentioned benefits.

5.1.1

For a system of approximators of the general procedure (Def

Note that

Definition 5.1

When determining the most common states across multiple neighborhood supports,

For the remaining neighborhoods whose supports do not contain the main
s_{\mathrm{ref}}

1.

After applying the above procedure, there may exist neighborhoods whose supports

The variant with TV (‘p02tv’) is motivated by the fact that
TV is a typical metric for determining the mixing time.
We also experiment with an OT-based variant (‘p02ot’) because OT considers
the state distance (as the underlying non-probabilistic metric)

8

We use the OT implementation of
flamary_2021_pot
.
,
making it on par with the MMD variant (‘p02md’).
Moreover, the neighborhood specification based on OT can serve as ground-truth

For OT computation therefore, we use a behavioral state similarity derived from
environment properties, such as transition and reward functions.
It is the
\pi

(
castro_2020_sim
, Thm 2)
,
specifically its state-action counterpart
(
lan_2021_mcrl
, Lemma 7)
.
That is, the distance between two states

and
s^{\prime}
under a policy
\pi
is
given by

d_{\pi}(s,s^{\prime})\coloneqq\max_{a\in\mathcal{A}}|q_{\gamma}^{\pi}(s,a)-q_{\gamma}^{\pi}(s^{\prime},a)|
,
where
q_{\gamma}^{\pi}
is the discounted state-action value of
\pi

(here, the discount factor
\gamma
is set to
0.999
).

9

To our knowledge, there is no behavioral state similarity metric for
non-discounted rewards thus far.

The variant ‘p02md’ relies on Algo

the anchor locations based on the MMD metric.

This mnemonic refers to the proposed stepwise-approximator variant, where

is whether the experiments involve approximation due to sampling
the initial state
S_{0}\sim\mathring{p}
and the next state
S_{t+1}\sim p(\cdot|s_{t},a_{t})

R_{t+1}\coloneqq r(s_{t},a_{t},s_{t+1})

Both share the following common properties (which are feasible to obtain
for environments described in Sec

).

•

The exact gain of a policy is used so that
the effect of our proposed method can be isolated.

•

Each experiment-episode (trial) is run long enough in order to

The maximum timestep in each experiment-episode is set to

\hat{t}_{\mathrm{max}}^{\mathrm{xep}}\leftarrow 10t_{\mathrm{mix}}

In sampling experiments, the scheme ‘buw’ is not feasible because

6.4
Experimental evaluation metrics and protocols

The training of a system of approximators is as follows.
We select one policy uniformly at random from the set of
all stationary deterministic policies, and
sample the random feature values as specified in Sec
6.2
.
Then, we run multiple
n_{\mathrm{xep}}
independent experiment-episodes (trials),
each is with
\hat{t}_{\mathrm{max}}^{\mathrm{xep}}+1
timesteps, as prescribed in Algo
1
.
This training procedure is carried out for each environment and
each approximation scheme (Sec
6.3
).

The quality of a system of approximators is indicated by
the accumulative total error of the square roots of stepwise errors
along one evaluation experiment-episode.
That is,

\varepsilon_{\!x}\coloneqq\sum_{t=0}^{\hat{t}_{\mathrm{max}}^{\mathrm{xep}}}\sqrt{e_{\!x}^{t}(\boldsymbol{w}_{\!t})},\ \text{
where $x$ is either $\mathbb{P}\mathbb{B}$ or $\mathrm{MS}$, and
$\boldsymbol{w}_{\!t}$ is of the neighborhood to which $t$ belongs}.

(30)

We perform evaluations using both stepwise
e_{\mathbb{PB}}^{t}
and
e_{\mathrm{MS}}^{t}
, which utilize
the stepwise state distribution
p^{t}
to weight state-wise errors as in
(
4
) and (
11
), respectively.
In particular, the exact
p^{t}
is used so that there
is no sampling-error in evaluation
(hence, one experiment-episode is sufficient for evaluation).

The use of
e_{\mathbb{PB}}^{t}
and
e_{\mathrm{MS}}^{t}
in (
30
) yields
two evaluation metrics, i.e.
\varepsilon_{\mathbb{PB}}
and
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{MS}}
.
The former
\varepsilon_{\mathbb{PB}}
serves as the gold standard since
e_{\mathbb{PB}}^{t}
is
what the stepwise approximator (‘pinf’) minimizes.
The
\varepsilon_{\mathbb{PB}}
value is computed by plugging-in
the learned (trained) parameter
\boldsymbol{w}_{\!t}
into
the
\tilde{e}_{\mathbb{PB}}
formula (
4
) with
\tilde{p}\leftarrow p^{t}
.
On the other hand, the latter
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{MS}}
is natural whenever
the true value is known (but is never told to the RL agent)
as for the environments described in Sec
6.1
.
For this, we predict the value of every state at every timestep
t
using

\boldsymbol{w}_{\!t}
, apply the prediction offset, i.e.
\hat{v}(s)\leftarrow\boldsymbol{w}_{\!t}^{\intercal}\boldsymbol{f}(s)+o_{t}
,
then plug-in the predicted value
\hat{v}(s)
to
\tilde{e}_{\mathrm{MS}}
formula (
11
)
weighted by
\tilde{p}\leftarrow p^{t}
.

7.1
Non-sampling experimental results

Tables

of ten schemes in six feature-to-state dimensional ratios (Sec

)
and six environments, modelled as unichain MDPs with transient states

From the
\varepsilon_{\mathbb{PB}}
standpoint, the results are as anticipated in that
the lowest error is from the stepwise approximator (pinf), whereas
the second and third lowests are from the maximum number of approximators
(pax
\cdot\ \cdot
) allowed by the feature-to-state dimensional ratios.
More specifically, those with MMD (paxmd) are on par with OT (paxot)
in most cases, where occasionally those with TV (paxtv) become
either the second or third lowest errors (in lieu of paxmd or paxot).

The advantage of having multiple approximators is also obvious based on
\varepsilon_{\mathbb{PB}}
,
especially as the dimensional ratio
\rho
decreases.
Those with a single approximator (i.e. buw and p01) have up to
100-fold larger errors than those with two approximators (p02
\cdot\ \cdot
).
The similar behaviour is also observed between ‘p02
\cdot\ \cdot
’ and
those with even more approximators, i.e. ‘pax
\cdot\ \cdot
’.
Among two-approximator schemes, those with a given
\char 12\relax^{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{1604433}{327680}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\mathrm{max}\mathstrut$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{1604433}{327680}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\mathrm{max}\mathstrut$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{1416433}{327680}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\mathrm{max}\mathstrut$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{1416427}{327680}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{max}\mathstrut$}}}}}}
(p02am) do not
necessarily yield the lowest
\varepsilon_{\mathbb{PB}}
.
This is because the first approximator of p02am may not estimate the values of
the least number of recurrent states, compared to p02tv, p02ot, and p02md.
Recall that for ‘p02
\cdot\ \cdot
’, the first approximator should be devoted,
as much as possible, to estimating transient states.
Some recurrent states however, may already have non-zero probabilities

From the
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{MS}}
standpoint, the stepwise approximator (pinf) achieves
the lowest value or at least, the second lowest in some environments.

\varepsilon_{\mathbb{PB}}

In contrast, the other approximator schemes do not achieve small

\varepsilon_{\mathrm{MS}}

the two-approximator scheme with a given
\char 12\relax^{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{1604433}{327680}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\mathrm{max}\mathstrut$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{1604433}{327680}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\mathrm{max}\mathstrut$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{1416433}{327680}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\mathrm{max}\mathstrut$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{1416427}{327680}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{max}\mathstrut$}}}}}}

Conclusions, limitations, and future works

We propose a system of seminorm LSTD approximators for estimating
the relative bias value from multiple transient and multiple recurrent states
in unichain MDPs.
To this end, we derive an expression for the minimizer of the seminorm LSTD
that enables approximation through sampling (as for model-free RL).
We also devise a general procedure for LSTD-based policy evaluation,
from which the relative value approximator emerges as a special case;
so do the other existing LSTD-based approximators for recurrent MDPs and
unichain MDPs with one 0-reward recurrent state.
Experimental results validate that a system with more approximators yields
the lower projected Bellman errors.
It is also empirically shown that timestep-neighborhoods can reasonably
be specified based on estimating the squared MMD among stepwise state distributions
(using their state samples).

The proposed method addresses the problem of minimizing MSBPE that is based on
the one-step Bellman operator
\mathbb{B}
in (
3
).
It is known that
\mathbb{B}
can be extended to its multi-step variant.
That is,

\boldsymbol{v}=\mathbb{B}^{m}[\boldsymbol{v}]\coloneqq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\sum_{\tau=0}^{m-1}\boldsymbol{P}^{\tau}(\boldsymbol{r}-\boldsymbol{g})}}\right\}+\boldsymbol{P}^{m}\boldsymbol{v},\quad\text{for $m\geq 1$ (where $m=1$ gives the one-step variant)}.

Another known extension is to utilize a weighted average over all
\mathbb{B}^{m}

for
m=1,2,\ldots
, which gives

\boldsymbol{v}=\mathbb{B}_{\!\lambda}[\boldsymbol{v}],\quad\text{where}\ \mathbb{B}_{\!\lambda}\coloneqq(1-\lambda)\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\lambda^{m-1}\mathbb{B}^{m},\ \text{for a trace-decay factor $\lambda\in[0,1)$}.

These two extensions essentially provide a way to control the bias-variance trade-off
in the value approximation.

One way to see this bias-variance trade-off is from
the semi-gradient TD viewpoint as follows.
Setting
m
to
1
leads to the one-step TD algorithm.
As in (
12
), it approximates the true value

v(s_{t})\approx r_{t+1}-g+\hat{v}(s_{t+1})
, which has low variance
(as it involves only one-step next state and reward samples)
but is biased towards the estimator
\hat{v}
.
On the other hand,
m

m
approaches infinity approximates

v(s_{t})\approx(r_{t+1}-g)+(r_{t+2}-g)+\ldots
, which is
unbiased (due to no involvement of
\hat{v}
)
but has high variance (due to an infinitely long sequence of reward samples).
Note that in practice, the gain
g
should also be approximated.

They lead to

\lambda
) and LSTD(
\lambda
) algorithms

(
sutton_1988_td

boyan_2002_lstd
)
.

m

\lambda

Additionally, the (finite) sample complexity of the proposed method deserves
a careful study.
This includes the relationship between the number of neighborhoods and
the number of samples to achieve a certain level of errors.
We anticipate an intricate interplay because a fewer number of neighborhoods
(equivalently, more neighbors per neighborhood) yields more violation to
the i.i.d sample condition in computing the sample means for
the minimizing parameter of the seminorm LSTD (Thm
3.1
).

Lastly, our proposed method can be modified to become
a system of semi-gradient seminorm TD approximators.
This is worth investigating because semi-gradient algorithms involve
an initial parameter value, which is paradoxically beneficial for
policy iteration RL methods in that it can be set to the parameter of
the last policy’s value approximator.
The modification should leverage the fact that
both semi-gradient TD and LSTD algorithms converge to the same TD fixed point
(Sec
2
).
