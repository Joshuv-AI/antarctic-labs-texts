Limiting spectral distribution of large dimensional Spearman’s rank correlation matrices
Zeyu Wu, Cheng Wang

Abstract
In this paper, we study the empirical spectral distribution of Spearman’s rank correlation matrices, under the assumption that the observations are independent and identically distributed random vectors and the features are correlated. We show that the limiting spectral distribution is the generalized Marc̆enko-Pastur law with the covariance matrix of the observation after standardized transformation. With these results, we compare several classical covariance/correlation matrices including the sample covariance matrix, Pearson’s correlation matrix, Kendall’s correlation matrix and Spearman’s correlation matrix.

Introduction

Statistical inference for covariance or correlation matrices are fundamental problems in high dimensional data analysis
[
10
,
7
]
. Recently, rank-based correlation matrices, e.g., Kendall’s tau and Spearman’s rho, have drawn increasing attention in a variety of fields. Due to the robustness of non-parametric statistics, these rank-based correlations have appealing properties in theory and have the potential to solve problems for high dimensional data with complex structure such as heavy-tailed distributions
[
12
]
.

In high dimensional data analysis,
Liu et al. 2012
and
Xue and Zou 2012
firstly used rank-based correlation matrices to conduct sparse estimation for the covariance matrix and the precision matrix (i.e, the inverse of the covariance matrix). For Gaussian distribution, there exists an explicit relationship between rank-based correlation and Pearson’s correlation. Exploiting this neat property,
Liu et al. 2009
extended the Gaussian graphical model to the non-paranormal distribution. Further, there are many works on hypothesis testing of covariance/correlation matrices, which are based on Kendall’s tau correlation matrix
\boldsymbol{\tau}_{n}
or Spearman’s rho correlation matrix
\boldsymbol{\rho}_{n}
. In details,
Bao et al. 2015
derived the asymptotic normal distributions of
\mbox{tr}(\boldsymbol{\rho}_{n}^{k}),~k\in\{2,3,\ldots\}
and conducted several statistics for testing the complete independence.
Han et al. 2017
studied the maximum norm of off-diagonal elements which converges weakly to Gumbel distribution and proposed test statistics based on Kendall’s tau and Spearman’s rho.
Leung and Drton 2018
considered the test statistics based on the squared Frobenius norm and see also
Mao 2017
.

Technically,
Liu et al. 2012
and
Xue and Zou 2012
focused on the sparse estimation of Kendall’s tau and Spearman’s rho. To guarantee the consistency, they need to bound the maximum norm between the empirical and population rank-based correlation matrices, i.e.,
\|\boldsymbol{\rho}_{n}-\boldsymbol{\rho}\|_{\infty}
and
\|\boldsymbol{\tau}_{n}-\boldsymbol{\tau}\|_{\infty}
. To control the maximum norm, the crucial issue is to bound the tail probability of each element uniformly. The main tools are the Hoeffding decomposition for U-statistics and some classical concentration inequalities such as Hoeffding and McDiarmid inequalities. As far as the hypothesis testing, to derive the asymptotic distribution of the test statistics, we need more refined results. Under the null hypothesis of independence, all the features are independent and it is doable to derive the distribution of the test statistics. Under the alternative case, it is challenging to analyze Kendall’s tau or Spearman’s rho since rank variables depend on all the samples. Thus, the existing works
[
6
,
11
,
22
,
16
, e.g.,]
only derived the distribution under the null case.

In random matrix theory, the same challenges also exist.
Bai and Zhou 2008
and
Bandeira et al. 2017
studied the limiting spectral distribution of Spearman’s rho and Kendall’s tau, respectively.
Bao et al. 2015
and
Li et al. 2021b
considered the asymptotic distribution of the test statistics involved Spearman’s rho and Kendall’s tau, respectively.
Bao 2019b
and
Bao 2019a
derived the Tracy–Widom limits for Spearman’s rho and Kendall’s tau, respectively. All these works are based on the independent assumption. For the data with a common covariance structure, the data matrix after ranking does not have independent columns or rows anymore and in random matrix theory, it is challenging to analyze such matrices and see
Bai and Zhou 2008
for more details. Recently, we derived the limiting spectral distribution (LSD) of Kendall’s rank correlation matrix with dependence in
Li et al. 2021a
and it is the first result on rank correlation matrices with dependence. In this paper, as a companion work, we study the limiting spectral distribution of Spearman’s rank correlation matrices. Under mild conditions, we show that the LSD follows the generalized Marc̆enko-Pastur law with a conditional covariance matrix. With these results, we can look insider into Spearman’s correlation matrix, and also its connections with other covariance/correlation matrices.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
, we revisit the Spearman’s rank correlation matrix and provide some trivial results when the data is correlated. In Section
3
, we formulate Spearman’s correlation matrix in the form of U-statistics and we present the main results on LSD in Section
4
. Finally, we provide discussions on several covariance/correlation matrices and also compare their LSDs through numerical simulations in Section
5
. All technical proofs are relegated to Appendix.

2
Spearman’s rank correlation matrix

We first introduce some necessary notation. Throughout the paper,
\mathbf{I}_{m}
is a
m\times m
identity matrix and
\mathbf{1}_{m}
is a
m\times 1
vector with all ones.
\|\cdot\|_{2}
denotes the Frobenius norm of a vector or matrix.
\|\cdot\|
is the spectral norm and
\|\cdot\|_{\infty}
is the element-wise maximum norm of a vector or matrix, respectively.

Given the independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) observations
\mathbf{X}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{X}_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
, we have the data matrix

\displaystyle\mathbf{X}=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{X}_{1}^{\top}\\
\vdots\\
\mathbf{X}_{n}^{\top}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}x_{11}&\cdots&x_{1p}\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
x_{n1}&\cdots&x_{np}\end{pmatrix}=(x_{ij})_{n\times p},

where each row is an observation and each column is a feature. Transforming each column of the data matrix into the order statistics
(r_{ij})_{n\times p}
and normalizing these order statistics, we get the standardized ranking matrix,

\displaystyle\mathbf{R}=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{R}_{1}^{\top}\\
\vdots\\
\mathbf{R}_{n}^{\top}\end{pmatrix}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{{\tiny def}}}}{{=}}\left(\sqrt{\frac{12}{n^{2}-1}}(r_{ij}-\frac{n+1}{2})\right)_{n\times p}.

With the ranking matrix,
Spearman 1904
introduced the famous Spearman’s rank correlation matrix

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\rho}_{n}=\frac{1}{n}\mathbf{R}^{\top}\mathbf{R}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{R}_{i}\mathbf{R}_{i}^{\top}.

(1)

From the statistical point of view, inducing the order statistics makes the correlation more robust to heavy-tailed distributions. From the technical point of view, the ranking violates the independent structure among the rows of the observations. In particular, we have the following proposition.

Proposition 1
.

Assuming
\mathbf{X}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{X}_{n},~i.i.d.\sim N(0,\mathbf{I}_{p})
, we have
\mathbb{E}\{\mathbf{R}^{\top}\mathbf{R}/n\}=\mathbf{I}_{p}
and
\mathbb{E}\{\mathbf{R}\mathbf{R}^{\top}/p\}=(n\mathbf{I}_{n}-\mathbf{1}_{n}\mathbf{1}_{n}^{\top})/(n-1)
.

From Proposition
1
, we can see that when the raw data is with i.i.d. rows, the ranking matrix
\mathbf{R}
does not have independent rows anymore. Since the columns of the data matrix
\mathbf{X}
is also i.i.d., the ranking matrix
\mathbf{R}
has i.i.d. columns and in special, each column
(r_{1j},\ldots,r_{nj})
is uniformly distributed from the permutations of
\{1,\ldots,n\}
.
Bai and Zhou 2008
studied this case and they proved that the LSD of
\boldsymbol{\rho}_{n}
is also the Marc̆enko-Pastur law.

On the other hand, although we assume
\mathbf{X}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{X}_{n},~i.i.d.\sim N(0,\mathbf{I}_{p})
, it actually covers a large class of distributions since the Spearman’s correlation is rank-based. For example, when all the features are independent and continuous, we can transform each feature into a standard normal distribution where the ranking matrix is invariant. It is referred to the monotonic invariance of rank-based correlation matrices introduced by
Weihs et al. 2018
and the non-paranormal model proposed by
Liu et al. 2009
. Thus, the independent case of previous works on Spearman’s correlation matrix
[
2
,
6
,
5
, e.g.,]
can be formulated as
\mathbf{X}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{X}_{n},~i.i.d.~\sim N(0,\mathbf{I}_{p})
.

Assuming that the data matrix is with i.i.d. entries is very limited. A natural extension is to consider general covariance structures. For the multivariate normal distribution with general
\Sigma
, we have the following result.

Proposition 2
.

Assuming
\mathbf{X}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{X}_{n},~i.i.d.\sim N(\mathbf{0},\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$})
, where
\Sigma_{ii}=1,~i\in\{1,\ldots,p\}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\{\frac{1}{n}\mathbf{R}^{\top}\mathbf{R}\}=\frac{3}{n+1}\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}_{1}+\frac{3(n-2)}{n+1}\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}_{2},~\mathbb{E}\{\frac{1}{p}\mathbf{R}\mathbf{R}^{\top}\}=\frac{n}{n-1}(\mathbf{I}_{n}-\frac{1}{n}\mathbf{1}_{n}\mathbf{1}_{n}^{\top}),

where

\displaystyle\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}_{1}=\left(\frac{2}{\pi}\arcsin(\Sigma_{ij})\right)_{p\times p}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{{\tiny def}}}}{{=}}\frac{2}{\pi}\arcsin(\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}),~\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}_{2}=\left(\frac{2}{\pi}\arcsin(\Sigma_{ij}/2)\right)_{p\times p}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{{\tiny def}}}}{{=}}\frac{2}{\pi}\arcsin(\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}/2).

From Proposition
2
, we can see that the rows and columns of the ranking matrix
\mathbf{R}
are both dependent if the data has a general covariance structure
\Sigma
. From the perspective of random matrix theory, it is challenging to analyze such matrices.

3
U-statistic of Spearman’s correlation

For each feature
j\in\{1,\ldots,p\}
, we invoke the empirical cumulative distribution function

\displaystyle\hat{F}_{j}(x)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}I(x_{ij}\leq x),

and the order statistics are

\displaystyle r_{ij}=n\cdot\hat{F}_{j}(x_{ij})=1+\sum_{k\neq i}I(x_{kj}\leq x_{ij}),~i\in\{1,\ldots,n\},~j\in\{1,\ldots,p\}.

Throughout the paper, we consider the continuous population distribution and assume that there is no ties in data matrix.

Defining the sign vector

\displaystyle\mathbf{A}_{ik}=\mbox{sign}\left(\mathbf{X}_{i}-\mathbf{X}_{k}\right)=\left(\mbox{sign}\left(x_{i1}-x_{k1}\right),\ldots,\mbox{sign}\left(x_{ip}-x_{kp}\right)\right)^{\top},

we have

\displaystyle r_{ij}-\frac{n+1}{2}=1+\sum_{k\neq i}\frac{1+\mbox{sign}\left(x_{ij}-x_{kj}\right)}{2}-\frac{n+1}{2}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k\neq i}\mbox{sign}\left(x_{ij}-x_{kj}\right).

In vector form, we can get

\displaystyle\mathbf{R}_{i}=

\displaystyle\sqrt{\frac{12}{n^{2}-1}}\begin{pmatrix}r_{i1}-\frac{n+1}{2}\\
\vdots\\
r_{ip}-\frac{n+1}{2}\end{pmatrix}=\sqrt{\frac{3}{n^{2}-1}}\sum_{k\neq i}\mathbf{A}_{ik}.

(2)

With these notations, we can rewrite the Spearman’s rank correlation matrix as follows

\displaystyle\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\rho}}_{n}=\frac{3}{n(n^{2}-1)}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left\{\sum_{k_{1},k_{2}\neq i}\mathbf{A}_{ik_{1}}\mathbf{A}_{ik_{2}}^{\top}\right\}.

(3)

Following the definition of U-statistics, we can divide the summation into two parts and each part is a U-statistic.

Proposition 3
.

Assuming the observations
\mathbf{X}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{X}_{n}
have no ties, the Spearman’s correlation matrix can be defined by

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\rho}_{n}=\frac{3}{n(n^{2}-1)}\sum_{i,j}^{*}\mathbf{A}_{ij}\mathbf{A}_{ij}^{\top}+\frac{3}{n(n^{2}-1)}\sum_{i,j,k}^{*}\mathbf{A}_{ij}\mathbf{A}_{ik}^{\top},

(4)

where
\sum^{*}
denotes summation over mutually different indices.

When
\mathbf{X}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{X}_{n}
are i.i.d. from a population with absolutely continuous densities, by Proposition
3
, we can see

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(\boldsymbol{\rho}_{n})=

\displaystyle\frac{3}{n(n^{2}-1)}n(n-1)\cdot\mbox{cov}\left(\mathbf{A}_{12}\right)+\frac{3}{n(n^{2}-1)}n(n-1)(n-2)\cdot\mbox{cov}\left(\mathbf{A}_{1}\right)=\frac{3}{n+1}\mbox{cov}\left(\mathbf{A}_{12}\right)+\frac{3(n-2)}{n+1}\mbox{cov}\left(\mathbf{A}_{1}\right),

where

\displaystyle\mathbf{A}_{i}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{{\tiny def}}}}{{=}}\mathbb{E}\{\mathbf{A}_{ik}\mid\mathbf{X}_{i}\}.

(5)

The population Spearman’s correlation matrix consists of two covariance matrices
\mbox{cov}\left(\mathbf{A}_{12}\right)
and
\mbox{cov}\left(\mathbf{A}_{1}\right)
. For the classical sample covariance matrix

\displaystyle\mathbf{S}_{n}=\frac{1}{2n(n-1)}\sum_{i,j}^{*}(\mathbf{X}_{i}-\mathbf{X}_{j})(\mathbf{X}_{i}-\mathbf{X}_{j})^{\top},

we know
\mbox{cov}(\mathbf{X}_{i}-\mathbf{X}_{j})=2\mbox{cov}(\mathbf{X}_{i})
. Thus, the
sign
function introduces a non-linear correlation into rank-based correlation matrices and these two covariance matrices are different, e.g.,

\displaystyle\mbox{cov}\left(\mathbf{A}_{12}\right)=\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}_{1}=\frac{2}{\pi}\arcsin(\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}),~\mbox{and}~\mbox{cov}\left(\mathbf{A}_{1}\right)=\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}_{2}=\frac{2}{\pi}\arcsin(\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}/2),

for the multivariate normal distribution
N(\mathbf{0},\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$})
.

Since both
\mbox{cov}\left(\mathbf{A}_{12}\right)
and
\mbox{cov}\left(\mathbf{A}_{1}\right)
can describe correlations among features,
an intuitive way is to conduct U-statistics for these two covariance matrices separately, e.g.,

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\tau}_{n}=\frac{1}{n(n-1)}\sum_{i,j}^{*}\mathbf{A}_{ij}\mathbf{A}_{ij}^{\top},

(6)

and

\displaystyle\tilde{\boldsymbol{\rho}}_{n}=\frac{3}{n(n-1)(n-2)}\sum_{i,j,k}^{*}\mathbf{A}_{ij}\mathbf{A}_{ik}^{\top}.

(7)

Interestingly,
\boldsymbol{\tau}_{n}
is exactly Kendall’s rank correlation matrix proposed by
[
15
]
and
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\rho}}_{n}
is an improved Spearman’s rank correlation matrix proposed by
[
13
]
. See also Example 3 of
[
11
]
. Our recent work
[
17
]
studied the LSD of
\boldsymbol{\tau}_{n}
and in this work, we consider Spearman’s correlation matrices
\boldsymbol{\rho}_{n}
and
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\rho}}_{n}
.

4
Limiting spectral distribution

For an
n\times n
Hermitian matrix
\mathbf{H}_{n}
whose eigenvalues are
\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{n}
, the empirical spectral distribution of
\mathbf{H}_{n}
is defined as

\displaystyle F^{\mathbf{H}_{n}}(x)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}I(\lambda_{i}\leq x).

The limit of
F^{\mathbf{H}_{n}}
is called the limiting spectral distribution of
\mathbf{H}_{n}
. In random matrix theory, the LSD is usually defined by its Stieltjes transform

\displaystyle s_{F}(z)=\int\frac{1}{x-z}dF(x),~z\in\mathbb{C}^{+},

where
\mathbb{C}^{+}
denotes the upper complex plane. With a Stieltjes transform
s(z)
, the distribution function can be obtained by the inversion formula

\displaystyle F(b)-F(a)=\lim_{\nu\to 0^{+}}\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{a}^{b}\Im s_{F}(x+i\nu)dx,

where
\Im(\cdot)
is the imaginary part and
i
is the imaginary unit.

Note

\displaystyle\tilde{\boldsymbol{\rho}}_{n}=\frac{3}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left\{\frac{1}{(n-1)(n-2)}\sum_{j,k\neq i}^{*}\mathbf{A}_{ij}\mathbf{A}_{ik}^{\top}\right\}

and for a given
i
,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(n-1)(n-2)}\sum_{j,k\neq i}^{*}\mathbf{A}_{ij}\mathbf{A}_{ik}^{\top}

is also a U-statistic for
\mathbf{A}_{i}\mathbf{A}_{i}^{\top}
. Intuitively, the improved Spearman’s rank correlation matrix
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\rho}}_{n}
is close to the random matrix

\displaystyle\mathbf{W}_{n}=\frac{3}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{A}_{i}\mathbf{A}_{i}^{\top}.

(8)

The following result shows that
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\rho}}_{n}
and
\mathbf{W}_{n}
share the same limiting spectral distribution.

Theorem 1
.

Assuming
\mathbf{X}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{X}_{n},~i.i.d.\sim N(\mathbf{0},\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$})
where
\Sigma_{ii}=1,~i\in\{1,\ldots,p\}
, and if

\mathrm{(i)}

p/n\to y\in(0,\infty)
;

\mathrm{(ii)}

\mbox{tr}\left(\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}^{2}\right)=o(p^{2})
;

we have

\displaystyle L\left(F^{\tilde{\boldsymbol{\rho}}_{n}},F^{\mathbf{W}_{n}}\right)\to 0,~\text{in probability},

(9)

where
L(\cdot,\cdot)
is the Levy distance between two distribution functions.

For Spearman’s rank correlation matrix
\boldsymbol{\rho}_{n}
, by Proposition
3
, we know

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\rho}_{n}=\frac{3}{n+1}\boldsymbol{\tau}_{n}+\frac{n-2}{n+1}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\rho}}_{n}=\frac{3}{n+1}\boldsymbol{\tau}_{n}-\frac{3}{n+1}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\rho}}_{n}+\tilde{\boldsymbol{\rho}}_{n},

where
\boldsymbol{\tau}_{n}
is the Kendall’s rank correlation matrix (
6
). We claim that
\boldsymbol{\rho}_{n}
and
\mathbf{W}_{n}
also share the same limiting spectral distribution. The following result establishes this claim.

Proposition 4

(Weak convergence)
.

Under the assumptions of Theorem
1
, we have

\displaystyle L\left(F^{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{n}},F^{\mathbf{W}_{n}}\right)\to 0,~\text{in probability}.

(10)

The proofs of Theorem
1
and Proposition
4
are based on Corollary A.41 of
Bai and Silverstein 2010
and we need to show

\mathbb{E}\left\|\tilde{\boldsymbol{\rho}}_{n}-\mathbf{W}_{n}\right\|_{2}^{2}=o(p)
and
\mathbb{E}\left\|\boldsymbol{\rho}_{n}-\mathbf{W}_{n}\right\|_{2}^{2}=o(p)
. To further refine the conclusion, e.g., to show the strong convergence, we need to prove
\left\|\tilde{\boldsymbol{\rho}}_{n}-\mathbf{W}_{n}\right\|_{2}^{2}/p\to 0,a.s.
which is challenging since the calculation of higher order moments of
\mathbf{A}_{i}
is hard. Here, we turn to the ranking statistics
\mathbf{R}
. Intuitively,

\displaystyle\mathbf{R}=\left(\sqrt{\frac{12}{n^{2}-1}}(r_{ij}-\frac{n+1}{2})\right)_{n\times p}=\sqrt{\frac{12n^{2}}{n^{2}-1}}\left(\hat{F}_{j}(x_{ij})-\frac{n+1}{2n}\right)_{n\times p}\approx 2\sqrt{3}\left(F_{j}(x_{ij})-\frac{1}{2}\right)_{n\times p}=\sqrt{3}(\mathbf{A}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{A}_{n})^{\top}.

Thus, we can control the difference between
F^{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{n}}
and
F^{\mathbf{W}_{n}}
by bounding
\mathbf{R}-\sqrt{3}(\mathbf{A}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{A}_{n})^{\top}
. Specifically, we will use Corollary A.42 of
Bai and Silverstein 2010
, i.e.,

\displaystyle L^{4}\left(F^{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{n}},F^{\mathbf{W}_{n}}\right)\leq

\displaystyle\frac{2\mbox{tr}\left(\boldsymbol{\rho}_{n}+\mathbf{W}_{n}\right)}{np^{2}}\|\mathbf{R}-\sqrt{3}(\mathbf{A}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{A}_{n})^{\top}\|_{2}^{2}

and then we can show the strong convergence as follows.

Theorem 2

(Strong convergence)
.

Assuming
\mathbf{X}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{X}_{n}
are i.i.d. continuous random vectors and
p/n\to y\in(0,\infty)
, we have

\displaystyle L\left(F^{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{n}},F^{\mathbf{W}_{n}}\right)\to 0,~\text{almost surely}.

(11)

It is noted that Theorem
2
provides a stronger conclusion with a weaker condition and the key technical tool is the Dvoretzky–Kiefer–Wolfowitz inequality for empirical cumulative distribution functions. By Proposition
4
or Theorem
2
, to study the LSD of
\boldsymbol{\rho}_{n}
, we can consider the matrix
\mathbf{W}_{n}
which is the sample covariance matrix of the random vectors
\sqrt{3}(\mathbf{A}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{A}_{n})
. It is noted that
\mathbf{A}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{A}_{n}
are i.i.d., and the LSD can be derived from classical results on sample covariance matrices, e.g., Theorem 1 of
Bai and Zhou 2008
. Assuming
\mathbf{X}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{X}_{n},~i.i.d.\sim N(\mathbf{0},\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$})
where
\Sigma_{ii}=1
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbf{A}_{i}=\mathbb{E}\{\mathbf{A}_{ij}\mid\mathbf{X}_{i}\}=\begin{pmatrix}2\Phi(x_{i1})-1\\
\vdots\\
2\Phi(x_{ip})-1\\
\end{pmatrix}.

Here
\Phi(\cdot)
is the cumulative distribution function of
N(0,1)
. Therefore, each entry of
(\mathbf{A}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{A}_{n})
follows the uniform distribution
U[-1,1]
and

\displaystyle\mbox{cov}(\mathbf{A}_{i})=\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}_{2}=\frac{2}{\pi}\arcsin(\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}/2).

Based on the main result of
Bai and Zhou 2008
, we can derive the LSD of Spearman’s rank correlation matrix as follows.

Theorem 3
.

Assume
\mathbf{X}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{X}_{n},~i.i.d.\sim N(\mathbf{0},\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$})
where
\Sigma_{ii}=1,~i\in\{1,\ldots,p\}
, and

\mathrm{(i)}

p/n\to y\in(0,\infty)
as
n\to\infty
;

\mathrm{(ii)}

the spectral norm of
\Sigma
is uniformly bounded by a constant
C
;

\mathrm{(iii)}

the empirical spectral distribution of
6\arcsin(\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}/2)/\pi
tends to a non-random probability distribution
H
.

Then, with probability 1,
F^{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{n}}
tends to a probability distribution, whose Stieltjes transform
m=m(z),~z\in\mathbb{C}^{+}
satisfies

\displaystyle m=\int\frac{1}{t(1-y-yzm)-z}dH(t).

(12)

Noting the Spearman’s correlation matrix is rank-based, Theorem
3
actually holds for the non-paranormal distribution proposed by
Liu et al. 2009
. In particular, for a random vector
\mathbf{Z}=(z_{1},\ldots,z_{p})^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
, there exist monotone functions
\left\{f_{j}\right\}_{j=1}^{p}
such that
\left(f_{1}(z_{1}),\ldots,f_{p}(z_{p})\right)\sim N(\mathbf{0},\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$})
where
\Sigma_{ii}=1,~i\in\{1,\ldots,p\}
. Denoting the cumulative distribution function of
z_{j}
as
F_{j}
,
Liu et al. 2009
show that
f_{j}(t)=\Phi^{-1}(F_{j}(t))
and the non-paranormal distribution model actually assumes

\displaystyle\left(\Phi^{-1}(F_{1}(z_{1})),\ldots,\Phi^{-1}(F_{p}(z_{p}))\right)\sim N(\mathbf{0},\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}).

We can see that the non-paranormal distribution extends the multivariate normal distribution and more discussions can be found in
Lu et al. 2021
. More generally, we can further extend the assumption to general distributions with some moment conditions and it is referred to
Li et al. 2021a
for more details. To illustrate the robustness of Spearman’s rank correlation, we consider a toy example where each element follows the Cauchy distribution.

Example 1
.

For the data matrix

\displaystyle\mathbf{X}=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{X}_{1}^{\top}\\
\vdots\\
\mathbf{X}_{n}^{\top}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}x_{11}&\cdots&x_{1p}\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
x_{n1}&\cdots&x_{np}\end{pmatrix}=(x_{ij})_{n\times p},

where
x_{ij}
follows the standard Cauchy distribution, we consider the monotone transformation

\displaystyle x_{ij}\mapsto\Phi^{-1}\left(\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{\pi}\arctan(x_{ij})\right)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{{\tiny def}}}}{{=}}y_{ij}.

As we know, the transformation does not change the ranking matrix
\mathbf{R}
and to analyze
\mathbf{R}
, we can make assumptions on
y_{ij}
without loss of generality. For the independent case, i.e.,
x_{i1},\ldots,x_{ip}
are independent, we have
(y_{i1},\ldots,y_{ip})^{\top}\sim N(0,\mathbf{I})
. For the dependent case, we need further assumptions to define the correlation structure. Noting
y_{ij}\sim N(0,1)
, a natural way is to assume

\displaystyle(y_{i1},\ldots,y_{ip})\sim N(\mathbf{0},\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$})

(13)

where
\Sigma_{ii}=1,~i\in\{1,\ldots,p\}
. This is exactly the non-paranormal distribution and
\Sigma
is called the latent generalized correlation matrix by
Lu et al. 2021
. In summary, we have the relationships

\displaystyle\text{Spearman's correlation}~(x_{ij},x_{ik})=\text{Spearman's correlation}~(y_{ij},y_{ik})=\frac{2}{\pi}\arcsin\left(\text{Pearson's correlation}~(y_{ij},y_{ik})\right),

where the second equality is due to the non-paranormal distribution assumption (
13
).

5
Discussions and simulations

In this section, we compare several important covariance/correlation matrices. Given the data
\mathbf{X}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{X}_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
, there are four classical covariance/correlation matrices in statistical applications:

•

Sample covariance matrix:

\displaystyle\mathbf{S}_{n}=\frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\mathbf{X}_{i}-\bar{\mathbf{X}})(\mathbf{X}_{i}-\bar{\mathbf{X}})^{\top}=\frac{1}{2n(n-1)}\sum_{i,j}^{*}(\mathbf{X}_{i}-\mathbf{X}_{j})(\mathbf{X}_{i}-\mathbf{X}_{j})^{\top}=\frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{X}_{i}\mathbf{X}_{i}^{\top}-\frac{n}{n-1}\bar{\mathbf{X}}\bar{\mathbf{X}}^{\top},

where
\bar{\mathbf{X}}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{X}_{i}
;

•

Pearson’s correlation matrix
[
24
]
:

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}_{n}=\{\mbox{diag}(\mathbf{S}_{n})\}^{-1/2}\mathbf{S}_{n}\{\mbox{diag}(\mathbf{S}_{n})\}^{-1/2},

where
\mbox{diag}(\cdot)
is a diagonal matrix with the diagonal entries of the matrix;

•

Kendall’s correlation matrix
[
15
]
:

Proof of Lemma

Firstly, it is easy to see
\mathbb{E}(\mathbf{A}_{12}\mathbf{A}_{13}^{\top})=\mathbb{E}(\mathbf{A}_{12}\mathbf{A}_{1}^{\top})=\mathbb{E}(\mathbf{A}_{1}\mathbf{A}_{1}^{\top})=\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}_{2}
,
which yields
\mathbb{E}(\mathbf{A}_{12}^{\top}\mathbf{A}_{13})=\mathbb{E}(\mathbf{A}_{12}^{\top}\mathbf{A}_{1})=\mathbb{E}(\mathbf{A}_{1}^{\top}\mathbf{A}_{1})=\mbox{tr}(\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}_{2})=p/3
. To derive the inequality of variances, we use the Hoeffding decomposition

\displaystyle\mbox{var}(\mathbf{A}_{12}^{\top}\mathbf{A}_{13})=\mathbb{E}\{\mathbf{A}_{12}^{\top}\mathbf{A}_{13}-\mbox{tr}(\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}_{2})\}^{2}=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\{\mathbf{A}_{12}^{\top}\mathbf{A}_{13}-\mathbf{A}_{12}^{\top}\mathbf{A}_{1}+\mathbf{A}_{12}^{\top}\mathbf{A}_{1}-\mbox{tr}(\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}_{2})\}^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle~\mathbb{E}(\mathbf{A}_{12}^{\top}\mathbf{A}_{13}-\mathbf{A}_{12}^{\top}\mathbf{A}_{1})^{2}+\mbox{var}(\mathbf{A}_{12}^{\top}\mathbf{A}_{1})+2\mathbb{E}(\mathbf{A}_{12}^{\top}\mathbf{A}_{13}-\mathbf{A}_{12}^{\top}\mathbf{A}_{1}){(\mathbf{A}_{12}^{\top}\mathbf{A}_{1}-\mbox{tr}(\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}_{2}))}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle~\mathbb{E}(\mathbf{A}_{12}^{\top}\mathbf{A}_{13}-\mathbf{A}_{12}^{\top}\mathbf{A}_{1})^{2}+\mbox{var}(\mathbf{A}_{12}^{\top}\mathbf{A}_{1})\geq\mbox{var}(\mathbf{A}_{12}^{\top}\mathbf{A}_{1}),

and similarly

\displaystyle\mbox{var}(\mathbf{A}_{12}^{\top}\mathbf{A}_{1})=

\displaystyle~\mathbb{E}(\mathbf{A}_{12}^{\top}\mathbf{A}_{1}-\mathbf{A}_{1}^{\top}\mathbf{A}_{1})^{2}+\mbox{var}(\mathbf{A}_{1}^{\top}\mathbf{A}_{1})+2\mathbb{E}(\mathbf{A}_{12}^{\top}\mathbf{A}_{1}-\mathbf{A}_{1}^{\top}\mathbf{A}_{1})(\mathbf{A}_{1}^{\top}\mathbf{A}_{1}-\mbox{tr}(\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}_{2}))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle~\mathbb{E}(\mathbf{A}_{12}^{\top}\mathbf{A}_{1}-\mathbf{A}_{1}^{\top}\mathbf{A}_{1})^{2}+\mbox{var}(\mathbf{A}_{1}^{\top}\mathbf{A}_{1})\geq\mbox{var}(\mathbf{A}_{1}^{\top}\mathbf{A}_{1}).

The calculation of

. The proof of Lemma

\displaystyle\mbox{tr}\left(\frac{1}{n}\tilde{\mathbf{R}}^{\top}\tilde{\mathbf{R}}\right)\leq p\|\tilde{\mathbf{R}}\|^{2}_{\infty}\leq\frac{12n^{2}p}{n^{2}-1}\left(\frac{n+1}{2n}\right)^{2}\leq 9p,~(n\geq 2).

Noting

\displaystyle\frac{1}{np}\|\mathbf{R}-\tilde{\mathbf{R}}\|_{2}^{2}\leq\frac{12n^{2}}{n^{2}-1}\max_{i,j}|\hat{F}_{j}(x_{ij})-F_{j}(x_{ij})|^{2}\leq\frac{12n^{2}}{n^{2}-1}\max_{j}\left\{\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}}|\hat{F}_{j}(x)-F_{j}(x)|^{2}\right\}=\frac{12n^{2}}{n^{2}-1}\left\{\max_{j}\|\hat{F}_{j}(x)-F_{j}(x)\|_{\infty}\right\}^{2},

we need to control
\|\hat{F}_{j}(x)-F_{j}(x)\|_{\infty},~j\in\{1,\ldots,p\}
simultaneously. Thus, we shall use the Dvoretzky–Kiefer–Wolfowitz (DKW) inequality which provides a refined result of the classical Glivenko–Cantelli Theorem. By DKW inequality,
\forall\epsilon>0
,

\displaystyle\Pr\left(\|\hat{F}_{j}(x)-F_{j}(x)\|_{\infty}\geq\epsilon\right)\leq 2e^{-2n\epsilon^{2}}

which yields

\displaystyle\Pr\left(\max_{j}\|\hat{F}_{j}(x)-F_{j}(x)\|_{\infty}\geq\epsilon\right)\leq\sum_{j=1}^{p}\Pr\left(\|\hat{F}_{j}(x)-F_{j}(x)\|_{\infty}\geq\epsilon\right)\leq 2pe^{-2n\epsilon^{2}}.

Since
p/n\to y
, we have
2pe^{-n\epsilon^{2}}\leq 1
for large enough
n
and then

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}2pe^{-2n\epsilon^{2}}=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\{2pe^{-n\epsilon^{2}}\}\cdot e^{-n\epsilon^{2}}<\infty.

By the Borel–Cantelli Lemma, we obtain

\displaystyle\max_{j}\|\hat{F}_{j}(x)-F_{j}(x)\|_{\infty}\to 0,~\text{almost surely},

and then

\displaystyle L\left(F^{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{n}},F^{\frac{1}{n}\tilde{\mathbf{R}}^{\top}\tilde{\mathbf{R}}}\right)\to 0,~\text{almost surely}.

(20)

Next, we bound the difference between
\tilde{\mathbf{R}}^{\top}\tilde{\mathbf{R}}/n
and
\mathbf{W}_{n}=3\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{A}_{i}\mathbf{A}_{i}^{\top}/n
. Noting

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathbf{R}}=\sqrt{\frac{12n^{2}}{n^{2}-1}}\left(F_{j}(x_{ij})-\frac{n+1}{2n}\right)_{n\times p}

and

\displaystyle\sqrt{3}(\mathbf{A}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{A}_{n})^{\top}=\sqrt{12}\left(F_{j}(x_{ij})-\frac{1}{2}\right)_{n\times p},

we can get

\displaystyle\left\|\tilde{\mathbf{R}}-\sqrt{3}(\mathbf{A}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{A}_{n})^{\top}\right\|_{\infty}\leq 2\sqrt{3}\left|\sqrt{\frac{n^{2}}{n^{2}-1}}-1\right|+\sqrt{3}\left|\frac{n+1}{\sqrt{n^{2}-1}}-1\right|\to 0.

Using Corollary A.42 of
Bai and Silverstein 2010
again, we have
L^{4}\left(F^{\mathbf{W}_{n}},F^{\frac{1}{n}\tilde{\mathbf{R}}^{\top}\tilde{\mathbf{R}}}\right)\to 0
. Combined with (
20
), we conclude that

\displaystyle L\left(F^{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{n}},F^{\mathbf{W}_{n}}\right)\to 0,~\text{almost surely}.

The proof is completed.

\square

Proof of Theorem
3

Since
\mathbf{A}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{A}_{n}
are i.i.d., we can apply Theorem 1.1 of
Bai and Zhou 2008
. It is sufficient to check assumption 1 of their theorem, i.e., for any non-random
p\times p
matrix
\mathbf{B}
,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{p^{2}}\mbox{var}\left(\mathbf{A}_{1}^{\top}\mathbf{B}\mathbf{A}_{1}\right)\to 0.

Noting
\mathbf{A}_{i}=2\Phi(\mathbf{X}_{i})-1
where
\Phi(\cdot)
is differentiable, the Gaussian Poincaré inequality together with
\|\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}\|\leq C
can yield the above conclusion. A detailed proof can be found in Lemma 3.3 of
[
17
]
. The proof is completed.

\square
