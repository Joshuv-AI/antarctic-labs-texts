Quantum Mixed State Compiling
Nic Ezzell, Elliott M. Ball, Aliza U. Siddiqui, Mark M. Wilde, Andrew T. Sornborger, Patrick J. Coles, Zoë Holmes

Abstract
The task of learning a quantum circuit to prepare a given mixed state is a fundamental quantum subroutine. We present a variational quantum algorithm (VQA) to learn mixed states which is suitable for near-term hardware. Our algorithm represents a generalization of previous VQAs that aimed at learning preparation circuits for pure states. We consider two different ansätze for compiling the target state; the first is based on learning a purification of the state and the second on representing it as a convex combination of pure states. In both cases, the resources required to store and manipulate the compiled state grow with the rank of the approximation. Thus, by learning a lower rank approximation of the target state, our algorithm provides a means of compressing a state for more efficient processing. As a byproduct of our algorithm, one effectively learns the principal components of the target state, and hence our algorithm further provides a new method for principal component analysis. We investigate the efficacy of our algorithm through extensive numerical implementations, showing that typical random states and thermal states of many body systems may be learnt this way. Additionally, we demonstrate on quantum hardware how our algorithm can be used to study hardware noise-induced states.



I
Introduction

The task of learning an unknown
d\times d
quantum state
\rho
is a fundamental primitive for quantum computing. The well known method of full quantum state tomography learns the matrix elements of
\rho
directly. The original scheme employs
\mathcal{O}(d^{2}/\epsilon^{2})
Pauli measurements to learn the state up to additive error
\epsilon
in trace distance
Nielsen and Chuang 2000
. Subsequent refinements require only
\mathcal{O}((\text{rank}(\rho)d/\epsilon^{2})\cdot\ln(d/\epsilon))
measurements
Haah et al. 2017
, or
\mathcal{O}(d/\epsilon^{2})
allowing for a small failure probability
O’Donnell and Wright 2016
. A recent improvement has found it is necessary to use at least
\Omega(\text{rank}(\rho)d/\epsilon)
measurements, and it was also conjectured to be sufficient
Yuen 2022
. In all cases, tomography aims to obtain a classical description of a quantum state, and as such, the number of measurements it requires scales exponentially with the number of qubits of the target state.

An alternative approach is to give a more operational meaning to learning. In practice, we are often not interested in the exact form of
\rho
but instead in its properties; i.e., we wish to estimate
o_{i}\coloneqq{\rm Tr}[\rho O_{i}]
for some observable
O_{i}
. Ref.
Aaronson 2019
proves that if each observable
O_{i}
, for
1\leqslant i\leqslant M
, is restricted to two-outcome measurements, then only
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(\epsilon^{-4}\log^{4}M\log d)
copies of
\rho
are sufficient to estimate each
o_{i}
up to additive error
\epsilon
by using a method called
shadow tomography

Huang et al. 2020
;
Elben et al. 2022
;
Huang et al. 2022
.
These results were expanded to simpler, experimentally tractable Clifford measurements and random Pauli measurements in Refs.
Huang et al. 2020
;
Paini et al. 2021
. In particular, it was shown that a polynomial number of Clifford (Pauli) measurements is sufficient to estimate any low rank (local) observable. The polynomial scaling achieved by shadow tomography is gained at the cost of providing only a partial description of the quantum state.

Yet another approach to quantum state learning, and the one we take here, is instead to solve the problem of state
compilation
. This involves learning a quantum circuit with which we may prepare an approximation of the target state. In this paper, we propose and demonstrate a near-term algorithm for approximately compiling an unknown quantum state by variationally training a parameterized quantum circuit. Similar to the shadow tomography case, one can use the final cost function value in our algorithm to place bounds on the deviation of observables estimated from the learnt state from those of the target state.

While the variational compilation of pure states has been explored in Refs.
Khatri et al. 2019
;
Jones and Benjamin 2022
;
Sharma et al. 2020
, here we focus on mixed state compilation. Specifically, we propose two different ansätze that can compile a mixed state either into a purification using ancilla or as a convex combination of pure states. The latter is similar to the Hamiltonian-based model approach in Ref.
Verdon et al. 2019
. In addition, our approach shares similarities with Refs.
LaRose et al. 2019
;
Cerezo et al. 2022
, which present algorithms to learn the diagonalization of a target state, but in contrast to our approach, these methods only apply to low-rank quantum states.

Quantum state compilation, in providing a quantum circuit description of a state, serves a different purpose from tomography or classical shadows, as summarized in Fig.
1
. In the first case, it may be used to ‘upload’ an unknown state of an experimental quantum system to a quantum computer. In other words, given a quantum system in an unknown target mixed state, our algorithms can learn a circuit representation of it for implementation on any digital quantum device. In this case, we can use the compilation to more efficiently store the state for later processing, compute properties of the state that are hard to measure directly, or use the state as the input to another algorithm, say for quantum simulation. Alternatively, a circuit to prepare the target state might already be known, but the aim would be to learn a more efficient (i.e., shorter depth and/or more noise resilient) circuit to prepare that state. These applications are particularly interesting when the unknown mixed state is generated by unknown noise as in (b). In this case, the compilation serves as a snapshot of how a noisy quantum computer corrupted a desired input state. This snapshot can then be used later or perhaps even on a more coherent machine for reliable processing.

In some cases, it may be of interest not to learn the target state perfectly but rather attempt to learn a low(er) rank approximation of it. In general, the resources required to manipulate a quantum state on quantum hardware grow with its rank. Thus, learning a lower rank approximation provides a means of compressing a state to store it more efficiently. The compression of quantum data has a long history tracing back to the early days of quantum information theory
Schumacher 1995
;
Cleve and DiVincenzo 1996
;
Nielsen and Chuang 2000
. More recently, the question of how well any given state may be approximated using a lower rank state was addressed analytically in Ref.
Ezzell et al. 2022
for the Hilbert–Schmidt and trace distances. Interestingly, the optimal lower rank approximation essentially corresponds to performing principal component analysis (PCA) (jump to Eq. (
3
) to look ahead) for the Hilbert–Schmidt distance (but not for trace distance). Hence, as we will show, one can also use our learned compilation to perform PCA with a desired cut-off rank. At the same time, the analytical expression for the optimal state from this work provides a natural benchmark for our learning task.

Our algorithm, which is summarized in Fig.
2
, involves variationally minimizing a cost function that is formulated in terms of the Hilbert–Schmidt distance between the target state and ansatz state. This cost can be efficiently measured using either a SWAP test or a Loschmidt echo circuit. In contrast to Ref.
Lloyd and Weedbrook 2018
, which sketches a quantum generative adversarial neural network that might be used to learn a mixed state, we further present local variants of our cost functions to mitigate the trainability barrier posed by barren plateaus
McClean et al. 2018
;
Cerezo et al. 2021
;
Holmes et al. 2021a
;
Holmes et al. 2022
;
Arrasmith et al. 2022
;
Larocca et al. 2022a
;
Sharma et al. 2022
;
Patti et al. 2021
;
Thanasilp et al. 2021
;
Uvarov and Biamonte 2021
;
Marrero et al. 2021
.

In the numerical simulations of our proposed algorithm, we demonstrate the applicability of both the purification and convex combination ansätze for learning full and lower rank approximations of a target mixed state. In particular, we numerically simulate the learning of typical random states, as well as of random thermal states of the Heisenberg XY model. We additionally implement our algorithm on quantum hardware in order to compile an unknown state generated by hardware noise.

II
Quantum Mixed State Compiling Algorithm

The Quantum Mixed State Compiling (QMSC) algorithm takes as input a mixed state
\rho
and a desired approximation rank
R
. Though not necessary, we assume
R\leqslant\text{rank}(\rho)\eqqcolon r
in defining our algorithm since this is both sensible and simplifies our discussion. In our algorithm, we take this a step further and constrain
R\leqslant r_{\epsilon}
, where
r_{\epsilon}
is the
\epsilon
-rank
1
1

1

We note that the notion of
\epsilon
-rank was also used in Ref.
Cerezo et al. 2020
, but its formal definition is slightly different than the one we choose to use here. A smooth version of the max-entropy (or
\alpha=0
Renyi entropy) provides yet another definition for
\epsilon
-rank that is distinct from ours
Renner and Wolf 2004
;
Renner 2005
.
of
\rho
, which counts the number of eigenvalues greater than
\epsilon>0
. This notion of approximate rank is designed to capture the intuition that the contribution of very small but non-zero eigenvalues can often in practice be ignored. The goal then is to optimize the classical parameters
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
of a parameterized trial state
\sigma(\boldsymbol{\alpha},R)
satisfying
\text{rank}(\sigma(\boldsymbol{\alpha^{*}},R))=R
, such that, for the optimized parameters
\boldsymbol{\alpha^{*}}
, the output state
\sigma(\boldsymbol{\alpha^{*}},R)
well approximates the target
\rho
.

In order to assess the closeness between our training state
\sigma
and our target state
\rho
, we employ the following cost function in terms of the Hilbert–Schmidt distance between the two states:

\displaystyle C(\boldsymbol{\alpha},R)

\displaystyle\equiv\left\|\rho-\sigma(\boldsymbol{\alpha},R)\right\|_{2}^{2}

(1)

\displaystyle\coloneqq{\rm Tr}[\rho^{2}]+{\rm Tr}[\sigma(\boldsymbol{\alpha},R)^{2}]-2{\rm Tr}[\rho\sigma(\boldsymbol{\alpha},R)]\,.

As discussed in more detail in Sections
II.3
and
II.4
, the Hilbert–Schmidt distance can be efficiently computed using a combination of SWAP tests and/or a Loschmidt-echo-like circuit. In Appendix
A
we detail how this cost can be reformulated such that it requires only local measurements
Cerezo et al. 2021
to mitigate the barrier to trainability posed by barren plateaus
McClean et al. 2018
;
Cerezo et al. 2021
;
Holmes et al. 2021a
;
Holmes et al. 2022
;
Arrasmith et al. 2022
;
Larocca et al. 2022a
;
Sharma et al. 2022
;
Patti et al. 2021
;
Thanasilp et al. 2021
;
Uvarov and Biamonte 2021
;
Marrero et al. 2021
.

If trained well, the learned state
\sigma(\boldsymbol{\alpha^{*}},R)
should be close to the solution to the Quantum Low-Rank Approximation Problem
Ezzell et al. 2022
. As discussed in Ref.
Ezzell et al. 2022
, the unique optimal state that minimizes the Hilbert–Schmidt distance, subject to a rank constraint, i.e., the state
\sigma(\boldsymbol{\alpha_{\rm opt}},R)
where

\boldsymbol{\alpha_{\rm opt}}\coloneqq\argmin C(\boldsymbol{\alpha},R)\,,

(2)

takes the form

\displaystyle\sigma(\boldsymbol{\alpha_{\rm opt}},R)

\displaystyle=\tau_{R}+\left(\frac{1-{\rm Tr}[\tau_{R}]}{R}\right)\Pi_{R},

(3)

\displaystyle\tau_{R}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\Pi_{R}\rho\Pi_{R}\,.

(4)

Here
\Pi_{R}
is a projector onto the eigenstates corresponding to the
R
largest eigenvalues of
\rho
. That is,
\Pi_{R}
is a projector onto the first
R
principal components of
\rho
. We note here that the projection
\Pi_{R}
is the same conceptually as the typical subspace projection
(
Ohya and Petz 2004
, page 31, Theorem 1.18)
used in quantum data compression
Schumacher 1995
(given that it projects onto the high probability subspace of the state
\rho
), and the quantum channel in (
3
) (i.e.,
(\cdot)\to\Pi_{R}(\cdot)\Pi_{R}+\operatorname{Tr}[(I-\Pi_{R})(\cdot)]\frac{\Pi_{R}}{R}
) is essentially the same as the encoding channel used in quantum data compression
(
Nielsen and Chuang 2000
, equation (12.50))
.

With this analytical solution, we can define a natural performance metric for the Quantum Mixed State Compilation algorithm as the difference between the found cost and optimal possible cost,

\Delta_{R}\coloneqq C(\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{*},R)-C(\boldsymbol{\alpha_{\text{opt}}},R).

(5)

More explicitly, this expression takes the form

\Delta_{R}=C(\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{*},R)-\sum_{i=R}^{r-1}\lambda_{i}^{2}+\sum_{i=0}^{R-1}(\lambda_{\sigma i}-\lambda_{i})^{2},

(6)

where
\{\lambda_{i}\}_{i=0}^{r-1}
denotes the set of eigenvalues of the target state. Of course when
\rho
is truly unknown, we can only report the optimal cost
C(\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{*},R)
, but in this work, we actually compute
\Delta_{R}
to verify that our algorithm is working as intended.

In this paper, we develop a practical, noisy intermediate-scale quantum (NISQ)-friendly algorithm to find
\sigma(\boldsymbol{\alpha^{*}},R)
, an approximation of
\sigma(\boldsymbol{\alpha_{\text{opt}}},R)
. In doing so, we also find the first
R
principal values and components of
\rho
, and so we are essentially performing quantum principal component analysis (PCA), analogous to some previously proposed algorithms
Lloyd et al. 2014
;
LaRose et al. 2019
;
Cerezo et al. 2022
. The precise form in which
\sigma(\boldsymbol{\alpha^{*}},R)
(or the first
R
principal components) is obtained depends on our choice of ansatz. We consider two different ansätze, one based on learning an approximate purification of
\rho
and the second on decomposing an approximation of it into a convex combination of pure states. We also discuss the complexity of computing our cost function, as well as its operational meaning. These are detailed in the following sections. A summary of our algorithm, which also describes the two ansätze choices, is given in Fig.
2
.

II.1
Complexity of Cost Function

For all VQAs, the purpose of using the quantum computer in the optimization loop is to estimate a cost function efficiently, which otherwise would be difficult to estimate classically. Hence, it is helpful to establish that the cost function is indeed classically hard to estimate. Previously proposed VQAs for quantum compiling
Khatri et al. 2019
and linear system solving
Bravo-Prieto et al. 2019
have established classical hardness via the DQC1 hardness of estimating the relevant cost function. (In the previous sentence, DQC1 stands for deterministic quantum computation with one clean qubit
Knill and Laflamme 1998
). We can make this same argument for the cost function in Eq. (
1
), as follows.

A special case of computing Eq. (
1
) is when the two states happen to be Choi states associated with unitary processes acting on a
d
dimensional Hilbert space. In this case, we write
\rho=|\phi_{U}\rangle\!\langle\phi_{U}|
and
\sigma=|\phi_{V}\rangle\!\langle\phi_{V}|
, where
|\phi_{U}\rangle=(\openone\otimes U)|\phi\rangle
,
|\phi_{V}\rangle=(\openone\otimes V)|\phi\rangle
, and
|\phi\rangle=(1/\sqrt{d})\sum_{j}|j\rangle|j\rangle
is the standard Bell state. Then the cost function in (
1
) becomes
C=2-2{\rm Tr}(\rho\sigma)
where
{\rm Tr}(\rho\sigma)=|{\rm Tr}(U^{\dagger}V)|^{2}/d^{2}
. Hence, in this special case we have that
C=2C_{\text{HST}}
where
C_{\text{HST}}
is the Hilbert–Schmidt test cost of Ref.
Khatri et al. 2019
. Estimating the latter was shown to be DQC1-hard in Ref.
Khatri et al. 2019
, and hence our cost function
C
is also DQC1-hard. Since efficient classical simulation of DQC1 would imply a collapse of the polynomial hierarchy
Fujii et al. 2018
;
Morimae 2017
, standard complexity assumptions imply the classical hardness of estimating
C
.

II.2
Operational Meaning of Cost Function

II.2.1
Observable estimation

There is a close connection between the Hilbert–Schmidt distance and trace distance for low-rank states
Coles et al. 2019
, which gives operational meaning to our cost function in this case. Namely when at least one of the states is low rank, then the two distance measures are essentially equivalent
Coles et al. 2019
. This can be seen from the following inequality relating the 2-norm to the 1-norm:

\|\rho-\sigma\|_{2}^{2}\leqslant\|\rho-\sigma\|_{1}^{2}\leqslant 4\mathcal{R}\cdot\|\rho-\sigma\|_{2}^{2}

(7)

for any two density matrices
\rho
and
\sigma
. Here,
\mathcal{R}=\text{rank}(\rho)\text{rank}(\sigma)/[\text{rank}(\rho)+\text{rank}(\sigma)]
is a quantity called the reduced rank, which is analogous to the reduced mass employed in physics.

In this sense, one can use the Hilbert–Schmidt distance, and hence our cost function, as a strong upper bound on the trace distance. In turn, the trace distance has operational meaning in terms of the difference of observable expectation values on the two states
Nielsen and Chuang 2000
,
\|\rho-\sigma\|_{1}=2\max_{P}{\rm Tr}(P(\rho-\sigma))
where the maximization is over all POVM (Positive Operator Valued Measure) elements
P
, i.e., operators satisfying
0\leqslant P\leqslant\openone
. Hence, our cost function inherits this operational meaning:

C(\boldsymbol{\alpha},R)\geqslant\frac{1}{\mathcal{R}}[{\rm Tr}(P\rho)-{\rm Tr}(P\sigma(\boldsymbol{\alpha},R))]^{2}

(8)

for any POVM element
P
, where
\mathcal{R}
is the reduced rank for
\rho
and
\sigma(\boldsymbol{\alpha},R)
.

II.2.2
Eigenvalue and Eigenvector Estimation

We also establish an operational meaning for our cost function in the context of minimizing the errors in the eigenvectors and eigenvalues of the compiled state. This is particularly relevant in the context of using our algorithm for PCA, since PCA precisely aims to extract the eigenvectors with the largest eigenvalues.

First, we note that our proposed cost is an upper bound on the squared difference between the true eigenvalues of
\rho
and the eigenvalues of the learnt state
\sigma
, as follows. Let us denote the eigenvalues of
\rho
in increasing order as
\{\lambda_{i}\}_{i=0}^{d-1}
and the eigenvalues of
\sigma
as
\{\mu_{i}\}_{i=0}^{d-1}
, with
d
the Hilbert space dimension. Then a measure of eigenvalue error is

\Delta_{\lambda}\coloneqq\sum_{i=0}^{d-1}(\lambda_{i}-\mu_{i})^{2}\,.

(9)

It then follows from the Hoffman–Wielandt theorem that

\Delta_{\lambda}\leqslant\left\|\rho-\sigma\right\|_{2}^{2}=C\,.

(10)

Thus if one can achieve a small cost value, the average error in the learnt eigenvalues is guaranteed to be small.

We remark that a small cost value might not be achievable since the optimal cost
C(\boldsymbol{\alpha_{\text{opt}}},R)
is often non-zero. In this case, one could consider an alternative measure of eigenvalue error that, unlike (
9
), would vanish for the optimal state in (
3
). Namely, one can consider the set
S_{R}
of states that have the correct
R
-largest eigenvalues up to an additive constant, and then define a measure of eigenvalue error as
\Delta_{S_{R}}(\sigma)=\min_{\tau\in S_{R}}\left\|\sigma-\tau\right\|_{2}^{2}
. Then it is clear that our cost function upper bounds this error as well:
C\geqslant\Delta_{S_{R}}
.

Second, we note that our proposed cost function is an upper bound on the eigenvector error measure introduced in Ref.
LaRose et al. 2019
. Specifically, a natural measure of the difference between the true eigenvectors of the target state,
\{|v_{i}\rangle\}_{i=0}^{r-1}
, and the learnt eigenvectors,
\{|u_{i}\rangle\}_{i=0}^{R-1}
, is given by

\Delta_{v}\coloneqq\sum_{i=0}^{R-1}\langle\delta_{i}|\delta_{i}\rangle

(11)

where

|\delta_{i}\rangle\coloneqq\rho|u_{i}\rangle-\mu_{i}|u_{i}\rangle\,.

(12)

Expanding this out we have that

\Delta_{v}=\sum_{i=0}^{R-1}\left(\langle u_{i}|\rho^{2}|u_{i}\rangle+\mu_{i}^{2}-2\mu_{i}\langle u_{i}|\rho|u_{i}\rangle\right)\leqslant\left\|\rho-\sigma\right\|_{2}^{2}\,

(13)

where for the inequality we use the fact that

\sum_{i=0}^{R-1}\langle u_{i}|\rho^{2}|u_{i}\rangle\leqslant\sum_{i=0}^{d-1}\langle u_{i}|\rho^{2}|u_{i}\rangle={\rm Tr}[\rho^{2}],

(14)

(i.e., the trace can be taken with respect to an arbitrary orthonormal basis).
Thus a small cost function guarantees a small eigenvector error by this measure.

II.3
State Purification Ansatz

Let us now move onto the different types of ansatz constructions. The state purification (SP) ansatz constructs trial states of the form

\sigma_{\mbox{\tiny SP}}(\boldsymbol{\theta},n_{A})\coloneqq{\rm Tr}_{A}[U_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(|0\rangle\!\langle 0|)^{\otimes(n+n_{A})}U_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\dagger}],

(15)

where
U_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
acts on the
n
system qubits (the same qubits where the state
\rho
resides) plus an ancilla register
A
composed of
n_{A}
qubits. In using
n_{A}
ancillas, we guarantee that
\text{rank}(\sigma)\leqslant 2^{n_{A}}
, but for a typical parameterized circuit
U_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
with randomly generated parameters, one has precisely
\text{rank}(\sigma)=2^{n_{A}}
. Thus, in practise, the SP ansatz is only compatible with controlling
R
in powers of two, so that
R=2^{n_{A}}
.
While there are many different possible choices in the ansatz for
U_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
, we suggest using either a problem-inspired approach obeying the symmetries of the target state
Gard et al. 2020
;
Gibbs et al. 2021
;
Larocca et al. 2022b
and/or an adaptive approach
Bilkis et al. 2021
to mitigate the problem posed by barren plateaus. A discussion of specific choices in our work for different ensembles is given in Appendix
B
.

Upon substitution of the SP ansatz into our proposed Hilbert–Schmidt distance-based cost function, we obtain

C_{\mbox{\tiny SP}}(\boldsymbol{\theta},n_{A})={\rm Tr}[\rho^{2}]+{\rm Tr}[\sigma(\boldsymbol{\theta},n_{A})^{2}]-2{\rm Tr}[\rho\sigma(\boldsymbol{\theta},n_{A})],

(16)

where we use
S
to denote the system of the states
\rho
and
\sigma(\boldsymbol{\theta},n_{A})
.
Below we describe efficient ways of computing each of these terms.

Purity of target

The purity
{\rm Tr}[\rho^{2}]
of the target
\rho
may be measured using a SWAP test
Barenco et al. 1997
or its destructive variant
Garcia-Escartin and Chamorro-Posada 2013
. For the estimate to be within
\varepsilon
additive error of the true value with probability not smaller than
1-\delta
,
the Hoeffding bound implies that it suffices to take
O(\varepsilon^{-2}\log\delta^{-1})
samples. Alternatively, as this term remains constant throughout the procedure, we may opt to neglect it and only focus on optimizing the remaining two parameter-dependent terms.

Purity of ansatz

The purity
{\rm Tr}[\sigma(\boldsymbol{\theta},n_{A})^{2}]
of the ansatz
\sigma(\boldsymbol{\theta},n_{A})
may again be measured via a SWAP test. It is also possible to make use of the fact that we are representing it via its purification and measure the purity of the ansatz via the Loschmidt-echo circuit shown in Fig.
2
. Again, from the Hoeffding bound, it suffices to take
O(\varepsilon^{-2}\log\delta^{-1})
samples.

Overlap term

The overlap term
{\rm Tr}[\rho\sigma(\boldsymbol{\theta},n_{A})]
can be evaluated using either a SWAP test or the Loschmidt-echo type circuit pictured in Fig.
2
. The latter requires fewer qubits and controlled unitaries and hence is more NISQ friendly. To see why this circuit works, note that since we prepare the trial state via a higher-dimensional purification found by evolving an initial all-zero state to the purification of
\rho
, we can rewrite the overlap term as follows

\displaystyle{\rm Tr}[\rho\sigma]

\displaystyle={\rm Tr}_{S}[\rho{\rm Tr}_{A}[U_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(|0\rangle\!\langle 0|)^{\otimes(n+n_{A})}U_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\dagger}]]

(17)

\displaystyle={\rm Tr}_{SA}[(\rho\otimes\openone_{A})(U_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(|0\rangle\!\langle 0|)^{\otimes(n+n_{A})}U_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\dagger})]

\displaystyle=2^{n_{A}}{\rm Tr}_{SA}\!\left[U_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\dagger}\left(\rho\otimes\frac{\openone_{A}}{2^{n_{A}}}\right)U_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(|0\rangle\!\langle 0|)^{\otimes(n+n_{A})}\right]\,.

This corresponds to preparing a maximally mixed state on the
n_{A}
qubit ancillary system alongside
\rho
which is prepared on the system register
S
, evolving the system and ancilla registers under
U_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
, and then performing an all-zero measurement. Due to the factor of
2^{n_{A}}
, the number of shots required to measure this cost within additive error
\epsilon
will scale exponentially in the number of ancilla qubits
n_{A}
. Hence the Loschmidt echo method for computing the overlap is only appropriate for learning low rank approximations to
\rho
where
n_{A}\in\text{poly}(\text{log}(n))
.

As discussed earlier, the above method may also be used to calculate the purity of our ansatz simply by replacing the initial state
\rho
with the trial state
\sigma
. However, the purity of our target may not be measured this way, as it requires knowledge of
a purification of
\rho
, which is precisely what we are trying to find.

II.4
Convex Combination of Pure States Ansatz

The convex combination of pure states (CCPS) ansatz constructs trial states of the form

\sigma_{\mbox{\tiny CCPS}}(\boldsymbol{\alpha},R)\coloneqq\sum_{i=0}^{R-1}p_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(i)U_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}|i\rangle\!\langle i|U_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\dagger}\,,

(18)

Here
\{|i\rangle\}_{i=0}^{R-1}
denotes a subset of the computational basis of
n
qubits,
\boldsymbol{\alpha}=(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\phi})
is a vector of parameters,
U_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
is a parameterized quantum circuit, and
p_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}
is a parameterized probability distribution. An appealing feature of this ansatz is that learning a rank-
R
approximation gives any rank
R^{\prime}<R
approximation for free since one can always ‘drop’ the eigenstates corresponding to the smallest
R-R^{\prime}
eigenvalues and then re-normalize (see Eq. (
31a
)).

Since the state that minimizes our cost,
\sigma(\boldsymbol{\alpha_{\rm opt}},R)
(i.e., the solution to the Quantum Low-Rank Approximation Problem given in Eq. (
3
)), is proportional to the first
R
principal components of the target state
\rho
, it follows that this ansatz can be used to learn the principal components of
\rho
.
More precisely, we have that the first
R
principal values of
\rho
are given by
\{p_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(i)\}_{i=0}^{R-1}
and its principal components are
\{U_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\rm opt}}|i\rangle\}_{i=0}^{R-1}
.

When the rank of the trial state is low, i.e.,
R\in\Omega(\text{poly}(n))
, such that we are learning a low rank approximation of the target state
\rho
, or for small scale problems, the vector of probabilities
\boldsymbol{p}_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}\coloneqq(p_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(0),\ldots,p_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(R-1))
may be stored simply as a classical vector. For learning high rank approximations to larger full rank states, the probability vector is exponentially large and thus cannot be explicitly stored efficiently. Rather the process will need to be sampled from. Such samples can be generated via classical neural networks, such as generative neural networks
Yang 2022
or Boltzmann machines
Montúfar 2016
. In this case, there are similarities between the CCPS ansatz and the Hamiltonian models considered in Ref.
Verdon et al. 2019
. Similarly to the SPA, for
U_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
we suggest using either a problem inspired ansatz obeying the symmetries of the target state
Gard et al. 2020
;
Gibbs et al. 2021
;
Larocca et al. 2022b
and/or an adaptive approach
Bilkis et al. 2021
. A discussion of specific choices in our work for different ensembles is given in Appendix
B
.

Upon substituting the expression for the convex combination of pure states ansatz into our proposed Hilbert–Schmidt distance cost function, we obtain

C_{\mbox{\tiny CCPS}}(\boldsymbol{\alpha},R)={\rm Tr}[\rho^{2}]+\sum_{i}p_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(i)^{2}\\
-2\sum_{i}p_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(i)\langle i|U^{\dagger}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\rho U_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}|i\rangle\,,

(19)

with
\boldsymbol{\alpha}=(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\phi})
.
Here we describe how each of the terms in the above cost may be efficiently computed.

Purity of target.

As for the state purification ansatz, the first term, i.e., the purity of the target state, may be computed using a SWAP test
Barenco et al. 1997
or its destructive variant
Garcia-Escartin and Chamorro-Posada 2013
(see also Ref.
Subaşı et al. 2019
).

Purity of ansatz.

The second term, the purity of the guessed state, is equal to the sum of the square of the probabilities of the parameterized distribution,
p_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(i)
.
For low rank approximations, i.e.,
R\in\text{poly}(n)
, one may store the probability vector classically and thus this term can be computed by basic arithmetic.

When the probability vector is too large to be stored explicitly, but rather is handled via sampling, the purity of the ansatz can be estimated with a classical version of the SWAP test.
Indeed, the approach is to take two independent samples from the distribution

p_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(i)
(call the random samples
I
and
J
). We then set an indicator random variable
\chi_{P}
as

\chi_{P}=0
if the samples are not equal, and
\chi_{P}=1
if the samples are equal. The
expectation of this random variable is then given by

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\chi_{P}]

\displaystyle=0\cdot\Pr[I\neq J]+1\cdot\Pr[I=J]

(20)

\displaystyle=\Pr[I=J]

(21)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j}p_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(i)p_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(j)\delta_{i,j}

(22)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}\left(p_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(i)\right)^{2}.

(23)

Thus, the random variable
\chi_{P}
is an unbiased estimator of the collision
probability,
\sum_{i}\left(p_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(i)\right)^{2}
, and takes
values between zero and one. The Hoeffding bound then applies, and we can take

O(\varepsilon^{-2}\log\delta^{-1})
independent samples of
\chi_{P}
in order to
estimate the collision probability
\sum_{i}\left(p_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(i)\right)^{2}
to within additive error
\varepsilon
with probability not smaller than
1-\delta
.

Overlap term.

A naive approach to computing the third term, the overlap between the guess
\sigma
and target
\rho
, would be to estimate each of the
\langle i|U^{\dagger}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\rho U_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}|i\rangle
terms using the Loschmidt echo circuit shown in Fig.
2
, followed by classical post processing. That is, we compute
\langle i|U^{\dagger}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\rho U_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}|i\rangle
by preparing the state
\rho
, performing the unitary
U^{\dagger}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
, and then measuring in the computational basis.
The total overlap term could simply be computed by weighting each of the probabilities
\langle i|U^{\dagger}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\rho U_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}|i\rangle
by the corresponding classical probability
p_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(i)
and taking their sum. This naive approach will work for small problems; however, even in the case where
p_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(i)
can be explicitly stored, this method will not be efficient for larger problems. The problem is that one needs enough shots to estimate all
2^{n}
probabilities
\langle i|U^{\dagger}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\rho U_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}|i\rangle
. Combining this observation with Hoeffding’s equality, it is apparent that this method requires an exponential number of shots,
O(2^{n}\varepsilon^{-2}\log\delta^{-1})
.

Instead, we propose computing the overlap term using a generalization of the classical SWAP test.
Let us define the distribution

q_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(i)\coloneqq\langle i|U^{\dagger}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\rho U_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}|i\rangle,

(24)

which can be sampled from via the Loschmidt echo circuit.
Then we see that

\operatorname{Tr}[\rho\sigma]=\sum_{i}p_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(i)q_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(i).

(25)

This quantity can be estimated by taking a sample from
p_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(i)
and an independent sample from

q_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(i)
(call the samples
I
and
J
) and setting an indicator random
variable
\chi_{O}
if the samples are not equal and
\chi_{O}=1
if the samples are
equal. The expectation of this random variable is given by

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\chi_{O}]

\displaystyle=0\cdot\Pr[I\neq J]+1\cdot\Pr[I=J]

(26)

\displaystyle=\Pr[I=J]

(27)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j}p_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(i)q_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(j)\delta_{i,j}

(28)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}p_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(i)q_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(i).

(29)

Thus, the random variable
\chi_{O}
is an unbiased estimator of the collision
probability
\operatorname{Tr}[\rho\sigma]
, and it takes values between zero
and one. The Hoeffding bound then applies, and we can take
O(\varepsilon^{-2}\log\delta^{-1})
independent samples of
\chi_{O}
in order to estimate this
collision probability to within additive error
\varepsilon
with probability not smaller than
1-\delta
.

II.5
Performing PCA with our Ansätze

Both ansätze also allow for principal component analysis (PCA) of
\rho
. To make this precise, we first write the target state as

\rho=\sum_{i=1}^{r}\lambda_{i}|v_{i}\rangle\!\langle v_{i}|

(30)

where
\lambda_{1}>\lambda_{2}>\cdots>\lambda_{r}
are the ordered principal values of
\rho
with associated principal components
|v_{i}\rangle
.

By inspection, the CCPS ansatz is directly an ansatz for the principal components of
\rho
. In other words, learning a CCPS representation of
\sigma(\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{*},R)
provides an estimate of the principal components,
|u_{i}\rangle\coloneqq U(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*})|i\rangle
, explicitly. It also provides an estimate of the principal values,
p_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(i)
, but due to normalization, these are expected to be different from
\lambda_{i}
by an additive constant when
\Delta_{R}
is small. As
R
approaches
r_{\epsilon}
, this additive constant goes to zero, and here
p_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(i)\approx\lambda_{i}
. Nevertheless, even when
R<r_{\epsilon}
, the values of
p_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(i)
are such that
\sigma(\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{*},R)
acts as the closest rank
R
proxy, so in this operational sense, it is still appropriate to call
p_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(i)
the principal value estimates.

One very useful property of these explicit principal component/value estimates is that they allow us to construct any numerically optimal
R^{\prime}<R
approximation by truncation,

\displaystyle\sigma(\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{*},R^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{R^{\prime}-1}\tilde{p}_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(i)U(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*})|i\rangle\!\langle i|U^{\dagger}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*}),

(31a)

\displaystyle\tilde{p}_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(i)

\displaystyle=p_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(i)+\left(1-\sum_{i=0}^{R^{\prime}-1}p_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(i)\right)/R^{\prime}.

(31b)

Indeed, this is perhaps one convincing way to view each
p_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(i)
as the appropriate operational sense of
“principal value”
when we truncate the rank.

Though less obvious, the SP ansatz can also be used for PCA by using a carefully designed circuit for the purification ansatz as described in Appendix
B
. The result is that computational basis measurements on the ancilla system prepare the principal vectors on the target system with probabilities given by the principal values. Thus, the knowledge of principal values/components here is implicit, and hence cannot be directly used to obtain lower rank approximations by truncation.

Finally, we remark that while PCA is a general procedure, it has an intuitive meaning for physically relevant classes of states. As an example, consider an XY thermal state. In general, the eigenvalues follow a Boltzmann distribution, and the eigenvectors are those of the XY Hamiltonian itself. At low temperature, we often say a state is
approximately in its ground-state.
More precisely, we mean,

\rho\approx\rho_{\epsilon}\equiv\sum_{i=1}^{q}\lambda_{i}|v_{i}\rangle\!\langle v_{i}|,\ \ \ \sum_{i=1}^{q}\lambda_{i}=1-\epsilon

(32)

for
\epsilon
small. In our language, we would say the state is approximately rank
q
, and when
q\ll r
as is typical for low temperature thermal states, it’s approximately low rank
2
2

2

We comment that this exact notion of low rank actually precisely agrees with the definition of epsilon-rank used in Ref.
Cerezo et al. 2020
. We employ a slightly different definition already given that is more amenable to NISQ experiments.
. By performing PCA with a target rank
R=q
, we find approximations of the eigenvectors with low energies
\{|v_{i}\rangle\}_{i=1}^{q}
and corresponding Boltzmann weights
\{\lambda_{i}\}_{i=1}^{q}
. Hence, our PCA algorithm can be thought of as a way to learn the Boltzmann weights and a means to prepare low-lying energy eigenvectors of a quantum thermal state which has also been explored in other NISQ friendly works
Guo et al. 2023
.

II.6
Comparison of Ansätze

The reliance on an ancillary system to compute the cost function for the SP ansatz naturally increases the resources required for computation. Furthermore, in general, for a typical choice of
U_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
, the guess state will have rank
R=2^{n_{A}}
. That is, one is limited to ranks of powers of two. In contrast, CCPS both requires no ancilla and allows for fine control over both output ranks. However, the need to learn
\boldsymbol{p_{\phi}}
under the constraint of convexity, in addition to
\boldsymbol{\theta}
, potentially increases the complexity of our optimization subroutine. Thus the choice as to whether to use CCPS or SP will depend in large part on whether classical resources (optimization power) or quantum resources (qubits available) are more constrained.

Of course, the choice as to whether to use the CCPS or SP ansatz may depend not only on the required resources but also the end goal of the subroutine. For example, if the end goal is principal component analysis, this is more readily performed using CCPS since it automatically finds the first
R
principal components of the target. Alternatively, one can imagine situations where it is desirable to learn the purification of the target. For example, the purification of a state opens up methods for computing entanglement measures between subsystems of that state
Vedral and Plenio 1998
;
Schumacher 1996
;
Wilde 2013
, the fidelity between two states (given Ulhmann’s theorem)
Chen et al. 2021
;
Rethinasamy et al. 2021
, as well as symmetry measures
LaBorde and Wilde 2021
.

II.7
Summary of Algorithm

The Quantum Mixed State Compiling algorithm is summarized in Fig.
2
. At its core it is composed of the following steps.

1.

Start with a target state
\rho
, and a desired rank
R
for the compiled state
\sigma_{R}
used to approximate
\rho
.

2.

Choose whether to learn the target using the purification or convex combination ansatz. In general, this decision will depend on the purpose for which the state is being learned and the resources available. A discussion of the circuits one must run in both cases is given in Appendix
B
.

3.

Minimize the Hilbert–Schmidt distance cost
C
, Eq. (
1
), using a hybrid quantum-classical optimization loop to find the trained parameters
\boldsymbol{\theta^{*}}
(in the case of the SP ansatz) and
(\boldsymbol{\theta^{*}},\boldsymbol{\phi^{*}})
(in the case of the CCPS ansatz) that approximately minimize
C
. If using a gradient based optimizer one can analytically compute the gradient of the cost using the parameter shift rule
Mitarai et al. 2018
;
Schuld et al. 2019
. For the case of the purity of the ansatz term for the state purification ansatz,
{\rm Tr}[\sigma(\boldsymbol{\theta},n_{A})^{2}]
, this rule needs modifying to account for correlations. For more details on computing the gradients, see Appendix
C
.

Here we briefly describe two ways in which prior information about the structure of the target state could be used to make the mixed state learning algorithm more efficient.

In the first instance, the purity of the target state may be computed and then we need only consider ‘guess’ states
\sigma_{R}
with the correct purity. That is, one may use the target purity as a constraint during the minimization of the Hilbert–Schmidt distance cost, Eq. (
1
). This amounts to maximizing the overlap term
{\rm Tr}[\rho\sigma]
subject to the constraint
{\rm Tr}[\sigma^{2}]={\rm Tr}[\rho^{2}]
. We note that this constrained optimization may not be compatible with the rank constraint if the desired rank is much lower than the true rank of the target, i.e.,
R\ll r
.

Going a step further, one could also simplify the task of learning a rank-
R
approximation to
\rho
by first learning the
R
largest eigenvalues of
\rho
and then using the mixed state learning algorithm to learn their corresponding eigenvectors. The largest eigenvalues of any state
\sigma
can be learnt non-variationally using the quantum algorithm proposed in Ref.
Subaşı et al. 2019
. Denoting the measured eigenvalues as
\{\lambda_{i}\}_{i=0}^{R-1}
, the eigenvectors may be learnt by maximizing the overlap term
\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}\langle i|U^{\dagger}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\rho U_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}|i\rangle
.

As discussed further in Appendix
A
, one theoretical advantage of these two modifications is that they require optimizing only a single overlap term rather than the difference between two purity terms and an overlap term. This overlap term takes the form of a standard Variational Quantum Eigensolver (VQE) cost and therefore can be readily transformed into a local cost for which we have trainability guarantees
Cerezo et al. 2021
.

III
Numerical Simulations

We begin our numerical simulations discussion with a brief summary of the target states and chosen optimizer. Additional details can be found in our open source code
Ezzell 2022
which includes our raw data and the scripts we used to generate it. We then discuss the results of compiling each of the listed states in separate sections.

III.1
Description of States and Optimizer

We discuss the performance of our QMSC algorithm for three main types of states:

1.

Random states drawn from the Bures measure.

2.

Thermal states of an XY chain with random coefficients at both low and high temperature.

3.

Noise-induced states generated by simple circuits on NISQ hardware. (We shall call them NISQ states for short.)

In the first two cases, we perform the entire optimization using idealized classical simulations. That is, we evaluate the cost functions using matrix operations on a classical machine with no error model and no shots (i.e., infinite precision). Henceforth, the use of
“idealized classical simulations”
will continue to have this precise meaning. This serves both as a proof-of-principle as well as a means to estimate an empirical idealized scaling. For the NISQ states, discussed in Section
IV
, we evaluate all cost functions on quantum hardware but use classical computation for the parameter updates, which is the standard variational quantum algorithm approach.

In each case, we consider a low rank approximation by setting
n_{A}=1
(or, equivalently,
R=2
) or a full (epsilon) rank approximation with
n_{A}=\lceil\log_{2}r_{\epsilon}\rceil
(or
R=2^{\lceil\log_{2}r_{\epsilon}\rceil}
). It is natural to wonder why we choose to use
R=2^{\lceil\log_{2}r_{\epsilon}\rceil}
instead of
R=r_{\epsilon}
. The reason is straightforward: we simply want both the SP and the CCPS ansatz to attempt to learn the same state to the same rank approximation to make a fair comparison, and we can only control the rank of the SP ansatz in powers of two.

We use the gradient-free Powell optimizer
Powell 1964
provided in the
scipy

Virtanen et al. 2020
optimization library. We find that Powell is more robust and generally outperforms the common
scipy
black box alternatives such as BFGS
Nocedal and Wright 2006
, Nelder-Mead
Nelder and Mead 1965
, SLSQP
Kraft 1988
, and COBYLA
Powell 1994
for our problems. Of course, the performance could be improved by using advanced VQA optimizers such as SPSA
Spall 1998
or ICANs
Kübler et al. 2020
;
Arrasmith et al. 2020
, but we do not pursue this refinement since we found Powell to give reasonable results.

III.2
Bures Random States Results

We first study the Bures random state distribution because it is a reasonable sampling distribution when nothing about the quantum state is known

;
Życzkowski et al. 2011
(see Appendix

n

n+n

n

2n

Given their lack of structure we see Bures random states as a good test case to compare the state purification and convex combination of pure states ansätze.
However, due to the unavoidable vanishing gradient problem—along with the large number of parameters needed for an unstructured state (see Appendix
B
)—we only test modest sizes. Specifically, we test from
n=1
to
n=4,5
for low rank approximations and from
n=1
to
n=3

\epsilon

III.4
PCA and state compression example

As discussed, the CCPS ansatz (see Eq. (
18
)) takes the form of a convex combination of the
R
estimated principal components of
\rho
weighted by the associated principal values. Hence, learning
\sigma_{\text{CCPS}}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{*},R)
is tantamount to performing principal component analysis (PCA). As a consequence, we get the form of any
R^{\prime}<R
approximation from the rank
R
solution for free by truncation (see Eq.
31a
). We explore the practical meaning of these two statements by example in Fig.

V
Discussion and Outlook

We have presented an algorithm to learn an unknown mixed state
\rho
. In particular, we have developed a procedure to learn a rank-
R
approximation to
\rho
, where
\text{rank}(\rho)\geqslant R
is assumed but not essential. Put precisely, our algorithm is a practical variational way to solve the quantum low-rank approximation problem
Ezzell et al. 2022
. Applications of this algorithm are numerous and include PCA, state compression, learning noise-induced states, and uploading of states onto quantum computers, as described in Fig.
1
.

We considered two ansatz constructions. If the purification ansatz is chosen, the end result is a unitary
V(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*})
such that
V(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*})|0\rangle^{\otimes n}\otimes|0\rangle^{\otimes n_{A}}
generates a purification of the rank-
R
approximation of
\rho
. By tracing out the
n_{A}
ancilla qubits, we get the desired rank
R
mixed state. For the convex combination of pure states (CCPS) ansatz, the final output is a classical vector
\boldsymbol{p}
containing the
R
principal values of
\rho
and a unitary
U(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*})
such that
U(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*})|i\rangle
for
i\in\{1,\ldots,R\}
gives the
R
principal vectors of
\rho
.

Our numerical simulations and hardware implementations indicate that our algorithm works well for a variety of state ensembles, including random XY spin chain thermal states at arbitrary temperatures and unknown states generated by hardware noise on superconducting qubit devices. Unsurprisingly, we found that learning XY thermal states was easier than random states because the additional structure of the problem opened up the possibility of using simpler ansätze. Additionally, while the SP ansatz performs better in numerical simulations (because its optimization problem is simpler), the CCPS ansatz, as expected, allows for larger hardware implementations because it requires fewer qubits.

For both ansätze, our algorithm provides a means of compressing the target state when
\text{rank}(\rho)>R
. For the purification ansatz, the reduction is in terms of the number of qubits; for the convex combination ansatz, the reduction is in terms of the number of pure states required to simulate the effect of the state. While the compression of states
Schumacher 1995
;
Jozsa et al. 1998
;
Schumacher and Westmoreland 2001
;
Horodecki et al. 2007
;
Plesch and Bužek 2010
;
Abdelhadi and Renes 2020
, and indeed data sets encoded in states
Pepper et al. 2019
, has been explored previously, much of the compression-based literature focuses on finding the compressed state via maximizing the degree to which the original state can be reconstructed via a successful decompression process
Romero et al. 2017
.

A particularly timely application of our algorithm is for quantum PCA. While quantum PCA was orginally proposed to have an exponential speedup over classical methods for low rank states
Lloyd et al. 2014
, it was later dequantized for the case of classical data analysis
Tang 2021
, reducing speedups for this case to being modest ones
Arrazola et al. 2020
. However, recently it was shown that these dequantization arguments break down for quantum data analysis
Cotler et al. 2021
and that quantum PCA for quantum data can indeed achieve an exponential quantum speedup
Huang et al. 2022
. Moreover a simple method to encode the covariance matrix into a density matrix was recently proposed
Gordon et al. 2022
, making quantum PCA an easily accessible application for near-term quantum computers. Hence, our approach for extracting the principal components of a density matrix is especially timely, in the quest for near-term quantum advantage.

In this article we have focused on coherent access models for quantum compilation. That is, computing the cost using the Loschmidt echo or SWAP test requires coherent interaction between the state we wish to compile and the device on which we wish to compile it. For this to be possible, the target quantum state either needs to be already prepared (potentially by some unknown process) on the quantum computer or we require a quantum sensor to mediate the interaction between the target quantum system and the quantum computer. The former is practically viable and reasonable to assume for applications such as learning noise-induced states or state compression. However, further developments in quantum sensors will be required to upload the unknown quantum state of an experimental system to a quantum computer in this coherent access model.

An alternative approach would be to use Clifford shadow tomography
Huang et al. 2020
;
Elben et al. 2022
;
Huang et al. 2022
to efficiently compute the overlap between the target state and a large set of guess compilations via independent measurements and then classical post-processing. This incoherent version of quantum compilation could be applied in situations where the unknown state is prepared on a platform that is very different from the hardware on which we wish to compile it. In this manner, this approach opens up new techniques for uploading the quantum state of experimental systems to quantum hardware. In Fig.
1
we sketch the difference between the coherent and incoherent access models. Additionally, Ref.
Huang et al. 2020
provides a means of upper bounding the copy complexity of such a compilation task. In the case of efficiently preparable target states and ansatz states, i.e., those that can be prepared via a circuit with
T\in\mathcal{O}(\operatorname{poly}(n))
local gates, then
\mathcal{O}(T\log(T/\epsilon)/\epsilon^{2})\leqslant\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(\operatorname{poly}(n)/\epsilon^{2})
copies of the target state
\rho
suffices to approximately evaluate the cost and its partial derivatives to precision
\epsilon
arbitrarily often
Caro et al. 2022
. Thus
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(\operatorname{poly}(n)/\epsilon^{2})
copies of the target
\rho
in theory suffice to compile it to precision
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(\epsilon)
.

The framework investigated in this article for mixed state compilation may more generally be applied to the compiling of quantum channels.
This follows from the fact that a channel may be represented via its Choi representation as a mixed state. That is, a channel may be fully characterized via the mixed state generated by applying a quantum channel to one half of a Bell state. Therefore one means of compiling a quantum channel would be to minimize the Hilbert–Schmidt distance between the Choi state corresponding to a target channel and an ansatz mixed state, formed by applying a parameterized channel to half a Bell state. In this sense, our algorithm further open up new avenues for learning unknown quantum processes.

A.1
Marginal Local Cost Function

A simple, naïve choice in 1-local cost could be formulated in terms of the Hilbert–Schmidt distance between the local reduced states. We call this cost function the marginal 1-local cost function, and it takes the same form for both the state purification and convex combination of pure states ansatz.

Definition 1
.

Given two quantum states

\displaystyle C_{M}^{(1)}\left(\rho,\sigma\right)

This can be measured using the methods outlined in Section

\rho

\rho=\sigma

In simple terms, different entangled states may have the same reduced states and so the local cost may vanish even when the global states are non-identical. For example, consider the case in which

\displaystyle C_{M}^{(1)}(\rho,\sigma)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left\|\rho_{j}-\sigma_{j}\right\|^{2}_{2}

(37)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left\|\openone/2-\openone/2\right\|^{2}_{2}=0\,.

(38)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{1+1-0}=\sqrt{2}\,,

A.2
Local Measurements on Global States

An alternative approach to formulating a local cost function is to continue to use the full

A.2.1
Local costs for CCPS ansatz

Consider the global Hilbert–Schmidt cost function in the CCPS ansatz framework:

C_{\mbox{\tiny CCPS}}(\boldsymbol{\alpha},R)={\rm Tr}[\rho^{2}]+\sum_{i}p_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(i)^{2}-2\sum_{i}p_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(i){\rm Tr}\left[U^{\dagger}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\rho U_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}H_{\textrm{G}}^{(i)}\right]\,,

to emphasise the globality of the measurement.
Since the purity of the target state is not optimized and the purity of the guess state is optimized fully classically, these terms can be left global without impeding trainability. However, for the overlap term, we seek to replace the global
H_{\textrm{G}}^{(i)}
term with some set of local measurements. There is freedom in how we may choose our local measurements, but one simple approach is to choose the average of all
1
-local measurements, as follows

Definition 3
.

For the CCPS ansatz, we can define a

as

C_{\textrm{L}}^{\rm CCPS}={\rm Tr}[\rho^{2}]+\sum_{i}p_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(i)^{2}-2\sum_{i}p_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(i){\rm Tr}_{S}\left[U_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\dagger}\rho U_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}H_{\rm L}^{\rm CCPS\,(i)}\right]\,\,,

(44)

with

H_{\rm L}^{\rm CCPS\,(i)}=\frac{1}{n}\sum^{n}_{j=1}|i_{j}\rangle\langle i_{j}|_{S_{j}}\otimes\openone_{S_{\overline{j}}}\,,

In order to prove faithfulness in the case of pure states, we first state and re-derive a result introduced in

Proposition 1
.

Let
\rho
be an arbitrary quantum state, and let
U_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
be a parameterized unitary matrix. Denoting
\rho_{U}=U_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\dagger}\rho U_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
, and
H_{\rm L}^{(i)}=H_{\rm L}^{\rm{CCPS}(i)}
, we have that

1-{\rm Tr}\left[\rho_{U}H_{\rm L}^{(i)}\right]\leqslant 1-{\rm Tr}\left[\rho_{U}H_{\rm G}^{(i)}\right]\leqslant n\left(1-{\rm Tr}\left[\rho_{U}H_{\rm L}^{(i)}\right]\right)

(46)

Proof.

We can write
H_{\textrm{L}}^{(i)}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j}^{n}H_{\textrm{L},j}^{(i)}
, where

H_{\textrm{L},j}^{(i)}=|i_{j}\rangle\langle i_{j}|_{S_{j}}\otimes\openone_{S_{\overline{j}}}

(47)

are projectors that mutually commute. Note that
\prod_{j=1}^{n}H_{\textrm{L},j}^{(i)}=H_{\rm{G}}^{(i)}
. We can associate events
E_{j}
with the projectors
H_{\textrm{L},j}
such that
\Pr[E_{j}]={\rm Tr}\left[\rho_{U}H_{\textrm{L},j}^{(i)}\right].
Then,
{\rm Tr}\left[\rho\prod_{j=1}^{n}H_{\textrm{L},j}^{(i)}\right]=\Pr\cap_{j=1}^{n}E_{j}
.
Recall, from basic probability theory, that for any set of events
\mathcal{A}\coloneqq\left\{A_{1},A_{2},\ldots,A_{n}\right\}
, it holds that

\Pr\!\left[\cup_{i=1}^{n}A_{i}\right]\geqslant\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Pr\!\left[A_{i}\right].

(48)

Choosing

, we see

\displaystyle\Pr\!\left[\cup_{j=1}^{n}\overline{E_{j}}\,\right]

\displaystyle\geqslant\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=j}^{n}\Pr\!\left[\,\overline{E_{j}}\,\right]

(49)

\displaystyle\implies 1-\Pr\!\left[\cap_{j=1}^{n}E_{j}\right]

\displaystyle\geqslant\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left(1-\Pr\!\left[E_{j}\right]\right)

\displaystyle\implies 1-\Pr\!\left[\cap_{j=1}^{n}E_{j}\right]

\displaystyle\geqslant 1-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}{\rm Tr}\!\left[\rho_{U}H^{(i)}_{\textrm{L},j}\right]

\displaystyle\implies 1-{\rm Tr}\!\left[\rho_{U}H_{G}^{(i)}\right]

\displaystyle\geqslant 1-{\rm Tr}\!\left[\rho_{U}H^{(i)}_{\textrm{L}}\right].

This is precisely the first desired inequality
1-{\rm Tr}\left[\rho_{U}H^{(i)_{\rm L}}\right]\leqslant 1-{\rm Tr}\left[\rho_{U}H_{G}^{(i)}\right]
. To prove the remaining inequality, observe that, via the union bound, we have

\displaystyle\Pr\!\left[\cup_{j=1}^{n}\overline{E_{j}}\,\right]

\displaystyle\leqslant\sum_{j=1}^{n}\Pr\!\left[\overline{E_{j}}\right]

(50)

\displaystyle\implies 1-\Pr\!\left[\cap_{j=1}^{n}E_{j}\right]

\displaystyle\leqslant\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left(1-\Pr\!\left[E_{j}\right]\right)

\displaystyle\implies 1-{\rm Tr}\!\left[\rho_{U}H_{G}^{(i)}\right]

\displaystyle\leqslant n\left(1-{\rm Tr}\!\left[\rho_{U}H^{(i)}_{L}\right]\right)

Thus,
1-{\rm Tr}_{SB}\left[\rho_{U}H^{(i)}_{\rm L}\right]\leqslant 1-{\rm Tr}_{SB}\left[\rho_{U}H^{(i)}_{\rm G}\right]\leqslant n\left(1-{\rm Tr}_{SB}\left[\rho_{U}H^{(i)}_{\rm L}\right]\right)
as required.
∎

Proposition 2
.

C_{\rm L}^{\rm CCPS}
is faithful for pure states, and “close to faithful” for states with low impurity. Specifically we have that

nC_{\rm L}^{\rm CCPS}\geqslant C_{\rm G}-\left(n-1\right)(\operatorname{Impurity}(\rho)+\operatorname{Impurity}(\sigma))\,,

(51)

where
\operatorname{Impurity}(X)\coloneqq 1-{\rm Tr}[X^{2}]
for
X=\rho
and
X=\sigma
and we write
C_{G}=\left\|\rho-\sigma\right\|_{2}^{2}
to emphasize the globality of the standard Hilbert–Schmidt distance cost.

Remark 3
.

It follows from Proposition
2
, that if
C_{L}^{D}=0
then

\,(n-1)\left(\operatorname{Impurity}(\rho)-\operatorname{Impurity}(\sigma)\right)\geqslant\left\|\rho-\sigma\right\|_{2}^{2}\,.

(52)

That is, if the target and trained state are pure, i.e.,
\operatorname{Impurity}(\sigma)=\operatorname{Impurity}(\rho)=0
, we have that
C_{L}=0
entails that
C_{G}=0
. More generally, if the impurities of the target and trained states are low,
C_{L}=0
entails that
C_{G}
is small.

Proof.

From (
46
), we have

\displaystyle 1-{\rm Tr}\left[\rho_{U}H^{(i)}_{\rm G}\right]\leqslant n\left(1-{\rm Tr}\left[\rho_{U}H^{(i)}_{\rm L}\right]\right)

(53)

\displaystyle\implies-{\rm Tr}\left[\rho_{U}H^{(i)}_{\rm G}\right]\leqslant(n-1)-n{\rm Tr}\left[\rho_{U}H^{(i)}_{\rm L}\right]

\displaystyle\implies-2\sum_{i}p_{\phi}(i){\rm Tr}\left[\rho_{U}H^{(i)}_{\rm G}\right]\leqslant-2n\sum_{i}p_{\phi}(i){\rm Tr}\left[\rho_{U}H^{(i)}_{\rm L}\right]+2(n-1)\sum_{i}p_{\phi}(i),

where
2(n-1)\sum_{i}p_{\phi}(i)=2(n-1)
. Adding the purity terms to both sides gives

\displaystyle C_{G}\leqslant{\rm Tr}\left[\rho^{2}\right]+{\rm Tr}\left[\sigma^{2}\right]-2n\sum_{i}p_{\phi}(i){\rm Tr}\left[\rho_{U}H^{(i)}_{\rm L}\right]+2(n-1)

(54)

\displaystyle\implies C_{G}\leqslant nC_{\rm L}^{\rm CCPS}+(n-1)\left(2-{\rm Tr}\left[\rho^{2}\right]-{\rm Tr}\left[\sigma^{2}\right]\right),

which can be rewritten as

nC_{\rm L}^{\rm CCPS}\geqslant C_{G}-(n-1)(\operatorname{Impurity}(\rho)+\operatorname{Impurity}(\sigma).

(55)

Accordingly, if
C_{\rm L}^{\rm CCPS}=0
, then

C_{G}\leqslant(n-1)(\operatorname{Impurity}(\rho)+\operatorname{Impurity}(\sigma))

(56)

Therefore if the purity of the target and trained states are zero, the cost is faithful; i.e.,
C_{\rm L}^{\rm CCPS}=0
implies
C_{\rm G}=0
. Similarly, for high purity states the cost is approximately faithful, i.e.,
C_{\rm L}^{\rm CCPS}=0
implies
C_{\rm G}
is small.
∎

Similar to the generalized marginal cost defined earlier, we can construct an extension of the low impurity local cost function, such that it is local on
k
qubits, for
k\leqslant n
.

One simple approach is to perform
n/k
measurements, on
k
qubits at a time. This naturally restricts us to only choosing
k
such that
k\mid n
. Defining

H_{\textrm{L}}^{k(i)}\coloneqq\frac{1}{(n/k)}\sum_{m=1}^{n/k}H_{\textrm{L}_{m}}^{k(i)},

(57)

with

H_{\textrm{L}_{m}}^{k(i)}\coloneqq|i_{\mathcal{P}_{m}}\rangle\langle i_{\mathcal{P}_{m}}|_{\mathcal{P}_{m}}\otimes\openone_{\overline{\mathcal{P}_{m}}},

(58)

where
\mathcal{P}_{m}
contains the
k
indices of the
k
qubits being measured over by the
m
-th operator, such that
\mathcal{P}_{1}\cup\cdots\cup\mathcal{P}_{n/k}
spans
\{1,\ldots,n\}
; we can define a
k
-local cost function as follows:

Definition 4
.

For
k\mid n
, we have the
k
-local cost function

C^{k}_{\rm L}={\rm Tr}\left[\rho^{2}\right]+\sum_{i}p_{\phi}(i)^{2}-2\sum_{i}p_{\phi}(i){\rm Tr}_{S}\left[U^{\dagger}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\rho U^{\dagger}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}H_{\textrm{L}}^{k(i)}\right]

(59)

Proposition 4
.

Let
\rho
be an arbitrary quantum state, and let
U_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
be a parametrized unitary matrix. Denoting
\rho_{U}=U^{\dagger}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\rho U_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
, we have that

1-{\rm Tr}\left[\rho_{U}H^{k(i)}_{\textrm{L}}\right]\leqslant 1-{\rm Tr}\left[\rho_{U}H_{\textrm{G}}^{(i)}\right]\leqslant\frac{n}{k}\left(1-{\rm Tr}\left[\rho_{U}H^{k(i)}_{\textrm{L}}\right]\right)

(60)

Proof.

Similarly to Proposition
1
, we can associate events
E_{i}
with the projections
H^{k(i)}_{\textrm{L}_{m}}
such that
\Pr\!\left[E_{i}\right]={\rm Tr}\left[\rho_{U}H^{k(i)}_{\textrm{L}_{m}}\right]
. Then,
{\rm Tr}\left[\rho_{U}\prod_{m=1}^{n/k}H^{k(i)}_{\textrm{L}_{m}}\right]=\Pr\!\left[\cup_{m=1}^{n/k}E_{m}\right]
. We have that

\displaystyle\Pr\!\left[\bigcup_{m=1}^{n/k}\overline{E_{m}}\right]

\displaystyle\geqslant\frac{1}{(n/k)}\sum_{m=1}^{n/k}\Pr\left[\,\overline{E_{m}}\,\right]

(61)

\displaystyle\implies 1-\Pr\!\left[\bigcap_{m=1}^{n/k}E_{m}\right]

\displaystyle\geqslant\frac{1}{(n/k)}\sum_{m=1}^{n/k}\left(1-\Pr\left[E_{m}\right]\right)

\displaystyle\implies 1-{\rm Tr}\left[\rho_{U}H^{(i)}_{\textrm{G}}\right]

\displaystyle\geqslant 1-\frac{1}{(n/k)}\sum_{m=1}^{n/k}{\rm Tr}\!\left[\rho_{U}H^{k(i)}_{\textrm{L}_{m}}\right],

forming one side of our inequality. We also have

\displaystyle\Pr\!\left[\bigcup_{m=1}^{n/k}\overline{E_{m}}\right]

\displaystyle\leqslant\sum_{m=1}^{n/k}\Pr\!\left[\,\overline{E_{m}}\,\right]

(62)

\displaystyle\implies 1-\Pr\!\left[\bigcap_{m=1}^{n/k}E_{m}\right]

\displaystyle\leqslant\sum_{m=1}^{n/k}\left(1-\Pr\!\left[E_{m}\right]\right)

\displaystyle\implies 1-{\rm Tr}\!\left[\rho_{U}H^{(i)}_{\textrm{G}}\right]

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{n}{k}\left(1-{\rm Tr}\!\left[\rho_{U}H^{k(i)}_{\textrm{L}}\right]\right).

Thus,

1-{\rm Tr}\left[\rho_{U}H^{k(i)}_{\textrm{L}}\right]\leqslant 1-{\rm Tr}\left[\rho_{U}H^{(i)}_{\textrm{G}}\right]\leqslant\frac{n}{k}\left(1-{\rm Tr}\left[\rho_{U}H^{k(i)}_{\textrm{L}}\right]\right).

(63)

∎

Proposition 5
.

The
k
-local cost function
C^{k}_{\rm L}
is faithful for pure states, and “close to faithful” for states with low purity, with this closeness increasing with
k
. Specifically we have that

\frac{n}{k}C^{k}_{\rm L}\geqslant C_{G}-\left(\frac{n}{k}-1\right)\left(\operatorname{Impurity}(\rho)+\operatorname{Impurity}(\sigma)\right).

(64)

Remark 6
.

It follows from Proposition
5
, that if
C^{k}_{L}=0
, then

\left(\frac{n}{k}-1\right)\left(\operatorname{Impurity}(\rho)+\operatorname{Impurity}(\sigma)\right)\geqslant\left\|\rho-\sigma\right\|^{2}_{2}.

(65)

Proof.

The proof is entirely analogous to that for Proposition
2
but with

.
∎

Comparing this to the inequality found for the
1
-local cost (
51
), we find that the
k
-local cost is ‘closer to faithful’ at low impurities than the
1
-local cost. It becomes increasingly faithful as
k
tends to

and, trivially, perfectly faithful for
k=n
. Thus similarly to the marginal local cost, we could start training on the
1
-local cost and as we approach the solution, add in
k
-local cost terms to drive the ansatz towards the global minimum.

A.2.2
Local costs for SP ansatz

Consider our global Hilbert–Schmidt cost function, in the SP ansatz framework:

\displaystyle C_{\mbox{\tiny{SP}}}(\rho,\sigma(\boldsymbol{\theta},n_{A}))

\displaystyle={\rm Tr}\!\left[\rho^{2}\right]+{\rm Tr}\!\left[\sigma\left(\boldsymbol{\theta},n_{A}\right)^{2}\right]-2{\rm Tr}\!\left[\rho\sigma\left(\boldsymbol{\theta},n_{A}\right)\right].

(66)

Without loss of generality, we can also express
\rho
via its purification for analysis, i.e.,

\rho={\rm Tr}_{A}\left[V_{\rho}(|0\rangle\!\langle 0|)^{\otimes(n+n_{A})}V_{\rho}^{\dagger}\right],

(67)

where
V_{\rho}
is the purifying unitary associated with the target state
\rho
.
Recall

{\rm Tr}[\rho\sigma]=d_{A}{\rm Tr}_{SA}\left[U_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\dagger}\left(\rho\otimes\frac{\openone_{A}}{d_{A}}\right)U_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(|0\rangle\!\langle 0|)^{\otimes(n+n_{A})}\right]

(68)

where
d_{A}
is the dimension of the environment system.

In order to construct a local cost, we can replace the global
(|0\rangle\!\langle 0|)^{\otimes(n+n_{A})}
projector in each term with a local measurement. The structure of this local measurement may, again, be chosen freely. In the case of learning
\rho
completely, (i.e.,
R=\text{rank}(\rho)
), one simple approach is to replace the global measurement with the average of all measurements local to one system qubit and one environment qubit, i.e.,

H_{\rm L}^{\rm D}=\frac{1}{n}\sum^{n}_{j=1}|0\rangle\!\langle 0|_{S_{j}}\otimes\openone_{S_{\overline{j}}}\otimes|0\rangle\!\langle 0|_{A_{j}}\otimes\openone_{A_{\overline{j}}}\,,

(69)

with
\overline{j}
denoting all qubits other than qubit
j
. We dub this the
doubly-local SP Hamiltonian
.

When seeking to learn a low-rank approximation (i.e., compression) of
\rho
, we need not make the measurement local on the ancilla. In this case we can use the following
singly-local SP Hamiltonian
,

H_{\rm L}^{\rm S}=\frac{1}{n}\sum^{n}_{j=1}|0\rangle\!\langle 0|_{S_{j}}\otimes\openone_{S_{\overline{j}}}\otimes|0\rangle\!\langle 0|_{A}\,.

(70)

Definition 5
.

For the state purification ansatz, we can define the
Doubly-
and
Singly-local costs
,

C_{\textrm{L}}^{\rm X}=c_{\rm L}^{\rm X}(V_{\rho},\rho)+c_{\rm L}^{\rm X}(U_{\boldsymbol{\theta}},\sigma)-2c_{\rm L}^{\rm X}(U_{\boldsymbol{\theta}},\rho)\,,

(71)

where

\displaystyle c_{\rm L}^{\rm X}(U_{\boldsymbol{\theta}},\rho)\coloneqq d_{A}\,{\rm Tr}_{SA}\left[U_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\dagger}\left(\rho_{S}\otimes\openone/d_{A}\right)U_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}H_{\rm L}^{\rm X}\right]\,,

(72)

with the freedom to choose arbitrary
H^{\rm{X}}_{\rm L},
e.g.
H^{\rm{D}}_{\rm L}
or
H^{\rm{S}}_{\rm L}
as defined above.

The terms
c_{\rm L}^{\rm X}(U_{\boldsymbol{\theta}},\sigma)
and
c_{\rm L}^{\rm X}(U_{\boldsymbol{\theta}},\rho)
can be measured using Loschmidt-echo type circuits (as discussed in Section
II
) but with the global measurements replaced with local ones. However, as in general one will not have access to
V_{\rho}
, it is generally not possible to measure
c_{\rm L}^{\rm X}(V_{\rho},\rho)
. Nonetheless, as this term remains constant throughout and does not contribute to the gradient (discussed further in App.
C
) it can be neglected without effecting the optimization procedure.

The methods used to prove faithfulness of
C^{\rm CCPS}_{\rm L}
for pure states do not carry over for the singly- and doubly-local SP costs, due to the factor of
d_{A}
. Thus, faithfulness for pure states for
C_{\rm L}^{\rm D}
and
C_{\rm L}^{\rm S}
remains an open question. However, we can prove faithfulness for tensor-product states in the SP picture.

Proposition 7
.

C^{\rm{D}}_{\rm L}
is faithful for tensor-product states.

Proof.

In the case of tensor-product mixed states, we can write
\rho=\bigotimes_{j=1}^{n}\rho_{S_{j}}
,
U_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}=\bigotimes_{j=1}^{n}U_{S_{j}A_{j}}^{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
. Thus the overlap term can be written as

\displaystyle c_{\rm L}^{\rm D}(U_{\boldsymbol{\theta}},\rho)

(73)

\displaystyle=d_{A}\,{\rm Tr}_{SA}\left[\left(\bigotimes_{k=1}^{n}U_{S_{k}A_{k}}^{\boldsymbol{\theta}{\dagger}}\left(\bigotimes_{k=1}^{n}\rho_{S_{k}}\otimes\openone_{A}/d_{A}\right)\bigotimes_{k=1}^{n}U_{S_{k}A_{k}}^{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\right)H_{\rm L}^{\rm D}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{d_{A}}{n}\,\sum_{j=1}^{n}{\rm Tr}_{SA}\left[\left(\bigotimes_{k=1}^{n}U_{S_{k}A_{k}}^{\boldsymbol{\theta}{\dagger}}\left(\bigotimes_{k=1}^{n}\rho_{S_{k}}\otimes\openone_{A}/d_{A}\right)\bigotimes_{k=1}^{n}U_{S_{k}A_{k}}^{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\right)\left(|0\rangle\!\langle 0|_{S_{j}}\otimes\openone_{S_{\overline{j}}}\otimes|0\rangle\!\langle 0|_{A_{j}}\otimes\openone_{A_{\overline{j}}}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\,\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}{\rm Tr}_{S_{j}A_{j}}\left[\left(U_{S_{j}A_{j}}^{\boldsymbol{\theta}{\dagger}}\left(\rho_{S_{j}}\otimes\openone_{A_{j}}\right)U_{S_{j}A_{j}}^{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\right)\left(|0\rangle\!\langle 0|_{S_{j}A_{j}}\right)\right]{\rm Tr}_{S_{\overline{j}}A_{\overline{j}}}\left[\left(\bigotimes_{k\neq j}\rho_{S_{k}}\otimes\openone_{A_{\overline{j}}}\right)\left(\openone_{S_{\overline{j}}A_{\overline{j}}}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{d_{A}}{2n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}{\rm Tr}_{S_{j}A_{j}}\left[U_{S_{j}A_{j}}^{\boldsymbol{\theta}\dagger}\left(\rho_{S_{j}}\otimes\openone_{A_{j}}\right)U_{S_{j}A_{j}}^{\boldsymbol{\theta}}|0\rangle\!\langle 0|_{S_{j}A_{j}}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{d_{A}}{2n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}{\rm Tr}[\rho_{S_{j}}\sigma_{S_{j}}]\,.

Thus, in this case, we have that

\displaystyle C_{L}^{\rm D}

\displaystyle=\frac{d_{A}}{2n}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{n}{\rm Tr}[\rho_{j}\rho_{j}]+\sum_{j=1}^{n}{\rm Tr}[\sigma_{j}\sigma_{j}]-2\sum_{j=1}^{n}{\rm Tr}[\rho_{j}\sigma_{j}]\right)

(74)

\displaystyle\propto\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left\|\rho_{j}-\sigma_{j}\right\|_{2}^{2},

which vanishes if and only if
\rho_{j}=\sigma_{j}
for all
j
, i.e., assuming
\rho
and
\sigma
are tensor-product states, iff
\rho=\sigma
.
∎

In this subsection, we have shown how by introducing local measurements directly into our cost function, we are able to construct local cost functions. For the case of the CCPS ansatz this construction is provably faithful for pure states and approximately faithful for high purity states. For the case of the SP ansatz the construction is faithful for product states.
Thus we expect these costs to prove useful for learning mixed states with relatively low impurities and low entanglement respectively.
However, for target states that are highly entangled and/or mixed, we are unable to provide guarantees on the behaviour of the cost function. In these cases, the function no longer resembles a distance measure, as positivity cannot be guaranteed. Thus the construction of a truly faithful, yet entirely local equivalent to the Hilbert–Schmidt distance remains an open question.

However, in practise one may create a cost that is both faithful and exhibits non vanishing gradients by taking a linear combination of the absolute value of the local cost and the global cost, i.e., by training on a cost of the form
\alpha|C_{L}|+(1-\alpha)C_{\rm Global}
for some choice of the local cost
C_{L}
and
0\leqslant\alpha\leqslant 1
. By tuning
\alpha
such that it is (close to) one at the start of the optimization and (close to) zero at later stages of the optimization, it should be possible to steer towards the global minimum.

B.1
Summarizing the circuits in our algorithm

We summarize the circuits used to evaluate our cost function to clarify in detail how one can implement our algorithm. In particular, we provide three circuits which sample the three terms in our cost function,

C(\boldsymbol{\alpha},R)\equiv{\rm Tr}[\rho^{2}]+{\rm Tr}[\sigma(\boldsymbol{\alpha},R)^{2}]-2{\rm Tr}[\rho\sigma(\boldsymbol{\alpha},R)]

B.1.1
SP Ansatz Circuits

The SP ansatz generates an

\sigma_{\mbox{\tiny SP}}(\boldsymbol{\theta},n_{A})\coloneqq{\rm Tr}_{A}[U_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(|0\rangle\!\langle 0|)^{\otimes(n+n_{A})}U_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\dagger}].

The translation of this procedure into a quantum circuit is straightforward and is shown for an

).

We begin by showing that the left SWAP test circuit is sufficient to measure
{\rm Tr}[\rho\sigma]
(we’ve dropped subscripts for simplicity). The first thing we need to know for this derivation is that

{\rm Tr}[(\rho\otimes\sigma)\text{SWAP}]={\rm Tr}[\rho\sigma].

(77)

As we’ll see, our goal will be to find an observable on the ancilla qubit alone whose expectation value gives us the left-hand side of Eq. (
77
). From this identity, the desired outcome follows, and this is why we call it a SWAP test method.

Consider the state right after the controlled SWAP (aka Fredkin) gate which we shall call
\omega
for concreteness. By simple Dirac notation manipulations, we arrive at,

\displaystyle\omega

\displaystyle\equiv\left(|0\rangle\!\langle 0|\otimes\mathds{1}+|1\rangle\!\langle 1|\otimes\text{SWAP}\right)\left(|+\rangle\!\langle+|\otimes\rho\otimes\sigma\right)\left(|0\rangle\!\langle 0|\otimes\mathds{1}+|1\rangle\!\langle 1|\otimes\text{SWAP}\right)

(78)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left\{|0\rangle\!\langle 0|\otimes\rho\otimes\sigma+|0\rangle\!\langle 1|\otimes(\rho\otimes\sigma\ \text{SWAP})+|1\rangle\!\langle 0|\otimes(\text{SWAP}\ \rho\otimes\sigma)+|1\rangle\!\langle 1|\otimes\sigma\otimes\rho\right\}.

(79)

The second line can be thought of as block matrix,

\omega=\frac{1}{2}\begin{pmatrix}\rho\otimes\sigma&(\rho\otimes\sigma)\text{SWAP}\\
\text{SWAP}(\rho\otimes\sigma)&\sigma\otimes\rho\end{pmatrix}.

(80)

Right before measurement we are then left with the state
H\omega H
. Since we then only make a measurement on the ancilla qubit, we will only need the diagonal entries of this block matrix to choose the right observable to measure. In particular, we find

H\omega H=\frac{1}{4}\begin{pmatrix}\rho\otimes\sigma+(\rho\otimes\sigma)\text{SWAP}+\text{SWAP}(\rho\otimes\sigma)+\sigma\otimes\rho&\cdots\\
\cdots&\rho\otimes\sigma-(\rho\otimes\sigma)\text{SWAP}-\text{SWAP}(\rho\otimes\sigma)+\sigma\otimes\rho\end{pmatrix}.

(81)

Staring at Eq. (
81
) in light of Eq. (
77
), we see that the right observable is
\sigma_{z}
. Indeed,

{\rm Tr}[\sigma_{z}H\omega H]={\rm Tr}[\rho\otimes\sigma\text{SWAP}]={\rm Tr}[\rho\sigma].

(82)

Note that the above derivation really only relied on the fact that
\text{SWAP}(\rho\otimes\sigma)\text{SWAP}=\sigma\otimes\rho
. Hence, this circuit works to evaluate
{\rm Tr}[\rho\sigma]
when
\rho
and
\sigma
are composed of an arbitrary number of qubits despite the way our diagram implies they are single qubit states. We simply chose to write it this way since it’s easier to interpret and reason in this way afterwards then try to worry about multiple qubits form the start.

Next, we discuss the right destructive SWAP variation. Let’s again begin by considering that
\rho
and
\sigma
are single qubit states. It turns out, the relevant measurement is the projection into the
|11\rangle\!\langle 11|
subspace. In particular, let

P_{11}={\rm Tr}[|11\rangle\!\langle 11|\cdot\text{H}\cdot\text{CNOT}\cdot\rho\otimes\sigma\cdot\text{CNOT}\cdot\text{H}]

(83)

be the probability that the final result from the destructive SWAP circuit is
|11\rangle
. Then,

An

As a sanity check, we can confirm that

There are no non-trivial symmetry operators

B.2.3
XY Model Ansatz

The XY model does exhibit symmetries. In particular, the spin model is particle conserving, and as a chain in 2D, it is invariant under any global rotation. As discussed in Ref.
Verstraete et al. 2009
, the structure of the XY model can be used to design a generically good ansatz using
\mathcal{O}(n^{2})
gates with circuit depth
\mathcal{O}(n\log n)
. However, as discussed in Appendix 3c of
Gibbs et al. 2022
, one can instead use
n
alternating layers of Givens rotations which still obeys the symmetries but results in a lower
2n
depth with a gate count of
n^{2}-n
. The Givens rotation is a single parameter gate,

G(\theta)=\begin{pmatrix}1&0&0&0\\
0&\cos(\theta/2)&-\sin(\theta/2)&0\\
0&\sin(\theta/2)&\cos(\theta/2)&0\\
0&0&0&1\end{pmatrix},

(91)

which rotates in the subspace where
|00\rangle
and
|11\rangle
are fixed.

In our work, however, we want to associate the state
|0\rangle^{\otimes n}
in the CCPS ansatz to the first principal component of
\rho
. Hence,
|0\rangle^{\otimes n}
should not in general be preserved. As a fix, we can consider other Givens rotations that differ from
G(\theta)
by an arbitrary permutation of basis elements. For example,

G^{\prime}(\theta)=\begin{pmatrix}0&1&0&0\\
\cos(\theta/2)&0&0&\sin(\theta/2)\\
-\sin(\theta/2)&0&0&\cos(\theta/2)\\
0&0&1&0\end{pmatrix},

(92)

is also a valid Givens rotation where the set of fixed states is permuted. To avoid attaching to a fixed choice, we define a Givens gate as

W(\theta_{1},\theta_{2},\theta_{3})=G(\theta_{1})G^{\prime}(\theta_{2})G(\theta_{3}),

(93)

which is analogous to an Euler decomposition of a rotation in 3D as
R_{X}(\theta_{1})R_{Y}(\theta_{2})R_{X}(\theta_{3})
. Using a tiling of this

is sufficient, and so use a total of

single-parameter gates with depth
3\log(n)

Gibbs et al. 2021

Appendix C
Gradient Analysis for State Purification Ansatz

The Hilbert–Schmidt distance cost for the state purification ansatz takes the form
C_{\mbox{\tiny SP}(\boldsymbol{\theta},n_{A})}={\rm Tr}[\rho^{2}]+{\rm Tr}[\sigma(\boldsymbol{\theta},n_{A})^{2}]-2{\rm Tr}[\rho\sigma(\boldsymbol{\theta},n_{A})]
, where the trial state
\sigma(\boldsymbol{\theta},n_{A})
is found via its purification, i.e.,
\sigma_{\mbox{\tiny SP}}(\boldsymbol{\theta},n_{A})\coloneqq{\rm Tr}_{A}[U_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(|0\rangle\!\langle 0|)^{\otimes(n+n_{A})}U_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\dagger}]
, for our vector of training parameters
\boldsymbol{\theta}
.
The gradient with respect to
\boldsymbol{\theta}
is given by

\nabla C_{\mbox{\tiny SP}\left(\boldsymbol{\theta},n_{A}\right)}=\nabla{\rm Tr}\!\left[\rho^{2}\right]+\nabla{\rm Tr}\!\left[\sigma\left(\boldsymbol{\theta},n_{A}\right)^{2}\right]-2\nabla{\rm Tr}\!\left[\rho\sigma\left(\boldsymbol{\theta},n_{A}\right)\right]\,.

(100)

The
{\rm Tr}[\rho^{2}]
term vanishes trivially due to a lack of dependence on
\boldsymbol{\theta}
, whilst the overlap term
{\rm Tr}[\rho\sigma(\boldsymbol{\theta},n_{A})]
obeys the standard parameter shift rule
Mitarai et al. 2018
;
Schuld et al. 2019
. In our work, we specifically use the
“Pauli parameter shift rule”
in which the circuit ansätze are described using single qubit gates of the form
P_{k}=e^{-i\theta\sigma_{k}/2}
for Pauli
\sigma_{k}
and parameter free CNOTs. In this case, the shift refers to running the circuit with
\theta\rightarrow\theta\pm\pi/2
(this will become clear in the next equation). This choice is motivated by our use of IBM devices where this is the appropriate gate set.

We can still use the Pauli parameter shift rule for the
{\rm Tr}[\sigma(\boldsymbol{\theta},n_{A})^{2}]
term after applying a matrix differentiation rule
(
Wilde 2011
, Eq. (11.175))
. Namely, given some function
f(x)
we have that
\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}{\rm Tr}[f(A(\theta))]={\rm Tr}\left[g(A(\theta))\frac{\partial A}{\partial\theta}\right]
where
g(x)\coloneqq\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}
. Thus we have that

\displaystyle\frac{\partial{\rm Tr}\left[\sigma(\boldsymbol{\theta},n_{A})^{2}\right]}{\partial\theta_{k}}

\displaystyle=2{\rm Tr}\left[\sigma(\boldsymbol{\theta},n_{A})\frac{\partial\sigma(\boldsymbol{\theta},n_{A})}{\partial\theta_{k}}\right]

(101)

\displaystyle={\rm Tr}\left[\sigma(\boldsymbol{\theta},n_{A})\sigma\left(\boldsymbol{\theta}+\frac{\pi}{2}\cdot\boldsymbol{e}_{k},n_{A}\right)\right]-{\rm Tr}\!\left[\sigma(\boldsymbol{\theta},n_{A})\sigma\left(\boldsymbol{\theta}-\frac{\pi}{2}\cdot\boldsymbol{e}_{k},n_{A}\right)\right]\,,

where in the second line we use the Pauli parameter shift rule (hence the
\pm\pi/2
) as applied directly to an operator rather than an expectation value.
Thus we have the complete analytic expression to compute
\nabla C_{\mbox{\tiny SP}\left(\boldsymbol{\theta},n_{A}\right)}
. We note that the gradient analysis for the local costs is entirely analogous.

Appendix D
Description of State Ensembles

D.1
Bures Random States

Before diving into what a Bures random state is specifically, it is helpful to discuss a few general considerations regarding generating random quantum states, as is done in the brief introduction of
Hall 1998
. Generally, random quantum states are selected from a distribution that is invariant under global unitary transformations. For pure states, this single property uniquely defines a probability measure known as the
Haar measure
. But for mixed states, this property does not uniquely specify the measure. Assuming the distribution of eigenvectors and eigenvalues is independent, then we can write the probability measure over mixed states as
Życzkowski et al. 2011

d\mu=d\nu(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\ldots,\lambda_{N})\times d\mu_{V},

(102)

where
d\mu_{V}
is the Haar measure and
d\nu
is the measure over the normalized eigenvalues. So the problem is that we must also specify
d\nu
, which, while trivial for pure states, is unclear for mixed states.

There are many protocols one can follow to determine
d\nu
, but the most mathematically straightforward is to define a measure from the normalized volume elements of a metric
Hall 1998
;
Życzkowski et al. 2011
. In this formalism, the key is then to choose an appropriate metric. The Bures distance, given by

D_{B}(\rho,\sigma)\coloneqq\sqrt{2-2{\rm Tr}\left[\sqrt{\sqrt{\rho}\sigma\sqrt{\rho}}\right]},

(103)

has many attractive properties as an unbiased choice. Many of these properties were first pointed out by Bures himself
Bures 1969
, but simpler explanations are provided in Hall
Hall 1998
and Zyczkowski
et al
.
Życzkowski et al. 2011
. The summary in Zyczkowski
et al
.
Życzkowski et al. 2011
is especially concise: the Bures metric (i) has an interpretation as a distinguishability measure
Bengtsson and Zyczkowski 2006
;
Hayashi 2004
, (ii) is the minimal monotone metric under quantum channels
Petz and Sudár 1996
, and (iii) gives the statistical distance when applied to two diagonal operators
Życzkowski et al. 2011
. The form of
d\nu
is given in Eq. (13) of Zyczkowski
et al
.
Życzkowski et al. 2011
, and for brevity, we call states drawn from this measure
Bures random
states.

For numerically tractable system sizes, generating a Bures random state is straightforward
Życzkowski et al. 2011
. First, we generate a
2^{n}\times 2^{n}
Ginibre random matrix
G

Ginibre 1965
with complex entries
6
6

6

The exact description is just a matrix whose entries are
G_{ij}=x+iy
where
x,y\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
.
. Then, we generate a Haar random unitary matrix
U
with the same dimensions. With these two matrices, the random state is given by

\rho_{B}=\frac{(\openone+U)GG^{\dagger}(\openone+U^{\dagger})}{{\rm Tr}[(\openone+U)GG^{\dagger}(\openone+U^{\dagger})]}.

(104)

An alternative, more physically motivated way to generate the states is to construct a superposition of a random bipartite state
|\psi_{1}\rangle=U_{AB}|0,0\rangle
with a local transformation of the same state,
|\psi_{2}\rangle=(V_{A}\otimes\openone)|\psi_{1}\rangle
and then trace out the
B

Życzkowski et al. 2011
),

\rho_{A}=\frac{{\rm Tr}_{B}|\phi\rangle\!\langle\phi|}{\langle\phi\hskip 1.0pt|\hskip 1.0pt\phi\rangle}\ \ \ |\phi\rangle\equiv[(\openone+V_{A})\otimes\openone]|\psi_{1}\rangle.

(105)

By XY thermal states, we mean states of the form

\displaystyle\rho^{(XY)}_{n}

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{-\beta H_{\text{XY}}}}{{\rm Tr}[e^{-\beta H_{\text{XY}}}]},

\displaystyle H_{\text{XY}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}J_{i}X_{i}X_{i+1}+K_{i}Y_{i}Y_{i+1},

for most choices of

here), we interpolate between the two extremes and simply get a Gibbs state with large

\displaystyle\sigma_{\text{opt}}(R)

\displaystyle=\tau_{R}+N_{R},

(108a)

\displaystyle\tau_{R}

\displaystyle\equiv\Pi_{R}\rho\Pi_{R},

(108b)

\displaystyle N_{R}

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{1-{\rm Tr}[\tau_{R}]}{R}\Pi_{R},

(108c)

with corresponding minimal Hilbert–Schmidt cost

C(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{\text{opt}},R)={\rm Tr}\left[\left(I-\Pi_{R}\right)\rho^{2}\right]+{\rm Tr}\left[N_{R}^{2}\right].

(109)

With a little algebra, it is easy to see that the first term corresponds to the sum of squares of the
r-R
eigenvalues of
\rho
not approximated (assuming
\text{rank}(\rho)=r)
, and the second term accounts for the constant offset between the first
R
eigenvalues of
\rho
and the
R
re-normalized eigenvalues of
\sigma_{\text{opt}}
.

Any compilation of
