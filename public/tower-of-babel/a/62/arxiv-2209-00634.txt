A (Co)Algebraic Framework for Ordered Processes Thanks: This work was partially supported by ERC grant Autoprobe (grant agreement 101002697).
Todd Schmid

Abstract
A recently published paper [ 1 ] offers a (co)algebraic framework for studying processes with monadic branching structures and recursion operators. The framework captures Milner’s algebra of regular behaviours [ 2 ] , but fails to give an honest account of a closely related calculus of probabilistic processes [ 3 ] We capture Stark and Smolka’s calculus by giving an alternative framework, aimed at studying a family of ordered process calculi with inequationally specified branching structures and recursion operators. We observe that the recent probabilistic extension of guarded Kleene algebra with tests [ 4 ] is a fragment of one of our calculi, along with other examples. We also compare the intrinsic order in our process calculi with the notion of similarity in coalgebra.



1
Introduction

Process calculi are commonly used models of nondeterminism and concurrency
[
5
]
and are known to be powerful tools for specifying and reasoning about stateful transition systems in general
[
6
,
7
]
.
Frameworks that capture these process types can therefore (a) help us understand the connections between different calculi and (b) guide the development of new process calculi by exhibiting generalisable design principles for these calculi.

In
[
1
]
it is shown that a large family of process calculi can be studied with the
processes parametrised framework
, which provides a generic toolset for studying transition systems with algebraically specified branching strutures.
The processes parametrised framework is motivated by two important examples of process calculi: the
algebra of regular behaviours
(or
\mathsf{ARB}
) introduced by Milner
[
2
]
for specifying labelled transition systems and the
algebra of probabilistic actions
(or
\mathsf{APA}
)
[
1
]
, based on the probabilistic calculus of Stark and Smolka
[
3
]
for specifying probabilistic labelled transition systems.
However, while the processes parametrised framework captures the algebra of regular behaviours, it fails to capture Stark and Smolka’s semantics of recursion.

In the processes paramterised framework, unguarded recursion calls are interpreted as deadlock.
For example, consider the following recursive specifications of a process called
\mathsf{x}
.

\displaystyle\mathsf{x}

\displaystyle=\mathsf{if(flip(heads))}~\{~\mathsf{run}~\mathsf{x};\}~\mathsf{else}~\{~\mathsf{print}~``\texttt{tails}";~\mathsf{run}~\mathsf{x};\}

(1)

\displaystyle\mathsf{x}

\displaystyle=\mathsf{if(flip(heads))}~\{~\mathsf{halt};\}~\mathsf{else}~\{~\mathsf{print}~``\texttt{tails}";~\mathsf{run}~\mathsf{x};\}

(2)

\displaystyle\mathsf{x}

\displaystyle=\mathsf{print}~``\texttt{tails}";\mathsf{run}~\mathsf{x};

(3)

Specification (1) is essentially a loop with an unguarded recursion call: it flips a coin to determine whether to recurse or to print “tails” and start over.
Specification (2) flips a coin to determine whether to enter deadlock or print the word “tails” and start over, and (3) simply repeatedly prints “tails”.
All three specifications exist in
\mathsf{APA}
as well as in the processes parametrised framework.
Intuitively, specification (1) is the same as specification (3), as any repeated coin toss eventually sees a tails-up coin with probability
1
.
Only Stark and Smolka’s calculus identifies specifications (1) and (3).
The semantics provided by the processes parametrised framework, on the other hand, replaces the unguarded recursive call in (1) with deadlock, producing specification (2).

The difference between Stark and Smolka’s calculus and the processes parametrised framework is that the former interprets unguarded recursion as a least fixed point.
In this paper, we propose the
ordered processes parametrised framework
, which provides a generic toolset for specifying and reasoning about processes with inequationally specified branching that admits least fixed points for unguarded recursive specifications.
It consists of a specification language for finite processes, a small-step semantics for executing specifications, a denotational semantics, and a sound and complete axiomatisation.

Notably, our framework also captures the
algebra of control flows
introduced in
[
1
]
for specifying propositional imperative programs
[
8
,
9
,
10
]
.
Milner’s algebra of regular behaviours does not find an exact instantiation, however, so the ordered processes parametrised framework is a variation on, not a generalisation of, the processes parametrised framework.

One of the most fascinating features of Milner’s
\mathsf{ARB}

[
2
]
is its fragment resembling the algebra of regular expressions.
Axiomatising this fragment has been the subject of intense research
[
11
,
12
,
13
,
14
,
15
,
16
]
, and only recently has a proof been announced that a Salomaa-style axiomatisation is complete.
The processes parametrised framework
[
1
]
proposes that a version of Milner’s regular expressions can be found in many other process calculi.
Kleene algebra variants such as
\mathsf{GKAT}

[
9
,
10
]
can also be obtained as star fragments.
Process calculi covered by the ordered processes parametrised framework also have star fragments, and the recently introduced probabilistic programming language
\mathsf{ProbGKAT}

[
4
]
can be obtained as a star fragment of one of our ordered process calculi.

The processes parametrised framework proposes a uniform axiomatisation of star fragments, conjectured in
[
1
]
to be complete.
While star fragments appear in our process calculi syntactically, it is unclear how to give a similarly uniform axiomatisation.
The issue is that resolving unguarded recursive calls in a star expression (called
loop tightening
in
\mathsf{GKAT}

[
9
]
, and
A_{11}
in Salomaa’s first axiomatisation of Kleene algebra
[
17
]
) can result in an expression that lies outside of the star fragment.
We propose another fragment, the
polystar
fragment, to overcome this technical difficulty and suggest a uniform axiomatisation.

Our development of the ordered processes parametrised framework uses the theoretical toolboxes of universal algebra
[
18
]
and coalgebra
[
19
,
20
]
.
We specifically rely on the theory of ordered algebras
[
21
,
22
]
for the interpretation of least fixed points, and on the theory of ordered coalgebras
[
23
,
24
]
for transition systems with partially ordered states.
Universal coalgebra proposes well-motivated and intuitive notions of behavioural equivalence for processes, and by adopting ordered coalgebraic models, it furthermore proposes a partial order on system behaviours that we incorporate into our semantics.

The partial order on system behaviours is related but not equivalent to
similarity

[
25
]
.
We shed new light on an old result in automata theory, that two-way similarity and bisimilarity do not coincide for nondeterministic automata
[
26
]
, by viewing it as a property of the algebraic theory underlying the branching structure of nondeterministic processes.

In short, we propose a framework for studying process calculi that specify ordered processes with inequationally characterised branching structures admitting recursion, instantiate the framework to several examples, and discuss its connection to existing tools for coalgebraic models of process algebra.
Our contributions are organised as follows:

•

We introduce a family of ordered process types parametrised by inequational theories in
Section

2
and
Section

3
and order their behaviours.

•

We equip each process type whose inequational theory admits a form of recursion with a specification language in
Section

4
and connect the resulting algebra with an algebra of regular behaviours in
Section

5
, where an adequacy result can be found.

•

We give a complete axiomatisation of the behaviour order in
Section

6
.

•

We introduce the naturally occurring
polystar fragments
of our calculi in
Section

7
and propose sound axioms for their behavioural order.

•

Finally, we tie the notion of similarity from coalgebra to the behaviour order in
Section

8
and study two-way similarity using the inequational theories of
Section

3
.

A discussion of related literature and further research directions can be found in
Section

9
.

2
Ordered Processes

We start with the introduction of a family of monotone labelled transition system types and a formal description of their behaviours.
Roughly, a monotone labelled transition system is a system whose states carry a partial order, with respect to which the transition structure is monotone.
We formalise these in the language of universal coalgebra
[
19
,
20
]
, which captures transition types as endofunctors on a category.

We work in the category
\mathbf{Pos}
of posets and monotone maps.
A poset is a pair
(X,\leq)
consiting of an
underlying set

X
and a partial order
{\leq}\subseteq X^{2}
.
We write
h:(X,\leq)\to(Y,\leq)
to denote that
h:X\to Y
is monotone.
For a given endofunctor
B
on
\mathbf{Pos}
, a (
monotone
B
-
)
coalgebra
is a triple
(X,\leq,\vartheta)
consisting of a set
X
of
states
, a partial order
\leq
on
X
called the
state order
, and a
structure map

\vartheta:(X,\leq)\to B(X,\leq)
.
A
coalgebra homomorphism

h:(X,\leq,\vartheta)\to(Y,\leq,\delta)
is a map
h:(X,\leq)\to(Y,\leq)
such that
\delta\circ h=B(h)\circ\vartheta
.

An
embedding
is a monotone map that reflects the partial order, and a
coalgebra embedding
is a colagebra homomorphism that is also an embedding.
A subset
U\subseteq X
is called a
subcoalgebra
of
(X,\leq,\vartheta)
if the inclusion map is a coalgebra embedding
(U,\leq,\vartheta_{U})\hookrightarrow(X,\leq,\vartheta)
for some
\vartheta_{U}:(U,\leq)\to B(U,\leq)
.
Subcoalgebras play an important role in
Section

6
.

Example 2.1
.

Letting
\operatorname{Id}
denote the identity, we define
\operatorname{Id}^{A}:\mathbf{Pos}\to\mathbf{Pos}
via the product order: given
(X,\leq)
, set
{(X,\leq)^{A}}=(X^{A},\leq^{A})
, where
X^{A}=\{f\mid f:A\to X\}
and

f\leq^{A}g

if and only if

{(\forall a\in A)}~f(a)\leq g(a)
.
Given
h:{(X,\leq)}\to(Y,\leq)
, we define
h^{A}:{(X^{A},\leq^{A})}\to(Y^{A},\leq^{A})
by
h^{A}(f)=h\circ f
.
Regular languages with their Brzozowski derivatives
[
27
]
constitute a monotone
\operatorname{Id}^{A}
-coalgebra
D:(\text{Reg},\subseteq)\to(\text{Reg},\subseteq)^{A}
, where
D(L)(a)=a^{-1}L
.

Let
M:\mathbf{Pos}\to\mathbf{Pos}
be an endofunctor called the
branching type
, and fix an infinite discrete poset
(\mathsf{Var},=)
of
return variables
and a poset
(\mathsf{Act},\leq_{\text{act}})
of
actions
(or
commands
).
We take return variables to be discretely ordered to ensure that substitution of return variables is monotone.

Definition 2.2
.

The
M
-branching system type
is
B_{M}=M((\mathsf{Var},=)+(\mathsf{Act},\leq_{\text{act}})\times\operatorname{Id})
.

We refer to
B_{M}
-coalgebras as
M
-branching monotone labelled transition systems
, or
monotone systems
for short.
Intuitively, monotone systems are a variation on the labelled transition systems studied in
[
2
]
, with extra decorations to denote branching structure.

Example 2.3
.

Monotone
\mathsf{GKAT}
-automata (with return variables)
are
B_{M}
-coalgebras for
M=\operatorname{Id}_{\bot}^{A}:=(\operatorname{Id}_{\bot})^{A}
.
Here,
(X,\leq)_{\bot}=(X\sqcup\{\bot\},\leq_{\bot})
is defined so that
x\leq_{\bot}y
if and only if either
x\leq y
in
(X,\leq)
or
x=\bot
, and
A
is a set of
atomic tests
.

\mathsf{GKAT}
-automata are the coalgebraic models for the while-program fragment of Kleene algebra with tests
[
28
]
, proposed first in
[
8
]
and then revisited in
[
9
]
.

\mathsf{GKAT}
-automaton on a three-element poset, with blue edges denoting its Hasse diagram.

Inequational Theories

In this section, we give an overview of the inequational theories we use to characterise monotone branching structures.

System types covered by the processes parametrised framework are parametrised by equational algebraic theories.
This is motivated by the observation that many branching structures for
ordinary
systems, coalgebras for functors of the form
N(\mathsf{Var}+\mathsf{Act}\times\operatorname{Id})
on
\mathbf{Sets}
(see
Section

8

For example, the branching structure of LTSs is
\mathcal{P}_{\omega}
, which constructs the free semilattice with bottom in
\mathbf{Sets}
.
Similarly, branching structures for monotone system types are free algebra constructons for algebraic theories axiomatising term inequivalences.

A (

is a pair
(S,\mathsf{IN})
consisting of a

\mathbf{Pos}

of inequations for terms in the signature
S

[
21
,
22
]

An
S
-algebra
is a triple

consisting of a poset
(X,\leq)
and a monotone

\alpha:S(X,\leq)\to(X,\leq)

A map

Iterative Inequational Theories

Inequational theories give syntactic descriptions of ordered branching structures.
Such a syntax enables recursive specifications of ordered processes to be written as partially ordered systems of equations.
For example, the process in
Example

2.3
can be written

x_{1}=\mathsf{a}x_{2}\mathbin{?}_{\alpha_{1}}\mathsf{v}\qquad x_{2}=\mathsf{a}x_{2}\mathbin{?}_{\alpha_{1}}\mathsf{v}\qquad x_{3}=\mathsf{a}x_{2}\mathbin{?}_{\alpha_{1}}0\qquad\qquad x_{1}<x_{2}>x_{3}

(4)

This is an example of a
guarded
system of equations, due to the fact that state variables only ever appear behind action symbols on the right-hand sides.
Intuitively, (
4
) can safely be replaced by the following
unguarded
system

x_{1}=\mathsf{a}x_{2}\mathbin{?}_{\alpha_{1}}\mathsf{v}\qquad x_{2}=x_{1}\qquad x_{3}=x_{1}\mathbin{?}_{\alpha_{1}}0\qquad\qquad x_{1}<x_{2}>x_{3}

By inspection, the syntax of unguarded systems is less restricted, as it allows us to reuse state variables where we might have given explicit syntactic descriptions of transitions.
However, the added freedom of allowing unguarded recursion in our specifications forces us to be explicit about the meanings of specifications such as
x_{1}=x_{1}\mathbin{?}_{\alpha_{1}}\mathsf{v}
and
x_{1}=x_{1}
.

We generalise Stark and Smolka’s approach to unguarded recursion
[
3
]
, which views these specifications as computations of certain least fixed points.
We call inequational theories with least fixed points
iterative
, a term we borrow from
[
43
,
44
,
45
]
.

Definition 3.6
.

An inequational theory
(S,\mathsf{IN})
presenting a monad
(M,\eta,\rho)
is

1.

for any poset
(X,\leq)
and any

\operatorname{Pfp}_{x}(p)=\{q\in S^{*}X\mid p(q,\vec{y})\sqsubseteq_{\mathsf{IN}}q\}

has a
\sqsubseteq_{\mathsf{IN}}
-least element

, called the
least

-fixed point
of
p
.

2.

for any

and
(V,\leq)
with
v\in V

M(\operatorname{id}+h):M((V,\leq)+(X,\leq))\to M((V,\leq)+(Y,\leq))

satisfies

M(\operatorname{id}+h)(\operatorname{lfp}_{v}p(\vec{u},\vec{x}))=\operatorname{lfp}_{v}M(\operatorname{id}+h)(p)(\vec{u},h(\vec{x}))

for any term
p(v,\vec{u},\vec{x})\in{M((V,\leq)+(X,\leq))}

with

and
\vec{x}\in X^{n}
.

Recall that we think of an
S

In the spirit of the Tarski-Knaster fixed point theorem, condition

This is useful for abstractly representing the redistribution of branching probabilities.

Also note that every iterative inequational theory admits a constant

Discussion

This paper is inspired by the body of work that begins with the process calculi of Milner
[
56
]
and Stark and Smolka
[
3
]
and finds its way to general specification systems for coalgebras in
\mathbf{Sets}
pioneered by Silva
[
6
,
57
]
, Myers
[
7
]
, and Milius
[
58
]
.
The processes parametrised framework
[
1
]
is a generalisation of the calculus of Milner that is perpendicular to those offered by Silva, Myers, and Milius.
It aims to capture system types with algebraically characterised branching structures, instead of coalgebras with Kripke polynomial signatures, and only accounts for a simplification of Stark and Smolka’s calculus.
The ordered processes parametrised framework is different: it generalises Stark and Smolka’s calculus, captures a weakening of Milner’s
\mathsf{ARB}
, and produces coalgebras and calculi in an entirely different category than
\mathbf{Sets}
.
These differences have interesting side-effects, like an intrinsic ordering of process terms that is contained in the similarity order
[
25
]
on discrete systems.

We suspect that it is possible to obtain other intrinsic structures on discrete systems by defining processes parametrised-like frameworks in other categories.
One example is metric spaces:
branching fixed-points could be constructed in quantitative algebraic theories
[
59
]
using the Banach fixed-point theorem
[
60
,
59
]
.
Any setting in which iterativity is given in abstract algebraic terms should admit a parametrised family of process algebras similar to ours.
We intend to employ the theory of iterative algebras
[
44
]
for this purpose in the future.

The idea of relating coinductive properties to branching structure, as in
Section

5
, also appears in the metric coalgebra literature, where metric bisimulation is related to behaviour
[
61
]
.
However, the combinatorial study of these relationships with direct reference to the algebraic presentation of the branching monad has seen little attention outside of the recent work of Gumm
[
62
]
.
One interesting use case for the algebraic analysis of branching is the characterisation of similarity in
Section

8
, which we expect can be generalised to other coinductive properties, such as metric bisimulation or trace equivalence
[
63
]
.

Lastly, we have several questions about star fragments and polystar fragments.
The most difficult question is whether the axioms in
Section

7
are complete for polystar fragments.
Another question is for which inequational theories do the polystar and star fragments specify the same set of behaviours.
This is the case for pointed convex algebra and guarded algebra, but is not the case for the theory of guarded convex algebra from
Example

7.1
.
A study of the different expressiveness levels of fragments of
\mathsf{Exp}
would be interesting.

The first kind of substitution that appears is

Lemma F.3

(Fundamental)
.

The coalgebraic structure map
\bar{\epsilon}:({\mathsf{Prc}},\sqsubseteq)\to B_{M}({\mathsf{Prc}},\sqsubseteq)
has a monotone inverse.

Proof.

By (
\mathsf{\mathsf{IN}}
),
({\mathsf{Prc}},\sqsubseteq,\bar{\operatorname{\mathsf{ev}}}|_{S})\models\mathsf{IN}
.
Thus, the monotone map
(-)^{\heartsuit}:(\mathsf{Var}+\mathsf{Act}\times{\mathsf{Prc}},\leq)\to({\mathsf{Prc}},\sqsubseteq)
defined by

lifts to a unique
S
-algebra homomorphism
(-)^{\heartsuit}:(B_{M}({\mathsf{Prc}},\sqsubset),\rho)\to({\mathsf{Prc}},\sqsubseteq,\bar{\operatorname{\mathsf{ev}}}|_{S})
.
This map is readily seen to be a right inverse to
\bar{\epsilon}
, as

\bar{\epsilon}(\mathsf{v}^{\heartsuit})=\bar{\epsilon}(\mathsf{v})=\mathsf{v}

and

\bar{\epsilon}(\langle\mathsf{a},[e]_{\equiv}\rangle^{\heartsuit})=\bar{\epsilon}([\mathsf{a}e]_{\equiv})=\langle\mathsf{a},[e]_{\equiv}\rangle

and both
\bar{\epsilon}
and
(-)^{\heartsuit}
are
S
-algebra homomorphisms.
It remains to see that
(-)^{\heartsuit}
is a left inverse to
\bar{\epsilon}
.
We prove that
\bar{\epsilon}([e]_{\equiv})^{\heartsuit}=[e]_{\equiv}
by induction on
e
.

•

\bar{\epsilon}([\mathsf{v}]_{\equiv})^{\heartsuit}=\mathsf{v}^{\heartsuit}=\mathsf{v}

•

\bar{\epsilon}([\mathsf{a}e]_{\equiv})^{\heartsuit}=\langle\mathsf{a},[e]_{\equiv}\rangle^{\heartsuit}=\mathsf{a}[e]_{\equiv}=[\mathsf{a}e]_{\equiv}

For the inductive step,

•

•

For the unguarded recursion case, let
\bar{\epsilon}(e)=q(\mathsf{v},\vec{\mathsf{u}},\langle\mathsf{a}_{i},f_{i}\rangle_{i\leq n})

By induction,

[e]_{\equiv}=\bar{\epsilon}([e]_{\equiv})^{\heartsuit}=(q(\mathsf{v},\vec{\mathsf{u}},\langle\mathsf{a}_{i},[f_{i}]_{\equiv}\rangle_{i\leq n}))^{\heartsuit}=q(\mathsf{v},\vec{\mathsf{u}},(\mathsf{a}_{i}[f_{i}]_{\equiv})_{i\leq n})

\displaystyle\bar{\epsilon}([\beta\mathsf{v}~e]_{\equiv})^{\heartsuit}

\displaystyle=(\operatorname{lfp}_{\mathsf{v}}q(\vec{\mathsf{u}},\langle\mathsf{a}_{i},[f_{i}]_{\equiv}\rangle_{i\leq n}))^{\heartsuit}

\displaystyle=\operatorname{lfp}_{\mathsf{v}}q(\vec{\mathsf{u}},\langle\mathsf{a}_{i},[f_{i}]_{\equiv}\rangle_{i\leq n}^{\heartsuit})

•

For the full recursion case, we need to first show that

and noting that

and

\displaystyle(\langle\mathsf{a},[e]_{\equiv}\rangle[[g]_{\equiv}\mathbin{/\!/}\mathsf{v}])^{\heartsuit}

\displaystyle=(\langle\mathsf{a},[e[g\mathbin{/}\mathsf{v}]]_{\equiv}\rangle)^{\heartsuit}

\displaystyle=[\mathsf{a}e[g\mathbin{/}\mathsf{v}]]_{\equiv}

\displaystyle=[\mathsf{a}e]_{\equiv}[[g]_{\equiv}\mathbin{/\!/}\mathsf{v}]

\displaystyle=\langle\mathsf{a},[e]_{\equiv}\rangle^{\heartsuit}[[g]_{\equiv}\mathbin{/\!/}\mathsf{v}]

Finally, we use the previous case to conclude that

\displaystyle\bar{\epsilon}([\mu\mathsf{v}~e]_{\equiv})^{\heartsuit}

\displaystyle=(\bar{\epsilon}([\beta\mathsf{v}~e]_{\equiv})[[\mu\mathsf{v}~e]_{\equiv}\mathbin{/\!/}\mathsf{v}])^{\heartsuit}

\displaystyle=(\bar{\epsilon}([\beta\mathsf{v}~e]_{\equiv}))^{\heartsuit}[[\mu\mathsf{v}~e]_{\equiv}\mathbin{/\!/}\mathsf{v}]

\displaystyle=[\beta\mathsf{v}~e]_{\equiv}[[\mu\mathsf{v}~e]_{\equiv}\mathbin{/\!/}\mathsf{v}]

\displaystyle=[\beta\mathsf{v}~e[\mu\mathsf{v}~e\mathbin{/\!/}\mathsf{v}]]_{\equiv}

Lemma I.1
.

The heavier-higher order is a partial order.

Proof.

The heavier-higher order is clearly reflexive and transitive.
To see that it is antisymmetric, let
\theta_{1}\leq^{hh}\theta_{2}\leq^{hh}\theta_{1}
.
Then in particular, where

\operatorname{\uparrow}x:=\{u\in X\mid x\leq u\}

we see that
\theta_{1}(\operatorname{\uparrow}x)=\theta_{2}(\operatorname{\uparrow}x)
for all
x\in X
. Since
\operatorname{supp}(\theta_{1})
is finite, it has maximal elements.
Let
x_{0}
be such an element of
X
, and observe that
\theta_{1}(\operatorname{\uparrow}x_{0})=\theta_{1}(x_{0})
.
We proceed by induction on the number of elements of
\operatorname{supp}(\theta_{1})
.

Suppose
\operatorname{supp}(\theta_{1})=\{x_{0}\}
.
Since
\theta_{2}(\operatorname{\uparrow}x_{0})=\theta_{1}(\operatorname{\uparrow}x_{0})
,
\theta_{2}(\operatorname{\uparrow}x_{0})>0
and so
\operatorname{supp}(\theta_{2})\cap\operatorname{\uparrow}x_{0}
is nonempty.
Let
x_{1}
be a maximal element of
\operatorname{supp}(\theta_{2})\cap\operatorname{\uparrow}x_{0}
.
Then
x_{0}\leq x_{1}
and
\theta_{2}(\operatorname{\uparrow}x_{1})=\theta_{2}(x_{1})
.
Hence,

\theta_{2}(x_{1})=\theta_{2}(\operatorname{\uparrow}x_{1})=\theta_{1}(\operatorname{\uparrow}x_{1})=\theta_{1}(\operatorname{\uparrow}x_{0})=\theta_{1}(x_{0})

Since this implies that
x_{1}\in\operatorname{supp}(\theta_{1})
,
x_{0}=x_{1}
.
It follows that
\theta_{1}(x_{0})=\theta_{2}(x_{0})
.

Now define
\theta_{i}^{\prime}(x)=\theta_{i}(x)
when
x\neq x_{0}
and
\theta_{i}(x)=0
otherwise,
i=1,2
.
Since
\theta_{i}={\theta_{i}(x_{0})\delta_{x_{0}}}+\theta_{i}^{\prime}
for
i=1,2
, by cancellativity of
\mathbf{P}
we know that

\theta_{1}(x_{0})\delta_{x_{0}}+\theta_{1}^{\prime}\leq^{hh}\theta_{2}(x_{0})\delta_{x_{0}}+\theta_{2}^{\prime}\leq^{hh}\theta_{1}(x_{0})\delta_{x_{0}}+\theta_{1}^{\prime}

implies
\theta_{1}^{\prime}\leq^{hh}\theta_{2}^{\prime}\leq^{hh}\theta_{1}^{\prime}
, because
\theta_{1}(x_{0})=\theta_{2}(x_{0})
.
It follows by induction that
\theta_{1}^{\prime}=\theta_{2}^{\prime}
.
Therefore
\theta_{1}=\theta_{2}
as well.
∎

Lemma I.2
.

For any monotone map
f:(X,\leq)\to(Y,\leq)
, the map
f^{\bullet}:\mathcal{D}_{\omega}X\to\mathcal{D}_{\omega}Y
is monotone wrt to the heavier-higher order.

Proof.

Let
U\subseteq Y
be an upper set of
(Y,\leq)
.
Then
f^{-1}(U)
is an upper set of
(X,\leq)
.
We have

\displaystyle f^{\bullet}\theta_{1}(U)

\displaystyle=\sum_{u\in U}f^{\bullet}\theta_{1}(u)=\sum_{u\in U}\sum_{f(x)=u}\theta_{1}(x)=\sum_{f(x)\in U}\theta_{1}(x)\leq\sum_{f(x)\in U}\theta_{2}(x)=f^{\bullet}\theta_{2}(U)

That
(\mathcal{D}_{\omega},\delta,\oplus)
is the free convex algebra follows directly from the following three facts.

Fact (1)

The transformation
\delta
is monotone with respect to the heavier-higher order on any given base partial order.
That is, for any
(X,\leq)
,
\delta:(X,{\leq})\to(\mathcal{D}_{\omega}X,{\leq^{hh}})
.

Fact (2)

(\mathcal{D}_{\omega}X,\leq^{hh},\oplus)
is an ordered convex algebra.
It suffices to see that given
\theta_{1},\theta_{2},\varphi\in\mathcal{D}_{\omega}X
and
r,s\in[0,1]
,

(i)

if
\theta_{1}\leq^{hh}\theta_{2}
, then
\theta_{1}+\varphi\leq^{hh}\theta_{2}+\varphi
, and

(ii)

if
r\leq s
, then
p\cdot\varphi\leq^{hh}q\cdot\varphi
.

Fact (3)

For any ordered convex algebra
(Y,\leq,\beta)
and any
f:(X,\leq)\to(Y,\leq)
, the inductively specified convex algebra homomorphism
f^{\beta}:(\mathcal{D}_{\omega},\rho)\to(Y,\beta)
is monotone with respect to the heavier-higher order, ie.
f^{\beta}:(\mathcal{D}_{\omega}X,\leq^{hh},\rho)\to(Y,\leq,\beta)
.

We are going to argue for Fact (3), as it has a nontrivial proof.

Proof (of Fact (3)).

Let
\theta_{1}\leq^{hh}\theta_{2}
in
\mathcal{D}_{\omega}(X,\leq)
, and
f:(X,\leq)\to(Y,\leq)
where
(Y,\leq,\beta)

We proceed by induction on
|\operatorname{supp}(\theta_{1})|

If
\operatorname{supp}(\theta_{1})=\emptyset

\theta_{1}=0\leq^{hh}\theta_{2}
.

For the induction step, let
z
be any maximal element of
\operatorname{supp}(\theta_{1})
and define

\theta_{i}^{\prime}(x)=\begin{cases}\theta_{i}(z)-\theta_{1}(z)&x=z\\
\theta_{i}(x)&\text{o.w.}\end{cases}

For any upper set
U

z

\theta_{i}(U)=\theta_{1}(z)+\theta_{i}^{\prime}(U)
