Weakly turbulent solution to the Schrödinger equation on the two-dimensional torus with real potential decaying to zero at infinity
Ambre Chabert

Abstract
We build a smooth time-dependent real potential on the two-dimensional torus, decaying as time tends to infinity in Sobolev norms along with all its time derivatives, and we exhibit a smooth solution to the associated Schrödinger equation on the two-dimensional torus whose H s H^{s} norms nevertheless grow logarithmically as time tends to infinity. We use Fourier decomposition in order to exhibit a discrete resonant system of interactions, which we are further able to reduce to a sequence of finite-dimensional linear systems along which the energy propagates to higher and higher frequencies. The constructions are very explicit and we can thus obtain lower bounds on the growth rate of the solution.



1
Introduction

1.1
Main result

In this paper, we build an explicit
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}
solution to the Schrödinger equation on the two-dimensional torus
\mathbb{T}^{2}:=\mathbb{R}^{2}/(2\pi\mathbb{Z})^{2}

(1.1)

i\partial_{t}u(t,x)=-\Delta u(t,x)+V(t,x)u(t,x)\quad(t,x)\in[0,+\infty)\times\mathbb{T}^{2}

where the potential
V(t,x)
is real, smooth on the interval
[0,+\infty)\times\mathbb{T}^{2}
, and decaying at infinity in Sobolev norms.

With a carefully chosen
V
, we are able to exhibit
weakly turbulent
behaviour, that is we are able to prove the following theorem

Theorem 1.1
.

There exist a real smooth potential
V(t,x)
, and a smooth function
u(t,x)
,
(t,x)\in[0,+\infty)\times\mathbb{T}^{2}
such that

(1.2)

i\partial_{t}u(t,x)=-\Delta u(t,x)+V(t,x)u(t,x)

Furthermore, given any small constant
\delta>0
, and any order
s>0
, there exists
c_{\delta,s}>0
such that as
t\to\infty

(1.3)

\|u(t)\|_{H^{s}}\geqslant c_{\delta,s}(\log t)^{s(1-\delta)}.

Finally, the potential
V
satisfies the bound

(1.4)

\forall k\in\mathbb{N},\ \forall s\geqslant 0\quad\lim_{t\to\infty}\|\partial_{t}^{k}V(t,\cdot)\|_{H^{s}}=0

We will in the last section explore possible upper bounds for the decay rate of
V
, which is subpolynomial, see (
5.16
).

1.2
Earlier work

The first exemple of unbounded growth of the Sobolev norms for the Schrödinger equation (
1.1
) on the torus
\mathbb{T}^{2}
was given by Bourgain in
[
Bou99
]
, although the potential
V
is chosen to be
quasiperiodic
. Bourgain proves that a logarithmic growth of the Sobolev norms can be achieved in this setting, and that is is optimal. Bourgain also studied the case of a random behaviour in time with certain smoothness conditions in
[
Bou99a
]
. Furthermore, Bourgain proves in those articles that with a bounded smooth potential
V
then the growth in any norm
H^{s}
is bounded by
t^{\varepsilon}
for all
\varepsilon>0
(with a constant that depends upon
s,V,\varepsilon
) ; and that for a potential
analytic
in time the bound can be refined to
(\log(t))^{\alpha}
.

With regards to the logarithmic growth rate we are able to achieve in the present article, it is necessarily subpolynomial as
V
is assumed to be smooth and bounded, but we may not use the logarithmic a priori bound as
V(t)
is not analytic in
t
in our construction. Still, logarithmic growth rate is nearly optimal as the optimal growth is necessarily subpolynomial.

The study of upper bounds on the possible growth rate of Sobolev norms of the solutions to linear Schrödinger equation has a long history. The general question can be formulated as follow : consider
u
a regular solution to

(1.5)

i\partial_{t}u=Hu+P(t)u

where
H
is either the Laplacian
-\Delta
on a
d
-dimensional torus, either more generally, when the domain is
\mathbb{R}^{d}
or even a manifold, a time-independent self-adjoint nonnegative operator with some assumptions on its spectrum, and
P(t)
is a smooth time-dependent family of pseudo-differential operators of order strictly lower than 2. Then one can try and prove upper bound on the growth rate of
\|u(t)\|_{H^{s}}
as
t\to\infty
.

[
MR17
]
proved, along with global well-posedness,
t^{\varepsilon}
upper bound on the growth rate in the case where
H
has an increasing spectral gap (as is the case for the Laplacian on Zoll manifolds) and
P(t)
is a smooth perturbation. This bound can be improved to
(\log(t))^{\gamma}
for some
\gamma>0
when
P(t)
is analytic in time, which is reminiscent of Bourgain’s bound. Using those results,
[
Bam+21
]
proves
t^{\varepsilon}
upper bounds on the growth rate of solutions to (
1.5
) in an abstract setting, which includes in particular the case where
H
is the Harmonic Oscillator in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
P(t)
is a pseudodifferential operator of order strictly lower than
H
depending in a quasiperiodic way on time. The first result of a
t^{\varepsilon}
upper bound with an
unbounded

P(t)
was obtained in
[
BLM22
]
on the torus
\mathbb{T}^{d}
with
H=-\Delta
. Finally,
t^{\varepsilon}
upper bounds have been proved for general hamiltonians of quantum integrable systems in
[
BL22
]
.

Regarding the dual question of exhibiting growth of Sobolev norms in solutions to (
1.5
), the recent articles of Maspero
[
Mas22
]
and
[
Mas23
]
proved the existence of solutions with (unbounded) polynomial growth in the case where
H
has a fixed spectral gap and
P(t)
is a potential
periodic
in time, using a resonance phenomenon. Loosening the time smoothness hypothesis, Erdogan, Killip and Schlag showed genericity of Sobolev norms growth when the potential is a stationary Markov process in
[
EKS03
]
. See also
[
EK09
]
,
[
Del10
]
,
[
Wan08
]
.

Regarding potentials whose Sobolev norms decay to zero with time more specifically, Raphael and Faou were able to exhibit logarithmic growth in the context where
H=-\Delta+|x|^{2}
is the harmonic oscillator on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
in
[
FR20
]
. Their method relies on
quasiconformal modulations
of so-called
bubble
solutions of the unperturbed Schrödinger equation. It is not surprising that we are able to exhibit logarithmic growth on the torus as the setting is similar. Indeed, both the harmonic oscillator on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
and the laplacian on the torus are operators with compact resolvant and a spectrum with geometric properties (as it is formed of points in a lattice) which allows for explicit resonance mechanism. Let us note that the author was able to prove in
[
Cha24
]
that their method extends to the case where the cubic nonlineariy
u|u|^{2}
is added to the equation, using a similar approximation scheme than in the present article.

The method we shall use here is inspired by the seminal work
[
Col+10
]
refined by
[
GK15
]
. Indeed, we use that on the two-dimensional torus, eigenfunctions of the Laplacian are given by
e^{in\cdot x}
for
n\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}
, with eigenvalue
|n|^{2}
. The lattice structure is then used to produce resonance phenomena between carefully chosen frequencies of the Fourier decomposition of the solution
u
. The idea is that only certain
resonant
interaction will dominate the behaviour of the solution, thus, using an arbitrarily small potential, we are able to transfer the energy of the solution to higher and higher frequencies, leading to growth of Sobolev norms.

1.3
Idea of the proof

The first step of the proof is directly inspired by
[
Col+10
]
. In section 2, we decompose the equation (
1.1
) in Fourier frequencies, thus reducing it to an infinite-dimensional ODE on the Fourier frequencies
(a_{n}(t))
of the solution. This enables us to exhibit some
resonant interactions
between Fourier frequencies, which will dominate the behaviour of the solution in terms of Sobolev norms. In that spirit, we first study a
Resonant Fourier System
where we drop the non-resonant interactions. We then build a family of Fourier frequencies
(m_{n})_{n\geqslant 0}
, satisfying carefully computed
orthogonality properties
, along which we are able to transfer energy to higher frequencies (as
|m_{n}|\to\infty
) with a well-tailored potential
V
for a solution
(a_{n}(t))
whose Fourier frequencies are almost supported on the
(m_{n})
.

In section 3, we give a detailed construction of a potential allowing said energy transfer to higher frequencies, thanks to the crucial point that, as we only consider resonant interactions, we may light up only specific Fourier frequencies in the potential, which further reduces the Resonant System to a
sequence of finite-dimensional linear systems
which we can explicitly solve.

In section 4 and 5, we prove that the solution to the Resonant System yields a solution to the full system up to a perturbation thanks to a Cauchy sequence scheme, thus controlling that the perturbation decays to zero as
t\to\infty
. We finally use the explicit construction of the solution to the Resonant System to deduce lower bounds on the growth of the Sobolev norm of the full solution, thus concluding to the proof of theorem (
1.1
)

2
Fourier decomposition and resonant system

2.1
Reduction to a Resonant Fourier System

We now show how (
1.1
) can be heuristically approximated by an easier equation, focusing on the
resonant
interactions. Indeed, as we wish to find smooth solutions of (
1.1
) we may write

(2.1)

u(t,x)=\displaystyle\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}}a_{n}(t)e^{i(n\cdot x-|n|^{2}t)}

We now set the potential to take the form

(2.2)

V(t,x)=-\displaystyle\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}}2v_{n}(t)\sin(|n|^2 t)e^{in\cdot x}

where
v_{-n}=v_{n}
is real. Thus, we need only find a solution to the
l^{2}
system

(
\mathcal{FS}
)

\partial_{t}a_{n}=\displaystyle\sum_{m\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}}a_{m}(t)v_{n-m}(t)\left(e^{-i\omega_{m,n}^{+}t}-e^{-i\omega_{m,n}^{-}t}\right)

where we set

\displaystyle\omega_{m,n}^{+}

\displaystyle:=|m|^{2}+|m-n|^{2}-|n|^{2}

\displaystyle\omega_{m,n}^{-}

\displaystyle:=|m|^{2}-|m-n|^{2}-|n|^{2}

Now, in the spirit of
[
Col+10
]
, we expect that the resonant interaction will dominate, that is interaction between frequencies
m,n
such that one of
\omega_{m,n}^{+}
or

\omega_{m,n}^{-}
is zero. We thus denote for
n\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}

\displaystyle\Gamma_{res}^{+}(n)

\displaystyle:=\{m\in\mathbb{Z}^{2},\ |m|^{2}+|m-n|^{2}-|n|^{2}=0\}

\displaystyle\Gamma_{res}^{-}(n)

\displaystyle:=\{m\in\mathbb{Z}^{2},\ |m|^{2}-|m-n|^{2}-|n|^{2}=0\}

and define the approximated system

(
\mathcal{RFS}
)

\partial_{t}a_{n}=\displaystyle\sum_{m\in\Gamma_{res}^{+}(n)}a_{m}(t)v_{n-m}(t)\ -\displaystyle\sum_{m\in\Gamma_{res}^{-}(n)}a_{m}(t)v_{n-m}(t)

We observe that
(\mathcal{RFS})
conserves the
l^{2}
norm. Indeed

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\|(a_{n})\|_{l^{2}}^{2}=2Re\left(\displaystyle\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}}\displaystyle\sum_{m\in\Gamma_{res}^{+}(n)}\overline{a_{n}(t)}a_{m}(t)v_{n-m}(t)-\displaystyle\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}}\displaystyle\sum_{m\in\Gamma_{res}^{-}(n)}\overline{a_{n}(t)}a_{m}(t)v_{n-m}(t)\right)

However,
m\in\Gamma_{res}^{+}(n)
if and only if
n\in\Gamma_{res}^{-}(m)
. Using moreover that
v_{-k}=v_{k}
we see that the RHS equals zero.

2.2
Geometric interpretation of the resonant frequencies

Now, we turn our attention to the geometric interpretation of the equation
\omega_{m,n}^{+/-}
= 0 : we first see that
\omega_{m,n}^{+}=0
if and only if

(2.3)

\begin{cases}m+(n-m)&=n\\
|m|^{2}+|n-m|^{2}&=|n|^{2}\end{cases}

which means that
m
is orthogonal to
n-m
. This can be reformulated by saying that
m
resonates with the
m+l
where
l\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}
is orthogonal to

.

Similarly we see that
\omega_{m,n}^{-}=0
if and only if
(n-m)
is orthogonal to

; which finally means that
m
and

are resonant frequencies if one of
m
or

is the sum of the other one and of an orthogonal vector. We may sum those facts up in a lemma.

Lemma 2.1
.

For all
n,m\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}
,
m\in\Gamma_{res}^{+}(n)
if and only if
m
and
n-m
are orthogonal. Moreover,
m\in\Gamma_{res}^{-}(n)
if and only if
n
and
n-m
are orthogonal.

2.3
Explicit family of resonant frequencies and further reduction

We shall now build a potential

and a specific solution to

.

There exists two families

of vectors of

\displaystyle(P_{1})\quad

\displaystyle m_{k}\neq 0,\quad l_{k}\neq 0

\displaystyle(P_{2})\quad

\displaystyle m_{k}\perp l_{k^{\prime}}\quad\iff\quad k=k^{\prime}

\displaystyle(P_{3})\quad

\displaystyle m_{k+1}=m_{k}+l_{k}

\displaystyle(P_{4})\quad

\displaystyle(P_{6})\quad

\displaystyle(P_{7})\quad

. Thus, at most
n
possible values of
a
are to be excluded. We also need that
m_{n+1}\cdot(m_{k}-l_{k})\neq 0
for
k\leqslant n
, which is always true for
k=n
if we set
a\geqslant 2
, and it follows from the construction of
(P_{5})
that
m\cdot(m_{k}-l_{k})\neq 0
as
m_{n}
is not parallel to
m_{k}-l_{k}
, hence this excludes at most
n
values of
a
. We finally need that
m_{k}\cdot(m_{n}-am)\neq 0
for
k\leqslant n-1
which excludes at most
n
values of
a
as
m\cdot m_{k}\neq 0
.

In order for
(P_{5})
to hold, we need on the one hand that
m_{k}-l_{k}
is not parallel to
m_{n+1}
for
k\leqslant n
, which excludes at most
n
values of
a
as this is always true for
k=n
and as
m_{k}-l_{k}
is not parallel to
m
for
k<n
thanks to
(P_{4})
. On the other hand, we need that
m_{n}-l_{n}=m_{n}-am
is not parallel to
m_{k}
for
k\leqslant n
which again excludes at most
n
values for
a
as
m
is not parallel to
m_{k}
for
k<n
thanks to
(P_{2})
.

In order for
(P_{6})
to hold, we need on the one hand that for
k\leqslant n-1
,
(m_{n+1}-l_{k})\cdot l_{k}\neq 0
which is equivalent to
am\cdot l_{k}\neq cste
. As we know that
m\cdot l_{k}\neq 0
(otherwise
m_{k}
is orthogonal to
m_{n}
) this excludes at most
n
values for
a
. One the other hand, we need that for
k\leqslant n-1

m_{k}-am
is not orthogonal to
am
which is ensured by the fact that
|m|>|m_{k}|
.

In order for
(P_{7})
to hold, we need on the one hand that for
k\leqslant n-1

m_{n}-am-l_{k}
is not orthogonal to
l_{k}
which excludes at most
n
values for
a
as
m\cdot l_{k}\neq 0
. On the other hand we need that for
k\leqslant n-1

m_{k}-l_{k}-am
is not orthogonal to
am
and once again this excludes at most
n
values for
a
.

In order for
(P_{8})
to hold, we need on the one hand that for
k\leqslant n

am+m_{k}
is not orthogonal to
am
, thus excluding at most
n
values for
a
, and on the other hand that for
k\leqslant n-1

l_{k}+m_{n}+am
is not orthogonal to
l_{k}
which excludes at most
n
values for
a
as
m\cdot l_{k}\neq 0
.

In order for
(P_{9})
to hold finally, we need on the one hand that for
k\leqslant n-1

am+m_{k}-l_{k}
is not orthogonal to
am
, thus excluding at most
n
values for
a
, and on the other hand that
l_{k}+m_{n}-am
is not orthogonal to
l_{k}
, excluding once again at most
n
values for
a
.

We thus finally see that any
a\geqslant 1
except maybe at most
C(n+1)
values can be chosen, where
C\geqslant 2
. Up to taking
C
a little larger we may thus find
n+1\leqslant a\leqslant C(n+1)
such that setting
l_{n}=am
enables the induction hypothesis to be satisfied.

By this procedure we are able to construct sequences for which the desired properties hold. Moreover there holds
n|m_{n}|\leqslant|l_{n}|\leqslant Cn|m_{n}|
and thus
n|m_{n}|\leqslant|m_{n+1}|\leqslant C^{\prime}n|m_{n}|
for
C^{\prime}=\sqrt{C+1}
thus proving the last part of the lemma.

3
Solution to the resonant system

Thanks to the previous section, we are now able to exhibit explicit
(r_{k}(t))
and an explicit solution
(p_{k}(t)),(s_{k}(t))
to (
2.4
) for which we control precisely the energy transfer between Fourier frequencies.
We turn to the explicit study of the mechanism that will allow energy transfer between frequencies. We start at
t=0
with well-chosen values for
p_{0},p_{1},s_{0}
and set the other
p_{k}
and
s_{k}
to be zero. The idea is then to locally fully transfer the energy from
(p_{k},s_{k},p_{k+1})
to
(p_{k+1},s_{k+1},p_{k+2})
in finite time, thus ensuring that for all given
n
, after a time
T_{n}
, we have that
p_{k}=s_{k}=0
for all
k\leqslant n
. Now, as
(\mathcal{RFS})
conserves the
l^{2}
norm, this ensures that the Sobolev
H^{s}
norm is greater that
|m_{n}|^{s}
for
t\geqslant T_{n}
.

3.1
General form of the solution to the linear system

Explicitly, find an interval
I=[t_{0},t_{1}]
and a smooth function
\phi
on
I
. Find
k\geqslant 1
. We look at the system

(3.1)

\begin{cases}\partial_{t}p_{k+1}&=\phi(t)p_{k}\\
\partial_{t}p_{k}&=-\phi(t)p_{k+1}-\phi(t)s_{k}\\
\partial_{t}s_{k}&=\phi(t)p_{k}\end{cases}

which corresponds to (
2.4
) when we only light up
r_{k}(t)=\phi(t)
, that is we set
r_{k^{\prime}}(t)=0
for
k^{\prime}\neq k
on
I
. The system can then be written in the form of a simple linear system

(3.2)

\partial_{t}\begin{pmatrix}p_{k+1}\\
p_{k}\\
s_{k}\end{pmatrix}=\phi(t)A\begin{pmatrix}p_{k+1}\\
p_{k}\\
s_{k}\end{pmatrix}

where we set

(3.3)

A=\begin{pmatrix}0&1&0\\
-1&0&-1\\
0&1&0\end{pmatrix}

Now, the solution with initial condition
\begin{pmatrix}p_{k+1}(t_{0})\\
p_{k}(t_{0})\\
s_{k}(t_{0})\end{pmatrix}
is given by

(3.4)

\begin{pmatrix}p_{k+1}(t)\\
p_{k}(t)\\
s_{k}(t)\end{pmatrix}=\exp\left(\left(\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\phi(s)ds\right)A\right)\begin{pmatrix}p_{k+1}(t_{0})\\
p_{k}(t_{0})\\
s_{k}(t_{0})\end{pmatrix}

Now, one can compute

(3.5)

\exp(T A)=\begin{pmatrix}\frac{1}{2}\left(\cos(T\sqrt{2})+1\right)&\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\sin(T\sqrt{2})&\frac{1}{2}\left(\cos(T\sqrt{2})-1\right)\\
-\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\sin(T\sqrt{2})&\cos(T\sqrt{2})&-\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\sin(T\sqrt{2})\\
\frac{1}{2}\left(\cos(T\sqrt{2})-1\right)&\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\sin(T\sqrt{2})&\frac{1}{2}\left(\cos(T\sqrt{2})+1\right)\end{pmatrix}

This explicit matrix allows us to build three moves in order to transfer a specific configuration from
(p_{k},s_{k},p_{k+1})
to
(p_{k+1},s_{k+1},p_{k+2})
in finite time.

3.1.1
First move

Start with

(3.6)

\begin{pmatrix}p_{k+1}(t_{0})\\
p_{k}(t_{0})\\
s_{k}(t_{0})\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\frac{1}{2}\\
-\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\\
\frac{1}{2}\end{pmatrix}

We set
\phi
a non-negative
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}
function with support in
[t_{0},t_{1}]
such that moreover
\int\phi=\frac{7\pi}{4\sqrt{2}}
. There holds

(3.7)

\begin{pmatrix}p_{k+1}(t_{1})\\
p_{k}(t_{1})\\
s_{k}(t_{1})\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\\
0\\
\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\end{pmatrix}

3.1.2
Second move

If we now set

(3.8)

\begin{pmatrix}p_{k+1}(t_{0})\\
p_{k}(t_{0})\\
s_{k}(t_{0})\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}0\\
1\\
0\end{pmatrix}

With the integral of
\phi
being
\frac{\pi}{2\sqrt{2}}
there holds

(3.9)

\begin{pmatrix}p_{k+1}(t_{1})\\
p_{k}(t_{1})\\
s_{k}(t_{1})\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\\
0\\
\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\end{pmatrix}

3.1.3
Third move

If finally we set

(3.10)

\begin{pmatrix}p_{k+1}(t_{0})\\
p_{k}(t_{0})\\
s_{k}(t_{0})\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}0\\
0\\
1\end{pmatrix}

and set the integral of
\phi
to be
\frac{\pi}{\sqrt{2}}
there holds

(3.11)

\begin{pmatrix}p_{k+1}(t_{1})\\

0\\
0\end{pmatrix}

3.2
Idea of the construction of the potential and the resonant solution

These easy observations yield the construction both of the potential
(r_{k}(t))
and of the solution
(p_{k}(t)),(s_{k}(t))
. We may represent the solution
(p_{k}(t)),(s_{k}(t))
as points in the following semi-infinite chain

p_{0}
s_{0}
p_{1}
s_{1}
p_{2}
s_{2}
p_{3}
s_{3}
p_{4}
s_{4}
p_{5}
s_{5}
p_{6}

where the arrows represent the possible interactions between the Fourier frequencies induced by the potential
(r_{k}(t))

Assume that, at
t=0
, we start with the configuration

p_{0}=-\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}
s_{0}=\frac{1}{2}
p_{1}=\frac{1}{2}
s_{1}=0
p_{2}=0

Then using first move if we light up only
r_{0}
during an appropriate time we may fully transfer the mass from
p_{0}
to
s_{0}
and
p_{1}
equally.

p_{0}=0
s_{0}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}
p_{1}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}
s_{1}=0
p_{2}=0

Now, we clear
p_{1}
using the second move, that is lighting up only
r_{1}(t)
we can fully transfer the mass from
p_{1}
to
s_{1}
and
p_{2}
equally.

p_{0}=0
s_{0}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}
p_{1}=0
s_{1}=\frac{1}{2}
p_{2}=\frac{1}{2}

Finally, we use third move to transfer fully the remaining mass from

to
p_{1}
lighting only

again

p_{0}=0
s_{0}=0
p_{1}=-\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}
s_{1}=\frac{1}{2}
p_{2}=\frac{1}{2}

Thus, we find exactly the same situation we started with with indexes incremented by one. This enables us to start a recursive scheme so that as time goes by we repeat these three moves to transfer the mass to higher frequencies. The idea to ensure that the potential
V
decreases in Sobolev norms as

is that, up to lighting
r_{k}
for a longer time, we may at each step choose it arbitrarily small.

3.3

\begin{pmatrix}p_{2}(4+t_{0}+t_{1})\\

Remark 3.1
.

Provided the

3.4
Explicit choice for
\beta_{k}
in order for

to decay

In order to prove theorem
(
1.1
)
, we need to ensure that
V
and all its derivative decay with respect to all Sobolev norms as
t\to\infty
. Now, from the construction we see that for all
t\geqslant 0
there is a unique
k(t)
such that
v_{n}=0
for all
n\neq\pm l_{k(t)}
. Now, find a
m\in\mathbb{N}
and a
s\geqslant 0
, there holds

(3.26)

\|\partial^{m}_{t}V(t,\cdot)\|_{H^{s}}\simeq\beta_{k(t)}\left|l_{k(t)}\right|^{s+2m}

As

when

, and thus as

, we need to ensure that
\beta_{k}
decays faster with respect to

that any power of

. A natural choice is

(3.27)

\beta_{k}:=|l_{k}|^{-|l_{k}|}

and we will see that this choice indeed enables us to close the estimates.

4
Approximation

4.1
Resonant solution + perturbation decomposition

In order to construct a solution to the full system
(\mathcal{FS})
we try and approximate it by the solution
(a_{n}(t))
built in the previous section. In that spirit, we set the solution
(b_{n}(t))
with the a priori form
b_{n}(t)=a_{n}(t)+c_{n}(t)
where
a_{n}(t)
is the solution to
(\mathcal{RFS})
built above and
c_{n}
is a perturbation. We may thus write

(4.1)

\partial_{t}(a_{n}+c_{n})=\displaystyle\sum_{m\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}}(a_{m}+c_{m})(t)v_{n-m}(t)\left(e^{-i\omega_{m,n}^{+}t}-e^{-i\omega_{m,n}^{-}t}\right)

and we already know that

(4.2)

\partial_{t}a_{n}=\displaystyle\sum_{m\in\Gamma_{res}^{+}(n)}a_{m}(t)v_{n-m}(t)\ -\displaystyle\sum_{m\in\Gamma_{res}^{-}(n)}a_{m}(t)v_{n-m}(t)

Thus we need
(c_{n})
to solve

(4.3)

\partial_{t}c_{n}=\displaystyle\sum_{m\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}}c_{m}(t)v_{n-m}(t)\left(e^{-i\omega_{m,n}^{+}t}-e^{-i\omega_{m,n}^{-}t}\right)+\displaystyle\sum_{m\notin\Gamma_{res}^{+}(n)}a_{m}(t)v_{n-m}(t)e^{-i\omega_{m,n}^{+}t}\ -\displaystyle\sum_{m\notin\Gamma_{res}^{-}(n)}a_{m}(t)v_{n-m}(t)e^{-i\omega_{m,n}^{-}t}

Our goal is now to build a solution
(c_{n})
to (
4.3
) which decays as
t\to\infty
. We will use a Cauchy sequence method: the equation (
4.3
) is globally well-posed in
l^{1}(\mathbb{Z})
so we may set for a given integer
N>0

(c_{n}^{N})
the solution on
\mathbb{R}_{+}
with initial condition
c^{N}(T_{N})=0
. There holds

\displaystyle c_{n}^{N}(t)

\displaystyle=-\displaystyle\sum_{m\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}}\int_{t}^{T_{N}}c_{m}^{N}(s)v_{n-m}(s)\left(e^{-i\omega_{m,n}^{+}s}-e^{-i\omega_{m,n}^{-}s}\right)ds

\displaystyle-\displaystyle\sum_{m\notin\Gamma_{res}^{+}(n)}\int_{t}^{T_{N}}a_{m}(s)v_{n-m}(s)e^{-i\omega_{m,n}^{+}s}ds\ +\displaystyle\sum_{m\notin\Gamma_{res}^{-}(n)}\int_{t}^{T_{N}}a_{m}(s)v_{n-m}(s)e^{-i\omega_{m,n}^{-}s}ds

from which we infer, for
t\leqslant T_{N}

\displaystyle\|(c_{n}^{N}(t))\|_{l^{1}}

\displaystyle\leqslant 2\int_{t}^{T_{N}}\|(c_{n}^{N}(s))\|_{l^{1}}\|(v_{n}(s))\|_{l^{1}}ds

\displaystyle+\displaystyle\sum_{n}\displaystyle\sum_{m\notin\Gamma_{res}^{+}(n)}\left|\int_{t}^{T_{N}}a_{m}(s)v_{n-m}(s)e^{i\omega_{m,n}^{+}s}ds\right|\ +\displaystyle\sum_{n}\displaystyle\sum_{m\notin\Gamma_{res}^{-}(n)}\left|\int_{t}^{T_{N}}a_{m}(s)v_{n-m}(s)e^{i\omega_{m,n}^{-}s}ds\right|

which we rewrite as the inequality for
t\leqslant T_{N}

(4.4)

\|(c_{n}^{N}(t))\|_{l^{1}}\leqslant\alpha(t)+\int_{t}^{T_{N}}\|(c_{n}^{N}(s))\|_{l^{1}}\beta(s)ds

By Gronwall’s lemma

(4.5)

\forall t\leqslant T_{N}\quad\|(c_{n}^{N}(t))\|_{l^{1}}\leqslant\alpha(t)+\int_{t}^{T_{N}}\alpha(s)\beta(s)\exp\left(\int_{t}^{s}\beta(\sigma)d\sigma\right)ds

4.2
Estimates on
\alpha(t)

First, let us study
\alpha(t)
. The set of pairs
(m,n-m)
,
n\in Z^{2}
and
m\notin\Gamma_{res}^{+}(n)
(resp
m\notin\Gamma_{res}^{-}(n)
) is equal to the set of pairs
(n_{1},n_{2})\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}
such that
n_{1}
et
n_{2}
aren’t orthogonal (resp
n_{2}
and
n_{1}+n_{2}
aren’t orthogonal). Moreover there stands
v_{n}(s)=0
for all
n\neq\pm l_{k}
for a given
k\geqslant 0
, and we recall that
v_{-n}=v_{n}
. Finally we know that
a_{n}(s)v_{l_{k}}(s)=0
as soon as
n\notin\{m_{k},m_{k}-l_{k},m_{k+1},m_{k+1}-l_{k+1},m_{k+2}\}=:E_{k}
. We may then write

(4.6)

\alpha(t)=\displaystyle\sum_{k\geqslant 0}\displaystyle\sum_{n\in E_{k}}I(k,n,t)

where
I(k,n)
is a sum of at most four quantities of the form

(4.7)

J(k,n,\omega,t):=\left|\int_{t}^{T_{n}}a_{n}(s)r_{k}(s)e^{i\omega s}ds\right|

and
\omega
is a frequency belonging to
\mathbb{Z}\backslash\{0\}
, thus ensuring
|\omega|\geqslant 1
. (This is here that we use the non resonance of the interactions).

We may now write

\displaystyle\int_{t}^{T_{n}}a_{n}(s)r_{k}(s)e^{i\omega s}ds

\displaystyle=\left[\left(\int_{s}^{t}a_{n}(\sigma)e^{i\omega\sigma}d\sigma\right)r_{k}(s)\right]_{t}^{T_{N}}-\int_{t}^{T_{N}}\left(\int_{s}^{t}a_{n}(\sigma)e^{i\omega\sigma}d\sigma\right)r_{k}^{\prime}(s)ds

The bracket term is equal to zero as
r_{k}
is zero at
T_{N}
for all
k
. Moreover we may infer from the construction of
r_{k}
that

(4.8)

\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}|r_{k}^{\prime}(s)|ds\leqslant C\beta_{k}

with
C
a universal constant independent of
k
(indeed, we use that
r_{k}
is a constant except maybe on a finite number of interval of length
2
where its derivative is bounded by
c\beta_{k}\|\phi^{\prime}\|_{\infty}
).

Finally there holds

(4.9)

\left|\int_{s}^{t}a_{n}(\sigma)e^{i\omega\sigma}d\sigma\right|\leqslant C

with
C
a universal constant independent of
s,n,t,\omega
. Indeed, for any
n
, using Remark (
3.1
), we know that, on the one hand,
|a_{n}|\leqslant 1
on
\mathbb{R}_{+}
, and on the other hand that, outside of a fixed finite number of intervals of length
2
(yielding a bounded contribution to the integral),
a_{n}
is either equal to zero, or equal to a finite linear combination with bounded number of terms of oscillating exponentials
e^{ift}
, with frequency
f=C^{\prime}\beta_{l}
with
C^{\prime}
a universal constant and
l\geqslant 0
. Thus, up to choosing
|m_{0}|
larger, we can impose that there always holds
|f|<1/2
. Hence, we are left with integrating oscillating exponentials
e^{i(f+\omega)\sigma}
where
|f+\omega|\geqslant 1/2
(since
|\omega|>1
). A simple integration is enough to conclude the proof of the claim.

This yields the bound

(4.10)

J(k,n,\omega,t)\leqslant C\beta_{k}

where
C
is a universal constant.

Moreover we see that
r_{k}(s)=0
for all
s\geqslant T_{k+1}
, thus there holds

(4.11)

J(k,n,\omega,t)=0\quad\forall t\geqslant T_{k+1}

From this we may infer the bound

(4.12)

\alpha(t)\leqslant C\displaystyle\sum_{k\geqslant k(t)}\beta_{k}

where we set
k(t)
the smallest non-negative integer such that
t\leqslant T_{k+1}
. Using moreover the fast decay of
\beta_{k}
we may further bound, up to taking a larger
C

(4.13)

\alpha(t)\leqslant C\beta_{k(t)}.

4.3
Estimates on
\beta(t)

As for
\beta(t)
, we see that for all
t
there is a unique
l(t)
such that
r_{k}(t)=0
as soon as
k\neq l(t)
, thus we find that

(4.14)

\beta(t)=4r_{l(t)}(t).

This yields the bound

\displaystyle\int_{t}^{s}\beta(\sigma)d\sigma

\displaystyle\leqslant 4\int_{0}^{s}r_{l(\sigma)}(\sigma)d\sigma

\displaystyle\leqslant C(k(s)+1);

indeed, we see that the integral of
r_{k}
over
\mathbb{R}_{+}
is a constant independent of
k
.

4.4
Conclusion of the estimates on
c^{N}

We may thus bound for
t\leqslant T_{N}

(4.15)

\|(c_{n}^{N}(t))\|_{l^{1}}\leqslant C\left(\beta_{k(t)}+\int_{t}^{T_{N}}\beta_{k(s)}\beta_{l(s)}\exp(C(k(s) + 1))ds\right)

Now, from the construction of
(r_{k}(s))
the holds
l(s)\geqslant k(s)
thus
\beta_{l(s)}\leqslant\beta_{k(s)}
. Therefore there holds (for
t\leqslant T_{N}
)

(4.16)

\|(c_{n}^{N}(t))\|_{l^{1}}\leqslant C\left(\beta_{k(t)}+\int_{t}^{T_{N}}\beta_{k(s)}^{2}\exp(C(k(s) + 1))ds\right)

Now,
k(s)
is equal to
k
on an intervall with measure
l_{k}
such that
l_{k}\beta_{k}
is equal to a constant, yielding the bound

(4.17)

\|(c_{n}^{N}(t))\|_{l^{1}}\leqslant C\left(\beta_{k(t)}+\displaystyle\sum_{k\geqslant k(t)}\beta_{k}e^{Ck}\right)

As
\beta_{k}
is decaying faster than a double exponential there stands finally

(4.18)

\forall t\leqslant T_{N}\quad\|(c_{n}^{N}(t))\|_{l^{1}}\leqslant C\beta_{k(t)}e^{Ck(t)}

5
Cauchy sequence and conclusion

5.1
Cauchy sequence

We now prove that
(c^{N})
is a Cauchy sequence in
l^{1}(\mathbb{Z})
. Set
M>N
; we look at the equation satisfied by
c^{M}-c^{N}

\displaystyle(c_{n}^{M}-c_{n}^{N})(t)

\displaystyle=-\displaystyle\sum_{m\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}}\int_{t}^{T_{N}}(c_{m}^{M}-c_{m}^{N})(s)v_{n-m}(s)\left(e^{i\omega_{m,n}^{+}s}-e^{i\omega_{m,n}^{-}s}\right)ds+c_{n}^{M}(T_{N})

\displaystyle-\displaystyle\sum_{m\notin\Gamma_{res}^{+}(n)}\int_{T_{N}}^{T_{M}}a_{m}(s)v_{n-m}(s)e^{-i\omega_{m,n}^{+}s}ds\ +\displaystyle\sum_{m\notin\Gamma_{res}^{-}(n)}\int_{T_{N}}^{T_{M}}a_{m}(s)v_{n-m}(s)e^{-i\omega_{m,n}^{-}s}ds

Thus

\displaystyle\|((c_{n}^{M}-c_{n}^{N})(t))\|_{l^{1}}

\displaystyle\leqslant 2\int_{t}^{T_{N}}\|((c_{n}^{M}-c_{n}^{N})(s))\|_{l^{1}}\|(v_{n}(s))\|_{l^{1}}ds+\|(c_{n}^{M}(T_{N}))\|_{l^{1}}

\displaystyle+\displaystyle\sum_{n}\displaystyle\sum_{m\notin\Gamma_{res}^{+}(n)}\left|\int_{T_{N}}^{T_{M}}a_{m}(s)v_{n-m}(s)e^{-i\omega_{m,n}^{+}s}ds\right|\ +\displaystyle\sum_{n}\displaystyle\sum_{m\notin\Gamma_{res}^{-}(n)}\left|\int_{T_{N}}^{T_{M}}a_{m}(s)v_{n-m}(s)e^{-i\omega_{m,n}^{-}s}ds\right|

\displaystyle\leqslant 2\int_{t}^{T_{N}}\|((c_{n}^{M}-c_{n}^{N})(s))\|_{l^{1}}\|(v_{n}(s))\|_{l^{1}}ds+C\beta_{k(T_{N})}e^{Ck(T_{N})}+C\beta_{k(T_{N})}

\displaystyle\leqslant 2\int_{t}^{T_{N}}\|((c_{n}^{M}-c_{n}^{N})(s))\|_{l^{1}}\|(v_{n}(s))\|_{l^{1}}ds+C\beta_{N-1}e^{C(N-1)}

Using backward Gronwall’s lemma

(5.1)

\|((c_{n}^{M}-c_{n}^{N})(t))\|_{l^{1}}\leqslant C\beta_{N-1}e^{C(N-1)}\left(1+\int_{t}^{T_{N}}\beta(s)\exp\left(\int_{t}^{s}\beta(\sigma)d\sigma\right)ds\right)

where
\beta(s)=2\|(v_{n}(s))\|_{l^{1}}
. We know that
\beta(s)=4r_{l(s)}(s)
and thus
\int_{s}^{t}\beta(\sigma)d\sigma\leqslant C(k(s)+1)
. There holds

(5.2)

\|((c_{n}^{M}-c_{n}^{N})(t))\|_{l^{1}}\leqslant C\beta_{N-1}e^{C(N-1)}\left(1+\int_{t}^{T_{N}}\beta_{k(s)}\exp(Ck(s))ds\right)

This upper bounds decays to zero as
N,M\to\infty
if we fix
t
. This shows that
(c^{N}(t))
is a Cauchy sequence in
l^{1}(\mathbb{Z}^{2})
and it thus converges to a
c(t)
such that, using integral form of the differential equation,
b=a+c
is a solution to
(\mathcal{RFS})
. There holds moreover

(5.3)

\|(c_{n}(t))\|_{l^{1}}\leqslant C\beta_{k(t)}e^{Ck(t)}

and this upper bound decays to zero as
t\to+\infty
as expected.

5.2
Growth of the Sobolev norm : qualitative result

In order to conclude, we recall that
\|(a_{n}(t))\|_{l^{2}}
is preserved and that for all
t\geqslant 0
there are at most 5 of the
a_{n}
that are nonzero. Therefore, we have on the one hand that for all
t\geqslant T_{n}
,
a_{k}=0
for
|k|<|m_{n}|
and on the other hand that there exists
|k|\geqslant|m_{n}|
such that
|a_{k}(t)|\geqslant\varepsilon
where
\varepsilon>0
is a universal constant. Now, if we set
N
large enough, we can ensure that
\|(c_{n}(t))\|_{l^{1}}\leqslant\varepsilon/2
for
t\geqslant T_{N}
. Therefore, for all

with
N
large enough, there exists
|k|\geqslant|m_{N}|
such that
b_{k}=a_{k}+c_{k}
satisfies

. Now, this ensures that

(5.4)

\forall t\geqslant T_{N}\quad\|(b_{n}(t))\|_{H^{s}}\geqslant|k|^{s}|b_{k}|\geqslant\varepsilon/2|m_{N}|^{s}

This already yields a qualitative result for theorem (
1.1
) as we already proved in section 3.4 that the potential
V
along with all its time derivatives are decaying in all Sobolev norms when

5.3
Quantitative estimates on the growth rate

We now investigate the quantitative bounds that we can hope to get on the rate of growth.

We first see that
T_{n}\leqslant C\beta_{n}^{-1}
using the fast decay of
\beta_{n}
. Moreover as
|l_{n}|\leqslant C^{n}n!
we find that

(5.5)

T_{n}\leqslant\exp\left(C^{n}n!\log\left(C^{n}n!\right)\right)

This yields the lower bound

(5.6)

\|(b_{n}(t))\|_{H^{s}}\geqslant\delta|m_{n(t)}|^{s}

where
\delta>0
is a constant, and
n(t)
the largest integer
n
such that
\exp\left(C^{n}n!\log\left(C^{n}n!\right)\right)\leqslant t
. Now, we know moreover that
|m_{n}|\geqslant c(n-1)!
, thus leading to the lower bound

(5.7)

\|u(t)\|_{H^{s}}\geqslant\varepsilon c^{s}((n(t)-1)!)^{s}.

In order to obtain better bounds, take
\eta>0
. We first use Stirling’s formula

(5.8)

n!\sim\left(\frac{n}{e}\right)^{n}\sqrt{2\pi n}

which ensures that provided
n
is large enough

(5.9)

C^{n}n!\log\left(C^{n}n!\right)\leqslant((1+\eta)n)^{(1+\eta)n}

Now set
f(x):=x^{x}
. We find that provided

(5.10)

f((1+\eta)n)\leqslant\log(t)

then, provided
n
is large enough, there stands that
n\leqslant n(t)
. Now, provided
n
is large enough, there also holds

(5.11)

(n-1)!\geqslant((1-\eta)n)^{(1-\eta)n}=f((1-\eta)n).

Thus, setting
E(x)
the largest integer
k
such that
k\leqslant x
we can find a lower bound of the form

\displaystyle\|u(t)\|_{H^{s}}

\displaystyle\geqslant\left(cf\left(\frac{1-\eta}{1+\eta}E(f^{-1}(\log(t)))\right)\right)^{s}

\displaystyle\geqslant c_{s,\eta}\exp\left(s\frac{(1-\eta)^{2}}{1+\eta}f^{-1}(\log(t))\log\left(\frac{(1-\eta)^{2}}{1+\eta}f^{-1}(\log(t))\right)\right)\ \text{provided t is large enough}

\displaystyle\geqslant c_{s,\eta}\exp\left(s\frac{(1-\eta)^{3}}{1+\eta}f^{-1}(\log(t))\log\left(f^{-1}(\log(t))\right)\right)\ \text{provided t is large enough}

\displaystyle\geqslant c_{s,\eta}\left(\log(t)\right)^{\frac{s(1-\eta)^{3}}{1+\eta}}.

As we may choose
\eta
arbitrarily, we find that given any
\delta,s>0
there exists
c_{\delta,s}>0
such that for
t>1

(5.12)

\|u(t)\|_{H^{s}}\geqslant c_{\delta,s}(\log t)^{s(1-\delta)},

thus concluding the proof of theorem (
1.1
)

5.4
Estimates on the decay rate of
V

We now prove similar upper bounds on the decay rate of the potential
V(t)
. Fix
s\geqslant 0
and
m\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
. Thanks to equation (
1.4
), we may bound

(5.13)

\|\partial^{m}_{t}V(t,\cdot)\|_{H^{s}}\leqslant c|l_{k(t)}|^{M-|l_{k(t)}|}

where
M=M_{m,s}>0
and
k(t)
is the unique
k\geqslant 0
such that
r_{k(t)}\neq 0
. We may furthemore infer from previous subsection that given
\delta>0
there exists
c_{\delta}>0
such that

(5.14)

|l_{k(t)}|\geqslant c_{\delta}(\log(t))^{1-\delta}

Thus

(5.15)

\|\partial^{m}_{t}V(t,\cdot)\|_{H^{s}}\leqslant C_{\delta}\exp((M_{m,s} - (\log(t))^{1 - \delta}) (1-\delta)\log(\log(t)))

As this holds for all
\delta>0
, we may conclude that for all
\delta>0
there exists
C_{\delta,m,s}
such that

(5.16)

\|\partial^{m}_{t}V(t,\cdot)\|_{H^{s}}\leqslant C_{\delta,m,s}\exp( - (\log(t))^{1 - \delta})\log(\log(t)))

As this yields a quantitative bounds for the decay of
V
, it should be noted that it is subpolynomial in the sense that the upper bound decays slower than
t^{-\varepsilon}
for all
\varepsilon>0
. It doesn’t seem that we can improve the bound, as on
[T_{N},T_{N+1}]

\|V(t)\|_{H^{1}}
is of order
\beta_{N}
, and
T_{N+1}
is of order
\beta_{N+1}^{-1}
. As for all
\varepsilon>0
asymptotically there holds
\beta_{N+1}^{\varepsilon}<<\beta_{N}
we thus cannot hope for a better bound.
