Direction Preferring Confidence Intervals
Tzviel Frostig, Yoav Benjamini, Ruth Heller

Abstract
Confidence intervals (CIs) are instrumental in statistical analysis, providing a range estimate of the parameters. In modern statistics, selective inference is common, where only certain parameters are highlighted. However, this selective approach can bias the inference, leading some to advocate for the use of CIs over p-values. To increase the flexibility of confidence intervals, we introduce direction-preferring CIs, enabling analysts to focus on parameters trending in a particular direction. We present these types of CIs in two settings: First, when there is no selection of parameters; and second, for situations involving parameter selection, where we offer a conditional version of the direction-preferring CIs. Both of these methods build upon the foundations of Modified Pratt CIs, which rely on non-equivariant acceptance regions to achieve longer intervals in exchange for improved sign exclusions. We show that for selected parameters out of m > 1 m>1 initial parameters of interest, CIs aimed at controlling the false coverage rate, have higher power to determine the sign compared to conditional CIs. We also show that conditional confidence intervals control the marginal false coverage rate (mFCR) under any dependency.



Keywords:
selective-inference, conditional confidence intervals, multiple hypotheses procedures

1
Introduction

Tukey 1991
lists three questions, in order of importance, to be answered sequentially about a parameter within the limit of acceptable uncertainty. i) what is the direction of the effect? ii) what is the minimal effect size? iii) what is the maximal effect size? He then turns to the use of confidence intervals (CIs) to answer these questions. If the sign (positive or negative effect) is indeterminable, a short two-sided interval is preferable. Conversely, if the sign is clear (suppose that it is positive), the lower end of the CI helps assess the minimal effect, ideally distancing from zero as much as possible. The upper end of the interval, though less critical, estimates the maximal effect and should certainly be finite and not too far away. The main thrust of Tukey’s work was to design CIs when multiple parameters are faced. Addressing multiplicity requires the inflation of the CIs which come at the expense of sign-determination. Tukey was trying to compromise between the three goals and the protection against selection, by superimposing the standard symmetric 95% CIs and Bonferroni CIs that offer simultaneous inference, using the latter at lower than usual levels (even 50%). While Tukey’s statement of the three goals have appealed to many, the compromises he designed have not received much attention to date.

The standard method to construct a (single) CI for a location family is to invert acceptance regions for testing the parameter. The acceptance regions are equivariant if their shape do not depend on the value of the parameter tested. Inverting equivariant acceptance regions results in a CI that is also equivariant, having the same shape around the estimate for any value of it, but as such it can not address to the differences in importance between the goals.
Non-equivariant CIs allow the designer to weight differently Tukey’s goals, extending the length of the CI in exchange for better sign determination or vice-versa
(
Pratt 1961
;
Hayter and Hsu 1994
;
Benjamini and Stark 1996
)
.

Incorporating into these efforts the concern about selective inference,
Benjamini et al. 1998
used the simultaneous approach to offer better separation from zero while ensuring simultaneous coverage.
Still, the improvements in sign determination and separation from zero was mainly useful for a small number of parameters. Methodological advances in the design of multiple CIs offer more attractive venues for compromise between addressing selection and sign determination. Rather than reducing the level of the Bonferroni intervals, adopt a compromising criterion, such as the false coverage rate (FCR) of
Benjamini and Yekutieli 2005
. FCR is the expected proportion of the the non-covering intervals constructed for the selected parameters. Another approach is to condition on the parameter being selected according to a specified selection criteria, and report the conditional CI. They too offer a set of selected CIs enjoying FCR control, see
Weinstein et al. 2013

Benjamini et al. 1998

(
Weinstein and Yekutieli 2020
)
.

All above previous suggestions treated both directions symmetrically. If two estimators turned out to be the same with opposite signs, one CI was the exact reflection of the other. In this work we suggest new direction-preferring confidence intervals, that addresses Tukey’s three goals in one direction better than it does in the other direction, allowing the analyst to emphasize one pre-chosen direction over the other.

In light of recent (arguable) recommendations to ditch the p-value and solely report CIs
(
Cumming 2014
)
, such CIs can be very useful. In typical medical applications, one sign of the parameter is of more interest then the other, and the analyst may prefer a more informative CI for the preferred sign.
For example, such CIs can be useful when analysing a new treatment, the interest primarly lies in whether the treatment is beneficial, and then in quantifying its minimal level of efficacy, while it is still of value to offer some quantitative assessment if the treatment is harmful
(
Bohrer 1979
)
. Moreover, in medical research it is rarely the case that only one measure of efficacy is reported. Reporting the results on multiple secondary outcomes is the rule, so developing methods for direction-preferring multiple CIs that are aligned with Tukey’s concerns is a worthy goal.

As far as we know, there are no methods that address this need directly.
Our main contributions are the following:

•

Recently,
Heller and Solari 2023
provided more general procedures that provide powerful simultaneous post-hoc bounds for the number of positive and non-positive parameters for any subset of parameters of interest.

Bauer et al. 1986
suggested testing positive vs non-positive hypotheses to achieve a more powerful Bonferroni correction. We adapt the philosophy to CIs, and introduce direction preferring CIs. The CIs have a preferred sign (for example positive) for which the CIs have better sign determination power, as well as a shorter length, and better effect size estimation, while still being able to determine if signal is non-positive.
Furthermore, we show how to correct the CIs to control the FCR directly or by the conditional coverage.

•

We prove that any conditional CIs can control a meaningful error measure, the marginal FCR (mFCR), under any dependency. Moreover, we demonstrate that the BY adjusted CIs
(
Benjamini and Yekutieli 2005
)
fail to control it. This result is of independent interest, suggesting that for highly dependent test statistics, for a meaningful error guarantee, it may be better to construct conditional CIs (if possible).

•

We prove that conditional CIs will have on average less power to determine the sign compared to FCR CIs (under some mild assumptions).

2
Preliminaries

Throughout the paper we will assume that
Y
follows a location family distribution. That is,
F_{\theta}(Y)=F(Y-\theta)
, where
\theta\in\mathcal{R}
is the location parameter and
F_{\theta}
is the cumulative distribution function of
Y
.
For simplicity we will assume that
F_{\theta}
is a symmetric unimodal distribution, however, with slight adjustments, the CIs presented can be modified to accommodate other types of distributions as well.
Denote by
q_{\theta}(\alpha)
the
1-\alpha
quantile of
Y
,
q_{\theta}(\alpha)=\left(F_{\theta}\right)^{-1}(1-\alpha)
.

The goal of the analyst is to construct confidence intervals for
\theta
. Let
\mathcal{A}(\theta_{0};\alpha)
be the acceptance region of testing
H_{0}:\theta=\theta_{0}
at significance level
\alpha
. A common approach to construct confidence intervals is by inverting the acceptance regions
(
Lehmann et al. 1986
)
. The confidence region constructed at confidence level
1-\alpha
is,

\mathcal{A}^{-1}(Y;\alpha)=\{\theta:Y\in\mathcal{A}(\theta;\alpha)\}.

The confidence intervals are taken as the convex hull of
\mathcal{A}^{-1}
, denoted by
\mathcal{I}(Y;\alpha)
, or simply
\mathcal{I}
when the meaning is clear.

2.1
Marginal CI

A natural approach in constructing acceptance regions is to seek the shortest one possible. Such a region can be defined as
\mathcal{A}_{srt}(\theta)=\{y:f_{\theta}(y)\geq\xi\}
, where
\xi
is the maximum value ensuring that
\mathbb{P}(Y\in\mathcal{A}_{srt})=1-\alpha
.

In a marginal setting, when considering only a single parameter without selection, the shortest acceptance region is the standard acceptance region for symmetric unimodal distributions,
F_{\theta}
. It is represented as

\mathcal{A}_{srt}(\theta;\alpha)=(\theta-q_{\theta}(\alpha/2),\theta+q_{\theta}(\alpha/2)).

Inverting the acceptance region results in the shortest confidence interval when considering a single parameter,

\mathcal{I}_{srt}(Y;\alpha)=(Y-q_{\theta}(\alpha/2),Y+q_{\theta}(\alpha/2)).

(1)

We refer to this CI as the shortest since it is the shortest acceptance region for unimodal distributions. The shortest CI is an example of an equivariant CI.

In contrast, the modified Pratt (MP) CI is a non-equivariant CI
(
Benjamini et al. 1998
)
. The idea behind the MP CI is to allow the inflation of up to
r
in the acceptance region length (compared to the shortest) in exchange for the acceptance region bound that determines the sign (for
\theta>0
, the lower bound, and for
\theta<0
, the upper bound), to grow further from
0
. For example, see Fig.
2
: for
\theta>0
, the lower bound of the MP acceptance region is higher than that of the shortest acceptance region, while for
\theta<0
, the upper bound of the MP acceptance region is lower. This results in better sign determination (i.e., whether
\theta\leq 0
, or
\theta\geq 0
) as evident in Table
1
: For
Y\sim N(\theta,1)
the MP CI determines
\theta\leq 0
when
Y<-1.65
, while the shortest achieves this for
Y<-1.96
. For a detailed description of the CI, see appendix
A
.

2.2
Conditional CI

Conditional CIs are tailored to the specific selection criterion used, and the CI is constructed only if the parameter is selected. The most prevalent mode of selection is selecting a parameter if its estimator
|Y|
is above a certain threshold. The selection threshold can be determined by an effect size or, by requiring significance, i.e., by being a multiple of standard error (say 1.96). Requiring significance is common practice in the medical research literature, as well as in the social sciences.
If a marginal CI is constructed only when the parameter is selected, then it no longer hold its coverage guarantee.
To handle such selection,
Weinstein et al. 2013
proposed confidence intervals that control the conditional coverage,

\mathbb{P}(\theta\in\mathcal{I}_{conditional}(Y)||Y|>c)\geq 1-\alpha.

We denote the conditional distribution
Y||Y|>c
by superscript
c
.
F^{c}_{\theta}
,
f^{c}_{\theta}
, and
q^{c}_{\theta}
are the distribution, density and quantile function of the conditional distribution. The conditional distribution is truncated at the selection threshold and the density scaled according to the total truncated probability.

For each of the confidence intervals discussed in the marginal setting (i.e., shortest and MP), there is the respective conditional CI which is constructed by replacing
f_{\theta}(y)
with
f^{c}_{\theta}
when obtaining the acceptance region.
For example, the shortest acceptance region is obtained by finding
\mathcal{A}_{srt}^{c}(\theta)=\{y:f_{\theta}^{c}(y)\geq\xi\}
, where
\xi
is such that
\mathbb{P}\left(Y\in\mathcal{A}_{srt}^{c}(\theta)\right)=1-\alpha
. See Fig.
2
for a visual representation and appendix
A
for a detailed description.

Remark 2.1

The MP CIs can have a shorter length compared to the shortest CI when
Y
is close to
0
.
For small
|Y|
, the CI is determined by the upper bound of an acceptance region obtained for
\theta<0
, and the lower bound of an acceptance region obtained for
\theta>0
(see Fig.
2
).
As both acceptance region bounds are closer to
0
compared to those of the shortest acceptance region, after the inversion, the resulting CI is shorter than the shortest CI.
This happens only while zero is included in the CI, so this does not aid in the determination of the direction of the effect. As
|Y|
grows larger, and the CI results in the inversion of acceptance regions of a sole sign, the MP CI becomes longer than the shortest CI.

3
Direction Preferring Confidence Intervals

We introduce new direction-preferring confidence intervals. These allow analysts to construct CIs based on a predisposition towards the sign of the parameters. For concreteness we will discuss positive-preferring CIs, as adapting them to be negative-preferring is straightforward by reversing the roles of the negative and positive
\theta
. The predisposition is in the sense that, for positive
\theta
, the analyst wishes to have higher power for positive determination (
\theta>0
) and as tight a CI as possible. Conversely, for negative
\theta
, the analyst will be content with positive sign exclusion (
\theta\leq 0
) and a longer CI.

We show how to construct such direction-preferring CIs for both the marginal and conditional setting. In both cases the parameter
r_{-}
controls the inflation of the acceptance region for
\theta\leq 0
. It is set according to the analyst sensitivity to the inflation of the CI length for
Y<0
. See Section
3.3
for some guidance on the selection of
r_{-}
.

\theta\leq 0

\theta>0

Y>0
and length comparable with the shortest CI.

The idea of inflating the acceptance region length in exchange for better sign determination was originally suggested in the marginal setting by
Benjamini et al. 1998
and in the conditional setting by
Weinstein et al. 2013
. As noted in the introduction, in these original suggestions, the inflation is symmetric for both signs and absent for
\theta=0
. The direction preferring CIs have two parameters controlling the inflation
r_{-}>1
, and
r_{+}=1
(in the positive preferring situation we address). Furthermore, the acceptance region does not revert to the shortest one when
\theta=0
, but is constructed as with negative
\theta
. The inversion of such acceptance region leads to confidence intervals that are more powerful for determining the sign of positive
\theta
compared to the shortest CI, and are shorter than the MP CI, at the expense of being longer than the MP for negative
\theta
.

Additionally, when
\theta
is sufficiently large such that the shortest acceptance region will no longer cover
0
, then the acceptance region reverts to the shortest one. As a result, for sufficiently large
Y
the CI is the same as the shortest one, resulting in shorter CIs compared to the MP CI.

We denote the positive-preferring acceptance region by
\mathcal{A}_{pp}
. The positive-preferring acceptance region in the marginal setting is,

\mathcal{A}_{pp}(\theta;r_{-},\alpha)=\begin{cases}\begin{aligned} &(\theta-q_{\theta}(\alpha/2),\theta+q_{\theta}(\alpha/2)),&\quad\theta\leq-q_{\theta}(\alpha/2)\\
&(\theta-q_{\theta}(\beta_{-}),\theta+q_{\theta}(\alpha-\beta_{-})),&\quad-q_{\theta}(\alpha/2)<\theta\leq 0\\
&(\theta-q_{\theta}(\alpha/2),\theta+q_{\theta}(\alpha/2)),&\quad 0<\theta\\
\end{aligned}\end{cases}

(2)

where,
\beta_{-}\in[\alpha/2,\alpha]
is the solution of
q_{\theta}(\alpha-\beta_{-})+q_{\theta}(\beta)=r_{-}2q_{\theta}(\alpha/2).

Positive preferring conditional CI

Suppose an analyst is interested in constructing positive preferring CIs for the selected parameter,
\theta

|Y|>c

3.4
Simulations comparing PP with MP and shortest CIs

By construction, the direction-preferring CIs will have better sign determination for the preferred sign compared to the shortest and MP CIs. The goal of the simulation is to quantify the increase in sign-determination, as well as the length and the minimal effect estimate.
We adopt Tukey’s point of view, in which
\theta\neq 0
. This implies we are interested in sign-determination with or without the exclusion of 0.
The test statistic
Y
is sampled from
N(\theta_{i},1)
where
\theta_{i}\in\{-4,-3.95,\ldots,4\}
. In the conditional setting samples are drawn from the truncated normal distribution
Y||Y|>1.96
.

Three metrics are compared: the average length of the CI; the average minimal effect estimate (for
\theta>0
it is the average of the CI lower bound, and for
\theta<0
it is the average of the CI upper bound); the probability of sign determination from the CIs (i.e., the probability that the interval includes only one sign).

r_{-}=1.3
, and for the MP CIs
r=1.3
. Results are based on 1000 data generations for every

Figure

As expected the PP CIs have for
\theta>0
: the same estimation of the minimal effect as the shortest CI, but with better sign determination; the same sign determination as the MP CIs, but with a much higher (more informative) minimal effect. On the other hand, for
\theta<0
, the PP CIs have: higher (less informative) minimal effect that the shortest CIs, with the same sign determination; worse sign determination that the MP Cis, with the same minimal effect estimation.

In terms of length, due to the reversion to the shortest CIs for sufficiently large estimator value, for large values of
|\theta|
the PP CIs have shorter length compared to the MP. For smaller
|\theta|
the MP CIs is the shortest.

3.5
A qualitative comparison of PP with asymmetric equivariant CIs

A different approach to construct direction preferring CIs, is by constructing equivariant CIs where the confidence level differs between the lower and upper bounds. In the marginal case the CI is

\mathcal{I}_{eq}(Y;\beta_{-},\beta_{+})=(Y-q_{\theta}(\beta_{-}),Y+q_{\theta}(\beta_{+})),

where
\beta_{-}+\beta_{+}=\alpha
. The analyst can control the preference for each direction of the parameter by adjusting
\beta_{+}
and
\beta_{-}
.

Figure 5:
Comparison of the PP (1.3), Shortest and Equivariant (1.3) CIs. The Equivariant (1.3) CI remains longer than the Shortest CIs for all the range of
Y
. Furthermore, for the negative signs, it has worst sign determination compared to the PP (1.3), but a higher lower bound for the positive
Y
.

To make a fair comparison between the equivariant CI and the PP (1.3) CI, we set
\beta_{+},\beta_{-}
such that the power for the positive sign-determination equals that of the PP (

) CI. Consequently, this setting results in equivariant CI being 1.3 times larger than the shortest CI. This happens since the acceptance region is
1.3
times larger than the shortest acceptance region.

As shown in Fig.

, this comparison highlights several benefits of the PP (1.3) CI over its equivariant counterpart. Specifically, the PP (1.3), excludes positive signs for

compared to
Y<-3.45

. The advantage of the Equivariant (1.3) CI is a higher lower-bound for

, but this comes at a cost of a much longer CI (

4
CIs for the selected parameters

In many modern scientific endeavours some selection of parameters takes place
(
Benjamini 2020
)
. When ignoring the selection the inference is biased and the coverage guarantees of the CIs no longer hold. Let
Y_{i}\sim N(\theta_{i},1)
denote the estimators of
m
parameters from which some are selected using a selection rule
S(.)
, which is a mapping from
\mathcal{R}^{m}
to the power set of
\{1,\ldots,m\}
. The set
S(Y)
consists of the indices of the selected parameters.
For each selected parameter
\theta_{j},j\in S(Y),
a CI
\mathcal{I}(Y_{j};\alpha)
is constructed. Denote by
\mathrm{V}
the number of non-covering CIs
\mathrm{V}=|\{j:\theta_{j}\notin\mathcal{I}(Y_{j}),j\in S(Y)\}|
.

There are a few different types of false coverage error rates that the CIs can control after selection. First, simultaneous non-coverage,
\mathbb{P}(\mathrm{V}\geq 1)
. The Bonferroni adjustment, using a confidence level of
1-\frac{\alpha}{m}
for each parameter, is the simplest adjustment that provides simultaneous coverage at level
1-\alpha
.

Second, the false coverage rate (FCR),

\mathrm{FCR}:\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{V}{\max(|S(Y)|,1)}\right].

FCR control was first introduced by
Benjamini and Yekutieli 2005
, who suggest adjusting the confidence level to
1-\frac{k}{m}\alpha
, where
k=|S(y)|
, to control the FCR at level
\alpha
. The adjused CIs will be henceforth referred to as BY05-adjusted CIs. The correction is proven to control the FCR at level
\alpha
for independent estimators in general. It was also proven to control the FCR for a specific type of positive dependency and one-sided selection. Empirically, the FCR is controlled under a wide variety of selection rules and dependencies.

Third, the conditional coverage
Weinstein et al. 2013
.
\mathcal{I}^{c}(Y;\alpha)
controls the conditional coverage probability at level
1-\alpha
if

\mathcal{I}^{c}(Y;\alpha):\mathbb{P}\left(\theta_{i}\in\mathcal{I}^{c}(Y_{i};\alpha)|i\in S(Y)\right)\geq 1-\alpha.

(4)

Conditional coverage control entails FCR control for independent test statistics
(
Weinstein et al. 2013
)
.The conditional control applies to each specific parameter individually, whereas the control of FWER or FCR is based on average expectations across all parameters. So,
the conditional coverage is controlled regardless of the number of parameters
m

4.1
Comparing BY05-adjusted and conditional CIs

Conditional CIs control the FCR as well as the conditional coverage (for independent estimators). This invites a comparison with the CIs designed to control the FCR, such as the BY05- adjusted CIs.

BY adjusted CIs require knowledge of both the number of selected parameters and the total parameter count, whereas conditional CIs depend solely on the estimator and the selection criteria. This distinction has practical implications: constructing conditional CIs necessitates obtaining the conditional distribution, while any existing CIs can be modified to control the FCR by simply adjusting the coverage probability. As a result, the BY CI adjustment is applicable to both PP and MP CIs suggested for the marginal setting. For example, the BY adjusted MP CI will simply be

The BY CIs are more restrictive, with the control holding solely for the specific set of selected parameters and their respective CIs.

Discussion

CIs are commonly used with hypothesis testing. Unlike CIs, hypothesis testing can indicate the direction or sign of a parameter but fails to convey the effect size and associated uncertainty in parameter estimation. This problem is exacerbated by ’selective inference,’ which occurs when only certain parameters are selected and reported. Considering the importance of adjusting CIs for selection, the question arises: which selection adjustment approach should be employed? These methods can be evaluated based on their flexibility and sign-determination capability. The simultaneous method is the most flexible, able to adjust any existing CIs and maintain control across any subset of selected parameters, albeit with the least power. The conditional probability control requires the construction of designated CIs but allows for subset selection while controlling the conditional coverage probability (the FCR and the mFCR), offering higher sign-determination capability compared to the simultaneous approach. Finally, the FCR control can be applied to any CI and offers the highest sign-determination power. However, its control is limited to the initially selected set of parameters; any further selection invalidates the FCR control.

Another consideration in construction of CIs is the sign-preference. Analysts can construct direction-preferring CIs by allocating the coverage probability
\alpha
differently between the ends of the CIs. While this approach yields better minimum effect size estimation for parameters of the preferred sign, it can significantly reduce sign-determination power for the non-preferred sign. The suggested non-equivariant direction-preferring CIs strike a better balance between these two needs. For the non-preferred sign, the weak sign-determination is barely affected, while the estimation of the minimal effect size is reduced. However, the preferred sign benefits from higher sign-determination power and improved minimal effect size estimation.

direction-preferring CIs may be particularly useful in secondary endpoint analysis in clinical trials where the analyst’s interest lies in detecting beneficial endpoints. They also allow identification of potentially harmful endpoints, though not less accurate estimate of the extent of harm. Furthermore, it is common in clinical trials to report only some secondary endpoints in subsequent studies. In such cases, it is recommended to use the conditional CIs approach, which ensures that the guarantees still hold for the subset of selected parameters.
