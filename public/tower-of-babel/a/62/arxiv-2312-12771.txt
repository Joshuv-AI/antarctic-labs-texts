Variational formulation and monolithic solution of computational homogenization methods.
Christian Hesch

Abstract




Abstract

In this contribution, we derive a consistent variational formulation for computational homogenization methods and show that traditional FE
2
and IGA
2
approaches are special discretization and solution techniques of this most general framework. This allows us to enhance dramatically the numerical analysis as well as the solution of the arising algebraic system. In particular, we expand the dimension of the continuous system, discretize the higher dimensional problem consistently and apply afterwards a discrete null-space matrix to remove the additional dimensions. A benchmark problem, for which we can obtain an analytical solution, demonstrates the superiority of the chosen approach aiming to reduce the immense computational costs of traditional FE
2
and IGA
2
formulations to a fraction of the original requirements. Finally, we demonstrate a further reduction of the computational costs for the solution of general non-linear problems.

Keywords
: Variational, multiscale, linearisation, FE
2
, IGA
2
, representative volume element, computational homogenization, null-space.

1
Introduction

Complex materials with dedicated morphological features like fibers or additive manufactured microstructures are nowadays used throughout the industry. To obtain a suitable model, continuum mechanical or thermomechanical formulations are often used based on infinitesimal strain measures. Obviously, every possible finite inhomogeneity can be resolved by an infinitesimal model, however, the computational costs of such a large scale approximative solution may economically not affordable or in certain cases technically not possible. Additionally, a material may appear perfectly homogeneous on the considered scale of approximations, whereas the microstructural information has been lost and often we are only interested in the macroscale solution. Therefore, two different approaches are common to include this information. First, the microstructure can be taken into account in a phenomenological way. The resulting constitutive relations can also incorporate size effects, leading to generalized theories for materials, see
[
14
,
1
,
2
]
for a general overview of gradient extended continua. In contrast, microstructural information about morphology and material properties can be accounted for in a more explicit manner by means of homogenization methods, assuming that the scales of the finite approximation and the morphological features on the microscale are clearly separated.

In the context of first-order homogenization schemes, we refer to
[
8
,
7
,
9
,
16
,
25
]
for fundamental analytical approaches and to
[
21
,
15
,
5
,
11
]
for seminal contributions to two-scale finite-element (FE) simulations. For computational multiscale techniques using the well-known FE
2
-methods we refer to the review article in
[
19
]
and to
[
13
]
for a general overview of computational multiscale techniques. The application to solids at finite strains including physically coupled problems in thermo-elasticity are presented in
[
24
,
23
]
and for electro-elasticity in
[
10
]
. The general extension on computational homogenization of strain gradient materials using Isogeometric Analysis, labeled IGA
2
-method, is presented in our preliminary work in
[
18
]
.

Traditional methods like FE
2
and IGA
2
consider a representative volume element (RVE) at every Gauss point. Since the primary idea was to utilize the RVE and the corresponding set of partial differential equations representing the static equilibrium of some kind of a microstructure in exchange of a constitutive law. However, we already know from concepts like the Hu-Washizu functional (see Zienkiewicz et al.
[
27
,
26
]
) that the relation between the strain energy function and the stress tensor can be considered as an independent equation in a weak form to be solved using again a finite element framework. To this end, the stress tensor is not evaluated at every Gauss point but rather interpolated using the applied shape functions for the solution of the weak form, see Bonet et al.
[
4
]
for details on the implementation of Hu-Washizu and Hellinger-Reissner type formulations.

In this work, we propose a dimensional expansion of the macroscopic system for the calculations within the RVE. The arising microscopical fluctuations are interpolated in the expanded, higher-dimensional framework using continuous shape functions on the microscale and discontinuous shape functions on the macroscale to allow again for a static condensation procedure, written here in the form of a null-space reduction scheme. With this monolithic framework, we can reconstruct traditional methods like FE
2
and IGA
2
by using Delta Dirac on the Gauss points and the application of a staggered scheme between the dimensions. However, based on the variational formulation we can now make use of sophisticated methods for the solution of the arising large scale algebraic system of equations. As already shown in Lange et al.
[
12
]
, monolithic solution techniques using static condensation procedures are by far superior to the original staggered scheme. Both, suitable choices of the macroscale shape functions and the application of sophisticated solution techniques lead to a dramatic reduction of the computational cost preserving the accuracy of the solution. Additionally, this most general approach allows for the application of highly efficient methods like recursive trust-region multigrid formulations, see, among others, Gross & Krause
[
6
]
.

3

4
. Representative examples are given in Section

2
Governing equations

We start with a short summary of the governing equations for the macro- and afterwards for the microscale. At the end of this section, we will introduce a common variational formulation for both scales. With regard to notation, Einstein’s summation convention is used in the following for clearer presentation.

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}^{\delta\bm{\varphi}}

2
evaluate the microscopic fluctuations at the integration points
\bm{X}_{\mathrm{ip}}^{b}
, which is achieved by formally setting
R^{b}(\bm{X})=\delta(\bm{X}-\bm{X}_{\mathrm{ip}}^{b})
,

b=1,\ldots n_{\mathrm{ip}}
(i.e.
n_{\mathrm{micro}}^{1}=n_{\mathrm{ip}}
), such that we obtain virtual fluctuations of the form

\delta\tilde{\bm{w}}^{\operatorname{h}}(\bm{X},\tilde{\bm{X}})=\sum\limits_{b=1}^{n_{\mathrm{ip}}}\sum\limits_{c\in\tilde{\omega}_{2}}\delta(\bm{X}-\bm{X}_{\mathrm{ip}}^{b})\tilde{R}^{c}(\tilde{\bm{X}})\delta\tilde{\bm{w}}_{b,c}.

(31)

Hence, (
24
) reads now

\sum\limits_{b=1}^{n_{\mathrm{ip}}}\,\delta\tilde{\bm{w}}_{b,c}\cdot\int\limits_{\Omega_{0}^{\operatorname{h}}}\tilde{\bm{P}}^{\operatorname{h}}(\bm{X}_{\mathrm{ip}}^{b},\tilde{\bm{X}})\nabla_{\hskip-2.56073pt\mbox{\scriptsize$\mathrm{\tilde{\bm{X}}}$}}\hskip 0.85358pt\tilde{R}^{c}(\tilde{\bm{X}})\,\operatorname{d}\!\tilde{V}=0,\quad\forall\delta\tilde{\bm{w}}_{b,c}\in\mathbb{R}^{n},

(32)

where we multiplied the equation by the factor
|\Omega_{0}^{\operatorname{h}}|
. Note, that the last statement explicitly reproduces a static equilibrium with regard to the boundary conditions presented in (
15
) on the microscale at each macroscale integration point.

According to the choice of the virtual fluctuations, we use Kronecker delta functions, the
discrete analogues of the Delta-Dirac pulses, to interpolate the solutions,
i.e. we introduce interpolations of the form

\tilde{\bm{w}}^{\operatorname{h}}(\bm{X},\tilde{\bm{X}})=\sum\limits_{b=1}^{n_{\mathrm{ip}}}\sum\limits_{c\in\tilde{\omega}_{2}}\delta^{\prime}(\bm{X}-\bm{X}_{\mathrm{ip}}^{b})\tilde{R}^{c}(\tilde{\bm{X}})\tilde{\bm{w}}_{b,c},\quad\delta^{\prime}(\bm{X}):=\begin{cases}1,&\text{if }\bm{X}=\bm{0},\\
0,&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

(33)

Insertion in (
28
) and (
32
) along with a suitable quadrature on the microscale yields

\displaystyle\sum_{b=1}^{n_{\mathrm{ip}}}\eta_{k}\left[\frac{1}{|\Omega_{0}^{\operatorname{h}}|}\sum\limits_{l=1}^{n_{\mathrm{ip}}^{micro}}\left[\tilde{\eta}_{l}\,\tilde{\bm{P}}^{\operatorname{h}}\left(\bm{F}^{\operatorname{h}}\left(\bm{X}_{\mathrm{ip}}^{b}\right),\tilde{\bm{F}}^{\prime,\operatorname{h}}\left(\bm{X}_{\mathrm{ip}}^{b},\tilde{\bm{X}}_{\mathrm{ip}}^{l}\right)\right)\right]\nabla_{\hskip-2.56073pt\mbox{\scriptsize$\mathrm{\bm{X}}$}}\hskip 0.85358ptN^{A}\left(\bm{X}_{\mathrm{ip}}^{b}\right)\right]=\bm{F}^{\operatorname{ext},A}_{\text{quad}},\quad A\in\omega,

(34)

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{l=1}^{n_{\mathrm{ip}}^{micro}}\tilde{\eta}_{l}\left[\tilde{\bm{P}}^{\operatorname{h}}\left(\bm{F}^{\operatorname{h}}\left(\bm{X}_{\mathrm{ip}}^{b}\right),\tilde{\bm{F}}^{\prime,\operatorname{h}}\left(\bm{X}_{\mathrm{ip}}^{b},\tilde{\bm{X}}_{\mathrm{ip}}^{l}\right)\right)\nabla_{\hskip-2.56073pt\mbox{\scriptsize$\mathrm{\tilde{\bm{X}}}$}}\hskip 0.85358pt\tilde{R}^{c}\left(\tilde{\bm{X}}_{\mathrm{ip}}^{l}\right)\right]=\bm{0},\quad\left(b,c\right)\in\tilde{\omega}_{1}\times\tilde{\omega}_{2},

(35)

where
\tilde{\eta}_{l}
are the quadrature weights at the
n_{\mathrm{ip}}^{micro}
integration point to evaluate the integral on
\Omega_{0}^{\operatorname{h}}
.

Remark 2
.

Eqs. (
34
) and (
35
) represent the classical FE
2
method, evaluated in a staggered solution
process to be discussed in the next section.

Other choices to approximate
\tilde{\bm{w}}
and
\delta\tilde{\bm{w}}
within
\mathcal{B}_{0}
using
suitable interpolation functions are certainly possible. We refer to
[
4
]
among others, where
discontinuous interpolation functions are introduced in the context of Hu-Washizu and Hellinger-Reissner formulations.
As the microscopic fluctuations are applied to calculate the stresses, we obtain similar continuity requirements for both.
The next step after the Dirac approach described above may be the use of piecewise constant approximation functions
on the macroscale. In particular, the construction of suitable bases no longer has to be based on the quadrature
formula used and the underlying system of equations generally has the form

\displaystyle\int\limits_{\mathcal{B}_{0}^{\operatorname{h}}}\frac{1}{|\Omega_{0}^{\operatorname{h}}|}\int\limits_{\Omega_{0}^{\operatorname{h}}}\tilde{\bm{P}}(\bm{F}^{\operatorname{h}}(\bm{X}),\tilde{\bm{F}}^{\prime,\operatorname{h}}(\bm{X},\tilde{\bm{X}}))\,\operatorname{d}\!\tilde{V}\,\nabla_{\hskip-2.56073pt\mbox{\scriptsize$\mathrm{\bm{X}}$}}\hskip 0.85358ptN^{A}(\bm{X})\,\operatorname{d}\!V=\bm{F}^{\mathrm{ext},A},

\displaystyle A\in\omega,

(36)

\displaystyle\int\limits_{\mathcal{B}_{0}^{\operatorname{h}}}\frac{1}{|\Omega_{0}^{\operatorname{h}}|}\int\limits_{\Omega_{0}^{\operatorname{h}}}\tilde{\bm{P}}(\bm{F}^{\operatorname{h}}(\bm{X}),\tilde{\bm{F}}^{\prime,\operatorname{h}}(\bm{X},\tilde{\bm{X}}))\nabla_{\hskip-2.56073pt\mbox{\scriptsize$\mathrm{\tilde{\bm{X}}}$}}\hskip 0.85358pt\bar{R}^{B}(\bm{X},\tilde{\bm{X}})\,\operatorname{d}\!\tilde{V}\,\operatorname{d}\!V=\bm{0},

\displaystyle B\in\tilde{\omega}.

(37)

Using linear or even higher-order shape functions for
\{R^{b}\}_{b=1}^{n_{\mathrm{micro}}^{1}}
can be done in a straight-forward
manner; however, we have to ensure that the shape functions are discontinuous at the element boundaries to allow for a block
diagonal sub-matrix of the Hessian to be used within the following null-space reduction scheme. Moreover, we have to ensure that
we do not obtain stability issues, as a higher-order interpolation of the micro-fluctuations (and thus, of the stresses to be calculated)
may conflict with a lower-order interpolation of the macroscale deformation map. Both equations (
36
) and
(
37
) can be rewritten as
\delta\bm{q}_{A}\cdot\bm{R}^{A}_{\mathrm{macro}}=0
and

\delta\tilde{\bm{w}}_{B}\cdot\bm{R}^{B}_{\mathrm{micro}}=0
, where
\bm{R}^{A}_{\mathrm{macro}}
and
\bm{R}^{B}_{\mathrm{micro}}
are the corresponding residual vector, to be used within a Newton-Raphson iteration as shown in the next section.

4
Solution of the multiscale problem

The non-linear multiscale framework at hand is solved by introducing a Newton-Raphson iteration,
noting that further enhancement like line-search or trust-region methods can be applied as well,
see
[
20
]
for details. In particular, we solve

[\delta\bm{q}_{A},\,\delta\tilde{\bm{w}}_{B}]\cdot\begin{bmatrix}\bm{K}^{AC}&\bm{D}^{AD}\\
\bm{E}^{BC}&\bm{L}^{BD}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\Delta\bm{q}_{C}\\
\Delta\tilde{\bm{w}}_{D}\end{bmatrix}=-[\delta\bm{q}_{A},\,\delta\tilde{\bm{w}}_{B}]\cdot\begin{bmatrix}\bm{R}^{A}_{\text{macro}}\\
\bm{R}^{B}_{\text{micro}}\end{bmatrix}.

(38)

Afterwards, both values are updated via
\bm{q}_{A}\leftarrow\bm{q}_{A}+\Delta\bm{q}_{A}
and
\tilde{\bm{w}}_{A}\leftarrow\tilde{\bm{w}}_{A}+\Delta\tilde{\bm{w}}_{A}
and the iteration restarts until
\|[\bm{R}^{\mathrm{T}}_{\text{macro}},\,\bm{R}^{\mathrm{T}}_{\text{micro}}]^{\mathrm{T}}\|<\epsilon_{NR}
with a predefined stop criterion
\epsilon_{NR}
. The tangent matrix is composed of four terms, given by

\bm{K}^{AC}=\int\limits_{\mathcal{B}_{0}^{\operatorname{h}}}\nabla_{\hskip-2.56073pt\mbox{\scriptsize$\mathrm{\bm{X}}$}}\hskip 0.85358pt(N^{A})\cdot\frac{1}{|\Omega_{0}^{\operatorname{h}}|}\int\limits_{\Omega_{0}^{\operatorname{h}}}\frac{\partial^{2}\tilde{\Psi}}{\partial\tilde{\bm{F}}\partial\tilde{\bm{F}}}\operatorname{d}\!\tilde{V}\,\nabla_{\hskip-2.56073pt\mbox{\scriptsize$\mathrm{\bm{X}}$}}\hskip 0.85358pt(N^{C})\operatorname{d}\!V

(39)

and

\bm{D}^{AD}=\int\limits_{\mathcal{B}_{0}^{\operatorname{h}}}\nabla_{\hskip-2.56073pt\mbox{\scriptsize$\mathrm{\bm{X}}$}}\hskip 0.85358pt(N^{A})\cdot\frac{1}{|\Omega_{0}^{\operatorname{h}}|}\int\limits_{\Omega_{0}^{\operatorname{h}}}\frac{\partial^{2}\tilde{\Psi}}{\partial\tilde{\bm{F}}\partial\tilde{\bm{F}}}\nabla_{\hskip-2.56073pt\mbox{\scriptsize$\mathrm{\tilde{\bm{X}}}$}}\hskip 0.85358pt(\bar{R}^{D})\operatorname{d}\!\tilde{V}\operatorname{d}\!V.

(40)

As the tangent matrix is the Hessian resulting from the condition in (
19
), the matrix is
symmetric and
\bm{E}=\bm{D}^{\mathrm{T}}
. Eventually, the last term is given by

\bm{L}^{BD}=\int\limits_{\mathcal{B}_{0}^{\operatorname{h}}}\frac{1}{|\Omega_{0}^{\operatorname{h}}|}\int\limits_{\Omega_{0}^{\operatorname{h}}}\nabla_{\hskip-2.56073pt\mbox{\scriptsize$\mathrm{\tilde{\bm{X}}}$}}\hskip 0.85358pt(\bar{R}^{B})\cdot\frac{\partial^{2}\tilde{\Psi}}{\partial\tilde{\bm{F}}\partial\tilde{\bm{F}}}\nabla_{\hskip-2.56073pt\mbox{\scriptsize$\mathrm{\tilde{\bm{X}}}$}}\hskip 0.85358pt(\bar{R}^{D})\operatorname{d}\!\tilde{V}\operatorname{d}\!V

(41)

and the corresponding global algebraic system reads

\begin{bmatrix}\bm{K}&\bm{D}\\
\bm{E}&\bm{L}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\Delta\bm{q}\\
\Delta\tilde{\bm{w}}\end{bmatrix}=-\begin{bmatrix}\bm{R}_{\text{macro}}\\
\bm{R}_{\text{micro}}\end{bmatrix},

(42)

where
\Delta\bm{q}
,
\Delta\tilde{\bm{w}}
summarize the unknown node data
\Delta\bm{q}_{A}
,
\Delta\tilde{\bm{w}}_{B}
and
the individual blocks are composed according to Eqs. (
38
)-(
41
).

4.1
Classical solution strategy

In classical FE
2
methods as presented in, e.g.,
[
10
]
for nonlinear electromechanical multiscale problems,
a staggered scheme is applied. First, (
42
) is solved using fixed
\bm{q}

§

§

Analogous to the introductory considerations,
\bm{q}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\cdot n_{\mathrm{node}}}
and

\tilde{\bm{w}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\cdot n_{\mathrm{micro}}}
summarize in the following the node degrees

\bm{q}_{A}
and

respectively.

until
\|\bm{R}_{\text{micro}}\|<\epsilon_{NR}^{\text{micro}}
. Then, the second line of (

\bm{E}\Delta\bm{q}+\bm{L}\Delta\tilde{\bm{w}}=\bm{0}.

(43)

Since the matrix

As we have to solve the linear system in the second line of (

4.2
Generalization of the solution strategy

The solution strategy introduced in the previous section can be rewritten as premultiplication with the rectangular
null-space matrix

\bm{P}=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{I},&-\bm{D}\bm{L}^{-1}\end{bmatrix}^{\mathrm{T}},

(46)

where
\bm{I}
is the unity matrix of dimension
{n_{\text{node}}\times n_{\text{node}}}
.
Now, instead of applying a staggered scheme, we multiply the linear system composed of both scales using the null-space matrix

\bm{P}^{\mathrm{T}}\cdot\begin{bmatrix}\bm{K}&\bm{D}\\
\bm{E}&\bm{L}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\Delta\bm{q}\\
\Delta\tilde{\bm{w}}\end{bmatrix}=-\bm{P}^{\mathrm{T}}\cdot\begin{bmatrix}\bm{R}_{\text{macro}}\\
\bm{R}_{\text{micro}}\end{bmatrix},

(47)

which yields

\left[\bm{K}-\bm{D}\bm{L}^{-1}\bm{E}\right]\Delta\bm{q}=-\left[\bm{R}_{\text{macro}}-\bm{D}\bm{L}^{-1}\bm{R}_{\text{micro}}\right].

(48)

Finally, we need to calculate
\Delta\tilde{\bm{w}}
via

\Delta\tilde{\bm{w}}=-\bm{L}^{-1}\left[\bm{R}_{\text{micro}}+\bm{E}\Delta\bm{q}\right],

(49)

and update both values
\bm{q}
and
\tilde{\bm{w}}
within the Newton-Raphson iteration.

Remark 3
.

For the non-linear case,
\|\bm{R}_{\text{micro}}\|
is usually unequal zero due to numerical issues, hence (
43
)
is never valid and may affect the quadratic convergence of the Newton-Raphson iteration. Thus, the proposed
approach is in general advantageous as we can apply the staggered scheme to (
48
). In comparison
to classical (staggered) schemes, the term
-\bm{D}\bm{L}^{-1}\bm{R}_{\text{micro}}
on the right-hand side
of (
48
) is introduced, noting again, that
\bm{R}_{\text{micro}}
is never exact zero.

6
Conclusions

The variational formulation of the multiscale system leads to a set of partial differential equations in a higher-dimensional space. Even constant shape functions of the fluctuations in direction of the macroscale match the results of Delta Dirac functions evaluated at every Gauss points, although the number of RVEs is dramatically reduced to one per element. Using higher-order shape functions produces the expected convergence rates, noting that the absolute error for linear shape functions for the fluctuations already surpasses the results of the traditional formulation.

With regard to (
19
), we can show that the discrete multiscale problem at hand can be considered as a finite-dimensional and non-convex minimization problem introducing a poly-convex strain energy function on the microscale. Using this as a discrete objective function, sophisticated methods like the recursive trust-region multigrid formulations as proposed in Gross & Krause
[
6
]
can be adapted as well, such that state of the art parallelization techniques can be applied instead of the usual „farming“ on clusters to solve for the physical equilibrium condition on the microscale within every macro Newton step.

Moreover, it is common to apply a staggered scheme for non-linear multiscale problems, searching first for a physical equilibrium condition on the microscale within each Newton-Raphson step on the macroscale. However, as long as the macroscale is not in equilibrium, the physical interpretation of the microscale is questionable. This leads to the curious situation, that a huge amount of additional computational power is required for the microscale solutions, without improving the solution on the macroscale. In the best case, we obtain quadratic convergence on the macroscale, in the worst case, the staggered scheme prevents the solution of the macroscale system e.g. for larger external load steps.

In contrast, the proposed null-space reduction scheme allows for a quadratic convergence simultaneously for both, the micro- and the macroscale without using any additional step in a staggered scheme. This yields a massive reduction of the computational effort for each iteration. Additionally, we could demonstrate that the monolithic solution is tremendously more robust. For the Cook’s membrane, the Newton scheme does not converge for the staggered scheme until we apply the external load in 22 load steps, whereas the monolithic scheme requires only two. Combined with the massive reduction of RVEs to be evaluated using constant shape functions in direction of the macroscale, the computational power required for the whole simulation is reduced to a fraction compared to the classical formulation.

Moreover, the applied methodology of null-space reduction schemes allows for a rather simple application on all kinds of multiscale problems. The development of a consistent linearization for generalized materials in the context of IGA
2
, e.g. for second or third-order materials is highly tedious, see the Appendix in our previous paper
[
18
]
. The same holds for multiphysical problems. For the proposed null-space reduction scheme, the construction of a consistent null-space matrix can now be done in a straight-forward manner once the global stiffness matrix of the monolithic macro-micro system has been generated.
