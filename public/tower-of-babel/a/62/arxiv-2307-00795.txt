Inference for Projection Parameters in Linear Regression: beyond d = o ⁡ ( n 1 / 2 ) d=o(n^{1/2})
Woonyoung Chang, Arun Kumar Kuchibhotla, Alessandro Rinaldo

Abstract
We consider the problem of inference for projection parameters in linear regression with increasing dimensions. This problem has been studied under a variety of assumptions in the literature. The classical asymptotic normality result for the least squares estimator of the projection parameter only holds when the dimension d d of the covariates is of a smaller order than n 1 / 2 n^{1/2} , where n n is the sample size. Traditional sandwich estimator-based Wald intervals are asymptotically valid in this regime. In this work, we propose a bias correction for the least squares estimator and prove the asymptotic normality of the resulting debiased estimator. Precisely, we provide an explicit finite sample Berry Esseen bound on the Normal approximation to the law of the linear contrasts of the proposed estimator normalized by the sandwich standard error estimate. Our bound, under only finite moment conditions on covariates and errors, tends to 0 as long as d = o ⁡ ( n 2 / 3 ) d=o(n^{2/3}) up to the polylogarithmic factors. Furthermore, we leverage recent methods of statistical inference that do not require an estimator of the variance to perform asymptotically valid statistical inference and that leads to a sharper miscoverage control compared to Wald’s. We provide a discussion of how our techniques can be generalized to increase the allowable range of d d even further.



1
Introduction

Linear regression is a fundamental statistical tool that has been widely used in various fields of research. The classical literature on linear regression studies the ordinary least square (OLS) estimation has focused primarily on the well-specified case, where the underlying truth postulates the linear relation between the response variable and covariates. As elucidated in works in an assumption-lean framework
(
Berk et al. 2019
;
Vansteelandt and Dukes 2022
)
, although the model assumption sometimes takes account of the pre-knowledge, the usage of model assumptions is dishonest when used because of the mathematical convenience. As real data often possess a highly nonlinear structure, relying on model assumptions and considering them as representing ground truth in inference may be problematic. Compared to the popularity of parametric regression in both practical studies in statistics and econometrics, the theoretically established properties of regression coefficients in an assumption-lean framework have just started to gain attraction. To this effort, some approaches start with a traditional estimator of a parameter indexing a parametric regression model and then characterize what estimand the estimator converges to, without assuming that the model is true
(
Kuchibhotla et al. 2020
;
Berk et al. 2019
)
. In particular, we focus on misspecified linear regression models comprised of a
d
-dimensional random vector of covariates
X
and a scalar random variable
Y
. If
\mathbb{P}_{X,Y}
admits the second moment, then the conditional expectation
\mathbb{E}[Y|X]
, which is not necessarily linear, is the best
L^{2}
approximation to
Y
among functions of
X
. It is well known that the best linear
L_{2}
approximation to
Y
is the linear function
\beta^{\top}X
is well-defined where the coefficients
\beta=\beta(\mathbb{P}_{X,Y})
is given by

\displaystyle\beta=\argmin_{\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathbb{E}[(Y-\theta^{\top}X)^{2}].

Provided that the population Gram matrix
\Sigma=\mathbb{E}_{X}[XX^{\top}]
is invertible, the solution is unique and given by the vector of
projection
parameters
(
Buja et al. 2019a
;
Buja et al. 2019b
)
,

\beta=\Sigma^{-1}\Gamma,

where
\Gamma=\mathbb{E}[XY]
. The projection parameter is traditionally estimated using the ordinary least square estimator (OLSE). Suppose that we observe a sample of
n
i.i.d observations
(X_{1},Y_{1}),\ldots,(X_{n},Y_{n})
from
\mathbb{P}_{X,Y}
. Then, the OLSE is defined as

\displaystyle\widehat{\beta}=\argmin_{\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(Y_{i}-\theta^{\top}X_{i})^{2}.

Provided that the sample Gram matrix
\widehat{\Sigma}:=n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}X_{i}^{\top}
is invertible with probability 1, then the OLSE is well-defined and can be expressed as

\displaystyle\widehat{\beta}=\widehat{\Sigma}^{-1}\widehat{\Gamma},

where
\widehat{\Gamma}=n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}Y_{i}
.

1.1
Related Works

Asymptotic Normality and Berry Esseen bound for the Least Square Estimator

In fixed-dimensional settings, the OLSE has been the subject of large sample theory and conventional Berry-Esseen bounds, demonstrated by
Van der Vaart 2000
and
Pfanzagl 1973
, respectively. With increasing dimensions,
Bickel and Freedman 1983
established the asymptotic normality of the least squares and the consistency of bootstrap, requiring
d=o(\sqrt{n})
under a well-specified linear model with fixed covariates and homogeneous errors. Additionally, a series of papers by
Portnoy 1984
;
Portnoy 1985
;
Portnoy 1986
;
Portnoy 1988
showcased various consistency and asymptotic normality properties for estimators, including M-estimators and maximum likelihood estimators. Translating these findings into our context mandates
d=o(\sqrt{n})
to ensure the
\sqrt{n}
-consistency of the OLSE within a well-specified model under a set of arguably strong assumptions on the data-generating process. In a more general setting, albeit postulating at least a partially linear model, the asymptotic normality of (non-)studentized OLSE has been investigated with additional regularity conditions on covariates and errors
(
Donald and Newey 1994
;
Cattaneo et al. 2015
;
Cattaneo et al. 2018a
;
Cattaneo et al. 2018b
)
. This reference list is far from complete. Some of these works pertain to the regime
d=o(n)
and occasionally allow the boundary case of
d=O(n)
.

An initial step toward the misspecified linear model with minimal assumptions on covariates and errors was made in
Mammen 1993
. They were concerned with linear contrasts of the regression coefficients and asserted the consistency of both the resampling bootstrap and the wild bootstrap in estimating the regression coefficient with increasing dimensions. They claimed that
d=o(n^{3/4})
is sufficient for consistent resampling bootstrap and that
d=o(\sqrt{n})
for the wild bootstrap. While their work provides useful intuition regarding the asymptotic behavior of the OLSE under model misspecification, their findings rely on heuristics and do not explicitly present the convergence rate.
Rinaldo et al. 2019
formulated the Berry Esseen bound for general nonlinear statistics under the misspecified setting. Their analysis, specifically applied to projection parameters, established a uniform Berry Esseen bound for entry-wise asymptotic normality of the Ordinary Least Square Estimator (OLSE), converging to
0
under
d=o(n^{1/5})
, disregarding polylogarithmic factors (refer to Theorem 2 therein). More recently,
Kuchibhotla et al. 2020
derived a novel finite sample bound for the studentized OLSE under finite moment assumptions on covariates and errors. They obtained a uniform Berry Esseen bound for entry-wise asymptotic normality of the OLSE, with their bound scaling as
(d/n)^{1/3}+d/\sqrt{n}
, disregarding polylogarithmic factors and under sufficient moment conditions on covariates and errors; See Theorem 10 of
Kuchibhotla et al. 2020
. Consequently, they required
d=o(\sqrt{n})
for the bound to vanish, facilitating the construction of simultaneous confidence intervals for the projection parameter coefficients under such dimension range. Notably, these intervals can exhibit a width of the order
1/\sqrt{n}
. It should be emphasized that the parameters of interest in this article are linear contrasts of the projection parameters which are different from those addressed in
Rinaldo et al. 2019
and
Kuchibhotla et al. 2020
.

De-biasing of the Least Square Estimator

Under misspecified linear models, extensive attention has focused on scenarios where
d=o(\sqrt{n})
, with limited exploration beyond this scaling despite the OLSE remaining well-defined for
d\leq n
. In cases where
d\gg\sqrt{n}
, the OLSE demonstrates a bias of order
d/\sqrt{n}

(
Mammen 1993
;
Cattaneo et al. 2019
)
, impeding valid inference on regression parameters without de-biasing. To tackle this,
Cattaneo et al. 2019
proposed the bias-corrected
M
-estimator via the Jackknife method, ensuring consistency and asymptotic normality of the two-step point estimate under
d=O(\sqrt{n})
, presenting bootstrap-based inferential methods as well.

The term “de-biasing” typically refers to a correction applied to the original regularized estimator, often necessary to resist the curse of dimensionality or to enhance prior knowledge about the geometric/intrinsic structure of data. Common examples in linear regression include the LASSO, ridge, and SLOP estimators. It is worth emphasizing that “de-biasing” here aims to address the “bias” mainly induced by model misspecification at the population level rather than a specific regularization technique.

Variance Estimation

In the context of linear regression, the inflating variance of regression coefficient estimates along with increasing dimension has prompted efforts to propose robust covariance estimates capable of accommodating dimensionality and potential heteroscedasticity. The degree-of-freedom-corrected covariance estimator, introduced by
MacKinnon and White 1985
, has served as a foundation for subsequent modifications within well-specified linear models, known as HC-class variance estimators. For comprehensive reviews of these estimators, we refer our readers to
Long and Ervin 2000
and
MacKinnon 2012
. Recent additions to the HC class include the HCK estimator proposed by
Cattaneo et al. 2018b
and the HCA estimator proposed by
Jochmans 2022
. However, in both cases, consistency of variance estimate requires a dimensionality constraint of
d=O(n^{1/2-\epsilon})
in misspecified linear models, albeit under different settings. Under a very similar setting to ours,
Kuchibhotla et al. 2020
noted that a dimensionality requirement of
d=o(n^{1/2})
appears to be unavoidable for the consistency of the sandwich estimator for the OLSE variance (see Lemma 8 of
Kuchibhotla et al. 2020
).

1.2
Contributions

In this paper, we employ an assumption-lean framework to offer a finite-sample distribution approximation for the projection parameter, imposing minimal assumptions. Specifically, our focus centers on estimating and inferring a scalar parameter
\theta:=c^{\top}\beta
in a predetermined direction
c\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. The main contributions of this study are summarized as follows.

•

We propose a bias-corrected least square estimator for the projection parameter. Theorem
3
presents the Berry Esseen bound for the approximation to the adjusted Normal distribution of the unnormalized linear contrast of the proposed estimator, and the bound is sharp and roughly scales like

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}+\frac{d^{3/2}}{n},

(1)

provided that
q_{x}\geq 12
and
q\geq 4
, where
q_{x}
and
q
represent the finite moments of the covariate
X
and the error
Y-X^{\top}\beta
, respectively; See also Corollary
1
. Consequently, achieving the vanishing bound necessitates the scaling
d=o(n^{2/3})
, strictly embracing the traditional requirement of
d=o(n^{1/2})
. Our result, to the best of our knowledge, presents the sharpest bound under the (possibly) misspecified linear model while relying on the weakest assumptions regarding the true data distribution.

•

While the Berry Esseen bound presented in Theorem
3
involves the unknown parameter for the asymptotic variance of the proposed estimator, Section
4
outlines at least two inference methods for constructing (non-)asymptotically valid confidence intervals. These methods do not necessitate estimating the asymptotic variance, thereby enabling us to utilize the sharp Berry Esseen bound from Theorem
3
. Our approach involves inferential techniques based on resampling and sample splitting, including HulC
(
Kuchibhotla et al. 2021
)
,
t-
statistic based inference
(
Ibragimov and Müller 2010
;
Lam 2022
)
, and wild bootstrap
(
Wu 1986
;
Mammen 1993
)
. We also illustrate these proposed methods through empirical examples in Section
5
.

•

Our main result, Theorem
5
, establishes a finite-sample Berry-Esseen bound for a studentized linear contrast of the bias-corrected estimator under the assumptions of finite moments for both the covariates and the errors. Our bound, disregarding the polylogarithmic factors, roughly scales as follows:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n^{1/3}}+\sqrt{\frac{d}{n}}+\frac{d^{3/2}}{n},

(2)

provided that
q_{x}\geq 12
and
q\geq 4
; See also Corollary
2
. Our bound immediately leads to the validity of the Wald confidence interval for a linear contrast of the projection parameters as outlined in Corollary
3
.

The slower convergence rate outlined in (
2
) compared to that in (
1
) is solely due to the additional convergence rate introduced by the sandwich variance estimator. However, it is noteworthy that the consistency of the sandwich variance estimator does not demand any additional dimension scaling beyond
d=o(n^{2/3})
. Moreover, as per Theorem
4
, the sandwich variance estimator attains consistency if
d=o(\min\{n^{1-1/q_{x}-1/q},n^{1-2/q_{x}-2/(3q)}\})
. To the best of our knowledge, our result on the consistency of the sandwich variance estimator allows the broadest range of dimension scaling in assumption lean linear regression literature.

•

One of the key quantities in our analysis is
\mathcal{D}_{\Sigma}=\|\Sigma^{-1/2}\widehat{\Sigma}\Sigma^{-1/2}-I_{d}\|_{\rm op}
, representing the difference between the population and the sample gram matrix in the operator norm. We introduce a new concentration inequality for
\mathcal{D}_{\Sigma}
under finite moment assumptions on covariates (
q_{x}\geq 4
), detailed in Proposition
22
. This result is inspired by recent findings on the universal spectral properties of random matrices, relating them to Gaussian random matrices with identical mean and covariance in their entries
(
Brailovskaya and van Handel 2023
)
. Our bound for
\mathcal{D}_{\Sigma}
matches the sharpness (up to polylogarithmic factors) of the one provided in
Tikhomirov 2018
, regarded as the sharpest to date. Notably, our bound avoids the necessity for a ‘large’
d
to ensure a ‘high’ probability concentration of the sample Gram matrix. Section
B.3
outlines the historical context of
\mathcal{D}_{\Sigma}
and offers a comparison of concentration inequalities in this regard.

Problem setup and Assumptions

2.1
Projection parameters

Let
(X_{1},Y_{1}),\ldots,(X_{n},Y_{n})\sim(X,Y)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}
be an independent sample of
n
observations. The
projection parameter

\beta
is defined as

\beta=\beta_{n}:=\argmin_{\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}[(Y_{i}-X_{i}^{\top}\theta)^{2}].

(3)

The minimizer
\beta
in (
3
) solves the linear equation that

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}[X_{i}(Y_{i}-X_{i}^{\top}\beta)]=0.

(4)

If the population gram matrix
\Sigma=\Sigma_{n}:=n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}[X_{i}X_{i}^{\top}]
is invertible, the projection parameter is well-defined and can be uniquely expressed as
\beta=\Sigma^{-1}\Gamma
where
\Gamma=\Gamma_{n}:=n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}[X_{i}Y_{i}]
. In the case where the observations follow the linear model
Y=X^{\top}\beta^{*}+\epsilon
with
\mathbb{E}[\epsilon|X]=0
, the projection parameter corresponds to the model parameter, i.e.,
\beta=\beta^{*}
. On the contrary, if the underlying truth exhibits a possibly non-linear structure, then the projection parameter gives the best linear approximation
X^{\top}\beta
to
Y
with respect to the joint distribution of
(X,Y)
. For a detailed discussion on the projection parameter and its interpretation, see
Buja et al. 2019a
;
Buja et al. 2019b
.

The projection parameter is estimated using the ordinary least square estimator (OLSE) which follows the traditional definition that

\widehat{\beta}=\widehat{\beta}_{n}:=\argmin_{\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(Y_{i}-X_{i}^{\top}\theta)^{2}.

If the sample population matrix
\widehat{\Sigma}=\widehat{\Sigma}_{n}:=n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}X_{i}^{\top}
is invertible with probability 1, then the OLSE is well defined and can be written as
\widehat{\beta}=\widehat{\Sigma}^{-1}\widehat{\Gamma}
where
\widehat{\Gamma}=\widehat{\Gamma}_{n}:=n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}Y_{i}
.

2.2
Assumptions

Our main results are built upon the following assumptions, primarily emphasizing minimal assumptions on the data generating process. These include moment conditions on both the response and covariates, thereby encompassing a wide range of distributions, such as heavy-tailed distributions and discrete distributions.

Assumption 1
.

There exists some
q\geq 2
and a constant
K_{y}>0
satisfying that

\left(\mathbb{E}[|Y_{i}-X_{i}^{\top}\beta|^{q}]\right)^{1/q}\leq K_{y},

for
i=1,\ldots,n
.

Assumption
1
imposes a moment condition solely on the error, allowing for heteroscedastic errors that may arbitrarily depend on the covariates and even accommodate heavy-tailed errors. This assumption is prevalent in recent literature on heteroskedastic linear regression
(
Mourtada et al. 2022
;
Kuchibhotla and Patra 2022
;
Han and Wellner 2019
;
Mendelson 2016
)
. Additionally, it is often assumed that the conditional variance of the error is uniformly bounded almost everywhere, described by

0<\inf_{x}v^{2}(x)\leq\sup_{x}v^{2}(x)<\infty,

(5)

where
v^{2}(x)=\mathbb{E}[(Y-X^{\top}\beta)^{2}|X=x]
. Here,
\inf_{x}
and
\sup_{x}
are essential infimum and essential supremum, respectively, with respect to the marginal distribution of
X
. Given (
5
), our results in the following section remain valid even under the relaxed moment assumptions on the covariates.

Assumption 2.a
.

There exists some
q_{x}\geq 2
and a constant
K_{x}\geq 1
satisfying that

\left(\mathbb{E}[|u^{\top}\Sigma^{-1/2}X_{i}|^{q_{x}}]\right)^{1/q_{x}}\leq K_{x},

for
i=1,\ldots,n
and
u\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
.

Assumption 2.b
.

There exists a constant
K_{x}>0
such that

\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(\frac{|u^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}X_{i}|^{2}}{K_{x}^{2}}\right)\right]\leq 2,

for all
i=1,\ldots,n
and
u\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
.

Assumption 2.c
.

There exists some
q_{x}\geq 2
and a constant
K_{x}\geq 1
satisfying that

\left(\mathbb{E}[|u^{\top}\Sigma^{-1/2}X_{i}|^{q_{x}}]\right)^{1/q_{x}}\leq K_{x},

for
i=1,\ldots,n
and
u\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
. Furthermore,
\Sigma^{-1/2}X_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
i=1,\ldots,n
, have
d
independent entries.

Assumption
2.a
implies
L^{q_{x}}-L^{2}
equivalence for one-dimensional marginals of covariates. This is a significant weakening of sub-Gaussianity in Assumption
2.b
. It is common to impose
q_{x}\geq 4
when studying the linear regression with increasing dimensions
(
Oliveira 2016
;
Catoni 2016
;
Mourtada et al. 2022
;
Mourtada 2022
;
Vaškevičius and
Zhivotovskiy 2023
)
even though we sometimes require a higher moment condition to analyze a higher order expansion of the least square estimate.

Furthermore, Assumption
2.a
implies the bounded-ness of
q_{x}
:th moment of the
\Sigma^{-1}
-norm of the random vector
X_{i}
. To illustrate, note that
\|X_{i}\|_{\Sigma^{-1}}^{2}=\sum_{j=1}^{d}(\mathbf{e}_{j}^{\top}\Sigma^{-1/2}X_{i})^{2}
for all
i=1,\ldots,n
. By applying Jensen’s inequality, we have:

\left(\frac{\|X_{i}\|_{\Sigma^{-1}}^{2}}{d}\right)^{q_{x}/2}\leq\frac{1}{d}\sum_{j=1}^{d}\left(\mathbf{e}_{j}^{\top}\Sigma^{-1/2}X_{i}\right)^{q_{x}},

which holds deterministically as long as
q_{x}\geq 2
. Consequently, it leads to

\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\frac{\|X_{i}\|_{\Sigma^{-1}}}{\sqrt{d}}\right)^{q_{x}}\right]\leq\frac{1}{d}\sum_{j=1}^{d}\mathbb{E}\left[(\mathbf{e}_{j}^{\top}\Sigma^{-1/2}X_{i})^{q_{x}}\right]\leq\max_{1\leq j\leq d}\mathbb{E}\left[(\mathbf{e}_{j}^{\top}\Sigma^{-1/2}X_{i})^{q_{x}}\right]\leq K_{x}^{q_{x}},

where the last inequality follows from Assumption
2.a
. This also emphasizes that the appropriate scaling of
\|X_{i}\|_{\Sigma^{-1}}
is proportional to
\sqrt{d}
as the dimension
d
grows.

Assumption
2.c
assumes that
\Sigma^{-1/2}
-normalized covariates have independent entries, in addition to the moment conditions. Under Assumption
2.c
, we can achieve a more precise control over the
L^{2}
-norm of covariates
(
Rio 2017
)
and the sample Gram matrix
(
Srivastava and Vershynin 2013
;
Mendelson and Paouris 2014
)
.

Assumption 3
.

Let

V:={\rm Var}\left[\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}(Y_{i}-X_{i}^{\top}\beta)\right]={\rm Var}\left[X_{1}(Y_{1}-X_{1}^{\top}\beta)\right].

There exist constants
0<\underline{\lambda}\leq\overline{\lambda}<\infty
satisfying that

\underline{\lambda}\leq\lambda_{\rm min}(\Sigma^{-1/2}V\Sigma^{-1/2})\leq\lambda_{\rm max}(\Sigma^{-1/2}V\Sigma^{-1/2})\leq\overline{\lambda}.

Assumption
3
ensures that both
V
and
\Sigma
scale in a similar order, serving as the only technical assumption in our paper. This condition appears inevitable to prevent the asymptotic variance of the least square estimator from inflating or vanishing. Specifically, noting that
V=\mathbb{E}[XX^{\top}(Y-X^{\top}\beta)^{2}]=\mathbb{E}[XX^{\top}\mathbb{E}[(Y-X^{\top}\beta)^{2}|X]]
the condition (
5
) is sufficient for Assumption
3
to be held.

3
Methods and Results

3.1
Approximation of the Sample Gram Matrix and Bias Characterization

In this section, we present an approximation of the distribution of
\sqrt{n}c^{\top}(\widehat{\beta}-\beta)
for any
c\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\{0_{d}\}
. To be presented results are mostly invariant to the scaling of
c\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. However, we sometimes consider those with
\|c\|_{\Sigma^{-1}}=1
, which gives a proper normalization,
\sqrt{n}c^{\top}(\widehat{\beta}-\beta)=\sqrt{n}\tilde{c}^{\top}\Sigma^{1/2}(\widehat{\beta}-\beta)
for some
\tilde{c}\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
.

Our analysis starts with the least square estimate
\widehat{\beta}
, particularly by approximating the sample Gram matrix
\widehat{\Sigma}
using Taylor series expansion. We note

\displaystyle\widehat{\Sigma}^{-1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Sigma^{-1}+\widehat{\Sigma}^{-1}(\Sigma-\widehat{\Sigma})\Sigma^{-1}

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle\Sigma^{-1}+\Sigma^{-1}(\Sigma-\widehat{\Sigma})\Sigma^{-1}.

(See Lemma 33 of
Kuchibhotla et al. 2020
.)
Consequently, we get the following approximation of
\widehat{\beta}-\beta
:

\displaystyle\widehat{\beta}-\beta

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\widehat{\Sigma}^{-1}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}(Y_{i}-X_{i}^{\top}\beta)

(6)

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle\Sigma^{-1}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}(Y_{i}-X_{i}^{\top}\beta)

\displaystyle+\,\Sigma^{-1}(\Sigma-\widehat{\Sigma})\Sigma^{-1}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}(Y_{i}-X_{i}^{\top}\beta).

(7)

The term (
6
) is the first-order approximation and is the average of influence function—with respect to the square loss—evaluated at independent data points. Using the conventional large sample approximation for the case when
d=o(\sqrt{n})
, the variability of
\widehat{\beta}
can be well-represented only through the term (
6
), and the second order term (
7
) or higher order terms are negligible. In particular, (
6
) is responsible for the asymptotic normality of
\sqrt{n}(\widehat{\beta}-\beta)
when
d=o(\sqrt{n})
. Here, however, the term (
7
) may create a non-vanishing bias when
d\gg\sqrt{n}
. To see this, we note that

\displaystyle\Sigma^{-1}(\Sigma-\widehat{\Sigma})\Sigma^{-1}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}(Y_{i}-X_{i}^{\top}\beta)

\displaystyle=

Under the control of

\displaystyle{\rm Var}\left[c^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}X(Y-X^{\top}\beta)\right]=c^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}V\Sigma^{-1}c,

\displaystyle\kappa_{c}

Remark 1
.

The conventional Berry Esseen type inequality implies the proximity between the distribution of the law of the estimator and the Normal distribution. In contrast, we show a distribution approximation to the “adjusted” Normal distribution, allowing a more precise representation of the approximation. The degree of adjustment is determined by the parameter
\kappa_{c}
, which turns out to scale as
O(\sqrt{d/n})
(see Lemma
16
). Hence, we can enhance the Berry-Esseen bound for the Normal approximation by incorporating an additional term of
O(\sqrt{d/n})
to the existing bounds in Theorem
1
, but yielding a slower convergence rate. More importantly, under the well-specified linear model, i.e.,
\mathbb{E}[Y|X]=X^{\top}\beta
, then it follows that
\kappa_{c}=0
from its definition in (
15
). Consequently, in this case, the presented Berry-Esseen bound in Theorem
1
can be employed for the Normal approximation as well.

Remark 2
.

The last part of Theorem
1
says that the OLSE does not necessarily require de-biasing under Assumption
2.c
with a sufficiently large finite moment of covariates and errors. To provide an intuition for this, let us consider the bias term
c^{\top}\mathcal{B}
in (
12
), which scales with its expected value
\mathbb{E}[c^{\top}\mathcal{B}]
. By examining the definition of the projection parameter, we can express
\mathbb{E}[c^{\top}\mathcal{B}]
as the covariance between two random variables:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[c^{\top}\mathcal{B}]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{E}[c^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}X(Y-X^{\top}\beta)\|X\|_{\Sigma^{-1}}^{2}]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{n}{\rm Cov}\left(c^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}X(Y-X^{\top}\beta),\|X\|_{\Sigma^{-1}}^{2}\right).

An application of Cauchy Schwarz’s inequality yields that

\left|\mathbb{E}[c^{\top}\mathcal{B}]\right|\leq\frac{1}{n}\left({\rm Var}\left[c^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}X(Y-X^{\top}\beta)\right]\right)^{1/2}\left({\rm Var}\left[\|X\|_{\Sigma^{-1}}^{2}\right]\right)^{1/2}.

(16)

The leading variance term on the right-hand side of (
16
), under the finite moment assumptions of covariates and errors, is
O(1)
. Meanwhile, the quantity
{\rm Var}[\|X\|_{\Sigma^{-1}}^{2}]
generally scales as
\mathbb{E}[\|X\|_{\Sigma^{-1}}^{4}]=O(d^{2})
, resulting in
|\mathbb{E}[c^{\top}\mathcal{B}]|=O(d/n)
. However, when an additional assumption of independent entries of covariates, i.e., Assumption
2.c
, is satisfied,
\|X\|_{\Sigma^{-1}}^{2}
roughly follows
\chi^{2}_{d}
distribution, thereby leading to
{\rm Var}[\|X\|_{\Sigma^{-1}}^{2}]=O(d)
. This significantly reduces the magnitude of the right-hand side of (
16
) to
O(\sqrt{d}/n)
. Consequently, the expected bias (when scaled by
\sqrt{n}
) is negligible if
d=o(n)
.

Remark 3
.

Regarding the results under Assumptions
2.a
and
2.c
, we conjecture that the dependence on logarithmic factors of our bound is suboptimal. The presence of seemingly spurious logarithmic factors mainly stems from the concentration inequality we employ for the sample Gram matrix, described in Proposition
22
in Section
B.3
. Although Theorem 1.1 of
Tikhomirov 2018
partially addresses our query when
q_{x}>4
, its application to our context (see Proposition
23
) requires further supposition:
d\to\infty
as
n\to\infty
.

Theorem
1
gives the Berry Essen bound for the linear contrast of the
properly-debiased
projection parameters under three different distributional assumptions on covariates. The first part of Theorem
1
regards the most general case which relies only on finite-moment assumptions. Ignoring the polylogarithmic factors, the Berry-Esseen bound tends to zero as long as

d=o\left(n^{\min\{2/3,4/5-8/(5q_{x})\}}\right),\quad n\to\infty.

If
q_{x}\geq 12
, this reduces to
d=o(n^{2/3})
and meets the dimension requirement for the vanishing Berry Esseen bound under Assumption
2.b
of sub-Gaussianity. Moreover, under Assumption
2.c

d=o(n^{2/3})
for all
q_{x}>4

3.2
Bias Estimation

The result given in Theorem
1
is mostly of theoretical importance as the inequality yet involves unknown parameters through bias
\mathcal{B}
and asymptotic variance
\sigma_{c}^{2}
. Therefore, to do inference on
\beta

\widehat{\mathcal{B}}
for
\mathcal{B}
defined as

\widehat{\mathcal{B}}=-\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\widehat{\Sigma}^{-1}X_{i}(Y_{i}-X_{i}^{\top}\widehat{\beta})\|X_{i}\|_{\widehat{\Sigma}^{-1}}^{2}.

(17)

The next result provides the consistency rate for the bias estimate in high dimensions and under mild moment conditions. As elucidated in Remark
2
, the OLSE does not necessitate a debiasing procedure under Assumption
2.c
with a sufficient number of moments of covariates and errors. Therefore, the following results will consider the cases under Assumption
2.a
and

Theorem 2
.

Suppose that Assumption
1
holds for
q>3
and that Assumption
3
holds. Fix

with

Suppose further that Assumption

Under the moment condition of Assumption
2.a

3.4
Consistency of the Sandwich Variance Estimator

Berry Esseen bound for the studentized bias-corrected LSE remains pertinent, notably in the construction of Wald-type confidence intervals. As previously discussed, accomplishing this entails devising an estimator for the asymptotic variance that ideally maintains consistency across a wide range of dimensions argued in Theorem
3
. For a specified query vector
c\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\{0_{d}\}
, the asymptotic variance of
\sqrt{n}c^{\top}(\widehat{\beta}_{\rm bc}-\beta)
can be approximated through
\sigma_{c}^{2}=c^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}V\Sigma^{-1}c
, as asserted in Theorem
3
. Notably, the
d\times d
matrix
\Sigma^{-1}V\Sigma^{-1}
is commonly referred to as the ‘sandwich variance’ for OLS in a misspecified linear regression setting
(
White 1980
;
Buja et al. 2019a
)
. A natural estimator for the sandwich variance is its plug-in counterpart
\widehat{\Sigma}^{-1}\widehat{V}\widehat{\Sigma}^{-1}
, termed the ‘sandwich variance estimator’, where
\widehat{\Sigma}
is the sample Gram matrix and
\widehat{V}
is expressed as:

\widehat{V}:=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}X_{i}^{\top}(Y_{i}-X_{i}^{\top}\widehat{\beta})^{2}.

To this end, we define
\widehat{\sigma}_{c}^{2}:=c^{\top}\widehat{\Sigma}^{-1}\widehat{V}\widehat{\Sigma}^{-1}c
for
c\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\{0_{d}\}
.

The consistency of the sandwich variance estimator has been studied under various model- and distributional- assumptions and has often been a stand-alone line of research. A recent significant finding in a closely related context is detailed in Lemma 7 of
Kuchibhotla et al. 2020
. In particular, they prove that
\|\widehat{\Sigma}^{-1}\widehat{V}\widehat{\Sigma}^{-1}-\Sigma^{-1}V\Sigma^{-1}\|_{\rm op}=o_{p}(1)
under conditions where
d=o(\min\{n^{1/2},n^{2(1-2/q_{x})/3}\})
, up to polylogarithmic factors, provided that
(1/q_{x}+1/q)^{-1}\geq 4
. Hence, directly applying their results to our scenario may be challenging, as we are considering situations beyond
d=o(\sqrt{n})
. Nevertheless, we have found that along a specific query vector
c\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the estimator
\widehat{\sigma}_{c}^{2}
maintains consistency across a broader range of dimensions, as elucidated in the following theorem.

Theorem 4
.

Suppose that Assumption
1
,
2.a
, and
3
holds. Fix
c\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\{0_{d}\}
, and assume
d+4\log(2n)\leq n/(18K_{x})^{2}
. If
(1/q_{x}+1/q)^{-1}\geq 4
and
(3/q_{x}+1/q)^{-1}\geq 2
, then there exists a constant
C=C(q,q_{x},K_{x},K_{y},\overline{\lambda},\underline{\lambda})
such that

\mathbb{P}\left(\left|\frac{\widehat{\sigma}_{c}^{2}}{\sigma_{c}^{2}}-1\right|\geq\eta_{n,d}\right)\leq\eta_{n,d},

where

\displaystyle\eta_{n,d}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle C\left(\frac{1}{n^{1/3}}+\sqrt{\frac{d+\log(2n)}{n}}+\frac{d\log(2n)}{n^{1-1/q_{x}}}\right)

\displaystyle+C\left[\left(\frac{d\log^{3/2}(2n)}{n^{1-2/q_{x}}}\right)^{\frac{1}{1+2/q_{x}}}+\left(\frac{d\log^{5/4}(2n)}{n^{1-1/q_{x}-1/q}}\right)^{\frac{2}{1+2/q_{x}+2/q}}+\left(\frac{d\log^{3/2}(2n)}{n^{1-2/q_{x}-2/(3q)}}\right)^{\frac{3}{1+3/q_{x}+1/q}}\right]

\displaystyle+C\left[\left(\frac{d\log^{5/4}(2n)}{n^{1-2/q_{x}}}\right)^{\frac{2}{1+4/q_{x}}}+\left(\frac{d\log^{3/4}(2n)}{n^{1-2/q_{x}}}\right)^{\frac{2+8/q_{x}+4/q}{1+6/q_{x}+2/q}}+\left(\frac{d\log^{1/2}(2n)}{n^{1-2/q_{x}-2/(3q)}}\right)^{\frac{3+12/q_{x}+6/q}{1+6/q_{x}+2/q}}\right].

Remark 4
.

The proof of Theorem
4
is extensive due to the complex nature of the sandwich variance estimator, encompassed in Section
A.7
. Theorem
4
primarily focuses on the concentration inequality for
|\widehat{\sigma}_{c}^{2}/\sigma_{c}^{2}-1|
, with the pivotal deterministic inequality established in Lemma
7
. This lemma argues that the difference between the plug-in sandwich variance estimate and its population counterparts can be deterministically bounded using several factors, including
\|\Sigma^{-1/2}\widehat{\Sigma}\Sigma^{-1/2}-I_{d}\|_{\rm op}
,
\|\widehat{\beta}-\beta\|_{\Sigma}
, and
\max_{1\leq i\leq n}|X_{i}^{\top}(\widehat{\beta}-\beta)|
, among others mentioned. Consequently, the proof relies on the concentration inequality of these quantities, which in themselves hold potential as areas of independent interest. The rate presented in Theorem
4
is a result of leveraging these concentration inequalities, aiming to achieve the widest possible scaling of
d
in terms of
n
. However, alternative choices remain viable.

Theorem
4
shows that for a given
c\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the variance estimate
\widehat{\sigma}_{c}^{2}
is consistent for the asymptotic variance
\sigma_{c}^{2}
as long as

d=o\left(n^{\min\{1-2/q_{x},1-1/q_{x}-1/q,1-2/q_{x}-2/(3q)\}}\right),

(20)

ignoring the polylogarithmic factors. Under the assumptions that
(1/q_{x}+1/q)^{-1}\geq 4
and
(3/q_{x}+1/q)^{-1}\geq 2
, it reduces to
d=o(n^{2/3})
. This strictly embraces the one obtained in Theorem
3
, but it should be emphasized that Theorem
3
relies on the moment conditions
q_{x}\geq 8
and
(1/q_{x}+1/q)^{-1}\geq 3
.

4
Confidence Region for the Projection Parameter

In the previous section, we have shown that the bias-corrected least square estimator is asymptotically Normal distributed. In this section, we present three methods that do not require a consistent estimate of the asymptotic variance but still enable us to get valid confidence intervals for the linear contrasts of the projection parameters.

4.1
HulC

The HulC
(
Kuchibhotla et al. 2021
)
is a statistical method that computes confidence regions for parameters by constructing convex hulls around a set of estimates.
Suppose that we split a sample of
n
i.i.d observations
(X_{1},Y_{1}),\ldots,(X_{n},Y_{n})
into
B
batches where each batch contains at least
\lfloor n/B\rfloor
observations
*
*

*

Kuchibhotla et al. 2021
noted that the batches need
not be equal-sized, but having approximately equal sizes yields good width properties.
. Denote the bias-corrected least square estimators obtained from
b
:th batch as
\widehat{\beta}_{\rm bc}^{(b)}
for
b=1,\ldots,B
. For any
c\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, define the maximum median bias
(
Kuchibhotla et al. 2021
)
for
\beta
as

\Delta_{c}:=\max_{1\leq b\leq B}\left(\frac{1}{2}-\min\left\{\mathbb{P}(c^{\top}(\widehat{\beta}_{\rm bc}^{(b)}-\beta)>0),\mathbb{P}(c^{\top}(\widehat{\beta}_{\rm bc}^{(b)}-\beta)<0)\right\}\right).

A key idea of the HulC is that the event

\min_{1\leq b\leq B}c^{\top}\widehat{\beta}_{\rm bc}^{(b)}\leq c^{\top}\beta\leq\max_{1\leq b\leq B}c^{\top}\widehat{\beta}_{\rm bc}^{(b)}

occurs unless either of the following happens: (1)
c^{\top}(\widehat{\beta}_{\rm bc}^{(b)}-\beta)>0
for all
1\leq b\leq B
or (2)
c^{\top}(\widehat{\beta}_{\rm bc}^{(b)}-\beta)<0
for all
1\leq b\leq B
. Consequently, we get

\mathbb{P}\left(\min_{1\leq b\leq B}c^{\top}\widehat{\beta}_{\rm bc}^{(b)}\leq c^{\top}\beta\leq\max_{1\leq b\leq B}c^{\top}\widehat{\beta}_{\rm bc}^{(b)}\right)\geq 1-\left(\frac{1}{2}-\Delta_{c}\right)^{B}-\left(\frac{1}{2}+\Delta_{c}\right)^{B}.

Since the bias-corrected OLS estimator is asymptotically Normal as shown in Corollory
1
,
\Delta_{c}
converges to the asymptotic median, which is
0
. The detailed procedure for constructing confidence interval at level
\alpha
is described in Algorithm
1
.

Theorem 6
.

Suppose that the assumptions made in Theorem

(i) hold, and denote the Berry Esseen bound given in Theorem

\epsilon_{n,d}

. Then, the false coverage rate of HulC CI can be controlled as

\sup_{c\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\{0_{d}\}}\left\{\mathbb{P}\left(c^{\top}\beta\notin{\rm CI}^{\rm HulC}_{c,\alpha}\right)-\alpha\right\}\leq\alpha\left(B^{2}\epsilon_{\frac{n}{B},d}^{2}+C\frac{B^{3}d}{n}\right),

for some constant
C=C(K_{x},K_{y},\underline{\lambda})
. If

and

Remark 6

(Comparison with Wald Confidence Interval)
.

The Wald CI and HulC CI are both (asymptotically) valid as demonstrated in Corollary

and Theorem

(

, slowly diverging as

4.3
Bootstrap Inference

This section presents a method for carrying out post-bias-correction inference in our scenario, based on a bootstrap approach. Specifically, we utilize the wild bootstrap technique
(
Wu 1986
)
to derive a computationally efficient approximation of the distribution of the bias-corrected OLS estimator. The idea is, as the residual bootstrap, to leave the covariates at their sample value, but to resample the response variable based on the residual values.
First, we compute from the original dataset that the OLS estimator
\widehat{\beta}
and the residuals
\widehat{\epsilon}_{i}:=Y_{i}-X_{i}^{\top}\widehat{\beta}
for
i=1,\ldots,n
. For
i=1,\ldots,n
, let
\xi_{i}^{*}
be i.i.d. bootstrap weights such that
\mathbb{E}\xi_{i}^{*}=0
,
\mathbb{E}{\xi_{i}^{*}}^{2}=1
, and
\mathbb{E}{\xi_{i}^{*}}^{3}=1
. Then, we generate a bootstrap sample of
n
observations
(X_{1},Y_{1}^{*}),\ldots,(X_{n},Y_{n}^{*})
by letting

Y_{i}^{*}=X_{i}^{\top}\widehat{\beta}+\widehat{\epsilon}_{i}\xi_{i}^{*},

for
i=1,\ldots,n
. Although Bootstrap weights are often drawn from the standard Normal distribution, we made use of the asymmetric weights in Section
5
, which was proposed in
Mammen 1993
;
\mathbb{P}(\xi^{*}=-(\sqrt{5}-1)/2)=(\sqrt{5}+1)/(2\sqrt{5})
and
\mathbb{P}(\xi^{*}=(\sqrt{5}+1)/2)=(\sqrt{5}-1)/(2\sqrt{5})
. Let estimator
\widehat{\beta}^{*}_{\rm bc}
be the bias-corrected least square estimator based on bootstrap observations,

\displaystyle\widehat{\beta}^{*}_{\rm bc}=\widehat{\beta}^{*}-\widehat{\mathcal{B}}^{*},

where
\widehat{\beta}^{*}
and
\widehat{\mathcal{B}}^{*}
are bootstrap counterparts of the OLS estimator and estimated bias, respectively;

\displaystyle\widehat{\beta}^{*}=\widehat{\Sigma}^{-1}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}Y_{i}^{*}\quad\mbox{and}\quad\widehat{\mathcal{B}}^{*}=-\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\widehat{\Sigma}^{-1}X_{i}(Y_{i}^{*}-X_{i}^{\top}\widehat{\beta}^{*})\|X_{i}\|_{\widehat{\Sigma}^{-1}}^{2}.

To construct the confidence interval, let
T_{b}^{*}=\widehat{\beta}_{\rm bc}^{*,(b)}-\widehat{\beta}
where
\widehat{\beta}_{\rm bc}^{*,(b)}
is the bootstrap bias-corrected estimator in
b
:th simulation. The wild bootstrap level
\alpha
bias-corrected confidence interval for
c^{\top}\beta
is given by

\displaystyle{\rm CI}_{\alpha}^{\rm WBS}=\left[\widehat{\beta}_{\rm bc}-\widehat{q}^{*}_{1-\alpha/2},\widehat{\beta}_{\rm bc}-\widehat{q}^{*}_{\alpha/2}\right],

where
\widehat{q}^{*}_{\alpha}=\inf\{t\in\mathbb{R}:\widehat{F}^{*}(t)\geq\alpha\}
is the empirical upper
\alpha
:th quantile of
\{T_{b}^{*}:1\leq b\leq B\}
with
\widehat{F}^{*}(t)=\frac{1}{B}\sum_{b=1}^{B}\mathbbm{1}(T_{b}^{*}\leq t)
.

Remark 7
.

The validity of the above wild bootstrap inference may require additional dimension requirements. Theorem
1
of
Mammen 1993
shows that wild bootstrap provides a consistent estimation of the unconditional distribution of
\sqrt{n}(\widehat{\beta}-\beta)
in a well-specified linear regression model, without any bias correction, under the condition that
d=o(\sqrt{n})
. More recently, Theorem
1
1 of
Kuchibhotla et al. 2020
establishes a finite sample approximation of the conditional bootstrap distribution of the law of OLSE in a similar setting to ours. In particular, the proof utilizes the Gaussian comparison bound
(
Lopes 2020
)
to compare the conditional distribution of the bootstrap estimate with the Normal distribution. However, the estimation of asymptotic variance appears to be inevitable in the process, and this adds an additional dimension requirement of
d=o(\sqrt{n})
, particularly when
q_{x}\geq 8
and
s\geq 4
(see Lemma 10 of
Kuchibhotla et al. 2020
). Furthermore, although not discussed in a linear regression context, Lemma 4.1 of
Zhilova 2020
gives a sequence of random variables that justifies the necessity of the condition
d=o(\sqrt{n})
for the consistency of the wild bootstrap when estimating sample mean.

6
Discussion

We provide an estimator for the bias of the ordinary least squares estimator that is consistent as long as the dimension
d
grows slower than
n^{2/3}
, where
n
is the sample size. The resulting debiased least squares estimator, after proper normalization, is asymptotically Normal at an
n^{1/2}
-rate as long as
d=o(n^{2/3})
. We also provide valid inference along any arbitrary direction for the projection parameter without having to estimate the variance. We achieve this inferential goal by leveraging the methods such as HulC. We believe the results of this paper can be extended to further expand the allowed growth rate of dimension. This extension would involve further expansion of the least squares estimator into higher order
U
-statistics and removing the bias.

\mathcal{R}

The proof involves two steps of approximations; (1) an approximation of the distribution of
\sqrt{n}c^{\top}(\widehat{\beta}-\beta-\mathcal{B})
to that of
\sqrt{n}\,c^{\top}\mathcal{U}
and (2) an approximation of the distribution of
\sqrt{n}\,c^{\top}\mathcal{U}
to the adjusted Normal distribution. For any
x\in\mathbb{R}
and
\epsilon>0
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\sqrt{n}c^{\top}(\widehat{\beta}-\beta-\mathcal{B})\leq x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\sqrt{n}c^{\top}(\mathcal{U}+\mathcal{R})\leq x)

(25)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\sqrt{n}\,c^{\top}\mathcal{U}\leq x+\epsilon)+\mathbb{P}(\left|\sqrt{n}c^{\top}\mathcal{R}\right|>\epsilon).

The first term on the right-hand side can be approximated to the adjusted Normal percentile by Lemma
16
as it says that

\displaystyle\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}}\left|\mathbb{P}(\sqrt{n}\,c^{\top}\mathcal{U}\leq x+\epsilon)-N_{c}\left(\frac{x+\epsilon}{\sigma_{c}}\right)\right|\leq\Delta_{n},

(26)

where we write
N_{c}(x)=\Phi(x)-\kappa_{c}\Phi^{(3)}(x)
(see (
14
) for the definition of
\kappa_{c}
). In addition, we have

\Delta_{n}=C\left(\frac{\bar{\lambda}^{3/2}K_{x}^{3}K_{y}^{3}}{\sqrt{n}}+\frac{\bar{\lambda}K_{x}^{6}K_{y}^{2}d}{2n}\right)=O\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}+\frac{d}{n}\right),

for some absolute constant
C>0
. Combining (
25
) and (
26
) leads to

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\sqrt{n}c^{\top}(\widehat{\beta}-\beta-\mathcal{B})\leq x)-N_{c}\left(\frac{x}{\sigma_{c}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle N_{c}\left(\frac{x+\epsilon}{\sigma_{c}}\right)-N_{c}\left(\frac{x}{\sigma_{c}}\right)+\Delta_{n}+\mathbb{P}(\left|\sqrt{n}c^{\top}\mathcal{R}\right|>\epsilon)

(27)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{\epsilon}{\sigma_{c}}\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}\left|N_{c}^{\prime}(t)\right|+\Delta_{n}+\mathbb{P}(\left|\sqrt{n}c^{\top}\mathcal{R}\right|>\epsilon).

Instead of (
25
), we can similarly argue that

\mathbb{P}(\sqrt{n}c^{\top}(\widehat{\beta}-\beta-\mathcal{B})>x)\leq\mathbb{P}(\sqrt{n}\,c^{\top}\mathcal{U}>x+\epsilon)+\mathbb{P}(\left|\sqrt{n}c^{\top}\mathcal{R}\right|>\epsilon),

for any
x\in\mathbb{R}
and
\epsilon>0
. This leads to an analog of (
27
), and consequently, we have

\left|\mathbb{P}(\sqrt{n}c^{\top}(\widehat{\beta}-\beta-\mathcal{B})\leq x)-N_{c}\left(\frac{x}{\sigma_{c}}\right)\right|\leq\frac{\epsilon}{\sigma_{c}}\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}\left|N_{c}^{\prime}(t)\right|+\Delta_{n}+\mathbb{P}(\left|\sqrt{n}c^{\top}\mathcal{R}\right|>\epsilon).

It follows from the definition of
N_{c}
that
\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}\left|N_{c}^{\prime}(t)\right|=(1+3\kappa_{c})/\sqrt{2\pi}
, and
\kappa_{c}
can be further bounded through Lemma
16
essentially leading to

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sigma_{c}}\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}\left|N_{c}^{\prime}(t)\right|\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma_{c}}\left(1+\frac{3K_{x}^{3}K_{y}}{2\sigma_{c}}\sqrt{\frac{d}{n}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma_{c}}\left(1+\frac{3K_{x}^{3}K_{y}}{2\sigma_{c}}\right)

(28)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sqrt{\frac{1}{2\pi\underline{\lambda}}}\left(1+\frac{3K_{x}^{3}K_{y}}{2\underline{\lambda}^{1/2}}\right).

We used
d\leq n
for the penultimate inequality, and the last inequality is due to that
\sigma_{c}^{2}\geq\underline{\lambda}
which follows from Assumption
3
. Hence, we have

\left|\mathbb{P}(\sqrt{n}c^{\top}(\widehat{\beta}-\beta-\mathcal{B})\leq x)-N_{c}\left(\frac{x}{\sigma_{c}}\right)\right|\leq\Delta_{n}+C\epsilon+\mathbb{P}\left(|\sqrt{n}c^{\top}\mathcal{R}|>\epsilon\right),

(29)

for
C=C(\underline{\lambda},K_{x},K_{y})
in (
28
). The next and final step involves harnessing the value of
\epsilon
on the right-hand side of (
29
), which will serve as our Berry Esseen bound. It is worth highlighting that Lemma
13
—
15
establishes the tail bound for the quantity

, contingent upon three different moment assumptions on covariates (Assumption
2.a
—
2.c
). In particular, their results have the form of

Hence, each of these scenarios necessitates distinct selections for

Under Assumption

(31)

\displaystyle\epsilon_{n,d}+N_{c}(t)-N_{c}\left((1-\delta)t\right)+\mathbb{P}\left(\frac{\widehat{\sigma}_{c}}{\sigma_{c}}\leq 1-\delta\right).

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left|\int_{1-\delta}^{1+\delta}N_{c}^{\prime}(ut)t\,du\right|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\int_{1-\delta}^{1+\delta}\left|N_{c}^{\prime}(ut)t\,du\right|\leq\frac{1}{1-\delta}\int_{1-\delta}^{1+\delta}\left|N_{c}^{\prime}(ut)ut\,du\right|\leq c\frac{2\delta}{1-\delta}.

For the last inequality, we use the fact that

\displaystyle\left\|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}T_{i}\right\|_{2}\leq\left\|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}T_{i}\right\|_{2}+\left\|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}T_{i}-\mathbb{E}T_{i}\right\|_{2}.

The leading term on the right-hand side can be bounded as

\displaystyle\|\mathbb{E}T_{i}\|_{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sup_{u\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\mathbb{E}\left[|\tilde{c}^{\top}\Sigma^{-1/2}X_{i}|(Y_{i}-X_{i}^{\top}\beta)^{2}(u^{\top}\Sigma^{-1/2}X_{i})\right]\leq K_{x}^{2}K_{y}^{2},

provided that
1/q_{x}+1/q\leq 1/2
. Furthermore, we observe that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left\|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}T_{i}-\mathbb{E}T_{i}\right\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\rm tr}\left({\rm Var}\left[\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}T_{i}\right]\right)=\frac{1}{n}{\rm tr}\left({\rm Var}\left[W_{1}\right]\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{E}\left[|\tilde{c}^{\top}\Sigma^{-1/2}X_{1}|^{2}(Y_{1}-X_{1}^{\top}\beta)^{4}\|X_{1}\|_{\Sigma^{-1}}^{2}\right]\leq K_{x}^{4}K_{y}^{4}\frac{d}{n},

provided that
1/q_{x}+1/q\leq 1/4
. The Chebyshev’s inequality completes the proof.
∎

A.7.2
Maximal Concentration of the Fitted Values

In this section, we aim to prove the following theorem. For the convenience of notation and clarity, we write the weighted harmonic sum of
q_{x}
and
q
as
q_{m,n}=(m/q_{x}+n/q)^{-1}
for
m,n>0
.

Theorem 11
.

Suppose that Assumption
1
,
2.a
,
3
hold for
q_{x}\geq 4
and
(1/q_{x}+1/q)^{-1}>2
. There exists constants
C=C(q_{x},q,K_{x},K_{y},\underline{\lambda})
such that for any
\delta_{0},\delta_{1},\delta_{2}>0
,

\max_{1\leq i\leq n}|X_{i}^{\top}(\widehat{\beta}-\beta)|\leq C\left(1-9K_{x}\sqrt{\frac{d+2\log(2n/\delta_{0})}{n}}\right)_{+}^{-1}\left\{\sqrt{\frac{d+\log(2n/\delta_{1})}{\delta_{1}^{2/q_{x}}n^{1-2/q_{x}}}}+\frac{\sqrt{d}}{\delta_{1}^{1/q_{2,1}}n^{1-1/q_{3,2}}}+\frac{d}{\delta_{2}^{1/q_{2,1}}n^{1-1/q_{2,1}}}\right\},

with probability at least
1-\delta_{0}-\delta_{1}-\delta_{2}
.

Remark 8
.

In the context of Theorem
11
, the concentration inequality we investigate is not a novel concept; one of its earliest demonstrations was provided by
Portnoy 1985
. They established that if the covariate vector and errors have independent Gaussian entries, then
\max_{1\leq i\leq n}|X_{i}^{\top}(\widehat{\beta}-\beta)|\xrightarrow{P}0
holds, given that
d=o(n^{2/3}/\log^{1/3}(n))
and
n\to\infty,
albeit without explicitly presenting the convergence rate. It is noteworthy that given the infinite number of moments of covariates and errors, our bound tends to 0 as long as
d=o(n)
.

Remark 9
.

A crude bound can be established as
|X_{i}^{\top}(\widehat{\beta}-\beta)|\leq\|X_{i}\|_{\Sigma^{-1}}\|\widehat{\beta}-\beta\|_{\Sigma}
. However, the right-hand side approximately scales as
O_{p}(d/\sqrt{n})
(see Proposition
27
), leading to a loose upper bound. The primary limitation of this bound stems from its failure to account for the dependency relationship between the two quantities,
X_{i}
and
\widehat{\beta}-\beta
. Heuristically, these two quantities should not exhibit high dependence, given that a single observation is expected to contribute a fraction of
1/n
when forming the estimate
\widehat{\beta}
. Consequently, we adopt a leave-one-out analysis to more effectively manage the subtle dependency structure.

proof of Theorem
11
.

We consider the leave-one-out least square estimate, defined as

\widehat{\beta}_{(-i)}:=\widehat{\Sigma}^{-1}_{(-i)}\frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{j\neq i}X_{j}Y_{j},

(45)

for
i=1,\ldots,n
where the
\widehat{\Sigma}_{(-i)}=\sum_{j\neq i}X_{j}X_{j}^{\top}/(n-1)
denotes the leave-one-out sample Gram matrix correspondingly. It follows from the Sherman-Morrison-Woodbury matrix identity that

\displaystyle X_{i}^{\top}(\widehat{\beta}-\beta)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle X_{i}^{\top}\widehat{\Sigma}^{-1}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}X_{j}(Y_{j}-X_{j}^{\top}\beta)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle X_{i}^{\top}\left(\frac{n}{n-1}\widehat{\Sigma}_{(-i)}^{-1}-\frac{\frac{n}{(n-1)^{2}}\widehat{\Sigma}_{(-i)}^{-1}X_{i}X_{i}^{\top}\widehat{\Sigma}_{(-i)}^{-1}}{1+\frac{1}{n-1}X_{i}^{\top}\widehat{\Sigma}_{(-i)}^{-1}X_{i}}\right)\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}X_{j}(Y_{j}-X_{j}^{\top}\beta)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{n}{n-1}\left(1+\frac{1}{n-1}X_{i}^{\top}\widehat{\Sigma}_{(-i)}^{-1}X_{i}\right)^{-1}X_{i}^{\top}\widehat{\Sigma}_{(-i)}^{-1}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}X_{j}(Y_{j}-X_{j}^{\top}\beta)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{n}{n-1}\left(1+\frac{1}{n-1}X_{i}^{\top}\widehat{\Sigma}_{(-i)}^{-1}X_{i}\right)^{-1}\left[\frac{n-1}{n}X_{i}^{\top}(\widehat{\beta}_{(-i)}-\beta)+\frac{1}{n}(X_{i}^{\top}\widehat{\Sigma}_{(-i)}^{-1}X_{i})(Y_{i}-X_{i}^{\top}\beta)\right].

Hence, we have

|X_{i}^{\top}(\widehat{\beta}-\beta)|\leq|X_{i}^{\top}(\widehat{\beta}_{(-i)}-\beta)|+\frac{1}{n-1}(X_{i}^{\top}\widehat{\Sigma}_{(-i)}^{-1}X_{i})|Y_{i}-X_{i}^{\top}\beta|.

(46)

Inspecting the rightmost term in (
46
), we note that

\frac{X_{i}^{\top}\widehat{\Sigma}_{(-i)}^{-1}X_{i}}{X_{i}^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}X_{i}}\leq\sup_{\theta\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\theta}\frac{\theta^{\top}\widehat{\Sigma}_{(-i)}^{-1}\theta}{\theta^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}\theta}\leq\frac{1}{\lambda_{\rm min}(\Sigma^{-1/2}\widehat{\Sigma}_{(-i)}\Sigma^{-1/2})}.

(47)

Hence, we have

\max_{1\leq i\leq n}|X_{i}^{\top}(\widehat{\beta}-\beta)|\leq\max_{1\leq i\leq n}|X_{i}^{\top}(\widehat{\beta}_{(-i)}-\beta)|+\frac{1}{n-1}\max_{1\leq i\leq n}\left\{\frac{(X_{i}^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}X_{i})|Y_{i}-X_{i}^{\top}\beta|}{\lambda_{\rm min}(\Sigma^{-1/2}\widehat{\Sigma}_{(-i)}\Sigma^{-1/2})}\right\}.

Combining Markov’s inequality and Hölder’s inequality yields that for any
t\geq 0
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left((X_{i}^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}X_{i})|Y_{i}-X_{i}^{\top}\beta|\geq t\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle t^{-q_{2,1}}\mathbb{E}\left[(X_{i}^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}X_{i})^{q_{2,1}}|Y_{i}-X_{i}^{\top}\beta|^{q_{2,1}}\right]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle t^{-q_{2,1}}\left\{\left(\mathbb{E}\left[(X_{i}^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}X_{i})^{q_{x}/2}\right]\right)^{2/q_{x}}\left(\mathbb{E}\left[|Y_{i}-X_{i}^{\top}\beta|^{q}\right]\right)^{1/q}\right\}^{q_{2,1}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left(\frac{K_{x}^{2}K_{y}d}{t}\right)^{q_{2,1}}.

Equivalently, we have

\mathbb{P}\left((X_{i}^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}X_{i})|Y_{i}-X_{i}^{\top}\beta|\geq K_{x}^{2}K_{y}d\delta^{-1/q_{2,1}}\right)\leq\delta,

for any
\delta\in(0,1)
. Meanwhile, Proposition
24
reads

\mathbb{P}\left(\lambda_{\rm min}(\Sigma^{-1/2}\widehat{\Sigma}_{(-i)}\Sigma^{-1/2})\geq 1-9K_{x}\sqrt{\frac{d+2\log(2/\delta_{0})}{n-1}}\right)\leq\delta_{0}.

Combining these yields that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n-1}\frac{(X_{i}^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}X_{i})|Y_{i}-X_{i}^{\top}\beta|}{\lambda_{\rm min}(\Sigma^{-1/2}\widehat{\Sigma}_{(-i)}\Sigma^{-1/2})}\leq C\left(1-9K_{x}\sqrt{\frac{d+2\log(4/\delta_{0})}{n}}\right)_{+}^{-1}K_{x}^{2}K_{y}\frac{d\delta^{-1/q_{2,1}}}{n},

holds with probability at least
1-\delta_{0}-\delta
with an absolute constant
C
. Consequently, the union-bound yields

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n-1}\max_{1\leq i\leq n}\left\{\frac{(X_{i}^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}X_{i})|Y_{i}-X_{i}^{\top}\beta|}{\lambda_{\rm min}(\Sigma^{-1/2}\widehat{\Sigma}_{(-i)}\Sigma^{-1/2})}\right\}\leq C\left(1-9K_{x}\sqrt{\frac{d+2\log(2n/\delta_{0})}{n}}\right)_{+}^{-1}K_{x}^{2}K_{y}\frac{d\delta^{-1/q_{2,1}}}{n^{1-1/q_{2,1}}},

with probability at least
1-\delta_{0}-\delta
for all
\delta_{0},\delta\in(0,1)
.

We now analyze
X_{i}^{\top}(\widehat{\beta}_{(-i)}-\beta)
. Since it is an inner product of independent quantities, we get from the conditional Markov’s inequality and Assumption
2.b
that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(|X_{i}^{\top}(\widehat{\beta}_{(-i)}-\beta)|\geq t\big|\widehat{\beta}_{(-i)}\right)\leq\|\widehat{\beta}_{(-i)}-\beta\|_{\Sigma}^{q_{x}}(K_{x}/t)^{q_{x}}\quad\mbox{almost surely,}

(48)

for
t\geq 0
. An application of Proposition
27
yields that

\displaystyle\|\widehat{\beta}_{(-i)}-\beta\|_{\Sigma}\leq C\left(1-9K_{x}\sqrt{\frac{d+2\log(2/\delta_{0})}{n}}\right)_{+}^{-1}\left[2\sqrt{\frac{d+\log(2/\delta)}{n\underline{\lambda}}}+\frac{C_{q_{x},q}K_{x}K_{y}d^{1/2}}{(\delta_{2}/n)^{1/q_{1,1}}n}\right],

(49)

with probability at least
1-\delta_{0}-\delta
. Denote the event in (
49
) as
\mathcal{C}_{i}
for
i=1,\ldots,n
. The law of total expectation combined with the conditional Markov’s inequality in (
48
) gives that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(|X_{i}^{\top}(\widehat{\beta}_{(-i)}-\beta)|\geq t\right)=\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbb{P}\left(|X_{i}^{\top}(\widehat{\beta}_{(-i)}-\beta)|\geq t\big|\widehat{\beta}_{(-i)}\right)\right]\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\|\widehat{\beta}_{(-i)}-\beta\|_{\Sigma}^{q_{x}}(K_{x}/t)^{q_{x}}\mathbbm{1}(\mathcal{C}_{i})+\mathbbm{1}(\mathcal{C}_{i}^{\complement})\right]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\underbrace{C\left(1-9K_{x}\sqrt{\frac{d+2\log(2/\delta_{0})}{n}}\right)_{+}^{-q_{x}}}_{=:C(\delta_{0})}t^{-q_{x}}\left[\left(\frac{d+\log(2/\delta)}{n}\right)^{q_{x}/2}+\frac{d^{q_{x}/2}}{\delta^{q_{x}/q_{1,1}}n^{q_{x}-q_{x}/q_{1,1}}}\right]+\delta_{0}+\delta.

To minimize the right-hand side with respect to
\delta
, we take
\delta=(d^{1/2}/(tn^{1-1/q_{1,1}}))^{q_{2,1}}
, resulting in

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(|X_{i}^{\top}(\widehat{\beta}_{(-i)}-\beta)|\geq t\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C(\delta_{0})\left[\left(\frac{d+\log(2nt)}{t^{2}n}\right)^{q_{x}/2}+\left(\frac{d^{1/2}}{tn^{(1-1/q_{1,1})}}\right)^{q_{2,1}}\right]+\delta_{0}.

The union-bound gives that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\max_{1\leq i\leq n}|X_{i}^{\top}(\widehat{\beta}_{(-i)}-\beta)|\geq t\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C(\delta_{0}/n)n\left[\left(\frac{d+\log(2nt)}{t^{2}n}\right)^{q_{x}/2}+\left(\frac{d^{1/2}}{tn^{(1-1/q_{1,1})}}\right)^{q_{2,1}}\right]+\delta_{0}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle C(\delta_{0}/n)\left[\left(\frac{d+\log(2nt)}{t^{2}n^{1-2/q_{x}}}\right)^{q_{x}/2}+\left(\frac{d^{1/2}}{tn^{(1-1/q_{3,2})}}\right)^{q_{2,1}}\right]+\delta_{0},

for all
t\geq 0
. We take

t=C(\delta_{0}/n)^{1/q_{x}}\left[\sqrt{\frac{d+\log(2n)}{\delta^{2/q_{x}}n^{1-2/q_{x}}}}+\frac{d^{1/2}}{\delta^{1/q_{2,1}}n^{1-1/q_{3,2}}}\right].

This yields that

\displaystyle\max_{1\leq i\leq n}|X_{i}^{\top}(\widehat{\beta}_{(-i)}-\beta)|\leq C\left(1-9K_{x}\sqrt{\frac{d+2\log(2n/\delta_{0})}{n}}\right)_{+}^{-1}\left[\sqrt{\frac{d+\log(2n/\delta)}{\delta^{2/q_{x}}n^{1-2/q_{x}}}}+\frac{d^{1/2}}{\delta^{1/q_{2,1}}n^{1-1/q_{3,2}}}\right],

with probability at least
1-\delta-\delta_{0}
. The right-hand side tends to
0
as long as
d=o(n^{1-2/q_{x}})
if
(1/q_{x}+1/q)^{-1}\geq 4
.
∎

\displaystyle\left(1-\frac{1}{n}\right)^{2}\mathbb{E}|c^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}(\Sigma-X_{1}X_{1}^{\top})\Sigma^{-1}X_{2}(Y_{2}-X_{2}^{\top}\beta)|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}|c^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}(\Sigma-X_{1}X_{1}^{\top})\Sigma^{-1}X_{2}(Y_{2}-X_{2}^{\top}\beta)|^{2}.

For any
v\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we note that Jensen’s inequality yields

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left|v^{\top}\Sigma^{-1/2}X_{2}(Y_{2}-X_{2}^{\top}\beta)\right|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\|v\|_{2}^{2}\left(\mathbb{E}\left|(v/\|v\|_{2})^{\top}\Sigma^{-1/2}X_{2}(Y_{2}-X_{2}^{\top}\beta)\right|^{s}\right)^{2/s}

(56)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\|v\|_{2}^{2}\left\{\left(\mathbb{E}\left|(v/\|v\|_{2})^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}X_{2}\right|^{q_{x}}\right)^{q/(q_{x}+q)}\left(\mathbb{E}\left|Y_{2}-X_{2}^{\top}\beta\right|^{q}\right)^{q_{x}/(q_{x}+q)}\right\}^{2/s}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\|v\|_{2}^{2}K_{x}^{2}K_{y}^{2},

where the first inequality is Jensen’s inequality, and the second inequality follows from Hölder’s inequality. Combining this with the independence of
(X_{1},Y_{1})
and
(X_{2},Y_{2})
yields

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left|c^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}(\Sigma-X_{1}X_{1}^{\top})\Sigma^{-1}X_{2}(Y_{2}-X_{2}^{\top}\beta)\right|^{2}\leq K_{x}^{2}K_{y}^{2}\mathbb{E}\|c^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}(\Sigma-X_{1}X_{1}^{\top})\Sigma^{-1/2}\|_{2}^{2}.

Inspecting the right-hand side, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\|c^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}(\Sigma-X_{1}X_{1}^{\top})\Sigma^{-1/2}\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\{c^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}(\Sigma-X_{1}X_{1}^{\top})\Sigma^{-1}(\Sigma-X_{1}X_{1}^{\top})\Sigma^{-1}c\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle c^{\top}\Sigma^{-1/2}\mathbb{E}\left(\Sigma^{-1/2}X_{1}X_{1}^{\top}\Sigma^{-1/2}\|X_{1}\|_{\Sigma^{-1}}^{2}-I\right)\Sigma^{-1/2}c

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle c^{\top}\Sigma^{-1/2}\mathbb{E}\left(\Sigma^{-1/2}X_{1}X_{1}^{\top}\Sigma^{-1/2}\|X_{1}\|_{\Sigma^{-1}}^{2}\right)\Sigma^{-1/2}c

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sup_{u\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\mathbb{E}\left\{\left(u^{\top}\Sigma^{-1/2}X_{1}\right)^{2}\|X_{1}\|_{\Sigma^{-1}}^{2}\right\}.

\|X_{1}\|_{\Sigma^{-1}}^{2}
, let

be the
j
:th canonical basis of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
for

. Then,

\displaystyle\sup_{u\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\mathbb{E}\left\{\left(u^{\top}\Sigma^{-1/2}X_{1}\right)^{2}\|X_{1}\|_{\Sigma^{-1}}^{2}\right\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sup_{u\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\mathbb{E}\left\{\left(u^{\top}\Sigma^{-1/2}X_{1}\right)^{2}\sum_{j=1}^{d}(\mathbf{e}_{j}^{\top}\Sigma^{-1/2}X_{1})^{2}\right\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sup_{u\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\sum_{j=1}^{d}\mathbb{E}\left\{\left(u^{\top}\Sigma^{-1/2}X_{1}\right)^{2}(\mathbf{e}_{j}^{\top}\Sigma^{-1/2}X_{1})^{2}\right\}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle d\sup_{u\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\max_{1\leq j\leq d}\mathbb{E}\left\{\left(u^{\top}\Sigma^{-1/2}X_{1}\right)^{2}(\mathbf{e}_{j}^{\top}\Sigma^{-1/2}X_{1})^{2}\right\}

\displaystyle d\sup_{u\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\mathbb{E}\left\{\left(u^{\top}\Sigma^{-1/2}X_{1}\right)^{4}\right\}\leq dK_{x}^{4}.

Here, the next-to-last inequality is Cauchy Schwarz inequality, and the last inequality is due to Assumption
2.a
. Combining all, we have

\displaystyle\gamma_{c}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{\bar{\lambda}}{2n}\mathbb{E}\left|c^{\top}\phi(X_{1},Y_{1},X_{2},Y_{2})\right|^{2}

(57)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{\bar{\lambda}K_{x}^{6}K_{y}^{2}d}{2n}.

This concludes the proof of the first part. The last part can be done by applying Theorem
2
of
Bentkus et al. 2009
, which proves the following: there exists a universal constant
C>0
such that

\displaystyle\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}}\left|\mathbb{P}\left(\sqrt{n}\frac{c^{\top}\mathcal{U}}{\sigma_{c}}\leq x\right)-\Phi(x)\right|\leq C\left(\eta_{c}+\gamma_{c}^{1/2}\right).

Finally, we control

. From its definition in (

) and Cauchy Schwarz’s inequality, we have

\displaystyle\kappa_{c}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\sigma_{c}\sqrt{n}}\left(\mathbb{E}\left[\left\{c^{\top}\phi(X_{1},Y_{1},X_{2},Y_{2})\right\}^{2}\right]\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{K_{x}^{3}K_{y}\sqrt{d}}{2\sigma_{c}\sqrt{n}}\leq\frac{\bar{\lambda}^{1/2}K_{x}^{3}K_{y}\sqrt{d}}{2\sqrt{n}}.

Lemma 17
.

Suppose that Assumption
1
and
2.c
hold with
(3/q_{x}+1/q)^{-1}\geq 2
. Then,

\mathbb{P}\left(\left|\sqrt{n}c^{\top}\mathcal{B}\right|>K_{x}^{3}K_{y}\left(\sqrt{\frac{d}{n}}+\frac{d}{n\sqrt{\delta}}\right)\right)\leq\delta,

(58)

for
\delta\in(0,1)

Recall that

for
i=1,\ldots,n
. Chebyshev’s inequality implies that for any

(59)

Since
\beta
is the projection parameter, note that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}c^{\top}V_{i}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}c^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}X_{i}(Y_{i}-X_{i}^{\top}\beta)\|X_{i}\|_{\Sigma^{-1}}^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\rm Cov}\left(c^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}X_{i}(Y_{i}-X_{i}^{\top}\beta),\|X_{i}\|_{\Sigma^{-1}}^{2}\right).

This leads to

\displaystyle\left|\mathbb{E}c^{\top}V_{i}\right|\leq\left[{\rm Var}\left\{c^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}X_{i}(Y_{i}-X_{i}^{\top}\beta)\right\}\right]^{1/2}\left\{{\rm Var}\left(\|X_{i}\|_{\Sigma^{-1}}^{2}\right)\right\}^{1/2}.

The primary term on the right-hand side can be bounded in a similar manner to the approach outlined in equation (
56
):

{\rm Var}\left\{c^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}X_{i}(Y_{i}-X_{i}^{\top}\beta)\right\}\leq\mathbb{E}\left|c^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}X_{i}\right|^{2}\left|Y_{i}-X_{i}^{\top}\beta\right|^{2}\leq K_{x}^{2}K_{y}^{2}.

(60)

\|X_{i}\|_{\Sigma^{-1}}
, write
Z_{i}=\Sigma^{-1/2}X_{i}
and
Z_{i}=(Z_{i}(1),\ldots,Z_{i}(d))^{\top}
where
\mathbb{E}Z_{i}(j)^{2}=1
and
Z_{i}(j)
for
1\leq j\leq d
are independent. Then, we get

\displaystyle{\rm Var}(\|X_{i}\|_{\Sigma^{-1}}^{2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\|X_{i}\|_{\Sigma^{-1}}^{4}-\left(\mathbb{E}\|X_{i}\|_{\Sigma^{-1}}^{2}\right)^{2}

(61)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{d}\mathbb{E}[Z_{i}(j)^{4}]+\sum_{j\neq j^{\prime}}\mathbb{E}[Z_{i}(j)^{2}Z_{i}(j^{\prime})^{2}]-d^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{d}\mathbb{E}[Z_{i}(j)^{4}]-d\leq d(K_{x}^{4}-1).

The last inequality is due to Assumption
2.a
. Combining (
60
) and (
61
), we get
|\mathbb{E}c^{\top}V_{i}|\leq K_{x}^{3}K_{y}\sqrt{d}
for
i=1,\ldots,n
, and this implies that

\displaystyle\left|\mathbb{E}\sqrt{n}c^{\top}\mathcal{B}\right|\leq K_{x}^{3}K_{y}\sqrt{\frac{d}{n}}.

(62)

Now we focus on the variance of the bias
\mathcal{B}
. We note that

\displaystyle{\rm Var}\left(\sqrt{n}c^{\top}\mathcal{B}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n^{2}}{\rm Var}(c^{\top}V_{1})\leq\frac{1}{n^{2}}\mathbb{E}[(c^{\top}V_{1})^{2}]=\frac{1}{n^{2}}\mathbb{E}(c^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}X_{i})^{2}(Y_{i}-X_{i}^{\top}\beta)^{2}\|X_{i}\|_{\Sigma^{-1}}^{4}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n^{2}}\mathbb{E}(c^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}X_{i})^{2}(Y_{i}-X_{i}^{\top}\beta)^{2}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{d}Z_{i}(j)^{2}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{d}{n^{2}}\mathbb{E}(c^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}X_{i})^{2}(Y_{i}-X_{i}^{\top}\beta)^{2}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{d}Z_{i}(j)^{4}\right)

where two inequalities are Cauchy Schwarz’s inequalities. Let

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(c^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}X_{i})^{2}(Y_{i}-X_{i}^{\top}\beta)^{2}Z_{i}(j)^{4}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left[\mathbb{E}|c^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}X_{i}|^{2l}|Y_{i}-X_{i}^{\top}\beta|^{2l}|Z_{i}(j)|^{4l}\right]^{1/l}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left[\left\{\mathbb{E}|c^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}X_{i}|^{q_{x}}\right\}^{2l/q_{x}}\left\{\mathbb{E}|Y_{i}-X_{i}^{\top}\beta|^{q}\right\}^{2l/q}\left\{\mathbb{E}|Z_{i}(j)|^{q_{x}}\right\}^{4l/q_{x}}\right]^{1/l}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle K_{x}^{6}K_{y}^{2}.

Hence, we get
{\rm Var}(\sqrt{n}c^{\top}\mathcal{B})\leq K_{x}^{6}K_{y}^{2}d^{2}/n^{2}
. Combining this with (
62
) in (
59
) completes the proof.

66

\displaystyle\left\|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Sigma^{-1/2}X_{i}X_{i}^{\top}\Sigma^{-1/2}\frac{\|X_{i}\|^{2}_{\Sigma^{-1}}}{d}\right\|_{\rm op}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left\|\mathbb{E}\Sigma^{-1/2}X_{1}X_{1}^{\top}\Sigma^{-1/2}\frac{\|X_{1}\|^{2}_{\Sigma^{-1}}}{d}\right\|_{\rm op}+\left\|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Sigma^{-1/2}X_{i}X_{i}^{\top}\Sigma^{-1/2}\frac{\|X_{i}\|^{2}_{\Sigma^{-1}}}{d}-\mathbb{E}\Sigma^{-1/2}X_{1}X_{1}^{\top}\Sigma^{-1/2}\frac{\|X_{1}\|^{2}_{\Sigma^{-1}}}{d}\right\|_{\rm op}.

The first part of the right-hand side can be bounded as

\displaystyle\left\|\mathbb{E}\Sigma^{-1/2}X_{1}X_{1}^{\top}\Sigma^{-1/2}\frac{\|X_{1}\|^{2}_{\Sigma^{-1}}}{d}\right\|_{\rm op}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sup_{\theta\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\mathbb{E}\left[(\theta^{\top}\Sigma^{-1/2}X_{1})^{2}\frac{\|X_{1}\|^{2}_{\Sigma^{-1}}}{d}\right]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sup_{\theta\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\mathbb{E}\left[(\theta^{\top}\Sigma^{-1/2}X_{1})^{4}\right]\leq K_{x}^{4}.

To control the second part, we let

Z_{i}=\Sigma^{-1/2}X_{i}X_{i}^{\top}\Sigma^{-1/2}\frac{\|X_{i}\|^{2}_{\Sigma^{-1}}}{d},\quad\mbox{for}\quad i=1,\ldots,n.

An application of Theorem 5.1(2) of
Tropp 2016
gives that

\left(\mathbb{E}\left\|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}Z_{i}-\mathbb{E}Z_{i}\right\|_{\rm op}^{2}\right)^{1/2}\leq C_{d}^{1/2}\left\|\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}Z_{i}-\mathbb{E}Z_{i}\right)^{2}\right]\right\|_{\rm op}^{1/2}+\frac{C_{d}}{n}\left(\mathbb{E}\left[\max_{1\leq i\leq n}\left\|Z_{i}-\mathbb{E}Z_{i}\right\|_{\rm op}^{2}\right]\right)^{1/2},

(67)

where
C_{d}=2(1+4\lceil\log d\rceil)
. The leading term on (
67
) can be bounded as

\displaystyle\left\|\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}Z_{i}-\mathbb{E}Z_{i}\right)^{2}\right]\right\|_{\rm op}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\left\|\mathbb{E}[Z_{1}^{2}]-(\mathbb{E}Z_{1})^{2}\right\|_{\rm op}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\left\|\mathbb{E}[Z_{1}^{2}]\right\|_{\rm op}\quad\because\mathbb{E}Z_{1}\mbox{ is symmetric and positive definite.}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{n}\left(\sup_{\theta\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\mathbb{E}\left[(\theta^{\top}\Sigma^{-1/2}X_{i})^{2}\frac{\|X_{1}\|^{6}_{\Sigma^{-1}}}{d^{2}}\right]\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{d}{n}\sup_{\theta\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\mathbb{E}[(\theta^{\top}\Sigma^{-1/2}X_{i})^{8}]\leq\frac{K_{x}^{8}d}{n}.

Meanwhile, the second part is controlled as

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\max_{i\in[n]}\left\|Z_{i}-\mathbb{E}Z_{i}\right\|_{\rm op}^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\max_{i\in[n]}\left\|Z_{i}\right\|_{\rm op}^{2}\right]\quad\because\mathbb{E}Z_{1}\mbox{ is positive definite.}

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left\|Z_{i}\right\|_{\rm op}^{2}\right]=n\mathbb{E}\left\|Z_{1}\right\|_{\rm op}^{2}

\displaystyle=n\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{\|X_{1}\|^{8}_{\Sigma^{-1}}}{d^{2}}\right]

\displaystyle\leq nd^{2}\sup_{\theta\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\mathbb{E}[(\theta^{\top}\Sigma^{-1/2}X_{i})^{8}]\leq K_{x}^{8}nd^{2}.

Combining these with (
67
), we get

\displaystyle\left(\mathbb{E}\left\|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}Z_{i}-\mathbb{E}Z_{i}\right\|_{\rm op}^{2}\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle K_{x}^{4}(C_{d}^{1/2}\vee C_{d})\left(\sqrt{\frac{d}{n}}+\frac{d}{\sqrt{n}}\right)\leq\frac{16K_{x}^{4}d\log(2d)}{\sqrt{n}}.

Consequently, Chebyshev’s inequality yields

\mathbb{P}\left(\left\|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}Z_{i}-\mathbb{E}Z_{i}\right\|_{\rm op}\geq t\right)\leq\left(\frac{16K_{x}^{4}d\log(2d)}{t\sqrt{n}}\right)^{2}.

Combining all, it follows that

\mathbb{P}\left(\left\|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Sigma^{-1/2}X_{i}X_{i}^{\top}\Sigma^{-1/2}\frac{\|X_{i}\|^{2}_{\Sigma^{-1}}}{d}\right\|_{\rm op}\geq K_{x}^{4}+\frac{16K_{x}^{4}d\log(2d)}{\delta\sqrt{n}}\right)\leq\delta,

for any
\delta\in(0,1)
.
∎

B.3

As an independent branch of research, estimating the covariance matrix
\Sigma

[
Rudelson 1999
,
Adamczak et al. 2010
]
, sub-Gaussian distributions

,
Vershynin 2018

[

,
Mendelson and Paouris 2012
,
Srivastava and Vershynin 2013
,
Mendelson and Paouris 2014
]
.

In our application, we primarily utilize the standard sample covariance matrix
\widehat{\Sigma}
with the standard operator norm

as a measure. Notably, some papers have explored truncated estimates, as seen in
[
Abdalla 2023
]
. Moreover, alternative studies have considered using the Frobenius norm as a metric
[

.

Proposition 22
.

Recall that
\mathcal{D}_{\Sigma}=\|\Sigma^{-1/2}\widehat{\Sigma}\Sigma^{-1/2}-I_{d}\|_{\rm op}
. The following concentration inequalities for
\mathcal{D}_{\Sigma}
hold under different moment assumptions on covariates.

1.

Under Assumption
2.b
, there exists a universal constant
C>0
such that

(68)

for all
\delta\in(0,1)
.

2.

Under Assumption
2.a
with
q_{x}\geq 4
, there exists a universal constant

such that

\mathbb{P}\left(\mathcal{D}_{\Sigma}\leq CK_{x}^{2}\left[\sqrt{\frac{d\log(2d/\delta)}{n}}+\frac{d\log^{3/2}(2d/\delta)}{\delta^{2/q_{x}}n^{1-2/q_{x}}}\right]\right)\geq 1-\delta,

(69)

for all
\delta\in(0,1)
.

3.

Under Assumption
2.c
with
q_{x}>4
, there exists a constant
C>0
that only depends on
q_{x}
that

\mathbb{P}\left(\mathcal{D}_{\Sigma}\leq CK_{x}^{2}\left[\sqrt{\frac{d\log(2d/\delta)}{n}}+\frac{d^{2/q_{x}}\log^{3/2}(2d/\delta)}{\delta^{2/q_{x}}n^{1-2/q_{x}}}\right]\right)\geq 1-\delta,

for all

.

proof of (
68
)

The result under Assumption
2.b
, which pertains to sub-Gaussianity, is well-known. Comprehensive proofs and discussions can be found in existing references such as Theorem 4.7.1 of
Vershynin 2018
or Theorem
1
of
Koltchinskii and Lounici 2017
.

proof of (
69
)

We use Theorem 2.7 of
Brailovskaya and van Handel 2023
. To state their results, some matrix parameters deserve to be defined. We put

\displaystyle\sigma_{\star}^{2}

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\left\|\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\Sigma^{-1/2}\widehat{\Sigma}\Sigma^{-1/2}-I_{d}\right)^{2}\right]\right\|_{\rm op},\quad\sigma_{\circ}^{2}:=\sup_{u,v\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\mathbb{E}\left[\left|u^{\top}\left(\widehat{\Sigma}-\mathbb{E}\widehat{\Sigma}\right)v\right|^{2}\right],

\displaystyle Z_{n}

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\max_{1\leq i\leq n}\left\|\Sigma^{-1/2}X_{i}X_{i}^{\top}\Sigma^{-1/2}-I_{d}\right\|_{\rm op},\quad\bar{R}^{2}:=\mathbb{E}[Z_{n}^{2}].

(71)

The existences of
\sigma_{\circ}^{2}
,
\sigma_{\star}^{2}
, and
\bar{R}^{2}
are guaranteed as long as
q_{x}\geq 4
.

Define a
d\times d
random matrix
G
with jointly Gaussian entries such that
\mathbb{E}G=I_{d}
and
{\rm Cov}(G)={\rm Cov}(\Sigma^{-1/2}\widehat{\Sigma}\Sigma^{-1/2})
. More precisely, we define the
d^{2}\times d^{2}
entry covariance matrix of
Y\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
as

{\rm Cov}(Y)_{ij,kl}=\mathbb{E}[(Y-\mathbb{E}Y)_{ij}(Y-\mathbb{E}Y)_{kl}],\quad 1\leq i,j,k,l\leq d.

(72)

Since jointly Gaussian random variables can be characterized with their first and second moments,
\mathbb{E}G
and
{\rm Cov}(G)
uniquely define the distribution of
G
(See, e.g., Section 2.1 of
Brailovskaya and van Handel 2023
). We denote the spectrum of the operator
M
, that is, a collection of eigenvalues, as
{\rm sp}(M)
. Let
d_{H}(A,B)
be a Hausdorff distance between two sets in
\mathbb{R}
, which is defined as
d_{H}(A,B)=\inf\left\{\epsilon>0:A\subset B+[-\epsilon,\epsilon],\,B\subset A+[-\epsilon,\epsilon]\right\}
. We are ready to state Theorem 2.7 of
Brailovskaya and van Handel 2023
; there exists a universal constant
C>0
such that

\mathbb{P}\left(d_{H}\left({\rm sp}(\Sigma^{-1/2}\widehat{\Sigma}\Sigma^{-1/2}),{\rm sp}(G)\right)\geq C\left(\sigma_{\circ}t^{1/2}+R^{1/3}\sigma_{\star}^{2/3}t^{2/3}+R\right)\mbox{ and }Z_{n}\leq R\right)\leq de^{-t},

(73)

for all
t\geq 0
and
R\geq\bar{R}^{1/2}\sigma_{\star}^{1/2}+\sqrt{2}\bar{R}
. This result readily controls the differences between extreme eigenvalues of
\Sigma^{-1/2}\widehat{\Sigma}\Sigma^{-1/2}
and
G
. In particular, we observe

\max\left\{|\lambda_{\rm max}(\Sigma^{-1/2}\widehat{\Sigma}\Sigma^{-1/2})-\lambda_{\rm max}(G)|,|\lambda_{\rm min}(\Sigma^{-1/2}\widehat{\Sigma}\Sigma^{-1/2})-\lambda_{\rm min}(G)|\right\}\leq d_{H}\left({\rm sp}(\Sigma^{-1/2}\widehat{\Sigma}\Sigma^{-1/2}),{\rm sp}(G)\right).

Since
\|M-I\|_{\rm op}=\max\{|\lambda_{\rm max}(M)-1|,|\lambda_{\rm min}(M)-1|\}
, the triangle inequality gives

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{\Sigma}\leq\|G-I\|_{\rm op}+d_{H}\left({\rm sp}(\Sigma^{-1/2}\widehat{\Sigma}\Sigma^{-1/2}),{\rm sp}(G)\right).

Since
G
has jointly Gaussian entries, its spectral properties are relatively well-known, and will be analyzed in Proposition
25
.

Now, we will have a closer look at (
73
) by analyzing the parameters
\sigma_{\star}^{2},\sigma_{\circ}^{2}
and
\bar{R}
. For
\sigma_{\star}^{2}
, we note that

\displaystyle\sigma_{\star}^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sup_{\theta\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\left|\mathbb{E}\left[\theta^{\top}\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\Sigma^{-1/2}X_{i}X_{i}^{\top}\Sigma^{-1/2}-I)\right)^{2}\theta\right]\right|=\sup_{\theta\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\left|\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}\left[\theta^{\top}(\Sigma^{-1/2}X_{i}X_{i}^{\top}\Sigma^{-1/2}-I)^{2}\theta\right]\right|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sup_{\theta\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\left|\mathbb{E}\left[\theta^{\top}(\Sigma^{-1/2}XX^{\top}\Sigma^{-1/2}-I)^{2}\theta\right]\right|=\frac{1}{n}\sup_{\theta\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\left|\mathbb{E}\left[(\theta^{\top}\Sigma^{-1/2}X)^{2}\|X\|_{\Sigma^{-1}}^{2}\right]-1\right|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{K_{x}^{4}d}{n}.

Meanwhile,
\sigma_{\circ}^{2}
can be controlled as

\displaystyle\sigma_{\circ}^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sup_{\theta\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\mathbb{E}\left(\theta^{\top}\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\Sigma^{-1/2}X_{i}X_{i}^{\top}\Sigma^{-1/2}-I)\right)\theta\right)^{2}=\sup_{\theta\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\mathbb{E}\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}[(\theta^{\top}\Sigma^{-1/2}X_{i})^{2}-1]\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sup_{\theta\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}\left[((\theta^{\top}\Sigma^{-1/2}X_{i})^{2}-1)^{2}\right]=\frac{1}{n}\sup_{\theta\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\left|\mathbb{E}\left[(\theta^{\top}\Sigma^{-1/2}X)^{4}\right]-1\right|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{K_{x}^{4}}{n}.

To analyze
\bar{R}^{2}
, we note that
\|\Sigma^{-1/2}X_{i}X_{i}^{\top}\Sigma^{-1/2}-I_{d}\|_{\rm op}=|\|X_{i}\|_{\Sigma^{-1}}^{2}-1|\leq\|X_{i}\|_{\Sigma^{-1}}^{2}+1
. This gives that

\displaystyle\bar{R}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n^{2}}\mathbb{E}\left[\max_{1\leq i\leq n}\|X_{i}\|_{\Sigma^{-1}}^{4}+1\right]\leq\frac{1}{n^{2}}+\frac{1}{n^{2}}\left(\mathbb{E}\left[\max_{1\leq i\leq n}\|X_{i}\|_{\Sigma^{-1}}^{q_{x}}\right]\right)^{4/q_{x}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1+K_{x}^{4}d^{2}}{n^{2-4/q_{x}}}\leq\frac{2K_{x}^{4}d^{2}}{n^{2-4/q_{x}}}.

We let

\displaystyle R(\delta)=K_{x}^{2}\left[\frac{2d}{\delta^{2/q_{x}}n^{1-2/q_{x}}}+\left(\frac{\sqrt{2}d}{n^{1-2/q_{x}}}\right)^{1/2}\left(\frac{d}{n}\right)^{1/4}\right],

for
\delta\in(0,1)
, so that
R(\delta)\geq\bar{R}^{1/2}\sigma_{\star}^{1/2}+\sqrt{2}\bar{R}
. In addition, we observe that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(Z_{n}\geq R(\delta)\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\max_{1\leq i\leq n}\left|\|X_{i}\|_{\Sigma^{-1}}^{2}-1\right|\geq nR(\delta)\right)

(74)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle n\mathbb{P}\left(\left|\|X\|_{\Sigma^{-1}}^{2}-1\right|\geq nR(\delta)\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{n\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\|X\|_{\Sigma^{-1}}^{2}-1\right|^{q_{x}/2}\right]}{[nR(\delta)]^{q_{x}/2}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{2K_{x}^{q_{x}}d^{q_{x}/2}}{n^{q_{x}/2-1}[R(\delta)]^{q_{x}/2}}\leq\frac{\delta}{2}.

On the other hand, since Young’s inequality yields
3R(\delta)^{1/3}\sigma_{\star}^{2/3}t^{2/3}\leq 2\sigma_{\star}t^{1/2}+R(\delta)t
, we have

\displaystyle\epsilon_{R(\delta)}(t)=\sigma_{\circ}t^{1/2}+R(\delta)^{1/3}\sigma_{\star}^{2/3}t^{2/3}+R(\delta)t\leq C\left(\sigma_{\star}t^{1/2}+R(\delta)t\right)\leq CK_{x}^{2}\left(\sqrt{\frac{dt}{n}}+\frac{dt^{3/2}}{\delta^{2/q_{x}}n^{1-2/q_{x}}}\right).

Here, we used the fact that
\sigma_{\circ}\leq\sigma_{\star}
. Hence, the inequality (
73
) reads

\mathbb{P}\left(d_{H}\left({\rm sp}(\widehat{\Sigma}),{\rm sp}(G)\right)\geq CK_{x}^{2}\left(\sqrt{\frac{dt}{n}}+\frac{dt^{3/2}}{\delta^{2/q_{x}}n^{1-2/q_{x}}}\right)\mbox{ and }Z_{n}\leq R(\delta)\right)\leq de^{-t}.

Taking
t=\log(2d/\delta)
and combining this with (
74
) implies that

\mathbb{P}\left(d_{H}\left({\rm sp}(\widehat{\Sigma}),{\rm sp}(G)\right)\geq CK_{x}^{2}\left(\sqrt{\frac{d\log(2d/\delta)}{n}}+\frac{d\log^{3/2}(2d/\delta)}{\delta^{2/q_{x}}n^{1-2/q_{x}}}\right)\right)\leq\delta.

Finally, we note that

\mathcal{D}_{\Sigma}\leq d_{H}\left({\rm sp}(\widehat{\Sigma}),{\rm sp}(G)\right)+\|G-I_{d}\|_{\rm op}.

The tail bound of
\|G-I_{d}\|_{\rm op}
, which is given in Proposition
25
completes the proof.

Proof of (
70
)

Under Assumption
2.c
, we prove that the random variable
Z_{n}
and its second moment
\bar{R}^{2}
(both defined in (
B.3
)) can be better controlled. Recall that
Z_{n}=\max_{1\leq i\leq n}\left|\|X_{i}\|_{\Sigma^{-1}}^{2}-1\right|
. Applying equation (1.9) of
Rio 2017
or equation (E.16) of
Yang and Kuchibhotla 2021
gives that if
q_{x}\geq 4
, then

\mathbb{P}\left(Z_{n}\geq\frac{d+1}{n}+\frac{K_{x}^{2}\sqrt{d\log(2n/\delta)}}{n}+C_{q_{x}}K_{x}^{2}\frac{d^{2/q_{x}}}{\delta^{2/q_{x}}n^{1-2/q_{x}}}\right)\leq\delta,

for
\delta\in(0,1)
where
C_{q_{x}}=1+4/q_{x}+q_{x}/6
. Let the right-hand side inside the probability as
\tilde{R}(\delta)
, so that

\tilde{R}(\delta)^{2}\leq CK_{x}^{2}\left[\frac{d^{2}+d\log(2n/\delta)}{n^{2}}+\frac{d^{4/q_{x}}}{\delta^{4/q_{x}}n^{2-4/q_{x}}}\right],

holds for some constant
C=C(q_{x})>0
. We note that with some possibly different constants,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[Z_{n}^{2}]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}(Z_{n}^{2}\geq t)\,dt=\left(\int_{0}^{\tilde{R}(1)^{2}}+\int_{\tilde{R}(1)^{2}}^{\tilde{R}(0)^{2}}\right)\mathbb{P}(Z_{n}^{2}\geq t)\,dt

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\tilde{R}(1)^{2}-\int_{0}^{1}\mathbb{P}(Z_{n}\geq\tilde{R}(\delta))d(\tilde{R}(\delta)^{2})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\tilde{R}(1)^{2}-\int_{0}^{1}\delta d(\tilde{R}(\delta)^{2})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\tilde{R}(1)^{2}+CK_{x}^{2}\left[\int_{0}^{1}\delta\left(\frac{d}{n^{2}\delta}+\frac{d^{4/q_{x}}}{\delta^{1+4/q_{x}}n^{2-4/q_{x}}}\right)d\delta\right]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle CK_{x}^{2}\left[\frac{d^{2}+d\log(2n)}{n^{2}}+\frac{d^{4/q_{x}}}{n^{2-4/q_{x}}}\right].

For the last inequality, the integral
\int_{0}^{1}1/\delta^{4/q_{x}}\,d\delta
needs to be finite, so the condition
q_{x}>4
is required. Consequently, we get

\bar{R}\leq CK_{x}^{2}\left[\frac{d}{n}+\frac{\sqrt{d\log(2n)}}{n}+\frac{d^{2/q_{x}}}{n^{1-2/q_{x}}}\right],

for some constant
C=C(q_{x})>0
. To apply (
73
), we let

R(\delta)=CK_{x}^{2}\left[\sqrt{\frac{d}{n}}+\frac{d^{2/q_{x}}}{\delta^{2/q_{x}}n^{1-2/q_{x}}}\right],

for some constant
C=C(q_{x})
such that
R(\delta)\geq\bar{R}^{1/2}\sigma_{\star}^{1/2}+\sqrt{2}\bar{R}
. The rest of the proof is analogous to the proof of (
68
).

Remark 10
.

The concentration of the sample Gram matrix requires control of the both upper tail and the lower tail. Under a moment assumption of Assumption
2.a
, not only the current bound in Proposition
22
but also the existing bounds in recent literature
[
Vershynin 2012
,
Mendelson and Paouris 2012
,
Srivastava and Vershynin 2013
,
Mendelson and Paouris 2014
,
Guédon et al. 2017
,
Tikhomirov 2018
]
requires an additional term compared to the optimal rate achieved under sub-Gaussian covariates (Assumption
2.b
). Interestingly, this discrepancy is responsible for the upper tail behavior of
\widehat{\Sigma}
as
Oliveira 2016
showed that a lower tail is defacto sub-Gaussian under a simple fourth-moment assumption, which is presented in Proposition
24
. The asymmetry between upper and lower tails may be of independent interest.

Tropp et al. 2015
applies and yields

\mathbb{P}\left(\|G-I_{d}\|_{\rm op}\geq t\right)\leq 2d\exp\left(-\frac{t^{2}}{2\sigma_{\star}^{2}}\right)\mbox{ for all }t\geq 0,\quad\mathbb{E}\|G-I_{d}\|_{\rm op}\leq\sqrt{2\sigma_{\star}^{2}\log(2d)},

(77)

where
\sigma_{\star}^{2}=\|\mathbb{E}[(G-\mathbb{E}G)(G-\mathbb{E}G)^{\top}]\|_{\rm op}
. Since
{\rm Cov}(G)={\rm Cov}(\widehat{\Sigma})
, the matrix parameter
\sigma_{\star}^{2}
matches the one defined in (
B.3
). That is,
\sigma_{\star}^{2}=\|\mathbb{E}[(\Sigma^{-1/2}\widehat{\Sigma}\Sigma^{1/2}-I_{d})^{2}]\|_{\rm op}
, and is shown to be bounded as

\sigma_{\star}^{2}\leq\frac{K_{x}^{4}d}{n}.

Combining this with (
77
) yields the desired result.
∎

Proposition 26
.

Suppose that Assumption
1
,
2.a
, and
3
holds such that
s=(1/q_{x}+1/q)^{-1}>2
. Then, there exists a constant
C_{s}
which only depends on
s
such that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\left\|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Sigma^{-1/2}X_{i}(Y_{i}-X_{i}^{\top}\beta)\right\|_{2}\geq 2\sqrt{\frac{d+\log(2/\delta)}{n\overline{\lambda}}}+C_{s}K_{x}K_{y}\frac{d^{1/2}}{\delta^{1/s}n^{1-1/s}}\right)\leq\delta,

for all
\delta\in(0,1)
.

proof of Proposition
26
.

This proposition has been demonstrated in
Kuchibhotla et al. 2020
, but we aim to furnish a self-contained proof for the sake of completeness and clarity. We let

W_{i}:=\Sigma^{-1/2}X_{i}(Y_{i}-X_{i}^{\top}\beta).

Since
\beta
is the projection parameter, we have
\mathbb{E}W_{i}=0_{d}
for
1\leq i\leq n
. An application of Theorem 4 of
Einmahl and Li 2008
with
\eta=\delta=1
yields

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\left\|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}W_{i}\right\|_{2}\geq 2\mathbb{E}\left\|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}W_{i}\right\|_{2}+t\right)\leq\exp\left(-\frac{n\overline{\lambda}t^{2}}{3}\right)+\frac{C_{s}}{(nt)^{s}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}\|W_{i}\|_{2}^{s},

(78)

for some constant
C_{s}
that only depends on
s
. Jensen’s inequality yields

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left\|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}W_{i}\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left(\mathbb{E}\left\|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}W_{i}\right\|_{2}^{2}\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left[{\rm tr}\left\{{\rm Var}\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}W_{i}\right)\right\}\right]^{1/2}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\left\{{\rm tr}\left(\Sigma^{-1/2}V\Sigma^{-1/2}\right)\right\}^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sqrt{\frac{d}{\overline{\lambda}^{1/2}n}},

where the last inequality follows from Assumption
3
. Now,
\mathbb{E}\|W_{i}\|_{2}^{s}
is controlled as

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\|W_{i}\|_{2}^{s}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\sum_{j=1}^{d}(e_{j}^{\top}\Sigma^{-1/2}X_{i})^{2}(Y_{i}-X_{i}^{\top}\beta)^{2}\right)^{s/2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle d^{s/2}\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{d}\sum_{j=1}^{d}\left|(e_{j}^{\top}\Sigma^{-1/2}X_{i})(Y_{i}-X_{i}^{\top}\beta)\right|^{s}\right]\quad\mbox{(Jensen's inequality)}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle d^{s/2}\max_{j\in[d]}\mathbb{E}\left|(e_{j}^{\top}\Sigma^{-1/2}X_{i})(Y_{i}-X_{i}^{\top}\beta)\right|^{s}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle d^{s/2}\max_{j\in[d]}\left[\mathbb{E}(e_{j}^{\top}\Sigma^{-1/2}X_{i})^{q_{x}}\right]^{s/q_{x}}\left[\mathbb{E}(Y_{i}-X_{i}^{\top}\beta)^{q}\right]^{s/q}\quad\mbox{(H\"{o}lder's inequality)}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle d^{s/2}(K_{x}K_{y})^{s}.

The last inequality is due to Assumption
1
and
2.a
. Taking

\displaystyle t=\sqrt{\frac{3\log(2/\delta)}{n\overline{\lambda}}}+C_{s}K_{x}K_{y}\frac{d^{1/2}}{\delta^{1/s}n^{1-1/s}},

in (
78
) yields the desired result.
∎

Proposition 27
.

Suppose that Assumption
1
,
2.a
, and
3
holds with
q_{x}\geq 4
and
s=(1/q_{x}+1/q)^{-1}>2
. Then, there exists a constant
C_{s}>0
, which only depends on
s
, such that for any
\delta\in(0,1)
, with probability at least
1-\delta_{1}-\delta_{2}
,

\displaystyle\|\widehat{\beta}-\beta\|_{\Sigma}\leq\left(1-9K_{x}\sqrt{\frac{d+2\log(2/\delta_{1})}{n}}\right)^{-1}_{+}\left[2\sqrt{\frac{d+\log(2/\delta_{2})}{n\overline{\lambda}}}+C_{s}K_{x}K_{y}\frac{d^{1/2}}{\delta_{2}^{1/s}n^{1-1/s}}\right].

Provided that
d+2\log(2n)\leq n/(18K_{x})^{2}
, taking
\delta_{1}=1/(2n)
and
\delta_{2}=1/(2n^{s/2-1})
yields that

\mathbb{P}\left(\|\widehat{\beta}-\beta\|_{\Sigma}\leq C\sqrt{\frac{d+\log(2n)}{n}}\right)\geq 1-\frac{1}{n^{\min\{1,s/2-1\}}},

for some constant
C=C(\overline{\lambda},q_{x},q,K_{x},K_{y})
.

proof of Proposition
27
.

We follow the proof of Proposition 26 of
Kuchibhotla et al. 2020
or Theorem 1.2 of
Oliveira 2016
. We note that

\Sigma^{1/2}(\widehat{\beta}-\beta)=\Sigma^{1/2}\widehat{\Sigma}^{-1}\Sigma^{-1/2}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Sigma^{-1/2}X_{i}(Y_{i}-X_{i}^{\top}\beta).

This implies that

\displaystyle\|\widehat{\beta}-\beta\|_{\Sigma}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left\|\Sigma^{1/2}\widehat{\Sigma}^{-1}\Sigma^{-1/2}\right\|_{\rm op}\left\|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Sigma^{-1/2}X_{i}(Y_{i}-X_{i}^{\top}\beta)\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\lambda_{\rm min}(\Sigma^{-1/2}\widehat{\Sigma}\Sigma^{-1/2})}\left\|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Sigma^{-1/2}X_{i}(Y_{i}-X_{i}^{\top}\beta)\right\|_{2}.

The quantities on the right-hand side are controlled using Proposition
24
and Proposition
26
, respectively.
∎

Appendix C
Auxilary Results

Lemma 28
.

Under the data generating process described in Section
5.2
, the projection parameter
\beta
is given by
\beta=3(1+2\rho^{2})\|\theta\|_{2}^{2}\theta+6(1-\rho^{2})\theta^{\odot 3}
.

Proof.

Let
X=(X(1),\ldots,X(d))^{\top}
,
Z=(Z(1),\ldots,Z(d))^{\top}
, and
W=(W(1),\ldots,W(d))^{\top}
, so that
X(j)=Z(j)W(j)
for
1\leq j\leq d
. We note that
\mathbb{E}XX^{\top}=I_{d}
since

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}X(1)^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}Z(1)^{2}\mathbb{E}W(1)^{2}=1\quad\mbox{ and }

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}X(1)X(2)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}Z(1)\mathbb{E}Z(2)\mathbb{E}W(1)W(2)=0.

Consequently, the projection parameter is
\beta=\mathbb{E}XY
. Write
\theta=(\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{d})^{\top}
, and the
i
th coordinate
\beta(i)
of
\beta
can be written as

\displaystyle\beta(i)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}X(i)(X^{\top}\theta)^{3}+\mathbb{E}X(i)\epsilon

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}X(i)\left(\sum_{j=1}^{d}\theta_{j}X(j)\right)^{3},

where the second equality follows from the independence of
\epsilon
and
X
. Unfolding the last expression, we get

\displaystyle\beta(i)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[X(i)\left\{\sum_{j=1}^{d}\theta_{j}^{3}X(j)^{3}+3\sum_{j\neq k}\theta_{j}^{2}\theta_{k}X(j)^{2}X(k)^{2}+6\sum_{j<k<l}\theta_{j}\theta_{k}\theta_{l}X(j)X(k)X(l)\right\}\right].

Each individual term on the right-hand side can be simply computed using the following moments;

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}X(1)^{4}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}Z(1)^{4}\mathbb{E}W(1)^{4}=9,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}X(1)^{3}X(2)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}Z(1)^{3}\mathbb{E}Z(2)\mathbb{E}W(1)^{3}W(2)=0,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}X(1)^{2}X(2)^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}Z(1)^{2}\mathbb{E}Z(2)^{2}\mathbb{E}W(1)^{2}W(2)^{2}=1+2\rho^{2},

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}X(1)^{2}X(2)X(3)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}Z(1)^{2}\mathbb{E}Z(2)\mathbb{E}Z(3)\mathbb{E}W(1)^{2}W(2)W(3)=0,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}X(1)X(2)X(3)X(4)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}Z(1)\mathbb{E}Z(2)\mathbb{E}Z(3)\mathbb{E}Z(4)\mathbb{E}W(1)W(2)W(3)W(4)=0.

This leads to that

\displaystyle\beta(i)=9\theta_{i}^{3}+3(1+2\rho^{2})\theta_{i}\sum_{j\neq i}\theta_{j}^{2}=(6-6\rho^{2})\theta_{i}^{3}+3(1+2\rho^{2})\|\theta\|_{2}^{2}\theta_{i}.

This completes the proof.
∎
