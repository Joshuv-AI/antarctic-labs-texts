Beating ( 1 − 1 / e ) (1-1/e) -Approximation for Weighted Stochastic Matching
Mahsa Derakhshan, Alireza Farhadi

Abstract




In the
stochastic weighted matching
problem, the goal is to find a large-weight matching of a graph when we are uncertain about the existence of its edges. In particular, each edge
e
has a known weight
w_{e}
but is realized independently with some probability
p_{e}
. The algorithm may
query
an edge to see whether it is realized. We consider the well-studied
query commit
version of the problem, in which any queried edge that happens to be realized must be included in the solution.

Gamlath, Kale, and Svensson [SODA’19] showed that when the input graph is bipartite, the problem admits a
(1-1/e)
-approximation. In this paper, we give an algorithm that for an absolute constant
\delta>0.0014
obtains a
(1-1/e+\delta)
-approximation, therefore breaking this prevalent bound.

Introduction

Matching under uncertainty is a well-studied topic in theoretical computer science, primarily due to applications in matching markets such as kidney exchange, labor markets, online advertising, etc. (See
[
24
,
13
,
5
,
22
]
and the references within.) In this paper, we study the max-weight
stochastic matching
problem on bipartite graphs in the
query commit
model. We are given a bipartite graph
G=(A\cup B,E)
along with a weight
w_{e}
and a parameter
p_{e}\in(0,1]
for any edge
e\in E
. Each edge of this graph is realized (or exists) independently, with a known probability
p_{e}
forming the realized subgraph
G_{p}
. We are unaware of the realization of the edges but can access them via queries. Our goal is to find a large-weight matching of
G_{p}
in the query commit model in which any edge joins the matching if and only if it is queried and realized. In other words, to add an edge to the matching, we have to ensure that it exists; thus, we have to query it. Moreover, if a queried edge is realized, we have to add it to our matching (i.e., commit to it). This also implies that if an edge has at least one matched end-point it cannot be queried.

In this paper, we focus on designing an approximation algorithm for the above problem, where the approximation ratio is defined with respect to the max-weight matching found by an algorithm that knows the realization of all the edges. For this problem, a
0.5
-approximation can be achieved via the greedy matching algorithm by simply iterating over the edges in the decreasing order of their weights and querying any edge with unmatched end-points. The first non-trivial algorithm for this problem designed by Gamlath, Kale, and Svensson
[
15
]
achieves a
(1-1/e)
approximation ratio, a very familiar bound that holds for many matching algorithms in various settings (e.g.,
[
16
,
9
,
24
,
23
,
16
]
). It is also known that
(1-1/e)
is the best approximation ratio achievable by Gamlath et al.’s algorithm
[
23
]
, which remains to be the state of the art for this problem. In this paper, we provide a novel algorithm, breaking this
(1-1/e)
barrier.

1.1
Our Techniques

In this section, we will give a brief overview of our ideas and techniques which allow us to design an algorithm with an approximation factor larger than
(1-1/e)
. Our algorithm is LP-based. We start with an LP which we borrow from Gamlath et al.
[
15
]
. This LP gives us an upper-bound for the optimal solution of the problem. We then design a rounding procedure that given the optimal solution of this LP, decides which edges to query and in which order. In this section, we first describe the LP. We then briefly explain how Gamlath et al. round the solution of this LP in order to find a
(1-1/e)
-approximate matching. Finally, we discuss our rounding procedure which results in beating the
(1-1/e)
-approximation.

The LP.

We will use an LP designed by Gamlath et al. The LP has a variable
x_{e}
for any edge, and its solution forms a fractional matching. Besides the natural matching constraints, the LP also has a special constraint for any subset of edges sharing an end-point to ensure that the probability of at least one of them being realized is not smaller than the total fraction of matching on them (Constraint (
1
) below). The LP clearly has exponentially many constraints, but Gamlath et al. show that it is polynomially solvable using the ellipsoid method. Below we provide the formulation of this LP in which
E_{u}
denotes the subset of edges that have
u
as one of their end-points.

\displaystyle\max_{\mathbf{x}}\qquad

\displaystyle\bm{\mathrm{x}}\cdot\bm{\mathrm{w}}\,

s.t.

\displaystyle\sum_{e\in F}x_{e}\leq\Pr[\text{an edge in $F$ exists}]

\displaystyle\forall u\in A\cup B,\forall F\subseteq E_{u}

(1)

\displaystyle x_{e}\geq 0

\displaystyle\forall e\in E

(2)

Now, let us briefly explain the
(1-1/e)
-approximation algorithm of Gamlath et al.
[
15
]
. In the rest of the paper, we will refer to this algorithm as
GKS
(The authors’ initials).

Gamlath et al.’s rounding procedure.

Based on the solution of the LP, they draw a random ordering over the edges satisfying some special properties. They then iterate over the edges in this order and decide whether to query them and subsequently add them to the matching or lose them forever. The query decisions are made dynamically and they consist of each vertex in part
B
of the graph running a prophet-secretary based algorithm
[
12
]
to decide which one of its edges should be queried. Due to making irrevocable query decisions, the algorithm may choose not to query some of the edges while both their end-points are unmatched at the end of the algorithm.

To prove that
GKS
finds a a
(1-1/e)
-approximate matching, they show that the expected weight of the matching edge connected to any vertex in set
u\in B
is at least
(1-1/e)
fraction of the total contribution of its edges to the optimal solution of the LP. That is

\sum_{e\in E_{u}}\Pr[\textnormal{{GKS}}\text{ adds }e\text{ to the matching}]\cdot w_{e}\geq(1-1/e)\sum_{e\in E_{u}}x_{e}w_{e}.

Later, Fu et al.
[
14
]
observe that replacing the prophet-secretary based approch with an OCRS algorithm makes it possible for each edge to join the matching with probability at least
(1-1/e)x_{e}
.

Our rounding procedure.

Our rounding procedure consists of two steps. We first design a rounding procedure, which, given the optimal solution of the LP, returns a
(1-1/e)
-approximate matching and prove some new desirable properties for it. We call this procedure
BaseMatching
. We then use this as a subroutine in our final algorithm which beats
(1-1/e)
-approximation. Below are the four important properties.

(P1)

For any vertex
u\in B
, let
\sigma_{u}
denote the probability with which this vertex is matched in the optimal solution of the LP. That is
\sigma_{u}=\sum_{e\ni u}x_{e}
. One of the desirable properties of
BaseMatching
is that if
\sigma_{u}
is smaller than one by a constant, then its edges will have a higher chance of joining the matching in comparison to the case of
\sigma_{u}=1
(by a constant fraction). As a result of this property, if
\sigma_{u}
is small enough for all the vertices in
u\in B
,
BaseMatching
itself achieves an approximation ratio larger than
(1-1/e)
.

(P2)

After running
BaseMatching
, any vertex of the graph remains unmatched with a constant probability. Similarly, any edge does not join the matching with a probability of at least
\Omega(x_{e})
. We will later explain why this property is helpful.

(P3)

For any edge
e=(v,u)
, we want to ensure that conditioned on that
e
is not queried by
BaseMatching
, there is a constant probability of both its end-points being unmatched. Property
(P2)
already implies that
u
and
v
remain unmatched with a large probability. This property, however, is about the correlation between these events and is the most challenging one to prove.
We will use this property to show that for some instances of the problem running
BaseMatching
for a second time on the remaining edges results in an improved approximation ratio.

In order to achieve these properties,
BaseMatching
takes the optimal solution of the LP, makes several changes to it and then feeds it to a modified version of
GKS
(which assumes all the edges have the same weight). One of these changes relies on a transformation function. This function takes
\bm{\mathrm{x}}
, the optimal solution of the LP and outputs
\tilde{\bm{\mathrm{x}}}
which is also a solution of the LP. For any edge
e
, we set
\tilde{x}_{e}=g(x_{e},\sigma)
where
\sigma
is a (carefully picked) constant no smaller than
\max_{u\in B}\sum_{e\ni u}x_{e}
, and

g(x,\sigma)=\frac{(e^{\sigma}-1)(\sigma-x)x}{\sigma(e^{\sigma}-e^{x})}.

It is not hard to see that for any
x_{e}
, this transformation results in
x_{e}\geq\tilde{x}_{e}
, therefore
\tilde{\bm{\mathrm{x}}}
will still be a valid solution of the LP. See Appendix
B
for more details about this function and the ideas behind choosing it.

We will use properties
(P2)
and
(P3)
to prove that after running
BaseMatching
, any edge
e
with a small enough
\tilde{x}_{e}/p_{e}
(smaller than a constant) remains
available
with a constant probability. We say an edge is available if it is not queried and its end-points are unmatched. Roughly speaking, for any edge
e
satisfying this constraint, property
(P2)
of
BaseMatching
implies that the edge is not queried with a constant probability and any of its end-points are also unmatched with a constant probability. The challenging part, however, is to prove that despite potential correlations, the probability of all three events happening together is still a constant. Therefore, the edge remains available with a constant probability. We use this feature of
BaseMatching
in a crucial way in order to break the
(1-1/e)
approximation ratio. The reason being that if edges with a small enough
\tilde{x}_{e}/p_{e}
constitute a constant fraction of the optimal solution of the LP, then we can run
BaseMatching
for a second time on the remaining available edges and use its output to enhance the matching.

Beating
(1-1/e)
-approximation

Now, let us briefly explain how we use the aforementioned properties of
BaseMatching
to design another algorithm with an improved approximation ratio. We claim that for any instance of the problem, rounding the optimal solution of the LP in at least one of the two following ways results in an improved approximation ratio.

(i)

simply run
BaseMatching
once on the whole graph and once on the remaining available edges.

(ii)

Prune the graph by deleting any edge with
\tilde{x}_{e}/p_{e}<\tau
for a carefully picked threshold
\tau
(which is a constant smaller than one), and then run
BaseMatching
on the remaining subgraph.

As we already discussed, we prove that edges with
\tilde{x}_{e}/p_{e}<\tau
will remain available with a constant probability after we run
BaseMatching
. Therefore, if at least a constant fraction of the optimal solution of the LP comes from these edges, then the first rounding achieves an improved approximation ratio. On the flip side, if these edges do not have a large contribution, by deleting them we are left with a solution of the LP which is close to optimal. We now claim that simply running
BaseMatching
on the remaining subgraph gives us a matching with an improved approximation ratio. Roughly speaking, we are able to take advantage of constraint (
1
) of the LP, in order to show the following for any vertex
u
: The sum of
{x}_{e}
of edges connected to
u
that satisfy
\tilde{x}_{e}/p_{e}\geq\tau
are upper-bounded by a small enough constant. This implies that in the remaining subgraph,
\sigma_{u}
of all the vertices in bounded away from one by a constant. Invoking property
(P1)
of
BaseMatching
, implies that simply running it on this remaining subgraph results in an improved approximation ratio. Using constraint (
1
) of the LP in this way is a technically challenging part of the paper which we discuss in Section
6
.

A problem, closely connected to the one studied in this paper, is the

problem first introduced by Karp, Vazirani, and Vazirani
[

Whenever an online vertex arrives, its edges are revealed, and the algorithm has to decide whether to match the vertex with an unmatched neighbor, irrevocably, or lose it forever. For this problem, Karp et al. present their celebrated ranking algorithm which achieves a tight

Our paper also relates to a line of work initiated by Blum et al.
[
8

which concerns the query complexity of the stochastic matching problem. In this model, the goal is to find a large matching of the graph while having only a constant number of queries per vertex. Here, the matching does not have to commit to the queried edges that are realized. After a long line of work on this problem, (See

Another model which is closely related to the one considered in this paper, is the price of information model (POI). It was first introduced by Singla

Preliminaries

We consider the stochastic matching problem on weighted bipartite graphs in the query-commit model. Our input is a bipartite graph
G=(A\cup B,E)
, a vector of weights
\bm{\mathrm{x}}=(x_{e})_{e\in E}
, and vector of probabilities
\bm{\mathrm{p}}=(p_{e})_{e\in E}
. Each edge exists or is realized with probability
p_{e}
forming subgraph
G_{\textbf{p}}
. We refer to
G_{\textbf{p}}
as the realized subgraph. We do not know which edges are realized but can access them via queries. We can query any edges
e\in E
to see whether it exists or not, and if it does, we must add it to the matching irrevocably. In other words, our matching must commit to the edge. Our goal is to design a polynomial-time algorithm which finds a matching
\mathcal{M}
of
G_{\textbf{p}}
with a large approximation ratio. The approximation ratio of the algorithm is defined as

\frac{\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{W}(\mathcal{M})]}{\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{W}(\textsc{OPT})]},

where
OPT
is the maximum weight matching of
G_{\textbf{p}}
(i.e., the optimal solution) and
\mathcal{W}(.)
is a function outputting the weight of a given matching. The expectation here is taken over both the randomization in the algorithm and realization of edges.

General notations.

For any
v\in A\cup B
we will use
E_{v}
to denote the subset of edges that have
v
as one of their end-points. Unless otherwise stated, we will refer to vertices of
B
using the letter
u
and vertices of
A
using the letter
v
. Moreover, we will write our edges as ordered pairs, meaning that for any edge
(v,u)\in E
we always have
v\in A
and
u\in B
.

Organization of the paper.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. In Section
3
we explain the LP which we borrow from Gamlath et al. This LP gives us an upper-bound on the optimal solution of the LP. In Section
4
, we first design
BaseMatching
, a rounding procedure which given the optimal solution of the LP outputs a
(1-1/e)
-approximate matching. Later in the section, we design
APXMatching
which uses
BaseMatching
to find a matching with an approximation ratio of
(1-1/e+0.0014)
. In Section
5
, we prove some desirable properties of our
BaseMatching
, and finally, in Section
6
we use these properties to analyze the approximation ratio of our algorithm.

The Rounding Procedure

Our rounding procedure consists of two parts. First is an algorithm which outputs a
(1-1/e)

Before presenting

Definition 4.1

(Examining an edge
e

.

We examine an edge to determine whether it is realized or not. If both its end-points are unmatched, examining
e

At some points in our algorithm, we also need to use the information about realization of an edge who has at least one matched end-point. In this scenario, we cannot query the edge because it cannot join the matching. Instead, we just toss a coin which with probability
p_{e}
decides that the edge is realized. In such cases, it is guaranteed that the edge will not join the matching.

runs an OCRS algorithm to decide which proposal it accepts, it is possible ensure that each edge joins the matching with probability at least
(1-1/e)x_{e}

Let us emphasize that this is a rephrased version of these algorithms for the sake of comparison with ours. Of course, to describe their algorithm using our language, we need to change what examining an edge means, but we will not go into more details here.

4.1
Our
(1-1/e)
-approximation Algorithm

Let us start by explaining the desirable properties that we are trying to achieve by designing a new
(1-1/e)
-approximate matching below. We call this algorithm
BaseMatching
. We first define

\sigma=\max_{u\in B}\sum_{e\in E_{u}}x_{e}.

These properties as follows. (We have briefly discussed these properties in Section
1.1
.)

(P1)

The probability of any edge joining the matching is at least
(1-1/e)x_{e}
and is also a decreasing function of
\sigma
. In other words, if
\sigma
is smaller than one by a constant, we want
BaseMatching
to match any edge with probability at least
(1-1/e+c)x_{e}
, where
c
is a constant. This implies that in this scenario,
BaseMatching
itself achieves an approximation ratio larger than
(1-1/e)
.

(P2)

The algorithm ensures that any vertex of the graph remains unmatched with a constant probability. Similarly, any edge does not join the matching with probability at least
\Omega(x_{e})
.
SimpleMatching
does not satisfy this property as it may match some of the edges and vertices with probability one. This property allows us to show that for some instances of the problem, running
BaseMatching
twice gives us an improved algorithm. That is, we once run
BaseMatching
on
G
and once on the edges that are not queried and have unmatched end-points after the first run.

(P3)

For any edge
e=(v,u)
, we want
BaseMatching
to ensure that conditioned on any edge
e
not being examined, there is a constant probability of both its end-points being unmatched. This, again is helpful in arguing that for instances of the problem running
BaseMatching
twice gives us an improved algorithm. Property
(P2)
already implies that all the vertices remain unmatched with a large probability. It can also be used to show that some particular edges remain unexamined with a constant probability. This property, however, is about the correlation between these events.

Algorithm
BaseMatching
is very similar to
SimpleMatching
with three main differences. The first one which allows us to satisfy property
(P1)
is due to a transformation function. Given the optimal solution of the LP, we transform it to a different solution which still satisfies all the LP constraints. We then give this as an input to a modified version of
SimpleMatching
.

To pick our transformation function we start by observing that if all the edges connected to a vertex
u\in B
have the same
x_{e}
and
x_{e}\rightarrow 0
, then in
SimpleMatching
each edge joins the matching with a probability that satisfies this property. Since the algorithm draws a uniform permutation over vertices in
A
, all of
u
’s edges have the same probability of joining the matching and that is

\displaystyle\frac{\Pr[u\text{ joins the matching}]}{|E_{u}|}=\frac{1-(1-x_{e})^{|E_{u}|}}{|E_{u}|}=\frac{1-(1-x_{e})^{x_{u}/x_{e}}}{x_{u}/x_{e}}=\frac{(1-e^{-x_{u}})}{x_{u}}\cdot x_{e}.

Note that here
\frac{(1-e^{-x_{u}})}{x_{u}}
is a decreasing function of
x_{u}
and for
x_{u}=1
it is exactly equal to
(1-1/e)
. Therefore, it satisfies property
(P1)
. Roughly speaking, based on this observation, we aim to modify the solution of the LP in a way that each edge
e=(v,u)
behaves as if instead of a single edge it was a collection of edges
e^{\prime}_{1}\dots e^{\prime}_{k}
with

and
\sum_{i=1}^{k}x_{e^{\prime}}=x_{e}
. Of course, we cannot simply replace
e
with
k
parallel edges between

and

and hope to achieve the desired bound since these edges all being representatives of a single edge cannot behave independently. However, we show that lowering
x_{e}
to

defined as follows gives us a similar effect.

Let us denote the modified solution by
{\tilde{\bm{\mathrm{x}}}}
. Assume that for every

We then set
\tilde{x}_{e}=g(x_{e},\sigma)
. When value of

is clear from the context we will simply use

instead of

Now, let us give some intuition about the second difference between algorithms

4.2

Now we are ready to discuss how we use

\displaystyle=\Pr[e\text{ is proposed}]\sum_{i=0}^{k}\Pr[\text{There are }i\text{ edges before }e\text{ in }\pi\wedge\text{none of them are proposed}]

\displaystyle\geq g(x_{e})\times\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sum_{i=0}^{k}\frac{1}{k+1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-g\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\delta/k}}\right)}}\right)^{i}}}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{g(x_{e})}{k}\times\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sum_{i=0}^{k}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\delta/k}}\right)}}\right)^{i}}}\right)

\displaystyle=g(x_{e})\times\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sum_{i=0}^{k}\frac{e^{-i\delta/k}}{k}}}\right),

where the third equality is due to
\lim_{x\rightarrow 0}g(x)=x
.
Since
k\rightarrow\infty
, we can replace the above summation with an integration as follows:

\displaystyle\sum_{i=0}^{k}\frac{e^{-i\delta/k}}{k}=\int_{0}^{1}e^{-y\delta}dy=\frac{1-e^{-\delta}}{\delta}=\frac{1-e^{-\sigma+x_{e}}}{\sigma-x_{e}}.

(3)

This gives us:

\displaystyle\Pr[e\in\mathcal{M}_{1}]

\displaystyle\geq\frac{(e^{\sigma}-1)(\sigma-x_{e})x_{e}}{\sigma(e^{\sigma}-e^{x_{e}})}\times\frac{1-e^{-\sigma+x_{e}}}{\sigma-x_{e}}

\displaystyle=\frac{x_{e}}{\sigma}\times\frac{(e^{\sigma}-1)(1-e^{-\sigma+x_{e}})}{e^{\sigma}-e^{x_{e}}}

\displaystyle=\frac{x_{e}}{\sigma}\times\frac{e^{\sigma}-e^{x_{e}}-1+e^{-\sigma+x_{e}}}{e^{\sigma}-e^{x_{e}}}

\displaystyle=\frac{x_{e}}{\sigma}\times\frac{(e^{\sigma}-e^{x_{e}})(1-e^{-\sigma})}{e^{\sigma}-e^{x_{e}}}

\displaystyle=\frac{x_{e}(1-e^{-\sigma})}{\sigma}

This completes the proof of our desired lower-bound.

Upper-bound:

Now, we proceed to prove the following upper-bound:

\Pr[e\in\mathcal{M}_{1}]\leq\frac{1}{2}(1+e^{\sigma})x_{e}.

Recall that we have already proved that this probability is maximized when
u
is only incident to one other edge
e^{\prime}
with
x_{e^{\prime}}=\sigma-x_{e}
. Hence, we have

\displaystyle\Pr\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[e\in\mathcal{M}_{1}}}\right]

\displaystyle=\Pr[e\text{ is proposed}]\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\Pr[\pi(e)<\pi(e^{\prime})]+\Pr[\pi(e)>\pi(e^{\prime})\wedge e^{\prime}\text{ is not proposed}]}}\right)

\displaystyle=g(x_{e})\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(0.5+0.5(1-g(x_{e^{\prime}})}}\right)

\displaystyle=g(x_{e})\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-0.5g(x_{e^{\prime}})}}\right)

Note that
g(x)/x=\frac{(e^{\sigma}-1)(\sigma-x)}{\sigma(e^{\sigma}-e^{x})}
is a decreasing function of
x
. As such,

\displaystyle g(x_{e})/x_{e}\leq\lim_{x\rightarrow 0}(g(x)/x)=\lim_{x\rightarrow 0}\frac{(e^{\sigma}-1)(\sigma-x)}{\sigma(e^{\sigma}-e^{x})}=\frac{(e^{\sigma}-1)\sigma}{\sigma(e^{\sigma}-1)}=1.

Moreover, combining this with the fact that
\lim_{x\rightarrow\sigma}(\sigma-x)/(e^{\sigma}-e^{x})=e^{-\sigma},
we get

g(x_{e^{\prime}})/x_{e^{\prime}}\geq\lim_{x\rightarrow\sigma}(g(x)/x)=\lim_{x\rightarrow\sigma}\frac{(e^{\sigma}-1)(\sigma-x)}{\sigma(e^{\sigma}-e^{x})}=\frac{(e^{\sigma}-1)e^{-\sigma}}{\sigma}=\frac{1-e^{-\sigma}}{\sigma}.

As a result, we have

\displaystyle\Pr\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[e\in\mathcal{M}_{1}}}\right]

\displaystyle=g(x_{e})\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-0.5g(x_{e^{\prime}})}}\right)

\displaystyle=x_{e}\frac{g(x_{e})}{x_{e}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-0.5x_{e^{\prime}}\frac{g(\sigma-x_{e^{\prime}})}{\sigma-x_{e^{\prime}}}}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq x_{e}(1-0.5x_{e^{\prime}}\frac{1-e^{-\sigma}}{\sigma})

\displaystyle\leq x_{e}(1-0.5(\sigma-x_{e})\frac{1-e^{-\sigma}}{\sigma})

\displaystyle\leq x_{e}(1-0.5(1-e^{-\sigma}))

\displaystyle\leq x_{e}(0.5+0.5e^{-\sigma})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{x_{e}}{2}(1+e^{-\sigma}).

This proves our desired upper-bound and completes the proof of this lemma.
∎

Consider the LP of the problem, we show that there is a adaptive algorithm that given any feasible solution of the problem achieves a matching with the weight of at least

in expectation.

According to the lemma
