A unified analysis for reaction diffusion models with application to the spiral waves dynamics of the Barkley model
Yahya Alnashri, Hasan Alzubaidi

Abstract
. Applying the gradient discretisation method (GDM), the paper develops a comprehensive numerical analysis for the reaction diffusion model. Using only three properties, this analysis provides convergence results for several conforming and non-conforming numerical schemes that align with the GDM. As an application of this analysis, the hybrid mimetic mixed (HMM) method for the reaction diffusion model is designed and its convergence established. Numerical experiments using the HMM method are presented to facilitate study of the creation of spiral waves in the Barkley model and the ways in which the waves behave when interacting with the boundaries of their generating medium.

Some arrhythmias can have fatal consequences if they are permitted to remain in cardiac tissues without undergoing treatment

A number of scholars have examined the mathematical modelling approaches that are available and, following the application of these methods, they have concluded that cardiac arrhythmias can be traced back to the free rotation of spiral waves. These waves, reactivate an area of tissue at a higher frequency than that associated with the normal sinoatrial node and, thereby, generate a higher than average heartbeat

When fibrillation develops in the ventricles of the heart, it causes the heart to tremor and its strength diminishes. Lacking the ability to pump blood at a consistent rate, the heart ultimately suffers from a cardiac arrest

This paper’s main purpose is to use the gradient discretisation method (GDM) to offer a complete and unified convergence analysis of the reaction-diffusion model. To our knowledge, this analysis provides the first results that are applicable to several conforming and non conforming methods. The GDM is an abstract setting to study the numerical analysis for linear or non linear, steady or time–dependent diffusion partial differential equations (PDEs). Based on a limit number of properties, the GDM can establish convergence of numerical schemes for various models with different boundary conditions. Various studies have established that the GDM covers several families of numerical schemes: conforming, non conforming and mixed finite elements methods (including the non-conforming, Crouzeix Raviart method and the Raviart Thomas method), the discontinuous Galerkin scheme, the vertex approximate gradient (VAG) scheme, hybrid mimetic mixed methods (which contain hybrid mimetic finite differences, hybrid finite volumes/SUSHI scheme and mixed finite volumes), nodal mimetic finite differences, and finite volumes methods (such as some multi-points flux approximation and discrete duality finite volume methods). 3.
Discrete Problem

As stated in the introduction section, the analysis of numerical schemes for the approximation of solutions to the reaction diffusion model is performed using the gradient discretisation method. This method first requires the reconstruction of a set of discrete spaces and operators, which is called gradient discretisation (GD).

Definition 3.1

(gradient discretisation for reaction diffusion model)
.

Let
\Omega
be an open subset of
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
(with
d\geq 1
) and

{\mathcal{D}}=(X_{{\mathcal{D}}},Y_{\mathcal{D}},\Pi_{\mathcal{D}},\Pi_{{\mathcal{D}}^{{}^{\prime}}},\nabla_{\mathcal{D}},J_{\mathcal{D}},J_{{\mathcal{D}}^{{}^{\prime}}},(t^{(n)})_{n=0,...,N}))

•

the two set of discrete unknowns

and

are finite dimensional vector spaces on
{\mathbb{R}}
,

•

the function reconstruction

is a linear,

•

the function reconstruction

is a linear, and must be defined so that

•

•

Let us introduce some notations to define the space–time reconstructions

A consistency of the method (refers to an interplant error), which ensures the accuracy of approximating smooth functions and their gradients by elements defined from the discrete space, and the convergence of the time steps to zero.

is
h_{\sigma}
.

(3)

\mathcal{P}=(x_{K})_{K\in{\mathcal{M}}}
is a family of points of
\Omega
indexed by
{\mathcal{M}}
and such that, for all
K\in{\mathcal{M}}
,
x_{K}\in K
(

is sometimes called the “centre” of

).
We then assume that all cells
K\in{\mathcal{M}}
are strictly
x_{K}
-star-shaped, meaning that
if
x\in\overline{K}
then the line segment
[x_{K},x)
is included in
K
.

For a given
K\in{\mathcal{M}}
, let
\mathbf{n}_{K,\sigma}
be the unit vector normal to
\sigma
outward to
K

and denote by
d_{K,\sigma}
the orthogonal distance between
x_{K}
and
\sigma\in\mathcal{E}_{K}
.
The size of the discretisation is
h_{\mathcal{M}}=\sup\{h_{K}\,:\;K\in{\mathcal{M}}\}
.

Let
{\mathcal{T}}
be a polytopal mesh. A gradient discretisation
(X_{\mathcal{D}},Y_{\mathcal{D}},\Pi_{\mathcal{D}},\Pi_{{\mathcal{D}}^{{}^{\prime}}},\nabla_{\mathcal{D}},J_{\mathcal{D}},J_{{\mathcal{D}}^{{}^{\prime}}})
in the setting of HMM method formats are then constructed by setting

\displaystyle X_{{\mathcal{D}}}=\{\varphi=((\varphi_{K})_{K\in\mathcal{M}},(\varphi_{\sigma})_{\sigma\in\mathcal{E}})\;:\;\varphi_{K}\in{\mathbb{R}},\,\varphi_{\sigma}\in{\mathbb{R}}\},

\displaystyle Y_{{\mathcal{D}}}=\{\varphi=(\varphi_{K})_{K\in\mathcal{M}}\;:\;\varphi_{K}\in{\mathbb{R}}\},

\displaystyle\forall K\in\mathcal{M}\,:\,\Pi_{\mathcal{D}}\varphi=\Pi_{{\mathcal{D}}^{{}^{\prime}}}\varphi=\varphi_{K}\mbox{ on $K$},

\displaystyle\forall w\in L^{2}(\Omega)\;:\;J_{\mathcal{D}}w=((w_{K})_{K\in{\mathcal{M}}},(w_{\sigma})_{\sigma\in{\mathcal{E}}_{K}})\in X_{\mathcal{D}},

\displaystyle\forall w\in L^{\infty}(\Omega),\;J_{{\mathcal{D}}^{{}^{\prime}}}w=(w_{K})_{K\in{\mathcal{M}}}\in Y_{\mathcal{D}},

\displaystyle\mbox{where }w_{k}=\frac{1}{|K|}\int_{K}w(\boldsymbol{x})\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x}\mbox{ and }w_{\sigma}=0,

\displaystyle\forall\varphi\in X_{\mathcal{D}},\;\forall K\in\mathcal{M},\,\forall\sigma\in\mathcal{E}_{K},

\displaystyle\nabla_{\mathcal{D}}\varphi=\nabla_{K}\varphi+\frac{\sqrt{d}}{d_{K,\sigma}}R_{K}(\varphi)\mathbf{n}_{K,\sigma}\mbox{ on }D_{K,\sigma},

where where a cell–wise constant gradient
\nabla_{K}(\varphi)
and a stabilisation term
R_{K}(\varphi)
are respectively defined by:

\nabla_{K}\varphi=\frac{1}{|K|}\sum_{\sigma\in{{{\mathcal{E}}}_{K}}}|\sigma|\varphi_{\sigma}\mathbf{n}_{K,\sigma}\mbox{ and }R_{K}(\varphi)=(\varphi_{\sigma}-\varphi_{K}-\nabla_{K}\varphi\cdot(\overline{x}_{\sigma}-x_{K}))_{\sigma\in{\mathcal{E}}_{K}}.\\

The gradient scheme (
3.5
) coming from such a GD can be given by: find
(u^{(n)})_{n=0,...,N}\in X_{\mathcal{D}}^{N+1}
and
(v^{(n)})_{n=0,...,N}\in Y_{\mathcal{D}}^{N+1}
, such that
u^{(0)}=J_{\mathcal{D}}u_{\rm ini}
and
v^{(0)}=J_{{\mathcal{D}}^{{}^{\prime}}}v_{\rm ini}
, and for all
n=0,...,N-1
,
u^{(n+1)}
and
v^{(n+1)}
satisfy, for all
\varphi\in X_{\mathcal{D}}
and for all
\psi\in Y_{\mathcal{D}}
,

\left.\begin{aligned} &\displaystyle\sum_{K\in{\mathcal{M}}}\frac{|K|}{\delta t^{(n+\frac{1}{2})}}\Big(u_{K}^{(n+1)}-u_{K}^{(n)}\Big)\varphi_{K}+\displaystyle\sum_{K\in{\mathcal{M}}}|K|\Lambda_{K}\nabla_{K}u^{(n+1)}\cdot\nabla_{K}\varphi\\
&\quad+\displaystyle\sum_{K\in{\mathcal{M}}}(R_{K}\varphi)^{T}\mathbb{B}_{K}R_{K}(u^{(n+1)})=\displaystyle\sum_{K\in{\mathcal{M}}}\varphi_{K}\displaystyle\int_{K}f(u_{K}^{(n+1)},v_{K}^{(n+1)})\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x},\\
&\displaystyle\sum_{K\in{\mathcal{M}}}\frac{|K|}{\delta t^{(n+\frac{1}{2})}}\Big(v_{K}^{(n+1)}-v_{K}^{(n)}\Big)\psi_{K}=\displaystyle\sum_{K\in{\mathcal{M}}}\psi_{K}\displaystyle\int_{K}g(u_{K}^{(n+1)},v_{K}^{(n+1)})\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x},\end{aligned}\right.

(4.1)

where
\mathbb{B}_{K}
is a symmetric positive definite matrix of size
\mbox{Card}({\mathcal{E}}_{K})
.

The above HMM scheme can be formulated as a finite volume scheme (a format used in the computational processes). To do so, introduce the linear fluxes
u\mapsto F_{K,\sigma}(u)
(for
K\in{\mathcal{M}}
and
\sigma\in{\mathcal{E}}_{K}
) defined in
[
2
]
:
for all
K\in{\mathcal{M}}
and all

,

\displaystyle\sum_{\sigma\in\mathcal{E}_{K}}|\sigma|F_{K,\sigma}(u)(w_{K}-w_{\sigma})={}

\displaystyle\mu\int_{K}\nabla_{\mathcal{D}}u\cdot\nabla_{\mathcal{D}}w\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x}.

Then Problem (
4.1
) can be recast as, for all
n=0,...,N-1
,

\displaystyle\frac{|K|}{\delta t^{(n+\frac{1}{2})}}\Big(u^{(n+1)}-u^{(n)}\Big)

\displaystyle+\sum_{\sigma\in\mathcal{E}_{K}}|\sigma|F_{K,\sigma}(u^{(n+1)})=|K|f_{K}^{(n+1)},\quad\forall K\in{\mathcal{M}}

\displaystyle\frac{|K|}{\delta t^{(n+\frac{1}{2})}}\Big(v^{(n+1)}-v^{(n)}\Big)

\displaystyle=|K|g_{K}^{(n+1)},\quad\forall K\in{\mathcal{M}}

\displaystyle F_{K,\sigma}(u^{(n+1)})+F_{L,\sigma}(u^{(n+1)})

\displaystyle=0,\quad\forall\sigma\in\mathcal{E}_{\rm int}\mbox{ with }{\mathcal{M}}_{\sigma}=\{K,L\},

\displaystyle F_{K,\sigma}(u^{(n+1)})

\displaystyle=0,\quad\forall K\in{\mathcal{M}}\,,\forall\sigma\in\mathcal{E}_{K}\mbox{ such that }\sigma\subset\partial\Omega,

where

f_{K}^{(n+1)}=\frac{1}{|K|}\int_{K}f(u_{K}^{(n+1)},v_{K}^{(n+1)})\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x}
and
g_{K}^{(n+1)}=\frac{1}{|K|}\int_{K}g(u_{K}^{(n+1)},v_{K}^{(n+1)})\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x},\;\forall K\in{\mathcal{M}}
.

Now, let us discuss the convergence of the scheme (
4.1
). Assume the existence of
\theta>0
such that,
for any
m\in{\mathbb{N}}
,

\displaystyle{\theta_{\mathcal{M}}:=}\max_{K\in{\mathcal{M}}_{m}}

\displaystyle\left(\displaystyle\max_{\sigma\in{\mathcal{E}}_{K}}\frac{h_{K}}{d_{K,\sigma}}+{\rm Card}({\mathcal{E}}_{K})\right)+\max_{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\scriptsize$\sigma\in{\mathcal{E}}_{m,\rm int}$}}}{{{\mathcal{M}}_{\sigma}=\{K,L\}}}}\left(\frac{d_{K,\sigma}}{d_{L,\sigma}}+\frac{d_{L,\sigma}}{d_{K,\sigma}}\right)\leq\theta

(4.2)

and, for all
K\in{\mathcal{M}}_{m}
and
\gamma\in{\mathbb{R}}^{{\mathcal{E}}_{K}}
,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\theta}\displaystyle\sum_{\sigma\in{\mathcal{E}}_{K}}|D_{K,\sigma}|\left|\frac{R_{K,\sigma}(\gamma)}{d_{K,\sigma}}\right|^{2}\leq{}

\displaystyle\displaystyle\sum_{\sigma\in{\mathcal{E}}_{K}}|D_{K,\sigma}|\left|\frac{(A_{K}R_{K}(\gamma))_{\sigma}}{d_{K,\sigma}}\right|^{2}

(4.3)

\displaystyle\leq{}

\displaystyle\theta\displaystyle\sum_{\sigma\in{\mathcal{E}}_{K}}|D_{K,\sigma}|\left|\frac{R_{K,\sigma}(\gamma)}{d_{K,\sigma}}\right|^{2}.

Under the above boundedness assumptions on the mesh regularity parameter
\theta_{\mathcal{M}}
, the limit-conformity and the compactness properties defined in Section
3
directly follow from the results in
[
19
, Theorem 13.18]
. It remains to prove the consistency. Proving that, for all
\bar{\varphi}\in H^{1}(\Omega)
,
\lim_{m\to\infty}S_{{\mathcal{D}}_{m}}(\bar{\varphi})=0
is as in the case of the
HMM method for PDEs, see
[
19
, Theorem 4.14]
. Also, with the same manner, we can show that, for all

,
\widehat{S}_{{\mathcal{D}}_{m}}(\bar{\psi})\to 0
, as

. Let
\varphi_{m}=((\varphi_{K})_{K\in{\mathcal{M}}},(\varphi_{\sigma})_{\sigma\in{\mathcal{E}}})\in X_{{\mathcal{D}}_{m}}
and

be the two interplants such that

. Using
[

It is proved in

that, for

and

Corollary 5.2
.

Assume (
2.1
) and let
{\mathcal{D}}
be a gradient discretisation. Then there exists at least one solution
(u,v)
to the gradient scheme (
3.5
).

Proof.

At each time step
n+1
, (
3.5
) provides square non linear equations on

and

. For a given

is is the solution to

\displaystyle=\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}f(\Pi_{\mathcal{D}}w_{1},\Pi_{{\mathcal{D}}^{{}^{\prime}}}w_{2})\Pi_{\mathcal{D}}\varphi(\boldsymbol{x})\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x},\quad\forall\varphi\in X_{\mathcal{D}},

\displaystyle\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\Pi_{{\mathcal{D}}^{{}^{\prime}}}\displaystyle\frac{v^{(n+1)}-v^{(n)}}{\delta t^{(n+\frac{1}{2})}}\Pi_{{\mathcal{D}}^{{}^{\prime}}}\psi(\boldsymbol{x})=\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}g(\Pi_{\mathcal{D}}w_{1},\Pi_{{\mathcal{D}}^{{}^{\prime}}}w_{2})\Pi_{\mathcal{D}}\psi(\boldsymbol{x})\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x},\quad\forall\psi\in Y_{\mathcal{D}}.

\displaystyle||\Pi_{\mathcal{D}}u^{(n+1)}||_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+||\nabla_{\mathcal{D}}u^{(n+1)}||_{L^{2}(\Omega)^{d}}\leq C_{4}||f||_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+||\Pi_{{\mathcal{D}}}u^{(n)}||_{L^{2}(\Omega)},

where

and

not depending on

or

. Hence, the kernel of the matrix associated with the above linear square system is reduced to

, and the matrix is invertible. We then can define the mapping

by

is the solution to (

∎

6.1.
Dynamics of the Barkley model

A pair of essential features are shared by all reaction-diffusion models: spatially localized excitation that undergoes diffusion in space and quickly reverts to a recovery state. Precisely, if the stimulus is strong enough, an excitable system will switch from a state of rest to a state of excitement, then quickly revert to a refractory state and back to rest. A new excitement cannot be created until a certain period (refractory time) has gone by. So an excitable system has the ability to support the wave propagation caused by strong disturbances when in a rest state driven by local nonlinearity coupled with diffusion
[
10

6
