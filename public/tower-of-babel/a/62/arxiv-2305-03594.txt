Images of Gaussian and other stochastic processes under closed, densely-defined, unbounded linear operators
Tadashi Matsumoto, T. J. Sullivan ,

Abstract
Abstract. Gaussian processes (GPs) are widely-used tools in spatial statistics and machine learning and the formulae for the mean function and covariance kernel of a GP T ​ u Tu that is the image of another GP u u under a linear transformation T T acting on the sample paths of u u are well known, almost to the point of being folklore. However, these formulae are often used without rigorous attention to technical details, particularly when T T is an unbounded operator such as a differential operator, which is common in many modern applications. This note provides a self-contained proof of the claimed formulae for the case of a closed, densely-defined operator T T acting on the sample paths of a square-integrable (not necessarily Gaussian) stochastic process. Our proof technique relies upon Hille’s theorem for the Bochner integral of a Banach-valued random variable. Keywords. Introduction

It is a basic fact in multivariate probability and statistics that, if
u\sim\mathcal{N}(m,C)
is a normally-distributed
\mathbb{R}^{p}
-valued random variable with mean vector
m\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
and covariance matrix
C\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}
, and
T\in\mathbb{R}^{q\times p}
, then the
\mathbb{R}^{q}
-valued random variable
Tu
satisfies

Tu\sim\mathcal{N}(Tm,TCT^{\ast}),

(1.1)

where
T^{\ast}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times q}
denotes the transpose/adjoint of
T
.
There is a similar formula for the case of a Gaussian stochastic process (GP)
u\sim\mathcal{GP}(m,k)
on an index set
X
with mean function
m(x)\coloneqq\mathbb{E}[u(x)]
and covariance kernel
k(x_{1},x_{2})\coloneqq\mathbb{C}\mathrm{ov}[u(x_{1}),u(x_{2})]
:
if
T
is a deterministic linear operator acting on functions in
\mathbb{R}^{X}
, i.e. acting pathwise on sample realisations of
u
, then

Tu\sim\mathcal{GP}(Tm,T_{1}T_{2}k),

(1.2)

where
T_{j}
denotes the application of
T
to the
j
th
argument of a function of two or more variables.
Where we write
T_{1}T_{2}k
, alternative notations such as
TkT^{\ast}
,
TT^{\ast}k
, and
T\overline{T}k
are also commonly used in the literature.
As a concrete prototypical example, as per
Rasmussen and Williams 2006
, if
u\sim\mathcal{GP}(m,k)
is a GP on
X=\mathbb{R}
, then
\frac{\mathrm{d}u}{\mathrm{d}x}\sim\mathcal{GP}\bigl(\frac{\mathrm{d}m}{\mathrm{d}x},\frac{\partial^{2}k}{\partial x_{1}\partial x_{2}}\bigr)
, and indeed this special case can be justified in the sense of mean-square differentiability
(
Papoulis and Pillai 2002
, Theorem 9A-2)
.

However, upon close examination, formula (
1.2
) is not completely trivial.
One elementary question is whether the operators
T_{1}
and
T_{2}
commute, or at least whether
T_{1}T_{2}k=T_{2}T_{1}k
;
this issue is arguably obscured rather than solved by notations like
TkT^{\ast}
.
A more subtle question is whether (
1.2
) is valid for arbitrary linear operators
T
, and in particular for
unbounded
operators.
This note addresses both these points.

The unbounded case has present-day practical relevance.
Many recent applications of GPs, such as linearly- and algorithmically-constrained GPs
(
Jidling et al. 2017
;
Lange-Hegermann 2018
;
Lange-Hegermann 2021
;
Besginow and Lange-Hegermann 2022
)
, numerical and physics-informed GPs
(
Pang and Karniadakis 2020
;
Raissi et al. 2018
)
and probabilistic numerical methods for differential equations
(
Cockayne et al. 2017
;
Hennig et al. 2022
;
Oates et al. 2019
;
Pförtner et al. 2022
;
Särkkä 2011
)
, seek to apply (
1.2
) in the case that
T
is an ordinary or partial differential operator.
These applications typically then wish to form the conditional distribution of
u
given an observation of
Tu
— for which it is necessary to know that
Tu
is a GP and to know its mean and covariance structure.
However, in the main, these papers mention few requirements on
T
and none provides a proof of (
1.2
) in the general unbounded case.
Some, such as
Pförtner et al. 2022
and
Härkönen et al. 2023
, do state (
1.2
) as a theorem, but add the restriction that
T
must be a bounded/continuous operator between Banach/Fréchet spaces of functions.
In the case of a differential operator, this can only be achieved by carefully setting up the function spaces in an artificial and inflexible way — e.g. by making
T=\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}x}
a bounded operator from
\mathcal{C}^{1}([0,1];\mathbb{R})
into
\mathcal{C}^{0}([0,1];\mathbb{R})
with their usual norms — that complicates repeated application of
T
(as needed, e.g., for filtering and data assimilation).
Other authors, e.g.
Lange-Hegermann 2021
, give a proof for a measurable linear operator
T
that commutes with expectation, but neither stress the sense in which the expectation is meant nor give sufficient conditions for this commutativity.

This note aims to give a short, self-contained, rigorous proof of (
1.2
), treating
T
as an unbounded operator.
Our main result, which rests upon Hille’s theorem for the Bochner integral, is the following:

.

Let

be real Banach spaces, and let

be a closed operator.
Let

be a probability space, and let
u\colon\Omega\times X\to\mathbb{R}
be a stochastic process such that
u(\omega,\hbox to5.71527pt{\hss$\cdot$\hss})\in\dom(T)

\mathbb{P}
-a.s.
Let

be given
\mathbb{P}
-a.e. by
v(\omega,x)\coloneqq(Tu(\omega,\hbox to5.71527pt{\hss$\cdot$\hss}))(x)
.

(a)

(Mean function.)
Suppose that

and

have Bochner mean functions
m_{u}\in\mathcal{U}
and
m_{v}\in\mathcal{V}
, i.e.
\int_{\Omega}\lVert u(\omega,\hbox to5.71527pt{\hss$\cdot$\hss})\rVert_{\mathcal{U}}\,\mathbb{P}(\mathrm{d}\omega)

\int_{\Omega}\lVert v(\omega,\hbox to5.71527pt{\hss$\cdot$\hss})\rVert_{\mathcal{V}}\,\mathbb{P}(\mathrm{d}\omega)

.

(b)

(Covariance function.)
Suppose in addition to
(a)
that

and that

and

and

respectively, and

, where
T_{1}
(resp.
T_{2}
) denotes the densely-defined closure of the tensor product operator
T\otimes I
(resp.
I\otimes T
).

(c)

(Gaussianity.)
Suppose in addition to
(a)
and
(b)
that the linear span of all point evaluation functionals is weakly-
\ast
sequentially dense

That is, each

Remark 1.2

(Necessity of assumptions)
.

It is necessary for

Stochastic processes

Given a stochastic process

2.3
Closed operators

Thorough treatments of unbounded operators are offered by e.g.
Kato 1995
and
Reed and Simon 1980
, the latter considering only the Hilbert space case.

A linear operator
T\colon\dom(T)\subseteq\mathcal{U}\to\mathcal{V}
, defined on a linear subspace
\dom(T)
of
\mathcal{U}
, is a
closed operator
if, whenever
(u_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subset\dom(T)
is such that
(u_{n},Tu_{n})
converges to some
(u,v)
, it follows that
u\in\dom(T)
and
v=Tu
;
equivalently, the graph
\graph(T)\coloneqq\{(u,Tu)\mid u\in\dom(T)\}
of
T
is a closed subspace of the direct sum
\mathcal{U}\oplus\mathcal{V}
with its norm
\lVert(u,v)\rVert_{\mathcal{U}\oplus\mathcal{V}}\coloneqq\lVert u\rVert_{\mathcal{U}}+\lVert v\rVert_{\mathcal{V}}
.
An operator
T
is called
closable
if it can be extended to a closed operator
\overline{T}
, and
\graph(\overline{T})
is the closure of
\graph(T)
in
\mathcal{U}\oplus\mathcal{V}
.
Note well that closedness of
T
is unrelated to closedness of
\dom(T)
.

Any operator
T
induces a norm on
\dom(T)
via
\lVert u\rVert_{T}\coloneqq\lVert u\rVert_{\mathcal{U}}+\lVert Tu\rVert_{\mathcal{V}}
, and it is easy to verify that
T
is a bounded operator with at most unit operator norm from
(\dom(T),\lVert\hbox to5.71527pt{\hss$\cdot$\hss}\rVert_{T})
into
(\mathcal{V},\lVert\hbox to5.71527pt{\hss$\cdot$\hss}\rVert_{\mathcal{V}})
.
Whenever
\mathcal{U}
and
\mathcal{V}
are Banach and
T
is a closed operator,
\graph(T)
is a Banach subspace of
\mathcal{U}\oplus\mathcal{V}
and
(\dom(T),\lVert\hbox to5.71527pt{\hss$\cdot$\hss}\rVert_{T})
is also a Banach space.

This note’s prototypical example is in fact the standard example of a closed operator defined on a dense but proper subspace, and which cannot be extended to a bounded operator on the whole space:

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}x}\colon\dom(\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}x})\coloneqq\mathcal{C}^{1}([0,1];\mathbb{R})\subsetneq\mathcal{C}^{0}([0,1];\mathbb{R})\to\mathcal{C}^{0}([0,1];\mathbb{R})
.
A more involved example, involving a partial differential operator and the solution of an elliptic partial differential equation (PDE), is discussed in
Section

4
.

2.4
Bochner integration

We now recall the essential elements of Bochner integration of Banach-space-valued random variables;
for a comprehensive treatment, see e.g.
Diestel and Uhl 1977
.

For
E\in\Sigma
,
\mathds{1}_{E}\colon\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
denotes the
indicator function
of
E
given by

\mathds{1}_{E}(\omega)\coloneqq\begin{cases}1,&\text{if $\omega\in E$,}\\
0,&\text{if $\omega\notin E$.}\end{cases}

A
simple

\mathcal{U}
-valued random variable
s\colon\Omega\to\mathcal{U}
takes the form
s(\omega)=\sum_{j=1}^{J}u_{j}\mathds{1}_{E_{j}}(\omega)
for some
J\in\mathbb{N}
,
u_{j}\in\mathcal{U}
, and
E_{j}\in\Sigma
;
its
expected value
or

is

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[s]\equiv\int_{\Omega}s(\omega)\,\mathbb{P}(\mathrm{d}\omega)\coloneqq\sum_{j=1}^{J}u_{j}\mathbb{P}(E_{j})\in\mathcal{U}.

A random variable
u\colon\Omega\to\mathcal{U}
is called
strongly measurable
if there exist simple random variables

such that
\lim_{n\to\infty}s_{n}(\omega)=u(\omega)
for
\mathbb{P}
-a.e.
\omega\in\Omega
.
If also
\mathbb{E}[\lVert u-s_{n}\rVert_{\mathcal{U}}]\to 0
as
n\to\infty
, then
u
is called
Bochner integrable
and its
Bochner integral
is defined by

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[u]\equiv\int_{\Omega}u(\omega)\,\mathbb{P}(\mathrm{d}\omega)\coloneqq\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{E}[s_{n}]\in\mathcal{U}.

Equivalently,
u
is Bochner integrable if and only if it is strongly measurable and
\mathbb{E}[\lVert u\rVert_{\mathcal{U}}]
is finite.
If
\mathcal{U}
happens to be a separable space, or if
u
is essentially separably valued, then Pettis’ measurability theorem
(
Diestel and Uhl 1977
, Theorem II.1.2)
ensures that strong measurability coincides with Borel measurability and also with
weak measurability
, i.e. measurability of every
\langle\ell|u\rangle\colon\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
for
\ell\in\mathcal{U}^{\prime}
, which is often easier to verify in practice.

If
u\colon\Omega\to\mathcal{U}
is Bochner integrable and
T\colon\mathcal{U}\to\mathcal{V}
is a bounded operator with values in another Banach space
\mathcal{V}
, then it is easily verified
2
2

2

The key insight is that, if the operator norm
\lVert T\rVert_{\textup{op}}
is finite, then
\mathbb{E}[\lVert v\rVert_{\mathcal{V}}]\leqslant\lVert T\rVert_{\textup{op}}\mathbb{E}[\lVert u\rVert_{\mathcal{U}}]
.
that
v\coloneqq Tu
is Bochner integrable and
\mathbb{E}[v]=T\mathbb{E}[u]
.
Our interest, however, lies in unbounded operators, for which the following result will be essential:

Theorem 2.2

(Hille’s theorem;
Diestel and Uhl 1977
, Theorem II.2.6
)
.

Let
\mathcal{U}
and
\mathcal{V}
be real Banach spaces and let
T\colon\dom(T)\subseteq\mathcal{U}\to\mathcal{V}
be a closed operator.
If

and

are both Bochner integrable, then
\mathbb{E}[Tu]=T\mathbb{E}[u]\in\mathcal{V}
.

Note that, when

,

However, if

Remark 2.3

(TANSTAAFL!)
.

The hypotheses of Hille’s theorem are equivalent to asking that
u
be Bochner integrable with respect to
\lVert\hbox to5.71527pt{\hss$\cdot$\hss}\rVert_{T}
and that

be a Banach space, on which

is automatically a bounded
\mathcal{V}
-valued operator.
However, there is “no free lunch”:
either one works with the weaker norm
\lVert\hbox to5.71527pt{\hss$\cdot$\hss}\rVert_{\mathcal{U}}
at the cost of working with a closed, unbounded operator;
or one works with a
(\lVert\hbox to5.71527pt{\hss$\cdot$\hss}\rVert_{T},\lVert\hbox to5.71527pt{\hss$\cdot$\hss}\rVert_{\mathcal{V}})
-bounded operator at the cost of needing

to be a Banach norm, which holds only when
T
is closed anyway.

4
Application to GP-based solution of PDEs

As noted in
Sections

1
and
2.3
, the standard example of the framework discussed in this note is the derivative operator on a space of continuous paths on an interval in
\mathbb{R}
.
We now give a slightly more involved worked example, one that is representative of the application of the above results to the numerical solution of an elliptic PDE using a GP model of the kind common in spatial statistics and machine learning.
This
probabilistic meshless method
(PMM)
(
Cockayne et al. 2017
;
Oates et al. 2019
)
can be seen as a form of generalised kriging
(
Krige 1951
;
Matheron 1963
)
with derivative-based observations, and the posterior mean function (
4.2
) of the PMM coincides with the symmetric collocation method
(
Cialenco et al. 2012
;
Fasshauer 1999
)
.
The additional benefit of the GP representation is that the posterior covariance structure (
4.3
) gives a meaningful estimate of the discretisation uncertainty vis-à-vis the PDE solution
(
Hennig et al. 2022
)
.

Let
X\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a bounded, open domain with smooth boundary
\partial X
and compact closure
\overline{X}
.
Let
L
be a second-order linear differential operator of the form

\displaystyle Lu\coloneqq-\nabla\cdot(a\nabla u)+b\cdot\nabla u-\nabla\cdot(bu)+cu

with
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}
coefficient functions
a\colon\overline{X}\to\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
,
b\colon\overline{X}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and
c\colon\overline{X}\to\mathbb{R}
.
Assume that
a(x)
is symmetric for each
x\in X
, so that
L
is formally self-adjoint with respect to the usual
L^{2}
inner product, and assume also that the eigenvalues of
a(x)
are bounded below, uniformly in
x
, by
\lambda_{\min}>0
, so that
L
is uniformly elliptic.

In the PMM, we are interested in forming a posterior GP whose sample paths are approximate solutions to the PDE

\begin{cases}Lu=f&\text{in $X$,}\\
Bu=g&\text{on $\partial X$,}\end{cases}

(4.1)

for prescribed
f\colon X\to\mathbb{R}
and
g\colon\partial X\to\mathbb{R}
, where
B\colon u\mapsto u|_{\partial X}
denotes the Dirichlet boundary value / Sobolev trace operator.
The sense in which the GP paths will be approximate solutions, in the spirit of collocation methods
(
Cialenco et al. 2012
;
Fasshauer 1999
)
, is that the GP will be conditioned so that (
4.1
) holds on a finite set of points in
\overline{X}
.

For the next paragraph we reason somewhat informally.
Fix finite point sets
\boldsymbol{\xi}^{\circ}\coloneqq\{\xi_{i}^{\circ}\}_{i=1}^{I}\subset X
and
\boldsymbol{\xi}^{\partial}\coloneqq\{\xi_{j}^{\partial}\}_{j=1}^{J}\subset\partial X
, let
\boldsymbol{\xi}\coloneqq\boldsymbol{\xi}^{\circ}\cup\boldsymbol{\xi}^{\partial}
, and let

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{y}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\begin{bmatrix}\vphantom{\Big|}f(\xi_{1}^{\circ})&\cdots&f(\xi_{I}^{\circ})&g(\xi_{1}^{\partial})&\cdots&g(\xi_{J}^{\partial})\end{bmatrix}^{\intercal}\in\mathbb{R}^{(I+J)\times 1}.

After positing a centred GP prior
u\sim\mathcal{GP}(0,k)
, the PMM conditions this GP on the linear observation
(Lu(\boldsymbol{\xi}^{\circ}),Bu(\boldsymbol{\xi}^{\partial}))=\boldsymbol{y}
;
the usual conditioning procedure for GPs yields a the PMM solution
\widetilde{u}\sim\mathcal{GP}(\widetilde{m},\widetilde{k})
to the PDE (
4.1
), with posterior mean
\widetilde{m}\colon X\to\mathbb{R}
and covariance
\widetilde{k}\colon X\times X\to\mathbb{R}
for
v
given by

\displaystyle\widetilde{m}(x)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\bigl(T_{2}k(x,\boldsymbol{\xi})\bigr)\bigl(T_{1}T_{2}k(\boldsymbol{\xi},\boldsymbol{\xi})\bigr)^{-1}\boldsymbol{y},

(4.2)

\displaystyle\widetilde{k}(x,x^{\prime})

\displaystyle\coloneqq k(x,x^{\prime})-\bigl(T_{2}k(x,\boldsymbol{\xi})\bigr)\bigl(T_{1}T_{2}k(\boldsymbol{\xi},\boldsymbol{\xi})\bigr)^{-1}\bigl(T_{1}k(\boldsymbol{\xi},x^{\prime})\bigr),

(4.3)

where
T_{1}k(\boldsymbol{\xi},x)\in\mathbb{R}^{(I+J)\times 1}
,
T_{2}k(x,\boldsymbol{\xi})\in\mathbb{R}^{1\times(I+J)}
, and
T_{1}T_{2}k(\boldsymbol{\xi},\boldsymbol{\xi})\in\mathbb{R}^{(I+J)\times(I+J)}
are defined by

\displaystyle T_{1}k(\boldsymbol{\xi},x)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\begin{bmatrix}\vphantom{\Big|}L_{1}k(\xi_{1}^{\circ},x)&\cdots&L_{1}k(\xi_{I}^{\circ},x)&k(\xi_{1}^{\partial},x)&\cdots&k(\xi_{J}^{\partial},x)\end{bmatrix}^{\intercal},

\displaystyle T_{2}k(x,\boldsymbol{\xi})

\displaystyle\coloneqq\begin{bmatrix}\vphantom{\Big|}L_{2}k(x,\xi_{1}^{\circ})&\cdots&L_{2}k(x,\xi_{I}^{\circ})&k(x,\xi_{1}^{\partial})&\cdots&k(x,\xi_{J}^{\partial})\end{bmatrix},

and

\displaystyle T_{1}T_{2}k(\boldsymbol{\xi},\boldsymbol{\xi})

\displaystyle\coloneqq\hskip-0.94722pt\begin{bmatrix}L_{1}L_{2}k(\xi_{1}^{\circ},\xi_{1}^{\circ})&\cdots&L_{1}L_{2}k(\xi_{1}^{\circ},\xi_{I}^{\circ})&L_{1}k(\xi_{1}^{\circ},\xi_{1}^{\partial})&\cdots&L_{1}k(\xi_{1}^{\circ},\xi_{J}^{\partial})\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
L_{1}L_{2}k(\xi_{I}^{\circ},\xi_{1}^{\circ})&\cdots&L_{1}L_{2}k(\xi_{I}^{\circ},\xi_{I}^{\circ})&L_{1}k(\xi_{I}^{\circ},\xi_{1}^{\partial})&\cdots&L_{1}k(\xi_{I}^{\circ},\xi_{J}^{\partial})\\
L_{2}k(\xi_{1}^{\partial},\xi_{1}^{\circ})&\cdots&L_{2}k(\xi_{1}^{\partial},\xi_{I}^{\circ})&k(\xi_{1}^{\partial},\xi_{1}^{\partial})&\cdots&k(\xi_{1}^{\partial},\xi_{J}^{\partial})\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
L_{2}k(\xi_{J}^{\partial},\xi_{1}^{\circ})&\cdots&L_{2}k(\xi_{J}^{\partial},\xi_{I}^{\circ})&k(\xi_{J}^{\partial},\xi_{1}^{\partial})&\cdots&k(\xi_{J}^{\partial},\xi_{J}^{\partial})\end{bmatrix}.

Heuristically,
T
is the operator
u\mapsto(Lu,Bu)
.
However, the crucial step missing from the above is to define the operator
T
properly and to verify that it satisfies the hypotheses of
Theorem

1.1
, so that
Tu
will indeed be a GP to which the conditioning formula can be applied.
The following outlines one such construction, with no attempt at making it the sharpest possible.

Let
\mathcal{H}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{X}
be a Hilbert space with respect to the usual
L^{2}
inner product, and assume that point evaluation
\delta_{x}
at each
x\in X
is a continuous linear functional on
\mathcal{H}
, i.e.
\mathcal{H}
is a reproducing kernel Hilbert space.
Assume also that continuous functions are dense in
\mathcal{H}
.
A good example of this setting is a space of Fourier band-limited functions on
X
.

The operator
u\mapsto(Lu,Bu)
is easily defined as an unbounded operator from
\mathcal{H}
or
L^{2}(X)
into
L^{2}(X)\oplus L^{2}(\partial X)
on an
L^{2}
-dense domain such as
\mathcal{C}^{2}(\overline{X};\mathbb{R})
.
This operator is closable, and even closed in some cases such as
d=1
or zero Dirichlet boundary conditions (
Behrndt and Langer 2007
, Section 4
;
Kato 1995
, Section III.5.5
).
Thus, we take
T\colon\dom(T)\subseteq\mathcal{H}\to L^{2}(X)\oplus L^{2}(\partial X)
to be the closure of this operator, and it possesses a densely defined adjoint as it is defined in a Hilbert space — although, in fact, one typically shows that
T
is closable by constructing the densely-defined adjoint explicitly for smooth enough functions.

For simplicity, we assume that the prior covariance kernel
k
is bounded and
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}
, e.g. the widely-used squared exponential / radial basis function kernel
k(x,x^{\prime})\coloneqq\exp(-\lvert x-x^{\prime}\rvert^{2})
and the rational quadratic kernel
k(x,x^{\prime})\coloneqq(1+\lvert x-x^{\prime}\rvert^{2})^{-1}
, since this ensures that the sample paths of
u\sim\mathcal{GP}(0,k)
are also smooth and a fortiori in
\dom(T)
.
For sharper results connecting the regularity of
k
to the regularity of draws from
\mathcal{GP}(0,k)
, see
Scheuerer 2010
.
Also, since this choice of
k
ensures that
u
is a.s. in
\mathcal{C}^{2}(\overline{X};\mathbb{R})
, Fernique’s theorem shows that
\lVert u\rVert_{\mathcal{C}^{2}}
is exponentially integrable, yielding finite moments of all orders for
\lVert u\rVert_{L^{2}(X)}
,
\lVert Lu\rVert_{L^{2}(X)}
, and
\lVert Bu\rVert_{L^{2}(\partial X)}
.

Appendix A

Lemma

\mathcal{U}
.
This is a simple claim, but not a trivial one.

Bogachev 1998
shows that a GP induces a Gaussian measure on the locally convex space

, i.e. each
\langle\delta_{x}|u\rangle\coloneqq u(x)
is Gaussian in
\mathbb{R}
.

It has already been noted by

\mathcal{U}^{\prime}
;

\ast

We may then pass from Gaussianity of

to Gaussianity of
\langle\ell|u\rangle
for

using a weakly-
\ast

This cannot be done using weak-

Proof of

For

\ast

Thus, if

, then there exists
\ell\in\mathcal{U}^{\prime}\setminus A

— which is the dual of
\mathcal{U}^{\prime}
with its weak-
\ast

\displaystyle\langle\ell|u\rangle=1\quad\text{but}\quad\langle a|u\rangle=0\text{ for all $a\in A$}.

(A.1)

However, a special case of (

) is that

\displaystyle\langle\ell|u\rangle=1\quad\text{but}\quad\underbrace{\langle\delta_{x}|u\rangle\equiv u(x)=0\text{ for all $x\in X$}}_{\iff u=0},

which is clearly a contradiction.

For
(b)
, if
\mathcal{U}
is separable and quasi-reflexive, then the total linear subspace
\Delta\subseteq\mathcal{U}^{\prime}
has order at most

That is,
(\Delta_{(1)})_{(1)}=\Delta_{(1)}
, i.e.

In the case that
\mathcal{U}
is a separable Banach space of bounded functions with respect to the supremum norm, and that element of
\mathcal{U}
attains its supremum norm, we appeal to
Banach 1932
(see also
Ostrovskii 2001

\Delta_{(1)}=\mathcal{U}^{\prime}
since there exists
M>0
such that, for all

, there exists

with

and

, where

For
(c)
, by

\displaystyle\log\mathbb{E}[\exp(\mathrm{i}\langle\ell|u\rangle)]=\mathrm{i}L(\ell)-\frac{1}{2}Q(\ell)\quad\text{for all $\ell\in\mathcal{U}^{\prime}$.}

To that end, let
\ell\in\mathcal{U}^{\prime}
be arbitrary.
Since
\mathcal{U}^{\prime}=\Delta_{(1)}
, there exists a sequence

in
\Delta

\ast
to
\ell

\ell_{n}=\sum_{j=1}^{J(n)}\alpha_{n,j}\delta_{x_{n,j}}

Then, appealing to Lebesgue’s dominated convergence theorem for the interchange of the limit and the expectation,

\displaystyle\log\mathbb{E}[\exp(\mathrm{i}\langle\ell|u\rangle)]

\displaystyle=\log\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(\mathrm{i}\left\langle\mathop{\textup{wk-$\ast$-lim}}_{n\to\infty}\ell_{n}\middle|u\right\rangle\right)\right]

Finally, if
