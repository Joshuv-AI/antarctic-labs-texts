Fermions with 𝑺 ​ 𝑼 ​ ( 𝟏 , 𝒏 ) SU(1,n) Spacetime Symmetry
Joseph Smith

Abstract
We construct theories of free fermions in ( 2 ​ n − 1 ) (2n-1) -dimensions with S ​ U ​ ( 1 , n ) SU(1,n) spacetime symmetry from the null reduction of fermions on a 2 ​ n 2n -dimensional Ω \Omega -deformed Minkowski background for n = 2 n=2 and n = 3 n=3 . These play a role in the 5d S ​ U ​ ( 1 , 3 ) SU(1,3) -invariant theories that are conjectured to offer a full description of certain 6d superconformal field theories. We find the ( 2 ​ n − 1 ) (2n-1) -dimensional manifestation of the supersymmetry of a free 2 ​ n 2n -dimensional boson-fermion system, which we use to fix the fermion two-point functions. It is then shown that the full 2 ​ n 2n -dimensional two-point function can be recovered through resummation. Limits of the theories are considered, and it is observed that both Galilean and Carrollian field theories appear in different regimes. We confirm that the correlation functions obey the S ​ U ​ ( 1 , n ) SU(1,n) Ward identities and the representations of the fermions under this group are discussed.

Though non-Lorentzian field theories have an existence outside of Lorentzian theories, the two are often connected. For instance, non-Lorentzian field theories can be constructed from a Lorentzian one through null reduction. One of the lightcone directions in

2.2
The Action for General Dimensions

With the geometric quantities calculated above in hand, we are ready to find the action of a fermion on the
\Omega
-deformed background. The action for a free massless Dirac spinor on a curved background with metric
\hat{g}
is

S=-\int d^{D}\hat{x}\,\hat{e}\hat{\bar{\psi}}\gamma^{a}\hat{e}_{a}^{\mu}\hat{\nabla}_{\mu}\hat{\psi}\ .

(19)

We are interested in performing a coordinate transformation of the form considered above, in which we write the transformed metric as

\hat{g}_{\mu\nu}(\hat{x})d\hat{x}^{\mu}d\hat{x}^{\nu}=\Delta^{2}(x)g_{\mu\nu}(x)dx^{\mu}dx^{\nu}\ .

(20)

Specifying a vielbein
e
for
g
, we can use the Lorentz transformation in eq. (
8
) to define the spinor field transformation

\hat{\psi}(\hat{x})=\Delta^{-\frac{D-1}{2}}S^{-1}[\Lambda]\psi(x)\ .

(21)

It can be shown that this choice leaves the form of the action invariant
Shapiro:2016pfm
, so we are left with

S=-\int d^{D}x\,e\bar{\psi}\gamma^{a}e^{\mu}_{a}\nabla_{\mu}\psi\ .

(22)

Let us now specialise to the transformation (
1
) in
D=2n
dimensions. We can substitute (
10
) and (
18
) into the action to obtain the
\Omega
-deformed spinor action

S=i\int d^{2n}x\,\psi^{{\dagger}}\Bigg[\sqrt{2}P_{+}\left(\partial_{+}+\frac{1}{8}\Omega_{ij}\gamma_{ij}\right)\psi+\sqrt{2}P_{-}\partial_{-}\psi+\gamma_{0i}D_{i}\psi\Bigg]\ ,

(23)

where we work with the projectors
P_{\pm}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\mathbbm{1}\pm\gamma_{01}\right)
and have defined the differential operator

D_{i}=\partial_{i}-\frac{1}{2}\Omega_{ij}x^{j}\partial_{-}\ .

(24)

In this form the complete symmetry group of the action after null reduction will not be manifest: we can remedy this by introducing the projections of
\psi
with respect to
P_{\pm}
, which we denote by

P_{+}\psi=\chi\;,\;P_{-}\psi=\lambda\ .

(25)

The action is then

S=i\int d^{2n}x\,\Bigg[\sqrt{2}\lambda^{{\dagger}}\partial_{-}\lambda+\chi^{{\dagger}}\gamma_{0i}D_{i}\lambda+\lambda^{{\dagger}}\gamma_{0i}D_{i}\chi+\sqrt{2}\chi^{{\dagger}}\left(\partial_{+}+\frac{1}{8}\Omega_{ij}\gamma_{ij}\right)\chi\Bigg]\ .

(26)

We would like to reduce our theory on the null interval. To do this, we take the fields to have mode expansions of the form

\displaystyle\lambda(x^{+},x^{-},x)=\sum_{k\in\mathcal{S}}\lambda_{k}(x^{-},x)e^{-\frac{ikx^{+}}{R}}\ ,

(27a)

\displaystyle\chi(x^{+},x^{-},x)=\sum_{k\in\mathcal{S}}\chi_{k}(x^{-},x)e^{-\frac{ikx^{+}}{R}}\ .

(27b)

The set of allowed mode numbers
\mathcal{S}
is fixed by requiring that the field obtained from a coordinate transformation of the Minkowski spinor,

\hat{\psi}(\hat{x})=\psi^{\prime}(x)\ ,

(28)

can be written as the Fourier series

\psi^{\prime}(x^{+},x^{-},x)=\sum_{l\in\mathbb{Z}}\psi^{\prime}_{l}(x^{-},x)e^{-\frac{ilx^{+}}{R}}\ ,

(29)

mirroring the analogous prescription for a scalar field
Lambert:2021nol
. This amounts to demanding consistency of the equation

\Delta^{-\frac{d-1}{2}}S^{-1}[\Lambda]\sum_{k\in\mathcal{S}}\psi_{k}(x^{-},x)e^{-\frac{ikx^{+}}{R}}=\sum_{l\in\mathbb{Z}}\psi^{\prime}_{l}(x^{-},x)e^{-\frac{ilx^{+}}{R}}\ .

(30)

As the Lorentz transformation is dependent on
x^{+}
the details of this will depend on the spacetime dimension. Substituting the mode expansion into (
26
) allows us to perform the integral over
x^{+}
, giving

S=2\pi iR\sum_{k\in\mathcal{S}}\int d^{2n-1}x\Bigg[\sqrt{2}\lambda_{k}^{{\dagger}}\partial_{-}\lambda_{k}+\chi_{k}^{{\dagger}}\gamma_{0i}D_{i}\lambda_{k}+\lambda_{k}^{{\dagger}}\gamma_{0i}D_{i}\chi_{k}+\sqrt{2}\chi^{{\dagger}}\left(\frac{1}{8}\Omega_{ij}\gamma_{ij}-\frac{ik}{R}\right)\chi_{k}\Bigg]\ .

(31)

This action was first constructed in
Lambert:2021nol
. We note that if we were only interested in using the reduction to construct a fermion with
SU(1,n)
spacetime symmetry (i.e. if we were to truncate to the pair
(\lambda_{k},\chi_{k})
) then we can relax the condition on
k
and instead take it to be any real number.

2.2.1
The Action for 5d

Let us now specialise (
31
) to specific dimensions, namely
D=6
and
D=4
. While most of (
31
) is independent of dimension, the term involving

is not. Expanding out the expression for

\Omega_{ij}\gamma_{ij}=\frac{2}{R}\gamma_{23}\left(\mathbbm{1}+\gamma_{01}\gamma_{*}\right)\ ,

(32)

where our convention for

. Let us now impose the condition that our fields are Weyl spinors with negative chirality: from this we see that

\Omega_{ij}\gamma_{ij}\chi=0

(33)

and the final term in the action vanishes, leaving the simplified 5d action

S^{(5d)}=2\pi iR\sum_{k\in\mathcal{S}^{(5d)}}\int d^{5}x\Bigg[\sqrt{2}\lambda_{k}^{{\dagger}}\partial_{-}\lambda_{k}+\chi_{k}^{{\dagger}}\gamma_{0i}D_{i}\lambda_{k}+\lambda_{k}^{{\dagger}}\gamma_{0i}D_{i}\chi_{k}-\frac{\sqrt{2}ik}{R}\chi^{{\dagger}}_{k}\chi_{k}\Bigg]\ .

Using the explicit form (

can be written as

\sum_{l\in\mathbb{Z}}\psi^{\prime}_{l}e^{-\frac{ilx^{+}}{R}}=\left(e^{\frac{2ix^{+}}{R}}A+e^{\frac{ix^{+}}{R}}B+C+e^{-\frac{ix^{+}}{R}}D\right)\sum_{k\in\mathcal{S}^{(5d)}}\psi_{k}e^{-\frac{ikx^{+}}{R}}

(35)

where

are
x^{+}
-independent matrices whose form will not be important. It immediately follows from this that the set of allowed modes is just
\mathcal{S}^{(5d)}=\mathbb{Z}
.

2.2.2
The Action for 3d

We can do the exact same analysis for
D=4

\Omega_{ij}\gamma_{ij}=\frac{2i}{R}\gamma_{01}\gamma_{*}\ ,

(36)

so the action of the matrix on the field

\Omega_{ij}\gamma_{ij}\chi=\pm\frac{2i}{R}\chi\ ,

(37)

depending on the chirality of our fields. Unlike in the 5d case no simplification occurs by choosing a particular sign; for definiteness we will take our fields to have positive chirality from here onwards. The effect of this is to shift

-dependent phase, where we take

\hat{\psi}(\hat{x})=\Delta^{-\frac{3}{2}}S_{+}^{-1}[\Lambda]e^{-\frac{ix^{+}}{4R}}\psi(x)

S^{(3d)}=2\pi iR\sum_{k\in\mathcal{S}^{(3d)}}\int d^{3}x\Bigg[\sqrt{2}\lambda_{k}^{{\dagger}}\partial_{-}\lambda_{k}+\chi_{k}^{{\dagger}}\gamma_{0i}D_{i}\lambda_{k}+\lambda_{k}^{{\dagger}}\gamma_{0i}D_{i}\chi_{k}-\frac{\sqrt{2}ik}{R}\chi_{k}^{{\dagger}}\chi_{k}\Bigg]\ .

(39)

Let us determine the set
\mathcal{S}^{(3d)}
. Examining the consistency condition (
30

15
) for the spinor representation and the modified transformation (
38

\sum_{l\in\mathbb{Z}}\psi_{l}^{\prime}e^{-\frac{ilx^{+}}{R}}=\left(e^{\frac{ix^{+}}{R}}A+B+e^{-\frac{ix^{+}}{R}}C\right)\sum_{k\in\mathcal{S}^{(3d)}}\psi_{k}e^{-\frac{ikx^{+}}{R}}

(40)

in terms of
x^{+}
-independent matrices

, so as for the 5d theory we have
\mathcal{S}^{(3d)}=\mathbb{Z}
.

2.3
A Simplification for Non-Zero
k

The action of the level-
k
mode takes the form

S_{k}=2\pi iR\int d^{2n-1}x\Bigg[\sqrt{2}\lambda_{k}^{{\dagger}}\partial_{-}\lambda_{k}+\chi_{k}^{{\dagger}}\gamma_{0i}D_{i}\lambda_{k}+\lambda_{k}^{{\dagger}}\gamma_{0i}D_{i}\chi_{k}-\frac{\sqrt{2}ik}{R}\chi_{k}^{{\dagger}}\chi_{k}\Bigg]

(41)

in both 3d and 5d. The field
\chi_{k}
plays a remarkably different role depending on whether
k=0
or
k\neq 0
. In the former case, the final term in the action vanishes. The field
\chi_{0}
is then a spinorial Lagrange multiplier imposing the constraint

\gamma_{0i}D_{i}\lambda_{k}=0\ .

(42)

However, when
k\neq 0

\chi_{k}
has the algebraic equation of motion

\chi_{k}=-\frac{iR}{\sqrt{2}k}\gamma_{0i}D_{i}\lambda_{k}\ .

(43)

We can substitute this back into the action to obtain

S_{k}=\frac{\sqrt{2}\pi R^{2}}{k}\int d^{2n-1}x\Bigg[\frac{2i}{R}\lambda_{k}^{{\dagger}}\left(k-\Gamma\right)\partial_{-}\lambda_{k}+\lambda_{k}^{{\dagger}}D^{2}\lambda_{k}\Bigg]\ ,

(44)

where we’ve defined the matrix

\Gamma=\frac{iR\Omega_{ij}\gamma_{ij}}{4}\ .

(45)

As mentioned above, the properties of this matrix are dimension dependent. Using (
36
) we see that in 3d we have

\Gamma\lambda_{k}=\frac{1}{2}\lambda_{k}\ ,

(46)

so the action is just

S^{(3d)}_{k}=\frac{\sqrt{2}\pi R^{2}}{k}\int d^{3}x\Bigg[\frac{2i}{R}\left(k-\frac{1}{2}\right)\lambda_{k}^{{\dagger}}\partial_{-}\lambda_{k}+\lambda_{k}^{{\dagger}}D^{2}\lambda_{k}\Bigg]\ .

(47)

In 5d it has the nice property that

P_{-}\Gamma^{2}=P_{-}\ ,

(48)

so we can define the pseudo-projector

P_{\pm}^{\Gamma}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\mathbbm{1}\pm\Gamma\right)

(49)

that behaves as a projector when acting on the subspace defined by projecting with
P_{-}
. Defining the fields

\lambda_{k}^{\pm}=P^{\Gamma}_{\pm}\lambda_{k}\ ,

(50)

the action decomposes as

S^{(5d)}_{k}=\frac{\sqrt{2}\pi R^{2}}{k}\int d^{5}x\Bigg[\frac{2i(k-1)}{R}\lambda_{k}^{+{\dagger}}\partial_{-}\lambda^{+}_{k}+\frac{2i(k+1)}{R}\lambda_{k}^{-{\dagger}}\partial_{-}\lambda^{-}_{k}+\lambda_{k}^{+{\dagger}}D^{2}\lambda^{+}_{k}+\lambda_{k}^{-{\dagger}}D^{2}\lambda^{-}_{k}\Bigg]\ .

(51)

By counting the degrees of freedom in our fields we observe that both
\lambda_{k}
in the 4d case and
\lambda_{k}^{\pm}

obtained in

We can use this form of the action to predict the form of the

two-point function without doing any detailed computations. Defining the

-dimensional scalar two-point function

\displaystyle G_{k}^{((2n-1)d)}\equiv\bra{\Omega}\phi_{k}(x_{1}^{-},x_{1})\bar{\phi}_{k}(x_{2}^{-},x_{2})\ket{\Omega}

(53)

for the action (

\bra{\Omega}\lambda_{k}(x_{1}^{-},x_{1})\lambda_{k}^{{\dagger}}(x_{2}^{-},x_{2})\ket{\Omega}=\frac{\sqrt{2}k}{R}P_{-}G_{k-\frac{1}{2}}^{(3d)}

(54)

in 3d, and

\bra{\Omega}\lambda_{k}(x_{1}^{-},x_{1})\lambda_{k}^{{\dagger}}(x_{2}^{-},x_{2})\ket{\Omega}=\frac{\sqrt{2}k}{R}P_{-}\left(P_{+}^{\Gamma}G_{k-1}^{(5d)}+P_{-}^{\Gamma}G_{k+1}^{(5d)}\right)

in 5d. The projection operators have been added by hand to project onto the subspaces in which each field lives. We will show in the next section that this intuition is correct.

3
SUSY and Correlation Functions

3.1
Supersymmetry in the Reduced Theory

Since the fermionic theory we are considering is free, the quantities of interest are the two-point functions of the fields. There are many ways we could compute these, but it will prove convenient to fix these using the supersymmetry present in a free theory with a single complex scalar and fermion and the known two-point functions for an
SU(1,n)
scalar field
Lambert:2021nol
.

We start with the action for a free theory in Minkowski spacetime consisting of complex scalar and Weyl spinor fields,

S=-\int d^{2n}\hat{x}\left(\eta^{\mu\nu}\hat{\partial}_{\mu}\bar{\hat{\phi}}\hat{\partial}_{\nu}\hat{\phi}+\bar{\hat{\psi}}\gamma^{\mu}\hat{\partial}_{\mu}\psi\right)\ .

(56)

This is invariant under the transformation

\displaystyle\delta\hat{\phi}=\bar{\hat{\xi}}\hat{\psi}\ ,

(57a)

\displaystyle\delta\hat{\psi}=-\hat{\partial}_{\mu}\hat{\phi}\gamma^{\mu}\hat{\xi}\ ,

(57b)

where
\hat{\xi}
is a Weyl spinor. We can then use the coordinate transformation (
1
) and perform the reduction along
x^{+}
, leaving us with an infinite tower of scalar and spinor theories in
(2n-1)
-dimensions. Since we still have a full description of the system (albeit in terms of an infinite number of fields) after transforming and taking the null reduction, the supersymmetry of the
2n
-dimensional theory will be present in some form in the
(2n-1)
-dimensional theory. As the supercharge transforms as a
2n
-dimensional spinor, its components will generically develop dependence on
x^{+}
and will therefore not commute with the generator of translations along the compactified null direction. This means that we expect some fraction of the supersymmetry transformations in the reduced theory will take us between levels of the Kaluza-Klein tower.

3.1.1
3d Supersymmetry

Let us first look at the 3d theory. Combining the scalar field action (
52
) for

and

\displaystyle\eta

\displaystyle=P_{-}\epsilon\ .

(59b)

\displaystyle=\eta^{{\dagger}}\lambda_{k}\ ,

(60a)

\displaystyle\delta\lambda_{k}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\sqrt{2}k}{R}\phi_{k-\frac{1}{2}}\eta\ ,

\displaystyle\delta\chi_{k}

\displaystyle=i\gamma_{0i}D_{i}\phi_{k-\frac{1}{2}}\eta\ ,

\displaystyle\delta\phi_{k+\frac{1}{2}}

(61b)

\displaystyle=-i\sqrt{2}\partial_{-}\phi_{k+\frac{1}{2}}\xi\ ,

\displaystyle=\frac{\sqrt{2}k}{R}\left(\eta_{1}^{{\dagger}}\eta_{2}-\eta_{2}^{{\dagger}}\eta_{1}\right)\phi_{k-\frac{1}{2}}\ ,

(62a)

\displaystyle[\delta_{\xi_{1}},\delta_{\xi_{2}}]\phi_{k-\frac{1}{2}}

(62c)

We observe that we have closure of the algebra for the commutators between transformations of the same chirality, but that this breaks down for commutators between transformations of mixed chirality. It naively appears that these transformations are not symmetries of the theory. However, since we have an infinite number of fields we can repackage them into a sum of the form

\displaystyle\hat{\eta}

\displaystyle=e^{\frac{i\alpha(1+c)}{2R}}\eta\ ,

(64a)

\displaystyle\hat{\xi}

\displaystyle=e^{-\frac{i\alpha(1-c)}{2R}}\xi\ ,

(64b)

where

\phi

\displaystyle[\delta_{\hat{\eta}_{1}},\delta_{\hat{\eta}_{2}}]\phi

\displaystyle=i\sqrt{2}\left(\eta_{1}^{{\dagger}}\eta_{2}-\eta_{2}^{{\dagger}}\eta_{1}\right)\partial_{\alpha}\phi\ ,

\displaystyle=i\sqrt{2}\left(\xi_{1}^{{\dagger}}\xi_{2}-\xi_{2}^{{\dagger}}\xi_{1}\right)\partial_{-}\phi\ ,

(65b)

\displaystyle[\delta_{\hat{\eta}},\delta_{\hat{\xi}}]\phi

\displaystyle=i\left(\xi^{{\dagger}}\gamma_{0i}\eta-\eta^{{\dagger}}\gamma_{0i}\xi\right)D_{i}\phi\ .

(65c)

As expected, the algebra closes on the new field. We are able to identify

3.2
Correlation Functions in the Reduced Theory

We would like to find the two-point functions of the fermionic theory. Up to a factor, these are solutions to the classical equation of motion and are not hard to find. However, we need to fix the overall normalisation. The simplest way to do this is to exploit the fact that the theory we’re interested in can be extended to the supersymmetric theory discussed above, so the normalisation of the fermion two-point functions can be fixed in terms of the known normalisation of scalar correlation functions found in
Lambert:2021nol

ds^{2}=-2dx^{+}\left(dx^{-}+\frac{1}{2}\Omega_{ij}x^{j}dx^{i}\right)+dx^{i}dx^{i}\ ,

(143)

with our coordinates taking values in the ranges
x^{+}\in(-\pi R,\pi R)
and
x^{-},x^{i}\in(-\infty,\infty)
. If we try and take the limit
R\to 0
we find the term involving
\Omega
diverges. However, we can remove all
R
-dependence in the metric by working with the coordinates

\displaystyle v

\displaystyle=\frac{x^{+}}{R}\ ,

(144a)

\displaystyle u

\displaystyle=Rx^{-}\ ,

(144b)

for which the metric becomes

ds^{2}=-2dv\left(du+\frac{1}{2}\tilde{\Omega}_{ij}x^{j}dx^{i}\right)+dx^{i}dx^{i}\ ,

(145)

with
\tilde{\Omega}_{ij}=R\Omega_{ij}
. The new coordinates have the ranges
v\in(-\pi,\pi)
and
u\in(-\infty,\infty)
, so all reference to
R
has disappeared from the theory. Naively, it appears that there should be no issues with taking
R\to 0
. The problem comes from the fact that (
144
) is not defined when
R=0
. In particular, when
R=0
the range of
x^{+}
collapses to the single point
x^{+}=0
whereas the range of
v
remains finite, so we are mapping between manifolds of different dimension. We can remedy this by restricting ourselves to the hypersurface

in the
R
-independent spacetime parameterised by
(v,u,x^{i})
. This is a null hypersurface of a
2n
-dimensional Lorentzian spacetime and defines a
(2n-1)
-dimensional Carrollian spacetime

. A field theory on this background will therefore be invariant under the
(2n-1)
-dimensional Carroll group. We note that none of the details here depend on
\Omega
, and the same structure will appear in more conventional null circle compactifications.

Let us illustrate this discussion with an example. Consider a complex scalar field
\hat{\phi}
. Its Lagrangian on the ’extended’ spacetime is

\mathcal{L}=\partial_{v}\hat{\bar{\phi}}\partial_{u}\hat{\phi}+\partial_{u}\hat{\bar{\phi}}\partial_{v}\hat{\phi}+\frac{1}{2}\tilde{\Omega}_{ij}x^{j}\left(\partial_{i}\hat{\bar{\phi}}\partial_{u}\hat{\phi}+\partial_{u}\hat{\bar{\phi}}\partial_{i}\hat{\phi}\right)-\frac{x^{2}}{4}\partial_{u}\hat{\bar{\phi}}\partial_{u}\hat{\phi}-\partial_{i}\hat{\bar{\phi}}\partial_{i}\hat{\phi}\ .

(146)

Expanding
\hat{\phi}
about
v=0

\hat{\phi}=\phi+vH+O(v^{2})\ ,

(147)

we can pull
\mathcal{L}
back to the
v=0
hypersurface to obtain the action

S=\int d^{2n-1}x\left(\bar{H}\partial_{u}\phi+\partial_{u}\bar{\phi}H+\frac{1}{2}\tilde{\Omega}_{ij}x^{j}\left(\partial_{i}\bar{\phi}\partial_{u}\phi+\partial_{u}\bar{\phi}\partial_{i}\phi\right)-\frac{x^{2}}{4}\partial_{u}\bar{\phi}\partial_{u}\phi-\partial_{i}\bar{\phi}\partial_{i}\phi\right)\ .

(148)

Note that we must treat
H
and
\phi
as independent fields. We see that
\bar{H}
is a Lagrange multiplier that imposes the constraint

\partial_{u}\phi=0\ ,

(149)

so we can rewrite the action in the equivalent form

S=\int d^{2n-1}x\left(\bar{H}\partial_{u}\phi+\partial_{u}\bar{\phi}H-\partial_{i}\bar{\phi}\partial_{i}\phi\right)\ .

(150)

We recognise this as the action (
134
) for a magnetic Carroll scalar field theory. Similarly, performing the same calculation for a fermion recovers the action (
142
). It does not appear possible to construct the action (
140
) using this approach, as this would require the initial Lagrangian to have terms that are second-order in derivatives to get the correct Lagrange multiplier structure.

The limit
R\to 0
is somewhat less natural than the previously studied
R\to\infty
limit. For instance, the correlation functions of both the scalar and fermion
SU(1,n)
theories vanish once it is taken. It would be interesting to see if the limit (possibly modified) can be used to make non-trivial statements about Carrollian field theories using quantities computed in
SU(1,n)
theories, or if the relationship between the two is limited to the construction of Carrollian actions from their
SU(1,n)
counterparts.

5
Reduction of the 2d Fermion

So far we have only been interested in theories in

The coordinate transformation (

Let us be more precise. We start with the 2d Minkowski metric in lightcone coordinates,

\mathfrak{su}(1,n)
Ward Identities

While we have discussed both the fermion action and its associated two-point functions in some depth, we have not yet shown that the theories really are invariant under the spacetime symmetry algebra
\mathfrak{su}(1,n)
as we have claimed multiple times. We will now remedy this by showing that the two-point functions satisfy the corresponding Ward identities.

Let us briefly review the

7
Conclusion

In this paper we have analysed fermions in
2n-1
dimensions with
SU(1,n)
spacetime symmetry. These were constructed through the null reduction of the
2n
-dimensional free fermion CFT on an
\Omega
-deformed Minkowski background. The two-point functions of the theory for
n=2
and
n=3
were found using the
(2n-1)
-dimensional remnants of the supersymmetry of a free complex scalar and free fermion in
2n
dimensions, and the known two-point functions for a scalar field with
SU(1,n)
symmetry. The full non-compact
2n
-dimensional two-point function was then reconstructed from the Kaluza-Klein tower of
(2n-1)
-dimensional fields. The analogous transformation for a 2d fermion was also considered, and it was found we could only reconstruct the left-moving fermion’s correlation function. We examined the
R\to\infty
DLCQ limit of the
(2n-1)
-dimensional two-point functions, showing that the
2n
-dimensional two-point function could be recovered through resummation, and commented on the
R\to 0
limit, which gave a theory with Carroll symmetry. Finally, it was shown that that the Ward identities for
SU(1,n)
spacetime symmetry are satisfied at each level in the Kaluza-Klein tower.

Though we have only discussed free field theories, it is interesting to compare the way in which
2n
-dimensional supersymmetry arises in the reduced theories with the supersymmetric structure of the 5d
SU(1,3)
-invariant interacting field theories found in
Lambert:2019jwi
;
Lambert:2020jjm
. For example, if we truncate the action of the 3d theory to the terms involving the fields
(\lambda_{k},\chi_{k},\phi_{k+\frac{1}{2}})
then our theory is invariant under the
\xi
-type supercharges, while the
\eta
-type supercharges of the full theory disappear. Similarly, if we only include the fields
(\lambda_{k},\chi_{k},\phi_{k-\frac{1}{2}})
this conclusion is reversed. The same story is true in 5d, where retaining only fields with the same value of
k
preserves the
\xi
-type supercharges and mixing levels preserves the
\eta
-type supercharges. As discussed in the introduction, this is exactly what occurs in the interacting theories; the Lagrangians only include a finite number of fields and are invariant under half the supercharges of the 6d theories they are conjectured to describe. The fact that the full higher-dimensional supercharge is recovered in our free theory lends weight to the proposal that the same happens when the interacting theories are treated non-perturbatively, as must be the case for the theories to provide complete descriptions of 6d SCFTs. We leave it to future work to show that the full superconformal symmetries of the free theory match the structure of the 5d interacting theories, i.e. that the conformal supercharges are present at each level in the reduced theory.

So far, the aforementioned 5d theories are the only known interacting field theories with
SU(1,n)
spacetime symmetry for
n\geq 2
. It would be interesting to find other examples; the methods outlined in this work and in
Lambert:2021nol
allow any 4d Lagrangian CFT to be reduced on the
\Omega
-deformed background, yielding a 3d theory with an infinite number of fields and
SU(1,2)
spacetime symmetry at each level. The task would then be to try and find a non-trivial truncation of the theory to a finite number of fields. The obvious candidate 4d theory is
\mathcal{N}=4
SYM, which could help elucidate the field-theoretic formulation of Spin Matrix Theories with
PSU(1,2|3)
symmetry
Baiguera:2022pll
. A larger class of theories that could also be studied are Lagrangian 4d
\mathcal{N}=2
SCFTs, which were classified in
Bhardwaj:2013qia
. Finding the structure of the reduced theories associated with these could shed light on applications of
SU(1,2)
theories to non-Lagrangian 4d
\mathcal{N}=2
SCFTs, such as the class
\mathcal{S}
theories
Gaiotto:2009we
;
Gaiotto:2009hg
.

\lambda_{3}=-i\lambda_{2}\ ,

(237)

\lambda_{1}=\ln\cos\left(\frac{x^{+}}{2R}\right)+\frac{ix^{+}}{2R}\ .

(238)

Substituting this back into the other two relations and reorganising gives the Lie algebra element’s components

\displaystyle\lambda^{01}

\displaystyle=\ln\cos\left(\frac{x^{+}}{2R}\right)\ ,

(239a)

\displaystyle\lambda^{23}

\displaystyle=\frac{x^{+}}{2R}\ ,

(239b)

\displaystyle\lambda^{03}=\lambda^{31}=\frac{1}{2\sqrt{2}R\sin\left(\frac{x^{+}}{2R}\right)}\Bigg[

\displaystyle x^{2}\left(\sin\left(\frac{x^{+}}{2R}\right)\ln\cos\left(\frac{x^{+}}{2R}\right)-\frac{x^{+}}{2R}\cos\left(\frac{x^{+}}{2R}\right)\right)

\displaystyle\hskip-19.91684pt-x^{3}\left(\cos\left(\frac{x^{+}}{2R}\right)\ln\cos\left(\frac{x^{+}}{2R}\right)+\frac{x^{+}}{2R}\sin\left(\frac{x^{+}}{2R}\right)\right)\Bigg]\ ,

(239c)

\displaystyle\lambda^{12}=-\lambda^{02}=\frac{1}{2\sqrt{2}R\sin\left(\frac{x^{+}}{2R}\right)}\Bigg[

\displaystyle x^{3}\left(\sin\left(\frac{x^{+}}{2R}\right)\ln\cos\left(\frac{x^{+}}{2R}\right)-\frac{x^{+}}{2R}\cos\left(\frac{x^{+}}{2R}\right)\right)

\displaystyle\hskip-19.91684pt+x^{2}\left(\cos\left(\frac{x^{+}}{2R}\right)\ln\cos\left(\frac{x^{+}}{2R}\right)+\frac{x^{+}}{2R}\sin\left(\frac{x^{+}}{2R}\right)\right)\Bigg]\ .

(239d)

We can rewrite this covariantly as

\displaystyle\lambda^{01}

\displaystyle=\ln\cos\left(\frac{x^{+}}{2R}\right)\ ,

(240a)

\displaystyle\lambda^{0i}=-\lambda^{1i}=-\frac{1}{2\sqrt{2}R\sin\left(\frac{x^{+}}{2R}\right)}\Bigg[

\displaystyle x^{i}\left(\cos\left(\frac{x^{+}}{2R}\right)\ln\cos\left(\frac{x^{+}}{2R}\right)+\frac{x^{+}}{2R}\sin\left(\frac{x^{+}}{2R}\right)\right)

\displaystyle\hskip-42.67912pt+R\Omega_{ij}x^{j}\left(\sin\left(\frac{x^{+}}{2R}\right)\ln\cos\left(\frac{x^{+}}{2R}\right)-\frac{x^{+}}{2R}\cos\left(\frac{x^{+}}{2R}\right)\right)\Bigg]\ ,

\displaystyle\lambda^{ij}

\displaystyle=\frac{\Omega_{ij}x^{+}}{2}\ .

(240c)

A straightforward calculation shows that if we use extend this to 6d and use it as the generator for a 6d Lorentz group element we recover (

Appendix C
Normalisation of Scalar Two-Point Functions

C.1
3d Scalar Fields

Let us consider the action for a mode of a free complex scalar field,

S_{k}=2\pi R\int d^{3}x\Bigg[\frac{2ik}{R}\bar{\phi}_{k}\partial_{-}\phi_{k}+\bar{\phi}_{k}D^{2}\phi_{k}\Bigg]\ .

(241)

We will ultimately require
k\in\mathbb{Z}+\frac{1}{2}
but shall only constrain it to be real for now. The equation of motion of the mode is

\mathcal{L}_{k}\phi_{k}\equiv\frac{2ik}{R}\partial_{-}\phi_{k}+D^{2}\phi_{k}=0\ ,

(242)

which has the obvious spherically-symmetric solution

\phi_{k}=c_{k}\frac{1}{\sqrt{z\bar{z}}}\left(\frac{z}{\bar{z}}\right)^{k}

(243)

away from the origin. The time-ordered two-point function will satisfy the equation of motion away from coincident insertion points, so we will also have

G_{k}\equiv\bra{\Omega}T\{\phi_{k}(x^{-},x)\bar{\phi}_{k}(0)\}\ket{\Omega}=c_{k}\frac{1}{\sqrt{z\bar{z}}}\left(\frac{z}{\bar{z}}\right)^{k}

(244)

up to a time-ordering factor that we will include below. We would like to fix the constant
c_{k}
. Standard QFT lore tells us that the time-ordered two-point function is the Green’s function of
\mathcal{L}
, and we should have

\mathcal{L}_{k}G_{k}=\frac{i}{2\pi R}\delta(x^{-})\delta^{(2)}(x)\ .

(245)

Let us calculate the left hand side of the Green’s function equation. This is most easily done by introducing the coordinates

z=re^{i\theta}

(246)

with
r\in[0,\infty)
and
\theta\in(0,\pi)
. With these, integration of spherically-symmetric functions over spacetime takes the form

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}d^{3}x=4\pi R\int_{0}^{\infty}dr\int_{0}^{\pi}d\theta\,r\ ,

(247)

and the two-point function is

G_{k}=\frac{c_{k}F_{k}(\theta)e^{2ik\theta}}{r}\ .

(248)

We have introduced the time-ordering factor
F_{k}(\theta)
defined by

F_{k}(\theta)=\begin{cases}1&\theta\in(\alpha,\beta)\\
0&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}\ ,

(249)

which restricts us to the interval with endpoints
\alpha
and
\beta
. We will keep these arbitrary at this point.

The operator
\mathcal{L}_{k}
is given in
(r,\theta)
coordinates by

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{k}=

\displaystyle-\frac{i}{R}\Bigg[\frac{1}{2}\left(e^{i\theta}-e^{-i\theta}\right)\left(r\partial_{r}^{2}+\partial_{r}+\frac{1}{r}\partial_{\theta}^{2}\right)-e^{i\theta}\left(k-\frac{1}{2}\right)\left(\partial_{r}+\frac{i}{r}\partial_{\theta}\right)

\displaystyle-e^{-i\theta}\left(k+\frac{1}{2}\right)\left(\partial_{r}-\frac{i}{r}\partial_{\theta}\right)\Bigg]\ .

(250)

As
G_{k}
is not differentiable at
r=0
and
\theta=\alpha,\beta
, we need to introduce the notion of the distributional derivative

12

Friedlander_Joshi_2003
for an introduction to working with differentiation of distributions.

(251)

As

\mathcal{L}_{k}G

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{k}G[f]=-2\pi i\int_{0}^{\infty}dr\int_{0}^{\pi}d\theta\,G\bigg[

\displaystyle(e^{i\theta}-e^{-i\theta})\left(r^{2}\partial_{r}^{2}f+3r\partial_{r}f+\partial_{\theta}^{2}f\right)+2i(e^{i\theta}+e^{-i\theta})\partial_{\theta}f

\displaystyle+2i\left(e^{i\theta}\left(k-\frac{1}{2}\right)-e^{-i\theta}\left(k+\frac{1}{2}\right)\right)\partial_{\theta}f

\displaystyle+2\left(e^{i\theta}\left(k-\frac{1}{2}\right)+e^{-i\theta}\left(k+\frac{1}{2}\right)\right)r\partial_{r}f\bigg]\ .

(252)

We see that this expression makes sense even when

through the action on a test function

(\mathcal{L}_{k}G_{k}[f])_{\epsilon}=\int_{\epsilon}^{\infty}dr\int_{0}^{\pi}G_{k}\mathcal{L}_{k}^{{\dagger}}f\ ,

(253)

where
\mathcal{L}_{k}^{{\dagger}}

We can now subsitute the expression (

in this expression. As
G_{k}

\displaystyle(\mathcal{L}_{k}G_{k}[f])_{\epsilon}=-2\pi ic_{k}\int_{\epsilon}^{\infty}dr\int_{\alpha}^{\beta}d\theta\Bigg[

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial r}\bigg(\left(e^{i(2k+1)\theta}-e^{i(2k-1)\theta}\right)(r\partial_{r}f+2f)

\displaystyle+2\left(e^{i(2k+1)\theta}\left(k-\frac{1}{2}\right)+e^{i(2k-1)\theta}\left(k+\frac{1}{2}\right)\right)f\bigg)

\displaystyle+\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\bigg(\left(e^{i(2k+1)\theta}-e^{i(2k-1)\theta}\right)\frac{1}{r}\partial_{\theta}f\bigg)\Bigg]

(254)

which evaluates to

\displaystyle(\mathcal{L}_{k}G_{k}[f])_{\epsilon}=

\displaystyle 2\pi c_{k}\delta_{0}[f]\bigg[e^{i(2k+1)\theta}+e^{i(2k-1)\theta}\bigg]_{\theta=\alpha}^{\theta=\beta}

\displaystyle+4\pi c_{k}\int_{\epsilon}^{\infty}\frac{dr}{r}\bigg[e^{2ik\theta}\sin\theta\partial_{\theta}f\bigg]_{\theta=\alpha}^{\theta=\beta}+O(\epsilon)\ .

(255)

We see that for generic values of
\alpha
and
\beta

\epsilon\to 0

\alpha=0
and
\beta=\pi

\Theta

\mathcal{L}_{k}G_{k}=-4\pi c_{k}\left(1+e^{2\pi ik}\right)\delta(x^{-})\delta^{(2)}(x)\ .

(256)

For generic
k

245

c_{k}=-\frac{i}{8\pi^{2}R\left(1+e^{2\pi ik}\right)}

(257)

, which are precisely the values we are interested in!

To find the resolution of this, we need to think about the 4d origin of the theory; since
\ket{\Omega}
is the vacuum of a free Lorentzian scalar, it must be annihilated by all lowering operators. In particular, this applies to the modes arising from the reduction on
x^{+}
, as these are eigenstates of
p_{+}
. As discussed in
Lambert:2021nol
, there is a slight subtlety here: the split into raising and lowering operators on
\ket{\Omega}
is naturally defined for the modes of
\hat{\phi}
, which for the 3d theory are related to the modes of
\phi
by

\hat{\phi}_{k+\frac{1}{2}}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\phi_{k}+\phi_{k+1}\right)\ .

(258)

Requiring our theory makes sense as the reduction of a well-defined 4d theory imposes

\hat{\phi}_{l}\ket{\Omega}=\bar{\hat{\phi}}_{-l}\ket{\Omega}=0\ \forall\,l>0\ ,

(259)

which is equivalent to the conditions

\phi_{k}\ket{\Omega}=\bar{\phi}_{-k}\ket{\Omega}=0\ \forall\,k>-\frac{1}{2}\ .

(260)

Taking time-ordering with respect to
x^{-}
, we then have

\bra{\Omega}T\{\phi_{k}(x^{-},x)\bar{\phi}_{k}(0)\}\ket{\Omega}=\begin{cases}\Theta(x^{-})\bra{\Omega}\phi_{k}(x^{-},x)\bar{\phi}_{k}(0)\ket{\Omega}&k>0\\
\Theta(-x^{-})\bra{\Omega}\bar{\phi}_{k}(0)\phi_{k}(x^{-},x)\ket{\Omega}&k<0\end{cases}\ .

(261)

The two-point functions of the modes are related to the two-point function of the 4d field by

\bra{\Omega}T\{\hat{\phi}(\hat{x})\hat{\bar{\phi}}(0)\}\ket{\Omega}=\cos\left(\frac{x^{+}}{2R}\right)\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}+\frac{1}{2}}e^{-\frac{ikx^{+}}{R}}G_{k}(x^{-},x)\ .

(262)

The 4d field satisfies the Green’s function equation

\hat{\square}\bra{\Omega}T\{\hat{\phi}(\hat{x})\hat{\bar{\phi}}(0)\}\ket{\Omega}=i\delta^{(4)}(\hat{x})\ ,

(263)

where the d’Alembertian
\hat{\square}=\hat{\partial}^{2}
is given by

\hat{\square}=\cos^{2}\left(\frac{x^{+}}{2R}\right)\left(-2\left(\partial_{+}+\frac{\tan\left(\frac{x^{+}}{2R}\right)}{2R}\right)\partial_{-}+D^{2}\right)

(264)

after transforming to un-hatted coordinates. The factor of
\cos^{2}\left(\frac{x^{+}}{2R}\right)
is evaluated on a delta function, and is therefore unity. Substituting the expansion (
262
) into the equation (
263
) and using the periodic delta function

\delta(x^{+})=\frac{1}{2\pi R}\sum_{l\in\mathbb{Z}}e^{-\frac{ilx^{+}}{R}}

(265)

to reduce on
x^{+}
, we find the infinite tower of relations

\mathcal{L}_{l-\frac{1}{2}}G_{l-\frac{1}{2}}+\mathcal{L}_{l+\frac{1}{2}}G_{l+\frac{1}{2}}=\frac{i}{\pi R}\delta(x^{-})\delta^{(2)}(x)

(266)

for
l\in\mathbb{Z}
.

We see that the set of equations
266
impose weaker constraints than the set
245
. However, they share one important property. If we examine the equation for
l=0
away from
x^{-}=0
, we have

\mathcal{L}_{-\frac{1}{2}}G_{-\frac{1}{2}}+\mathcal{L}_{\frac{1}{2}}G_{\frac{1}{2}}=0\ .

(267)

However,
G_{\frac{1}{2}}
and
G_{-\frac{1}{2}}
are only non-trivial for non-overlapping ranges of
x^{-}
. This means that if we restrict our attention to
x^{-}>0
we find the equation

\mathcal{L}_{\frac{1}{2}}G_{\frac{1}{2}}=0\ ,

(268)

whereas if we focus on
x^{-}<0
we find the analogous statement for
G_{-\frac{1}{2}}
. This propagates through the tower of equations, and we see that all the two-point functions satisfy the classical equations of motion away from singularities.

We will now use the modified set of equation to fix
c_{k}
. It is necessary to consider the cases
l>0
,
l=0
, and
l<0
separately. Let us start with
l>0
, which encompasses the two-point functions with
k>0
. In this regime, both correlators will be of the form

G_{k}=\begin{cases}\frac{c_{k}e^{2ik\theta}}{r}&\theta\in\left(0,\frac{\pi}{2}\right)\\
0&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}\ .

(269)

Using (
C.1
), we have

(\mathcal{L}_{k}G_{k}[f])_{\epsilon}=-4c_{k}\pi\left(\delta_{0}[f]-\int_{\epsilon}^{\infty}\frac{dr}{r}\,e^{ik\pi}\left(\partial_{\theta}f\right)_{\theta=\frac{\pi}{2}}\right)+O(\epsilon)\ .

(270)

Since this means the left hand side of (
266
) becomes (after taking
\epsilon\to 0

\mathcal{L}_{l-\frac{1}{2}}G_{l-\frac{1}{2}}[f]+\mathcal{L}_{l+\frac{1}{2}}G_{l+\frac{1}{2}}[f]=-4\pi(c_{l-\frac{1}{2}}+c_{l+\frac{1}{2}})\delta_{0}[f]-4\pi i(c_{l-\frac{1}{2}}-c_{l+\frac{1}{2}})\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{dr}{r}e^{il\pi}\left(\partial_{\theta}f\right)_{\theta=\frac{\pi}{2}}\ ,

(271)

we see that we must take

c_{k}=-\frac{i}{8\pi^{2}R}

(272)

for
k>0
. The calculation for
l<0
is almost identical; as we have

G_{k}=\begin{cases}\frac{c_{k}e^{2ik\theta}}{r}&\theta\in\left(\frac{\pi}{2},\pi\right)\\
0&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

(273)

for
k<0
, (
C.1
) evaluates to

(\mathcal{L}_{k}G_{k}[f])_{\epsilon}=4\pi c_{k}\left(\delta_{0}[f]-\int_{\epsilon}^{\infty}\frac{dr}{r}\,e^{ik\pi}\left(\partial_{\theta}f\right)_{\theta=\frac{\pi}{2}}\right)+O(\epsilon)\ .

(274)

After taking
\epsilon\to 0
we see that (
266
) gives

\mathcal{L}_{l-\frac{1}{2}}G_{l-\frac{1}{2}}[f]+\mathcal{L}_{l+\frac{1}{2}}G_{l+\frac{1}{2}}[f]=4\pi(c_{l-\frac{1}{2}}+c_{l+\frac{1}{2}})\delta_{0}[f]+4\pi i(c_{l-\frac{1}{2}}-c_{l+\frac{1}{2}})\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{dr}{r}e^{il\pi}\left(\partial_{\theta}f\right)_{\theta=\frac{\pi}{2}}\ ,

(275)

so the conditions are satisfied if

c_{k}=\frac{i}{8\pi^{2}R}

(276)

for
k<0
. Finally, we must check the case
l=0
, which links the two previous results. Using our prior calculations we have

\mathcal{L}_{-\frac{1}{2}}G_{-\frac{1}{2}}[f]+\mathcal{L}_{\frac{1}{2}}G_{\frac{1}{2}}[f]=4\pi\left(c_{-\frac{1}{2}}-c_{\frac{1}{2}}\right)\delta_{0}[f]+4\pi i\left(c_{-\frac{1}{2}}+c_{\frac{1}{2}}\right)\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{dr}{r}\,\left(\partial_{\theta}f\right)_{\theta=\frac{\pi}{2}}\ .

(277)

Substituting (
272
) and (
276
) into this gives

\mathcal{L}_{-\frac{1}{2}}G_{-\frac{1}{2}}[f]+\mathcal{L}_{\frac{1}{2}}G_{\frac{1}{2}}[f]=\frac{i}{\pi R}\delta_{0}[f]\ ,

(278)

which is consistent with (
266
).

As a test of these results, we can resum (
262
) and check that we get the correct result. Using the
i\epsilon
prescription described in section
3.2
we find

\displaystyle\bra{\Omega}T\{\hat{\phi}(\hat{x})\hat{\bar{\phi}}(0)\}\ket{\Omega}

\displaystyle=-\frac{i\cos\left(\frac{x^{+}}{2R}\right)}{8\pi^{2}R}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\left(\Theta(x^{-})\left(\frac{ze^{-\frac{ix^{+}}{R}}}{\bar{z}}\right)^{k+\frac{1}{2}}-\Theta(-x^{-})\left(\frac{ze^{-\frac{ix^{+}}{R}}}{\bar{z}}\right)^{-\left(k+\frac{1}{2}\right)}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{i\cos\left(\frac{x^{+}}{2R}\right)}{8\pi^{2}R}\frac{1}{ze^{-\frac{ix^{+}}{2R}}-\bar{z}e^{\frac{ix^{+}}{2R}}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4\pi^{2}\left(-2\hat{x}^{+}\hat{x}^{-}+\hat{x}^{2}\right)}\ ,

(279)

which is the two-point function of a canonically normalised 4d massless scalar field, as hoped.

C.2
5d Scalar Fields

We can perform the exact same analysis for the reduction of a 6d scalar field to a 5d theory. We will use the same spherically-symmetric coordinates as in the 3d case. The two-point function obtained from the classical equations of motion is

G_{k}=\frac{c_{k}F_{k}(\theta)e^{2ik\theta}}{r^{2}}\ ,

(280)

where we will be interested in taking
k\in\mathbb{Z}
. Integrals over spherically-symmetric functions take the form

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{5}}d^{5}x=\left(4\pi R\right)^{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\pi}drd\theta\,r^{2}\sin\theta\ .

(281)

In these coordinates, the classical equation of motion is

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{k}G_{k}=\frac{-2i}{R}\Bigg[

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4}\left(e^{i\theta}-e^{-i\theta}\right)\left(r\partial_{r}^{2}G+\partial_{r}G+\frac{1}{r}\partial_{\theta}^{2}G\right)-\frac{1}{2}e^{i\theta}(k-1)\left(\partial_{r}G+\frac{i}{r}\partial_{\theta}G\right)

The same procedure as above allows us to define the regularised distributional derivative of

\displaystyle(\mathcal{L}_{k}G_{k}[f])_{\epsilon}=-4\pi^{2}R\int_{\epsilon}^{\infty}dr\int_{0}^{\pi}d\theta\,G_{k}\Bigg[

\displaystyle\left(e^{i\theta}-e^{-i\theta}\right)^{2}\left(r^{3}\partial_{r}^{2}f+5r^{2}\partial_{r}f+4rf+r\partial_{\theta}^{2}f\right)

\displaystyle+2(r^{2}\partial_{r}f+2rf)\left((k-1)(e^{2i\theta}-1)+(k+1)(1-e^{-2i\theta})\right)

\displaystyle+4i\left(e^{2i\theta}-e^{-2i\theta}\right)r\partial_{\theta}f-4\left(e^{2i\theta}+e^{-2i\theta}\right)rf

\displaystyle+2ir\partial_{\theta}f\Big((k-1)(e^{2i\theta}-1)-(k+1)(1-e^{-2i\theta})\Big)

\displaystyle-4rf\left((k-1)e^{2i\theta}-(k+1)e^{-2i\theta}\right)\Bigg]\ .

(283)

Subsituting in our expression for
G_{k}
gives

\displaystyle\left(\mathcal{L}_{k}G_{k}[f]\right)_{\epsilon}=-4\pi^{2}R\,c_{k}\int_{\epsilon}^{\infty}dr\int_{\alpha}^{\beta}d\theta\Bigg[

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial r}\bigg(e^{2i(k+1)\theta}\left(r\partial_{r}f+2(k+1)f\right)+e^{2i(k-1)\theta}\left(r\partial_{r}f-2(k-1)f\right)

\displaystyle-2e^{2ik\theta}\left(r\partial_{r}f+2f\right)\bigg)+\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\bigg(\frac{1}{r}\partial_{\theta}f\bigg(e^{2i(k+1)\theta}

\displaystyle+e^{2i(k-1)\theta}-2e^{2ik\theta}\bigg)\bigg)\Bigg]\ ,

(284)

which localises to boundary contributions as expected. When none of the exponents vanish (i.e.
k\neq-1,0,1

\left(\mathcal{L}_{k}G_{k}[f]\right)_{\epsilon}=8\pi^{2}R\,c_{k}\Bigg[e^{2ik\theta}\bigg(\delta_{0}[f]\left(\sin 2\theta+\frac{i}{k}\right)-\left(\cos 2\theta-1\right)\int_{\epsilon}^{\infty}\frac{dr}{r}\,\partial_{\theta}f\bigg)\Bigg]_{\theta=\alpha}^{\theta=\beta}+O(\epsilon)\ ,

(285)

with the exceptional cases

\displaystyle(\mathcal{L}_{1}G_{1})_{\epsilon}

\displaystyle=4\pi^{2}R\,c_{1}\Bigg[e^{2i\theta}\bigg(i\delta_{0}[f]\left(2-e^{2i\theta}\right)-2\left(\cos 2\theta-1\right)\int_{\epsilon}^{\infty}\frac{dr}{r}\,\partial_{\theta}f\bigg)\Bigg]_{\theta=\alpha}^{\theta=\beta}+O(\epsilon)\ ,

(286a)

\displaystyle(\mathcal{L}_{0}G_{0})_{\epsilon}

\displaystyle=8\pi^{2}R\,c_{0}\Bigg[\delta_{0}[f]\left(\sin 2\theta-2\theta\right)-\left(\cos 2\theta-1\right)\int_{\epsilon}^{\infty}\frac{dr}{r}\,\partial_{\theta}f\Bigg]_{\theta=\alpha}^{\theta=\beta}+O(\epsilon)\ ,

(286b)

\displaystyle(\mathcal{L}_{-1}G_{-1}[f])_{\epsilon}

\displaystyle=4\pi^{2}R\,c_{-1}\Bigg[e^{-2i\theta}\bigg(i\delta_{0}[f]\left(e^{-2i\theta}-2\right)-2\left(\cos 2\theta-1\right)\int_{\epsilon}^{\infty}\frac{dr}{r}\,\partial_{\theta}f\bigg)\Bigg]_{\theta=\alpha}^{\theta=\beta}+O(\epsilon)\ .

(286c)

For generic values of
k
, we can take
\alpha=0
and
\beta=\pi
to obtain (after taking the
\epsilon\to 0
limit)

\mathcal{L}_{k}G_{k}[f]=\frac{8\pi^{2}R\left(e^{2\pi ik}-1\right)}{k}c_{k}\delta_{0}[f]\ ,

(287)

so the Green’s function equation (
245
) is satisfied if we take

c_{k}=\frac{k}{16\pi^{3}R^{2}\left(e^{2\pi ik}-1\right)}\ .

(288)

However, like the 3d case this is not possible when
k\in\mathbb{Z}
, which is the case of interest.

Let us follow the same steps as before to obtain the correct equations for the tower of 5d two-point functions from the 6d Green’s function equation. The time-ordered 6d two-point function is given in terms of the modes by

\bra{\Omega}T\{\hat{\phi}(\hat{x}^{+},\hat{x}^{-},\hat{x})\bar{\hat{\phi}}(0)\}\ket{\Omega}=\cos^{2}\left(\frac{x^{+}}{2R}\right)\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}e^{-\frac{ikx^{+}}{R}}G_{k}(x^{-},x)\ .

(289)

The modes of
\hat{\phi}
are related to those of

\hat{\phi}_{k}=\frac{1}{4}\left(\phi_{k-1}+2\phi_{k}+\phi_{k+1}\right)\ ,

\phi_{k}\ket{\Omega}=\bar{\phi}_{-k}\ket{\Omega}=0\ \forall\,k>-1\ .

(291)

This means that the mode two-point functions are of the form

G_{k}(x^{-}.x)=\begin{cases}\Theta(x^{-})\bra{\Omega}\phi_{k}(x^{-},x)\bar{\phi}_{k}(0)\ket{\Omega}&k>0\\
0&k=0\\
\Theta(-x^{-})\bra{\Omega}\bar{\phi}_{k}(0)\phi_{k}(x^{-},x)\ket{\Omega}&k<0\end{cases}\ .

(292)

In particular, we immediately have the condition
c_{0}
= 0.

Acting with the 6d d’Alembertian

\hat{\square}=\cos^{2}\left(\frac{x^{+}}{2R}\right)\left(-2\left(\partial_{+}+\frac{1}{R}\tan\left(\frac{x^{+}}{2R}\right)\right)+D^{2}\right)

(293)

and rearranging, we see that the Green’s function equation

\hat{\square}\bra{\Omega}T\{\hat{\phi}(\hat{x}^{+},\hat{x}^{-},\hat{x})\bar{\hat{\phi}}(0)\}\ket{\Omega}=i\delta^{(6)}(\hat{x})

(294)

\mathcal{L}_{k-1}G_{k-1}+\mathcal{L}_{k+1}G_{k+1}+2\mathcal{L}_{k}G_{k}=\frac{2i}{\pi R}\delta(x^{-})\delta^{(4)}(x)

(295)

for

for

and
\alpha=\pi
,
\beta=\frac{\pi}{2}
for
k<0
