Abstract
In this paper, we consider estimation and inference for the unknown parameters and function involved in a class of generalized hierarchical models. Such models are of great interest in the literature of neural networks (such as Bauer and Kohler 2019 ). We propose a rectified linear unit (ReLU) based deep neural network (DNN) approach, and contribute to the design of DNN by i) providing more transparency for practical implementation, ii) defining different types of sparsity, iii) showing the differentiability, iv) pointing out the set of effective parameters, and v) offering a new variant of rectified linear activation function (ReLU), etc. Asymptotic properties are established accordingly, and a feasible procedure for the purpose of inference is also proposed. We conduct extensive numerical studies to examine the finite-sample performance of the estimation methods, and we also evaluate the empirical relevance and applicability of the proposed models and estimation methods to real data. Keywords : Estimation Theory; Deep Neural Network; Hierarchical Model; ReLU JEL classification : C14, C45, G12

Introduction

In recent decades, there has been a notable emphasis on deep neural networks (DNNs) in the literature of econometrics, (e.g.,
Chen and White 1999
;
Ai and Chen 2003
;
Shintani and Linton 2004
;
Farrell et al. 2021
). Initially applied in machine learning, DNNs have since expanded into various fields, such as economics, finance, social sciences, among others. Related to the applications of DNN,
LeCun et al. 2015
offer a comprehensive overview of practical topics, while
Athey 2019
discusses its capacity in social science. Additionally,
Bartlett et al. 2021
and
Fan et al. 2021
offer reviews on recent methodological advancements.

As the most important part of DNN, a variety of activation functions have been proposed and investigated theoretically and numerically (
Dubey et al. 2022
). The rectified linear activation function (ReLU) sees its popularity due to its simplicity and partial linearity:

\displaystyle\sigma(x)=x\vee 0\quad\text{with}\quad x\in\mathbb{R}.

ReLU is a piecewise linear function that will output the input directly if it is positive, otherwise, it will output zero. Compared to Sigmoid functions, ReLU has a low computational cost, which makes it efficient for large-scale neural networks practically.
Schmidt-Hieber 2020
and
Fan and Gu 2022
for example establish some fundamental results with respect to using ReLU.

However, there are still properties related to ReLU that remain unknown. To be more specific, we now define a simple DNN using ReLU for activation function, and then briefly review the relevant literature.

The current literature agrees that ReLU is designed to offer sparsity, which leads to computational efficiency (e.g.,
Hoefler et al. 2021
;
Glorot et al. 2011
). However, there have only been few efforts to explain how sparsity should be defined and why it occurs. To the best of our knowledge, there are already two types of sparsity involved: (1) non-active neurons and (2) parameters that are not effective. Additionally, the literature implicitly agrees that
\mathbf{W}_{m}
can be estimated through a minimization process. It is noteworthy that ReLU is piecewise linear, and it is not yet clear how to handle the accumulated (non-)differentiability through layers in both theory and practice. While the concern raised here actually exists in some well known software packages, to the best of our knowledge, no satisfactory treatment has been offered. We summarize a very brief survey about existing software packages in Appendix
A1
.

Moving on to our discussion about modelling, when it comes to practical analysis using DNN based models and methods, the existing literature of model building primarily focuses on fully nonparametric models (e.g.,
Chen and White 1999
and many extensions since then), with only a few mentions of semiparametric settings (e.g.,
Kohler and Krzyźak 2017
,
Bauer and Kohler 2019
, and references therein). It is not clear how to estimate and recover the parameters of interest for the generalized hierarchical models mentioned in Definition 1 of
Bauer and Kohler 2019
. There is a lack of investigations in this area of research, and it is also related to the (non-)differentiability of ReLU. As far as we know, these questions have not been thoroughly investigated.

Meanwhile, the current literature heavily focuses on independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) data (e.g.,
Bauer and Kohler 2019
;
Farrell et al. 2021
), while largely neglecting the implications of asymptotic properties when dealing with dependent data. This is especially significant for applications in the fields of finance and economics, such as those studied by
Gu et al. 2020
,
Chen et al. 2022
, and
Fallahgoul et al. 2024
, where accounting for dependence can pose challenges in constructing inference. The literature on this topic dates at least back to
Newey and West 1987
, with a comprehensive review provided by
Shao 2015
. In this paper, we aim to address this gap by training DNN with time series data, establishing asymptotic properties, and offering valid inference.

In what follows, in order to address the aforementioned concerns collectively, we follow the Definition 1 of
Bauer and Kohler 2019
and consider a class of generalized hierarchical models of the form: for
1\leq t\leq T
,

\displaystyle y_{t}=f_{\star}(\mathbf{z}_{1t}^{\top}\,\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\star 1},\ldots,\mathbf{z}_{rt}^{\top}\,\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\star r})+\varepsilon_{t}\equiv f_{\star}(x_{1t},\cdots,x_{rt})+\varepsilon_{t},

(1.2)

To the best of our knowledge, there have been no attempts to infer
(\theta_{\star 1},\cdots,\theta_{\star r})
in the relevant DNN literature, although
Kohler and Krzyźak 2017
and
Bauer and Kohler 2019
draw to hierarchical models. The main goals of this paper are to estimate both

In summary, this paper adds the following main contributions to the relevant literature:

1.

We enhance the design of DNN by i) providing more transparency for practical implementation, ii) defining different types of sparsity, iii) showing the differentiability, iv) pointing out the set of effective parameters, and v) offering a new variant of ReLU, etc.

2.

We investigate a class of generalized hierarchical models, and propose a ReLU based DNN approach for the unified estimation of both the parameters of interest and the unknown function.

3.

We allow our models and methods to be applicable to dependent time series data and then establish a valid implementable procedure for the purpose of inference.

4.

We conduct extensive numerical results to validate the theoretical findings before we also demonstrate the empirical relevance and applicability of the proposed model and estimation method to real data.

2.2
The Design

We present the design of neural network in this subsection, and establish some fundamental results regarding function approximation. First, we present the following lemma building on Definition
1.1
.

Lemma 2.1
.

For
\forall(x,y)\in[0,1]^{2}
, construct a DNN with
m+3
hidden layers:

\displaystyle\nn(\boldsymbol{\ell}^{m}_{x,y}\,|\,\mathbf{W}^{\star}_{m+3})\quad\text{with}\quad\mathbf{W}^{\star}_{m+3}=\{\mathbf{v}_{1}^{\star},\ldots,\mathbf{v}_{m+3}^{\star};\mathbf{w}_{1}^{\star},\ldots,\mathbf{w}_{m+3}^{\star}\},

where

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\ell}_{x,y}^{m}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{x-y+1}{2},\ x-y+1,\ x+y+2^{-m},\ \frac{x+y}{2},\ x+y,\ \frac{1}{2}\right)^{\top},

\displaystyle\mathbf{v}_{k}^{\star}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\mathbf{1}_{2}\otimes(0,2^{1-2k},0)^{\top}&\text{for }k\in[m+1]\\
-1&\text{for }k\in\{m+2,m+3\}\end{array}\right.,

\displaystyle\mathbf{w}_{k}^{\star}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\mathbf{I}_{2}\otimes\widetilde{\mathbf{w}}&\text{for }k\in[m]\\
(-1,1,-1,1-1,1)&\text{for }k=m+1\\
-1&\text{for }k=m+2\\
1&\text{for }k=m+3\end{array}\right.,

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathbf{w}}=\begin{pmatrix}\frac{1}{2}&-\frac{1}{2}&0\\
1&-1&0\\
1&-1&1\end{pmatrix}.

Here,
\nn(\boldsymbol{\ell}^{m}_{x,y}\,|\,\mathbf{W}^{\star}_{m+3})
is piecewise linear in
x
and
y
, and
\frac{\partial}{\partial x}[\nn(\boldsymbol{\ell}^{m}_{x,y}\,|\,\mathbf{W}^{\star}_{m+3})]

Let
\mathbb{C}_{m}=[0,1-2^{-m}]\times[0,1]
. The following results hold:

1.

0\leq\nn(\boldsymbol{\ell}^{m}_{x,y}\,|\,\mathbf{W}^{\star}_{m+3})\leq 1
on
[0,1]^{2}
, and
\nn(\boldsymbol{\ell}^{m}_{x,y}\,|\,\mathbf{W}^{\star}_{m+3})=1

on
\mathbb{C}_{m}
,

3.

\left\|\frac{\partial}{\partial x}[\nn(\boldsymbol{\ell}^{m}_{x,y}\,|\,\mathbf{W}^{\star}_{m+3})]-y\right\|_{\infty}^{\mathbb{C}_{m}}\leq 2^{-m-1}
.

In Lemma

\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\star}
varies with respect to
\mathbf{x}_{0}
. Although
h\to 0
is not required in Lemma
2.2
, it is essential to include the condition here from the perspective of function approximation. Building on Lemma
2.3
, for
\forall M\in\mathbb{N}
, we can subdivide
[-a,a]^{r}
into
M^{r}
cubes of side length
h=\frac{2a}{M}
. For comprehensibility, we label these cubes by
C_{\mathbf{x}_{\mathbf{i}}}
with
\mathbf{i}\in[M]^{r}
, where
\mathbf{x}_{\mathbf{i}}
represents the point at the bottom left corner of each cube. Mathematically,
C_{\mathbf{x}_{\mathbf{i}}}
is expressed as

\displaystyle C_{\mathbf{x}_{\mathbf{i}}}=\{\mathbf{x}\,|\,\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_{\mathbf{i}}\in[0,h]^{r}\}.

(2.3)

Using the partition, we further present the first theorem of this paper.

Theorem 2.1
.

Let Assumption
1
hold and
h\to 0
. There exists
\mathbf{B}_{\star}=\{\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\star\mathbf{i}}\,|\,\mathbf{i}\in[M]^{r}\}
such that

\displaystyle\|f_{\star}(\mathbf{x})-\mathscr{N}(\mathbf{x}\,|\,\mathbf{B}_{\star})\|_{\infty}^{[-a,a]^{r}}=O(h^{p}+2^{-m}),

where
\mathscr{N}(\mathbf{x}\,|\,\mathbf{B}_{\star})=\sum_{\mathbf{i}}I(\mathbf{x}\in C_{\mathbf{x}_{\mathbf{i}}})\cdot\mathbf{N}(\mathbf{x}\,|\,\mathbf{x}_{\mathbf{i}})^{\top}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\star\mathbf{i}}
.

Theorem
2.1
shows that we can recover
f_{\star}(\mathbf{x})
on
[-a,a]^{r}
via HDNN. Having Theorem 1 in hand, we are ready to work on the estimation of (
1.2
) using data. In both theory and practice, the partial derivatives of an unknown function are also of great interest, as they allow us to further calculate marginal effects of some important variables. Along this line, we present some useful discussion and Corollary
4.1
in Section
4
later.

3
Estimation and an Asymptotic Theory

In this section, we consider the estimation of (
1.2
). To facilitate the development, we define a few more symbols. Let

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Theta}=\{\boldsymbol{\theta}=(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{1}^{\top},\ldots,\boldsymbol{\theta}_{r}^{\top})^{\top}\,|\,\boldsymbol{\theta}_{j}\text{ is }d_{j}\times 1,\,\|\boldsymbol{\theta}_{j}\|=1,\,\theta_{j,1}>0,\,j\in[r]\},

(3.1)

where
\theta_{j,1}
stands for the
1^{st}
element of
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{j}
. As in (
1.3
), we write
(\mathbf{z}_{1t}^{\top}\,\boldsymbol{\theta}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{z}_{rt}^{\top}\,\boldsymbol{\theta}_{r})^{\top}=\mathbf{z}_{t}\,\boldsymbol{\theta}
when no misunderstanding arises.

Still, we partition
[-a,a]^{r}
into
M^{r}
cubes with side length
h=\frac{2a}{M}
, and work with
\{\mathbf{x}_{\mathbf{i}}\,|\,\mathbf{i}\in[M]^{r}\}
as in (
2.3
). To accommodate the structure of (
1.2
), for
\forall\boldsymbol{\theta}
we map
\{\mathbf{x}_{\mathbf{i}}\,|\,\mathbf{i}\in[M]^{r}\}
to
\{\mathbf{z}_{\mathbf{i}}\,|\,\mathbf{z}_{\mathbf{i}}=\diag\{\mathbf{x}_{\mathbf{i}}\}\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\diag},\mathbf{i}\in[M]^{r}\}
, where
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\diag}=\diag\{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{1}^{\top},\ldots,\boldsymbol{\theta}_{r}^{\top}\}
, and the dimension of
\mathbf{z}_{\mathbf{i}}
is obviously consistent with
\mathbf{z}_{t}
. We then group
\mathbf{z}_{t}
’s using the following sets:

\displaystyle C_{\mathbf{i}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\{\mathbf{z}\,|\,(\mathbf{z}-\mathbf{z}_{\mathbf{i}})\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\diag}^{\top}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\diag}\,\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\diag}^{\top})^{-1}\mathbf{1}_{r}\in[0,h]^{r}\}

(3.2)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\{\mathbf{z}\,|\,\mathbf{z}\,\boldsymbol{\theta}-\mathbf{x}_{\mathbf{i}}\in[0,h]^{r}\},

where the second equality follows from
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\diag}\,\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\diag}^{\top}=\mathbf{I}_{r}
and
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\diag}^{\top}\mathbf{1}_{r}=\boldsymbol{\theta}
by (
3.1
). Here,
C_{\mathbf{i}}
’s are equivalent to
C_{\mathbf{x}_{\mathbf{i}}}
’s of (
2.3
).

With these symbols in hand, we conduct the following minimization procedure:

\displaystyle(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}},\widehat{\mathbf{B}})=\argmin Q_{T}(\boldsymbol{\theta},\mathbf{B}),

(3.3)

where
Q_{T}(\boldsymbol{\theta},\mathbf{B})=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}[y_{t}-\mathscr{N}(\mathbf{z}_{t}\,\boldsymbol{\theta}\,|\,\mathbf{B})]^{2}
,
\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\boldsymbol{\Theta}
, and
\mathbf{B}=\{\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\mathbf{i}}\,|\,\|\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\mathbf{i}}\|<\infty\}
. By Theorem
2.1
, for
\forall\mathbf{x}_{0}\in[-a,a]^{r}
,
f_{\star}(\mathbf{x}_{0})
is naturally estimated by

\displaystyle\widehat{f}(\mathbf{x}_{0})=\mathscr{N}(\mathbf{x}_{0}\,|\,\widehat{\mathbf{B}}).

(3.4)

To facilitate the theoretical development, we impose the following conditions.

Assumption 2
.

1.

\{(\mathbf{z}_{t},\varepsilon_{t})\,|\,t\in[T]\}
are strictly stationary and
\alpha
-mixing with mixing coefficient

\displaystyle\alpha(k)=\sup_{A\in\mathcal{F}_{-\infty}^{0},B\in\mathcal{F}_{k}^{\infty}}|\Pr(A)\Pr(B)-\Pr(A\cap B)|\ \ \mbox{for $k\geq 1$}

satisfying
\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\alpha(k)^{\nu/(2+\nu)}<\infty
for some
\nu>0
, where
\mathcal{F}_{-\infty}^{0}
and
\mathcal{F}_{k}^{\infty}
are the
\sigma
-algebras generated by
\{(\mathbf{z}_{s},\varepsilon_{s})\,|\,s\leq 0\}
and
\{(\mathbf{z}_{s},\varepsilon_{s})\,|\,s\geq t\}
, respectively. In addition, suppose that almost surely
E[\varepsilon_{1}\,|\,\mathbf{z}_{1}]=0
,
E[\varepsilon_{1}^{2}\,|\,\mathbf{z}_{1}]=\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2}
, and
E[|\varepsilon_{1}|^{2+\nu}\,|\,\mathbf{z}_{1}]<\infty
.

2.

E[f_{\star}(\mathbf{z}_{1}\,\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\star})-f_{\star}(\mathbf{z}_{1}\,\boldsymbol{\theta})]^{2}
is uniquely minimized on
\boldsymbol{\Theta}
, and
\inf_{\boldsymbol{\Theta}\times[-a,a]^{r}}\phi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{w})\geq c_{0}>0
, where
\phi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{w})
defines the density function of
\mathbf{z}_{1}\,\boldsymbol{\theta}
, and is Lipschitz continuous on
[-a,a]^{r}
.

Assumption
2
.1 is standard (see, for example, Chapter 2 of
Fan and Yao 2003
; Chapter 2 and Appendix A of
Gao 2007
), and it requires strict stationarity. As alluded below model (
1.3
), under the strict stationarity, model (
1.3
) covers the dynamic case where a vector of lagged dependent variables of the form
\mathbf{Y}_{t-1}=\left(y_{t-1},\cdots,y_{t-p}\right)^{\top}
can be part of
\mathbf{z}_{t}
. In such dynamic cases, the relevant literature (see,
Tong 1990
;
Fan and Yao 2003
;
Gao 2007
;
Teräsvirta et al. 2010
, for example) shows that
y_{t}
of model (
1.3
) can be strictly stationary when
f_{\star}(\cdot)
satisfies
\lim_{\|\mathbf{x}\|\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\left|f_{\star}(\mathbf{x})\right|}{\|\mathbf{x}\|}<1
.

In Assumption
2
.2, the condition about
E[f_{\star}(\mathbf{z}_{1}\,\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\star})-f_{\star}(\mathbf{z}_{1}\,\boldsymbol{\theta})]^{2}
is necessary even in the case
f_{\star}(\cdot)
is fully known. As
f_{\star}(\cdot)
also needs to be estimated, we impose one more condition on
\phi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
, which can be easily justified. For example, if
\mathbf{z}_{1}\,\boldsymbol{\theta}
follows a multivariate normal/
t
distribution, the condition automatically holds. Notably, although we only infer
f_{\star}(\cdot)
on
[-a,a]^{r}
, it does not mean that
\mathbf{z}_{1}\,\boldsymbol{\theta}
has to belong to a compact set. In Section
6
, we discuss how to relax the condition on
a
being finite.

Using Assumption
2
, we present the consistency in the following lemma.

Lemma 3.1
.

Under Assumptions
1
-
2
, as
(h,Th^{r})\to(0,\infty)
,

1.

\frac{1}{M^{r}}\sum_{\mathbf{i}\in[M]^{r}}\|\mathbf{H}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{\mathbf{i}}-\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\star\mathbf{i}})\|^{2}=o_{P}(1)
,

2.

\|\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}-\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\star}\|=o_{P}(1)
,

where
\mathbf{H}=\diag\{h^{\|\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{1}\|_{1}},\ldots,h^{\|\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{r_{\vartheta}}\|_{1}}\}
with
\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{j}
’s being defined in Lemma
2.3
.

After we have established Lemma
3.1
, we recall the notation introduced in Section
1
before we establish the following asymptotic distribution in the second theorem of this paper.

Theorem 3.1
.

Suppose that
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{11}+\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{12}+\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{12}^{\top}
and
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{11}
are positive definite, where

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{11}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2}E[I_{a,1}\,\mathbf{f}^{(1)}_{\star}(\mathbf{z}_{1}\,\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\star})\,\widetilde{\mathbf{z}}_{1}\widetilde{\mathbf{z}}_{1}^{\top}\mathbf{f}^{(1)}_{\star}(\mathbf{z}_{1}\,\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\star})],

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{12}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lim_{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T-1}(1-t/T)E[\varepsilon_{1}\varepsilon_{1+t}\,I_{a,1}I_{a,1+t}\,\mathbf{f}^{(1)}_{\star}(\mathbf{z}_{1}\,\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\star})\,\widetilde{\mathbf{z}}_{1}\widetilde{\mathbf{z}}_{1+t}^{\top}\,\mathbf{f}^{(1)}_{\star}(\mathbf{z}_{1+t}\,\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\star})],

in which
\widetilde{\mathbf{z}}_{t}=\mathbf{z}_{t}^{\top}\mathbf{1}_{r}
.

Under Assumptions
1
and
2
, as
(h,Th^{r})\to(0,\infty)
, for
\forall\mathbf{x}_{0}\in[-a,a]^{r}

\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\mathbf{x}_{0}}^{-1/2}\mathbf{D}_{T}\left[\begin{pmatrix}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}-\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\star}\\
\widehat{f}(\mathbf{x}_{0})-f_{\star}(\mathbf{x}_{0})\end{pmatrix}+\mathbf{c}_{bias}+O_{P}(2^{-m})\right]\to_{D}N(\mathbf{0}_{d+1},\mathbf{I}_{d+1}),

(3.5)

where
\|\mathbf{c}_{\rm bias}\|=O_{P}(h^{p})
with its detailed form available from (
B3.1
) of the supplementary appendix,
\mathbf{D}_{T}=\diag\{\sqrt{T}\mathbf{I}_{d},\sqrt{Th^{r}}\}
, and

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\mathbf{x}_{0}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\diag\{\mathbf{I}_{d},\boldsymbol{\psi}_{r_{\vartheta}}(\mathbf{x}_{0})^{\top}\mathbf{H}^{-1}\}\cdot\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\cdot\diag\{\mathbf{I}_{d},\mathbf{H}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\psi}_{r_{\vartheta}}(\mathbf{x}_{0})\},

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Sigma}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\diag\{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{11}^{-1}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{11}+\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{12}+\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{12}^{\top})\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{11}^{-1},\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2}\mathbf{I}_{r_{\vartheta}}\}.

There are two bias terms involved in Theorem
3.1
. The term
2^{-m}
arises due to the use of ReLU, and it can be negligible as long as
m
is sufficiently large. Also,
m
is proportional to the number of layers, so it explains why increasing the layers of DNN can improve estimation accuracy exponentially. The term
\mathbf{c}_{\rm bias}
comes from the nonparametric nature of DNN. It is pointed out that the bias terms remain with the estimate of
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\star}
as well, because the approximation error term involved in the above Theorem 1 is not necessarily negligible asymptotically, unless a type of under-smoothing condition:
\sqrt{T}\left(h^{p}+2^{-m}\right)=o(1)
, is imposed.

In addition to the involvement of two bias terms in equation (
3.5
), a long-run covariance matrix is involved in
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{12}
. These terms make the asymptotic distribution in (
3.5
) infeasible in practice. Therefore, we propose a bootstrap procedure for the purpose of inference for
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\star}
.

1.

For each bootstrap replication, we draw
\ell
-dependent time series
\{\eta_{t}\,|\,t\in[T]\}
. where
E[\eta_{t}]=0
,
E[\eta_{t}^{2}]=1
,
E[\eta_{t}^{4}]<\infty
,
E[\eta_{t}\eta_{s}]=a\left(\frac{t-s}{\ell}\right)
,
(\frac{1}{\ell},\frac{\ell}{\sqrt{T}})\to(0,0)
,
a(\cdot)
is a symmetric kernel defined on
[-1,1]
satisfying that
a(0)=1
and
K_{a}(x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}a(u)e^{-iux}du\geq 0
for
x\in\mathbb{R}
.

2.

Construct
y_{t}^{*}=\mathscr{N}(\mathbf{z}_{t}\,\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\,|\,\widehat{\mathbf{B}})+\widehat{\varepsilon}_{t}\cdot\eta_{t}
, where
\widehat{\varepsilon}_{t}=y_{t}-\mathscr{N}(\mathbf{z}_{t}\,\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\,|\,\widehat{\mathbf{B}})
. Conduct estimation using
\{y_{t}^{*},\mathbf{z}_{t}\}
as in (
3.3
) to obtain
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{*}
.

3.

Repeat Steps 1 and 2
R
times, where
R
is sufficiently large.

The condition
K_{a}(x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}a(u)e^{-iux}du\geq 0
for
x\in\mathbb{R}
essentially regulates the kernel function, which together with other restrictions imposed on
a(\cdot)
are satisfied by a few commonly used kernels, such as the Bartlett and Parzen kernels. More choices of the kernel function can be found in
Andrews 1991
and
Shao 2010
for example. To get
\eta_{t}
’s, one may use
N(\mathbf{0},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\eta})
with
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\eta}=\{a\left(\frac{t-s}{\ell}\right)\}_{T\times T}
for ease of implementation.

Corollary 3.1
.

Let the conditions of Theorem
3.1
hold. If, in addition,
\sqrt{T}(h^{p}+2^{-m})\to 0
as
(T,m)\rightarrow(\infty,\infty)
, then we have for
\forall\mathbf{x}_{0}\in[-a,a]^{r}
,

\displaystyle\sup_{\mathbf{w}}\left|\text{Pr}\left\{\sqrt{T}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}-\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\star})\leq\mathbf{w}\right\}-\text{Pr}^{*}\left\{\sqrt{T}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{*}-\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}})\leq\mathbf{w}\right\}\right|=o_{P}(1),

where
\text{Pr}^{*}
is the probability measure induced by the bootstrap procedure.

Because the biases come from diverse and intricate resources, we require a under-smoothing condition (i.e.,
\sqrt{T}(h^{p}+2^{-m})\to 0
) in Corollary
3.1
. Up to this point, we have completed our theoretical development for model (
1.2
).

4
Extensions

Before we conduct simulation results and discuss real data analysis, we discuss about how to smooth ReLU and also how to estimate marginal effects in this section.

Smoothed ReLU
— In order to improve the finite sample performance of ReLU, many variants have been proposed, such as Leaky ReLU, parametric ReLU, Gaussian-error linear unit (GELU), etc. The rise of these variants is most likely due to the (non)–differentiability. See
Dubey et al. 2022
for a comprehensive review. Here, although it is not the main focus of the paper, we also offer a new variant, i.e., smoothed ReLU.

We first introduce an assumption.

Assumption 3
.

Suppose that
\phi(\cdot)
is nonnegative,
\int\phi(u)dv=1
, and
\int|u|\phi(u)du<\infty
.

It is readily seen that conventional density functions satisfy this assumption. Then for positive integer
s
, we define

\sigma_{s}(u)=\int\sigma(x)\phi_{s}(x-u)dx\quad\text{with}\quad\phi_{s}(u)=s\phi(su).

(4.1)

The function sequence
\{\sigma_{s}(u)\}
is called a regular sequence of
\sigma(u)
. For
\sigma_{s}(u)
, the following lemma holds.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let Assumption
3
hold.

1.

\sigma_{s}(u),\ s=1,2,\cdots,
are smooth, and

\|\sigma_{s}(u)-\sigma(u)\|_{\infty}^{\mathbb{R}}\leq c\cdot s^{-1}

for some absolute constant
c
.

2.

Furthermore, let
\phi(\cdot)
be defined on
[-1,1]
, and be symmetric. Then we obtain that

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}0\leq\sigma_{s}(u)-\sigma(u)\leq O(1)s^{-1},&\text{ for }|u|\leq s^{-1},\\
\\
\sigma_{s}(u)-\sigma(u)=0,&\text{ for }|u|\geq s^{-1}.\end{array}\right.

It is worth emphasizing that
\sigma_{s}(u)
’s of Lemma
4.1
are completely independent of the results in Sections
2
and
3
. Therefore, using triangle inequality and letting
s
be sufficiently large. We can easily extend the results of Sections
2
and
3
via this lemma. Here, we basically trade smoothness with an additional approximation error due to the use of
\sigma_{s}(u)
’s. In Section
A2.1
, we further draw Figure
A4
to demonstrate this lemma.

Marginal Effects
— Lemma
2.1
shows the feasibility of derivatives, which may enable us to compute the marginal effects of different key variables. For example, we can have the following corollary immediately.

Corollary 4.1
.

Let
h\to 0
and
p>r
. Under Assumption 1, for
\forall\mathbf{x}_{0}\in[-a,a)^{r}
, there exits
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\star}
such that for
\forall\boldsymbol{\delta}=(\delta_{1},\ldots,\delta_{r})^{\top}
with
\delta_{j}\in\{0,1\}
and
j\in[r]
,

\displaystyle\|f_{\star}^{(\boldsymbol{\delta})}(\mathbf{x})-\mathbf{N}_{\boldsymbol{\delta}}(\mathbf{x}\,|\,\mathbf{x}_{0})^{\top}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\star}\|_{\infty}^{C_{\mathbf{x}_{0},h}}=O(h^{p-\|\boldsymbol{\delta}\|_{1}}+2^{-m}),

where

\displaystyle\mathbf{N}_{\boldsymbol{\delta}}(\mathbf{x}\,|\,\mathbf{x}_{0})=\begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{1}^{\boldsymbol{\delta}}\,\mathcal{N}_{\boldsymbol{\ell}^{m}}(\boldsymbol{\ell}_{\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_{0},\,\overline{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{1}}\,|\,\mathbf{W}^{\star}_{m+3})\\
\vdots\\
\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{r_{\vartheta}}^{\boldsymbol{\delta}}\,\mathcal{N}_{\boldsymbol{\ell}^{m}}(\boldsymbol{\ell}_{\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_{0},\,\overline{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{r_{\vartheta}}}\,|\,\mathbf{W}^{\star}_{m+3})\end{pmatrix}\quad\text{and}\quad\overline{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{j}=\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\delta}}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{j}).

In a similar fashion to the establishment of Theorem
3.1
, we can estimate the marginal effects consistently.

5.2
A Real Data Analysis

In this subsection, we examine the bond return predictability. Understanding the predictability of bond returns has always been a central topic in finance (e.g.,
Ludvigson and Ng 2009
;
Andreasen et al. 2020
;
Borup et al. 2023
). The literature seems to agree that excess bond returns are forecastable by financial indicators such as yield spreads (
Andreasen et al. 2020
), while also acknowledges the nonlinearity from modelling perspective (
Borup et al. 2023
). In addition, to avoid any variable selection problem, one often conducts principal component analysis (PCA) to collect some key factors from a large group of macro variables to be the key predictors of the forecasting model (
Ludvigson and Ng 2009
). The above features naturally fit our example of Section
4
.

We consider two benchmark models in this section:

\displaystyle y_{t+1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle a+\varepsilon_{t+1},

(5.2)

\displaystyle y_{t+1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle a+\mathbf{x}_{t}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\beta}+\varepsilon_{t+1},

(5.3)

where
y_{t+1}
denotes the 1-month log excess holding period return on
a 2-month zero-coupon treasury bond. Here (
5.2
) is a simple constant mean model, while (
5.3
) is a typical one step ahead linear model including some key predictors. Specifically,
\mathbf{x}_{t}
includes a set of variables adopted in
Borup et al. 2023
and covers the period from December 1961 to December 2018: 1) yields spreads computed as the difference between the yield on a bond with 2 months to maturity and the implied yield on a one-month treasury bill obtained from CRSP; 2) forward spreads computed as the difference between the 2-month forward rate and the one-month yield; 3) the first principal component (PC) of yields obtained using 12, 24, 36, 48, and 60 month yields; 4) a linear combination of forward rates obtained from projecting 12, 24, 36, 48, and 60 month forward rates onto the mean excess bond return across the maturity spectrum; and 5) a linear combination of macroeconomic factors obtained using the FRED-MD database and estimated analogously to CP. As the first two predictors are observed directly, they form
\mathbf{z}_{1t}
. The last three predictors are obtained from estimation, so they form
\mathbf{z}_{2t}
.

Having the dataset ready, we first partition the data into a training set
S_{tr}
(data from 1961 to 2011) and a test set
S_{te}
(data from 2012 and 2018) (referred to as Partition I). For each model, we run regression using training set only to obtain the parameter estimates, and calculate
\widehat{y}_{t}
for the test set. For the DNN approach, we also let
m=3,4,5
to examine the impact of different layers practically.

To evaluate the performance of different methods, we calculate

\displaystyle\text{RMSE}=\left\{\frac{1}{\sharp S_{te}}\sum_{t\in S_{te}}(\widehat{y}_{t}-y_{t})^{2}\right\}^{1/2}\quad\text{and}\quad\text{CS}=\frac{1}{\sharp S_{te}}\sum_{t\in S_{te}}I(\widehat{y}_{t}\cdot y_{t}>0),

(5.4)

where
\sharp S_{te}
stands for the cardinality of
S_{te}
. The first measure is a typical root mean squared errors based on the test set, and the second measure examines the sign prediction and reports the percentage of correct sign prediction. There is a vast literature regarding sign prediction (e.g.,
Christoffersen and Diebold 2006
and many extensions since then), which argues that return is not predictable in general, but one may better forecast the sign of return. Although predicting the sign is not the focus of the paper, it is interesting to see how DNN performs practically along this line of research.

6
Conclusion with Discussion

Before concluding, we provide a few useful remarks. Note that we require
f_{\star}(\mathbf{x})
to be defined on a compact set, but do not impose restriction on the range of
\{\mathbf{z}_{t}\}
. In fact, for time series data, it may make more sense to assume that
a
is diverging, which is indeed achievable. Suppose that
\mathbf{z}_{t}
follows a sub-Gaussian distribution. In this case, after some algebra we can relax the condition on
a
to
\sqrt{\log(Th^{r})}\cdot a\to\infty.
Apparently, there is a price that we have to pay, which is the slow rate of convergence. A similar treatment has also been discussed in
Li et al. 2016
for example, so we do not further elaborate it here.

The total number of layers that we require for Theorem
2.1
is

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}(m+3)\cdot\lceil\log_{2}r\rceil&\text{for }r\geq 2\\
m+3&\text{for }r=1\end{array}\right..

The width of most layers in the hierarchy is 6. The sparsity occurs naturally in view of Definition
2.1
and Lemma
2.1
.

Connection with Some Existing Studies
— Recently,
Du et al. 2021
and
Farrell et al. 2021
apply DNN to study treatment effects using micro datasets, and
Keane and Neal 2020
apply DNN to study climate data. Our proposed model and method can be extendable to related topics, and the proposed estimation method offers a simple implementational procedure that complements existing studies. Specifically, we have a clear understanding of the nonlinear minimization process.

To conclude, we consider the estimation of both the parameters of interest and the unknown function involved in a class of hierarchical models in the literature of DNN. We contribute to the design of DNN by i) providing more transparency for practical implementation, ii) defining different types of sparsity, iii) showing the differentiability, iv) pointing out the set of effective parameters, and v) offering a new variant of rectified linear activation function (ReLU), etc. The asymptotic properties of the proposed estimates are derived accordingly, and they can be applied to a wider class of non- and semi-parametric models. Finally, we conduct extensive numerical studies to examine the theoretical findings.

\displaystyle g(\ell/2\cdot 2^{-m})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{R}_{m}(\ell\cdot 2^{-m-1})\|_{1}

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{R}_{m+1}(\ell\cdot 2^{-m-1})\|_{1},

) and

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-u\int_{-1}^{-su}\phi(x)dx+\frac{1}{s}\int_{-su}^{1}x\phi(x)dx,

where the first equality follows from the definition of
\sigma(\cdot)
. Note further that if
su\geq 1
(i.e.,
u\geq s^{-1}
), we obtain that

\displaystyle\sigma_{s}(u)-\sigma(u)=0

by the definition of
\phi(\cdot)
. Therefore, it remains to consider the case
0\leq su\leq 1
(i.e.,
0\leq u\leq\frac{1}{s}
), then it is obvious that

\displaystyle\sigma_{s}(u)-\sigma(u)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-u\int_{-1}^{-su}\phi(x)dx+\frac{1}{s}\int_{-su}^{su}x\phi(x)dx+\frac{1}{s}\int_{su}^{1}x\phi(x)dx

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-u\int_{-1}^{-su}\phi(x)dx+\frac{1}{s}\int_{su}^{1}x\phi(x)dx

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle u\int_{1}^{su}\phi(x)dx+\frac{1}{s}\int_{su}^{1}x\phi(x)dx

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{su}^{1}\frac{x-su}{s}\phi(x)dx,

where the second equality follows from
\phi(x)
being symmetric, and the third equality follows from integration by substitution. As
\phi(x)
is nonnegative by Assumption
3
, it immediately yields that

\displaystyle 0\leq\sigma_{s}(u)-\sigma(u)\leq O(1)\frac{1}{s}.

For case (ii), it is easy to know that

\displaystyle\sigma_{s}(u)-\sigma(u)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{s}\int_{-su}^{1}x\phi(x)dx.

For
su\leq-1
(i.e.,
u\leq-s^{-1}
), we have

\displaystyle\sigma_{s}(u)-\sigma(u)=0

by the definition of
\phi(\cdot)
. Thus, it remains to consider
-1\leq su\leq 0
(i.e.,
-\frac{1}{s}\leq u\leq 0
), then it is obvious that

\displaystyle 0\leq\sigma_{s}(u)-\sigma(u)\leq O(1)\frac{1}{s}.

Collecting the results for both cases (i) and (ii), we conclude that for
|u|\leq s^{-1}

\displaystyle 0\leq\sigma_{s}(u)-\sigma(u)\leq O(1)s^{-1},

and for
|u|>s^{-1}

\displaystyle\sigma_{s}(u)-\sigma(u)=0.

The proof is now completed.
\blacksquare
