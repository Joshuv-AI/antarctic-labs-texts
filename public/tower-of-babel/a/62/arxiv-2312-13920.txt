Abstract
We introduce and study the notion of orthogonality for two operators in the context of weighted backward shifts on ℓ p ​ ( ℤ + ) \ell_{p}(\mathbb{Z}_{+}) , 1 ≤ p < ∞ 1\leq p<\infty . Two continuous linear operators T 1 T_{1} and T 2 T_{2} acting on a Polish topological vector space X X are said to be orthogonal if any two Borel probability measures m 1 m_{1} and m 2 m_{2} on X X which are respectively T 1 T_{1}\, - invariant and T 2 T_{2}\, - invariant and satisfy m 1 ​ ( { 0 } ) = m 2 ​ ( { 0 } ) = 0 m_{1}(\{0\})=m_{2}(\{0\})=0 must be orthogonal. In this note, we provide several conditions on the weights 𝒖 {\boldsymbol{u}} and 𝒗 {\boldsymbol{v}} implying orthogonality or non-orthogonality of the associated weighted shifts B 𝒖 B_{\boldsymbol{u}} and B 𝒗 B_{\boldsymbol{v}} , and we investigate in some detail the case where the invariant measures are product measures.

1.
Introduction

1.1.
Weighted shifts

This note is a contribution to the study of the dynamics of weighted backward shifts acting on
\ell_{p}\,
- spaces,
1\leq p<\infty
. More specifically, we will be concerned with
invariant measures
for such weighted shifts.

Let
X
be one of the (real or complex) Banach spaces
\ell_{p}=\ell_{p}(\mathbb{Z}_{+})
,
1\leq p<\infty
, and denote by
(e_{n})_{n\geq 0}
the canonical basis of
X
. Let
\boldsymbol{w}=(w_{n})_{n\geq 1}
be a
weight sequence
,
i.e.
a bounded sequence of non-zero scalars.The backward shift associated with
\boldsymbol{w}
is the operator
B_{\boldsymbol{w}}:X\to X
defined by
B_{\boldsymbol{w}}e_{0}:=0
and
B_{\boldsymbol{w}}e_{n}:=w_{n}e_{n-1}
for
n\geq 1
. Weighted shift operators occupy a central place in operator theory. On the one hand, their explicit form permits a systematic study, so that they are natural “test” operators for any question that comes to mind. On the other hand, their theory is quite rich, and their behaviour remains in some aspects rather mysterious. We refer the reader to the classical paper
[
22
]
, which proposes a systematic study of weighted shifts including their representations as multiplication operators on certain Banach spaces of holomorphic functions and their spectral properties, and to the authoritative book
[
18
]
, which deals with the study of cyclic vectors for the unweighted backward shift
B
on
\ell_{2}(\mathbb{Z}_{+})
.

Many important dynamical properties are completely characterized for weighted shifts
B_{\boldsymbol{w}}
and can be expressed in a simple way in terms of the weights
w_{n}
, see e.g
[
19
]
,
[
20
]
,
[
12
]
,
[
7
]
,
[
8
]
. For instance,
B_{\boldsymbol{w}}
is
hypercyclic
,
i.e.
it admits a vector with a dense orbit, if and only if

\overline{\lim}_{n\to\infty}\,|w_{1}\cdots w_{n}|=\infty
, and it is
topologically mixing
if and only if
|{w_{1}\cdots w_{n}}|\to\infty
as
n\to\infty
. See
[
16
]
for proofs of these results as well as extensions to a large class of sequence spaces. Observe that these characterizations are the same on any
\ell_{p}
. By contrast,
chaoticity
of a weighted shift on
X=\ell_{p}
depends explicitely on
p
(recall that an operator is chaotic if it is hypercyclic with a dense set of periodic points):
B_{\boldsymbol{w}}
is chaotic on
\ell_{p}
if and only
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{|w_{1}\cdots w_{n}|^{p}}<\infty
. For the class of weighted shift operators of
\ell_{p}
, chaos is an especially important notion since it turns out to be equivalent to strong dynamical properties such as
frequent hypercyclicity
(the existence of a vector whose orbit visits each non-empty open set along a set of integers having positive lower density) or
\mathcal{U}
- frequent hypercyclicity
(same as frequent hypercyclicity with lower density replaced by upper density). Indeed, by an important result of Bayart and Ruzsa
[
6
]
, frequent hypercyclicity and
\mathcal{U}
- frequent hypercyclicity of a weighted shift
B_{\boldsymbol{w}}
on
\ell_{p}
are both equivalent to the condition
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{|w_{1}\cdots w_{n}|^{p}}<\infty
.
We refer the reader to the books
[
4
]
and
[
16
]
for more on frequent hypercyclicity and related questions, and to the paper
[
11
]
for a study of the relation between chaos and frequent hypercyclicity for weighted shifts on a large class of Fréchet sequence spaces.

The condition
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{|w_{1}\cdots w_{n}|^{p}}<\infty
also turns out to be of special importance in the study of invariant measures for the weighted backward shift
B_{\boldsymbol{w}}
. Recall that a Borel probability measure
m
on
X
is said to be
B_{\boldsymbol{w}}\,
- invariant if

m\bigl(B_{\boldsymbol{w}}^{-1}(A)\bigr)=m(A)
for every Borel set
A\subseteq X
. Under the condition
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{|w_{1}\cdots w_{n}|^{p}}<\infty
, the operator
B_{\boldsymbol{w}}
acting on
X=\ell_{p}
admits plenty of invariant measures, of quite different kinds: discrete measures associated with periodic points, Gaussian measures with full support (with respect to some of which
B_{\boldsymbol{w}}
is ergodic or even strongly mixing), continuous measures with full support which are very far from being Gaussian, … See
[
2
]
or
[
4
]
*Chapter 5 for more information on (shift-invariant) Gaussian measures; and
[
15
]
, which testifies of the richness of the class of invariant measures for backward shifts by showing that under the condition
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{|w_{1}\cdots w_{n}|^{p}}<\infty
, the backward shift
B_{\boldsymbol{w}}
is
universal for ergodic system
in the sense of Glasner and Weiss
[
14
]
: for every ergodic transformation
T
on a standard Lebesgue probability space
(Z,\mathcal{B},\mu)
, there exists a
B_{\boldsymbol{w}}\,
- invariant Borel probability measure
m
on
X=\ell_{p}
with full support such that the two dynamical systems
(Z,\mathcal{B},\mu;T)
and
(X,\mathcal{B}_{X},m;B_{\boldsymbol{w}})
are isomorphic (where
\mathcal{B}_{X}
is the Borel
\sigma
-algebra of
X
).

Invariant measures for weighted backward shifts are thus far from being completely understood (and in some sense, they will never be). In this note, our aim is to contribute to their understanding by studying possible links between invariant measures for
two different
weighted shifts
B_{\boldsymbol{u}}
and
B_{\boldsymbol{v}}
. More specifically, we will be concerned with the notion of
orthogonality
of two weighted shifts.

1.2.
Orthogonality

Before giving the definition we will be playing with, we fix some notation. The scalar field is denoted by
\mathbb{K}
. All measures on
X=\ell_{p}(\mathbb{Z}_{+})
are understood to be Borel probability measures. Given a weight sequence
\boldsymbol{w}
, we denote by
\mathcal{P}_{\boldsymbol{w}}(X)
the set of all
B_{\boldsymbol{w}}\,
- invariant measures on
X
. If
m
and
m^{\prime}
are two measures on
X
, we write
m\perp m^{\prime}
to indicate that
m
and
m^{\prime}
are orthogonal (
i.e.
mutually singular), and
m\ll m^{\prime}
to indicate that
m
is absolutely continuous with respect to
m^{\prime}
. If
m\ll m^{\prime}
and
m^{\prime}\ll m
, we say that
m
and
m^{\prime}
are equivalent and we write
m\sim m^{\prime}
.

We now define orthogonality of two weighted backward shifts.

Definition 1.1
.

Two weighted backward shifts
B_{\boldsymbol{u}}
and
B_{\boldsymbol{v}}
on
X=\ell_{p}
are said to be
orthogonal
if the following holds true: whenever
m_{\boldsymbol{u}}\in\mathcal{P}_{\boldsymbol{u}}(X)
,
m_{\boldsymbol{v}}\in\mathcal{P}_{\boldsymbol{v}}(X)
and
m_{\boldsymbol{u}}(\{0\})=0=m_{\boldsymbol{v}}(\{0\})
, it follows that
m_{\boldsymbol{u}}\perp m_{\boldsymbol{v}}
.

Removing the
\delta_{0}\,
- parts of the measures, we see that this definition can be reformulated as follows:
B_{\boldsymbol{u}}
and
B_{\boldsymbol{v}}
are orthogonal if and only if whenever
m_{\boldsymbol{u}}\in\mathcal{P}_{\boldsymbol{u}}(X)
and
m_{\boldsymbol{v}}\in\mathcal{P}_{\boldsymbol{v}}(X)
are non-orthogonal, it must be that
m_{\boldsymbol{u}}(\{0\})>0

and

m_{\boldsymbol{v}}(\{0\})>0
. Equivalently: there exists no (Borel probability) measure on
X
which is absolutely continuous with respect to both a
B_{\boldsymbol{u}}\,
- invariant measure and a
B_{\boldsymbol{v}}\,
- invariant measure, except the Dirac mass
\delta_{0}
. Thus,
informally speaking,
B_{\boldsymbol{u}}
and
B_{\boldsymbol{v}}
are orthogonal if their invariant measures “have nothing to say to each other”, unless they charge the singleton
\{0\}
. The main question we want to investigate is the following.

Question 1.2
.

Characterize the pairs of weight sequences
(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v})
such that the associated weighted shifts
B_{\boldsymbol{u}}
and
B_{\boldsymbol{v}}
acting on
X=\ell_{p}
are orthogonal.

The motivation for looking at this question comes from a recent work of Charpentier, Ernst, Mestiri and Mouze
[
10
]
. Let
B
be the unweighted backward shift acting on the complex Hilbert space
\ell_{2}(\mathbb{Z}_{+})
, and let
\Lambda\subseteq\mathbb{C}
. In
[
10
]
, it is shown that the operators
\lambda B
,
\lambda\in\Lambda
admit a
common frequently hypercyclic vector
if and only if the set
\{|\lambda|;\;\lambda\in\Lambda\}
is a countable relatively compact subset of
(1,\infty)
. In particular, if
a,b\in\mathbb{C}
and
|a|,|b|>1
, then the operators
aB
and
bB
have a common frequently hypercyclic vector. This is by no means obvious if
|a|\neq|b|
. So it is tempting to ask whether this result could be retrieved in a “soft” way by measure-theoretic arguments. Indeed, imagine that it were possible to find two measures
m_{a}
and
m_{b}
on
\ell_{2}
, with full support, invariant and
ergodic
for
aB
and
bB
respectively, such that one of them is absolutely continuous with respect to the other, say
m_{a}\ll m_{b}
. By the pointwise ergodic theorem, it would follow that
m_{a}\,
- almost every
x\in\ell_{2}
is frequently hypercyclic for
aB
and
m_{b}\,
- almost every
x\in\ell_{2}
is frequently hypercyclic for
bB
; hence
m_{a}\,
- almost every
x
would be frequently hypercyclic for both
aB
and
bB
since
m_{a}\ll m_{b}
, so one could conclude in particular that
aB
and
bB
have a common frequently hypercyclic vector. However, such measures
m_{a}
and
m_{b}
simply do not exist: we will see below that the operators
aB
and
bB
are in fact orthogonal.

1.3.
Organization of the paper

In Section
2
, we complement existing results by showing that a weighted backward shift
B_{\boldsymbol{w}}
on
X=\ell_{p}(\mathbb{Z}_{+})
admits a non-trivial invariant measure
if and only if

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{|{w_{1}\cdots w_{n}}|^{p}}<\infty
. In Section
3
, we define orthogonality in a very general context, we give a simple condition ensuring orthogonality (Theorem
3.2
), and we use this to show, among other things, that if
T
is any continuous linear operator acting on a Polish topological vector space
X
and
a,b\in\mathbb{K}
are such that
|a|\neq|b|
, then the operators
aT
and
bT
are orthogonal (Corollary
3.6
). We also deduce from our criterion that two weighted shifts that are “far from being similar” must be orthogonal (Theorem
3.10
). In Section
4
, we use
periodic vectors
to give examples of non-orthogonal weighted shifts; in particular, we show that there exist non-orthogonal weighted shifts which are not similar (Example
4.5
). Section
5
is devoted to invariant
product measures
for weighted shifts acting on
\ell_{p}
, and their role in our study of orthogonality. We give a necessary and sufficient condition for two weighted shifts
B_{\boldsymbol{u}}
and
B_{\boldsymbol{v}}
to admit equivalent invariant Gaussian product measures (Corollary
5.8
), and then we examine to what extent the existence of non-orthogonal invariant product measures implies the existence of equivalent Gaussian product measures (Theorem
5.13
and Proposition
5.22
). Section
6
contains some additional facts. Some of these facts are actually used in earlier proofs, but we prefered to postpone them for the sake of fluid reading. We conclude the paper with a few natural questions.

2.
Existence of non-trivial invariant measures

It is, of course, not very interesting to study orthogonality of two weighted shifts if one does not know at least that each of them admits non-trivial invariant measures,
i.e.
invariant measures different from the Dirac mass
\delta_{0}
. The following proposition says exactly when this happens for a weighted shift acting on
\ell_{p}
.

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
B_{\boldsymbol{w}}
be a weighted backward shift acting on
X=\ell_{p}
. Then
B_{\boldsymbol{w}}
admits a non-trivial invariant measure if and only if
\sum\limits_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{|w_{1}\cdots w_{n}|^{p}}<\infty
.

One can give a short proof of Proposition
2.1
by using a (quite non-trivial) lemma inspired by
[
6
]
, which can be extracted from the proof of
[
11
]
*[Theorem 2.1]. Since this lemma might be useful in other situations, we state it explicitely. Let us denote by
e_{0}^{*}
the first coordinate functional on
X=\ell_{p}
.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
B_{\boldsymbol{w}}
be a weighted shift acting on
X=\ell_{p}(\mathbb{Z}_{+})
. Assume that one can find a vector
x\in\ell_{p}
and a set of integers
\mathcal{N}
such that

(i)

\mathcal{N}
has positive upper density,

(ii)

\sup_{n\in\mathcal{N}}\|B_{\boldsymbol{w}}^{n}x\|<\infty
,

(iii)

\inf_{n\in\mathcal{N}}|\langle e_{0}^{*},B_{\boldsymbol{w}}^{n}x\rangle|>0
.

Then, one can conclude that
\sum\limits_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{|w_{1}\cdots w_{n}|^{p}}<\infty
.

Sketch of the proof of Lemma
2.2
.

Write
x=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}x_{n}e_{n}
.
Let
C_{1}:=\sup_{n\in\mathcal{N}}\|B_{\boldsymbol{w}}^{n}x\|
and
C_{2}:=\inf_{n\in\mathcal{N}}|\langle e_{0}^{*},B_{\boldsymbol{w}}^{n}x\rangle|=\inf_{n\in\mathcal{N}}|{w_{1}\cdots w_{n}}\,x_{n}|
.
If we let
v_{n}:=(w_{1}\cdots w_{n})^{-1}
, we have for every
n\in\mathcal{N}
and every integer
M\geq 0
,

\displaystyle\Biggl\|\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m\in\mathcal{N}\\
n\leq m\leq n+M\end{subarray}}v_{m-n}e_{m-n}\Biggr\|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{C_{2}}\Biggl\|\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m\in\mathcal{N}\\
n\leq m\leq n+M\end{subarray}}v_{m-n}\frac{x_{m}}{v_{m}}e_{m-n}\Biggr\|=\frac{1}{C_{2}}\Biggl\|\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leq k\leq M\\
k+n\in\mathcal{N}\end{subarray}}v_{k}\frac{x_{k+n}}{v_{k+n}}e_{k}\Biggr\|\leq\frac{C_{1}}{C_{2}}\cdot

We then conclude thanks to
[
11
]
*[Lemma 2.5].
∎

Proof of Proposition
2.1
.

As mentioned in the introduction, it is well-known that if
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{|w_{1}\cdots w_{n}|^{p}}<\infty
, then
B_{\boldsymbol{w}}
admits lots of non-trivial invariant measures. Perhaps the simplest such measure is the Dirac mass
\delta_{x}
at the fixed point
x:=e_{0}+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{{w_{1}\cdots w_{n}}}\,e_{n}
.

Conversely, assume that
B_{\boldsymbol{w}}
admits a non-trivial invariant measure
m
. By the ergodic decomposition theorem, we may assume that
m
is an ergodic measure for
B_{\boldsymbol{w}}
. Since
m\neq\delta_{0}
, we may choose a point
u\neq 0
in the support of
m
. Then
B_{\boldsymbol{w}}^{n}u\in{\rm supp}(m)
for all
n\geq 0
by the
B_{\boldsymbol{w}}\,
- invariance of
m
, so we may assume that
\langle e_{0}^{*},u\rangle\neq 0
. Let
\alpha>0
be such that
|\langle e_{0}^{*},u\rangle|>\alpha
, and consider the open set

U:=\bigl\{z\in X;\;\|z-u\|<1\quad\hbox{and}\quad|\langle e_{0}^{*},z\rangle|>\alpha\bigr\}.

Since
U
is a neighbourhood of
u
, we have
m(U)>0
. By the pointwise ergodic theorem, it follows that one can find
x\in X
such that the set
\mathcal{N}_{B_{\boldsymbol{w}}}(x,U):=\{n\in\mathbb{Z}_{+};\;B_{\boldsymbol{w}}^{n}x\in U\}
admits a positive density,
i.e.

\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N+1}\#\bigl(\llbracket 0,N\rrbracket\cap\mathcal{N}_{B_{\boldsymbol{w}}}(x,U)\bigr)
exists and is (strictly) positive; in particular,
\mathcal{N}_{B_{\boldsymbol{w}}}(x,U)
has positive
upper
density. So we have found a vector
x\in\ell_{p}
and a set of integers
\mathcal{N}
with positive upper density such that
\|B_{\boldsymbol{w}}^{n}x\|<1+\|u\|
and
|\langle e_{0}^{*},B_{\boldsymbol{w}}^{n}x\rangle|>\alpha
for all
n\in\mathcal{N}
.
By Lemma
2.2
, this concludes the proof. ∎

Remark 2.3
.

Proposition
2.1
is valid in a more general setting: the same proof shows that if
X
is a Banach sequence space for which
(e_{n})_{n\geq 0}
is a boundedly complete unconditional basis, then a weighted shift
B_{\boldsymbol{w}}
acting on
X
admits a non-trivial invariant measure if and only if the series
\sum\frac{1}{{w_{1}\cdots w_{n}}}\,e_{n}
is convergent in
X
. We do not know if the analogous result holds for weighted shifts acting on
c_{0}(\mathbb{Z}_{+})
.

3.
A general criterion for orthogonality

Although the definition of orthogonality was given in the introduction for pairs of weighted shifts only, it makes sense in a much more general context. If
T
is any continuous self-map of a Polish (
i.e.
separable and completely metrizable) space
X
, let us denote by
\mathcal{P}_{T}(X)
the set of all
T\,
- invariant (Borel probability) measures on
X
.

Definition 3.1
.

Let
X
be a Polish space, and let (P) be any property of measures on
X
. We say that two continuous self-maps
T_{1},T_{2}
of
X
are
orthogonal with respect to measures satisfying
(P)
if the following holds true: whenever
m_{1}\in\mathcal{P}_{T_{1}}(X)

and
m_{2}\in\mathcal{P}_{T_{2}}(X)
satisfy (P), it follows that
m_{1}\perp m_{2}
.

In accordance with Definition
1.1
, if
X
is a Polish topological vector space and
T_{1},T_{2}
are continuous linear operators on
X
, we will say that
T_{1}
and
T_{2}
are
orthogonal
if they are orthogonal with respect to measures not charging the singleton
\{0\}
.

We now present a general sufficient condition for orthogonality. Recall that if
(A_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is a sequence of subsets of
X
, then
\varlimsup\,A_{n}
denotes the set of all
x\in X
that belong to infinitely many
A_{n}
’s.

Theorem 3.2
.

Let
X
be a Polish space, and let
\Lambda
be a Borel subset of
X
. Let also
T_{1}
and
T_{2}
be two continuous self-maps of
X
. Assume that for any compact set
K\subseteq X
with

K\cap\Lambda=\emptyset
, there exists an infinite set
I\subseteq\mathbb{N}
such that

\overline{\lim_{n\in I}}\;\,T_{1}^{-n}(K)\cap T_{2}^{-n}(K)=\emptyset.

(3.1)

Then,
T_{1}
and
T_{2}
are orthogonal with respect to measures not charging
\Lambda
.

Proof.

Let
m_{1}\in\mathcal{P}_{T_{1}}(X)
and
m_{2}\in\mathcal{P}_{T_{2}}(X)
be such that
m_{1}(\Lambda)=0=m_{2}(\Lambda)
. We have to show that
m_{1}\perp m_{2}
, and this will be an easy consequence of the following fact.

Fact 3.3
.

For any
\varepsilon>0
, one can find a Borel set
E_{\varepsilon}\subseteq X
such that
m_{1}(E_{\varepsilon})<\varepsilon
and
m_{2}(E_{\varepsilon})>1-\varepsilon
.

Proof of Fact
3.3
.

By regularity of the measures
m_{1}
and
m_{2}
and since
m_{1}
and
m_{2}
do not charge
\Lambda
, there exists a compact set
K\subseteq X\setminus\Lambda
such that
m_{1}(K)>1-\varepsilon/2
and
m_{2}(K)>1-\varepsilon/2
. Let
I
be an infinite subset of
\mathbb{N}
such that (
3.1
) holds true. Then
m_{1}\bigl(T_{1}^{-n}(K)\cap T_{2}^{-n}(K)\bigr)\to 0
as
n\to\infty
along
I
by Fatou’s Lemma; so one can find an integer
n\geq 1
such that
m_{1}\bigl(T_{1}^{-n}(K)\cap T_{2}^{-n}(K)\bigr)<\varepsilon/2
. Since
m_{1}\bigl(T_{1}^{-n}(K)\bigr)=m_{1}(K)>1-\varepsilon/2
, it follows that

m_{1}\bigl(T_{2}^{-n}(K)\bigr)<\varepsilon
. On the other hand,
m_{2}\bigl(T_{2}^{-n}(K)\bigr)=m_{2}(K)>1-\varepsilon
; so we may take
E:=T_{2}^{-n}(K)
.
∎

If we now define
E:=\bigcup_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\bigcap_{n\in\mathbb{N}}E_{{\frac{2^{-n}}{k}}}
, we see that
m_{1}(E)=0
and
m_{2}(E)=1
, which shows that
m_{1}\perp m_{2}
.
∎

Remark 3.4
.

The assumption in Theorem
3.2
is here to ensure that for any compact set
K\subseteq X\setminus\Lambda
and any measure
m
on
X
, it holds that
\inf_{n\geq 1}m\bigl(T_{1}^{-n}(K)\cap T_{2}^{-n}(K)\bigr)=0
. However, it is very likely that (
3.1
) is a too strong assumption. More generally, it would be nice to have a “measure-free” characterization of the sequences of Borel sets
(A_{n})_{n\geq 1}\subseteq X
such that
\inf_{n\geq 1}m(A_{n})=0
for every measure
m
on
X
. Note that there is a very simple characterization if “
\inf
” is replaced by “
\lim
”: we have
\lim_{n\to\infty}m(A_{n})=0
for every measure
m
on
X
if and only if
\varlimsup A_{n}=\emptyset
. On the other hand, for a sequence
(A_{n})_{n\geq 1}
of non-empty Borel subsets of
X
, consider the following statements.

(i)

There exists an infinite set
I\subseteq\mathbb{N}
such that
\varlimsup_{n\in I}A_{n}=\emptyset
.

(ii)

\inf_{n\geq 1}m(A_{n})=0
for every measure
m
on
X
.

(iii)

\inf_{n\geq 1}m(A_{n})=0
for every discrete measure
m
on
X
.

(iv)

\inf_{n\geq 1}m(A_{n})=0
for every finitely supported measure
m
on
X
.

(v)

For any finite set
F\subseteq X
, there exist infinitely many
n
such that
A_{n}\cap F=\emptyset
.

Then (i)
\implies
(ii)
\implies
(iii)
\iff
(iv)
\iff
(v), but (v) does not imply (ii) and (ii) does not imply (i).

Proof.

(i)
\implies
(ii) by Fatou’s Lemma, and obviously (ii)
\implies
(iii)
\implies
(iv). Moreover, it is rather clear that in fact (iii)
\iff
(iv), and that (v)
\implies
(iv).

To prove that (iv)
\implies
(v), assume that for some finite set
F\subseteq X
and some integer
N
, we have
A_{n}\cap F\neq\emptyset
for all
n>N
. Choose a point
a_{i}\in A_{i}
for
i=1,\dots,N
. Then, the finitely supported measure
m:=\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{1}{N}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{a_{i}}+\frac{1}{\#F}\sum\limits_{x\in F}\delta_{x}\right)
is such that
\inf_{n\geq 1}m(A_{n})>0
.

To show that (v) does not imply (ii), let
X:=[0,1]
, and define

A_{n}:=\{t\in[0,1];\;|\sin(2\pi nt)|\geq 1/\pi\}.

By Dirichlet’s theorem, for any finite set
F\subseteq[0,1]
one can find an increasing sequence of integers
(n_{k})
such that
\sin(2\pi n_{k}t)\to 0
on
F
; so (v) is satisfied. However, we have
\int_{0}^{1}|\sin(2\pi nt)|\,dt=2/\pi
for all
n\geq 1
. Hence
m(A_{n})\geq 1/\pi
for all
n\geq 1
, where

m
is Lebesgue measure on
[0,1]
.

Let us now show that (ii) does not imply (i). This example is due to N. de Rancourt. Consider the Cantor space
\Delta=\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{N}}
identified with the power set of
\mathbb{N}
. Let

X:=\left\{J\subseteq\mathbb{N};\;\sum_{n\in J}\frac{1}{n}\leq 1\right\},

which is a closed subset of
\Delta
and hence a (compact) Polish space. For each
n\in\mathbb{N}
, let

A_{n}:=\{J\in X;\;n\in J\}.

It is clear that the sequence
(A_{n})
does not satisfy (i): indeed, for any infinite set
I\subseteq\mathbb{N}
, one can find an infinite set
J\subseteq I
such that
\sum_{n\in J}\frac{1}{n}\leq 1
, and this
J
belongs to
\varlimsup A_{n}
by definition.

However,
(A_{n})
satisfies (ii); in fact, for any measure
m
on
X
, we have
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{m(A_{n})}{n}<\infty
. Indeed, we have

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{m(A_{n})}{n}=\int_{X}\left(\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n}\,\mathbf{1}_{A_{n}}(J)\right)dm(J)=\int_{X}\left(\sum_{n\in J}\frac{1}{n}\right)dm(J)\leq 1.

Here is yet another example showing that (ii) does not imply (i), which is a kind of twin of the previous one. Let

X:=\left\{x\in[0,\pi];\;\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{|\sin(2^{n}x)|}{n}\leq 1\right\},

which is a closed subset of
[0,\pi]
. For each
n\geq 1
, let

A_{n}:=\{x\in X;\;|\sin(2^{n}x)|\geq 1/2\}.

With some effort, one can prove the following:
if
(m_{i})_{i\geq 1}
is a sufficiently fast increasing sequence of integers and if we define

x:=\frac{\pi}{2}\,\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{2^{m_{i}}},

then
x\in A_{m_{k}}
for every
k\geq 1
.
It follows imediately that the sequence
(A_{n})
does not satisfy (i). However, one shows in the same way as above that
(A_{n})
satisfies (ii).
∎

Remark 3.5
.

With the notation of Theorem
3.2
, consider the following statements.

(1)

For any compact set
K\subseteq X\setminus\Lambda
, it holds that
\overline{\lim}\;T_{1}^{-n}(K)\cap T_{2}^{-n}(K)=\emptyset
.

(2)

It is not possible to find
x\in X
and an increasing sequence of integers
(n_{k})
such that the sequences
(T_{1}^{n_{k}}x)
and
(T_{2}^{n_{k}}x)
both converge to a limit not belonging to
\Lambda
.

Then (2)
\implies
(1), and (1)
\iff
(2) if
\Lambda
is a
closed
subset of
X
.

Proof.

It should be rather clear that (2) implies (1). Indeed, let
K
be a compact subset of
X\setminus\Lambda
, and assume that
\overline{\lim}\;T_{1}^{-n}(K)\cap T_{2}^{-n}(K)\neq\emptyset
. Then, there exists
x\in X
and an increasing sequence of integers
(n_{k})_{k\geq 0}
such that
T_{1}^{n_{k}}x\in K
and

T_{2}^{n_{k}}x\in K
for all
k\geq 0
. Since
K
is compact, we may assume, upon extracting subsequences, that both sequences
(T_{1}^{n_{k}}x)
and
(T_{2}^{n_{k}}x)
converge,
T_{1}^{n_{k}}x\to u\in K
and
T_{2}^{n_{k}}x\to v\in K
; and since
K\subseteq X\setminus\Lambda
, this shows that (2) is not satisfied.

Conversely, assume that
\Lambda
is closed and that (2) is not satisfied,
i.e.
there exist
x\in X
and an increasing sequence of integers
(n_{k})_{k\geq 0}
such that
T_{1}^{n_{k}}x\to u
and
T_{2}^{n_{k}}x\to v
, where
u,v\in X\setminus\Lambda
. Since
X\setminus\Lambda
is open, one can find
k_{0}
such that
T_{1}^{n_{k}}x,T_{2}^{n_{k}}x\in X\setminus\Lambda
for all
k\geq k_{0}
.
Then the set
K:=\{T_{1}^{n_{k}}x;\;k\geq k_{0}\}\cup\{T_{2}^{n_{k}}x;\;k\geq k_{0}\}\cup\{u,v\}
is a compact subset of
X\setminus\Lambda
and
x\in T_{1}^{-n_{k}}(K)\cap T_{2}^{-n_{k}}(K)
for all
k\geq k_{0}
, so that (1) is not satisfied.
∎

Here is a first application of Theorem
3.2
.

Corollary 3.6
.

Let
X
be a Polish topological vector space, and let
T\in\mathcal{L}(X)
. Let also
a,b\in\mathbb{K}
, and assume that
aT
and
bT
admit non-trivial invariant measures. Then,
aT
and
bT
are orthogonal if and only if
|a|\neq|b|
. More precisely:
aT
and
bT
are orthogonal if
|a|\neq|b|
; and they share a non-trivial invariant measure if
|a|=|b|
.

Proof.

To prove that
aT
and
bT
are orthogonal if
|a|\neq|b|
, we use Theorem
3.2
and Remark
3.5
with
\Lambda=\{0\}
. Assume that there exists
x\in X
and an increasing sequence of integers
(n_{k})
such that
a^{n_{k}}T^{n_{k}}x\to u\in X
and
b^{n_{k}}T^{n_{k}}x\to v\in X
. We must show that
u=0
or

; and this follows from the continuity of the map

(\lambda,z)\mapsto\lambda z
: indeed, assuming for example that
|a|>|b|
and writing

b^{n_{k}}T^{n_{k}}x=(b/a)^{n_{k}}\,a^{n_{k}}T^{n_{k}}x
, we see that

.

The converse will follow from Proposition

below.∎

Remark 3.7
.

If
B_{\boldsymbol{w}}
is a weighted shift acting on
X=\ell_{p}(\mathbb{Z}_{+})
and
a,b\in\mathbb{K}
are such that
|a|=|b|=:r
with
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{(r^{n}|w_{1}\cdots w_{n}|)^{p}}<\infty
, then the operators
aB_{\boldsymbol{w}}
and
bB_{\boldsymbol{w}}
admit equivalent ergodic Gaussian measures with full support. This will follow from Theorem
5.7
below.

Corollary
3.6
leads to naive speculations regarding orthogonality of weighted shifts. Recall that two operators
T_{1},T_{2}
acting on
X
are said to be
similar
if there exists an invertible operator
J
such that
T_{1}=JT_{2}J^{-1}
. By
[
22
]
*[p. 54, Th. 2(a)], two weighted shifts
B_{\boldsymbol{u}}
and
B_{\boldsymbol{v}}
acting on
\ell_{p}
are similar if and only if

\varliminf\;\left|\frac{u_{1}\cdots u_{n}}{v_{1}\cdots v_{n}}\right|>0\qquad{\rm and}\qquad\varlimsup\;\left|\frac{u_{1}\cdots u_{n}}{v_{1}\cdots v_{n}}\right|<\infty.

(3.2)

When
\boldsymbol{u}
and
\boldsymbol{v}
have the form
\boldsymbol{u}=a\boldsymbol{w}
and
\boldsymbol{v}=b\boldsymbol{w}
for some weight sequence
\boldsymbol{w}
and
a,b\in\mathbb{K}
, then
\frac{u_{1}\cdots u_{n}}{v_{1}\cdots v_{n}}=\left(\frac{a}{b}\right)^{n}
, and hence (
3.2
) holds true if and only if
|a|=|b|
. So it is tempting to “conjecture” that two weighted shifts
B_{\boldsymbol{u}}
and
B_{\boldsymbol{v}}
are orthogonal if and only if they are not similar. This is however not true, as will be seen in Section
4
below. Nevertheless, we now present two results showing that this is not
that far
from being true.

In what follows we denote by
e_{j}^{*}
,
j\geq 0
the coordinate functionals on
X=\ell_{p}(\mathbb{Z}_{+})
. And if
x\in X
, we write
x=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}x_{j}e_{j}
where
x_{j}=\langle e_{j}^{*},x\rangle
.

Proposition 3.8
.

Let
B_{\boldsymbol{u}}
and
B_{\boldsymbol{v}}
be two weighted shifts acting on
X=\ell_{p}
. Assume that
B_{\boldsymbol{u}}
and
B_{\boldsymbol{v}}
are not similar, i.e. that

\hbox{either}\quad\underline{\lim}\,\left|\frac{u_{1}\cdots u_{n}}{v_{1}\cdots v_{n}}\right|=0\qquad\hbox{or}\quad\overline{\lim}\,\left|\frac{u_{1}\cdots u_{n}}{v_{1}\cdots v_{n}}\right|=\infty.

(3.3)

Then,
B_{\boldsymbol{u}}
and
B_{\boldsymbol{v}}
are orthogonal with respect to measures not charging
\Lambda_{e_{0}^{*}}:=\ker(e_{0}^{*})
.

Proof.

We apply Theorem
3.2
with
\Lambda:=\Lambda_{e_{0}^{*}}
. So let
K
be a compact subset of
X\setminus\ker(e_{0}^{*})
. Choose some constants
M<\infty
and
\gamma>0
such that
\|z\|\leq M
and
|\langle e_{0}^{*},z\rangle|\geq\gamma
for all
z\in K
.
If
x=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}x_{j}e_{j}\in X
then, for every
n\geq 1
, we have

\langle e_{0}^{*},B_{\boldsymbol{u}}^{n}x\rangle={u_{1}\cdots u_{n}}\,x_{n}\qquad{\rm and}\qquad\langle e_{0}^{*},B_{\boldsymbol{v}}^{n}x\rangle={v_{1}\cdots v_{n}}\,x_{n};

so we see that

B_{\boldsymbol{u}}^{-n}(K)\subseteq\bigl\{x\in X;\;|x_{n}|\cdot|u_{1}\cdots u_{n}|\geq\gamma\bigr\}

and

B_{\boldsymbol{v}}^{-n}(K)\subseteq\bigl\{x\in X;\;|x_{n}|\cdot|v_{1}\cdots v_{n}|\leq M\bigr\}.

Hence, the following implication holds :

B_{\boldsymbol{u}}^{-n}(K)\cap B_{\boldsymbol{v}}^{-n}(K)\neq\emptyset\implies\left|\frac{u_{1}\cdots u_{n}}{v_{1}\cdots v_{n}}\right|\geq\frac{\gamma}{M}\cdot

It follows that if
\underline{\lim}\,\left|\frac{u_{1}\cdots u_{n}}{v_{1}\cdots v_{n}}\right|=0
, then
B_{\boldsymbol{u}}^{-n}(K)\cap B_{\boldsymbol{u}}^{-n}(K)=\emptyset
for infinitely many
n
; and symmetrically, if
\overline{\lim}\,\left|\frac{u_{1}\cdots u_{n}}{v_{1}\cdots v_{n}}\right|=\infty
then
B_{\boldsymbol{v}}^{-n}(K)\cap B_{\boldsymbol{u}}^{-n}(K)=\emptyset
for infinitely many
n
. By Theorem
3.2
, this concludes the proof.
∎

Remark 3.9
.

One may observe that for any weight sequence
\boldsymbol{w}
and every
n\geq 1
, we have
B_{\boldsymbol{w}}^{-n}(\Lambda_{e_{0}^{*}})=\Lambda_{e_{n}^{*}}
. Hence, a measure
m\in\mathcal{P}_{\boldsymbol{w}}(X)
does not charge
\Lambda_{e_{0}^{*}}
if and only if it does not charge
\Lambda_{e_{n}^{*}}
. Therefore,
m
does not charge
\Lambda_{e_{0}^{*}}
if and only if it is supported on the set
\{x\in X;\;\langle e_{n}^{*},x\rangle\neq 0\;\,\hbox{for all $n\geq 0$}\}
.

The next theorem shows that “full” orthogonality of
B_{\boldsymbol{u}}
and
B_{\boldsymbol{v}}
can be deduced from stronger assumptions on the weights
{\boldsymbol{u}}
and
{\boldsymbol{v}}
.

Theorem 3.10
.

Let
B_{\boldsymbol{u}}
and
B_{\boldsymbol{v}}
be two weighted shifts acting on
X=\ell_{p}
. Assume that
either

\varliminf_{n\to\infty}\;\max_{0\leq d\leq N}\,\left|\frac{u_{1}\cdots u_{n+d}}{v_{1}\cdots v_{n+d}}\right|=0\qquad\hbox{for all $N\geq 0$}

(3.4)

or

\varlimsup_{n\to\infty}\;\min_{0\leq d\leq N}\,\left|\frac{u_{1}\cdots u_{n+d}}{v_{1}\cdots v_{n+d}}\right|=\infty\qquad\hbox{for all $N\geq 0$}.

(3.5)

Then,
B_{\boldsymbol{u}}
and
B_{\boldsymbol{v}}
are orthogonal.

Proof.

We apply Theorem
3.2
once again, with
\Lambda:=\{0\}
. So, let
K
be a compact subset of
X\setminus\{0\}
.

We first note that there exists an integer
N
such that
\max\limits_{0\leq d\leq N}|z_{d}|>0
for every
z\in K
. Indeed, the compact set
K
is covered by the open sets
O_{d}:=\{z\in X;\;|z_{d}|>0\}
.

Choose
\gamma>0
such that
\max\limits_{0\leq d\leq N}|z_{d}|\geq\gamma
for every
z\in K
, and
M<\infty
such that
\|z\|\leq M
for every
z\in K
. As in the proof of Proposition
3.8
, we get that for every
n\geq 1
,

B_{\boldsymbol{u}}^{-n}(K)\subseteq\left\{x\in X;\;\max_{0\leq d\leq N}|u_{1+d}\cdots u_{n+d}|\cdot|x_{n+d}|\geq\gamma\right\}

and

B_{\boldsymbol{v}}^{-n}(K)\subseteq\left\{x\in X;\;\max_{0\leq d\leq N}|v_{1+d}\cdots v_{n+d}|\cdot|x_{n+d}|\leq M\right\};

so that

B_{\boldsymbol{u}}^{-n}(K)\cap B_{\boldsymbol{v}}^{-n}(K)\neq\emptyset\implies\max_{0\leq d\leq N}\left|\frac{u_{1+d}\cdots u_{n+d}}{v_{1+d}\cdots v_{n+d}}\right|\geq\frac{\gamma}{M}\cdot

Now, we have
\max_{0\leq d\leq N}\left|\frac{u_{1+d}\cdots u_{n+d}}{v_{1+d}\cdots v_{n+d}}\right|\leq C_{N}\,\max_{0\leq d\leq N}\left|\frac{u_{1}\cdots u_{n+d}}{v_{1}\cdots v_{n+d}}\right|
for some constant
C_{N}
. Hence, if (
3.4
) is satisfied, then one can find an infinite set
I\subseteq\mathbb{N}
such that

\max\limits_{0\leq d\leq N}\left|\frac{u_{1+d}\cdots u_{n+d}}{v_{1+d}\cdots v_{n+d}}\right|\to 0\qquad\hbox{as}\quad n\to\infty\;\;\hbox{along $I$};

and it follows that
B_{\boldsymbol{u}}^{-n}(K)\cap B_{\boldsymbol{v}}^{-n}(K)=\emptyset
for all

n\in I
sufficiently large. Symmetrically, if (
3.5
) is satisfied, then one can find an infinite set
I\subseteq\mathbb{N}
such that
B_{\boldsymbol{v}}^{-n}(K)\cap B_{\boldsymbol{u}}^{-n}(K)=\emptyset
for all

n\in I
sufficiently large. So Theorem
3.2
applies.
∎

.

Assume that the weight sequences
{\boldsymbol{u}}

are bounded below, i.e.
\inf_{n\geq 1}|u_{n}|>0

4.
Non-orthogonality
via
periodic points

In this section, our aim is to present some examples of non-orthogonal weighted shifts using measures supported by periodic orbits. We first note the following easy fact.

Fact 4.1
.

Let
X
be a Polish topological vector space, and let
T_{1},T_{2}\in\mathcal{L}(X)
. If
T_{1}
and
T_{2}
share a non-zero periodic point, then they are not orthogonal.

Proof.

Let
x\neq 0
be a common periodic point for
T_{1}
and
T_{2}
; so
T^{d_{1}}x=x=T^{d_{2}}x
for some
d_{1},d_{2}\in\mathbb{N}
. For
i=1,2
, the measure
m_{i}:=\frac{1}{d_{i}}\sum_{n=0}^{d_{i}-1}\delta_{T_{i}^{n}x}
is
T_{i}\,
- invariant, and
m_{i}(\{0\})=0
since
x\neq 0
. Moreover,
m_{1}
and
m_{2}
are not orthogonal since

m_{1}(\{x\})>0
and
m_{2}(\{x\})>0
.
∎

Theorem 4.2
.

Let
B_{\boldsymbol{u}}
and
B_{\boldsymbol{v}}
be two weighted shifts on
X=\ell_{p}(\mathbb{Z}_{+})
. Assume that
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{|u_{1}\cdots u_{n}|^{p}}<\infty
and
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{|v_{1}\cdots v_{n}|^{p}}<\infty
. The following are equivalent.

(1)

B_{\boldsymbol{u}}
and
B_{\boldsymbol{v}}
share a non-zero periodic point;

(2)

there exist
d\in\mathbb{N}
and
0\leq j\leq d-1
such that
u_{1+j}\cdots u_{dm+j}=v_{1+j}\cdots v_{dm+j}
for all
m\geq 1
;

(3)

there exist
d\in\mathbb{N}
and
0\leq j\leq d-1
, and a non-zero scalar
C
such that
u_{1}\cdots u_{dm+j}=C\,v_{1}\cdots v_{dm+j}
for all
m\geq 0

(
where an empty product is declared to be equal to
1
)
.

Proof.

(1)
\implies
(2) Assume that
B_{\boldsymbol{u}}
and
B_{\boldsymbol{v}}
share a non-zero periodic point
x
. Let
d_{1}
be the period of
x
as a periodic point of
T_{1}
, and let
d_{2}
be the period of
x
as a periodic point of
T_{2}
. Setting
d:=d_{1}d_{2}
, we then have
B_{\boldsymbol{u}}^{d}x=x=B_{\boldsymbol{v}}^{d}x
(and hence

B_{\boldsymbol{u}}^{md}x=x=B_{\boldsymbol{v}}^{md}x
for all
m\geq 1
.) Writing
x=\sum_{j\geq 0}x_{j}e_{j}
, we have

x_{j+md}=\frac{1}{u_{j+1}\cdots u_{j+md}}\,x_{j}=\frac{1}{v_{j+1}\cdots v_{j+md}}\,x_{j}\qquad\hbox{for all $j\geq 0$}.

Since
x\neq 0
, one can find
0\leq j\leq d-1
such that
x_{j}\neq 0
, and then
u_{j+md}\cdots u_{j+1}=v_{j+md}\cdots v_{j+1}
for all
m\geq 1
.

(2)
\implies
(3) This is clear: if (2) is satisfied for some
d
and
0\leq j\leq d-1
, then (3) is satisfied with the same
d
and
j
and
C:=\frac{u_{1}\cdots u_{j}}{v_{1}\cdots v_{j}}
(so
C=1
if
j=0
).

(3)
\implies
(1) Assume that (3) holds true for some
d,j,C
. Since
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{|u_{1}\cdots u_{n}|^{p}}<\infty
, the vector

x:=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{u_{1}\cdots u_{md+j}}\,e_{md+j}=\frac{1}{C}\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{v_{1}\cdots v_{md+j}}\,e_{md+j}\in\ell_{p}

is well-defined, and clearly
B_{\boldsymbol{u}}^{d}x=x=B_{\boldsymbol{v}}^{d}x
. Thus,
x
is a non-zero common periodic point for
B_{\boldsymbol{u}}
and
B_{\boldsymbol{v}}
.
∎

Corollary 4.3
.

Let
B_{\boldsymbol{u}}
and
B_{\boldsymbol{v}}
be two weighted shifts on
\ell_{p}(\mathbb{Z}_{+})
, with
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{|u_{1}\cdots u_{n}|^{p}}<\infty
and
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{|v_{1}\cdots v_{n}|^{p}}<\infty
. Assume that there exist
d\in\mathbb{N}
,
0\leq j\leq d-1
and a scalar
C\neq 0
such that
u_{1}\cdots u_{dm+j}=C\,v_{1}\cdots v_{dm+j}
for all
m\geq 0
. Then
B_{\boldsymbol{u}}
and
B_{\boldsymbol{v}}
are not orthogonal.

Example 4.4
.

Assume that
u_{j}=2
for all
j\geq 1
,
v_{1}=3
and
v_{j}=2
for all
j\geq 2
. Then
B_{\boldsymbol{u}}
and
B_{\boldsymbol{v}}
acting on
\ell_{p}
share a non-zero fixed point, and hence they are not orthogonal.

In this example, the operators
B_{\boldsymbol{u}}
and
B_{\boldsymbol{v}}
are obviously similar. We now give an example of non-orthogonal weighted shifts which are not similar.

Example 4.5
.

Let
(r_{k})_{k\geq 1}
be an increasing sequence of integers, and let
{\boldsymbol{u}}
and
{\boldsymbol{v}}
be the two weight sequences defined as follows:

u_{j}:=\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}2&\hbox{if}&\hbox{$j\not\in\{5r_{k}+1,5r_{k}+4;\;k\geq 1\}$}\\
\frac{1}{k}&\hbox{if}&\hbox{$j=5r_{k}+1$ or $j=5r_{k}+4\;$ for some $k\geq 1$}\end{array}\right.

and

v_{j}:=\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}2&\hbox{if}&\hbox{$j\not\in\{5r_{k}+2,5r_{k}+3;\;k\geq 1\}$}\\
\frac{1}{k}&\hbox{if}&\hbox{$j=5r_{k}+2$ or $j=5r_{k}+3\;$ for some $k\geq 1$.}\end{array}\right.

If
(r_{k})
is sufficiently fast increasing, then
B_{\boldsymbol{u}}
and
B_{\boldsymbol{v}}
acting on
\ell_{p}
share a non-zero periodic point and hence they are not orthogonal. However, we have

\varliminf\;\frac{u_{1}\cdots u_{n}}{v_{1}\cdots v_{n}}=0\qquad{\rm and}\qquad\varlimsup\;\frac{u_{1}\cdots u_{n}}{v_{1}\cdots v_{n}}=\infty,

so that, in particular,
B_{\boldsymbol{u}}
and
B_{\boldsymbol{v}}
are not similar.

Proof.

It is clear that if
(r_{k})
is sufficiently fast increasing, then
\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{|u_{1}\cdots u_{n}|^{p}}<\infty
and
\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{|v_{1}\cdots v_{n}|^{p}}<\infty
.

Observe that if
m
is a positive integer and if we consider the largest
k
such that
r_{k}\leq m
, then either
r_{k}=m
or
5r_{k}+4<5m
. Since
u_{1}\cdots u_{5r_{k}}=v_{1}\cdots v_{5r_{k}}
by definition of the weights, it follows that

u_{1}\cdots u_{5m}=v_{1}\cdots v_{5m}\qquad\hbox{for all $m\geq 1$}.

Hence,
B_{\boldsymbol{u}}
and
B_{\boldsymbol{v}}
share a non-zero periodic point by Theorem
4.2
.

However, for any
k\geq 1
we have

\frac{u_{1}\cdots u_{5r_{k}+1}}{v_{1}\cdots v_{5r_{k}+1}}=\frac{1}{2k}\qquad{\rm and}\qquad\frac{u_{1}\cdots u_{5r_{k}+3}}{v_{1}\cdots v_{5r_{k}+3}}=2k,

so that
\varliminf\;\frac{u_{1}\cdots u_{n}}{v_{1}\cdots v_{n}}=0
and
\varlimsup\;\frac{u_{1}\cdots u_{n}}{v_{1}\cdots v_{n}}=\infty
.

∎

Remark 4.6
.

In the above example, the weights are not bounded below. This is not accidental: indeed, if two weighted shifts
B_{\boldsymbol{u}}
and
B_{\boldsymbol{v}}
share a non-zero periodic point and if

\inf_{n\geq 1}|u_{n}|>0
and
\inf_{n\geq 1}|v_{n}|>0
, then
B_{\boldsymbol{u}}
and
B_{\boldsymbol{v}}
are necessarily similar by Corollary
3.11
. (Alternatively, this follows easily from Theorem
4.2
.)

5.
Non-orthogonality
via
product measures

5.1.
Invariant product measures for weighted shifts

The question we consider in this section is the following: given two weighted shifts
B_{\boldsymbol{u}}
and
B_{\boldsymbol{v}}
on
X=\ell_{p}(\mathbb{Z}_{+})
, when is it possible to find invariant measures
m_{\boldsymbol{u}}
and
m_{\boldsymbol{v}}
(for
B_{\boldsymbol{u}}
and
B_{\boldsymbol{v}}
respectively) not charging
\{0\}
which are not orthogonal and are also
product measures
?

The terminology requires some explanation since product measures should be defined on products of measurable spaces and
X=\ell_{p}
is not such a product space. However,
\ell_{p}
is contained in the product space
\Omega:=\mathbb{K}^{\mathbb{Z}_{+}}
. It is well-known that the Borel
\sigma
-algebra of
\Omega
(induced by the product topology) is identical with the product
\sigma
-algebra
\otimes_{n\geq 0}\mathcal{B}_{\mathbb{K}}
. Moreover,
\ell_{p}
is a Borel subset of
\Omega
when

\Omega
is endowed with the product topology, and a subset of
\ell_{p}
is Borel in
(\ell_{p},\|\,\cdot\,\|_{p})
if and only if is Borel in
\Omega
. It follows that if
\mu
is a Borel probability measure on
\Omega
, then the restriction of

\mu
to
\ell_{p}
is a Borel measure on
\ell_{p}
endowed with its usual topology (but not a probability measure unless
\mu(\ell_{p})=1
); and conversely, any Borel probability measure on
\ell_{p}
can be considered as a Borel probability measure on
\Omega
(supported on
\ell_{p}
). We will say that a Borel probability measure
m
on
\ell_{p}
is a
product measure on
\ell_{p}
if
m
is the restriction to
\ell_{p}
of a (probability) product measure
\mu
on
\Omega
,
i.e.

\mu=\bigotimes_{n\geq 0}\mu_{n}
where each
\mu_{n}
is a Borel probability measure on
\mathbb{K}
, such that
\mu(\ell_{p})=1
. In this case, the measure
\mu
is uniquely determined by
m
since

\mu(B)=m(B\cap\ell_{p})
for every Borel set
B\subseteq\Omega
, so we identify
m
and
\mu
and simply write
m=\otimes_{n\geq 0}\mu_{n}
.

The following essentially obvious remark will be used repeatedly.

Fact 5.1
.

Let
X
be a Borel subset of
\Omega=\mathbb{K}^{\mathbb{Z}_{+}}
, and let
\mu
be a Borel (probability) measure on
\Omega
such that
\mu(X)=1
. Set
m:=\mu_{|X}
. Let also
T:\Omega\to\Omega
be a Borel map such that
T(X)\subseteq X
.Then
\mu
is
T\,
- invariant if and only if
m
is

(T_{|X})\,
- invariant.

Proof.

The measure
\mu
is
T\,
- invariant if and only if
\mu(T^{-1}(B))=\mu(B)
for every Borel set
B\subseteq\Omega
. Now, we have
\mu(B)=m(X\cap B)
and
\mu(T^{-1}(B))=m(X\cap T^{-1}(B))=m\bigl((T_{|X})^{-1}(X\cap B)\bigr)
because
T(X)\subseteq X
. So
\mu
is
T\,
- invariant
if and only if
m(X\cap B)=m\bigl((T_{|X})^{-1}(X\cap B)\bigr)
for every Borel set
B\subseteq\Omega
, which means exactly that
m
is
(T_{|X})\,
- invariant.
∎

From this observation, it follows that if
m
is a product measure on
X=\ell_{p}
and if we denote by
\mu
the measure
m
considered as a measure on
\Omega=\mathbb{K}^{\mathbb{Z}_{+}}
, then
m
is invariant under some weighted shift
B_{\boldsymbol{w}}
if and only if
\mu
is invariant under the natural extension of
B_{\boldsymbol{w}}
to
\Omega
(defined by the same formula and also denoted by
B_{\boldsymbol{w}}
). The next lemma says precisely when this happens.

Lemma 5.2
.

Let
\mu=\otimes_{n\geq 0}\mu_{n}
be a product measure on
\Omega=\mathbb{K}^{\mathbb{Z}_{+}}
, and let
{\boldsymbol{w}}
be a weight sequence. Then,
\mu
is
B_{\boldsymbol{w}}\,
- invariant if and only if, for each
n\geq 1
, the measure
\mu_{n}
is the image of
\mu_{0}
under the map
t\mapsto\frac{1}{w_{1}\cdots w_{n}}\,t
, i.e.
\mu_{n}(A)=\mu_{0}(w_{1}\cdots w_{n}A)
for every Borel set
A\subseteq\mathbb{K}
.

Proof.

For any Borel sets
A_{0}\,\dots,A_{N}\subseteq\mathbb{K}
, denote by
[A_{0},\dots,A_{N}]\subseteq\Omega
the “cylinder set” defined as follows:

[A_{0},\dots,A_{N}]=\bigl\{t=(t_{j})_{j\geq 0}\in\Omega;\;t_{j}\in A_{j}\quad\hbox{for $j=0,\dots,N$}\bigr\}.

Since the cylinder sets generate the Borel
\sigma
-algebra of
\Omega
, the measure
\mu
is
B_{\boldsymbol{w}}\,
- invariant if and only if

\mu\bigl(B_{\boldsymbol{w}}^{-1}([A_{0},\dots,A_{N}])\bigr)=\mu\bigl([A_{0},\dots,A_{N}]\bigr)

for all Borel sets
A_{0},\dots,A_{N}\subseteq\mathbb{K}
. Now, by definition of
B_{\boldsymbol{w}}
we have

B_{\boldsymbol{w}}^{-1}([A_{0},\dots,A_{N}]\bigr)=\bigl\{t=(t_{j})_{j\geq 0}\in\Omega;\;w_{j+1}t_{j+1}\in A_{j}\quad\hbox{for $j=0,\dots,N$}\bigr\},

so that

B_{\boldsymbol{w}}^{-1}([A_{0},\dots,A_{N}]\bigr)=\left[\mathbb{K},(1/{w_{1}})A_{0},\dots,(1/{w_{N+1}})A_{N}\right].

Since
\mu=\otimes_{j\geq 0}\mu_{j}
, it follows that
\mu
is
B_{\boldsymbol{w}}\,
- invariant if and only if

\prod_{j=0}^{N}\mu_{j}(A_{j})=\prod_{j=0}^{N}\mu_{j+1}\bigl((1/w_{j+1})A_{j}\bigr)

for every
N\geq 0
and all Borel sets
A_{0},\dots,A_{N}\subseteq\mathbb{K}
. This is clearly equivalent to the fact that

\mu_{j+1}((1/w_{j+1})A)=\mu_{j}(A)\qquad\hbox{for all $j\geq 0$ and every Borel set $A\subseteq\mathbb{K}$},

which proves the lemma.
∎

Corollary 5.3
.

Let
\mu=\otimes_{n\geq 0}\mu_{n}
be a product measure on
\Omega=\mathbb{K}^{\mathbb{Z}_{+}}
, and let
{\boldsymbol{w}}
be a weight sequence. Assume that
\mu_{0}
has a density
\mathtt{p}
with respect to Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{K}
.
Then
\mu
is
B_{\boldsymbol{w}}\,
- invariant if and only if, for each
n\geq 1
, the measure
\mu_{n}
has a density
\mathtt{p}_{n}
given by
\mathtt{p}_{n}(t):=|w_{1}\cdots w_{n}|^{d}\,\mathtt{p}(w_{1}\cdots w_{n}t)
, where
d=1
if
\mathbb{K}=\mathbb{R}
and
d=2
if
\mathbb{K}=\mathbb{C}
.

Proof.

This follows immediately from Lemma
5.2

∎

Corollary 5.4
.

Let
\mu=\otimes_{n\geq 0}\mu_{n}
be a product measure on
\Omega=\mathbb{K}^{\mathbb{Z}_{+}}
, and let
{\boldsymbol{w}}
be a weight sequence such that
\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{|w_{1}\cdots w_{n}|^{p}}<\infty
. If
\mu
is
B_{\boldsymbol{w}}\,
- invariant and if the measure
\mu_{0}
is such that
\int_{\mathbb{K}}|t|^{p}d\mu_{0}(t)<\infty
, then
\mu(\ell_{p})=1
.

Proof.

By Lemma
5.2
, we have

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\left(\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}|t_{n}|^{p}\right)d\mu(t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\int_{\mathbb{K}}|t_{n}|^{p}d\mu_{n}(t_{n})

\displaystyle=\left(\int_{\mathbb{K}}|s|^{p}d\mu_{0}(s)\right)\times\left(1+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{|w_{1}\cdots w_{n}|^{p}}\right)<\infty.

In particular, we see that
\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}|t_{n}|^{p}<\infty
for
\mu\,
- almost every
t\in\Omega
,
i.e.

\mu(\ell_{p})=1
.
∎

To conclude this section, we point out the following general fact.

Fact 5.5
.

Let
B_{\boldsymbol{w}}
be a weighted shift on
\ell_{p}
where
{\boldsymbol{w}}
satisfies
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{|{w_{1}\cdots w_{n}}|^{p}}<\infty
, and let
m=\otimes_{n\geq 0}\mu_{n}
be a
B_{\boldsymbol{w}}\,
- invariant product measure on
\ell_{p}
. If the measure
\mu_{0}
has full support, then
m
is an ergodic measure with full support for
B_{\boldsymbol{w}}
.

Proof.

Let
(\xi_{n})_{n\geq 0}
be a sequence of independent
\mathbb{K}\,
- valued random variables with law
\mu_{0}
. As a measure on
\mathbb{K}^{\mathbb{Z}_{+}}
,
m
is the distribution of the
\mathbb{K}^{\mathbb{Z}_{+}}
- valued random variable

\xi:=\xi_{0}e_{0}+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\xi_{n}}{{w_{1}\cdots w_{n}}}\,e_{n}
. Since
m(\ell_{p})=1
, we have
\xi\in\ell_{p}
almost surely, which means that the series defining
\xi
is almost surely convergent with respect to the
\ell_{p}\,
- norm. Moreover, since
\mu_{0}
has full support, the measure
m
has full support. By
[
1
]
*[Proposition 2.5], it follows that
m
is an ergodic measure (in fact, a strongly mixing measure) for
B_{\boldsymbol{w}}
.
∎

5.2.
Non-orthogonality of product measures

A basic tool in the study of product measures is a famous classical result of Kakutani
[
17
]
giving a necessary and sufficient condition for the equivalence of two infinite product probability measures
\nu=\otimes_{n\geq 0}\nu_{n}
and
\nu^{\prime}=\otimes_{n\geq 0}\nu^{\prime}_{n}
defined on a product of measurable spaces
(\Omega,\mathcal{B})=\otimes_{n\geq 0}(\Omega_{n},\mathcal{B}_{n})
.

Let us first recall the definition of the so-called
Hellinger integral

H(\alpha,\beta)
of two probability measures
\alpha,\beta
on some measurable space
(T,\mathcal{T})
:

H(\alpha,\beta)=\int_{T}\sqrt{\frac{d\alpha}{d\tau}}\,\sqrt{\frac{d\beta}{d\tau}}\,d\tau,

where
\tau
is any sigma-finite positive measure on
(T,\mathcal{T})
such that
\alpha
and
\beta
are absolutely continuous with respect to
\tau
. If we write

\alpha=\frac{d\alpha}{d\beta}\,\beta+\nu
the Lebesgue-Nikodym decomposition of
\alpha
with respect to
\beta
, then
\left(\frac{d\alpha}{d\tau}-\frac{d\alpha}{d\beta}\,\frac{d\beta}{d\tau}\right)\frac{d\beta}{d\tau}=0

\tau\,
- a.e. since
\nu\perp\beta
, so
\frac{d\alpha}{d\tau}\,\frac{d\beta}{d\tau}=\frac{d\alpha}{d\beta}\left(\frac{d\beta}{d\tau}\right)^{2}
and hence

H(\alpha,\beta)=\int_{T}\sqrt{\frac{d\alpha}{d\beta}}\,d\beta;

which shows that
H(\alpha,\beta)
is indeed independent of the choice of
\tau
. Note that we always have
0\leq H(\alpha,\beta)\leq 1
by the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality; and that
H(\alpha,\beta)=1
if and only if
\alpha=\beta
, whereas
H(\alpha,\beta)=0
if and only if
\alpha\perp\beta
.

Kakutani’s Theorem as we will need it can now be stated as follows.

Theorem 5.6
.

Let
\nu=\otimes_{n\geq 0}\nu_{n}
and
\nu^{\prime}=\otimes_{n\geq 0}\nu^{\prime}_{n}
be two product probability measures on
(\Omega,\mathcal{B})=\otimes_{n\geq 0}(\Omega_{n},\mathcal{B}_{n})
. The measures
\nu
and
\nu^{\prime}
are non-orthogonal if and only if

\prod_{n=0}^{\infty}H(\nu_{n},\nu^{\prime}_{n})>0.

Moreover, under the assumption that
\nu_{n}\sim\nu^{\prime}_{n}
for each
n\geq 0
, the measures
\nu
and
\nu^{\prime}
are either orthogonal or equivalent.

Note that this is not exactly what is proved in
[
17
]
: the main result of
[
17
]
states that under the assumption that
\nu_{n}\sim\nu^{\prime}_{n}
for all
n
, the measures
\nu
and
\nu^{\prime}
are equivalent if
\prod_{n=0}^{\infty}H(\nu_{n},\nu^{\prime}_{n})>0
and orthogonal otherwise. However, the above version of the theorem is certainly well-known (it is stated for example in
[
21
]
), and it can be obtained by slight modifications of Kakutani’s original proof; see Section
6
for more details.

5.3.
Gaussian product measures

A particularly interesting class of product measures is that of
Gaussian product measures
. Our definition will be very restrictive: we will say that a product measure
\mu=\otimes_{n\geq 0}\mu_{n}
on
\Omega=\mathbb{K}^{\mathbb{Z}_{+}}
is a Gaussian product measure if for each
n\geq 0
, the measure
\mu_{n}
is a Gaussian measure on
\mathbb{K}
of the form
\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{n}^{2})
for some
\sigma_{n}>0
,
i.e.

\mu_{n}
is a centered Gaussian measure with covariance matrix
\sigma_{n}^{2}I_{\mathbb{K}}
. Accordingly, we say that a measure
m
on
X=\ell_{p}
is a Gaussian product measure if
m
is the restriction to
\ell_{p}
of a Gaussian product measure
\mu
on
\Omega
such that
\mu(\ell_{p})=1
.

By Fernique’s integrability theorem (see e.g.
[
9
]
), if
m
is a Gaussian measure on
X=\ell_{p}
, then
m
admits moments of all orders and in particular
\int_{X}\|x\|^{p}dm<\infty
. It follows that if
\mu=\otimes_{n\geq 0}\mu_{n}
is a Gaussian product measure on
\Omega=\mathbb{K}^{\mathbb{Z}_{+}}
, then
\mu
is supported on
\ell_{p}
if and only if
\int_{\Omega}\bigl(\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}|t_{n}|^{p}\bigr)\,d\mu(t)<\infty
. Moreover, if
\mu
is invariant under some weighted shift
B_{\boldsymbol{w}}
, then
\int_{\Omega}|t_{n}|^{p}d\mu(t)=\frac{1}{|w_{1}\cdots w_{n}|^{p}}\,\int_{\mathbb{K}}|t|^{p}d\mu_{0}(t)
for all
n\geq 1
by Lemma
5.2
. Altogether, we see that a weighted shift
B_{\boldsymbol{w}}
acting on
\ell_{p}
admits an invariant Gaussian product measure if and only if
\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{|w_{1}\cdots w_{n}|^{p}}<\infty
, and that if this holds, then any
B_{\boldsymbol{w}}\,
- invariant Gaussian product measure on
\mathbb{K}^{\mathbb{Z}_{+}}
is supported on
\ell_{p}
. (For the “only if” part, one could also have used Proposition
2.1
rather than Fernique’s integrability theorem.) We refer the reader to
[
2
]
,
[
3
]
,
[
5
]
and
[
4
]
*Chapter 5 for an in-depth study of invariant Gaussian measures for operators on Hilbert or Banach spaces. We just point out here that if
{\boldsymbol{w}}
is a weight sequence such that
\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{|w_{1}\cdots w_{n}|^{p}}<\infty
and if
m
is a

B_{\boldsymbol{w}}\,
- invariant Gaussian product measure on
\ell_{p}
, then
m
is an ergodic measure with full support for
B_{\boldsymbol{w}}
(see Fact
5.5
).

The next theorem will provide a necessary and sufficient condition for two weighted backward shifts
B_{\boldsymbol{u}}
and
B_{\boldsymbol{v}}
on
X=\ell_{p}
to admit equivalent invariant Gaussian product measures.

Theorem 5.7
.

Let
{\boldsymbol{u}}
and
{\boldsymbol{v}}
be two weight sequences, and let
\mu_{\boldsymbol{u}}=\otimes_{n\geq 0}\mu_{{\boldsymbol{u}},n}
and
\mu_{\boldsymbol{v}}=\otimes_{n\geq 0}\mu_{{\boldsymbol{v}},n}
be two Gaussian product measures on
\mathbb{K}^{\mathbb{Z}_{+}}
invariant under
B_{\boldsymbol{u}}
and
B_{\boldsymbol{v}}
respectively, with
\mu_{{\boldsymbol{u}},0}=\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2})
and
\mu_{{\boldsymbol{v}},0}=\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{\prime 2})
. Then
\mu_{{\boldsymbol{u}}}
and
\mu_{{\boldsymbol{v}}}
are either equivalent or orthogonal, and they are equivalent if and only if

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\left(1-{\frac{\sigma^{\prime}}{\sigma}}\,{\frac{|{u_{1}\cdots u_{n}}|}{|{v_{1}\cdots v_{n}}|}}\,\right)^{2}<\infty.

(5.1)

From this result, we immediately deduce

Corollary 5.8
.

Let
B_{\boldsymbol{u}}
and
B_{\boldsymbol{v}}
be two weighted shifts on
X=\ell_{p}
, where the weight sequences
{\boldsymbol{u}}
and
{\boldsymbol{v}}
are such that
\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{|u_{1}\cdots u_{n}|^{p}}<\infty
and
\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{|v_{1}\cdots v_{n}|^{p}}<\infty
. The following are equivalent.

(a)

B_{\boldsymbol{u}}
and
B_{\boldsymbol{v}}
admit equivalent invariant Gaussian product measures.

(b)

There exists a constant
\kappa>0
such that
\sum\limits_{n=1}^{\infty}\left(1-\kappa\,{\frac{|{u_{1}\cdots u_{n}}|}{|{v_{1}\cdots v_{n}}|}}\,\right)^{2}<\infty
.

Here is another consequence of Theorem
5.7
.

Corollary 5.9
.

Let
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}
be a countable family of weight sequences. Assume that
\sum\limits_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{|{w_{1}\cdots w_{n}}|^{p}}<\infty
for all
{\boldsymbol{w}}\in\boldsymbol{\Lambda}
and
\sum\limits_{n=1}^{\infty}\left(1-{\frac{|{u_{1}\cdots u_{n}}|}{|{v_{1}\cdots v_{n}}|}}\,\right)^{2}<\infty
for any
\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}\in\boldsymbol{\Lambda}
. Then, there exists a Gaussian measure with full support
m
on
X=\ell_{p}
with the following property: given any sequence of Borel sets
(A_{i})_{i\geq 0}\subseteq X
with
m(A_{i})>0
, there exists
x\in X
such that for every
{\boldsymbol{w}}\in\mathbf{\Lambda}
and all
i\geq 0
, the set
\mathcal{N}_{B_{\boldsymbol{w}}}(x,A_{i}):=\{n\in\mathbb{N};\;B_{\boldsymbol{w}}^{n}x\in A_{i}\}
has a positive density.

Proof.

For each
{\boldsymbol{w}}\in\boldsymbol{\Lambda}
, let
m_{\boldsymbol{w}}=\otimes_{n\geq 0}\mu_{{\boldsymbol{w}},n}
be the
B_{\boldsymbol{w}}\,
- invariant Gaussian product measure on
\ell_{p}
defined by
\mu_{{\boldsymbol{w}},0}:=\mathcal{N}(0,1)
. This is an ergodic measure with full support for
B_{\boldsymbol{w}}
. Since the measures
m_{\boldsymbol{w}}
,
{\boldsymbol{w}}\in\boldsymbol{\Lambda}
are pairwise equivalent by Theorem
5.7
and since
\mathbf{\Lambda}
is countable, the result follows from the pointwise ergodic theorem, taking
m:=m_{{\boldsymbol{w}}_{0}}
for any
{\boldsymbol{w}}_{0}\in\mathbf{\Lambda}
. ∎

Remark 5.10
.

Taking as
(A_{i})_{i\geq 0}
a countable basis of open sets for
X
in Corollary
5.9
, we get a vector
x\in X
which is frequently hypercyclic for all operators
B_{\boldsymbol{w}}
,
{\boldsymbol{w}}\in\mathbf{\Lambda}
. However, it turns out that something much stronger holds true: if
{\boldsymbol{u}}
and
{\boldsymbol{v}}
are two weight sequences such that
\frac{{u_{1}\cdots u_{n}}}{{v_{1}\cdots v_{n}}}
has a non-zero limit as
n\to\infty
, then
B_{\boldsymbol{u}}
and
B_{\boldsymbol{v}}
have in fact
the same
frequently hypercyclic vectors. This result, which is due to S. Charpentier and the third author, will be proved in Section
6
.

Before proving Theorem
5.7
, we use Corollary
5.8
to give an example of two non-orthogonal weighted shifts sharing no non-zero periodic points.

Example 5.11
.

Let
{\boldsymbol{u}}
and
{\boldsymbol{v}}
be the weight sequences defined as follows:

{\boldsymbol{u}}_{n}:=2\qquad{\rm and}\qquad{\boldsymbol{v}}_{n}:=2\,\frac{1+\varepsilon_{n}}{1+\varepsilon_{n-1}},

where
\varepsilon_{0}=0
and
(\varepsilon_{n})_{n\geq 1}
is a decreasing sequence of positive real numbers such that
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\varepsilon_{n}^{2}<\infty
. Then
B_{\boldsymbol{u}}
and
B_{\boldsymbol{v}}
acting on
X=\ell_{p}
share no non-zero periodic point, but they admit equivalent invariant Gaussian product measures (and hence they are not orthogonal).

Proof.

We have
{u_{1}\cdots u_{n}}=2^{n}
and
{v_{1}\cdots v_{n}}=2^{n}(1+\varepsilon_{n})
for all
n\geq 1
. In particular,
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{({u_{1}\cdots u_{n}})^{p}}<\infty
and
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{({v_{1}\cdots v_{n}})^{p}}<\infty
. Since
\frac{{u_{1}\cdots u_{n}}}{{v_{1}\cdots v_{n}}}=\frac{1}{(1+\varepsilon_{n})}
is increasing, the conditions of Theorem
4.2
cannot be satisfied, so
B_{\boldsymbol{u}}
and
B_{\boldsymbol{v}}
do not share any non-zero periodic point. However

1-{\frac{{u_{1}\cdots u_{n}}}{{v_{1}\cdots v_{n}}}}=1-\frac{1}{1+\varepsilon_{n}}\sim\varepsilon_{n}\qquad\hbox{as $n\to\infty$},

so
B_{\boldsymbol{u}}
and
B_{\boldsymbol{v}}
admit equivalent invariant Gaussian product measures by Corollary
5.8
.
∎

It is now time to prove Theorem
5.7
.

Proof of Theorem
5.7
.

In what follows, we set
d:=1
if
\mathbb{K}=\mathbb{R}
and
d:=2
if
\mathbb{K}=\mathbb{C}
.

By Kakutani’s Theorem, we just have to show that
\prod_{n=1}^{\infty}H(\mu_{{\boldsymbol{u}},n},\mu_{{\boldsymbol{v}},n})>0
if and only if (
5.1
) is satisfied.

By Lemma
5.2
, the measures
\mu_{{\boldsymbol{u}},n}
and
\mu_{{\boldsymbol{v}},n}
are uniquely determined by
\mu_{{\boldsymbol{u}},0}=\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2})
and
\mu_{{\boldsymbol{v}},0}=\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{\prime 2})
. Explicitely,

\mu_{{\boldsymbol{u}},n}=\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{n}^{2})\qquad\hbox{with}\quad\sigma_{n}^{2}=\frac{\sigma^{2}}{|{u_{1}\cdots u_{n}}|^{2}}

and

\mu_{{\boldsymbol{v}},n}=\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{n}^{\prime 2})\qquad\hbox{with}\quad\sigma_{n}^{\prime 2}=\frac{\sigma^{\prime 2}}{|{v_{1}\cdots v_{n}}|^{2}}\cdot

The computation of
H(\mu_{{\boldsymbol{u}},n},\mu_{{\boldsymbol{v}},n})
now relies on the following fact.

Fact 5.12
.

If
\mu
and
\mu^{\prime}
are two Gaussian measures on
\mathbb{K}
of the form
\mu=\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2})
and
\mu^{\prime}=\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{\prime 2})
, then

H(\mu,\mu^{\prime})=\left(\frac{2\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}{\sigma^{2}+\sigma^{\prime 2}}\right)^{d/2}=\left(\frac{2(\sigma^{\prime}/\sigma)}{1+(\sigma^{\prime}/\sigma)^{2}}\right)^{d/2}\cdot

Proof of Fact
5.12
.

The measures
\mu
and
\mu^{\prime}
are absolutely continuous with respect to Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{K}
with densities
\frac{1}{({2\pi}\sigma^{2})^{d/2}}e^{-|t|^{2}/2\sigma^{2}}
and
\frac{1}{({2\pi}\sigma^{\prime 2})^{d/2}}e^{-|t|^{2}/2\sigma^{\prime 2}}
. Hence,

\displaystyle H(\mu,\mu^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{({2\pi\sigma\sigma^{\prime})^{d/2}}}\,\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{-\frac{1}{4}\left(\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}+\frac{1}{\sigma^{\prime 2}}\right)|t|^{2}}dt=\frac{1}{({2\pi\sigma\sigma^{\prime})^{d/2}}}\times\left(\frac{4\pi}{\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}+\frac{1}{\sigma^{\prime 2}}}\right)^{d/2}\cdot

∎

Going back to the measures
m_{\boldsymbol{u}}=\otimes_{n\geq 0}\mu_{{\boldsymbol{u}},n}
and
m_{\boldsymbol{v}}=\otimes_{n\geq 0}\mu_{{\boldsymbol{v}},n}
, let us set for each
n\geq 1
:

\lambda_{n}:={\frac{\sigma^{\prime}_{n}}{\sigma_{n}}}={\frac{\sigma^{\prime}}{\sigma}}\,{\frac{|{u_{1}\cdots u_{n}}|}{|{v_{1}\cdots v_{n}}|}}\cdot

By Fact
5.12
, we have

H(\mu_{{\boldsymbol{u}},n},\mu_{{\boldsymbol{v}},n})=\left(\frac{2\,\lambda_{n}}{{1+\lambda_{n}^{2}}}\right)^{d/2}\cdot

Since
0\leq H(\mu_{{\boldsymbol{u}},n},\mu_{{\boldsymbol{v}},n})\leq 1
for all
n\geq 1
, it follows that

\prod_{n=1}^{\infty}H(\mu_{{\boldsymbol{u}},n},\mu_{{\boldsymbol{v}},n})>0\quad\hbox{if and only if}\quad\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\left(1-\left(\frac{2\,\lambda_{n}}{{1+\lambda_{n}^{2}}}\right)^{d/2}\right)<\infty.

This can be satisfied only if
\frac{2\,\lambda_{n}}{{1+\lambda_{n}^{2}}}\to 1
as
n\to\infty
; and an examination of the function
\Psi(\lambda):=\frac{2\,\lambda}{{1+\lambda^{2}}}
shows that this is equivalent to the fact that
\lambda_{n}\to 1
. In this case, writing
\lambda_{n}=1+u_{n}
and
using Taylor’s formula, we see that

1-\left(\frac{2\,\lambda_{n}}{{1+\lambda_{n}^{2}}}\right)^{d/2}\sim\frac{d}{4}\,u_{n}^{2}=\frac{d}{4}\,(1-\lambda_{n})^{2}.

So we get that

\prod_{n=1}^{\infty}H(\mu_{{\boldsymbol{u}},n},\mu_{{\boldsymbol{v}},n})>0\quad\hbox{if and only if}\quad\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}(1-\lambda_{n})^{2}<\infty,

which is (
5.1
). Theorem
5.7
is proved.
∎

5.4.
Products of absolutely continuous measures

It is natural to believe that some condition strictly weaker than (b) of Corollary
5.8
might still yield the existence of (non-Gaussian) equivalent non-trivial invariant product measures for the backward shifts
B_{\boldsymbol{u}}
and
B_{\boldsymbol{v}}
. The next theorem shows that this is in fact not the case, at least for
product measures whose marginals are absolutely continuous with respect to Lebesgue measure.

We point out that it is possible to deduce parts (1ii) and (2) of this theorem from a Theorem of Shepp
[
21
]
concerning (non-)orthogonality of translates of a product measure on
\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{Z}_{+}}
, namely
[
21
]
*Theorem 1. However, we are going to give here a self-contained proof which looks rather different from what is done in
[
21
]
(except, of course, for the use of Kakutani’s Theorem), and we will indicate in Section
6
how Shepp’s result can be used in our context; see Theorem
6.11
.

For simplicity, we will assume that
\mathbb{K}=\mathbb{R}
and that all the weights are positive.

Theorem 5.13
.

Let
{\boldsymbol{u}}
and
{\boldsymbol{v}}
be two positive weight sequences. Assume that the backward shifts
B_{\boldsymbol{u}}
and
B_{\boldsymbol{v}}
acting on the real space
X=\ell_{p}
admit invariant product measures
m_{\boldsymbol{u}}=\otimes_{n\geq 0}\mu_{{\boldsymbol{u}},n}
and
m_{{\boldsymbol{v}}}=\otimes_{n\geq 0}\mu_{{\boldsymbol{v}},n}
such that
\mu_{{\boldsymbol{u}},0}
and
\mu_{{\boldsymbol{v}},0}
are absolutely continuous with respect to Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{R}
,
\mu_{{\boldsymbol{u}},0}=\mathtt{p}(t)dt
and
\mu_{{\boldsymbol{v}},0}=\mathtt{q}(t)dt
.

(1)

If
m_{\boldsymbol{u}}
and
m_{\boldsymbol{v}}
are not orthogonal, then:

(i)

the quotient
\frac{{u_{1}\cdots u_{n}}}{{v_{1}\cdots v_{n}}}
has a limit
a\in(0,\infty)
as
n\to\infty
, and
\mathtt{q}(t)=a\mathtt{p}(at)
almost everywhere;

(ii)

we have
\sum\limits_{n=1}^{\infty}\left(1-\frac{1}{a}\,{\frac{{u_{1}\cdots u_{n}}}{{v_{1}\cdots v_{n}}}}\right)^{2}<\infty
.

(2)

Assume that the function
f:=\sqrt{\mathtt{p}}
is continuous on
\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
and
\mathcal{C}^{1}
-
smooth except at a finite number of points with
tf^{\prime}(t)\in L_{2}(\mathbb{R})
, and that
\mathtt{q}(t)=a\mathtt{p}(at)
for some
a\in(0,\infty)
. Then,
m_{\boldsymbol{u}}
and
m_{\boldsymbol{v}}
are non-orthogonal
if and only if

\sum\limits_{n=1}^{\infty}\left(1-\frac{1}{a}\,{\frac{{u_{1}\cdots u_{n}}}{{v_{1}\cdots v_{n}}}}\right)^{2}<\infty
.

(3)

Assume that
f=\sqrt{\mathtt{p}}
is
\mathcal{C}^{1}
-
smooth except at a finite number of points with
tf^{\prime}(t)\in L_{2}(\mathbb{R})
, that
\mathtt{p}
has at least one discontinuity point in

\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
, and that
\mathtt{q}(t)=a\mathtt{p}(at)
for some
a\in(0,\infty)
. Then,
m_{\boldsymbol{u}}
and
m_{\boldsymbol{v}}
are non-orthogonal if and only if
\sum\limits_{n=1}^{\infty}\left|1-\frac{1}{a}\,{\frac{{u_{1}\cdots u_{n}}}{{v_{1}\cdots v_{n}}}}\right|<\infty
.

From part (1) of this theorem and Theorem
5.7
, we immediately deduce

Corollary 5.14
.

Let
B_{\boldsymbol{u}}
and
B_{\boldsymbol{v}}
be two shifts with positive weights acting on the real space
\ell_{p}
. The following are equivalent.

(a)

B_{\boldsymbol{u}}
and
B_{\boldsymbol{v}}
are not orthogonal with respect to product measures whose marginals are absolutely continuous with respect to Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{R}
.

(b)

There exists
\kappa>0
such that
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\left(1-\kappa\,\frac{{u_{1}\cdots u_{n}}}{{v_{1}\cdots v_{n}}}\right)^{2}<\infty
.

(c)

B_{\boldsymbol{u}}
and
B_{\boldsymbol{v}}
admit equivalent invariant Gaussian product measures.

We point out that Corollary
5.14
is also valid in the complex case and for not necessarily positive weights; see Corollary
6.12
.

For the proof of Theorem
5.13
, we will need the following lemma.

Lemma 5.15
.

Let
h\in L_{2}(\mathbb{R})
be non-zero and real-valued, and let
\mathcal{P}h:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
be the function defined by

\mathcal{P}h(\alpha):=\int_{\mathbb{R}}h(x)h(x+\alpha)\,dx.

(a)

The function
\mathcal{P}h
is continuous and even, with
\mathcal{P}h(\alpha)\leq\|h\|_{2}^{2}
for avery
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
and
\mathcal{P}h(0)=\|h\|_{2}^{2}
.

(b)

If
h
belongs to the Sobolev space
W^{1,2}(\mathbb{R})
, then
\mathcal{P}h
is
\mathcal{C}^{2}
-
smooth on
\mathbb{R}
, with
(\mathcal{P}h)^{\prime}(0)=0
and
(\mathcal{P}h)^{\prime\prime}(0)<0
. In particular,
\|h\|_{2}^{2}-\mathcal{P}h(\alpha)\sim c\alpha^{2}
as
\alpha\to 0
, for some constant
c>0
.

(c)

If
h\not\in W^{1,2}(\mathbb{R})
, then
(\|h\|_{2}^{2}-\mathcal{P}h(\alpha))/\alpha^{2}\to\infty
as
\alpha\to 0
.

(d)

Assume that
h
is
\mathcal{C}^{1}
-
smooth except at finitely many points, with
h^{\prime}\in L_{2}(\mathbb{R})
, and that
h
has at least one discontinuity point. Then
\mathcal{P}h
is left-differentiable and right-differentiable at
0
, with
(\mathcal{P}h)^{\prime}(0^{-})>0
and
(\mathcal{P}h)^{\prime}(0^{+})<0
.

This lemma has the following immediate consequence.

Corollary 5.16
.

For any non-zero real-valued
h\in L_{2}(\mathbb{R})
, there exists a constant
c>0
such that
\|h\|_{2}^{2}-\mathcal{P}h(\alpha)\geq c\alpha^{2}
for
\alpha
sufficiently close to
0
.

Note that using Plancherel’s formula, it can be seen that this can be stated equivalently as follows: for any
g\in L_{2}(\mathbb{R})\setminus\{0\}
with real-valued Fourier transform, there exists a constant
c
such that
\int_{\mathbb{R}}|g(t)|^{2}\bigl(1-\cos(\alpha t)\bigr)\,dt\geq c\alpha^{2}
for
\alpha
sufficiently close to
0
.

Proof of Lemma
5.15
.

For notational brevity, let us set
F:=\mathcal{P}h
.

(a) We have
F(\alpha)=\langle h,\tau_{\alpha}h\rangle_{L_{2}(\mathbb{R})}
, where
\tau_{\alpha}h(x):=h(x+\alpha)
, so
F
is continuous by continuity of the map
\alpha\mapsto\tau_{\alpha}h
from
\mathbb{R}
into
L_{2}(\mathbb{R})
. It is clear that
F
is even and that
F(0)=\|h\|_{2}^{2}
. Finally,
F(\alpha)\leq\|h\|_{2}^{2}
by the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality.

(b) Since
h\in W^{1,2}(\mathbb{R})
, the map
\alpha\mapsto\tau_{\alpha}h
is
\mathcal{C}^{1}\,
- smooth from
\mathbb{R}
into
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
, with derivative

\alpha\mapsto\tau_{\alpha}h^{\prime}
. Since
F(\alpha)=\langle h,\tau_{\alpha}h\rangle_{L_{2}(\mathbb{R})}
, it follows that
F
is
\mathcal{C}^{1}\,
- smooth on
\mathbb{R}
, with

\displaystyle F^{\prime}(\alpha)=\langle h,\tau_{\alpha}h^{\prime}\rangle_{L_{2}(\mathbb{R})}=\int_{\mathbb{R}}h(u)h^{\prime}(u+\alpha)\,du=\int_{\mathbb{R}}h(u-\alpha)h^{\prime}(u)\,du.

Hence, the same argument shows that
F
is in fact
\mathcal{C}^{2}\,
- smooth on
\mathbb{R}
, with

F^{\prime\prime}(\alpha)=-\int_{\mathbb{R}}h^{\prime}(u-\alpha)h^{\prime}(u)\,du.

We have
F^{\prime}(0)=0
because
F
is even;
and

F^{\prime\prime}(0)=-\int_{\mathbb{R}}h^{\prime 2}<0.

The strict inequality holds because if we had
F^{\prime\prime}(0)=0
, then
h^{\prime}
would be equal to
0
almost everywhere, so
h
would be
0
since it belongs to
W^{1,2}(\mathbb{R})
.

(c) Note that

F(\alpha)=\langle h,\tau_{\alpha}h\rangle_{L_{2}(\mathbb{R})}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\|h\|_{2}^{2}+\|\tau_{\alpha}h\|^{2}-\|h-\tau_{\alpha}h\|_{2}^{2}\right)=\|h\|_{2}^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\|h-\tau_{\alpha}h\|_{2}^{2},

so that
\|h\|_{2}^{2}-F(\alpha)=\frac{1}{2}\|h-\tau_{\alpha}h\|_{2}^{2}
. Now since
h\not\in W^{1,2}(\mathbb{R})
, we have
\lim_{\alpha\to 0}\left\|\frac{\tau_{\alpha}h-h}{\alpha}\right\|=\infty
. Indeed, otherwise one could find a sequence
(\alpha_{n})
tending to
0
such that
\frac{\tau_{\alpha_{n}}h-h}{\alpha_{n}}
has a weak limit

, and this would give that
h\in W^{1,2}(\mathbb{R})
with
h^{\prime}=u
. This proves (c).

(d) Since
F
is an even function, it is enough to show that
F
is right-differentiable at
0
with
F^{\prime}(0^{+})<0
.

Let
u_{0}<\dots<u_{N}
be the discontinuity points of
h
. Also, let
I_{0}:=(-\infty,u_{0})
,
I_{N+1}:=(u_{n},\infty)
and
I_{k}:=(u_{k-1},u_{k})
for
1\leq k\leq N
. Note that by assumption on
h
, the restriction of
h
to each interval
I_{k}
belongs to the Sobolev space
W^{1,2}(I_{k})
. In particular,
h
has a left limit
h(u_{k}^{-})
and a right limit
h(u_{k}^{+})
at each point
u_{k}
.

For every
\alpha>0
, we have

F(\alpha)=\sum_{k=0}^{N}\int_{I_{k}}h(u)h(u+\alpha)\,du=:\sum_{k=0}^{N+1}F_{k}(\alpha).

We consider separately the functions
F_{0}
,
F_{N+1}
and
F_{k}
for
1\leq k\leq N
.

Let us start with
F_{k}
,
1\leq k\leq N
. For
0<\alpha<u_{k}-u_{k-1}
, we write

F_{k}(\alpha)=\int_{u_{k-1}}^{u_{k}-\alpha}h(u)h(u+\alpha)\,du+\int_{u_{k}-\alpha}^{u_{k}}h(u)h(u+\alpha)\,du=:F_{k,1}(\alpha)+F_{k,2}(\alpha).

Consider the open triangle
\Omega:=\{(\alpha,\beta);\;u_{k-1}<\beta<u_{k}\;{\rm and}\;0<\alpha<u_{k}-\beta\}
, and the function

. Since the restriction of

to

belongs to the Sobolev space

, the map
G
is

- smooth on
\Omega

and
\partial_{\beta}G(\alpha,\beta)=h(\beta)h(\beta+\alpha)
. Moreover,

is continuous on
\overline{\Omega}
and its partial derivatives extend continuously to

\partial_{\alpha}G(\alpha,u_{k}-\alpha)=\int_{u_{k-1}}^{u_{k}-\alpha}h(u)h^{\prime}(u+\alpha)\,du

\partial_{\beta}G(\alpha,u_{k}-\alpha)=h(u_{k}-\alpha)h(u_{k}^{-})

F_{k,1}(\alpha)=G(\alpha,u_{k}-\alpha)
is
\mathcal{C}^{1}
- smooth on

. To see this, observe that for any

F_{k,1}^{\prime}(\alpha)\to\int_{u_{k-1}}^{u_{k}}h(u)h^{\prime}(u)\,du-h(u_{k}^{-})^{2}
as

F_{k,1}

\displaystyle F_{k,1}^{\prime}(0^{+})

\displaystyle=\int_{u_{k-1}}^{u_{k}}h(u)h^{\prime}(u)\,du-h(u_{k}^{-})^{2}

\displaystyle=\left[\frac{1}{2}h(u)^{2}\right]_{u_{k-1}}^{u_{k}}-h(u_{k}^{-})^{2}

Similar arguments show that

. Hence,

is right-differentiable at

, with

One shows in the same way that
F_{0}(\alpha)=\int_{-\infty}^{u_{0}}h(u)h(u+\alpha)\,du
and
F_{N+1}(\alpha)=\int_{u_{N}}^{\infty}h(u)h(u+\alpha)\,du
are right-differentiable at

, with

F_{0}^{\prime}(0^{+})=h(u_{0}^{-})h(u_{0}^{+})-\frac{1}{2}\,h(u_{0}^{-})^{2}\qquad{\rm and}\qquad F_{N+1}^{\prime}(0^{+})=-\frac{1}{2}\,h(u_{N}^{+})^{2}.

Altogether, we see that
F=\sum_{k=0}^{N+1}F_{k}
is right-differentiable at
0
, with

\displaystyle F^{\prime}(0^{+})

\displaystyle=h(u_{0}^{-})h(u_{0}^{+})-\frac{1}{2}\,h(u_{0}^{-})^{2}

\displaystyle\qquad+\sum_{k=1}^{N}\left(h(u_{k}^{-})h(u_{k}^{+})-\frac{1}{2}\,\bigl(h(u_{k}^{-})^{2}+h(u_{k-1}^{+})^{2}\bigr)\right)-\frac{1}{2}\,h(u_{N}^{+})^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{N}h(u_{k}^{-})h(u_{k}^{+})-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=0}^{N}\bigl(h(u_{k}^{-})^{2}+h(u_{k}^{+})^{2}\bigr)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=0}^{N}\left(h(u_{k}^{+})-h(u_{k}^{-})\right)^{2}<0.

∎

We can now give the proof of Theorem
5.13
.

Proof of Theorem
5.13
.

In what follows, we set
f:=\sqrt{\mathtt{p}}
and
g:=\sqrt{\mathtt{q}}
. Also, for each
n\geq 1
, let

\lambda_{n}:=\frac{{u_{1}\cdots u_{n}}}{{v_{1}\cdots v_{n}}}\cdot

The following simple computation will be essential for the proof.

Fact 5.17
.

For every
n\geq 1
, we have

H(\mu_{{\boldsymbol{u}},n},\mu_{{\boldsymbol{v}},n})=\Psi(\lambda_{n}),

where
\Psi:(0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
is the function defined by

\Psi(\lambda):=\sqrt{\lambda}\int_{\mathbb{R}}f(\lambda t)g(t)\,dt.

Proof of Fact
5.17
.

Denoting by
\tau
the Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{R}
, we have

\frac{d\mu_{{\boldsymbol{u}},n}}{d\tau}(t)={u_{1}\cdots u_{n}}\ \mathtt{p}({u_{1}\cdots u_{n}}t)\qquad{\rm and}\qquad\frac{d\mu_{{\boldsymbol{v}},n}}{d\tau}(t)={v_{1}\cdots v_{n}}\ \mathtt{q}({v_{1}\cdots v_{n}}t).

Hence

\displaystyle H(\mu_{{\boldsymbol{u}},n},\mu_{{\boldsymbol{v}},n})

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\sqrt{{u_{1}\cdots u_{n}}}\,f({u_{1}\cdots u_{n}}t)\times\sqrt{{v_{1}\cdots v_{n}}}\,g({v_{1}\cdots v_{n}}t)\,dt

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{{u_{1}\cdots u_{n}}}{{v_{1}\cdots v_{n}}}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}f\left({\frac{{u_{1}\cdots u_{n}}}{{v_{1}\cdots v_{n}}}}\,s\right)\,g(s)\,ds

\displaystyle=\Psi(\lambda_{n}).

∎

By Fact
5.17
and Kakutani’s Theorem, we know that
m_{\boldsymbol{u}}
and
m_{\boldsymbol{v}}
are not orthogonal if and only if

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}(1-\Psi(\lambda_{n}))<\infty;

(5.2)

and in that case, we have in particular that

\Psi(\lambda_{n})\to 1\quad\hbox{as $n\to\infty$}.

(5.3)

Proof of
(1i)
in Theorem
5.13
.

We first note that we must have
\varliminf\lambda_{n}>0
and
\varlimsup\lambda_{n}<\infty
. Indeed, otherwise
B_{\boldsymbol{u}}
and
B_{\boldsymbol{v}}
are orthogonal with respect to measures not charging
\ker(e_{0}^{*})
by Proposition

. The measures

\mu_{\boldsymbol{u}}
and
\mu_{\boldsymbol{v}}
have this property since
\mu_{{\boldsymbol{u}},0}
and
\mu_{{\boldsymbol{v}},0}
do not charge
\{0\}
, so this cannot happen.

Now, the key point is the following fact.

Fact 5.18
.

The function
\Psi
is continuous on
(0,\infty)
with
0\leq\Psi(\lambda)\leq 1
, there is at most one
\lambda\in(0,\infty)
such that
\Psi(\lambda)=1
, and if
\Psi(\lambda)=1
then
\mathtt{q}(t)=\lambda\mathtt{p}(\lambda t)
almost everywhere..

Proof of Fact
5.18
.

It is clear that
\Psi(\lambda)\geq 0
. Moreover, by the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, we have

\Psi(\lambda)\leq\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}}\lambda\mathtt{p}(\lambda t)\,dt\right)^{1/2}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mathtt{q}(t)\,dt\right)^{1/2}=1.

If
\Psi(\lambda)=1
then, by the equality case in Cauchy-Schwarz’s inequality and since
\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mathtt{p}=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mathtt{q}=1
, we must have
\mathtt{q}(t)=\lambda\mathtt{p}(\lambda t)
almost everywhere. It follows easily that there can be at most one
\lambda
such that
\Psi(\lambda)=1
.
Indeed, assume that
\Psi(\lambda)=1=\Psi(\lambda^{\prime})
for some
0<\lambda<\lambda^{\prime}
. Then
\lambda\mathtt{p}(\lambda t)=\lambda^{\prime}\mathtt{p}(\lambda^{\prime}t)
almost everywhere. Setting
c:=\lambda^{\prime}/\lambda>1
, it follows that
\mathtt{p}(x)=c\,\mathtt{p}(cx)
almost everywhere. Hence
\mathtt{p}(x)=c^{n}\mathtt{p}(c^{n}x)
almost everywhere
for every

. So, with

, we obtain that

\int_{I_{n}}\mathtt{p}(x)\,dx=\int_{I_{0}}c^{n}\mathtt{p}(c^{n}x)\,dx=\int_{I_{0}}\mathtt{p}(x)\,dx\qquad\hbox{for all $n\in\mathbb{Z}$}.

Since the intervals

form a partition of
(0,\infty)
and
\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathtt{p}(x)\,dx<\infty
, this implies that
\mathtt{p}(x)=0
almost everywhere on
(0,\infty)
. Similarly
\mathtt{p}(x)=0
almost everywhere on
(-\infty,0)
, hence
\mathtt{p}(x)=0
almost everywhere on
\mathbb{R}
, which is a contradiction since
\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mathtt{p}=1
.

Finally, to prove that
\Psi
is continuous, we note that
\Psi(\lambda)=\sqrt{\lambda}\,\langle f_{\lambda},g\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}
, where
f_{\lambda}(t):=f(\lambda t)
. Since
f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
, the map

\lambda\mapsto f_{\lambda}
is continuous from
(0,\infty)
into
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
; so
\Psi
is indeed continuous. ∎

It is now easy to conclude the proof of (1i). Since
\Psi(\lambda_{n})\to 1
and
\Psi
is continuous on
(0,\infty)
, any cluster point
\lambda\in(0,\infty)
of the sequence
(\lambda_{n})
must be such that
\Psi(\lambda)=1
; hence,
(\lambda_{n})
has at most one cluster point in
(0,\infty)
by Fact

. Since
0<\varliminf\lambda_{n}\leq\varlimsup\,\lambda_{n}<\infty
, it follows that
(\lambda_{n})
has a limit

; and by Fact

again, we have (

and)
\mathtt{q}(t)=a\mathtt{p}(at)
almost everywhere.∎

Proof of
(1ii)
in Theorem
5.13
.

Let
\Phi:(0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
and

\Phi(\lambda):=\int_{\mathbb{R}}f(t)f(\lambda t)\,dt\qquad{\rm and}\qquad\Theta(\lambda):=\sqrt{\lambda}\,\Phi(\lambda).

Since
g(t)=\sqrt{a}f(at)
almost everywhere by (1i), we have

\Psi(\lambda)=\sqrt{\lambda}\int_{\mathbb{R}}f(\lambda t)\times\sqrt{a}f(at)\,dt=\sqrt{\lambda/a}\,\Phi(\lambda/a)=\Theta(\lambda/a).

So, we see that
0\leq\Theta(\lambda)\leq 1
for every
\lambda\in(0,\infty)
, and by (
5.2
):

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\bigl(1-\Theta(\lambda_{n}/a)\bigr)<\infty.

(5.4)

From that, we have to deduce that

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\left(1-\frac{1}{a}\,\lambda_{n}\right)^{2}<\infty.

(5.5)

To do this, we perform a change of variable in order to apply Lemma
5.15
. If we set
\lambda=e^{\alpha}
, then

\displaystyle\Theta(\lambda)

\displaystyle=e^{\alpha/2}\left(\int_{0}^{+\infty}f(t)f(e^{\alpha}t)\,dt+\int_{-\infty}^{0}\ f(t)f(e^{\alpha}t)\,dt\right)

\displaystyle=e^{\alpha/2}\int_{\mathbb{R}}f(e^{u})f(e^{\alpha+u})\,e^{u}du+e^{\alpha/2}\int_{\mathbb{R}}f(-e^{u})f(-e^{\alpha+u})\,e^{u}du

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}h_{+}(u)h_{+}(u+\alpha)\,du+\int_{\mathbb{R}}h_{-}(u)h_{-}(u+\alpha)\,du,

where
h_{+}
and
h_{-}
are the functions defined on
\mathbb{R}
by

h_{+}(x):=f(e^{x})e^{x/2}\qquad{\rm and}\qquad h_{-}(x):=f(-e^{x})e^{x/2}.

Note that the functions
h_{+}
and
h_{-}
belong to
L_{2}(\mathbb{R})
, and that

\|h_{+}\|_{2}^{2}+\|h_{-}\|_{2}^{2}=\int_{\mathbb{R}}f^{2}=1.

So, with the notation of Lemma
5.15
, we have

\Theta(\lambda)=\mathcal{P}h_{+}\bigl(\log(\lambda)\bigr)+\mathcal{P}h_{-}\bigl(\log(\lambda)\bigr).

(5.6)

Since at least one of
h_{\pm}
is non-zero, it follows that there exists a constant
c>0
such that

1-\Theta(\lambda)\geq c\,\log(\lambda)^{2}
for
\lambda
sufficiently close to
1
. Hence
1-\Theta(\lambda)\geq c(\lambda-1)^{2}
for
\lambda
sufficiently close to
1
(and some other constant
c>0
), so that (
5.5
) is indeed a direct consequence of (
5.4
) since we already know that
\lambda_{n}\to a
. This concludes the proof of (1ii).
∎

Proof of
(2)
in Theorem
5.13
.

By Kakutani’s Theorem and with the notation of the proof of (1ii), we have to show that

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\bigl(1-\Theta(\lambda_{n}/a)\bigr)<\infty\quad\hbox{if and only if}\quad\sum\limits_{n=1}^{\infty}\left(1-\frac{1}{a}\,{\frac{{u_{1}\cdots u_{n}}}{{v_{1}\cdots v_{n}}}}\right)^{2}<\infty.

By (1ii), the “only if” implication is already known. To prove the converse, we keep the notation of the proof of (1ii). So we have as above

\Theta(\lambda)=\mathcal{P}h_{+}\bigl(\log(\lambda)\bigr)+\mathcal{P}h_{-}\bigl(\log(\lambda)\bigr),

where the functions
h_{+}
and
h_{-}
are defined by

h_{\pm}(x)=f(\pm e^{x})e^{x/2}.

Note that by assumption on
f
, the functions
h_{+}
and
h_{-}
are continuous on
\mathbb{R}
and
\mathcal{C}^{1}\,
- smooth except at a finite number of points. Moreover,
h^{\prime}_{\pm}\in L_{2}(\mathbb{R})
. Indeed, we have
h_{\pm}^{\prime}(x)=\pm f^{\prime}(\pm e^{x})e^{3x/2}+\frac{1}{2}f(\pm e^{x})e^{x/2}
almost everywhere, and both terms belong to
L_{2}(\mathbb{R})
since

\int_{\mathbb{R}}f^{\prime}(\pm e^{x})^{2}e^{3x}dx=\int_{\pm(0,\infty)}t^{2}f^{\prime}(t)^{2}dt<\infty\qquad{\rm and}

\int_{\mathbb{R}}f(\pm e^{x})^{2}e^{x}\,dx=\int_{\pm(0,\infty)}f(t)^{2}dt<\infty.

So
h_{\pm}
belongs to the Sobolev space
W^{1,2}(\mathbb{R})
. Hence, by Lemma
5.15
and since
\|h_{+}\|_{2}^{2}+\|h_{-}^{2}\|_{2}=1
, we know that the function
F:=\mathcal{P}h_{+}+\mathcal{P}h_{-}
is
\mathcal{C}^{2}
- smooth on
\mathbb{R}
, with
F(0)=1
,
F^{\prime}(0)=0
and
F^{\prime\prime}(0)<0
. It follows that there is a constant
c>0
such that
1-\Theta(\lambda)\sim c(\lambda-1)^{2}
as
\lambda\to 1
; and this concludes the proof of the “if” implication in (2).
∎

Proof of
(3)
in Theorem
5.13
.

We keep the notation of the proofs of (1ii) and (2). This time, we have to show that

\sum\limits_{n=1}^{\infty}\bigl(1-\Theta(\lambda_{n}/a)\bigr)<\infty\quad\hbox{if and only if}\quad\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\left|1-\frac{1}{a}\,\lambda_{n}\right|<\infty.

(5.7)

As above, let
F:=\mathcal{P}h_{+}+\mathcal{P}h_{-}
, so that
\Theta(\lambda)=F\bigl(\log(\lambda)\bigr)
. By assumption on

\mathtt{p}
, the functions
h_{+}(x)=f(e^{x})e^{x/2}
and
h_{-}(x)=f(-e^{x})e^{x/2}
are
\mathcal{C}^{1}
- smooth except at finitely many points, with
h^{\prime}_{\pm}\in L_{2}(\mathbb{R})
, and at least one of them has a discontinuity point. By Lemma
5.15
, it follows that there exists two constants
c>0
and
c^{\prime}<\infty
such that
c\,|\alpha|\leq 1-F(\alpha)\leq c^{\prime}\,|\alpha|
for
\alpha
sufficiently close to
0
. Hence
c\,|1-\lambda|\leq 1-\Theta(\lambda)\leq c^{\prime}\,|1-\lambda|
for
\lambda
sufficiently close to
1
, and (
5.7
) follows.
∎

The proof of Theorem
5.13
is now complete. ∎

Remark 5.19
.

The proofs of parts (2) and (3) of Theorem
5.13
rely on the local properties of the function
\Theta(\lambda)=\sqrt{\lambda}\int_{\mathbb{R}}f(t)f(\lambda t)\,dt
at

\lambda=1
. It is perhaps worth noticing that if
\Theta
happens to be differentiable at
\lambda=1
, then we necessarily
have
\Theta^{\prime}(1)=0
. This is because
\Theta
is “symmetric with respect to
1
”,
i.e.

\Theta(1/\lambda)=\Theta(\lambda)
.

Remark 5.20
.

Part (3) of Theorem
5.13
may be applied for example if
\mu_{{\boldsymbol{u}},0}
is the uniform distribution over some bounded interval
I\subseteq\mathbb{R}
and
\mu_{{\boldsymbol{v}},0}
is the uniform distribution over
\frac{1}{a}\,I
.

Remark 5.21
.

Part (2) of Theorem
5.7
remains valid under the following assumption on
f=\sqrt{p}
: the restrictions of
f
to the intervals
(0,\infty)
and
(-\infty,0)
have locally integrable derivatives (in the distribution sense), and
tf^{\prime}(t)\in L_{2}(\mathbb{R})
.

Proof.

Looking back at the proof of (2), we see that the only thing to check is that the functions
h_{+}
and
h_{-}
belong to the Sobolev space
W^{1,2}(\mathbb{R})
. We do this for
h_{+}
and, for notational simplicity, we set
h:=h_{+}
and we denote by
f
the restriction of
f
to
(0,\infty)
. So we have to show that if
f\in L_{2}(0,\infty)
has a distributional derivative
f^{\prime}\in L_{1}^{\rm loc}(0,\infty)
such that
tf(t)\in L_{2}(0,\infty)
, then
h(x)=f(e^{x})e^{x/2}\in W^{1,2}(\mathbb{R})
.

Since
f\in L_{2}(0,\infty)
, it is clear that
h\in L_{2}(\mathbb{R})
. To find the distributional derivative of
h
, let us fix a test function
\varphi\in\mathcal{D}(\mathbb{R})
. Let
\psi\in\mathcal{D}(0,\infty)
be the function defined by the relation
\varphi(x)=\psi(e^{x})e^{x/2}
,
i.e.

\psi(t)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{t}}\,\varphi(\log(t))
. Writing
\varphi^{\prime}(x)=e^{3x/2}\psi^{\prime}(e^{x})+\frac{1}{2}e^{x/2}\psi(e^{x})
and using the change of variable
x=\log(t)
, an elementary computation reveals that

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}h(x)\,\varphi^{\prime}(x)\,dx

\displaystyle=-\int_{0}^{\infty}\left(e^{3x/2}f^{\prime}(e^{x})+\frac{1}{2}\,e^{x/2}f(e^{x})\right)\varphi(x)\,dx.

This means that
h
has a distributional derivative
h^{\prime}\in L_{1}^{\rm loc}(\mathbb{R})
given (as expected) by
h^{\prime}(x)=e^{3x/2}f^{\prime}(e^{x})+\frac{1}{2}e^{x/2}f(e^{x}).
Since
f\in L_{2}(0,\infty)
and
tf^{\prime}(t)\in L_{2}(0,\infty)
, we see that
h^{\prime}\in L_{2}(\mathbb{R})
, hence
h\in W^{1,2}(\mathbb{R})
.

∎

5.5.
Products of discrete measures

Theorem
5.13
(3) shows that for weighted shifts acting on
\ell_{p}
, to admit non-orthogonal invariant measures which are products of absolutely continuous measures having some “singularities” is in fact a strictly stronger requirement on the weights than to admit equivalent
invariant Gaussian product measures. The next proposition goes along the same lines, in an even more dramatic way.

Proposition 5.22
.

Let
{\boldsymbol{u}}
and
{\boldsymbol{v}}
be two weight sequences.

(a)

If
B_{\boldsymbol{u}}
and
B_{\boldsymbol{v}}
admit non-orthogonal invariant product measures
m_{\boldsymbol{u}}=\otimes_{n\geq 0}\mu_{{\boldsymbol{u}},n}
and
m_{{\boldsymbol{v}}}=\otimes_{n\geq 0}\mu_{{\boldsymbol{v}},n}
such that
\mu_{{\boldsymbol{u}},0}
and
\mu_{{\boldsymbol{v}},0}
have non-zero discrete parts and do not charge
\{0\}
, then
\left|\frac{{u_{1}\cdots u_{n}}}{{v_{1}\cdots v_{n}}}\right|
is eventually constant
(
and hence
B_{\boldsymbol{u}}
and
B_{\boldsymbol{v}}
admit equivalent invariant Gaussian product measures
)
.

(b)

Assume that
B_{\boldsymbol{u}}
and
B_{\boldsymbol{v}}
admit equivalent non-trivial invariant product measures or, more generally, non-trivial invariant product measures
m_{\boldsymbol{u}}=\otimes_{n\geq 0}\mu_{{\boldsymbol{u}},n}
and
m_{{\boldsymbol{v}}}=\otimes_{n\geq 0}\mu_{{\boldsymbol{v}},n}
such that
\mu_{{\boldsymbol{u}},n}
and
\mu_{{\boldsymbol{v}},n}
have the same support for each
n\geq 0
. Let
S:={\rm supp}(\mu_{{\boldsymbol{u}},0})={\rm supp}(\mu_{{\boldsymbol{v}},0})
. If either
S
is compact or
0\not\in S
, then
\left|\frac{{u_{1}\cdots u_{n}}}{{v_{1}\cdots v_{n}}}\right|=1
for all
n\geq 1
; and hence
{\boldsymbol{u}}={\boldsymbol{v}}
if
{\boldsymbol{u}}
and
{\boldsymbol{v}}
are positive weight sequences.

Proof.

For each
n\geq 1
, let us set as usual
\lambda_{n}:=\frac{{u_{1}\cdots u_{n}}}{{v_{1}\cdots v_{n}}}\cdot

(a) Since the measures
\mu_{{\boldsymbol{u}},n}
and
\mu_{{\boldsymbol{v}},n}
do not charge
\{0\}
, one can find a countable multiplicative subgroup
\mathcal{S}
of
\mathbb{K}\setminus\{0\}
such that the discrete parts of all measures
\mu_{{\boldsymbol{u}},n}
and
\mu_{{\boldsymbol{v}},n}
are supported on
\mathcal{S}
and
\mathcal{S}
contains the set
\{u_{n};\;n\geq 1\}\cup\{v_{n};\;n\geq 1\}
. Denote by
\tau_{\mathcal{S}}
the counting measure on
\mathcal{S}
. Let also
\tau_{c}
be a continuous measure such that the continuous parts of all measures
\mu_{{\boldsymbol{u}},n}
and
\mu_{{\boldsymbol{v}},n}
are absolutely continuous with respect to
\tau_{c}
. Then, one can take
\tau:=\tau_{c}+\tau_{\mathcal{S}}
to compute

H(\mu_{{\boldsymbol{u}},n},\mu_{{\boldsymbol{v}},n})
, for every
n\geq 0
.

For each
n\geq 0
, write

\mu_{{\boldsymbol{u}},n}=\alpha_{n}\tau_{c}+\mathtt{p}_{n}\tau_{\mathcal{S}}\qquad{\rm and}\qquad\mu_{{\boldsymbol{v}},n}=\beta_{n}\tau_{c}+\mathtt{q}_{n}\tau_{\mathcal{S}}

for some non-negative measurable functions
\alpha_{n},\mathtt{p}_{n},\beta_{n},\mathtt{q}_{n}
. Without loss of generality, we may assume that
\alpha_{n}\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{S}}=\beta_{n}\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{S}}=0
and
\mathtt{p}_{n}\mathbf{1}_{\mathbb{K}\setminus\mathcal{S}}=\mathtt{q}_{n}\mathbf{1}_{\mathbb{K}\setminus\mathcal{S}}=0
, so that we also have

\mu_{{\boldsymbol{u}},n}=(\alpha_{n}+\mathtt{p}_{n})\tau\qquad{\rm and}\qquad\mu_{{\boldsymbol{v}},n}=(\beta_{n}+\mathtt{q}_{n})\tau.

Hence,

H(\mu_{{\boldsymbol{u}},n},\mu_{{\boldsymbol{v}},n})=\int_{\mathbb{K}}\sqrt{\alpha_{n}+\mathtt{p}_{n}}\,\sqrt{\beta_{n}+\mathtt{q}_{n}}\,d\tau=\langle\,\sqrt{\alpha_{n}+\mathtt{p}_{n}},\sqrt{\beta_{n}+\mathtt{q}_{n}}\;\rangle_{L_{2}(\tau)}.

Now, by Kakutani’s Theorem we know that
H(\mu_{{\boldsymbol{u}},n},\mu_{{\boldsymbol{v}},n})\to 1
as
n\to\infty
. Since
\|\sqrt{\alpha_{n}+\mathtt{p}_{n}}\|_{L_{2}(\tau)}=\|\sqrt{\beta_{n}+\mathtt{q}_{n}}\|_{L_{2}(\tau)}=1
, it follows that

\bigl\|\sqrt{\alpha_{n}+\mathtt{p}_{n}}-\sqrt{\beta_{n}+\mathtt{q}_{n}}\,\bigr\|_{L_{2}(\tau)}\to 0.

Moreover, by assumption on
\alpha_{n},\beta_{n},\mathtt{p}_{n},\mathtt{q}_{n}
, we have

\bigl\|\sqrt{\alpha_{n}+\mathtt{p}_{n}}-\sqrt{\beta_{n}+\mathtt{q}_{n}}\bigr\|_{L_{2}(\tau)}^{2}=\bigl\|\sqrt{\alpha_{n}}-\sqrt{\beta_{n}}\bigr\|_{L_{2}(\tau_{c})}^{2}+\bigl\|\sqrt{\mathtt{p}_{n}}-\sqrt{\mathtt{q}_{n}}\bigr\|_{L_{2}(\tau_{\mathcal{S}})}^{2}.

Hence, we have in particular that
\|\sqrt{\mathtt{p}_{n}}-\sqrt{\mathtt{q}_{n}}\|_{L_{2}(\tau_{\mathcal{S}})}^{2}\to 0
as
n\to\infty
; and since
\tau_{\mathcal{S}}
is the counting measure on
\mathcal{S}
, this means that

\sum_{s\in\mathcal{S}}\Bigl(\,\sqrt{\mathtt{p}_{n}(s)}-\sqrt{\mathtt{q}_{n}(s)}\,\Bigr)^{2}\to 0\qquad\hbox{as $n\to\infty$}.

(5.8)

So far, we have not used the fact that the measures
m_{\boldsymbol{u}}
and
m_{\boldsymbol{v}}
are invariant under
B_{\boldsymbol{u}}
and
B_{\boldsymbol{v}}
. Set
\mathtt{p}:=\mathtt{p}_{0}
and
\mathtt{q}:=\mathtt{q}_{0}
. Then, the discrete parts of
\mu_{{\boldsymbol{u}},0}
and
\mu_{{\boldsymbol{v}},0}
are respectively
\sum_{s\in\mathcal{S}}\mathtt{p}(s)\,\delta_{s}
and
\sum_{s\in\mathcal{S}}\mathtt{q}(s)\,\delta_{s}
. So, by the invariance properties and since
\mathcal{S}
is a multiplicative group containing
{u_{1}\cdots u_{n}}
and
{v_{1}\cdots v_{n}}
, the discrete parts of
\mu_{{\boldsymbol{u}},n}
and
\mu_{{\boldsymbol{v}},n}
(for
n\geq 1
) are respectively
\sum_{s\in\mathcal{S}}\mathtt{p}({u_{1}\cdots u_{n}}s)\,\delta_{s}
and
\sum_{s\in\mathcal{S}}\mathtt{q}({v_{1}\cdots v_{n}}s)\,\delta_{s}
; in other words, we have
\mathtt{p}_{n}(s)=\mathtt{p}({u_{1}\cdots u_{n}}s)
and
\mathtt{q}_{n}(s)=\mathtt{q}({v_{1}\cdots v_{n}}s)
for all
s\in\mathcal{S}
. Hence, if we set
f:=\sqrt{\mathtt{p}}
and
g:=\sqrt{\mathtt{q}}
, (
5.8
) can be re-written as follows:

\sum_{s\in\mathcal{S}}\bigl(f({u_{1}\cdots u_{n}}s)-g({v_{1}\cdots v_{n}}s)\bigr)^{2}\to 0\qquad\hbox{as $n\to\infty$}

or, equivalently,

\sum_{s\in\mathcal{S}}\bigl(f(\lambda_{n}s)-g(s)\bigr)^{2}\to 0.

It follows in particular,
f(\lambda_{n}s)\to g(s)
for all
s\in\mathcal{S}
. So, taking any
s
such that
g(s)>0
(such an
s
exists since the discrete part of
\mu_{{\boldsymbol{v}},0}
is non-zero), we see that one can find
\varepsilon>0
, e.g.
\varepsilon:=g(s)/2
, such that
f(\lambda_{n}s)\geq\varepsilon
for all
n
sufficiently large. It follows that the sequence
(\lambda_{n})
can take only finitely many distinct values: indeed, otherwise
f(s^{\prime})\geq\varepsilon
for infinitely many
s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}
, a contradiction since
f\in\ell_{2}(\mathcal{S})
. Now, assume that
|\lambda_{n}|
is not eventually constant. Then, since
\lambda_{n}
takes only finitely many values,
\lambda_{n}\in\mathcal{S}
and
f(\lambda_{n}s)\to g(s)
for all
s\in\mathcal{S}
, we see that one can find
\lambda,\lambda^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}
with
|\lambda|\neq|\lambda^{\prime}|
such that
f(\lambda s)=f(\lambda^{\prime}s)
for all
s\in\mathcal{S}
. Setting
\alpha:=\lambda/\lambda^{\prime}
, we then have
f(\alpha^{k}s)=f(s)
for any
s\in\mathcal{S}
and all
k\in\mathbb{N}
; and taking any
s
such that
f(s)>0
(again, such an
s
exists), we obtain a contradiction since
f\in\ell_{2}(\mathcal{S})
and the
\alpha^{k}
,
k\in\mathbb{N}
are pairwise distinct. This concludes the proof of (a).

(b) Let us fix
n\geq 1
and, towards a contradiction, assume that
|\lambda_{n}|\neq 1
, say
|\lambda_{n}|<1
.

By the invariance properties, we have
{\rm supp}(\mu_{{\boldsymbol{u}},n})=\frac{1}{{u_{1}\cdots u_{n}}}\,S
and
{\rm supp}(\mu_{{\boldsymbol{v}},n})=\frac{1}{{v_{1}\cdots v_{n}}}\,S
, so that
\lambda_{n}S=S
. Hence
\lambda_{n}^{r}S=S
for all
r\in\mathbb{Z}
. If
S
is compact, it follows that
S=\{0\}
by letting
r\to+\infty
; hence
\mu_{{\boldsymbol{u}},0}=\delta_{0}=\mu_{{\boldsymbol{v}},0}
, which is the required contradiction. If
0\not\in S
, then we get
S=\emptyset
by letting
r\to-\infty
, which is another contradiction.
∎

Corollary 5.23
.

Let
{\boldsymbol{u}}
and
{\boldsymbol{v}}
be positive weight sequences such that
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{({u_{1}\cdots u_{n}})^{p}}<\infty
and
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{({v_{1}\cdots v_{n}})^{p}}<\infty
. The weighted shifts
B_{\boldsymbol{u}}
and
B_{\boldsymbol{v}}
acting on
\ell_{p}
are not orthogonal with respect to product measures whose marginals have non-zero discrete parts and do not charge
\{0\}
if and only if
\frac{{u_{1}\cdots u_{n}}}{{v_{1}\cdots v_{n}}}
is eventually constant; and in that case they admit equivalent invariant product measures with purely discrete marginals not charging
\{0\}
.

Proof.

The “only if” part follows from Proposition
5.22
(a). Conversely, assume that
\lambda_{n}:=\frac{{u_{1}\cdots u_{n}}}{{v_{1}\cdots v_{n}}}
is eventually constant, say
\lambda_{n}=\lambda
for
n>n_{0}
. Let
\mathcal{S}
be the multiplicative subgroup of
(0,\infty)
generated by the set

\{u_{n};\;n\geq 1\}\cup\{v_{n};\;\geq 1\}
. Let
\mathtt{p}:\mathcal{S}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
be a strictly positive probability density function such that
\sum_{s\in\mathcal{S}}\mathtt{p}(s)\,s^{p}<\infty
, and let
\mathtt{q}:\mathcal{S}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
be the probability density function defined by
\mathtt{q}(s):=\mathtt{p}(\lambda s)
. Finally, let

\mu_{{\boldsymbol{u}},0}:=\sum_{s\in\mathcal{S}}\mathtt{p}(s)\delta_{s}
and
\mu_{{\boldsymbol{v}},0}:=\sum_{s\in\mathcal{S}}\mathtt{q}(s)\delta_{s}
, and denote by
m_{\boldsymbol{u}}=\oplus_{n\geq 0}\mu_{{\boldsymbol{u}},n}
and
m_{\boldsymbol{v}}=\oplus_{n\geq 0}\mu_{{\boldsymbol{v}},n}
the associated
B_{\boldsymbol{u}}\,
- invariant and
B_{\boldsymbol{v}}\,
- invariant measures on
\mathbb{K}^{\mathbb{Z}_{+}}
. By Corollary
5.4
and the assumption on
{\boldsymbol{u}}
,
{\boldsymbol{v}}
and
\mathtt{p}
, the measures
m_{\boldsymbol{u}}
and
m_{\boldsymbol{v}}
are supported on
\ell_{p}
, and they have purely discrete marginals not charging
\{0\}
. Finally, by the proof of Proposition
5.22
(a), we have
H(\mu_{{\boldsymbol{u}},n},\mu_{{\boldsymbol{v}},n})=\sum_{s\in\mathcal{S}}\sqrt{\mathtt{p}(\lambda_{n}s)\mathtt{q}(s)}
for all
n\geq 0
, so that

H(\mu_{{\boldsymbol{u}},n},\mu_{{\boldsymbol{v}},n})=\sum_{s\in\mathcal{S}}\mathtt{q}(s)=1
for all
n>n_{0}
. Since
\mu_{{\boldsymbol{u}},n}\sim\mu_{{\boldsymbol{v}},n}
for all
n\geq 0
, it follows that
m_{\boldsymbol{u}}\sim m_{\boldsymbol{v}}
. This concludes the proof.
∎

Remark 5.24
.

In Proposition
5.22
(b), one cannot replace “equivalent” by “non-orthogonal”. For example, let
{\boldsymbol{u}}
and
{\boldsymbol{v}}
be defined as follows:
u_{n}=2
for all
n\geq 1
,
v_{1}=1
,
v_{2}=4
and
v_{n}=2
for all
n\geq 3
. Then
{\boldsymbol{u}}\neq{\boldsymbol{v}}
(!), and yet
B_{\boldsymbol{u}}
and
B_{\boldsymbol{v}}
admit non-orthogonal product measure
m_{\boldsymbol{u}}=\otimes_{n\geq 0}\mu_{{\boldsymbol{u}},n}
and
m_{\boldsymbol{v}}=\otimes_{n\geq 0}\mu_{{\boldsymbol{v}},n}
for which
\mu_{{\boldsymbol{u}},0}=\mu_{{\boldsymbol{v}},0}
has a compact support not containing
0
; for example,
\mu_{{\boldsymbol{u}},0}=\mu_{{\boldsymbol{v}},0}
could be the uniform distribution on the interval
[1,3]
.

6.
Additional facts

6.1.
Invariant measures with symmetry properties

It is clear that if an operator
T
acting on a Polish topological vector space
X
admits a non-trivial invariant measure
m
, then the measure
\widetilde{m}
defined by
\widetilde{m}(A):=\frac{1}{2}(m(A)+m(-A))
is a non-trivial invariant measure for both
T
and
-T
, and that
\widetilde{m}
is additionally
symmetric
,
i.e.

\widetilde{m}(-A)=\widetilde{m}(A)
for every Borel set
A\subseteq X
. The next proposition goes along the same lines.

Proposition 6.1
.

Let
X
be a Polish space, and let
T
be a continuous self-map of
X
admitting an invariant measure
m
. Let also
\mathbf{G}
be a compact abelian group acting continuously on
X
, and assume that
Tg=\beta(g)T
for all
g\in\mathbf{G}
, where
\beta:\mathbf{G}\to\mathbf{G}
is a continuous map invariant under the Haar measure of
\mathbf{G}
. Let
\widetilde{m}
be the measure on
X
defined by

\widetilde{m}(A):=\int_{\mathbf{G}}m(g^{-1}A)\,dg,

where
dg
denotes integration with respect to the Haar measure of
\mathbf{G}
. Then
\widetilde{m}
is
\mathbf{G}\,
- invariant and both
gT\,
- invariant and
Tg\,
- invariant for every
g\in\mathbf{G}
.

Proof.

It is clear that
\widetilde{m}
is
\mathbf{G}\,
- invariant. Moreover, if
h\in\mathbf{G}
then, for any bounded Borel function
f:X\to\mathbb{R}^{+}
, we have

\displaystyle\int_{X}f\circ(\beta(h)T)\,d\widetilde{m}

\displaystyle=\int_{X}\left(\int_{\mathbf{G}}f(\beta(h)T(gx))\,dg\right)dm(x)

\displaystyle=\int_{X}\left(\int_{\mathbf{G}}f(\beta(h)\beta(g)T(x))\,dg\right)dm(x)

\displaystyle=\int_{X}\left(\int_{\mathbf{G}}f(\beta(h)gT(x))\,dg\right)dm(x)

\displaystyle=\int_{X}\left(\int_{\mathbf{G}}f(gT(x))\,dg\right)dm(x)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbf{G}}\left(\int_{X}f(gu)\,dm(u)\right)dg

\displaystyle=\int_{X}f\,d\widetilde{m}.

Since
\beta
is necessarily onto (because the Haar measure of
\mathbf{G}
has full support), it follows that
\widetilde{m}
if
gT\,
- invariant for every
g\in\mathbf{G}
; and hence also
Tg\,
- invariant since
Tg=\beta(g)T
.
∎

Corollary 6.2
.

Let
X
be a Polish space, and let
T_{1},T_{2}
be two continuous self-maps of
X
. Let also
\mathbf{G}
be a compact abelian group acting continuously on
X
, and assume that
T_{i}g=gT_{i}
for all
g\in\mathbf{G}
and
i=1,2
. Finally, let
x_{0}\in X
be a fixed point for the action of
\mathbf{G}
. If
T_{1}
and
T_{2}
admit equivalent
(
resp. non-orthogonal
)
invariant measures
m_{1},m_{2}
not charging
\{x_{0}\}
, then they also admit equivalent
(
resp. non-orthogonal
)
invariant measures not charging
\{x_{0}\}
which are additionally
\mathbf{G}\,
- invariant.

Proof.

Let
\widetilde{m_{1}}
and
\widetilde{m_{2}}
be defined as in Proposition
6.1
. It is clear that
\widetilde{m_{1}}
,
\widetilde{m_{2}}
do not charge
\{x_{0}\}
. So we just have to check that if
m_{1}\sim m_{2}
then
\widetilde{m_{1}}\sim\widetilde{m_{2}}
, and that if
\widetilde{m_{1}}\perp\widetilde{m_{2}}
then
m_{1}\perp m_{2}
.

Assume that
m_{1}\sim m_{2}
. Let
A\subseteq X
be a Borel set such that
\widetilde{m_{1}}(A)=0
. Then
m_{1}(gA)=0
for almost every
g\in\mathbf{G}
; hence
m_{2}(gA)=0
for almost every
g\in\mathbf{G}
since
m_{2}\ll m_{1}
, and hence
\widetilde{m_{2}}(A)=0
. This shows that
\widetilde{m_{2}}\ll\widetilde{m_{1}}
; and similarly
\widetilde{m_{1}}\ll\widetilde{m_{2}}
.

Assume that
\widetilde{m_{2}}\perp\widetilde{m_{1}}
. Let
A\subseteq X
be a Borel set such that
\widetilde{m_{1}}(A)=0
and
\widetilde{m_{2}}(X\setminus A)=0
. Then
m_{1}(gA)=0
for almost every
g\in\mathbf{G}
and
m_{2}(X\setminus gA)=m_{2}(g(X\setminus A))=0
for almost every
g\in\mathbf{G}
. So one can find at least one
g
such that
m_{1}(gA)=0=m_{2}(X\setminus gA)
, which shows that
m_{1}\perp m_{2}
.
∎

Corollary 6.3
.

Let
X
be a Polish topological vector space, and let
T\in\mathcal{L}(X)
. Assume that
\kappa T
admits a non-trivial invariant measure for some

- invariant for all

such that
|a|=|\kappa|
.

Proof.

Apply Proposition
6.1
with the group
\mathbf{G}:=\{\omega\in\mathbb{K};\;|\omega|=1\}
acting by multiplication on

.
∎

Corollary 6.4
.

Let
B_{\boldsymbol{u}}
be a backward shift acting on
\ell_{p}
. If
B_{\boldsymbol{u}}
admits a non-trivial invariant measure, then there exists a measure

on
\ell_{p}
with full support which is invariant for all backward shifts

such that
|v_{n}|=|u_{n}|
for all

.

Since

, we know that

with full support (which can even be taken to be Gaussian and ergodic for

for every

is (the restriction to

of) the canonical backward shift. Hence, considering

, we may apply Proposition
6.1
. This gives a measure
\widetilde{m}
on

which is

- invariant for every weight sequence

. Moreover, since

and

∎

6.2.
Product measures charging small subspaces

The following proposition says in essence that whether or not a product measure
\mathbb{K}^{\mathbb{Z}_{+}}
invariant under a weighted shift
B_{\boldsymbol{w}}
is supported on some “small” subspace of
\mathbb{K}^{\mathbb{Z}_{+}}
depends on the rate of growth of the products
{w_{1}\cdots w_{n}}
.

Proposition 6.6
.

Let
\mu_{0}
be a Borel probability measure on
\mathbb{K}
, let
{\boldsymbol{w}}
be a sequence of non-zero scalars, and let
\mu_{\boldsymbol{w}}=\otimes_{n\geq 0}\mu_{n}
be the
B_{\boldsymbol{w}}
- invariant measure on
\mathbb{K}^{\mathbb{Z}_{+}}
defined by
\mu_{0}
, i.e.
\mu_{n}(A)=\mu_{0}({w_{1}\cdots w_{n}}A)
for each
n\geq 1

and every Borel set
A\subseteq\mathbb{K}
.

(a)

Let
p\in[1,\infty)
. If
\int_{\mathbb{K}}|t|^{p}d\mu_{0}(t)<\infty
and
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{|{w_{1}\cdots w_{n}}|^{p}}<\infty
, then
\mu_{\boldsymbol{w}}(\ell_{p})=1
.

(b)

If there exists a summable sequence of positive real numbers
(\varepsilon_{n})
such that

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\mu_{0}\bigl(|t|>|{w_{1}\cdots w_{n}}|\,\varepsilon_{n}\bigr)<\infty,

then
\mu_{\boldsymbol{w}}(\ell_{1})=1
.

(c)

If
|{w_{1}\cdots w_{n}}|\not\to\infty
and
\mu_{0}\neq\delta_{0}
, then
\mu_{\boldsymbol{w}}(c_{0})=0
. If
|{w_{1}\cdots w_{n}}|\not\to\infty
and
\mu_{0}
does not have compact support, then
\mu_{\boldsymbol{w}}(\ell_{\infty})=0
.

Proof.

(a) This is Corollary
5.4
.

(b) Since
\ell_{1}
is a tail subset of
\mathbb{K}^{\mathbb{Z}_{+}}
, we have
\mu_{\boldsymbol{w}}(\ell_{1})=0
or
1
, by Kolmogorov’s
0\,
-
\,1
law. Let

A:=\bigl\{x\in\mathbb{K}^{\mathbb{Z}_{+}};\;|x_{n}|\leq\varepsilon_{n}\quad\hbox{for all $n\geq 1$}\bigr\}.

Obviously
A\subseteq\ell_{1}
, and we have

\displaystyle\mu_{\boldsymbol{w}}(A)

\displaystyle=\prod_{n=1}^{\infty}\mu_{n}\bigl(|t|\leq\varepsilon_{n}\bigr)

\displaystyle=\prod_{n=1}^{\infty}\mu_{0}\bigl(|t|\leq|{w_{1}\cdots w_{n}}|\,\varepsilon_{n}\bigr).

Since
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\bigl(1-\mu_{0}(|t|\leq|{w_{1}\cdots w_{n}}|\,\varepsilon_{n})\bigr)<\infty
by assumption, it follows that
\mu_{\boldsymbol{w}}(A)>0
, and hence
\mu_{\boldsymbol{w}}(\ell_{1})=1
.

(c) Assume that
\mu_{\boldsymbol{w}}(c_{0})>0
. Then, there exists a compact set
K\subseteq c_{0}
such that
\mu_{\boldsymbol{w}}(K)>0
. By the well-known description of the compact subsets of
c_{0}
, one can find a sequence of positive real numbers
(\varepsilon_{n})
tending to
0
such that

K\subseteq\bigl\{x\in\mathbb{K}^{\mathbb{Z}_{+}};\;|x_{n}|\leq\varepsilon_{n}\quad\hbox{for all $n\geq 0$}\bigr\}.

As in the proof of (b), it follows that

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\mu_{0}\bigl(|t|>|{w_{1}\cdots w_{n}}|\,\varepsilon_{n}\bigr)<\infty.

In particular,
\mu_{0}(|t|>|{w_{1}\cdots w_{n}}|\,\varepsilon_{n})\to 0
as
n\to\infty
. If
\mu_{0}\neq\delta_{0}
, this implies that no subsequence of
(|{w_{1}\cdots w_{n}}|\varepsilon_{n})
can tend to
0
; and hence
|{w_{1}\cdots w_{n}}|\to\infty
.

The second part of (c) is proved in a similar way.
∎

Corollary 6.7
.

For any probability measure
\mu_{0}
on
\mathbb{K}
, one can find a sequence of positive real numbers
{\boldsymbol{w}}
such that
\mu_{\boldsymbol{w}}(\ell_{1})=1
.

Proof.

This is clear by (b): choose a sequence of positive numbers
(X_{n})
such that
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\mu_{0}(|t|>X_{n})<\infty
, and then take
{\boldsymbol{w}}
such that
2^{-n}{w_{1}\cdots w_{n}}\geq X_{n}
for all
n\geq 1
.
∎

Corollary 6.8
.

If the measure
\mu_{0}
is such that
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\mu_{0}(|t|>C^{n})<\infty
for some constant
C
, then
\mu_{\boldsymbol{w}}(\ell_{1})=1
for any weight sequence
{\boldsymbol{w}}
such that
\varliminf|{w_{1}\cdots w_{n}}|^{1/n}>C
. In particular, if
\mu_{0}(|t|>X)=O\bigl(\log(X)^{-\alpha}\bigr)
as
X\to\infty
for some constant
\alpha>1
, then
\mu_{\boldsymbol{w}}(\ell_{1})=1
for any weight sequence
{\boldsymbol{w}}
such that
\varliminf|w_{n}|^{1/n}>1
.

Proof.

Take
\varepsilon_{n}:=(C/\rho)^{n}
in (b), where
\rho:=\varliminf|w_{n}|^{1/n}
.
∎

6.3.
When frequently hypercyclic vectors are the same

As mentioned in Remark
5.10
, the following result is due to S. Charpentier and the third author. We thank S. Charpentier for allowing us to include it here.

Proposition 6.9
.

Let
{\boldsymbol{u}}
and
{\boldsymbol{v}}
be two weight sequences such that
B_{\boldsymbol{u}}
and
B_{\boldsymbol{v}}
are frequently hypercyclic on
\ell_{p}
. If
\frac{{u_{1}\cdots u_{n}}}{{v_{1}\cdots v_{n}}}
has a non-zero limit as
n\to\infty
, then
B_{\boldsymbol{u}}
and
B_{\boldsymbol{v}}
have the same frequently hypercyclic vectors.

Proof.

In what follows, we set
a:=\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}\frac{{u_{1}\cdots u_{n}}}{{v_{1}\cdots v_{n}}}\in\mathbb{K}\setminus\{0\}.

Let
x
be a frequently vector for
B_{{\boldsymbol{u}}}
; we want to show that
x
is also a frequenty hypercyclic vector for
B_{\boldsymbol{v}}
. So, we fix
y=\sum_{k=0}^{d}y_{k}e_{k}\in c_{00}
and
\varepsilon>0
, and our task is to show that the set
\mathcal{N}_{B_{\boldsymbol{v}}}\bigl(x,B(y,\varepsilon)\bigr)
has positive lower density.

Since
x
is a frequently hypercyclic vector for
B_{\boldsymbol{u}}
, it is enough to find a vector
z\in\ell_{p}
and
\alpha>0
such that any sufficiently large
n\in\mathcal{N}_{B_{\boldsymbol{u}}}\bigl(x,B(z,\alpha)\bigr)
belongs to
\mathcal{N}_{B_{\boldsymbol{v}}}\bigl(x,B(y,\varepsilon)\bigr)
.
We consider

z:=\sum_{k=0}^{d}a\,\frac{v_{1}\cdots v_{k}}{u_{1}\cdots u_{k}}\,y_{k}\,e_{k}\in c_{00}

and

\alpha:=\varepsilon/2K\qquad{\rm where}\quad K:=\sup_{n,k\geq 0}\left|\frac{v_{k+1}\cdots v_{k+n}}{u_{k+1}\cdots u_{k+n}}\right|.

Note that
K<\infty
since for every
n,k\geq 0
, we have

\left|\frac{v_{k+1}\cdots v_{k+n}}{u_{k+1}\cdots u_{k+n}}\right|=\left|\frac{u_{1}\cdots u_{k}}{v_{1}\cdots v_{k}}\right|\left|\frac{v_{1}\cdots v_{k+n}}{u_{1}\cdots u_{k+n}}\right|\leq\left(\sup_{m\geq 1}\left|\frac{u_{1}\cdots u_{m}}{v_{1}\cdots v_{m}}\right|\right)\left(\sup_{m\geq 1}\left|\frac{v_{1}\cdots v_{m}}{u_{1}\cdots u_{m}}\right|\right).

Let us show that if
n\in\mathcal{N}_{B_{{\boldsymbol{u}}}}\bigl(x,B(z,\frac{\varepsilon}{2K})\bigr)
is large enough, then
n\in\mathcal{N}_{B_{\boldsymbol{v}}}\bigl(x,B(y,\varepsilon)\bigr)
. We have

\displaystyle\|B_{{\boldsymbol{v}}}^{n}x-y\|^{p}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{d}|(v_{k+1}\cdots v_{k+n})x_{k+n}-y_{k}|^{p}+\sum_{k=d+1}^{\infty}|(v_{k+1}\cdots v_{k+n})x_{k+n}|^{p}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k=0}^{d}\left|\frac{v_{k+1}\cdots v_{k+n}}{u_{k+1}\cdots u_{k+n}}\right|^{p}\left|(u_{k+1}\cdots u_{k+n})x_{k+n}-\frac{u_{k+1}\cdots u_{k+n}}{v_{k+1}\cdots v_{k+n}}\,y_{k}\right|^{p}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+\sum_{k=d+1}^{\infty}\left|\frac{v_{k+1}\cdots v_{k+n}}{u_{k+1}\cdots u_{k+n}}\right|^{p}|(u_{k+1}\cdots u_{k+n})x_{k+n}|^{p}

\displaystyle\leq K^{p}\|B_{{\boldsymbol{u}}}^{n}x-\tilde{z}^{(n)}\|^{p}

where

\tilde{z}^{(n)}:=\sum_{k=0}^{d}\frac{u_{k+1}\cdots u_{k+n}}{v_{k+1}\cdots v_{k+n}}y_{k}\,e_{k}.

Therefore, if
n\in\mathcal{N}_{B_{{\boldsymbol{u}}}}\bigl(x,B(z,\frac{\varepsilon}{2K})\bigr)
, we get

\|B_{{\boldsymbol{v}}}^{n}x-y\|\leq K\|B_{{\boldsymbol{u}}}^{n}x-z\|+K\|z-\tilde{z}^{(n)}\|\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{2}+K\|z-\tilde{z}^{(n)}\|;

and the desired result follows because

\displaystyle\|z-\tilde{z}^{(n)}\|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k\leq d}\left|z_{k}-\frac{u_{k+1}\cdots u_{k+n}}{v_{k+1}\cdots v_{k+n}}\,y_{k}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\|y\|_{1}\max_{k\leq d}\left|a\,\frac{v_{1}\cdots v_{k}}{u_{1}\cdots u_{k}}-\frac{u_{k+1}\cdots u_{k+n}}{v_{k+1}\cdots v_{k+n}}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\|y\|_{1}\left(\max_{k\leq d}\left|\frac{v_{1}\cdots v_{k}}{u_{1}\cdots u_{k}}\right|\right)\left(\sup_{m\geq n}\left|a-\frac{u_{1}\cdots u_{m}}{v_{1}\cdots v_{m}}\right|\right)\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}0.

∎

6.4.
Using Shepp’s Theorem

In this section, we show how results like Shepp’s theorem from
[
21
]
mentioned before Theorem
5.13
can be used in the context of weighted shifts. Specifically, we will make use of the following theorem. For any measure
m
on
X=\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{Z}_{+}}
and

\boldsymbol{\alpha}\in X
, let us denote by
m_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}
the translate of
m
by
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
, which is the measure on
X
defined by

m_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}(A):=m(A+\boldsymbol{\alpha}).

Theorem 6.10
.

Let
\widetilde{\mu}
and
\widetilde{\mu}^{\prime}
be two measures on
\mathbb{R}
, and let
\widetilde{m}
and
\widetilde{m}^{\prime}
be the product measures on
\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{Z}_{+}}
with marginals

\widetilde{\mu}_{n}:=\widetilde{\mu}
and
\widetilde{\mu}_{n}^{\prime}:=\widetilde{\mu}^{\prime}
. Let also
\boldsymbol{\alpha}=(\alpha_{n})_{n\geq 0}\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{Z}_{+}}
, and assume that the measures
\widetilde{m}
and

\widetilde{m}^{\prime}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}
are not orthogonal.

(1)

If
\widetilde{\mu}=\widetilde{\mu}^{\prime}
, then
\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\alpha_{n}^{2}<\infty
.

(2)

If
\widetilde{\mu}
and
\widetilde{\mu}^{\prime}
have a moment of order
2
, then
\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(\alpha_{n}-\alpha)^{2}<\infty
for some
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
.

Proof.

(1) is (the first third of)
[
21
]
*Theorem 1.

To prove (2), we use a method devised by Dudley
[
13
]
in order to generalize Shepp’s theorem. Denote by
e_{n}^{*}
,
n\geq 0
the coordinate linear functionals on
\Omega:=\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{Z}_{+}}
. The assumption on
\widetilde{\mu}
and
\widetilde{\mu}^{\prime}
implies that
e_{n}^{*}
belongs to
L_{2}(\Omega,\widetilde{m})\cap L_{2}(\Omega,\widetilde{m}^{\prime})
with
L_{2}\,
- norms
respectively equal to
\int_{\mathbb{R}}x^{2}\,d\widetilde{\mu}(x)
and
\int_{\mathbb{R}}x^{2}\,d\widetilde{\mu}^{\prime}(x)
, hence independent of
n
. Likewise,
\int_{\Omega}e_{n}^{*}\,d\widetilde{m}=\int_{\mathbb{R}}x\,d\widetilde{\mu}(x)
and
\int_{\Omega}e_{n}^{*}\,d\widetilde{m}^{\prime}=\int_{\mathbb{R}}x\,d\widetilde{\mu}^{\prime}(x)
do not depend on
n
.

Assume first that
\int_{\Omega}e_{n}^{*}\,d\widetilde{m}=0
and
\int_{\Omega}e_{n}^{*}\,d\widetilde{m}^{\prime}=0
for all
n\geq 0
. We claim that in this case, we have
\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\alpha_{n}^{2}<\infty
. Towards a contradiction, assume that
\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\alpha_{n}^{2}=\infty
. Then, one can find a sequence of real numbers
(\beta_{n})
such that
\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\beta_{n}^{2}<\infty
,
\beta_{n}\alpha_{n}\geq 0
for all
n
and
\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\beta_{n}\alpha_{n}=\infty
. Now, consider the measure
\tau:=\frac{1}{2}(\widetilde{m}+\widetilde{m}^{\prime})
. By our assumption, the sequence
(e_{n}^{*})_{n\geq 0}
is orthogonal and bounded in
L_{2}(\Omega,\tau)
. Since
\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\beta_{n}^{2}<\infty
, it follows that the series
\sum\beta_{n}e_{n}^{*}
is convergent in
L_{2}(\Omega,\tau)
. Hence, there is an increasing sequence of integers
(N_{k})_{k\geq 0}
such that if we define
f_{k}:=\sum_{n=0}^{N_{k}}e_{n}^{*}
, then the sequence of linear functionals
(f_{k})
converges
\tau\,
- a.e. on
\Omega
. So, the linear subspace

E:=\bigl\{x\in\Omega;\;\hbox{the sequence $(f_{k}(x))$ is convergent}\bigr\}\subseteq\Omega

is such that
\tau(E)=1
and hence
\widetilde{m}(E)=1
. On the other hand, we have
f_{k}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})=\sum_{n=0}^{N_{k}}\beta_{n}\alpha_{n}
, so the sequence
(f_{k}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}))
is
not
convergent,
i.e.

\boldsymbol{\alpha}\not\in E
; and since
E
is a linear subspace of
\Omega
, it follows that

E\cap(E+\boldsymbol{\alpha})=\emptyset
. So we have
\tau(E+\boldsymbol{\alpha})=0
, and hence
\widetilde{m}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}^{\prime}(E)=\widetilde{m}^{\prime}(E+\boldsymbol{\alpha})=0
. Since
\widetilde{m}
and
\widetilde{m}^{\prime}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}
are not orthogonal, this is a contradiction.

Now, let us consider the general case. Let
c:=\int_{\mathbb{R}}x\,d\widetilde{\mu}(x)
and
c^{\prime}:=\int_{\mathbb{R}}x\,d\widetilde{\mu}^{\prime}(x)
, so that
\int_{\Omega}e_{n}^{*}\,d\widetilde{m}=c
and
\int_{\Omega}e_{n}^{*}\,d\widetilde{m}^{\prime}=c^{\prime}
for all
n\geq 0
. Then
\int_{\Omega}e_{n}^{*}\,d\widetilde{m}_{\mathbf{c}}=0
and
\int_{\Omega}e_{n}^{*}\,d\widetilde{m}^{\prime}_{\mathbf{c}^{\prime}}=0
for all
n\geq 0
, where
\mathbf{c}:=(c,c,\dots)
and
\mathbf{c}^{\prime}:=(c^{\prime},c^{\prime},\dots)
. Since the measures
\widetilde{m}_{\mathbf{c}}
and
\bigr(\widetilde{m}^{\prime}_{\mathbf{c}^{\prime}}\bigl)_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}+\mathbf{c}-\mathbf{c}^{\prime}}=\bigl(\widetilde{m}^{\prime}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\bigr)_{\mathbf{c}}
are not orthogonal, it follows that
\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(\alpha_{n}-(c^{\prime}-c))^{2}<\infty
.
∎

From Theorem
6.10
, it is essentially a formal matter to deduce the following theorem, which is not far from saying that if two weighted shifts are not orthogonal with respect to product measures whose marginals do not charge
\{0\}
, then they admit equivalent invariant Gaussian product measures.

Theorem 6.11
.

Let
{\boldsymbol{u}}
and
{\boldsymbol{v}}
be two sequences of non-zero scalars, and let
m_{\boldsymbol{u}}=\otimes_{n\geq 0}\mu_{n,{\boldsymbol{u}}}
and
m_{\boldsymbol{v}}=\otimes_{n\geq 0}\mu_{n,{\boldsymbol{v}}}
be two product measures on
\mathbb{K}^{\mathbb{Z}_{+}}
, respectively
B_{\boldsymbol{u}}\,
- invariant and
B_{\boldsymbol{v}}\,
- invariant, with
\mu_{0,{\boldsymbol{u}}}(\{0\})=0=\mu_{0,{\boldsymbol{v}}}(\{0\})
. Assume that the measures

m_{\boldsymbol{u}}
and
m_{\boldsymbol{v}}
are not orthogonal. Finally, let
\lambda_{n}:=\frac{{u_{1}\cdots u_{n}}}{{v_{1}\cdots v_{n}}}
for all
n\geq 1
.

(1)

If there exists
a\in\mathbb{K}\setminus\{0\}
such that
\mu_{0,{\boldsymbol{v}}}(A)=\mu_{0,{\boldsymbol{u}}}(aA)
for every Borel set
A\subseteq\mathbb{K}
, then
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\bigl(1-|a^{-1}\lambda_{n}|\bigr)^{2}<\infty
.

(2)

If
\int_{\mathbb{K}\setminus\{0\}}\bigl(\log|t|\bigr)^{2}d\mu_{0,{\boldsymbol{u}}}(t)<\infty
and
\int_{\mathbb{K}\setminus\{0\}}\bigl(\log|t|\bigr)^{2}d\mu_{0,{\boldsymbol{v}}}(t)<\infty
, then there exists
a\in\mathbb{K}\setminus\{0\}
such that
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\bigl(1-|a^{-1}\lambda_{n}|\bigr)^{2}<\infty
.

Proof.

(1) Since
\mu_{0,{\boldsymbol{u}}}(\{0\})=0=\mu_{0,{\boldsymbol{v}}}(\{0\})
, we view
\mu_{0,{\boldsymbol{u}}}
and
\mu_{0,{\boldsymbol{v}}}
as measures on
G:=\mathbb{K}\setminus\{0\}
, and hence we view
m_{\boldsymbol{u}}
and
m_{\boldsymbol{v}}
as measures on
G^{\mathbb{Z}_{+}}
. Note that
G^{\mathbb{Z}_{+}}
is an abelian group under entry-wise multiplication.

Consider the measures
m=\otimes_{n\geq 0}\mu_{n}
and
m^{\prime}=\otimes_{n\geq 0}\mu^{\prime}_{n}
where
\mu_{n}:=\mu_{0,{\boldsymbol{u}}}
and
\mu^{\prime}_{n}:=\mu_{0,{\boldsymbol{v}}}
for all
n\geq 0
. If we set
\mathbf{U}:=(1,u_{1},u_{1}u_{2},\dots)
,
\mathbf{V}:=(1,v_{1},v_{1}v_{2},\dots)
and
\mathbf{a}:=(a,a,a,\dots)
, then we have for every Borel set
B\subseteq G^{\mathbb{Z}_{+}}
:

m_{\boldsymbol{u}}(B)=m(\mathbf{U}B)\qquad{\rm and}\qquad m_{\boldsymbol{v}}(B)=m^{\prime}(\mathbf{V}B)=m(\mathbf{a}\mathbf{V}B).

Now, consider the map
L:G^{\mathbb{Z}_{+}}\to\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{Z}_{+}}
defined by
L(t_{0},t_{1},\dots):=\bigl(\log|t_{0}|,\log|t_{1}|,\dots\bigr)
. Denote by
\widetilde{m}_{\boldsymbol{u}}
,
\widetilde{m}_{\boldsymbol{v}}
,
\widetilde{m}
and
\widetilde{m}^{\prime}
the images of
m_{\boldsymbol{u}}
,
m_{\boldsymbol{v}}
,
m

and
m^{\prime}
under this map
L
. Then
\widetilde{m}=\otimes_{n\geq 0}\widetilde{\mu}_{n}
and
\widetilde{m}^{\prime}=\otimes_{n\geq 0}\widetilde{\mu}^{\prime}_{n}
, where
\widetilde{\mu}_{n}
and

\widetilde{\mu}^{\prime}_{n}
are the images of
\mu_{0,{\boldsymbol{u}}}
and
\mu_{0,{\boldsymbol{v}}}
under the map
t\mapsto\log|t|
, for every
n\geq 0
. Moreover, since
L
is a group homomorphism, we have for every Borel set
B\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{Z}_{+}}
:

\widetilde{m}_{\boldsymbol{u}}(B)=\widetilde{m}\bigl(B+L(\mathbf{U})\bigl)\qquad{\rm and}\qquad\widetilde{m}_{\boldsymbol{u}}(B)=\widetilde{m}\bigl(B+L(\mathbf{aV})\bigl).

Now, the measures
\widetilde{m}_{\boldsymbol{u}}=m_{\boldsymbol{u}}\circ L^{-1}
and
\widetilde{m}_{\boldsymbol{v}}=m_{\boldsymbol{v}}\circ L^{-1}
are not orthogonal since
m_{\boldsymbol{u}}
and
m_{\boldsymbol{v}}
are not orthogonal. So their translates by
-L(\mathbf{aV})
are not orthogonal either. In other words, if we set

\boldsymbol{\alpha}:=L(\mathbf{U}\mathbf{V}^{-1}\mathbf{a}^{-1})=(0,\log|a^{-1}\lambda_{1}|,\log|a^{-1}\lambda_{2}|,\dots),

then the measure
\widetilde{m}
and
\widetilde{m}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}
are not orthogonal. By Theorem
6.10
(1), it follows that
\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\alpha_{n}^{2}<\infty
,
i.e.

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}(\log|a^{-1}\lambda_{n}|)^{2}<\infty;

which concludes the proof.

(2) The proof is similar, using Theorem
6.10
(2).
∎

Corollary 6.12
.

Let
B_{\boldsymbol{u}}
and
B_{\boldsymbol{v}}
be two weighted shifts acting on
\ell_{p}
. The following are equivalent.

(a)

B_{\boldsymbol{u}}
and
B_{\boldsymbol{v}}
are not orthogonal with respect to product measures whose marginals are absolutely continuous with respect to Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{K}
.

(b)

B_{\boldsymbol{u}}
and
B_{\boldsymbol{v}}
are not orthogonal with respect to product measures whose marginals do not charge
\{0\}
and are such that
\log|t|\in L_{2}
.

(c)

There exists
\kappa>0
such that
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\left(1-\kappa\left|\frac{{u_{1}\cdots u_{n}}}{{v_{1}\cdots v_{n}}}\right|\right)^{2}<\infty
.

(d)

B_{\boldsymbol{u}}
and
B_{\boldsymbol{v}}
admit equivalent invariant Gaussian product measures.

Proof.

By Theorem
6.11
and keeping the same notation, we just have to check that if
\mu_{0,{\boldsymbol{u}}}
and
\mu_{0,{\boldsymbol{v}}}
are absolutely continuous with respect to Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{K}
, then there exists
a\in\mathbb{K}\setminus\{0\}
such that
\mu_{0,{\boldsymbol{v}}}(A)=\mu_{0,{\boldsymbol{u}}}(aA)
for every Borel set
A\subseteq\mathbb{K}
. When
\mathbb{K}=\mathbb{R}
this is done in the proof of (1i) in Theorem
5.13
. We repeat the argument here, in a way that makes it work when
\mathbb{K}=\mathbb{C}
as well. Write
\mu_{0,{\boldsymbol{u}}}=\mathtt{p}(t)dt
and
\mu_{0,{\boldsymbol{v}}}=\mathtt{q}(t)dt
. Then
\Psi(\lambda_{n})\to 1
as
n\to\infty
, where
\Psi:\mathbb{K}\setminus\{0\}\to[0,1]
is the function defined by
\Psi(\lambda):=|\lambda|^{d/2}\,\int_{\mathbb{K}}f(\lambda t)g(t)\,dt
and
d=1
or
2
depending on whether
\mathbb{K}=\mathbb{R}
or
\mathbb{C}
. Moreover, since
B_{\boldsymbol{u}}
and
B_{\boldsymbol{v}}
must be similar by Proposition
3.8
, the sequence
(\lambda_{n})
has a cluster point
a\in\mathbb{K}\setminus\{0\}
. Then
\Psi(a)=1
by continuity of
\Psi
, and it follows that
\mathtt{q}(t)=|a|^{d}\,\mathtt{p}(at)
almost everywhere.∎

6.5.
Kakutani’s Theorem

In this section, our aim is to prove the following variant of Kakutani’s Theorem from
[
17
]
.

Theorem 6.13
.

Let
\nu=\otimes_{n\geq 0}\nu_{n}
and
\nu^{\prime}=\otimes_{n\geq 0}\nu^{\prime}_{n}
be two product probability measures on
\Omega=\prod_{n\geq 0}\Omega_{n}
. The measures
\nu
and
\nu^{\prime}
are orthogonal if and only if
\prod_{n=0}^{\infty}H(\nu_{n},\nu^{\prime}_{n})=0
. Moreover, if
\prod_{n=0}^{\infty}H(\nu_{n},\nu^{\prime}_{n})>0
and
\nu_{n}\ll\nu^{\prime}_{n}
for all
n
, then
\nu\ll\nu^{\prime}
.

Note that this variant was used in the proofs of Theorem
5.13
and Proposition
5.22
. We give a rather detailed proof since we were not able to locate one in the literature. However, this is essentially a “copy and paste” of Kakutani’s original proof of his theorem.

Proof of Theorem
6.13
.

For each
n\geq 0
, let us choose a Radon-Nikodym derivative of
\nu_{n}
with respect to
\nu_{n}^{\prime}
,
i.e.
a measurable function
\mathtt{p}_{n}:\Omega_{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{+}
such that

\nu_{n}=\mathtt{p}_{n}\,\nu^{\prime}_{n}+\alpha_{n},

where
\alpha_{n}
is a positive measure orthogonal to
\nu_{n}^{\prime}
. Let also
f_{n}:=\sqrt{\mathtt{p}_{n}}
. With this notation, we have

H(\nu_{n},\nu^{\prime}_{n})=\int_{\Omega_{n}}\sqrt{\mathtt{p}_{n}}\,d\nu^{\prime}_{n}=\int_{\Omega_{n}}f_{n}\,d\nu^{\prime}_{n}.

(i) Assume that
\prod_{n=0}^{\infty}H(\nu_{n},\nu^{\prime}_{n})>0
, and let us show that the measures
\nu
and
\nu^{\prime}
are not orthogonal, with
\nu\ll\nu^{\prime}
if
\nu_{n}\ll\nu^{\prime}_{n}
for all
n
.

For each
k\geq 0
, let
F_{k}:=\otimes_{n=0}^{k}f_{n}
,
i.e.

F_{k}:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{+}
is the function defined by

F_{k}(\omega):=\prod_{n=0}^{k}f_{n}(\omega_{n}).

We claim that
F_{k}
converges in
L_{2}(\Omega,\nu^{\prime})
to some
F:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{+}
. Indeed, if
0\leq p<q
, then

\displaystyle\|F_{q}-F_{p}\|_{L_{2}(\nu^{\prime})}^{2}

\displaystyle=\|F_{p}\|^{2}_{L_{2}(\nu^{\prime})}\left\|1-\otimes_{n=p+1}^{q}f_{n}\right\|^{2}_{L_{2}(\nu^{\prime})}

\displaystyle\leq\left\|1-\otimes_{n=p+1}^{q}f_{n}\right\|^{2}_{L_{2}(\nu^{\prime})}

\displaystyle\leq 2\left(1-\prod_{n=p+1}^{q}H(\nu_{n},\nu^{\prime}_{n})\right);

so we see that
(F_{k})_{k\geq 0}
is a Cauchy sequence in
L_{2}(\Omega,\nu)
.

By
L_{2}\,
- convergence and since
\int_{\Omega_{n}}f_{n}^{2}\,d\nu^{\prime}_{n}\geq\left(\int_{\Omega_{n}}f_{n}\,d\nu^{\prime}_{n}\right)^{2}
for all
n\geq 0
, we have

\int_{\Omega}F^{2}\,d\nu^{\prime}=\lim_{k\to\infty}\int_{\Omega}F_{k}^{2}\,d\nu^{\prime}\geq\prod_{n=0}^{\infty}H(\nu_{n},\nu^{\prime}_{n})^{2}>0.

Moreover, for any cylinder set
A=[A_{0},\dots,A_{N}]\subseteq\Omega
, we have

\displaystyle\nu(A)

\displaystyle=\prod_{n=0}^{N}\nu_{n}(A_{n})

\displaystyle\geq\prod_{n=0}^{N}\int_{A_{n}}\mathtt{p}_{n}\,d\nu^{\prime}_{n}

\displaystyle=\prod_{n=0}^{N}\int_{A_{n}}f_{n}^{2}\,d\nu^{\prime}_{n}\geq\int_{A}F^{2}\,d\nu^{\prime},

where the last inequality follows from the fact that
\int_{\Omega_{n}}f_{n}^{2}\,d\nu^{\prime}_{n}\leq 1
for every
n>N
.

Therefore, the positive measure
F^{2}\,\nu^{\prime}
is non-zero and absolutely continuous with respect to both
\nu^{\prime}
and
\nu
, and hence
\nu
and
\nu^{\prime}
are not orthogonal. Finally, if
\nu_{n}\ll\nu^{\prime}_{n}
for all
n
,
i.e.

\alpha_{n}=0
, then we see that
\nu(A)=\int_{A}F^{2}\,d\nu^{\prime}
for all cylinder sets
A
, so
\nu\ll\nu^{\prime}
.

(ii) Now, assume that
\prod_{n=0}^{\infty}H(\nu_{n},\nu^{\prime}_{n})=0
, and let us show that
\nu\perp\nu^{\prime}
. It is enough to show that for any
\varepsilon>0
, one can find a measurable set
E\subseteq\Omega
such that
\nu^{\prime}(E)<\varepsilon
and
\nu(\Omega\setminus E)<\varepsilon
.

Let
\varepsilon>0
, and let us choose
N\geq 0
such that
\prod_{n=0}^{N}H(\nu_{n},\nu^{\prime}_{n})<\varepsilon
,
i.e.

\int_{\Omega}F_{N}\,d\nu^{\prime}<\varepsilon.

Let us also choose, for each
n\in\llbracket 0,N\rrbracket
, a measurable set
A_{n}\subseteq\Omega_{n}
such that
\nu^{\prime}_{n}(A_{n})=0
and
\alpha_{n}(\Omega_{n}\setminus A_{n})=0
, and let

E:=\{\omega\in\Omega;\;F_{N}(\omega)\geq 1\}\cup\{\omega\in\Omega;\;\omega_{n}\in A_{n}\;\hbox{for some $n\in\llbracket 0,N\rrbracket$}\}.

We have on the one hand
\nu^{\prime}(E)=\nu^{\prime}(F_{N}\geq 1)<\varepsilon
; and, on the other hand,

\displaystyle\nu(\Omega\setminus E)

\displaystyle=\otimes_{n=0}^{\infty}(\mathtt{p}_{n}\,\nu^{\prime}_{n}+\alpha_{n})\,\bigl(\{F_{N}<1\}\cap[\Omega_{0}\setminus A_{0},\dots,\Omega_{N}\setminus A_{N}]\bigr)

\displaystyle=\otimes_{n=0}^{N}(\mathtt{p}_{n}\,\nu^{\prime}_{n})\bigl(\{(\omega_{0},\dots,\omega_{N});\;\sqrt{\mathtt{p}_{0}(\omega_{0})\cdots\mathtt{p}_{N}(\omega_{N})}<1\}\bigr)

\displaystyle=\int_{\sqrt{\mathtt{p}_{0}\otimes\cdots\otimes\mathtt{p}_{N}}<1}\mathtt{p}_{0}\otimes\cdots\otimes\mathtt{p}_{N}\,d\nu^{\prime}

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\Omega}\sqrt{\mathtt{p}_{0}\otimes\cdots\otimes\mathtt{p}_{N}}\,d\nu^{\prime}=\int_{\Omega}F_{N}\,d\nu^{\prime}<\varepsilon.

This concludes the proof.
∎

7.
Some questions

Of course, the main open question that remains at the end of this work is the one from which we started.

Question 7.1
.

Find a characterization of the pairs of weight sequences
({\boldsymbol{u}},{\boldsymbol{v}})
such that the weighted shifts
B_{\boldsymbol{u}}
and
B_{\boldsymbol{v}}
acting on
\ell_{p}
are orthogonal.

In Section
3
, we saw that orthogonality might be related to non-similarity, in a way which is not yet clear. In particular, in view of Theorem
3.10
, the following question is natural.

Question 7.2
.

Is it true that if
B_{\boldsymbol{u}}
and
B_{\boldsymbol{v}}
admit non-trivial invariant measures and are orthogonal, then either

\varliminf_{n\to\infty}\;\max_{0\leq d\leq N}\,\left|\frac{u_{1}\cdots u_{n+d}}{v_{1}\cdots v_{n+d}}\right|=0\qquad\hbox{for all $N\geq 0$}

or

\varlimsup_{n\to\infty}\;\min_{0\leq d\leq N}\,\left|\frac{u_{1}\cdots u_{n+d}}{v_{1}\cdots v_{n+d}}\right|=\infty\qquad\hbox{for all $N\geq 0$}?

In the same spirit, Example
4.5
shows that non-similarity does not imply orthogonality. Conversely, it would be quite interesting to know whether similarity implies non-orthogonality.

Question 7.3
.

Suppose that
B_{\boldsymbol{u}}
and
B_{\boldsymbol{v}}
admit non-trivial invariant measures and are similar,
i.e.

0<\varliminf\,\left|\frac{{u_{1}\cdots u_{n}}}{{v_{1}\cdots v_{n}}}\right|\leq\varlimsup\,\left|\frac{{u_{1}\cdots u_{n}}}{{v_{1}\cdots v_{n}}}\right|<\infty.

Does it follow that
B_{\boldsymbol{u}}
and
B_{\boldsymbol{w}}
are non-orthogonal?

Note that a positive answer to Question
7.2
would imply a positive answer to Question
7.3
. A very special case of the latter has a positive answer by Corollary
6.4
: if
B_{\boldsymbol{u}}
and
B_{\boldsymbol{v}}
admit non-trivial invariant measures and are
unitarily
similar, then they are non-orthogonal.

Next, in view of Theorem
5.13
, Proposition
5.22
and Theorem
6.11
, it is natural to ask whether the existence of equivalent non-trivial invariant product measures always implies the existence of equivalent invariant
Gaussian
product measures.

Question 7.4
.

Is it true in general that if
B_{\boldsymbol{u}}
and
B_{\boldsymbol{v}}
admit equivalent non-trivial invariant product measures
m_{\boldsymbol{u}}=\otimes_{n\geq 0}\mu_{{\boldsymbol{u}},n}
and
m_{\boldsymbol{v}}=\otimes\mu_{{\boldsymbol{v}},n}
, then they admit equivalent invariant Gaussian product measures?

From a more general point of view, the following question seems also natural.

Question 7.5
.

If
T_{1}
and
T_{2}
are non-orthogonal operators, does it follow that they admit equivalent non-trivial invariant measures?

In the same spirit, one may ask

Question 7.6
.

When do two weighted shifts
B_{\boldsymbol{u}}
and
B_{\boldsymbol{v}}
share a common non-trivial invariant measure?

Finally, the following seems to be unknown.

Question 7.7
.

Let
B_{\boldsymbol{w}}
be a weighted shift acting on
c_{0}(\mathbb{Z}_{+})
. Is it true that
B_{\boldsymbol{w}}
admits a non-trivial invariant measure if and only if
|{w_{1}\cdots w_{n}}|\to\infty
as
n\to\infty
?
