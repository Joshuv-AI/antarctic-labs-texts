Tameness in least fixed-point logic and McColm’s conjecture
Siddharth Bhaskar, Alex Kruckman

Abstract
. We investigate four model-theoretic tameness properties in the context of least fixed-point logic over a family of finite structures. We find that each of these properties depends only on the elementary (i.e., first-order) limit theory, and we completely determine the valid entailments among them. In contrast to the context of first-order logic on arbitrary structures, the order property and independence property are equivalent in this setting. McColm conjectured that least fixed-point definability collapses to first-order definability exactly when proficiency fails. McColm’s conjecture is known to be false in general. However, we show that McColm’s conjecture is true for any family of finite structures whose limit theory is model-theoretically tame.

1.
Introduction

Least fixed-point (
\mathrm{LFP}
) logic is obtained by extending first-order (
\mathrm{FO}
) logic by a quantifier denoting the least fixed-point of a relational operator. The difference between
\mathrm{FO}
and
\mathrm{LFP}
definability over classes of finite structures is a central question in finite model theory. McColm conjectured that the existence of arbitrarily long elementary inductions would suffice to separate
\mathrm{LFP}
from
\mathrm{FO}

[
McC90
]
. This conjecture was refuted by two separate constructions due to Gurevich, Immerman, and Shelah
[
GIS94
]
. However, instances of this conjecture remain interesting: in particular, any resolution of the
ordered conjecture
, which states that
\mathrm{LFP}
is more expressive than
\mathrm{FO}
over every class of totally ordered structures, would resolve a longstanding open problem in computational complexity
[
KV92
]
.

Recent work, e.g., Adler and Adler
[
AA14
]
, has shown that certain model-theoretic tameness properties introduced by Shelah
[
She90
]
are relevant to finite model theory, generalizing assumptions like
bounded cliquewidth
and
bounded treewidth
which permit, e.g., fast algorithms for formula evaluation
[
Cou90
]
. Here we study four such properties,
\mathrm{NOP}
,
\mathrm{NIP}
,
\mathrm{NSOP}
, and
\mathrm{NTP}_{2}
. We show that any counterexample to McColm’s conjecture must fail all of them, thus establishing in a precise sense that any such counterexample must be complicated (Theorem
23
).

This result was foreshadowed by McColm himself, who observed in
[
McC90
]
that any counterexample to his conjecture must fail another model-theoretic tameness property, viz., the negation of the finite cover property (
\mathrm{NFCP}
). Our work complements Lindell and Weinstein
[
LW00
]
, who show that any counterexample cannot be
recursion-theoretically
tame.

In the course of this investigation, we formulate
\mathrm{LFP}
versions of these four properties. In
\mathrm{FO}
logic,
\mathrm{NOP}\Rightarrow\mathrm{NSOP}
and
\mathrm{NOP}\Rightarrow\mathrm{NIP}\Rightarrow\mathrm{NTP}_{2}
, but no other entailments hold in general between these four properties. By contrast, in
\mathrm{LFP}
logic over finite structures, we have
\mathrm{NOP}\Leftrightarrow\mathrm{NIP}\Rightarrow\mathrm{NTP}_{2}\Rightarrow\mathrm{NSOP}
(Corollary
25
). Moreover, both of the implications in
\mathrm{NIP}\Rightarrow\mathrm{NTP}_{2}\Rightarrow\mathrm{NSOP}
are strict (Theorem
28
). We find the equivalence
\mathrm{NOP}\Leftrightarrow\mathrm{NIP}
especially remarkable: intuitively, it says that “order implies randomness” in this context.

Finally, we find that each of the properties of families of finite structures that we study, viz.,
\mathrm{FO}=\mathrm{LFP}
, proficiency, and
\mathrm{LFP}\mathchar 45\relax(\mathrm{NOP},\mathrm{NIP},\mathrm{NSOP},\mathrm{NTP}_{2})
, depend only on the elementary limit theory of the class of structures (Lemma
9
, Corollary
13
, and Corollary
22
). This relatively innocuous observation seems not to have been explicitly mentioned before, but the resulting shift in perspective, from classifying structures to classifying theories, brings these questions closer to the spirit of classical model theory.

1.1.
Least fixed-point logic

We assume familiarity with
\mathrm{FO}
and
\mathrm{LFP}
definability, and we very briefly review the latter. (See, e.g., Libkin
[
Lib04
]
for a reference.) Ordinary (first-order) variables are denoted by lowercase Latin letters, e.g.,
x,y,z
. Relational (second-order) variables are denoted by uppercase Latin letters, e.g.,
P,Q,R,S
. Every relational variable comes with an arity, but this is not made explicit in the notation.

Following the typical model-theoretic convention, we will also use, e.g.,
x
, to denote a tuple of (first-order) variables, not just a single variable. We write
|x|
to indicate the length of the tuple
x
. Below, when we write, e.g.,
\varphi(x,S)
, we mean that
\varphi
is a formula,
x
is a tuple of first-order variables (which includes all free first-order variables in
\varphi
), and
S
is a single second-order variable which is free in
\varphi
(but
\varphi
may have other free second-order variables).

We use boldface letters, especially
\mathbf{A}
and
\mathbf{M}
, to denote structures. Their respective domains are denoted in lightface, e.g.,
A
and
M
. We denote a class of structures by
\mathcal{C}
; its elements are always assumed to share a common signature.

{defi}

A formula
\varphi(x,S)
is
positive elementary in
S
if each occurrence of
S
is in the scope of an even number of negations. In addition,
\varphi(x,S)
is
operative
if it is positive elementary in
S
and the arity of
S
is
|x|
.

An operative formula
\varphi(x,S)
is so called because for any structure
\mathbf{A}
, given together with interpretations of all free second-order variables in
\varphi
other than
S
,
\varphi
defines a monotone operator
A^{|x|}\to A^{|x|}
by

R\mapsto\{a\in A^{|x|}\mid\mathbf{A}\models\varphi(a,R)\}.

For every ordinal
\alpha
, we define

\varphi^{\alpha}=\begin{cases}\emptyset&\text{if }\alpha=0\\
\{a\in A^{|x|}\mid\mathbf{A}\models\varphi(a,\varphi^{\beta})\}&\text{if }\alpha=\beta+1\\
\bigcup_{\beta<\alpha}\varphi^{\beta}&\text{if }\alpha\text{ is a limit}\end{cases}

The relations
\varphi^{\alpha}
are called the
stages
of
\varphi
on
\mathbf{A}
. The
closure ordinal

\|\varphi\|_{\mathbf{A}}
is the least ordinal
\Gamma
such that
\varphi^{\Gamma}=\varphi^{\Gamma+1}
. (Note that if
A
is finite,
\|\varphi\|_{\mathbf{A}}
must be finite.) The relation
\varphi^{\Gamma}
is the least fixed-point of the monotone operator defined by
\varphi
, and is written
\varphi^{\infty}
.

{defi}

The class of
\mathrm{LFP}
formulas
is obtained from the class of atomic formulas (in first- and second-order variables) by closing under boolean connectives, first-order quantifiers, and the least fixed-point quantifier: If
\varphi(x,S)
is an operative
\mathrm{LFP}
formula and
t
is a tuple of terms of length
|x|
, then
[\mathbf{lfp}\,Sx.\varphi](t)
is an
\mathrm{LFP}
formula, in which the free first-order variables are those in
t
and the free second-order variables are those in
\varphi
, except for
S
, which is bound by the quantifier.

The standard semantics of first-order logic are extended to the least fixed-point quantifier as follows: A structure
\mathbf{A}
(given together with an interpretation of the free first- and second-order variables) satisfies
[\mathbf{lfp}\,Sx.\varphi](t)
if and only if the interpretation of the tuple of terms
t
is in the relation
\varphi^{\infty}
.

{defi}

A
query

R(x)
of arity
n
over a class of structures
\mathcal{C}
is an isomorphism-invariant family of
n
-ary relations
R^{A}\subseteq A^{n}
for each
\mathbf{A}\in\mathcal{C}
. It is
\mathrm{LFP}
-definable
in case it is defined by some
\mathrm{LFP}
formula with no free second-order variables, uniformly over all structures in
\mathcal{C}
. An important special case is a
boolean
query, whose arity is zero.
\mathrm{LFP}
-definable boolean queries are defined by
\mathrm{LFP}
sentences.

We say
\mathrm{LFP}=\mathrm{FO}
over a class of structures
\mathcal{C}
if every query which is
\mathrm{LFP}
-definable over
\mathcal{C}
is defined by a first-order formula. Otherwise, we say
\mathrm{LFP}>\mathrm{FO}
over
\mathcal{C}
.

{defi}

An operative formula
\varphi(x,S)
is
basic
if it is first-order (i.e., it does not contain any instances of the least fixed-point quantifier) and it has no free second-order variables other than
S
.

Remark 1
.

We are primarily concerned with definability over families of finite structures. Immerman
[
Imm86
]
, building on the work of Moschovakis
[
Mos74
]
, proves the following normal form for
\mathrm{LFP}
formulas over finite structures:

(Qy)\,([\mathbf{lfp}\,Sx.\varphi](t))

for a basic operative formula
\varphi
, a tuple of terms
t
, and a string of first-order quantifiers
Qy
binding some of the free variables in
t
.

When working with a class of finite structures, this normal form allows us to restrict attention to
\mathrm{LFP}
formulas containing a single least fixed-point quantifier. In particular, we only need to consider least fixed-point quantification of basic operative formulas. On the other hand, when we want to show that a particular relation is
\mathrm{LFP}
-definable, we will freely make use of the full syntax in Definition
1.1
.

{defi}

Operative formulas
\varphi(x,S)
and
\psi(y,T)
are
complementary on finite structures
in case
|x|=|y|
and for every finite structure
\mathbf{A}
,

\mathbf{A}\models\forall z\,\left([\mathbf{lfp}\,Sx.\varphi](z)\leftrightarrow\lnot[\mathbf{lfp}\,Ty.\psi](z)\right).

Fact 2

(
[
Imm86
]
)
.

For every basic operative formula
\varphi(x,S)
, there exists a basic operative formula
\psi(y,T)
, such that
\varphi(x,S)
and
\psi(y,T)
are complimentary on finite structures.

1.2.
Proficiency and McColm’s conjecture

{defi}

We say that a class of finite structures
\mathcal{C}
is
proficient
if there exists a basic operative formula
\varphi(x,S)
such that

\sup\{\|\varphi\|_{\mathbf{A}}:\mathbf{A}\in\mathcal{C}\}=\omega.

Remark 3
.

For any basic operative formula
\varphi(x,S)
and any finite
n
, the stage
\varphi^{n}
is definable by a first-order formula, uniformly over all structures. It follows immediately that if a class of finite structures
\mathcal{C}
is not proficient, then
\mathrm{LFP}=\mathrm{FO}
over
\mathcal{C}
. This observation was made originally in

[
McC90
]
.

In 1990, McColm
[
McC90
]
conjectured that the following
three properties are equivalent, for any family of finite structures
\mathcal{C}
:

(1)

\mathcal{C}
is proficient.

(2)

\mathrm{FO}<\mathrm{LFP}
over
\mathcal{C}
.

(3)

\mathrm{FO}<\mathcal{L}_{\infty\omega}^{\omega}
over
\mathcal{C}
.

The implication from 2 to 1 is Remark
3
, and the implication from 3 to 1 is also easy. In 1992, Kolaitis and Vardi
[
KV92
]
proved the equivalence
of 1 and 3. In 1994, Gurevich, Immerman, and Shelah
[
GIS94
]

constructed two examples of a proficient family of structures for
which
\mathrm{FO}=\mathrm{LFP}
, thus establishing that 1 does not imply 2.

Historically, the equivalence of 1 and 2 has been called
McColm’s first conjecture
and the equivalence of 1 and 3
McColm’s second conjecture
(see, e.g.,
[
KV92
]
). In the interests of brevity, we will simply say
McColm’s conjecture
to mean the equivalence of 1 and 2, following the usage in
[
GIS94
]
.

1.3.
Elementary limit theories

In model theory, the most important invariant of a structure is its theory. In the present paper, the most important invariant of a family of finite structures is its limit theory.

{defi}

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a class of finite structures. For a first-order sentence
\varphi
, we write
\mathcal{C}\models\varphi
in case all but finitely many structures in
\mathcal{C}
satisfy
\varphi
. The
(elementary) limit theory
of a family
\mathcal{C}
of finite structures is

\mathrm{Th}(\mathcal{C})=\{\varphi\mid\varphi\text{ is a first-order sentence, and }\mathcal{C}\models\varphi\}.

Unlike the theory
\mathrm{Th}(\mathbf{A})
of a structure
\mathbf{A}
, limit theories are not always complete — nor consistent! But it is easy to see that
\mathrm{Th}(\mathcal{C})
is consistent if and only if
\mathcal{C}
is infinite.
Henceforth, we will only consider infinite families of finite structures.

Lemma 4
.

For any class of finite structures
\mathcal{C}
,
\mathrm{Th}(\mathcal{C})
is closed under logical consequence.

Proof 1.1
.

Suppose
\mathrm{Th}(\mathcal{C})\models\varphi
. By compactness,
\Delta\models\varphi
for some finite subset
\Delta\subseteq\mathrm{Th}(\mathcal{C})
. Each sentence in
\Delta
is true in all but finitely many structures in
\mathcal{C}
, and
\Delta
is finite, so all but finitely many structures in
\mathcal{C}
satisfy
\Delta
, and hence satisfy
\varphi
. Thus
\varphi\in\mathrm{Th}(\mathcal{C})
.

{defi}

A first-order theory
T
has the
finite model property
if for every sentence
\varphi
, if
T\models\varphi
, then
\varphi
has a finite model.

Lemma 5
.

Let
T
be a countable first-order theory. Then
T
has the finite model property if and only if
T\subseteq\mathrm{Th}(\mathcal{C})
for some infinite class of finite structures
\mathcal{C}
.

Proof 1.2
.

Suppose
T
has the finite model property. Enumerate
T
as
\{\varphi_{n}\mid n\in\mathbb{N}\}
, and let
\psi_{n}=\bigwedge_{i=0}^{n}\varphi_{i}
for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Then
T\models\psi_{n}
, so
\psi_{n}
has a finite model
\mathbf{A}_{n}
(which we may assume to be distinct from
\mathbf{A}_{m}
for all
m<n
). Letting
\mathcal{C}=\{A_{n}\mid n\in\mathbb{N}\}
, each sentence
\varphi_{n}\in T
is satisfied by
\mathbf{A}_{m}
for all
m\geq n
, so
\mathcal{C}\models\varphi_{n}
, and hence
T\subseteq\mathrm{Th}(\mathcal{C})
.

Conversely, suppose
T\subseteq\mathrm{Th}(\mathcal{C})
for some infinite class of finite structures
\mathcal{C}
. If
T\models\varphi
, then
\varphi\in\mathrm{Th}(\mathcal{C})
, by Lemma
4
. Since
\mathcal{C}
is infinite, there exists some
\mathbf{A}\in\mathcal{C}
such that
\mathbf{A}\models\varphi
, so
T
has the finite model property.

1.4.
Model-theoretic dividing lines

Stability theory originated in the work of Shelah in his program to classify the models of certain complete first-order theories. He discovered a robust division of theories into “stable” and “unstable.” The former are “tame” in the sense that their definable sets are highly structured, in a way that makes it possible to classify their models (under certain additional hypotheses); the latter are “wild” in the sense that they interpret combinatorial objects such as infinite linear orders and random graphs, and they necessarily have too many models to admit a nice classification.

A critical observation about the stable/unstable dichotomy is that stability can be defined by the absence of a simple combinatorial configuration in
definable sets,
namely the
order property
(described below). Subsequent work in “neo-stability” has generalized stability theory to increasingly more inclusive notions of tameness, each of which is defined by the absence of a particular configuration in
definable sets.
The goal is to find robust dividing lines, such that it is possible to prove structure theorems on the tame side and non-structure theorems on the wild side. For an interactive guide to the these dividing lines, see
[
Con
]
; see Hodges
[
Hod87
]
for a discussion of structure theorems.

Any formula
\varphi(x;y)
, whose free variables are partitioned into a tuple
x
and a tuple
y
, defines a bipartite graph relation
R_{\varphi}
between
M^{|x|}
and
M^{|y|}
for any structure
\mathbf{M}
, by
(a,b)\in R_{\varphi}
if and only if
\mathbf{M}\models\varphi(a;b)
. From another point of view, such a formula defines a family of
S_{\varphi}
of subsets of
M^{|x|}
: writing
\varphi(M;b)
for
\{a\in M^{|x|}\mid\mathbf{M}\models\varphi(a;b)\}
, we let
S_{\varphi}=\{\varphi(M;b)\mid b\in M^{|y|}\}
. By a combinatorial configuration, we usually mean some concrete property of the graph
R_{\varphi}
or the family of sets
S_{\varphi}
.

We will now give the precise definitions of the combinatorial properties we will consider in this paper, all
of which are originally due to Shelah
[
She90
]
. See below for further discussion.

{defi}

Let
\varphi(x;y)
be any formula (
\mathrm{FO}
or
\mathrm{LFP}
), whose free variables are partitioned into a tuple
x
and a tuple
y
. Let
n
be a natural number, and let
\mathbf{M}
be a structure.

•

\varphi
has an
n
-instance of the order property
(
\mathrm{OP}(n)
) in
\mathbf{M}
if there exist tuples
a_{1},\dots,a_{n}\in M^{|x|}
and
b_{1},\dots,b_{n}\in M^{|y|}
such that
\mathbf{M}\models\varphi(a_{i};b_{j})
if and only if
i\leq j
.

•

\varphi
has an
n
-instance of the independence property
(
\mathrm{IP}(n)
) in
\mathbf{M}
if there exist tuples
a_{i}\in M^{|x|}
for all
i\in\{1,\dots,n\}
and
b_{X}\in M^{|y|}
for all
X\subseteq\{1,\dots,n\}
such that
\mathbf{M}\models\varphi(a_{i};b_{X})
if and only if
i\in X
.

•

\varphi
has an
n
-instance of the strict order property
(
\mathrm{SOP}(n)
) in
\mathbf{M}
if there exist tuples
b_{1},\dots,b_{n}\in M^{|y|}
such that
\varphi(M;b_{i})\subseteq\varphi(M;b_{j})
if and only if
i\leq j
.

•

We say
\varphi(x;y)
has an
n
-instance of the tree property
of the second kind
(
\mathrm{TP}_{2}(n)
) in
\mathbf{M}
if there are tuples
b_{i,j}\in M^{|y|}

for
1\leq i,j\leq n
such that for any
i
and any
j\neq k
,
\varphi(M;b_{i,j})\cap\varphi(M;b_{i,k})=\emptyset
, but for any function
f\colon\{1,\dots,n\}\to\{1,\dots,n\}
,

\bigcap_{i=1}^{n}\varphi(M;b_{i,f(i)})\neq\emptyset.

{defi}

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a class of structures. For each property
P
in
\{\mathrm{OP},\mathrm{IP},\mathrm{SOP},\mathrm{TP}_{2}\}
, we say
\mathcal{C}

has

\mathrm{LFP}\mathchar 45\relax P
(resp.
\mathrm{FO}\mathchar 45\relax P
) if there exists an
\mathrm{LFP}
formula (resp. an
\mathrm{FO}
formula)
\varphi(x;y)
such that for each
n
, there exists a structure
\mathbf{M}\in\mathcal{C}
such that
\varphi
has
P(n)
in
\mathbf{M}
.

Let
T
be a theory. We say that
T
has
\mathrm{FO}\mathchar 45\relax P
if the class of models of
T
has
\mathrm{FO}\mathchar 45\relax P
.

If a class of structures
\mathcal{C}
or a theory
T
does not have
(\mathrm{FO}/\mathrm{LFP})\mathchar 45\relax\mathrm{OP}
(resp.
\mathrm{IP}
,
\mathrm{SOP}
,
\mathrm{TP}_{2}
), we say it has
(\mathrm{FO}/\mathrm{LFP})\mathchar 45\relax\mathrm{NOP}
(resp.
\mathrm{NIP}
,
\mathrm{NSOP}
,
\mathrm{NTP}_{2}
).

Remark 6
.

Our definitions of these properties differ from the standard definitions in model theory, which consist of a single infinite configuration, rather than a sequence of finite configurations. For example, according to the standard definition, a theory
T
has the order property if there exists an
\mathrm{FO}
formula
\varphi(x;y)
, a model
\mathbf{M}\models T
, and tuples
(a_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
in
M^{|x|}
and
(b_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
in
M^{|y|}
such that
\mathbf{M}\models\varphi(a_{i},b_{j})
if and only if
i\leq j
.

In the context of a first-order theory
T
, our definitions are equivalent to the standard ones, by an application of the compactness theorem. But compactness is not available in the context of
\mathrm{LFP}
definability, and the standard infinitary definitions are not meaningful over classes of finite structures.

Another advantage of using finite configurations is that the presence or absence of an
n
-instance of one of our properties in a structure
\mathbf{M}
is expressible by a single sentence. For example,
\varphi(x;y)
has
\mathrm{OP}(n)
in
\mathbf{M}
if and only if

\mathbf{M}\models\exists x_{1}\dots\exists x_{n}\exists y_{1}\dots\exists y_{n}\left(\bigwedge_{i\leq j}\varphi(x_{i};y_{j})\land\bigwedge_{i>j}\lnot\varphi(x_{i};y_{j})\right).

For
P\in\{\mathrm{OP},\mathrm{IP},\mathrm{SOP},\mathrm{TP}_{2}\}
, we denote by
P_{\varphi}(n)
the sentence expressing that
\varphi(x;y)
has
P(n)
. This leads immediately to the following lemma.

Lemma 7
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a class of finite structures. For any
P
in
\{\mathrm{OP},\mathrm{IP},\mathrm{SOP},\mathrm{TP}_{2}\}
,
\mathcal{C}
has
\mathrm{FO}\mathchar 45\relax P
if and only if
\mathrm{Th}(\mathcal{C})
has
\mathrm{FO}\mathchar 45\relax P
.

Proof 1.3
.

Suppose
\mathcal{C}
does not have
\mathrm{FO}\mathchar 45\relax P
. Then for every
\mathrm{FO}
formula
\varphi(x;y)
, there is some
n\in\mathbb{N}
such that for all
\mathbf{M}\in\mathcal{C}
,
\varphi(x;y)
does not have
P(n)
in
\mathbf{M}
. That is,
\lnot P_{\varphi}(n)
is true in every structure in
\mathcal{C}
, so
\lnot P_{\varphi}(n)\in\mathrm{Th}(\mathcal{C})
. Thus
\varphi(x;y)
does not have
P(n)
in any model of
T
, so
T
does not have
\mathrm{FO}\mathchar 45\relax P
.

Conversely, suppose
\mathrm{Th}(\mathcal{C})
does not have
\mathrm{FO}\mathchar 45\relax P
. Then for every
\mathrm{FO}
formula
\varphi(x;y)
, there is some
n\in\mathbb{N}
such that for all
\mathbf{M}\models\mathrm{Th}(\mathcal{C})
,
\varphi(x;y)
does not have
P(n)
in
\mathbf{M}
. By Lemma
4
,
\lnot P_{\varphi}(n)\in\mathrm{Th}(\mathcal{C})
, so
\varphi(x;y)
does not have
P(n)
in any structures in
\mathcal{C}
, except for finitely many exceptions. Each of these exceptional structures are finite, so there is some maximum
N
such that
\varphi(x;y)
has
P(N)
in any structure in
\mathcal{C}
. Thus
\mathcal{C}
does not have
\mathrm{FO}\mathchar 45\relax P
.

Intuitively, a formula
\varphi(x;y)
has the order property if arbitrarily long linear orders are represented in the bipartite graph
R_{\varphi}
, in the sense that the “half-graphs” appear as induced subgraphs:
a_{1},\dots,a_{n}
and
b_{1},\dots,b_{n}
with
a_{i}R_{\varphi}b_{j}
if and only if
i\leq j
. The independence property and the strict order property are two natural strengthenings of this condition:
\mathrm{IP}
is equivalent to the condition that
arbitrary
bipartite graphs appear as induced subgraphs of
R_{\varphi}
, and
\mathrm{SOP}
says that arbitrarily long linear orders appear as chains in the family of sets
S_{\varphi}
.

It is not hard to see that any formula with
\mathrm{IP}(n)
or
\mathrm{SOP}(n+1)
in a structure has
\mathrm{OP}(n)
in that structure. At the level of of complete first-order theories, the converse is true (but the same formula need not serve as the witness). This important dichotomy is due to Shelah: an unstable (
\mathrm{OP}
) theory must exhibit order (
\mathrm{SOP}
) or randomness (
\mathrm{IP}
).

Fact 8

(
[
She90
, Theorem II.4.7]
)
.

A first-order theory
T
has
\mathrm{FO}\mathchar 45\relax\mathrm{OP}
if and only if it has
\mathrm{FO}\mathchar 45\relax\mathrm{IP}
or
\mathrm{FO}\mathchar 45\relax\mathrm{SOP}
.

The tree property of the second kind is admittedly somewhat less intuitive. Roughly speaking, a formula
\varphi(x;y)
has the tree property of the second kind if the family of sets
S_{\varphi}
includes arbitrarily many arbitrarily large families of disjoint sets, such that these families interact independently. It is not hard to see that any formula with
\mathrm{TP}_{2}(n)
in a structure has
\mathrm{IP}(n)
in that structure.

The name
\mathrm{TP}_{2}
comes from another important dichotomy identified by Shelah: a theory is called
simple
if it does not have the tree property (
\mathrm{TP}
), and a theory has the tree property if and only if it has the tree property of the first kind (
\mathrm{TP}_{1}
) or the tree property of the second kind (
\mathrm{TP}_{2}
). Unlike
\mathrm{TP}_{2}
, the configurations defining the properties
\mathrm{TP}
and
\mathrm{TP}_{1}
are visibly related to trees. We will not consider the properties
\mathrm{TP}
and
\mathrm{TP}_{1}
in this paper.

1.5.
Examples via Fraïssé limits

Fraïssé theory is a fruitful source of examples in model theory and provides an important connection between classes of finite structures and the model theory of complete first-order theories. If a class
\mathcal{C}
of finite structures is isomorphism-closed and countable up to isomorphism, and has the hereditary property, the joint embedding property, and the amalgamation property, then it admits a unique countable
Fraïssé limit

\mathbf{M}_{\mathcal{C}}
. This structure is universal and homogeneous for
\mathcal{C}
, in the sense that a finite structure is in
\mathcal{C}
if and only if it embeds in
\mathbf{M}_{\mathcal{C}}
, and any two such embeddings are conjugate by an automorphism of
\mathbf{M}_{\mathcal{C}}
. The theory
\mathrm{Th}(\mathbf{M}_{\mathcal{C}})
is called the
generic theory
of
\mathcal{C}
. For more on Fraïssé theory, see
[
Hod93
, Section 7.1]
.

Here are some examples of generic theories, and which properties from Definition
1.4
they do and do not satisfy:

•

T_{\infty}
, the theory of an infinite set with no additional structure. This is the generic theory of the class of finite sets. It has
\mathrm{FO}\mathchar 45\relax\mathrm{NOP}
(and hence
\mathrm{FO}\mathchar 45\relax\mathrm{NIP}
,
\mathrm{FO}\mathchar 45\relax\mathrm{NSOP}
, and
\mathrm{FO}\mathchar 45\relax\mathrm{NTP}_{2}
).

•

\mathrm{DLO}
, the theory of dense linear orders without endpoints. This is the generic theory of the class of finite linear orders. It has
\mathrm{FO}\mathchar 45\relax\mathrm{SOP}
(and hence
\mathrm{FO}\mathchar 45\relax\mathrm{OP}
), but
\mathrm{FO}\mathchar 45\relax\mathrm{NIP}
(and hence
\mathrm{FO}\mathchar 45\relax\mathrm{NTP}_{2}
).

•

T_{\mathrm{rg}}
, the theory of the random graph. This is (by definition) the generic theory of the class of finite graphs. It has
\mathrm{FO}\mathchar 45\relax\mathrm{IP}
(and hence
\mathrm{FO}\mathchar 45\relax\mathrm{OP}
), but
\mathrm{FO}\mathchar 45\relax\mathrm{NSOP}
and
\mathrm{FO}\mathchar 45\relax\mathrm{NTP}_{2}
.

•

T_{\mathrm{org}}
, the generic theory of the class of finite graphs equipped with an ordering of their vertices. It has
\mathrm{FO}\mathchar 45\relax\mathrm{IP}
and
\mathrm{FO}\mathchar 45\relax\mathrm{SOP}
(and hence
\mathrm{FO}\mathchar 45\relax\mathrm{OP}
), but
\mathrm{FO}\mathchar 45\relax\mathrm{NTP}_{2}
.

•

T_{\mathrm{feq}}
, the generic theory of the class of finite parameterized equivalence relations. The language consists of two unary predicates
O
and
P
(for “objects” and “parameters”), and one ternary relation symbol
E
. A parameterized equivalence relation is a structure such that
O
and
P
partition the domain, and for every element
a
satisfying
P
, the binary relation
E(a,x,y)
is an equivalence relation on the elements satisfying
O
. This theory has
\mathrm{FO}\mathchar 45\relax\mathrm{TP}_{2}
(and hence
\mathrm{FO}\mathchar 45\relax\mathrm{IP}
and
\mathrm{FO}\mathchar 45\relax\mathrm{OP}
) but
\mathrm{FO}\mathchar 45\relax\mathrm{NSOP}
.

•

T_{\mathrm{aba}}
, the theory of atomless Boolean algebras. This is the generic theory of the class of finite Boolean algebras. It has
\mathrm{FO}\mathchar 45\relax\mathrm{SOP}
and
\mathrm{FO}\mathchar 45\relax\mathrm{TP}_{2}
(and hence
\mathrm{FO}\mathchar 45\relax\mathrm{IP}
and
\mathrm{FO}\mathchar 45\relax\mathrm{OP}
).

We will return to several of these examples in Section
3
below.

2.
\mathrm{FO}
characterizations of
\mathrm{LFP}
properties

Many important properties of a class of finite structures
\mathcal{C}
depend only on the elementary limit theory
\mathrm{Th}(\mathcal{C})
, in the sense that any two classes of structures with the same limit theory agree on the property in question. Lemma
7
shows this holds of the FO properties from Definition
1.4
. In this section, we show that it additionally holds for proficiency, “FO = LFP over
\mathcal{C}
,” and all the LFP properties from Definition
1.4
.

2.1.
Proficiency and
\mathrm{FO}=\mathrm{LFP}

By Remark
3
, each finite stage
\varphi^{n}
of a basic operative formula
\varphi(x,S)
is definable by a first order formula, uniformly over all structures. We also use
\varphi^{n}(x)
to denote any such formula. The context that the symbol
\varphi^{n}
appears in will distinguish whether we mean the formula or the relation it defines.

{defi}

A theory
T
is
not proficient
in case for every basic operative formula
\varphi(x,S)
, there is a natural number
n
such that

T\models(\forall x)(\varphi^{n}(x)\leftrightarrow\varphi^{n+1}(x)).

Otherwise, we say that
T
is
proficient
.

Lemma 9
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a class of finite structures. Then
\mathcal{C}
is proficient if and only if
\mathrm{Th}(\mathcal{C})
is proficient.

Proof 2.1
.

Suppose that
\mathcal{C}
is not proficient. Then for each basic operative formula
\varphi(x,S)
, there is a bound
n\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\|\varphi\|_{\mathbf{A}}\leq n
for all
\mathbf{A}\in\mathcal{C}
. Therefore, for all
\mathbf{A}\in\mathcal{C}
,
\mathbf{A}\models(\forall x)(\varphi^{n}(x)\leftrightarrow\varphi^{n+1}(x)),
and hence
\mathrm{Th}(\mathcal{C})\models(\forall x)(\varphi^{n}(x)\leftrightarrow\varphi^{n+1}(x)).

Conversely, suppose that
\mathrm{Th}(\mathcal{C})
is not proficient, and let
\varphi
be any basic operative formula. Then, there is some
n\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\mathrm{Th}(\mathcal{C})\models(\forall x)(\varphi^{n}(x)\leftrightarrow\varphi^{n+1}(x)).
Hence, for all but finitely many
\mathbf{A}\in\mathcal{C}
,
\mathbf{A}\models(\forall x)(\varphi^{n}(x)\leftrightarrow\varphi^{n+1}(x)),
and therefore
\|\varphi\|_{\mathbf{A}}\leq n
for all but finitely many
\mathbf{A}\in\mathcal{C}
. Since there are only finitely many exceptional structures,
\sup\{\|\varphi\|_{\mathbf{A}}:\mathbf{A}\in\mathcal{C}\}
must still be finite.

Corollary 10
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
and
\mathcal{D}
be classes of finite structures.
If
\mathrm{Th}(\mathcal{C})=\mathrm{Th}(\mathcal{D})
, then
\mathcal{C}
is proficient if and only if
\mathcal{D}
is proficient.

The proof of Lemma
9
actually shows that if
\mathrm{Th}(\mathcal{C})\subseteq\mathrm{Th}(\mathcal{D})
, and
\mathcal{D}
is proficient, then
\mathcal{C}
is proficient. Similar refinements, replacing equality of limit theories with containment, can be observed for many of the results in this section.

{defi}

Let
\varphi(x,S)
be a basic operative formula, and let
\mathcal{C}
be a class of finite structures. We say that
\varphi^{\infty}
is elementary over
\mathcal{C}
if there is a first-order formula
\gamma(x)
which defines the query
\varphi^{\infty}
over
\mathcal{C}
.

Lemma 11
.

\varphi^{\infty}
is elementary over
\mathcal{C}
if and only if there is a first-order formula
\gamma(x)
which defines the query
\varphi^{\infty}
over all but finitely many structures in
\mathcal{C}
.

Proof 2.2
.

One direction is trivial. For the other direction, suppose
\gamma(x)
defines
\varphi^{\infty}
over all but finitely many structures in
\mathcal{C}
. Since every finite structure is determined up to isomorphism by a first-order sentence, and every automorphism-invariant relation on a finite structure is definable by a first-order formula, we can modify
\gamma(x)
so that it defines
\varphi^{\infty}
in each of the finitely many exceptional cases.

Note that the sentence

\forall x\,([\mathbf{lfp}\,Sx.\varphi](x)\leftrightarrow\gamma(x)),

which expresses that
\gamma
defines
\varphi^{\infty}
, is not first-order. So it does not follow directly from Lemma
11
that elementarity of
\varphi^{\infty}
over
\mathcal{C}
is a property of the limit theory
\mathrm{Th}(\mathcal{C})
. Nevertheless, this turns out to be true, as we will now show.

Lemma 12
.

Let
\varphi(x,S)
be a basic operative formula. Then
\varphi^{\infty}
is elementary over
\mathcal{C}
if and only if there exists a first-order formula
\theta(x)
such that

\displaystyle\forall x\,(\varphi(x,\theta)\leftrightarrow\theta)

\displaystyle\in\mathrm{Th}(\mathcal{C}),\text{and}

\displaystyle\forall x\,(\psi(x,\neg\theta)\leftrightarrow\neg\theta)

\displaystyle\in\mathrm{Th}(\mathcal{C}),

where
\psi(x,S)
is a basic operative formula which is complementary to
\varphi(x,S)
on finite structures.

Proof 2.3
.

Suppose
\varphi^{\infty}
is elementary relative to
\mathcal{C}
, witnessed by
\theta
. In an arbitrary structure
\mathbf{A}\in\mathcal{C}
,
\theta
defines
\varphi^{\infty}
, which is a fixed-point for
\varphi
. Since
\psi(x,S)
and
\varphi(x,S)
are complementary on finite structures,
\neg\theta
defines
\psi^{\infty}
, which is a fixed-point for
\psi
. So
\mathbf{A}
satisfies the sentences in the statement of the lemma.

Conversely, suppose these two sentences are in
\mathrm{Th}(\mathcal{C})
. Then for all but finitely many structures
\mathbf{A}\in\mathcal{C}
, the relation
\theta^{\mathbf{A}}
defined by
\theta
over
\mathbf{A}
is a fixed-point of
\varphi
, and its complement
(\lnot\theta)^{\mathbf{A}}
, which is defined by
\lnot\theta
, is a fixed point of
\psi
. Since
\varphi^{\infty}
and
\psi^{\infty}
are the least fixed-points of
\varphi
and
\psi
,
\varphi^{\infty}\subseteq\theta^{\mathbf{A}}
and
\psi^{\infty}\subseteq(\neg\theta)^{\mathbf{A}}
. Since
\varphi^{\infty}
and
\psi^{\infty}
are complements,
\varphi^{\infty}=\theta^{\mathbf{A}}
. This is true for all but finitely many structures in
\mathcal{C}
, so
\varphi^{\infty}
is elementary over
\mathcal{C}
by Lemma
11
.

Corollary 13
.

If
\mathrm{Th}(\mathcal{C})=\mathrm{Th}(\mathcal{D})
, then
\mathrm{LFP}=\mathrm{FO}
over
\mathcal{C}
if and only if
\mathrm{LFP}=\mathrm{FO}
over
\mathcal{D}
.

Proof 2.4
.

Suppose that every
\mathrm{LFP}
-definable query over
\mathcal{C}
is
\mathrm{FO}
-definable. Consider an arbitrary
\mathrm{LFP}
-definable query
R
over
\mathcal{D}
. By the normal form for
\mathrm{LFP}
formulas over finite structures (Remark
1
), we may assume that
R
is defined by the
\mathrm{LFP}
formula
(Qy)\,([\mathbf{lfp}\,Sx.\varphi](t))
. Then it suffices to show that
\varphi^{\infty}
is elementary over
\mathcal{D}
.

By assumption,
\varphi^{\infty}
is elementary over
\mathcal{C}
. But
\mathrm{Th}(\mathcal{C})=\mathrm{Th}(\mathcal{D})
, so by Lemma
12
,
\varphi^{\infty}
is elementary over
\mathcal{D}
. The converse follows in the same way.

Remark 14
.

Even though
\mathrm{FO}=\mathrm{LFP}
is a property of limit theories, we have
not
proven (and in fact it is not true) that
\mathrm{FO}=\mathrm{LFP}
over
\mathcal{C}
if and only if
\mathrm{FO}=\mathrm{LFP}
over the class of models of
\mathrm{Th}(\mathcal{C})
. This stands in contrast to the properties FO-
P
and proficiency, which do pass (Lemmata
7
and
9
) between
\mathcal{C}
and models of
\mathrm{Th}(\mathcal{C})
.

We learned Lemmata
9
and
12
from Steven Lindell through personal communication. As an immediate consequence of Lemma
9
and Corollary
13
, we deduce the following.

Corollary 15
.

If
\mathrm{Th}(\mathcal{C})=\mathrm{Th}(\mathcal{D})
, then
\mathcal{C}
satisfies McColm’s conjecture if and only if
\mathcal{D}
does.

Remark 16
.

If we were to define the
\mathrm{LFP}
limit theory
to be the set of all
\mathrm{LFP}
sentences that hold of all but finitely many structures in
\mathcal{C}
, then both
\mathrm{FO}=\mathrm{LFP}
and proficiency could easily seen to depend only on the
\mathrm{LFP}
limit theory. One might naturally wonder whether the
\mathrm{LFP}
limit theory itself depends only on the (elementary) limit theory; this would contain Lemma
9
and Corollary
13
as special cases.

However, this is not the case. For example, the family of even-sized linear orders and the family of odd-sized linear orders are two families with the same (elementary) limit theory: the complete theory of infinite discrete linear orders with endpoints. But they are distinguished by their
\mathrm{LFP}
limit theories, since parity of the domain is an
\mathrm{LFP}
-definable boolean query over ordered structures.

2.2.
Model-theoretic dividing lines

We will now show that each
\mathrm{LFP}
property defined in Definition
1.4
depends only on the elementary limit theory of a family of finite structures. We start by identifying proficiency with
\mathrm{LFP}\mathchar 45\relax\mathrm{SOP}
. Key to this argument is the
\mathrm{LFP}
-definability of the
stage comparison relation

[
Mos74
]
.

{defi}

For any basic operative formula
\varphi(x,S)
with
|x|=k
, any structure
\mathbf{A}
, and any
a\in A^{k}
, the
stage of
a
,
\|a\|_{\varphi}
, is the least ordinal
\alpha
such that
a\notin\varphi^{\alpha}
, or
\infty
if
\alpha\notin\varphi^{\infty}
. The
stage comparison relation

\preceq_{\varphi}
is defined by
a\preceq_{\varphi}b
if and only if
\|a\|_{\varphi}\leq\|b\|_{\varphi}
.

Fact 17

(
[
Mos74
, Theorem 2A.2]
)
.

For any basic operative formula
\varphi(x,S)
, the stage comparison relation
\preceq_{\varphi}
is
\mathrm{LFP}
-definable over the class of all structures.

By a
partial preorder
, we mean a reflexive transitive relation
\preceq
on some set
X
. We get a partial order if we take the quotient by the equivalence relation
x\preceq y\wedge y\preceq x
. A partial preorder is
linear
if the associated partial order is linear. By a chain in a partial preorder
\preceq
, we mean a subset
X
which is linearly ordered by
\preceq
. In particular, for
x,y\in X
,
x\preceq y
and
y\preceq x
implies
x=y
.

Note that for any basic operative formula
\varphi(x,S)
with
|x|=k
and any structure
\mathbf{A}
, the stage comparison relation
\preceq_{\varphi}
is always a linear preorder on
A^{k}
, whose associated linear order is a well-order.

Theorem 18
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a class of finite structures. The following are equivalent:

(1)

\mathcal{C}
is proficient.

(2)

There is some
n\in\mathbb{N}
and some
\mathrm{LFP}
formula
\psi
such that
\psi
defines a linear preorder on
n
-tuples in every structure in
\mathcal{C}
, and this linear preorder has arbitrarily long finite chains in structures in
\mathcal{C}
.

(3)

There is some
n\in\mathbb{N}
and some
\mathrm{LFP}
formula
\psi
such that
\psi
defines a partial preorder on
n
-tuples in every structure in
\mathcal{C}
, and this partial preorder has arbitrarily long finite chains in structures in
\mathcal{C}
.

Proof 2.5
.

(
1\Rightarrow 2
) Suppose
\varphi(x,S)
is a basic operative formula with
|x|=n
, which witnesses proficiency of
\mathcal{C}
. Its stage comparison relation
\preceq_{\varphi}
, which is
\mathrm{LFP}
-definable by Fact
17
, linearly preorders the
n
-tuples from each
\mathbf{M}\in\mathcal{C}
, and this linear preorder contains a chain of length
\|\varphi\|_{\mathbf{M}}
. By proficiency, there is no finite bound on the lengths of these chains.

(
2\Rightarrow 3
) Trivially.

(
3\Rightarrow 1
) Suppose
\lambda(y_{1};y_{2})
defines a partial preorder
\preceq
which has arbitrarily long finite chains in structures in
\mathcal{C}
. In any finite partial
preorder, we define the
height
of an element to be one more
than the maximum height among its (strict) predecessors, or 0 if it has none.
Since
\lambda
has arbitrarily long chains, elements in the preorder
defined by
\lambda
will have arbitrarily large heights.

Let
\varphi(y;T)
say that all of
y
’s strict predecessors are
in
T
. In symbols,

\varphi(y;T)\equiv\forall y^{\prime}\,((\lambda(y^{\prime},y)\wedge\neg\lambda(y,y^{\prime}))\to T(y^{\prime})).

(Notice
T
occurs positively in
\varphi
.) Then it is easy to
show by induction that the stages
\varphi^{n}
of
\varphi

are exactly those elements of height
<n
, and hence
\varphi
witnesses that
\mathcal{C}
is
proficient.

Theorem 19
.

\mathcal{C}
is proficient if and only if it has
\mathrm{LFP}\mathchar 45\relax\mathrm{SOP}
.

Proof 2.6
.

Suppose that the
\mathrm{LFP}
formula
\varphi
witnesses that
\mathcal{C}
has
\mathrm{LFP}\mathchar 45\relax\mathrm{SOP}
. Define

\psi(y_{1};y_{2})\equiv\forall x\ (\varphi(x;y_{1})\to\varphi(x;y_{2})),

so that for each
\mathbf{M}\in\mathcal{C}
and
b_{1},b_{2}\in M^{|y|}
,

\mathbf{M}\models\psi(b_{1};b_{2})\text{ if and only if }\varphi(M;b_{1})\subseteq\varphi(M;b_{2}),

where (as in Section
1.4
),
\varphi(M;b)=\{a\in M^{|x|}\mid\mathbf{M}\models\varphi(a;b)\}
. Then
\psi
defines a partial preorder on
M^{|y|}
. If
\varphi
has
\mathrm{SOP}(n)
in
M
, then this partial preorder on
M^{|y|}
contains a chain of length
n
. Since

\varphi
has the strict order property, we have arbitrarily long chains in structures in
\mathcal{C}
, so
\mathcal{C}
is proficient by Theorem
18
.

Conversely, suppose that
\mathcal{C}
is proficient. By Theorem
18
, there exists an
\mathrm{LFP}
formula
\lambda(y_{1};y_{2})
that defines a partial preorder with
arbitrarily long chains in structures in
\mathcal{C}
. The formula
\lambda
itself
witnesses the strict order property: given
n
, pick
\mathbf{M}\in\mathcal{C}

which contains a chain
b_{1},\dots,b_{n}
. Then

\lambda(M^{|y_{1}|};b_{1})\subsetneq\dots\subsetneq\lambda(M^{|y_{1}|};b_{n}).

Among all the
\mathrm{LFP}
properties from Definition
1.4
, the strict order property turns out to be the strongest, in that in entails all the others. This contrasts with the first-order case where, in general, the strict order property does not imply the independence property or the tree property of the second kind.

Lemma 20
.

If
\mathcal{C}
has
\mathrm{LFP}\mathchar 45\relax\mathrm{SOP}
, then it also
has
\mathrm{LFP}\mathchar 45\relax\mathrm{OP}
,
\mathrm{LFP}\mathchar 45\relax\mathrm{IP}
, and
\mathrm{LFP}\mathchar 45\relax\mathrm{TP}_{2}
.

Proof 2.7
.

As noted in Section
1.4
,
\mathrm{LFP}\mathchar 45\relax\mathrm{SOP}
easily implies
\mathrm{LFP}\mathchar 45\relax\mathrm{OP}
.
For the other two properties, we consider the family
\mathcal{N}
of all finite linear orders. Identify the unique linear order of size
n
with
the set
n=\{0,1,\dots,n-1\}
equipped with its natural ordering.
It is well known that over
\mathcal{N}
, the graphs of addition and multiplication are
\mathrm{LFP}
-definable; hence, so is the graph of exponentiation
[
Lin
]
. Therefore, since the relations
\mathrm{bit}(x;y)
:

``\text{the $x$-th bit of $y$ base $2$ is $1$}"

and
\mathrm{factor}(x;y,z)
:

``y^{z}\text{ is the largest power of $y$ dividing }x"

are first-order definable over
\mathcal{N}
with addition, multiplication, and exponentiation, they are
\mathrm{LFP}
-definable over
\mathcal{N}
.

The relation
\mathrm{bit}(x;y)
has
\mathrm{IP}(n)
in
m
for sufficiently large
m
, witnessed by
a_{i}=i-1
for
i\in\{1,\dots,n\}
and
b_{X}=\sum_{j\in X}2^{j-1}
for

X\subseteq\{1,\dots,n\}
. The relation
\mathrm{factor}(x;y,z)
has TP2(
n
)
in
(m,<)
for sufficiently large
m
, witnessed by
b_{i,j}=(p_{i},j)
, where
(p_{i})_{i\in\omega}

is an enumeration of the primes: for any function
f\colon\{1,\dots,n\}\to\{1,\dots,n\}
, we have
a_{f}=\prod_{i=1}^{n}p_{i}^{f(i)}\in\mathrm{factor}(M;p_{i},f(i))
for all

. Hence,
\mathcal{N}
has
\mathrm{LFP}\mathchar 45\relax\mathrm{IP}
and
\mathrm{LFP}\mathchar 45\relax\mathrm{TP}_{2}
.

Now suppose
\mathcal{C}
has
\mathrm{LFP}\mathchar 45\relax\mathrm{SOP}
. Since there is some
\mathrm{LFP}
formula
\psi
which defines a linear preorder on
n
-tuples with arbitrarily long chains in structures in
\mathcal{C}
(by Theorem
18
and Theorem
19
), we can repeat the constructions of
\mathrm{bit}
and
\mathrm{factor}
above to get formulas witnessing that
\mathcal{C}
has
\mathrm{LFP}\mathchar 45\relax\mathrm{IP}
and
\mathrm{LFP}\mathchar 45\relax\mathrm{TP}_{2}
.

To be a little more concrete, suppose

\varphi(x;y)
witnesses
\mathrm{LFP}\mathchar 45\relax\mathrm{IP}
or
\mathrm{LFP}\mathchar 45\relax\mathrm{TP}_{2}
over
\mathcal{N}
.
Simply replace each variable
v
in
\varphi
by
n
variables
v_{1},\dots,v_{n}
, replace
v=w
by
(v\leq w\land w\leq v)
, replace
v\leq w
by
\psi(v_{1},\dots,v_{n};w_{1},\dots,w_{n})
,
and proceed by induction on the construction of
\varphi
in the
obvious way. This gives us a new formula
\varphi^{\star}(x^{\star};y^{\star})

witnessing
\mathrm{LFP}\mathchar 45\relax\mathrm{IP}
or
\mathrm{LFP}\mathchar 45\relax\mathrm{TP}_{2}
over
\mathcal{C}
.

Theorem 21
.

For any class
\mathcal{C}
of finite structures and any

in
\{\mathrm{OP},\mathrm{IP},\mathrm{SOP},\mathrm{TP}_{2}\}
,

has

.

.

In the forwards direction, if
\mathcal{C}

, but is not proficient, then
\mathrm{FO}=\mathrm{LFP}
over
\mathcal{C}
(Remark

), so

has
\mathrm{FO}\mathchar 45\relax P
.

Conversely, if
\mathcal{C}
is proficient, then it has
\mathrm{LFP}\mathchar 45\relax P
by Theorems
19
and
20
. Otherwise, if
\mathcal{C}
has
\mathrm{FO}\mathchar 45\relax P
, then it trivially has
\mathrm{LFP}\mathchar 45\relax P
as well.

Theorem
21
, when combined with Lemmata
7
and
9
, has the immediate consequence that all the
\mathrm{LFP}
properties depend only on the elementary limit theory of a family of structures.

Corollary 22
.

Suppose
\mathcal{C}
and
\mathcal{D}
are families of structures with the same limit theory, and let
P
be any property in
\{\mathrm{OP},\mathrm{IP},\mathrm{SOP},\mathrm{TP}_{2}\}
. Then

\mathcal{C}
has
\mathrm{LFP}\mathchar 45\relax P
if and only if
\mathcal{D}
has
\mathrm{LFP}\mathchar 45\relax P
.

It also has the nice consequence that any tame class of structures (in the sense of first-order model theory) satisfies McColm’s conjecture.

Theorem 23
.

For any family of finite structures
\mathcal{C}
, if
\mathcal{C}
has
\mathrm{FO}\mathchar 45\relax\mathrm{NOP}
,
\mathrm{FO}\mathchar 45\relax\mathrm{NIP}
,
\mathrm{FO}\mathchar 45\relax\mathrm{NTP}_{2}
, or
\mathrm{FO}\mathchar 45\relax\mathrm{NSOP}
, then
\mathcal{C}
satisfies McColm’s conjecture.

Proof 2.9
.

Let
P\in\{\mathrm{OP},\mathrm{IP},\mathrm{SOP},\mathrm{TP}_{2}\}
be a property such that
\mathcal{C}
does not have
\mathrm{FO}\mathchar 45\relax P
. To show that
\mathcal{C}
satisfies McColm’s conjecture, it suffices to show that if
\mathcal{C}
is proficient, then
\mathrm{LFP}\neq\mathrm{FO}
over
\mathcal{C}
.

So we assume
\mathcal{C}
is proficient. Then
\mathcal{C}
has
\mathrm{LFP}\mathchar 45\relax P
, by Theorem
19
and Lemma
20
. Since it does not have
\mathrm{FO}\mathchar 45\relax P
,
\mathrm{LFP}\neq\mathrm{FO}
over
\mathcal{C}
, as desired.

3.
Entailments between
\mathrm{LFP}
properties

We continue towards determining all valid entailments among the
\mathrm{LFP}
properties. First, we show that an important dichotomy remains true in the
\mathrm{LFP}
context.

Theorem 24
.

For any family
\mathcal{C}
of finite structures,

\mathcal{C}
has
\mathrm{LFP}\mathchar 45\relax\mathrm{OP}
if and only if
\mathcal{C}
has
\mathrm{LFP}\mathchar 45\relax\mathrm{SOP}
or
\mathrm{LFP}\mathchar 45\relax\mathrm{IP}
.

Proof 3.1
.

Since both
\mathrm{LFP}\mathchar 45\relax\mathrm{SOP}
and
\mathrm{LFP}\mathchar 45\relax\mathrm{IP}
entail
\mathrm{LFP}\mathchar 45\relax\mathrm{OP}
, it suffices to show the forwards direction.

Suppose
\mathcal{C}
has
\mathrm{LFP}\mathchar 45\relax\mathrm{OP}
. If
\mathcal{C}
is proficient, then it has
\mathrm{LFP}\mathchar 45\relax\mathrm{SOP}
by Theorem
19
. If
\mathcal{C}
is not proficient, then
\mathcal{C}
has
\mathrm{FO}\mathchar 45\relax\mathrm{NSOP}
and
\mathrm{FO}\mathchar 45\relax\mathrm{OP}
by Theorem
22
, so
\mathrm{Th}(\mathcal{C})
has
\mathrm{FO}\mathchar 45\relax\mathrm{NSOP}
and
\mathrm{FO}\mathchar 45\relax\mathrm{OP}
by Lemma
7
. It follows that
\mathrm{Th}(\mathcal{C})
has
\mathrm{FO}\mathchar 45\relax\mathrm{IP}
by Fact
8
. Hence
\mathcal{C}
has
\mathrm{FO}\mathchar 45\relax\mathrm{IP}
, and therefore
\mathrm{LFP}\mathchar 45\relax\mathrm{IP}
.

By Theorems
20
,
24
, and propositional reasoning, we obtain the following.

Corollary 25
.

For any family
\mathcal{C}
of finite structures,

\mathcal{C}\models\mathrm{LFP}\mathchar 45\relax\mathrm{OP}\iff\mathcal{C}\models\mathrm{LFP}\mathchar 45\relax\mathrm{IP}.

Therefore,

\mathcal{C}\models\mathrm{LFP}\mathchar 45\relax\mathrm{SOP}\implies\mathcal{C}\models\mathrm{LFP}\mathchar 45\relax\mathrm{TP}_{2}\implies\mathcal{C}\models\mathrm{LFP}\mathchar 45\relax\mathrm{OP}\iff\mathcal{C}\models\mathrm{LFP}\mathchar 45\relax\mathrm{IP}.

We would like to give examples showing that the first two implications above are strict. To do this, we will employ countably categorical first-order theories with the finite model property.

A theory is
countably categorical
if it
has only one countable model up to isomorphism.
Various equivalent formulations of countable categoricity were proven in the 50’s and 60’s by Ryll-Nardzewski, Svenonius, and Engler. These established countable categoricity as a robust and important property of first-order theories. Fraïssé theory is an important source of examples of countably categorical theories: every Fraïssé limit in a finite relational language has a countably category complete theory. For more information and history on countable categoricity, see Chapter 7 of Hodges
[
Hod93
]
.

Though seemingly separate notions, proficiency and countable categoricity are intimately related: roughly speaking, non-proficiency is the “finite variable logic version” of countable categoricity. The Ryll-Nardzewski theorem
[
Hod93
, Theorem 7.3.1]
asserts that countable categoricity is equivalent to the finiteness of the set of complete
n
-types over
T
, for all
n
. Non-proficiency essentially weakens this condition to the finiteness of the set of all
n
-types
in
m
-variable logic
, for all
n
and
m\geq n

[
DLW96
, Theorem 23]
. We now prove more carefully that countable categoricity implies non-proficiency, for first-order theories.

Lemma 26
.

Every countably categorical first-order theory is non-proficient.

Proof 3.2
.

Suppose that
T
is countably categorical, fix a basic operative formula
\varphi(x,S)
, and consider the first-order formulas
\varphi^{n}(x)
defining its stages. By the Ryll-Nardzewski theorem, there are only finitely many pairwise non-
T
-equivalent formulas with free variables from
x
. Thus there must be some
m\in\mathbb{N}
and
n<m
such that
T\models(\forall x)(\varphi^{n}(x)\leftrightarrow\varphi^{m}(x))
. Since, for all
j
,
T\models(\forall x)(\varphi^{j}(x)\rightarrow\varphi^{j+1}(x))
, it must be the case that
T\models(\forall x)(\varphi^{m}(x)\leftrightarrow\varphi^{m+1}(x))
, and
\|\varphi\|_{\mathbf{M}}\leq m
for all models
\mathbf{M}\models T
.

Since
\varphi
was chosen arbitrarily,
T
is non-proficient.

Remark 27
.

In particular, if
T
is a countable and countably categorical theory with the finite model property, then there is some family of finite structures
\mathcal{C}
with limit theory
T
, by Lemma
5
. Since
T
is countably categorical, it is non-proficient, and hence so is
\mathcal{C}
, by Lemma
9
. Therefore, FO = LFP over
\mathcal{C}
. In addition,
\mathcal{C}
inherits any property FO-
P
(or its negation) from
T
itself, by Lemma
7
.

To complete the classification, we show that both of the one-way implications in Corollary
25
are strict.

Theorem 28
.

There exists a class of finite structures
with
\mathrm{LFP}\mathchar 45\relax\mathrm{IP}
but without
\mathrm{LFP}\mathchar 45\relax\mathrm{TP}_{2}
and a class of finite structures
with
\mathrm{LFP}\mathchar 45\relax\mathrm{TP}_{2}
but without
\mathrm{LFP}\mathchar 45\relax\mathrm{SOP}
.

Proof 3.3
.

To exhibit a class of structures with a certain combination of LFP-properties, it suffices to exhibit a class of structures with the same combination of FO-properties, over which FO = LFP. By Lemma
26
and Remark
27
, it suffices to exhibit a countable, complete, and countably categorical theory with the finite model property, with the same combination of FO-properties.
This is exactly what we do. See Section
1.4
for definitions of our example theories. In this proof, we drop the prefix
\mathrm{FO}\mathchar 45\relax
.

For
\mathrm{IP}
but
\mathrm{NTP}_{2}
, consider
T_{\mathrm{RG}}
, the theory of the random
graph. This is well-known to be countably categorical with the finite
model property, to have
\mathrm{IP}
, and to be simple; a first-order theory
is simple if it does not have the tree property (
\mathrm{TP}
), which implies
that it does not have
\mathrm{TP}_{2}
.

For
\mathrm{TP}_{2}
but
\mathrm{NSOP}
, consider
T_{\mathrm{feq}}
, the generic theory
of parameterized equivalence relations. For discussions of this theory,
see
[
CR16
]
and
[
Kru19
]
. In
[
CR16
]
, Chernikov and Ramsey
establish (Corollary 6.20) that
T_{\mathrm{feq}}
does not have the property

\mathrm{SOP}_{1}
, which implies that it does not have
\mathrm{SOP}
, and (Corollary 6.18)
that
T_{\mathrm{feq}}
is not simple by witnessing
\mathrm{TP}_{2}
directly.
A proof that
T_{\mathrm{feq}}
has the finite model property is
given in
[
Kru19
]
.

4.
Further work

Our results suggest that it may be fruitful to examine
\mathrm{SOP}
,
\mathrm{TP}_{2}
,
\mathrm{IP}
, and
\mathrm{OP}
beyond the first-order context. In particular, it would be interesting to examine the extend to which weaker fixed-point logics (like transitive closure logic) recover
\mathrm{TP}_{2}
and
\mathrm{IP}
from
\mathrm{SOP}
. Another direction is a program to recover complexity-theoretic tameness properties of families of finite structures (like fast formula evaluation) from model-theoretic tameness assumptions, generalizing assumptions like bounded treewidth and cliquewidth.

In the spirit of classification theory, we might hope to deduce some positive concrete information about, e.g.,
\mathrm{LFP}\mathchar 45\relax\mathrm{NOP}
classes of finite structures that distinguish them from the merely stable (
\mathrm{FO}\mathchar 45\relax\mathrm{NOP}
) classes. One might hope to develop some kind of asymptotic structure theory (like Shelah’s classification of models of certain stable theories) for finite classes which are stable and non-proficient.

Finally, we believe that the observation that
\mathrm{FO}=\mathrm{LFP}
is a property of the elementary limit theory of a class of finite structures strongly suggests a model-theoretic approach to difficult questions like the ordered conjecture. At the very least, it gives us a new set of powerful tools to test and clarify where, exactly, the difficulty lies.
