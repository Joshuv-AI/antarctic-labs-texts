Neutron Star Crust Can Support A Large Ellipticity
J.A. Morales, C.J. Horowitz

Abstract
Non-axisymmetrical deformations of the crust on rapidly rotating neutron stars are one of the main targets of searches for continuous gravitational waves. The maximum ellipticity, or fractional difference in moments of inertia, that can be supported by deformations of the crust (known as “mountains”) provides an important upper limit on the strength of these continuous gravitational wave sources. We use the formalism developed by Gittins and Andersson, along with a deforming force that acts mainly in the transverse direction, to obtain a maximum ellipticity of 7.4 × \times 10 -6 . This is larger than the original results that Gittins and Andersson obtained but consistent with earlier calculations by Ushomirsky, Cutler and Bildsten. This suggests that rotating neutron stars could be strong sources of continuous gravitational waves.

Mechanisms to produce NS mountains may be complex and uncertain. Therefore an important first step is to calculate the maximum mountain that the crust can support. This maximum NS mountain is useful for two reasons.

First it provides a bound on the possible strength of CGW sources that is similar to limits from energy conservation or spin down. For example, there have been many searches for CGWs from the Crab pulsar
Abbott
et al. 2019e
;
Abbott et al. 2022
. These searches are promising because the Crab is young and energetic. Searches have been able to significantly beat the spin down limit so it is now know that less than 0.02 % of the spin down power of the Crab is going into CGWs. However, the Crab spins relatively slowly (about 30 Hz). Therefore, it takes a large deformation of the crust in order to produce detectable CGWs. Improved searches may soon beat the maximum mountain limit (i.e., set an observational upper limit on the ellipticity that is smaller than the maximum ellipticity the crust can support). When this is done, CGW searches will be directly probing the shape of the crust of the Crab and setting realistic limits on its deformation.

In addition, calculating the maximum mountain provides insight into mechanisms that may produce large deformations. For example, the crust is strongest near its base at high densities. This region gives a large contribution to the maximum mountain and suggests that mechanisms that build large mountains likely involve the high density inner crust.

Ushomirsky
et al. 2000
have calculated the maximum mountain in an intuitive formalism. They assume the crust can be strained near its breaking strain everywhere and write the maximum deformation the crust can support as a simple integral of the crust breaking stress divided by the local gravitational acceleration. This yields a maximum ellipticitiy (fractional difference in moments of inertia) of 2.8
\times
10
-6
for a 1.4 M
⊙
, 10 km radius NS (see Sec.
III.4
and Table
1

Recently,

under the influence of a force
. Their three chosen forces suggested that the maximum elastic deformation that a NS crust can support is approximately
10

et al. 2000

et al. 2021

In general, an applied force is likely to first break the crust near the surface where the density is low and the crust is very weak. If one limits the force to never break the crust anywhere, this early crust breaking can greatly constrain the strength of the applied force and prevent achieving a large ellipticity. In this paper we avoid breaking the weak outer crust by carefully choosing both the radial and angular form of the applied force. This allows us to use stronger forces and achieve much larger ellipticities. Our maximum ellipticity of 7.4
\times
10
-6

II
The Unperturbed Star

We consider a non-rotating NS with spherical symmetry, a mass of
1.4

M_{\odot}
and a radius of
10
km, whose matter is in the form of a cold perfect fluid, with mass density
\rho

p(\rho)=K\rho^{1+1/n}

(1)

as our equation of state, with

, because it describes well the matter inside massive NSs and it is the equation of state that

We suppose that the outer
\sim

1

km

\mu(\rho)=\kappa\rho

where
\kappa=10^{16}

cm^{2}s^{-2}

et al. 2006
. This form of the shear modulus facilitates a comparison between our results and

Kadau 2009
. This is the von-Mises stress criterion applied to the crust of NSs. The breaking strain
\bar{\sigma}_{br}=0.1
was obtained using MD simulations, and is the breaking strain that
Gittins
et al. 2021
used in their maximum ellipticity calculations. We assume that the core-crust transition density that marks the base of the crust is
\rho_{base}
= 2
\times
10
14
g/cm
3
. This was the density that both
Ushomirsky
et al. 2000
and
Gittins
et al. 2021
used for the base of the crust.
Gittins
et al. 2021
considered a background NS with a thin fluid ocean between the top of the crust and the surface. They assumed that the crust-ocean transition density that marks the top of the crust is
\rho_{top}
= 10
6
g/cm
3
. For numerical convenience, we assume that the crust-ocean interface occurs when the density is
\rho_{top}
= 2.18
\times
10
8
g/cm
3
. Calculations with
10^{7}
g/cm
3

\leq\rho_{top}\leq 10^{11}
g/cm
3
give similar results. We denote the radius of the bottom interface by
R_{1}
and the radius of the top interface by

The Newtonian equations that describe the structure of our solidified background star in equilibrium are the following:

m^{\prime}=4\pi r^{2}\rho,

(3)

p^{\prime}=-\rho\Phi^{\prime},

(4)

\Phi^{\prime}=\frac{Gm}{r^{2}}\,.

(5)

In these equations,

Along with the equation of state (

III
The Perturbed Star

When the star deforms and deviates from perfect sphericity, several mass multipole moments are induced. These moments are related to perturbations in density of the form

\delta\rho_{tot}(\vec{r})=\sum_{l,m}\delta\rho_{lm}(r)Y_{lm}(\theta,\phi)

(6)

where
(l,m)
denotes the mode of the multipole.
Each moment is defined as

Q_{lm}\equiv\int_{0}^{R}\delta\rho_{lm}(r)r^{l+2}dr

(7)

where
R
is the stellar radius. The (
l
,
m
)=(
2
,
2
) mode is the dominant contribution to the CGW signal that a NS mountain emits. Therefore, from now on, we suppose that this is the only moment that the perturbations on the star have. Consequently, we set
l=m=2
and let
\delta\rho(r)\equiv\delta\rho_{22}(r)
. The ellipticity
\epsilon
is the fractional difference in moments of inertia,

\epsilon=\frac{I_{xx}-I_{yy}}{I_{zz}}=\sqrt{\frac{8\pi}{15}}\frac{Q_{22}}{I_{0}}

(8)

where
I_{zz}=I_{0}=10^{45}
g cm
2
is the moment of inertia with respect to the axis of rotation of the background star.
Gittins
et al. 2021
chose this value of
I_{0}
to facilitate their comparison of results with observational papers, and it is the one we will use for our calculations as well.

Inspired by these definitions, we will denote any
(l,m)=(2,2)
first-order perturbation
G
by

G_{tot}(\vec{r})=G(r)Y_{lm}(\theta,\phi)\,.

(9)

III.1
Fluid Perturbations

For a fluid within the star, it is important to consider both the Euler equation

\nabla_{i}p+\rho\nabla_{i}\Phi=0

(10)

and the Poisson equation

\nabla^{2}\Phi=4\pi G\rho\,.

(11)

Variations on (
10
) give the perturbed Euler equation,

\nabla_{i}\delta p_{tot}+\delta\rho_{tot}\nabla_{i}\Phi+\rho\nabla_{i}\delta\Phi_{tot}=0\,.

(12)

Now, suppose that an
(l,m)=(2,2)
force density of the form

f_{i}=f_{r}(r)Y_{lm}\nabla_{i}r+f_{\perp}(r)r\nabla_{i}Y_{lm}

(13)

acts on the fluid as a first-order perturbation. Then, the perturbed Euler equation (
12
) becomes

\nabla_{i}\delta p_{tot}+\delta\rho_{tot}\nabla_{i}\Phi+\rho\nabla_{i}\delta\Phi_{tot}-f_{i}=0\,.

(14)

Also, it is important to consider variations to the Poisson equation (
11
), which give

\nabla^{2}\delta\Phi_{tot}=4\pi G\delta\rho_{tot}\,.

(15)

The perturbed Poisson equation (
15
) gives

\delta\Phi^{\prime\prime}+\frac{2}{r}\delta\Phi^{\prime}-\frac{\beta^{2}}{r^{2}}\delta\Phi=4\pi G\delta\rho

(16)

where
\beta^{2}\equiv l(l+1)
. The angular component of (
14
) gives

\delta\rho=-\frac{1}{c_{s}^{2}}(\rho\delta\Phi-f_{\perp}r)\,.

(17)

If the fluid region in consideration contains the center of the star, we require that

\delta\Phi(0)=0

(18)

as we want
\delta\Phi
to be regular at the center. On the other hand, if the fluid region in consideration contains the surface of the star (
r=R
), then

\delta\Phi^{\prime}(R)+\frac{l+1}{R}\delta\Phi(R)=0\,.

(19)

III.2
Crustal Perturbations

Strain can build up within the solid crust. The addition of strain in the crust leads to the elastic Euler equation

\nabla_{i}p+\rho\nabla_{i}\Phi-\nabla_{j}t^{ij}=0

(20)

where we suppose that the additional term is a first-order perturbation describing the elastic forces. Therefore, by varying the elastic Euler equation (
20
), we obtain

\nabla_{i}\delta p_{tot}+\delta\rho_{tot}\nabla_{i}\Phi+\rho\nabla_{i}\delta\Phi_{tot}-\nabla^{j}t_{ij}=0\,.

(21)

In the presence of the perturbed force density (
13
), the perturbed elastic Euler equation (
21
) becomes

\nabla_{i}\delta p_{tot}+\delta\rho_{tot}\nabla_{i}\Phi+\rho\nabla_{i}\delta\Phi_{tot}-\nabla^{j}t_{ij}-f_{i}=0\,.

(22)

In the last equations,
t_{ij}
is the shear stress tensor, which is given by

t_{ij}=\mu\left(\nabla_{i}\xi_{j}+\nabla_{j}\xi_{i}-\frac{2}{3}g_{ij}\nabla_{k}\xi^{k}\right)\,.

(23)

\vec{\xi}
is the displacement vector that is suitable for polar perturbations
Ushomirsky
et al. 2000
, and it is given by the expression

\xi^{i}=\xi_{r}(r)Y_{lm}\nabla^{i}r+\frac{r}{\beta}\xi_{\perp}(r)\nabla^{i}Y_{lm}\,.

(24)

To make the application of the boundary conditions easier, we can identify the traction vector

T^{i}=(\delta p_{tot}\delta^{ij}-t^{ij})\nabla_{j}r\,.

(25)

Gittins
et al. 2021
wrote the traction vector in a form that is even more convenient than that given by (
25
):

T^{i}=[\delta p-T_{1}]Y_{lm}\nabla^{i}r-rT_{2}\nabla^{i}Y_{lm}\,.

(26)

The radial and tangential components of the perturbed elastic Euler equation (
22
), the traction vector (
26
), the displacement vector (
24
), and the perturbed Poisson equation (
15
) give the following set of coupled ordinary differential equations (ODEs)
Gittins
et al. 2021
:

\xi_{r}^{\prime}=\frac{1}{r}\xi_{r}-\frac{\beta}{2r}\xi_{\perp}+\frac{3}{4\mu}T_{1},

(27)

\xi_{\perp}^{\prime}=-\frac{\beta}{r}\xi_{r}+\frac{1}{r}\xi_{\perp}+\frac{\beta}{\mu}T_{2},

(28)

\left(1+\frac{3c_{s}^{2}\rho}{4\mu}\right)T_{1}^{\prime}=\rho\delta\Phi^{\prime}-f_{r}

-\left[(c_{s}^{2})^{\prime}(3\rho+r\rho^{\prime})+c_{s}^{2}\left(\frac{3\beta^{2}\rho}{2r}+\rho^{\prime}-\frac{r\rho^{\prime 2}}{\rho}+r\rho^{\prime\prime}\right)\right]\frac{1}{r}\xi_{r}\\

+\left[(c_{s}^{2})^{\prime}3\rho+c_{s}^{2}\left(\frac{3\rho}{r}+\rho^{\prime}\right)\right]\frac{\beta}{2r}\xi_{\perp}\\

-\left[\frac{3}{r}+(c_{s}^{2})^{\prime}\frac{3\rho}{4\mu}+c_{s}^{2}\left(\frac{3\rho}{r}-\frac{\rho\mu^{\prime}}{\mu}+\rho^{\prime}\right)\frac{3}{4\mu}\right]T_{1}\\

+\left(1+\frac{3c_{s}^{2}\rho}{2\mu}\right)\frac{\beta^{2}}{r}T_{2},\\

(29)

T_{2}^{\prime}=\frac{\rho}{r}\delta\Phi-f_{\perp}-c_{s}^{2}(3\rho+r\rho^{\prime})\frac{1}{r^{2}}\xi_{r}\\

+\left[\frac{3c_{s}^{2}\rho}{2}+\left(1-\frac{2}{\beta^{2}}\right)\mu\right]\frac{\beta}{r^{2}}\xi_{\perp}+\left(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{3c_{s}^{2}\rho}{4\mu}\right)\frac{1}{r}T_{1}-\frac{3}{r}T_{2},

(30)

\delta\Phi^{\prime\prime}+\frac{2}{r}\delta\Phi^{\prime}-\frac{\beta^{2}}{r^{2}}\delta\Phi=4\pi G\delta\rho

(31)

where

\delta\rho=-\left(\frac{3\rho}{r}+\rho^{\prime}\right)\xi_{r}+\frac{3\beta\rho}{2r}\xi_{\perp}-\frac{3\rho}{4\mu}T_{1}\,.

(32)

The boundary conditions at either the core-crust transition (
r=R_{1}
) or the crust-ocean transition (
r=R_{2}
) are the continuity of both the radial and tangential components of the traction (
26
). These give:

T_{2E}=0,

(33)

\rho\delta\Phi_{F}-f_{\perp F}r=\left(1+\frac{3c_{s}^{2}\rho}{4\mu}\right)T_{1E}

+c_{s}^{2}\left[\left(\frac{3\rho}{r}+\rho^{\prime}\right)\xi_{rE}-\frac{3\beta\rho}{2r}\xi_{\perp E}\right]\,.\\

(34)

In (
33
) and (
34
), the subscripts
F
and
E
refer to the fluid region and the elastic region, respectively. On the surface of the star (
r=R
) the boundary condition is the same as (
19
). We assume the density is smooth, so we require the perturbed gravitational potential
\delta\Phi
and and its radial derivative
\delta\Phi^{\prime}
to be continuous at the solid-liquid interfaces. Furthermore, the radial displacement
\xi_{r}
must be continuous at these interfaces. The same is not necessarily true for tangential displacement
\xi_{\perp}
.

III.3
Gittins et al. calculations

Gittins
et al. 2021
considered background stars similar to those described in Sec.
II
. The background star they considered was a completely fluid star. We’ll call it the
fluid star
. The second background star they considered consisted of a fluid core between
0

R_{1}
, a solid crust between
R_{1}
and
R_{2}
, and a fluid ocean between
R_{2}
and

. When both of these background star models were perturbed by the force (

To get the maximum ellipticity that a NS crust can support, they considered two maximum ellipticies: the maximum ellipticity of a deformed fluid-solid star and the maximum ellipticity of a deformed fluid star.
Gittins
et al. 2021
first considered a fluid-solid star. Both the fluid regions and the crust were deformed non-spherically. They varied the amplitude of the force perturbations [finding at the same time the corresponding solutions (

,

,

,

,

,

\bar{\sigma}\geq\bar{\sigma}_{br}=0.1

Gittins
et al. 2021
;

Kadau 2009

Gittins
et al. 2021
found that the strain at a single point

is given by

\bar{\sigma}^{2}=\frac{5}{256\pi}\bigg\{6\text{sin}^{2}\theta\bigg[3\text{sin}^{2}\theta\text{cos}^{2}2\phi\bigg(\frac{T_{1}}{\mu}\bigg)^{2}\\

+4(3+\text{cos}2\theta-2\text{sin}^{2}\theta\text{cos}4\phi)\bigg(\frac{T_{2}}{\mu}\bigg)^{2}\bigg]\\

+(35+28\text{cos}2\theta+\text{cos}4\theta+8\text{sin}^{4}\theta\text{cos}4\phi)\left(\frac{\xi_{\perp}}{r}\right)^{2}\bigg\}

(35)

This procedure gave them the maximum ellipticity
\epsilon_{s}
. Then, they considered a completely fluid star and deformed it using the same perturbed force density amplitude that broke one point of the crust of the fluid-solid star, found the corresponding solution (

) and calculated its maximum ellipticity

To scrutinize
Gittins
et al. 2021
maximum ellipticity calculation method for an explicit force, we used the following two numerical methods:

Method 1
: If the force (
13
) could be written as the gradient of a potential
\chi_{tot}
,

f_{i}=-\rho\nabla_{i}\chi_{tot}

(36)

we grouped together
\delta\Phi_{tot}
and
\chi_{tot}
into the variable
U_{tot}=\delta\Phi_{tot}+\chi_{tot}
(as
Gittins
et al. 2021
did) so that the ODEs were homogeneous. For instance,
Gittins
et al. 2021
choose
\chi_{tot}=Ar^{2}Y_{lm}
(see equation (
40
)). Then, we generated

arbitrary linearly independent solutions with a fourth-order Runge-Kutta algorithm and found a linear combination whose

for the fluid-solid star and

Method 2
: If the equations couldn’t be written in a homogeneous form, we integrated the equations with a fourth-order Runge-Kutta algorithm that operated within a globally convergent Newton method (GCNM)

. The GCNM ensured that all of the boundary conditions of both the fluid-solid star and the fluid star were satisfied.

III.4
Ushomirsky et al. calculations

Ushomirsky
et al. 2000
performed the first estimate of the maximum ellipticity that a NS crust can support. They used Newtonian gravity and the Cowling approximation, where the perturbed gravitational potential
\delta\Phi=0
. The fluid parts were in equilibirum and undeformed with respect to the background star. On the other hand, the crust was deformed with respect to the background star, with deformations that could be described by (
21
) [with
\delta\Phi=0
]. Since the only part that was deformed was the crust, the crust was the only part of the star that contributed to the quadrupole moment integral (
7
). To perform the integral in the crustal region, they assumed that all of the points of the crust were maximally strained, according to the von-Miss stress criterion. Furthermore, they imposed the vanishing of the shear stresses below and above the crust. They found that a NS crust can sustain a maximum quadrupole moment

Q_{22}^{max}\approx\gamma\bar{\sigma}_{br}I

(37)

where
\gamma\equiv 6\left[\sqrt{\frac{32\pi}{15}}+\sqrt{\frac{96\pi}{5}}\right]\approx 62.13
is a numerical pre-factor,
g(r)=Gm(r)/r^{2}
is the local gravitational acceleration, and

I\equiv\int_{R_{1}}^{R_{2}}\frac{\mu r^{3}}{g}dr\,.

(38)

Note that this formula for the maximum quadrupole moment only includes shear stresses on the crust. With the equation of state (
1
) and the shear modulus (
2
), we found that (
37
) reduces to

Q_{22}^{max}\approx 2.2\times 10^{39}\left(\frac{0.1}{\bar{\sigma}_{br}}\right)g\ cm^{2}\,,

(39)

for a canonical NS with mass of 1.4 M
⊙
and radius of 10 km.

However, one can make better choices for the force
f_{i}
that yield much larger ellipticities. We expect
f_{i}
that generates a large ellipticitiy satisfies two requirements. First,
f_{i}
should strain most the region near the base of the crust because the calculation of
Ushomirsky
et al. 2000
reveals that the regions near the base of the crust contribute more to the maximum ellipticity when all the crust is maximally strained (see Figures
5
). Also,
f_{i}
should act primarily in the angular or transverse direction. If a transverse force helps generate a mountain, one can take full advantage of the shear modulus and breaking strain. On the other hand, a radial force will compete with the large incompressibility of the fluid part of the star. As a result elastic forces will only move the mountain a very small radial distance because these forces are quickly counter balanced by a large pressure from a tiny change in the volume of the fluid.

One might think that force that satisfies these two requirements is one that is proportional to the background density (and, therefore, proportional to the shear modulus within the crust), and acts mainly in the angular direction. Such a force is given by

f_{i}=B\rho r\nabla_{i}Y_{lm}\,.

(41)

Nonetheless, this force breaks the crust at the top, leaving most of the crust far from being maximally strained, as shown in Figures
3
and
4
. Therefore, we introduce the force

f_{i}=B\rho r\nabla_{i}Y_{lm}+C\Theta(r-r_{c})\rho(r-r_{c})^{2}\nabla_{i}rY_{lm}\,.

(42)

Here
B
is a constant that is adjusted until the crust breaks at one point, and
r_{c}=9.99
km. The constant
C
is chosen so that we are able to satisfy the additional constraint

T_{1E}(R_{2})=0\,.

(43)

This constraint prevents the crust breaking at its very top and allows a larger
B
and ellipticity. Note that the extra radial piece in the force (
42
) is turned on smoothly to ensure the density is smooth.

V
Discussion

The keys to obtain a large maximum ellipticity are to first avoid breaking the very weak, low density outer crust and second to strain near its breaking strain the strong, high density inner crust mainly in the angular direction. This allows us to obtain a maximum ellipticity over
10^{-6}
with either the
Gittins
et al. 2021
or
Ushomirsky
et al. 2000
formalism. Although it is true that
Ushomirsky
et al. 2000
does not explicitly satisfy boundary conditions at the crust-core interface, it is easy to imagine a small modification of their formalism that will. If in a small region near the interface one relaxes the assumption that all of the crust is strained to the breaking strain, one may be able to adjust the strain to satisfy the boundary conditions.

In a second paper,
Gittins &
Andersson 2021
find even smaller maximum ellipticities when they use a shear modulus that is even smaller for the weak outer crust. This result may be misleading. For the limited range of forces that they consider they are not able to strain the inner crust significantly without first breaking the very weak outer crust. With better choices for the form of the force they may be able to produce larger ellipticities. We have not included relativistic effects in this paper. Relativity was found to only modestly reduce the maximum ellipticitiy compared to full nonrelativistic calculations which do not make the Cowling approximation
Johnson-McDaniel &
Owen 2013
.

Our large maximum ellipticity provides a useful upper limit to the strength of GWs from rotating NSs. For example the Crab pulsar was formed in a supernova (SN) only one thousand years ago. NS kick velocities indicate that supernovae are energetic and very likely asymmetric at the few percent level. Some of this energy and asymmetry could have been stored in crustal deformations. One mechanism to deform the crust is fall back from the SN. This could have partially melted the newly formed crust on one side and left the NS with a large ellipticity
Janka
et al. 2022
. Unfortunately, the SN involves complex physics and is difficult to simulate in full detail. Nevertheless, the maximum ellipticity the crust can support still provides a useful upper limit to how large any possible birth deformations can be. Present GW limits for the Crab are not quite at but near this upper limit. As these searches improve and “beat” the crust deformation limit their discovery potential may grow and they will directly set realistic limits on the shape of the Crab pulsar.

VI
Conclusions

The maximum ellipticity that the NS crust can support provides an important limit on the strength of CGWs from rotating NSs. In this work, we have used the
Gittins
et al. 2021
formalism with an improved deforming force (
42
) to strain the crust mainly in the angular direction in such a way as to produce a maximum ellipticity of
7.4\times 10^{-6}
. This is larger than the results that
Gittins
et al. 2021
obtained for three simple forces but consistent with the earlier estimate of
Ushomirsky
et al. 2000
. This is a promising result for CGW searches because it suggests that rotating NSs could be strong CGW sources.
