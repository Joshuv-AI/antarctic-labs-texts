Veldkamp Quadrangles and Polar Spaces
Bernhard Mühlherr and Richard M. Weiss

Abstract
. Veldkamp polygons are certain graphs Γ = ( V , E ) \Gamma=(V,E) such that for each v ∈ V v\in V , Γ v \Gamma_{v} is endowed with a symmetric anti-reflexive relation ≡ v \equiv_{v} . These relations are all trivial if and only if Γ \Gamma is a thick generalized polygon. A Veldkamp polygon is called flat if no two vertices have the same set of vertices that are opposite in a natural sense. We explore the connection between Veldkamp quadrangles and polar spaces. Using this connection, we give the complete classification of flat Veldkamp quadrangles in which some but not all of the relations ≡ v \equiv_{v} are trivial.

1.
Introduction

In
2.8
, we introduce the notion of a Veldkamp polygon. A Veldkamp polygon
is a graph
\Gamma=(V,E)
such that for each vertex
v\in V
, the set
\Gamma_{v}
of vertices
adjacent to
v
is endowed with a symmetric, anti-reflexive relation
\equiv_{v}
satisfying
several axioms. The relations
\equiv_{v}
are called the
local opposition relations

of
\Gamma
. If
(u,v,w)
is a path of length
2
and
u\equiv_{v}w
, we say that
the path
(u,v,w)
is
straight
.
An arbitrary path is straight if every subpath of length
2
is straight. Thus every path is
straight if and only if the local opposition relations
\equiv_{v}
are all trivial
(in the sense that
u\equiv_{v}w
for all distinct
u,w\in\Gamma_{v}
).
This is the case if and only if
\Gamma
is a thick generalized polygon.

The thick generalized polygons that arise in the theory of algebraic groups all satisfy
the Moufang condition. This is a transitivity condition on the root groups. There
are natural extensions of the notion
of a root group and the Moufang condition to Veldkamp polygons. A Veldkamp polgon
satisfying this Moufang condition is the same thing as a Tits polygon, a notion
introduced in
[
3
]
.

There is a standard way to construct a Tits polygon from an irreducible spherical building of
rank at least
3
and a
Tits index that is compatible in a suitable sense.
Every irreducible spherical building of rank at least
3
has at least one
compatible Tits index. Thus the theory of Tits polygons allows us, in particular,
to study an arbitrary spherical building as a rank
2
structure on which the methods
in
[
12
]
can be brought to bear (as in
[
3
]
–
[
9
]
).
This was our original reason for
introducing Tits polygons. In
[
4
]
, we also showed that there is an equivalence between
Tits polygons and a certain class of root graded groups.

In
[
15
, Def. 3.1]
, Veldkamp introduced the notion of a Veldkamp plane. (Veldkamp
called them Barbilian planes.)
This notion arose in his work on projective geometry over rings.
In the same paper Veldkamp showed the importance of the notion of
the stable rank of a ring (a notion he found in
K
-theory) in ring-geometry,
a subject first studied by Corrado Segre early in the 20th century (see
[
16
]
).
Veldkamp was able to show that many of the basic results of projective
geometry, with suitable modifications, hold over rings of stable rank
2
. In particular, he showed that
the geometries that arise from a free module of rank
3
over an associative ring of stable
rank
2
and Veldkamp planes satisfying something slightly stronger than the Moufang condition
(see
[
15
, Def. 4.16]
) are essentially the same. This result was subsequently
strengthened first by Faulkner in
[
2
, Thm 9]
and then by
ourselves in
[
5
, Thms. 7.2 and 7.3]
.

A Veldkamp plane as defined by Veldkamp is the same thing as a
Veldkamp
n
-gon as defined in
2.8
with
n=3
,
where the local opposition relations play the role of the relation

\not\approx
in
[
15
, Def. 3.1]
.
Thus our notion of a Veldkamp polygon is a natural generalization of the notion of a
Veldkamp plane.

We say that
a Veldkamp polygon is
green
if some (or all) of its local opposition relations are trivial,
and we call a Veldkamp polygon
flat
if no two vertices have the same set of vertices
that are opposite as defined in
2.9
.
All generalized polygons are both green and flat.

In this paper, the focus is on Veldkamp quadrangles (i.e. a Veldkamp
n
-gon with
n=4
).
We prove four theorems.
In our first theorem (see
4.23
,
4.26
and
4.30
),
we show that every green Veldkamp quadrangle
\Gamma
has a canonical
flat quotient
\bar{\Gamma}
(which is also a green Veldkamp quadrangle) and a natural
surjective homomorphism

\pi
from
\Gamma
to
\bar{\Gamma}
.

In our second theorem (see
5.18
and
5.22
),
we show that there is a one-to-one correspondence (up to isomorphism) between
flat green Veldkamp quadrangles and non-degenerate polar spaces with thick lines.

The notion of a polar space
was also introduced by Veldkamp. Polar spaces of rank
2
are generalized quadrangles.
Non-degenerate polar spaces of finite rank at least
3
and characteristic
different from
2
were classified by Veldkamp in
[
14
]
.
This result was extended to arbitrary characteristic
by Tits in
[
11
]
. Several people contributed to the extension of Tits’ result
to include polar spaces of infinite rank. We cite
[
10
]
for this part of the
classification.

In our third theorem (see
6.16
),
we use the classification of non-degenerate polar spaces of rank at least
3

to show that every flat green Veldkamp quadrangle is either a generalized quadrangle or
a Tits quadrangle obtained from a quadratic space or a pseudo-quadratic space (which we
interpret to include the symplectic case) or a Tits quadrangle of type
D_{3}
or type
E_{7}

as defined in
6.13
and
6.15
.
In particular, a flat green Veldkamp quadrangle that is not a generalized quadrangle always
satisfies the Moufang condition.

There are, of course, also many generalized quadrangles
that do not satisfy the Moufang condition.
Generalized quadrangles that do satisfy the Moufang condition were classified in
[
12
, 17.4]
.
There are six families. Three of them have a natural description in terms of polar spaces,
but three of them do not; see
6.17
.

In our fourth theorem (see
6.5
and
6.12
),
we show that to every non-degenerate quadratic space and
every non-degenerate pseudo-quadratic space (with infinite Witt index allowed),
there does, in fact, exist a corresponding flat green Veldkamp quadrangle.

a,b
in a group and we apply permutations from left to right.

2.
Veldkamp polygons

In this section we introduce the notion of a Veldkamp polygon. In
2.15
,
we describe the connection between Veldkamp polygons and generalized polygons.

Definition 2.1
.

An
opposition relation
on a set
X
is a relation on
X

that is symmetric and anti-reflexive. Let
\equiv
be an opposition relation on
X
.
We say that two elements of
X
are
opposite
if
x\equiv y
.
We say that
\equiv
is
trivial
if every two distinct elements of
X
are opposite.

Definition 2.2
.

Let
\equiv
be an opposition relation on a set
X
. For each
k\geq 1
,
we say that
\equiv
is
k
-plump
if for every subset
S
of
X

such that
|S|\leq k
, there exists an element of
X
that is opposite
every element of
S
. Note that if
\equiv
is

k
-plump for some
k\geq 1
, then it is also
(k-1)
-plump and
|X|\geq k+1
.

Notation 2.3
.

By
graph
we mean a pair
(V,E)
consisting of a
set
V
and a set
E
of
2
-element subsets of
V
. The elements of

V
and
E
are called
vertices
and
edges
. For each
v\in V
,
we let
\Gamma_{v}
denote the set of
neighbors
of
v
. In other words,

\Gamma_{v}=\{u\in V\mid\{u,v\}\in E\}.

Let
\Gamma=(V,E)
be a graph. A
subgraph
of
\Gamma
is a pair
(V_{1},E_{1})
,
where
V_{1}
is a non-empty subset of
V
and
E_{1}
is a subset of
\{e\in E\mid e\subset V_{1}\}
.
For each non-empty subset
V_{1}
of
V
, the
subgraph of
\Gamma

spanned by
V_{1}
is the subgraph
(V_{1},E_{1})
, where

E_{1}=\{e\in E\mid e\subset V_{1}\}
.
An
s
-path
in
\Gamma
is a sequence
(x_{0},x_{1},\dots,x_{s})

of
s+1
vertices
x_{i}
for
some
s\geq 0
such that
x_{i-1}\in\Gamma_{x_{i}}
for all
i\in[1,s]
.
and
x_{i-2}\neq x_{i}
for all
i\in[2,s]
. (Here
[i,j]
denotes the closed interval
i,i+1,\ldots,j

if
i\leq j
and
[i,j]=\emptyset
if
i>j
.) A
path
is an
s
-path for some

s\geq 0
; the parameter
s
is called the
length
of the path.
The graph
\Gamma
is
connected
if there is a path from any given vertex
to any other given vertex. The distance
{\rm dist}(x,y)
between two
vertices is the minimal length of a path from
x
to
y
(assuming that there is one).
For each
v\in V
, let

\Gamma_{m}(v)=\{u\in V\mid{\rm dist}(u,v)=m\}.

Thus
\Gamma_{1}(v)=\Gamma_{v}
for all
v\in V
.

Notation 2.4
.

An
s
-path is
closed
if its first and last vertices are the same and
s\geq 3
.
An
s
-circuit
is the subgraph with vertex set
\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{s}\}

and edge set
\big\{\{x_{i-1},x_{i}\}\mid i\in[1,s]\big\}
for some closed

s
-path
(x_{0},x_{1},\ldots,x_{s})
. A
circuit
is an
s
-circuit for some
s\geq 3
.

Notation 2.5
.

Let
\Gamma=(V,E)
be a graph endowed with an opposition relation
\equiv_{v}

on
\Gamma_{v}
for each
v\in V
.
We say that two vertices

x,y
are
opposite at
v
if
x,y\in\Gamma_{v}
and
x\equiv_{v}y
.
A path
(v_{0},v_{1},\ldots,v_{s})
is called
straight
if

v_{i-1}
is opposite
v_{i+1}
at
v_{i}
for all
i\in[1,s-1]
.
A circuit is
straight
if every path it contains is straight.
We say that
\Gamma
is
k
-plump
for some
k\geq 1
if all its local
opposition relations are
k
-plump as defined in
2.2
.

Definition 2.6
.

A
Veldkamp graph
is a graph
\Gamma=(V,E)

endowed with a
2
-plump opposition relation on
\Gamma_{v}
for each
v\in V
.

Remark 2.7
.

Since every Veldkamp graph is
2
-plump (and therefore also
1
-plump),
it follows that for every
s\geq 0
,
a straight
s
-path in a Veldkamp graph can be extended to a straight
(s+1)
-path.

Definition 2.8
.

Let
n\geq 2
. A
Veldkamp
n
-gon
is a Veldkamp graph
\Gamma

such that the following hold:

(VP1)

\Gamma
is connected and bipartite.

(VP2)

For each
k\in[1,n-1]
and each straight
k
-path
\alpha=(v_{0},\ldots,v_{k})
,

\alpha
is the unique straight path from
v_{0}
to
v_{k}
of length at most
k
.

(VP3)

Every straight
(n+1)
-path is contained in a straight
2n
-circuit.

Note that by (VP2), the straight
2n
-circuit in (VP3) is unique. A
Veldkamp polygon

is a Veldkamp
n
-gon for some
n\geq 2
.

Definition 2.9
.

Let
\Gamma=(V,E)
be a Veldkamp
n
-gon for some
n\geq 2
. A
root
in
\Gamma
is
a straight
n
-path. Two vertices
x
and
y

are
opposite
if there is a root from
x
to
y

and two edges
e,f
of
\Gamma
are
opposite
if there exists a straight
(n+1)
-path
whose first and last edges are
e
and
f
. By (VP2), neither a vertex nor an edge
can be opposite itself, so both of these relations are, in fact, opposition relations
(on
V
and on
E
). The set of vertices opposite
a given vertex
x
is denoted by
x^{\rm op}
and
\Gamma
is called
flat

if
x^{\rm op}=y^{\rm op}
for
x,y\in V
implies that
x=y
.

Suppose for the rest of this section that
\Gamma=(V,E)
is a Veldkamp
n
-gon for some
n\geq 2
.

Proposition 2.10
.

Suppose that
x,y\in V
are opposite and that
z\in\Gamma_{y}
. Then there exists a unique
root
(x,\ldots,z,y)
from
x
to
y
containing
z
.

Proof.
Uniqueness holds by (VP2). Since
x
and
y
are opposite, there is a root

(x,\ldots,w,y)
from
x
to
y
. Since
\Gamma
is
2
-plump,
we can choose
u\in\Gamma_{y}
opposite
w
and
z

at
y
. Thus
(x,\ldots,w,y,u)
is a straight
(n+1)
-path. By (VP3),
it follows that there exists a root
(x,\ldots,u,y)
from
x
to
y
that contains

u
. Then
(x,\ldots,u,y,z)
is a straight
(n+1)
-path and the claim follows by a second
application of (VP3).

\Box

Remark 2.11
.

Suppose that
u,v\in V
and
{\rm dist}(u,v)
is even. Since
\Gamma
is bipartite,
every vertex in
u^{\rm op}
is at even distance to every vertex in
v^{\rm op}

Proposition 2.12
.

For each pair
e_{1},e_{2}
of edges of
\Gamma
, there exists an
edge that is opposite both
e_{1}
and
e_{2}
.

Proof.
Let
(x_{0},\ldots,x_{k})
be a
k
-path in
\Gamma
for some
k\geq 1
and
let
c_{i}
denote the edge
\{x_{i-1},x_{i}\}
for each
i\in[1,k]
.
Since
\Gamma
is connected, it will suffice
to show that there exists an edge opposite both
c_{1}
and
c_{k}
.
We proceed by induction with respect to
k
. By
2.7
,
the claim holds for
k=1
. Suppose that
k\geq 2
and that

f
is an edge that is opposite
c_{1}
and
c_{k-1}
.
Let
y
be the unique vertex of

that is opposite
x_{k-1}
.
Since
\Gamma
is bipartite,

y
is opposite to a unique vertex on

. By
2.11
,
this vertex is at even distance from
x_{k-1}
. Thus
y
is opposite
x_{p}
,
where

if

Let
\alpha=(x_{k-1},x_{k},\ldots,y)

from
x_{k-1}
to

x_{k}
and let
u
be unique the element of
\Gamma_{y}
contained

. If

is odd, let

\beta=(x_{0},x_{1},\ldots,y)
be the unique root from
x_{0}
to

containing
x_{1}

and if

is even, let
\beta=(x_{1},x_{0},\ldots,y)
be the unique root from

to

.
In both cases, let

\Gamma_{y}

Since

\alpha

\Box

Corollary 2.13
.

Let
u,v
be vertices such that
{\rm dist}(u,v)
is even.
Then
u^{\rm op}\cap v^{\rm op}\neq\emptyset
.

Proof.
Let
e
be an edge containing
u
and let
f
be an edge containing
v
.
By
2.12
, there exists an edge
g
opposite both
e
and
f
.
Let
x
be the unique vertex of
g
that is opposite
u

and let
y
be the unique vertex of
g
that is opposite

.
By

,

\Box

Definition 2.14
.

We recall that a
generalized
n
-gon
for some
n\geq 2
is a bipartite graph
\Omega
such that
the following hold:

(i)

{\rm diam}(\Omega)=n
.

(ii)

\Omega
contains no circuits of length less than
2n
.

(iii)

|\Omega_{v}|\geq 2
for all vertices
v
.

A generalized
n
-gon
\Omega
is
thick
if
|\Omega_{v}|\geq 3
for all vertices
v
.

Proposition 2.15
.

If the local opposition relations are all trivial, then
\Gamma
is a thick generalized
n
-gon,
and every thick generalized
n
-gon, when endowed with the trivial local opposition relation
at every vertex, is a Veldkamp
n
-gon.

Proof.
Suppose that all local opposition relations of
\Gamma
are trivial. Then every path is straight.
By (VP1),
\Gamma
is connected. By (VP3), therefore,
{\rm diam(\Gamma)}\leq n
.
By (VP2),
\Gamma
contains no circuits
of length less than
2n
. This implies that
{\rm diam}(\Gamma)\geq n
.
Since
\Gamma
is
2
-plump, we have

|\Gamma_{x}|\geq 3
for all vertices
x
. It follows that
\Gamma
is a thick generalized
n
-gon.

Suppose, conversely, that
\Omega
is a thick generalized

n
-gon endowed with the trivial local opposition relation at every vertex,
so that every path is straight.
Since
\Omega
is thick, it is
2
-plump.
By
2.14
(ii),
\Omega
satisfies (VP2) and by
2.14
(i),
it follows that (VP1) and (VP3) hold. Thus
\Omega
is a Veldkamp
n
-gon.

\Box

Proposition 2.16
.

Suppose that
(x_{0},x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
and
(y_{0},y_{1},\ldots,y_{n})
are two roots
with
x_{0}=y_{0}
and
x_{n}=y_{n}
. Then
x_{1}
and
y_{1}
are opposite at
x_{0}

if and only if
x_{n-1}
and
y_{n-1}
are opposite at
x_{n}
.

Proof.
Suppose that
x_{n-1}
and
y_{n-1}
are opposite at
x_{n}
. Then

(x_{0},x_{1},\ldots,x_{n-1},x_{n},y_{n-1})

is a straight
(n+1)
-path.
By (VP3), there exists a root
\alpha:=(z_{0},z_{1},\ldots,z_{n})
such that

z_{0}=x_{0}
,
z_{n-1}=y_{n-1}
,
z_{n}=x_{n}
and
z_{1}
is opposite

at
x_{0}
.
By (VP2), it follows that
\alpha=(y_{0},y_{1},\ldots,y_{n})
. Hence

is opposite

at
x_{0}
.
The converse holds by symmetry.

\Box

Proposition 2.17
.

Suppose that
\equiv_{v}
is trivial for some
v\in V
.
Then
\equiv_{w}
is trivial for all
w

at even distance from

and if, in addition,

\equiv_{w}
is trivial

.

Proof.
Let
(x_{0},\ldots,x_{n})
be a root.
By

,
\equiv_{x_{0}}
is trivial if and only if
\equiv_{x_{n}}
is trivial.
Thus if

, then by

,

If

\Box

3.
Veldkamp quadrangles

In this section we focus on the case
n=4
. Let
\Gamma=(V,E)
be a Veldkamp quadrangle.
Since
\Gamma

is connected and bipartite, there is a unique decomposition of
V
into two subsets
such that every edge contains one vertex from each of them. We choose one of these
two subsets and call it
P
and the other we call
L
.
We refer to the elements of
P
as
points
and to the elements of
L
as
lines
.

Notation 3.1
.

We call
\Gamma

green
if the local opposition relations at lines are all trivial.

We assume for the rest of this section
that our Veldkamp quadrangle
\Gamma
is green.

Proposition 3.2
.

\Gamma
does not contain circuits of length
4
.

Proof.
Let
(a,x,b,y,c)
be a
4
-path with
c=a\in P
. Since
\Gamma
is green,

(a,x,b)
and
(a,y,b)
are distinct straight paths from
a
to
b
.
By (VP2), this is not allowed.

\Box

Notation 3.3
.

Let
x,y
be vertices such that
{\rm dist}(x,y)=2
. By
3.2
,
there is a unique vertex in
\Gamma_{x}\cap\Gamma_{y}
. We denote this vertex by
x\wedge y
.

Proposition 3.4
.

A
6
-circuit does not contain any straight
2
-paths

(x,a,y)
such that
a\in P
.

Proof.
Let
(a,x,b,y,c,z,a)
be a closed
6
-path with
a\in P
. It suffices to show that

(x,b,y)
is not straight. Suppose that it is.
Then
(a,x,b,y,c)
is a root, so by
2.9
,
a
and
c
are opposite. By
2.10
,
there is a root
(a,u,d,z,c)
from
a
to
c
that contains
z
.
Thus
(a,z)
and
(a,u,d,z)
are both straight paths from
a
to
z
. By (VP2),
this is not allowed.

\Box

Proposition 3.5
.

If
a,b\in P
are opposite, then
{\rm dist}(a,b)=4
.

Proof.
Suppose that
a,b\in P
are opposite.
By
2.9
, there is a root from
a
to
b
. In particular,

{\rm dist}(a,b)\leq 4
. By
3.2
,
a\neq b
and then by

,
{\rm dist}(a,b)\neq 2
.
Hence
{\rm dist}(a,b)=4
.

\Box

Proposition 3.6
.

{\rm diam}(\Gamma)=4
.

Proof.
By
3.5
, it suffices to show that

Let

. If

\Box

Proposition 3.7
.

Let

and suppose that

.

\Gamma_{2}(b)\cap a^{\rm op}=\emptyset.

Now choose

. We claim that

(3.9)

\Gamma_{x}\backslash\{b\}\subset\Gamma_{2}(a).

By

, we can choose
c\in a^{\rm op}\cap b^{\rm op}
.
Let
(b,x_{1},x_{2},x_{3},c)

to

.

),

.

Choose

opposite

at

. Let
(c,z_{1},z_{2},z_{3},b)

to

. The closed
8
-path

If

, then

.

. To prove (

. The path

(c,w_{1},w_{2},w_{3},d)

to

.

, we have

to

.

) holds as claimed.

By

, (
3.8
) and

(3.10)

\Gamma_{2}(e)=\Gamma_{2}(f)

It remains to show that

\Box

.

4.
Flat quotients

Our goal in this section is to show that every green Veldkamp quadrangle has a
canonical flat quotient. The precise result is formulated in
4.23
and
4.30
.

For the rest of this section, we assume that
\Gamma
is a green Veldkamp quadrangle
and continue with the notation in the previous section.

Notation 4.1
.

A
weed
is a non-straight
4
-path
(a,x,b,y,c)
such that

a,b,c\in P
and
{\rm dist}(a,c)=4
.

Remark 4.2
.

Note that a root cannot also be a weed since roots are straight
and weeds are not.

Remark 4.3
.

Let
\alpha=(a,x,b,y,c)
be a weed. The point
b
is called the
middle point
of

\alpha
. By
3.2
, the middle point of a weed
\alpha
is uniquely determined by the two
lines contained in
\alpha
.

Notation 4.4
.

Let
a\simeq b
for
a,b\in P
if either
a=b
or there is a weed that begins at
a
and ends at
b
.

Proposition 4.5
.

Suppose that
u\in v^{\rm op}
for some
u,v\in P\cup L
and
a\simeq b

for some
a,b\in P
such that
a\neq b
. Then the following hold:

(i)

Every
4
-path from
u
to
v
is a root and for every
w\in\Gamma_{u}
,
there is a unique
4
-path from
u
to
v
that contains
w
.

(ii)

For every
x\in\Gamma_{a}
, there exists a
4
-path from
a
to
b
containing
x
,
every
4
-path from
a
to
b
is a weed and
a\not\in b^{\rm op}
.

Proof.
Let
w\in\Gamma_{u}
. By
3.5
and
3.6
, we have
{\rm dist}(w,v)=3
. Hence
there exists a
4
-path

\alpha=(u,p,q,w,v)
from
u
to
v
that contains
w
.
By
2.10
, there exists a root
\beta=(u,m,n,w,v)
from

u
to
v
that passes through

. By
3.4
,
q=n
. By
3.2
, therefore,

\alpha=\beta
. Hence
\alpha
is a root and
\alpha
is the only
4
-path from

u
to
v
that contains

. Thus (i) holds.

Let
x\in\Gamma_{a}
.
Since

, there exists a weed
\alpha
from
a
to

.
Hence
{\rm dist}(a,b)=4
and by

, there exists
a
4
-path

from

to

containing

. Applying (i), we observe

is not

\Box

Proposition 4.6
.

Let
a,b\in P
. Then the following hold:

(i)

a\simeq b
if and only if
a^{\rm op}=b^{\rm op}
.

(ii)

If

, then
\Gamma_{2}(a)=\Gamma_{2}(b)
.

Proof.
It suffices to assume that
a\neq b
.
Suppose that
a\simeq b
. Then
{\rm dist}(a,b)=4
but
by
4.5
(ii),

and
b
are not opposite.
By
3.7
, therefore,
a^{\rm op}=b^{\rm op}
and
\Gamma_{2}(a)=\Gamma_{2}(b)
.
Suppose, conversely, that

.
If
{\rm dist}(a,b)=2
, then (because
\Gamma
is green) there exists a root
\alpha
starting at
a

and containing

, but then the last vertex of

is contained in

. Hence
{\rm dist}(a,b)=4
by

. Let
\beta

be a
4
-path from

to

. Since

is not opposite itself, it is not opposite

. It follows that

is a weed

.

\Box

Notation 4.7
.

By
4.6
(i),
\simeq
is an equivalence relation. We denote the equivalence class
of a point

by

.

Notation 4.8
.

Let

for

and

Thus if

Proposition 4.9
.

Let

for some

. Suppose that

is opposite

at

. Then

is opposite

at

.

Proof.
Since

, there exist

is a weed. By

and

(ii), we have

and
\Gamma_{2}(b)=\Gamma_{2}(c)
.
Choose

. By

, the points

are pairwise

and

are opposite at

,
(d,x,a,y,b)

and

are opposite.
By

, therefore,

.
Hence

, we have

.
Suppose now that

and

are not opposite at

. Then

. Since

,

.

is opposite

.

\Box

.

Let

and suppose that

. Then

.

It suffices to assume that

and, by symmetry, to show that

Let

and choose

. By

(i), there exists a root

. The lines

.

.

\Box

We will need the following result in the proof of

.

Lemma 4.11
.

Let
a,b,c,e,f\in P
and

and
suppose that

, that

-paths
and that the

-paths
(y,c,t,e,z)
,

.

Proof.
Note that
(a,y,c,v,f)
is a root and hence
a
and
f
are opposite.
By
3.5
,
a\not\in\Gamma_{2}(f)

and hence
b\neq f
. By
3.6
, therefore, it suffices to show that
{\rm dist}(b,f)\neq 4
.
We suppose that
{\rm dist}(b,f)=4
.
Since

, we have
\Gamma_{2}(b)\neq\Gamma_{2}(f)
. By

(ii),
it follows that
b
and

are opposite.
By
4.5
(i), therefore,
\alpha:=(b,u,c,v,f)

Suppose now that
v=t
. Then

lies on the root

and on the closed
6
-path

\xi
. By
3.4
, this is impossible. Hence

.
Hence by
3.2
, we have
e\neq f
. Suppose that there exists

.
By
4.5
(i),
(b,w,e,r,f)
is a root. Since
(x,b,w,e,z)

.
By

(i),
\delta:=(r,f,v,c,y)
is a root.
By

,
r\neq t
and

. It follows that

is a closed
6
-path.
The path
(r,f,v)
is contained in both

and

. By

again, this is impossible. Hence the line

.

Since
b
and
f
are opposite, there exists a root
(b,w,g,p,f)
from

to

. Since

, we have

.
Since
(x,b,w,e,z)
is a root, it follows that

. By

(i),
therefore,
(y,c,v,f,p)

and

(i).

and on the closed

,

.

\Box

Proposition 4.12
.

Let
(x,a,y)
be a path for some
a\in P
and some
x,y\in L
and suppose
that
x^{\rm op}=y^{\rm op}
. Then for each

,

If

and containing

and

, but then the last vertex on this root is in

. Hence

Choose

.
Let

and let
(y,c,t,e,z)
be the root from

to

that

. Then
(a,y,c,t,e)
is also a root, so

is opposite

.
Hence by

,

.
Since

,

(x,b,w,e,z)
from

to

that contains

.
Since

. Thus by

,

.
We want to show that

, it suffices to

By

,

and

. Hence

is a closed

-path.
By

, therefore,

is not straight. Since

is straight,

. Since

Now suppose that

.
Thus

By

,

(i), therefore,

\Box

Proposition 4.13

Let

Suppose that

By

, we have

Since

Since

(4.14)

\Gamma_{x}\subset\Gamma_{2}(b).

Let

to

that contains

.

We claim that also

) at

.

(i), therefore,

.

), therefore,

.

as claimed. Thus
(c,w,d,v,b)
is a root.
By

5.
Polar spaces

In this section we describe the connection between polar spaces and Veldkamp quadrangles.
The main results are
5.18
and
5.22
. The proofs require
a few basic properties of polar spaces for which we will cite results from
[
1
, Chapter 7]
.

Definition 5.1
.

A
line space
is a pair
S=(P,L)
consisting of a set
P
of points and a subset
L

of
2^{P}
such that
|x|\geq 2
for all
x\in L
.
The elements of
L
are called
lines
.
A line
x
is
thick
if
|x|\geq 3
and
S
is called
thick
if all its lines are thick.
Two points are called
collinear
if there is an element of
L
that contains them both

Notation 5.2
.

Let
S=(P,L)
be a line space. The associated biparite graph
\Gamma=\Gamma_{S}
is
the graph with vertex set
P\cup L
and edge set consisting of all pairs
\{a,x\}

such that
a\in P
,
x\in L
and
a\in x
.

Notation 5.3
.

Let
S=(P,L)
be a line space. For each
a\in L
,
we denote by
a^{\perp}
the set of all points collinear with
a
(including
a
itself)
and we set

X^{\perp}=\bigcap_{a\in X}a^{\perp}

for all subsets
X
of
P
. A
subspace
is a subset
X
of
P
such that for all

x\in L
, either
x\subset X
or
|x\cap X|\leq 1
. We will often identify a subspace

X
with the line space

S_{X}:=(X,\{x\in L\mid x\subset X\}).

For all subsets
X
of
P
, let
\langle X\rangle
denote the intersection of all
the subspaces that contain
X
. This intersection is itself a subspace. It is called the subspace

generated by
X
.

Notation 5.4
.

A
linear space
is a line space such that for every two points there is a unique
line containing them both. A
partially linear space
is a line space such that for
every two points there is at most one line containing them both. If

a,b
are distinct collinear points in a partially linear space, then the unique line containing them
both is denoted by
ab
.

Definition 5.5
.

A
projective plane
is a linear space
S=(P,L)
such that
|L|\geq 2
and

x\cap y\neq\emptyset
for all
x,y\in P
. A
projective space
is a linear space
such that every subspace generated by the union of two lines with non-empty intersection is a
projective plane.

Definition 5.6
.

Let
S=(P,L)
be a projective space. The
dimension
of
S
is the maximal
length
k
of a properly ascending chain of subspaces
X_{i}
such that

\emptyset\neq X_{1}\subset X_{2}\subset\cdots\subset X_{k}=P.

It is denoted by
{\rm dim}(S)
. Thus, in particular, lines are
subspaces of dimension
1
and
{\rm dim}(S)=\infty
if there no bound on the
length of such chains. A non-singular subspace of dimension
2
is called
a non-singular
plane
.

Definition 5.7
.

A
polar space
is a line space
S=(P,L)
such that the following holds:

(BS) For all
a\in P
and
x\in L
, either
|a^{\perp}\cap x|=1
or
x\subset a^{\perp}
.

Note that every subspace of a polar space is itself a polar space.

Definition 5.8
.

Let
S=(P,L)
be a line space. A subspace
X
of
S
is called
singular
if
every two points of
X
are collinear. Thus lines are, in particular, singular
subpaces.

Definition 5.9
.

A polar space
S=(P,L)
is
non-degenerate
if
P\neq a^{\perp}
for all
a\in P
.

Proposition 5.10
.

Let
S
be a non-degenerate polar space. Then the following hold:

(i)

S
is a partially linear space.

(ii)

Singular subspaces are projective spaces.

Proof.
(i) holds by
[
1
, Thm. 7.4.11]
and (ii) by
[
1
, Thm. 7.4.13(iv)]
.

\Box

Definition 5.11
.

In light of
5.10
(ii), we can define the

rank
of a non-degenerate polar space
S=(P,L)
to be one more than the supremum of the set

\{{\rm dim}(E)\mid\text{$E$ is a non-singular subspace}\}.

The rank is denoted by
{\rm rk}(S)
. Thus
{\rm rk}(S)=1
if and only if
L=\emptyset

but
P\neq\emptyset
,
and
{\rm rk}(S)=2
if and only if
L\neq\emptyset
but lines are maximal singular
subspaces.

A polar space is called thick in this paper (see
