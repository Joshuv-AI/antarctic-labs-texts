Time-Reversal Invariant Topological Moiré Flatband: A Platform for the Fractional Quantum Spin Hall Effect
Yi-Ming Wu, Daniel Shaffer, Zhengzhi Wu, Luiz H. Santos

Abstract
Motivated by recent observation of the quantum spin Hall effect in monolayer germanene and twisted bilayer transition-metal-dichalcogenides (TMDs), we study the topological phases of moiré twisted bilayers with time-reversal symmetry and spin s z s_{z} conservation. By using a continuum model description which can be applied to both germanene and TMD bilayers, we show that at small twist angles, the emergent moiré flatbands can be topologically nontrivial due to inversion symmetry breaking. Each of these flatbands for each spin projection admits a lowest-Landau-level description in the chiral limit and at magic twist angle. This allows for the construction of a many-body Laughlin state with time-reversal symmetry which can be stabilized by a short-range pseudopotential, and therefore serves as an ideal platform for realizing the so-far elusive fractional quantum spin Hall effect with emergent spin-1/2 U(1) symmetry.

.It is this similarity that makes it possible to obtain quantum (spin) Hall effect in the full or partially filled moiré flat bands.

Many-body wavefunctions.
-
Since
\Omega
plays the same role as
l_{B}^{2}
in the GMP algebra, we can identify each moiré unit cell as the magnetic unit cell which hosts a single magnetic flux. For a parallelogram system with the widths
\bm{L}_{1}=N_{1}\bm{a}_{1}
and
\bm{L}_{2}=N_{2}\bm{a}_{2}
as shown in Fig.
1
(a), the general twisted periodic boundary condition for each particle on the many-body wavefunction implies
\Psi(\{z_{i}\}|z_{j}=L_{1,2})=e^{i\phi_{1,2}}\Psi(\{z_{i}\}|z_{j}=0)
, where
\phi_{1,2}
are not necessarily zero. Following the logic of Ref.
Haldane and Rezayi 1985
;
Ledwith et al. 2020
, we have for spin-up fermions

\displaystyle\Psi_{\uparrow,m}(\{z_{j}\})=

\displaystyle e^{iKZ}\prod_{j=1}^{N_{e}}F_{\uparrow+}(\bm{r}_{j})\prod_{\nu=1}^{m}\sigma_{L}(Z-iZ_{\nu})\times

(9)

\displaystyle\prod_{i<j}\left[\sigma_{L}(z_{i}-z_{j})\right]^{m}

where we have assumed there are
N_{e}
spin-up fermions so that the filling fraction is
1/m=N_{e}/N_{s}
. Note that we need to keep
m
an odd integer in order to maintain fermionic properties. Here
Z=\sum_{j}z_{j}
and the values of
K
and
Z_{0}:=\sum_{\nu}Z_{\nu}
are chosen to satisfy

e^{iKL_{1,2}}=(-1)^{N_{s}+N_{1,2}}e^{i\frac{\pi L_{1,2}^{*}Z_{0}}{N_{s}S}+i\phi_{1,2}}.

(10)

The sigma function with subscript ‘L’ is defined similarly to the previous discussion, but with the unit cell enlarged to the whole sample spanned by
L_{1}
and
L_{2}
instead of
a_{1}
and
a_{2}
. Since the sigma function vanishes as
\sigma_{L}(z)\sim z
when
z\to 0
, this wavefunction scales as
(z_{i}-z_{j})^{m}
whenever there are two particles approaching each other, so it can be stablized by some pseudopotential similar to Haldane’s.
The difference from Haldane’s pseudopotential is that the ideal flat band pseudopotential not only depends on the relative angular momentum between two particles, but also on their center-of-mass (COM), so that the general form of the interactions can be written as
V(\bm{r}_{1},\bm{r}_{2})=\sum_{M,m}v_{M,m}\hat{P}_{M,m}
where
\hat{P}_{M,m}
is the projector
Wang et al. 2021
. This can be traced back to the fact that the LLL wavefunction obeys the magnetic translation group
Zak 1964
while the ideal flatband wavefunction in our case does not. One simple realization that stablize the wavefunction in Eq.(
9
) is to consider sufficiently short range (Hubbard-like) interactions, for which the COM gets frozen, and the pseudopotential can be modeled by
V(\bm{r})=\sum_{m^{\prime}=0}^{m^{\prime}<m}v_{m^{\prime}}(\nabla^{2})^{m^{\prime}}\sum_{i}\delta(\bm{r}-\bm{R}_{i})
where
\bm{R}_{i}
denote all lattice sites and all
m^{\prime}>0
should be odd. For the spin-down fermions, the construction is exactly the same,

\displaystyle\Psi_{\downarrow,m^{\prime}}(\{\bar{w}_{j}\})=

\displaystyle e^{-i\bar{Q}\bar{W}}\prod_{j=1}^{N_{e}^{\prime}}F_{\downarrow-}(\bm{r}_{j})\prod_{\nu=1}^{m^{\prime}}\sigma_{L}(\bar{W}-i\bar{W}_{\nu})\times

(11)

\displaystyle\prod_{i<j}\left[\sigma_{L}(\bar{w}_{i}-\bar{w}_{j})\right]^{m^{\prime}}

with
\bar{W}=\sum_{j}\bar{w}_{j}
, and
\bar{Q}
and
\bar{W}_{0}:=\sum_{\nu}\bar{W}_{\nu}
satisfying conditions similar to Eq.(
10
).

Upon combining the two spin components (
9
) and (
11
), we identify

\Psi_{\text{FQSH}}(\{z_{j},w_{j}\})=\Psi_{\uparrow,m}(\{z_{j}\})\Psi_{\downarrow,m}(\{\bar{w}_{j}\})

(12)

as a candidate wavefunction describing a FQSH state with spin filling fraction
\nu_{spin}=\frac{1}{m}
, which hosts conserved spin current and fractional electronic excitations
Bernevig and Zhang 2006
;
Levin and Stern 2009
;
Neupert et al. 2011b
;
Santos et al. 2011
.
In short samples compared with the electron mean free path, the presence of a helical edge state may be revealed by the low-temperature quantization of the longitudinal conductance
G=2e^{2}/h

Konig et al. 2007
, expected to persist for an interacting Luttinger liquid edge
Maslov and Stone 1995
. Furthermore, charge fractionalization could be sensed via shot noise measurements
De-Picciotto et al. 1998
;
Saminadayar et al. 1997
, providing two complementing experimental signatures of time-reversal symmetric fractionalization.

Remarkably, the ideal flat band condition
Ledwith et al. 2020
;
Wang et al. 2021
ensures that (
12
) is the ground state of a local time-reversal symmetric Haldane pseudo-potential interaction, which establishes a microscopic mechanism for
time-reversal invariant fractional topological order in moiré flat band systems. The many-body wavefunction (
12
) can be generalized by multiplication by terms
\sim\prod_{r<s}(z_{r}-\bar{w}_{s})^{n}
, which represent correlations between opposite spins
Bernevig and Zhang 2006
;
Santos et al. 2011
. Achieving such FQSH states would require different local interactions, a question that merits further investigation.

Discussion.
- We have shown that twisted bilayer 2D materials with spin-orbit coupling (TMD and germanene)
can give rise to ideal flat bands with TRS at magic twist angle and in the chiral limit, serving as an ideal platform for realizing FQSH effect. There are, however, two obstacles that can potentially destroy the FQSH state. The first one is the competition with other symmetry breaking phases, in particular ferromagnetism
Mai et al. 2023
. The true ground state depends on the details of the interactions, so given that moiré systems have much higher tunability of interactions compared to other platforms, we expect that the FQSH state considered here is indeed in a physically accessible regime.
The second obstacle is that a finite
w_{AA}
spoils the ideal flat band condition and renders the Berry curvature more inhomogeneous in
\bm{k}
-space. Comparing energies of different competing states in this case, e.g. using exact diagonalization and DMRG, is an interesting question which we leave for a future study.

I
A.LLL wavefunction on a torus and the construction of Laughlin wavefunction.

The LLL wavefunction on a torus was first studied in Ref.
Haldane and Rezayi 1985
, where the theta function was used to account for the double-periodicity nature of the wavefunction. There the theta functions is periodic in terms of the boundaries
L_{1}
and
L_{2}
, which necessarily involves a product of many theta functions. Alternatively, one can define the problem on a lattice, and the unit cell is chosen arbitrarily but must enclose an area of
2\pi l_{B}^{2}
where a unit flux passes. The introducing of lattice makes it easy to compare with real lattice system with a flat Chern band. Below we discuss both of these two pictures separately.

I.1
LLL without lattice

I.1.1
single-particle wavefunction

Here we closely follow the logic of Ref.
Haldane and Rezayi 1985
. The single particle LLL wavefunction in Landau gauge
\bm{A}(\bm{r})=(-yB,0,0)
can be written as

\psi(x,y)=e^{-{y^{2}}/{2}}f(z).

(13)

Here we have set
l_{B}^{2}=1
, and
f(z)
is a holomorphic function defined on the whole plane. Due to the presence of the exponentially decaying prefactor
e^{-{y^{2}}/{2}}
,
f(z)
can be unbounded in the
y
-direction, so the total wavefunction
\psi(x,y)
can still be normalizable.
The magnetic translation operator
t(\bm{L})
acting on a wavefunction is (assuming
\bm{B}
is in the
z
-direction)

t(\bm{L})\psi(\bm{r})\equiv\psi(\bm{r}+\bm{L})=e^{i\bm{L}\cdot[-i\bm{\nabla}-e\bm{A}(\bm{r})]+ie\bm{B}\cdot(\bm{r}\times\bm{L})}\psi(\bm{r}).

(14)

Suppose we defined the system size as a parallelogram with width
|\bm{L}_{1}|
and
|\bm{L}_{2}|
and and angle
\varphi
between them. Then the total number of flux piercing this sample is given by

N_{s}=\frac{A}{2\pi l_{B}^{2}}=\frac{|\bm{L}_{1}||\bm{L}_{2}|\sin\varphi}{2\pi}.

(15)

The twisted boundary condition on the wave function implies,

\psi(\bm{L}_{1,2})=\psi(0)e^{i\phi_{1,2}}.

(16)

Let’s further assume
\bm{L}_{1}
is in
x
-direction without loss of generality. Then we can immediately see

\boxed{f(L_{1})=f(0)e^{i\phi_{1}}}.

(17)

The boundary condition in
\bm{L}_{2}
direction also applies to the prefactor
e^{-{y^{2}}/{2}}
, so we will have

e^{-(|\bm{L}_{2}|\sin\varphi)^{2}/2}f(L_{2})=f(0)e^{i\phi_{2}}.

(18)

Using the definition of
N_{s}
, we can also write the above expression as

\boxed{e^{-\pi N_{s}\frac{|\bm{L}_{2}|}{|\bm{L}_{1}|}\sin\varphi}f(L_{2})=f(0)e^{i\phi_{2}}}.

(19)

The solution of the above two boxed equations are given by the Jacobi theta function,

f(z)=e^{ikz}\prod_{\nu=1}^{N_{s}}\vartheta_{1}\left(\frac{z-z_{\nu}}{L_{1}},\tilde{\eta}\right)

(20)

where
\tilde{\eta}=L_{2}/L_{1}
and the theta function
\vartheta_{1}
is defined as

\vartheta_{1}(u,\tilde{\eta}):=-i\sum_{l\in\mathbb{Z}}(-1)^{l}q^{(l+1/2)^{2}}e^{i\pi(2l+1)u},~q:=e^{i\pi\tilde{\eta}},

(21)

which has the following properties,

\displaystyle\vartheta_{1}(u+n,\tilde{\eta})

\displaystyle=(-1)^{n}\vartheta_{1}(u,\tilde{\eta}),

(22)

\displaystyle\vartheta_{1}(u+n\tilde{\eta},\tilde{\eta})

\displaystyle=(-1)^{n}e^{-i\pi(2nu+n^{2}\tilde{\eta})}\vartheta_{1}(u,\tilde{\eta}),

\displaystyle\vartheta_{1}(-u,\tilde{\eta})

\displaystyle=-\vartheta_{1}(u,\tilde{\eta}),

\displaystyle\vartheta_{1}(u,\tilde{\eta})

\displaystyle=0~~~\text{for}~~u=n+m\tilde{\eta},~m,n\in\mathbb{Z}

\displaystyle\vartheta_{1}(u,\tilde{\eta})

\displaystyle\sim u~~~\text{for}~~u\to 0.

We now need to choose proper
k
and
z_{\nu}
in order to satisfy the boxed boundary condition in Eq.(
17
) and (
19
). Using the properties of
\vartheta_{1}
listed above, and defining
z_{0}=\sum_{\nu}^{N_{s}}z_{\nu}
, it is easy to see that
k
and
z_{0}
satisfy the following equations,

\displaystyle e^{ikL_{1}}

\displaystyle=(-1)^{N_{s}}e^{i\phi_{1}},

(23)

\displaystyle e^{ikL_{2}}

\displaystyle=(-1)^{N_{s}}e^{i\left(\phi_{2}-2\pi z_{0}/L_{1}+\pi N_{s}\frac{|\bm{L}_{2}|}{|\bm{L}_{1}|}\cos\varphi\right)}.

Note the second equation is slightly different from that in Ref.
Haldane and Rezayi 1985
, and by setting
\varphi=\pi/2
, i.e. when the
\bm{L}_{1}
and
\bm{L}_{2}
are perpendicular to each other, the equation is reduced to the one in Ref.
Haldane and Rezayi 1985
. From Eq.(
23
) it is easy to see that, if
(k,z_{0})
is the solution, so is
(k+n_{1}2\pi/L_{1},z_{0}-n_{1}L_{2}+n_{2}L_{1})
with
n_{1},n_{2}\in\mathbb{Z}
. The number of the linearly independent solutions is equal to the number of zeros of
f(z)
, which is
N_{s}
.

I.1.2
Laughlin wavefunction

The construction of the many-body Laughlin wavefunction proceeds as follows. Suppose there are
N_{e}
electrons, so the filling factor is given by
N_{e}/N_{s}=1/m
. We will assume
m\geq 3
is some odd integer. The wavefunction is given by the ansatz,

\Psi(\{z_{i}\})=F(Z)\prod_{i<j}g(z_{i}-z_{j})

(24)

where
F(Z)
is a function which depends on the center-of-mass coordinate
Z
and
g(z_{i}-z_{j})
is the Jastrow factor which only depends on the relative coordinate. Recall the usual Laughlin wavefunction is

\Psi_{\text{LW}}(\{z_{i}\})=e^{-\sum_{i}^{N_{e}}|z_{i}|^{2}/4}\prod_{i<j}(z_{i}-z_{j})^{m},

(25)

hence we need

g(z_{i}-z_{j})\sim(z_{i}-z_{j})^{m}~~\text{for}~~z_{i}\to z_{j}.

(26)

One possibility is

g(z)=\left[\vartheta_{1}\left(\frac{z}{L_{1}},\tilde{\eta}\right)\right]^{m},

(27)

which leads to the following boundary condition for a generic single particle, say
z_{1}

\displaystyle g(L_{1}-z_{j})

\displaystyle=(-1)^{m}g(-z_{j})

(28)

\displaystyle g(L_{2}-z_{j})

\displaystyle=(-1)^{m}e^{-im\pi\left(-2\frac{z_{j}}{L_{1}}+\frac{L_{2}}{L_{1}}\right)}g(-z_{j}).

Therefore, for the product of
N_{e}-1
particles, we have

\displaystyle\prod_{j}g(L_{1}-z_{j})

\displaystyle=(-1)^{N_{s}-m}\prod_{j}g(-z_{j})

(29)

\displaystyle\prod_{j}g(L_{2}-z_{j})

\displaystyle=(-1)^{N_{s}-m}e^{i2\pi m\frac{Z}{L_{1}}-i(N_{s}-m)\pi\frac{L_{2}}{L_{1}}}\prod_{j}g(-z_{j})

If we require that the total wavefunction satisfies

\Psi_{\text{LW}}(\{z_{j}|z_{i}=L_{1,2}\})=\Psi_{\text{LW}}(\{z_{j}|z_{i}=0\})e^{i\phi_{1,2}}

(30)

then the center-of-mass factor
F(Z)
must satisfy

\displaystyle F(Z+L_{1})

\displaystyle=F(Z)(-1)^{N_{s}-m}e^{i\phi_{1}},

(31)

\displaystyle F(Z+L_{2})

\displaystyle=F(Z)(-1)^{N_{s}-m}e^{-i2\pi m\frac{Z}{L_{1}}+i(N_{s}-m)\pi\frac{L_{2}}{L_{1}}}e^{i\phi_{2}}.

The general solution for
F(Z)
can be expressed as

F(Z)=e^{iKZ}\prod_{\nu=1}^{m}\vartheta_{1}\left(\frac{Z-Z_{\nu}}{L_{1}},\tilde{\eta}\right)

(32)

Likewise, we need to properly choose
K
and
Z_{0}:=\sum_{\nu}Z_{\nu}
to solve Eq.(
31
). This puts constraints on
K
and
Z_{0}
, namely

\displaystyle e^{iKL_{1}}

\displaystyle=(-1)^{N_{s}}e^{i\phi_{1}},

(33)

\displaystyle e^{iKL_{2}}

\displaystyle=(-1)^{N_{s}}e^{-i2\pi\frac{Z_{0}}{L_{1}}}e^{iN_{s}\pi\tilde{\eta}}e^{i\phi_{2}}.

I.2
Another choice: LLL wave function with lattice

I.2.1
Symmetric gauge

Here it is useful to switch to symmetric gauge
where
\bm{A}=(-yB/2,xB/2,0)
, the magnetic translation operator acting on
\psi(\bm{r})
has a simple form (again assuming
eB=l_{B}^{-2}=1
)

t(\bm{L})=e^{i\bm{L}\cdot(-i\bm{\nabla})+\frac{i}{2}(\bm{r}\times\bm{L})\cdot\hat{z}}.

(34)

The wavefunction that simultaneously diagonalize the Hamiltonian and this translation operator can be given by the modified Weierstrass sigma function
Haldane 2018

\sigma(z)=a_{1}e^{\frac{\pi a_{1}^{*}z^{2}}{2Aa_{1}}}\frac{\vartheta_{1}\left(\frac{z}{a_{1}},\eta\right)}{\vartheta_{1}^{\prime}(0,\eta)}

(35)

where
\vartheta_{1}
is the theta function defined above, and
\vartheta_{1}^{\prime}
denotes its derivative.
A
is the area of the unit cell, and in the lattice spanned by
a_{1}
and
a_{2}
we have
A=2\pi
. Upon translated by a lattice
l=ma_{1}+na_{2}
with
m,n\in\mathbb{Z}
, it changes as

\sigma(z+l)=\xi(l)e^{\frac{\pi l^{*}}{A}(z+l/2)}\sigma(z)

(36)

where
\xi(l)=1
if
l/2
is also on the lattice and
\xi(l)=-1
otherwise. In particular, if
l=a_{j}
with
j=1,2
, we have

\sigma(z+a_{j})=-e^{\frac{a_{j}^{*}}{2}(z+a_{j}/2)}\sigma(z).

(37)

Now if we write the wavefunction as

\boxed{\psi_{S}(x,y)=e^{-|z|^{2}/4}f_{k}(z)}

(38)

then the holomorphic function
f_{k}(z)
is given by

\boxed{f_{k}(z)=\sigma(z+ik)e^{-|k|^{2}/4+ik^{*}z/2}}.

(39)

An important property of writing the LLL wave function using this modified sigma function is that it does not dependent on the specific choice of the lattice, i.e. it’s modular-invariant. One can design an artificial lattice spanned by the lattice vector
\bm{a}_{1}
and
\bm{a}_{2}
with the unit cell area being
2\pi
(it is
2\pi l_{B}^{2}
when
l_{B}
is not set to unity). Then the system under consideration can be described using two integers
N_{1}
and
N_{2}
, such that
L_{1}=N_{1}a_{1}
and
L_{1}=N_{2}a_{2}
, and the total number of fluxes passing through the system is given by
N_{s}=N_{1}N_{2}
.
Under translation
\bm{l}=m\bm{a}_{1}+n\bm{a}_{2}
, this wavefunction transforms as

\displaystyle\psi_{S}(\bm{r}+\bm{l})=e^{-|z+l|^{2}/4}f_{k}(z+l)

(40)

\displaystyle=e^{-\frac{|z|^{2}+|k|^{2}}{4}-\frac{|l|^{2}}{4}-\frac{z^{*}l+l^{*}z}{4}+i\frac{k^{*}z+k^{*}l}{2}}\sigma(z+l+ik)

\displaystyle=\xi(l)e^{i\frac{k^{*}l+l^{*}k}{2}+\frac{l^{*}z-z^{*}l}{4}}\psi_{S}(\bm{r})

\displaystyle=\xi(l)e^{i\bm{k}\cdot\bm{l}+\frac{i}{2}(\bm{r}\times\bm{l})\cdot\hat{z}}\psi_{S}(\bm{r}).

Note this wavefunction transforms in a similar but not exactly the same way as Bloch wavefunction transforms under spatial translation.
Using this properties, one can explicitly show that

t(\bm{l}_{1})t(\bm{l}_{2})\psi_{S}(\bm{r})=t(\bm{l}_{2})t(\bm{l}_{1})\psi_{S}(\bm{r})e^{i(\bm{l}_{1}\times\bm{l}_{2})\cdot\hat{z}}

(41)

as it should be.
The periodic boundary condition in Eq.(
16
) then implies
\bm{k}
must obey

\displaystyle\bm{k}\cdot\bm{L}_{1}

\displaystyle=2\pi n_{1}+N_{1}\pi+\phi_{1},

(42)

\displaystyle\bm{k}\cdot\bm{L}_{2}

\displaystyle=2\pi n_{2}+N_{2}\pi+\phi_{2},

with
n_{1},n_{2}\in\mathbb{Z}
.

Using the relation between sigma function and the theta function, it is possible to express the wavefunction only in terms of theta function. In addition, without loss of generality, we can always choose
a_{1}
to be real. It is then easy to see

\boxed{\psi_{S}(x,y)=e^{i\frac{\bm{k}\cdot\bm{a}_{1}}{a_{1}}z}\frac{a_{1}e^{-\frac{|z|^{2}-z^{2}}{4}-\frac{|k|^{2}+k^{2}}{4}}}{\vartheta_{1}^{\prime}(0,\eta)}{\vartheta_{1}\left(\frac{z+ik}{a_{1}},\omega\right)}}.

(43)

Note this expression is different from Eq.(
20
) in a sense that it contains only one holomorphic theta function. But the number of zeros enclosed by the sample boundary remains the same. By making use of Eq.(
), it is easy to realize that the function

u_{\bm{k}}:=\psi_{S}(\bm{r})e^{-i\bm{k}\cdot\bm{r}}\equiv N_{\bm{k}}\tilde{u}_{k}(\bm{r})

(44)

is a normalized
k
-holomorphic function
\tilde{u}_{k}(\bm{r})
times a
\bm{k}
-dependent complex normalization factor
N_{\bm{k}}
(such that
||u_{\bm{k}}||^{2}=|N_{\bm{k}}|^{2}
). This observation is useful, since the quantum geometric tensor
\eta(\bm{k})
, defined as

\eta_{ab}(\bm{k})=\frac{\braket{\partial_{k_{a}}u_{\bm{k}}|\partial_{k_{b}}u_{\bm{k}}}}{|N_{\bm{k}}|^{2}}-\frac{\braket{\partial_{k_{a}}u_{\bm{k}}|u_{\bm{k}}}\braket{u_{\bm{k}}|\partial_{k_{b}}u_{\bm{k}}}}{|N_{\bm{k}}|^{4}}

(45)

is actually independent of
N_{\bm{k}}
. A simple manipulation shows that when substituting
u_{\bm{k}}=N_{\bm{k}}\tilde{u}_{k}
into this definition, the derivatives of
N_{\bm{k}}
from the first and the second part of Eq.(
45
) cancel out, which leads to

\eta_{ab}(\bm{k})={\braket{\partial_{k_{a}}\tilde{u}_{k}|\partial_{k_{b}}\tilde{u}_{k}}}-{\braket{\partial_{k_{a}}\tilde{u}_{k}|\tilde{u}_{k}}\braket{\tilde{u}_{k}|\partial_{k_{b}}\tilde{u}_{k}}}.

(46)

Therefore, the factor
N_{\bm{k}}
, although depending on
\bm{k}
, does not determine the properties of the ideal flatband.

We close by noting that the wavefunction in Landau gauge can be readily obtained by applying the gauge transformation, namely,

\displaystyle\psi_{L}(x,y)

\displaystyle=\psi_{S}(x,y)e^{-ixy/2}

(47)

\displaystyle=e^{i\frac{\bm{k}\cdot\bm{a}_{1}}{a_{1}}z}\frac{a_{1}e^{-\frac{y^{2}}{2}-\frac{|k|^{2}+k^{2}}{4}}}{\vartheta_{1}^{\prime}(0,\eta)}{\vartheta_{1}\left(\frac{z+ik}{a_{1}},\omega\right)}.

I.2.2
Laughlin wavefunction

Using sigma function, we can write the ansatz for the many-body wavefunction similar to that in Eq.(

), but since we will be using sigma functions, we write the ansatz as

\Psi(\{z_{i}\})=e^{-\sum_{i}\frac{|z_{i}|^{2}}{4}}F(Z)\prod_{i<j}g(z_{i}-z_{j}),

where

(49)

and

\sigma_{L}(z)=\frac{L_{1}}{\pi}e^{\frac{L_{1}^{*}z^{2}}{4N_{s}L_{1}}}\frac{\vartheta_{1}\left(\frac{z}{L_{1}},\tilde{\eta}\right)}{\vartheta^{\prime}(0,\tilde{\eta})}.

(50)

Similar to Eq.(

\sigma_{L}(z+L_{1,2})=-e^{\frac{L_{1,2}^{*}}{2N_{s}}(z+L_{1,2}/2)}\sigma(z).

Clearly

\displaystyle\prod_{j}g(L_{1}-z_{j})

\displaystyle\prod_{j}g(L_{2}-z_{j})

\displaystyle=(-1)^{N_{s}-m}e^{\frac{|L_{2}|^{2}}{4N_{s}}(N_{s}-m)-\frac{mL_{2}^{*}}{2N_{s}}Z}\prod_{j}g(-z_{j}).

\displaystyle\frac{F(Z+L_{1})}{F(Z)}

\displaystyle KL_{2}

.
It is also straightforward to see that under translation operation

t(\bm{L}_{1})t(\bm{L}_{2})\Psi(\{z_{i}\})=t(\bm{L}_{2})t(\bm{L}_{1})\Psi(\{z_{i}\})e^{i(\bm{L}_{1}\times\bm{L}_{2})\cdot\hat{z}}.

(56)

II
B. Laughlin wavefunction for a generic ideal flatband

From above we see the single particle LLL wavefunction (in symmetric gauge) can be conveniently written as

\psi(\bm{r})=e^{-\frac{|z|^{2}}{4}}e^{-\frac{|k|^{2}}{4}}\left[e^{i\frac{k^{*}z}{2}}\sigma\left(z+i\frac{A}{2\pi}k\right)\right],

(57)

where we re-introduced
A=2\pi l_{B}^{2}
but in our convention
l_{B}=1
it reduces to
A=2\pi
.
It contains three part. The first one is the factor
e^{-\frac{|z|^{2}}{4}}
which depends only on
\bm{r}
and makes sure the wavefunction decays at large distances.
The second term
e^{-\frac{|k|^{2}}{4}}
is a factor which depends solely on
\bm{k}
.
The the last term inside
[...]
has the nice property that it becomes a holomorphic function in
k
when multiplied by the factor
e^{-i\bm{k}\cdot\bm{r}}
, as

\bm{k}\cdot\bm{r}=\frac{1}{2}\left(k^{*}z+z^{*}k\right).

(58)

The manybody Laughlin wavefunction is given by

\displaystyle\Psi(\{z_{i}\})=

\displaystyle e^{iKZ}\left(\prod_{i=1}^{N_{e}}e^{-\frac{|z_{i}|^{2}}{4}}\right)\prod_{\nu=1}^{m}\sigma_{L}(Z-iZ_{\nu})\times

(59)

\displaystyle\prod_{i<j}\left[\sigma_{L}(z_{i}-z_{j})\right]^{m},

with
K
and
Z_{0}=\sum_{\nu}Z_{\nu}
satisfying Eq.(
55
).

In fact, as suggested in Ref.
Wang et al. 2021
, any ideal flatband wavefunction with Chern number
\mathcal{C}=1
can be written in a way similar to Eq.(
57
), namely

\psi_{\text{IFB}}(\bm{r})=F(\bm{r})N(\bm{k})\left[e^{i\frac{k^{*}z}{2}}\sigma\left(z+i\frac{A}{2\pi}k\right)\right]

(60)

where
F(\bm{r})
depends on the lattice details, and
N(\bm{k})
is some normalization factor which is less important as we already see in Eq.(
45
) and (
46
).

As an example, now let’s come back to the magic angle chiral limit flatband wavefunctoin for, say
\mathcal{C}=+1
,

\displaystyle\chi_{+,\bm{k}}(\bm{r})

\displaystyle=e^{i\bm{k}\cdot\bm{a}_{1}\frac{z-z_{0}}{a_{1}}}\frac{\vartheta_{1}\left(\frac{z-z_{0}}{a_{1}}-\frac{k}{b_{2}},\eta\right)}{\vartheta_{1}\left(\frac{z-z_{0}}{a_{1}},\eta\right)}\chi_{+}^{\kappa}(\bm{r})

(61)

\displaystyle=e^{i\bm{k}\cdot\bm{a}_{1}\frac{z-z_{0}}{a_{1}}}\frac{\vartheta_{1}\left(\frac{z-z_{0}+i\frac{A}{2\pi}k}{a_{1}},\eta\right)}{\vartheta_{1}\left(\frac{z-z_{0}}{a_{1}},\eta\right)}\chi_{+}^{\kappa}(\bm{r}).

It is more convenient to work with the wavefunction with origin shifted to
\bm{r}_{0}
, so we define a new
\tilde{\chi}_{+,\bm{k}}(\bm{r})=\chi_{+,\bm{k}}(\bm{r}+\bm{r}_{0})
. After some manipulation we rewrite it is as

\displaystyle\tilde{\chi}_{+,\bm{k}}(\bm{r})=

\displaystyle\frac{\chi_{+}^{\kappa}(\bm{r}+\bm{r}_{0})e^{-\frac{\pi a_{1}^{*}z^{2}}{2Aa_{1}}}}{\vartheta_{1}\left(\frac{z}{a_{1}},\eta\right)}\frac{\pi\vartheta^{\prime}(0,\eta)}{a_{1}}e^{\frac{Aa_{1}^{*}k^{2}}{8\pi a_{1}}}\times

(62)

\displaystyle\left[e^{i\frac{k^{*}z}{2}}\sigma\left(z+i\frac{A}{2\pi}k\right)\right].

From this expression we can easily read off the factors
F(\bm{r})
and
N(\bm{r})
introduced in Eq.(
60
) in this case.

As a directly generalization of Eq.(
59
), the manybody wavefunction ansatz can be written by modifying
F(\bm{r})
accordingly. Therefore, for the magic angle chiral limit flatband, the manybody Laughlin wavefunction ansatz is given by

\displaystyle\Psi_{\text{IFB}}(\{z_{i}\})=

\displaystyle e^{iKZ}\left(\prod_{i=1}^{N_{e}}\frac{\chi_{+}^{\kappa}(\bm{r}_{i}+\bm{r}_{0})e^{-\frac{\pi a_{1}^{*}z_{i}^{2}}{2Aa_{1}}}}{\vartheta_{1}\left(\frac{z_{i}}{a_{1}},\eta\right)}\right)\times

(63)

\displaystyle\prod_{\nu=1}^{m}\sigma_{L}(Z-iZ_{\nu})\prod_{i<j}\left[\sigma_{L}(z_{i}-z_{j})\right]^{m}.

Likewise, by using the quasiperiodic properties of
\sigma_{L}
and
\vartheta_{1}
, it is easy to see the values of
K
and
Z_{0}=\sum_{\nu}Z_{\nu}
must satisfy

\displaystyle e^{iKL_{1}}

\displaystyle=(-1)^{N_{s}+N_{1}}e^{i\frac{\pi L_{1}^{*}Z_{0}}{N_{s}A}+i\phi_{1}},

(64)

\displaystyle e^{iKL_{2}}

\displaystyle=(-1)^{N_{s}+N_{2}}e^{i\frac{\pi L_{2}^{*}Z_{0}}{N_{s}A}+i\phi_{2}},

in order to meet the periodic boundary conditions,
which are similar to Eq.(
55
).
