Abstract
. We introduce the Cox homotopy algorithm for solving a sparse system of polynomial equations on a compact toric variety X Σ X_{\Sigma} . The algorithm lends its name from a construction, described by Cox, of X Σ X_{\Sigma} as a GIT quotient X Σ = ( ℂ k ∖ Z ) ⫽ G X_{\Sigma}=(\mathbb{C}^{k}\setminus Z)\sslash G of a quasi-affine variety by the action of a reductive group. Our algorithm tracks paths in the total coordinate space ℂ k \mathbb{C}^{k} of X Σ X_{\Sigma} and can be seen as a homogeneous version of the standard polyhedral homotopy, which works on the dense torus of X Σ X_{\Sigma} . It furthermore generalizes the commonly used path tracking algorithms in (multi)projective spaces in that it tracks a set of homogeneous coordinates contained in the G G -orbit corresponding to each solution. The Cox homotopy combines the advantages of polyhedral homotopies and (multi)homogeneous homotopies, tracking only mixed volume many solutions and providing an elegant way to deal with solutions on or near the special divisors of X Σ X_{\Sigma} . In addition, the strategy may help to understand the deficiency of the root count for certain families of systems with respect to the BKK bound.

1.
Introduction

In this paper, our aim is to solve systems of polynomial equations
\hat{\mathcal{F}}(x)=0,
where
\hat{\mathcal{F}}=(\hat{f}_{1}(x),\ldots,\hat{f}_{n}(x))
and
x=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
. More specifically, we are concerned with the case where some isolated solutions of
\hat{\mathcal{F}}(x)=0
lie near infinity. To make this precise, one must consider solutions in a suitable compactification of
\mathbb{C}^{n}.
Common choices for the compactification are the complex projective space
\mathbb{P}^{n}
or, more generally, a product of projective spaces
\mathbb{P}^{n_{1}}\times\cdots\times\mathbb{P}^{n_{s}}
where
n_{1}+\cdots n_{s}=n.
In each of these compactifications, the number of solutions to a generic system is a fixed number given by Bézout’s theorem or its extension to a product of projective spaces. This observation gives the basis for numerical homotopy continuation methods—specifically, the (homogeneous) total degree homotopy and more general multihomogeneous homotopies. These may be used to find all isolated solutions to
\hat{\mathcal{F}}
starting from a suitably generic start system. Conventionally, the solutions to the (multi)homogeneous start system all lie in
\mathbb{C}^{n}.
To find solutions to
\hat{\mathcal{F}}
at infinity, it is standard to use homogeneous coordinates and work in a generic affine patch.

In practice, infinite or nearly-infinite solutions to our target system
\hat{\mathcal{F}}
present challenges for homotopy continuation. When tracking paths affinely, solutions of large magnitude are hard to estimate accurately. Even when homogeneous coordinates are used, these solutions are often singular or nearly-singular. Moreover, a large discrepancy between the number of start and target solutions may result in wasted computational resources.

The usual multihomogeneous start systems depend only on the degrees of
\hat{f}_{1},\ldots,\hat{f}_{n}.
In contrast, the polyhedral homotopy introduced in
[
23
,
44
]
takes the Newton polytopes of
\hat{f}_{1},\ldots,\hat{f}_{n}
into account.

Our Cox homotopy offers the following advantages:

1)

By working in a compact space, we reduce the risk of prematurely truncating paths (see Experiments

3)

2.1.
Background on homotopy continuation

A great variety of homotopy continuation methods exist, all of which adhere to the same setup—a system whose solutions are known (a
start system
) is used to solve some other system (a
target system
).
Popular implementations include
Bertini

[
5
]
,
HOM4PS-3

[
11
]
,
HomotopyContinuation.jl

[
9
]
,
NAG4M2

[
29
]
, and
PHCpack

[
43
]
. The numerical predictor-corrector methods lying at the core of homotopy continuation are treated at length in
[
1
]
. In the context of polynomial systems, the standard references are
[
33
,
37
,
6
]
.

For our purposes, a
homotopy

{\mathcal{H}}(x;\tau)
in variables
x=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
consists of polynomials
H_{1}(x;\tau),\ldots,H_{n}(x;\tau)
depending on an additional
tracking parameter

\tau
.
We use the convention that the start and target system are given by
{\mathcal{G}}(x)={\mathcal{H}}(x,1)
and
{\mathcal{F}}(x)={\mathcal{H}}(x,0),
respectively.
Thus, from any start solution
x_{0}\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
satisfying
{\mathcal{G}}(x_{0})=0,
homotopy continuation seeks to numerically approximate solution paths
x(\tau)
satisfying the initial-value problem

(1)

\begin{split}\displaystyle\frac{\partial\,{\mathcal{H}}}{\partial\,x}\,\displaystyle\frac{\partial\,x}{\partial\,\tau}=-\displaystyle\frac{\partial\,{\mathcal{H}}}{\partial\,\tau},\qquad\qquad x(1)=x_{0}.\end{split}

In the terminology of
[
21
, Def. 4.5]
, a solution path
x(\tau)
is said to be
trackable
on an interval
I\subset[0,1]
if the Jacobian
\partial\,{\mathcal{H}}/\partial x
is nonsingular at
(x(\tau),\tau)
for all
\tau\in I.

Typically, a randomization scheme such as the well-known
gamma trick
is used so that
x(\tau)
is trackable on
(0,1]
with probability-one.
If
x(\tau)
and
\tilde{x}(\tau)
are distinct paths trackable on
I\subset[0,1],
then the paths are disjoint in the sense that
x(\tau)\neq\tilde{x}(\tau)
for any
\tau\in I.
This follows from the inverse function theorem.

A trackable path on
(0,1]
converges to the
endpoint

\lim_{\tau\to 0^{+}}x(\tau),
provided that this limit exists, and otherwise
diverges.

To eliminate divergent solution paths, we may instead compute endpoints in some compactification of
\mathbb{C}^{n}
. It is common practice to work in (multi)projective spaces.

The first step for doing homotopy continuation in (multi)projective space is to
homogenize
the equations in an appropriate way. We will use the following convention in the rest of this paper to distinguish between homogeneous and non-homogeneous notation.

Convention 1
.

We denote by
t=(t_{1},\ldots,t_{n})
a set of affine/toric variables and by
x=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k})
a set of homogeneous variables. Equations or systems of equations that are not homogeneous will be indicated by a circumflex, which is dropped after homogenization. For instance, homogenizing the system
\hat{\mathcal{F}}(t)=0
given by
\hat{f}_{1}(t)=\cdots=\hat{f}_{n}(t)=0
, we obtain
{\mathcal{F}}(x)=0
given by
f_{1}(x)=\cdots=f_{n}(x)=0
.
∎

By homogenizing the equations
\hat{f}_{1}=\cdots=\hat{f}_{n}=0
in an appropriate way, one is essentially extending the given relations on
\mathbb{C}^{n}
to relations on
\mathbb{P}^{n_{1}}\times\cdots\times\mathbb{P}^{n_{s}}
. The obtained equations have well-defined vanishing loci in
\mathbb{P}^{n_{1}}\times\cdots\times\mathbb{P}^{n_{s}}
because of their homogeneity with respect to the appropriate multigrading. This is equivalent to reinterpreting each of the
\hat{f}_{i}
as a section of a line bundle on (multi)projective space, such that the given equation corresponds to the trivialization of this section in one of the affine charts. The graded pieces of the multihomogeneous coordinate ring of
\mathbb{P}^{n_{1}}\times\cdots\times\mathbb{P}^{n_{s}}
are in one-to-one correspondence with (isomorphism classes of) line bundles. For instance, for each
d\in\mathbb{Z}
,
\mathscr{O}_{\mathbb{P}^{n}}(d)
is the line bundle on
\mathbb{P}^{n}
whose global sections are represented by homogeneous polynomials (in the standard sense) of degree
d
. Here’s an example which shows the influence of the choice of compactification/homogenization on the solutions to the system.

Example 2.1
.

Consider
\hat{\mathcal{F}}(t)=(\hat{f}_{1}(t),\hat{f}_{2}(t))=0,
where

\displaystyle\hat{f}_{1}

\displaystyle=1+t_{1}+t_{2}+t_{1}t_{2}+t_{1}^{2}t_{2}+t_{1}^{3}t_{2},\qquad\hat{f}_{2}=2+t_{2}+t_{1}t_{2}+t_{1}^{2}t_{2}.

and

A

To get a unique representative for each point in
\mathbb{P}^{2},
we may augment

with a generic equation of the form

Genericity of

For solutions in

2.2.
Toric varieties and the Cox construction

In this subsection we recall the construction of a toric variety as a GIT quotient. This construction was described by Cox in
[
12
]
, and it is referred to as the
Cox construction
. We should mention that the result was described earlier in the analytic category by Audin, Delzant and Kirwan, see
[
3
, Chapter 6]
and references therein. For basic theory on toric varieties, the reader is referred to
[
14
, Chapters 1-4]
or
[
18
]
. Paragraph 5.5.1 in
[
40
]
contains a short introduction with some worked out examples.

Let
T=(\mathbb{C}^{*})^{n}
be the
algebraic torus
of dimension
n
. Its character and cocharacter lattices are denoted by
M=\Hom_{\mathbb{Z}}(T,\mathbb{C}^{*})\simeq\mathbb{Z}^{n}
and
N=\Hom_{\mathbb{Z}}(M,\mathbb{Z})
respectively. A normal toric variety
X
comes from a rational polyhedral fan
\Sigma
in
N_{\mathbb{R}}=N\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}\mathbb{R}
. We will denote the set of cones of dimension
d
in
\Sigma
by
\Sigma(d)
. We are interested in the case where
\Sigma
is the
normal fan

\Sigma_{\mathscr{P}}
of a full-dimensional lattice polytope
\mathscr{P}\subset M_{\mathbb{R}}=M\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}\mathbb{R}
. Such fans are
complete
, which means that
\bigcup_{\sigma\in\Sigma}\sigma=N_{\mathbb{R}}
, and the corresponding toric variety
X
is compact. We will sometimes denote
X=X_{\Sigma}
to emphasize the correspondence between
X
and its fan.

Each
\rho_{i}\in\Sigma(1)
has a unique primitive ray generator
u_{i}\in N
. It is convenient to collect the
u_{i}
in a matrix

F=[u_{1}~\cdots~u_{k}]\in\mathbb{Z}^{n\times k}.

In our context, the
u_{i}
represent inward pointing
facet normals
of the polytope
\mathscr{P}
. For this reason, we call
F
the
facet matrix
. This matrix represents a lattice homomorphism
F:N^{\prime}\rightarrow N
, where
N^{\prime}=\mathbb{Z}^{k}
, which is compatible with the fans
\Sigma^{\prime}
and
\Sigma
in
N_{\mathbb{R}}^{\prime}
and
N_{\mathbb{R}}
respectively. Here
\Sigma^{\prime}
is the fan of
\mathbb{C}^{k}
(i.e. the positive orthant in
\mathbb{R}^{k}
and all its faces), with some cones missing. It follows that
F
gives a toric morphism
\pi:X_{\Sigma^{\prime}}\rightarrow X_{\Sigma}
, where
X_{\Sigma^{\prime}}=\mathbb{C}^{k}\setminus Z
and
Z
is a union of coordinate subspaces. In this setting, the affine space
\mathbb{C}^{k}
is called the
total coordinate space
and
Z
is the
base locus
. The base locus is defined by a monomial ideal
B
in the coordinate ring
S=\mathbb{C}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{k}]
of
\mathbb{C}^{k}
:

B=\left\langle\prod_{i\text{ s.t.\ }\rho_{i}\not\subset\sigma}x_{i}~|~\sigma\in\Sigma(n)\right\rangle\subset S\quad\text{and}\quad Z=V_{\mathbb{C}^{k}}(B).

The ideal
B
is also called the
irrelevant ideal
of
S
.
Restricting the morphism
\pi
to the torus
(\mathbb{C}^{*})^{k}
we get the Laurent monomial map

(2)

\pi|_{(\mathbb{C}^{*})^{k}}=F\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}\mathbb{C}^{*}:(\mathbb{C}^{*})^{k}\rightarrow T

where
(z_{1},\ldots,z_{k})\overset{\pi}{\mapsto}(z^{F_{1,:}},\ldots,z^{F_{n,:}})
(this uses the short notation
z^{a}=z_{1}^{a_{i}}\cdots z_{k}^{a_{k}}
and
F_{i,:}
for the
i
-th row of
F
). The kernel of
\pi|_{(\mathbb{C}^{*})^{k}}
(as a group homomorphism) is a subgroup
G\subset(\mathbb{C}^{*})^{k}
which acts on
\mathbb{C}^{k}\setminus Z
and the morphism
\pi
is constant on
G
-orbits. The following theorem uses some terminology for GIT quotients from
[
14
, Section 5.0]
.

Theorem 2.1

(
[
12
]
)
.

The morphism
\pi:\mathbb{C}^{k}\setminus Z\rightarrow X_{\Sigma}
coming from
F=[u_{1}~\cdots~u_{k}]
is an almost geometric quotient for the action of
G
on
\mathbb{C}^{k}\setminus Z
. Moreover, the open subset
U\subset X_{\Sigma}
for which
\pi|_{\pi^{-1}(U)}
is a geometric quotient is the largest simplicial toric subvariety of
X
, which is such that
(X_{\Sigma}\setminus U)
has codimension at least 3 in
X_{\Sigma}
.

Important for us is that this means that there is a
very large
Zariski open subset
U\subset X_{\Sigma}
such that
G
-orbits in
\pi^{-1}(U)
are in one-to-one correspondence with points of
U
. The fan of
U
is the subfan of
\Sigma
consisting of all its simplicial cones.

Example 2.2
.

A familiar example is given by the construction of the projective space
X_{\Sigma}=\mathbb{P}^{n}
as a quotient of
\mathbb{C}^{n+1}\setminus\{0\}
, in which case
\pi:\mathbb{C}^{n+1}\setminus\{0\}\rightarrow\mathbb{P}^{n}
is given by
(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n+1})\mapsto(x_{1}:x_{2}:\cdots:x_{n+1})
. Since
\mathbb{P}^{n}
is simplicial, this quotient is geometric, i. e.
X_{\Sigma}=U
.
∎

To associate the ring
S
(with its irrelevant ideal
B
) to our toric variety
X_{\Sigma}
, we equip it with a grading such that the vanishing locus of homogeneous elements in
\mathbb{C}^{k}\setminus Z
is stable under the action of
G
. The grading is by the
divisor class group

\Cl(X_{\Sigma})
of
X_{\Sigma}
, which is the group of Weil divisors modulo linear equivalence. For toric varieties, this group is easy to describe explicitly. Let
D_{1},\ldots,D_{k}
be the torus invariant prime divisors on
X_{\Sigma}
corresponding to
\rho_{1},\ldots,\rho_{k}
respectively. We have the exact sequence

(3)

0\rightarrow M\overset{F^{\top}}{\longrightarrow}\bigoplus_{i=1}^{k}\mathbb{Z}\cdot D_{i}\overset{\mathcal{P}}{\longrightarrow}\Cl(X_{\Sigma})\rightarrow 0,

where the map
F^{\top}
sends a character to its divisor (we will think of this map as a lattice map
\mathbb{Z}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}^{k}
given by the transpose of our matrix
F
) and
\mathcal{P}
takes a torus invariant divisor to its class in
\Cl(X_{\Sigma})
(see
[
14
, Theorem 4.1.3]
). This shows that
\Cl(X_{\Sigma})\simeq\mathbb{Z}^{k}/\im F^{\top}
and every element of
\Cl(X_{\Sigma})
can be written as the class
[D]
of some torus invariant divisor
D=\sum_{i=1}^{k}a_{i}D_{i}
. For an element
\alpha=[\sum_{i=1}^{k}a_{i}D_{i}]\in\Cl(X_{\Sigma})
, we define the vector subspace

S_{\alpha}=\bigoplus_{F^{\top}m+a\geq 0}\mathbb{C}\cdot x^{F^{\top}m+a},

where the sum ranges over all
m\in M
satisfying
\langle{u_{i},m}\rangle+a_{i}\geq 0
(here
\langle{\cdot,\cdot}\rangle
denotes the usual pairing between
N\simeq\mathbb{Z}^{n}
and its dual
M\simeq\mathbb{Z}^{n}
). One can check that this definition is independent of the chosen representative for
\alpha
and if
f=\sum_{F^{\top}m+a\geq 0}c_{m}x^{F^{\top}m+a}\in S_{\alpha}
, then for
g\in G\subset(\mathbb{C}^{*})^{k}
we have

f(g\cdot x)=\sum_{F^{\top}m+a\geq 0}c_{m}(g\cdot x)^{F^{\top}m+a}=g^{a}f(x).

It follows that
f\in S_{\alpha}
has a well defined vanishing locus

V_{X_{\Sigma}}(f)=\{p\in X_{\Sigma}~|~f(x)=0\textup{ for some }x\in\pi^{-1}(p)\}

and this definition extends trivially to
V_{X_{\Sigma}}(f_{1},\ldots,f_{s})=\bigcap_{i=1}^{s}V_{X_{\Sigma}}(f_{i})
for elements
f_{i}\in S_{\alpha_{i}}
. An element
f\in S_{\alpha}
is called
homogeneous of degree
\alpha
. The ring
S
, with its grading by
\Cl(X_{\Sigma})
and its irrelevant ideal
B
, is called the
Cox ring
of
X_{\Sigma}
.
In conclusion, we summarize some terminology related to the Cox construction of
X_{\Sigma}
in the table below.

\begin{matrix}&\text{Algebra}&&\text{Geometry}\\
\hline\cr\text{Cox ring}&S=\bigoplus_{\alpha\in\Cl(X_{\Sigma})}S_{\alpha}&\overset{\maxspec(\cdot)}{\longrightarrow}&\mathbb{C}^{k}&\text{total coordinate space}\\
\text{irrelevant ideal}&B&\overset{V_{\mathbb{C}^{k}}(\cdot)}{\longrightarrow}&Z&\text{base locus}\\
\text{class group}&\Cl(X_{\Sigma})&\overset{\Hom_{\mathbb{Z}}(\cdot,\mathbb{C}^{*})}{\longrightarrow}&G&\text{reductive group}\end{matrix}

2.3.
Homogenization of sparse polynomial systems

The standard homogenization used for sending polynomials to the multihomogeneous coordinate ring of
\mathbb{P}^{n_{1}}\times\cdots\times\mathbb{P}^{n_{s}}
generalizes nicely for the compact toric varieties
X
we consider in this paper and their Cox ring
S
. Line bundles on
X
are in one-to-one correspondence with elements of the Picard group
\Pic(X)\subset\Cl(X)
, consisting of Cartier divisors modulo linear equivalence
[
14
, Chapter 4]
. Sections of these line bundles are homogeneous polynomials in
S
.

The homogenization procedure we will use is described in detail in Section 3 of
[
39
]
. We briefly recall how this works. Let
\hat{f}_{1},\ldots,\hat{f}_{n}\in\mathbb{C}[M]=\mathbb{C}[t_{1}^{\pm 1},\ldots,t_{n}^{\pm 1}]
be a given set of Laurent polynomials and let
\mathscr{A}_{1},\ldots,\mathscr{A}_{n}
be their supports. That is,
\hat{f}_{i}
can be written as
\hat{f}_{i}=\sum_{m\in\mathscr{A}_{i}}c_{i,m}t^{m}
where
c_{i,m}\neq 0
. For
i=1,\ldots,n
, let
\mathscr{P}_{i}=\conv(\mathscr{A}_{i})
be the Newton polytope of
\hat{f}_{i}
(see for instance
[
13
, Chapter 7, §1]
).
The Minkowski sum of these polytopes is denoted by
\mathscr{P}=\mathscr{P}_{1}+\cdots+\mathscr{P}_{n}
and it is assumed to have dimension
n
. The normal fan
\Sigma_{\mathscr{P}}
of
\mathscr{P}
gives the toric variety
X=X_{\Sigma_{\mathscr{P}}}
. For each
i
, there is a canonical way of associating a nef torus invariant Cartier divisor
D_{\mathscr{P}_{i}}=\sum_{j=1}^{k}a_{i,j}D_{i}
to
\mathscr{P}_{i}
. The class of this divisor in
\Pic(X)
is denoted by
\alpha_{i}=[D_{\mathscr{P}_{i}}]\in\Pic(X)
. The vector space of Laurent polynomials with Newton polytope
\mathscr{P}_{i}
is the vector space of sections of the vector bundle
\mathscr{O}_{X}(\alpha_{i})
on
X

[
14
, Proposition 4.3.3]
. Moreover, this vector space can be identified with the degree
\alpha_{i}
part of the Cox ring
S
of
X

[
14
, Proposition 5.3.7]
. In summary, we have

\bigoplus_{m\in\mathscr{P}_{i}\cap M}\mathbb{C}\cdot t^{m}\simeq\Gamma(X,\mathscr{O}_{X}(\alpha_{i}))\simeq S_{\alpha_{i}}.

The
homogenization to the Cox ring
of
\hat{f}_{i}
is given by the coefficients
a_{i,j}
defining
D_{\mathscr{P}_{i}}
. Explicitly, we obtain the homogeneous polynomials
f_{i}
from
\hat{f}_{i}
by

(4)

\hat{f}_{i}=\sum_{m\in\mathscr{A}_{i}}c_{i,m}t^{m}\mapsto f_{i}=\sum_{m\in\mathscr{A}_{i}}c_{i,m}x^{F^{\top}m+a_{i}},

where
F
the facet matrix. Note that since
\mathscr{A}_{i}\subset\mathscr{P}_{i}\cap M
, we have that
F^{\top}m+a_{i}\geq 0
, so that the
f_{i}
are indeed polynomials.

We will think of the
n
-tuple
(f_{1},\ldots,f_{n})
as a section of the rank
n
vector bundle
\mathscr{O}_{X}(\alpha_{1})\oplus\cdots\oplus\mathscr{O}_{X}(\alpha_{n})
on
X
. The zero locus of this section is the vanishing locus
V_{X}(f_{1},\ldots,f_{n})
. It contains the points defined by
\hat{f}_{1}=\cdots=\hat{f}_{n}=0
in
T
, denoted by
V_{T}(\hat{f}_{1},\ldots,\hat{f}_{n})
.
Since
\mathscr{O}_{X}(\alpha_{1})\oplus\cdots\oplus\mathscr{O}_{X}(\alpha_{n})
is a rank
n
vector bundle on a variety of dimension
n
, the expected dimension of
V_{X}(f_{1},\ldots,f_{n})
is 0. A well-known result by Bernstein, Khovanskii and Kushnirenko tells us how many points to expect. The statement involves the
mixed volume

\MV(\mathscr{P}_{1},\ldots,\mathscr{P}_{n})
of the polytopes
\mathscr{P}_{1},\ldots,\mathscr{P}_{n}
.

Theorem 2.2

(BKK Theorem)
.

Let
f_{i}\in S_{\alpha_{i}}
be as above. The variety
V_{X}(f_{1},\ldots,f_{n})
contains at most
\MV(\mathscr{P}_{1},\ldots,\mathscr{P}_{n})
isolated points on
X
. If
V_{X}(f_{1},\ldots,f_{n})
is finite, then it consists of exactly
\MV(\mathscr{P}_{1},\ldots,\mathscr{P}_{n})
points, counting multiplicities. For generic choices of the coefficients of the
f_{i}
, the number of roots in
T\subset X
is exactly equal to
\MV(\mathscr{P}_{1},\ldots,\mathscr{P}_{n})
and all roots have multiplicity one.

Proof.

See
[
18
, §5.5]
.
∎

The number
\MV(\mathscr{P}_{1},\ldots,\mathscr{P}_{n})
is often referred to as the
BKK bound
. We are mainly interested in the case where
(f_{1},\ldots,f_{n})
defines points
outside of

T
, that is, on the boundary
X\setminus T
of the torus in

.

3.
G
-orbits in the Cox construction

Let
X=X_{\Sigma}
be a compact toric variety corresponding to a complete fan
\Sigma
and let
U\subset X
be as in Theorem
2.1
. As mentioned in the introduction, our homotopy algorithm will track a set of Cox coordinates for a point
p\in U\subset X
by slicing the
G
-orbit
\pi^{-1}(p)
with a linear space of complementary dimension. In order to understand what this dimension is and how many representatives
there are in such a linear space,
this section is devoted to an explicit description of the dimension and degree of the projective closure of
G
-orbits
G\cdot z\subset\mathbb{C}^{k}\setminus Z
in the Cox construction.

3.1.
Orbit parametrization

Taking
\Hom_{\mathbb{Z}}(-,\mathbb{C}^{*})
of the exact sequence (
3
)
gives the explicit description for
G
,

G=\ker\Hom_{\mathbb{Z}}(F^{\top},\mathbb{C}^{*})=\{g\in(\mathbb{C}^{*})^{k}~|~g^{F_{1,:}}=\cdots=g^{F_{n,:}}=1\},

as a subgroup of
(\mathbb{C}^{*})^{k}
. Our first goal is to parametrize the orbit

G\cdot z=\{g\cdot z=(g_{1}z_{1},\ldots,g_{k}z_{k})\in(\mathbb{C}^{*})^{k}~|~g\in G\}

for
z\in\mathbb{C}^{k}\setminus Z
.
Note that if
\Cl(X)\simeq\mathbb{Z}^{k-n}
is free, then
G=\Hom_{\mathbb{Z}}(\Cl(X),\mathbb{C}^{*})\simeq(\mathbb{C}^{*})^{k-n}
is a torus. In general,
G
is a
quasitorus
of dimension
k-n
, i.e. it is isomorphic to the direct sum of a torus and a finite abelian group.

To find the orbit parametrization of
G\cdot z
, the first step is to compute the Smith normal form of the transpose of the facet matrix
F^{\top}\in\mathbb{Z}^{k\times n}
:

PF^{\top}Q=\text{diag}(s_{1},\ldots,s_{n}),

where
\text{diag}(s_{1},\ldots,s_{n})
is a diagonal matrix of size
k\times n
with the invariant factors
s_{i}
of
F^{\top}
on its diagonal. We note that
s_{i}\neq 0
since
F
comes from a complete fan.
The submatrix
P^{\prime\prime}
of
P
containing the last
k-n
rows of
P
is a
\mathbb{Z}
-basis for the kernel of
F:\mathbb{Z}^{k}\rightarrow N
. We denote the submatrix of
P
containing its first
n
rows by
P^{\prime}
. We have that

\Cl(X)\simeq\mathbb{Z}^{k}/\im~F^{\top}\simeq\mathbb{Z}/s_{1}\mathbb{Z}\oplus\cdots\oplus\mathbb{Z}/s_{n}\mathbb{Z}\oplus\mathbb{Z}^{k-n}.

Note that, since the matrix
P
is a representation of the map
\mathcal{P}
from (
3
), we can write this isomorphism explicitly as

(5)

[\sum_{i=1}^{k}a_{i}D_{i}]\mapsto(\underbrace{(P^{\prime}a)_{1}+s_{1}\mathbb{Z},\ldots,(P^{\prime}a)_{n}+s_{n}\mathbb{Z}}_{\mathbb{Z}/s_{1}\mathbb{Z}\oplus\cdots\oplus\mathbb{Z}/s_{n}\mathbb{Z}},\underbrace{(P^{\prime\prime}a)_{1},\ldots,(P^{\prime\prime}a)_{k-n}}_{\mathbb{Z}^{k-n}})

where
(P^{\prime}a)_{i}
is the
i
-th entry of the matrix-vector product
P^{\prime}a
, and likewise for
P^{\prime\prime}a
. We also have that

G=\Hom_{\mathbb{Z}}(\Cl(X),\mathbb{C}^{*})=W_{1}\oplus\cdots\oplus W_{n}\oplus(\mathbb{C}^{*})^{k-n}

where
W_{i}\subset\mathbb{C}^{*}
is the multiplicative group of
s_{i}
-th roots of unity.
The inclusion
G\hookrightarrow(\mathbb{C}^{*})^{k}
is the dual map
\mathcal{P}^{\vee}=\Hom_{\mathbb{Z}}(\mathcal{P},\mathbb{C}^{*})
of (
5
), given by

(6)

G\simeq\left(\bigoplus_{i=1}^{n}W_{i}\right)\oplus(\mathbb{C}^{*})^{k-n}\rightarrow(\mathbb{C}^{*})^{k},\quad(w,\lambda)\mapsto(w^{P_{:,1}^{\prime}}\lambda^{P_{:,1}^{\prime\prime}},\ldots,w^{P_{:,k}^{\prime}}\lambda^{P_{:,k}^{\prime\prime}}),

where
P_{:,i}^{\prime}
denotes the
i
-th column of
P^{\prime}
and likewise for
P^{\prime\prime}
.
We conclude that
G\subset(\mathbb{C}^{*})^{k}
is a union of tori isomorphic to

T_{P^{\prime\prime}}=\{(\lambda^{P_{:,1}^{\prime\prime}},\ldots,\lambda^{P_{:,k}^{\prime\prime}})\in(\mathbb{C}^{*})^{k}~|~\lambda\in(\mathbb{C}^{*})^{k-n}\}\simeq(\mathbb{C}^{*})^{k-n}.

Moreover, the following statement follows immediately from this discussion.

Lemma 3.1
.

For
z\in\mathbb{C}^{k}\setminus Z
, the orbit
G\cdot z
of
z=(z_{1},\ldots,z_{k})
is parametrized by the map

(7)

\left(\bigoplus_{i=1}^{n}W_{i}\right)\oplus(\mathbb{C}^{*})^{k-n}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}^{k}\setminus Z\quad\text{given by}\quad(w,\lambda)\mapsto(w^{P_{:,1}^{\prime}}\lambda^{P_{:,1}^{\prime\prime}}z_{1},\ldots,w^{P_{:,k}^{\prime}}\lambda^{P_{:,k}^{\prime\prime}}z_{k}).

Remark 3.2
.

Note that the image of (
6
) is the orbit
G\cdot z
of
z=(1,\ldots,1)
.

For a subset
\mathcal{W}\subset\mathbb{C}^{k}\setminus Z
, we denote its Zariski closure in
\mathbb{P}^{k}
by

\overline{\mathcal{W}}=\overline{\{(1:z_{1}:\cdots:z_{k})\in\mathbb{P}^{k}~|~(z_{1},\ldots,z_{k})\in\mathcal{W}\}}\subset\mathbb{P}^{k}.

By applying Lemma
3.1
to
\overline{G\cdot z}
, we immediately realize the following corollary.

Corollary 3.3
.

For
z\in\mathbb{C}^{k}\setminus Z
, the dimension and degree of the projective variety
\overline{G\cdot z}
depend only on which
(\mathbb{C}^{*})^{k}
-orbit
z
belongs to. Equivalently, they only depend on the set of indices
\mathscr{I}=\{i~|~z_{i}\neq 0\}
.

3.2.

By Corollary
3.3
, the dimension of

is constant on the dense torus

3.3.
Orbit degree

For a finite set
\mathscr{A}=\{\alpha_{0},\ldots,\alpha_{s}\}\subset\mathbb{Z}^{\ell}
we use the notation
X_{\mathscr{A}}\subset\mathbb{P}^{s}
for the projective toric variety obtained as the closure of the image of
(\mathbb{C}^{*})^{\ell}\rightarrow\mathbb{P}^{s}
, where
(t_{1},\ldots,t_{\ell})\mapsto(t^{\alpha_{1}}:\cdots:t^{\alpha_{s}})
.
We associate to
\mathscr{A}
the matrix
A
of
\ell
-tuples in
\mathscr{A}
, i.e.
A=[\alpha_{0}~\cdots~\alpha_{s}]:\mathbb{Z}^{s+1}\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}^{\ell}
.
One may compute the degree of the projective variety
X_{\mathscr{A}}
using Kushnirenko’s Theorem.

Theorem 3.5

(Kushnirenko’s theorem
[
28
]
)
.

Let
X_{\mathscr{A}}\subset\mathbb{P}^{s}
be the projective toric variety defined by

A=\begin{bmatrix}1&\cdots&1\\
m_{0}&\cdots&m_{s}\end{bmatrix}:\mathbb{Z}^{s+1}\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}^{\ell}.

Let
\Delta=\conv(m_{0},\ldots,m_{s})\subset\mathbb{R}^{\ell-1}
be the polytope obtained by taking the convex hull of the lattice points
m_{0},\ldots,m_{s}\in\mathbb{Z}^{\ell-1}
and suppose that
\Delta
has dimension
\ell-1
. Then,

\deg X_{\mathscr{A}}=\frac{(\ell-1)!}{q}\Vol(\Delta),

where
\Vol(\cdot)
denotes the Euclidean volume and
q
is the lattice index of
\im([m_{0}~\cdots~m_{s}]:\mathbb{Z}^{s+1}\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}^{\ell-1})
in
\mathbb{Z}^{\ell-1}
. That is, for almost all choices of coefficients
c_{ij}\in\mathbb{C}
, the system of equations

\sum_{j=0}^{s}c_{ij}\lambda^{m_{j}}=0,\qquad i=\ell-1

has exactly
(\ell-1)!\Vol(\Delta)
solutions
\lambda\in(\mathbb{C}^{*})^{\ell-1}
.

With the notation of Subsection
2.2
, let
\mathscr{I}\subset\{1,\ldots,k\}
be a subset of indices such that there is a cone
\sigma\in\Sigma
containing each
\rho_{i}\in\Sigma(1),i\notin\mathscr{I}
. Equivalently,
\mathscr{I}
is such that
\textup{cone}(e_{i},i\notin\mathscr{I})\in\Sigma^{\prime}
, where
e_{i}
is the
i

4.
Cox homotopies: coefficient-parameter theory and algorithms

As in the previous section,
X=X_{\Sigma}
is an
n
-dimensional toric variety such that
\pi:\mathbb{C}^{k}\setminus Z\to X
is an almost geometric quotient which is constant over
G
-orbits on
\mathbb{C}^{k}\setminus Z
.
Moreover, on the dense open subset
U\subset X
,
\pi_{\pi^{-1}(U)}:\pi^{-1}(U)\rightarrow U
is geometric.

This section describes our homotopy algorithm for solving polynomial systems on
X
by tracking points in the total coordinate space
\mathbb{C}^{k}
.
For any
p\in U
, we will slice the
G
-orbit
\pi^{-1}(p)
with a general linear space of dimension
n
to get degree-many representatives for
\pi^{-1}(p)
.
Theorem
4.1
establishes that the orbits in our homotopy remain disjoint.
Thus, we may track only one representative per orbit when the target system is generic in the sense of Theorem
2.2
.
In Section
4.2
, we consider the case of a non-generic target system.
This presents a subtlety not encountered in the multihomogeneous case—namely, some paths in the total space
\mathbb{C}^{k}
may either diverge or converge to a point in the base locus (see Example
4.1
).
We propose an endgame in Section
4.2
for finding a path whose endpoint represents a point in
X.

Pseudocode for the main algorithm and subroutines are in Section
4.3
.
Finally, in Subsection
4.4
we outline the orthogonal patching strategy considered in Experiment
2
.

4.1.
Coefficient-parameter homotopy in the dense torus

As our homotopy is akin to a coefficient-parameter homotopy, we consider the
family
of all systems with fixed supports
\mathscr{A}=(\mathscr{A}_{1},\ldots,\mathscr{A}_{n})
in
M
(cf. Subsection
2.3
). Thus, we consider
\hat{h}_{i}(t;c)=\sum_{m\in\mathscr{A}_{i}}c_{i,m}t^{m},\,i=1,\ldots,n,
where the parametric coefficients
c_{i,m}
may vary. We denote the affine space of parameters by
\mathbb{C}^{\mathscr{A}}
. We assume hereafter that the Minkowski sum
\mathscr{P}=\mathscr{P}_{1}+\cdots+\mathscr{P}_{n}
, where
\mathscr{P}_{i}=\conv(\mathscr{A}_{i}),
has full dimension
n,
so that the BKK number
\MV(\mathscr{P}_{1},\ldots,\mathscr{P}_{n})
from Theorem
2.2
is positive.

The homotopy considered in our main algorithm (Algorithm
2
) is given by

(12)

\left({\mathcal{H}}\left(x;\tau\right),\mathcal{L}(x)\right)=0,

where
\mathcal{L}(x)=Ax+b
determines an (affine) linear space
L=\mathcal{L}^{-1}(0)=\{x\in\mathbb{C}^{k}\mid\mathcal{L}(x)=0\},
the path
\phi:[0,1]\to\mathbb{C}^{\mathscr{A}}
is assumed to be smooth, and
{\mathcal{H}}
is obtained by homogenizing

(13)

\hat{\mathcal{H}}(t;\tau)=\left(\hat{h}_{1}\left(t;\phi(\tau)\right),\ldots,\hat{h}_{n}\left(t;\phi(\tau)\right)\right)=0.

We also need that
\phi,

A,
and
b
satisfy certain genericity conditions as outlined in Theorem
4.1
.
We will refer to such homotopies as
Cox homotopies.

Theorem
4.1
gives the basis for Algorithm
2
. To prove this theorem, it is natural to consider the incidence variety defined for fixed
L
by

(14)

\widehat{\mathcal{V}}_{\mathscr{A},L}=\left\{(x,c)\in\left(\left(\mathbb{C}^{k}\setminus Z\right)\cap L\right)\times\mathbb{C}^{\mathscr{A}}\mid{\mathcal{F}}(x;c)=0\right\}.

Solution paths for the Cox homotopy in Equation (
12
) correspond to
lifts
of the parameter path
\phi
to the variety
\widehat{\mathcal{V}}_{\mathscr{A},L}.

For generic
\phi,

A,
and
b,
Theorem
4.1
implies that there are exactly
d\delta
such paths, where
d
denote the degree of the
G
-orbit closure
\overline{G\cdot(e_{1}+\cdots+e_{k})}.

Note that

may be computed using Remark
3.7
.

Theorem 4.1
.

There exist Zariski open subsets
\mathcal{U}_{\mathscr{A}}\subset\mathbb{C}^{\mathscr{A}}
and
\mathcal{U}_{A,b}\subset\mathbb{C}^{(k-n)\times k}\times\mathbb{C}^{k-n}
such that for all
c^{*}\in\mathcal{U}_{\mathscr{A}}
and all
(A,b)\in\mathcal{U}_{A,b}
, with
\mathcal{L}(x)=Ax+b
, we have

(1)

V_{T}(\hat{\mathcal{H}}(t;c^{*}))=\{\zeta_{1},\ldots,\zeta_{\delta}\}
consists of
\delta
points,

(2)

V_{(\mathbb{C}^{*})^{k}}({\mathcal{H}}(x;c^{*}),\mathcal{L}(x))=\{z_{ij}~|~i=1,\ldots,\delta,j=1,\ldots,d\}
consists of
d\delta
points,

(3)

for some labeling of these points,
\pi(z_{ij})=\zeta_{i}
for all
i,j
.

For fixed
(A,b)\in\mathcal{U}_{A,b}
and a smooth function
\phi:[0,1]\rightarrow\mathcal{U}_{\mathscr{A}}
,

(4)

the homotopy
\hat{\mathcal{H}}(t;\phi(\tau))=0
has
\delta
trackable paths
\zeta_{i}:[0,1]\to(\mathbb{C}^{*})^{n},i=1,\dots,\delta,

(5)

the homotopy
({\mathcal{H}}(x;\phi(\tau)),\mathcal{L}(x))=0
has
d\delta
trackable paths
z_{ij}:[0,1]\to(\mathbb{C}^{*})^{k},

i=1,\ldots,\delta,j=1,\ldots,d
,

(6)

after relabeling, we have that
\pi\circ z_{ij}=\zeta_{i}
for all
i,j
.

Proof.

The variety
V_{(\mathbb{C}^{*})^{k}}({\mathcal{H}}(x;c^{*}))
is a union of
G
-orbits, which have dimension
k-n
by Lemma
3.4
.
We take
\mathcal{U}_{A,b}
such that
G\cdot(e_{1}+\cdots+e_{k})\cap L
always consists of
d
points for
L=\mathcal{L}^{-1}(0)
.
Theorem
2.2
, together with Remark
3.7
, implies that the coordinate projection
\pi_{\mathscr{A}}:\widehat{\mathcal{V}}_{\mathscr{A},L}\to\mathbb{C}^{\mathscr{A}}
is a dominant map with generically finite fibers.
More precisely,
\pi_{\mathscr{A}}
is a branched cover of degree
d\delta,
meaning that the fiber
\pi_{\mathscr{A}}^{-1}(c)
consists of
d\delta
points for all
c\in\mathcal{U}_{\mathscr{A}},
where
\mathbb{C}^{\mathscr{A}}\setminus\mathcal{U}_{\mathscr{A}}
denotes the

of
\pi_{\mathscr{A}}.

The set
\mathcal{U}_{\mathscr{A}}
is Zariski open by
[
36
, Theorem 2.29]
.
We also observe that
\pi_{\mathscr{A}}
has a factorization induced by
\pi
. That is, we may write
\pi_{\mathscr{A}}=\pi_{\mathscr{A}}^{1}\circ\pi_{\mathscr{A}}^{2}
where
\pi_{\mathscr{A}}^{1}
and
\pi_{\mathscr{A}}^{2}
are branched covers of degree

respectively.
So far we have shown (1)–(3).
Now, whenever

and

the derivative

is an invertible linear map by
[

.
This gives (5).
Similarly, (4) follows for

by considering

and applying the chain rule.
For (6), we simply note that
(A,b)\in\mathcal{U}_{A,b}
implies there are exactly
d

for each
\zeta_{i}
satisfying
\zeta_{i}=\pi\circ z_{ij}.

∎

Remark 4.2
.

It is possible to allow the linear space
L
to change as
\tau
moves from
1
to
0
. In other words, we could consider a Cox homotopy given by
({\mathcal{H}}(x;\tau),\mathcal{L}_{\tau}(x))=0
where
L(\tau)=\mathcal{L}_{\tau}^{-1}(0)
is a sufficiently generic, continuous path in the Grassmannian
G(n,k)
. In Subsection
4.4
we will exploit this observation to propose the strategy of
orthogonal slicing
for Cox homotopies.

Remark 4.3
.

Since the complement of
\mathcal{U}_{\mathscr{A}}
is of complex codimension at least 1 in
\mathbb{C}^{\mathscr{A}}
, the image of a general 1-real-dimensional parameter path
\phi:[0,1]\rightarrow\mathbb{C}^{\mathscr{A}}
with
c_{0}=\phi(0)\in\mathcal{U}_{\mathscr{A}}
,
c_{1}=\phi(1)\in\mathcal{U}_{\mathscr{A}}
is contained in
\mathcal{U}_{\mathscr{A}}
. A standard trick for obtaining such a general path is by setting
\phi(\tau)=(1-\tau)c_{0}+\gamma\tau c_{1}
, where
\gamma\in\mathbb{C}
is a random constant. This is known as the
gamma trick
, see for instance
[
37
, Lemma 7.1.3]
. In this case, the Cox homotopy
({\mathcal{H}},\mathcal{L})
is given by
{\mathcal{H}}(x;\tau)=(1-\tau){\mathcal{G}}+\gamma\tau{\mathcal{F}}
, where
{\mathcal{F}}(x)={\mathcal{H}}(x;0)
and
{\mathcal{G}}(x)={\mathcal{H}}(x;1)
. If the start system
({\mathcal{G}},\mathcal{L})
is suitably generic, we can set
\gamma=1
. Homotopies obtained in this way are often called
straight line homotopies

[
6
, Sec 2.1]
.

An important consequence of Theorem
4.1
is that for parameter paths
\phi:[0,1]\rightarrow\mathcal{U}_{\mathscr{A}}
it is sufficient to track only
\delta=\MV(P_{1},\ldots,P_{n})
paths in the Cox homotopy
({\mathcal{H}},\mathcal{L})
to track the paths defined by
{\mathcal{H}}(x;\tau)
on
X
. Indeed, for
i=1,\ldots,\delta
, it suffices to track only one of the paths
z_{ij}(\tau),j=1,\ldots,d
for
\tau
going from 1 to 0, since all of these will land on a representative of the same
G
-orbit when
\tau=0
.

4.2.
Degenerating orbits and a specialized endgame

For a set
\mathscr{A}=(\mathscr{A}_{1},\ldots,\mathscr{A}_{n})
of supports, we fix a smooth parameter path
\phi:[0,1]\rightarrow\mathbb{C}^{\mathscr{A}}
and consider the corresponding homotopy
\hat{\mathcal{H}}(t;\tau)
. We denote
\hat{\mathcal{F}}(t)=\hat{\mathcal{H}}(t;0),~\hat{\mathcal{G}}(t)=\hat{\mathcal{H}}(t;1)
and
{\mathcal{F}},{\mathcal{G}},{\mathcal{H}}
for the associated homogeneous counterparts. We assume that
\hat{\mathcal{G}}
defines
\delta=\MV(\mathscr{P}_{1},\ldots,\mathscr{P}_{n})
isolated solutions in
T\subset X
, but
\hat{\mathcal{F}}
does not. That is,
|V_{T}(\hat{\mathcal{F}})|<\delta
.

In this setup, if
\phi
is suitably generic, Theorem
4.1
tells us that
\hat{\mathcal{H}}(t;\tau),~\tau\in(0,1]
defines
\delta
disjoint paths
\tau\mapsto\zeta_{i}(\tau),i=1,\ldots,\delta
in the dense torus
T
. In this section we investigate what happens for
\tau\rightarrow 0
. Since
|V_{T}(\hat{\mathcal{F}})|<\delta
by assumption, at least one of these paths, say
\zeta_{i}(\tau)
, moves out of the torus
T
for
\tau\rightarrow 0
. By compactness of
X\supset T
, the endpoint
\lim_{\tau\rightarrow 0^{+}}\zeta_{i}(\tau)
exists in
X
and it satisfies

\zeta_{i}(0)=\lim_{\tau\rightarrow 0^{+}}\zeta_{i}(\tau)\in X\setminus T.

We assume that
\zeta_{i}(0)\in U
is an isolated point of
V_{X}({\mathcal{F}})
, where
U
is as in Theorem
2.1
.
We first show that in an analytic neighborhood
\mathcal{U}\subset\mathbb{C}
of
\tau=0
, the
G
-orbit
\pi^{-1}(\zeta_{i}(\tau))
has a representative
r(\tau)=(r_{1}(\tau),\ldots,r_{k}(\tau))
given by Puiseux series. We use the notation
\mathbb{C}[[\tau]]
for the power series ring,
\mathbb{C}\{\!\{\tau\}\!\}
for the field of Puiseux series, and
\val:\mathbb{C}\{\!\{\tau\}\!\}\rightarrow\mathbb{Q}
for its standard valuation. As in Section
3
, we let
(\mathbb{C}^{*})^{\mathscr{I}}
be the
(\mathbb{C}^{*})^{k}
-orbit
\{z\in\mathbb{C}^{k}\setminus Z~|~z_{i}\neq 0,\text{ for all }i\in\mathscr{I}\text{ and }z_{i}=0\text{ for all }i\notin\mathscr{I}\}
and work with index sets
\mathscr{I}
such that
(\mathbb{C}^{*})^{\mathscr{I}}\subset\pi^{-1}(U)
.

Lemma 4.4
.

Let
\tau\mapsto\zeta_{i}(\tau)
be a solution path of
\hat{\mathcal{H}}(t;\tau)
such that
\zeta_{i}(0)\in U
is an isolated point in
V_{X}({\mathcal{F}})
, where
U
is as in Theorem
2.1
. In a neighborhood
\mathcal{U}\subset\mathbb{C}
of
\tau=0
, there is an algebraic function
r:\mathcal{U}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}^{k}\setminus Z,~r\in\mathbb{C}\{\!\{\tau\}\!\}^{k}
such that
\pi^{-1}(\zeta_{i}(\tau))=G\cdot r(\tau)
for all
\tau\in\mathcal{U}
. Moreover, if
\mathscr{I}
is such that
\zeta_{i}(0)\in\pi((\mathbb{C}^{*})^{\mathscr{I}})
, the valuations of
r(\tau)=(r_{1}(\tau),\ldots,r_{k}(\tau))
satisfy

\val(r_{i}(\tau))>0,~i\notin\mathscr{I}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\val(r_{i}(\tau))=0,~i\in\mathscr{I}.

Proof.

is parametrized by

where we view
z
as a row vector of length
k
.
For
\lambda=(1,\ldots,1)
, we find that the tangent space to the orbit at
z
is given by the simple expression

z+c^{\top}P^{\prime\prime}\text{diag}(z_{1},\ldots,z_{k}),\quad c\in\mathbb{C}^{k-n},

where
\text{diag}(z_{1},\ldots,z_{k})
is a diagonal
k\times k
matrix with the coordinates of
z
on its diagonal. It follows that
x
is in the normal space to the orbit
G\cdot z
at
z
if and only if
x-z
is orthogonal to the rows of
P^{\prime\prime}\text{diag}(z_{1},\ldots,z_{k})
.

For a smooth path
(z(\tau),\tau)\in(\mathbb{C}^{k}\setminus Z)\times(0,1]
satisfying
{\mathcal{H}}(z(\tau),\tau)=0
, we define

\mathcal{L}(x;\tau)=\text{conj}(P^{\prime\prime}\text{diag}(z_{1}(\tau),\ldots,z_{k}(\tau)))(x-z(\tau)),

where
\text{conj}(\cdot)
takes the (entry-wise) complex conjugate. This suggests Algorithm
4
for tracking one path in the total coordinate space of
X
, using orthogonal slicing for collecting representatives on the orbits.

Experiment 1

(A problem from computer vision)
.

The

For more details on the model and problem, we refer to

For generic parameters

Homogenizing these equations as in Section

Cox coordinates.
In our experiment, we consider
nearly degenerate systems

For
\epsilon=10^{-7},
the

Our implementation of Algorithm

finds a representative in

Since the generic orbit degree

appearing in Theorem

Ignorging the pre-computation of the polytope

Experiment 3

(Solving equations on a weighted projective space)
.

Consider the system of polynomial equations

\displaystyle\hat{f}_{1}=(3+\varepsilon_{1})t_{1}^{2}+7t_{1}t_{2}+7t_{2}^{2}+9t_{1}+3t_{2}+9t_{3}+2

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle\hat{f}_{2}=(3+\varepsilon_{2})t_{1}^{2}+7t_{1}t_{2}+7t_{2}^{2}+5t_{1}+2t_{2}+3t_{3}+4

\displaystyle=0,

in the variables

. The parameters
\varepsilon_{i}
are assigned random complex values of modulus
10^{-12}
. The normalized volume of the Newton polytope of these equations is 4, which is equal to the number of solutions in

in HomotopyContinuation.jl, we find only 2 solutions. The polynomials

, we find all 4 solutions in

have absolute value

. Tracking these solutions in

6.
Conclusion

We have introduced Cox homotopies for tracking solution paths of sparse polynomial systems in a compact toric variety
X
. The algorithm makes explicit use of the construction of
X
as a GIT quotient of a quasi-affine space by the action of a reductive group
G
. We have described the degree of (the closure of)
G
-orbits in this construction in terms of volumes of orbit polytopes, lattice indices, and invariant factors. As we have shown in our experiments, Cox homotopies, as a generalization of (multi)projective homotopies, allow us to deal with solutions on or near the boundary of the dense torus in
X
in an elegant way, avoiding premature truncation of solution paths and providing insight in the solution structure ‘at infinity’. It inherits the advantage of polyhedral homotopies that only the BKK number many paths need to be tracked. Experiments show that our algorithms provide the first steps towards performing numerical irreducible decomposition in
X
.
