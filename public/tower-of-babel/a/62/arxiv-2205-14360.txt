A discrete analogue of Terrell’s characterization of rectangular distributions
Nickos Papadatos

Abstract
George R. Terrell (1983, Ann. Probab. , vol. 11(3), pp. 823–826) showed that the Pearson coefficient of correlation of an ordered pair from a random sample of size two is at most one-half, and the equality is attained only for rectangular (uniform over some interval) distributions. In the present note it is proved that the same is true for the discrete case, in the sense that the correlation coefficient attains its maximal value only for discrete rectangular (uniform over some finite lattice) distributions.

Key words and phrases
: discrete rectangular distribution; order
statistics;
Hahn polynomials; Pearson coefficient of correlation.

1
Introduction and main result

For independent, identically distributed, random variables
X_{1}
,
X_{2}
,
from a probability distribution function
F
, the corresponding order statistics
will be denoted by
X_{1:2}\leq X_{2:2}
, that is,
X_{1:2}=\min\{X_{1},X_{2}\}
,

X_{2:2}=\max\{X_{1},X_{2}\}
, and the Pearson coefficient of correlation
by

\rho_{12}:=\frac{\mbox{\rm\hskip 0.86108ptCov\hskip 0.86108pt}(X_{1:2},X_{2:2})}{\sqrt{\mbox{\rm\hskip 0.86108ptVar\hskip 0.86108pt}X_{1:2}}\sqrt{\mbox{\rm\hskip 0.86108ptVar\hskip 0.86108pt}X_{2:2}}}.

For a random pair
(X,Y)
, the correlation coefficient,

\rho(X,Y)
, is well defined (and belongs to the interval
[-1,1]
)
if and only if both
X
,
Y
are non-degenerate with finite second moment.
Thus, for
\rho_{12}
to be well defined it is necessary and sufficient that
F
is non-degenerate and posses finite second moment (so that

0<\mbox{\rm\hskip 0.86108ptVar\hskip 0.86108pt}X_{j:2}<\infty
,
j=1,2
).

It is known for a long time
that
\rho_{12}>0
; this follows immediately if we
take expectations to
X_{1:2}X_{2:2}=X_{1}X_{2}
and
X_{1:2}+X_{2:2}=X_{1}+X_{2}
, yielding

\mbox{\rm\hskip 0.86108ptCov\hskip 0.86108pt}(X_{1:2},X_{2:2})=\left(\mbox{\rm\hskip 0.86108ptI\hskip-2.15277ptE\hskip 0.86108pt}X_{2:2}-\mbox{\rm\hskip 0.86108ptI\hskip-2.15277ptE\hskip 0.86108pt}X_{1}\right)^{2}>0
, since

\mbox{\rm\hskip 0.86108ptI\hskip-2.15277ptE\hskip 0.86108pt}X_{1}<\mbox{\rm\hskip 0.86108ptI\hskip-2.15277ptE\hskip 0.86108pt}X_{2:2}
for any non-degenerate
F
.
At the time of 80’s, David Scott and Robert
Bartoszyński, in connection with a problem in cell division,
proposed to George R. Terrell the conjecture that
\rho_{12}
is never
greater then one-half. This was proved true:

Theorem 1.1
.

(Terrell, 1983).

If
F
is a continuous distribution with
finite variance then
\rho_{12}\leq 1/2
, with equality
if and only if
F
is a rectangular distribution.

Terrell’s result is based on the development of a function in
a series of Legendre polynomials, it is quite complicated,
and imposes the unnecessary restriction that
F
is continuous.
Székely and Móri (1985)
interpreted Terrell’s result as a maximal correlation problem –
see Gebelein (1941), Rényi (1959) – that is,

R(X_{1:2},X_{2:2}):=\sup_{g_{1},g_{2}}\rho(g_{1}(X_{1:2}),g_{2}(X_{2:2}))

where the supremum is taken over
non-constant functions
g_{1}\in L^{2}(X_{1:2})
,
g_{2}\in L^{2}(X_{2:2})
.
In this way, Székely and Móri improved Terrell’s
result in four directions.
First, they removed the restriction that
F
is continuous;
second, they simplified Terrell’s proof;
third, they showed that the correlation coefficient of

g_{1}(X_{1:2})
and
g_{2}(X_{2:2})
is less than
1/2
,
for any distribution and any
functions
g_{1}(X_{1:2})
and
g_{2}(X_{2:2})
;
fourth, and most important,
they extended these results to any sample size
n\geq 2
,
obtaining the inequality (denote by
X_{1:n}\leq\cdots\leq X_{n:n}

the order statistics from a random sample of size
n
from
F
)

\rho(X_{i:n},X_{j:n})\leq\sqrt{\frac{i(n+1-j)}{j(n+1-i)}},\ \ \ 1\leq i<j\leq n,\ \ n\geq 2,

(1.1)

which is valid for all non-degenerate
distribution functions
F
with
\mbox{\rm\hskip 0.86108ptVar\hskip 0.86108pt}X_{i:n}+\mbox{\rm\hskip 0.86108ptVar\hskip 0.86108pt}X_{j:n}<\infty
.
The equality in (
1.1
), for a single value of
(i,j,n)
,
characterizes the rectangular distributions.

Subsequently, Nevzorov (1992) obtained a similar inequality for upper records,
in which the equality characterizes the location-scale family
of the standard exponential random variable.
Later on, López-Blázquez and Castaño-Martínez (2006)
proved that the TSM inequality (
1.1
) is also valid
when the order statistics are based on a without-replacement sample of size
n
,
taken from a finite population
{\cal P}=\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{N}\}
with
N>n

distinct elements.

It is shown in Papadatos and Xifara (2013) that all
presenting results are based, essentially, in the following
polynomial regression property (PRP):

\mbox{\rm\hskip 0.86108ptI\hskip-2.15277ptE\hskip 0.86108pt}(X^{k}|Y)=A_{k}Y^{k}+P_{k-1}(Y),\ \ \ \mbox{\rm\hskip 0.86108ptI\hskip-2.15277ptE\hskip 0.86108pt}(Y^{k}|X)=B_{k}X^{k}+Q_{k-1}(X),\ \ k=1,2,\ldots,

(1.2)

where
P_{k-1}
and
Q_{k-1}
are polynomials of degree at most
k-1
.
If a random pair
(X,Y)
satisfies (
1.2
)
then, under mild conditions, its maximal correlation,
R(X,Y)
,
equals to
\sup_{k\geq 1}\sqrt{A_{k}B_{k}}
, so one simply has to
calculate the principal coefficients
A_{k}
and
B_{k}
in
(
1.2
), and choose the value of
k=k_{0}

that maximizes the products
A_{k}B_{k}
. If this value of
k_{0}

is unique, then the equality in the inequality

\rho(g_{1}(X),g_{2}(Y))\leq R(X,Y)

is attained if and only if
\mbox{\rm\hskip 0.86108ptI\hskip-2.15277ptP\hskip 0.86108pt}[g_{1}(X)=c_{1}+\lambda_{1}\phi_{k_{0}}(X)]=1
,

\mbox{\rm\hskip 0.86108ptI\hskip-2.15277ptP\hskip 0.86108pt}[g_{2}(Y)=c_{2}+\lambda_{2}\psi_{k_{0}}(Y)]=1
, and

\lambda_{1}\lambda_{2}\mbox{sign}(A_{k_{0}})>0
,
where
\{\phi_{k}\}_{k=0}^{\infty}
is the complete, orthonormal
polynomial system
in
L^{2}(X)
(with the convention that each
\phi_{k}
has positive
principal coefficient)
and
\{\psi_{k}\}_{k=0}^{\infty}
the corresponding system
in
L^{2}(Y)
.
Let us denote by
U_{1:n}<\cdots<U_{n:n}
the order statistics
either
from uniform in the interval
(0,1)
, or from the discrete
uniform in
\{1,\ldots,N\}
(the latter in the without-replacement case).
It is a simple exercise to verify
that the pair
(U_{i:n},U_{j:n})
posses the PRP
(
1.2
),
and it is easy to calculate
A_{k}
and
B_{k}

(in the without-replacement case this is slightly more complicated);
also, it is plain to check the PRP for upper records
(W_{n},W_{m})

(
n<m
) from the standard exponential, and to obtain the
constants
A_{k}
and
B_{k}
in (
1.2
).
Then, the PRP method yields all the presenting results
at once and, in the discrete case, it provides the exact
characterization of those populations
{\cal P}

that attain the upper bound in the TSM inequality (
1.1
),
even if ties are allowed;
see Section 3 in Papadatos and Xifara (2013).
In all of the forgoing results, the sequence

A_{k}B_{k}
is uniquely maximized by its first term,
hence,
R=\sqrt{A_{1}B_{1}}
, and the equality is attained by
linear functions
g_{1}
,
g_{2}
; equivalently,

R(X,Y)=|\rho(X,Y)|
. While the equality
|\rho|=R

often appears to problems regarding maximal correlation
under PRP, it is not always true; see Papadatos (2014).

In the present note we shall prove the following discrete
analogue of Theorem
1.1
. First, we provide
a useful definition, cf. Balakrishnan
et al.
(2003).

Definition 1.1
.

(Uniform distribution on

{\cal P}=\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{N}\}
in the possible presence of ties).
Let
k_{1}
of the
x_{i}
’s be equal to
y_{1}
,
k_{2}
of the
x_{i}
’s
be equal to
y_{2}
, and so on, where
k_{1}+\cdots+k_{m}=N
and,
with no loss of generality,
y_{1}<\cdots<y_{m}
.
We say that
X
is uniform on
{\cal P}=\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{N}\}

if
\mbox{\rm\hskip 0.86108ptI\hskip-2.15277ptP\hskip 0.86108pt}(X=y_{j})=k_{j}/N
,
j=1,\ldots,m
.

The main result is the following.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
X_{1}
,
X_{2}
be independent random variables with uniform
distribution on
{\cal P}=\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{N}\}
, where
N\geq 2
and

x_{1}\leq\cdots\leq x_{N}
with
x_{1}<x_{N}
.
Then, under the notation of Theorem

1.1
,

\rho_{12}\leq\frac{1-N^{-2}}{2+N^{-2}},

(1.3)

and the equality is attained if and only if

{\cal P}
is a discrete lattice, that is,

x_{i+1}-x_{i}=\lambda>0

(constant),
i=1,\ldots,N-1
.

A straightforward computation shows that the upper bound
in (
1.3
) equals to
\rho_{12}

when
{\cal P}={\cal P}_{0}:=\{1,\ldots,N\}
. If
U_{1:2}\leq U_{2:2}

are the order statistics from
{\cal P}_{0}
, it can be checked
that the random pair
(U_{1:2},U_{2:2})
does not posses
the PRP (
1.2
); in particular,

U_{1:2}
does not have linear regression on
U_{2:2}
. Indeed,
for
x,y\in{\cal P}_{0}
with
x\leq y
,

\mbox{\rm\hskip 0.86108ptI\hskip-2.15277ptP\hskip 0.86108pt}(U_{1:2}=x|U_{2:2}=y)=2/(2y-1)
if
x<y
,
and
\mbox{\rm\hskip 0.86108ptI\hskip-2.15277ptP\hskip 0.86108pt}(U_{1:2}=y|U_{2:2}=y)=1/(2y-1)
. Hence, one finds

\mbox{\rm\hskip 0.86108ptI\hskip-2.15277ptE\hskip 0.86108pt}(U_{1:2}|U_{2:2}=y)=y^{2}/(2y-1)
, and this regression
(with
y
restricted to
{\cal P}_{0}
) is, clearly, nonlinear, unless
N=2
.
Therefore, (
1.2
) fails, and this
verifies the essentially different nature of the present problem, compared
to the preceding ones. Notice that the case
N=2
is trivial, since

\rho_{12}\equiv 1/3
for every choice of
x_{1}
,
x_{2}
, with
x_{1}<x_{2}

(the coefficient of correlation is location-scale invariant).

2
Proof of Theorem
1.2

We shall apply the ordinary method of Terrell (1983) to the case
where
U
follows a discrete uniform in
{\cal P}_{0}=\{1,\ldots,N\}
.
In this case, the orthonormal polynomial system is provided
by Hahn polynomials,
\{\psi_{k}\}_{k=0}^{N-1}
,
where

\psi_{k}(x)=N^{1/2}{N+k\choose 2k+1}^{-1/2}{2k\choose k}^{-1/2}\sum_{j=0}^{k}(-1)^{k-j}{k+j\choose j}{N-1-j\choose k-j}{x-1\choose j}.

The following two properties will be used in
the sequel; cf. López-Blázquez and
Castaño-Martínez (2006).
First,

\psi_{k}(x)=A_{k}x^{k}+B_{k}x^{k-1}+\cdots,

where

A_{k}=\frac{\sqrt{N}}{k!}{2k\choose k}^{1/2}{N+k\choose 2k+1}^{-1/2},\ \ \ \ \ B_{k}=-\frac{k(N+1)\sqrt{N}}{k!}{2k-1\choose k}{N+k\choose 2k+1}^{-1/2}{2k\choose k}^{-1/2}.

(2.1)

Second, the polynomials
\psi_{k}
are orthonormal in

L^{2}(U)\equiv\{g:{\cal P}_{0}\to\mathbb{R}\}
, that is,

\mbox{\rm\hskip 0.86108ptI\hskip-2.15277ptE\hskip 0.86108pt}\psi_{k}(U)\psi_{m}(U)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{x=1}^{N}\psi_{k}(x)\psi_{m}(x)=\delta_{k,m}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ccc}1,&\mbox{if}&k=m,\\
0,&\mbox{if}&k\neq m,\end{array}\right.

and this implies that any function
g:{\cal P}_{0}\to\mathbb{R}
has a unique
development in Hahn polynomial series, namely,

g(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\delta_{k}\psi_{k}(x),\ \ \ \mbox{where}\ \ \delta_{k}=\mbox{\rm\hskip 0.86108ptI\hskip-2.15277ptE\hskip 0.86108pt}\psi_{k}(U)g(U)\ \ (k=0,\ldots,N-1).

(2.2)

The constants
\{\delta_{k}\}_{k=0}^{N-1}
are the Fourier coefficients of
g
.

An arbitrary
{\cal P}=\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{N}\}
(with
x_{1}\leq\cdots\leq x_{N}
)
is the image of some nondecreasing
g:{\cal P}_{0}\to\mathbb{R}
,
namely,
g(i)=x_{i}
,
i=1,\ldots,N
. In other words, if
X
is uniformly
distributed over
{\cal P}
(in the sense of Definition
1.1
)
and
U
is uniformly distributed over

{\cal P}_{0}
then
X
and
g(U)
have the same distribution.
Therefore,
(g(U_{1}),g(U_{2}))
and
(X_{1},X_{2})
have the same distribution,
where
X_{1},X_{2}
are independent copies of
X
, and
U_{1},U_{2}
are
independent copies of
U
.
Since
g
is
nondecreasing, it holds
(\min\{g(U_{1}),g(U_{2})\},\max\{g(U_{1}),g(U_{2})\})=(g(\min\{U_{1},U_{2}\}),g(\max\{U_{1},U_{2}\}))=(g(U_{1:2}),g(U_{2:2}))
.
This shows that the random pairs
(X_{1:2},X_{2:2})
and
(g(U_{1:2}),g(U_{2:2}))

have the same distribution;
hence,
\rho_{12}=\rho(g(U_{1:2}),g(U_{2:2}))
.
Expanding
g
according to (
2.2
), we will be able to
express
\rho_{12}
as a function of
\delta_{1},\ldots,\delta_{N-1}
,
and, next, we shall maximize the resulting function. The calculations,
below, do not a-priori impose any monotonicity restrictions on
g
;
the only assumption is that
g
is non-constant (otherwise, the variance
of
X
is zero and
\rho_{12}
is undefined).

Lemma 2.1
.

(Hahn representation).

Expand an arbitrary
g:{\cal P}_{0}\to\R
in a Hahn polynomial series
as in (
2.2
),
and let
\sigma_{1}^{2}=\mbox{\rm\hskip 0.86108ptVar\hskip 0.86108pt}g(U_{1:2})
,

\sigma_{2}^{2}=\mbox{\rm\hskip 0.86108ptVar\hskip 0.86108pt}g(U_{2:2})
,

\sigma_{12}=\mbox{\rm\hskip 0.86108ptCov\hskip 0.86108pt}(g(U_{1:2}),g(U_{2:2}))
. Then,

\displaystyle\sigma_{1}^{2}

\displaystyle\hskip-4.30554pt=\hskip-4.30554pt

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{N-1}\delta_{k}^{2}-\frac{\delta_{1}^{2}}{3}\Big(1-\frac{1}{N^{2}}\Big)-\frac{2}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N-2}\lambda_{k}\delta_{k}\delta_{k+1}

(2.3)

\displaystyle\sigma_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\hskip-4.30554pt=\hskip-4.30554pt

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{N-1}\delta_{k}^{2}-\frac{\delta_{1}^{2}}{3}\Big(1-\frac{1}{N^{2}}\Big)+\frac{2}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N-2}\lambda_{k}\delta_{k}\delta_{k+1}

(2.4)

\displaystyle\sigma_{12}

\displaystyle\hskip-4.30554pt=\hskip-4.30554pt

\displaystyle\frac{\delta_{1}^{2}}{3}\Big(1-\frac{1}{N^{2}}\Big),

(2.5)

where, in (
2.3
) and (
2.4
),

\lambda_{k}=(k+1)\sqrt{\frac{N^{2}-(k+1)^{2}}{(2k+1)(2k+3)}},\ \ \ k=1,\ldots,N-1.

(2.6)

Proof:

If
\delta_{1}=\cdots=\delta_{N-1}=0
then
g\equiv\delta_{0}
(constant)
and the result is trivial, since
\sigma_{1}^{2}=\sigma_{2}^{2}=\sigma_{12}=0
.
Assume that
\beta:=\delta_{1}^{2}+\cdots+\delta_{N-1}^{2}>0
, and set

h(x):=(g(x)-\delta_{0})/\sqrt{\beta}
, so that

h(x)=\sum_{k=1}^{N-1}d_{k}\psi_{k}(x),\ \ \mbox{ where }\ d_{k}=\delta_{k}/\sqrt{\beta}\ \ (k=1,\ldots,N-1),

are the Fourier coefficients of
h
. By construction,

\sum_{k=1}^{N-1}d_{k}^{2}=1
,

\sigma_{1}^{2}=\beta\mbox{\rm\hskip 0.86108ptVar\hskip 0.86108pt}h(U_{1:2})
,

\sigma_{2}^{2}=\beta\mbox{\rm\hskip 0.86108ptVar\hskip 0.86108pt}h(U_{2:2})
,

\sigma_{12}=\beta\mbox{\rm\hskip 0.86108ptCov\hskip 0.86108pt}(h(U_{1:2}),h(U_{2:2}))
.
The orthonormality of
\psi_{k}
shows that
the random variable
Y:=h(U)
is standardized; indeed,

\mbox{\rm\hskip 0.86108ptI\hskip-2.15277ptE\hskip 0.86108pt}Y=\sum_{k=1}^{N-1}d_{k}\mbox{\rm\hskip 0.86108ptI\hskip-2.15277ptE\hskip 0.86108pt}\psi_{k}(U)=0,\ \ \ \ \mbox{\rm\hskip 0.86108ptI\hskip-2.15277ptE\hskip 0.86108pt}Y^{2}=\sum_{k=1}^{N-1}\sum_{m=1}^{N-1}d_{k}d_{m}\mbox{\rm\hskip 0.86108ptI\hskip-2.15277ptE\hskip 0.86108pt}\psi_{k}(U)\psi_{m}(U)=\sum_{k=1}^{N-1}d_{k}^{2}\mbox{\rm\hskip 0.86108ptI\hskip-2.15277ptE\hskip 0.86108pt}\psi_{k}(U)^{2}=1,

where, the first equality is satisfied because
\psi_{k}

(
k\geq 1
) is orthogonal to
\psi_{0}\equiv 1
, and the second one
follows from the orthonormality of
\psi_{k}
and the definition
of
d_{k}
.
We now write
Y_{1}=h(U_{1})
,
Y_{2}=h(U_{2})
,
Z_{1}=h(U_{1:2})
,

Z_{2}=h(U_{2:2})
, with
U_{1}
,
U_{2}
being independent uniform
from
{\cal P}_{0}
; notice that the inequality
Z_{1}\leq Z_{2}

may fail, since
h
has not been assumed monotonic.
According to the argument above,
Y_{1},Y_{2}
are independent
and standardized. Taking expectations
to the obvious identities
Z_{1}+Z_{2}=Y_{1}+Y_{2}
and

Z_{1}Z_{2}=Y_{1}Y_{2}
we see that
\mbox{\rm\hskip 0.86108ptI\hskip-2.15277ptE\hskip 0.86108pt}Z_{1}=-\mbox{\rm\hskip 0.86108ptI\hskip-2.15277ptE\hskip 0.86108pt}Z_{2}
and
\mbox{\rm\hskip 0.86108ptI\hskip-2.15277ptE\hskip 0.86108pt}Z_{1}Z_{2}=0
.
Hence,
\mbox{\rm\hskip 0.86108ptCov\hskip 0.86108pt}(Z_{1},Z_{2})=(\mbox{\rm\hskip 0.86108ptI\hskip-2.15277ptE\hskip 0.86108pt}Z_{2})^{2}
; equivalently,

\sigma_{12}=\beta(\mbox{\rm\hskip 0.86108ptI\hskip-2.15277ptE\hskip 0.86108pt}Z_{2})^{2}
.
The relation
2=\mbox{\rm\hskip 0.86108ptVar\hskip 0.86108pt}(Y_{1}+Y_{2})=\mbox{\rm\hskip 0.86108ptVar\hskip 0.86108pt}(Z_{1}+Z_{2})=\mbox{\rm\hskip 0.86108ptVar\hskip 0.86108pt}Z_{1}+\mbox{\rm\hskip 0.86108ptVar\hskip 0.86108pt}Z_{2}+2(\mbox{\rm\hskip 0.86108ptI\hskip-2.15277ptE\hskip 0.86108pt}Z_{2})^{2}

shows that
\mbox{\rm\hskip 0.86108ptVar\hskip 0.86108pt}Z_{1}=2-\mbox{\rm\hskip 0.86108ptVar\hskip 0.86108pt}Z_{2}-2(\mbox{\rm\hskip 0.86108ptI\hskip-2.15277ptE\hskip 0.86108pt}Z_{2})^{2}=2-(\mbox{\rm\hskip 0.86108ptI\hskip-2.15277ptE\hskip 0.86108pt}Z_{2})^{2}-\mbox{\rm\hskip 0.86108ptI\hskip-2.15277ptE\hskip 0.86108pt}Z_{2}^{2}
and

\sigma_{1}^{2}=\beta\big(2-(\mbox{\rm\hskip 0.86108ptI\hskip-2.15277ptE\hskip 0.86108pt}Z_{2})^{2}-\mbox{\rm\hskip 0.86108ptI\hskip-2.15277ptE\hskip 0.86108pt}Z_{2}^{2}\big)
.
Since
\mbox{\rm\hskip 0.86108ptVar\hskip 0.86108pt}Z_{2}=\mbox{\rm\hskip 0.86108ptI\hskip-2.15277ptE\hskip 0.86108pt}Z_{2}^{2}-(\mbox{\rm\hskip 0.86108ptI\hskip-2.15277ptE\hskip 0.86108pt}Z_{2})^{2}
implies

\sigma_{2}^{2}=\beta\big(\mbox{\rm\hskip 0.86108ptI\hskip-2.15277ptE\hskip 0.86108pt}Z_{2}^{2}-(\mbox{\rm\hskip 0.86108ptI\hskip-2.15277ptE\hskip 0.86108pt}Z_{2})^{2}\big)
, all
quantities
\sigma_{1}^{2}
,
\sigma_{2}^{2}
,
\sigma_{12}
,
(and
\rho_{12}
),
are expressed in terms of
\mbox{\rm\hskip 0.86108ptI\hskip-2.15277ptE\hskip 0.86108pt}Z_{2}=\mbox{\rm\hskip 0.86108ptI\hskip-2.15277ptE\hskip 0.86108pt}h(U_{2:2})

and
\mbox{\rm\hskip 0.86108ptI\hskip-2.15277ptE\hskip 0.86108pt}Z_{2}^{2}=\mbox{\rm\hskip 0.86108ptI\hskip-2.15277ptE\hskip 0.86108pt}h(U_{2:2})^{2}
.

The probability mass function of
U_{2:2}
is given by

\mbox{\rm\hskip 0.86108ptI\hskip-2.15277ptP\hskip 0.86108pt}(U_{2:2}=j)=(2j-1)/N^{2}
,
j=1,\ldots,N
, and therefore,

\mbox{\rm\hskip 0.86108ptI\hskip-2.15277ptE\hskip 0.86108pt}Z_{2}=\frac{1}{N^{2}}\sum_{j=1}^{N}(2j-1)h(j)=\frac{1}{N}\mbox{\rm\hskip 0.86108ptI\hskip-2.15277ptE\hskip 0.86108pt}(2U-1)h(U)=\frac{2}{N}\mbox{\rm\hskip 0.86108ptI\hskip-2.15277ptE\hskip 0.86108pt}Uh(U),

where the last equality follows from
\mbox{\rm\hskip 0.86108ptI\hskip-2.15277ptE\hskip 0.86108pt}h(U)=\mbox{\rm\hskip 0.86108ptI\hskip-2.15277ptE\hskip 0.86108pt}Y=0
. Since
each
\psi_{k}(U)
(
k\geq 2
) is orthogonal to any polynomial of degree
at most one, hence to
U
, we have

\mbox{\rm\hskip 0.86108ptI\hskip-2.15277ptE\hskip 0.86108pt}Uh(U)=d_{1}\mbox{\rm\hskip 0.86108ptI\hskip-2.15277ptE\hskip 0.86108pt}U\psi_{1}(U)+\sum_{k=2}^{N-1}d_{k}\mbox{\rm\hskip 0.86108ptI\hskip-2.15277ptE\hskip 0.86108pt}U\psi_{k}(U)=d_{1}\mbox{\rm\hskip 0.86108ptI\hskip-2.15277ptE\hskip 0.86108pt}U\psi_{1}(U)=\frac{d_{1}}{A_{1}}\mbox{\rm\hskip 0.86108ptI\hskip-2.15277ptE\hskip 0.86108pt}\psi_{1}(U)(A_{1}U+B_{1})=\frac{d_{1}}{A_{1}},

because
A_{1}U+B_{1}=\psi_{1}(U)
and
\psi_{1}
is orthogonal to constants.
Substituting the value of
A_{1}

from (
2.1
) we obtain

\mbox{\rm\hskip 0.86108ptI\hskip-2.15277ptE\hskip 0.86108pt}Z_{2}=2d_{1}/(NA_{1})=(1-N^{-2})^{1/2}d_{1}/\sqrt{3}
, and the relations

\sigma_{12}=\beta(\mbox{\rm\hskip 0.86108ptI\hskip-2.15277ptE\hskip 0.86108pt}Z_{2})^{2}
and
d_{1}=\delta_{1}/\sqrt{\beta}

imply
(
2.5
).

The computation of the second moment of
Z_{2}
is more involved. Write

\mbox{\rm\hskip 0.86108ptI\hskip-2.15277ptE\hskip 0.86108pt}Z_{2}^{2}=\frac{1}{N^{2}}\sum_{j=1}^{N}(2j-1)h(j)^{2}=\frac{1}{N}\mbox{\rm\hskip 0.86108ptI\hskip-2.15277ptE\hskip 0.86108pt}(2U-1)h(U)^{2}=\frac{2}{N}\mbox{\rm\hskip 0.86108ptI\hskip-2.15277ptE\hskip 0.86108pt}Uh(U)^{2}-\frac{1}{N},

(2.7)

because
\mbox{\rm\hskip 0.86108ptI\hskip-2.15277ptE\hskip 0.86108pt}Y^{2}=\mbox{\rm\hskip 0.86108ptI\hskip-2.15277ptE\hskip 0.86108pt}h(U)^{2}=1
. It remains to compute

\mbox{\rm\hskip 0.86108ptI\hskip-2.15277ptE\hskip 0.86108pt}Uh(U)^{2}=\sum_{k,m=1}^{N-1}d_{k}d_{m}\mbox{\rm\hskip 0.86108ptI\hskip-2.15277ptE\hskip 0.86108pt}U\psi_{k}(U)\psi_{m}(U)=\sum_{k=1}^{N-1}d_{k}^{2}\mbox{\rm\hskip 0.86108ptI\hskip-2.15277ptE\hskip 0.86108pt}U\psi_{k}(U)^{2}+2\sum_{k=1}^{N-2}d_{k}d_{k+1}\mbox{\rm\hskip 0.86108ptI\hskip-2.15277ptE\hskip 0.86108pt}U\psi_{k}(U)\psi_{k+1}(U);

all other terms (with
m>k
) vanish, due to the orthogonality of
\psi_{m}(U)

to the polynomial
U\psi_{k}(U)
(of degree
k+1
) when
m\geq k+2
.
We proceed to compute
\mbox{\rm\hskip 0.86108ptI\hskip-2.15277ptE\hskip 0.86108pt}U\psi_{k}(U)^{2}
and
\mbox{\rm\hskip 0.86108ptI\hskip-2.15277ptE\hskip 0.86108pt}U\psi_{k}(U)\psi_{k+1}(U)
.
Write
\psi_{k}(x)=A_{k}x^{k}+B_{k}x^{k-1}+P_{k-2}(x)
, where

P_{k-2}
is a polynomial of degree at most
k-2
(
P_{-1}\equiv 0
).
Then,

\mbox{\rm\hskip 0.86108ptI\hskip-2.15277ptE\hskip 0.86108pt}U\psi_{k}(U)^{2}=\mbox{\rm\hskip 0.86108ptI\hskip-2.15277ptE\hskip 0.86108pt}U\psi_{k}(U)(A_{k}U^{k}+B_{k}U^{k-1}+P_{k-2}(U))=A_{k}\mbox{\rm\hskip 0.86108ptI\hskip-2.15277ptE\hskip 0.86108pt}U^{k+1}\psi_{k}(U)+B_{k}\mbox{\rm\hskip 0.86108ptI\hskip-2.15277ptE\hskip 0.86108pt}U^{k}\psi_{k}(U),

because
\psi_{k}(U)
is orthogonal to the polynomial
UP_{k-2}(U)
.
Next, we compute

\displaystyle\mbox{\rm\hskip 0.86108ptI\hskip-2.15277ptE\hskip 0.86108pt}U^{k+1}\psi_{k}(U)

\displaystyle\hskip-4.30554pt=\hskip-4.30554pt

\displaystyle\frac{1}{A_{k+1}}\mbox{\rm\hskip 0.86108ptI\hskip-2.15277ptE\hskip 0.86108pt}\psi_{k}(U)\Big(A_{k+1}U^{k+1}+B_{k+1}U^{k}+P_{k-1}(U)-B_{k+1}U^{k}-P_{k-1}(U)\Big)

\displaystyle\hskip-4.30554pt=\hskip-4.30554pt

\displaystyle\frac{1}{A_{k+1}}\mbox{\rm\hskip 0.86108ptI\hskip-2.15277ptE\hskip 0.86108pt}\psi_{k}(U)\Big(\psi_{k+1}(U)-B_{k+1}U^{k}-P_{k-1}(U)\Big)

\displaystyle\hskip-4.30554pt=\hskip-4.30554pt

\displaystyle-\frac{B_{k+1}}{A_{k+1}}\mbox{\rm\hskip 0.86108ptI\hskip-2.15277ptE\hskip 0.86108pt}U^{k}\psi_{k}(U),

where the last equality follows from the orthogonality of
\psi_{k}

to both
\psi_{k+1}
and
P_{k-1}
. Therefore,

\mbox{\rm\hskip 0.86108ptI\hskip-2.15277ptE\hskip 0.86108pt}U\psi_{k}(U)^{2}=A_{k}\mbox{\rm\hskip 0.86108ptI\hskip-2.15277ptE\hskip 0.86108pt}U^{k+1}\psi_{k}(U)+B_{k}\mbox{\rm\hskip 0.86108ptI\hskip-2.15277ptE\hskip 0.86108pt}U^{k}\psi_{k}(U)=\left(B_{k}-\frac{A_{k}B_{k+1}}{A_{k+1}}\right)\mbox{\rm\hskip 0.86108ptI\hskip-2.15277ptE\hskip 0.86108pt}U^{k}\psi_{k}(U).

The calculation of
\mbox{\rm\hskip 0.86108ptI\hskip-2.15277ptE\hskip 0.86108pt}U^{k}\psi_{k}(U)
is easy:

\mbox{\rm\hskip 0.86108ptI\hskip-2.15277ptE\hskip 0.86108pt}U^{k}\psi_{k}(U)=\frac{1}{A_{k}}\mbox{\rm\hskip 0.86108ptI\hskip-2.15277ptE\hskip 0.86108pt}\psi_{k}(U)(A_{k}U^{k}+B_{k-1}U^{k-1}+\cdots)=\frac{1}{A_{k}}\mbox{\rm\hskip 0.86108ptI\hskip-2.15277ptE\hskip 0.86108pt}\psi_{k}(U)^{2}=\frac{1}{A_{k}}.

Hence, using the fact that
B_{k}/A_{k}=-k(N+1)/2
,
see (
2.1
), we obtain

\mbox{\rm\hskip 0.86108ptI\hskip-2.15277ptE\hskip 0.86108pt}U\psi_{k}(U)^{2}=\frac{B_{k}}{A_{k}}-\frac{B_{k+1}}{A_{k+1}}=\frac{N+1}{2};

surprisingly, this expectation is independent of
k
.
Proceeding similarly, and in view of (
2.1
), we compute

\mbox{\rm\hskip 0.86108ptI\hskip-2.15277ptE\hskip 0.86108pt}U\psi_{k}(U)\psi_{k+1}(U)=\mbox{\rm\hskip 0.86108ptI\hskip-2.15277ptE\hskip 0.86108pt}U(A_{k}U^{k}+B_{k}U^{k-1}+\cdots)\psi_{k+1}(U)=A_{k}\mbox{\rm\hskip 0.86108ptI\hskip-2.15277ptE\hskip 0.86108pt}U^{k+1}\psi_{k+1}(U)=\frac{A_{k}}{A_{k+1}}=\frac{\lambda_{k}}{2},

with
\lambda_{k}
as in (
2.6
).
Combining the preceding formulae (recall that
\sum_{k=1}^{N-1}d_{k}^{2}=1
)
we obtain

\mbox{\rm\hskip 0.86108ptI\hskip-2.15277ptE\hskip 0.86108pt}Uh(U)^{2}=\frac{N+1}{2}+\sum_{k=1}^{N-2}\lambda_{k}d_{k}d_{k+1}.

Then, from (
2.7
),

\mbox{\rm\hskip 0.86108ptI\hskip-2.15277ptE\hskip 0.86108pt}Z_{2}^{2}=1+(2/N)\sum_{k=1}^{N-2}\lambda_{k}d_{k}d_{k+1}
.
Moreover, since
(\mbox{\rm\hskip 0.86108ptI\hskip-2.15277ptE\hskip 0.86108pt}Z_{2})^{2}=(1-N^{-2})d_{1}^{2}/3
we have

\mbox{\rm\hskip 0.86108ptVar\hskip 0.86108pt}Z_{2}=1-(1-N^{-2})d_{1}^{2}/3+(2/N)\sum_{k=1}^{N-2}\lambda_{k}d_{k}d_{k+1}
, and
the relation
\sigma_{2}^{2}=\beta\mbox{\rm\hskip 0.86108ptVar\hskip 0.86108pt}Z_{2}
yields (
2.4
), because

\beta d_{1}^{2}=\delta_{1}^{2}
and
\beta d_{k}d_{k+1}=\delta_{k}\delta_{k+1}

(recall that
\beta=\delta_{1}^{2}+\cdots+\delta_{N-1}^{2}
).
Similarly, substituting the preceding expressions for
(\mbox{\rm\hskip 0.86108ptI\hskip-2.15277ptE\hskip 0.86108pt}Z_{2})^{2}
and
\mbox{\rm\hskip 0.86108ptI\hskip-2.15277ptE\hskip 0.86108pt}Z_{2}^{2}

to the relation
\mbox{\rm\hskip 0.86108ptVar\hskip 0.86108pt}Z_{1}=2-(\mbox{\rm\hskip 0.86108ptI\hskip-2.15277ptE\hskip 0.86108pt}Z_{2})^{2}-\mbox{\rm\hskip 0.86108ptI\hskip-2.15277ptE\hskip 0.86108pt}Z_{2}^{2}
we obtain

\mbox{\rm\hskip 0.86108ptVar\hskip 0.86108pt}Z_{1}=1-(1-N^{-2})d_{1}^{2}/3-(2/N)\sum_{k=1}^{N-2}\lambda_{k}d_{k}d_{k+1}
,
and since
\sigma_{1}^{2}=\beta\mbox{\rm\hskip 0.86108ptVar\hskip 0.86108pt}Z_{1}
, (
2.3
) follows.

Lemma 2.2
.

We define the rational functions
R_{0}(x)\equiv 1

and, recurrently,

R_{k}(x):=1-\frac{k^{2}(1-k^{2}/x)}{(4k^{2}-1)R_{k-1}(x)},\ \ \ \ k=1,2,\ldots.

Then, the following inequalities hold true:

\frac{k+1}{2k+1}<R_{k}(x)<1,\ \ x>k^{2},\ \ k=1,2,\ldots.

(2.8)

Proof:

It is easily seen that
R_{1}(x)=(2+x^{-1})/3
, which is strictly decreasing
on

[1,\infty)
, with
R_{1}(1)=1
and
R_{1}(\infty)=2/3
; hence,
(
2.8
) is fulfilled for
k=1
.
Suppose that (
2.8
) is true for some
k-1\geq 1
, that
is,
k/(2k-1)<R_{k-1}(x)<1
,
x>(k-1)^{2}
;
equivalently,

1<\frac{1}{R_{k-1}(x)}<\frac{2k-1}{k},\ \ \ x>(k-1)^{2}.

If
x>k^{2}
, multiplying the preceding inequality
by the positive quantity
k^{2}(1-k^{2}/x)/(4k^{2}-1)

we get

L(x):=\frac{k^{2}(1-k^{2}/x)}{4k^{2}-1}<\frac{k^{2}(1-k^{2}/x)}{(4k^{2}-1)R_{k-1}(x)}=1-R_{k}(x)<U(x):=\frac{k(1-k^{2}/x)}{2k+1},\ \ \ x>k^{2}.

It is easily seen that
L
is strictly increasing in
x
,
x>k^{2}
,
so
L(x)>L(k^{2})=0
, and
U
is also strictly increasing with

U(x)<U(\infty)=k/(2k+1)
. This shows that
0<1-R_{k}(x)<k/(2k+1)

for
x>k^{2}
and verifies the inductional step,
completing the proof.

Lemma 2.3
.

For
N\geq 2
, there exist nonnegative numbers

\{\alpha_{k}\}_{k=1}^{N-1}
and
\{\beta_{k}\}_{k=1}^{N-1}

satisfying the following four properties:

(i)

\alpha_{1}=\frac{1}{3}\Big(2+\frac{1}{N^{2}}\Big)
,
\beta_{N-1}=0
;

(ii)

\alpha_{k}>\beta_{k-1}>0
,
k=2,\ldots,N-1
;

(iii)

\alpha_{k}+\beta_{k-1}=1
,
k=2,\ldots,N-1
;

(iv)

\alpha_{k}\beta_{k}=\frac{(k+1)^{2}}{(2k+1)(2k+3)}\Big(1-\frac{(k+1)^{2}}{N^{2}}\Big)
,

k=1,\ldots,N-1
.

Proof:
Define
\alpha_{k}:=R_{k}(N^{2})
and
\beta_{k}:=1-R_{k+1}(N^{2})
,

k=1,\ldots,N-1
, where the functions
R_{k}
are
defined in Lemma
2.2
. Then, (i)
and (iii) are obvious, and (ii) follows from (
2.8
),
since
\alpha_{k}=R_{k}(N^{2})>(k+1)/(2k+1)>1/2
, because
N^{2}>k^{2}
,
and, by the same reasoning,
\beta_{k}\in[0,1/2)
(more precisely,

\beta_{k}>0
unless
k=N-1
). Finally, (iv) is trivial
for
k=N-1
(since
\beta_{N-1}=0
), and for
k=1,\ldots,N-2
,

\alpha_{k}\beta_{k}=\alpha_{k}(1-\alpha_{k+1})=\alpha_{k}\frac{(k+1)^{2}(1-(k+1)^{2}/N^{2})}{[4(k+1)^{2}-1]\alpha_{k}}=\frac{(k+1)^{2}}{(2k+1)(2k+3)}\Big(1-\frac{(k+1)^{2}}{N^{2}}\Big),

due to the recurrent relation of Lemma
2.2
,
namely,

1-R_{k+1}(N^{2})=\frac{(k+1)^{2}(1-(k+1)^{2}/N^{2})}{[4(k+1)^{2}-1]R_{k}(N^{2})}.

Hence, the lemma is proved.

Lemma 2.4
.

(Terrell-Hahn representation).

Consider the numbers
\{\alpha_{k}\}_{k=1}^{N-1}
and

\{\beta_{k}\}_{k=1}^{N-1}
as in Lemma
2.3
.
Then, with the convention
\delta_{N}:=0
,

\sigma_{1}^{2}=\sum_{k=1}^{N-1}\left(\sqrt{\alpha_{k}}\delta_{k}-\sqrt{\beta_{k}}\delta_{k+1}\right)^{2},\ \ \ \ \sigma_{2}^{2}=\sum_{k=1}^{N-1}\left(\sqrt{\alpha_{k}}\delta_{k}+\sqrt{\beta_{k}}\delta_{k+1}\right)^{2},

where
\sigma_{1}^{2}=\mbox{\rm\hskip 0.86108ptVar\hskip 0.86108pt}g(U_{1:2})
and
\sigma_{2}^{2}=\mbox{\rm\hskip 0.86108ptVar\hskip 0.86108pt}g(U_{2:2})

are as in Lemma
2.1
.

Proof:

Let
s_{1}^{2}
be the first sum. Expanding the squares we have

s_{1}^{2}=\sum_{k=1}^{N-1}\alpha_{k}\delta_{k}^{2}+\sum_{k=1}^{N-1}\beta_{k}\delta_{k+1}^{2}-2\sum_{k=1}^{N-1}\sqrt{\alpha_{k}\beta_{k}}\delta_{k}\delta_{k+1}=\alpha_{1}\delta_{1}^{2}+\sum_{k=2}^{N-1}(\alpha_{k}+\beta_{k-1})\delta_{k}^{2}-2\sum_{k=1}^{N-2}\sqrt{\alpha_{k}\beta_{k}}\delta_{k}\delta_{k+1},

and Lemma
2.3
implies that

s_{1}^{2}=\frac{1}{3}\Big(2+\frac{1}{N^{2}}\Big)\delta_{1}^{2}+\sum_{k=2}^{N-1}\delta_{k}^{2}-\frac{2}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N-2}\lambda_{k}\delta_{k}\delta_{k+1},

with
\lambda_{k}
as in (
2.6
).
Observing that
(2+N^{-2})/3=1-(1-N^{-2})/3
,
s_{1}^{2}
reduces to
the expression of
\sigma_{1}^{2}
, given in (
2.3
).
The derivation of the expression for
\sigma_{2}^{2}

is completely similar.

Theorem 2.1
.

For
N\geq 2
and any nonconstant
g:{\cal P}_{0}=\{1,\ldots,N\}\to\R
,

\rho(g(U_{1:2}),g(U_{2:2}))\leq\frac{1-N^{-2}}{2+N^{-2}},

with equality if and only if
g
is linear.

Proof:

Let
g(U)=\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\delta_{k}\psi_{k}(U)
be the Hahn representation
of
g
, with
\delta_{1}^{2}+\cdots+\delta_{N-1}^{2}>0
(otherwise,
g
is constant).
If
\sigma_{i}^{2}=\mbox{\rm\hskip 0.86108ptVar\hskip 0.86108pt}g(U_{i:2})
(
i=1,2
) and

\sigma_{12}=\mbox{\rm\hskip 0.86108ptCov\hskip 0.86108pt}(g(U_{1:2}),g(U_{2:2}))

then, from Lemma
2.4
(and with the convention
\delta_{N}=0
),

\displaystyle\sigma_{1}^{2}\sigma_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\hskip-4.30554pt=\hskip-4.30554pt

\displaystyle\Bigg\{\sum_{k=1}^{N-1}\left(\sqrt{\alpha_{k}}\delta_{k}-\sqrt{\beta_{k}}\delta_{k+1}\right)^{2}\Bigg\}\Bigg\{\sum_{k=1}^{N-1}\left(\sqrt{\alpha_{k}}\delta_{k}+\sqrt{\beta_{k}}\delta_{k+1}\right)^{2}\Bigg\}

\displaystyle\hskip-4.30554pt\geq\hskip-4.30554pt

\displaystyle\Bigg\{\sum_{k=1}^{N-1}\left(\sqrt{\alpha_{k}}\delta_{k}-\sqrt{\beta_{k}}\delta_{k+1}\right)\left(\sqrt{\alpha_{k}}\delta_{k}+\sqrt{\beta_{k}}\delta_{k+1}\right)\Bigg\}^{2}

\displaystyle\hskip-4.30554pt=\hskip-4.30554pt

\displaystyle\Bigg\{\sum_{k=1}^{N-1}\left(\alpha_{k}\delta_{k}^{2}-\beta_{k}\delta_{k+1}^{2}\right)\Bigg\}^{2}

\displaystyle\hskip-4.30554pt=\hskip-4.30554pt

\displaystyle\Bigg\{\alpha_{1}\delta_{1}^{2}+\sum_{k=2}^{N-1}\left(\alpha_{k}-\beta_{k-1}\right)\delta_{k}^{2}\Bigg\}^{2}

\displaystyle\hskip-4.30554pt\geq\hskip-4.30554pt

\displaystyle\alpha_{1}^{2}\delta_{1}^{4},\Bigg.

where the second inequality holds true because
\alpha_{k}-\beta_{k-1}>0

and
\alpha_{1}>0
,
see Lemma
2.3
(ii), while the first one is a simple
application of the well-known Cauchy inequality. On substituting the value
of
\alpha_{1}
from Lemma
2.3
(i), the previous
argument verifies the inequality

\left(\sigma_{1}^{2}\sigma_{2}^{2}\right)^{1/2}\geq\frac{1}{3}\Big(2+\frac{1}{N^{2}}\Big)\delta_{1}^{2},

in which the equality holds if and only if
\delta_{k}=0
for all
k\geq 2
, i.e.,
when
g
is linear. From (
2.5
) of Lemma
2.1
,

\delta_{1}^{2}=3\sigma_{12}/(1-N^{-2})
; thus,

(\sigma_{1}^{2}\sigma_{2}^{2})^{1/2}\geq\sigma_{12}(2+N^{-1})/(1-N^{-2})
, and this
is equivalent to the desired inequality.

Applying Theorem
2.1
to the particular
case where
g
is
given by
g(i)=x_{i}
,
i=1,\ldots,N
(which is nondecreasing and non-constant),
we conclude the result of Theorem
1.2
.

3
Concluding Remarks

Remark 3.1
.

(An improvement to the covariance/expectation bound for
n=2
).
Since the variance of
X=g(U)
equals to

\sigma^{2}=\delta_{1}^{2}+\cdots+\delta_{N-1}^{2}\geq\delta_{1}^{2}
,
an immediate consequence of (
2.5
) is the Bessel-type
inequality
\mbox{\rm\hskip 0.86108ptCov\hskip 0.86108pt}(X_{1:2},X_{2:2})\leq\sigma^{2}(1-N^{-2})/3
;
this is similar to the bound

\mbox{\rm\hskip 0.86108ptCov\hskip 0.86108pt}(X_{1:2},X_{2:2})\leq\sigma^{2}(1-2N^{-1})/(3(1-N^{-1}))
,
obtained by Balakrishnan
et al.
(2003) in the
without-replacement case. Notice that the equality in any of these
two bounds is attained only for lattice populations.
Both bounds converge to
\sigma^{2}/3
as
N\to\infty
, and the
maximizing population (provided
\mu=\mbox{\rm\hskip 0.86108ptI\hskip-2.15277ptE\hskip 0.86108pt}X
and
\sigma^{2}
are held
fixed), converges weakly to the uniform

{\cal U}(\mu-\sigma\sqrt{3},\mu+\sigma\sqrt{3})
distribution.
Papathanasiou (1990) proved that
the inequality
\mbox{\rm\hskip 0.86108ptCov\hskip 0.86108pt}(X_{1:2},X_{2:2})\leq\sigma^{2}/3
holds
true
for random samples
X_{1},X_{2}
from any distribution with
finite variance
\sigma^{2}
,
and the equality characterizes the location-family of
rectangular distributions with fixed variance.
Later on, Balakrishnan and Balasubramanian (1993) observed
that, in view of the obvious identity

\mbox{\rm\hskip 0.86108ptCov\hskip 0.86108pt}(X_{1:2},X_{2:2})=(\mbox{\rm\hskip 0.86108ptI\hskip-2.15277ptE\hskip 0.86108pt}X_{2:2}-\mu)^{2}
,
the supremum of this covariance equals to the
smallest upper bound
for
\mbox{\rm\hskip 0.86108ptI\hskip-2.15277ptE\hskip 0.86108pt}X_{2:2}-\mu
in terms of
\sigma
.
The form of the latter bound, called HDG bound, is long known
for
\mbox{\rm\hskip 0.86108ptI\hskip-2.15277ptE\hskip 0.86108pt}X_{n:n}
(any
n\geq 2
), and it is proved
in two classical papers (appeared in consecutive pages
of the same journal!) by Hartley and David (1954)
and Gumbel (1954). The particular case (
n=2
) of the HDG bound
yields the inequality
\mbox{\rm\hskip 0.86108ptI\hskip-2.15277ptE\hskip 0.86108pt}X_{2:2}\leq\mu+\sigma/\sqrt{3}
,
in which the equality characterizes

{\cal U}(\mu-\sigma\sqrt{3},\mu+\sigma\sqrt{3})
.
Clearly, this is equivalent to Papathanasiou’s covariance bound.
The improvement
on the HDG bound for uniform populations (with
N
elements, in the
sense of Definition
1.1
) is an immediate consequence
of the discrete covariance bound. More precisely,

\mbox{\rm\hskip 0.86108ptI\hskip-2.15277ptE\hskip 0.86108pt}X_{2:2}=\mu+(\mbox{\rm\hskip 0.86108ptI\hskip-2.15277ptE\hskip 0.86108pt}X_{2:2}-\mu)=\mu+\Big(\mbox{\rm\hskip 0.86108ptCov\hskip 0.86108pt}(X_{1:2},X_{2:2})\Big)^{1/2}\leq\mu+(1-N^{-2})^{1/2}\ \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{3}},

and the equality characterizes the lattice population with
mean
\mu
and variance
\sigma^{2}
, that is,

x_{k}=\mu+\sigma\sqrt{3}(2k-N-1)/\sqrt{N^{2}-1}
,
k=1,\ldots,N
.

Remark 3.2
.

(Connection to continuous case).
One may define the functional

\rho_{12}(\cdot)
with domain
{\cal F}:=\{F:F
is a distribution function
with finite non-zero variance
\}
as

\rho_{12}(F):=\rho(X_{1:2},X_{2:2}),\ \ \ \mbox{$X_{1}$, $X_{2}$ independent with distribution $F$},

where

X_{1:2}\leq X_{2:2}
are the order statistics.
For
N\geq 2
, we denote by
{\cal F}_{N}:=\{{\cal P}:{\cal P}=\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{N}\}
is
any uniform population with at least two
distinct elements
\}
(uniform in the sense of Definition
1.1
).
Then it can be verified that

{\cal F}_{0}:=\cup_{N\geq 2}{\cal F}_{N}
is dense in
{\cal F}
with
respect
to the weak topology of probability measures, and the functional

\rho_{12}
is continuous.
Theorem
2.1
shows that
\sup_{F\in{\cal F}_{0}}\rho_{12}(F)=1/2
,
hence, the same is true for
\sup_{F\in{\cal F}}
, and this implies the bound in
Terrell’s result, Theorem
1.1
.

Remark 3.3
.

(An equivalent minimization problem).
To simplify notation, set

\gamma_{k}=2\lambda_{k}/N
(
k=1,\ldots,N-1
) with
\lambda_{k}

as in (
2.6
),
and
\lambda=\alpha_{1}
as in Lemma
2.3
(i);
that is,

\lambda=\frac{1}{3}\Big(2+\frac{1}{N^{2}}\Big),\ \ \ \gamma_{k}=\frac{2(k+1)}{N}\sqrt{\frac{N^{2}-(k+1)^{2}}{(2k+1)(2k+3)}},\ \ k=1,\ldots,N-1.

According to Lemma
2.1
, we have

\Big(\frac{1}{\rho_{12}}\Big)^{2}=\frac{9N^{4}}{(N^{2}-1)^{2}}\ \frac{\Big(\lambda\delta_{1}^{2}+\delta_{2}^{2}+\cdots+\delta_{N-1}^{2}\Big)^{2}-\Big(\gamma_{1}\delta_{1}\delta_{2}+\gamma_{2}\delta_{2}\delta_{3}+\cdots+\gamma_{N-2}\delta_{N-2}\delta_{N-1}\Big)^{2}}{\delta_{1}^{4}},

where this expression should be treated as
+\infty
for
\delta_{1}=0
.
The main difficulty in the proof of Theorem
2.1

was to minimize the above ratio w.r.t.
(\delta_{1},\ldots,\delta_{N-1})\in\mathbb{R}^{N-1}\setminus\{\mbox{\boldmath$0$}\}
,
and the main result showed that the minimizing points are coincide
with the axis
\{(\delta_{1},0,\ldots,0),\delta_{1}\neq 0\}
.
The ratio is scale invariant, so, dividing each
\delta_{k}

by
\delta_{1}\neq 0
(since
\delta_{1}=0
makes
the ratio infinite, and, certainly, cannot be a minimizer)
we can assume that
\delta_{1}=1
. Then, with a slight change
of notation (remove the constant scalar and set
n=N-1
,
x_{k}=\delta_{k}
),
we define

f(x_{2},\ldots,x_{n}):=\Big(\lambda+x_{2}^{2}+\cdots+x_{n}^{2}\Big)^{2}-\Big(\gamma_{1}x_{2}+\gamma_{2}x_{2}x_{3}+\cdots+\gamma_{n-1}x_{n-1}x_{n}\Big)^{2}.

Consequently, it remains to minimize the (four degree polynomial)
function
f
and to prove that its global minimum is uniquely
attained at
0
,
so that
f\geq f(\mbox{\boldmath$0$})=\lambda^{2}
. Despite the fact that
this (unrestricted) minimization problem looks like a simple exercise,
this is not so; the minimization points (if exist) depend on the parameters

\lambda
,
\gamma_{1},\ldots,\gamma_{n-1}
.
To see this, set
n=3
,
\lambda_{0}=11/6

(since
\lambda=\lambda_{0}
when
n=3
),
and write
f(x_{2},x_{3};\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2})=(\lambda_{0}+x_{2}^{2}+x_{3}^{2})^{2}-\big(\gamma_{1}x_{2}+\gamma_{2}x_{2}x_{3}\big)^{2}
.
It can be checked
that the function
f(x_{2},x_{3};4/3,2/3)
,
though positive, is globally minimized at the points
(x_{2},x_{3})
with

x_{2}=\pm\Big\{66\sqrt{6}-81\Big\}^{1/2}\Big/16,\ \ \ x_{3}=(3\sqrt{6}-5)\Big/16,

not at
0
. Also,
f(\cdot\ ;1,2)

is not bounded below; the minimum value of

f(\cdot\ ;1,8/5)
is negative;

f(\cdot\ ;1,1)
attains its global
minimum at
0
. These examples (in the simplest case
n=3
)
indicate that
the multi-parameter case is too complicated, to enable an
explicit solution. In the contrary, the proposed
Terrell-type method of proof succeed in obtaining
Theorems
1.2
,
2.1
.
Motivated from these examples, a natural question of mathematical nature
would ask for (necessary and) sufficient conditions
on the arbitrary positive parameters
\lambda
,
\gamma_{1},\ldots,\gamma_{n-1}
,
guaranteing that the minimum of
f(x_{2},\ldots,x_{n})
, above, is uniquely attained
at
0
.

Remark 3.4
.

(Connection to maximal correlation).
It is important to make clear that the assertion of Theorem

2.1

cannot be
reduced to a maximal correlation problem (unless
N=2
),
in contrast to all of the existing results
mentioned in Section
1
. To see this,
it is useful to recall
Rényi’s (1959) characterization
of the maximal correlation of
(X,Y)
, namely,

R(X,Y)^{2}=\mbox{$\sup_{f}$}\mbox{\rm\hskip 0.86108ptI\hskip-2.15277ptE\hskip 0.86108pt}\Big[\big(\mbox{\rm\hskip 0.86108ptI\hskip-2.15277ptE\hskip 0.86108pt}f(X)|Y\big)^{2}\Big],

where the supremum is taken over
f
with
\mbox{\rm\hskip 0.86108ptI\hskip-2.15277ptE\hskip 0.86108pt}f(X)=0
,

\mbox{\rm\hskip 0.86108ptI\hskip-2.15277ptE\hskip 0.86108pt}f(X)^{2}=1
. Applying Rényi’s result to

(X,Y)=(U_{1:2},U_{2:2})
, and writing
f(k)=x_{k}
,
k=1,\ldots,N
,
it is seen that

R^{2}:=R(U_{1:2},U_{2:2})^{2}=\frac{1}{N^{2}}\max\sum_{j=1}^{N}\frac{1}{2j-1}\left\{2(x_{1}+\cdots+x_{j-1})+x_{j}\right\}^{2},

where the maximum is taken
over
x_{1},\ldots,x_{N}
satisfying

\sum_{k=1}^{N}(2N-2k+1)x_{k}=0,\ \ \ \ \ \sum_{k=1}^{N}(2N-2k+1)x_{k}^{2}=N^{2}.

For
N=3
one finds a maximization point explicitly, namely,

x_{1}^{*}=-\sqrt{\frac{2}{5}+\frac{13}{10\sqrt{19}}},\ \ \ \ \ x_{2}^{*}=\sqrt{1-\frac{2}{\sqrt{19}}},\ \ \ \ \ x_{3}^{*}=\sqrt{4-\frac{1}{2\sqrt{19}}},

yielding
R=(2+\sqrt{19})/15>(1-N^{-2})/(2+N^{-2})=8/19
.
Setting
f(k)=x_{k}^{*}
and
g(Y)=(1/R)\mbox{\rm\hskip 0.86108ptI\hskip-2.15277ptE\hskip 0.86108pt}(f(X)|Y)
,
that is,
g(k)=y_{k}^{*}
with

y_{1}^{*}=-\sqrt{4-\frac{1}{2\sqrt{19}}},\ \ \ \ \ y_{2}^{*}=-\sqrt{1-\frac{2}{\sqrt{19}}},\ \ \ \ \ y_{3}^{*}=\sqrt{\frac{2}{5}+\frac{13}{10\sqrt{19}}},

if is easy to check that the pair
(f,g)
is maximally correlated:

\mbox{\rm\hskip 0.86108ptI\hskip-2.15277ptE\hskip 0.86108pt}f(U_{1:2})=\mbox{\rm\hskip 0.86108ptI\hskip-2.15277ptE\hskip 0.86108pt}g(U_{2:2})=0
,
\mbox{\rm\hskip 0.86108ptI\hskip-2.15277ptE\hskip 0.86108pt}f(U_{1:2})^{2}=\mbox{\rm\hskip 0.86108ptI\hskip-2.15277ptE\hskip 0.86108pt}g(U_{2:2})^{2}=1
, and
\rho(f(U_{1:2}),g(U_{2:2}))=\mbox{\rm\hskip 0.86108ptI\hskip-2.15277ptE\hskip 0.86108pt}f(U_{1:2})g(U_{2:2})=(2+\sqrt{19})/15
.

The situation is similar for all
N
, namely,
it can be shown that
\rho_{N}:=(1-N^{-2})/(2+N^{-2})<R_{N}<1/2
,
N\geq 3
,
where
R_{N}
is the maximal correlation of an ordered pair from the uniform
population,
{\cal P}_{0}
, of size
N
.
Clearly, for
N\geq 4
it would be quite surprising if
R_{N}

could be expressed in a closed form.
Since the maximal correlation is attainable in our case (the corresponding
operator is compact; see Rényi’s, 1959),
the TSF inequality (
1.1
) implies that
R_{N}<1/2
.
Certainly,
R_{N}\geq\rho_{N}
. In order to prove that

R_{N}>\rho_{N}
, it suffices to find a pair of functions with
coefficient of correlation greater than
\rho_{N}
.
Testing small perturbations of linear
functions, we were led to define
f_{0}(x):=x-3x^{2}/N^{3}
,
g_{0}(y):=y+3y^{2}/N^{3}

(both strictly increasing).
Set
\rho_{0}=\rho(f_{0}(U_{1:2}),g_{0}(U_{2:2}))
,

\sigma_{1}^{2}=\mbox{\rm\hskip 0.86108ptVar\hskip 0.86108pt}f_{0}(U_{1:2})
,

\sigma_{2}^{2}=\mbox{\rm\hskip 0.86108ptVar\hskip 0.86108pt}g_{0}(U_{2:2})
.
For
N\geq 3
we computed (with the help
of Mathematica)

\displaystyle\frac{60^{2}\sigma_{1}^{2}\sigma_{2}^{2}N^{14}(2N^{2}+1)^{2}}{(N^{2}-1)^{2}(N^{2}-4)}\left(\rho_{0}^{2}-\rho_{N}^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\hskip-4.30554pt=\hskip-4.30554pt

\displaystyle-666-1332N+846N^{2}+2952N^{3}+1077N^{4}

\displaystyle-828N^{5}-934N^{6}-792N^{7}-485N^{8}+34N^{10}+20N^{12}.

The substitution
N=n+3
(
n\geq 0
)
shows that the right-hand side,

w(n)
, is strictly positive:

\displaystyle w(n)

\displaystyle\hskip-4.30554pt=\hskip-4.30554pt

\displaystyle 7010100+35183016n+72768816n^{2}+86119956n^{3}+66523137n^{4}+35823456n^{5}

\displaystyle+13910474n^{6}+3946848n^{7}+815185n^{8}+119820n^{9}+11914n^{10}+720n^{11}+20n^{12}.

Hence,
R_{N}\geq\rho_{0}>\rho_{N}
for every
N\geq 3
.

The main result of the present note, although quite specialized,
gives rise to a number of questions that, at least to
author’s view, are of some interest.
Let
U_{1:n}\leq\cdots\leq U_{n:n}
be the ordered sample
from the uniform population
{\cal P}_{0}=\{1,\ldots,N\}
.
For
1\leq i<j\leq n
we define

R_{N}=R_{N}(\mbox{$i$,$j$:$n$}):=\mbox{$\sup_{f,g}$}\ \rho(f(U_{i:n}),g(U_{j:n})),\ \ \ \ \ \ R^{\prime}_{N}=R^{\prime}_{N}(\mbox{$i$,$j$:$n$}):=\mbox{$\sup_{g}$}\ \rho(g(U_{i:n}),g(U_{j:n})),

where the suprema are taken over non-constant
f
,
g

(with domain
{\cal P}_{0}
). The first one is the well-known maximal
correlation; the second appeared
in Theorem
2.1

for
i=1
,
j=2
,
n=2
. Moreover, define

\rho_{N}=\rho_{N}(\mbox{$i$,$j$:$n$}):=\rho(U_{i:n},U_{j:n}),\ \ \ \ \ \ R^{\prime\prime}_{N}=R^{\prime\prime}_{N}(\mbox{$i$,$j$:$n$}):=\mbox{$\sup_{g\nearrow}$}\ \rho(g(U_{i:n}),g(U_{j:n})),

where the supremum is taken over nondecreasing non-constant
g
.
It is obvious that for all
i
,
j
,
n
,
N
,

0<\rho_{N}\leq R_{N}^{\prime\prime}\leq R_{N}^{\prime}\leq R_{N}<[i(n+1-j)]^{1/2}[j(n+1-i)]^{-1/2}
; see (
1.1
).
Our preliminary calculations indicate the relation

\rho_{N}=R_{N}^{\prime\prime}=R_{N}^{\prime}<R_{N}
for
all
i
,
j
,
n
,
N
, e.g.,

R_{N}^{\prime}(\mbox{$1$,$3$:$3$})=(3-7N^{-2})/(9-N^{-2})

(checked for small
N
).
If the foregoing relation holds true for all choices
of
i
,
j
,
n
,
N
,
it will verify that the population
{\cal P}_{0}
dominates the
coefficient of correlation of order statistics from any
uniform population
{\cal P}=\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{N}\}
, as in
the continuous case (as well as the without-replacement one).
An extra difficulty here is due to the lack of a simple formula for

\rho_{N}(\mbox{$i$,$j$:$n$})
.

Another natural (but more complicated) extension would be to relax
the uniformity assumption.
One possible way to do this is to endow the population
with a fixed probability vector

{\mbox{\boldmath$p$}}=(p_{1},\ldots,p_{N})

with
p_{k}>0
,
k=1,\ldots,N
.
Then
{\cal P}_{0}({\mbox{\boldmath$p$}})=\{1,\ldots,N\}
is as in the uniform case,
but, now, the point
k
has probability
p_{k}
.
The problem is to obtain the corresponding results
(in an obvious notation) for some of the quantities

\rho_{N}(\mbox{$i$,$j$:$n$};{\mbox{\boldmath$p$}})
,

R^{\prime\prime}_{N}(\mbox{$i$,$j$:$n$};{\mbox{\boldmath$p$}})
,

R^{\prime}_{N}(\mbox{$i$,$j$:$n$};{\mbox{\boldmath$p$}}_{N})
,

R_{N}(\mbox{$i$,$j$:$n$};{\mbox{\boldmath$p$}})
.
The value of
R^{\prime\prime}_{N}
(and
R_{N}^{\prime}
) is of special interest,
since it provides the least upper bound of the
correlation of order statistics among all populations

{\cal P}({\mbox{\boldmath$p$}})
endowed with
p
, i.e.,
containing
N
points
x_{1}\leq\cdots\leq x_{N}
,
x_{1}<x_{N}
, and
the probability of
x_{k}
is
p_{k}
.
Here, in analogy to Definition

1.1
, if ties appear in the population,
e.g., if
x_{k-1}<x_{k}=x_{k+1}=\cdots=x_{s}<x_{s+1}
, then
the probability of
y=x_{k}

is defined to be equal to
p_{k}+\cdots+p_{s}
.
In this setup,
it would be of interest to characterize the population that
maximizes the correlation of, e.g., the extreme order statistics,

\rho\big(X_{1:n},X_{n:n};{\mbox{\boldmath$p$}}\big)
.
Clearly, the actual value
of
R^{\prime\prime}_{N}
depends on
p
, and even the form
of
R^{\prime}_{3}(\mbox{$1$,$2$:$2$};{\mbox{\boldmath$p$}})
is quite complicated.
To see this, consider the (binomial-type) vectors

{\mbox{\boldmath$p$}}=(1/4,1/2,1/4)
and
{\mbox{\boldmath$p$}}=(1/16,3/8,9/16)
.
For the first case we calculate
\rho_{3}=R^{\prime}_{3}=R^{\prime\prime}_{3}=9/23
, while
for the second case one finds

\rho_{3}=169\sqrt{3}/\sqrt{655027}=0.361674...\hskip-2.15277pt<0.362354...\hskip-2.15277pt=R^{\prime}_{3}=R^{\prime\prime}_{3}
,
where
R_{3}^{\prime}
is the unique positive
root of an even, 6th-degree,
polynomial, with (large) integer coefficients.
If all
p_{k}
are rational numbers,
p_{k}=\alpha_{k}/\beta_{k}
,
we can write
p_{k}=c_{k}/M
(in lowest terms). Then,
Theorem
2.1
(applied with ties)
yields the inequality

R_{N}^{\prime}(\mbox{$1$,$2$:$2$};{\mbox{\boldmath$p$}})\leq R^{\prime}_{M}(\mbox{$1$,$2$:$2$})=(1-M^{-2})/(2+M^{-2})
,
a bound that is (often) poor,
since
M
is usually much larger than
N
.
For instance, for the previous (binomial-type) examples
one finds the inaccurate bounds
R_{3}^{\prime}\leq 5/11
(
M=4
)
and
R_{3}^{\prime}\leq 85/171=0.497...
(
M=16
),
respectively.

Finally, fix
N\geq 2
and define

{\cal M}_{N}:=\Big\{{\mbox{\boldmath$p$}}=(p_{1},\ldots,p_{N}):0\leq p_{k}<1\ \ (k=1,\ldots,N),\ \ p_{1}+\cdots+p_{N}=1\Big\}.

It is fairly expected that, as
p
varies in
{\cal M}_{N}
,
the coefficient of correlation
of any two order statistics from
{\cal P}_{0}({\mbox{\boldmath$p$}})

will be maximized in the uniform case,

{\mbox{\boldmath$p$}}=(1/N,\ldots,1/N)
, but no proof is available; we also conjecture
that a similar result is true for the without-replacement case.
