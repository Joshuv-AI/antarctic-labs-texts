Bridging Adversarial and Nonstationary Multi-armed Bandit
Ningyuan Chen, Shuoguang Yang, Hailun Zhang

Abstract
In the multi-armed bandit framework, there are two formulations that are commonly employed to handle time-varying reward distributions: adversarial bandit and nonstationary bandit. Although their oracles, algorithms, and regret analysis differ significantly, we provide a unified formulation in this paper that smoothly bridges the two as special cases. The formulation uses an oracle that takes the best action sequences within a switch budget. Depending on the switch budget, it turns into the oracle in hindsight in the adversarial bandit and dynamic oracle in the nonstationary bandit. We provide algorithms that attain the optimal regret with the matching lower bound. The optimal regret displays distinct behavior in two regimes.

Introduction

The framework of online learning or multi-armed bandit is developed to handle the situation where the agent has to interact repeatedly with an unknown environment.
The agent has to simultaneously learn the rewards of available decisions (or arms) and search for the optimal arm.
The framework has seen great success in many applications, including dynamic pricing, clinical trials, and inventory management
Bubeck and
Cesa-Bianchi 2012
,
den Boer 2015
,
Bouneffouf and
Rish 2019
.

The classic multi-armed bandit considers the stationary environment,
in which the reward distribution of the arms remains constant over time.
This is hardly true in many applications.
For example, in dynamic pricing, the market environment that determines the reward of each arm (charged price) evolves constantly.
It causes the reward of each arm to drift as well.
To address this issue, there are two major frameworks proposed in the literature that do not impose any additional structures on the non-stationarity.

•

Adversarial bandit. The environment evolves in an arbitrary and adversarial way.
As a result, the agent has to prepare for the worst-case realization of the mean reward over the horizon.
To measure the performance of a policy, the
regret
is calculated against a
static oracle
, i.e., the best arm in hindsight over the whole period.
The well-known EXP3 algorithm proposed in
Auer
et al. 1995
achieves the near-optimal regret
\tilde{O}(\sqrt{T})
1
1

1

We use
\tilde{O}(\cdot)
to denote the asymptotic rate omitting the logarithmic factors.
, where
T
is the length of the learning horizon.

•

Nonstationary bandit. The drift of the environment is controlled by a variational budget
V_{T}
.
Note that
V_{T}=0
corresponds to the stationary setting and
V_{T}=\Omega(T)
corresponds to the adversarial setting.
The policy needs to adapt to
V_{T}
and its regret is measured against the
dynamic oracle
, i.e., the best arm in each period.
It has been shown in
Besbes
et al. 2014
,
Besbes
et al. 2019
that the optimal regret is
\tilde{O}(V^{1/3}_{T}T^{2/3})
.

Comparing the two frameworks, there are two major differences.
First, in terms of the nonstationarity in the environment, the adversarial framework allows for more drastic changes.
In particular, the total variation over time can be linear in
T
.
On the other hand, the nonstationary bandit allows for a variational budget
V_{T}
which must be sublinear in
T
; otherwise, it leads to linear regret from the above discussion.
As a result, the adversarial bandit is harder to learn.
Second, despite the difficulty, one can achieve smaller regret (
T^{1/2}
versus
V_{T}^{1/3}T^{2/3}
) in the adversarial bandit, because the oracle against which the regret is measured is weaker.
The static oracle is the best single arm over the horizon in hindsight, unlike the dynamic oracle, which pulls the best arm in each period.
Therefore, the learning in the adversarial bandit has a lower standard than that in the nonstationary bandit.
The two differences drive distinct algorithmic designs, analyses, and regret bounds in the two formulations.

1.1
Our Results

Motivated by the difference of the oracles in the two formulations,
we propose a unified oracle that sits in between the static and dynamic oracles.
That is, we define an oracle that depends on “switch” budget
S_{T}
, i.e., the optimal action sequences that will not switch more than
S_{T}
times.
Specifically, the oracle takes the following form

\max\limits_{H(j_{1},\cdots,j_{t})\leq S_{T}}\sum^{T}_{t=1}\mu_{t,j_{t}},

where
H(\cdot)
is the hardness of the sequence
(j_{1},\cdots,j_{t})
, i.e., the number of switches throughout the sequence.
It is clear that if
S_{T}=1
, i.e., no switch is allowed, then this oracle reduces to the static oracle used in adversarial bandit;
if
S_{T}\geq T
, then it reduces to the dynamic oracle used in nonstationary bandit.

The switch budget
S_{T}
has a natural interpretation in practice.
It essentially reflects the expectation the agent has for the algorithm.
For example, suppose the agent is a firm entering a new business and faces unknown market demand as well as seasonal
shifts in the market environment.
The arms are the prices the firm can charge and the reward is the profit.
If the firm is ambitious, then s/he may choose the switch budget to be the number of days a month and benchmark against the daily optimal prices.
Otherwise the firm may choose the switch budget to be one and benchmark against the monthly optimal prices.
Therefore, the choice of the switch budget reflects the conservativeness of the agent.
As a result, the regret shows how well the algorithm is measured by different degrees of conservativeness.

We design an algorithm for a switch budget
S_{T}
and a variational budget
V_{T}
.
The algorithm attains the following regret.
When
S_{T}
is large, i.e.,
S_{T}\geq K^{-1/3}V_{T}^{2/3}T^{1/3}
(
K
is the number of arms), then the regret is bounded by
{\cal O}((KV_{T})^{1/3}T^{2/3})
;
When
S_{T}
is relatively small, i.e.,
S_{T}\leq K^{-1/3}V_{T}^{2/3}T^{1/3}
, then the regret is bounded by
{\cal O}(\sqrt{KS_{T}T})
.
The regret is shown to be near-optimal in the two regimes.

Our result implies that the regret has a phase transition.
When the switch budget is large, the regret doesn’t depend on the switch budget and the regret is similar to that of nonstationary bandit;
when the switch budget is small, the regret doesn’t depend on the variational budget and the regret is similar to that of adversarial bandit.

Related Literature

This paper is related to the nonstationary bandit literature.
It extends the classic stochastic multi-armed bandit problems by introducing time-varying reward distributions of the arms.
Earlier papers consider discrete changes or a finite set of change points, such as
Auer
et al. 2002
,
Garivier and
Moulines 2011
, and thus is sometimes referred to as switching bandit.
Recent literature of this stream includes
Auer
et al. 2019
,
Chen
et al. 2019
.
In
Besbes
et al. 2014
,
Besbes
et al. 2019
, the changes of the mean rewards can be continuous and a variational budget is imposed.
This setting is further investigated in various extensions, including continuous arms
Besbes
et al. 2015
, unknown variational budget
Cheung
et al. 2019a
, MDPs
Cheung
et al. 2019b
and contextual bandit
Luo
et al. 2018
.
The oracle we consider in this work encapsulates the nonstationary bandit formulation considered in
Besbes
et al. 2014
as a special case.
Our novelty is mainly in the formulation of the unified oracle instead of the algorithmic design and analysis.
There are other formulations to incorporate the nonstationarity, such as
Wu and
Zheng 2022
,
Zhou
et al. 2021
, which are not considered in this paper.

The framework of adversarial bandit is considered in
Auer
et al. 1995
and has been studied extensively in the last few decades.

Bubeck and
Cesa-Bianchi 2012
provides a review of the literature.
Our study is related to the literature on the design of algorithms that are agnostic to the faced bandit problem (adversarial or stochastic)
and can yet achieve optimal or near-optimal regret in both adversarial and stochastic bandit problems
Bubeck and
Slivkins 2012
,
Seldin and
Slivkins 2014
,
Auer and
Chiang 2016
.
Our work differs from this literature in that the agent may face a continuous spectrum of formulations between the adversarial and stochastic (nonstationary) bandit problems.
The agent doesn’t need to detect the specific formulation (associated with the sizes of the windows), which is given as prior information.
This paper is also related to the papers on stochastic bandit with adversarial corruption
Lykouris
et al. 2018
,
Gupta
et al. 2019
,
Golrezaei et al. 2021
.
While this literature uses the dynamic oracle like the nonstationary bandit, we focus on the impact of the conservativeness of the oracle (switch budget) instead of adversarial changes in the distribution.

Problem Formulation

Let
[K]\triangleq\{1,2,\dots,K\}
be the set of arms and
T
be the length of the horizon.
In each period
t\in[T]\triangleq\left\{1,\dots,T\right\}
, the agent chooses an action
A_{t}\in[K]
and a random reward
Y_{t,A_{t}}
is observed and collected.
Conditional on
A_{t}=k
, the random reward
Y_{t,k}\in[0,1]
in period
t
is independent of everything else and has mean
\mu_{t,k}\in[0,1]
, without loss of generality.

The agent does not know
\{\mu_{t,k}\}_{t\in[T],k\in[K]}
initially.
She implements a policy
\pi
to guide the decision in each period.
That is,
A_{t}=\pi_{t}(A_{1},Y_{1,A_{1}},A_{2},Y_{2,A_{2}}\dots,A_{t-1},Y_{t-1,A_{t-1}})
is a mapping from the history prior to period
t
to an arm in period
t
.
The objective of the agent is to maximize the total reward over the horizon
\sum_{t=1}^{T}Y_{t,A_{t}}
.

In the classic multi-armed bandit (MAB) framework, the expected total reward is benchmarked against the “expected reward of the best arm” when
\{\mu_{t,k}\}_{t\in[T],k\in[K]}
were known.
Since we investigate the nonstationary case in which
\mu_{t,k}
may vary over time, there are typically two ways to define the reward of the best arm(s).
One is the dynamic oracle
\sum_{t=1}^{T}\max_{k\in[K]}\mu_{t,k}
.
For the dynamic oracle, it pulls the best arm at time
t
:

a_{t}^{*}=\argmax_{k\in[K]}\mu_{t,k}.

The other is the best arm in hindsight or a static oracle
\max_{k\in[K]}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mu_{t,k}
.
Clearly, the dynamic oracle is a higher standard than the static oracle, which is targeted at the best arm in each period.
If
\mu_{t,k}
varies arbitrarily, then without the knowledge, the agent cannot earn nearly as much reward as the dynamic oracle.
On the other hand, it has been shown in the adversarial bandit literature that algorithms can be developed to nearly match the static oracle in terms of regret.

In this study, we provide a framework to smoothly bridge the two types of oracles.
For a given (deterministic) arm sequence
a_{1},\dots,a_{T}
, we define

H(a_{1},\dots,a_{T})\triangleq\sum_{t=1}^{T-1}\mathbb{I}(a_{t}\neq a_{t+1})+1.

In other words,
H(\cdot)
measures the number of switches (plus one for convenience) in the arm sequence.
We define the
unified oracle
as

\mathcal{O}(s,T,\{\mu_{t,k}\}_{t\in[T],k\in[K]})\triangleq\max\{\sum^{T}_{t=1}\mu_{t,a_{t}}:H(a_{1},\dots,a_{T})\leq s\}.

Note that
\mathcal{O}(\cdot)
is a deterministic function. It measures the performance of an arm sequence under the constraint that the number of switches in the sequence is at most
s-1
.
The value of
s
is the “switch” budget of the unified oracle.

Remark 2.1
.

Our definition of unified oracle is inspired by the hardness function
H(\cdot)
from
Auer
et al. 2002
. To illustrate how to compute the unified oracle, consider the following simple example.
Let
T=K=3
, and
(\mu_{1,1},\mu_{1,2},\mu_{1,3})=(1,0,0)
,
(\mu_{2,1},\mu_{2,2},\mu_{2,3})=(0,1,0)
,
(\mu_{3,1},\mu_{3,2},\mu_{3,3})=(0,0,1)
.
It is clear that if
s=1
, the optimal arm sequence is
(i,i,i)
for any
i\in\{1,2,3\}
, and the unified oracle takes value
\sum^{3}_{t=1}\mu_{t,i}=1
.
If
s=2
, then an optimal arm sequence is
(1,2,2)
and the unified oracle equals to
\mu_{1,1}+\mu_{2,2}+\mu_{3,2}=2
.
If
s=3
, then the optimal arm sequence is
(1,2,3)
, and the unified oracle equals to
\mu_{1,1}+\mu_{2,2}+\mu_{3,3}=3
.
Therefore, a larger switch budget generally leads to better performance of the oracle.

To make the connection to dynamic and static oracles, note that when
s=1
,
we must have
a_{1}=\dots=a_{T}
.
As a result,
\mathcal{O}(1,T,\{\mu_{t,k}\}_{t\in[T],k\in[K]})
is the performance of the best arm in hindsight, regardless of the nonstationarity of the mean rewards.
On the other hand, when
s=T
, then
O(T,T,\{\mu_{t,k}\}_{t\in[T],k\in[K]})
is the performance of the dynamic oracle, because the switch budget is vaccuous and one can let
a_{t}=\argmax\{\mu_{t,k},k\in[K]\}
.
As
s
varies from
1
to
T
, the unified oracle smoothly interpolates between static and dynamic oracles.

Next, we define the (pseudo) regret of a policy
\pi
,
given
T
,
s=S_{T}
, and
\{\mu_{t,k}\}_{t\in[T],k\in[K]}
:

\displaystyle R^{\pi}(T;S_{T},\{\mu_{t,k}\}_{t\in[T],k\in[K]})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}(S_{T},T,\{\mu_{t,k}\}_{t\in[T],k\in[K]})-\mathbb{E}\left(\sum^{T}_{t=1}\mu_{t,A_{t}}\right)

The expectation is taken with respect to the randomness of the history when implementing
\pi
as well as the internal randomization of the policy itself.
As one may expect, the regret depends on how much and frequently the expected rewards
\mu_{t,k}
change over time.
To control for that, we use a metric called the variational budget that has been introduced in the literature to track the cumulative variation of the mean rewards, denoted by
V_{T}
.
More precisely, consider the set of mean rewards

\begin{split}\mathcal{L}(V)=\Big\{\{\mu_{t,k}\}\in[0,1]^{T\times K}:\sum_{t=1}^{T}\sup_{k\in[K]}|\mu_{t,k}-\mu_{t+1,k}|\leq V\Big\}\end{split}

(1)

whose variation over the horizon is bounded by
V
.
See, e.g.,
Besbes
et al. 2014
,
Besbes
et al. 2019
for similar setups.
In the rest of the paper, we investigate the regret

R^{\pi}(T;S_{T},V_{T})=\sup_{\{\mu_{t,k}\}\in\mathcal{L}(V_{T})}R^{\pi}\Big(T;S_{T},\left\{\mu_{t,k}\right\}\Big)

(2)

as a function of
T
, the switch budget
S_{T}
and the variational budget
V_{T}
.
Note that although both
S_{T}
and
V_{T}
are referred to as “budget”,
S_{T}
measures the conservativeness of the oracle while
V_{T}
measures the nonstationarity of the environment.

Remark 2.2
.

When
S_{T}=1
and
V_{T}=T
, the regret
R^{\pi}(T;S_{T},V_{T})
reduces to the adversarial bandit problem benchmarked against a static oracle
(
Auer
et al. 1995
)
.
When
S_{T}=T
and
V_{T}=o(T)
, the regret
R^{\pi}(T;S_{T},V_{T})
reduces to the nonstationary bandit problem benchmarked against a dynamic oracle
(
Besbes
et al. 2014
)
.

Before introducing the algorithm and the analysis, we explicitly state what the initial knowledge of the agent.
The agent has the information of
K
,
T
,
S_{T}
and
V_{T}
, as well as the fact that
\mu_{t,k},Y_{t,k}\in[0,1]
for all
t
and
k
.
The knowledge of
K
is commonly assumed in the literature.
The knowledge of
T
is mostly innocuous.
If
T
is unknown, we can apply the techniques in corollary 8.4 of
Auer
et al. 2002
to modify the algorithm to achieve a similar result.
The switch budget
S_{T}
is usually an intentional choice of the agent instead of a parameter in the problem: it encodes how conservative the agent is and the balance she wants to strike between dynamic and static oracles.
Therefore, it is reasonable to assume the knowledge.
Lastly, we assume the knowledge of the variational budget in our model for simplicity.
It is possible to extend to unknown
V_{T}
using the techniques developed in
Cheung
et al. 2019a
,
Auer
et al. 2019
.
We leave it for future research.

The Adaptive EXP3.S (AE3) Algorithm

In this section, we present an algorithm that achieves near-optimal regret benchmarked against the unified oracle.
The algorithm is referred to as the
AE3
Algorithm.
It is based on the EXP3.S algorithm (Algorithm
1
) as a subroutine, which is proposed in
Auer
et al. 2002
.
We briefly introduce the idea of the EXP3.S algorithm.
In Algorithm
1
, the weights
w_{t,k}
and the learning rate
\gamma
determine the probability of choosing each arm at time
t
(Step 3 and 4).
More precisely, with probability
\gamma
, the arms are chosen uniformly randomly (the second term in Step 3).
With probability
1-\gamma
, the arms are chosen with a probability proportional to the weights of the arms (the first term in Step 3).
Step 5 updates the weights of the arms based on the observed reward.
Note that
\hat{X}_{t,k}
can be regarded as an unbiased estimator for
\mu_{t,k}
and is used to update the weight exponentially.
EXP3.S is based on the well-known EXP3 algorithm
(
Auer
et al. 1995
)
, which is commonly used to handle adversarial bandit problems.
EXP3.S introduces another parameter
\alpha
to update the weights, which adds some flexibility to scenarios where the number of switches in the optimal arm’s identity is finite.

The AE3 algorithm operates based on the following intuitions.
When the value of
S_{T}
is small, the unified oracle closely resembles a static oracle. In this case, the algorithm follows the adversarial framework described by
Auer
et al. 2002
to achieve optimal learning rate tuning.
When the value of
S_{T}

Besbes

The distinction in tuning the learning rate
\gamma

However, with a stronger oracle, the optimal

A few remarks are in line. Firstly, when

et al. 2019

By sharing information within the same batch and “forgetting” information across different batches, this algorithm achieves near-optimal learning efficiency.
In contrast, there is no need to restart the EXP3.S algorithm in Algorithm
2

\alpha
and
\gamma
.
Secondly,

In fact, whenever

3.1
Regret Analysis of Algorithm
2

In this section, we provide an upper bound for the regret of Algorithm
2
with
\alpha=\frac{1}{T}
and defer the detailed proof to the Appendix
B
.

Theorem 3.1
.

Suppose
KV_{T}\leq T
,
K\geq 2
and
S_{T}\log(KT)\geq e
.
The regret of Algorithm
2
with
\alpha=\frac{1}{T}
is bounded by

R^{AE3}(T;S_{T},V_{T})\leq\begin{cases}\bar{C}(KV_{T}\log(KT))^{1/3}T^{2/3}&S_{T}>K^{-1/3}V_{T}^{2/3}T^{1/3}\\
4\sqrt{e-1}\sqrt{KS_{T}T\log(KT)}&S_{T}\leq K^{-1/3}V_{T}^{2/3}T^{1/3},\end{cases}

where
\bar{C}
is an absolute constant.

There are a few implications of the results.
First, the two cases are separated by a threshold of
S_{T}V_{T}^{-2/3}
.
When the switch budget is large or the variational budget is small, the regret is independent of
S_{T}
and is of the same order as that in
Besbes
et al. 2014
,
Besbes
et al. 2019
.
This corresponds to the regime of nonstationary MAB.
When the switch budget is small or the variational budget is large, the regret is independent of
V_{T}
and increases in
S_{T}
.
When
S_{T}=1
, it is consistent with the regret in
Auer
et al. 1995
,
corresponding to the regime of adversarial MAB.
The two regimes have distinct regret behaviors and thus display a phase transition.
Second, the threshold of
S_{T}
that separates the two cases, satisfies

S_{T}V_{T}^{-2/3}=(T/K)^{1/3}
.
We may investigate a few extreme cases: if
V_{T}=\Omega(T)
or
S_{T}=O(T^{1/3})
, then the problem always falls into the adversarial regime and the regret is
{\cal O}(\sqrt{S_{T}T})
.
The variational budget doesn’t affect the regret anymore and the regret is solely determined by the switch budget.
Indeed at the boundary, the two regret expressions are of the same order except for a constant term and a term of order
\log(KT)
.

4
Lower Bound

In this section, we derive a lower bound for the regret of any policy for the problem.
We show that the regret derived in Theorem
3.1
cannot be further improved.

Theorem 4.1
.

For given
T
,
K
,
V_{T}
,
S_{T}
and
K
satisfying
T\geq K\geq 2
and
V_{T}\leq T/K
, the regret for any policy
\pi
is lower bounded by

R^{\pi}(T;S_{T},V_{T})\geq\begin{cases}\left(\frac{1}{16}-\frac{\sqrt{2\log(4/3)}}{16}\right)(KV_{T})^{1/3}T^{2/3}&S_{T}\geq K^{-1/3}V_{T}^{2/3}T^{1/3}\\
\left(\frac{1}{16}-\frac{\sqrt{2\log(4/3)}}{16}\right)\sqrt{KS_{T}T}&S_{T}\leq K^{-1/3}V_{T}^{2/3}T^{1/3}\end{cases}

We provide the detailed proof in the Appendix

We briefly discuss how we construct the problem instances under the two distinct scenarios for the lower bounds.

m
batches
\mathcal{T}_{1},\cdots,\mathcal{T}_{m}
of size
\Delta
(possibly except the last batch
\mathcal{T}_{m}
), where the value of
\Delta
is specified later. We denote by
T_{j}
the number of time periods within batch
\mathcal{T}_{j}
.
It is easy to see that
T_{j}=\Delta
except for

We construct the following bandit instances:
for periods in batch
i
(

k_{i}\in[K]
and set
\mu_{t,k_{i}}=1/2+\epsilon
and
\mu_{t,s}=1/2
for
s\neq k_{i}
in the entire batch.
More precisely, we construct
K^{m}
uniformly distributed bandit instances, denoted by
(k_{1},\dots,k_{m})\in[K]^{m}

For an instance
(k_{1},\dots,k_{m})

\end{cases}

(3)

When
S_{T}\geq K^{-1/3}V_{T}^{2/3}T^{1/3}

6
Future Directions

This paper provides a general formulation that smoothly bridges the nonstationary and adversarial bandit problems studied in the literature.
The proposed unified oracle allows the agent to fine-tune the expectation and conservativeness of the algorithm.
We believe that it provides a flexible framework to handle nonstationary environments.
However, this study also points to several important open directions.
In particular, does there exist a policy or an algorithm that can always achieve the optimal regret while being agnostic to the choice of switch budget
S_{T}
?
Since
S_{T}
only affects the performance of the oracle and does not affect the objective performance of the policy (expected total reward), one would expect that the policy may not need the input of
S
.
This is not the case of our AE3 algorithm and it remains an open direction.
It is worth mentioning that relatedly
Auer
et al. 2019
present a challenge in developing an algorithm that achieves optimal regret bounds in an adversarial setting without requiring pre-defined adjustments based on the number of arm changes.

Proof of Theorem
3.1
:

Firstly, we study the regret when
S_{T}<K^{-1/3}V_{T}^{2/3}T^{1/3}
.
The idea of proof mainly follows
Auer
et al. 2002
.
We denote by
W_{t}
the sum of all weights at time
t
:
W_{t}=\sum^{K}_{k=1}w^{k}_{t}
, and
{\bf j}^{*}=(j^{*}_{1},\cdots,j^{*}_{T})\in\argmax\limits_{H(j_{1},\cdots,j_{t})\leq S}\sum^{T}_{t=1}\mu_{t,j_{t}}
be a sequence of actions with
H({\bf j}^{*})=S_{T}
(the case of
H({\bf j}^{*})<S_{T}
can be proven similarly).
Thus,
(1,2\cdots,T)
is partitioned into segments

\displaystyle[T_{1},\cdots,T_{2}),[T_{2},\cdots,T_{3}),\cdots,[T_{S},\cdots,T_{S+1})

where
T_{1}=1,T_{S+1}=T+1
and
j^{*}_{T_{s}}=\cdots=j^{*}_{T_{s+1}-1}
for each segment
s\in\{1,\cdots,S\}
.
Following the proof of Theorem 8.1 in
Auer
et al. 2002
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{W_{t+1}}{W_{t}}

\displaystyle\leq 1+\frac{\gamma/K}{1-\gamma}X_{t}^{\pi}+\frac{(e-2)(\gamma/K)^{2}}{1-\gamma}\sum^{K}_{k=1}\hat{X}^{k}_{t}+e\alpha,

where
X^{\pi}_{t}
is the random reward obtained under the policy
\pi
at time
t
.
Taking logarithms on both sides of and summing over all
t\in[T_{s},T_{s+1})
, we get

\displaystyle\log\frac{W_{T_{s+1}}}{W_{T_{s}}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\gamma/K}{1-\gamma}\sum^{T_{s+1}-1}_{t=T_{s}}X_{t}^{\pi}+\frac{(e-2)(\gamma/K)^{2}}{1-\gamma}\sum^{T_{s+1}-1}_{t=T_{s}}\sum^{K}_{k=1}\hat{X}^{k}_{t}+e\alpha(T_{s+1}-T_{s}).

(4)

Let
j
be the action such that
j^{*}_{T_{s}}=\cdots=j^{*}_{T_{s+1}-1}=j
.
Since

\displaystyle w^{j}_{T_{s+1}}\geq w^{j}_{T_{s}+1}\exp\left\{\frac{\gamma}{K}\sum^{T_{s+1}-1}_{t=T_{s}+1}\hat{X}^{j}_{t}\right\}\geq\frac{e\alpha}{K}W_{T_{s}}\exp\left\{\frac{\gamma}{K}\sum^{T_{s+1}-1}_{t=T_{s}+1}\hat{X}^{j}_{t}\right\}\geq\frac{\alpha}{K}W_{T_{s}}\exp\left\{\frac{\gamma}{K}\sum^{T_{s+1}-1}_{t=T_{s}}\hat{X}^{j}_{t}\right\}

where the last inequality holds because
\gamma\hat{X}^{j}_{t}/K\leq 1
.
We have

\displaystyle\log\frac{W_{T_{s+1}}}{W_{T_{s}}}\geq\log\frac{w^{j}_{T_{s+1}}}{W_{T_{s}}}\geq\log(\frac{\alpha}{K})+\frac{\gamma}{K}\sum^{T_{s+1}-1}_{t=T_{s}}\hat{X}^{j}_{t}.

(5)

Putting together (
4
) and (
5
), we have

\displaystyle\sum^{T_{s+1}-1}_{t=T_{s}}X_{t}^{\pi}\geq(1-\gamma)\sum^{T_{s+1}-1}_{t=T_{s}}\hat{X}^{j}_{t}-\frac{K\log(K/\alpha)}{\gamma}-(e-2)\frac{\gamma}{K}\sum^{T_{s+1}-1}_{t=T_{s}}\sum^{K}_{k=1}\hat{X}^{k}_{t}-\frac{e\alpha K}{\gamma}(T_{s+1}-T_{s}).

Summing over all segments
s=1,\cdots,S_{T}
, taking expectation with respect to the
noisy rewards and actions of algorithm Exp3.S, and using the fact that
\mu^{k}_{t}\leq 1
for all
k,t
, we have the following result

\displaystyle R^{\pi}(T;S_{T},V_{T})\leq\frac{KS_{T}\log(K/\alpha)+Ke\alpha T}{\gamma}+(e-1)\gamma T.

By choosing
\alpha=1/T
and
\gamma=\min\left\{1,\sqrt{\frac{K(S\log(KT)+e)}{(e-1)T}}\right\}
, the desired results follow.

Next we look at the regret when
S_{T}\geq K^{-1/3}V_{T}^{2/3}T^{1/3}
.
Since
a_{t}^{*}=\max_{k\in[K]}\mu_{t,k}
is defined as the best arm for time period
t
, we have
\sum_{t=1}^{T}\max_{k\in[K]}\mu_{t,k}\geq\max\limits_{H(j_{1},\cdots,j_{t})\leq S_{T}}\sum^{T}_{t=1}\mu_{t,j_{t}}=\mathcal{O}(S_{T},T,\{\mu_{t,k}\}_{t\in[T],k\in[K]})
.
Therefore, the regret is bounded by that against the dynamic oracle for any policy
\pi
, i.e.,

R^{\pi}(T;S_{T},V_{T})\leq\sum_{t=1}^{T}\max_{k\in[K]}\mu_{t,k}-\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{E}[\mu_{t,A_{t}}].

(6)

Instead of establishing an upper bound of
R^{\pi}(T;S,V)
, we only need to provide an upper bound for the regret with respect to the dynamic oracle (
6
).
The proof is completed by Theorem 3 of
Besbes
et al. 2019
.

In summary, we obtain the following worst-case performance of Algorithm
2
:
When
S_{T}>K^{-1/3}V_{T}^{2/3}T^{1/3}
, the regret is bounded by
R^{AE3}(T;S_{T},V_{T})\leq\bar{C}(KV_{T}\log(KT))^{1/3}T^{2/3}

for some absolute constant
\bar{C}
.
When
S_{T}\leq K^{-1/3}V_{T}^{2/3}T^{1/3}
, the regret is bounded by

R^{AE3}(T;S_{T},V_{T})\leq 4\sqrt{e-1}\sqrt{KS_{T}T\log(KT)}
.

Proof of Theorem
4.1
:

To construct problem instances to establish lower bounds, we first group the time horizon
[1,T]
into
m=\lfloor\frac{T}{\Delta}\rfloor
batches
\mathcal{T}_{1},\cdots,\mathcal{T}_{m}
of size
\Delta
(perhaps except
\mathcal{T}_{m}
), where

\mathcal{T}_{j}=\{t:(j-1)\Delta+1\leq t\leq\max\{T,j\Delta\}\}
for all
j=1,\cdots,m
.
Let
T_{j}
be the number of time periods in
\mathcal{T}_{j}
, it is obvious that
T_{j}=\Delta
for
j=1,\cdots,m-1
, and
\Delta\leq T_{m}<2\Delta
.

We construct the following bandit instances:
for periods in batch
i
(
t\in\mathcal{T}_{i}
), we uniformly randomly pick an arm
k_{i}\in[K]
and set
\mu_{t,k_{i}}=1/2+\epsilon_{i}
and
\mu_{t,s}=1/2
for
s\neq k_{i}
in the entire batch.
More precisely, we construct
K^{m}
uniformly distributed bandit instances, denoted by
(k_{1},\dots,k_{m})\in[K]^{m}
.
For an instance
(k_{1},\dots,k_{m})
, the mean reward satisfies

\mu_{t,k}=\begin{cases}1/2+\epsilon_{i}&t\in\mathcal{T}_{i},k=k_{i},\\
1/2&\text{otherwise}.\\
\end{cases}

(7)

The choice of
\epsilon_{i}
will be specified shortly.
The random reward has a Bernoulli distribution based on the mean in (
7
).
We establish the lower bound for the following cases.

Case one:
S_{T}\geq K^{-1/3}V_{T}^{2/3}T^{1/3}
.
This case is similar to Theorem 1 of
Besbes
et al. 2019
.
We set
\Delta=\lceil K^{1/3}(T/V_{T})^{2/3}\rceil
and
\epsilon=\min\left\{\frac{1}{4}\sqrt{\frac{K}{\Delta}},\frac{V_{T}\Delta}{T}\right\}
.
For each batch
\mathcal{T}_{j}
, since
V_{T}\leq T/K
, we have

\sqrt{\frac{K}{\Delta}}\leq\sqrt{\frac{K}{K^{1/3}(T/V_{T})^{2/3}}}=\sqrt{\frac{(KV)^{2/3}}{T^{2/3}}}\leq 1.

Therefore, it is easy to see that

\epsilon_{j}=\min\left\{\frac{1}{4}\sqrt{\frac{K}{\Delta}},\frac{V_{T}\Delta}{T}\right\}\leq\frac{1}{4},

(8)

and the constructed instances (
7
) satisfy
\mu_{t,k}\in[0,1]
.
Moreover, because
\mu_{t,k}
remains constant for
t
in a batch, the total variation is bounded by

\sum_{t=1}^{T-1}\sup_{k\in[K]}|\mu_{t,k}-\mu_{t+1,k}|\leq\sum_{j=1}^{m-1}\epsilon\leq m\epsilon\leq m\frac{V_{T}\Delta}{T}\leq V_{T},

(9)

where we have used the fact that when
t
moves from batch
\mathcal{T}_{j}
to batch
\mathcal{T}_{j+1}
, the change in
\mu_{t,k}
is at most
\epsilon
for all
k
.
Therefore, the variational budget is satisfied.

Next we consider the regret for the MAB instances constructed above.
For any policy
\pi
, the regret
R^{\pi}(T;S,(k_{1},\dots,k_{m}))
under the instance
(k_{1},\dots,k_{m})

can be expressed as

\displaystyle R^{\pi}(T;S_{T},(k_{1},\dots,k_{m}))

\displaystyle=\max\limits_{H(j_{1},\cdots,j_{t})\leq S_{T}}\sum^{T}_{t=1}\mu_{t,j_{t}}-\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{E}[\mu_{t,\pi_{t}}]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left(\frac{1}{2}+\epsilon\right)\Delta-\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sum_{t\in\mathcal{T}_{i}}\left(\frac{1}{2}+\epsilon\mathbb{I}\{\pi_{t}=k_{i}\}\right)

\displaystyle=\epsilon\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sum_{t\in\mathcal{T}_{i}}\mathbb{I}\{\pi_{t}\neq k_{i}\}.

The second equality follows from the fact that
S_{T}\geq\lfloor T/\Delta\rfloor=m
and the construction of the instances (
7
).
We next use a variant of the Assouad method
Yu 1997
to show the lower bound.
More precisely, because the instances satisfy the variational budget, we have

\displaystyle R^{\pi}(T;S_{T},V_{T})

\displaystyle\geq\max_{(k_{1},\dots,k_{m})\in[K]^{m}}R^{\pi}(T;S_{T},(k_{1},\dots,k_{m}))\geq\frac{1}{K^{m}}\sum_{(k_{1},\dots,k_{m})\in[K]^{m}}R^{\pi}(T;S_{T},(k_{1},\dots,k_{m}))

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\epsilon}{K^{m}}\sum_{(k_{1},\dots,k_{m})\in[K]^{m}}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sum_{t\in\mathcal{T}_{i}}\mathbb{P}(\pi_{t}\neq k_{i})

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\epsilon}{K^{m}}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sum_{(k_{1},\dots,k_{i-1},k_{i+1},\dots,k_{m})\in[K]^{m-1}}\sum_{k_{i}\in[K]}\sum_{t\in\mathcal{T}_{i}}\mathbb{P}(\pi_{t}\neq k_{i})

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\epsilon}{K^{m}}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sum_{(k_{1},\dots,k_{i-1},k_{i+1},\dots,k_{m})\in[K]^{m-1}}\left(KT_{i}-\sum_{k_{i}\in[K]}\sum_{t\in\mathcal{T}_{i}}\mathbb{P}(\pi_{t}=k_{i})\right).

(10)

Next we focus on the term
\sum_{k_{i}\in[K]}\sum_{t\in\mathcal{T}_{i}}\mathbb{P}(\pi_{t}=k_{i})
.
Denote the probability measure of
\left\{\pi_{t}\right\}_{t\in\mathcal{T}_{i}}
generated by the instance
(k_{1},\dots,k_{i-1},k_{i})
by
\mathbb{P}_{k}
when
k_{i}=k\in[K]
.
Here we omit the dependence on
(k_{1},\dots,k_{i-1})
which is treated as given in analyzing the term.
Our goal is thus to provide an upper bound for
\sum_{k\in[K]}\sum_{t\in\mathcal{T}_{i}}\mathbb{P}_{k}(\pi_{t}=k)
.
Applying Lemma A.1 from
Auer
et al. 2002
, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{t\in\mathcal{T}_{i}}\mathbb{P}_{k}(\pi_{t}=k)\leq\frac{1}{K}\sum_{t\in\mathcal{T}_{i}}\sum_{k^{\prime}\in[K]}\mathbb{P}_{k^{\prime}}(\pi_{t}=k)+\frac{T_{i}}{2}\sqrt{-\frac{1}{K}\sum_{t\in\mathcal{T}_{i}}\sum_{k^{\prime}\in[K]}\mathbb{P}_{k^{\prime}}(\pi_{t}=k)\log(1-4\epsilon_{i}^{2})}.

Therefore, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{k\in[K]}\sum_{t\in\mathcal{T}_{i}}\mathbb{P}_{k}(\pi_{t}=k_{i})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{K}\sum_{t\in\mathcal{T}_{i}}\sum_{k^{\prime}\in[K]}\sum_{k\in[K]}\mathbb{P}_{k^{\prime}}(\pi_{t}=k)

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{T_{i}}{2}\sum_{k\in[K]}\sqrt{-\frac{1}{K}\sum_{t\in\mathcal{T}_{i}}\sum_{k^{\prime}\in[K]}\mathbb{P}_{k^{\prime}}(\pi_{t}=k)\log(1-4\epsilon^{2})}

\displaystyle\leq T_{i}+\frac{T_{i}}{2}\sqrt{-\sum_{t\in\mathcal{T}_{i}}\sum_{k^{\prime}\in[K]}\sum_{k\in[K]}\mathbb{P}_{k^{\prime}}(\pi_{t}=k)\log(1-4\epsilon^{2})}

\displaystyle\leq T_{i}+\frac{T_{i}}{2}\sqrt{-KT_{i}\log(1-4\epsilon^{2})}.

In the second inequality, we have used Jensen’s inequality and
\sum_{k\in[K]}\mathbb{P}_{k^{\prime}}(\pi_{t}=k)=1
.
Therefore, we have

(
