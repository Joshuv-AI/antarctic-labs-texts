1.
Introduction

In the article, integers, nonnegative integers and natural numbers
are denoted by
\mathbb{Z}
,
\mathbb{Z}_{+}
and
\mathbb{N}
respectively. An
integral polynomial
is a polynomial
taking integer values at the
integers. The polynomials
p(n)
and
q(n)
are
essentially distinct
if
p(n)-q(n)
is not a constant function.

1.1.
Combinatorial motivation

A subset
S
of
\mathbb{Z}
is
piecewise syndetic
if there exists
N\in\mathbb{N}
such that

\bigcup_{i=1}^{N}(S-i)
contains arbitrarily long intervals. In general, a subset
S
of a countable abelian group
(G,+)
is
piecewise syndetic
if there exists a finite subset
F
of
G
such that
\bigcup_{i\in F}(S-i)
is
thick
in
G
, i.e. it contains a shifted copy of any finite subset of
G
. The set of all piecewise syndetic subsets of
G
will be denoted by
\mathcal{F}_{ps}(G)
, or simply
\mathcal{F}_{ps}.

Van der Waerden’s theorem states that each piecewise syndetic subset of
\mathbb{Z}
contains arbitrarily long arithmetic progressions. That is, if
S\subseteq\mathbb{Z}
is piecewise syndetic, then for all
d\in\mathbb{N}
, the set
\{(m,n)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}:m,m+n,\ldots,m+(d-1)n\in S,n\neq 0\}
is not empty.
Furstenberg and Glasner
[
15
]
obtained the following beautiful result using the Stone-Čech compactification of
\mathbb{Z}
.

Theorem
(Furstenberg-Glasner)

Let
d\in\mathbb{N}
and
S
be piecewise syndetic in
\mathbb{Z}
, then

\{(m,n)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}:m,m+n,\ldots,m+(d-1)n\in S\}

is piecewise syndetic in
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
.

Later Beiglböck
[
6
]
provided a combinatorial proof for the Furstenberg-Glasner’s result just using van der Waerden’s theorem.
Bergelson and Hindman
[
7
]
extended this result to apply to
many notions of largeness in arbitrary semigroups and to partition regular
structures other than arithmetic progressions. One of the questions asked in
[
7
, Question 4.7]
is as follows:

Question 1
.

Let
d\in\mathbb{N}
and
p_{i}
be an integral polynomial with
p_{i}(0)=0
,
1\leq i\leq d
.
Is it true that whenever
S
is piecewise syndetic in
\mathbb{Z}
, then

\{(m,n)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}:m+p_{1}(n),m+p_{2}(n),\ldots,m+p_{d}(n)\in S\}

is piecewise syndetic in
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
?

1.2.
Dynamical motivation

By a
topological dynamical system
(t.d.s. or system for short) we mean a pair
(X,T)
, where
X
is a compact metric space (with a metric
\rho
) and
T:X\to X
is a homeomorphism.
For
x\in X
, the
orbit of
x\in X
is defined by
\mathcal{O}(x,T)=\{T^{n}x:n\in\mathbb{Z}\}
.
A t.d.s.
(X,T)
is
transitive
if for any pair of non-empty open subsets
U
and
V
of
X
, there is
n\in\mathbb{Z}
with
U\cap T^{-n}V\not=\emptyset
;
it is
weakly mixing
if
(X\times X,T\times T)
is transitive; and
it is
minimal
if
\mathcal{O}(x,T)
is dense in
X
for every
x\in X
. A point
x\in X

is
minimal
or
uniformly recurrent
if the subsystem
(\overline{\mathcal{O}(x,T)},T)
is minimal.
Recall that a subset
S
of
\mathbb{Z}
is
syndetic
if it has a bounded gap,
i.e. there is
N\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\{i,i+1,\cdots,i+N\}\cap S\neq\emptyset
for every
i\in{\mathbb{Z}}
.
S
is
thick
if it
contains arbitrarily long runs of integers. It is easy to verify that
a subset
S
of
\mathbb{Z}
is piecewise syndetic if and only if it is an
intersection of a syndetic set with a thick set. Let
(X,T)
be a t.d.s. and
x\in X
. A classic result states that
x
is a minimal
point if and only if
N_{T}(x,U)\triangleq\{n\in\mathbb{Z}:T^{n}x\in U\}
is syndetic for any neighborhood

U
of
x
(
[
21
, Chapter 4]
).

The Birkhoff recurrence theorem claims that any t.d.s.
(X,T)
has a recurrent point
x
, that is, there is some increasing sequence
\{n_{k}\}_{k=1}^{\infty}
of
\mathbb{N}
such that
T^{n_{k}}x\to x,
as
k\to\infty
.
Birkhoff recurrence theorem has the following generalization: for any
d\in\mathbb{N}
, there exist some
x\in X
and some increasing sequence
\{n_{k}\}_{k=1}^{\infty}
of
\mathbb{N}
such that
T^{in_{k}}x\to x,
as
k\to\infty

simultaneously for
i=1,\ldots,d
. We refer to this theorem as
the multiple Birkhoff recurrence theorem
, which is equivalent to van der Waerden’s Theorem (
[
13
, Theorem 2.6]
,

[
16
, Theorem 1.4]
). The multiple Birkhoff recurrence theorem can be deduced from the multiple recurrence theorem of Furstenberg
[
12
, Theorem 1.5]
which was proved by using deep measure theoretic tools. It is Furstenberg and Weiss who presented a topologically dynamical proof of the theorem (
[
16
, Theorem 1.4]
). In the theorem, such a point
x
is called a
multiply recurrent point
, i.e.,
x^{\otimes d}\triangleq(x,x,\ldots,x)\in X^{d}
is a recurrent point of
\tau_{d}\triangleq T\times T^{2}\times\cdots\times T^{d}
.

Furstenberg
[
12
]
proved Szemerédi’s theorem via the multiple Poincaré recurrence theorem, which can be used to prove
that for a t.d.s.
(X,T)
and any
T
-invariant Borel probability measure
\mu

on
X
, for
\mu
almost all
x\in X
, every
d\in\mathbb{N}
and
every neighborhood
U
of
x
the set
N_{\tau_{d}}(x^{\otimes d},U^{d})=\{n\in\mathbb{Z}:(\tau_{d})^{n}x^{\otimes d}\in U^{d}=U\times\cdots\times U\}

has positive upper density. Can we say more properties related to
N_{\tau_{d}}(x^{\otimes d},U^{d})
?
In
[
14
]
Furstenberg asked a question about multiply uniform recurrence:
Does there always exist a point
x
such that
x^{\otimes d}
is a minimal point for
\tau_{d}
, i.e.
N_{\tau_{d}}(x^{\otimes d},U^{d})
is syndetic
for each neighborhood
U
of
x
?
Recall that a t.d.s.
(X,T)
is
distal
if for any
x\not=y\in X
,
\liminf_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\rho(T^{n}x,T^{n}y)>0
.
For a distal t.d.s.
(X,T)
and any
x\in X
,
x^{\otimes d}
is not only a recurrent point of
\tau_{d}
,
but also a minimal point of
\tau_{d}
(
[
14
, Proposition 5.11]
). In
[
27
]
Huang, Shao and Ye demonstrated that there exists a t.d.s.
(X,T)
without any multiply
minimal points, which gives a negative answer to the question by Furstenberg.

According to this negative answer, one can ask a weaker question:
Let
(X,T)
be a t.d.s. and
d\in\mathbb{N}
.
Is there a point
x\in X
such that
x^{\otimes d}
is piecewise syndetic recurrent? That is, for any neighborhood

U
of
x
, is the set

N_{\tau_{d}}(x^{\otimes d},U^{d})=\{n\in\mathbb{Z}:(\tau_{d})^{n}x^{\otimes d}\in U\times\cdots\times U\}

piecewise syndetic?

For weakly mixing minimal t.d.s. there do exist such points (see Remark
2.8
below). In general, we conjecture that it is not true. Nervelessness, we have the following result:

Theorem

[
27
]

Let
(X,T)
be a minimal system and
d\in\mathbb{N}
. Then for any non-empty open
subset
U
of
X
, there is some
x\in U
such that

N_{\tau_{d}}(x^{\otimes d},U^{d})
is piecewise syndetic.

Moreover, we think that this result is sharp, since it was shown in
[
27
]
for a minimal PI system
(X,T)
and
x\in X
,
(x,x)
is minimal under
T\times T^{2}
if and only if for each neighborhood
U
of
x
,
\{n\in\mathbb{Z}:T^{n}x\in U,T^{2n}x\in U\}\in\mathcal{F}_{ps}
, and we
conjecture that there is a minimal PI system
(X,T)
which has no point
x\in X
such that
x
is multiply minimal,
for more discussions on this aspect see
[
27
]
.
According to the result obtained in
[
27
]
, it is natural to ask the following question.

Question 2
.

Let
(X,T)
be a minimal t.d.s. and
d\in\mathbb{N}
. Let
p_{i}
be an integral polynomial with
p_{i}(0)=0
,
1\leq i\leq d
.
Is it true that for each non-empty open set
U
, there is a point
x\in U
such that

N_{\{p_{1},\ldots,p_{d}\}}(x,U):=\{n\in\mathbb{Z}:T^{p_{1}(n)}x\in U,\ldots,T^{p_{d}(n)}x\in U\}

is piecewise syndetic?

1.4.
The main ideas of the proofs

During the past several decades, one of the most important achievements in ergodic theory is that for a measure preserving system

(X,\mathcal{X},\mu,T)
, each
f\in L^{\infty}(X,\mu)
can be decomposed into three parts, i.e.
f=f_{\rm unif}+f_{\rm nil}+f_{\rm sml}
, where
f_{\rm nil}
is the structured
one,
f_{\rm unif}
is the random one and
f_{\rm sml}
is the error one, see for example
[
23
, Theorem 16.10]
. The structured part
f_{\rm nil}
depends on the algebraic
structure of the
d
-step pro-nilfactor of
(X,\mathcal{X},\mu,T)
.

Searching for the corresponding result in topological dynamics (minimal systems) has a long history, starting from the early work
of Glasner in 1994
[
18
]
. Finally, the problem was settled down in the recent work of Glasner, Huang, Shao, Weiss and Ye

[
20
]
by showing a saturation theorem for linear polynomials, which opens a door for possible study of many questions. In ergodic theory, when dealing with
questions related to polynomials, a powerful tool is the van der Corput lemma. Unfortunately, there is no similar tool in topological dynamics.
Thus, the saturation theorem for polynomials in topological dynamics can not be deduced from the linear one directly and one needs extra work to
complete it. In the recent nice work by Qiu
[
34
]
, the author proved a weak version of the saturation theorem for polynomials, which allows him
to give a complete answer to a well known question concerning the density of polynomial orbits in a totally minimal systems (for some previous
progress see
[
20
]
). One of the main contributions and tools in the current work is Theorems
3.6
and
3.10
(strengthening the result of Qiu) which we will refer as the saturation theorems for polynomials.

By Furstenberg’s correspondence principle, we can associate each piecewise syndetic subset
S
of
\mathbb{Z}
with a minimal system
(X,T)
, whose dynamical properties are related to combinatorial properties of
S
. The other main contribution of the current
paper is that we further associate
(X,T)
and given polynomials
\mathcal{A}=\{p_{1},\ldots,p_{d}\}
two dynamical systems:
one is
\mathbb{Z}
action
(W_{x}^{\mathcal{A}},\sigma)
, and the other one is
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
action
(M_{\infty}(X,\mathcal{A}),\langle T^{\infty},\sigma\rangle)

(see Section
4
).
For example, when
\mathcal{A}=\{n^{2}\}
and
x\in X
we define

W_{x}^{\mathcal{A}}=\overline{\mathcal{O}}((T^{n^{2}}x)_{n\in\mathbb{Z}},\sigma)=\overline{\{(\ldots,T^{(n-1)^{2}}x,\underset{\bullet}{T}^{n^{2}}x,T^{(n+1)^{2}}x,\ldots):n\in\mathbb{Z}\}}\subset X^{\mathbb{Z}},

where
``\ \underset{\bullet}{\ }"
means the
0
-th coordinate, and
\sigma
is the left shift;
and

\begin{split}M_{\infty}(X,\mathcal{A})&=\overline{\mathcal{O}}((T^{n^{2}}x)_{n\in\mathbb{Z}},\langle T^{\infty},\sigma\rangle)\\
&=\overline{\{(\ldots,T^{m+(n-1)^{2}}x,\underset{\bullet}{T}^{m+n^{2}}x,T^{m+(n+1)^{2}}x,\ldots):n,m\in\mathbb{Z}\}}\subset X^{\mathbb{Z}},\end{split}

where
T^{\infty}=\cdots\times T\times T\times\cdots
, and
\langle T^{\infty},\sigma\rangle

is the group generated by
T^{\infty}
and
\sigma
.
We believe that such a consideration is new, which is not only very powerful in the current paper, but also provides a useful tool to study some other questions.
Note that the idea was inspired by some consideration carried out in
[
23
, Chapter 14]
for nilmanifolds and the shift
\sigma
. Moreover, in a companion paper

[
28
]

by the same authors, we will construct measure preserving transformations associated with a given measure preserving transformation and
given polynomials, and derive some applications.

By showing the density of minimal points in
M_{\infty}(X,\mathcal{A})
(Theorem
7.8
), we obtain our Theorem E (using Theorems
4.10
and
7.8
), and by lifting the property
from
\infty
-step pro-nilfactors, we show Theorem D. And then, Theorems A and B follow from Theorems E and D immediately.
We remark that the proof of Theorem D can be argued by the same method used in the proof of Theorem E, i.e. by showing that
if
\deg(p_{i})\geq 2,\ 1\leq i\leq d
, then the minimal points are dense in
W_{x}^{\mathcal{A}}
under
\sigma
, and then dealing further when there
are linear terms. Here we adopt another proof
to show how to use the saturation theorem for polynomials in a different way.

Precisely, to show Theorem D we use the saturation theorem for polynomials and Leibman’s result on the polynomial orbits on
nilmanifolds. When there are no linear terms in
\mathcal{A}
(resp.
(X,T)
is weakly mixing or distal), the situation is relatively
simple and we can get a stronger result (Theorem C). But
when there are linear terms in
\mathcal{A}
, things are much involved and we need some extra discussion, see the Claim in the proof
of Theorem C.

Unfortunately, we do not know how to show Theorem E by using the idea of the proof of Theorem D.
To illustrate the idea and the difficult we face in the proof of Theorem E,
we first give the proof when
(X,T)
is distal (see Theorem
5.7
). From the proof of the distal case, we realize that we need suitable elements in
{\rm Aut}(X,T)
, the group of automorphisms of
(X,T)
.
To find the elements that we need, we pass our discussion to the universal minimal flow to find rich elements in
{\rm Aut}(X,T)
for our purpose,
i.e. we use the regularizer from the theory of minimal flows to prove our theorem. Since our approach to show Theorem E uses heavy tools, it will be nice if one finds some elementary arguments to prove it.

To finish the subsection we state the following corollary of Theorem E.
Note that a t.d.s. is an
M
-system if it is transitive and the set of minimal points is dense.

Corollary F
:
Let
(X,T)
be a t.d.s. and
d\in\mathbb{N}
. Let
\mathcal{A}=\{p_{1},\ldots,p_{d}\}

be an family of integral polynomials with
p_{i}(0)=0
,
1\leq i\leq d
. Then

(1)

(X,T)
has dense minimal points if and only if so does
(M_{\infty}(X,\mathcal{A}),\langle T^{\infty},\sigma\rangle)
.

(2)

(X,T)
is an
M
-system if and only if so is
(M_{\infty}(X,\mathcal{A}),\langle T^{\infty},\sigma\rangle)
.

We remark that generally the minimality of
(X,T)
does not imply the minimality of
(M_{\infty}(X,\mathcal{A}),\langle T^{\infty},\sigma\rangle)
, see

.

2.
Preliminaries

In this section we give some necessary notions and some known facts used in the paper.
Note that, instead of just considering a single transformation
T
,
we will consider commuting
transformations
T_{1}
,
\ldots
,
T_{k}
of
X
. We only recall some basic
definitions and properties of systems for
one transformation. Extensions to the general case are
straightforward.

2.1.
Topological dynamical systems

2.1.1.

By a
topological dynamical system
(t.d.s.) we mean a pair
(X,T)
, where

X
is a compact Hausdorff space (in the paper we mainly deal with a compact metric space
X
with a metric
\rho
except in
Section
7
) and
T:X\to X
is a
homeomorphism. Let
(X,T)
be a t.d.s. and
x\in X
. Then
\mathcal{O}(x,T)=\{T^{n}x:n\in\mathbb{Z}\}
denotes the

orbit
of
x
. A subset
A\subseteq X
is called
invariant
(or
T
-invariant) if
TA=A
. When
Y\subseteq X
is a closed and
invariant subset of the system
(X,T)
, we say that the system

(Y,T|_{Y})
is a
subsystem
of
(X,T)
. Usually we will omit the subscript, and denote
(Y,T|_{Y})
by
(Y,T)
.
If
(X,T)
and
(Y,S)
are two t.d.s., their
product system
is the
system
(X\times Y,T\times S)
.

When there are more than one t.d.s. involved, usually we should use different symbols to denote different transformations on different spaces, for example,
(X,T),(Y,S),(Z,H)
etc. But when no confusing, it is convenient to use only one symbol
T
for all transformations in all t.d.s. involved, for example,
(X,T),(Y,T),(Z,T)

2.2.1.

A

2.2.2.

The proof of the following lemma is easy.

Lemma 2.2
.

We have the following

(1)

is dense in

(2)

(3)

Let

Proof.

(1) and (3) are standard facts. It remains to show (2). Let

T
and

Let

Let
(X,T)

One says that:

2.3.
Furstenberg families

Let us recall some notions related to Furstenberg families (for
details see
[
1
, Chapter 2]
or
[
13
, Chapter 9]
). We only discuss subsets of
\mathbb{Z}
here, and it is similar for subsets of
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
.

2.3.1.

Let
\mathcal{P}=\mathcal{P}({\mathbb{Z}})
be the collection
of all non-empty subsets of
\mathbb{Z}
. A subset
\mathcal{F}
of
\mathcal{P}
is a
(Furstenberg) family
, if it is hereditary upwards, i.e.,
F_{1}\subseteq F_{2}
and
F_{1}\in\mathcal{F}
imply
F_{2}\in\mathcal{F}
. A family
\mathcal{F}
is

proper
if it is a proper subset of
\mathcal{P}
, i.e. neither empty
nor all of
\mathcal{P}
. If a proper family

\mathcal{F}
is closed under finite intersections, then
\mathcal{F}
is called a
filter
. For a family
\mathcal{F}
, the
dual family
is

\mathcal{F}^{*}=\{F\in\mathcal{P}:{\mathbb{Z}}\setminus F\notin\mathcal{F}\}=\{F\in\mathcal{P}:F\cap F^{\prime}\neq\emptyset\ for\ all\ F^{\prime}\in\mathcal{F}\}.

Denote by
\mathcal{F}_{inf}
the family consisting of all infinite subsets of
\mathbb{Z}
.
The
collection of all syndetic (resp. thick) subsets of
\mathbb{Z}
is denoted by

\mathcal{F}_{s}
(resp.
\mathcal{F}_{t}
). Note that
\mathcal{F}_{s}^{*}=\mathcal{F}_{t}
and
\mathcal{F}_{t}^{*}=\mathcal{F}_{s}
. Denote the set of
all piecewise syndetic sets by
\mathcal{F}_{ps}
. A set
F
is called
thickly syndetic
if for every
N\in\mathbb{N}
the positions where intervals with length
N
runs begin form
a syndetic set. Denote the set of
all thickly syndetic sets by
\mathcal{F}_{ts}
, and we have
\mathcal{F}_{ts}
is a filter and
\mathcal{F}^{*}_{ps}=\mathcal{F}_{ts},\mathcal{F}^{*}_{ts}=\mathcal{F}_{ps}
.

2.3.2.

Let
\mathcal{F}
be a family and
(X,T)
be a t.d.s. We say
x\in X
is

\mathcal{F}
-
recurrent
if for each neighborhood
U
of
x
,
N(x,U)\in\mathcal{F}
. So the usual recurrent point is just
\mathcal{F}_{inf}
-recurrent one.
The following proposition characterizes piecewise syndetic recurrence (see for example
[
25
, Lemma 2.1]
or
[
1
, Theorem 3.1]
).

Proposition 2.4
.

Let
(X,T)
be a transitive t.d.s. Then the following are equivalent:

(1)

(X,T)
is an
M
-system, i.e., it is transitive and the minimal points are dense in
X
;

(2)

each transitive point
x
is
\mathcal{F}_{ps}
-recurrent;

(3)

there is a transitive point
x
that is
\mathcal{F}_{ps}
-recurrent.

Remark 2.5
.

Proposition
2.4
still holds for t.d.s. under
\mathbb{Z}^{k}
-actions.
Let
T_{1},T_{2},\ldots,T_{k}:X\rightarrow X
be commuting homeomorphisms (

). Let

be the group generated by

. Then

is an

and any neighbourhood

.
For a proof, we refer to
[

, Theorem 3.1]
.

2.4.
Some facts about hyperspaces

2.4.1.

Let
X
be a compact metric space. Let
2^{X}
be the collection of nonempty closed subsets of
X
.
Let
\rho
be the metric on
X
,
then one may define a metric on
2^{X}
as follows:

\begin{split}\rho_{H}(A,B)&=\inf\{\epsilon>0:A\subseteq B_{\epsilon}(B),B\subseteq B_{\epsilon}(A)\}\\
&=\max\{\max_{a\in A}\rho(a,B),\max_{b\in B}\rho(b,A)\},\end{split}

where
\rho(x,A)=\inf_{y\in A}\rho(x,y)
and
B_{\epsilon}(A)=\{x\in X:\rho(x,A)<\epsilon\}
.
The metric
\rho_{H}
is called the
Hausdorff metric
of
2^{X}
.

Let
\{A_{i}\}_{i=1}^{\infty}
be an arbitrary sequence of subsets of
X
. Define

\liminf A_{i}=\{x\in X:\text{for any neighbourhood $U$ of $x$, $U\cap A_{i}\neq\emptyset$ for all but finitely many $i$}\};

\limsup A_{i}=\{x\in X:\text{for any neighbourhood $U$ of $x$, $U\cap A_{i}\neq\emptyset$ for infinitely many $i$}\}.

We say that
\{A_{i}\}_{i=1}^{\infty}
converges to
A
, denoted by
\lim_{i\to\infty}A_{i}=A
, if

\liminf A_{i}=\limsup A_{i}=A.

Now let
\{A_{i}\}_{i=1}^{\infty}\subseteq 2^{X}
and
A\in 2^{X}
. Then
\lim_{i\to\infty}A_{i}=A
if and only if
\{A_{i}\}_{i=1}^{\infty}
converges to
A
in
2^{X}
with respect to the Hausdorff metric.

2.4.2.

Let
X,Y
be two compact metric spaces. Let
F:Y\rightarrow 2^{X}
be a map and
y\in Y
.
We say that
F
is
upper semi-continuous (u.s.c.)
at
y
if whenever
\lim y_{i}=y
, one has that
\limsup F(y_{i})\subseteq F(y)
. If
F
is u.s.c. at every point of
Y
, then we say that
F
is u.s.c.
We say
F
is
lower semi-continuous (l.s.c.)
at
y
if whenever
\lim y_{i}=y
, one has that
\liminf F(y_{i})\supset F(y)
. If
F
is l.s.c. at every point of
Y
, then we say that
F
is l.s.c.

It is easy to verify that
F:Y\rightarrow 2^{X}
is u.s.c. at
y\in Y
if and only if for each
\epsilon>0
there exists a neighbourhood
U
of
y
such that
F(U)\subseteq B_{\epsilon}(F(y))
; and
F:Y\rightarrow 2^{X}
is l.s.c. at
y\in Y
if and only if for each
\epsilon>0
there exists a neighbourhood
U
of
y
such that
F(y)\subseteq B_{\epsilon}(F(y^{\prime}))
for all
y^{\prime}\in U
.

If
f:X\rightarrow Y
is a continuous map, then it is easy to verify that

F=f^{-1}:Y\rightarrow 2^{X},y\mapsto f^{-1}(y)

is u.s.c. Let
(X,T)
be t.d.s. Then the map

F:X\rightarrow 2^{X},x\mapsto\overline{\mathcal{O}(x,T)}

is l.s.c. (see Subsection
3.2
).

We have the following well known result, for a proof see
[
30
, p.70-71]
and
[
29
, p.394]
, or
[
11
]
.

Theorem 2.6
.

Let
X,Y
be compact metric spaces. If
F:Y\rightarrow 2^{X}
is u.s.c. (or l.s.c.),
then the points of continuity of
F
form a dense
G_{\delta}
set in
Y
.

Let

2.6.

2.6.1.

Let
G

[g,h]=ghg^{-1}h^{-1}
for the commutator of

The commutator subgroups

2.6.2.

Let

.
The Haar measure
\mu
of

X

and let

be the
transformation
x\mapsto tx
of

is
called a

-step nilsystem
. In the topological setting we omit the measure
and just say that

is a

-step nilsystem. For more details on nilsystems, refer to
[

, Chapter 11]
.
Here are some basic properties of nilsystems.

Theorem 2.9
.

[

,
33
]

Let

-step nilsystem with

(1)

(X,T)
is uniquely ergodic if and only if
(X,\mu,T)
is
ergodic if and only if
(X,T)
is minimal if and only if

(2)

, where

\rho(T^{{\bf n}\cdot\epsilon}x^{\prime},T^{{\bf n}\cdot\epsilon}y^{\prime})<\delta\ \text{for every $\epsilon=(\epsilon_{1},\ldots,\epsilon_{d})\in\{0,1\}^{d}\setminus\{00\cdots 0\}$},

3.2.
A l.s.c. map

Recall that for metric spaces
X
and
Y
, the map
F:Y\rightarrow 2^{X}
is l.s.c. at

if and only if for each
\epsilon>0
there exists a neighbourhood
U
of
y
with
F(y)\subseteq B_{\epsilon}(F(y^{\prime}))
for all
y^{\prime}\in U
.

Let
(X,T)
be a t.d.s. and
d\in\mathbb{N}
. Let
\mathcal{A}=\{p_{1},\ldots,p_{d}\}
be a set of integral polynomials and define

\mathcal{O}_{\mathcal{A}}((x_{1},\ldots,x_{d}))=\mathcal{O}_{\{p_{1},\ldots,p_{d}\}}((x_{1},\ldots,x_{d}))=\{(T^{p_{1}(n)}x_{1},\ldots,T^{p_{d}(n)}x_{d}):n\in\mathbb{Z}\},

and

\overline{\mathcal{O}}_{\mathcal{A}}((x_{1},\ldots,x_{d}))=\overline{\{(T^{p_{1}(n)}x_{1},\ldots,T^{p_{d}(n)}x_{d}):n\in\mathbb{Z}\}}.

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
(X,T)
be a t.d.s. and
d\in\mathbb{N}
. Let
\mathcal{A}=\{p_{1},\ldots,p_{d}\}
be a set of integral polynomials and define

G_{\mathcal{A}}:X^{d}\rightarrow 2^{X^{d}},(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d})\mapsto\overline{\mathcal{O}}_{\mathcal{A}}((x_{1},\ldots,x_{d})).

Then
G_{\mathcal{A}}
is l.s.c.

Proof.

Let
{\bf x}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d})\in X^{d}
and
\epsilon>0
. Let
g(n)({\bf x})=(T^{p_{1}(n)}x_{1},\ldots,T^{p_{d}(n)}x_{d})
for
n\in\mathbb{Z}
. Then
G_{\mathcal{A}}({\bf x})=\overline{\mathcal{O}}_{\mathcal{A}}({\bf x})=\overline{\{g(n){\bf x}:n\in\mathbb{Z}\}}
. Since
G_{\mathcal{A}}({\bf x})
is compact, there exist
m_{1},m_{2}\ldots,m_{k}\in\mathbb{Z}
such that
\{g(m_{1})({\bf x}),g(m_{2})({\bf x}),\ldots,g(m_{k})({\bf x})\}
is
\epsilon/2
-dense in
G_{\mathcal{A}}({\bf x})
.

Since
T
is continuous, there is some
\delta>0
such that whenever
\rho_{d}({\bf x},{\bf x^{\prime}})<\delta
,

\rho_{d}(g(m_{i})({\bf x}),g(m_{i})({\bf x^{\prime}}))<\epsilon/2,\ 1\leq i\leq k,

where
\rho_{d}
is the metric of
X^{d}
. Now we show for each
{\bf x^{\prime}}\in X^{d}
, if
\rho_{d}({\bf x},{\bf x^{\prime}})<\delta
, then
G_{\mathcal{A}}({\bf x})\subseteq B_{\epsilon}(G_{\mathcal{A}}({\bf x^{\prime}}))
.
In fact, let
{\bf y}\in G_{\mathcal{A}}({\bf x})
. Then there is some
i\in\{1,2\ldots,k\}
such that
\rho_{d}(g(m_{i})({\bf x}),{\bf y})<\epsilon/2
. As
\rho_{d}(g(m_{i})({\bf x}),g(m_{i})({\bf x^{\prime}}))<\epsilon/2
, we have that
\rho_{d}(g(m_{i})({\bf x^{\prime}}),{\bf y})<\epsilon
. That means
{\bf y}\in B_{\epsilon}(G_{\mathcal{A}}({\bf x^{\prime}}))
.
The proof is complete.
∎

\{n_{i}\}_{i=1}^{\infty}\subseteq\mathbb{Z}

(T^{p_{1}(n_{i})}y^{i},T^{p_{2}(n_{i})}y^{i},\ldots,T^{p_{d}(n_{i})}y^{i})\rightarrow{\bf y}=(y_{1},y_{2},\ldots,y_{d}),\ i\to\infty.

Since
y^{i}\in Y_{0}
, there is some
x^{i}\in X_{0}^{*}
with
y^{i}=\pi(x^{i})
and then by Theorem
3.6
,

({\pi^{*}}^{(d)})^{-1}(T^{p_{1}(n_{i})}y^{i},T^{p_{2}(n_{i})}y^{i},\ldots,T^{p_{d}(n_{i})}y^{i})\subseteq\overline{\mathcal{O}_{\mathcal{A}}}((x^{i})^{\otimes d})\subseteq N_{\mathcal{A}}(X^{*}).

As
\pi^{*}
is open,
({\pi^{*}}^{(d)})^{-1}
is continuous and it follows that

(\pi^{(d)})^{-1}(y_{1},y_{2},\ldots,y_{d})\subseteq N_{\mathcal{A}}(X^{*}).

Thus we conclude that
({\pi^{*}}^{(d)})^{-1}\Big(N_{\mathcal{A}}(X_{\infty}^{*})\Big)\subseteq N_{\mathcal{A}}(X^{*})
. The proof is complete.
∎

4.
The induced systems
N_{\infty}(X,\mathcal{A})
and
M_{\infty}(X,\mathcal{A})
for polynomials

In this section, for a given finite set
\mathcal{A}
of integral polynomials and a t.d.s.
(X,T)
, we define t.d.s.

N_{\infty}(X,\mathcal{A})
and
M_{\infty}(X,\mathcal{A})
of
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
-actions. We will show that in fact they are isomorphic, which enable us to use
them freely in different situations.
Moreover, we will show that Theorem E is equivalent to the denseness of
minimal points of
M_{\infty}(X,\mathcal{A})
.

4.1.
The definition of
N_{\infty}(X,\mathcal{A})

4.1.1.
Notation

Let
d\in\mathbb{N}
and
\mathcal{A}=\{p_{1},p_{2},\cdots,p_{d}\}
be a collection of integral polynomials with
p_{i}(0)=0
,
1\leq i\leq d
.
A point of
(X^{d})^{\mathbb{Z}}
is denoted by

{\bf x}=({\bf x}_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}=\Big((x^{(1)}_{n},x^{(2)}_{n},\cdots,x^{(d)}_{n})\Big)_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}.

Let
\vec{p}=(p_{1},p_{2},\cdots,p_{d})
and let
T^{\vec{p}(n)}:X^{d}\rightarrow X^{d}
be defined by

(4.1)

T^{\vec{p}(n)}(x_{1},x_{2},\cdots,x_{d})=(T^{p_{1}(n)}x_{1},T^{p_{2}(n)}x_{2},\cdots,T^{p_{d}(n)}x_{d}).

Recall that
T^{(d)}=T\times T\times\cdots\times T
(
d
-times).
Define
T^{\infty}:(X^{d})^{{\mathbb{Z}}}\rightarrow(X^{d})^{{\mathbb{Z}}}
by

T^{\infty}({\bf x}_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}=(T^{(d)}{\bf x}_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}.

Let
\sigma:(X^{d})^{{\mathbb{Z}}}\rightarrow(X^{d})^{{\mathbb{Z}}}

be the shift map, i.e., for all
({\bf x}_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}\in(X^{d})^{{\mathbb{Z}}}

(\sigma{\bf x})_{n}={\bf x}_{n+1},\ \forall n\in{\mathbb{Z}}.

4.1.2.
Definition of
N_{\infty}(X,\mathcal{A})

Let
x^{\otimes d}=(x,x,\ldots,x)\in X^{d}
and

\Delta_{\infty}(X)=\{x^{(\infty)}\triangleq(\ldots,x^{\otimes d},x^{\otimes d},\ldots)\in(X^{d})^{{\mathbb{Z}}}:x\in X\}.

For each
x\in X
, put

(4.2)

\omega_{x}^{\mathcal{A}}\triangleq(T^{\vec{p}(n)}x^{\otimes d})_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}=\big((T^{p_{1}(n)}x,T^{p_{2}(n)}x,\ldots,T^{p_{d}(n)}x)\big)_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\in(X^{d})^{{\mathbb{Z}}},

and set

(4.3)

N_{\infty}(X,\mathcal{A})=\overline{\bigcup\{\mathcal{O}(\omega_{x}^{\mathcal{A}},\sigma):x\in X\}}\subseteq(X^{d})^{{\mathbb{Z}}}.

Remark 4.1
.

We have the following

(1)

It is clear that
N_{\infty}(X,\mathcal{A})
is invariant under the action of
T^{\infty}
and
\sigma
, and
T^{\infty}\circ\sigma=\sigma\circ T^{\infty}
. Thus

(N_{\infty}(X,\mathcal{A}),\langle T^{\infty},\sigma\rangle)
is a
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
-t.d.s.

(2)

If
(X,T)
is transitive, then for each transitive point
x
of
(X,T)
,
N_{\infty}(X,\mathcal{A})=\overline{\mathcal{O}(\omega_{x}^{\mathcal{A}},\langle T^{\infty},\sigma\rangle)}
.

(3)

Sometimes we identify points in
(X^{d_{1}+d_{2}})^{\mathbb{Z}}
as
(X^{d_{1}})^{\mathbb{Z}}\times(X^{d_{2}})^{\mathbb{Z}}
as follows:

\Big((x^{(1)}_{n},\cdots,x^{(d_{1}+d_{2})}_{n})\Big)_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}=\Big(\big((x^{(1)}_{n},\cdots,x^{(d_{1})}_{n})\big)_{n\in\mathbb{Z}},\big((x^{(d_{1}+1)}_{n},\cdots,x^{(d_{1}+d_{2})}_{n})\big)_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\Big).

(4)

Similarly, we may define systems in
(X^{d})^{\mathbb{Z}_{+}}
associated with the polynomials
\mathcal{A}
. In such a case,

\sigma
is a continuous surjective map but not a homeomorphism.

4.2.
Some examples

The action
T^{\infty}
on
N_{\infty}(X,\mathcal{A})
is clear. To make readers familiar with the definition, we provide below several examples to describe the action under
\sigma
.

4.2.1.
The case
\mathcal{A}=\{p\}
with
p(n)=n

In this case we show that for a minimal t.d.s.
(X,T)
,

(4.4)

(N_{\infty}(X,\mathcal{A}),\sigma)\cong(X,T),

where
\cong
means two systems are isomorphic.

Now
p(n)=n
, and we have for each
x\in X

\omega^{\mathcal{A}}_{x}=(\ldots,T^{-2}x,T^{-1}x,\underset{\bullet}{x},Tx,T^{2}x,\ldots)=(T^{n}x)_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}},

where
``\ \underset{\bullet}{\ }"
means the
0
-th coordinate.
Note that

\sigma\omega_{x}^{\mathcal{A}}=(\ldots,T^{-1}x,x,\underset{\bullet}{T}x,T^{2}x,T^{3}x,\ldots)=T^{\infty}\omega_{x}^{\mathcal{A}}=\omega_{Tx}^{\mathcal{A}}.

It follows that
\sigma^{n}\omega_{x}^{\mathcal{A}}=\omega^{\mathcal{A}}_{T^{n}x}
for all
n\in\mathbb{Z}
. By this fact and the minimality of
(X,T)
it is easy to verify that

N_{\infty}(X,\mathcal{A})=\{\omega_{x}^{\mathcal{A}}:x\in X\}.

Thus if we define

\phi:(X,T)\rightarrow(N_{\infty}(X,\mathcal{A}),\sigma),\ x\mapsto\omega_{x}^{\mathcal{A}},

and then it is an isomorphism between them. So

(N_{\infty}(X,\mathcal{A}),\sigma)\cong(X,T).

Also it is easy to see that
\overline{\mathcal{O}}(\omega_{x}^{\mathcal{A}},\sigma)=N_{\infty}(X,\mathcal{A})
for each
x\in X
in this case.

4.2.2.
The case for linear polynomials

Let
\mathcal{A}=\{p_{1},p_{2},\ldots,p_{d}\}
, where
p_{i}(n)=a_{i}n,\ 1\leq i\leq d
and
a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{d}
are distinct non-zero integers.
In this case we show that for a minimal t.d.s.
(X,T)
,

(4.5)

(N_{\infty}(X,\mathcal{A}),\sigma)\cong({N}_{\mathcal{A}}(X,T),\tau_{\vec{a}}),

where
{N}_{\mathcal{A}}(X,T)=\overline{\mathcal{O}}(\Delta_{d}(X),\tau_{\vec{a}})
is as defined in (
3.4
), and
\tau_{\vec{a}}=T^{a_{1}}\times T^{a_{2}}\times\cdots\times T^{a_{d}}

with
\vec{a}=(a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{d})
.

As
\mathcal{A}=\{p_{1},p_{2},\ldots,p_{d}\}
, we have that for each
x\in X

\omega_{x}^{\mathcal{A}}=\Big((T^{a_{1}n}x,T^{a_{2}n}x,\ldots,T^{a_{d}n}x)\Big)_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}=(\ldots,\tau^{-1}_{\vec{a}}x^{\otimes d},\underset{\bullet}{x}^{\otimes},\tau_{\vec{a}}x^{\otimes d},\tau_{\vec{a}}^{2}x^{\otimes d},\ldots)\in(X^{d})^{\mathbb{Z}}.

Note that

\begin{split}\sigma\omega^{\mathcal{A}}_{x}&=\Big((T^{a_{1}(n+1)}x,T^{a_{2}(n+1)}x,\ldots,T^{a_{d}(n+1)}x)\Big)_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}\\
&=\Big(\tau_{\vec{a}}(T^{a_{1}n}x,T^{a_{2}n}x,\ldots,T^{a_{d}n}x)\Big)_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}=\tau_{\vec{a}}^{\infty}\omega_{x}^{\mathcal{A}},\end{split}

where
\tau_{\vec{a}}^{\infty}=\cdots\times\tau_{\vec{a}}\times\tau_{\vec{a}}\times\cdots,

and for all
n\in{\mathbb{Z}}

\begin{split}\sigma^{n}\omega_{x}^{\mathcal{A}}&=({\tau_{\vec{a}}^{\infty}})^{n}\omega_{x}^{\mathcal{A}}\\
&=\big(\ldots,\tau^{-1}_{\vec{a}}\tau_{\vec{a}}^{n}x^{\otimes d},\underset{\bullet}{\tau_{\vec{a}}^{n}x^{\otimes d}},\tau_{\vec{a}}\tau_{\vec{a}}^{n}x^{\otimes d},\tau_{\vec{a}}^{2}\tau_{\vec{a}}^{n}x^{\otimes d},\ldots\big)\\
&=\widetilde{\tau_{\vec{a}}}\big(\ldots,\tau_{\vec{a}}^{n}x^{\otimes d},\underset{\bullet}{\tau_{\vec{a}}^{n}x^{\otimes d}},\tau_{\vec{a}}^{n}x^{\otimes d},\ldots\big),\end{split}

where
\widetilde{\tau_{\vec{a}}}=\cdots\times\tau^{-1}_{\vec{a}}\times\underset{\bullet}{{\rm id}_{X^{d}}}\times\tau_{\vec{a}}\times\tau^{2}_{\vec{a}}\times\cdots
.
Thus we have that

N_{\infty}(X,\mathcal{A})=\{(\ldots,\tau^{-1}_{\vec{a}}{\bf y},\underset{\bullet}{\bf y},\tau_{\vec{a}}{\bf y},\tau_{\vec{a}}^{2}{\bf y},\ldots):{\bf y}\in{N}_{d}(X,T)\}.

Now we define

\phi:({N}_{\mathcal{A}}(X,T),\tau_{\vec{a}})\rightarrow(N_{\infty}(X,\mathcal{A}),\sigma),\ {\bf y}\mapsto(\ldots,\tau^{-1}_{\vec{a}}{\bf y},\underset{\bullet}{\bf y},\tau_{\vec{a}}{\bf y},\tau_{\vec{a}}^{2}{\bf y},\ldots),

and it is an isomorphism. Thus

(N_{\infty}(X,\mathcal{A}),\sigma)\cong({N}_{\mathcal{A}}(X,T),\tau_{\vec{a}}),

and

(N_{\infty}(X,\mathcal{A}),\langle T^{\infty},\sigma\rangle)\cong({N}_{\mathcal{A}}(X,T),\langle T^{(d)},\tau_{\vec{a}}\rangle).

Similarly, we have that
\big(\overline{\mathcal{O}}(\omega_{x}^{\mathcal{A}},\sigma),\sigma\big)\cong\big(\overline{\mathcal{O}}(x^{\otimes d},\tau_{\vec{a}}),\tau_{\vec{a}}\big)

for each
x\in X
.

4.2.3.
Weakly mixing systems

Let
(X,T)
be a weakly mixing minimal t.d.s. In this case
N_{\infty}(X,\mathcal{A})
will be very big in
(X^{d})^{\mathbb{Z}}
. The following ones are special cases proved
in Section 5 (see Theorem
5.10
).

Case
(I):
\mathcal{A}=\{p\}
with
p(n)=n^{2}

In this case for each
x\in X
,

\omega_{x}^{\mathcal{A}}=(\ldots,T^{p(-1)}x,\underset{\bullet}{x},T^{p(1)}x,T^{p(2)}x,\ldots)=(T^{p(n)}x)_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}.

We have that

(4.6)

(N_{\infty}(X,\mathcal{A}),\sigma)\cong(X^{\mathbb{Z}},\sigma),

and there is a residual subset
X_{0}
of
X
such that for all
x\in X_{0}
,
\overline{\mathcal{O}}(\omega_{x}^{\mathcal{A}},\sigma)=X^{\mathbb{Z}}
.

Case
(II):
\mathcal{A}=\{p_{1},p_{2}\}
with
p_{1}(n)=n
and
p_{2}(n)=n^{2}
.

In this case for each
x\in X
,
\omega_{x}^{\mathcal{A}}
is the point

\Big(\ldots,(T^{p_{1}(-1)}x,T^{p_{2}(-1)}x),\underset{\bullet}{(x,x)},(T^{p_{1}(1)}x,T^{p_{2}(1)}x),\ldots\Big)=\Big((T^{p_{1}(n)}x,T^{p_{2}(n)}x)\Big)_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}.

We have that

(4.7)

(N_{\infty}(X,\mathcal{A}),\sigma)\cong(X\times X^{\mathbb{Z}},T\times\sigma^{\prime}),

where
\sigma^{\prime}:X^{\mathbb{Z}}\rightarrow X^{\mathbb{Z}}
is the left shift, and there is a residual subset
X_{0}
of
X
such that for all
x\in X_{0}
,
\overline{\mathcal{O}}(\omega_{x}^{\mathcal{A}},\sigma)\cong X\times X^{\mathbb{Z}}
.

4.3.
The definition of
M_{\infty}(X,\mathcal{A})

In the previous subsection we see that when
\mathcal{A}=\{p_{1},p_{2},\ldots,p_{d}\}
with
p_{i}(n)=a_{i}n,1\leq i\leq d
and
a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{d}

are distinct non-zero integers,
(N_{\infty}(X,\mathcal{A}),\sigma)\cong({N}_{\mathcal{A}}(X,T),\tau_{\vec{a}})
. In this subsection, we define

M_{\infty}(X,\mathcal{A})
, and particularly we pay attention to the case when
\mathcal{A}
contains at least one linear element,
and give an equivalent way to look at
(N_{\infty}(X,\mathcal{A}),\sigma)
.

4.3.1.
Definition of
M_{\infty}(X,\mathcal{A})

Assume that
\mathcal{A}=\{p_{1},\ldots,p_{s},p_{s+1},\ldots,p_{d}\}
, where
p_{i}(n)=a_{i}n,1\leq i\leq s
with

s\geq 0
,
a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{s}\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}
are distinct, and
\deg p_{i}\geq 2,s+1\leq i\leq d
.
Note that
s=0
means that
\mathcal{A}=\{p_{1},\ldots,p_{d}\}
with
\deg p_{i}\geq 2
,
1\leq i\leq d
.

We have that

\omega_{x}^{\mathcal{A}}=\Big((T^{a_{1}n}x,\ldots,T^{a_{s}n}x,T^{p_{s+1}(n)}x,\ldots,T^{p_{d}(n)}x)\Big)_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}\in(X^{d})^{\mathbb{Z}}.

Define
\widetilde{\sigma}:X^{s}\times(X^{d-s})^{\mathbb{Z}}\rightarrow X^{s}\times(X^{d-s})^{\mathbb{Z}}
as follow:
for
((x_{1},\ldots,x_{s}),{\bf x})\in X^{s}\times(X^{d-s})^{\mathbb{Z}}
, let

\widetilde{\sigma}\Big(\big((x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{s}),{\bf x}\big)\Big)=\Big(\big((T^{a_{1}}x_{1},T^{a_{2}}x_{2},\ldots,T^{a_{s}}x_{s}),\sigma^{\prime}{\bf x}\big)\Big),

where
\sigma^{\prime}
is the left shift on
(X^{d-s})^{\mathbb{Z}}
. Recall that
\tau_{\vec{a}}=T^{a_{1}}\times\ldots\times T^{a_{s}}
. So,
\widetilde{\sigma}=\tau_{\vec{a}}\times\sigma^{\prime}
.
Let

(4.8)

\xi_{x}^{\mathcal{A}}=\Big((x,\ldots,x),(T^{p_{s+1}(j)}x,\ldots,T^{p_{d}(j)}x)_{j\in{\mathbb{Z}}}\Big)\in X^{s}\times(X^{d-s})^{\mathbb{Z}}.

Then for
n\in{\mathbb{Z}}
,

(4.9)

\widetilde{\sigma}^{n}\xi_{x}^{\mathcal{A}}=\Big((T^{a_{1}n}x,\ldots,T^{a_{s}n}x),(T^{p_{s+1}(n+j)}x,\ldots,T^{p_{d}(n+j)}x)_{j\in{\mathbb{Z}}}\Big)\in X^{s}\times(X^{d-s})^{\mathbb{Z}}.

We set

(4.10)

M_{\infty}(X,\mathcal{A})=\overline{\bigcup\{\mathcal{O}(\xi_{x}^{\mathcal{A}},\widetilde{\sigma}):x\in X\}}\subseteq X^{s}\times(X^{d-s})^{\mathbb{Z}}.

It is clear that
M_{\infty}(X,\mathcal{A})
is invariant under the action of
T^{\infty}
and
\widetilde{\sigma}
, and
(M_{\infty}(X,\mathcal{A}),\langle T^{\infty},\widetilde{\sigma}\rangle)
is a t.d.s.

4.3.2.

Now we show that

Lemma 4.2
.

For any t.d.s.
(X,T)
we have

(M_{\infty}(X,\mathcal{A}),\langle T^{\infty},\widetilde{\sigma}\rangle)\cong(N_{\infty}(X,\mathcal{A}),\langle T^{\infty},\sigma\rangle),

and for each
x\in X

\big(\overline{\mathcal{O}}(\omega_{x}^{\mathcal{A}},\sigma),\sigma\big)\cong\big(\overline{\mathcal{O}}(\xi_{x}^{\mathcal{A}},\widetilde{\sigma}),\widetilde{\sigma}\big).

Proof.

Note that for all
n\in{\mathbb{Z}}
,

(4.11)

\begin{split}\sigma^{n}\omega_{x}^{\mathcal{A}}&=\Big((T^{a_{1}(n+j)}x,\ldots,T^{a_{s}(n+j)}x,T^{p_{s+1}(n+j)}x,\ldots,T^{p_{d}(n+j)}x)\Big)_{j\in{\mathbb{Z}}}\\
&=\Big(\big(\tau_{\vec{a}}^{n+j}(x^{\otimes s})\big)_{j\in{\mathbb{Z}}},(\sigma^{\prime})^{n}\big(T^{p_{s+1}(j)}x,\ldots,T^{p_{d}(j)}x\big)_{j\in{\mathbb{Z}}}\Big)\\
&=\Big(\widetilde{\tau_{\vec{a}}}\big(\ldots,\tau_{\vec{a}}^{n}x^{\otimes s},\underset{\bullet}{\tau_{\vec{a}}^{n}x^{\otimes s}},\tau_{\vec{a}}^{n}x^{\otimes s},\ldots\big),(\sigma^{\prime})^{n}\big(T^{p_{s+1}(j)}x,\ldots,T^{p_{d}(j)}x\big)_{j\in{\mathbb{Z}}}\Big),\end{split}

where
\widetilde{\tau_{\vec{a}}}=\ldots\tau^{-1}_{\vec{a}}\times\underset{\bullet}{{\rm id}_{X^{s}}}\times\tau_{\vec{a}}\times\tau^{2}_{\vec{a}}\times\ldots
. By (
4.9
) and (
4.11
), we see that for sequences
\{x_{i}\}_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}
of
X
and
\{n_{i}\}_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}
of
\mathbb{Z}
,

\widetilde{\sigma}^{n_{i}}\xi_{x_{i}}^{\mathcal{A}}\to({\bf y},{\bf x})\in M_{\infty}(X,\mathcal{A})

if and only if

\sigma^{n_{i}}\omega_{x_{i}}^{\mathcal{A}}\to\Big((\ldots,\tau^{-1}_{\vec{a}}{\bf y},\underset{\bullet}{\bf y},\tau_{\vec{a}}{\bf y},\tau_{\vec{a}}^{2}{\bf y},\ldots),{\bf x}\Big)\in N_{\infty}(X,\mathcal{A}).

Now we define

\phi:(M_{\infty}(X,T),\widetilde{\sigma})\rightarrow(N_{\infty}(X,\mathcal{A}),\sigma),\ ({\bf y},{\bf x})\mapsto\Big((\ldots,\tau^{-1}_{\vec{a}}{\bf y},\underset{\bullet}{\bf y},\tau_{\vec{a}}{\bf y},\tau_{\vec{a}}^{2}{\bf y},\ldots),{\bf x}\Big),

and it is an isomorphism. Thus

(M_{\infty}(X,T),\widetilde{\sigma})\cong(N_{\infty}(X,\mathcal{A}),\sigma),

and

(M_{\infty}(X,\mathcal{A}),\langle T^{\infty},\widetilde{\sigma}\rangle)\cong(N_{\infty}(X,\mathcal{A}),\langle T^{\infty},\sigma\rangle).

Similarly, it is easy to verify that for all
x\in X
,

\big(\overline{\mathcal{O}}(\omega_{x}^{\mathcal{A}},\sigma),\sigma\big)\cong\big(\overline{\mathcal{O}}(\xi_{x}^{\mathcal{A}},\widetilde{\sigma}),\widetilde{\sigma}\big).

∎

4.3.3.
Compare
N_{\infty}(X,\mathcal{A})
with
M_{\infty}(X,\mathcal{A})

We have show that
(N_{\infty}(X,\mathcal{A}),\langle T^{\infty},\sigma\rangle)
is isomorphic to
(M_{\infty}(X,\mathcal{A}),\langle T^{\infty},\widetilde{\sigma}\rangle).

Now we compare them. The definition of
N_{\infty}(X,\mathcal{A})
is cleaner than
M_{\infty}(X,\mathcal{A})
, as we do not care whether there are linear terms in
\mathcal{A}
or not. But when we study the dynamical properties and
\mathcal{A}
contains linear terms,
M_{\infty}(X,\mathcal{A})
is easy to handle than
N_{\infty}(X,\mathcal{A})
, which we will see in the sequel.

4.3.4.

In the sequel when there is no room for confusion,
we will use
\sigma
instead of
\widetilde{\sigma}

when studying
(M_{\infty}(X,\mathcal{A}),\langle T^{\infty},\widetilde{\sigma}\rangle)
.

4.3.5.

Let
(X,T)
be a transitive t.d.s. and
{\rm Trans}_{T}
be the set of transitive points, which is a residual subset of
X
. By definition, for each
x\in{\rm Trans}_{T}
we have that
M_{\infty}(X,\mathcal{A})=\overline{\mathcal{O}(\xi_{x}^{\mathcal{A}},\langle T^{\infty},{\sigma}\rangle)}\subseteq X^{s}\times(X^{d-s})^{\mathbb{Z}}.
Thus

(M_{\infty}(X,\mathcal{A}),\langle T^{\infty},\sigma\rangle)
is a transitive t.d.s. and for each
x\in{\rm Trans}_{T}
,
\xi_{x}^{\mathcal{A}}
is a transitive point.
In particular, if
(X,T)
is minimal, then
(M_{\infty}(X,\mathcal{A}),\langle T^{\infty},\sigma\rangle)
is a transitive t.d.s. and for each
x\in X
,
\xi_{x}^{\mathcal{A}}
is a transitive point.

4.3.6.
A remark

First we recall the following polynomial multiple recurrence theorem by Bergelson and Leibman.

Theorem 4.3
.

[
8
, Corollary 1.8]

Let
X
be a compact metric space, and let
\Gamma
be an abelian group of its homeomorphisms such that
(X,\Gamma)
is minimal. Let
T_{1},\ldots,T_{d}\in\Gamma
,
k\in\mathbb{N}
and let
p_{i,j}
be integral polynomial with
p_{i,j}(0)=0
for all
1\leq i\leq k
and
1\leq j\leq d
. Then there exists
a residual set
X_{0}\subseteq X
satisfying that for each
x\in X_{0}
there exists an increasing sequence

\{n_{m}\}_{m=1}^{\infty}
of
\mathbb{N}
such that for each
i=1,\ldots,k
,

T_{1}^{p_{i,1}(n_{m})}\cdots T_{d}^{p_{i,d}(n_{m})}x\rightarrow x,\ m\to\infty.

By the above result it is easy to obtain

Remark 4.4
.

Let
(X,T)
be a minimal t.d.s. There is a residual subset
\widehat{X}_{pol}
of
X
such that for each
x\in\widehat{X}_{pol}
and any finite set
\mathcal{A}=\{p_{1},\ldots,p_{d}\}
of integral polynomials vanishing at zero, there exists an increasing sequence
\{n_{m}\}_{m=1}^{\infty}
of
\mathbb{N}
satisfying

\sigma^{n_{m}}\xi_{x}^{\mathcal{A}}\to\xi_{x}^{\mathcal{A}},\ m\to\infty.

4.4.
Condition
(\spadesuit)
and saturated properties of
M_{\infty}(X,\mathcal{A})

4.4.1.

Note that for a given integral polynomials
p
with
p(0)=0
, for each
j\in\mathbb{Z}
the polynomial
p^{[j]}
defined by

p^{[j]}(n)=p(n+j)-p(j),\ \forall n\in\mathbb{Z}

appears naturally in the definition of
M_{\infty}(X,\mathcal{A})
and
N_{\infty}(X,\mathcal{A})
. This causes problems when applying the saturation theorems (it requires the polynomials are distinct) to polynomials
\{p_{1},\ldots,p_{d}\}
, it may happen that

p_{i}^{[k_{1}]}=p_{j}^{[k_{2}]}

for some
1\leq i\not=j\leq d
and
k_{1},k_{2}\in\mathbb{Z}
. For example when
p_{1}(n)=n^{2}
and
p_{2}(n)=n^{2}+2n
, we have
p_{1}^{[1]}=p_{2}^{[0]}
. To overcome this difficulty we introduce the following condition
(\spadesuit)
.

4.4.2.
Condition
(\spadesuit)

Definition 4.5
.

Let
\mathcal{A}=\{p_{1},p_{2},\ldots,p_{d}\}
be a family of integral polynomials. We say
\mathcal{A}
satisfies
condition
(\spadesuit)
if
p_{1}(0)=\ldots=p_{d}(0)=0
and

(1)

p_{1}(n)=a_{1}n,p_{2}(n)=a_{2}n,\ldots,p_{s}(n)=a_{s}n
, where
s\geq 0
, and
a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{s}
are distinct non-zero integers;

(2)

\deg p_{j}\geq 2,s+1\leq j\leq d
;

(3)

for each
i\neq j\in\{s+1,s+2,\ldots,d\}
,
p_{j}^{[k]}\neq p_{i}^{[t]}
for any
k,t\in\mathbb{Z}
.

We have the following simple observation which indicates that the condition
(\spadesuit)
does not affect our discussion.

Lemma 4.6
.

Let
k\in\mathbb{N}
and
Q=\{q_{1},q_{2},\ldots,q_{k}\}
be integral polynomials with
q_{i}(0)=0,1\leq i\leq k
. Then there is
1\leq d\leq k

and
\mathcal{A}=\{p_{1},\ldots,p_{d}\}\subset\{q_{1},\ldots,q_{k}\}
such that
\mathcal{A}
satisfies condition
(\spadesuit)
, and

5.2.2.
Distal extensions and group extensions

Let

One says that

topological group
K
such that the following

(1)

acts continuously on
X
from the right: the

(x,k)\mapsto xk
is

(2)

the fibers of
\pi
are the
K

\pi^{-1}(\{\pi(x)\})=xK
for any

When

is a

Note that a group extension is equicontinuous, and an equicontinous extension is distal.

Let

mapping every fiber of
\pi
into itself.

Let
\pi:X\rightarrow Y
be a weak group extension as above. Then for each

,

defines an element of

Lemma 6.1

7.1.3.
RIC extensions

Let
\pi:(X,T)\rightarrow(Y,T)
be a factor map of minimal t.d.s., and
x_{0}\in X
,
y_{0}=\pi(x_{0})
. Let

. We say that

,
p\in{\bf M}
,

(7.1)

\pi^{-1}(y)=p\circ\big(u\pi^{-1}(y_{0})\big).

Note that every distal extension is RIC, and every RIC extension is open.

Every factor map between minimal systems can be
lifted to a RIC extension by proximal extensions (see
[
10
, Theorem 5.13]
or
[
37
, Chapter VI]
).

Theorem 7.1
.

Given a factor map
\pi:(X,T)\rightarrow(Y,T)
of minimal systems, there exists a commutative diagram of factor maps (called RIC-diagram)

\begin{CD}X@<{\theta^{\prime}}<{}<X^{\prime}\\
@V{}V{\pi}V@V{}V{\pi^{\prime}}V\\
Y@<{\theta}<{}<Y^{\prime}\end{CD}

such that:

and

(b)

\pi^{\prime}
is a RIC extension;

is the unique minimal set in

and

are the restrictions to

of the projections of

onto

respectively.

Let

Then
{\bf y}=\pi^{\infty}({\bf x})\in N_{\infty}(Y,\mathcal{A})

(N_{\infty}(Y,\mathcal{A}),\mathcal{G})
is minimal, and there is some

and a net

Without loss of generality, assume that

7.3.

Now we are ready to prove an equivalence form of Theorem E. In the rest of the paper we put

is an

7.3.1.
Idea of the proof

Since the proof is long, we describe the idea of the proof first.
Let
(X,T)
be minimal and
\pi:X\longrightarrow X_{\infty}
be the factor map. By the discussion in Section

,

(M_{\infty}({X_{\infty}},\mathcal{A}),\mathcal{G})
is minimal. Using the RIC-diagram (modifications by proximal extensions), we obtain

which is RIC. By Lemma
7.7
,
(M_{\infty}({X_{\infty}^{*}},\mathcal{A}),\mathcal{G})
is also minimal.

The natural idea to show the density of minimal point in

is that

of
\mathcal{G}
and a non-empty open set

for the distal case. When we apply

{\rm Aut}(X,T)
to realize the idea. Thus, we need to pass our discussion to the universal minimal system to get suitable elements in
{\rm Aut}(X,T)
for our purpose.

The proof is done in four steps: firstly we construct the extension (
Step 1
below), secondly we transfer the question into
some extension of
(M_{\infty}(X^{*},\mathcal{A}),\mathcal{G})
(
Step 2
below);

In this section, we give some combinatoric consequences. Theorem A is a corollary of the following theorem.

Theorem 8.1
.

Let

and
d\in\mathbb{N}
, and let

be an integral polynomial with

,

Proof.

Let

Let
{\bf x}=1_{S}\in\Sigma_{2}
be the indicator function, i.e.,

if and only if

.
Let

is piecewise syndetic,

with

actually syndetic. Since
