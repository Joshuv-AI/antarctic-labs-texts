On the sum of the values of a polynomial at natural numbers which form a decreasing arithmetic progression
Bakir FARHI

Abstract

The results and the proofs

Our main result is the following:

Theorem 2.1
.

Let
d
be a positive integer. Then, a polynomial
P\in{\mathbb{R}}[X]
belongs to
\mathscr{E}_{d}
if and only if it has the form:

P(X)=\binom{X+d}{d}f(X+d)-\binom{X}{d}f(X),

(2.1)

where
f\in{\mathbb{R}}[X]
. Besides, if (
2.1
) holds then we have for any natural number
n
:

P(n-d)+P(n-2d)+P(n-3d)+\dots=\binom{n}{d}f(n).

Proof.

Let
P\in{\mathbb{R}}[X]
.

• Suppose that
P\in\mathscr{E}_{d}
; that is there exists
S\in{\mathbb{R}}[X]
for which we have for any natural number
n
:

P(n)+P(n-d)+P(n-2d)+\dots=S(n).

(2.2)

Using (
2.2
), we have in particular:

S(0)=P(0)~,~S(1)=P(1)~,~\dots~,~S(d-1)=P(d-1).

This shows that the real polynomial
(S(X)-P(X))
vanishes at
0,1,\dots,d-1
. Consequently, there exists
f_{0}\in{\mathbb{R}}[X]
such that:

S(X)-P(X)=X(X-1)\cdots(X-d+1)f_{0}(X)=\binom{X}{d}f(X),

where
f:=d!f_{0}\in{\mathbb{R}}[X]
. By specializing the obtained polynomial equality
S(X)-P(X)=\binom{X}{d}f(X)
to
X=n\in{\mathbb{N}}
, we get (according to (
2.2
)):

P(n-d)+P(n-2d)+P(n-3d)+\dots=\binom{n}{d}f(n)~~~~~~~~~~(\forall n\in{\mathbb{N}}).

(2.3)

Next, by applying (
2.3
) for
(n+d)
(instead of
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
), we get

P(n)+P(n-d)+P(n-2d)+\dots=\binom{n+d}{d}f(n+d)~~~~~~~~~~(\forall n\in{\mathbb{N}}).

(2.4)

Then, by subtracting (
2.3
) from (
2.4
), we obtain that:

P(n)=\binom{n+d}{d}f(n+d)-\binom{n}{d}f(n)~~~~~~~~~~(\forall n\in{\mathbb{N}}).

Finally, since the two sides of the last formula are both polynomials in
n
then its validity for any
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
implies its validity as a polynomial identity; that is

P(X)=\binom{X+d}{d}f(X+d)-\binom{X}{d}f(X),

as required.

• Conversely, suppose that
P
has the form (
2.1
). Then, for any
n,q\in{\mathbb{N}}
, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{q}P(n-kd)

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\sum_{k=1}^{q}\left\{\binom{n-(k-1)d}{d}f(n-(k-1)d)-\binom{n-kd}{d}f(n-kd)\right\}}_{\text{telescopic sum}}

\displaystyle=\binom{n}{d}f(n)-\binom{n-qd}{d}f(n-qd).

By taking in particular
q=\lfloor\frac{n}{d}\rfloor
and putting
r:=n-qd
(so
r
is the remainder of the euclidean division of
n
by
d
, and consequently it is the smallest natural number among the integers
n,n-d,n-2d,n-3d,\dots
), we find that:

P(n-d)+P(n-2d)+P(n-3d)+\dots=\binom{n}{d}f(n)-\binom{r}{d}f(r).

But since
r\in\{0,1,\dots,d-1\}
, we have
\binom{r}{d}=0
; hence

P(n-d)+P(n-2d)+P(n-3d)+\dots=\binom{n}{d}f(n),

showing that the sum
P(n-d)+P(n-2d)+P(n-3d)+\dots
is polynomial in
n
; thus
P\in\mathscr{E}_{d}
, as required. This completes the proof of the theorem.
∎

From Theorem
2.1
, we derive the following corollary which determines an explicit basis for the
{\mathbb{R}}
-vector space
\mathscr{E}_{d}
(
d\in{\mathbb{N}}^{*}
).

Corollary 2.2
.

Let
d
be a positive integer. Then the family of real polynomials

\mathscr{B}:={\left(\binom{X+d}{d}(X+d)^{k}-\binom{X}{d}X^{k}\right)}_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}

constitutes a basis for the
{\mathbb{R}}
-vector space
\mathscr{E}_{d}
.

Proof.

• First, let us show that
\mathscr{B}
generates
\mathscr{E}_{d}
. For any given
P\in\mathscr{E}_{d}
, we can write (according to Theorem
2.1
):

P(X)=\binom{X+d}{d}f(X+d)-\binom{X}{d}f(X),

for some
f\in{\mathbb{R}}[X]
. Then, by expressing
f
in the canonical basis of
{\mathbb{R}}[X]
; that is in the form:

f(X)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}a_{k}X^{k}

(with
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
and
a_{0},a_{1},\dots,a_{n}\in{\mathbb{R}}
), we obtain that:

\displaystyle P(X)

\displaystyle=\binom{X+d}{d}\sum_{k=0}^{n}a_{k}(X+d)^{k}-\binom{X}{d}\sum_{k=0}^{n}a_{k}X^{k}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{n}a_{k}\left[\binom{X+d}{d}(X+d)^{k}-\binom{X}{d}X^{k}\right],

which is an expression of
P
as a linear combination (with real coefficients) of elements of
\mathscr{B}
. Therefore,
\mathscr{B}
generates
\mathscr{E}_{d}
.

• Now, let us show that
\mathscr{B}
is a free family in the
{\mathbb{R}}
-vector space
{\mathbb{R}}[X]
. Let
K
be a finite nonempty subset of
{\mathbb{N}}
and
{(\lambda_{k})}_{k\in K}
be a family of real numbers such that:

\sum_{k\in K}\lambda_{k}\left(\binom{X+d}{d}(X+d)^{k}-\binom{X}{d}X^{k}\right)=0.

(2.5)

So, we have to show that
\lambda_{k}=0
for every
k\in K
. By putting
Q(X):=\binom{X}{d}\sum_{k\in K}\lambda_{k}X^{k}\in{\mathbb{R}}[X]
, we have

\eqref{eq*}\Longleftrightarrow Q(X+d)=Q(X)\Longleftrightarrow Q\text{ is $d$-periodic}.

But if
Q
is not zero, we have that
\deg Q\geq\deg\binom{X}{d}=d\geq 1
; so
Q
cannot be
d
-periodic (because a real continuous function on
{\mathbb{R}}
which is
d
-periodic is inevitably bounded, while a real polynomial with degree
\geq 1
is never bounded). Thus
Q
is zero, implying that
\lambda_{k}=0
for every
k\in K
. Consequently,
\mathscr{B}
is free.

In conclusion,
\mathscr{B}
is a basis of the
{\mathbb{R}}
-vector space
\mathscr{E}_{d}
. The corollary is proved.
∎

P
is necessarily the zero polynomial (the second inclusion
\{0_{{\mathbb{R}}[X]}\}\subset\mathscr{E}_{d}\cap{\mathbb{R}}_{d-2}[X]
is trivial). From
P\in\mathscr{E}_{d}
, we deduce (by Theorem
2.1
) that there is
f\in{\mathbb{R}}[X]
such that:

P(X)=\binom{X+d}{d}f(X+d)-\binom{X}{d}f(X).

If we suppose
P\neq 0_{{\mathbb{R}}[X]}
, we would have
f\neq 0_{{\mathbb{R}}[X]}
and

\deg P=\deg\left(\binom{X}{d}f(X)\right)-1=d+\deg f-1\geq d-1,

which contradicts the fact that
P\in{\mathbb{R}}_{d-2}[X]
. Hence
P=0_{{\mathbb{R}}[X]}
, as required. This confirms that
\mathscr{E}_{d}\cap{\mathbb{R}}_{d-2}[X]=\{0_{{\mathbb{R}}[X]}\}
.

• Now, let us show that
\mathscr{E}_{d}+{\mathbb{R}}_{d-2}[X]={\mathbb{R}}[X]
. So, let
P\in{\mathbb{R}}[X]
and let us show the existence of two polynomials
Q\in\mathscr{E}_{d}
and
R\in{\mathbb{R}}_{d-2}[X]
such that
P=Q+R
(the other inclusion
\mathscr{E}_{d}+{\mathbb{R}}_{d-2}[X]\subset{\mathbb{R}}[X]
is trivial). To do so, let us consider
P^{*}\in{\mathbb{R}}[X]
such that:

P^{*}(X+1)-P^{*}(X)=P(dX)

(such
P^{*}
exists because
P(dX)\in{\mathbb{R}}[X]
). Let us then consider the euclidean division (in
{\mathbb{R}}[X]
) of
P^{*}
by the polynomial
\binom{dX}{d}
(which is of degree
d
):

P^{*}(X)=\binom{dX}{d}q(X)+r(X),

(2.6)

where
q,r\in{\mathbb{R}}[X]
and
\deg r\leq d-1
. Using (
2.6
), we have that

P^{*}(X+1)-P^{*}(X)=\binom{dX+d}{d}q(X+1)-\binom{dX}{d}q(X)+r(X+1)-r(X);

that is

P(dX)=\binom{dX+d}{d}q(X+1)-\binom{dX}{d}q(X)+r(X+1)-r(X).

By substituting in this last equality
X
by
\frac{X}{d}
, we get

P(X)=\binom{X+d}{d}q\left(\frac{X+d}{d}\right)-\binom{X}{d}q\left(\frac{X}{d}\right)+r\left(\frac{X}{d}+1\right)-r\left(\frac{X}{d}\right).

So, it suffices to take

\displaystyle Q(X)

\displaystyle:=\binom{X+d}{d}q\left(\frac{X+d}{d}\right)-\binom{X}{d}q\left(\frac{X}{d}\right)

and

\displaystyle R(X)

\displaystyle:=r\left(\frac{X}{d}+1\right)-r\left(\frac{X}{d}\right)

to have
P=Q+R
, with
Q\in\mathscr{E}_{d}
(according to Theorem
2.1
) and
R\in{\mathbb{R}}_{d-2}[X]
(since
\deg R=\deg r-1\leq d-2
). Consequently, we have
{\mathbb{R}}[X]=\mathscr{E}_{d}+{\mathbb{R}}_{d-2}[X]
, as required. This completes the proof of the corollary.
∎

From Corollary
2.3
, we derive the following important corollary relating to the particular case
d=2
.

Corollary 2.4
.

There is a unique real sequence
{(c_{n})}_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
such that:

\left(X^{n}-c_{n}\right)\in\mathscr{E}_{2}~~~~~~~~~~(\forall n\in{\mathbb{N}}).

Besides, we have
c_{2n}=0
for any positive integer
n
.

Proof.

By Corollary
2.3
, we have

{\mathbb{R}}[X]=\mathscr{E}_{2}\oplus{\mathbb{R}}_{0}[X].

Since
{\mathbb{R}}_{0}[X]
is constituted by constant polynomials, this shows in particular that for any
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
, the monomial
X^{n}
can be written in a unique way as
X^{n}=P_{n}(X)+c_{n}
, with
P_{n}\in\mathscr{E}_{2}
and
c_{n}\in{\mathbb{R}}
. In other words, for any
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
, there is a unique
c_{n}\in{\mathbb{R}}
such that
(X^{n}-c_{n})\in\mathscr{E}_{2}
. This confirms the first part of the corollary. Next, for any
n\in{\mathbb{N}}^{*}
, the immediate formula:

N^{2n}+(N-2)^{2n}+(N-4)^{2n}+\dots=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=0}^{N}(N-2k)^{2n}~~~~~~~~~~(\forall N\in{\mathbb{N}})

insures that
X^{2n}\in\mathscr{E}_{2}
; hence
c_{2n}=0
. This confirms the second part of the corollary and achieves the proof.
∎

For the sequel, we let
{(c_{n})}_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}

2.4

Theorem 2.5
.

For every natural number

Proof.

Let

be a fixed natural number. We exactly follow the second part of the proof of Corollary
2.3
(i.e., the proof of the fact that
{\mathbb{R}}[X]=\mathscr{E}_{d}+{\mathbb{R}}_{d-2}[X]
) by taking

and

. We thus need a polynomial

such that:

P^{*}(X+1)-P^{*}(X)=P(2X)=(2X)^{n}=2^{n}X^{n}.

According to Formula (
1.1
), we can take

P^{*}(X)=\frac{2^{n}}{n+1}B_{n+1}(X).

Then, consider the euclidean division (in
{\mathbb{R}}[X]
) of this chosen polynomial
P^{*}(X)
by the polynomial
\binom{2X}{2}=X(2X-1)
:

P^{*}(X)=X(2X-1)q(X)+r(X),

where

and

. Expressing

as

(

\frac{2^{n}}{n+1}B_{n+1}(X)=X(2X-1)q(X)+aX+b.

(2.7)

According to the second part of the proof of Corollary
2.3

c_{n}=R(X):=r\left(\frac{X}{2}+1\right)-r\left(\frac{X}{2}\right)=a\left(\frac{X}{2}+1\right)+b-\left(a\frac{X}{2}+b\right)=a.

So, we have to show that

. By taking in (
2.7
) successively

and

, we obtain that:

\displaystyle b

\displaystyle=\frac{2^{n}}{n+1}B_{n+1}(0)=\frac{2^{n}}{n+1}B_{n+1},

and

\displaystyle\frac{a}{2}+b

\displaystyle=\frac{2^{n}}{n+1}B_{n+1}\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{2^{n}}{n+1}\left(\frac{1}{2^{n}}-1\right)B_{n+1}~~~~~~~~~~(\text{according to \eqref{eqJJ}})

\displaystyle=\frac{1-2^{n}}{n+1}B_{n+1}.

Thus

\displaystyle a

\displaystyle=2\left[\frac{1-2^{n}}{n+1}B_{n+1}-\frac{2^{n}}{n+1}B_{n+1}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{2\left(1-2^{n+1}\right)}{n+1}B_{n+1}

\displaystyle=E_{n}(0)=\frac{G_{n+1}}{n+1}~~~~~~~~~~(\text{according to \eqref{eqJJJ}}),

as required. This completes the proof.
∎

Now, we will derive from our main theorem

that the Ibn al-Banna method (exposed in §

P
in the form
P(X)=\alpha(X)+\alpha(X-1)+\dots+\alpha(X-d+1)
, where
\alpha\in{\mathbb{R}}[X]
and
\alpha(-1)=\alpha(-2)=\dots=\alpha(-d+1)=0
.

Theorem 2.7

(Generalizability of the Ibn al-Banna method)
.

Let
d\geq 2
be an integer. Then a real polynomial
P
belongs to
\mathscr{E}_{d}
if and only if there exists
\alpha\in{\mathbb{R}}[X]
, satisfying
\alpha(-1)=\alpha(-2)=\dots=\alpha(-d+1)=0
, such that:

P(X)=\alpha(X)+\alpha(X-1)+\dots+\alpha(X-d+1).

Proof.

Let
P\in{\mathbb{R}}[X]
be fixed.

• Suppose that
P\in\mathscr{E}_{d}
and let us show that
P
is of the form required by the theorem. According to Theorem
2.1
, there exists
f\in{\mathbb{R}}[X]
such that:

P(X)=\binom{X+d}{d}f(X+d)-\binom{X}{d}f(X).

Now, consider
\alpha\in{\mathbb{R}}[X]
defined by:

\alpha(X):=\binom{X+d}{d}f(X+d)-\binom{X+d-1}{d}f(X+d-1).

So, we have clearly

\alpha(-1)=\alpha(-2)=\dots=\alpha(-d+1)=0.

Next, we have

\displaystyle\alpha(X)+\alpha(X-1)+\dots+\alpha(X-d+1)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{d-1}\alpha(X-k)

\displaystyle\hskip-113.81102pt=\underbrace{\sum_{k=0}^{d-1}\left\{\binom{X-k+d}{d}f(X-k+d)-\binom{X-k+d-1}{d}f(X-k+d-1)\right\}}_{\text{telescopic sum}}

\displaystyle\hskip-113.81102pt=\binom{X+d}{d}f(X+d)-\binom{X}{d}f(X)

\displaystyle\hskip-113.81102pt=P(X).

Therefore
P
has the form required by the theorem.

• Conversely, suppose that there is
\alpha\in{\mathbb{R}}[X]
, satisfying
\alpha(-1)=\alpha(-2)=\dots=\alpha(-d+1)=0
, such that:

P(X)=\alpha(X)+\alpha(X-1)+\dots+\alpha(X-d+1)

and let us show that
P\in\mathscr{E}_{d}
. For a given
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
, denoting by
r_{n}
the remainder of the euclidean division of
n
by
d
(so
r_{n}\in\{0,1,\dots,d-1\}
), we have

\displaystyle P(n)+P(n-d)+P(n-2d)+\dots

\displaystyle=P(n)+P(n-d)+P(n-2d)+\dots+P(r_{n})

\displaystyle=\phantom{+}\alpha(n)+\alpha(n-1)+\dots+\alpha(n-d+1)

\displaystyle\phantom{=}~+

\displaystyle\phantom{=+}~\alpha(n-d)+\alpha(n-d-1)+\dots+\alpha(n-2d+1)

\displaystyle\phantom{=}~+

\displaystyle\phantom{=+}~\alpha(n-2d)+\alpha(n-2d-1)+\dots+\alpha(n-3d+1)

\displaystyle\phantom{=}~+

\displaystyle\phantom{=}~~\vdots

\displaystyle\phantom{=}~+

\displaystyle\phantom{=+}~\alpha(r_{n})+\alpha(r_{n}-1)+\dots+\alpha(r_{n}-d+1)

\displaystyle=\alpha(n)+\alpha(n-1)+\dots+\alpha(r_{n}-d+1).

But since
-d+1\leq r_{n}-d+1\leq 0
and
\alpha(k)=0
for
k=-1,-2,\dots,-d+1
, it follows that:

P(n)+P(n-d)+P(n-2d)+\dots=\alpha(n)+\alpha(n-1)+\dots+\alpha(0),

which is polynomial in
n
(since
\alpha\in{\mathbb{R}}[X]
). This concludes that
P\in\mathscr{E}_{d}
, as required. The proof of the theorem is complete.
∎

Study of the spaces
\mathscr{E}_{d}
through formal power series and generalization

We briefly present in what follows a new approach (using convolution products and formal power series) to studying the
{\mathbb{R}}
-vector space
\mathscr{E}_{d}
(
d\in{\mathbb{N}}^{*}
) and expressing in a closed form the sums of the type:

P(n)+P(n-d)+P(n-2d)+\dots~~~~~~~~~~(P\in{\mathbb{R}}[X],n\in{\mathbb{N}}).

Then, we lean on the obtained result in order to generalize the spaces
\mathscr{E}_{d}
. The results concerning the generalized spaces in question, which are analog to the above results on the
\mathscr{E}_{d}
’s, are given without proofs.

By definition,
the ordinary convolution product
of two real sequences
\mathbf{a}={(a_{n})}_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
and
\mathbf{b}={(b_{n})}_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
is the real sequence
{(\mathbf{a}*\mathbf{b})}_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
, defined by:

{(\mathbf{a}*\mathbf{b})}_{n}:=\sum_{k=0}^{n}a_{k}b_{n-k}~~~~~~~~~~(\forall n\in{\mathbb{N}}).

It is known that the convolution product constitutes a commutative and associative law of composition on the set of the real sequences. In addition, the Dirac delta sequence
\boldsymbol{\delta}={(\delta_{n})}_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
, defined by:
\delta_{0}=1
and
\delta_{n}=0
(
\forall n\geq 1
) is the neutral element of
*
. Finally, every real sequence
{(a_{n})}_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
with
a_{0}\neq 0
has an inverse element for
*
. Further,
the ordinary generating function
of a given real sequence
\mathbf{u}={(u_{n})}_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
is the formal power series defined by:

\sigma(\mathbf{u}):=\sum_{n=0}^{+\infty}u_{n}X^{n}.

When
\mathbf{a}={(a_{n})}_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
and
\mathbf{b}={(b_{n})}_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
are two real sequences, we have the fundamental formula:

\sigma(\mathbf{a})\cdot\sigma(\mathbf{b})=\sigma(\mathbf{a}*\mathbf{b}).

Next, for a positive integer
d
, let
\boldsymbol{\delta}^{(d)}={(\delta_{n}^{(d)})}_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
denote the real sequence defined by:

\delta_{n}^{(d)}:=\begin{cases}1&\text{if $n$ is a multiple of $d$}\\
0&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}~~~~~~~~~~(\forall n\in{\mathbb{N}}).

(Notice that
\boldsymbol{\delta}^{(1)}\equiv 1
and
\boldsymbol{\delta}^{(+\infty)}=\boldsymbol{\delta}
). So, the ordinary generating function of
\boldsymbol{\delta}^{(d)}
(
d\in{\mathbb{N}}^{*}
) is given by:

\displaystyle\sigma(\boldsymbol{\delta}^{(d)})

\displaystyle:=\sum_{n=0}^{+\infty}\delta_{n}^{(d)}X^{n}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\in{\mathbb{N}}\\
n\equiv 0\ (\mathrm{mod}\ d)\end{subarray}}X^{n}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{+\infty}X^{dk};

that is

\sigma(\boldsymbol{\delta}^{(d)})=\frac{1}{1-X^{d}}.

(2.8)

For simplicity, for
P\in{\mathbb{R}}[X]
and
d\in{\mathbb{N}}^{*}
, we let
S_{P,d}
denote the real sequence of general term
S_{P,d}(n)
(
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
) defined by:

S_{P,d}(n):=P(n)+P(n-d)+P(n-2d)+\dots

(recall that the sum on the right stops at
P(r)
, where
r
is the remainder of the euclidean division of
n
by
d
). So, given
d
a positive integer and
P
a real polynomial, we have for any natural number
n
:

\displaystyle S_{P,d}(n)

\displaystyle:=P(n)+P(n-d)+P(n-2d)+\dots

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\delta_{k}^{(d)}P(n-k)

\displaystyle=\left(\boldsymbol{\delta}^{(d)}*P\right)(n);

hence the fundamental formula of this approach:

S_{P,d}=\boldsymbol{\delta}^{(d)}*P.

(2.9)

We also need for this approach the following well-known theorem which characterizes the ordinary generating functions of polynomial sequences.

Theorem 1

(
[
8
, Corollary 4.3.1]
)
.

Let
{(a_{n})}_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
be a non-zero real sequence. Then
{(a_{n})}_{n}
is polynomial in
n
if and only if its ordinary generating function is a rational function of the form
\frac{U(X)}{(1-X)^{\alpha}}
, with
\alpha\in{\mathbb{N}}^{*}
,
U\in{\mathbb{R}}[X]
,
\deg U<\alpha
and
U(1)\neq 0
. In addition, in the situation where
\sigma(a_{n})=\frac{U(X)}{(1-X)^{\alpha}}

(
with the above conditions on
U
and
\alpha
)
, the degree of
a_{n}

(
as a polynomial in
n
)
is equal to
(\alpha-1)
.

According to Theorem
1
, it is associated to every polynomial
P\in{\mathbb{R}}[X]
a unique polynomial
A_{P}\in{\mathbb{R}}[X]
satisfying the identity of formal power series:

\sum_{n=0}^{+\infty}P(n)X^{n}=\frac{A_{P}(X)}{(1-X)^{\deg P+1}}.

(2.10)

If, in addition,
P\neq 0_{{\mathbb{R}}[X]}
then we have
A_{P}\neq 0_{{\mathbb{R}}[X]}
,
A_{P}(1)\neq 0
and
\deg A_{P}\leq\deg P
. The correspondence
P\mapsto A_{P}
provides a simple characterization of the property of belonging to a certain space
\mathscr{E}_{d}
for a given real polynomial. This characterization inspires us a generalization of the
{\mathbb{R}}
-vector spaces
\mathscr{E}_{d}
. First, we have the following proposition:

Proposition 2.8
.

Let
d
be a positive integer and
P
be a real polynomial. Then
P
belongs to
\mathscr{E}_{d}
if and only if
A_{P}
is a multiple
(
in
{\mathbb{R}}[X]
)
of the polynomial
(1+X+X^{2}+\dots+X^{d-1})
.

Proof.

The result of the proposition is obvious for
P=0_{{\mathbb{R}}[X]}
. Suppose for the sequel of this proof that
P\neq 0_{{\mathbb{R}}[X]}
and set
s:=\deg P\in{\mathbb{N}}
. By using successively Formulas (
2.9
), (
2.8
) and (
2.10
), we get

\displaystyle\sigma(S_{P,d})

\displaystyle=\sigma(\boldsymbol{\delta}^{(d)}*P)

\displaystyle=\sigma(\boldsymbol{\delta}^{(d)})\cdot\sigma(P)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{1-X^{d}}\sum_{n=0}^{+\infty}P(n)X^{n}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(1-X)(1+X+X^{2}+\dots+X^{d-1})}\cdot\frac{A_{P}(X)}{(1-X)^{s+1}}

\displaystyle=\frac{A_{P}(X)}{(1+X+X^{2}+\dots+X^{d-1})(1-X)^{s+2}}.

(2.11)

• Suppose that
A_{P}
is a multiple (in
{\mathbb{R}}[X]
) of the polynomial
(1+X+X^{2}+\dots+X^{d-1})
and let
U(X):=\frac{A_{P}(X)}{1+X+X^{2}+\dots+X^{d-1}}\in{\mathbb{R}}[X]
. Then
\deg U=\deg A_{P}-(d-1)\leq\deg A_{P}\leq\deg P=s
and
U(1)=\frac{A_{P}(1)}{d}\neq 0
. Next, Formula (
2.11
) becomes:
\sigma(S_{P,d})=\frac{U(X)}{(1-X)^{s+2}}
, which implies (according to Theorem
1
) that
S_{P,d}(n)
is polynomial in
n
; that is
P\in\mathscr{E}_{d}
.

• Conversely, suppose that
P\in\mathscr{E}_{d}
; that is
S_{P,d}(n)
is polynomial in
n
. By Theorem
1
, the power series
\sigma(S_{P,d})
has the form:

\sigma\left(S_{P,d}\right)=\frac{U(X)}{(1-X)^{\alpha}},

where
\alpha\in{\mathbb{N}}^{*}
,
U\in{\mathbb{R}}[X]
,
\deg U<\alpha
and
U(1)\neq 0
. It follows (according to (
2.11
)) that:

\frac{U(X)}{(1-X)^{\alpha}}=\frac{A_{P}(X)}{\left(1+X+X^{2}+\dots+X^{d-1}\right)\left(1-X\right)^{s+2}}.

Thus

\left(1+X+X^{2}+\dots+X^{d-1}\right)\left(1-X\right)^{s+2}U(X)=\left(1-X\right)^{\alpha}A_{P}(X).

This shows in particular that
(1+X+X^{2}+\dots+X^{d-1})\mid(1-X)^{\alpha}A_{P}(X)
. But since
(1+X+X^{2}+\dots+X^{d-1})
is coprime with
(1-X)^{\alpha}
(because
(1+X+X^{2}+\dots+X^{d-1})
does not vanish at
X=1
), it follows (according to Gauss’s lemma) that
(1+X+X^{2}+\dots+X^{d-1})\mid A_{P}(X)
, as required. This completes the proof of the proposition.
∎

By carefully observing the proof of Proposition
2.8
, we see that the polynomial
(1+X+X^{2}+\dots+X^{d-1})
which appears there has no particularity apart from the fact that it does not vanish at
X=1
. This inspires us a generalization of the spaces
\mathscr{E}_{d}
in the following way.

Definition 2.9

(Generalization of the spaces
\mathscr{E}_{d}
)
.

Let
D\in{\mathbb{R}}[X]
such that
D(1)\neq 0
. We define
\mathscr{E}_{D}
as the set of all polynomials
P\in{\mathbb{R}}[X]
for which the polynomial
A_{P}
is a multiple of
D
(in
{\mathbb{R}}[X]
).

Notice that for all positive integer
d
, we have (according to Proposition
2.8
)

\mathscr{E}_{d}=\mathscr{E}_{(1+X+X^{2}+\dots+X^{d-1})}.

Further, we show quite easily that for all
D\in{\mathbb{R}}[X]
, with
D(1)\neq 1
, the set
\mathscr{E}_{D}
constitutes a
{\mathbb{R}}
-linear subspace of
{\mathbb{R}}[X]
. A basis of
\mathscr{E}_{D}
(as a
{\mathbb{R}}
-vector space) is specified by the following theorem, the proof of which (left to the reader’s imagination) mainly uses Theorem
1
.

Theorem 2.10
.

Let
d
be a positive integer and
D
be a real polynomial of degree
(d-1)
such that
D(1)\neq 0
. Let
h\in{\mathbb{R}}[X]
defined by the formal power series identity:

\frac{D(X)}{(1-X)^{\alpha}}=\sum_{n=0}^{+\infty}h(n)X^{n}.

Then, the family of polynomials
(h,h*1,h*X,h*X^{2},\dots)
constitutes a basis for the
{\mathbb{R}}
-vector space
\mathscr{E}_{D}
.
\square

Remark 2.11
.

We can derive our main theorem
2.1
on the spaces
\mathscr{E}_{d}
from the general theorem
2.10
as follows.

•

First, show that we have for all positive integer
d
:

\frac{1+X+X^{2}+\dots+X^{d-1}}{(1-X)^{d}}=\sum_{n=0}^{+\infty}\left(\binom{n+d}{d}-\binom{n}{d}\right)X^{n}.

•

(Less easy!). Show the following statement:

For all positive integer
d
and all polynomial
f\in{\mathbb{R}}[X]
, there exists a unique polynomial
g\in{\mathbb{R}}[X]
, which vanishes at
(-1)
, such that:

\left(\binom{n+d}{d}-\binom{n}{d}\right)*f(n)=\binom{n+d}{d}g(n+d)-\binom{n}{d}g(n).

In addition, the correspondence
f\mapsto g
from
{\mathbb{R}}[X]
to
\{g\in{\mathbb{R}}[X]:g(-1)=0\}
is bijective.

We finally derive from Theorem
2.10
the below corollary, generalizing Corollary
2.3
.

Corollary 2.12
.

In the context of Theorem
2.10
, we have

\mathscr{E}_{D}\oplus{\mathbb{R}}_{d-2}[X]={\mathbb{R}}[X].

In particular, we have

\mathrm{codim}\,_{{\mathbb{R}}[X]}\mathscr{E}_{D}=d-1.

\square
