Eigenvalue processes of symmetric tridiagonal matrix-valued processes associated with Gaussian beta ensemble
Satoshi Yabuoku

Abstract
We consider the symmetric tridiagonal matrix-valued process associated with Gaussian beta ensemble (G β \beta E) by putting independent Brownian motions and Bessel processes on the diagonal entries and upper (lower)-diagonal ones, respectively. Then, we derive the stochastic differential equations that the eigenvalue processes satisfy, and we show that eigenvalues of their (indexed) principal minor sub-matrices appear in the stochastic differential equations. By the Cauchy’s interlacing argument for eigenvalues, we can characterize the sufficient condition that the eigenvalue processes never collide with each other almost surely, by the dimensions of the Bessel processes.

Introduction

In random matrix theory, Dyson
[
7
]
introduced symmetric, hermitian and symplectic matrix-valued processes having matrix entries as independent Ornstein-Uhlenbeck processes, and he derived the stochastic differential equations (SDEs for short) that their eigenvalue processes satisfy:

\displaystyle d\lambda_{i}(t)=\sqrt{\frac{2}{\beta}}dB_{i}(t)+\sum_{j(\neq i)}\frac{1}{\lambda_{i}(t)-\lambda_{j}(t)}dt-\frac{\lambda_{i}(t)}{2}dt,\ \ i=1,\dots,N,

(1.1)

where
B_{i},i=1,\dots,N
are independent one-dimensional standard Brownian motions, and the three matrix symmetries correspond to
\beta=1,2
and
4
, respectively. The invariant measure of the diffusion process
\boldsymbol{\lambda}=(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{N})
satisfying (
1.1
) is

\displaystyle\mu_{\beta,N}(dx)=\frac{1}{Z_{\beta,N}}e^{-\frac{\beta}{4}\sum_{i=1}^{N}x_{i}^{2}}\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq N}|x_{i}-x_{j}|^{\beta}\prod_{i=1}^{N}dx_{i},

(1.2)

and (
1.2
) is the probability distribution of eigenvalues of GOE, GUE and GSE with
\beta=1,2
and
4
, respectively
[
3
,
15
]
. Such a Gibbs measure is called a Coulomb gas because of the logarithmic interactions.
Dyson’s results of matrix-valued processes are generalized by Katori and Tanemura
[
13
]
, and it is proved that when matrix entries are replaced with independent Brownian motions, their eigenvalue processes satisfy the SDEs (up to a time change):

\displaystyle d\lambda_{i}(t)=\sqrt{2}dB_{i}(t)+{\beta}\sum_{j(\neq i)}\frac{1}{\lambda_{i}(t)-\lambda_{j}(t)}dt,\ \ \beta>0,\ \ i=1,\dots,N.

(1.3)

Here, the three matrix symmetries correspond to
\beta=1,2
and 4, respectively, also see
[
3
, Section 4.3]
. Dyson
[
7
]
also found that the system of independent one-dimensional Brownian motions with the condition that they never collide with each other satisfy (
1.3
), also see
[
14
]
and references therein. Such stochastic processes satisfying (
1.1
) or (
1.3
) are called Dyson’s Brownian motion. In this paper, we refer to Dyson’s Brownian motion as a solution of (
1.3
). The solutions of SDEs (
1.1
) and (
1.3
) with general
\beta>0
are studied. Rogers and Shi
[
16
]
proved that if
\beta\geq 1
, these solutions never collide with each other nor explode almost surely. Cépa and Lépingle
[
5
]
proved the existence of unique strong solution for
\beta>0
with a reflecting boundary condition.

Recently, constructions of eigenvalue processes which satisfy SDEs (
1.3
) for general
\beta>0
are reported. As mentioned above, it is clear for
\beta=1,2
and 4. Allez and Guionnet
[
2
]
constructed a sequence of symmetric (hermitian) matrix-valued processes whose eigenvalue processes converge weakly to a stochastic process, and the limiting process satisfies (
1.3
) for
0<\beta\leq 1
(
0<\beta\leq 2
, respectively) with the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck drift. Holcomb and Paquette consider a symmetric tridiagonal matrix-valued process whose eigenvalue process satisfies (
1.3
) (or (
1.1
)) and give a sufficient condition of such matrices in
[
11
, Theorem 6]
. Their construction relies on the Lanczos algorithm. Fukushima, Tanida and Yano proved
[
8
]
that a
2\times 2
symmetric matrix-valued process, which is defined by putting independent Brownian motions and Bessel processes on diagonal entries and off-diagonal ones, respectively, has the eigenvalue process satisfying (
1.3
) with general
\beta>0
. Note that this symmetric matrix-valued process is a natural time dependent model of Gaussian beta ensemble (G
\beta
E for short), introduced by Dumitriu and Edelman
[
6
]
. G
\beta
E is a well-known model because this is a generalization of GOE, GUE and GSE in the following sense.
For
\beta>0
, G
\beta
E is an
N\times N
symmetric tridiagonal matrix
H_{\beta}^{tri}
defined by

\displaystyle H_{\beta}^{tri}=\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{\beta}}\begin{pmatrix}g_{1}&\chi_{(N-1)\beta}&0&\cdots&\cdots&0\\
\chi_{(N-1)\beta}&g_{2}&\chi_{(N-2)\beta}&&&\vdots\\
0&\chi_{(N-2)\beta}&&\ddots&&\vdots\\
\vdots&&\ddots&\ddots&\chi_{2\beta}&0\\
\vdots&&&\chi_{2\beta}&g_{N-1}&\chi_{\beta}\\
0&\cdots&\cdots&0&\chi_{\beta}&g_{N}\end{pmatrix},

(1.4)

where
\{g_{i}\}_{1\leq i\leq N}
are independent Gaussian random variables
\mathcal{N}(0,2)
, and
\{\chi_{k\beta}\}_{1\leq k\leq N-1}
are independent
\chi
-distributed random variables with shape parameter
k\beta
and independent of
\{g_{i}\}_{1\leq i\leq N}
. Then, Dumitriu and Edelman
[
6
]
proved that eigenvalue probability distribution of
H_{\beta}^{tri}
is (
1.2
) with
\beta>0
. For related topics of G
\beta
E, see
[
1
, Chapter 20]
and
[
3
, Section 4.5]
.

In this paper, we introduce the
N\times N
symmetric tridiagonal matrix-valued process
H_{\alpha}(t)
defined by (
2.1
), associated with G
\beta
E. More precisely,
H_{\alpha}(t)
is constructed by putting independent Brownian motions and Bessel processes with dimension
\alpha_{k},k=1,\dots,N-1
on diagonal entries and off-diagonal ones, respectively. Note that
H_{\alpha}(t)
does not meet the sufficient condition that their eigenvalue process satisfies (
1.3
) as reported in
[
11
]
. In our main result (Theorem
2.1
), we derive the SDEs (
2.1
) of eigenvalue process of
H_{\alpha}(t)
for general
N>0
, and we conclude that several eigenvalues of indexed principal minor sub-matrices of
H_{\alpha}(t)
appear in the SDEs. Hence, the SDEs that eigenvalue processes of
H_{\alpha}(t)
satisfy are non-Markov type. Formally, drift terms of the obtained SDEs tell us that the eigenvalues of
H_{\alpha}(t)
interact with their minor eigenvalues, and these interactions are similar to that of Dyson’s Brownian motion (
1.3
), see Remark
2.2
. Moreover, we characterize the sufficient condition that eigenvalue processes of
H_{\alpha}(t)
never collide with each other almost surely, by using the dimensions
\alpha_{k},k=1,\dots,N-1
(and starting points) of Bessel processes. As a corollary, for
2\times 2
(sub-)matrices, we recover the construction of eigenvalue processes done in
[
8
]
. We remark some related topics for minor eigenvalue processes. In the each case of symmetric, hermitian and symplectic-matrix valued process, namely Dyson’s Brownian motion with
\beta=1,2
and 4 respectively, the eigenvalue process with their minor eigenvalues has been studied in
[
4
]
. Adler, Nordenstam and van Moerbeke proved that eigenvalue processes of two consecutive minors for these matrices are diffusion processes, and they derive the SDEs that these processes satisfy. Our SDEs (
2.1
) slightly look like their SDEs in
[
4
, (2.27)]
, however, ours are more complicated. They also showed that in the case of three consecutive minors, eigenvalue processes with minor eigenvalues are non-Markov. On the other hand, in the context of non-intersecting diffusion processes, multilevel versions of Dyson’s Brownian motions are studied
[
9
]
and known as corner process. Note that the eigenvalue processes with their minors and corner processes are very different processes as mentioned in
[
4
]
.

The organization of the paper is the following. Preliminaries and our main results are in Section
2
. We give proofs of Theorem
2.1
and Corollary
2.4
in Section
3
. Appendix provides some linear algebraic tools.

Preliminaries and Main Results

Let
\alpha_{k},k=1,\dots,N-1

\alpha=(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{N-1})

\displaystyle H_{\alpha}(t):=\begin{pmatrix}\sqrt{2}B_{1}(t)&X_{1}(t)&0&\cdots&\cdots&0\\
X_{1}(t)&\sqrt{2}B_{2}(t)&X_{2}(t)&&&\vdots\\
0&X_{2}(t)&&\ddots&&\vdots\\
\vdots&&\ddots&\ddots&X_{N-2}(t)&0\\
\vdots&&&X_{N-2}(t)&\sqrt{2}B_{N-1}(t)&X_{N-1}(t)\\

(2.1)

where
B_{k},k=1,\dots,N
are independent one-dimensional standard Brownian motions defined on a filtered probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\{\mathcal{F}_{t}\}_{t\geq 0},{\rm P})
, and
X_{k},k=1,\dots,N-1
are independent Bessel processes started at

with dimension
\alpha_{k}
, denoted by
{\rm BES}^{\alpha_{k}}(x_{k})

\displaystyle\begin{cases}&dX_{k}(t)=dB_{kk+1}(t)+\dfrac{\alpha_{k}-1}{2}\frac{1}{X_{k}(t)}dt\\
&X_{k}(0)=x_{k}\end{cases},

where
B_{kk+1},k=1,\dots,N
are independent one-dimensional standard Brownian motions which are independent of
\{B_{k}\}_{1\leq k\leq N}
.
Recall that for

,

,

is a diffusion process on

which is the solution of

\begin{cases}&dX(t)=dB(t)+\dfrac{\alpha-1}{2}\frac{1}{X(t)}dt,\ t<T_{0}^{x,\alpha}\\
&X(0)=x\end{cases},

(2.2)

where

is a one-dimensional standard Brownian motion, and
T_{0}^{x,\alpha}:=\inf\{t>0;X(t)=0\}

at the origin. Note that (
2.2

T_{0}^{x,\alpha}
and if

, the point 0 is polar, that is,

\displaystyle T_{0}^{x,\alpha}=\infty,\ \ a.s.,

(2.3)

so that

lies in the positive half line
(0,\infty)
almost surely. On the other hand, if
0<\alpha<2
,
{\rm P}(T_{0}^{x,\alpha}<\infty)=1

is recurrent, see
[
17
, Chapter XI]
. Hence, to make

when there exists

0<\alpha_{k}<2
. Note that

can be seen as a time evolution of G

E because if we take

1.4
) we have
\frac{1}{\sqrt{\beta}}(H_{\alpha}(1)-H_{\alpha}(0))=H_{\beta}^{tri}
in distribution.

For

, denote

as the

. By definition of

\displaystyle T_{{\rm col}}:=\min_{p,q;\ q-p>1}T_{{\rm col}}^{p,q}.

(2.5)

\displaystyle H_{\alpha}(0)\ {\rm\ has\ simple\ spectrum,\ that\ is,\ }\lambda_{1}(0)<\dots<\lambda_{N}(0).

Lemma A.1

(Drift terms of Dyson’s model and characteristic polynomial)
.

Assume that an
N\times N
matrix
A
has simple spectrum
\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{N}
, and the characteristic polynomial is
f(\lambda):=\det(\lambda I_{N}-A)
. Then for
i=1,\dots,N
,

\displaystyle\frac{f_{\lambda\lambda}(\lambda_{i})}{f_{\lambda}(\lambda_{i})}=2\sum_{j(\neq i)}\frac{1}{\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{j}}.

(A.1)

Proof.

By definition of eigenvalues,

f(\lambda)=\prod_{j=1}^{N}(\lambda-\lambda_{j})=(\lambda-\lambda_{i})\prod_{j(\neq i)}(\lambda-\lambda_{j})
. Differentiating this with respect to
\lambda
, we have

\displaystyle f_{\lambda}(\lambda)

\displaystyle=\prod_{j(\neq i)}(\lambda-\lambda_{j})+(\lambda-\lambda_{i})\sum_{j(\neq i)}\prod_{k(\neq i,j)}(\lambda-\lambda_{k}),

\displaystyle f_{\lambda\lambda}(\lambda)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j(\neq i)}\prod_{k(\neq i,j)}(\lambda-\lambda_{k})+\sum_{j(\neq i)}\prod_{k(\neq i,j)}(\lambda-\lambda_{k})+(\lambda-\lambda_{i})\sum_{j(\neq i)}\sum_{k(\neq i,j)}\prod_{\ell(\neq i,j,k)}(\lambda-\lambda_{\ell}).

Substituting
\lambda=\lambda_{i}
,

\displaystyle f_{\lambda}(\lambda_{i})=\prod_{j(\neq i)}(\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{j}),\ f_{\lambda\lambda}(\lambda_{i})=2\sum_{j(\neq i)}\prod_{k(\neq i,j)}(\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{k}).

On the other hand, the right-hand side of (A.1) is

\displaystyle\sum_{j(\neq i)}\frac{1}{\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{j}}=\frac{\sum_{j(\neq i)}\prod_{k(\neq i,j)}(\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{k})}{\prod_{j(\neq i)}(\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{j})},

and the claim holds.
∎

Lemma A.4

(Cauchy-Binet formula,
[
10
]
)
.

Let
A\in M_{m,n}(\mathbb{C})
. For index sets
\alpha=\{i_{1},\dots,i_{p}\}\subseteq\{1,\dots,m\},\ p\leq m
and
\beta=\{j_{1},\dots,j_{q}\}\subseteq\{1,\dots,n\},\ q\leq n
, the
p\times q
submatrix
A(\alpha,\beta)
is defined as
A(\alpha,\beta)_{r,s}:=A_{i_{r},j_{s}}
. Here,
i_{1}<\cdots<i_{p}
and
j_{1}<\cdots<j_{q}
hold and these index sets are ordered lexicographically. When
\sharp\alpha=\sharp\beta=k\leq min\{m,n\}
, the
k
-th compound matrix of
A
is defined as the
\binom{m}{k}\times\binom{n}{k}
matrix whose
(\alpha,\beta)
entry is
\det(A(\alpha,\beta))
and we denote this by
C_{k}(A)
.

Let
A\in M_{m,k}(\mathbb{C})
,
B\in M_{k,n}(\mathbb{C})
and
C:=AB
. We fix the index sets
\alpha\subseteq\{1,\dots,m\}
and
\beta\subseteq\{1,\dots,n\}
where
\sharp\alpha=\sharp\beta=r\leq\min\{m,k,n\}
. Then the determinant of the submatrix
C(\alpha,\beta)
has an expression:

\displaystyle\det(C(\alpha,\beta))=\sum_{\gamma}\det(A(\alpha,\gamma))\det(B(\gamma,\beta)),

(A.4)

where the summation is taken over all index sets
\gamma\subseteq\{1,\dots,k\}
of cardinality
r

Lemma A.6

(Strong Cauchy’s interlacing law,
[
10
]
)
.

Let
A
be an
n\times n
symmetric tridiagonal matrix:

\displaystyle A:=\begin{pmatrix}a_{1}&b_{1}&0&\cdots&\cdots&\cdots&0\\
b_{1}&a_{2}&b_{2}&\ddots&&&\vdots\\
0&b_{2}&a_{3}&\ddots&\ddots&&\vdots\\
\vdots&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\vdots&&\ddots&\ddots&a_{n-2}&b_{n-2}&0\\
\vdots&&&\ddots&b_{n-2}&a_{n-1}&b_{n-1}\\
0&\cdots&\cdots&\cdots&0&b_{n-1}&a_{n}\end{pmatrix}.

Let
\lambda_{1}\leq\cdots\leq\lambda_{n}
and
\eta_{1}\leq\cdots\leq\eta_{n-1}
be the eigenvalues of A and that of the
(n-1)
-th leading principal minor of
A
, respectively. Suppose
b_{i}\neq 0
for
1\leq i\leq n-1
, then the strong Cauchy’s interlacing law holds:

\displaystyle\lambda_{1}<\eta_{1}<\lambda_{2}<\cdots<\lambda_{n-1}<\eta_{n-1}<\lambda_{n}.

(A.6)

In particular, the eigenvalues
\{\lambda_{i}\}_{1\leq i\leq n}
of
A
are distinct.

Proof.

For
2\leq k\leq n
, let
A^{(k)}
be the
k
-th leading principal minor of
A
, and
f_{k}(\lambda):=\det(\lambda I_{k}-A^{(k)})
. Expanding determinants, we have

\displaystyle f_{k}(\lambda)=(\lambda-a_{k})f_{k-1}(\lambda)-b_{k-1}^{2}f_{k-2}(\lambda),\ \ 2\leq k\leq n,

(A.7)

where
f_{0}(\lambda)\equiv 1
. By the Cauchy’s interlacing law
[
10
]
, we have

\lambda_{1}\leq\eta_{1}\leq\lambda_{2}\leq\cdots\leq\eta_{n-1}\leq\lambda_{n}.

Hence, to show the claim, it is sufficient to prove the strong interlacement. Suppose that there exists
\lambda_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
such that
f_{n}(\lambda_{0})=f_{n-1}(\lambda_{0})=0
. Then, by the assumption of
\{b_{i}\}
and using (
A.7
) for
k=n-1,\dots,2
repeatedly, we have
f_{0}(\lambda_{0})=0
, which is the contradiction.
∎
