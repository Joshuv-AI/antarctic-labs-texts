Population inference of spin-induced quadrupole moments as a probe for non-black hole compact binaries
Muhammed Saleem, N. V. Krishnendu, Abhirup Ghosh, Anuradha Gupta, W. Del Pozzo, Archisman Ghosh, K. G. Arun

Abstract
Gravitational wave (GW) measurements of physical effects such as spin-induced quadrupole moments can distinguish binaries consisting of black holes from non-black hole binaries. While these effects may be poorly constrained for single-event inferences with the second-generation detectors, combining information from multiple detections can help uncover features of non-black hole binaries. The spin-induced quadrupole moment has specific predictions for different types of compact objects, and a generalized formalism must consider a population where different types of compact objects co-exist. In this study, we introduce a hierarchical mixture-likelihood formalism to estimate the fraction of non- binary black holes in the population . We demonstrate the applicability of this method using simulated GW signals injected into Gaussian noise following the design sensitivities of the Advanced LIGO Advanced Virgo detectors. We compare the performance of this method with a traditionally-followed hierarchical inference approach. Both the methods are equally effective to hint at inhomogeneous populations, however, we find the mixture-likelihood approach to be more natural for mixture populations comprising compact objects of diverse classes. We also discuss the possible systematics in the mixture-likelihood approach, caused by several reasons, including the limited sensitivity of the second-generation detectors, specific features of the astrophysical population distributions, and the limitations posed by the waveform models employed. Finally, we apply this method to the LIGO-Virgo detections published in the second GW transient catalog (GWTC-2) and find them consistent with a binary black hole population within the statistical precision.

II
Review: Gravitational-wave measurements of spin-induced multipole moments

The spin-induced multipole moments arise due to the spinning motion of the compact objects in the binary and these effects appear in the gravitational waveform along with the self-spin terms. The leading order effect
Poisson 1998
;
Krishnendu et al. 2017
;
Marsat 2015
at the second post-Newtonian (2PN) order can be schematically represented in the following form,

Q=-\kappa\,\mathbf{\chi}^{2}\,m^{3},

(1)

where
Q
is the spin-induced quadrupole moment scalar,

m
is the mass and
\chi
is the dimensionless spin parameter, defined as
\vec{\chi}=\vec{S}/m^{2}
where
\vec{S}
is the spin angular momentum of the compact object.

The spin-induced quadrupole moment parameter
\kappa
has a unique value, unity, for Kerr BH according to the no-hair conjecture

Hansen 1974
;
Carter 1971
;
Gürlebeck 2015b
, whereas, for any other compact object, its value can be different from unity. For example, for spinning NSs, the value of
\kappa
varies between
\sim 2-14
depending upon the internal structure of the star
Laarakkers and Poisson 1999
;
Pappas and
Apostolatos 2012a
;
Pappas and
Apostolatos 2012b
. Also, calculations show that the value of
\kappa
can vary roughly between 10 to 150 for boson stars
Ryan 1997
while for gravastars
Mazur and Mottola 2004

\kappa
can be negative as well
Uchikata and Yoshida 2016
;
Uchikata et al. 2016
. In Fig.
1
we compare gravitational waveforms of BBHs (i.e.,
\kappa=1
) and non-BBHs (
\kappa\neq 1
) for two different values of spin parameters.
Both the binaries have same masses
(m_{1},m_{2})=(20,10)M_{\odot}
while non-BBHs have
(\kappa_{1},\kappa_{2})=(40,25)
. Highly spinning binaries have
(\chi_{1},\chi_{2})=(0.6,0.5)
while slowly spinning binaries have
(\chi_{1},\chi_{2})=(0.15,0.1)
, assuming the spins aligned to the orbital angular momentum axis. We used
IMRPhenomPv2

Ajith et al. 2011
;
Husa et al. 2016
;
Hannam et al. 2014
waveform model to simulate the time-domain GW signal. We see that as the spins of the binary components increase, the de-phasing between the BBH and non-BBH waveforms increases.

A Bayesian framework to measure the
\kappa
parameters was demonstrated in
Krishnendu et al. 2019
, to constrain the nature of the stellar mass compact binaries detected by Advanced LIGO and Advanced Virgo detectors. It was shown that the spin-induced quadrupole moment measurements can be used to distinguish the observed BBHs from non-BBHs for inspiral dominated systems with moderate to high spins
Krishnendu et al. 2019
.

In this framework, one uses BBH waveforms which allow the spin-induced quadrupole moment coefficient
\kappa
to vary around the expected Kerr value as
\kappa=1+\delta\kappa
. Here the parametrized deformations (labeled as
\delta\kappa
) represents the deviations from the BH nature. It is pointed out in
Krishnendu et al. 2019
that the simultaneous measurement of
\delta\kappa_{1}
and
\delta\kappa_{2}
is difficult due to strong correlations between binary parameters in the gravitational waveform. In order to capture the deviation from the BBH nature Ref.
Krishnendu et al. 2019
proposed to measure their symmetric combination
\delta\kappa_{s}=(\delta\kappa_{1}+\delta\kappa_{2})/2
, assuming the anti-symmetric combination
\delta\kappa_{a}=(\delta\kappa_{1}-\delta\kappa_{2})/2
vanishes for a BBH signal. The
\delta\kappa_{a}=0
assumption also implies that the individual compact objects in the binary system are of the same nature. For cases with any violation of this assumption, we would expect an offset in
\delta\kappa_{s}
posterior distribution, and such cases need more investigations keeping
\delta\kappa_{1}
and
\delta\kappa_{2}
as separate parameters. (See
Krishnendu and Yelikar 2019
for a more detailed discussion). The applicability of this test has been further explored in the context of expected detections from the third generation GW detectors
Krishnendu et al. 2018
.

III
Formalism

In a universe where all the massive compact objects are BBHs,
\delta\kappa_{s}
assumes the unique and universal value
\delta\kappa_{s}=0
. However, if we admit the possibility that compact objects come in many flavors, this universality assumption would be wrong, and the inference on
\delta\kappa_{s}
obtained by naively multiplying each observation’s likelihood would lead to erroneous conclusions. Even for the binaries that are made up of some specific class of exotic compact objects with a unique equation of state, the value of
\delta\kappa_{s}
could vary depending on the intrinsic properties such as masses and spins as is found to be the case for boson stars
Ryan 1997
. In such a case where the value of
\delta\kappa_{s}
can vary from event to event, one would aim to infer the underlying distribution of
\delta\kappa_{s}
associated with the compact binary population. This is the context in which we discuss the two combining approaches and their applicability.

Below, we introduce our notations for the Bayesian inference variables, followed by the formalisms for hierarchical combining and mixture-likelihood approach.

III.1
Bayesian inference: basic notations

The first step in our formalism is to perform the Bayesian parameter estimation of all the detected GW events. Here, we briefly overview the Bayesian inference method employed to estimate the spin-induced quadrupole moment parameter,
\delta\kappa_{s}
.
We define
\vec{\theta}
as the vector representing the set of parameters that describes a BBH merger on quasi-circular orbits. This includes masses, spins, luminosity distance, time and phase of arrival, and the angles describing the sky-location and binary orientation. The data from the j
th
event is labeled as
d_{\rm j}
, and the set of data from N events together is denoted as
\vec{d}
.
\mathcal{H}
is our hypothesis that the data
d_{\rm j}
carries a signal
{h}_{\rm j}(\vec{\theta},\delta\kappa_{s})
plus colored Gaussian random noise.
Under this hypothesis, the posterior for the binary parameters can be written as,

p(\vec{\theta},\delta\kappa_{s}\,|d_{\rm j},\,\mathcal{H})=\frac{\pi(\vec{\theta},\delta\kappa_{s}\,|\mathcal{H})\,\mathcal{L}(d_{\rm j}\,|\,\vec{\theta},\delta\kappa_{s},\,\mathcal{H})}{\mathcal{Z}^{nbh}_{\rm j}},

(2)

where

\mathcal{L}(d_{\rm j}\,|\,\vec{\theta},\delta\kappa_{s},\,\mathcal{H})
is the likelihood of
d_{\rm j}
being the data given the parameters
\{\vec{\theta},\delta\kappa_{s}\}
, and
\pi(\vec{\theta},\delta\kappa_{s}\,|\mathcal{H})
is the prior probability of parameters {
\vec{\theta}
.
\delta\kappa_{s}
}.
The evidence
\mathcal{Z}^{nbh}_{\rm j}=P(d_{\rm j}\,|\,\mathcal{H})
is obtained by marginalizing the likelihood over the prior,

\mathcal{Z}^{nbh}_{\rm j}=\int\pi(\vec{\theta},\delta\kappa_{s})\,\mathcal{L}(d_{\rm j}|\vec{\theta},\delta\kappa_{s})\,d\vec{\theta}\,d\delta\kappa_{s},

(3)

where, the superscript ‘
nbh
’ stands for the non-BBH hypothesis and we have dropped
\mathcal{H}
for brevity. The BBH hypothesis is a special case obtained by fixing
\delta\kappa_{s}=0
in the likelihood
\mathcal{L}(d_{\rm j}|\vec{\theta},\delta\kappa_{s}=0)
which we simply write as
\mathcal{L}(d_{\rm j}|\vec{\theta})
and the corresponding evidence can be expressed as,

\mathcal{Z}^{bh}_{\rm j}=\int\pi(\vec{\theta})\,\mathcal{L}(d_{\rm j}|\vec{\theta})\,d\vec{\theta}\,.

(4)

The posterior on
\delta\kappa_{s}
can be obtained by marginalizing Eq. (
2
) over the BBH parameters as

p(\delta\kappa_{s}\,|d_{\rm j},\,\mathcal{H})=\int p(\vec{\theta},\delta\kappa_{s}\,|d_{\rm j},\,\mathcal{H})\,d\vec{\theta}.

(5)

The
IMRPhenomPv2
signal model
Ajith et al. 2011
;
Husa et al. 2016
;
Hannam et al. 2014
used for the Bayesian analysis includes
\delta\kappa_{s}
as a free parameter along with the BBH parameters
\vec{\theta}
. We assume a uniform prior on
\delta\kappa_{s}
in
[-500,500]
. For the component masses, we consider uniform priors on in the range
[4,100]M_{\odot}
. The priors on component spin-magnitudes are uniform in [0,1] and their orientations assumed to be isotropic. We choose uniform in co-moving volume ranging between
[10,5000]
.
The parameter estimation is performed using the
lalinference_nest
sampler available in the
LALInference
library package
Veitch et al. 2015
. The posterior samples as well as the Bayesian evidences in Eq. (
3
) and (
4
) are obtained as raw outputs from
lalinference_nest
.

Below, we discuss the two different approaches for combining
\delta\kappa_{s}
measurements from the Bayesian analysis of individual events.

III.2
Hierarchical combining approach: population distribution of
\delta\kappa_{s}

In this approach, we assume that
\delta\kappa_{s}
follows some underlying distribution governed by a set of hyper-parameters
\vec{\alpha}
, similar to the method demonstrated in
Isi et al. 2019
.
The posterior on
\vec{\alpha}
given the data
\vec{d}
can be written as

p(\vec{\alpha}|\vec{d})\propto\mathcal{L}(\vec{d}|\vec{\alpha})\,p(\vec{\alpha}),

(6)

where the proportionality becomes equality by normalizing the right-hand side to unity. The prior
p(\vec{\alpha})
is taken to be flat assuming no prior knowledge of the underlying distribution of the hyper-parameters
\vec{\alpha}
. Here
\mathcal{L}(\vec{d}|\vec{\alpha})
is the likelihood function which can be obtained as a product of likelihoods of
\vec{\alpha}
from individual events, as

\mathcal{L}(\vec{d}|\vec{\alpha})=\prod_{j=1}^{N}\mathcal{L}(d_{\rm j}|\vec{\alpha}).

(7)

which can be further expanded by re-writing the likelihood for the j
{}^{\text{th}}
event as a marginalization over the
\delta\kappa_{s}
parameter,

\mathcal{L}(\vec{d}|\vec{\alpha})=\prod_{j=1}^{N}\left[\int\mathcal{L}(d_{\rm j}|\delta\kappa_{s})\,p(\delta\kappa_{s}|\vec{\alpha})\,d\delta\kappa_{s}\right].

(8)

In the above equation, the term
\mathcal{L}(d_{\rm j}|\delta\kappa_{s})
in the integral is the likelihood of
\delta\kappa_{s}
for the j
th
event, marginalized over the BBH parameters. Since we use a uniform prior on
\delta\kappa_{s}
in the single-event analyses, this likelihood will be the same as the posterior given in Eq. (
5
). The other term in the integral of Eq. (
8
),
p(\delta\kappa_{s}|\vec{\alpha})
is the predicted distribution of
\delta\kappa_{s}
given the hyper-parameters
\vec{\alpha}
. In this study, as mentioned earlier, we assume a Gaussian distribution with hyper-parameters
\vec{\alpha}=\{\mu,\sigma\}
which implies to,

p(\delta\kappa_{s}|\vec{\alpha})=\mathcal{N}(\mu,\sigma^{2})\,.

(9)

In this study, we assume
\mu
and
\sigma
to have uniform priors in the ranges
[-150,150]
and
[0,300]
respectively and we use the
Dynesty
sampler from
Bilby
to sample over
\mu
and
\sigma
as per the likelihood given in Eq. (
8
).

Once the posterior of
\mu
and
\sigma
(or
\vec{\alpha}
in general) is computed, the population distribution of
\delta\kappa_{s}
can be obtained as,

p(\delta\kappa_{s}|\vec{d})=\int p(\delta\kappa_{s}|\vec{\alpha})\,p(\vec{\alpha}|\vec{d})d\vec{\alpha}\,,

(10)

where we have marginalized
\delta\kappa_{s}
over the inferred distributions of the hyper-parameters
\vec{\alpha}
.

III.3
Mixture-likelihood approach: Estimating the fraction of non-BBH events

Unlike the hierarchical approach in the preceding section, here we ask a more generic question “what
fraction
of the detected population are from non-BBH events?”. We try to answer this with a
mixture-likelihood
which parametrizes the presence of non-BBH events as
f_{\text{nbh}}
, defined as the
fraction
of total detected signals that are from non-BBH events. Mixture likelihoods have been used in literature for various problems, see e.g., Ref.
Smith and Thrane 2018
.

Let’s start with the single-event likelihood expression for the non-BBH model,

\mathcal{L}(d_{\rm j}|\vec{\theta},\delta\kappa_{s})\propto\text{exp}\left({-\frac{1}{2}\left(d_{\rm j}-{h}_{\rm j}(\vec{\theta},\delta\kappa_{s})\big|d_{\rm j}-{h}_{\rm j}(\vec{\theta},\delta\kappa_{s})\right)}\right).

(11)

Here
(|)
represents the noise-weighted inner product, defined as

(x|y)=4\Re\int_{0}^{\infty}x(f)^{*}y(f)/\rm{S_{n}(f)}\,df
, where the
∗
indicates complex conjugate and
\rm{S_{n}(f)}
is the one-sided power spectral density (PSD) of the noise. Suppose a fraction
f_{\text{nbh}}
of the overall detectable signals are from non-BBH events, then the probability of any single event being a non-BBH will be equal to
f_{\text{nbh}}
. On the complementary side, the probability of any event being a BBH will be equal to (
1-f_{\text{nbh}}
), as BBH and non-BBH are two mutually exclusive and exhaustive
2

To take into account these possibilities, we can re-write the likelihood as a sum of the BBH and non-BBH likelihoods weighted by their respective probabilities as

\mathcal{L}(d_{\rm j}|\vec{\theta},\delta\kappa_{s},f_{\text{nbh}})=(1-f_{\text{nbh}})\,\mathcal{L}(d_{\rm j}|\vec{\theta})+f_{\text{nbh}}\,\mathcal{L}(d_{\rm j}|\vec{\theta},\delta\kappa_{s})\,.

\vec{\theta}

\delta\kappa_{s}

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(d_{\rm j}|f_{\text{nbh}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(1-f_{\text{nbh}})\times\int\pi(\vec{\theta})\,\mathcal{L}(d_{\rm j}|\vec{\theta})\,d\vec{\theta}

(13)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle f_{\text{nbh}}\times\int\pi(\vec{\theta},\delta\kappa_{s})\,\mathcal{L}(d_{\rm j}|\vec{\theta},\delta\kappa_{s})\,d\vec{\theta}\,d\delta\kappa_{s},

where the integrals on the R.H.S are the evidences for the BBH and non-BBH models, defined in Eq. (

\mathcal{L}(d_{\rm j}|f_{\text{nbh}})=(1-f_{\text{nbh}})\,\,\mathcal{Z}^{bh}_{\rm j}+f_{\text{nbh}}\,\mathcal{Z}^{nbh}_{\rm j}.

Note that in going from Eq.

For a population of N detected events, the combined likelihood can be written as,

\mathcal{L}^{\text{pop}}(\vec{d}|f_{\text{nbh}})=\prod_{j=1}^{N}\left((1-f_{\text{nbh}})\,\mathcal{Z}_{j}^{bh}+f_{\text{nbh}}\,\,\mathcal{Z}_{j}^{nbh}\right)\,.

(15)

Equation (

for a population and can be evaluated for any value of
f_{\text{nbh}}
, by only knowing the evidences of BBH and non-BBH models for all the events in the population.
With the above likelihood evaluated, we can express the posterior on
f_{nbh}

p(f_{\text{nbh}}|\vec{d})\propto\pi(f_{\text{nbh}})\,\,\mathcal{L}^{\text{pop}}(\vec{d}|f_{\text{nbh}})\,,

(16)

where the prior on
f_{\text{nbh}}
can be taken as uniform,
\mathcal{U}(0,1)
owing to the most generic and uninformative case.

Additionally, one can also define three mutually exclusive population-hypotheses based on the

1.

f_{\text{nbh}}=0
: “all the events are BBHs”,

2.

0<f_{\text{nbh}}<1
: “the population is a mixture of BBH and non-BBH events”,

3.

f_{\text{nbh}}=1
: “all the events are non-BBHs”.

It is straightforward to compute the Bayes factors between any of these two hypotheses. The Bayes factor between
“all are BBH” and “mixture of BBH and non-BBH events” can be obtained as

\mathcal{B}_{mix}^{BBH}=\frac{\mathcal{L}^{\text{pop}}(\vec{d}|f_{\text{nbh}}=0)}{\int\limits_{\tiny 0<f_{\text{nbh}}<1}\mathcal{L}^{\text{pop}}(\vec{d}|f_{\text{nbh}})\,\pi(f_{\text{nbh}})\,{\rm d}f_{\text{nbh}}},

(17)

where the denominator included marginalizing the likelihood in Eq. (
15
) over the relevant range of
f_{\text{nbh}}
.
Similarly, the Bayes factor between “all are BBH” and “all are non-BBH” events can be obtained as,

\mathcal{B}_{non-BBH}^{BBH}=\frac{\mathcal{L}^{\text{pop}}(\vec{d}|f_{\text{nbh}}=0)}{\mathcal{L}^{\text{pop}}(\vec{d}|f_{\text{nbh}}=1)}\,.

(18)

IV
Simulated compact binary population

To demonstrate the performance of different combining approaches, we simulate a set of compact binary populations that include only BBH signals, only non-BBH signals, and different types of mixtures that include BBH and non-BBH signals at different proportions. In the subsections below, we describe the steps we followed to construct the populations.

IV.1
Masses and spins

1.

We first choose a representative mass model from which we draw the component masses. The primary masses (
m_{1}
) follow a distribution what is referred to as
Model-C
in
Abbott et al. 2019
which is a power-law function smoothened at the lower mass end and embedded with a Gaussian peak towards the higher mass end. The secondary masses (
m_{2}
) are drawn from a smoothened power-law conditional on the primary masses such that
m_{1}\geq m_{2}
.

2.

For each binary, the magnitudes of the two component-spins are drawn according to the
Default Model
as named in
Abbott
et al. 2021d
. In this model, the spin magnitudes
a_{i}
(
i={1,2}
) are drawn from a beta distribution

p(a_{i}|\alpha_{a},\beta_{a})\propto a_{i}^{\alpha_{a}-1}(1-a_{i}^{\beta_{a}-1})\,,

(19)

where
\alpha_{a}
and
\beta_{a}
are shape parameters.
We choose
\alpha_{a}=2.75
and
\beta_{a}=6.00
to make sure that we do not have sources with
a_{i}\sim 0
, as non-spinning compact objects do not carry imprints of spin-induced multipole moments.

3.

The spin orientations are randomly drawn from a mixture of
isotropic
and
aligned-to-orbital-angular-momentum
orientations. In other words, the populations include binaries with precessing spins and binaries with non-precessing spins.

Note that there are several mass and spin models in literature
Abbott
et al. 2021d
which can explain the current GW data, and our choice here is arbitrary since they do not affect the conclusions of this study.

IV.2
Source selection based on signal-to-noise-ratio

The sources with masses and spins as described above are distributed uniformly in co-moving volume up to a redshift of 0.5
Abbott
et al. 2018a
;
Abbott
et al. 2021d
. The inclination and polarisation angles are chosen so that the binary orientations are isotropically distributed w.r.t detectors.

We construct our populations from sources that pass the following two criteria:

\displaystyle\rm{SNR}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle 10,

\displaystyle\rm{SNR_{insp}}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle 2\,\rm{SNR_{post-insp}},

(20)

where, SNR is the optimal network signal-to-noise ratio with the HLV network, assuming all of them at their designed sensitivity
Collaboration 2009
;
LIGO Scientific
Collaboration 2018a
;
Acernese and et.al. 2006
;
Acernese et al. 2015

\rm{SNR_{insp}}

\rm{SNR_{post-insp}}

This cut-off frequency is calculated, given the total mass of the binary (M) as,
f_{\rm{cut}}=0.018/M

Ajith et al. 2011
;
Husa et al. 2016
;
Hannam et al. 2014

The second criterion imposes the inspiral SNR to be at least twice the post-inspiral SNR, which makes sure that there are enough number of waveform cycles in the inspiral phase. This is because the spin-induced quadrupole moment effects predominantly affect the inspiral phase, as modeled in the current waveform models. However, with the advancements in numerical relativity simulations, future waveform models might accurately account for their evolution in the post-inspiral phase, which in turn would allow us to test
higher-mass
binaries whose SNR dominates in the post-inspiral phase.

VI
Understanding the systematic biases

The bias in the
f_{\text{nbh}}
posterior towards the BBH value (
f_{\text{nbh}}=0
) as discussed above and shown in Fig.
3
could be a sum of various effects in play. We discuss these effects in the present section.

VI.1
Biased single-event inferences

One of the possible reasons for the systematic bias in the inference of
f_{\text{nbh}}
is the choices of spins and SNRs of the individual events in the populations. It has been shown that the single-event
\delta\kappa_{s}
inferences are better when the spins are higher, and of course, improves further with higher SNR
Krishnendu and Yelikar 2019
while our populations include many events with low SNRs and low spins.

To investigate this, we collect events from our simulations that have effective spins
|\chi_{\rm{eff}}|\geq 0.15
and the network
\rm{SNR_{insp}}\geq 20

4
4

4

\chi_{\rm{eff}}
is the effective inspiral spin parameter captures the spin effects of non-precessing binary system which is defined in terms of component masses
m_{i}
and dimensionless spins
\chi_{i}=\vec{S_{i}}\cdot\hat{L}/m_{i}^{2}
as,
\chi_{\rm{eff}}=\frac{m_{1}\chi_{1}+m_{2}\chi_{2}}{m_{1}+m_{2}}

Ajith et al. 2011
.
. Out of all the BBH and non-BBH simulations that span over six populations, we obtain 16 BBH and 8 non-BBH events satisfying the above criterion. We construct two populations out of them: (1) All the 8 non-BBH events together, with a true value of
f_{\text{nbh}}=1
, and (2) all the 16 BBH and 8 non-BBH events together, with
f_{\text{nbh}}=0.33
.

We estimate
f_{\text{nbh}}
for these two populations using the mixture-likelihood approach and find that the posteriors are consistent with the true values at very high confidences, as illustrated on the left panel of Fig.
6
. The fact that we only had 8 and 24 events for these two populations has been reflective in the respective statistical uncertainties in Fig.
6
(left panel). This finding indicates that the bias in
f_{\text{nbh}}
primarily arises because our population contains many low-spin-low-SNR events.

VI.2
Prior effects

Throughout the Bayesian inference, we have assumed the prior on
\delta\kappa_{s}
as uniform in [-500,500]. The injected populations, on the other hand, have
\delta\kappa_{s}
drawn from narrower ranges with non-uniform distributions. Prior ranges that are wider than required will be penalized by Ocaam’s factor. Below, we show that our prior choices can partly account for the bias in
f_{\text{nbh}}
estimates. The right panel of Fig.
6
shows how the
f_{\text{nbh}}
posterior would change if we were to perform the analysis with the same prior as that of the injected
\delta\kappa_{s}
distribution. In order to achieve this, we did a prior re-weighting as described in Appendix
A
. We have taken an example from our populations with
f_{\text{nbh}}=1
and find that the posterior on
f_{\text{nbh}}
after prior re-weighting (dashed curves) is closer to the true values.

Of course, in reality, we can not pre-acquire the knowledge of the underlying distribution. However, we can always assume a hyper-parametrized prior model characterizing the underlying distribution and marginalize the likelihood over the hyperparameters to obtain the posteriors on
f_{\text{nbh}}
. In Appendix
A
, we have derived the formalism of performing this hyperparameter-marginalization though its detailed demonstration is differed for a future work.

VI.3
Non-identical nature of the binary components

Another possible, though minor, reason is our assumption of
\delta\kappa_{a}=0
(i.e.,
\delta\kappa_{1}=\delta\kappa_{2}
) in the analysis while our injections did not assume this. In other words, we have injected different values for
\delta\kappa_{1}
and
\delta\kappa_{2}
while in the Bayesian sampling, we assumed them to have equal values. This can lead to a bias in the estimated
\delta\kappa_{s}
and the Bayes factor. Indeed, this is a prospect to explore in detail in a future study. Regardless of the
\delta\kappa_{a}=0
assumption, the posterior could capture the true value of
f_{\text{nbh}}
, if the SNR and spins are high enough, as shown in the left panel of Fig.
6
.

At higher detector sensitivity and with better waveform models such as those with higher modes
Shaik et al. 2020
;
Varma and Ajith 2017
;
Calderón Bustillo
et al. 2016
;
Capano et al. 2014
;
Littenberg et al. 2013
;
Kalaghatgi et al. 2020
, some further correlations between binary parameters are expected to break, leading to improved estimates of
\kappa
parameters and improved Bayes factors, for the individual events. This will eventually improve the measurement of
f_{\text{nbh}}
.

VII
Conclusion and further remarks

We have discussed how to effectively
combine
gravitational-wave data from multiple detections to probe sub-populations that include non-BH compact binaries. Our method is based on using spin-induced quadrupole moment as a physical parameter that distinguishes BBH from non-BBHs. We first analyzed the efficacy of the previously-employed hierarchical combining approach that relies on a Gaussian assumption for the population distribution of the spin-induced quadrupole moment parameter. Next, we introduced a mixture-likelihood approach (Sec.
III.3
) that estimates the fraction (
f_{\text{nbh}}
) of non-BBH signals present in the observed population.

We simulated various populations that included BBH and non-BBH signals at different proportions. Our results show that both approaches are good for homogeneous populations like a BBH-only population. Also, both the approaches would signal if the population has complex nature with subpopulations being present. The mixture-likelihood approach is a natural choice to capture such complex distributions of non-BBH sub-populations. We applied the method on the LIGO-Virgo detected GW events from the GWTC-2 catalog and found them consistent with a BBH population.

Though the mixture-likelihood approach effectively rules out the BBH population hypothesis for the simulated populations that included some fraction of non-BBH signals, we notice that the method suffers from systematics in measuring the fraction of non-BBH signals precisely. We investigated possible reasons for these biases in Sec.
VI
.

In the future, with more realistic astrophysical population models, our estimates may improve. This is anticipated primarily because the spin-induced quadrupole moment estimates from individual events and, hence, the population can change according to the intrinsic mass-spin distributions. Also, with more accurate waveform models being available, our measurements could further improve as they could help break some of the degeneracies that lead to the systematics in this study. We would also include the effect of selection bias in the inference of
f_{\text{nbh}}
which would allow re-defining the
fraction

f_{\text{nbh}}
as the fraction of non-BBH signals in the universe, rather than the fraction in the
observed
signals, in the future.

Finally, though we have demonstrated the mixture-likelihood approach using the measurement of spin-induced quadrupole moment parameters, the method is generic enough to include in or to be applied for the other physical parametrizations that distinguish BBH from non-BBH signals (as mentioned in Sec.
I
).

Appendix A
Marginalizing the mixture likelihood over the hyper parameters of
\delta\kappa_{s}
distribution

In Eq. (
16
), we have considered a uniform prior on
\delta\kappa_{s}
in estimating the evidence
\mathcal{Z}^{nbh}
. In a more generic treatment, we should hyper-parametrize this prior and marginalize over, as the underlying
\delta\kappa_{s}
distribution in the universe is unknown (See Ref.
Smith et al. 2020
for a comprehensive treatment of marginalizing over the prior hyper-parameters, though in a different context). Thus, we first rewrite Eq. (
16
) with a generic prior on
\delta\kappa_{s}
, labelled as
\Lambda
. That means, Eq. (
15
) takes the form,

\mathcal{L}^{\text{pop}}(\vec{d}|f_{\text{nbh}},\Lambda)=\prod_{i=1}^{N}\left[(1-f_{\text{nbh}})\,\mathcal{Z}_{i}^{bh}+f_{\text{nbh}}\,\,\mathcal{Z}_{i}^{nbh}(\Lambda)\right],

(21)

where
\mathcal{Z}_{i}^{nbh}(\Lambda)
is the generalized version of Eq. (
3
),

\mathcal{Z}^{nbh}(\Lambda)=\int\pi(\vec{\theta},\delta\kappa_{s}|\Lambda)\,\mathcal{L}(d|\vec{\theta},\delta\kappa_{s})\,d\vec{\theta}\,d\delta\kappa_{s}.

(22)

The only difference being that the prior on
\delta\kappa_{s}
is now informed by the hyper parameters
\Lambda
. Also, this hyper parameter space
\Lambda
could in general be a complex multi-dimensional parameter space. Perhaps, the simplest case one could consider is a zero-centered top-hat function, with only one parameter, namely
\lambda
. This would be a prior pretty much like the one we have used in our analysis,
\pi(\delta\kappa_{s}|\lambda)\sim U(-\lambda,\lambda)
. Alternatively, if one assumes that all the non-BBH signals are from a certain class of exotic compact objects and have their
\delta\kappa_{s}
distribution coming from a localized distribution, then a Gaussian with unknown mean and width would be a good representation,
i.e.
,

\Lambda=\{\mu,\sigma^{2}\}

Given a hyper-parameter model, the corresponding evidence
\mathcal{Z}^{nbh}(\Lambda)
at any given point in the
\Lambda
-space can be evaluated by re-weighting the prior, as discussed in Appendix
A.1
).

Once we have the tools to evaluate the likelihood, we can sample from the likelihood using any sampler, over the parameters,
\{f_{\text{nbh}},\Lambda\}
. This would give us the posterior probability density function as,

p(f_{\text{nbh}},\Lambda|{d_{\rm j}})=\int\frac{\mathcal{L}^{pop}(d_{\rm j}|f_{\text{nbh}},\Lambda)\,\pi(f_{\text{nbh}})\,\pi(\Lambda)}{\mathcal{Z}^{pop}_{\Lambda}},

(23)

with

\mathcal{Z}^{pop}_{\Lambda}=\int\mathcal{L}^{pop}(d_{\rm j}|f_{\text{nbh}},\Lambda)\,\pi(f_{\text{nbh}})\,\pi(\Lambda)\,df_{\text{nbh}}\,d\Lambda.

(24)

Now we can obtain the posterior on
f_{\text{nbh}}
marginalized over
\Lambda
as,

p(f_{\text{nbh}}|d_{\rm j})=\int p(f_{\text{nbh}},\Lambda|d_{\rm j})d\Lambda\,.

(25)

Employing a nested sampling algorithm for the sampling, from the likelihood given in Eq. (
22
) the output will by default provide the probability of fraction defined in Eq. (
23
). The priors on
f_{\text{nbh}}
and
\Lambda
can be taken as uniform. Given the posterior distribution on
\delta\kappa_{s}
, we can also reconstruct the population distribution of
\delta\kappa_{s}
as,

p(\delta\kappa_{s}|d_{\rm j})=\int p(\delta\kappa_{s}|\Lambda)\,p(\Lambda|d_{\rm j})\,d\Lambda\,.

(26)

Equation (
26
) is identical to Eq. (
10
) with the only visible difference being the hyper parameter vector
\vec{\alpha}
replaced by
\Lambda
. The different notions of hyper parameters in Eq. (
26
) and Eq. (
10
) are purposeful, while
\vec{\alpha}
represents the distribution of
\delta\kappa_{s}
from the entire population,
\Lambda
by definition represents the distribution of
f_{\text{nbh}}
, the fraction of non-BBHs in the population. The remaining
(1-f_{\text{nbh}})
fraction of sources have a delta function prior, given by
\delta(\delta\kappa_{s}-0)
.

A.1
Evidence estimation by prior re-weighting

Suppose we have the evidence estimated already assuming one prior, in our case a prior on
\delta\kappa_{s}
assuming U[-500, 500]. Let us call this prior as
\Phi
. Now the evidence for this prior
\mathcal{Z}^{nbh}(\Phi)
, according to the Bayes theorem, can be written as,

\mathcal{Z}^{nbh}(\Phi)\,p(\vec{\theta},\delta\kappa_{s}\,|d_{\rm j},\,\Phi)=\pi(\vec{\theta},\delta\kappa_{s}\,|\Phi)\,\mathcal{L}(d_{\rm j}\,|\,\vec{\theta},\delta\kappa_{s}),

(27)

With the prior
\Lambda
, the new evidence will be as given by Eq. (
22
). Plugging Eq. (
27
) to Eq. (
22
), we get

\mathcal{Z}^{nbh}(\Lambda)=\mathcal{Z}^{nbh}(\Phi)\int\frac{\pi(\vec{\theta},\delta\kappa_{s}|\Lambda)}{\pi(\vec{\theta},\delta\kappa_{s}\,|\Phi)}\,p(\vec{\theta},\delta\kappa_{s}\,|d_{\rm j},\,\Phi)d\vec{\theta}\,d\delta\kappa_{s}\,,

(28)

where the probability distribution
p(\vec{\theta},\delta\kappa_{s}\,|d_{\rm j},\,\Phi)
is known and the above integral can be approximated as a Monte-Carlo average over the posterior samples,
i.e.
,

\mathcal{Z}^{nbh}(\Lambda)=\mathcal{Z}^{nbh}(\Phi)\,\times\left[\frac{1}{n}\,\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{\pi(\vec{\theta},\delta\kappa_{s}|\Lambda)}{\pi(\vec{\theta},\delta\kappa_{s}|\Phi)}\right],

(29)

where
n
is the number of samples in the
\delta\kappa_{s}
posterior distribution. Both
\pi(\vec{\theta},\delta\kappa_{s}|\Lambda)
and
\pi(\vec{\theta},\delta\kappa_{s}|\Phi)
must be normalized distributions.
Throughout the analysis, we assume that the priors on
\delta\kappa_{s}
and
\vec{\theta}
are uncorrelated and hence we can decouple

\pi(\vec{\theta})
from both numerator and denominator
so that they cancel each other. This leaves,

\mathcal{Z}^{nbh}(\Lambda)=\mathcal{Z}^{nbh}(\Phi)\,\times\left[\frac{1}{n}\,\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{\pi(\delta\kappa_{s}|\Lambda)}{\pi(\delta\kappa_{s}|\Phi)}\right],

(30)

Equation (
30
) makes it easier to compute the likelihood in Eq. (
21
) for any instance of
\Lambda
. The reader may refer to Ref.
Thrane and Talbot 2019
for a detailed treatment of the prior-re-weighting procedure.
