Linear isometries of Hol ( 𝔻 ) \mathop{\rm Hol}\nolimits(\mathbb{D})
I. Chalendar, L. Oger, J. R. Partington

Abstract
. A complete characterisation is given of all the linear isometries of the Fréchet space of all holomorphic functions on the unit disc, when it is given one of the two standard metrics: these turn out to be weighted composition operators of a particular form. Operators similar to an isometry are also classified. Further, the larger class of operators isometric when restricted to one of the defining seminorms is identified. Finally, the spectra of such operators are studied.



2020 Mathematics Subject Classification
47B33, 30H05, 47A10

1.
Introduction

Let
X
be a topological space of holomorphic functions on the open unit disc
\mathbb{D}
. For
m
a holomorphic function on
\mathbb{D}
and
\varphi
a holomorphic self-map of
\mathbb{D}
, then the linear operator defined by

W_{m,\varphi}(f)=m(f\circ\varphi),\quad f\in X,

is called the
weighted composition operator
with symbols
m
and
\varphi
.

Observe that
W_{m,\varphi}(f)=M_{m}C_{\varphi}(f)
, where
M_{m}(f)=mf
is the
multiplication operator
with symbol
m
and
C_{\varphi}(f)=f\circ\varphi
is the
composition operator
with symbol
\varphi
. If
m
is identically
1
, then
W_{m,\varphi}=C_{\varphi}
, and if
\varphi
is the identity, then
W_{m,\varphi}=M_{m}
. Weighted composition operators are fundamental in the study of Banach and Hilbert spaces that embed continuously in
\mathop{\rm Hol}\nolimits(\mathbb{D})
, the algebra of all holomorphic functions on
\mathbb{D}
.
Indeed, the study of the geometry of a space
X
is centered on the identification of the linear isometries on
X
, and there is an obvious connection between weighted composition operators and isometries.

This connection can be traced back to Banach himself. In
[
5
]
, Banach proved that the surjective isometries on
{\mathcal{C}}(K)
, the space of continuous real-valued functions on a compact metric space
K
, are of the form
T:f\mapsto m(f\circ\varphi)
, with
\left\lvert m\right\rvert\equiv 1
and
\varphi
a homeomorphism of
K
onto itself.

Although the characterisation of isometries is an open problem for most Banach spaces of holomorphic functions, there are many spaces for which the isometries are known.

On the Hardy space
H^{p}
of the open unit disc
\mathbb{D}
,
p\neq 2
, Forelli has shown that the isometries are certain weighted composition operators (
[
12
]
).

On the Hilbertian Hardy space
H^{2}
on
\mathbb{D}
, there are too many isometries to have a complete characterisation. However, in
[
7
, Prop. 4.2]
, Chalendar and Partington showed that provided that the symbol
\varphi
is inner, there exist weighted composition
operators

W_{m,\varphi}
that are isometries.
Recall that on
H^{2}
,
C_{\varphi}
is isometric if and only if
\varphi
is inner and
\varphi(0)=0
(see
[
21
]
), whereas
W_{m,\varphi}
is isometric implies that
\varphi
is inner but no extra condition on
\varphi
is required.

On the Bergman space
A^{p}
of
\mathbb{D}
,
p\neq 2
, Kolaski (
[
16
]
) showed that the surjective isometries are weighted composition operators. Zorboska (
[
22
]
) initiated the study of isometric weighted composition operators on
A^{2}
of
\mathbb{D}
. Those classes of operators were also considered in
[
7
]
on weighted Bergman spaces.

On the disc algebra
A(\mathbb{D})
, El-Gebeily and Wolfe (
[
11
]
) showed that the isometries are of two types: either they are weighted composition operators (« Type 1 » isometries), or we have to add an extension operator to these « Type 1 » isometries.

On the Bloch space, the first study of the isometries was made by Cima and Wogen in
[
8
]
. They analyzed the isometries on the subspace of the Bloch space of the open unit disc whose elements fix the origin. On this space, they showed that the surjective isometries are normalized compressions of weighted composition operators induced by disc automorphisms, whereas Colonna (
[
9
]
) gave a characterisation of the isometric composition operators on the Bloch space.

We will not give an exhaustive list of contributions in this area but we would like to emphasize the fact that the weighted composition operators play a central role in the study of the isometries on several spaces of holomorphic functions on
\mathbb{D}
.

The goal of this paper is to characterise the linear isometries on the set
\mathop{\rm Hol}\nolimits(\mathbb{D})
of all holomorphic functions on the open unit disc
\mathbb{D}
. This is a Fréchet space, endowed with the family of seminorms defined by

\left\|f\right\|_{\infty,1-1/p}:=\sup_{\left\lvert z\right\rvert\leq 1-1/p}\left\lvert f(z)\right\rvert,\qquad f\in\mathop{\rm Hol}\nolimits(\mathbb{D}),\qquad p\in\mathbb{N}.

Other sequences are possible, and the results we prove will apply to these too.

These seminorms are associated with the topology of uniform convergence on all compact subsets of
\mathbb{D}
. An isometry of
\mathop{\rm Hol}\nolimits(\mathbb{D})
is simply an isometry for all the seminorms
\left\|\cdot\right\|_{\infty,1-1/p}
, thanks to the following lemma.

Lemma 1.1
.

Let
X
be a Fréchet space, and
(\left\|\cdot\right\|_{k})_{k\geq 1}
an associated increasing family of semi-norms.
A linear operator
T:X\to X
is isometric, considering the distance
d_{X}
defined by

d_{X}(x,y)=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}2^{-k}\min(1,\left\|x-y\right\|_{k}),

if and only if for all
x\in X
and
k\in\mathbb{N}
,
\left\|Tx\right\|_{k}=\left\|x\right\|_{k}
.

Proof.

Assume that for all
x\in X
and
k\in\mathbb{N}
,
\left\|Tx\right\|_{k}=\left\|x\right\|_{k}
. Then, for all
x,y\in X
,

\displaystyle d_{X}(Tx,Ty)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{2^{k}}\min(1,\left\|Tx-Ty\right\|_{k})

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{2^{k}}\min(1,\left\|T(x-y)\right\|_{k})=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{2^{k}}\min(1,\left\|x-y\right\|_{k})=d_{X}(x,y).

Hence, the operator
T
is isometric on
X
for the distance
d_{X}
.

Conversely, if there exist
x\in X
and
p\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\left\|Tx\right\|_{p}\neq\left\|x\right\|_{p}
, consider the smallest such
p
(that is
\left\|Tx\right\|_{q}=\left\|x\right\|_{q}
for all
q<p
). Assume that
\left\|Tx\right\|_{p}<\left\|x\right\|_{p}
, and take
y=x/\left\|x\right\|_{p}
.
Recall that for
q<p<r
,

\left\|y\right\|_{q}\leq\left\|y\right\|_{p}=1\leq\left\|y\right\|_{r}.

Then, since
\left\|y\right\|_{p}>\left\|Ty\right\|_{p}
,

\displaystyle d_{X}(y,0)=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{2^{k}}\min(1,\left\|y\right\|_{k})

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{p-1}\frac{1}{2^{k}}\left\|y\right\|_{k}+\frac{1}{2^{p}}\left\|y\right\|_{p}+\sum_{k=p+1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{2^{k}}

\displaystyle>\sum_{k=1}^{p-1}\frac{1}{2^{k}}\left\|Ty\right\|_{k}+\frac{1}{2^{p}}\left\|Ty\right\|_{p}+\sum_{k=p+1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{2^{k}}\min(1,\left\|Ty\right\|_{k})

\displaystyle=d_{X}(Ty,0).

Finally, the operator
T
is not isometric on
X
, for the distance
d_{X}
.

If we have the reverse inequality
\left\|Tx\right\|_{p}>\left\|x\right\|_{p}
, then by taking
z=x/\left\|Tx\right\|_{p}
, since
\left\|z\right\|_{p}<\left\|Tz\right\|_{p}=1
, the same calculations give

\displaystyle d_{X}(Tz,0)=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{2^{k}}\min(1,\left\|Tz\right\|_{k})

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{p-1}\frac{1}{2^{k}}\left\|Tz\right\|_{k}+\frac{1}{2^{p}}\left\|Tz\right\|_{p}+\sum_{k=p+1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{2^{k}}

\displaystyle>\sum_{k=1}^{p-1}\frac{1}{2^{k}}\left\|z\right\|_{k}+\frac{1}{2^{p}}\left\|z\right\|_{p}+\sum_{k=p+1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{2^{k}}\min(1,\left\|z\right\|_{k})

\displaystyle=d_{X}(z,0),

so
T
is also not isometric on
X
for the classical distance.
∎

A similar result holds for another commonly-used metric on
\mathop{\rm Hol}\nolimits(\mathbb{D})
.

Lemma 1.2
.

Let
X
be a Fréchet space, and
(\left\|\cdot\right\|_{k})_{k\geq 1}
the associated increasing family of semi-norms.
A linear operator
T:X\to X
is isometric, considering the distance
d^{\prime}_{X}
defined by

d^{\prime}_{X}(x,y)=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}2^{-k}\frac{\left\|x-y\right\|_{k}}{1+\left\|x-y\right\|_{k}},

if and only if for all
x\in X
and
k\in\mathbb{N}
,
\left\|Tx\right\|_{k}=\left\|x\right\|_{k}
.

Proof.

The « if » implication is clear, and we now prove the « only if » implication.

Let
a_{k}=\|x\|_{k}
and
b_{k}=\|Tx\|_{k}
.
These are increasing sequences and we can clearly assume that
not all the
a_{k}
are
0
and not all the
b_{k}
are 0,
as otherwise
x=0
. So let us suppose
that
a_{k}>0
for
k\geq k_{1}
and similarly
b_{k}>0
for
k\geq k_{2}
(so that
a_{k}=0

if
k<k_{1}
and
b_{k}=0
if
k<k_{2}
).

We have that for each
\lambda>0

\sum_{k=k_{1}}^{\infty}\frac{1}{2^{k}}\frac{\lambda a_{k}}{1+\lambda a_{k}}=\sum_{k=k_{2}}^{\infty}\frac{1}{2^{k}}\frac{\lambda b_{k}}{1+\lambda b_{k}}.

Divide by
\lambda
, set
\lambda=1+s
and write
\alpha_{k}=1/a_{k}
and
\beta_{k}=1/b_{k}
. We have

\sum_{k=k_{1}}^{\infty}\frac{1}{2^{k}}\frac{1}{\alpha_{k}+1+s}=\sum_{k=k_{2}}^{\infty}\frac{1}{2^{k}}\frac{1}{\beta_{k}+1+s},

initially for
s>0
but, because these series define
H^{1}(\mathbb{C}_{+})\cap H^{2}(\mathbb{C}_{+})
functions on the right half-plane
\mathbb{C}_{+}
, they define the same function. These are sums of reproducing kernels (see, for example,
[
19
, Sec. 1.2]
), and we may take an inner product with the function
e^{-ws}\in H^{\infty}(\mathbb{C}_{+})
for
w>0
to get

\sum_{k=k_{1}}^{\infty}\frac{1}{2^{k}}\exp(-w(\alpha_{k}+1))=\sum_{k=k_{2}}^{\infty}\frac{1}{2^{k}}\exp(-w(\beta_{k}+1)).

Again, these are equal as
H^{\infty}(\mathbb{C}_{+})
functions of
w
, so
on
w=iy
for
y\in\mathbb{R}
they are equal almost everywhere, and they are both almost-periodic functions of
y
, so that
\alpha_{k}=\beta_{k}
for all
k
(this standard fact can be found in many places: for example,
[
19
, Sec. 5.1]
). This completes the proof.
∎

The paper is organised as follows. First, in Section
2
, we characterise the linear isometries of
\mathop{\rm Hol}\nolimits(\mathbb{D})
, using only two different seminorms. We prove (Theorem
2.1
) that they are trivial weighted composition operators in the following sense: they are defined by

T_{\alpha,\beta}(f)(z)=\alpha f(\beta z),\qquad f\in\mathop{\rm Hol}\nolimits(\mathbb{D}),\quad z\in\mathbb{D},\quad\left\lvert\alpha\right\rvert=\left\lvert\beta\right\rvert=1.

We will mainly need the two following results. The first one comes from
[
2
, Proposition 2.1]
and gives a necessary and sufficient condition to be a composition operator on
\mathop{\rm Hol}\nolimits(\mathbb{D})
. The second one is a new characterisation of finite Blaschke products.

Lemma 1.3
.

Let
T:\mathop{\rm Hol}\nolimits(\mathbb{D})\to\mathop{\rm Hol}\nolimits(\mathbb{D})
be linear and continuous. Then
T
is a composition
operator if and only if for all
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
,
Te_{n}=(Te_{1})^{n}
, with
e_{n}(z)=z^{n}
.

The following is a stronger form of a result due to Kamowitz
[
15
, Lem. 1.3]
.
Note that the proof below gives more, in that the weaker assumption that

g
is holomorphic in an annulus
A:=\{z\in\mathbb{C}:1-\varepsilon<|z|<1+\varepsilon\}

with
g(A\cap\overline{\mathbb{D}})\subset\overline{\mathbb{D}}
is sufficient
for the conclusion on
X
.

Theorem 1.4
.

Let
\varepsilon>0
and
g\in\mathop{\rm Hol}\nolimits((1+\varepsilon)\mathbb{D})
such that
g(\overline{\mathbb{D}})\subset\overline{\mathbb{D}}
.

Then
X:=\{\xi\in\mathbb{T}:\left\lvert g(\xi)\right\rvert=1\}
is either finite, or is equal to
\mathbb{T}
. Moreover, if
X=\mathbb{T}
, then
g
is a finite Blaschke product.

Proof.

Note that
g(\mathbb{D})\subset\mathbb{D}
. Indeed, by the open mapping theorem, if there exists
z_{0}\in\mathbb{D}
such that
\left\lvert g(z_{0})\right\rvert=1
, then there exists some
z_{1}\in\mathbb{D}
close to
z_{0}
such that
\left\lvert g(z_{1})\right\rvert>1
, which is impossible.

Consider now
g
as a continuous map from
\mathbb{T}
to
\overline{\mathbb{D}}
. Then
X=g^{-1}(\mathbb{T})
is a closed subset of
\mathbb{T}
(since
g(\mathbb{D})\subset\mathbb{D}
).

Assume that
X
is not finite.
Then, the set
\mathbb{T}\backslash X
is an open subset of
\mathbb{T}
, that is a union of an infinite number of open intervals of
\mathbb{T}
.
We look at the edges of these intervals, which lie in
X
.
If none of these edges is a limit of points of
X
, then the set
X
only consists of isolated points (the edges). But since
X
is infinite, by the Bolzano–Weierstrass theorem,
X
must contain a limit point, contradicting the assumption.
Hence, one of the edges is indeed a limit point of
X
, denoted in the following as
z_{0}
.

This point satisfies
\left\lvert g(z_{0})\right\rvert=1
, and there exist two sequences
(u_{n})\subset X
and
(v_{n})\subset\mathbb{T}\backslash X
(it is sufficient to take points from the chosen interval) which tend to
z_{0}
, such that
\left\lvert g(u_{n})\right\rvert=1
and
\left\lvert g(v_{n})\right\rvert<1
.

We set
\phi_{1}
,
\phi_{2}
two conformal maps from
\mathbb{T}
to
\mathbb{R}\cup\{\infty\}
defined by

\phi_{1}(z)=i\frac{z-z_{0}}{z+z_{0}},\qquad\phi_{2}(z)=i\frac{z-g(z_{0})}{z+g(z_{0})}.

They satisfy
\phi_{1}(z_{0})=0
and
\phi_{2}(g(z_{0}))=0
. Denote
h=\phi_{2}\circ g\circ\phi_{1}^{-1}
.

Since
\phi_{1}^{-1}
is holomorphic near
0
, as is
\phi_{2}
near
g(z_{0})
, the function
h
is holomorphic around
0
, satisfies
h(0)=0
and there exist two sequences
(a_{n}),(b_{n})\subset\mathbb{R}
(corresponding to
(u_{n})
and
(v_{n})\subset\mathbb{T}
) such that
a_{n}\to 0
,
b_{n}\to 0
,
h(a_{n})\in\mathbb{R}
and
h(b_{n})\not\in\mathbb{R}
. However,

h^{\prime}(0)=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{h(a_{n})}{a_{n}}\in\mathbb{R}.

The map
h_{1}:z\mapsto h(z)/z-h^{\prime}(0)
is also holomorphic around
0
, and satisfies
h_{1}(a_{n})\in\mathbb{R}
. Then, in a same way, we get
h_{1}^{\prime}(0)=h^{\prime\prime}(0)\in\mathbb{R}
.
Iterating this reasoning, all the derivatives of
h
at
0
must be real, so
h(b_{n})\in\mathbb{R}
(since
b_{n}\in\mathbb{R}
). Impossible.

Finally, we must have
\mathbb{T}\backslash X=\varnothing
, i.e.,
X=\mathbb{T}
, that is
g(\mathbb{T})\subset\mathbb{T}
. Hence,
g
is a finite Blaschke product, using
[
14
,
18
]
.
∎

Next, in Section
3
, we focus on the composition, multiplication, and weighted composition operators that are similar to a linear isometry of
\mathop{\rm Hol}\nolimits(\mathbb{D})
. We obtain a complete description of those operators (Theorem
3.2
).

In Section
4
, we give a characterisation of the linear isometries for a single seminorm
\left\|\cdot\right\|_{\infty,1-1/p}
(Theorem
4.2
). There are more than for two seminorms, as expected, and the results from the disc algebra, by El-Gebeily and Wolfe (
[
11
]
), will be in the spotlight in this section.
We also need the following lemma.

Lemma 1.5
.

Let
\varepsilon>0
, and
f\in\mathop{\rm Hol}\nolimits((1+\varepsilon)\mathbb{D})
such that
f(\overline{\mathbb{D}})=\overline{\mathbb{D}}
and
f((1+\varepsilon)\mathbb{D})\subset(1+\varepsilon)\mathbb{D}
. Then,
f
is a rotation.

Proof.

Using Theorem
1.4
, we know that
f
is a finite Blaschke product. Moreover, the zeroes of
f
are in
(1+\varepsilon)^{-1}\mathbb{D}
. We consider three cases.

(i)

If
f(z)=e^{i\theta}z^{n}
, for some integer
n\geq 2
, then for
z_{0}=1+a\varepsilon
,

\left\lvert f(z_{0})\right\rvert=\left(1+a\varepsilon\right)^{n}>1+\varepsilon,

for
a\in(0,1)
sufficiently close to 1. Hence,
f((1+\varepsilon)\mathbb{D})\not\subset(1+\varepsilon)\mathbb{D}
.

(ii)

Assume that
f(z)=e^{i\theta}\frac{z-\alpha}{1-\overline{\alpha}z}
, for some
0<\left\lvert\alpha\right\rvert<\frac{1}{1+\varepsilon}
. Since
f
is continuous, we have

f((1+\varepsilon)\mathbb{D})\subset(1+\varepsilon)\mathbb{D}\;\Longrightarrow\;f((1+\varepsilon)\overline{\mathbb{D}})\subset(1+\varepsilon)\overline{\mathbb{D}}.

Hence, for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
,

\left\lvert\frac{(1+\varepsilon)e^{ix}-\alpha}{1-(1+\varepsilon)\overline{\alpha}e^{ix}}\right\rvert\leq 1+\varepsilon\iff\left\lvert\frac{1-\frac{\alpha e^{-ix}}{1+\varepsilon}}{1-(1+\varepsilon)\overline{\alpha}e^{ix}}\right\rvert\leq 1.

In particular, for
x
such that
\alpha e^{-ix}=\left\lvert\alpha\right\rvert
, we obtain

(1)

1-\frac{\left\lvert\alpha\right\rvert}{1+\varepsilon}\leq 1-(1+\varepsilon)\left\lvert\alpha\right\rvert\iff\frac{1}{1+\varepsilon}\geq 1+\varepsilon.

This last inequality is impossible.

(iii)

General case
: Let
f
be a finite Blaschke product which is not a rotation. Assume that
f
has at least two zeroes (counting the multiplicities), and that
0
is not the only zero of
f
. Hence, we may write

f(z)=e^{i\theta}z^{n}\frac{z-\alpha}{1-\overline{\alpha}z}\times\prod_{i=1}^{k}\frac{z-\alpha_{i}}{1-\overline{\alpha}_{i}z},

with
0<\left\lvert\alpha\right\rvert,\left\lvert\alpha_{i}\right\rvert<\frac{1}{1+\varepsilon}
. Once again, since
f
is continuous, we must have

f((1+\varepsilon)\overline{\mathbb{D}})\subset(1+\varepsilon)\overline{\mathbb{D}}.

Take
z_{0}=(1+\varepsilon)e^{i\arg(\alpha)}
. Then, since the inequalities (
1
) are impossible,

\left\lvert\frac{z_{0}-\alpha}{1-\overline{\alpha}z_{0}}\right\rvert=\left\lvert\frac{(1+\varepsilon)-\left\lvert\alpha\right\rvert}{1-(1+\varepsilon)\left\lvert\alpha\right\rvert}\right\rvert=(1+\varepsilon)\left\lvert\frac{1-\frac{\left\lvert\alpha\right\rvert}{1+\varepsilon}}{1-(1+\varepsilon)\left\lvert\alpha\right\rvert}\right\rvert>1+\varepsilon.

Moreover,
\left\lvert e^{i\theta}z_{0}^{n}\right\rvert>1
. Finally, since
\left\lvert z_{0}\right\rvert>1
and for all
i\in\{1,\cdots,k\}
,
\left\lvert\alpha_{i}\right\rvert<1
, we get

\left\lvert\prod_{i=1}^{k}\frac{z_{0}-\alpha_{i}}{1-\overline{\alpha}_{i}z_{0}}\right\rvert>1.

Thus,
\left\lvert f(z_{0})\right\rvert>1+\varepsilon
, a contradiction.

To conclude, the function
f
must be a rotation.
∎

Finally, in Section 5, we compute, using the results of
[
4
]
, the spectra of the operators found in the previous sections. The common property of these weighted composition operators is that the symbol is a rotation. Hence, we have to consider separately the operators with constant weights and those whose weights are finite Blaschke products.

2.
Isometries of
\mathop{\rm Hol}\nolimits(\mathbb{D})

We begin by characterising all the isometric operators of
\mathop{\rm Hol}\nolimits(\mathbb{D})
. To do this, we first focus on the operators that are isometric for
two
different seminorms
\left\|\cdot\right\|_{\infty,p}
.

Theorem 2.1

(Main theorem)
.

Let
0<r_{1}<r_{2}<1
be fixed, and
T:\mathop{\rm Hol}\nolimits(\mathbb{D})\to\mathop{\rm Hol}\nolimits(\mathbb{D})
a linear and continuous operator such that for all
f\in\mathop{\rm Hol}\nolimits(\mathbb{D})
and
i\in\{1,2\}
,

\left\|T(f)\right\|_{\infty,r_{i}}=\left\|f\right\|_{\infty,r_{i}}=\sup_{\left\lvert z\right\rvert\leq r_{i}}\left\lvert f(z)\right\rvert.

Then there exist two constants
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{T}
such that for all
f\in\mathop{\rm Hol}\nolimits(\mathbb{D})
,

T(f)(z)=\alpha f(\beta z)=:T_{\alpha,\beta}(f)(z).

Proof.

We are going to use Lemma
1.3
. Recall that for all
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
,
e_{n}(z)=z^{n}
.

Step 1
: We begin by showing that
Te_{0}=\alpha e_{0}
, with
\left\lvert\alpha\right\rvert=1
.
Indeed, since
T
is isometric for
\left\|\cdot\right\|_{\infty,r_{1}}
and
\left\|\cdot\right\|_{\infty,r_{2}}
, we obtain

\left\|Te_{0}\right\|_{\infty,r_{1}}=\left\|Te_{0}\right\|_{\infty,r_{2}}=1.

Using the maximum modulus principle, the map
Te_{0}
is constant and unimodular.

In the following, consider
\tilde{T}:=\overline{\alpha}T
, so that
\tilde{T}
is still isometric for the two seminorms
\left\|\cdot\right\|_{\infty,r_{1}}
and
\left\|\cdot\right\|_{\infty,r_{2}}
, and
\tilde{T}e_{0}=e_{0}
.

Step 2
: Set
r=r_{1}
or
r_{2}
. We show that for all
n\geq 1
,

r^{n}\mathbb{T}\subset(\tilde{T}e_{n})(r\overline{\mathbb{D}})=:K.

Indeed, assume that there exists
\xi=r^{n}e^{i\theta}
such that
\xi\not\in K
, which is a compact subset of
\overline{\mathbb{D}}
. Then
\delta=d(\xi,K)>0
, and
K\subset r^{n}\overline{\mathbb{D}}\backslash D(\xi,\delta)
. We set
f=e_{0}+e^{-i\theta}e_{n}
. Hence,

\tilde{T}f=e_{0}+e^{-i\theta}\tilde{T}e_{n}.

Now, compare the seminorms: we have
\left\|f\right\|_{\infty,r}=1+r^{n}
and

\displaystyle\left\|\tilde{T}f\right\|_{\infty,r}

\displaystyle=\sup_{\left\lvert z\right\rvert\leq r}\left\lvert 1+e^{-i\theta}(\tilde{T}e_{n})(z)\right\rvert

\displaystyle=\sup_{w\in K}\left\lvert 1+e^{-i\theta}w\right\rvert\leq\sup_{\left\lvert z\right\rvert\leq r^{n},\left\lvert z-r^{n}\right\rvert\geq\delta}\left\lvert 1+z\right\rvert<1+r^{n}.

This is impossible, since
\tilde{T}
is isometric for
\left\|\cdot\right\|_{\infty,r}
. Therefore,
r^{n}\mathbb{T}\subset K
.

Moreover, because
\tilde{T}
is an isometry, we get
K\subset r^{n}\overline{\mathbb{D}}
.

In the following, we set
f_{n}=\tilde{T}e_{n}
, and for all
0<r<1
, we consider
g_{n,r}
defined by

g_{n,r}(z)=\frac{1}{r^{n}}f_{n}(rz).

Then
g_{n,r}
is holomorphic on
r^{-1}\mathbb{D}
, and satisfies
\mathbb{T}\subset g_{n,r}(\overline{\mathbb{D}})\subset\overline{\mathbb{D}}
.
Since
g_{n,r}(\mathbb{D})\subset\mathbb{D}
, if we set
X=\{\xi\in\mathbb{T}:\left\lvert g_{n,r}(\xi)\right\rvert=1\}
, then if
X
was finite, we would have
g_{n,r}(X)
also finite. Impossible because
\mathbb{T}\subset g_{n,r}(\overline{\mathbb{D}})
. Therefore,
X=\mathbb{T}
by Theorem
1.4
.
This implies that the maps
B_{1}=g_{n,r_{1}}
and
B_{2}=g_{n,r_{2}}
are finite Blaschke products. Hence,

f_{n}(z)=\begin{cases}r_{1}^{n}B_{1}(z/r_{1}),&\left\lvert z\right\rvert<r_{1},\\
r_{2}^{n}B_{2}(z/r_{2}),&\left\lvert z\right\rvert<r_{2}.\end{cases}

Step 3
: Let us write all the terms of the Blaschke products, for
\left\lvert z\right\rvert<r_{1}<r_{2}
. There exist numbers
\alpha_{1},\cdots,\alpha_{K_{1}}
and
\beta_{1},\cdots,\beta_{K_{2}}
such that
0<\left\lvert\alpha_{i}\right\rvert,\left\lvert\beta_{j}\right\rvert<1
and for
\left\lvert z\right\rvert<r_{1}
,

(2)

\displaystyle r_{1}^{n-N_{1}}e^{i\theta_{1}}z^{N_{1}}\prod_{i=1}^{K_{1}}\frac{\frac{z}{r_{1}}-\alpha_{i}}{1-\overline{\alpha}_{i}\frac{z}{r_{1}}}=r_{2}^{n-N_{2}}e^{i\theta_{2}}z^{N_{2}}\prod_{j=1}^{K_{2}}\frac{\frac{z}{r_{2}}-\beta_{j}}{1-\overline{\beta}_{j}\frac{z}{r_{2}}}

(3)

\displaystyle\iff\;

\displaystyle r_{1}^{n-N_{1}}e^{i\theta_{1}}z^{N_{1}}\prod_{i=1}^{K_{1}}\frac{z-r_{1}\alpha_{i}}{r_{1}-\overline{\alpha}_{i}z}=r_{2}^{n-N_{2}}e^{i\theta_{2}}z^{N_{2}}\prod_{j=1}^{K_{2}}\frac{z-r_{2}\beta_{j}}{r_{2}-\overline{\beta}_{j}z}

We study the poles and zeroes of the Blaschke products.
In (
3
), the zero 0 has same order on each side of the equation, so
N_{1}=N_{2}=:N
.
Moreover, the zeroes and poles of the two Blaschke products should coincide, so
K_{1}=K_{2}=:K
, and for each
i\in\{1,\cdots,K\}
, there exists

such that

\alpha_{i}r_{1}=\beta_{j}r_{2}\quad\text{and}\quad\frac{r_{1}}{\overline{\alpha}_{i}}=\frac{r_{2}}{\overline{\beta}_{j}}.

In particular, we obtain

\frac{r_{1}}{r_{2}}=\frac{\beta_{j}}{\alpha_{i}}=\frac{\overline{\alpha}_{i}}{\overline{\beta}_{j}},

so
\left\lvert\alpha_{i}\right\rvert^{2}=\left\lvert\beta_{j}\right\rvert^{2}
, and
r_{1}=r_{2}
. Impossible.
Ergo, the Blaschke products
B_{1}
and
B_{2}
are of the form

B_{1}(z)=e^{i\theta_{1}}z^{N_{1}},\qquad B_{2}(z)=e^{i\theta_{2}}z^{N_{2}}.

Step 4
: Putting the formulas in the expression of
f_{n}
, we get

f_{n}(z)=r_{1}^{n}e^{i\theta_{1}}\frac{z^{N_{1}}}{r_{1}^{N_{1}}}=r_{2}^{n}e^{i\theta_{2}}\frac{z^{N_{2}}}{r_{2}^{N_{2}}},\qquad\left\lvert z\right\rvert<r_{1}\leq r_{2}.

The order of the zero
0
is unique, so we obtain
N_{1}=N_{2}=:N
, and

r_{1}^{n-N}e^{i\theta_{1}}z^{N}=r_{2}^{n-N}e^{i\theta_{2}}z^{N}.

Identifying the modulus and argument of the coefficients, we have
r_{1}^{n-N}=r_{2}^{n-N}
, so
n=N
(since
r_{1}\neq r_{2}
), and
e^{i\theta_{1}}=e^{i\theta_{2}}=:\delta_{n}
, that is

f_{n}(z)=e^{i\theta}z^{n}=\delta_{n}e_{n}(z),\qquad\left\lvert z\right\rvert<r_{1}.

Using analytic continuation, the last equality is valid for
z\in\mathbb{D}
, so we have proved that for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
, there exists
\delta_{n}\in\mathbb{T}
such that
\tilde{T}e_{n}=\delta_{n}e_{n}
.

Step 5
: Since
\tilde{T}
is isometric, for all
r\in\{r_{1},r_{2}\}
,

\displaystyle\sup_{\left\lvert z\right\rvert=r}\left\lvert\tilde{T}(e_{k}+e_{k+1}+e_{k+2})(z)\right\rvert

\displaystyle=\sup_{\left\lvert z\right\rvert=r}\left\lvert(\delta_{k}e_{k}+\delta_{k+1}e_{k+1}+\delta_{k+2}e_{k+2})(z)\right\rvert

\displaystyle=\sup_{\left\lvert z\right\rvert=r}\left\lvert\delta_{k}z^{k}+\delta_{k+1}z^{k+1}+\delta_{k+2}z^{k+2}\right\rvert

\displaystyle=\sup_{\left\lvert z\right\rvert=r}\left\lvert z^{k}+z^{k+1}+z^{k+2}\right\rvert.

Dividing by
z^{k}
, we obtain

\sup_{\left\lvert z\right\rvert=r}\left\lvert\delta_{k}+\delta_{k+1}z+\delta_{k+2}z^{2}\right\rvert=\sup_{\left\lvert z\right\rvert=r}\left\lvert 1+z+z^{2}\right\rvert=1+r+r^{2}.

Factorising by
\delta_{k}
, since
\left\lvert\delta_{k}\right\rvert=1
, we have

\sup_{\left\lvert z\right\rvert=r}\left\lvert 1+\frac{\delta_{k+1}}{\delta_{k}}z+\frac{\delta_{k+2}}{\delta_{k}}z^{2}\right\rvert=\sup_{\left\lvert z\right\rvert=r}\left\lvert 1+z+z^{2}\right\rvert=1+r+r^{2}.

Using the triangle inequality, for all
z
satisfying
\left\lvert z\right\rvert=r
,

\left\lvert 1+\frac{\delta_{k+1}}{\delta_{k}}z+\frac{\delta_{k+2}}{\delta_{k}}z^{2}\right\rvert\leq 1+r+r^{2},

with equality if and only if

0=\arg(1)\equiv\arg\left[\frac{\delta_{k+1}}{\delta_{k}}z\right]\equiv\arg\left[\frac{\delta_{k+2}}{\delta_{k}}z^{2}\right]\mod 2\pi.

Denoting
\theta=\arg[z]
, this gives

0\equiv\arg[\delta_{k+1}]-\arg[\delta_{k}]+\theta\equiv\arg[\delta_{k+2}]-\arg[\delta_{k}]+2\theta\mod 2\pi.

Finally,

\displaystyle\arg{[\delta_{k}]}-2\arg{[\delta_{k+1}]}+\arg{[\delta_{k+2}]}

\displaystyle=\;

\displaystyle(\arg{[\delta_{k+2}]}-\arg{[\delta_{k}]}+2\theta)-2(\arg{[\delta_{k+1}]}-\arg{[\delta_{k}]}+\theta)\equiv 0\mod 2\pi.

Step 6
: We show by induction that for all
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
,

\arg[\delta_{n}]\equiv n\arg[\delta_{1}]\mod 2\pi
.

n=0
or
1
: It is immediate (see Step 1 for
n=0
).

Assume that the formula is valid for some nonnegative integers
n
and
n+1
. From Step 5,
\arg{[\delta_{n}]}-2\arg{[\delta_{n+1}]}+\arg{[\delta_{n+2}]}\equiv 0\mod 2\pi
.
Using induction hypothesis, we have

n\arg[\delta_{1}]-2(n+1)\arg[\delta_{1}]+\arg[\delta_{n+2}]\equiv 0\mod 2\pi.

After rearranging the terms, we obtain
\arg[\delta_{n+2}]\equiv(n+2)\arg[\delta_{1}]\mod 2\pi
.

Conclusion:
For all
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
,
\tilde{T}(e_{n})=\delta_{1}^{n}e_{n}
.
Hence,
\tilde{T}(e_{n})=(\tilde{T}(e_{1}))^{n}
, so
\tilde{T}
is a composition operator, with symbol
\varphi=\tilde{T}(e_{1})=\delta_{1}e_{1}
, by Lemma
1.3
.
Finally, multiplying by
\alpha
, for all
f\in\mathop{\rm Hol}\nolimits(\mathbb{D})
,

(Tf)(z)=\alpha f(\delta_{1}z).\qed

We obtain the following corollary.

Corollary 2.2
.

The only linear operators
T:\mathop{\rm Hol}\nolimits(\mathbb{D})\to\mathop{\rm Hol}\nolimits(\mathbb{D})
that are isometries of
\mathop{\rm Hol}\nolimits(\mathbb{D})
are those of the form
T_{\alpha,\beta}
, with
\left\lvert\alpha\right\rvert=\left\lvert\beta\right\rvert=1
.

Proof.

Let
T
be a linear isometry of
\mathop{\rm Hol}\nolimits(\mathbb{D})
. Then, in particular,
T
is a linear isometry for two different seminorms associated with
\mathop{\rm Hol}\nolimits(\mathbb{D})
, using Lemma
1.1
. Theorem
2.1
concludes.

Conversely, let
\left\lvert\alpha\right\rvert=\left\lvert\beta\right\rvert=1
. Then, for all
p\in\mathbb{N}
and
f\in\mathop{\rm Hol}\nolimits(\mathbb{D})
,

\left\|T_{\alpha,\beta}(f)\right\|_{\infty,1-1/p}=\sup_{\left\lvert z\right\rvert\leq 1-1/p}\left\lvert\alpha f(\beta z)\right\rvert=\sup_{\left\lvert w\right\rvert\leq 1-1/p}\left\lvert f(w)\right\rvert=\left\|f\right\|_{\infty,1-1/p}.

Hence,
T_{\alpha,\beta}
is a linear isometry of
\mathop{\rm Hol}\nolimits(\mathbb{D})
.
∎

3.
Operators similar to an isometry on
\mathop{\rm Hol}\nolimits(\mathbb{D})

In this section, we will focus on linear operators of
\mathop{\rm Hol}\nolimits(\mathbb{D})
that are
similar
to an isometry of
\mathop{\rm Hol}\nolimits(\mathbb{D})
. Let us recall the concept of
similarity
.

Definition 3.1
.

Two linear operators
T,V\in\mathcal{L}(\mathop{\rm Hol}\nolimits(\mathbb{D}))
are
similar
if there exists some
U\in\mathcal{L}(\mathop{\rm Hol}\nolimits(\mathbb{D}))
invertible such that
U^{-1}TU=V
.

In the following, we will only consider some classes of operators: composition, multiplication, and weighted composition ones. The goal of this section is to obtain a characterisation of such operators that are similar to a linear isometry of
\mathop{\rm Hol}\nolimits(\mathbb{D})
. The main theorem is the following.

Theorem 3.2
.

Let
T
be a continuous operator on
\mathop{\rm Hol}\nolimits(\mathbb{D})
.

(i)

T=C_{\varphi}
is a composition operator similar to a linear isometry of
\mathop{\rm Hol}\nolimits(\mathbb{D})
if and only if
\varphi
is bijective and elliptic, i.e., with a fixed point on
\mathbb{D}
.

(ii)

T=M_{m}
is a multiplication operator similar to a linear isometry of
\mathop{\rm Hol}\nolimits(\mathbb{D})
if and only if
m
is constant and unimodular.

(iii)

T=W_{m,\varphi}
is a weighted composition operator similar to a linear isometry of
\mathop{\rm Hol}\nolimits(\mathbb{D})
if and only if
\varphi
is bijective and elliptic with
\sigma_{p}(W_{m,\varphi})\cap\mathbb{T}\neq\varnothing
.

It is now time to prove this result. We recall that the linear isometries of
\mathop{\rm Hol}\nolimits(\mathbb{D})
are defined by

T_{\alpha,\beta}(f)(z)=\alpha f(\beta z),\qquad f\in\mathop{\rm Hol}\nolimits(\mathbb{D}),\qquad\left\lvert\alpha\right\rvert=\left\lvert\beta\right\rvert=1.

as we proved in Theorem
2.1
.

3.1.
Composition operators - Proof of Theorem
3.2
.(
i
)

First, assume that
\varphi
is bijective and elliptic. Denote by
\alpha\in\mathbb{D}
its fixed point. Consider the map
\psi
defined by

\psi(z)=\frac{\alpha-z}{1-\overline{\alpha}z}.

Then,
\psi\in\mathop{\rm Hol}\nolimits(\mathbb{D})
,
\psi
is bijective,
\psi^{-1}=\psi
, and
\psi(\alpha)=0
. Hence,
\tilde{\varphi}=\psi\circ\varphi\circ\psi
is a bijective self-map of
\mathbb{D}
, with a fixed point at
0
. By Schwarz’s Lemma (
[
1
,
20
]
), there exists
\beta\in\mathbb{C}
such that
\left\lvert\beta\right\rvert=1
and
\tilde{\varphi}(z)=\beta z
. Hence,

C_{\psi}\circ C_{\varphi}\circ C_{\psi}=C_{\tilde{\varphi}}=T_{1,\beta}.

The operator
C_{\varphi}
is therefore similar to a linear isometry of
\mathop{\rm Hol}\nolimits(\mathbb{D})
.

Now, consider
\varphi
not both bijective and elliptic. If
C_{\varphi}
was similar to a linear isometry of
\mathop{\rm Hol}\nolimits(\mathbb{D})
, then we would write

C_{\varphi}=UVU^{-1}\iff U^{-1}C_{\varphi}U=V,

with
V
isometric. Iterating this equality
n
times, and we get

U^{-1}C_{\varphi}^{n}U=U^{-1}C_{\varphi^{[n]}}U=V^{n}.

Thanks to Lemma
1.1
, for all
n,p\in\mathbb{N}
and
f\in\mathop{\rm Hol}\nolimits(\mathbb{D})
,

\left\|(U^{-1}C_{\varphi}^{n}U)(f)\right\|_{\infty,1-1/p}=\left\|V^{n}(f)\right\|_{\infty,p}=\left\|f\right\|_{\infty,1-1/p}.

Since
U
is invertible, writing
g=U(f)
, for all
p\in\mathbb{N}
and
g\in\mathop{\rm Hol}\nolimits(\mathbb{D})
,

\left\|(U^{-1}C_{\varphi}^{n})(g)\right\|_{\infty,1-1/p}=\left\|U^{-1}(g\circ\varphi^{[n]})\right\|_{\infty,1-1/p}=\left\|U^{-1}(g)\right\|_{\infty,1-1/p}.

By the Denjoy–Wolff theory (
[
6
,
10
]
), there exists a point
\omega\in\overline{\mathbb{D}}
such that the iterates
\varphi^{[n]}
of
\varphi
converge uniformly on all compact subsets of
\mathbb{D}
to
\omega
. Finally, considering
g(z)=z-\omega
, we have
g\in\mathop{\rm Hol}\nolimits(\mathbb{D})
, and using the continuity of
U
,

\left\|U^{-1}(g)\right\|_{\infty,1-1/p}=\left\|U^{-1}(\varphi^{[n]}-\omega)\right\|_{\infty,1-1/p}\xrightarrow[n\to+\infty]{}0.

However,
g\not\equiv 0
, so
U^{-1}(g)\not\equiv 0
(because
U
is invertible). Thus, there exists
p\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\left\|U^{-1}(g)\right\|_{\infty,1-1/p}\neq 0
, leading to an absurdity.

Conclusion:
C_{\varphi}
is similar to a linear isometry of
\mathop{\rm Hol}\nolimits(\mathbb{D})
if and only if
\varphi
is bijective and elliptic.
The proof of Theorem
3.2
.
(i)
is then complete.

3.2.
Multiplication operators - Proof of Theorem
3.2
.(
ii
)

Assume that
m
is constant and unimodular. Denote by
\alpha
this constant. Therefore, for all
f\in\mathop{\rm Hol}\nolimits(\mathbb{D})
, we may write

[M_{m}(f)](z)=m(z)f(z)=\alpha f(z)=[T_{\alpha,1}(f)](z).

Hence,
M_{m}=T_{\alpha,1}
is a linear isometry of
\mathop{\rm Hol}\nolimits(\mathbb{D})
.

Conversely, let
M_{m}
be similar to a linear isometry of
\mathop{\rm Hol}\nolimits(\mathbb{D})
. We may write
M_{m}=UVU^{-1}
,
with
U
invertible and
V
isometric. Iterating this
n
times, we obtain

M_{m}^{n}=M_{m^{n}}=UV^{n}U^{-1},

with
V^{n}
also isometric (by induction). We will consider two cases.

Assume that for all
z\in\mathbb{D}
,
\left\lvert m(z)\right\rvert<1
. Then, for all
p\in\mathbb{N}
,
\left\|m\right\|_{\infty,1-1/p}=c<1
. Hence,

(4)

\left\|m^{n}\right\|_{\infty,1-1/p}=c^{n}\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}0.

Consider
\mathds{1}
, the map defined by
\mathds{1}(z)=1
for all
z\in\mathbb{D}
. Then, for all
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, since
V^{n}
is isometric and
V^{n}U^{-1}=U^{-1}M_{m^{n}}
, for all
p\in\mathbb{N}
, by Lemma
1.1
,

\displaystyle\left\|U^{-1}(\mathds{1})\right\|_{\infty,1-1/p}=\left\|V^{n}U^{-1}(\mathds{1})\right\|_{\infty,1-1/p}

\displaystyle=\left\|U^{-1}M_{m^{n}}(\mathds{1})\right\|_{\infty,1-1/p}

\displaystyle=\left\|U^{-1}(m^{n})\right\|_{\infty,1-1/p}\xrightarrow[n\to+\infty]{}0,

using the continuity of
U
and (
4
).
But
U^{-1}(\mathds{1})\not\equiv 0
(since
U
is invertible), so there exists
p\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\left\|U^{-1}(\mathds{1})\right\|_{\infty,1-1/p}\neq 0
.
Impossible.

Assume that there exists
z_{0}\in\mathbb{D}
such that
\left\lvert m(z_{0})\right\rvert>1
.
Then, for all
p\in\mathbb{N}
,
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
and
f\in\mathop{\rm Hol}\nolimits(\mathbb{D})
, we have

\left\|m^{n}f\right\|_{\infty,1-1/p}=\left\|UV^{n}U^{-1}(f)\right\|_{\infty,1-1/p}.

In particular, for
p=p_{0}
such that
\left\lvert z_{0}\right\rvert\leq 1-\frac{1}{p_{0}}
and
f=\mathds{1}
,

\left\|UV^{n}U^{-1}(\mathds{1})\right\|_{\infty,1-1/p_{0}}=\left\|m^{n}\right\|_{\infty,1-1/p_{0}}\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}+\infty.

However,
U
is continuous and
V
is isometric, so there exist
C>0
and
q\in\mathbb{N}
such that

\left\|UV^{n}U^{-1}(\mathds{1})\right\|_{\infty,1-1/p_{0}}\leq C\left\|U^{-1}(\mathds{1})\right\|_{\infty,1-1/q}<+\infty.\vskip 6.0pt plus 2.0pt minus 2.0pt

The two cases lead to an absurdity. Ergo, for all
z\in\mathbb{D}
,
\left\lvert m(z)\right\rvert\leq 1
, and there exists
z_{0}\in\mathbb{D}
such that
\left\lvert m(z_{0})\right\rvert=1
. Using the maximum modulus principle, we have shown that
m
is constant, of modulus one.

Conclusion:
M_{m}
is similar to a linear isometry of
\mathop{\rm Hol}\nolimits(\mathbb{D})
if and only if
m
is constant and unimodular.
The proof of Theorem
3.2
.
(ii)
is then complete.

Remark 3.3
.

We have proved that there is no multiplication operator similar to a linear isometry of
\mathop{\rm Hol}\nolimits(\mathbb{D})
, other than the isometries themselves.

3.3.
Weighted composition operators - Proof of Theorem
3.2
.(
iii
)

To obtain the third and last result of Theorem
3.2
, we begin with a simple observation.

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
\varphi:\mathbb{D}\to\mathbb{D}
holomorphic, and
m\in\mathop{\rm Hol}\nolimits(\mathbb{D})
.

If
W_{m,\varphi}
is similar to a linear isometry of
\mathop{\rm Hol}\nolimits(\mathbb{D})
, then
W_{m,\varphi}
is invertible.

Proof.

If the operator
W_{m,\varphi}
is similar to a linear isometry of
\mathop{\rm Hol}\nolimits(\mathbb{D})
, then we have

W_{m,\varphi}=UVU^{-1},

with
U
invertible and
V
isometric. Since all linear isometries of
\mathop{\rm Hol}\nolimits(\mathbb{D})
are invertible (Theorem
2.1
and
[
3
, Proposition 2.1]
),
W_{m,\varphi}
is also invertible, with inverse
UV^{-1}U^{-1}
.
∎

Therefore, in the following, using
[
3
, Proposition 2.1]
, we may consider only weighted composition operators with symbol
\varphi
bijective, and weight
m
such that
\forall z\in\mathbb{D},m(z)\neq 0
.

1
st
case
: Assume that
\varphi
is not elliptic. Then, by
[
3
, Proposition 2.3, Theorems 6.1 & 7.1]
, there are only two possiblities: either
\sigma_{p}(W_{m,\varphi})=\mathbb{C}^{*}
, or
\sigma_{p}(W_{m,\varphi})=\varnothing
.

If
\sigma_{p}(W_{m,\varphi})=\mathbb{C}^{*}
, consider
f\in\mathop{\rm Hol}\nolimits(\mathbb{D})\backslash\{0\}
such that
W_{m,\varphi}(f)=2f
.
Suppose that
W_{m,\varphi}
is similar to a linear isometry of
\mathop{\rm Hol}\nolimits(\mathbb{D})
. We may write
W_{m,\varphi}=UVU^{-1}
, with
U
invertible and
V
isometric. An easy induction gives
W_{m,\varphi}^{n}=UV^{n}U^{-1}
for

, so

W_{m,\varphi}^{n}(f)=2^{n}f=UV^{n}U^{-1}f.

Since
U
,
U^{-1}
and
V
are continuous, there exists a constant
c>0
such that

\left\|UV^{n}U^{-1}f\right\|_{\infty,1/2}\leq c.

But
f\not\equiv 0
, so
\left\|f\right\|_{\infty,1/2}>0
, and

\left\|W_{m,\varphi}^{n}(f)\right\|_{\infty,1/2}=\left\|2^{n}f\right\|_{\infty,1/2}=2^{n}\left\|f\right\|_{\infty,1/2}\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}+\infty.

We have obtained an absurdity.

If
\sigma_{p}(W_{m,\varphi})=\varnothing
and
W_{m,\varphi}
is similar to a linear isometry
V
of
\mathop{\rm Hol}\nolimits(\mathbb{D})
, then

\varnothing=\sigma_{p}(W_{m,\varphi})=\sigma_{p}(UVU^{-1})=\sigma_{p}(V),

since
U
is invertible. But
\sigma_{p}(V)\neq\varnothing
, using Theorem
2.1
and
[
4
, Theorems 3.4 & 3.7]
.

1
st
conclusion:
In order to make
W_{m,\varphi}
similar to a linear isometry of
\mathop{\rm Hol}\nolimits(\mathbb{D})
, the symbol
\varphi
must be bijective and elliptic.

Now, we consider symbols
\varphi
that are elliptic and bijective. Without loss of generality (using the map
\psi
p.
3.1
), we will assume that the fixed point of
\varphi
is
0
.
Using Schwarz’s Lemma (
[
1
,
20
]
), we can write

\varphi(z)=\beta z,\qquad\left\lvert\beta\right\rvert=1.

Definition 3.5
.

Let
\varphi:z\mapsto\beta z
, with
\left\lvert\beta\right\rvert=1
.

The map
\varphi
is
periodic
if there exists
N\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\beta^{N}=1
.

The map
\varphi
is
aperiodic
if for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
\beta^{n}\neq 1
.

First, we will assume that
\left\lvert m(0)\right\rvert=1
. Indeed, we have the following lemma.

Lemma 3.6
.

Let
\varphi:\mathbb{D}\to\mathbb{D}
be elliptic and bijective such that
\varphi(0)=0
, and
m\in\mathop{\rm Hol}\nolimits(\mathbb{D})
.

If
W_{m,\varphi}
is similar to a linear isometry of
\mathop{\rm Hol}\nolimits(\mathbb{D})
, then
\left\lvert m(0)\right\rvert=1
.

Proof.

If
\left\lvert m(0)\right\rvert<1
, then considering the map
g=U^{-1}\mathds{1}
, with
\mathds{1}(z)=1
, we obtain

\left\|U^{-1}V^{n}Ug\right\|_{\infty,0}=\left\|W_{m,\varphi}^{n}(g)\right\|_{\infty,0}=\left\lvert m(0)\right\rvert^{n}\left\lvert g(0)\right\rvert\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}0.

Using the continuity of
U
, we get
1=\left\|\mathds{1}\right\|_{\infty,0}=\left\|V^{n}Ug\right\|_{\infty,0}\longrightarrow 0
, which is impossible.

If
\left\lvert m(0)\right\rvert>1
, then

\left\|UV^{n}U^{-1}\mathds{1}\right\|_{\infty,0}=\left\|W_{m,\varphi}^{n}(\mathds{1})\right\|_{\infty,0}=\left\lvert m(0)\right\rvert^{n}\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}+\infty.

However, there exists
p\in\mathbb{N}
and

such that

\left\|UV^{n}U^{-1}\mathds{1}\right\|_{\infty,0}\leq c\left\|V^{n}U^{-1}\mathds{1}\right\|_{\infty,1-1/p}=c\left\|U^{-1}\mathds{1}\right\|_{\infty,1-1/p}<+\infty.

This leads to an absurdity.
∎

Now, we consider separately periodic and aperiodic symbols.

2
nd
case
: Assume that
\varphi:z\mapsto\beta z
is elliptic periodic. Then, there exists
N\in\mathbb{N}
such that

W_{m,\varphi}^{N}=M_{m_{N}},\quad\text{with}\quad m_{N}(z)=\prod_{k=0}^{N-1}(m\circ\varphi^{[k]})(z)=\prod_{k=0}^{N-1}m(\beta^{k}z).

If
W_{m,\varphi}
is similar to a linear isometry of
\mathop{\rm Hol}\nolimits(\mathbb{D})
, then so is
W_{m,\varphi}^{N}=M_{m_{N}}
.
Using Theorem
3.2
.
(ii)
(proved in Section
3.2
), the map
m_{N}
must be constant, and unimodular.

Conversely, if
m_{N}
is constant and unimodular, thanks to
[
4
, Lemma 3.5]
, since
\left\lvert m(0)\right\rvert=1
, we may write

m=\exp(\tilde{m}),\quad\text{with}\quad\tilde{m}=i\theta+\sum_{k=1}^{N-1}z^{k}f_{k}(z^{N}),\qquad\theta\in\mathbb{R},\qquad f_{1},\cdots,f_{N-1}\in\mathop{\rm Hol}\nolimits(\mathbb{D}).

Moreover, we show that there exists
U
invertible such that
UW_{m,\varphi}U^{-1}=V
, with
V
a linear isometry of
\mathop{\rm Hol}\nolimits(\mathbb{D})
. Indeed, we look at particular
U
and
V
:

U=M_{w},\qquad V=T_{e^{i\theta},\beta},

with
w\in\mathop{\rm Hol}\nolimits(\mathbb{D})
not vanishing on
\mathbb{D}
,
e^{i\theta}
in the definition of
\tilde{m}
, and
\beta
in the definition of
\varphi
.
Note that since
w
does not vanish on
\mathbb{D}
, we may write
w=\exp(\tilde{w})
.

Then, for all
h\in\mathop{\rm Hol}\nolimits(\mathbb{D})
and
z\in\mathbb{D}
,

\displaystyle(UW_{m,\varphi}U^{-1})(h)(z)=V(h)(z)

\displaystyle\iff\frac{h(\beta z)}{w(\beta z)}m(z)w(z)=e^{i\theta}h(\beta z)

\displaystyle\iff\frac{w(z)}{w(\beta z)}\exp\left[\sum_{k=1}^{N-1}z^{k}f_{k}(z^{N})\right]=1

\displaystyle\iff\exp\left[\tilde{w}(z)-\tilde{w}(\beta z)+\sum_{k=1}^{N-1}z^{k}f_{k}(z^{N})\right]=1

\displaystyle\Longleftarrow\;\;\tilde{w}(z)-\tilde{w}(\beta z)+\sum_{k=1}^{N-1}z^{k}f_{k}(z^{N})=0.

Consider
\displaystyle f_{k}(z)=\sum_{\ell\geq 0}a_{\ell}^{(k)}z^{\ell}
and
\displaystyle\tilde{w}(z)=\sum_{\ell\geq 0}b_{\ell}z^{\ell}
.
We are searching for maps
\tilde{w}
such that

(5)

\displaystyle\tilde{w}(z)-\tilde{w}(\beta z)+\sum_{k=1}^{N-1}z^{k}f_{k}(z^{N})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell\geq 0}\left[b_{\ell}(1-\beta^{\ell})z^{\ell}+\sum_{k=1}^{N-1}a_{\ell}^{(k)}z^{N\ell+k}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{N-1}\left[\sum_{\ell\geq 0}\left(b_{N\ell+k}(1-\beta^{k})+a_{\ell}^{(k)}\right)z^{N\ell+k}\right]=0.

Finally, for all
\ell\geq 0
, considering
b_{N\ell}=0
and

b_{N\ell+k}=\frac{a_{\ell}^{(k)}}{\beta^{k}-1},\qquad 1\leq k\leq N-1,

we obtain the equation (
5
).
Hence, for this particular
w
, we get
M_{w}W_{m,\varphi}M_{w}^{-1}=T_{e^{i\theta},\beta}
.

2
nd
conclusion:
If
\varphi
is periodic, then the operator
W_{m,\varphi}
is similar to a linear isometry of
\mathop{\rm Hol}\nolimits(\mathbb{D})
if and only if the map
m_{N}
is constant and unimodular, that is if and only if
\sigma_{p}(W_{m,\varphi})\cap\mathbb{T}\neq\varnothing
, by
[
4
, Theorem 3.4]
.

3
rd
case
: Assume that
\varphi
is elliptic aperiodic.

If
W_{m,\varphi}
is similar to a linear isometry of
\mathop{\rm Hol}\nolimits(\mathbb{D})
, then we may write

U^{-1}W_{m,\varphi}U=T_{\alpha,\beta},

for some
\left\lvert\alpha\right\rvert=1
,
\left\lvert\beta\right\rvert=1
. We apply this equality for
f=\mathds{1}
. Hence,

(U^{-1}W_{m,\varphi}U)(\mathds{1})=T_{\alpha,\beta}(\mathds{1})=\alpha\mathds{1}\iff m(U\mathds{1}\circ\varphi)=\alpha(U\mathds{1}).

Therefore,
\alpha\in\mathbb{T}
is an eigenvalue of
W_{m,\varphi}
.

Conversely, assume that
\sigma_{p}(W_{m,\varphi})\cap\mathbb{T}\neq\varnothing
. Let
\lambda\in\sigma_{p}(W_{m,\varphi})\cap\mathbb{T}
, and
f\in\mathop{\rm Hol}\nolimits(\mathbb{D})
such that

W_{m,\varphi}(f)=m(f\circ\varphi)=\lambda f.

Then, for all
z\in\mathbb{D}
,
f(z)\neq 0
. Indeed, assume that there exists
z_{0}\in\mathbb{D}
such that
f(z_{0})=0
. Since
\lambda\neq 0
, by induction, for all
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, we get
(f\circ\varphi^{[n]})(z_{0})=f(\beta^{n}z_{0})=0
. Because
\varphi
is aperiodic, it implies that
f=0
on
\{z\in\mathbb{C}:\left\lvert z\right\rvert=\left\lvert z_{0}\right\rvert\}
, so
f=0
on
\mathbb{D}
using analytic continuation. Let
U=M_{f}
. Then,
U
is invertible, and for all
g\in\mathop{\rm Hol}\nolimits(\mathbb{D})
,

\displaystyle(U^{-1}W_{m,\varphi}U)(g)=(U^{-1}W_{m,\varphi})(fg)

\displaystyle=U^{-1}(m(f\circ\varphi)(g\circ\varphi))

\displaystyle=\lambda U^{-1}(f(g\circ\varphi))

\displaystyle=\lambda(g\circ\varphi)=T_{\lambda,\beta}(g).

Hence,
W_{m,\varphi}
is similar to a linear isometry of
\mathop{\rm Hol}\nolimits(\mathbb{D})
.

Final conclusion:
A weighted composition operator
W_{m,\varphi}
is similar to a linear isometry of
\mathop{\rm Hol}\nolimits(\mathbb{D})
if and only if
\varphi
is bijective and elliptic with
\sigma_{p}(W_{m,\varphi})\cap\mathbb{T}\neq\varnothing
. By
[
4
, Proposition 3.3 and 3.6]
a necessary condition to get a nonempty point spectrum is that
m
does not vanish on
\mathbb{D}
.

4.
Isometries of
\mathop{\rm Hol}\nolimits(\mathbb{D})
with a single seminorm

We now consider the isometries for a
single
seminorm.
In this case, we expect to get more operators. This is indeed the case.
In order to show it, we will use an analogy with the disc algebra
A(\mathbb{D})
, a case already discussed by El-Gebeily and Wolfe in
[
11
]
.

We begin this section with a useful proposition.

Proposition 4.1

(
[
11
]
)
.

Let
S:A(\mathbb{D})\to A(\mathbb{D})
an isometry. Then there exists a closed subset
K\subset\mathbb{T}
, and two maps
\rho:K\to\mathbb{T}
continuous,
\phi:K\to\mathbb{T}
continuous and onto, such that for all
f\in A(\mathbb{D})
and
\xi\in K
,

\rho(\xi)\times(Sf)(\xi)=f(\phi(\xi)).

It will allow us to prove the main theorem of this section.

Theorem 4.2
.

Let
0<r<1
fixed, and
T:\mathop{\rm Hol}\nolimits(\mathbb{D})\to\mathop{\rm Hol}\nolimits(\mathbb{D})
a linear continuous operator such that for all
f\in\mathop{\rm Hol}\nolimits(\mathbb{D})
,

\left\|T(f)\right\|_{\infty,r}=\left\|f\right\|_{\infty,r}.

Then there exist a map
B_{1}
, which is constant or a finite Blaschke product, and
\beta\in\mathbb{T}
such that for all
f\in\mathop{\rm Hol}\nolimits(\mathbb{D})
,

T(f)(z)=B_{1}(z/r)f(\beta z).

Proof.

To begin, let us consider
U:\mathop{\rm Hol}\nolimits(\mathbb{D})\to\mathop{\rm Hol}\nolimits(r^{-1}\mathbb{D})
the operator defined by

U(f)(z)=f(rz),\qquad\left\lvert z\right\rvert\leq 1/r.

Then, by setting
S=UTU^{-1}:\mathop{\rm Hol}\nolimits(r^{-1}\mathbb{D})\to\mathop{\rm Hol}\nolimits(r^{-1}\mathbb{D})
, the operator
S
is isometric for
\left\|\cdot\right\|_{\infty,1}
, that is, for the disc algebra norm.

Note that the set of all polynomials is dense in
\mathop{\rm Hol}\nolimits(r^{-1}\mathbb{D})
and in
A(\mathbb{D})
for the norm
\left\|\cdot\right\|_{\infty,1}
. Hence, we can extend
S
to an operator
\tilde{S}:A(\mathbb{D})\to A(\mathbb{D})
so that
\tilde{S}
is still isometric for
\left\|\cdot\right\|_{\infty,1}
. In the following, we will continue to write
S
even if we consider
\tilde{S}
.

Step 1
: Recall that for all
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, we denote
e_{n}(z)=z^{n}
, and
f_{n}=Se_{n}
. Let
K
be the set defined in Proposition
4.1
. Then for all
\xi\in K
,

\rho(\xi)\times f_{0}(\xi)=1,\qquad\rho(\xi)\times f_{1}(\xi)=\phi(\xi).

Using the first equation, we obtain that for all
\xi\in K
,
\rho(\xi)\neq 0
. We can say more: since
\rho
is continuous on the compact set
K
, there exists
\delta>0
such that
\left\lvert\rho(\xi)\right\rvert\leq\delta
for
\xi\in K
. We have the same result for
f_{0}
. Furthermore,

\phi(\xi)=\frac{f_{1}(\xi)}{f_{0}(\xi)},\qquad\xi\in K.

We focus on the Lebesgue measure
m(K)
of the set
K
relatively to
\mathbb{T}
. We will show that
m(K)>0
. Indeed, first, we can say that
K
is not finite (otherwise, we would have
\phi(K)=\mathbb{T}
also finite, impossible).

If the map
f_{0}
is constant, then
\phi
is of the form
cf_{1}
, with
c\in\mathbb{C}
. Hence, we can extend
\phi
to a holomorphic function on
r^{-1}\mathbb{D}
, which is in particular
\mathcal{C}^{1}
on
\mathbb{T}
.
Assume that
m(K)=0
, we would get
m(\phi(K))=m(\mathbb{T})=0
, impossible.

If the map
f_{0}
is not constant, since it is analytic on
r^{-1}\mathbb{D}
, because the zeroes are isolated, there is only a finite number of zeroes of
f_{0}
that are unimodular. Denote them by
\xi_{1},\cdots,\xi_{n}
. Therefore, the map
\phi
can be extended to a
\mathcal{C}^{1}
map on
\mathbb{T}\backslash\{\xi_{1},\cdots,\xi_{n}\}
, such that
\phi(K\backslash\{\xi_{1},\cdots,\xi_{n}\})
contains
\mathbb{T}
, except at most
n
points.
Assume that
m(K)=0
, we would get
m(\mathbb{T}\backslash\{n\text{ points}\})=0
, impossible.

Finally, we have proved that
m(K)>0
.

Step 2
: By
[
17
, Theorem 1.1]
and
[
11
, Proposition 1]
, there exist a function
\varphi\in H^{\infty}
of the form
\varphi=h_{1}/h_{2}
, with
h_{1},h_{2}\in A(\mathbb{D})
, and
h_{2}=0
on a certain subset
L\subset\mathbb{T}
; also, another map
\psi\in A(\mathbb{D})
such that
\left\|\varphi\right\|_{\infty,1}=\left\|\psi\right\|_{\infty,1}=1
,
\varphi(K)=\mathbb{T}
,
\psi=0
on
L
and
\left\lvert\psi\right\rvert=1
on
K
satisfying

S(f)=\psi(f\circ\varphi),\qquad f\in A(\mathbb{D}).\vskip 6.0pt plus 2.0pt minus 2.0pt

Recall that at the beginning,
S
is defined for
f\in\mathop{\rm Hol}\nolimits(r^{-1}\mathbb{D})
. Thus, we obtain
\varphi\in\mathop{\rm Hol}\nolimits(r^{-1}\mathbb{D})
, that is
L=\varnothing
. Hence, the map
\psi
does not vanish on
\mathbb{T}
.
In addition, by setting
X=\{\xi\in\mathbb{T}:\left\lvert\varphi(\xi)\right\rvert=1\}
, if
X
was finite, we would get
\varphi(X)
finite. This is impossible since
\varphi(K)=\mathbb{T}
, so
\mathbb{T}\subset\varphi(\overline{\mathbb{D}})
. By Theorem
1.4
,
\varphi
is a finite Blaschke product.

Now, assume that
\psi
is not constant. If
\psi
is not a finite Blaschke product, note that since
\left\|\psi\right\|_{\infty,1}=1
, we have
\psi(\overline{\mathbb{D}})\subset\overline{\mathbb{D}}
. By Theorem
1.4
, the set
X=\{\xi\in\mathbb{T}:\left\lvert\psi(\xi)\right\rvert=1\}
is finite. Choose
z_{0}\in\mathbb{T}\backslash X
such that
\varphi^{-1}(\varphi(z_{0}))\subset\mathbb{T}\backslash X
; this is possible since
X
is finite. Set

g(z)=\frac{\overline{\varphi(z_{0})}z+1}{2}.

Then
\left\|g\right\|_{\infty,1}=1
, and
\left\lvert g(\varphi(z))\right\rvert=1\iff\varphi(z)=\varphi(z_{0})\iff z\in\varphi^{-1}(\varphi(z_{0}))\subset\mathbb{T}\backslash X
. Thus,

\left\lvert g(\varphi(z))\right\rvert=1\Longrightarrow\left\lvert\psi(z)\right\rvert<1\Longrightarrow\left\lvert S(g)(z)\right\rvert=\left\lvert\psi(z)\right\rvert\left\lvert g(\varphi(z))\right\rvert<1.

Therefore,
\left\|S(g)\right\|_{\infty,1}<1
, which is impossible because
S
is isometric for
\left\|\cdot\right\|_{\infty,1}
. To conclude,
X=\mathbb{T}
(by Theorem
1.4
), and
\psi
is a finite Blaschke product.

Finally,
S(f)(z)=B_{1}(z)f(B_{2}(z))
,
with
B_{1}
and
B_{2}
of the wanted form. We have only left to go back to
T
via the invertible operator
U
, and that gives

\displaystyle T(f)(z)

\displaystyle=(U^{-1}S)(Uf)(z)

\displaystyle=U^{-1}(B_{1}(z)\times(Uf)(B_{2}(z)))

\displaystyle=U^{-1}(B_{1}(z)\times f(rB_{2}(z))

\displaystyle=B_{1}(z/r)f(rB_{2}(z/r)).

We finish the proof by noting that since the equality must hold for all
f\in\mathop{\rm Hol}\nolimits(\mathbb{D})
, if we denote
\phi(z)=rB_{2}(z/r)
, we must have
\phi(\mathbb{D})\subset\mathbb{D}
.
Moreover,
\phi(r\overline{\mathbb{D}})=r\overline{\mathbb{D}}
, so by a renormalisation and Lemma
1.5
,
\phi
is a rotation. Hence, there exists
\left\lvert\beta\right\rvert=1
such that

T(f)(z)=B_{1}(z/r)f(\beta z).\qed

5.
Spectral study of the isometries

In this section, we make a spectral study of the isometries of
\mathop{\rm Hol}\nolimits(\mathbb{D})
, endowed with two or a single seminorm. We will consider separately the two cases.

5.1.
Spectra of the isometries for two seminorms

First, we focus on the isometries of
\mathop{\rm Hol}\nolimits(\mathbb{D})
, that is the operators

T_{\alpha,\beta}:f\longmapsto\alpha f(\beta\cdot).

Following the results of
[
4
]
, the results depend on the nature of the number
\beta
(cf. Definition
3.5
).

If
\beta
is periodic, then
\sigma(T_{\alpha,\beta})=\sigma_{p}(T_{\alpha,\beta})=\{\alpha\beta^{k}:k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\}
.

If
\beta
is aperiodic, then

\sigma_{p}(T_{\alpha,\beta})=\{\alpha\beta^{k}:k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\}\subset\sigma(T_{\alpha,\beta})\subset\mathbb{T},

and if we consider for
\tau>2
the set of all
Diophantine numbers of order
\tau
, defined by

\mathcal{D}(\tau)=\{\xi\in\mathbb{R}:\exists\gamma>0,\forall p\in\mathbb{Z},\forall q\in\mathbb{N},\left\lvert p/q-\xi\right\rvert\geq\gamma q^{-\tau}\},

we obtain a little improvement: for
\beta=e^{2i\pi\xi}
,
\xi\in\mathcal{D}(\tau)
,

\sigma(T_{\alpha,\beta})\subset\{e^{2i\pi x}:x\not\in\mathbb{Q}\}\cup\{1\}.

5.2.
Spectra of the isometries for one seminorm

Now, let
0<r<1
. Using the results of Section
4
, we want to find the spectra of the operators defined by

T(f)(z)=B(z/r)f(\beta z),

with
B
constant or a finite Blaschke product, and
\beta\in\mathbb{T}
.

1
st
case
: If
B
is a constant map of modulus one, denote by
\xi\in\mathbb{T}
the constant. Then we obtain
T=T_{\xi,\beta}
, so the spectral study has already been done previously.

2
nd
case
: If
B
is a finite Blaschke product, then since
B
is vanishing on
\mathbb{D}
, we obtain
\sigma_{p}(T)=\varnothing
(see
[
4
, Proposition 3.3 and 3.6]
).
Now, to compute the spectrum of
T
, consider once again the two subcases depending on the nature of
\beta
.

If
\beta
is periodic, then there exists
N\in\mathbb{N}
,
N\geq 2
such that
\beta^{N}=1
and
\beta^{k}\neq 1
for
k<N
.
It follows that for all
f\in\mathop{\rm Hol}\nolimits(\mathbb{D})
,

T^{N}(f)(z)=m_{N}(z)f(z),\qquad m_{N}(z)=\prod_{j=0}^{N-1}B\left(\frac{\beta^{j}z}{r}\right).

Hence, by
[
4
, Proposition 3.2]
, we have
\sigma(T)=\{\lambda\in\mathbb{C}:\lambda^{N}\in m_{N}(\mathbb{D})\}
.

If
\beta
is aperiodic, then by
[
4
, Corollary 2.2]
,
\{\beta^{n}B(0):n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\}\subset\sigma(T)
. Moreover, using
[
4
, Proposition 7.2]
,
we have
\sigma(T)\subset D(0,M)
, with
M
defined by

M=\lim_{R\to 1}M_{R},\qquad M_{R}=\exp\left(\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\log\left\lvert B(Re^{it}/r)\right\rvert\mathrm{d}t\right).

Note that if
m(z)=B(z/r)
, we can write

m(z)=z^{N}\tilde{m}(z),\quad\text{with}\quad\tilde{m}(z)=\frac{e^{i\theta}}{r^{N}}\prod_{j=1}^{K}\frac{z-r\alpha_{j}}{r-\overline{\alpha}_{j}z},

with
\alpha_{1},\cdots,\alpha_{K}\neq 0
. Since
m
is well defined in
\overline{\mathbb{D}}
, the equation (7.2) of
[
4
]
is also valid for
M_{R}
with
R\to 1
, that is for
M_{1}=M
. Hence,

M=\left\lvert\tilde{m}(0)\right\rvert\prod_{j=1}^{K}\frac{1}{\left\lvert r\alpha_{j}\right\rvert}=\left\lvert\frac{e^{i\theta}}{r^{N}}\prod_{j=1}^{K}\frac{-r\alpha_{j}}{r}\right\rvert\prod_{j=1}^{K}\frac{1}{\left\lvert r\alpha_{j}\right\rvert}=\frac{1}{r^{N}}\prod_{j=1}^{K}\frac{\left\lvert\alpha_{j}\right\rvert}{r\left\lvert\alpha_{j}\right\rvert}=\frac{1}{r^{N+K}}.

Therefore, if
B
has
d
zeroes (counting the multiplicities), then

\{\beta^{n}B(0):n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\}\subset\sigma(T)\subset D(0,r^{-d}).

Thus, the spectrum of
T
contains a set which is dense in the circle centered at
0
and of radius
\left\lvert B(0)\right\rvert
.
