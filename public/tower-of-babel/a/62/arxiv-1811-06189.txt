Abstract
. In this self-contained paper, we present a theory of the piecewise linear minimal valid functions for the 1-row Gomory–Johnson infinite group problem. The non-extreme minimal valid functions are those that admit effective perturbations. We give a precise description of the space of these perturbations as a direct sum of certain finite- and infinite-dimensional subspaces. The infinite-dimensional subspaces have partial symmetries; to describe them, we develop a theory of inverse semigroups of partial bijections, interacting with the functional equations satisfied by the perturbations. Our paper provides the foundation for grid-free algorithms for the Gomory–Johnson model, in particular for testing extremality of piecewise linear functions whose breakpoints are rational numbers with huge denominators.

Introduction

1.1.
Finite group relaxations
R_{f}(P,{\mathbb{Z}})
of integer programs and hierarchies of
valid inequalities

A powerful method to derive cutting planes for unstructured integer linear
optimization problems is to study relaxations with more structure and
convenient properties. The pioneering relaxation in this line of research on
general-purpose cutting planes is
Gomory’s
finite group relaxation

[
10
]
, whose convex hull is known
as the
corner polyhedron
.

The relaxations are structured around the
simplex method, applied to the continuous relaxation, and are therefore
suitable for generating cuts in a linear-programming-based cutting-plane
procedure. The group relaxation is obtained by forgetting about the
nonnegativity of all basic variables, retaining only their integrality.
Viewed in the space of nonbasic variables, the equations of the simplex
tableau are replaced by congruences modulo the abelian group (
{\mathbb{Z}}
-module)
generated by the columns of the basis matrix. Quotienting out by this group,
one obtains a “group equation,” which gives the relaxation its name.
Further relaxations are obtained by picking just one or a few rows of the
system, or more generally by condensing the system by means of group
homomorphisms; see
[
10
]
for its remarks on the use of (additive) group
characters.

In the present paper, we restrict ourselves to 1-row (“cyclic”) group
relaxations, which after aggregation of non-basic variables with identical
coefficients can be brought to the form

(1.1)

\displaystyle\sum_{p\in P}y(p)\,p\in f+{\mathbb{Z}}

\displaystyle y(p)\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}\ \ \textrm{for all $p\in P$}

where
P
is a finite subset of an additive group
G=\frac{1}{q}{\mathbb{Z}}\supset{\mathbb{Z}}

and
f\in G\setminus{\mathbb{Z}}
, so
f+{\mathbb{Z}}
is a coset of the subgroup
{\mathbb{Z}}
in
G
.
This is called Gomory’s
finite (cyclic) group problem
.
We denote the convex hull of
y
by
R_{f}(P,{\mathbb{Z}})
; it is a polyhedron of blocking
type. Therefore every nontrivial valid linear inequality can be written in the form

\langle\pi,y\rangle:=\sum_{p\in P}\pi(p)y(p)\geq 1
; then we call
\pi

a
valid function
. If
\pi^{\prime}\leq\pi
are two valid functions, then the
valid inequality
\langle\pi,y\rangle\geq 1
is a conic combination of

\langle\pi^{\prime},y\rangle\geq 1
and nonnegativity inequalities
y(p)\geq 0
. Thus it suffices
to consider the
minimal (valid) functions

\pi
, defined by the
property

(M)

\displaystyle\text{if $\pi^{\prime}$ is valid and $\pi^{\prime}\leq\pi$}

\displaystyle\text{then $\pi^{\prime}=\pi$.}

A stronger notion is that of
extreme functions

\pi
, defined by the property

(E)

\displaystyle\text{if $\pi^{+}$ and $\pi^{-}$ are minimal and $\pi=\tfrac{1}{2}(\pi^{+}+\pi^{-})$}

\displaystyle\text{then $\pi=\pi^{+}=\pi^{-}$}.

Extreme functions correspond to facet-defining inequalities for
R_{f}(P,{\mathbb{Z}})
.
Following the traditions of polyhedral combinatorics, we are interested in
describing families of extreme functions and making them available for
cutting-plane algorithms.

1.2.
Master problems
R_{f}(G,{\mathbb{Z}})
and the subadditive characterization of minimal functions

Gomory’s approach was to consider
master problems
for this purpose.
The sets of solutions
y
to 1-row group relaxations
R_{f}(P)
for subsets
P
of the
same group
G
inject into the
master group relaxation

(1.2)

\displaystyle\sum_{p\in G}y(p)\,p\in f+{\mathbb{Z}}

\displaystyle y\colon G\to{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}\textrm{ has finite support}

by setting
y(p)=0
for
p\notin P
. We denote its convex hull by

R_{f}(G,{\mathbb{Z}})
. This is an infinite-dimensional set. By Gomory’s master theorem

[
10
, Theorem 13]
, every extreme function
\pi^{\prime}
for
R_{f}(P,{\mathbb{Z}})
is
obtained from some extreme function
\pi
for a master problem
R_{f}(G,{\mathbb{Z}})

with
P\subseteq G
by restricting the function,
\pi^{\prime}=\pi|_{P}
. Moreover,
Gomory
[
10
]
gave a characterization of the minimal functions for the
master problem
R_{f}(G,{\mathbb{Z}})
by the following functional inequalities and
equations:

(1.3a)

\displaystyle\pi(x)\geq 0

\displaystyle\text{for }x\in G,

(1.3b)

\displaystyle\pi(x+z)=\pi(x)

for
x\in G
,
z\in{\mathbb{Z}}

(periodicity)

(1.3c)

\displaystyle\pi(0)=0,\ \pi(f)=1,

(1.3d)

\displaystyle\Delta\pi(x,y)\geq 0

\displaystyle\text{for }x,y\in G

(subadditivity),

(1.3e)

\displaystyle\Delta\pi(x,f-x)=0

\displaystyle\text{for }x\in G

(symmetry condition),

where
\Delta\pi(x,y)=\pi(x)+\pi(y)-\pi(x+y)
is the
subadditivity
slack
function. By quotienting out by
{\mathbb{Z}}
, this system describes a
polyhedron in
{\mathbb{R}}^{G/{\mathbb{Z}}}
. Extreme functions are then simply the vertices of this
polyhedron; thus some of the subadditivity inequalities
\Delta\pi(x,y)\geq 0
are tight, i.e., additivity holds.

There is a possible viewpoint on the extreme functions for the infinite group problems as
“robust” cut-generating functions that ignore the

. As a matter of fact, in a numerical implementation, the

A natural algorithmic focus lies on piecewise linear valid functions, though a
part of the literature
[
1
,
2
]
also studies more complicated
functions. (When we discuss piecewise linear functions in this paper, we
include the discontinuous case with possible jumps at breakpoints, which
includes important examples such as the Gomory fractional cut,

gomory_fractional

.)

For
{\mathbb{Z}}
-periodic piecewise linear functions, the
characterization (
1.3
) of minimal functions gives a simple
algorithm for testing minimality, based on enumerating the vertices of a certain polyhedral complex;
see
[
3
, section 2.2]
and
[
16
, section
5]
. For testing the extremality of a
piecewise linear minimal function, however, in contrast to the finite group
case, we cannot directly use polyhedral methods any more. Since the quotient

{\mathbb{R}}/{\mathbb{Z}}
is not finite, we have to use infinite-dimensional methods of
functional equations and inequalities. The most important lemma from this
theory is the
Gomory–Johnson interval lemma, variants of which has been used in virtually
all proofs of extremality in the literature.

1.4.
The space
\tilde{\Pi}^{\pi}
of effective perturbations
\tilde{\pi}
of a minimal valid function

Recall that by definition (
E
), a
minimal valid function
\pi
is extreme if it cannot be written as a convex
combination of two other minimal valid functions
\pi^{+}
,
\pi^{-}
. A fruitful
approach to extremality testing, introduced by Basu et al. in Part I of the present series of
papers
[
3
]
, has been to consider the
difference function (
perturbation
)
\bar{\pi}=\pi^{+}-\pi
, which
allows us to write
\pi^{1}=\pi+\bar{\pi}
and
\pi^{2}=\pi-\bar{\pi}
.
(Recently, Di Summa
[
9
]
obtained a
breakthrough result on the question of piecewise linearity of
extreme functions using this approach.)
It is
convenient to build a space from the notion of perturbation functions.
Following Part V
[
16
, section 6]
, we define the space

(1.4)

\tilde{\Pi}^{\pi}=\left\{\,\tilde{\pi}\colon{\mathbb{R}}\to{\mathbb{R}}\mid\exists\,\epsilon>0\text{ s.t.\ }\pi^{\pm}=\pi\pm\epsilon\tilde{\pi}\text{ are minimal valid}\,\right\}

of

effective perturbation functions
for the minimal valid
function
\pi

. This is a vector space
(
9.0
), a subspace of the space
of bounded functions. The function
\pi
is
extreme if and only if the space
\tilde{\Pi}^{\pi}
is trivial.

If additivity (
\Delta\pi(x,y)=0
) holds for some
(x,y)
, then by convexity
also
\Delta\tilde{\pi}(x,y)=0
holds for every effective perturbation

\tilde{\pi}\in\tilde{\Pi}^{\pi}
. This is also true for additivity in
the limit
[
3
, Lemma 2.7]
; see also

[
16
, Lemma 6.1]
. Because
\pi
is assumed
to be piecewise linear, the infinite system of functional equations describing
additivity and limit-additivity of
\tilde{\pi}
can be structured
(“combinatorialized”) according to a certain polyhedral complex

[
3
,
16
]
.

1.5.
Finite-dimensional and equivariant perturbations

In Part I of the present series, Basu et
al.
[
3
]
gave the first algorithm to
decide extremality of a piecewise linear function with rational breakpoints
in some
“grid” (group)
G=\frac{1}{q}{\mathbb{Z}}
.

In a
first step, one tests whether there exists a nontrivial perturbation for
\pi

in the
finite-dimensional subspace of
\tilde{\Pi}^{\pi}
that consists of the
functions
\hrefhttps://github.com/mkoeppe/cutgeneratingfunctionology/search?q=%22def+interpolate_to_infinite_group(%22(\tilde{\pi}|_{G})
, where

\tilde{\pi}|_{G}
is an effective perturbation function for the restriction

\pi|_{G}
to the finite group problem
R_{f}(G,{\mathbb{Z}})
.

Otherwise, one may assume that
\tilde{\pi}|_{G}=0
. Under this assumption, the
interval lemma forces
\tilde{\pi}|_{C}=0
for certain
directly covered

intervals
C
. Basu et al.’s crucial observation was that if there are any
remaining
uncovered
intervals, then one-dimensional families of
additivity equations impose a type of symmetry of the perturbation function.
By analyzing the required symmetry, one can construct a perturbation function
and prove nonextremality of
\pi
.

Consider the additivity equations

(1.5)

\Delta\tilde{\pi}(x,t)=\tilde{\pi}(x)+\tilde{\pi}(t)-\tilde{\pi}(x+t)=0,\quad\text{for $x\in D$},

where
D
is an interval and
t\in\frac{1}{q}{\mathbb{Z}}
is a grid point. Because

\tilde{\pi}(t)=0
, this simplifies to

(1.6a)

\tilde{\pi}(x)=\tilde{\pi}(x+t)\quad\text{for $x\in D$}.

We then say that
\tilde{\pi}
is
invariant
under the action of the
translation

\tau_{t}\colon x\mapsto x+t
(restricted to the interval
D
).
Likewise, a second type of one-dimensional families of additivity equations
simplifies to

(1.6b)

\tilde{\pi}(x)=-\tilde{\pi}(r-x)\quad\text{for $x\in D$}.

Here a negative sign comes in. We call
\rho_{r}\colon x\mapsto r-x
a

reflection
. By assigning a
character

\chi(\tau_{t})=+1
and

\chi(\rho_{r})=-1
to the translations and reflections, we can unify
equations (
1.6
) as

(1.7)

\tilde{\pi}(x)=\chi(\gamma)\,\tilde{\pi}(\gamma(x))\quad\text{for $x\in D$},

where
\gamma
is either a translation or a reflection. We then say that

\tilde{\pi}
is
equivariant
under the action of
\gamma
.

By analyzing the group
\Gamma\subset\Aff({\mathbb{R}})
generated by all relevant
translations and reflections, Basu et al. constructed a universal template
function
\psi\colon{\mathbb{R}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
, a continuous piecewise linear function with
breakpoints in
\frac{1}{4q}{\mathbb{Z}}
, which is equivariant under the action of the
group
\Gamma
. Taking

(1.8)

\tilde{\pi}(x)=\begin{cases}\psi(x)&\text{for $x$ in uncovered intervals},\\
0&\text{for $x$ in covered intervals}\end{cases}

then gives an effective perturbation function.
(A revised construction in Basu et al.’s survey

[
6
, section 8.2]
gives a continuous piecewise linear
function
\tilde{\pi}
with breakpoints in
\frac{1}{3q}{\mathbb{Z}}
.)

1.6.1.
Method: Inverse semigroups as the language of partial symmetries

Group actions are the standard language to describe symmetries of mathematical
objects. The use of group actions was fruitful in Part I of
our series to
obtain the first algorithm for testing extremality. However, group actions do
not provide a complete theory of the effective perturbations. This becomes
most apparent in
[
3
, section 5]
, where
Basu et al. introduce a family of extreme functions with irrational
breakpoints,
bhk_irrational

. Here the group
\Gamma

generated by the translations and
reflections only gives the correct result when a certain non-group-theoretic
reachability condition
[
3
, Assumption 5.1, Lemma
5.2]
is satisfied.

The underlying reason is that the restriction of the translations and
reflections to the interval domains
D
in
(
1.6
) is not considered in
the reflection group. Indeed, what the translations and reflections describe
is not a full symmetry of the perturbation function, but only a
partial
symmetry
within the uncovered intervals.

The correct language to describe partial symmetries is the less well-known
theory of inverse-semigroup actions. An
inverse
semigroup

(\Gamma,\circ,\cdot^{-1})
, following
[
22
, page
7]
, is a semigroup, i.e., a set
\Gamma

closed under an associative operation
\circ
, satisfying the additional axiom that

(
\exists!
inverse)

\begin{gathered}\text{for every $\omega\in\Gamma$, there exists a unique element
$\omega^{-1}\in\Gamma$}\\
\text{such that $\omega=\omega\circ\omega^{-1}\circ\omega$
and $\omega^{-1}=\omega^{-1}\circ\omega\circ\omega^{-1}$}.\end{gathered}

The equations in the axiom describe the familiar properties of a
pseudoinverse, but due to the required uniqueness, we will simply refer to

\omega^{-1}
as the
inverse
of
\omega
. In his
monograph
[
22
]
, Lawson points out that

the relationship between inverse semigroups and partial symmetries is a
generalization of the relation between groups and symmetries.

Concretely, inverse semigroups arise as semigroups of partial bijections of a
set, where the operation
\circ
is the composition and
\cdot^{-1}
is the
inverse of a partial bijection. We define the restrictions of the previously
defined translations and
reflections to open intervals
D
. We denote them by
\tau_{t}|_{D}
and

\rho_{r}|_{D}
and consider them
as partial bijections of
{\mathbb{R}}
to itself,
with domains
\dom(\tau_{t}|_{D})=D=\dom(\rho_{r}|_{D})
and images

\im(\tau_{t}|_{D})=\tau_{t}(D)=D+t
and
\im(\rho_{r}|_{D})=\rho_{r}(D)=r-D
. We refer to these partial bijections as
moves
.
The composition of two moves
\gamma_{1}|_{D_{1}}
and
\gamma_{2}|_{D_{2}}
is
defined as

(1.9)

\gamma_{2}|_{D_{2}}\circ\gamma_{1}|_{D_{1}}=\gamma_{2}\circ\gamma_{1}|_{D_{1}\cap\gamma_{1}^{-1}(D_{2})};

see
Figure 2
.
The domain of the composition is either an open interval or the empty set.
(By definition, there are exactly two empty moves: the empty translation

\tau|_{\emptyset}
and the empty reflection
\rho|_{\emptyset}
.)
Note that the inverse of a move
\gamma|_{D}
, given by
(\gamma|_{D})^{-1}=\gamma^{-1}|_{\gamma(D)}
, is not an inverse in a group-theoretic sense:
The compositions

(1.10)

\gamma|_{D}\circ(\gamma|_{D})^{-1}=\tau_{0}|_{\gamma(D)}\quad\text{and}\quad(\gamma|_{D})^{-1}\circ\gamma|_{D}=\tau_{0}|_{D}

are only
partial identities
(restrictions of the identity
\tau_{0}
to
intervals) and therefore not neutral elements but merely idempotents
(
Figure 4
).

We develop methods that center around inverse semigroups of moves and their
generating sets. We study the set of moves that are respected by the
effective perturbations of a given minimal function
\pi
. We analyze the
closure properties (axioms) that it satisfies: algebraically, it is an inverse
semigroup; but additional order-theoretic and analytic closure properties come
in. Starting from an initial set (
move ensemble
)
\Omega^{0}
, we can
then form the closure
with respect to the axioms. We call it the
moves closure
of
\Omega^{0}

(or
closed move semigroup
generated by
\Omega^{0}
) and denote it by

\mathop{\mathrm{clsemi}_{A}}(\Omega^{0})
.

In the first part of the paper, we develop these methods in full generality,
without using any specific properties of the Gomory–Johnson model.
Then we turn to the study of piecewise linear functions; here we make the
assumption of continuity from at least one side of the origin.

For all piecewise linear functions with rational breakpoints, we will show
that
\mathop{\mathrm{clsemi}_{A}}(\Omega^{0})
has a simple structure: Its graph consists of a finite
union of line segments and rectangles. (We say that it is
finitely
presented
.) It will become clear that we can compute
\mathop{\mathrm{clsemi}_{A}}(\Omega^{0})
in
finitely many steps using a completion-type algorithm, using only the
algebraic and order-theoretic axioms, by manipulating finite presentations of
generating systems. However, this algorithm is not the focus of the present
paper: We defer all computational questions to the forthcoming companion paper

[
14
]
.

Instead, an important point of our paper is that finitely presented closures

\mathop{\mathrm{clsemi}_{A}}(\Omega^{0})
arise in a more general context, through the interplay of
the order-theoretic, algebraic, and analytic closure properties. Move
ensembles whose graphs are connected open sets extend to open rectangles
already in the joined semigroup (
4.1
).
Our key theorem using the analytic properties is

Theorem 7.3
: Rectangles appear in the closure
whenever there is a convergent sequence of moves.
(In part I of our series, we have observed a glimpse of this phenomenon
already, in a specific arithmetic context
.)
Empirically, for all
families of piecewise linear minimal valid functions in the literature
(see
[
17
]
for an electronic compendium), even if the
breakpoints are irrational, the closure has a finite presentation. This
includes the function
bhk_irrational

, which we mentioned
above. Again, we defer questions regarding the computation of this closure,
which then needs to use the additional axioms, to our forthcoming
paper
[
14
]
.

1.6.2.
Result: Precise description of the space of equivariant perturbations

Under the above assumptions, the finite presentation of
\mathop{\mathrm{clsemi}_{A}}(\Omega^{0})

allows us to read off a precise description of the space of
equivariant perturbations as a direct sum decomposition of vector subspaces
(
Theorem 10.6
).

One component in the decomposition is a finite-dimensional space, consisting
of (possibly discontinuous) piecewise linear functions. In contrast to the
grid-based algorithm, the set of breakpoints of these functions is not fixed,
but it is computed by our algorithm. The finite-dimensional space is then
described by a system of finitely many linear equations
(
10.3
).

Then, for each of the finitely many
uncovered components
(defined in

section 10
), there is a component that is an
infinite-dimensional space isomorphic to the space
of Lipschitz functions on a compact interval that vanish on the boundary.
More specifically, our algorithm computes an open interval
D
, the

fundamental domain
, on which we take the space of Lipschitz
functions
\tilde{\pi}
that vanish on the boundary
\partial D
. Additionally
there are finitely many moves
\gamma_{j}|_{D}
with pairwise disjoint images

\gamma_{j}(D)
that together extend the functions equivariantly to the whole
uncovered component. Outside of the component, the functions in this space
are zero. This is
Theorem 10.4
.

This description of the space strengthens previous results.
The method of Part I
[
3
]
, described in

subsection 1.5
, guarantees to
construct a piecewise linear effective perturbation if the space is nontrivial;
but it does not provide a complete description of the space.
A theorem regarding direct sum decomposition appeared in
[
5
, Theorem
3.14]
, but it is limited to the grid case.

We remark that the precise description of the perturbation space of a minimal
function
\pi
enables us to strengthen (
lift
) it by constructing a
direction in the space of effective perturbations. By our theorem, the
problem of finding such a direction decomposes into subproblems; one finite-dimensional,
the others independent variational problems over Lipschitz functions.

1.6.3.
Computational implications: Grid-free algorithms, natural
proofs

We only sketch the computational implications of the present paper because we
will elaborate on them in our companion paper. The inverse semigroup theory
lays the foundation for grid-free algorithms for minimal valid functions,
including automated extremality tests, which are detached from the finite
group problem. A grid-free test is faster for functions whose breakpoints are
rational numbers with huge denominators; and it enables computations for
functions with irrational breakpoints. More importantly, the grid-free
algorithms can give natural extremality proofs, similar to the general proof
pattern of extremality proofs in the published literature. In this way, the
grid-free algorithms enable automated extremality proofs for smoothly
parameterized families of extreme functions, as described in

[
18
]
.

2.
Translation and reflection moves. Their algebraic and
order-theoretic structure

2.1.
Group
\Gamma({\mathbb{R}})
of unrestricted translations
\tau_{t}
and
reflections
\rho_{r}
, character
\chi

Definition 2.0
.

For a point
r\in{\mathbb{R}}
, define the (unrestricted)
reflection

\rho_{r}\colon{\mathbb{R}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
,
x\mapsto r-x
. For a vector
t\in{\mathbb{R}}
, define
the (unrestricted)
translation

\tau_{t}\colon{\mathbb{R}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
,
x\mapsto x+t
.

The set

\Gamma({\mathbb{R}})=\{\,\rho_{r},\tau_{t}\mid r\in{\mathbb{R}},\;t\in{\mathbb{R}}\,\}
of all translations and reflections, with the operations
of function composition
\circ
and inverse
\cdot^{-1}
, has the structure of
a group.
It is a subgroup of
the group
\Aff({\mathbb{R}})
of regular affine transformations of
{\mathbb{R}}
.

To denote an element that can be either a translation or a
reflection, we will usually use the letter
\gamma
.
To recover whether an element
\gamma
is a translation or a reflection,
we assign a
character

\chi(\rho_{r})=-1
to every reflection and

\chi(\tau_{t})=+1
to every translation.
The map
\gamma\mapsto\chi(\gamma)

is a group character, i.e., a homomorphism, so compositions of elements follow
the rule

(2.1)

\chi(\gamma_{1}\circ\gamma_{2})=\chi(\gamma_{1})\cdot\chi(\gamma_{2}).

2.2.
Restricted moves

\gamma|_{D}\in{}\Gamma^{\subseteq}({\mathbb{R}})
as partial bijections of
{\mathbb{R}}

As we mentioned in the introduction, compared to

[
3
]
, where finitely generated subgroups
of
\Gamma({\mathbb{R}})
were used for the grid-based extremality test algorithm, in this
paper we develop a more detailed theory using restricted moves with domains.
Our terminology is based on the monograph

[
22
]
on inverse semigroups.

We begin by restricting translations and reflections
\gamma\in\Gamma({\mathbb{R}})
to open
interval domains
D\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}
.

Definition 2.0
.

Let
\gamma\in\Gamma({\mathbb{R}})
be a translation or reflection, and let
D\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}
be an open interval
or the empty set.

(1)

The
move

\gamma|_{D}
is the partial function with domain
D

and image
\gamma(D)
, defined by
\gamma|_{D}(x)=\gamma(x)
for

x\in D
.

(2)

The
character
of
\gamma|_{D}
is the character of
\gamma
.

(3)

Two moves
\gamma_{1}|_{D_{1}},\gamma_{2}|_{D_{2}}
with open interval
domains
D_{1},D_{2}
are
equal
if

\gamma_{1}=\gamma_{2}
and
D_{1}=D_{2}
. A move with an open interval
domain is not
equal
to a move with an empty domain.
We identify all translations with empty domain and denote this object by

\tau|_{\emptyset}
. Likewise, we identify all reflections with empty domain
and denote this object by
\rho|_{\emptyset}
. The empty translation and the
empty reflection are not
equal
; they are distinct objects with

\chi(\tau|_{\emptyset})=+1
and
\chi(\rho|_{\emptyset})=-1
.

(4)

The set of all moves is denoted by
\Gamma^{\subseteq}({\mathbb{R}})
.

2.2.1.
Remark on the relation to pseudogroups

Inverse semigroups of partial homeomorphisms between open subsets of a
topological space are known as
pseudogroups

[
22
, section 1.2]
. However, our theory differs in the
following ways: (1) We only allow open intervals (and the empty set) as
domains of the partial functions, rather than arbitrary open subsets. The
reason for our choice will become clear in
section 5
, where we
will use moves to describe systems of functional equations.
(2) Less importantly, we have two empty moves, one for each possible
character, rather than a unique empty move.

2.3.
Graphs of moves

We find it convenient to describe the graphs of moves. The
graph
of

\gamma|_{D}
is the set

\graph(\gamma|_{D})=\{\,(x,y)\in{\mathbb{R}}\times{\mathbb{R}}\mid x\in D,\;\gamma(x)=y\,\}.

Figures showing the graphs have already appeared in

Figure 2
and

Figure 3
. To emphasize that the domains of all
nonempty moves are open intervals, we decorate the endpoints of the moves by
hollow circles, indicating that the endpoints are not part of the graphs.

2.4.
Restriction partial order
\subseteq
on moves

The set of all moves comes with a natural partial order.

Definition 2.0
.

\gamma_{1}|_{D_{1}}
is a
restriction
of
\gamma_{2}|_{D_{2}}
, denoted
\gamma_{1}|_{D_{1}}\subseteq\gamma_{2}|_{D_{2}}
, if
D_{1}\subseteq D_{2}
,
\chi(\gamma_{1})=\chi(\gamma_{2})
, and
\gamma_{1}(x)=\gamma_{2}(x)
for
x\in D_{1}
.

Thus, in this partial order, translations and reflections are incomparable. We have

\tau|_{\emptyset}\subseteq\tau_{t}|_{D}
for all translations and likewise

\rho|_{\emptyset}\subseteq\rho_{r}|_{D}
.

Definition 2.0
.

Given
\gamma|_{D}
and an open interval (or empty set)
D^{\prime}\subseteq D
, the

restriction
of
\gamma|_{D}
to
D^{\prime}
is the move

(\gamma|_{D})\big|_{D^{\prime}}=\gamma|_{D^{\prime}}
. Given an open interval (or empty set)

I^{\prime}\subseteq\gamma(D)
, the
corestriction
of
\gamma|_{D}
to
I^{\prime}
is
the move
{}_{I^{\prime}}\big|(\gamma|_{D})=\gamma|_{D\cap\gamma^{-1}(I^{\prime})}
.

2.5.
Inverse semigroup structure
(\Gamma^{\subseteq}({\mathbb{R}}),\circ,\cdot^{-1})

Let
\gamma_{1}|_{D_{1}}
and
\gamma_{2}|_{D_{2}}
be two moves. As noted in the
introduction, their composition

\gamma_{2}|_{D_{2}}\circ\gamma_{1}|_{D_{1}}
is defined as

\gamma_{2}\circ\gamma_{1}|_{D_{1}\cap\gamma_{1}^{-1}(D_{2})}

(
Figure 2
). The domain of this partial
bijection is either an open interval or the empty set; so it is again a move.
It is clear that the composition operation
\circ
is associative. Hence the
moves form a semigroup
(\Gamma^{\subseteq}({\mathbb{R}}),\circ)
.

As we have noted already, a move
\gamma|_{D}
also has a (unique) inverse given by

(\gamma|_{D})^{-1}=\gamma^{-1}|_{\gamma(D)}

(
Figure 3
) satisfying the
laws (
1.10
)
(
Figure 4
). Hence the moves
form an inverse semigroup
(\Gamma^{\subseteq}({\mathbb{R}}),\circ,\cdot^{-1})
. Its
idempotent elements are exactly the

partial identities
, which are restrictions of the identity translation

\tau_{0}
to open intervals
D
together with the empty
translation
\tau|_{\emptyset}
. (The empty reflection is not idempotent; we
have
\rho|_{\emptyset}\circ\rho|_{\emptyset}=\tau|_{\emptyset}
.)

The inverse semigroup structure interacts with the restriction partial order
(
subsection 2.4
) as follows
[
22
, Proposition
1.1.4]
. If

\gamma|_{D^{\prime}}\subseteq\gamma|_{D}
, then

\gamma|_{D^{\prime}}^{-1}\subseteq\gamma|_{D}^{-1}
; moreover, this restriction can
be expressed as a composition with an idempotent:

\gamma|_{D^{\prime}}=(\gamma|_{D})\big|_{D^{\prime}}=\gamma|_{D}\circ\tau_{0}|_{D^{\prime}}
.
Finally, if
\gamma_{i}|_{D^{\prime}_{i}}\subseteq\gamma_{i}|_{D_{i}}
for
i=1,2
, then

\gamma_{2}|_{D^{\prime}_{2}}\circ\gamma_{1}|_{D^{\prime}_{1}}\subseteq\gamma_{2}|_{D_{2}}\circ\gamma_{1}|_{D_{1}}
.

.

(3.1)

\begin{gathered}\Bigl\{\,\gamma|_{D}\mathrel{\Big|}\text{$D\subseteq\bigcup_{I\in\mathfrak{I}}I$, where $\gamma|_{I}\in\Omega$ for $I\in\mathfrak{I}$, $D$ empty or open interval}\,\Bigr\}.\end{gathered}

Proof.

This set clearly satisfies (

) and

∎

3.1.3.
Presentation by the set

of maximal elements

Definition 3.0
.

For a move ensemble
\Omega

denote the set of maximal

Lemma 3.0
.

A join-closed move ensemble
\Omega^{\vee}
is equal to the restriction closure and to the

(3.2)

\Omega^{\vee}=\mathop{\mathrm{restrict}}(\maxdom(\Omega^{\vee}))=\mathop{\mathrm{join}}(\maxdom(\Omega^{\vee}))

Proof.

Let
\gamma|_{D}\in\Omega^{\vee}

Move ensembles as set-valued maps

Definition 3.0
.

Let

3.3.
Graphs
\graph(\Omega),\graph_{+}(\Omega),\graph_{-}(\Omega)
of move ensembles
\Omega

We introduced graphs of moves in

.
For a move ensemble
\Omega
we define the
translation moves graph

\displaystyle\graph_{+}(\Omega)

\displaystyle=\bigcup\bigl\{\,\graph(\gamma|_{D})\mathrel{\big|}\gamma|_{D}\in\Omega\text{ and }\chi(\gamma)=1\,\bigr\},

consisting of line segments
with slopes

, and the
reflection moves graph

\displaystyle\graph_{-}(\Omega)

\displaystyle=\bigcup\bigl\{\,\graph(\gamma|_{D})\mathrel{\big|}\gamma|_{D}\in\Omega\text{ and }\chi(\gamma)=-1\,\bigr\},

consisting of line segments with slopes

.
The
graph
of
\Omega
is

\graph(\Omega)=\graph_{+}(\Omega)\cup\graph_{-}(\Omega).

We also define the

,

\graph_{\pm}(\Omega)=\graph_{+}(\Omega)\cap\graph_{-}(\Omega).

The map
\Omega\mapsto(\graph_{+}(\Omega),\graph_{-}(\Omega))
becomes an
injection if restricted to the join-closed move
ensembles

. Hence these pairs of graphs faithfully
represent all join-closed move ensembles. (In our
figures showing these graphs, we superimpose the translation graph (blue) and
reflection graph (red).)

We can go back from graphs to ensembles using the following notation.

Definition 3.0
.

Let

. We define the (join-closed) move ensembles

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathrm{moves}}\nolimits_{+}(O)

\displaystyle=\bigl\{\,\tau_{t}|_{D}\mathrel{\big|}\graph(\tau_{t}|_{D})\subset O,\;D\text{ an open interval or empty}\,\bigr\},

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathrm{moves}}\nolimits_{-}(O)

\displaystyle=\bigl\{\,\rho_{r}|_{D}\mathrel{\big|}\graph(\rho_{r}|_{D})\subset O,\;D\text{ an open interval or empty}\,\bigr\},

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathrm{moves}}\nolimits(O)

\displaystyle=\bigl\{\,\gamma|_{D}\mathrel{\big|}\graph(\gamma|_{D})\subset O,\;D\text{ an open interval or empty}\,\bigr\}.

\mathop{\mathrm{moves}}\nolimits(O)=\mathop{\mathrm{moves}}\nolimits_{+}(O)\cup\mathop{\mathrm{moves}}\nolimits_{-}(O)
.

4.1.
Move semigroups
\Gamma
; move semigroups
\mathop{\mathrm{i{}semi}}(\Omega)

\Omega

Definition 4.0
.

A move ensemble
\Gamma
is a
move semigroup
(or, an
inverse subsemigroup
of

\Gamma^{\subseteq}({\mathbb{R}})
)
if it satisfies the following axioms:

(composition)

\displaystyle\gamma^{\prime}|_{D^{\prime}}\circ\gamma|_{D}\in\Gamma\text{ for all }\gamma|_{D},\gamma^{\prime}|_{D^{\prime}}\in\Gamma,

(inv)

\displaystyle(\gamma|_{D})^{-1}\in\Gamma\text{ for all }\gamma|_{D}\in\Gamma.

Definition 4.0
.

For a move ensemble
\Omega
, the
move semigroup

\mathop{\mathrm{i{}semi}}(\Omega)

generated by
\Omega
is the smallest move semigroup containing

\Omega
.

Definition 4.0
.

A move semigroup
\Gamma
is
finitely generated
if there exists a
finite set
\Omega
such that
\Gamma=\mathop{\mathrm{i{}semi}}(\Omega)
.

Lemma 4.0
.

Let
{\Omega}^{\mathrm{inv}}
be a move ensemble satisfying
(
inv
).
Then
\mathop{\mathrm{i{}semi}}({\Omega}^{\mathrm{inv}})
is the set of all finite compositions

\gamma^{k}|_{D_{k}}\circ\dots\circ\gamma^{1}|_{D_{1}}
of moves
\gamma^{i}|_{D_{i}}\in{\Omega}^{\mathrm{inv}}
.

Remark 4.0
.

Since the domains of moves in
\Omega
are empty or open intervals, any move

\gamma|_{D}\in\mathop{\mathrm{i{}semi}}(\Omega)
also has a domain
D
that is empty or an open
interval. If
\gamma|_{D}\in\Omega
, then the idempotent

(\gamma|_{D})^{-1}\circ\gamma|_{D}=\tau_{0}|_{D}
is an element of
\mathop{\mathrm{i{}semi}}(\Omega)
. The
inverse semigroup generated by the empty set is the empty set.

4.2.
Move semigroups and joins; joined move semigroups

\mathop{\mathrm{jsemi}}(\Omega)
generated by ensembles
\Omega

Move semigroups generated by joined ensembles are not automatically
join-closed.
On the other hand, joining does preserve the semigroup properties.

Lemma 4.0
.

Let
\Gamma
be a move semigroup.
Then the joined ensemble
\mathop{\mathrm{join}}(\Gamma)
is a move semigroup.
In particular, for a move ensemble

\mathop{\mathrm{join}}(\mathop{\mathrm{i{}semi}}(\Omega))=\mathop{\mathrm{i{}semi}}(\mathop{\mathrm{join}}(\mathop{\mathrm{i{}semi}}(\Omega))).

Proof.

Let
\gamma|_{D},\gamma^{\prime}|_{D^{\prime}}\in\mathop{\mathrm{join}}(\Gamma)
.
We first show that
\mathop{\mathrm{join}}(\Gamma)
satisfies the axiom (
composition
).
By equation (

),
there exist collections

and
\mathfrak{I}^{\prime}
of open intervals, such that

D\subseteq\bigcup_{I\in\mathfrak{I}}I
,
D^{\prime}\subseteq\bigcup_{I^{\prime}\in\mathfrak{I}^{\prime}}I^{\prime}

\gamma|_{I},\gamma^{\prime}|_{I^{\prime}}\in\Gamma
for all
I\in\mathfrak{I},I^{\prime}\in\mathfrak{I}^{\prime}

We know that

\gamma^{\prime}|_{I^{\prime}}\circ\gamma|_{I}=(\gamma^{\prime}\circ\gamma)|_{\gamma^{-1}(I^{\prime})\cap I}\in\Gamma,\text{ for all }I\in\mathfrak{I}\text{ and }I^{\prime}\in\mathfrak{I}^{\prime},

since
\Gamma
satisfies (

), and that

\gamma^{-1}(D^{\prime})\cap D\subseteq\gamma^{-1}\bigl(\bigcup_{I^{\prime}\in\mathfrak{I}^{\prime}}I^{\prime}\bigr)\cap\bigl(\bigcup_{I\in\mathfrak{I}}I\bigr)=\bigcup_{I\in\mathfrak{I},\,I^{\prime}\in\mathfrak{I}^{\prime}}\bigl(\gamma^{-1}(I^{\prime})\cap I\bigr).

\gamma^{\prime}|_{D^{\prime}}\circ\gamma|_{D}=(\gamma^{\prime}\circ\gamma)|_{\gamma^{-1}(D^{\prime})\cap D}\in\mathop{\mathrm{join}}(\Gamma)

We know that

We conclude that

5.1.

\Omega

Move ensembles encode a system of functional equations as follows.

Definition 5.0
.

Let
\Omega
be a move ensemble and let

be a function.

(1)

We say that
\theta
is

\Omega
-equivariant
(in short,

\theta

respects

\Omega

\gamma|_{D}\in\Omega
there exists a constant
c_{\gamma|_{D}}^{\theta}
such
that

(5.1)

\theta(\gamma|_{D}(x))=\chi(\gamma)\theta(x)+c^{\theta}_{\gamma|_{D}}\quad\text{ for }x\in D,

where
\chi(\gamma)=\pm 1
is the character of
\gamma
.

(2)

If all constants
c_{\gamma|_{D}}^{\theta}
can be chosen to be zero,
then we say that
\theta
is
\Omega
-equivariant
(or,

equivariant under the action of
\Omega
).

Throughout the paper, we will be working with affinely
\Omega
-equivariant
functions. At the very end, in
section 10
, an
important space of
\Omega
-equivariant functions will appear.

Remark 5.0
.

It now becomes clear why singletons
\{x\}
are not allowed as
the domain
D
of a move. The functional
equation (
5.1
) would degenerate to a
single equation with an independent constant
c^{\theta}_{\gamma|_{\{x\}}}
.
The equation and the constant can be eliminated from the system.

Some trivial relations between the constants
c^{\theta}_{\gamma|_{D}}
are
induced by the restriction partial order on moves
(
subsection 2.4
). If
\emptyset\neq D\subset D^{\prime}
,
thus
\gamma|_{D}\subseteq\gamma|_{D^{\prime}}
and
D\neq\emptyset
, then
necessarily
c^{\theta}_{\gamma|_{D}}=c^{\theta}_{\gamma|_{D^{\prime}}}
.

Thus it is natural to work with restriction-closed ensembles, as defined in

subsubsection 3.1.1
.

Lemma 5.0
.

For a space
\Theta
of functions, we denote by

the
set of affinely
\Omega
-equivariant functions in

.
If
\Theta
is a vector space, then so is

.

Proof.

Let
\theta_{1},\theta_{2}\in\Theta
and

. Let

\theta=a_{1}\theta_{1}+a_{2}\theta_{2}
. Then
\theta\in\Theta
.
Moreover, let
c^{\theta_{1}}_{\gamma|_{D}}
for
\gamma|_{D}\in\Omega
and

c^{\theta_{2}}_{\gamma|_{D}}
for
\gamma|_{D}\in\Omega
be the families of
constants that satisfy (

) for

\theta_{1}
and
\theta_{2}
, respectively. Then

c^{\theta}_{\gamma|_{D}}=a_{1}c^{\theta_{1}}_{\gamma|_{D}}+a_{2}c^{\theta_{2}}_{\gamma|_{D}}

) for

.

6.
Kaleidoscopic joined ensembles and bounded functions. Finite
presentations by moves and components

6.1.
Cauchy–Pexider functional equation
f(x)+g(y)=h(x+y)

Recall from
subsection 5.1
that move ensembles encode systems
of functional equations. We now bring a first result on functional equations
to use. The following result on the Cauchy–Pexider functional equation on
bounded domains appeared in
[
5
, Theorem 4.3]
. Here we state it
for functions of a single real variable. It is a variant of the
Gomory–Johnson interval lemma, which has been used throughout the extreme
functions literature. Note that it requires a weak assumption regarding the
function space. Boundedness is sufficient; see
[
5
]
for a more
detailed discussion.

Lemma 6.0

(Convex additivity domain lemma)
.

Let
f,g,h\colon{\mathbb{R}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
be bounded functions and let
E\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
be
open, convex, and bounded. Suppose that

f(x)+g(y)=h(x+y)\quad\text{ for all }(x,y)\in E.

Define the projections

p_{1}(x,y)=x,\quad p_{2}(x,y)=y,\quad p_{3}(x,y)=x+y

as functions from
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
to
{\mathbb{R}}
.
Then
f,g,h
are affine with the same slopes on the domains
p_{1}(E),p_{2}(E),p_{3}(E)
, respectively.

6.2.
Kaleidoscopic move ensembles

When we are only interested in
bounded
functions that respect a move
ensemble
\Omega
, then it follows from
6.0
that we can
replace
\Omega
by a move ensemble
\Omega^{\boxtimes}
with more
convenient properties.

Lemma 6.0
.

Let
\theta\colon{\mathbb{R}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
be a bounded function.
Let
D,I\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}
be open intervals. The following are equivalent:

(1)

\theta
respects
\mathop{\mathrm{moves}}\nolimits_{+}(D\times I)
,

(2)

\theta
respects
\mathop{\mathrm{moves}}\nolimits_{-}(D\times I)
,

(3)

\theta
respects
\mathop{\mathrm{moves}}\nolimits(D\times I)
,

(4)

\theta
is affine on
D
and
I
with the same slope.

Proof.

We first show that (1) implies (
4
).
By assumption, the function
\theta
satisfies
equation (
5.1
) for all
\tau_{t}|_{D_{t}}
,
where
t\in\{\,y-x\mid x\in D,\;y\in I\,\}
and

D_{t}=\{\,x\in D\mid x+t\in I\,\}
. Thus, there exists a function

c\colon I+(-D)\to{\mathbb{R}}
such that

\theta(x+t)=\theta(x)+c(t)\qquad\text{for all $(x,x+t)\in D\times I$}.

The function
c
is bounded because
\theta
is.
Then, by Lemma
6.0
with
f=h=\theta
and
g=c
, we have that

\theta
affine on
D
and
I
with the same slope.
The proofs that each of (2) and (3)
implies (
4
) are similar; we omit them.

Now we show that (
4
) implies (1).
Fix
t=y-x
for some
x\in D,y\in I
.
Since
\theta
is affine on
D
and
I
with the same slope, there exist scalars
a,b,b^{\prime}
such that
\theta(x)=a\cdot x+b
for all
x\in D
and
\theta(x)=a\cdot x+b^{\prime}
for all
x\in I
. But then for all
x\in D
such that
x+t\in I
, we have that
\theta(x+t)-\theta(x)=a\cdot t
, which is constant. Therefore,
\theta
respects
\tau_{t}|_{D_{t}}
.
Again the proofs that (
4
) also implies (2) and (3) is
similar and we omit them.
∎

Motivated by these results, we make the following definitions.

Definition 6.0
.

A move ensemble
\Omega^{\boxtimes}
is a
kaleidoscopic joined ensemble
if it satisfies
(
restrict
), (
continuation
), and the following axiom:

(kaleido)

\begin{gathered}\text{ for open intervals }D,I\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}\\
\mathop{\mathrm{moves}}\nolimits_{+}(D\times I)\subseteq\Omega^{\boxtimes}\text{ if and only if }\mathop{\mathrm{moves}}\nolimits_{-}(D\times I)\subseteq\Omega^{\boxtimes}.\end{gathered}

6.3.
Covered intervals, connected covered components

Definition 6.0
.

For a kaleidoscopic joined ensemble
\Omega^{\boxtimes}
and an open interval
D

such that
\mathop{\mathrm{moves}}\nolimits(D\times D)\subseteq\Omega^{\boxtimes}
, we say
that
D
is a

in
\Omega^{\boxtimes}
.

Let
\Gamma^{\boxtimes}
be a kaleidoscopic joined move semigroup. For two open intervals

D_{1},D_{2}
, if

\mathop{\mathrm{moves}}\nolimits\bigl((D_{1}\cup D_{2})\times(D_{1}\cup D_{2})\bigr)\subseteq\Gamma^{\boxtimes},

then we
say that both
D_{1}
and
D_{2}
are covered intervals in the same

connected covered component
of
\Gamma^{\boxtimes}
. (Here the word
“connected” does not refer to the topology of
{\mathbb{R}}
, in contrast
to
subsection 4.3
.)
It follows from

4.1
that this is an equivalence relation.
However, we want to define the notion of a

connected covered component
also for kaleidoscopic joined ensembles

\Omega^{\boxtimes}
that are not semigroups. In this case there is no
equivalence relation (transitivity fails), but we still use the word
“components” in the following definition.

Definition 6.0
.

Let
\Omega^{\boxtimes}
be a kaleidoscopic joined ensemble. Let
C
be an open
set such that
\mathop{\mathrm{moves}}\nolimits(C\times C)\subseteq\Omega^{\boxtimes}
.
Then
C
is called a
connected covered component
of

\Omega^{\boxtimes}
. Any two covered intervals

are said
to be
connected
by the component
C
.

The connected covered components of
\Omega^{\boxtimes}
are partially
ordered by set inclusion. The maximal elements in this partial order suffice
to describe all covered intervals.

Corollary 6.0
.

Let
\theta
be a bounded function. Suppose
\theta
respects
a kaleidoscopic joined ensemble
\Omega^{\boxtimes}
. Let
C
be a connected
covered component of
\Omega^{\boxtimes}
.
Then
\theta
is affine on all
open intervals in
C
with a common slope.

Proof.

Let

be open intervals. Then

, and hence
\theta
respects

\mathop{\mathrm{moves}}\nolimits(D\times I)
. By

6.0
(
4

\theta
is affine on

D
and

with the same slope.
∎

(Later in
section 10

connected uncovered components.)

7.
Limit-closed ensembles and continuous functions. Closed move semigroups

Let
A\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}
be an open set. We now consider the space

{\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{b}}({A})}
of bounded continuous functions on

.
For
{\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{b}}({A})}
, some notions of convergence of moves are natural
to study.

7.1.
Limit-closed move ensembles
\bar{\Omega}
; closures

\mathop{\mathrm{lim}}(\Omega)
,

\mathop{\mathrm{arblim}}(\Omega)

7.1.1.
Convergence of unrestricted moves

Definition 7.0
.

A sequence
\{\gamma^{i}\}_{i\in{\mathbb{N}}}\subseteq\Gamma({\mathbb{R}})
of
unrestricted moves
converges

(1)

to an unrestricted translation
\tau_{t}\in\Gamma({\mathbb{R}})
if all
but finitely many
\gamma^{i}
are translations
\tau_{t^{i}}
and
t^{i}\to t
.

(2)

to an unrestricted reflection
\rho_{r}\in\Gamma({\mathbb{R}})
if all
but finitely many
\gamma^{i}
are reflections
\rho_{r^{i}}
and
r^{i}\to r
.

7.1.2.
Limits closure

Definition 7.0
.

We define the
limits closure

\mathop{\mathrm{lim}}(\Omega)

of a moves
ensemble
\Omega
to be the smallest (by set inclusion) moves
ensemble
\bar{\Omega}
containing
\Omega
that satisfies the following axiom.

(lim)

\begin{gathered}\text{Let $D$ be an open interval.}\\
\text{If $\gamma^{i}\to\gamma$ and $\gamma^{i}|_{D}\in\bar{\Omega}$ for all $i$,}\ \text{then $\gamma|_{D}\in\bar{\Omega}$.}\end{gathered}

We note that the domain
D
is fixed for all moves in the sequence. Thus, the
limits closure will in general not satisfy (
continuation
) and (
inv
). Instead we can consider the following axiom.

Definition 7.0
.

Define
\mathop{\mathrm{arblim}}(\Omega)
to be the smallest moves ensemble
\bar{\Omega}
containing
\Omega

that satisfies the following axiom.

(arblim)

\begin{gathered}\text{If $\gamma^{i}\to\gamma$, $l^{i}\to l$, $u^{i}\to u$ and $\gamma^{i}|_{(l^{i},u^{i})}\in\bar{\Omega}$ for all
$i$,}\\
\text{then $\gamma|_{(l,u)}\in\bar{\Omega}$.}\end{gathered}

For our purposes, when considered together with (
continuation
), the
notions turn out to be equivalent.

Theorem 7.1
.

Let
\Omega^{\vee}
be a join-closed move ensemble.
Then

\mathop{\mathrm{join}}(\mathop{\mathrm{lim}}(\Omega^{\vee}))=\mathop{\mathrm{join}}(\mathop{\mathrm{arblim}}(\Omega^{\vee})).

Proof.

It is clear that
\mathop{\mathrm{lim}}(\Omega^{\vee})\subseteq\mathop{\mathrm{arblim}}(\Omega^{\vee})
, so it suffices to show that

(7.1)

\mathop{\mathrm{arblim}}(\Omega^{\vee})\subseteq\mathop{\mathrm{join}}(\mathop{\mathrm{lim}}(\Omega^{\vee})).

Let
\tau_{t}|_{(l,u)}\in\mathop{\mathrm{arblim}}(\Omega^{\vee})
. By (
arblim
),
there is a convergent sequence
\{\tau_{t^{i}}|_{(l^{i},u^{i})}\}_{i\in{\mathbb{N}}}
of
moves in
\Omega^{\vee}
such that
l^{i}\to l
,
u^{i}\to u
and
t^{i}\to t
. For every integer
j>\frac{2}{u-l}
, there exists a large integer
n_{j}

such that for any
i\geq n_{j}
, we have
l_{i}<l+\frac{1}{j}
and

u-\frac{1}{j}<u_{i}
. Since
\Omega^{\vee}
satisfies (
continuation
),

\tau_{t^{i}}|_{D_{j}}\in\Omega^{\vee}
for any
i\geq n_{j}
, where
D_{j}:=(l+\frac{1}{j},u-\frac{1}{j})
. Since
t_{i}\to t
, we have
\tau_{t}|_{D_{j}}\in\mathop{\mathrm{lim}}(\Omega^{\vee})
for every
j
, hence
\tau_{t}|_{(l,u)}\in\mathop{\mathrm{join}}(\mathop{\mathrm{lim}}(\Omega^{\vee}))
. We showed that (
7.1
)
holds for translations. The proof for reflections is similar.
∎

Theorem 7.2
.

Let
\Omega^{\vee}
be a join-closed move ensemble.
The following are equivalent.

(1)

\Omega^{\vee}
satisfies (
lim
).

(2)

\Omega^{\vee}
satisfies (
arblim
).

The proof is essentially the same and we omit it.

7.1.3.
Respecting limits

Lemma 7.2

(Limits)
.

Let
D
be an open interval and let
\theta
be continuous on
D
. If there
exists a sequence
\gamma^{i}\to\gamma
such that
\theta

respects
\gamma^{i}|_{D}
for all
i
, then
\theta
also respects
\gamma|_{D}
.

Proof.

We prove the lemma for a sequence
t_{i}\to t
such that
\theta
respects
the translations
\tau_{t_{i}}|_{D}
for all
i
. We will show that
\theta

also respects
\tau_{t}|_{D}
.

Since
\theta
is continuous on
D
,
\theta
is also continuous on
\tau_{t_{i}}(D)
for all
i
.
Fix
\bar{x}\in D
. Since
t_{i}\to t
, and
\bar{x}\in\intr(D)
since
D

is open, there exists an
\hat{i}
such that for all
i\geq\hat{i}
, we have

\bar{x}+t\in D+t_{i}
. Hence, for a neighborhood
N_{\bar{x}}
of
\bar{x}
,

\theta
is continuous in
N_{\bar{x}}+t
.
Now, for all
x\in N_{\bar{x}}
,

\theta(x+t)-\theta(x)=\lim_{t_{i}\to t}\theta(x+t_{i})-\theta(x)=\lim_{i\to\infty}c^{\theta}_{\tau_{t_{i}}|_{D}}.

Since the limit on the right-hand side is independent of
x
, we define

c^{\theta}_{\tau_{t}|_{N_{\bar{x}}}}
to be this limit. Thus,
\theta
respects

\tau_{t}|_{N_{\bar{x}}}
.

Now the connected open set
D
is covered by the open neighborhoods
N_{\bar{x}}
of each
\bar{x}\in D
. It follows that

c^{\theta}_{\tau_{t}|_{N_{\bar{x}}}}=c^{\theta}_{\tau_{t}|_{N_{\bar{x}^{\prime}}}}
for
all
\bar{x},\bar{x}^{\prime}\in D
. Therefore,
\theta
respects
\tau_{t}|_{D}
.
Moreover,
\theta
is continuous on
\tau_{t}|_{D}
.

The proof for a sequence of reflections is the same.
∎

Limit-closed move semigroups

Lemma 7.2
.

Let
\Gamma
be a move semigroup. Then
\mathop{\mathrm{arblim}}(\Gamma)
is also a move

Proof.

It is clear that
\mathop{\mathrm{arblim}}(\Gamma)
satisfies (
inv
), as
\Gamma
satisfies (
inv
). We now show that
\mathop{\mathrm{arblim}}(\Gamma)
satisfies (
composition
). Let
\gamma_{1}|_{D_{1}},\gamma_{2}|_{D_{2}}\in\mathop{\mathrm{arblim}}(\Gamma)
such that
\gamma_{1}|_{D_{1}}\circ\gamma_{2}|_{D_{2}}
is not an empty move.
\gamma_{1}|_{D_{1}}
and
\gamma_{2}|_{D_{2}}
are the (
arblim
) of sequences of moves
\{\gamma_{1}^{i}|_{D_{1}^{i}}\}_{i\in{\mathbb{N}}}
and
\{\gamma_{2}^{i}|_{D_{2}^{i}}\}_{i\in{\mathbb{N}}}
in
\Gamma
. Since
\Gamma
satisfies (
composition
),
\gamma^{i}|_{D^{i}}:=(\gamma_{1}^{i}|_{D_{1}^{i}})\circ(\gamma_{2}^{i}|_{D_{2}^{i}})\in\Gamma
for every

. The (
arblim
) of the sequence
\{\gamma^{i}|_{D^{i}}\}_{i\in{\mathbb{N}}}
is
\gamma_{1}|_{D_{1}}\circ\gamma_{2}|_{D_{2}}

\gamma_{1}|_{D_{1}}\circ\gamma_{2}|_{D_{2}}\in\mathop{\mathrm{arblim}}(\Gamma)
. This show that
\mathop{\mathrm{arblim}}(\Gamma)
is a semigroup.
∎

Lemma 7.2
.

Let
\Gamma^{\vee}
be a join-closed semigroup. Then
\mathop{\mathrm{join}}(\mathop{\mathrm{lim}}(\Gamma^{\vee}))=\mathop{\mathrm{join}}(\mathop{\mathrm{arblim}}(\Gamma^{\vee}))

Proof.

It follows from
7.2
,
4.0
and
Theorem 7.1
.
∎

Theorem 7.3

(Limits imply components)
.

Let
\Gamma^{\vee}
be a join-closed move semigroup. Assume that
\gamma|_{D}
is the
limit move (in the sense of
\mathop{\mathrm{lim}}
or
\mathop{\mathrm{arblim}}
)
of a sequence
\{\gamma^{i}|_{D^{i}}\}_{i\in{\mathbb{N}}}

\Gamma^{\vee}

\gamma^{i}\neq\gamma

. Let

(1)

\gamma

\mathop{\mathrm{moves}}\nolimits_{+}((D\cup I)\times(D\cup I))\subseteq\mathop{\mathrm{join}}(\mathop{\mathrm{lim}}(\Gamma^{\vee}))

(2)

If

\mathop{\mathrm{moves}}\nolimits_{-}((D\times I)\cup(I\times D)),\mathop{\mathrm{moves}}\nolimits_{+}((D\times D)\cup(I\times I))\subseteq\mathop{\mathrm{join}}(\mathop{\mathrm{lim}}(\Gamma^{\vee})).

Lemma 7.3

7.2.3.

Lemma 7.3

(Extend moves by continuity)

Applied to the simple case of

7.3.

Now all axioms that we have introduced above come together.

Definition 7.3
.

A

is a limits-closed extension-closed
kaleidoscopic joined move
semigroup, i.e., a move ensemble that satisfies all the following axioms:
(
composition
), (
inv
), (
continuation
), (
restrict
), (
extendA
), (

), and (
kaleido
).

Definition 7.3
.

Let
\Omega
be a move ensemble with
\dom(\Omega),\im(\Omega)\subseteq A
.
We define the
closed move semigroup

\mathop{\mathrm{clsemi}_{A}}(\Omega)
generated by

(or just

of

) to be the smallest (by set inclusion) closed move semigroup
containing
\Omega
.

Lemma 7.3
.

Let
{\mathbb{L}}
be the family of closed move semigroups containing
\Omega
. Then

\mathop{\mathrm{clsemi}_{A}}(\Omega)=\bigcap{\mathbb{L}}=\bigcap_{\Omega^{\prime}\in{\mathbb{L}}}\Omega^{\prime}
.

Proof.

First of all,
\bigcap{\mathbb{L}}
contains
\Omega
.
We show that
\bigcap{\mathbb{L}}
is a closed move semigroup.
Note that each axiom is a closure property of a set
\Omega^{\prime}
of the
form: For all
(\Omega_{1},\Omega_{2})\in\mathbb{X}
, if
\Omega_{1}\subseteq\Omega^{\prime}
,
then
\Omega_{2}\subseteq\Omega^{\prime}
.
Now if
\Omega_{1}\subseteq\bigcap{\mathbb{L}}
, then

\Omega_{1}\subseteq\Omega^{\prime}
for all
\Omega^{\prime}\in{\mathbb{L}}
, and thus

\Omega_{2}\subseteq\Omega^{\prime}
for all
\Omega^{\prime}\in{\mathbb{L}}
. This implies

\Omega_{2}\subseteq\bigcap{\mathbb{L}}
.

On the other hand,
\bigcap{\mathbb{L}}
is contained in each of the
ensembles

and is therefore the smallest closed move

\Omega
.
∎

Remark 7.3
.

In contrast to
4.0
(regarding
(

) and (

) and the axioms of an inverse semigroup),
we do not know whether

can be obtained by
applying a finite sequence of closures with respect to the individual

(Main theorem on the moves closure)
.

Suppose
\theta
is bounded and continuous on

. If
\theta
respects a
move ensemble
\Omega
with
\dom(\Omega),\im(\Omega)\subseteq A
, then

\theta
respects the moves closure

.

Proof of
Theorem 7.4
.

Let
\theta|_{A}
denote the restriction of

to

. We consider the

of moves that

introduced in

. By definition,

Since, by assumption,

respects

, we have

.
By

,

is a

8.
The initial additive move ensemble

We will now apply the theory of the previous sections to compute the effective
perturbation spaces of minimal valid functions. Let

\pi
is
nonnegative,
{\mathbb{Z}}
-periodic, and satisfies

\pi(f)=1

(8.1)

\Delta\pi(x,y)=\pi(x)+\pi(y)-\pi(x+y)

\Delta\pi(x,y)\geq 0

Since

{\mathbb{Z}}

The initial move ensemble

We begin by defining an ensemble of

and

.

(1)

and

\displaystyle=\pi(x)+\pi(t)-\pi(x+t)=0

\displaystyle=\pi(x)+\pi(r-x)-\pi(r)=0

is any translation
\tau_{\bar{t}}|_{D}
,

\bar{t}\in(-1,1)
and

\displaystyle\quad\text{or}\quad\lim_{t\to\bar{t}^{-}}\Delta\pi(x,t)=0

\displaystyle\forall x\in D

or any reflection
\rho_{\bar{r}}|_{D}
, where
\bar{r}\in(0,2)
, and
\rho_{\bar{r}}(D)\subseteq A

(3)

An

in

.

The property of the moves

and image

will be preserved

10.7.
Equivariant perturbation space
\tilde{\Pi}^{\pi}_{\textrm{zero}({\mathcal{T}})}

Let
\tilde{\pi}_{\mathrm{zero}({\mathcal{T}})}\in\tilde{\Pi}^{\pi}_{\textrm{zero}({\mathcal{T}})}
be an equivariant perturbation of
\pi
.
By
6.0
(or
6.0
) and
9.0
,

\tilde{\pi}_{\mathrm{zero}({\mathcal{T}})}
is affine linear on all
covered intervals. By definition,

\tilde{\pi}_{\mathrm{zero}({\mathcal{T}})}(t)=\tilde{\pi}_{\mathrm{zero}({\mathcal{T}})}(t^{-})=\tilde{\pi}_{\mathrm{zero}({\mathcal{T}})}(t^{+})=0
for every

t\in\verts({\mathcal{T}})
, and
\partial C\subseteq\verts({\mathcal{T}})
. Therefore,
\tilde{\pi}_{\mathrm{zero}({\mathcal{T}})}
is zero on
\cl(C)
. If the set of uncovered points

U^{\prime}=\emptyset
, then
\tilde{\pi}_{\mathrm{zero}({\mathcal{T}})}\equiv 0
. Otherwise,
recall from
subsection 10.4
that
U^{\prime}
is partitioned into connected uncovered components
U_{1},U_{2},\dots,U_{l}
.
The following theorem gives the characterization of the projection of a perturbation
\tilde{\pi}_{\mathrm{zero}({\mathcal{T}})}
onto the space of functions with support contained in a connected uncovered component
U_{i}
.

Theorem 10.4

(Characterization of the equivariant perturbations
supported on an uncovered component)
.

Suppose that Assumptions
10.0
,
10.0
and
10.0
hold.
Let
U_{i}=\bigcup\gamma_{j}(D)
be a connected uncovered component, where
D
is the fundamental domain for
U_{i}
and
\gamma_{j}|_{D}\in\Omega|_{U^{\prime}}
(
j=1,\dots,p
). Let
\tilde{\pi}_{i}\colon{\mathbb{R}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
be a
{\mathbb{Z}}
-periodic function such that
\tilde{\pi}_{i}(x)=0
for
x\not\in U_{i}
. Then
\tilde{\pi}_{i}\in\tilde{\Pi}^{\pi}_{\mathrm{zero}({\mathcal{T}})}
if and only if

(1)

\tilde{\pi}_{i}
is Lipschitz continuous on
D
;

(2)

\tilde{\pi}_{i}(x)=\tilde{\pi}_{i}(x^{-})=\tilde{\pi}_{i}(x^{+})=0
for
x\in\partial D
;

(3)

\tilde{\pi}_{i}(x)=\chi(\gamma_{j})\tilde{\pi}_{i}(\gamma_{j}(x))
for
x\in D
,
j=1,\dots,p
.

Proof.

Let
\tilde{\pi}_{i}\in\tilde{\Pi}^{\pi}_{\textrm{zero}({\mathcal{T}})}
. Since
\pi
is continuous on
D
, by

9.0
,
\tilde{\pi}_{i}
is Lipschitz continuous on
{\mathbb{R}}
. Hence, the condition (i) holds. The condition (ii) is clearly satisfied, as
\tilde{\pi}_{i}(x)=\tilde{\pi}_{i}(x^{-})=\tilde{\pi}_{i}(x^{+})=0
for each
x\in\verts({\mathcal{T}})
. Since
\tilde{\pi}_{i}
respects
\Omega|_{U^{\prime}}
, the condition (iii) also holds.

Conversely, let
\tilde{\pi}_{i}\colon{\mathbb{R}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
be a
{\mathbb{Z}}
-periodic function such that
\tilde{\pi}_{i}(x)=0
for
x\not\in U_{i}
and the conditions (i)–(iii) hold.
It follows from (ii) that
\tilde{\pi}_{i}(x)=\tilde{\pi}_{i}(x^{-})=\tilde{\pi}_{i}(x^{+})=0
for
x\in\partial U_{i}
. Since
\tilde{\pi}_{i}(x)=0
for
x\not\in U_{i}
, we have

(10.13)

\tilde{\pi}_{i}(x)=\tilde{\pi}_{i}(x^{-})=\tilde{\pi}_{i}(x^{+})=0\text{ for }x\in[0,1]\setminus U_{i}\supseteq B^{\prime}\cup C.

We claim that
\tilde{\pi}_{i}
satisfies all the additivities (including the limits) that
\pi
has. Indeed, let
F
be a face of
\Delta{\mathcal{T}}
and let
(x,y)\in F
such that
\Delta\pi_{F}(x,y)=0
. We show that
(\Delta\tilde{\pi}_{i})_{F}(x,y)=0
by distinguishing the following three cases.

(a) If
(x,y)
is an additive vertex of
\Delta{\mathcal{T}}
, then by
Theorem 10.3
and (
10.13
), we have
(\Delta\tilde{\pi}_{i})_{F}(x,y)=0
.

(b) If
(x,y)
is contained in the relative interior of an edge
F^{\prime}
of

\Delta{\mathcal{T}}
, then
F^{\prime}\subseteq F
and
F^{\prime}
is an additive face of

\Delta{\mathcal{T}}
. Consider the move
\gamma|_{D^{\prime}}
associated with
F^{\prime}
. We have
either
D^{\prime}
and
\gamma(D^{\prime})\subseteq(0,1)\setminus U_{i}
, or
D^{\prime}
and

\gamma(D^{\prime})\subseteq U_{i}
. In the former case, the claim holds because
of (
10.13
); and in the latter case,
the structure of
\Delta{\mathcal{T}}
(
Theorem 10.3
)
implies that
\gamma|_{D^{\prime}}\in\Omega|_{U^{\prime}}
, and thus

(\Delta\tilde{\pi}_{i})_{F}(x,y)=0
by the condition (iii).

(c) If
(x,y)
is contained in the relative interior of a two-dimensional face
F^{\prime}
of
\Delta{\mathcal{T}}
, then
F^{\prime}=F
is a two-dimensional additive face of
\Delta{\mathcal{T}}
. We have
x,y,(x+y)\bmod 1\in C
, hence the claim follows from (
10.13
).

We showed that
\tilde{\pi}_{i}\in\bar{\Pi}^{E_{\bullet}}
, where
E_{\bullet}=E_{\bullet}(\pi,{\mathcal{T}})
is the family from the proof of
10.3
. Then,
[
19
, Theorem 3.1]
implies that
\tilde{\pi}_{i}\in\tilde{\Pi}^{\pi}
. Therefore,
\tilde{\pi}_{i}\in\tilde{\Pi}^{\pi}_{\textrm{zero}({\mathcal{T}})}
.
∎

For
i=1,\dots,l
, denote the space of functions
\tilde{\pi}_{i}
as in the
theorem by
\tilde{\Pi}^{\pi}_{U_{i}}
. It is independent of the choice of
fundamental domain.

Theorem 10.5

(Direct sum decomposition of equivariant perturbations by
uncovered components)
.

We have the direct sum decomposition
\tilde{\Pi}^{\pi}_{\mathrm{zero}({\mathcal{T}})}=\tilde{\Pi}^{\pi}_{U_{1}}\oplus\dots\oplus\tilde{\Pi}^{\pi}_{U_{l}}
, i.e.,
if
\tilde{\pi}\in\tilde{\Pi}^{\pi}_{\mathrm{zero}({\mathcal{T}})}
,
then it has a unique decomposition

\tilde{\pi}=\tilde{\pi}_{1}+\tilde{\pi}_{2}+\dots+\tilde{\pi}_{l}

such that
\tilde{\pi}_{i}\in\tilde{\Pi}^{\pi}_{U_{i}}
for
i=1,\dots,l
.

Proof.

Let
\tilde{\pi}\in\tilde{\Pi}^{\pi}_{\textrm{zero}({\mathcal{T}})}
.
For
i=1,2,\dots,l
, define
\tilde{\pi}_{i}:{\mathbb{R}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
,

\tilde{\pi}_{i}(x)=\tilde{\pi}(x)
if

and
\tilde{\pi}_{i}(x)=0
otherwise.
Then

(10.14)

\tilde{\pi}=\tilde{\pi}_{1}+\tilde{\pi}_{2}+\dots+\tilde{\pi}_{l},

where each
\tilde{\pi}_{i}
(

.
∎

Each of the component functions

10.8.
Decomposition theorem for effective perturbations

The following perturbation decomposition theorem, a generalization of
[
5
, Lemma 3.14]
without assuming
\pi
is continuous and
\verts({\mathcal{T}})=\frac{1}{q}{\mathbb{Z}}
, shows that the effective perturbation space
\tilde{\Pi}^{\pi}
is the direct sum of the finite-dimensional perturbation space
\tilde{\Pi}^{\pi}_{{\mathcal{T}}}
and the equivariant perturbation space
\tilde{\Pi}^{\pi}_{\mathrm{zero}({\mathcal{T}})}
.

Theorem 10.6

(Perturbation decomposition theorem)
.

Under Assumptions
10.0
,
10.0
and
10.0
,
for every effective perturbation
\tilde{\pi}\in\tilde{\Pi}^{\pi}
, there exist a unique finite-dimensional perturbation
\tilde{\pi}_{\mathcal{T}}\in\tilde{\Pi}^{\pi}_{\mathcal{T}}
and a unique equivariant perturbation
\tilde{\pi}_{\mathrm{zero}({\mathcal{T}})}\in\tilde{\Pi}^{\pi}_{\mathrm{zero}({\mathcal{T}})}
such that

\tilde{\pi}=\tilde{\pi}_{\mathcal{T}}+\tilde{\pi}_{\mathrm{zero}({\mathcal{T}})}.

Proof.

Let
\tilde{\pi}\in\tilde{\Pi}^{\pi}
be an effective perturbation. By
[
16
, Corollary 6.5]

, the limits
\tilde{\pi}(t^{-})
and
\tilde{\pi}(t^{+})
exist for every
t\in\verts({\mathcal{T}})
. Let
\tilde{\pi}_{\mathcal{T}}
be the unique piecewise linear function over
{\mathcal{T}}
such that
\tilde{\pi}_{\mathcal{T}}(t)=\tilde{\pi}(t)
,
\tilde{\pi}_{\mathcal{T}}(t^{-})=\tilde{\pi}(t^{-})
and
\tilde{\pi}_{\mathcal{T}}(t^{+})=\tilde{\pi}(t^{+})
for every
t\in\verts({\mathcal{T}})
. Define
\tilde{\pi}_{\mathrm{zero}({\mathcal{T}})}=\tilde{\pi}-\tilde{\pi}_{\mathcal{T}}
. Note that
\tilde{\pi}_{\mathcal{T}}
is the unique piecewise linear function over
{\mathcal{T}}
such that
\tilde{\pi}_{\mathrm{zero}({\mathcal{T}})}(t)=\tilde{\pi}_{\mathrm{zero}({\mathcal{T}})}(t^{-})=\tilde{\pi}_{\mathrm{zero}({\mathcal{T}})}(t^{+})=0
for every
t\in\verts({\mathcal{T}})
. It is left to show that
\tilde{\pi}_{\mathcal{T}},\tilde{\pi}_{\mathrm{zero}({\mathcal{T}})}\in\tilde{\Pi}^{\pi}
.

We first show that
\tilde{\pi}_{\mathcal{T}}\in\tilde{\Pi}^{\pi}
, by applying
10.3
. It suffices to show that
\tilde{\pi}_{\mathcal{T}}
satisfies condition (iii) of
10.3
. Let
(x,y)
be an additive vertex of a face
F\in\Delta{\mathcal{T}}
with
\Delta\pi_{F}(x,y)=0
. By
[
16
, Lemma 6.1]

,
\Delta\pi_{F}(x,y)=0
implies that
\Delta\tilde{\pi}_{F}(x,y)=0
. Since
(x,y)
is an additive vertex of
\Delta{\mathcal{T}}
,
Theorem 10.3
implies that
x,y,z\in B^{\prime}\cup C
, where
z=(x+y)\bmod 1
. We have
\tilde{\pi}_{\mathcal{T}}(t)=\tilde{\pi}(t)
,
\tilde{\pi}_{\mathcal{T}}(t^{-})=\tilde{\pi}(t^{-})
and
\tilde{\pi}_{\mathcal{T}}(t^{+})=\tilde{\pi}(t^{+})
for
t=x,y
or
z
, and hence
\Delta(\tilde{\pi}_{\mathcal{T}})_{F}(x,y)=\Delta\tilde{\pi}_{F}(x,y)=0
. Therefore,
\tilde{\pi}_{\mathcal{T}}\in\tilde{\Pi}^{\pi}
.

Since the vector space
\tilde{\Pi}^{\pi}
contains both
\tilde{\pi}
and
\tilde{\pi}_{\mathcal{T}}
, we obtain that
\tilde{\pi}_{\mathrm{zero}({\mathcal{T}})}=\tilde{\pi}-\tilde{\pi}_{\mathcal{T}}\in\tilde{\Pi}^{\pi}
.
∎

10.9.
Relation of the moves closure to the semigroup

\Gamma^{\mathrm{resp}}(\tilde{\Pi}^{\pi})
of respected moves

In this section, still under the assumptions from
subsection 10.1
,
we establish the relation between
\mathop{\mathrm{clsemi}_{A}}(\Omega^{0})
and two other
move semigroups:

(1)

the semigroup
\Gamma^{\mathrm{resp}}(\tilde{\Pi}^{\pi})
of moves respected by all
effective perturbation functions
\tilde{\pi}
,

(2)

the semigroup

\Gamma^{\mathrm{resp}}(\{\pi\}\cup\tilde{\Pi}^{\pi})=\Gamma^{\mathrm{resp}}(\pi+\tilde{\Pi}^{\pi})
of moves respected by
\pi
and its perturbations.

We already know from
9.0
that

(10.15)

\mathop{\mathrm{clsemi}_{A}}(\Omega^{0})\subseteq\Gamma^{\mathrm{resp}}(\pi+\tilde{\Pi}^{\pi})\subseteq\Gamma^{\mathrm{resp}}(\tilde{\Pi}^{\pi}).

In the case of an extreme function
\pi
, the space
\tilde{\Pi}^{\pi}
of
effective perturbations is trivial; and thus,

\Gamma^{\mathrm{resp}}(\tilde{\Pi}^{\pi})=\Gamma^{\subseteq}({\mathbb{R}})
.

More generally, whenever a function
\theta
is affine on intervals

D_{1},D_{2},\dots,D_{k}
with the same slope, then

\mathop{\mathrm{moves}}\nolimits((D_{1}\cup\dots\cup D_{k})\times(D_{1}\cup\dots\cup D_{k}))\subseteq\Gamma^{\mathrm{resp}}(\theta)
. Thus, we have
the following:

Lemma 10.6
.

Suppose the space
\tilde{\Pi}^{\pi}_{{\mathcal{T}}}
of finite-dimensional
perturbations is trivial.

(1)

Let
C
be the set of covered points. Then
\mathop{\mathrm{moves}}\nolimits(C\times C)\subseteq\Gamma^{\mathrm{resp}}(\tilde{\Pi}^{\pi})
.

(2)

Let
D_{1},\dots,D_{k}\subseteq C
be covered intervals on which

\pi
is affine with the same slope. Then
\mathop{\mathrm{moves}}\nolimits((D_{1}\cup\dots\cup D_{k})\times(D_{1}\cup\dots\cup D_{k}))\subseteq\Gamma^{\mathrm{resp}}(\pi+\tilde{\Pi}^{\pi})
.

.

.

Suppose the finite-dimensional perturbation space has a positive dimension.
Recall its description using slope variables
\tilde{s}_{i}^{c}
(for the

C_{i}
) and
\tilde{s}_{i}^{u}
(for the connected

) from

.

Consider these move ensembles restricted to the set

of uncovered points in

.
We have the following theorem.

Theorem 10.7
.

Under Assumptions
10.0
,

10.0
, and
10.0
, we have
that

(10.16)

\mathop{\mathrm{clsemi}_{A}}(\Omega^{0})|_{U}=\Gamma^{\mathrm{resp}}(\pi+\tilde{\Pi}^{\pi})|_{U}=\Gamma^{\mathrm{resp}}(\tilde{\Pi}^{\pi})|_{U}.

where
U
is the set of uncovered points in
(0,1)
.

Proof.

We use the notations of the present section. By (
10.15
), it suffices
to show that if the domain of a move
\gamma|_{D}\in\Gamma^{\mathrm{resp}}(\tilde{\Pi}^{\pi})
is contained in
U
, then
\gamma|_{D}\in\mathop{\mathrm{clsemi}_{A}}(\Omega^{0})
.

Recall that we can write an arbitrary connected uncovered component

in the form of

,
where
I
is the fundamental domain for
U_{i}
,
\gamma_{j}|_{I}\in\mathop{\mathrm{clsemi}_{A}}(\Omega^{0})
,
and the open intervals
\gamma_{j}(I)
are disjoint.
As
\mathop{\mathrm{clsemi}_{A}}(\Omega^{0})
is join-closed and extension-closed,
by taking sub-moves, it suffices to show that
if a move
\gamma|_{D}
satisfies that

and the unrestricted move
\gamma\neq\gamma_{j}
for all

, then

\gamma|_{D}\not\in\Gamma^{\mathrm{resp}}(\tilde{\Pi}^{\pi})
.

Consider a move
\gamma|_{D}
where
D\subseteq I
and
\gamma\neq\gamma_{j}
for all

. There exists an open interval
D^{\prime}\subseteq D
such that
\gamma(D^{\prime})\cap\gamma_{j}(D^{\prime})=\emptyset
for all

. We can construct a perturbation
\tilde{\pi}
such that

(1)

\tilde{\pi}
is non-zero and Lipschitz continuous on

;

(2)

\tilde{\pi}(x)=\tilde{\pi}(x^{-})=\tilde{\pi}(x^{+})=0
for

;

\tilde{\pi}(x)=\chi(\gamma_{j})\tilde{\pi}(\gamma_{j}(x))
for

,

; and

\tilde{\pi}(x)=0
for

.

Since
\tilde{\pi}|_{D^{\prime}}\not\equiv 0
but
\tilde{\pi}|_{\gamma(D^{\prime})}\equiv 0
,
we have that

, and hence
\gamma|_{D}\not\in\Gamma^{\mathrm{resp}}(\tilde{\pi})
.
By

,
\tilde{\pi}\in\tilde{\Pi}^{\pi}_{\mathrm{zero}({\mathcal{T}})}\subseteq\tilde{\Pi}^{\pi}
.
Therefore,

.
We conclude that (

) holds.
