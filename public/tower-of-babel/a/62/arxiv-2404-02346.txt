A lattice Boltzmann approach for acoustic manipulation
E. Castro-Ávila, Paolo Malgaretti, Jens Harting, J. D. Muñoz

Abstract
We employ a lattice Boltzmann method to compute the acoustic radiation force produced by standing waves on a compressible object for the density matched case. Instead of simulating the fluid mechanics equations directly, the proposed method uses a lattice Boltzmann model that reproduces the wave equation, together with a kernel interpolation scheme, to compute the first order perturbations of the pressure and velocity fields on the object’s surface and, from them, the acoustic radiation force. The procedure reproduces with excellent accuracy the theoretical expressions by Gor’kov and Wei for the sphere as the 3D case and an infinitely long cylinder as the 2D case, respectively, even with a modest number of lattice Boltzmann cells. The proposed method shows to be a promising tool for simulating phenomena where the acoustic radiation force plays a relevant role, like acoustic tweezers or the acoustic manipulation of microswimmers, with applications in medicine and engineering.

II
The second order acoustic radiation force

The results in this section were first developed by L. V. King
[
17
]
. Hereby, we present a summary of that work. Consider an inviscid and compressible fluid in an irrotational flow with pressure
P
, density
\rho
, velocity
\vec{u}
, and momentum
\vec{J}=\rho\vec{u}
. The fluid is described by the mass conservation law,

\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial t}+\vec{\nabla}\cdot\vec{J}=0\quad,

(1)

and Euler’s equation, which can be rewritten as a momentum conservation law,

\frac{\partial\vec{J}}{\partial t}=-\vec{\nabla}P+\vec{\nabla}\cdot\left(\vec{u}\otimes\vec{J}\right)=-\nabla\cdot\Pi\quad.

(2)

Here,
\Pi
is the momentum flux density tensor, described as

\Pi=\mathbb{I}P+\vec{u}\otimes\vec{J}\quad.

(3)

By second Newton’s law, the force
\vec{F}
acting on a static object immersed in the fluid equals the momentum exchange of the fluid across a volume control around the object
[
31
, app. A]

\vec{F}=\int\frac{\partial\vec{J}}{\partial t}dV=-\oint\Pi\cdot\vec{dS}

(4)

Acoustic waves propagate in the fluid as small variations in pressure, density and velocity around steady values. So, the three fields can be written as follows:

\rho=\rho_{0}+\rho_{1}\quad,\quad P=p_{0}+p_{1}\quad,\quad\vec{u}=\vec{0}+\vec{u}_{1}\quad,

(5)

where
p_{1}
,
\rho_{1}
and
\vec{u}_{1}
are first order perturbations mentioned in Sec.
I
, and we assume
u_{0}=0
(that is, the fluid is at rest). Since acoustic waves in a fluid are adiabatic compressions
[
32
]
, the first order pressure and density fluctuations are related as
[
33
, p. 120]

p_{1}=c_{0}^{2}\rho_{1}\quad,

(6)

where
c_{0}^{2}
is the adiabatic speed of sound in the fluid. By taking first order contributions only (as in Eq. (
5
)), Eqs. (
2
) and (
1
) are linearized as

\rho_{0}\frac{\partial\vec{u}_{1}}{\partial t}+\nabla p_{1}=0\quad,

(7a)

\frac{\partial\rho_{1}}{\partial t}+\rho_{0}\nabla\cdot\vec{u}_{1}=0\quad.

(7b)

By combining Eqs. (
7
), and (
6
), one obtains that
p_{1}
fulfills a wave equation,

\frac{1}{c_{0}^{2}}\frac{\partial^{2}p_{1}}{\partial t^{2}}=\nabla^{2}p_{1}\quad.

(8)

Since the flow is irrotational, the velocity
\vec{u}=\vec{u}_{1}
can be written as the gradient of a scalar velocity potential
\phi
, and Eq. (
7a
) transforms to give

\vec{u}_{1}=\nabla\phi\quad\mbox{,}\quad p_{1}=-\rho_{0}\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial t}\quad.

(9)

By replacing Eq. (
9
) into Eq. (
7b
), and using Eq. (
6
), we obtain that the velocity potential also fulfills a wave equation,

\frac{1}{c_{0}^{2}}\frac{\partial^{2}\phi}{\partial t^{2}}=\nabla^{2}\phi\quad.

(10)

In most experiments, the time scale of the object’s motion is much larger than the oscillation period, and, thus, only the time average of the acoustic radiation force
\langle\vec{F}\rangle
is relevant for particle’s dynamics. If only first order fluctuations were considered (
5
), acoustic radiation force (
4
) would reduce to

\vec{F}=-\oint P\vec{dS}\quad,

(11)

because the term
\vec{u}\otimes\vec{J}
is second order.
Since the pressure perturbation oscillates harmonically in time,
p_{1}\propto e^{i\omega t}
, the time average of (
11
) automatically vanishes, and there is no first order contribution to the time-averaged force.
The origin of that force lies, therefore, in higher-order contributions.
By extending the fluctuations up to second order,

\rho=\rho_{0}+\rho_{1}+\rho_{2}\quad,

(12a)

P=p_{0}+p_{1}+p_{2}\quad\text{ and}

(12b)

\vec{u}=\vec{0}+\vec{u}_{1}+\vec{u}_{2}\quad,

(12c)

one obtains that the gradient for the perturbations in the pressure field can be written in terms of first order contributions only (please see App.
A
for details) as

\nabla(P-p_{0})=\nabla\left(\frac{p_{1}^{2}}{2\rho_{0}c_{0}^{2}}-\frac{\rho_{0}}{2}u_{1}^{2}-\rho_{0}\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial t}\right)\quad,

(13)

leading to the following expression for the acoustic radiation force, written in index notation:

\langle F_{i}\rangle=-\oint\left\langle\left(-\rho_{0}\frac{u_{1}^{2}}{2}+\frac{p_{1}^{2}}{2\rho_{0}c_{0}^{2}}\right)\delta_{ij}+\rho_{0}u_{1i}u_{1j}\right\rangle dS_{j}\quad,

(14)

where the triangular brackets
\left<\cdot\right>
denote time averages.
The contribution of the term
\rho_{0}\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial t}
in Eq. (
13
) vanishes because the velocity potential
\phi\propto e^{i\omega t}
is also harmonic.
Eq. (
14
) allows us to compute the time-averaged acoustic radiation force (or force-per-length in the 2D case) on an immersed object from the pressure (
p_{1}
) and velocity (
\vec{u}_{1}
) fields obtained by simulating the acoustic waves directly (Eqs. (
8
) and (
7a
)), instead of solving the full Navier-Stokes equations.

III
Acoustic radiation force on a sphere

Figure 1
:
Decomposition into incident and scattering fields.

The results presented in this section were deducted by K. Yosioka and Y. Kawasima
[
19
]
by using a Hankel functions expansion, and by L. P. Gor’kov via a multipole expansion
[
20
,
31
]
. We aim to compute the average force by acoustic waves acting on a spherical object immersed in a liquid. Let us assume that the object’s radius
R_{p}
is much smaller than the wavelength
\lambda
, i.e.
R_{p}\ll\lambda
or
kR_{p}\ll 1
. Also, it is assumed that the viscous penetration depth
\delta=\sqrt{2\nu/\omega}
, with
\nu
the kinetic viscosity, is much smaller than
R_{p}
, so that the condition
\delta/R_{p}\ll 1
is satisfied. Under such condition, viscosity can be neglected (For a more detailed discussion on the effects of viscosity, see Appendix
D
). Note that larger values of
kR_{p}
would also break the validity of the presented model. Improved models on the acoustic radiation force using expansions to greater orders in
kR_{p}
are found in
[
34
]
and
[
35
]
.
With this approximation, it is possible to solve Eq. (
10
) by dividing the potential, pressure and velocity fields into an incident and a scattered part as shown in Fig.
1

[
20
]
, that is

\phi=\phi_{\text{in}}+\phi_{\text{sc}}\quad,\quad p_{1}=p_{\text{in}}+p_{\text{sc}}\quad,\quad\vec{u}_{1}=\vec{u}_{\text{in}}+\vec{u}_{\text{sc}}\mbox{.}

(15)

The incident fields
(in)

are the solution for the ongoing waves as if there was no spherical particle, while the scattered fields
(sc)

are the differences between the total fields and the incident ones. By replacing (
15
) into (
14
), three main terms appear: The first one only depends on the incident fields and it does not contribute to the force; the second one depends on the scattered field
\phi_{\text{sc}}
only, which will be proportional to
R_{p}^{6}
and, thus, vanishes because of
R_{p}\ll\lambda
.
The last and only surviving term depends on both
\phi_{\text{in}}
and
\phi_{\text{sc}}
, and the force reduces to (see App.
B
for details)

\langle\vec{F}\rangle=-\rho_{0}\int\left\langle\vec{u}_{\text{in}}\left(\nabla^{2}\phi_{\text{sc}}-\frac{1}{c_{0}^{2}}\frac{\partial^{2}\phi_{\text{sc}}}{\partial t^{2}}\right)\right\rangle dV\quad.

(16)

The term between parenthesis is not zero because the object acts as a local source for the scattered field. Thus, a retarded-time multipolar expansion is proposed as a solution for
\phi_{\text{sc}}
,

\displaystyle\phi_{sc}

\displaystyle=-\frac{a(t^{\prime})}{r^{\prime}}-\frac{\vec{A}(t^{\prime})\cdot\vec{r}^{\prime}}{r^{\prime 3}}+\dots

\displaystyle\approx\phi_{\text{mp}}+\phi_{\text{dip}}

(17)

where
\vec{r}^{\prime}=\vec{r}-\vec{v}t
is the position of the center of the sphere, moving with velocity
\vec{v}
, and
t^{\prime}=t-r/c
is a retarded time (with
t^{\prime}\approx t
close to the object)
[
36
,
37
]
. If
kR_{p}
is greater enough, more terms should be considered in (
17
) in this expansion. Corrections for larger
kR_{p}
are not reviewed in this work, but the reader should refer to
[
34
,
35
]
.

Figure 2
:
The object’s pulsating expansions and contractions induce spherical waves coinciding with the monopole term of the scattered velocity potential field.

The monopole term
\phi_{mp}=a(t)/r`
can be found by assuming that the particle is able to compress and expand isotropically as shown in figure
2
, changing its volume
V_{p}
in response to the incident perturbations
p_{\text{in}}
as

dp_{in}=-B_{p}\frac{dV_{p}}{V_{p}}\quad\mbox{, or}\quad\frac{\partial p_{in}}{\partial t}=-\frac{B_{p}}{V_{p}}\frac{dV_{p}}{dt}\quad,

(18)

with
B_{p}=c_{p}^{2}\rho_{p}
the particle’s bulk modulus,
c_{p}
the particle’s speed of sound and
\rho_{p}
the particle’s density. Thus, by using (
6
),

\frac{dV_{p}}{dt}=-V_{p}\frac{c_{0}^{2}}{\rho_{p}c_{p}^{2}}\frac{\partial\rho_{\text{in}}}{\partial t}\quad.

(19)

Now, let us consider a spherical region
\Omega
of radius
R_{\Omega}
concentric to the sphere of radius
R_{p}
, with
\lambda\gg R_{\Omega}>R_{p}
. When the sphere expands, the mass flux leaving
\Omega
through its surface
\partial\Omega
equals the rate at which the sphere pushes fluid out of it,

\displaystyle\dot{m}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial}{\partial t}[(\rho_{0}+\rho_{\text{in}})V_{p}]

(20)

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\dot{\rho}dV=\oint_{\partial\Omega}(\rho_{0}\gradient\phi_{\text{mp}})\cdot\hat{r}dS=4\pi a(t)\rho_{0}\quad,

(21)

with
\hat{r}
the radial unitary vector. By
(
18
) and (
19
) the total derivative in (
20
) becomes

\dot{m}=-V_{p}\frac{c_{0}^{2}}{\rho_{p}c_{p}^{2}}\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\left(\rho_{0}\rho_{\text{in}}+\frac{1}{2}\rho_{\text{in}}^{2}\right)+V_{p}\frac{\partial\rho_{\text{in}}}{\partial t}\mbox{ .}

(22)

Because the incident density is a perturbation of the total density of the fluid (i.e.
\rho_{0}\gg\rho_{\text{in}}
) the term
\frac{1}{2}\rho_{\text{in}}^{2}
is much smaller than
\rho_{0}\rho_{in}
and can be vanished. Therefore,

\dot{m}=4\pi a(t)\rho_{0}=V_{p}\frac{\partial\rho_{\text{in}}}{\partial t}\left(1-\frac{\rho_{0}c_{0}^{2}}{\rho_{p}c_{p}^{2}}\right)\quad,

(23)

and
a(t)
turns out to be

a(t)=\frac{R_{p}^{3}}{3\rho_{0}}f_{1}\frac{\partial\rho_{\text{in}}}{\partial t}\quad\mbox{, with}\quad f_{1}=1-\frac{\rho_{0}c_{0}^{2}}{\rho_{p}c_{p}^{2}}\quad.

(24)

Figure 3
:
The accelerated motion of the object pushes the surrounding fluid and induces an
added mass
, i.e. a drag force proportional to the acceleration. That force allows to compute the dipole term of the scattered velocity potential field.

The dipole term

\phi_{\text{dip}}=-\vec{A}\cdot\vec{r}^{\prime}/r^{\prime 3}

(25)

comes into play when the sphere moves with a changing velocity
\vec{v}
in a fluid with velocity
\vec{u}_{1}
. The velocity potential for this situation is the sum of the one for a sphere at rest in a fluid approaching with velocity
\vec{u}_{1}
plus the one of a sphere moving with velocity
\vec{v}
in a fluid at rest
[
38
]
,

\phi=\left(1+\frac{R_{p}^{3}}{2r`^{3}}\right)\left(\vec{u}_{1}\cdot\vec{r}^{\prime}\right)-\frac{R_{p}^{3}}{2r`^{3}}\left(\vec{v}\cdot\vec{r}^{\prime}\right)\quad.

(26)

The potential can be rearranged as

\phi=-\frac{R_{p}^{3}}{2r`^{3}}\left(\vec{v}-\vec{u}_{1}\right)\cdot\vec{r}^{\prime}+\left(\vec{u}_{1}\cdot\vec{r}^{\prime}\right)\quad,

(27)

where the last term is easily identified as the incident potential
\phi_{in}
, and the first term, as the one of a sphere moving in a fluid at rest with velocity
\left(\vec{v}-\vec{u}_{1}\right)
(see Fig.
3
). Indeed, by comparing
\phi_{in}
in (
27
) with (
25
), one can identify

\vec{A}(r^{\prime})=-\frac{R_{p}^{3}}{2}\left(\vec{v}-\vec{u}_{1}\right)\quad.

(28)

On the one side and according to
[
38
]
, the force on the sphere produced by that potential is

F_{sc}=-M_{a}\left(\frac{\partial\vec{v}}{\partial t}-\frac{\partial\vec{u}_{1}}{\partial t}\right)\quad,

(29)

with

M_{a}=\frac{2}{3}\pi R_{p}^{3}\rho_{0}

(30)

the
added mass
for the sphere.
On the other hand, if the sphere was made of fluid, it would move with velocity
\vec{v}=\vec{u}_{1}
and, therefore, the force on the sphere from a potential
\phi=\vec{u}_{1}\cdot\vec{r}^{\prime}
must be exactly the one we need to move that fluid sphere with an acceleration
\frac{\partial\vec{u}_{1}}{\partial t}

F_{in}=M_{f}\frac{\partial\vec{u}_{1}}{\partial t}\quad\mbox{, with}\quad M_{f}=\frac{4}{3}\pi R_{p}^{3}\rho_{0}\quad.

(31)

The total force acting on the sphere of density
\rho_{p}
and mass
M_{p}=4\pi R_{p}^{3}/3\rho_{p}
is the sum of those two forces. Thus,

M_{p}\frac{\partial\vec{v}}{\partial t}=-M_{a}\left(\frac{\partial\vec{v}}{\partial t}-\frac{\partial\vec{u}_{1}}{\partial t}\right)+M_{f}\frac{\partial\vec{u}_{1}}{\partial t}\quad.

(32)

By replacing the expressions for the masses, we obtain

\left(\rho_{p}+\frac{\rho_{0}}{2}\right)\frac{\partial\vec{v}}{\partial t}=\frac{3}{2}\rho_{0}\frac{\partial\vec{u}_{1}}{\partial t}\quad.

(33)

If we assume that
\vec{v}=0
when
\vec{u}_{1}=0
, integrating with respect to time
t
gives us

\vec{v}=\frac{3\rho_{0}}{2\rho_{p}+\rho_{0}}\vec{u}_{1}\quad.\quad

(34)

and the vector field
\vec{A}(t)
becomes

A(t)=\frac{R_{p}^{3}}{2}f_{2}\vec{u}_{1}\quad\mbox{, with}\quad f_{2}=\frac{2(\rho_{p}-\rho_{0})}{2\rho_{p}+\rho_{0}}\quad.

(35)

By plugging (
24
) and (
35
) into
(
17
) and (
16
), and by solving the integration (see Appendix
C
for details), we have

\langle F_{i}\rangle=-\partial_{i}V_{p}\left(f_{1}\frac{1}{2\rho_{0}c_{0}^{2}}\langle p^{\prime 2}_{\text{in}}\rangle+\frac{3}{4}\rho_{0}f_{2}\langle v^{2}_{\text{in}}\rangle\right)=-\nabla U

(36)

where the potential
U
is defined as

U=V_{p}\left(f_{1}\frac{1}{2\rho_{0}c_{0}^{2}}\langle p^{\prime 2}_{\text{in}}\rangle+\frac{3}{4}\rho_{0}f_{2}\langle v^{2}_{\text{in}}\rangle\right)\quad.

(37)

That is the Gor’kov potential. This potential is commonly used for acoustic levitation of small objects, even regardless of the object‘s shape as soon as
R_{0}\ll\lambda
is satisfied. In the particular case of incident stationary waves,

p_{1}(x,t)=P_{a}\sin(\omega t)(\cos kx)\quad,

(38)

with
P_{a}
the pressure amplitude, Eq. (
9
) give us the velocity as

u_{1x}(x,t)=-\frac{P_{a}}{c_{0}\rho_{0}}\cos\omega t\sin kx\quad.

(39)

By plugging (
38
) and (
9
) into the Gor’kov potential and solving the time-average integration, this potential for standing waves becomes

U=\frac{V_{p}P_{a}^{2}}{4\rho_{0}c_{0}^{2}}\left(f_{1}\cos^{2}kx+\frac{3}{2}f_{2}\sin^{2}kx\right)\quad.

(40)

The force can be gathered using (
36
), (
24
) and (
35
), leading to

F_{x}=-\frac{\pi R_{p}^{3}P_{a}^{2}k}{3\rho_{0}c_{0}^{2}}\Phi(\tilde{\rho},\tilde{\kappa})\sin 2kx\quad,

(41)

where
\tilde{\rho}=\rho_{p}/\rho_{0}
,
\tilde{\kappa}=\kappa_{p}/\kappa_{0}
and
\Phi(\tilde{\rho},\tilde{\kappa})
is defined as

\Phi(\tilde{\rho},\tilde{\kappa})=\frac{5\tilde{\rho}-2}{2\tilde{\rho}+1}-\tilde{\kappa}\quad.

(42)

This is the theoretical result to compare with in 3D.

IV
Acoustic radiation force per length on a cylinder

The results of this section were developed by Wei
[
22
]
. Nevertheless, instead of following the deduction by Wei, let us develop the same procedure as in the previous section, but in two dimensions, a procedure that will give the same results. As in (
16
), the acoustic radiation force-per-length is computed from the scattered potential as

\langle F_{i}\rangle=-\rho_{0}\int\left\langle u_{\text{in}}^{i}\left(\nabla^{2}\phi_{sc}-\frac{1}{c_{0}^{2}}\frac{\partial^{2}\phi_{sc}}{\partial t^{2}}\right)\right\rangle dA\quad{.}

(43)

Because the cylinder is infinite in height, (
43
) computes a force per unit length since the cylinder would get an infinite surface and the integration is done along the circular boundary of its cross-section. A retarded time multipolar expansion is also a good approximation in 2D,

\phi_{sc}=-a(t^{\prime})\log{r}-\frac{\vec{A}(t^{\prime})\cdot\vec{r}^{\prime}}{r^{\prime 2}}+\dots

(44)

with
\vec{r}^{\prime}=\vec{r}-\vec{v}t
and
t^{\prime}=t-r/c\approx t
.

As in the 3D case, the scalar field
a(t)
in the monopole term
\phi_{mp}=-a(t^{\prime})\log{r}
can be found by assuming an isotropic compression and expansion of the cylinder due to the incident field
p_{\text{in}}
by

dp_{in}=-B_{p}^{\text{2D}}\frac{dA_{p}}{A_{p}}\quad\mbox{, or}\quad\frac{\partial p_{in}}{\partial t}=-\frac{B_{p}^{\text{2D}}}{A_{p}}\frac{dA_{p}}{dt}\quad,

(45)

with
A_{p}
the cylinder’s area,
B_{p}^{\text{2D}}=-AdP/dA=c_{p}^{2}\rho_{p}
the particle’s bulk modulus in 2D. Furthermore, since
p_{in}=\rho_{in}c_{0}^{2}
, with
c_{0}
the speed of sound in the fluid,

\frac{dA_{p}}{dt}=-A_{p}\frac{c_{0}^{2}}{\rho_{p}c_{p}^{2}}\frac{\partial\rho_{\text{in}}}{\partial t}\quad.

(46)

Consider a circular region
\Sigma
of radius
R_{\Sigma}
concentric to the cylinder with
\lambda\gg R_{\Sigma}>R_{p}
. On the one side, the mass flux leaving
\Sigma
through its boundary
\partial\Sigma
equals the rate at which the cylinder pushes fluid out of it

\dot{m}=\oint_{\partial\Sigma}(\rho_{0}\vec{\nabla}\phi_{mp})\cdot\hat{r}dl=2\pi a(t)\rho_{0}\quad,

(47)

On the other hand,

\dot{m}=\frac{\partial}{\partial t}[(\rho_{0}+\rho_{\text{in}})A_{p}]\approx A_{p}\frac{\partial\rho_{\text{in}}}{\partial t}\left(1-\frac{\rho_{0}c_{0}^{2}}{\rho_{p}c_{p}^{2}}\right)\mbox{ ,}

(48)

where we used (
45
) and (
46
). By equaling (
47
) and (
48
) by
taking into account once again that
\frac{1}{2}\rho_{\text{in}}^{2}
is much smaller than
\rho_{0}\rho_{\text{in}}
, we finally get

a(t)=\frac{R_{p}^{2}}{2\rho_{0}}\frac{\partial\rho_{in}}{\partial t}f_{1}\quad\mbox{, with}\quad f_{1}=1-\frac{\kappa_{p}}{\kappa_{0}}\quad.

(49)

Like in the 3D case the velocity potential around a cylinder traveling with velocity
\vec{v}
in a fluid moving with velocity
\vec{u}_{1}
is
[
38
]

\phi=\left(1+\frac{R_{p}^{2}}{r`^{2}}\right)\left(\vec{u}_{1}\cdot\vec{r}^{\prime}\right)-\frac{R_{p}^{2}}{r`^{2}}\left(\vec{v}\cdot\vec{r}^{\prime}\right)\quad,

(50)

that can be rearranged as

\phi=-\frac{R_{p}^{2}}{r`^{2}}\left(\vec{v}-\vec{u}_{1}\right)\cdot\vec{r}^{\prime}+\left(\vec{u}_{1}\cdot\vec{r}^{\prime}\right)\quad.

(51)

Here, again, the last term is the incident potential
\phi_{in}
, and the first term, the one of a cylinder moving in a fluid at rest with velocity
\left(\vec{v}-\vec{u}_{1}\right)
. By comparing
\phi_{in}
in (
51
) with the dipole potential
\phi_{dip}=-\frac{\vec{A}(t)\cdot\vec{r}^{\prime}}{r^{\prime 2}}
we identify

\vec{A}(r^{\prime})=-R_{p}^{2}\left(\vec{v}-\vec{u}_{1}\right)\quad.

(52)

The force on the cylinder produced by that potential is

F_{sc}=-M_{a}\left(\frac{\partial\vec{v}}{\partial t}-\frac{\partial\vec{u}_{1}}{\partial t}\right)\quad\mbox{, with}\quad M_{a}=\pi R_{p}^{2}\rho_{0}

(53)

the
added mass
for the cylinder
[
39
]
.

As in the 3D case, if the cylinder were made of fluid, it would move with velocity
\vec{v}=\vec{u}_{1}
and, therefore, the force on the cylinder from a potential
\phi=\vec{u}_{1}\cdot\vec{r}^{\prime}
must be exactly the one we need to move that fluid cylinder with an acceleration
\frac{\partial\vec{u}_{1}}{\partial t}

F_{in}=M_{f}\frac{\partial\vec{u}_{1}}{\partial t}\quad\mbox{, with}\quad M_{f}=\pi R_{p}^{2}\rho_{0}\quad.

(54)

The total force-per-length acting on the cylinder of density
\rho_{p}
and mass
M_{p}=\pi R_{p}^{2}\rho_{p}
is the sum of those two contributions. Thus,

M_{p}\frac{\partial\vec{v}}{\partial t}=-M_{a}\left(\frac{\partial\vec{v}}{\partial t}-\frac{\partial\vec{u}_{1}}{\partial t}\right)+M_{f}\frac{\partial\vec{u}_{1}}{\partial t}\quad.

(55)

By replacing the expressions for the masses, we obtain

\left(\rho_{p}+\rho_{0}\right)\frac{\partial\vec{v}}{\partial t}=2\rho_{0}\frac{\partial\vec{u}_{1}}{\partial t}\quad.

(56)

If we assume that
\vec{v}=0
when
\vec{u}_{1}=0
, integrating with respect to time
t
gives us

\vec{v}=\frac{2\rho_{0}}{\rho_{0}+\rho_{p}}\vec{u}_{1}\quad.\quad

(57)

and the vector field
\vec{A}(t)
becomes

A(t)=R_{p}^{2}f_{2}\vec{u}_{\text{in}}\quad\mbox{, with}\quad f_{2}=\frac{\rho_{0}-\rho_{p}}{\rho_{0}+\rho_{p}}\quad.

(58)

After plugging in (
49
) and (
58
) into (
43
) and using the same standing incident wave written in (
38
) and (
39
), the two-dimensional Gor’kov’s potential takes the form deducted by Wei et al.

U=\frac{A_{p}p_{0}^{2}}{4\rho_{0}c_{0}^{2}}\left(f_{1}\cos^{2}kx+f_{2}\sin^{2}kx\right)

(59)

and the acoustic radiation force-per-length is

F_{x}=-\frac{\pi R_{p}^{2}P_{a}^{2}k}{4\rho_{0}c_{0}^{2}}\Phi(\tilde{\rho},\tilde{\kappa})\sin 2kx\quad,

(60)

where
\tilde{\rho}=\rho_{p}/\rho_{0}
,
\tilde{\kappa}=\kappa_{p}/\kappa_{0}
and
\Phi(\tilde{\rho},\tilde{\kappa})
is defined as

\Phi(\tilde{\rho},\tilde{\kappa})=\frac{3-\tilde{\rho}}{1+\tilde{\rho}}-\tilde{\kappa}\quad.

(61)

This last expression of the force-per-length (
60
) coincides with the equations 18 and 22 of the paper of Wei et al.
[
22
, page. 204]
,validating this shorter deduction.

In an LBM, space is divided into cells, and a set of velocity vectors
\vec{v}_{i}
allows for transport from each cell to the neighboring ones (Fig.
4
).At each cell position
\vec{x}
and associated with each velocity vector
\vec{v}_{i}
, there is at least one variable
f_{i}(\vec{x},t)
, called a
distribution function
, which represents the density of some hypothetical particles there, moving with that velocity and transporting information from cell to cell. At each time step, the
f_{i}
are, first, mixed inside every cell into new values
f_{i}^{\rm new}
by using Boltzmann’s transport equation (collision) and, then, transported to the neighboring cells along the vector they are associated with (advection). Because all the information for the collision is inside the cell itself and the advection is blind, all cells can evolve each time step in a fully independent way. That makes the LBM suitable to run in parallel on multiple CPUs or graphic cards
[
40
,
41
]
.

In an LBM for acoustics
[
28
]
, the fluid density
\rho_{1}
, the momentum
\vec{J}_{1}=\rho_{0}\vec{u}_{1}
and the pressure
p_{1}
at any cell are computed as

\rho_{1}=\sum_{i}f_{i}\quad,\quad\vec{J}_{1}=\sum_{i}\vec{v}_{i}f_{i}\quad,\quad p_{1}=c^{2}\rho_{1}\quad,

(62)

where
c
is the speed of sound for the fluid.
At each time step
\Delta t
every cell computes new values
f_{i}^{\rm new}
for the distribution functions by using Boltzmann’s transport equation in the BGK (Bhatnagar-Gross-Krook) approximation,

f_{i}^{\rm new}(\vec{x},t)=\left(1-\frac{\Delta t}{\tau}\right)f_{i}+\left(\frac{\Delta t}{\tau}\right)f_{i}^{eq}\quad\mbox{, with}\quad\tau=\frac{1}{2}\mbox{,}

(63)

where the equilibrium values
f_{i}^{eq}
are computed from the macroscopic fields (
62
). In contrast with LBM schemes for fluids,
\tau=1/2
is perfectly stable for acoustics and other linear equations
[
42
,
43
]
.
Next, the new values
f_{i}^{\rm new}
are transported to the neighboring cells as

f_{i}(\vec{x}+\Delta t\vec{v}_{i})=f_{i}^{\rm new}(\vec{x},t)\quad.

(64)

By performing a Chapman-Enskog expansion it is shown that the macroscopic fields (
62
) satisfy in the continuous limit the following conservative equations
[
44
]
:

\partialderivative{\rho_1}{t}+\divergence{J_1}=0\quad,

(65a)

\partialderivative{\vec J_1}{t}+\divergence{\Pi^{(0)}}=0\quad,

(65b)

with

\Pi^{(0)}=\sum_{i}\vec{v}_{i}\otimes\vec{v}_{i}f_{i}^{eq}\quad.

(66)

If the equilibrium distribution functions
f_{i}^{eq}
are chosen in such a way that
\Pi^{(0)}
becomes diagonal,

\Pi^{(0)}=\begin{bmatrix}p_{1}&0\\
0&p_{1}\end{bmatrix}\quad\mbox{,}\quad\nabla\cdot\Pi^{(0)}=\nabla p_{1}\quad,

(67)

taking divergence on both sides of (
65b
) and using (
67
) and (
65b
) show us that the pressure fulfills

\partialderivative[2]{\rho_1}{t}+\nabla^{2}\rho_{1}=0\quad.

(68)

We can diagonalize
\Pi^{(0)}
by
choosing a velocity set
\vec{v}_{i}
with weights
w_{i}
such that

\displaystyle\sum_{i}w_{i}=1\quad,\quad\sum_{i}w_{i}v_{i\alpha}v_{i\beta}=c_{s}^{2}\delta_{\alpha\beta}

(69)

\displaystyle\sum_{i}\omega_{i}v_{i\alpha}=\sum_{i}\omega_{i}v_{i\alpha}v_{i\beta}v_{i\gamma}=0\quad,

(70)

where
v_{i\alpha}
is the
\alpha-
th component (
\alpha\in x,y,z
) of
\vec{v}_{i}
and
c_{s}^{2}
is a constant. The equilibrium distribution functions

f_{i}^{eq}=\begin{cases}\rho_{1}(1+(1/c_{s}^{2})c_{0}^{2}(\omega_{0}-1))&\text{if}\quad i=0\\
(1/c_{s}^{2})\omega_{i}(c_{0}^{2}\rho_{1}+\vec{v}_{i}\cdot\vec{J}_{1})&\text{if}\quad i\neq 0\end{cases}\quad

(71)

make
\Pi^{(0)}
diagonal (Eq. (
67
)), as desired. Here,
c_{s}
is the speed of sound and
w_{0}
is the weight associated with the null vector
\vec{v}_{0}=(0,0)
. Examples are D2Q5, D2Q9 and D3Q19 with
c_{s}^{2}=1/3
, and D3Q7, with
c_{s}^{2}=1/4
(Fig.
4
).
The method has been extended to curvilinear cells and has been employed to simulate the normal modes in trumpets and even in the human Cochlea
[
43
]
.

Boundary conditions, like a vibrating wall at
x_{0}=0
, can be set, first, by computing the equilibrium functions
f_{i}^{eq}
with the desired value
\rho_{1}(x_{0}=0,y,z)=\rho_{a}\sin(\omega t)
for the density (whereas
\vec{J}_{1}
is computed from Eq. (
62
), as usual) and, next, by overwriting
f_{i}^{\rm new}
with that equilibrium value. This operation, which we call
imposing fields
, is performed after collision and before advection, i.e.

f_{i}^{\rm new}(\vec{x},t)=f_{i}^{eq}\left(\rho_{1}(x_{0}=0,y,z),\vec{J}_{1}(\vec{x},t)\right)\quad.

(72)

The same procedure is employed to set the initial conditions.
Other boundary conditions, like partially absorbing walls at
x_{n}=Lx-1
with
Lx
the length of the simulation domain in
x
direction, are set by performing a bounce-back step (instead of the usual collision),

f_{i}(\vec{x}_{n},t_{n+1})=\Phi f_{j}(\vec{x}_{n},t_{n})\quad,

(73)

where
j
is the index satisfying
\vec{v}_{j}=-\vec{v}_{i}

and
\Phi\in[0,1)
is a damping factor, which we set as
\Phi=0.99
.
Finally, the results are plotted from the values
f_{i}^{\rm new}
after imposing fields and before advection.

VI
Wave-particle interaction and interpolation

To describe the wave-particle interaction in our LBM scheme it is useful to write the acoustic radiation force (
14
) in terms of the quantities
\vec{J}_{1}=\rho_{0}\vec{u}_{1}
and
\rho_{1}=p_{1}/c_{0}^{2}
that are computed by the LBM for waves, that is

\langle F_{i}\rangle=-\frac{1}{\rho_{0}}\oint\left\langle\left(-\frac{J_{1}^{2}}{2}+\frac{\rho_{1}^{2}c_{0}^{2}}{2}\right)\delta_{ij}+J_{1i}J_{1j}\right\rangle dS_{j}\quad.

(77)

This expression seems to be just a function of the density
\rho_{0}
and the speed of sound
c_{0}
in the fluid, but the values
\vec{J}_{1}
and
\rho_{1}
depend on the way the interface is modeled. The boundary conditions for the acoustic wave at the interface are that the pressure
p_{1}
and the normal velocity
\vec{u}_{1}\cdot\hat{n}
(with
\hat{n}
a vector normal to the surface) must be continuous. Those conditions are equivalent to

\rho_{1}^{(0)}c_{0}^{2}=\rho_{1}^{(p)}c_{p}^{2}\quad,\quad\frac{\vec{J}_{1}^{(0)}\cdot\hat{n}}{\rho_{0}^{(0)}}=\frac{\vec{J}_{1}^{(p)}\cdot\hat{n}}{\rho_{0}^{(p)}}\quad,

(78)

where the superscripts
(0)
and
(p)
identify the values of the fields
\vec{J}_{1}
and
\rho_{1}
at the fluid side and the object side, respectively.

For the sake of ease, we choose a much simpler approach, assuming that mean densities on both media are equal (
\rho_{0}^{(0)}=\rho_{0}^{(p)}
) and that only the speed of sound changes, as

c(\vec{r})=c_{0}-\frac{c_{0}-c_{p}}{2}\left(1+\tanh{\left(\frac{|\vec r-\vec r_0|^2 - R_p^2}{d}\right)}\right)\quad,

(79)

where
\vec{r}=(x,y,z)
,
\vec{r}^{\prime}=(x_{0},y_{0},z_{0})
and
d
is the “square thickness” of the interface, which was set as
d=4
for our simulations. In Fig.
8
a three-dimensional density map of function (
79
) is illustrated.
This approach is enough to verify that our procedure reproduces Gor’kov and Wei theoretical expressions for the acoustic radiation force on a sphere (3D) and a cylinder (2D), as we will see in the following Section.

The acoustic radiation force (
77
) is computed by dividing the object’s boundary into
N
surface elements (which are segments in 2D and triangles in 3D) and by computing the normal vector
\hat{n}
and the fields
\rho_{1}
and
\vec{J}_{1}
at the center
\vec{R}_{k}
of each element, but those points do not usually coincide with the nodes
\vec{R}^{\text{LB}}
of the LB mesh (Fig.

). To calculate the fields at
\vec{R}_{k}
we use kernel interpolation
[
45
,

]

\displaystyle\rho_{\text{I}}(\vec{R}_{k})

\displaystyle=\sum_{l}\rho_{1}(\vec{R}_{l}^{\text{LB}})\delta(|\vec{R}_{l}^{\text{LB}}-\vec{R}_{k}|)\quad,

(80)

\displaystyle\vec{J}_{\text{I}}(\vec{R}_{k})

\displaystyle=\sum_{l}\vec{J}_{1}(\vec{R}_{l}^{\text{LB}})\delta(|\vec{R}_{l}^{\text{LB}}-\vec{R}_{k}|)\quad,

(81)

with weights depending on the distance
r=|\vec{R}_{l}^{\text{LB}}-\vec{R}_{k}|

\frac{1}{3}\left(1+\sqrt{-3r^{2}+1}\right)&|r|\leq\frac{1}{2}\\
0&\text{elsewhere}\end{cases}\quad.

VIII
Conclusions

We employed a lattice Boltzmann model that reproduces the wave equation to compute the acoustic radiation force on a compressible object immersed in an inviscid fluid. Instead of simulating the full Navier-Stokes equations (NSE), our proposal relies on the solution of the wave equation from which, via an interpolation scheme, the first order perturbations in the velocity
\vec{u}_{1}
and the pressure
p_{1}
are computed on the object’s surface and, from them, the force.
We tested our novel numerical approach by computing the acoustic radiation force on a cylinder (in 2D) and on a sphere (in 3D) produced by a standing wave with a single nodal plane. All tests were done in the density matched case.
The method reproduces with excellent accuracy the theoretical predictions by Gor’kov
[
20
]
and Wei et al.
[
22
]
for the sphere and the cylinder, respectively, in all studied cases.
The procedure to compute the acoustic radiation force from the fields
p_{1}
and
\vec{u}_{1}
is general, and can be performed with any numerical method for solving the wave equation.

p_{1}
and
\vec{u}_{1}
, with second order accuracy, and hence it needs fewer cells to compute the acoustic radiation force than other methods simulating the full Navier-Stokes equations. Actually, widely used LBMs for fluids compute with second order accuracy the zeroth order density and velocity fields and, with only first order accuracy the perturbative fields
p_{1}
and
\vec{u}_{1}
. Accordingly, our method obtains excellent results by using grids of just
250\times 64
cells for the 2D case and
250\times 64\times 64
for the 3D one, whereas a previous work using a LBM for the full NSE
[
29
]
requires
600\times 600
cells to obtain reliable results in 2D. Our method is also very fast. When implemented in C++ it takes up to
3
minutes on a laptop with a single Intel Core-i5 processor to compute the acoustic radiation force on a cylinder with the simulation domain described above. Similarly, when implemented in our 3D solver
3nskog
it takes no longer than
30
minutes by running on four processors to compute the acoustic radiation force on a sphere.

The values of
p_{1}
and
\vec{u}_{1}
on the object’s surface and the acoustic radiation force computed from them rely on the boundary conditions employed to model the object itself. For simplicity, in our tests we used just a smooth change in the speed of sound when crossing from the fluid to the object, assuming that the mean densities in both media are equal (which is the density-matched case). On the one hand, such an approximation can be enough for nearly buoyant particles. On the other hand, this strategy assures that both the pressure and the normal velocity are continuous at the interface. The actual boundary conditions (Eq. (
78
)) relate both the momenta and the densities on both sides of the interface. Finding the way to implement those more general boundary conditions in our LBM will be an interesting subject for future work.

The proposed approach can be used to compute the acoustic radiation force on an object of any shape in 2D or 3D, like microbots or self-assembled microswimmers. Moreover, it can be coupled with standard numerical integration algorithms
to compute the three-dimensional movement of such objects due to acoustic radiation forces and introducing viscous drag forces when needed, according to
[
50
]
(see Supplementary Material
Movie.gif
).
This opens a broad spectrum of future applications in the acoustic manipulation of many objects.
In addition, being able to have both waves and fluids in the same LBM code allows to simulate multi-physics applications efficiently. For example, the method could be employed for the study of systems like acoustic-driven bubbles or reactions among fluids pushed by micro-particles of solid catalyst driven by acoustic forces, just to mention a few.
All those are interesting subjects for future research.

The present work introduces a fast and accurate numerical procedure to compute the acoustic radiation force on an object immersed in an inviscid fluid. The proposed procedure shows to be a promising tool for the study of the many phenomena in medicine and engineering where that force plays a relevant role.
