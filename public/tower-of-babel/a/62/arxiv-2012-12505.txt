1.
Introduction and statement of results

We consider a Hamiltonian
H=\Delta_{g}+V
defined on

\mathbb{R}^{n}
, where
g
is an asymptotically Euclidean Riemannian
metric in the sense defined below (an example is any smooth, compactly supported perturbation of the flat metric), and
V\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
is a real valued potential
function which is short range and satisfies symbolic estimates in the sense that

|D_{z}^{\alpha}V(z)|\leq C\langle z\rangle^{-\gamma-|\alpha|}

for some
\gamma>1
.
We study the scattering problem for the nonlinear Helmholtz equation

(1.1)

(H-\lambda^{2})u=N[u],

for certain polynomial nonlinearities
N
. The admissible nonlinearities are defined below, but for now we note that examples include

N[u]=\big(c_{1}|u|^{p-1}+c_{2}|\nabla u|^{p-1}\big)u,

where
p\geq 3
is an odd integer. For prescribed, sufficiently small data
f\in H^{k}(\mathbb{S}^{n-1})
, we seek
u
solving equation (
1.1
), such that

(1.2)

u(r,y)\sim r^{-(n-1)/2}\Big(e^{-i\lambda r}f(y)+e^{+i\lambda r}b(y)\Big),\qquad b\in H^{k^{\prime}}(\mathbb{S}^{n-1}).

Here
f
is the “incoming” data and

b
is the “outgoing” data. We refer to the association
f\mapsto b
as the “nonlinear scattering matrix.” In the linear setting, where
N[u]\equiv 0
, the map

f\mapsto b
is called the linear scattering matrix and denoted
S_{\mathrm{lin}}(\lambda)
.
In this setting, it is a pseudodifferential operator of order zero on
\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
composed with the antipodal map.

The results from
[
GHSZ20
]
include the following.

Theorem
.

Assume that
(p-1)(n-1)/2>2
and suppose that
k-4>(n-1)/2
,

. Then there is

sufficiently
small, such that for all

(

The purpose of this article is to prove, as is expected from the inherent

Theorem 1
.

Assume that

(p-1)\frac{n-1}{2}>2\text{ and }k>\max(1,\frac{n-1}{2}).

(

To elaborate on the uniqueness statement, we show that, for an
appropriate microlocalizing pseudodifferential operator
A_{-}
(see (
2.12
)) to the
incoming radial set, with

Note the convergence in (

) is in
H^{k-2}(\mathbb{S}^{n-1})
. This reflects the well known phenomenon from the linear
setting whereby an asymptotic expansion for an incoming (or outgoing)
approximate eigenfunction is produced by computing successive terms
in a formal expansion in negative powers of

. This process in general produces a
“distributional” expansion, in which coefficients of higher
order terms have decreasing regularity. For example, in flat Euclidean space, an incoming approximate
eigenfunction
(\Delta_{0}-\lambda^{2})u_{-}\in\mathscr{S}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
with incoming data
f\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{S}^{n-1})

admits an asymptotic expansion

\begin{gathered}u_{-}\sim r^{-(n-1)/2}e^{i\lambda r}\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}r^{-j}v_{j}(y),\\
v_{0}=f,\quad v_{j+1}=\frac{1}{2i(j+1)\lambda}\left(\Delta_{\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}+\frac{(n-1)(n-3)}{4}-j(j+1)\right)v_{j}.\end{gathered}

From this one sees immediately that with
f\in H^{k}
one can
obtain a partial expansion of an approximate eigenfunction in which
each subsequent term has a coefficient two orders rougher than the
previous one. It therefore seems very natural that the convergence in our
theorem takes place in
H^{k-2}(\mathbb{S}^{n-1})
. See also Remark
3.8
.

Our methods extend to prove a generalization of this result in the

(M^{\circ},g)

is the interior of a compact manifold

in a neighborhood of
\partial M
where

is a boundary defining function, i.e.
\partial M=\{x=0\}

\partial M

\partial M

In the Euclidean case,

On a general asymptotically conic manifold, writing

The admissible nonlinearities are those
N[u]\mathrel{\vbox{\par\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=N(u,\overline{u},\nabla u,\nabla\overline{u},\nabla^{(2)}u,\nabla^{(2)}\overline{u})

and their derivatives up to order two, with

.

Let

For ease of exposition, we return to the Euclidean case in the remainder of this introduction.
Given

to

At issue in this

, and how this can
be understood in terms of the mapping properties of the Poisson

, and the resolvent.

The construction of the nonlinear eigenfunction

[

]

with many antecedents in the Euclidean case, e.g.
[

Using this we prove a relationship between

. This is best understood using the theory of scattering

lies in weighted Sobolev spaces

of scattering pseudodifferential operators in

which are characteristic on the incoming/outgoing

)

of the operators

2.1.
Weighted Sobolev spaces and scattering pseudodifferential
operators

We confine most of our introductory discussion to the case of
Euclidean space. Letting
\mathscr{S}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
denote the space of Schwartz
functions and
\mathscr{S}^{\prime}
the tempered distributions,
each
u\in\mathscr{S}^{\prime}
lies in some weighted
L^{2}
-based Sobolev
space

(2.1)

H^{s,l}(\mathbb{R}^{n})=\langle z\rangle^{-l}H^{m}(\mathbb{R}^{n}),

for
s,l\in\mathbb{R}
.

Recall the scattering symbols and
scattering pseudodifferential operators, defined for
m,l\in\mathbb{R}
by

\begin{gathered}S^{s,l}(\mathbb{R}^{n})=\{a(z,\zeta)\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n}_{z}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\zeta}):\|a\|_{s,l;N}<\infty\mbox{ for
all }N\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\}\\
\Psi_{\mathrm{sc}}^{s,l}(\mathbb{R}^{n})=\{\text{Op}(a):a\in S^{s,l}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\},\end{gathered}

where

\|a\|_{s,l;N}=\sum_{|\alpha|+|\beta|\leq N}\sup_{z,\zeta}|\langle z\rangle^{-l+|\alpha|}\langle\zeta\rangle^{-s+|\beta|}D_{z}^{\alpha}D_{\zeta}^{\beta}a(z,\zeta)|,

Here
\text{Op}(a)
denotes the operator with integral kernel
\int e^{-i(z-z^{\prime})}a(z,\zeta)d\zeta
. Thus the
scattering pseudodifferential operators are by definition (here) the
left quantizations of scattering symbols.

There is a notion of principal symbol attached to scattering
PsiDO’s which includes their leading order behavior at spatial infinity. The (filtered) algebra of
scattering PsiDO’s admits a natural mapping to the graded algebra of
scattering symbols,

\Psi_{\mathrm{sc}}^{s,l}\longrightarrow S^{[s,l]}:=S^{s,l}/S^{s-1,l-1}.

Given
A\in\Psi_{\mathrm{sc}}^{s,l}
its principal symbol will be denoted by

\sigma_{s,l}(A)
.

We work here exclusively with classical scattering symbols, which are
functions
a(z,\zeta)
with joint asymptotic expansions in
z,\zeta

as
|z|,|\zeta|\to\infty
. We will need to construct explicit
symbols whose corresponding operators will function as microlocal
cutoffs. In general, classical symbols can be written

a\equiv\langle z\rangle^{l}\langle\zeta\rangle^{s}a_{0}(z,\zeta),

where
a_{0}(z,\zeta)
is bounded and is smooth in
r^{-1}=|z|^{-1}
and
|\zeta|^{-1}
. This
characterization of the asymptotic behavior of
a_{0}
is
equivalent to the statement that
a_{0}
extends to an element in

C^{\infty}(\overline{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\times\overline{\mathbb{R}^{n}})

where
\overline{\mathbb{R}^{n}}
is the radial compactification of

\mathbb{R}^{n}
in which the map
z\mapsto z/(1+\langle z\rangle)
realizes
\mathbb{R}^{n}
as the interior of the unit ball

\mathbb{B}^{n}
, and the overline notation denotes this entire structure (the
map from Euclidean space into the ball as opposed to just the ball.)

For
[a]\in S^{[s,l]}
, i.e.
[a]
the principal symbol of a
scattering PsiDO, the value of
a_{0}
is determined here only to
the addition of elements in
S^{s-1,l-1}
, so in particular, in sets of bounded frequency,

|\zeta|<C
, with
\mathop{\rm supp}(\chi\colon\mathbb{R}_{t}\to\mathbb{R})\subset\{|t|\leq C\}
,

\chi(\zeta)(a_{0}(z,\zeta)-a(\hat{z},\zeta))\equiv 0\qquad(\mbox{mod }S^{s-1,l-1}),

with a similar expression in the
|z|<C
regions.
An
A\in\Psi_{\mathrm{sc}}^{s,l}
is by definition
scattering elliptic
if its
principal symbol is invertible in the graded algebra
\bigcup_{s,l}S^{[s,l]}
. The microlocal notion
of scattering ellipticity will be used as follows. On regions of
large frequency
|\zeta|>C
, scattering ellipticity is implied by
the uniform estimate

\sigma_{s,l}(A)(z,\zeta)\geq C\langle z\rangle^{l}\langle\zeta\rangle^{s}\mbox{ for }|\zeta|>C.

Below, the operators of interest are (scattering) elliptic on such large frequency
regions but are not globally scattering elliptic, hence the more
detailed estimates coming from propagation
phenomena will arise from analysis on sets of bounded frequency, whence we will
typically need only to discuss the “spatial” principal symbol of
A\in\Psi_{\mathrm{sc}}^{m,l}
, defined for classical scattering symbols by

\sigma_{\text{base},\,l}(A)(\hat{z},\zeta)=\lim_{r\to\infty}\langle z\rangle^{-l}a(z,\zeta).

Note that the behavior in
\zeta
of this function is in general symbolic but not
homogeneous. In this region, (scattering) ellipticity is the same familiar
ellipticity but in the
z
directions;
(\hat{z}_{0},\zeta_{0})
is in the
elliptic set if
\sigma_{s,l}(A)(z,\zeta)\geq C\langle z\rangle^{l}

in a region
|z|>C,|\hat{z}-\hat{z}_{0}|,|\zeta-\zeta_{0}|<\epsilon
. It is straight forward to check that this is equivalent to

\sigma_{\text{base},\,l}(A)(\hat{z}_{0},\zeta_{0})\neq 0
.

The basic boundedness property of scattering
pseudodifferential operators is that for
A\in\Psi_{\mathrm{sc}}^{s^{\prime},l^{\prime}}
,

(2.2)

A\colon H^{s,l}\longrightarrow H^{s-s^{\prime},l-l^{\prime}}\mbox{ is bounded.}

The residual operators are

\Psi^{-\infty,-\infty}=\bigcap_{m,l}\Psi_{\mathrm{sc}}^{m,l},

which have Schwartz kernels in
\mathscr{S}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n})
, and in particular
A\in\Psi_{\mathrm{sc}}^{-\infty,-\infty}
is bounded
between
any
two weighted
L^{2}
-based Sobolev spaces; in
particular since
H^{s,l}\subset H^{s^{\prime},l^{\prime}}
for any
s>s^{\prime},l>l^{\prime}
, residual
A
’s define compact operators on
L^{2}
.
If
A
is globally scattering elliptic then the map in (
2.2
) is
Fredholm, since in that case there is an approximate inverse

B\in\Psi_{\mathrm{sc}}^{-s,-l}
such that
\operatorname{Id}-AB,\operatorname{Id}-BA\in\Psi_{\mathrm{sc}}^{-\infty,-\infty}
.

All of the foregoing material extends directly to the general
asymptotically conical case; see
[
GHSZ20
, Section 2.2]
for
further details. In the general case, one has the fiberwise radial
compactification of the scattering cotangent bundle
{}^{\mathrm{sc}}\overline{T}^{*}M
which is a manifold with corners of codimension

2
; there are boundary defining fuctions
x
for spatial infinity
and
\rho
for fiber infinity (which in the Euclidean case can be
taken to be
\langle z\rangle^{-1}
and
\langle\zeta\rangle^{-1}
, respectively) and the symbol estimates are
essentially the same as the Euclidean space symbol estimates written in
terms of
x
and
\rho
. The scattering pseudodifferential
operators
\Psi_{\mathrm{sc}}^{s,l}(M)
are the quantizations of these symbols,
and the scattering Sobolev spaces, denoted
H^{s,l}_{\mathrm{sc}}(M)
,
consist of distributions

with
Au\in L^{2}(M)
for all
A\in\Psi_{\mathrm{sc}}^{s,l}(M)
.

2.2.
Mapping properties of
H-\lambda^{2}
and the resolvent

Analysis of
H-\lambda^{2}
as a scattering differential operator was
first carried out by Melrose in
[
Me94
]
. We review the relevant
material again on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. We have

H-\lambda^{2}\in\Psi_{\mathrm{sc}}^{2,0}

\sigma_{2,0}(H-\lambda^{2})=|\zeta|^{2}+V-\lambda^{2}\geq C\langle\zeta\rangle^{2},\quad\text{for $|\zeta|>C$}.

In the general asymptotically Euclidean case, we get that the spatial principal symbol is
rather simple:

(2.3)

\sigma_{\text{base},\,2}(H-\lambda^{2})(\hat{z},\zeta)=|\zeta|^{2}-\lambda^{2}.

In particular
H-\lambda^{2}
is not globally scattering elliptic.
Its
characteristic set
\Sigma
, which is a subset of
\mathbb{S}^{n-1}_{\hat{z}}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\zeta}
, is the vanishing locus of
the fiber principal symbol:

(2.4)

\Sigma=\{(\hat{z},\zeta):|\zeta|^{2}-\lambda^{2}=0\}.

To understand the action of the flow of the Hamilton vector field on

\Sigma
, we can work in polar coordinates
(r,y)
, where

y
are arbitrary coordinates in a coordinate patch on the sphere
\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
;
these automatically give rise to dual coordinates
(\nu,\eta)
, and
we rescale the angular dual variable
\eta
by writing
r\mu=\eta
. Via this localization and rescaling we effectively pass to the case of
an arbitrary asymptotically conic manifold.

Noting that in these variables we have

|\zeta|^{2}=\nu^{2}+|\mu|_{h}^{2},

where
|\mu|_{h}
denotes the norm with respect to the dual of metric
h
in the definition of the asymptotically conic metric
g
. Apart from giving the obvious rewriting of the
characteristic set as
\Sigma=\{\nu^{2}+|\mu|_{h}^{2}=\lambda^{2}\}
, it clarifies the behavior of the Hamilton vector field,
which, recall, is defined with respect to arbitrary coordinates and
their canonical dual coordinates to be

H_{p}:=\frac{\partial p}{\partial\nu}\frac{\partial}{\partial r}-\frac{\partial p}{\partial r}\frac{\partial}{\partial\nu}+\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}\Big(\frac{\partial p}{\partial\tilde{\mu}_{j}}\frac{\partial}{\partial y_{j}}-\frac{\partial p}{\partial y_{j}}\frac{\partial}{\partial\tilde{\mu}_{j}}\Big).

Scattering calculus propagation results are phrased in terms of the
natural conformal rescaling (or reweighting) of this vector field, namely
\mathsf{H}_{p}:=\langle z\rangle H_{p},

and with
p=\sigma_{2,0}(H-\lambda^{2})
, we obtain

(2.5)

\mathsf{H}_{p}=-2\nu(x\partial_{x}+R_{\mu})+2|\mu|_{h}^{2}\partial_{\nu}+H_{\partial M,h},

where
R_{\mu}
is the radial vector field in
\mu
and
H_{\partial M,h}
is the Hamilton vector field on
\partial M
for the metric

h
. (Note that the apparent discrepancy in sign between
(
2.5
) and
[
Me94
, Eq. 8.19]
is due to our usage of

\nu
, the dual variable to
r
, in contrast with Melrose’s use of

\tau=-\nu
.)

From this one deduces that the two submanifolds

(2.6)

\mathscr{R}_{\pm}:=\{\mu=0=x,\ \nu=\pm\lambda\}

are sinks (
+
) or sources (
-
) for the (rescaled)
Hamilton flow, i.e. the flow of
\mathsf{H}_{p}
, on the characteristic
set
\Sigma
. The well-known formula for the principal symbol of a
commutator of pseudodifferential operators also extends to the
scattering setting, namely if
A\in\Psi_{\mathrm{sc}}^{s,l},B\in\Psi_{\mathrm{sc}}^{s^{\prime},l^{\prime}}
with
a=\sigma_{s,l}(A),b=\sigma_{s^{\prime},l^{\prime}}(B)
, then
[A,B]\in\Psi_{\mathrm{sc}}^{s+s^{\prime}-1,l+l^{\prime}-1}
and

\sigma_{s+s^{\prime}-1,l+l^{\prime}-1}(i[A,B])=\{a,b\}=H_{a}b,

where here
\{\cdot,\cdot\}
denote the Poisson bracket.

The most important example of the use of commutators for us will be
those of the form
[H,Q]
where
H
is the Hamiltonian and
Q\in\Psi_{\mathrm{sc}}^{0,0}
, in particular we will take
Q
to have various
microsupport properties (discussed below) which will allow them to act
as microlocal cutoffs to various portions of scattering phase space.
Particularly
simple examples include symbols of the form

q(x,y,\nu,\mu)=\chi(x)\chi((|\mu|_{h}^{2}+\nu^{2})/2\lambda^{2})\phi(\nu),\qquad\phi\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{n}).

where
\chi
is a smooth
function with
\chi(t)=1
for
t\leq 1
and

\chi(t)=0
for
t\geq 2
. (This
q
is then a function whose
support lies in a neighborhood of
\Sigma
and whose restriction to
\Sigma

depends only on
\nu
.) Then in fact
Q\in\Psi_{\mathrm{sc}}^{-\infty,0}
and

[H,Q]\in\Psi_{\mathrm{sc}}^{-\infty,-1}
, whence

(2.7)

\begin{gathered}\langle z\rangle\sigma_{-\infty,-1}(i[H,Q])=\mathsf{H}_{p}q=2|\mu|_{h}^{2}\phi^{\prime}(\nu)\mbox{ in the region }|x|<1,\ |\mu|_{h}^{2}+\nu^{2}<2\lambda^{2},\\
\mbox{and }\langle z\rangle\sigma_{-\infty,-1}(i[H,Q])=0\mbox{ for }|\mu|_{h}^{2}+\nu^{2}\geq 2\lambda.\end{gathered}

We can now define the modules of pseudodifferential operators

\mathscr{M}_{\pm}
used to measure the regularity of distributions in
the domain and range of our formulation of the resolvent mapping.
Specifically

(2.8)

\mathscr{M}_{\pm}:=\{A\in\Psi_{\mathrm{sc}}^{1,1}:\mathscr{R}_{\pm}\subset\Sigma_{1,1}(A)\},

or, in words,
\mathscr{M}_{+}
is the vector space of scattering
pseudodifferential operators of order
(1,1)
which are characteristic
on the “outgoing” radial set
\mathscr{R}_{+}
and
\mathscr{M}_{-}
is
the same for
\mathscr{R}_{-}
. Locally an element
A\in\Psi_{\mathrm{sc}}^{1,1}
lies in
\mathscr{M}_{\pm}
if and only if it can be written

A=B_{0}r(D_{r}\mp\lambda)+\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}B_{i}D_{y_{i}}+B^{\prime}

with
B_{0},B_{i},B^{\prime}\in\Psi_{\mathrm{sc}}^{0,0}
. Note that
\mathscr{M}_{+}
and
\mathscr{M}_{-}

both contain the identity operator. Also,
\mathscr{M}_{\pm}
contain a elliptic element of
order
(1,1)
, namely the radial operator
r(D_{r}\mp\lambda)
, at the opposite radial set

\mathscr{R}_{\mp}
.

We also need the ‘small’ module of angular derivatives

\mathscr{N}=\{\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}B_{i}D_{y_{i}}+B^{\prime}:B_{i},B^{\prime}\in\Psi_{\mathrm{sc}}^{0,0}\}=\mathscr{M}_{+}\cap\mathscr{M}_{-}.

Theorem 2.7 of
[
GHSZ20
]
then gives the following mapping property for
the resolvent. Let
s,l\in\mathbb{R},\kappa,k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, and define

(2.9)

\begin{gathered}\mathscr{Y}^{s,l;\kappa,k}_{\pm}:=H^{s,l;\kappa,k}_{\pm}=\{u\in H^{s,l}:\mathscr{N}^{k}\mathscr{M}_{\pm}^{\kappa}u\subset H^{s,l}\}\\
\mathscr{X}^{s,l;\kappa,k}_{\pm}:=\{u\in H_{\pm}^{s,l;\kappa,k}:(H-\lambda^{2})u\in H_{\pm}^{s-2,l+1;\kappa,k}\}.\end{gathered}

Then

(2.10)

R(\lambda\pm i0)\colon\mathscr{Y}_{\pm}^{s,l+1;\kappa,k}\longrightarrow\mathscr{X}_{\pm}^{s+2,l;\kappa,k}\mbox{ provided }k\geq 1,\ l\in(-\frac{1}{2}-k,-\frac{1}{2}).

Here, the value
l=-1/2
is referred to as the “threshold” value; it is the critical spatial
order for which we need different “radial propagation estimates” for
l
greater than vs.
less than
-1/2
. See
[
GHSZ20
, Section 3]
. The condition in (
2.10
)
is that
l
is below threshold, but that
l+k
is above threshold. That means that module
regularity for
\mathscr{M}_{\pm}
of order
k
gives above threshold regularity at the opposite
radial set
\mathscr{R}_{\mp}
, which is the key to obtaining estimates such as (
2.10
).
This statement can be rephrased as follows: for such
k\in\mathbb{N}
and
l\in\mathbb{R}
,
the operator
H-\lambda^{2}
is an isomorphism from

\mathscr{X}_{\pm}^{s,l;k,k^{\prime}}
to
\mathscr{Y}_{\pm}^{s-2,l+1;k,k^{\prime}}
, and its inverse is the resolvent
R(\lambda\pm i0)
.

The reason for introducing the small module
\mathscr{N}
to treat
the nonlinear problem is explained in detail in the introduction of

[
GHSZ20
]
. We remark here only that for multiplication of
distributions in
H_{+}^{s,l;\kappa,k}
, large module regularity produces loss in decay – see
e.g.
[
GHSZ20
, Cor. 2.10]
from which one has
H_{+}^{s,l;\kappa,k}\cdot H_{+}^{s,l;\kappa,k}\subset H_{+}^{s,2l+(n/2)-\kappa;\kappa,k}
. Small
module regularity is used to minimize this loss of
\kappa
in the spatial order.

2.3.
Microlocalization

It will be important to microlocalize distributions both near to and
away from the radial set. To this end we recall some features of the operator wavefront set
\WF^{\prime}(A)
of
A\in\Psi_{\mathrm{sc}}^{s,l}(M)
. We will work mostly with those
A
which are
compactly microlocalized in frequency. Concretely, this means that

A=\text{Op}(a(z,\zeta))+E,\quad\mathop{\rm supp}a(z,\zeta)\subset\{|\zeta|<C\},\ \kappa_{E}\in\mathscr{S}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}),

for some
C>0
(
\kappa_{E}
is the Schwartz kernel of
E
).
The condition on
\kappa_{E}
is equivalent to
E
being a
residual
operator, meaning

E\in\Psi_{\mathrm{sc}}^{-\infty,-\infty}:=\bigcap_{m,l\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}}\Psi_{\mathrm{sc}}^{m,l}.

For such
A
,
\WF^{\prime}(A)\subset\mathbb{S}^{n-1}_{\hat{z}}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\zeta}
is by definition the complement of
the set
(\hat{z},\zeta)
such that
a
is Schwartz in
\zeta
in the

\hat{z}
direction.

Clearly
\WF^{\prime}(A)=\varnothing\implies A\in\Psi_{\mathrm{sc}}^{-\infty,-\infty}
, and thus for any
S,L\in\mathbb{R}
,

\WF^{\prime}(A)=\varnothing\implies A\colon H^{S,L}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\longrightarrow\mathscr{S}(\mathbb{R}^{n}).

Also, wavefront sets have the expected algebraic property of supports,
namely, for
A\in\Psi_{\mathrm{sc}}^{s,l},B\in\Psi_{\mathrm{sc}}^{s^{\prime},l^{\prime}}
,

\WF^{\prime}(AB)\subset\WF^{\prime}(A)\cap\WF^{\prime}(B).

Two particularly useful examples of such operators are those of the
form
Q_{\pm}\in\Psi_{\mathrm{sc}}^{0,0}
which are microlocalized near the two
components of the radial set
\mathscr{R}_{\pm}
. This can be done
by defining
Q_{\pm}
explicitly in a way similar to the definition of

Q
above (
2.7
), specifically one can take

(2.11)

Q_{\pm}=\text{Op}(q_{\pm}),\quad q_{\pm}(x,y,\nu,\mu)=\chi(x)\chi((|\mu|_{h}^{2}+\nu^{2})/2\lambda^{2})\chi((\nu\mp\lambda)/\epsilon),

where again
\chi(s)=1
for
s\leq 1
and

\chi(s)=0
for
s\geq 2
.

Alternatively, one can simply break up phase space into a region where

\nu
is positive and its complement, excluding one radial set from each.
We choose an operator
A_{+}\in\Psi^{0,0}_{\text{cl}}(M)
such that
A_{+}
is
microlocally equal to the identity in a neighbourhood of
\mathscr{R}_{+}
, and
microlocally equal to
0
in a neighbourhood of
\mathscr{R}_{-}
. We also let

A_{-}=\operatorname{Id}-A_{+}
; thus,
A_{-}
is microlocally equal to the identity
in a neighbourhood of
\mathscr{R}_{-}
, and microlocally equal to
0
in a
neighbourhood of
\mathscr{R}_{+}
. It is convenient to choose
A_{+}
such that its
principal symbol
a
is a function only of
\nu
in a neighbourhood of
the characteristic variety of
H
, and is monotone. Indeed, we can
take

(2.12)

A_{+}=\text{Op}(a),\quad a(x,\nu)=\chi(x)\chi((|\mu|_{h}^{2}+\nu^{2})/2\lambda^{2})\tilde{\chi}(\nu),

where
\chi
is as in the definition of
Q_{\pm}
and
\tilde{\chi}(\nu)\equiv 1
in
\nu>\lambda/4
and
\tilde{\chi}(\nu)\equiv 0
for

\nu<-\lambda/4
, and is monotone in between.

While microlocalization gives us a concrete mechanism for analyzing
frequency-localized spatial decay, it will be useful to note that
this is also possible using anisotropic Sobolev spaces, meaning
Sobolev spaces
H^{s,\mathsf{l}}
in which the parameters
s,\mathsf{l}
are
themselves functions on phase space. Since the operators under
consideration here are elliptic outside a compact set in frequency,
we will always take
s
constant, but we shall employ variable spatial weights

\mathsf{l}_{\pm}\in S^{0,0}
satisfying

(2.13)

\begin{gathered}\text{$\mathsf{l}_{+}$ is equal to $-1/2$ outside small neighbourhoods of $\mathscr{R}_{+}$ and $\mathscr{R}_{-}$,}\\
\text{$\mathsf{l}_{+}$ equals $-1/2\mp\delta$ in smaller neighbourhoods of $\mathscr{R}_{\pm}$, }\\
\text{$\mathsf{l}_{+}$ is nonincreasing along the Hamilton flow of $P$ within $\Sigma(P)$,}\\
\text{and $\mathsf{l}_{-}=-1-\mathsf{l}_{+}$ has corresponding properties with $\mathscr{R}_{-}$ and $\mathscr{R}_{+}$ switched.}\end{gathered}

For definiteness, we suppose that for some sufficiently small
\delta\in[0,0.01]
, we have
\mathsf{l}_{+}
equal to
-1/2
outside the sets
\{x^{2}+|\mu|_{h}^{2}+(\nu\mp\lambda)^{2}\leq 4\delta\lambda^{2}\}
,
and is equal to
-1/2\mp\delta
within the sets
\{x^{2}+|\mu|_{h}^{2}+(\nu\mp\lambda)^{2}\leq\delta\lambda^{2}\}
.
Thus, distributions in
H^{s,\mathsf{l}_{+}}
lie in
H^{s,-1/2-\epsilon}
and are microlocally
H^{s,-1/2+\epsilon}
near
\mathscr{R}_{-}
; that is, they are below threshold regularity at
\mathscr{R}_{+}
and above threshold at
\mathscr{R}_{-}
, with a corresponding statement true with reversed signs for
H^{s,\mathsf{l}_{-}}
.
Then by Theorem 3.2 of
[
GHSZ20
]
we have isomorphisms

(2.14)

\begin{gathered}H-\lambda^{2}\colon\mathscr{X}^{s,\mathsf{l}_{\pm}}\longrightarrow\mathscr{Y}^{s-2,\mathsf{l}_{\pm}+1},\\
\mathscr{Y}^{s-2,\mathsf{l}_{\pm}+1}=H^{s-2,\mathsf{l}_{\pm}+1},\quad\mathscr{X}^{s,\mathsf{l}_{\pm}}=\{u\in H^{s,\mathsf{l}_{\pm}}:(H-\lambda^{2})u\in\mathscr{Y}^{s-2,\mathsf{l}_{\pm}+1}\}.\end{gathered}

The inverse maps are the incoming/outgoing resolvent
R(\lambda\pm i0)
.
As with (
2.10
), we notice
the sharp difference of one in the spatial regularity, and the avoidance of the threshold value at the radial sets.

Moreover, we can combine variable order spaces with module regularity, obtaining isomorphisms

(2.15)

\begin{gathered}H-\lambda^{2}\colon\mathscr{X}_{\pm}^{s,\mathsf{l}_{\pm};\kappa,k}\longrightarrow\mathscr{Y}_{\pm}^{s-2,\mathsf{l}_{\pm}+1;\kappa,k},\\
\mathscr{Y}_{\pm}^{s^{\prime},\mathsf{l}^{\prime};\kappa,k}:=\{u\in\mathscr{Y}_{\pm}^{s^{\prime},\mathsf{l}^{\prime}}\mid\mathscr{N}^{k}\mathscr{M}_{\pm}^{\kappa}\,u\subset\mathscr{Y}_{\pm}^{s^{\prime},\mathsf{l}^{\prime}}\}\\
\mathscr{X}_{\pm}^{s,\mathsf{l}_{\pm};\kappa,k}:=\{u\in\mathscr{Y}_{\pm}^{s,\mathsf{l}_{\pm};\kappa,k}:(H-\lambda^{2})u\in\mathscr{Y}_{\pm}^{s-2,\mathsf{l}_{\pm}+1;\kappa,k}\}.\end{gathered}

We emphasize that in (
2.15
), we can allow
\kappa=0
, that is, only consider small module
regularity, in contrast to (
2.10
).

These variable order module regularity results do not appear explicitly in our previous work
[
GHSZ20
]
, but such
results follow readily from the propagation estimates in Section 3 of that paper. (The reason for
restricting the module regularity spaces to constant orders in
[
GHSZ20
]
is that it is more convenient to analyze multiplicative
properties of such spaces when the orders are constant.)
We shall not review variable order spaces in detail here, referring the
reader to
[
Va18
]
. Here we only mention one elementary property of such spaces.
Namely, we can conclude
containment in these types of variable order spaces using
microlocalization. For example, it is straightforward to show that, given
a tempered distribution
u
, for
\epsilon>0
sufficiently small and
Q_{+}\in\Psi_{\mathrm{sc}}^{0,0}
as in (
2.11
),
if
Q_{+}u\in H^{s,-1/2-\delta}
and
(\operatorname{Id}-Q_{+})u\in H^{s,-1/2+\delta}
, then
u\in H^{s,\mathsf{l}_{+}}
.

We note that in all these considerations, the value of
\mathsf{l}_{\pm}
off

\Sigma
is essentially irrelevant, since off
\Sigma
one has
elliptic estimates for
H-\lambda^{2}
.

3.
Mapping properties of the linear Poisson operator

The Poisson operator furnishes a distorted or generalized Fourier transform for the operator
H
. Constructions
in the Euclidean case go back a long way in scattering theory, see e.g.
[
Ik60
]
,
[
Is82
]
. We use the results of
Melrose and Zworski
[
MeZw96
]
, who constructed the Poisson
operator
P(\lambda)
,
\lambda>0
, for the Helmholtz operator
H=\Delta-\lambda^{2}
on asymptotically conic manifolds (scattering metrics).
Their results extend readily to the case of a Schrödinger operator with potential in the
conormal space
\mathscr{A}^{\gamma}(M)
, for
\gamma>1
, which is by definition the space of
smooth functions
V
on
M^{\circ}
satisfying estimates near
\partial M
of the form

(3.1)

\Big|(xD_{x})^{j}D_{y}^{\alpha}V(x,y)\Big|\leq Cx^{\gamma},\quad j\geq 0,\ \alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{n-1}.

These estimates on
V
are equivalent to (
1.7
) in the case of Euclidean space.

The Poisson operator has the property that it maps a smooth function
\phi\in C^{\infty}(\partial M)
to a function
u_{0}
satisfying
Hu_{0}=0
, with the asymptotics

(3.2)

u_{0}=u_{-}+u_{+},\quad u_{\pm}=r^{-(n-1)/2}e^{\pm i\lambda r}f_{\pm},\quad f_{\pm}\in C(M),\quad f_{-}\big|_{\partial M}=\phi.

In fact the
f_{\pm}
can be taken to lie in
C^{\infty}(M)+\mathscr{A}^{\gamma-1}(M)
(which are continuous up to the boundary, as
\gamma-1>0
); moreover, if
V
is equal to
r^{-2}
times a smooth function on
M
, then the
f_{\pm}
can be taken in
C^{\infty}(M)
.
We shall also define
P(-\lambda)
, again for
\lambda>0
, to be the operator mapping
\phi\in C^{\infty}(\partial M)
to a function
u_{0}
satisfying
Hu_{0}=0
, with the asymptotics

u_{0}=u_{-}+u_{+},\quad u_{\pm}=r^{-(n-1)/2}e^{\pm i\lambda r}f_{\pm},\quad f_{\pm}\in C^{\infty}(M)+\mathscr{A}^{\gamma-1}(M),\quad f_{+}\big|_{\partial M}=\phi.

Recall that
C^{\infty}
functions on
M
, as opposed to
C^{\infty}
functions on
M^{\circ}
, are (by definition) smooth functions of
x=1/r
and
y
near
\partial M
.

The adjoint operator
P(\lambda)^{*}
will also play a role in this article. To describe it, let
v
be a Schwartz function
1
1

1

By a Schwartz function on
M
we mean a smooth function that vanishes, with all its derivatives, at the boundary. When
M
is the radial compactification of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
this corresponds to the usual meaning of Schwartz function.
on
M
, and let
u_{\pm}=\pm R(\lambda\pm i0)v
. Then each
u_{\pm}
has a similar expansion as in (
3.2
), but with only the outgoing (
+
)/incoming(
-
) oscillation
[
Me94
, Proposition 12]
:

(3.3)

u_{\pm}=r^{-(n-1)/2}e^{\pm i\lambda r}f_{\pm},\quad f_{\pm}\in C^{\infty}(M)+\mathscr{A}^{\gamma-1}(M).

We claim that
P(\lambda)^{*}v=2i\lambda f_{-}|_{\partial M}
. To see that this is true, we use a pairing identity that follows from Green’s formula. Suppose that
u_{1}
and
u_{2}
are two functions of the form

u_{i}=r^{-(n-1)/2}\Big(e^{-i\lambda r}f_{i,-}+e^{+i\lambda r}f_{i,+}\Big),\quad f_{i,\pm}\in C^{\infty}(M)+\mathscr{A}^{\gamma-1}(M).

Suppose further that
(H-\lambda^{2})u_{i}=O(r^{-(n+1)/2-\epsilon})
for some
\epsilon>0
. Then the following identity holds
[
Me94
, Equation (13.1)]
(see also
[
HV99

):

\int_{M}\Big(u_{1}\overline{(H-\lambda^{2})u_{2}}-((H-\lambda^{2})u_{1})\overline{u_{2}}\Big)d\mathrm{vol}_{g}=2i\lambda\int_{\partial M}\Big(f_{1,+}\overline{f_{2,+}}-f_{1,-}\overline{f_{2,-}}\Big)d\mathrm{vol}_{h},

where we take the restrictions to
\partial M
in the integral over the boundary.
To prove this one notes that the LHS is absolutely integrable, so it can be obtained as the limit, as
R\to\infty
, of the integral restricted to
\{r\leq R\}
. Then one applies Green’s
formula and uses the asymptotic form of the functions
u_{i}
to see that the limit as
R\to\infty
is the RHS.

We apply this with
u_{1}=P(\lambda)a
,
a\in C^{\infty}(\partial M)
and
u_{2}=u_{-}
above. Then
(H-\lambda^{2})u_{1}=0
, and
f_{2,+}=0
, while
f_{1,-}=a
. We obtain

(3.4)

\int_{M}(P(\lambda)a)\overline{v}=2i\lambda\int_{\partial M}a\overline{f_{-}}.

This immediately yields

(3.5)

P(\lambda)^{*}v=2i\lambda f_{-}|_{\partial M}.

A similar argument with
u_{+}
shows

(3.6)

P(-\lambda)^{*}v=2i\lambda f_{+}|_{\partial M}.

Moreover, applying
P(\pm\lambda)
to
f_{\mp}

u_{+}+u_{-}
, since this is the unique eigenfunction with incoming/outgoing data equal to

. It follows that we have

(3.7)

P(\pm\lambda)P(\pm\lambda)^{*}=2\lambda i\Big(R(\lambda+i0)-R(\lambda-i0)\Big),

acting on Schwartz functions. Notice that the quantity in (

d\lambda/4\pi
. Note also the simple consequence of (

) and (

(3.8)

P(\pm\lambda)^{*}

\partial M

P(\pm\lambda)\colon L^{2}(\partial M)\longrightarrow H^{s,-1/2-\delta}(M)

Indeed, first note that both

, the assumption on
F
implies that
Q_{\pm}F
is in the space
\mathscr{Y}_{+}^{s,1/2+\delta;1,k-1}
, while
Q_{3}F
is in
\mathscr{Y}_{+}^{s,1/2;1,k-1}
.

We notice that all of the commutators
[H,Q_{\bullet}]
have boundary order
1
and microlocally vanish near
\mathscr{R}_{+}\cup\mathscr{R}_{-}
, so
r^{2}[H,Q_{\bullet}]
belongs in both modules,
\mathscr{M}_{+}
and
\mathscr{M}_{-}
. We write
u_{1}=Q_{+}u
,
u_{2}=Q_{-}u
, and
u_{3}=Q_{3}u
.

Write
\tilde{u}_{1}=\chi(r)r^{(n-1)/2}e^{-i\lambda r}u_{1}
, where
\chi
is supported in
r>R
and identically
1
near
r\geq 2R
. Our first goal is to show that
\tilde{u}_{1}(r,y)
has a limit
b(y)
in
H^{k-2}(\partial M)
, and that
\tilde{u}_{1}(r,y)-b(y)=O_{H^{k-2}(\partial M)}(r^{-\epsilon})
as
r\to\infty
.
To do this, we write the operator
H-\lambda^{2}
in the form

(3.24)

H-\lambda^{2}=D_{r}^{2}-i(n-1)r^{-1}D_{r}+r^{-2}L+r^{-2}\tilde{L}+V-\lambda^{2},

where
L
is a second order differential operator involving only tangential
D_{y_{j}}
derivatives, and
\tilde{L}
is a scattering differential operator of order
(1,0)
. Since
(H-\lambda^{2})u=F
, we obtain

(3.25)

\Big(D_{r}+\lambda\Big)\Big(D_{r}-\lambda-\frac{i(n-1)}{2r}\Big)u_{1}=Q_{+}F+[H,Q_{+}]u-r^{-2}\tilde{L}u_{1}\\
+\frac{1}{2r^{2}}\Big(i(n-1)r(D_{r}-\lambda)+(n-1)-2L\Big)u_{1}\,-Vu_{1}.

We are going to show that the RHS lies in
\mathscr{Y}^{s,1/2+\delta;0,k-2}
, with the norm in this space bounded by

(3.26)

C\|F\|_{\mathscr{Y}_{+}^{s,\mathsf{l}_{\max}+1;1,k-1}}.

This has already been noted for the first term,
Q_{+}F
. For the remaining terms, it suffices to bound them by

(3.27)

C\|u\|_{\mathscr{Y}^{s+2,-1/2-\delta;1,k-1}}

since, as we have seen in (
2.15
), this is bounded by (
3.26
). For the second term, we use the fact that
[H,Q_{+}]
is
r^{-2}
times a small module element, so the
\mathscr{Y}^{s,1/2+\delta;0,k-2}
-norm of this term is bounded by the
\mathscr{Y}^{s+1,-3/2+\delta;0,k-1}
norm of
u
, which is bounded by (
3.27
) since
\delta<1/2
according to (
3.21
). The next term is very similar: since
r^{-2}\tilde{L}
has order
(1,-2)
, the
\mathscr{Y}^{s,1/2+\delta;0,k-2}
-norm of this term is bounded by the
\mathscr{Y}^{s+1,-3/2+\delta;0,k-2}
norm of
u
, which is again bounded by (
3.27
).
For the term with the
r^{-2}
prefactor, we observe that
the differential operator in large parentheses is contained in
\mathscr{M}_{+}\cdot\mathscr{N}
, that is, one factor in the large module and one in the small module. It follows that the
\mathscr{Y}^{s,1/2+\delta;0,k-2}
-norm of this term is bounded by the
\mathscr{Y}^{s,-3/2+\delta;1,k-1}
norm of
u_{1}
, which again is bounded by (
3.27
). Finally, for the
V
term this follows from the fact that
V
satisfies the conormal estimates (
1.7
), hence multiplication by
V
is a scattering pseudodifferential operator of order
(0,-\gamma)
. Recalling (
3.21
), we have
-1/2-\delta+\gamma>1/2+\delta
, and it follows that the
\mathscr{Y}^{s,1/2+\delta;0,k-2}
-norm of
Vu_{1}
is bounded by the
\mathscr{Y}^{s,-1/2-\delta;0,k-2}
-norm of
u_{1}
, which is bounded by (
3.27
).

Now observe the operator
D_{r}+\lambda
is elliptic everywhere on
\WF^{\prime}(Q_{+})
, since
Q_{+}
is microsupported near
\mathscr{R}_{+}
. Thus we may write invert this operator microlocally; that is, we can write

\operatorname{Id}=J(D_{r}+\lambda)+R^{\prime},

where
J\in\Psi_{\mathrm{sc}}^{-1,0}
is a microlocal inverse, and the microsupport of the remainder
R^{\prime}
is disjoint from
Q_{+}
. Then for any scattering pseudodifferential operator
A
,
R^{\prime}AQ_{+}u
can be bounded by (a suitable multiple of) any Sobolev norm of
u
, for example
\|u\|_{s+2,-1/2-\delta}
, which in turn is bounded by (
3.27
).
Applying this operator identity to
AQ_{+}u=Au_{1}
, where
A=(D_{r}-\lambda-i(n-1)/(2r))
, then applying (
3.25
) and the estimate (
3.26
) on the RHS of this equation, we find

(3.28)

\begin{gathered}\Big(D_{r}-\lambda-\frac{i(n-1)}{2r}\Big)u_{1}\in\mathscr{Y}^{s,1/2+\delta;0,k-2},\\
\Big\|\Big(D_{r}-\lambda-\frac{i(n-1)}{2r}\Big)u_{1}\Big\|_{\mathscr{Y}^{s,1/2+\delta;0,k-2}}\leq C\|F\|_{\mathscr{Y}_{+}^{s,\mathsf{l}_{\max}+1;1,k-1}}.\end{gathered}

Now, using
s\geq 0
and observing that

D_{r}\tilde{u}_{1}=\chi(r)r^{(n-1)/2}e^{-i\lambda r}\Big(D_{r}-\lambda-\frac{i(n-1)}{2r}\Big)u_{1}+(D_{r}\chi)r^{(n-1)/2}e^{-i\lambda r}u_{1},

we find that
D_{r}\tilde{u}_{1}\in H^{0,1/2+\delta-(n-1)/2;0,k-2}
, or equivalently

D_{r}\tilde{u}_{1}\in r^{n/2-1-\delta}L^{2}\big(([R,\infty),r^{n-1}dr);H^{k-2}(\partial M)\big),

with a corresponding norm estimate (where we used the support property of
D_{r}\chi
for the inclusion in
H^{0,1/2+\delta-(n-1)/2;0,k}
of the second term). Combining this estimate with the inclusions

(3.29)

\begin{gathered}r^{n/2-1-\delta}L^{2}\big(([R,\infty),r^{n-1}dr);H^{k-2}(\partial M)\big)\subseteq r^{-1/2-\delta}L^{2}\big(([R,\infty),dr);H^{k-2}(\partial M)\big)\\
\subseteq r^{-\epsilon}L^{1}\big(([R,\infty),dr);H^{k-2}(\partial M)\big),\qquad 0<\epsilon<\delta,\end{gathered}

we find

(3.30)

\Big\|r^{\epsilon}D_{r}\tilde{u}_{1}\Big\|_{L^{1}\big(([R,\infty),dr);H^{k-2}(\partial M)\big)}\leq C\|F\|_{\mathscr{Y}_{+}^{s,\mathsf{l}_{\max}+1;1,k-1}}.

We note that, since
\tilde{u}_{1}
is locally
H^{1}
in
r
with values in
H^{k-2}(\partial M)
, it is in fact continuous in
r
with values in
H^{k-2}(\partial M)
. By (
3.30
), we can integrate to infinity to find

b(y)=\int_{R}^{\infty}\partial_{r^{\prime}}\tilde{u}_{1}(r^{\prime},y)\,dr^{\prime},\quad\|b\|_{H^{k-2}(\partial M)}\leq C\|F\|_{\mathscr{Y}_{+}^{s,\mathsf{l}_{\max}+1;1,k-1}},

is well-defined as an element of
H^{k-2}(\partial M)
.
Moreover,

(3.31)

\begin{gathered}\tilde{u}_{1}(r,y)-b(y)=-\int_{r}^{\infty}\partial_{r^{\prime}}\tilde{u}_{1}(r^{\prime},y)\,dr^{\prime},\\
\Big\|\tilde{u}_{1}(r,y)-b(y)\Big\|_{H^{k-2}(\partial M)}\leq Cr^{-\epsilon}\Big\|r^{\epsilon}D_{r}\tilde{u}_{1}\Big\|_{L^{1}\big(([R,\infty),dr);H^{k-2}(\partial M)\big)}\\
\leq Cr^{-\epsilon}\|F\|_{\mathscr{Y}_{+}^{s,\mathsf{l}_{\max}+1;1,k-1}}.\end{gathered}

To treat
u_{2}
, we apply a similar argument with the role of
\mathscr{R}_{+}
and
\mathscr{R}_{-}
interchanged. In this case we define
\tilde{u}_{2}=\chi(r)r^{(n-1)/2}e^{+i\lambda r}u_{2}
and write (
3.25
) in the form

(3.32)

\Big(D_{r}-\lambda\Big)\Big(D_{r}+\lambda-\frac{i(n-1)}{2r}\Big)u_{2}\\
=Q_{-}F+[H,Q_{-}]u_{2}+\frac{i(n-1)}{2r^{2}}(r(D_{r}+\lambda))u_{+}+\Big(\frac{n-1}{2r^{2}}-r^{-2}L-r^{-2}\tilde{L}-V\Big)u_{+}.

Folllowing the same reasoning as above, we find that
\tilde{u}_{2}
has a limit as
r\to\infty
, with the same
O(r^{-\epsilon})
of convergence. But here, the mapping property of the
outgoing
resolvent near the
incoming
radial set (the inverse mapping to (
2.15
), see
[
GHSZ20
, Theorem 3.1]
) shows that
u_{2}
is actually in
H^{s,-1/2+\delta}
, i.e. the spatial order is above threshold, since
\mathsf{l}_{+}=-1/2+\delta
on the microsupport of
Q_{-}
. Therefore,
since we know that
\tilde{u}_{2}
has a limit in
H^{k-2}(\partial M)
as
r\to\infty
, this limit must be zero (were the limit nonzero, then
\tilde{u}_{2}
would fail to lie in the space
H^{s,-1/2+\delta-(n-1)/2}
for
\delta>0
). It then follows that
\chi(r)r^{(n-1)/2}e^{-i\lambda r}u_{2}=e^{-2i\lambda r}\tilde{u}_{2}
also has a zero limit in
H^{k-2}(\partial M)
as
r\to\infty
, with the same rate of convergence as
\tilde{u}_{2}
.

It remains to discuss
u_{3}
. We claim that
u_{3}
is an element of
\mathscr{Y}_{+}^{s+2,1/2-\delta;1,k-2}
. We argue separately in the microlocal regions (i) near the characteristic variety
\Sigma
, and (ii) away from
\Sigma
. The mapping property (
2.15
) shows that
u
is in
\mathscr{Y}_{+}^{s+2,-1/2-\delta;1,k-1}
.

In region (i), since
u_{3}
is microsupported away from the radial sets, the small module
\mathscr{N}
is elliptic there, so we can trade one order of small module regularity for a gain of one spatial order. In region (ii), we already have
u\in\mathscr{Y}_{+}^{s+2,+1/2-\delta;1,k-1}
since
H-\lambda^{2}
is elliptic there and we incur no loss in the spatial regularity from applying the resolvent in this region.

Now using the
\mathscr{M}_{+}
module regularity, and replacing the differential order with zero, we see that

r\big(D_{r}-\lambda-i\frac{n-1}{2r}\big)u_{3}\in H^{0,1/2-\delta;0,k-2},

that is,

\big(D_{r}-\lambda-i\frac{n-1}{2r}\big)u_{3}\in H^{0,3/2-\delta;0,k-2},

which is stronger than (
3.28
) as
\delta<1/2
. We can thus apply the same reasoning as for
u_{1}
to obtain a limits
for
\chi(r)r^{(n-1)/2}e^{\pm i\lambda r}u_{3}
(which are necessarily
zero, for the same reason as for
u_{2}
), with the same rate of convergence.

The estimates in (
3.22
) are obtained by adding the contributions from
u_{1}
,
u_{2}
and
u_{3}
. The estimates (
3.23
) are obtained similarly.

∎

Remark 3.5
.

From the proof above we see the following: if
Q\in\Psi_{\mathrm{sc}}^{0,0}
is microsupported away from
\mathscr{R}_{+}
, then
\mathscr{L}(\lambda)(Qu_{+})=0
. Similarly,
if
Q^{\prime}\in\Psi_{\mathrm{sc}}^{0,0}
is microsupported away from
\mathscr{R}_{-}
, then
\mathscr{L}(-\lambda)(Q^{\prime}u_{-})=0
. Also note that
\mathscr{L}(\lambda)(Qu_{+})=0
immediately implies
\mathscr{L}(-\lambda)(Qu_{+})=0
. In the same way,
\mathscr{L}(-\lambda)(Q^{\prime}u_{-})=0
implies
\mathscr{L}(\lambda)(Q^{\prime}u_{-})=0
.

Proposition 3.6
.

Let
f\in H^{k}(\partial M)
, with
k\in\mathbb{N}
,
k\geq 2
, and let
u=P(\lambda)f
. Then
A_{-}u
is such that the limit

(3.33)

\mathscr{L}(-\lambda)(A_{-}u)=\lim_{r\to\infty}r^{(n-1)/2}e^{i\lambda r}(A_{-}u)(r,\cdot)

exists in
H^{k-2}(\partial M)
. Moreover, this limit is
f
. Similarly, the limit

(3.34)

\mathscr{L}(\lambda)(A_{+}u)=\lim_{r\to\infty}r^{(n-1)/2}e^{-i\lambda r}(A_{+}u)(r,\cdot)

exists in
H^{k-2}(\partial M)
as
r\to\infty
. Moreover, this
limit is in
H^{k}(\partial M)
.
Both limits are achieved with an
O(r^{-\epsilon})
convergence rate, as in Proposition
3.4
.

Proof.

As we have already seen in Proposition
3.3
,
A_{-}u
is the incoming resolvent applied to
[H,A_{-}]u
, and
A_{+}u
is the outgoing resolvent applied to
[H,A_{+}]u=-[H,A_{-}]u
.
We have also seen in Corollary
3.2
that for
f\in H^{k}(\partial M)
,
[H,A_{\pm}]u
is in the module regularity space

\mathscr{Y}_{\pm}^{s,\mathsf{l}_{\max}+1;k,0}
. Therefore, the existence of the limits (
3.33
) and (
3.34
) in
H^{k-2}(\partial M)
follows from Proposition
3.4
.

We next note that, for
f\in C^{\infty}(\partial M)
, and
u=P(\lambda)f
, the limit
\mathscr{L}(-\lambda)(A_{-}u)
is exactly
f
. This is a defining property of the Poisson kernel; see
[
MeZw96
, Equations (0.2), (0.4)]
. However, we will elaborate on this point, as it is closely related to the form of (
3.18
). Suppose, without loss of generality, that
f
is supported in a small neighbourhood of
b\in\partial M
. Outside any microlocal neighbourhood of
\mathscr{R}_{-}
, the contribution of
A_{-}u
to this limit is zero, as it is in
H^{s,k}
for every
k
by Proposition
3.1
. Therefore, given the canonical relation of
P(\lambda)
, see
[
MeZw96
, Propositions 4 and 19]
, we can replace
A_{-}P(\lambda)f
by
QP(\lambda)f
where

Q
is microsupported near
\mathscr{R}_{-}
and its kernel is supported near
(b,b)
. The kernel of
QP(\lambda)
is then as in (
3.18
), that is,

e^{-i\lambda\cos d_{h}(y,y^{\prime})/x}\tilde{a}(x,y,y^{\prime})+e(x,y,y^{\prime}),

except that
\tilde{a}(x,y,y^{\prime})
is now supported close to
(0,b,b)
but not in a
deleted
neighbourhood, as was the case in (
3.18
). In fact we have

\tilde{a}(0,y,y)=(\lambda/(2\pi))^{(n-1)/2}e^{-i(n-1)\pi/4}\text{ for $y$ near $b$. }

Then, in normal coordinates around
y
, we have

\begin{gathered}\cos d_{h}(y,y^{\prime})=1-\frac{|y-y^{\prime}|^{2}}{2}+O(|y-y^{\prime}|^{3}),\\
dh(y^{\prime})=dy^{\prime}(1+O(|y-y^{\prime}|)),\end{gathered}

the stationary phase lemma shows that indeed
\mathscr{L}(-\lambda)(QP(\lambda)f)(y)=f(y)
in a neighbourhood of
b
.

Now for arbitrary
f\in H^{k}(\partial M)
, we choose a sequence of smooth
f_{j}
converging to
f
in
H^{k}(\partial M)
. Let
u_{j}=P(\lambda)f_{j}
; then
[H,A_{-}]u_{j}
converges to
[H,A_{-}]u
in
\mathscr{Y}_{-}^{s,\mathsf{l}_{\max}+1;1,k-1}
using Corollary
3.2
. As above, we have
A_{-}u_{j}=R(\lambda-i0)[H,A_{-}]u_{j}
.
Using (
3.22
) in Proposition
3.4
,
\lim_{j\to\infty}\mathscr{L}(-\lambda)A_{-}u_{j}
exists in
H^{k-2}(\partial M)
and is
\mathscr{L}(-\lambda)A_{-}u
. On the other hand, since the
f_{j}
are smooth,
\mathscr{L}(-\lambda)A_{-}u_{j}
is precisely
f_{j}
, which converge to
f
in
H^{k}
, and so
a fortiori
in
H^{k-2}
, showing that
\mathscr{L}(-\lambda)A_{-}u=f
.

To obtain the result for
A_{+}u
, we could just appeal to the main result of
[
MeZw96
]
that says that the limit is the scattering matrix
S(\lambda)
applied to
f
, and since
S(\lambda)
is an FIO of order zero, then
S(\lambda)f
is in
H^{k}
. However, we prefer a direct argument. Using the formula (
3.7
), we see that
u
can be expressed as

u=\frac{1}{2\lambda i}P(-\lambda)P(-\lambda)^{*}[H,A_{+}]u.

That is,
u
is equal to
P(-\lambda)f^{\prime}
, where
f^{\prime}=(2\lambda i)^{-1}P(-\lambda)^{*}[H,A_{+}]u
. Now, arguing as in the proof of the converse to Proposition
3.3
, but for
P(-\lambda)
instead of
P(\lambda)
, we see that
f^{\prime}
is in
H^{k}(\partial M)
.
Now applying the argument in the first half of this proof, with signs switched, we conclude that the limit (
3.34
) exists in
H^{k-2}(\partial M)
and is equal to
f^{\prime}\in H^{k}(\partial M)
.

The statement about the convergence rate is shown by applying Proposition
3.4
to
F=[H,A_{\pm}]u
.
∎

Remark 3.7
.

The previous proof shows that the operators
\mathscr{L}(\pm\lambda)\circ R(\lambda\pm i0)
in Proposition
3.4
coincide with
\pm(2i\lambda)^{-1}P(\mp\lambda)^{*}
. Also, we remark that
\mathscr{L}(\lambda)A_{+}P(\lambda)f
, the limit in (
3.34
), is precisely
S_{\mathrm{lin}}(\lambda)f
, the linear scattering matrix applied to
f
.

Remark 3.8
.

There is a subtlety here: the limiting functions in (
3.33
) and (
3.34
) are more regular than one would expect based on the topology of convergence.
In fact, the convergence does
not
take place, in general, in the topology of
H^{k}(\partial M)
. To see this, consider the operator that maps

f
to
r_{0}^{(n-1)/2}P(\lambda)f
restricted to
\{r=r_{0}\}
. This is a semiclassical FIO of order zero on
\partial M
(with
1/r_{0}
playing the role of semiclassical parameter)
but the canonical relation has fold singularities. This is best seen in the case of flat Euclidean space, where the phase function of this FIO
is
\Phi(\hat{z},\omega)=-\lambda\hat{z}\cdot\omega
,
z=r\hat{z}
,
\hat{z},\omega\in\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
.
Such an FIO cannot be expected to be (and is not) bounded on
H^{k}
uniformly in
r_{0}
, and hence,
convergence cannot be expected to take place, even weakly, in
H^{k}
as
r_{0}\to\infty
.

We combine the previous two propositions to obtain

Proposition 3.9
.

The limits in Proposition
3.4
lie in
H^{k}(\partial M)
.

Proof.

In the notation of Proposition
3.4
, we have
F\in\mathscr{Y}_{\pm}^{s,\mathsf{l}_{\text{max}}+1;1,k-1}
, and therefore it lies in both of the variable order module regularity spaces
\mathscr{Y}^{s,\mathsf{l}_{\pm}+1;0,k}
. It follows that
u_{\pm}\mathrel{\vbox{\par\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=R(\lambda\pm i0)F
lies in
\mathscr{X}^{s+2,\mathsf{l}_{\pm};0,k}
. In particular,
u\mathrel{\vbox{\par\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=u_{+}-u_{-}
is in
H^{s+2,k-1/2}
microlocally away from small neighbourhoods of
\mathscr{R}_{+}
and
\mathscr{R}_{-}
, since
\mathsf{l}_{\pm}=-1/2
there. (Notice that, on the characteristic set but away from the radial sets, the small module is elliptic, so small module regularity of order
k
affords
k
orders of spatial regularity. Away from the characteristic set,
u
is rapidly decaying by microlocal ellipticity, since
(H-\lambda^{2})u=0
.)

We next observe that, by (
3.7
),
u=P(\lambda)f^{\prime}
with
f^{\prime}=(2\lambda i)^{-1}P(\lambda)^{*}F
. Using (
3.11
) we see that
f^{\prime}\in L^{2}
. Applying Proposition
3.3
, with
k=0
, we have
u=(R(\lambda+i0)-R(\lambda-i0))[H,A_{+}]u
. But then, since
[H,A_{+}]u
is in
H^{s+1,k+1/2}
, and microlocalized away from
\mathscr{R}_{+}
and
\mathscr{R}_{-}
, Proposition
3.3
shows that
u=P(\lambda)f
with
f\in H^{k}(\partial M)
.

Consider the limit
\mathscr{L}(\lambda)u_{+}
. We claim that is the same as
\mathscr{L}(\lambda)A_{+}u
. In fact, the difference is

\begin{gathered}\mathscr{L}(\lambda)(u_{+}-A_{+}u)\\
=\mathscr{L}(\lambda)(u_{+}-A_{+}u_{+}-A_{+}u_{-})\\
=\mathscr{L}(\lambda)(A_{-}u_{+}-A_{+}u_{-}).\end{gathered}

Using Remark
3.5
, we see that
\mathscr{L}(\lambda)A_{-}u_{+}=0
and
\mathscr{L}(-\lambda)A_{+}u_{-}=0
, which implies that
\mathscr{L}(\lambda)A_{+}u_{-}=0
. Thus

\mathscr{L}(\lambda)u_{+}=\mathscr{L}(\lambda)A_{+}u
. Similarly,
\mathscr{L}(-\lambda)u_{-}=\mathscr{L}(-\lambda)A_{-}u
. The conclusion then follows from applying Proposition
3.6
to
u
.
∎

4.
Proof of the main theorem

We now elaborate on the construction and regularity of nonlinear
Helmholtz eigenfunction
u
, whose asymptotic behavior is the subject
of the main theorem. We begin by discussing (linear) generalized
eigenfunctions.

Given
f\in H^{k}(\partial M)
, we let
u_{0}=P(\lambda)f
and decompose using Proposition
3.3
into

u_{0}=u_{-}+u_{+},\qquad u_{\pm}=A_{\pm}u_{0}\in\mathscr{X}_{\pm}^{s+2,-1/2-\delta;k,0}(M)

where
A_{\pm}
are as in (
2.12
). According to Proposition
3.6
,
we have

(4.1)

u_{0}=u_{-}+u_{+}=r^{-(n-1)/2}\Big(e^{-i\lambda r}f(y)+e^{+i\lambda r}b_{0}(y)+O_{H^{k-2}}(r^{-\epsilon})\Big),

where
b_{0}
is in
H^{k}(\partial M)
.

To address the nonlinear problem, following
[
GHSZ20
]
, we obtain a nonlinear Helmholtz eigenfunction
u
satisfying

(4.2)

\begin{gathered}u=u_{-}+w,\quad u\mbox{ solves \eqref{eq:equation}},\\
u_{-}\in\mathscr{X}_{-}^{s+2,-1/2-\delta;1,k-1},\quad w\in\mathscr{X}_{+}^{s+2,-1/2-\delta;1,k-1},\quad s\in\mathbb{N},\\
w=u_{+}+R(\lambda+i0)N[u_{-}+w].\end{gathered}

Moreover, if the nonlinearity
N
involves products of
degree
p
, then, as described in detail in Section 4.2 of

[
GHSZ20
]
,

N[u_{-}+w]\in H_{+}^{s,\ell^{\prime};1,k-1}

provided

(4.3)

\ell^{\prime}\leq\frac{(p-1)(n-1)}{2}-\frac{3}{2}-p\delta.

The contraction mapping argument which produces this
w
requires that

\ell^{\prime}=1/2-\delta
for the same
\delta
appearing in
(
2.13
) and (
3.21
),
whence the bound for
p
in (
1.3
), which in fact allows for

\ell^{\prime}=1/2+\delta
for
\delta>0
sufficiently small to satisfy
all the above conditions. Thus
F=N[u_{-}+w]
satisfies the
assumptions of Proposition
3.4
