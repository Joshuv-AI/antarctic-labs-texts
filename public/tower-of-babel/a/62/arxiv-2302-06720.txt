Abstract
. We formalize the observation that the same summability methods converge in a Banach space X X and its dual X ∗ X^{*} . At the same time we determine conditions under which these methods converge in the weak and weak*-topologies on X X and X ∗ X^{*} respectively. We also derive a general limitation theorem, which yields a necessary condition for the convergence of a summability method in X X . These results are then illustrated by applications to a wide variety of function spaces, including spaces of continuous functions, Lebesgue spaces, the disk algebra, Hardy and Bergman spaces, the BMOA space, the Bloch space, and de Branges–Rovnyak spaces. Our approach shows that all these applications flow from just two abstract theorems.

1.
Introduction

Let
X
be a Banach space of holomorphic functions on the open unit disk
{\mathbb{D}}
,
and suppose that
X
contains the polynomials.
Every function
f\in X
has a Taylor expansion
f(z)=\sum_{j\geq 0}a_{j}z^{j}
,
which converges locally uniformly on
{\mathbb{D}}
to
f(z)
.
However, it can happen that the series fails to converge to
f
in the norm of
X
.
This is the case, for example, whenever polynomials are not dense in
X
,
but it may occur even when they are dense.
Here is a short list of examples illustrating various possibilities, ranging from ‘best’ to ‘worst’.

(1)

If
X
is the Hardy space
H^{2}
, then the Taylor series of
f
does converge to
f
in the norm of
X
.
The same is true if
X
is the Dirichlet space or the Bergman space.

(2)

If
X
is the disk algebra
A({\mathbb{D}})
, then the Taylor series of
f
may fail to converge in the norm of
X

(du Bois-Reymond’s example),
but its Cesàro means do converge in norm (Fejér’s theorem).

(3)

If
X
is a de Branges–Rovnyak space
{\mathcal{H}}(b)
, then, for certain choices of
b
and
f
, the Cesàro means may fail to converge in norm, though polynomials are still dense in
X
(see
[
3
]
).

(4)

If
X
fails to have the bounded approximation property, then no lower-triangular summation
method can converge in norm for every function, even though polynomials may still be dense in
X

(see
[
13
]
).

In this article we are mainly interested in cases like (2) and (3), where
some summability methods work and others do not, and the problem is to determine the range of methods
that do work. Our starting point is the fact that, typically, the same methods tend to work in
X
and
in its dual
X^{*}
. This is because the convergence of a summability method often boils down to whether a certain sequence of summation operators is uniformly bounded in norm, and an operator has the same norm as its adjoint.
We formalize this idea, at the same time linking it to weak- and weak*-convergence in
X
and
X^{*}
respectively. We also derive a general limitation theorem, namely a necessary condition for the convergence of summability method in a given Banach space. The proofs of these results are carried out in two steps:
in §
2
we establish a general operator-theoretic result, which is then used
in §
3
to derive the abstract summability theorems.

The rest of the article is devoted to various examples and applications of these results.
In §
4
we consider continuous-function and Lebesgue spaces, as well as the disk algebra.
In §
5
we treat the Hardy and Bergman spaces and their relatives,
\bmoa
and the Bloch space.
Finally, in §
6
, we consider reproducing kernel Hilbert spaces of holomorphic functions, and, in particular, de Branges–Rovnyak spaces.
Some of the applications are already known, others are slight generalizations of known results, and some are completely new.
Our approach shows that they all flow from just two abstract theorems.

2.
Operator theory

In what follows,
X
is a real or complex Banach space.
We write
X^{*}
for the dual space of
X
,
and
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
for the duality pairing between
X
and
X^{*}
.
We use
w
and
w^{*}
to denote the weak and weak* topologies on
X
and
X^{*}
respectively.
Lastly, given a bounded operator
T
on
X
,
we write
T^{*}
for the adjoint operator on
X^{*}
, defined by the relation

\langle x,T^{*}\phi\rangle=\langle Tx,\phi\rangle\quad(x\in X,~\phi\in X^{*}).

The purpose of this section is to establish the following result.

Theorem 2.1
.

Let
(T_{n})_{n\geq 0}
be a sequence of bounded, finite-rank operators on
X

such that

(2.1)

T_{n}T_{m}(X)\subset T_{m}(X)\quad\text{and}\quad T_{n}^{*}T_{m}^{*}(X^{*})\subset T_{m}^{*}(X^{*})\qquad(m,n\geq 0).

Let

Y:=\overline{\spn(\cup_{m\geq 0}T_{m}(X))}\quad\text{and}\quad Z:=\overline{\spn(\cup_{m\geq 0}T_{m}^{*}(X^{*}))},

where the closures are taken in the norm topologies of
X
and
X^{*}
respectively.
Then the following statements are equivalent:

(1)

T_{n}x\to x
in
(X,w)
for all
x\in X
;

(2)

T_{n}x\to x
in
(X,\|\cdot\|)
for all
x\in X
;

(3)

T_{n}^{*}\phi\to\phi
in
(X^{*},w^{*})
for all
\phi\in X^{*}
;

(4)

T_{n}^{*}\phi\to\phi
in
(X^{*},w^{*})
for all
\phi\in Z
, and
Z
is
w^{*}
-sequentially dense in
X^{*}
and
Y=X
;

(5)

T_{n}^{*}\phi\to\phi
in
(X^{*},\|\cdot\|)
for all
\phi\in Z
, and
Z
is
w^{*}
-sequentially dense in
X^{*}

and
Y=X
.

If, further,
X
is reflexive, then these are equivalent to:

(1)

T_{n}^{*}\phi\to\phi
in
(X^{*},\|\cdot\|)
for all
\phi\in X^{*}
.

We shall prove this result via a series of lemmas, beginning with a very simple one.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
(T_{n})_{n\geq 0}
be a sequence of bounded operators on
X
.
The following statements are equivalent:

(1)

T_{n}x\to x
in
(X,w)
for all
x\in X
;

(2)

T_{n}^{*}\phi\to\phi
in
(X^{*},w^{*})
for all
\phi\in X^{*}
.

Proof.

We have

\displaystyle T_{n}x\overset{w}{\longrightarrow}x\quad\forall x\in X

\displaystyle\iff\langle T_{n}x,\phi\rangle\to\langle x,\phi\rangle\quad\forall\phi\in X^{*},\forall x\in X

\displaystyle\iff\langle x,T_{n}^{*}\phi\rangle\to\langle x,\phi\rangle\quad\forall x\in X,\forall\phi\in X^{*}

\displaystyle\iff T_{n}^{*}\phi\overset{w^{*}}{\longrightarrow}\phi\quad\forall\phi\in X^{*}.\qed

We next establish a similar result relating weak convergence in
X
to norm convergence.
In order to obtain an equivalence, we need to impose some conditions on the operators
T_{n}
.

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
(T_{n})_{n\geq 0}
be a sequence of bounded, finite-rank operators on
X
such that

(2.2)

T_{n}T_{m}(X)\subset T_{m}(X)\qquad(m,n\geq 0).

Then the following statements are equivalent:

(1)

T_{n}x\to x
in
(X,w)
for all
x\in X
;

(2)

T_{n}x\to x
in
(X,\|\cdot\|)
for all
x\in X
.

Remarks
.

(1) Lemma
2.3
fails without the assumption ‘finite-rank’.
For example, if
X:=\ell^{2}({\mathbb{Z}}^{+})
and
T_{n}:=I+S^{n}
,
where
S
is the unilateral shift on
\ell^{2}({\mathbb{Z}}^{+})
, then
T_{n}T_{m}=T_{m}T_{n}
for all
m,n
,
so (
2.2
) holds, and

T_{n}x-x=S^{n}x\overset{w}{\longrightarrow}0\quad\forall x\in\ell^{2}({\mathbb{Z}}^{+}),

but

\|T_{n}e_{0}-e_{0}\|_{2}=\|e_{n}\|_{2}\not\to 0.

(2) Lemma
2.3
also fails without the assumption (
2.2
).
For example, if
X=\ell^{2}({\mathbb{Z}}^{+})
and

T_{n}:=\sum_{j=0}^{n}(e_{j}\otimes e_{j})+(e_{n}\otimes e_{0})
,
then each
T_{n}
is a bounded, finite-rank operator, and

T_{n}x-x=-\sum_{j>n}\langle x,e_{j}\rangle e_{j}+\langle x,e_{0}\rangle e_{n}\overset{w}{\longrightarrow}0\quad\forall x\in\ell^{2}({\mathbb{Z}}^{+}),

but

\|T_{n}e_{0}-e_{0}\|_{2}=\|e_{n}\|_{2}\not\to 0.

Note that, in this example, if
m<n
,
then
T_{m}(X)=\spn\{e_{0},e_{1},\dots,e_{m}\}
,
while
T_{n}T_{m}(X)=\spn\{e_{0}+e_{n},e_{1},\dots,e_{m}\}
.

Proof of Lemma
2.3
.

It is enough to prove that (i)
\Rightarrow
(ii), since the reverse implication is obvious.
Suppose then that
T_{n}x\to x
weakly for all
x\in X
.
We need to show that it also converges in norm.
This will be done in four steps.

The first step is to show that
\|T_{n}x-x\|\to 0
if

x\in\spn(\cup_{m}T_{m}(X))
.
Fix
m
and let
\psi
be a continuous linear functional on
T_{m}(X)
.
By the Hahn–Banach theorem,
we can extend
\psi
to a continuous linear functional
\phi
on the whole of
X
.
Therefore, for all
x\in T_{m}(X)
, we have

\langle T_{n}x,\psi\rangle=\langle T_{n}x,\phi\rangle\to\langle x,\phi\rangle=\langle x,\psi\rangle.

This shows that
T_{n}x\to x
in the weakly in
T_{m}(X)
. As
\dim(T_{m}(X))<\infty
,
the weak topology and norm topology coincide, so
\|T_{n}x-x\|\to 0
for all
x\in T_{m}(X)
.
Finally, by linearity, it follows that
\|T_{n}x-x\|\to 0
for all
x\in\spn(\cup_{m}T_{m}(X))
,
as claimed.

The second step is to show that
\spn(\cup_{m}T_{m}(X))
is norm-dense in
X
.
Suppose the contrary. Then, by the Hahn–Banach theorem,
there exists
\phi\in X^{*}\setminus\{0\}
such that

\phi=0
on
\spn(\cup_{m}T_{m}(X))
.
For all
m\geq 1
, we have

\langle x,T_{m}^{*}\phi\rangle=\langle T_{m}x,\phi\rangle=0\quad(x\in X),

so
T_{m}^{*}\phi=0
. Also, from Lemma
2.2
,
we know that
T_{m}^{*}\phi\to\phi
in
(X^{*},w^{*})
. Hence
\phi=0
.
This contradicts the choice of
\phi
.
We conclude that, as claimed,
\spn(\cup_{m}T_{m}(X))
is norm-dense in
X
.

The third step is to show that
\sup_{n}\|T_{n}\|<\infty
. For each
x\in X
, the sequence
(T_{n}x)
converges weakly, so it is weakly bounded. By the Banach–Steinhaus theorem, it is also norm-bounded, i.e.,

\sup_{n}\|T_{n}x\|<\infty
. As this holds for each
x\in X
, a second application of Banach–Steinhaus shows that
\sup_{n}\|T_{n}\|<\infty
, as claimed.

The fourth and final step is to show that
\|T_{n}x-x\|\to 0
for all
x\in X
.
Let
x\in X
and let
\epsilon>0
. By steps 2 and 3,
there exists
x_{0}\in\spn(\cup_{m}T_{m}(X))
such that
\|x-x_{0}\|<\epsilon/(1+\sup_{n}\|T_{n}\|)
.
By step 1, there exists
n_{0}
such that
\|T_{n}x_{0}-x_{0}\|<\epsilon
for all
n\geq n_{0}
.
Then, if
n\geq n_{0}
, we have

\|T_{n}x-x\|\leq\|T_{n}(x-x_{0})\|+\|T_{n}x_{0}-x_{0}\|+\|x_{0}-x\|<3\epsilon.

Thus
\|T_{n}x-x\|\to 0
, as was to be proved.
∎

If
X
is reflexive, then we may interchange the roles of
X
and
X^{*}
in
Lemma
2.3
, and deduce the following corollary.

Corollary 2.4
.

Suppose that
X
is reflexive.
Let
(T_{n})_{n\geq 0}
be a sequence of bounded, finite-rank operators on
X
such that

(2.3)

T_{n}^{*}T_{m}^{*}(X^{*})\subset T_{m}^{*}(X^{*})\qquad(m,n\geq 0).

Then the following statements are equivalent:

(1)

T_{n}^{*}\phi\to\phi
in
(X^{*},w^{*})
for all
\phi\in X^{*}
;

(2)

T_{n}^{*}\phi\to\phi
in
(X^{*},\|\cdot\|)
for all
\phi\in X^{*}
.

Remark
.

If
X
is not reflexive, then Corollary
2.4
may break down.
For example, let
X:=\ell^{1}({\mathbb{Z}}^{+})
and let
T_{n}:\ell^{1}({\mathbb{Z}}^{+})\to\ell^{1}({\mathbb{Z}}^{+})
be the projection onto the first
n
coordinates. Its adjoint
T_{n}^{*}:\ell^{\infty}({\mathbb{Z}}^{+})\to\ell^{\infty}({\mathbb{Z}}^{+})
is also the projection onto the first
n
coordinates.
The sequences
(T_{n})
and
(T_{n}^{*})
satisfy
(
2.2
) and (
2.3
) respectively.
Also
\|T_{n}x-x\|_{1}\to 0
for all
x\in\ell^{1}({\mathbb{Z}}^{+})
,
so by Lemmas
2.2
and
2.3

we have
T_{n}^{*}\phi\to\phi
weak* for all
\phi\in\ell^{\infty}({\mathbb{Z}}^{+})
.
However, if
\phi_{0}:=(1,1,1,\dots)
, then

\|T_{n}^{*}\phi_{0}-\phi_{0}\|_{\infty}\not\to 0
.
Note that, in this example, the norm-closure of
\spn\cup_{n}T_{n}^{*}(X^{*})
is
c_{0}
.

Here is a version of Corollary
2.4
valid for all
X
, not necessarily reflexive.

Lemma 2.5
.

Let
(T_{n})_{n\geq 0}
be a
sequence of bounded, finite-rank operators on
X
such that

(2.4)

T_{n}(T_{m}(X))\subset T_{m}(X)\quad\text{and}\quad T_{n}^{*}(T_{m}^{*}(X^{*}))\subset T_{m}^{*}(X^{*})\quad(m,n\geq 0).

Let

Y:=\overline{\spn(\cup_{m\geq 0}T_{m}(X))}\quad\text{and}\quad Z:=\overline{\spn(\cup_{m\geq 0}T_{m}^{*}(X^{*}))},

where the closures are taken in the norm topologies of
X
and
X^{*}
respectively.
Then the following statements are equivalent:

(1)

T_{n}^{*}\phi\to\phi
in
(X^{*},w^{*})
for all
\phi\in X^{*}
;

(2)

T_{n}^{*}\phi\to\phi
in
(X^{*},w^{*})
for all
\phi\in Z
, and
Z
is
w^{*}
-sequentially dense in
X^{*}
and
Y=X
;

(3)

T_{n}^{*}\phi\to\phi
in
(X^{*},\|\cdot\|)
for all
\phi\in Z
, and
Z
is
w^{*}
-sequentially dense in
X^{*}
and
Y=X
.

For the proof, we need a further lemma.

Lemma 2.6
.

Let
Z
be a subspace of
X^{*}
that is
w^{*}
-sequentially dense in
X^{*}
.
Then the
w^{*}
-closure of the unit ball of
Z
contains a positive multiple of the unit ball of
X^{*}
.

Proof.

Let
C
be the closure in
(X^{*},w^{*})
of the unit ball of
Z
.
Given
\phi\in X^{*}
, there exists a sequence
(\phi_{n})
in
Z
such that
\phi_{n}
is
w^{*}
-convergent to
\phi
.
By the Banach–Steinhaus theorem,
since
(\phi_{n})
is
w^{*}
-bounded, it is norm-bounded. Hence there exists an integer
m\geq 1

such that
\phi\in mC
.
Thus we have
\cup_{m\geq 1}mC=X^{*}
.
Each set
mC
is
w^{*}
-closed in
X^{*}
,
so it is certainly norm-closed.
We may therefore apply Baire’s theorem to deduce that
there exists
m_{0}
such that
m_{0}C
has non-empty norm-interior.
As
m_{0}C
is a convex, symmetric set,
it follows that
0
belongs to the norm interior of
m_{0}C
.
In other words,
C
contains a ball around
0
.
∎

Proof of Lemma
2.5
.

[(i)
\Rightarrow
(ii)]: Suppose that (i) holds.
Then it is obvious that
T_{n}^{*}\phi\to\phi
for all
\phi\in Z
,
and also that
Z
is
w^{*}
-sequentially dense in
X^{*}
.
Finally, by Lemmas
2.2
and
2.3
,
(i) implies that
T_{n}x\to x
in norm for all
x\in X
,
and this entails that
Y=X
.

[(ii)
\Rightarrow
(iii)]: Suppose that (ii) holds.
By (ii), we have
T_{n}^{*}\phi\to\phi
in
(X^{*},w^{*})
for each
\phi\in Z
.
By the Banach–Steinhaus theorem, applied to the sequence
(T_{n}^{*}|_{Z})
on
Z
,
we have
\sup_{n}\|T_{n}^{*}|_{Z}\|<\infty
.

Let
m\geq 0
. Then
T_{n}^{*}\phi\to\phi
in
(X^{*},w^{*})
for all

\phi\in T_{m}^{*}(X^{*})
. Since
\dim(T_{m}^{*}(X^{*}))<\infty
,
it follows that
T_{n}^{*}\phi\to\phi
in norm for all

\phi\in T_{m}^{*}(X^{*})
. As this holds for each
m\geq 0
,
we deduce that
T_{n}^{*}\phi\to\phi
in norm for all

\phi\in\spn(\cup_{m\geq 0}T_{m}^{*}(X^{*}))
.
Lastly, as
\sup_{n}\|T_{n}^{*}|_{Z}\|<\infty
,
it follows that
T_{n}^{*}\phi\to\phi
in norm for all

\phi\in Z
.

[(iii)
\Rightarrow
(i)]: Suppose that (iii) holds.
As noted above,
\sup_{n}\|T_{n}^{*}|_{Z}\|<\infty
.
By (iii) and Lemma
2.6
,
the unit ball of
Z
is
w^{*}
-dense in a ball of radius
r>0
in
X^{*}
.
It follows that, for every operator
T
on
X
, we have
\|T^{*}\|\leq\|T^{*}|_{Z}\|/r
. Combining these facts,
we deduce that
K:=\sup_{n}\|T_{n}^{*}\|<\infty
.

Let
m\geq 0
and let
Z_{m}:=\{\phi|_{T_{m}(X)}:\phi\in Z\}
.
Since
Z
is
w^{*}
-sequentially dense in
X^{*}

and
\dim T_{m}(X)<\infty
, it follows that

Z_{m}
is norm-dense in
T_{m}(X)^{*}
.
Let
x\in T_{m}(X)
. Let
\psi\in T_{m}(X)^{*}
and let
\epsilon>0
. Then there exists
\phi\in Z
with
\|\psi-\phi|_{T_{m}(X)}\|<\epsilon
. Since
\|T_{n}^{*}\phi-\phi\|\to 0
, there exists
N
such that

n\geq N\quad\Rightarrow\quad\|T_{n}^{*}\phi-\phi\|<\epsilon.

Then

\langle T_{n}x-x,\,\psi\rangle=\langle x,T_{n}^{*}\phi-\phi\rangle+\langle T_{n}x-x,\,\psi-\phi|_{T_{m}(X)}\rangle,

so, for all
n\geq N
,

|\langle T_{n}x-x,\,\psi\rangle|\leq\|x\|\|T_{n}^{*}\phi-\phi\|+(K+1)\|x\|\|\psi-\phi|_{T_{m}(X)}\|\leq(\|x\|+K+1)\epsilon.

Thus
T_{n}x\to x
weakly for all
x\in T_{m}(X)
. As
\dim T_{m}(X)<\infty
, it follows that

T_{n}x\to x
in norm for all
x\in T_{m}(X)
.
As this holds for all
m\geq 0
,
we deduce that
T_{n}x\to x
in norm for all
x\in\spn(\cup_{m}T_{m}(X))
.
Since
\sup_{n}\|T_{n}\|=\sup_{n}\|T_{n}^{*}\|=K<\infty
, it follows that

T_{n}x\to x
for all
x\in Y
.

By (iii), we have
Y=X
. Thus
T_{n}x\to x
in norm for all
x\in X
.
By Lemmas
2.2
and
2.3
,
this implies
T_{n}^{*}\phi\to\phi
in
(X^{*},w^{*})
for all
\phi\in X^{*}
.
∎

Remark
.

Although statements (i)–(iii) are all about the adjoint operators
T_{n}^{*}
,
we nonetheless need the invariance assumption that

T_{n}(T_{m}(X))\subset T_{m}(X)
in (
2.4
).
This assumption is used in the proof of the implication
(iii)
\Rightarrow
(i),
and the result is actually false without this assumption.
Here is a counterexample.

Let
X:=\ell^{1}({\mathbb{N}})
. For
n\geq 1
define
T_{n}:\ell^{1}({\mathbb{N}})\to\ell^{1}({\mathbb{N}})
by

T_{n}:=P_{2n}(S^{n}+I),

where
S:\ell^{1}({\mathbb{N}})\to\ell^{1}({\mathbb{N}})
is the unilateral shift
and
P_{2n}:\ell^{1}({\mathbb{N}})\to\ell^{1}({\mathbb{N}})
is the projection onto the first
2n
coordinates.
Taking adjoints, we have
T_{n}^{*}:\ell^{\infty}({\mathbb{N}})\to\ell^{\infty}({\mathbb{N}})
given by

T_{n}^{*}=(S^{*n}+I)P_{2n}
.
The following properties are easily verified:

•

T_{m}(X)=\spn\{e_{1},\dots,e_{2m}\}
for all
m\geq 1
.

•

T_{m}^{*}(X^{*})=\spn\{e_{1},\dots,e_{2m}\}
for all
m\geq 1
.

•

T_{n}^{*}(T_{m}^{*}(X^{*}))\subset T_{m}^{*}(X^{*})
for all
m,n\geq 1
.

•

Y=\ell^{1}({\mathbb{N}})=X
.

•

Z=c_{0}({\mathbb{N}})
, which is
w^{*}
-sequentially dense in
X^{*}=\ell^{\infty}({\mathbb{N}})
.

If
\phi\in c_{0}({\mathbb{N}})
, then we have

\|T_{n}^{*}\phi-\phi\|_{\infty}\leq\|S^{*n}\phi\|_{\infty}+\|P_{2n}\phi-\phi\|_{\infty}\to 0\quad(n\to\infty).

Thus
T_{n}^{*}\phi\to\phi
in norm for all
\phi\in Z
. Therefore (iii) holds.

However, if
\phi\in\ell^{\infty}({\mathbb{N}})\setminus c_{0}({\mathbb{N}})
,
then

\langle e_{1},\,T_{n}^{*}\phi-\phi\rangle=\langle T_{n}e_{1}-e_{1},\,\phi\rangle=\langle e_{n+1},\phi\rangle\not\to 0\quad(n\to\infty),

and so
T_{n}^{*}\phi\not\to\phi
weak*. Therefore (i) fails.

Finally, the main result of the section,
Theorem
2.1
, follows by combining
Lemmas
2.2
,
2.3
and
2.5
.

3.
Summability

3.1.
The basic set-up

In the rest of the paper,
we consider the following set-up. As before,
X
denotes a Banach space with dual space
X^{*}
.
Let
(e_{k})_{k\geq 0}
and
(\psi_{k})_{k\geq 0}
be sequences in
X
and
X^{*}
respectively such that

\left\{\begin{aligned} \langle e_{j},\psi_{k}\rangle&=0,\quad\forall j,k,~j\neq k,\\
\langle e_{k},\psi_{k}\rangle&\neq 0,\quad\forall k.\end{aligned}\right.

For each
k\geq 0
, define
P_{k}:X\to X
by

P_{k}:=\frac{e_{k}\otimes\psi_{k}}{\langle e_{k},\psi_{k}\rangle}.

Explicitly,

P_{k}(x):=\frac{\langle x,\psi_{k}\rangle}{\langle e_{k},\psi_{k}\rangle}e_{k}\quad(x\in X).

Clearly
P_{k}(e_{k})=e_{k}
and
P_{k}(e_{j})=0
if
j\neq k
.
It is easy to see that
P_{k}
is a rank-one projection (
P_{k}^{2}=P_{k}
) with

\|P_{k}\|=\frac{\|e_{k}\|\|\psi_{k}\|}{|\langle e_{k},\psi_{k}\rangle|}.

Its adjoint
P_{k}^{*}:X^{*}\to X^{*}
is given by

P_{k}^{*}=\frac{\psi_{k}\otimes e_{k}}{\langle e_{k},\psi_{k}\rangle}.

Let
A=(a_{nk})_{n,k\geq 0}
be an infinite matrix of complex scalars such that,
for each
n\geq 0
,

(3.1)

\sum_{k\geq 0}|a_{nk}|\|P_{k}\|<\infty.

We define the associated summation operators
S_{n}^{A}:X\to X~(n\geq 0)

by the absolutely convergent series

S_{n}^{A}:=\sum_{k\geq 0}a_{nk}P_{k}.

Explicitly, we have

\displaystyle S_{n}^{A}(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\geq 0}a_{nk}\frac{\langle x,\psi_{k}\rangle}{\langle e_{k},\psi_{k}\rangle}e_{k}\quad(x\in X),

\displaystyle(S_{n}^{A})^{*}(\phi)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\geq 0}a_{nk}\frac{\langle e_{k},\phi\rangle}{\langle e_{k},\psi_{k}\rangle}\psi_{k}\quad(\phi\in X^{*}).

3.2.
The main equivalence

With this notation established, our goal is to prove the following theorem.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
Z
be the norm-closure in
X^{*}
of
\spn\{\psi_{k}:k\geq 0\}
.
Suppose that there exists at least one matrix
A^{0}
satisfying (
3.1
)
such that
S_{n}^{A^{0}}x\to x
(either weakly or in norm) for all
x\in X
.
Then, for every matrix
A
satisfying (
3.1
),
the following statements are equivalent:

(1)

S_{n}^{A}(x)\to x
in
(X,w)
for all
x\in X
;

(2)

S_{n}^{A}(x)\to x
in
(X,\|\cdot\|)
for all
x\in X
;

(3)

(S_{n}^{A})^{*}(\phi)\to\phi
in
(X^{*},w^{*})
for all
\phi\in X^{*}
;

(4)

(S_{n}^{A})^{*}(\phi)\to\phi
in
(X^{*},w^{*})
for all
\phi\in Z
;

(5)

(S_{n}^{A})^{*}(\phi)\to\phi
in
(X^{*},\|\cdot\|)
for all
\phi\in Z
.

If, further
X
, is reflexive, then these are equivalent to:

(1)

(S_{n}^{A})^{*}(\phi)\to\phi
in
(X^{*},\|\cdot\|)
for all
\phi\in X^{*}
.

Proof.

By (
3.1
), for each
n\geq 0
, there exists
K_{n}\geq 0
such that

\sum_{k>K_{n}}|a_{nk}|\|P_{k}\|<2^{-n}.

Define
T_{n}^{A}:X\to X
by

T_{n}^{A}:=\sum_{k=0}^{K_{n}}a_{nk}P_{k}.

Clearly we have
\|S_{n}^{A}-T_{n}^{A}\|<2^{-n}
for all
n
,
so each of the statements (i)–(vi) holds iff it holds with
S_{n}^{A}
replaced by
T_{n}^{A}
.

The operators
T_{n}^{A}
are bounded, finite-rank operators on
X
.
The images of
T_{n}^{A}
and
(T_{n}^{A})^{*}
are given by

(3.2)

\displaystyle T_{n}^{A}(X)

\displaystyle=\spn\{e_{k}:1\leq k\leq K_{n},~a_{nk}\neq 0\},

(3.3)

\displaystyle(T_{n}^{A})^{*}(X^{*})

\displaystyle=\spn\{\psi_{k}:1\leq k\leq K_{n},~a_{nk}\neq 0\}.

It follows that
T_{n}^{A}T_{m}^{A}(X)\subset T_{m}^{A}(X)
and
(T_{n}^{A})^{*}(T_{m}^{A})^{*}(X^{*})\subset(T_{m}^{A})^{*}(X^{*})
for all
m,n
.
Thus Theorem
2.1
applies.

We claim that, for each
k
, there is at least one
n
such that
a_{nk}\neq 0
.
Indeed, if
a_{nk}=0
for all
n
,
then
T_{n}^{A}(e_{k})=0
and
(T_{n}^{A})^{*}(\psi_{k})=0
for all
n
, and so
(under the relevant assumption (i)–(vi)),
either
e_{k}=0
or
\psi_{k}=0
.
Neither of these can be true, since we are assuming that
\langle e_{k},\psi_{k}\rangle\neq 0
.

It follows that, in the notation of Theorem
2.1
,

\displaystyle Y

\displaystyle:=\overline{\spn(\cup_{m\geq 0}(T_{m}^{A})(X))}=\overline{\spn\{e_{k}:k\geq 0\}},

\displaystyle Z

\displaystyle:=\overline{\spn(\cup_{m\geq 0}(T_{m}^{A})^{*}(X^{*}))}=\overline{\spn\{\psi_{k}:k\geq 0\}}.

In particular, this reconciles the definition of
Z
given in the statement of Theorem
3.1

with that in Theorem
2.1
.

From Theorem
2.1
, applied with
A=A^{0}
,
we deduce that
Y=X
and that
Z
is
w^{*}
-sequentially dense in
X^{*}
.
Reapplying Theorem
2.1
with a general
A
now gives the result.
∎

Remarks
.

(1) The implications (i)
\iff
(ii)
\iff
(iii)
\Longrightarrow
(iv)
\iff
(v) hold without the assumption about
A^{0}
.
The existence of
A^{0}
is needed only for the implication (iv)
\Longrightarrow
(iii).

(2) In the concrete examples that we shall treat below,
the existence of
A^{0}
will be an obvious consequence of some version of Fejér’s theorem.

(3)
However, there do exist examples where there exists no matrix
A^{0}
satisfying (ii). For instance, this is the case whenever
the Banach space
X
fails to have the bounded approximation property (see
[
13
]
).
The article
[
10
]
contains another such example, in which
X
is actually a Hilbert space.

3.3.
Limitation theorems

Let
A=(a_{nk})_{n,k\geq 0}
be an infinite scalar matrix satisfying (
3.1
).
We say that
A
admits a left inverse if there exists a scalar matrix
B=(b_{jn})_{j,n\geq 0}
such that

(3.4)

\sum_{n\geq 0}|b_{jn}|<\infty\quad(j\geq 0)\qquad\text{and}\qquad\sum_{n\geq 0}b_{jn}a_{nk}=\begin{cases}1,&j=k,\\
0,&j\neq k.\end{cases}

Theorem 3.2
.

Suppose that
S_{n}^{A}(x)\to x
(weakly or in norm) for all
x\in X
, and that
A
admits a left inverse
B
.
Then, writing
B_{j}:=\sum_{n\geq 0}|b_{jn}|
, we have

\frac{\|e_{j}\|\|\psi_{j}\|}{|\langle e_{j},\psi_{j}\rangle|}=O(B_{j})\quad(j\to\infty).

Remark
.

This theorem is of interest when
\|e_{j}\|\|\psi_{j}\|/|\langle e_{j},\psi_{j}\rangle|
grows with
j
,
since it then places limitations on possible matrices
A
for which summability holds.
Hardy
[
8
, p.57]
calls such results limitation theorems.

Proof.

For each
j\geq 0
, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{n\geq 0}b_{jn}S_{n}^{A}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n\geq 0}b_{jn}\Bigl(\sum_{k\geq 0}a_{nk}P_{k}\Bigr)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\geq 0}\Bigl(\sum_{n\geq 0}b_{jn}a_{nk}\Bigr)P_{k}=\sum_{k\geq 0}\delta_{jk}P_{k}=P_{j},

the exchange of sums being justified by absolute convergence in (
3.1
) and (
3.4
).
Now as
S_{n}^{A}(x)\to x
for all
x\in X
, we have
\sup_{n}\|S_{n}^{A}(x)\|<\infty
for each
x\in X
,
and hence, by the Banach–Steinhaus theorem,
\sup_{n}\|S_{n}^{A}\|<\infty
. It follows that

\|P_{j}\|=\Bigl\|\sum_{n\geq 0}b_{jn}S_{n}^{A}\Bigr\|\leq\sum_{n\geq 0}|b_{jn}|\|S_{n}^{A}\|\leq B_{j}\sup_{n}\|S_{n}^{A}\|,

in other words,
\|P_{j}\|=O(B_{j})
. Finally, since
\|P_{j}\|=\|e_{j}\|\|\psi_{j}\|/|\langle e_{j},\psi_{j}\rangle|
,
the result follows.
∎

In practice, the matrix
B
is not known explicitly, so estimating
B_{j}
may be problematic.
Here is one case where we can do it.

We denote by
W^{+}({\mathbb{D}})
the holomorphic Wiener algebra, namely

W^{+}({\mathbb{D}}):=\Bigl\{f(z)=\sum_{k\geq 0}\widehat{f}(k)z^{k}:\sum_{k\geq 0}|\widehat{f}(k)|<\infty\Bigr\}.

Theorem 3.3
.

Let
f\in W^{+}({\mathbb{D}})
with
f(0)\neq 0
, and let
(\gamma_{n})_{n\geq 0}
be increasing sequence in
(0,\infty)
.
Let
A=(a_{nk})
be the lower-triangular matrix with entries given by

a_{nk}:=\gamma_{n}^{-1}\widehat{(1/f)}(n-k)\quad(0\leq k\leq n).

Then
A
has a left inverse
B
, where
B=(b_{jn})
is a lower-triangular matrix and

\sum_{n\geq 0}|b_{jn}|\asymp\gamma_{j}\quad(j\to\infty).

Proof.

Let
B
be the lower-triangular matrix with entries
(b_{jn})
given by

b_{jn}:=\gamma_{n}\widehat{f}(j-n)\quad(0\leq n\leq j).

For each fixed
j
, there are only finitely many
n
for which
b_{jn}\neq 0
, so
the first condition in (
3.4
) is clearly satisfied. As for the second condition,
we have

\displaystyle\sum_{n\geq 0}b_{jn}a_{nk}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n\geq 0}\gamma_{n}\widehat{f}(j-n)\gamma_{n}^{-1}\widehat{(1/f)}(n-k)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p,q\geq 0\\
p+q=j-k\end{subarray}}\widehat{f}(p)\widehat{(1/f)}(q)=\widehat{1}(j-k)=\delta_{jk}.

Thus
B
is indeed a left inverse of
A
. Further, we have

\sum_{n\geq 0}|b_{jn}|=\sum_{n=0}^{j}\gamma_{n}|\widehat{f}(j-n)|\leq\gamma_{j}\sum_{\ell\geq 0}|\widehat{f}(\ell)|

and

\sum_{n\geq 0}|b_{jn}|=\sum_{n=0}^{j}\gamma_{n}|\widehat{f}(j-n)|\geq\gamma_{j}|\widehat{f}(0)|,

By assumption, we have
\sum_{\ell\geq 0}|\widehat{f}(\ell)|<\infty
and
|\widehat{f}(0)|>0
. It follows that

\sum_{n\geq 0}|b_{jn}|\asymp\gamma_{j}
, as claimed.
∎

Remark
.

In this case, it is easy to see that in fact
B
is a two-sided inverse of
A
.

We illustrate these results by applying them to one particular family of summability methods,
namely the Cesàro means.
Given
\alpha>-1
, let
A=(a_{nk})_{n,k\geq 0}
be the lower-triangular matrix defined by

(3.5)

a_{nk}:=\binom{n}{k}\Big/\binom{n+\alpha}{k}\quad(0\leq k\leq n).

(As usual, binomial coefficients with non-integer arguments are defined using the Gamma function.)
With this choice of
A
, we write

\sigma_{n}^{\alpha}(x):=S_{n}^{A}(x)\quad(x\in X).

In particular, we write
s_{n}
for
\sigma_{n}^{0}
and
\sigma_{n}
for
\sigma_{n}^{1}
.

We remark that, if
-1<\alpha\leq\beta
, then
\sigma_{n}^{\beta}
includes
\sigma_{n}^{\alpha}
in the sense that,
if
\sigma_{n}^{\alpha}(x)\to x
for some
x\in X
, then also
\sigma_{n}^{\beta}(x)\to x
. (For scalars this is well known;
for Banach spaces, it follows by
[
11
, Theorem 5.1]
.)

The following limitation theorem is an abstract version of
[
8
, Theorem 46]
.

Theorem 3.4
.

Let
\alpha\geq 0
. If
\sigma_{n}^{\alpha}(x)\to x
(weakly or in norm) for all
x\in X
, then

\frac{\|e_{j}\|\|\psi_{j}\|}{|\langle e_{j},\psi_{j}\rangle|}=O(j^{\alpha})\quad(j\to\infty).

Proof.

Let
A
be the Cesàro matrix defined by (
3.5
).
A computation gives

a_{nk}=\binom{n}{k}\Big/\binom{n+\alpha}{k}=\binom{n-k+\alpha}{\alpha}\Bigl/\binom{n+\alpha}{\alpha}=\gamma_{n}^{-1}\widehat{g}(n-k),

where
(\gamma_{n})
is the increasing sequence given by

\gamma_{n}:=\binom{n+\alpha}{\alpha},

and where
\widehat{g}(m)
are the Taylor coefficients of the function

g(z):=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\binom{m+\alpha}{\alpha}z^{m}=(1-z)^{-\alpha-1}.

Clearly
g=1/f
, where
f(z):=(1-z)^{\alpha+1}
.
Since the Taylor coefficients of
f
satisfy

\sum_{k\geq 1}|\widehat{f}(k)|=\sum_{k\geq 1}\Bigl|\frac{\widehat{(f^{\prime})}(k-1)}{k}\Bigr|\leq\Bigl(\sum_{k\geq 1}\frac{1}{k^{2}}\Bigr)^{1/2}\|f^{\prime}\|_{H^{2}}<\infty,

we have
f\in W^{+}({\mathbb{D}})
, and Theorem
3.3
applies. We deduce that
A
has a lower-triangular inverse
B=(b_{jn})
such that

\sum_{n\geq 0}|b_{jn}|=O(\gamma_{j})=O\Bigl(\binom{j+\alpha}{\alpha}\Bigr)=O(j^{\alpha})\quad(j\to\infty),

the last equality by Stirling’s formula. The result now follows by applying Theorem
3.2
.
∎

4.
Applications in spaces of continuous functions

4.1.
Fourier series

Probably the best-known applications of summability are to Fourier series,
so, for our first example, we see what the abstract theory developed in the previous
two sections tells us about this case.

Let
{\mathbb{T}}
denote the unit circle. We write
C({\mathbb{T}})
for the space of complex-valued continuous
functions on
{\mathbb{T}}
, with the usual sup-norm
\|f\|_{\infty}:=\sup_{\mathbb{T}}|f|
.
The dual space of
C({\mathbb{T}})
may be identified with
M({\mathbb{T}})
,
the space of finite complex Borel measures on
{\mathbb{T}}
, the duality being given by

\langle f,\mu\rangle:=\int_{\mathbb{T}}f(\zeta)\,d\mu(\overline{\zeta})\quad(f\in C({\mathbb{T}}),~\mu\in M({\mathbb{T}})).

Under this pairing,
M({\mathbb{T}})
inherits the norm of
C({\mathbb{T}})^{*}
, which is just the total-variation norm.

The absolutely continuous measures on
{\mathbb{T}}
form a closed subspace of
M({\mathbb{T}})
, which can be identified with

L^{1}({\mathbb{T}})
via
g\leftrightarrow g(e^{it})dt/2\pi
. The restriction of the total variation norm to
L^{1}({\mathbb{T}})

is just the usual
L^{1}
norm.

We now proceed to apply the theory developed in §
3
.
Let
X:=C({\mathbb{T}})
, and for
k\in{\mathbb{Z}}
let
e_{k}:=e^{ikt}
and
\psi_{k}:=e^{ikt}\,dt/2\pi
.
(Here the index
k
runs through all the integers rather than just the positive integers,
but this creates no problems.)
Then
\|e_{k}\|_{\infty}=\|\psi_{k}\|_{1}=1
and
\langle e_{k},\psi_{k}\rangle=1
for all
k\in{\mathbb{Z}}
.

Let
A=(a_{nk})_{n\geq 0,k\in{\mathbb{Z}}}
be an infinite matrix of complex scalars
such that
\sum_{k\in{\mathbb{Z}}}|a_{nk}|<\infty
for each
n\geq 0
. Then, for each
n\geq 0
, we have

\displaystyle S_{n}^{A}(f)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\in{\mathbb{Z}}}a_{nk}\frac{\langle f,\psi_{k}\rangle}{\langle e_{k},\psi_{k}\rangle}e_{k}=\sum_{k\in{\mathbb{Z}}}a_{nk}\widehat{f}(k)e^{ikt}\quad(f\in C({\mathbb{T}})),

\displaystyle(S_{n}^{A})^{*}(\mu)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\in{\mathbb{Z}}}a_{nk}\frac{\langle e_{k},\mu\rangle}{\langle e_{k},\psi_{k}\rangle}\psi_{k}=\sum_{k\in{\mathbb{Z}}}a_{nk}\widehat{\mu}(k)e^{ikt}\,\frac{dt}{2\pi}\quad(\mu\in M({\mathbb{T}})).

By Fejér’s theorem, we have
\|\sigma_{n}f-f\|_{\infty}\to 0
as
n\to\infty
for all
f\in C({\mathbb{T}})
,
so there exists at least one matrix
A^{0}
for which
\|S_{n}^{A^{0}}(f)-f\|_{\infty}\to 0
. Thus
Theorem
3.1
applies. In the notation of Theorem
3.1
,

Z
is the norm-closure in
M({\mathbb{T}})
of
\spn\{e^{ikt}dt/2\pi:k\in{\mathbb{Z}}\}
, which is exactly
L^{1}({\mathbb{T}})
.
Thus we obtain the following theorem.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
(a_{nk})_{n\geq 0,k\in{\mathbb{Z}}}
be an infinite matrix of complex scalars such that
\sum_{k\in{\mathbb{Z}}}|a_{nk}|<\infty

for each
n\geq 0
. Then the following statements are equivalent:

(1)

\sum_{k\in{\mathbb{Z}}}a_{nk}\widehat{f}(k)e^{ikt}\to f
in
(C({\mathbb{T}}),w)
, for all
f\in C({\mathbb{T}})
;

(2)

\sum_{k\in{\mathbb{Z}}}a_{nk}\widehat{f}(k)e^{ikt}\to f
in
(C({\mathbb{T}}),\|\cdot\|_{\infty})
), for all
f\in C({\mathbb{T}})
;

(3)

\sum_{k\in{\mathbb{Z}}}a_{nk}\widehat{\mu}(k)e^{ikt}dt/2\pi\to\mu
in
(M({\mathbb{T}}),w^{*})
, for all
\mu\in M({\mathbb{T}})
;

(4)

\sum_{k\in{\mathbb{Z}}}a_{nk}\widehat{g}(k)e^{ikt}dt/2\pi\to g(e^{it})dt/2\pi
in
(M({\mathbb{T}}),w^{*})
for all
g\in L^{1}({\mathbb{T}})
;

(5)

\sum_{k\in{\mathbb{Z}}}a_{nk}\widehat{g}(k)e^{ikt}\to g
in
(L^{1}({\mathbb{T}}),\|\cdot\|_{1})
, for all
g\in L^{1}({\mathbb{T}})
.

We now use this theorem to deduce some classical results about Cesàro summation of Fourier series.
Since the summation index
k
runs over
{\mathbb{Z}}
rather than
{\mathbb{Z}}^{+}
, the definition of
\sigma_{n}^{\alpha}
needs to be
modified accordingly, taking
a_{nk}:=\binom{n}{|k|}/\binom{n+\alpha}{|k|}
for
|k|\leq n
and
a_{nk}:=0
for
|k|>n
. As usual, we write
s_{n}
for
\sigma_{n}^{0}
and
\sigma_{n}
for
\sigma_{n}^{1}
(this was already implicit when we quoted
Fejér’s theorem above).

Theorem 4.2
.

(1)

If
\mu=\delta_{1}
, then
s_{n}(\mu)\not\to\mu
in
(M({\mathbb{T}}),w^{*})
.

(2)

There exists
f\in C({\mathbb{T}})
such that
s_{n}(f)\not\to f
in
(C({\mathbb{T}}),w)
.

(3)

There exists
g\in L^{1}({\mathbb{T}})
such that
s_{n}(g)\not\to g
in
(M({\mathbb{T}}),w^{*})
.

Proof.

(1) If
\mu=\delta_{1}
, then
\widehat{\mu}(k)=1
for all
k\in{\mathbb{Z}}
, so

s_{n}(\mu)=\sum_{k=-n}^{n}e^{ikt}\frac{dt}{2\pi}=D_{n}(t)\,\frac{dt}{2\pi},

where
D_{n}(t)
is the Dirichlet kernel. We know that

\|s_{n}(\mu)\|_{M({\mathbb{T}})}=\|D_{n}\|_{1}\asymp\log n\to\infty,

so
(s_{n}(\mu))
is not weak*-convergent.

Parts (2) and (3) now follow from (1) by applying Theorem
4.1
.
∎

Remark
.

Part (2) is a weak form of a famous result of du Bois-Reymond,
who showed that the Fourier series of a continuous function may diverge at a point.

Theorem 4.3
.

Let
\alpha>0
.

(1)

\|\sigma_{n}^{\alpha}(f)-f\|_{\infty}\to 0
for all
f\in C({\mathbb{T}})
.

(2)

\|\sigma_{n}^{\alpha}(g)-g\|_{1}\to 0
for all
g\in L^{1}({\mathbb{T}})
.

(3)

\sigma_{n}^{\alpha}(\mu)\to\mu
in
(M({\mathbb{T}}),w^{*})
for all
\mu\in M({\mathbb{T}})
.

Proof.

Part (1) is a classical result of M. Riesz
[
14
]
.
Parts (2) and (3) follow, using Theorem
4.1
.
∎

4.2.
The disk algebra

The disk algebra
A({\mathbb{D}})
consists of those holomorphic functions on
the open unit disk
{\mathbb{D}}
that have a continuous extension to
\overline{{\mathbb{D}}}
.
It is a Banach space (indeed a Banach algebra) with respect to the sup-norm.

By the maximum principle, the map
f\mapsto f|_{\mathbb{T}}
is an isometry
of
A({\mathbb{D}})
into
C({\mathbb{T}})
, so
A({\mathbb{D}})
can be identified with a closed subspace
A
of
C({\mathbb{T}})
.
In fact
A=\{f\in C({\mathbb{T}}):\widehat{f}(k)=0~\forall k<0\}
.
Therefore the dual of
A({\mathbb{D}})
may be identified with the quotient
M({\mathbb{T}})/A^{\perp}
,
where

A^{\perp}:=\Bigl\{\mu\in M({\mathbb{T}}):\int_{\mathbb{T}}f\,d\mu=0~\forall f\in A\Bigr\}.

By the F. & M. Riesz theorem, if
\mu\in A^{\perp}
,
then
\mu
is absolutely continuous with respect to Lebesgue measure on
{\mathbb{T}}
, say
\mu=h\,dt/2\pi
. The condition that

h\,dt/2\pi\in A^{\perp}
is equivalent to
h\in\overline{H^{1}_{0}}
,
where

\overline{H}_{0}^{1}:=\{h\in L^{1}({\mathbb{T}}):\widehat{h}(k)=0~\forall k\geq 0\}.

Thus we can identify the dual space
A({\mathbb{D}})^{*}
with
M({\mathbb{T}})/\overline{H^{1}_{0}}
.

There is another way to express this duality, using
Cauchy transforms. Given
\mu\in M({\mathbb{T}})
, we define its
Cauchy transform
K\mu:{\mathbb{D}}\to{\mathbb{C}}
by

K\mu(z):=\int_{\mathbb{T}}\frac{d\mu(\zeta)}{1-\overline{\zeta}z}\quad(z\in{\mathbb{D}}).

Notice that
K\mu\equiv 0\iff\mu\in\overline{H_{0}^{1}}
.
Hence the map
[\mu]\mapsto K\mu
is a linear isomorphism
of
M({\mathbb{T}})/\overline{H_{0}^{1}}
onto
{\mathcal{K}}
, where

{\mathcal{K}}:=\{K\mu:\mu\in M({\mathbb{T}})\}.

We endow
{\mathcal{K}}
with the norm that makes this isomorphism an isometry, namely

\|K\mu\|_{\mathcal{K}}:=\|[\mu]\|_{M({\mathbb{T}})/\overline{H_{0}^{1}}}=\dist(\mu,\overline{H_{0}^{1}})\quad(\mu\in M({\mathbb{T}})).

Thus, finally, the dual of
A({\mathbb{D}})
may be identified with
{\mathcal{K}}
,
the duality pairing being given by

\langle f,K\mu\rangle:=\int_{\mathbb{T}}f(\zeta)\,d\mu(\overline{\zeta})=\lim_{r\to 1^{-}}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\widehat{f}(k)\widehat{\mu}(k)r^{k}\quad(f\in A({\mathbb{D}}),~K\mu\in{\mathcal{K}}).

We now apply the theory developed in §
3
.
Let
X:=A({\mathbb{D}})
and
X^{*}={\mathcal{K}}
.
For
k\geq 0
, we define
e_{k}\in A({\mathbb{D}})
by
e_{k}(z):=z^{k}
, and
\psi_{k}\in{\mathcal{K}}
by

\psi_{k}(z):=K\Bigl(e^{ikt}\,\frac{dt}{2\pi}\Bigr)(z)=z^{k}.

It is easily checked that
\|e_{k}\|_{\infty}=\|\psi_{k}\|_{\mathcal{K}}=1
and that
\langle e_{k},\psi_{k}\rangle=1
for all
k
.

Let
A=(a_{nk})_{n,k\geq 0}
be an infinite matrix of complex scalars
such that
\sum_{k\geq 0}|a_{nk}|<\infty
for each
n\geq 0
. Then, for each
n\geq 0
, we have

\displaystyle S_{n}^{A}(f)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\geq 0}a_{nk}\frac{\langle f,\psi_{k}\rangle}{\langle e_{k},\psi_{k}\rangle}e_{k}=\sum_{k\geq 0}a_{nk}\widehat{f}(k)z^{k}\quad(f\in A({\mathbb{D}})),

\displaystyle(S_{n}^{A})^{*}(K\mu)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\geq 0}a_{nk}\frac{\langle e_{k},K\mu\rangle}{\langle e_{k},\psi_{k}\rangle}\psi_{k}=\sum_{k\geq 0}a_{nk}\widehat{\mu}(k)z^{k}\quad(K\mu\in{\mathcal{K}}).

Just as in the case of
C({\mathbb{T}})
,
Fejér’s theorem implies that
\|\sigma_{n}f-f\|_{\infty}\to 0
as
n\to\infty
for all
f\in A({\mathbb{D}})
,
so there exists at least one matrix
A^{0}
for which
\|S_{n}^{A^{0}}(f)-f\|_{\infty}\to 0
. Thus
Theorem
3.1
applies. In the notation of Theorem
3.1
,

Z
is the norm-closure in
{\mathcal{K}}
of
\spn\{K(e^{ikt}dt/2\pi):k\geq 0\}
, which is
L^{1}({\mathbb{T}})/\overline{H^{1}_{0}}
.
Thus we obtain the following theorem.

Theorem 4.4
.

Let
(a_{nk})_{n,k\geq 0}
be an infinite matrix of complex scalars such that
\sum_{k\in{\mathbb{Z}}}|a_{nk}|<\infty

for each
n\geq 0
. Then the following statements are equivalent:

(1)

\sum_{k\geq 0}a_{nk}\widehat{f}(k)z^{k}\to f
in
(A({\mathbb{D}}),w)
, for all
f\in A({\mathbb{D}})
;

(2)

\sum_{k\geq 0}a_{nk}\widehat{f}(k)z^{k}\to f
in
(A({\mathbb{D}}),\|\cdot\|_{\infty})
for all
f\in A({\mathbb{D}})
;

(3)

\sum_{k\geq 0}a_{nk}\widehat{\mu}(k)z^{k}\to K\mu
in
({\mathcal{K}},w^{*})
, for all
\mu\in M({\mathbb{T}})
;

(4)

\sum_{k\geq 0}a_{nk}\widehat{g}(k)z^{k}\to Kg
in
({\mathcal{K}},w^{*})
, for all
g\in L^{1}({\mathbb{T}})
;

(5)

\sum_{k\geq 0}a_{nk}\widehat{g}(k)z^{k}\to g
in
(L^{1}/\overline{H^{1}_{0}},\|\cdot\|_{L^{1}/\overline{H^{1}_{0}}})
, for all
g\in L^{1}({\mathbb{T}})
.

5.
Application to Hardy spaces and Bergman spaces

5.1.
Hardy spaces,
\bmoa
and
\vmoa

We begin by reviewing the definitions and some basic facts about these spaces.
All the details can be found in
[
20
, Chapter 9]
.

For
1\leq p<\infty
, the Hardy space
H^{p}
is defined as the set of
f\in\hol({\mathbb{D}})
such that

\|f\|_{H^{p}}:=\sup_{r<1}\Bigl(\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}|f(re^{i\theta})|^{p}\,d\theta\Bigr)^{1/p}<\infty.

Also
H^{\infty}
is the set of bounded holomorphic functions on
{\mathbb{D}}
, with

\|f\|_{H^{\infty}}:=\sup_{\mathbb{D}}|f|.

The space
\bmoa
of holomorphic functions of bounded mean oscillation can be characterized as the
space of
f\in H^{2}
such that

\|f\|_{\bmoa}:=|f(0)|+\sup_{{\mathbb{D}}}(P|f|^{2}-|f|^{2})^{1/2}<\infty,

where
P|f|^{2}
denotes the Poisson integral of
|f|^{2}
.
The space
\vmoa
of holomorphic functions of vanishing mean oscillation
is the closed subspace of
\bmoa
consisting of those
f\in\bmoa

such that
(P|f|^{2}-|f|^{2})(z)\to 0
as
|z|\to 1
.

All the spaces
H^{p},\vmoa,\bmoa
are Banach spaces and contain the polynomials.
Polynomials are dense in
H^{p}~(1\leq p<\infty)
and in
\vmoa
, but not in
H^{\infty}
or
\bmoa
,
since neither of the latter is separable.

We have the following identifications of dual spaces (up to isomorphism):
(\vmoa)^{*}\cong H^{1}
and
(H^{1})^{*}\cong\bmoa
.
The pairings are given by

\displaystyle\langle f,g\rangle

\displaystyle:=\lim_{r\to 1^{-}}\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}f(re^{i\theta})g(re^{-i\theta})\,d\theta\quad(f\in\vmoa,~g\in H^{1}),

\displaystyle\langle g,h\rangle

\displaystyle:=\lim_{r\to 1^{-}}\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}g(re^{i\theta})h(re^{-i\theta})\,d\theta\quad(g\in H^{1},~h\in\bmoa).

We now proceed to apply the theory developed in §
3
.
Let
X:=H^{1}
, let
e_{k}:=z^{k}\in H^{1}
and let
\psi_{k}:=z^{k}\in\bmoa
.
Then
\|e_{k}\|=1
,
\|\psi_{k}\|\asymp 1
and
\langle e_{k},\psi_{k}\rangle=1
for all
k\geq 0
.

Let
(a_{nk})_{n,k\geq 0}
be a matrix of complex scalars such that
\sum_{k}|a_{nk}|<\infty
for each
n
.
Then

\displaystyle S_{n}^{A}(g)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\geq 0}a_{nk}\frac{\langle g,\psi_{k}\rangle}{\langle e_{k},\psi_{k}\rangle}e_{k}=\sum_{k\geq 0}a_{nk}\widehat{g}(k)z^{k}\quad(g\in H^{1}),

\displaystyle(S_{n}^{A})^{*}(h)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\geq 0}a_{nk}\frac{\langle e_{k},h\rangle}{\langle e_{k},\psi_{k}\rangle}\psi_{k}=\sum_{k\geq 0}a_{nk}\widehat{h}(k)z^{k}\quad(h\in\bmoa).

By an appropriate version of Fejér’s theorem,
\|\sigma_{n}(g)-g\|_{H^{1}}\to 0
for all
g\in H^{1}
.
Theorem
3.1
therefore applies.
In the notation of Theorem
3.1
,
Z
is the norm-closure in
\bmoa
of
\spn\{z^{k}:k\geq 0\}
,
which is exactly
\vmoa
.
We thus obtain the following result.

Theorem 5.1
.

Let
(a_{nk})_{n,k\geq 0}
be an infinite matrix of complex scalars such that
\sum_{k}|a_{nk}|<\infty
for each
n
.
Then the following statements are equivalent:

(1)

\sum_{k\geq 0}a_{nk}\widehat{g}(k)z^{k}\to g
in
(H^{1},w)
, for all
g\in H^{1}
;

(2)

\sum_{k\geq 0}a_{nk}\widehat{g}(k)z^{k}\to g
in
(H^{1},\|\cdot\|_{H^{1}})
, for all
g\in H^{1}
;

(3)

\sum_{k\geq 0}a_{nk}\widehat{h}(k)z^{k}\to h
in
(\bmoa,w^{*})
, for all
h\in\bmoa
;

(4)

\sum_{k\geq 0}a_{nk}\widehat{f}(k)z^{k}\to f
in
(\vmoa,w)
, for all
f\in\vmoa
;

(5)

\sum_{k\geq 0}a_{nk}\widehat{f}(k)z^{k}\to f
in
(\vmoa,\|\cdot\|_{\bmoa})
, for all
f\in\vmoa
.

Remark
.

Explicitly, statement (iii) means that, for all
h\in\bmoa
and all
g\in H^{1}
,

\lim_{n\to\infty}\lim_{r\to 1^{-}}\sum_{k\geq 0}a_{nk}r^{k}\widehat{g}(k)\widehat{h}(k)=\lim_{r\to 1^{-}}\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}g(re^{i\theta})h(re^{-i\theta})\,d\theta.

We now specialize to the case of Cesàro means.

Theorem 5.2
.

(1)

There exists
g\in H^{1}
such that
s_{n}(g)\not\to g
in
(H^{1},w)
.

(2)

There exists
f\in\vmoa
such that
s_{n}(f)\not\to f
in
(\vmoa,w)
.

Proof.

It is well known that there exists
g\in H^{1}
such that
\|s_{n}(g)-g\|_{H1}\not\to 0
.
This follows easily from the fact that the Riesz projection
P_{+}:L^{1}({\mathbb{T}})\to H^{1}
is unbounded
(see e.g.
[
6
, Ch. III, §1]
).
Parts (1) and (2) both follow by applying the equivalences in Theorem
5.1
.
∎

Remark
.

Using much the same idea,
Zhu has previously shown that there exists
f\in\vmoa
such that
\|s_{n}(f)-f\|_{\bmoa}\not\to 0
(see
[
19
, Corollary 5]
).

Theorem 5.3
.

Let
\alpha>0
.

(1)

\|\sigma_{n}^{\alpha}(g)-g\|_{H^{1}}\to 0
for all
g\in H^{1}
.

(2)

\sigma_{n}^{\alpha}(h)\to h
in
(\bmoa,w^{*})
for all
h\in\bmoa
.

(3)

\|\sigma_{n}^{\alpha}(f)-f\|_{\bmoa}\to 0
for all
f\in\vmoa
.

Proof.

Part (1) is a classical result of Hardy
[
7
]
.
Parts (2) and (3) follow, using Theorem
5.1
.
∎

5.2.
Bergman and Bloch spaces

Our development parallels that in §
5.1
.
Once again, we begin by reviewing the definitions and some basic facts about these spaces.
The details are in
[
20
, Chapters 4 and 5]
.

For
1\leq p<\infty
, the Bergman space
A^{p}
is defined as the space of
f\in\hol({\mathbb{D}})
such that

\|f\|_{A^{p}}:=\Bigl(\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{\mathbb{D}}|f(z)|^{p}\,dA(z)\Bigr)^{1/p}<\infty,

where
dA
denotes area measure on
{\mathbb{D}}
.

The Bloch space
{\mathcal{B}}
consists of those
f\in\hol({\mathbb{D}})
such that

\|f\|_{{\mathcal{B}}}:=|f(0)|+\sup_{{\mathbb{D}}}(1-|z|^{2})|f^{\prime}(z)|<\infty.

The little Bloch space
{\mathcal{B}}_{0}

is the closed subspace of
{\mathcal{B}}
consisting of those functions
f\in{\mathcal{B}}

such that
(1-|z|^{2})|f^{\prime}(z)|\to 0
as
|z|\to 1
.

All the spaces
A^{p},{\mathcal{B}},{\mathcal{B}}_{0}
are Banach spaces and contain the polynomials.
Polynomials are dense in
A^{p}~(1\leq p<\infty)
and in
{\mathcal{B}}_{0}
, but not in
{\mathcal{B}}
,
since the latter is not separable.

We have the following identifications of dual spaces (up to isomorphism):
({\mathcal{B}}_{0})^{*}\cong A^{1}
and
(A^{1})^{*}\cong{\mathcal{B}}
.
The pairings are given by

\displaystyle\langle f,g\rangle

\displaystyle:=\lim_{r\to 1^{-}}\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{|z|<r}f(z)g(\overline{z})\,dA(z)\quad(f\in{\mathcal{B}}_{0},~g\in A^{1}),

\displaystyle\langle g,h\rangle

\displaystyle:=\lim_{r\to 1^{-}}\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{|z|<r}g(z)h(\overline{z})\,dA(z)\quad(g\in A^{1},~h\in{\mathcal{B}}).

Once again, we apply the theory developed in §
3
.
Let
X:=A^{1}
, let
e_{k}:=z^{k}\in A^{1}
and let
\psi_{k}:=z^{k}\in{\mathcal{B}}
.
Then
\|e_{k}\|_{A^{1}}\asymp 1/(k+1)
and
\|\psi_{k}\|_{\mathcal{B}}\asymp 1
,
and
|\langle e_{k},\psi_{k}\rangle|\asymp 1/(k+1)
,
where the implied constants are independent of
k
.

Let
(a_{nk})_{n,k\geq 0}
be a matrix of complex scalars such that
\sum_{k}|a_{nk}|<\infty
for each
n
.
Then

\displaystyle S_{n}^{A}(g)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\geq 0}a_{nk}\frac{\langle g,\psi_{k}\rangle}{\langle e_{k},\psi_{k}\rangle}e_{k}=\sum_{k\geq 0}a_{nk}\widehat{g}(k)z^{k}\quad(g\in A^{1}),

\displaystyle(S_{n}^{A})^{*}(h)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\geq 0}a_{nk}\frac{\langle e_{k},h\rangle}{\langle e_{k},\psi_{k}\rangle}\psi_{k}=\sum_{k\geq 0}a_{nk}\widehat{h}(k)z^{k}\quad(h\in{\mathcal{B}}).

It is known that
\|\sigma_{n}(g)-g\|_{A^{1}}\to 0
for all
g\in A^{1}
(see Theorem
5.8
(i) below).
Theorem
3.1
therefore applies.
In the notation of Theorem
3.1
,

Z
is the norm-closure in
{\mathcal{B}}
of
\spn\{z^{k}:k\geq 0\}
,
which is exactly
{\mathcal{B}}_{0}
.
We thus obtain the following result.

Theorem 5.4
.

Let
(a_{nk})_{n,k\geq 0}
be an infinite matrix of complex scalars such that
\sum_{k}|a_{nk}|<\infty
for each
n
.
Then the following statements are equivalent:

(1)

\sum_{k\geq 0}a_{nk}\widehat{g}(k)z^{k}\to g
in
(A^{1},w)
, for all
g\in A^{1}
;

(2)

\sum_{k\geq 0}a_{nk}\widehat{g}(k)z^{k}\to g
in
(A^{1},\|\cdot\|_{A^{1}})
, for all
g\in A^{1}
;

(3)

\sum_{k\geq 0}a_{nk}\widehat{h}(k)z^{k}\to h
in
({\mathcal{B}},w^{*})
, for all
h\in{\mathcal{B}}
;

(4)

\sum_{k\geq 0}a_{nk}\widehat{f}(k)z^{k}\to f
in
({\mathcal{B}}_{0},w)
, for all
f\in{\mathcal{B}}_{0}
;

(5)

\sum_{k\geq 0}a_{nk}\widehat{f}(k)z^{k}\to f
in
({\mathcal{B}}_{0},\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal{B}})
, for all
f\in{\mathcal{B}}_{0}
.

Remark
.

Explicitly, statement (iii) means that, for all
h\in{\mathcal{B}}
and all
g\in A^{1}
,

\lim_{n\to\infty}\lim_{r\to 1^{-}}\sum_{k\geq 0}a_{nk}r^{k}\widehat{g}(k)\widehat{h}(k)=\lim_{r\to 1^{-}}\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{|z|<r}g(z)h(\overline{z})\,dA(z).

5.3.
Relationship between Hardy and Bergman spaces

The following result describes the relationship between summability in
H^{p}
and summability in
A^{p}
.

Theorem 5.5
.

Let
1\leq p<\infty
.
Let
A:=(a_{nk})_{n,k\geq 0}
be an infinite matrix of complex scalars such that
\sum_{k}|a_{nk}|<\infty
for each
n
.
If
\|S_{n}^{A}(f)-f\|_{H^{p}}\to 0
for all
f\in H^{p}
, then
\|S_{n}^{A}(g)-g\|_{A^{p}}\to 0
for all
g\in A^{p}
.

For the proof, we need a lemma.

Lemma 5.6
.

Let
1\leq p<\infty
.

(1)

We have
H^{p}\subset A^{p}
, and
\|f\|_{A^{p}}\leq\|f\|_{H^{p}}
for all
f\in H^{p}
.

(2)

Let
S:\hol({\mathbb{D}})\to\hol({\mathbb{D}})
be a linear map such that
(Sf)_{r}=S(f_{r})
for all
f\in\hol({\mathbb{D}})
and all
r\in(0,1)
.
If
\|Sf\|_{H^{p}}\leq C\|f\|_{H^{p}}
for all
f\in H^{p}
, then
\|Sg\|_{A^{p}}\leq C\|g\|_{A^{p}}
for all
g\in A^{p}
.

Proof.

(1) By Fubini’s theorem, we have the identity

\|f\|_{A^{p}}^{p}=\int_{0}^{1}\|f_{r}\|_{H^{p}}^{p}\,2r\,dr.

As
\|f_{r}\|_{H^{p}}\leq\|f\|_{H_{p}}~\forall r\in(0,1)
,
we obtain
\|f\|_{A^{p}}^{p}\leq\int_{0}^{1}\|f\|_{H^{p}}^{p}\,2r\,dr=\|f\|_{H^{p}}^{p}
.

(2) Again by the above identity, if
f\in A^{p}
, then

\displaystyle\|Sf\|_{A^{p}}^{p}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\|(Sf)_{r}\|_{H^{p}}^{p}\,2r\,dr=\int_{0}^{1}\|S(f_{r})\|_{H^{p}}^{p}\,2r\,dr

\displaystyle\leq C^{p}\int_{0}^{1}\|f_{r}\|_{H^{p}}^{p}\,2r\,dr=C^{p}\|f\|_{A^{p}}^{p}.\qed

Remark
.

In fact a result of Hardy and Littlewood shows that we even have
H^{p}\subset A^{2p}
. For a simple proof of this, and
an example showing that
2p
is sharp, see the article of Vukotić
[
18
]
. However, we do not need this here.

Proof of Theorem
5.5
.

Assume that
\|S_{n}^{A}(f)-f\|_{H^{p}}\to 0
for all
f\in H^{p}
.
By part (1) of the lemma,
\|S_{n}^{A}(f)-f\|_{A^{p}}\to 0
for all
f\in H^{p}
.
By part (2) of the lemma,
\|S_{n}^{A}:A^{p}\to A^{p}\|\leq\|S_{n}^{A}:H^{p}\to H^{p}\|
for all
n
,
and by the Banach–Steinhaus theorem,
\sup_{n}\|S_{n}^{A}:H^{p}\to H^{p}\|<\infty
.
The standard density argument now gives that
\|S_{n}^{A}(g)-g\|_{A^{p}}\to 0
for all
g\in A^{p}
.
∎

Once again, we finish the section by applying the above work to classical Cesàro means.

Theorem 5.7
.

(1)

If
1<p<\infty
, then
\|s_{n}(g)-g\|_{A^{p}}\to 0
.

(2)

There exists
g\in A^{1}
such that
s_{n}(g)\not\to g
in
(A^{1},w)
.

(3)

There exists
f\in{\mathcal{B}}_{0}
such that
s_{n}(f)\not\to f
in
({\mathcal{B}}_{0},w)
.

Proof.

Part (1) is a consequence of the (well-known) corresponding result in
H^{p}
and Theorem
5.5
.
By a result of Zhu
[
19
, Theorem 9]
, there exists
g\in A^{1}
such that
\|s_{n}(g)-g\|_{A^{1}}\not\to 0
.
Parts (2) and (3) both follow by applying the equivalences in Theorem
5.4
.
∎

Remark
.

Zhu has previously shown that there exists
f\in{\mathcal{B}}_{0}

such that
\|s_{n}(f)-f\|_{{\mathcal{B}}}\not\to 0
(see
[
19
, Corollary 11]
).
The same result had also been obtained earlier by Anderson, Clunie and Pommerenke
[
2
]
, but with a slightly different identification of the dual of
{\mathcal{B}}_{0}
and the predual of
{\mathcal{B}}
.

Theorem 5.8
.

Let
\alpha>0
.

(1)

\|\sigma_{n}^{\alpha}(g)-g\|_{A^{1}}\to 0
for all
g\in A^{1}
.

(2)

\sigma_{n}^{\alpha}(h)\to h
in
({\mathcal{B}},w^{*})
for all
h\in{\mathcal{B}}
.

(3)

\|\sigma_{n}^{\alpha}(f)-f\|_{{\mathcal{B}}}\to 0
for all
f\in{\mathcal{B}}_{0}
.

Proof.

Part (1) follows from the corresponding result for
H^{1}
, together with Theorem
5.5
.
Parts (2) and (3) are consequences of (1), using the equivalences in Theorem
5.4
.
∎

6.
Applications in Hilbert spaces

6.1.
Abstract set-up

In the case of a Hilbert space,
we can repeat the analysis of §
3
using the inner product
in place of the duality pairing. The fact that an inner product is sesquilinear rather than bilinear
leads to some slight differences between the two theories.

Throughout this section,
H
denotes a complex Hilbert space
with inner product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
.
Also, if
T
is a bounded linear operator on
H
, then

T^{*}
denotes the Hilbert-space adjoint of
T
,
namely the unique operator on
H
such that

\langle Tg,h\rangle=\langle g,T^{*}h\rangle\quad(g,h\in H).

The following theorem is the analogue of Theorem
2.1
.
It is proved in just the same way, so we omit the details.
Of course, since a Hilbert space is reflexive,
there is no distinction between weak- and weak*-convergence.

Theorem 6.1
.

Let
(T_{n})_{n\geq 1}
be a sequence of bounded, finite-rank operators on
H

such that

T_{n}T_{m}(H)\subset T_{m}(H)\quad\text{and}\quad T_{n}^{*}T_{m}^{*}(H)\subset T_{m}^{*}(H)\qquad(m,n\geq 0).

Then the following statements are equivalent:

(1)

T_{n}h\to h
weakly for all
h\in H
;

(2)

T_{n}h\to h
in norm for all
h\in H
;

(3)

T_{n}^{*}h\to h
weakly for all
h\in H
;

(4)

T_{n}^{*}h\to h
in norm for all
h\in H
.

Now suppose that
(e_{k})_{k\geq 0}
and
(f_{k})_{k\geq 0}
are two sequences in
H
such that

\left\{\begin{aligned} \langle e_{j},f_{k}\rangle&=0,\quad\forall j,k,~j\neq k,\\
\langle e_{k},f_{k}\rangle&\neq 0,\quad\forall k.\end{aligned}\right.

For each
k\geq 0
, define
P_{k}:H\to H
by

P_{k}:=\frac{e_{k}\otimes f_{k}}{\langle e_{k},f_{k}\rangle}.

Explicitly,

P_{k}(h):=\frac{\langle h,f_{k}\rangle}{\langle e_{k},f_{k}\rangle}e_{k}\quad(h\in H).

Clearly
P_{k}(e_{k})=e_{k}
and
P_{k}(e_{j})=0
if
j\neq k
.
It is easy to see that
P_{k}
is a rank-one projection with

\|P_{k}\|=\frac{\|e_{k}\|\|f_{k}\|}{|\langle e_{k},f_{k}\rangle|}.

Its adjoint
P_{k}^{*}:H\to H
is given by

P_{k}^{*}=\frac{f_{k}\otimes e_{k}}{\langle f_{k},e_{k}\rangle}.

Let
A=(a_{nk})_{n,k\geq 0}
be an infinite matrix of complex scalars such that,
for each
n\geq 0
,

\sum_{k\geq 0}|a_{nk}|\|P_{k}\|<\infty.

We define the associated summation operators
S_{n}^{A}:H\to H~(n\geq 0)

by the absolutely convergent series

S_{n}^{A}:=\sum_{k\geq 0}a_{nk}P_{k}.

Explicitly, we have

\displaystyle S_{n}^{A}(h)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\geq 0}a_{nk}\frac{\langle h,f_{k}\rangle}{\langle e_{k},f_{k}\rangle}e_{k}\quad(h\in H),

\displaystyle(S_{n}^{A})^{*}(h)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\geq 0}\overline{a}_{nk}\frac{\langle h,e_{k}\rangle}{\langle f_{k},e_{k}\rangle}f_{k}\quad(h\in H).

With this notation established, we have the following equivalence theorem.
It follows from Theorem
6.1
in just the same way that
Theorem
3.1
follows from Theorem
2.1
.
We omit the details.

Theorem 6.2
.

The following statements are equivalent:

(1)

S_{n}^{A}(h)\to h
weakly, for all
h\in H
.

(2)

S_{n}^{A}(h)\to h
in norm, for all
h\in H
.

(3)

(S_{n}^{A})^{*}(h)\to h
weakly, for all
h\in H
.

(4)

(S_{n}^{A})^{*}(h)\to h
in norm, for all
h\in H
.

There are also Hilbert-space versions of the limitation theorems,
Theorem
3.2
and Theorem
3.4
.
The proofs are the same as before.

Theorem 6.3
.

Suppose that
S_{n}^{A}(x)\to x
(weakly or in norm) for all
x\in H
, and that
A
admits a left inverse
B
.
Then, writing
B_{j}:=\sum_{n\geq 0}|b_{jn}|
, we have

\frac{\|e_{j}\|\|f_{j}\|}{|\langle e_{j},f_{j}\rangle|}=O(B_{j})\quad(j\to\infty).

Theorem 6.4
.

Let
\alpha\geq 0
. If
\sigma_{n}^{\alpha}(x)\to x
(weakly or in norm) for all
x\in H
, then

\frac{\|e_{j}\|\|f_{j}\|}{|\langle e_{j},f_{j}\rangle|}=O(j^{\alpha})\quad(j\to\infty).

6.2.
Reproducing kernel spaces of holomorphic functions

We shall now apply these ideas to reproducing kernel spaces of holomorphic functions
on the unit disk.

Let
H
be a Hilbert space of holomorphic functions on
{\mathbb{D}}

such that:

•

convergence in the norm of
H
implies pointwise convergence on
{\mathbb{D}}
;

•

H
contains the polynomials.

The first assumption implies that, for each
w\in{\mathbb{D}}
, the functional

h\mapsto h(w)
is continuous on
H
, so, by the Riesz representation theorem,
there exists a unique
k_{w}\in H
such that

h(w)=\langle h,k_{w}\rangle\quad(h\in H).

We define
K:H\times H\to{\mathbb{C}}
by

K(z,w):=k_{w}(z)=\langle k_{w},k_{z}\rangle\quad(z,w\in{\mathbb{D}}).

The function
K
is the reproducing kernel of
H
. Clearly it satisfies

K(w,z)=\overline{K(z,w)}
, and
K(z,w)
is holomorphic in
z
for each fixed
w
.
Therefore it is anti-holomorphic in
w
for each fixed
z
.
We shall need the following simple lemma about derivatives of
K
.

Lemma 6.5
.

For each
w\in{\mathbb{D}}
and each
n\geq 0
, let

k_{w,n}(z):=\frac{1}{n!}\frac{\partial^{n}}{\partial\overline{w}^{n}}K(z,w)\quad(z\in{\mathbb{D}}).

Then
k_{w,n}\in H
and

(6.1)

\langle h,k_{w,n}\rangle=\frac{h^{(n)}(w)}{n!}\quad(h\in H).

Proof.

As norm-convergence in
H
implies local uniform convergence
on
{\mathbb{D}}
, the map
h\mapsto h^{(n)}(w)/n!
is a continuous linear functional on
H
,
so, by the Riesz theorem again, there exists

k_{w,n}\in H
such that (
6.1
) holds.
It remains to identify
k_{w,n}
, which we do as follows.
For each
z\in{\mathbb{D}}
, we have

\displaystyle k_{w,n}(z)

\displaystyle=\langle k_{w,n},k_{z}\rangle=\overline{\langle k_{z},k_{w,n}\rangle}

\displaystyle=\overline{\frac{k_{z}^{(n)}(w)}{n!}}=\overline{\frac{1}{n!}\frac{\partial^{n}}{\partial w^{n}}K(w,z)}=\frac{1}{n!}\frac{\partial^{n}}{\partial\overline{w}^{n}}K(z,w).\qed

Now we define sequences
(e_{j})_{j\geq 0}

and
(f_{j})_{j\geq 0}
in
H
by

e_{j}:=z^{j}
and
f_{j}:=k_{0,j}
.
Note that, if
h\in H
, then

\langle h,f_{j}\rangle=\frac{h^{(j)}(0)}{j!}=\widehat{h}(j).

In particular,

\langle e_{i},f_{j}\rangle=\delta_{ij}
,
so the theory outlined in §
6.1
applies. Note also that
\|e_{j}\|=\|z^{j}\|_{H}
and

\|f_{j}\|^{2}=\|k_{0,j}\|^{2}=\langle k_{0,j},k_{0,j}\rangle=\frac{1}{j!}k_{0,j}^{(j)}(0)=\frac{1}{j!^{2}}\frac{\partial^{2j}K}{\partial z^{j}\partial\overline{w}^{j}}(0,0).

Theorem
6.2
leads to the following result.

Theorem 6.6
.

Let
(a_{nj})_{n,j\geq 0}
be an infinite matrix of complex scalars such that

\sum_{j\geq 0}|a_{nj}|\|z^{j}\|\|k_{0,j}\|<\infty
for each
n\geq 0
.
Then the following statements are equivalent:

(1)

\sum_{j\geq 0}a_{nj}\widehat{h}(j)z^{j}\to h
weakly as
n\to\infty
, for all
h\in H
;

(2)

\sum_{j\geq 0}a_{nj}\widehat{h}(j)z^{j}\to h
in norm as
n\to\infty
, for all
h\in H
;

(3)

\sum_{j\geq 0}\overline{a}_{nj}\langle h,z^{j}\rangle k_{0,j}\to h
weakly as
n\to\infty
, for all
h\in H
;

(4)

\sum_{j\geq 0}\overline{a}_{nj}\langle h,z^{j}\rangle k_{0,j}\to h
in norm as
n\to\infty
, for all
h\in H
.

The following result is a limitation theorem for reproducing kernel spaces.
It is an immediate consequence of Theorem
6.4
.

Theorem 6.7
.

Let
H
be a reproducing-kernel space of holomorphic functions on
{\mathbb{D}}
that contains the polynomials.
If
\alpha\geq 0
and
\sigma_{n}^{\alpha}(h)\to h
(weakly or in norm) for all
h\in H
, then

\|z^{j}\|\|k_{0,j}\|=O(j^{\alpha})\quad(j\to\infty).

6.3.
De Branges–Rovnyak spaces

We now specialize to the case where
H={\mathcal{H}}(b)
, the de Branges–Rovnyak space with symbol
b
.
Here
b
is an element of the unit ball of
H^{\infty}
. By definition,
{\mathcal{H}}(b)
is the reproducing-kernel space on
{\mathbb{D}}

with kernel

K(z,w)=\frac{1-b(z)\overline{b(w)}}{1-z\overline{w}}\quad(z,w\in{\mathbb{D}}).

The space
{\mathcal{H}}(b)
contains the polynomials iff
b
is a non-extreme point of the unit ball
of
H^{\infty}
, and in this case the polynomials are dense in
{\mathcal{H}}(b)
. It is known that
b
is non-extreme iff its boundary values satisfy
\log(1-|b|^{2})\in L^{1}({\mathbb{T}})
. Henceforth, we assume that this is the case.
For further information on
{\mathcal{H}}(b)
-spaces, we refer to
[
16
]
and
[
4
,
5
]
.

The following result will be useful in what follows.

Proposition 6.8
.

Let
b
be a non-extreme point of the unit ball of
H^{\infty}
.
Then
\inf_{j\geq 0}\|k_{0,j}\|_{{\mathcal{H}}(b)}>0
.

Proof.

We compute an expression for
k_{0,j}
in
{\mathcal{H}}(b)
. Using Leibniz’ theorem, we have

\displaystyle k_{0,j}(z)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{j!}\frac{\partial^{j}}{\partial\overline{w}^{j}}K(z,0)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{j!}\sum_{i=0}^{j}\binom{j}{i}\frac{\partial^{i}}{\partial\overline{w}^{i}}\Bigl(1-b(z)\overline{b(w)}\Bigr)\Bigl|_{w=0}\frac{\partial^{j-i}}{\partial\overline{w}^{j-i}}\Bigl(1-z\overline{w}\Bigr)^{-1}\Bigl|_{w=0}

\displaystyle=z^{j}-b(z)\sum_{i=0}^{j}\overline{\widehat{b}(i)}z^{j-i}.

Thus, viewed as functions on
{\mathbb{T}}
,

k_{0,j}=z^{j}(1-b\overline{s_{j}(b)}).

Also, expanding
K(z,w)=(1-b(z)\overline{b(w)})/(1-z\overline{w})
as a double power series in
z,\overline{w}
,
and computing the coefficient of
z^{j}\overline{w}^{j}
, we find that

\|k_{0,j}\|_{{\mathcal{H}}(b)}^{2}=\frac{1}{j!^{2}}\frac{\partial^{2j}K}{\partial z^{j}\partial\overline{w}^{j}}(0,0)=1-\sum_{i=0}^{j}|\widehat{b}(i)|^{2}=1-\|s_{j}(b)\|_{H^{2}}^{2}.

In particular, we have

1\geq\|k_{0,j}\|_{{\mathcal{H}}(b)}^{2}\geq 1-\|b\|_{H^{2}}^{2}\quad(j\geq 0).

As
b
is non-extreme,
\|b\|_{H^{2}}<\|b\|_{H^{\infty}}\leq 1
or
\|b\|_{H^{2}}=\|b\|_{H^{\infty}}<1
.
Either way,
1-\|b\|_{H^{2}}^{2}>0
. Hence
\inf_{j\geq 0}\|k_{0,j}\|_{{\mathcal{H}}(b)}>0
, as claimed.
∎

Remark
.

A similar result holds in the more general finite-rank
{\mathcal{H}}[B]
-spaces studied
by Aleman and Malman in
[
1
]
.
However, not all reproducing kernel function spaces have this property.
For example, in the classical Dirichlet space on the unit disk,
we have
\|k_{0,j}\|\asymp 1/j
.

Feeding this information into the results of §
6.2
, we deduce the following results.
(We write
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{b}
for the inner product in
{\mathcal{H}}(b)
.)

Theorem 6.9
.

Let
b
be a non-extreme point of the unit ball of
H^{\infty}
.
Let
(a_{nj})_{n,j\geq 0}
be a matrix of complex scalars such that

\sum_{j\geq 0}|a_{nj}|\|z^{j}\|_{{\mathcal{H}}(b)}<\infty

for each
n\geq 0
.
Then the following statements are equivalent:

(1)

\sum_{j\geq 0}a_{nj}\widehat{h}(j)z^{j}\to h
weakly in
{\mathcal{H}}(b)
, for all
h\in{\mathcal{H}}(b)
;

(2)

\sum_{j\geq 0}a_{nj}\widehat{h}(j)z^{j}\to h
in norm in
{\mathcal{H}}(b)
, for all
h\in{\mathcal{H}}(b)
;

(3)

\sum_{j\geq 0}\overline{a}_{nj}(1-b\overline{s_{j}(b)})\langle h,z^{j}\rangle_{b}z^{j}\to h
weakly in
{\mathcal{H}}(b)
, for all
h\in{\mathcal{H}}(b)
;

(4)

\sum_{j\geq 0}\overline{a}_{nj}(1-b\overline{s_{j}(b)})\langle h,z^{j}\rangle_{b}z^{j}\to h
in norm in
{\mathcal{H}}(b)
, for all
h\in{\mathcal{H}}(b)
.

Theorem 6.10
.

Let
b
be a non-extreme point of the unit ball of
H^{\infty}
.
If
\alpha\geq 0
and
\sigma_{n}^{\alpha}(h)\to h
(weakly or in norm)
for all
h\in{\mathcal{H}}(b)
, then

\|z^{j}\|_{{\mathcal{H}}(b)}=O(j^{\alpha})\quad(j\to\infty).

The following consequence is worth pointing out explicitly.

Corollary 6.11
.

Let
b
be a non-extreme point of the unit ball of
H^{\infty}
.
If
s_{n}(h)\to h
(weakly or in norm) for all
h\in{\mathcal{H}}(b)
, then
\sup_{j\geq 0}\|z^{j}\|_{{\mathcal{H}}(b)}<\infty
.

The de Branges–Rovnyak spaces in which the powers of
z
are bounded in norm form
an interesting class in their own right. For example, they are precisely the
{\mathcal{H}}(b)
-spaces that
contain
H^{\infty}
. Also, they are characterized by the condition that
1/(1-|b|^{2})\in L^{1}({\mathbb{T}})
.
For more on this, see
[
15
, §4]
.

For
\alpha>0
, the convergence of
\sigma_{n}^{\alpha}(h)
to
h
for every
h\in{\mathcal{H}}(b)
also
has implications for
b
. To derive these, we need an explicit formula for
\|z^{j}\|_{{\mathcal{H}}(b)}
,
which requires that we delve a little further into the theory
of de Branges–Rovnyak spaces. The details can be found in
[
16
]
and
[
5
]
.

As mentioned earlier, if
b
is a non-extreme point of the unit ball of
H^{\infty}
,
then
\log(1-|b|^{2})\in L^{1}({\mathbb{T}})
. This implies that
there exists a unique outer function
a
on
{\mathbb{D}}
with
a(0)>0

such that
|b|^{2}+|a|^{2}=1
a.e. on
{\mathbb{T}}
.
This function
a
is sometimes called the
pythagorean complement of
b
.
Writing
\phi:=b/a
, we obtain a function in the Smirnov class
N^{+}
,
namely the space of quotients of
H^{\infty}
-functions with outer denominators.
Conversely, all Smirnov functions are obtained in this way. There is thus
a one-to-one correspondence
b\leftrightarrow\phi
,
between non-extreme points
b
of the unit ball of
H^{\infty}
and functions
\phi\in N^{+}
.
Expanding
\phi(z)
as a Taylor series, say
\phi(z)=\sum_{j\geq 0}c_{j}z^{j}
, we have

(6.2)

\|z^{j}\|_{{\mathcal{H}}(b)}^{2}=1+\sum_{i=0}^{j}|c_{i}|^{2}.

A simple proof of (
6.2
) can be found for example in
[
15
, p.81]
.

Theorem 6.12
.

Let
b
be a non-extreme point of the unit ball of
H^{\infty}
and let
\phi

be the corresponding function in the Smirnov class.
If
\alpha\geq 0
and
\sigma_{n}^{\alpha}(h)\to h
(weakly or in norm)
for all
h\in{\mathcal{H}}(b)
, then

(6.3)

\phi(z)=O\Bigl((1-|z|)^{-\alpha-1/2}\Bigr)\quad(|z|\to 1^{-}).

Proof.

As above, let us write
\phi(z)=\sum_{j\geq 0}c_{j}z^{j}
.
Summing by parts, we have

|\phi(z)|\leq\sum_{j\geq 0}|c_{j}||z|^{j}=(1-|z|)\sum_{j\geq 0}\Bigl(\sum_{i=0}^{j}|c_{i}|\Bigr)|z|^{j}\quad(|z|<1).

By Cauchy–Schwarz and formula (
6.2
),

\sum_{i=0}^{j}|c_{i}|\leq(j+1)^{1/2}\Bigl(\sum_{i=0}^{j}|c_{i}|^{2}\Bigr)^{1/2}\leq(j+1)^{1/2}\|z^{j}\|_{{\mathcal{H}}(b)}\quad(j\geq 0).

From Theorem
6.10
, it follows that

\sum_{i=0}^{j}|c_{i}|=O(j^{\alpha+1/2})\quad(j\to\infty).

Feeding this information back into the inequality for
\phi
, we find that there is a constant
C
such that

|\phi(z)|\leq C(1-|z|)\sum_{j\geq 0}j^{\alpha+1/2}|z|^{j}\quad(|z|<1).

The conclusion (
6.3
) follows.
∎

We conclude with two concrete examples.

Corollary 6.13
.

Let
\phi(z):=z/(1-z)

and let
b
be the corresponding non-extreme point of the unit ball of
H^{\infty}
.
If
\sigma^{\alpha}_{n}(h)\to h
for all
h\in{\mathcal{H}}(b)
, then
\alpha\geq 1/2
.

Remark
.

In this case
b
can be determined explicitly, namely as

b(z)=\frac{(1-w_{0})z}{1-w_{0}z},

where
w_{0}:=(3-\sqrt{5})/2
.
This example is taken from
[
17
]
, where it is shown that, with this particular choice of
b
,
the de Branges–Rovynak space
{\mathcal{H}}(b)
is equal to the local Dirichlet space
{\mathcal{D}}(\delta_{1})

(with the same norm).
It was shown in
[
9
]
that, if
\alpha>1/2
,
then
\sigma_{n}^{\alpha}(h)\to h
in norm for all
h\in{\mathcal{D}}(\delta_{1})
,
but that there exists
h_{0}\in{\mathcal{D}}(\delta_{1})
such that
\sigma_{n}^{1/2}(h_{0})\not\to h_{0}
.
Thus the constant
1/2
in Corollary
6.13
is sharp.

Corollary 6.14
.

Let

\phi(z):=\exp\Bigl(\sqrt{\frac{1+z}{1-z}}\Bigr)\quad(z\in{\mathbb{D}}).

Then
\phi\in N^{+}
. If
b
is the corresponding non-extreme point of the unit ball of
H^{\infty}
,
then there exists no
\alpha\geq 0
such that
\sigma_{n}^{\alpha}(h)\to h
for all
h\in{\mathcal{H}}(b)
.

Proof.

The function
\sqrt{(1+z)/(1-z)}
belongs to
H^{1}
.
Its exponential is therefore an outer function,
so
\phi\in N^{+}
.
Now apply Theorem
6.12
.
∎

Remark
.

Thus, in this case, even though polynomials are dense in
{\mathcal{H}}(b)
,
there is no Cesàro summability method that always converges.
Such examples were previously obtained in
[
3
]
and
[
12
]

using inductive constructions. Our method has the virtue that it yields a
(fairly simple) explicit function
\phi
that does the trick.
