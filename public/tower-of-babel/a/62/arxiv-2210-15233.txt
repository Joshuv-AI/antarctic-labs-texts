Towards bosonization of Virasoro coadjoint orbits
Anton Alekseev 1, Olga Chekeres 2, Donald R. Youmans 3,4

Abstract
. We show that Schwarzian theories associated to certain hyperbolic and parabolic Virasoro coadjoint orbits admit bosonization, i.e. a global S 1 S^{1} -equivariant Darboux chart in which the corresponding path integral becomes Gaussian. In this chart, correlation functions of bilocals, time-ordered and out-of-time ordered, can be computed explicitly. We conjecture that a similar global chart exists for the Teichmüller orbit.



1.
Introduction

Coadjoint orbits of the Virasoro group have experienced a surge of interest in recent years due to their independent appearance in two-dimensional gravity models
[
14
,
15
]
and in condensed matter SYK-models
[
13
]
.

Associated to any Virasoro coadjoint orbit, there is a physical system whose configuration space of fields is given by the orbit itself.
The action functional of the model is given by the moment map of rigid
S^{1}
-rotations on the orbit:

(1)

\mu_{b_{0}}(f)=\int_{S^{1}}\left(b_{0}(f(x))f^{\prime 2}(x)+\frac{c}{12}S(f)\right)dx,

where
f(x+2\pi)=f(x)+2\pi
is a diffeomorphism of the circle, and
S(f)
is the Schwarzian derivative:

S(f)=\frac{f^{\prime\prime\prime}}{f^{\prime}}-\frac{3}{2}\left(\frac{f^{\prime\prime}}{f^{\prime}}\right)^{2}.

In the action (
1
), the periodic function
b_{0}(x)
characterizes the orbit. In this paper, we will consider Virasoro orbits with
b_{0}={\rm const}
. Even in this case, the action (
1
) defines a family of rather complicated nonlinear theories.

Nevertheless, it was realized in
[
19
]
that these theories are one-loop exact.
In this article we go one step further and show that for a wide class of Virasoro coadjoint orbits the action (
1
) actually defines a free theory.
This is an instance of bosonization which unravels the hidden structure of a non-interacting theory.
Classical examples of bosonization were provided by Gawedzki-Kupiainen in the case of WZW models
[
7
]
(see also
[
1
,
8
]
).

Our work is inspired by some classical results in equivariant symplectic geometry. In more detail, consider a symplectic manifold
M
with a Hamiltonian
S^{1}
-action and an isolated fixed point
p\in M
. Then, the equivariant Darboux theorem states that there is a neighborhood of
p
and a coordinate system such that the symplectic form is constant, the
S^{1}
-action linear, and the moment map of the
S^{1}
-action quadratic. Furthermore, if
p
is an extremum of the moment map, the size of the Darboux chart is controlled by the next fixed point of the
S^{1}
-action
[
11
]
. If there are no other fixed points, this equivariant Darboux chart is global.

On a Virasoro coadjoint orbit with
b_{0}={\rm const}
there is a unique fixed point of rigid rotations
r(x)=x+\theta
which is given by
f(x)=x

[
6
]
.
For a large class of orbits which include certain hyperbolic and elliptic orbits as well as the Teichmüller orbit, this fixed point is a maximum of the moment map.

In this paper, we show that hyperbolic Virasoro orbits with constant representative
b_{0}<0
and the parabolic orbit with
b_{0}=0
do admit a global Darboux chart. Hence, the corresponding Schwarzian theories admit bosonization. Furthermore, observables of the model (
1
) are bilocals
\mathcal{O}(x,y)
. In Darboux coordinates, they take an exponential form, and their correlation functions are given by Gaussian integrals. In particular, correlation functions of two bilocals
\mathcal{O}(x_{1},x_{2})\mathcal{O}(x_{3},x_{4})
can be computed for time-ordered
x_{1}<x_{2}<x_{3}<x_{4}
or out-of-time-ordered
x_{1}<x_{3}<x_{2}<x_{4}
configurations.
Comparison of these correlation functions is the standard probe for quantum chaos, see
e.g.

[
16
]
.
Such a comparison, however, is beyond the scope of this article.
Note that in the finite dimensional case correlation functions can be explicitly computed on coadjoint orbits of the unitary group
U(n)
using Gelfand-Zeitlin Darboux coordinates (see
[
18
]
).

We put forward the following conjecture:

Conjecture:
Elliptic Virasoro orbits with
0<b_{0}<\frac{c}{24}
and the exceptional Teichmüller orbit (
b_{0}=\frac{c}{24}
) admit global equivariant Darboux charts.

In the case of the Teichmüller orbit, we give its presentation as a codimension 2 submanifold in an (infinite dimensional) Darboux chart. We believe that this presentation may give a good starting point for proving the conjecture.

The article is structured as follows: In Section
2
, we recall some basic facts about the geometry of Virasoro coadjoint orbits.
In Section
3
, we prove the main theorem of this article:

Theorem:
Hyperbolic Virasoro coadjoint orbits
\mathcal{O}_{b_{0}}
with
b_{0}<0
and the parabolic coadjoint orbit
\mathcal{O}_{0}
admit global equivariant Darboux charts.

The proof is given by an explicit construction.
In Section
4
, we discuss the geometry of the Teichmüller orbit and put forward a conjecture on the existence of the global equivariant Darboux chart.
Section
5
is devoted to the computation of the partition function, and of correlation functions for one and two bilocals (time-orderd and out-of-time ordered) of the Schwarzian theory associated to a hyperbolic coadjoint orbit.

2.
Geometry of Virasoro coadjoint orbits

In this Section, we recall the definition and basic properties of coadjoint orbits of the central extension of the group of orientation preserving diffeomorphisms of the circle. Usually, these orbits are referred to as Virasoro coadjoint orbits.

Let
\Diff^{+}(S^{1})
be the group of orientation preserving diffeomorphisms of the circle and
\widetilde{\Diff}^{+}(S^{1})
its universal cover.
Elements of
\widetilde{\Diff}^{+}(S^{1})
are functions
f\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
satisfying
f^{\prime}(x)>0
and the quasi-periodicity condition

f(x+2\pi)=f(x)+2\pi.

The Lie algebra
\vect(S^{1})
of
\Diff^{+}(S^{1})
is given by vector fields on the circle.
It admits a universal central extension, the Virasoro algebra
\mathfrak{vir}
.
As a vector space
\mathfrak{vir}=\vect(S^{1})\oplus\mathbb{R}
.
To simplify notation, we denote the generator of the central
\mathbb{R}
by
1
.
Elements of
\mathfrak{vir}
are pairs
(L(x)\partial_{x},k)
where
L(x)
is a periodic function and
k\in\mathbb{R}
.
The Lie bracket is defined by formula

(2)

[L_{1}+k_{1},L_{2}+k_{2}]=[L_{1},L_{2}]+\frac{1}{12}\int_{S^{1}}dx\ L_{1}^{\prime\prime\prime}(x)L_{2}(x).

In particular, if one introduces a basis
\ell_{m}=\frac{1}{2\pi}e^{imx}\partial_{x}
of (the complexification of)
\vect(S^{1})
, then one recovers the standard representation of the Virasoro algebra

(3)

2\pi i[\ell_{m},\ell_{n}]=(m-n)\ell_{m+n}+\frac{1}{12}m^{3}\delta_{m+n,0},

Remark 1
.

In the literature it is common to choose the generator of the central
\mathbb{R}
to be
c
, and to normalize the central term in (
3
) by
ic/24\pi
, see
e.g.

[
21
]
. If one then considers the basis
L_{n}=ie^{inx}\partial_{x}
, with
L_{0}
shifted by
c/24
,
Equation (
3
) takes on the more familiar form

[L_{m},L_{n}]=(m-n)L_{m+n}+\frac{c}{12}(m^{3}-m)\delta_{m+n,0}.

In this case,
c
is known as the central charge.

Elements of the (smooth) dual
\mathfrak{vir}^{*}
can be identified with pairs
(b,-c)
where
b=b(x)dx^{2}
is a quadratic differential on the circle and
c\in\mathbb{R}
. The pairing between
\mathfrak{vir}
and
\mathfrak{vir}^{*}
is defined by

(4)

\left\langle b-c,L+k\right\rangle=\int_{S^{1}}b(x)L(x)dx-ck.

From (
2
) one finds the infinitesimal coadjoint action of
\vect(S^{1})\subset\mathfrak{vir}

(5)

\ad^{*}_{L}(b-c)=\left(2L^{\prime}(x)b(x)+L(x)b^{\prime}(x)+\frac{c}{12}L^{\prime\prime\prime}(x)\right)dx^{2},

which integrates to

(6)

Ad^{*}_{f^{-1}}(b-c)=b^{f}-c=\left(f^{\prime 2}b(f(x))+\frac{c}{12}S(f)\right)dx^{2}-c,

where
S(f)
is the Schwarzian derivative of
f

S(f)=\frac{f^{\prime\prime\prime}}{f^{\prime}}-\frac{3}{2}\left(\frac{f^{\prime\prime}}{f^{\prime}}\right)^{2}.

Coadjoint orbits are hence of the form

\mathcal{O}_{b}=\{b^{f}(x)dx^{2}\mid f\in\widetilde{\Diff}^{+}(S^{1})\}\cong\widetilde{\Diff}^{+}(S^{1})/\widetilde{\Stab}(b)\cong\Diff^{+}(S^{1})/\Stab(b).

Here
\widetilde{\Stab}(b)\subset\widetilde{\Diff}^{+}(S^{1})
is the stabilizer subgroup of
b
under the action of
\widetilde{\Diff}^{+}(S^{1})
and
\Stab(b)
is the stabilizer subgroup of
b
under the action of
\Diff^{+}(S^{1})
. Since the action (
6
) of
\widetilde{\Diff}^{+}(S^{1})
on quadratic differentials factors through the action of
\Diff^{+}(S^{1})
, the quotient spaces are equal to each other.

In what follows we will restrict our consideration to the case of
b
being a constant quadratic differential,
i.e.

b(x)=b_{0}
.
It is known that for generic values of
b_{0}
, the stabilizer subgroup
\Stab(b)
is isomorphic to
S^{1}
while for the special values

(7)

b_{0}=\frac{ck^{2}}{24},

it is isomorphic to a
k
-fold cover of
\PSL(2,\mathbb{R})
.
Orbits with
b_{0}<0
are called hyperbolic, with
b_{0}>0
and
b_{0}\neq ck^{2}/24
elliptic, with
b_{0}=ck^{2}/24
exceptional, and the orbit with
b_{0}=0
is parabolic
(see
[
6
,
9
,
12
,
17
,
21
]
for details of the classification).

Recall that coadjoint orbits of Lie groups carry canonical Kirillov-Kostant-Souriau (KKS) symplectic structures
[
10
]
. In more detail, let
G
be a Lie group with Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
, and let
\mathcal{O}_{\xi}\subset\mathfrak{g}^{*}
be a coadjoint orbit through a point
\xi\in\mathfrak{g}^{*}
.
The canonical
G
-invariant symplectic form on
\mathcal{O}_{\xi}
at the point
\xi
is given by

(8)

\varpi_{\xi}(x,y)=\langle\xi,[x,y]\rangle,\qquad x,y\in\mathfrak{g}.

Here
x,y
in
\varpi_{\xi}(x,y)
stand for fundamental vector fields on
\mathcal{O}_{\xi}
.

Since
\mathcal{O}_{\xi}
is diffeomorphic to
G/\Stab(\xi)
, the 2-form
\varpi_{\xi}
can be pulled back to
G
under the projection
\pi\colon G\to G/\Stab(\xi)\cong\mathcal{O}_{\xi}
:

(9)

\omega=\pi^{*}\varpi\in\Omega^{2}(G).

At the identity,
e\in G
, one has

\omega_{e}(x,y)=\langle\xi,[x,y]\rangle,\qquad x,y\in T_{e}G=\mathfrak{g}.

To find the expression of
\omega
at any point
g
, one uses left translation on the group: for
x_{g},y_{g}\in T_{g}G
two left-invariant vector fields one has

\omega_{g}(x_{g},y_{g})=(L_{g^{-1}})^{*}\omega_{e}(x_{g},y_{g})=\langle\xi,[(L_{g^{-1}})_{*}x_{g},(L_{g^{-1}})_{*}y_{g}]\rangle.

This allows us to write
\omega_{g}
very concisely in terms of the Maurer-Cartan form
Y\in\Omega^{1}(G,\mathfrak{g})
:

(10)

\omega_{g}=\frac{1}{2}\langle\xi,[Y(g),Y(g)]\rangle.

Remark 2
.

Recall that the Maurer-Cartan form
Y
satisfies the following identities:
\langle Y(g),x_{g}\rangle=x
and

(11a)

\displaystyle\delta Y(g)+\frac{1}{2}[Y(g),Y(g)]=0,

(11b)

\displaystyle\delta\xi^{g}=ad^{*}(Y(g))\xi^{g}.

Here and henceforth
\delta
stands for the de Rham differential on the group and
\xi^{g}=Ad^{*}_{g}\xi
.
This allows one to write
\omega_{g}
as:

(12)

\omega_{g}=\frac{1}{2}\langle\delta\xi^{g},Y(g)\rangle

Returning to diffeomorphisms of the circle, consider the Maurer-Cartan form on the Virasoro group,
c.f.

[
3
,
5
]
)

(13)

\hat{Y}=\frac{\delta f}{f^{\prime}}+\delta k+\frac{1}{24}\int_{0}^{2\pi}dx\ \frac{\delta f}{f^{\prime}}\left(\frac{f^{\prime\prime}}{f^{\prime}}\right)^{\prime}.

Here
k
denotes the coordinate on the central
\mathbb{R}
, and
\delta k
is the corresponding one-form.

For
\hat{Y}
, equations (
11
) take the form

(14a)

\displaystyle\delta\hat{Y}+\frac{1}{2}[\hat{Y},\hat{Y}]=0,

(14b)

\displaystyle\delta(b^{f}-c)=ad^{*}(\hat{Y})b^{f},

where
[\cdot,\cdot]
is the Lie bracket of
\mathfrak{vir}
,
c.f.
(
2
), and the coadjoint action is given by (
5
).
The KKS symplectic form at a point
b-c\in\mathfrak{vir}^{*}
is then given by

(15)

\begin{split}\omega_{b^{f}-c}&=\frac{1}{2}\left\langle b^{f}-c,[\hat{Y},\hat{Y}]\right\rangle\\
&=\frac{1}{2}\int_{S^{1}}\left(\delta b^{f}\wedge\frac{\delta f}{f^{\prime}}\right)dx\\
&=\int_{S^{1}}\left(b_{0}\delta f^{\prime}\wedge\delta f+\frac{c}{24}\delta\log f^{\prime}\wedge\delta\log(f^{\prime})^{\prime}\right)dx.\end{split}

A direct proof of the identities (
14
) and (
15
) is given in Appendix
A
.

Coadjoint orbits
\mathcal{O}_{b_{0}}\cong\Diff^{+}(S^{1})/\Stab(b_{0})
carry a Hamiltonian action of
\Diff^{+}(S^{1})
.
In what follows, we will be interested in the action of the subgroup
S^{1}\subset\Diff^{+}(S^{1})
given by rigid rotations of the circle.
Expressed in terms of
f\in\widetilde{\Diff}^{+}(S^{1})
, this action takes the form

(16)

\mathbb{R}\ni\theta\colon f(x)\mapsto f(x+\theta),

which descends to an
S^{1}
-action on the orbit.
Its moment map is given by

(17)

\mu_{S^{1}}=\int_{S^{1}}b^{f}(x)dx.

It is known (see for instance
[
6
]
) that
\Stab(b_{0}dx^{2})=S^{1}
and that
b_{0}
is the unique fixed point of the circle action (
16
)
on
\mathcal{O}_{b_{0}}
.

3.
Darboux coordinates on Virasoro orbits

The aim of this Section is to introduce global Darboux coordinates on hyperbolic Virasoro coadjoint orbits which admit a constant representative
b_{0}dx^{2}
and on the parabolic orbit which corresponds to the vanishing quadratic differential.

3.1.
Darboux charts in finite dimensions

In this Section, we recall the classical equivariant Darboux theorem in the finite dimensional situation.

Consider a symplectic manifold
(M^{2n},\omega)
endowed with a Hamiltonian
S^{1}
-action. Let
\mu:M\to\mathbb{R}
be the corresponding moment map,
and let
p\in M
be an isolated fixed point of the
S^{1}
-action.
The equivariant Darboux theorem
[
20
]
states that there is a neighborhood of
p
which is equivariantly symplectomorphic to a neighborhood of
0\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
endowed with the standard symplectic form

(18)

\omega_{0}=\frac{i}{2}\sum_{j}dz_{j}\wedge d\bar{z}_{j},

moment map

(19)

\mu=\mu(p)+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j}k_{j}\lvert z_{j}\rvert^{2},

and
S^{1}
-action

(20)

z_{j}\mapsto e^{ik_{j}\theta}z_{j}.

Here
0\neq k_{j}\in\mathbb{Z}
are the weights of the
S^{1}
-action at
p
.

In general, there are no estimates on the size of an equivariant Darboux chart.
However, there is a special situation where a rather precise estimate is available. The following theorem is a reformulation of the result by Karshon-Tolman
[
11
]
:

Theorem 1
.

Assume that all the weights
k_{j}
are positive and that the
S^{1}
-action is free on
\mu^{-1}(a,b)
, where
a=\mu(p)
. Then, there is an
S^{1}
-equivariant symplectomorphism from
\mu^{-1}([a,b))
to the ball
\{(z_{1},\dots,z_{n});\sum k_{j}|z_{j}|^{2}<2(b-a)\}
equipped with the
S^{1}
-action (
20
), symplectic structure (
18
) and moment map (
19
).
A similar result holds when all the weights
k_{j}
are negative.

Theorem
1
implies that if all the weights are of the same sign and if
p
is the only fixed point, then the corresponding equivariant Darboux chart is global (that is, it covers all of
M
).

As an example, consider the unit two-sphere
S^{2}
embedded in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
with its South pole
S
removed and endowed with the standard symplectic form
\omega=dz\wedge d\varphi
.
Here
z
denotes the
z
-coordinate on
\mathbb{R}^{3}
and
\varphi
is the polar angle.
Consider the
S^{1}
-action which rotates the sphere around the
z
-axis.
This action is Hamiltonian with moment map
\mu\colon(x,y,z)\mapsto z
.
The circle action admits a unique fixed point, the North pole, sitting at
z=+1
. Around the fixed point, the Darboux coordinates are given by

\begin{split}x&=\sqrt{2(1-z)}\sin(\varphi),\\
y&=\sqrt{2(1-z)}\cos(\varphi),\end{split}

such that

\begin{split}\omega&=dx\wedge dy\\
\mu&=1-\frac{1}{2}(x^{2}+y^{2}).\end{split}

This chart is indeed global on
S^{2}\backslash\{S\}
.

The crucial observation is that for a large class of Virasoro coadjoint orbits the circle action (
16
) admits a unique fixed point (see
e.g.

[
6
]
).
Moreover, there is a class of orbits for which all the weights at this fixed point have the same sign.
This suggests that these orbits may admit a global Darboux chart. Note that the
Karshon-Tolman theorem cannot be directly applied to this situation since
Virasoro coadjoint orbits are infinite dimensional.

3.2.
Hyperbolic orbits with
b_{0}<0

In this Section, we construct global equivariant Darboux charts on Virasoro coadjoint orbits passing through constant quadratic differentials
b_{0}dx^{2}
for
b_{0}<0
and
c>0
.

We will need the following notation: for
\alpha>0
, let

\displaystyle\tilde{B}_{\alpha}

\displaystyle=\{F\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}_{+};F^{\prime}(x)>0,F(x+2\pi)=e^{2\pi\alpha}F(x)\}.

and

\displaystyle\tilde{A}_{\alpha}

\displaystyle=\{u\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R};u(x+2\pi)=u(x)+2\pi\alpha\}

Furthermore, we introduce two maps

r_{\alpha}
and
p_{\alpha}
and their composition
q_{\alpha}=p_{\alpha}\circ r_{\alpha}
.
The map

r_{\alpha}\colon\widetilde{\Diff}^{+}(S^{1})\to\tilde{B}_{\alpha}

is given by the formula

r_{\alpha}\colon f\mapsto F(x)=(g_{\alpha}\circ f)(x)=\frac{e^{\alpha f(x)}}{\alpha},

where
g_{\alpha}(y)=e^{\alpha y}/{\alpha}
.
The second map,

p_{\alpha}\colon\tilde{B}_{\alpha}\to\tilde{A}_{\alpha}

is defined by the formula

p_{\alpha}\colon F\mapsto u(x)=\log(F^{\prime}(x)).

It is easy to see that the quasi-periodic properties of
f
imply the quasi-periodic properties of
F
, and that those of
F
imply those of
u
.

Proposition 3.1
.

The maps
r_{\alpha}
and
p_{\alpha}
are bijections.

Proof.

The inverse of the map
r_{\alpha}
is given by

r_{\alpha}^{-1}\colon F\mapsto f(x)=\frac{1}{\alpha}\,\log(\alpha F(x)).

Furthermore, we have
f^{\prime}(x)=F^{\prime}(x)/(\alpha F(x))>0
and

f(x+2\pi)=\frac{1}{\alpha}\,\log(\alpha F(x+2\pi))=\frac{1}{\alpha}\,\log\left(\alpha e^{2\pi\alpha}F(x)\right)=f(x)+2\pi.

The inverse of
p_{\alpha}
is given by

p_{\alpha}^{-1}\colon u\mapsto F(x)=\frac{1}{e^{2\pi\alpha}-1}\left(e^{2\pi\alpha}\int_{0}^{x}e^{u(s)}ds+\int_{x}^{2\pi}e^{u(s)}ds\right).

Note that
F^{\prime}(x)=e^{u(x)}>0
and
F(2\pi)=e^{2\pi\alpha}F(0)
, as required.
∎

The composition map
q_{\alpha}
is also a bijection given by the formula

q_{\alpha}\colon f\mapsto u(x)=\alpha f(x)+\log(f^{\prime}(x)).

Its inverse
q_{\alpha}^{-1}
is given by an explicit (albeit a bit cumbersome) expression:

q_{\alpha}^{-1}:u\mapsto f(x)=\frac{1}{\alpha}\left(\log\left(e^{2\pi\alpha}\int_{0}^{x}e^{u(s)}ds+\int_{x}^{2\pi}e^{u(s)}ds\right)+\log\left(\frac{\alpha}{e^{2\pi\alpha}-1}\right)\right).

In what follows, we will be interested in the quotient space

\widetilde{\Diff}^{+}(S^{1})/\mathbb{R}\cong\Diff^{+}(S^{1})/S^{1}.

Here the action of
\mathbb{R}
is given by
f(x)\mapsto f(x)+\phi
.
It is convenient to introduce a special notation for quotient spaces

B_{\alpha}=\tilde{B}_{\alpha}/\mathbb{R}

under the action
F(x)\mapsto e^{2\pi\alpha\phi}F(x)
, and for quotient spaces

A_{\alpha}=\tilde{A}_{\alpha}/\mathbb{R}

under the action
u(x)\mapsto u(x)+\alpha\phi
.

It is easy to see that the maps
r_{\alpha},p_{\alpha}
and
q_{\alpha}=p_{\alpha}\circ r_{\alpha}
descend to bijections between these quotient spaces. In particular the bijection

q_{\alpha}:\Diff^{+}(S^{1})/S^{1}\to A_{\alpha}

lands in the affine space
A_{\alpha}

modelled on the space of
2\pi
-periodic functions with zero mean:

u(x)=\alpha x+\sum_{n\neq 0}u_{n}e^{inx},

where
u_{-n}=\bar{u}_{n}
.

One of the main results of this article is the following theorem:

Theorem 2
.

The map
q_{\alpha}
is an equivariant symplectic isomorphism between
the hyperbolic coadjoint orbit with
b_{0}=-c\alpha^{2}/24
and the affine space

A_{\alpha}
with symplectic form

(21)

\omega=\frac{c}{24}\int_{S^{1}}(\delta u\wedge\delta u^{\prime})dx=\frac{i\pi c}{6}\sum_{n>0}n\,\delta u_{n}\wedge\delta\bar{u}_{n},

action of
\widetilde{\Diff}^{+}{S^{1}}

(22)

h\colon u\mapsto u^{h}(x)=u(h(x))+\log(h^{\prime}(x)),

and moment map

(23)

\mu(u)=\frac{c}{12}\left(u^{\prime\prime}(x)-\frac{1}{2}\,u^{\prime}(x)^{2}\right).

Proof.

First, we show that the map
q_{\alpha}
intertwines the actions of
\widetilde{\Diff}^{+}(S^{1})
on itself (by right multiplications) and on the space of quasi-periodic functions
u(x)
:

\displaystyle f\circ h

\displaystyle\mapsto

\displaystyle\alpha f(h(x))+\log((f\circ h)^{\prime}(x))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\alpha f(h(x))+\log(f^{\prime}(h(x)))+\log(h^{\prime}(x))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle u(h(x))+\log(h^{\prime}(x)).

These actions descend to actions on the quotient spaces
\widetilde{\Diff}^{+}(S^{1})/\mathbb{R}\cong\Diff^{+}(S^{1})/S^{1}
and
A_{\alpha}
.

Next, we transfer the moment map and the symplectic form from the Virasoro coadjoint orbit to the space
B_{\alpha}
.
Note that the function
g_{\alpha}
has the following properties:
g^{\prime}_{\alpha}(x)>0
,
S(g_{\alpha})=-\alpha^{2}/2=12b_{0}/c
, and

g^{\prime\prime}_{\alpha}/g^{\prime}_{\alpha}=\alpha
is constant.

By the properties of Schwarzian derivative, we have

\mu=b_{0}f^{\prime}(x)^{2}+\frac{c}{12}S(f)=\frac{c}{12}\left(S(g_{\alpha})\circ f+S(f)\right)=\frac{c}{12}\,S(F).

Furthermore,

(24)

\begin{split}\omega&=\frac{c}{24}\int_{S^{1}}\left(\delta\log F^{\prime}\wedge\delta\log(F^{\prime})^{\prime}\right)dx-\left.\frac{g^{\prime\prime}_{\alpha}(f(x))}{g^{\prime}_{\alpha}(f(x))}\frac{\delta f\wedge\delta f^{\prime}}{f^{\prime}}\right\rvert_{0}^{2\pi}\\
&=\frac{c}{24}\int_{S^{1}}\left(\delta\log F^{\prime}\wedge\delta\log(F^{\prime})^{\prime}\right)dx.\end{split}

Finally, we use the map
p_{\alpha}
to transfer the moment maps and the symplectic form to the affine space
A_{\alpha}
:

(25)

\mu=\frac{c}{12}\left(u^{\prime\prime}(x)-\frac{1}{2}\,u^{\prime}(x)^{2}\right),\hskip 8.5359pt\omega=\frac{c}{24}\int_{S^{1}}(\delta u\wedge\delta u^{\prime})dx.

This completes the proof.
∎

Theorem
2
provides global
S^{1}
-equivariant Darboux charts on all hyperbolic Virasoro coadjoint orbits
\mathcal{O}_{b_{0}}
passing through constant quadratic differentials
b_{0}dx^{2}
with
b_{0}<0
. The
S^{1}
-action in this Darboux chart is given by

\theta:u(x)\mapsto u(x+\theta).

In terms of Fourier components
u_{n}
, we have
u_{n}\mapsto e^{in\theta}u_{n}
, and the weights of the action at the fixed point
u_{n}=0
are given by
n=1,2,\dots
. The moment map of the
S^{1}
-action is given by

(26)

\mu_{S^{1}}=\int_{S^{1}}\mu(u)dx=-\frac{c}{24}\int_{S^{1}}u^{\prime}(x)^{2}dx=-\frac{c}{12}\left(2\pi\alpha^{2}+4\pi\sum_{n>0}n^{2}|u_{n}|^{2}\right).

Furthermore, the group
\Diff^{+}(S^{1})
acts on the symplectic affine space
A_{\alpha}
by affine Hamiltonian transformations (
22

23

u(x)
.

3.3.
The parabolic orbit
b_{0}=0

In this Section, we consider the special case of the parabolic Virasoro orbit which corresponds to the vanishing quadratic differential.

We define a map

q_{0}\colon\widetilde{\Diff}^{+}(S^{1})\to\tilde{A}_{0}=\{u:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R};u(x+2\pi)=u(x)\}

given by the formula

q_{0}\colon f\mapsto u(x)=\log(f^{\prime}(x)).

It descends to a map (that we again denote by
q_{0}
)

q_{0}\colon\widetilde{\Diff}^{+}(S^{1})/\mathbb{R}\to A_{0}=\{u:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R};u(x+2\pi)=u(x)\}/\mathbb{R}.

Here the the
\mathbb{R}
-actions are as follows:
f(x)\mapsto f(x)+\phi,u(x)\mapsto u(x)+\psi
.

Proposition 3.2
.

The map
q_{0}:\widetilde{\Diff}^{+}(S^{1})/\mathbb{R}\to A_{0}
is a bijection.

Proof.

The inverse map is given by the following formula:

q_{0}^{-1}:u\mapsto f(x)=f(0)+\frac{2\pi}{\int_{0}^{2\pi}e^{u(s)}ds}\int_{0}^{x}e^{u(t)}dt.

Note
q_{0}^{-1}
is a well defined map on
A_{0}
since
f(x)
doesn’t change under shifts
u(x)\mapsto u(x)+\psi
. It defines a class of diffeomorphisms
f(x)
modulo transformations
f(x)\mapsto f(x)+\phi
which amount to the choice of
f(0)
.
∎

Similar to Theorem
2
, we obtain the following result:

Theorem 3
.

The map
q_{0}
is an equivariant symplectomorphism between the parabolic coadjoint orbit corresponding to
b_{0}=0
and the vector space
A_{0}
with the symplectic form, the action of
\widetilde{\Diff}^{+}(S^{1})
and the moment map given by equations (
21
),(
22
) and (
23
).

Proof.

For the action, we compute

u^{h}(x)=\log((f\circ h)^{\prime}(x))=\log(f^{\prime}(h(x)))+\log(h^{\prime}(x))=u(h(x))+\log(h^{\prime}(x)).

The moment map is given by

\mu=\frac{c}{12}S(f)=\frac{c}{12}\left(u^{\prime\prime}(x)-\frac{1}{2}u^{\prime}(x)^{2}\right).

Finally, for the symplectic form we obtain,

\omega=\frac{c}{24}\int_{S^{1}}(\delta\log(f^{\prime})\wedge\delta(\log(f^{\prime}))^{\prime})dx=\frac{c}{24}\int_{S^{1}}(\delta u\wedge\delta u^{\prime})dx.

∎

Theorem
3
provides a global
S^{1}
-equivariant Darboux chart on the coadjoint orbit passing through the vanishing quadratic differential. Again, we actually obtain a stronger result: the action of the diffeomorphism group
\Diff^{+}(S^{1})
on
A_{0}
is by affine Hamiltonian transformations with moment map given by a quadratic polynomial in
u(x)
.

4.
The Teichmüller orbit

Recall that generic elliptic orbits corresponding to
b_{0}>0
and exceptional orbits corresponding to
b_{0}=ck^{2}/24
each possess a unique fixed point of the
S^{1}
-action of rigid rotations
x\mapsto x+\theta
. Therefore, one can conjecture that they also admit global equivariant Darboux charts:

Conjecture:
Virasoro coadjoint orbits corresponding to
b_{0}>0
admit global
S^{1}
-equivariant Darboux charts.

To the best of our knowledge, this conjecture remains out of reach. However, techniques of the last Section can be applied to the case of exceptional orbits, and they give insight into their geometry. In this Section, we focus our attention on the Techmüller orbit which corresponds to
k=1
.
By abuse of notation, we will denote for any
f(x)\in\widetilde{\Diff}^{+}(S^{1})
its class
[f(x)]\in\Diff^{+}(S^{1})
also by
f(x)
.

Recall that the Teichmüller orbit is the unique Virasoro coadjoint orbit whose stabiliser is isomorphic to
the group
{\rm PSL}(2,\mathbb{R})
:

\mathcal{O}_{\rm Teich}\cong{\rm Diff}^{+}(S^{1})/{\rm PSL}(2,\mathbb{R}).

The corresponding action is given by Möbius transformations

F(x)\mapsto\frac{aF(x)+b}{cF(x)+d},

where

F(x)=2\tan\left(\frac{f(x)}{2}\right).

Following the strategy of the previous Section, we define

v(x)=\log(F^{\prime}(x))=-\log(\cos^{2}(f(x)/2))+\log(f^{\prime}(x)).

Under the action of diffeomorphisms,
v(x)
transforms according to (
22
). Indeed,

v^{h}(x)=\log(F(h(x))^{\prime})=\log(F^{\prime}(h(x))+\log(h^{\prime}(x))=v(h(x))+\log(h^{\prime}(x)).

However,
v(x)
has a singularity at a point
y
where
f(y)=\pi
.
In more detail, it belongs to the space

C=\{v\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R};v(x+2\pi)=v(x),v(x)=-\log(\sin^{2}((x-y)/2))+u(x)\},

where
y\in\mathbb{R}
is some point on the real axis, and
u(x)
is smooth. The singularity of
v(x)
at
x=y
is of the form
v(x)\sim-2\log(|x-y|)
. However, it is more convenient to use the function
\sin^{2}((x-y)/2)
since it is
2\pi
-periodic and has the same behavior at zero.

The space
C
is a vector bundle over the circle with projection map

(27)

v(x)\mapsto y~{\rm mod}~2\pi\mathbb{Z},

and with fibers
C_{y}
isomorphic to spaces of smooth periodic functions (hence, fibers are independent of
y
). It is easy to check that the action (
22
) is well defined on
C
.

We define a subbundle
D\subset C
as follows:

D=\left\{v\in C;\;u^{\prime}(y)=0,\int_{S^{1}}\frac{e^{u(x)}-e^{u(y)}}{\sin^{2}\left((x-y)/2\right)}\,dx=0\right\}.

Note that for a smooth function
u(x)
with
u^{\prime}(y)=0
the expression under the integral is smooth at
x=y
, and the integral is well defined. The fiber of
D_{y}
is singled out by the two equations. While the equation
u^{\prime}(y)=0
is linear, the integral condition is highly non-linear, and therefore the fiber
D_{y}
is not a vector space.

It will be convenient to introduce one more space as follows: consider configurations of two points on the circle

{\rm Conf}_{2}(S_{1})=\{(y,z)\in S^{1}\times S^{1};y\neq z\}.

It has a natural projection to the first point:
(y,z)\mapsto y
. Denote by
E
the pull-back of the bundle
D
under this projection. That is, for all
z\neq y
the fiber
E_{y,z}=D_{y}
. Definitions of the spaces
D
and
E
are justified by the following result:

Theorem 4
.

For
f\in\Diff^{+}(S^{1})
, the map

(28)

u\colon f\mapsto u(x)=\log(\sin^{2}((x-y)/2))-\log(\cos^{2}(f(x)/2))+\log(f^{\prime}(x)),

where
f(y)=\pi
, takes values in
D
. Furthermore, the map
f\mapsto(u(x),y,z)
with

f(z)=0
defines a bijection
\Diff^{+}(S^{1})\to E
.

Remark 3
.

The change of variable
v(x)
in (
27
) has been previously used in
[
4
]
for the analysis of correlation functions of the SYK model.
Due to the singularity of
v(x)
, a cut-off is needed to regularize the integral constraint.
In (
28
), the singularity is subtracted from the beginning.

Proof.

The function
u(x)
admits an expansion at
x=y
. Its first terms read:

\displaystyle u(x)

\displaystyle=\log\left(\frac{(x-y)^{2}}{4}\right)-\log\left(\frac{f^{\prime}(y)^{2}(x-y)^{2}}{4}\right)-\frac{f^{\prime\prime}(y)}{f^{\prime}(y)}\,(x-y)

\displaystyle+\log(f^{\prime}(y))+\frac{f^{\prime\prime}(y)}{f^{\prime}(y)}\,(x-y)+O((x-y)^{2})

\displaystyle=-\log(f^{\prime}(y))+O((x-y)^{2}).

That is, the function
u(x)
is
2\pi
-periodic and smooth at
x=y
. We also observe that
u(y)=-\log(f^{\prime}(y))
and
u^{\prime}(y)=0
.

Next, consider the function

\Phi(x)=2\tan\left(\frac{f(x)}{2}\right)+\frac{2}{f^{\prime}(y)}\,{\rm cotan}\left(\frac{x-y}{2}\right).

At
x=y
, it admits a Laurent series expansion whose first terms are given by

\Phi(x)=-\frac{4}{f^{\prime}(y)}\,\frac{1}{x-y}+2\frac{f^{\prime\prime}(y)}{f^{\prime}(y)}+\frac{4}{f^{\prime}(y)}\,\frac{1}{x-y}+O(x-y)=2\frac{f^{\prime\prime}(y)}{f^{\prime}(y)}+O(x-y).

Hence,
\Phi(x)
is smooth and
2\pi
-periodic. This implies

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\int_{S^{1}}\Phi^{\prime}(x)dx=\int_{S^{1}}\left(\frac{f^{\prime}(x)}{\cos^{2}(f(x)/2)}-\frac{e^{u(y)}}{\sin^{2}((x-y)/2)}\right)\,dx

\displaystyle=\int_{S^{1}}\frac{e^{u(x)}-e^{u(y)}}{\sin^{2}((x-y)/2)}\,dx,

and we conclude that
u(x)
indeed belongs to
D
.

The inverse map
E\to\Diff^{+}(S^{1})
is given by the formula

(u(x),y,z)\mapsto f(x)=2\arctan\left(\frac{1}{2}\int_{z}^{x}\frac{e^{u(s)}}{\sin^{2}((s-y)/2)}\,ds\right).

It is easy to check that if
(u(x),y,z)\in E
, then this expression defines an element
f\in\Diff^{+}(S^{1})
with
f(z)=0
and
f(y)=\pi
. This completes the proof.
∎

Theorem
4
gives rise to the following description of the Teichmüller orbit:

Proposition 4.1
.

\mathcal{O}_{\rm Teich}\cong\left\{u\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R};u(x+2\pi)=u(x),u(\pi)=u^{\prime}(\pi)=0,\int_{S^{1}}\frac{e^{u(x)}-1}{\cos^{2}(x/2)}\,dx=0\right\}.

Proof.

Since

\Diff^{+}(S^{1})\cong E
, the space
E
inherits the free
{\rm PSL}(2,\mathbb{R})
action. This action admits a section: each
{\rm PSL}(2,\mathbb{R})
orbit has exactly one point with
f(0)=0,f(\pi)=\pi
and
f^{\prime}(\pi)=1
. This implies that
y=\pi,z=0
and
u(\pi)=-\log(f^{\prime}(\pi))=0
. Hence, the Teichmüller orbit is identified with the fiber
E_{\pi,0}
with an extra condition of
u(\pi)=0
.
∎

We now describe the moment map and the symplectic form on the Teichmüller orbit in terms of this model.

Theorem 5
.

The moment map and the symplectic form on the Teichmüller orbit are given by

(29)

\mu=\frac{c}{12}\left(u^{\prime\prime}(x)-\frac{1}{2}u^{\prime}(x)^{2}-u^{\prime}(x)\tan\left(\frac{x}{2}\right)+\frac{1}{2}\right),

(30)

\omega=\frac{c}{24}\int_{S^{1}}(\delta u\wedge\delta u^{\prime})\,dx.

Proof.

For the moment map, we compute

\mu=\frac{c}{12}\left(\frac{1}{2}f^{\prime}(x)^{2}+S(f)\right)=\frac{c}{12}\left((S(g)\circ f)f^{\prime}(x)^{2}+S(f)\right)=\frac{c}{12}\,S(F)

where
g(y)=2\tan(y/2)
and
F(x)=g(f(x))
. Here we have used the composition formula for the Schwarzian derivative and the fact that
S(g)=1/2
. Then,
since

F^{\prime}(x)=\frac{e^{u(x)}}{\cos^{2}(x/2)},

we obtain

S(F)=u^{\prime\prime}(x)-\frac{1}{2}u^{\prime}(x)^{2}-u^{\prime}(x)\tan\left(\frac{x}{2}\right)+\frac{1}{2},

as required. Note that the expression
u^{\prime}(x)\tan(x/2)
is smooth at
x=\pi
since
u^{\prime}(\pi)=0
.

For the calculation of
\omega
, we use equation (
24
) with the periodic function
g(y)=\tan(y/2)
. Observe that
\delta\log(F^{\prime})=\delta u
since the factor
\cos^{2}(x/2)
does not contribute in the de Rham differential. This completes the proof.
∎

Theorem
5
implies the following expression for the moment of rigid
S^{1}
-rotations:

(31)

\mu_{S^{1}}=\frac{c}{12}\,\int_{S^{1}}\left(u^{\prime}(x)\tan\left(\frac{x}{2}\right)-\frac{1}{2}u^{\prime}(x)^{2}\right)+\frac{\pi c}{12}.

Observe that in this model the symplectic form (
30
) is constant, and the moment map (
31
) is quadratic. However, this is not an equivariant Darboux chart because the function
u(x)
satisfies the non-linear integral constraint. The Conjecture in the beginning of the Section states that one can find a transformation which would remedy this problem and obtain a model of the Teichmüller orbit on a vector space with constant symplectic structure and quadratic moment map.

5.
Partition function and correlation functions of bilocal operators

In this section we compute the partition function and correlation functions of bilocal operators in Darboux charts on hyperbolic Virasoro coadjoint orbits. While the partition function readily recovers known results
[
2
,
19
]
, the Darboux chart makes the exact computation of correlation functions feasible.

5.1.
Partition function

The partition function of a Schwarzian theory is a path integral of the form

(32)

Z(t)=\int d\lambda(f)e^{t\mu_{S^{1}}(f)},

where
\mu_{S^{1}}(f)
is the moment map for rigid
S^{1}
-rotations,
t
is a parameter, and

d\lambda(f)
stands for some (usually ill defined) measure on
{\rm Diff}^{+}(S^{1})
.

In the case of hyperbolic coadjoint orbits, the global equivariant Darboux chart allows to define the path integral (
32
) as a Gaussian integral. Indeed, recall that for

u(x)=\alpha f(x)+\log f^{\prime}(x),

with
\alpha^{2}=-24b_{0}/c
, the symplectic form
\omega
and the moment map
\mu_{S^{1}}
of rigid rotations are of the following form,
c.f.
(
25
) and (
26
):

\displaystyle\omega

\displaystyle=\frac{c}{24}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\left(\delta u(x)\wedge\delta u^{\prime}(x)\right)dx,

\displaystyle\mu_{S^{1}}

\displaystyle=-\frac{c}{24}\int_{0}^{2\pi}u^{\prime 2}(x)dx.

Here
u(x+2\pi)=u(x)+2\pi\alpha
.

Inspired by the finite dimensional situation, one can argue that the integration measure
d\lambda(f)
is the formal Liouville measure

d\lambda(f)=\mathcal{D}u\cdot{\rm Pf}_{\zeta}(\omega),

where
\mathcal{D}u
is the formal Lebesgues measure (which is ill defined), and

{\rm Pf}_{\zeta}(\omega)
is the
\zeta
-regularized Pfaffian of the symplectic form

{\rm Pf}_{\zeta}(\omega)={\rm Pf}_{\zeta}\left(\frac{c}{12}\partial_{x}\right)=\left(\frac{24\pi}{c}\right)^{1/2}.

While different elements in the path integral (
32
) are ill defined, the whole expression is a well defined Gaussian integral. Indeed, we obtain

(33)

Z(t)={\rm Pf}_{\zeta}(\omega)\,\int\mathcal{D}u\,e^{t\mu_{S^{1}}(u)}=\left(\frac{24\pi}{c}\right)^{1/2}\frac{e^{t\mu_{S^{1}}(u_{cl})}}{\sqrt{\det_{\zeta}(-\frac{ct}{12}\Delta)}}

where
\Delta
denotes the Laplacian on the circle and
u_{cl}
is a solution of the classical equations of motion
u^{\prime\prime}(x)=0
satisfying the quasi-periodicity condition
u(x+2\pi)=u(x)+2\pi\alpha
,
i.e.

u_{cl}(x)=\alpha x+u_{0}.

The expression
\det_{\zeta}
stands the
\zeta
-regularized determinant.
In the case at hand it is given by

\det\nolimits_{\zeta}(-\frac{ct}{12}\Delta)=\frac{48\pi^{2}}{ct}.

Putting things together, we conclude that

(34)

Z(t)=\left(\frac{t}{2\pi}\right)^{1/2}\exp\left(-\frac{ct\alpha^{2}\pi}{12}\right)=\left(\frac{t}{2\pi}\right)^{1/2}e^{2\pi b_{0}t}

This recovers the results of
[
2
,
19
]
.

5.2.
Correlator of a single bilocal operator

In the literature, one often studies
{\rm PSL}(2,\mathbb{R})
-invariant bilocal quantities
\mathcal{O}(x_{1},x_{2})
which in terms of
F(x)=e^{\alpha f(x)}/\alpha
are given by

(35)

\mathcal{O}(x_{1},x_{2})=\frac{F(x_{2})-F(x_{1})}{\sqrt{F^{\prime}(x_{2})F^{\prime}(x_{1})}},

where we assume that
x_{2}>x_{1}
.
In terms of the diffeomorphism
f(x)
, one finds the expression

\mathcal{O}(x_{1},x_{2})=\frac{2}{\alpha}\frac{\sinh\left(\frac{f(x_{2})-f(x_{1})}{2}\right)}{\sqrt{f^{\prime}(x_{2})f^{\prime}(x_{1})}},

and in Darboux coordinates
u(x)=\log F^{\prime}(x)
, we obtain

(36)

\mathcal{O}(x_{1},x_{2})=e^{-(u(x_{1})+u(x_{2}))/2}\int_{x_{1}}^{x_{2}}e^{u(s)}ds.

In this Section, we will compute the correlation function (the Gaussian expectation value) of the following form:

(37)

\begin{split}\left\langle\mathcal{O}(x_{1},x_{2})\right\rangle&=\frac{1}{Z(t)}\int d\lambda(f)\mathcal{O}(x_{1},x_{2})e^{t\mu_{S^{1}}(f)}\\
&=\frac{{\rm Pf}_{\zeta}(\omega)}{Z(t)}\int_{x_{1}}^{x_{2}}ds\int\mathcal{D}u\ e^{\mathcal{S}(u,s,t)}.\end{split}

Here,

\mathcal{S}(u,s,t)=-\frac{ct}{24}\int_{0}^{2\pi}u^{\prime 2}(x)dx-\frac{1}{2}u(x_{1})-\frac{1}{2}u(x_{2})+u(s)

is a quadratic expression in
u
(in the physics language, the effective action).
1
1

1

It is standard to denote the action functional by the letter
\mathcal{S}
; the action
\mathcal{S}(u,s,t)
should not be confused with the Schwarzian derivatives
S(f)
and
S(F)
.

We observe that the correlator (
37
) is again a Gaussian integral and hence exactly solvable

(38)

\begin{split}\left\langle\mathcal{O}(x_{1},x_{2})\right\rangle&=\frac{{\rm Pf}_{\zeta}(\omega)}{Z(t)}\int_{x_{1}}^{x_{2}}ds\ \frac{e^{\mathcal{S}(u_{cl},s,t)}}{\sqrt{\det_{\zeta}(-\frac{ct}{12}\Delta)}}\\
&=e^{-2\pi b_{0}t}\int_{x_{1}}^{x_{2}}ds\ e^{\mathcal{S}(u_{cl},s,t)},\end{split}

where
u_{cl}
denotes the critical point (in this case, the maximiser) of the quadratic functional
\mathcal{S}(u,s,t)
.
The function
u_{cl}
is continuous, and it is subject to the following conditions:

(i)

away from
x_{1},x_{2}
and
s
,
u_{cl}(x)=mx+n
is a linear function

(ii)

u_{cl}
satisfies the quasi-periodicity condition
u_{cl}(x+2\pi)=u_{cl}(x)+2\pi\alpha

(iii)

u^{\prime}_{cl}
is discontinuous

(39)

\begin{cases}u_{cl}^{\prime}(x_{1}+\varepsilon)-u_{cl}^{\prime}(x_{1}-\varepsilon)=\phantom{-}\frac{6}{ct}\\
u_{cl}^{\prime}(x_{2}+\varepsilon)-u_{cl}^{\prime}(x_{2}-\varepsilon)=\phantom{-}\frac{6}{ct}\\
u_{cl}^{\prime}(s\phantom{{}_{2}}+\varepsilon)-u_{cl}^{\prime}(s\phantom{{}_{2}}-\varepsilon)=-\frac{12}{ct}\end{cases}

This problem is solved by a piecewise linear Ansatz

\begin{split}u_{cl}(x)=\begin{cases}mx+n\qquad&x\leq x_{1}\\
mx_{1}+n+a(x-x_{1})\qquad&x_{1}<x\leq s\\
mx_{1}+n+a(s-x_{1})+b(x-s)\qquad&s<x\leq x_{2}\\
mx_{1}+n+a(s-x_{1})+b(x_{2}-s)+c(x-x_{2})\qquad&x_{2}\leq 2\pi\end{cases}\end{split}

where we assume that
x_{1}<s<x_{2}
.
From (
39
), we find a relation among the slopes

a=m+\frac{6}{ct},\qquad b=m-\frac{6}{ct},\qquad c=m.

The quasi-periodicity condition then yields an expression for
m
as a function of
s
:

m(s)=\alpha-\frac{3}{ct\pi}\left(2s-x_{1}-x_{2}\right).

Putting things together, we obtain

(40)

\mathcal{S}(u_{cl},s,t)=\frac{3}{2ct}(x_{2}-x_{1})-\frac{3}{4\pi ct}\left(2s-x_{1}-x_{2}-\frac{ct\pi}{3}\alpha\right)^{2},

and

(41)

\begin{split}\left\langle\mathcal{O}(x_{1},x_{2})\right\rangle&=e^{-2\pi b_{0}t}\int_{x_{1}}^{x_{2}}ds\ e^{\mathcal{S}(u_{cl},s,t)}\\
&=\pi e^{-2\pi b_{0}t+\frac{3x_{21}}{2ct}}\sqrt{\frac{ct}{12}}\Big(G(x_{21})-G(-x_{21})\Big)\end{split}

where we set
x_{21}=x_{2}-x_{1}
and

G(x)=\erf\left(\sqrt{-2\pi b_{0}}+x\sqrt{\frac{3}{4\pi ct}}\right).

5.2.1.
Classical vs quantum calculus

In this Section, we use an example of the correlator
\langle\mathcal{O}(x_{1},x_{2})\rangle

to illustrate the difference between the standard (classical) calculus, and calculus under the Gaussian path integral.

In more detail, for
f(x)
a diffeomorphism of the circle the bilocal quantity
\mathcal{O}(x_{1},x_{2})
is a smooth function in two variables.
In particular, it admits a Taylor expansion
\mathcal{O}(x_{1},x_{2})
near the diagonal
x_{1}=x_{2}
.
As we will see, this no longer holds under the Gaussian path integral.

Recall that in terms of
F=e^{\alpha f(x)}/\alpha
the moment map and the bilocal observable take the form

(42)

\mu_{S^{1}}(F)=\frac{c}{12}\int_{0}^{2\pi}S(F)dx,\qquad\mathcal{O}(x_{1},x_{2})=\frac{F(x_{2})-F(x_{1})}{\sqrt{F^{\prime}(x_{2})F^{\prime}(x_{1})}}.

We will be interested in the Taylor expansion in
\varepsilon
of the following quantity:

(43)

\left\langle\int_{0}^{2\pi}\mathcal{O}(x,x+\varepsilon)dx\right\rangle.

First, we perform the Taylor expansion inside the correlator,

(44)

\mathcal{O}(x,x+\varepsilon)=\varepsilon-\frac{\varepsilon^{3}}{12}S(F)+\mathcal{O}\left(\varepsilon^{4}\right)

and substitute it in the path integral:

(45)

\begin{split}\left\langle\int_{0}^{2\pi}\mathcal{O}(x,x+\varepsilon)dx\right\rangle&=2\pi\varepsilon-\frac{\varepsilon^{3}}{c}\left\langle\frac{c}{12}\int_{0}^{2\pi}S(F)(x)dx\right\rangle+\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{4})\\
&=2\pi\varepsilon-\frac{\varepsilon^{3}}{c}\left\langle\mu_{S^{1}}(F)\right\rangle+\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{4}).\end{split}

One can compute the expectation
\left\langle\mu_{S^{1}}(F)\right\rangle
as follows:

\left\langle\mu_{S^{1}}(F)\right\rangle=\frac{d\log(Z(t))}{dt}=2\pi b_{0}+\frac{1}{2t}.

Hence,

(46)

\left\langle\int_{0}^{2\pi}\mathcal{O}(x,x+\varepsilon)dx\right\rangle=2\pi\varepsilon-\frac{\varepsilon^{3}}{c}\left(2\pi b_{0}+\frac{1}{2t}\right)+\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{4}).

On the other hand, (

\begin{split}Y^{\prime}&=\frac{\delta f^{\prime}}{f^{\prime}}-\frac{f^{\prime\prime}}{f^{\prime}}\frac{\delta f}{f^{\prime}}\\

\displaystyle=b^{\prime}(f(x))f^{\prime 2}\delta f+2b(f(x))f^{\prime\prime}\delta f+\frac{c}{12}S(f)^{\prime}\frac{\delta f}{f^{\prime}}.

\displaystyle ad^{*}(Y)b^{f}

\displaystyle=2Y^{\prime}b^{f}(x)+Y(b^{f}(x))^{\prime}+\frac{c}{12}Y^{\prime\prime\prime}
