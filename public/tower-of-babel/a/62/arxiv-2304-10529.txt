Vardan Oganesyan


Abstract




The first step towards symplectic homotopy theory

Abstract.
We consider two categories related to symplectic manifolds:

1. Objects are symplectic manifolds and morphisms are symplectic embeddings.

2. Objects are symplectic manifolds endowed with compatible almost complex structure and morphisms are pseudoholomorphic maps.

We define new homotopy theories for these categories. In particular, we give definitions of homotopy equivalent symplectic manifolds and define new cohomology theories. Theses cohomology theories are functorial, homotopy invariant and have other interesting properties.

We also construct triangulated persistence category of symplectic manifolds. This allows to apply machinery developed Biran, Cornea, and Zhang and define distances between symplectic manifolds.

1
Introduction

This paper was inspired by motivic homotopy theory of algebraic varieties and by the following question of Fukaya (asked in
[
5
]
)

Can one quantize Adams spectral sequence?

This leads us to a different but similar question:

Can we define the homotopy theory of symplectic manifolds? Can we define symplectic stable homotopy theory and the Adams spectral sequence?

In this paper we make the first step towards symplectic homotopy theory, but there is still a lot of work required to be done to answer the above questions.

We consider the following categories:

1. Objects are symplectic manifolds and morphisms are symplectic embeddings.

2. Objects are symplectic manifolds endowed with compatible almost complex structure and morphisms are pseudoholomorphic maps.

We denote these categories by the same symbol
\mathcal{S}ymp
and the reader can replace
\mathcal{S}ymp
by any of the mentioned categories.

These categories are not rich enough to do homotopy theory. We do not have all limits and colimits (in sense of category theory) and as a result we can not even define simple operations
X/W
,
X\vee Y
,
X\wedge Y
. Also, all naive definitions of homotopy equivalent morphisms do not result in an equivalence relation. There are more disadvantages of these categories, but we already see that we need to add more objects and morphisms into the mentioned categories to be able to do homotopy theory.

We can try to solve all the mentioned problems by considering symplectic structures on nonsmooth manifolds, but this is technically very complicated and seems to be not flexible enough.

We have a natural method to add morphisms and objects into our category. This method was introduced by Grothendieck and is known in algebraic geometry. Let us discuss a philosophical aspect here and give rigorous definitions in Section
5
. In functional analysis we have generalized functions. Roughly speaking, generalized functions are functionals on the space of functions. Our set of functions can be embedded into the space of functionals, i.e. each function defines a generalized function. Of course in order to obtain something useful we need to work with a certain subset of ‘nice’ functionals. It turns out that similar idea works for any small category
\mathcal{C}
. Let
\mathcal{C}^{op}
be the opposite category. We consider a category of functors from
\mathcal{C}^{op}
to a category with required properties (sets, abelian groups). This category of functors is called a category of presheves on
\mathcal{C}
and is denoted by
PrSh_{\mathcal{C}}
. It turns out that
PrSh_{\mathcal{C}}
has all limits and colimits and we are able to define analogues of quotients, smash products and other operations. On the other hand,
PrSh_{\mathcal{C}}
is very big and we loose all information about
\mathcal{C}
. In other words,
\mathcal{C}
may have some colimits (not all, but some of them) and the category of presheves does not respect existing colimits. To solve the mentioned problem we need to consider a category of sheaves.

We need to apply this technique to the mentioned symplectic categories. We denote the resulting categories by
Sh_{\mathcal{S}ymp}
, where
Sh
stands for sheaves. Roughly speaking, each element of
Sh_{\mathcal{S}ymp}
defines a sheaf on all symplectic manifolds and
\mathcal{S}ymp
is naturally embedded into
Sh_{\mathcal{S}ymp}
. As a result, we replace our manifolds
X
by functors
\mathcal{F}_{X}
with no information loss:
X
,
Y
are isomorphic if and only if
\mathcal{F}_{X}
is naturally isomorphic to
\mathcal{F}_{Y}
(see Section
5
). The advantage is that now we can define quotients, wedge products, and symplectic spheres (with the understanding that those are sheaves, not manifolds). Moreover, now we are able to define homotopy equivalent maps (see Section
\ref{homequivalence})
.

We also define new cohomology groups. These cohomology groups have all required properties: functoriality, homotopy invariance,… We discuss all details in Sections
10
and
11
. Note that there are not so many functorial nvariants in symplectic geometry.

In fact, we get that any object
X\in\mathcal{S}ymp
defines a cohomology theory and any morphism
f:X\rightarrow Y
defines a map between cohomology theories associated with
X
and
Y
.

We also construct triangulated persistence category of symplectic manifolds (see Section
12
). This allows to apply machinery developed Biran, Cornea, and Zhang (see
[
1
]
,
[
2
]
) and define distances between symplectic manifolds.

Let us note that this technique is not completely new in symplectic geometry. Similar methods was used by Pardon in
[
12
]
to construct the fundamental class.

In fact, homotopy theory of some other ‘nonflexible’ categories exist. In
1990-2000
Morel, Suslin, and Voevodsky defined
A^{1}-
homotopy theory of algebraic varieties. This theory helped to solve many open problems in algebraic geometry; the interested reader can find all details in
[
10
,
11
,
18
]
. Our definitions are partially inspired by these papers.

Since the technique used in this paper is not widely known by symplectic geometers, we provide all definitions and give geometric interpretation of all results. We only assume that the reader is familiar with basic concepts of category theory.

2
Some reasons to read this paper

In this section we discuss some relations of known invariants and open problems to the cohomology groups defined in this paper. These relations will be studied in further papers and in this paper we only mention them.

The reader may ask if it is useful to state some open problems in terms of the defined cohomology groups. The answer is yes. As soon as we define a cohomology theory with all the nice properties (functoriality, homotopy invariance, …), we immediately get some tools to study these problems. For example, we get exact sequences, spectral sequences and other tools, like in algebraic topology.
In this paper we make the first step and only define cohomology groups and prove that our groups have all the mentioned nice properties.

In this Section we simplify our definitions to be able to explain the basic ideas.

3
Motivation

3.1
Theorem of Dold-Thom

Let
(X,e)
be some nice based topological space. Denote by
SP^{k}(X,e)
the symmetric product, i.e.

SP^{k}(X,e)=X^{k}/\Sigma_{k}=(\underbrace{X\times\ldots\times X}_{k})/\Sigma_{k},

where
\Sigma_{k}
is the symmetric group acting on
X^{k}
by permuting the factors. We denote elements
a\in SP^{k}(X,e)
by unordered set
a=\{p_{1},\ldots,p_{k}\}
. We have the following embedding

SP^{k}(X,e)\hookrightarrow SP^{k+1}(X,e),\;\;\;\{p_{1},\ldots,p_{k}\}\hookrightarrow\{e,p_{1},\ldots,p_{k}\}

So, we get a sequence of embeddings

SP^{0}(X,e)=e\in X=SP^{1}(X,e)\hookrightarrow SP^{2}(X,e)\hookrightarrow\ldots

We define the infinite symmetric product

SP(X,e)=\bigcup_{k=0}^{\infty}SP^{k}(X,e)=colim(SP^{k}(X,e)),

where a set
V\subset SP(X,e)
is closed if and only if
V\cap SP^{k}(X,e)
is closed for any
k
. In other words, points of
SP(X,e)
are infinite unordered sets
\{\ldots,e,p_{1},\ldots,p_{k},e\ldots\}
, where only finitely many terms are not the basepont
e
.

Remark 3.1
.

For simplicity, we write
\{p_{1},\ldots,p_{k}\}
instead of
\{\ldots,e,p_{1},\ldots,p_{k},e,\ldots\}
, where
p_{j}\neq e
for any
j=1,\ldots,k
.

The infinite symmetric product is commutative, associative monoid with unity (a group without inverses). Indeed, we define

\begin{gathered}\{p_{1},\ldots,p_{r}\}+\{q_{1},\ldots,q_{s}\}=\{p_{1},\ldots,p_{r},q_{1},\ldots,q_{s}\},\\
\{e\}+\{p_{1},\ldots,p_{r}\}=\{p_{1},\ldots,p_{r}\}.\end{gathered}

Let
\Delta^{n}
be the standard
n-
simplex. Consider a monoid

Map(\Delta^{n},SP(X,e)),

where
Map
stands for all continuous maps. Since there are
n+1
embeddings
i_{k}:\Delta^{n-1}\hookrightarrow\Delta^{n}
as facets, we get
n+1
induced maps (restrictions)

\begin{gathered}\partial_{k}:Map(\Delta^{n},SP(X,e))\rightarrow Map(\Delta^{n-1},SP(X,e)),\\
\partial_{k}=f\circ i_{k},\;\;f\in Map(\Delta^{n},SP(X,e)).\end{gathered}

Let
Map(\Delta^{n},SP(X,e))^{+}
be the Grothendieck group of the monoid
Map(\Delta^{n},SP(X,e))
(e formally add inverses respecting the structure of the monoid). Denote

TC_{n}=Map(\Delta^{n},SP(X,e))^{+}

If
f\in Map(\Delta^{n},SP(X,e))
, then we denote its inverse in
TC_{n}
by
-f
. We define the following map:

d:TC_{n}\rightarrow TC_{n-1},\;\;\;d=\sum\limits_{k=0}^{n}(-1)^{k}\partial_{k}.

Direct computations show that
d^{2}=0
and we get a chain complex

\rightarrow TC_{n+1}\rightarrow TC_{n}\rightarrow TC_{n-1}\rightarrow\ldots\rightarrow TC_{0}\rightarrow 0.

Example 3.2
.

Consider
TC_{0}(X)=Map(\Delta^{0},SP(X,e))^{+}
, where
\Delta^{0}
is a point. We see that
TC_{0}
is isomorphic to
\mathbb{Z}[X]/\mathbb{Z}[e]
, i.e. formal finite linear combinations of points of
X
modulo multiples of
e
.

The theorem below was proved by Dold and Thom in
1958
.

Theorem 3.3
.

(Dold-Thom,
[
4
]
)
Let
H(TC_{*},d)
be the homology groups of the complex
(TC_{*},d)
. There is an isomorphism between
H(TC_{*},d)
and the ordinary reduced singular homology groups
\widetilde{H}_{*}^{sing}(X,\mathbb{Z})
.

Remark 3.4
.

Dold and Thom also proved that
\widetilde{H}_{*}(X,\mathbb{Z})
is isomorphic to
\pi_{*}(SP(X,e))
.

The complex of Dold-Thom has an alternative construction. Let
X
be some nice topological space (not based). Consider

\coprod_{k=1}SP^{k}(X),

where
\sqcup
is the disjoint union. Note that
SP^{0}(X)
is not included into the disjoint union. We define the addition on
\coprod_{k=1}SP^{k}(X)
as before

\{p_{1},\ldots,p_{r}\}+\{q_{1},\ldots,q_{s}\}=\{p_{1},\ldots,p_{r},q_{1},\ldots,q_{s}\}.

and get the addition on
Map(\Delta^{n},\coprod_{k=1}SP^{k}(X))
. We say that an element of
f\in Map(\Delta^{n},SP^{n}(X,e))
is irreducible if

f\neq f_{1}+f_{2},\quad f_{1},f_{2}\in Map(\Delta^{n},\coprod_{k=1}SP^{k}(X)),

We denote the set of irreducible maps by
Ir(Map(\Delta^{n},\coprod_{k=1}SP^{k}(X)))
. Then, we consider a group of finite formal linear combinations

K_{n}(X)=\mathbb{Z}[Ir(Map(\Delta^{n},\coprod_{k=1}SP^{k}(X)))].

In the same way, we can define a group

K(Y,X)=\mathbb{Z}[Ir(Map(Y,\coprod_{k=1}SP^{k}(X)))],

(3.1)

where
Y
is a topological spaces. Indeed, the structure of
\Delta_{n}
is not used to define
K_{n}
.

Example 3.5
.

Consider a map
f:\Delta^{0}\rightarrow SP^{k}(X)
. Since
\Delta^{0}
is a point, we get that

is a point

This shows that

. Therefore,

is isomorphic to finite formal linear combinations of points of

with integer coefficients.

We define the restriction
\partial_{k}
and the differential

by the same formulas (given above) and get a chain complex

\rightarrow K_{n+1}\rightarrow K_{n}\rightarrow K_{n-1}\rightarrow\ldots\rightarrow K_{0}\rightarrow 0

.

(Dold-Thom,
[

Let

We have the following question:

Assume that spaces

3.2
Motivic cohomology groups of algebraic varieties

The reader may skip this section. In this section we give semi-true facts about motivic cohomology groups, but show how the ideas explained in the previous section can be used to define new invariants of algebraic varieties.

Let
X
be an algebraic variety. We define an algebraic
n-
simplex

\Delta^{n}_{alg}=\{(z_{0},\ldots,z_{n})\in\mathbb{C}^{n+1}\;|\;z_{0}+\ldots+z_{n}=1\}

We define embedding of
(n-1)-
simplex into
n-
simplex

i_{k}(\Delta^{n-1})=\Delta^{n}\cap\{z_{k}=0\},\;\;\;k=0,\ldots,n.

Let us note that
SP^{N}(X)
is also an algebraic variety and assume that we know how to define infinite symmetric product
SP(X)
. Let
Map_{alg}(\cdot,\cdot)
be the set of algebraic maps between algebraic varieties. Arguing as in the previous section, we note that
Map_{alg}(\Delta^{n},SP(X))
is a monoid. We consider the Grothendieck group and a chain complex

\begin{gathered}C_{n}=Map_{alg}(\Delta^{n},SP(X))^{+},\\
d:C_{n}\rightarrow C_{n-1},\;\;\;d=\sum\limits_{k=1}^{n+1}(-1)^{k}\partial_{k},\;\;\;\partial_{k}(f)=f\circ i_{k},\;\;\;d^{2}=0.\end{gathered}

The homology groups
H^{sus}_{*}(X)=H(C_{*}(X),d)
are called the Suslin homology of
X
.

Example 3.7
.

Let
S_{g}
be a Riemann surface of genus
g
and
J(S_{g})
be its Jacobian. We have

H_{0}^{sus}(S_{g})=\mathbb{Z}\times J(S_{g}).

In particular, if
T
is
2-
torus, then
H_{0}^{sus}(T)=\mathbb{Z}\times T
.

Let
Y
be an algebraic variety and
U\subset Y
be a Zarisky open subset. As in the previous section, we get a presheaf of chain complexes

U\rightarrow Map_{alg}^{+}(\Delta^{*}\times U,\;SP(X)).

This presheaf is a sheaf (we do not discuss topology) and we get hypercohomology groups of this sheaf. As a result, we get that

Any variety
X
assign hepercohomology groups to all varieties
Y
. This assignment is called the motive of
X
.

Many nontrivial algebraic invariants are hidden in these hypercohomology groups.

3.3
Some problems to define symplectic motivic cohomology groups

Let
X
be a symplectic manifold. We can not mimic algebraic construction of motivic cohomology groups and define symplectic analogue. There are the following problems:

1. The symmetric product
SP^{N}(X)
is not a smooth manifold. Singularities in algebraic geometry come in a natural way, but not in symplectic geometry. To be able to study symplectic (or some other maps) between
Y
and
SP^{N}(X)
we need to define a symplectic structure on
SP^{N}(X)
. In fact,
SP^{N}(X)
is stratified by smooth symplectic maps and we can try to work with stratified symplectic manifolds. This is very inconvenient because it’s hard to deal with maps between stratified spaces and it’s hard to prove that something is functorial. So, we need to find some convenient symplectic analogue of
SP^{N}(X)
.

2. We need to define symplectic simplices. Standard topological simplices do not work for us. Algebraic simplices
\Delta^{n}_{alg}
(defined in the previous section) are symplectic manfolds and inherit symplectic structure from
\mathbb{C}^{n+1}
. Unfortunately, this symplectic structure looks ugly.

There is a bigger problem. When we define some cohomology groups, we want them to be homotopy invariant. The author do not know how to define homotopy invariant cohomology groups of symplectic manifolds using algebraic simplices.

To avoid the mentioned problems, we consider cubical complexes instead of simplicial complexes.

4.2

We consider a category, where

5.2
Category of presheaves on
\mathcal{S}ymp

Denote by
\mathcal{S}ymp^{op}
the opposite category and by
\mathcal{S}et
the category of sets. Let us consider the category of presheaves
PrSh_{\mathcal{S}ymp}
, i.e. functors

\mathcal{F}:\mathcal{S}ymp^{op}\rightarrow\mathcal{S}et.

Recall that
\mathcal{S}ymp
stands for either
\mathcal{S}\mathcal{S}ymp
, or
\mathcal{J}\mathcal{S}ymp
.

Remark 5.1
.

We can also consider functors to the category of topological spaces. All theorems below work for presheaves of topological spaces if we assume that
\mathcal{S}\mathcal{S}ymp(Y,X)
and
\mathcal{J}\mathcal{S}ymp
induce topology from the space of all continuous maps from
Y
to
X
. To simplify this paper we consider presheaves of sets.

Morphisms of the category
PrSh_{\mathcal{S}ymp}
are natural transformations. Let us recall that a natural transformation
\eta
from
\mathcal{F}_{1}
to
\mathcal{F}_{2}
is a family of maps
\eta_{X}:\mathcal{F}_{1}(X)\rightarrow\mathcal{F}_{2}(X)
, for all
X\in\mathcal{S}ymp
, such that the following diagram commutes for any
f\in\mathcal{S}ymp(X,Y)

\begin{gathered}\mathcal{F}_{1}(X)\xrightarrow{\eta_{X}}\mathcal{F}_{2}(X)\\
\Big\uparrow f^{*}\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\Big\uparrow f^{*}\\
\mathcal{F}_{1}(Y)\xrightarrow{\eta_{Y}}\mathcal{F}_{2}(Y)\end{gathered}

We say that
\eta
is natural isomorphism if
\eta_{X}
is a bijection for any
X
.

Let us unwind this definition. Let
\mathcal{F}
be a presheaf and
X
be a symplectic manifold. For any open subsets
V\subset U
of
X
we have sets
\mathcal{F}(X)
,
\mathcal{F}(U)
,
\mathcal{F}(V)
. Let
i_{V}:V\hookrightarrow U
,
i_{U}:U\hookrightarrow X
be embeddings. Since
\mathcal{F}
is a functor from the opposite category, we get maps of sets

\begin{gathered}\mathcal{F}(i_{U}):\mathcal{F}(X)\rightarrow\mathcal{F}(U),\;\;\;\mathcal{F}(i_{V}):\mathcal{F}(U)\rightarrow\mathcal{F}(V),\\
\mathcal{F}(i_{U}\circ i_{V})=\mathcal{F}(i_{V})\circ\mathcal{F}(i_{U}).\end{gathered}

This means that we have a presheaf on
X
. As a result we see that

\begin{gathered}\text{ any object of $PrSh_{\mathcal{S}ymp}$ defines a presheaf of sets}\\
\text{on any manifold from $\mathcal{S}ymp$}.\end{gathered}

If
f\in\mathcal{F}(X)
, then we denote

\mathcal{F}(i_{U})(f)=f|_{U}.

A functor
\mathcal{F}
is called representable if there exists
X\in\mathcal{S}ymp
such that for any
Z\in\mathcal{S}ymp
we have

\mathcal{F}(Z)=\mathcal{S}ymp(Z,X).

We denote this functor by
\mathcal{F}_{X}
. For any
f\in\mathcal{S}ymp(Y,X)
and
h\in\mathcal{S}ymp(Y,Z)
we get

\begin{gathered}f_{*}:\mathcal{F}_{Y}(Z)\rightarrow\mathcal{F}_{X}(Z),\;\;\;\;f_{*}:\mathcal{S}ymp(Z,Y)\rightarrow\mathcal{S}ymp(Z,X)\\
h^{*}:\mathcal{F}_{X}(Z)\rightarrow\mathcal{F}_{X}(Y),\;\;\;\;h^{*}:\mathcal{S}ymp(Z,X)\rightarrow\mathcal{S}ymp(Y,X)\\
\end{gathered}

From the Yoneda Lemma we have

PrSh_{\mathcal{S}ymp}(\mathcal{F}_{X},\mathcal{F}_{Y})=\mathcal{S}ymp(X,Y).

So, we identify
X
with the functor
\mathcal{F}_{X}
and see that
\mathcal{S}ymp
is a full subcategory of
PrSh_{\mathcal{S}ymp}
.

Lemma 5.2
.

Manifolds
X,Y\in\mathcal{S}ymp
are isomorphic if and only if
\mathcal{F}_{X}
is naturally isomorphic to
\mathcal{F}_{Y}
, i.e there is a natural bijection between
\mathcal{S}ymp(Z,X)
and
\mathcal{S}ymp(Z,Y)
for any
Z\in\mathcal{S}ymp
.

Proof.

Assume that
f:X\rightarrow Y
is an isomorphism. Then,
f_{*}:\mathcal{S}ymp(Z,X)\rightarrow\mathcal{S}ymp(Z,Y)
provides the required natural bijection.

Assume that there is natural bijection
\eta_{Z}:\mathcal{S}ymp(Z,X)\rightarrow\mathcal{S}ymp(Z,Y)
for any
Z
. If
Z=X
, then we get the bijection

\mathcal{S}ymp(X,X)\xrightarrow{\eta_{X}}\mathcal{S}ymp(X,Y).

If
Z=Y
, then we have the bijection

\mathcal{S}ymp(Y,X)\xrightarrow{\eta_{Y}}\mathcal{S}ymp(Y,Y).

Denote
f=\eta_{X}(id_{X})
and
g=\eta_{Y}^{-1}(id_{Y})
. By definition we have the following commutative diagram

\begin{gathered}\mathcal{S}ymp(X,X)\xrightarrow{\eta_{X}}\mathcal{S}ymp(X,Y)\\
\uparrow f^{*}\qquad\qquad\uparrow f^{*}\\
\mathcal{S}ymp(Y,X)\xrightarrow{\eta_{Y}}\mathcal{S}ymp(Y,Y)\end{gathered}

We see that
\eta_{X}\circ f^{*}=f^{*}\circ\eta_{Y}
. Let us apply this identity to
g
and obtain

\eta_{X}\circ g(f)=f\Leftrightarrow g(f)=\eta_{X}^{-1}\circ f=id_{X}.

We get
f(g)=id_{Y}
by considering similar diagram and replacing
f
by
g
.

∎

The category
PrSh_{\mathcal{S}ymp}
has all limits and colimits. Let us show how to find colimits of presheaves. Assume that we want to find
colim(\mathcal{F}_{\alpha})
, where
\alpha
belongs to some index set. Since
\mathcal{F}_{\alpha}(Z)\in\mathcal{S}et
and all colimits exist in the category of sets, we get that
colim(\mathcal{F}_{\alpha}(Z))
exists for any
Z
. One can show that

colim(\mathcal{F}_{\alpha})(Z)=colim(\mathcal{F}_{\alpha}(Z)).

All limits can be computed in the same way.

We see that
PrSh_{\mathcal{S}ymp}
is some kind of generalization of the category
\mathcal{S}ymp
. Unfortunately, this category is too rich and we need to get rid of some objects. There is a standard argument showing that
PrSh_{\mathcal{S}ymp}
destroys geometry. Assume that
U,V\subset X
are open subsets and
U\cup V=X
. Let
i_{U}
,
i_{V}
be corresponding embeddings of
U\cap V
into
U
and
V
, respectively. Then, the fact that
U\cup V=X
in terms of category theory means that
X
is the colimit of the following diagram

\begin{gathered}U\cap V\longrightarrow U\\
\quad\big\downarrow\qquad\quad\big\downarrow\\
\quad V\;\longrightarrow\;X\end{gathered}

(5.1)

In other words,
U\sqcup V/(i_{U}(x)\sim i_{V}(x))=X
, where
\sqcup
stands for the disjoint union. In
PrSh_{\mathcal{S}ymp}
we have the corresponding representable functors
\mathcal{F}_{X}
,
\mathcal{F}_{U}
,
\mathcal{F}_{V}
,
\mathcal{F}_{U\cap V}
. Since we replace our objects by functors, it is natural to expect that
\mathcal{F}_{X}
is the colimit of the following diagram

\begin{gathered}\quad\quad\quad\mathcal{F}_{U\cap V}\longrightarrow\mathcal{F}_{U}\\
\big\downarrow\\
\mathcal{F}_{V}\end{gathered}

(5.2)

i.e. is ‘union’ of functors
\mathcal{F}_{U}
,
\mathcal{F}_{V}
. Unfortunately, the colimit
\widetilde{\mathcal{F}}
of diagram
(\ref{desiredcolimit})
evaluated at a manifold
Y
is given by

\widetilde{\mathcal{F}}(Y)=\mathcal{F}_{U}(Y)\cup\mathcal{F}_{V}(Y),

where
\cup
is union of sets (not disjoint). We see that
\widetilde{\mathcal{F}}(Y)
consists of maps from
Y
to
U
and from
Y
to
V
. Note that
\mathcal{F}_{X}(Y)
consists of maps from
Y
to
X
and the image of
Y
is not supposed to be entirely in
U
or
V
. So, we do not want
\widetilde{\mathcal{F}}
to be the colimit.

Since symplectic manifolds are constructed by gluing open symplectic manifolds, it is very important to avoid the mentioned problem. So, we lose our geometry when we consider presheaves. We want the representable functor
\mathcal{F}_{X}
to be obtained by ‘gluing’ functors
\mathcal{F}_{U}
,
\mathcal{F}_{V}
. The problem mentioned above says that presheaves do not preserve existing colimits.

5.3
Category of sheaves on
\mathcal{S}ymp

As we already discussed, any element of
PrSh_{\mathcal{S}ymp}
defines a presheaf of sets on any
X\in\mathcal{S}ymp
. We need to consider a category of sheaves
Sh_{\mathcal{S}ymp}
on
\mathcal{S}ymp
. Sheaves allow to add new colimits taking into account already existing ones.

Definition 5.3
.

A presheaf
\mathcal{F}\in PrSh_{\mathcal{S}ymp}
is a sheaf if
\mathcal{F}
defines a sheaf of sets on any
X\in\mathcal{S}ymp
. Let us give more details. Let
\cup_{\alpha}U_{\alpha}=X
be a covering of
X
by open subsets. A presheaf
\mathcal{F}\in PrSh_{\mathcal{S}ymp}
is a sheaf if and only if the following is satisfied for any
X
and any covering:

1. If
f,g\in\mathcal{F}(X)
satisfy
f|_{U_{\alpha}}=g|_{U_{\alpha}}
for any
\alpha
, then
f=g
.

2. If
f_{\alpha}\in\mathcal{F}(U_{\alpha})
satisfy
f_{\alpha}|_{U_{\alpha}\cap U_{\beta}}=f_{\beta}|_{U_{\alpha}\cap U_{\beta}}
for any
\alpha,\beta
, then there exists
f\in\mathcal{F}(X)
such that
f|_{U_{\alpha}}=f_{\alpha}
.

3.
\mathcal{F}(\emptyset)=pt
, where
\emptyset
is the empty set and
pt
is one-element set.

We denote sheaves on
\mathcal{S}ymp
by
Sh_{\mathcal{S}ymp}
.

Let us give an equivalent definition

Definition 5.4
.

A presheaf
\mathcal{F}\in PrSh_{\mathcal{S}ymp}
is a sheaf if for any
X
and any open covering
\cup_{\alpha}U_{\alpha}=X
by open sets
the following is an equalizer diagram

\mathcal{F}(X)\rightarrow\prod\limits_{\alpha}\mathcal{F}(U_{\alpha})\rightrightarrows\prod\limits_{\beta,\gamma}\mathcal{F}(U_{\beta}\cap U_{\gamma}),

where
\prod
is the product of sets.

Remark 5.5
.

Any representable presheaf
\mathcal{F}_{X}
is a sheaf, i.e.
\mathcal{F}_{X}\in Sh_{\mathcal{S}ymp}
.

Let us show that
\mathcal{F}_{X}
is the colimit of diagram
(\ref{desiredcolimit})
in the category
Sh_{\mathcal{S}ymp}
. Let
\mathcal{F}\in Sh_{\mathcal{S}ymp}
be an arbitrary sheaf. Since
X
is the colimit of diagram
(\ref{unionofsets})
, we get that (by definition of sheaves)
\mathcal{F}_{U\cap V}
is colimit of the following diagram:

\begin{gathered}\mathcal{F}(U\cap V)\longleftarrow\mathcal{F}(U)\\
\big\uparrow\quad\quad\;\;\quad\big\uparrow\\
\mathcal{F}(V)\longleftarrow\mathcal{F}(X)\end{gathered}

From Yoneda lemma we obtain that
\mathcal{F}(X)=Sh_{\mathcal{S}ymp}(\mathcal{F}_{X},\mathcal{F})
and the diagram above is equivalent to

\begin{gathered}Sh_{\mathcal{S}ymp}(\mathcal{F}_{U\cap V},\mathcal{F})\longleftarrow Sh_{\mathcal{S}ymp}(\mathcal{F}_{U},\mathcal{F})\\
\big\uparrow\qquad\qquad\qquad\big\uparrow\\
Sh_{\mathcal{S}ymp}(\mathcal{F}_{V},\mathcal{F})\longleftarrow Sh_{\mathcal{S}ymp}(\mathcal{F}_{X},\mathcal{F})\end{gathered}

Since
\mathcal{F}
is arbitrary, we get that
\mathcal{F}_{X}
is the colimit of diagram
(\ref{desiredcolimit})
in the category
Sh_{\mathcal{S}ymp}
. In the same way, we can prove similar fact about finite number of open subsets.

So, without loss of any information, we make the replacement (let us recall that
\mathcal{S}ymp
stands for either
\mathcal{S}\mathcal{S}ymp
, or
\mathcal{J}\mathcal{S}ymp
)

X\leftarrow\mathrel{\mkern-14.0mu}\xrightarrow{replace\;by\;sheaf}\mathcal{S}ymp(-,X)=\mathcal{F}_{X}\in Sh_{\mathcal{S}ymp}.

We have the following theorem:

Theorem 5.6
.

(see
[
9
]
) There exists a functor
Sheaf

Sheaf:\;\;PrSh_{\mathcal{S}ymp}\rightarrow Sh_{\mathcal{S}ymp}

called sheafification functor such that

1.
\mathcal{F}\rightarrow Sh(\mathcal{F})
is isomorphism if and only if
\mathcal{F}
is a sheaf

2. To compute a colimit in
Sh_{\mathcal{S}ymp}
we need first to compute it in
PrSh_{\mathcal{S}ymp}
and then apply the sheafification functor
Sheaf
.

Intersection and union of submanifolds.

\begin{gathered}\mathcal{F}_{W_{1}\cap W_{2}}\longrightarrow\mathcal{F}_{W_{1}}\\

into

as a colimit of the following diagram in the category of

\begin{gathered}\mathcal{F}_{W_{1}\cap W_{2}}\longrightarrow\mathcal{F}_{W_{1}}\\
\big\downarrow\quad\quad\;\;\quad\big\downarrow\\
\mathcal{F}_{W_{2}}\longrightarrow\mathcal{F}_{W_{1}\cup W_{2}}\end{gathered}

Quotient.

be a symplectic submanifold (pseudoholomorphic, in case of

6.1
Topological symmetric products and sheaves

In this section we discuss how to define topological symmetric products in terms of sheaves. This section should be considered as a philosophical motivation and we skip some details. We use similar ideas to define symmetric products in other categories.

Let
X
be some nice topological space. Let
X^{n}
be the
n
th power of
X
and let
\Sigma_{n}
be the symmetric group acting on
X^{n}
by permuting the factors. We define the symmetric product as in Section
3.1

SP^{n}(X)=X^{n}/\Sigma_{n}.

Let
Y
be a nice topological space. Consider the set of continuous maps
C^{0}(Y,X^{n})
. Any map
f\in C^{0}(Y,X^{n})
is defined by
n
ordered set of continuous maps
(f_{1},\ldots,f_{n})
, where
f_{i}:Y\rightarrow X
. The symmetric group
\Sigma_{n}
acts on
X^{n}
and as a result acts on
C^{0}(Y,X^{n})
. We take a quotient
C^{0}(Y,X^{n})/\Sigma_{n}
and note that elements of the quotient are unordered set of maps. Two elements
\{f_{1},\ldots,f_{n}\},\{g_{1},\ldots,g_{n}\}\in C^{0}(Y,X^{n})/\Sigma_{n}
are equal if

\{f_{1}(y),\ldots,f_{n}(y)\}=\{g_{1}(y),\ldots,g_{n}(y)\}\;\;\;\text{for any $y\in Y$},

where the equality is equality of unordered sets.

Example 6.1
.

Let
\mathbb{R}
be the real line. Consider maps from
\mathbb{R}
to
\mathbb{R}

f_{1}(x)=x,\;\;\;f_{2}(x)=-x,\;\;\;g_{1}(x)=|x|,\;\;\;g_{2}(x)=-|x|.

We see that
\{f_{1},f_{2}\}=\{g_{1},g_{2}\}\in C^{0}(\mathbb{R},\mathbb{R}^{2})/\Sigma_{2}
.

Any element
\{f_{1},\ldots,f_{n}\}\in C^{0}(Y,X^{n})/\Sigma_{n}
defines a subset
F\subset Y\times X
in the following way

F=\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}\bigcup_{y\in Y}(y,f_{i}(y).

Note that if
f_{1}(y),\ldots,f_{n}(y)
are distinct for any
y
, then the projection
F\rightarrow Y
is trivial
n
-covering.

In terms of Section
5.2
we have a presheaf on the category of topological spaces

Y\rightarrow C^{0}(Y,X^{n})/\Sigma_{n}.

In particular, we get a presheaf on each
Y
by assigning

U\rightarrow C^{0}(U,X^{n})/\Sigma_{n}\quad\text{to each open $U\subset Y$}.

Let us show that
C^{0}(U,X^{n})/\Sigma_{n}
is not a sheaf. Let
U_{1},U_{2}\subset Y
be open subsets such that
U_{1}\cup U_{2}=Y
. Consider a subset
F\subset Y\times X
such that the projection
\pi:F\rightarrow Y
is some nontrivial
n
-covering. Assume that the covering is trivial over
U_{1}
and
U_{2}
. Then, the sections of these coverings provide the following elements

\{f_{1},\ldots,f_{n}\}\in C^{0}(U_{1},X^{n})/\Sigma_{n},\quad\{g_{1},\ldots,g_{n}\}\in C^{0}(U_{2},X^{n})/\Sigma_{n}.

Moreover,
\{f_{1},\ldots,f_{n}\}=\{g_{1},\ldots,g_{n}\}
on

. Since
F\rightarrow Y
is not trivial, we see that
F
is not defined by any
h\in C^{0}(Y,X^{n})/\Sigma_{n}
. This shows that we can not glue maps
\{f_{1},\ldots,f_{n}\}
and
\{g_{1},\ldots,g_{n}\}
and the presheaf
C^{0}(\cdot,X^{n})/\Sigma_{n}
is not a sheaf.

We sheafify

and get a sheaf

TCor_{n}(Y,X)=Sheaf(C^{0}(\cdot,X^{n})/\Sigma_{n})(Y),

where
T
stands for topological and

for correspondence. We denote elements of

by letters

We also abuse notation and denote subsets of
Y\times X
also by symbols
F,G,\ldots
We will see below that these objects are related.

Example 6.2
.

Elements of

are standard continuous maps from

.

Example 6.3
.

We see that

Any element

Proof.

For any point

there exists a small neighborhood

and unordered set of maps

∎

Definition 6.5

Let

Example 7.5

Let us prove the following lemma:

Lemma 7.6

Consider

Lemma 8.2

\begin{gathered}(G+\;\text{isotropic correspondence})\circ F=G\circ F+\;\text{isotropic correspondence}\\

(F\vee G)|_{U\times V}\;\;+\;\;\text{isotropic correspondence}\Rightarrow\\
(F+H)\vee G=F\vee G\;+\;\text{isotropic correspondence $H\circ pr_{Y}$}.\end{gathered}

We see that

(recall that

Definition 9.1
.

We say that
F,G\in C(Y,X)
are

-homotopic if there exists

-homotopy equivalent maps by

Lemma 9.2
.

Assume that there exists

. Then,
M
-homotopy relation defines equivalence relation on

Remark 9.3
.

If we replace

by
\mathcal{S}ymp(Y,X)
, then the homotopy relation does not define equivalence relation. For example, if

, then any map

is not homotopy equivalent to itself. We even can not replace

by

(transitivity is not satisfied).

Proof.

Let
\gamma\in\mathcal{S}ymp(M,M)
be a map such that

\gamma(p_{0})=p_{1},\quad\gamma(p_{1})=p_{0}.

Note that
\gamma
exists for any connected
M
and we assumed that
M
is connected. Denote by
id_{Y}\times\gamma
the obvious map from
Y\times M
to
Y\times M
.

Reflexivity
. We need to show that
F\simeq F
, where
F\in SC(Y,X)
. Lemma
8.7
says that
(F\vee I)\circ i_{0}=F
as elements of
SCor(Y,X)
. In the same way,
(F\vee I)\circ i_{1}=F
. Since
F\vee I\in SCor(Y\times M,X)
, we get
F\vee I
is the required homotopy.

Symmetry.
We need to show that
F\simeq G\Rightarrow G\simeq F
. Assume that a homotopy between
F
,
G
is provided by
H
and
H\circ i_{0}=F
,
H\circ i_{1}=G
. Then,
H\circ(id_{Y}\times\gamma)
provides a homotopy between
G
and
F
. Indeed,

\begin{gathered}(H\circ(id_{Y}\times\gamma))\circ i_{0}(Y)=(H\circ(id_{Y}\times\gamma))(Y\times p_{0})=H|_{Y\times p_{1}}=G,\\
(H\circ(id_{Y}\times\gamma))\circ i_{1}(Y)=(H\circ(id_{Y}\times\gamma))(Y\times p_{1})=H|_{Y\times p_{0}}=F.\end{gathered}

Transitivity.
Assume that
F_{1}\simeq F_{2}
and
F_{2}\simeq F_{3}
, where

. We need to show that
F_{1}\simeq F_{3}
. We have

\begin{gathered}H_{1}\in SC(Y\times M,X),\;\;\;H_{2}\in SC(Y\times M,X),\\
H_{1}\circ i_{0}=F_{1},\;\;H_{1}\circ i_{1}=F_{2},\;\;H_{2}\circ i_{0}=F_{2},\;\;H_{2}\circ i_{1}=F_{3}.\end{gathered}

Consider

H=H_{1}-H_{2}\circ(id_{Y}\times\gamma)+F_{3}\vee I\in SC(Y\times M,X).

We obtain (see Lemma
8.7

\begin{gathered}H\circ i_{0}=F_{1}-F_{3}+F_{3}=F_{1},\\

So, transitivity is proved.

Let us prove the following theorem

Theorem 9.4
.

Let
\varphi_{t}\in\mathcal{S}\mathcal{S}ymp(Y,X)
be a symplectic isotopy. If there exists
I\in SCor(M,X)
, then
\varphi_{0}
is
M
-homotopic to
\varphi_{1}
.

Proof.

Let
\ell:M\rightarrow[0,1]
be a function that is equal to
0
in a neighborhood of
p_{0}
and is equal to
1
in a neighborhood of
p_{1}
. Let
U\subset M
be a small open subsets such that
I|_{U}
defined by an unordered set of maps from
M
to
X
. Let us define the following unordered set of maps on

H|_{Y\times U}=\{\varphi_{\ell(q)}(y),\;I|_{U}\},\quad\text{where $y\in Y$ and $q\in U$}.

Since
\varphi_{t}
is symplectomorphism, we have
\varphi_{t}^{*}\omega_{X}=\omega_{Y}
for any
t
. By definition, maps
I|_{U}
pull back
\omega_{X}
to
\omega_{M}
. This shows that the maps defining
H|_{Y\times U}
pull back
\omega_{X}
to
\omega_{Y}\oplus\omega_{M}
and

H|_{X\times U}\in SCor(Y\times U,X).

Let
U_{\alpha}
be a covering of
M
by small open subsets. Since

, we can glue correspondences
H|_{Y\times U_{\alpha}}
and get a correspondence

H\in SCor(Y\times M,X).

We see that
H\circ i_{0}\in SCor(Y,X)
and

H|_{Y\times p_{0}}=\{\varphi_{\ell(p_{0})}(x),\;I|_{p_{0}}\},\quad\text{where $I|_{p_{0}}$ is a set of maps $p_{0}\rightarrow X$}.

This means that

is an isotropic correspondence. Since

, we get

\begin{gathered}H|_{Y\times p_{0}}=\varphi_{0}+I|_{p_{0}}=\varphi_{0}\;\;+\;\;\text{isotropic correspondence}=\\
\varphi_{0}\;\;\text{as elements of $SCor(Y,X)$}.\end{gathered}

In the same way

\begin{gathered}H|_{Y\times p_{1}}=\varphi_{1}+I|_{p_{1}}=\varphi_{1}\;\;+\;\;\text{isotropic correspondence}=\\

-homotopy between

and
\varphi_{1}
.

∎

.

and

F_{0},F_{1}

F_{0}^{*},F_{1}^{*}:C_{n}^{M}(Z,X)\rightarrow C_{n}^{M}(Y,X),\;\;\;H^{*}:C_{n}^{M}(Z,X)\rightarrow C_{n}^{M}(Y\times M,X).

Any element
\sigma\in C_{n}^{M}(Y\times M,X)
can be considered as an element of
C_{n+1}^{M}(Y,X)
. So, composing this with
H^{*}
we get

K:C_{n}^{M}(Z,X)\xrightarrow{H^{*}}C_{n}^{M}(Y\times M,\;X)\rightarrow C_{n+1}^{M}(Y,X).

Direct computation shows that

dK=F_{0}^{*}-F_{1}^{*}\pm Kd.

This means that
F_{0}^{*},F_{1}^{*}
are chain homotopic. If
Z=Y\times M
,
F_{0}=i_{0}
,
F_{1}=i_{1}
,
H=id_{Y}\times id_{M}
, then we get that
i_{0}^{*}=i_{1}^{*}
.

Let us prove the second part. Denote by
H\in SC(X\times M,Z)
the homotopy between
G_{1},G_{2}
. We have the chain maps

(G_{0})_{*},(G_{1})_{*}:C_{n}^{M}(Y,X)\rightarrow C_{n}^{M}(Y,Z),\;\;\;H_{*}:C_{n}^{M}(Y,X\times M)\rightarrow C_{n}^{M}(Y,Z).

Let us recall that multiplying by the diagonal
id_{M}\subset M\times M
we can transfer any element of
C(Y,X)
into
C(Y\times M,X\times M)
. We have

C_{n}^{M}(Y,X)=C(Y\times M^{n},\;X)\xrightarrow{\times id_{M}}C(Y\times M^{n}\times M,\;X\times M)=C_{n+1}^{M}(Y,X\times M).

Composing with
H_{*}
we get

K:C_{n}^{M}(Y,X)\xrightarrow{\times id_{M}}C_{n+1}^{M}(Y,X\times M)\xrightarrow{H_{*}}C_{n+1}(Y,Z).

We see that

\begin{gathered}d^{n+1,0}(H_{*}\circ(\sigma\times id_{M}))=H_{*}\circ(\sigma\times p_{0})=(G_{0})_{*}\circ\sigma\\
d^{n+1,1}(H_{*}\circ(\sigma\times id_{M}))=H_{*}\circ(\sigma\times p_{1})=(G_{1})_{*}\circ\sigma.\end{gathered}

This implies that

dK=(G_{0})_{*}-(G_{1})_{*}\pm Kd.

So,
(G_{0})_{*},(G_{1})_{*}
are chain homotopic. The last statement can be proved by assuming that
Z=X\times M
,
G_{0}=j_{0}
,
G_{1}=j_{1}
,
H=id_{X}\times id_{M}
.

∎

Since

Triangulated persistence category of symplectic manifolds

Triangulated Persistence Categories are defined by Biran, Cornea, and Zhang in
[
1
]
,
[
2
]
. We do not give definitions here and the reader can find all the details in the mentioned papers.

Remark 12.1
.

In a similar way triangulated persistence category of almost complex manifolds can be constructed. We consider only simplectic manifolds to simplify the paper.

12.1
Topological motivation

Let
Y
,
Z
,
X
be some nice topological spaces. We defined abelian group
K(Y,X)
by formula
(\ref{requiredfor})
. Recall that (discussed at the beginning of Section
8.1
) maps

Z\rightarrow SP^{k}(Y),\;\;\;Y\rightarrow SP^{n}(X)

can be composed and we obtain a map
Z\rightarrow SP^{nk}(X)
. Extending this composition linearly to linear combinations of such maps we get bilinear operation

K(Y,X)\times K(Z,Y)\xrightarrow{\circ}K(Z,X).

Let
\mathcal{T}op
be the category of topological spaces. Consider a category
\mathcal{T}
, where

Ob(\mathcal{T})=Ob(\mathcal{T}op),\;\;\;Mor_{\mathcal{T}}(Y,X)=K(Y,X).

Let
Y\sqcup Z
be the disjoint union. Then, the standard inclusions
i_{Y}:Y\hookrightarrow Y\sqcup Z
,
i_{Z}:Z\hookrightarrow Y\sqcup Z
provides a map

K(Y\sqcup Z,X)\xrightarrow{(\circ i_{Y},\;\circ i_{X})}K(Y,X)\oplus K(Z,X).

Easy to see that this map is isomorphism of groups. This implies that
Y\sqcup Z
is the coproduct of
Y
and
Z
in the category
\mathcal{T}
. Since the set of morphisms is abelian and compositions are bilinear, we get that
Y\sqcup Z
is also a product. Note that the empty set is the zero object of
\mathcal{T}
. As a result, we see that all finite products and coproducts exist and get the following lemma.

Lemma 12.2
.

The category
\mathcal{T}
is additive.

Consider bounded below cochain complexes
Com^{-}(\mathcal{T})
and its homotopy category
\mathcal{K}(\mathcal{T})
. It is known that homotopy category of chain complexes of any additive category is triangulated (see
[
8
, Th. 11.2.6]
).

We started with topological spaces and constructed the triangulated category. The goal of this section to develop similar idea for symplectic manifolds.

12.2
Triamgulated category of symplectic manifolds

Recall that in Section
7.2
we defined bounded symplectic correspondences
SC_{b}(Y,X)
. Let
\widetilde{\mathcal{B}}
be a category, where

\begin{gathered}\text{$Ob(\widetilde{\mathcal{B}})=\;$ \emph{connected} symplectic manifolds},\\
Mor_{\widetilde{\mathcal{B}}}(Y,X)=SC_{b}(Y,X).\end{gathered}

Let
Z_{1},\ldots,Z_{n},Z_{1}^{\prime},\ldots,Z_{k}^{\prime},Z_{1}^{\prime\prime},\ldots,Z_{m}^{\prime\prime}
be connected symplectic manifolds. Define
n\times k
and
k\times m
matrices
F
,
G
with entries
(F)_{i,j}\in SC_{b}(Z_{i},Z_{j}^{\prime})
and
(G)_{i,j}\in SC_{b}(Z_{i}^{\prime},Z_{j}^{\prime\prime})
, respectively. Then, we define their composition
G\circ F
as an
n\times m
matrix with following entries

(G\circ F)_{i,j}=\sum\limits_{\ell=1}^{k}G_{\ell,j}\circ F_{i,\ell}\in SC_{b}(Z_{i},Z_{j}^{\prime\prime}).

(12.1)

where
\circ
is the defined composition of correspondences (see Section
8.1
). In other words, we multiply these matrices.

Consider matrices
F_{1},F_{2}
and
G_{1},G_{2}
with entries from
SC_{b}(Z_{i},Z_{j}^{\prime})
and
SC_{b}(Z_{i}^{\prime},Z_{j}^{\prime\prime})
, respectively. We define composition between linear combinations of such matrices linearly, i.e.

(\lambda_{1}G_{1}+\lambda_{2}G_{2})\circ(\mu_{1}F_{1}+\mu_{2}F_{2})=\sum\limits_{i,j=1}^{2}\lambda_{i}\mu_{j}F_{i}\circ G_{j}.

If
dim(Y)\neq dim(X)
, then the disjoint union
Y\sqcup X
is not a manifold, but we want to have a category with all disjoint unions. Let us assume that the empty set is a symplectic manifod. Define a new category
\mathcal{A}
such that

\begin{gathered}\text{$Ob(\mathcal{B})=$ \lx@text@lbrace all disjoint unions of connected symplectic manifolds $\sqcup_{i=1}^{n}Z_{i}$, $\;n$ is arbitrary\lx@text@rbrace }\\
Mor_{\mathcal{B}}\big(\sqcup_{i=1}^{n}Z_{i},\;\sqcup_{j=1}^{k}Z_{j}^{\prime}\big)=\;\{\text{finite formal finite linear combinations of the defined}\\
\text{ $n\times k$ matrices with entries from $SC_{b}(Z_{i},Z_{j}^{\prime})$}\},\\
Mor_{\mathcal{B}}(\sqcup_{i=1}^{n}Z_{i},\;\emptyset)=Mor_{\mathcal{B}}(\emptyset,\;\sqcup_{i=1}^{n}Z_{i})=0,\\
\text{Composition is defined by formula $\ref{prodmat}$}.\end{gathered}

In particular, for connected symplectic
Y
,
X
we have

Mor_{\mathcal{B}}(Y,X)=SC_{b}(Y,X).

Lemma 12.3
.

The category
\mathcal{B}
is additive, where
Y\oplus X
is the disjoint union
Y\sqcup X
and the empty set is the zero object. The category
\widetilde{\mathcal{B}}
is full subcategory of
\mathcal{B}
.

Proof.

Let
Z_{1},Z_{2}
be symplectic manifolds. We have an
1\times 2
matrices
\delta_{k}\in Mor_{\mathcal{A}}(Z_{k},Z_{1}\sqcup Z_{2})
such that

\delta_{1}=(id_{Z_{1}},0),\;\;\;\delta_{2}=(0,id_{Z_{2}}).

We get the following map

Mor_{\mathcal{B}}(Z_{1}\sqcup Z_{2},\sqcup_{j=1}^{m}Z_{j}^{\prime})\xrightarrow{(\circ\delta_{1},\circ\delta_{2})}\bigoplus_{i=1}^{2}Mor_{\mathcal{B}}(Z_{i},\sqcup_{j=1}^{m}Z_{j}^{\prime}).

Definitions show that the map above is isomorphism (we write matrices as direct sums of their columns). This implies that
Z_{1}\sqcup Z_{2}
is coproduct of
Z_{1}
and
Z_{2}
. In the same way we can show that coproduct of arbitrary elements exists.

Since the set of morphisms are abelian groups and compositions are bilinear, we obtain that all coproducts are also products. We see that the empty set is the zero object. As a result, all finite limits and colimits exist and
\mathcal{B}
is additive.

The last part of the lemma follows from definitions.

∎

Consider a category
Com^{-}(\mathcal{B})
of bounded below cochain complexes. Let us recall that objects of this category are cochain complexes
(C_{\bullet},d_{C})

0\rightarrow C_{-k}\xrightarrow{d^{-k}_{C}}\ldots\rightarrow C_{n-1}\xrightarrow{d^{n-1}_{C}}C_{n}\xrightarrow{d^{n}_{C}}\ldots,\;\;\;\;C_{i}\in\mathcal{B}.

Morphisms between
(C_{\bullet},d_{C})
and
(K_{\bullet},d_{K})
are chain maps, i.e collection of morphisms
F_{n}\in Mor_{\mathcal{B}}(C_{n},K_{n})
such that
F_{n}\circ d_{C}^{n-1}=d_{K}^{n-1}\circ F_{n-1}
for any
n
. A chain map
F
is called isomorphism if each
F_{n}
is isomorphism. We say that two chain maps
F,G:(C_{\bullet},d_{C})\rightarrow(K_{\bullet},d_{K})
are homotopic if there exist morphisms
H_{n}\in Mor_{\mathcal{B}}(C_{n},K_{n-1})
such that
F_{n}-G_{n}=d_{K}^{n-1}H_{n}\pm H_{n+1}d^{n}_{C}
for any
n
.

Example 12.4
.

Let
M=(M,p)
be a symplectic manifold with a marked point and
M^{n}
be its
n
th power. We have embeddings
i_{k}:M^{n}\rightarrow M^{k}\times p\times M^{n-k}\subset M^{n+1}
and define
d^{n}=\sum\limits_{k=0}^{n}(-1)^{k}i_{k}\in SC_{b}(M^{n},M^{n+1})
. We see that
d^{n+1}d^{n}=0
and define a chain complex

0\rightarrow Y,\xrightarrow{d^{0}}Y\times M\xrightarrow{id_{Y}\times d^{1}}\ldots\xrightarrow{id_{Y}\times d^{n-1}}Y\times M^{n}\xrightarrow{id_{Y}\times d^{n}}\ldots

Here
d^{0}
maps
Y
to
Y\times p\subset Y\times M
. We denote this complex by
Y_{\bullet}(M,p)
. Consider a complex
X_{\bullet}(M,p)
and note that any
F\in SC_{b}(Y,X)
defines a chain map from
Y_{\bullet}(M,p)\rightarrow X_{\bullet}(M,p)
with
F_{n}=F\times id_{M^{n}}
.

Example 12.5
.

Let
M=(M,p_{0},p_{1})
be a symplectic manifold with two marked points. We have embeddings
d_{i,\varepsilon}:M^{i}\times p_{\varepsilon}\times M^{n-i}\rightarrow M^{n+1}
, where
\varepsilon=0,1
. Define
d_{n+1}=\sum\limits_{\varepsilon=0}^{1}\sum\limits_{i=0}^{n}(-1)^{\varepsilon+i}d_{i,\varepsilon}
and note that
d_{n+1}\circ d_{n}=0
. We get the following chain complex

0\rightarrow Y\xrightarrow{d^{0}}Y\times M\xrightarrow{id_{Y}\times d^{1}}\ldots\xrightarrow{id_{Y}\times d^{n-1}}Y\times M^{n}\xrightarrow{id_{Y}\times d^{n}}\ldots

and denote this complex by
Y_{\bullet}(M,p_{0},p_{1})
. Consider another complex
X_{\bullet}(M,p_{0},p_{1})
. As in the previous example, any
F\in SC_{b}(Y,X)
defines a chain map from
Y_{\bullet}(M,p_{0},p_{1})\rightarrow X_{\bullet}(M,p_{0},p_{1})
with
F_{n}=F\times id_{M^{n}}
.

Consider a category
\mathcal{K}(\mathcal{B})
, where

\begin{gathered}Ob(\mathcal{K}(\mathcal{B}))=Ob(Com^{-}(\mathcal{B})),\\
Mor_{\mathcal{K}(\mathcal{B})}(C_{\bullet},K_{\bullet})=\{\text{chain maps}\}/\simeq,\end{gathered}

where
F\simeq G
if they are chain homotopic.

Lemma 12.6
.

The category
\mathcal{K}(\mathcal{B})
is triangulated.

Proof.

It is known that the homotopy category of chain complexes of any additive category is triangulated (see
[
8
, Th. 11.2.6]
).
∎

12.3
Triangulated persistence category of symplectic manifolds

In the previous section we constructed the category
\mathcal{B}
and the triangulated category
\mathcal{K}(\mathcal{B})
. The goal of this section is to construct a refinement of these categories and get a triangulated persistene category
\mathcal{C}
.

Let us consider pairs
(Z,r)
, where
Z
is
connected
symplected manifolds and
r\in\mathbb{R}
. Also, consider formal elements

(Z_{1},r_{1})\oplus\ldots\oplus(Z_{n},r_{n}),

where
Z_{1},\ldots,Z_{n}
are connected symplectic manifolds. We get a new category
\mathcal{A}
such that

\begin{gathered}\text{$Ob(\mathcal{A})=$ \lx@text@lbrace elements of the form $(Z_{1},r_{1})\oplus\ldots\oplus(Z_{n},r_{n})$, where $n$ is arbitrary\lx@text@rbrace, }\\
Mor_{\mathcal{A}}\big(\oplus_{i=1}^{n}(Z_{i},r_{i}),\;\oplus_{j=1}^{k}(Z_{j}^{\prime},r_{j}^{\prime})\big)=Mor_{\mathcal{B}}\big(\sqcup_{i=1}^{n}Z_{i},\;\sqcup_{j=1}^{k}Z_{j}^{\prime}\big),\\
Mor_{\mathcal{A}}(\oplus_{i=1}^{n}(Z_{i},r_{i}),\;\emptyset)=Mor_{\mathcal{A}}(\emptyset,\;\oplus_{i=1}^{n}(Z_{i},r_{i}))=0.\end{gathered}

The set of morphisms are independent of constants in the pairs
(Z_{i},r_{i})
. The reason to introduce these pairs is explained below, where we discuss a filtration.

Lemma 12.7
.

The category
\mathcal{A}
is naturally equivalent to
\mathcal{B}
. The category
\mathcal{A}
is additive, where the addition of
(Y,r)
and
(X,s)
is
(Y,r)\oplus(X,s)
. The empty set is the zero object.

Proof.

There is a functor
I:\mathcal{B}\rightarrow\mathcal{A}

I(\sqcup_{i=1}^{n}Z_{i})=\oplus_{i=1}^{n}(Z,0),\;\;\;I(F)=F,

where
F
is a morpism. By definition, this functor is full and faithful. Also, any element
\oplus_{i=1}^{n}(Z_{i},r)
is isomorphic to
\oplus_{i=1}^{n}(Z_{i},0)=I(\sqcup_{i=1}^{n}Z_{i})
. This implies that
\mathcal{B}
is naturally equivalent to
\mathcal{A}
.

The rest of the lemma mimics the proof of Lemma
12.3
where we need to replace
\sqcup_{i=1}^{n}Z_{i}
by
\oplus_{i=1}^{n}(Z_{i},r_{i})
.
∎

We discussed (see formula
(\ref{filtration}))
that bounded correspondences
SC_{b}(Y,Z)
are filtered. We define the filtration on the set of morphisms of
\mathcal{A}
in the following way:

\begin{gathered}Mor_{\mathcal{A}}((Y,r),(X,s);\;k)=SC_{b}((Y,r),(X,s);\;k)=SC_{b}(Y,X;\;k+r-s),\\
SC_{b}\big(\oplus_{i=1}^{n}(Z_{i},r_{i}),\;\oplus_{j=1}^{m}(Z_{j}^{\prime},r_{j}^{\prime});\;k\big)=\bigoplus_{i,j}SC_{b}((Z_{i},r_{i}),(Z_{j}^{\prime},r_{j}^{\prime});\;k)=\\
=\bigoplus_{i,j}SC_{b}(Z_{i},Z_{j}^{\prime};\;k+r_{i}-r_{j}^{\prime}).\end{gathered}

By definition of the composition of correspondences (see Section
8.1
), we have

\begin{gathered}SC_{b}((Z^{\prime},r^{\prime}),(Z^{\prime\prime},r^{\prime\prime});\;k)\times SC_{b}((Z,r),(Z^{\prime},r^{\prime});\;\ell)=\\
SC_{b}(Z^{\prime},Z^{\prime\prime};\;k+r^{\prime}-r^{\prime\prime})\times SC_{b}(Z,Z^{\prime};\;\ell+r-r^{\prime})\xrightarrow{\circ}\\
SC_{b}(Z,Z^{\prime\prime};\;k+\ell+r-r^{\prime\prime})=SC_{b}((Z,r),(Z^{\prime\prime},r^{\prime\prime});\;k+\ell).\end{gathered}

There is a subcategory
\mathcal{A}_{0}\subset\mathcal{A}
, where

\begin{gathered}Ob(\mathcal{A}_{0})=Ob(\mathcal{A}),\\
Mor_{\mathcal{A}_{0}}(\oplus_{i=1}^{n}(Z_{i},r_{i}),\;\oplus_{j=1}^{m}(Z_{j}^{\prime},r_{j}^{\prime}))=SC_{b}(\oplus_{i=1}^{n}(Z_{i},r_{i}),\;\oplus_{j=1}^{m}(Z_{j}^{\prime},r_{j}^{\prime});\;0).\end{gathered}

Lemma 12.8
.

The category
\mathcal{A}_{0}
is additive.

Proof.

The proof mimics the proof of Lemma
12.3
.
∎

There are endofunctors
\Sigma^{s}:\mathcal{A}\rightarrow\mathcal{A}
for any
s\in\mathbb{R}
defined in the following way:

\Sigma^{s}(\oplus_{i=1}^{n}(Y_{i},r_{i}))=\oplus_{i=1}^{n}(Y_{i},r_{i}+s),\;\;\;\text{$\Sigma^{s}$ is identity on morphisms}.

We see that
\Sigma^{r}\circ\Sigma^{s}=\Sigma^{r+s}
and
\Sigma^{0}=id_{\mathcal{A}}
.

Example 12.9
.

By definition,

SC_{b}((Y,r),(Y,s);\;s-r)=SC_{b}(Y,Y;\;0).

We denote the morphism in
SC_{b}((Y,r),(Y,s);\;s-r)
that corresponds to the identity map of
Y
by
id_{Y}^{r,s}
. Note that

id_{Y}^{r^{\prime},s}\circ id_{Y}^{r,r^{\prime}}=id_{Y}^{r,s}.

Consider the category of bounded below cochain complexes
Com^{-}(\mathcal{A})
and define

Hom^{r}(C_{\bullet},K_{\bullet})=\{\text{chain maps with components $F_{n}\in SC_{b}(C_{n},K_{n};\;r)$ }\}/\simeq_{r},

where the relation
\simeq_{r}
is chain homotopy via a homotopy with components
H_{n}\in SC_{b}(C_{n},K_{n};\;r)
. If
r<s
, then any
F\in Hom^{r}(C_{\bullet},K_{\bullet})
belongs also to
Hom^{s}(C_{\bullet},K_{\bullet})
. So, we get the following maps

i_{r,s}:Hom^{r}(C_{\bullet},K_{\bullet})\rightarrow Hom^{s}(C_{\bullet},K_{\bullet}).

Note that
i_{r,s}
are not inclusions in general because maps may be homotopic via correspondences from
SC_{b}(C_{n},K_{n};\;s)
but not homotopic via correspondences
SC_{b}(C_{n},K_{n};\;r)
. On the other hand, if a morphism is equal to zero in
Hom^{r}(C_{\bullet},K_{\bullet})
, then it is equal to zero in
Hom^{s}(C_{\bullet},K_{\bullet})
.

Lemma 12.10
.

The composition

Hom^{r}(Y_{\bullet},X_{\bullet})\times Hom^{s}(Z_{\bullet},Y_{\bullet})\xrightarrow{\circ}Hom^{r+s}(Z_{\bullet},X_{\bullet}).

is well-defined.

Proof.

Consider
F+\widetilde{F}
and
G+\widetilde{G}
, where
F\in Hom^{r}(C_{\bullet},K_{\bullet})
,
G\in Hom^{s}(L_{\bullet},C_{\bullet})
,
\widetilde{F},\widetilde{G}
are equal to zero in
Hom^{r}(C_{\bullet},K_{\bullet})
and
Hom^{s}(L_{\bullet},C_{\bullet})
, respectively. Then,

(F+\widetilde{F})\circ(G+\widetilde{G})=F\circ G+F\circ\widetilde{G}+\widetilde{F}\circ G+\widetilde{F}\circ\widetilde{G}.

Let
H
be a homotopy between
\widetilde{G}
and
0
, i.e. there are maps
H_{n}\in C(Z_{n},Y_{n-1};\;s)
such that
\widetilde{G}_{n}=d_{Y}^{n-1}H_{n}\pm H_{n+1}d_{Z}^{n}
. We see that

\begin{gathered}F_{n-1}\circ\widetilde{H}_{n}\in C(C_{n},K_{n-1};\;r+s)\\
d_{K}^{n-1}(F_{n-1}\circ H_{n})\pm(F_{n}\circ H_{n+1})d^{n}_{L}=F_{n}\circ(d_{K}^{n-1}H_{n}\pm H_{n+1}d^{n}_{L})=F_{n}\circ\widetilde{G}_{n}.\end{gathered}

This means that
F\circ\widetilde{G}=0
in
Hom^{r+s}(L_{\bullet},K_{\bullet})
. In the similar way, we show that
\widetilde{F}\circ G=0
in
Hom^{r+s}(L_{\bullet},K_{\bullet})
. This also shows that
\widetilde{F}\circ\widetilde{G}
is homotopic to
0
in
Hom^{r+s}(L_{\bullet},K_{\bullet})
. As a result, we see that the composition is well-defined.

∎

We define categories
\mathcal{C}
,
\mathcal{C}_{0}
, and
\mathcal{C}_{\infty}
, where

\begin{gathered}Ob(\mathcal{C})=Ob(\mathcal{C}_{0})=Ob(\mathcal{C}_{\infty})=Ob(Com^{-}(\mathcal{A})),\\
Mor_{\mathcal{C}}(C_{\bullet},K_{\bullet})=\coprod\limits_{r}Hom^{r}(C_{\bullet},K_{\bullet}),\;\;\;Mor_{\mathcal{C}_{0}}(C_{\bullet},K_{\bullet})=Hom^{0}(C_{\bullet},K_{\bullet}),\\
Mor_{\mathcal{C}_{\infty}}=\varinjlim Hom^{r}(C_{\bullet},K_{\bullet})\end{gathered}

and the limit is taken with respect to maps
i_{r,s}
.

We have a functor
T:\mathcal{C}\rightarrow\mathcal{C}

T(C_{\bullet},d_{C})=(C_{\bullet+1},-d_{C}),\;\;\;T(F_{n})=F_{n+1},

where
F_{n}
are the components of a chain map
F
. We have the same functor
\mathcal{C}_{0}\rightarrow\mathcal{C}_{0}
and denote it also by

.

Lemma 12.11
.

The category
\mathcal{C}_{0}
is triangulated, where

is the translation functor.

Proof.

The category
\mathcal{C}_{0}
coincides with the homotopy category of cochain complexes of
\mathcal{A}_{0}
. Since
\mathcal{A}_{0}
is additive, we get that
\mathcal{C}_{0}
is triangulated (see
[
8
, Th. 11.2.6]
)

∎

Lemma 12.12
.

The category
\mathcal{C}
is persistence category.

Proof.

Let
r^{\prime},s^{\prime}
be integers such that
r<r^{\prime}
and
s<s^{\prime}
. We need to prove that
i_{r,r^{\prime}}(F)\circ i_{s,s^{\prime}}(G)=i_{r+s,r^{\prime}+s^{\prime}}(F\circ G)
. Consider
F+F^{\prime}
and
G+G^{\prime}
, where
F^{\prime}=0
and
G^{\prime}=0
in
Hom^{r^{\prime}}(C_{\bullet},K_{\bullet})
and
Hom^{s^{\prime}}(L_{\bullet},C_{\bullet})
, respectively. Arguing as Lemma
12.10
, we can show that
F^{\prime}\circ G=0
,
F\circ G^{\prime}=0
, and
F^{\prime}\circ G^{\prime}=0
in
Hom^{r^{\prime}+s}(L_{\bullet},K_{\bullet})
,
Hom^{r+s^{\prime}}(L_{\bullet},K_{\bullet})
, and
Hom^{r^{\prime}+s^{\prime}}(L_{\bullet},K_{\bullet})
, respectively. Since
r^{\prime}+s
and
r+s^{\prime}
are less than
r^{\prime}+s^{\prime}
, we get that all the mentioned terms are equal to zero in
Hom^{r^{\prime}+s^{\prime}}(L_{\bullet},K_{\bullet})
. So, we have

i_{r,r^{\prime}}(F)\circ i_{s,s^{\prime}}(G)=i_{r+s,r^{\prime}+s^{\prime}}(F\circ G).

By definition, this means that
\mathcal{C}
is persistence category.

∎

Lemma 12.13
.

The categories
\mathcal{C}_{\infty}
and
\mathcal{K}(\mathcal{B})
(from the previous section) are naturally equivalent.

Proof.

There is embedding
I:\mathcal{K}(\mathcal{B})\rightarrow\mathcal{C}_{\infty}
, where each cochain complex
C_{\bullet}
with
C_{n}=\sqcup_{i=1}^{n}Z_{i}
goes to cochain with
I(C_{\bullet})_{n}=\oplus_{i=1}^{n}(Z_{i},0)
and
I

\oplus_{i=1}^{n}(Z_{i},r_{i})
is isomorphic to
\oplus_{i=1}^{n}(Z_{i},0)
. We get that any chain complex from
\mathcal{C}_{\infty}
is isomorphic to an object of

. Since the functor

is natural equivalence.

∎

The category
\mathcal{C}
inherits endofunctors
\Sigma^{s}:\mathcal{C}\rightarrow\mathcal{C}
. We define
\Sigma^{r}(C_{\bullet},d_{C})
such that

(\Sigma^{r}C_{\bullet})_{n}=\Sigma^{r}C_{n},\;\;\text{$\Sigma^{r}$ is identity on morphisms}.

Example 12.14
.

We see that the identity map
id_{C_{\bullet}}:C_{\bullet}\rightarrow C_{\bullet}

id_{C_{n}}\in SC_{b}(C_{n},C_{n};\;0)

Hom^{0}(C_{\bullet},C_{\bullet})
.

We constructed morphisms

id_{C_{\bullet}}^{r,s}:\Sigma^{r}C_{\bullet}\rightarrow\Sigma^{s}C_{\bullet}.

We see that

id_{C_{\bullet}}^{r^{\prime},s}\circ id_{C_{\bullet}}^{r,r^{\prime}}=id_{C_{\bullet}}^{r,s}.

In general, the identity map provides natural transformation between functors
\Sigma^{r}
and
\Sigma^{s}
. We have the following identities

\begin{gathered}Hom^{s}(\Sigma^{r}C_{\bullet},K_{\bullet})=Hom^{s+r}(C_{\bullet},K_{\bullet}),\;\;\;Hom^{s}(C_{\bullet},\Sigma^{r}K_{\bullet})=Hom^{s-r}(C_{\bullet},K_{\bullet}),\\
Hom^{s}(\Sigma^{r}C_{\bullet},\Sigma^{k}K_{\bullet})=Hom^{s+r-k}(C_{\bullet},K_{\bullet}).\end{gathered}

Lemma 12.15
.

The functor
T
and endofunctors
\Sigma^{r}
commute for any

.

Proof.

This follows from definitions.

We define a complex
(C_{\bullet}\oplus K_{\oplus},d_{C\oplus K})
as a complex with

(C_{\bullet}\oplus K_{\bullet})_{n}=C_{n}\oplus K_{n},\;\;\;d_{C\oplus K}=\begin{pmatrix}d_{C}&0\\
0&d_{K}\end{pmatrix}.

By definition,
(\Sigma^{r}(C_{\bullet}\oplus K_{\bullet}))_{n}=\Sigma^{r}C_{n}\oplus\Sigma^{r}K_{n}
and
\Sigma^{r}(d_{C\oplus K})=d_{C\oplus K}

Let
F:C_{\bullet}\rightarrow K_{\bullet}
be a chain map. We define a cone of
F

Cone(F)=(T(Y_{\bullet})\oplus Z_{\bullet},d_{cone}).\;\;\;d_{cone}=\begin{pmatrix}-d_{C}&0\\
F&d_{K}\end{pmatrix}.

A cochain complex
C_{\bullet}
is called
r-
acyclic if the

in
Hom^{r}(C_{\bullet},C_{\bullet})
, i.e.
id_{C_{\bullet}}^{0,0}
homotopic to zero via homotopy with components in
SC_{b}(C_{n},C_{n-1};\;r)

Proof of Theorem
2.2
. First, let us give the following definition.

Definition 13.1

A complex manifold

We need the following criteria proved by Campana.

Theorem 13.2
.

(see
\cite[cite]{[\@@bibref{}{Campana}{}{}, p. 212]})

A compact complex manifold is projective if and only if it is Kahler and algebraically connected.

Let
\mathbb{Z}[X]
be a group of finite formal linear combinations of points of
X

JC_{0}^{\mathbb{C}P^{1}}(pt,X)=\mathbb{Z}[X],\quad JC_{1}^{\mathbb{C}P^{1}}(pt,X)=JC(\mathbb{C}P^{1},X).

If

and

If

\pi^{-1}(U)=\bigcup_{i=1}^{N}(U,f_{i}(U)),\;\;\;\text{where $f_{i}:U\rightarrow X$ are holomorphic}.
