Multimarked Spatial Search by Continuous-Time Quantum Walk
Pedro H. G. Lugão, Renato Portugal, Mohamed Sabri, Hajime Tanaka

Abstract
The quantum-walk-based spatial search problem aims to find a marked vertex using a quantum walk on a graph with marked vertices. We describe a framework for determining the computational complexity of spatial search by continuous-time quantum walk on arbitrary graphs by providing a recipe for finding the optimal running time and the success probability of the algorithm. The quantum walk is driven by a Hamiltonian derived from the adjacency matrix of the graph modified by the presence of the marked vertices. The success of our framework depends on the knowledge of the eigenvalues and eigenvectors of the adjacency matrix. The spectrum of the Hamiltonian is subsequently obtained from the roots of the determinant of a real symmetric matrix M M , the dimensions of which depend on the number of marked vertices. The eigenvectors are determined from a basis of the kernel of M M . We show each step of the framework by solving the spatial searching problem on the Johnson graphs with a fixed diameter and with two marked vertices. Our calculations show that the optimal running time is O ⁡ ( N ) O(\sqrt{N}) with an asymptotic probability of 1 + o ⁡ ( 1 ) 1+o(1) , where N N is the number of vertices. Keywords: Continuous-time quantum walk, spatial quantum search, Johnson graph, multiple marked vertices

Introduction

Farhi and Gutmann
[
1
]
introduced the continuous-time quantum walk as a discrete-space version of the Schrödinger equation, where the positions of the particle are the vertices of a graph, specifically, a tree whose nodes represent solutions to a decision problem
[
2
]
. Childs and Goldstone
[
3
]
used the continuous-time quantum walk to address the spatial search problem with one marked vertex, the goal being to locate the marked vertex when the walk departs from an initial state that is easy to prepare. The evolution is based on a Hamiltonian, which includes an extra term that depends on the location of the marked vertex and differs from the Hamiltonian used by Farhi and Gutmann, which is solely based on the graph’s adjacency matrix. Childs and Goldstone analyzed the search on lattices, hypercubes, and complete graphs to determine whether the quantum-walk-based search algorithm is faster than the random-walk-based one on the same graphs. They obtained negative results in some cases, for instance, on
d
-dimensional lattices with
d\leqslant 4
. However, this should not be interpreted as definitive evidence that it is impossible to find a quantum-walk-based search algorithm that provides a speedup over the best random-walk-based case. In fact, using alternative discrete-time quantum walk models, it is possible to achieve a quadratic speedup for
d
-dimensional lattices with
d\geqslant 2

[
4
,
5
]
.

The origin of the quantum-walk-based spatial search problem lies in the confluence of two proposals: Farhi and Gutmann’s 1998 suggestion for accelerating random walks on decision trees using a continuous-time quantum walk
[

[

The first formulation investigates whether a quantum walk on a graph

G

[
8

[

Numerous papers in the literature address this formulation in both the discrete-time

,

,

,

The second formulation asks whether the number of steps that a classical random walk on a graph

takes to find a marked vertex

can be reduced by implementing a quantum walk on any graph

, so that the quantum walk on

, who showed that the quantum hitting time of a quantum walk on the bipartite graph

. Many papers in the literature have addressed the second formulation in both the discrete-time

. Apers et al.
[

go along the same line by continuous-time quantum walk.
Somma and Ortiz

[

In this work, we address the first formulation of the spatial search problem by continuous-time quantum walk on arbitrary graphs with multiple marked vertices. Additionally, we provide a framework to determine the computational complexity of the search algorithm that is successful when the gap of two key eigenvalues
\lambda^{\pm}
of the Hamiltonian is small enough. Note that our aim differs from the one described in references
[
13
,
20
]
, which focus on obtaining optimality conditions, namely, a time complexity that has a quadratic speedup compared to random walks. Our method may result in non-optimal time complexity, but it remains interesting if it is better than
O(N)
. For instance, the
d
-dimensional lattice with multiple marked vertices, where
d=2
and
d=3
, are interesting candidates. These were analyzed in the reference
[
3
]
, but only for the case with one marked vertex.
As an example of our framework, we analyze in full detail the search algorithm on the Johnson graph with two marked vertices. The framework is based on a real symmetric matrix
M
, which depends on the entries of the adjacency matrix and on the locations of the marked vertices. The fact that the determinant of this matrix is zero allows us to find the spectrum of the Hamiltonian. Then, we can find the gap between
\lambda^{\pm}
and all quantities necessary to determine the algorithm’s optimal running time and the success probability assuming that the gap tends to zero when the number of vertices increases. The relevant quantities are basically the overlaps between the marked vertices and the initial condition with the eigenvectors of the Hamiltonian associated with
\lambda^{\pm}

The Johnson graph

\pi k\sqrt{N}/(2\sqrt{2})
with asymptotic probability

The structure of this paper is as follows. In Sec.

2
Multimarked framework

Let
G(V,E)
be a finite connected simple graph with vertex set
V
and edge set
E
.
We associate
G
with a Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
with computational basis
\{|v\rangle:v\in V\}
, as is usually done in the definition of the continuous-time quantum walk
[
1
]
.
Let
W
be the set of marked vertices.
We consider the time-independent Hamiltonian of the form
[
3
]

H=-\gamma A-\sum_{w\in W}\big|w\big\rangle\big\langle w\big|,

(1)

where
A
denotes the adjacency matrix of
G(V,E)
, and
\gamma
is a real and positive parameter.
The spatial search algorithm starts with the initial state
\big|\psi(0)\big\rangle
, which is a normalized eigenvector of
-\gamma A
associated with the largest eigenvalue of
A
. If
G
is regular,
\big|\psi(0)\big\rangle
is the uniform superposition of the computational basis

\big|\psi(0)\big\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{v\in V}|v\rangle,

where
N
is the number of vertices.
The quantum state at time
t
is therefore given by

\big|\psi(t)\big\rangle=\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}Ht}\big|\psi(0)\big\rangle.

The probability of finding a marked vertex at time
t
is

p(t)=\sum_{w\in W}\left|\big\langle w\big|\psi(t)\big\rangle\right|^{2}.

(2)

Our goal is to find the optimal values of parameters
t
and
\gamma
so that the success probability is as high as possible.

2.1
Eigenvalues and eigenvectors of the Hamiltonian

For an operator
U
, let
\sigma(U)
denote the spectrum of
U
.
Suppose that the adjacency matrix
A
of
G(V,E)
has exactly
k+1
distinct eigenvalues
\phi_{0}>\phi_{1}>\dots>\phi_{k}
.
For
0\leqslant\ell\leqslant k
, let
P_{\ell}
denote the orthogonal projector onto the eigenspace of
A
in
\mathcal{H}

for the eigenvalue
\phi_{\ell}
, that is,

A=\sum_{\ell=0}^{k}\phi_{\ell}P_{\ell}.

Let
\lambda
and
\big|\lambda\big\rangle
be an eigenvalue and a normalized eigenvector of
H
, respectively, that is

H\big|\lambda\big\rangle=\lambda\big|\lambda\big\rangle

and
\big\langle\lambda\big|\lambda\big\rangle=1
. Hamiltonian
H
and operator
-\gamma A
may share common eigenvalues and eigenvectors, as shown in the next Proposition.

Proposition 1
.

Let
|\lambda\rangle
be an eigenvector of
H
associated with eigenvalue
\lambda
. Then,

\lambda\in\sigma(-\gamma A)
and
(-\gamma A)\big|\lambda\big\rangle=\lambda\big|\lambda\big\rangle
if and only if
\big\langle w\big|\lambda\big\rangle=0
for all
w\in W
.

Proof.

Using the definition of
H
, we obtain

\sum_{w\in W}\big|w\big\rangle\big\langle w\big|\lambda\big\rangle=-(\gamma A+H)\big|\lambda\big\rangle.

If
\lambda\in\sigma(-\gamma A)
and
(-\gamma A)\big|\lambda\big\rangle=\lambda\big|\lambda\big\rangle
, the right-hand side is zero and the entries of the vector
\sum_{w\in W}\big|w\big\rangle\big\langle w\big|\lambda\big\rangle
must be zero.
Then, we have
\big\langle w\big|\lambda\big\rangle=0
for all
w\in W
.
On the other hand, if
\big\langle w\big|\lambda\big\rangle=0
for all
w\in W
, the left-hand side is zero. Since
H\big|\lambda\big\rangle=\lambda\big|\lambda\big\rangle
, we have
\lambda\in\sigma(-\gamma A)
and
(-\gamma A)\big|\lambda\big\rangle=\lambda\big|\lambda\big\rangle
.
∎

In Eq. (
2
), it is evident that eigenvectors
\big|\lambda\big\rangle
, which satisfy
\big\langle w\big|\lambda\big\rangle=0
for all
w\in W
, do not contribute to the computation of the probability
p(t)
for locating a marked vertex. These eigenvectors correspond to eigenvalues
\lambda
that are part of the spectrum of
-\gamma A
. In the continuation, we are interested in eigenvectors
\big|\lambda\big\rangle
for which
\big\langle w\big|\lambda\big\rangle\neq 0
, at least for one marked vertex
w
. The eigenvectors
\big|\lambda\big\rangle
fulfilling the condition
\big\langle\psi(0)\big|\lambda\big\rangle=0
are also irrelevant to the dynamics. To discern which eigenvectors of
H
should be disregarded, the following proposition is necessary.

Proposition 2
.

Let
\lambda
be a real number such that
\lambda\notin\sigma(-\gamma A)
.
Define the real symmetric matrix
M^{\lambda}=\big(M_{ww^{\prime}}^{\lambda}\big)_{w,w^{\prime}\in W}
, where

M_{ww^{\prime}}^{\lambda}=\delta_{ww^{\prime}}+\sum_{\ell=0}^{k}\frac{\big\langle w\big|P_{\ell}\big|w^{\prime}\big\rangle}{\lambda+\gamma\phi_{\ell}}.

(3)

Then,
\lambda
is an eigenvalue of
H
if and only if
\det(M^{\lambda})=0
.

Proof.

(
\Rightarrow
)
Assume that
\lambda
is an eigenvalue of
H
and
\big|\lambda\big\rangle
is the corresponding eigenvector. From the definition of
H
, we have

P_{\ell}H\big|\lambda\big\rangle=-P_{\ell}\left(\gamma A+\sum_{w\in W}\big|w\big\rangle\big\langle w\big|\right)\big|\lambda\big\rangle,

(4)

and since
\lambda\notin\sigma(-\gamma A)
, we can rewrite the above equation as

P_{\ell}\big|\lambda\big\rangle=-\frac{1}{\lambda+\gamma\phi_{\ell}}\sum_{w\in W}\big\langle w\big|\lambda\big\rangle P_{\ell}\big|w\big\rangle.

(5)

Given that
\sum_{\ell=0}^{k}P_{\ell}=I
, for a fixed marked vertex
w
, we obtain

\big\langle w\big|\lambda\big\rangle=\sum_{\ell=0}^{k}\big\langle w\big|P_{\ell}\big|\lambda\big\rangle.

Then, using Eq. (
5
), we derive

\big\langle w\big|\lambda\big\rangle=-\sum_{\ell=0}^{k}\frac{1}{\lambda+\gamma\phi_{\ell}}\left(\sum_{w^{\prime}\in W}\big\langle w^{\prime}\big|\lambda\big\rangle\big\langle w\big|P_{\ell}\big|w^{\prime}\big\rangle\right),

which can be rewritten as

\sum_{w^{\prime}\in W}M_{ww^{\prime}}^{\lambda}\big\langle w^{\prime}\big|\lambda\big\rangle=0,

(6)

where
\big(M_{ww^{\prime}}^{\lambda}\big)_{w,w^{\prime}\in W}
is as defined in Eq. (
3
).
Since
\lambda\not\in\sigma(-\gamma A)
, the vector
\big\langle w\big|\lambda\big\rangle\big\rvert_{w\in W}
is nonzero by Proposition
1
, and hence it is a
0
-eigenvector of
M^{\lambda}
.
It follows that
\det\big(M^{\lambda}\big)=0.

(
\Leftarrow
)
Assume
\lambda
is a real number not in
\sigma(-\gamma A)
such that
\det(M^{\lambda})=0
.

M^{\lambda}
has a 0-eigenvector, denoted as
u(w)\big\rvert_{w\in W}
. Construct
k+1
vectors
\big|\lambda_{\ell}\big\rangle
, inspired by Eq. (
5
), as follows:

\big|\lambda_{\ell}\big\rangle=-\frac{1}{\lambda+\gamma\phi_{\ell}}\sum_{w\in W}u(w)P_{\ell}\big|w\big\rangle.

Note that
P_{\ell}\big|\lambda_{\ell^{\prime}}\big\rangle=\delta_{\ell\ell^{\prime}}\big|\lambda_{\ell}\big\rangle
, or equivalently,
A\big|\lambda_{\ell}\big\rangle=\phi_{\ell}\big|\lambda_{\ell}\big\rangle
.
Our candidate for an eigenvector of
H
associated with
\lambda
is

\big|\lambda\big\rangle=\sum_{\ell=0}^{k}\big|\lambda_{\ell}\big\rangle.

(7)

First, we claim that
\big|\lambda\big\rangle
is nonzero.
Indeed, if
\big|\lambda\big\rangle=0
, then we have
0=P_{\ell}\big|\lambda\big\rangle=\big|\lambda_{\ell}\big\rangle
for all
\ell
, and hence

0=-\sum_{\ell=0}^{k}(\lambda+\gamma\phi_{\ell})\big|\lambda_{\ell}\big\rangle=\sum_{\ell=0}^{k}\sum_{w\in W}u(w)P_{\ell}\big|w\big\rangle=\sum_{w\in W}u(w)\big|w\big\rangle

by
\sum_{\ell=0}^{k}P_{\ell}=I
, but this is impossible because
u(w)\big\rvert_{w\in W}
is nonzero.
We next claim that
H\big|\lambda\big\rangle=\lambda\big|\lambda\big\rangle
. Indeed, using the definitions of
H
and
\big|\lambda_{\ell}\big\rangle
, we have

\displaystyle H\big|\lambda\big\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell=0}^{k}\frac{\gamma\phi_{\ell}}{\lambda+\gamma\phi_{\ell}}\sum_{w\in W}u(w)P_{\ell}\big|w\big\rangle+

\displaystyle\quad\sum_{w,w^{\prime}\in W}\left(\sum_{\ell=0}^{k}\frac{\big\langle w\big|P_{\ell}\big|w^{\prime}\big\rangle}{\lambda+\gamma\phi_{\ell}}\right)u(w^{\prime})\big|w\big\rangle.

The expression in parentheses becomes
M_{ww^{\prime}}^{\lambda}-\delta_{ww^{\prime}}
by Eq. (
3
).
Since
u(w)\big\rvert_{w\in W}
is a
0
-eigenvector of
M^{\lambda}
,
we find

\displaystyle H\big|\lambda\big\rangle=\sum_{\ell=0}^{k}\frac{\gamma\phi_{\ell}}{\lambda+\gamma\phi_{\ell}}\sum_{w\in W}u(w)P_{\ell}\big|w\big\rangle-\sum_{w\in W}u(w)\big|w\big\rangle.

Applying
\sum_{\ell=0}^{k}P_{\ell}
on the second term of the right-hand side, the equation simplifies to

\displaystyle H\big|\lambda\big\rangle

\displaystyle=\lambda\left(-\sum_{\ell=0}^{k}\frac{1}{\lambda+\gamma\phi_{\ell}}\sum_{w\in W}u(w)P_{\ell}\big|w\big\rangle\right),

which concludes the proof, considering Eq. (
7
).
∎

Given a graph
G(V,E)
with a set
W
of marked vertices, the proof of Proposition
2
outlines a method for determining the eigenvalues
\lambda\notin\sigma(-\gamma A)
of the Hamiltonian (
1
), assuming we know the eigenvalues and eigenprojections of the adjacency matrix. This method involves solving the equation
\det(M^{\lambda})=0
for an unknown real number
\lambda
. Note that the numerator of
\det(M^{\lambda})
is a polynomial
P(\lambda)
of degree at most
(k+1)|W|
in the indeterminate
\lambda
. There can be at most
(k+1)|W|
roots of
P(\lambda)=0
, some of which may lie in
\sigma(-\gamma A)
. Additionally, since
A
is a real symmetric matrix,
M^{\lambda}
is also real symmetric and thus orthogonally diagonalizable.

The proof of Proposition
2
also shows how to identify a basis for the eigenspace associated with
\lambda
. The multiplicity of
\lambda
is equal to the multiplicity of the zero eigenvalue of
M^{\lambda}
. Given an orthogonal basis of
0
-eigenvectors of
M^{\lambda}
, and applying Eq. (
7
), we can derive an orthogonal basis for the
\lambda
-eigenspace of
H
. Consequently, as a corollary, the multiplicity of
\lambda
is constrained by the upper bound of
|W|
.

Given that the initial state is
\big|\phi_{0}\big\rangle
, an eigenvector
\big|\lambda\big\rangle
such that
\big\langle\lambda\big|\phi_{0}\big\rangle=0
does not affect the quantum walk dynamics. The criterion to ensure that
\big\langle\lambda\big|\phi_{0}\big\rangle\neq 0
when eigenvector
\big|\lambda\big\rangle
is obtained using Eq. (
7
) is

\sum_{w\in W}u(w)\big\langle\phi_{0}\big|w\big\rangle\neq 0.

(8)

In the case of a regular graph
G(V,E)
, it is necessary to assert that
\sum_{w\in W}u(w)\neq 0
.

Proposition 3
.

Let
\lambda^{-}
be the smallest eigenvalue of
H
and
\big|\lambda^{-}\big\rangle
be an associated eigenvector. Then,
\lambda^{-}
is simple,
\big\langle\lambda^{-}\big|\phi_{0}\big\rangle\neq 0
, and
\lambda^{-}<-\gamma\phi_{0}
for any
\gamma>0
.

Proof.

Since
-H
is irreducible and nonnegative, by the Perron–Frobenius theorem (see, e.g.,
[
36
,
37
,
38
]
),
\lambda^{-}
is simple and the entries of
\big|\lambda^{-}\big\rangle
are all nonnegative or all nonpositive.
In particular,
\big\langle\lambda^{-}\big|\phi_{0}\big\rangle\neq 0
.
Moreover, since
\gamma A
and
-H-\gamma A=\sum_{w\in W}\big|w\big\rangle\big\langle w\big|
are both nonnegative and the latter is nonzero, we have
\lambda^{-}<-\gamma\phi_{0}
for any
\gamma>0
.
∎

Let
\lambda^{+}
denote the eigenvalue of
H
that is the
(|W|+1)
-smallest, considering the multiplicity of each eigenvalue. If
\lambda^{+}
is in the spectrum
\sigma(-\gamma A)
, or if the eigenvectors
\big|\lambda^{+}\big\rangle
corresponding to
\lambda^{+}
satisfy
\big\langle\lambda^{+}\big|\phi_{0}\big\rangle=0
for
\gamma>0
, meaning that
\big|\lambda^{+}\big\rangle
is derived from a
0
-eigenvector of
M^{\lambda}
fulfilling condition (
8
), then we must select the next smallest eigenvalue. We claim that there exists a specific value of
\gamma
for which the eigenvalue
-\gamma\phi_{0}
of
-\gamma A
is positioned midway between
\lambda^{-}
and
\lambda^{+}
. Our approach uses
\lambda^{\pm}
and this
\gamma
in the asymptotic limit. Essentially, for any graph
G(V,E)
we claim the existence of a
\gamma
value satisfying

\lambda^{\pm}=-\gamma\phi_{0}\pm\epsilon,

where
|\lambda^{-}+\gamma\phi_{0}|=|\lambda^{+}+\gamma\phi_{0}|=\epsilon
.

Before we present the proof of this claim, let us first offer some intuitive insights into its validity. Considering
H
as defined in Eq. (
1
), when
\gamma=0
, the eigenvalues of
H
are
-1
(with multiplicity
|W|
) and
0
(with multiplicity
N-|W|
), where
N
is the dimension of the Hilbert space. For small, nonzero
\gamma
, the eigenvalue of
-1
splits into
|W|
eigenvalues (which are not necessarily distinct) corresponding to different eigenvectors. Note that, based on a dimensionality analysis, at least one of these
|W|
eigenvectors
\big|\lambda\big\rangle
satisfies
\big\langle\lambda\big|\phi_{0}\big\rangle\neq 0
, aligned with
\big\langle\lambda^{-}\big|\phi_{0}\big\rangle\neq 0
. In the case when
|W|\geq 2
, at least two eigenvalues will be different because
\lambda^{-}
, the smallest eigenvalue, is simple. As previously described,
\lambda^{+}
represents the
(|W|+1)
-th eigenvalue of
H
, considering the multiplicity of each eigenvalue. Consequently,
\lambda^{+}
is selected as the smallest eigenvalue of
H
that approaches 0 when
\gamma
tends to 0. Due to the stipulation in constraint (
8
) and the condition
\lambda^{+}\notin\sigma(-\gamma A)
, there are instances where
\lambda^{+}
needs to be the
j
-th eigenvalue, where
j
exceeds
|W|+1
. It is essential to avoid any eigenvalues that converge towards
-1
as
\gamma
decreases when choosing
\lambda^{+}
because, in such scenarios, the gap between
\lambda^{-}
and
\lambda^{+}
becomes too small.

When
\gamma
is small, the eigenvalue
-\gamma\phi_{0}
of
-\gamma A
is near zero, placing it close to
\lambda^{+}
on the real line spectrum. Conversely, as
\gamma
increases,
-\gamma\phi_{0}
becomes increasingly aligned with
\lambda^{-}
, indicating an one-to-one correlation between the eigenvalues of
-\gamma A
and
H
. We have established that for small
\gamma
, the eigenvalue
-\gamma\phi_{0}
is positioned near the right of the range
[\lambda^{-},\lambda^{+}]
. As
\gamma
increases, this eigenvalue shifts leftward, eventually nearing the left end of this range. Given that the determinants
|H-\lambda I|
and
|-\gamma A-\phi I|
are continuous real functions of
\gamma
, by applying the intermediate value theorem, we deduce that a specific
\gamma
exists where
-\gamma\phi_{0}
is exactly at the midpoint of
[\lambda^{-},\lambda^{+}]
.

Proposition 4
.

Let
\lambda^{-}
be the smallest eigenvalues of
H
. Let
\lambda^{+}
be an eigenvalue of
H
such that
\lambda^{+}>\lambda^{-}
. Then, there exists
\gamma>0
such that

-\gamma\phi_{0}=\frac{\lambda^{-}+\lambda^{+}}{2},

where
\phi_{0}
is the largest eigenvalue of
A
.

Proof.

For convenience, set
\tau=\gamma/(1+\gamma)
.
Note that
\tau\in(0,1)
.
Also, let
\mu^{\pm}(\tau)=\lambda^{\pm}(\gamma)/(1+\gamma)
, so that they are eigenvalues of

(1-\tau)H(\gamma)=-\tau A-(1-\tau)\sum_{w\in W}\big|w\big\rangle\big\langle w\big|.

Then, observe that the values
\mu^{\pm}(\tau)
also make sense for
\tau=0,1
.
In particular,

\mu^{-}(0)=-1,\qquad\mu^{+}(0)\leqslant 0,\qquad\mu^{-}(1)=-\phi_{0},\qquad\mu^{+}(1)=-\phi_{\ell}

for some
\ell\geqslant 1
, where
\phi_{\ell}
is the
(\ell+1)^{\mathrm{st}}
largest eigenvalue of
A
.
We have used that
\phi_{0}
is a simple eigenvalue of
A
because
G(V,E)
is connected.
Moreover, note that
\mu^{\pm}(\tau)
are continuous in
\tau
using Weyl’s Perturbation Theorem
[
39
]
.
Now, consider the continuous function

h(\tau)=\mu^{-}(\tau)+\mu^{+}(\tau)+2\phi_{0}\tau.

We have
h(0)\leqslant-1<0
and
h(1)=\phi_{0}-\phi_{\ell}>0
.
Hence, by the intermediate value theorem, there exists
\tau\in(0,1)
such that
h(\tau)=0
.
The proof is complete after converting back to variable
\gamma=\tau/(1-\tau)
.
∎

To illustrate an eigenvalue
\lambda
to avoid because it is in
\sigma(-\gamma A)
, consider the complete bipartite graph
K_{n,n}
with
n
marked vertices in one part. The eigenvalues of
H
are ordered as
\lambda^{-}<-1<0<\lambda^{+}
. The second smallest eigenvalue is
-1
with a multiplicity of
n-1
. The next eigenvalue,
0
, belongs to
\sigma(-\gamma A)
, making
\lambda^{+}
the
(n+2)
-th and final eigenvalue of

.

2.2
Asymptotic analysis

Let us consider a graph class characterized by some parameters that determine the number of vertices
N
. Usually, the spectral gap of the Hamiltonian tends to zero when the number of vertices
N
increases. In this case, the analysis of the search algorithm is simpler in the asymptotic regime.

In cases where one vertex is marked, several studies have employed a technique to analyze quantum-walk-based search algorithms. This technique uses two key eigenvectors of the evolution operator, ensuring significant overlap between both the initial and marked states and the space spanned by these eigenvectors
[
3
,
4
,
6
]
. In this work, we extend and generalize this technique to cases with multiple marked vertices. The asymptotic computational complexity of the search algorithm primarily depends on the eigenvalues
\lambda^{\pm}
, where
\lambda^{-}
is the smallest eigenvalue of
H
, and
\lambda^{+}
is the
(|W|+1)
-smallest, taking into account the multiplicity of each eigenvalue. Besides,
\lambda^{+}
must satisfy the conditions
\lambda^{+}\notin\sigma(-\gamma A)
and
\big\langle\lambda^{+}\big|\phi_{0}\big\rangle\neq 0
for
\gamma>0
. The value of
\gamma
is described in Proposition
4
, which positions
-\gamma\phi_{0}
in the middle of the range
[\lambda^{-},\lambda^{+}]
. Since calculating this optimal
\gamma
might be too difficult, we describe an alternative way to obtain
\gamma
, which asymptotically approaches the optimal one. Thus, we can write

\lambda^{\pm}=-\gamma\phi_{0}\pm\epsilon+o(\epsilon).

(9)

In this case, the gap between
\lambda^{-}
and
\lambda^{+}
is asymptotically
2\epsilon>0
. Note that
2\epsilon
is larger than the spectral gap of
H
unless
|W|=1
. We will detail a method to calculate
\gamma
needed in Eq. (
9
) below.

Using Eq. (
3
), matrices
M^{\lambda^{\pm}}
are given by

M_{ww^{\prime}}^{\lambda^{\pm}}=\delta_{ww^{\prime}}+\sum_{\ell=0}^{k}\frac{\big\langle w\big|P_{\ell}\big|w^{\prime}\big\rangle}{\gamma(\phi_{\ell}-\phi_{0})\pm{\epsilon}+o(\epsilon)}.

Up to second order in
\epsilon
we obtain

M_{ww^{\prime}}^{\lambda^{\pm}}=\pm\frac{\big\langle w\big|P_{0}\big|w^{\prime}\big\rangle}{\epsilon}+\delta_{ww^{\prime}}-\frac{S^{(1)}_{ww^{\prime}}}{\gamma}\mp\frac{\epsilon S^{(2)}_{ww^{\prime}}}{\gamma^{2}}+O(\epsilon^{2}),

(10)

where

S^{(1)}_{ww^{\prime}}=\sum_{\ell=1}^{k}\frac{\big\langle w\big|P_{\ell}\big|w^{\prime}\big\rangle}{\phi_{0}-\phi_{\ell}}

and

S^{(2)}_{ww^{\prime}}=\sum_{\ell=1}^{k}\frac{\big\langle w\big|P_{\ell}\big|w^{\prime}\big\rangle}{(\phi_{0}-\phi_{\ell})^{2}}.

Note that the sums
S^{(1)}_{ww^{\prime}}
and
S^{(1)}_{ww^{\prime}}
, as well as
\big\langle w\big|P_{0}\big|w^{\prime}\big\rangle
, can be calculated if we know the eigenvalues and eigenprojections of
A
. The unknown quantities in Eq. (
10
) are
\gamma
and
\epsilon
.

One of the goals of the asymptotic analysis is to determine analytical values of
\gamma
and
\epsilon
. By using Eq. (
10
), the condition
\det(M^{\lambda^{\pm}})=0
, and considering the numerator up to the order of
\epsilon^{2}
, we derive a quadratic equation in the form
\epsilon^{2}+a(\gamma)\epsilon-b(\gamma)=O(\epsilon^{3})
. This equation cannot contain the linear term, as this is the only way to achieve the
\pm\epsilon
term in Eq. (
9
). Consequently, we use the equation
a(\gamma)=0
to ascertain the value of
\gamma
, which is asymptotically optimal. Once
\gamma
is computed,
\epsilon
can be determined as
\sqrt{b(\gamma)}
.

Proposition
4
ensures that Eq. (
9
) is applicable to any graph
G(V,E)
. Furthermore, this asymptotic analysis is valid for any graph such that
\epsilon\ll 1
or for any graph class where the gap between
\lambda^{-}
and
\lambda^{+}
approaches 0 as
N
tends toward infinity. An example where the gap between
\lambda^{-}
and
\lambda^{+}
does not approach zero is the class of complete bipartite graphs
K_{n,n}
with
n
marked vertices in one part. In this case, using that
\phi_{0}=n
, the optimal parameters are
\lambda^{\pm}=(-1\pm\sqrt{2})/2
and
\gamma=1/2n
. This yields a fixed
\epsilon=1/\sqrt{2}
. Note that the behavior of the gap depends on the placement of the marked vertices. In the same graph
K_{n,n}
, if the marked vertices are arranged in a different configuration than the one above, the gap approaches zero.

2.3
Calculation of the computational complexity

Using an orthonormal basis
{\big|\lambda\big\rangle}
of eigenvectors of
H
, we can express the probability of finding a marked vertex as a function of time
t
(refer to Eq. (

)) as follows:

p_{\text{exact}}(t)=\sum_{w\in W}\Big|\sum_{\lambda}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\lambda t}\big\langle w\big|\lambda\big\rangle\big\langle\lambda\big|\psi(0)\big\rangle\Big|^{2}.

The exact eigenvalues
\lambda
correspond to the roots of
\det(M^{\lambda})=0
. However, calculating these roots and the exact expression for
p_{\text{exact}}(t)
often proves too complex. In the asymptotic regime, where
N\rightarrow\infty
and
\epsilon\rightarrow 0
, it is preferable to use Eq. (
10
) as long as the computational complexity is determined by
\lambda^{\pm}
. Matrices
M^{\lambda^{\pm}}
are simpler to compute as their entries depend only on two sums and the term
\big\langle w\big|P_{0}\big|w^{\prime}\big\rangle
. To calculate an approximate

in this regime, we focus on the dominant terms:

p_{\text{approx}}(t)=\sum_{w\in W}\left|\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\epsilon t}\big\langle w\big|\lambda^{-}\big\rangle\big\langle\lambda^{-}\big|\psi(0)\big\rangle+\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\epsilon t}\big\langle w\big|\lambda^{+}\big\rangle\big\langle\lambda^{+}\big|\psi(0)\big\rangle\right|^{2}.

(11)

\big|\lambda^{-}\big\rangle
is a normalized eigenvector of
H
associated with the smallest root
\lambda^{-}
of
\det(M^{\lambda})=0
and

\big|\lambda^{+}\big\rangle
with the
(|W|+1)
-smallest root
\lambda^{+}
, subjected to the restrictions
\lambda^{+}\notin\sigma(-\gamma A)
and
\big\langle\lambda^{+}\big|\phi_{0}\big\rangle\neq 0
.
Eigenvalue
\lambda^{-}
is always simple but if eigenvalue
\lambda^{+}
is not simple, which is uncommon, we must replace
\big|\lambda^{+}\big\rangle\big\langle\lambda^{+}\big|
with the eigenprojection
P^{+}=\sum\big|\lambda^{+}\big\rangle\big\langle\lambda^{+}\big|
in equation (
11
), where the sum runs over an orthonormal basis of the
\lambda^{+}
-eigenspace. The multiplicity of
\lambda^{+}
is at most
|W|
.

Note that
p_{\text{exact}}(t)\geq p_{\text{approx}}(t)
. The terms of
p_{\text{approx}}(t)
are calculated as follows. The value of
\gamma
is derived from the equation
a(\gamma)=0
and
\epsilon
from
\sqrt{b(\gamma)}
, as described in Sec.
2.2
. Now, let us turn our attention to calculating
\big|\big\langle w\big|\lambda^{+}\big\rangle\big|
. The approach for calculating
\big|\big\langle w\big|\lambda^{-}\big\rangle\big|
follows a similar pattern. Remember that
\big\langle w\big|\lambda^{+}\big\rangle\bigr\rvert_{w\in W}
is a
0
-eigenvector of
M^{\lambda^{+}}
, with entries
\big\langle w\big|\lambda^{+}\big\rangle
for each
w\in W
. Given that
M^{\lambda^{+}}
is already determined, we can identify a normalized
0
-eigenvector of
M^{\lambda^{+}}
, which has entries denoted as
u(w)
for each
w\in W
. In cases where
\lambda^{+}
is not a simple eigenvalue, it becomes necessary to establish an orthonormal basis for the kernel of
M^{\lambda^{+}}
. Following this,

\big\langle w\big|\lambda^{+}\big\rangle={c^{+}}\,u(w),

(12)

where
{c^{+}}
is a complex number. Using
\sum_{\ell}\left\|P_{\ell}\big|\lambda^{+}\big\rangle\right\|^{2}=1

and Eq. (
5
), we obtain

\frac{1}{\left|{c^{+}}\right|^{2}}=\sum_{\ell=0}^{k}\frac{1}{\left|\lambda^{+}+\gamma\phi_{\ell}\right|^{2}}\,\Big\|\sum_{w\in W}u(w)P_{\ell}\big|w\big\rangle\Big\|^{2}.

The dominant term is

\frac{1}{\left|{c^{+}}\right|}=\frac{1}{\epsilon}\,\Big\|\sum_{w\in W}u(w)P_{0}\big|w\big\rangle\Big\|+O(1).

To calculate
\big\langle w\big|\lambda^{+}\big\rangle
instead of its absolute value, we still have to find
{c^{+}}
instead of
\left|{c^{+}}\right|
. This is easily solved by rescaling
\big|\lambda^{+}\big\rangle
with a unit complex number
\big|\lambda^{+}\big\rangle\rightarrow\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\theta}\big|\lambda^{+}\big\rangle
so that
{c^{+}}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\theta}
is a positive real number. After rescaling
\big|\lambda^{+}\big\rangle
, we have
\big\langle w\big|\lambda^{+}\big\rangle=|c^{+}|\,u(w)
.

The last terms of
p_{\text{approx}}(t)
we need to determine are
\big|\big\langle\lambda^{\pm}\big|\psi(0)\big\rangle\big|
. We define
\big|\psi(0)\big\rangle
as a normalized eigenvector of
-\gamma A
corresponding to the eigenvalue

. In the special case where the graph

is regular,

is the uniform superposition and thus becomes an eigenvector with the eigenvalue
-\gamma d
, where

is the degree of the graph. Setting
\ell=0
and applying Eq (
5
), we derive

\big\langle\psi(0)\big|\lambda^{\pm}\big\rangle=\mp\frac{1}{\epsilon}\sum_{w\in W}\big\langle\psi(0)\big|w\big\rangle\big\langle w\big|\lambda^{\pm}\big\rangle.

(13)

Given that all terms on the right-hand side are assumed to be known, we now have a method to calculate
\big\langle\lambda^{\pm}\big|\psi(0)\big\rangle
.

Note that we have calculated all terms of
p_{\text{approx}}(t)
as given by Eq. (
11
). These terms are obtained from Eqs. (
12
) and (
13
), while
\epsilon
and
\gamma
are calculated using the method described in Sec.
2.2
. The framework described here avoids the need to calculate full expressions for the eigenvectors
\big|\lambda^{\pm}\big\rangle
. To determine the computational complexity of the search algorithm, we still need to identify the optimal running time, which is the value of
t_{\text{opt}}
that corresponds to the first peak of
p_{\text{approx}}(t)
, and the corresponding success probability, which is
p_{\text{approx}}(t_{\text{opt}})
.

The effectiveness of this framework hinges on the substantial overlap of both the initial state and the marked states with the space spanned by
\big|\lambda^{\pm}\big\rangle
. To analyze this, we can set a small upper bound for the terms not included in Eq. (
11
) concerning the initial condition. These missing terms are upper-bounded by

\displaystyle\sum_{\lambda\neq\lambda^{\pm}}\big|\big\langle\psi(0)\big|\lambda\big\rangle\big|^{2}\leqslant\frac{1}{\left(\lambda^{\circ}+\gamma\phi_{0}\right)^{2}}\sum_{\lambda\neq\lambda^{\pm}}\left(\lambda+\gamma\phi_{0}\right)^{2}\big|\big\langle\psi(0)\big|\lambda\big\rangle\big|^{2},

where
\lambda^{\circ}
represents the eigenvalue of
H
immediately following
\lambda^{-}
. Using Eq. (
5
) and taking

, we find
\left(\lambda+\gamma\phi_{0}\right)\big\langle\psi(0)\big|\lambda\big\rangle=-\sum_{w}\big\langle\psi(0)\big|w\big\rangle\big\langle w\big|\lambda\big\rangle
. This leads to the upper bound

\displaystyle\sum_{\lambda\neq\lambda^{\pm}}\big|\big\langle\psi(0)\big|\lambda\big\rangle\big|^{2}\leqslant\frac{|W|+2}{\left(\lambda^{\circ}+\gamma\phi_{0}\right)^{2}}\sum_{w,w^{\prime}\in W}\big\langle w\big|P_{0}\big|w^{\prime}\big\rangle.

Typically, the right-hand side tends to decrease. For example, in regular graphs, the summation on the right-hand side simplifies to
|W|^{2}/N
. If the gap between the eigenvalue
\lambda^{\circ}
and
-\gamma\phi_{0}
is sufficiently large, such that it diminishes more slowly than

, then the overlap
|\big\langle\psi(0)\big|\lambda\big\rangle|^{2}
for the excluded eigenvectors in Eq. (
11
) approaches zero. If the error approaches zero asymptotically, we can state that
p_{\text{exact}}(t)=p_{\text{approx}}(t)+o(1)
and the framework described in this work is successful.

There is an interesting case where the success probability
p_{\text{approx}}(t)
is the square of a sinusoidal function. This occurs when the two terms within the summation of Eq. (
11

\big\langle\lambda^{+}\big|\psi(0)\big\rangle\big\langle w\big|\lambda^{+}\big\rangle=-\big\langle\lambda^{-}\big|\psi(0)\big\rangle\big\langle w\big|\lambda^{-}\big\rangle+o(1)

(14)

for every

11

p_{\text{approx}}(t)=4\left|\big\langle\lambda^{-}\big|\psi(0)\big\rangle\right|^{2}\sum_{w\in W}\left|\big\langle w\big|\lambda^{-}\big\rangle\right|^{2}\sin^{2}{\epsilon t}+o(1)+o(\epsilon t).

(15)

From this, we can determine the optimal running time as

t_{\mathrm{run}}=\frac{\pi}{2\epsilon},

(16)

which shows that the gap between
\lambda^{\pm}
determines the optimal running time. A larger gap means a smaller runtime.
This expression leads to the success probability being

p_{\mathrm{succ}}=4\left|\big\langle\lambda^{-}\big|\psi(0)\big\rangle\right|^{2}\sum_{w\in W}\left|\big\langle w\big|\lambda^{-}\big\rangle\right|^{2}+o(1).

(17)

In the next section, we confirm that condition (
14
) applies to a quantum walk on the Johnson graph with two marked vertices. This condition is also observed in cases with one marked vertex
[
3

35
]
and multiple marked vertices
[

4
Final remarks

We have developed a framework to assess analytically the computational complexity of spatial search algorithms using continuous-time quantum walks on graphs with multiple marked vertices. This framework is underpinned by four propositions that not only lay the mathematical foundation but also offer a practical method for computing the eigenvalues and eigenvectors of the Hamiltonian. Additionally, these propositions facilitate the determination of the optimal value for the parameter
\gamma
in the search algorithm. A key step in this process involves calculating a basis of eigenvectors of the kernel of a symmetric matrix, the dimension of which equals the number of marked vertices. This method applies to any graph whose associated Hamiltonian exhibits a sufficiently small gap between
\lambda^{\pm}
. In simpler scenarios, the success probability of the search algorithm can be represented as
a^{2}\sin^{2}\epsilon t
, with
2\epsilon
usually being the gap. The optimal running time is
t=\pi/2\epsilon
, leading to a success probability
p_{\mathrm{succ}}=a^{2}
. For the search algorithm to achieve optimality, implying a time complexity of
O(\sqrt{N})
, it is crucial that
\epsilon
scales as
O(1/\sqrt{N})
and
a
maintains an order of
\Omega(1)
.

We demonstrated our framework with an analysis of the spatial search algorithm on the Johnson graph
J(n,k)
featuring two marked vertices. Our analysis indicates that, with
k
fixed and the distance
\delta
between the marked vertices known beforehand, the optimal running time is
\pi\sqrt{N}/(2\sqrt{2})
, where
N
is the total number of vertices. In cases where
\delta
is not known, the algorithm must be executed
k
times, each assuming a different
\delta
. This results in a total running time of at most
\pi k\sqrt{N}/(2\sqrt{2})
, maintaining a success probability of
1+o(1)
as
n
approaches infinity.

Usually, the condition for the search algorithm be optimal is that the spectral gap of the Hamiltonian is sufficiently larger than the overlap of the initial condition with the
marked vertex
[
20
]
. This condition holds true in the cases analyzed in the literature when there is only one marked vertex, to the best of our knowledge. The problem becomes more intricate in the presence of multiple marked vertices since the time complexity depends on specific vertex placements in the general case. As discussed above, in Johnson graphs with two marked vertices, the time complexity is influenced by the distance between the marked vertices.

The results of this work can accelerate numerical calculations of the computational complexity of search algorithms on arbitrary graphs. In this context, certain analytical expressions from Sections
2.2
and
2.3
can be circumvented. The numerical approach is outlined as follows. Start by determining numerically
\phi_{0}
and
\big|\phi_{0}\big\rangle
. Next, use the entries of matrix
M^{\lambda}
, as defined in Eq. (
3
), to identify
\lambda^{\pm}
and
\gamma
. Since they need to be found concurrently, we assign numerical values to both
\gamma
and
\lambda
and then check whether det
(M^{\lambda})=0
. If this is not the case, we change
\gamma
and
\lambda
until locating the roots. Following this,
\epsilon
is deduced from
|\lambda^{-}+\gamma\phi_{0}|
. We then we proceed to numerically calculate
\big\langle\lambda^{\pm}\big|\phi_{0}\big\rangle
and
\big\langle w\big|\lambda^{\pm}\big\rangle
for all
w
.
\big\langle\lambda^{+}\big|\phi_{0}\big\rangle
and
\big\langle w\big|\lambda^{+}\big\rangle
must be nonzero for at least one
w
, otherwise
\lambda^{+}
does not fulfill the required conditions. Afterwards, we compute the success probability
p(t)
using Eq. (
11
). By plotting
p(t)
, we can evaluate whether the curve resembles the square of a sinusoidal function and whether the peak is prominent.
