An Algebraic Characterisation of First-Order Logic with Neighbour PubID: pubid: 978-1-6654-4895-6/21/$31.00 ©2021 IEEE
Amaldev Manuel, Dhruv Nevatia

Abstract
We give an algebraic characterisation of first-order logic with the neighbour relation, on finite words. For this, we consider languages of finite words over alphabets with an involution on them. The natural algebras for such languages are involution semigroups. To characterise the logic, we define a special kind of semidirect product of involution semigroups, called the locally hermitian product. The characterisation theorem for FO with neighbour states that a language is definable in the logic if and only if it is recognised by a locally hermitian product of an aperiodic commutative involution semigroup, and a locally trivial involution semigroup. We then define the notion of involution varieties of languages, namely classes of languages closed under Boolean operations, quotients, involution, and inverse images of involutory morphisms. An Eilenberg-type correspondence is established between involution varieties of languages and pseudovarieties of involution semigroups.

Introduction

We give an algebraic characterisation of a logic over finite words, namely
the first-order logic with the neighbour relation
. Let

Obtaining an algebraic characterisation for the class

The characterisation of

In the case of

is the language

. As we have seen, this language is not in

. Therefore,

.

Our results

It was already observed in
[
8
]
that one needs to extend semigroups with an involution (also called
\star
-semigroups) — an involution on a semigroup
S
is an operation
\star
such that
(a^{\star})^{\star}=a
and
(ab)^{\star}=b^{\star}a^{\star}
, for each
a,b\in S
— to characterise
\mathrm{wLRTT}
. The involution operation is a generalisation of the reversal operation on words. An involutory alphabet
\mathscr{A}
is a finite alphabet
A
with a bijection
\dagger
on it. The map
\dagger
extends to words over
A
as
(a_{1}\ldots a_{n})^{\dagger}=a_{n}^{\dagger}\ldots a_{1}^{\dagger}
, where each
a_{i}\in A
. This map is an involution on the semigroup
A^{+}
. We lift the notions of recognisability and syntactic semigroups to the case of languages over involutory alphabets. Syntactic algebras for such languages are semigroups with an involution. A finite alphabet
A
can be seen as an involutory alphabet with the identity function
\mathit{id}
as
\dagger
; such alphabets are called
hermitian
. This just means that
A^{+}
is equipped with the reverse operation as involution. Syntactic algebras for languages over hermitian alphabets are semigroups with involutions that are generated by their hermitian elements (
a
is hermitian if
a^{\star}=a
).

Assume
(A,\dagger)
and
(B,\star)
are involutory alphabets and
h\colon A^{+}\rightarrow B^{+}
is a morphism. The morphism
h
is involutory if
h(w^{\dagger})=h(w)^{\star}
.
An
involution variety of languages
maps each involutory alphabet
\mathscr{A}=(A,\dagger)
to a class of languages over
A
that is closed under involutions, Boolean operations, quotients with respect to words and inverse images under involutory morphisms. We establish an Eilenberg-type correspondence between involution varieties of languages and pseudovarieties of finite involution semigroups — classes of finite involution semigroups that are closed under finite direct products, sub-
\star
-semigroups and quotients.

If
\mathscr{S}=(S,\star)
and
\mathscr{T}=(T,\dagger)
are involution semigroups, a two-sided action of
T
on
S
is
compatible
with the involution if
(tst^{\prime})^{\star}=t^{\prime\dagger}s^{\star}t^{\dagger}
, where
tst^{\prime}
denotes the left action by
t\in T
and right action by
t^{\prime}\in T
on the element
s\in S
. A compatible two-sided action of
T
on
S
is
locally hermitian
if for all idempotents
e\in T
and elements
s\in S
it holds that
ese^{\dagger}=es^{\star}e^{\dagger}
. Intuitively this means that within an
idempotent context that looks the same on the outside from either direction, one can reverse factors. Bilateral semidirect products with locally hermitian actions are called locally hermitian semidirect products. If
\mathbf{V}
and
\mathbf{W}
are pseudovarieties of involution semigroups, the locally hermitian product of
\mathbf{V}
and
\mathbf{W}
is the pseudovariety generated by all locally hermitian semidirect products of involution semigroups from
\mathbf{V}
and
\mathbf{W}
.
It is shown that a language is
\mathrm{wLRTT}
if and only if it is recognised by a locally hermitian semidirect product of an aperiodic commutative semigroup and a locally trivial semigroup. This result can also be stated in terms of the corresponding pseudovarieties of involution semigroups
\mathbf{Acom}^{\star}
and
\mathbb{L}\mathbf{1}^{\star}
. Therefore, we have an algebraic characterisation of the class
\mathrm{FO}(A,\mathit{min},\mathit{max},\mathit{N})
.

Related work

The
\mathrm{FO}
and
\mathrm{MSO}
logics using the predicates

\mathit{N}
and
\mathit{bet}
were introduced in
[
8
,
9
]
. Formulas of these logics that do not use the constants
\mathit{min}
and
\mathit{max}
, are self-dual, i.e., replacing each predicate by its dual, for instance

, results in an equivalent formula.
Therefore,
\mathrm{MSO}(A,\mathit{N})
and
\mathrm{MSO}(A,\mathit{bet})
recognise precisely the class of
reversible
regular languages — a language is reversible if it is invariant under taking the reverse of words in it. Similarly,
\mathrm{FO}(A,\mathit{bet})
recognises all aperiodic reversible languages. In
[
8
]
, the question of deciding membership in these classes was studied. For the logics
\mathrm{MSO}(A,\mathit{bet}),\mathrm{FO}(A,\mathit{bet})
, the existing decidable characterisations of
\mathrm{MSO}(A,<)
and
\mathrm{FO}(A,<)
immediately yield an answer. For the case of
\mathrm{FO}(A,\mathit{N})
, a partial answer was given; it was shown that if a language
L
is definable in
\mathrm{FO}(A,\mathit{N})
, then its syntactic semigroup satisfies the identity
ex^{\star}e^{\star}=exe^{\star}
, in addition to those defining the class
\mathrm{LTT}
.

A different but related
between
predicate
(namely
a(x,y)
, for
a\in A
, is true if there is an
a
-labelled position
between positions
x
and
y
) was introduced in

[
11
,
12
,
13
]
. They
characterise the expressive power of two-variable first-order logic with the order relation (
\mathrm{FO}^{2}(<)
)
enriched with the between predicates
a(x,y)
for
a\in A
, and show an
algebraic characterisation of the resulting family of languages.

Since the class of involution semigroups contains many important classes of semigroups such as inverse semigroups, there is substantial literature on varieties of involution semigroups from a semigroup theoretic perspective (see
[
6
]
for a survey). However we are not aware of a language theoretic study of pseudovarieties of finite involution semigroups or results similar to ours.

Orgranisation of the paper

In Section
II
, we describe involution semigroups and extend the notions of recognisability by semigroups to the case of languages with an involution. In Section
III
, involutory semidirect products are defined, and we introduce the notion of locally hermitian semidirect product. Then, it shown that a language is
\mathrm{wLRTT}
if and only if it is recognised by the locally hermitian semidirect product of an aperiodic commutative semigroup and a locally trivial semigroup. We introduce the notion of involution varieties of languages and pseudovarieties of involution semigroups, and prove an Eilenberg correspondence between them in Section

Languages over Involutory Alphabets

II-A

Recognisable languages

Let
A
be a finite alphabet. Then,
A^{+}
denotes the set of nonempty finite words over
A
, and
A^{*}
denotes the set of all finite words over
A
including the empty word
\epsilon
. Extending this notation,

denotes the subset of

consisting of words of length at most

, including
\epsilon

A semigroup

is a set together with an associative binary operation. Unless specified, the semigroup operation is written as

, for semigroup elements

; but we omit notating it whenever possible. If the operation has an identity, then

is called a monoid. All semigroups and monoids we consider are finite, except when stated otherwise.
For a finite alphabet

, the set

forms a semigroup under concatenation, while the set

is a monoid with the empty word
\epsilon
as the identity.
The set

forms a

of

if

is closed under the semigroup operation.

Let
(T,+)
be a semigroup. A

that satisfies the equation

, for all

If

and

are monoids, then

is a

if additionally

to the identity of

. A semigroup

is the image of a subsemigroup of

under some morphism.

Let
A
be a finite alphabet. A language

is

by a semigroup

if there is a morphism

and a subset

such that

. The set

[

]

The above definitions are easily adapted to the case of languages over

II-B

Languages over Involutory Alphabets

A finite
involutory alphabet

\mathscr{A}=(A,\dagger)
is a finite alphabet
A
with a bijection
\dagger\colon A\rightarrow A
such that
(a^{\dagger})^{\dagger}=a
for each letter
a\in A
. The function
\dagger
is extended to words over
A
as

w^{\dagger}=a_{n}^{\dagger}\ldots a_{1}^{\dagger}~,

(2)

if
w=a_{1}\cdots a_{n}\in A^{+}
. Clearly,
{w^{\dagger}}^{\dagger}=w
. Moreover,

, for all words

. Hence, the operation

is an

is a language, then

denotes the set

If

is the reverse operation

is the set

. Unless specified, a finite alphabet is taken to be a hermitian alphabet, i.e. with the reverse operation as the involution.

Definition 3

An involution semigroup (also called a

We say that

is

Example 1

Let

Let

However, the map

Proposition 1
.

If a language is recognised by a semigroup, then it is recognised by a

Proof.

Therefore, languages recognised by involution semigroups are precisely the class of recognisable languages.

Let

III
Involutory Semidirect Products and The Characterisation of FO

In this section, we first define the involutory semidirect product of two involution semigroups. Then, a particular case, called locally hermitian semidirect product, is defined. It is then shown that

\star
-semigroups and locally trivial
\star
-semigroups.

(4)

IV
Varieties of Involutory Languages

Next, we introduce the notion of varieties of involutory languages, and relate it to pseudovarieties of finite involution semigroups by proving an Eilenberg-type correspondence. For this purpose, we define the notion of a syntactic involution semigroup, and show that it possesses properties similar to that of syntactic semigroups. Finally, Theorem
2
is stated in terms of pseudovarieties of involution semigroups.

It is possible to state the results in this section in a much more general setting using categories, following
[
3
,
4
]
, where
the notions of recognisability, languages and language varieties etc. are developed for
monads and a variety theorem for them is proved. Variety theorems of many settings, for instance finite/infinite words, finite trees etc. can be seen as instances of this unified result.
In this framework, the involution operation is a monad that satisfies a certain distributive law. One advantage of this approach is that the variety theorem for involutary languages can be deduced from the general case. But it also requires the significantly more complex language of categories. For the sake of simplicity we do the variety theorem in the usual way.

V
Conclusion

The membership problem for a class of languages
\mathcal{C}
asks: is the given regular language a member of
\mathcal{C}
. The membership question is decidable for many families, the archetypical example is the class of aperiodic languages
[
20
]
. The membership problem for locally threshold testable (
\mathrm{LTT}
) languages is equivalent to the problem of finding out if a given semigroup divides a semigroup in
\mathbf{Acom}*\mathbb{L}\mathbf{1}
. Since
\mathbf{V}*\mathbb{L}\mathbf{1}=\mathbf{V}*\mathbf{D}

[
21
]
(where
\mathbf{D}
is the pseudovariety of
definite
semigroups that satisfies
se=e
for all
e^{2}=e
) the problem is slightly simplified.
There are two main approaches to solving this problem, the first one is using the Derived
Category theorem
[
25
,
24
,
18
,
22
]
and the other is using Presburger arithmetic
[
2
,
19
]
. Both these approaches crucially depend on the fact that a semigroup
S
divides a semigroup in
\mathbf{V}*\mathbf{D}
if and only if it divides a semigroup in
\mathbf{V}*\mathbf{D}_{n}
(pseudovariety
\mathbf{D}_{n}
satisfies the identity
yx_{1}\cdots x_{n}=x_{1}\cdots x_{n}
), where
n=|S|

[
21
]
. This is known as the delay theorem.

The key idea behind the delay theorem is that given a finite semigroup
S
, one can insert an idempotent in any product
x_{1}\cdots x_{n}
,
x_{i}\in S
, of length
n=|S|
without affecting the value of the product. This construction is not a left-to-right symmetric one. Hence, it is not clear if a delay theorem holds in the case of involutory semidirect products. This is the major hurdle in obtaining the decidability of membership of
\mathrm{wLRTT}
.

The algebraic characterisation of the class
\mathrm{wLRTT}
presented in this work, is not decidable, i.e., the characterisation does not yield an algorithm to check if a given recognisable language is
\mathrm{wLRTT}
or not. In the case of
\mathrm{LTT}
languages, the decidability is obtained by an algebraic characterisation in terms of categories
[
25
]
. The next logical step in our work is to extend the theory to categories.
