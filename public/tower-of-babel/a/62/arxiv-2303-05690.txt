Introduction and Main results

In this article, we are concerned with existence and concentration behaviour of solutions for the following singularly perturbed coupled fractional Schrödinger equations

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\epsilon(-\Delta)^{\frac{1}{2}}\varphi+V(x)\varphi=\displaystyle\frac{\partial K(\varphi,\psi)}{\partial\psi}\;\text{in}\;\mathbb{R},\\
\epsilon(-\Delta)^{\frac{1}{2}}\psi+V(x)\psi=\displaystyle\frac{\partial K(\varphi,\psi)}{\partial\varphi}\;\text{in}\;\mathbb{R},\\
\end{cases}

(1.1)

where
(-\Delta)^{\frac{1}{2}}
is the fractional Laplacian operator, defined for a measurable function
u:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
by

(-\Delta)^{\frac{1}{2}}u(x)=\frac{1}{\pi}P.V.\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{u(x)-u(y)}{|y|^{2}}dy=-\frac{1}{2\pi}P.V.\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{u(x+y)+u(x-y)-2u(x)}{|y|^{2}}dy

and
\epsilon>0
. The external Schrödinger potential satisfies the following condition:

(V)

V\in C(\mathbb{R},~\mathbb{R})
and
0<V_{0}:=\inf_{\mathbb{R}}V(x)<\lim_{|x|\rightarrow\infty}V(x)=V_{\infty}<\infty.

The Hamiltonian
K
is of the form
K(\varphi,\psi)=G(\psi)-F(\varphi)
, with
\displaystyle F(t)=\int\limits_{0}^{t}f(s)ds
and
\displaystyle G(t)=\int\limits_{0}^{t}g(s)ds
and the nonlinearities
f,~g
satisfy the following assumptions:

(H_{1})

f
and
g
are continuous functions.

(H_{2})

f(t)=o(t^{2})
and
g(t)=o(t^{2})
as
t\rightarrow 0
.

(H_{3})

There exists
\mu>2
such that for any
t\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}

\displaystyle 0\leq\mu F(t)\leq tf(t)~\text{and}~0\leq\mu G(t)\leq g(t)t,

where
F(t)=\displaystyle\int\limits_{0}^{t}f(s)ds
and
G(t)=\displaystyle\int\limits_{0}^{t}g(s)ds
.

(H_{4})

There exists
M>0
such that for any
t\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}

\displaystyle 0<F(t)\leq M|f(t)|~\text{and}~0<G(t)\leq M|g(t)|.

(H_{5})

f(t)/|t|
and
g(t)/|t|
are strictly increasing in
\mathbb{R}
.

We know from classical Sobolev embedding that
H^{\frac{1}{2}}(\mathbb{R})\subseteq L^{p}(\mathbb{R})
for all
p\in[2,\infty)
, but
H^{\frac{1}{2}}(\mathbb{R})\not\subseteq L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
. The following result of Ozawa
[
31
]
, later extended by Iula et al.
[
26
]
(see also
[
22
]
):

Lemma 1.1

For any
\beta>0
and
u\in H^{\frac{1}{2}}(\mathbb{R})
, we have

\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}}(\exp(\beta u^{2}-1))dx<\infty

(1.2)

shows that
H^{\frac{1}{2}}(\mathbb{R})
is also embedded in a Orlicz space of exponential growth type.
Moreover the optimal constant in the embedding is given by

\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}u\in H^{\frac{1}{2}}(\mathbb{R})\\
\|u\|_{\frac{1}{2}}\leq 1\end{subarray}}\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}}(\exp(\beta u^{2}-1))dx\begin{cases}<\infty~&\text{if}~\beta\leq\pi,\\
=\infty~&\text{if}~\beta>\pi.\end{cases}

From Lemma
1.1
, it is not difficult to show that for any
p\geq 1
and for any
K\subset\subset\mathbb{R}
, the map
H^{\frac{1}{2}}(\mathbb{R})\ni u\mapsto e^{u^{2}}\in L^{p}(K)
is continuous with respect to the strong topology but not relatively compact with respect to the weak topology.
According to Lemma
1.1
, we give the definition of critical exponential growth:

(H_{6})

(
critical exponential growth
) There exists
\beta_{0}>0
such that

\lim\limits_{t\rightarrow+\infty}\frac{f(t)}{e^{\beta t^{2}}}=\begin{cases}0~&\text{if}~\beta>\beta_{0},\\
+\infty~&\text{if}~\beta<\beta_{0}\end{cases}~\text{and}~\lim\limits_{t\rightarrow+\infty}\frac{g(t)}{e^{\beta t^{2}}}=\begin{cases}0~&\text{if}~\beta>\beta_{0},\\
+\infty~&\text{if}~\beta<\beta_{0}.\end{cases}

Due to the critical behaviour in
(H_{6})
that induces the lack of weak compactness in the Trudinger-Moser embedding, the minimizing sequences do not satisfy in general the Palais smale type condition. To overcome this difficulty, we assume the following growth condition for getting the compactness of suitable associated Palais Smale sequences:

(H_{7})

There exists
\kappa_{0}
and
r_{1}
positive such that

\displaystyle\liminf\limits_{|t|\rightarrow\infty}f(t)te^{-\beta_{0}t^{2}}\geq\kappa_{0}>

\displaystyle\max\left\{\frac{8e^{\frac{1}{2}}}{\beta_{0}}V_{0},\frac{\pi}{\beta_{0}r_{1}}\right\}

\displaystyle\text{and}~\liminf\limits_{|t|\rightarrow\infty}g(t)te^{-\beta_{0}t^{2}}\geq\kappa_{0}>\max\left\{\frac{8e^{\frac{1}{2}}}{\beta_{0}}V_{0},\frac{\pi}{\beta_{0}r_{1}}\right\}.

We also emphasize that the associated energy functional for Hamitonian elliptic systems is strongly indefinite, that is, its quadratic part is respectively coercive and anti-coercive in infinite dimensional subspaces of the energy space. This poses additional difficulties to get a priori estimates in the study of such systems.

In this paper, we will focus on the existence of the minimal energy solutions, the so-called ground states. These solutions play a fundamental role both from the theoretical point of view as well as from the physical meaning (see for instance
[
3
]

2
Functional setting and generalized Nehari manifold

We recall that Sobolev space
H^{\frac{1}{2}}(\mathbb{R})
is the collection

H^{\frac{1}{2}}(\mathbb{R})=\left\{u\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}):\frac{u(x)-u(y)}{|x-y|}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R})\right\},

endowed with the inner product

\displaystyle\langle u,v\rangle_{\frac{1}{2}}=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}}\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{(u(x)-u(y))(v(x)-v(y))}{|x-y|^{2}}dxdy+\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}}V_{0}uvdx,~u,v\in H^{\frac{1}{2}}(\mathbb{R}),

and the associated norm is given by
\displaystyle\|u\|_{\frac{1}{2}}^{2}=\langle u,u\rangle_{\frac{1}{2}}.

Remark 2.1

In view of
[
19
, Proposition 3.6]
, we also have

\displaystyle\|(-\Delta)^{\frac{1}{4}}u\|_{L^{2}}^{2}:=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}}\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{|u(x)-u(y)|^{2}}{|x-y|^{2}}dxdy,~\text{for all}~u\in H^{\frac{1}{2}}(\mathbb{R}).

Here the fractional Laplacian
(-\Delta)^{\frac{1}{4}}
of a function
u:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
in the Schwartz class is defined by

(-\Delta)^{\frac{1}{4}}u=\mathcal{F}^{-1}(|\xi|^{\frac{1}{2}}(\mathcal{F}u)),~\text{for all }~\xi\in\mathbb{R},

where
\mathcal{F}
denotes the Fourier transform

\mathcal{F}(u)(\xi)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}}e^{-i\xi.x}u(x)dx.

Thus we also have

\|u\|_{\frac{1}{2}}^{2}=\|(-\Delta)^{\frac{1}{4}}u\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+V_{0}\|u\|_{L^{2}}^{2}.

The natural space that contains all the solutions of problem (
1.3
) is the space
W=H^{\frac{1}{2}}(\mathbb{R})\times H^{\frac{1}{2}}(\mathbb{R})
with the inner product

\langle w_{1},w_{2}\rangle_{W}:=\langle u_{1},u_{2}\rangle_{\frac{1}{2}}+\langle v_{1},v_{2}\rangle_{\frac{1}{2}},~w_{k}=(u_{k},v_{k})\in W,~k=1,2,

and the associated norm
\|w\|^{2}_{W}=\|(u,v)\|^{2}_{W}=\|u\|_{\frac{1}{2}}^{2}+\|v\|_{\frac{1}{2}}^{2}.

We introduce the following subspaces

\displaystyle W^{+}:=\{(u,u)|~u\in H^{\frac{1}{2}}(\mathbb{R})\}~\text{and}~W^{-}:=\{(u,-u)|~u\in H^{\frac{1}{2}}(\mathbb{R})\}.

Then for each
w=(u,v)\in W
, we have the straightforward decomposition:

w=w^{+}+w^{-}=\left((u+v)/2,(u+v)/2\right)+\left((u-v)/2,(v-u)/2\right).

(2.1)

Thus it is clear that
W=W^{+}\oplus W^{-}
.

We define the associated energy functional to problem (
1.3
) by

\mathcal{J}(w)=\mathcal{J}(u,v):=\langle u,v\rangle_{\frac{1}{2}}-\Phi(w),~w\in W,

where
\displaystyle\Phi(w)=\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}}(F(u)+G(v))dx
. By the hypothesis on
f
and
g
, we see that

\displaystyle\Phi(0)=0,~\langle\Phi^{\prime}(w),w\rangle>2\Phi(w)>0,~\text{for all}~w\in W\setminus\{0\}.

Also using Lemma
1.1
and from standard arguments, we can see that
\mathcal{J}
is well-defined and of class
C^{1}
with

\displaystyle\langle\mathcal{J}^{\prime}(u,v),(\varphi,\psi)\rangle=\langle u,\psi\rangle_{\frac{1}{2}}+\langle v,\varphi\rangle_{\frac{1}{2}}-\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}}(f(u)\varphi+g(v)\psi)dx,~\varphi,~\psi\in H^{\frac{1}{2}}(\mathbb{R}).

(2.2)

Definition 2.2

We call
w=(u,v)
a weak solution of (
1.3
) if
w\in W
and is a critical point of the associated energy functional
\mathcal{J}
.

Some remarks are in order:

Remark 2.3

1.

Take
w=(u,v)\in W
. Then using the decomposition in (
2.1
), we easily see that

\mathcal{J}(w)=\frac{1}{2}\|w^{+}\|_{W}^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\|w^{-}\|_{W}^{2}-\Phi(w),

which shows that
\mathcal{J}
is a strongly indefinite functional.

2.

If
w=(u,v)\in W\setminus\{0\}
and
\mathcal{J}^{\prime}(w)=0
, then by
(H_{3})

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}(w)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{J}(w)-\frac{1}{2}\langle\mathcal{J}^{\prime}(w),w\rangle

\displaystyle=\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}}\left(\frac{1}{2}f(u)u-F(u)\right)dx+\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}}\left(\frac{1}{2}g(u)u-G(u)\right)dx>0.

On the other hand, if
w=(u,-u)\in W^{-}
, we have by
(H_{3})
that

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}(w)=-\|u\|_{\frac{1}{2}}^{2}-\Phi(w)\leq 0.

Consequently, if
w\in W
is a nontrivial critical point of
\mathcal{J}
, then
w\in W\setminus W^{-}
.

The ground state solutions of (
1.3
) are obtained by minimizing the energy functional
\mathcal{J}
over the generalized Nehari manifold

\mathcal{N}=\left\{w\in W\setminus W^{-}:\langle\mathcal{J}^{\prime}(w),w\rangle=0,~\langle\mathcal{J}^{\prime}(w),\varphi\rangle=0,~\text{for all }~\varphi\in W^{-}\right\}

whose type was initially introduced by
[
32
, Pankov]
. In view of Remark
2.3
, we see that any nontrivial critical point of
\mathcal{J}
in fact lies in
\mathcal{N}
. The ground state solutions will be obtained as nontrivial critical points of
\mathcal{J}
in
\mathcal{N}
. To obtain such points, for any
w\in W\setminus W^{-}
, we set

\widetilde{W}(w):=W^{-}\oplus\mathbb{R}^{+}w,

where
\mathbb{R}^{+}:=\{t\in\mathbb{R}:~t\geq 0\}
. Then observe that

\widetilde{W}(w)=W^{-}\oplus\mathbb{R}^{+}w^{+}=\widetilde{W}(w^{+}).

We note that the various results proved in
[
15
, Section 2]
for the local system in dimension two can be straightforwardly extended for the nonlocal system (
1.3

\displaystyle\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}}\left(\frac{1}{2}f({u}_{0}){u}_{0}-F({u}_{0})\right)dx+\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}}\left(\frac{1}{2}g({v}_{0}){v}_{0}-G({v}_{0})\right)dx

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}(w_{0})-\frac{1}{2}\langle\mathcal{J}^{\prime}(w_{0}),w_{0}\rangle=\mathcal{J}(w_{0}).

(4.5)

On the other hand, since
w_{0}\not\equiv 0
and
\mathcal{J}^{\prime}(w_{0})=0
, one has
\mathcal{J}(w_{0})\geq m^{\ast}
. Thus
w_{0}\in\mathcal{S}
. Next we prove that
w_{n}\rightarrow w_{0}
in
W
. By (
4
) and
\mathcal{J}(w_{0})=m^{\ast}
, we have

\lim\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}}\left(\frac{1}{2}f({u}_{n}){u}_{n}-F({u}_{n})\right)dx=\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}}\left(\frac{1}{2}f({u}_{0}){u}_{0}-F({u}_{0})\right)dx

and

\lim\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}}\left(\frac{1}{2}g({v}_{n}){v}_{n}-G({v}_{n})\right)dx=\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}}\left(\frac{1}{2}g({v}_{0}){v}_{0}-G({v}_{0})\right)dx.

Now by
(H_{3})
, we get

\displaystyle 0\leq\frac{\mu-2}{2}F(u_{n})\leq\frac{1}{2}f(u_{n})u_{n}-F(u_{n}),~0\leq\frac{\mu-2}{2}G(v_{n})\leq\frac{1}{2}g(v_{n})v_{n}-G(v_{n}).

Thus by the dominated convergence theorem, we have

\displaystyle\lim\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}}F(u_{n})dx=\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}}F(u_{0})dx,~\lim\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}}G(v_{n})dx=\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}}G(v_{0})dx.

and so

\displaystyle\lim\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{1}{2}f({u}_{n}){u}_{n}dx=\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{1}{2}f({u}_{0}){u}_{0}dx,~\lim\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{1}{2}g({v}_{n}){v}_{n}dx=\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{1}{2}g({v}_{0}){v}_{0}dx.

(4.6)

Now from
\displaystyle\langle J^{\prime}(u_{n},v_{n}),(0,u_{n})\rangle=0
and
\displaystyle\langle J^{\prime}(u_{0},v_{0}),(0,u_{n})\rangle=0
, we obtain

\|u_{n}\|_{\frac{1}{2}}^{2}=\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}}g(v_{n})u_{n}dx~\text{and}~\langle u_{n},u_{0}\rangle_{\frac{1}{2}}=\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}}g(v_{0})u_{n}dx.

(4.7)

By using the fact that
u_{n}\rightharpoonup u_{0}
weakly in
H^{\frac{1}{2}}(\mathbb{R})
, we conclude that

\lim\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}}g(v_{0})u_{n}dx=\|u_{0}\|_{\frac{1}{2}}^{2}.

(4.8)

Combining (
4.7
) and (
4.8
) to prove the required convergence, it is sufficient to show that

\lim\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}}(g(v_{n})-g(v_{0}))u_{n}dx=0.

(4.9)

We divide the proof of (
4.9
) into two steps:

Step 1.
In this step we prove

\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}}|g(v_{n})-g(v_{0})|dx\rightarrow 0,~\text{as}~n\rightarrow\infty.

(4.10)

Using Proposition
3.1
and Vitali’s convergence theorem, we can see that
\{g(v_{n})\}
is relatively compact in
L^{1}_{\text{loc}}(\mathbb{R})
. Thus to prove (
4.10
) it is sufficient to show that there exists
R^{\prime}>0
large enough such that

\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}\setminus B_{R^{\prime}}(0)}|g(v_{n})|dx\leq\epsilon.

Indeed using
(H_{2})
there exists
\delta>0
such that

|g(t)|\leq\epsilon t^{2},~\text{for}~|t|\leq\delta.

(4.11)

Now for any
R>0
and
K>0
, using Proposition
3.1
and (
4.11
) we have

\displaystyle\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}\setminus B_{R}(0)}|g(v_{n})|dx=

\displaystyle\int\limits_{\{x\in\mathbb{R}\setminus B_{R}(0):~|v_{n}(x)|\leq\delta\}}|g(v_{n})|dx+\int\limits_{\{x\in\mathbb{R}\setminus B_{R}(0):~\delta\leq|v_{n}(x)|\leq K\}}|g(v_{n})|dx

\displaystyle+\int\limits_{\{x\in\mathbb{R}\setminus B_{R}(0):~|v_{n}(x)|\geq K\}}|g(v_{n})|dx

\displaystyle\leq\epsilon\|v_{n}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}^{2}+\int\limits_{\{x\in\mathbb{R}\setminus B_{R}(0):~\delta\leq|v_{n}(x)|\leq K\}}|g(v_{n})|dx+\frac{C}{K}.

(4.12)

Again using (
4.6
) for any
\epsilon^{\prime}>0
, we have for
n
large

\displaystyle\int\limits_{\{x\in\mathbb{R}\setminus B_{R}(0):~\delta\leq|v_{n}(x)|\leq K\}}|g(v_{n})|dx\leq\frac{1}{\delta}\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}\setminus B_{R}(0)}g(v_{n})v_{n}dx\leq\frac{1}{\delta}\left(\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}\setminus B_{R}(0)}g(v_{0})v_{0}dx+\epsilon^{\prime}\right).

(4.13)

From (
4
), (
4.13
) and again employing Proposition
3.1
, we conclude for
R^{\prime}>0
,
K^{\prime}>0
and
n
large enough that

\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}\setminus B_{R^{\prime}}(0)}|g(v_{n})|dx\leq\epsilon.

This completes Step 1.

Step 2.
In this step we complete the proof of (
4.9
). Now for any
K>0
, we have

\displaystyle\left|\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}}(g(v_{n})-g(v_{0}))u_{n}dx\right|\leq K\int\limits_{|u_{n}|\leq K}|g(v_{n})-g(v_{0})|dx+\int\limits_{|u_{n}|\geq K}|g(v_{n})-g(v_{0})||u_{n}|dx\leq KI_{1}+I_{2},

where

I_{1}=\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}}|g(v_{n})-g(v_{0})|dx~\text{and}~I_{2}=\int\limits_{\{|u_{n}|\geq K\}}|g(v_{n})-g(v_{0})||u_{n}|dx.

Using Step 1, we see that
I_{1}\rightarrow 0
as
n\rightarrow\infty
. Now we estimate
I_{2}
. We have

\displaystyle I_{2}\leq

\displaystyle\int\limits_{\{|u_{n}|\geq K,|v_{n}|\leq\delta\}}|g(v_{n})u_{n}|dx+\int\limits_{\{|u_{n}|\geq K,\delta\leq|v_{n}|\leq K^{\prime}\}}|g(v_{n})u_{n}|dx+\int\limits_{\{|u_{n}|\geq K,|v_{n}|\geq K^{\prime}\}}|g(v_{n})u_{n}|dx

\displaystyle+\frac{C}{K}\|u_{n}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}^{2},

where
K^{\prime}>0
and
\delta>0
is chosen so that by
(H_{1})
, we have

g(t)\leq\epsilon t,~\text{for}~|t|\leq\delta.

Thus by
(H_{1})
, Hölder’s inequality and Proposition
3.1
, we have

I_{2}\leq C\epsilon+\frac{2C}{K}+I_{2,1},

where

I_{2,1}=\int\limits_{\{|u_{n}|\geq K,|v_{n}|\geq K^{\prime}\}}|g(v_{n})u_{n}|dx.

Lastly, we will estimate
I_{2,1}.

We need the following inequality (see
[
18
, Lemma 4.1]
)

st\leq t^{2}(e^{t^{2}}-1)+|s|(\log|s|)^{\frac{1}{2}},~\text{for all}~t\in\mathbb{R},~|s|\geq e^{\frac{1}{\sqrt[3]{4}}}.

(4.14)

Since from
(H_{6})
,
f
and
g
have
\beta_{0}
-critical growth of Trudinger-Moser type and hence for any fixed
\epsilon>0
, there exists
C_{\epsilon}>0
such that

|f(t)|,~|g(t)|\leq C_{\epsilon}e^{(\beta_{0}+\epsilon)t^{2}},~\text{for}~t\in\mathbb{R}.

(4.15)

By Proposition
3.3
, choosing
\delta>0
such that
m^{\ast}\in\left(0,\frac{\pi}{\beta_{0}}-\delta\right)
and taking

\hat{u}_{n}=\left(\frac{\pi}{\beta_{0}}-\delta\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\frac{u_{n}}{\|u_{n}\|_{\frac{1}{2}}},

we can write

\displaystyle\int\limits_{\{|u_{n}|\geq K,|v_{n}|\geq K^{\prime}\}}|g(v_{n})u_{n}|dx=\frac{\|u_{n}\|_{\frac{1}{2}}}{\left(\frac{\pi}{\beta_{0}}-\delta\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}}\int\limits_{\{|u_{n}|\geq K,|v_{n}|\geq K^{\prime}\}}|g(v_{n})||\hat{u}_{n}|dx=\frac{\|u_{n}\|_{\frac{1}{2}}}{\left(\frac{\pi}{\beta_{0}}-\delta\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}}I_{2,1}^{\prime},

(4.16)

where

I_{2,1}^{\prime}=\frac{C_{\epsilon}}{\sqrt{\beta_{0}}}\int\limits_{\{|u_{n}|\geq K,|v_{n}|\geq K^{\prime}\}}\frac{g(v_{n})}{C_{\epsilon}}\sqrt{\beta_{0}}|\hat{u}_{n}|dx.

Setting

A_{n}:=\{x\in\mathbb{R}:|u_{n}(x)|\geq K,~|v_{n}(x)|\geq K^{\prime},~|g(v_{n}(x))|/C_{\epsilon}\geq e^{\frac{1}{\sqrt[3]{4}}}\}

and

B_{n}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}:|u_{n}(x)|\geq K,~|v_{n}(x)|\geq K^{\prime},~|g(v_{n}(x))|/C_{\epsilon}\leq e^{\frac{1}{\sqrt[3]{4}}}\},

and using inequality (
4.14
) with
s=g(v_{n})/C_{\epsilon}
and
t=\sqrt{\beta_{0}}|\hat{u}_{n}|
, we can estimate

\displaystyle I_{2,1}^{\prime}\leq\frac{C_{\epsilon}}{\sqrt{\beta_{0}}}\int\limits_{A_{n}}\frac{|g(v_{n})|}{C_{\epsilon}}\left[\log\left(\frac{|g(v_{n})|}{C_{\epsilon}}\right)\right]^{\frac{1}{2}}dx+\int\limits_{B_{n}}|g(v_{n})\hat{u}_{n}|dx+C_{\epsilon}\sqrt{\beta_{0}}\int\limits_{A_{n}}\hat{u}_{n}^{2}(e^{\beta_{0}\hat{u}_{n}^{2}}-1)dx.

Using (
4.15
), we get

\displaystyle I_{2,1}^{\prime}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\frac{\beta_{0}+\epsilon}{\beta_{0}}}\int\limits_{A_{n}}g(v_{n})v_{n}dx+\int\limits_{B_{n}}|g(v_{n})\hat{u}_{n}|dx+C_{\epsilon}\sqrt{\beta_{0}}\int\limits_{A_{n}}\hat{u}_{n}^{2}(e^{\beta_{0}\hat{u}_{n}^{2}}-1)dx.

(4.17)

Using (
4.6
), we see that the first integral is uniformly small for
K^{\prime}
large enough. Now

\displaystyle\frac{\|u_{n}\|_{\frac{1}{2}}}{\left(\frac{\pi}{\beta_{0}}-\delta\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}}\int\limits_{B_{n}}|g(v_{n})||\hat{u}_{n}|dx\leq C_{\epsilon}e^{\frac{1}{\sqrt[3]{4}}}\int\limits_{B_{n}}|u_{n}|dx\leq\frac{C}{K}\int\limits_{B_{n}}|u_{n}|^{2}dx\leq\frac{C^{\prime\prime}}{K}

which can be made sufficiently small for
K
large enough. Lastly, we have

\displaystyle\int\limits_{A_{n}}\hat{u}_{n}^{2}(e^{\beta_{0}\hat{u}_{n}^{2}}-1)dx\leq\frac{1}{K}\int\limits_{A_{n}}\hat{u}_{n}^{3}(e^{\beta_{0}\hat{u}_{n}^{2}}-1)dx.

Since
\displaystyle\|\hat{u}_{n}\|_{\frac{1}{2}}^{2}=\frac{\pi}{\beta_{0}}-\delta
, there exists
p>1
such that
\displaystyle p\beta_{0}\left(\frac{\pi}{\beta_{0}}-\delta\right)<\pi
. Thus by Trudinger-Moser inequality, as
n\rightarrow\infty
,

\int\limits_{A_{n}}\hat{u}_{n}^{2}(e^{\beta_{0}\hat{u}_{n}^{2}}-1)dx\leq\frac{1}{K}\left(\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}}|\hat{u}_{n}|^{3q}dx\right)^{\frac{1}{q}}\left(\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}}(e^{p\beta_{0}\hat{u}_{n}^{2}}-1)dx\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}

where
\displaystyle\frac{1}{p}+\frac{1}{q}=1
, which can be made arbitrarily small for
K
sufficiently large. Hence using (
4.16
), (
4.17
) and Proposition
3.1
we see that
I_{2,1}
can be made arbitrarily small for
K
and
K^{\prime}
large enough. This completes the proof of (
4.9
). Thus

\|u_{n}\|_{\frac{1}{2}}\rightarrow\|u_{0}\|_{\frac{1}{2}},~\text{as}~n\rightarrow\infty

and hence
u_{n}\rightarrow u_{0}
strongly in
H^{\frac{1}{2}}(\mathbb{R})
. Similarly,
v_{n}\rightarrow v_{0}
strongly in
H^{\frac{1}{2}}(\mathbb{R})
. This proves that
\mathcal{S}
is compact up to translation.
\square

5
Pohožaev type identity

In this subsection, we assume
f,g
are locally lipschitz and establish a Pohožaev type identity (
1.4
) for the system (
1.3
) following the approach in
[
6
]
.
For
\epsilon\in(0,1]
we construct a family of functions
\{w_{\epsilon}\}_{\epsilon}
which approximate
w=(u,v)\in\mathcal{S}
. For
\epsilon\in(0,1]

w_{\epsilon}=(u_{\epsilon},v_{\epsilon})
is the solution of the following system

\displaystyle\begin{cases}(-\Delta)^{\frac{1}{2}}u_{\epsilon}+V_{0}u_{\epsilon}=g_{\epsilon}\;\text{in}\;\mathbb{R},\\
(-\Delta)^{\frac{1}{2}}v_{\epsilon}+V_{0}v_{\epsilon}=f_{\epsilon}\;\text{in}\;\mathbb{R}.\\
\end{cases}

(5.1)

where the choice of
f_{\epsilon}
,
g_{\epsilon}
is provided by the following lemma (see
[
6
, Lemma 3.1]
):

Lemma 5.1

Let
\mu
be a standard compactly supported positive mollifier and for
\epsilon\in(0,1]
assume

\mu_{\epsilon}(x)=\frac{1}{\epsilon}\mu\left(\frac{x}{\epsilon}\right),~x\in\mathbb{R}.

Again let
\hslash\in C_{0}^{\infty}(B_{1})
be a function such that

0\leq\hslash\leq 1,~\hslash\equiv 1~\text{on}~B_{\frac{1}{2}},~|\hslash^{\prime}|\leq C.

For every
\epsilon\in(0,1]
, we define
\hslash_{\epsilon}(x)=\hslash(\epsilon x)
, for
x\in\mathbb{R}
. Now define the operator
\mathcal{H}_{\epsilon}:H^{\frac{-1}{2}}(\mathbb{R})\rightarrow H^{\frac{-1}{2}}(\mathbb{R})
as

\langle\mathcal{H}_{\epsilon}(\Lambda),\psi\rangle:=\langle\Lambda,\hslash_{\epsilon}(\psi\ast\mu_{\epsilon})\rangle,~\text{for every }~\psi\in H^{\frac{1}{2}}(\mathbb{R}).

Then
\mathcal{H}_{\epsilon}(\Lambda)\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
for every
\Lambda\in H^{\frac{-1}{2}}(\mathbb{R})
and

\lim\limits_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0}\|\mathcal{H}_{\epsilon}(\Lambda)-\Lambda\|_{\frac{-1}{2}}=0.

Recalling that
f(u),~g(v)\in L_{\text{loc}}^{1}(\mathbb{R})\cap H^{\frac{-1}{2}}(\mathbb{R})
and using the above lemma we define

f_{\epsilon}=\mathcal{H}_{\epsilon}(f(u))=\left(f(u)\hslash_{\epsilon}\right)\ast\mu_{\epsilon}~\text{and}~g_{\epsilon}=\mathcal{H}_{\epsilon}(g(v))=\left(g(v)\hslash_{\epsilon}\right)\ast\mu_{\epsilon}.

Then we have the following

f_{\epsilon}\rightarrow f(u)~\text{and}~g_{\epsilon}\rightarrow g(v)~\text{~}\text{strongly in }~H^{\frac{-1}{2}}(\mathbb{R}),

(5.2)

\sup\limits_{\epsilon\in(0,1]}(\|f_{\epsilon}\|_{\infty}+\|g_{\epsilon}\|_{\infty})\leq C<+\infty,~f_{\epsilon}\rightarrow f(u),~g_{\epsilon}\rightarrow g(v)~\text{in}~L_{\text{loc}}^{p}(\mathbb{R}),~\text{for every}~p\geq 1,

(5.3)

\text{and}~\sup\limits_{\epsilon\in(0,1]}(\|f_{\epsilon}\|_{\frac{1}{2}}+\|g_{\epsilon}\|_{\frac{1}{2}})\leq D<+\infty.

(5.4)

Equation (
5.2
) follows from Lemma
5.1
, while equation (
5.3
) follows using the fact that
u,v\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
. Now to prove (
5.4
) we use the Fourier transform (denoted by
\mathcal{F}
). We first note that for
\xi\in\mathbb{R}
:

\displaystyle|\xi|^{\frac{1}{2}}|\mathcal{F}(f_{\epsilon})(\xi)|=|\xi|^{\frac{1}{2}}|\mathcal{F}((f(u)\hslash_{\epsilon})\ast\mu_{\epsilon})(\xi)|=|\xi|^{\frac{1}{2}}|\mathcal{F}((f(u)\hslash_{\epsilon}))(\xi)||\mathcal{F}(\mu_{\epsilon})(\xi)|\leq C|\xi|^{\frac{1}{2}}|\mathcal{F}((f(u)\hslash_{\epsilon}))(\xi)|,

where
C>0
is a constant independent of
\epsilon
. Thus to prove (
5.4
) it is sufficient to show that (
5.4
) holds for
f(u)\hslash_{\epsilon}
. Now using (
5.3
), that
u\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
and since
f
is locally Liptschitz we get that

\displaystyle\|f(u)\hslash_{\epsilon}\|_{\frac{1}{2}}^{2}=

\displaystyle\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}}\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{|f(u(x))\hslash_{\epsilon}(x)-f(u(y))\hslash_{\epsilon}(y)|^{2}}{|x-y|^{2}}dxdy

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}}\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{|f(u(x))-f(u(y))|^{2}}{|x-y|^{2}}dxdy+C\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}}\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{|\hslash_{\epsilon}(x)-\hslash_{\epsilon}(y)|^{2}}{|x-y|^{2}}dxdy

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}}\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{|u(x)-u(y)|^{2}}{|x-y|^{2}}dxdy+C\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}}\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{|\hslash(x)-\hslash(y)|^{2}}{|x-y|^{2}}dxdy<+\infty.

Similarly (
5.4
) holds for
g_{\epsilon}
.

The next proposition gives the existence of the family
\{w_{\epsilon}\}_{\epsilon}
.

Proposition 5.2

For
\epsilon\in(0,1]
, the minimization problem

j:=\inf\limits_{z=(\varphi,\psi)\in W}J_{\epsilon}(z),

has a unique solution
w_{\epsilon}\in W
where

J_{\epsilon}(z)=\frac{1}{2}\|z\|_{W}^{2}-\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}}(f_{\epsilon}\psi+g_{\epsilon}\varphi)dx,~\text{for every}~z=(\varphi,\psi)\in W.

Moreover we have

\langle w_{\epsilon},z\rangle_{W}-\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}}(f_{\epsilon}\psi+g_{\epsilon}\varphi)dx=0,

(5.5)

for every
z=(\varphi,\psi)\in W
.

Proof.

We first prove that the functional
J_{\epsilon}
is coercive. Indeed, using Young’s inequality, we have

\displaystyle J_{\epsilon}(z)\geq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\|z\|_{W}^{2}-\|f_{\epsilon}\|_{\frac{-1}{2}}\|\psi\|_{\frac{1}{2}}-\|g_{\epsilon}\|_{\frac{-1}{2}}\|\varphi\|_{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4}\|z\|_{W}^{2}-4\left(\|f_{\epsilon}\|_{\frac{-1}{2}}^{2}+\|g_{\epsilon}\|_{\frac{-1}{2}}^{2}\right).

(5.6)

This implies that
j\in\mathbb{R}
and that any minimizing sequence
\{z_{n}\}\subset W
is bounded. Then, the existence and uniqueness of
w_{\epsilon}
follows from the weak lower semi-continuity and strict convexity of
J_{\epsilon}
.
Finally, (
5.5
) results from the first order optimality condition.
\square

The next proposition shows that the sequence
\{w_{\epsilon}\}_{\epsilon}
actually converges strongly to
w
in
W
.

Proposition 5.3

We have

\lim\limits_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0}\|w_{\epsilon}-w\|_{W}=0.

Proof.

Note that
\{w_{\epsilon}\}_{\epsilon}
is bounded in
W
. Indeed, since
J_{\epsilon}(w_{\epsilon})\leq J_{\epsilon}(0)=0
and using (
5
), we obtain

\|w_{\epsilon}\|_{W}^{2}\leq 16\left(\|f_{\epsilon}\|_{\frac{-1}{2}}^{2}+\|g_{\epsilon}\|_{\frac{-1}{2}}^{2}\right).

Since
f_{\epsilon},~g_{\epsilon}
are uniformly bounded in
H^{\frac{-1}{2}}(\mathbb{R})
, we get the claimed bound. Up to an extraction of a subsequence let us denote the weak limit of
w_{\epsilon}
,
\hat{w}=(\hat{u},\hat{v})
. We first prove that
\hat{w}=w
. For this, note that since
w_{\epsilon}\rightharpoonup\hat{w}
in
W
,
f_{\epsilon}\rightarrow f(u)
and
g_{\epsilon}\rightarrow g(v)
in
H^{\frac{-1}{2}}(\mathbb{R})
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\|\hat{w}\|^{2}_{W}-\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}}(f(u)\hat{v}+g(v)\hat{u})dx\leq

\displaystyle\liminf\limits_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0}\left(\frac{1}{2}\|w_{\epsilon}\|_{W}^{2}-\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}}(f(u)v_{\epsilon}+g(v)u_{\epsilon})dx\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\liminf\limits_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0}\left(\frac{1}{2}\|w_{\epsilon}\|_{W}^{2}-\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}}(f_{\epsilon}v_{\epsilon}+g_{\epsilon}u_{\epsilon})dx\right).

Now let
\bf h
=
(\varphi,\psi)\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})\times C_{0}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
. Using that
\mathrm{argmin}J_{\epsilon}=w_{\epsilon}
and the fact that
f_{\epsilon}\rightarrow f(u)
and
g_{\epsilon}\rightarrow g(v)
in
L_{\text{loc}}^{1}(\mathbb{R})
we infer that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\|\hat{w}\|^{2}_{W}-\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}}(f(u)\hat{v}+g(v)\hat{u})dx\leq

\displaystyle\liminf\limits_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0}\frac{1}{2}\|\textbf{h}\|_{W}^{2}-\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}}(f_{\epsilon}\psi+g_{\epsilon}\varphi)dx

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\|\textbf{h}\|_{W}^{2}-\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}}(f(u)\psi+g(v)\varphi)dx.

By density of
C_{0}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})\times C_{0}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
in
W
, the previous estimate implies that
\hat{w}
minimizes the strictly convex functional

W\ni\textbf{h}\mapsto\frac{1}{2}\|\textbf{h}\|_{W}^{2}-\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}}(f(u)\psi+g(v)\varphi)dx.

Since
w
is a critical point of the latter, the strict convexity forces
w=\hat{w}
. Recall that

\|u\|_{\frac{1}{2}}^{2}=\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}}g(v)udx~\text{and}~\|v\|_{\frac{1}{2}}^{2}=\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}}f(u)vdx.

Similarly from (
5.5
), we have

\|u_{\epsilon}\|_{W}^{2}=\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}}g_{\epsilon}u_{\epsilon}dx~\text{and}~\|v_{\epsilon}\|_{W}^{2}=\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}}f_{\epsilon}v_{\epsilon}dx.

By the strong convergence of
f_{\epsilon}
to
f(u)
and
g_{\epsilon}
to
g(u)
in
H^{\frac{-1}{2}}(\mathbb{R})
, we conclude that

\displaystyle\|w\|_{W}^{2}=\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}}(f(u)v+g(v)u)dx=\lim\limits_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0}\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}}(f_{\epsilon}v_{\epsilon}+g_{\epsilon}u_{\epsilon})dx=\lim\limits_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0}\|w_{\epsilon}\|_{W}^{2}.

Finally the conclusion follows using the uniform convexity of the norm.
\square

5.1
Regularity for the approximating problem

In this subsection we will prove some regularity estimates for the solutions
w_{\epsilon}
of (
5.1
). We first recall the following useful inequality:

Lemma 5.4

[
5
, Lemma C.1]

Let
1<p<\infty
and
\beta\geq 1
. For every
a,~b,~m\geq 0
there holds

|a-b|^{p-2}(a-b)(a_{m}^{\beta}-b_{m}^{\beta})\geq\frac{\beta p^{p}}{(\beta+p-1)^{p}}\left(a_{m}^{\frac{\beta+p-1}{p}}-b_{m}^{\frac{\beta+p-1}{p}}\right)^{p}

where we set
a_{m}=\min\{a,m\}
and
b_{m}=\min\{b,m\}
.

Proposition 5.5

Let
\epsilon\in(0,1]
and
w_{\epsilon}=(u_{\epsilon},v_{\epsilon})
be the solution of (
5.1
). Then we have
u_{\epsilon},~v_{\epsilon}\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
.

Proof.

We prove the proposition by using Moser iterations type technique. For every
L>0
, we define

h_{L}(t)=\begin{cases}\min\{t,L\},~&\text{if}~t\geq 0,\\
0,~&\text{if}~t<0.\end{cases}

Now define

u_{\epsilon,L}=h_{L}\circ u_{\epsilon},~v_{\epsilon,L}=h_{L}\circ v_{\epsilon}\in H^{\frac{1}{2}}(\mathbb{R})\cap L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}).

Then for every
\beta\geq 1
, taking
(u_{\epsilon,L}^{\beta},0)
as a test function in (
5.5
) and using Lemma
5.4
, we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{4\beta}{(\beta+1)^{2}}\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}}\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{\left((u_{\epsilon,L}(x))^{\frac{\beta+1}{2}}-(u_{\epsilon,L}(y))^{\frac{\beta+1}{2}}\right)}{|x-y|^{2}}dxdy\leq\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}}|g_{\epsilon}|u_{\epsilon,L}^{\beta}dx.

By the Sobolev inequality, we have for any
q>2

\displaystyle\left(\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}}u_{\epsilon,L}^{q\frac{\beta+1}{2}}dx\right)^{\frac{2}{q}}\leq C\frac{1}{\beta}\left(\frac{\beta+1}{2}\right)^{2}\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}}|g_{\epsilon}|u_{\epsilon,L}^{\beta}dx.

By using the uniform bound on
|g_{\epsilon}|
and
\beta>1
we obtain

\displaystyle\|u_{\epsilon,L}\|_{L^{q\frac{\beta+1}{2}}}\leq C^{\frac{1}{\beta+1}}\left(q\frac{\beta+1}{2}\right)^{\frac{2}{\beta+1}}\|u_{\epsilon,L}\|_{L^{\beta}}^{\frac{\beta}{\beta+1}}

for some constant
C>0
. By setting

\beta_{n+1}=q\frac{\beta_{n}+1}{2},~\beta_{0}=q,~\zeta_{n}=\frac{\beta_{n}}{\beta_{n}+1}<1,

we obtain
\beta_{n}\rightarrow+\infty
and

\|u_{\epsilon,L}\|_{\beta_{n+1}}\leq C^{\frac{1}{\beta_{n+1}}}\beta_{n+1}^{\frac{q}{\beta_{n+1}}}\|u_{\epsilon,L}\|_{L^{\beta_{n}}}^{\zeta_{n}}.

On iterating the above inequality, we get for any
n\geq 1
,

\|u_{\epsilon,L}\|_{L^{\beta_{n}+1}}\leq C^{\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n+1}\frac{1}{\beta_{i}}}\left(\prod\limits_{i=1}^{n+1}\beta_{i}^{\frac{1}{\beta_{1}}}\right)^{q}\|u_{\epsilon,L}\|_{L^{q}}^{\prod\limits_{i=1}^{n}\zeta_{i}}.

Now
\beta_{n}
can be determined explicitly: by setting
\sigma=q/2>1
, that is

\beta_{n}=\sigma^{n}\beta_{0}+\displaystyle\frac{\sigma^{n+1}-\sigma}{\sigma-1}.

So it holds

\lim\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\beta_{n}}{\sigma^{n}}=\beta_{0}+\frac{\sigma}{\sigma-1},~\text{and}~\sum\limits_{i=1}^{+\infty}\frac{1}{\beta_{i}}<+\infty~\text{and}~\prod\limits_{i+1}^{+\infty}\beta_{i}^{\frac{1}{\beta_{1}}}<+\infty.

Lastly we see

\displaystyle\lim\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}\prod\limits_{i=1}^{n}\sigma_{i}=\lim\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}\prod\limits_{i=1}^{n}\sigma^{n+1}\frac{\beta_{0}}{\beta_{n+1}}=\frac{q-2}{q-1}.

Thus we finally have

\|u_{\epsilon,L}\|_{\infty}\leq\displaystyle C\|u_{\epsilon,L}\|_{q}^{\frac{q-2}{q-1}}.

We now let
L\rightarrow\infty
, which gives
u_{\epsilon}^{+}\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
. By repeating the argument above for
u_{\epsilon}^{-}
, we get
u_{\epsilon}\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
. Similarly we have
v_{\epsilon}\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
.
\square

Proposition 5.6

We have
Du_{\epsilon},~Dv_{\epsilon}\in H^{\frac{1}{2}}(\mathbb{R})
for any
\epsilon\in(0,1]
, where
D
denotes the derivative (with respect to the variable
x\in\mathbb{R}
). Moreover

\|Du_{\epsilon}\|_{\frac{1}{2}}\leq\|Dg_{\epsilon}\|_{\frac{-1}{2}}~\text{and}~\|Dv_{\epsilon}\|_{\frac{1}{2}}\leq\|Df_{\epsilon}\|_{\frac{-1}{2}}.

Proof.

Let us take
h\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
. We use the following notations in the course of the proof:

If
h\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
and
\varphi:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is a measurable function, we set

\varphi_{h}(x)=\varphi(x+h)~\text{and}~\delta_{h}\varphi(x)=\varphi(x+h)-\varphi(x).

In case
\varphi:\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, we use the same notation for

\varphi_{h}(x,y)=\varphi(x+h,y+h)~\text{and}~\delta_{h}\varphi(x,y)=\varphi(x+h,y+h)-\varphi(x,y).

We also set

\mathcal{G}\varphi(x,y)=\frac{\varphi(x)-\varphi(y)}{|x-y|^{\frac{1}{2}}}~\text{and}~d\mu=\frac{dxdy}{|x-y|}.

For the sake of convenience we drop the subscript
\epsilon
. Now using the quotient difference method, let
\psi\in H^{\frac{1}{2}}(\mathbb{R})
and take
(\psi_{-h},0)
as test function in (
5.5
) and using change of variables, we get

\displaystyle\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}}\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}}\mathcal{G}u_{h}\mathcal{G}\psi d\mu+\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}}u_{h}\psi dx-\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}}g_{h}\psi dx=0.

(5.7)

Also by taking
(\psi,0)
as test function in (
5.5
), we have

\displaystyle\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}}\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}}\mathcal{G}u\mathcal{G}\psi d\mu+\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}}u\psi dx-\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}}g\psi dx=0.

(5.8)

On subtracting (
5.8
) from (
5.7
), we have the following equation:

\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}}\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}}\delta_{h}\mathcal{G}u\mathcal{G}\psi d\mu+\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}}\delta_{h}u\psi dx-\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}}\delta_{h}g\psi dx=0,

(5.9)

which holds true for every
\psi\in H^{\frac{1}{2}}(\mathbb{R})
. We now test (
5.9
) with the test function
\psi=\delta_{h}u
, we obtain

\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}}\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}}\delta_{h}\mathcal{G}u\mathcal{G}\delta_{h}ud\mu+\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}}|\delta_{h}u|^{2}dx-\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}}\delta_{h}g\delta_{h}udx=0.

(5.10)

Observing that

\mathcal{G}(\delta_{h}u)=\frac{u(x+h)-u(y+h)-(u(x)-u(y))}{|x-y|^{\frac{1}{2}}}=\delta_{h}\mathcal{G}u

and so from (
5.10
) we obtain

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{G}(\delta_{h}u)\|_{\frac{1}{2}}^{2}=\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}}\delta_{h}g\delta_{h}udx\leq\|\delta_{h}g\|_{\frac{-1}{2}}\|\delta_{h}u\|_{\frac{1}{2}}.

Now divide by
|h|
and use
[
6
, Lemma 2.2]
to get

\left\|\frac{\delta_{h}u}{h}\right\|_{\frac{1}{2}}\leq\|g^{\prime}\|_{\frac{-1}{2}}.

(5.11)

In particular, we have that

\sup\limits_{|h|>0}\left\|\frac{\delta_{h}u}{h}\right\|_{L^{2}}<+\infty.

Since
\displaystyle\frac{\delta_{h}u}{h}\rightarrow Du
in the sense of distribution, we get
Du\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
. Moreover, there exists
\{h_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subset\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
converging to
0
such that

\frac{\delta_{h_{n}}u}{h_{n}}\rightarrow Du~\text{strongly in}~L^{2}(\mathbb{R}).

We can thus pass to the limit in (
5.11
) by using Fatou’s Lemma and get the desired result. Similarly the result holds for
v
.
\square

Remark 5.7

Since by (
5.4
),
f_{\epsilon},g_{\epsilon}
are uniformly bounded in
H^{\frac{1}{2}}(\mathbb{R})
, we have
Df_{\epsilon},~Dg_{\epsilon}
are uniformly bounded in
H^{\frac{-1}{2}}(\mathbb{R})
. This combined with
Proposition

imply that
Du_{\epsilon},~Dv_{\epsilon}
are uniformly bounded in
H^{\frac{1}{2}}(\mathbb{R})
.

Proposition 5.8

We have
Du_{\epsilon},~Dv_{\epsilon}\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
, for every
\epsilon\in(0,1]
.

Proof.

The proof to show
Du_{\epsilon}\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
follows similarly as the proof of Proposition
5.5
by taking the test function
\left(h_{L}\left(\frac{\delta_{h}u}{h}\right)^{\beta},0\right)
in (
5.9
), where
\beta\geq 1
and
h_{L}
are as in Proposition
5.5
and using the fact that
Dg_{\epsilon}\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
. Similarly, we get
Dv_{\epsilon}\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
.
\square

5.2
End of Theorem
1.6

In this subsection we complete the proof of the Pohožaev identity (
1.4
).

Proof.

Following
[
6
]
, we will divide the proof into various intermediate steps:

Step 1: Construction of the perturbation
. Let
r>1
and
\eta\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
be a positive cut-off function supported in
B_{r}
. For
|t|<\delta<1
(
\delta
depending on
\eta
), the map defined by

x\mapsto x+t\eta(x)x:=\bar{x}=H_{t}(x)

is a smooth diffeomorphism of
\mathbb{R}
which is uniformly bilipschitz for
|t|<\delta
, i.e.,

\sup\limits_{|t|<\delta}(\|DH_{t}(x)\|_{L^{\infty}}+\|DH_{t}^{-1}\|_{L^{\infty}})<+\infty.

Since
\partial_{t}H_{t}(x)=\eta(x)x
, it holds

\partial_{t}(H_{t}^{-1}(H_{t}(x)))+DH_{t}^{-1}(H_{t}(x))\partial_{t}H_{t}(x)=0,

so that

\partial_{t}H_{t}^{-1}(\bar{x})=-DH_{t}^{-1}(\bar{x})\eta(H_{t}^{-1}(\bar{x}))H_{t}^{-1}(\bar{x}),~~\partial_{t}H_{t}^{-1}(\bar{x})_{|_{t=0}}=-\eta(\bar{x})\bar{x}.

(5.12)

Moreover for any fixed
\bar{x}
, we have

\partial_{t}DH_{t}^{-1}(\bar{x})=D\partial_{t}H_{t}^{-1}(\bar{x})~\text{and}~\partial_{t}DH_{t}^{-1}(\bar{x})_{|_{t=0}}=-D(\bar{x}\eta(\bar{x})).

(5.13)

Now we define

u_{\epsilon,t}=u_{\epsilon}\circ H_{t}(x)=u_{\epsilon}(x+t\eta(x)x)~\text{and}~v_{\epsilon,t}=v_{\epsilon}\circ H_{t}(x)=v_{\epsilon}(x+t\eta(x)x).

By using the change of variable
\bar{x}=H_{t}(x)
, we see that

\displaystyle\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}}\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}}\mathcal{G}u_{\epsilon,t}\mathcal{G}v_{\epsilon,t}d\mu=

\displaystyle\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}}\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{(u_{\epsilon}(\bar{x})-u_{\epsilon}(\bar{y}))(v_{\epsilon}(\bar{x})-v_{\epsilon}(\bar{y}))}{|H_{t}^{-1}(\bar{x})-H_{t}^{-1}(\bar{y})|^{2}}DH_{t}^{-1}(\bar{x})DH_{t}^{-1}(\bar{y})d\bar{x}d\bar{y}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}}\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}}\mathcal{G}u_{\epsilon}\mathcal{G}v_{\epsilon}\mathcal{S}_{t}\mathcal{R}_{t}^{2}d\mu,

(5.14)

where we set for
x\neq y

\mathcal{R}_{t}(x,y)=\frac{|x-y|}{|H_{t}^{-1}(x)-H_{t}^{-1}(y)|},~\mathcal{S}_{t}(x,y)=DH_{t}^{-1}(x)DH_{t}^{-1}(y).

Observe that, for
|t|<\delta
,

\sup\limits_{|t|<\delta}(\|\mathcal{S}_{t}\|_{L^{\infty}}+\|\mathcal{R}_{t}\|_{L^{\infty}})<+\infty

(5.15)

and for any
(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
, the maps
t\mapsto\mathcal{S}_{t}(x,y)
and
t\mapsto\mathcal{R}_{t}(x,y)
are smooth. Also we have

\partial_{t}\mathcal{R}_{t}^{2}=-2\mathcal{R}_{t}^{2}\frac{H_{t}^{-1}(x)-H_{t}^{-1}(y)}{|H_{t}^{-1}(x)-H_{t}^{-1}(y)|}\frac{\partial_{t}H_{t}^{-1}(x)-\partial_{t}H_{t}^{-1}(y)}{|H_{t}^{-1}(x)-H_{t}^{-1}(y)|},

\partial_{t}\mathcal{S}_{t}=\partial_{t}DH_{t}^{-1}(x)DH_{t}^{-1}(y)+DH_{t}^{-1}(x)\partial_{t}DH_{t}^{-1}(y).

According to (
5.12
) and the bilipschitz character of
H_{t}
,

\frac{|\partial_{t}H_{t}^{-1}(y)-\partial_{t}H_{t}^{-1}(y)|}{|H_{t}^{-1}(x)-H_{t}^{-1}(y)|}\leq\|DH_{t}\|_{L^{\infty}}\text{Lip}(D(H_{t}^{-1})\eta(H_{t}^{-1})H_{t}^{-1})\leq C(\|\eta\|_{C^{2}})<+\infty.

By (
5.13
) and (
5.15
), we infer that
t\mapsto\partial_{t}\mathcal{S}_{t}
and
t\mapsto\partial_{t}\mathcal{R}_{t}
are continuous for any
(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
and

\sup\limits_{|t|<\delta}\|\partial_{t}\mathcal{S}_{t}\|_{L^{\infty}}+\|\partial_{t}\mathcal{R}_{t}\|_{L^{\infty}}<+\infty.

Step 2: Differentiating under the integral sign.
According to (
5.2
), our aim is to prove the following chain of equalities for
t=0

\displaystyle\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}}\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{d}{dt}\left(\mathcal{G}u_{\epsilon,t}\mathcal{G}v_{\epsilon,t}\right)d\mu=\frac{d}{dt}\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}}\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}}\mathcal{G}u_{\epsilon,t}\mathcal{G}v_{\epsilon,t}d\mu=

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}}\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}}\mathcal{G}u_{\epsilon}\mathcal{G}v_{\epsilon}\mathcal{S}_{t}\mathcal{R}_{t}^{2}d\mu

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}}\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{d}{dt}\left(\mathcal{G}u_{\epsilon}\mathcal{G}v_{\epsilon}\mathcal{S}_{t}\mathcal{R}_{t}^{2}\right)d\mu.

(5.16)

First note that since
u_{\epsilon}
is Lipschitz, all the integrands above are well defined. We are going to use
[
6
, Theorem 5.2]
here to interchange the integral and the differential. In view of this, we claim that the maps

\displaystyle t\mapsto\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}}\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}}\mathcal{G}u_{\epsilon,t}\mathcal{G}v_{\epsilon,t}d\mu=\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}}\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}}\mathcal{G}u_{\epsilon}\mathcal{G}v_{\epsilon}\mathcal{S}_{t}\mathcal{R}_{t}d\mu,

\displaystyle t\mapsto\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}}\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{d}{dt}\mathcal{G}u_{\epsilon,t}Gv_{\epsilon,t}d\mu,

\displaystyle t\mapsto\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}}\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{d}{dt}\left(\mathcal{G}u_{\epsilon}\mathcal{G}v_{\epsilon}\mathcal{S}_{t}\mathcal{R}_{t}^{2}\right)d\mu

are well defined and continuous in
|t|\leq\delta
.
The first map is continuous by the dominated convergence using the smoothness of
\mathcal{S}_{t}
and
\mathcal{R}_{t}
and the bound (
5.15
). For the second map, using the change of variables
\bar{x}=H_{t}(x)
, we have

\displaystyle\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}}\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{d}{dt}\mathcal{G}u_{e,t}\mathcal{G}v_{\epsilon,t}d\mu

\displaystyle=\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}}\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}}(\mathcal{G}u_{\epsilon,t}\mathcal{G}(\eta xDv_{\epsilon}\circ H_{t})+\mathcal{G}(\eta xDu_{\epsilon}\circ H_{t})\mathcal{G}v_{\epsilon,t})d\mu

(5.17)

\displaystyle=\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}}\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}}(\mathcal{G}u_{\epsilon}\mathcal{G}(\eta\circ H_{t}^{-1}H_{t}^{-1}Dv_{\epsilon})+\mathcal{G}(\eta\circ H_{t}^{-1}H_{t}^{-1}Du_{\epsilon})\mathcal{G}v_{\epsilon})\mathcal{R}_{t}^{2}\mathcal{S}_{t}d\mu,

and the integrand is pointwise continuous in
t
, therefore it is sufficient to dominate it uniformly in
|t|\leq\delta
. Using (
5.15
), we obtain from (
5.17
) that

|\mathcal{G}u_{\epsilon}\mathcal{G}(\eta\circ H_{t}^{-1}H_{t}^{-1}Dv_{\epsilon})\mathcal{R}_{t}^{2}\mathcal{S}_{t}|\leq C|\mathcal{G}u_{\epsilon}\mathcal{G}(\eta\circ H_{t}^{-1}H_{t}^{-1}Dv_{\epsilon})|.

Notice that supp
(\eta\circ H_{t}^{-1})\subset B_{R+1}
, hence the last factor is supported in
\mathcal{A}:=(B_{R+1}\times\mathbb{R})\cup(\mathbb{R}\times B_{R+1})
. Note that

\displaystyle|\mathcal{G}(\eta\circ H_{t}^{-1}H_{t}^{-1}Dv_{\epsilon})|\leq

\displaystyle\left|\mathcal{G}(\eta\circ H_{t}^{-1}H_{t}^{-1})\frac{Dv_{\epsilon}(x)+Dv_{\epsilon}(y)}{2}\right|

\displaystyle+\left|\mathcal{G}Dv_{\epsilon}\frac{\eta(H_{t}^{-1}(x))H_{t}^{-1}(x)+\eta(H_{t}^{-1}(y))H_{t}^{-1}(y)}{2}\right|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\|Dv_{\epsilon}\|_{L^{\infty}}|\mathcal{G}(\eta\circ H_{t}^{-1}H_{t}^{-1})|+\|\eta(H_{t}^{-1})H_{t}^{-1}\|_{L^{\infty}}|\mathcal{G}Dv_{\epsilon}|.

Using the bounds

\sup\limits_{|t|<\delta}\|\eta(H_{t}^{-1})H_{t}^{-1}\|_{L^{\infty}}=\|\eta x\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq R

and

\sup\limits_{|t|<\delta}|\mathcal{G}(\eta\circ H_{t}^{-1}H_{t}^{-1})\leq C(\eta,\delta)\min\{|x-y|^{\frac{-1}{2}},|x-y|^{\frac{1}{2}}\}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2},d\mu),

we have

\displaystyle|\mathcal{G}u_{\epsilon}\mathcal{G}(\eta\circ H_{t}^{-1}H_{t}^{-1}Dv_{\epsilon})\mathcal{R}_{t}^{2}\mathcal{S}_{t}|\leq C|\mathcal{G}u_{\epsilon}|_{\chi_{\mathcal{A}}}\left(|\mathcal{G}v_{\epsilon}|+\min\{|x-y|^{\frac{-1}{2}},|x-y|^{\frac{1}{2}}\}\right)\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2},d\mu).

Similarly the second term is dominated by a
L^{1}
function.
Thus we get the required continuity due to the dominated convergence theorem.

Lastly, we have

\displaystyle\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}}\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{d}{dt}\left(\mathcal{G}u_{\epsilon}\mathcal{G}v_{\epsilon}\mathcal{S}_{t}\mathcal{R}_{t}^{2}\right)d\mu=\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}}\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}}\mathcal{G}u_{\epsilon}\mathcal{G}v_{\epsilon}\left(\partial_{t}\mathcal{S}_{t}\mathcal{R}_{t}^{2}+\mathcal{S}_{t}\partial_{t}\mathcal{R}_{t}^{2}\right)d\mu.

(5.18)

Thus we conclude by dominated convergence theorem.

Thus (
5.2
) is proved for
|t|<\delta
and the right-hand sides of (
5.17
) and (
5.18
) are equal.

Step 3: Pohožaev identity for the approximating problem.
We evaluate right hand sides of (
5.17
) and (
5.18
) at
t=0
to get

\displaystyle\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}}\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}}(\mathcal{G}u_{\epsilon}\mathcal{G}(\eta xDv_{\epsilon})+\mathcal{G}v_{\epsilon}\mathcal{G}(\eta xDu_{\epsilon}))d\mu=2\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}}\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}}\mathcal{G}u_{\epsilon}\mathcal{G}v_{\epsilon}\left[\frac{x-y}{|x-y|}.\frac{\eta(x)x-\eta(y)y}{|x-y|}-D(\eta(x)x)\right]d\mu.

Since
(\eta xDv_{\epsilon},\eta xDu_{\epsilon})
is an admissible test function for (
5.5
), we obtain the identity

\displaystyle\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}}(g_{\epsilon}\eta xDv_{\epsilon}+f_{\epsilon}\eta xDu_{\epsilon})dx-

\displaystyle nV_{0}\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}}(u_{\epsilon}\eta xDv_{\epsilon}+v_{\epsilon}\eta xDu_{\epsilon})dx

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}}\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}}\mathcal{G}u_{\epsilon}\mathcal{G}v_{\epsilon}\left[\frac{x-y}{|x-y|}.\frac{\eta(x)x-\eta(y)y}{|x-y|}-D(\eta(x)x)\right]d\mu.

(5.19)

Step 4: Taking the limit.
We now let
\epsilon\rightarrow 0
in the previous equality. For the right hand side since
\displaystyle\frac{x-y}{|x-y|}.\frac{\eta(x)x-\eta(y)y}{|x-y|}
,
D(\eta(x)x)
are bounded and by Proposition
5.3
,
\mathcal{G}u_{\epsilon}\rightarrow\mathcal{G}u
and
\mathcal{G}v_{\epsilon}\rightarrow\mathcal{G}v
strongly in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2},d\mu)
, we have

\displaystyle\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}}\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}}\mathcal{G}u_{\epsilon}\mathcal{G}v_{\epsilon}\left[\frac{x-y}{|x-y|}.\frac{\eta(x)x-\eta(y)y}{|x-y|}\right]d\mu-\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}}

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}u_{\epsilon}\mathcal{G}v_{\epsilon}D(\eta(x)x)d\mu

\displaystyle\rightarrow

\displaystyle\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}}\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}}\mathcal{G}u\mathcal{G}v\left[\frac{x-y}{|x-y|}.\frac{\eta(x)x-\eta(y)y}{|x-y|}-D(\eta(x)x)\right]d\mu.

Now using integration by parts, we have

\displaystyle\lim\limits_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0}\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}}(u_{\epsilon}\eta xDv_{\epsilon}+v_{\epsilon}\eta xDu_{\epsilon})dx=\lim\limits_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0}\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}}D(u_{\epsilon}v_{\epsilon})\eta xdx

\displaystyle=-\lim\limits_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0}\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}}u_{\epsilon}v_{\epsilon}D(\eta(x)x)dx

\displaystyle=-\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}}uvD(\eta(x)x)dx.

Now to compute
\displaystyle\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}}g_{\epsilon}\eta xDv_{\epsilon}dx
, we split it as

\displaystyle\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}}g_{\epsilon}\eta xDv_{\epsilon}dx=\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}}(g_{\epsilon}-g(v_{\epsilon}))\eta xDv_{\epsilon}dx+\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}}g(v_{\epsilon})\eta xDv_{\epsilon}dx.

Again using integrating by parts, we get

\displaystyle\lim\limits_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0}\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}}g(v_{\epsilon})\eta xDv_{\epsilon}dx=-\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}}G(v)D(\eta(x)x)dx.

(5.20)

For the other term, note that
v_{\epsilon}
is uniformly bounded by Proposition
5.5
and
g
is continuous. Since by Proposition
5.3
we have
v_{\epsilon}\rightarrow v
in
L^{p}(\mathbb{R})
for every
p\in[2,\infty)
,
g_{\epsilon}-g(v_{\epsilon})\rightarrow 0
in
L^{p}_{\text{loc}}(\mathbb{R})
. Indeed for every compact set
K\subset\mathbb{R}
we have

\displaystyle\|g_{\epsilon}-g(v_{\epsilon})\|_{L^{p}(K)}\leq\|g_{\epsilon}-g(v)\|_{L^{p}(K)}+\|g(v)-g(v_{\epsilon})\|_{L^{p}(K)}.

The first term of the right hand side of the above expression converges to
0
thanks to Lemma
5.1
, while by applying the dominated convergence theorem the second term tends to
0
.

Now we claim that

\displaystyle\lim\limits_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0}\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}}(g_{\epsilon}-g(v_{\epsilon}))\eta xDv_{\epsilon}dx=0.

(5.21)

Indeed, using (
5.2
) and Remark
5.7
, we get

\displaystyle\left|\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}}(g_{\epsilon}-g(v_{\epsilon}))\eta xDv_{\epsilon}dx\right|\leq C\|g_{\epsilon}-g(v_{\epsilon})\|_{\frac{-1}{2}}\|\eta xDv_{\epsilon}\|_{\frac{1}{2}}\rightarrow 0~\text{as}~\epsilon\rightarrow 0.

Combining (
5.20
) and (
5.21
), we obtain

\displaystyle\lim\limits_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0}\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}}g_{\epsilon}\eta xDv_{\epsilon}dx=-\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}}G(v)D(\eta(x)x)dx.

Similarly, we have

\displaystyle\lim\limits_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0}\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}}f_{\epsilon}\eta xDu_{\epsilon}dx=-\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}}F(u)D(\eta(x)x)dx.

Thus by letting
\epsilon\rightarrow 0
in (

), we obtain

\displaystyle\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}}(F(u)+G(v))D(\eta(x)x)dx-

\displaystyle V_{0}\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}}uvD(\eta(x)x)dx

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}}\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}}\mathcal{G}u\mathcal{G}v\left[\frac{x-y}{|x-y|}.\frac{\eta(x)x-\eta(y)y}{|x-y|}-D(\eta(x)x)\right]d\mu.

(5.22)

Step 5: Conclusion.
Finally, we take
\eta
of the form
\eta_{R}(x)=\phi(x/R)
with
\phi\in C_{0}^{\infty}(B_{1})
positive such that
\phi\equiv 1
in
B_{\frac{1}{2}}
. Clearly

\displaystyle\left|\frac{x-y}{|x-y}.\frac{\eta_{R}(x)x-\eta_{R}(y)y}{|x-y|}\right|\leq\|\phi\|_{L^{\infty}}+\|D\phi\|_{L^{\infty}},

and

\displaystyle|D(\eta_{R}(x)x)|\leq\left|\frac{1}{R}D\phi\left(\frac{x}{R}\right)x\right|+\left|\phi\left(\frac{x}{R}\right)\right|\leq C(\|\phi\|_{L^{\infty}}+\|D\phi\|_{L^{\infty}}).

Moreover, for any

as

. Hence, using Dominated Convergence theorem (note that

), we can let

6.1

First note that by setting

\displaystyle\begin{cases}(-\Delta)^{\frac{1}{2}}u+V_{\epsilon}(x)u=g(v),\\

(6.1)

We next consider (
6.1

as the completion of

with respect to the inner product

\langle u,v\rangle_{\frac{1}{2},\epsilon}:=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}}\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{(u(x)-u(y))(v(x)-v(y))}{|x-y|^{2}}dxdy+\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}}V_{\epsilon}(x)uvdx

and the norm

Let

with the inner product

\langle w_{1},w_{2}\rangle_{\epsilon}:=\langle u_{1},u_{2}\rangle_{\frac{1}{2},\epsilon}+\langle v_{1},v_{2}\rangle_{\frac{1}{2},\epsilon},~w_{i}=(u_{i},v_{i})\in W_{\epsilon},~i=1,2,

and the norm
\|w\|_{\epsilon}^{2}=\|(u,v)\|_{\epsilon}^{2}=\|u\|_{\frac{1}{2},\epsilon}^{2}+\|v\|_{\frac{1}{2},\epsilon}^{2}

For each

The associated energy functional corresponding to (

and

for all

. Moreover,
\mathcal{J}_{\epsilon}
can be written as follows

\mathcal{J}_{\epsilon}(w):=\frac{1}{2}\|w^{+}\|_{\epsilon}^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\|w^{-}\|_{\epsilon}^{2}-\Phi(w).

Definition 6.1

We know that if

then

), the so-called ground-state level.

For
