Abstract
We show that approximate graph colouring is not solved by any level of the affine integer programming ( AIP \AIP ) hierarchy. To establish the result, we translate the problem of exhibiting a graph fooling a level of the AIP \AIP hierarchy into the problem of constructing a highly symmetric crystal tensor. In order to prove the existence of crystals in arbitrary dimension, we provide a combinatorial characterisation for realisable systems of tensors; i.e., sets of low-dimensional tensors that can be realised as the projections of a single high-dimensional tensor.

Given two

The

It is customary to study

.

By letting

\PCSPs

. Recent works on

Remarkably, most algorithmic techniques in constraint satisfaction can be broadly classified into two distinct classes: Algorithms enforcing some type of local consistency, and algorithms
related to (generalisations of) linear equations.

The first class of algorithms is based on the following idea: Rather than directly checking for the existence of a global map between

\mathbf{G}
and
\mathbf{H}
satisfying constraints (i.e.,
a homomorphism), which may not be doable in polynomial time, one
tries to draw an
atlas
of
charts
covering each region of the instance
\mathbf{G}
. The charts are partial homomorphisms, i.e., homomorphisms from a substructure of
\mathbf{G}
to
\mathbf{H}
; the atlas must have the property that the maps are consistent, i.e., whenever two regions overlap, there exist charts of the regions that agree on the intersection. The
bounded width
(or
local-consistency checking
) algorithm outputs
Yes
if and only if such an atlas exists – which can be checked in polynomial time provided that the size of each chart is bounded
[
34
]
. More powerful versions of this technique require that the charts of each region should be sampled according to some probability distribution. In this case, the consistency requirement of the atlas is stronger, as it asks that, whenever two regions overlap,
the probability distribution over the charts of the intersection should be exactly the
marginal
of the distributions over the charts of the two regions. Concretely, checking for the existence of such a “random atlas” amounts to solving a linear program, and results in the so-called Sherali-Adams LP hierarchy
[
53
]
, which is provably more powerful than bounded width
[
3
]
. Treating probabilities as vectors satisfying certain orthogonality requirements gives an even stronger
algorithm based on semidefinite programming, known as the sum-of-squares or Lasserre SDP hierarchy
[
54
,
51
,
48
]
.
In general, the existence of a (random) atlas is not sufficient to deduce that a
planisphere
(i.e., a global map satisfying all constraints) exists.
In fact, if P
\neq

we do not expect polynomial-time algorithms to solve NP-hard problems. Thus, a well-established line

[

[

]
for lower bounds on SDPs.

Any
\PCSP

\{0,1\}
.
The second class of algorithms essentially consists in solving the equations
using (some variant or a generalisation of) Gaussian elimination. This requires relaxing the problem by admitting a larger range for the variables in the equations (as, in general, the system cannot be efficiently solved over
\{0,1\}
).
In particular,
it is possible to solve the system in polynomial time over
\mathbb{Z}

(
[

]
, cf. also
[

]
) – which results in the

,

that we consider in this work. The “base level” of this algorithm

.
This algorithmic technique is substantially different from the first class of algorithms: Instead of looking for an atlas of charts faithfully describing regions of the world – i.e., a system of
local assignments satisfying the constraints

In the context of
\CSPs
, the elusive interaction between consistency-checking

[

]
,
that was finally settled in

.

If, as conjectured, AGC is an NP-hard problem and P

In a striking sequence of

Recent work
[

Contributions

In this paper, we focus on the second class of algorithms and show that no level of the affine integer programming hierarchy solves AGC.
Recently,
[
29
]
described a linear-algebraic characterisation of the algorithm in terms of
a geometric construction called
tensorisation
.
Using this characterisation as a black box, we translate the problem of finding an instance of AGC fooling the algorithm into the problem of finding a tensor with many symmetries, which we call a
crystal
. Our main technical contribution is the construction of crystals. More precisely, we prove the following result: Given a collection of low-dimensional tensors (“pictures”) satisfying a compatibility requirement, it is possible to build a high-dimensional tensor
such that by projecting it onto low-dimensional hyperplanes one recovers the pictures.
Variants of this problem have appeared in the literature in combinatorial matrix theory. In particular, the problem of constructing a matrix (i.e., a
2
-dimensional tensor) having prescribed row-sum and column-sum vectors (i.e.,
1
-dimensional projections) has been studied for different classes of matrices, such as nonnegative integer matrices
[
23
]
,
(0,1)
matrices
[
30
,
52
]
, alternating-sign matrices
[
57
]
, and sign-restricted matrices
[
22
]
, see also the survey
[
8
]
. For example, the Gale-Ryser theorem
[
52
]

provides a characterisation, based on the notion of majorisation, of the pairs of vectors
\mathbf{r},\mathbf{c}
for which there exists a
(0,1)
matrix whose row-sum and column-sum vectors are
\mathbf{r}
and
\mathbf{c}
, respectively.
In a similar fashion, we not only show that a tensor having prescribed low-dimensional projections exists, but we also prove that a natural necessary combinatorial condition is in fact also sufficient for a system of low-dimensional “picture” tensors in order to be the set of projections of a high-dimensional
tensor.

We point out that our proof is constructive, as it allows to explicitly find a tensor with the desired characteristics.
As far as we know, the problem of reconstructing a tensor from low-dimensional projections has
hitherto
only been studied for matrices (but cf.
[
21
]
, where a related problem is investigated in three dimensions in the restricted setting of alternating-sign
3
-dimensional tensors). However, in order to rule out affine integer programming as an algorithm to solve AGC for all numbers of colours, we need to build crystals of arbitrarily high dimension
and hence approach the reconstruction problem for arbitrarily high-dimensional tensors.
In addition to its direct application
to the non-solvability of AGC,
we believe that our result might be of independent interest to the linear algebra and tensor theory communities.

2
Overview

Let
k\geq 2
be an integer.
Given a set
V
, we define
{V\choose\leq k}=\{S\subseteq V:1\leq|S|\leq k\}
.
Let
\mathbf{G}
and
\mathbf{H}
be two digraphs. We introduce a variable
\lambda_{S}(f)
for every
S\in{{\mathcal{V}}(\mathbf{G})\choose\leq k}

and every function
f:S\to{\mathcal{V}}(\mathbf{H})
, and a variable
\lambda_{\mathbf{g}}(f)
for every
\mathbf{g}=(g_{1},g_{2})\in{\mathcal{E}}(\mathbf{G})
and every
f:\{g_{1},g_{2}\}\to{\mathcal{V}}(\mathbf{H})
.
The
k
-th level of the
\AIP
hierarchy is given by the following constraints:

\begin{array}[]{lll}\mbox{(AIP1)}&\displaystyle\sum_{f:S\to{\mathcal{V}}(\mathbf{H})}\lambda_{S}(f)=1&S\in{{\mathcal{V}}(\mathbf{G})\choose\leq k}\\
\mbox{(AIP2)}&\displaystyle\lambda_{R}(f)=\sum_{\tilde{f}:S\to{\mathcal{V}}(\mathbf{H}),\;\tilde{f}|_{R}=f}\lambda_{S}(\tilde{f})&\emptyset\neq R\subseteq S\in{{\mathcal{V}}(\mathbf{G})\choose\leq k},f:R\to{\mathcal{V}}(\mathbf{H})\\
\mbox{(AIP3)}&\displaystyle\lambda_{R}(f)=\sum_{\tilde{f}:\{g_{1},g_{2}\}\to{\mathcal{V}}(\mathbf{H}),\;\tilde{f}|_{R}=f}\lambda_{\mathbf{g}}(\tilde{f})&\mathbf{g}\in{\mathcal{E}}(\mathbf{G}),\emptyset\neq R\subseteq\{g_{1},g_{2}\},f:R\to{\mathcal{V}}(\mathbf{H})\\
\mbox{(AIP4)}&\displaystyle\lambda_{\mathbf{g}}(f)=0&\mathbf{g}\in{\mathcal{E}}(\mathbf{G}),f:\{g_{1},g_{2}\}\to{\mathcal{V}}(\mathbf{H})\mbox{ with }f(\mathbf{g})\not\in{\mathcal{E}}(\mathbf{H}).\end{array}

We say that
\AIP^{k}(\mathbf{G},\mathbf{H})=\textsc{Yes}
if the system above admits a solution such that all variables take integer values.
For a fixed
k
, this can be checked in polynomial time in the number of vertices of the input digraph
\mathbf{G}
by solving a polynomial-sized system of linear equations over the integers
[
40
]
.
(For the “base level” of the hierarchy
k=1
, cf. Appendix
A
.)

Let
\tilde{\mathbf{H}}
be a digraph such that
\mathbf{H}\to\tilde{\mathbf{H}}
.
One easily checks that
\AIP^{k}(\mathbf{G},\mathbf{H})=\textsc{Yes}
if
\mathbf{G}\to\mathbf{H}
;
we say that the
k
-th level of
\AIP

solves

\PCSP(\mathbf{H},\tilde{\mathbf{H}})
if
\mathbf{G}\to\tilde{\mathbf{H}}
whenever
\AIP^{k}(\mathbf{G},\mathbf{H})=\textsc{Yes}
.
Clearly, if
\AIP^{k}(\mathbf{G},\mathbf{H})=\textsc{Yes}
then
\AIP^{k^{\prime}}(\mathbf{G},\mathbf{H})=\textsc{Yes}
for any level
k^{\prime}

lower
than
k
.
It follows that if some level of the hierarchy solves
\PCSP(\mathbf{H},\tilde{\mathbf{H}})
then any
higher
level of the hierarchy also solves it.
It is worth noticing that the
\AIP
hierarchy does not enforce consistency, in the sense that it is possible that a partial assignment is given nonzero weight without being a partial homomorphism. This is in sharp contrast to the “consistency-enforcing” algorithms mentioned in the Introduction, such as the bounded-width, Sherali-Adams LP, and Lasserre SDP hierarchies.
We now state the first main result of this work.

Theorem 1
.

No level of the
\AIP
hierarchy solves approximate graph colouring; i.e., for any fixed
3\leq c\leq d
, there is no
k
such that the
k
-th level of
\AIP
solves
\PCSP(\mathbf{K}_{c},\mathbf{K}_{d})
.

2.1
Affine integer programming and tensors

In order to prove Theorem
1
, we need to find instances of AGC that fool the
\AIP
hierarchy. Rather than working with the hierarchy itself, we shall lift the analysis to a tensor-theoretic framework. Next, we define some terminology on tensors that will be used throughout the paper.

Given
n
in the set
\mathbb{N}
of positive integer numbers, we let
[n]=\{1,\dots,n\}
. We also let
[0]=\emptyset
.
Given a tuple
\mathbf{n}=(n_{1},\dots,n_{q})\in\mathbb{N}^{q}
for some
q\in\mathbb{N}
, we let
[\mathbf{n}]=[n_{1}]\times\dots\times[n_{q}]
.
Given a tuple
\mathbf{b}=(b_{1},\dots,b_{q})\in[\mathbf{n}]
and a tuple
\mathbf{i}=(i_{1},\dots,i_{p})\in[q]^{p}
for
p,q\in\mathbb{N}
,
the
projection
of
\mathbf{b}
onto
\mathbf{i}
is the tuple
\mathbf{b}_{\mathbf{i}}=(b_{i_{1}},\dots,b_{i_{p}})
. Notice that
\mathbf{b}_{\mathbf{i}}\in[\mathbf{n}_{\mathbf{i}}]
. For
\tilde{\mathbf{n}}\in\mathbb{N}^{p}
, the

of two tuples
\mathbf{b}=(b_{1},\dots,b_{q})\in[\mathbf{n}]
and
\mathbf{c}=(c_{1},\dots,c_{p})\in[\tilde{\mathbf{n}}]
is the tuple
(\mathbf{b},\mathbf{c})=(b_{1},\dots,b_{q},c_{1},\dots,c_{p})
. Notice that
(\mathbf{b},\mathbf{c})\in[(\mathbf{n},\tilde{\mathbf{n}})]
.
It will be handy to extend the notation above to include tuples of length zero.
For any set
S
, we define
S^{0}=\{{\bm{\epsilon}}\}
, where
{\bm{\epsilon}}
denotes the empty tuple. For any tuple
\mathbf{x}
, we let
\mathbf{x}_{\bm{\epsilon}}={\bm{\epsilon}}
and
(\mathbf{x},{\bm{\epsilon}})=({\bm{\epsilon}},\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{x}
. We also define
[{\bm{\epsilon}}]=\{{\bm{\epsilon}}\}
.
For
n\in\mathbb{N}
, define the tuple
{\langle n\rangle}=(1,\dots,n)
. Also, let
{\langle 0\rangle}={\bm{\epsilon}}
. Given a tuple
\mathbf{x}
,
\#(\mathbf{x})
is the cardinality of the set of elements appearing in
\mathbf{x}
.

Let
\mathbb{N}_{0}=\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
.
Take a set
S
, an integer
q\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, and a tuple
\mathbf{n}\in\mathbb{N}^{q}
. We denote by
\mathcal{T}^{\mathbf{n}}(S)
the set of
tensors
on
q

modes
of sizes
n_{1},\dots,n_{q}
whose entries are in
S
; formally,
\mathcal{T}^{\mathbf{n}}(S)
is the set of functions from
[\mathbf{n}]
to
S
.
We sometimes denote a tensor in
\mathcal{T}^{\mathbf{n}}(S)
by
T=(t_{\mathbf{i}})_{\mathbf{i}\in[\mathbf{n}]}
, where
t_{\mathbf{i}}\in S
is the image of
\mathbf{i}
under
T
.
For example,
\mathcal{T}^{n}(S)
and
\mathcal{T}^{(m,n)}(S)
are the sets of
n
-vectors and
m\times n
matrices, respectively, having entries in
S
. Notice that
\mathcal{T}^{\bm{\epsilon}}(S)
is the set of functions from
[{\bm{\epsilon}}]=\{{\bm{\epsilon}}\}
to
S
, which we identify with
S
.
We will often consider
cubical
tensors, all of whose modes have equal size; i.e., tensors in the set
\mathcal{T}^{n\cdot\mathbf{1}_{q}}
for some
n\in\mathbb{N}
, where
\mathbf{1}_{q}
is the all-one tuple of length
q
.

We shall usually consider tensors having entries in the ring of integers
\mathbb{Z}
.
For
k,\ell,m\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, take
\mathbf{n}\in\mathbb{N}^{k}
,
\mathbf{p}\in\mathbb{N}^{\ell}
, and
\mathbf{q}\in\mathbb{N}^{m}
.
The
contraction
of two tensors
T=(t_{\mathbf{i}})_{\mathbf{i}\in[(\mathbf{n},\mathbf{p})]}\in\mathcal{T}^{(\mathbf{n},\mathbf{p})}(\mathbb{Z})
and
\tilde{T}=(\tilde{t}_{\mathbf{i}})_{\mathbf{i}\in[(\mathbf{p},\mathbf{q})]}\in\mathcal{T}^{(\mathbf{p},\mathbf{q})}(\mathbb{Z})
, denoted by

T\overset{\mathrm{\ell}}{\ast}\tilde{T}
,
is the tensor in
\mathcal{T}^{(\mathbf{n},\mathbf{q})}(\mathbb{Z})
such that, for
\mathbf{i}\in[\mathbf{n}]
and
\mathbf{j}\in[\mathbf{q}]
, the
(\mathbf{i},\mathbf{j})
-th entry of
T\overset{\mathrm{\ell}}{\ast}\tilde{T}
is given by

\sum_{\mathbf{z}\in[\mathbf{p}]}t_{(\mathbf{i},\mathbf{z})}\tilde{t}_{(\mathbf{z},\mathbf{j})}
.
If at least one of
k
and

equals zero – i.e., if we are contracting over all modes of

\tilde{T}
, we write

for

It is not hard to see that tensor contraction is associative, in the sense that
(T\overset{\mathrm{\ell}}{\ast}\tilde{T})\overset{\mathrm{m}}{\ast}\hat{T}=T\overset{\mathrm{\ell}}{\ast}(\tilde{T}\overset{\mathrm{m}}{\ast}\hat{T})
for any
\hat{T}\in\mathcal{T}^{(\mathbf{q},\textbf{r})}(\mathbb{Z})
, where
\textbf{r}\in\mathbb{N}^{n}
for some
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
. On the other hand, the order of operations matters for the “
\ast
” operator. For example, if
\overline{T}\in\mathcal{T}^{\mathbf{n}}(\mathbb{Z})
, the expression
(\overline{T}\ast T)\ast\tilde{T}
is well defined but the expression
\overline{T}\ast(T\ast\tilde{T})
is not, in general. For this reason, we define “
\ast
” to be left-associative; i.e.,

T_{1}\ast T_{2}\ast T_{3}
means
(T_{1}\ast T_{2})\ast T_{3}
.
The next example shows that contraction generalises various linear-algebraic products.

Example 2
.

For
m,n,p\in\mathbb{N}
, consider the tensors
c\in\mathcal{T}^{{\bm{\epsilon}}}(\mathbb{Z})=\mathbb{Z}
,
\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}\in\mathcal{T}^{m}(\mathbb{Z})
,
\mathbf{w}\in\mathcal{T}^{n}(\mathbb{Z})
,
M,N\in\mathcal{T}^{(m,n)}(\mathbb{Z})
, and
Q\in\mathcal{T}^{(n,p)}(\mathbb{Z})
. The following products can be seen as examples of contraction:

c\mathrel{\overset{\makebox[0.0pt]{\mbox{\tiny 0}}}{\ast}}\mathbf{u}=c\ast\mathbf{u}=c\mathbf{u}
,
c\mathrel{\overset{\makebox[0.0pt]{\mbox{\tiny 0}}}{\ast}}M=c\ast M=cM
(multiplication times scalar);

\mathbf{u}\mathrel{\overset{\makebox[0.0pt]{\mbox{\tiny 1}}}{\ast}}\mathbf{v}=\mathbf{u}\ast\mathbf{v}=\mathbf{u}^{T}\mathbf{v}
(inner product);

\mathbf{u}\mathrel{\overset{\makebox[0.0pt]{\mbox{\tiny 0}}}{\ast}}\mathbf{w}=\mathbf{u}\mathbf{w}^{T}
(outer product);

M\mathrel{\overset{\makebox[0.0pt]{\mbox{\tiny 1}}}{\ast}}Q=MQ
(standard matrix product);

M\mathrel{\overset{\makebox[0.0pt]{\mbox{\tiny 2}}}{\ast}}N=\tr(M^{T}N)
(Frobenius inner product).

Let
q\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
and
\mathbf{n}\in\mathbb{N}^{q}
.
Given
\mathbf{i}\in[\mathbf{n}]
, we denote by
E_{\mathbf{i}}
the
\mathbf{i}
-th standard unit tensor
; i.e., the tensor in

\mathcal{T}^{\mathbf{n}}(\mathbb{Z})
all of whose entries are
0
, except the

\mathbf{i}
-th entry that is
1
. Observe that, for any
T\in\mathcal{T}^{\mathbf{n}}(\mathbb{Z})
, we may express the
\mathbf{i}
-th entry of
T
as

E_{\mathbf{i}}\ast T
.
If
q=1
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
, and
i\in[n]
, notice that
E_{i}

Definition 24

(

Let
A,B

•

f(\mathbf{a})=f(\mathbf{a}_{\mathbf{i}})
for any
\mathbf{a}\in A^{L}

\mathbf{i}

•

f(\mathbf{a},b,b)=f(\mathbf{a},c,c)

\mathbf{a}\in A^{L-2}

.

Given
L\in\mathbb{N}
and two digraphs
\mathbf{H},\tilde{\mathbf{H}}
such that
\mathbf{H}\to\tilde{\mathbf{H}}
, an
L
-ary polymorphism
from
\mathbf{H}
to
\tilde{\mathbf{H}}
is a homomorphism from
\mathbf{H}^{L}
to
\tilde{\mathbf{H}}
, where

is the

\mathbf{H}

and whose edge set is
\mathcal{E}(\mathbf{H}^{L})=\{(\mathbf{h},{\bm{\ell}}):\mathbf{h},{\bm{\ell}}\in\mathcal{V}(\mathbf{H})^{L}\mbox{ and }(h_{i},\ell_{i})\in\mathcal{E}(\mathbf{H})\;\forall i\in[L]\}
.

The next result characterises the power of the affine integer programming relaxation (denoted by
\AIP

Theorem 25

(
[
6
]
)
.

\AIP
solves
\PCSP(\mathbf{H},\tilde{\mathbf{H}})
if and only if there exist
L

\mathbf{H}
to
\tilde{\mathbf{H}}
for any odd
L\geq 3
.

Let the vertex set of
\mathbf{K}_{2}
be
\mathcal{V}(\mathbf{K}_{2})=\{0,1\}
.
Given an odd integer
L\geq 3
, consider the function
f:\{0,1\}^{L}\to\{0,1\}
defined by
\mathbf{h}\mapsto\sum_{i=1}^{L}(-1)^{i+1}h_{i}\mod 2
, and notice that it is alternating. We claim that
f
is a polymorphism from
\mathbf{K}_{2}
to itself – i.e., a homomorphism from
\mathbf{K}_{2}^{L}
to
\mathbf{K}_{2}
. Take
\mathbf{h},{\bm{\ell}}\in\{0,1\}^{L}
and suppose that
(\mathbf{h},{\bm{\ell}})\in\mathcal{E}(\mathbf{K}_{2}^{L})
; i.e.,
h_{i}+\ell_{i}=1
for each
i\in[L]
.
If
f(\mathbf{h})=f({\bm{\ell}})
, we find

\displaystyle f(\mathbf{h})

\displaystyle=_{2}\sum_{i=1}^{L}(-1)^{i+1}h_{i}=_{2}\sum_{i=1}^{L}(-1)^{i+1}\ell_{i}=_{2}\sum_{i=1}^{L}(-1)^{i+1}(1-h_{i})=_{2}\sum_{i=1}^{L}(-1)^{i+1}-\sum_{i=1}^{L}(-1)^{i+1}h_{i}

\displaystyle=_{2}1-f(\mathbf{h}),

so
2f(\mathbf{h})=_{2}1
, a contradiction. As a consequence,
f(\mathbf{h})\neq f({\bm{\ell}})
, so
f((\mathbf{h},{\bm{\ell}}))\in\mathcal{E}(\mathbf{K}_{2})
. Hence,
f
is a polymorphism, as claimed.

\AIP
