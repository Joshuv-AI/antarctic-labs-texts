Inverse eigenvalue and related problems for hollow matrices described by graphs
F. Scott Dahlgren, Zachary Gershkoff, Leslie Hogben, Sara Motlaghian, Derek Young

Abstract
A hollow matrix described by a graph G G is a real symmetric matrix having all diagonal entries equal to zero and with the off-diagonal entries governed by the adjacencies in G G . For a given graph G G , the determination of all possible spectra of matrices associated with G G is the hollow inverse eigenvalue problem for G G . Solutions to the hollow inverse eigenvalue problems for paths and complete bipartite graphs are presented. Results for related subproblems such as possible ordered multiplicity lists, maximum multiplicity of an eigenvalue, and minimum number of distinct eigenvalues are presented for additional families of graphs.



Keywords
inverse eigenvalue problem; hollow matrix; maximum multiplicity; minimum number of distinct eigenvalues; ordered multiplicity list; minimum rank; maximum nullity

05C50, 15A18, 15A29, 15B57

Introduction

Inverse eigenvalue problems, which refer to determining all possible multisets of eigenvalues (spectra) for matrices fitting some description, appear in various contexts throughout engineering and the mathematical, physical, biological, and social sciences. Graphs can be used to describe relationships in an application and the eigenvalues of associated matrices govern the behavior of the system. The
inverse eigenvalue problem of a graph (IEP-
G
)
refers to determining the possible spectra (multisets of eigenvalues) of real symmetric matrices whose pattern of nonzero off-diagonal entries is described by the edges of a given graph.

More precisely, a graph
G=(V(G),E(G))
consists of a finite nonempty set of vertices
V(G)
and a set
E(G)
of edges, which are two element subsets of vertices. The edge
\{v_{i},v_{j}\}
is often denoted by
v_{i}v_{j}
. The
order
of
G
is the number of vertices in
G
.
The
set of symmetric matrices described by
G
is

\mathcal{S}(G)=\{A=[a_{ij}]\in S_{n}(\mathbb{R}):a_{ij}\neq 0\mbox{ if and only if }v_{i}v_{j}\in E(G)\mbox{ for all }1\leq i<j\leq n\},

where
n
denotes the order of
G
and
S_{n}(\mathbb{R})
denotes that set of symmetric
n\times n
real matrices. Thus the IEP-
G
is to determine the possible spectra of the matrices in
\mathcal{S}(G)
. Let
\mathcal{A}(G)
denote the adjacency matrix of
G
, i.e.,
a_{ij}=1
if
v_{i}v_{j}\in E(G)
and
a_{ij}=0
otherwise; clearly
\mathcal{A}(G)\in\mathcal{S}(G)
, as are several other matrices associated with a graph (such as the Laplacian).

The IEP-
G
is a very challenging problem and was originally approached through the study of subproblems, such at the maximum multiplicity of an eigenvalue, the minimum number of distinct eigenvalues, and ordered multiplicity lists for eigenvalues.
The
maximum multiplicity of
G
is
\operatorname{M}(G)=\max\{\operatorname{mult}_{A}(\lambda):A\in\mathcal{S}(G)\}
, where

\operatorname{mult}_{A}(\lambda)
denotes the multiplicity of
\lambda
as an eigenvalue of
A
. Since
A\in\mathcal{S}(G)
implies
A-\lambda I\in\mathcal{S}(G)
,
\operatorname{M}(G)=\max\{\operatorname{null}A:A\in\mathcal{S}(G)\}
. A related parameter is the minimum rank of
G
,

\operatorname{mr}(G)=\min\{\operatorname{rank}A:A\in\mathcal{S}(G)\}
.

Our focus is on the inverse eigenvalue problem for hollow symmetric matrices described by a graph. A
hollow matrix
is a square matrix all of whose diagonal entries are zero. In some applications for which spectra need to be determined, the diagonal entrees are known to be zero. For a graph
G
of order
n
, the matrices in

\mathcal{S}_{0}(G)=\{A\in\mathcal{S}(G):a_{ii}=0,\ 1\leq i\leq n\}

can also be viewed as weighted adjacency matrices of
G
.
The
hollow symmetric inverse eigenvalue problem of a graph (HIEP-
G
)
is to determine the possible spectra of
\mathcal{S}_{0}(G)
.

The HIEP-
G
is also a very hard problem, and related problems that shed light on the HIEP-
G
are studied, as is the case for the IEP-
G
.
The
maximum hollow nullity
and
minimum hollow rank
of
G
, defined in
[
7
]
as
maximum zero-diagonal nullity
and
minimum zero-diagonal rank
, are

\operatorname{M}_{0}(G)=\max\{\operatorname{null}A:A\in\mathcal{S}_{0}(G)\}\mbox{ and }\operatorname{mr}_{0}(G)=\min\{\operatorname{rank}A:A\in\mathcal{S}_{0}(G)\}.

As usual,
\operatorname{mr}_{0}(G)+\operatorname{M}_{0}(G)=|V(G)|
.
However, the hollow maximum nullity is not generally the maximum multiplicity of an eigenvalue of a hollow matrix, because it refers only to the maximum multiplicity of eigenvalue zero. The
maximum hollow multiplicity
of
G
is defined to be

\operatorname{MM}_{0}(G)=\max\{\operatorname{mult}_{A}(\lambda):A\in\mathcal{S}_{0}(G)\}.

For a symmetric matrix
A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
,
q(A)
is the number of distinct eigenvalues of
A
.
Define

q(G)=\min\{q(A):A\in\mathcal{S}(G)\}\mbox{ and }q_{0}(G)=\min\{q(A):A\in\mathcal{S}_{0}(G)\}.

Let the distinct eigenvalues of a symmetric matrix
A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
be denoted by

\mu_{1}(A)<\cdots<\mu_{q}(A)
with multiplicities
m_{1}(A),\dots,m_{q}(A)
, respectively.
The
ordered multiplicity list of
A
is
{\bf m}(A)=(m_{1}(A),\dots,m_{q}(A))
. The
set of ordered multiplicity lists of
G
and the
set of hollow ordered multiplicity lists of
G
are

{\bf m}(G)=\{{\bf m}(A):A\in\mathcal{S}(G)\}\mbox{ and }{\bf m}_{0}(G)=\{{\bf m}(A):A\in\mathcal{S}_{0}(G)\}.

We present full solutions to the HIEP-
G
for paths, complete bipartite graphs, and graphs of order at most three in Section
4

q_{0}(G)
,
\operatorname{M}_{0}(G)

The
trace constraint
for hollow matrices (
1
) is that the sum of the eigenvalues is zero. When the spectrum is written as distinct eigenvalues and multiplicities, i.e.,
\{\mu_{1}^{(m_{1})},\mu_{2}^{(m_{2})},\dots,\mu_{q}^{(m_{q})}\}

\sum_{i=1}^{q}m_{i}\mu_{i}=0.

(1)

We will see that the trace constraint is a powerful tool for analyzing spectra of hollow matrices.
An ordered multiplicity list
(m_{1},m_{2},\dots,m_{q})\in{\bf m}_{0}(G)
is
hollow spectrally arbitrary
if for every set of real numbers
\mu_{1}<\mu_{2}<\dots<\mu_{q}
that satisfies (
1
) there is some
A\in\mathcal{S}_{0}(G)
such that
\operatorname{spec}(A)=\{\mu_{1}^{(m_{1})},\mu_{2}^{(m_{2})},\dots,\mu_{q}^{(m_{q})}\}
.

Let
A=[a_{ij}]
be a hollow symmetric matrix. Given a generalized cycle
\mathcal{C}
of
\mathcal{G}(A)

\operatorname{nc}(\mathcal{C})
denote the number of distinct cycles (of order 3 or more) in
\mathcal{C}
, and
\operatorname{ne}(\mathcal{C})
denote the number of even components of
\mathcal{C}
, i.e., the number of cycles of even order at least four plus the number of edges. With a generalized cycle
\mathcal{C}
, we can associate a permutation
\pi_{\mathcal{C}}
of the vertices of
\mathcal{C}
as follows: For each cycle in
\mathcal{C}
, fix an orientation and then associate a directed graph cycle
(v_{j_{1}},v_{j_{2}},\dots,v_{j_{\ell}})
with the cyclic permutation
(v_{j_{1}}v_{j_{2}}\cdots\,v_{j_{\ell}})
. Each edge component
\{v_{i_{1}},v_{i_{2}}\}
of
\mathcal{C}
is associated with the transposition
(v_{i_{1}}v_{i_{2}})
. The permutation
\pi_{\mathcal{C}}
is defined to be the product of these associated permutation cycles. There are
2^{\operatorname{nc}(\mathcal{C})}
different choices for the orientation of the cycles of
\mathcal{C}
, and each choice yields a permutation that has the same sign as
\pi_{\mathcal{C}}
, namely
(-1)^{\operatorname{ne}(\mathcal{C})}
.
Recall that

p_{A}(x)=\det(xI-A)=x^{n}-S_{1}(A)x^{n-1}+S_{2}(A)x^{n-2}+\dots\pm S_{n}(A)

where
S_{k}(A)
is the sum of all order

(so
S_{n}(A)=\det A
and
S_{1}(A)=\operatorname{tr}A
). Viewing

as a weighted adjacency matrix of

[

that

can be computed using generalized cycles of

S_{k}(A)=\sum_{\mathcal{C}\in\operatorname{cyc}_{k}(\mathcal{G}(A))}(-1)^{\operatorname{ne}(\mathcal{C})}2^{\operatorname{nc}(\mathcal{C})}a_{i_{1}\pi_{\mathcal{C}}(i_{1})}\dots a_{i_{k}\pi_{\mathcal{C}}(i_{k})},

(2)

where the sum over the empty set is zero. (If
A
has nonzero diagonal elements then the formula becomes much less useful and a looped graph must be used to describe
A

2

\operatorname{mr}_{0}(G)
.
Observe that
\operatorname{mr}_{0}(G)\leq r
if
G
has no generalized cycles of order greater than
r
, and
\operatorname{mr}_{0}(G)=r
if
G
has no generalized cycles of order greater than
r
and
G
has a unique generalized cycle of order
r
.

When studying the ranks of matrices in
\mathcal{S}(G)
, one studies only minimum rank, because it is well known and easy to see that the maximum rank is the order of the graph

, and every rank between the minimum and maximum ranks is realizable. However, there are many graphs for which the maximum rank of a hollow matrix described by a graph
G
must be less than the order of
G
.
The
maximum hollow rank
of a graph

is

\operatorname{MR}_{0}(G)=\max\{\operatorname{rank}A:\penalty\ A\in\mathcal{S}_{0}(G)\}.

.

For a graph

,

Maximum nullity, minimum number of distinct eigenvalues, and ordered multiplicity lists all provide information that can in some cases be used to solve the inverse eigenvalue problem for a specific graph or family of graphs. Recently the Strong Spectral Property (SSP) and the Strong Multiplicity Property (SMP) were introduced in
[
5
]
. These tools, which extend the Strong Arnold Property for nullity, have been the focus of much recent research in the IEP-
G
. While we do not provide the details here, a fundamental principle of a strong property is subgraph monotonicity. In the case of the Strong Arnold Property, this means that the existence of a matrix
A\in\mathcal{S}(G)
that has the Strong Arnold Property implies the existence of
B\in\mathcal{S}(H)
with
\operatorname{null}B\geq\operatorname{null}A
for any graph
H
having
G
as a subgraph. For the Strong Spectral Property, if
A\in\mathcal{S}_{0}(G)
has this property and
G
is a subgraph of
H
, then there is a matrix
B\in\mathcal{S}_{0}(H)
such that
\operatorname{spec}(A)\subseteq\operatorname{spec}(B)
.
This naturally raises the question of the existence of strong properties for hollow matrices. Unfortunately, hollow matrices do not seem well suited to strong properties, as we show in the next example.

Example 1.2
.

For any graph
G
of order
n
,
G\circ K_{1}
is constructed from
G
by adding a leaf to each vertex of
G
. Note that
\operatorname{M}_{0}(G\circ K_{1})=0
, i.e.,
\operatorname{mult}_{A}(0)=0
for
A\in\mathcal{S}_{0}(G\circ K_{1})
, because
G\circ K_{1}
has a unique generalized cycle of order
2n
. For example,
\operatorname{mult}_{A}(0)=n-2
for
A\in K_{1,n-1}
and
\operatorname{mult}_{B}(0)=0
for
B\in\mathcal{S}_{0}(K_{1,n-1}\circ K_{1})
. This implies that there cannot be a nonzero numerical “strong” property below
\operatorname{M}_{0}
that is induced subgraph monotone, and there cannot be a strong spectral property for hollow matrices.

The general failure of strong properties for hollow matrices is not surprising, because the proofs of subgraph monotonicity for strong properties are based on small perturbations, using the fact that for a sufficiently small perturbation a nonzero entry remains nonzero but a zero entry can be changed to nonzero. However, some perturbation techniques can still be used, as in the proof of Proposition
2.18
, where we show the ordered multiplicity list with all entries equal to one can alwaysde be realized.

Preliminary results

In this section we present examples and general results about the HIEP-G and related parameters.

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
G
be a graph of order
n\geq 2
. If
(m_{1},m_{2})
is an ordered multiplicity list of
G
, then
(m_{1},m_{2})
is hollow spectrally arbitrary.

Proof.

When there are only two distinct eigenvalues, the trace constraint (
1

\operatorname{spec}(A)=\{\mu_{1}^{(k)},\mu_{2}^{(n-k)}\}
,
\mu_{2}=-\frac{k}{n-k}\mu_{1}
(observe that both
\mu_{1}
and
\mu_{2}
are nonzero). Given that one specific spectrum
\operatorname{spec}(A^{\prime})=\{{\mu^{\prime}}_{1}^{(k)},{\mu^{\prime}}_{2}^{(n-k)}\}
is realized by
A^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}_{0}(G)
, the matrix
A=\frac{\mu_{1}}{{\mu^{\prime}}_{1}}A^{\prime}
has
\operatorname{spec}(A)=\{\mu_{1}^{(k)},\mu_{2}^{(n-k)}\}
.
∎

Example 2.2
.

Note that
\operatorname{spec}(\mathcal{A}(K_{n}))=\{n-1,(-1)^{(n-1)}\}

Remark 2.3

If

\operatorname{spec}(G)=\operatorname{spec}(G_{1})\cup\operatorname{spec}(G_{2})

The

of a graph

is

\operatorname{M}_{+}(G)=\max\{\operatorname{null}A:A\in\mathcal{S}(G)\mbox{ and $A$ is positive semidefinite}\}.

Let
A
be a positive semidefinite matrix of rank

R=[{\bf r}_{1},\dots,{\bf r}_{n}]
such that

It is well-known that the least

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
G
be a graph with no isolated vertices. Then there exists a matrix
B\in\mathcal{S}_{0}(G)
such that
m_{1}(B)=\operatorname{M}_{+}(G)
.

Proof.

Let
m=\operatorname{M}_{+}(G)
. Choose a positive semidefinite matrix
A\in\mathcal{S}(G)
such that
\operatorname{null}A=m
. Then there is an
(n-m)\times n

such that

. Define
A^{\prime}=D^{T}AD

is 1, and

. Define

. ∎

Note that the process of constructing

For any graph

Proposition 2.6

If

where

if

and

if
0\in\operatorname{spec}(A)
.

The process of taking the Cartesian product with
K_{2}
can be repeated without raising

beyond the first Cartesian product.

Corollary 2.13
.

If there exists
A\in\mathcal{S}_{0}(G)
with

and
\operatorname{spec}(A)=-\operatorname{spec}(A)

q_{0}(G\,\Box\,K_{2}\,\Box\,\cdots

\,\Box\,K_{2})\leq q_{0}(G)+1

2.2
\operatorname{M}_{0}(G)
and
\operatorname{MM}_{0}(G)

In this section, we focus on the maximum hollow nullity
\operatorname{M}_{0}(G)
and maximum hollow multiplicity
\operatorname{MM}_{0}(G)
of a graph
G
.
It is well known that adding a dominating vertex to a graph with no isolated vertices does not change the minimum rank (or positive semidefinite minimum rank): if
A\in\mathcal{S}(G)
and
\operatorname{rank}A=\operatorname{mr}(G)
, choose
{\bf v}
such that every entry of
A{\bf v}
is nonzero and construct the matrix
B
as in the next proof. It is not so simple to find such a
{\bf v}
when the diagonal must be zero (and it is not always possible).

Lemma 2.14
.

Let
A
be an
n\times n
hollow symmetric matrix that has a nonzero eigenvalue
\lambda
with
\operatorname{mult}_{A}(\lambda)\geq 2
and an eigenvector
{\bf w}
for
\lambda
that has every entry nonzero. Then there exists a vector
{\bf v}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that both
{\bf v}^{T}A{\bf v}=0
and every entry of
A{\bf v}
is nonzero. Furthermore,
B=\begin{bmatrix}A&A{\bf v}\\
{\bf v}^{T}A&{\bf v}^{T}A{\bf v}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathcal{S}_{0}(\mathcal{G}(A)\vee K_{1})
and
\operatorname{rank}B=\operatorname{rank}A
.

Proof.

Denote the eigenvalues of
A
by
\lambda_{1}=\lambda,\lambda_{2}=\lambda,\lambda_{3},\dots,\lambda_{n}
(no ordering implied). Let
{\bf u}
be a multiple of
{\bf w}
of length one, and choose a basis of orthonormal eigenvectors
{\bf x}_{1}={\bf u},{\bf x}_{2},\dots,{\bf x}_{n}
with
A{\bf x}_{i}=\lambda_{i}{\bf x}_{i}
. For a real number
a
with
-\sqrt{2}<a<\sqrt{2}
, define

{\bf v}_{a}=a{\bf x}_{1}+\sqrt{2-a^{2}}{\bf x}_{2}+{\bf x}_{3}+\dots+{\bf x}_{n}.

Since
{\bf x}_{1},\dots,{\bf x}_{n}
are orthonormal eigenvectors,

A{\bf v}_{a}=a\lambda{\bf u}+\sqrt{2-a^{2}}\lambda{\bf x}_{2}+\lambda_{3}{\bf x}_{3}+\dots+\lambda_{n}{\bf x}_{n}

and

{\bf v}_{a}^{T}A{\bf v}_{a}=a^{2}\lambda+(2-a^{2})\lambda+\lambda_{3}+\dots+\lambda_{n}=\operatorname{tr}(A)=0.

To have all entries of
A{\bf v}_{a}
nonzero, choose
a
to avoid the solutions to the equations

a\lambda({\bf u})_{j}+\sqrt{2-a^{2}}\lambda({\bf x}_{2})_{j}+\lambda_{3}({\bf x}_{3})_{j}+\dots+\lambda_{n}({\bf x}_{n})_{j}

for
j=1,\dots,n
(note that each equation is nontrivial because
\lambda({\bf u})_{j}\neq 0
). The last statement is immediate, given
{\bf v}={\bf v}_{a}
with appropriate choice of
a
.
∎

Lemma 2.15
.

Let
G
be a graph with no isolated vertices such that
(m_{1},\dots,m_{r})\in{\bf m}_{0}(G)
and let
H=G\,\vee K_{1}
. Then there exists
B\in\mathcal{S}_{0}(H)
with
m_{1}(B)=m_{1}+1
, and
\operatorname{MM}_{0}(H)\geq m_{1}+1
.

Proof.

Let
A\in\mathcal{S}_{0}(G)
such that
{\bf m}(A)=(m_{1},\dots,m_{r})
, and let
\mu=-\mu_{1}(A)
. Define
A^{\prime}=A+\mu I_{n}
so
A^{\prime}
is positive semidefinite. Then there is an
(n-m_{1})\times n
matrix
R=[{\bf r}_{1},\dots,{\bf r}_{n}]
such that
A^{\prime}=R^{T}R
. Then
{\bf r}_{i}^{T}{\bf r}_{i}=\mu
for
i=1,\dots,n
since every diagonal entry of
A^{\prime}
is equal to
\mu
, and for
j\neq i
,
{\bf r}_{i}^{T}{\bf r}_{j}\neq 0
if and only if
ij\in E(G)
. Choose an
(n-m_{1})
-vector
{\bf r}_{n+1}
such that
{\bf r}_{i}^{T}{\bf r}_{n+1}\neq 0
for
i=1,\dots,n
and
{\bf r}_{n+1}^{T}{\bf r}_{n+1}=\mu
(a random vector normalized to have length
\mu
will work with high probability). Define
R^{\prime}=[{\bf r}_{1},\dots,{\bf r}_{n},{\bf r}_{n+1}]
and
A^{\prime\prime}=R^{\prime T}R^{\prime}
. Then
A^{\prime\prime}\in\mathcal{S}(H)
and every diagonal entry of
A^{\prime\prime}
is
\mu
, so
B=A^{\prime\prime}-\mu I_{n+1}\in\mathcal{S}_{0}(H)
. Since
\operatorname{rank}A^{\prime\prime}=\operatorname{rank}A^{\prime}=m_{1}
,
\operatorname{null}A^{\prime\prime}=m_{1}+1
and
m_{1}(B)=m_{1}+1
. Thus
\operatorname{MM}_{0}(H)\geq m_{1}+1
.
∎

Corollary 2.16
.

If there exists
A\in\mathcal{S}_{0}(G)
such that
\operatorname{MM}_{0}(A)=\operatorname{mult}_{A}(\mu_{1}(A))
, then
\operatorname{MM}_{0}(G\vee K_{1})=\operatorname{MM}_{0}(G)+1
.

Proof.

Suppose there exists
A\in\mathcal{S}_{0}(G)
such that
\operatorname{MM}_{0}(A)=\operatorname{mult}_{A}(\mu_{1}(A))
. Then
\operatorname{MM}_{0}(G\vee K_{1})\geq\operatorname{MM}_{0}(G)+1
by Lemma
2.15
. Interlacing implies
\operatorname{MM}_{0}(G\vee K_{1})\leq\operatorname{MM}_{0}(G)+1
. ∎

Note that the process of constructing
B
in Lemma
2.15
preserves the nullity but not the spectrum. Interlacing provides some control of other multiple eigenvalues but multiplicities may each be reduced by one and the number of distinct eigenvalues may increase.

2.3
Simple eigenvalues and ordered multiplicity lists

It is well known that every graph allows a matrix
A\in\mathcal{S}(G)
having every eigenvalue simple. This is not the case for a graph
G
of order
n
that has
\operatorname{MR}_{0}(G)\leq n-2
, since
\operatorname{mult}_{A}(0)\geq n-\operatorname{MR}_{0}(G)\geq 2
for
A\in\mathcal{S}_{0}(G)
. In this section we show that every graph
G
allows a matrix of maximum hollow rank in which every nonzero eigenvalue is simple.

Recall that
\operatorname{M}(P_{n})=1
, from which it is immediate that
\operatorname{MM}_{0}(P_{n})=1
and
q_{0}(P_{n})=n
.
Thus
(1,1,\dots,1)\in\mathcal{S}_{0}(P_{n})
for all
n
.

Lemma 2.17
.

For any cycle
C_{n}
, there is a matrix
A\in\mathcal{S}_{0}(C_{n})
such that
{\bf m}(A)=(1,1,\dots,1)
and every eigenvalue of
A
is nonzero.

Proof.

Let
P_{n}
be obtained from
C_{n}
by deleting the edge
\{1,n\}
. Choose
A\in\mathcal{S}_{0}(P_{n})
. Note that the eigenvalues of
A
are distinct.
Let
H
be the graph with
V(H)=V(G)
and
E(H)=\{1,n\}
. Define
A_{\varepsilon}=A+\varepsilon\mathcal{A}(H)
. Then for
\varepsilon>0
,
A_{\varepsilon}\in\mathcal{S}_{0}(C_{n})
, and the eigenvalues of
A_{\varepsilon}
are distinct for
\varepsilon
sufficiently small by continuity. If
n
is even, then the eigenvalues of
A
are all nonzero, so the eigenvalues of
A_{\varepsilon}
are nonzero for
\varepsilon
sufficiently small. If
n
is odd, then
\operatorname{mr}_{0}(C_{n})=n
, so the eigenvalues of
A_{\varepsilon}
are all nonzero.

∎

Proposition 2.18
.

Let
G
be a graph of order
n
. There is a matrix
A\in\mathcal{S}_{0}(G)
that has
\operatorname{MR}_{0}(G)
simple nonzero eigenvalues. If
\operatorname{MR}_{0}(G)\geq n-1
, then there is a matrix
A\in\mathcal{S}_{0}(G)
such that
{\bf m}(A)=(1,1,\dots,1)
.

Proof.

Choose a generalized cycle
\mathcal{C}
of order
\operatorname{MR}_{0}(G)
. Note that
\mathcal{C}
is the disjoint union of
k\geq 1
cycles
C_{n_{i}}
(where an edge is denoted by
C_{2}
). For each component
C_{n_{i}}
, there is a matrix
A_{i}\in\mathcal{S}_{0}(C_{n_{i}})
that has
n_{i}
distinct nonzero eigenvalues. Choose nonzero
\alpha_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
such that the set of eigenvalues of the matrices
\alpha_{i}A_{i}
has
n
distinct nonzero elements. Then the eigenvalues of
A=\alpha_{1}A_{1}\oplus\dots\oplus\alpha_{k}A_{k}
are distinct and nonzero. Let
H
be the graph with
V(H)=V(G)
and
E(H)=\{e\in E(G):e\not\in E(\mathcal{C})\}
. Define
A_{\varepsilon}=A+\varepsilon\mathcal{A}(H)
. Then for
\varepsilon>0
,
A_{\varepsilon}\in\mathcal{S}_{0}(G)
, and the eigenvalues of

are distinct and nonzero for

sufficiently small by continuity. The last statement is immediate.
∎

The minimum number of distinct eigenvalues of a graph is an active area of research, whereas the maximum number of distinct eigenvalues of a matrix in
\mathcal{S}(G)
is the order of the graph
G
. For hollow matrices, the maximum number of distinct eigenvalues is provided by Proposition

.

Corollary 2.19
.

Let
G
be a graph of order
n
. The maximum number of distinct eigenvalues of a matrix in
\mathcal{S}_{0}(G)
is
\min\{\operatorname{MR}_{0}(G)+1,n\}
.

3
Bipartite graphs

For a hollow matrix described by a bipartite graph, the spectrum is symmetric about the origin, i.e.,
\operatorname{spec}(A)=-\operatorname{spec}(A)
for any
A\in\mathcal{S}_{0}(G)
. That result (Theorem
3.2
) is established in this section, together with numerous consequences and other results specific to bipartite graphs. Results in this section are used to solve the hollow IEP-
G
for all complete bipartite graphs
K_{m,n}
in Section
4
. An
order
k
principal minor of
A
is the determinant of a
k\times k
principal submatrix of
A
.

Remark 3.1
.

Suppose
G
is a bipartite graph, and let
A\in\mathcal{S}_{0}(G)
. Then
A
has even rank, because

A=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}0&B\\
B^{T}&0\\
\end{array}\right]

for some matrix
B
, so the rank of
A
is twice the rank of
B
.
Let
S\subset V(G)
such that
|S|=k
, with
k
odd. Then, the induced subgraph
G[S]
is again bipartite. Since
\operatorname{rank}A[S]
must be even,
A[S]\in\mathcal{S}_{0}(G[S])
cannot be of full rank. Therefore, the corresponding principal minor is zero. Hence, every odd order principal minor of
A
is zero.

Theorem 3.2
.

Suppose
G
is a bipartite graph on
n
vertices, and
A\in\mathcal{S}_{0}(G)
. Then
\operatorname{spec}(A)=-\operatorname{spec}(A)
.

Proof.

Let
p_{A}(x)=x^{n}+a_{n-1}x^{n-1}+\dots+a_{1}x+a_{0}
be the characteristic polynomial of
A
. Recall that
a_{n-k}=(-1)^{k}S_{k}(A)
where
S_{k}(A)
is the sum of all order
k
principal minors of
A
.
From Remark
3.1
, the characteristic polynomial of
A
is

p_{A}(x)=x^{n}+S_{2}(A)x^{n-2}+S_{4}(A)x^{n-4}+\dots

because
S_{k}(A)=0
for odd
k
.
First, we suppose
n
is even.
We reparameterize
p_{A}
with
y=x^{2}
:

p_{A}(x)=f_{A}(y):=y^{n/2}+S_{2}(A)y^{n/2-1}+S_{4}(A)y^{n/2-2}+\dots+S_{n/2}(A).

Next, we factor

f_{A}(y)=(y-\alpha_{1})(y-\alpha_{2})\cdots(y-\alpha_{n/2}),

where
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\dots,\alpha_{n/2}
are non-negative real numbers because
A
is a real symmetric matrix. Since
p_{A}(x^{2})=f_{A}(y)
,

\displaystyle p_{A}(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(x^{2}-\alpha_{1})(x^{2}-\alpha_{2})\cdots(x^{2}-\alpha_{n/2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(x-\sqrt{\alpha_{1}})(x+\sqrt{\alpha_{1}})(x-\sqrt{\alpha_{2}})(x+\sqrt{\alpha_{2}})\cdots(x-\sqrt{\alpha_{n/2}})(x+\sqrt{\alpha_{n/2}}).

Therefore,
\operatorname{spec}(A)=-\operatorname{spec}(A)
for even
n
.

Next, we suppose
n
is odd. Then, from Remark
3.1
, the characteristic polynomial of
A
is

\displaystyle p_{A}(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle x^{n}+S_{2}(A)x^{n-2}+S_{4}(A)x^{n-4}+\dots+S_{n-1}(A)x

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle x(x^{n-1}+S_{2}(A)x^{n-3}+\dots+S_{n-1}(A)).

We can therefore define
f_{A}(y)
where
p_{A}(x)=xf_{A}(y)
with
y=x^{2}
and
f_{A}(y)
factors as in the case where
n
is even. As the remaining eigenvalue is zero,
\operatorname{spec}(A)=-\operatorname{spec}(A)
for odd
n
.
∎

Corollary 3.3
.

If
G
is bipartite,
G\neq\overline{K_{n}}
, and the order of
G
is odd, then
q_{0}(G)\geq 3
.

Corollary 3.4
.

Suppose
G
is a bipartite graph of order
2k+1
such that
G
has no isolated vertices and
\operatorname{M}_{+}(G)=k
. Then
(k,1,k)\in{\bf m}_{0}(G)
.

Proof.

By Lemma
2.4
, there exists
B\in\mathcal{S}_{0}(G)
such that
m_{1}(B)=k
. Since
\operatorname{spec}(B)=-\operatorname{spec}(B)
,
\operatorname{spec}(B)=\{(-\lambda)^{(k)},0,\lambda^{(k)}\}
.

∎

Note that Lemma
2.4
can also be used to show there is an ordered multiplicity list of the form

where
m_{1}=\operatorname{M}_{+}(G)
whenever

is bipartite with no isolated vertices.

Proposition 3.5
.

Let
G
be a graph of even order
n
such that
\operatorname{mr}_{0}(G)\geq n-1
and there exists
v\in V(G)
such that

is bipartite.
If
m_{1}+\dots+m_{r-1}=\frac{n-2}{2}=m_{r+1}+\dots+m_{s}
, then
(m_{1},\dots,m_{r-1},2,m_{r+1},\dots,m_{s})\not\in{\bf m}_{0}(G)
.

Proof.

Let
k=\frac{n-2}{2}
. Suppose
m_{1}+\dots+m_{r-1}=\frac{n-2}{2}=m_{r+1}+\dots+m_{s}
and
A\in\mathcal{S}_{0}(G)
has
(m_{1},\dots,m_{r-1},2,

m_{r+1},\dots,m_{s})\in{\bf m}(A)
. Denote the eigenvalues of

by
\alpha_{1}\leq\dots\leq\alpha_{k-1}<\alpha_{k}=\alpha_{k+1}<\alpha_{k+2}\leq\dots\leq\alpha_{n}
. Let

, and denote its eigenvalues in order by

. Since

is bipartite,
\beta_{i}=-\beta_{n-i}
for

. Since

is odd,
\beta_{k}=0

\alpha_{k}=\alpha_{k+1}
,

\operatorname{rank}A\leq n-2

.
∎

If
G
is a bipartite graph and

, then
\operatorname{spec}(A)
has the form

\{\mu_{1}^{(m_{1})},\dots,\mu_{k}^{(m_{k})},(-\mu_{k})^{(m_{k})},\dots,(-\mu_{1})^{(m_{1})}\}\mbox{ or }\{\mu_{1}^{(m_{1})},\dots,\mu_{k}^{(m_{k})},0^{(m_{k+1})},(-\mu_{k})^{(m_{k})},\dots,(-\mu_{1})^{(m_{1})}\}.

.

.

Suppose

Corollary 3.7
.

Let
G
be a bipartite graph. Then

and

.

A real (respectively, complex)

is orthogonal (respectively, unitary) if

(respectively,

). Orthogonal matrices play an important role in the study of bipartite graphs

for arbitrary graphs
[
1
]
. A graph

Remark 3.8
.

Let
G
be a graph. Since the only possible eigenvalues of an orthogonal matrix are

and
-1
, it is immediate that if

is orthogonal, then

(since

must have an edge to allow an orthogonal matrix). Now suppose that

is a bipartite graph, the order of

and
\frac{1}{\mu}A
is orthogonal (because any real symmetric matrix with all eigenvalues equal to

is potentially hollow orthogonal.
Thus a bipartite graph

is potentially hollow orthogonal if and only if

.

When studying the IEP-

(without the restriction that matrices are hollow), we have the opportunity to shift (add

Although the emphasis in this section is on bipartite graphs, orthogonal matrices play a broader role, as illustrated in the next result.

Proposition 3.9
.

Let

Suppose there exists

If

.
∎

Corollary 3.10

Let

The

\beta_{ij}=0

The next result does not restrict matrices to being hollow.

Theorem 3.11
.

Let
G
be a bipartite graph with no isolated vertices having partite sets of orders

. Then

\mathcal{B}_{G}

Proof.

It is immediate that

is potentially orthogonal implies

and

and

be unitary. Then
U=\begin{bmatrix}D&B\\

with
D=\operatorname{diag}(d_{1},\dots,d_{r})
and

\begin{bmatrix}I_{r}&O_{r,r^{\prime}}\\
O_{r,r^{\prime}}&I_{r^{\prime}}\end{bmatrix}=I=U^{T}U=U^{2}=\begin{bmatrix}D^{2}+BB^{T}&DB+BD^{\prime}\\
B^{T}D+D^{\prime}B^{T}&(D^{\prime})^{2}+B^{T}B\end{bmatrix}.

Suppose that

0=(U^{2})_{i,r+j}=d_{i}b_{i,r+j}+b_{i,r+j}d^{\prime}_{j},\mbox{ so }d^{\prime}_{j}=-d_{i}.

and

implies

. Since

is positive semidefinite,

implies

is unitary, so
\mathcal{B}_{G}
is potentially unitary. Since both

and

are full rank,

.

Finally, suppose

∎
