The statistics of Rayleigh-Levy flight extrema
Francis Bernardeau, Christophe Pichon

Abstract
Rayleigh-Levy flights have played a significant role in cosmology as simplified models for understanding how matter distributes itself under gravitational influence. These models also exhibit numerous remarkable properties that enable the prediction of a wide range of characteristics. Here, we derive the one and two point statistics of extreme points within Rayleigh-Levy flights spanning one to three dimensions, stemming directly from fundamental principles. In the context of the mean field limit, we provide straightforward closed-form expressions for Euler counts and their correlations, particularly in relation to their clustering behaviour over long distances. Additionally, quadratures allow for the computation of extreme value number densities. A comparison between theoretical predictions in 1D and Monte Carlo measurements shows remarkable agreement. Given the widespread use of Rayleigh-Levy processes, these comprehensive findings offer significant promise not only in astrophysics but also in broader applications beyond the field.

Introduction

The geometry and structure of cosmic fields form a complex physical system that develops from homogeneity through the interplay of expansion and long-range forces. Its statistical properties should emulate those of such a class of systems. From the perspective of observational cosmology, its evolution mirrors both the history of the universe’s expansion rate and the dynamic growth of embedded substructures

A key property of Rayleigh-Levy flights involves the structure of the higher order correlation functions. In such a model, not only are they known but they also display the type of behaviour one expects in the context of cosmological models. We know from theory in the perturbative regime
(
Bernardeau et al. 2002
)
, and to a large extend from observations
(
Yu & Hou 2022

that the
p

This assumption and its consequences are addressed in more details in the Appendices
A

Conversely, from a theoretical perspective, the Rayleigh-Levy flights model falls within the class of models whose

-point correlation
functions follow the so-called hierarchical Ansatz
(
Fry 1984
)
, so that the
they can be expressed as a sum of products of two-points correlations

\xi_{p}({\bm{r}}_{1},\dots,{\bm{r}}_{p})=\sum_{t\in{\rm trees}}Q_{p}(t)\,\prod_{{\rm lines}\in t}\xi({\bm{r}}_{i},{\bm{r}}_{j})\,,

(6)

This process actually embodies a more specific class of models, which correspond to cases where the

Q_{p}(t)=\prod_{{\rm vertices}\in t}\nu_{q}\,,

(7)

where the vertices values
\nu_{q}
are numbers associated with each vertex and depend only on their connectivity,

(

B
and)
.
In the case of the flight models we consider here, we have only two non-zero vertices,
\nu_{1}
and
\nu_{2}

In general, the corresponding cumulant generating function of the underlying density field in cells of arbitrary profile given by
\mathcal{C}_{i}({\bm{x}})

\varphi(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{n})={\hskip-5.69046pt}\sum_{p_{1},\dots,p_{n}}\langle\rho_{1}^{p_{1}}\dots\rho_{n}^{p_{n}}\rangle_{c}\,\frac{\lambda_{1}^{p_{1}}}{p_{1}!}\,.

(8)

Note that in the Gaussian limit this expression is restricted to terms with

A remarkable property of the tree models in general, and of the Levy flight model in particular, is that the discrete summations that appear in this expression (over

as it allows for an exploration of their properties in a regime that can be arbitrarily far from the Gaussian case. This is the motivation for this study.

Appendix

In short, the calculation is based on writing the mean field solution for a finite number of cells assumed to be infinitely close to one another; the derivatives are then obtained via finite differences.

5
Discussion and conclusions

Rayleigh-Levy flights can serve as numerical models portraying matter distribution within highly nonlinear fields in cosmology. In contrast to standard Markovian processes, Rayleigh-Levy flights display long-range correlations across all orders, which are precisely understood. Thus, these models serve as simplified representations, capturing statistical properties of the cosmic density field after gravitational instabilities reach its full development, although we are well aware they do not precisely replicate the outcomes of these instabilities. Despite this, they exhibit key properties, such as hierarchical structure in the higher correlation functions, making their study a valuable reference point.

Let us emphasise that Rayleigh-Levy flights differ from simple nonlinear transformations
(like a lognormal fields introduced by
Coles & Jones 1991
, which are now widely used as toy models)
, as they exhibit hierarchical properties across scales. This makes them the only known example (at least to us) of an actual random process whose outcome displays the expected scaling properties in correlation functions. Although it exhibits such a non-trivial structure, it is yet simple enough to allow for a wide range of explicit predictions.

Utilizing the mean field approximation specifically enables the derivation of closed analytical forms for crucial parameters, such as Euler number densities and their correlations across significant separations: equations (
20
) and (
32
), along with corresponding quadratures for extreme value counts, represent the primary outcomes of this study.
Importantly, our predictions hold irrespective of the density field’s variance. While aligning with expectations for a Gaussian field in the regime of small variances, our numerical experiments confirm their validity across all variance values.

Several key insights emerge from our findings:

1.

Critical point density depends
7
7

7

They also depend on the specificities of the model through
structural quantities encoded for instance in the scale cumulant generating function in the context of the large-deviation principle,
(
Bernardeau & Reimberg 2016
, see e.g.)
.
on three quantities related to density variance, gradient variance, and variance of the second-order derivative, denoted as
\xi_{0}
,
\xi_{1}
, and
\xi_{2}
, respectively.
These can be re-expressed using two dimensionless parameters,
\xi_{0}
and
\gamma=\xi_{1}/\sqrt{\xi_{0}\xi_{2}}
, along with
\xi_{1}
, inversely proportional to distance squared. This implies that number densities scale as
\xi_{1}^{N/2}
in N dimensions. Remarkably, while extrema number counts correlate with both
\xi_{0}
and
\gamma
, Euler number counts remain independent of
\gamma
. This hints at the impact of small-scale features on peak counts, creating pairs of peaks and troughs at roughly the same height, ultimately leaving Euler number densities unaffected. We speculate that this property may extend to all hierarchical models.

2.

Concerning point correlations, akin to matter fields, we find that critical points of all types exhibit a factorized structure in the large separation limit, as given in (
31
). This pattern resembles the separate universe approach and has been validated through our numerical experiments. It enables the derivation of bias parameters for various types of points, as given in particular in (
32
).

3.

Notably, bias parameters of maxima, Euler points, and thresholded density fields converge to the same limit in the high-density limit. This suggests that regions with rare high-density values likely host one extremum, sharing identical correlation properties in this limit. This supports simplified approaches where peak correlations mirror those of high-density regions in more complex systems
(
Bernardeau & Schaeffer 1999
, see for instance)
.

Beyond the paper’s scope, extending equations (

Given their common occurrence, the comprehensive findings from this study hold promise not just in astrophysics but also in broader applications beyond the field.

Appendix B
The hierarchical tree models

The Rayleigh-Levy flight model is one representative of a large class of models, the so-called hierarchical tree models, that have been put forward in cosmology as a way to model the density field in the highly nonlinear regime. Such models has been presented in details in
Bernardeau & Schaeffer 1999
. We recall here how they are defined, together with the basic equation that allows the derivation of their cumulant-generating function. Hierarchical tree models are a general class of non-Gaussian fields whose n-point correlation functions,
\xi_{p}({\bm{r}}_{1},\dots,{\bm{r}}_{p})
, follow the so-called hierarchical Ansatz

\xi_{p}({\bm{r}}_{1},\dots,{\bm{r}}_{p})=\sum_{t\in{\rm trees}}Q_{p}(t)\,\prod_{{\rm lines}\in t}\xi({\bm{r}}_{i},{\bm{r}}_{j}),

(43)

where
\xi({\bm{r}}_{i},{\bm{r}}_{j})
is the two-point function, while the sum is made over all possible trees that join the
p
points (diagram without loops), and the tree value,
Q_{p}
, is obtained by the product of a fixed weight (that depends only on the tree’s topology), and the product of the two-point correlation functions, for all pairs that are connected together in the given tree. More specifically it is assumed that

Q(t)=\prod_{{\rm vertices}\in t}\nu_{p},

(44)

where
\nu_{p}
is a weight attributed to all vertices with
p
incoming lines (assuming
\nu_{0}=\nu_{1}=1
for completion).
In this formalism, the vertex generating function is generally introduced as

\zeta(\tau)=\sum_{p}\frac{\nu_{p}}{p!}\tau^{p}.

(45)

Such models are thus entirely defined by i) the two-point functions
\xi(r)
and ii) the vertex-generating function

\zeta(\tau)
. What the previous section has shown is that the Rayleigh-Levy flight model (in the continuous limit)
correspond to a specific hierarchical tree model, with
\nu_{2}=1/2
,
\nu_{q}=0
for
q\geq 3
.

\displaystyle\times\xi_{p}({\bm{x}}_{1,1},\dots,{\bm{x}}_{1,p_{1}},\dots{\bm{x}}_{n,1},\dots,{\bm{x}}_{n,p_{n}}).

(46)

We then wish to build the cumulant-generating function (CGF) of the densities in each cells. It is given by

\displaystyle\varphi(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{n})

\displaystyle=\log\langle e^{\sum\lambda_{i}\rho_{i}}\rangle\,,

(47)

\displaystyle=\sum_{p_{1},\dots,p_{n}}\langle\rho_{1}^{p_{1}}\dots\rho_{n}^{p_{n}}\rangle_{c}\,\frac{\lambda_{1}^{p_{1}}}{p_{1}!}\dots\frac{\lambda_{n}^{p_{n}}}{p_{n}!}.

(48)

This function represents the generating function of (averaged) cumulants, where the power of the
\lambda_{i}
counts the number of points in each cells.
When the cumulants follow the tree structure described in the previous paragraph, each term that appear in this function then corresponds to a specific tree. Following a method pioneered by
Janninck & des Cloizeau 1987
,
Bernardeau & Schaeffer 1992
showed that it is actually possible to perform the summation over such a set of trees, leading to a formal expression we re-derive briefly below.

To do so, let us first now define a larger class of objects,
\phi[\nu_{p}({\bm{x}}),\xi({\bm{x}},{\bm{x}}^{\prime})]
, (note the different symbol) which represents the sum of trees with vertices of order
p
having the weight
\nu_{p}({\bm{x}})
and lines having the weight
\xi({\bm{x}},{\bm{x}}^{\prime})
, and where
{\bm{x}}
are space variables that are subsequently integrated in the whole domain. In the context of tree theory, a point that reaches a one-point vertex
\nu_{1}({\bm{x}})
are usually called a ”leaf”. The function we are interested in,
\varphi(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{n})
, is precisely equal to
\phi[\nu_{p}({\bm{x}}),\xi({\bm{x}},{\bm{x}}^{\prime})]
for the specific choice

\nu_{p}({\bm{x}})=\nu_{p}\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}\mathcal{C}_{i}({\bm{x}})\,.

(49)

Let us now consider
\phi[\nu_{p}({\bm{x}}),\xi({\bm{x}},{\bm{x}}^{\prime})]
as a functional of
\nu_{1}(x)
only, the other quantities it depends on being fixed.
We can then define
\tau({\bm{x}})
as the functional derivative of
\phi
with respect to
\nu_{1}({\bm{x}})

\tau({\bm{x}})=\frac{\delta\phi}{\delta\nu_{1}({\bm{x}})}\,.

(50)

Then
\tau({\bm{x}})
appears to be the generating function of all trees with (at least) one leaf at position
x
. This is a sub-part of rooted-trees
8
8

8

In graph theory rooted trees are trees that emerge from a vertex that have been singled out, not necessarily a leaf.

The key relevant property is then that

\displaystyle\hskip-7.11317pt\tau({\bm{x}})\!=

\displaystyle\!\!\int\!\!{\rm d}{\bm{x}}^{\prime}\xi({\bm{x}},{\bm{x}}^{\prime})\nu_{1}({\bm{x}}^{\prime})\!+\!\!\!\int\!\!\!{\rm d}{\bm{x}}^{\prime}\xi({\bm{x}},{\bm{x}}^{\prime})\!\!\sum_{p=2}^{\infty}\!\!\frac{\nu_{p}({\bm{x}}^{\prime})}{(p\!-\!1)!}\tau^{p-1}({\bm{x}}^{\prime}).\!\!\!

(52)

\frac{\delta\psi[\tau({\bm{x}})]}{\delta\tau({\bm{x}})}=\nu_{1}({\bm{x}})\,.

(53)

As
\xi({\bm{x}},{\bm{x}}^{\prime})
is a definite positive operator, the relation (

\nu_{1}({\bm{x}})=\int{\rm d}{\bm{x}}^{\prime}\xi^{-1}({\bm{x}},{\bm{x}}^{\prime})\tau_{1}({\bm{x}}^{\prime})\!-\!\sum_{p=2}^{\infty}\frac{\nu_{p}({\bm{x}})}{(p-1)!}\tau^{p-1}({\bm{x}})\,,

(54)

and formally integrated via equation (

\psi=\frac{1}{2}\int{\rm d}{\bm{x}}\,{\rm d}{\bm{x}}^{\prime}\ \tau({\bm{x}})\xi^{-1}({\bm{x}},{\bm{x}}^{\prime})\tau({\bm{x}}^{\prime})-\int{\rm d}{\bm{x}}\sum_{p=2}^{\infty}\frac{\nu_{p}({\bm{x}})}{p!}\tau^{p}({\bm{x}})\,,

with the correct boundary conditions (it should vanish when
\nu_{1}({\bm{x}})
does). From equation (

\phi

\hskip-2.84544pt\phi\!=\!\int\!\!{\rm d}{\bm{x}}\sum_{p=1}^{\infty}\!\!\frac{\nu_{p}({\bm{x}})}{p!}\tau({\bm{x}})^{p}\!-\!\frac{1}{2}\!\int\!\!{\rm d}{\bm{x}}{\rm d}{\bm{x}}^{\prime}\tau({\bm{x}})\xi^{-1}({\bm{x}},{\bm{x}}^{\prime})\tau({\bm{x}}^{\prime}),

(55)

which solves the formal calculation.
We can then apply this result for our specific setting (using equation

to derive the CGF we are interested in.
It is then convenient to re-express

in equation (

\displaystyle\varphi(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{n})

\displaystyle=\sum_{j}\lambda_{j}\int{{\rm d}{\bm{x}}}\,\mathcal{C}_{j}({\bm{x}})\zeta(\tau({\bm{x}}))

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j}\lambda_{j}\int{{\rm d}{\bm{x}}}\,\mathcal{C}_{j}({\bm{x}})\tau({\bm{x}})\zeta^{\prime}(\tau({\bm{x}}))\,,

(56)

and the equation for the r-CGF function reads

Appendix C
The minimal tree model

The minimal tree model (MTM) is defined as a tree model for which only vertices with 2 outcoming lines exist.
It is therefore associated with a vertex generating function of the form

\zeta(\tau)=1+\tau+\frac{\nu_{2}}{2}\tau^{2}\,.

(61)

It has been shown
(in
Balian & Schaeffer 1989
, from the behaviour of the void probability function)
that the only possible
value for
\nu_{2}
is
\nu_{2}=1/2
. This is precisely the case of the Rayleigh Levy flight model.
From equation (
61
), this model is thus characterized by

\zeta_{{\rm MTM}}(\tau)=\left(1+\frac{\tau}{2}\right)^{2}.

(62)

In this case the stationary equations for a set of cells, equation (
58
) reads

\tau_{i}=\sum_{j}\lambda_{j}\xi_{ij}(1+\tau_{j}/2)\,,

(63)

and equation (
60
) becomes

\varphi_{n}(\{\lambda_{i}\})=\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}\left(1+\frac{\tau_{i}}{2}\right).

(64)

As the right hand side of equation (
63
) is linear in
\tau
, this system can be solved by the simple inversion
of a matrix. In practice it is therefore relatively easy to derive the expression of
\tau_{i}

(and therefore of the cumulant generating function) for a finite number of cells.

C.1
The mean field solution

From equations (
63
)-(
64
) when only one cell is considered,
the one point mean field solution turns out to be

\varphi_{1}(\lambda_{\rho};\xi_{0})=\frac{2\lambda_{\rho}}{2-\xi_{0}\lambda_{\rho}}\,.

(65)

It gives the expression of the CGF for a single variable
\rho
of mean unity and mean square
\xi_{0}
.
The successive reduced cumulants of such a quantity can be easily computed by Taylor expansion.

Note that
if the random variable
\rho
is rescaled to have a mean of
\overline{\rho}
(instead of one), its CGF is

\varphi_{1}(\overline{\rho}\lambda;\xi_{0})
, which, given equation (

\overline{\rho}\varphi_{1}(\lambda;\overline{\rho}\xi_{0})

C.2
MTM composition law and scale convolution

Let us define generically the cumulant generating function
\varphi_{n}(\{\lambda_{i}\};\{\xi_{ij}\})
of a set of
n
cells, of variables
\lambda_{i}
for cells whose 2-point correlations are
\xi_{ij}
. Then the following identity is satisfied by
\varphi_{n}

\varphi_{n}(\{\lambda_{i}\};\,\{\xi_{ij}+\xi\})=\varphi_{1}(\varphi_{n}(\{\lambda_{i}\};\{\xi_{ij}\});\,\xi).

(66)

This important scaling, that we will use repetitively throughout, may seem at first view as an awkward relation, as it states that the
\xi
in
\varphi_{n}
can be shifted by successive application of the function
\varphi_{1}
. This result seems specific to Rayleigh-Levy flights .

Let us first briefly demonstrate the property. To avoid confusion in the notation let us define
\tau_{i}

and
\hat{\tau}_{i}
as the solutions of the following systems

\displaystyle\tau_{i}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j}\lambda_{j}\xi_{ij}(1+\tau_{j}/2)\,,

(67a)

\displaystyle\hat{\tau}_{i}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j}\lambda_{j}(\xi_{ij}+\xi)(1+\hat{\tau}_{j}/2)\,.

(67b)

Then let us also define
\mu_{i}
and
\hat{\mu}_{i}
as

\mu_{i}=1+{\tau_{i}}/{2}
and
\hat{\mu}_{i}=1+{\hat{\tau}_{i}}/{2}

so that

\displaystyle\varphi_{n}

\displaystyle\equiv\varphi_{n}(\{\lambda_{i}\};\{\xi_{ij}\})=\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}\mu_{i}\,,

(68a)

\displaystyle\hat{\varphi}_{n}

\displaystyle\equiv\varphi_{n}(\{\lambda_{i}\};\{\xi_{ij}+\xi\})=\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}\hat{\mu}_{i}.

(68b)

Then
\hat{\mu}_{j}
is the only solution of the system

\sum_{j}M_{ij}\hat{\mu}_{j}=1\,,

(69)

where the matrix
M_{ij}
is defined as

M_{ij}=\left(\delta_{ij}-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j}\lambda_{j}(\xi_{ij}+\xi)\right).

(70)

Now, since

\sum_{j}M_{ij}\mu_{j}=1-\frac{\xi}{2}\sum_{j}\lambda_{j}\mu_{j}\,,

(71)

this implies that
{\mu_{i}}/\left({1-\frac{\xi}{2}\sum_{j}\lambda_{j}\mu_{j}}\right)

is the solution of equation (
69
), so that
by identification we have

\hat{\mu}_{i}=\frac{\mu_{i}}{1-\frac{\xi}{2}\sum_{j}\lambda_{j}\mu_{j}}.

(72)

Hence,

\hat{\varphi}_{n}=\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}\frac{\mu_{i}}{1-\frac{\displaystyle\xi}{2}\sum_{j}\lambda_{j}\mu_{j}}=\frac{\varphi_{n}}{1-\frac{\xi}{2}\varphi_{n}}=\varphi_{1}(\varphi_{n};\xi)\,,

(73)

which establishes the relation.

Equation (
66
) reflects some interesting physical properties it is related to: scale composition when cell correlations are built as a two-steps procedure. Indeed, let us consider a set of random Rayleigh-Levy flights experiments, in which instead of having of fixed number of points in each sample, the sample density is itself drawn from the one-point PDF derived from an MTM process. We denote
\rho_{s}
the sample density (whose average is set to unity). Its CGF is then
\varphi_{1}(\lambda_{s};\xi_{s})
, where
\xi_{s}
is the variance of the sample density.

We then note that for each realization the cell densities,
\rho_{i}
scale like
1/\rho_{s}
(by definition
\rho_{i}
is defined with respect to the mean of the survey) and that its correlation functions
\xi_{ij}
scale like
1/\rho_{s}
(this is a consequence of the expression of the scaling of the two-point function in the Rayleigh Levy model).
To be more precise let us define
\overline{\xi}_{ij}
as the cell correlations when the sample density is equal to unity. We then have

\xi_{ij}=\overline{\xi}_{ij}/\rho_{s}.

(74)

Let us then define the densities
\hat{\rho}_{i}
which represent the “true” density in the sample (that is when the sample density is taken into account) as

\hat{\rho}_{i}=\rho_{s}\,\rho_{i},

(75)

and aim at building the joint PDF of
\hat{\rho}_{i}
. This PDF is formally given by

\displaystyle P

\displaystyle(\{\hat{\rho}_{i}\})=\int\frac{{\rm d}\rho_{s}}{\rho_{s}^{n}}P(\rho_{s})P(\{\rho_{i}/\rho_{s}\})\,,

(76)

\displaystyle=\int\frac{{\rm d}\rho_{s}}{\rho_{s}^{n}}P(\rho_{s})\!\!\int\!\!\Pi_{i}\frac{{\rm d}\lambda_{i}}{2\pi{\rm i}}\exp\left(-\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}\hat{\rho}_{i}/\rho_{s}+\varphi_{n}(\{\lambda_{i}\};\{\xi_{ij}\})\right)\,,

where the dependence in
\rho_{s}
is also present in the expression of
\xi_{ij}
.
Then, making the change of variable

\hat{\lambda}_{i}=\lambda_{i}/\rho_{s}\,,

(77)

and noting that

\varphi_{n}(\{\lambda_{i}\};\{\xi_{ij}\})=\rho_{s}\varphi_{n}(\{\hat{\lambda}_{i}\};\{\overline{\xi}_{ij}\})\,,

(78)

we have

\displaystyle P(\{\hat{\rho}_{i}\})

\displaystyle=\int{{\rm d}\rho_{s}}\int\frac{{\rm d}\mu}{2\pi{\rm i}}\int\Pi_{i}\frac{{\rm d}\lambda_{i}}{2\pi{\rm i}}

\displaystyle\hskip-28.45274pt\times\exp\left(-\sum_{i}\hat{\lambda}_{i}\hat{\rho}_{i}-\mu\rho_{s}+\rho_{s}\varphi_{n}(\{\hat{\lambda}_{i}\};\{\overline{\xi}_{ij}\})+\varphi_{1}(\mu,\xi_{s})\right).

(79)

Now the integral over
\rho_{s}
leads to a Dirac delta function in
\mu-\varphi_{n}(\{\hat{\lambda}_{i}\};\{\overline{\xi}_{ij}\})
leading to

\displaystyle P(\{\hat{\rho}_{i}\})\!=\!\!\int\!\!\Pi_{i}\frac{{\rm d}\lambda_{i}}{2\pi{\rm i}}\exp\left(\!-\!\!\sum_{i}\!\hat{\lambda}_{i}\hat{\rho}_{i}\!+\!\varphi_{1}(\varphi_{n}(\{\hat{\lambda}_{i}\};\{\overline{\xi}_{ij}\}),\xi_{s})\!\right)\!.

(80)

This expression means formally that the CGF of
\hat{\rho}_{i}
is this two-step construction given by
\varphi_{1}(\varphi_{n}(\{\hat{\lambda}_{i}\};\{\overline{\xi}_{ij}\}),\xi_{s})
. The relation (
66
) ensures that it is also given by
\varphi_{n}(\{\hat{\lambda}_{i}\};\{\overline{\xi}_{ij}+\xi_{s}\})
, which states that survey density fluctuations can, in this model, be taken into account via a simple shift in the cell correlation amplitudes.

A useful practical consequences of property (
66
) is that one could set any peculiar element of
\xi_{ij}

to zero. For instance the CGF of 2 identical cells of density variance
\overline{\xi}
and of cross correlation
\xi_{12}
is given by

\varphi_{2}(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2};\overline{\xi},\xi_{12})=\varphi_{1}(\varphi_{1}(\lambda_{1};\overline{\xi}-\xi_{12})+\varphi_{1}(\lambda_{2};\overline{\xi}-\xi_{12});\xi_{12})\,.

It is possible to extend such a construction for a larger number of cells
9
9

9

up to 3 cells at 1D, 5 cells at 2D and 7 cells at 3D.
, but for specific configurations only. Note finally that when one needs to construct the density CGF for a large number of cells it is convenient to set
\overline{\xi}=0
for all cells as it makes the system in
\tau_{i}
sparser.

C.3
Computing PDF in the minimal tree model

The basic quantity one wishes to have access to is the PDF, defined as the inverse Laplace transform of the cumulant generating function
\varphi_{1}(\lambda_{\rho})
, that is

P(\rho)=\int_{-{\rm i}\infty}^{+{\rm i}\infty}\frac{{\rm d}\lambda_{\rho}}{2\pi{\rm i}}\exp\left(-\lambda_{\rho}\,\rho+\varphi_{1}(\lambda_{\rho})\right)\,,

(81)

where the integral runs formally along the imaginary axis, but can be moved in the complex plane as long as no poles or singularities are encountered along the path.

The following relation will be exploited throughout this paper

\hskip-2.84544pt\!\int\!\frac{{\rm d}\lambda_{\rho}}{2\pi{\rm i}}\exp\left(\!{-\lambda_{\rho}\,\rho\!+\!\frac{a_{c}}{\lambda_{c}\!-\!\lambda_{\rho}}}\!\right)\!=\!\delta_{\textsc{d}}(\rho)\!+\!a_{c}e^{-\lambda_{c}\rho}\,_{0}{\tilde{F}}_{1}\!\left(2;a_{c}\rho\right),

(82)

where
\delta_{\textsc{d}}
is the one-dimensional Dirac distribution and
{}_{0}{\tilde{F}}_{1}(a;z)
is the regularized confluent hypergeometric function

\,{}_{0}F_{1}(a;z)/\Gamma(a)
. This formula can be applied directly to
\varphi_{1}(\lambda_{\rho};\xi_{0})
to derive
P(\rho)
. It can also be applied after successive derivatives with respect to
a_{c}
from which on can establish that

\displaystyle\int\frac{{\rm d}\lambda_{\rho}}{2\pi{\rm i}}\left(\frac{1}{\lambda_{c}-\lambda_{\rho}}\right)^{n}

\displaystyle\exp\left(-\lambda_{\rho}\,\rho+\frac{a_{c}}{\lambda_{c}-\lambda_{\rho}}\right)=

\displaystyle e^{-\lambda_{c}\rho}\left(\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}\,a_{c}}\right)^{n}\left\{a_{c}\,{}_{0}{\tilde{F}}_{1}\left(2;a_{c}\rho\right)\right\}\,,

(83)

for
n\geq 1
.
Then, the application of equation (
82
) to the expression (
65
) leads to

P(\rho)=e^{-2/\xi_{0}}\delta_{\textsc{d}}(\rho)+\frac{4}{\xi_{0}^{2}}e^{-\frac{2(\rho+1)}{\xi_{0}}}\,_{0}{\tilde{F}}_{1}\left(2;\frac{4\rho}{\xi_{0}^{2}}\right)\,,

(84)

which yields the PDF in the mean field approximation. One can see that it involves the sum of two terms, a Dirac term term for
\rho=0
, and a continuous contribution. This highlights one of the key feature of this model, which is that the probability of having empty regions of finite size remains finite even in the continuous limit.

C.4
Beyond the mean field approximation

Let us explore the possibility of solving the consistency relations beyond the mean field approximation, which provides means to explore the validity of this approximation. The calculations will be limited to the 1D case and to a Gaussian filter and its derivatives. We therefore assume that

\mathcal{C}_{0}(x)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\exp\left(-\frac{x^{2}}{2}\right).

(85)

The idea is then to expand
\tau(x)
in e.g. equation (
10
) on its natural ortho-normal basis, namely the basis of the Hermite Polynomials.
More precisely,
\tau(x)\mathcal{C}_{0}(x)
being bounded at large distance, it is possible to expand it as

\tau(x)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\tau_{n}\,{h_{n}(x)},\,\,\mathrm{with}\,\,h_{n}(x)=\frac{1}{2^{n/2}\sqrt{n!}}H_{n}\left(\frac{x}{\sqrt{2}}\right),

(86)

noting that

\int{\rm d}x\,{h_{n}(x)}\,{h_{n^{\prime}}(x)}\,\mathcal{C}_{0}(x)=\delta_{nn^{\prime}}.

(87)

We are specifically interested in the joint CGF,
\varphi(\{\lambda_{0},\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}\})
involving fields dual to the density, the first and second derivatives. The window functions for the latter can be expressed in terms of the Hermite polynomials as

\mathcal{C}_{q}(x)=\left(\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}x}\right)^{q}\mathcal{C}_{0}(x)=\sqrt{q!}\,h_{q}(x)\mathcal{C}_{0}(x)\,,

(88)

for

and

\int{\rm d}x\,\mathcal{C}_{q}(x){h_{n}(x)}=\sqrt{q!}\delta_{qn}.

(89)

As a result, the consistency relation (
10

now reads

\displaystyle\tau(x)\!=\!\int\!{\rm d}x^{\prime}\xi(x,x^{\prime})\left(1+\frac{\tau(x^{\prime})}{2}\right)\mathcal{C}_{0}(x^{\prime})\sum_{q=0}^{2}\lambda_{q}\sqrt{q!}\,h_{q}(x^{\prime}),

(90)

which can then be transformed into a system in
\tau_{p}
after integration with weight

\displaystyle\tau_{p}=\sum_{q=0}^{2}\lambda_{q}\left[\Xi_{p0}^{(q)}+\sum_{p^{\prime}=0}^{\infty}\frac{\tau_{p^{\prime}}}{2}\Xi_{pp^{\prime}}^{(q)}\right],

(91)

with

\Xi_{pp^{\prime}}^{(q)}=\int{\rm d}x\ {\rm d}x^{\prime}\ \mathcal{C}_{0}(x){h_{p}(x)}\xi(x,x^{\prime}){h_{p^{\prime}}(x^{\prime})}\mathcal{C}_{q}(x^{\prime})\,.

(92)

The expression of CGF for the density and its gradients derives from equation (

\varphi(\{\lambda_{q}\})=\lambda_{0}+\sum_{q=0}^{2}\lambda_{q}\frac{\tau_{q}}{2}\sqrt{q!}\,,

(93)

\mathcal{C}_{q}(x)
and

Note that those quantities depend on the actual shape and amplitude of the correlation function. In the following, we simply assume that

corresponding to
\alpha=1/2
for a 1D Rayleigh-Levy flight. It implies that
\Xi_{pp^{\prime}}^{(q)}/\xi_{0}
are all fixed quantities and that the result can therefore be expressed in term of
\xi_{0}
only.

We present hereafter the result of such derivations for the density,
when the Hermite expansion in
\tau
is truncated at increasing order, from 0 (where the r-CGF is assumed to be constant) to 4
(in practice we have been able to derive the CGF up to 10th order as illustrated in the figures below). We thus have

\displaystyle\hskip-5.69046pt\varphi_{0}(\lambda)

\displaystyle\!=\frac{2\lambda}{2-\lambda\xi_{0}}\,,

(94a)

\displaystyle\varphi_{2}(\lambda)

\displaystyle\!=-\frac{\lambda\left(5\lambda\xi_{0}-64\right)}{2\lambda^{2}\xi_{0}^{2}-37\lambda\xi_{0}+64}\,,

(94b)

\displaystyle\varphi_{4}(\lambda)

The computation of extrema densities is relies on the knowledge of the joint PDF of the local density, its first and second order

D.2
Spatial homogeneity & isotropy

In the cosmological context, density fields are statistically homogeneous and isotropic. For the hierarchical tree models, it is ensured by the fact that the two-point correlation function
\xi_{2}({\bm{x}},{\bm{x}}^{\prime})
depends on

|{\bm{x}}-{\bm{x}}^{\prime}|
only.

A number of properties follows. The first immediate one, for the 1D case, is that the expectation value of any gradient should vanish. That implies in particular that the expectation value of
\rho_{,x}^{2}+\rho\rho_{,xx}
vanishes, which implies equation (
96

which in terms, after summing over

(100)

An alternative formulation at the level of the PDF reads

\hskip-8.5359pt\int\!\!{\rm d}\rho_{\!,xx}\left\{\rho_{\!,x}\frac{\partial P(\rho,\rho_{\!,x},\rho_{\!,xx})}{\partial\rho}+\rho_{,xx}\frac{\partial P(\rho,\rho_{\!,x},\rho_{\!,xx})}{\partial\rho_{\!,x}}\right\}=0\,.

(101)

\rho_{,x}

\rho_{,x}=0

n_{\rm 1D}^{\rm Euler}(\rho)=\frac{\partial}{\partial\rho}\int_{-\infty}^{0}\rho_{,x}{\rm d}\rho_{,x}P(\rho,\rho_{,x})\,.

(102)

It shows that the Euler number density does not depend actually on the way
\rho_{,xx}
is correlated to the density and its gradient. This is not so for the extrema counts. This points to an interesting feature of Euler number density: it is significantly more insensitive to small scale fluctuations compared to other observables.

Unfortunately this simplification does not directly extend to heigher dimensions.

D.3

The 3D Euler and extrema counts can be similarly derived, although the calculation is somewhat more sophisticated. To sort out the results let us take full advantage of the rotation invariance of the result. Let us be a bit more precise.
The quantities,
\rho

\rho_{,i}

\rho_{,ij}

\mathcal{R}_{i}^{j}

\hat{\lambda}_{i}
and
\hat{\lambda}_{ij}

\displaystyle\hat{\rho}_{,i}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{R}_{i}^{i^{\prime}}\rho_{,i^{\prime}}\,,\quad\hat{\rho}_{,ij}=\mathcal{R}_{i}^{i^{\prime}}\mathcal{R}_{j}^{j^{\prime}}\rho_{,i^{\prime}j^{\prime}}\,.

\displaystyle\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}\rho_{,i}=\sum_{i}\hat{\lambda}_{i}\hat{\rho}_{,i}\,,\quad\sum_{i\geq j}\lambda_{ij}\rho_{,ij}=\sum_{i\geq j}\hat{\lambda}_{ij}\hat{\rho}_{,ij}.

(146)

\hat{\rho}_{,i}

\lambda_{i}=\mathcal{R}_{i}^{i^{\prime}}\hat{\lambda}_{,i^{\prime}}\,,

(147)

\lambda_{i}
is a rank-1 co-tensor. This is not the case for
\lambda_{ij}
as the sum in equation (
146

\lambda_{xy}/2&\lambda_{yy}&\lambda_{yz}/2\\

\sum_{i\geq j}\lambda_{ij}\rho_{,ij}=\sum_{ij}{\lambda^{R}}_{ij}\rho_{,ij}.

(149)

{\lambda^{R}}_{ij}
transforms as a rank-2 co-tensor. From the definition of equation (
47
),
the CGF itself is also clearly invariant under rotations. We then expect it to depend
on combinations of
\lambda_{i}
and
{\lambda^{R}}_{ij}
that are scalar invariant.
