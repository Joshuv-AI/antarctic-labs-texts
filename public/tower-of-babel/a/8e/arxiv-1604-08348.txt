Abstract
. We introduce two new operations (compositional products and implication) on Weihrauch degrees, and investigate the overall algebraic structure. The validity of the various distributivity laws is studied and forms the basis for a comparison with similar structures such as residuated lattices and concurrent Kleene algebras. Introducing the notion of an ideal with respect to the compositional product, we can consider suitable quotients of the Weihrauch degrees. We also prove some specific characterizations using the implication. In order to introduce and study compositional products and implications, we introduce and study a function space of multi-valued continuous functions. This space turns out to be particularly well-behaved for effectively traceable spaces that are closely related to admissibly represented spaces.

2.
Background on Weihrauch degrees

Weihrauch reducibility compares multi-valued functions on represented
spaces. A
represented space
is a pair
\mathbf{X}=(X,\delta_{X})
where
\delta_{X}:\subseteq{\mathbb{N}^{\mathbb{N}}}\to X
is a partial surjection, called
representation
.
In general we use the symbol “
\subseteq
” in order to indicate that a function is potentially partial. Represented spaces form the foundation of computable analysis
[
48
]
, and canonically capture many settings of mathematics. See
[
40
]
for an introduction to their theory. Two ubiquitous constructions on represented spaces will be coproducts
and products. We have
(X,\delta_{X})\sqcup(Y,\delta_{Y})=((\{0\}\times X)\cup(\{1\}\times Y),\delta_{X\sqcup Y})
with
\delta_{X\sqcup Y}(0p)=(0,\delta_{X}(p))
and
\delta_{X\sqcup Y}(1p)=(1,\delta_{Y}(p))
. For the product, it is
(X,\delta_{X})\times(Y,\delta_{Y})=(X\times Y,\delta_{X\times Y})
with
\delta_{X\times Y}(\langle p,q\rangle)=(\delta_{X}(p),\delta_{q}(q))
, where
\langle,\rangle
denotes some standard pairing on Baire space (to be used again quite often).

Multi-valued functions (symbol:
f:\subseteq\mathbf{X}\rightrightarrows\mathbf{Y}
) may be formalized as relations via their graph. However, the composition of two (multi-valued) functions (denoted by by
f\circ g
or by
fg
) is not the composition of relations. Instead,
z\in(f\circ g)(x)
if and only if
(\forall y\in g(x))\;\ y\in\operatorname{dom}(f)
and
(\exists y\in g(x))\;\ z\in f(y)
(rather than just the latter condition). As a consequence, the category of multi-valued functions behaves very different from the category of relations, which has been explored in
[
41
]
.

Using represented spaces we can define the concept of a realizer:

Definition
\thethm

(Realizer)
.

Let
f:\subseteq(X,\delta_{X})\rightrightarrows(Y,\delta_{Y})
be a multi-valued function on represented spaces.
A function
F:\subseteq{\mathbb{N}^{\mathbb{N}}}\to{\mathbb{N}^{\mathbb{N}}}
is called a
realizer
of
f
, in symbols
F\vdash f
, if

\delta_{Y}F(p)\in f\delta_{X}(p)
for all
p\in\operatorname{dom}(f\delta_{X})
.

Realizers allow us to transfer the notions of computability
and continuity and other notions available for Baire space to any represented space;
a function between represented spaces will be called
computable
, if it has a computable realizer, etc.
Now we can define Weihrauch reducibility. By
\textnormal{id}:{\mathbb{N}^{\mathbb{N}}}\to{\mathbb{N}^{\mathbb{N}}}
we denote the identity.

The idea of Weihrauch reducibility is to capture the reduction of one multi-valued function
f
to another such function
g

in the sense that one application of
g
can be used in a computable way in order to compute
f
.
We distinguish between ordinary and strong Weihrauch reducibility: in the first case the reduction can fully
exploit the given input, in the latter case the input to
f
can only be used to determine an instance of
g
, but not afterwards.

Definition
\thethm

(Weihrauch reducibility)
.

Let
f,g
be multi-valued functions on represented spaces.
Then
f
is said to be
Weihrauch reducible
to
g
, in symbols
f\leq_{\textrm{W}}g
, if there are computable
functions
K,H:\subseteq{\mathbb{N}^{\mathbb{N}}}\to{\mathbb{N}^{\mathbb{N}}}
such that
K\langle\textnormal{id},GH\rangle\vdash f
for all
G\vdash g
.

Analogously, we say that
f
is
strongly Weihrauch reducible
to
g
, in symbols
f\leq_{\textrm{sW}}g
, if there are computable

K,H:\subseteq{\mathbb{N}^{\mathbb{N}}}\to{\mathbb{N}^{\mathbb{N}}}
such that
KGH\vdash f
for all
G\vdash g
.
The relation
\leq_{\textrm{W}}
is reflexive and transitive. We use
\equiv_{\textrm{W}}
to denote equivalence regarding
\leq_{\textrm{W}}
,
and by
<_{\textrm{W}}
we denote strict reducibility. By
\mathcal{W}
we refer to the partially ordered set of equivalence classes.

Strictly speaking, the class of represented spaces is not a set. However, the Weihrauch degree of the multi-valued function

There are a few important operations defined on multi-valued functions that are compatible with Weihrauch reducibility, and hence induce corresponding operations on
\mathcal{W}

Definition
\thethm
.

Given
f:\subseteq\mathbf{X}\rightrightarrows\mathbf{Y}
and

Besides the three binary operations, there are also two unary ones we are interested in. For these, we first need corresponding constructions on represented spaces. Let

\delta_{1}:{\mathbb{N}^{\mathbb{N}}}\to\{1\}

\mathbf{X}^{n+1}=\mathbf{X}^{n}\times\mathbf{X}

and
\widehat{\mathbf{X}}=\mathbf{X}\times\mathbf{X}\times\ldots
.

Definition
\thethm

Given

, define

and

\widehat{f}(x_{0},x_{1},\ldots)=\mathop{\mathsf{X}}_{i\in{\mathbb{N}}}f(x_{i})

The operations

Sometimes, it is convenient to use the following characterization of Weihrauch reducibility.
For
f,g:\subseteq\mathbf{X}\rightrightarrows\mathbf{Y}
, we say that
f

g
(
g

f
), in symbols
f\sqsubseteq g
, if
\operatorname{dom}(g)\subseteq\operatorname{dom}(f)
and
(\forall x\in\operatorname{dom}(g))\;f(x)\subseteq g(x)
(see also
[

By
\Delta_{\mathbf{X}}:\mathbf{X}\to\mathbf{X}\times\mathbf{X},x\mapsto(x,x)
we denote the canonical
diagonal map
of
\mathbf{X}
and for
f:\subseteq\mathbf{X}\rightrightarrows\mathbf{Y}
and
g:\subseteq\mathbf{X}\rightrightarrows\mathbf{Z}

we denote by
(f,g):=(f\times g)\circ\Delta_{\mathbf{X}}
the

of
f
and

.

Lemma
\thethm
.

Let
f:\subseteq\mathbf{X}\rightrightarrows\mathbf{Y}
and
g:\subseteq\mathbf{U}\rightrightarrows\mathbf{V}
be multi-valued functions on represented spaces.
Then
f\leq_{\textrm{W}}g
, if and only if there is a represented space
\mathbf{W}
and computable
k:\subseteq\mathbf{W}\times\mathbf{V}\rightrightarrows\mathbf{Y}
and
h:\subseteq\mathbf{X}\rightrightarrows\mathbf{W}\times\mathbf{U}

with
k\circ(\textnormal{id}_{\mathbf{W}}\times g)\circ h\sqsubseteq f
.
Without loss of generality, we can choose
\mathbf{W}={\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}
in this statement.

Proof.

Let
f\leq_{\textrm{W}}g
. Then there are computable functions
H,K:\subseteq{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}\to{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}

such that
K\langle\textnormal{id},GH\rangle
is a realizer of
f
whenever
G
is a realizer of
g
.
Now

h:\subseteq\mathbf{X}\rightrightarrows{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}\times\mathbf{U}
with
h:=(\textnormal{id},\delta_{U}H)\circ\delta_{X}^{-1}

and
k:\subseteq{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}\times\mathbf{V}\rightrightarrows\mathbf{Y}
with
k:=\delta_{Y}\circ K\circ\langle\textnormal{id}\times\delta_{V}^{-1}\rangle

are computable and we obtain

k\circ(\textnormal{id}\times g)\circ h=\delta_{Y}\circ K\langle\textnormal{id},\delta_{V}^{-1}g\delta_{U}H\rangle\circ\delta_{X}^{-1}\sqsubseteq f,

where the last mentioned relation holds since for every
p\in\operatorname{dom}(\delta_{V}^{-1}g\delta_{U})
and

q\in\delta_{V}^{-1}g\delta_{U}(p)
there is a realizer
G\vdash g
with
G(p)=q
, and for this realizer we have
K\langle\textnormal{id},GH\rangle\vdash f
.

Let now
k:\subseteq\mathbf{W}\times\mathbf{V}\rightrightarrows\mathbf{Y}
and
h:\subseteq\mathbf{X}\rightrightarrows\mathbf{W}\times\mathbf{U}
be computable with
k\circ(\textnormal{id}_{\mathbf{W}}\times g)\circ h\sqsubseteq f

and let
H,K:\subseteq{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}\to{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}
be computable realizers of
h
and
k
, respectively.
Let
H_{1},H_{2}:\subseteq{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}\to{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}
be such that
H(p)=\langle H_{1}(p),H_{2}(p)\rangle

and let
\pi:{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}\times{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}\to{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}},(p,q)\mapsto\langle p,q\rangle
.
Let
G\vdash g
. We note that this implies
\langle\textnormal{id}\times G\rangle\circ\pi^{-1}\vdash\textnormal{id}_{\mathbf{W}}\times g

and hence
K\circ\langle\textnormal{id}\times G\rangle\circ\pi^{-1}\circ H\vdash k\circ(\textnormal{id}_{\mathbf{W}}\times g)\circ h\sqsubseteq f
.
We obtain

K\circ\langle H_{1}\times\textnormal{id}\rangle\circ\pi^{-1}\circ\langle\textnormal{id},G\circ H_{2}\rangle=K\circ\langle H_{1},G\circ H_{2}\rangle=K\circ\langle\textnormal{id}\times G\rangle\circ\pi^{-1}\circ H\vdash f.

Hence,
K_{0}:=K\circ\langle H_{1}\times\textnormal{id}\rangle\circ\pi^{-1}
and
H_{2}
are computable functions
that satisfy
K_{0}\langle\textnormal{id},GH_{2}\rangle\vdash f
. The choice of
K_{0}
and
H_{2}
is independent of
G

and hence they witness
f\leq_{\textrm{W}}g
.

2.1.
An alternative definition and special degrees

There are three special degrees relevant to the structural properties of
\mathcal{W}
. The first example is the degree
1
with representatives
\textnormal{id}_{1}
and
\textnormal{id}_{\mathbb{N}^{\mathbb{N}}}
(and any computable multi-valued function with a computable point in its domain). The second example is the degree
0
of the nowhere defined multi-valued function. By Definition
2
any partial function on Baire space is a realizer of the nowhere defined multi-valued function, hence we find
0
to be the bottom element of
\mathcal{W}
. The third degree (to be the top degree) is more complicated to introduce, and will require a detour through an alternative definition of Weihrauch reducibility.

Weihrauch reducibility was originally defined on sets of partial functions on Baire space (i.e., sets of potential realizers), cf.
[
46
,
47
]
. Definition
2
then is the special case of the following where the sets involved are indeed sets of realizers of some multi-valued function.

Definition
\thethm
.

Given sets
F
,
G
of partial functions on Baire space, say
F\leq_{\textrm{W}}G
if and only if there are computable
functions
K,H:\subseteq{\mathbb{N}^{\mathbb{N}}}\to{\mathbb{N}^{\mathbb{N}}}
such that
K\langle\textnormal{id},gH\rangle\in F
for all
g\in G
. We write
\mathcal{P}\mathcal{W}
for the resulting degree structure.

There is an operator
\mathcal{R}
taking any set
F
of potential realizers to the set
\mathcal{R}(F)
of realizers of a multi-valued function that has all the functions in
F
as realizers and as little more as possible.
Given some set
F
of partial functions
g:\subseteq{\mathbb{N}^{\mathbb{N}}}\to{\mathbb{N}^{\mathbb{N}}}
, let

\mathcal{R}(F):=\{f:\subseteq{\mathbb{N}^{\mathbb{N}}}\to{\mathbb{N}^{\mathbb{N}}}:(\forall p\in{\mathbb{N}^{\mathbb{N}}})(\exists g\in F)\;f(p)=g(p)\}.

As usual, equality extends to the case of undefined values in this definition.
As explored in further detail in
[
38
]
this operator can be seen as an
interior operator
on
(\mathcal{P}\mathcal{W},\leq_{\textrm{W}})
, which means that
\mathcal{R}(F)\leq_{\textrm{W}}F
,
\mathcal{R}(F)\leq_{\textrm{W}}\mathcal{R}(G)

whenever
F\leq_{\textrm{W}}G
, and
\mathcal{R}(F)\leq_{\textrm{W}}\mathcal{R}(\mathcal{R}(F))
. In fact, we even have
\mathcal{R}(\mathcal{R}(F))=\mathcal{R}(F)
.
All sets of realizers of multi-valued functions are fixed points of
\mathcal{R}

[
38
]
.
Since all the (non-empty) elements in the range of
\mathcal{R}
are sets of realizers of multi-valued functions,
we can also see
\mathcal{R}
as an operator
\mathfrak{R}
from
\mathcal{P}\mathcal{W}
to
\mathcal{W}
.
In this case,

\mathfrak{R}(F)(p):=\{g(p):g\in F\},

where
\operatorname{dom}(\mathfrak{R}(F)):=\{p\in{\mathbb{N}^{\mathbb{N}}}:(\forall g\in F)\;p\in\operatorname{dom}(g)\}
.
Then
\mathcal{R}(F)
is the set of realizers of
\mathfrak{R}(F)
for all non-empty
F
.
Clearly, we also have
\mathcal{R}(\emptyset)=\emptyset
, but
\emptyset
is the set of realizers of some multi-valued
function if and only if the Axiom of Choice does not hold for Baire space.
Hence, the Weihrauch lattice
\mathcal{W}
has a natural top element if an only if the Axiom of Choice does not hold for Baire space.
A multi-valued function with realizers can never be the top element of
\mathcal{W}
.
But it is natural to extend
\mathcal{W}
by attaching an additional top element
\infty
to it and then we will arrive at:

Convention
\thethm
.

We will assume that
\mathfrak{R}:\mathcal{P}\mathcal{W}\to\mathcal{W}
satisfies
\mathfrak{R}(\emptyset)=\infty
.

The operations on
\mathcal{W}
can (almost) be obtained from corresponding operations on
\mathcal{P}\mathcal{W}
via the operator
\mathfrak{R}
. For
\odot
among the operations
\times
,
\sqcup
that map single-valued functions to single-valued functions, we can define them pointwise via
F\odot G:=\{f\odot g:f\in F\wedge g\in G\}

in order to obtain
\mathfrak{R}(F\odot G)=\mathfrak{R}(F)\odot\mathfrak{R}(G)
.
The closure operators
∗
and
\widehat{\phantom{f}}
can be handled similarly.
In case of
\sqcap
, a suitable definition for sets of functions can be given by

F\uplus G:=\{(0,f\circ\pi_{1}):f\in F\}\cup\{(1,g\circ\pi_{2}):g\in G\}.

Here
\pi_{i}
denotes the projection on the
i
–th component.
In this case we obtain
\mathfrak{R}(F\uplus G)=\mathfrak{R}(F)\sqcap\mathfrak{R}(G)
.

The one exception to the equivalence of the operations as defined in this subsection and in Definition
2
is that under the latter and the failure of the Axiom of Choice for Baire space, we would expect
0\times\infty=0
. The operation inherited from
\mathcal{P}\mathcal{W}
however satisfies
0\times\infty=\infty
. The latter yields the nicer algebraic structure. Thus, we adopt the following:

Convention
\thethm
.

0\times\infty=\infty\times 0=\infty
and more generally,
\mathbf{a}\times\infty=\infty\times\mathbf{a}=\infty
for every Weihrauch degree
\mathbf{a}
.

We emphasize that we usually adopt the Axiom of Choice and hence no ambiguities are to be expected.

In order to introduce the two new operations
\star
and
\rightarrow
, we would like to use reduction witnesses as inputs and outputs of multi-valued functions. The problem we face is that the usual exponential (i.e., function space construction) in the category of represented spaces is with respect to continuous functions (cf.
[
40

), whereas the reduction witnesses are partial multi-valued functions.
2
2

2

And even if we would only compare total single-valued functions with Weihrauch reducibility, we would still need to make use of partial multi-valued functions here!
As a substitute we introduce the space of
strongly continuous
multi-valued functions, which contains sufficiently many elements to witness all reductions, and behaves sufficiently like an exponential to make the following constructions work.

3.1.
The space of strongly continuous multi-valued functions

Fix a universal Turing machine UTM, and then let for any

the partial function
\Phi_{p}:\subseteq{\mathbb{N}^{\mathbb{N}}}\to{\mathbb{N}^{\mathbb{N}}}
be defined via
\Phi_{p}(q)=r

\langle p,q\rangle
writes on the output tape infinitely often and thus produces
r
;
q\notin\operatorname{dom}(\Phi_{p})
if and only if the machine writes only finitely many times on the output tape. Partial functions of the form
\Phi_{p}
for computable
p\in{\mathbb{N}^{\mathbb{N}}}
are called
strongly computable
(e.g., in
[
48
]
), in analogy, call all partial functions of the form
\Phi_{p}

strongly continuous
.

The notions of strong computability and strong continuity can be lifted to multi-valued functions between represented spaces.
For every representation
\delta_{X}
of a set

Given two represented spaces

That is,

\mathbf{X}
for
i>n
, and
u_{j}
some computable point in
\mathbf{U}
for
j>m
.
Furthermore, let
K:\subseteq\left(\bigsqcup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\mathbf{X}^{n}\right)\times\left(\bigsqcup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\mathbf{U}^{n}\right)\times\left(\bigsqcup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}(\mathbf{Y}\times\mathbf{V})^{n}\right)\to\left(\bigsqcup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\mathbf{Y}^{n}\right)\times\left(\bigsqcup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\mathbf{V}^{n}\right)
be defined via

\displaystyle K((n,(x_{1},...,x_{n})),(m,(u_{1},...,u_{m})),(k,((y_{1},v_{1}),...,(y_{k},v_{k}))))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle((n,(y_{1},...,y_{n})),(m,(v_{1},...,v_{m})))

where being in the domain of
K
requires
k\geq\max\{n,m\}
. Now
H
and
K
witness the claim.

(2)

Here the missing direction is
\widehat{(\mathbf{a}\sqcup\mathbf{b})}\leq_{\textrm{W}}\widehat{\mathbf{a}}\times\widehat{\mathbf{b}}
, and we consider representatives
f
of
\mathbf{a}
and
g
of
\mathbf{b}
as above.
Let
x^{c}\in\mathbf{X}
and
u^{c}\in\mathbf{U}
be computable points.
Now define
H:(\mathbf{X}\sqcup\mathbf{U})^{\mathbb{N}}\to\mathbf{X}^{\mathbb{N}}\times\mathbf{U}^{\mathbb{N}}
via

H((i_{0},x_{0}),(i_{1},x_{1}),\ldots)=((y_{0},y_{1},\ldots),(z_{0},z_{1},\ldots))
where
y_{l}=x_{l}
and
z_{l}=u^{c}
if and only if
i_{l}=0
; and
y_{l}=x^{c}
and
z_{l}=x_{l}
if and only if
i_{l}=1
.
Next, define
K:(\mathbf{X}\sqcup\mathbf{U})^{\mathbb{N}}\times\mathbf{Y}^{\mathbb{N}}\times\mathbf{V}^{\mathbb{N}}\to(\mathbf{Y}\sqcup\mathbf{V})^{\mathbb{N}}

by requiring
K(((i_{0},x_{0}),(i_{1},x_{1}),\ldots),(y_{0}^{0},y_{1}^{0},\ldots),(y_{0}^{1},y_{1}^{1},\ldots))=((i_{0},y_{0}^{i_{0}}),(i_{1},y_{1}^{i_{1}}),\ldots)
. Now
H
and
K
witness the reduction.
∎

We close this section with considering some special expressions.

Proposition
\thethm

(Special expressions)
.

Let
\mathbf{a}
be pointed. Then:

(1)

\mathbf{a}\rightarrow(\mathbf{a}\sqcup\mathbf{b})=\mathbf{a}\rightarrow(1\sqcup\mathbf{b})

(2)

(\mathbf{c}\sqcup\mathbf{b})\rightarrow(\mathbf{c}\sqcup\mathbf{a})\leq_{\textrm{W}}(\mathbf{b}\rightarrow\mathbf{a})

In general, the reduction “
\leq_{\textrm{W}}
” cannot be replaced by “
=
”.

Proof.

(1)

To show
\mathbf{a}\rightarrow(\mathbf{a}\sqcup\mathbf{b})\leq_{\textrm{W}}\mathbf{a}\rightarrow(1\sqcup\mathbf{b})
, by Corollary
3.3
we may show
\mathbf{a}\sqcup\mathbf{b}\leq_{\textrm{W}}\mathbf{a}\star(\mathbf{a}\rightarrow(1\sqcup\mathbf{b}))
instead. For this, we first show
\mathbf{a}\leq_{\textrm{W}}\mathbf{a}\star(\mathbf{a}\rightarrow(1\sqcup\mathbf{b}))
, which in turn follows from
1\leq_{\textrm{W}}\mathbf{a}\rightarrow(1\sqcup\mathbf{b})
, monotonicity of
\star
and
1
being the neutral element for
\star
. Then we show
\mathbf{b}\leq_{\textrm{W}}\mathbf{a}\star(\mathbf{a}\rightarrow(1\sqcup\mathbf{b}))
by noting that by Corollary
3.3
we have
1\sqcup\mathbf{b}\leq_{\textrm{W}}\mathbf{a}\star(\mathbf{a}\rightarrow(1\sqcup\mathbf{b}))
, and that
\sqcup
is the supremum. Noting again that
\sqcup
is the supremum, this direction is complete.
For the other direction, just use monotonicity of
\rightarrow
in the second component, together with pointedness of
\mathbf{a}
, i.e.,
1\leq_{\textrm{W}}\mathbf{a}
.

(2)

If
\mathbf{a}
is pointed, then also
(\mathbf{b}\to\mathbf{a})
. Hence we obtain with Proposition
4.3

\mathbf{c}\leq_{\textrm{W}}(\mathbf{c}\sqcup\mathbf{b})\sqcup(\mathbf{b}\to\mathbf{a})\leq_{\textrm{W}}(\mathbf{c}\sqcup\mathbf{b})\star(\mathbf{b}\to\mathbf{a}).

On the other hand, Corollary
3.3
implies
\mathbf{a}\leq_{\textrm{W}}\mathbf{b}\star(\mathbf{b}\to\mathbf{a})
and since
\sqcup
is the supremum
and
\star
is monotone we obtain
(\mathbf{c}\sqcup\mathbf{a})\leq_{\textrm{W}}(\mathbf{c}\sqcup\mathbf{b})\star(\mathbf{b}\to\mathbf{a})
, which implies
the claim by Corollary
3.3
.
As a counterexample for the other direction, consider
\mathbf{a}=\mathbf{c}\equiv_{\textrm{W}}\lim
and
\mathbf{b}=1
.
∎

5.
Embeddings of the Medvedev degrees and ideals

It was observed in
[
5
]
that the Medvedev degrees
\mathfrak{M}
admit an embedding as a meet-semilattice into the Weihrauch degrees; this embedding is obtained by mapping non-empty
A\subseteq{\mathbb{N}^{\mathbb{N}}}
to
c_{A}:\{1\}\rightrightarrows{\mathbb{N}^{\mathbb{N}}}
with
c_{A}(1)=A
and
A=\emptyset
to
\infty
. Furthermore, Higuchi and P.
[
23
]
investigated mapping
A\subseteq{\mathbb{N}^{\mathbb{N}}}
to
d_{A}:A\to\{1\}
, which induces a lattice embedding of
\mathfrak{M}^{\mathrm{op}}
into
\mathcal{W}
. In particular, they noted that the image of
\mathfrak{M}^{\mathrm{op}}
under
d_{(\cdot)}
is exactly the lower cone
\{\mathbf{a}\in\mathcal{W}:\mathbf{a}\leq_{\textrm{W}}1\}=\{\mathbf{a}\sqcap 1:\mathbf{a}\in\mathcal{W}\}
.

As a side note, it shall be pointed out that via the lattice embedding,
[
43
, Lemma 6.1]
we see that any countable distributive lattice can be embedded into the Weihrauch lattice
\mathcal{W}
. In particular, this means that Theorem
4.1
already contains the fullest possible extent of valid algebraic rules expressible in terms of
\sqcap
and
\sqcup
.

Now we are able to provide an internal characterization of the image of
\mathfrak{M}
under
c_{(\cdot)}
by observing that it coincides with
\{\mathbf{a}\rightarrow 1:\mathbf{a}\in\mathcal{W}\}
. Moreover,
c_{(\cdot)}
and
d_{(\cdot)}
are related via
c_{A}\equiv_{\textrm{W}}d_{A}\rightarrow 1
.
In general, for
f:\subseteq\mathbf{X}\rightrightarrows\mathbf{Y}
and
1=\textnormal{id}:\{0\}\to\{0\}
we have
\mathcal{M}(\mathbf{Y},\{0\})\cong\{0\}
and hence
we can identify
(f\to 1)
with the problem
(f\to 1):\{0\}\rightrightarrows\mathbf{X},0\mapsto\operatorname{dom}(f)
.
In particular,
(f\to 1)
is pointed.

If we combine the observation in
[
5
]
that
\times
as supremum in
\mathfrak{M}
is mapped by
c_{(\cdot)}
to
\times
in
\mathcal{W}
with Proposition
4.2
(8) we see that
\{\mathbf{a}\rightarrow 1:\mathbf{a}\in\mathcal{W}\}
is closed under
\star
and
\times
. However, it is not closed under
\sqcup
. Finally, note that the downwards closure of
\{\mathbf{a}\rightarrow 1:0<_{\textrm{W}}\mathbf{a}\in\mathcal{W}\}
is the collection of all continuous multi-valued functions.

Proposition
\thethm

(Continuity)
.

f
is continuous
\iff(\exists g>_{\textrm{W}}0)\;f\leq_{\textrm{W}}(g\to 1)
.

Proof.

If
f
is continuous, then
f
is computable in some oracle
p\in{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}
. Hence we obtain
f\leq_{\textrm{W}}(d_{\{p\}}\to 1)
and
d_{\{p\}}>_{\textrm{W}}0
.
On the other hand, if
g>_{\textrm{W}}0
, then
\operatorname{dom}(g)\not=\emptyset
and hence
(g\to 1)
has a constant and hence continuous realizer.
If
f\leq_{\textrm{W}}(g\to 1)
then
f
has to be continuous too.
∎

This shows that the notion of continuity is definable in the structure of the Weihrauch lattice.
The facts above motivate the following definition:

Definition
\thethm
.

Call
\mathfrak{A}\subseteq\mathcal{W}
a
\star
–ideal
, if
\mathfrak{A}
is downwards closed,
1\in\mathfrak{A}
and
\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}\in\mathfrak{A}
implies
\mathbf{a}\star\mathbf{b},\mathbf{a}\sqcup\mathbf{b}\in\mathfrak{A}
.
A
\star
–ideal
\mathfrak{A}
is
prime
, if
\mathbf{a}\sqcap\mathbf{b}\in\mathfrak{A}
implies
\mathbf{a}\in\mathfrak{A}
or
\mathbf{b}\in\mathfrak{A}
.
A
\star
–ideal is
etheric
, if for any
\mathbf{a}\in\mathfrak{A}
,
\mathbf{b}\in\mathcal{W}
there is a
\mathbf{a}^{\prime}\in\mathfrak{A}
such that
\mathbf{a}\star\mathbf{b}\leq_{\textrm{W}}\mathbf{b}\star\mathbf{a}^{\prime}
.
If the downwards closure of
\mathfrak{B}\subseteq\mathcal{W}
is a
\star
–ideal, we call
\mathfrak{B}
a
\star
–preideal
.
A
\star
–preideal is
prime
(
etheric
) if its downwards closure is.

We obtain the following example of a prime etheric
\star
–preideal in the Weihrauch lattice
\mathcal{W}
.

Proposition
\thethm
.

\mathfrak{A}=\{\mathbf{a}\rightarrow 1:0<_{\textrm{W}}\mathbf{a}\in\mathcal{W}\}
is a prime etheric
\star
–preideal.

Proof.

To see that
\mathfrak{A}
is a
\star
–preideal, it only remains to be shown that its downward closure is closed under
\sqcup
. For this, note that
1\leq_{\textrm{W}}\mathbf{a}
for any
0\not=\mathbf{a}\in\mathfrak{A}
, and that
1\leq_{\textrm{W}}\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}
implies
\mathbf{a}\sqcup\mathbf{b}\leq_{\textrm{W}}\mathbf{a}\times\mathbf{b}
by Proposition
4.3
. That
\mathfrak{A}
is prime is the statement of
[
23
, Proposition 4.8]
. That
\mathfrak{A}
is etheric is a consequence of the even stronger observation that for
\mathbf{a}\in\mathfrak{A}
,
\mathbf{b}\in\mathcal{W}
we find
\mathbf{a}\star\mathbf{b}=\mathbf{a}\times\mathbf{b}
by Proposition
4.2
(8).
∎

The prerequisites of
\star
–preideals allow us to define quotients of the Weihrauch degrees preserving the structure, as we shall explore next.
We start with the definition of Weihrauch reducibility relative to a subset
\mathfrak{A}\subseteq\mathcal{W}
.

Definition
\thethm
.

Let
\mathfrak{A}\subseteq\mathcal{W}
. For
\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}\in\mathcal{W}
let
\mathbf{a}\leq_{\textrm{W}}^{\mathfrak{A}}\mathbf{b}
abbreviate
(\exists\mathbf{c}\in\mathfrak{A})\;\mathbf{a}\leq_{\textrm{W}}\mathbf{b}\star\mathbf{c}
.

By Corollary
3.3
we obtain the following immediate characterization of Weihrauch reducibility relative to
\mathfrak{A}
.

Corollary
\thethm
.

If
\mathfrak{A}
is downwards closed, then

\mathbf{a}\leq_{\textrm{W}}^{\mathfrak{A}}\mathbf{b}\iff(\mathbf{b}\to\mathbf{a})\in\mathfrak{A}
.

Now we can formulate the main result on quotient structures
\mathcal{W}/\mathfrak{A}
of the Weihrauch lattice.

Theorem
\thethm
.

If
\mathfrak{A}
is a
\star
–preideal, then
\leq_{\textrm{W}}^{\mathfrak{A}}
is a preorder. Denote its degrees by
\mathcal{W}/\mathfrak{A}
. Now
\sqcap
,
\sqcup
, and
\times
all induce operations on
\mathcal{W}/\mathfrak{A}
. In particular,
(\mathcal{W}/\mathfrak{A},\sqcap,\sqcup)
is a lattice. If
\mathfrak{A}
is etheric, then also
\star
and
\rightarrow
induce operations on
\mathcal{W}/\mathfrak{A}
.

Proof.

We consider the different parts of the claim step by step:

(preorder):

There has to be some
\mathbf{s}_{1}\in\mathfrak{A}
with
1\leq_{\textrm{W}}\mathbf{s}_{1}
, monotonicity of
\star
then implies for any
\mathbf{a}\in\mathcal{W}
that
\mathbf{a}\leq_{\textrm{W}}\mathbf{a}\star\mathbf{s}_{1}
(via Proposition
4.1
, Observation
4.1
), hence
\mathbf{a}\leq_{\textrm{W}}^{\mathfrak{A}}\mathbf{a}
.
If
\mathbf{a}\leq_{\textrm{W}}^{\mathfrak{A}}\mathbf{b}
and
\mathbf{b}\leq_{\textrm{W}}^{\mathfrak{A}}\mathbf{c}
, then by definition of
\leq_{\textrm{W}}^{\mathfrak{A}}
there are
\mathbf{d}_{1}
,
\mathbf{d}_{2}\in\mathfrak{A}
such that
\mathbf{a}\leq_{\textrm{W}}\mathbf{b}\star\mathbf{d}_{1}
and
\mathbf{b}\leq_{\textrm{W}}\mathbf{c}\star\mathbf{d}_{2}
. Monotonicity of
\star
(Proposition
4.1
again) then implies
\mathbf{a}\leq_{\textrm{W}}\mathbf{c}\star\mathbf{d}_{2}\star\mathbf{d}_{1}
.
\mathfrak{M}
being a
\star
–preideal means there is some
\mathbf{d}\in\mathfrak{M}
with
\mathbf{d}_{2}\star\mathbf{d}_{1}\leq_{\textrm{W}}\mathbf{d}
, monotonicity of
\star
again implies
\mathbf{a}\leq_{\textrm{W}}\mathbf{c}\star\mathbf{d}
, equivalently
\mathbf{a}\leq_{\textrm{W}}^{\mathfrak{A}}\mathbf{c}
, thus establishing transitivity of
\leq_{\textrm{W}}^{\mathfrak{A}}
.

(operations are invariant):

It suffices to show that the operations are monotone with respect to
\leq_{\textrm{W}}^{\mathfrak{A}}
. Assume
\mathbf{a}\leq_{\textrm{W}}^{\mathfrak{A}}\mathbf{a}^{\prime}
(via
\mathbf{a}\leq_{\textrm{W}}\mathbf{a}^{\prime}\star\mathbf{c}
,
\mathbf{c}\in\mathfrak{A}
) and
\mathbf{b}\leq_{\textrm{W}}^{\mathfrak{A}}\mathbf{b}^{\prime}
(via
\mathbf{b}\leq_{\textrm{W}}\mathbf{b}^{\prime}\star\mathbf{c}^{\prime}
,
\mathbf{c}^{\prime}\in\mathfrak{A}
). As the downwards closure of
\mathfrak{A}
is closed under
\sqcup
, we may safely assume
\mathbf{c}=\mathbf{c}^{\prime}
. As
\mathfrak{A}
is a
\star
–preideal, there must be some
\mathbf{d}\in\mathfrak{A}
with
\mathbf{c}\times\mathbf{c}\leq_{\textrm{W}}\mathbf{d}
. Then

(
\sqcap
):

\mathbf{a}\sqcap\mathbf{b}\leq_{\textrm{W}}(\mathbf{a}^{\prime}\star\mathbf{c})\sqcap(\mathbf{b}^{\prime}\star\mathbf{c})\leq_{\textrm{W}}(\mathbf{a}^{\prime}\sqcap\mathbf{b}^{\prime})\star(\mathbf{c}\times\mathbf{c})\leq_{\textrm{W}}(\mathbf{a}^{\prime}\sqcap\mathbf{b}^{\prime})\star\mathbf{d}
by Proposition
4.2
(7). Hence
\mathbf{a}\sqcap\mathbf{b}\leq_{\textrm{W}}^{\mathfrak{A}}\mathbf{a}^{\prime}\sqcap\mathbf{b}^{\prime}
.

(
\sqcup
):

\mathbf{a}\sqcup\mathbf{b}\leq_{\textrm{W}}(\mathbf{a}^{\prime}\star\mathbf{c})\sqcup(\mathbf{b}^{\prime}\star\mathbf{c})\leq_{\textrm{W}}(\mathbf{a}^{\prime}\sqcup\mathbf{b}^{\prime})\star\mathbf{c}
by Proposition
4.2
(3), which implies
\mathbf{a}\sqcup\mathbf{b}\leq_{\textrm{W}}^{\mathfrak{A}}\mathbf{a}^{\prime}\sqcup\mathbf{b}^{\prime}
.

(
\times
):

\mathbf{a}\times\mathbf{b}\leq_{\textrm{W}}(\mathbf{a}^{\prime}\star\mathbf{c})\times(\mathbf{b}^{\prime}\star\mathbf{c})\leq_{\textrm{W}}(\mathbf{a}^{\prime}\times\mathbf{b}^{\prime})\star(\mathbf{c}\times\mathbf{c})\leq_{\textrm{W}}(\mathbf{a}^{\prime}\times\mathbf{b}^{\prime})\star\mathbf{d}
by Proposition
4.2
(6). Hence
\mathbf{a}\times\mathbf{b}\leq_{\textrm{W}}^{\mathfrak{A}}\mathbf{a}^{\prime}\times\mathbf{b}^{\prime}
.

(
\star
):

\mathbf{a}\star\mathbf{b}\leq_{\textrm{W}}\mathbf{a}^{\prime}\star\mathbf{c}\star\mathbf{b}^{\prime}\star\mathbf{c}
. If
\mathfrak{A}
is etheric, there is some
\mathbf{e}\in\mathfrak{A}
with
\mathbf{c}\star\mathbf{b}^{\prime}\leq_{\textrm{W}}\mathbf{b}^{\prime}\star\mathbf{e}
. As the downwards closure of
\mathfrak{A}
is closed under
\star
, there is some
\mathbf{f}\in\mathfrak{A}
with
\mathbf{e}\star\mathbf{c}\leq_{\textrm{W}}\mathbf{f}
. So
\mathbf{a}\star\mathbf{b}\leq_{\textrm{W}}(\mathbf{a}^{\prime}\star\mathbf{b}^{\prime})\star(\mathbf{e}\star\mathbf{c})\leq_{\textrm{W}}(\mathbf{a}^{\prime}\star\mathbf{b}^{\prime})\star\mathbf{f}
, i.e.,
\mathbf{a}\star\mathbf{b}\leq_{\textrm{W}}^{\mathfrak{A}}\mathbf{a}^{\prime}\star\mathbf{b}^{\prime}
.

(
\rightarrow
):

For
\to
we need to show anti-monotonicity in the first argument instead.
By assumption and Corollary
3.3
we have
(\mathbf{a}^{\prime}\rightarrow\mathbf{a})\leq_{\textrm{W}}\mathbf{c}
.
Since
\mathfrak{A}
is etheric, there is some
c^{\prime}\in\mathfrak{A}
such that
\mathbf{c}\star(\mathbf{a}\to\mathbf{b})\leq_{\textrm{W}}(\mathbf{a}\to\mathbf{b})\star\mathbf{c}^{\prime}
.
Now we obtain with Theorem
3.3
and Proposition
4.1
(2)

\mathbf{b}\leq_{\textrm{W}}\mathbf{a}^{\prime}\star(\mathbf{a}^{\prime}\rightarrow\mathbf{a})\star(\mathbf{a}\rightarrow\mathbf{b})\leq_{\textrm{W}}\mathbf{a}^{\prime}\star\mathbf{c}\star(\mathbf{a}\rightarrow\mathbf{b})\leq_{\textrm{W}}\mathbf{a}^{\prime}\star(\mathbf{a}\rightarrow\mathbf{b})\star\mathbf{c}^{\prime}.

By Corollary
3.3
this implies
(\mathbf{a}^{\prime}\rightarrow\mathbf{b})\leq_{\textrm{W}}(\mathbf{a}\rightarrow\mathbf{b})\star\mathbf{c}^{\prime}
, which is the desired reduction.
To see that
\rightarrow
is monotone in the second component, we note that
\mathbf{b}\leq_{\textrm{W}}\mathbf{b}^{\prime}\star\mathbf{c}\leq_{\textrm{W}}\mathbf{a}\star(\mathbf{a}\rightarrow\mathbf{b}^{\prime})\star\mathbf{c}
.
Now
(\mathbf{a}\rightarrow\mathbf{b})\leq_{\textrm{W}}(\mathbf{a}\rightarrow\mathbf{b}^{\prime})\star\mathbf{c}
follows by Corollary
3.3
, which implies our claim.

(lattice):

Since there is some
\mathbf{s}_{1}\in\mathfrak{A}
with
1\leq_{\textrm{W}}\mathbf{s}_{1}
, we have
\mathbf{a}\leq_{\textrm{W}}^{\mathfrak{A}}\mathbf{a}\sqcup\mathbf{b}
,
\mathbf{b}\leq_{\textrm{W}}^{\mathfrak{A}}\mathbf{a}\sqcup\mathbf{b}
,
\mathbf{a}\sqcap\mathbf{b}\leq_{\textrm{W}}^{\mathfrak{A}}\mathbf{a}
and
\mathbf{a}\sqcap\mathbf{b}\leq_{\textrm{W}}^{\mathfrak{A}}\mathbf{b}
. If
\mathbf{a}\leq_{\textrm{W}}^{\mathfrak{A}}\mathbf{c}
and
\mathbf{b}\leq_{\textrm{W}}^{\mathfrak{A}}\mathbf{c}
, without loss of generality both via
\mathbf{d}\in\mathfrak{A}
, then we have
\mathbf{a}\sqcup\mathbf{b}\leq_{\textrm{W}}\mathbf{c}\star\mathbf{d}
, i.e.,
\mathbf{a}\sqcup\mathbf{b}\leq_{\textrm{W}}^{\mathfrak{A}}\mathbf{c}
. If
\mathbf{c}\leq_{\textrm{W}}^{\mathfrak{A}}\mathbf{a}
and
\mathbf{c}\leq_{\textrm{W}}^{\mathfrak{A}}\mathbf{b}
, again without loss of generality both via
\mathbf{d}\in\mathfrak{A}
, we find
\mathbf{c}\leq_{\textrm{W}}(\mathbf{a}\star\mathbf{d})\sqcap(\mathbf{b}\star\mathbf{d})\leq_{\textrm{W}}(\mathbf{a}\sqcap\mathbf{b})\star(\mathbf{d}\times\mathbf{d})
by Proposition
4.2
(7), thus also
\mathbf{c}\leq_{\textrm{W}}^{\mathfrak{A}}\mathbf{a}\sqcap\mathbf{b}
.
∎

In the next proposition we show that proper
\star
–ideals still distinguish the constants.

Proposition
\thethm
.

Let
\mathfrak{A}\subsetneqq\mathcal{W}
be a
\star
–ideal. Then
0<_{\textrm{W}}^{\mathfrak{A}}1<_{\textrm{W}}^{\mathfrak{A}}\infty
.

Proof.

\mathfrak{A}\subsetneqq\mathcal{W}
is equivalent to
\infty\notin\mathfrak{A}
. Now note that for
\mathbf{a}\neq\infty
we have
0\star\mathbf{a}=0
and
1\star\mathbf{a}=\mathbf{a}
.
∎

\star
–preideals are found in the various classes of functions central to the investigations in

(see

for an overview). Moreover, the reductions studied there turn out to be the duals of the restrictions of the corresponding reductions

to

6.
Applications of the implication

As the implication on Weihrauch degrees has not been studied previously, a few examples where it appears naturally should be illuminating. In fact, it turns out that some degrees expressible as implications between commonly studied degrees have already appeared in the literature, sometimes explicitly and sometimes implicitly.

6.1.
Examples

Our first example shall be
\frac{1}{2}\mathsf{C}_{{[0,1]}}
, which was explicitly investigated in
[
13
]
related to Orevkov’s construction showing the non-constructivity of Brouwer’s Fixed Point theorem. The multi-valued function
\frac{1}{2}\mathsf{C}_{{[0,1]}}
takes as input some non-empty closed subset
A\in\mathcal{A}({[0,1]})
of the unit interval, and produces a pair of points in the unit interval, at least one of which has to lie in
A
.

Proposition
\thethm
.

\frac{1}{2}\mathsf{C}_{{[0,1]}}\equiv_{\textrm{W}}(\mathsf{C}_{\{0,1\}}\rightarrow\mathsf{C}_{{[0,1]}})

Proof.

Given
x_{0},x_{1}\in{[0,1]}
and
A\in\mathcal{A}({[0,1]})
, we can compute
\{i:x_{i}\in A\}\in\mathcal{A}(\{0,1\})
. Hence,
\mathsf{C}_{{[0,1]}}\leq_{\textrm{W}}\mathsf{C}_{\{0,1\}}\star\frac{1}{2}\mathsf{C}_{{[0,1]}}
. This in turn provides us with
(\mathsf{C}_{\{0,1\}}\rightarrow\mathsf{C}_{{[0,1]}})\leq_{\textrm{W}}\frac{1}{2}\mathsf{C}_{{[0,1]}}
by Corollary
3.3
.

It remains to show
\frac{1}{2}\mathsf{C}_{{[0,1]}}\leq_{\textrm{W}}(\mathsf{C}_{\{0,1\}}\rightarrow\mathsf{C}_{{[0,1]}})
.
Let us assume that
\mathsf{C}_{[0,1]}\leq_{\textrm{W}}\mathsf{C}_{\{0,1\}}\star f
. Then upon input of a non-empty
A\in\mathcal{A}({[0,1]})
the function
f
determines
a set
B\in\mathcal{A}(\{0,1\})
so that from
A
and
i\in B
one can compute a point
x\in A
.
In other words, there is a computable multi-valued function
h:\subseteq\mathcal{A}({[0,1]})\times\{0,1\}\rightrightarrows{[0,1]}

such that
h(A,i)\in A
if
i\in B
. We can assume that
h(A,i)
is defined for all non-empty closed
A\subseteq{[0,1]}
and
i\in\{0,1\}
,
which turns the pair
(h(A,0),h(A,1))
into a valid output for
\frac{1}{2}\mathsf{C}_{{[0,1]}}(A)
.
∎

The second example has implicitly been present in
[
27
]
, reductions from it were employed to prove that certain transformations of planar continua are not computable, e.g., in
[
27
, Theorem 3.1]
. The problem is
\mathsf{Almost\mbox{-}EC}:\mathcal{A}(\mathbb{N})\rightrightarrows\mathcal{O}(\mathbb{N})
where
U\in\mathsf{Almost\mbox{-}EC}(A)
if and only if
(A\setminus U)\cup(U\setminus A)
is finite. Consider for comparison
\mathsf{EC}=\textnormal{id}:\mathcal{A}(\mathbb{N})\to\mathcal{O}(\mathbb{N})
, and note
\mathsf{EC}\equiv_{\textrm{W}}\lim
(e.g.,
[
44
]
).

Proposition
\thethm
.

The purpose of this section is to discuss the class of

Definition
\thethm

We call a representation

Being effectively traceable is very closely related to being effectively open and effectively fiber-overt.

Spaces admitting effectively fiber-compact representations are precisely the subspaces of computable metric spaces, whereas any effective topological space has an effectively-fiber overt representation, and conversely, every space with an effectively fiber-overt representation is countably-based.

By

8.
Comparison to other structures

As mentioned in the introduction, there is a strong resemblance between our algebraic operations and the logical connectives in intuitionistic linear logic
[
20
]
. In this,
0
is vacuous truth,
1
is non-vacuous truth and
\infty
is falsity.
In
\sqcap
we find disjunction, in
\sqcup
the additive conjunction and
\times
is the the multiplicative conjunction. In a deviation from the usual setting, we have a second multiplicative conjunction in
\star
, which is not commutative. Likewise, we have two exponentials
∗
and
\widehat{\phantom{f}}
rather than just one. As the commutative multiplicative conjunction
\times
does not have an associated implication, it has to be
\star
that appears in the
modus ponens
deduction rule. To the extent that non-commutative conjunctions have been studied in substructural logics
[
18
]
, the typical requirement is for both the left- and the right-implication to exists – here we only have the right-implication. Whether there are sensible proof systems corresponding to Weihrauch reducibility regardless is an open question.

8.1.
The specification view

Another way of reading elements of
\mathcal{W}
is as specifications linking preconditions to postconditions:

•

In
\mathbf{a}\leq_{\textrm{W}}\mathbf{b}
, some valid precondition for
\mathbf{b}
must be realized whenever the initial condition is a valid precondition for
\mathbf{a}
, and assuming that the
b
-subroutine works correctly, the complete procedure needs to satisfy
\mathbf{a}
.

•

The operation
\sqcap
is non-deterministic combination:
\mathbf{a}\sqcap\mathbf{b}
takes queries to
\mathbf{a}
and
\mathbf{b}
and solves either of them. In terms of specifications: The preconditions for
\mathbf{a}\sqcap\mathbf{b}
are conjunctions of preconditions for
\mathbf{a}
,
\mathbf{b}
, the postconditions are disjunctions.

•

\sqcup
is choice: A query to
\mathbf{a}\sqcup\mathbf{b}
is either a query to
\mathbf{a}
or to
\mathbf{b}
, and the corresponding answer has to be given.
\times
is parallel application:
\mathbf{a}\times\mathbf{b}
takes queries to
\mathbf{a}
and
\mathbf{b}
and solves them both.
\star
is sequential application:
\mathbf{a}\star\mathbf{b}
is the hardest problem which can be solved by first using
\mathbf{b}
exactly once, and then
\mathbf{a}
exactly once.

•

0
is the problem without queries (hence trivially solvable),
1
is the degree of computable problems with computable queries.
\infty
is the problem without solutions, hence impossible to solve.

•

\mathbf{a}^{*}
takes a finite (possibly 0) number of queries to
\mathbf{a}
and answers them all,
\widehat{\mathbf{a}}
takes
\omega
-many queries to
\mathbf{a}
and answers them all.

As such, it seems natural to compare the algebraic structure of the Weihrauch degrees to algebras arising in the study of (program) specification. In particular, there are some similarities to
concurrent Kleene algebras

[
24
,
25
]
.
