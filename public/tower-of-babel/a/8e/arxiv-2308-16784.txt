Dropout ensemble Kalman inversion for high dimensional inverse problems Thanks: Submitted to the editors DATE.
Shuigen Liu, Sebastian Reich, Xin T. Tong

Abstract
Ensemble Kalman inversion (EKI) is an ensemble-based method to solve inverse problems. Its gradient-free formulation makes it an attractive tool for problems with involved formulation. However, EKI suffers from the “subspace property”, i.e., the EKI solutions are confined in the subspace spanned by the initial ensemble. It implies that the ensemble size should be larger than the problem dimension to ensure EKI’s convergence to the correct solution. Such scaling of ensemble size is impractical and prevents the use of EKI in high dimensional problems. To address this issue, we propose a novel approach using dropout regularization to mitigate the subspace problem. We prove that dropout-EKI converges in the small ensemble settings, and the computational cost of the algorithm scales linearly with dimension. We also show that dropout-EKI reaches the optimal query complexity, up to a constant factor. Numerical examples demonstrate the effectiveness of our approach.

Ensemble Kalman inversion, dropout, high dimension, small ensemble

Introduction

Inverse problems arise in various scientific and engineering problems
[
29
,
17
,
2
,
9
]
, where one is interested in recovering unknown parameters from indirect and often noisy observations. Mathematically, the relationship between the unknown parameter, denoted by
u\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{u}}
, and the observed data, denoted by
y\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{y}}
, can often be described by the following model:

y=\mathcal{G}(u)+\eta.

(1)

Here
\mathcal{G}:\mathbb{R}^{d_{u}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d_{y}}
is the forward map. It describes the physical law behind the observation procedure, and it is often corrupted by some observation noise, which is modeled by
\eta\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{y}}
in
eq.

1
.

Directly solving
eq.

1
is often problematic, since the inverse of
\mathcal{G}
might not be well-defined, and we need to mitigate the corruption from noise. A standard way to handle these issues is regularization
[
2
,
9
]
. In this paper, we will focus on the well-known Tikhonov regularization
[
7
]
approach, which is also referred to as
l_{2}
or ridge regularization. Mathematically, this involves finding the minimizer
u^{*}
of the loss function

l(u)=\frac{1}{2}\|y-\mathcal{G}(u)\|^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\|\mathcal{C}_{0}^{-1/2}u\|^{2}.

(2)

The first part of
eq.

2
is the data misfit, which measures the fitness of a candidate solution
u
when compared with data. The second part of
eq.

2
is the regularization term, it measures how well
u
fits some prior information, e.g. sparsity, intensity and smoothness. Here we choose the Tikhonov regularization, so the prior information is encoded through a positive-definite structural matrix
\mathcal{C}_{0}
. It is worth pointing out that
eq.

2
can be seen as negative log-density of the Bayesian posterior of
eq.

1
, if we assume
u
has prior
\mathcal{N}(0,\mathcal{C}_{0})
and
\eta\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I)
. In this context, the minimizer
u^{*}
will become the maximum-a-priori estimator of the posterior density. More details of such connection can be found in
[
29
,
15
,
26
]
.

Minimizing
eq.

2
can be computational challenging in practice. In many applications,
\mathcal{G}
is formulated by a PDE, of which the derivative (or adjoint model) can be too complicate to derive or too large to process.
Ensemble Kalman inversion (EKI) is a derivative-free computational method well-suited for these applications. In short, it employs an ensemble with a Kalman-like formula to avoid the computation of derivatives. By iterating the formula, the EKI ensemble will collapse to a single point which minimizes
eq.

2
. EKI has shown impressive inversion skills in various problems
[
15
,
18
]
. A series of numerical analyses of EKI have been made recently to justify EKI’s performance in linear, nonlinear and stochastic settings
[
26
,
27
,
3
,
8
,
33
,
24
]
.

One fundamental problem of EKI is its implementation on high dimensional problems.
This is characterized by the “subspace property” of EKI
[
15
,
27
]
, which indicates that all ensemble members remains in the linear subspace
\mathcal{V}_{0}
spanned by the initial ensemble. This implies that if we want to obtain
u^{*}
,
\mathcal{V}_{0}
needs to be rich enough and the ensemble size needs to surpass
d_{u}
in general. But such scaling in ensemble size is impractical for large-scale problems. In practice, this problem can be mitigated by exploiting certain specific inverse problem structures. For example,
[
6
]
discusses how to select the best
\mathcal{V}_{0}
so
u^{*}
is likely to be in
\mathcal{V}_{0}
. In another recent work,
[
30
]
considers localization techniques, which can leverage the spatial dependence within an inverse problems. On the other hand, these methods all require certain prior knowledge of the solution structures, so they cannot be universal.

In this paper, we propose to tackle the subspace issue using dropout technique originated from the deep learning literature
[
28
,
32
,
16
,
10
]
.
In the training process of deep neural networks, the dropout technique randomly dropout neurons to avoid overfitting. In theory, it is usually understood as a regularization method
[
28
,
32
,
31
]
or a Bayesian neural network approximation
[
10
]
. In
[
25
]
, dropout is proposed as a heuristic for EKI when implemented on high dimensional logistic regression problems. The idea is justified in
[
25
]
with numerical evidences, but there is not much rigorous understanding.
In this paper, we propose a new formulation of dropout EKI (DEKI), so it can be applied to high dimensional general nonlinear inverse problems with a fixed ensemble size.

Moreover, we prove that by properly adapting the step sizes, DEKI scheme converges exponentially fast for linear problems, or strongly convex nonlinear problems in the final stage. The overall computational cost of DEKI scales linearly with dimension
d_{u}
, which is optimal for high dimensional problems. We also investigate the query complexity of the zeroth order algorithms for inverse problems, and show that the DEKI scheme achieves optimal query complexity up to a constant in the problem class we consider.

The paper is organized as follows. In
section

2
, we derive the DEKI scheme and compare it with related methods. In
section

3
, we provide the convergence analysis of the DEKI, showing the exponential convergence rate. The query complexity is also analyzed. In
section

4
, we show the numerical performance of DEKI on two examples, confirming our theoretic results.

Dropout EKI scheme

In this section, we start with a brief review of EKI formulation designed for
eq.

1
. We then implement the dropout technique and derive DEKI. Finally we provide some comparison of DEKI with related EKI schemes.

2.1
Review: EKI and subspace property

When minimizing the regularized loss function
eq.

2
, it is instructive to formulate it as

l(u)=\frac{1}{2}\left\|z-\mathcal{H}(u)\right\|^{2},\quad\text{where }\mathcal{H}(u)=\begin{bmatrix}\mathcal{G}(u)\\
\mathcal{C}_{0}^{-1/2}u\end{bmatrix},~z=\begin{bmatrix}y\\
0\end{bmatrix}.

(3)

[
7
]
investigated applying vanilla EKI from
[
15
]
on
eq.

3
. In particular, if we denote the ensemble as
\{u_{n}^{(j)}\}_{j=1}^{J}
, the (Tikhonov) EKI scheme reads

u_{n+1}^{(j)}=u_{n}^{(j)}+h_{n}C_{n}^{uz}(h_{n}C_{n}^{zz}+I)^{-1}(z-\mathcal{H}(u_{n}^{(j)})),

(4)

where
C_{n}^{uz},C_{n}^{zz}
are the empirical covariance matrices of the deviations, and
I
denotes the identity matrix. Denote

\begin{split}&\overline{u}_{n}=\frac{1}{J}\sum_{j=1}^{J}u_{n}^{(j)},\quad\tau_{n}^{(j)}=u^{(j)}_{n}-\overline{u}_{n},\quad\overline{\mathcal{H}(u_{n})}=\frac{1}{J}\sum_{j=1}^{J}\mathcal{H}(u_{n}^{(j)}).\\
&C_{n}^{uu}=\frac{1}{J-1}\sum_{j=1}^{J}\tau_{n}^{(j)}\otimes\tau_{n}^{(j)},\quad C_{n}^{uz}=\frac{1}{J-1}\sum_{j=1}^{J}\tau_{n}^{(j)}\otimes\left(\mathcal{H}(u_{n}^{(j)})-\overline{\mathcal{H}(u_{n})}\right)\\
&C_{n}^{zz}=\frac{1}{J-1}\sum_{j=1}^{J}\left(\mathcal{H}(u_{n}^{(j)})-\overline{\mathcal{H}(u_{n})}\right)\otimes\left(\mathcal{H}(u_{n}^{(j)})-\overline{\mathcal{H}(u_{n})}\right).\end{split}

(5)

The
h_{n}
in
eq.

4
is the “stepsize” of the algorithm. The continuous limit formulation can be derived if we fix
h_{n}\equiv h
and let
h\to 0
.
[
8
]
has shown that if
h_{n}
increases with
n
, the convergence speed can be improved.

The implementation of EKI involves iterating
eq.

4
. In general, the EKI ensemble will collapse onto a single point, and we can use the ensemble mean
\bar{u}_{n}
as a candidate minimizer of
eq.

1
. When we have sufficient ensemble,
J>d_{u}
, the update
eq.

4
can be interpreted as a Gauss-Newton type iteration
[
8
]
.

2.2
Naive DEKI

Using mathematical induction, it is not difficult to show that
u_{n}^{(j)}\in\mathcal{V}_{0}=\text{span}\{u_{0}^{(j)}\}_{j=1}^{J}
, which is the “subspace property” discussed in the introduction. At a high level, it is not hard to break the “subspace property”, since we can modify
eq.

4
so that the new ensembles leave
\mathcal{V}_{0}
(e.g. adding naive
randomization). But we also need some guidelines to find reasonable modifications to ensure it converges correctly. We derive these guidelines from a variational perspective. In specific, the fixed point of the iterations should be the minimizer (or a stationary point) of
eq.

2
.

For the simplicity of illustration, we will assume the problem is linear, so
\mathcal{H}(u)=Hu
for some matrix
H
. In this case,
C^{uz}_{n}=C^{uu}_{n}H^{\rm T}
.
Then if
u^{(j)}_{n}
is the stationary point of
eq.

4
, we find

0=C^{uu}_{n}H^{\rm T}(h_{n}HC^{uu}_{n}H^{\rm T}+I)^{-1}(z-Hu_{n}^{(j)}).

(6)

Meanwhile,
u^{(j)}_{n}
is a minimizer of
eq.

6
, iff
0=H^{\rm T}(z-Hu_{n}^{(j)}).
This can be guaranteed by
eq.

6
if
C^{uu}_{n}
has full rank
d_{u}
. But with vanilla EKI, the rank of
C^{uu}_{n}
is at most
J-1
, which is less than
d_{u}
for
J\leq d_{u}
.

Next, we investigate how dropout technique can resolve this issue. Since rank deficiency of
C^{uu}_{n}
is the root of the problem, we consider applying dropout on the ensemble deviations
\tau_{n}^{(j)}=u_{n}^{(j)}-\overline{u}_{n}
, which leads to a dropout ensemble
\{\tilde{u}_{n}^{(j)}\}_{j=1}^{J}
,

\tilde{u}_{n}^{(j)}=\overline{u}_{n}+\tilde{\tau}_{n}^{(j)},\quad\tilde{\tau}_{n}^{(j)}=\rho_{n}\circ\tau_{n}^{(j)},\quad\rho_{n}(s)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\rm i.i.d.}}}{{\sim}}\text{Bernoulli}(\lambda).

(7)

Here
1-\lambda\in(0,1)
is the dropout rate,
\rho_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{u}}
is the dropout mask vector, which has components being i.i.d Bernoulli random variables indicating the indices of the dropout components, i.e.
\{s:\rho_{n}(s)=0\}
. In (
7
),
\circ
denotes the Hadamard product, i.e.
a\circ b(i)=a(i)b(i)
. Note here we take the dropout mask vector
\rho_{n}
to be the same over all ensemble members. Such dropout technique follows the idea in
[
25
]
, and we generalize it by introducing the dropout ensemble
\tilde{u}_{n}^{(j)}
, making it conveniently applicable in general nonlinear problems. In principle, one can also use different dropout mask vectors for different ensemble members. We have tested this variant, but its numerical performance is similar to our version and its analysis harder, so we omit its discussion in this paper.

Throughout the paper, we will add tilde on symbols related to the dropout ensemble. In particular, we have the dropout ensemble covariance:

\begin{split}&\tilde{C}_{n}^{uu}=\frac{1}{J-1}\sum_{j=1}^{J}\tilde{\tau}_{n}^{(j)}\otimes\tilde{\tau}_{n}^{(j)},\quad\tilde{C}_{n}^{uz}=\frac{1}{J-1}\sum_{j=1}^{J}\tilde{\tau}_{n}^{(j)}\otimes\left(\mathcal{H}(\tilde{u}_{n}^{(j)})-\overline{\mathcal{H}(\tilde{u}_{n})}\right),\\
&\tilde{C}_{n}^{zz}=\frac{1}{J-1}\sum_{j=1}^{J}\left(\mathcal{H}(\tilde{u}_{n}^{(j)})-\overline{\mathcal{H}(\tilde{u}_{n})}\right)\otimes\left(\mathcal{H}(\tilde{u}_{n}^{(j)})-\overline{\mathcal{H}(\tilde{u}_{n})}\right).\end{split}

(8)

Replacing the empirical covariance
C_{n}^{uz}
and
C_{n}^{zz}
in
eq.

4
with their dropout versions will lead to the naive DEKI.
From numerical experiments, we find it can already resolve the “subspace property”, namely it will converge to accurate numerical inversion result even if
J<d_{u}
, although the convergence speed might be slow. We will add further numerical modifications to accelerate the convergence.

But before that, it is instructive to provide the intuition why dropout can help with the rank deficiency of sample covariance. Similar to other stochastic algorithms such as stochastic gradient descent, the average effect of using
\tilde{C}^{uu}_{n}
can be characterized by the its conditional average
\mathbb{E}_{n}\tilde{C}_{n}^{uu}
, which is taken conditioned on the information of the current ensemble
\{u_{n}^{(j)}\}_{j=1}^{J}
. With some elementary probability, it can be derived that (see
eq.

23
)

\mathbb{E}_{n}\tilde{C}_{n}^{uu}=\lambda(1-\lambda)\text{\rm diag}(C_{n}^{uu})+\lambda^{2}C_{n}^{uu}\succeq\lambda(1-\lambda)\text{\rm diag}(C_{n}^{uu}),

where
\text{\rm diag}(C_{n}^{uu})
is the diagonal part of
C_{n}^{uu}
, i.e.
\text{\rm diag}(C_{n}^{uu})(s,t)=\delta_{st}C_{n}^{uu}(s,t)
, where
\delta_{st}
is the standard Kronecker delta. It is easy to see that
\text{\rm diag}(C_{n}^{uu})
is full rank if the diagonal elements of
C_{n}^{uu}
are strictly positive.

2.3
DEKI with mean–deviation separation

The convergence of naive DEKI is slow because there is a tradeoff between approximation and optimization: in order to approximate the nonlinear map
\mathcal{H}
more accurately, we would need the ensemble covariance collapse quickly; but if the ensemble collapse too quickly, the ensemble will stop moving based on
eq.

4
. Moreover, analyzing the convergence of naive DEKI is also very challenging, since
\tilde{C}^{uu}_{n}
is very difficult to track.

An easy way to resolve this issue is to use separate dynamics for the mean and deviation update. To illustrate, note that
eq.

4
can also be formulated as

\displaystyle\overline{u}_{n+1}=~

\displaystyle\overline{u}_{n}+h_{n}C_{n}^{uz}(I+h_{n}C_{n}^{zz})^{-1}(z-\overline{\mathcal{H}(u_{n})}),

(9a)

\displaystyle\tau_{n+1}^{(j)}=~

\displaystyle\tau_{n}^{(j)}+h_{n}C_{n}^{uz}(I+h_{n}C_{n}^{zz})^{-1}(\overline{\mathcal{H}(u_{n})}-\mathcal{H}(u_{n}^{(j)})).

(9b)

We will use different
h_{n},C_{n}^{uz}
and
C_{n}^{zz}
for the two separated dynamics.

Since the ensemble mean will be used as the candidate solution, we hope the stationary point of its dynamics will also be a stationary point of
l(u)
. Therefore we will use the dropout covariances in
eq.

9a
. We will also use a step size
\tilde{h}_{n}=\tilde{h}\|C^{uu}_{n}\|^{-1}
, an adaptive choice adopted from
[
18
]
. Note the factor
\|C^{uu}_{n}\|^{-1}
is added to counter balance the fact that the ensemble will collapse, i.e.
C^{uu}_{n}\to 0
. This leads to

\overline{u}_{n+1}=\overline{u}_{n}+\tilde{h}_{n}\tilde{C}_{n}^{uz}(I+\tilde{h}_{n}\tilde{C}_{n}^{zz})^{-1}(z-\mathcal{H}(\overline{u}_{n})),

(10)

where
\tilde{C}_{n}^{uz},\tilde{C}_{n}^{zz}
are generated by
eq.

8
.

As for the dynamics of ensemble deviation
eq.

9b
, we seek a modification where analysis of the ensemble covariance
C^{uu}_{n}
is available. When
\mathcal{H}
is a linear map,
C^{uu}_{n}
is known to have explicit Kalman type update formula
[
15
,
26
]
. For this reason, we seek a linearization of
eq.

9b
, in the form of

\tau_{n+1}^{(j)}=\tau_{n}^{(j)}-h_{n}C_{n}^{uu}H_{n}^{\rm T}(I+h_{n}H_{n}C_{n}^{uu}H_{n}^{\rm T})^{-1}H_{n}\tau_{n}^{(j)}.

(11)

We set the step size
h_{n}=h\|C^{uu}_{n}\|^{-1}
, where
h
is a constant that can be smaller than
\tilde{h}
, so the ensemble will not collapse too fast.

In order for
eq.

11
to approximate
eq.

9b
, a natural choice will be

H_{n}=C_{n}^{zu}(C_{n}^{uu})^{\dagger},

where
(C_{n}^{uu})^{\dagger}
is the pseudo-inverse of
C_{n}^{uu}
. Notably,
eq.

11
will then be identical to
eq.

9b
if
\mathcal{H}
is linear. In our numerical test, this choice already works. But for theoretical analysis below, we would require
H_{n}
to be uniformly bounded w.r.t.
n
in
l_{2}
norm, which may not hold for all nonlinear maps. On the other hand, we note that a truncated version is always available by linearzing the forward map:

H_{n}=\begin{bmatrix}G_{n}\\
\mathcal{C}_{0}^{-1/2}\end{bmatrix},\quad G_{n}:=\mathop{\arg\min}_{\|G\|_{2}\leq M_{G}}\left\|C_{n}^{zu}(C_{n}^{uu})^{\dagger}-\begin{bmatrix}G\\
\mathcal{C}_{0}^{-1/2}\end{bmatrix}\right\|_{\rm F}^{2}.

(12)

Here
M_{G}
is some big prefixed constant and
\left\|\cdot\right\|_{\rm F}
denotes the Frobenius norm. The optimization problem of
eq.

12
can be solved simply by truncation on the singular value, see details in
appendix

C
.

In summary, the
dropout EKI (DEKI)
scheme is given by the mean update
eq.

10
using dropout ensemble covariance, and the deviation update
eq.

11
using a truncated linearization
eq.

12
. The algorithm is provided as
algorithm

1
. We note that while its formulation is more involved than EKI
eq.

4
, DEKI’s computational cost per iteration is only a constant multiple of the EKI. Our numerical analysis below will show that DEKI will obtain a solution of
\epsilon
accuracy with
O(d_{u}\log\epsilon^{-1}/J)
iterations when the problem is strongly convex.

Remarks 1
.

(1) We can also use
\tilde{C}^{uu}_{n}
in
eq.

11
. We observe in numerical tests that as long as the ensemble is stable, using dropout in
eq.

11
behaves similarly as the DEKI scheme. However, this variant is difficult to analyze rigorously, so we present our current formulation.

(2) The step size
\tilde{h}_{n}=\tilde{h}\|C_{n}^{uu}\|^{-1}
offsets the ensemble collapse and ensures the fast convergence of EKI. Another popular choice is the constant step size
h_{n}\equiv h
, which is favorable when one wishes to allow noisy update scheme, or the smoothness constant is hard to estimate. But such choice leads to slower convergence
[
18
]
, and with rate that might scale with the dimension
d_{u}
. One can also consider
h_{n}=hn^{-\alpha}
used in
[
8
]
, which leads to accelerated convergence. We mainly consider the first choice here, and the thorough comparison of different step size choices is out of scope of this paper.

2.4
Related literature

2.4.1
EKI methodology

EKI has been introduced in
[
15
]
as an extension of the popular ensemble Kalman filter (EnKF) methodology to the treatment of inverse problems in the context of optimization. Both the EnKF and EKI have recently been surveyed in
[
4
]
primarily from a mean field perspective. However, practical algorithm use finite ensemble sizes and establishing convergence and computational efficiency for nonlinear problems have remained an open problem.

Here we focus first on the finite ensemble size effect and later also address the issue of computational efficiency. As discussed before, the subspace property will prevent the ensemble to converge to the optimal solution. However, some asymptotic behavior of EKI can be analyzed in linear
[
26
,
27
]
and nonlinear
[
7
,
33
]
cases. Roughly speaking, the ensemble converges to the optimal solution projected onto the subspace spanned by the initial ensemble. Localization
[
30
]
is one method to overcome this issue, which will be discussed later in detail. Interested readers are referred to
[
8
,
33
]
for further recent results on EKI.

EKI can also be understood as a zeroth order optimization method
[
15
,
7
,
8
]
. In particular, it can be viewed as a gradient flow using the empirical covariance as a preconditioner
[
26
,
33
]
. It approximates the gradients by the finite differences, making the method derivative-free. Among zeroth order optimization methods, it is known that there are methods with complexity
\mathcal{O}(d_{u}\log\varepsilon^{-1})
to achieve
\varepsilon
accuracy for strongly convex functions, such as the Nesterov random search method
[
22
]
. The complexity of DEKI matches this result, and in fact we show that this rate is optimal even for linear inverse problems.

2.4.2
Dropout in deep learning

Dropout technique is first introduced in neural network (NN) by Hinton et al.
[
28
]
as an implicit regularization technique to avoid overfitting. The idea is to randomly omit parts of the neurons in a NN during training. This method has proven to be very effective in improving the performance of NN in a wide range of tasks
[
28
]
and is accepted as a common practice now. There is a long literature exploring the practical implementation of different dropout methods in different types of NNs. Popular methods include the original dropout
[
28
]
, dropconnect
[
32
]
, variational dropout
[
16
]
, Monte Carlo dropout
[
10
]
etc.

Despite its rich application in deep learning, theoretic understanding of dropout is still limited. Most practitioners regard dropout as an implicit regularization method
[
28
,
32
,
31
]
. By randomly dropping out neurons, dropout decreases the dependence on individual neurons to avoid overfitting. As a regularization method, dropout can also be viewed as a noisy perturbation of the output of the neurons, which encourages the model to learn more robust features. Another explanation is to interpret dropout as a Bayesian approximation
[
10
]
, where a probability distribution is imposed over the parameters, and dropout is used to draw samples of the parameters approximately. Then the ability to avoid overfitting can be justified by the use of a Bayesian model.

The dropout method we use in DEKI is similar to dropout NN only at a high level. Dropout in DEKI decreases the dependence on different parameters to avoid spurious long-distance correlation over the components, which is a well-known issue especially in the high dimensional and small ensemble settings. On the other hand, dropout in DEKI has different structures compared to that in the NN. One obvious difference is that dropout acts linearly in DEKI, while it acts highly nonlinearly in NN. Another difference is that dropout in DEKI is applied to the deviations, so that it preserves the structure of the parameters. But in NN, dropout might change the topology of the parameters. The exact reason and benefits of dropout for EKI should be analyzed independently.

2.4.3
Localized EKI

Localization is a crucial technique used in EnKF to deal with the sampling errors due to a limited ensemble size
[
13
,
14
,
11
,
23
]
. The idea is to artificially reduces the covariance between distant locations, typically by applying a distance-based weighting function.
[
30
]
investigated the application of localization in EKI, and showed that the localized EKI (LEKI) scheme can be applied in the small ensemble settings and overcome the subspace issue of EKI effectively.

Our scheme bears a resemblance to the LEKI, but avoids the potentially-involved design of
the localization methods, and is computationally cheaper since DEKI can be implemented using only matrix-vector multiplication while LEKI cannot. At a high level, DEKI can also be viewed as a stochastic implementation of the LEKI, see
eq.

23
for details. We also emphasize that there are other possible ways to apply dropout in EKI, and here we just choose one version to illustrate the general idea and show theoretic properties.

3
Analysis of DEKI

The analysis is divided into two parts. First we show the ensemble collapses in a controllable way by studying the dynamic of the spectrum of the ensemble covariance, from which we obtain lower bounds of the diagonal entries. Second we prove the convergence of DEKI by approximating it locally by a Gauss–Newton type scheme and derive contraction using the lower bounds obtained before. Here an important observation is that the dropout emphasizes the diagonal elements of the ensemble covariance in average, see
eq.

23
for details.

3.1
Assumptions

We make the following assumptions on the regularized forward operator
\mathcal{H}

eq.

3
, the linearized map
H_{n}

eq.

12
and the
l^{2}
-loss

eq.

Assumptions 2
.

The following conditions hold:

1.

Bounded Hessian.
\mathcal{H}\in C^{2}
and
\exists H>0
s.t.

\sup_{u}\left(\sum_{s=1}^{d_{z}}\left\|\nabla^{2}\mathcal{H}_{s}(u)\right\|_{2}^{2}\right)^{1/2}\leq H,

(13)

where
\mathcal{H}_{s}
denotes the
s
-th component of
\mathcal{H}
.

2.

Boundedness of
H_{n}
.
\exists 0<\gamma\leq M
s.t.
\forall n\in\mathbb{N}
,

\gamma^{2}I\preceq H_{n}^{\rm T}H_{n}\preceq M^{2}I.

(14)

3.

L
-smoothness of
l(u)
.
\exists L>0
, s.t.
\forall u,v\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{u}}
,

\|\nabla l(u)-\nabla l(v)\|\leq L\left\|u-v\right\|.

(15)

4.

Polyak-Łojasiewicz (PL) condition for
l(u)
.
\exists c>0
s.t.
\forall u\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{u}}
,

\left\|\nabla l(u)\right\|^{2}\geq c(l(u)-l_{\min}),

(16)

where
l_{\min}:=\min_{u}l(u)
.

Remarks 3
.

(1) The bounded Hessian condition

13
directly follows from that of the forward map
\mathcal{G}
. The boundedness of

can be achieved when we take
H_{n}=[G_{n}^{\rm T},\mathcal{C}_{0}^{-1/2}]^{\rm T}
defined in

12
. Notice
H_{n}^{\rm T}H_{n}=G_{n}^{\rm T}G_{n}+\mathcal{C}_{0}^{-1}
, since
\left\|G_{n}\right\|\leq M_{G}
, suppose
\mathcal{C}_{0}^{-1}\succeq\gamma_{0}^{2}I
, then (

\gamma=\gamma_{0}
and

.

(2) The

-smoothness and PL condition for

are standard assumptions in optimization. Note PL condition is a weaker condition than strong convexity, and is widely adopted in the machine learning literature when lacking convexity
[

3.2
Controllable ensemble collapse

The linearization
eq.

12
applied to the covariance evolution produces a behavior similar to that of a linear forward map. It allows us to have good controls on the ensemble collapse as follows:

Proposition 4
.

Consider the DEKI scheme for the regularized problem
eq.

3
. Assume that the linearized map
H_{n}
satisfies
\gamma^{2}I\preceq H_{n}^{\rm T}H_{n}\preceq M^{2}I
. Denote
C_{n}^{uu}
as the empirical covariance. Choose adaptive step sizes
h_{n}=\theta\left\|C_{n}^{uu}\right\|^{-1}
, where
\theta\leq M^{-2}
. Then the modified condition number of
C_{n}^{uu}
is uniformly bounded:

\forall n\in\mathbb{N},\quad\kappa(C_{n}^{uu}):=\frac{\lambda_{1}(C_{n}^{uu})}{\lambda_{r}(C_{n}^{uu})}\leq\bar{\kappa}=\max\left\{\kappa(C_{0}^{uu}),\frac{3M^{2}}{2\gamma^{2}}\right\}.

(17)

where
\lambda_{k}(C_{n}^{uu})
is the
k
-th largest eigenvalues of
C_{n}^{uu}
and
r
is the rank of
C_{n}^{uu}
, which is invariant under evolution:
\text{\rm rank}(C_{n}^{uu})\equiv r:=\text{\rm rank}(C_{0}^{uu})
. Moreover, the ensemble collapses exponentially,

\|C_{n}^{uu}\|\leq\|C_{0}^{uu}\|(1+\gamma^{2}\theta)^{-2n}.

(18)

Proof.

Rewrite the deviation update
eq.

11
as

\begin{split}\tau_{n+1}^{(j)}=~&\left[I-h_{n}C_{n}^{uu}H_{n}^{\rm T}(I+h_{n}H_{n}C_{n}^{uu}H_{n}^{\rm T})^{-1}H_{n}\right]\tau_{n}^{(j)}\\
=~&(I+h_{n}C_{n}^{uu}H_{n}^{\rm T}H_{n})^{-1}\tau_{n}^{(j)},\end{split}

where we use the Sherman-Morrison-Woodbury formula (see
lemma

15
) by taking
A=I,U=h_{n}C_{n}^{uu}H_{n}^{\rm T}
and
V=H_{n}^{\rm T}
.

Recall by definition
C_{n}^{uu}=\frac{1}{J-1}\sum_{j=1}^{J}\tau_{n}^{(j)}(\tau_{n}^{(j)})^{\rm T}
. Therefore,

C_{n+1}^{uu}=(I+h_{n}C_{n}^{uu}H_{n}^{\rm T}H_{n})^{-1}C_{n}^{uu}(I+h_{n}H_{n}^{\rm T}H_{n}C_{n}^{uu})^{-1}.

By assumption,
\gamma^{2}I\preceq H_{n}^{\rm T}H_{n}\preceq M^{2}I.
Apply
lemma

17
to
C_{n+1}^{uu}
, we obtain

C_{n}^{uu}(I+M^{2}h_{n}C_{n}^{uu})^{-2}\preceq C_{n+1}^{uu}\preceq C_{n}^{uu}(I+\gamma^{2}h_{n}C_{n}^{uu})^{-2}.

(19)

Notice when
0\leq x\leq a^{-1}
,

\frac{x}{1+3ax}\leq\frac{x}{(1+ax)^{2}}\leq\frac{x}{1+2ax}.

Applying
lemma

16
(3), when
h_{n}=\theta\|C_{n}^{uu}\|^{-1}
and
\theta\leq M^{-2}\leq\gamma^{-2}
, it holds

C_{n}^{uu}(I+3M^{2}h_{n}C_{n}^{uu})^{-1}\preceq C_{n+1}^{uu}\preceq C_{n}^{uu}(I+2\gamma^{2}h_{n}C_{n}^{uu})^{-1}.

Denote
\lambda_{k}(C_{n}^{uu})
as the
k
-th largest eigenvalues of
C_{n}^{uu}
, then (see
lemma

16
(2))

\frac{\lambda_{k}(C_{n}^{uu})}{1+3h_{n}M^{2}\lambda_{k}(C_{n}^{uu})}\leq\lambda_{k}(C_{n+1}^{uu})\leq\frac{\lambda_{k}(C_{n}^{uu})}{1+2h_{n}\gamma^{2}\lambda_{k}(C_{n}^{uu})}.

(20)

Hence
\lambda_{k}(C_{n}^{uu})>0\Leftrightarrow\lambda_{k}(C_{n+1}^{uu})>0
, which implies that the rank of
C_{n}^{uu}
remains constant
r=\text{\rm rank}(C_{0}^{uu})
. From
eq.

20
, we get for
1\leq k\leq r
,

2h_{n}\gamma^{2}\leq\lambda_{k}^{-1}(C_{n+1}^{uu})-\lambda_{k}^{-1}(C_{n}^{uu})\leq 3h_{n}M^{2}.

Denote the modified condition number
\kappa_{n}=\lambda_{1}(C_{n}^{uu})/\lambda_{r}(C_{n}^{uu})
. Then

\begin{split}\kappa_{n+1}=~&\frac{\lambda_{r}^{-1}(C_{n+1}^{uu})}{\lambda_{1}^{-1}(C_{n+1}^{uu})}\leq\frac{\lambda_{r}^{-1}(C_{n}^{uu})+3h_{n}M^{2}}{\lambda_{1}^{-1}(C_{n}^{uu})+2h_{n}\gamma^{2}}\\
\leq~&\max\left\{\frac{\lambda_{r}^{-1}(C_{n}^{uu})}{\lambda_{1}^{-1}(C_{n}^{uu})},\frac{3M^{2}}{2\gamma^{2}}\right\}=\max\{\kappa_{n},3M^{2}/2\gamma^{2}\}.\end{split}

By induction, we deduce that

\kappa_{n}\leq\bar{\kappa}:=\max\{\kappa_{0},3M^{2}/2\gamma^{2}\}.

Finally, applying
lemma

16
(1) to
eq.

19
, we obtain

\begin{split}\|C_{n+1}^{uu}\|\leq\lambda_{\max}\Big(&C_{n}^{uu}(I+\gamma^{2}h_{n}C_{n}^{uu})^{-2}\Big)=\frac{\|C_{n}^{uu}\|}{(1+\gamma^{2}h_{n}\|C_{n}^{uu}\|)^{2}}.\\
\Rightarrow~&\|C_{n}^{uu}\|\leq\|C_{0}^{uu}\|(1+\gamma^{2}\theta)^{-2n}.\end{split}

Note the equality above holds since
\psi(x)=x(1+\gamma^{2}h_{n}x)^{-2}
is monotone increasing on
x\in[0,\|C_{n}^{uu}\|]
, due to the fact that when
h_{n}=\theta\|C_{n}^{uu}\|^{-1}
and
\theta\leq M^{-2}\leq\gamma^{-2}
,

\gamma^{2}h_{n}x\leq\gamma^{2}h_{n}\|C_{n}^{uu}\|\leq 1\Rightarrow\psi^{\prime}(x)=\frac{1-\gamma^{2}h_{n}x}{(1+\gamma^{2}h_{n}x)^{2}}\geq 0.

The convergence analysis also requires the control of the diagonal elements of the covariance matrix, whose proof is mainly based on the invariance of the column space of the covariance matrix. We prove

Proposition 5
.

Under the settings of
proposition

4
. The lower bound for the diagonal elements of
C_{n}^{uu}
holds:

\begin{split}\min_{s}C_{n}^{uu}&(s,s)\geq\bar{\kappa}^{-1}\left\|C_{n}^{uu}\right\|\min_{s}P(s,s),\end{split}

(21)

where
\bar{\kappa}
is the upper bound in
eq.

17
, and
P\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{u}\times d_{u}}
is the the orthogonal projector onto the column space
\mathcal{V}_{n}=\text{Im}(C_{n}^{uu})
. The projector is invariant under evolution since
\mathcal{V}_{n}
is invariant:
\mathcal{V}_{n}\equiv\mathcal{V}=\text{Im}(C_{0}^{uu})
.

Remark 6
.

To obtain a meaningful lower bound, we should assume

\min_{s}P(s,s)>0.

(22)

It is equivalent to
\min_{s}C_{0}^{uu}(s,s)>0
, which is necessary since the dropout procedure cannot explore directions with zero covariance. This condition can be achieved easily by using Gaussian initialization.

Proof.

First we prove that the column space of
C_{n}^{uu}
is invariant. Note
\text{Im}(C_{n}^{uu})=\text{span}\{\tau_{n}^{(j)}:j=1,\dots J\}
. Observe that for any
v\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{u}}
that satisfies
\forall 1\leq j\leq J,v^{\rm T}\tau_{n}^{(j)}=0
, it holds

v^{\rm T}\tau_{n+1}^{(k)}=v^{\rm T}\left(\tau_{n}^{(k)}+\frac{h_{n}}{J-1}\sum_{j=1}^{J}\tau_{n}^{(j)}(H_{n}(u_{n}^{(j)})-\overline{H_{n}(u_{n})})^{\rm T}w_{n}^{(j)}\right)=0,

where we denote
w_{n}^{(j)}=(I+h_{n}H_{n}C_{n}^{uu}H_{n}^{\rm T})^{-1}(\overline{H_{n}(u_{n})}-H_{n}(u_{n}^{(j)}))
. Thus by induction, we obtain
\forall n,1\leq j\leq J,v^{\rm T}\tau_{n}^{(j)}=0
. This implies that

\text{Im}(C_{n}^{uu})\subset\text{Im}(C_{0}^{uu}).

While by
proposition

4
,
\text{\rm rank}(C_{n}^{uu})=\text{\rm rank}(C_{0}^{uu})
, we conclude that
\text{Im}(C_{n}^{uu})=\text{Im}(C_{0}^{uu})
.

To control the diagonal element of
C_{n}^{uu}
, consider the singular value decomposition
C_{n}^{uu}=Q_{n}\Lambda_{n}Q_{n}^{\rm T}
, then

C_{n}^{uu}(s,s)=\sum_{t=1}^{r}\lambda_{t}(C_{n}^{uu})(Q_{n}(s,t))^{2}.

Notice
P=Q_{n}Q_{n}^{\rm T}
is the orthogonal projector onto the invariant colunm space
\mathcal{V}=\text{Im}(C_{0}^{uu})
, and therefore is invariant. Then we can control the lower bound of the diagonal element as follows:

\min_{s}C_{n}^{uu}(s,s)\geq\lambda_{r}(C_{n}^{uu})\min_{s}\sum_{t=1}^{r}(Q_{n}(s,t))^{2}\geq\bar{\kappa}^{-1}\|C_{n}^{uu}\|\min_{s}P(s,s).

3.3
Convergence to optimal solution

The convergence of DEKI is mainly guaranteed by the positive definiteness of average dropout ensemble covariance, this is similar to the localization effect used in LEKI. In particular, we claim that

\bar{C}_{n}^{uu}=\mathbb{E}_{n}\tilde{C}_{n}^{uu}=\lambda(1-\lambda)\text{\rm diag}(C_{n}^{uu})+\lambda^{2}C_{n}^{uu},

(23)

where
\text{\rm diag}(C_{n}^{uu})
is the diagonal part of
C_{n}^{uu}
, i.e.
\text{\rm diag}(C_{n}^{uu})(s,t)=\delta_{st}C_{n}^{uu}(s,t)
, and here
\delta_{st}
is the standard kronecker delta. If we denote
\Psi=\lambda^{2}I+\lambda(1-\lambda)E
, where
E(s,t)\equiv 1
, then
\bar{C}_{n}^{uu}=\Psi\circ C_{n}^{uu}
.

To prove
eq.

23
, just notice when
s\neq t
,

\begin{split}\bar{C}_{n}^{uu}(s,t)=~&\mathbb{E}_{n}\left(\frac{1}{J-1}\sum_{j=1}^{J}\rho_{n}(s)\rho_{n}(t)\tau_{n}^{(j)}(s)\tau_{n}^{(j)}(t)\right)\\
=~&\frac{1}{J-1}\sum_{j=1}^{J}\lambda^{2}\tau_{n}^{(j)}(s)\tau_{n}^{(j)}(t)=\lambda^{2}C_{n}^{uu}(s,t).\end{split}

And when
s=t
,

\begin{split}\bar{C}_{n}^{uu}(s,s)=~&\mathbb{E}_{n}\left(\frac{1}{J-1}\sum_{j=1}^{J}\rho_{n}^{2}(s)(\tau_{n}^{(j)}(s))^{2}\right)\\
=~&\frac{1}{J-1}\sum_{j=1}^{J}\lambda(\tau_{n}^{(j)}(s))^{2}=\lambda C_{n}^{uu}(s,s).\end{split}

The convergence analysis is based on the connection of EKI with Gauss-Newton. To be specific, notice the mean evolution
eq.

10
in DEKI can be approximated by the following Gauss-Newton type iteration

\overline{u}_{n+1}^{\prime}=\overline{u}_{n}+\tilde{h}_{n}\tilde{C}_{n}^{uu}G_{n}^{\rm T}(I+\tilde{h}_{n}G_{n}\tilde{C}_{n}^{uu}G_{n}^{\rm T})^{-1}(z-\mathcal{H}(\overline{u}_{n})),

(24)

where
G_{n}=\nabla\mathcal{H}(\overline{u}_{n})
is the Jacobian at
\overline{u}_{n}
. Note it is the Gauss-Newton update for the loss function

l_{n}(u)=\|u-\overline{u}_{n}\|_{\tilde{C}_{n}^{uu}}^{2}+\tilde{h}_{n}\|\mathcal{H}(u)-z\|^{2}.

We can bound the difference of the two updates by the following lemma. It takes a similar form as Proposition 3.3 in
[
8
]
, but the upper bound depends on
d_{u}
there. Here we improve the bounds so that there is no dimension dependence.

Proposition 7
.

Under the settings of
theorem

8
, it holds

\|\overline{u}_{n+1}-\overline{u}_{n+1}^{\prime}\|\leq C\tilde{h}_{n}\|\tilde{C}_{n}^{uu}\|^{3/2}\|z-\mathcal{H}(\overline{u}_{n})\|,

(25)

where
C=(J-1)^{3/2}H
is a constant independent of
d_{u}
and
H
is the bound of the Hessian of
\mathcal{H}
defined in
eq.

13
.

The proof is postponed to
section

A.1
.
Notice though the ensemble evolves stochastically,
proposition

4
,
proposition

5
and
proposition

7
hold deterministically once the intiailization is fixed. This mainly comes from
the fact that we do not use the dropout covariance matrices in the evolution of the
ensemble deviation.
The proposition shows that DEKI can be approximated well by the Gauss-Newton update as the ensemble collapses. It is expected to obtain exponential convergence for such scheme under the PL condition
eq.

16
. To be specific, we prove the main theorem as follows.

Theorem 8
.

Consider the DEKI scheme for the regularized problem
eq.

3
in the high dimensional regime
d_{u}\gg J
. Assume
2
holds, and use standard Gaussian initialization so that
eq.

22
holds. Choose adaptive step sizes

h_{n}=\theta\|C_{n}^{uu}\|^{-1},\quad\tilde{h}_{n}=\mu\|C_{n}^{uu}\|^{-1},

(26)

where
\theta\leq M^{-2},\mu\leq L^{-1}
, and
L,M
are defined in
2
. Then there exists
n_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
s.t. when
n>n_{0}
, the
l^{2}
-loss
eq.

2
converges exponentially,

\mathbb{E}_{n_{0}}l(\overline{u}_{n})-l_{\min}\leq(1-\beta)^{n-n_{0}}(l(\overline{u}_{n_{0}})-l_{\min}+C_{2}),

(27)

where
\beta\in(0,1)
and
C_{2}
are some constants that can be determined explicitly.

Remarks 9
.

(1) The convergence rate
\beta
admits explicitly expression
eq.

34
where
\beta_{0}
in defined in
eq.

30
. Under mild scaling assumptions, one can show that

(see

11
). Therefore, to reach
\varepsilon
-accuracy, the needed steps are

N_{\varepsilon}=\mathcal{O}\left(d_{u}\log\varepsilon^{-1}/J\right).

(28)

Such linear scaling w.r.t. the dimension

is optimal. For more details, please see
proposition

.

(2) Here

is the warm-up steps and can be taken as some fixed number depending only on the initialization, or in other words,

is a
\mathcal{F}_{0}
-measurable random variable. One can show that

using the scaling arguments in

is usually viewed as

quantity, and the term

is harmless to our result. From

l(\overline{u}_{n_{0}})\leq C^{\prime}l(\overline{u}_{0})
.

(3) Computational costs comparison. For vanilla EKI, the ensemble size should be at least
J=\mathcal{O}(d_{u})
, and it takes
\mathcal{O}(\log\varepsilon^{-1})
steps to reach
\varepsilon
-accuracy. In each step, it takes
J
evaluations of the forward map, and
\mathcal{O}(J^{2}(d_{u}+d_{y}))
additional costs due to the matrix-vector multiplications. Thus the overall costs would be
\mathcal{O}(d_{u}\log\varepsilon^{-1})
forward evaluations and
\mathcal{O}(d_{u}^{2}(d_{u}+d_{y})\log\varepsilon^{-1}))
additional costs. For DEKI, it takes
\mathcal{O}(J^{-1}d_{u}\log\varepsilon^{-1})
steps when

is small, and thus the overall costs will be
\mathcal{O}(d_{u}\log\varepsilon^{-1})
forward evaluations, and
\mathcal{O}(Jd_{u}(d_{u}+d_{y})\log\varepsilon^{-1})
additional costs. Since

(4) We can also obtain almost sure convergence with the same linear rate

The supermartingale convergence theorem implies

Remark 12
.

We justify the two assumptions
\overline{\kappa}=\mathcal{O}(1)
and
c=\mathcal{O}(M^{2})
in
lemma

11
.

(1) By definition,
\overline{\kappa}=\max\{\kappa(C_{0}^{uu}),\frac{3M^{2}}{2\gamma^{2}}\}
. First note
\frac{M^{2}}{\gamma^{2}}
is approximately the condition number of the regularized map
\mathcal{H}
. It is reasonable to assume that it is dimension independent, or otherwise the problem itself is ill-posed. For
\kappa(C_{0}^{uu})
, by definition it is the ratio of the largest and smallest nonzero eigenvalues of
C_{0}^{uu}
. In the high dimensional regime where
J\ll d_{u}
, using Gaussian initialization would make
C_{0}^{uu}
close to the projection
P
, and thus
\kappa(C_{0}^{uu})=\mathcal{O}(1)
.

(2) Note
M^{2}\sim\gamma^{2}
as we assume the condition number
M^{2}/\gamma^{2}=\mathcal{O}(1)
. It is also natrual to assume that
c\sim\gamma^{2}
, as they are both the lower bounds of the regularized map
\mathcal{H}
. When
\mathcal{H}
is linear, one can even take
c=\gamma^{2}
. Since
\nabla^{2}l=\mathcal{H}^{\rm T}\mathcal{H}
, the Polyak-Łojasiewic number can be taken as
c=\lambda_{\min}(\mathcal{H}^{\rm T}\mathcal{H})=\gamma^{2}
. Finally,
c=\mathcal{O}(M^{2})
follows easily from
M^{2}\sim\gamma^{2}
and
c\sim\gamma^{2}

3.4
Query complexity analysis

In this section, we consider the lower bound of query complexity for zeroth order algorithms to solve inverse problems, which is closely related to the optimization problems. In the optimization literature, zeroth order algorithms are those who can only evaluate the loss function
l
at a query point, but not the derivatives of
l
. The query complexity is the number of query points needed for the algorithm to reach a certain accuracy. We mention that since the first formal study in
[
20
]
, a lot of results are obtained for different types of optimization algorithms and problem classes, for instance
[
21
,
5
,
1
]
.

It is natural to extend to the inverse problem setting for zeroth order algorithms that can only evaluate
\mathcal{G}(u)
at a query point
u
. Ensemble Kalman type algorithms clearly are zeroth order algorithms, and their query complexity will be
nJ
, where
n
is iteration number needed and
J
is the ensemble size. Query complexity is important because the evaluation of
\mathcal{G}
, which is often a simulation of an expensive black box model, is the main cost of the operation.

theorem

8
indicates the query complexity of DEKI is
\mathcal{O}(d_{u}\log\varepsilon^{-1})
. Next, we show such linear dependence on
d_{u}
is optimal.

Mathematically speaking, a zeroth order algorithm
A
solving
eq.

1
can be described as a map of the form

A:(U,Y,y)\mapsto u.

Here
U=(u_{1},\dots,u_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{u}\times n}
are the query points,
Y=(y_{1},\dots,y_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{y}\times n}
are the model outputs (i.e.
y_{i}=\mathcal{G}(u_{i})
),
y\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{y}}
is the observation data that we try to invert, and
u\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{u}}
is the output of the algorithm. We say
A(U,Y,y)
is a zeroth order algorithm with
n

admissible
query, if the query points
u_{1},\ldots,u_{n}
are obtained from some admissible rule, i.e.
u_{i+1}=B(y,u_{t},\mathcal{G}(u_{t}),t\leq i)
for some update rule
B
, and thus the output is a map
A(U,\mathcal{G}(U),y)
.

Next we show that even for linear inverse problems, with
n\leq d_{u}/2
query points, any algorithms cannot identify the optimal solution to
eq.

2
accurately.

Proposition 13
.

Consider solving
eq.

2
for linear forward maps with regularization operator
\mathcal{C}_{0}^{-1/2}=I
. Then for any zeroth order algorithm
A
using any
n
admissible query
U\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{u}\times n}
where
n\leq d_{u}/2
, it holds that

\max_{G:\|G\|\leq 2}\|A(U,GU,y)-u^{*}(G)\|>0.1\left\|y\right\|,

where
u^{*}(G)
is the optimal solution to
eq.

2
.

Proofs can be found in
section

A.4
. The proposition implies that we need at least
n>d_{u}/2
queries of the forward map to ensure the algorithm
A
finds the optimal solution within
\mathcal{O}(1)
error. For DEKI, in each step we use
2J
queries, so that at least
\mathcal{O}(d_{u}/J)
steps are needed. Also note that DEKI reaches the optimal
\varepsilon
-dependence (
\mathcal{O}(\log\varepsilon^{-1})
) as a zeroth order stochastic algorithm, see
[
21
,
22
]
. This proves the optimality of DEKI.

Numerical examples

We will apply DEKI to two numerical examples with comparison to vanilla EKI and LEKI. For fair comparison, we use the same step size of these EKI type algorithms.

4.1
Linear transport equation

Consider the inverse problem of determining the initial wave based on observations on a later wave field. Here we consider the toy model where the wave speed is constant. The one-dimensional linear transport equation is

\frac{\partial U}{\partial t}(x,t)+a\frac{\partial U}{\partial x}(x,t)=0,\quad x\in[0,1].

For simplicity, we use the periodic boundary condition

\{x\}=x-\lfloor x\rfloor

Suppose we can observe the wave at time

(35)

where

\sigma=10^{-2}
is the observational noise. Introduce a computational grid with

(36)

35

In numerical experiments,

is randomly generated

5
Conclusion

We investigate the application of the dropout technique in EKI method and propose the DEKI scheme. We show that this method mitigates the subspace issue of the vanilla EKI, and performs well in high dimensional inverse problems using small ensemble, both theoretically and numerically. With careful analysis on the dimension dependence, we prove that DEKI converges exponentially for strongly convex problems and the computational complexity scales linearly with dimension. We also show that such scaling is optimal by conducting the query complexity analysis adapting from the optimization literature, which is new for inverse problems.

The idea of incorporating dropout technique into EKI follows the trial of EnKBF with dropout in
[
25
]
. The numerical analysis here is a first step to understand the effectiveness of dropout in these methods. It would be interesting to investigate if the idea can be extended and work as a simpler but effective technique for other ensemble methods in high dimensional problems. It is also interesting to look at the interplay of our DEKI method with traditional zeroth order optimization methods, providing new perspective to understand them and generating new algorithms.

Lemma 16
.

(Comparison) Let
A,B\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
be two positive semidefinite matrices such that
A\preceq B
, then it holds that

(1)

\lambda_{k}(A)\leq\lambda_{k}(B)
, where
\lambda_{k}(M)
is the
k
-th largest eigenvalue of a matrix
M
.

(2)

\forall C\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n},CAC^{\rm T}\preceq CBC^{\rm T}
.

(3)

\forall\psi,\varphi~\text{\rm s.t. }\psi(x)\leq\varphi(x),~\psi(A)\preceq\varphi(A)
. As a corollary, when
\lambda_{k}(A)\in[a,b]
for all
k
and
\psi(x)\leq\varphi(x)
when
x\in[a,b]
, then
\psi(A)\preceq\varphi(A)
.

(4)

\forall\psi:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
that is monotone increasing,
\psi(A)\preceq\psi(B)
. Similarly, for monotone decreasing function
\psi
,
\psi(A)\succeq\psi(B)
.

Proof.

For (1), from Courant minimax principle,

\lambda_{k}(A)=\max_{V\subset\mathbb{R}^{n},\dim V=k}\min_{0\neq u\in V}\frac{u^{\rm T}Au}{u^{\rm T}u}.

Since
A\preceq B\Rightarrow u^{\rm T}Au\leq u^{\rm T}Bu
, we conclude that
\lambda_{k}(A)\leq\lambda_{k}(B)
. For (2), notice

\forall u\in\mathbb{R}^{m},~u^{\rm T}CAC^{\rm T}u=(C^{\rm T}u)^{\rm T}A(C^{\rm T}u)\leq(C^{\rm T}u)^{\rm T}B(C^{\rm T}u)=u^{\rm T}CBC^{\rm T}u.

For (3), denote the eigen decomposition of
A
is
A=Q\Lambda Q^{\rm T}
, then by definition
\psi(A)=Q\psi(\Lambda)Q^{\rm T}
and
\varphi(A)=Q\varphi(\Lambda)Q^{\rm T}
. Notice

\psi(\Lambda)=\text{\rm diag}\{\psi(\lambda_{1}),\dots,\psi(\lambda_{n})\}\preceq\text{\rm diag}\{\varphi(\lambda_{1}),\dots,\varphi(\lambda_{n})\}=\varphi(\Lambda),

since
\psi(\lambda_{k})\leq\varphi(\lambda_{k})
. By (2), we conclude that
\psi(A)\preceq\varphi(A)
. For the corollary, just notice we can modify
\psi(x)=\varphi(x)
outside
[a,b]
and
\psi(A),\varphi(A)
are unchanged. For (4), notice from (1),
\lambda_{k}(A)\leq\lambda_{k}(B)
, the conclusion follows by (2) and the monotonicity of
\psi
using the same claim.

Lemma 17
.

Let
A,B\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
be two positive semidefinite matrices. If there exists

0<m\leq M,\text{\rm s.t. }mI\preceq A\preceq MI
, then it holds

B(I+MB)^{-2}\preceq(I+BA)^{-1}B(I+AB)^{-1}\preceq B(I+mB)^{-2}.

(58)

Proof.

We first prove the inequality for nonsingular
B
. Notice

\begin{split}&(I+BA)^{-1}B(I+AB)^{-1}\\
=~&(B^{1/2}(I+B^{1/2}AB^{1/2})B^{-1/2})^{-1}B(B^{-1/2}(I+B^{1/2}AB^{1/2})B^{1/2})^{-1}\\
=~&B^{1/2}(I+B^{1/2}AB^{1/2})^{-2}B^{1/2}.\end{split}

Using
lemma

16
(2), since
mI\preceq A\preceq MI
, we obtain

mB\preceq B^{1/2}AB^{1/2}\preceq MB,

As a result, using
lemma

16
(4) for
\psi(x)=(1+x)^{-2}
,

\begin{split}B^{1/2}(I+MB)^{-2}B^{1/2}\preceq~&B^{1/2}(I+B^{1/2}AB^{1/2})^{-2}B^{1/2}\\
\preceq~&B^{1/2}(I+mB)^{-2}B^{1/2}.\end{split}

The conclusion follows by observing that the bounds are of functions of
B
are thus the sequence of multiplication is unimportant.

If
B
is nonsingular, we can use the same argument to show

B_{\epsilon}(I+MB_{\epsilon})^{-2}\preceq(I+B_{\epsilon}A)^{-1}B_{\epsilon}(I+AB_{\epsilon})^{-1}\preceq B_{\epsilon}(I+mB_{\epsilon})^{-2}.

with
B_{\epsilon}=B+\varepsilon I
and take limit
\varepsilon\rightarrow 0
.

Appendix C
Implementation of the linearization

First notice it is not necessary to compute
H_{n}
explicitly, since the DEKI scheme only requires its action on the ensemble deviation, i.e.
H_{n}\tau_{n}^{(j)}=\begin{bmatrix}G_{n}\tau_{n}^{(j)}\\
\mathcal{C}_{0}^{-1/2}\tau_{n}^{(j)}\end{bmatrix}
. So it suffices to compute
G_{n}\tau_{n}^{(j)}
. Denote

T_{n}=[u_{n}^{(1)}-\overline{u}_{n},\dots,u_{n}^{(J)}-\overline{u}_{n}]\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{u}\times J},

Y_{n}=[\mathcal{G}(u_{n}^{(1)})-\overline{\mathcal{G}(u_{n})},\dots,\mathcal{G}(u_{n}^{(J)})-\overline{\mathcal{G}(u_{n})}]\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{y}\times J},

Perform the reduced QR decomposition on
T_{n}
and
Y_{n}
:

T_{n}=V_{n}Q_{n},\quad V_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{u}\times r};\quad Y_{n}=W_{n}R_{n},\quad W_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{y}\times r^{\prime}}.

where
r
and
r^{\prime}
are the ranks of
T_{n}
and
Y_{n}
respectively. Denote
\Lambda_{n}=Q_{n}Q_{n}^{\rm T}
, and then

(J-1)C_{n}^{uu}:=T_{n}T_{n}^{\rm T}=V_{n}Q_{n}Q_{n}^{\rm T}V_{n}^{\rm T}=V_{n}\Lambda_{n}V_{n}^{\rm T},

(J-1)C_{n}^{yu}:=Y_{n}T_{n}^{\rm T}=W_{n}R_{n}Q_{n}^{\rm T}V_{n}^{\rm T},

\Rightarrow~C_{n}^{yu}(C_{n}^{uu})^{\dagger}=W_{n}R_{n}Q_{n}^{\rm T}\Lambda_{n}^{-1}V_{n}^{\rm T}.

It is direct to verify that the optimal
G_{n}
to
eq.

12
is

G_{n}=W_{n}\mathcal{T}_{M}(R_{n}Q_{n}^{\rm T}\Lambda_{n}^{-1})V_{n}^{\rm T},

where
\mathcal{T}_{M}
is the truncation operator on singular values:

\mathcal{T}_{M}A=U\min\{\Lambda,M\}V^{\rm T},\quad\text{if }A=U\Lambda V^{\rm T}\text{ is the SVD}.

Here
\min
is performed entrieswise. Therefore,

[G_{n}\tau_{n}^{(1)},\dots,G_{n}\tau_{n}^{(J)}]=G_{n}T_{n}=W_{n}\mathcal{T}_{M}(R_{n}Q_{n}^{\rm T}\Lambda_{n}^{-1})Q_{n}.

The algorithm is summarized as below.

1.

Compute the deviation matrix
T_{n}
and
Y_{n}
.

2.

Perform the reduced QR decomposition:
T_{n}=V_{n}Q_{n},Y_{n}=W_{n}R_{n}
.

3.

Compute
G_{n}T_{n}=W_{n}\mathcal{T}_{M}\left[R_{n}Q_{n}^{\rm T}(Q_{n}Q_{n}^{\rm T})^{-1}\right]Q_{n}
.

The main computational cost is the reduced QR decomposition of
T_{n},Y_{n}
and the SVD in the truncation operator. First notice that
R_{n}Q_{n}^{\rm T}\Lambda_{n}^{-1}\in\mathbb{R}^{r^{\prime}\times r}
, so that the SVD costs
\mathcal{O}(r^{2}r^{\prime})=\mathcal{O}(J^{3})
operations, which has no dimension dependence. For the reduced QR decomposition, since
T_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{u}\times J},Y_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{y}\times J}
, the computational cost would be
\mathcal{O}((d_{u}+d_{y})J^{2})
, which is linear in the dimension. Therefore, it is not a major computational cost in DEKI (see remarks after
theorem

8
).

Appendix D
LEKI and localization

LEKI method is proposed in
[
30
]
. We use the following discrete-time scheme:

u_{n+1}^{(j)}=u_{n}^{(j)}+h_{n}\tilde{C}_{n}^{uz}\left(\mathcal{H}(u_{n}^{(j)})-z\right)+\sigma^{2}\xi_{n}^{(j)}.

The localized covariance is defined via a localization matrix
\Psi\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{z}\times d_{u}}
and

\tilde{C}_{n}^{uz}=\Psi\circ C_{n}^{uz}.

And
\xi_{n}^{(j)}
is some artificial noise chosen following
[
30
]
so that

\frac{1}{J-1}\sum_{j=1}^{J}\left(\xi_{n}^{(j)}\otimes\tau_{n}^{(j)}+\tau_{n}^{(j)}\otimes\xi_{n}^{(j)}\right)=\Sigma_{n},

where the diagonal entries of
\Sigma_{n}
are all
1
. The noise level is fixed
\sigma=10^{-3}
.

For the linear transport model, we compare two different localization designs. Both uses the Gaspari Cohn function
\psi

[
14
]
, and

\Psi(i,j)=\psi(|i-i_{\rm o}(j)|/r_{\rm\rm loc}),

where
i_{\rm o}(j)
is the coordinate of the parameter that mostly influences the
j
-th observation, and
r_{\rm loc}
is the localization radius, see
[
30
]
. The one with knowledge of the speed uses
r_{\rm loc}=1.5
and

i_{\rm o}(j)=\lfloor d_{y}(i/d_{u}+a)\rfloor.

And the one without the knowledge simply chooses
i_{\rm o}(j)=j
with the same localization radius.
