Abstract
. The aim of this paper is to solve an important inverse source problem which arises from the well-known inverse scattering problem. We propose to truncate the Fourier series of the solution to the governing equation with respect to a special basis of L 2 L^{2} . By this, we obtain a system of linear elliptic equations. Solutions to this system are the Fourier coefficients of the solution to the governing equation. After computing these Fourier coefficients, we can directly find the desired source function. Numerical examples are presented.

1.
Introduction

Let
d\geq 1
be the spatial dimension.
Fix a wavenumber
k>0
.
Let
[\underline{\theta},\overline{\theta}]
be an interval of angles.
Let
u_{0}=u_{0}({\bf x},\theta)
,
({\bf x},\theta)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times[\underline{\theta},\overline{\theta}]
, be the incident plane wave that illuminates the medium at an angle
\theta
.
This choice of
u_{0}
as the incident wave arises from the well-known inverse scattering problem, stated later.
Let
u({\bf x},\theta)
,
({\bf x},\theta)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times[\underline{\theta},\overline{\theta}]
, be the wave function that is governed by the Helmholtz equation and the Sommerfeld radiation condition

(1.1)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\Delta u({\bf x},\theta)+k^{2}c_{0}({\bf x})u({\bf x},\theta)=-k^{2}p({\bf x})u_{0}({\bf x},\theta)&{\bf x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\\
\partial_{|{\bf x}|}u({\bf x},\theta)-{\rm i}ku({\bf x},\theta)=o(|{\bf x}|^{\frac{1-d}{2}})&|{\bf x}|\to\infty.\end{array}\right.

Here,
c_{0}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to[1,\infty)
and
u_{0}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\times[\underline{\theta},\overline{\theta}]\to\mathbb{C}
are given functions.
The function
c_{0}
represents the background dielectric constant of the medium.

Let
\Omega
be an open and bounded domain of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with a smooth boundary. Assume that the source function
p
is compactly contained in
\Omega
.
We are interested in the following problem.

Problem 1.1

(Inverse source problem)
.

Determine the source function
p({\bf x})
for all
{\bf x}\in\Omega
from the following boundary measurements

(1.2)

f({\bf x},\theta)=u({\bf x},\theta)\quad\mbox{and}\quad g({\bf x},\theta)=\partial_{\nu}u({\bf x},\theta)

for all
({\bf x},\theta)\in\partial\Omega\times[\underline{\theta},\overline{\theta}].

Here,
\nu({\bf x})
is the unit normal vector to
\partial\Omega
at
{\bf x}
.

This inverse source problem is the linearization of the nonlinear inverse scattering problem. Since the inverse scattering problem has many real-world applications; mostly in exploring some inaccessible regions from external measurement;
for e.g, bio-medical imaging, nano-sciences, security, seismic exploration.
Therefore, Problem
1.1
is significant in those fields.
The solver for Problem
1.1
proposed in this paper consists of three steps.

(1)

We derive a system of linear elliptic equations. Solution of this system is a vector involving the first
N
Fourier coefficients of the function
w=-u/(k^{2}u_{0})
,
N\in\mathbb{N}
, with respect to a special basis of
L^{2}
introduced in
[
35
]
.

(2)

We apply the quasi-reversibility method developed in
[
49
]
to solve the system obtained in Step
1
.

(3)

Directly compute the desired source from the solution obtained in Step
2
.

The inverse source problem and some of its versions were studied intensively. We cite to
[
7
,
8
,
9
,
24
,
27
,
28
]
for the uniqueness, stability and numerical methods to solve inverse source problems for the case when the medium is homogeneous; i.e.
c_{0}=\mbox{constant}
.
The numerical reconstruction methods in those publications are based on the least squares optimization method.
Good quality reconstructions due to those approaches are achieved only when the wave number
k
is large.
We observe that when
k
is large, the data is very sensitive with noise. This is because of the high oscillation of the data.
Unlike this, the reconstructive method in this paper and the method in
[
49
]
do not require the data at high frequency. Therefore, we can reconstruct the source with reasonable value of
k
. The difficulty about the noise is overcome.

As mentioned in step
2
above, we only compute the first
N
Fourier coefficients of the function
w=-u/(k^{2}u_{0})
.
That means we only solve Problem
1.1
in an “Galerkin” approximation context.
Rigorously verifying the convergence of this approximation as
N\to\infty
is extremely challenging. We assume that this approximation is valid.
In contrast, this numerical approach is very effective for many kinds of inverse problems; see e.g.
[
47
,
36
,
48
,
50
,
41
]
. This is the reason we employ this technique again in this paper.

.
In Section

In Section

2.
The significance of Problem
1.1

Problem
1.1
is the linearization of the nonlinear inverse scattering problem, which has many real-world applications. We will list some of the important applications later.
Let
c:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to[1,\infty)
be the spatially distributed dielectric constant of the medium.
For each angle
\theta\in[\underline{\theta},\overline{\theta}],
define

\xi(\theta)=(\cos\theta,\sin\theta,0,\dots,0)\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}

that represents the direction of the angle
\theta
in the
x_{1}x_{2}
-plane.
Here,
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}=\{{\bf x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d},|{\bf x}|=1\}
is the unit sphere in
\mathbb{R}^{d}.

We use the plane wave, so-called the incident plane wave, of the form

(2.1)

v_{0}({\bf x},\theta)=e^{{\rm i}k{\bf x}\cdot\xi(\theta)}\quad\mbox{for all }({\bf x},\theta)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times[\underline{\theta},\overline{\theta}],k>0,

to illuminate the medium.
The incident wave propagates in space and scatters.
The resulting total wave
v({\bf x},\theta)
is governed by the Helmholtz equation

(2.2)

\Delta v({\bf x},\theta)+k^{2}c({\bf x})v({\bf x},\theta)=0\quad\mbox{for all }{\bf x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\theta\in[\underline{\theta},\overline{\theta}]

and the Sommerfeld radiation condition

\partial_{|{\bf x}|}v_{\rm sc}({\bf x},\theta)-{\rm i}kv_{\rm sc}({\bf x},\theta)=o(|{\bf x}|^{\frac{1-d}{2}})

where

(2.3)

v_{\rm sc}({\bf x},\theta)=v({\bf x},\theta)-v_{0}({\bf x},\theta)\quad\mbox{for all }({\bf x},\theta)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times[\underline{\theta},\overline{\theta}]

is the scattering wave.

By (
2.1
), the angle between the
x_{1}
axis and the propagation of incident wave in the
x_{1}x_{2}
plane is
\theta
. Roughly speaking,
\theta
can be considered as the angle of view. Since we consider the case of complete angles of view, we choose
[\underline{\theta},\overline{\theta}]=[0,2\pi].
The study of Problem
1.1
in the case of partial angle of views will be studied later.

The inverse scattering problem is formulated as follows.

Problem 2.1

(The inverse scattering problem)
.

Let
\Omega
be a bounded and open domain of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with smooth boundary.
Given the boundary measurement
v_{\rm sc}({\bf x},\theta)
and
\partial_{\nu}v_{\rm sc}({\bf x},\theta)
for all
({\bf x},\theta)\in\partial\Omega\times[\underline{\theta},\overline{\theta}],

determine the spatially distributed dielectric constant
c({\bf x})
for all
{\bf x}\in\Omega
,

Why is the inverse scattering problem interesting and significant?

According to the formulation of Problem
2.1
, we want to compute the information of the spatially distributed dielectric constant of the medium from the external measurement of the wave field.
The knowledge of the reconstructed spatially distributed dielectric constant of a medium directly provides significant information about unknown objects inside that medium; for e.g, position, shape, size and physical properties. Typical examples of those objects are anti-personnel explosive devices buried under the ground, cancerous tumors inside living tissues, and nano structures.
Therefore, solving the inverse scattering problem has important applications in bio-medical imaging, nondestructive testing, radar, security, optical physics, seismic exploration, nano science.
Hence, the inverse scattering problem and the related ones have been intensively studied.

The current paper contributes to the field by solving the linearization of the inverse scattering problem.
Assume that the dielectric constant
c
is a perturbation of a background function
c_{0}
.
For simplicity, in this section, we choose
c_{0}
to be identically 1.
That means, the function
c
is of the form

(2.4)

c({\bf x})=1+\eta p({\bf x})\quad\mbox{for all }{\bf x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}

where
0<\eta\ll 1
and
p
is a function that indicate the unknown inclusion.
Therefore, the wave function
v
satisfies

(2.5)

\Delta v({\bf x},\theta)+k^{2}(1+\eta p({\bf x}))v({\bf x},\theta)=0\quad\mbox{for all }({\bf x},\theta)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times[\underline{\theta},\overline{\theta}].

It is not hard to verify that

(2.6)

\Delta v_{0}({\bf x},\theta)+k^{2}v_{0}({\bf x},\theta)=0\quad\mbox{for all }({\bf x},\theta)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times[\underline{\theta},\overline{\theta}].

Let
u=v_{\rm sc}/\eta
. It follows from (
2.5
) and (
2.6
) that

(2.7)

\Delta u({\bf x},\theta)+k^{2}u({\bf x},\theta)=-k^{2}p({\bf x})v({\bf x},\theta)\quad\mbox{for all }({\bf x},\theta)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times[\underline{\theta},\overline{\theta}].

It is obvious that
\lim_{\eta\to 0}v({\bf x},\theta)=v_{0}({\bf x},\theta)
. Thus,
since
\eta
is a small number, equation (
2.7
) can be approximated as

(2.8)

\Delta u({\bf x},\theta)+k^{2}u({\bf x},\theta)=-k^{2}p({\bf x})v_{0}({\bf x},\theta)\quad\mbox{for all }({\bf x},\theta)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times[\underline{\theta},\overline{\theta}].

By choosing
u_{0}
as the incident wave
v_{0}
, we derive Problem
1.1
.
While the argument above only give an example for the motivation to solve Problem
1.1
, our method to solve inverse problem is not limited in the context of the inverse scattering problem. That means, the proposed method in this paper can be applied in the general case when
u_{0}
is not necessary to be the incident wave
v_{0}
.

3.
An approximation context

Define

(3.1)

w({\bf x},\theta)=-\frac{u({\bf x},\theta)}{k^{2}u_{0}({\bf x},\theta)}\quad\mbox{or }\quad u({\bf x},\theta)=-k^{2}w({\bf x},\theta)u_{0}({\bf x},\theta)

for all
({\bf x},k)\in\Omega\times[\underline{\theta},\overline{\theta}].

Since
\Delta u({\bf x},\theta)=-k^{2}\Delta[w({\bf x},\theta)u_{0}({\bf x},\theta)]
, we have for all
({\bf x},\theta)\in\Omega\times[\underline{\theta},\overline{\theta}]

(3.2)

\Delta u({\bf x},\theta)=-k^{2}\big[w({\bf x},\theta)\Delta u_{0}({\bf x},\theta)+u_{0}({\bf x},\theta)\Delta w({\bf x},\theta)+2\nabla w({\bf x},\theta)\cdot\nabla u_{0}({\bf x},\theta)\big].

On the other hand,
it follows from the Helmholtz equation in (
1.1
) and the second identity in (
3.1
) that

\displaystyle\Delta u({\bf x},\theta)

\displaystyle=-k^{2}\big[c_{0}({\bf x})u({\bf x},\theta)+p({\bf x})u_{0}({\bf x},\theta)\big]

\displaystyle=-k^{2}\big[-k^{2}c_{0}({\bf x})w({\bf x},\theta)u_{0}({\bf x},\theta)+p({\bf x})u_{0}({\bf x},\theta)\big]

for all
({\bf x},\theta)\in\Omega\times[\underline{\theta},\overline{\theta}].

This and (
3.2
) imply

w({\bf x},\theta)\Delta u_{0}({\bf x},\theta)+u_{0}({\bf x},\theta)\Delta w({\bf x},\theta)+2\nabla w({\bf x},\theta)\cdot\nabla u_{0}({\bf x},\theta)\\
=-k^{2}c_{0}({\bf x})w({\bf x},\theta)u_{0}({\bf x},\theta)+p({\bf x})u_{0}({\bf x},\theta)

or all
({\bf x},\theta)\in\Omega\times[\underline{\theta},\overline{\theta}].

Therefore,

(3.3)

\Delta w({\bf x},\theta)+\Big[\frac{\Delta u_{0}({\bf x},\theta)}{u_{0}({\bf x},\theta)}+k^{2}c_{0}({\bf x})\Big]w({\bf x},\theta)+2\nabla w({\bf x},\theta)\cdot\frac{\nabla u_{0}({\bf x},\theta)}{u_{0}({\bf x},\theta)}=p({\bf x})

for all
({\bf x},\theta)\in\Omega\times[\underline{\theta},\overline{\theta}].

To eliminate the unknown
p
, we differentiate (
3.3
) with respect to
\theta
.
We obtain

(3.4)

\Delta\partial_{\theta}w({\bf x},\theta)+\partial_{\theta}\Big[\frac{\Delta u_{0}({\bf x},\theta)}{u_{0}({\bf x},\theta)}+k^{2}c_{0}({\bf x})\Big]w({\bf x},\theta)\\
+\Big[\frac{\Delta u_{0}({\bf x},\theta)}{u_{0}({\bf x},\theta)}+k^{2}c_{0}({\bf x})\Big]\partial_{\theta}w({\bf x},\theta)+2\nabla\partial_{\theta}w({\bf x},\theta)\cdot\frac{\nabla u_{0}({\bf x},\theta)}{u_{0}({\bf x},\theta)}\\
+2\nabla w({\bf x},\theta)\cdot\partial_{\theta}\Big[\frac{\nabla u_{0}({\bf x},\theta)}{u_{0}({\bf x},\theta)}\Big]=0

for all
({\bf x},\theta)\in\Omega\times[\underline{\theta},\overline{\theta}].

Solving the third order equation (
3.4
) is extremely challenging.
An analytic and numerical approach to solve it is not developed yet.
Suggested by
[
49
]
, we only consider an approximation context due to a truncation of the Fourier series of the solution
w({\bf x},\theta)
with respect to the special basis.
This basis was first introduced in
[
35
]
.
We briefly recall the construction of this basis.
For each
m\geq 1
, define
\phi_{m}(\theta)=(\theta-\theta_{0})^{m-1}\exp(\theta-\theta_{0})

where
\theta_{0}=(\underline{\theta}+\overline{\theta})/2
. The sequence
\{\phi_{m}\}_{m=1}^{\infty}
is complete in
L^{2}(\underline{\theta},\overline{\theta})
.
Applying the Gram-Schmidt orthonormalization procedure to the sequence
\{\phi_{m}\}_{m=1}^{\infty}
, we obtain an orthonormal basis in
L^{2}(\underline{\theta},\overline{\theta}),
denoted by
\{\Psi_{m}\}_{m=1}^{\infty}
. It
is not hard to verify that for each
m,
the function
\Psi_{m}(\theta)
has the
form

\Psi_{m}(\theta)=P_{m-1}(\theta-\theta_{0})\exp(\theta-\theta_{0}),

where
P_{m-1}
is a polynomial of the degree
(m-1)
. The
following result plays an important role in our analysis.

Proposition 3.1

(see
[
35
]
)
.

For
m,n\geq 1
, we have

(3.5)

s_{mn}=\int_{\underline{\theta}}^{\overline{\theta}}\Psi_{m}(\theta)\Psi_{n}^{\prime}(\theta)d\theta=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}1&\mbox{if }n=m,\\
0&\mbox{if }n<m.\end{array}\right.

Consequently, let
N>1
be an integer. Then, the
N\times N
matrix

(3.6)

S=(s_{mn})_{m,n=1}^{N}

has determinant
1
and is invertible.

Recall the function
w
defined in (
3.1
).
For each
({\bf x},\theta)\in\Omega\times[\underline{\theta},\overline{\theta}]
, we write

(3.7)

w({\bf x},\theta)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}w_{n}({\bf x})\Psi_{n}(\theta)\simeq\sum_{n=1}^{N}w_{n}({\bf x})\Psi_{n}(\theta)

where

(3.8)

w_{n}({\bf x})=\int_{\underline{\theta}}^{\overline{\theta}}w({\bf x},\theta)\Psi_{n}(\theta)d\theta,\quad n=1,2,\dots

Remark 3.1

(The choice of
N
)
.

The cut-off number
N
in (
3.7
) is chosen numerically as follows.
For each
N
, we define

\varphi(N)=\Big\|w({\bf x},\theta)-\sum_{n=1}^{N}w_{n}({\bf x})\Psi_{n}(\theta)\Big\|_{L^{\infty}(\Gamma\times[\underline{\theta},\overline{\theta}])}

where
\Gamma
is a subset of
\partial\Omega.

Since the function
u
is given on
\partial\Omega\times[\underline{\theta},\overline{\theta}])
, using (
3.1
) and (
3.8
), we can compute
\varphi(N)
directly. We then choose
N
such that
\varphi(N)
is sufficiently small. In our computation,
N=35
. With this choice of
N
, we have
\varphi(N)<5\times 10^{-3}.

See Figure
1
for an illustration.

From now on, we assume that the approximation in (
3.7
) is valid.
Plugging the truncating formula (
3.7
) into (
3.4
), we obtain

(3.9)

\sum_{n=1}^{N}\Delta w_{n}({\bf x})\Psi_{n}^{\prime}(\theta)+\partial_{\theta}\Big[\frac{\Delta u_{0}({\bf x},\theta)}{u_{0}({\bf x},\theta)}+k^{2}c_{0}({\bf x})\Big]\sum_{n=1}^{N}w_{n}({\bf x})\Psi_{n}(\theta)\\
+\Big[\frac{\Delta u_{0}({\bf x},\theta)}{u_{0}({\bf x},\theta)}+k^{2}c_{0}({\bf x})\Big]\sum_{n=1}^{N}w_{n}({\bf x})\Psi_{n}^{\prime}(\theta)+2\sum_{n=1}^{N}\nabla w_{n}({\bf x})\Psi_{n}^{\prime}(\theta)\cdot\frac{\nabla u_{0}({\bf x},\theta)}{u_{0}({\bf x},\theta)}\\
+2\sum_{n=1}^{N}\nabla w_{n}({\bf x})\Psi_{n}(\theta)\cdot\partial_{\theta}\Big[\frac{\nabla u_{0}({\bf x},\theta)}{u_{0}({\bf x},\theta)}\Big]=0

for all
({\bf x},\theta)\in\Omega\times[\underline{\theta},\overline{\theta}]
.
For each
m\in\{1,\dots,N\},
we multiply
\Psi_{m}(\theta)
to both sides of (
3.9
). Then, we integrate the resulting equation with respect to
\theta
on
[\underline{\theta},\overline{\theta}]
.
We get

(3.10)

\sum_{n=1}^{N}s_{mn}\Delta w_{n}({\bf x})+\sum_{n=1}^{N}a_{mn}({\bf x})w_{n}({\bf x})+\sum_{n=1}^{N}{\bf b}_{mn}({\bf x})\cdot\nabla w_{n}({\bf x})=0

for all
{\bf x}\in\Omega
.
Here,

\displaystyle s_{mn}

\displaystyle=\int_{\underline{\theta}}^{\overline{\theta}}\Psi_{n}^{\prime}(\theta)\Psi_{m}(\theta)d\theta,

\displaystyle a_{mn}({\bf x})

\displaystyle=\int_{\underline{\theta}}^{\overline{\theta}}\Big[\partial_{\theta}\Big(\frac{\Delta u_{0}({\bf x},\theta)}{u_{0}({\bf x},\theta)}+k^{2}c_{0}({\bf x})\Big)\Psi_{n}(\theta)

\displaystyle\hskip 85.35826pt+\Big(\frac{\Delta u_{0}({\bf x},\theta)}{u_{0}({\bf x},\theta)}+k^{2}c_{0}({\bf x})\Big)\Psi_{n}^{\prime}(\theta)\Big]\Psi_{m}(\theta)d\theta,

\displaystyle{\bf b}_{mn}({\bf x})

\displaystyle=2\int_{\underline{\theta}}^{\overline{\theta}}\Big[\frac{\nabla u_{0}({\bf x},\theta)}{u_{0}({\bf x},\theta)}\Psi_{n}^{\prime}(\theta)+\partial_{\theta}\Big(\frac{\nabla u_{0}({\bf x},\theta)}{u_{0}({\bf x},\theta)}\Big)\Psi_{n}(\theta)\Big]\Psi_{m}(\theta)d\theta

for all
m,n\in\{1,\dots,N\}.

Equation (
3.10
), with
m\in\{1,\dots,N\}
, forms a linear system of linear elliptic equations for the vector
W=(w_{n})_{n=1}^{N}.

We next derive the boundary conditions for
W
.
Due to (
1.2
) and (
3.8
), for all
{\bf x}\in\partial\Omega
and
n\in\{1,\dots,N\},
we have

(3.11)

w_{n}({\bf x})=\int_{\underline{\theta}}^{\overline{\theta}}\frac{u({\bf x},\theta)}{-k^{2}u_{0}({\bf x},\theta)}\Psi_{n}(\theta)d\theta=\int_{\underline{\theta}}^{\overline{\theta}}\frac{f({\bf x},\theta)}{-k^{2}u_{0}({\bf x},\theta)}\Psi_{n}(\theta)d\theta

and

\displaystyle\partial_{\nu}w_{n}({\bf x})

\displaystyle=\int_{\underline{\theta}}^{\overline{\theta}}\partial_{\nu}\Big(\frac{u({\bf x},\theta)}{-k^{2}u_{0}({\bf x},\theta)}\Big)\Psi_{n}(\theta)d\theta

\displaystyle=\int_{\underline{\theta}}^{\overline{\theta}}\frac{\partial_{\nu}u({\bf x},\theta)u_{0}({\bf x},\theta)-u({\bf x},\theta)\partial_{\nu}u_{0}({\bf x},\theta)}{-k^{2}u_{0}^{2}({\bf x},\theta)}\Psi_{n}(\theta)d\theta

(3.12)

\displaystyle=\int_{\underline{\theta}}^{\overline{\theta}}\frac{g({\bf x},\theta)u_{0}({\bf x},\theta)-f({\bf x},\theta)\partial_{\nu}u_{0}({\bf x},\theta)}{-k^{2}u_{0}^{2}({\bf x},\theta)}\Psi_{n}(\theta)d\theta.

Due to (
3.10
), (
3.11
) and (
3.12
),
the vector
W
satisfies

(3.13)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\Delta W({\bf x})+S^{-1}A({\bf x})W({\bf x})+S^{-1}{\bf B}({\bf x})\cdot\nabla W({\bf x})=0&{\bf x}\in\Omega,\\
W({\bf x})=F({\bf x})&{\bf x}\in\partial\Omega,\\
\partial_{\nu}W({\bf x})=G({\bf x})&{\bf x}\in\partial\Omega\end{array}\right.

where
S=(s_{mn})_{m,n=1}^{N}
,
A({\bf x})=(a_{mn}({\bf x}))_{m,n=1}^{N}
,
{\bf B}({\bf x})=({\bf b}_{mn}({\bf x}))_{m,n=1}^{N}
and

(3.14)

\displaystyle F({\bf x})

\displaystyle=\displaystyle\Big(\int_{\underline{\theta}}^{\overline{\theta}}\frac{f({\bf x},\theta)}{-k^{2}u_{0}({\bf x},\theta)}\Psi_{n}(\theta)d\theta\Big)_{n=1}^{N},

(3.15)

\displaystyle G({\bf x})

\displaystyle=\displaystyle\Big(\int_{\underline{\theta}}^{\overline{\theta}}\frac{g({\bf x},\theta)u_{0}({\bf x},\theta)-f({\bf x},\theta)\partial_{\nu}u_{0}({\bf x},k)}{-k^{2}u_{0}^{2}({\bf x},\theta)}\Psi_{n}(\theta)d\theta\Big)_{n=1}^{N}.

The invertibility of
S
is an important property of the basis

See Proposition
3.1
and the proof in
[
35
]
.
Using other basis, for e.g the popular trigonometric basis, is not suitable because the corresponding

Solving Problem

satisfying (
3.13
).

w({\bf x},\theta)
for all
({\bf x},\theta)\in\overline{\Omega}\times[\underline{\theta},\overline{\theta}]
via (
3.7
). Then, we can compute the source function
p
by using (
3.3
).
This procedure is summarized in Algorithm
1
.

\{\Psi\}_{n\geq 1}
(see

]
) and choose a cut-off number

as in Remark

.

) by the quasi-reversibility method (see Section

) for a vector value function

The source function is given by

p^{\rm comp}({\bf x})=\frac{-1}{\overline{\theta}-\underline{\theta}}\int_{\underline{\theta}}^{\overline{\theta}}\Big[\Delta w^{\rm comp}({\bf x},\theta)+\Big[\frac{\Delta u_{0}({\bf x},\theta)}{u_{0}({\bf x},\theta)}+k^{2}c_{0}({\bf x})\Big]w^{\rm comp}({\bf x},\theta)\\
+2\nabla w^{\rm comp}({\bf x},\theta)\cdot\frac{\nabla u_{0}({\bf x},\theta)}{u_{0}({\bf x},\theta)}\Big]d\theta

for all

of Algorithm

.

4.
The quasi-reversibility method

For the convenience in the analysis, we consider in this section the boundary data
W=F
and
\partial_{\nu}W=G
respectively as the “indirect data” of Problem
1.1
.
In fact, having the data of the inverse problem, see (
1.2
), in hand, we can use the explicit formulas in (
3.14
) and (
3.15
) to compute
F
and
G
.
Let
\delta>0
be a noise level.
Let
F^{\delta}
and
G^{\delta}
and
F^{*}
and
G^{*}
be the noisy and noiseless versions of
F
and
G
respectively.
The corresponding noiseless versions are denoted by
F^{*}
and
G^{*}
.
By noise, we mean that there exists an “error” function
\mathcal{E}\in H^{2}(\Omega)^{N}
such that

(4.1)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\mathcal{E}|_{\partial\Omega}=F^{\delta}-F^{*},\\
\partial_{\nu}\mathcal{E}|_{\partial\Omega}=G^{\delta}-G^{*},\\
\|\mathcal{E}\|_{H^{2}(\Omega)}\leq\delta.\end{array}\right.

Remark 4.1
.

The existence of the error function in (
4.1
) imply that the noise can be regularized; for e.g.
[
2
, Section 5]
for the noise model such that it can be smooth out. This condition is significant only for the proof of the convergence theorem (see Theorem
4.4
and its proof in
[
49
]
). However, in numerical study, we do not have to smooth the noise.
In fact, we compute the noisy data
F^{\delta}
and
G^{\delta}
using formulas (
3.14
) and(
3.15
) with
f
and
g
replaced by
f^{\delta}
and
f^{\delta}
respectively. Here,

(4.2)

f^{\delta}=f^{*}(1+\delta\mbox{rand}),\quad g^{\delta}=g^{*}(1+\delta\mbox{rand})

where
rand
is a function taking uniformly distributed random numbers in
[-1,1]
and
f^{*}
and
g^{*}
are the noiseless versions of
f
and
g
respectively.

Define the set of admissible solution

(4.3)

H=\big\{V\in H^{2}(\Omega):V|_{\partial\Omega}=F^{\delta}\mbox{and }\partial_{\nu}V|_{\partial\Omega}=G^{\delta}\big\}.

We assume that the set
H\not=\emptyset.

Due to the presence of noise in the given data, problem (
3.13
) is over-determined. It is natural to solve it using the quasi-reversibility method, which is similar to the least-squares optimization together with a Tikhonov regularization term. That means, we minimize the functional

J(W)=\int_{\Omega}\big|\Delta W+S^{-1}A({\bf x})W({\bf x})+S^{-1}{\bf B}({\bf x})\cdot\nabla W({\bf x})\big|^{2}d{\bf x}+\epsilon\|W\|_{H^{2}(\Omega)^{N}}^{2}

subject to the boundary conditions
W|_{\partial\Omega}=F^{\delta}
and
\partial_{\nu}W|_{\partial\Omega}=G^{\delta}
.

Remark 4.2
.

The presence of the Tikhonov regularization term is significant. In the theoretical part, it makes the functional
J
coercive, which is important for the existence of a minimizer of
J
.
In practice, we cannot obtain good numerical result without the presence of this Tikhonov regularization term.
In our numerical study in Section
4
, we choose
\epsilon=10^{-5}
. This value of
\epsilon
was chosen by a trial and error process.
We manually try many values of
\epsilon
for test 1 until we obtain good numerical result. Then, we use this value for all other tests and for several noise levels
\delta\in[5\%,50\%]
.

We have the theorem.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
F^{\delta}
and
G^{\delta}
be the noisy boundary data for
W
. Here,
\delta>0
is the noise level in the sense of (
4.1
). Assume that the set
H
defined in (
4.3
) is nonempty. Then, for any
\epsilon>0
, the functional
J
has a unique minimizer in
H
.
Denote by
W_{\rm min}
the obtained minimizer.
Moreover, let
W^{*}
be true solution to (
3.13
). Then, the following estimate holds true

(4.4)

\|W^{\delta}-W^{*}\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)^{N}}^{2}\leq C\Big(\delta^{2}+\epsilon\|W^{*}\|_{H^{2}(\Omega)^{N}}^{2}\Big).

For brevity, we do not present the proof of Theorem
4.4
. We refer the reader to
[
49
, Theorem 3.1 and Theorem 5.1]
for the proof of this theorem. We also refer to

[
47
, Proposition 4.1 and Theorem 4.1]
for the proof of a similar theorem when
J
involves the boundary integrals of the data.

Remark 4.3
.

The quasi-reversibility method we employ here is based on least squares optimization with a special Tikhonov regularization term.
In general, to solve the over-determined boundary value problem (
3.13
), one can use the least squares optimization method with many other choices for the regularization term. It is worth to study the convergence of the method with each of such choice.

6.
Concluding remarks

In this paper, we solve an inverse source problem.
This inverse source problem is the linearization of the highly nonlinear and severely ill-posed inverse scattering problem.
In order to solve the inverse source problem, in the first step, we derive a system of linear elliptic PDEs in which the source function is absent.
We solve this system by the quasi-reversibility method.
We choose the quasi-reversibility method because its convergence as noise tends to
0
was proved.
The efficiency of our method is confirmed by some interesting numerical examples.
