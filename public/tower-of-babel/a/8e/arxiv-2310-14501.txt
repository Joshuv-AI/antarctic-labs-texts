Introduction

Networks arising in the sciences are often modeled as latent space graphs. Each node in a network has a latent feature vector and the probability of connection between two nodes is a function of the two feature vectors. One instance is the case of (random) geometric graphs in which each feature vector is a (random) element of a metric space and the connection function is determined by the distance between the two vectors. Applications of random geometric graphs include protein-protein interactions and viral spread in the biological sciences

1.1
Main Results for
L_{\infty}
Geometry

The
L_{\infty}
case is special because of the following factorization property over coordinates:
\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\|_{\infty}\leq\tau
holds if and only if
|x_{i}-y_{i}|\leq\tau
holds for each
i\in[d].
This means that each edge
(u,v)
is the
\mathsf{AND}
of
d

independent
edges in the 1-dimensional random geometric graphs over the different coordinates. In comparison,
previously studied
L_{2}
models have a (weighted)
\mathsf{MAJORITY}
combinatorics. For instance, in the spherical case
\langle\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\rangle\geq\rho
if and only if

\sum_{i=1}^{d}|x_{i}|\times|y_{i}|\times\mathrm{sign}(x_{i}y_{i})\geq\rho.
Here, each
\mathrm{sign}(x_{i}y_{i})
is an independent 1-dimensional edge and the values
|x_{i}|\times|y_{i}|
are the corresponding weights.

Factorization over the induced independent 1-dimensional random geometric graphs makes the computation of expected (signed) subgraph counts tractable as computations in one dimension are naturally much simpler (see
3.1
). Signed subgraph counts are fundamental in studying random graph distributions as they are the Fourier coefficients of the probability density.
The factorization property, also utilized in
[
FGK+23a
]
, is the first main ingredient in our results in the
L_{\infty}
case.

The second ingredient is combining the induced 1-dimensional structures via the
\mathsf{AND}
function. While in certain special cases this step is nearly trivial (e.g., in
Theorem

1.3
we only need to do it for
K_{2,t}
subgraphs and in
Theorem

1.4
for triangles and 4-cycles), in full generality it requires a careful analysis of the compatibility of induced 1-dimensional structures. We carry out such an analysis in
Section

3.4
by viewing each 1-dimensional structure as a polymer and expanding the product over the
d
coordinates. A rearrangement of terms yields a tremendous amount of cancellations that leaves us with an expression for the expected signed subgraph counts similar to the celebrated cluster expansion formula (e.g.,
[
MM40
,
KP86
,
FV17
]
) from statistical physics (which has found many other applications in combinatorics, e.g.
[
SS05
]
). In our case, the compatibility criterion is given by the size of the overlap of different 1-dimensional structures. What makes a cluster-expansion-like formula appealing is a rapid decay of terms which means that terms corresponding to small clusters determine its asymptotics (as in the Koteckỳ-Preiss theorem
[
KP86
]
). The derivation and analysis of this formula is our technical and conceptual highlight in the
L_{\infty}
case.

\mathbf{x}^{3}
is adjacent to
\mathbf{x}^{1}
and
\mathbf{x}^{2_{a}}
if and only if
(\mathbf{x}^{3})_{1}\not\in(-\lambda,\lambda)\cup(1-\lambda,1+\lambda),
and

(\mathbf{x}^{3})_{i}\not\in(1-\lambda,1+\lambda)
for
i\in\{2,3,\ldots,d\}.
In contrast,
\mathbf{x}^{1}

and
\mathbf{x}^{2_{b}}
are adjacent and only at distance
1/2
, but the set of latent vectors adjacent to
\mathbf{x}^{1},\mathbf{x}^{2_{b}}
has the much smaller measure
(1-2\lambda)^{d}=\frac{1}{4}(1+o(1)).
A point
\mathbf{x}^{3}
is adjacent to
\mathbf{x}^{1}
and
\mathbf{x}^{2_{b}}
if and only if
(\mathbf{x}^{3})_{i}\not\in(\frac{1}{2}-\lambda,\frac{1}{2}+\lambda)\cup(1-\lambda,1+\lambda)\;\forall i.
This lack of correlation causes (signed) triangle counts to be suboptimal.

The 4-cycle statistic on cycle
\{1,3,2,4\}
measures the correlation between two-step paths 1–3–2 and 1–4–2 from 1 to 2. This statistic does not suffer from the same issue as signed triangle counts because it measures the correlation between two objects of the same type.

In
Section

3.3
, we see yet another reason why bipartite subgraph tests are more informative in the
L_{\infty}
model. It has to do with the fact that all short cycles in the complements of the induced 1-dimensional random geometric graphs are of even length.

Finally, we show that the advantage of counting signed four cycles over counting signed triangles in
\mathsf{RGG}(n,\mathbb{T}^{d},\mathsf{Unif},\sigma^{\infty}_{p},p)
extends beyond the task of distinguishing from Erdős-Rényi . The existing literature on dimension estimation is fully focused on triangle-based statistics
[
BDE+14
,
AM22
,
FGK+23
]
. Not much is known about the optimality of these statistics beyond the case of
L_{2}
geometry. We show that indeed, the simple signed 4-cycle counting test is stronger than the signed triangle test also for the problem of estimating the dimension in
\mathsf{RGG}(n,\mathbb{T}^{d},\mathsf{Unif},\sigma^{\infty}_{p},p).
Specifically, we consider the following problem.

Problem 1.8
.

On input
n,p
and
\mathbf{G},
where
\mathbf{G}\sim\mathsf{RGG}(n,\mathbb{T}^{d},\mathsf{Unif},\sigma^{\infty}_{p},p),
find the unknown dimension
d
exactly with high probability.

Of course, one can also consider variants of this problem, such as when the expected density
p
is unknown or when one allows for a small error in estimating
d.
We focus on this simplest version as our goal is to demonstrate the advantage of counting signed four-cycles over counting signed three-cycles. The precise statement is given in
Section

3.5.2
. For now, we say that our formal notion of success of exact recovery of dimension via polynomial tests (given in
Definition

3.15
) exactly captures prior work on the problem
[
BDE+14
,
FGK+23
]
and it mimics the more common framework of hypothesis testing using low-degree polynomial tests (for example,
[
Hop18
]
).

Proposition 1.9

(Informal, Simple Tests for Dimension Estimation)
.

Consider
1.8
under assumption
Eq.

A
with known value of
\delta
such that
d\geq n^{\delta}.

1.

The signed 4-cycle statistic recovers the dimension
d
correctly w.h.p. if and only if
d=\tilde{o}((np)^{2/3}).

2.

The signed triangle statistic recovers the dimension
d
correctly w.h.p. if and only if
d=\tilde{o}((np)^{1/2}).

It is important to note that
Proposition

1.9
holds under the assumption

requiring

and

The setting of
[

1.2
Additional Results

1.2.1
L_{q}
Geometry for
q<\infty

So far, we have shown that random geometric graphs with
L_{\infty}
geometry behave qualitatively and quantitatively differently from
L_{2}
models with respect to
Eq.

1
. This motivates the question of understanding
Eq.

1
under other geometries as well, in particular
L_{q}.

The choice of latent space
(\mathbb{T}^{d},\mathsf{Unif})
for comparison of random geometric graphs with
L_{q}
geometries is natural. A large class of natural symmetries of
\mathbb{T}^{d}
such as coordinate permutations and translations (
\mathbf{x}\longrightarrow\mathbf{c}+\mathbf{x}
for a fixed
\mathbf{c}
) are isometries for any
L_{q}
metric: there exists a transitive group of isometries for any
L_{q}
metric over
\mathbb{T}^{d}
that is also measure-preserving. This leads to the following desirable
homogeneity
property: for any fixed
x\in\mathbb{T}^{d}
and
\mathbf{y}\sim\mathsf{Unif}(\mathbb{T}^{d}),
the distance
\|x-\mathbf{y}\|_{q}
(hence,
\sigma(x,\mathbf{y})
) has the same distribution.

The analysis of
L_{q}
models, however, turns out to be much more difficult when
q<\infty
as the factorization over 1-dimensional random geometric graphs does not hold any longer. In particular, this makes the computation of signed subgraph counts much more difficult and we have not succeeded to perform such a computation even for triangles.

One special case in which we manage to bound the signed subgraph count is the case of bipartite graphs
K_{2,t},
which is enough to prove an analogue of
Theorem

1.3
. What makes this calculation simpler is that the signed expectation of
K_{2,t}
has a very natural interpretation as the
t
-th centered moment of the self-convolution of
\sigma^{q}_{1/2}.
Using the Bernstein-McDiarmid inequality (see
2.2
), we bound the centered moments of
\sigma
by revealing the
d
coordinates one at a time. The technical highlight of this argument is proving that each coordinate (say
x_{d}
) is marginally nearly uniform on
\mathbb{T}^{1}
even conditioned on the value of
\sigma^{q}_{1/2}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})
when
q\ll d.
The reason for this phenomenon is that the the contribution of the remaining
d-1
coordinates, i.e.
\sum_{i=1}^{d-1}|x_{i}-y_{i}|_{C}^{q},
is sufficiently anticoncentrated and, thus, there are no spikes in its distribution that would bias
x_{d}
strongly when conditioning on
\sigma^{q}_{1/2}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}).
The formal statement is given in
5.1
and we prove it by adapting an anticoncentration inequality of Bobkov and Chistyakov
[
BC14
]
to random variables with unbounded density (see
Appendix

D
).

In our analogue of
Theorem

1.3
,
we fix
p=1/2
and vary
q
so that we obtain a meaningful comparison of the convergence to Erdős-Rényi for different geometries.

Theorem 1.10
.

Suppose that
q\geq 1.

1.

If
q=o(d/\log d),
then

{\mathsf{TV}}\Big(\mathsf{RAG}(n,\mathbb{T}^{d},\sigma^{q}_{1/2},1/2),\mathsf{G}(n,1/2)\Big)=o(1)
whenever
dq=\tilde{\omega}(n^{3}).

2.

If
q=\Omega(d/\log d),
then

{\mathsf{TV}}\Big(\mathsf{RAG}(n,\mathbb{T}^{d},\sigma^{q}_{1/2},1/2),\mathsf{G}(n,1/2)\Big)=o(1)
whenever
d^{2}=\tilde{\omega}(n^{3}).

This statement interpolates between known results for
L_{2}
models where convergence to Erdős-Rényi occurs when
d=\tilde{\omega}(n^{3})
(for example, in the spherical case
[
BDE+14
]
) and
L_{q}
models when convergence occurs for
d^{2}=\tilde{\omega}(n^{3})
(see
Theorem

1.3
).

As already mentioned, we did not manage to prove algorithmic upper bounds for distinguishing general
L_{q}
geometry. We present some minimal progress and conjectures in
Appendix

A
, based on a Fourier-analytic interpretation of signed subgraph counts similar to
[
BB23
, Observation 2.1]
.
We can only rigorously show the following entropy-based upper bound which, however, does not obviously lead to a computationally efficient test.

Theorem 1.11
.

Take any
q\in[1,+\infty]
and any
p
such that
1/2\geq p\geq 1/n.
If
d=o(np/\log n),
then

{\mathsf{TV}}\Big(\mathsf{RGG}(n,\mathbb{T}^{d},\mathsf{Unif},\sigma^{\infty}_{p},p),\mathsf{G}(n,p)\Big)=1-o(1).

Interestingly, this gives the same bound as the signed 4-cycle test in the case of
L_{\infty}
geometry (in
Theorem

1.4
).

1.2.2
Random Algebraic Graphs

What makes the Bernstein-McDiarmid analysis feasible in the case of
Theorem

1.10
is that the coordinates of
\mathbb{T}^{d}
are independent. It turns out that the method can be extended to other cases of a product structure.

Definition 1.12

(Random Algebraic Graph over an Abelian Group
[
BB23
]
)
.

Suppose that
\mathcal{G}
is a finite Abelian group or a finite-dimensional torus
\mathbb{T}^{d}.
Let
\mathsf{Unif}
be the uniform measure
8
8

8

That is, the Haar measure in the case of
\mathbb{T}^{d}.
over
\mathcal{G}
and let
\sigma:\mathcal{G}\longrightarrow[0,1]
be a measurable function such that
\sigma(\mathbf{g})=\sigma(-\mathbf{g})
holds a.s. and
{\mathbf{E}}_{\mathbf{g}\sim\mathsf{Unif}(\mathcal{G})}[\sigma(\mathbf{g})]=p.
Then, the random algebraic graph
\mathsf{RAG}(n,\mathcal{G},\sigma,p)
is a random graph over vertex set
[n]
with distribution of its adjacency matrix
\mathbf{A}
given by

{\mathbf{P}}[\mathbf{A}=G]={\mathbf{E}}_{\mathbf{x}^{1},\mathbf{x}^{2},\ldots,\mathbf{x}^{n}\mathrel{{\mathop{\sim}\limits}}\mathsf{Unif}(\mathcal{G})}\Bigg[\prod_{i<j}\sigma(\mathbf{x}^{i}-\mathbf{x}^{j})^{G_{ij}}(1-\sigma(\mathbf{x}^{i}-\mathbf{x}^{j}))^{1-G_{ij}}\Bigg].

(2)

For any choice of
n,d,q,p,
the random geometric graph
\mathsf{RGG}(n,\mathbb{T}^{d},\mathsf{Unif},\sigma^{q}_{p},p)
is also a random algebraic graph under the choice
\mathcal{G}=\mathbb{T}^{d}
and
\sigma(\mathbf{g})=\mathds{1}[\|\mathbf{g}\|_{q}\leq\tau^{q}_{p}].
Overloading notation, we will also use
\sigma^{q}_{p}
as one function-argument, that is
\sigma^{q}_{p}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})=\sigma^{q}_{p}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}).

In
[
BB23
]
, the authors study random algebraic graphs over
\{\pm 1\}^{d}
with general connections
\sigma.
They derive a general criterion based on the sizes of Fourier coefficients on each level that guarantee
{\mathsf{TV}}\Big(\mathsf{RAG}(n,\{\pm 1\}^{d},\sigma,p),\mathsf{G}(n,p)\Big)=o(1)
in
[
BB23
, Theorem 3.1.]
. Using a much simpler argument, based on the combination of
Eq.

4
and Bernstein’s inequality, we also recover such a criterion.

Theorem 1.13
.

Suppose that
\sigma:\{\pm 1\}^{d}\longrightarrow[0,1]
is a connection with expectation
p.
Then,

{\mathsf{TV}}\Big(\mathsf{RAG}(n,\{\pm 1\}^{d},\sigma,p),\mathsf{G}(n,p)\Big)^{2}=O\Bigg(\frac{n^{3}\sum_{i=1}^{d}{\mathbf{Inf}}_{i}[\sigma]^{2}}{p^{2}(1-p)^{2}}\Bigg).

A detailed comparison between
Theorem

1.13
and
[
BB23
, Theorem 3.1.]
is provided in
Appendix

B
. For now, we simply show two very quick applications of the theorem.

Corollary 1.14
.

{\mathsf{TV}}\Big(\mathsf{RAG}(n,\{\pm 1\}^{d},\sigma,p),\mathsf{G}(n,p)\Big)=o(1)
in the following cases:

1.

If
\sigma
is
\frac{1}{r\sqrt{d}}
-Lipschitz and
d=o\big(\frac{n^{3}}{p^{2}r^{4}}\big).

2.

If
\sigma(\mathbf{g})=\mathds{1}\Big[\sum_{i=1}^{d}g_{i}\geq\tau^{\{\pm 1\}^{d}}_{p}\Big],
where
\tau^{\{\pm 1\}^{d}}_{p}
is defined so that
{\mathbf{E}}[\sigma]=p,
and

d=\tilde{\omega}(n^{3}p^{2}).

Proof.

For the first statement, observe that whenever
\sigma
is
(r\sqrt{d})^{-1}
-Lipschitz, by the definition of influence,

{\mathbf{Inf}}_{i}[\sigma]={\mathbf{E}}_{\mathbf{x}\sim\mathsf{Unif}(\{\pm 1\}^{d})}\Bigg[\Big(\frac{\sigma(\mathbf{x})-\sigma(\mathbf{x}^{\oplus i})}{2}\Big)^{2}\Bigg]\leq\frac{1}{r^{2}d},

where
\mathbf{x}^{\oplus i}
denotes the vector
\mathbf{x}
with the
i
-th coordinate flipped. We used

|\sigma(\mathbf{x})-\sigma(\mathbf{x}^{\oplus i})|\leq\frac{2}{r\sqrt{d}}
which follows directly from the Lipschitzness assumption.
The conclusion follows from
Theorem

1.13
.

For the second statement, again consider

\displaystyle{\mathbf{Inf}}_{i}[\sigma]
.
The expression
\sigma(\mathbf{x})-\sigma(\mathbf{x}^{\oplus i})
is non-zero only if
\mathbf{x}
has
\frac{d+\tau_{p}}{2}
or
\frac{d+\tau_{p}}{2}-1
ones. A simple calculation (carried out, for example, in
[
BB23
, Proof of Proposition 4.7]
)
shows that the probability of this happening is
O(p\sqrt{\log\frac{1}{p}}/{\sqrt{d}}).
This means that each influence is of order
\tilde{O}(p^{2}/d)
and the conclusion follows.
∎

2.1
Statistical Detection of Latent Space Structure

Information Theory.

We use the standard notions for Total Variation and KL-distance (for example,
[
PWng
]
). Specifically, for two distributions
\mathbf{P},\mathbf{Q}
over the same measurable spaces
(\Omega,\mathcal{F})
, such that
\mathbf{P}
is absolutely continuous with respect to
\mathbf{Q},

\begin{split}&{\mathsf{TV}}(\mathbf{P},\mathbf{Q})=\sup_{A\in\mathcal{F}}|\mathbf{P}(A)-\mathbf{Q}(A)|=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}\Big|\frac{d\mathbf{P}(\omega)}{d\mathbf{Q}(\omega)}-1\Big|d\mathbf{Q}(\omega),\\
&{\mathsf{KL}}(\mathbf{P}\|\mathbf{Q})=\int_{\Omega}\frac{d\mathbf{P}(\omega)}{d\mathbf{Q}(\omega)}\log\frac{d\mathbf{P}(\omega)}{d\mathbf{Q}(\omega)}d\mathbf{Q}(\omega).\end{split}

(3)

Total variation appears naturally in hypothesis testing settings as
1-{\mathsf{TV}}(\mathbf{P},\mathbf{Q})
is the minimal sum of Type I and Type II errors when testing between
\mathbf{P}
and
\mathbf{Q}
with a single sample (e.g.
[
PWng
]
). In practice, it is usually more convenient to work and compute with
{\mathsf{KL}}.
Importantly, this is enough for proving convergence in total variation due to the celebrated inequality of Pinsker stating that

{\mathsf{TV}}(\mathbf{P},\mathbf{Q})^{2}\leq\frac{1}{2}{\mathsf{KL}}(\mathbf{P},\mathbf{Q}).

A Bound on the KL divergence due to Liu and Racz.

In
[
LR21a
]
, the authors give the following convenient bound on the
{\mathsf{KL}}
divergence between
\mathsf{G}(n,p)
and a probabilistic latent space graph. Specialized to random algebraic graphs (which encompass graphs
\mathsf{RGG}(n,\mathbb{T}^{d},\mathsf{Unif},\sigma^{q}_{p},p)
), their bound reads as follows:

\begin{split}&{\mathsf{KL}}\Big(\mathsf{RAG}(n,\mathcal{G},\sigma,p)\|\mathsf{G}(n,p)\Big)\leq\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\log\Big({\mathbf{E}}_{\mathbf{x}\sim\mathsf{Unif}{(\mathcal{G})}}\bigg[\Big(1+\frac{\gamma(\mathbf{x})}{p(1-p)}\Big)^{k}\bigg]\Big),\\
&\text{where }\gamma(\mathbf{x})\coloneqq{\mathbf{E}}_{\mathbf{z}\sim\mathsf{Unif}{\mathcal{G}}}\bigg[(\sigma(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{z})-p)(\sigma(\mathbf{z})-p)\bigg]={\mathbf{E}}_{\mathbf{z}\sim\mathsf{Unif}{\mathcal{G}}}\bigg[\sigma(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{z})\sigma(\mathbf{z})\bigg]-p^{2}.\end{split}

(4)

Over random algebraic graphs,
\gamma(\mathbf{x})=\sigma*\sigma(\mathbf{x})-p^{2},
where
\sigma*\sigma(\mathbf{x}):={\mathbf{E}}_{\mathbf{z}\sim\mathsf{Unif}{\mathcal{G}}}\bigg[\sigma(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{z})\sigma(\mathbf{z})\bigg]

is the self-convolution. Thus, one can expand the left hand-side of
Eq.

4
either in terms of the moments of
\sigma*\sigma
or in terms of the moments of
\sigma*\sigma-p^{2}.

It turns out that in the case of
\mathsf{RGG}(n,\mathbb{T}^{d},\mathsf{Unif},\sigma^{\infty}_{p},p),
one can easily compute (up to lower-order terms) the moments of
\sigma*\sigma
and this is enough to prove

Theorem

1.3
.

Remark 2.1
.

In
Appendix

C
, we discuss two combinatorial interpretations of
Eq.

4
which connect the bound of Liu and Racz to different notions of pseudorandomness appearing in the literature.
One is related to the recent break-through work of Kelly and Meka on 3-term arithmetic progressions
[
KM23
]
and the other to the classic work of Chung-Graham-Wilson on quasi-random graphs
[
CGW88
]
. To the best of our knowledge, these interpretations were not known to Liu and Racz.

The Bernstein-McDiarmid Approach.

In the case of
L_{q}
geometry for
q<\infty,
calculating the moments of
\sigma*\sigma
seems out of reach. Our proof of
Theorem

1.10
instead exploits the product structure of
\mathbb{T}^{d}
to bound the moments of
\gamma
via the Bernstein-McDiarmid inequality.

Claim 2.2

(
[
Han
, Corollary 5.6 and Problem 5.2]
)
.

Let
\mathbf{g}_{1},\mathbf{g}_{2},\ldots,\mathbf{g}_{d}
be independent random variables and
\gamma
a function of
(\mathbf{g}_{1},\mathbf{g}_{2},\ldots,\mathbf{g}_{d}).

Denote
\mathbf{g}_{-i}\coloneqq(\mathbf{g}_{1},\mathbf{g}_{2},\ldots,\mathbf{g}_{i-1},\mathbf{g}_{i+1},\ldots,\mathbf{g}_{d})
and

\begin{split}&D_{i}\gamma(\mathbf{g}_{-i})\coloneqq\sup_{\mathbf{g}^{+}_{i}}\gamma((\mathbf{g}_{1},\mathbf{g}_{2},\ldots,\mathbf{g}_{i-1},\mathbf{g}_{i}^{+},\mathbf{g}_{i+1},\ldots,\mathbf{g}_{d}))-\inf_{\mathbf{g}^{-}_{i}}\gamma((\mathbf{g}_{1},\mathbf{g}_{2},\ldots,\mathbf{g}_{i-1},\mathbf{g}_{i}^{-},\mathbf{g}_{i+1},\ldots,\mathbf{g}_{d})),\\
&{\mathbf{Var}}_{i}[\gamma(\mathbf{g}_{-i})]\coloneqq{\mathbf{Var}}_{\mathbf{g}_{i}}[\gamma((\mathbf{g}_{1},\mathbf{g}_{2},\ldots,\mathbf{g}_{i-1},\mathbf{g}_{i},\mathbf{g}_{i+1},\ldots,\mathbf{g}_{d}))|\mathbf{g}_{-i}].\end{split}

Then, for any positive

{\mathbf{P}}\bigg[\gamma(\mathbf{g})\geq t+{\mathbf{E}}[\gamma(\mathbf{g})]\bigg]\leq\exp\Bigg(-\min\bigg(\frac{t^{2}}{4\sum_{j=1}^{d}\|{\mathbf{Var}}_{i}[\gamma]\|_{\infty}},\frac{t}{2\max_{i}\|D_{i}\gamma\|_{\infty}}\bigg)\Bigg).

Furthermore, for some absolute constant
C,

\|\gamma\|_{k}\leq C\Bigg(\sqrt{k}\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{d}\|{\mathbf{Var}}_{i}[\gamma]\|_{\infty}}+k\max_{i}\|D_{i}f\|_{\infty}\Bigg).

We bound
{\mathbf{Var}}_{i}[\sigma],D_{i}[\gamma]
for
\gamma
defined as in
Eq.

4
via a careful combination of Fourier-theoretic and anticoncentration arguments to obtain
Theorem

1.10
. We also derive
Theorem

1.13
as a combination of
Eq.

4
and
2.2
.

2.2
Computational Detection of Latent Space Structure

To solve
Eq.

1
, one observes a certain
n
-vertex graph
G
and needs to compute a function
f(G)
based on which to decide between
H_{0}
and
H_{1}.
The graph
G
is simply a sequence of
\binom{n}{2}
bits. It is well-known that any function of
0/1
vectors is simply a polynomial
[
ODo14
]
. For computationally efficient tests, one needs to be able to compute
f
in time polynomial in
n.

Signed Subgraph Counts.

Most important to the current paper are polynomials corresponding to signed-subgraph counts. Namely, suppose that we want to test between two graph distributions over
n
vertices in which each edge appears with a marginal probability
p.
Let
H=\{(i_{1},j_{1}),(i_{2},j_{2}),\ldots,(i_{k},j_{k})\}
be any subgraph of
K_{n}.
Then, we define the signed weight of
H
as the polynomial

\mathsf{SW}_{H}(G)\coloneqq\sum_{(ij)\in E(H)}(G_{ij}-p).

(5)

For brevity and uniformity with the
\mathsf{SW}
notation, for a set of edges
H=\{(i_{1},j_{1}),(i_{2},j_{2}),\ldots,(i_{k},j_{k})\},
denote

\mathsf{W}_{H}(G)=\prod_{(ij)\in H}G_{ij}=\mathds{1}[G_{ij}=1\;\forall(ij)\in H].

Respectively, the signed count of
H
in
\mathbf{G}
is

\mathsf{SC}_{H}(G)=\sum_{H_{1}\subseteq E(K_{n})\;:H_{1}\sim H}\mathsf{SW}_{H_{1}}(G),

(6)

where the sum is over all subgraphs of
K_{n}
isomorphic to
H.
Note that whenever
H
has a constant number of edges, the polynomial
\mathsf{SC}_{H}(G)
is certainly efficiently computable.

Clearly
{\mathbf{E}}_{\mathbf{G}\sim\mathsf{G}(n,p)}\mathsf{SC}_{H}(\mathbf{G})=0,
which leads to the following approach to
Eq.

1
appearing in
[
BDE+14
]
. Upon observing
G,
compute
\mathsf{SC}_{H}(G)
and, if sufficiently close to 0, report
H_{0}.
Else report
H_{1}.
Using Chebyshev’s inequality, this can be formalized as follows.

Definition 2.3

(Success of the Signed Subgraph Count )
.

We say that signed
H
-count statistical test
\mathsf{SC}_{H}(G)
succeeds in distinguishing between
\mathsf{G}(n,p)
and
\mathsf{RGG}
if

\big|{\mathbf{E}}_{\mathbf{G}\sim\mathsf{RGG}}\big[\mathsf{SC}_{H}(\mathbf{G})\big]\big|=\omega\big(\sqrt{{\mathbf{Var}}_{\mathbf{K}\sim\mathsf{G}(n,p)}\big[\mathsf{SC}_{H}(\mathbf{K})\big]+{\mathbf{Var}}_{\mathbf{G}\sim\mathsf{RGG}}\big[\mathsf{SC}_{H}(\mathbf{G})\big]}\big).

(7)

Indeed, if this is the case, one can solve
Eq.

1
with Type I and Type II errors both of order
o(1)
by comparing
\mathsf{SC}_{H}(G)
to
\frac{1}{2}{\mathbf{E}}_{\mathbf{G}\sim\mathsf{RGG}}\Big[\mathsf{SC}_{H}(\mathbf{G})\Big].

If, on the other hand,

\big|{\mathbf{E}}_{\mathbf{G}\sim\mathsf{RGG}}\big[\mathsf{SC}_{H}(\mathbf{G})\big]\big|=o\big(\sqrt{{\mathbf{Var}}_{\mathbf{K}\sim\mathsf{G}(n,p)}\big[\mathsf{SC}_{H}(\mathbf{K})\big]+{\mathbf{Var}}_{\mathbf{G}\sim\mathsf{RGG}}\big[\mathsf{SC}_{H}(\mathbf{G})\big]}\big),

(8)

we say that the signed
H
-count statistical test fails with high probability.

In this work, we are mostly interested in the case of triangles,
H=C_{3},
and 4-cycles,
H=C_{4}.

Low-Degree Tests.

In
Definition

2.3
, one can replace
\mathsf{SW}_{H}(\cdot)
with any polynomial
f(\cdot)
and compare

\big|{\mathbf{E}}_{\mathbf{G}\sim\mathsf{RGG}}\big[f(\mathbf{G})\big]-{\mathbf{E}}_{\mathbf{G}\sim\mathsf{G}(n,p)}\big[f(\mathbf{G})\big]\big|\text{ and }\sqrt{{\mathbf{Var}}_{\mathbf{K}\sim\mathsf{G}(n,p)}\big[f(\mathbf{K})\big]+{\mathbf{Var}}_{\mathbf{G}\sim\mathsf{RGG}}\big[f(\mathbf{G})\big]}.

High-probability success and failure are similarly defined.

A popular conjecture
[
Hop18
]
states that all polynomial time algorithms for solving (sufficiently noisy) hypothesis testing questions in high-dimension are captured by polynomials of degree
O(\log n).
Indeed, there is growing evidence in support of this conjecture. Clearly, low degree polynomial tests capture (signed) counts of small subgraphs (note that one can even capture the first
O((\log n)/k)
moments of the (signed) counts
of a graph
H
with
k
edges), which have proven powerful in detecting random geometric graphs
[
BDE+14
]
, planted cliques and colorings
[
KVW+23
]
, the number of communities in a stochastic block model
[
RSW+22
]
and others.
Low-degree polynomials further capture spectral methods
[
KWB19
]
,
constant round approximate message passing algorithms
[
MW22
]
, and statistical query algorithms
[
BBH+21
]
. Thus, a lot of recent work in high-dimensional statistics has focused on ruling out low-degree polynomial algorithms for statistical problems. This constitutes strong evidence that the respective statistical problems cannot be solved in polynomial time.

Formally, in the case of
Eq.

1
one needs to show that there exists some function
D(n)=\omega(\log n)
such that for all degree
D=D(n)
polynomials
f,
it is the case that

\big|{\mathbf{E}}_{\mathbf{G}\sim\mathsf{RGG}}\big[f(\mathbf{G})\big]-{\mathbf{E}}_{\mathbf{G}\sim\mathsf{G}(n,p)}\big[f(\mathbf{G})\big]\big|=o\big(\sqrt{{\mathbf{Var}}_{\mathbf{K}\sim\mathsf{G}(n,p)}\big[f(\mathbf{K})\big]+{\mathbf{Var}}_{\mathbf{G}\sim\mathsf{RGG}}\big[f(\mathbf{G})\big]}\big).

One way to prove such an inequality is by bounding the following quantity
[
Hop18
]
:

\mathsf{ADV}_{\leq D}:=\max_{f\;:\;deg(f)\leq D}\frac{{\mathbf{E}}_{\mathbf{G}\sim\mathsf{RGG}}\big[f(\mathbf{G})\big]}{\sqrt{{\mathbf{E}}_{\mathbf{K}\sim\mathsf{G}(n,p)}\big[f(\mathbf{K})^{2}\big]}}\,.

(9)

In particular, if
\mathsf{ADV}_{\leq D}=1+o(1),
then statistical test
f(\cdot)
fails with large probability (e.g.
[
RSW+22
]
).

It turns out that the product structure of
\mathsf{G}(n,p)
yields a convenient formula for
\mathsf{ADV}_{\leq D}.

The set of polynomials
\{\mathsf{SW}_{H}\times(p(1-p))^{-|E(H)|/2}\}_{H\subseteq E(K_{n})\;0\leq|E(H)|\leq D}
forms an orthonormal basis of the polynomials of degree up to
D
with respect to
\mathsf{G}(n,p).
A standard application of the Cauchy-Schwartz inequality (e.g.
[
Hop18
]
) shows that

\mathsf{ADV}^{2}_{\leq D}-1=\sum_{H\subseteq E(K_{n})\;1\leq|E(H)|\leq D}{\mathbf{E}}_{\mathbf{G}\sim\mathsf{RGG}}\big[\mathsf{SW}_{H}\times(p(1-p))^{-|E(H)|/2}\big]^{2}.

We summarize in the following proposition.

Proposition 2.4
.

If there exists some
D=\omega(\log n)
such that

\sum_{H\subseteq E(K_{n})\;1\leq|E(H)|\leq D}{\mathbf{E}}_{\mathbf{G}\sim\mathsf{RGG}}\big[\mathsf{SW}_{H}\times(p(1-p))^{-|E(H)|/2}\big]^{2}=o(1),

then the Type I plus Type II error of any degree
D
polynomial in solving
Eq.

1
is of order
1-o(1).

We use the bounds from
Proposition

1.6
and this proposition to prove
Theorem

1.5
. We note that low-degree polynomials are similarly used in the literature for estimation and refutation tasks (e.g.
[
SW22
,
RSW+22
]
). We discuss this in more detail in
Section

3.5.2
in the context of estimating the dimension of a graph sampled from
\mathsf{RGG}(n,\mathbb{T}^{d},\mathsf{Unif},\sigma^{\infty}_{p},p).

3.1
The Cluster-Expansion Approach to Bounding Expected (Signed) Weights

Recall Assumption (
A
).
Note that
\tau^{\infty}_{p}
satisfies

(\tau^{\infty}_{p})^{d}=p.
Indeed, this is the case since
p={\mathbf{P}}[\|\mathbf{x}\|_{\infty}\leq\tau^{\infty}_{p}]={\mathbf{P}}[|x_{1}|_{C}\leq\tau^{\infty}_{p}]^{d}.
This immediately implies that
\tau^{\infty}_{p}=1-\lambda_{p}^{\infty},
where
\lambda_{p}^{\infty}=\frac{\log(1/p)}{d}(1+o(1)).
We will write
\sigma,\lambda,\tau
instead of
\sigma_{p}^{\infty},\lambda_{p}^{\infty},\tau_{p}^{\infty}
for brevity.

Fix some subgraph
H\subseteq K_{n}
defined by edges
e_{1},e_{2},\ldots,e_{k}.
We want to understand
{\mathbf{E}}_{\mathbf{G}\sim\mathsf{RGG}(n,\mathbb{T}^{d},\mathsf{Unif},\sigma^{\infty}_{p},p)}[\mathsf{SW}_{H}(\mathbf{G})]
and
{\mathbf{E}}_{\mathbf{G}\sim\mathsf{RGG}(n,\mathbb{T}^{d},\mathsf{Unif},\sigma^{\infty}_{p},p)}[\mathsf{W}_{H}(\mathbf{G})].
We will describe how to utilize the
AND
structure of
L_{\infty}
random geometric graphs, described in the introduction, towards this goal. This is done in several steps, which can be similarly applied in other instances of
AND
structure (see
Section

8
).

Step 1: Factorizing Expected Weights over Independent Coordinates.

The main reason that the analysis over the
L_{\infty}
model is simple is that the different coordinates factorize. Namely,
e_{\ell}=(i_{\ell},j_{\ell})
is an edge if and only if
|x_{u}^{i_{\ell}}-x_{u}^{j_{\ell}}|_{C}\leq 1-\lambda
for each coordinate
u\in[d].
Using the independence of coordinates under the distribution
\mathsf{Unif}(\mathbb{T}^{d}),

{\mathbf{E}}_{\mathbf{G}\sim\mathsf{RGG}(n,\mathbb{T}^{d},\mathsf{Unif},\sigma^{\infty}_{p},p)}[\mathsf{W}_{H}(\mathbf{G})]={\mathbf{E}}_{\mathbf{G}\sim\mathsf{RGG}(n,\mathbb{T}^{1},\mathsf{Unif},\sigma^{\infty}_{1-\lambda},1-\lambda)}[\mathsf{W}_{H}(\mathbf{G})]^{d}.

(10)

Step 2: Computations Over a Single Coordinate via Inclusion-Exclusion.

It turns out that computing the one-dimensional quantities over the graph complement
\overline{\mathbf{G}}
is simpler than computing them over
\mathbf{G}.
The intuitive reason is that in the complement each edge appears only with very low probability
\lambda=\tilde{\Theta}(1/d).
In other words, the appearance of an edge is a very restrictive event that largely determines the configuration of latent vectors. Concretely,
for a set of edges
A,
denote by
\chi(A)
the probability that no edge of
A
appears in
\mathbf{G}\sim\mathsf{RGG}(n,\mathbb{T}^{1},\mathsf{Unif},\sigma^{\infty}_{1-\lambda},1-\lambda)
, i.e.,

\chi(A):={\mathbf{P}}_{\mathbf{G}\sim\mathsf{RGG}(n,\mathbb{T}^{1},\mathsf{Unif},\sigma^{\infty}_{1-\lambda},1-\lambda)}[G_{ij}=0\text{ for all }ij\in A]\,.

(11)

Equivalently,
\chi(A)
is the probability that each edge in set
A
appears in the random geometric graph over
\mathbb{T}^{1}
with connection
\sigma(x,y)_{\lambda}^{1,>}=\mathds{1}[|x-y|_{C}\geq 1-\lambda]
and expected density
\lambda
:

\chi(A)={\mathbf{E}}_{\mathbf{H}\sim\mathsf{RGG}(n,\mathbb{T}^{1},\mathsf{Unif},\sigma(x,y)_{\lambda}^{1,>},\lambda)}[\mathsf{W}_{A}(\mathbf{H})]\,.

The reason this is feasible to compute is that the event
\big\{\sigma(x,y)_{\lambda}^{1,>}=1\big\}
significantly constrains the relative locations of
x,y
on
\mathbb{T}^{1}
: They are at distance
1-\tilde{O}(d^{-1}),
so they are nearly diametrically opposite.

Now, one can simply use the principle of inclusion-exclusion to convert the computations in the complement to computations over the original graph:

\begin{split}{\mathbf{E}}_{\mathbf{G}\sim\mathsf{RGG}(n,\mathbb{T}^{1},\mathsf{Unif},\sigma^{\infty}_{1-\lambda},1-\lambda)}[\mathsf{W}_{H}(\mathbf{G})]=\sum_{A\subseteq E(H)}(-1)^{|E(A)|}\chi(A).\end{split}

(12)

Step 3: Measuring Perturbations From Erdős-Rényi .

Here, we take an approach inspired by statistical-physics of measuring perturbations from the ‘‘ground state’’ Erdős-Rényi graph.
9
9

9

While no familiarity with statistical physics is needed to follow the argument, we will borrow some terminology with the purpose of explaining our approach in familiar language.
Measuring perturbations from Erdős-Rényi is a very natural approach as that is the null model against which we are testing.

We first measure perturbations from Erdős-Rényi at the level of single subgraphs appearing in the 1-dimensional complements, as in
Eq.

12
. Namely, define

\psi(A)\coloneqq\chi(A)-\lambda^{|E(A)|}.

(13)

This is the deviation from the probability of all edges in
A
appearing in
\mathsf{G}(n,\lambda).
Recalling
Eq.

12
, we immediately get a perturbative expression for
{\mathbf{E}}_{\mathbf{G}\sim\mathsf{RGG}(n,\mathbb{T}^{1},\mathsf{Unif},\sigma^{\infty}_{1-\lambda},1-\lambda)}[\mathsf{W}_{H}(\mathbf{G})]
:

\begin{split}&{\mathbf{E}}_{\mathbf{G}\sim\mathsf{RGG}(n,\mathbb{T}^{1},\mathsf{Unif},\sigma^{\infty}_{1-\lambda},1-\lambda)}[\mathsf{W}_{H}(\mathbf{G})]=\\
&=\sum_{A\subseteq H}(-1)^{|E(A)|}\chi(A)=\sum_{A\subseteq H}(-1)^{|E(A)|}(\psi(A)+\lambda^{|E(A)|})\\
&=(1-\lambda)^{|E(H)|}+\mathsf{Err}(H,\lambda),\qquad\text{ where }\quad\mathsf{Err}(H,\lambda)\coloneqq\sum_{A\subseteq H}(-1)^{|E(A)|}\psi(A).\end{split}

(14)

We interpret each subgraph
A
of
H
as a
polymer
and the quantity
(-1)^{|E(A)|}\psi(A)
as the
weight
of the polymer. In that view, the expression
\mathsf{Err}(H,\lambda)
is the sum of the weights of polymers which captures “the first order” deviation from the ground state
(1-\lambda)^{|E(H)|}.
The quantity
(1-\lambda)^{|E(H)|}
is a natural ground state for the expected weight of
H
in one dimension as it corresponds to the expected weight when edges are independent.

Now,
Eq.

10
allows us to obtain a similar expression in the
d
-dimensional case:

\begin{split}&{\mathbf{E}}_{\mathbf{G}\sim\mathsf{RGG}(n,\mathbb{T}^{d},\mathsf{Unif},\sigma^{\infty}_{p},p)}[\mathsf{W}_{H}(\mathbf{G})]=\Big((1-\lambda)^{|E(H)|}+\mathsf{Err}(H,\lambda)\Big)^{d}=\sum_{i=0}^{d}\binom{d}{i}(1-\lambda)^{(d-i)|E(H)|}\mathsf{Err}(H,\lambda)^{i}.\end{split}

(15)

Again, the term
(1-\lambda)^{d|E(H)|}=p^{|E(H)|}
corresponding to
i=0
is the “ground state” weight of
H
in
\mathsf{G}(n,p).
Each term of the form
\mathsf{Err}(H,\lambda)^{i}
is composed of products of
i
-tuples of polymer weights, and, thus, can be interpreted as “the
i
-th order” perturbation from the ground state.

Step 4: Bounds on Polymer Weights.

To derive a bound from
Eq.

15
, one needs to bound the polymer weights and, subsequently, the
\mathsf{Err}(H,\lambda)
term. Those are relatively straightforward computations as they are all over a 1-dimensional random geometric graph (recall
Eq.

11
). In
Section

3.2
, we prove the following claim, which is used extensively. It shows that perturbations
\psi(A)
are indeed small.

Claim 3.1
.

For every set of edges
A
such that
V(A)\leq{1}/{8\lambda},
the following hold:

1.

If
A
can be decomposed as
A_{1}\cup A_{2},
where
|V(A_{1})\cap V(A_{2})|\leq 1,
then

\chi(A)=\chi(A_{1})\chi(A_{2}).

2.

If
A
is a forest, then
\chi(A)=\lambda^{|E(A)|}
and
\psi(A)=0.

3.

\chi(A)\leq\lambda^{|V(A)|-1}
whenever
A
is connected.

4.

If
A
is not bipartite,
\chi(A)=0.
In particular,
\psi(C_{2m+1})=-\lambda^{2m+1}.

5.

|\psi(A)|\leq 2\cdot\lambda^{\max\{|V(A)|/2+1,|V(A)|-\mathsf{numc}(A)\}},
where

\mathsf{numc}(A)
denotes the number of connected components of
A.

6.

If
m\leq 1/{8\lambda}
, then

\chi(C_{m})=\lambda^{m-1}\phi(m-1),
where

\phi(m)\coloneqq{\mathbf{P}}[U_{1}+U_{2}+\cdots+U_{m-1}\in[-1,1]]
for
U_{1},U_{2},\ldots,U_{m-1}\mathrel{{\mathop{\sim}\limits}}[-1,1]
.
10
10

10

One can easily check that
\phi(1)=1,\phi(2)=3/4,\phi(3)=2/3,\phi(m-1)=\Theta(m^{-1/2}).

Using
3.1
and triangle inequality, one can easily derive (tight) upper bounds on
\mathsf{Err}(H,\lambda).
In combination with
Eq.

15
, this is enough to provide tight bounds on the expected weights of subgraphs. We delay this to
Section

3.3
and now proceed to the much more subtle case of signed weights.

Step 5: From Unsigned Weights to Signed Weights - Again PIE.

Signed subgraph weights do not immediately factorize over the independent coordinates. That is, while in the unsigned case we have
\mathds{1}[\|x^{i}-x^{j}\|_{\infty}\leq 1-\lambda]=\prod_{u=1}^{d}\mathds{1}[\|x^{i}_{u}-x^{j}_{u}\|_{\infty}\leq 1-\lambda],
no such expression holds for
(\mathds{1}[\|x^{i}-x^{j}\|_{\infty}\leq 1-\lambda]-p).
11
11

11

One cannot expect
(\mathds{1}[\|x^{i}-x^{j}\|_{\infty}\leq 1-\lambda]-p)
to always be a
d
-th power, for example because
\mathds{1}[\|x^{i}-x^{j}\|_{\infty}\leq 1-\lambda]-p
might be negative while a
d
’th power is always positive when
d
is even.

Instead, we reduce to what we know about unsigned weights:

\begin{split}&{\mathbf{E}}_{\mathbf{G}\sim\mathsf{RGG}(n,\mathbb{T}^{d},\mathsf{Unif},\sigma^{\infty}_{p},p)}[\mathsf{SW}_{H}(\mathbf{G})]={\mathbf{E}}\Big[\prod_{i=1}^{k}(\mathbf{G}_{e_{i}}-p)\Big]\\
&=\sum_{A\subseteq E(H)}(-p)^{|E(H)|-|E(A)|}{\mathbf{E}}_{\mathbf{G}\sim\mathsf{RGG}(n,\mathbb{T}^{d},\mathsf{Unif},\sigma^{\infty}_{p},p)}[\mathsf{W}_{A}(\mathbf{G})]\\
&=\sum_{A\subseteq E(H)}(-1)^{|E(H)|-|E(A)|}(1-\lambda)^{d(|E(H)|-|E(A)|))}{\mathbf{E}}_{\mathbf{G}\sim\mathsf{RGG}(n,\mathbb{T}^{d},\mathsf{Unif},\sigma^{\infty}_{p},p)}[\mathsf{W}_{A}(\mathbf{G})].\end{split}

(16)

Using
Eq.

15
for any
A\subseteq H,
we obtain

\begin{split}&{\mathbf{E}}_{\mathbf{G}\sim\mathsf{RGG}(n,\mathbb{T}^{d},\mathsf{Unif},\sigma^{\infty}_{p},p)}[\mathsf{SW}_{H}(\mathbf{G})]=\\
&=\sum_{A\subseteq H}(-1)^{|E(H)|-|E(A)|}(1-\lambda)^{d(|E(H)|-|E(A)|)}\sum_{i=0}^{d}\binom{d}{i}(1-\lambda)^{(d-i)|E(A)|}\mathsf{Err}(A,\lambda)^{i}\\
&=\sum_{i=0}^{d}\binom{d}{i}(1-\lambda)^{(d-i)|E(H)|}\sum_{A\subseteq H}(-1)^{|E(H)|-|E(A)|}(1-\lambda)^{i(|E(H)|-|E(A)|)}\mathsf{Err}(A,\lambda)^{i}.\end{split}

(17)

Step 6: The Cluster Expansion Perspective on Signed Subgraph Counts.

Again,
\mathsf{Err}(A,\lambda)^{i}
is the sum of products of
i
-tuples of weights of polymers or, equivalently, “the
i
-th order” deviation from the ground state. When we sum over
A\subseteq H,
each
i
-tuple will appear with some coefficient which captures the compatibility of the respective
i
-tuple. Specifically, in
Eq.

29
, we expand (using a similar approach to the formal derivation of the cluster expansion formula, e.g. in
[
FV17
, Chapter 5]
) each
\mathsf{Err}(A,\lambda)^{i}
as a sum of
i
-tuples of polymer weights:

\displaystyle\sum_{A\subseteq E(H)}(-1)^{|E(H)|-|E(A)|}(1-\lambda)^{i(|E(H)|-|E(A)|)}\mathsf{Err}(A,\lambda)^{i}

(18)

\displaystyle=\sum_{K_{1},K_{2},\ldots,K_{i}\subseteq E(H)}(1-(1-\lambda)^{i})^{|E(H)|-|E(K_{1}\cup\cdots\cup K_{i})|}(-1)^{|E(K_{1})|+\cdots+|E(K_{i})|}\psi(K_{1})\psi(K_{2})\cdots\psi(K_{i}).

(19)

Eq.

19
is the
i
-th order of the “cluster expansion” for signed subgraph weights.
Note that the ground state captured by the terms appearing when
i=0
vanishes as

\sum_{A\subseteq H}(-1)^{|E(H)|-|E(A)|}=\sum_{j=0}^{|E(H)|}\binom{|E(H)|}{j}(-1)^{|E(H)|-j}=0.

The fact that the ground state is 0 is intuitive, because in the case of independent edges each signed subgraph weight has expectation 0. It remains to interpret the “soft” compatibility criterion captured by
the coefficient

(1-(1-\lambda)^{i})^{|E(H)|-|E(K_{1}\cup K_{2}\cdots\cup K_{i})|}.
Whenever
|E(K_{1}\cup K_{2}\cdots\cup K_{i})|
is small, this coefficient is very small as
1-(1-\lambda)^{i}=\tilde{O}(d^{-1}).
This means that polymers
K_{1},K_{2},\ldots,K_{i}
are more compatible when

|E(K_{1}\cup K_{2}\cdots\cup K_{i})|
is smaller.
Such a compatibility criterion should not be surprising—it says that the subgraphs
K_{j}
corresponding to different coordinates are more compatible when they are more similar (so that their union does not blow up).

Our final goal will be to bound the
i
-th order deviation from the ground state for each
i.
We will prove the following proposition which immediately gives the desired bound on signed subgraph counts
Proposition

1.6
.

Proposition 3.2
.

Recall the definition of
\mathsf{Err}(A,\lambda)
in (
15
).
For
1\leq i\leq d,
the following inequality holds:

\Bigg|\sum_{A\subseteq H}(-1)^{|E(H)|-|E(A)|}(1-\lambda)^{i(|E(H)|-|E(A)|)}\mathsf{Err}(A,\lambda)^{i}\Bigg|\leq\frac{1}{(4d)^{i}}\times\Big(\frac{(\log d)^{C}}{d}\Big)^{|V(H)|/2}.

(20)

In proving
Proposition

3.2
, there are two conceptually distinct regimes for
i,
as is common in the asymptotic analysis of sums (in particular, in the cluster-expansion formula).

1.

Small values of
i
(
Section

3.4.2
).
We use
Eq.

19
.
By
3.1
,
|\psi(K_{j})|\leq(2\lambda)^{|V(K_{j})|/2+1}.
Thus, whenever

is large, the total weight
|\psi(K_{1})\psi(K_{2})\cdots\psi(K_{i})|
of the

i
-tuples for which
\sum_{j=1}^{i}|V(K_{j})|
is small:

Claim 3.3

(Rephrasing of
3.13
)
.

Let
i\geq 2,0\leq b\leq i
be integers and
a>0
be a real number. Then, the number of
i
-tuples

of

such that

is at most

\exp\Big(b(\log i)+a^{2}i\log|E(H)|+|E(H)|b\Big).

To handle the few potentially “high-energy” terms - for which

is small - we
use a comparison inequality. Namely,

2.

Large values of
i

(

3.4.3

in

18

.

or, equivalently,
\mathsf{numc}(A)>|V(A)|/2-1.

Thus, we have two cases. First,
\mathsf{numc}(A)=|V(A)|/2,
in which case

must be the union of
|V(A)|/2
disjoint edges, but then

by item 2. Or,
\mathsf{numc}(A)=|V(A)|/2-1/2,

must be the union of a triangle and

disjoint edges. In that case, using items 1, 2, and 4,

so
\psi(A)=-\lambda^{|E(A)|}=-\lambda^{|V(A)|/2+3/2}
.

Item 6.

Let
C_{m}
be the cycle on
m
vertices
1,2,\ldots,m.
Note that whenever
(ij)
is an edge in
\mathbf{H},
then
x_{i}=x_{j}+1+\lambda_{ij},
where
\lambda_{ij}\in[-\lambda,\lambda].
Thus, using that the path

is a tree and item 2,

\displaystyle\chi(C_{m})={\mathbf{E}}[\mathsf{W}_{C_{m}}(\mathbf{H})]={\mathbf{P}}[H_{1,m}=1,H_{1,2}=1,\ldots,H_{m-1,m}]

\displaystyle={\mathbf{P}}\big[H_{1,m}=1\big|H_{1,2}=1,\ldots,H_{m-1,m}\big]{\mathbf{P}}\big[H_{1,2}=1,\ldots,H_{m-1,m}\big]

\displaystyle={\mathbf{P}}\big[|x_{1}-x_{m}|_{C}\geq 1-\lambda\big|x_{i+1}=x_{i}+1+\lambda_{i,i+1},|\lambda_{i,i+1}|\leq\lambda\forall i\big]\cdot\lambda^{m-1}

\displaystyle={\mathbf{P}}\Big[|x_{1}-x_{m}|_{C}\geq 1-\lambda\big|x_{m}=1+x_{1}+\sum_{i=1}^{m-1}\lambda_{i,i+1},|\lambda_{i,i+1}|\leq\lambda\forall i\Big]\cdot\lambda^{m-1}

\displaystyle={\mathbf{P}}\Big[\sum_{i=1}^{m-1}\lambda_{i,i+1}\in[-\lambda,\lambda]\big||\lambda_{i,i+1}|\leq\lambda\forall i\Big]\cdot\lambda^{m-1}=\lambda^{m-1}\phi(m-1).

This completes the proof. ∎

Proposition 3.5

Suppose that

p^{m}\Big(1+\frac{d(\lambda^{m-1}\phi(m-1)-\lambda^{m})}{(1-\lambda)^{m}}+{O}\big({d^{2}\lambda^{2(m-1)}}\big)\Big)\quad\text{when $m$ is even}.\\
\end{cases}

(22)

Proof.

We use

\begin{split}D_{d}\gamma(\mathbf{g}_{-d})\;&=\sup_{\mathbf{g}^{M}_{d}}\gamma(\mathbf{g}_{-d},\mathbf{g}^{M}_{d})-\inf_{\mathbf{g}^{m}_{d}}\gamma(\mathbf{g}_{-d},\mathbf{g}^{m}_{d})\\
&=\sup_{\mathbf{g}^{M}_{d}}\big|B_{L_{q}(\mathbb{T}^{d}),\tau}(\mathbf{g}_{-d},\mathbf{g}^{M}_{d})\cap B_{L_{q}(\mathbb{T}^{d}),\tau}(\mathbf{0})\big|-\inf_{\mathbf{g}^{m}_{d}}\big|B_{L_{q}(\mathbb{T}^{d}),\tau}(\mathbf{g}_{-d},\mathbf{g}^{m}_{d})\cap B_{L_{q}(\mathbb{T}^{d}),\tau}(\mathbf{0})\big|\\
&=|B_{L_{q}(\mathbb{T}^{d}),\tau}(\mathbf{g}_{-d},0)\cap B_{L_{q}(\mathbb{T}^{d}),\tau}(\mathbf{0})\big|-\big|B_{L_{q}(\mathbb{T}^{d}),\tau}(\mathbf{g}_{-d},1)\cap B_{L_{q}(\mathbb{T}^{d}),\tau}(\mathbf{0})\big|\\
&\leq|B_{L_{q}(\mathbb{T}^{d}),\tau}(\mathbf{g}_{-d},0)\backslash B_{L_{q}(\mathbb{T}^{d}),\tau}(\mathbf{g}_{-d},1)|\\
&=|B_{L_{q}(\mathbb{T}^{d}),\tau}(\mathbf{0})\backslash B_{L_{q}(\mathbb{T}^{d}),\tau}(\mathbf{0}_{-d},1)|.\end{split}

(57)

Now, observe that a point
(h_{1},h_{2},\ldots,h_{d-1},h_{d})
is in
B_{L_{q}(\mathbb{T}^{d}),\tau}(\mathbf{0})\backslash B_{L_{q}(\mathbb{T}^{d}),\tau}(\mathbf{0}_{-d},1)
if and only if

\begin{split}\sum_{i=1}^{d-1}|h_{i}|_{C}^{q}\leq\tau^{q}-|h_{d}|_{C}^{q}\quad\text{ and }\quad\sum_{i=1}^{d-1}|h_{i}|_{C}^{q}\geq\tau^{q}-|1-h_{d}|_{C}^{q}=\tau^{q}-(1-|h_{d}|_{C})^{q}.\end{split}

Clearly, one needs to have
|h_{d}|_{C}\in[0,1/2]
for this event to occur.
Since each
|h_{i}|_{C}
is uniformly distributed on
[0,1],
we conclude that the probability of this event is

\begin{split}&\int_{0}^{1/2}{\mathbf{P}}\Bigg[\tau^{q}-(1-\ell)^{q}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{d-1}|h_{i}|_{C}^{q}\leq\tau^{q}-\ell^{q}\Bigg]d\ell\\
&\leq\int_{0}^{1}{\mathbf{P}}\Bigg[\tau^{q}-\ell^{q}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{d-1}|h_{i}|_{C}^{q}\leq\tau^{q}\Bigg]d\ell\\
&=\int_{0}^{1}F([\tau^{q}-\ell^{q},\tau^{q}])d\ell=\int_{0}^{1}\psi(\ell)d\ell=O\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{qd}}\right),\end{split}

(58)

as desired.

5.1.2
Bounding Marginal Variances

For the second moment, we will first rewrite
\gamma.
By definition,

\begin{split}\gamma(\mathbf{g})+\frac{1}{4}=&\int_{B_{L_{q}(\mathbb{T}^{d}),\tau}(\mathbf{0})}\mathds{1}\bigg[\mathbf{z}\in B_{L_{q}(\mathbb{T}^{d}),\tau}(\mathbf{g})\bigg]d\mathbf{z}\\
=&\int_{B_{L_{q}(\mathbb{T}^{d}),\tau}(\mathbf{0})}\mathds{1}\Bigg[\sum_{i=1}^{d}|g_{i}-z_{i}|_{C}^{q}\leq\tau^{q}\Bigg]d\mathbf{z}.\end{split}

(59)

Now, fix
\mathbf{g}_{-d}
and denote

\kappa(u)=\int_{B_{L_{q}(\mathbb{T}^{d}),\tau}(\mathbf{0})}\mathds{1}\bigg[\sum_{i=1}^{d-1}|g_{i}-z_{i}|_{C}^{q}+|u-z_{d}|_{C}^{q}\leq\tau^{q}\bigg]d\mathbf{z}.

{\mathbf{Var}}_{U\sim\mathsf{Unif}(\mathbb{S}^{1})}[\kappa(U)]
is exactly
{\mathbf{Var}}_{d}[\gamma(\mathbf{g}_{-d})].
By definition,

\begin{split}{\mathbf{E}}_{U\sim\mathsf{Unif}(\mathbb{S}^{1})}[\kappa(U)]&={\mathbf{E}}_{U}\Bigg[\int_{B_{L_{q}(\mathbb{T}^{d}),\tau}(\mathbf{0})}\mathds{1}[\sum_{i=1}^{d-1}|g_{i}-z_{i}|_{C}^{q}+|U-z_{i}|_{C}^{q}\leq\tau^{q}]d\mathbf{z}\Bigg]\\
&={\mathbf{E}}_{V}\Bigg[\int_{B_{L_{q}(\mathbb{T}^{d}),\tau}(\mathbf{0})}\mathds{1}[\sum_{i=1}^{d-1}|g_{i}-z_{i}|_{C}^{q}+|V|_{C}^{q}\leq\tau^{q}]d\mathbf{z}\Bigg]:={\mathbf{E}}[\rho(V)],\end{split}

where
\rho
is defined by the last equation, i.e.,

\rho(v)\coloneqq\int_{B_{L_{q}(\mathbb{T}^{d}),\tau}(\mathbf{0})}\mathds{1}\bigg[\sum_{i=1}^{d-1}|g_{i}-z_{i}|_{C}^{q}+|v|_{C}^{q}\leq\tau^{q}\bigg]d\mathbf{z}.

On the other hand,

\begin{split}&{\mathbf{E}}_{U\sim\mathsf{Unif}(\mathbb{S}^{1})}[\kappa^{2}(U)]\\
&={\mathbf{E}}_{U}\Bigg[\int_{B_{L_{q}(\mathbb{T}^{d}),\tau}(\mathbf{0})}\mathds{1}\bigg[\sum_{i=1}^{d-1}|g_{i}-z^{1}_{i}|_{C}^{q}+|U-z^{1}_{i}|_{C}^{q}\leq\tau^{q}\bigg]d\mathbf{z}^{1}\\
&\quad\quad\times\int_{B_{L_{q}(\mathbb{T}^{d}),\tau}(\mathbf{0})}\mathds{1}\bigg[\sum_{i=1}^{d-1}|g_{i}-z^{2}_{i}|_{C}^{q}+|U-z^{2}_{i}|_{C}^{q}\leq\tau^{q}\bigg]d\mathbf{z}^{2}\Bigg]\\
&={\mathbf{E}}_{U}\Bigg[\int_{B_{L_{q}(\mathbb{T}^{d}),\tau}(\mathbf{0})}\mathds{1}\bigg[\sum_{i=1}^{d-1}|g_{i}-z^{1}_{i}|_{C}^{q}+|V|_{C}^{q}\leq\tau^{q}\bigg]d\mathbf{z}^{1}\\
&\quad\quad\times\int_{B_{L_{q}(\mathbb{T}^{d}),\tau}(\mathbf{0})}\mathds{1}\bigg[\sum_{i=1}^{d-1}|g_{i}-z^{2}_{i}|_{C}^{q}+|V+z^{1}_{i}-z^{2}_{i}|_{C}^{q}\leq\tau^{q}\bigg]d\mathbf{z}^{2}\Bigg]\\
&={\mathbf{E}}_{V,R}[\rho(V)\rho(V+R)],\end{split}

where
V=U-z^{1}_{d}\sim\mathsf{Unif}(\mathbb{S}^{1})
and

R\sim z^{1}_{d}-z^{2}_{d}
and
V,R
are independent. It follows that

{\mathbf{Var}}[\kappa]={\mathbf{E}}_{V,R}[\rho(V)\rho(V+R)]-{\mathbf{E}}[\rho(V)]^{2}.

(60)

Since
\rho:\mathbb{S}^{1}\longrightarrow[0,1]
is clearly
L_{2}
-integrable, we can write its Fourier series. Furthermore, as
\rho(v)=\rho(-v)
and
\rho
is real, we can write

\rho(v)=\widehat{\rho}(0)+\sum_{k\geq 1}2\widehat{\rho}(k)\cos(\pi kv).

In particular, we have
{\mathbf{E}}_{V\sim\mathsf{Unif}(\mathbb{S}^{1})}[\rho(V)]=\widehat{\rho}(0)
and, using the convolution formula,

\begin{split}{\mathbf{E}}_{V,R}[\rho(V)\rho(V+R)]=\widehat{\rho}(0)^{2}+\sum_{k\geq 1}2{\widehat{\rho}(k)^{2}}{\mathbf{E}}_{R}[\cos(\pi kR)].\end{split}

(61)

Putting all of this together, we have

\begin{split}&{\mathbf{Var}}_{d}[\gamma(\mathbf{g}_{-d})]=\sum_{k\geq 1}2{\widehat{\rho}(k)^{2}}{\mathbf{E}}_{R}[\cos(\pi kR)]\leq\bigg(\sum_{k\geq 1}2{\widehat{\rho}(k)^{2}}\bigg)\times\sup_{k\geq 1}{\mathbf{E}}_{R}[\cos(\pi kR)]\\
&={\mathbf{Var}}_{V\sim\mathsf{Unif}(\mathbb{S}^{1})}[\rho(V)]\times\sup_{k\geq 1}{\mathbf{E}}_{R}[\cos(\pi kR)].\end{split}

(62)

We will now bound the variance of
\rho
and the cosine expectations separately. That is, we will show that

{\mathbf{Var}}_{V\sim\mathsf{Unif}(\mathbb{S}^{1})}[\rho(V)]=O\bigg(\frac{1}{d}\bigg)\quad\text{ and }\quad\sup_{k\geq 1}{\mathbf{E}}_{R}[\cos(\pi kR)]=O\bigg(\frac{1}{qd}\bigg),

which is enough.

1) Variance of
\rho.

\begin{split}{\mathbf{Var}}[\rho]\;&\leq{\mathbf{E}}_{V}\bigg[\big(\rho(0)-\rho(V)\big)^{2}\bigg]\\
&={\mathbf{E}}_{V}\Bigg[\Bigg(\int_{B_{L_{q}(\mathbb{T}^{d}),\tau}(\mathbf{0})}\mathds{1}\bigg[\sum_{i=1}^{d-1}|g_{i}-z_{i}|_{C}^{q}\leq\tau^{q}\bigg]d\mathbf{z}-\mathds{1}\bigg[\sum_{i=1}^{d-1}|g_{i}-z_{i}|_{C}^{q}+|V|_{C}^{q}\leq\tau^{q}\bigg]d\mathbf{z}\Bigg)^{2}\Bigg]\\
&={\mathbf{E}}_{V}\Bigg[\Bigg(\int_{B_{L_{q}(\mathbb{T}^{d}),\tau}(\mathbf{0})}\mathds{1}\bigg[\sum_{i=1}^{d-1}|g_{i}-z_{i}|_{C}^{q}\in[\tau^{q},\tau^{q}-|V|^{q}_{C}]\bigg]d\mathbf{z}\Bigg)^{2}\Bigg]\\
&\leq{\mathbf{E}}_{V}\Bigg[\Bigg(\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\mathds{1}\bigg[\sum_{i=1}^{d-1}|g_{i}-z_{i}|_{C}^{q}\in[\tau^{q},\tau^{q}-|V|^{q}_{C}]\bigg]d\mathbf{z}\Bigg)^{2}\Bigg].\end{split}

Since the integral is over the entire torus, the variables
\{|g_{i}-z_{i}|_{C}\}_{i=1}^{d-1}
are iid uniformly distributed over
[0,1],
just like
V.
Therefore, the last expression equals

\int_{0}^{1}F([\tau^{q}-\ell^{q},\tau^{q}])^{2}d\ell=O\left(\frac{1}{d}\right),

(63)

where we used
5.1
.

2) Cosine Expectation.

We need to find

\sup_{k\geq 1}{\mathbf{E}}_{R}[\cos(\pi kR)],\quad R\sim z^{1}_{d}-z^{2}_{d},

(64)

where
z^{1}_{d},z^{2}_{d}
are independent copies of the last coordinate of uniformly random point in
B_{L_{q}(\mathbb{T}^{d}),\tau}(\mathbf{0}).

First, we will make a few simple observations abound the density of
R.
Let the density of
z^{1}_{d}
be
\nu(x).
Note that

\nu(x)\propto F(\tau^{q}-|x|_{C}^{q})

since
(z_{1},z_{2},\ldots,z_{d-1},x)\in B_{L_{q}(\mathbb{T}^{d}),\tau}(\mathbf{0})
if and only if

\sum_{i=1}^{d-1}|z_{i}|_{C}^{q}\leq\tau^{q}-x^{q},

but
|z_{1}|_{C},|z_{2}|_{C},\ldots,|z_{d-1}|_{C}
are iid
\mathsf{Unif}([0,1])
variables. In particular, this has the following implications:

1.

|x|_{C}\longrightarrow\nu(x)
is positive and decreasing.

2.

\nu
is even, i.e.
\nu(x)=\nu(-x).

Now,
R\sim z^{1}_{d}-z^{2}_{d}\sim z^{1}_{d}+z^{2}_{d}.
Thus, if
\mu
is the distribution of
R,
clearly
\mu=\nu*\nu.
In particular:

\begin{split}&\text{1. $\mu$ is even, i.e. $\mu(y)=\mu(-y),$}\\
&\text{2. $|y|_{C}\longrightarrow\mu(y)$ is decreasing.}\end{split}

(65)

The first fact is trivial. The second fact for
y\in[0,1]
can be shown as follows. First, note that
\nu^{\prime}(x)\leq 0
for
x\in[0,1]
as
|x|_{C}\longrightarrow\nu(x)
is decreasing
and
\nu^{\prime}(x)=-\nu^{\prime}(-x)
since
x
is even.
Now,

\begin{split}\mu^{\prime}(y)\;&=(\nu*\nu)^{\prime}(y)=(\nu^{\prime}*\nu)(y)=\int_{-1}^{1}\nu^{\prime}(x)\nu(y-x)dx\\
&=\int_{0}^{1}\nu^{\prime}(x)\nu(y-x)dx+\int_{-1}^{0}\nu^{\prime}(x)\nu(y-x)dx\\
&=\int_{0}^{1}\nu^{\prime}(x)\nu(y-x)dx+\int_{0}^{1}\nu^{\prime}(-x)\nu(y+x)dx\\
&=\int_{0}^{1}\nu^{\prime}(x)\big(\nu(y-x)-\nu(y+x)\big)dx.\end{split}

We know that
\nu^{\prime}(x)\leq 0
for
x\in[0,1].
On the other hand
\nu(y-x)\geq\nu(y+x)
holds because
|z|_{C}\longrightarrow\nu(z)
is decreasing and

|y-x|_{C}\leq|y+x|_{C}
whenever
x,y\in[0,1].
To show the last part, note that
|y-x|_{C}\in\{y-x,x-y\},
and

|y+x|_{C}\in\{y+x,2-y-x\}.
However,

1.

y-x,x-y\leq y+x
whenever
x,y\geq 0.

2.

y-x,x-y\leq 2-y-x
whenever
x,y\leq 1.

We split the rest of the proof into two claims.

Claim 5.3
.

\sup_{k\geq 1}{\mathbf{E}}_{R}[\cos(\pi kR)]\leq 2{\mathsf{TV}}\Big(R,\mathsf{Unif}([-1,1])\Big).

Proof.

Let
(R,U)
be an optimal coupling of a
\mathsf{Unif}([-1,1])
random variable
U
with
R.
Then,

\begin{split}{\mathbf{E}}[\cos(\pi kR)]=&{\mathbf{E}}[\cos(\pi kR)-\cos(\pi kU)]={\mathbf{E}}\bigg[\mathds{1}[U\neq R]\big(\cos(\pi kR)-\cos(\pi kU)\big)\bigg]\\
\leq&\|\cos(\pi kR)-\cos(\pi kU)\|_{\infty}\times{\mathbf{E}}\bigg[\mathds{1}[U\neq R]\bigg]\leq 2{\mathsf{TV}}\Big(R,\mathsf{Unif}([-1,1])\Big).\end{split}

(66)

∎

Claim 5.4
.

{\mathsf{TV}}\Big(R,\mathsf{Unif}([-1,1])\Big)=O(1/dq).

Proof.

We use properties of the aforementioned density
\mu.
Let
U\sim\mathsf{Unif}([-1,1]).

\begin{split}{\mathsf{TV}}(R,U)&=\sup_{A}\bigg({\mathbf{P}}[U\in A]-{\mathbf{P}}[R\in A]\bigg)\\
&=\sup_{A}\int_{A}\bigg(\frac{1}{2}-\mu(u)\bigg)dy\leq|A|\times\bigg(\frac{1}{2}-\inf_{y}\mu(y)\bigg)\leq\bigg(\frac{1}{2}-\inf_{y}\mu(y)\bigg).\end{split}

(67)

Our last step will be to show that
\inf_{y}\mu(y)=\frac{1}{2}-O\bigg(\frac{1}{dq}\bigg).
As we know,
\nu(x)\propto F(\tau^{q}-|x|_{C}^{q})=F(\tau^{q})-\psi(|x|_{C})=C-\psi(|x|_{C}),
where

C=F(\tau^{q})={\mathbf{P}}\bigg[\sum_{i=1}^{d-1}U_{i}^{q}\leq\tau^{q}\bigg]\geq{\mathbf{P}}\bigg[\sum_{i=1}^{d}U_{i}^{q}\leq\tau^{q}\bigg]\geq\frac{1}{2}.

Thus,

\mu(y)=(\nu*\nu)(y)\propto\int_{-1}^{1}F(\tau^{q}-|x|_{C}^{q})F(\tau^{q}-|y-x|_{C}^{q})dx=\int^{1}_{-1}(C-\psi(|x|_{C}))(C-\psi(|y-x|_{C}))dx.

Now, we will find the normalizing constant in
\propto.

\begin{split}K\;&=2\int^{1}_{-1}\int^{1}_{-1}(C-\psi(|x|_{C}))(C-\psi(|y-x|_{C}))dxdy=2\bigg(\int^{1}_{-1}(C-\psi(|x|_{C}))dx\bigg)^{2}\\
&=2\bigg(2C-\int_{-1}^{1}\psi(|x|_{C})dx\bigg)^{2}=2\Bigg(4C^{2}-4C\int_{-1}^{1}\psi(|x|_{C})dx+\bigg(\int_{-1}^{1}\psi(|x|_{C})dx\bigg)^{2}\Bigg)\\
&=8C^{2}-8C\int_{-1}^{1}\psi(|x|_{C})dx+O\bigg(\frac{1}{dq}\bigg),\end{split}

(68)

where we used
5.1
. Note that
K=\Theta(1)
since
C\geq 1/2
and
\int_{-1}^{1}\psi(|x|_{C})dx=O(\sqrt{\frac{1}{qd}})=o(1)
by
5.1
.
However, we know that
\inf_{y}\mu(y)=\mu(1)
by
Eq.

65
, so

\begin{split}\mu(1)\;&=\frac{1}{K}\int_{-1}^{1}(C-\psi(|x|_{C}))(C-\psi(|1-x|_{C}))dx\\
&=\frac{1}{K}\Bigg(4C^{2}-2C\bigg(\int_{-1}^{1}\psi(|x|_{C})dx+\int_{-1}^{1}\psi(|1-x|_{C})dx\bigg)+\int_{-1}^{1}\psi(|x|_{C})dx\times\int_{-1}^{1}\psi(|1-x|_{C})dx\bigg)\\
&\geq\frac{1}{K}\Bigg(4C^{2}-2C\bigg(\int_{-1}^{1}\psi(|x|_{C})dx+\int_{-1}^{1}\psi(|1-x|_{C})dx\bigg)\Bigg)\\
&=\frac{1}{K}\Bigg(4C^{2}-4C\int_{-1}^{1}\psi(|x|_{C})dx\Bigg)\\
&=\frac{K/2-O\bigg(\frac{1}{dq}\bigg)}{K}=\frac{1}{2}-O\bigg(\frac{1}{dq}\bigg),\end{split}

with which the desired bound on the cosine expectation follows.
∎

5.2
The Proof for Large
q

When
q=\Omega(d/\log d),
we will follow a similar strategy as in the proof for the case of
q=o(d/\log d).
Namely, our goal will be to prove that for any integer
k,

{\mathbf{E}}_{\mathbf{g}\sim\mathsf{Unif}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}[|\gamma(\mathbf{g})|^{k}]^{1/k}\leq C(\log d)^{C}\frac{k}{d}.

(69)

for some absolute constant
C.
Following the same steps as in
Eq.

55
, this will be enough to conclude the second part of
Theorem

1.10
. Again, we will use the Bernstein-McDiarmid approach to bounding the moments of
of
\gamma.
Our goal, this time, is to show the following.

1.

Small Marginal Increments:

\|D_{i}\gamma\|_{\infty}=\tilde{O}(\frac{1}{d})
for all
i.

2.

Small Marginal Variances:

\|{\mathbf{Var}}_{i}[\gamma]\|_{\infty}=\tilde{O}(\frac{1}{d^{3}})
for all
i.

We use the following anticoncentration results instead of
5.1
. The rest of the proof is exactly the same.

Claim 5.5
.

Suppose that

U_{1},U_{2},\ldots,U_{d-1}
are iid
\mathsf{Unif}([0,1])
random variables and
q=\Omega(d/\log d).
Let
F
be the CDF of
\sum_{i=1}^{d-1}U_{i}^{q}.
Then, for
\psi(\ell)\coloneqq F(\tau^{q})-F(\tau^{q}-\ell^{q})=F([\tau^{q}-\ell^{q},\tau^{q}]),
we have

\int_{0}^{1}\psi(\ell)d\ell=\tilde{O}\left(\frac{1}{d}\right)\quad\text{ and }\quad\int_{0}^{1}\psi(\ell)^{2}d\ell=\tilde{O}\left(\frac{1}{d}\right).

(70)

The proof of
5.1
is substantially different (and much simpler) than the proof of
5.1
. As we will need one of the ingredients in the next section as well, we present it in full detail here.

Lemma 5.6
.

Suppose that

U_{1},U_{2},\ldots,U_{d-1}
are iid
\mathsf{Unif}([0,1])
random variables and
q\geq 1.
Then, for any interval
[a,b],

{\mathbf{P}}\bigg[\sum_{i=1}^{d-1}U_{i}^{q}\in[a,b]\bigg]\leq b^{(d-1)/q}-a^{(d-1)/q}.

Proof.

The main idea is to reduce the computation to a computation for
q=\infty.

Let
W=\max(U_{1},\ldots,U_{d-1})
and

V_{1},V_{2},\ldots,V_{d-2}\mathrel{{\mathop{\sim}\limits}}\mathsf{Unif}([0,1])
be
independent of
W.
Then,
\sum_{i=1}^{d-1}U_{i}^{q}
has the same distribution as
W^{q}\times\big(1+\sum_{j=1}^{d-2}V_{j}^{q}\big).

This follows simply by conditioning on the maximal value
w
of
U_{1},U_{2},\ldots,U_{d-1}.
Denote
T=1+\sum_{j=1}^{d-2}V_{j}^{q}
and observe that
T\geq 1
a.s. This implies that

\begin{split}&{\mathbf{P}}\bigg[\sum_{i=1}^{d-1}U_{i}^{q}\in[a,b]\bigg]={\mathbf{P}}\bigg[W^{q}\times T\in[a,b]\bigg]\leq\sup_{t\geq 1}{\mathbf{P}}\bigg[W^{q}\times t\in[a,b]\bigg]=\sup_{t\geq 1}{\mathbf{P}}\bigg[\big(\frac{a}{t}\big)^{1/q}\leq W\leq\big(\frac{b}{t}\big)^{1/q}\bigg].\end{split}

Now, since
W
is the maximum of
d-1
iid
\mathsf{Unif}([0,1])
random variables,
{\mathbf{P}}[W\leq x]=x^{d-1}
for any
x\in[0,1].
Thus,

\displaystyle{\mathbf{P}}\bigg[\big(\frac{a}{t}\big)^{1/q}\leq W\leq\big(\frac{b}{t}\big)^{1/q}\bigg]=\big(\frac{b}{t}\big)^{\frac{d-1}{q}}-\big(\frac{a}{t}\big)^{\frac{d-1}{q}}\leq b^{(d-1)/q}-a^{(d-1)/q},

where we used
t\geq 1.

∎

Now, we are ready to prove
5.5
.

Proof of
5.5
.

Suppose that
q\geq d/(C^{\prime}\log d)
for some absolute constant
C^{\prime}.
We begin by proving the following two simple statements:

1.

\tau\geq 1-1/d.
Recall that
\tau
is defined as the radius of a
1/2
volume ball in
(\mathbb{T}^{d},L_{q}).
Let
U_{1},U_{2},\ldots,U_{d}
be iid
\mathsf{Unif}([0,1])
random variables. Thus,

1/2={\mathbf{P}}\big[\tau\geq\|(U_{1},U_{2},\ldots,U_{d})\|_{q}\big].
However,

{\mathbf{P}}\big[1-1/d\geq\|(U_{1},U_{2},\ldots,U_{d})\|_{q}\big]\leq{\mathbf{P}}\big[1-1/d\geq\|(U_{1},U_{2},\ldots,U_{d})\|_{\infty}\big]=(1-1/d)^{d}\leq 1/e<1/2,

which means that
\tau\geq 1-1/d.

2.

\tau^{q}\leq C^{\prime\prime}(\log d)
for some constant
C^{\prime\prime}
depending solely on
C^{\prime}.
Observe that each variable
U^{q}_{i}
has expectation
1/(q+1),
variance lass than

{\mathbf{E}}[U_{i}^{2q}]=1/(2q+1)
and is bounded between 0 and 1. Thus, by
2.2
,

{\mathbf{P}}\bigg[\sum_{j=1}^{d}U_{j}^{q}\geq t+d/(q+1)\bigg]\leq\exp\Big(-\min\big\{\Theta(t^{2}/(d/q)),\Theta(t)\big\}\Big).

In particular, this means that setting
t=C^{\prime\prime}\times\max(1,d/q)\leq C^{\prime\prime}\times C^{\prime}\times(\log d)
for large enough
C^{\prime\prime},
we obtain a tail bound less than
1/2.
Thus,
\tau^{q}\leq C^{\prime\prime}\log d
for some
C^{\prime\prime}.

Now, we go back to proving
5.5
. We begin with the first inequality.

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\psi(\ell)d\ell=\int_{0}^{1}{\mathbf{P}}\bigg[\tau^{q}-\ell^{q}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{d-1}U_{i}^{d-1}\leq\tau^{q}\bigg]d\ell

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1-\frac{(\log d)^{3}}{d}}{\mathbf{P}}\bigg[\tau^{q}-\ell^{q}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{d-1}U_{i}^{d-1}\leq\tau^{q}\bigg]d\ell+\int_{1-\frac{(\log d)^{3}}{d}}^{1}{\mathbf{P}}\bigg[\tau^{q}-\ell^{q}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{d-1}U_{i}^{d-1}\leq\tau^{q}\bigg]d\ell

\displaystyle\leq{\mathbf{P}}\bigg[\tau^{q}-\bigg(1-\frac{(\log d)^{3}}{d}\bigg)^{q}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{d-1}U_{i}^{d-1}\leq\tau^{q}\bigg]+\frac{(\log d)^{3}}{d}.

All that is left to do is bound
{\mathbf{P}}\bigg[\tau^{q}-\bigg(1-\frac{(\log d)^{3}}{d}\bigg)^{q}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{d-1}U_{i}^{d-1}\leq\tau^{q}\bigg].
Using
Lemma

5.6
,

\begin{split}&{\mathbf{P}}\bigg[\tau^{q}-\bigg(1-\frac{(\log d)^{3}}{d}\bigg)^{q}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{d-1}U_{i}^{d-1}\leq\tau^{q}\bigg]\\
&\leq(\tau^{q})^{(d-1)/q}-\Bigg(\tau^{q}-\Bigg(1-\frac{(\log d)^{3}}{d}\Bigg)^{q}\Bigg)^{(d-1)/q}\\
&=(\tau^{q})^{(d-1)/q}\times\Bigg[1-\Bigg(1-\bigg(\frac{1-(\log d)^{3}/d}{\tau}\bigg)^{q}\Bigg)^{(d-1)/q}\Bigg].\end{split}

(71)

Since
\tau\geq 1-1/d,
it is the case that

\frac{1-(\log d)^{3}/d}{\tau}\leq 1-(\log d)^{3}/(2d).
Thus,

\bigg(\frac{1-(\log d)^{3}/d}{\tau}\bigg)^{q}\leq\bigg(1-(\log d)^{3}/(2d)\bigg)^{q}\leq\exp\bigg(-(\log d)^{3}q/(2d)\bigg)\leq\exp\bigg(-\Theta((\log d)^{2})\bigg).

It follows that

\displaystyle\Bigg(1-\bigg(\frac{1-(\log d)^{3}/d}{\tau}\bigg)^{q}\Bigg)^{(d-1)/q}\geq\Bigg(1-\exp\bigg(-\Theta((\log d)^{2})\bigg)\Bigg)^{(d-1)/q}

\displaystyle\geq\Bigg(1-\exp\bigg(-\Theta((\log d)^{2})\bigg)\Bigg)^{C^{\prime}(\log d)}=1-\exp\bigg(-\Theta((\log d)^{2})\bigg).

Therefore,

The second inequality follows directly as

8
Discussion

We studied the question of detecting
L_{q}
geometry in random geometric graphs. Our work shows that for different values of
q,
not only the limits of computational and statistical detection vary, but also the optimal algorithms are different. In particular, contrary to previous work, we show that the signed triangle count is not always optimal as the signed 4-cycle test might succeed in a polynomially larger range. This, however, opens more questions than it answers. What other tests besides counting signed 3- and 4- cycles can be optimal for detecting latent geometry? Are there instances in which a statistical-computational gap for detecting geometry is present? A positive answer to this question might even be hidden in the
\mathsf{RGG}(n,\mathbb{T}^{d},\mathsf{Unif},\sigma^{q}_{p},p)
models considered in the current paper as our statistical lower-bounds and computationally efficient algorithmic upper bounds are essentially nowhere matching.

Similarly, one can study other statistical tasks related to random geometric graphs with
L_{q}
geometry. Especially intriguing seems the task of efficiently embedding a sample from
\mathsf{RGG}(n,\mathbb{T}^{d},\mathsf{Unif},\sigma^{q}_{p},p)
into
(\mathbb{T}^{d},\|\cdot\|_{q})
so that marginal distances are non-trivially approximated. This question will most likely require new ideas, different from previous work on embedding random geometric graphs. The spectral approach of
[
LS23
]
heavily relies on an inner product structure, which is only present in
\mathsf{RGG}(n,\mathbb{T}^{d},\mathsf{Unif},\sigma^{q}_{p},p)
when
q=2.
The optimization framework of
[
MMY20
]
works in settings of
L_{q}
geometry for general
q,
but only gives strong poly-time guarantees for connection functions bounded away from
0
and
1,
i.e.
c\leq\sigma(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\leq 1-c
for some
c>0.
This however, is not the case in
\mathsf{RGG}(n,\mathbb{T}^{d},\mathsf{Unif},\sigma^{q}_{p},p)
as
\sigma^{q}_{p}
only takes values 0 and 1.

Finally, we compared the
L_{\infty}
geometry giving rise to an
AND
structure in random geometric graphs and the
L_{2}
geometry giving rise to a (weighted)
MAJORITY
structure (as would any
L_{C}
when
C=O(1)
). It could be interesting to consider an extension of these constructions for general
f:\{0,1\}^{d}\longrightarrow\{0,1\}
beyond
AND
an
MAJORITY
.
One way to formalize is the following. One first samples
d
graphs on
n
vertices
\mathbf{G}^{1},\mathbf{G}^{2},\ldots,\mathbf{G}^{d}
from some fixed “1-dimensional” distribution
\mathcal{G}
and then forms the
d
dimensional graph
\mathbf{G}
in which
\mathbf{G}_{ij}=f(\{\mathbf{G}^{u}_{ij}\}_{u=1}^{d}).
When do such graphs converge to Erdős-Rényi ? When can they be represented as random geometric graphs? We note that in the special case
f=\textsf{AND}
(respectively,
f=\textsf{OR}
under taking a complement) one can apply our cluster-expansion based approach for
any
1-dimensional distribution
\mathcal{G}.
An example of the
AND
structure beyond
L_{\infty}
toric random geometric graphs are random intersection graphs (e.g.
[
BBN20
]
).

A Fourier-based Approach to Signed Cycle Counts.

We end with a Fourier-based approach to computing the signed cycle counts for
\mathsf{RGG}(n,\mathbb{T}^{d},\mathsf{Unif},\sigma^{q}_{p},p)
(which extends to any random algebraic graph over

).

We begin with some brief refresher on Fourier analysis over
\mathbb{T}^{d}.
Recall that we defined
\mathbb{T}^{d}
as a product of
d
circles of circumference 2, or, equivalently,
\mathbb{T}^{d}=\mathbf{R}^{d}/\sim,
where
\mathbf{x}\sim\mathbf{y}
if and only if
\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\in 2\mathbb{Z}^{d}.

Similarly to the Boolean case, we will use the fact that any
L_{2}
-integrable function

can be uniquely written as

f(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\widehat{f}(\mathbf{v})\exp(i\pi\langle\mathbf{v},\mathbf{x}\rangle).
We make the following simple well-known observation. If

f(\mathbf{x})=f(-\mathbf{x})
for all
\mathbf{x},
then each coefficient
\widehat{f}(\mathbf{v})
is real and, furthermore,
\widehat{f}(\mathbf{v})=\widehat{f}(-\mathbf{v}).
Indeed, this follows by uniqueness as

\sum_{\mathbf{v}}\widehat{f}(\mathbf{v})\exp(-i\pi\langle\mathbf{v},\mathbf{x}\rangle)=f(-\mathbf{x})=f(\mathbf{x})=\overline{f(\mathbf{x})}=\sum_{\mathbf{v}}\overline{\widehat{f}(\mathbf{v})\exp(i\pi\langle\mathbf{v},\mathbf{x}\rangle)}=\sum_{\mathbf{v}}\overline{\widehat{f}(\mathbf{v})}\exp(-i\pi\langle\mathbf{v},\mathbf{x}\rangle).

Finally, recall that
\widehat{f}(\mathbf{0})=\int_{\mathbb{T}}^{d}f(\mathbf{x})d\mathbf{x}.

Now,
\sigma^{q}_{p}
is clearly
L_{2}
-integrable. Thus, for any signed
k
-cycle weight,

\begin{split}&{\mathbf{E}}_{\mathbf{G}\sim\mathsf{RGG}(n,\mathbb{T}^{d},\mathsf{Unif},\sigma^{q}_{p},p)}\Big[\mathsf{SW}_{C_{k}}(\mathbf{G})\Big]\\
&={\mathbf{E}}_{\mathbf{g}_{1},\mathbf{g}_{2},\cdots,\mathbf{g}_{k}\mathrel{{\mathop{\sim}\limits}}\mathsf{Unif}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}\Bigg[\prod_{i=1}^{k}(\sigma(\mathbf{g}_{i}-\mathbf{g}_{i+1})-p)\Bigg]\\

&=\sum_{\mathbf{v}_{1},\mathbf{v}_{2},\cdots,\mathbf{v}_{k}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}\backslash\mathbf{0}}\int_{(\mathbb{T}^{d})^{k}}\widehat{\sigma}(\mathbf{v}_{1})\widehat{\sigma}(\mathbf{v}_{1})\cdots\widehat{\sigma}(\mathbf{v}_{k})\exp(-i\pi\sum_{i=1}^{k}\langle\mathbf{v}_{i},\mathbf{g}_{i}-\mathbf{g}_{i+1}\rangle)\\
&=\sum_{\mathbf{v}_{1},\mathbf{v}_{2},\cdots,\mathbf{v}_{k}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}\backslash\mathbf{0}}\int_{(\mathbb{T}^{d})^{k}}\widehat{\sigma}(\mathbf{v}_{1})\widehat{\sigma}(\mathbf{v}_{1})\cdots\widehat{\sigma}(\mathbf{v}_{k})\exp(-i\pi\sum_{i=1}^{k}\langle\mathbf{g}_{i},\mathbf{v}_{i}-\mathbf{v}_{i-1}\rangle)\\
&=\sum_{\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}\backslash\mathbf{0}}\widehat{\sigma}(\mathbf{v})^{k},\end{split}

where the last line follows from the simple observation that if

&=O(t^{i-1})-\frac{2t(2t-1)(2t-2)\cdots(2t-i+1)}{i!}\Big(1-\frac{2t(2t-2)(2t-4)\cdots(2t-2i+4)(2t-2)}{2t(2t-1)(2t-2)\cdots(2t-i+1)}\Big).\end{split}

(83)

Now, observe that

\frac{2t(2t-2)(2t-4)\cdots(2t-2i+4)(2t-2)}{2t(2t-1)(2t-2)\cdots(2t-i+1)}\geq\Big(\frac{2t-2i}{2t}\Big)^{i}=(1-i/t)^{i}\geq 1-i^{2}/t,

where we used that
4\leq i\leq t
and Bernoulli’s inequality. Furthermore,

\frac{2t(2t-2)(2t-4)\cdots(2t-2i+4)(2t-2)}{2t(2t-1)(2t-2)\cdots(2t-i+1)}=\frac{2t-2}{2t-1}\prod_{j=2}^{i-2}\frac{2t-2j}{2t-j-1}<1.

Hence, the desired sum is of order

\begin{split}O(t^{i-1})-O\Big(\frac{(2t)^{i}}{i!}\frac{i^{2}}{t}\Big)=O(t^{i-1}).\end{split}

(84)

It follows that the sum in the small
i
case is bounded by

\sum_{i=4}^{4\log\frac{1}{\lambda}}O(t^{i-1}\lambda^{i})=O(t^{3}\lambda^{4})=o(t^{2}\lambda^{3}),

where again we used
t\lambda=o(1).

Altogether, using that
(1-2\lambda)^{t}\geq 1-2\lambda t=\Theta(1),

\begin{split}&{\mathbf{E}}[(\sigma*\sigma(\mathbf{g}))^{t}]=\Big((1-\lambda)^{2t}+\Theta(t^{2}\lambda^{3})\Big)^{d}\\
&=\Big((1-\lambda)^{2t}(1+\Theta(t^{2}\lambda^{3})\Big)^{d}\\
&=(1-\lambda)^{2dt}(1+\Theta(t^{2}\lambda^{3}))^{d}\\
&=p^{2t}(1+\Theta(dt^{2}\lambda^{3})),\end{split}

(85)

where again we used that
t=o(\lambda^{-1}),

dt^{2}\lambda^{3}=o(1)
and

(1-\lambda)^{d}=p
by definition.

Proof of
Proposition

3.16

We know that
\lambda^{\infty}_{p}
satisfies

(1-\lambda^{\infty}_{p})^{d}=p.
Thus,

\lambda^{\infty}_{p}=1-p^{1/d}=1-\exp(\log p/d)=1-\exp(-(\log 1/p)/d).

By the usual Taylor series expansion,

\exp(-x)=1-x+x^{2}/2+O(x^{3})
when
x=o(1).
Similarly,

\lambda^{\infty}_{p}/(1-\lambda^{\infty}_{p})=p^{-1/d}-1
and we argue in the same way.
