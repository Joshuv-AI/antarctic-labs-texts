On matrix models and their q q -deformations Preprint: UUITP-25/20 arXiv: 2007.10354
Luca Cassia, Rebecca Lodin, and Maxim Zabzine

Abstract
Motivated by the BPS/CFT correspondence, we explore the similarities between the classical β \beta -deformed Hermitean matrix model and the q q -deformed matrix models associated to 3d 𝒩 = 2 \mathcal{N}=2 supersymmetric gauge theories on D 2 × q S 1 D^{2}\times_{q}S^{1} and S b 3 S_{b}^{3} by matching parameters of the theories. The novel results that we obtain are the correlators for the models, together with an additional result in the classical case consisting of the W W -algebra representation of the generating function. Furthermore, we also obtain surprisingly simple expressions for the expectation values of characters which generalize previously known results.

2.1
Definitions

Let us begin by recalling some details about classical matrix models. The simplest example of a matrix model is the Hermitean 1-matrix model (reviewed in

The observables of the theory are the traces

\mathcal{Z}(\tau)=\int_{H_{N}}\mathd\Phi\,\mathe^{-\tr\,V(\Phi)+\sum_{s=1}^{\infty}\tau_{s}\tr\Phi^{s}}\penalty\ .

(1)

Here

N\times N

Hermitean matrices while the measure

is the standard Lebesgue measure on

which is invariant under conjugation by unitary matrices.
The generating function (
1

V
there can be analytical issues with defining these integrals and for any specific choice one should perform an in-depth study.

For a function

or

\langle\mathcal{O}\rangle=\int_{H_{N}}\mathd\Phi\,\mathcal{O}(\Phi)\,\mathe^{-\tr\,V(\Phi)}\penalty\ ,

(2)

\langle\mathcal{O}\rangle_{\tau}=\int_{H_{N}}\mathd\Phi\,\mathcal{O}(\Phi)\,\mathe^{-\tr\,V(\Phi)+\sum_{s=1}^{\infty}\tau_{s}\tr\Phi^{s}}\penalty\ .

(3)

(4)

2.2
Virasoro constraints

It is often very useful in QFT to consider Ward identities for the path integral of the theory at hand. In the case of matrix models the QFT is 0-dimensional and the Ward identities have a very clear differential geometric interpretation. Let the partition function be described as the integral over a domain
X
of the differential form
\Omega
.
If we consider an infinitesimal diffeomorphism generated by the vector field
\xi
over
X
, then we can use the vector to deform infinitesimally the form
\Omega
and then compute the integral of the variation as the Lie derivative, namely

\delta\mathcal{Z}=\int_{X}\mathcal{L}_{\xi}\Omega\penalty\ .

(20)

Since
\Omega
is a top form on
X
, we can write the Lie derivative as an exact form

\mathcal{L}_{\xi}\Omega=\mathd\iota_{\xi}\Omega

(21)

therefore its integral on
X
can only receive contribution by evaluating the form
\iota_{\xi}\Omega
at the boundary of
X
(by Stokes theorem). Assuming that this form vanishes at the boundary, we get a non-trivial constraint equation corresponding to the fact that the variation
\delta\mathcal{Z}
is identically zero.

In the case of the matrix models in Section
2.1
there is a natural family of vector fields given by

\xi_{n}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\lambda_{i}^{n+1}\frac{\partial}{\partial\lambda_{i}},

(22)

which correspond to the generators of a Virasoro Lie algebra
{Vir}
diagonally embedded into
{Vir}^{N}
, so that the vectors
\xi_{n}
are invariant under permutations of the coordinates
\lambda_{i}
.
The differential form
\Omega
is the integrand in (
10

X=\mathbb{R}^{N}
.
Writing explicitly the top form as
\Omega=f(\lambda)\prod_{i=1}^{N}\mathd\lambda_{i}

\mathcal{L}_{\xi_{n}}\Omega=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{\partial}{\partial\lambda_{i}}\left[\lambda_{i}^{n+1}f(\lambda)\right]\prod_{i=1}^{N}\mathd\lambda_{i}

2.3
Solving the constraints

A legitimate question one might ask at this point is how strong are the Virasoro constraints in (
26

\mathcal{Z}^{\mathsf{p}}(\tau)
and if so, what is the degeneracy of the solution? The answer to these questions was found in

\mathcal{Z}^{\mathsf{p}}(\tau)

Morozov_2019

\mathsf{p}=1,2
was also found in the
\beta

We will now review the details of the derivation of the solution to the constraints and the issues one encounters when such a unique solution does not exists.

2.3.1
\mathsf{p}=1

The case
\mathsf{p}=1
is the only one that admits a determinant insertion as discussed in (
14
), thus in what follows we will always assume dependence on
\nu
in the case of
\mathsf{p}=1
. The
n=-1
constraint in (
26
) is not well defined as a differential equation as it contains both
negative powers of the
\{\lambda_{i}\}
as well as additional boundary terms. For these reasons we restrict ourselves to consider only Virasoro constraints for
n\geq 0

\displaystyle a_{1}\partial_{n+1}\mathcal{Z}^{\mathsf{p}=1}(\tau)=\bigg[\beta\sum_{a+b=n}\partial_{a}\partial_{b}

\displaystyle+\left((1-\beta)(n+1)+\nu+2\beta N\right)\partial_{n}+

(28)

\displaystyle+\sum_{s=1}^{\infty}s\tau_{s}\partial_{s+n}+\delta_{n,0}N\left(\nu+\beta(N-1)+1\right)\bigg]\mathcal{Z}^{\mathsf{p}=1}(\tau)\penalty\ ,

where from now on we use
\partial_{n}=\frac{\partial}{\partial\tau_{n}}
to ease notation. To obtain the solution we re-sum all constraints to construct the operator

\mathsf{U}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(n+1)\tau_{n+1}\mathsf{U}_{n}=a_{1}D-W_{-1}

(29)

which we have rewritten as the difference of two operators:
D=\sum_{s=1}^{\infty}s\tau_{s}\partial_{s}
is the dilatation operator and

\displaystyle W_{-1}=

\displaystyle\beta\sum_{n,m=1}^{\infty}(n+m+1)\tau_{n+m+1}\partial_{n}\partial_{m}+\sum_{n,m=1}^{\infty}nm\tau_{n}\tau_{m}\partial_{n+m-1}+

(30)

\displaystyle+\tau_{1}N(\nu+\beta(N-1)+1)+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\left(\nu+(1-\beta)(n+1)+2\beta N\right)(n+1)\tau_{n+1}\partial_{n}

is a “shifted”
W
-algebra generator also called
cut-and-join operator
. Here the word shifted refers to the fact that
W_{-1}
is of degree 1 with respect to the grading introduced by the operator
D
(i.e.
[D,W_{-1}]=W_{-1}
).

Let us analyze the properties of these operators. First we remark that they are linear operators acting on the infinite dimensional vector space underlying the commutative ring
\mathbb{C}[[\tau_{1},\tau_{2},\dots]]
. This vector space has a natural basis over
\mathbb{C}
given by the monomials, i.e. products of times labeled by integer partitions
\rho

\prod_{a\in\rho}\tau_{a}\penalty\ .

(31)

The ordering of the basis of the vector space is the one induced by the ordering on integer partitions, namely ordering by degree and lexicographic ordering between partitions of equal degree

\emptyset<\{1\}<\{1,1\}<\{2\}<\{1,1,1\}<\{2,1\}<\{3\}<\dots\penalty\ .

(32)

With these conventions in place one can show that
\mathsf{U}
is triangular and that
D
is its diagonal part while
W_{-1}
is its off-diagonal part. More precisely, one finds that
\mathsf{U}
is triangular also with respect to the weaker partial order induced by the monomial degree only (partition size).
Moreover, we have that
D
acts on monomials as multiplication by the degree of the corresponding partition,

D\prod_{a\in\rho}\tau_{a}=\big(\sum_{a\in\rho}a\big)\prod_{a\in\rho}\tau_{a}=|\rho|\,\prod_{a\in\rho}\tau_{a}

(33)

so that
\det\mathsf{U}=\det D=0
because there is one zero-eigenvalue corresponding to the empty partition. However, since all other eigenvalues are non-zero, the kernel of
\mathsf{U}
is exactly 1-dimensional. This means that the generating function
\mathcal{Z}^{\mathsf{p}=1}(\tau)
, regarded as a vector in this space, is uniquely defined up to a constant multiplicative factor which corresponds to the normalization of the trivial correlator
c_{\emptyset}
.

The full solution can be derived by recursively solving the equation

a_{1}D\mathcal{Z}_{(d)}^{\mathsf{p}=1}(\tau)=W_{-1}\mathcal{Z}_{(d-1)}^{\mathsf{p}=1}(\tau),\quad\quad\quad\mathcal{Z}_{(d)}^{\mathsf{p}=1}(\tau)=\sum_{\rho\vdash d}\frac{1}{|\Aut(\rho)|}c_{\rho}\prod_{a\in\rho}\tau_{a}

(34)

where
\rho\vdash d
denotes that
\rho
is an integer partition of
d
with
d
being the degree in times. Then, using the fact that
W_{-1}
is of degree 1, we can write

\mathcal{Z}^{\mathsf{p}=1}(\tau)=\sum_{d=0}^{\infty}\frac{W_{-1}^{d}}{a_{1}^{d}d!}\cdot c_{\emptyset}=\exp\left(\frac{W_{-1}}{a_{1}}\right)\cdot c_{\emptyset}\penalty\ .

(35)

A full solution of the
\mathsf{p}=1
model, up to degree 3 is given by the correlators

\displaystyle c_{\{3\}}

\displaystyle=\frac{{N}(\nu+\beta({N}-1)+1)\left(\nu^{2}+5\nu+5\beta\nu({N}-1)+\beta({N}-1)(\beta(5{N}-6)+11)+6\right)}{a_{1}^{3}}c_{{\emptyset}},

(36)

\displaystyle c_{\{2,1\}}

\displaystyle=\frac{{N}(\nu+\beta({N}-1)+1)(\nu+2\beta({N}-1)+2)({N}(\nu+\beta({N}-1)+1)+2)}{a_{1}^{3}}c_{{\emptyset}},

\displaystyle c_{\{1,1,1\}}

\displaystyle=\frac{{N}(\nu+\beta({N}-1)+1)({N}(\nu+\beta({N}-1)+1)+1)({N}(\nu+\beta({N}-1)+1)+2)}{a_{1}^{3}}c_{{\emptyset}},

\displaystyle c_{\{2\}}

\displaystyle=\frac{{N}(\nu+\beta({N}-1)+1)(\nu+2\beta({N}-1)+2)}{a_{1}^{2}}c_{{\emptyset}},

\displaystyle c_{\{1,1\}}

\displaystyle=\frac{{N}(\nu+\beta({N}-1)+1)({N}(\nu+\beta({N}-1)+1)+1)}{a_{1}^{2}}c_{{\emptyset}},

\displaystyle c_{\{1\}}

\displaystyle=\frac{{N}(\nu+\beta({N}-1)+1)}{a_{1}}c_{{\emptyset}}\penalty\ .

Observe that all correlators of degree higher than 1 are proportional to
c_{\{1\}}
, which is a consequence of the fact that
\deg(W_{-1})=1
and that there is only 1 partition in degree 1.

Our solution of the
\mathsf{p}=1
model is slightly more general than the one in
Itoyama:2017xid
;
Mironov:2017och
as we allow for a determinant deformation of parameter
\nu
. For the special case
\nu=0
for the correlators above, we recover the formulas of
Itoyama:2017xid
.

Averages of characters

Another remarkable property of this model is that of
super-integrability

Mironov:2017och
;
Mironov:2018ekq
, meaning that there are some observables whose expectation values satisfy a particularly nice formula. Namely, one observes that expectation values of characters can be expressed as simple combinations of the same characters evaluated at some specific “points” (see
Morozov:2018eiq
for the original observation of this fact).

In the case of the
\beta
-deformed model, the natural characters to consider are the 1-parameter family of symmetric polynomials called Jack polynomials
\mathsf{Jack}_{\rho}(p_{k})
.
Using the solution we derived in (
35
), one can explicitly check that

\left\langle\mathsf{Jack}_{\rho}(p_{k})\right\rangle^{\mathsf{p}=1}=\frac{\mathsf{Jack}_{\rho}(p_{k}=\beta^{-1}(\nu+\beta(N-1)+1))}{\mathsf{Jack}_{\rho}(p_{k}=\beta^{-1}{a_{1}}\delta_{k,1})}\mathsf{Jack}_{\rho}(p_{k}=N)\,c_{\emptyset}\penalty\ .

(37)

Finally, in the limit
\beta=1
, the Jack polynomials degenerate to Schur polynomials (characters of the un-deformed model) whose averages satisfy the analogous relation

\left.\left\langle\mathsf{Schur}_{\rho}(p_{k})\right\rangle^{\mathsf{p}=1}\right|_{\beta=1}=\frac{\mathsf{Schur}_{\rho}(p_{k}=N+\nu)}{\mathsf{Schur}_{\rho}(p_{k}=a_{1}\delta_{k,1})}\mathsf{Schur}_{\rho}(p_{k}=N)\,c_{\emptyset}\penalty\ .

(38)

While we are not aware of an analytical proof of these relations, we have been able to check that they hold for all partitions of degree 9 and lower.

2.3.2
\mathsf{p}=2

The case of
\mathsf{p}=2
is a generalization of the familiar Hermitean Gaussian matrix model, with generating function given by

\mathcal{Z}^{\mathsf{p}=2}(\tau)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\prod_{i=1}^{N}\mathd\lambda_{i}\prod_{1\leq i\neq j\leq N}(\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{j})^{\beta}\mathe^{-a_{1}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\lambda_{i}-\frac{1}{2}a_{2}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\lambda_{i}^{2}+\sum_{s=1}^{\infty}\tau_{s}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\lambda_{i}^{s}}\penalty\ .

(39)

As we here require the
n=-1
constraint in order to solve the model, we let
\nu=0
and thus we can re-sum all the constraints starting from
n=-1
. In order to obtain an operator
\mathsf{U}
whose diagonal is proportional to the dilatation operator
D
, we need to shift the weight of the re-summation as

\mathsf{U}=\sum_{n=-1}^{\infty}(n+2)\tau_{n+2}\mathsf{U}_{n}=a_{2}D-(W_{-2}-a_{1}L_{-1})

(40)

where

\begin{split}W_{-2}=&\beta\sum_{n,m=1}^{\infty}(n+m+2)\tau_{n+m+2}\partial_{n}\partial_{m}+(1-\beta)\sum^{\infty}_{n=1}(n+1)(n+2)\tau_{n+2}\partial_{n}+\\
&+\sum_{n,m=1}^{\infty}nm\tau_{n}\tau_{m}\partial_{n+m-2}+2\beta N\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}(n+2)\tau_{n+2}\partial_{n}+(\beta N^{2}+(1-\beta)N)2\tau_{2}+\tau_{1}^{2}N\end{split}

is an operator of degree 2, while

is defined as in (

\mathsf{p}=1
leads to the conclusion that
\mathsf{U}
is a triangular operator with a 1-dimensional kernel, and therefore that the solution to the equation

In order to give a
W
-algebra representation of the generating function we first consider the simpler case of
a_{1}=0
, then we re-introduce the parameter
a_{1}
by shifting
\tau_{1}\mapsto\tau_{1}-a_{1}
. For

for which one can write

\left.\mathcal{Z}^{\mathsf{p}=2}(\tau)\right|_{a_{1}=0}=\exp\left(\frac{1}{2a_{2}}W_{-2}\right)\cdot c_{\emptyset}\penalty\ .

(42)

Then we use the fact that

[L_{-1},W_{-2}]=0

(43)

together with the Virasoro constraint for

and

to write the full solution as

\displaystyle\mathcal{Z}^{\mathsf{p}=2}(\tau)

\displaystyle=\exp\left(-a_{1}\partial_{1}\right)\left[\left.\mathcal{Z}^{\mathsf{p}=2}(\tau)\right|_{a_{1}=0}\right]

(45)

\displaystyle=\exp\left(-\frac{a_{1}}{a_{2}}L_{-1}\right)\left[\left.\mathcal{Z}^{\mathsf{p}=2}(\tau)\right|_{a_{1}=0}\right]

\displaystyle=\exp\left(\frac{1}{2a_{2}}W_{-2}-\frac{a_{1}}{a_{2}}L_{-1}\right)\cdot c_{\emptyset}\penalty\ .

An explicit solution up to degree 3 is given by the correlators

\displaystyle c_{\{3\}}

\displaystyle=-\frac{a_{1}{N}\left(3a_{2}({\beta}({N}-1)+1)+a_{1}^{2}\right)}{a_{2}^{3}}c_{\emptyset}\penalty\ ,

(46)

\displaystyle c_{\{2,1\}}

\displaystyle=-\frac{a_{1}{N}\left(a_{2}\left({\beta}{N}^{2}-{\beta}{N}+{N}+2\right)+a_{1}^{2}{N}\right)}{a_{2}^{3}}c_{\emptyset}\penalty\ ,

\displaystyle c_{\{1,1,1\}}

\displaystyle=-\frac{a_{1}{N}^{2}\left(a_{1}^{2}{N}+3a_{2}\right)}{a_{2}^{3}}c_{\emptyset}\penalty\ ,

\displaystyle c_{\{2\}}

\displaystyle=\frac{{N}\left(a_{2}({\beta}({N}-1)+1)+a_{1}^{2}\right)}{a_{2}^{2}}c_{\emptyset}\penalty\ ,

\displaystyle c_{\{1,1\}}

\displaystyle=\frac{{N}\left(a_{1}^{2}{N}+a_{2}\right)}{a_{2}^{2}}c_{\emptyset}\penalty\ ,

\displaystyle c_{\{1\}}

\displaystyle=-\frac{a_{1}{N}}{a_{2}}c_{\emptyset}\penalty\ .

Averages of characters

As observed in

\left\langle\mathsf{Jack}_{\rho}(p_{k})\right\rangle^{\mathsf{p}=2}=\frac{\mathsf{Jack}_{\rho}\left(p_{k}=(-1)^{k}\beta^{-1}({a_{1}\delta_{k,1}+a_{2}\delta_{k,2}})\right)}{\mathsf{Jack}_{\rho}(p_{k}=\beta^{-1}{a_{2}}\delta_{k,1})}\mathsf{Jack}_{\rho}(p_{k}=N)\,c_{\emptyset}\penalty\ .

Similarly, in the Schur limit where

\left.\left\langle\mathsf{Schur}_{\rho}(p_{k})\right\rangle^{\mathsf{p}=2}\right|_{\beta=1}=\frac{\mathsf{Schur}_{\rho}\left(p_{k}=(-1)^{k}({a_{1}\delta_{k,1}+a_{2}\delta_{k,2}})\right)}{\mathsf{Schur}_{\rho}(p_{k}=a_{2}\delta_{k,1})}\mathsf{Schur}_{\rho}(p_{k}=N)\,c_{\emptyset}\penalty\ ,

2.3.3
Comments on
\mathsf{p}\geq 3

Now consider higher values of
\mathsf{p}
in (
10
).
By re-summing all constraints in (
26
) for
n\geq-1
with weight
(n+\mathsf{p})\tau_{n+\mathsf{p}}
we obtain the equation

a_{\mathsf{p}}\underbrace{\left(D-\sum_{k=1}^{\mathsf{p}-2}k\tau_{k}\partial_{k}\right)}_{\text{diagonal}}\mathcal{Z}^{\mathsf{p}}(\tau)=\underbrace{\left(W_{-\mathsf{p}}-\sum_{k=1}^{\mathsf{p}-1}a_{\mathsf{p}-k}K_{-k}\right)}_{\text{off-diagonal}}\mathcal{Z}^{\mathsf{p}}(\tau)

(49)

where the r.h.s. is a sum of shifted cut-and-join operators

\displaystyle W_{-\mathsf{p}}=

\displaystyle(\mathsf{p}-1)\tau_{1}\tau_{\mathsf{p}-1}N+(\beta N^{2}+(1-\beta)N)\mathsf{p}\tau_{\mathsf{p}}+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\sum_{m=\mathsf{p}-1}^{\infty}nm\tau_{n}\tau_{m}\partial_{n+m-\mathsf{p}}

(50)

\displaystyle+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\left[2\beta N+(1-\beta)(n+1)\right](n+\mathsf{p})\tau_{n+\mathsf{p}}\partial_{n}+\beta\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}(n+m+\mathsf{p})\tau_{n+m+\mathsf{p}}\partial_{n}\partial_{m}\penalty\ ,

and

K_{-k}:=\sum_{n=\mathsf{p}-1}^{\infty}n\tau_{n}\partial_{n-k}+\delta_{k,\mathsf{p}-1}(\mathsf{p}-1)\tau_{\mathsf{p}-1}N\penalty\ ,

(51)

with
\deg W_{-\mathsf{p}}=\mathsf{p}
and
\deg K_{-k}=k
.
The l.h.s. of (
49
) is of degree zero, therefore it corresponds to the diagonal part of the triangular operator
\mathsf{U}=\sum_{n\geq-1}(n+\mathsf{p})\tau_{n+\mathsf{p}}\mathsf{U}_{n}
, while
W_{-\mathsf{p}}
and
K_{-k}
are of positive degree and therefore they represents the off-diagonal part of
\mathsf{U}
.

For
\mathsf{p}\geq 3
however, we immediately notice that the kernel of
\mathsf{U}
is of dimension greater than 1. The kernel of the operator
D-\sum_{k=1}^{\mathsf{p}-2}k\tau_{k}\partial_{k}
is in fact infinite dimensional and corresponds to the span of all monomials which do not contain times
\tau_{k}
for
k>\mathsf{p}-2
.
For example, if
\mathsf{p}=3
all monomials of the form
\tau_{1}^{\ell}
for all positive integer powers
\ell
are annihilated by the diagonal part of
\mathsf{U}
. This means that equation (
49
) does not provide a recursion relation expressing the corresponding correlator
c_{\{1,\dots,1\}}
as a linear combination of correlators of lower degree.
Consequently one should consider these coefficients as additional background data that needs to be specified independently in order to fully determine the generating function. As remarked in
Cordova:2016jlu
, for finite values of
N
one can always find additional relations between such correlators because only at most
N
of those can be linearly independent for a matrix of finite size. Therefore one can reduce the indeterminacy of the system of equations coming from the Virasoro constraints to a finite amount of information. Nevertheless, one cannot write a full solution for the generating function either in terms of correlators or in
W
-algebra representation.

We now present a formal way to repackage all the information that can be obtained from the recursion (for earlier attempts see
Alexandrov:2003pj
;
Alexandrov:2004ed
;
Alexandrov:2004ud
). From the integral representation of the generating function we can derive the additional identities

\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial\tau_{k}}+k\frac{\partial}{\partial a_{k}}\right)\mathcal{Z}^{\mathsf{p}}(\tau;a)=0\penalty\ ,\quad\quad\quad k=1,\dots,\mathsf{p}

(52)

where we have also explicitly written the dependence of the generating function on the coupling constants
a_{k}
. If we substitute (
52
) in the l.h.s. of (
49
) we can rewrite the term
-\sum_{k=1}^{\mathsf{p}-2}k\tau_{k}\partial_{k}
as the operator

\sum_{k=1}^{\mathsf{p}-2}k^{2}\tau_{k}\frac{\partial}{\partial a_{k}}\penalty\ ,

(53)

which is now no longer of zero degree in the times (in fact, since
\partial/\partial a_{k}
has degree zero, every term in the sum has the same degree as
\tau_{k}
) which means that it is not a diagonal operator.
If we write
\widetilde{W}
for the off-diagonal part of
\mathsf{U}
,

\widetilde{W}:=\frac{W_{-\mathsf{p}}}{a_{\mathsf{p}}}-\sum_{k=1}^{\mathsf{p}-1}\frac{a_{\mathsf{p}-k}}{a_{\mathsf{p}}}K_{-k}-\sum_{k=1}^{\mathsf{p}-2}k^{2}\tau_{k}\frac{\partial}{\partial a_{k}}\penalty\ ,

(54)

we have that (
49
) becomes

D\mathcal{Z}^{\mathsf{p}}(\tau;a)=\widetilde{W}\mathcal{Z}^{\mathsf{p}}(\tau;a)\penalty\ .

(55)

This constraint is still triangular (with respect to a basis of
\mathbb{C}[[\tau_{1},\tau_{2},\dots]]
) but now its diagonal component is the operator
D
, which we know has 1-dimensional kernel and in particular it is invertible over the complement of its kernel.
A formal solution can now be obtained by splitting the generating function as

\mathcal{Z}^{\mathsf{p}}(\tau;a)=c_{\emptyset}(a)+\mathcal{Z}^{\mathsf{p}}_{\perp}(\tau;a)\penalty\ ,

(56)

where
c_{\emptyset}(a)\equiv\mathcal{Z}^{\mathsf{p}}(0;a)
is the component of
\mathcal{Z}^{\mathsf{p}}(\tau;a)
which sits in the kernel of
D
, while
\mathcal{Z}^{\mathsf{p}}_{\perp}(\tau;a)
is the component which sits in the complement of
\ker D
(i.e.
\mathcal{Z}^{\mathsf{p}}_{\perp}(0;a)=0
). Then we can write

\left(D-\widetilde{W}\right)\mathcal{Z}^{\mathsf{p}}_{\perp}(\tau;a)=\widetilde{W}c_{\emptyset}(a)\penalty\ ,

(57)

and observing that
D
and
(D-\widetilde{W})
are invertible operators when restricted to the image of
\widetilde{W}
(which is contained in the complement of
\ker D
), we obtain

\displaystyle\mathcal{Z}^{\mathsf{p}}(\tau;a)

\displaystyle=\left(1+(D-\widetilde{W})^{-1}\widetilde{W}\right)c_{\emptyset}(a)

(58)

\displaystyle=\left(1+(1-D^{-1}\widetilde{W})^{-1}D^{-1}\widetilde{W}\right)c_{\emptyset}(a)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(D^{-1}\widetilde{W})^{n}c_{\emptyset}(a)\penalty\ .

This formal expression for the generating function automatically implements the additional constraints (
52
) but only for
1\leq k\leq\mathsf{p}-2
, precisely because in the operator
\widetilde{W}
the derivatives with respect to
a_{\mathsf{p}-1}
and
a_{\mathsf{p}}
do not appear.
This implies that our solution (
58
) in general does not satisfy (
52
) if
k=\mathsf{p}-1
or
k=\mathsf{p}
. Without loss of generality then we can assume
a_{\mathsf{p}}=1
and
a_{\mathsf{p}-1}=0
and repeat the argument that leads to (
58
). The final answer now is totally unambiguous and only depends on an appropriate choice of the correlation function

c_{\emptyset}(a_{1},\dots,a_{\mathsf{p}-2})\equiv\int_{\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{N}}\prod_{i=1}^{N}\mathd\lambda_{i}\,\Delta(\lambda)^{2\beta}\exp\left(-a_{1}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\lambda_{i}-\dots-\frac{a_{\mathsf{p}-2}}{\mathsf{p}-2}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\lambda_{i}^{\mathsf{p}-2}-\frac{1}{\mathsf{p}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\lambda_{i}^{\mathsf{p}}\right)\penalty\ .

(59)

Because
\widetilde{W}
contains derivatives in the variables
a_{k}
, the recursion relations are no longer polynomial in the correlators
c_{\rho}(a)
. In fact we have that
c_{\emptyset}(a)
acts as a generating function for all the correlators that the recursion could not fix and the formal solution (
58
) expresses them as
a
-derivatives of
c_{\emptyset}(a)
.

For example, if
\mathsf{p}=3
we can write the solution up to degree 4 as

\displaystyle c_{\{4\}}

\displaystyle=\left(a_{1}^{2}{N}-2(\beta({N}-1)+1)\frac{\partial}{\partial a_{1}}\right)c_{\emptyset}(a_{1}),

(60)

\displaystyle c_{\{3,1\}}

\displaystyle=-\left(\left(\beta{N}^{2}-\beta{N}+{N}+1\right)\frac{\partial}{\partial a_{1}}+a_{1}\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial a_{1}}\right)^{2}\right)c_{\emptyset}(a_{1}),

\displaystyle c_{\{2,2\}}

\displaystyle=\left(a_{1}^{2}{N}^{2}-2\frac{\partial}{\partial a_{1}}\right)c_{\emptyset}(a_{1}),

\displaystyle c_{\{2,1,1\}}

\displaystyle=-{N}\left(2\frac{\partial}{\partial a_{1}}+a_{1}\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial a_{1}}\right)^{2}\right)c_{\emptyset}(a_{1}),

\displaystyle c_{\{1,1,1,1\}}

\displaystyle=\left(-\frac{\partial}{\partial a_{1}}\right)^{4}c_{\emptyset}(a_{1}),

\displaystyle c_{\{3\}}

\displaystyle=\left((\beta({N}-1){N}+{N})+a_{1}\frac{\partial}{\partial a_{1}}\right)c_{\emptyset}(a_{1}),

\displaystyle c_{\{2,1\}}

\displaystyle={N}\left(1+a_{1}\frac{\partial}{\partial a_{1}}\right)c_{\emptyset}(a_{1}),

\displaystyle c_{\{1,1,1\}}

\displaystyle=\left(-\frac{\partial}{\partial a_{1}}\right)^{3}c_{\emptyset}(a_{1}),

\displaystyle c_{\{2\}}

\displaystyle=-a_{1}{N}c_{\emptyset}(a_{1}),

\displaystyle c_{\{1,1\}}

\displaystyle=\left(-\frac{\partial}{\partial a_{1}}\right)^{2}c_{\emptyset}(a_{1}),

\displaystyle c_{\{1\}}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\partial}{\partial a_{1}}c_{\emptyset}(a_{1}).

It is curious to notice that while the integral representation of (

) is natural from the point of view of the definition of the matrix model, the solution of the Virasoro constraints does make sense also for an arbitrary function
c_{\emptyset}(a_{1},\dots,a_{\mathsf{p}-2})
which does not necessarily admit an integral representation of that form.

fibration over
S^{1}
such that the
D^{2}
fiber is rotated of a parameter

The
U(1)

Besides the
\mathcal{N}=2
vector multiplet, we consider also an adjoint chiral multiplet of mass

N_{f}
fundamental anti-chiral fields of masses

. Moreover, we also turn on a Fayet-Iliopoulos (FI) parameter

The partition function can be computed via supersymmetric localization

;
Yoshida:2014ssa
with the result

\mathcal{Z}_{D^{2}\times_{q}S^{1}}=\oint_{\mathcal{C}}\prod_{i=1}^{N}\frac{\mathd\lambda_{i}}{\lambda_{i}}\mathcal{Z}_{D^{2}\times_{q}S^{1}}^{\mathrm{cl}}(\lambda)\mathcal{Z}_{D^{2}\times_{q}S^{1}}^{\mathrm{1-loop}}(\lambda)\penalty\ ,

(61)

where the contour

is a middle dimensional cycle in

defined by taking the product of
N

Beem:2012mb

However we will avoid discussing such difficulties and assume that an appropriate contour exists by defining the generating function as an analytically well-defined solution to a set of partial differential equations obtained via algebraic manipulations of the integrand. More specifically, these equations will be the
q
-Virasoro constraints discussed in Section

. In the generic case the two approaches are equivalent.

The integrand of the partition function is defined as the product of the classical contribution

\mathcal{Z}_{D^{2}\times_{q}S^{1}}^{\mathrm{cl}}(\lambda)=\prod_{i=1}^{N}\lambda_{i}^{\kappa_{1}}

(62)

and the product of 1-loop determinants

\mathcal{Z}_{D^{2}\times_{q}S^{1}}^{\mathrm{1-loop}}(\lambda)=\prod_{1\leq k\neq l\leq N}\frac{(\lambda_{k}/\lambda_{l};q)_{\infty}}{(t\lambda_{k}/\lambda_{l};q)_{\infty}}\prod_{j=1}^{N}\prod_{k=1}^{N_{f}}(q\lambda_{j}u_{k};q)_{\infty}\penalty\ ,

(63)

coming from the contributions of the vector, adjoint chiral and fundamental anti-chiral multiplets.
Here

is the
q
-Pochhammer symbol defined in (

The partition function in (

\Delta_{q,t}(\lambda)=\prod_{1\leq k\neq l\leq N}\frac{(\lambda_{k}/\lambda_{l};q)_{\infty}}{(t\lambda_{k}/\lambda_{l};q)_{\infty}}=\prod_{1\leq k\neq l\leq N}\prod_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{1-\lambda_{k}/\lambda_{l}q^{n}}{1-t\lambda_{k}/\lambda_{l}q^{n}}

can be seen as the
q
-deformed Vandermonde determinant, while the remaining contributions can be interpreted as
q
-deformations of the classical potential
V(\lambda_{i})
. The contribution
\mathcal{Z}_{D^{2}\times_{q}S^{1}}^{\mathrm{cl}}(\lambda)
for instance has the form of a determinant insertion, however the actual exponential of the determinant will receive corrections from the measure

In order to make the connection to the potential in (
16

\prod_{j=1}^{N}\prod_{k=1}^{N_{f}}(q\lambda_{j}u_{k};q)_{\infty}=\exp\left(-\sum_{s=1}^{\infty}\frac{p_{s}(u)}{s(q^{-s}-1)}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\lambda_{i}^{s}\right)\penalty\ ,

(65)

where

are the power-sum variables for the masses

p_{s}(u):=\sum_{k=1}^{N_{f}}u_{k}^{s}\penalty\ .

(66)

Once we define the generating function

\mathcal{Z}_{D^{2}\times_{q}S^{1}}^{N_{f}}(\tau)=\oint_{\mathcal{C}}\prod_{i=1}^{N}\frac{\mathd\lambda_{i}}{\lambda_{i}}\mathcal{Z}_{D^{2}\times_{q}S^{1}}^{\mathrm{cl}}(\lambda)\mathcal{Z}_{D^{2}\times_{q}S^{1}}^{\mathrm{1-loop}}(\lambda)\,\mathe^{\sum_{s=1}^{\infty}\tau_{s}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\lambda_{i}^{s}}\penalty\ ,

\tau_{s}\mapsto\tau_{s}-\frac{p_{s}(u)}{s(q^{-s}-1)}\penalty\ .

(68)

Notice however that here all of the times must be shifted, as opposed to the classical case where only a finite number (corresponding to the integer

) had a non-trivial shift.

For a generic (polynomial) operator
\mathcal{O}=\mathcal{O}(\lambda)
we define its expectation value using the notation

\left\langle\mathcal{O}\right\rangle_{\tau}^{N_{f}}=\oint_{\mathcal{C}}\prod_{i=1}^{N}\frac{\mathd\lambda_{i}}{\lambda_{i}}\mathcal{O}(\lambda)\mathcal{Z}_{D^{2}\times_{q}S^{1}}^{\mathrm{cl}}(\lambda)\mathcal{Z}_{D^{2}\times_{q}S^{1}}^{\mathrm{1-loop}}(\lambda)\,\mathe^{\sum_{s=1}^{\infty}\tau_{s}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\lambda_{i}^{s}}\penalty\ ,

(69)

where

admits a formal power series expansion in times, whose coefficients are the correlators

of the theory.

We pause here to explain the physical meaning of the generating function
\mathcal{Z}_{D^{2}\times_{q}S^{1}}^{N_{f}}(\tau)
. From the point of view of the gauge theory on
D^{2}\times_{q}S^{1}
, one is interested in computing expectation values of gauge invariant quantities such as the Wilson loops. These correspond to characters of
U(N)
evaluated on the holonomy of the gauge connection around some BPS closed curve. In the case of the background at hand, there is one BPS loop corresponding to the zero section of the
D^{2}
bundle over
S^{1}
, and it is invariant under the
U(1)
action on the fibers. Since characters of the unitary group are given by the Schur polynomials, one can write any Wilson loop expectation value as the average of some Schur polynomial written on the basis of power-sum variables
p_{s}
,

\mathrm{WL}_{\rho}=\langle\mathsf{Schur}_{\rho}(p_{k}=\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}^{k})\rangle^{N_{f}}\penalty\ ,

(70)

where
\rho
is the integer partition labeling the highest weight of the representation (see Appendix
B
for a short review of symmetric functions and Schur polynomials).

3.1.2
S_{b}^{3}

A different but intimately related model is that of an
\mathcal{N}=2
YM-CS theory on the squashed 3-sphere

. We consider
U(N)
gauge group and the same matter content as before. The 3d geometry is that defined by the equation

\omega_{1}|z_{1}|^{2}+\omega_{2}|z_{2}|^{2}=1,\quad\quad\quad z_{1},z_{2}\in\mathbb{C}

(71)

where
\omega_{1},\omega_{2}\in\mathbb{R}
are the squashing parameters. The dependence of the partition function on the squashing is often indicated via a real parameter

;
Hama:2010av
;
Imamura:2011wg
;
Alday:2013lba

\omega_{1},\omega_{2}\in\mathbb{C}
.

Topologically, we can think of the 3-sphere as the gluing of two solid tori, i.e. two copies of
D^{2}\times S^{1}
whose boundaries are identified via a modular transformation which acts by exchanging the two fundamental cycles of
T^{2}
. Each half of the sphere can then be though to define a copy of the theory on
D^{2}\times_{q_{\alpha}}S^{1}
where now each copy has its own modular parameter

, which we can express through the squashing parameters of the sphere as

q_{1}=\mathe^{2\pi\mathi\frac{\omega_{2}}{\omega_{1}}},\quad\quad\quad q_{2}=\mathe^{2\pi\mathi\frac{\omega_{1}}{\omega_{2}}}\penalty\ .

(72)

This simple geometric picture eventually leads to the very non-trivial property of factorization of the
S^{3}_{b}
partition function into a product of holomorphic blocks
Pasquetti:2011fj
. Here we are interested in yet another consequence of this factorization, namely the fact that the 3-sphere partition function satisfies two independent sets of
q
-Virasoro constraints as shown in
Nedelin:2016gwu
, hence the name
modular double

As in the previous model, the theory on
S^{3}_{b}
has an
\mathcal{N}=2
vector, an adjoint chiral of mass

and
N_{f}
anti-chiral fundamental fields of masses

-Virasoro constraints which can be written as PDEs in the time variables. In the case

this was first shown in

where a unit CS level had to be introduced.
Here we generalize that condition to arbitrary number of flavors

by imposing that

N_{f}=2\kappa_{2}\penalty\ ,

(73)

or equivalently, that the effective

Remark
.
As observed in the pole analysis of the partition function in
(
Cassia:2019sjk
, Appendix C)
, the choice of shift operator
\hat{\mathsf{M}}_{i,\alpha}
is motivated by the requirement that when we shift the contour of integration in the LHS of the constraint equation in order to reabsorb the action of the shift in (
97
), we should not cross any poles of the integrand.
Since the only poles that can come in between the two contours are the ones due to the fundamental matter fields, the choice of the chirality of these fields is restricted by the sign of the shift in the variables. Namely, for negative shift of the
X_{i}
variables, we need to shift the contour to the left and therefore we cannot have any poles to the left of the imaginary axis. This implies that all fundamental matter fields should be anti-chiral rather than chiral. Their precise number is then fixed by the value of the bare CS level as in (
73
).
However, if we were to use the opposite shift operator
\hat{\mathsf{M}}_{i,\alpha}^{-1}
, we would need to shift the contour in the opposite direction, and by doing so we might cross some poles along the way. To avoid this problem we can substitute all anti-chiral fundamental multiplets with chiral fundamentals. The change of chirality inverts the position of the poles of the corresponding double sine functions thus allowing us to shift the contour.
By reproducing the analysis of the
q
-Virasoro constraints we find that the corresponding condition on the number of fundamental chiral fields is
N_{f}=-2\kappa_{2}
, which can only be satisfied for negative bare CS level. The rest of the derivation is completely equivalent provided we adopt the substitution of parameters

q_{\alpha}\mapsto q_{\alpha}^{-1},\quad t_{\alpha}\mapsto t_{\alpha}^{-1},\quad r_{\alpha}\mapsto r_{\alpha}^{-1},\quad u_{k,\alpha}\mapsto u_{k,\alpha}^{-1}.

(105)

This is indeed compatible with the symmetry properties of the partition function
proved in
(
vdBult:2007
, Proposition 5.3.16)
.

Averages of Macdonald Polynomials

From the point of view of the

These polynomials are the natural quantum generalization of the Schur polynomials and as such they can be interpreted as the correct quantum characters of the model. We now show that their averages do indeed satisfy a special property of the form
\langle\mathsf{character}\rangle=\mathsf{character}

Introducing the functions

\pi^{(N)}_{k}:=\frac{t^{\frac{k}{2}N}-t^{-\frac{k}{2}N}}{t^{\frac{k}{2}}-t^{-\frac{k}{2}}},\quad\quad\quad\hat{\pi}^{(N)}_{k}:=r^{\frac{k}{2}}q^{\frac{k}{2}}t^{k(N-1)}\frac{r^{\frac{k}{2}}q^{\frac{k}{2}}t^{\frac{k}{2}(N-1)}-r^{-\frac{k}{2}}q^{-\frac{k}{2}}t^{-\frac{k}{2}(N-1)}}{t^{\frac{k}{2}}-t^{-\frac{k}{2}}}

\left\langle\mathsf{Macdonald}_{\rho}(p_{k})\right\rangle^{N_{f}=1}=\frac{\mathsf{Macdonald}_{\rho}(p_{k}=\hat{\pi}^{(N)}_{k})}{\mathsf{Macdonald}_{\rho}(p_{k}=\delta^{\ast}_{k,1})}\,\mathsf{Macdonald}_{\rho}(p_{k}=\pi^{(N)}_{k})\,c_{\emptyset}\penalty\ .

In the semi-classical limit

In order to make the semi-classical limit

Evaluating the average of Macdonald polynomials on the explicit solution that we found, we are able to write the following identity

\left\langle\mathsf{Macdonald}_{\rho}(p_{k})\right\rangle^{N_{f}=2}=\frac{\mathsf{Macdonald}_{\rho}\left(p_{k}=(-1)^{k}t^{\frac{k}{2}{N}}\frac{\left(u_{1}^{k}+u_{2}^{k}\right)}{1-t^{k}}\right)}{\mathsf{Macdonald}_{\rho}\left(p_{k}=(-1)^{k}t^{\frac{k}{2}}\frac{(u_{1}u_{2})^{k}}{1-t^{k}}\right)}\mathsf{Macdonald}_{\rho}(p_{k}=\pi_{k}^{(N)})\,c_{\emptyset}\penalty\ ,

3.3.3
Comments on
N_{f}\geq 3

Our analysis indicates that the only cases that admit a full and unique solution (independent of the normalization of the empty correlator) are those of
N_{f}=1,2
. For higher values of
N_{f}
we have a similar situation to that of the classical models at
\mathsf{p}\geq 3
. The
q
-Virasoro constraints have an infinite dimensional kernel, corresponding to the fact that one needs to specify more initial conditions to solve the recursion.
We believe that the situation here mirrors what we discussed in Section
2.3.3
, however the actual formulas are too cumbersome and unilluminating to write down explicitly. We remark however that computer calculations suggest that the actual recursion relations can be solved iteratively by the same procedure delineated there.
We do however expect that a more subtle study of possible analytical issues is required in this
q
-deformed case, especially with regards to the dependence on the mass parameters
u_{k}
. The definition of the matrix model for instance might have ambiguities related to the choice of contour, similar to the so called Dijkgraaf-Vafa phase of a classical matrix model
Dijkgraaf:2002fc
;
Dijkgraaf:2002vw
.

The semi-classical behavior is also not so straightforward.
This limit can, in fact, be defined by the choice of masses which solves the equations

p_{k}(u)=q^{-k}(1-q^{k})a_{k}\penalty\ ,\quad\quad\quad k=1,\dots,N_{f}\penalty\ .

(143)

In terms of the
u_{k}
, these form a system of
N_{f}
equations of increasing degree up to
N_{f}
. Over the complex numbers there are
N_{f}!
solutions which are all equivalent upon permutations of the
u_{k}
, however writing such a solution explicitly is in general not possible. What we expect is that upon substitution in (
68
) we get a shift of the times such that only the first
N_{f}
terms are non-vanishing in the limit
q\to 1
. Then the constants
a_{k}
parametrizing the solution, can be identified with the coupling constants of the semi-classical model where
\mathsf{p}=N_{f}
.

4
Conclusion

In this paper we give an outline of the procedure to obtain and recursively solve the Virasoro and
q
-Virasoro constraints in the case of the
classical

\beta
-deformed Hermitean 1-matrix model and the
quantum
3d
\mathcal{N}=2
theory with
U(N)
gauge group on
D^{2}\times_{q}S^{1}
and
S_{b}^{3}
. We present the solution of the models in terms of explicit expressions for the first few correlators, and additionally in the classical case we can also express the solution using the
W
-representation of the generating function. Moreover, we deduce novel formulas of the form
\langle\mathsf{character}\rangle=\mathsf{character}
in the spririt of
Morozov:2018eiq
as given in (
37
), (
47
), (
129
) and (
142
).
Finally, we explicitly match the classical models with their
q
-deformations by showing the existence of a well-defined semi-classical expansion around a formal deformation parameter
q=\mathe^{\hbar}
, for small
\hbar
.

There are several directions for further investigation.

•

One obvious direction is to extend the
W
-representation of the generating function to the quantum models, an investigation which has been initiated in
Morozov_2019
. This is a much more difficult task than in the classical case due to the more involved constraint equations.

•

Another interesting direction might be to try to find an analytical proof of the formulas for expectation values of characters. These were all unexpected a priori, and the formulas appearing in this paper have only been verified by explicitly checking the equations for partitions up to some finite degree.

•

Another open question is regarding the condition (
73
) on the effective CS level. In particular how one can treat models when
\kappa_{2}^{\textrm{eff}}
is different from zero.

•

Furthermore, another question is whether the procedure of obtaining and solving the Virasoro constraints can be generalized to other root systems, in the sense that the above derivation is valid only for a
U(N)
gauge group or
A_{N}
from a matrix model perspective. The question is then if there are other versions of the derivation for other Lie groups, which then both offers a definition of the matrix model, together with a set of Virasoro constraints which may or may not be solvable.

•

Another interesting question to consider, is why the system of equations in the Virasoro constraints are triangular and give rise to a finite recursion relation. From the explicit Virasoro constraints it is clear that the equations are triangular, but why this is so in the first place is not obvious.

•

There is also the question if there is a deeper reason to why
\mathsf{p}\geq 3
in the classical case and
N_{f}\geq 3
in the quantum case cannot be solved completely. At the level of the constraint equations we understand why this is not possible, but it is still not clear if there is a more fundamental physical reason to why this is the case. Following the parallel with the classical models at
p\geq 3
one is lead to expect that the quantum models at
N_{f}\geq 3
present ambiguities in the
q
-Virasoro solution due to the presence of multiple inequivalent phases of the Dijkgraaf-Vafa type, which would be induced by Stokes phenomena appearing in the choice of integration contour. Further analytical studies are required to determine if this picture is correct.

•

Finally, one can speculate on the physical implications of our results for the corresponding 3d
\mathcal{N}=2
gauge theories. It was observed in
Nazzal:2018brc
;
Razamat:2018zel
that a certain type of
q
-difference operator acting on a 4d
\mathcal{N}=1
index could be realized explicitly as the insertion of a supersymmetric surface defect in spacetime. One might reasonably expect that the Macdonald operator in (
100
) also can be realized similarly as the insertion of a supersymmetric defect in 3d. In particular, since the 4d surface defect wraps around the
S^{1}
direction, upon compactification to 3d one should expect the defect to become a line defect wrapping some equator of the
S^{3}_{b}
. We leave this question for future investigations.

(z;q)_{\infty}=\prod_{k=0}^{\infty}(1-zq^{k})\penalty\ ,

(144)

with
z\in\mathbb{C}
and
|q|<1
. The analytic continuation to the region
|q|>1
is given by

(z;q)_{\infty}=\frac{1}{\left(q^{-1}z;q^{-1}\right)_{\infty}}\penalty\ .

(145)

When
|z|<1
and
|q|\neq 1
one can define the quantum dilogarithm
Faddeev:1993rs

\mathrm{Li}_{2}(z;q)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{z^{n}}{n(1-q^{n})}\penalty\ ,

(146)

which can be used to rewrite the
q
-Pochhammer as

(z;q)_{\infty}=\exp\left(-\mathrm{Li}_{2}(z;q)\right)\penalty\ .

(147)

Secondly, we introduce the double sine function
Kurokawa:2003
;
Narukawa:2003
.
For
\underline{\omega}\equiv(\omega_{1},\omega_{2})\in\mathbb{C}^{2}
with
\mathrm{Re}(\omega_{1})>0
,
\mathrm{Re}(\omega_{2})>0
and
z\in\mathbb{C}
,
the double sine function is defined by the regularized infinite product

S_{2}\left(z|\underline{\omega}\right)=\prod_{n_{1},n_{2}\geq 0}\frac{n_{1}\omega_{1}+n_{2}\omega_{2}+z}{n_{1}\omega_{1}+n_{2}\omega_{2}+\omega-z}\penalty\ ,\quad\quad\quad\omega=\omega_{1}+\omega_{2}\penalty\ ,

(148)

which satisfies the inversion property

S_{2}\left(z|\underline{\omega}\right)S_{2}\left(\omega-z|\underline{\omega}\right)=1\penalty\ .

(149)

For generic values of the parameters
\omega_{1},\omega_{2}
such that
\mathrm{Im}(\frac{\omega_{2}}{\omega_{1}})\neq 0
, the double Sine function has the following infinite product representation

S_{2}(z|\underline{\omega})=\mathe^{\frac{\mathi\pi}{2}B_{22}(z|\underline{\omega})}\left(\mathe^{\frac{2\pi\mathi}{\omega_{1}}z};\mathe^{2\pi\mathi\frac{\omega}{\omega_{1}}}\right)_{\infty}\left(\mathe^{\frac{2\pi\mathi}{\omega_{2}}z};\mathe^{2\pi\mathi\frac{\omega}{\omega_{2}}}\right)_{\infty}\penalty\ ,

where

B_{22}(z|\underline{\omega})=\frac{1}{\omega_{1}\omega_{2}}\left(\left(z-\frac{\omega}{2}\right)^{2}-\frac{\omega_{1}^{2}+\omega_{2}^{2}}{12}\right)\penalty\ .

Appendix B
Symmetric functions and characters

The Schur polynomials denoted by
\mathsf{Schur}_{\gamma}(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{N})
are labeled by partitions
\gamma
and are defined as the irreducible characters of the group
U(N)
. As such they form an orthonormal (linear) basis in the space of all polynomial characters which, by definition, are invariant under the action of the Weyl group
S_{N}
. This means that Schur polynomials form a basis for all symmetric functions.

For any partition
\gamma=\{\gamma_{1},\dots,\gamma_{N}\}
whose elements obey
\gamma_{1}\geq\dots\geq\gamma_{N}\geq 0
one can compute the Schur polynomial
\mathsf{Schur}_{\gamma}(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{N})
as a ratio of determinants as in the Weyl character formula

\mathsf{Schur}_{\gamma}(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{N})=\frac{\det\limits_{ij}\,\lambda_{i}^{N+\gamma_{j}-j}}{\det\limits_{ij}\,\lambda_{i}^{N-j}}\penalty\ .

(152)

Introducing the power-sum variables
p_{k}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\lambda_{i}^{k}
one can expand Schur polynomials as

\mathsf{Schur}_{\gamma}(p_{k})=\sum_{\rho}\frac{\chi^{\gamma}_{\rho}}{|{\Aut}(\rho)|}\prod_{a\in\rho}\frac{p_{a}}{a}\penalty\ ,

(153)

where
\chi^{\gamma}_{\rho}
is the character of the representation of the symmetric group indexed by the partition
\gamma
evaluated at elements of cycle type
\rho
. Here we also introduced the notation
{\Aut}(\rho)
for the automorphism group of the partition
\rho
, namely the group of permutations of the parts of
\rho
which are of equal length. The order of this group can be computed as

|{\Aut}(\rho)|=\prod_{a\in\rho}\frac{\partial}{\partial\tau_{a}}\cdot\prod_{b\in\rho}\tau_{b}\penalty\ .

(154)

For symmetric Schur polynomials (
153
) takes the simpler form

\mathsf{Schur}_{\{m\}}(p_{k})=\sum_{\gamma\vdash m}\frac{1}{|\Aut(\gamma)|}\prod_{a\in\gamma}\frac{p_{a}}{a}\penalty\ ,

(155)

where
\gamma\vdash m
denotes that
\gamma
is an integer partition of
m
.

If we define the scalar product of two symmetric functions by

(f|g)=\frac{1}{(2\pi\mathi)^{N}N!}\oint_{|\lambda|=1}\prod_{i=1}^{N}\frac{\mathd\lambda_{i}}{\lambda_{i}}\Delta(\lambda)\Delta(\lambda^{-1})f(\lambda)g(\lambda^{-1})\penalty\ ,

(156)

then Schur polynomials are orthogonal

(\mathsf{Schur}_{\gamma}|\mathsf{Schur}_{\rho})=\delta_{\gamma,\rho}\penalty\ .

(157)

Schur polynomials also satisfy the Cauchy identity
(
Macdonald
, Chapter I, (4.3))

\exp\left(\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{\tau_{k}p_{k}}{k}\right)=\sum_{\gamma}\mathsf{Schur}_{\gamma}\left(\tau_{k}\right)\mathsf{Schur}_{\gamma}\left(p_{k}\right)\penalty\ ,

(158)

where the summation on the right hand side is over all partitions
\gamma
.
Using the plethystic substitution given by
\tau_{k}=z^{k}
together with (
155
) we obtain the useful formula

\exp\left(\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{z^{k}p_{k}}{k}\right)=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}z^{m}\mathsf{Schur}_{\{m\}}(p_{1},\dots,p_{m})\penalty\ .

(159)

The Jack and Macdonald polynomials are defined as 1- or 2-parameter deformations of the Schur polynomials (see
Macdonald
). More specifically, for any
\beta\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
we define Jack polynomials
\mathsf{Jack}_{\gamma}(p_{k})
as the symmetric functions orthogonal with respect to the inner product

(f|g)_{\beta}=\frac{1}{(2\pi\mathi)^{N}N!}\oint_{|\lambda|=1}\prod_{i=1}^{N}\frac{\mathd\lambda_{i}}{\lambda_{i}}\left(\Delta(\lambda)\Delta(\lambda^{-1})\right)^{\beta}f(\lambda)g(\lambda^{-1})\penalty\ .

(160)

Given parameters
|q|<1
and
|t|<1
we can define Macdonald polynomials
\mathsf{Macdonald}_{\gamma}(p_{k})
as a family of symmetric functions orthogonal with respect to the inner product

(f|g)_{q,t}=\frac{1}{(2\pi\mathi)^{N}N!}\oint_{|\lambda|=1}\prod_{i=1}^{N}\frac{\mathd\lambda_{i}}{\lambda_{i}}\Delta_{q,t}(\lambda)f(\lambda)g(\lambda^{-1})\penalty\ .

(161)

For concreteness we only consider Jack and Macdonald polynomials in the
P
-basis.
In the limit
t\to q
, Macdonald polynomials degenerate to Schur polynomials, while for
t=q^{\beta}
they give the Jack polynomials.

Appendix C
Relating constraint generators to

We now wish to obtain a relation between the generators of the constraint
