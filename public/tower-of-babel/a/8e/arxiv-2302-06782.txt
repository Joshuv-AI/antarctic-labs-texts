Finite-sample bounds to the normal limit under group sequential sampling
Julian Aronowitz, Jay Bartroff

Abstract
In group sequential analysis, data is collected and analyzed in batches until pre-defined stopping criteria are met. Inference in the parametric setup typically relies on the limiting asymptotic multivariate normality of the repeatedly computed maximum likelihood estimators (MLEs), a result first rigorously proved by Jennison and Turnbull, 1997 under general regularity conditions. In this work, using Stein’s method we provide optimal order, non-asymptotic bounds on the distance for smooth test functions between the joint group sequential MLEs and the appropriate normal distribution under the same conditions. Our results assume independent observations but allow heterogeneous (i.e., non-identically distributed) data. We examine how the resulting bounds simplify when the data comes from an exponential family. Finally, we present a general result relating multivariate Kolmogorov distance to smooth function distance which, in addition to extending our results to the former metric, may be of independent interest.

Introduction

Sequential analysis is a powerful statistical framework in which sample sizes are not determined before conducting a study, and is the dominant statistical methodology in many types of clinical trials
(
Bartroff et al., 2013
)
, among other applications. In clinical trials in particular, data is often collected and analyzed in
groups
until the conditions of a pre-defined stopping criteria are met. In their authoritative textbook on this topic,

note that group sequential analysis can significantly reduce the time required to conduct clinical trials over the traditional fixed sample size designs.

Group sequential analysis is typically based on repeatedly computed (after each group) maximum likelihood estimators (MLEs), and therefore all the properties of the statistical test (e.g., expected sample size, type I error probability, power) are functions of the
joint
distribution of these repeatedly computed MLEs. Suppose independent but not necessarily identically distributed observations
Y_{1},\dots,Y_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{t}
are divided into
K

denoting the number of observations seen up to and including group
k
for

. Let

be the probability mass or density function of

with
\theta\in\Theta\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
. We let
\hat{\theta}_{k}=\hat{\theta}_{k}(Y_{1},Y_{2},\dots,Y_{n_{k}})\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times 1}
be the MLE based on the observations in the first
k
groups and

Natural questions that then arise from this formulation are how many samples are required, and in which groups should the samples be allocated, for the asymptotics to ‘kick in’ and a normal approximation to become appropriate without introducing too much error? One form of an answer to this question would be an upper bound on the error of the multivariate normal approximation of the group sequential MLE. In this work we provide an optimal order bound on the distance between the group sequential MLE and the appropriate normal distribution under the same regularity conditions with heterogeneous independent data (our Theorem
3.1

Anastasiou, 2018

Background

2.1

Stein’s Method

The results detailed below make heavy use of Stein’s method bounds in combination with multivariate Taylor series arguments. We do not attempt to give a complete introduction to Stein’s method here, but rather focus on the relevant multivariate results. Readers interested in a more broad introduction are referred to
Chen et al., 2010

Stein’s method for multivariate normal approximations hinges on the following multivariate version of the Stein equation. Let
f:\mathbb{R}^{q}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be twice differentiable and
D^{2}
the second derivative, or Hessian matrix, of
f
. Then for

a multivariate Stein equation is

\mathrm{Tr}D^{2}f(\bm{w})-\bm{w}\cdot\nabla f(\bm{w})=h(\bm{w})-Nh.

Here
Nh
denotes the expectation
\mathbb{E}h(Z)
, where
Z
is a
q
-dimensional standard normal vector.
The following bound of
Goldstein and Rinott, 1996
relates derivatives of the Stein equation solution

to those of the test function
h
:

\left\lVert{\frac{\partial^{k}f(\bm{w})}{\prod_{j=1}^{k}\partial w_{i_{j}}}}\right\rVert\leq\frac{1}{k}\left\lVert{\frac{\partial^{k}h(\bm{w})}{\prod_{j=1}^{k}\partial w_{i_{j}}}}\right\rVert,\quad k\geq 1,

(3)

when the
k
th partial derivative of
h
exists.

\displaystyle c=\max_{k\in\{1,\dots,K\}}\left|{\bar{I}^{-1/2}_{n}(G_{k};\theta_{0})}\right|.

.

Although the bound in the theorem appears complex, the fact that it is in general of optimal order
\mathcal{O}(n^{-1/2})
is not hard to see. Assume that
[\bar{I}_{n}(k,\theta_{0})]_{j}=\mathcal{O}(1)
. By Theorem
2.3
,

\sqrt{n}\mathbb{E}[\hat{\theta}_{k}-\theta_{0}]\rightarrow 0_{d}.

Thus
\mathbb{E}[\hat{\theta}_{k}-\theta_{0}]_{j}=o(1/\sqrt{n})
. Again from Theorem
2.3
,

n\bar{I}^{\frac{1}{2}}_{n}(k,\theta_{0})\mathrm{Cov}[\hat{\theta}_{k}]\bar{I}^{\frac{1}{2}}_{n}(k,\theta_{0})\rightarrow 1_{d\times d}.

Using
[\bar{I}_{n}(k,\theta_{0})]_{j}=\mathcal{O}(1)
, we see that
n\mathrm{Var}\left[{[\hat{\theta_{k}}]_{j}}\right]\rightarrow 1
and thus
\mathrm{Var}\left[{[\hat{\theta_{k}}]_{j}}\right]=\mathcal{O}(1/n)
. It follows that

\mathbb{E}([\hat{\theta}_{k}-\theta_{0}]^{2}_{j})=\mathrm{Var}\left[{[\hat{\theta_{k}}]_{j}}\right]+(\mathbb{E}[\hat{\theta}_{k}-\theta_{0}]_{j})^{2}=\mathcal{O}(n^{-1}).

(6)

From
(R2)
and
(R6)
and independence of

\mathbb{E}\Big(\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\theta_{i}\partial\theta_{l}}\ell_{k}(\theta_{0},Y)+n\bar{I}_{n}(k,\theta_{0})_{li}\Big)^{2}=\sum_{j=1}^{n_{k}}\mathrm{Var}\left[{\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\theta_{i}\partial\theta_{l}}\log(f_{j}(Y_{j}\mid\theta_{0}))}\right]

(7)

showing that the left hand side of the above equation is

that

and

4

Application to Observations from an Exponential Family

In this section we specialize Theorem
3.1
to the case where observations are i.i.d. from an exponential family. We then show that this bound is of optimal order
\mathcal{O}(n^{-1/2})
and calculate the bound explicitly in the case of lifetime data from an exponential distribution.

4.1

Notation and Setup

We slightly modify some of our notation to be more in line with the exponential family literature. We say that the distribution of
Y
is a canonical multi-parameter exponential family distribution if for
\bm{\eta}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
the density of
Y
is,

f(y;\bm{\eta})=\exp\left\{\sum_{j=1}^{d}\eta_{j}T_{j}(y)-A(\bm{\eta})+S(y)\right\}\mathbbm{1}_{\{y\in B\}}

(8)

where
B
is the support of
y
that does not depend on
\bm{\eta}
, and
T(y)=[T_{1}(y),\dots,T_{d}(y)]^{\intercal}
is the natural sufficient statistic. Here, the natural parameter
\bm{\eta}
plays the role of the parameter of interest
\theta
above. We will use
\eta
and
\bm{\eta}
interchangeably as long as the multidimensionality of
\eta
is clear from the context. The cumulant function
A(\eta)
satisfies

\frac{\partial}{\partial\eta_{i}}A(\eta)=\mathbb{E}_{\eta}\left[{T_{i}(Y)}\right],\quad\mathrm{Var}_{\eta}\left[{T(Y)}\right]=H(A(\eta))=\nabla_{\eta}^{2}A(\eta).

(9)

To ease the notational burden we use the following simplified notation for the mixed moments of the sufficient statistics:

\displaystyle\mu_{i}=\mu_{i}(\eta)=\mathbb{E}_{\eta}\left[{T_{i}(Y)}\right]

\displaystyle\mu_{ij}=\mu_{ij}(\eta)=\mathbb{E}_{\eta}\left[{T_{i}(Y)T_{j}(Y)}\right]

\displaystyle\mu_{ijk}=\mu_{ijk}(\eta)=\mathbb{E}_{\eta}\left[{T_{i}(Y)T_{j}(Y)T_{k}(Y)}\right].

In Section
4.2
we will also make use of the third partial derivative of
A(\eta)
which appear in Theorem
3.1
. We have

\frac{\partial^{3}}{\partial\eta_{i}\partial\eta_{j}\partial\eta_{k}}A(\eta)=\int\exp\{S(y)\}T_{i}(y)\bigg[\\
\left(T_{j}(y)-\frac{\partial}{\partial\eta_{j}}A(\eta)\right)\left(T_{k}(y)-\frac{\partial}{\partial\eta_{k}}A(\eta)\right)a(y,\eta)-a(y,\eta)\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\eta_{j}\partial\eta_{k}}A(\eta)\bigg]dy\\
=\mu_{ijk}-\mu_{ij}\mu_{k}-\mu_{ik}\mu_{j}+\mu_{i}\mu_{j}\mu_{k}-\mathrm{Cov}\left[{T_{j}(Y)},{T_{k}(Y)}\right]\mu_{i}\\
=\mu_{ijk}-\mu_{ij}\mu_{k}-\mu_{ik}\mu_{j}-\mu_{jk}\mu_{i}+2\mu_{i}\mu_{j}\mu_{k}.

(10)

Define

\mu_{iul}^{\epsilon}=\mu_{iul}^{\epsilon}(\eta_{0})=\max_{\{\eta:\left|{\eta_{j}-\eta_{0,j}}\right|<\epsilon,\;\forall j\in\{1,\dots,d\}\}}\left|{\mu_{ilk}-\mu_{il}\mu_{k}-\mu_{ik}\mu_{l}-\mu_{lk}\mu_{i}+2\mu_{i}\mu_{l}\mu_{k}}\right|

(11)

The joint cumulant generating function of
W=(T_{1}(Y),\dots,T_{d}(Y))^{\intercal}
is then

K(s)=\log\mathbb{E}\left[{e^{s^{\intercal}W}}\right]=A(s+\eta)-A(\eta).

Via this expression, the cumulants can be found by taking derivatives of
A(\eta)
, as follows:

\nabla_{s^{n}}K(s)\big\rvert_{s=0}=\nabla_{s^{n}}A(s+\eta)\big\rvert_{s=0}=\nabla_{\eta^{n}}A(\eta).

Thus partial derivatives of
A(\eta)
yield joint cummulants of
[T_{1}(Y),\dots,T_{d}(Y)]
which in turn are polynomial functions of the moments of
[T_{1}(Y),\dots,T_{d}(Y)]
.

We now turn to MLEs of exponential families, which we denote by
\hat{\eta}
. The score function is

\nabla_{\eta}\ell(y^{n};\eta)=\nabla_{\eta}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{d}\eta_{j}T_{j}(y_{i})-A(\eta)+S(y_{i})\right)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}T(y_{i})-n\nabla_{\eta}A(\eta)

amd setting this equal to zero and using (
9
) yields

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}T(y_{i})=\nabla_{\eta}A(\eta)\big\rvert_{\eta=\hat{\eta}}=\mathbb{E}_{\hat{\eta}}\left[{T(Y)}\right].

Since the log-likelihood of an exponential family is strictly concave, if the MLE exists it is a global maximum. The mean function is

\tau(\eta)=[\tau_{1}(\eta),\dots,\tau_{d}(\eta)]^{\intercal}=[\mathbb{E}_{\eta}\left[{T_{1}(Y)}\right],\dots,\mathbb{E}_{\eta}\left[{T_{d}(Y)}\right]]^{\intercal},

we write compactly as

\tau(\hat{\eta})=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}T(y_{i})\quad\mbox{or, equivalently,}\quad\hat{\eta}=\tau^{-1}\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}T(y_{i})\right).

(12)

We note that (
12
) matches the form (
2
) and thus the bounds found in
Anastasiou and Ley, 2017
and
Anastasiou and Gaunt, 2018
can be used on the distance to normality for MLEs of exponential families. This fact was noted in
Anastasiou and Ley, 2017
for single parameter exponential families but the generalization to multi-parameter exponential families was not explicitly addressed in
Anastasiou and Gaunt, 2018
.

4.2

Bound to Normality for MLEs of Exponential Families

With the notation in place we are ready to state the specialization of Theorem
3.1
to exponential families in Corollary
4.1
. The bound (
13
) that results can be written completely in terms of mixed moments up to the third order of the sufficient statistics
T_{1}(Y),\dots,T_{d}(Y)
, the true value of the natural parameter
\eta_{0}
, and the sample. Additional knowledge of the distribution, in particular the cumulant function
A(\eta)
and
S(Y)
, is not required. Although the bound in Corollary
4.1
inherits the optimal order of
n^{-1/2}
from Theorem
3.1
, a direct analysis of the bound’s order is more straightforward due to the observations being i.i.d., and we carry this out following the corollary in Section
4.3
.

Corollary 4.1
.

Let
Y_{1},Y_{2},\dots,Y_{n}
be i.i.d with probability density (or mass) function
f(y|\eta)
, for which the parameter space is an open subset of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Suppose
f
is an exponential family defined by (
8
) and the observations
Y_{1},Y_{2},\dots,Y_{n_{k}}
are available at analysis
k=1,\dots,K
. Assume that the MLE
\hat{\eta}_{k}
exists and that conditions
(R1)
-
(R6)
are satisfied at each analysis
k
. Let
\{Y^{\prime}_{i},i=1,2,\dots,n\}
be an independent copy of
\{Y_{i},i=1,2,\dots,n\}
. For
Z\sim N_{q}(0_{q},1_{q\times q}),h\in\mathcal{H}
, where
\mathcal{H}
is as in (
1
) and
\mu^{\epsilon}_{iul}
defined by (
11
), it holds that

\left|{\mathbb{E}[h(\sqrt{n}J^{-\frac{1}{2}}_{n}(\hat{\eta}^{K}-\eta_{0}^{K}))]-\mathbb{E}[h(Z)]}\right|\leq\frac{\left\lVert{h}\right\rVert_{1}}{\sqrt{n}}K_{1}(\eta_{0})\\
+\frac{q^{2}c^{2}\left\lVert{h}\right\rVert_{2}}{4}K_{2}(\eta_{0})+\frac{q^{3}c^{3}\left\lVert{h}\right\rVert_{3}}{12}K_{3}(\eta_{0})+\frac{2\left\lVert{h}\right\rVert}{\epsilon^{2}}\mathbb{E}\big[\sum_{j=1}^{q}Q_{j}^{2}\big],

(13)

where,

\displaystyle K_{1}(\eta_{0})=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k_{1}=1}^{K}\sum_{k_{2}=k_{1}}^{\min\{k_{1}+1,K\}}n_{k_{1}}\left(\frac{\left|{G_{k_{2}}}\right|}{n}\right)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\sum_{l=1}^{d}\sum_{j=1}^{d}\mathrm{Var}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\left[{T(Y)}\right]_{lj}\Bigg\{

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{d}\sum_{u=1}^{d}\mu^{\epsilon}_{iul}\Bigg(\mathbb{E}\Bigg[\left(\tau^{-1}\left(\frac{1}{n_{k_{1}}}\sum_{s=1}^{n_{k_{1}}}T(y_{s})\right)_{i}-\eta_{0,i}\right)^{2}\times

\displaystyle\left(\tau^{-1}\left(\frac{1}{n_{k_{1}}}\sum_{s=1}^{n_{k_{1}}}T(y_{s})\right)_{u}-\eta_{0,u}\right)^{2}\Bigg]\Bigg)^{\frac{1}{2}}\Bigg\}

\displaystyle K_{2}(\eta)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\left(\frac{\left|{G_{k}}\right|}{n}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\Big\{\sum_{j=1}^{d}\Big[\mathrm{Var}\left[{(T_{j}(Y)-\mu_{j}(\eta_{0}))^{2}}\right]\Big]^{\frac{1}{2}}+

\displaystyle 2\sum_{i<j}\Big[\mathrm{Var}\left[{(T_{i}(Y)-\mu_{i}(\eta_{0}))(T_{j}(Y)-\mu_{j}(\eta_{0}))}\right]\Big]^{\frac{1}{2}}\Big\}

\displaystyle K_{3}(\eta_{0})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\mathbb{E}\Big[\sum_{j=1}^{d}\left|{T_{j}(Y^{\prime})-T_{j}(Y)}\right|\Big]^{3}

\displaystyle c=\left|{\mathrm{Var}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\left[{T(Y)}\right]}\right|\max_{k\in\{1,\dots,K\}}\left(\frac{\left|{G_{k}}\right|}{n}\right)^{-\frac{1}{2}}.

4.3

The bound in Corollary
4.1
is of order
\mathcal{O}(n^{-1/2})

In Section
3
we analyzed the order of the bound in Theorem
3.1
and found it is of order
n^{-1/2}
given some mild assumptions. That it is still
\mathcal{O}(n^{-1/2})
when specialized to the exponential family case is no surprise, and can be seen more directly here. Letting
\left|{G_{k}}\right|/n\rightarrow\gamma_{k}\in(0,1)
as in Theorem
2.3
, the term
c
in the bound satisfies

c\rightarrow\left|{\mathrm{Var}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\left[{T(Y)}\right]}\right|\max_{k\in\{1,\dots,K\}}\gamma_{k}^{-\frac{1}{2}}.

and thus
c=\mathcal{O}(1)
. For the same reason,
K_{2}(\eta)=\mathcal{O}(n^{-1/2})
. The function
K_{3}(\eta)
is exactly equal to a constant times
n^{-1/2}
and so is
\mathcal{O}(n^{-1/2})
. The last term in the bound,

\frac{2\left\lVert{h}\right\rVert}{\epsilon^{2}}\mathbb{E}\left[{\sum_{j=1}^{q}Q_{j}^{2}}\right]

depends on the order of

\mathbb{E}\left[{Q_{j}^{2}}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[{(\hat{\eta}_{j}-\eta_{0,j})^{2}}\right]=\mathrm{Var}\left[{\hat{\eta}_{j}}\right]+(\mathbb{E}\left[{Q_{j}}\right])^{2}=\mathcal{O}\left(n^{-1}\right).

This leaves only
K_{1}(\eta_{0})
to be considered. For the entire bound to be
\mathcal{O}(n^{-1/2})
,
K_{1}(\eta)
should be
\mathcal{O}(1)
since
K_{1}(\eta)
is multiplied by
n^{-1/2}
. This is the case if and only if

\Bigg(\mathbb{E}\Bigg[\left(\tau^{-1}\left(\frac{1}{n_{k_{1}}}\sum_{s=1}^{n_{k_{1}}}T(y_{s})\right)_{i}-\eta_{0,i}\right)^{2}\left(\tau^{-1}\left(\frac{1}{n_{k_{1}}}\sum_{s=1}^{n_{k_{1}}}T(y_{s})\right)_{u}-\eta_{0,u}\right)^{2}\Bigg]\Bigg)^{\frac{1}{2}}

is
\mathcal{O}(n^{-1})
. By the Cauchy-Schwartz inequality, the above is bounded by

(\mathbb{E}\left[{(\hat{\eta}_{i}-\eta_{0,i})^{4}}\right]\mathbb{E}\left[{(\hat{\eta}_{u}-\eta_{0,u})^{4}}\right])^{\frac{1}{4}}.

Thus the desired order follows from the fact that the fourth central moment of a component of the MLE is
\mathcal{O}\left(n^{-2}\right)
; see
de A. Cysneiros et al., 2001
for the univariate exponential case and
Peers and Iqbal, 1985
for the general multivariate case.

The choice of
\epsilon
in the bound should be handled on a case-by-case basis and can be chosen to minimize the sum of the
K_{1}
term and the final
\frac{2\left\lVert{h}\right\rVert}{\epsilon^{2}}
term. Notice that smaller
\epsilon
increases the final term while decreases
\mu_{iul}^{\epsilon}
in
K_{2}
.

4.4

4.1
ans we thus omit them.

Corollary 4.2
.

Let

be independent such that

with

A Multivariate Kolmogorov/Smooth Function Bound

In this section we give a general upper bound on the multivariate
Kolmogorov
distance

d_{kol}(W,Z)=\sup_{\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}}\left|\mathbbm{1}\{W\leq\bm{x}\}-\mathbbm{1}\{Z\leq\bm{x}\}\right|

(14)

of two
p
-dimensional random vectors
W,Z
, in terms of the smooth function class distances, such as those in our results in Sections
3
and
4
. In (
14
),
\mathbbm{1}\{W\leq\bm{x}\}
is the indicator function of the
p
-dimensional “lower quadrant”
\{W\leq\bm{x}\}=\{W_{1}\leq x_{1},\ldots,W_{p}\leq x_{p}\}
. These bounds may be of independent interest, but another reason we include them here is because they open the door to applying our smooth function bounds for the group sequential MLEs to the normal, to Kolmogorov distance, which may be desirable in statistical applications.

First we modify our notation slightly to show more explicitly the dependence on dimension and derivative order. For

\bm{k}=(k_{1},\dots,k_{p})\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{p}
, let
\left|{\bm{k}}\right|=\sum_{i=1}^{p}k_{i}
,
and for functions
h:\mathbb{R}^{p}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
whose partial derivatives

h^{\bm{k}}(\bm{x})=\frac{\partial^{k_{1}+\cdots+k_{p}}h}{\partial^{k_{1}}x_{1}\dots\partial^{k_{p}}x_{p}}\quad\text{exist for all}\quad 0\leq\left|{\bm{k}}\right|\leq m,

and
\left\lVert{\cdot}\right\rVert
the supremum norm, let
L_{m}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{p})
be the collection of all functions
h:\mathbb{R}^{p}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
with

\left\lVert{h}\right\rVert_{L_{m}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{p})}=\max_{0\leq\left|{\bm{k}}\right|\leq m}\left\lVert{h^{(\bm{k})}}\right\rVert

finite. For random vectors
X
and
Y
in
\mathbb{R}^{p}
, letting

\mathcal{H}_{m,\infty,p}=\{h\in L_{m}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{p}):\left\lVert{h}\right\rVert_{L_{m}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{p})}\leq 1\},

define

d_{m,\infty,p}(X,Y)=\left\lVert{\mathcal{L}(X)-\mathcal{L}(Y)}\right\rVert_{\mathcal{H}_{m,\infty,p}}=\sup_{h\in\mathcal{H}_{m,\infty,p}}\left|{\mathbb{E}\left[{h(X)}\right]-\mathbb{E}\left[{h(Y)}\right]}\right|.

Connecting to the previous sections, note that, for example, Theorem
3.1
gives a bound on
d_{3,\infty,q}
between the standardized group sequential MLE and the
q
-dimensional standard normal, and Theorem
3.3
a bound on
d_{2,\infty,q}
.

We first state our general result in Theorem
5.1
, and then specialize to the
m=3
case in Corollary
5.1
.

Theorem 5.1
.

Suppose
W
and
Z
are
p
-dimensional,
p\geq 2
, random vectors such that
Z
has a density bounded by a constant
C_{1}
. Then there exists a constant
C_{2}\geq 1
that depends only on
m
,
m\geq 1
, such that

d_{\text{kol}}(W,Z)\leq d_{m,\infty,p}(W,Z)^{\frac{p-1}{m+p-1}}\left(C_{2}^{p}+p+C_{1}d_{m,\infty,p}(W,Z)^{\frac{1}{m+p-1}}\right).

Proof.

Assume
d_{m,\infty,p}(W,Z)\leq 1
since otherwise the bound is trivial. Consider
S_{m}:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
such that
S_{m}\in L_{m}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
and

S_{m}(x)=\begin{cases}0&\text{if }x\leq 0\\
f(x)&\text{if }0\leq x\leq 1\\
1&\text{if }1\leq x\end{cases}

where
f
is some function with
0\leq f(x)\leq 1
for all
0\leq x\leq 1
. Let
h_{z,\alpha}(x)=S_{m}(-x/\alpha+(\alpha+z)/\alpha)
and notice that since
h_{z,\alpha}(x)
dominates
\mathbbm{1}_{\{x\leq z\}}
,

\displaystyle P(W\leq\bm{z})-P(Z\leq\bm{z})

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\left[{\prod_{i=1}^{p}h_{z_{i},\alpha}(W_{i})}\right]-\mathbb{E}\left[{\prod_{i=1}^{p}h_{z_{i},\alpha}(Z_{i})}\right]

(15)

\displaystyle+\mathbb{E}\left[{\prod_{i=1}^{p}h_{z_{i},\alpha}(Z_{i})}\right]-P(Z\leq\bm{z}).

We begin by bounding the first two terms on the right hand side of (
15
). Since
h_{z_{i},\alpha}\in L_{m}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
, the function
\bm{h}_{\bm{z},\alpha}:=\prod_{i=1}^{p}h_{z_{i},\alpha}
is in
L_{m}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{p})
. We have

\left\lVert{\bm{h}_{\bm{z},\alpha}}\right\rVert_{L_{m}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{p})}=\max_{0\leq\left|{\bm{k}}\right|\leq m}\left\lVert{\bm{h}_{\bm{z},\alpha}^{(\bm{k})}}\right\rVert=\max_{0\leq\left|{\bm{k}}\right|\leq m}\prod_{i}^{p}\left\lVert{h_{z_{i},\alpha}^{(k_{i})}}\right\rVert\\
=\max_{0\leq\left|{\bm{k}}\right|\leq m}\prod_{i}^{p}\left(\frac{1}{\alpha}\right)^{k_{i}}\left\lVert{S_{m}^{(k_{i})}}\right\rVert\leq\max_{0\leq\left|{\bm{k}}\right|\leq m}\prod_{i}^{p}\left(\frac{1}{\alpha}\right)^{k_{i}}\left\lVert{S_{m}}\right\rVert_{L_{m}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})}\\
=\left(\frac{1}{\alpha}\right)^{m}\left\lVert{S_{m}}\right\rVert_{L_{m}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})}^{p}.

Thus

\frac{\alpha^{m}\bm{h}_{\bm{z},\alpha}}{\left\lVert{S_{m}}\right\rVert_{L_{m}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})}^{p}}\in\mathcal{H}_{m,\infty,p}.

Then the first two terms of the right hand side of (
15
) are bounded by

\mathbb{E}\left[{\prod_{i=1}^{p}h_{z_{i},\alpha}(W_{i})}\right]-\mathbb{E}\left[{\prod_{i=1}^{p}h_{z_{i},\alpha}(Z_{i})}\right]\leq\frac{C_{2}^{p}d_{m,\infty,p}(W,Z)}{\alpha^{m}}

(16)

for
C_{2}=\left\lVert{S_{m}}\right\rVert_{L_{m}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})}
. Furthermore,
1=\left\lVert{S_{m}}\right\rVert\leq\left\lVert{S_{m}}\right\rVert_{L_{m}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})}=C_{2}
.

Now we consider the last two terms of (
15
). For
\bm{\alpha}=(\alpha,\dots,\alpha)\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
,

\mathbb{E}\left[{\prod_{i=1}^{p}h_{z_{i},\alpha}(Z_{i})}\right]-P(Z\leq\bm{z})\leq P(Z\leq\bm{z}+\bm{\alpha})-P(Z\leq\bm{z}).

(17)

The right hand side of (
17
) can be bounded by a
(p+1)
-dimensional rectangle of base
\alpha^{p}
, height
C_{1}
, and
p
‘legs’ parallel to each axis with base
\alpha^{p-1}
and length
\Phi(z_{i})
. Thus

\displaystyle P(Z\leq\bm{z}+\bm{\alpha})-P(Z\leq\bm{z})

\displaystyle\leq C_{1}\alpha^{p}+\sum_{i=1}^{p}\Phi(z_{i})\alpha^{p-1}

\displaystyle\leq C_{1}\alpha^{p}+p\alpha^{p-1}.

(18)

Combining (
15
) with (
16
) and (
5
) gives

P(W\leq\bm{z})-P(Z\leq\bm{z})\leq\frac{C_{2}^{p}d_{m,\infty,p}(W,Z)}{\alpha^{m}}+C_{1}\alpha^{p}+p\alpha^{p-1}.

(19)

Letting
\alpha=d_{m,\infty,p}(W,Z)^{1/(m+p-1)}
and plugging into the above equation yields,

P(W\leq\bm{z})-P(Z\leq\bm{z})\leq d_{m,\infty,p}(W,Z)^{\frac{p-1}{m+p-1}}\left(C_{2}^{p}+p+C_{1}d_{m,\infty,p}(W,Z)^{\frac{1}{m+p-1}}\right).

Similarly,

P(W\leq\bm{z})-P(Z\leq\bm{z})\geq-d_{m,\infty,p}(W,Z)^{\frac{p-1}{m+p-1}}\left(C_{2}^{p}+p+C_{1}d_{m,\infty,p}(W,Z)^{\frac{1}{m+p-1}}\right)

which completes the proof.
∎

Corollary 5.1
.

The Kolmogorov distance is bounded by
d_{3,\infty,p}(\cdot,\cdot)
as follows,

d_{\text{kol}}(W,Z)\leq d_{3,\infty,p}(W,Z)^{\frac{p-1}{2+p}}\left(52.5^{p}+p+\frac{d_{3,\infty,p}(W,Z)^{\frac{1}{2+p}}}{(2\pi)^{p/2}}\right).

Proof.

We follow the proof of Theorem
5.1
. Let
S_{m}
be the Hermite interpolation of some function
f
between
f(0)=0
and
f(1)=1
with
f^{(i)}(0)=f^{(i)}(1)=0
for all
i=1,\dots,m
. Thus
S_{m}\in L_{m}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
with

6

Conclusion and Future Directions

We have generalized the results of
Anastasiou and Reinert, 2017
and
Anastasiou, 2018
to find optimal order bounds for the joint distribution of a sequence of maximum likelihood estimates based on accumulating data. The approximate normality of this joint distribution is an essential assumption underlying the statistics of group sequential hypothesis testing, the dominant paradigm in clinical trials. The specialization of this bound to exponentials families in Corollary
4.1
is the simplest such bound covering this case in the non-sequential setting, that we are aware of.

A direction in which these results may be generalized is to examine log-likelihood functions of the form

\sum_{i\in G_{k}}\log f_{i}(Y_{i},\theta)+g_{k}(\mathcal{Y}_{k},\theta),

with
\mathcal{Y}_{k}=\{Y_{i}:i\in G_{k}\}
.
This form may provide a way of relaxing the independence assumption between samples that could be amenable to Stein’s method. It is also the form of some log-likelihood functions of generalized linear mixed models (GLMMs) with random stage effects, for example, the GLMM with Poisson response variable and canonical log link function.
