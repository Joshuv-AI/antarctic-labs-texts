Abstract
. We present explicit formulas for Moore-Penrose inverses of some families of set inclusion matrices arising from sets, vector spaces, and designs.



2020 Mathematics Subject Classification
Primary 05B20, 15A09; Secondary 05A30, 15A03.

1.
Introduction

The incidence algebra
\mathcal{A}_{\mathcal{P}}
of a locally finite poset
\mathcal{P}
is the associative algebra of all real-valued functions on
\mathcal{P}\times\mathcal{P}
that vanish on pairs
(p,q)
with
p\not\preccurlyeq q
. The addition being the ordinary sum of functions while the multiplication
f*g
of two elements
f,g
of
\mathcal{A}_{\mathcal{P}}
is defined as

(f*g)(x,y)=\sum_{x\leq z\leq y}f(x,y)g(y,z).

The zeta function
\zeta_{\mathcal{P}}
of
\mathcal{P}
(or
\mathcal{A}_{\mathcal{P}}
) is the constant function
\zeta_{\mathcal{P}}(x,y)=1
on all
(x,y)
satisfying
x\preccurlyeq y
. The zeta function
\zeta_{\mathcal{P}}
is invertible in the algebra
\mathcal{A}_{\mathcal{P}}
. The inverse of
\zeta_{\mathcal{P}}
is called the Möbius function
\mu_{\mathcal{P}}
of
\mathcal{P}
generalizing the classical Möbius function from number theory as well as inclusion-exclusion principle from set theory. Möbius function of posets has studied since 1935 by Weisner
[
18
,
19
]
and Hall
[
9
]
for the lattice of subgroups of finite groups, and later, in a general setting, by Rota
[
15
]
in 1964.

The incidence matrix
\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{P}}
of
\mathcal{P}
is the
0-1
matrix whose rows and columns are indexed by elements of
\mathcal{P}
with
(p,q)
entry being
1
if
p\preccurlyeq q
and
0
otherwise. The matrix
\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{P}}
is actually the matrix representation of the zeta function of
\mathcal{P}
. Accordingly,
\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{P}}
is invertible and its inverse is the matrix representation of the Möbius function of
\mathcal{P}
.

Beside incidence matrices
\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{P}}
of posets, their submatrices, we still call incidence matrices, are also of particular interest when studying relationships between two classes of objects that appear naturally in various contexts like design theory, coding theory, algebraic graph theory, representation theory, finite geometry, etc., see for instance
[
3
,
6
,
10
,
20
,
21
]
. Set inclusion incidence matrices arising from subsets of a set or subspaces of a vertex space are typical and most accessible examples of incidence matrices having many applications in combinatorial contexts, in particular design theory and extremal set theory
[
4
,
8
,
20
,
21
]
.

Given a set
S
with
n
elements and
0\leq r\leq c\leq n
, the
set inclusion incidence matrix

M=M(n;r,c)
of
S
is an
\binom{n}{r}\times\binom{n}{c}
matrix defined as follows: the rows and columns of
M
are indexed by
r
-subsets and
c
-subsets of
S
, respectively, with
(R,C)
-entry equal to
\delta_{R\subseteq C}
, where

\delta_{R\subseteq C}:=\begin{cases}1,&R\subseteq C,\\
0,&R\nsubseteq C\end{cases}

is the Kronecker delta. The set inclusion matrix
M(n;r,c)
of a vector space
V
of dimension
n
over a finite field with
q
elements is defined analogously with rows and columns are indexed by
r
-dimensional and
s
-dimensional subspaces of
V
, respectively. In this case,
M(n;r,c)
is an
\binom{n}{r}_{q}\times\binom{n}{c}_{q}
matrix with
\binom{n}{i}_{q}
being the Gaussian binomial coefficient. The above definitions can be extended to set inclusion matrices of sub-algebras of any algebraic structures as well.

A
generalized inverse
of an
m\times n
matrix
A
is an
n\times m
matrix
G
satisfying
AGA=A
. The
Moore-Penrose inverse
of
A
, denoted by
A^{+}
, is a generalized inverse of
A
satisfying the following three more equations:

A^{+}AA^{+}=A^{+},\quad(AA^{+})^{T}=AA^{+},\quad(A^{+}A)^{T}=A^{+}A,

where
M^{T}
denotes the transpose of a matrix
M
(see
[
13
,
14
]
). It is known that any two Moore-Penrose inverses of a matrix are equal and that any complex matrix has a Moore-Penrose inverse (see
[
5
,
11
]
for basic properties of Moore-Penrose inverses). Notice that Moore-Penrose inverses need not exist over any field.

Wilson
[
21
]
studies set inclusion matrices and gives a formula for their rank over fields of prime characteristics. Extending the results of Wilson, Bapat
[
1
]
gives implicit formulas for the Moore-Penrose inverse of set inclusion matrices over fields of characteristic zero. Moreover, a necessary and sufficient condition for these matrices to admit Moore-Penrose inverses over fields of prime characteristics as well as an implicit formula for their Moore-Penrose inverses (when exist) is offered.

The aim of this paper is to give explicit combinatorial formulas for Moore-Penrose inverses of several classes of incidence matrices. In Section 2, we introduce set inclusion incidence matrices and give their Moore-Penrose inverses. Likewise, in Section 3, we introduces set includion incidence matrices arising from vector spaces and give their Moore-Penrose inverses by extending the results of Section 2. Using information on designs, we also give a simple formula for Moore-Penrose inverses of incidence matrices of designs in Section 4.

2.
Set inclusion incidence matrices

Let
r
,
c
, and
n
be non-negative integers satisfying
0\leq r\leq c\leq n
. The set inclusion incidence matrix
M=M(n;r,c)
is an
\binom{n}{r}\times\binom{n}{c}
matrix defined as follows: the rows and columns of
M
are indexed by
r
-subsets and
c
-subsets of
[n]:=\{1,\ldots,n\}
, respectively, with
(R,C)
-entry equal to
\delta_{R\subseteq C}
. Let
M^{*}
be the matrix whose rows and columns are indexed by
c
-subsets and
r
-subsets of
[n]
, respectively, and its
(C,R)
-entry is given by

M^{*}_{C,R}=(-1)^{r-i}\frac{\binom{c-i-1}{r-i}}{\binom{N-r}{c-r}\binom{N-c}{r-i}},

where
N=\max\{n,r+c\}
and
i=|R\cap C|
. We show that
M^{*}
is the Moore-Penrose inverse of
M
. Indeed,
M^{*}
has more interesting properties described in the next lemma. To this end, we apply the following result of Ruiz.

Proposition 2.1

(
[
16
]
)
.

Let
n\geq 0
and
p(x)
be a polynomial of degree less than
n
. Then

\sum_{i=0}^{n}(-1)^{i}\binom{n}{i}p(i)=0.

Lemma 2.2
.

With the above notation, we have

(i)

MM^{*}=I
if
n\geq r+c
.

(ii)

M^{*}M=I
if
n\leq r+c
.

Proof.

(i) Let
R
and
R^{\prime}
be
r
-subsets of
[n]
and put
k:=|R\cap R^{\prime}|
. A simple verification shows that

(MM^{*})_{R,R^{\prime}}=\sum_{i=k}^{r}(-1)^{r-i}\frac{\binom{r-k}{i-k}\binom{n-2r+k}{c-r-i+k}\binom{c-i-1}{r-i}}{\binom{n-r}{c-r}\binom{n-c}{r-i}}.

If
R=R^{\prime}
, then
k=r
and
(MM^{*})_{R,R^{\prime}}=1
. Now, assume that
R\neq R^{\prime}
, i.e.
k<r
. Then

\displaystyle(MM^{*})_{R,R^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=\frac{(c-r)(-1)^{r}}{(r-k)\binom{n-r}{r-k}}\sum_{i=k}^{r}(-1)^{i}\binom{c-i-1}{r-k-1}\binom{r-k}{i-k}

\displaystyle=\frac{(c-r)(-1)^{r-k}}{(r-k)\binom{n-r}{r-k}}\sum_{j=0}^{r-k}(-1)^{j}\binom{c-k-j-1}{r-k-1}\binom{r-k}{j}=0

by Proposition
2.1
for
\binom{c-k-j-1}{r-k-1}
is a polynomial of order less than
r-k
in
j
.

(ii) Let
C
and
C^{\prime}
be
c
-subsets of
[n]
and put
k:=|C\cap C^{\prime}|
. Since
n\leq r+c
, we have
N=r+c
and
2c-n\leq k\leq c
. A simple verification shows that

(M^{*}M)_{C,C^{\prime}}=\sum_{i=\max\{0,r+k-c\}}^{\min\{k,r\}}(-1)^{r-i}\frac{\binom{k}{i}\binom{c-k}{r-i}\binom{c-i-1}{r-i}}{\binom{c}{r}\binom{r}{i}}.

If
C=C^{\prime}
, then
k=c
and
(M^{*}M)_{C,C^{\prime}}=1
. Now, suppose that
C\neq C^{\prime}
, i.e.
k<c
. Then

\displaystyle(M^{*}M)_{C,C^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=\frac{(c-r)(-1)^{r}}{(c-k)\binom{c}{k}}\sum_{i=\max\left\{0,k+r-c\right\}}^{\min\left\{k,r\right\}}(-1)^{i}\binom{c-i-1}{c-k-1}\binom{c-k}{r-i}

\displaystyle=\frac{c-r}{(c-k)\binom{c}{k}}\sum_{j=\max\left\{0,r-k\right\}}^{\min\left\{r,c-k\right\}}(-1)^{j}\binom{c-r+j-1}{c-k-1}\binom{c-k}{j}.

From
2c-k=|C\cup C^{\prime}|\leq n\leq r+c
, we get
c-k\leq r
so that
\min\{r,c-k\}=c-k
. Also,
\binom{c-r+j-1}{c-k-1}=0
when
j<r-k
. Thus

(M^{*}M)_{C,C^{\prime}}=\frac{c-r}{(c-k)\binom{c}{k}}\sum_{j=0}^{c-k}(-1)^{j}\binom{c-r+j-1}{c-k-1}\binom{c-k}{j}=0

by Proposition
2.1
for
\binom{c-r+j-1}{c-k-1}
is a polynomial of degree less than
c-k
in
j
.
∎

Theorem 2.3
.

M^{+}=M^{*}
.

Proof.

First observe that
MM^{*}
and
M^{*}M
are symmetric for any
(R,R^{\prime})
-entry of
MM^{*}
and
(C,C^{\prime})
-entry of
M^{*}M
depends only on the size of intersections
R\cap R^{\prime}
and
C\cap C^{\prime}
, respectively. On the other hand, by Lemma
3.5
, either
MM^{*}=I
or
M^{*}M=I
. Therefore
MM^{*}M=M
and
M^{*}MM^{*}=M^{*}
so that
M^{*}=M^{+}
is the Moore-Penrose inverse of
M
.
∎

Bapat
[
1
]
gives necessary and sufficient conditions for
M
to have a Moore-Penrose inverse over fields of prime characteristics, and gives an implicit formula for the Moore-Penrose inverse when it exists. It is evident that
M^{*}
is a Moore-Penrose inverse of
M
over a field of characteristic
p
when denominators of the given entries are non-zero. Hence,
M^{*}
yields an explicit formula for the Moore-Penrose inverse of
M
over a field of characteristic
p
provided that
p
does not divides
\binom{N-r}{c-r}\binom{N-c}{0}\cdots\binom{N-c}{r}
, for instance for
p>\max\{n-r,c\}
.

3.
Vector space inclusion incidence matrices

Let
r
,
c
, and
n
be non-negative integers satisfying
0\leq r\leq c\leq n
and
q
be a prime power. The vector space inclusion incidence matrix
M=M(n,q;r,c)
is an
\binom{n}{r}_{q}\times\binom{n}{c}_{q}
matrix defined as follows: the rows and columns of
M
are indexed by
r
–dimensional subspaces and
c
-dimensional subspaces of
GF(q)^{n}
, respectively, with
(R,C)
-entry equal to
\delta_{R\subseteq C}
. Let
M^{*}
be the matrix whose rows and columns are indexed by
c
-dimensional subspaces and
r
-dimensional subspaces of
GF(q)^{n}
, respectively, and its
(C,R)
-entry is given by

M^{*}_{C,R}=(-1)^{r-i}\frac{\binom{c-i-1}{r-i}_{q}}{\binom{N-r}{c-r}_{q}\binom{N-c}{r-i}_{q}}\cdot\frac{1}{q^{(c-r)(r-i)+\binom{r-i}{2}}},

where
N=\max\{n,r+c\}
and
i=\dim(R\cap C)
. As in the previous section, we aim to show that
M^{*}
is the Moore-Penrose inverse of
M
. To this end, we need to recall some notation and prove some preliminary lemmas.

In what follows, for a non-negative integer
n
and prime power
q
,
[n]_{q}
and
[n]_{q}!
stand for the
q
-integer
(q^{n}-1)/(q-1)
and the
q
-factorial
[n]_{q}\cdots[1]_{q}
, respectively. Also, for
0\leq m\leq n
, the
q
-binomial
[n]_{q}!/[m]_{q}![n-m]_{q}!
is denoted by
\binom{n}{m}_{q}
. Note that the
q
-binomial
\binom{n}{m}_{q}
is defined to be zero if the condition
0\leq m\leq n
does not hold.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
V
be a vector space of dimension
n
over a finite field with
q
elements and
0\leq k\leq i\leq r\leq c\leq n-r
. Let
R
and
R^{\prime}
be subspaces of
V
of dimension
r
with a
k
-dimensional intersection. Then the number of subspaces
C\supseteq R
of
V
of dimension
c
such that
C\cap R^{\prime}
is
i
-dimensional is equal to

\binom{r-k}{i-k}_{q}\binom{n-2k+r}{c-r-i+k}_{q}q^{(c-r-i+k)(r-i)}.

Proof.

Since
C\supseteq R
we can work in
V/R
and assume without loss of generality that
R=0
. Then
C\cap R^{\prime}
has dimension
i-k
. Since
R^{\prime}
has dimension
r-k
there are
\binom{r-k}{i-k}
choices for
C\cap R^{\prime}
. Passing to the quotient
V/C\cap R^{\prime}
, we can further assume that
C\cap R^{\prime}=0
. Then
V
,
C
, and
R^{\prime}
have dimensions
n-r-i+k
,
c-r-i+k
, and
r-i
, respectively, with
C\cap R^{\prime}=0
. The number of subspaces
C
of
V
with these properties is equal to

\binom{n-2k+r}{c-r-i+k}_{q}q^{(c-r-i+k)(r-i)}

(see
[
7
, Lemma 2.6]
), from which the result follows.
∎

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
V
be a vector space of dimension
n
over a finite field with
q
elements and
0\leq i\leq k\leq r\leq c
. Let
C
and
C^{\prime}
be subspaces of
V
of dimension
c
with a
k
-dimensional intersection. Then the number of subspaces
R\supseteq C^{\prime}
of
V
of dimension
r
such that
R\cap C
is
i
-dimensional is equal to

\binom{k}{i}_{q}\binom{c-k}{r-i}_{q}q^{(r-i)(k-i)}.

Proof.

First observe that there are
\binom{k}{i}
choices for
R\cap C
as
R\cap C
is an
i
-dimensional subspace of
C\cap C^{\prime}
of dimension
k
. Passing to the quotient
V/R\cap C
, we can assume without loss of generality that
R\cap C=0
. Then
R
,
C\cap C^{\prime}
, and
C^{\prime}
, have dimensions
r-i
,
k-i
, and
c-i
, respectively, with
R\cap(C\cap C^{\prime})=0
. The number of subspaces
R
of
V
with these properties is equal to

\binom{c-k}{r-i}_{q}q^{(r-i)(k-i)}

(see
[
7
, Lemma 2.6]
), from which the result follows.
∎

In order to obtain a
q
-analogue of Proposition
2.1
, we need to use Gauss’ binomial formula.

Proposition 3.3

(Gauss’ binomial formula
[
12
, 5.5]
)
.

Let
x
,
a
, and
q
be real numbers and
n\geq 1
be a positive integer. Then

(x+a)(x+qa)\cdots(x+q^{n-1}a)=\sum_{i=0}^{n}\binom{n}{i}_{q}q^{\binom{i}{2}}a^{i}x^{n-i}.

Corollary 3.4
.

Let
n
be a positive integer. Then

\sum_{i=0}^{n}(-1)^{i}\binom{n}{i}_{q}\cdot\frac{1}{q^{i(n-i)+\binom{i}{2}}}\cdot p_{m}(q^{i})=0

for all polynomials
p_{m}(x)
of degree
0\leq m<n
.

Proof.

The result follows by replacing
x
by
-q^{m}a
(
0\leq m<n
) in Gauss’ binomial formula and assuming that
p_{m}(x)=x^{m}
.
∎

Now, we can proceed to show that
M^{*}
is actually the Moore-Penrose inverse of
M
by showing that the results of Lemma
3.5
remain unchanged in the case of vector space inclusion incidence matrices.

Lemma 3.5
.

With the notation above, we have

(i)

MM^{*}=I
if
n\geq r+c
;

(ii)

M^{*}M=I
if
n\leq r+c
.

Proof.

(i) Let
R
and
R^{\prime}
be subspaces of
GF(q)^{n}
of dimension
r
and put
k:=\dim(R\cap R^{\prime})
. A simple verification shows that

(MM^{*})_{R,R^{\prime}}=\sum_{i=k}^{r}(-1)^{r-i}\frac{\binom{r-k}{i-k}_{q}\binom{n-2r+k}{c-r-i+k}_{q}\binom{c-i-1}{r-i}_{q}}{\binom{n-r}{c-r}_{q}\binom{n-c}{r-i}_{q}}\cdot\frac{1}{q^{(i-k)(r-i)+\binom{r-i}{2}}}.

If
R=R^{\prime}
, then
k=r
and
(MM^{*})_{R,R^{\prime}}=1
. Now, assume that
R\neq R^{\prime}
, i.e.
k<r
. Then

\displaystyle(MM^{*})_{R,R^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=\frac{[c-r]_{q}(-1)^{r}}{[r-k]_{q}\binom{n-r}{r-k}_{q}}\sum_{i=k}^{r}(-1)^{i}\binom{c-i-1}{r-k-1}_{q}\binom{r-k}{i-k}_{q}\cdot\frac{1}{q^{(i-k)(r-i)+\binom{r-i}{2}}}

\displaystyle=\frac{[c-r]_{q}(-1)^{r-k}}{[r-k]_{q}\binom{n-r}{r-k}_{q}}\sum_{j=0}^{r-k}(-1)^{j}\binom{c-k-j-1}{r-k-1}_{q}\binom{r-k}{j}_{q}\cdot\frac{1}{q^{j(r-k-j)+\binom{r-k-j}{2}}}

\displaystyle=\frac{[c-r]_{q}(-1)^{r-k}}{[r-k]_{q}\binom{n-r}{r-k}_{q}}\sum_{l=0}^{r-k}(-1)^{l}\binom{c-r+l-1}{r-k-1}_{q}\binom{r-k}{l}_{q}\cdot\frac{1}{q^{l(r-k-l)+\binom{l}{2}}}=0

by Corollary
3.4
for
\binom{c-r+l-1}{r-k-1}_{q}
is a polynomial of order less than
r-k
in
q^{l}
.

(ii) Let
C
and
C^{\prime}
be subspaces of
GF(q)^{n}
of dimension
c
and put
k:=\dim(C\cap C^{\prime})
. Since
n\leq r+c
, we have
N=r+c
and
2c-n\leq k\leq c
. A simple verification shows that

(M^{*}M)_{C,C^{\prime}}=\sum_{i=\max\{0,r+k-c\}}^{\min\{k,r\}}(-1)^{r-i}\frac{\binom{k}{i}_{q}\binom{c-k}{r-i}_{q}\binom{c-i-1}{r-i}_{q}}{\binom{c}{r}_{q}\binom{r}{i}_{q}}\cdot\frac{1}{q^{(c-r-k+i)(r-i)+\binom{r-i}{2}}}.

If
C=C^{\prime}
, then
k=c
and
(M^{*}M)_{C,C^{\prime}}=1
. Now, suppose that
C\neq C^{\prime}
, i.e.
k<c
. Then

\displaystyle(M^{*}M)_{C,C^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=\frac{[c-r]_{q}(-1)^{r}}{[c-k]_{q}\binom{c}{k}_{q}}\sum_{i=\max\left\{0,k+r-c\right\}}^{\min\left\{k,r\right\}}(-1)^{i}\binom{c-i-1}{c-k-1}_{q}\binom{c-k}{r-i}_{q}\cdot\frac{1}{q^{(c-r-k+i)(r-i)+\binom{r-i}{2}}}

\displaystyle=\frac{[c-r]_{q}}{[c-k]_{q}\binom{c}{k}_{q}}\sum_{j=\max\left\{0,r-k\right\}}^{\min\left\{r,c-k\right\}}(-1)^{j}\binom{c-r+j-1}{c-k-1}_{q}\binom{c-k}{j}_{q}\cdot\frac{1}{q^{(c-k-j)j+\binom{j}{2}}}.

From
2c-k=\dim(C+C^{\prime})\leq n\leq r+c
, we get
c-k\leq r
so that
\min\{r,c-k\}=c-k
. Also,
\binom{c-r+j-1}{c-k-1}=0
when
j<r-k
. Thus

(M^{*}M)_{C,C^{\prime}}=\frac{[c-r]_{q}}{[c-k]_{q}\binom{c}{k}_{q}}\sum_{j=0}^{c-k}(-1)^{j}\binom{c-r+j-1}{c-k-1}_{q}\binom{c-k}{j}_{q}\cdot\frac{1}{q^{(c-k-j)j+\binom{j}{2}}}=0

by Corollary
3.4
for
\binom{c-r+j-1}{c-k-1}
is a polynomial of degree less than
c-k
in
q^{j}
.
∎

Utilizing the same line of proof as in Theorem

we can prove the following.

Theorem 3.6
.

M^{+}=M^{*}
.

As in the previous section,
M^{*}
yields an explicit formula for the Moore-Penrose inverse of

over a field of characteristic

does not divides

, for instance for any

4.
t
-Design inclusion incidence matrices

A
t-(v,k,\lambda)
design
\mathcal{D}
is a collection of blocks as
k
-subsets of a
v
-element set, say
[v]
, such that every
t
-subset of
[v]
belongs to
\lambda
blocks. It is known that every
t-(v,k,\lambda)
design is an
s-(v,k,\lambda_{s})
, where

\lambda_{s}=\lambda\cdot\frac{\binom{v-s}{t-s}}{\binom{k-s}{t-s}}

for all
0\leq s\leq t
. Let
b:=\lambda\binom{v}{t}/\binom{k}{t}
be the number of blocks in
\mathcal{D}
. For any
0\leq s\leq t
, let
M_{s}:=M_{s}(t,v,k,\lambda)
be the
\binom{v}{s}\times b
matrix defined as follows: rows and columns of
M
are indexed by
s
-subsets of
[v]
and blocks, respectively, with
(R,C)
-entry equal to
\delta_{R\subseteq C}
. It is a well-known result that
M_{1}M_{1}^{T}=(\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2})I+\lambda_{2}J
, where
I
and
J
are the identity matrix and the matrix of all
1
’s with appropriate sizes, respectively. Using this fact, we can simply compute the Moore-Penrose inverse of the point-block incidence matrix
M_{1}
of block designs.

Proposition 4.1
.

We have
M_{1}^{+}=M_{1}^{T}((\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2})I+\lambda_{2}J)^{-1}
. More precisely,

(M_{1}^{+})_{B,u}=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{\lambda_{1}},&u\in B,\\
-\frac{1}{\lambda_{1}}\cdot\frac{k-1}{v-k},&u\notin B.\end{cases}

Proof.

We know from
[
2
, Lemma 4.4]
that the
n\times n
matrix
xI+yJ
is an invertible matrix if and only if
x(x+ny)\neq 0
, from which it follows that
X:=(\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2})I+\lambda_{2}J
is an invertible matrix. Since
M_{1}(M_{1}^{T}X^{-1})=I
we observe that

M_{1}(M_{1}^{T}X^{-1})M_{1}=M_{1}\quad\text{and}\quad(M_{1}^{T}X^{-1})M_{1}(M_{1}^{T}X^{-1})=M_{1}^{T}X^{-1}.

On the other hand,

\frac{1}{x}I-\frac{y}{x(x+ny)}J

\displaystyle(M_{1}^{+})_{B,u}

\displaystyle=(M_{1}^{T}X^{-1})_{B,u}=\sum_{v\in B}(X^{-1})_{v,u}

\displaystyle=-\frac{k\lambda_{2}}{(\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2})(\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2}+v\lambda_{2})}=-\frac{1}{\lambda_{1}}\cdot\frac{k-1}{v-k}

\lambda_{2}=(k-1)/(v-1)\lambda_{1}

\displaystyle(M_{1}^{+})_{B,u}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2}}-\frac{1}{\lambda_{1}}\cdot\frac{k-1}{v-k}=\frac{1}{\lambda_{1}},

as required
