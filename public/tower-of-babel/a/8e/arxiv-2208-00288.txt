Information engine in a nonequilibrium bath Preprint: APS/123-QED
Tushar K. Saha, Jannik Ehrich, Momčilo Gavrilov, Susanne Still, David A. Sivak, John Bechhoefer

Abstract
Information engines can convert thermal fluctuations of a bath at temperature T T into work at rates of order k B ​ T k_{\mathrm{B}}T per relaxation time of the system. We show experimentally that such engines, when in contact with a bath that is out of equilibrium, can extract much more work. We place a heavy, micron-scale bead in a harmonic potential that ratchets up to capture favorable fluctuations. Adding a fluctuating electric field increases work extraction up to ten times, limited only by the strength of applied field. Our results connect Maxwell’s demon with energy harvesting and an estimate of efficiency shows that information engines in nonequilibrium baths can greatly outperform conventional engines.



Maxwell’s famous thought experiment proposed a way to convert information about thermal fluctuations into energy
Maxwell 1872
. Exploring “Maxwell demons” has improved our understanding of the second law of thermodynamics
Leff and Rex 2002
;
Parrondo et al. 2015
. Building them, as “information engines”, a concept inspired originally by Szilard’s model
Szilard 2003
, has allowed tests of the second law applied to mesoscopic length scales
Toyabe et al. 2010
;
Koski et al. 2014
;
Chida et al. 2017
;
Paneru et al. 2018
;
Ribezzi-Crivellari and Ritort 2019
;
Cottet et al. 2017
. The ability of an information engine to extract work from a single heat bath is reconciled with the second law because the cost of sensing fluctuations and exploiting the relevant information equals or exceeds the energy extracted
Szilard 2003
;
Bennett 1982
;
Parrondo et al. 2015
;
Horowitz and Sandberg 2014
.

That information-processing costs compensate for the extracted energy implicitly assumes that the measuring device operates at the same temperature as the engine itself. If the temperature of the engine bath exceeds that of the bath connected to the measuring device, net work can be extracted
Still 2020
. But the range of temperatures available for heat baths is small, which limits engine power, even with optimized information processing
Still and Daimer 2022
. For example, the ratio of boiling-water to freezing-water temperatures is
373/273\approx 1.4
, and the ratio of temperatures in an internal-combustion heat engine is
\lesssim 8
, with practical efficiencies
\lesssim 0.5

Ghojel 2020
.

In this paper, we show experimentally that this limitation can be overcome by immersing the information engine in a nonequilibrium heat bath. The environment is out of equilibrium at macroscopic scales but has practically unchanged local temperature
T
. The measuring device of the engine is in contact with an equilibrium heat bath also at temperature
T
. Such an information engine extracts energy from both thermal and nonequilibrium fluctuations. Similar ideas have been proposed theoretically to take advantage of active fluctuations produced by bacteria swimming in a bath
Paneru et al. 2022
and to increase the output of a Szilard engine
Malgaretti and Stark 2022
.

With carefully chosen experimental parameters, our engine can extract work at up to ten times the maximum rate achievable when connected to an equilibrium bath at temperature
T
. Moreover, we show that energy extraction is constrained only by practical experimental limits on the nonequilibrium forcing of the external environment. The energy extracted can, in principle, exceed the costs associated with necessary information processing and control by orders of magnitude.

Experimental setup.
—The information engine consists of an optically trapped, heavy bead in water that acts as a thermal bath at room temperature
T

Saha et al. 2021
. To force the bath out of equilibrium, a fluctuating electric field is applied via electrodes
Martínez et al. 2012
, as shown in Fig.
1
(a). Unlike previous experimental implementations of baths with higher effective temperatures that were based on digitally generated noise
Martínez et al. 2012
;
Chupeau et al. 2018
;
Militaru et al. 2021
;
Goerlich et al. 2021a
, the fluctuating field here arises from a physical reservoir, the amplified Johnson voltage noise of a resistor at temperature
T
. The nonequilibrium-noise strength is regulated by a variable-gain amplifier, whose bandwidth is set by a low-pass filter with adjustable cutoff frequency. Under gravity, the bead fluctuates about a mean position because of thermal and nonequilibrium forces acting on it. Further details on the setup are given in the Appendix.

System dynamics.
—We model the trapped bead as a spring-mass system, as shown in Fig.
1
(b). The information engine operates by raising the trap position when the bead fluctuates above the trap center. The upward fluctuation of the bead, increasing its gravitational potential energy, is rectified by ratcheting the trap position. We update the trap position to convert the thermal fluctuations from the nonequilibrium bath into gravitational energy, carefully choosing the distance the trap is moved, so that the shifted trap potential does not perform work on the bead
Saha et al. 2021
.

\lambda

\displaystyle\dot{x}(t)

\displaystyle=\underbrace{-\left[x(t)-\lambda(t)\right]}_{\text{restoring force}}\,-\underbrace{\delta_{\mathrm{g}}}_{\text{grav.}}+\underbrace{\xi(t)}_{\text{thermal}}+\underbrace{\zeta(t)}_{\text{noneq.}},

(1)

where we have rescaled lengths by the bead position’s equilibrium standard deviation
\sigma\equiv k_{\mathrm{B}}T/\kappa
in the trap with strength
\kappa

\tau_{\mathrm{r}}\equiv\gamma/\kappa
of a bead with Stokes’ friction coefficient
\gamma

\delta_{\mathrm{g}}\equiv\Delta mg/(\kappa\sigma)

\Delta m=\Delta\rho(4/3)\pi r^{3}

We measure the bead position at a sampling time

\displaystyle\lambda_{k+1}=\lambda_{k}+\alpha\,(x_{k}-\lambda_{k})\,\Theta(x_{k}-\lambda_{k})\,,

(2)

\Theta(\cdot)

Nonequilibrium bath.
—The second noise
\zeta(t)
in Eq. (
1
) describes random electrokinetic forcing of strength
D_{\mathrm{ne}}
. The electrokinetic forces combine electroosmotic and electrophoretic effects on the bead
Cohen 2006

The fluctuating electrokinetic forces arise from the amplified, low-pass-filtered Johnson noise of a resistor. In scaled units, the noise term obeys

\displaystyle f_{\mathrm{ne}}^{-1}\,\dot{\zeta}(t)=-\zeta(t)+\sqrt{D_{\mathrm{ne}}}\,\tilde{\xi}(t)\,,

(3)

where
f_{\mathrm{ne}}
is the
cutoff
frequency set by the filter frequency,
\expectationvalue{\tilde{\xi}(t)}=0
, and
\expectationvalue{\tilde{\xi}(t) \tilde{\xi}(t')}=2\,\delta(t-t^{\prime})

\expectationvalue{\zeta(t)\zeta(t')}=D_{\mathrm{ne}}\,f_{\mathrm{ne}}\e^{-f_{\mathrm{ne}}|t-t^{\prime}|}

f_{\mathrm{ne}}\to\infty

Hänggi and Jung 1995
;
Szamel 2014
;
Goerlich et al. 2021b
;

from 0 to 83.5 and

from 10 Hz to 24 kHz. The trap’s cutoff frequency

Energy measurements.
—
At each time step, the stored gravitational (free) energy of the bead changes by

\displaystyle\Delta F_{k+1}=\delta_{\mathrm{g}}\,\left(\lambda_{k+1}-\lambda_{k}\right)\,.

(4)

The average output power is measured using
\dot{F}~=~\sum_{k=0}^{N}\expectationvalue{\Delta F_k}/Nt_{\mathrm{s}}
, where the ensemble average
\expectationvalue{\cdot}
is estimated by averaging over multiple trajectories, each of length
N
time steps.

The work done by the trap on the bead is

\displaystyle W_{k+1}=\tfrac{1}{2}\left[(x_{k+1}-\lambda_{k+1})^{2}-(x_{k+1}-\lambda_{k})^{2}\right]\,,

(5)

and the average trap power is measured using
\dot{W}~=~\sum_{k=0}^{N}\expectationvalue{W_k}/Nt_{\mathrm{s}}
.

Equation (
5
) implies that the trap power would be zero for
\alpha=2
if there were no feedback delay and no measurement uncertainty
Saha et al. 2021
; however, the one-step delay implies that the zero-work condition is realized at a lower feedback gain, empirically
\alpha\approx 1.8

Saha et al. 2022

Efficiency.
—As we have seen, operating an information engine in a nonequilibrium bath with noise strength
D_{\mathrm{ne}}
(and effective temperature
T_{\mathrm{ne}}
in the white-noise limit) can increase output power. At the same time, the measuring device and controller that gather and exploit the information used to power the engine are in contact with an equilibrium bath at temperature
T
and thus independent of the nonequilibrium driving force
D_{\mathrm{ne}}
.

We therefore expect the ratio
\Upsilon
of these two powers (power extracted over minimum operating power), one possible measure of “efficiency,” to increase with
D_{\mathrm{ne}}
. (Note that
\Upsilon
differs from the Carnot efficiency and, as we will see, is not bounded by unity.) To estimate this increase, we consider the minimum additional work needed to run the controller, which equals the reduction due to the controller’s dynamics in the conditional entropy
Cover and Thomas 2006

H[\Lambda|X]
of the trap position
\lambda
given the particle position
x

Ehrich et al. 2022
. Therefore the
information power
required to measure, erase information, and control the engine is

\displaystyle P_{\mathrm{info}}\equiv\frac{H[\Lambda_{k-1}|X_{k}]-H[\Lambda_{k}|X_{k}]}{t_{\mathrm{s}}}\,.

(11)

In the white-noise limit (
f_{\mathrm{ne}}\to\infty
), the two conditional entropies can be estimated from simulations and analytical approximations (see Appendix). Figure
4
(a) compares the input information power
P_{\mathrm{info}}
with the output rate
\dot{F}
of free-energy gain, Eq. (
9
), as a function of the noise strength
D_{\mathrm{ne}}
. While the engine output grows as
D_{\mathrm{ne}}^{1/2}
, the input power saturates for large
D_{\mathrm{ne}}
, in principle permitting extraction of orders of magnitude more power than required to run the engine, because larger noise strength offers more fluctuations to rectify but does not affect the controller.

Figure
4
(b) shows the engine’s efficiency
\Upsilon\equiv\dot{F}/P_{\mathrm{info}}
, the ratio of output to input powers. With sufficiently strong nonequilibrium fluctuations, output power can exceed input power. In contrast, a “conventional” engine that drags the particle upwards against gravity at the same velocity
v=\dot{F}/\delta_{\mathrm{g}}
requires trap power
\dot{W}=v^{2}+\delta_{\mathrm{g}}v
and has efficiency
<1
(see Appendix). This conventional strategy is more efficient at low output power, illustrating that rectifying purely thermal fluctuations is inefficient when using a measuring device and controller that operate at the same temperature. However, with sufficiently strong nonequilibrium fluctuations the information ratchet can extract energy more efficiently. Experimentally, we do not reach the regime of larger output than input power; however, with the largest experimental noise strengths, the information ratchet is more efficient than the conventional dragging strategy. Increasing the electric field strengths by using more closely spaced electrodes and more careful choices of electrode material and bath composition could substantially increase the achievable
D_{\mathrm{ne}}
and lead to output powers that exceed minimum information-processing costs.

Discussion and Conclusion.
—We have shown experimentally that an information engine in contact with a nonequilibrium bath can extract and store an order of magnitude greater power than it can in the same bath without active fluctuations. If the measuring and control devices are at the nominal temperature of the bath (without external forcing) and if the forcing of the nonequilibrium bath is sufficiently strong, then more energy can be extracted than the minimum energy needed to run the measurement-and-control system. Our experiments achieved an efficiency of
48.5%, limited only by the amount of forcing we were able to supply the bath.

To understand the significance of these experimental results, we recall that information engines can be connected to two heat baths, a higher-temperature one for the engine itself and a lower-temperature one for the measuring device and controller
Still 2020
. This generalized information engine framework was used recently to automate not only the demon’s function
Szilard 2003
but also its information processing
Still 2020
. Such engines can produce net positive work output and, with optimal information processing, have efficiencies that approach the Carnot limit
Still and Daimer 2022
. Similarly, passive ratchets can extract net work only when connected to heat baths at different temperatures
Smoluchowski 1912
;
Feynman et al. 1963
;
Parrondo and Español 1996
.

As discussed in the Introduction, typical temperature ratios are
\lesssim 10
; however, although an information engine is limited by the Carnot efficiency associated with the ratio of (effective) temperatures of the two baths, it extracts energy from only a very small fraction of modes of the high-temperature bath. In particular, as Fig.
S4
(a) shows, an engine need only be supplied with modes slightly exceeding
f_{\mathrm{c}}
(e.g.,
f_{\mathrm{ne}}\sim 10^{3}
Hz) to achieve half the maximum possible output. By contrast, equipartition implies that a bath in equilibrium at a temperature
T
has modes with equal energies up to phonon frequencies,
k_{\textrm{B}}T/h\approx\mathcal{O}(10^{13})
Hz, with
h
Planck’s constant
Nyquist 1928
. Thus, the fraction of forced modes is only
10^{-10}
. Put another way, if the nonequilibrium forcing is removed and the bath returns to equilibrium, its temperature does not measurably increase.

Engines can thus extract work from nonequilibrium modes, while the associated measuring device remains at equilibrium. Given a white-noise spectrum with frequencies 10–100
\times
higher than the engine cutoff frequency, we can assign an effective temperature to the nonequilibrium bath. Because work extraction draws on so few modes, the effective temperature of the bath can be orders of magnitude higher than physical temperatures.

Very high effective temperature and correspondingly large work-extraction rates are implicit in many old technologies: Sailboats move because their sails are constantly adjusted to catch the wind, and wind turbines similarly generate power by adjusting their rotors to be normal to the fluctuating wind direction
Stavrakakis 2012
. On a smaller scale, self-winding watches, first developed in the 18th century
Watkins 2016
, rectify the nonequilibrium fluctuations supplied by movements of the wearer’s arm
Parrondo and Español 1996
. More recent experimental realizations include ratchets driven by granular gasses
Eshuis et al. 2010
;
Joubaud et al. 2012
;
Gnoli et al. 2013a
;
Gnoli et al. 2013b
;
Lagoin et al. 2022
that achieve effective temperatures up to
10^{15}\times
room temperature
Rouyer and Menon 2000
;
Feitosa and Menon 2004
;
Chastaing et al. 2017
and rotors driven by turbulence
Francois et al. 2020
.

In the above examples, the scale of the system correlates with the power extracted. Our experiments use micron-scale beads and extract powers of 2.97
k_{\mathrm{B}}T/\tau_{\mathrm{r}}\approx 10^{-17}
W. For granular media, millimeter-sized beads lead to extracted powers of
10^{-6}
W
Lagoin et al. 2022
. For wind turbines, the 100-m scale blades lead to extracted powers of
10^{6}
W
Mathew and Philip 2012
. Thus, larger length scales increase the power that can be extracted from a fluctuating environment.

Nonequilibrium fluctuations can also be generated by active media
Ramaswamy 2010
;
Marchetti et al. 2013
;
Sokolov et al. 2010
;
Di Leonardo et al. 2010
;
Olson Reichhardt and Reichhardt 2017
;
Vizsnyiczai et al. 2017
;
Pietzonka et al. 2019
;
Fodor and Cates 2021
;
Speck 2016
;
Fodor et al. 2016
;
Mandal et al. 2017
;
Pietzonka and Seifert 2018
;
Dabelow et al. 2019
;
Caprini et al. 2019
;
Dabelow and Eichhorn 2021
such as (suspensions of) microswimmers
Elgeti et al. 2015
and active Brownian particles
Bechinger et al. 2016
. The vastly higher values of effective temperature that one can achieve relative to physical temperatures suggest the potential for drastic efficiency increases. Indeed, we have seen that the work extracted can in principle exceed the minimum information costs associated with the engine function. We then speculate that exploitation of fluctuations, as shown here, could be an organizing principle for molecular machinery, where strong nonequilibrium fluctuations
Mizuno et al. 2007
;
Gallet et al. 2009
have been shown to speed up various cellular processes
Ariga et al. 2021
;
Tripathi et al. 2022
.

Finally, our results also highlight a different way to understand energy harvesting
Beeby et al. 2006
;
Priya 2007
;
Cook-Chennault et al. 2008
;
Mitcheson et al. 2008
by microscopic devices. Such analyses are often specific to the type of systems analyzed. For example, mechanical energy harvesters depend heavily on resonant-forcing mechanisms that make inefficient use of the spectrum of fluctuations
Gammaitoni et al. 2011
. Our approach gives maximum estimates (for a given fluctuation spectrum) of the power that can in principle be extracted and can thus serve as benchmarks for existing systems and may suggest new extraction strategies. The question, which has only begun to be addressed in special cases, is whether “intelligently chosen interventions”
Liu et al. 2019
can outperform standard passive rectification strategies, such as full-wave rectifier bridge circuits based on diodes or Brownian ratchets
Lopez et al. 2008
.

Numerical simulations

We simulate the dynamics of the engine with nonequilibrium fluctuations using discrete-time dynamics to model the evolution of the bead and the nonequilibrium noise from one time step to the next. To this end, we integrate over one time step the coupled Langevin equations for the bead
x
[Eq. (1)]
and for the nonequilibrium noise
\zeta
[Eq. (3)].
Then the new particle position
x_{k+1}
and the next value of the noise
\zeta_{k+1}
are drawn from a bivariate Gaussian distribution
\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{r};\bm{\mu},\mathbf{\Sigma})
in
\mathbf{r}
with mean
\bm{\mu}
and covariance matrix
\mathbf{\Sigma}
. Here,

\displaystyle p(x_{k+1},\zeta_{k+1}|x_{k},\zeta_{k})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{N}\left[\begin{pmatrix}x_{k+1}\\
\zeta_{k+1}\end{pmatrix};\begin{pmatrix}\mu_{x_{k+1}}(x_{k},\zeta_{k})\\
\e^{-f_{\mathrm{ne}}t_{\mathrm{s}}}\zeta_{k}\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}c_{xx}&c_{x\zeta}\\
c_{x\zeta}&c_{\zeta\zeta}\end{pmatrix}\right]

(S1)

with mean

\displaystyle\mu_{x_{k+1}}(x_{k},\zeta_{k})=\left(x_{k}-\lambda_{k}+\delta_{\mathrm{g}}\right)\e^{-t_{\mathrm{s}}}+\lambda_{k}-\delta_{\mathrm{g}}+\zeta_{k}\frac{1}{1-f_{\mathrm{ne}}}\left(\e^{-f_{\mathrm{ne}}t_{\mathrm{s}}}-\e^{-t_{\mathrm{s}}}\right)\,,

(S2)

and covariances

\displaystyle c_{xx}

\displaystyle=\frac{4D_{\mathrm{ne}}f_{\mathrm{ne}}^{2}}{(1-f_{\mathrm{ne}})^{2}(1+f_{\mathrm{ne}})}\,\e^{-(1+f_{\mathrm{ne}})t_{\mathrm{s}}}-\frac{D_{\mathrm{ne}}f_{\mathrm{ne}}}{(1-f_{\mathrm{ne}})^{2}}\e^{-2f_{\mathrm{ne}}t_{\mathrm{s}}}-\frac{D_{\mathrm{ne}}f_{\mathrm{ne}}^{2}+(1-f_{\mathrm{ne}})^{2}}{(1-f_{\mathrm{ne}})^{2}}\e^{-2t_{\mathrm{s}}}+\frac{1+D_{\mathrm{ne}}f_{\mathrm{ne}}+f_{\mathrm{ne}}}{1+f_{\mathrm{ne}}}

(S3a)

\displaystyle c_{x\zeta}

\displaystyle=D_{\mathrm{ne}}\Bigg[\frac{2f_{\mathrm{ne}}^{2}}{1-f_{\mathrm{ne}}^{2}}\e^{-(1+f_{\mathrm{ne}})t_{\mathrm{s}}}-\frac{f_{\mathrm{ne}}}{1-f_{\mathrm{ne}}}\e^{-2f_{\mathrm{ne}}t_{\mathrm{s}}}+\frac{f_{\mathrm{ne}}}{1+f_{\mathrm{ne}}}\Bigg]

(S3b)

\displaystyle c_{\zeta\zeta}

\displaystyle=D_{\mathrm{ne}}f_{\mathrm{ne}}\left(1-\e^{-2f_{\mathrm{ne}}t_{\mathrm{s}}}\right)\,,

(S3c)

Equation (
S1
) was used to simulate the relaxation of the bead in a static potential at
\lambda_{k}

\displaystyle\lambda_{k}=\lambda_{k-1}+2\,\Theta(x_{k}-\lambda_{k-1})\,(x_{k}-\lambda_{k-1})\,,

where

from

Appendix D
Nonequilibrium noise strength

Here we discuss the method used to determine experimentally the strength
D_{\mathrm{ne}}
of the nonequilibrium noise source. The measurements are done independently from the information-engine experiments. We determine
D_{\mathrm{ne}}
by measuring the variance of bead displacements
\Delta x:=x_{k+1}-x_{k}
in a static trap in a bath with nonequilibrium-noise cutoff frequency (
f_{\mathrm{ne}}=24~\mathrm{kHz})
;
see Fig.
S3
. At that frequency the bead essentially experiences white-noise fluctuations, such that (in scaled units)
\mathrm{Var}(\Delta x)\approx(1+D_{\mathrm{ne}})\left(1-\e^{-t_{\mathrm{s}}}\right)
, which in principle permits determination of
D_{\mathrm{ne}}
.

To also account for small corrections arising from the finite frequency of the nonequilibrium noise, we calculate the full expression for the variance of bead displacements. The dynamics of the bead in a static trap with nonequilibrium noise are given by the propagator in Eq. (
S1
) and setting
\delta_{\mathrm{g}}=\lambda_{k}=0
. Specifically,

\displaystyle p(x_{k+1}|x_{k},\zeta_{k})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{N}\left[x_{k+1};\mu_{x_{k+1}}(x_{k},\zeta_{k}),c_{xx}\right]\,,

(S5)

a Gaussian with mean
\mu_{x_{k+1}}(x_{k},\zeta_{k})
and variance
c_{xx}
given by Eqs. (
S2
) and (
S3a
), respectively.

From Eq. (
S1
) we also obtain the stationary probability distribution by taking the limit
t_{\mathrm{s}}\to\infty
,

\displaystyle\pi(x_{k},z_{k})=\mathcal{N}\left[\begin{pmatrix}x_{k}\\
\zeta_{k}\end{pmatrix};\begin{pmatrix}0\\
0\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}1+\frac{D_{\mathrm{ne}}f_{\mathrm{ne}}}{1+f_{\mathrm{ne}}}&\frac{D_{\mathrm{ne}}f_{\mathrm{ne}}}{1+f_{\mathrm{ne}}}\\
\frac{D_{\mathrm{ne}}f_{\mathrm{ne}}}{1+f_{\mathrm{ne}}}&D_{\mathrm{ne}}f_{\mathrm{ne}}\end{pmatrix}\right]\,.

(S6)

The distribution of the increment given
x_{k}
and
\zeta_{k}
reads

\displaystyle p(\Delta x|x_{k},\zeta_{k})=\mathcal{N}\left[\Delta x;\mu_{x}(x_{k},\zeta_{k})-x_{k},c_{xx}\right]\,.

(S7)

Averaging this distribution over the stationary distribution of
x_{k}
and
\zeta_{k}
in Eq. (
S6
) yields the average variance of the increment,

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}(\Delta x)

\displaystyle=(e^{-t_{\mathrm{s}}}-1)^{2}\left(1+\frac{D_{\mathrm{ne}}f_{\mathrm{ne}}}{1+f_{\mathrm{ne}}}\right)

\displaystyle\quad+2\frac{(e^{-t_{\mathrm{s}}}-1)}{1-f_{\mathrm{ne}}}\left(\e^{-f_{\mathrm{ne}}t_{\mathrm{s}}}-\e^{-t_{\mathrm{s}}}\right)\frac{D_{\mathrm{ne}}f_{\mathrm{ne}}}{1+f_{\mathrm{ne}}}

\displaystyle\quad+\left[\frac{1}{1-f_{\mathrm{ne}}}\left(\e^{-f_{\mathrm{ne}}t_{\mathrm{s}}}-\e^{-t_{\mathrm{s}}}\right)\right]^{2}D_{\mathrm{ne}}f_{\mathrm{ne}}+c_{xx}

(S8a)

\displaystyle=\frac{2f_{\mathrm{ne}}D_{\mathrm{ne}}}{f_{\mathrm{ne}}^{2}-1}\left(f_{\mathrm{ne}}-1-f_{\mathrm{ne}}e^{-t_{\mathrm{s}}}+e^{-f_{\mathrm{ne}}t_{\mathrm{s}}}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad+2\left(1-e^{-t_{\mathrm{s}}}\right)\,.

(S8b)

The increase of bead displacement variance relative to the equilibrium case thus reads

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{Var}(\Delta x)}{\mathrm{Var}_{0}(\Delta x)}

\displaystyle=1+D_{\mathrm{ne}}\frac{f_{\mathrm{ne}}\left(f_{\mathrm{ne}}-1-f_{\mathrm{ne}}e^{-t_{\mathrm{s}}}+e^{-f_{\mathrm{ne}}t_{\mathrm{s}}}\right)}{\left(f_{\mathrm{ne}}^{2}-1\right)\left(1-e^{-t_{\mathrm{s}}}\right)}\,.

(S9)

Equation (
S9
) was used to estimate
D_{\mathrm{ne}}
in experiments, where the typical parameter values were
t_{\mathrm{s}}=20
µs
/\tau_{\mathrm{r}}=0.025
and
f_{\mathrm{ne}}=24~\mathrm{kHz}/f_{\mathrm{c}}=118
.
Note that, in the white-noise limit where
f_{\mathrm{ne}}\gg 1
, the right-hand size of Eq. (
S9
) reduces to
1+D_{\mathrm{ne}}
, indicating that the position increments result from white-noise fluctuations with a higher effective temperature, in agreement with Eq. (7) in the main text.

Appendix E
Information efficiency

Reference
Ehrich et al. 2022
shows that to carry out feedback control, a work
W_{k}^{\mathrm{add}}
is needed in addition to the trap work. With no nonequilibrium noise (
D_{\mathrm{ne}}=0
) and for feedback rules that are independent of the current controller state, i.e., rules of the form
p_{\mathrm{c}}(z_{k}|x_{k})
, with
z_{k}
the state of the feedback controller, the additional work is no less than the change in entropy achieved by the control operation,

\displaystyle W_{k}^{\mathrm{add}}

\displaystyle\geq k_{\mathrm{B}}T\left(H[Z_{k-1}|X_{k}]-H[Z_{k}|X_{k}]\right)

(S10a)

\displaystyle=k_{\mathrm{B}}T\left(H[X_{k},Z_{k-1}]-H[X_{k},Z_{k}]\right)

(S10b)

\displaystyle=:-k_{\mathrm{B}}T\Delta H^{Z}_{k}\,,

(S10c)

where
T
is the temperature of the feedback-controlled system and the controller, and
\Delta H^{Z}_{k}
is the change in joint entropy
(
Cover and Thomas 2006
, Chap. 2.2)
due to the dynamics of the controller
Z
.

Our information engine differs from the description in Ref.
Ehrich et al. 2022
in two ways: First, the particle (but not the controller) is subjected to additional colored nonequilibrium noise whose exponential correlation leads to long-time correlations between particle positions, thus violating the Markov assumption. Second, the feedback rule (
S4
) is
recursive
: the next trap position
\lambda_{k}
depends not only on the current particle position
x_{k}
but also on the current trap position
\lambda_{k-1}
.

The first difference is addressed by focusing on the white-noise limit of the nonequilibrium noise,
f_{\mathrm{ne}}\to\infty
, thus ensuring Markovian particle dynamics at an effective temperature
T_{\mathrm{ne}}
which is higher than the temperature
T
of the controller. The second difference does not impact the minimum additional work in Eqs. (
S10
), as we show below.

E.1
Recursive controller architecture

Reference
Ehrich et al. 2022
shows that Eq. (
S10
) is saturated by a control protocol that quasistatically and reversibly carries the conditional controller state from the distribution before the controller update,
p(z_{k-1}|x_{k})
, to the distribution after the controller update,
p(z_{k}|x_{k})
, by choosing a time-dependent control potential for the controller state
Z
such that the conditional controller distributions correspond to the equilibrium distribution under the control potential at the beginning and end of the controller update. The update can be made arbitrarily short yet still reversible when the controller mobility during the update is large.

Here we require recursive controller updates
p_{\mathrm{c}}(z_{k}|x_{k},z_{k-1})
. The protocol of Ref.
Ehrich et al. 2022
cannot be applied directly because, during its update to
p_{\mathrm{c}}(z_{k}|x_{k})
, all memory of the previous controller state
z_{k-1}
was lost; however, this can be remedied by adding a temporary auxiliary variable
u
to the controller, as follows: Starting out in an uncorrelated reference state, the controller state
z_{k-1}
is copied to the auxiliary variable
u
. Next, the controller update is carried out as described in Ref.
Ehrich et al. 2022
with
u
taking the role of the previous controller state
z_{k-1}
:
p_{\mathrm{c}}(z_{k}|x_{k},z_{k-1}=u)
. Finally, the auxiliary variable is reset to the initial uncorrelated reference state. Reference
Wolpert et al. 2019
argues that such auxiliary, or hidden, variables are generally necessary when implementing computations with Markovian dynamics.

As in Ref.
Ehrich et al. 2022
, all steps are carried out quasistatically and reversibly, such that the total heat flow equals the total change in joint entropy,

\displaystyle\Delta H^{Z}_{k}

\displaystyle:=H[X_{k},Z_{k}]+H_{\mathrm{ref}}[U]-\left(H[X_{k},Z_{k-1}]+H_{\mathrm{ref}}[U]\right)

(S11a)

\displaystyle=H[X_{k},Z_{k}]-H[X_{k},Z_{k-1}]\,,

(S11b)

where
H_{\mathrm{ref}}[U]
is the entropy of the auxiliary variable’s reference state. Consequently, the additional work needs to at least compensate the decrease in joint entropy by the controller.

For our information ratchet, a minimal model of the controller state
z_{k}
is simply the trap position
\lambda_{k}
. Using rescaled units and dividing by the sampling time
t_{\mathrm{s}}
gives the lower bound for the
additional power
required to update the trap position,

\displaystyle P_{\mathrm{info}}

\displaystyle:=-\frac{H[X_{k},\Lambda_{k}]-H[X_{k},\Lambda_{k-1}]}{t_{\mathrm{s}}}

(S12a)

\displaystyle=\frac{H[\Lambda_{k-1}|X_{k}]-H[\Lambda_{k}|X_{k}]}{t_{\mathrm{s}}}\,,

(S12b)

which we call
information power
, since it is defined purely in terms of the information gathered through the operation of feedback control. It represents the minimum additional power that must be supplied to the engine to make the feedback step thermodynamically consistent. The real experimental equipment dissipates orders of magnitude more power than this lower bound; however, this need not deter us from studying the fundamental limits of this information engine.

E.2
Steady-state ratcheting

To evaluate Eq. (
S12b
), we require the conditional distributions
p(\lambda_{k-1}|x_{k})
before the trap update and
p(\lambda_{k}|x_{k})
after the trap update.

We use the fact that, after many feedback steps, the distributions of the
relative coordinates

\displaystyle r_{k}

\displaystyle:=\lambda_{k}-x_{k}

(S13a)

\displaystyle r_{k}^{-}

\displaystyle:=\lambda_{k-1}-x_{k}

(S13b)

become stationary, i.e.,

\displaystyle p(\lambda_{k}|x_{k})

\displaystyle=p(r_{k}+x_{k}|x_{k})\to\pi(r_{k})

(S14a)

\displaystyle p(\lambda_{k-1}|x_{k})

\displaystyle=p(r_{k}^{-}+x_{k}|x_{k})\to\pi^{-}(r_{k}^{-})\,.

(S14b)

This allows us to rewrite the conditional entropy

\displaystyle H[\Lambda_{k}|X_{k}]

\displaystyle=-\int\mathrm{d}x_{k}\int\mathrm{d}\lambda_{k}\,p(x_{k},\lambda_{k})\ln p(\lambda_{k}|x_{k})

(S15a)

\displaystyle=-\int\mathrm{d}\lambda_{k}p(\lambda_{k})\int\mathrm{d}r_{k}\,\pi(r_{k})\ln\pi(r_{k})

(S15b)

\displaystyle=-\int\mathrm{d}r_{k}\,\pi(r_{k})\ln\pi(r_{k})

(S15c)

\displaystyle=:H[R_{k}]\,,

(S15d)

in terms of the entropy of the relative variable,
H[R_{k}]
, and similarly for

\displaystyle H[\Lambda_{k-1}|X_{k}]

\displaystyle=-\int\mathrm{d}r_{k}\,\pi^{-}(r_{k}^{-})\ln\pi^{-}(r_{k}^{-})

(S16a)

\displaystyle=:H[R_{k}^{-}]\ .

(S16b)

E.2.1
Approximations for steady-state relative distributions

It remains to determine the stationary distributions
\pi(r)
and
\pi^{-}(r^{-})
for which, unfortunately, no closed-form solutions exist. We can, however, approximate them to low order in the sampling time
t_{\mathrm{s}}
.

With feedback sufficiently fast, every fluctuation over the threshold is immediately caught and the trap moved in response such that the relative coordinate is reflected to the other side of the threshold. Hence, the dynamics relative to the trapping potential can be approximated by diffusion with a reflecting boundary at
r=0
, which has the stationary distribution

\displaystyle\pi(r)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}A\,\exp\left[-\frac{(r-\delta_{\mathrm{g}})^{2}}{2(1+D_{\mathrm{ne}})}\right]&r\geq 0\\
0&r<0\end{cases}\,,

(S17)

i.e., the equilibrium distribution at relative temperature
1+D_{\mathrm{ne}}
but truncated at
r=0
. Normalization implies

\displaystyle A=\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi(1+D_{\mathrm{ne}})}}\left[1+\erf\left(\frac{\delta_{\mathrm{g}}}{\sqrt{2(1+D_{\mathrm{ne}})}}\right)\right]^{-1}\,.

(S18)

This approximation holds to leading order in the sampling time
t_{\mathrm{s}}
, as we now demonstrate.

The dynamics in Eq. (1) of the main text
in the white-noise (
f_{\mathrm{ne}}\to\infty
) limit lead to the propagator

\displaystyle p_{x}(x_{k+1}|x_{k},\lambda_{k})=\mathcal{N}\Big[x_{k+1};\mu_{x}(x_{k},\lambda_{k};t_{\mathrm{s}}),c(t_{\mathrm{s}})\Big]\,,

(S19)

a Gaussian with mean

\displaystyle\mu_{x}(x_{k},\lambda_{k};t_{\mathrm{s}})=(x_{k}-\lambda_{k})\e^{-t_{\mathrm{s}}}+\lambda_{k}-(1-\e^{-t_{\mathrm{s}}})\delta_{\mathrm{g}}\,

(S20)

and variance

\displaystyle c(t_{\mathrm{s}})=(1+D_{\mathrm{ne}})(1-\e^{-2t_{\mathrm{s}}})\,.

(S21)

In relative coordinates,

\displaystyle p_{r}(r^{-}|r)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{N}\Big[r^{-};r\e^{-t_{\mathrm{s}}}+(1-\e^{-t_{\mathrm{s}}})\delta_{\mathrm{g}},c(t_{\mathrm{s}})\big]\,.

(S22)

Thus, the stationary distribution before feedback is

\displaystyle\pi^{-}(r^{-})

\displaystyle=\int\mathrm{d}r\,p_{r}(r^{-}|r)\,\pi(r)

(S23a)

\displaystyle=\frac{A}{2}\exp\left[-\frac{(r^{-}-\delta_{\mathrm{g}})^{2}}{2(1+D_{\mathrm{ne}})}\right]

(S23b)

\displaystyle\quad\times\left\{1-\erf\left[\frac{(\delta_{\mathrm{g}}-r^{-})\e^{-t_{\mathrm{s}}}-\delta_{\mathrm{g}}}{\sqrt{2(1+D_{\mathrm{ne}})(1-\e^{-2t_{\mathrm{s}}})}}\right]\right\}\,.

In relative coordinates the feedback rule (
S4
) is

\displaystyle r=r^{-}-2r^{-}\Theta(r^{-})\,.

(S24)

This amounts to reflecting the negative tail of the distribution
\pi^{-}(r^{-})
about
r^{-}=0
, giving, for
r^{-}>0
and to leading order in
t_{\mathrm{s}}
,

\displaystyle\pi^{-}(r)+\pi^{-}(-r)

\displaystyle=\frac{A}{2}\exp\left[-\frac{(r^{-}-\delta_{\mathrm{g}})^{2}}{2(1+D_{\mathrm{ne}})}\right]

\displaystyle\quad\times\Bigg[2-\erf\left(\frac{r}{\sqrt{2(1+D_{\mathrm{ne}})t_{\mathrm{s}}}}\right)

\displaystyle\quad+\erf\left(\frac{r}{\sqrt{2(1+D_{\mathrm{ne}})t_{\mathrm{s}}}}\right)\Bigg]

(S25a)

\displaystyle=\pi(r)\,.

(S25b)

Thus, reflecting the stationary distribution recovers the truncated Gaussian in Eq. (
S17
).

With these approximations, we recover the correct rate of free-energy gain for
t_{\mathrm{s}}\to 0
. Using the fact that
\lambda_{k}=\lambda_{k-1}+2(x_{k}-\lambda_{k-1})
if
x_{k}-\lambda_{k}>0
, we get

\displaystyle\dot{F}

\displaystyle=\frac{\delta_{\mathrm{g}}}{t_{\mathrm{s}}}\int_{-\infty}^{x_{k}}\mathrm{d}\lambda_{k-1}\,2(x_{k}-\lambda_{k-1})p(\lambda_{k-1}|x_{k})

(S26a)

\displaystyle=-\frac{2\delta_{\mathrm{g}}}{t_{\mathrm{s}}}\int_{-\infty}^{0}\mathrm{d}r^{-}\,r^{-}\,\pi^{-}(r^{-})\,.

(S26b)

For small
t_{\mathrm{s}}
, the left tail of
\pi^{-}(r^{-})
is dominated by the contribution from the error function in Eq. (
S23b
), giving

\displaystyle\pi^{-}(r^{-})

\displaystyle\approx\frac{A}{2}\exp\left[-\frac{\delta_{\mathrm{g}}^{2}}{2(1+D_{\mathrm{ne}})}\right]

(S27)

\displaystyle\quad\times\left\{1-\erf\left[\frac{(\delta_{\mathrm{g}}-r^{-})\e^{-t_{\mathrm{s}}}-\delta_{\mathrm{g}}}{\sqrt{2(1+D_{\mathrm{ne}})(1-\e^{-2t_{\mathrm{s}}})}}\right]\right\}

and thus

\displaystyle\dot{F}

\displaystyle\overset{t_{\mathrm{s}}\to 0}{\longrightarrow}(1+D_{\mathrm{ne}})\,A\,\exp\left[-\frac{\delta_{\mathrm{g}}^{2}}{2(1+D_{\mathrm{ne}})}\right]\,.

(S28)

Together with
A
from Eq. (
S18
), this is Eq. (9)
of the main text, which itself originates from Ref.
Saha et al. 2021
, where it was calculated for

We use
\pi(r)
[Eq. (
S17
)] to numerically evaluate
H[R_{k}]
[Eq. (
S15b
)] and
\pi^{-}(r^{-})
[Eq. (

)], then numerically calculate

[Eq. (

in Eq. (

where

\displaystyle\to\begin{cases}\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi D_{\mathrm{ne}}}}\,\exp\left[-\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{r}{\sqrt{D_{\mathrm{ne}}}}\right)^{2}\right],&r\geq 0\\
0,&r<0\end{cases}

(S30a)

\displaystyle=\pi(r/\sqrt{D_{\mathrm{ne}}})

(S30b)

\displaystyle\pi^{-}(r^{-})

\displaystyle=\frac{A}{2}\exp\left[-\frac{(r^{-})^{2}}{2D_{\mathrm{ne}}}\right]

(S30c)

\displaystyle\quad\times\left\{1+\erf\left[\frac{r^{-}\e^{-t_{\mathrm{s}}}}{\sqrt{2D_{\mathrm{ne}}(1-\e^{-2t_{\mathrm{s}}})}}\right]\right\}

\displaystyle=\pi^{-}(r^{-}/\sqrt{D_{\mathrm{ne}}})\,.

(S30d)

Scaling the width of a distribution by a factor
a
, while keeping it normalized, results in an entropy increase of
\ln|a|

(
Cover and Thomas 2006
, Chap. 8.6)
. Since this scaling affects both distributions
\pi(r)
and
\pi^{-}(r^{-})
, the entropy difference in Eq. (
S12b
) remains unchanged, resulting in saturating information power for
D_{\mathrm{ne}}\to\infty
.

Figure 4(a) in the main text
compares the input information power
P_{\mathrm{info}}
with the output free-energy gain
\dot{F}
for different nonequilibrium-noise strengths
D_{\mathrm{ne}}
. When the noise is purely thermal, the costs of running the information engine exceed the benefit gained from extracting thermal fluctuations, in accordance with the second law. However, when the nonequilibrium environment supplies sufficiently large fluctuations, the output can dwarf the input by orders of magnitude.

E.3
Comparison to raising the particle without feedback

We contrast the information power necessary to run the information engine with the trap power required to drag the particle upwards without feedback. The equation of motion of the particle position
x
in a trap that is moved upwards at velocity
v
is

\displaystyle\dot{x}

\displaystyle=-\left[x-\lambda(t)\right]-\delta_{\mathrm{g}}+\xi(t)+\zeta(t)\,,

(S31)

where
\lambda(t)=vt
is the time-dependent trap position, and
\xi(t)
and
\zeta(t)
are the zero-mean thermal white noise and zero-mean nonequilibrium colored noise, respectively (see main text).

Then, the rate of work done on the particle (the trap power) is

\displaystyle\dot{W}

\displaystyle=\left\langle\partial_{\lambda}\left\{\frac{1}{2}\left[x-\lambda(t)\right]^{2}\right\}\dot{\lambda}(t)\right\rangle

(S32a)

\displaystyle=-v\left\langle x-\lambda(t)\right\rangle

(S32b)

\displaystyle=v^{2}+v\,\delta_{\mathrm{g}}\,,

(S32c)

where we used the average of Eq. (
S31
) in line (
S32c
),
v=\langle\dot{x}\rangle=-\langle[x-\lambda(t)]\rangle-\delta_{\mathrm{g}}
.

With
\dot{F}=v\delta_{\mathrm{g}}
, we compare the efficiency of the information engine with that of a feedback-free pulling strategy that achieves the same output power:

\displaystyle\dot{W}

\displaystyle=\dot{F}+\frac{\dot{F}^{2}}{\delta_{\mathrm{g}}^{2}}

(S33a)

\displaystyle>\dot{F}\,,

(S33b)

which indicates that the efficiency is bounded by one,
\dot{F}/\dot{W}<1
. Figure 4(b)
in the main text compares the efficiencies of both driving strategies. In contrast to the feedback-free pulling strategy, the information engine can have efficiency (or coefficient of performance) far greater than one.
