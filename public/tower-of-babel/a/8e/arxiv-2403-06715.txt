Abstract
. Motivated in part by a problem in simulated tempering (a form of Markov chain Monte Carlo) we seek to minimise, in a suitable sense, the time it takes a (regular) diffusion with instantaneous reflection at 0 and 1 to travel to 1 1 and then return to the origin (the so-called commute time from 0 to 1). Substantially extending results in a previous paper, we consider a dynamic version of this problem where the control mechanism is related to the diffusion’s drift via the corresponding scale function. We are only able to choose the drift at each point at the time of first visiting that point and the drift is constrained on a set of the form [ 0 , ℓ ) ∪ ( i , 1 ] [0,\ell)\cup(i,1] . This leads to a type of stochastic control problem with infinite dimensional state.

1.
Introduction

Suppose that
X^{\mu}
is a diffusion on
[0,1]
, started at 0 and given by

(1.1)

X^{\mu}_{t}=x+\int_{0}^{t}\sigma(X^{\mu}_{u})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}B_{u}+\int_{0}^{t}\mu(X^{\mu}_{u})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}u\,\,\,\text{ on $\,$ (0,1)}

with instantaneous reflection at 0 and 1 (see
[
10
]
or
[
7
]
for details).

Define
T_{x}
to be the first time that the diffusion
reaches
x
, then define
\mathcal{S}=\mathcal{S}(X^{\mu})
, the commute time (between 0 and 1),
by

\mathcal{S}(X^{\mu}):=\inf\left\{t>T_{1}(X^{\mu}):\>X^{\mu}_{t}=0\right\}.

In
[
8
]
, motivated by a question arising in simulated tempering (see
[
2
]
), we considered the following problem (and several variants and generalisations):

Problem 1.1
.

Minimise the expected commute time
\mathbb{E}[\,\mathcal{S}\,]
; i.e. find

\inf_{\mu}\mathbb{E}_{x}[\mathcal{S}(X^{\mu})],

or, more generally,
find, for suitable positive functions
f
,

(1.2)

\Phi(x):=\inf_{\mu}[\Phi(\mu,x)]\text{ where }\Phi(\mu,x):=\mathbb{E}_{x}[\int_{0}^{\mathcal{S}}f(X^{\mu}_{t})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t],

and where the drift at each level must be chosen at or before
X^{\mu}
’s first visit to that level and thereafter remains fixed.

The commute time is defined for random walks on graphs in
[
1
]
. The original commute time identity (a version of which we give later in (
3.1
)), was only discovered in 1989 and first appeared in
[
3
]
.
We gave the optimal drift to minimise the quantity in (
1.2
) in Theorem 4.6 of
[
8
]
, under the assumption that
\mu
was already fixed on some interval
[0,y)
, the drift is otherwise unconstrained and the starting state is in
[0,y)
. We left open the question of the optimal control when
\mu
is, initially, fixed on some other interval.
The key observation in
[
8
]
was that we can follow the same solution as for the static case – where we choose the drift function at time 0 – because ‘there can be no surprises’ (in the path of
X
). This statement is no longer valid when the set on which the drift is constrained is not of the form
[0,y)
and we gave, as an example in Remark 4.1 of
[
8
]
, a heuristic argument for why a different solution would be optimal in the case where the constraint set was of the form
(i,1]
.

Our aim, in the current paper, is to present the solution (in Theorem
4.2
) to the dynamic problem in this case, where the ‘surprises’ are how far down the controlled process gets before time
T_{1}
.

The structure of the paper is as follows: in section 2 we give a formal definition of the model; section 3 is devoted to calculating the candidate value function; section 4 gives the proof that this is correct and we then give some concluding remarks.

2.
The model and some notation

2.1.
The model

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{F}^{x,s_{0}}=

\displaystyle\{s\in\mathcal{C}_{F}^{s_{0}}\text{ s.t. there exists a p.m. }\mathbb{P}_{s,x}:\>\>\text{ with }X_{0}=x\;\mathbb{P}_{s,x}-a.s.\text{ and }

(2.5)

\displaystyle\phantom{xxx}ds(X_{t},\omega)=s^{\prime}(X_{t},\omega)\sigma(X_{t})dB_{t},\text{ for some }\mathbb{P}_{s,x}-\text{Brownian Motion},\;B\},

We denote the expectation corresponding to such a p.m.

Recall that

We are able to choose

More precisely, we will assume the following:

Assumption 2.1

For a given level
\ell

\ell<i

has been fixed at every level on

For a given positive cost function

(2.7)

2.3.
Heuristic for the optimal strategy.

Strategy
s
has been fixed on
C_{0}:=[0,\ell)\cup(i_{0},1]
, and we need to determine how to proceed on
[\ell,i_{0}]
. Since we are only allowed to choose
s^{\prime}
once for each level, we choose a strategy at
y\in(\ell,i_{0})
before time
T_{1}
only if such a level is reached from above before hitting
1
. Conversely, if
\mathcal{I}_{T_{1}}>y
, then we need only choose the drift at level
y
after the process has hit level 1. Consequently we are not constrained to enable the process to hit level 1 (again) so may choose an arbitrarily large downward drift at any such level.

Our optimal control should respect this and we proceed to calculate the optimal control in this class.

3.2.
A calculus of variations approach

We wish to find

To do this, we first treat

Lemma 3.5

V(i):=\kappa+bc+\inf_{t\geq b}\left\{ab\left(1+\frac{k}{t}+\ln\frac{t}{b}\right)+\frac{\beta^{2}(i)}{e^{1+\frac{k}{t}+\ln\frac{t}{b}}\bigl(\phi(1+\frac{k}{t})-\phi(1+\frac{k}{t}+\ln\frac{t}{b})\bigr)}\right\}

Fixing

4.
The dynamic solution

Adopting the usual dynamic approach, we want to give the (conditional) future optimal payoff when
\mathcal{I}_{t}=i
and
X_{t}=x
(with
x\geq i
) (and the process has not yet reached level 1).

Since we cannot control the process until it reaches
\mathcal{I}_{t}
again, the payoff is given by

\displaystyle V(s,i,x)=V(i,x)=\mathbf{E}_{s,x}\int_{0}^{T_{1}\wedge T_{i}}f(X_{u})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}u+\frac{\tilde{s}(x)}{\tilde{s}(i)}V(i)+\left(1-\frac{\tilde{s}(x)}{\tilde{s}(i)}\right)\tilde{V}(x),

where

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}_{x}\int_{0}^{T_{1}\wedge T_{i}}f(X_{u})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}u

\displaystyle=-\int_{x}^{1}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}us^{\prime}(u)\tilde{m}_{f}(u)+\frac{\tilde{s}(x)}{\tilde{s}(i)}\int_{i}^{1}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}us^{\prime}(u)\tilde{m}_{f}(u)

\displaystyle\text{and }\tilde{V}(x)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{s,1}\int_{0}^{T_{i}}f(X_{u})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}u+\mathbb{E}_{l}^{*l}\int_{0}^{T_{0}}f(X_{u})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}u.

Observe that
\kappa=\mathbb{E}_{l}^{*l}\int_{0}^{T_{0}}f(X_{u})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}u
.

Equivalently, we can rewrite this as

\displaystyle V(i,x)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{E}_{s,x}\int_{0}^{T_{i}}f(X_{u})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}u+\frac{\tilde{s}(x)}{\tilde{s}(i)}V(i)+\left(1-\frac{\tilde{s}(x)}{\tilde{s}(i)}\right)\kappa

\displaystyle=\int_{i}^{x}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}us^{\prime}(u)\tilde{m}_{f}(u)+\frac{\tilde{s}(x)}{\tilde{s}(i)}V(i)+\left(1-\frac{\tilde{s}(x)}{\tilde{s}(i)}\right)\kappa

\displaystyle=\int_{i}^{x}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}us^{\prime}(u)\tilde{m}_{f}(u)+\frac{\tilde{s}(x)}{\tilde{s}(i)}(V(i)-\kappa)+\kappa

As is usual, to show that

really is the optimal payoff, we consider the

corresponding to using the policy

until time

and then behaving optimally.
What is non-standard here is the ‘phase change’ at time

— after

\mathcal{S}
just looks like

.

Consequently, we enlarge the state by including a flag process

so that the generic state becomes

(or more precisely
(s|_{C_{t}},C_{t},X_{t},F_{t})

),
and define

(4.1)

S^{s}_{t}=\int_{0}^{\mathcal{S}\wedge t}f(X_{u})du+V(s,C_{t},X_{\mathcal{S}\wedge t})1_{(t<T_{1})}+\tilde{V}(s,C_{{T_{1}}}X_{\mathcal{S}\wedge t})1_{(t\geq T_{1})},

where
\tilde{V}
is our proposed ‘post-
T_{1}

(4.2)

\tilde{V}(s,C,x)=\tilde{V}(x):=\begin{cases}\int_{i}^{x}s^{\prime}(v){\tilde{m}_{f}}(v)dv+\kappa&\text{ for }x\geq i\\
\kappa&\text{ for }x\in(\ell,i]\\
\int_{0}^{x}s^{\prime}(v)m_{f}(v,\ell)dv&\text{ for }x\in[0,\ell].\end{cases}

Theorem 4.1
.

Suppose that
(S^{s}_{t\wedge\mathcal{S}})_{t\geq 0}
is a
\mathbb{P}_{s,i}
-submartingale for any admissible policy
s
and

1.

\mathbb{E}_{s,x,i,0}[S^{\hat{s}}_{T_{1}}]=V(s,x,i)
for some admissible policy
\hat{s}
with

2.

\liminf\mathbb{E}_{\hat{s}^{n},x,i,1}[S^{\hat{s}^{n}}_{\mathcal{S}}]=\tilde{V}(s,C,x)
,
for some sequence of admissible policies
(\hat{s}^{n})_{n\geq 1}
with
\hat{s}^{n}\in\mathcal{A}^{i,s_{0}}_{C}

then

\mathbb{V}(s,C,x,f)=\begin{cases}V(s,C,x):&f=0\\
\tilde{V}(s,C,x):&f=1,\end{cases}

and
\hat{s}^{*}
is an optimal policy.

Proof.

Suppose that
s
is admissible and
(S^{s}_{t\wedge\mathcal{S}})_{t\geq 0}
is a submartingale and
F_{0}=1
then

\tilde{V}(s,C,x)=S_{0}\leq\mathbb{E}_{s,x,i,1}[S^{s}_{t\wedge\mathcal{S}}]=\mathbb{E}_{s,x}[\int_{0}^{T_{0}\wedge t}f(X_{u})du+\tilde{V}(s,C,X^{s}_{\\
T_{0}\wedge t})].

Letting

we see, by dominated convergence, that

\tilde{V}(s,C,x)\leq\mathbb{E}_{s,x}[\int_{0}^{T_{0}}f(X_{t})dt].

Minimising over admissible

yields
\mathbb{V}(s,C,x,1)\geq\tilde{V}(s,C,x).

Conversely,
\liminf\mathbb{E}_{\hat{s}^{n},x,i,1}[S^{\hat{s}^{n}}_{\mathcal{S}}]=\tilde{V}(s,C,x)
implies that

Now suppose that
\mathcal{S}
is admissible and
(S^{s}_{t\wedge\mathcal{S}})_{t\geq 0}

Since

is arbitrary we deduce

Conversely,
\mathbb{E}_{x,i,0}[S^{\hat{s}}_{\mathcal{S}}]=V(s,C,x,i)
implies that
\mathbb{V}(s,C,x,0)\leq V(s,C,x,i)

∎

5.
Concluding remarks and further problems

We remark first that the optimally controlled process is not a diffusion nor even strong Markov.
To make it into one we have to adopt an infinite dimensional statespace recording the choice of
s^{\prime}
at each level so far visited.

Although we have solved the minimisation problem when
C_{0}
is of the form
[0,\ell)\cup(i,1]
the problem remains open for other forms of constraint sets.
It is tempting to speculate that a similar approach to the one adopted here would, with much further work, yield a result when
C_{0}
is of the form

[0,\ell_{1})\cup(i_{2},\ell_{2})\cup\cdots\cup(i_{n},1]

and
X_{0}\geq i_{n}
but we have no suggestions as to the form of optimal controls for any other forms of the constraint set. A complete solution would be fascinating.

The corresponding network problem, where
X
is a reversible Markov chain on a specified graph
is equally interesting and merits further exploration.
