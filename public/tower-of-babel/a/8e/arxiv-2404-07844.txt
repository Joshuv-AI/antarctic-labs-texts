Adaptive Hyperbolic-cross-space Mapped Jacobi Method on Unbounded Domains with Applications to Solving Multidimensional Spatiotemporal Integrodifferential Equations
Yunhong Deng, Sihong Shao, Alex Mogilner, Mingtao Xia

Abstract
In this paper, we develop a new adaptive hyperbolic-cross-space mapped Jacobi (AHMJ) method for solving multidimensional spatiotemporal integrodifferential equations in unbounded domains. By devising adaptive techniques for sparse mapped Jacobi spectral expansions defined in a hyperbolic cross space, our proposed AHMJ method can efficiently solve various spatiotemporal integrodifferential equations such as the anomalous diffusion model with reduced numbers of basis functions. Our analysis of the AHMJ method gives a uniform upper error bound for solving a class of spatiotemporal integrodifferential equations, leading to effective error control.

Recently, novel adaptive techniques for spectral methods

However, compared to sparse spectral methods with spectral expansion in a proper hyperbolic cross space, full-tensor-product Hermite spectral expansions use much more basis functions without substantially improving accuracy and could thus be computationally ineffective.

\displaystyle\big(u(\boldsymbol{x},0),\tilde{v}\big)=\big(u_{0}(\boldsymbol{x}),\tilde{v}\big),\,\,\forall(v,\tilde{v})\in L^{2}\big([0,T];H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\big)\times H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d}).

We assume that

\displaystyle a\big(u,v;t\big)\leq C_{0}\|u\|_{H^{1}}\|v\|_{H^{1}},\,\,c_{0}\|u\|_{H^{1}}^{2}\leq a\big(u,u;t\big).

\forall u,v,\phi\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\Longrightarrow\big(f(u;t)-f(v;t),\phi\big)\leq L\|u-v\|_{L^{2}}\|\phi\|_{L^{2}}.

2.2
Hyperbolic-cross-space mapped Jacobi approximation

Now, we introduce the mapped Jacobi functions
[
14
,
15
,
18
,
16
]
defined in
\mathbb{R}
. We denote
\{j_{n}^{\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}}(\xi)\}_{n=0}^{\infty}
to be the set of Jacobi polynomials defined on the interval
(-1,1)
with two fixed parameters
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}>-1

[
13
, Chapter 3.2]
. Fixing
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}
, the Jacobi polynomials form a set of orthogonal basis functions
w.r.t.
the weight function
w^{\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}}\coloneqq(1-\xi)^{\alpha_{1}}(1+\xi)^{\alpha_{2}}
.

Given a one-to-one mapping
h_{\beta,r}(x)
from
x\in\mathbb{R}
to
\xi\in(-1,1)
, we can formulate a novel orthogonal basis in the unbounded domain
\mathbb{R}
, through the images of the Jacobi polynomials under the mapping
\xi\coloneqq h_{\beta,r}(x)
,

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}j_{m}^{\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}}(\xi)j_{n}^{\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}}(\xi)w^{\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}}(\xi)\frac{\text{d}\xi}{\text{d}x}\text{d}x=

\displaystyle\sqrt{\gamma_{m}\gamma_{n}}\delta_{m,n},

(2.3)

where
\gamma_{n}\coloneqq\int_{-1}^{1}(j_{n}^{\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}})^{2}w^{\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}}\text{d}\xi
. Here, we consider a family of mappings
h_{\beta,r}(x)
in
[
14
, Section 2.2]
defined by

\frac{\text{d}h_{\beta,r}(x)}{\text{d}x}=\beta\big(1-h_{\beta,r}^{2}(x)\big)^{1+r/2}\text{ and }h_{\beta,r}(0)=0.

(2.4)

Here,
\beta
is the scaling factor, and
r\geq 0
is a non-negative integer. For
r=0,1
,
h_{\beta,r}(x)
can computed explicitly as follows:

h_{\beta,r}(x)=\begin{cases}\tanh(\beta x)&\text{logarithmic mapping}~r=0\\
\displaystyle\frac{\beta x}{\sqrt{1+\beta^{2}x^{2}}}&\text{algebraic mapping}~r=1\end{cases}

(2.5)

Using the mapping
h_{\beta,r}(x)
defined in Eq. (
2.4
), we define the mapped Jacobi functions on the unbounded domain
\mathbb{R}
as

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}_{n,\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},r}^{\beta,x_{0}}(x)\coloneqq\frac{1}{\sqrt{\gamma_{n}}}j_{n}^{\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}}\big(h_{\beta,r}(x-x_{0})\big)\mu_{\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}}\big(h_{\beta,r}(x-x_{0})\big),

(2.6)

where
\mu_{\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}}\big(h_{\beta,r}(x)\big)\coloneqq\sqrt{w^{\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}}\big(h_{\beta,r}(x)\big)h^{\prime}_{\beta,r}(x)}
is the modified weight function. This modified weight function
\mu_{\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}}(x)
makes the mapped Jacobi functions
\{\mathcal{J}_{n,\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},r}^{\beta,x_{0}}\}
a complete and orthogonal basis of the Hilbert space
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})

[
15
, Proposition 2.2]
. For notational simplicity, we omit
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}
for the mapped Jacobi basis functions and the mapping parameter
r
in the subindex of
\mathcal{J}_{n,\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},r}^{\beta,x_{0}}(x)
,
i.e.
, we use the notation
\mathcal{J}_{n}^{\beta,x_{0}}(x)
instead.

Detailed theoretical properties of the mapped Jacobi functions can be found in
[
14
,
15
,
16
,
18
]
. Different from the generalized Hermite functions which decay at an exponential rate of
e^{-\frac{1}{2}|\beta x|^{2}}
for large
|x|
, the decaying rate of the mapped Jacobi basis functions can be tuned by choosing an appropriate
r
in the mapping Eq. (
2.4
). For example, when using the algebraic mapping (
r=1
in Eq. (
2.4
)), the mapped Jacobi basis functions decay at a rate of
|\beta x|^{-1}

[
15
]
for large

|x|
, which are suitable for approximating a function that decays faster than
|x|^{-1}
for large
|x|

[
14
]
.

We shall use sparse mapped Jacobi spectral expansions defined in the hyperbolic cross space
V_{N,\gamma}^{\boldsymbol{\beta},\boldsymbol{x}_{0}}
characterized by the hyperbolic cross index set
\Upsilon_{N,\gamma}
(defined in Table
1
). The following two inverse inequalities hold for the mapped Jacobi spectral expansions in the hyperbolic cross space
V_{N,\gamma}^{\boldsymbol{\beta},\boldsymbol{x}_{0}}
.

Lemma 2.2
.

For all
U_{N,\gamma}^{\boldsymbol{\beta},\boldsymbol{x}_{0}}\in V_{N,\gamma}^{\boldsymbol{\beta},\boldsymbol{x}_{0}}
,

\big\|\partial_{x_{i}}U_{N,\gamma}^{\boldsymbol{\beta},\boldsymbol{x}_{0}}\big\|_{L^{2}}\leq\beta_{i}^{3/2}N_{\alpha,r}^{1/2}\big\|U_{N,\gamma}^{\boldsymbol{\beta},\boldsymbol{x}_{0}}\big\|_{L^{2}},

(2.7)

If additionally restricting that
r\leq 1
in Eq. (
2.4
), we have

\big\|x_{i}\partial_{x_{i}}U_{N,\gamma}^{\boldsymbol{\beta},\boldsymbol{x}_{0}}\big\|_{L^{2}}\leq\beta_{i}^{1/2}N_{\alpha,r}^{1/2}\big\|U_{N,\gamma}^{\boldsymbol{\beta},\boldsymbol{x}_{0}}\big\|_{L^{2}},

(2.8)

where
N_{\alpha,r}\coloneqq 2N(N+\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2}+1)+2(1+\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2}+r/2)^{2}
.

2.3
Numerical scheme

Here, we describe the AHMJ method to solve the model problem Eq. (
1.1
). We define two function spaces,
X_{N,\gamma}^{\boldsymbol{\beta},\boldsymbol{x}_{0}}(t_{0},t_{1})
and
Y_{N,\gamma}^{\boldsymbol{\beta},\boldsymbol{x}_{0}}(t_{0},t_{1})
:

\displaystyle X_{N,\gamma}^{\boldsymbol{\beta},\boldsymbol{x}_{0}}(t_{0},t_{1})

\displaystyle\coloneqq\big\{U_{N,\gamma}^{\boldsymbol{\beta},\boldsymbol{x}_{0}}\in L^{2}([t_{0},t_{1}];V_{N,\gamma}^{\boldsymbol{\beta},\boldsymbol{x}_{0}}):\partial_{t}U_{N,\gamma}^{\boldsymbol{\beta},\boldsymbol{x}_{0}}\in L^{2}([t_{0},t_{1}];V_{N,\gamma}^{\boldsymbol{\beta},\boldsymbol{x}_{0}})\big\},

(2.9)

\displaystyle Y_{N,\gamma}^{\boldsymbol{\beta},\boldsymbol{x}_{0}}(t_{0},t_{1})

\displaystyle\coloneqq L^{2}\big([t_{0},t_{1}];V_{N,\gamma}^{\boldsymbol{\beta},\boldsymbol{x}_{0}}\big)\times V_{N,\gamma}^{\boldsymbol{\beta},\boldsymbol{x}_{0}}.

X_{N,\gamma}^{\boldsymbol{\beta},\boldsymbol{x}_{0}}(t_{0},t_{1})
is a subspace of the Sobolev-Bochner space
X(t_{0},t_{1})
, which inherits the norm
\|\cdot\|_{X(t_{0},t_{1})}
defined in Eq. (
2.1
). The space
Y_{N,\gamma}^{\boldsymbol{\beta},\boldsymbol{x}_{0}}(t_{0},t_{1})
is equipped with the norm

\|\boldsymbol{v}\|_{Y(t_{0},t_{1})}^{2}\coloneqq\|(v,\tilde{v})\|_{Y(t_{0},t_{1})}^{2}\coloneqq\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{1}}\|v\|_{H^{1}}^{2}\text{d}t+\|\tilde{v}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}.

(2.10)

To obtain a continuous-time mapped Jacobi approximation to the solution
u(\boldsymbol{x},t)
of the model problem Eq. (
1.1
), we wish to find

\tilde{U}_{N,\gamma}^{\boldsymbol{\beta},\boldsymbol{x}_{0}}(\boldsymbol{x},t)\coloneqq\sum_{\boldsymbol{n}\in\Upsilon_{N,\gamma}}\tilde{u}_{\boldsymbol{n}}^{\boldsymbol{\beta},\boldsymbol{x}_{0}}(t)\mathcal{J}_{\boldsymbol{n}}^{\boldsymbol{\beta},\boldsymbol{x}_{0}}(\boldsymbol{x})\in X_{N,\gamma}^{\boldsymbol{\beta},\boldsymbol{x}_{0}}(0,T),

(2.11)

such that
\forall\big(v_{N},\tilde{v}_{N}\big)\in Y_{N,\gamma}^{\boldsymbol{\beta},\boldsymbol{x}_{0}}(0,T)
,

\displaystyle\big(\partial_{t}\tilde{U}_{N,\gamma}^{\boldsymbol{\beta},\boldsymbol{x}_{0}},v_{N}\big)+a\big(\tilde{U}_{N,\gamma}^{\boldsymbol{\beta},\boldsymbol{x}_{0}},v_{N};t\big)=\big(f(\tilde{U}_{N,\gamma}^{\boldsymbol{\beta},\boldsymbol{x}_{0}};t),v_{N}\big),~\forall t\in[0,T],\,\,\big(\tilde{U}_{N,\gamma}^{\boldsymbol{\beta},\boldsymbol{x}_{0}}(\cdot,0),\tilde{v}_{N}\big)=\big(u_{0}(\cdot),\tilde{v}_{N}\big).

(2.12)

We rearrange the coefficients in the hyperbolic-cross-space mapped Jacobi spectral expansion
\tilde{U}_{N,\gamma}^{\boldsymbol{\beta},\boldsymbol{x}_{0}}
into a vector by arranging the coefficients in dictionary order,
i.e.
, we shall define the following order relation on the index set
\Upsilon_{N,\gamma}
:

\boldsymbol{n}^{1}\leq\boldsymbol{n}^{2}:\exists~i\in\mathbb{N}^{+}\text{ such that }n_{i}^{1}\leq n_{i}^{2}\text{ and }\forall j<i,~n_{j}^{1}=n_{j}^{2}.

(2.13)

Thus, the basis functions can be indexed by
\{1,\cdots,|\Upsilon_{N,\gamma}|\}
, and
\tilde{U}_{N,\gamma}^{\boldsymbol{\beta},\boldsymbol{x}_{0}}
can be rewritten as

\tilde{U}_{N,\gamma}^{\boldsymbol{\beta},\boldsymbol{x}_{0}}(\boldsymbol{x},t)\coloneqq\sum_{i=1}^{|\Upsilon_{N,\gamma}|}\tilde{u}_{i}^{\boldsymbol{\beta},\boldsymbol{x}_{0}}(t)\mathcal{J}_{\boldsymbol{n}^{i}}^{\boldsymbol{\beta},\boldsymbol{x}_{0}}(\boldsymbol{x}),

(2.14)

Denoting

\tilde{\boldsymbol{u}}_{N,\gamma}^{\boldsymbol{\beta},\boldsymbol{x}_{0}}(t)\coloneqq\big(\tilde{u}_{1}^{\boldsymbol{\beta},\boldsymbol{x}_{0}}(t),\cdots,\tilde{u}_{|\Upsilon_{N,\gamma}|}^{\boldsymbol{\beta},\boldsymbol{x}_{0}}(t)\big),

(2.15)

\tilde{\boldsymbol{u}}_{N,\gamma}^{\boldsymbol{\beta},\boldsymbol{x}_{0}}(t)
satisfies the following ODE

\displaystyle\frac{\text{d}}{\text{d}t}\tilde{\boldsymbol{u}}_{N,\gamma}^{\boldsymbol{\beta},\boldsymbol{x}_{0}}+A_{N}^{\boldsymbol{\beta}}(t)\tilde{\boldsymbol{u}}_{N,\gamma}^{\boldsymbol{\beta},\boldsymbol{x}_{0}}=F_{N}^{\boldsymbol{\beta}}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{u}}_{N,\gamma}^{\boldsymbol{\beta},\boldsymbol{x}_{0}};t),~\forall t\in[0,T],\,\,\tilde{u}_{i}^{\boldsymbol{\beta},\boldsymbol{x}_{0}}(0)=\big(u_{0},\mathcal{J}_{\boldsymbol{n}^{i}}^{\boldsymbol{\beta},\boldsymbol{x}_{0}}\big),~\forall\boldsymbol{n}\in\Upsilon_{N,\gamma}.

(2.16)

Additionally, when acting on
\tilde{\boldsymbol{u}}_{N,\gamma}^{\boldsymbol{\beta},\boldsymbol{x}_{0}}
, the
i^{\text{th}}
components of
A_{N}^{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\tilde{\boldsymbol{u}}_{N,\gamma}^{\boldsymbol{\beta},\boldsymbol{x}_{0}}
and
F_{N}^{\boldsymbol{\beta}}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{u}}_{N,\gamma}^{\boldsymbol{\beta},\boldsymbol{x}_{0}})
are calculated by

\displaystyle\big(A_{N}^{\boldsymbol{\beta}}(t)\tilde{\boldsymbol{u}}_{N,\gamma}^{\boldsymbol{\beta},\boldsymbol{x}_{0}}\big)_{i}

\displaystyle=\big(f\big(\tilde{U}_{N,\gamma}^{\boldsymbol{\beta},\boldsymbol{x}_{0}};t\big),\mathcal{J}_{\boldsymbol{n}^{i}}^{\boldsymbol{\beta},\boldsymbol{x}_{0}}\big).

(2.17)

(2.18)

the

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{u}_{N,\gamma}^{\boldsymbol{\beta},\boldsymbol{x}_{0}}(t_{\ell+1})

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{u}_{N,\gamma}^{\boldsymbol{\beta},\boldsymbol{x}_{0}}(t_{\ell})+\Delta t\sum_{s=1}^{q}b^{s}_{RK}G_{N}^{\boldsymbol{\beta}}(\boldsymbol{w}_{s},t_{\ell}+c^{s}_{RK}\Delta t),

(2.19)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{w}_{s}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{u}_{N,\gamma}^{\boldsymbol{\beta},\boldsymbol{x}_{0}}(t_{\ell})+\Delta t\sum_{r=1}^{q}a^{rs}_{RK}G_{N}^{\boldsymbol{\beta}}(\boldsymbol{w}_{r},t_{\ell}+c^{r}_{RK}\Delta t),

(2.20)

The numerical solution at

(2.21)

Theorem 3.2

4
Numerical Results

In this section, we first present the AHMJ method. To be specific, we introduce two hyperbolic-cross-space frequency indicators (
\mathcal{F}_{x_{i}}
and
\mathcal{F}_{p}
) tailored for hyperbolic cross space to properly adjust the scaling factors in each dimension and adjust the expansion order
N
of the hyperbolic cross space
V_{N,\gamma}^{\boldsymbol{\beta},\boldsymbol{x}_{0}}
.

First, for a hyperbolic-cross-space mapped Jacobi spectral expansion
U_{N,\gamma}^{\boldsymbol{\beta},\boldsymbol{x}_{0}}\in V_{N,\gamma}^{\boldsymbol{\beta},\boldsymbol{x}_{0}}
, we define the hyperbolic-cross-space frequency indicator in the
i^{\text{th}}
dimension as

\mathcal{F}_{x_{i}}(U_{N,\gamma}^{\boldsymbol{\beta},\boldsymbol{x}_{0}})\coloneqq\frac{\big\|U_{N,\gamma}^{\boldsymbol{\beta},\boldsymbol{x}_{0}}-\pi_{N,\gamma,i}^{\boldsymbol{\beta},\boldsymbol{x}_{0}}U_{N,\gamma}^{\boldsymbol{\beta},\boldsymbol{x}_{0}}\big\|_{L^{2}}}{\|U_{N,\gamma}^{\boldsymbol{\beta},\boldsymbol{x}_{0}}\|_{L^{2}}},\quad\forall i\in\{1,\cdots,d\}.

(4.1)

Here,
\pi_{N,\gamma,i}^{\boldsymbol{\beta},\boldsymbol{x}_{0}}
denotes the projection operator onto the space spanned by basis functions whose indices fall into the following index set

\Upsilon_{N,\gamma,i}\coloneqq\Big\{\boldsymbol{n}:\big|\big(n_{1},\cdots,\tfrac{3}{2}n_{i},\cdots,n_{d}\big)\big|_{\text{mix}}\cdot\big|\big(n_{1},\cdots,\tfrac{3}{2}n_{i},\cdots,n_{d}\big)\big|_{\infty}^{-\gamma}\leq N^{1-\gamma}\Big\},

(4.2)

(n_{1},\cdots,\tfrac{3}{2}n_{i},\cdots,n_{d})
indicates that the
i^{\text{th}}
component of
\boldsymbol{n}
is multiplied by a factor
\tfrac{3}{2}
(following the common
\tfrac{2}{3}
-rule
[
35
,
19
]
).
\mathcal{F}_{x_{i}}
thus measures the high-frequency components in the
i^{\text{th}}
direction of
U_{N,\gamma}^{\boldsymbol{\beta},\boldsymbol{x}_{0}}
.
\mathcal{F}_{x_{i}}
can help us adjust
\beta_{i}
, the scaling factor in the
i^{\text{th}}
dimension.

Next, we define the hyperbolic-cross-space expansion order frequency indicator
\mathcal{F}_{p}
for
U_{N,\gamma}^{\boldsymbol{\beta},\boldsymbol{x}_{0}}\in V_{N,\gamma}^{\boldsymbol{\beta},\boldsymbol{x}_{0}}

\mathcal{F}_{p}(U_{N,\gamma}^{\boldsymbol{\beta},\boldsymbol{x}_{0}})\coloneqq\frac{\|U_{N,\gamma}^{\boldsymbol{\beta},\boldsymbol{x}_{0}}-\pi_{N,\gamma,p}^{\boldsymbol{\beta},\boldsymbol{x}_{0}}U_{N,\gamma}^{\boldsymbol{\beta},\boldsymbol{x}_{0}}\|_{L^{2}}}{\|U_{N,\gamma}^{\boldsymbol{\beta},\boldsymbol{x}_{0}}\|_{L^{2}}}.

(4.3)

Here,
\pi_{N,\gamma,p}^{\boldsymbol{\beta},\boldsymbol{x}_{0}}
denotes the projection operator onto the space spanned by basis functions whose indices fall into the following index set

\Upsilon_{N,\gamma,p}\coloneqq\Big\{\boldsymbol{n}:|\boldsymbol{n}|_{\text{mix}}\cdot|\boldsymbol{n}|_{\infty}^{-\gamma}\leq\big(\tfrac{2}{3}N\big)^{1-\gamma}\Big\}.

(4.4)

\mathcal{F}_{p}
measures the overall high-frequency components in
U_{N,\gamma}^{\boldsymbol{\beta},\boldsymbol{x}_{0}}
. For one-dimensional spectral expansions, the hyperbolic-cross-space frequency indicators
\mathcal{F}_{x}
and
\mathcal{F}_{p}
coincide with the frequency indicators introduced in
[
19
,
20
]
.

Previous adaptive techniques for applying Hermite functions to solve multiple-dimensional spatiotemporal equations
use a “direct truncation" strategy
[
21
]
to calculate the direct-truncation-strategy frequency indicators in the
i^{\text{th}}
direction, and such strategies are mainly applied to the case of using the full-tensor-product spectral expansions instead of the sparse spectral expansions. As a comparison, we implement previous adaptive techniques for our sparse mapped Jacobi functions to solve multidimensional
spatiotemporal equations, which we refer to as the ADMJ method. Specifically, the ADMJ method calculates the direct-truncation-strategy frequency indicator in the
i^{\text{th}}
direction for adjusting
\beta_{i}
using the following formula

\tilde{\mathcal{F}}_{x_{i}}(U_{N,\gamma}^{\boldsymbol{\beta},\boldsymbol{x}_{0}})\coloneqq\frac{\|U_{N,\gamma}^{\boldsymbol{\beta},\boldsymbol{x}_{0}}-\tilde{\pi}_{N,\gamma,i}^{\boldsymbol{\beta},\boldsymbol{x}_{0}}U_{N,\gamma}^{\boldsymbol{\beta},\boldsymbol{x}_{0}}\|_{L^{2}}}{\|U_{N,\gamma}^{\boldsymbol{\beta},\boldsymbol{x}_{0}}\|_{L^{2}}},\quad\forall i\in\{1,\cdots,d\}.

(4.5)

Here,
\tilde{\pi}_{N,\gamma,i}^{\boldsymbol{\beta},\boldsymbol{x}_{0}}
denotes the projection operator onto the space spanned by basis functions whose indices fall into the following index set

\tilde{\Upsilon}_{N,\gamma,i}\coloneqq\Big\{\boldsymbol{n}\in\Upsilon_{N,\gamma}:n_{i}\leq\tfrac{2}{3}N\Big\}.

(4.6)

The direct-truncation-strategy expansion order frequency indicator
\tilde{\mathcal{F}}_{p}
for adjusting the expansion order

5
Conclusions

In this paper, we proposed an adaptive hyperbolic-cross-space mapped Jacobi (AHMJ) method for efficiently solving multidimensional spatiotemporal integrodifferential equations in unbounded domains whose solutions decay algebraically at infinity. We devised two hyperbolic-cross-space frequency indicators
\mathcal{F}_{x_{i}}
and
\mathcal{F}_{p}
in Eqs. (
4.1
) and (
4.3
) for efficiently implementing adaptive techniques to sparse multidimensional spectral expansions defined in hyperbolic cross spaces
[
23
,
25
]
. Our AHMJ method is more robust compared to previous adaptive techniques for spectral methods
[
19
,
20
,
22
]
and can effectively reduce the number of basis functions needed while maintaining accuracy. Additionally. we provided an upper error bound for applying our AHMJ method to solve a wide range of spatiotemporal integrodifferential equations. We showed that the error of implementing our AHMJ method can be effectively controlled
as long as we chose an appropriate hyperbolic cross space and properly implemented the adaptive techniques for the sparse spectral expansions.

A promising future direction is to figure out an appropriate strategy for modifying the hyperbolic cross space index
\gamma
, and to develop adaptive techniques on the asymmetric hyperbolic cross space as presented in
[
8
]
. This could require further investigation on how to tackle the heterogeneity of a multidimensional function. Furthermore, considering operator-splitting strategies for forwarding time, which are easier to implement than high-order implicit Runge-Kutta schemes, could be prospective
[
37
,
38
,
39
,
40
]
.
Also, it is prospective to extend hyperbolic-cross-space adaptive techniques to generalized Laguerre functions in the semi-unbounded domain
\mathbb{R}^{+}

[
41
,
42
,
43
]
.
Finally, applying the proposed AHMJ method to solve inverse problems of reconstructing spatiotemporal equations
[
44
]
is worth further research.

Appendix A
Proof of Theorem
2.1

In this section, we prove the existence of a solution
u\in X(0,T)
to the model problem (
1.1
). We denote

\mathscr{V}^{1}\coloneqq L^{2}\big([0,T];H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\big)\text{ and }\mathscr{V}^{-1}\coloneqq L^{2}\big([0,T];H^{-1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\big).

(A.1)

\mathscr{V}^{1}
and
\mathscr{V}^{-1}
are equipped with norms

\|u\|_{\mathscr{V}^{1}}^{2}\coloneqq\int_{0}^{T}\|u\|_{H^{1}}^{2}\text{d}t\text{ and }\|u\|_{\mathscr{V}^{-1}}^{2}\coloneqq\int_{0}^{T}\|u\|_{H^{-1}}^{2}\text{d}t.

(A.2)

Fix
w\in X(0,T)
and consider the following problem of finding
u\in X(0,T)
such that

\displaystyle\big(\partial_{t}u,v\big)+a\big(u,v;t\big)

\displaystyle=\big(f(w;t),v\big),~(\boldsymbol{x},t)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times[0,T],

(A.3)

\displaystyle\big(u(x,0),\tilde{v}(x)\big)

\displaystyle=\big(u_{0}(x),\tilde{v}(x)\big),\,\,\forall(v,\tilde{v})\in L^{2}\big([0,L],H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\big)\times H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d}).

Since
w\in X(0,T)\subseteq C([0,T];L^{2}(\mathbb{R}))
, using
[
28
, Theorem 1, page 473]
, we have

f(w;t)\in C\big([0,T];L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\big).

(A.4)

Using
[
28
, Theorem 2, page 513]
, we can prove that
\forall w\in X(0,T)
there exists a
G(w)\in X(0,T)
which solves the following parabolic equation

\displaystyle\big(\partial_{t}G(w),v\big)+a\big(G(w),v;t\big)

\displaystyle=\big(f(w;t),v\big),

(A.5)

\displaystyle\big(G(w)(\cdot,0),\tilde{v}\big)

\displaystyle=\big(u_{0}(\cdot),\tilde{v}\big),\,\,\forall(v,\tilde{v})\in L^{2}\big([0,L],H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\big)\times H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d}).

The existence of a weak solution
u(\boldsymbol{x},t)
to the model problem Eq. (
1.1
) can be established by proving the existence of a fixed point to the mapping
G
such that
G(w)=w\in X(0,T)
. We shall prove the following lemma.

Lemma A.1
.

For the mapping
G
defined in Eq. (
A.5
), there exists
u(\boldsymbol{x},t)\in\mathscr{V}^{1}
such that
u=G(u)
.

Proof.

The existence of the mapping
G
is guaranteed by
[
28
, Theorem 2, page 513]
. We define a sequence
\{u^{n}\}_{n=0}^{\infty}
in the Banach space
X(0,t)
by
u^{n+1}=G(u^{n})
such that

\displaystyle\big(\partial_{t}u^{n+1},v\big)+a\big(u^{n+1},v;t\big)

\displaystyle=\big(f(u^{n};t),v\big),~\forall v\in L^{2}\big([0,T],H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\big),\,\,u^{n+1}(\boldsymbol{x},0)

\displaystyle=u_{0}(\boldsymbol{x}).

(A.6)

We first prove the existence of the fixed point
u\in\mathscr{V}^{1}
such that
G(u)=u
. Next, we prove that for this fixed point
u
we have
\partial_{t}u\in H^{-1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and then show the convergence of
\{\partial_{t}u^{n}\}_{n=0}^{\infty}
in
\mathscr{V}^{-1}
. By shifting the index
n
in Eq (
A.6
), we have

\displaystyle\big(\partial_{t}u^{n},v\big)+a\big(u^{n},v;t\big)

\displaystyle=\big(f(u^{n-1};t),v\big),~\forall v\in L^{2}\big([0,T],H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\big),

(A.7)

\displaystyle u^{n}(x,0)

\displaystyle=u_{0}(x).

By subtracting Eq. (
A.7
) from Eq. (
A.6
), for
x^{n+1}\coloneqq u^{n+1}-u^{n}
, we have

\big(\partial_{t}x^{n+1},v\big)+a\big(x^{n+1},v;t\big)=\big(f(u^{n};t)-f(u^{n-1};t),v\big),\forall v\in L^{2}\big([0,T],H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\big).

(A.8)

We can take
v=x^{n+1}
in Eq. (
A.8
) to obtain

(\partial_{t}x^{n+1},x^{n+1})+a(x^{n+1},x^{n+1};t)=\big(f(u^{n};t)-f(u^{n-1};t),x^{n+1}\big).

(A.9)

Additionally, by the coercity condition of the operators
a(u,v;t)
and the Lipschitz continuity of
f(u;t)
, we have

\displaystyle c_{0}\|x^{n+1}\|_{H^{1}}^{2}\leq a(x^{n+1},x^{n+1};t),\,\,\big(f(u^{n};t)-f(u^{n-1};t),x^{n+1}\big)\leq L\|x^{n}\|_{L^{2}}\|x^{n+1}\|_{L^{2}}.

(A.10)

Therefore, we have

\displaystyle(\partial_{t}x^{n+1},x^{n+1})+c_{0}\|x^{n+1}\|^{2}_{H^{1}}

\displaystyle\leq(\partial_{t}x^{n+1},x^{n+1})+a(x^{n+1},x^{n+1};t)=\big(f(u^{n};t)-f(u^{n-1};t),x^{n+1}\big)

(A.11)

\displaystyle\leq L\|x^{n}\|_{L^{2}}\|x^{n+1}\|_{L^{2}}\leq\frac{L^{2}}{2c_{0}}\|x^{n}\|_{H^{1}}^{2}+\frac{c_{0}}{2}\|x^{n+1}\|_{H^{1}}^{2}.

Integrating the above inequality over time, we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\|x^{n+1}(\cdot,T)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\|x^{n+1}(\cdot,0)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+c_{0}\int_{0}^{T}\|x^{n+1}\|^{2}_{H^{1}}\text{d}t

(A.12)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{L^{2}}{2c_{0}}\int_{0}^{T}\|x^{n}\|_{H^{1}}^{2}\text{d}t+\frac{c_{0}}{2}\int_{0}^{T}\|x^{n+1}\|_{H^{1}}^{2}\text{d}t.

Since
\|x^{n+1}(\cdot,0)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}=0
, we can show that

\int_{0}^{T}\|x^{n+1}(\cdot,t)\|_{H^{1}}^{2}\text{d}t\leq\frac{L^{2}}{c_{0}^{2}}\int_{0}^{T}\|x^{n}(\cdot,t)\|_{H^{1}}^{2}\text{d}t.

(A.13)

Thus, we have proved that

\|u^{n+1}-u^{n}\|_{\mathscr{V}^{1}}\leq\frac{L}{c_{0}}\|u^{n}-u^{n-1}\|_{\mathscr{V}^{1}},

(A.14)

Using the Banach contraction principle:

L<c_{0}\Longrightarrow\exists!~u\in\mathscr{V}^{1}:~u=\lim_{n\to\infty}u^{n}\text{ such that }u=G(u).

(A.15)

∎

Next, we shall prove the following result for
\partial_{t}u
:

Lemma A.2
.

For the same
u(\boldsymbol{x},t)
as defined in Eq. (
A.15
), we have

\partial_{t}u\in\mathscr{V}^{-1}.

(A.16)

Additionally, we show the strong convergence of the sequence
\partial_{t}u^{n}(\boldsymbol{x},t)
to
\partial_{t}u(\boldsymbol{x},t)
w.r.t. the
H^{-1}
norm:

\lim_{n\to\infty}\|\partial_{t}u^{n}-\partial_{t}u\|_{H^{-1}}=0.

(A.17)

Proof.

Since
u
is the fixed point of the mapping
G
, we have

\big(\partial_{t}u,v\big)=\big(f(u;t),v\big)-a\big(u,v,t\big),~\forall v\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d}),

(A.18)

Using the Cauchy inequality, we have

\displaystyle\big(\partial_{t}u,v\big)=\big(f(u;t),v\big)-a\big(u,v;t\big)\leq\big(\|f(u;t)\|_{L^{2}}+C_{0}\|u\|_{H^{1}}\big)\|v\|_{H^{1}}.

(A.19)

Therefore,
\partial_{t}u
defines a continuous linear functional for
v\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.
By the definition of the dual space
\mathscr{V}^{-1}=L^{2}\big([0,T];H^{-1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\big)
, we conclude that
\partial_{t}u\in\mathscr{V}^{-1}
.

Next, to show the convergence of the
\{\partial_{t}u^{n}\}_{n=0}^{\infty}
in the Banach space
\mathscr{V}^{-1}
, we prove that
\{\partial_{t}u^{n}\}_{n=0}^{\infty}
is a Cauchy sequence
\mathscr{V}^{-1}
. Denote
x^{n+1}\coloneqq u^{n+1}-u^{n}
. By Eq. (
A.6
), we have

\displaystyle\|\partial_{t}x^{n+1}\|_{H^{-1}}

\displaystyle=\sup_{v\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R})}\frac{\big(\partial_{t}x^{n+1},v\big)}{\|v\|_{H^{1}}}

(A.20)

\displaystyle=\sup_{v\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R})}\frac{\big(f(u^{n})-f(u^{n-1}),v\big)-a\big(x^{n+1},v;t\big)}{\|v\|_{H^{1}}}\leq C_{0}\|x^{n+1}\|_{H^{1}}+L\|x^{n}\|_{H^{1}}.

Integrating the above inequality over time, we can show that

\int_{0}^{T}\|\partial_{t}x^{n+1}\|_{H^{-1}}^{2}\text{d}t\leq 2C_{0}^{2}\int_{0}^{T}\|x^{n+1}\|_{H^{1}}^{2}\text{d}t+2L^{2}\int_{0}^{T}\|x^{n}\|_{H^{1}}^{2}\text{d}t.

(A.21)

In other words,

\|\partial_{t}x^{n+1}\|_{\mathscr{V}^{-1}}^{2}\leq 2C_{0}^{2}\|x^{n+1}\|_{\mathscr{V}^{1}}^{2}+2L^{2}\|x^{n}\|_{\mathscr{V}^{1}}^{2}.

(A.22)

Since we have proved that
\|x^{n+1}\|_{\mathscr{V}^{1}}\leq\|x^{n}\|_{\mathscr{V}^{1}}
in Eq. (
A.14
), we find

\|\partial_{t}x^{n+1}\|_{\mathscr{V}^{-1}}^{2}\leq(2L^{2}+2C_{0}^{2})\|x^{n}\|_{\mathscr{V}^{1}}^{2}.

(A.23)

Furthermore, by leveraging the property of the Cauchy sequence
\{x^{n}\}_{n=0}^{\infty}
in
\mathscr{V}^{1}
, we have

\|\partial_{t}u^{m}-\partial_{t}u^{n}\|_{\mathscr{V}^{-1}}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{m-n}\|\partial_{t}x^{n+i}\|_{\mathscr{V}^{-1}}~\forall n<m.

(A.24)

Therefore, by Eq. (
A.23
), we have

\|\partial_{t}u^{m}-\partial_{t}u^{n}\|_{\mathscr{V}^{-1}}\leq(2C_{0}^{2}+2L^{2})^{1/2}\sum_{i=0}^{m-n-1}\|x^{n+i}\|_{\mathscr{V}^{1}}.

(A.25)

Using the inequality (
A.13
), we have

\displaystyle\|\partial_{t}u^{m}-\partial_{t}u^{n}\|_{\mathscr{V}^{-1}}

\displaystyle\leq(2C_{0}^{2}+2L^{2})^{1/2}\sum_{i=0}^{m-n-1}q^{i}\|x^{n}\|_{\mathscr{V}^{1}}\leq(2C_{0}^{2}+2L^{2})^{1/2}\frac{1}{1-q}\|x^{n}\|_{\mathscr{V}^{1}}

(A.26)

\displaystyle\leq(2C_{0}^{2}+2L^{2})^{1/2}\frac{1}{1-q}\|u^{n+1}-u^{n}\|_{\mathscr{V}^{1}},

where
q=L/c_{0}
. Because
\|u^{n+1}-u^{n}\|_{\mathscr{V}^{1}}\to 0
as
n\to\infty
, we find

\forall\varepsilon\in\mathbb{R},~\exists N\text{ such that }\forall m>n>N\Longrightarrow\|\partial_{t}u^{m}-\partial_{t}u^{n}\|_{\mathscr{V}^{-1}}\leq\varepsilon,

(A.27)

which implies that
\{\partial_{t}u^{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
converges in
\mathscr{V}^{-1}
.
∎

Finally, by combining Lemma
A.1
and Lemma
A.2
,
\{u^{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
converge to
u(\boldsymbol{x},t)
in the space
X(0,T)
, and

u=G(u)\in X(0,T).

(A.28)

Therefore, the solution to the equation (
1.1
) exists and is unique.

Appendix B
Proof of Proposition
1

In this section, we prove that the bilinear form defined in (
2.2
):

a\big(u,v;t\big)\coloneqq\big(G\ast\nabla u,\nabla v\big)+\varepsilon\big((u,v)+(\nabla u,\nabla v)\big),

(B.1)

satisfies the continuous condition and the coercive condition such that

\displaystyle a\big(u,v;t\big)\leq C_{0}\|u\|_{H^{1}}\|v\|_{H^{1}},\,\,c_{0}\|u\|_{H^{1}}^{2}\leq a\big(u,u;t\big).

(B.2)

To prove the continuity of
a(u,v;t)
, we use the following lemma
[
45
, Lemma 1.2.30, page 28]
.

Lemma B.1

(Young’s inequality)
.

For
u\in L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and
G\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, we have

\|G\ast u\|_{L^{p}}\leq\|G\|_{L^{1}}\|u\|_{L^{p}}.

(B.3)

By the Cauchy-Schwartz inequality, we have

\displaystyle a\big(u,v;t\big)

\displaystyle=\big(G\ast\nabla u,\nabla v\big)+\varepsilon\big(u,v\big)+\varepsilon\big(\nabla u,\nabla v\big)

(B.4)

\displaystyle\leq\|G\ast\nabla u\|_{L^{2}}\|\nabla v\|_{L^{2}}+\varepsilon\|u\|_{L^{2}}\|v\|_{L^{2}}+\varepsilon\|\nabla u\|_{L^{2}}\|\nabla v\|_{L^{2}}

By the Young’s inequality (Lemma
B.1
),

\|G\ast\nabla u\|_{L^{2}}\leq\|G\|_{L^{1}}\|\nabla u\|_{L^{2}}

(B.5)

Therefore, combining Eqs. (
B.4
) and (
B.5
), we have

\displaystyle a\big(u,v;t\big)\leq\big(\varepsilon+\|G\|_{L^{1}}\big)\|\nabla u\|_{L^{2}}\|\nabla v\|_{L^{2}}+\varepsilon\|u\|_{L^{2}}\|v\|_{L^{2}}\leq\big(2\varepsilon+\|G\|_{L^{1}}\big)\|u\|_{H^{1}}\|v\|_{H^{1}}

(B.6)

The coercive condition on
a\big(u,v;t\big)
can be proved by using the Fourier transform,

a\big(u,u;t\big)=\left(\mathscr{F}(G)\mathscr{F}(\nabla u),\overline{\mathscr{F}(\nabla u)}\right)+\varepsilon\|u\|_{H^{1}}^{2}.

(B.7)

Since we have already assume that
\mathscr{F}(G)\geq 0
, we have

\left(\mathscr{F}(G)\mathscr{F}(\nabla u),\overline{\mathscr{F}(\nabla u)}\right)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mathscr{F}(G)|\mathscr{F}(\nabla u)|^{2}dx\geq 0.

(B.8)

Therefore, by Eq. (
B.7
), we have

a\big(u,u;t\big)\geq\left(\mathscr{F}(G)\mathscr{F}(\nabla u),\overline{\mathscr{F}(\nabla u)}\right)+\varepsilon\|u\|_{H^{1}}^{2}\geq\varepsilon\|u\|_{H^{1}}^{2}.

(B.9)

Appendix C
Proof of Lemma
2.2

In this section, we first prove the inverse inequalities (
2.7
) and (
2.8
) for 1D mapped Jacobi spectral expansions. Next, we extend the inverse inequalities to
d
-dimensional sparse mapped Jacobi spectral expansions defined in a hyperbolic cross space.

C.1
Inverse inequalities for 1-dimensional mapped Jacobi spectral expansions

1-D mapped Jacobi spectral expansion
U_{N}^{\beta,x_{0}}
can be written as

\displaystyle U_{N}^{\beta,x_{0}}(x)

\displaystyle=p_{N}(\xi)\mu_{\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}}(\xi),

(C.1)

Herein,
p_{N}(\xi)
is a polynomial spanned by the Jacobi polynomials
\{j_{n}^{\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}}(\xi)\}_{n=0}^{N}
,

p_{N}(\xi)\coloneqq\sum_{n=0}^{N}u_{n}^{\beta,x_{0}}j_{n}^{\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}}(\xi)\text{ where }\xi\coloneqq h_{\beta,r}(x-x_{0}),

(C.2)

and
\mu_{\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}}(\xi)\coloneqq\sqrt{\omega^{\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}}(\xi)\frac{\text{d}\xi}{\text{d}x}}
is the modified weight function.

First, we prove Eq. (
2.7
) for mapped Jacobi spectral expansions,
i.e.
,

\big\|\partial_{x}U_{N}^{\beta,x_{0}}\big\|_{L^{2}}\leq\beta^{3/2}N_{\alpha,r}^{1/2}\big\|U_{N}^{\beta,x_{0}}\big\|_{L^{2}}

(C.3)

We define an auxiliary function (introduced in
[
18
]
)

g(\xi)\coloneqq\beta(1-\xi^{2})\frac{\text{d}x}{\text{d}\xi}=\frac{1}{(1-\xi^{2})^{r/2}}.

(C.4)

It can be verified that
1\leq g(\xi)
. Thus, the following inequality holds true for
U_{N}^{\beta,x_{0}}
:

\big\|\partial_{x}U_{N}^{\beta,x_{0}}\big\|^{2}_{L^{2}}\leq\big\|g^{1/2}\partial_{x}U_{N}^{\beta,x_{0}}\big\|^{2}_{L^{2}}=\int_{\mathbb{R}}g(\xi)\big(\partial_{x}(p_{N}\mu_{\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}})\big)^{2}\d{x}.

(C.5)

A direct computation shows that

\displaystyle\partial_{x}\big(p_{N}(\xi)\mu_{\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}}(\xi)\big)

\displaystyle=\partial_{x}p_{N}(\xi)\mu_{\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}}(\xi)+p_{N}(\xi)\partial_{x}\mu_{\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}}(\xi)

(C.6)

\displaystyle=p_{N}^{\prime}\mu_{\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}}\frac{\text{d}\xi}{\text{d}x}+p_{N}\mu_{\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}}^{\prime}\frac{\text{d}\xi}{\text{d}x}.

Therefore, from the Cauchy inequality, we have

\big\|\partial_{x}U_{N}^{\beta,x_{0}}(x)\big\|^{2}_{L^{2}}\leq 2\int_{-1}^{1}g(\xi)\Big(p_{N}^{\prime}\mu_{\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}}\frac{\text{d}\xi}{\text{d}x}\Big)^{2}\text{d}\xi+2\int_{-1}^{1}g(\xi)\Big(p_{N}\mu_{\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}}^{\prime}\frac{\text{d}\xi}{\text{d}x}\Big)^{2}\text{d}\xi

(C.7)

To prove the inverse inequality Eq. (
2.7
), it is sufficient to derive a bound for the RHS of Eq. (
C.7
). For the first term on RHS of Eq. (
C.7
), we have

\displaystyle g(\xi)\Big(p_{N}^{\prime}\mu_{\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}}\frac{\text{d}\xi}{\text{d}x}\Big)^{2}

\displaystyle=\beta(1-\xi^{2})\frac{\text{d}x}{\text{d}\xi}(p_{N}^{\prime})^{2}\mu_{\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}}^{2}\Big(\frac{\text{d}\xi}{\text{d}x}\Big)^{2}

(C.8)

\displaystyle=\beta(1-\xi^{2})\frac{\text{d}x}{\text{d}\xi}(p_{N}^{\prime})^{2}w^{\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}}\Big(\frac{\text{d}\xi}{\text{d}x}\Big)^{3}=\beta(p_{N}^{\prime})^{2}w^{\alpha_{1}+1,\alpha_{2}+1}\Big(\frac{\text{d}\xi}{\text{d}x}\Big)^{2}.

Integrating Eq. (
C.8
) over
\xi\in[1,1]
, we have

\displaystyle\int_{-1}^{1}g(\xi)\Big(p_{N}^{\prime}\mu_{\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}}\frac{\text{d}\xi}{\text{d}x}\Big)^{2}\text{d}\xi

\displaystyle=\int_{-1}^{1}\beta(p_{N}^{\prime})^{2}w^{\alpha_{1}+1,\alpha_{2}+1}\Big(\frac{\text{d}\xi}{\text{d}x}\Big)^{2}\text{d}\xi

(C.9)

\displaystyle=\beta^{3}\int_{-1}^{1}(p_{N}^{\prime})^{2}w^{\alpha_{1}+1,\alpha_{2}+1}(1-\xi^{2})^{2+r}\text{d}\xi\leq\beta^{3}\int_{-1}^{1}(p_{N}^{\prime})^{2}w^{\alpha_{1}+1,\alpha_{2}+1}\text{d}\xi

Using the inverse inequalities of Jacobi polynomials
[
13
, Theorem 3.31]
, we have

\displaystyle\int_{-1}^{1}g(\xi)\Big(p_{N}^{\prime}\mu_{\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}}\frac{\text{d}\xi}{\text{d}x}\Big)^{2}\text{d}\xi\leq\int_{-1}^{1}(p_{N}^{\prime})^{2}w^{\alpha_{1}+1,\alpha_{2}+1}\text{d}\xi

(C.10)

\displaystyle\leq\beta^{3}\lambda_{N}^{\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}}\int_{-1}^{1}p_{N}^{2}w^{\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}}\text{d}\xi=\beta^{3}\lambda_{N}^{\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}(U_{N}^{\beta,x_{0}})^{2}\text{d}x,

where
\lambda_{N}^{\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}}\coloneqq N(N+\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2}+1)
.

Furthermore, for the second term on the RHS of Eq. (
C.7
), we have

\displaystyle g(\xi)\Big(p_{N}\mu_{\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}}^{\prime}\frac{\text{d}\xi}{\text{d}x}\Big)^{2}

\displaystyle=g(\xi)\Big(p_{N}\frac{\mu_{\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}}^{\prime}}{\mu_{\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}}}\mu_{\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}}\frac{\text{d}\xi}{\text{d}x}\Big)^{2}

(C.11)

\displaystyle=g(\xi)\Big(\frac{\mu_{\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}}^{\prime}}{\mu_{\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}}}\Big(\frac{\text{d}\xi}{dx}\Big)^{3/2}\Big)^{2}\Big(p_{N}\mu_{\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}}\Big(\frac{\text{d}x}{\text{d}\xi}\Big)^{1/2}\Big)^{2}

Additionally, a direct computation shows that

\displaystyle g(\xi)^{1/2}\frac{\mu_{\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}}^{\prime}}{\mu_{\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}}}

\displaystyle\Big(\frac{\text{d}\xi}{\text{d}x}\Big)^{3/2}=\Big(\beta(1-\xi^{2})\frac{\text{d}x}{\text{d}\xi}\Big)^{1/2}\Big(\frac{\mu_{\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}}^{\prime}}{\mu_{\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}}}\Big)\Big(\frac{\text{d}\xi}{\text{d}x}\Big)^{3/2}

(C.12)

\displaystyle=\beta^{1/2}(1-\xi^{2})^{1/2}\Big(\frac{\mu_{\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}}^{\prime}}{\mu_{\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}}}\Big)\frac{\text{d}\xi}{\text{d}x}

\displaystyle=\beta^{3/2}(1-\xi^{2})^{(3+r)/2}\frac{(w^{\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}})^{\prime}\frac{\text{d}\xi}{\text{d}x}-\beta w^{\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}}(2+r)\xi(1-\xi^{2})^{r/2}}{2w^{\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}}\frac{\text{d}\xi}{\text{d}x}}

\displaystyle=\beta^{3/2}(1-\xi^{2})^{(3+r)/2}\Big(\frac{(w^{\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}})^{\prime}}{2w^{\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}}}-\frac{\beta(2+r)\xi(1-\xi^{2})^{r/2}}{2\frac{\text{d}\xi}{\text{d}x}}\Big)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\beta^{3/2}(1-\xi^{2})^{(1+r)/2}\big(-\alpha_{1}(1+\xi)+\alpha_{2}(1-\xi)+\xi(2+r)\big)

\displaystyle\leq\beta^{3/2}(1+\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2}+r/2).

Therefore, we have

\displaystyle g(\xi)\Big(p_{N}(\xi)\mu_{\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}}^{\prime}\frac{\text{d}\xi}{\text{d}x}\Big)^{2}

\displaystyle=g(\xi)\Big(\frac{\mu_{\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}}^{\prime}}{\mu_{\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}}}\Big(\frac{\text{d}\xi}{dx}\Big)^{3/2}\Big)^{2}\Big(p_{N}\mu_{\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}}\Big(\frac{\text{d}x}{\text{d}\xi}\Big)^{1/2}\Big)^{2}

(C.13)

\displaystyle\leq\beta^{3}(1+\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2}+r/2)^{2}\Big(p_{N}\mu_{\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}}\Big(\frac{\text{d}x}{\text{d}\xi}\Big)^{1/2}\Big)^{2}.

By integrating Eq. (
C.13
) over
\xi\in[-1,1]
, we have

\int_{-1}^{1}g(\xi)\Big(p_{N}(\xi)\mu_{\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}}^{\prime}\frac{\text{d}\xi}{\text{d}x}\Big)^{2}\text{d}\xi\leq\beta^{3}(1+\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2}+r/2)^{2}\|U_{N}^{\beta,x_{0}}(x)\|^{2}_{L^{2}}

(C.14)

Finally, plugging in the estimation of the two terms on the RHS of Eq. (
C.7
) using Eqs. (
C.10
) and (
C.14
), we have

\|\partial_{x}U_{N}^{\beta,x_{0}}(x)\|^{2}_{L^{2}}\leq\beta^{3}N_{\alpha,r}\|U_{N}^{\beta,x_{0}}(x)\|^{2}_{L^{2}},

(C.15)

where
N_{\alpha,r}=2N(N+\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2}+1)+2(1+\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2}+r/2)^{2}
.

Next, we prove Eq. (
2.8
) for mapped Jacobi spectral expansions,
i.e.
,

\big\|x\partial_{x}U_{N}^{\beta,x_{0}}(x)\big\|_{L^{2}}\leq\beta^{1/2}N_{\alpha,r}^{1/2}\big\|U_{N}^{\beta,x_{0}}(x)\big\|_{L^{2}},

(C.16)

we additionally assume that
r\leq 1
. When
r=1
, we have

\xi=h_{\beta,1}(x)=\frac{\beta x}{\sqrt{1+\beta^{2}x^{2}}}.

(C.17)

Also, we have

.
When

. Therefore, we can show that

(C.18)

Similar to Eq. (
C.7

\|x\partial_{x}U_{N}^{\beta,x_{0}}\|^{2}_{L^{2}}\leq 2\int_{-1}^{1}x^{2}g(\xi)\Big(p_{N}^{\prime}\mu_{\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}}\frac{\text{d}\xi}{\text{d}x}\Big)^{2}+x^{2}g(\xi)\Big(p_{N}\mu_{\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}}^{\prime}\frac{\text{d}\xi}{\text{d}x}\Big)^{2}\text{d}\xi.

(C.19)

Furthermore, the upper bound for the first term on the RHS of Eq. (
C.19

C.8

\displaystyle\int_{-1}^{1}x^{2}g(\xi)\Big(p_{N}^{\prime}\mu_{\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}}

\displaystyle\frac{\text{d}\xi}{\text{d}x}\Big)^{2}\text{d}\xi=\beta\int_{-1}^{1}x^{2}(p_{N}^{\prime})^{2}w^{\alpha_{1}+1,\alpha_{2}+1}\left(\frac{\text{d}\xi}{\text{d}x}\right)^{2}\text{d}\xi

(C.20)

\displaystyle=\beta^{3}\int_{-1}^{1}x^{2}(p_{N}^{\prime})^{2}w^{\alpha_{1}+1,\alpha_{2}+1}(1-\xi^{2})^{2+r}\text{d}\xi

\displaystyle\leq\beta\int_{-1}^{1}(p_{N}^{\prime})^{2}w^{\alpha_{1}+1,\alpha_{2}+1}(1-\xi^{2})^{1+r}\text{d}\xi

\displaystyle\leq\beta\int_{-1}^{1}(p_{N}(\xi)^{\prime})^{2}w^{\alpha_{1}+1,\alpha_{2}+1}\text{d}\xi

\displaystyle\leq\beta\lambda_{N}^{\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}(U_{N,x_{0}}^{\beta})^{2}\text{d}x.

Additionally, the upper bound for the second term on the RHS of Eq. (

\displaystyle x^{2}g(x)\Big(\frac{\mu_{\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}}^{\prime}}{\mu_{\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}}}\Big)^{2}\Big(\frac{\text{d}\xi}{\text{d}x}\Big)^{3}

\displaystyle=\frac{x^{2}\beta^{3}}{4}(1-\xi^{2})^{1+r}\big(\xi(2+r)-(1+\xi)\alpha_{1}+(1-\xi)\alpha_{2}\big)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\beta(1+\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2}+r/2)^{2}.

Therefore, we have

\displaystyle\int_{-1}^{1}x^{2}g(\xi)\Big(p_{N}\mu_{\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}}^{\prime}\frac{\text{d}\xi}{\text{d}x}\Big)^{2}\text{d}\xi

\displaystyle\leq\beta(1+\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2}+r/2)^{2}\int_{-1}^{1}\Big(p_{N}\mu_{\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}}\Big(\frac{\text{d}x}{\text{d}\xi}\Big)^{1/2}\Big)^{2}\text{d}\xi

\|x\partial_{x}U_{N}^{\beta,x_{0}}(x)\|^{2}_{L^{2}}\leq\beta N_{\alpha,r}\|U_{N}^{\beta,x_{0}}(x)\|^{2}_{L^{2}}.

(C.23)
