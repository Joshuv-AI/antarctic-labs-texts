Learning with Exposure Constraints in Recommendation Systems
Omer Ben-Porat, Rotem Torkan

Abstract
Recommendation systems are dynamic economic systems that balance the needs of multiple stakeholders. A recent line of work studies incentives from the content providers’ point of view. Content providers, e.g., vloggers and bloggers, contribute fresh content and rely on user engagement to create revenue and finance their operations. In this work, we propose a contextual multi-armed bandit setting to model the dependency of content providers on exposure. In our model, the system receives a user context in every round and has to select one of the arms. Every arm is a content provider who must receive a minimum number of pulls every fixed time period (e.g., a month) to remain viable in later rounds; otherwise, the arm departs and is no longer available. The system aims to maximize the users’ (content consumers) welfare. To that end, it should learn which arms are vital and ensure they remain viable by subsidizing arm pulls if needed. We develop algorithms with sub-linear regret, as well as a lower bound that demonstrates that our algorithms are optimal up to logarithmic factors.

Introduction

Recommendation systems (RSs) are the principal ingredient of many online services and platforms like Youtube, Quora, Substack, and Medium. Algorithmically, those platforms treat the task of recommendation as a matching problem. RSs match a user’s context, i.e., their past interactions, demographics, etc., to an item from a predetermined list of items, e.g., news articles, which will hopefully satisfy that user. The quality of a user-content match is initially unclear, so many data-driven approaches have been proposed to determine a matching’s quality; for instance, collaborate filtering

A recent body of research addresses tradeoffs among stakeholders
[

Online platforms have three main stakeholders: The commercial company that runs the platform, content consumers, and content providers. Content consumers, which we refer to as users for simplicity, enjoy the RSs’ content. Content providers, e.g., bloggers and vloggers, create value for RSs by providing relevant and attractive content constantly. Content providers benefit from RSs too, both explicitly by, e.g., obtaining revenue flow from advertisements when users watch their content and interact with it; and implicitly when they gain popularity on the RS that can later be directed to other channels (such as affiliate programs, marketing campaigns, etc.). Observing that an RS’s recommendation policy affects content providers, several works address content providers’ incentives and develop RSs that incorporate their strategic behavior
[
47
,
32
]
. For instance,
Ben-Porat et al. 2020
consider content provider deviations to produce more revenue-effective items to increase their revenue. Furthermore,
Mladenov et al. 2020a
model the dependency of content providers on user engagement to create revenue and finance their operation. However, these works and others operate under complete information assumptions, entirely neglecting the user-content uncertainty.

In this work, we propose a model that accounts for both content providers’ strategic behavior
and
user-content uncertainty. We consider a contextual multi-armed bandit problem, where the arms are content providers, and in each round, a new user context arrives at the system. The rounds are divided into phases, for instance, a week, month, quarter, or year. The reward of every arm in each phase is the number of pulls it gets, where arm pulls model exposure or user engagement. Arms require a minimum reward in a phase to produce new content for the following phase; otherwise, the arm ceases to create new content and departs. The minimum number of pulls represents the cost of revenue, i.e., the cost of producing and delivering the content to consumers. We assume that contents become obsolete from phase to phase, so, in every round, the system can only recommend the content of arms that are still viable. The system is fully aware of the exposure thresholds but has no information about each arm’s desirability: Some of the arms are of low quality, and their departure does not affect the user welfare.

We model uncertainty in the stochastic sense; namely, we assume that the context arrival distribution and user-arm match qualities are initially unknown but can be learned through experimentation. Since failing to satisfy an arm’s exposure constraint in a phase leads to its irrevocable departure, the system should not only balanced exploration and exploitation but also protect the reward of arms it perceives as vital for long-term user welfare. We use the standard notion of regret to quantify the system’s performance, where the baseline is an algorithm that is fully aware of the context arrival distribution and the user-arm match qualities.

1.1
Our Contribution

Our contribution is three-fold. First, we simultaneously address user-content uncertainty and content providers’ strategic behavior, an area that has been under-explored in current literature to the best of our knowledge. Coupling these two elements brings us closer to real-world RSs’ challenges. The second contribution is technical: We devise low-regret algorithms for our model. We begin by assuming that both context arrival distribution and user-arm reward distribution are known. As it turns out, even this task, which we term the stochastic optimization task and study in Section
3
, is non-trivial. We propose two algorithms that differ in their runtime and performance guarantees. Those algorithms are later leveraged to craft a
\tilde{O}(T^{\nicefrac{{2}}{{3}}})
-regret for several parameter regimes when arrival and reward distributions are unknown. We also provide a lower bound that demonstrates our algorithms are optimal up to logarithmic factors. Our third contribution is a deeper understanding of the notion of
arm subsidy
. Arm subsidy is when we deliberately pull a sub-optimal arm to ensure it remains viable, thereby trading short-term and long-term welfare. Although our model is stylized, this view allows us to analyze the different motivations for arm subsidy (see Subsection
2.2
) and conclude it is vital for long-term social welfare, strengthening the
ideas argued by previous work
[

Model

We adopt the blogging motivation as our ongoing example. There is a set of arms (content providers)
K=[k]
, and a sequence of user contexts of length
T
. We assume that contexts compactly represent users by their
type

U
the set of user contexts,
U=[n]
, and use the terms context and user type interchangeably.

In each round
t
,
1\leq t\leq T
, a user of type
u_{t}
arrives at the system. We assume a stationary arrival distribution
\boldsymbol{P}
over
U
; namely, we assume that
u_{1},\dots,u_{T}\sim\boldsymbol{P}^{T}
. After observing the user type
u_{t}\in U
, the system pulls an arm, denoted by
a_{t}\in K
. The arm pull models displaying content
a_{t}
to the user of that round. Afterward, the system gets feedback, for instance, a like or a rating, modeling the user’s
utility
from that content, which we denote by
v_{t}
. We further assume that for every user type
u\in U
and every arm
a\in K
, the associated utility is a random variable drawn from a distribution
D_{u,a}
. We let
\boldsymbol{\mu}
denote the matrix of expected utilities, where
\mu_{u,a}
is the expected utility of a user type
u\in U
from an arm
a\in K
. In other words,
\mu_{u,a}=\mathbb{E}_{v\sim D_{u,a}}[v]
. For simplicity, we assume that the distributions
D_{u,a}
for every
a\in K
and
u\in U
are supported in the
[0,1]
interval; hence,
v_{t}\in[0,1]
for every
1\leq t\leq T
almost surely.

An online decision making algorithm is a function from the history
\left(u_{t^{\prime}},a_{t^{\prime}},v_{t^{\prime}}\right)_{t^{\prime}=1}^{t-1}
and a user type to an arm. That is, the algorithm selects the arm to pull given the history and the context
u_{t}
in round
t
. The
reward
of an algorithm
A
is the expected sum of user utilities. Formally,

\mathbb{E}[r^{A}]=\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}v_{t}^{A}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mu_{u_{t},a_{t}^{A}}\right].

(1)

When the algorithm
A
is clear from the context, we remove the superscript
A
.

Thus far, we have defined a standard contextual
MAB
setting with stochastic utilities, a discrete set of contexts (or types), and stochastic context arrival. In the next subsection, we present a novel component that captures the strategic behavior of content providers.

2.1
Exposure Constraints

Our model departs from previous works by considering the following
exposure constraint
:
Each arm requires a minimal amount of exposure, i.e., the number of times the system pulls it in a given time frame, to keep creating fresh content.
Arms are content providers who depend on interactions with users for income (or on exposure as a proxy); thus, an arm
a\in K
that receives less exposure than
\delta_{a}
might face difficulties in paying their periodic bills. This constraint represents the providers’ need for exposure and the tendency to churn the system in lack thereof.

Specifically, every arm requires a predetermined number of exposures in every
phase
, where phase lengths are fixed and comprised of
\tau
rounds. Namely, at the end of every phase, each arm
a\in K
examines the number of exposures it received, and if it is less than some given and publicly known threshold
\delta_{a}\in\{0,1,\dots\tau\}
, then arm
a
departs and cannot be pulled anymore.

1

1

If the exposure thresholds are private, linear regret is inevitable; see Subsection
5.2
.
To exemplify,
\tau
could be the number of days in a month, where each day is a round. An arm
a\in K
could require at least
\delta_{a}
impressions every month to keep creating fresh content in the following months, otherwise, it churns the system. Observe that we have
\nicefrac{{T}}{{\tau}}
phases.

We further denote by
K_{t}\subseteq K
the subset of arms that are still available in round
t
.
2
2

2

Alternatively, one could say that all arms are always available, but the utility from arms
K\setminus K_{t}
is 0 almost surely at round
t
. Indeed, in such a case, pulling an arm from

is weakly dominated by any other arm selection.
Mathematically, the exposure constraint is modeled as follows. An arm

is viable in round

if in every phase prior to the current phase
\lfloor\nicefrac{{t}}{{\tau}}\rfloor+1
,

received at least
\delta_{a}
pulls. Namely, if

\forall j,1\leq j\leq\lfloor\nicefrac{{t}}{{\tau}}\rfloor:\sum_{t=(j-1)\cdot\tau+1}^{j\cdot\tau}\mathds{1}_{[a=a_{t}]}\geq\delta_{a},

where
\mathds{1}_{[a=a_{t}]}
indicates the event that
a=a_{t}
. Rewriting the expected reward defined in Equation (

\mathbb{E}[r^{A}]=\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}v^{A}_{t}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mu_{u_{t},a^{A}_{t}}\cdot\mathds{1}_{[a^{A}_{t}\in K_{t}^{A}]}\right].

2.2
Arm Subsidy

A surprising phenomenon that our model exhibits is
arm subsidy
: Deliberately pulling a sub-optimal arm to maintain its exposure constraint. Indeed, such short-term harm is sometimes required to achieve long-term social welfare (i.e., the expected reward in Equation (
2
)). To illustrate this concept and the reasons it is vital, we present the following examples.

Example 1
.

Consider the instance with
k=2
arms,
n=2
user types, arrival distribution
\boldsymbol{P}=(0.5,0.5)
, phase length
\tau=100
, exposure thresholds
\boldsymbol{\delta}=(40,40)
, and expected utility matrix
\boldsymbol{\mu}=\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}1&0\\
0&1\end{smallmatrix}\bigr).

The exposure thresholds suggest that each arm requires
40
pulls in each phase of length
100
rounds. To showcase why arm subsidy is needed, let us assume that the algorithm knows the utility matrix
\boldsymbol{\mu}
. Consider a myopic algorithm that pulls the best arm for every user type and overlooks the exposure constraints. The myopic algorithm pulls arm
i
for user type
i
, for
i\in\{1,2\}
, since it yields the best utility, resulting in an expected utility of 1 in every round (where the expectation is taken over the user type).
Apparently, the myopic algorithm is optimal; however, the exposure constraints complicate the setting. At some phase, fewer than
\delta_{i}=40
type
i
-users will arrive for some
i\in\{1,2\}
.
3
3

3

Formally, let
E_{j}
denote the event that less than 40 type
i
-users (for some
i\in\{1,2\}
) arrive at phase
j
. Then,
\Pr[E_{j}]\approx 0.035
; hence, the probability one arm will depart after at most 200 phases is
\Pr\left[\cup_{j=1}^{200}E_{j}\right]\approx 0.999
.
W.l.o.g., assume that
i=1
; thus, the myopic algorithm would not satisfy arm
1
’s exposure constraint. For all rounds in the remaining phases, the only available arm is arm 2, and all type
1
-users receive a zero utility; therefore, the reward is halved and user welfare has severely deteriorated.

In the instance given in Example
1
, an algorithm might decide to subsidize arm
1
by pulling it for some type
2
users, deliberately harming the short-term reward to ensure that arm
1
will not depart (and similarly for arm
2
). In such a case, the subsidy stems from the variance of the user arrival—protecting arms against rare events. However, subsidies might be required more often, as we demonstrate in Example
2
below.

Example 2
.

Consider the instance with
k=2
arms,
n=2
user types, arrival distribution
\boldsymbol{P}=(0.5,0.5)
, phase length
\tau=100
, exposure thresholds
\boldsymbol{\delta}=(10,60)

\boldsymbol{\mu}=\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}1&0\\

Notice that arm
1
(
2
) requires

(

100

\boldsymbol{\mu}
.
The myopic algorithm selects arm
i
for type
i
-users. However, in a typical phase, there will be less than
\delta_{2}=60
type
2
-users, causing arm
2
to depart.
4
4

4

Let
E_{j}
be the event that more than 60 type
2
-users arrive at phase
j
. Consequently,
\Pr[E_{j}]<0.03
, and the probability that arm
2
will depart after at most two phases is
1-\Pr\left[E_{1}\cap E_{2}\right]\approx 0.999
.

Therefore, without subsidy, arm
2
departs early and the myopic algorithm receives a reward of

.
In contrast, consider an algorithm that subsidizes arm
2

2
for roughly

of type

For the instance in Example

Consider a modification of the instance of Example

2.3
Algorithmic Tasks

First and foremost, we study the learning task, in which the goal is to find an online decision-making algorithm that minimizes regret (formally explained later in Subsection
2.5
). The algorithm knows the number of arms
k
, number of user types
n
, phase length
\tau
, number of rounds
T
, and exposure thresholds
\boldsymbol{\delta}
; however, it is unaware of the expected utilities
\boldsymbol{\mu}
and the arrival distribution
\boldsymbol{P}
.

A second, auxiliary task that is crucial for both quantifying regret and the learning algorithm we propose in Section
4
is the stochastic optimization task. In this task, the algorithm is aware of
\boldsymbol{P},\boldsymbol{\mu}
, and we want to maximize the expected reward in Equation (
2
). Each user type
u_{t}
is known in the Bayesian sense, i.e., the algorithm knows
\boldsymbol{P}
and that
u_{t}\sim\boldsymbol{P}
, but observes the realized value only in round
t
and not before. Once the algorithm observes
u_{t}
in round
t
, it can compute the utility
\mu_{u_{t},a}
for every
a\in K
since it knows
\boldsymbol{\mu}
.

We denote
OPT
as the algorithm that maximizes the expected reward in the stochastic optimization task. Naturally,
OPT
serves as a benchmark for the learning task as well.
In contrast to standard stochastic
MAB
settings, where the optimal algorithm is myopic—pulling the best arm in every round (or the best arm per the given context in that round)—
OPT
is more complex. To see this, recall that in Examples
1
and
2
, the optimal policy is not myopic as it has to decide whether and how to subsidize. To that end, we study the stochastic optimization task in Section
3
.

Reward Notation

To clarify when we refer to algorithms for the stochastic optimization task, we adopt a different notation for the reward, albeit calculated similarly (see Equation (
2
)). We use the tilde notation, i.e.,
\mathbb{E}[\tilde{r}^{A}]
, to denote the reward for a stochastic optimization algorithm
A
.

2.4
Parametric Assumptions

Similarly to previous works, we consider the number of rounds
T
to be the scaling factor. Namely, we assume the other parameters are either constants or functions of
T
. More specifically, we assume that the number of arms
k
and the number of user types
n
are constants, and that
P_{u}
is a constant that is bounded away from zero for every user type

.

Furthermore, we make two simplifying assumptions, allowing us to focus on regimes we believe are more appropriate to the problem at hand. Importantly, we relax these two assumptions in Section
5
. First, we ensure that exploration can be fast enough.

Assumption 1
.

There exists a constant
\gamma\in(0,\nicefrac{{1}}{{k}}]
, such that

\sum_{a\in K}\max(\delta_{a},\gamma\cdot\tau)\leq\tau.

(3)

For instance, if
\delta_{a}\leq\frac{\tau}{k}
for every
a\in K
, then Inequality (
3
) is satisfied with
\gamma=\frac{1}{k}
. We remark that
\gamma
is treated as a constant that does not depend on
\tau
. With this assumption, one can satisfy all arms’ exposure constraints in a phase while exploring each arm at least
\lfloor\gamma\tau\rfloor

\tau
is not too large.

Assumption 2
.

The phase length
\tau
is upper bounded by
O(T^{\nicefrac{{2}}{{3}}})
.

Assumption
2

2.5
Regret

Recall that Subsection
2.3
defines
OPT
as the optimal algorithm for the stochastic optimization task. To quantify the success of an online decision-making algorithm in the learning task, we adopt the standard notion of
regret
, where the baseline is
OPT
. Formally, the regret
R^{A}
of an algorithm
A
is the difference between its reward and the reward of
OPT
, i.e.,
\mathbb{E}[R^{A}]=\mathbb{E}[\tilde{r}^{\textnormal{OPT}}]-\mathbb{E}[r^{A}]
. Observe that
\mathbb{E}[\tilde{r}^{\textnormal{OPT}}]
is constant (given the instance parameters); thus, minimizing the regret is equivalent to maximizing the reward.

Having defined the notion of regret, we now show that planning for subsidy is essential. Optimal algorithms for the standard multi-armed bandit algorithms that are not modified for exposure constraints, e.g.,
UCB

[
42
]
, do not subsidize arms at all. The following Theorem
1
suggests that avoiding subsidy as well as blind subsidy, ensuring all arms receive their minimal exposure regardless of the rewards, results in high regret.

Theorem 1
.

Any algorithm that either

1.

maintains all arms always, i.e., has
K_{1}=K_{2}=\dots=K_{T}
with probability 1; or,

2.

avoids subsidy

suffers a regret of
\Omega(T)
in some instances.

The proof of Theorem
1
appears in Appendix
A.1
. This theorem calls for devising algorithms that incorporate careful subsidies while exploring and exploiting. In the next section, we take the first step toward that goal and study the stochastic optimization task.

3.1
Phase-Independent and Committed Algorithms

In this subsection, we highlight a narrow class of simple algorithms yet potentially approximately optimal, as we prove in Theorem
2
. We say that an algorithm is
phase-independent
if it pulls an arm in any round based on the sequence of users arriving on that phase only, ignoring previous phases. More formally,
6
6

6

In fact, phase-independent algorithms are also independent of the realized rewards. However, since we care about maximizing the expectation
\mathbb{E}[\tilde{r}]
, we can treat the expected rewards and not the realized ones.

Property 1

(Phase independence)
.

An algorithm
A
is phase-independent if for every two phases
i,j
such that
0\leq i<j<\frac{T}{\tau}
and every
t
such that
1\leq t\leq\tau
it holds that,

\text{if }\left(u_{i\cdot\tau+l}\right)_{l=1}^{t}=\left(u_{j\cdot\tau+l}\right)_{l=1}^{t}\text{, then }a^{A}_{i\cdot\tau+t}=a^{A}_{j\cdot\tau+t}.

Phase-independent algorithms are easier to analyze, since we optimize over
\tau
and not
T>>\tau
rounds. Another useful property is the following. We say that an algorithm is
committed
to a subset of arms if it pulls arms from that subset only
and
satisfies the exposure constraints of every arm in that subset. Put differently,

Property 2

(Committed)
.

An algorithm
A
is committed to an arm subset
Z\subseteq K
if for all
t
,
1\leq t\leq T
it always holds that

•

a_{t}^{A}\in Z
; and,

•

Z\subseteq K^{A}_{t}
.

For an algorithm
A
to be committed to
Z
, it must satisfy the two conditions of Property
2
with a probability of
1
(over the randomization of user type arrivals and rewards). Further, the second condition, i.e.,
Z\subseteq K^{A}_{t}
, means that
A
must ensure that the arms
Z
receive their exposure minimums and subsidize if needed.

Naturally, an optimal algorithm need not be committed. Two extreme cases are the first phase, where we can temporarily exploit arms with high rewards that are too costly to subsidize; and the last phase, where we have no incentive to subsidize at all. Committed algorithms cannot pick arms outside their committed subset (the first condition of Property
2
). Due to similar reasons, optimal algorithms need not be phase-independent.
However, as we prove in Theorem
2
, focusing on phase-independent and committed algorithms suffices for finding good approximations.

For convenience, we denote by
\tilde{r}^{A}_{i:j}
the reward of an algorithm
A
in rounds
i,\dots,j
; namely,
\tilde{r}^{A}_{i:j}=\sum_{t=i}^{j}v_{t}^{A}
. Clearly,
\tilde{r}^{A}_{i:j}
depends on the arms pulled in rounds
1,\dots,i-1
. In particular,
\tilde{r}^{A}_{1:\tau}
is the reward of the first phase. Since we focus on phase-independent and committed algorithms (recall Properties
1
and

We begin by explaining the offline matching problem. The input is a subset

The function

3.3.2

Next, we rely on the concentration of type arrival distribution

(6)

(later, we replace
SOB
with
\textnormal{DP}^{\star}
and
\textnormal{LCB}^{\star}
).
EES
has two stages. The first stage is exploration, executed in Lines
2
-
8
. In Line
2
we initiate the arm pull counters for every arm. Then, we enter the for loop in Line
3
for
\lceil\nicefrac{{T^{\nicefrac{{2}}{{3}}}}}{{\gamma\tau}}\rceil
phases. The if clause in Line
4
asks whether there is an arm that was not pulled at least
\max(\delta_{a},\gamma\tau)
times in this phase. Recall that
\delta_{a}
is the exposure threshold, and
\gamma\tau
is the amount of arm pulls we require to achieve good parameter estimation (as we explain shortly). In the complementary case, where all arms
a\in K
were pulled at least
\max(\delta_{a},\gamma\tau)
times each, we pull an arm chosen uniformly at random. Either way, we update the arm pulls counter
N(a)
. When the phase terminates, Line
8
sends us back to Line
2
to start a new phase. Notice that by the end of the exploration stage, all arms are still viable. To see this, recall that our assumption in Inequality (
3
) suggests that at some point in every phase, all arms
a
will be pulled at least
\max(\delta_{a},\gamma\tau)
times, which is by definition greater than the exposure constraint
\delta_{a}
.

After the exploration stage ends, the exploitation stage begins. Line
9
calculates the
empirical distribution
\hat{\boldsymbol{P}}
and the utility matrix estimate
\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}
. Namely, for all
u\in U
and
a\in K
,

\displaystyle\hat{P}_{u}=\frac{1}{T_{0}}\sum_{t=1}^{T_{0}}\mathds{1}_{[u_{t}=u]}\quad,\quad\hat{\mu}_{u,a}=\frac{\sum_{t=1}^{T_{0}}v_{t}\cdot\mathds{1}_{[u_{t}=u,a_{t}=a]}}{\sum_{t=1}^{T_{0}}\mathds{1}_{[u_{t}=u,a_{t}=a]}},

where
T_{0}=\frac{1}{\gamma}T^{\nicefrac{{2}}{{3}}}
. We formally prove in Appendix
A.9
that

\left\lVert\boldsymbol{P}-\hat{\boldsymbol{P}}\right\rVert_{\infty}\leq O\left(\sqrt{\nicefrac{{\log T}}{{T^{\nicefrac{{2}}{{3}}}}}}\right)

holds w.h.p., where
\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{\infty}
is the
L_{\infty}
norm. Similarly, we show that
\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\mu}-\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}\right\rVert_{\infty}\leq O(\sqrt{\nicefrac{{\log T}}{{T^{\nicefrac{{2}}{{3}}}}}})
holds w.h.p. as well. Ultimately, in Line
10
we call the stochastic optimization black-box
SOB
with the known instance parameters and the estimates
\hat{\boldsymbol{P}},\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}
, and follow it for the next
T-\nicefrac{{1}}{{\gamma}}\cdot T^{\nicefrac{{2}}{{3}}}
rounds. Working towards bounding the regret, we introduce the following stability property.

Property 3

(stability)
.

A stochastic optimization algorithm
A
is stable if for every two user arrivals sequences
\boldsymbol{q},\boldsymbol{q}^{\prime}\in U^{\tau}
disagreeing on exactly one entry, it holds that

|\tilde{r}_{1:\tau}^{A}(\boldsymbol{q})-\tilde{r}_{1:\tau}^{A}(\boldsymbol{q}^{\prime})|=O(1).

In words, a stochastic optimization algorithm is stable if
changing one entry in the type arrival sequence does not affect the reward much. Clearly,
\textnormal{DP}^{\star}
and
\textnormal{LCB}^{\star}
are stable. We are now ready to analyze the reward of
EES
in terms of the stochastic optimization black box
SOB
.

Theorem 5
.

Let
err(SOB)=\sup\left\{\mathbb{E}[\tilde{r}^{\textnormal{PICO}}]-\mathbb{E}[\tilde{r}^{SOB}]\right\}
be the approximation error of
SOB
w.r.t.
PICO
. Then,

\mathbb{E}[r^{EES(SOB)}]\geq\mathbb{E}[\tilde{r}^{SOB}]-\tilde{O}\left((\sqrt{\gamma}n^{2}+\nicefrac{{1}}{{\gamma}})\cdot T^{\nicefrac{{2}}{{3}}}\right)-err(SOB).

Theorem
5
introduces a new quantity for lower-bounding a learning algorithm’s reward, denoted by
err
. For the stochastic optimization algorithms
\textnormal{LCB}^{\star}
and
\textnormal{DP}^{\star}
from Section
3
, Equation (
5
) and Theorem
4
hint that
err(\textnormal{DP}^{\star})=0
and
err(\textnormal{LCB}^{\star})=O(\nicefrac{{T}}{{\sqrt{\tau}}})
, respectively. On the right-hand side of Theorem
5
,
\mathbb{E}[\tilde{r}^{SOB}]
is the reward of
SOB
with the actual instance parameters
\boldsymbol{\mu},\boldsymbol{P}
; namely, this inequality quantifies the difference between no information about
\boldsymbol{\mu},\boldsymbol{P}
(the left-hand side) and complete information of
\boldsymbol{\mu},\boldsymbol{P}
(the right-hand side). we now prove Theorem
5
.

Proof of
Theorem

5
.

Fix a reward matrix
\boldsymbol{\mu}
and arrival distribution
\boldsymbol{P}
. Throughout the proof, we use
\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}},\hat{\boldsymbol{P}}
as the estimates
EES
calculates in Line
9
. The following proposition suggests that the estimates must be close to the true parameters w.h.p.

Proposition 5
.

Let
\epsilon_{1}^{u}=\sqrt{\frac{\log T}{P_{u}\cdot T^{\nicefrac{{2}}{{3}}}-\sqrt{P_{u}\cdot T^{\nicefrac{{2}}{{3}}}\log T}}}
for every
u\in U
and let
\epsilon_{2}=\sqrt{\frac{\log T}{\frac{1}{\gamma}\cdot T^{\nicefrac{{2}}{{3}}}}}
. It holds that

\displaystyle\Pr\Big[\exists u\in U,a\in K:|\mu_{u,a}-\hat{\mu}_{u,a}|>\epsilon_{1}^{u}\textnormal{ or }|P_{u}-\hat{P}_{u}|>\epsilon_{2}\Big]\leq\frac{4\cdot k\cdot n+2\cdot n}{T^{2}}.

The proof of Proposition
5
and all other formal statements inside the proof of Theorem
5
appear in Appendix
A.9
. Notice that
\epsilon_{1}^{u}=O\left(\sqrt{\nicefrac{{\log T}}{{T^{\nicefrac{{2}}{{3}}}}}}\right)
for every
u\in U
since
P_{u}
is a constant for every
u\in U
(recall our assumption in Subsection
2.4
).
Proposition
5
helps us bounding the probability of the
clean event
, i.e., sampling rewards and type arrivals such that
\forall u\in U,a\in K:|\mu_{u,a}-\hat{\mu}_{u,a}|\leq\epsilon_{1}^{u}
and
|P_{u}-\hat{P}_{u}|\leq\epsilon_{2}
. The bad event causes an expected regret of at most
T\cdot\frac{4\cdot k\cdot n+2\cdot n}{T^{2}}=O(\frac{kn}{T})=O(1)
, so we can ignore it in what follows.

Let
A
be any stochastic optimization algorithm. For ease of notation, we denote by
V^{\boldsymbol{\mu},\boldsymbol{P}}(A)
the expected reward of
A
when the instance parameters are
\boldsymbol{\mu},\boldsymbol{P}
. I.e.,

V^{\boldsymbol{\mu},\boldsymbol{P}}(A)=\mathbb{E}_{u_{1},\dots,u_{T}\sim\boldsymbol{P}^{T}}[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mu_{u_{t},a^{A}_{t}}].

Next, Proposition
6
shows that the reward discrepancy in case we slightly perturb
\boldsymbol{\mu},\boldsymbol{P}
is limited.

Proposition 6
.

Let
A
be any stochastic optimization algorithm satisfying Properties
1
-
3
. Then,

\forall\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{P}^{\prime}:|V^{\boldsymbol{\mu},\boldsymbol{P}}(A(\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{P}^{\prime}))-V^{\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}},\hat{\boldsymbol{P}}}(A(\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{P}^{\prime}))|\leq\tilde{O}(\sqrt{\gamma}n^{2}\cdot T^{\nicefrac{{2}}{{3}}}).

We leverage Proposition
6
to show the following claim.

Proposition 7
.

Let
A
be any stochastic optimization algorithm satisfying Properties
1
-
3
. Then,

V^{\boldsymbol{\mu},\boldsymbol{P}}(A(\boldsymbol{\mu},\boldsymbol{P}))-V^{\boldsymbol{\mu},\boldsymbol{P}}(A(\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}},\hat{\boldsymbol{P}}))\leq\tilde{O}(\sqrt{\gamma}n^{2}\cdot T^{\nicefrac{{2}}{{3}}})+err(A).

Finally, we lower bound
EES(SOB)
’s reward using Proposition
7
.

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[r^{EES(SOB)}]\geq\mathbb{E}\left[\tilde{r}_{\frac{T^{\nicefrac{{2}}{{3}}}}{\gamma}:T}^{SOB(\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}},\hat{\boldsymbol{P}})}\right]

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{E}[\tilde{r}_{1:T}^{SOB(\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}},\hat{\boldsymbol{P}})}]-O\left(\nicefrac{{1}}{{\gamma}}\cdot T^{\nicefrac{{2}}{{3}}}\right)

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{E}[\tilde{r}^{SOB(\boldsymbol{\mu},\boldsymbol{P})}]-O\left(\left(\nicefrac{{1}}{{\gamma}}+\sqrt{\gamma}n^{2}\right)T^{\nicefrac{{2}}{{3}}}\right)-err(SOB).

∎

To conclude the analysis, we present the consequences of executing
EES
with
\textnormal{DP}^{\star}
and
\textnormal{LCB}^{\star}
from Section
3
. Inequality (
5
) hints that
\mathbb{E}[\tilde{r}^{\textnormal{DP}^{\star}}]=\mathbb{E}[\tilde{r}^{\textnormal{PICO}}]
, and Theorem
2
bounds the difference between
\mathbb{E}[\tilde{r}^{\textnormal{PICO}}]
and
\mathbb{E}[\tilde{r}^{\textnormal{OPT}}]
. Applying these to Theorem
5
,

\mathbb{E}[r^{EES(\textnormal{DP}^{\star})}]\geq\mathbb{E}[\tilde{r}^{\textnormal{OPT}}]-k\cdot\tau-\tilde{O}\left((\sqrt{\gamma}n^{2}+\nicefrac{{1}}{{\gamma}})\cdot T^{\nicefrac{{2}}{{3}}}\right)-err(\textnormal{DP}^{\star}).

Since
err(\textnormal{DP}^{\star})=0
, when rearranging, we get a regret of

\mathbb{E}[R^{EES(\textnormal{DP}^{\star})}]=\mathbb{E}[\tilde{r}^{\textnormal{OPT}}]-\mathbb{E}[r^{EES(\textnormal{DP}^{\star})}]\leq\tilde{O}\left((\sqrt{\gamma}n^{2}+\nicefrac{{1}}{{\gamma}})\cdot T^{\nicefrac{{2}}{{3}}}+k\cdot\tau\right).

Using
\textnormal{LCB}^{\star}
as the stochastic optimization black-box, Theorem
2
and Theorem
4
suggest that

\mathbb{E}[R^{EES(\textnormal{LCB}^{\star})}]\leq\tilde{O}\left((\sqrt{\gamma}n^{2}+\nicefrac{{1}}{{\gamma}})\cdot T^{\nicefrac{{2}}{{3}}}+k\cdot\tau+\nicefrac{{nT}}{{\sqrt{\tau}}}\right).

summarizes the performance of

and

under the regime we focus on, i.e., when

are constants and

. In particular, for
\tau=\Theta(T^{\nicefrac{{2}}{{3}}})
, the regret of both algorithms is
\tilde{O}(T^{\nicefrac{{2}}{{3}}})
.
EES(\textnormal{LCB}^{\star})
is significantly faster, and is appealing even if

. The runtime improvement of

results in inferior regret bound compared to

. Consequently, when

\mathbb{E}[R^{EES(\textnormal{LCB}^{\star})}]=\tilde{O}(T^{\nicefrac{{5}}{{6}}})

4.2
Lower Bound

Having shown that
EES
incurs a regret of
\tilde{O}(T^{\nicefrac{{2}}{{3}}})
for every
\tau\leq O(T^{\nicefrac{{2}}{{3}}})
, we now turn to show that such regret is inevitable. Namely, we show that for any algorithm, there exists an instance in which it incurs a regret of
\Omega(T^{\nicefrac{{2}}{{3}}})
.

Theorem 6
.

Fix any
\tau
such that
\tau=O(T^{\nicefrac{{2}}{{3}}})
, and any arbitrary learning algorithm
A
. Then, there exists an instance for which
\mathbb{E}[R^{A}]=\Omega(T^{\nicefrac{{2}}{{3}}})
.

Proof of
Theorem

6
.

We show two indistinguishable instances, and prove that any algorithm incurs a regret of
\Omega(T^{\nicefrac{{2}}{{3}}})
on at least one of them. For both instances
n=2,k=3,\boldsymbol{P}=(1-p,p),\boldsymbol{\delta}=(0,p\tau,p\tau)
for any constant
0<p<\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}
. The instances differ in their expected rewards. We assume Bernoulli rewards, where Instance 1 has
\boldsymbol{\mu}=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}&0&0\\
0&\nicefrac{{1+\epsilon_{1}}}{{2}}&\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}\end{smallmatrix}\right)
and Instance 2 has
\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\prime}=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}&0&0\\
0&\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}&\nicefrac{{1+\epsilon_{1}}}{{2}}\end{smallmatrix}\right)
, where
\epsilon_{1}=\frac{1}{2\sqrt{2c}}T^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{3}}}
for a constant
c
such that
c\cdot T^{\nicefrac{{2}}{{3}}}>\tau
. We pick each instance w.p.
\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}
.

Fix a learning algorithm
A
, and let
t^{\star}
denote the last round of the last phase that all arms are viable (possibly a random variable). For example, if the first arm to depart departs by the end of phase
i
, then
t^{\star}=(i-1)\cdot\tau
(the last round in phase
i-1
). We prove the following lemma in Appendix
A.10
.

Lemma 2
.

For any algorithm
A
,
\mathbb{E}[R^{A}]\geq\Omega(p\cdot t^{\star})
for both instances.

Next, we conduct a case analysis on
t^{\star}
. If
t^{\star}\geq T^{\nicefrac{{2}}{{3}}}
, then the regret is
\mathbb{E}[R^{A}]=\Omega(T^{\nicefrac{{2}}{{3}}})
by Lemma
2
. Otherwise, an arm departs after
t^{\star}+\tau\leq T^{\nicefrac{{2}}{{3}}}+\tau\leq 2\cdot\max(T^{\nicefrac{{2}}{{3}}},\tau)
rounds. If arm
a_{1}
is the departing arm, every type
1
-user generates no reward, leading to a regret of
\mathbb{E}[R^{A}]=\Theta(T)
.

The remaining case is when
t^{\star}<T^{\nicefrac{{2}}{{3}}}
and arm
a_{2}
or
a_{3}
is the departing arm. Depending on the instance, one of these arms is instrumental in achieving low regret. In Instance 1 with rewards
\boldsymbol{\mu}
, arm
a_{2}
’s departure leads to losing
\nicefrac{{\epsilon_{1}}}{{2}}
every time a type
2
-user enters the system. The same applies for Instance 2 with rewards
\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\prime}
and arm
a_{3}
. The following lemma uses standard KL divergence machinery for indistinguishability that will turn useful for our analysis.

Lemma 3
.

No algorithm can distinguish the two instances with a probability of at least
\nicefrac{{3}}{{4}}
after
2\max(T^{\nicefrac{{2}}{{3}}},\tau)
rounds.

As a corollary of Lemma
3
, the instrumental arm departs with a probability of at least
\nicefrac{{1}}{{4}}
. Otherwise, we could use
A
for distinguishing the two instances, in contradiction to Lemma
3
. Consequently, with a probability of at least
\nicefrac{{1}}{{4}}
,
A
loses
\nicefrac{{\epsilon_{1}}}{{2}}=\Theta(T^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{3}}})
for every type 2-user in the remaining rounds. As a result,
\mathbb{E}[R^{A}]\geq\frac{1}{4}\cdot p(T-t^{\star})\cdot\frac{\epsilon_{1}}{2}=\Omega(T^{\frac{2}{3}})
.
∎

Interestingly, the construction of Theorem
6
does not assume uncertainty about
\boldsymbol{P}
. Namely, it holds even if the learning algorithm has complete information on the user type distribution
\boldsymbol{P}
.

5
Extensions and Further Analysis

This section discusses some of our results and relaxes several modeling assumptions.

5.1
Exhaustive Arm Subset Selection is Inevitable

Both
\textnormal{DP}^{\star}
and
\textnormal{LCB}^{\star}
exhaustively iterate over all subsets
Z\subseteq K
to find
Z^{\star}
(see Line
2
of Algorithm
1
and Line
2
of Algorithm
2
, respectively). Unfortunately, as we now show, this exhaustive search over
2^{k}
elements is inevitable.

To show this, we present the following task. Assume that we are given the user type arrival sequence
\boldsymbol{u}\in U^{\tau}
of a phase. Our goal is to maximize the sum of rewards under the commitment constraint: Using only arms for which the exposure constraint is satisfied by the end of the phase. This task is equivalent to a two-stage task, where we first decide on the subset of arms
Z\subseteq K
to commit to, and then match the users in
\boldsymbol{u}
optimally to arms in
Z
. Notice that this is a deterministic, complete information task. We term this task the
planning task
and remark that
Mladenov et al. 2020a
study this task as a special case. As it turns out, even this deterministic task can require a runtime of
O(2^{k})
.

Proposition 8
.

Solving the planning task requires
\Omega(2^{k})
run time for some instances.

The proof, which appears in Appendix
B
, uses a reduction from the exact cover problem (an NP-complete problem).
Since the planning case is computationally hard in terms of
k
, the stochastic variant of finding
OPT
(or non-constant approximations to
OPT
) is also hard.

Mladenov et al. 2020a
suggested in their work several approximations, e.g., using sub-modular optimization or LP rounding. Those approximations lead to linear regret w.r.t.
OPT
, since they achieve constant approximations. However, if we require the regret benchmark to run in polynomial time, those approximations and others could be useful. We consider such approximations in Subsection
5.5
.

5.2
Unknown Exposure Thresholds

Recall that our model assumes complete information about the exposure thresholds
\boldsymbol{\delta}
. An obvious question is whether one could relax this assumption. Unfortunately, Proposition
9
suggests that uncertainty about
\boldsymbol{\delta}
implies high regret.

Proposition 9
.

Let
A
be any algorithm that does not get
\boldsymbol{\delta}
as input. It holds that
\mathbb{E}[R^{A}]=\Omega(T)
.

The proof of Proposition
9
appears in Appendix
A.11
. We keep the challenge of different modeling of exposure constraints for future work.

5.3
Relaxing Assumption
1

This subsection discusses the consequences of relaxing the Assumption
1
. Inequality (
3
) serves our previous analysis in two crucial ways. First, it allows us to explore all arms for several phases. And second, it ensures that we can explore fast enough. We now analyze the ramifications of violating it. All the proofs of the claims we mention in this subsection appear in Appendix
C
.

Assume that
\sum_{a\in K}\delta_{a}>\tau
. In such a case, the lower bound we presented in Subsection
4.2
increases dramatically. In particular,

•

If
\sum_{a\in K}\delta_{a}\geq\tau+\min_{a}{\delta_{a}}
, then the lower bound becomes linear in
T
.

•

Else,
\sum_{a\in K}\delta_{a}<\tau+\min_{a}{\delta_{a}}
. We show a lower bound of
\Omega\left(T\cdot\sqrt{\nicefrac{{k}}{{\tau}}}\right)
.

Interestingly, these two cases have different regret. To see this, let us focus on the arm
a_{\min}
with the lowest exposure threshold, i.e.,
a_{\min}=\argmin_{a}\delta_{a}
. In the first case, if we pull
a_{\min}
even once, one of the remaining arms
K\setminus\{a_{\min}\}
must depart by the end of the first phase since
\sum_{a\in K\setminus\{a_{\min}\}}\delta_{a}>\tau-1
. So we either avoid pulling
a_{\min}
or letting one arm in
K\setminus\{a_{\min}\}
depart. In the second case, however, we can explore all arms at least once before deciding which exposure constraints to satisfy.

We also investigate cases where fast exploration is not guaranteed. Namely, we have that
\sum_{a=1}^{k}\delta_{a}\leq\tau
but a constant
\gamma
such that
\sum_{a\in K}\max(\delta_{a},\gamma\cdot\tau)\leq\tau
does not exist. Consider the following weaker version of
Assumption
1
: We assume there exists a function
f=f(\tau)
such that
\sum_{a\in K}\max(\delta_{a},f(\tau))\leq\tau.

Notice that
f(\tau)
can be any positive function, e.g.,
O(1)
or
\sqrt{\tau}
. Moreover, we obtain the special case of Assumption
1
if
f(\tau)=\gamma\tau
for a constant
\gamma
. For this relaxation, we show a modified version of Theorem
5
, suggesting a regret of
O(\nicefrac{{\tau}}{{f(\tau)}}T^{\nicefrac{{2}}{{3}}})
. We also describe how to modify
EES
slightly to achieve a better regret of
O((\nicefrac{{\tau}}{{f(\tau)}})^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{3}}}T^{\nicefrac{{2}}{{3}}})
.

5.4
Longer Phase Length

In Assumption
2
, we limited the phase length to be
\tau=O(T^{\nicefrac{{2}}{{3}}})
. We exploited this fact in Theorem
2
, which promises that algorithms satisfying Properties
1
and
2
incur a factor of
k\tau
in their reward. The following Proposition
10
asserts that focusing on phase-independent and committed algorithms can lead to a reward decrease of at least
\tau
in some instances.

Proposition 10
.

There exists an instance such that
\mathbb{E}[\tilde{r}^{\textnormal{OPT}}]-\mathbb{E}[\tilde{r}^{\textnormal{PICO}}]=\Omega(\tau)
.

For long phase length, namely
\tau=\omega(T^{\nicefrac{{2}}{{3}}})
, Proposition
10
conveys a negative result. It suggests that executing
EES
with any stochastic optimization algorithm
Alg
that satisfies Properties
1
and
2
as a black box yields a regret of
\mathbb{E}[R^{EES(Alg)}]=\Omega(\tau)=\omega(T^{\nicefrac{{2}}{{3}}})
.

Instead, we develop a different approach for cases where
\tau=\omega(T^{\nicefrac{{2}}{{3}}})
. We devise
L-LCB
, which extends
\textnormal{LCB}^{\star}
.
L-LCB
is not phase-independent and not committed, and can thus avoid the
\tau
approximation factor that Proposition
10
hints. Furthermore, we prove that the regret of
EES
with
L-LCB
as a black-box is still
\tilde{O}(T^{\nicefrac{{2}}{{3}}})
. From the lower bound perspective, we show a bound of
\Omega(\nicefrac{{T}}{{\sqrt{\tau}}})
for the
\tau=\omega(T^{\nicefrac{{2}}{{3}}})
regime. Tightening the lower bound and developing optimal algorithms that settle the gap for this regime are left for future work. All the proofs of the insights mentioned in this subsection appear in Appendix
D
.

5.5
Polynomial Runtime Algorithm

In Section
4
, we present a low-regret algorithm for the learning task that exhibits a runtime factor of
\Omega(2^{k})
, which is unrealistic for large values of

k
. However, Proposition
8
implies that exponential runtime is not a virtue of our algorithms but rather an inherent property of the problem:
OPT
and even
PICO
incur a
\Omega(2^{k})
runtime factor. The idea that efficient and low-regret algorithms are compared against inefficient benchmarks is cumbersome and unfair. Consequently, in this subsection, we adopt a more lenient regret benchmark, namely a
(1-\nicefrac{{1}}{{e}})
approximation to
OPT
. Such a relaxed benchmark allows us to develop algorithms with both low-regret and efficient runtime.

We devise an algorithm tailored for the learning task with a runtime of
O(T+\tau^{3}k^{5})
. This algorithm yields a
\tilde{O}(T^{\nicefrac{{2}}{{3}}}+\tau+\nicefrac{{T}}{{\sqrt{\tau}}})
regret with respect to the aforementioned weaker benchmark. Elaborated details of the algorithm and its comprehensive analysis are available in Appendix
E
. The foundation of this algorithm and its analysis are mainly based on two prior works:

•

Mladenov et al. 2020a
that investigate the planning task as a special case and establish its sub-modularity; and

•

Nie et al. 2023
, which introduce methods for constructing low regret algorithms for various computationally hard problems. Notably, this includes the task of maximizing sub-modular functions while adhering to cardinality constraints.

5.6
The
match
Algorithm

In this subsection, we explain the computation of
match(c(\boldsymbol{u}),Z)
function (see Subsection
3.3
). We first construct a bipartite graph
G(V,E)
where
V=\mathcal{L}\cup\mathcal{R}
.
The left side contains user nodes: Each user type
u\in U
is represented by
c(\boldsymbol{u})_{u}
identical nodes in
\mathcal{L}
, and
\lvert\mathcal{L}\rvert=\tau
. The right side
\mathcal{R}
comprises of arm duplicates: Each arm
a\in Z
is represented by
\tau
identical nodes in
\mathcal{R}
(
\lvert\mathcal{R}\rvert=\lvert Z\rvert\cdot\tau\leq k\tau
). We can name the nodes in
\mathcal{R}
such that
\mathcal{R}=\{x^{a}_{i}:1\leq i\leq\tau,a\in Z\}
. There is an edge between every node in
\mathcal{L}
and every node in
\mathcal{R}
, i.e., the bipartite graph is complete.

The weight of an edge between a type
u
-user node
y\in\mathcal{L}
and a node of arm
a

x^{a}_{i}
is
2+\mu_{u,a}
if
i
is among the first
\delta_{a}
copies of
a
in
\mathcal{R}
, and
\mu_{u,a}
otherwise. Formally,

w(y,x^{a}_{i})=\begin{cases}2+\mu_{u,a}&i\leq\delta_{a}\\
\mu_{u,a}&o.w.\end{cases}.

The final step is to perform maximum weighted bipartite matching on
G
via, e.g., the Hungarian algorithm.
G
has
\tau+k\tau
nodes, so the run time of
match(c(\boldsymbol{u}),Z)
is
O(k^{3}\tau^{3})
. It only remains to prove optimality and validity. Namely, showing that the resulting match maximizes the phase reward, and is valid, i.e., each user is matched to exactly one arm, and each arm is matched at least
\delta_{a}
times.

Let us start with validity. Each user is represented by one node in
\mathcal{L}
; thus, they are matched exactly once (by the correctness of the maximum weighted bipartite matching). In addition, each arm in
a\in Z
is matched at least
\delta_{a}
times. Otherwise, there is a better match since
\forall u\in U,a\in K:0\leq\mu_{u,a}\leq 1
and the first
\delta_{a}
copy nodes of every arm have a higher weight. We do not match arms
a\in K\setminus Z
at all. Therefore, the resulting match of
match(c(\boldsymbol{q}),Z)
is valid. The optimality of the matching also implies the resulting match maximizes the reward among all the valid matches given
Z
. Since otherwise, there is a match in the graph with a higher weight, which is a contradiction.

6
Discussion

We present a MAB setting where arms have exposure constraints, i.e., arms can abandon the system unless they receive enough pulls per phase. In Section
3
we study the stochastic optimization task, where user arrival sequences are sampled from a known distribution and devised
\textnormal{DP}^{\star}
and
\textnormal{LCB}^{\star}
. Then, Section
4
proposes
EES
, which is a meta-algorithm for the learning task. Combined with
\textnormal{DP}^{\star}
or
\textnormal{LCB}^{\star}
,
EES
achieves low regret.
Our work is among the first to introduce strategic behavior via exposure constraints in the face of uncertainty. As such, there are several future directions.

First, from a technical point of view, our algorithms have optimal regret up to logarithmic factors under the assumptions of Section
2.4
. Obtaining optimal algorithm under different assumptions and other regimes, for example, if
\tau=\Omega(T^{\nicefrac{{2}}{{3}}})
(as seen in Subsection
5.4
) is left for future work.

Second, our model relies on the assumption that the exposure thresholds of the providers are publicly known. Note this is a rather strong assumption that does not apply in most real-world cases. Regrettably, as demonstrated in Subsection
5.2
, any algorithm lacking knowledge of these thresholds incurs a linear regret. Future research could involve exploring other models that account for uncertainty surrounding the thresholds. For instance, one could consider a mechanism design model in which arms report their thresholds to the system, potentially manipulating and misreporting. In such a model, the system might pursue both truthfulness and the optimization of user welfare.

Finally and more conceptually, studying other exposure constraints is a promising direction. Our constraints are hard and require minimal periodic exposure, but constraints that are soft, e.g., an arm departs with a probability that decreases with the amount of exposure it receives are also realistic and deserve future attention.

Appendix B
The Planning Task

We define the planning task in Subsection
5.1
. This section formally presents it and proves Proposition
8
.

B.1
Mathematical formulation

The planning task is the following optimization problem.

\argmax_{X,Z}\sum_{t=1}^{\tau}\sum_{j=1}^{k}\mu_{u_{t},j}\cdot X_{t,j}\quad,

such that

1.

\forall t\in\{1,\dots,\tau\}:\sum_{j=1}^{k}X_{t,j}=1
(one arm per round).

2.

\forall j\in K:\sum_{t=1}^{\tau}X_{t,j}\geq\delta_{j}\cdot Z_{j}
(commitment, see Property
2
).

3.

\forall t\in\{1,\dots,\tau\},j\in K:X_{t,j}\leq Z_{j}
(commitment, see Property
2
) .

4.

\forall t\in\{1,\dots,\tau\},\forall j\in K:X_{t,j}\in\{0,1\}.

5.

\forall j\in K:Z_{j}\in\{0,1\}.

The binary matrix
X
represents the matching of users to arms, namely
X_{t,j}=1
if and only if the user
u_{t}
is matched to an arm
j
. The binary variables
Z_{j}
for
j\in K
get
Z_{j}=1
if and only if arm
j
is in the committed subset. Notice that Constraints
4
and
5
are binary constraints, so this problem is a Mixed Integer Linear Programming that is generally hard to solve. For ease of presentation, we denote the value of this optimization problem by
f=f(X,Z)
. We want to find
\argmax_{X,Z}f(X,Z)
.

In Subsection
B.2
, we show that finding
X,Z
that maximize
f
is an NP-hard problem.

Proof.

. Recall that we represent the planning problem by the tuple

, where
\boldsymbol{q}\in U^{\tau}
is known (complete information). Next, we define the Exact-Cover problem.

. We overload the notation of

Proof of Proposition
22
.

In this proof, we assume that we are in the ”clean” event. I.e., we assume that for every
u\in U
it holds that
|P_{u}-\hat{P}_{u}|\leq\tilde{O}(T^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{3}}})
. We can assume it since we show in the proof of Proposition
5
that the ”bad” event happens with probability
\leq\nicefrac{{2n}}{{T^{3}}}
. Consequently, its effect on the expected rewards is negligible. It stems directly from the definition of
\boldsymbol{c}^{+}
that

\sum_{u\in U}\lvert\boldsymbol{c}^{+}_{u}-\hat{\boldsymbol{c}}^{+}_{u}\rvert=\sum_{u\in U}\lvert P_{u}\tau-\hat{P}_{u}\tau\rvert=\tilde{O}(n\tau T^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{3}}}).

Let’s fix
Z\subseteq K
. Assuming that
match(\hat{\boldsymbol{c}}^{+},Z,\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}})
chooses the same matching as
match(\boldsymbol{c}^{+},Z,\boldsymbol{\mu})
, its expected reward is at least
match(\boldsymbol{c}^{+},Z,\boldsymbol{\mu})
’s expected reward minus two factors: The difference in aggregate values (
\tilde{O}(n\tau T^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{3}}})
) and the difference in preferences scaled by the matching size (
\tau\cdot\tilde{O}(T^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{3}}})
).

As
match
is an optimal algorithm, if
match(\hat{\boldsymbol{c}}^{+},Z,\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}})
does not choose the same match as
match(\boldsymbol{c}^{+},Z,\boldsymbol{\mu})
, it means that its reward would be better than the reward it could achieve by selecting the match of
match(\boldsymbol{c}^{+},Z,\boldsymbol{\mu})
. Overall, we get that

match(\hat{\boldsymbol{c}}^{+},Z,\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}})\geq match(\boldsymbol{c}^{+},Z,\boldsymbol{\mu})-\tilde{O}(n\tau T^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{3}}})-\tau\cdot\tilde{O}(T^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{3}}})=match(\boldsymbol{c}^{+},Z,\boldsymbol{\mu})-\tilde{O}(n\tau T^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{3}}}).

From symmetry, it holds for every
Z\subseteq K
that

\lvert match(\boldsymbol{c}^{+},Z,\boldsymbol{\mu})-match(\hat{\boldsymbol{c}}^{+},Z,\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}})\rvert\leq\tilde{O}(n\tau T^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{3}}}).

∎
