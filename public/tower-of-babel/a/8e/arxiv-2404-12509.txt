Compositional Neural Textures
Peihan Tu, Li-Yi Wei, Matthias Zwicker

Abstract
. Texture plays a vital role in enhancing visual richness in both real photographs and computer-generated imagery. However, the process of editing textures often involves laborious and repetitive manual adjustments of textons, which are the recurring local patterns that characterize textures. This work introduces a fully unsupervised approach for representing textures using a compositional neural model that captures individual textons. We represent each texton as a 2D Gaussian function whose spatial support approximates its shape, and an associated feature that encodes its detailed appearance. By modeling a texture as a discrete composition of Gaussian textons, the representation offers both expressiveness and ease of editing. Textures can be edited by modifying the compositional Gaussians within the latent space, and new textures can be efficiently synthesized by feeding the modified Gaussians through a generator network in a feed-forward manner. This approach enables a wide range of applications, including transferring appearance from an image texture to another image, diversifying textures, texture interpolation, revealing/modifying texture variations, edit propagation, texture animation, and direct texton manipulation. The proposed approach contributes to advancing texture analysis, modeling, and editing techniques, and opens up new possibilities for creating visually appealing images with controllable textures.

1.
Introduction

Texture plays a vital role in enhancing visual richness in both real photographs and computer-generated imagery by providing intricate details, patterns, and surface characteristics.
In practice, users often need to create or adjust textures in an image, but the process can be challenging and time-consuming, often requiring laborious manual adjustments due to the repetitive details.
To avoid ambiguity, we follow the definition of “textures” as repetitive patterns
[
Wei et al., 2009
]
instead of general images mapped to 3D objects
[
Blinn and Newell, 1976
;
Heckbert, 1986
;
Cao et al., 2023
;
Chen et al., 2022
]
.

Previous research in texture manipulation has explored various applications, ranging from global control
[
Hertzmann et al., 2001
;
Rosenberger et al., 2009
;
Kaspar et al., 2015
]
,
regional variations
[
Matusik et al., 2005
;
Frühstück et al., 2019
;
Yu et al., 2019
;
Bellini et al., 2016
;
Dekel et al., 2015
]
, to individual textons
[
Brooks and Dodgson, 2002
]
.
These approaches rely on domain-specific priors for different applications or categories of textures.
Ideally, we would like to have a general-purpose approach that can be applied to a wide range of textures and editing tasks across different scales.

In this paper, we introduce a texture representation that decomposes textures into a composition of recurring neural elements (Figure
1a
), disentangling the geometry and appearance of each element. We obtain this representation by developing an autoencoder architecture that is trained in a fully unsupervised manner.
Our representation is human-intelligible, applicable to a wide range of textures that exhibit sufficient self-repetitions, and enhancing various editing techniques (Figure
1
).
Textures can be edited by manipulating the compositional neural representations in a deep latent space, and efficiently generated by feeding the manipulated representations through a generator (decoder) network in a feed-forward manner.
The supported editing operations include, but are not limited to,
transferring appearance from a texture to another image (Figures
1b
and
1c
), diversifying textures (Figure
4
), texture interpolation and morphing (Figure
1d
), revealing/modifying texture variations (Figure
1e
), edit propagation (Figure
1f
), texture animation (supplementary video), and direct manipulation of textons (Figure
1g
).

Our main idea of representing textures using a compositional neural model is rooted in the understanding that textures are characterized by their recurring patterns, which can vary from regular to irregular formations. Within each texture, basic recurring patterns are known as textons, acting as building blocks that define the texture’s unique appearance and structure.
However, extracting textons from diverse textures poses considerable challenges due to their ambiguous nature and the difficulty in defining them rigorously.
One possible texton extraction approach is to use supervised learning, for example, by training image segmentation networks.
While supervised approaches have achieved state-of-the-art performance for extracting well-defined concepts from images
[
Krizhevsky et al., 2012
;
Minaee et al., 2021
]
using massive, annotated datasets,
these are less suitable for extracting textons due to their numerous and ambiguous nature that can be challenging for consistent annotations with reasonable quality, cost, and time.
Therefore, we develop a fully unsupervised learning approach to extract neural textons.

First, we propose a set of desired properties of (neural) textons detailed in Section
3.1
, including
1) discrete objectness, 2) spatial compactness, 3) reshuffling invariance and 4) transformation equivariance.
These properties enable the textons to represent diverse sets of textures while maintaining human interpretability for intuitive texture editing.
As per these properties, second,
we represent each neural texton as a 2D Gaussian with an associated appearance feature.
A Gaussian approximates a texton’s shape via its spatial support, and the appearance feature encodes the texton’s exact appearance.
This separation facilitates the enforcement of geometric transformation equivariance.
Further, the separate encoding of rough spatial and detailed appearance information in Gaussian covariances and associated features offers comprehensibility and intuitive user control over textons
[
Hertz et al., 2022
;
Epstein et al., 2022
]
.
Third,
we develop unsupervised training methods to instill the desired properties in neural textons by using proper loss functions.
For example, the reshuffling invariance property dictates that randomly reshuffling the appearances among textons should not modify the texture’s overall appearance.
We thus design two training losses to assess the texture similarity between the input texture and a reconstructed texture with reshuffled texton appearance features.
Finally,
we observe that the training losses may present contradictory behaviors, where minimizing one could lead to the increase of another, and address this issue by carefully adjusting the training schedules to achieve a balanced trade-off between these losses.

Our main contributions are: 1) proposing the first compositional neural representation for textures;
2) demonstrating its versatility for supporting various texture editing applications without additional model training;
3) illustrating our approach’s unique capabilities in modeling various textures as textons through comparisons and ablation studies;
and 4) planning to release our code and models to facilitate future research in texture editing and representation.

We propose a compositional neural texture representation by modeling textures as neural textons in a deep latent space with disentangled structure and appearance.
The proposed compositional representation is applicable to a number of texture editing applications and a wide variety of textures.

Compositional Neural Representations

In certain data domains such as programming languages, vector graphics, and engineering designs,
the underlying compositional structure is either inherently present or can be parsed using specific rules.
However, in other domains like 2D images and 3D reconstructions,

To address this, compositional neural representations have been proposed, utilizing a neural latent space to represent various data types compositionally.
This approach has been explored in several raster data types,

3.1
.
Section
3.2

Section

3.1.
Properties of textons

As texton is conceptually ambiguous, we determine 4 desired properties of neural textons which are essential for the textons to be interpretable and useful for texture editing.

Property 1 (Discrete Objectness).

A texton embodies a distinct element within the image that can be identified as either present or absent in its entirety,
and a texture can be fully described by a discrete set of textons.
This discrete set representation facilitates intuitive and interpretable image editing, allowing users to engage in a more natural and user-friendly editing experience.

Property 2 (Spatial Compactness).

A neural texton describes the spatial extent and appearance of a
local
region within a texture.

Property 3 (Reshuffling Invarance).

For a texture with a homogenous type/appearance, random reshuffling of appearances among its textons should span the stochastic (sub)space of a texture without modifying the texture’s overall appearance. That is, reshuffled texton appearances should result in a different version of the original texture.

Property 4 (Transformation Equivariance).

When neural textons undergo a spatial transformation, the resulting image should reflect the same transformation, preserving the spatial relationships and appearance of the textons from the original texture.

These properties are enforced via a combined design of neural texton representation (Section
3.2
), autoencoder architecture (Section
3.3
), and unsupervised training strategies (Section
3.4
).

3.2.
Textures as Compositions of Neural Textons

We represent an image texture as a composition of Gaussians
\{\textbf{g}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
in the autoencoder latent space,
where each Gaussian
\textbf{g}_{i}
represents a texton and
n
is the number of Gaussians.
The choice of Gaussian representation is motivated by its inherent compactness (
Property
2
(
Spatial Compactness
)) and ability to intuitively approximate texton shape.
To facilitate learning the optimal number of Gaussians required for a texture, we assign a weight parameter to each Gaussian
\delta_{i}\in[0,1]
.
As we model textures as a discrete set of textons (
Property
1
(
Discrete Objectness
)),

\textbf{g}_{i}
exists (is valid) in the set if
\delta_{i}
is near 1 and otherwise if
\delta_{i}
is near 0.
Each Gaussian is given by its center
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{i}
and covariance matrix
\mathbf{U}_{i}
that encodes the approximated shape of the texton.
To enhance the encoding of texton geometry and the subsequent geometry-appearance disentanglement, we include a 2D unit-vector
\mathbf{\nu}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
(
\|\mathbf{\nu}_{i}\|=1
) representing the anisotropic direction of textons in addition to Gaussian covariance.
Using
\mathbf{\nu}_{i}
enables the network to more effectively model the directional properties of textons, such as those of an equilateral triangle shape, which are not fully captured by covariance alone.
Furthermore,
\textbf{g}_{i}
contains an appearance parameter given by a feature vector
\mathbf{f}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{f}}
, which is used to encode local texton appearance.
The separate encoding of texton geometry and appearance is fundamental to geometry-appearance disentanglement required by
Property
3
(
Reshuffling Invarance
) and
Property
4
(
Transformation Equivariance
).

In summary, our compositional Gaussian representation
\mathbf{z}
is defined as

\mathbf{z}=\{\textbf{g}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
,

where

\textbf{g}_{i}=\{\delta_{i},\boldsymbol{\mu}_{i},\mathbf{U}_{i},\mathbf{\nu}_{i},\mathbf{f}_{i}\}.

Figure
1a
visualizes the representation of 4 example textures as compositions of Gaussians.

3.3.1.
Encoder

The encoder
\mathcal{E}
includes an image encoder
\mathcal{E}_{FC}
, a segmentation network
\mathcal{E}_{s}
, and a Gaussian parameter estimation layer
\mathcal{E}_{g}
.
Figure
2
illustrates the encoder architecture.

\mathcal{E}_{FC}
is a shallow, fully convolutional (FC) network in order to avoid encoding non-local structure information, as per
Property
2
(
Spatial Compactness
).
\mathcal{E}_{FC}

takes input images
I\in\mathbb{R}^{H\times W\times 3}
to generate feature maps, and has two branches: the appearance branch produces an appearance feature map
\mathbf{F}_{a}\in\mathbb{R}^{H\times W\times d_{a}}
and the geometry branch produces a direction map
\mathbf{V}\in\mathbb{R}^{H\times W\times 2}
.
The Mask2Former architecture is used
[
Cheng et al., 2022
]
as
\mathcal{E}_{s}
and trained end-to-end with the other networks.

\mathcal{E}_{s}
takes the image
I
and generates pixel-wise, softmax-normalized
n
segmentation masks
\mathbf{S}\in[0,1]^{n\times H\times W}
. Each segment is used to compute a Gaussian.
n
is fixed and is also the number of Gaussians.
For more details about
\mathcal{E}_{FC}
and
\mathcal{E}_{s}
, please refer to Appendix D.1.
Given the
n
-segmentation masks
\mathbf{S}
, the appearance feature map
\mathbf{F}_{a}
, and the directional map
\mathbf{V}
,
\mathcal{E}_{g}
computes the composition of Gaussians
\{\textbf{g}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
, as detailed below.

Spatial parameters

The mean
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{i}=\frac{\sum_{\mathbf{p}}\mathbf{p}\cdot\mathbf{S}_{i}(\mathbf{p})}{\mathfrak{S}_{i}}
and covariance
\mathbf{U}_{i}=\frac{\sum_{\mathbf{p}}(\mathbf{p}-\boldsymbol{\mu}_{i})^{T}(\mathbf{p}-\boldsymbol{\mu}_{i})\cdot\mathbf{S}_{i}(\mathbf{p})}{\mathfrak{S}_{i}}
of the
i^{\text{th}}
Gaussian
\textbf{g}_{i}
is computed as the average and covariance of image coordinates
\mathbf{p}=[x,y]
weighted by the
i^{\text{th}}
segmentation mask
\mathbf{S}_{i}
,
where
\mathfrak{S}_{i}=\sum_{\mathbf{p}}\mathbf{S}_{i}(\mathbf{p})
is the normalization factor computed from the
{i}^{\text{th}}
segment.
The direction
\mathbf{\nu}_{i}
is computed as the weighted average (pooling) of directional map
\mathbf{V}
within each image segment as

(1)

\mathbf{\nu}_{i}=\frac{\sum_{\mathbf{p}}\mathbf{V}(\mathbf{p})\mathbf{S}_{i}(\mathbf{p})}{\mathfrak{S}_{i}},

followed by normalization
\mathbf{\nu}_{i}=\mathbf{\nu}_{i}/\|\mathbf{\nu}_{i}\|
.

Gaussian weight

The Gaussian weight
\delta_{i}
is used to adaptively adjust the number of “valid” Gaussians by indicating the existence of each Gaussian. While this existence weight should ideally be binary as indicated by
Property
1
(
Discrete Objectness
), during model training it is crucial to ensure that gradients are effectively back-propagated through the entire network.
Hence, we introduce a differentiable, randomized sampling mechanism for computing
\delta_{i}\sim B(p_{i})
as a sample from Bernoulli distribution
B
.
\delta_{i}
takes the value 1 with the probability
p_{i}=\frac{\sum_{\mathbf{p}}\mathbf{S}_{i}(\mathbf{p})\mathbf{S}_{i}(\mathbf{p})}{\mathfrak{S}_{i}}
computed
as the average of mask
\mathbf{S}_{i}
weighted by
\mathbf{S}_{i}
.
If the mask has either high probability values (such as in a local region) or zero values (like those outside the region), which might indicate a generally reliable mask,
p_{i}
will be high and
\delta_{i}\sim B(p_{i})
, thus the Gaussian
\textbf{g}_{i}
is more likely to exist (
\delta_{i}=1
).
Conversely, if many pixels have low but non-zero probability values, implying uncertainty in the segmentation,
p_{i}
will be low and the Gaussian
\textbf{g}_{i}
is more likely not to exist (
\delta_{i}=0
).
Because sampling from a (categorical) Bernoulli distribution is not differentiable, during training we apply the Gumbel-softmax trick
[
Jang et al., 2016
]
to approximate the hard sampling process from a categorical distribution.
During inference, we round the continuous values of
p_{i}\in[0,1]
to obtain binary values
\delta_{i}\in\{0,1\}
, aligning with
Property
1
(
Discrete Objectness
).

We find that using the categorical sampling mechanism (
\delta_{i}\sim B(p_{i})
) during training generates more aligned and more reliable texton segmentations and thus higher-quality latent Gaussians than directly setting
\delta_{i}
as
p_{i}
.
We hypothesize that the sampling mechanism inherently promotes weights to converge towards 0 or 1, thereby ensuring binary outcomes (
Property
1
(
Discrete Objectness
)),
while simultaneously maintaining
exploration
capabilities through randomized sampling when searching for good texton representations.
We could not achieve the same balanced effects by adjusting the training losses or loss schedulers without this sampling mechanism.

Appearance parameters

Similarily to Equation (
1
), the appearance feature
\mathbf{f}_{i}=\frac{\sum_{\mathbf{p}}\mathbf{F}_{a}(\mathbf{p})\mathbf{S}_{i}(\mathbf{p})}{\mathfrak{S}_{i}}

is the weighted average of image appearance features
\mathbf{F}_{a}
,
followed by normalization
\mathbf{f}_{i}=\mathbf{f}_{i}/\|\mathbf{f}_{i}\|
.

1
(
Discrete Objectness
)

In the process of encoding textures as Gaussisans
\{\textbf{g}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
, we calculate several losses on the intermediate segmentation masks
\mathbf{S}
during end-to-end training (Figure
2
).
One is the entropy loss
L_{E}
, which encourages reliable hard segmentation and thus discrete textons with
p_{i}\rightarrow 1
or
p_{i}\rightarrow 0
,

(4)

L_{E}=-\frac{1}{HW}\sum_{\mathbf{p}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{S}_{i}(\mathbf{p})\log(\mathbf{S}_{i}(\mathbf{p})).

L_{E}
is minimized when segmentation masks
\mathbf{S}
are hard with probabilities either 0 or 1, or equivalently when
p_{i}=0
or
1
.

Property
2
(
Spatial Compactness
)

We also apply a compactness loss
L_{C}

[
Yang et al., 2020
]
, especially during the initial phase of training (see Appendix D.3) on the segmentation masks so that each segment
\mathbf{S}_{i}
and thus
\textbf{g}_{i}
is spatially compact and localized,

(5)

\displaystyle L_{C}=\frac{1}{HW}\sum_{\mathbf{p}}\lVert\mathbf{p}^{\prime}-\mathbf{p}\rVert_{2}^{2},

where
\mathbf{p}^{\prime}=\sum_{i}\boldsymbol{\mu}_{i}\cdot\mathbf{S}_{i}(\mathbf{p})
.

Property
3
(
Reshuffling Invarance
)

Random reshuffling of texton appearances
\mathbf{f}
(with other geometric parameters unchanged) should result in a different version of original texture without altering overall texture appearance.
More specifically,
appearance feature reshuffling
, denoted as
\pi
, permutes appearance features within Gaussians to obtain another set of Gaussians
\{\tilde{\textbf{g}}_{i}\}=\pi(\{\textbf{g}_{i}\})
.
In this randomized process
\{\tilde{\textbf{g}}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}=\pi(\{\textbf{g}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n})
,
when the appearance feature
\mathbf{f}_{i}
in
\textbf{g}_{i}
moves to
\textbf{g}_{j}
originally with feature
\mathbf{f}_{j}
,

\textbf{g}_{j}
becomes
\tilde{\textbf{g}}_{j}
and the resulting appearance feature
\tilde{\mathbf{f}_{j}}
in
\tilde{\textbf{g}}_{j}
is calculated as

(6)

\tilde{\mathbf{f}_{j}}=\tau_{i\rightarrow j}\mathbf{f}_{i}+(1-\tau_{i\rightarrow j})\mathbf{f}_{j}.

where
\tau_{i\rightarrow j}\in[0,1]
is the reshuffling coefficient from
\mathbf{f}_{i}
to
\mathbf{f}_{j}
.
During inference, the
appearance feature reshuffling
is only applied among valid discrete textons with their Gaussian weights
\delta
rounded as 1 (while invalid ones have
\delta=0
), and
\tau_{i\rightarrow j}
is computed as

(7)

\displaystyle\tau_{i\rightarrow j}=\delta_{i}\delta_{j}

In the training phase, however, as the network evolves to determine the ideal number of Gaussians representing textons by continuously adjusting the Gaussian weight
\delta
,
it is crucial to “prioritize”
appearance feature reshuffling
among Gaussians that are more likely to represent textons.
While
\delta_{i}
can be used to determine the likelihood of a Gaussian representing a texton after effective network training,
\delta_{i}
is not a reliable indicator of likelihood due to the immaturity of the texton representation during training.
For example, even if
\delta_{i}
is close to 1, the Gaussian may not effectively represent a texton if the area of its corresponding texton segmentation mask
A_{i}=\sum_{\mathbf{p}}\mathbf{S}_{i}
is close to 0.
Therefore, we define the likelihood score of a Gaussian
\textbf{g}_{i}
representing a texton as a tuple
(A_{i},p_{i})
.
A Gaussian
\textbf{g}_{i}
is either likely (if
A_{i}\gg 0
and
p_{i}\gg 0
) or unlikely (if
A_{i}\approx 0
or
p_{i}\approx 0
) to represent a texton.
To prioritize reshuffling among Gaussians that are more likely to represent textons, the reshuffling coefficient
\tau_{i\rightarrow j}
is computed as

(8)

\tau_{i\rightarrow j}=\left(\min\left(\max\left(\frac{A_{i}}{A_{j}},\frac{p_{i}}{p_{j}}\right),1\right)\right)^{\gamma}.

Intuitively, Equation (
8
) will produce a higher value of
\tau_{i\rightarrow j}
if
\textbf{g}_{i}
is more likely than
\textbf{g}_{j}
to represent a texton.
And for the case where
A_{j}\approx 0
or
p_{j}\approx 0
,
\textbf{g}_{j}
will be ignored during the Gaussian splatting process regardless of its
\tau_{i\rightarrow j}
value.

\gamma=0.5
is a non-negative temperature hyperparameter controls the softness of the reshuffling operation, which will be discussed in Appendix D.2.

The Gaussians
\{\tilde{\textbf{g}}_{i}\}
with reshuffled features are reconstructed as the image
\tilde{I}_{r}=\mathcal{G}\left(h(\{\tilde{\textbf{g}}_{i}\})\right)
that should resemble the input image
I
in terms of texture similarity.
To this end, we calculate texture loss
L_{T}
between the original input
I
and image
\tilde{I}_{r}
by using the patch-based texture loss
[
Zhou et al., 2023
]

and non-saturating GAN losses on image patches
L_{PGAN}

[
Park et al., 2020
]
to train
\mathcal{E}
and
\mathcal{G}
.
Please refer to Appendix D.2 for more implementation details.
While enforcing
Property
2
(
Spatial Compactness
) encourages small segmentations/Gaussians, enforcing
Property
3
(
Reshuffling Invarance
) encourages segmentations/Gaussians to capture the shape and location of individual textons so that reshuffling won’t destory texture structure.
Figure
3

reshuffling
shows the reshuffling operation
\pi
and losses to enforce
Property
3
(
Reshuffling Invarance
).

Property
4
(
Transformation Equivariance
)

A consistency loss
L_{Csis}
is defined to reduce the differences between two sets of Gaussians
T(\mathcal{E}(I))
and
\mathcal{E}(T(I))
with associated segmentation masks.
The first set,
T(\mathcal{E}(I))
, is derived by initially extracting Gaussians from the input texture
I
using our encoder
\mathcal{E}
, and subsequently applying the transformation
T
;
the second set,
\mathcal{E}(T(I))
, is obtained by first applying
T
to the input texture
I
, and then extracting Gaussians with the encoder
\mathcal{E}
.
Similarily to
[
Cheng et al., 2022
;
Carion et al., 2020
]
, we compute the bipartite matching
\sigma
between the two sets of Gaussians and segmentation masks and
L_{Csis}
is the sum of differences between matched Gaussians and masks.
In addition, we calculate the reconstruction loss
L^{\prime}_{R}
, using the same formulation as in Equation (
2
), but between transformed images
\hat{I}=T(I)
and images reconstructed from
T\left(\mathcal{E}(I)\right)
while masking out regions transformed outside image boundaries.
Please refer to Appendix D.2 for more details.
Figure
3

disentangling
shows the training mechanism to enforce
Property
4
(
Transformation Equivariance
).

3.5.
Overall Losses

Our final objective function for the encoder
\mathcal{E}
and generator
\mathcal{G}
is
L=L_{R}+L^{\prime}_{R}+w_{E}L_{E}+w_{C}L_{C}+w_{Csis}L_{Csis}+w_{T}L_{T}+w_{GAN}L_{GAN}+w_{PGAN}L_{PGAN}
.
During training, we fix
w_{T},w_{GAN}
and
w_{PGAN}
.
While
w_{E},w_{C}
,
w_{Csis}
are adjused to maintain a balance in the unsupervised training process.
See Appendix D.3 for more training details.

We use 90% images for training and 10% images for testing (applications and analysis).

4.
Applications

We showcase various applications for our compositional neural texture representation.
Each application only involves encoding input textures as latent Gaussians
\{\textbf{g}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
and editing
\{\textbf{g}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
, and the output textures are generated efficiently by feeding the edited latent Gaussians through the generator network in a feed-forward manner.

We don’t require any additional training or fine-tuning for each application
.
For implementation details and more results of each application, please refer to Appendix B.
For efficiency reference, the feed-forward processing of a
256\times 256
image using our autoencoder takes 0.16 seonds on a single NVIDIA A10G (24GB) in PyTorch,
and this is achieved without any specific optimizations for speed.

Texture Diversification

By
appearance feature reshuffling
among valid textons (with rounded
\delta=1
during inference), we can create diverse versions of the same texture, as shown in Figure
4
.

Texture Transfer

Texture transfer combines the structure of one texture with the appearance of another (Figure
5
).
Our results are created by taking the Gaussians from the structure-providing (SP) texture, appearance features from the appearance-providing (AP) texture,
and we only adjust the mean of Gaussian appearance features of the SP texture to match that of the AP texture.

Image stylization

We can stylize a natural image using textures.
While our network is not trained on natural images, a small patch of a natural image can be effectively treated as a texture.
Figure
6a
shows the results by transferring textures to a content image patch by patch.
Users can also transfer different textures to image regions specified by control masks, as shown in Figure
6b
.
Moreover, Figure
6c
shows scale control over transferred textons, achieved by varying the scale of Gaussians derived from content image patches.

Revealing and Modifying Texture Variations

We can modify non-local texture variations by editing variations within the Gaussian’s appearance and geometry parameters.
To edit the variations, we first calculate the means of Gaussian appearance and geometry parameters and edit their deviations from the means.
Figure
7
show the edited image when we reduce and increase the variations in appearance and geometry parameters respectively.

Texture Interpolation

Our representation can be applied for interpolating textures by interpolating latent Gaussians, such as their spatial positions, features and weights.
The interpolated texture is reconstructed from the interpolated Gaussians.
Figures
8a
to
8e
show interpolated textures with various interpolation coefficients
\eta
.
Our method can also generate smooth transition of two textures in one image (Figure
8f
) with spatially varying interpolation coefficient.

5.
Analysis

We compare our method to related approaches and evaluate our approach via ablation studies, both qualitatively and quantitatively.

Comparison to “Segment Anything”

To evaluate the quality of our extracted textons, we compare our segmentation produced by
\mathcal{E}_{s}
against the “Segment Anything” (SA) model
[
Meta, 2023
]
.
The SA model has been trained on a dataset of 1 billion masks with remarkable ability to segment images with zero prior knowledge.
Figure
11
shows the comparisons with the SA model. See Appendix C.1 for more comparisons.
These results indicate SA is incapable of reliably extracting textons, a fundamental aspect that contributes to the versatility of our method across a variety of texture applications.

Comparisons on Texture Transfer

We compare our approach with prior methods
[
Kolkin et al., 2019
;
Tumanyan et al., 2022
;
Cheng et al., 2023
]
tailored for combining appearance from one image and content/structure from another image.
Figure
12
shows the comparisons.
Our method generates more visually appealing results without clear artifacts,
effectively capturing both the structure and appearance from respective SP and AP textures, demonstrating the unique capacity of our approach in disentangled structure-appearance texture modeling.
Note we not only disentangle structure from texture appearance but also explicitly represent them as different Gaussian parameters, enabling various applications (Section
4
) beyond texture transfer.
See Appendix C.2 for more comparisons.

Ablation Studies

We qualitatively and quantitatively evaluate our method via ablation studies to demonstrate the importance of the method components, including loss functions and architectures.
The ablation studies suggest our combined design of loss functions and architectures are crucial for the overall performance of our method. See Appendix C.3 to C.5 for details about the ablation studies.

Figure 13.
Improper compositional representation for elongated structures.

For elongated, continuous structures, it might be improper to model them as localized textons.
Editing individual textons might discrupt the continuous structures.

Our approach relies on the assumption of random reshuffling of texton appearances (
Property
3
(
Reshuffling Invarance
)) that can result in a different version of the original texture without altering its overall appearance.
Consequently, while our method can extract non-stationary structure and generate spatially-varying textures during inference (e.g. Figures
6b
and
8f
), the network is inherently more suitable for textures that exhibit consistent texton appearances throughout the image domain.

Our current method may struggle with patterns with elongated, continuous structures.
In these patterns, the notion of textons might not be clear and each elongated structure might be represented by multiple textons in our current implementation as we impose
Property
2
(
Spatial Compactness
) on learned textons.
Figure
13
shows such an example where texton editing can disrupt continuous structures.
Our approach might not capture the largest possible textons, which would be more aligned with user intentions during editing.
However, in applications such as texture transfer, this limitation is less observable, as shown in Figure 17 (3rd and 4th columns) in the appendix, because the sequences of Gaussians that encode elongated or large structures remain spatially intact during appearance transfer.
Although textons are ill-defined with ambiguity especially for highly unstructured or elongated textures (e.g. textons could be single hair or strands of hair in hair textures), the practical utility of the extracted textons on various textures using the 4 properties (Section
3.1
) is evident by the various applications.

The Gaussian representation may lose fine details, such as sharp edges or corners due to the smoothness nature of Gaussian splatted feature maps and the limited number of Gaussians used. The texture representation has a focus on editing capability with a trade-off versus modeling capability.
Extending the single-level representation to a multi-level representation (e.g., Gaussians of different sizes for different levels) could be an interesting future research direction to better preserve editing capability while increasing modeling capability, especially for sharp texture features.
In addition, as we model a texture as a set of textons that can fully cover the texture, spatial manipulation may expose undefined background to cause extra artifacts.
Therefore, another interesting future direction is to model a texture as textons in the foreground plus a background to improve both editing and modeling capabilities.
Finally, our approach could incorporate supervised training signals from pre-existing semantic segmentation models (e.g. segment anything) to extract semantic textons more accurately.

In this paper, we propose a framework for compositional modeling of repetitive textures.
Using our framework, we present various texture editing applications using simple, baseline approaches, which could be improved with more dedicated mechanisms in the future.
For example, in texture interpolation applications (Figure
8
),
we could interpolate Gaussians via a softer mechanism using optimal transport instead of via the hard approach by solving assignment problems (see Appendix B.5).
Additionally, our approach could extend beyond the presented use cases,
such as texture rectification
[
Hao et al., 2023
]
,
by analyzing and rectifying Gaussian centers, directions and shapes,
and weathering
[
Bellini et al., 2016
]
,
by analyzing and manipulating the appearance features,
font stylization by focusing on font shape, and video stylization by imposing intuitive coherence directly on latent Gaussians.
Furthermore, our framework could be adapted to model repetitive phenomena in other data types, such as materials, vector patterns, 3D geometry, and audio.
