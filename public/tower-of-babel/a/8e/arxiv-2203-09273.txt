Abstract
. We give a relation between the radius and the dimension in which the asymptotic formula in the Waring problem holds in a multiplicative and dimension-free fashion.



2020 Mathematics Subject Classification
11P05, 11P55

1.
Introduction

Let
{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
denote the set of nonnegative natural numbers
{\mathbb{N}}_{0}=\{0,1,2,\ldots,\}.

In 1770 Waring made the statement that for each
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
there exist
d\in{\mathbb{N}}
such that every natural number
N
can be written as

(1.1)

N=n_{1}^{k}+n_{2}^{k}+\ldots+n_{d}^{k},\quad\text{for}\,\,n_{i}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}.

In the same year Lagrange gave a positive answer to the case
k=4
of Waring’s problem.
The first proof which concerns every
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
was given by Hilbert
[
7
]
in 1909. Hilbert’s proof relied on, what is today called, Hilbert’s identity which follows from some algebraic properties of polynomials. In the 1920’ Hardy and Ramanujan
[
5
]
and Hardy and Littlewood
[
6
]
began the study of questions related to Waring’s problem from a more quantitative perspective. Namely, for
N\in{\mathbb{N}}
by
r_{k}(N)
we denote the number of
d
-tuples
(n_{1},n_{2},\ldots,n_{d})\in{\mathbb{N}}^{d}
which solve the equation (
1.1
). In
[
5
,
6
]
the authors were interested in the asymptotic size of
r_{k}(N)
as
N
approaches infinity.

The circle method was pioneered by Hardy and Littlewood in
[
6
]
to prove that for
k\geq 2
,
d\geq 2^{k}+1

we have

(1.2)

\displaystyle r_{k}(N)=\mathfrak{S}(N)\frac{\Gamma\left(1+\frac{1}{k}\right)^{d}}{\Gamma\left(\frac{d}{k}\right)}N^{d/k-1}+\mathcal{O}\big(N^{d/k-1-\delta}\big),

for some
\delta>0.
Here
\Gamma
is the Gamma function,
\mathfrak{S}(N)
is the
singular series
given by

(1.3)

\mathfrak{S}(N)=\mathfrak{S}_{d}^{k}(N):=\sum_{q=1}^{\infty}\sum_{\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{a=1}{(a, q)=1}}^{q}G(a/q)^{d}\boldsymbol{e}\left(-Na/q\right)

with
\boldsymbol{e}\left(z\right)=\exp(2\pi{\boldsymbol{i}}z),
and
G(a/q)
is the Gaussian sum

\displaystyle G_{k}(a/q)=G(a/q):=\frac{1}{q}\sum_{r=1}^{q}\boldsymbol{e}\left(\frac{a}{q}r^{k}\right).

The asymptotic formula (
1.2
) holds for ’large enough’
N,
depending on
k
and
d.

The purpose of our note is to establish for a fixed
k\in\mathbb{N}
a range of
N
(depending on the dimension
d
) with a precise meaning for ’large enough’ for which the asymptotic formula holds.

Theorem 1.4
.

For each
k\in\mathbb{N}
with
k\geq 2
there exists a constant
C_{k}>0
such that, for
d\geq 2^{k+1}
and
N\geq C_{k}d^{20d}
, we have

(1.5)

r_{k}(N)=\mathfrak{S}(N)\frac{\Gamma\left(1+\frac{1}{k}\right)^{d}}{\Gamma\left(\frac{d}{k}\right)}N^{d/k-1}(1+o(1)),

where the implicit constants in the symbol
o(1)
are independent of the dimension
d.

In other words Theorem
1.4
states that for
N\geq C_{k}d^{20d}
one has a dimension-free asymptotic formula for
r_{k}(N)
in the Waring problem (
1.1
) with a multiplicative error term. Apart from the case
k=2
we have not found in the existing literature anything about uniformities with respect to radii and dimensions in (
1.5
). The threshold
N\geq C_{k}d^{20d}
is rather weak and surely far from being optimal in any sense. In particular, in the case
k=2
, Mirek, Szarek, and the third author
[
11
]
have recently obtained a much better range of
N
for a closely related Waring problem. Namely, let
\tilde{r}_{2}(N)
denote the number of
d
-tuples of integer numbers
(n_{1},n_{2},\ldots,n_{d})\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}
which solve the equation

n_{1}^{2}+n_{2}^{2}+\ldots+n_{d}^{2}=N.

It was proved in
[
11
, Theorem 3.1, eq. 3.17]
that for
d\geq 16
one has

\tilde{r}_{2}(N)=\mathfrak{S}(N)\frac{\pi^{d/2}}{\Gamma(d/2)}N^{d/2-1}\big(1+o(1)\big),

whenever
N>Cd^{3}.
The proof from
[
11
]
can be repeated almost verbatim to give a similar formula in our case

r_{2}(N)=\mathfrak{S}(N)\frac{\pi^{d/2}}{2^{d}\Gamma(d/2)}N^{d/2-1}\big(1+o(1)\big)=\mathfrak{S}(N)\frac{\Gamma\left(3/2\right)^{d}}{\Gamma\left(d/2\right)}N^{d/2-1}(1+o(1)).

The error term
o(1)
in the above formula goes to zero as
N\to\infty
in a dimension-free fashion in the range
N>Cd^{3}.

Remark 1.6
.

When
k
-spheres are replaced by
k
-balls one can obtain a huge improvement over the threshold
N\geq C_{k}d^{20d}
. Indeed, let

B_{k}(N):=\Big\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\colon\sum_{j=1}^{d}|x_{j}|^{k}\leq N\Big\}

be the closed unit ball of radius
N^{1/k}
with respect to the
k
-norm and denote by
\tilde{b}_{k}(N)
the number of
d
-tuples of integer numbers
(n_{1},n_{2},\ldots,n_{d})\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}
which belong to
B_{k}(N).

Then, from
[
9
, Lemmas 3.1 and 3.3]
(general
k\geq 2
) and
[
3
, Lemmas 3.1 and 3.3]
(
k=2
) it follows that

(1.7)

c_{k}|B_{k}(N)|\leq\tilde{b}_{k}(N)\leq C_{k}|B_{k}(N)|,

whenever
N\geq T_{k}d^{k},
with
T_{k}>0
being a constant depending only on
k
. The symbol
|B_{k}(N)|
in (
1.7
) denotes the
d
-dimensional Lebesgue measure of
B_{k}(N)
while
c_{k}
and
C_{k}
are constants independent of the dimension
d.
Note that (
1.5
) together with Proposition
3.14
imply an estimate in the spirit of (
1.7
) only within the regime
N\geq C_{k}d^{20d}.
A property that is crucial in establishing (
1.7
) is the nestedness of the
B_{k}(N)
balls, which is obviously not available in the present context.

The methods employed in the proof of Theorem
1.4
are the standard application of the Hardy–Littlewood circle method. Our contribution consists of carefully checking what is the smallest
N
(in terms of
d
) for which the classical method works. The analysis is naturally split into the consideration of major arcs and minor arcs. In fact for a most part of the proof a much weaker condition
N>C_{k}d^{ck^{2}}
is sufficient. The only part where we really need to restrict to
N>C_{k}d^{20d}
lies in the major arc part. More precisely, the replacement of the exponential sum by an exponential integral is problematic in Lemma
3.26
, see Remark
3.31
. In
[
11
]
this problem does not appear due to the use of different methods based on Poisson summation formulas for theta functions. It seems crucial there that
k=2.

2.
Notation

We now set up notation and terminology that will be used throughout the paper. The parameter
k\in\mathbb{N}
is fixed throughout the paper and will always mean the power in Waring’s problem (
1.1
). Therefore whenever we say that a constant is universal we mean that it may only depend on
k.

2.1.
Basic notation

The sets
\mathbb{Z},\mathbb{R}
and
\mathbb{C}
have the standard meaning.

1.

We denote
\mathbb{N}:=\{1,2,\ldots\}
and by
\mathbb{P}
we mean the set of all prime numbers.

2.

For two natural numbers
a,q
the greatest common divisor of
a
and
q
is denoted by
(a,q)
.

3.

For any
x\in\mathbb{R}
we will use the floor function

{\lfloor{x}\rfloor}:=\max\{n\in\mathbb{Z}:n\leq x\}.

Moreover, for
N\in\mathbb{N}
we denote

X_{N}:={\lfloor{N^{1/k}}\rfloor}.

4.

We will use the convention that
\boldsymbol{e}\left(z\right):=e^{2\pi{\boldsymbol{i}}z}
for every
z\in\mathbb{C}
, where
\boldsymbol{i}^{2}=-1
. For
X\in\mathbb{N}
and
\xi\in\mathbb{R}
we denote

f_{X}(\xi):=\sum_{n=1}^{X}\boldsymbol{e}\left(\xi n^{k}\right).

5.

For any
x>0
we will use the Gamma function given by

\Gamma(x):=\int_{0}^{\infty}{t^{x-1}e^{-t}{\rm d}t}.

6.

For
k\geq 2,

k\in\mathbb{N}
we also set

\sigma(k):=\frac{1}{k(k-1)}.

2.2.
Asymptotic notation and magnitudes

1.

For two nonnegative quantities

A,B
we write
A\lesssim_{\delta}B
(
A\gtrsim_{\delta}B
) if
there is an absolute constant
C_{\delta}>0
(which possibly depends
on
\delta>0
) such that
A\leq C_{\delta}B
(
A\geq C_{\delta}B
). We
will write
A\simeq_{\delta}B
when
A\lesssim_{\delta}B
and

A\gtrsim_{\delta}B
hold simultaneously. We will omit the subscript

\delta
if it is irrelevant.

2.

For two nonnegative quantities
A,B
we will also use the convention that
A\lesssim^{d}B
(
A\gtrsim^{d}B
) if
there is an absolute constant
C>0
such that
A\leq C^{d}B
(
A\geq C^{d}B
). We
will write
A\simeq^{d}B
when
A\lesssim^{d}B
and

A\gtrsim^{d}B
hold simultaneously. Sometimes we abuse notation and write
A\lesssim_{\delta}^{d}B
(
A\gtrsim_{\delta}^{d}B
) if
there is an absolute constant
C_{\delta}>0
(which possibly depends
on
\delta>0
) such that
A\leq C_{\delta}^{d}B
(
A\geq C_{\delta}^{d}B
). In the same manner we will write
A\simeq_{\delta}^{d}B
when
A\lesssim_{\delta}^{d}B
and

A\gtrsim_{\delta}^{d}B
hold simultaneously.

3.

For a function
f:X\to\mathbb{C}
and positive-valued
function
g:X\to(0,\infty)
, write
f=\mathcal{O}(g)
if there exists a
constant
C>0
such that
|f(x)|\leq Cg(x)
for all
x\in X
. We will
also write
f=\mathcal{O}_{\delta}(g)
if the implicit constant depends on

\delta
. For two functions
f,g:X\to\mathbb{C}
such that
g(x)\neq 0
for
all
x\in X
we write
f=o(g)
if
\lim_{x\to\infty}f(x)/g(x)=0
.

3.
The circle method of Hardy and Littlewood

The circle method was developed by Hardy and Littlewood in order to handle additive problems in the number theory. It was significantly improved by Vinogradov
[
15
]
in 1928 and was used by him to obtain the asymptotic formula in the Goldbach ternary representation problem. The main innovation introduced by Vinogradov was the usage of the exponential sums instead of infinite series which greatly simplified the method. Usually the application of the circle method is followed by using estimates for the exponential sums like Weyl’s or Vinogradov’s inequality. Over the years the circle method has become a widely used tool in analytic number theory as well as in harmonic analysis, see
[
16
,
17
,
1
,
11
]
and references given there. We recommend an excellent treatise on the subject due to Vaughan
[
13
]
. For more general view on the analytic number theory we refer to the monograph by Iwaniec and Kowalski
[
8
]
.

Now, let us show how to derive the asymptotic formula (
1.5
) with the aid of the circle method. Let
k\geq 2
be a fixed integer and denote

S_{N}=\{(n_{1},n_{2},\ldots,n_{d})\in{\mathbb{N}}^{d}:\ n_{1}^{k}+n_{2}^{k}+\ldots+n_{d}^{k}=N\}.

Observe, that for
X_{N}=\lfloor N^{1/k}\rfloor
one can write

\displaystyle r_{k}(N)

\displaystyle=\sum_{(n_{1},n_{2},\ldots,n_{d})\in{\mathbb{N}}^{d}}{\mathds{1}_{{S_{N}}}}(n_{1},n_{2},\ldots,n_{d})

\displaystyle=\sum_{n_{1}=1}^{X}\ldots\sum_{n_{d}=1}^{X}\int_{0}^{1}\boldsymbol{e}\left(\xi(n_{1}^{k}+n_{2}^{k}+\ldots+n_{d}^{k})\right)e^{-2\pi i\xi N}{\rm d}\xi

(3.1)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\big(f_{X_{N}}(\xi)\big)^{d}\boldsymbol{e}\left(-\xi N\right){\rm d}\xi.

Therefore, our task is reduced to finding the asymptotics for the integral (
3.1
). We follow the approach taken by Hardy and Littlewood and decompose the unit interval
[0,1]
into two disjoint sets, called the major arcs
\mathfrak{M}_{X_{N}}
and the minor arcs
\mathfrak{m}_{X_{N}}
, and evaluate the integral over both sets separately. The major arcs consist of such real numbers
\xi\in[0,1]
which can be ”well approximated” by rational numbers
a/q
with
(a,q)=1
. For
\xi\in\mathfrak{M}_{X_{N}}
we are able to show that

f_{X_{N}}(\xi)\approx G(a/q)\int_{0}^{N^{1/k}}\boldsymbol{e}\left((\xi-a/q)x^{k}\right){\rm d}x.

On the other hand, on the minor arcs, which are the complement of the major arcs, the integral (
3.1
) is negligible.

Following this idea, for fixed
N\in{\mathbb{N}}
and
\alpha\in(0,1/4)
we
define the family of the
major arcs

\mathfrak{M}_{X_{N}}:=\bigcup_{1\leq q\leq X_{N}^{\alpha}}\bigcup_{\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{a=1}{(a, q)=1}}^{q}\mathfrak{M}_{X_{N}}(a/q),

where

\mathfrak{M}_{X_{N}}(a/q):=\big\{\xi\in[0,1]:|\xi-a/q|\leq X_{N}^{-k+\alpha}\big\}\quad\text{with}\quad q\leq X_{N}^{\alpha}.

We see that if
a/q
varies over the rational fractions with small
denominators (
1\leq q\leq X_{N}^{\alpha}
and
(a,q)=1
) then

\mathfrak{M}_{X_{N}}(a/q)
are disjoint.

The
minor arcs
will be the set

\mathfrak{m}_{X_{N}}=[0,1]\setminus\mathfrak{M}_{X_{N}}.

In view of this partition we obtain that

(3.2)

\begin{split}r_{k}(N)&=\int_{\mathfrak{M}_{X_{N}}}\big(f_{X_{N}}(\xi)\big)^{d}\boldsymbol{e}\left(-\xi N\right){\rm d}\xi+\int_{\mathfrak{m}_{X_{N}}}\big(f_{X_{N}}(\xi)\big)^{d}\boldsymbol{e}\left(-\xi N\right){\rm d}\xi\\
&:=M_{k}(N)+m_{k}(N).\end{split}

Now our task is to estimate the quantities
M_{k}(N)
and
m_{k}(N)
.

3.1.
Minor arc estimate

We start with estimating the term
m_{k}(N)
related to the minor arcs.

Proposition 3.3
.

For each
k\geq 2
there exists a constant
C_{k,\alpha}>0
such that, for
d\geq 2^{k+1}
and
N\geq C_{k,\alpha}d^{5k^{2}/\alpha}
we have

(3.4)

m_{k}(N)=\mathfrak{S}(N)\frac{\Gamma\left(1+\frac{1}{k}\right)^{d}}{\Gamma\left(\frac{d}{k}\right)}N^{d/k-1}\cdot o(1),

where the implicit constants in the symbol
o(1)
are independent of the dimension
d.

We make use of a sharpened form of Weyl’s inequality proven by Bourgain
[
2
]
(see also
[
4
, Lemma 3.1]
).

Lemma 3.5

(
[
2
, Theorem 5]
)
.

Suppose that
\xi\in[0,1]
has a rational approximation
a/q
satisfying

(a,q)=1,\quad q\in\mathbb{N},\quad\Big|\xi-\frac{a}{q}\Big|\leq\frac{1}{q^{2}}.

Then for every
\varepsilon>0
there is a constant
C_{\varepsilon}>0
such that for every

X\in\mathbb{N}

(3.6)

\displaystyle|f_{X}(\xi)|\leq C_{\varepsilon}X^{1+\varepsilon}\bigg(\frac{1}{q}+\frac{1}{X}+\frac{q}{X^{k}}\bigg)^{\sigma(k)}.

Proof.

When
k\geq 3
this is proved in
[
2
, Theorem 5]
. When
k=2
we have
2^{1-k}=\sigma(k),
hence the statement follows from the classical Weyl inequality, see e.g.
[
13
, Lemma 2.4]
.
∎

As a consequence of Lemma
3.5
we obtain an estimate for
f_{X}(\xi)
on minor arcs.

Corollary 3.7
.

Let
N\in\mathbb{N}
and assume that
\xi\in\mathfrak{m}_{X_{N}}.
Then for
every
\varepsilon>0
there is a constant
C_{\varepsilon}>0
such that

(3.8)

\displaystyle|f_{X_{N}}(\xi)|\leq C_{\varepsilon}X_{N}^{1+\varepsilon-\alpha\sigma(k)}.

Proof.

If
\xi\in\mathfrak{m}_{X_{N}}
then by Dirichlet’s pigeonhole principle one can always find
X_{N}^{\alpha}<q\leq X_{N}^{k-\alpha}
and
1\leq a\leq q
such that
(a,q)=1
and

\Big|\xi-\frac{a}{q}\Big|\leq\frac{1}{qX_{N}^{k-\alpha}}\leq\frac{1}{q^{2}}.

Hence, by applying Lemma
3.5
we obtain that, for every
\varepsilon>0,
one has

\left|f_{X_{N}}(\xi)\right|\lesssim_{\varepsilon}X_{N}^{1+\varepsilon}\bigg(\frac{1}{q}+\frac{1}{X_{N}}+\frac{q}{X_{N}^{k}}\bigg)^{\sigma(k)}\lesssim X_{N}^{1+\varepsilon}\big(X_{N}^{-\alpha}+X_{N}^{-1}+X_{N}^{-\alpha}\big)^{\sigma(k)}\lesssim X_{N}^{1+\varepsilon-\alpha\sigma(k)}.

∎

The following estimate for the Gauss sums can be found in
[
4
]
.

Lemma 3.9

(
[
4
, Lemma 6.4]
)
.

Assume that
1\leq a\leq q
satisfies
(a,q)=1.
Then for
every
k\geq 2
there is a constant
C_{k}>0
such that

(3.10)

\displaystyle\big|G(a/q)\big|\leq C_{k}q^{-1/k}.

Finally, we will need an improved version of the classical Hua’s lemma (see
[
4
, Lemma 3.2]
) proved by Bourgain.

Lemma 3.11

(
[
2
, Theorem 10]
)
.

For every
X\in\mathbb{N}
and every
\varepsilon>0
there is a constant
C_{\varepsilon}>0
such that for each
k\in\mathbb{N}
we have

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\big|f_{X}(\xi)\big|^{k(k+1)}{\rm d}\xi\leq C_{\varepsilon}X^{k^{2}+\varepsilon}.

Having stated Lemmas
3.5
and
3.11
we now proceed with the proof of Proposition
3.3
.

Proof of Proposition
3.3
.

Let us remind that
X_{N}={\lfloor{N^{1/k}}\rfloor}
. By applying Lemmas
3.7
and
3.11
, for any
\varepsilon>0
, one obtains

\displaystyle\Big|\int_{\mathfrak{m}_{X_{N}}}\big(f_{X_{N}}(\xi)\big)^{d}\boldsymbol{e}\left(-\xi N\right){\rm d}\xi\Big|

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{\xi\in\mathfrak{m}_{X_{N}}}\big|f_{X_{N}}(\xi)\big|^{d-k(k+1)}\int_{0}^{1}\big|f_{X_{N}}(\xi)\big|^{k(k+1)}{\rm d}\xi

\displaystyle\lesssim_{k,\varepsilon}C_{\varepsilon}^{d}X_{N}^{(1-\alpha\sigma(k)+\varepsilon)(d-k(k+1))}X_{N}^{k^{2}+\varepsilon}\lesssim C_{\varepsilon}^{d}N^{\frac{d}{k}-1-\frac{\gamma}{k}},

where
\gamma=(\alpha\sigma(k)-\varepsilon)(d-k(k+1))-\varepsilon
is non-negative provided that we take
\varepsilon<\alpha\sigma(k)(d-k(k+1))/(1+d-k(k+1))
. Hence we just proved that

(3.12)

|m_{k}(N)|\lesssim_{k,\varepsilon}C_{\varepsilon}^{d}N^{\frac{d}{k}-1-\frac{\gamma}{k}}.

Since one has
\Gamma\left(1+\frac{1}{k}\right)^{d}\simeq_{k}^{d}1
and
\Gamma\left(\frac{d}{k}\right)\simeq_{k}^{d}d^{d/k}
we see that

(3.13)

\frac{\Gamma\left(1+\frac{1}{k}\right)^{d}}{\Gamma\left(\frac{d}{k}\right)}\simeq_{k}^{d}d^{-d/k}.

If
d\geq 2^{k+1}
and
\varepsilon=\sigma(k)\alpha/20
then
\gamma\geq\sigma(k)\alpha d/5
. Hence, by applying (
3.12
) we get

|m_{k}(N)|\lesssim_{k}^{d}N^{-d\frac{\sigma(k)\alpha}{5k}}N^{d/k-1}.

In view of (
3.13
) and Proposition
3.14
we can write

\displaystyle|m_{k}(N)|\Big(\mathfrak{S}(N)\frac{\Gamma\left(1+\frac{1}{k}\right)^{d}}{\Gamma\left(\frac{d}{k}\right)}N^{d/k-1}\Big)^{-1}\lesssim_{k}(C_{k}d^{1/k}N^{-\frac{\sigma(k)\alpha}{5k}})^{d}.

Therefore, if we take
N\geq C_{k}d^{5k^{2}/\alpha}
for an appropriately large
C_{k}>0
we reach

|m_{k}(N)|\Big(\mathfrak{S}(N)\frac{\Gamma\left(1+\frac{1}{k}\right)^{d}}{\Gamma\left(\frac{d}{k}\right)}N^{d/k-1}\Big)^{-1}=o(1).

This completes the proof of Proposition
3.3
.
∎

The next proposition asserts us that the singular series is uniformly bounded, up to multiplicative bounds, with respect to
N\in\mathbb{N}
and
d\geq 2^{k+1}
.

Proposition 3.14
.

For any
k\geq 2
and
d\geq 2^{k+1}
there exists constants
a_{k},A_{k}>0
such that

(3.15)

a_{k}^{d}\leq\mathfrak{S}(N)\leq A_{k}.

Proof.

At first we note that for any
a/q
with
(a,q)=1
we have
|G(a/q)|\leq 1
. Hence, by Corollary
3.9
the fact that
d\geq 2^{k+1}
we obtain the estimate

(3.16)

\Big|\sum_{\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{a=1}{(a, q)=1}}^{q}G(a/q)^{d}\boldsymbol{e}\left(-Na/q\right)\Big|\leq\sum_{\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{a=1}{(a, q)=1}}^{q}|G(a/q)|^{2^{k+1}}\lesssim_{k}q^{1-(2^{k+1})/k}\lesssim q^{-3/2}.

Consequently, for
d\geq 2^{k+1}
, we get

|\mathfrak{S}(N)|\leq\sum_{q=1}^{\infty}\Big|\sum_{\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{a=1}{(a, q)=1}}^{q}G(a/q)^{d}\boldsymbol{e}\left(-Na/q\right)\Big|\lesssim_{k}\sum_{q=1}^{\infty}q^{-3/2}\lesssim_{k}1.

Since
\mathfrak{S}(N)>0
we obtain the upper bound for
\mathfrak{S}(N)
. To prove the lower bound let us define

\chi_{N}(p):=\sum_{h=0}^{\infty}\sum_{\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{a=1}{(a, p^h)=1}}^{p^{h}}G(a/p^{h})^{d}\boldsymbol{e}\left(-Na/p^{h}\right),\quad p\in\mathbb{P}.

It is known, see
[
12
, Lemma 5.7]
, that

(3.17)

\mathfrak{G}(N)=\prod_{p\in\mathbb{P}}\chi_{N}(p).

Moreover, by
[
12
, Lemma 5.10]
there is a natural number
\gamma_{k}
which depends only on
k\in\mathbb{N}
such that

(3.18)

\chi_{N}(p)\geq p^{\gamma_{k}(1-d)}>0,

provided that
d\geq 2^{k+1}
. By following the proof of
[
12
, Lemma 5.7, eq. (5.14)]
and using the estimate (
3.16
) one can prove that there is
p_{0}=p_{0}(k)
such that

1/2\leq\prod_{p\geq p_{0}}\chi_{N}(p)\leq 3/2.

Hence, by (
3.17
) and (
3.18
) we can estimate

\mathfrak{G}(N)\geq 1/2\prod_{p\leq p_{0}}\chi_{N}(p)\geq 1/2\prod_{p\leq p_{0}}p^{\gamma_{k}(1-d)}\geq 1/2^{d}\prod_{p\leq p_{0}}p^{-\gamma_{k}d}=:a_{k}^{d}>0.

∎

Remark 3.19
.

The condition
d\geq 2^{k+1}
may be weakened to
d\geq 2k+1
when
k
is odd and
d\geq 4k
when
k
is even. This threshold is related to the estimate (
3.18
) which holds for
d\geq 2k+1
(
k
odd) and
d\geq 4k
(
k
even). It is interesting whenever the singular series may be bounded independently of the dimension
d
. In
[
11
, Inequality (3.27)]
the authors were able to prove that for
k=2
and
d\geq 16
we have

\frac{1}{2}\leq\mathfrak{G}(N)\leq\frac{3}{2}.

Their argument heavily relies on the fact that
k=2
.

3.2.
Major arc estimate

Now we need to estimate the contribution from the major arc term
M_{k}(N)
. Recall that for
1\leq q\leq X_{N}^{\alpha}
we constructed the major arcs
\mathfrak{M}_{X_{N}}(a/q)
for some fixed
\alpha\in(0,1/4)
which will be adjusted later.

Proposition 3.20
.

For each
k\in\mathbb{N}
there exists a constant
C_{k,\alpha}>0
such that, for
d\geq 2^{k+1}
and
N\geq C_{k,\alpha}d^{d/\delta}
, with some
0<\delta<\min\{1-4\alpha,\alpha\}
, we have

(3.21)

M_{k}(N)=\mathfrak{S}(N)\frac{\Gamma\left(1+\frac{1}{k}\right)^{d}}{\Gamma\left(\frac{d}{k}\right)}N^{d/k-1}(1+o(1)),

where the implicit constants in the symbol
o(1)
are independent of the dimension
d.

The proof of Proposition
3.20
is based on several approximations of the major arc term
M_{k}(N).
At first, denote by

(3.22)

v(\theta):=\int_{0}^{X_{N}}\boldsymbol{e}\left(\theta z^{k}\right){\rm d}z,\quad\theta\in\mathbb{R}

the continuous counterpart of
f_{X_{N}}(\theta)
and let

(3.23)

\mathcal{A}^{1}(N):=\sum_{1\leq q\leq X_{N}^{\alpha}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a=1\\
(a,q)=1\end{subarray}}^{q}G(a/q)^{d}\int_{\mathfrak{M}_{X_{N}}(a/q)}\big(v(\xi-a/q)\big)^{d}\boldsymbol{e}\left(-\xi N\right){\rm d}\xi,\quad N\in\mathbb{N}

be our first approximation of
M_{k}(N).
In order to approximate
f_{X_{N}}(\theta)
by its continuous counterpart
v(\theta)
we make use of the following approximation lemma due to van der Corput.

Lemma 3.24

(
[
4
, Lemma 9.1]
)
.

Suppose
g(x)
is a real function which is twice differentiable for
A\leq x\leq B
with
A,B\in\mathbb{R}
. Suppose further that, in this interval, one has

0\leq g^{\prime}(x)\leq\frac{1}{2},\quad g^{\prime\prime}(x)\geq 0.

Then

(3.25)

\sum_{A\leq n\leq B}\boldsymbol{e}\left(g(n)\right)=\int_{A}^{B}\boldsymbol{e}\left(g(x)\right){\rm d}x+\mathcal{O}(1).

Lemma 3.26
.

For each
k\in\mathbb{N}
there exists a constant
C_{k}>0
such that, for
d\in\mathbb{N}
and
N\geq C_{k}d^{d/\delta}
, with
0<\delta<1-4\alpha
, we have

(3.27)

M_{k}(N)-\mathcal{A}^{1}(N)=\mathfrak{S}(N)\frac{\Gamma\left(1+\frac{1}{k}\right)^{d}}{\Gamma\left(\frac{d}{k}\right)}N^{d/k-1}\cdot o(1),

where the implicit constants in the symbol
o(1)
are independent of the dimension
d.

Proof.

Fix a fraction
a/q
with
1\leq q\leq X_{N}^{\alpha}
and
a\leq q
such that
(a,q)=1
. Consider the corresponding major arc
\mathfrak{M}_{X_{N}}(a/q)
. Let us collect together those values of
n
in the sum defining
f_{X_{N}}(\xi)
which are in the same residue class and write

(3.28)

f_{X_{N}}(\xi)=\sum_{r=1}^{q}\boldsymbol{e}\left(ar^{k}/q\right)\sum_{-\frac{r}{q}<n\leq\frac{X_{N}-r}{q}}\boldsymbol{e}\left((\xi-a/q)(qn+r)^{k}\right).

For
x\in(-q^{-1}r,q^{-1}(X_{N}-r)]
we denote

g(x):=(\xi-a/q)(qx+r)^{k}.

If
\xi-a/q\geq 0
then for
N\geq(2k)^{2k}>(2k)^{k/(1-2\alpha)}
one has

0\leq g^{\prime}(x)=k(\xi-a/q)q(qx+r)^{k-1}\leq 1/2

since
|\xi-a/q|\leq X_{N}^{-k+\alpha}
and
qx+r\leq X_{N}
. Moreover we have
g^{\prime\prime}(x)>0
. Hence, we may apply Lemma
3.24
to the inner sum in (
3.28
). In the case when
\xi-a/q<0
we use Lemma
3.24
to the complex conjugate sum of the inner sum. Consequently, by noting that
q\leq X_{N}^{\alpha}
we conclude that

f_{X_{N}}(\xi)=\sum_{r=1}^{q}\boldsymbol{e}\left(ar^{k}/q\right)\int_{-r/q}^{(X_{N}-r)/q}\boldsymbol{e}\left((\xi-a/q)(qx+r)^{k}\right){\rm d}x+\mathcal{O}(X_{N}^{\alpha}),\quad\xi\in\mathfrak{M}_{X_{N}}(a/q).

If we change variable
qx+r=y
in the above integral we obtain

f_{X_{N}}(\xi)=G(a/q)v(\xi-a/q)+\mathcal{O}(X_{N}^{\alpha}),\quad\xi\in\mathfrak{M}_{X_{N}}(a/q).

Now, by using the identity
x^{d}-y^{d}=(x-y)(x^{d-1}+x^{d-2}y+\cdots+xy^{d-2}+y^{d-1})
and the obvious bounds
|f_{X_{N}}(\xi)|\leq X_{N}
and
|G(a/q)\cdot v(\xi-\frac{a}{q})|\leq X_{N}
we get

(3.29)

\Big|\big(f_{X}(\xi)\big)^{d}-\big(G(a/q)\cdot v(\xi-a/q)\big)^{d}\Big|\lesssim d\cdot X_{N}^{\alpha+d-1},\qquad\xi\in\mathfrak{M}_{X_{N}}(a/q).

Using (
3.29
) and recalling definitions (
3.2
) and (
3.23
) we estimate

\displaystyle|M_{k}(N)-\mathcal{A}^{1}(N)|\lesssim d\,X_{N}^{\alpha+d-1}\cdot\sum_{1\leq q\leq X_{N}^{\alpha}}\sum_{\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{a=1}{(a, q)=1}}^{q}\left|\mathfrak{M}_{X_{N}}(a/q)\right|\lesssim d\,X_{N}^{d-k+4\alpha-1}.

Hence we see that

(3.30)

N^{-d/k+1}|M_{k}(N)-\mathcal{A}^{1}(N)|\lesssim d\cdot X_{N}^{4\alpha-1},

and using (
3.13
) we get

\displaystyle\mathfrak{S}(N)^{-1}\frac{\Gamma\left(\frac{d}{k}\right)}{\Gamma\left(1+\frac{1}{k}\right)^{d}}N^{-d/k+1}|M_{k}(N)-\mathcal{A}^{1}(N)|\lesssim_{k}^{d}d^{d/k}\cdot X_{N}^{4\alpha-1}.

Therefore, taking
0<\delta<1-4\alpha
we obtain for
X_{N}\geq d^{d/(k\delta)}
the estimate

\mathfrak{S}(N)^{-1}\frac{\Gamma\left(\frac{d}{k}\right)}{\Gamma\left(1+\frac{1}{k}\right)^{d}}N^{-d/k+1}|M_{k}(N)-\mathcal{A}^{1}(N)|\lesssim_{k}^{d}X_{N}^{4\alpha-1+\delta}.

The above justifies (
3.27
) and completes the proof of Lemma
3.26
.
∎

Remark 3.31
.

The threshold
N\geq C_{k}d^{d/\delta}
appears in Lemma
3.26
because of the method in which we estimate the difference between the exponential sum and the exponential integral. The proof is based on the simple one-dimensional estimate (
3.29
) which translates into the decay of order
dX_{N}^{4\alpha-1}
in (
3.30
). The same issue appears if we consider
(f_{X_{N}}(\xi))^{d}
as a sum over
\mathbb{N}^{d}
and use mean value theorem to estimate the difference between the sum and the integral. In order to obtain a polynomial threshold of the form
N>C_{k}d^{ck^{j}},
for some
j\in\mathbb{N}
we would need a decay in (
3.30
) of an order
X_{N}^{-d\gamma},
where
\gamma
is a non-negative constant. This issue seems to persist whenever the method employed to solve the Waring problem uses a one-dimensional error term bound or the mean value theorem. It is worth mentioning that this is an approach commonly used in the literature. For instance the threshold
N\geq C_{k}d^{d/\delta}
also appears if one applies methods developed by Magyar
[
10
]
. This is because of
[
10
, Lemma 4]
which uses a mean value theorem at the beginning of p. 929. Similarly, the higher order asymptotics formula in Waring’s problem proved recently by Vaughan and Wooley
[
14
]
makes use of a one-dimensional error term bound in
[
14
, eq. (3.3), (3.4)]
. This then translates to a requirement that
N\geq C_{k}d^{d/\delta}
in order to have a dimension-free control on the error bound in
[
14
, Lemma 3.1]
. On the other hand in the proof of
[
11
, Theorem 3.1, eq. 3.17]
the authors do not use such a reduction to a one-dimensional estimate. They were able to resort explicitly to the Poisson summation formula in the case
k=2.
This leads to a much lower threshold for
k=2
. Whether this is possible for general
k
is not clear to us.

M_{k}(N)
is based on a replacement of the range of integration in

We let

For proving Lemma
3.35

Lemma 3.33

For each

For each

Proof.

By Lemma

Consequently, summing over the pairs

3.9

\displaystyle|\mathcal{A}^{1}(N)-\mathcal{A}^{2}(N)|

\displaystyle\mathfrak{S}(N)^{-1}\frac{\Gamma\left(\frac{d}{k}\right)}{\Gamma\left(1+\frac{1}{k}\right)^{d}}N^{-d/k+1}|\mathcal{A}^{1}(N)-\mathcal{A}^{2}(N)|

Now, taking

\displaystyle\mathfrak{S}(N)^{-1}\frac{\Gamma\left(\frac{d}{k}\right)}{\Gamma\left(1+\frac{1}{k}\right)^{d}}N^{-d/k+1}|\mathcal{A}^{1}(N)-\mathcal{A}^{2}(N)|=o(1).

3.3.
Comments and questions

Let us state some comments and questions concerning our results.

1.

By choosing an appropriate small
\alpha\in(0,1/4)
we could achieve in Lemma
3.26
a better threshold. Namely, we would get that it is enough to take
N\geq C_{k}d^{(1+\delta)d}
for some
C_{k}>0
and small
\delta>0
. This would imply that for a large
k\in\mathbb{N}
and
d\geq 2^{k+1}
it is enough to take
N\geq C_{k}d^{(1+\delta)d}
in Theorem
1.4
. It is still a growth of order
d^{d}\,
instead of the expected by us polynomial growth
d^{k^{j}}
, for some
j\in\mathbb{N}
. It seems that our approximation method in Lemma
3.26
is not good enough to achieve a lower order of the threshold for
N
. In the case of
k=2
we know that the formula (
1.5
) holds for
d\geq 16
and
N\gtrsim d^{3}
. Is that the optimal threshold for
N
? What about the other values of
k
?

2.

Denote by
G_{\rm multi}(k)
the smallest integer such that for
d\geq G_{\rm multi}(k)
the multiplicative formula (
1.5
) holds. What is the optimal value of
G_{\rm multi}(k)
? Our theorem provides the bound
G_{\rm multi}(k)\leq 2^{k+1}
with the threshold
N\gtrsim_{k}d^{20d}
. By a careful analysis of our proof one can note that for large
k\in\mathbb{N}
it is enough to take
G_{\rm multi}(k)\leq k(k+1)
for
N\gtrsim_{k}d^{d^{2}}
. Is that trade-off between
G_{\rm multi}(k)
and the threshold for
N
necessary? In the case of the usual additive formula (
1.3
) it is known, see
[
2
, eq. (6.16)]
, that for large
k\in\mathbb{N}
it holds for
d\geq G_{\rm add}(k)
with

G_{\rm add}(k)\leq k^{2}-k+\mathcal{O}(k).

What is the relation between
G_{\rm multi}(k)
and
G_{\rm add}(k)
? It is natural to expect that
G_{\rm add}(k)\leq G_{\rm multi}(k)
since we need some place to get rid of the dependence on the dimension. Does the equality
G_{\rm add}(k)=G_{\rm multi}(k)
hold?

3.

Let us consider the generalized Waring problem

(3.44)

c_{1}n_{1}^{k}+c_{2}n_{2}^{k}+\ldots+c_{d}n_{d}^{k}=N,

where
c_{1},\ldots,c_{s}
are given positive integers and
n_{1},\ldots,n_{d}
are arbitrary positive integers. We assume that
c_{1},\ldots,c_{s}
do not all have a common factor greater than
1
. Additionally, in order to ensure solvability, we assume that the congruence

c_{1}n_{1}^{k}+c_{2}n_{2}^{k}+\ldots+c_{d}n_{d}^{k}\equiv N\quad(\rm{mod}\,\,p^{h})

has a solution for every
p\in\mathbb{P}
and large
h\in\mathbb{N}
. For more details see
[
4
, Chapter 7]
or
[
18
, Chapter 11 and 12]
. Denote by
c=(c_{1},c_{2},\ldots,c_{d})
and
\boldsymbol{c}=\max_{1\leq i\leq d}c_{i}
. The singular series for the generalized Waring problem (
3.44
) is given by

\mathfrak{S}_{c}(N):=\sum_{q=1}^{\infty}\sum_{\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{a=1}{(a, q)=1}}^{q}\prod_{i=1}^{d}G(c_{i}a/q)\boldsymbol{e}\left(-Na/q\right).

Let
r_{k}^{c}(N)
denote the number of solutions of the equation (
3.44
). By following the presented approach and by using some facts from
[
18
, Chapter 11 and 12]
one can prove the following.

Theorem 3.45
.

For each
k\in\mathbb{N}
with
k\geq 2
there exists a constant
C_{\boldsymbol{c},k}>0
such that, for
d\geq 2^{k+1}
and
N\geq C_{\boldsymbol{c},k}d^{20d}
, we have

(3.46)

r_{k}^{c}(N)=\frac{\mathfrak{S}_{c}(N)}{(c_{1}c_{2}\cdots c_{d})^{1/k}}\frac{\Gamma\left(1+\frac{1}{k}\right)^{d}}{\Gamma\left(\frac{d}{k}\right)}N^{d/k-1}(1+o(1)),

where the implicit constants in the symbol
o(1)
depend only on
\boldsymbol{c}
and
k
.
