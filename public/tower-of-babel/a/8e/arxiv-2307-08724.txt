On hardness of computing analytic Brouwer degree
Somnath Chakraborty

Abstract
. We prove that counting the analytic Brouwer degree of rational coefficient polynomial maps in Map ⁡ ( ℂ d , ℂ d ) \operatorname{Map}(\mathbb{C}^{d},\mathbb{C}^{d}) — presented in degree-coefficient form — is hard for the complexity class ♯ ​ P \operatorname{\sharp P} , in the following sense: if there is a randomized polynomial time algorithm that counts the Brouwer degree correctly for a good fraction of all input instances (with coefficients of bounded height where the bound is an input to the algorithm), then P ♯ ​ P = BPP \operatorname{P}^{\operatorname{\sharp P}}=\operatorname{BPP} .



1.
Introduction

Computational problems arising from algebraic geometry and
algebraic topology have been pivotal in theoretical
advancement of complexity theory, which — in turn
— shaped up feasibility of efficient algorithm design.
A classic example is
Sperner’s lemma
in
[
17
]

— a combinatorial version of Brouwer’s fixed point theorem,
that has found widespread application in algorithm design
and combinatorics. We recall that Brouwer’s fixed point theorem
states that a self map of the hypersphere
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}

must have a fixed point, posing an obstruction to
extendability of the identity map of
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}

to the disk
\mathbb{B}^{d}
. Note that, if
\phi:\mathbb{B}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
extends the identity map

\operatorname{id}_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}
continuously, then
a topological Brouwer degree (
definition

3.2
) consideration of the
induced maps of homology groups in

\textstyle{\operatorname{H}^{d-1}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1})\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\iota^{\star}}
\scriptstyle{id_{\star}}
\textstyle{\operatorname{H}^{d-1}(\mathbb{B}^{d})\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\phi^{\star}}
\textstyle{\operatorname{H}^{d-1}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1})}

immediately results in a contradiction.
In fact, more generally, for any given smooth
oriented manifolds
\operatorname{M},\operatorname{N}

with compatibly oriented boundary
\partial\operatorname{M}
, a smooth map
\phi:\partial\operatorname{M}\rightarrow\operatorname{N}
is extendable
to
M
if and only if
\deg_{\boldsymbol{n}}(\phi)=0
for every regular (
definition

3.1
)
value
\boldsymbol{n}\in\operatorname{N}

[
11
, theorem 5.1.8]
.
This brings to the forefront the question of
complexity of computing the Brouwer degree at regular
values of maps between ‘nice’ manifolds.

The algorithmic aspects of extendability of a map
from a subdomain to the entire domain has traditionally
been subject to numerous bodies of work.
In a discrete setting, an analogous
problem dealing with partial function
extension — for a certain restricted class of functions
— has a long thread of work in theoretical
computer science, where this connects to areas as diverse
as property testing and quantum computation;
we refer to
[
15
]

for a detailed overview of work related to extension
of partial function in discrete domain.
Recent work of
[
18
]

shows that the extendability question becomes
undecidable when one goes to the world of
continuous functions — defined as a simplicial
map between finite simplicial complexes or, even more
generally, simplicial sets. The idea in
[
18
]

revolves around
Hilbert’s tenth problem
—
dealing with existence of integral solution to a given
polynomial map.

As stated above, a standard result in Brouwer degree theory
relates the existence of zeros and nonvanishing
of Brouwer degree of maps
[
7
, corollary 2.2.12]
.
Note here that the results of
[
18
]
does not
imply undecidability of Brouwer degree for simplicial maps between
simplicial complexes, as the undecidability in
[
18
]

might be coming from the fact that Brouwer
degree of the relevant map has to be checked
for being zero at every regular value. Recently,

[
9
]
devised an “effective” algorithm
for computing Brouwer degrees of maps between
cubical sets in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
; however, the complexity
of the algorithm in
[
9
]
remained open.

In this article we prove that evaluating the
analytic Brouwer degree of rational coefficient
polynomial maps in
\operatorname{Map}(\mathbb{C}^{d},\mathbb{C}^{d})
— presented in degree-coefficient form
— is hard for the complexity class

\operatorname{\sharp P}
, in that (
a la

[
4
]
)
if there is a randomized polynomial time
algorithm that counts the Brouwer degree correctly
for a good fraction of all input instances, then

\operatorname{P}^{\operatorname{\sharp P}}=\operatorname{BPP}
. In essence, the main idea
evolves around showing that — under the
assumption that
\boldsymbol{0}
is a regular
value of a polynomial map
\boldsymbol{\phi}
—
the Brouwer degree coincides with the number of complex
zeros of
\boldsymbol{\phi}
; we discuss this in more detail
in
section

1.1
.

Our approach to the problem of evaluating
analytic Brouwer degree and its complexity
via the analogous aspects of
counting zeros of polynomial maps also connects
to the literature on zero counting and algorithm to
find zeros of polynomial maps — mainly via
some incarnation of homotopy method (
[
12
]
)
— a theme previously studied in the real computational model
of Blum-Shub-Smale in
[
16
]
, via Bezout’s theorem.
However, it must be emphasized that a
straightforward application of
Bezout’s theorem to affine polynomial maps
with integral and/or rational coeffcients
does not yield the desired results because

•

the count via Bezout’s theorem includes the
zeros “at infinity”, with its multiplicity, for the
homegenized polynomials, and these do not correspond
to actual affine zeros;

•

the ’generic’ polynomial systems in the sense
of Bezout’s theorem (or any ‘rational enough’ sense, for
that matter) does not
a priori
include
a good chunk of rational/integer polynomial systems.

In fact, the investigation in

[
16
]
is also about (counting average number
of real zeros of) homogenized polynomials
(having fixed sets of indices and random coefficients)
over complex field. On the other hand, the identification
of zero counts to the mixed volume —
a la

theorem of Bernstein-Kushnirenko-Khovanski —
formulates the question
of identifying generic polynomial system to applying
Hilbert’s nullstellensatz to a (potentially infinite) set of
polynomial systems of “initial terms”;
see, for example,
[
12
]
for more details.

We circumvent this obstruction by realizing that the
polynomial systems in
\operatorname{Map}(\mathbb{C}^{d},\mathbb{C}^{d})
consisting of those having
\boldsymbol{0}

as a regular value is a subset of a generic
d
-system
of
d
-variate polynomials, and — for every fixed sets
of indices — the supported polynomial systems with
random Gaussian (in fact, any absolutely continuous random)
coefficients have
\boldsymbol{0}
as a regular value;
an easy application of pigeon-box principle
then allows control over the choice of coefficients
via
lemma

5.4
. We note that, deciding if a given
polynomial map has
\boldsymbol{0}
as regular value
is an ideal membership question which is in general
EXPSPACE-complete; see
[
14
]
for more details.

To the best of our knowledge, the computational
approach to counting zeros of polynomial maps
progressed through two connected avenues.
On the one hand, several symbolic algorithms
have been developed for computing the zeros of rational
polynomial maps (for example, see
[
5
]
);
in most of the cases, their run times depend on factors such
as degree of intermediate varieties. Parallel to this,
there are approaches to count (or bound) the
(average) number of (real) zeros of polynomial
maps with fixed sets of supports and random coefficients;
see, for example
[
6
, theorem 5.5]
.
As a corollary of our main result, we can show

\operatorname{\sharp P}
-hardness of counting
the exact number of real zeros of polynomial maps
(in even dimensions).

We now present our main theorems: these will be proved in

section

6
below, building on technical results of

section

5
.

Main Theorem

(
theorem

6.2
)
.

Suppose
\mathscr{P}
is an algorithm that solves a

\operatorname{\sharp P}
-complete problem. Then there
is an algorithm that runs in time that is oracle-polynomial
in the input size for solving the following problem:

instance:

given integer
d>0
and polynomials

\phi_{1},\cdots,\phi_{d}\in\mathbb{Q}_{\geq 0}[\iota][\boldsymbol{z}]

such that

–

\phi_{j}(\boldsymbol{0})\neq\boldsymbol{0}

for each

;

–

\boldsymbol{0}
is a regular value of
\boldsymbol{\phi}=(\phi_{1},\dots,\phi_{d})
;

task:

compute the value of
\deg({\boldsymbol{\phi}})
.

Main Theorem

(
theorem

6.3
)
.

Suppose
\mathscr{P}
is a (randomized) polynomial time
algorithm that counts the Brouwer degree of
of
d
-variate rational polynomial maps with respect to
\boldsymbol{0}
. Then
\operatorname{P}^{\operatorname{\sharp P}}=\operatorname{BPP}
.

1.1.
Brief outline of the main steps

We show that — if
\boldsymbol{0}
is a regular
value of a polynomial map
\boldsymbol{\phi}
—
then the analytic Brouwer degree coincides
with the number of complex zeros of
\boldsymbol{\phi}
. Next, polynomial systems in
\operatorname{Map}(\mathbb{C}^{d},\mathbb{C}^{d})
consisting of those having
\boldsymbol{0}

as a critical value is a low-dimensional
subset of the
d
-system of
d
-variate
polynomials, and — for every fixed sets
of indices — the supported polynomial systems with
random Gaussian (in fact, any absolutely continuous random)
coefficients have
\boldsymbol{0}
as a regular value.
Furthermore, when the component
polynomials are with fixed support but random Gaussian
coefficients,
\boldsymbol{0}
becomes a regular
value with probability 1. Moreover, we show that
this regularity condition remains true even when
the randomness is only on the constant coefficients,
which allows us to have good fraction of polynomials
with coefficients of appropriately bounded height
to have
\boldsymbol{0}
as regular value. This
paves a way to apply the results of
[
8
]

to convert the complexity question of zero-counting to that of
complexity of mixed volume of integer polytopes.

2.
Preliminaries: polynomials and root counting

Let
\mathbb{C}
be the complex field, and for
integer
d>1
, let
\boldsymbol{z}
denote the

d
-dimensional variable
\boldsymbol{z}\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\cdot$ }\hss}\raisebox{-1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}}=(z_{1},\cdots,z_{d})
. Let
\mathbb{C}[\boldsymbol{z}]

be the ring of
d
-variate polynomials with
complex coefficients.

For
\phi_{1},\dots,\phi_{k}\in\mathbb{C}[\boldsymbol{z}]
,
the zero-set

\mathbb{Z}_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\cdot$ }\hss}\raisebox{-1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}}=\{\boldsymbol{z}\in\mathbb{C}^{d}:\phi_{1}(\boldsymbol{z})=\cdots=\phi_{k}(\boldsymbol{z})=\boldsymbol{0}\}

is the affine
algebraic set of the ideal
(\phi_{1},\dots,\phi_{k})
.
The affine algebraic sets are precisely the closed subsets
of the
Zariski topology
of
\mathbb{C}^{d}
.
We write the affine algebraic set
\mathbb{C}^{d}

interchangably as
\mathbb{A}_{\mathbb{C}}^{d}
.
By
[
10
, proposition 1.5]
,
any affine algebraic set can always be expressed as finite
union of irreducible affine algebraic subsets —
i.e.
,
those algebraic subsets that can not be expressed as
union of two nonempty proper closed subsets.
The dimension of an affine algebraic subset is the
length of the longest strictly ascending chain of
irreducible closed subsets.

Remark 1
.

An application of
Krull’s Hauptidealsatz

shows that the dimension of the algebraic set

\mathbb{Z}_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}
is at least
d-k
;
for more details, see
[
10
, proposition 1.13]
.

Definition 2.1
.

For a polynomial
\phi\in\mathbb{C}[\boldsymbol{z}]
,
the support of
\phi
is the subset
S\subseteq\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{d}
such that

\phi(\boldsymbol{z})=\sum_{\boldsymbol{n}\in S}c_{\boldsymbol{n}}\boldsymbol{z}^{\boldsymbol{n}}

for some complex
c_{\boldsymbol{n}}
,

\boldsymbol{n}\in S
; we write
\operatorname{supp}(\phi)\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\cdot$ }\hss}\raisebox{-1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}}=S
. The convex hull
\mathscr{Q}\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\cdot$ }\hss}\raisebox{-1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}}=\operatorname{conv-hull}(S)
is the Newton polytope
of the polynomial
\phi
.

Let
\mathcal{A}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{A}_{d}\subseteq\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{d}
be non-empty finite
subsets — each of which contains the origin.
Let
\boldsymbol{\phi}_{1},\dots,\boldsymbol{\phi}_{d}
be the polynomials

\phi_{j}(\boldsymbol{z})\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\cdot$ }\hss}\raisebox{-1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}}=\sum_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}\in\mathcal{A}_{j}}c_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}{\boldsymbol{z}}^{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}

where
(c_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}})_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}\in\mathcal{A}_{1}\cup\dots\mathcal{A}_{d}}
is a vector of complex coefficients.
Thus,
\mathscr{Q}_{j}
is the Newton
polytope of
\phi_{j}
for each
j\in[d]
.

The zeros of polynomials are intimately connected to
their respective Newton polytopes. For example, in
one-dimension, the Newton polytope of a polynomial
is just a line segment joining
0
and the degree of
the polynomial, and its length is precisely the number
of zeros of the polynomial, counting multiplicities.

To deal with the multidimensional polynomial maps

\boldsymbol{\phi}\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\cdot$ }\hss}\raisebox{-1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}}=(\phi_{1},\dots,\phi_{d})
in

\operatorname{Map}(\mathbb{C}^{d},\mathbb{C}^{d})
, we
need the notion of mixed volume.

Definition 2.2

(mixed volume)
.

Let
\mathscr{Q}_{1},\dots,\mathscr{Q}_{d}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
be the convex hulls
of
\mathcal{A}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{A}_{d}
,
respectively. The (normalized) mixed
volume
\operatorname{MV}(\mathcal{A}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{A}_{d})
is defined as follows:

\operatorname{MV}(\mathcal{A}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{A}_{d})\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\cdot$ }\hss}\raisebox{-1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}}=d!~\frac{\partial^{d}}{\partial t_{1}\cdots\partial t_{d}}\operatorname{vol}(t_{1}\mathscr{Q}_{1}+\dots+t_{d}\mathscr{Q}_{d})\Bigg\rvert_{t_{1}=\cdots=t_{d}=0}\,.

Here
\operatorname{vol}(\cdot)

denotes the standard Lebesgue measure in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

The mixed volume is a coordinate-wise translation
invariant measure on the cellular
\sigma
-algebra

\underbrace{\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\times\cdots\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}_{d}\,,

where
\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
is the Borel

\sigma
-algebra on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

The following theorem of Bernstein connects the zero-count
of a system of polynomials to the mixed
volume of corresponding system of Newton polytopes.

Fact 2.3

(Bernstein-Koushnirenko-Khovanski,
[
3
,
13
]
)
.

Let
\mathcal{A}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{A}_{d}\subseteq\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{d}
be non-empty finite
subsets — each of which contains the origin.
For a generic polynomial system
\boldsymbol{\phi}\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\cdot$ }\hss}\raisebox{-1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}}=(\phi_{1},\dots,\phi_{d})
satisfying
\operatorname{supp}(\phi_{j})=\mathcal{A}_{j}
for each
j\in[d]
,
the equality

\operatorname{MV}(\mathcal{A}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{A}_{d})=|\mathbb{Z}_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}|

holds.

3.
Analytic Brouwer degree

Let
\boldsymbol{\phi}\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\cdot$ }\hss}\raisebox{-1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}}=(\phi_{1},\dots,\phi_{d})
be
a polynomial function defined on
\mathbb{C}^{d}
. Consider the
standard identification
\mathbb{C}\cong\mathbb{R}^{2}
—
written in coordinates as
z=x+\iota y
, where
\iota

is a fixed square root of
-1
. To

\boldsymbol{\phi}
is associated the real polynomial
function
\boldsymbol{\psi}\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\cdot$ }\hss}\raisebox{-1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}}=(\psi_{1}^{(1)},\psi_{1}^{(2)},\dots,\psi_{d}^{(1)},\psi_{d}^{(2)})
, where
\psi_{j}(\boldsymbol{z})\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\cdot$ }\hss}\raisebox{-1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}}=\psi_{1}^{(1)}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})+\iota\psi_{1}^{(2)}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})

for all
j\in[d]
. For
R>0
and
\boldsymbol{z}_{0}\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\cdot$ }\hss}\raisebox{-1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}}=(\boldsymbol{x}_{0},\boldsymbol{y}_{0})
, write

\mathbb{D}_{R}(\boldsymbol{z}_{0})\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\cdot$ }\hss}\raisebox{-1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}}=\{(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}:\max\{|\!|\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{x}_{0}|\!|_{2},|\!|\boldsymbol{y}-\boldsymbol{y}_{0}|\!|_{2}\}<R\}\,.

Let
J_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(\boldsymbol{z})
denote the complex Jacobian matrix of

\boldsymbol{\phi}
at
\boldsymbol{z}
.

Definition 3.1

(regular value)
.

For a polynomial map
\boldsymbol{\phi}\in\operatorname{Map}(\mathbb{C}^{d},\mathbb{C}^{d})
,
a point
\boldsymbol{w}\in\mathbb{C}^{d}
is a regular value
if

Z_{\boldsymbol{\phi}-\boldsymbol{w}}\cap\{\boldsymbol{z}\in\mathbb{C}^{d}:\det J_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}=0\}=\emptyset\,.

Note that, the regularity condition
is a nontrivial since
\dim Z_{\boldsymbol{\phi}-\boldsymbol{w}}\geq 0
, by
remark

1
above.

Assume that
\boldsymbol{0}
is
a regular value of
\boldsymbol{\phi}
. Furthermore, assume that
\boldsymbol{\phi}^{-1}(\boldsymbol{0})\subseteq\mathbb{C}^{d}
is a finite
set; then there is an
\epsilon_{0}>0
such that for each

\boldsymbol{z}_{0}\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\cdot$ }\hss}\raisebox{-1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}}=(\boldsymbol{x}_{0},\boldsymbol{y}_{0})\in\boldsymbol{\psi}^{-1}(\boldsymbol{0})
, the following
holds:

\mathbb{D}_{\epsilon_{0}}(\boldsymbol{z}_{0})\cap\boldsymbol{\psi}^{-1}(\boldsymbol{0})=\{(\boldsymbol{x}_{0},\boldsymbol{y}_{0})\}\,.

For each
\epsilon\in(0,\epsilon_{0})
,
consider the function

\gamma_{d,\epsilon}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\cdot$ }\hss}\raisebox{-1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}}=\eta_{d,\epsilon}e^{-\frac{|\!|(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})|\!|^{2}_{2}}{2\epsilon^{2}}}\,,

where
\eta_{d,\epsilon}
is such that

1=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}\gamma_{d,\epsilon}1_{\mathbb{D}_{\epsilon}(\boldsymbol{0})}(\boldsymbol{z})~d\boldsymbol{z}
.
Let

\mathbb{B}_{\epsilon,\boldsymbol{\phi}}\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\cdot$ }\hss}\raisebox{-1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}}=\{(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})\in\mathbb{R}^{2d}:\boldsymbol{\psi}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})\in\mathbb{D}_{\epsilon}(\boldsymbol{0})\}\,.

Definition 3.2

(Brouwer degree)
.

The analytic Brouwer degree of
\boldsymbol{\phi}

is defined to be

(1)

\deg(\boldsymbol{\phi})\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\cdot$ }\hss}\raisebox{-1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}}=\lim_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}\gamma_{d,\epsilon}(\boldsymbol{\psi}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}))1_{\mathbb{B}_{\epsilon,\boldsymbol{\phi}}}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})\det\det J_{\boldsymbol{\psi}}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})~d\boldsymbol{x}~d\boldsymbol{y}\,.

Here
\det J_{\boldsymbol{\psi}}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})
denotes the Jacobian determinant of

\boldsymbol{\psi}
at
(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})
.

For details on Brouwer degree on manifolds, we
refer to
[
7
]
, and
[
11
]

\mathbb{B}_{\epsilon,\boldsymbol{\phi}}\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\cdot$ }\hss}\raisebox{-1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}}=\{(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})\in\mathbb{R}^{2d}:\boldsymbol{\psi}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})\in\mathbb{D}_{\epsilon}(\boldsymbol{0})\}\,.

Recall that the analytic Brouwer degree of
\boldsymbol{\phi}

is defined to be

\deg(\boldsymbol{\phi})\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\cdot$ }\hss}\raisebox{-1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}}=\lim_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}\gamma_{d,\epsilon}(\boldsymbol{\psi}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}))1_{\mathbb{B}_{\epsilon,\boldsymbol{\phi}}}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})\det\det J_{\boldsymbol{\psi}}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})~d\boldsymbol{x}~d\boldsymbol{y}\,,

where
\det J_{\boldsymbol{\psi}}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})
denotes the Jacobian determinant of

\boldsymbol{\psi}
at
(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})
.

Lemma 5.1
.

Suppose that
\boldsymbol{0}
is
a regular value of
\boldsymbol{\phi}
; if

\mathbb{Z}_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}
is finite then

(2)

\deg(\boldsymbol{\phi})=|\mathbb{Z}_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}|\,.

Proof.

Suppose that
\mathbb{Z}_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}=\{\boldsymbol{z}_{1},\dots,\boldsymbol{z}_{t}\}
, in
which case, one has
|\mathbb{Z}_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}|=t
. Since
\boldsymbol{0}
is
regular value of
\boldsymbol{\phi}
, the differentials

D_{\boldsymbol{z}_{j}}\boldsymbol{\phi}
are invertible
for each
j\in[d]
. The standard matrices in

\operatorname{GL}_{2d}(\mathbb{R})
— that
the linear transformations

\begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{x}\\
\boldsymbol{y}\end{pmatrix}=\boldsymbol{z}\mapsto(D_{\boldsymbol{z}_{j}}\boldsymbol{\phi})(\boldsymbol{z})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}

represent — are precisely the real

\operatorname{GL}_{d}(\mathbb{C})
is connected,
the determinant of
D_{(\boldsymbol{x}_{j},\boldsymbol{y}_{j})}\boldsymbol{\psi}

We may assume — without loss of

\epsilon_{0}>0

as mentioned before, the restrictions

\boldsymbol{\phi}\Big\rvert_{\mathbb{D}_{\epsilon_{0}}(\boldsymbol{z}_{1})},\dots,\boldsymbol{\phi}\Big\rvert_{\mathbb{D}_{\epsilon_{0}}(\boldsymbol{z}_{t})}

are all diffeomorphisms onto their respective open images,
written

(3)

\mathbb{Z}_{j,\epsilon_{0}}\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\cdot$ }\hss}\raisebox{-1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}}=\boldsymbol{\phi}(\mathbb{D}_{\epsilon_{0}}(\boldsymbol{z}_{j}))\,.

There is an
\epsilon_{1}\in(0,\epsilon_{0})

is such that

\mathbb{D}_{\epsilon_{1}}(\boldsymbol{0})\subseteq\bigcap_{j=1}^{t}\mathbb{Z}_{j,\epsilon_{0}}\,.

We now define

By a compactness argument, there is a

\delta_{0}\in(0,\epsilon_{1})

(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})\in\overline{\mathbb{D}}_{R}(\boldsymbol{0})\setminus\left(\bigcup_{j=1}^{t}\mathbb{Y}_{j,\epsilon_{1}}\right)~\Rightarrow~|\!|\boldsymbol{\psi}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})|\!|_{2}>\delta_{0}

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}\gamma_{d,\epsilon}(\boldsymbol{\psi}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}))1_{\mathbb{B}_{\epsilon,\boldsymbol{\phi}}}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})\det J_{\boldsymbol{\psi}}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})~d\boldsymbol{x}~d\boldsymbol{y}

\displaystyle=~

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{Y}_{j,\epsilon_{1}}}\gamma_{d,\epsilon}(\boldsymbol{\psi}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}))1_{\mathbb{B}_{\epsilon,\boldsymbol{\phi}}}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})\det J_{\boldsymbol{\psi}}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})~d\boldsymbol{x}~d\boldsymbol{y}

By change of variable, this yields

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}\gamma_{d,\epsilon}(\boldsymbol{\psi}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}))1_{\mathbb{B}_{\epsilon,\boldsymbol{\phi}}}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})\det J_{\boldsymbol{\psi}}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})~d\boldsymbol{x}~d\boldsymbol{y}

\displaystyle=~

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{Y}_{j,\epsilon_{1}}}\gamma_{d,\epsilon}(\boldsymbol{\psi}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}))1_{\mathbb{B}_{\epsilon,\boldsymbol{\phi}}}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})\det J_{\boldsymbol{\psi}}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})~d\boldsymbol{x}~d\boldsymbol{y}

\displaystyle=~

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{t}\int_{\boldsymbol{\psi}(\mathbb{Y}_{j,\epsilon_{1}})}\gamma_{d,\epsilon}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})1_{\mathbb{D}_{\epsilon}(\boldsymbol{0})}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})~d\boldsymbol{x}~d\boldsymbol{y}

In the limiting case, we have
\mathbb{D}_{\epsilon}(\boldsymbol{0})\subseteq\boldsymbol{\phi}(\mathbb{Y}_{j,\epsilon_{1}})

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{t}\int_{\boldsymbol{\psi}(\mathbb{Y}_{j,\epsilon_{1}})}\gamma_{d,\epsilon}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})1_{\mathbb{D}_{\epsilon}(\boldsymbol{0})}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})~d\boldsymbol{x}~d\boldsymbol{y}

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}\gamma_{d,\epsilon}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})1_{\mathbb{D}_{\epsilon}(\boldsymbol{0})}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})~d\boldsymbol{x}~d\boldsymbol{y}

\displaystyle=~

\displaystyle 1

in the limit. This completes the proof.
∎

Suppose that
\mathcal{A}_{j}\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\cdot$ }\hss}\raisebox{-1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}}=\{\alpha_{1,j},\dots,\alpha_{|\mathcal{A}_{j}|,j}\}
for

. Let

For a vector
\boldsymbol{b}\in\mathbb{C}^{|\mathcal{A}|}
, write
\boldsymbol{\phi}_{\boldsymbol{b}}

for the polynomial map
\mathbb{C}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}^{d}

\boldsymbol{\phi}_{\boldsymbol{b},j}(\boldsymbol{z})\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\cdot$ }\hss}\raisebox{-1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}}=\sum_{k=1}^{|\mathcal{A}_{j}|}(c_{\alpha_{k,j}}+b_{\alpha_{k,j}})\boldsymbol{z}^{\alpha_{k,j}}\,.

Fact 5.2

(Bullinskaya)
.

For integers
m,k,d>0
, let
\mathcal{Y}=\{\operatorname{Y}(\boldsymbol{t}):\boldsymbol{t}\in\boldsymbol{W}\}
be a random
field with values in
\mathbb{R}^{m+k}

\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{m+k}

\boldsymbol{I}\subseteq\boldsymbol{W}
.
We assume that
\mathcal{Y}
satisfies the following

•

\boldsymbol{t}\in\boldsymbol{W}

\operatorname{Y}(\boldsymbol{t})

and there

;

.

Then, almost surely, there is no point

.

Corollary 5.3
.

Let
\boldsymbol{\psi}_{\boldsymbol{b}}\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\cdot$ }\hss}\raisebox{-1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}}=(\psi_{\boldsymbol{b},1},\dots,\psi_{\boldsymbol{b},2d})

\boldsymbol{0}

\boldsymbol{0}
is a regular value of

\boldsymbol{\phi}_{\boldsymbol{b}}
with

Let
\boldsymbol{W}\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\cdot$ }\hss}\raisebox{-1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}}=\mathbb{R}^{2d}
,

\boldsymbol{I}\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\cdot$ }\hss}\raisebox{-1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}}=\boldsymbol{W}
, and

. For each

\boldsymbol{t}\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\cdot$ }\hss}\raisebox{-1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}}=(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})

\operatorname{Y}(\boldsymbol{t})\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\cdot$ }\hss}\raisebox{-1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}}=\begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{\psi}_{\boldsymbol{b}}(\boldsymbol{t})\\

Lemma 5.4
.

Given non-empty finite subsets
\mathcal{A}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{A}_{d}\subseteq\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{d}

— none of which contains the origin,
let
\psi_{1},\cdots,\psi_{d}\in\mathbb{Q}[i][\boldsymbol{z}]
be polynomials supported on them.
For a
d
-variate complex random vector

\boldsymbol{c}\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\cdot$ }\hss}\raisebox{-1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}}=(c_{1},\cdots,c_{d})
with a continuous
density with respect to the Lebesgue measure
on
\mathbb{C}^{d}
, define the random polynomials

\phi_{j}(\boldsymbol{z})\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\cdot$ }\hss}\raisebox{-1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}}=-c_{j}+\psi_{j}(\boldsymbol{z})
for each
j\in[d]
. Then

\boldsymbol{0}
is a regular value of

\boldsymbol{\phi}\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\cdot$ }\hss}\raisebox{-1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}}=(\phi_{1},\dots,\phi_{d})^{\top}
with probability 1

Proof.

Consider the matrix

\displaystyle\operatorname{M}_{\phi}(\boldsymbol{z})

\displaystyle\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\cdot$ }\hss}\raisebox{-1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}}=\begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{\phi}(\boldsymbol{z})&D_{1}\boldsymbol{\phi}(\boldsymbol{z})&\cdot&\cdot&\cdot&D_{d}\boldsymbol{\phi}(\boldsymbol{z})\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}-\boldsymbol{c}+\boldsymbol{\psi}(\boldsymbol{z})&D_{1}\boldsymbol{\psi}(\boldsymbol{z})&\cdot&\cdot&\cdot&D_{d}\boldsymbol{\psi}(\boldsymbol{z})\end{pmatrix}

Since
J_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(\boldsymbol{z})=J_{\boldsymbol{\psi}}(\boldsymbol{z})
, the
hypersurface

\mathcal{H}_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\cdot$ }\hss}\raisebox{-1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}}=\{\boldsymbol{z}:\det J_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(\boldsymbol{z})=0\}

is (deterministic) affine manifold of dimension
d-1
.
In particular, the image
\mathcal{H}_{\boldsymbol{\psi}}\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\cdot$ }\hss}\raisebox{-1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}}=\boldsymbol{\psi}(\mathcal{H}_{\boldsymbol{\phi}})

is a complex analytic manifold of dimension at most
d-1
.
Thus,

\operatorname{Pr}[\boldsymbol{c}\in\boldsymbol{\psi}(\mathcal{H}_{\boldsymbol{\phi}})]=0\,,

which shows that
\boldsymbol{0}

is a regular value of
\boldsymbol{\phi}
with probability 1.
∎

Note that, by Bezout’s theorem, we know that

\deg(\boldsymbol{\phi})\leq D_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\cdot$ }\hss}\raisebox{-1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}}=\prod_{j=1}^{d}\deg(\phi_{j})\,.

Therefore, if we assume that the real and imaginary
parts of each coordinate of

\boldsymbol{c}
are iid uniform samples from
rationals of height at most
D_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}
, then

\operatorname{Pr}[\boldsymbol{c}\in\boldsymbol{\psi}(\mathcal{H}_{\boldsymbol{\phi}})]

is “exponentially small”. Formally, we have the

Lemma 5.5
.

For any non-empty finite subsets

\mathcal{A}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{A}_{d}\subseteq\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{d}

Suppose that

Lemma 5.6
.

6.
Complexity of analytic Brouwer degree

We now derive the statements of our main results. The
final main complexity theoretic tool we use is the following:

Fact 6.1

(
[
4
]
)
.

If there is a polynomial-time algorithm that
counts the permanent of integer matrices on a
constant fraction of all input instances, then

\operatorname{P}^{\operatorname{\sharp P}}=\operatorname{BPP}

.

Suppose
\mathscr{P}

.

7.
Conclusion

We showed that counting the analytic Brouwer degree
of maps is a hard computational problem, even in presence
of coins. In particular, we showed that the restricted problem
for polynomial maps having regular value
\boldsymbol{0}

is complete in the class
\operatorname{\sharp P}
.

It remains open however to find the precise complexity
of counting the analytic Brouwer degree of smooth maps
defined on submanifolds of
\mathbb{C}^{d}
. The
BSS model is more apprpriate for such problems
— because presentation of the space and maps
requires going out of the world of integers,
although simple topological manifolds such as spheres
and disks can be presented in Turing model by
specifying an underlying simplicial structure,
in which case, maps will then correspond to
simplicial maps; this is because these maps must
be geometrically realizable in Turing model in
terms of interesting but simple maps such as polynomials.
We leave this question open for future work.
