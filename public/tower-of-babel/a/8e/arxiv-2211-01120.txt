Variational Hierarchical Mixtures for Probabilistic Learning of Inverse Dynamics
Hany Abdulsamad, Peter Nickl, Pascal Klink, and Jan Peters

Abstract
Well-calibrated probabilistic regression models are a crucial learning component in robotics applications as datasets grow rapidly and tasks become more complex. Unfortunately, classical regression models are usually either probabilistic kernel machines with a flexible structure that does not scale gracefully with data or deterministic and vastly scalable automata, albeit with a restrictive parametric form and poor regularization. In this paper, we consider a probabilistic hierarchical modeling paradigm that combines the benefits of both worlds to deliver computationally efficient representations with inherent complexity regularization. The presented approaches are probabilistic interpretations of local regression techniques that approximate nonlinear functions through a set of local linear or polynomial units. Importantly, we rely on principles from Bayesian nonparametrics to formulate flexible models that adapt their complexity to the data and can potentially encompass an infinite number of components. We derive two efficient variational inference techniques to learn these representations and highlight the advantages of hierarchical infinite local regression models, such as dealing with non-smooth functions, mitigating catastrophic forgetting, and enabling parameter sharing and fast predictions. Finally, we validate this approach on large inverse dynamics datasets and test the learned models in real-world control scenarios.

Following this introduction, we believe that local regression with a proper generative Bayesian treatment can potentially serve as a powerful general-purpose function approximator. Moreover, as a probabilistic and efficient representation, it can drive many low-level applications in control and robotics that favor fast predictions and do not require deep representations.

We introduce two probabilistic graphical mixture models for local regression in the upcoming sections. The first,

[

ILR
, and like other local regression techniques that rely on locally linear or polynomial approximations, it maintains a one-to-one correspondence between the activations and local regression units. To address this limitation, we introduce

We benchmark the models on a range of tasks highlighting their strengths, such as dealing with heteroscedasticity, non-continuous functions, and multi-modal activation. Additionally, we test on large real-world high-dimensional datasets to benchmark learning the inverse dynamics of robotic manipulators. Most importantly, we deploy an instance of

II
Preliminaries

This section introduces related concepts, such as Bayesian linear regression, Bayesian mixture models, Dirichlet processes, and variational inference.

II-A

Bayesian Linear Regression

We start by discussing the Bayesian treatment of a single component of a Bayesian local regression model, namely Bayesian linear regression
[
61
]
. The conditional data likelihood takes a feature vector
\bm{\mathbf{x}}\in\mathds{R}^{m}
as a random input variable and returns a random response variable
\bm{\mathbf{y}}\in\mathds{R}^{d}
according to a linear mapping
\bm{\mathbf{A}}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\mathds{R}^{m}\to\mathds{R}^{d}
, a bias vector
\bm{\mathbf{c}}
, and additive zero-mean noise with a precision matrix
\bm{\mathbf{V}}

\bm{\mathbf{y}}=\bm{\mathbf{A}}\bm{\mathbf{x}}+\bm{\mathbf{c}}+\bm{\mathbf{e}},\quad\bm{\mathbf{e}}\sim\operatorname{N}(\bm{\mathbf{0}},\bm{\mathbf{V}}).

For a fully Bayesian treatment, we consider all parameters of this model to be random variables on which we place proper conjugate priors. In this case, we place
matrix-normal (MN)
and
normal-Wishart (NW)
priors on the matrix
\bm{\mathbf{A}}
, the bias coefficient
\bm{\mathbf{c}}
, and precision matrix
\bm{\mathbf{V}}

p(\bm{\mathbf{A}},\bm{\mathbf{c}},\bm{\mathbf{V}})=\operatorname{MN}(\bm{\mathbf{A}}\,|\,\bm{\mathbf{M}},\bm{\mathbf{V}},\bm{\mathbf{K}})\operatorname{N}(\bm{\mathbf{c}}\,|\,\bm{\mathbf{\theta}},\rho\bm{\mathbf{V}})\operatorname{W}(\bm{\mathbf{V}}\,|\,\bm{\mathbf{\Phi}},\eta),

where
\bm{\mathbf{M}}
, the mean of
\bm{\mathbf{A}}
, is a
d\times m
matrix and
\bm{\mathbf{V}}
and
\bm{\mathbf{K}}
are
d\times d
and
m\times m
that serve as row and column precision matrices of
\bm{\mathbf{A}}
, respectively. The mean
\bm{\mathbf{\theta}}
is an
m
-dimensional vector, and the scalar
\rho
modulates the amplitude of the precision. Finally, the parameters of the Wishart distribution are the
d\times d
positive definite scale matrix
\bm{\mathbf{\Phi}}
and the degrees of freedom
\eta
. Due to the conjugate nature of the priors, the joint posteriors
p(\bm{\mathbf{A}},\bm{\mathbf{c}},\bm{\mathbf{V}}\,|\,\mathcal{D})
are matrix-normal and normal-Wishart distributions, conditioned on the data of
N
independent and identically distributed data pairs
\mathcal{D}=\{(\bm{\mathbf{x}}_{1},\bm{\mathbf{y}}_{1}),\ldots,(\bm{\mathbf{x}}_{N},\bm{\mathbf{y}}_{N})\}
.

II-B

Bayesian Finite Mixture Models

Gaussian mixture models (GMMs)
are hierarchical latent variable models with universal approximation capabilities for arbitrary continuous densities. This insight is of central interest when connected to density estimation for local regression models, which are themselves universal nonlinear function approximators
[
5
]
. A finite
K
-component Gaussian mixture of a random variable
\bm{\mathbf{x}}
is a weighted linear combination of densities

\vskip-2.84544ptp(\bm{\mathbf{x}}\,|\,\bm{\mathbf{\theta}})=\sum_{k=1}^{K}p(\bm{\mathbf{z}}=k\,|\,\bm{\mathbf{\pi}})p(\bm{\mathbf{x}}\,|\,\bm{\mathbf{\theta}}_{k})=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\pi_{k}\operatorname{N}(\bm{\mathbf{x}}\,|\,\bm{\mathbf{\mu}}_{k},\bm{\mathbf{\Lambda}}_{k}),

with
K
unique mean vectors
\bm{\mathbf{\mu}}_{k}
and precision matrices
\bm{\mathbf{\Lambda}}_{k}
. The latent quantity
\bm{\mathbf{z}}
is a one-hot random variable distributed according to a categorical distribution
p(\bm{\mathbf{z}})=\operatorname{Cat}(\bm{\mathbf{\pi}})
, governed by the weights
\bm{\mathbf{\pi}}=\{\pi_{1},\dots,\pi_{K}\}
that satisfy
0\leq\pi_{k}\leq 1
and
\sum_{k=1}^{K}\pi_{k}=1
.

The Bayesian extension of this model
[
58
]
introduces a conjugate normal-Wishart prior on the means and precision matrices
(\bm{\mathbf{\mu}}_{k},\bm{\mathbf{\Lambda}}_{k})\sim\operatorname{NW}(\bm{\mathbf{\lambda}})
, where
\bm{\mathbf{\lambda}}
contains the hyperparameters. Furthermore, a conjugate Dirichlet prior, with a concentration parameter
\alpha
, is placed on the mixing weights
\bm{\mathbf{\pi}}\sim\operatorname{Dir}(\bm{\mathbf{\alpha}})
. This Bayesian perspective has proven effective in regularizing the shortcomings of
GMMs
by allowing superfluous components to fall back onto their priors instead of severely over-fitting to small clusters. This effect can be understood as sparsification bias over
K

[
62
,
63
]
.

II-C

Dirichlet Process and Stick-Breaking

Scaling the finite mixtures in Section
II-B
to infinite components requires placing a nonparametric
Dirichlet process (DP)
prior on the parameters of the mixture. A
DP
is a distribution over probability measures
\operatorname{G}
. We write
\operatorname{G}\sim\operatorname{DP}(\alpha,\operatorname{H})
, where
\alpha
is the concentration parameter and
\operatorname{H}
is the base measure
[
64
,
65
]
. Intuitively, a Dirichlet process is a distribution over distributions, meaning each draw
\operatorname{G}
is itself a distribution. The base distribution
\operatorname{H}
is the mean of the
DP
, and the concentration parameter
\alpha
can be interpreted as an inverse variance. The larger
\alpha
is, the smaller the variance, and the process concentrates more of its mass around the mean distribution
\operatorname{H}
.

We will rely on the stick-breaking construction
[
66
]
of a
DP
as an algorithmic realization. Stick-breaking delivers an infinite sequence of mixture weights
\pi_{k}
of an infinite mixture model from the stochastic process

\pi_{k}=s_{k}\prod_{l=1}^{k-1}\left(1-s_{l}\right),\quad s_{k}\sim\operatorname{Beta}(1,\alpha).

This process is sometimes denoted as
\bm{\mathbf{\pi}}\sim\operatorname{GEM}(\alpha)

[
64
]
. The stick-breaking procedure describes how the random variables
s_{k}
, representing stick lengths, are drawn from a beta distribution and combined to obtain the mixture weights
\pi_{k}
. If the concentration parameter
\alpha
increases, the magnitude of the mixing weights
\pi_{k}
decreases on average, and the number of possible active components increases.

This representation of
DPs
can be used to replace the priors placed on the finite Gaussian mixture model
[
59
]
. In such a setting, the base
\operatorname{H}
is a normal-Wishart distribution, and the sampled measure
\operatorname{G}\sim\operatorname{DP}(\alpha,\operatorname{NW})
is a draw of an unbounded number of parameters
(\bm{\mathbf{\mu}}_{k},\bm{\mathbf{\Lambda}}_{k})\sim\operatorname{NW}(\bm{\mathbf{\lambda}})
for an infinite number of clusters, associated with an infinite number of weights
\pi_{k}
generated by the stick-breaking process. These draws from a Dirichlet process are discrete with probability one, which leads to the clustering effect of the
DP
. Eventually, the same parameters will be sampled over and over, forcing the associated data points to cluster.

II-D

Variational Inference of Structured Models

MCMC

[
67
]
, and
VI

[
68
]
have become the two main approaches for (approximate) probabilistic inference in graphical models. While
MCMC
constructs a stochastic sampling process that converges to the posterior,
VI
formulates the inference task as a deterministic optimization problem. Despite its reliance on a coarser functional posterior approximation,
VI
is often preferred in settings with large number of parameters. Moreover, the deterministic nature of
VI
circumvents the issue of label switching in Monte-Carlo-based posterior inference of mixture models.

In a nutshell, in variational inference, a typically intractable posterior is approximated by a tractable functional distribution
q(\bm{\mathbf{\beta}})
that minimizes the
Kullback-Leibler divergence (KL)
to true posterior
p(\bm{\mathbf{\beta}}\,|\,\mathcal{D})

q^{*}(\bm{\mathbf{\beta}})=\argmin\quad\kl(q(\bm{\mathbf{\beta}})\,||\,p(\bm{\mathbf{\beta}}\,|\,\mathcal{D))},

where
\mathcal{D}
is observed data and the vector
\bm{\mathbf{\beta}}
subsumes both the parameters
\bm{\mathbf{\theta}}
and hidden indicators
\bm{\mathbf{z}}
in structured models. Note that the
KL
in the above objective is mode-seeking, meaning it will lock on one mode of a possibly multi-modal posterior. In general, the posterior
p(\bm{\mathbf{\beta}}\,|\,\mathcal{D})
is unknown, because the normalizer
p(\mathcal{D})
is not tractable. In consequence, the
KL
cannot be minimized directly but rather optimized via a related objective that is equal up to the constant term equivalent to the evidence
p(\mathcal{D})

\displaystyle\kl(q\,||\,p)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{q}\left[\log q(\bm{\mathbf{\beta}})\right]-\mathbb{E}_{q}\left[\log p(\bm{\mathbf{\beta}}\,|\,\mathcal{D})\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{q}\left[\log q(\bm{\mathbf{\beta}})\right]-\mathbb{E}_{q}\left[\log p(\mathcal{D},\bm{\mathbf{\beta}})\right]+p(\mathcal{D})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{q}\left[\log q(\bm{\mathbf{\beta}})\right]-\mathbb{E}_{q}\left[\log p(\mathcal{D},\bm{\mathbf{\beta}})\right]+\mathrm{const}.

This modified objective is denoted as the negative
evidence lower bound (ELBO)
and can be reformulated to take the traditional maximization objective of
VI
algorithms

\displaystyle\mathrm{ELBO}(q)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{q}\left[\log p(\mathcal{D},\bm{\mathbf{\beta}})\right]-\mathbb{E}_{q}\left[\log q(\bm{\mathbf{\beta}})\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{q}\left[\log p(\mathcal{D}\,|\,\bm{\mathbf{\beta}})\right]+\mathbb{E}_{q}\left[\log p(\bm{\mathbf{\beta}})\right]-\mathbb{E}_{q}\left[\log q(\bm{\mathbf{\beta}})\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{q}\left[\log p(\mathcal{D}\,|\,\bm{\mathbf{\beta}})\right]-\kl(q(\bm{\mathbf{\beta}})\,||\,p(\bm{\mathbf{\beta}})).

The choice of the approximate posterior distribution
q(\bm{\mathbf{\beta}})
is open. In this paper, we focus on variational Bayes methods that rely on the (structured) mean-field assumption as a general recipe for maximizing the
ELBO

[
69
,
45
]
. This approximation requires that the posterior factorizes over the set of the hidden variables
q(\bm{\mathbf{\beta}})=\prod_{i=1}^{M}q_{i}(\beta_{i})
. It is emphasized that no other assumptions are made about
q(\bm{\mathbf{\beta}})
. The resulting posterior will be determined solely by the assumed likelihood and priors.

Specifically, we follow the scheme of
variational Bayes expectation-maximization (VBEM)
as a probabilistic generalization of
expectation-maximization (EM)
. This approach constitutes a coordinate ascent scheme that iteratively optimizes the
ELBO
for individual factors of the approximate posterior
q(\bm{\mathbf{\beta}})
while holding the others constant

\ln q_{j}(\beta_{j})=\mathbb{E}_{q_{i\neq j}}\left[\log p(\mathcal{D},\bm{\mathbf{\beta}})\right]+\mathrm{const}.

A more practical version of this optimization can be achieved by using
stochastic variational inference (SVI)
, a batched stochastic gradient ascent approach. In the case of the conjugate exponential family,
SVI
not only facilitates scalability over large datasets but also resembles a natural gradient ascent algorithm on the
ELBO
with favorable convergence properties
[
70
]
.

III
The Infinite Mixture of Local Linear Regression Model

Using the previously presented concepts of Bayesian linear regression, Bayesian mixture models, and Dirichlet processes, we now construct the Bayesian
infinite local regression (ILR)
model.

Our approach to tackling the regression problem is a Bayesian joint density estimation technique over the input
\bm{\mathbf{x}}
and target
\bm{\mathbf{y}}
, both conditioned on a latent discrete label
\bm{\mathbf{z}}
. To avoid the need for a fixed number of components over
\bm{\mathbf{z}}
, we assume a generative model driven by a stick-breaking process with a concentration parameter
\alpha
and a base distribution
\operatorname{H}
, which is a product of a normal-Wishart, a Matrix-normal, and Wishart distribution.

The data generation proceeds as depicted in Figure
2
. The stick-breaking process is sampled to generate the categorical weights
\bm{\mathbf{\pi}}
, mixture activations
\{\bm{\mathbf{\mu}}_{k},\bm{\mathbf{\Lambda}}_{k}\}_{k=1}^{\infty}
, and regression parameters
\{\bm{\mathbf{A}}_{k},\bm{\mathbf{c}}_{k},\bm{\mathbf{V}}_{k}\}_{k=1}^{\infty}

\begin{gathered}\bm{\mathbf{\pi}}(\bm{\mathbf{s}})\sim\operatorname{GEM}(\alpha),\\
\bm{\mathbf{\Lambda}}_{k}\sim\operatorname{W}(\bm{\mathbf{\Psi}},\nu),\,\,\bm{\mathbf{\mu}}_{k}\sim\operatorname{N}(\bm{\mathbf{m}},\kappa\bm{\mathbf{\Lambda}}_{k}),\\
\bm{\mathbf{V}}_{k}\sim\operatorname{W}(\bm{\mathbf{\Phi}},\eta),\,\,\bm{\mathbf{A}}_{k}\sim\operatorname{MN}(\bm{\mathbf{M}},\bm{\mathbf{K}},\bm{\mathbf{V}}_{k}),\,\,\bm{\mathbf{c}}_{k}\sim\operatorname{N}(\bm{\mathbf{\theta}},\rho\bm{\mathbf{V}}_{k}),\end{gathered}

and those parameters are used to generate the one-hot labels
\bm{\mathbf{z}}_{n}
and input-output data pairs
\bm{\mathbf{x}}_{n},\bm{\mathbf{y}}_{n}

\displaystyle\bm{\mathbf{z}}_{n}\sim\operatorname{Cat}(\bm{\mathbf{\pi}}(\bm{\mathbf{s}})),\,\,\bm{\mathbf{x}}_{n}\sim\operatorname{N}(\bm{\mathbf{\mu}}_{z_{n}},\bm{\mathbf{\Lambda}}_{z_{n}}),

\displaystyle\bm{\mathbf{y}}_{n}\sim\operatorname{N}(\bm{\mathbf{A}}_{z_{n}}\bm{\mathbf{x}}_{n}+\bm{\mathbf{c}}_{z_{n}},\bm{\mathbf{V}}_{z_{n}}).

Notice that the densities over the input space, parameterized by
(\bm{\mathbf{\mu}}_{k},\bm{\mathbf{\Lambda}}_{k})
, naturally play the role of basis functions or so-called receptive fields as in the receptive field weighted regression (RFWR)
[
31
]
and locally weighted projection regression (LWPR)
[
32
]
algorithms.

Given these modeling assumptions, the regression objective can be cast as a joint density inference problem of the posterior
p(\bm{\mathbf{Z}},\bm{\mathbf{s}},\bm{\mathbf{\mu}},\allowbreak\bm{\mathbf{\Lambda}},\bm{\mathbf{A}},\bm{\mathbf{c}},\bm{\mathbf{V}}\,|\,\bm{\mathbf{X}},\bm{\mathbf{Y}})
, where
\bm{\mathbf{X}}=\{\bm{\mathbf{x}}_{1},\dots,\bm{\mathbf{x}}_{N}\}
,
\bm{\mathbf{Y}}=\{\bm{\mathbf{y}}_{1},\dots,\bm{\mathbf{y}}_{N}\}
, and
\bm{\mathbf{Z}}=\{\bm{\mathbf{z}}_{1},\dots,\bm{\mathbf{z}}_{N}\}
. The result of this inference is then leveraged to perform predictions via conditioning and marginalization.

III-A

Variational Bayes for
ILR

For inference, we focus on a variational Bayes
EM
algorithm that alternates between a variational expectation step (E-step) and a maximization step (M-step). Deriving such an algorithm for this model requires pinning down the following definitions of the likelihood, prior, and posterior.

Complete Data Likelihood.
For the general case of multivariate regression with
m
inputs and
d
outputs, we assume the following structured joint likelihood over the inputs, outputs, and indicator variables

\displaystyle p(\bm{\mathbf{X}},\bm{\mathbf{Y}},\bm{\mathbf{Z}}\,|\,.)

\displaystyle=p(\bm{\mathbf{Z}})\,p(\bm{\mathbf{X}}\,|\,\bm{\mathbf{Z}})\,p(\bm{\mathbf{Y}}\,|\,\bm{\mathbf{X}},\bm{\mathbf{Z}})

\displaystyle=\begin{aligned} &\prod_{n=1}^{N}\operatorname{Cat}(\bm{\mathbf{z}}_{n}\,|\,\bm{\mathbf{\pi}}(\bm{\mathbf{s}}))\\
&\times\prod_{n=1}^{N}\prod_{k=1}^{\infty}\operatorname{N}(\bm{\mathbf{x}}_{n}\,|\,\bm{\mathbf{\mu}}_{k},\bm{\mathbf{\Lambda}}_{k})^{z_{nk}}\\
&\times\prod_{n=1}^{N}\prod_{k=1}^{\infty}\operatorname{N}(\bm{\mathbf{y}}_{n}\,|\,\bm{\mathbf{A}}_{k}\bm{\mathbf{x}}_{n}+\bm{\mathbf{c}}_{k},\bm{\mathbf{V}}_{k})^{z_{nk}},\end{aligned}

where the dimensions of all quantities follow the notation of Bayesian linear regression.

Infinite Conjugate Prior.
We construct a factorized conjugate infinite mixture prior

p(\bm{\mathbf{s}},\bm{\mathbf{\mu}},\bm{\mathbf{\Lambda}},\bm{\mathbf{A}},\bm{\mathbf{c}},\bm{\mathbf{V}})=\begin{aligned} &p(\bm{\mathbf{s}})\,p(\bm{\mathbf{\mu}}|\bm{\mathbf{\Lambda}})\,p(\bm{\mathbf{\Lambda}})\\
&\times p(\bm{\mathbf{A}}|\bm{\mathbf{V}})\,p(\bm{\mathbf{c}}|\bm{\mathbf{V}})\,p(\bm{\mathbf{V}}).\end{aligned}

This prior assumes that the cluster means
\bm{\mathbf{\mu}}_{k}
and precision matrices
\bm{\mathbf{\Lambda}}_{k}
are sampled from normal-Wishart distributions

p(\bm{\mathbf{\mu}}\,|\,\bm{\mathbf{\Lambda}})\,p(\bm{\mathbf{\Lambda}})=\prod_{k=1}^{\infty}\operatorname{N}(\bm{\mathbf{\mu}}_{k}\,|\,\bm{\mathbf{m}}_{0},\kappa_{0}\bm{\mathbf{\Lambda}}_{k})\operatorname{W}(\bm{\mathbf{\Lambda}}_{k}\,|\,\bm{\mathbf{\Psi}}_{0},\nu_{0}),

while matrix-normal-Wishart and normal-Wishart priors are placed on the regression coefficients
(\bm{\mathbf{A}}_{k},\bm{\mathbf{c}}_{k})
and the precision matrices
\bm{\mathbf{V}}_{k}

\displaystyle p(\bm{\mathbf{A}}\,|\,\bm{\mathbf{V}})p(\bm{\mathbf{c}}\,|\,\bm{\mathbf{V}})p(\bm{\mathbf{V}})=\prod_{k=1}^{\infty}

\displaystyle\operatorname{MN}(\bm{\mathbf{A}}_{k}\,|\,\bm{\mathbf{M}}_{0},\bm{\mathbf{K}}_{0},\bm{\mathbf{V}}_{k})

\displaystyle\hskip-14.22636pt\times\operatorname{N}(\bm{\mathbf{c}}_{k}\,|\,\bm{\mathbf{\theta}}_{0},\rho_{0}\bm{\mathbf{V}}_{k})\operatorname{W}(\bm{\mathbf{V}}_{k}\,|\,\bm{\mathbf{\Phi}}_{0},\eta_{0}).

The parameters
\pi_{k}
are generated by a stick-breaking process
\pi_{k}(\bm{\mathbf{s}})=s_{k}\prod_{l=1}^{k-1}(1-s_{l})
, where the stick lengths
\bm{\mathbf{s}}
are independently beta distributed

p(\bm{\mathbf{s}})=\prod_{k=1}^{\infty}\operatorname{Beta}(s_{k}\,|\,1,\alpha_{0}).

Truncated Mean-Field Posterior.
We rely on a structured mean-field approximation of the posterior
[
69
]
that factorizes between the labels
q(\bm{\mathbf{Z}})
and the remaining parameters
q(\bm{\mathbf{s}},\bm{\mathbf{\mu}},\bm{\mathbf{\Lambda}},\bm{\mathbf{A}},\bm{\mathbf{c}},\bm{\mathbf{V}})
, thus automatically leading to the decomposition
p(.\,|\,\mathcal{D})\approx q(\bm{\mathbf{Z}})\,q(\bm{\mathbf{s}})\,q(\bm{\mathbf{\mu}},\bm{\mathbf{\Lambda}})\,q(\bm{\mathbf{A}},\bm{\mathbf{c}},\bm{\mathbf{V}})
.
Further, we follow
[
59
]
by allowing a truncation of the posterior while maintaining an infinite prior, so that
q(s_{\scriptscriptstyle K}\!=\!1)=1
, implying that
\pi_{k}=0
for
k>K

p(.\,|\,\mathcal{D})\approx\begin{aligned} &\prod_{n=1}^{N}\operatorname{Cat}(\bm{\mathbf{z}}_{n}\,|\,\bm{\mathbf{r}}_{n})\prod_{k=1}^{K-1}\operatorname{Beta}(s_{k}\,|\,\gamma_{k},\alpha_{k})\\
&\times\prod_{k=1}^{K}\operatorname{N}(\bm{\mathbf{\mu}}_{k}\,|\,\bm{\mathbf{m}}_{k},\kappa_{k}\bm{\mathbf{\Lambda}}_{k})\operatorname{W}(\bm{\mathbf{\Lambda}}_{k}\,|\,\bm{\mathbf{\Psi}}_{k},\nu_{k})\\
&\times\begin{aligned} \prod_{k=1}^{K}&\operatorname{MN}(\bm{\mathbf{A}}_{k}\,|\,\bm{\mathbf{M}}_{k},\bm{\mathbf{K}}_{k},\bm{\mathbf{V}}_{k})\\
&\times\operatorname{N}(\bm{\mathbf{c}}_{k}\,|\,\bm{\mathbf{\theta}}_{k},\rho_{k}\bm{\mathbf{V}}_{k})\operatorname{W}(\bm{\mathbf{V}}_{k}\,|\,\bm{\mathbf{\Phi}}_{k},\eta_{k}),\end{aligned}\end{aligned}

where
\bm{\mathbf{r}}_{n}
are the expected responsibilities of the mixture. During evaluation, the truncation threshold
K
is chosen to be very high and is seldom reached.

Variational Expectation Step.
In the E-step, the responsibilities are computed by following the recipe of
VBEM
in Section
II-D
. The responsibilities are the variational parameters of the posterior categorical

\displaystyle\log q(\bm{\mathbf{Z}})

\displaystyle=\begin{aligned} &\mathbb{E}_{q(\bm{\mathbf{s}})}\left[\log p(\bm{\mathbf{Z}}\,|\,\bm{\mathbf{\pi}}(\bm{\mathbf{s}}))\right]+\mathbb{E}_{q(\bm{\mathbf{\mu}},\bm{\mathbf{\Lambda}})}\left[\log p(\bm{\mathbf{X}}\,|\,\bm{\mathbf{Z}})\right]\\
&+\mathbb{E}_{q(\bm{\mathbf{A}},\bm{\mathbf{c}},\bm{\mathbf{V}})}\left[\log p(\bm{\mathbf{Y}}\,|\,\bm{\mathbf{X}},\bm{\mathbf{Z}})\right]+\const\end{aligned}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=1}^{N}\sum_{k=1}^{K}z_{nk}\log r_{nk}.

The expectations associated with the data likelihoods of
\bm{\mathbf{x}}_{n}
and
\bm{\mathbf{y}}_{n}
can be straightforwardly computed
[
71
]
. However, the expectations associated with infinite-dimensional categorical require more careful consideration
[
59
]
. We provide the necessary details in the appendix.

Variational Maximization Step.
The M-step updates the remaining variational distributions given an approximation of the categorical posterior as follows

\displaystyle\log q(\bm{\mathbf{s}})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{q(\bm{\mathbf{Z}})}\left[\log p(\bm{\mathbf{Z}}\,|\,\bm{\mathbf{\pi}}(\bm{\mathbf{s}}))\right]+\log p(\bm{\mathbf{s}})+\const,

\displaystyle\log q(\bm{\mathbf{\mu}},\bm{\mathbf{\Lambda}})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{q(\bm{\mathbf{Z}})}\left[\log p(\bm{\mathbf{X}}\,|\,\bm{\mathbf{Z}})\right]+\log p(\bm{\mathbf{\mu}},\bm{\mathbf{\Lambda}})+\const,

\displaystyle\log q(\bm{\mathbf{A}},\bm{\mathbf{c}},\bm{\mathbf{V}})

\displaystyle=\begin{aligned} &\mathbb{E}_{q(\bm{\mathbf{Z}})}\left[\log p(\bm{\mathbf{Y}}\,|\,\bm{\mathbf{X}},\bm{\mathbf{Z}})\right]\\
&+\log p(\bm{\mathbf{A}},\bm{\mathbf{c}},\bm{\mathbf{V}})+\const.\end{aligned}

Each of these updates reflects a conjugate computation of
K
log-posterior densities given a log-prior and a log-likelihood weighted by the responsibilities
r_{nk}=\mathbb{E}[z_{nk}]
. We provide more details and general computational recipes based on conjugacy rules in the appendix.

III-B

Posterior Predictive Distribution

For predicting the function value
\hat{\bm{\mathbf{y}}}
conditioned on a test query
\hat{\bm{\mathbf{x}}}
, we marginalize the likelihood over the posterior parameters to get the joint posterior predictive density. To make the marginalization tractable, we replace the true posterior with our approximate variational posterior
q(.\,|\,\mathcal{D})
inferred under a training dataset
\mathcal{D}
. The conditional predictive for a single component
\bm{\mathbf{z}}=k
is a conditional multivariate Student’s t-distribution of the form

\displaystyle p(\hat{\bm{\mathbf{y}}}\,|\,\hat{\bm{\mathbf{x}}},\hat{\bm{\mathbf{z}}}\!=\!k,\mathcal{D})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{q(\bm{\mathbf{A}},\bm{\mathbf{c}},\bm{\mathbf{V}})}\left[p(\hat{\bm{\mathbf{y}}}\,|\,\hat{\bm{\mathbf{x}}},\bm{\mathbf{A}}_{k},\bm{\mathbf{c}}_{k},\bm{\mathbf{V}}_{k})\right]

\displaystyle=\operatorname{T}\left(\bm{\mathbf{M}}_{k}\hat{\bm{\mathbf{x}}}+\bm{\mathbf{\theta}}_{k},a_{k}\bm{\mathbf{\Phi}}_{k},\eta_{k}+1\right),

where we have defined

a_{k}=1-\bm{\mathbf{u}}^{\top}\left(\bm{\mathbf{L}}_{k}+\bm{\mathbf{u}}\bm{\mathbf{u}}^{\top}\right)^{-1}\bm{\mathbf{u}},

with
\bm{\mathbf{u}}=[\hat{\bm{\mathbf{x}}},\,1]^{\top}
and
\bm{\mathbf{L}}_{k}=\operatorname{Block}\left(\bm{\mathbf{K}}_{k},\rho_{k}\right)
.

Additionally, the joint activation of a component
k
is a Student’s t-distribution weighted by the expected categorical probability under the posterior stick-breaking process

\displaystyle p(\hat{\bm{\mathbf{x}}},\hat{\bm{\mathbf{z}}}\!=\!k\,|\,\mathcal{D})

\displaystyle\propto\mathbb{E}_{q(\bm{\mathbf{s}})}\left[p(\hat{\bm{\mathbf{z}}}\!=\!k\,|\,\bm{\mathbf{\pi}}(\bm{\mathbf{s}}))\right]\mathbb{E}_{q(\bm{\mathbf{\mu}},\bm{\mathbf{\Lambda}})}\left[p(\hat{\bm{\mathbf{x}}}\,|\,\bm{\mathbf{\mu}}_{k},\bm{\mathbf{\Lambda}}_{k})\right]

\displaystyle=\begin{aligned} &\frac{\gamma_{k}}{\gamma_{k}+\alpha_{k}}\prod_{l=1}^{k-1}\left(1-\frac{\gamma_{l}}{\gamma_{l}+\alpha_{l}}\right)\\
&\times\operatorname{T}\left(\bm{\mathbf{\mu}}_{k},\frac{\kappa_{k}}{1+\kappa_{k}}\bm{\mathbf{\Psi}}_{k},\nu_{k}+1\right).\end{aligned}

These
K
-activation probabilities enable two prediction techniques. A
mode-prediction
, where the most likely active component is selected and used to perform prediction with the corresponding linear regression model, or a
mean-prediction
, that averages the predictions of all components weighted by their activation probabilities.

III-C

Computational Complexity

We calculate the training-time computational cost to be
\mathcal{O}(NK(d+m)^{3})
, which can be straightforwardly reduced to
\mathcal{O}(LK(d+m)^{3})
by applying stochastic updates
[
70
]
, where
L
is the batch size. This result shows linear scalability with the data, which is considerably more efficient than simple variants of
GPR
. The test-time complexity of a mean prediction is
\mathcal{O}(K(d^{3}+dm))
, which combines the input membership query and the linear matrix transformation for every model
k
. This computation is, in contrast to
GPR
, independent of the training data size, hence the advantage of memoryless locally-parametric representations during real-time critical applications.

IV
The Hierarchical Infinite Mixture of Local Linear Regression Model

The local regression model presented in Section
III
offers a very flexible and well-regularized alternative to previously developed approaches
[
30
,
32
,
42
]
. However, like similar local regression representations, it cannot account for shift-invariance across the input space. This drawback can cause it to generate duplicate regression components to account for similar local function trends across disconnected regions of the input space. This limitation results from the hierarchical design that directly couples activations and local function units via a one-to-one correspondence and enforces a uni-modal activation per regression component. This coupling can be observed in the definition of the likelihood in Section
III
, where the activation and the local regression units share the same assignment variable.

It stands to reason that
ILR
does not offer enough flexibility and hinders parameter sharing between components. Therefore, we present a modified formulation of
ILR
that explicitly accounts for shift-invariance in the input space and provides the freedom to create regression units with multi-modal, theoretically infinitely-modal, activations, if needed. The resulting model,
hierarchical infinite local regression (HILR)
, is an infinite mixture over infinite mixtures that shares similarities with existing representations developed for hierarchical clustering
[
72
,
73
,
74
]
.

We start by describing the generative process of the hierarchical mixture over mixtures as depicted in Figure
3
.
HILR
consists of two levels. At the upper level, a meta Dirichlet process generates the stick-breaking weights
\bm{\mathbf{\omega}}
, the meta-activations
\{\bm{\mathbf{\tau}}_{m},\bm{\mathbf{\Lambda}}_{m}\}_{m=1}^{\infty}
, and the shared slope and output precision matrices
\{\bm{\mathbf{A}}_{m},\bm{\mathbf{V}}_{m}\}_{m=1}^{\infty}

\begin{gathered}\bm{\mathbf{\omega}}(\bm{\mathbf{t}})\sim\operatorname{GEM}(\beta),\\
\bm{\mathbf{\Lambda}}_{m}\sim\operatorname{W}(\bm{\mathbf{\Psi}},\nu),\,\,\bm{\mathbf{\tau}}_{m}\sim\operatorname{N}(\bm{\mathbf{m}},\lambda\bm{\mathbf{\Lambda}}_{m}),\\
\bm{\mathbf{V}}_{m}\sim\operatorname{W}(\bm{\mathbf{\Phi}},\eta),\,\,\bm{\mathbf{A}}_{m}\sim\operatorname{MN}(\bm{\mathbf{M}},\bm{\mathbf{K}},\bm{\mathbf{V}}_{m}),\end{gathered}

where
\beta
is the concentration parameter of the upper-level
DP
and
\bm{\mathbf{t}}
are the generated stick lengths.

HILR
’s ability to account for shift-invariance is a result of allowing multi-modal activations. That way, every regression unit can be associated with multiple local regions in the input space. This structure is realized by endowing every upper-level component
m
with its own local
DP
. These lower-level
DPs
, with a concentration parameter
\alpha
, generate the weights
\bm{\mathbf{\pi}}_{m}
, the activation centers
\{\bm{\mathbf{\mu}}_{mk}\}_{k=1}^{\infty}
and the shift coefficients
\{\bm{\mathbf{c}}_{mk}\}_{k=1}^{\infty}

\displaystyle\bm{\mathbf{\pi}}_{m}(\bm{\mathbf{s}}_{m})\sim\operatorname{GEM}(\alpha),

\displaystyle\bm{\mathbf{\mu}}_{mk}\sim\operatorname{N}(\bm{\mathbf{\tau}}_{m},\kappa\bm{\mathbf{\Lambda}}_{m}),\,\,\bm{\mathbf{c}}_{mk}\sim\operatorname{N}(\bm{\mathbf{\theta}},\rho\bm{\mathbf{V}}_{m}).

We assume here that the lower-level centers
\bm{\mathbf{\mu}}_{mk}
and coefficients
\bm{\mathbf{c}}_{mk}
are tied via their respective upper-level precision matrices
\bm{\mathbf{\Lambda}}_{m}
and
\bm{\mathbf{V}}_{m}
.

Finally, given all necessary parameters, the upper- and lower-level labels
\bm{\mathbf{h}}_{n},\bm{\mathbf{z}}_{n}
and the data pairs
\bm{\mathbf{x}}_{n},\bm{\mathbf{y}}_{n}
are sampled according to the likelihoods

\displaystyle\bm{\mathbf{h}}_{n}\sim\operatorname{Cat}(\bm{\mathbf{\omega}}(\bm{\mathbf{t}})),\,\,\bm{\mathbf{z}}_{n}\sim\operatorname{Cat}(\bm{\mathbf{\pi}}(\bm{\mathbf{s}}),\bm{\mathbf{h}}_{n}),

\displaystyle\bm{\mathbf{x}}_{n}\sim\operatorname{N}(\bm{\mathbf{\mu}}_{h_{n},z_{n}},\bm{\mathbf{\Lambda}}_{h_{n}}),\,\,\bm{\mathbf{y}}_{n}\sim\operatorname{N}(\bm{\mathbf{A}}_{h_{n}}\bm{\mathbf{x}}_{n}+\bm{\mathbf{c}}_{h_{n},z_{n}},\bm{\mathbf{V}}_{h_{n}}).

Notice how the upper-level generative process resembles the structure of
ILR
. However, in this model, the meta-activations
(\bm{\mathbf{\tau}}_{m},\bm{\mathbf{\Lambda}}_{m})
induce another hierarchy over the lower-level multi-modal activation centers
\bm{\mathbf{\mu}}_{mk}
. Moreover, the upper-level
DP
only accounts for the shared slope of the local regression units
\bm{\mathbf{A}}_{m}
, whereas the shift coefficients
\bm{\mathbf{c}}_{mk}
are induced at the lower level, as they are influenced by the individual activation centers
\bm{\mathbf{\mu}}_{mk}
.

Similar to the procedure in Section
III
, we aim to infer a posterior over the parameters of this model given a set of inputs
\bm{\mathbf{X}}=\{\bm{\mathbf{x}}_{1},\dots,\bm{\mathbf{x}}_{N}\}
and outputs
\bm{\mathbf{Y}}=\{\bm{\mathbf{y}}_{1},\dots,\bm{\mathbf{y}}_{N}\}
. To that end, we derive a structured variational Bayes expectation-maximization algorithm for this model. We start the derivation by defining the complete data likelihood, the infinite prior, and the mean-field posterior factorization.

Complete Data Likelihood.
The likelihood model is a two-level precision-tied joint density over the observations
(\bm{\mathbf{X}},\bm{\mathbf{Y}})
and the one-hot upper- and lower-labels
(\bm{\mathbf{H}},\bm{\mathbf{Z}})

\displaystyle p(.)

\displaystyle=p(\bm{\mathbf{H}})\,p(\bm{\mathbf{Z}}\,|\,\bm{\mathbf{H}})\,p(\bm{\mathbf{X}}\,|\,\bm{\mathbf{H}},\bm{\mathbf{Z}})\,p(\bm{\mathbf{Y}}\,|\,\bm{\mathbf{H}},\bm{\mathbf{Z}},\bm{\mathbf{X}})

\displaystyle=\begin{aligned} &\prod_{n=1}^{N}\operatorname{Cat}(\bm{\mathbf{h}}_{n}\,|\,\bm{\mathbf{\omega}}(\bm{\mathbf{t}}))\\
&\times\prod_{n=1}^{N}\operatorname{Cat}(\bm{\mathbf{z}}_{n}\,|\,\bm{\mathbf{\pi}}(\bm{\mathbf{s}}),\bm{\mathbf{h}}_{n})\\
&\times\prod_{n=1}^{N}\prod_{m=1}^{\infty}\prod_{k=1}^{\infty}\operatorname{N}(\bm{\mathbf{x}}_{n}\,|\,\bm{\mathbf{\mu}}_{mk},\bm{\mathbf{\Lambda}}_{m})^{z_{nk}\times h_{nm}}\\
&\times\prod_{n=1}^{N}\prod_{m=1}^{\infty}\prod_{k=1}^{\infty}\operatorname{N}(\bm{\mathbf{y}}_{n}\,|\,\bm{\mathbf{A}}_{m}\bm{\mathbf{x}}_{n}+\bm{\mathbf{c}}_{mk},\bm{\mathbf{V}}_{m})^{z_{nk}\times h_{nm}},\end{aligned}

where the conditioning of the lower-level labels
\bm{\mathbf{z}}_{n}
on the upper-level labels
\bm{\mathbf{h}}_{n}
manifests as a one-hot selector of the corresponding lower-level categoricals

\displaystyle\operatorname{Cat}(\bm{\mathbf{z}}_{n}\,|\,\bm{\mathbf{\pi}}(\bm{\mathbf{s}}),\bm{\mathbf{h}}_{n})

\displaystyle=\prod_{m=1}^{\infty}\operatorname{Cat}(\bm{\mathbf{z}}_{n}\,|\,\bm{\mathbf{\pi}}_{m}(\bm{\mathbf{s}}_{m}))^{h_{nm}}

\displaystyle=\prod_{m=1}^{\infty}\prod_{k=1}^{\infty}\pi_{mk}^{z_{nk}\times h_{nm}}.

Infinite Conjugate Prior.
We assume a factorized two-level precision-tied conjugate infinite mixture prior

p(\bm{\mathbf{t}},\bm{\mathbf{s}},\bm{\mathbf{\mu}},\bm{\mathbf{\tau}},\bm{\mathbf{\Lambda}},\bm{\mathbf{A}},\bm{\mathbf{c}},\bm{\mathbf{V}})=\begin{aligned} &p(\bm{\mathbf{t}})\,p(\bm{\mathbf{s}})\\
&\times p(\bm{\mathbf{\mu}}\,|\,\bm{\mathbf{\tau}},\bm{\mathbf{\Lambda}})\,p(\bm{\mathbf{\tau}}\,|\,\bm{\mathbf{\Lambda}})\,p(\bm{\mathbf{\Lambda}})\\
&\times p(\bm{\mathbf{A}}\,|\,\bm{\mathbf{V}})\,p(\bm{\mathbf{c}}|\bm{\mathbf{V}})\,p(\bm{\mathbf{V}}).\end{aligned}

The meta-activation prior is a normal-Wishart distribution over the meta-centers
\bm{\mathbf{\tau}}_{m}
and precision matrices
\bm{\mathbf{\Lambda}}_{m}

p(\bm{\mathbf{\tau}}\,|\,\bm{\mathbf{\Lambda}})p(\bm{\mathbf{\Lambda}})=\prod_{m=1}^{\infty}\operatorname{N}(\bm{\mathbf{\tau}}_{m}\,|\,\bm{\mathbf{m}}_{0},\lambda_{0}\bm{\mathbf{\Lambda}}_{m})\operatorname{W}(\bm{\mathbf{\Lambda}}_{m}\,|\,\bm{\mathbf{\Psi}}_{0},\nu_{0}),

while the activation centers
\bm{\mathbf{\mu}}_{mk}
are sampled from a conditional normal distribution

p(\bm{\mathbf{\mu}}\,|\,\bm{\mathbf{\tau}},\bm{\mathbf{\Lambda}})=\prod_{m=1}^{\infty}\prod_{k=1}^{\infty}\operatorname{N}(\bm{\mathbf{\mu}}_{mk}\,|\,\bm{\mathbf{\tau}}_{m},\kappa_{0}\bm{\mathbf{\Lambda}}_{m}).

The mappings
\bm{\mathbf{A}}_{m}
and precision matrices
\bm{\mathbf{V}}_{m}
are sampled form a matrix-normal-Wishart

p(\bm{\mathbf{A}}\,|\,\bm{\mathbf{V}})p(\bm{\mathbf{V}})=\prod_{m=1}^{\infty}\operatorname{MN}(\bm{\mathbf{A}}_{m}\,|\,\bm{\mathbf{M}}_{0},\bm{\mathbf{K}}_{0},\bm{\mathbf{V}}_{m})\operatorname{W}(\bm{\mathbf{V}}_{m}\,|\,\bm{\mathbf{\Phi}}_{0},\eta_{0}).

while the biases
\bm{\mathbf{c}}_{mk}
are drawn from a
K
-tied conditional normal distribution

p(\bm{\mathbf{c}}\,|\,\bm{\mathbf{V}})=\prod_{m=1}^{\infty}\prod_{k=1}^{\infty}\operatorname{MN}(\bm{\mathbf{c}}_{mk}\,|\,\bm{\mathbf{\theta}}_{0},\rho_{0}\bm{\mathbf{V}}_{m}).

Finally, the stick-breaking priors
p(\bm{\mathbf{t}}
,
\bm{\mathbf{s}})
follow the definitions from Section
III

\displaystyle p(\bm{\mathbf{t}})

\displaystyle=\prod_{m=1}^{\infty}\operatorname{Beta}(t_{m}\,|\,1,\beta_{0}),

\displaystyle p(\bm{\mathbf{s}})

\displaystyle=\prod_{m=1}^{\infty}\prod_{k=1}^{\infty}\operatorname{Beta}(s_{mk}\,|\,1,\alpha_{0}).

Truncated Mean-Field Posterior.
We assume a structured decomposition of the posterior that leads to conjugate computation while maintaining the dependencies between the discrete labels, the input activations, and the regression parameters, respectively. Moreover, we apply the truncation scheme from
[
59
]
to establish a tractable posterior approximation while maintaining an infinite prior

\displaystyle p(.\,|\,\mathcal{D})

\displaystyle\approx q(\bm{\mathbf{H}})\,q(\bm{\mathbf{Z}}\,|\,\bm{\mathbf{H}})\,q(\bm{\mathbf{t}})\,q(\bm{\mathbf{s}})\,q(\bm{\mathbf{\mu}},\bm{\mathbf{\tau}},\bm{\mathbf{\Lambda}})\,q(\bm{\mathbf{A}},\bm{\mathbf{c}},\bm{\mathbf{V}})

\displaystyle\hskip-14.22636pt=\begin{aligned} &\prod_{n=1}^{N}\operatorname{Cat}(\bm{\mathbf{h}}_{n}\,|\,\bm{\mathbf{g}}_{n})\prod_{n=1}^{N}\operatorname{Cat}(\bm{\mathbf{z}}_{n}\,|\,\bm{\mathbf{r}}_{n},\bm{\mathbf{h}}_{n})\\
&\times\prod_{m=1}^{M-1}\operatorname{Beta}(t_{m}\,|\,\delta_{m},\beta_{m})\\
&\times\prod_{m=1}^{M-1}\prod_{k=1}^{K-1}\operatorname{Beta}(s_{mk}\,|\,\gamma_{mk},\alpha_{mk})\\
&\times\prod_{m=1}^{M}\operatorname{N}(\bm{\mathbf{\tau}}_{m}\,|\,\bm{\mathbf{m}}_{m},\lambda_{m}\bm{\mathbf{\Lambda}}_{m})\operatorname{W}(\bm{\mathbf{\Lambda}}_{m}\,|\,\bm{\mathbf{\Psi}}_{m},\nu_{m})\\
&\times\prod_{m=1}^{M}\operatorname{MN}(\bm{\mathbf{A}}_{m}\,|\,\bm{\mathbf{M}}_{m},\bm{\mathbf{K}}_{m},\bm{\mathbf{V}}_{m})\operatorname{W}(\bm{\mathbf{V}}_{m}\,|\,\bm{\mathbf{\Phi}}_{m},\eta_{m})\\
&\times\prod_{m=1}^{M}\prod_{k=1}^{K}\operatorname{N}(\bm{\mathbf{\mu}}_{mk}\,|\,\bm{\mathbf{\tau}}_{m},\kappa_{mk}\bm{\mathbf{\Lambda}}_{m})\operatorname{N}(\bm{\mathbf{c}}_{mk}\,|\,\bm{\mathbf{\theta}}_{mk},\rho_{mk}\bm{\mathbf{V}}_{k}),\end{aligned}

where
\bm{\mathbf{g}}_{n}
and
\bm{\mathbf{r}}_{n}
are the upper- and lower-level posterior responsibilities, respectively.

Variational Expectation Step.
The E-step computes the joint posterior categorical over joint labels
\bm{\mathbf{H}}
and
\bm{\mathbf{Z}}

\displaystyle\log q(\bm{\mathbf{Z}}\,|\,\bm{\mathbf{H}})

\displaystyle=\begin{aligned} &\mathbb{E}_{q(\bm{\mathbf{s}})}\left[\log p(\bm{\mathbf{Z}}\,|\,\bm{\mathbf{H}})\right]\\
&+\mathbb{E}_{q(\bm{\mathbf{\mu}},\bm{\mathbf{\tau}},\bm{\mathbf{\Lambda}})}\left[\log p(\bm{\mathbf{X}}\,|\,\bm{\mathbf{H}},\bm{\mathbf{Z}})\right]\\
&+\mathbb{E}_{q(\bm{\mathbf{A}},\bm{\mathbf{c}},\bm{\mathbf{V}})}\left[\log p(\bm{\mathbf{Y}}\,|\,\bm{\mathbf{H}},\bm{\mathbf{Z}},\bm{\mathbf{X}})\right]+\const\end{aligned}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=1}^{N}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\sum_{k=1}^{K}h_{nm}z_{nmk}\log r_{nmk},

\displaystyle\log q(\bm{\mathbf{H}})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{q(\bm{\mathbf{t}})}\left[\log p(\bm{\mathbf{H}})\right]+\log q(\bm{\mathbf{Z}}\,|\,\bm{\mathbf{H}})+\const

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=1}^{N}\sum_{m=1}^{M}h_{nm}\log g_{nm},

where these expectations can computed in a similar fashion to Section
III
. More details can be found in the appendix.

Variational Maximization Step.
The M-step updates the variational gating, activations, and regression parameters

\displaystyle\log q(\bm{\mathbf{t}})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{q(\bm{\mathbf{H}})}\left[\log p(\bm{\mathbf{H}})\right]+\log p(\bm{\mathbf{t}})+\const,

\displaystyle\log q(\bm{\mathbf{s}})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{q(\bm{\mathbf{H}},\bm{\mathbf{Z}})}\left[\log p(\bm{\mathbf{Z}}\,|\,\bm{\mathbf{H}})\right]+\log p(\bm{\mathbf{s}})+\const,

\displaystyle\log q(\bm{\mathbf{\mu}})

\displaystyle=\begin{aligned} &\mathbb{E}_{q(\bm{\mathbf{H}},\bm{\mathbf{Z}},\bm{\mathbf{\tau}},\bm{\mathbf{\Lambda}})}\left[\log p(\bm{\mathbf{X}}\,|\,\bm{\mathbf{H}},\bm{\mathbf{Z}})\right]\\
&+\mathbb{E}_{q(\bm{\mathbf{\tau}},\bm{\mathbf{\Lambda}})}\left[\log p(\bm{\mathbf{\mu}}\,|\,\bm{\mathbf{\tau}},\bm{\mathbf{\Lambda}})\right]+\const,\end{aligned}

\displaystyle\log q(\bm{\mathbf{\tau}},\bm{\mathbf{\Lambda}})

\displaystyle=\begin{aligned} &\mathbb{E}_{q(\bm{\mathbf{H}},\bm{\mathbf{Z}},\bm{\mathbf{\mu}})}\left[\log p(\bm{\mathbf{X}}\,|\,\bm{\mathbf{H}},\bm{\mathbf{Z}})\right]+\log p(\bm{\mathbf{\tau}},\bm{\mathbf{\Lambda}})\\
&+\mathbb{E}_{q(\bm{\mathbf{\mu}})}\left[\log p(\bm{\mathbf{\mu}}\,|\,\bm{\mathbf{\tau}},\bm{\mathbf{\Lambda}})\right]+\const,\end{aligned}

\displaystyle\log q(\bm{\mathbf{c}})

\displaystyle=\begin{aligned} &\mathbb{E}_{q(\bm{\mathbf{H}},\bm{\mathbf{Z}},\bm{\mathbf{A}},\bm{\mathbf{V}})}\left[\log p(\bm{\mathbf{Y}}\,|\,\bm{\mathbf{H}},\bm{\mathbf{Z}},\bm{\mathbf{X}})\right]\\
&+\mathbb{E}_{q(\bm{\mathbf{V}})}\left[\log p(\bm{\mathbf{c}}\,|\,\bm{\mathbf{V}})\right]+\const,\end{aligned}

\displaystyle\log q(\bm{\mathbf{A}},\bm{\mathbf{V}})

\displaystyle=\begin{aligned} &\mathbb{E}_{q(\bm{\mathbf{H}},\bm{\mathbf{Z}},\bm{\mathbf{c}})}\left[\log p(\bm{\mathbf{Y}}\,|\,\bm{\mathbf{H}},\bm{\mathbf{Z}},\bm{\mathbf{X}})\right]+\log p(\bm{\mathbf{A}},\bm{\mathbf{V}})\\
&+\mathbb{E}_{q(\bm{\mathbf{c}})}\left[\log p(\bm{\mathbf{c}}\,|\,\bm{\mathbf{V}})\right]+\const.\end{aligned}

As previously stated, these updates resemble posterior computations weighted by the responsibilities
g_{nm}=\mathbb{E}[h_{nm}]
and
r_{nmk}=\mathbb{E}[z_{nmk}]
. The appendix provides further details.

IV-A

Posterior Predictive Distribution

Prediction with
HILR
is akin to that with
ILR
, as described in Section
III-B
. We briefly state the conditional predictive for a component
\bm{\mathbf{h}}=m
and an activation
\bm{\mathbf{z}}=k
as

\displaystyle p(\hat{\bm{\mathbf{y}}}\,|\,\hat{\bm{\mathbf{x}}},\hat{\bm{\mathbf{h}}}\!=\!m,\hat{\bm{\mathbf{z}}}\!=\!k,\mathcal{D})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{q(\bm{\mathbf{A}},\bm{\mathbf{c}},\bm{\mathbf{V}})}\left[p(\hat{\bm{\mathbf{y}}}\,|\,\hat{\bm{\mathbf{x}}},\bm{\mathbf{A}}_{m},\bm{\mathbf{c}}_{mk},\bm{\mathbf{V}}_{m})\right]

\displaystyle=\operatorname{T}\left(\bm{\mathbf{M}}_{m}\hat{\bm{\mathbf{x}}}+\bm{\mathbf{\theta}}_{mk},a_{mk}\bm{\mathbf{\Phi}}_{m},\eta_{m}+1\right),

where the computation of
a_{mk}
is similar to that in Section
III-B
. Further, the weight of the
k-
th activation of the
m-
th component is computed as follows

\displaystyle p(\hat{\bm{\mathbf{x}}},\hat{\bm{\mathbf{h}}}\!=\!m,\hat{\bm{\mathbf{z}}}\!=\!k\,|\,\mathcal{D})

\displaystyle\propto\begin{aligned} &\mathbb{E}_{q(\bm{\mathbf{t}})}\left[p(\hat{\bm{\mathbf{h}}}\!=\!m\,|\,\bm{\mathbf{\omega}}(\bm{\mathbf{t}}))\right]\\
&\times\mathbb{E}_{q(\bm{\mathbf{s}}_{m})}\left[p(\hat{\bm{\mathbf{z}}}\!=\!k\,|\,\bm{\mathbf{\pi}}_{m}(\bm{\mathbf{s}}_{m}))\right]\\
&\times\mathbb{E}_{q(\bm{\mathbf{\mu}},\bm{\mathbf{\tau}},\bm{\mathbf{\Lambda}})}\left[p(\hat{\bm{\mathbf{x}}}\,|\,\bm{\mathbf{\mu}}_{mk},\bm{\mathbf{\Lambda}}_{m})\right]\end{aligned}

\displaystyle\hskip-56.9055pt=\begin{aligned} &\frac{\delta_{m}}{\delta_{m}+\beta_{m}}\prod_{l=1}^{m-1}\left(1-\frac{\delta_{l}}{\delta_{l}+\beta_{l}}\right)\\
&\times\frac{\gamma_{mk}}{\gamma_{mk}+\alpha_{mk}}\prod_{l=1}^{k-1}\left(1-\frac{\gamma_{ml}}{\gamma_{ml}+\alpha_{ml}}\right)\\
&\times\operatorname{T}\left(\bm{\mathbf{\mu}}_{mk},\frac{\kappa_{mk}}{1+\kappa_{mk}}\bm{\mathbf{\Psi}}_{m},\nu_{m}+1\right).\end{aligned}

V
Empirical Evaluation

We evaluate the presented models on a range of tasks. Our goals are (1) to highlight the advantages of
ILR
and
HILR
, such as dealing with out-of-distribution predictions, recovering an input-dependent noise function, hierarchical gating, sharing parameters, and the ability to perform Bayesian sequential updates, (2) to benchmark the models on high dimensional datasets from real robots, and (3) to deploy the models in a real-world scenario to further empirically demonstrate its validity. A reference implementation can be found under
https://github.com/hanyas/mimo
.

VI
Conclusion

In this paper, we presented two probabilistic hierarchical local regression models,
ILR
and
HILR
, and derived an efficient variational inference technique for data-driven learning. These representations are based on infinite mixtures from Bayesian nonparametrics. We situate our contributions as the next iteration in a large family of local linear regression techniques such as
RFWR
,
LWPR
, and
LGR
. We have shown that placing Dirichlet process priors on Bayesian mixtures of local regression units can regularize model complexity with minor loss in performance and without relying on heuristics. Moreover, we have highlighted the advantage of the two-level architecture of
HILR
in compressing the learned models and reducing the number of active experts. Empirical evaluation indicates that the models outperform
LWPR
and
LGR
, and are competitive with sparse
GPR
and products of experts. Finally, we have empirically confirmed the practicality of this approach for online inverse dynamics control on a Barrett-WAM robot.

Nonetheless, these presented concepts still suffer from multiple drawbacks. The mean-field assumption is a source of significant errors in posterior inference. Collapsed formulations of Dirichlet process priors promise better approximations
[
76
]
. In addition, Bayesian mixture models are influenced by a large number of hyperparameters, which cannot be directly optimized via empirical Bayes
[
77
]
, leading to lower predictive performance when compared to optimized
GPR
. Nonetheless, the
ELBO
offers an objective for hyperparameter optimization. Naive gradient-based techniques have proven too brittle due to their reliance on Euclidean distance metrics. A natural-gradient approach appears to be a suitable alternative in the future.

Further development of hierarchical local regression may focus on treating
ILR
and
HILR
as layers in a multi-layered representation. This extension would allow the models to benefit from intermediate nonlinear projections into high dimensional spaces that have proven powerful in deep neural networks. Another practical consideration is to incorporate physical inductive biases such as inverse dynamics
[
78
]
to facilitate learning meaningful quantities.

and

E-Step of Infinite Linear Regression

\displaystyle\log q(\bm{\mathbf{Z}})

\displaystyle=\begin{aligned} &\mathbb{E}_{q(\bm{\mathbf{s}})}\left[\log p(\bm{\mathbf{Z}}|\bm{\mathbf{\pi}}(\bm{\mathbf{s}}))\right]\\
&+\mathbb{E}_{q(\bm{\mathbf{\mu}},\bm{\mathbf{\Lambda}})}\left[\log p(\bm{\mathbf{X}}|\bm{\mathbf{Z}})\right]\\
&+\mathbb{E}_{q(\bm{\mathbf{A}},\bm{\mathbf{c}},\bm{\mathbf{V}})}\left[\log p(\bm{\mathbf{Y}}|\bm{\mathbf{Z}},\bm{\mathbf{X}})\right]+\const\end{aligned}

\displaystyle\hskip-28.45274pt=\begin{aligned} &\const+\sum_{n=1}^{N}\Big[\mathbb{E}_{q(\bm{\mathbf{s}})}\left[\log\operatorname{Cat}(\bm{\mathbf{z}}_{n}|\bm{\mathbf{\pi}}(\bm{\mathbf{s}}))\right]\\
&\!+\!\sum_{k=1}^{K}z_{nk}\mathbb{E}_{q(\bm{\mathbf{\mu}},\bm{\mathbf{\Lambda}})}\left[\log\operatorname{N}(\bm{\mathbf{x}}_{n}|\bm{\mathbf{\mu}}_{k},\bm{\mathbf{\Lambda}}_{k})\right]\\
&\!+\!\sum_{k=1}^{K}z_{nk}\mathbb{E}_{q(\bm{\mathbf{A}},\bm{\mathbf{c}},\bm{\mathbf{V}})}\left[\log\operatorname{N}(\bm{\mathbf{y}}_{n}|\bm{\mathbf{A}}_{k}\bm{\mathbf{x}}_{n}+\bm{\mathbf{c}}_{k},\bm{\mathbf{V}}_{k})\right]\Big]\end{aligned}

\displaystyle\hskip-28.45274pt=\sum_{n=1}^{N}\sum_{k=1}^{K}z_{nk}\log r_{nk},

where

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{q(\bm{\mathbf{s}})}\left[\log\operatorname{Cat}(\bm{\mathbf{z}}_{n}|\bm{\mathbf{\pi}}(\bm{\mathbf{s}}))\right]

\displaystyle\!=\!\begin{aligned} &\sum_{k=1}^{K}z_{nk}\mathbb{E}_{q(s_{k})}\left[\log s_{k}\right]\\
&+\sum_{k=1}^{K}z_{nk}\mathbb{E}_{q(s_{k})}\left[\sum_{l=1}^{k-1}\log(1-s_{l})\right].\end{aligned}

The individual expectations of the
log-sticks
under the beta posteriors are given by

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{q(s_{k})}\left[\log s_{k}\right]

\displaystyle=\Psi\left(\gamma_{k}\right)-\Psi\left(\gamma_{k}+\alpha_{k}\right),

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{q(s_{k})}\left[\log(1-s_{k})\right]

\displaystyle=\Psi\left(\alpha_{k}\right)-\Psi\left(\gamma_{k}+\alpha_{k}\right),

where
\Psi
is the Digamma function.

M-Step of Infinite Linear Regression

\displaystyle\log q(\bm{\mathbf{s}})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{q(\bm{\mathbf{Z}})}\left[\log p(\bm{\mathbf{Z}}|\bm{\mathbf{\pi}}(\bm{\mathbf{s}}))\right]+\log p(\bm{\mathbf{s}})+\const

\displaystyle=\begin{aligned} &\sum_{n=1}^{N}\sum_{k=1}^{K}r_{nk}\log\left[s_{k}\prod_{l=1}^{k-1}(1-s_{l})\right]\\
&+\sum_{k=1}^{K-1}\log\operatorname{Beta}(s_{k}|1,\alpha_{0})+\const,\end{aligned}

\displaystyle\log q(\bm{\mathbf{\mu}},\bm{\mathbf{\Lambda}})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{q(\bm{\mathbf{Z}})}\left[\log p(\bm{\mathbf{X}}|\bm{\mathbf{Z}})\right]+\log p(\bm{\mathbf{\mu}},\bm{\mathbf{\Lambda}})+\const

\displaystyle=\begin{aligned} &\sum_{n=1}^{N}\sum_{k=1}^{K}r_{nk}\log\operatorname{N}(\bm{\mathbf{x}}_{n}|\bm{\mathbf{\mu}}_{k},\bm{\mathbf{\Lambda}}_{k})\\
&+\sum_{k=1}^{K}\log\operatorname{N}(\bm{\mathbf{\mu}}_{k}|\bm{\mathbf{m}}_{0},\kappa_{0}\bm{\mathbf{\Lambda}}_{k})\\
&+\sum_{k=1}^{K}\log\operatorname{W}(\bm{\mathbf{\Lambda}}_{k}|\bm{\mathbf{\Psi}}_{0},\nu_{0})+\const,\end{aligned}

\displaystyle\log q(\bm{\mathbf{A}},\bm{\mathbf{c}},\bm{\mathbf{V}})

\displaystyle=\begin{aligned} &\mathbb{E}_{q(\bm{\mathbf{Z}})}\left[\log p(\bm{\mathbf{Y}}|\bm{\mathbf{Z}},\bm{\mathbf{X}})\right]\\
&+\log p(\bm{\mathbf{A}},\bm{\mathbf{c}},\bm{\mathbf{V}})+\const\end{aligned}

\displaystyle=\begin{aligned} &\sum_{n=1}^{N}\sum_{k=1}^{K}r_{nk}\log\operatorname{N}(\bm{\mathbf{y}}_{n}|\bm{\mathbf{A}}_{k}\bm{\mathbf{x}}_{n}+\bm{\mathbf{c}}_{k},\bm{\mathbf{V}}_{k})\\
&+\sum_{k=1}^{K}\log\operatorname{MN}(\bm{\mathbf{A}}_{k}|\bm{\mathbf{M}}_{0},\bm{\mathbf{K}}_{0},\bm{\mathbf{V}}_{k})\\
&+\sum_{k=1}^{K}\log\operatorname{N}(\bm{\mathbf{c}}_{k}|\bm{\mathbf{\theta}}_{0},\rho_{0}\bm{\mathbf{V}}_{k})\\
&+\sum_{k=1}^{K}\log\operatorname{W}(\bm{\mathbf{V}}_{k}|\bm{\mathbf{\Phi}}_{0},\eta_{0})+\const,\end{aligned}

where
r_{nk}=\mathbb{E}_{q(\bm{\mathbf{Z}})}\left[z_{nk}\right]
are the expected responsibilities computed in the E-step. Further details on performing these updates via general exponential family recipes are provided in the upcoming sections.

\displaystyle\log q(\bm{\mathbf{t}})\begin{aligned} &=\mathbb{E}_{q(\bm{\mathbf{H}})}\left[\log p(\bm{\mathbf{H}}|\bm{\mathbf{\omega}}(\bm{\mathbf{t}}))\right]+\log p(\bm{\mathbf{t}})+\const\\

&+\mathbb{E}_{q(\bm{\mathbf{\mu}})}\left[\log p(\bm{\mathbf{\mu}}|\bm{\mathbf{\tau}},\bm{\mathbf{\Lambda}})\right]+\const\end{aligned}\\
&=\begin{aligned} &\sum_{n=1}^{N}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\hat{g}\,\hat{r}\,\mathbb{E}_{q(\bm{\mathbf{\mu}})}\left[\log\operatorname{N}(\bm{\mathbf{x}}_{n}|\bm{\mathbf{\mu}}_{mk},\bm{\mathbf{\Lambda}}_{m})\right]\\

&+\sum_{m=1}^{M}\log\operatorname{W}(\bm{\mathbf{\Lambda}}_{m}|\bm{\mathbf{\Psi}}_{0},\nu_{0})\\

&+\const,\end{aligned}\end{aligned}

\displaystyle\log q(\bm{\mathbf{c}})\begin{aligned} &=\begin{aligned} &\mathbb{E}_{q(\bm{\mathbf{H}},\bm{\mathbf{Z}},\bm{\mathbf{A}},\bm{\mathbf{V}})}\left[\log p(\bm{\mathbf{Y}}|\bm{\mathbf{H}},\bm{\mathbf{Z}},\bm{\mathbf{X}})\right]\\
&+\mathbb{E}_{q(\bm{\mathbf{V}})}\left[\log p(\bm{\mathbf{c}}|\bm{\mathbf{V}})\right]+\const\end{aligned}\\
&=\begin{aligned} &\sum_{n=1}^{N}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\hat{g}\,\hat{r}\,\\

&+\sum_{m=1}^{M}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\mathbb{E}_{q(\bm{\mathbf{V}})}\left[\operatorname{MN}(\bm{\mathbf{c}}_{mk}|\bm{\mathbf{\theta}}_{0},\rho_{0}\bm{\mathbf{V}}_{m})\right]\\

Appendix B

Our work mainly considers random variables with probability density functions belonging to the exponential family. The unified minimal parameterization of this class of distributions lends itself to convenient and efficient posterior computation when paired with conjugate priors.

We assume the natural form for a probability density of a random variable
\bm{\mathbf{x}}

f(\bm{\mathbf{x}}|\bm{\mathbf{\eta}})=h(\bm{\mathbf{x}})\exp\left[\bm{\mathbf{\eta}}\cdot\bm{\mathbf{t}}(\bm{\mathbf{x}})-a(\bm{\mathbf{\eta}})\right],

where
h(\bm{\mathbf{x}})
is the base measure,
\bm{\mathbf{\eta}}
are the natural parameters,
\bm{\mathbf{t}}(\bm{\mathbf{x}})
are the sufficient statistics and
a(\bm{\mathbf{\eta}})
is the log-partition function, or log-normalizer. Following the same notation, a conjugate prior
g(\bm{\mathbf{\eta}}|\bm{\mathbf{\lambda}})
to the likelihood
f(\bm{\mathbf{x}}|\bm{\mathbf{\eta}})
has the form

g(\bm{\mathbf{\eta}}|\bm{\mathbf{\lambda}})=h(\bm{\mathbf{\eta}})\exp\left[\bm{\mathbf{\lambda}}\cdot\bm{\mathbf{t}}(\bm{\mathbf{\eta}})-a(\bm{\mathbf{\lambda}})\right],

with prior sufficient statistics
\bm{\mathbf{t}}(\bm{\mathbf{\eta}})=[\bm{\mathbf{\eta}},\,-a(\bm{\mathbf{\eta}})]^{\top}
and hyperparameters
\bm{\mathbf{\lambda}}=[\bm{\mathbf{\alpha}},\,\bm{\mathbf{\beta}}]^{\top}
. By applying Bayes’ rule, we can directly infer the posterior
q(\bm{\mathbf{\eta}}|\bm{\mathbf{x}})

\displaystyle q(\bm{\mathbf{\eta}}|\bm{\mathbf{x}})

\displaystyle\propto f(\bm{\mathbf{x}}|\bm{\mathbf{\eta}})g(\bm{\mathbf{\eta}}|\bm{\mathbf{\lambda}})

\displaystyle\propto\exp\left[\bm{\mathbf{\rho}}(\bm{\mathbf{x}},\bm{\mathbf{\lambda}})\cdot\bm{\mathbf{t}}(\bm{\mathbf{\eta}})-a(\bm{\mathbf{\rho}})\right],

where the posterior natural parameters
\bm{\mathbf{\rho}}(\bm{\mathbf{x}},\bm{\mathbf{\lambda}})
are a function of the likelihood sufficient statistics
\bm{\mathbf{t}}(\bm{\mathbf{x}})
and prior hyperparameters
[\bm{\mathbf{\alpha}},\,\bm{\mathbf{\beta}}]

\bm{\mathbf{\rho}}(\bm{\mathbf{x}},\bm{\mathbf{\lambda}})=\left[\bm{\mathbf{\alpha}}+\bm{\mathbf{t}}(\bm{\mathbf{x}}),~\bm{\mathbf{\beta}}+\bm{\mathbf{1}}\right]^{\top}.

The structure of the resulting posterior reveals a simple recipe for data-driven inference. By moving into the natural space, the posterior parameters are computed by combining the prior hyperparameters with the likelihood’s sufficient statistics and log-partition function. By definition, every exponential family distribution has a minimal natural parameterization that leads to a unique decomposition of these quantities
[
79
]
.

Appendix C

We present an outline of all M-step updates. We use an adapted form of the exponential natural parameterization, as it offers a clear methodology on how to derive and implement such updates for all relevant distributions.

Gaussian with Normal-Wishart Prior

A weighted Gaussian likelihood over a random variable
\bm{\mathbf{x}}\in\mathds{R}^{d}
has the following precision-based parameterization

\displaystyle p(\bm{\mathbf{X}}|\bm{\mathbf{\mu}},\bm{\mathbf{\Lambda}})

\displaystyle=\prod_{n=1}^{N}\operatorname{N}(\bm{\mathbf{x}}_{n}|\bm{\mathbf{\mu}},\bm{\mathbf{\Lambda}})^{w_{n}}

\displaystyle\propto\exp\left\{\begin{bmatrix}\bm{\mathbf{\Lambda}}\bm{\mathbf{\mu}}\\[5.0pt]
\bm{\mathbf{\mu}}^{\top}\bm{\mathbf{\Lambda}}\bm{\mathbf{\mu}}\\[5.0pt]
\bm{\mathbf{\Lambda}}\\[5.0pt]
\log|\bm{\mathbf{\Lambda}}|\end{bmatrix}\!\cdot\!\begin{bmatrix}\sum_{n=1}^{N}w_{n}\bm{\mathbf{x}}_{n}\\[5.0pt]
-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{n=1}^{N}w_{n}\\[5.0pt]
-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{n=1}^{N}w_{n}\bm{\mathbf{x}}_{n}\bm{\mathbf{x}}_{n}^{\top}\\[5.0pt]
\frac{1}{2}\sum_{n=1}^{N}w_{n}\end{bmatrix}\right\},

where
w_{n}
are the importance weights. The conjugate prior
p(\bm{\mathbf{\mu}},\bm{\mathbf{\Lambda}})
is a normal-Wishart distribution with zero mean

\displaystyle p(\bm{\mathbf{\mu}},\bm{\mathbf{\Lambda}})

\displaystyle=\operatorname{N}(\bm{\mathbf{\mu}}|\bm{\mathbf{0}},\kappa_{0}\bm{\mathbf{\Lambda}})\operatorname{W}(\bm{\mathbf{\Lambda}}|\bm{\mathbf{\Psi}}_{0},\nu_{0})

\displaystyle\propto\exp\left\{\begin{bmatrix}\bm{\mathbf{0}}\\[5.0pt]
-\frac{1}{2}\kappa_{0}\\[5.0pt]
-\frac{1}{2}\bm{\mathbf{\Psi}}_{0}^{-1}\\[5.0pt]
\frac{1}{2}(\nu_{0}-d)\end{bmatrix}\!\cdot\!\begin{bmatrix}\bm{\mathbf{\Lambda}}\bm{\mathbf{\mu}}\\[5.0pt]
\bm{\mathbf{\mu}}^{\top}\bm{\mathbf{\Lambda}}\bm{\mathbf{\mu}}\\[5.0pt]
\bm{\mathbf{\Lambda}}\\[5.0pt]
\log|\bm{\mathbf{\Lambda}}|\end{bmatrix}\right\}.

The resulting posterior
q(\bm{\mathbf{\mu}},\bm{\mathbf{\Lambda}})
is also a normal-Wishart

\displaystyle q(\bm{\mathbf{\mu}},\bm{\mathbf{\Lambda}})

\displaystyle\!=\!\operatorname{N}(\bm{\mathbf{\mu}}|\bm{\mathbf{m}},\kappa\bm{\mathbf{\Lambda}})\operatorname{W}(\bm{\mathbf{\Lambda}}|\bm{\mathbf{\Psi}},\nu)

\displaystyle\!\propto\exp\left\{\!\begin{bmatrix}\sum_{n=1}^{N}w_{n}\bm{\mathbf{x}}_{n}\\[5.0pt]
-\frac{1}{2}(\kappa_{0}+\sum_{n=1}^{N}w_{n})\\[5.0pt]
-\frac{1}{2}(\bm{\mathbf{\Psi}}_{0}^{-1}+\sum_{n=1}^{N}w_{n}\bm{\mathbf{x}}_{n}\bm{\mathbf{x}}_{n}^{\top})\\[5.0pt]
\frac{1}{2}(\nu_{0}-d+\sum_{n=1}^{N}w_{n})\end{bmatrix}\!\cdot\!\begin{bmatrix}\bm{\mathbf{\Lambda}}\bm{\mathbf{\mu}}\\[5.0pt]
\bm{\mathbf{\mu}}^{\top}\bm{\mathbf{\Lambda}}\bm{\mathbf{\mu}}\\[5.0pt]
\bm{\mathbf{\Lambda}}\\[5.0pt]
\log|\bm{\mathbf{\Lambda}}|\end{bmatrix}\!\right\}.

The resulting posterior parameters are

\displaystyle\kappa=\kappa_{0}+\sum_{n=1}^{N}w_{n},

\displaystyle\bm{\mathbf{m}}=1/\kappa\sum_{n=1}^{N}w_{n}\bm{\mathbf{x}}_{n},

\displaystyle\nu=\nu_{0}+\sum_{n=1}^{N}w_{n},

\displaystyle\bm{\mathbf{\Psi}}=(\bm{\mathbf{\Psi}}_{0}^{-1}+\sum_{n=1}^{N}w_{n}\bm{\mathbf{x}}_{n}\bm{\mathbf{x}}_{n}^{\top}-\kappa\,\bm{\mathbf{m}}\,\bm{\mathbf{m}}^{\top})^{-1}.

Linear-Gaussian with Matrix-Normal-Wishart Prior

A weighted linear-Gaussian likelihood takes a random input variable
\bm{\mathbf{x}}\in\mathds{R}^{d}
and returns a response random variable
\bm{\mathbf{y}}\in\mathds{R}^{m}
according to a linear mapping
\bm{\mathbf{A}}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\mathds{R}^{d}\to\mathds{R}^{m}

\displaystyle p(\bm{\mathbf{Y}}|\bm{\mathbf{X}},\bm{\mathbf{A}},\bm{\mathbf{V}})

\displaystyle=\prod_{n=1}^{N}\operatorname{N}(\bm{\mathbf{y}}_{n}|\bm{\mathbf{x}}_{n},\bm{\mathbf{A}},\bm{\mathbf{V}})^{w_{n}}

\displaystyle\propto\exp\left\{\begin{bmatrix}\bm{\mathbf{V}}\bm{\mathbf{A}}\\[5.0pt]
\bm{\mathbf{A}}^{\top}\bm{\mathbf{V}}\bm{\mathbf{A}}\\[5.0pt]
\bm{\mathbf{V}}\\[5.0pt]
\log|\bm{\mathbf{V}}|\end{bmatrix}\cdot\begin{bmatrix}\bm{\mathbf{Y}}\bm{\mathbf{W}}\bm{\mathbf{X}}^{\top}\\[5.0pt]
-\frac{1}{2}\bm{\mathbf{X}}\bm{\mathbf{W}}\bm{\mathbf{X}}^{\top}\\[5.0pt]
-\frac{1}{2}\bm{\mathbf{Y}}\bm{\mathbf{W}}\bm{\mathbf{Y}}^{\top}\\[5.0pt]
\frac{1}{2}\sum_{n=1}^{N}w_{n}\end{bmatrix}\right\},

where
w_{n}
are the weights and
\bm{\mathbf{W}}=\mathrm{diag}(w_{n})
is the diagonal weight matrix. The data matrices
\bm{\mathbf{X}}
and
\bm{\mathbf{Y}}
are of size
d\times N
and
m\times N
, respectively. The conjugate prior
p(\bm{\mathbf{A}},\bm{\mathbf{V}})
is a matrix-normal-Wishart with zero mean

\displaystyle p(\bm{\mathbf{A}},\bm{\mathbf{V}})

\displaystyle=\operatorname{N}(\bm{\mathbf{A}}|\bm{\mathbf{0}},\bm{\mathbf{V}},\bm{\mathbf{K}}_{0})\operatorname{W}(\bm{\mathbf{V}}|\bm{\mathbf{\Psi}}_{0},\nu_{0})

\displaystyle\propto\exp\left\{\begin{bmatrix}\bm{\mathbf{0}}\\[5.0pt]
-\frac{1}{2}\bm{\mathbf{K}}_{0}\\[5.0pt]
-\frac{1}{2}\bm{\mathbf{\Psi}}_{0}^{-1}\\[5.0pt]
\frac{1}{2}(\nu_{0}-m-1+d)\end{bmatrix}\cdot\begin{bmatrix}\bm{\mathbf{V}}\bm{\mathbf{A}}\\[5.0pt]
\bm{\mathbf{A}}^{\top}\bm{\mathbf{V}}\bm{\mathbf{A}}\\[5.0pt]
\bm{\mathbf{V}}\\[5.0pt]
\log|\bm{\mathbf{V}}|\end{bmatrix}\right\}.

The posterior
q(\bm{\mathbf{\mu}},\bm{\mathbf{\Lambda}})
is matrix-normal-Wishart

\displaystyle q(\bm{\mathbf{A}},\bm{\mathbf{V}})

\displaystyle=\operatorname{N}(\bm{\mathbf{A}}|\bm{\mathbf{M}},\bm{\mathbf{V}},\bm{\mathbf{K}})\operatorname{W}(\bm{\mathbf{V}}|\bm{\mathbf{\Psi}},\nu)

\displaystyle\hskip-25.00003pt\!\propto\!\exp\left\{\!\begin{bmatrix}\bm{\mathbf{Y}}\bm{\mathbf{W}}\bm{\mathbf{X}}^{\top}\\[5.0pt]
-\frac{1}{2}(\bm{\mathbf{K}}_{0}+\bm{\mathbf{X}}\bm{\mathbf{W}}\bm{\mathbf{X}}^{\top})\\[5.0pt]
-\frac{1}{2}(\bm{\mathbf{\Psi}}_{0}^{-1}+\bm{\mathbf{Y}}\bm{\mathbf{W}}\bm{\mathbf{Y}}^{\top})\\[5.0pt]
\frac{1}{2}(\nu_{0}-m-1+d+\sum_{n=1}^{N}w_{n})\end{bmatrix}\!\cdot\!\begin{bmatrix}\bm{\mathbf{V}}\bm{\mathbf{A}}\\[5.0pt]
\bm{\mathbf{A}}^{\top}\bm{\mathbf{V}}\bm{\mathbf{A}}\\[5.0pt]
\bm{\mathbf{V}}\\[5.0pt]
\log|\bm{\mathbf{V}}|\end{bmatrix}\!\right\}.

The standard-form posterior parameters are

\displaystyle\bm{\mathbf{K}}=\bm{\mathbf{K}}_{0}+\bm{\mathbf{X}}\bm{\mathbf{W}}\bm{\mathbf{X}}^{\top},

\displaystyle\bm{\mathbf{M}}=\bm{\mathbf{Y}}\bm{\mathbf{W}}\bm{\mathbf{X}}^{\top}\bm{\mathbf{K}}^{-1},

\displaystyle\nu=\nu_{0}+\sum_{n=1}^{N}w_{n},

\displaystyle\bm{\mathbf{\Psi}}=(\bm{\mathbf{\Psi}}_{0}^{-1}+\bm{\mathbf{Y}}\bm{\mathbf{W}}\bm{\mathbf{Y}}^{\top}-\bm{\mathbf{M}}\,\bm{\mathbf{K}}\,\bm{\mathbf{M}}^{\top})^{-1}.

Infinite Categorical with Stick-Breaking Prior

We assume one-hot random variable
\bm{\mathbf{z}}
of infinite size with a categorical likelihood

\displaystyle p(\bm{\mathbf{Z}}|\bm{\mathbf{\pi}}(\bm{\mathbf{s}}))

\displaystyle=\prod_{n=1}^{N}\operatorname{Cat}(\bm{\mathbf{z}}_{n}|\bm{\mathbf{\pi}}(\bm{\mathbf{s}}))^{w_{nk}}

\displaystyle=\prod_{n=1}^{N}\prod_{k=1}^{\infty}\left[\left(s_{k}\prod_{l=1}^{k-1}\left(1-s_{l}\right)\right)^{z_{nk}}\right]^{w_{nk}},

where
w_{n}
are the weights. The prior is an infinitely factorized beta distribution over the stick lengths
s_{k}

\displaystyle p(\bm{\mathbf{s}})

\displaystyle=\prod_{k=1}^{\infty}\operatorname{Beta}(s_{k}|\gamma_{0},\alpha_{0})

\displaystyle\propto\prod_{k=1}^{\infty}s_{k}^{\gamma_{0}-1}\left(1-s_{k}\right)^{\alpha_{0}-1},

and the posterior is also a factorized beta density truncated up to a level
K

59
]

q(\bm{\mathbf{s}})=\prod_{k=1}^{K}\operatorname{Beta}(s_{k}|\gamma_{k},\alpha_{k}),

where

\displaystyle\gamma_{k}

\displaystyle=\gamma_{0}+\sum_{n=1}^{N}w_{nk},

\displaystyle\alpha_{k}

\displaystyle=\alpha_{0}+\sum_{n=1}^{N}\sum_{l=k+1}^{K}w_{nl}.
