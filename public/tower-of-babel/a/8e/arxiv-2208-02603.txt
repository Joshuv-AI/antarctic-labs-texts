Abstract
. We discuss problems of simultaneous tiling. This means that we have an object (set, function) which tiles space with two or more different sets of translations. The most famous problem of this type is the Steinhaus problem which asks for a set simultaneously tiling the plane with all rotates of the integer lattice as translation sets.

§3. The Steinhaus tiling problem.

In the
Steinhaus tiling problem
we are seeking a tile that can tile
simultaneously
with many different sets of translations. The most important case is: can we find a subset of the plane which can tile (by translations) with all rotates of the integer lattice
{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}
? In some sense we are asking for a set in the plane that can behave simultaneously like all these rotated squares (Fig.
5
).

Figure 5.
The rotated squares are fundamental domains of all rotates of
{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}

There are two major variations of the Steinhaus problem: the
measurable
and the
set-theoretic
case. In the measurable case we demand our tile to be a Lebesgue measurable subset of
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
and we are, at the same time, relaxing our requirements and are allowing a subset of measure 0 of space not to be covered exactly once by the translates of the tile. In the set-theoretic case we allow the tile to by any subset and we typically ask that every point is covered exactly once, allowing no exceptions.

22

{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
when tiling by all rotates of the set
B={\mathbb{Z}}\times{\left\{{0}\right\}}

A
cannot be measurable as was shown
recently in
[
18

Sierpinski
[
26

showed that a bounded set
A
which is either closed or open cannot have the
lattice Steinhaus property (that is, intersect all rigid motions of
{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}
at exactly one point – another way to say that

tiles precisely with all rotates of
{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}
).
Croft

§4. The Steinhaus problem in Fourier space.

Most of the results on the measurable Steinhaus problem start by observing that if
E\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
is Steinhaus then every rotate
R_{\theta}E
of
E
tiles with
{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}
, which means that for every angle
\theta
the Fourier Transform
\widehat{{\mathds{1}}_{R_{\theta}E}}
vanishes on
{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}\setminus{\left\{{0}\right\}}
since
{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}
is the dual lattice of itself. This implies that
\widehat{{\mathds{1}}_{E}}
vanishes on all rotates of
{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}
. In other words
\widehat{{\mathds{1}}_{E}}
vanishes on all circles centered at the origin that go through at least one integer lattice point.

Figure 6.
The Fourier Transform of any Steinhaus set must vanish on all circles centered at the origin that go through at least one integer lattice point

The number of these circles is large. There are a little less than
O(R^{2})
such circles of radius
\leq R
. Many zeros of a function sometimes imply decay at infinity, and, by the usual uncertaintly principle (both
f
and
\widehat{f}
cannot decay fast at infinity), since
\widehat{{\mathds{1}}_{E}}
is small at infinity it follows that
{\mathds{1}}_{E}
is large (e.g.,
E
cannot be bounded).

§5. Allowing functions instead of sets in the Steinhaus problem.

Let us now relax our requirements and allow our tile to be a function instead of a set (instead of indicator function, in other words).
Satisfying the requirements of the Steinhaus tiling problem with a function is generally much easier than with a set. The problem becomes interesting only if one asks for further properties that this function should have. Therefore we try to find a function with
small support
, or to prove that the support of such a function must necessarily be large. Asking for
f
to have a small support goes against
f
having the ability to tile space, especially with many different sets of translations
T
. The reason is that for
f
to tile by translations with
T
its Fourier transform must contain a rich set of zeros
[
16
]
. This set of zeros must be able to support the Fourier transform of the measure
\delta_{T}=\sum_{t\in T}\delta_{t}
(which encodes the set of translations). By the well known uncertainty principle in harmonic analysis a rich set of zeros for
\widehat{f}
usually requires (in various different senses) a large support for
f

[
9
]
.

It is very easy to take
\widehat{f}
to vanish on the required circles, but one must do it in a way that ensures that
f
is itself
small
in some sense, such as the diameter of its support or the volume of its support.

§6. Small diameter of the support: lower bounds.

The first thing that comes to mind is to take
f
to be a convolution. It takes a moment to verify that if
f
tiles with a set of translates
T
then so does
g*f
for any
g\in L^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
. One can either verify this by checking the definition of tiling for
g*f
or observe that tiling is a condition that can be checked on the Fourier side
[
16
]
and
\widehat{g*f}=\widehat{g}\cdot\widehat{f}
has an even richer set of zeros that
\widehat{f}
.

So, since
\widehat{f}
has to vanish on the dual lattices
\Lambda_{i}^{*}\setminus{\left\{{0}\right\}}
we can take

(5)

f={\mathds{1}}_{D_{1}}*{\mathds{1}}_{D_{2}}*\cdots*{\mathds{1}}_{D_{N}},

where
D_{i}
is a fundamental parallelepiped of
\Lambda_{i}
. Since
D_{i}+\Lambda_{i}
is a tiling it follows that
\widehat{{\mathds{1}}_{D_{i}}}
vanishes on
\Lambda_{i}^{*}\setminus{\left\{{0}\right\}}
and that
f
vanishes on their union and hence tiles with all
\Lambda_{i}
. This can be slightly generalized by taking, instead if the indicator functions
{\mathds{1}}_{D_{i}}
any function
f_{i}
that tiles with
\Lambda_{i}

(6)

f=f_{1}*f_{2}*\cdots f_{N}.

Figure 7.
The fundamental doamins of several lattices. A constant fraction of them project to a set of large diameter onto one of the coordinate axes.

The following observation (see detailed proof in
[
21
]
) was already made in
[
19
]
in the case
f_{i}={\mathds{1}}_{D_{i}}
.

Theorem 1
.

If
\Lambda_{1},\ldots,\Lambda_{N}
are lattices in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
of volume
c_{1}\leq{\rm vol\,}\Lambda_{i}
and
f=f_{1}*f_{2}*\cdots*f_{N}
then

(7)

{\rm diam\,}{\rm supp\,}f\geq C_{d}N.

The reason is that a constant fraction of the supports of the
f_{i}
project onto a constant fraction of their diameter onto
some
line, say one of the axes. This implies (obvious if the
f_{i}
are nonnegative; one needs the Titchmarsh convolution theorem in the general case) that so does the support of the convolution
f=f_{1}*\cdots*f_{N}
(shown in Fig.
7
for the
f_{i}
being the indicator functions of fundamental parallelepipeds of the lattices).

If the lattices
\Lambda_{i}
satisfy some “roundness” assumption, e.g. if each
\Lambda_{i}
is assumed to have a fundamental domain of diameter bounded independent of
N
(as in the important case when all the lattices are rotates of
{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}
), then the convolution tile (
5
) has diameter which is also at most
C\cdot N
.

On the other hand we have the following rather general lower bound for the diameter of the support
[
19
]
assuming only a certain genericity assumption (
8
) on the
\Lambda_{i}
.

Theorem 2
.

If
\Lambda_{1},\ldots,\Lambda_{N}\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
,
d\geq 1
, are lattices of volume equal to 1 such that

(8)

\Lambda_{i}\cap\Lambda_{j}={\left\{{0}\right\}}\ \ \text{ for all $i\neq j$,}

then if
f
tiles with all these lattices we have

(9)

{\rm diam\,}{\rm supp\,}f\geq C_{d}N^{1/d}.

The main question is therefore:

Question 1
.

Can the gap between the lower bound (
9
) and the linear upper bound
O(N)
achievable by the convolution tile (
5
) (in the case of “round” lattices, having fundamental domains bounded in diameter by a constant) be bridged?

Are there examples of lattices
\Lambda_{i}
,
i=1,2,\ldots,N
, satisfying (
8
) and a non-zero function
f\in L^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
that tiles with all
\Lambda_{i}
and such that

{\rm diam\,}{\rm supp\,}f=o(N)?

In other words, do there exist collections of lattices for which a common tile
f
can be found which is diameter-wise more efficient than the convolution construction (
5
)?

§7. A case of large diameter.

We observe now
[
21
]
that for some collections of lattices the linear upper bound cannot be improved.
The lattices given are both “round” (have a fundamental domain bounded independent of
N
) and satisfy the genericity assumption (
8
). There are however

so, in some sense, this is not a generic situtation.

Theorem 3
.

For
d\geq 1
and for each
N
there are lattices
\Lambda_{1},\ldots,\Lambda_{N}\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, of volume 1, such that if
f\in L^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
,
\int f\neq 0
, tiles with all of them then

{\rm diam\,}{\rm supp\,}f\geq C_{d}N.

Proof.

We give the proof in the case
d=2

d>2
and it is even easier in dimension
d=1

3
which have only very long common tiles.

Take
\Lambda_{i}^{*}
to be generated by the two vectors

u_{i}=(0,a_{i}),v_{i}=(1/a_{i},0),

where the numbers
a_{1},\ldots,a_{N}
are linearly independent over
{\mathbb{Q}}
and

0.9<a_{i}<1.

If
f
tiles with all
\Lambda_{i}
then

vanishes on all points of the form

(0,k\cdot a_{i}),\ \ i=1,2,\ldots,N,\ \ k\in{\mathbb{Z}}\setminus{\left\{{0}\right\}}.

Since all these points are different it follows that the density of zeros on the
y
-axis is
\geq C\cdot N
. This implies that

{\rm diam\,}{\rm supp\,}\pi_{2}(f)\geq C\cdot N

(say, by Jensen’s formula) where
\pi_{2}(f)
is the one-variable function

\pi_{2}(f)(y)=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}f(x,y)\,dx.

(This is not an identically zero function by our assumption on the integral of

.)
This in turn implies

{\rm diam\,}{\rm supp\,}f\geq C\cdot N.

∎

§8. Small volume of the support.

Another measure of the size of the support is its volume. Can we construct a common tile
f
for the lattices
\Lambda_{i}
such that
{\left|{{\rm supp\,}f}\right|}
is small?

In the case of
f
given by (
5
) it is clear that

{\rm supp\,}{f}=D_{1}+D_{2}+\ldots+D_{N}.

To keep things concrete let us assume that all
{\left|{D_{i}}\right|}=1
in (
5
) (unimodular lattices). Then the Brunn-Minkowski inequality
[
7
]
says that

{\left|{{\rm supp\,}f}\right|}={\left|{D_{1}+\cdots+D_{N}}\right|}\geq\left({\left|{D_{1}}\right|}^{1/d}+\cdots+{\left|{D_{N}}\right|}^{1/d}\right)^{d}\geq N^{d}.

This lower bound

{\left|{{\rm supp\,}f}\right|}\geq CN^{d}

clearly holds also for functions of the form

(10)

f=f_{1}*f_{2}*\cdots*f_{N},\ \ \ f_{i}\geq 0,

where for all
i=1,2,\ldots,N
we assume that the
nonnegative
function
f
tiles with
\Lambda_{i}
.

We have proved
[
21
]
:

Theorem 4
.

For any collection of lattices
\Lambda_{1},\ldots,\Lambda_{N}
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
of volume at least 1 and any common tile
f
for them of the form

f=f_{1}*f_{2}*\cdots*f_{N},\ \ \ f_{i}\geq 0,

with
f_{i}
tiling with
\Lambda_{i}
, we have

{\left|{{\rm supp\,}f}\right|}\geq N^{d}.

But when the functions

are signed (or complex) we only have

{\rm supp\,}{f}\subseteq{\rm supp\,}{f_{1}}+\cdots{\rm supp\,}{f_{N}},

not necessarily equality, which brings us to the next question.

If

is given by (
10

{\left|{{\rm supp\,}f}\right|}\geq CN^{d}?

§9. Small length of the support in
d=1
.

In the simplest case in dimension
d=1
, and for two lattices only, a basic question is to ask if the convolution (
10
) is best in terms of the length of the support. Here we can give
[
21
]
a simple lower bound assuming a nonnegative function.

Theorem 5
.

Suppose the nonnegative
f:{\mathbb{R}}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{\geq 0}
is measurable and tiles with both
\Lambda_{1}={\mathbb{Z}}
and with
\Lambda_{2}=\alpha{\mathbb{Z}}
, where
\alpha\in(0,1)
:

(12)

\sum_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}f(x-n)=1,\ \ \ \sum_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}f(x-n\alpha)=\frac{1}{\alpha},\ \ \text{for almost every $x\in{\mathbb{R}}$}.

Then

(13)

{\left|{{\rm supp\,}f}\right|}\geq{\left\lceil{\frac{1}{\alpha}}\right\rceil}\alpha\geq 2\alpha.

Remark 1
.

If we assume the first equation in (
12
) then the constant in the second equation is forced to be
1/\alpha
. This is because
\int f=1
(from the first equation), so repeating
f
at a set of translates of density
1/\alpha
will give a constant (assuming it tiles) at that level.

Remark 2
.

Notice that if
\alpha
is just a little less than 1 then (
13
) gives a lower bound of
2\alpha
, which shows that the convolution
{\mathds{1}}_{[0,1]}*{\mathds{1}}_{[0,\alpha]}
is almost optimal in this case, having support of size
1+\alpha
.

But if, on the other hand,
\alpha
is just over
1/2
then the lower bound is just over 1 but the convolution upper bound is just over
3/2
, a considerable gap.

Proof.

From the first equation in (
12
) it follows that
f(x)\leq 1
for almost every
x
. For the second equation to be true it therefore follows that for almost every
x\in{\mathbb{R}}
there are at least
{\left\lceil{1/\alpha}\right\rceil}
different values of
n\in{\mathbb{Z}}
such that
f(x-n\alpha)>0
.
Using this for almost all
x\in[0,\alpha)
(which ensures that for different
x
the locations
x-n\alpha
are also different) gives (
13
).

∎

§10. Very small diameter of the support with relations among the lattices.

If we have
N
lattices

\Lambda_{1},\ldots,\Lambda_{N}\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{d}

we can find a function that tiles with them all, namely the function
f
in (
5
). If our lattices are assumed to each have a fundamental domain bounded by
\sim 1
then
{\rm diam\,}{\rm supp\,}f=O(N)
, and this cannot be improved for functions
f
arising from (
5
). We show here
[
21
]
that we can choose the lattices
\Lambda_{j}
so that a common tiling function exists which is much more tight than that, tighter even than what Theorem
2
imposes. Of course our lattices will not satisfy the genericity condition (
8
) of Theorem
2
, but will satisfy a lot of relations (their intersection will be a large lattice, in terms of density).

Fix a large prime
p
and consider the group
{\mathbb{Z}}_{p}^{d}
. Any nonzero element
g
of this group generates a cyclic subgroup of order
p
. It follows that
{\mathbb{Z}}_{p}^{d}
has

\frac{p^{d}-1}{p-1}\sim p^{d-1}=:N

different cyclic subgroups. For each such subgroup
G
, which we now view as a subset of
{\left\{{0,1,\ldots,p-1}\right\}}^{d}
, consider the lattice

\Lambda_{G}=(p{\mathbb{Z}})^{d}+G.

Figure 10.
How we construct the
(p{\mathbb{Z}})^{d}
-periodic set
\Lambda_{G}
from the subgroup
G
of
{\mathbb{Z}}_{p}^{d}

This contains the lattice
\Lambda=(p{\mathbb{Z}})^{d}
and has volume

{\rm vol\,}\Lambda_{G}=\frac{{\rm vol\,}(p{\mathbb{Z}})^{d}}{{\left|{G}\right|}}=p^{d-1}=N.

The function
f={\mathds{1}}_{[0,p)^{d}}
,
[0,p)^{d}
being a fundamental domain of
\Lambda
, tiles with
\Lambda
and, therefore, with any larger group, so
f
is a common tile of all
\Lambda_{G}
.

In order to make the volume of the
\Lambda_{G}
equal to 1 we shrink everything by
N^{1/d}
:

\Lambda_{G}^{\prime}=N^{-1/d}\Lambda_{G},\ \ \ f^{\prime}(x)=f(N^{1/d}x).

So we have
\sim N
lattices
\Lambda^{\prime}_{G}
of volume 1 and a common tile
f^{\prime}
for them with

{\rm diam\,}{\rm supp\,}f^{\prime}={\rm diam\,}{\rm supp\,}f\cdot N^{-1/d}=\sqrt{d}\,pN^{-1/d}=\sqrt{d}\,N^{\frac{1}{d-1}-\frac{1}{d}}=\sqrt{d}\,N^{\frac{1}{d(d-1)}}.

We have proved:

Theorem 6
.

In dimension
d\geq 2
and for arbitrarily large
N
we can find
N
lattices of volume
1
and a common tile
f
for them with

{\rm diam\,}{\rm supp\,}f=O_{d}\left(N^{\frac{1}{d(d-1)}}\right),

and, consequently, with

(14)

{\left|{{\rm supp\,}f}\right|}=O_{d}\left(N^{\frac{1}{d-1}}\right).

Question 6
.

Derive a lower bound for
{\rm diam\,}{\rm supp\,}f
, for
f
tiling with
\Lambda_{1},\ldots,\Lambda_{N}\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
and with

(or just

) under no algebraic conditions for the lattices
\Lambda_{j}
, assuming only that

.

In Theorem

§11. Almost matching upper and lower bounds for the diameter,

The construction that we used to prove Theorem

that, if we allow relations among the lattices, we can achieve
{\rm diam\,}{\rm supp\,}f=o(N)
in dimension 1 as well.

Let us start by defining

\lambda_{j}=\frac{1}{N+j},\ \ \ \Lambda_{j}=\lambda_{j}{\mathbb{Z}},\ \ \ (j=1,2,\ldots,N).

We will first construct a function
f
which tiles with all the
\Lambda_{j}
,
j=1,2,\ldots,N
, such that

{\rm diam\,}{\rm supp\,}f=o(1).

The Fourier transform of such an
f
must vanish on the dual lattices

\Lambda_{j}^{*}=\lambda_{j}^{-1}{\mathbb{Z}}=(N+j){\mathbb{Z}},\ \ \ (j=1,2,\ldots,N)

except at 0.
Write

U=\bigcup_{j=1}^{N}(N+j){\mathbb{Z}}\ \setminus{\left\{{0}\right\}}.

By a result of Erdős
[
6
]

U
, the set of integers which are divisible by one of the integers in
{\left\{{N+1,N+2,\ldots,2N}\right\}}
, has density tending to 0 with
N
. Tenenbaum
[
28
]
has given the estimate that this density is at most

(15)

\frac{1}{\log^{\delta-o(1)}N},

where
\delta=0.086071\cdots
is an explicit constant.

It is an important result of Beurling
[
2
]
that if
\Lambda
is a uniformly discrete set of real numbers of upper density
\rho
then for any
\epsilon>0
we can find a continuous function
f
, not identically zero, supported by the interval
[0,\rho+\epsilon]
such that
\widehat{f}(\lambda)=0
for all
\lambda\in\Lambda
. We can even ask that
\widehat{f}(0)=1
if
0\notin\Lambda
. By Tenenbaum’s estimate (
15
) we can take
\rho=\log^{-\delta+o(1)}N
and the set
U
, being a set of integers and thus uniformly discrete, satisfies the assumptions of Beurling’s theorem, so there is a function
f
supported in the interval
[0,\log^{-\delta+o(1)}N]
, with integral 1, such that
\widehat{f}=0
on
U
. It follows that
f
tiles with all
\Lambda_{j}
.

We now scale by a factor of
N

f^{\prime}(x)=f(x/N),\ \ \ \Lambda_{j}^{\prime}=N\Lambda_{j},\ \ \ {\rm diam\,}{\rm supp\,}f^{\prime}=O(N\log^{-\delta+o(1)}N)

and obtain the first half of the following theorem.

Theorem 7
.

We can find
N
lattices
\Lambda_{j}\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}
of with
{\rm vol\,}\Lambda_{j}\sim 1
and a function
f
with
\int f>0
and supported in an interval of length

\frac{N}{\log^{\delta-o(1)}N)}

which tiles with all
\Lambda_{j}
.

Furthermore, for any
\epsilon>0
any such function
f
must have

{\rm diam\,}{\rm supp\,}f\gtrsim_{\epsilon}N^{1-\epsilon}.

Arguing similarly we can also prove the lower bound for
{\rm diam\,}{\rm supp\,}f
in Theorem
7
. If we assume that
f
tiles with all
\Lambda_{j}=\lambda_{j}{\mathbb{Z}}
, with, say,
1\leq\lambda_{j}\leq 2
,
j=1,2,\ldots,N
, then
\widehat{f}
vanishes on

\bigcup_{j=1}^{N}\lambda_{j}^{-1}{\mathbb{Z}}\ \ \setminus{\left\{{0}\right\}}.

If this set is large then Jensen’s formula implies that
{\rm diam\,}{\rm supp\,}f
is also large.
It was proved in
[
8
, Theorem 1.1, special case
\ell=n
]
that, for any
\epsilon>0
, the above union of arithmetic progressions contains at least
c_{\epsilon}N^{2-\epsilon}
points in
[0,2N]
. By Jensen’s formula then we have
{\rm diam\,}{\rm supp\,}f\gtrsim_{\epsilon}N^{1-\epsilon}
and this completes the proof of Theorem
7
.
