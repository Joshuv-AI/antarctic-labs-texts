The SIR model in a moving population: propagation of infection and herd immunity
Duncan Dauvergne, Allan Sly

Abstract
In a collection of particles performing independent random walks on ℤ d \mathbb{Z}^{d} we study the spread of an infection with SIR dynamics. Susceptible particles become infected when they meet an infected particle. Infected particles heal and are removed at rate ν \nu . We show that when ν \nu is small, with positive probability the infection survives forever and grows linearly. Furthermore, after the infection reaches a region, it quickly passes through and leaves behind a herd immunity regime consisting of recovered particles, a small positive density of susceptible particles, and no infected particles. One notable feature of this model is the simultaneously existence of supercritical and subcritical phases on either side of an infection front of O ⁡ ( 1 ) O(1) width.

Introduction

We study a model for the spread of an infection in a collection of moving interacting particles, based on the well-known epidemiological
SIR
(Susceptible-Infected-Removed) model. The process is initiated from a Poisson process of susceptible particles on
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
with density
\mu>0
. All particles perform independent continuous-time simple random walks on
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
. At time
0
a single infected particle is added at the origin
0
. When an infected particle and a susceptible particle meet at a vertex, the susceptible particle becomes infected. Infected particles recover at rate
\nu>0
, after which point they are removed and no longer affect the process.
Our goal is to understand the evolution of this process and how the infection spreads over time.

This model fits within a broader class of dynamical interacting particle systems including Susceptible-Infected (SI also called A/B or X/Y) and Susceptible-Infected-Susceptible (SIS) models of infection spread, multi-particle Diffusion Limited Aggregation (mDLA), frog models, and activated random walks. See Section
1.2
for discussion of related work on these models. Each of these models can be viewed as an infection spreading through a population. Typically, we are interested in understanding the rate of spread and properties of the infected region.
In contrast to the other models, the challenge here is that particles remain infected for only
O(1)
time before being removed, and so particle density decreases as the infection spreads. Therefore, even if the infection survives forever we do not expect to see a growing ball of infected particles. Rather, we expect a growing sphere of particles of
O(1)
width dividing the plane between an outer supercritical region with a high density of susceptible particles and a low density interior in a subcritical
herd immunity
regime with too few susceptible particles to sustain an epidemic.

The SIR model originates in epidemiology and is generally studied in the mean field setting where any particle can infect any other
[
KM27
,
RH17
]
. In large populations its evolution can be studied by deterministic differential equations (see
[
And91
]
for a survey of the plethora of variants and applications). It has also been studied in the case where particles occupy fixed vertices in a graph and can infect neighbouring vertices. In the lattice setting, this model was first studied in detail by Kuulasma
[
Kuu82
]
building on work in simpler models by Mollison
[
Mol77
]
.
In the case of random graphs with given degree distributions, Newman
[
New02
]
characterized the critical threshold for infection spread.

The case of moving particles is more mathematically challenging as the dynamic environment of particles has a complicated dependence on the infection process. Moreover, an additional challenge is that, unlike in various SI or SIS models, it lacks any obvious monotonicity in the density and recovery rate parameters
\mu
and
\nu
. For example, if we couple two SIR models at different densities, then while the higher density model initially has more infections, this may cause particles to recover earlier, decreasing the density and breaking the monotone coupling between the models.

Our first main theorem shows that the SIR model displays qualitatively different behaviour at high and low recovery rates.
We let
{\bf S_{t}}
and
{\bf I_{t}}
denote the sets of susceptible and infected particles at time
t
, respectively. For this next theorem, we let
\mathbb{P}(d,\mu,\nu)
denote the
survival probability
for the infection process in dimension
d
, density
\mu
and recovery rate
\nu
. That is,
\mathbb{P}(d,\mu,\nu)
is the probability that
{\bf I_{t}}\neq\emptyset
for all
t>0
.

Theorem 1.1
.

Fix any density
\mu>0
and any dimension
d\geq 2
. Then there exist
\nu^{-}=\nu^{-}(\mu,d),\nu^{+}=\nu^{+}(\mu,d)\in(0,\infty)
such that if
\nu>\nu^{+}
, then
\mathbb{P}(d,\mu,\nu)=0
and if
\nu<\nu^{-}
then
\mathbb{P}(d,\mu,\nu)>0
. Moreover,
\mathbb{P}(d,\mu,\nu)\to 1
as
\nu\to 0
.

We expect that
\mathbb{P}(d,\mu,\nu)
is a monotone function of
\nu
and therefore there exists a critical recovery rate
\nu_{c}\in(0,\infty)
. However, proving this monotonicity appears to be a difficult problem for the reasons discussed above. Indeed, closely related processes are not monotone on certain graphs, see
[
DH06
,
CS21b
]
.

The fact that
\mathbb{P}(d,\mu,\nu)=0
for all large enough
\nu
was previously established by Kesten and Sidoravicius in
[
KS06
]
for the SIS model where particles become susceptible again after recovering from the infection. Their result immediately implies ours since the infection process for the SIS model stochastically dominates the infection process for the SIR model and so this part of Theorem
1.1
is not new. However, their proof is quite involved as it has to deal with the possibility of particles becoming reinfected. In the SIR setting, a much more straightforward supermartingale argument works, see Section
2
. Recently, Grimmett and Li
[
GL20
]
have shown that
\mathbb{P}(d,\mu,\nu)=0
for large enough
\nu
in a Brownian version of our SIR model. Their broad proof strategy is similar to ours but differs in the precise setup (and naturally, there are also different technicalities that come from working in discrete vs. continuous space). The proof that
\mathbb{P}(d,\mu,\nu)\to 1
as
\nu\to 0
is much more difficult and is given in Section
4
.

A version of Theorem
1.1
also holds if we first fix
\nu
and allow the density
\mu
to vary instead. Indeed, the results of Section
2
show that for fixed
\nu
, if
\mu
is low enough then
\mathbb{P}(d,\mu,\nu)=0
. A variant of the arguments in Section
4
would show that
\mathbb{P}(d,\mu,\nu)\to 1
as
\mu\to\infty
, but the details are sufficiently different that we do not include the proof here.

Remark 1.2

(Dimension
1
)
.

In dimension
1
, it is not difficult to check that the SIR process dies out exponentially quickly at every recovery rate
\nu
and every density
\mu
. Indeed, by Theorem 1 in
[
KS05
]
, there exists a constant
C>0
such that the set of infected particles
I_{t}
is contained in the interval
[-Ct,Ct]
with exponentially high probability. Since particles move diffusively and each particle is only infected for an
O(1)
amount of time, this implies that there is a constant
C^{\prime}\in{\mathbb{N}}
such that at most times
t
, at most
C^{\prime}
total particles are infected. At any such time
t
there is a positive probability depending on
C^{\prime}
but not on
t
that all of these particles recover before encountering any susceptible particles. Hence the infection must die out exponentially quickly.

Theorem
1.1
establishes that the SIR process can exhibit both recovery regimes and survival regimes. While the recovery regime is not particularly interesting, there are many possibilities for how the process might behave in the survival regime. The next theorem describes the particular behaviour of the process in the survival regime.

Theorem 1.3
.

Fix any density
\mu>0
, any dimension
d\geq 2
, and
\nu<\nu^{-}(\mu,d)
. Then there is a positive probability event
\mathcal{G}_{\nu}
with
\mathbb{P}[\mathcal{G}_{\nu}]\to 1
as
\nu\to 0
, such that on
\mathcal{G}_{\nu}
the following events hold. Here
c,C>0
are constants that depend on
\nu,\mu,d
.

1.

(Survival)
{\bf I_{t}}\neq\emptyset
for all
t\geq 0
.

2.

(Linear growth) For all large enough
t
, we have

{\bf I_{t}}\subset B(0,Ct)\setminus B(0,ct).

3.

(Infection Duration) For every site
x\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, let
D_{x}
be the length of time between the first appearance of an infected particle at site
x
and the last appearance. If an infected particle never reaches site
x
, set
D_{x}=0
. Then for all
x\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
and

we have

\mathbb{P}(D_{x}>m\;|\;\mathcal{G}_{\nu})\leq C\exp(-m^{c/\log\log m}).

(1)

In particular, on
\mathcal{G}_{\nu}
there exists a random constant
D>0
such that for all
x\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
we have

D_{x}\leq D+[\log(\|x\|_{2}+3)]^{C\log\log\log(\|x\|_{2}+3)}.

(2)

4.

(Herd Immunity in the centre) We have

\liminf_{t\to\infty}\frac{|{\bf S}_{t}\cap B(0,ct)|}{t^{d}}>0.

We strongly believe that
\mathcal{G}_{\nu}
can be taken to be the survival event

\{\textbf{I}_{t}\neq\emptyset\text{ for all }t\}.

However, our methods do not suffice to show this.

1.1
Proof Sketch

In
[
DS21
]
we studied the evolution of infections in the SI model where particles do not recover. Previous work of Kesten and Sidoravicius
[
KS05
,
KS08
]
had analyzed this model in the setting where susceptible and infected particles move with the same speed. Under this assumption, unlabeled particle trajectories are independent random walks and are not affected by the infection process. This work left open the problem of models where the particle environment was dependent on the infection process.
Our work in
[
DS21
]
developed a technical framework to establish linear growth in general SI models which is flexible enough to study many other models.
In the present work we use it to understand the different phenomena that occur in SIR models, particularly the way in which the infection process divides the plane into subcritical and supercritical regimes divided by an infected region of
O(1)
width.

We divide the lattice into blocks of fixed side length
L
chosen based on the recovery rate
\nu
(see equation (
25
)). We define a colouring process on the set of blocks where a block is coloured the first time an infected particle enters it or if it has been next to a coloured block for a sufficient period of time (see Section
4.1
for the precise definition). In order to establish sufficient spatial independence, we only observe particles that have previously visited the coloured region. Outside of the coloured region, the conditional distribution of the particles is conditionally Poisson with a random intensity that can be bounded from below. This description allows us to define the SIR process in a spatially independent way according to the colouring process.
To each block we associate an independent Poisson process and use this to generate the particles inside that block at the time it is coloured, as well as their future trajectories and healing times.

The lower bound on the intensity implies a high-probability lower bound on the number of particles present in a block
B
at the random time
\tau_{B}
when it is first coloured. This implies that for most blocks, if they are infected at time
\tau_{B}
then the infection will quickly be propagated to the neighbouring blocks irrespective of where the infection first enters. Those that do not have this property are labeled as
blue seeds
. Our block construction of the SIR process using independent Poisson processes guarantees that the blue seed process is stochastically dominated by a highly subcritical percolation in the low recovery rate regime. To establish that the infection survives and grows linearly we couple the colouring process to a competitive growth model called Sidoravicius–Stauffer percolation (SSP) first used to study multi-particle DLA
[
SS19
]
.

Our analysis here goes beyond the questions addressed in
[
DS21
]
, and a particular focus is the study of the herd immunity regime left behind after the infection passes through a region. Our aim is to show that in this herd immunity regime, there are no infected particles remaining while a small density of susceptible particles remains uninfected. To show that the infection dies out locally after the wave of infections passes through, we first use random walk estimates to show that the density of unremoved particles is small in regions that were coloured long ago. We then combine this with a more delicate version of the supermartingale argument used to analyze the high recovery rate regime to prove that the infection dies out completely with high probability.

To show that there is a small density of susceptible particles that never become infected we show that locally each block has a small probability of having a particle that survives for a long period of time after
\tau_{B}
. After the wave of infections has passed through, it is unlikely that there are ever infected particles in a neighbourhood of this susceptible particle. In order to show that many such particles survive we show that this event can be bounded below by one that is essentially local.

A particular challenge in establishing the features of the herd immunity regime is to show that the events in question can essentially be defined locally using the independent Poisson blocks, i.e. that they do not depend on the relative colouring times
\tau_{B}
or on the behaviour of the coupled Sidoravicius–Stauffer percolation, both of which in principle can have long range dependencies.

1.2
Related work

The type of SIR model we study in this paper fits within a broader class of dynamical interacting particle systems
built from random walks on the lattice. If we take our SIR model and make all susceptible particles stationary, then we recover the frog model on
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
(with recovery). Alves, Machado, and Popov
[
AMP02a
]
showed that
this model exhibits a phase transition, obtained asymptotics for the critical recovery rate, and found a shape theorem in the SI setting where recovery is not permitted
[
AMP02b
]
. The frog model has also been well-studied in the SIS (Susceptible-Infected-Susceptible) setting, where frogs recover from the infection and return to a stationary susceptible state under the name ‘activated random walks’, e.g. see
[
DRS10
,
Rol20
,
ST18
]
for review articles and recent progress.

The case when both susceptible and infected particles are allowed to move is more mathematically challenging as the dynamic environment of particles has a much more complicated dependence on the infection process. In the SI and SIS settings, as discussed above this model was seriously attacked in a series of papers of Kesten and Sidoravicius
[
KS05
,
KS08
,
KS06
]
, culminating in a shape theorem for the infected region in the SI model. See
[
KRS12
]
for a survey of problems about these types of models and
[
BS20
,
BS21
]
for more recent work.

A version of the SI model on
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
where susceptible particles can move but infected particles cannot move and instead form a stationary growing aggregate is known as multi-particle diffusion limited aggregation (mDLA). While the character of mDLA is somewhat different (for example, the aggregate has a fractal-like structure at low densities), the techniques used to study mDLA are useful here. Indeed, this line of work introduced SSP, known there as first passage percolation in a hostile environment (FPPHE), as a tool in the study of mDLA in
[
SS19
]
. See
[
FS20
,
CS21a
,
CS21b
]
for further developments on FPPHE. A simpler version of the block Poisson description of the SIR process was also first used to study mDLA in one dimension in
[
Sly21
]
.

2
SIR recovery

In this section, we show that in the high recovery rate regime, the infection process dies out exponentially quickly. A few modifications to the argument will also allow us to give a criterion for the SIR infection recovering in a local window where particles have an initial low density. This will be used later to establish that in the low recovery rate regime, the infection dies out quickly in a region after the infection front has passed through.

Proposition 2.1
.

Consider an SIR process with recovery rate
\nu
, started from a random initial configuration of susceptible particles that is stochastically dominated by a Poisson process of intensity
\nu/8
with an initial infected particle added at the origin

. Then for all

\mathbb{P}(\mathbf{I}_{t}\neq\emptyset)\leq\mathbb{E}\mathbf{I}_{t}\leq(1+\nu/8)e^{-\nu t/2}.

for

•

For all

.

•

a_{i}\neq a_{j}

•

For all

(r,r+h]
. In this case, every active chain in
\mathscr{C}_{u}^{r,Q}(r)
extends uniquely to an active chain in
\mathscr{C}_{u}^{r,Q}(r+h)
and

.

(ii)

{\overline{q}}^{h}\cap(r,r+h]=\emptyset
, the particle
q
does not move in the interval
(r,r+h]
, and exactly one particle

jumps onto the square

in the interval
(r,r+h]
, does not heal in that interval, and does not jump again in that interval. In this case,

.

(iii)

{\overline{q}}^{h}\cap(r,r+h]=\emptyset
and the particle
q
jumps exactly once in the interval
(r,r+h]
from the location
y={\overline{q}}(r)
to a neighbouring location

with
|(P\setminus Q)\cap\{y^{\prime}\}|=N
. No other particles in
P
jump onto or off of

or
y^{\prime}
in the interval
(r,r+h]
and no particles heal in the interval
(r,r+h]
. In this case,

equals

I^{r,Q}(r)(N+1)\exp\left(\frac{\nu(d(y,A)-d(y^{\prime},A))}{8}\right).

(6)

Note that in the
A=\mathbb{Z}^{2}
setting, this is simply
I^{r,Q}(r)(N+1)
. In the
A\neq\mathbb{Z}^{2}
setting, using that
\nu\leq 1
, this is bounded above by
I^{r,Q}(r)(N+1)(\nu/7+1)
.

(iv)

The particle
q
does not jump in the interval
(r,r+h]
, no particles in
P\setminus Q
jump from another square onto
{\overline{q}}(r)
in that interval, and
q
heals once in that interval. In this case

.

(v)

Other possibilities occur. In all these possibilities, if

denotes the collection of all jump times and healing times of all particles in

, then
|E\cap(r,r+h]|\geq 2
.

To help bound the probability of (i)-(v) conditioned on
I^{r,Q}(t)
, observe that conditioning on
I^{r,Q}(r)
gives no information about particles
p\in P\setminus Q
. Moreover, the location of the particles in
P\setminus Q
at time

is stochastically dominated by a Poisson process of intensity
\nu/8,
since this holds at time
0
. With this, we can easily see that conditionally on

,

\begin{split}&\mathbb{P}(i)=1-O(h),\qquad\mathbb{P}(ii)\leq\nu h/8+O(h^{2}),\qquad\mathbb{P}(iii)=h+O(h^{2}),\\
\qquad&\mathbb{P}(iv)=\nu h+O(h^{2}),\qquad\mathbb{P}(v)=O(h^{2}).\end{split}

(7)

To get the estimate (
5
), we also need to understand the conditional expectation of (
6
) given
I^{r,Q}(r)
and that the event in (iii) holds. Up to an
O(h)
term, conditioning on (iii) is the same as conditioning on the particle

to jump in
(r,r+h]
. Under this conditioning,
N
is stochastically dominated by a Poisson random variable of mean
\nu/8
. Therefore

\mathbb{E}\left(I^{r,Q}(r+h)-I^{r,Q}(r)\mid I^{r,Q}(r),(iii)\right)\leq 3\nu I^{r,Q}(r)/8+O(h).

Also, conditionally on (v) occurring and
I^{r,Q}(r)
, since
|P|\leq n
it is easy to check using ideas similar to the proof of Lemma

\mathbb{E}(I^{r,Q}(r+h)-I^{r,Q}(r)\mid I^{r,Q}(r),(v))\leq cI^{r,Q}(r)
for some constant

. Putting this all together with the bounds in (

), and the explicit changes to

on events (i), (ii), and (iv) gives (
5
). Next, again using that

is finite it is straightforward to check using the decomposition (i)-(v) above that the random variables

\frac{1}{h}\mathbb{E}\left(I^{r,Q}(r+h)-I^{r,Q}(r)\;|\;I^{r,Q}(r)\right)

are uniformly integrable as we vary

over
(0,1)
. Therefore the bound (

) holds with the relevant random variables replaced with their expectations. Summing over the finitely many sequences
Q
with labels in
P\cup P^{\prime}
, we then get that for all

, we have the right-hand derivative bound

\limsup_{h\to 0^{+}}\frac{\mathbb{E}I(s+h)-\mathbb{E}I(s)}{h}\leq-\frac{\nu\mathbb{E}I(s)}{2}.

(8)

The first inequality in the lemma then follows from Lemma
2.3
applied to the function
\mathbb{E}I
. The continuity of
\mathbb{E}I
required for the lemma holds by Lemma
2.2
.

Now we extend this to the case when

are potentially infinite. Note that

is always countable by the stochastic domination of the initial configuration by a Poisson process. Let
\{p_{i}:i\in{\mathbb{N}}\},\{p_{i}^{\prime}:i\in{\mathbb{N}}\}
be enumerations of

, and let

be defined in the same way as

but with the set

replaced by the set of active chains on

started at

. Then for all

is monotone increasing, and

almost surely, so by the monotone convergence theorem,

∎

Proof of Proposition
2.1
.

By Markov’s inequality and Lemma
2.4
we have

\mathbb{P}({\bf I_{t}}\neq\emptyset)\leq\mathbb{E}\mathbf{I}_{t}\leq\mathbb{E}|\mathscr{C}_{0,0}(t)|=\mathbb{E}|I_{0,\mathbb{Z}^{2}}(t)|\leq\mathbb{E}|I_{0,\mathbb{Z}^{2}}(0)|e^{-\nu t/2}.

Local recovery in the global survival regime

We prove Proposition

Under assumption (

To prove Lemma

Lemma 2.7

Let

is stochastically dominated by a Poisson random variable of mean
\gamma

is stochastically dominated by a Poisson process with intensity
\gamma
.

Results of this form are well-known. For example, Lemma

was shown in
[

]
, Lemma 1 for Bernoulli random variables and Bernoulli processes in place of Poisson random variables and Poisson processes. Russo’s proof works verbatim in the context above, and so we omit it.

We will also use a standard random walk estimate. This estimate will be used throughout the paper so we record it here as a lemma. We leave the proof as an exercise for the reader using Azuma’s inequality and basic large deviations.

Lemma 2.8
.

Let
X:[0,\infty)\to\mathbb{Z}^{2}
be a continuous time random walk. Then for all

and

, we have

\mathbb{P}(X(t)=u)\leq\frac{C}{t}\exp\left(-\frac{c|u|^{2}}{|u|+t}\right),\;\;\mathbb{P}(\max_{0\leq r\leq t}|X(t)|\geq m)\leq C\exp\left(-\frac{cm^{2}}{m+t}\right).

Proof of Lemma
2.6
.

By Lemma
2.7
, it is enough to show that for any
t\in[M^{2}/8,4M^{2}],z\in[-3M,3M]^{2}
and any finite point configuration

on
{[-3M,3M]^{2}\setminus\{z\}}

\mathbb{P}(|P(t)\cap\{z\}|\in\cdot\mid P(t)|_{[-3M,3M]^{2}\setminus\{z\}}=f)

is stochastically dominated by a mean
C\delta
Poisson distribution.
Equation (
12

P^{\prime}\subset P
and any function

\mathbb{P}\left(|P(t)\cap\{z\}|\in\cdot\;\Big|\;{\overline{p}}(t)=f(p)\;\forall p\in P^{\prime},{\overline{p}}(t)\notin[-3M,3M]^{2}\setminus\{z\}\;\forall p\in P\setminus P^{\prime}\right)

is stochastically dominated by a mean

|P(t)\cap\{z\}|=\sum_{p\in P\setminus P^{\prime}}\mathbf{1}({\overline{p}}(t)=z).

(13)

This is a sum of independent Bernoulli random variables of mean

\mu_{p}:=\mathbb{P}({\overline{p}}(t)=z\mid{\overline{p}}(t)\notin[-3M,3M]^{2}\setminus\{z\}).

Now, for all

\mathbb{P}({\overline{p}}(t)\notin[-3M,3M]^{2})\geq c.

Combining this with the bound in Lemma

Also, a Bernoulli random variable of mean

3
Sidoravicius–Stauffer percolation

Proving the existence of a survival regime for the SIR process is much more involved than showing the existence of a recovery regime. To do this, we will couple the SIR model to a competing growth process that we call Sidoravicius–Stauffer percolation (SSP). The original variant of SSP was first studied in
[
SS19
]
. We adapted the framework from
[
SS19
]
in
[
DS21
]
in order to understand infection spread in random walks. Many of the results of
[
DS21
]
will be required here, though we do not need the same level of generality used in that paper. In this section, we introduce SSP and gather the necessary results.

Informally, in SSP on
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
, a set of red vertices grows outwards from the origin according to a set of edge weights, similarly to first passage percolation. Within
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
, there is a collection of blue seeds
\mathfrak{B}_{*}
which cannot be invaded by the red growth process. Whenever the red process attempts to invade a blue seed, a competing blue growth process is activated. Once activated, the blue process will grow outward from a blue seed at a slow, constant speed. The red and blue processes can only invade squares that are not already part of one of the two coloured processes.

Within this framework, we also allow for squares adjacent to the blue process to become activated at arbitrary times. When these squares are activated, they will turn red and join the red process unless they belong to the set of blue seeds, in which case they will turn blue.

3.1
Constructing the process

We will define a pair of coupled growth processes
\mathfrak{R},\mathfrak{B}:[0,\infty)\to\{S:S\subset\mathbb{Z}^{2}\}
. The growth of these processes will be governed according to weights on the set of directed edges

E:=\{(u,v)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}:|u-v|=1\}.

We will need the following data to define
\mathfrak{R},\mathfrak{B}
.

•

A constant
\kappa>0
, referred to as the
parameter
of the process. In
[
DS21
]
, we let the parameter
\kappa
be a function from
E\to(0,\infty)
. In the current paper, we are only concerned with the case where
\kappa
is constant.

•

A collection of blue seeds
\mathfrak{B}_{*}\subset\mathbb{Z}^{2}
.

•

Edge weight functions
X_{\mathfrak{R}}:E\to[0,1]
and
X_{\mathfrak{B}}:E\to[0,\kappa]
. We will think of
X_{\mathfrak{R}},X_{\mathfrak{B}}
as clocks governing the growth of the red and blue processes. Edge weights
X_{\mathfrak{B}}(e)=\kappa
will encourage the spread of the blue process, whereas other edge weights will encourage the spread of the red process.

•

We have allowed for the caveat that clocks may have a value of
0
. This corresponds to instantaneous invasion. To ensure that the process is still well-defined even when instantaneous invasion is allowed, we require that there are no directed cycles
(u_{0},u_{1},\dots u_{n}=u_{0})
with

X_{\mathfrak{R}}(u_{i-1},u_{i})\wedge X_{\mathfrak{B}}(u_{i-1},u_{i})=0

for all
i=1,\dots,n
.

We define
\mathfrak{R}
and
\mathfrak{B}
by recording a time
T(u)
when each vertex
u
gets added to the process, and a colour
C(u)
for that vertex. We initialize the process by setting
T(0)=0
, and setting
C(0)=\mathfrak{R}
if
0\notin\mathfrak{B}_{*}
and
C(0)=\mathfrak{B}
if
0\in\mathfrak{B}_{*}
. The rules for assigning the other times and colours are as follows.

For every edge
(u,v)\in E
, at time
T(u)+X_{C(u)}(u,v)
, the edge
(u,v)
will ring, and the process will update accordingly. If
T(v)<T(u)+X_{C(u)}(u,v)
, then nothing happens. This corresponds to the case when
v
has already been added to one of the processes by this time. Otherwise, we set
T(v)=T(u)+X_{C(u)}(u,v)
, and colour
v
according the following four rules.

1.

If
v\in\mathfrak{B}_{*}
, set
C(v)=\mathfrak{B}
.

2.

If
C(u)=\mathfrak{R}
and
v\notin\mathfrak{B}_{*}
, then set
C(v)=\mathfrak{R}
.

3.

If
C(u)=\mathfrak{B},v\notin\mathfrak{B}_{*}
, and
X_{\mathfrak{B}}(u,v)<\kappa
, then set
C(v)=\mathfrak{R}
.

4.

If
C(u)=\mathfrak{B},v\notin\mathfrak{B}_{*}
and
X_{\mathfrak{B}}(u,v)=\kappa
, then set
C(v)=\mathfrak{B}
.

We must specify what happens if
T(u)+X_{C(u)}(u,v)=T(u^{\prime})+X_{C(u^{\prime})}(u^{\prime},v)
for two different vertices
u,u^{\prime}
. In this case, if the colours assigned to
v
via the two edges
(u,v)
and
(u,v^{\prime})
are different, we set
C(v)=\mathfrak{B}
. For
t\in[0,\infty)
, we set

\begin{split}\mathfrak{R}(t)&=\{u\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}:T(u)\leq t,C(u)=\mathfrak{R}\}\quad\text{and}\\
\quad\mathfrak{B}(t)&=\{u\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}:T(u)\leq t,C(u)=\mathfrak{B}\}.\end{split}

(16)

When working with SSP, we will always assume that for any
t
, only finitely many squares
u
satisfy
T(u)<t
.
Under this
finite speed
assumption, the colouring process is well-defined. From now on, all processes we consider have finite speed.

In
[
DS21
]
, we showed that as long as
\kappa
is sufficiently large, we can find a well-controlled set
\mathfrak{C}
depending only on the initial configuration of blue seeds
\mathfrak{B}_{*}
, such that under certain conditions on
\mathfrak{B}_{*}
, we have
\mathfrak{B}(\infty)\subset\mathfrak{C}
.

The set
\mathfrak{C}
is built via a multi-scale construction.
Let
r_{0}=1\leq r_{1}<r_{2}<\dots
be a sequence of integer scales satisfying

\gamma:=\prod_{i=0}^{\infty}\left(1+\frac{10^{12}r_{i}}{r_{i+1}}\right)<2,

(17)

and such that
r_{i+1}=(i+1)^{2}r_{i}
for all large enough
i
. For closed disks and annuli, we write

D(x,r)=\{y\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}:|y-x|\leq r\},\qquad A(x,r,R)=\{y\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}:r\leq|y-x|\leq R\}.

Here and throughout the paper, we use the union-of-disks notation

D(A,r)=\{y\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}:d(y,A)\leq r\}=\bigcup_{x\in A}D(x,r).

Now, for a set of blue seeds
\mathfrak{B}_{*}\subset\mathbb{Z}^{2}
, define

A_{1}(\mathfrak{B}_{*}):=\{x\in\mathfrak{B}_{*}:\mathfrak{B}_{*}\cap D(x,r_{1}/3)=\{x\}\},

We then recursively define
\mathfrak{B}_{*,k}=\mathfrak{B}_{*}\setminus\cup_{i=1}^{k-1}A_{i}(\mathfrak{B}_{*})
, and let

A_{k}(\mathfrak{B}_{*}):=\{x\in\mathfrak{B}_{*,k}:\mathfrak{B}_{*,k}\cap A(x,r_{k-1},r_{k}/3)=\emptyset\}.

Finally, for a set
A\subset\mathbb{Z}^{2}
, we let
[A]
be the union of
A
and all bounded components of
A^{c}
.

Theorem 3.1
.

Consider an SSP
(\mathfrak{R},\mathfrak{B})
on
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
initiated from a set of blue seeds
\mathfrak{B}_{*}
with parameter
\kappa>4000
. Suppose that

1.

\mathfrak{B}_{*}=\bigcup_{k=1}^{\infty}A_{k}(\mathfrak{B}_{*})
, and

2.

, where
\mathfrak{D}:=\bigcup_{k=1}^{\infty}D(A_{k}(\mathfrak{B}_{*}),r_{k}/100)

Then

, where
\mathfrak{C}:=\bigcup_{k=1}^{\infty}D(A_{k}(\mathfrak{B}_{*}),100r_{k-1})

Lemma 3.2

Lemma 3.3

(Lemma 2.12,

]
)

Consider any collection of blue seeds

.

\mathfrak{D}_{k}:=\bigcup_{i=1}^{k}D(A_{i}(\mathfrak{B}_{*}),r_{i}/100)\qquad\text{ and }\qquad\mathfrak{C}_{k}:=\bigcup_{i=1}^{k}D(A_{i}(\mathfrak{B}_{*}),100r_{i-1}).

(Lemma 2.13,
[

]
)
.

Assume that

is stochastically dominated by an i.i.d. Bernoulli process with parameter

(24)

4
SIR survival

In this section we give a coupling of the SIR process
X_{t}
with initial Poisson density
\mu

with an SSP satisfying the conditions of Theorem
3.5
. In this coupling the red sites in the SSP will correspond to spatial blocks in
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
where the SIR infection spreads efficiently. The initial configuration of blue seeds will be dominated by a Bernoulli process with parameter
p=p(\nu)
where
p\to 0
as
\nu\to 0
, allowing us to conclude Theorem
1.1
by applying Theorem
3.5
. Throughout this section and in the remainder of the paper the density
\mu
is fixed and all constants
c,C,\dots
are allowed to depend on
\mu
.

4.1
A block construction for the SIR process

Fix a side length
L=2^{k}
for some large
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
. We choose
L
so that

\nu^{-1/6}\leq L\leq 2\nu^{-1/6}

(25)

and subdivide
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
into a collection of dyadic blocks

\mathscr{B}=\mathscr{B}_{L}:=\Big\{zL+\big\{-L/2,\ldots,L/2-1\big\}^{2}:z\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}\Big\}.

(26)

This induces a natural map
f=f_{L}:\mathbb{Z}^{2}\to\mathscr{B}_{L}
. For
B,B^{\prime}\in\mathscr{B}
, we also define the
block distance

d_{\mathscr{B}}(B,B^{\prime})=|f^{-1}(B)-f^{-1}(B^{\prime})|

and for
B\neq B^{\prime}
note the inequalities

d(B,B^{\prime})<Ld_{\mathscr{B}}(B,B^{\prime}),\qquad d_{\mathscr{B}}(B,B^{\prime})<4{\left\lceil d(B,B^{\prime})/L\right\rceil}.

(27)

Both
d
and
d_{\mathscr{B}}
will be useful measurements in the paper for analyzing distance.
For the arguments in the remainder of the paper,
L
is the more natural parameter than
\nu
and so we work exclusively with a sufficiently large
L
rather than sufficiently small
\nu
, invoking the relationship (
25
) when necessary.

Given a side length
L
, for some small
\alpha\in(0,1)
, let
\xi=\xi_{L}=\alpha L^{2}
. This will be the minimum speed at which the colouring of blocks spreads. We will now define a
colouring process
for blocks
B\in\mathscr{B}_{L}
from the SIR process.

Definition 4.1
.

We will let
\tau_{B}
denote the (stopping) time when a block
B
is first coloured. For a vertex
u\in B
we will use
\tau_{u}
as shorthand for
\tau_{B}
. A block
B
becomes coloured at time
t
if one of the following events happens:

(a)

A neighbouring block
B^{\prime}
(i.e.
d_{\mathscr{B}}(B,B^{\prime})=1
) was coloured at time
\tau_{B^{\prime}}=t-\xi
.

(b)

An infected particle enters
B
for the first time at time
t
.

(c)

A block
B^{\prime}
becomes coloured according to rule (b) at time
t
by an infected particle entering at location
x\in B^{\prime}
, and
\inf\{\|x-u\|_{\infty}:u\in B\}\leq\alpha L
.

In case (c) we call this a multi-colouring, where up to three blocks may be coloured simultaneously.
In case (b) we call the infected particle that entered
B
the
ignition particle
of
B
. In case (c) the ignition particle is the ignition particle for block
B^{\prime}
. In cases (b) and (c) we say that the block
B
is
ignited
at the location
x
where an infected particle first entered
B
(for case (b)) or first entered the relevant neighbour of
B
(for case (c)). All ignition locations are within
L^{\infty}
-distance
\alpha L
of
B
.

We say that a particle
a
is
coloured
at the first time it enters a coloured block, write
B(a)
for the block in which
a
was coloured, and write
T(a)
for the time when
a
was coloured. This can happen either because the block became coloured or because the particle entered a coloured block. We let

X_{t}^{*}
denote the process of all particles up to time
t
that satisfy
T(a)\leq t
, and let
\mathscr{F}_{t}^{*}
denote the
\sigma
-algebra generated by
X_{t}^{*}
. We let
\iota_{a}
be the stopping time when particle
a
becomes infected.

A key element of our analysis is to regard the collection of random walks as given by a Poisson process. We let
\mathfrak{W}
be the space of cadlag sample paths
w(t):\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{Z}^{2}
such that

\frac{1}{t}|w(t)|\to 0

(28)

as
|t|\to\infty
. Elements of
\mathfrak{W}
will represent the trajectories of particles. To represent healing times of particles we let we let
\mathfrak{Q}
be the set of simple point measures on
\mathbb{R}
and let
\mathfrak{W}^{+}=\mathfrak{W}\times\mathfrak{Q}
. An element of
\mathfrak{W}^{+}
will represent the trajectory of a particle together with the set of times at which it receives a healing event.
Let
\mathscr{W}^{+}_{u}
denote the measure on
\mathfrak{W}^{+}
given by the pair of

(a)

a continuous time random walk on
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
over all time
t\in\mathbb{R}
that is at
u\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}
at time
0
and

(b)

an independent rate
\nu
Poisson process on
\mathbb{R}
.

Note that a simple random walk satisfies (
28
) almost surely. Furthermore, let
\mathscr{W}^{+}=\sum_{u\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}}\mathscr{W}^{+}_{u}
. We let
\mathscr{P}
denote a Poisson process on
\mathfrak{W}^{+}
with intensity measure
\mu\mathscr{W}^{+}
. If we remove the initial infected particle from the origin, we can interpret all remaining particles and their trajectories as being given by a sample from
\mathscr{P}
.

In order to obtain spatial independence of various events we will give an alternative construction of the SIR process with the same law based on a collection of Poisson processes
\mathscr{P}_{B},B\in\mathscr{B}
which are IID and equal in distribution to
\mathscr{P}
. We call a particle
a
in
\mathscr{P}_{B}

simple
, let
a(t)
denote its trajectory, and let
a^{h}\subset\mathbb{R}
its set of healing times.

Let
\mathscr{M}_{B}
denote the
\sigma
-algebra generated by

(i)

the independent Poisson process
\mathscr{P}_{B}
,

(ii)

a pair
(W_{B,\operatorname{ig}},W_{B,\operatorname{ig}}^{h})
sampled independently from
\mathscr{W}^{+}_{0}
, which will encode the trajectory and healing process of an ignition particle.

Note that the
(\mathscr{P}_{B},W_{B,\operatorname{ig}},W_{B,\operatorname{ig}}^{h}),B\in\mathscr{B}
are IID.

We will build the SIR process
X_{t}
according to the
\mathscr{M}_{B}
in such a way that particles coloured in a block
B
correspond to particles in
\mathscr{P}_{B}
but with a time shift of length
\tau_{B}
. We will abuse notation somewhat by conflating a particle
a
in some
\mathscr{P}_{B}
with a particle in
X_{t}
matched according to our construction. Define

{\overline{a}}(t):=a(t-\tau_{B}),\qquad{\overline{a}}^{h}=a^{h}-\tau_{B}.

(29)

These will be, respectively, the trajectory and healing process of
a
in
X_{t}
. Particle locations in
\mathscr{P}_{B}
at time
0
tell us the particle locations in
X_{t}
at time
\tau_{B}
.

It will be enough to construct the process of previously coloured particles
X_{t}^{*}
at every time
t
, since every particle is eventually coloured. This holds since the colouring process spreads at least linearly by Definition
4.1
(a), whereas particles spread diffusively.
Let
B_{0}
be the block containing the origin so that
\tau_{B_{0}}=0
. At time
0
we add all simple
\mathscr{P}_{B_{0}}
-particles that are in
B_{0}
at time
0
to
X_{t}^{*}
plus an infected ignition particle at the origin. The simple particles evolve according to their paths
a(t)
while the initially infected particle evolves according to
W_{B_{0},\operatorname{ig}}(t)
. Particles become infected if they enter the same vertex as another infected particle. New particles can enter
X_{t}^{*}
in two ways, either when a new block becomes coloured or when an uncoloured particle enters a coloured block for the first time.

Case 1: A newly coloured block:
When a block
B
is coloured for the first time according to Definition
4.1
we add particles to
X_{t}^{*}
as follows. If a particle
a
from
\mathscr{P}_{B}
is in
B
at time
0
, then we add it to
X_{t}^{*}
at time
t=\tau_{B}
if for all
0\leq t<\tau_{B}
we have that
\tau_{a(t-\tau_{B})}>t
. This condition is equivalent to saying that a particle with trajectory
a(t-\tau_{B})
first hits a coloured block at time
\tau_{B}
. The trajectory and healing process of this particle is given by
{\overline{a}}(t),{\overline{a}}^{h}
.

If
B
is ignited at a vertex
x\in\partial B
, the ignition particle follows special rules. Instead of continuing to follow the trajectory given by the block it was initially coloured by, when it becomes the ignition particle of
B
at time
\tau_{B}
we alter its future trajectory to
x+W_{B,\operatorname{ig}}(t-\tau_{B})
and its future healing process to
\tau_{B}+W^{h}_{B,ig}
. Note that some particles may ignite multiple boxes at distinct times, in which case their future trajectories and healing processes will change more than once.

Case 2: Particle first entering a coloured block:
For times
t\in(\tau_{B},\tau_{B}+\xi]
new particles are revealed in
B
in the process
X_{t}^{*}
according to the following rules. If
a
is a particle in
\mathscr{P}_{B}
that enters
B
at time
t-\tau_{B}
, then we add it to
X_{t}^{*}
at time
t
if for all
0\leq s<t
we have that
\tau_{a(s-\tau_{B})}>t
. This condition is equivalent to saying that a particle with trajectory
a(s-\tau_{B})
first hits a coloured block at time
t
. Again, the trajectory and healing process of this particle are given by
{\overline{a}}(s),{\overline{a}}^{h}
. Particles can only join
X^{*}_{t}
in this way during the time interval
(\tau_{B},\tau_{B}+\xi]
since at time
\tau_{B}+\xi
the neighbouring blocks of
B
are already coloured by construction.

With this construction, a particle
a
from
\mathscr{P}_{B}
is added with trajectory
{\overline{a}}
if and only if
B(a)=B
. It follows from independence properties of Poisson processes that the construction above indeed describes an SIR process. This is shown in Section 5 of
[
DS21
]
in the setting when particles cannot heal. The proof goes through verbatim with the minor addition of healing processes.

We would like to partition the set of all particles in the SIR process
X_{t}
into sets
H_{B},B\in\mathscr{B}
where particle trajectories in each
H_{B}
are entirely determined by
\mathscr{M}_{B}
. However, this is not quite possible since some particles that are coloured in
B
may become ignition particles for other blocks and hence their trajectories will not be entirely
\mathscr{M}_{B}
-measurable. To deal with this, it will be convenient to think of a particle
a\in H_{B}
as healing when it becomes the ignition particle for a new block
B^{\prime}
, and a new infected particle
W_{B^{\prime},\operatorname{ig}}\in H_{B^{\prime}}
appearing instantaneously in its place.

Let
H_{B}
be the set of all particles that are first coloured in
B
. Definition
4.1
(a) guarantees that our colouring process is moving at a linear speed depending only on
L
, which helps to ensure that there are typically many particles in
H_{B}
. With this in mind, define
H^{-}_{B}
to be all particles
a\in\mathscr{P}_{B}
that satisfy the following additional constraint:

\sup_{t\leq 0}d(a(t),B)-L\lfloor|t\xi^{-1}|/4\rfloor=0.

(30)

We call the particles in
H_{B}^{-}

principal particles
and claim that
H_{B}^{-}\subset H_{B}
. Indeed, let
a\in H_{B}^{-}
. Equation (
30
) implies that
a(0)\in B
. Now suppose
B^{\prime}
gets coloured before
B
. To show that
H_{B}^{-}\subset H_{B}
, we must check that
a(t)\notin B^{\prime}
for all
t\geq\tau_{B^{\prime}}-\tau_{B}
.
By Definition
4.1
(a) and the bound (
27
), we have the bound

|\tau_{B}-\tau_{B^{\prime}}|\leq d_{\mathscr{B}}(B,B^{\prime})\xi<4{\left\lceil d(B,B^{\prime})/L\right\rceil}\xi.

(31)

Condition (
30
) then implies that
d(a(t),B)<d(B,B^{\prime})
for all
t\geq\tau_{B^{\prime}}-\tau_{B}
, so
a(t)\notin B^{\prime}
.

Also let
H_{B}^{+}
be the set of all particles

4.2
Blue Seeds

In this section we give a set of conditions on
\mathscr{M}_{B}
which will ensure the efficient spread of the infection to the neighbouring blocks if the block
B
is ignited, i.e. coloured according to rule (b) or (c). The complement of this event will correspond to marking the block as a blue seed in our SSP coupling. For the remainder of Section
4
, we fix an arbitrary
\kappa>4000
.

Setting some notation, let

U_{x}=\{B\in\mathscr{B}_{L}:\inf_{y\in B}\|x-y\|_{\infty}\leq\alpha L\}.

When the meaning is clear from context, we will also let
U_{x}
denote the set of vertices contained in the union of the blocks in
U_{x}
. By construction,
U_{x}
is a rectangle in the plane made of either 1, 2, or 4 blocks. It includes the block containing
x
.

Furthermore, if
x
is the ignition site for the block
B
, all blocks in
U_{x}
are coloured at or before time
\tau_{B}
. Set

\partial B^{\#}=\bigcup_{B^{\prime}\in\mathscr{B}_{L}}\{x\in\partial B^{\prime}:B\in U_{x}\},

which is the set of possible ignition locations for the ignition particle of
B
(the shape of the set
\partial B^{\#}
resembles a hashtag
\#
).

The ignition particle for
B
is infected, starts at
x\in\partial B^{\#}
and follows the path
x+W_{B_{x},\operatorname{ig}}((t-\tau_{B}))
for
t\geq\tau_{B}
, where
B_{x}
is the block containing
x
. Define the event

\mathcal{A}^{(1)}_{B}=\bigcap_{B^{\prime}:d(B,B^{\prime})\leq 2}\Big\{\sup_{0\leq s\leq\xi/\log\log L}|W_{B^{\prime},\operatorname{ig}}(s)|\leq\frac{1}{2}\alpha L\Big\}.

Since
d(B_{x},B)\leq 2
for all
x\in\partial B^{\#}
, this event ensures that the ignition particle for
B
remains in
U_{x}
until time
\tau_{B}+\xi/\log\log L
, and hence cannot become the ignition particle of another block prior to this time.

For
x\in\partial B^{\#}
and each block
B^{\prime}\not\in U_{x}
with
d(B,B^{\prime})=1
, define the event

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}^{(2)}_{B,B^{\prime},x}=\bigg\{\exists a\in H_{B}^{-},\;

\displaystyle\exists\;0\leq s\leq s^{\prime}<\tfrac{\xi}{\log\log L}\text{ s.t.}

\displaystyle a(s)=x+W_{B_{x},\operatorname{ig}}(s);\quad

\displaystyle a(s^{\prime})\in B^{\prime};\quad\forall\;0\leq s^{\prime\prime}<s^{\prime},a(s^{\prime\prime})\in U_{x}\bigg\}.

This event asks for at least one principal particle in
B
to have a trajectory that intersects the ignition particle’s trajectory at some time
s
, and to stay within
U_{x}
until some time
s^{\prime}
, at which point it enters
B^{\prime}
.
Finally let
\mathcal{A}^{(3)}_{B}
be the event that the ignition particles of all blocks
B^{\prime}
with
d(B,B^{\prime})\leq 2
have no healing events in the interval
[0,L^{3}]
and let
\mathcal{A}^{(4)}_{B}
be the event that no particle in
H_{B}^{-}
has a healing event in the interval
[0,L^{3}]
. Finally we want that many principal particles which do not become ignition particles become infected. Thus we define

\displaystyle Q_{B,x}=\Big\{a\in H_{B}^{-}

\displaystyle:d(a(0),B^{c})\geq\tfrac{1}{10}L,\exists s\in[0,\tfrac{\xi}{\log\log L}],a(s)=x+W_{B_{x},\operatorname{ig}}(s)\Big\},

and

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}^{(5)}_{B,x}=\Big\{|Q_{B,x}|\geq L^{2-\log^{-1/2}L}\Big\}.

We combine these events and define

\mathcal{A}_{B}=\mathcal{A}_{B}^{(1)}\cap\mathcal{A}^{(3)}_{B}\cap\mathcal{A}^{(4)}_{B}\cap\bigcap_{x\in\partial B^{\#}}\bigg(\mathcal{A}^{(5)}_{B,x}\cap\bigcap_{\begin{subarray}{c}B^{\prime}\in\mathscr{B}\setminus U_{x}\\
d(B,B^{\prime})\leq 1\end{subarray}}\mathcal{A}^{(2)}_{B,B^{\prime},x}\bigg)

(32)

We call the block
B
a
blue seed
if
(\mathcal{A}_{B})^{c}
holds. It follows from the definition that
\mathcal{A}_{B}
is measurable with respect to the
\sigma
-algebra generated by
\{\mathscr{M}_{B^{\prime}},d(B^{\prime},B)\leq 2\}
.

We have asked for many things from our event
\mathcal{A}_{B}
that together will ensure the efficient spread of the infection. The simplest consequence of the definition is that if
\mathcal{A}_{B}
holds and
B
is ignited, then all its neighbouring blocks in
\mathscr{B}_{L}
will be coloured before time
\tau_{B}+\xi/\kappa
.

Lemma 4.2
.

Suppose that a block
B\in\mathscr{B}_{L}
is ignited at time
\tau_{B}
at location
x
and that
\mathcal{A}_{B}
holds. Then any neighbouring block
B^{\prime}
is coloured by time
\tau_{B}+\xi/\kappa
.

Proof.

First, we may assume that
B^{\prime}\notin U_{x}
, since else
B^{\prime}
is coloured by time
\tau_{B}
.
Select some
a\in H_{B}^{-}
and
s,s^{\prime}
that make the event
\mathcal{A}^{(2)}_{B,B^{\prime},x}
hold. The particle
a
must be susceptible at time
\tau_{B}
and at time
\tau_{B}+s^{\prime}
will meet the ignition particle which by
\mathcal{A}^{(3)}_{B}
is still infected at that time. Therefore
a
will become infected at or prior to to time
\tau_{B}+s^{\prime}
. Moreover, it will reach
B^{\prime}
at time
\tau_{B}+s\leq\tau_{B}+\xi/\kappa
and by
\mathcal{A}^{(4)}_{B}
it will not heal in the interval
[\tau_{B},\tau_{B}+\xi/\kappa]
. Therefore this particle will ignite
B^{\prime}
at time
\tau_{B}+s
if
B^{\prime}
has not already been previously coloured.
∎

4.3
Tools for analyzing random walks

In this part we gather a couple of preliminary estimates used in the proof of Proposition
4.4
. With
W_{t}
a continuous time random walk, for
x\in B
define

p_{x}=\mathbb{P}\Big[\forall t\geq 0:d(x+W_{t},B^{c})\leq L\lfloor|t\xi^{-1}|/4\rfloor\Big]

At time
\tau_{B}
the principal particles
H_{B}^{-}
are Poisson distributed with intensity
p_{x}\mu
.
For any
x
such that
d(x,B^{c})\geq\frac{1}{10}L
we have that

\displaystyle p_{x}

\displaystyle\geq 1-\mathbb{P}[\sup_{0\leq t\leq 3\alpha L^{2}}|W_{t}|>\frac{1}{10}L^{2}]-\sum_{j\geq 1}\mathbb{P}[\sup_{0\leq t\leq 3(j+1)\alpha L^{2}}|W_{t}|>Lj]\geq\frac{3}{4},

(34)

for all large enough
L
and
\alpha\leq c
by Lemma
2.8
. The next lemma then follows from a standard concentration estimate on Poisson random variables.

Lemma 4.7
.

Let
L
be sufficiently large. The size of
|H_{B}^{+}|,|Q_{B}|
and
|H_{B}^{-}|
are all Poisson distributed with the following means:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}|H_{B}^{-}|

\displaystyle=\sum_{x\in B}p_{x}\mu\geq\frac{1}{3}\mu L^{2}

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}|Q_{B}|

\displaystyle=\sum_{x\in B,d(u,B^{c})\geq L/10}p_{x}\mu\geq\frac{1}{3}\mu L^{2}

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}|H_{B}^{+}|

\displaystyle=\sum_{x\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}}\mu\mathbb{P}[\exists t\in[0,\xi]:W_{t}+x\in B]\leq\frac{3}{2}\mu L^{2}.

In particular, for some
c>0
we have that

\mathbb{P}[|H_{B}^{+}|\leq 2\mu L^{2},|H_{B}^{-}|\geq|Q_{B}|\geq\frac{1}{4}\mu L^{2}]\geq 1-\exp(-c\mu L^{2}).

(35)

Next, let
H
be a set of particles and let
x:[s,s+T]\to\mathbb{Z}^{2}
be an arbitrary cadlag path.
The following lemma will be useful for counting how many particles in
H
hit
x
. With this in mind, let

N(H,s,T,x):=\sum_{a\in H}\mathbf{1}(\{t\in[s,s+T]:a(t)=x(t)\}\neq\emptyset)

be the number of
H
-particles that intersect the path
x
and let

R(H,s,T,x):=\sum_{a\in H}\int_{s}^{s+T}\mathbf{1}(a(t)=x(t))dt.

be the aggregate intersection time of the
H
-particles with
x(t)
.

Lemma 4.8
.

There exists a constant
C>0
such that for any set of particles
H
performing continuous-time simple random walks and any path
x:[s,s+T]\to\mathbb{Z}^{2}
, for
T\geq 2
we have

\mathbb{E}[N(H,s,T,x)\mid\mathscr{F}_{s}]\geq\frac{1}{C\log T}\mathbb{E}[R(H,s,T,x)\mid\mathscr{F}_{s}].

Here
\mathscr{F}_{s}
is the
\sigma
-algebra generated by the walks in
H
up to time
s
.
The same results holds if the walks in
H
instead perform a different Markov chain with transition matrix
Q_{x,y}^{t,t^{\prime}}
satisfying the following bound for all
t_{1}>0
,
T\geq 2,
and
x\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}
:

\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{1}+T}\sup_{y\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}}Q_{x,y}^{t_{1},t}dt\leq C\log T.

(36)

Proof.

First note that the transition probability for a simple random walk satisfies (
36
).
Now, for each particle
a\in H
let

\displaystyle n_{a}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}[\{t\in[s,s+T]:a(t)=x(t)\}\neq\emptyset\mid\mathscr{F}_{s}],

\displaystyle r_{a}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\Big[\int_{s}^{s+T}\mathbf{1}(a(t)=x(t))dt\Bigm|\mathscr{F}_{s}\Big].

Setting
\varsigma_{a}=\inf\{t\geq s:a(t)=x(t)\}
we have

\displaystyle r_{a}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\Big[\mathbf{1}(\varsigma_{a}\leq s+T)\int_{\varsigma_{a}}^{s+T}Q^{\varsigma_{a},t}_{x(\varsigma_{a}),x(t)}dt\Bigm|\mathscr{F}_{s}\Big]

\displaystyle\leq C\log(T)\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{1}(\varsigma_{a}\leq s+T)\mid\mathscr{F}_{s}]\leq C\log(T)n_{a}.

Summing over
a\in H
yields the result.
∎

We will also need a slight variant of Lemma
4.8
. Define

\displaystyle N^{\prime}(H,s,T,x)

\displaystyle:=\sum_{a\in H}\mathbf{1}(\{t\in[s,s+T]:a(t^{\prime})=x(t)\text{ for all }t^{\prime}\in[t,t+1]\}\neq\emptyset)

\displaystyle R^{\prime}(H,s,T,x)

\displaystyle:=\sum_{a\in H}\int_{s}^{s+T}\mathbf{1}(a(t^{\prime})=x(t)\text{ for all }t^{\prime}\in[t,t+1])dt

Lemma 4.9
.

There exist constants
C_{1},C_{2}>0
such that for any set of particles
H
performing continuous time simple random walks and any path
x:[s,s+T]\to\mathbb{Z}^{2}
, for

we have

\mathbb{E}[N^{\prime}(H,s,T,x)\mid\mathscr{F}_{s}]\geq\frac{1}{C\log T}\mathbb{E}[R^{\prime}(H,s,T,x)\mid\mathscr{F}_{s}].

Proof.

For each particle
a\in H
let

\displaystyle n_{a}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}[\{t\in[s,s+T]:a(t^{\prime})=x(t)\text{ for all }t^{\prime}\in[t,t+1]\}\neq\emptyset\mid\mathscr{F}_{s}],

\displaystyle r_{a}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\Big[\int_{s}^{s+T}\mathbf{1}(a(t^{\prime})=x(t)\text{ for all }t^{\prime}\in[t,t+1])dt\Bigm|\mathscr{F}_{s}\Big].

Setting
\varsigma_{a}=\inf\{t\geq s:a(t)=x(t)\}
we have

\displaystyle r_{a}

\displaystyle=e^{-1}\mathbb{E}\Big[\mathbf{1}(\varsigma_{a}\leq s+T)\int_{\varsigma_{a}}^{s+T}Q^{\varsigma_{a},s+T}_{x(\varsigma_{a}),x(t)}dt\Bigm|\mathscr{F}_{s}\Big]

which yields the result after summing over

.
∎

Remark 4.10
.

The counting function

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\Big[N\leq\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}[N\mid\mathscr{F}_{s}]\;\Big|\;\mathscr{F}_{s}\Big]\leq\exp(-c\mathbb{E}[N\mid\mathscr{F}_{s}]).

Now let

\displaystyle Q^{\prime}_{B,x}=\Big\{a\in Q^{{}^{\prime\prime}}_{B,x}

\displaystyle:\exists s\in[0,\tfrac{\xi}{2\log\log L}]\text{ such that }a(s)=x+W_{B_{x},\operatorname{ig}}(s)\Big\}.

Since
x+W_{B_{x},\operatorname{ig}}(s)\in U_{x}^{*}
for
s\in[0,\tfrac{\xi}{2\log\log L}]
on the event
\mathcal{A}^{(1)}_{B}
, by Lemma
4.8
we have that

\mathbb{E}[|Q^{\prime}_{B,x}|\mid\mathcal{A}^{(1)}_{B}]\geq\frac{C}{\log L}\mathbb{E}[R(x+W_{B_{x},\operatorname{ig}})\mid\mathcal{A}_{B}^{(1)}]\geq L^{2}\exp(-C_{3}(\log\log L)).

(40)

Now, conditional on
\mathcal{A}^{(1)}_{B}
and all particle trajectories from
\mathscr{P}_{B}
up to time
\xi/(2\log\log L)
, each particle in
Q^{\prime}_{B,x}
performs a simple random walk after time
\xi/(2\log\log L)
. Therefore under this conditioning, each particle in
Q^{\prime}_{B,x}
has probability at least
\exp(-C_{4}(\log\log L))
of exiting
U_{x}
through
B^{\prime}
for any neighbouring block
B^{\prime}\not\subset U_{x}
by time
\tfrac{\xi}{2\log\log L}
. Calling the set of particles that does this
Q_{B,B^{\prime},x}^{\prime}
, we therefore have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[|Q^{\prime}_{B,B^{\prime},x}|\mid\mathcal{A}^{(1)}_{B}]

\displaystyle\geq\exp(-C_{4}(\log\log L))\mathbb{E}[|Q^{\prime}_{B,x}|\mid\mathcal{A}^{(1)}_{B}]

\displaystyle\geq L^{2}\exp(-(C_{3}+C_{4})(\log\log L)).

Conditional on the trajectory of
W_{B_{x},\operatorname{ig}}
, we also have that
Q^{\prime}_{B,B^{\prime},x}
is a Poisson random variable and so

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\Big[|Q^{\prime}_{B,B^{\prime},x}|\Bigm|\mathcal{A}^{(1)}_{B}\Big]

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{P}\Big[\hbox{Poisson}\big(L^{2}\exp(-(C_{3}+C_{4})(\log\log L))\big)\geq L^{2-\log^{-1/2}L}]

\displaystyle\geq 1-L^{-100}

for
L
sufficiently large. Now,
Q^{\prime}_{B,B^{\prime},x}\subset Q_{B,x}
for any
B^{\prime}
, so

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\Big[\mathcal{A}^{(5)}_{B,x}\Bigm|\mathcal{A}^{(1)}_{B}\Big]\geq 1-L^{-100}.

(41)

Also,
\mathcal{A}^{(2)}_{B,B^{\prime},x}
is contained in the event where
|Q^{\prime}_{B,B^{\prime},x}|\neq\emptyset
for all
B^{\prime}\notin U_{x}
with
d(B,B^{\prime})=1
, so similarly

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\Big[\mathcal{A}^{(2)}_{B,B^{\prime},x}\Bigm|\mathcal{A}^{(1)}_{B}\Big]\geq 1-4L^{-100}.

(42)

Note that the argument for (
41
) and (
42
) goes through verbatim if we condition on
\mathcal{A}^{(1)}_{B}\cap\mathcal{W}
rather than just on
\mathcal{A}^{(1)}_{B}
, since this stricter conditioning just determines some of the ignition trajectories.
Combining (
37
), (
38
), (
41
) and (
42
) together with a union bound we have that

\mathbb{P}[(\mathcal{A}_{B})^{c}]\to 0

as
\nu\to 0
(or equivalently as
L\to\infty
), completing the proof of Proposition
4.4
. Similarly,
\mathbb{P}[(\mathcal{A}_{B})^{c}\mid\mathcal{W}]\to 0
, yielding Lemma
4.5
.

5
Local and global events

The remainder of the paper is devoted to the proof of the more refined Theorem
1.3
. One of the difficulties in proving this theorem is to separate local and global effects on the process. In this section we define events which will help us to deal with this. Here and throughout the remainder of the paper, for a set of blocks
\mathscr{D}\subset\mathscr{B}
, we write
\mathscr{M}_{\mathscr{D}}
for the
\sigma
-algebra generated by the
\mathscr{M}_{B},B\in\mathscr{D}
.

Suppose that we wish to understand the behaviour of the SIR process
X_{t}
in a spatial window near a block
B
and in a window of time centered at the colouring time
\tau_{B}
. In this window, we would like to say that
X_{t}
is essentially governed only by the
\sigma
-algebras
\mathscr{M}_{B^{\prime}}
for
B^{\prime}
close to
B
. However, there are three global effects that can disrupt this:

1.

The behaviour of the coupled SSP from Proposition
4.3
in blocks near
B
.

2.

The presence of particles coloured in far away blocks coming close to
B
.

3.

The differences
\tau_{B^{\prime}}-\tau_{B}
, for all blocks
B^{\prime}
close to
B
.

We handle the first of these effects by looking not at the exact behaviour of the SSP, but rather only the encapsulating sets
[\mathfrak{C}]
and
[\mathfrak{D}]
. First, throughout the proof we assume that
L
is sufficiently large so that Theorem
3.5
holds in the coupled SSP. Next, we have no control over the SSP or the SIR process unless we are working on the event

\mathcal{C}=\Big\{\mathfrak{B}_{*}=\bigcup_{i=1}^{\infty}A_{i}(\mathfrak{B}_{*}),0\in[\mathfrak{D}]^{c},[\mathfrak{D}]^{c}\subset\mathfrak{R}(\infty),[\mathfrak{D}]\text{ has only bounded components}\Big\}.

(43)

This is essentially the event
\mathcal{E}
in (
23
), with
[\mathfrak{C}]
replaced by
[\mathfrak{D}]
in two places.
It satisfies the same probability bound as
\mathcal{E}
since
\mathfrak{C}\subset\mathfrak{D}
and
[\mathfrak{D}]
only has bounded components almost surely for
p
small enough by Lemma
3.4
. We choose to only work with
[\mathfrak{D}]
to simplify things moving forward.

On the event
\mathcal{C}
, the only information we need about the SSP will be contained in the set
[\mathfrak{D}]
. We can use inequality (
21
) in Lemma
3.4
to estimate the probability that
[\mathfrak{D}]
is not determined locally. For a block
B
and
r\in(0,\infty]
, let

D_{\mathscr{B}}(B,r)=\{B^{\prime}\in\mathscr{B}:d_{\mathscr{B}}(B,B^{\prime})\leq r\}.

Define the encapsulating set
[\mathfrak{D}(B,r)]
to be the version of
[\mathfrak{D}]
generated using only blue seeds from blocks in
D_{\mathscr{B}}(B,r)
. Note that these sets are nondecreasing in
r
by Lemma
3.2
. The set
[\mathfrak{D}(B,r)]
is a local approximation of
[\mathfrak{D}]
. The two sets are typically equal away from the boundary of
D_{\mathscr{B}}(B,r)
; in particular they typically coincide on the block
B
. Indeed, we have the following estimate.

Lemma 5.1
.

For
r<r^{\prime}\in(0,\infty]
, define the event

\mathcal{D}_{B}(r,r^{\prime})=\{\{B\}\cap[\mathfrak{D}(B,r)]=\{B\}\cap[\mathfrak{D}(B,r^{\prime})]\}.

(44)

Then as long as
\nu
is sufficiently small given
\mu
, we have
\mathbb{P}[\mathcal{D}_{B}(r,r^{\prime})]\geq 1-\exp(-cr^{c/\log\log r})
for some
c>0
.

Moving forward, we will write
\mathcal{D}_{B}(r)=\mathcal{D}_{B}(r,\infty)
. It will also be useful to note the transitivity relationship

\mathcal{D}_{B}(r,r^{\prime})\cap\mathcal{D}_{B}(r^{\prime},r^{\prime\prime})=\mathcal{D}_{B}(r,r^{\prime\prime})

(45)

for
r<r^{\prime}<r^{\prime\prime}
, which follows from the monotonicity of the sets
[\mathfrak{D}(B,r)]
in
r
. We will typically use this with
r^{\prime\prime}=\infty
. In this case, (
45
) splits the global event
\mathcal{D}_{B}(r)
into a local part
\mathcal{D}_{B}(r,r^{\prime})
which is measurable given the
\sigma
-algebra
\mathscr{M}_{D_{\mathscr{B}}(B,r^{\prime}+2)}
, and a global part
\mathcal{D}_{B}(r^{\prime})
whose probability decays quickly with
r^{\prime}
.

Proof.

If
\{B\}\cap[\mathfrak{D}(B,r^{\prime})]\neq\{B\}\cap[\mathfrak{D}(B,r)]
, then by monotonicity
B\notin[\mathfrak{D}(B,r)]
but
B\in[\mathfrak{D}(B,r^{\prime})]
.
We claim that then
B
is in a component of
[\mathfrak{D}(B,r^{\prime})]
of radius at least
r/2
, and hence is also in a component of
[\mathfrak{D}]
of radius at least
r/2
. This will imply the lemma via (
20
) in Lemma
3.4
.

Setting some notation, let

\mathfrak{B}_{*}^{1}=\mathfrak{B}_{*}\cap D(B,r),\qquad\mathfrak{B}_{*}^{2}=\mathfrak{B}_{*}\cap D(B,r^{\prime}),

and for each blue seed
B^{\prime}
and
i=1,2
, let
k_{i}(B^{\prime})\in{\mathbb{N}}
be the unique level with
B^{\prime}\in A_{k}(\mathfrak{B}_{*}^{i})
. Suppose that the component of
B
in
[\mathfrak{D}(B,r^{\prime})]
has radius less than
r/2
. Then this component only contains blue seeds in the smaller disk
D_{\mathscr{B}}(B,r/2)
. One of these blue seeds
B^{\prime}
must satisfy
k_{1}(B^{\prime})<k_{2}(B^{\prime})
and so
B^{\prime}\in\mathfrak{B}^{2}_{*,k_{1}(B^{\prime})+1}\setminus\mathfrak{B}^{1}_{*,k_{1}(B^{\prime})+1}
. Therefore by applying the first part of Lemma
3.3
, with
X=D_{\mathscr{B}}(B,r/2)
, we must have that

D_{\mathscr{B}}(D_{\mathscr{B}}(B,r/2),r_{k_{1}(B^{\prime})}/2)\not\subset D_{\mathscr{B}}(B,r),

and hence
r_{k_{1}(B^{\prime})}>r
. Therefore
r_{k_{2}(B^{\prime})}>10^{12}r_{k_{1}(B^{\prime})}>100r
by (
17
) and so by the definition of
\mathfrak{D}
the component of
B^{\prime}
in
[\mathfrak{D}(B,r^{\prime})]
has radius at least
r
. This is a contradiction.
∎

We can handle the second effect with straightforward bounds on the deviations of random walks.
For this next lemma, recall that
H_{B}^{++}
consists of all trajectories that may be contributed to the SIR process from
\mathscr{P}_{B}
plus the ignition trajectory for
B
.

Lemma 5.2
.

For
t\in\mathbb{R},s,m>0
, and a block
B
, define the event

\mathcal{E}_{B}(t,s,m)=\bigcap_{a\in H_{B}^{++}}\{\sup_{r\in[-s,s]}|a(t)-a(t+r)|\leq m\}.

(46)

Then
\mathbb{P}[\mathcal{E}_{B}(t,s,m)]\geq 1-CL^{2}e^{-\frac{cm^{2}}{m+s}}
, where
C,c>0
are constants depending on
\mu
.

Proof.

This bound is immediate from Lemma
2.8
, Lemma
4.7
, and a union bound.
∎

The third effect is more difficult to understand, as we have not established any control over the correlations between different
\tau_{B}
’s. We will therefore deal with the issue of the random differences
\tau_{B}-\tau_{B^{\prime}}
with a mixture of different methods. Our main tool will be the bound from (
31
) which crudely guarantees the Lipschitz estimate

|\tau_{B}-\tau_{B}^{\prime}|\leq L^{2}d(B,B^{\prime}).

(47)

As an immediate application of (
47
) we use Lemma
5.2
to control particles that come close to
B
from far away.

Corollary 5.3
.

For blocks
B,B^{\prime}
and
s>0
, define the event

\mathcal{L}_{B}(B^{\prime},s):=\mathcal{E}_{B^{\prime}}(0,s+L^{2}d(B,B^{\prime}),d(B,B^{\prime})/4).

Then on
\mathcal{L}_{B}(B^{\prime},s)
, we have that

\inf\{d(\bar{a}(t),B):t\in[\tau_{B}-s,\tau_{B}+s],a\in H_{B^{\prime}}^{++}\}\geq d(B,B^{\prime})/2.

(48)

Moreover,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}[\mathcal{L}_{B}(B^{\prime},s)]

\displaystyle\geq 1-CL^{2}\exp\left(-\frac{cd(B,B^{\prime})^{2}}{s+L^{2}d(B,B^{\prime})}\right).

Proof.

By (
47
), we have

[\tau_{B}-s,\tau_{B}+s]\subset[\tau_{B}^{\prime}-s-L^{2}d(B,B^{\prime}),\tau_{B}^{\prime}+s+L^{2}d(B,B^{\prime})].

Any particle
a\in H_{B^{\prime}}^{++}
is contained in the block
B^{\prime}
at some point in the interval
[\tau_{B^{\prime}},\tau_{B^{\prime}}+\xi]
.
Therefore for (
48
) to fail, there must be some particle
a\in H_{B^{\prime}}^{++}
whose path in the time interval
[-s-L^{2}d(B,B^{\prime}),s+L^{2}d(B,B^{\prime})]
has diameter at least
d(B,B^{\prime})/2
. This particle would have to travel distance
d(B,B^{\prime})/4
from its location at time
0
in the time interval
[-s-L^{2}d(B,B^{\prime}),s+L^{2}d(B,B^{\prime})]
, implying
\mathcal{L}_{B}(B^{\prime},s)^{c}
.
Lemma
5.2
gives the quantitative bound.
∎

While Lemma
5.1
and Corollary
5.3
do not allow us to rule out all global effects on the local behaviour of the SIR process
X_{t}
, they do allow us to simplify our study to only consider blocks within a
(d_{\mathscr{B}}(B,0)^{1/6}+m)
-radius of
B
for some constant
m
(here
0
is the block containing
(0,0)
). The following corollary is immediate from these results, and sets up the global event
\mathcal{G}_{\nu}
in Theorem
1.3
.

Corollary 5.4
.

For any density
\mu>0
and
\nu<\nu^{-}
, as long as
m=m_{\nu}\in{\mathbb{N}}
is large enough the following event has positive probability.

\mathcal{G}_{\nu}:=\mathcal{C}\cap\bigcap_{B\in\mathscr{B}}\left(\mathcal{D}_{B}(d_{\mathscr{B}}(0,B)^{1/6}+m)\cap\bigcap_{B^{\prime}:d_{\mathscr{B}}(B,B^{\prime})\geq m+d_{\mathscr{B}}(0,B)^{1/6}}\mathcal{L}_{B}(B^{\prime},[d(B,B^{\prime})]^{3/2})\right).

Moreover, by letting
m_{\nu}\to\infty
as
\nu\to 0
we can guarantee that
\mathbb{P}[\mathcal{G}_{\nu}]\to 1
as
\nu\to 0
.

Moving forward, we set
m_{B}=m+d_{\mathscr{B}}(0,B)^{1/6}
to be the locality radius for
B
and assume
\nu
is small enough so that
\mathbb{P}[\mathcal{G}_{\nu}]\geq 1/2
.

6
Upper bound on the density of remaining susceptible particles

In this section, we show that the density of infected and susceptible particles in a region decreasing dramatically shortly after the colouring process passes through. When combined with the estimate in Proposition
2.5
, this will later allow us to conclude that the infection dies out locally after the colouring process passes through (see Section
8
).

6.1
Local infection spread

Fix any block
B
.
We first establish that the following holds for some small fixed
\delta>0
: on the event
\mathcal{G}_{\nu}
,

all particles in
H_{B}
are infected by time
L^{2+\delta}+\tau_{B}
and heal by time
L^{7}

(49)

with probability superpolynomial in
L
.

We will construct a collection of events whose intersection with
\mathcal{G}_{\nu}
implies (
49
). The first of these events,
\mathcal{J}_{B}
, will be a long-range event controlling the coupled SSP, and the remaining events will have short range, only depending on the
\sigma
-algebras
\mathscr{M}_{B^{\prime}}
for
B^{\prime}
in a small radius of
B
. Throughout this section, all bounds hold for sufficiently large
L
given a fixed
\delta>0
.

We let the block
B
be our frame of reference. Setting some notation for the section, let
\mathscr{C}=\mathscr{C}_{B}:=D_{\mathscr{B}}(B,4L^{\delta}),\mathscr{C}^{-}=\mathscr{C}^{-}_{B}:=D_{\mathscr{B}}(B,2L^{\delta})
.

Let
\mathcal{J}_{B}
be the event

\mathcal{J}_{B}=\Big\{\forall B^{\prime}\in\mathscr{C}^{-}:\{B^{\prime}\}\cap[\mathfrak{D}(B^{\prime},L^{\delta/20})]=\{B^{\prime}\}\cap[\mathfrak{D}(B^{\prime},m_{B^{\prime}})]\Big\}.

Using the notation of Lemma
5.1
, we have
\mathcal{J}_{B}=\bigcap_{B^{\prime}\in\mathscr{C}^{-}}\mathcal{D}_{B^{\prime}}(L^{\delta/20},m_{B^{\prime}})
. By equation (
45
), on the event
\mathcal{G}_{\nu}\cap\mathcal{J}_{B}
the components of
\mathscr{C}^{-}\cap[\mathfrak{D}]
agree with a local approximation of radius
L^{\delta/20}
.
By Lemma
5.1
we have that

\mathbb{P}[\mathcal{J}_{B}]\geq 1-\exp(-L^{c\delta/\log\log L}).

(50)

We move on to defining the short-range events.
Our next event implies that every local neighbourhood of radius
L^{\delta/15}
blocks has density at least
\frac{2}{3}
of red blocks. We set

\mathcal{L}_{B}=\Big\{\forall B^{\prime}\in\mathscr{C}^{-}:|\{B^{\prime\prime}\subset D_{\mathscr{B}}(B^{\prime},L^{\delta/15}):B^{\prime\prime}\in[\mathfrak{D}(B^{\prime\prime},L^{\delta/20})]\}|\leq\frac{1}{3}L^{2\delta/15}\Big\}

and have the following strong estimate.

Lemma 6.1
.

We have
\mathbb{P}[\mathcal{L}_{B}]\geq 1-\exp(-cL^{\delta/30})
.

Proof.

For each block
B^{\prime\prime}
we have that
\mathbb{P}(B^{\prime\prime}\in[\mathfrak{D}(B^{\prime\prime},L^{\delta/20})])\leq\frac{1}{4}
for large enough
L
by Corollary
4.6
and (
19
) in Lemma
3.4
. Moreover, any collection of events

\{B_{i}\in[\mathfrak{D}(B_{i},L^{\delta/20})]\},\quad i=1,\dots,k

are independent if
d_{\mathscr{B}}(B_{i},B_{j})>2L^{\delta/20}
for all
i\neq j
.
Applying a standard concentration estimate, see Lemma
6.2
immediately below, yields the bound.
∎

Lemma 6.2
.

Let
p>0
, and let
\{Y_{x}\}_{x\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}}
be a field of Bernoulli
(p_{x})
random variables where
p_{x}\leq p
for all
x
. Suppose that
\{Y_{x}\}_{x\in A}
are independent for any finite set
A\subset\mathbb{Z}^{2}
whose pairwise distances are at least
R
. Then for any set
\Lambda
and any
y>2p|\Lambda|
,

\mathbb{P}[\sum_{x\in\Lambda}Y_{x}\geq y]\leq R^{2}\exp(-cy^{2}/(R^{2}|\Lambda|)).

Similarly, if
p_{x}\geq p
for all
x
then for any set
\Lambda
and any
y<p|\Lambda|/2
we have

\mathbb{P}[\sum_{x\in\Lambda}Y_{x}\leq y]\leq R^{2}\exp(-cy^{2}/(R^{2}|\Lambda|)).

Proof.

We only prove the first inequality as the second is similar. For
(j_{1},j_{2})\in\{0,\ldots,R-1\}^{2}
let

\Lambda_{j_{1},j_{2}}=\{(x_{1},x_{2})\in\Lambda:x_{1}\equiv j_{1}\ \hbox{mod}\ R,x_{2}\equiv j_{2}\ \hbox{mod}\ R\}

If
\sum_{x\in\Lambda}Y_{x}\geq y
then there must exist some
(j_{1},j_{2})
such that

\sum_{x\in\Lambda_{j_{1},j_{2}}}Y_{x}\geq y\left(\frac{1}{4R^{2}}+\frac{3|\Lambda_{j_{1},j_{2}}|}{4|\Lambda|}\right).

By Hoeffding’s inequality and using that
\mathbb{E}\sum_{x\in\Lambda_{j_{1},j_{2}}}Y_{x}\leq|\Lambda_{j_{1},j_{2}}|p\leq\tfrac{|\Lambda_{j_{1},j_{2}}|y}{2|\Lambda|}
we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}[\sum_{x\in\Lambda_{j_{1},j_{2}}}Y_{x}

\displaystyle\geq|\Lambda_{j_{1},j_{2}}|y\left(\frac{1}{4R^{2}|\Lambda_{j_{1},j_{2}}|}+\frac{3}{4|\Lambda|}\right)]

\displaystyle\leq\exp\left(-2|\Lambda_{j_{1},j_{2}}|y^{2}\left(\frac{1}{4R^{2}|\Lambda_{j_{1},j_{2}}|}+\frac{1}{4|\Lambda|}\right)^{2}\right)\leq\exp\left(-c\frac{y^{2}}{R^{2}|\Lambda|}\right).

The final inequality follows by minimizing the exponent in
|\Lambda_{j_{1},j_{2}}|
. The lemma follows by a union bound.
∎

On the event
\mathcal{G}_{\nu}\cap\mathcal{J}_{B}\cap\mathcal{L}_{B}
, for every
B^{\prime}\in\mathscr{C}^{-}
, each ball
D_{\mathscr{B}}(B^{\prime},L^{\delta/15})
is coloured at most one-third blue. All these balls are completely contained in the larger set
\mathscr{C}
. Our next event controls the movement of particles from this larger set.
Define

\mathcal{N}_{B}:=\bigcap_{B^{\prime}\in\mathscr{C}}\bigcap_{a\in H_{B^{\prime}}^{++}}\Big\{|a(t)-a(0)|\leq L^{1+\frac{\delta}{10}}\lceil t/L^{2}\rceil^{\frac{1}{2}+\frac{\delta}{5}}\quad\forall t\in[0,L^{3}]\Big\}.

With notation as in Lemma
5.2
, we have

\mathcal{N}_{B}\supset\bigcap_{B^{\prime}\in\mathscr{C}}\bigcap_{i=1}^{L}\mathcal{E}_{B}(0,iL^{2},(iL^{2})^{1/2+\delta/10}),

so by that lemma and a union bound we have

\mathbb{P}[\mathcal{N}_{B}]\geq 1-\frac{1}{2}\exp(-cL^{\delta/3})

(51)

for large enough
L
.
If
B^{\prime}\in\mathscr{C}
and
B^{\prime\prime}\notin D_{\mathscr{B}}(B^{\prime},L^{3\delta/5})
then
d(B,B^{\prime\prime})\geq L^{1+3\delta/5}/5
by (
27
). The Lipschitz bound (
47
) then implies that on the event
\mathcal{N}_{B}
a particle in
H_{B}^{++}
will not reach
B^{\prime\prime}
before time
\tau_{B}^{\prime\prime}\wedge(\tau_{B^{\prime}}+L^{3})
and thus cannot become the ignition particle for
B^{\prime\prime}
prior to time
\tau_{B^{\prime}}+L^{3}
. It follows that at most
L^{3\delta}
particles in
\bigcup\{H_{B^{\prime}}^{++}:B^{\prime}\in\mathscr{C}\}
become ignition particles prior to time
\tau_{B^{\prime}}+L^{3}
.

A significant difficulty in checking when particles near
B
become infected given only the information in in
\mathscr{M}_{\mathscr{C}}
is that this does not tell us the relative times of the
\tau_{B^{\prime}}
. To accommodate this we will discretize the relative times
\tau_{B}-\tau_{B^{\prime}}
and take a union bound over this discretization. Similarly, we don’t know exactly which blocks are blue so we will take a union bound over the possibilities.

For a block
B
let
\mathscr{U}=\mathscr{U}_{B}=\{0,1\}^{\mathscr{C}}\times\mathbb{Z}^{\mathscr{C}}\times(\mathbb{Z}^{2})^{\mathscr{C}}
.
Let

\displaystyle\mathscr{U}^{*}=\mathscr{U}^{*}_{B}=\bigg\{(r,s,x)\in\mathscr{U}

\displaystyle:\max_{B^{\prime}\in\mathscr{C}}|s(B^{\prime})|\leq 9L^{\delta}\xi,\qquad x(B^{\prime})\in(B^{\prime})^{\#}\;\forall B^{\prime}\in\mathscr{C},

\displaystyle\sum_{B^{\prime\prime}\in D_{\mathscr{B}}(B^{\prime},L^{\delta/15})}r(B^{\prime\prime})\geq\frac{1}{3}L^{2\delta/15}\qquad\forall B^{\prime}\in\mathscr{C}^{-}\bigg\}.

We will be interested in the random variable
U_{B}\in\mathscr{U}
defined by
U_{B}=(r_{B},s_{B},x_{B})
where
r_{B}(B^{\prime\prime})
is the indicator that block
B^{\prime}
is red,
x_{B}(B^{\prime})
is the ignition location for
B^{\prime}
and
s_{B}(B^{\prime})=\lfloor\tau_{B}-\tau_{B^{\prime}}\rfloor
.

Since the colouring process spreads at least at rate
\xi
(see (
31
)) we have that
\max_{B^{\prime}\in\mathscr{C}^{-}}|s_{B}(B^{\prime})|\leq 9L^{\delta}\xi
and
x_{B}(B^{\prime})\in(B^{\prime})^{\#}
for all
B^{\prime}
by construction. Moreover, the condition on
r
required for membership in
\mathscr{U}^{*}
holds for
r_{B}
on the event
\mathcal{G}_{\nu}\cap\mathcal{J}_{B}\cap\mathcal{L}_{B}
. Therefore on this event,
U_{B}\in\mathscr{U}^{*}
. Note that

|\mathscr{U}^{*}|\leq L^{cL^{\delta}}.

(52)

For
u=(r,s,x)\in\mathscr{U}_{B}^{*}
we say a particle
a\in H_{B}^{+}
is
covered
by a particle
a^{\prime}\in Q_{B^{\prime},x(B^{\prime})}
with respect to
u
if
r(B^{\prime})=1
and there exists
t\in[\frac{1}{2}L^{2+\delta},L^{2+\delta}-1]
and
y\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}
such that
a(t)=y
and
a^{\prime}(t^{\prime}+s(B^{\prime}))=y
for all
t^{\prime}\in[t,t+1]
. By construction, if
(r,s,x)=(r_{B},s_{B},x_{B})
and
a
is covered by
a^{\prime}
for
t
and

8
The proof of Theorem
1.3

The results of Section
6
guarantee that most particles in a region recover in an
O(1)
amount of time after the infection front initially visits that region. The results of Section
2.1
then guarantee that the infection must die out in this region soon afterwards, and so the infection will typically pass through a region in an
O(1)
amount of time. The results of Section
7
ensure that a particle has some probability of surviving for an arbitrarily long time after the infection enters its block, which together with the results of Sections
6
and
2.1
indicates that some particles will survive forever. In summary, together the results of these three sections suggest the main content Theorem
1.3
. However, putting them together is delicate. This is the goal of the present section.
