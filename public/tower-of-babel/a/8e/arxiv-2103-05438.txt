Electric Vector Potential Approach in Electrostatics : The Surface Electrode
Robert Salazar, Camilo Bayona-Roa, Gabriel Téllez

Abstract
Electric vector potential Θ ⁡ ( 𝒓 ) \Theta(\boldsymbol{r}) is a legitimate but rarely used tool to calculate the steady electric field in free-charge regions. Commonly, it is preferred to employ the scalar electric potential Φ ⁡ ( 𝒓 ) \Phi(\boldsymbol{r}) rather than Θ ⁡ ( 𝒓 ) \Theta(\boldsymbol{r}) in most of the electrostatic problems. However, the electric vector potential formulation can be a viable representation to study certain systems. One of them is the surface electrode SE, a planar finite region 𝒜 − \mathcal{A}_{-} kept at a fixed electric potential with the rest grounded including a gap of thickness ν \nu between electrodes. In this document we use the Helmholtz Decomposition Theorem and the electric vector potential formulation to provide integral expressions for the surface charge density and the electric field of the SE of arbitrary contour ∂ 𝒜 \partial\mathcal{A} . We also present an alternative derivation of the result found in [M. Oliveira and J. A. Miranda 2001 Eur. J. Phys. 22 31] for the gapless ( ν = 0 \nu=0 ) surface electrode GSE without invoking any analogy between the GSE and magnetostatics. It is shown that electric vector potential and the electric field of the gapped circular SE at any point can be obtained from an average of the gapless solution on the gap. Keywords: Electric vector potential, surface-electrode, Helmholtz Decomposition, Green’s theorem.

Introduction

Calculation of electric field via the scalar electric potential
\Phi(\boldsymbol{r})
is an standard procedure in in electrostatics. However, the steady electric field in free charge regions is an irrotational field
\nabla\times\boldsymbol{E}=0
but also divergence free
\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{E}=0

\boldsymbol{E}=\nabla\times\Theta(\boldsymbol{r})\hskip 14.22636pt\textbf{if}\hskip 14.22636pt\rho(\boldsymbol{r})=0

\nabla^{2}\Theta(\boldsymbol{r})=0\hskip 14.22636pt\textbf{if}\hskip 14.22636pt\rho(\boldsymbol{r})=0

\nabla\cdot\Theta(\boldsymbol{r})=0
is imposed. Even when electric vector potential is a valid choice to place some electrostatics problems, it is commonly preferred the scalar potential. A similar situation occurs in magnetostatics where vector magnetic potential is usual a better choice than the scalar magnetic potential in free-current regions. Helmholtz decomposition theorem

The Helmholtz theorem
[
14
]
[
15
, p. 147]
[
16
,
17
]
states that a twice continuously differentiable field
\boldsymbol{P}(\boldsymbol{r})
on a bounded region
\Gamma\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
can be decomposed into a irrotational (curl-free) plus a solenoidal (divergence-free) fields as follows

\boldsymbol{P}(\boldsymbol{r})=-\nabla\zeta(\boldsymbol{r})+\nabla\times\boldsymbol{\Omega}(\boldsymbol{r})

where

\zeta(\boldsymbol{r})=\frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{\Gamma}\frac{\nabla^{\prime}\cdot\boldsymbol{P}(\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime})}{|\boldsymbol{r}-\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}|}d^{3}\boldsymbol{r}-\frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{\partial\Gamma}\frac{\hat{n}^{\prime}\cdot\boldsymbol{P}(\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime})}{|\boldsymbol{r}-\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}|}d^{2}\boldsymbol{r}

and

\boldsymbol{\Omega}(\boldsymbol{r})=\frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{\Gamma}\frac{\nabla^{\prime}\times\boldsymbol{P}(\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime})}{|\boldsymbol{r}-\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}|}d^{3}\boldsymbol{r}-\frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{\partial\Gamma}\frac{\hat{n}^{\prime}\times\boldsymbol{P}(\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime})}{|\boldsymbol{r}-\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}|}d^{2}\boldsymbol{r}

when
\boldsymbol{P}(\boldsymbol{r})
decreases asymptotically (
r\rightarrow\infty
). Decomposing the electric field
\boldsymbol{P}(\boldsymbol{r})=\boldsymbol{E}(\boldsymbol{r})
in the region
\mathfrak{D}
then

\boldsymbol{E}(\boldsymbol{r})=-\nabla\zeta(\boldsymbol{r})+\nabla\times\boldsymbol{\Omega}(\boldsymbol{r})

(3)

where

\zeta(\boldsymbol{r})=\frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_{o}}\int_{\Gamma}\frac{\rho(\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime})}{|\boldsymbol{r}-\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}|}d^{3}\boldsymbol{r}-\frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{\partial\Gamma}\frac{\hat{n}^{\prime}\cdot\boldsymbol{E}(\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime})}{|\boldsymbol{r}-\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}|}d^{2}\boldsymbol{r}

where we have used the Gauss’s law
\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{E}(\boldsymbol{r})=\rho(\boldsymbol{r})/\epsilon_{o}
. Here
\hat{n}^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime})
is the normal outward vector of
\partial\mathcal{D}
, and it is
\hat{n}^{\prime}=-\hat{z}
on
\partial\mathcal{D}_{z=0}
. Note that volume integral is zero since there is no charge density in
\mathcal{D}
. However, there is an unknown but not null charge density spread on
\partial\mathcal{D}_{z=0}
given by

\sigma(x,y)=2\epsilon_{o}\lim_{z\to 0^{+}}E_{z}(x,y,z)

(4)

therefore

\zeta(\boldsymbol{r})=\frac{1}{8\pi\epsilon_{o}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\frac{\sigma(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})}{|\boldsymbol{r}-\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}|}dx^{\prime}dy^{\prime}.

On the other hand, the field
\boldsymbol{\Omega}(\boldsymbol{r})
can be obtained from

\boldsymbol{\Omega}(\boldsymbol{r})=\frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{\mathfrak{D}}\frac{\nabla^{\prime}\times\boldsymbol{E}(\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime})}{|\boldsymbol{r}-\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}|}d^{3}\boldsymbol{r}-\frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{\partial\mathfrak{D}}\frac{\hat{n}^{\prime}\times\boldsymbol{E}(\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime})}{|\boldsymbol{r}-\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}|}d^{2}\boldsymbol{r}.

which can be simplified as follows

\boldsymbol{\Omega}(\boldsymbol{r})=\frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\frac{\hat{z}\times\boldsymbol{E}(\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime})}{|\boldsymbol{r}-\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}|}dx^{\prime}dy^{\prime}.

(5)

because the steady electric field is irrotational. Since
\hat{z}\times\boldsymbol{E}(\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime})=(\partial_{y}\Phi(\boldsymbol{r}),-\partial_{x}\Phi(\boldsymbol{r}),0)
in Cartesian coordinates, then the components of
\boldsymbol{\Omega}(\boldsymbol{r})=(\Omega_{x}(\boldsymbol{r}),\Omega_{x}(\boldsymbol{r}),0)
are given by

\Omega_{x}(\boldsymbol{r})=\frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\frac{1}{|\boldsymbol{r}-\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}|}\partial_{y}\Phi(x^{\prime},y^{\prime},0)dx^{\prime}dy^{\prime}\hskip 14.22636pt\mbox{and}\hskip 14.22636pt\Omega_{y}(\boldsymbol{r})=-\frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\frac{1}{|\boldsymbol{r}-\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}|}\partial_{x}\Phi(x^{\prime},y^{\prime},0)dx^{\prime}dy^{\prime}.

The
x
-component of
\boldsymbol{\Omega}(\boldsymbol{r})
can be simplified by performing a partial integration with respect the
y^{\prime}
-coordinate

\Omega_{x}(\boldsymbol{r})=\frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left[\lim_{y^{\prime}\to\infty}\frac{\Phi(x^{\prime},y^{\prime},0)}{|\boldsymbol{r}-\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}|}-\lim_{y^{\prime}\to-\infty}\frac{\Phi(x^{\prime},y^{\prime},0)}{|\boldsymbol{r}-\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}|}-\int_{\mathbb{R}}\Phi(x^{\prime},y^{\prime},0)\frac{y-y^{\prime}}{|\boldsymbol{r}-\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}|^{3}}dy^{\prime}\right]dx^{\prime}.

Now, the Dirichlet boundary condition states that

\Phi(x^{\prime},y^{\prime},0)=V_{o}\hskip 14.22636pt\textbf{if}\hskip 14.22636pt(x^{\prime},y^{\prime},0)\in\mathcal{A}_{-}\hskip 14.22636pt\mbox{and}\hskip 14.22636pt\Phi(x^{\prime},y^{\prime},0)=0\hskip 14.22636pt\textbf{if}\hskip 14.22636pt(x^{\prime},y^{\prime},0)\in\mathcal{A}_{+}.

In the gap the scalar electric potential is unknown. Therefore

\Omega_{x}(\boldsymbol{r})=-\frac{V_{o}}{4\pi}\int_{\mathcal{A}_{-}}\frac{y-y^{\prime}}{|\boldsymbol{r}-\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}|^{3}}dx^{\prime}dy^{\prime}-\frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{\mathcal{G}}\Phi(x^{\prime},y^{\prime},0)\frac{y-y^{\prime}}{|\boldsymbol{r}-\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}|^{3}}dx^{\prime}dy^{\prime}

this is

\Omega_{x}(\boldsymbol{r})=-\frac{V_{o}}{4\pi}\int_{\mathcal{A}_{-}}\frac{\partial}{\partial y^{\prime}}\left(\frac{1}{|\boldsymbol{r}-\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}|}\right)dx^{\prime}dy^{\prime}-\frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{\mathcal{G}}\Phi_{\mathcal{G}}(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\frac{y-y^{\prime}}{|\boldsymbol{r}-\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}|^{3}}dx^{\prime}dy^{\prime}\hskip 14.22636pt\mbox{since}\hskip 14.22636pt\frac{\partial}{\partial y^{\prime}}\left(\frac{1}{|\boldsymbol{r}-\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}|}\right)=\frac{y-y^{\prime}}{|\boldsymbol{r}-\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}|^{3}}.

With
\Phi_{\mathcal{G}}(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})
the scalar electric potential in
\mathcal{G}
. A similar procedure can be used to simplify
\Omega_{x}(\boldsymbol{r})
, the result is

\Omega_{y}(\boldsymbol{r})=\frac{V_{o}}{4\pi}\int_{\mathcal{A}_{-}}\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{\prime}}\left(\frac{1}{|\boldsymbol{r}-\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}|}\right)dx^{\prime}dy^{\prime}+\frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{\mathcal{G}}\Phi_{\mathcal{G}}(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\frac{x-x^{\prime}}{|\boldsymbol{r}-\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}|^{3}}dx^{\prime}dy^{\prime}

The vector field
\boldsymbol{\Omega}(\boldsymbol{r})
takes the form

\boldsymbol{\Omega}(\boldsymbol{r})=\Omega_{x}(\boldsymbol{r})\hat{x}+\Omega_{y}(\boldsymbol{r})\hat{y}=\frac{V_{o}}{4\pi}\int_{\mathcal{A}_{-}}\boldsymbol{\xi}(\boldsymbol{r},\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime})d^{2}\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}+\frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{\mathcal{G}}\Phi_{\mathcal{G}}(\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime})\boldsymbol{\xi}(\boldsymbol{r},\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime})d^{2}\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}

with

\boldsymbol{\xi}(\boldsymbol{r},\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}):=\left\{-\hat{x}\frac{\partial}{\partial y^{\prime}}\left(\frac{1}{|\boldsymbol{r}-\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}|}\right)+\hat{y}\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{\prime}}\left(\frac{1}{|\boldsymbol{r}-\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}|}\right)\right\}

which can be written as follows

\boldsymbol{\Omega}(\boldsymbol{r})=\frac{V_{o}}{4\pi}\int_{\mathcal{A}_{-}}\left\{\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{\prime}}\left(\frac{\hat{y}}{|\boldsymbol{r}-\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}|}\right)-\frac{\partial}{\partial y^{\prime}}\left(\frac{\hat{x}}{|\boldsymbol{r}-\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}|}\right)\right\}dx^{\prime}dy^{\prime}+\frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{\mathcal{G}}\Phi_{\mathcal{G}}(\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime})\boldsymbol{\xi}(\boldsymbol{r},\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime})d^{2}\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}

The first term in the right of the previous equation can be simplified by using the the Green’s theorem which states that

\int_{\mathcal{A}}\left[\frac{\partial}{\partial x}M(x,y)-\frac{\partial}{\partial y}L(x,y)\right]dxdy=\rcirclerightint_{\partial\mathcal{A}}L(x,y)dx+M(x,y)dy

(6)

for a flat region
\mathcal{A}
bounded by
\partial\mathcal{A}
oriented positively. Identifying

and
L(x,y)
as follows

M\longrightarrow\frac{\hat{y}}{|\boldsymbol{r}-\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}|}\hskip 14.22636pt\mbox{and}\hskip 14.22636ptL\longrightarrow\frac{\hat{x}}{|\boldsymbol{r}-\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}|}

then

\boldsymbol{\Omega}(\boldsymbol{r})=\frac{V_{o}}{4\pi}\oint_{\partial\mathcal{A}_{-}}\frac{\hat{x}}{|\boldsymbol{r}-\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}|}dx^{\prime}+\frac{\hat{y}}{|\boldsymbol{r}-\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}|}dy^{\prime}+\frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{\mathcal{G}}\Phi_{\mathcal{G}}(\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime})\boldsymbol{\xi}(\boldsymbol{r},\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime})d^{2}\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}

or

\boldsymbol{\Omega}(\boldsymbol{r})=\frac{1}{4\pi}\left[V_{o}\oint_{\partial\mathcal{A}_{-}}\frac{d\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}}{|\boldsymbol{r}-\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}|}+\int_{\mathcal{G}}\Phi_{\mathcal{G}}(\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime})\boldsymbol{\xi}(\boldsymbol{r},\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime})d^{2}\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}\right].

Hence, the field

depends exclusively on the shape of the contour
\partial\mathcal{A}
. It is expected that the scalar field

adding both equations and dividing by 2, then

\boldsymbol{E}(\boldsymbol{r})=-\nabla\left(\frac{\Phi(\boldsymbol{r})}{2}\right)+\nabla\times\left(\frac{\boldsymbol{\Theta}(\boldsymbol{r})}{2}\right)

(7)

Comparing Eqs. (

\nabla\times[\psi(\boldsymbol{r})\times\boldsymbol{F}(\boldsymbol{r})]=\psi(\boldsymbol{r})\nabla\times\boldsymbol{F}(\boldsymbol{r})+\nabla\psi(\boldsymbol{r})\times\boldsymbol{F}(\boldsymbol{r})

\boxed{\boldsymbol{E}^{\mbox{\tiny{GSE}}}(\boldsymbol{r})=\frac{V_{o}}{2\pi}\textbf{\mbox{sgn}}(z)\rcirclerightint_{\partial\mathcal{A}}\frac{d\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}\times(\boldsymbol{r}-\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime})}{|\boldsymbol{r}-\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}|^{3}}}

\lim_{\nu\to 0}\boldsymbol{\Theta}(\boldsymbol{r})=\frac{V_{o}}{2\pi}\rcirclerightint_{\partial\mathcal{A}}\frac{d\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}}{|\boldsymbol{r}-\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}|}=\boldsymbol{\Theta}^{\mbox{\tiny{GSE}}}(\boldsymbol{r})

since
d\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}=\left[\dot{{R}}(\phi^{\prime})\hat{u}(\phi^{\prime})+{R}(\phi^{\prime})\hat{\phi}(\phi^{\prime})\right]d\phi^{\prime}
recovering Eq. (
10
) as we expected.

4.4
The magnetic analogue of the gapped SE

The electric field of the gapped SE can be obtained by applying the curl to Eq. (
27
). To this aim, we may use

\nabla\times\frac{\boldsymbol{{W}}_{\nu}(\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime})}{|\boldsymbol{r}-\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}|}=\nabla\left(\frac{1}{|\boldsymbol{r}-\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}|}\right)\times\boldsymbol{{W}}_{\nu}(\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime})=\boldsymbol{{W}}_{\nu}(\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime})\times\frac{\boldsymbol{r}-\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}}{|\boldsymbol{r}-\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}|^{3}}.

therefore

\boldsymbol{E}(\boldsymbol{r})=\frac{V_{o}}{2\pi}\textbf{\mbox{sgn}}(z)\int_{{G}}\frac{\boldsymbol{{W}}_{\nu}(\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime})d^{2}\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}\times(\boldsymbol{r}-\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime})}{|\boldsymbol{r}-\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}|^{3}}\hskip 7.11317pt\mbox{BSL law for the gapped SE}

(30)

is a generalization of the Eq. (
12
) but including a gap of thickness
\nu
between both electrodes. Of course, we may recover the gapless result Eq. (
12
) from Eq. (
30
) by using Eq. (
29
). Note that Eq. (
30
) is similar to the one employed for the computation of the magnetic field
\boldsymbol{B}(\boldsymbol{r})
due to a ribbon on a circuit
{G}
of thickness
\nu
carrying a current surface density
\boldsymbol{K}

\boldsymbol{B}(\boldsymbol{r})=\frac{\mu_{o}}{4\pi}\int_{{G}}\frac{\boldsymbol{K}(\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime})d^{2}\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}\times(\boldsymbol{r}-\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime})}{|\boldsymbol{r}-\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}|^{3}}

which is a formulation of the Biot-Savart law in magnetostatics. In the electrostatic problem, the weight vector
\boldsymbol{{W}}_{\nu}(\boldsymbol{r})
is playing the role of density current
\boldsymbol{K}(\boldsymbol{r})
. Sign function in Eq. (
30
) is absent in the biot-savart magnetic analogue since the electric field is not a fully solenoidal field as occurs with the magnetic field. The divergence in polar coordinates of the weight vector is

\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{{W}}_{\nu}(\boldsymbol{r})=\frac{1}{u}\frac{\partial}{\partial u}\left[u(\boldsymbol{{W}}_{\nu})_{u}\right]+\frac{1}{u}\frac{\partial}{\partial\phi}\left[(\boldsymbol{{W}}_{\nu})_{\phi}\right]

where the components of the weight vector are given by Eq. (
28
), hence

\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{{W}}_{\nu}(\boldsymbol{r})=\frac{1}{uV_{o}}\left[\frac{\partial^{2}\Phi_{\mathcal{G}}}{\partial u\partial\phi}-\frac{\partial^{2}\Phi_{\mathcal{G}}}{\partial\phi\partial u}\right]=0.

This is also a property that density current in magnetostatics has
\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{K}(\boldsymbol{r})=0
. In the case of a bidimensional current ribbon loop, this continuity equation ensures the charge conservation, thus the incoming and outcoming charge of each point of the ribbon loop must be equal. Magnetostatics is described by using the Ampere’s law

\nabla\times\boldsymbol{B}(\boldsymbol{r})=\mu_{o}\boldsymbol{J}(\boldsymbol{r})

with
\boldsymbol{J}(\boldsymbol{r})
the volume charge density. For the case of a ribbon loop
\boldsymbol{J}=\delta(z)\boldsymbol{K}
for each point belonging to the ribbon. At this point, one can formulate the following trick question : by virtue of the analogies and the Ampere’s law, should
\nabla\times\boldsymbol{E}(\boldsymbol{r})
be proportional to
\boldsymbol{{W}}_{\nu}(\boldsymbol{r})
since the weight vector in the SE gap? Of course, We know that the answer to that question is no, since the
\nabla\times\boldsymbol{E}(\boldsymbol{r})
is strictly zero in electrostatics. Even when, the weight vector
\boldsymbol{{W}}_{\nu}(\boldsymbol{r})
a non-zero vector field on the gap, the electric field given by Eq. (
30
) must be zero. To check that let us take the curl of this expression

\nabla\times\boldsymbol{E}(\boldsymbol{r})=\nabla\times\left\{2\textbf{\mbox{sgn}}(z)\frac{V_{o}}{4\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\frac{\delta(z)\boldsymbol{{W}}_{\nu}(\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime})d^{3}\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}\times(\boldsymbol{r}-\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime})}{|\boldsymbol{r}-\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}|^{3}}\right\}

using the vector calculus identity Eq. (
11
), then

\nabla\times\boldsymbol{E}(\boldsymbol{r})=2\textbf{\mbox{sgn}}(z)\nabla\times\left\{\frac{V_{o}}{4\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\frac{\delta(z)\boldsymbol{{W}}_{\nu}(\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime})d^{3}\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}\times(\boldsymbol{r}-\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime})}{|\boldsymbol{r}-\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}|^{3}}\right\}+2\nabla\textbf{\mbox{sgn}}(z)\times\left\{\frac{V_{o}}{4\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\frac{\delta(z)\boldsymbol{{W}}_{\nu}(\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime})d^{3}\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}\times(\boldsymbol{r}-\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime})}{|\boldsymbol{r}-\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}|^{3}}\right\}

this can be written as follows

\nabla\times\boldsymbol{E}(\boldsymbol{r})=2\textbf{sgn}(z)\delta(z)V_{o}\left[\boldsymbol{{W}}_{\nu}(\boldsymbol{r})+\hat{z}\times\frac{\boldsymbol{E}(\boldsymbol{r})}{V_{o}}\right]

(31)

if
z\neq 0
, where we have used
\nabla\textbf{sgn}(z)=2\delta(z)\hat{z}
and

\nabla\times\left\{\frac{V_{o}}{4\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\frac{\delta(z)\boldsymbol{{W}}_{\nu}(\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime})d^{3}\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}\times(\boldsymbol{r}-\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime})}{|\boldsymbol{r}-\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}|^{3}}\right\}=V_{o}\boldsymbol{{W}}_{\nu}(\boldsymbol{r}).

Since
\delta(z)=0
if
z\neq 0
, then we have
\nabla\times\boldsymbol{E}(\boldsymbol{r})=0
from Eq. (
31
) for any point in the
\mathbb{R}^{3}
not belonging to the SE and its gap as it should be.

Conclusion

We demonstrated that it is possible to use standard vector calculus theorems to derive the electrostatic vector potential of the SE. Computation of electric field from the electric vector potential
\boldsymbol{\Theta}(\boldsymbol{r})
is straightforward and algebraically less demanding than the one obtained from
\Phi(\boldsymbol{r})
when no analogies with magnetostatics are used (see Section
3.1
). As an illustrative example, the electric vector potential of circular SE, was used to determine its surface charge density finding that this system is globally neutral. As a future work, we expect that the strategy described in this document eventually could be used to map electrostatic problems to simpler ones including BSL integrals. One candidate is the non-planar SE where the magnetic analogue in general is unknown due to the surface curvature.

B.1
First identity

Using the product rule

\nabla\left[f(\boldsymbol{r})g(\boldsymbol{r})\right]=\left[\nabla f(\boldsymbol{r})\right]g(\boldsymbol{r})+f(\boldsymbol{r})\left[\nabla g(\boldsymbol{r})\right]

then

\nabla\left[\lim_{z^{\prime}\rightarrow 0^{+}}\frac{z-z^{\prime}}{|\boldsymbol{r}-\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}|^{3}}\right]=\lim_{z^{\prime}\rightarrow 0^{+}}(z-z^{\prime})\nabla\lim_{z^{\prime}\rightarrow 0^{+}}\frac{1}{|\boldsymbol{r}-\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}|^{3}}+\left[\nabla\lim_{z^{\prime}\rightarrow 0^{+}}(z-z^{\prime})\right]\lim_{z^{\prime}\rightarrow 0^{+}}\frac{1}{|\boldsymbol{r}-\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}|^{3}}

Now

\nabla\lim_{z^{\prime}\rightarrow 0^{+}}\frac{1}{|\boldsymbol{r}-\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}|^{3}}=-\lim_{z^{\prime}\rightarrow 0^{+}}\nabla^{\prime}\frac{1}{|\boldsymbol{r}-\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}|^{3}}

and

\nabla\lim_{z^{\prime}\rightarrow 0^{+}}(z-z^{\prime})=\nabla z=\hat{z}=-\lim_{z^{\prime}\rightarrow 0^{+}}\nabla^{\prime}(z-z^{\prime})

\nabla\left[\lim_{z^{\prime}\rightarrow 0^{+}}\frac{z-z^{\prime}}{|\boldsymbol{r}-\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}|^{3}}\right]=\lim_{z^{\prime}\rightarrow 0^{+}}(z-z^{\prime})\left[-\lim_{z^{\prime}\rightarrow 0^{+}}\nabla^{\prime}\frac{1}{|\boldsymbol{r}-\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}|^{3}}\right]+\left[-\lim_{z^{\prime}\rightarrow 0^{+}}\nabla^{\prime}(z-z^{\prime})\right]\lim_{z^{\prime}\rightarrow 0^{+}}\frac{1}{|\boldsymbol{r}-\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}|^{3}}

this is

\nabla\left[\lim_{z^{\prime}\rightarrow 0^{+}}\frac{z-z^{\prime}}{|\boldsymbol{r}-\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}|^{3}}\right]=-\lim_{z^{\prime}\rightarrow 0^{+}}(z-z^{\prime})\left[\nabla^{\prime}\left(\frac{1}{|\boldsymbol{r}-\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}|^{3}}\right)+\frac{\nabla^{\prime}(z-z^{\prime})}{|\boldsymbol{r}-\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}|^{3}}\right]

using again the product rule

\nabla\left[\lim_{z^{\prime}\rightarrow 0^{+}}\frac{z-z^{\prime}}{|\boldsymbol{r}-\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}|^{3}}\right]=-\left[\lim_{z^{\prime}\rightarrow 0^{+}}\nabla^{\prime}\frac{z-z^{\prime}}{|\boldsymbol{r}-\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}|^{3}}\right].

(35)
