Kondo-Zeno crossover in the dynamics of a monitored quantum dot
Matthieu Vanhoecke, Marco Schirò

Abstract
Continuously monitoring a quantum system can strongly affect its properties and even suppress its coherent evolution via the Quantum Zeno effect. Well understood for few body quantum systems, the role of quantum measurements on entangled many-body states is still largely unexplored. Here we focus on one of the simplest entangled many-body state, arising via the Kondo effect in a strongly interacting quantum dot coupled to a metallic bath, and investigate the effect of continuous monitoring of the dot total charge. We show that the decay rate of an initially polarized spin displays a crossover from Kondo screening, with a decay rate controlled by interactions, to Quantum Zeno effect, with a decay rate which decreases with bare dissipation as the monitoring rate is increased. Remarkably we show that the long-lived Kondo state is robust to weak dissipation, as further confirmed by the dot spectral function which features a clear Kondo peak at finite dissipation, even in a regime where charge fluctuations and the associated Hubbard bands have been quenched by the monitoring protocol. We derive an effective model for the long-time dynamics which is described, at weak dissipation, by a non-Hermitian Kondo model with complex-valued spin exchange which is known to host exotic low-energy physics and a dissipative phase transition between Kondo and non-Kondo steady-state. Finally, as the dephasing is increased heating due to doublon production takes over and control the spin decay.

In this work we investigate the effect of continuous monitoring on the dynamics of Kondo effect. Specifically we study the time evolution of an interacting quantum dot coupled to a metallic bath, in presence of additional dissipation (dephasing) due to monitoring of quantum dot’s total charge (see Fig.
1

We show that the dynamics of an initially polarised spin displays a crossover from Kondo to Zeno screening, as dissipation is increased, as highlighted by a non-monotonous spin-relaxation rate as a function of dissipation. Using a Schrieffer-Wolff transformation, we show that the effective model controlling this crossover takes the form of a non-Hermitian Kondo model, with complex (i.e. coherent and dissipative) Kondo coupling and in presence of additional dissipative terms describing doublon production. The former controls the physics at weak monitoring and leads to a robust Kondo effect even in presence of dissipation, as we further confirm by computing the spectral function and the impurity entanglement, while the latter dominate at strong dephasing and give rise to local heating.

Effect of Finite Temperature

We now consider the effect of a finite temperature
T
in the metallic bath on the spin relaxation and the Kondo-Zeno crossover. To this aim we compute the dynamics of the dot magnetization and extract the spin relaxation rate
\tau_{K}^{-1}
, that we plot it in Fig.
3
(a) as a function of
\gamma
for different values of temperatures

U=8\Gamma
. We see that upon increasing temperature the Kondo-Zeno crossover drifts towards lower value of dissipation. In particular we see that the weak dissipation regime is strongly temperature dependent, which is compatible with the interpretation given above that this regime is where Kondo physics remain intact. At large dissipation on the other hand the spin-decay rate is essentially temperature independent. If we now fix the dissipation and increase temperature, see Fig.
3
(b), we see that the spin decay rate generally increases with temperature, i.e. the dynamics becomes faster due to thermal excitations in the bath. However the dependence from the dissipation rate
\gamma
reveals a non-monotonic behavior: at low

we see that the spin decay rate increases with
\gamma

\gamma

depends on the interaction strength (See Supplementary Note 3). We can interpret this result as a finite-temperature signature of the Kondo-Zeno crossover discussed before. Our results imply that the Kondo side of the crossover is strongly dependent on temperature while the Zeno side is robust to finite temperature effects.

Dot Spectral Function and Kondo Resonance

A key quantity to characterize the emergence of the Kondo effect, which is also experimentally accessible via tunneling spectroscopy, is the dot spectral function
A_{\sigma}(\omega)
defined as the imaginary part of the dot retarded Green’s function
G^{R}_{\sigma}(\omega)

[
5
]
,

\displaystyle A_{\sigma}(\omega)=-\frac{1}{\pi}\Im\left[G^{R}_{\sigma}(\omega)\right]\,.

(5)

where in the time domain
G^{R}_{\sigma}(t,t^{\prime})=-i\theta(t-t^{\prime})\langle\{d_{\sigma}(t),d^{\dagger}_{\sigma}(t^{\prime})\}\rangle

Adding a small dephasing rate

In this regime the spectral function features essentially only a coherent Kondo peak at zero frequency, while charge fluctuations responsible for the Hubbard bands have been quenched by the monitoring process. This is a striking result that is entirely due to the interplay between interaction and dissipation: indeed for a non-interacting dot one can show that dephasing continuously reduces the strength of the coherent peak in the spectral function (see Supplementary Note 3). The robustness of the Kondo peak to weak charge monitoring is consistent with our results on the spin-decay rate
\tau_{K}^{-1}

2

4

We emphasize however that a direct connection between spin decay rate and width of the coherent Kondo peak is well established only in absence of dissipation, while for

Effective Model For Kondo-Zeno Crossover

In order to understand the emergence of the Kondo-Zeno crossover we derive an effective model that describes our system in the regime when local energy scales, such as interaction
U
and dissipation
\gamma
are larger than the coupling to the metallic bath
V_{\mathbf{k}}
. In equilibrium this is achieved by the standard Schrieffer-Wolff transformation
[
5
]
which effectively integrates out charge fluctuations and leads to the Kondo model, describing the antiferromagnetic coupling between the dot’s spin and the bath. In the present case the dynamics of the system is non-unitary due to the monitoring process and described by the Lindblad master equation and this has important consequences on the nature of the effective model. In particular, using a dissipative Schrieffer-Wolff transformation (See Supplementary Note 4) on the dissipative Anderson impurity model in Eq (
S99
) we can obtain an effective non-unitary dynamics at second order in the hybridization. Projecting the dynamics onto the singly occupied sector of the dot, which as we have seen above is justified for strong interactions and weak dissipation, we can show that this non-unitary dynamics is generated by a non-Hermitian Hamiltonian of the form

\displaystyle H_{\rm eff,K}=-\sum_{\mathbf{q}\mathbf{k}}J_{\mathbf{k}\mathbf{q}}\left(\Phi_{\mathbf{q}}^{\dagger}\frac{\vec{\sigma}}{2}\Phi_{\mathbf{k}}\right)\cdot\vec{S}_{\rm d}

(6)

where
\Phi^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{k}}=(c_{\mathbf{k}\uparrow}^{\dagger},c_{\mathbf{k}\downarrow}^{\dagger})
and
\vec{S}_{\rm d}=\sum_{\sigma\sigma^{{}^{\prime}}}d^{\dagger}_{\sigma}\vec{\tau}_{\sigma\sigma^{{}^{\prime}}}d_{\sigma^{{}^{\prime}}}
is the dot spin with
\vec{\tau}_{\sigma\sigma^{{}^{\prime}}}
the Pauli matrices. Eq. (
6
) describes a non-Hermitian Kondo model
[
52
,
55
,
65
]
, due to the complex-valued spin-exchange coupling
J_{\mathbf{k}\mathbf{q}}
renormalised by dissipation, which reads (by setting
\epsilon_{\mathbf{k}}=\epsilon_{\mathbf{q}}=0
and dropping the momentum dependence of the hybridization
V_{\mathbf{k}}\simeq V
)

\displaystyle J_{\mathbf{q}\mathbf{k}}=J_{R}+iJ_{I}=-\frac{8V^{2}U}{U^{2}+\gamma^{2}}-i\frac{8V^{2}\gamma}{U^{2}+\gamma^{2}}

(7)

The effective non-Hermitian Hamiltonian captures the competition between Kondo and Zeno screening. This is more clearly seen by considering first the weak dissipation regime
V\ll\gamma\ll U
. Here we have
J_{R}\gg J_{I}
and the system is in the Kondo regime, with few excited doublons that can be safely projected out, yet with a small imaginary Kondo coupling. Results on the non-Hermitian Kondo model suggest a transition from Kondo to a non-Kondo state above a critical value of
J_{I}

[
52
,
66
,
65
]
. This picture is compatible with our NCA results both for the spin-decay rate, which at large
U
shows robustness against dissipation (See. Fig.
2
(b)) and for the impurity spectral function (See. Fig.
4
(a)). On the other hand, as we increase the dissipation above
\gamma\sim U
new physics emerge. First, we see from Eq. (
7
) that
J_{I}
becomes maximal and then decreases to zero as
1/\gamma
. This is consistent with the spin-decay rate
\tau_{K}^{-1}
which therefore in this regime appears to be controlled by dissipation rather than Kondo screening. Furthermore for
\gamma\sim U
other dissipative channels open up beyond the single-occupied manifold, such as doublon-holon production terms, which enter the effective Lindbladian and control the dynamics of the system (See Supplementary Note 4). As confirmed by the NCA results in this regime doublon production becomes substantial and the system locally heats up, leading also to a decay of the initially polarized spin.

Finally we conclude by briefly discussing the role played by charge monitoring. In particular, we can show that when the monitoring protocol acts on the spin rather than on the charge degrees of freedom of the quantum dot, then the Kondo peak is quickly destroyed by even a weak dissipation (See Supplementary Note 5).
This result is consistent with our interpretation that the non-trivial Kondo physics in our problem arises because the monitoring protocol does not compete with the spin sector. Interestingly, it can be again understood by means of a Schrieffer-Wolff transformation, where now the effective non-Hermitian Kondo model is supplemented by a dissipative, quantum jump-like, spin-spin Kondo interaction at the same order as the purely non-Hermitian coupling (See Supplementary Note 5). Such a dissipative coupling, which is also anisotropic thus breaking the full spin symmetry of the Kondo state, is likely responsible for the rapid destruction of the Kondo state, even though a full renormalization group study of its interplay with non-Hermitian Kondo couplings is left for future work. This result highlights the key role played by our monitoring protocol to give rise to a non trivial Kondo effect.

Discussion

In this work we have studied the dissipative dynamics of a strongly correlated quantum dot, described by the Anderson impurity model, under monitoring of the dot’s total charge. We have shown that while charge fluctuations are quenched by the monitoring protocol, the spin dynamics retains a non-trivial many-body dynamics which is strongly affected by the dissipation and displays a crossover in the spin relaxation rate between Kondo screening due to interactions and Zeno effect due to monitoring. This crossover leaves clear signatures at finite temperatures as well as in the steady-state spectral function of the dot, where the Kondo peak at weak monitoring remains robust to dissipation, while Hubbard bands are collapsed due to monitoring of the charge.

The response of a strongly correlated Kondo state to monitoring is a topic that has attracted quite some interest recently. Previous investigations have focused on the effect of projective measurements of singly occupied population on the steady-state spectral function using weak coupling perturbation theory
[
55
]
or on dissipation due to two-body losses
[
67
]
. Here instead we focus on the dissipative Lindblad dynamics in the strongly correlated regime, treating interaction and dissipation on equal footing via our NCA dynamical map. This allows us to access the full crossover of the spin relaxation rate from Kondo to Zeno regime and to reveal the effect of monitoring on the Kondo peak and the high-energy Hubbard bands in the spectral function.

We have shown that the Kondo-Zeno crossover can be understood at weak dissipation in terms of a non-Hermitian Kondo model, obtained as effective model at long times through a Schrieffer-Wolff transformation on the Lindbladian. Our interpretation based on the non-Hermitian Kondo model differs significantly from previous attempts that invoke the no-click limit corresponding to full post-selection
[
52
,
55
]
to justify this mapping. Here, it rather emerges from the competition between Coulomb repulsion and charge monitoring: deep in the Kondo regime, when charge fluctuations are frozen due to strong Coulomb repulsion, the leftover local moment can only dissipate through a bath-mediated spin exchange, given in Eq. (
7
), while other dissipative processes are blocked at particle-hole symmetry, as long as the doublon production remains limited and the projection onto the single occupied manifold controlled. On the other hand, upon increasing the dephasing rate, heating due to doublon production dominates the spin decay. Our results highlight the robustness of Kondo effect to weak charge monitoring, before heating starts to kick in.

The Kondo-Zeno crossover can be experimentally observed in quantum dots monitored by a nearby quantum point contact acting as a charge detector
[
1
,
33
]
. We note that real-time spin dynamics in quantum dot can be accessed, providing a direct signature of the crossover. In addition, the evolution of the spectral function of the dot under monitoring could be also observed via tunneling spectroscopy through a second lead
[
68
]
.

This work opens up several avenues for future research. The existence of the Kondo-Zeno crossover scale
\tau_{K}^{-1}
raises the question of whether an effective Kondo temperature can be defined for the dissipative Anderson impurity model and if so, what could be the associated scaling theory. In this respect, a natural future direction, relevant both for its fundamental interest and for experimental applications, would be to look for signatures of Kondo-Zeno crossover in the steady-state transport. Furthermore looking at the dynamics of the conditional state and associated non-linear probes, such as block-entanglement entropy or purity of quantum trajectories, could unveil measurement-induced transitions in this problem.

To tackle the dissipative Anderson Impurity Model we first reformulate the Lindblad many-body dynamics in Eq. (

) using the purification/superfermion representation
[

,
2
,
71
,
3
,
73
,
74
,
75
,
4
]
. This amounts to represent density matrices as pure states
|\rho_{t}\rangle
in an extended Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}\otimes\tilde{\mathcal{H}}
that contains a copy of our degrees of freedom. In this formalism the Linbdlad master equation takes the form of a Schrodinger-like equation
\partial_{t}|\rho_{t}\rangle=\mathcal{L}|\rho_{t}\rangle
generated by a non-Hermitian operator
\mathcal{L}
, the Lindbladian, which reads (See Supplementary Note 2)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}=-i\left(H-\tilde{H}\right)+\gamma\left(n\tilde{n}-\frac{1}{2}n^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\tilde{n}^{2}\right)

(8)

where
H
is given in Eq. (
S100
) and
\tilde{H}
takes the very same form in terms of fermions living in the
\tilde{\mathcal{H}}
Hilbert space,
\tilde{c}_{\mathbf{k},\sigma},\tilde{c}^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{k},\sigma}
and
\tilde{d}_{\sigma},\tilde{d}^{\dagger}_{\sigma}
for the bath and dot operators respectively, which satisfy the usual fermionic algebra (See Supplementary Note 2). We note the analogy between the doubling of the degrees of freedom due to
\tilde{\mathcal{H}}
and the Keldysh formalism
[
77
,
78
]
: the first term in Eq. (
8
) can be seen as the coherent evolution along the two independent Keldysh contours while the dissipation term induces an explicit coupling between them and a back-action (non-Hermitian contribution). We can then perform an exact hybridization expansion in the system-bath coupling, starting from the density matrix in the interaction picture
[
47
,
6
,
5
]
,
and obtain an exact equation for the dressed impurity time-evolution operator
\mathcal{V}(t,0)=\mbox{Tr}_{\rm bath}\left[\exp\left(\mathcal{L}t\right)\right]
, which reads (See Supplementary Note 2)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\mathcal{V}(t,0)=\mathcal{L}_{\rm dot}\mathcal{V}(t,0)+\int_{0}^{t}d\tau\Sigma(t,\tau)\mathcal{V}(\tau,0)

(9)

where
\mathcal{L}_{\rm dot}=-i(H_{\rm dot}-\tilde{H}_{\rm dot})+\gamma\left(n\tilde{n}-\frac{1}{2}n^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\tilde{n}^{2}\right)
is the impurity Linbdladian, while the self-energy
\Sigma(t,\tau)
takes into account the effect of the metallic bath and it is a priori given by an infinite class of one-particle irreducible diagrams. To close the hierarchy we use the NCA dynamical map
[
6
]
corresponding to a self-consistent approximation on the series for
\mathcal{V}
, by keeping only the compact diagrams in which hybridization lines do not cross
[
80
,
81
,
59
,
8
,
82
,
83
,
84
]

. This amounts to restrict the self-energy to the form
\Sigma(t,\tau)\equiv\Sigma_{NCA}(t,\tau)

\displaystyle\Sigma_{NCA}(\tau,\bar{\tau})

\displaystyle=-i\sum_{\sigma\alpha,\bar{\alpha}}\Psi^{\alpha}_{\sigma}\mathcal{V}\left(\tau,\bar{\tau}\right)\bar{\Psi}^{\bar{\alpha}}_{\sigma}\Delta_{\sigma}^{\alpha\bar{\alpha}}\left(\tau,\bar{\tau}\right)

\displaystyle+i\sum_{\sigma\alpha,\bar{\alpha}}\bar{\Psi}^{\bar{\alpha}}_{\sigma}\mathcal{V}\left(\tau,\bar{\tau}\right)\Psi^{\alpha}_{\sigma}\Delta_{\sigma}^{\alpha\bar{\alpha}}\left(\bar{\tau},\tau\right)

(10)

which is itself a function of
\mathcal{V}(t,0)
, hence the self-consistent non-perturbative nature of this method. In Eq. (
10
) we have collected the dot operators in
\mathcal{H}
and
\tilde{\mathcal{H}}
into a field (See Supplementary Note 2)

\bar{\Psi}_{\sigma}=(d^{\dagger}_{\sigma}\;\tilde{d}_{\sigma})
whose components are labelled by the index
\alpha=0,1
and introduced the real-time hybridization function
\Delta_{\sigma}^{\alpha\bar{\alpha}}(\tau,\bar{\tau})
, that keeps into account the full non-Markovian nature of the metallic bath (See Supplementary Note 2 for definition.) The resulting dynamical map based on the NCA resummation is then solved self-consistently to compute impurity observables and correlation functions discussed in the manuscript. We conclude by briefly describing how to compute the impurity spectral function within the NCA map. We start from the retarded Green’s function of the dot which is defined as

\displaystyle G^{R}_{\sigma}(t,t^{\prime})=-i\theta(t-t^{\prime})\langle\{d_{\sigma}(t),d^{\dagger}_{\sigma}(t^{\prime})\}\rangle

(11)

where
\langle\cdots\rangle
implies the average over the steady state and
\{A,B\}=AB+BA
is the anti-commutator. The spectral function of the impurity,
A_{\sigma}(\omega)
, is defined by going in Fourier space and taking the imaginary part of the retarded Green’s function, i.e.
A_{\sigma}(\omega)=-\frac{1}{\pi}\Im\left[G^{R}_{\sigma}(\omega)\right]
. We can obtain the impurity Green’s function from the knowledge of the NCA dynamical map superoperator
\mathcal{V}(t)
and the impurity steady-state density matrix, a result analog to the quantum regression theorem for Lindblad evolution
[
7
]
. In particular, using the superfermion representation one can show that

\displaystyle G^{R}_{\sigma}(t,0)

\displaystyle=-i\theta(t)\langle\{d_{\sigma}(t),d_{\sigma}^{\dagger}\}\rangle

\displaystyle=-i\theta(t)\langle I|d_{\sigma}\mathcal{V}(t)d_{\sigma}^{\dagger}|\rho_{\rm dot,ss}\rangle-\theta(t)\langle I|d_{\sigma}\mathcal{V}(t)\tilde{d}_{\sigma}|\rho_{\rm dot,ss}\rangle

(12)

In practice we compute first the steady-state density matrix (see Supplementary Note 2) and then solve the NCA equations at long-times to obtain
\mathcal{V}(t)
and perform Fourier transform to obtain the spectral function.

Appendix 1
Supplementary Note 1: Lindblad Dynamics from continuous monitoring

We consider a quantum dot coupled to a metallic lead and to a monitoring device which measures some Hermitian operator
O
. We focus here on a homodyne detection scheme corresponding to a quantum-state diffusion protocol described by the following stochastic Schrodinger equation
[
1
]

\displaystyle d|\psi(\xi_{t})\rangle

\displaystyle=-idtH|\psi(\xi_{t})\rangle+d\xi_{t}\left(O-\langle O\rangle\right)|\psi(\xi_{t})\rangle-\frac{\gamma dt}{2}\left(O-\langle O\rangle\right)^{2}|\psi(\xi_{t})\rangle

(S1)

Here we follow the standard terminology and define the state
|\psi(\xi_{t})\rangle
as the ensemble of quantum trajectories, each labeled by a specific history of measurement outcomes
\xi_{t}
.
The first term in Eq. (
S1
) describes the deterministic evolution, a coherent dynamics generated by the dot+lead Hamiltonian

\displaystyle H=\sum_{\mathbf{k},\sigma}\varepsilon_{\mathbf{k}}c^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{k},\sigma}c_{\mathbf{k},\sigma}+\sum_{\mathbf{k},\sigma}\left(V_{\mathbf{k}}d_{\sigma}^{\dagger}c_{\mathbf{k},\sigma}+h.c\right)+\varepsilon_{d}\sum_{\sigma}d^{\dagger}_{\sigma}d_{\sigma}+Un_{\uparrow}n_{\downarrow}

(S2)

The continuous monitoring of the operator
O
has two effects on the evolution of the state of the system, as seen in the last terms in
Eq. (
S1
). It introduces a local stochastic term whose noise couples to the fluctuations
O-\langle O\rangle
, as well as a back-action term proportional to the fluctuation squared,
(O-\langle O\rangle)^{2}
. Here, we defined the expectation value
\langle\circ\rangle_{\xi}=\langle\psi(\xi_{t})|\circ|\psi(\xi_{t})\rangle
and the stochastic real variable
d\xi_{t}
, satisfying the standard rules from Îto calculus
\overline{d\xi_{t}}=0
and
(d\xi_{t})^{2}=\gamma dt
. The stochastic variable
d\xi_{t}
can be interpreted as an infinitesimal fluctuating noise term.

From the stochastic Schrodinger equation in Eq. (
S1
) one can obtain the density matrix
\rho(\xi_{t})=|\psi(\xi_{t})\rangle\langle\psi(\xi_{t})|
and write down an equation of motion for its evolution. Using the properties of the Îto noise this reads

\displaystyle d\rho(\xi_{t})=-i[H,\rho(\xi_{t})]+\gamma O\rho(\xi_{t})O-\frac{\gamma}{2}\left\{\rho(\xi_{t}),O^{2}\right\}+d\xi_{t}\left\{O-\langle O\rangle_{\xi},\rho(\xi_{t})\right\}

(S3)

Due to the properties of the Îto noise which is uncorrelated at different times, the average of Eq. (
S3
) yields the following Lindblad equation for the averaged state
\rho_{t}\equiv\overline{\rho(\xi_{t})}

\partial_{t}\rho_{t}=-i\left[H,\rho_{t}\right]+\gamma O\rho_{t}O-\frac{\gamma}{2}\{O^{2},\rho_{t}\}

(S4)

which coincides with the one given in the main text for Hermitian jump operators.

Supplementary Note 2: Hybridization Expansion and Non-Crossing Diagrams

2.1
Vectorization of the Lindbladian

We start by applying the superfermion or vectorization formalism
[
2
,
3
]
to the case of the master equation for a dissipative quantum impurity model, i.e.

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\rho_{t}=-i\left[H,\rho_{t}\right]+\gamma L\rho_{t}L^{\dagger}-\frac{\gamma}{2}\{L^{\dagger}L,\rho_{t}\}

(S5)

where
H=H_{\rm dot}+H_{\rm bath}+H_{\rm hyb}
is the dot plus bath Hamiltonian given in the main text while
L
is the impurity jump operator, corresponding in our case to the dot total charge
L=\sum_{\sigma}n_{\sigma}
. We introduce the Hilbert spaces
\mathcal{H}
for the impurity and the bath degrees of freedom and its doubled tilde-version
\tilde{\mathcal{H}}
and the associated identity operators

\displaystyle I

\displaystyle=\sum_{m}|m\rangle\langle m|

(S6)

\displaystyle\tilde{I}

\displaystyle=\sum_{m}|\tilde{m}\rangle\langle\tilde{m}|\,.

(S7)

written in terms of two orthonormal basis
|m\rangle,|\tilde{m}\rangle
. We can also duplicate all the degrees of freedom in the problem, namely the impurity and the bath fermions, and introduce the associated creation/annihilation operators
d_{\sigma},\tilde{d}_{\sigma}
and
c_{\mathbf{k},\sigma},\tilde{c}_{\mathbf{k},\sigma}
and their Hermitian conjugate. The key step is now to vectorize the identity operator, introducing the so called left vacuum
[
3
,
2
]
(or vectorized identity)

\displaystyle|I\rangle=\sum_{m}\left(-i\right)^{m}|m\rangle\otimes|\tilde{m}\rangle

(S8)

The vectorized identity is particularly useful as it allows to write any operator
O
as a vector

\displaystyle|O\rangle=O|I\rangle=O\otimes\tilde{I}|I\rangle

(S9)

In particular, the vectorized density matrix reads:

\displaystyle|\rho\rangle=\rho|I\rangle

(S10)

Using the left vacuum one can write down the trace of any operator over the density matrix as

\displaystyle\langle O(t)\rangle=\mbox{Tr}\left(\rho_{t}O\right)\equiv\langle I|O|\rho_{t}\rangle

(S11)

Following these rules we can rewrite the Linblad master equation as a non-unitary Schrodinger type of equation

\displaystyle\partial_{t}|\rho_{t}\rangle=\mathcal{L}|\rho_{t}\rangle

(S12)

where
\mathcal{L}
is the vectorized Lindbladian. The advantage of the vectorization formalism is that the superoperator structure usually needed to treat Lindbladian problems and the associated hybridization expansion is now encoded by doubling the local Hilbert space and working with an additional quantum number, similar to an orbital degrees of freedom.
The Lindbladian
\mathcal{L}
has now three contributions

\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{L}_{\rm dot}+\mathcal{L}_{\rm bath}+\mathcal{L}_{\rm hyb}

(S13)

The first one
\mathcal{L}_{\rm dot}
is the free Lindbladian for the dissipative impurity. By using the super-fermions rules
[
4
,
3
]
(
d_{\sigma}|I\rangle=-i\tilde{d}^{\dagger}_{\sigma}|I\rangle
and
d_{\sigma}^{\dagger}|I\rangle=-i\tilde{d}_{\sigma}|I\rangle
) and since we consider only the dissipation on the impurity degrees of freedom, we can formally write the impurity Lindbladian
\mathcal{L}_{\rm dot}
as:

\mathcal{L}_{\rm dot}=-i\left(H_{\rm dot}-\tilde{H}_{\rm dot}\right)+\gamma\left(s_{L}L\tilde{L}-\frac{1}{2}L^{\dagger}L-\frac{1}{2}\tilde{L}^{\dagger}\tilde{L}\right)

(S14)

where
s_{L}
is an extra sign depending on the fermionic (
s_{L}=-i
) or bosonic (
s_{L}=1
) nature of the jumps operator. The second term in Eq. (
S13
)
is the bath Lindbladian and reads
\mathcal{L}_{\rm bath}=-i\left(H_{\rm bath}-\tilde{H}_{\rm bath}\right)
. Finally
the impurity-bath Lindbladian
\mathcal{L}_{\rm hyb}
can be written in compact form by introducing the following fields

\displaystyle\Phi_{\sigma}=\sum_{\mathbf{k}}V_{\mathbf{k}}\begin{pmatrix}c_{\mathbf{k},\sigma}\\
\tilde{c}_{\mathbf{k},\sigma}^{\dagger}\end{pmatrix}\hskip 9.24994pt\Psi_{\sigma}=\begin{pmatrix}d_{\sigma}\\
\tilde{d}_{\sigma}^{\dagger}\end{pmatrix}

(S15)

which group together the operators living in the space
\mathcal{H}
and
\tilde{\mathcal{H}}
. Using these fields we can write the system-bath term in a more compact way:

\mathcal{L}_{\rm hyb}=-i\left(H_{\rm hyb}-\tilde{H}_{\rm hyb}\right)=-i\sum_{\sigma\alpha}\left(\bar{\Phi}^{\alpha}_{\sigma}\Psi^{\alpha}_{\sigma}+\bar{\Psi}^{\alpha}_{\sigma}\Phi^{\alpha}_{\sigma}\right)

(S16)

where we have introduced a label
\alpha=0,1
which denotes the Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
or
\tilde{\mathcal{H}}
(
d_{\sigma}=\Psi_{\sigma}^{\alpha=0}
and
\tilde{d}^{\dagger}_{\sigma}=\Psi_{\sigma}^{\alpha=1}
). At this point we can write the formal solution of the vectorized master equation (
S12
)

|\rho_{t}\rangle=\mathcal{T}_{t}\exp\left(\int_{0}^{t}\mathcal{L}(\tau)d\tau\right)|\rho_{0}\rangle

(S17)

where we have introduced the time ordering operator
\mathcal{T}_{t}
in the Superfermions representation. Unlike the standard Keldysh contour, here the time ordering is defined as:

\displaystyle t_{\alpha}>\bar{t}_{\beta}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}t>\bar{t}\hskip 9.24994pt\text{when}\hskip 9.24994pt\alpha=\beta\in\mathcal{H},\tilde{\mathcal{H}}\\
\forall t,\bar{t}\hskip 9.24994pt\text{when}\hskip 9.24994pt\alpha\in\mathcal{H}\hskip 9.24994pt\beta\in\tilde{\mathcal{H}}\end{array}\right.

namely it corresponds to the regular time-ordering if
\alpha=\beta
, while if these indexes are different and
\alpha
belongs to
\mathcal{H}
then
t_{\alpha}
is always greater than
\bar{t}_{\beta}
. In the opposite case,
\alpha
belonging to
\tilde{\mathcal{H}}
would imply that
t_{\alpha}
is never greater than
\bar{t}_{\beta}
. This ordering allows to define a time-ordering operator
\mathcal{T}_{t}
such that two operators,
\psi_{1}
and
\psi_{2}
, being
\psi
a creation or annihilation fermionic operator living in the
\mathcal{H}(\tilde{\mathcal{H}})
Hilbert space, anticommute under time-ordering:

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{t}\psi_{1}(t_{\alpha})\psi_{2}(t_{\beta})=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\psi_{1}(t_{\alpha})\psi_{2}(t_{\beta})\hskip 9.24994pt\text{if}\hskip 9.24994ptt_{\alpha}>t_{\beta}\\
-\psi_{2}(t_{\beta})\psi_{1}(t_{\alpha})\hskip 9.24994pt\text{otherwise
}\end{array}\right.

Eq. (
S17
) represents the starting point to perform the hybridization expansion, namely an expansion order by order in the system-bath coupling
\mathcal{L}_{\rm hyb}
, as we will discuss in the next section.

2.2
Hybridization Expansion and NCA Dynamical Map

In this Section we derive the perturbation series (called Hybridization expansion) for the evolution superoperator in powers of the impurity-bath coupling, up to all orders in this coupling. In the interaction picture we can write the time evolution operator as

\displaystyle|\rho_{t}\rangle=\mathcal{V}_{0}(t)\mathcal{T}_{t}exp\left(\int_{0}^{t}\mathcal{L}_{\rm hyb}(s)ds\right)|\rho_{0}\rangle

(S22)

where
\mathcal{V}_{0}(t)=\exp\left(\mathcal{L}_{0}t\right)
is the time evolution operator in the decoupled limit, i.e.
\mathcal{L}_{0}=\mathcal{L}_{\rm dot}+\mathcal{L}_{\rm bath}
, while the time dependence of
\mathcal{L}_{\rm hyb}
is given by the free evolution
\mathcal{L}_{\rm hyb}\left(\tau\right)=e^{-\mathcal{L}_{0}\tau}\mathcal{L}_{\rm hyb}e^{\mathcal{L}_{0}\tau}
. By using a standard Taylor expansion for the bath-impurity terms we can write all the order of
|\rho_{t}\rangle

\displaystyle|\rho_{t}\rangle=\sum_{n}\frac{1}{n!}\int_{0}^{t}d\tau_{1}\cdots d\tau_{n}\mathcal{V}_{0}(t)\mathcal{T}_{t}\left[\mathcal{L}_{\rm hyb}(\tau_{1})\mathcal{L}_{\rm hyb}(\tau_{2})\cdots\mathcal{L}_{\rm hyb}(\tau_{n})\right]|\rho_{0}\rangle

(S23)

Due to the fact that the coupling between the bath and the impurity degrees of freedom is linear, we only have to consider the even terms of the expansion

\displaystyle|\rho_{t}\rangle=\sum_{n}\frac{1}{2n!}\int_{0}^{t}d\tau_{1}\cdots d\tau_{2n}\mathcal{V}_{0}(t)\mathcal{T}_{t}\left[\mathcal{L}_{\rm hyb}(\tau_{1})\mathcal{L}_{\rm hyb}(\tau_{2})\cdots\mathcal{L}_{\rm hyb}(\tau_{2n})\right]|\rho_{0}\rangle\,.

(S24)

Finally, in terms of the spinors
\Psi
and
\Phi
, the time evolution of the density matrix becomes

\displaystyle|\rho_{t}\rangle=\sum_{n}\sum_{\{\sigma,\bar{\sigma}\}}\frac{(-1)^{n}}{(n!)^{2}}\int_{0}^{t}d\tau_{1}\cdots d\tau_{n}\int_{0}^{t}d\bar{\tau}_{1}\cdots d\bar{\tau}_{n}\mathcal{V}_{0}\mathcal{T}_{t}\left[\bar{\Psi}_{\sigma}(\tau_{1})\Phi_{\sigma}(\tau_{1})\cdots\bar{\Phi}_{\sigma}(\tau_{n})\Psi_{\sigma}(\tau_{n})\right]|\rho_{0}\rangle

(S25)

We now take the average over the bath
Tr_{\rm bath}\left[\cdots\right]=\langle I_{\rm bath}|\cdots|\rho_{0}\rangle
and use the fact that the initial state

|\rho_{0}\rangle
is factorized, i.e. initially we take the initial density matrix as
\rho_{0}=\rho_{\rm dot,0}\otimes\rho_{\rm bath,0}
. Then, since the bath is non-interacting, we can use the Wick theorem in order to trace out the bath degrees of freedom and obtain the hybridization expansion for the dressed impurity propagator
\langle I_{\rm bath}|\rho_{t}\rangle\equiv\mathcal{V}(t,0)|\rho_{\rm dot,0}\rangle
which finally reads
[
5
]

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}(t,0)|\rho_{\rm dot,0}\rangle=\sum_{n}\sum_{\{\sigma,\bar{\sigma}\}}\frac{(-i)^{n}}{(n!)^{2}}\int_{0}^{t}\prod_{i}d\tau_{i}d\bar{\tau}_{i}\exp\left(\mathcal{L}_{\rm dot}t\right)\mathcal{T}_{t}\left[\bar{\Psi}_{\sigma}(\tau_{1})\cdots\Psi_{\sigma}(\tau_{n})\right]|\rho_{\rm dot,0}\rangle\rm Det_{\sigma}\left[\{\bar{\Phi}_{\sigma},\Phi_{\sigma}\}\right]\rm Det_{\bar{\sigma}}\left[\{\bar{\Phi}_{\bar{\sigma}},\Phi_{\bar{\sigma}}\}\right]

(S26)

where
\rm Det_{\sigma}\left[\{\bar{\Phi}_{\bar{\sigma}},\Phi_{\bar{\sigma}}\}\right]
are determinants built out of the real-time hybridization functions of the bath defined as

\displaystyle\Delta_{\sigma}^{\alpha\bar{\alpha}}(\tau,\bar{\tau})=-i\langle I_{\rm bath}|\mathcal{T}_{t}\left[\bar{\Phi}_{\sigma}^{\alpha}(\tau)\Phi_{\sigma}^{\bar{\alpha}}(\bar{\tau})\right]|\rho_{\rm bath,0}\rangle

(S27)

For a non-interacting bath, as the one we consider here, a simple calculation gives
[
5
]

\displaystyle\Delta_{\sigma}^{01}(\tau,\bar{\tau})

\displaystyle=\int d\epsilon\left(1-n_{F}(\epsilon)\right)\Gamma_{\sigma}(\epsilon)e^{-i\epsilon(\tau-\bar{\tau})}

(S28)

\displaystyle\Delta_{\sigma}^{10}(\tau,\bar{\tau})

\displaystyle=-\int d\epsilon n_{F}(\epsilon)\Gamma_{\sigma}(\epsilon)e^{-i\epsilon(\tau-\bar{\tau})}

(S29)

where
n_{F}(\epsilon)
is the Fermi distribution and
\Gamma(\epsilon)
the energy-dependent hybridization for the spin channel
\sigma
, defined in the main text. From this result the diagonal components of
\Delta_{\sigma}^{\alpha\bar{\alpha}}(\tau,\bar{\tau})
can be obtained, for example
\Delta_{\sigma}^{0}(\tau,\bar{\tau})=i\theta(\tau-\bar{\tau})\Delta_{\sigma}^{10}(\tau,\bar{\tau})+i\theta(\bar{\tau}-\tau)\Delta_{\sigma}^{01}(\tau,\bar{\tau})
.

The hybridization expansion we have derived in Eq. (
S26
) is a bare expansion in the dot-bath coupling. It can be reorganized in a self-consistent diagrammatic expansion by identifying one-particle irreducible diagrams and introducing a self-energy
\Sigma(\tau,\tau^{\prime})
to obtain a Dyson equation for
\mathcal{V}(t,0)
of the form
[
6
]

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}(t,0)=\mathcal{V}_{\rm dot}(t,0)+\int_{0}^{t}d\tau\int_{0}^{\tau}d\bar{\tau}\mathcal{V}_{\rm dot}\left(t,\tau\right)\Sigma\left(\tau,\bar{\tau}\right)\mathcal{V}\left(\bar{\tau},0\right)

(S30)

which can be rewritten as in the main text upon taking a time-derivative on both sides of the equation and using
\partial_{t}\mathcal{V}_{\rm dot}(t,0)=\mathcal{L}_{\rm dot}\mathcal{V}_{\rm dot}(t,0)
. As in any interacting diagrammatic theory the self-energy is not known in closed form. It can however reorganised as an expansion in diagrams with increasing number of crossing hybridization lines
[
7
,
6
]
. The lowest order self-consistent level of this hierarchy is the so called non-crossing approximation (NCA) which corresponds to keeping only the compact diagrams in which hybridization lines do not cross. The formal expression for the NCA self-energy is therefore the one given in the main text, which reads

\displaystyle\Sigma_{NCA}(\tau,\bar{\tau})=-i\sum_{\sigma}\sum_{\alpha,\bar{\alpha}}\left[\Psi^{\alpha}_{\sigma}\mathcal{V}\left(\tau,\bar{\tau}\right)\bar{\Psi}^{\bar{\alpha}}_{\sigma}\Delta_{\sigma}^{\alpha\bar{\alpha}}\left(\tau,\bar{\tau}\right)-\bar{\Psi}^{\bar{\alpha}}_{\sigma}\mathcal{V}\left(\tau,\bar{\tau}\right)\Psi^{\alpha}_{\sigma}\Delta_{\sigma}^{\alpha\bar{\alpha}}\left(\bar{\tau},\tau\right)\right]

(S31)

which describes a rainbow diagram with a dot vertex

\bar{\Psi}_{\sigma}^{\bar{\alpha}}
at time
\bar{\tau}
, a dressed dot propagation from
\bar{\tau}
to
\tau
with propagator
\mathcal{V}(\tau,\bar{\tau})
and a hybridization line
\Delta_{\sigma}^{\alpha\bar{\alpha}}\left(\tau,\bar{\tau}\right)
and finally another dot vertex operator
\Psi_{\sigma}^{\alpha}
at time
\tau
, with the second term in Eq. (
S31
) describing the diagram with
\tau,\bar{\tau}
exchanged.

2.2.1
Steady-state Condition

Figure S1:
NCA results for the non-dissipative Anderson Impurity Model, corresponding to
\gamma=0
. (a) Spin relaxation rate as a function of interaction
U
. (b) Impurity spectral function for the half-filled case at fixed
U=5\Gamma
and different temperatures.

Within NCA we can obtain an equation directly for the steady-state density matrix of the impurity
[
6
]
, which is useful for example when computing spectral functions. If we assume a unique steady state, the associated density matrix is defined as
|\rho_{\rm dot,ss}\rangle=\mathcal{V}(\mathcal{1},0)|\rho_{\rm dot,0}\rangle
. While in principle this would require to perform the full transient dynamics from an arbitrary initial condition, here we show how to obtain
|\rho_{ss}\rangle
directly from the stationary state propagator
\mathcal{V}\left(t,t^{\prime}\right)
.

By definition, the steady-state density matrix satisfies the condition

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\mathcal{V}(t,0)|\rho_{\rm dot,0}\rangle\underset{t\rightarrow\mathcal{1}}{\longrightarrow}0

(S32)

where
\rho_{0}
is an arbitrary initial state. By using the Dyson equation we get:

\displaystyle\underset{t\rightarrow\mathcal{1}}{lim}\partial_{t}\mathcal{V}(t,0)|\rho_{\rm dot,0}\rangle=\underset{t\rightarrow\mathcal{1}}{lim}\left(\mathcal{L}_{\rm dot}\mathcal{V}(t,0)+\int_{0}^{t}d\tau\Sigma_{NCA}(t,\tau)\mathcal{V}\left(\tau,0\right)\right)|\rho_{\rm dot,0}\rangle=0

(S33)

By making a change of variable in the convolution integral the condition reads

\displaystyle\underset{t\rightarrow\mathcal{1}}{lim}\left(\mathcal{L}_{\rm dot}\mathcal{V}(t,0)+\int_{0}^{t}d\tau\Sigma_{NCA}(t,t-\tau)\mathcal{V}\left(t-\tau,0\right)\right)|\rho_{\rm dot,0}\rangle=0

(S34)

We have now to assume some hypothesis, the first one is that the system is supposed to loose memory of the initial conditions, this mean that the self energy must vanish when
\tau\approx t\rightarrow\mathcal{1}
. Under this hypothesis the convolution integral in the Dyson equation can be cutoff when
t-\tau>t_{memory}
. Then when the self energy is non-zero the propagator
\mathcal{V}\left(t-\tau,0\right)
is stationary and can be replaced by
\mathcal{V}(\mathcal{1},0)
. So under the previous hypothesis, the steady state density matrix satisfies:

\displaystyle\left(\mathcal{L}_{\rm dot}+\int_{0}^{\mathcal{1}}\Sigma_{NCA}\left(\tau\right)d\tau\right)|\rho_{\rm dot,ss}\rangle=0

(S35)

This condition depends only on the stationary state propagator
\mathcal{V}(\tau)
through the NCA self-energy
\Sigma_{NCA}(\tau)
.

NCA Results for the non-dissipative Anderson Impurity Model

We start presenting NCA results for the non-dissipative Anderson Impurity Model, corresponding to the limit

This model can be still solved with our NCA dynamical map, the main difference is that the local impurity problem is now described by Hamiltonian evolution rather than Lindblad. We consider first the spin relaxation dynamics, namely we start from a factorized initial density matrix where the impurity is polarized and the bath is in equilibrium at zero temperature and suddenly switch-on the hybridization, leading to the dynamics of the Kondo effect. We compute the evolution of the dot magnetization as a function of time which relaxes exponentially to zero and extract the spin-relaxation rate or inverse Kondo time,

We note that the size of the coherent Kondo peak is quantitatively underestimated in NCA. Going beyond the NCA approximation in our self-consistent dynamical map, i.e. including the one-crossing diagrams

3.2
Impurity Spectral Function for the Dissipative Resonant Level Model

We now consider the dissipative but non-interacting case,
U=0
, corresponding to a dissipative Resonant Level model. We stress that due to the monitoring of the total charge the model is still interacting, i.e. non-gaussian. However the specific nature of the dissipative interaction makes it possible to close exactly the equations of motion for the retarded Green’s function in terms of a Dyson equation. We obtain therefore

\displaystyle G^{R}_{\sigma}(\omega)=\frac{1}{(G^{R}_{0\sigma})^{-1}-\Sigma^{R}_{\sigma}(\omega)}

(S36)

where the bare retarded Green’s function of the impurity reads
(G^{R}_{0\sigma})^{-1}=\omega-\epsilon_{d}+i\eta
, while the self-energy
\Sigma^{R}_{\sigma}(\omega)

\displaystyle\Sigma^{R}_{\sigma}(\omega)=-i\frac{\gamma}{2}+\sum_{\mathbf{k}}\frac{|V_{\mathbf{k}}|^{2}}{\omega-\epsilon_{\mathbf{k}}+i\eta}=\mathcal{PP}\sum_{\mathbf{k}}\frac{|V_{\mathbf{k}}|^{2}}{\omega-\epsilon_{\mathbf{k}}}-\frac{i}{2}\left[\gamma+\Gamma(\omega)\right]

(S37)

where
\Gamma(\epsilon)=2\pi\sum_{\mathbf{k}}|V_{\mathbf{k}}|^{2}\delta(\epsilon-\epsilon_{\mathbf{k}})
is the hybridization function.
One can therefore obtain the spectral function in closed form in the wide-bandwidth limit

\displaystyle A_{\sigma}(\omega)=-\frac{1}{\pi}Im\left[G^{R}_{\sigma}(\omega)\right]=\frac{1}{2\pi}\frac{(\gamma+\Gamma)}{(\omega-\epsilon_{d})^{2}+(\gamma+\Gamma)^{2}/4}

(S38)

From this expression we can immediately read out the renormalised width
\Gamma^{*}
,

\displaystyle\Gamma^{\ast}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\gamma+\Gamma\right)

(S39)

In Fig. (
S2
)(a) we plot the spectral function obtained with NCA in the
U=0
case for increasing value of the dephasing rate. We see that the resonance at the Fermi level is broadened and at the same time the value at
\omega=0
decreases with
\gamma
. A comparison with the exact result for the
U=0
case is shown in Fig. (
S2
)(b-c), concerning the weight at
\omega=0
and the width of the resonance, demonstrating the perfect agreement between NCA and the exact result. We note that from the exact expression in Eq. (
S38
) we conclude that the value of the spectral function at
\omega=0
is affected by the dissipative interaction (monitoring rate/dephasing), even though the system remains half-filled. This is different than in the unitary case, where for
U\neq 0
the value of the zero frequency spectral function is not renormalised and points towards a breakdown of the Luttinger theorem for dissipative quantum impurity models.

Appendix 4
Supplementary Note 4: Schrieffer-Wolff Transformation

In this section, we provide a detailed derivation of the non-Hermitian Kondo model by using a generalized Schrieffer-Wolff (SW) transformation for open quantum systems
[
8
,
9
,
10
]
, formulated here directly in the vectorized superfermion representation. We start from the Linbdladian in the superfermion representation that we write as

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{L}_{0}+\mathcal{L}_{\rm hyb}

(S41)

where
\mathcal{L}_{0}
is the diagonal part (with respect to the hybridization) of the Lindbladian that includes the decoupled impurity and bath terms as well as the local charge dephasing
\mathcal{L}_{0}=\mathcal{L}_{\rm dot}+\mathcal{L}_{\rm bath}
, while
\mathcal{L}_{\rm hyb}=-i\left(H_{\rm hyb}-\tilde{H}_{\rm hyb}\right)
is the off-diagonal term. As in the equilibrium case, the SW transformation allows us to integrate out the bath-impurity coupling terms by introducing a non-unitary (similarity) transformation
S
such that the new Lindbladian

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\rm eff}=e^{S}\mathcal{L}e^{-S}=\mathcal{L}+\left[S,\mathcal{L}\right]+\frac{1}{2}\left[S,\left[S,\mathcal{L}\right]\right]+\cdots

(S42)

is diagonal order by order in a perturbative expansion. In particular this can be achieved by choosing the generator
S
to be fully off-diagonal with respect to the hybridization and to satisfy the condition

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\rm hyb}+[S,\mathcal{L}_{0}]=0

(S43)

which then gives an expression for the effective Lindbladian which reads

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\rm eff}=\mathcal{L}_{0}+\frac{1}{2}[S,\mathcal{L}_{\rm hyb}]

(S44)

In the following we first derive the form of the generator
S
of the Schrieffer-Wolff transformation and then write down the resulting effective Lindbladian operator.

4.1
The Generator

The condition for the generator suggests that
S
should have a structure both diagonal and off-diagonal in the two Hilbert spaces. We therefore parametrize it as following

\displaystyle S=\sum_{\mathbf{k}\sigma}X_{\mathbf{k}\sigma}\left(c^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{k}\sigma}d_{\sigma}-hc\right)+\sum_{\mathbf{k}\sigma}\tilde{X}_{\mathbf{k}\sigma}\left(\tilde{c}^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{k}\sigma}\tilde{d}_{\sigma}-hc\right)

(S45)

where the operators
X_{\mathbf{k}\sigma}
read

\displaystyle X_{\mathbf{k}\sigma}

\displaystyle=s_{\mathbf{k}}+t_{\mathbf{k}}n_{\bar{\sigma}}+x_{\mathbf{k}}\tilde{n}+w_{\mathbf{k}}n_{\bar{\sigma}}\tilde{n}+h_{\mathbf{k}}\tilde{n}_{\uparrow}\tilde{n}_{\downarrow}+g_{\mathbf{k}}n_{\bar{\sigma}}\tilde{n}_{\uparrow}\tilde{n}_{\downarrow}

(S46)

\displaystyle=\left(s_{\mathbf{k}}+t_{\mathbf{k}}n_{\bar{\sigma}}\right)+\left(x_{\mathbf{k}}+w_{\mathbf{k}}n_{\bar{\sigma}}\right)\tilde{n}+\left(h_{\mathbf{k}}+g_{\mathbf{k}}n_{\bar{\sigma}}\right)\tilde{n}_{\uparrow}\tilde{n}_{\downarrow}

(S47)

and similarly for
\tilde{X}_{\mathbf{k}}
upon switching tilde and not-tilde,i.e.

\displaystyle\tilde{X}_{\mathbf{k}\sigma}

\displaystyle=\tilde{s}_{\mathbf{k}}+\tilde{t}_{\mathbf{k}}\tilde{n}_{\bar{\sigma}}+\tilde{x}_{\mathbf{k}}n+\tilde{w}_{\mathbf{k}}\tilde{n}_{\bar{\sigma}}n+\tilde{h}_{\mathbf{k}}n_{\uparrow}n_{\downarrow}+\tilde{g}_{\mathbf{k}}\tilde{n}_{\bar{\sigma}}n_{\uparrow}n_{\downarrow}

(S48)

\displaystyle=\left(\tilde{s}_{\mathbf{k}}+\tilde{t}_{\mathbf{k}}\tilde{n}_{\bar{\sigma}}\right)+\left(\tilde{x}_{\mathbf{k}}+\tilde{w}_{\mathbf{k}}\tilde{n}_{\bar{\sigma}}\right)n+\left(\tilde{h}_{\mathbf{k}}+\tilde{g}_{\mathbf{k}}\tilde{n}_{\bar{\sigma}}\right)n_{\uparrow}n_{\downarrow}

(S49)

While the first two terms in
X_{\mathbf{k}\sigma},\tilde{X}_{\mathbf{k}\sigma}
are diagonal in the Hilbert spaces, and represent straightforward generalisations of the generator for the standard AIM, the last terms represent the new addition due to dissipation. These terms couple the two sectors
\mathcal{H}
and
\tilde{\mathcal{H}}
and they are needed to satisfy the condition (
S43
) due to the structure of the dissipation. To proceed it is convenient to evaluate few commutators between
S
and different operators, namely

\displaystyle[S,n_{\sigma}]

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathbf{k}}X_{\mathbf{k}\sigma}\left(c^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{k}\sigma}d_{\sigma}+hc\right)

(S50)

\displaystyle[S,n_{\uparrow}n_{\downarrow}]

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathbf{k}\sigma}X_{\mathbf{k}\sigma}\left(c^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{k}\sigma}d_{\sigma}+hc\right)n_{\bar{\sigma}}

(S51)

\displaystyle[S,n_{\mathbf{k}\sigma}]

\displaystyle=-X_{\mathbf{k}\sigma}\left(c^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{k}\sigma}d_{\sigma}+hc\right)

(S52)

Similar relations, upon sending tilde-operators into non-tilde and viceversa, hold for the commutator with
\tilde{n}_{\sigma},\tilde{n}_{\uparrow}\tilde{n}_{\downarrow},\tilde{n}_{\mathbf{k}\sigma}
. Finally we need to evaluate the commutator between
S
and the dissipation term coupling the two sectors, namely

\displaystyle[S,n\tilde{n}]

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathbf{k}\sigma}X_{\mathbf{k}\sigma}\left(c^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{k}\sigma}d_{\sigma}+hc\right)\tilde{n}+\sum_{\mathbf{k}\sigma}\tilde{X}_{\mathbf{k}\sigma}\left(\tilde{c}^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{k}\sigma}\tilde{d}_{\sigma}+hc\right)n

(S53)

Using these results we can now evaluate the commutator between
S
and
\mathcal{L}_{0}
and obtain the conditions that fix the generator
S
. From this equation, using the commutators above, we get the following conditions

\displaystyle\left(\varepsilon_{\mathbf{k}}-\varepsilon_{d}+i\gamma/2\right)X_{\mathbf{k}\sigma}-(U-i\gamma)X_{\mathbf{k}\sigma}n_{\bar{\sigma}}-i\gamma X_{\mathbf{k}\sigma}\tilde{n}

\displaystyle=V_{\mathbf{k}}

(S54)

\displaystyle\left(\varepsilon_{\mathbf{k}}-\varepsilon_{d}-i\gamma/2\right)\tilde{X}_{\mathbf{k}\sigma}-(U+i\gamma)\tilde{X}_{\mathbf{k}\sigma}\tilde{n}_{\bar{\sigma}}+i\gamma\tilde{X}_{\mathbf{k}\sigma}n

\displaystyle=V_{\mathbf{k}}

(S55)

which we can solve to obtain the coefficients

\displaystyle s_{\mathbf{k}}

\displaystyle=\frac{V_{\mathbf{k}}}{\left(\varepsilon_{\mathbf{k}}-\varepsilon_{d}+i\gamma/2\right)}

(S56)

\displaystyle t_{\mathbf{k}}

\displaystyle=\frac{(U-i\gamma)s_{\mathbf{k}}}{\left(\varepsilon_{\mathbf{k}}-\varepsilon_{d}-U+3i\gamma/2\right)}=\frac{(U-i\gamma)V_{\mathbf{k}}}{\left(\varepsilon_{\mathbf{k}}-\varepsilon_{d}-U+3i\gamma/2\right)\left(\varepsilon_{\mathbf{k}}-\varepsilon_{d}+i\gamma/2\right)}

(S57)

\displaystyle x_{\mathbf{k}}

\displaystyle=\frac{i\gamma s_{\mathbf{k}}}{\left(\varepsilon_{\mathbf{k}}-\varepsilon_{d}-i\gamma/2\right)}=\frac{i\gamma V_{\mathbf{k}}}{\left(\varepsilon_{\mathbf{k}}-\varepsilon_{d}\right)^{2}+\gamma^{2}/4}

(S58)

\displaystyle w_{\mathbf{k}}

\displaystyle=\frac{(U-i\gamma)x_{\mathbf{k}}+i\gamma t_{\mathbf{k}}}{\left(\varepsilon_{\mathbf{k}}-\varepsilon_{d}-U+i\gamma/2\right)}

(S59)

\displaystyle h_{\mathbf{k}}

\displaystyle=\frac{2i\gamma x_{\mathbf{k}}}{\left(\varepsilon_{\mathbf{k}}-\varepsilon_{d}-i3\gamma/2\right)}=-\frac{2\gamma^{2}}{\left(\varepsilon_{\mathbf{k}}-\varepsilon_{d}\right)^{2}+\gamma^{2}/4}\frac{V_{\mathbf{k}}}{\left(\varepsilon_{\mathbf{k}}-\varepsilon_{d}+i3\gamma/2\right)}

(S60)

\displaystyle g_{\mathbf{k}}

\displaystyle=\frac{(U-i\gamma)h_{\mathbf{k}}+2i\gamma w_{\mathbf{k}}}{\left(\varepsilon_{\mathbf{k}}-\varepsilon_{d}-U-i\gamma/2\right)}

(S61)

Similarly, solving for the coefficients entering in the tilde-spce part
\tilde{X}_{\mathbf{k}\sigma}
we discover that
\tilde{s}_{\mathbf{k}}=s^{*}_{\mathbf{k}}
and similarly for the other coefficients.

4.2
Derivation of the Effective Lindbladian

The effective Lindbladian is obtained from the expression

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\rm eff}=\mathcal{L}_{0}+\frac{1}{2}[S,\mathcal{L}_{\rm hyb}]=\mathcal{L}_{0}+\frac{1}{2}[S,\mathcal{L}_{\rm hyb}]=\mathcal{L}_{0}-\frac{i}{2}[S,H_{\rm hyb}-\tilde{H}_{\rm hyb}]

(S63)

We now evaluate each of the two commutator separately, involving
H_{\rm hyb},\tilde{H}_{\rm hyb}
respectively. First we have

\displaystyle[S,H_{\rm hyb}]

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathbf{k}\mathbf{p}}\sum_{\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}X_{\mathbf{k}\sigma}V_{\mathbf{p}}[\left(c^{\dagger}_{\textbf{k}\sigma}d_{\sigma}-hc\right),\left(c^{\dagger}_{\textbf{p}\sigma^{\prime}}d_{\sigma^{\prime}}+hc\right)]

(S64)

\displaystyle+\sum_{\mathbf{k}\mathbf{p}}\sum_{\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}V_{\mathbf{p}}[X_{\mathbf{k}\sigma},\left(c^{\dagger}_{\textbf{p}\sigma^{\prime}}d_{\sigma^{\prime}}+hc\right)]\left(c^{\dagger}_{\textbf{k}\sigma}d_{\sigma}-hc\right)

(S65)

\displaystyle+\sum_{\mathbf{k}\mathbf{p}}\sum_{\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}V_{\mathbf{p}}[\tilde{X}_{\mathbf{k}\sigma},\left(c^{\dagger}_{\textbf{p}\sigma^{\prime}}d_{\sigma^{\prime}}+hc\right)]\left(\tilde{c}^{\dagger}_{\textbf{k}\sigma}\tilde{d}_{\sigma}-hc\right)

(S66)

Similarly, for the tilde term we have

\displaystyle[S,\tilde{H}_{\rm hyb}]

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathbf{k}\mathbf{p}}\sum_{\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}\tilde{X}_{\mathbf{k}\sigma}V_{\mathbf{p}}[\left(\tilde{c}^{\dagger}_{\textbf{k}\sigma}\tilde{d}_{\sigma}-hc\right),\left(\tilde{c}^{\dagger}_{\textbf{p}\sigma^{\prime}}\tilde{d}_{\sigma^{\prime}}+hc\right)]

(S67)

\displaystyle+\sum_{\mathbf{k}\mathbf{p}}\sum_{\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}V_{\mathbf{p}}[\tilde{X}_{\mathbf{k}\sigma},\left(\tilde{c}^{\dagger}_{\textbf{p}\sigma^{\prime}}\tilde{d}_{\sigma^{\prime}}+hc\right)]\left(\tilde{c}^{\dagger}_{\textbf{k}\sigma}\tilde{d}_{\sigma}-hc\right)

(S68)

\displaystyle+\sum_{\mathbf{k}\mathbf{p}}\sum_{\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}V_{\mathbf{p}}[X_{\mathbf{k}\sigma},\left(\tilde{c}^{\dagger}_{\textbf{p}\sigma^{\prime}}\tilde{d}_{\sigma^{\prime}}+hc\right)]\left(c^{\dagger}_{\textbf{k}\sigma}d_{\sigma}-hc\right)

(S69)

We see that we have therefore to evaluate two types of commutators:

\displaystyle[\left(c^{\dagger}_{\textbf{k}\sigma}d_{\sigma}-hc\right),\left(c^{\dagger}_{\textbf{p}\sigma^{\prime}}d_{\sigma^{\prime}}+hc\right)]

\displaystyle=[c^{\dagger}_{\textbf{k}\sigma}d_{\sigma},d^{\dagger}_{\sigma^{\prime}}c_{\textbf{p}\sigma^{\prime}}]-[d^{\dagger}_{\sigma}c_{\textbf{k}\sigma},c^{\dagger}_{\textbf{p}\sigma^{\prime}}d_{\sigma^{\prime}}]

(S70)

\displaystyle=(c^{\dagger}_{\textbf{k}\sigma}c_{\textbf{p}\sigma^{\prime}}\delta_{\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}+hc)-\delta_{\mathbf{k}\mathbf{p}}\delta_{\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}(d^{\dagger}_{\sigma^{\prime}}d_{\sigma}+hc)

(S71)

and equivalently for the tilde space

\displaystyle[\left(\tilde{c}^{\dagger}_{\textbf{k}\sigma}\tilde{d}_{\sigma}-hc\right),\left(\tilde{c}^{\dagger}_{\textbf{p}\sigma^{\prime}}\tilde{d}_{\sigma^{\prime}}+hc\right)]=(\tilde{c}^{\dagger}_{\textbf{k}\sigma}\tilde{c}_{\textbf{p}\sigma^{\prime}}\delta_{\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}+hc)-\delta_{\mathbf{k}\mathbf{p}}\delta_{\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}(\tilde{d}^{\dagger}_{\sigma^{\prime}}\tilde{d}_{\sigma}+hc)

(S72)

as well as

\displaystyle[X_{\mathbf{k}\sigma},\left(c^{\dagger}_{\textbf{p}\sigma^{\prime}}d_{\sigma^{\prime}}+hc\right)]=\left(t_{\mathbf{k}}+w_{\mathbf{k}}\tilde{n}+g_{\mathbf{k}}\tilde{n}_{\uparrow}\tilde{n}_{\downarrow}\right)\delta_{\bar{\sigma}\sigma^{{}^{\prime}}}\left(c^{\dagger}_{\textbf{p}\sigma^{\prime}}d_{\sigma^{\prime}}-hc\right)

(S73)

\displaystyle[\tilde{X}_{\mathbf{k}\sigma},\left(\tilde{c}^{\dagger}_{\textbf{p}\sigma^{\prime}}\tilde{d}_{\sigma^{\prime}}+hc\right)]=\left(\tilde{t}_{\mathbf{k}}+\tilde{w}_{\mathbf{k}}n+\tilde{g}_{\mathbf{k}}n_{\uparrow}n_{\downarrow}\right)\delta_{\bar{\sigma}\sigma^{{}^{\prime}}}\left(c^{\dagger}_{\textbf{p}\sigma^{\prime}}d_{\sigma^{\prime}}-hc\right)

(S74)

\displaystyle[X_{\mathbf{k}\sigma},\left(\tilde{c}^{\dagger}_{\textbf{p}\sigma^{\prime}}\tilde{d}_{\sigma^{\prime}}+hc\right)]=-\left(x_{\mathbf{k}}+w_{\mathbf{k}}n_{\bar{\sigma}}\right)\left(\tilde{c}^{\dagger}_{\textbf{p}\sigma^{\prime}}\tilde{d}_{\sigma^{\prime}}-hc\right)

(S75)

\displaystyle+\left(h_{\mathbf{k}}+g_{\mathbf{k}}n_{\bar{\sigma}}\right)\left(\tilde{c}^{\dagger}_{\textbf{p}\sigma^{\prime}}\tilde{d}_{\sigma^{\prime}}-hc\right)\left(\delta_{\sigma^{{}^{\prime}}\downarrow}\tilde{n}_{\uparrow}+\delta_{\sigma^{{}^{\prime}}\uparrow}\tilde{n}_{\downarrow}\right)

(S76)

\displaystyle[\tilde{X}_{\mathbf{k}\sigma},\left(c^{\dagger}_{\textbf{p}\sigma^{\prime}}d_{\sigma^{\prime}}+hc\right)]=-\left(\tilde{x}_{\mathbf{k}}+\tilde{w}_{\mathbf{k}}\tilde{n}_{\bar{\sigma}}\right)\left(c^{\dagger}_{\textbf{p}\sigma^{\prime}}d_{\sigma^{\prime}}-hc\right)

(S77)

\displaystyle+\left(\tilde{h}_{\mathbf{k}}+\tilde{g}_{\mathbf{k}}\tilde{n}_{\bar{\sigma}}\right)\left(c^{\dagger}_{\textbf{p}\sigma^{\prime}}d_{\sigma^{\prime}}-hc\right)\left(\delta_{\sigma^{{}^{\prime}}\downarrow}\tilde{n}_{\uparrow}+\delta_{\sigma^{{}^{\prime}}\uparrow}\tilde{n}_{\downarrow}\right)

(S78)

We can then evaluate the commutators between
S
and
H_{\rm hyb},\tilde{H}_{\rm hyb}
entering Eq. (
S63
). We obtain

\displaystyle[S,H_{\rm hyb}]

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathbf{k}\mathbf{p}\sigma}X_{\mathbf{k}\sigma}V_{\mathbf{p}}\left(c^{\dagger}_{\textbf{k}\sigma}c_{\textbf{p}\sigma}+hc\right)-2\sum_{\mathbf{k}\sigma}X_{\mathbf{k}\sigma}V_{\mathbf{k}}d^{\dagger}_{\sigma}d_{\sigma}

(S79)

\displaystyle-\sum_{\mathbf{k}\mathbf{p}\sigma}V_{\mathbf{p}}\left(t_{\mathbf{k}}+w_{\mathbf{k}}\tilde{n}+g_{\mathbf{k}}\tilde{n}_{\uparrow}\tilde{n}_{\downarrow}\right)\left(c^{\dagger}_{\textbf{p}\bar{\sigma}}d_{\bar{\sigma}}-hc\right)\left(c^{\dagger}_{\textbf{k}\sigma}d_{\sigma}-hc\right)

(S80)

\displaystyle-\sum_{\mathbf{k}\mathbf{p}}\sum_{\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}V_{\mathbf{p}}\left(\tilde{x}_{\mathbf{k}}+\tilde{w}_{\mathbf{k}}\tilde{n}_{\bar{\sigma}}\right)\left(c^{\dagger}_{\textbf{p}\sigma^{\prime}}d_{\sigma^{\prime}}-hc\right)\left(\tilde{c}^{\dagger}_{\textbf{k}\sigma}\tilde{d}_{\sigma}-hc\right)

(S81)

\displaystyle+\sum_{\mathbf{k}\mathbf{p}}\sum_{\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}V_{\mathbf{p}}\left(\tilde{h}_{\mathbf{k}}+\tilde{g}_{\mathbf{k}}\tilde{n}_{\bar{\sigma}}\right)\left(c^{\dagger}_{\textbf{p}\sigma^{\prime}}d_{\sigma^{\prime}}-hc\right)n_{\bar{\sigma^{\prime}}}\left(\tilde{c}^{\dagger}_{\textbf{k}\sigma}\tilde{d}_{\sigma}-hc\right)

(S82)

while for the one involving
\tilde{H}_{\rm hyb}
is is sufficient to swap all tilde operators into non-tilde ones in the equation above and take complex conjugation of the coefficients. Using these results and Eq. (
S63
) we can rewrite the effective Lindbladian in a more compact form. To this extent it is useful to introduce the spinors
\Phi^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{k}}=\left(c^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{k}\uparrow}c^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{k}\downarrow}\right)
and

\Psi^{\dagger}_{d}=\left(d^{\dagger}_{\uparrow}d^{\dagger}_{\downarrow}\right)
. The effective Lindbladian is composed of different contributions,

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\rm eff}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{L}_{0}+\mathcal{L}_{\rm bath}+\mathcal{L}_{\rm Kondo}+\mathcal{L}_{\rm scatt}+\mathcal{L}_{\rm pair}+\mathcal{L}_{\rm diss}

(S83)

which we now discuss in detail.
\mathcal{L}_{\rm Kondo}
describes a Kondo coupling between the dot spin and the spin of the bath,

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\rm Kondo}=i\sum_{\mathbf{k}\mathbf{q}}J_{\mathbf{q}\mathbf{k}}\left(\Phi_{\mathbf{q}}^{\dagger}\frac{\vec{\sigma}}{2}\Phi_{\mathbf{k}}\right)\cdot\vec{S}_{d}+\text{TildeVersion}

(S84)

with a coupling

J_{\mathbf{q}\mathbf{k}}=V_{\mathbf{q}}\left(t_{\mathbf{k}}+w_{\mathbf{k}}\tilde{n}\right)+V_{\mathbf{k}}\left(t_{\mathbf{q}}+w_{\mathbf{q}}\tilde{n}\right)

which is in general complex.
\mathcal{L}_{\rm scatt}
describes a scattering potential for the conduction electrons,

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\rm scatt}=-i\sum_{\mathbf{k}\mathbf{q}}\left(\frac{1}{2}W_{\mathbf{q}\mathbf{k}}+\frac{1}{4}J_{\mathbf{q}\mathbf{k}}\left(\Psi_{d}^{\dagger}\Psi_{d}\right)\right)\Phi_{\mathbf{q}}^{\dagger}\Phi_{\mathbf{k}}+\text{TildeVersion}

(S85)

with coupling constant

W_{\mathbf{q}\mathbf{k}}=V_{\mathbf{q}}\left(s_{\mathbf{k}}+x_{\mathbf{k}}\tilde{n}\right)+V_{\mathbf{k}}\left(s_{\mathbf{q}}+x_{\mathbf{q}}\tilde{n}\right)

\mathcal{L}_{\rm pair}
describes a pair tunneling term

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\rm pair}=\frac{i}{2}\sum_{\mathbf{q}\mathbf{k}\sigma}T_{\mathbf{q}\mathbf{k}}\left(c_{\mathbf{q}\sigma}^{\dagger}c_{\mathbf{k}\bar{\sigma}}^{\dagger}d_{\bar{\sigma}}d_{\sigma}+h.c\right)+\text{TildeVersion}

(S86)

with amplitude

T_{\mathbf{q}\mathbf{k}}=V_{\mathbf{k}}\left[t_{\mathbf{k}}+g_{\mathbf{k}}\tilde{n}_{\uparrow}\tilde{n}_{\downarrow}+w_{\mathbf{k}}\tilde{n}\right]

\mathcal{L}_{\rm dot}
describes a local Lindblad contribution

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\rm dot}=

\displaystyle i\sum_{\mathbf{k}}V_{\mathbf{k}}\left(2t_{\mathbf{k}}+2w_{\mathbf{k}}\tilde{n}+2g_{\mathbf{k}}\tilde{n}_{\uparrow}\tilde{n}_{\downarrow}\right)n_{\uparrow}n_{\downarrow}-i\sum_{\mathbf{k}}V_{\mathbf{k}}\left(2\tilde{t}_{\mathbf{k}}+2\tilde{w}_{\mathbf{k}}n+2\tilde{g}_{\mathbf{k}}n_{\uparrow}n_{\downarrow}\right)\tilde{n}_{\uparrow}\tilde{n}_{\downarrow}

\displaystyle+i\left(\sum_{\mathbf{k}}V_{\mathbf{k}}\left[s_{\mathbf{k}}+x_{\mathbf{k}}\tilde{n}+h_{\mathbf{k}}\tilde{n}_{\uparrow}\tilde{n}_{\downarrow}\right]\right)\left(\sum_{\sigma}n_{\sigma}\right)-i\left(\sum_{\mathbf{k}}V_{\mathbf{k}}\left[\tilde{s}_{\mathbf{k}}+\tilde{x}_{\mathbf{k}}n+\tilde{h}_{\mathbf{k}}n_{\uparrow}n_{\downarrow}\right]\right)\left(\sum_{\sigma}\tilde{n}_{\sigma}\right)

(S87)

and finally
\mathcal{L}_{\rm diss}
describes a dissipative term

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\rm diss}

\displaystyle=i\left[\sum_{\mathbf{q}\mathbf{k}}H_{\mathbf{q}\mathbf{k}}\left(\Phi_{\mathbf{q}}^{\dagger}\frac{\vec{\sigma}}{2}\Phi_{\mathbf{k}}\right)\cdot\vec{S}_{d}+\sum_{\mathbf{q}\mathbf{k}}\left(\frac{1}{2}I_{\mathbf{q}\mathbf{k}}+\frac{1}{4}H_{\mathbf{q}\mathbf{k}}\left(\Psi_{d}^{\dagger}\Psi_{d}\right)\right)\Phi_{\mathbf{q}}^{\dagger}\Phi_{\mathbf{k}}\right]\tilde{n}_{\uparrow}\tilde{n}_{\downarrow}+\text{TildeVersion}

(S88)

\displaystyle+\frac{i}{2}\sum_{\mathbf{q}\mathbf{k}\sigma\mu}\Gamma_{\mathbf{q}\mathbf{k}}\left(c_{\mathbf{k}\sigma}^{\dagger}d_{\sigma}+c_{\mathbf{k}\sigma}d_{\sigma}^{\dagger}\right)\left(\tilde{c}_{\mathbf{q}\mu}^{\dagger}\tilde{d}_{\mu}+\tilde{c}_{\mathbf{q}\mu}\tilde{d}_{\mu}^{\dagger}\right)

(S89)

with coupling constants

\displaystyle I_{\mathbf{q}\mathbf{k}}=V_{\mathbf{q}}h_{\mathbf{k}}+V_{\mathbf{k}}h_{\mathbf{q}}

\displaystyle H_{\mathbf{q}\mathbf{k}}=V_{\mathbf{q}}g_{\mathbf{k}}+V_{\mathbf{k}}g_{\mathbf{q}}

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\mathbf{q}\mathbf{k}}=\left[x_{\mathbf{k}}V_{\mathbf{q}}-\tilde{x}_{\mathbf{q}}V_{\mathbf{k}}\right]+\tilde{n}_{\bar{\mu}}\left[h_{\mathbf{k}}V_{\mathbf{q}}-\tilde{w}_{\mathbf{q}}V_{\mathbf{k}}\right]+\tilde{n}_{\bar{\mu}}n_{\bar{\sigma}}\left[g_{\mathbf{k}}V_{\mathbf{q}}-\tilde{g}_{\mathbf{q}}V_{\mathbf{k}}\right]

(S90)

It is first of all useful to comment on the structure of the effective Lindbladian as compared to the equilibrium case, when the Schrieffer-Wolff on the Anderson Impurity model leads to a Kondo model. Indeed we notice that some of the effective terms which are generated in Eq. (
S83
) would be also present in equilibrium. This is the case for example of the Kondo coupling
\mathcal{L}_{\rm Kondo}
, the scattering potential
\mathcal{L}_{\rm scatt}
, the pair-hopping term
\mathcal{L}_{\rm pair}
or the local term
\mathcal{L}_{\rm dot}
. The effect of dissipation here is to renormalize the coupling constants
J_{\mathbf{q}\mathbf{k}},W_{\mathbf{q}\mathbf{k}},T_{\mathbf{q}\mathbf{k}}
, leading to either complex (dissipative) interactions or to introduce coupling between Hilbert space sectors (tilde/non-tilde operators), which as we know describe dissipative processes. For example, we can see in Eq. (
S87
) that dissipation induces a renormalization to the bare Hubbard repulsion
U
, compatible with the spectral function shown in the main text, which shows a drift of the Hubbard bands with
\gamma
, as well as an effective doublon-doublon dissipation.

Dephasing is however also responsible for generating intrinsically new terms such as
\mathcal{L}_{\rm diss}
, which couples operators in the two Hilbert spaces and introduce new dissipative processes which for example create or destroy a doublon/holon with a rate
\Gamma_{\mathbf{q}\mathbf{k}}
. These dissipative terms couple the singly occupied spin sector to the charge fluctuations and therefore allow the system to go out from the singly occupied manifold. In the equilibrium low-energy limit of the AIM, one then proceeds by projecting the effective theory in the singly-occupied manifold, which is the low-energy one, to obtain a Kondo model. In the next section we show that the dynamics in this subspace is described by a non-Hermitian Kondo model. Our analysis on the effective Lindbladian, as well as our NCA results, show however that this projection can only be valid in certain regimes of dephasing, roughly for
\gamma<U
, before heating due to doublon production take over.

Supplementary Note 5: Spin versus Charge Monitoring

In the previous Sections we have argued that the problem of an Anderson dot under continuous monitoring of its total charge displays an interesting non-Hermitian Kondo physics, due to the fact that ultimately the monitoring of the total charge tries to quench charge fluctuations but leaves unaffected the spin sector. To provide further evidence in support of this picture in this Section we focus on a dissipative Anderson model with continuous monitoring of the dot spin. This problem is described again by a Linbdlad master equation of the form

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\rho_{t}=-i\left[H,\rho_{t}\right]+\gamma\left(L\rho_{t}L^{\dagger}-\frac{1}{2}\{L^{\dagger}L,\rho_{t}\}\right)

(S99)

where
H
is the Anderson impurity Hamiltonian given in the main text

\displaystyle H=\sum_{\mathbf{k},\sigma}\varepsilon_{\mathbf{k}}c^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{k},\sigma}c_{\mathbf{k},\sigma}+\sum_{\mathbf{k},\sigma}\left(V_{\mathbf{k}}d_{\sigma}^{\dagger}c_{\mathbf{k},\sigma}+h.c\right)+H_{\rm dot}\,.

(S100)

while the jump operator is now proportional to the dot’s spin
z
component, i.e. it reads now

\displaystyle L=L^{\dagger}=\sum_{\sigma}\sigma n_{\sigma}\equiv S_{z}\,,

(S101)

where
\gamma
is now the spin monitoring (or spin dephasing) rate. For this model we study again the spin relaxation time and the steady-state dot spectral function, that we plot in Fig.
S6
. The spin dynamics starting from a factorised initial state displays an exponential relaxation towards zero, from which we can extract the lifetime that we plot in Fig.
S6
(panel a). We clearly see that also for the spin dephasing case the Kondo-Zeno crossover is well visible. To understand the effect of spin-dephasing on Kondo physics we have to focus on the regime of weak dissipation. Here we see that the spin relaxation time appears to grow linearly with
\gamma
, even in the strongly correlated regime
U\gg\Gamma
, differently from the case of charge monitoring. This suggests that spin dephasing has a stronger effect on Kondo physics. This result is further confirmed by looking at the dot spectral function that we plot in Fig.
S6
(panel b). Here we can see that quite strikingly the coherent Kondo peak is suppressed very effectively by spin depahsing, which on the other hand leave almost untouched the Hubbard bands.
This result confirms the picture that monitoring the charge of the dot provides a more gentle way to control and modify the Kondo physics rather than acting directly on the spin degrees of freedom.

We can obtain further insights onto this problem by turning again to the Schrieffer-Wolff transformation discussed in the Supplementary Note 4. In particular we can now repeat the previous analysis and derive an effective model for the spin-dephasing problem. This reads

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\rm eff}=e^{S}\mathcal{L}e^{-S}\simeq\mathcal{L}_{0}+\frac{1}{2}[S,\mathcal{L}_{\rm hyb}]

(S102)

where the genetator
S
takes now the form

\displaystyle S=\sum_{\mathbf{k}\sigma}X_{\mathbf{k}\sigma}\left(c^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{k}\sigma}d_{\sigma}-hc\right)+\sum_{\mathbf{k}\sigma}\tilde{X}_{\mathbf{k}\sigma}\left(\tilde{c}^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{k}\sigma}\tilde{d}_{\sigma}-hc\right)

(S103)

where the operators
X_{\mathbf{k}\sigma}
read

\displaystyle X_{\mathbf{k}\sigma}

\displaystyle=s_{\mathbf{k}\sigma}+t_{\mathbf{k}\sigma}n_{\bar{\sigma}}+x_{\mathbf{k}\sigma}\tilde{S}_{z}+w_{\mathbf{k}\sigma}n_{\bar{\sigma}}\tilde{S}_{z}+h_{\mathbf{k}\sigma}\tilde{S}_{z}^{2}+g_{\mathbf{k}\sigma}n_{\bar{\sigma}}\tilde{S}_{z}^{2}\,.

We note that the
z-

S
via the condition
\mathcal{L}_{\rm hyb}+[S,\mathcal{L}_{0}]=0
. After some lengthy algebra one finally for the effective Lindbladian the form

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{eff}=\mathcal{L}_{0}+\mathcal{L}_{\rm Kondo}+\mathcal{L}_{\rm scatt}+\mathcal{L}_{\rm pair}+\mathcal{L}_{\rm Kondo-diss}

(S105)

where
\mathcal{L}_{0}
describes the diagonal part of the Lindbladian,
\mathcal{L}_{\rm Kondo},\mathcal{L}_{\rm scatt},\mathcal{L}_{\rm pair}

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\rm Kondo-diss}=i\sum_{\mathbf{q}\mathbf{k}}J^{zz}_{\mathbf{k}\mathbf{q}}\tilde{S}_{z}(\Phi_{\mathbf{q}}^{\dagger}\sigma^{z}\Phi_{\mathbf{k}})+\mbox{Tilde Version}

(S106)

namely a dissipative Kondo coupling between the

Crucially, this term survives even in the single occupied sector and describe a genuine dissipative term, coupling the two Hilbert spaces (Keldysh contour), thus driving the system away from the physics of the non-Hermitian Kondo model present in the charge monitoring problem. We can speculate that it is precisely this term the one responsible for the fragility of the Kondo peak in presence of spin dephasing as shown in the NCA results. A full theoretical study of the Anderson Impurity Model with spin dephasing is beyond the scope of this work and left for future study.
