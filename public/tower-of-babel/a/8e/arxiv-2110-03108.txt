Introduction

The Pieri rules in the theory of symmetric functions (see, e.g.,

over some commutative ring (say, over
\mathbb{Z}
); we furthermore set
h_{0}:=1
and

\lambda=\left(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\lambda_{3},\ldots\right)
is an
integer partition

1

1

of nonnegative integers such

\lambda_{i}=0
.

corresponding

can be defined by

s_{\lambda}=\det\left(\left(h_{\lambda_{i}-i+j}\right)_{i,j\in\left[n\right]}\right),

(1)

where

(2)

\mu/\lambda

(3)

vertical
p
-strip

There is a less-known variant of the first Pieri rule (
2
),
which sometimes appears as a stepping stone to its proof (e.g., in

[
Tamvak13
, §2.4]
). In order to state it, we agree to define a
“Schur function”
s_{\lambda}
by the
equality (
1
) not just whenever
\lambda
is a partition,
but also whenever
\lambda=\left(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\ldots,\lambda_{n}\right)
is any
n
-tuple of integers (not necessarily nonnegative, not
necessarily weakly decreasing). This does not significantly extend the notion
of a “Schur function”, since any such

s_{\lambda}
equals either
0
or
\pm s_{\mu}
for an (honest) partition

\mu
. (This follows easily from basic properties of determinants.) Now, if

\lambda=\left(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\ldots,\lambda_{n}\right)
is any
integer partition with
\lambda_{n}=0
, and if
p\in\mathbb{N}
is arbitrary,
then

s_{\lambda}h_{p}=\sum\limits_{\mu}s_{\mu},

(4)

where the sum now ranges over all
n
-tuples
\mu=\left(\mu_{1},\mu_{2},\ldots,\mu_{n}\right)\in\mathbb{N}^{n}
such that

\left(\mu_{i}\geq\lambda_{i}\text{ for each }i\right)\text{ and }\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\left(\mu_{i}-\lambda_{i}\right)=p.

In other words, if
\lambda=\left(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\ldots,\lambda_{n}\right)
is any integer partition with
\lambda_{n}=0
, and if

p\in\mathbb{N}
is arbitrary, then

s_{\lambda}h_{p}=\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}\beta=\left(\beta_{1},\beta_{2},\ldots,\beta_{n}\right)\in\mathbb{N}^{n};\\
\beta_{1}+\beta_{2}+\cdots+\beta_{n}=p\end{subarray}}s_{\lambda+\beta},

(5)

where
\lambda+\beta
denotes the entrywise sum of the
n
-tuples
\lambda

and
\beta
.

Note that each addend in the sum in (
2
) is also contained
in the sum in (
4
), but (usually) not the other way around:
An
n
-tuple
\mu
appearing on the right hand side of (

might fail to be a partition, and even if it is one, it may violate the

for some

each other out. This cancellation argument appears (in some slightly different
contexts) in
[
Tamvak13

[
LakTho07

5

It should

We will also show a

We shall now prepare for the proof of Theorem

First, we introduce a right action of the symmetric group

Definition 2.3

Let

\eta\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}

Finally, we will use the

Besides the above generalities, our proof of Theorem

Lemma 2.8
.

Let
n\in\mathbb{P}

\nu\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}

\eta\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}

\left|\nu\right|=\left|\eta\right|

\left\{\eta_{1},\eta_{2},\ldots,\eta_{n-1}\right\}\subseteq\left\{\nu_{1},\nu_{2},\ldots,\nu_{n}\right\}\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \text{and}\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \left|\left\{\eta_{1},\eta_{2},\ldots,\eta_{n-1}\right\}\right|=n-1.

Proof 2.9

(Proof of Lemma
2.8
.)
.

The claim that we must prove can be restated
as “the
n
-tuple
\nu
is a permutation of
\eta
” (that is, “the
n
-tuple
\nu
can be
obtained from
\eta
by permuting the entries”). Thus, we can
permute the entries of
\nu
without loss of generality (since neither the
truth of this claim, nor the integer
\left|\nu\right|
, nor the set

\left\{\nu_{1},\nu_{2},\ldots,\nu_{n}\right\}
change when we permute the
entries of
\nu
).

The
n-1
numbers
\eta_{1},\eta_{2},\ldots,\eta_{n-1}
are distinct (since

\left|\left\{\eta_{1},\eta_{2},\ldots,\eta_{n-1}\right\}\right|=n-1
). Furthermore, each of these
n-1
numbers appears as an entry in the

n
-tuple
\nu
(since
\left\{\eta_{1},\eta_{2},\ldots,\eta_{n-1}\right\}\subseteq\left\{\nu_{1},\nu_{2},\ldots,\nu_{n}\right\}
). Since these

n-1
numbers are distinct, they must therefore appear as
distinct

entries in
\nu
(that is, no two of the
n-1
numbers
\eta_{1},\eta_{2},\ldots,\eta_{n-1}
can appear in the same position of
\nu
). By
permuting the entries of
\nu
, we can therefore ensure that these
n-1

numbers
\eta_{1},\eta_{2},\ldots,\eta_{n-1}
are the
first
n-1

entries of
\nu
in this very order; i.e., that we have

\eta_{i}=\nu_{i}\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \text{for each }i\in\left[n-1\right].

(8)

Thus, let us WLOG assume that (
8
) holds (since we
can permute the entries of
\nu
without loss of generality). Now, summing up
the equalities (
8
) over all
i\in\left[n-1\right]
, we obtain
\eta_{1}+\eta_{2}+\cdots+\eta_{n-1}=\nu_{1}+\nu_{2}+\cdots+\nu_{n-1}
. However,

\displaystyle\left|\nu\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\eta\right|=\eta_{1}+\eta_{2}+\cdots+\eta_{n}=\underbrace{\left(\eta_{1}+\eta_{2}+\cdots+\eta_{n-1}\right)}_{=\nu_{1}+\nu_{2}+\cdots+\nu_{n-1}}+\eta_{n}

\displaystyle=\left(\nu_{1}+\nu_{2}+\cdots+\nu_{n-1}\right)+\eta_{n}.

Comparing this with

\left|\nu\right|=\nu_{1}+\nu_{2}+\cdots+\nu_{n}=\left(\nu_{1}+\nu_{2}+\cdots+\nu_{n-1}\right)+\nu_{n},

we obtain
\left(\nu_{1}+\nu_{2}+\cdots+\nu_{n-1}\right)+\eta_{n}=\left(\nu_{1}+\nu_{2}+\cdots+\nu_{n-1}\right)+\nu_{n}

. Thus, the equality

. Hence, this equality holds for all

\eta=\nu
. Thus, the

\nu
is a permutation of
\eta
. But this is precisely what we
needed to show. Thus, Lemma

is proven.

Our second lemma is about an integer determinant:

Let

(9)

The sum

\begin{cases}\left(-1\right)^{\sigma},&\text{if }\nu=\eta\circ\sigma\text{ for some
}\sigma\in S_{n};\\
0,&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

Proof 2.12

(Proof of Lemma
2.10
.)
.

The definition of
\eta

\eta=\left(1,2,\ldots,n\right)+\left(\underbrace{0,0,\ldots,0}_{n-1\text{ zeroes}},p\right)=\left(1,2,\ldots,n-1,n+p\right).

Thus,

\left(\eta_{1},\eta_{2},\ldots,\eta_{n}\right)=\eta=\left(1,2,\ldots,n-1,n+p\right).

(10)

In other words, we have

\eta_{i}=i\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \text{for each }i\in\left[n-1\right]

(11)

and

\eta_{n}=n+p.

(12)

It follows easily that the
n

\eta_{1},\eta_{2},\ldots,\eta_{n}
are distinct (since
p\in\mathbb{N}
).

We are in one of the following two cases:

Case 1:
We have
\left[n-1\right]\not\subseteq\left\{\nu_{1},\nu_{2},\ldots,\nu_{n}\right\}

Case 2:

(13)

On the other hand,

\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}\sigma\in S_{n};\\
\nu=\eta\circ\sigma\end{subarray}}\left(-1\right)^{\sigma}=\left(\text{empty sum}\right)=0.

Comparing this with (

\det\left(\left(\left[\nu_{i}\geq j\right]\right)_{i,j\in\left[n\right]}\right)=\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}\sigma\in S_{n};\\
\nu=\eta\circ\sigma\end{subarray}}\left(-1\right)^{\sigma}

Let us now consider Case 2. In this case, we have

However, (

Moreover, from

\left|\left\{\eta_{1},\eta_{2},\ldots,\eta_{n-1}\right\}\right|=\left|\left\{1,2,\ldots,n-1\right\}\right|=n-1

Thus,

satisfying
\nu=\eta\circ\sigma

\pi
is the

n

\eta_{1},\eta_{2},\ldots,\eta_{n}

6

Therefore, if

.
. Hence, the sum

\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}\sigma\in S_{n};\\

\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}\sigma\in S_{n};\\
\nu=\eta\circ\sigma\end{subarray}}\left(-1\right)^{\sigma}=\left(-1\right)^{\pi}.

(14)

We have
\nu=\eta\circ\pi=\left(\eta_{\pi\left(1\right)},\eta_{\pi\left(2\right)},\ldots,\eta_{\pi\left(n\right)}\right)
(by

On the other hand, it is well-known (see, e.g.,

In other words: If
\left(a_{i,j}\right)_{i,j\in\left[n\right]}

then

\det\left(\left(a_{\tau\left(i\right),j}\right)_{i,j\in\left[n\right]}\right)=\left(-1\right)^{\tau}\cdot\det\left(\left(a_{i,j}\right)_{i,j\in\left[n\right]}\right).

Applying this to

and

\det\left(\left(\left[\eta_{\pi\left(i\right)}\geq j\right]\right)_{i,j\in\left[n\right]}\right)=\left(-1\right)^{\pi}\cdot\det\left(\left(\left[\eta_{i}\geq j\right]\right)_{i,j\in\left[n\right]}\right).

\det\left(\left(\left[\nu_{i}\geq j\right]\right)_{i,j\in\left[n\right]}\right)=\left(-1\right)^{\pi}\cdot\det\left(\left(\left[\eta_{i}\geq j\right]\right)_{i,j\in\left[n\right]}\right).

\text{(since it is easy}\\
\text{to see that }\eta_{i}\geq i\text{)}\end{subarray}}=\prod\limits_{i=1}^{n}1=1.

Thus, (

\nu=\eta\circ\sigma\end{subarray}}\left(-1\right)^{\sigma}

Our third lemma is another expression for the same determinant as in Lemma

2.10
:

Lemma 2.13
.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
. For any permutation
\sigma\in S_{n}
, we let
\overline{\sigma}
denote the
n
-tuple
\left(\sigma\left(1\right),\sigma\left(2\right),\ldots,\sigma\left(n\right)\right)\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
.

Let
\nu\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
be an
n
-tuple. Then,

\det\left(\left(\left[\nu_{i}\geq j\right]\right)_{i,j\in\left[n\right]}\right)=\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}\sigma\in S_{n};\\
\nu-\overline{\sigma}\in\mathbb{N}^{n}\end{subarray}}\left(-1\right)^{\sigma}.

Proof 2.14

(Proof of Lemma
2.13
.)
.

If
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{B}
are two
equivalent logical statements, then
\left[\mathcal{A}\right]=\left[\mathcal{B}\right]
. Thus, for each
\sigma\in S_{n}
, we have

\displaystyle\left[\nu-\overline{\sigma}\in\mathbb{N}^{n}\right]

\displaystyle=\left[\left(\nu-\overline{\sigma}\right)_{i}\in\mathbb{N}\text{ for
each }i\in\left[n\right]\right]

\displaystyle=\left[\nu_{i}-\sigma\left(i\right)\in\mathbb{N}\text{ for each }i\in\left[n\right]\right]

\displaystyle\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \left(\begin{array}[c]{c}\text{since each }i\in\left[n\right]\text{ satisfies }\left(\nu-\overline{\sigma}\right)_{i}=\nu_{i}-\overline{\sigma}_{i}=\nu_{i}-\sigma\left(i\right)\\
\text{(because the definition of }\overline{\sigma}\text{ yields }\overline{\sigma}_{i}=\sigma\left(i\right)\text{)}\end{array}\right)

\displaystyle=\left[\nu_{i}\geq\sigma\left(i\right)\text{ for each }i\in\left[n\right]\right]

\displaystyle\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \left(\begin{array}[c]{c}\text{since two integers }a\text{ and }b\text{ satisfy }a-b\in\mathbb{N}\\
\text{if and only if }a\geq b\end{array}\right)

\displaystyle=\left[\nu_{1}\geq\sigma\left(1\right)\text{ and }\nu_{2}\geq\sigma\left(2\right)\text{ and }\cdots\text{ and }\nu_{n}\geq\sigma\left(n\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\prod\limits_{i=1}^{n}\left[\nu_{i}\geq\sigma\left(i\right)\right]

(21)

(because for any
n
logical statements
\mathcal{A}_{1},\mathcal{A}_{2},\ldots,\mathcal{A}_{n}
, we have

\left[\mathcal{A}_{1}\text{
and }\mathcal{A}_{2}\text{ and }\cdots\text{ and }\mathcal{A}_{n}\right]=\prod\limits_{i=1}^{n}\left[\mathcal{A}_{i}\right]
).

Now, the definition of the determinant of a matrix yields

\displaystyle\det\left(\left(\left[\nu_{i}\geq j\right]\right)_{i,j\in\left[n\right]}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{\sigma\in S_{n}}\left(-1\right)^{\sigma}\underbrace{\prod\limits_{i=1}^{n}\left[\nu_{i}\geq\sigma\left(i\right)\right]}_{\begin{subarray}{c}=\left[\nu-\overline{\sigma}\in\mathbb{N}^{n}\right]\\
\text{(by (\ref{pf.lem.nu-det2.short.ive}))}\end{subarray}}

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{\sigma\in S_{n}}\left(-1\right)^{\sigma}\left[\nu-\overline{\sigma}\in\mathbb{N}^{n}\right].

Recall that a truth value

is always either
1

or
0
, depending on whether the statement
\mathcal{A}
is true or false.
Hence, each addend of the sum on the right hand side of

\left(-1\right)^{\sigma}\cdot 1
or of the form
\left(-1\right)^{\sigma}\cdot 0

\left(-1\right)^{\sigma}
, whereas the addends of the form
\left(-1\right)^{\sigma}\cdot 0
can be discarded (since they are

). Thus, the sum
simplifies as follows:

\sum\limits_{\sigma\in S_{n}}\left(-1\right)^{\sigma}\left[\nu-\overline{\sigma}\in\mathbb{N}^{n}\right]=\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}\sigma\in S_{n};\\
\nu-\overline{\sigma}\in\mathbb{N}^{n}\end{subarray}}\left(-1\right)^{\sigma}.

Combining this with (

), we obtain

\det\left(\left(\left[\nu_{i}\geq j\right]\right)_{i,j\in\left[n\right]}\right)=\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}\sigma\in S_{n};\\
\nu-\overline{\sigma}\in\mathbb{N}^{n}\end{subarray}}\left(-1\right)^{\sigma}.

This proves Lemma
2.13
.

Lemma 2.15

Proof 2.16

(Proof of Theorem

.

\displaystyle t_{\alpha+\beta}

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}\beta\in\mathbb{N}^{n};\\
\left|\beta\right|=p\end{subarray}}t_{\alpha+\beta}

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}\beta\in\mathbb{N}^{n};\\
\left|\beta\right|=p\end{subarray}}\ \ \sum\limits_{\sigma\in S_{n}}\left(-1\right)^{\sigma}\prod\limits_{i=1}^{n}h_{\alpha_{i}+\left(\beta+\overline{\sigma}\right)_{i},\ i}

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{\sigma\in S_{n}}\left(-1\right)^{\sigma}\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}\beta\in\mathbb{N}^{n};\\
\left|\beta\right|=p\end{subarray}}\ \ \prod\limits_{i=1}^{n}h_{\alpha_{i}+\left(\beta+\overline{\sigma}\right)_{i},\ i}.

(26)

Now, fix a permutation
\sigma\in S_{n}
. We shall rewrite the sum

\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}\beta\in\mathbb{N}^{n};\\
\left|\beta\right|=p\end{subarray}}\ \ \prod\limits_{i=1}^{n}h_{\alpha_{i}+\left(\beta+\overline{\sigma}\right)_{i},\ i}
in terms of
\beta+\overline{\sigma}
. Indeed, we have the
following equality of summation signs:

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}\beta\in\mathbb{N}^{n};\\
\left|\beta\right|=p\end{subarray}}

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}\beta\in\mathbb{N}^{n};\\
\left|\beta\right|+\left|\overline{\sigma}\right|=p+\left|\overline{\sigma}\right|\end{subarray}}\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \left(\begin{array}[c]{c}\text{since the condition \textquotedblleft}\left|\beta\right|=p\text{\textquotedblright\ is clearly}\\
\text{equivalent to \textquotedblleft}\left|\beta\right|+\left|\overline{\sigma}\right|=p+\left|\overline{\sigma}\right|\text{\textquotedblright}\end{array}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}\beta\in\mathbb{N}^{n};\\
\left|\beta+\overline{\sigma}\right|=\left|\eta\right|\end{subarray}}\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \left(\begin{array}[c]{c}\text{since Lemma \ref{lem.additive} yields }\left|\beta\right|+\left|\overline{\sigma}\right|=\left|\beta+\overline{\sigma}\right|\text{,}\\
\text{and since }p+\left|\overline{\sigma}\right|=\left|\overline{\sigma}\right|+p=\left|\eta\right|\text{ (by
(\ref{pf.thm.pre-pieri.short.modsigbar1}))}\end{array}\right).

Hence,

\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}\beta\in\mathbb{N}^{n};\\
\left|\beta\right|=p\end{subarray}}\ \ \prod\limits_{i=1}^{n}h_{\alpha_{i}+\left(\beta+\overline{\sigma}\right)_{i},\ i}=\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}\beta\in\mathbb{N}^{n};\\
\left|\beta+\overline{\sigma}\right|=\left|\eta\right|\end{subarray}}\ \ \prod\limits_{i=1}^{n}h_{\alpha_{i}+\left(\beta+\overline{\sigma}\right)_{i},\ i}.

However,
\mathbb{Z}^{n}
is a group (under addition). Hence, when
\beta

runs over
\mathbb{Z}^{n}
, the sum
\beta+\overline{\sigma}
also runs over

\mathbb{Z}^{n}
. (Formally speaking, this is saying that the map

\displaystyle\mathbb{Z}^{n}

\displaystyle\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}^{n},

\displaystyle\beta

\displaystyle\mapsto\beta+\overline{\sigma}

is a bijection.) Thus, when
\beta
runs over
\mathbb{N}^{n}
, the sum

\beta+\overline{\sigma}
runs over the set of all
\nu\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
that
satisfy
\nu-\overline{\sigma}\in\mathbb{N}^{n}
. Therefore, we can substitute

\nu
for
\beta+\overline{\sigma}
in the sum
\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}\beta\in\mathbb{N}^{n};\\
\left|\beta+\overline{\sigma}\right|=\left|\eta\right|\end{subarray}}\ \ \prod\limits_{i=1}^{n}h_{\alpha_{i}+\left(\beta+\overline{\sigma}\right)_{i},\ i}
. We thus obtain

\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}\beta\in\mathbb{N}^{n};\\
\left|\beta+\overline{\sigma}\right|=\left|\eta\right|\end{subarray}}\ \ \prod\limits_{i=1}^{n}h_{\alpha_{i}+\left(\beta+\overline{\sigma}\right)_{i},\ i}=\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}\nu\in\mathbb{Z}^{n};\\
\nu-\overline{\sigma}\in\mathbb{N}^{n};\\
\left|\nu\right|=\left|\eta\right|\end{subarray}}\ \ \prod\limits_{i=1}^{n}h_{\alpha_{i}+\nu_{i},\ i}.

Hence, our above computation becomes

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}\beta\in\mathbb{N}^{n};\\
\left|\beta\right|=p\end{subarray}}\ \ \prod\limits_{i=1}^{n}h_{\alpha_{i}+\left(\beta+\overline{\sigma}\right)_{i},\ i}

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}\beta\in\mathbb{N}^{n};\\
\left|\beta+\overline{\sigma}\right|=\left|\eta\right|\end{subarray}}\ \ \prod\limits_{i=1}^{n}h_{\alpha_{i}+\left(\beta+\overline{\sigma}\right)_{i},\ i}

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}\nu\in\mathbb{Z}^{n};\\
\nu-\overline{\sigma}\in\mathbb{N}^{n};\\
\left|\nu\right|=\left|\eta\right|\end{subarray}}\ \ \prod\limits_{i=1}^{n}h_{\alpha_{i}+\nu_{i},\ i}.

(27)

Forget that we fixed
\sigma
. We thus have proved
(
27
) for each permutation
\sigma\in S_{n}
. Now, (
26
) becomes

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}\beta\in\mathbb{N}^{n};\\
\left|\beta\right|=p\end{subarray}}t_{\alpha+\beta}

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{\sigma\in S_{n}}\left(-1\right)^{\sigma}\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}\beta\in\mathbb{N}^{n};\\
\left|\beta\right|=p\end{subarray}}\ \ \prod\limits_{i=1}^{n}h_{\alpha_{i}+\left(\beta+\overline{\sigma}\right)_{i},\ i}

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{\sigma\in S_{n}}\left(-1\right)^{\sigma}\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}\nu\in\mathbb{Z}^{n};\\
\nu-\overline{\sigma}\in\mathbb{N}^{n};\\
\left|\nu\right|=\left|\eta\right|\end{subarray}}\ \ \prod\limits_{i=1}^{n}h_{\alpha_{i}+\nu_{i},\ i}\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \left(\text{by
(\ref{pf.thm.pre-pieri.short.innersum})}\right)

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\sum\limits_{\sigma\in S_{n}}\ \ \sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}\nu\in\mathbb{Z}^{n};\\
\nu-\overline{\sigma}\in\mathbb{N}^{n};\\
\left|\nu\right|=\left|\eta\right|\end{subarray}}}_{=\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}\nu\in\mathbb{Z}^{n};\\
\left|\nu\right|=\left|\eta\right|\end{subarray}}\ \ \sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}\sigma\in S_{n};\\
\nu-\overline{\sigma}\in\mathbb{N}^{n}\end{subarray}}}\left(-1\right)^{\sigma}\prod\limits_{i=1}^{n}h_{\alpha_{i}+\nu_{i},\ i}

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}\nu\in\mathbb{Z}^{n};\\
\left|\nu\right|=\left|\eta\right|\end{subarray}}\ \ \sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}\sigma\in S_{n};\\
\nu-\overline{\sigma}\in\mathbb{N}^{n}\end{subarray}}\left(-1\right)^{\sigma}\prod\limits_{i=1}^{n}h_{\alpha_{i}+\nu_{i},\ i}

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}\nu\in\mathbb{Z}^{n};\\
\left|\nu\right|=\left|\eta\right|\end{subarray}}\underbrace{\left(\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}\sigma\in S_{n};\\

\text{(by Lemma

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}\nu\in\mathbb{Z}^{n};\\

\text{(by Lemma \ref{lem.nu-det})}\end{subarray}}\cdot\prod\limits_{i=1}^{n}h_{\alpha_{i}+\nu_{i},\ i}

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}\nu\in\mathbb{Z}^{n};\\
\left|\nu\right|=\left|\eta\right|\end{subarray}}\left(\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}\sigma\in S_{n};\\

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}\nu\in\mathbb{Z}^{n};\\
\left|\nu\right|=\left|\eta\right|\end{subarray}}\ \ \sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}\sigma\in S_{n};\\
\nu=\eta\circ\sigma\end{subarray}}\left(-1\right)^{\sigma}\prod\limits_{i=1}^{n}h_{\alpha_{i}+\nu_{i},\ i}.

(28)

On the other hand,

\operatorname*{rowdet}\left(\left(h_{\alpha_{i}+\eta_{j},\ i}\right)_{i,j\in\left[n\right]}\right)=\sum\limits_{\sigma\in S_{n}}\left(-1\right)^{\sigma}\prod\limits_{i=1}^{n}h_{\alpha_{i}+\eta_{\sigma\left(i\right)},\ i}

(29)

(by (
24
), applied to
a_{i,j}=h_{\alpha_{i}+\eta_{j},\ i}
). However, for each
\sigma\in S_{n}
, the
n
-tuple

\eta\circ\sigma
belongs to
\mathbb{Z}^{n}
and satisfies
\left|\eta\circ\sigma\right|=\left|\eta\right|
(by Proposition

2.4
). In other words, for each
\sigma\in S_{n}
, the

n
-tuple
\eta\circ\sigma
is a
\nu\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
satisfying
\left|\nu\right|=\left|\eta\right|
. Hence, any sum ranging over all

\sigma\in S_{n}
can be split according to the value of
\eta\circ\sigma
. In
other words, we have the following equality of summation signs:

\sum\limits_{\sigma\in S_{n}}=\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}\nu\in\mathbb{Z}^{n};\\
\left|\nu\right|=\left|\eta\right|\end{subarray}}\ \ \sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}\sigma\in S_{n};\\
\eta\circ\sigma=\nu\end{subarray}}=\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}\nu\in\mathbb{Z}^{n};\\
\left|\nu\right|=\left|\eta\right|\end{subarray}}\ \ \sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}\sigma\in S_{n};\\
\nu=\eta\circ\sigma\end{subarray}}.

Thus, (
29
) becomes

\displaystyle\operatorname*{rowdet}\left(\left(h_{\alpha_{i}+\eta_{j},\ i}\right)_{i,j\in\left[n\right]}\right)

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\sum\limits_{\sigma\in S_{n}}}_{=\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}\nu\in\mathbb{Z}^{n};\\
\left|\nu\right|=\left|\eta\right|\end{subarray}}\ \ \sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}\sigma\in S_{n};\\
\nu=\eta\circ\sigma\end{subarray}}}\left(-1\right)^{\sigma}\prod\limits_{i=1}^{n}h_{\alpha_{i}+\eta_{\sigma\left(i\right)},\ i}

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}\nu\in\mathbb{Z}^{n};\\
\left|\nu\right|=\left|\eta\right|\end{subarray}}\ \ \sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}\sigma\in S_{n};\\
\nu=\eta\circ\sigma\end{subarray}}\left(-1\right)^{\sigma}\prod\limits_{i=1}^{n}\underbrace{h_{\alpha_{i}+\eta_{\sigma\left(i\right)},\ i}}_{\begin{subarray}{c}=h_{\alpha_{i}+\nu_{i},\ i}\\
\text{(since }\nu=\eta\circ\sigma\text{ entails }\nu_{i}=\eta_{\sigma\left(i\right)}\text{,}\\
\text{so that }h_{\alpha_{i}+\nu_{i},\ i}=h_{\alpha_{i}+\eta_{\sigma\left(i\right)},\ i}\text{)}\end{subarray}}

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}\nu\in\mathbb{Z}^{n};\\
\left|\nu\right|=\left|\eta\right|\end{subarray}}\ \ \sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}\sigma\in S_{n};\\
\nu=\eta\circ\sigma\end{subarray}}\left(-1\right)^{\sigma}\prod\limits_{i=1}^{n}h_{\alpha_{i}+\nu_{i},\ i}.

Comparing this with (
28
), we obtain

\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}\beta\in\mathbb{N}^{n};\\
\left|\beta\right|=p\end{subarray}}t_{\alpha+\beta}=\operatorname*{rowdet}\left(\left(h_{\alpha_{i}+\eta_{j},\ i}\right)_{i,j\in\left[n\right]}\right).

This proves Theorem
2.1
.

3.1

Corollary 3.1
.

Let

R
for all

(30)

\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}\beta\in\mathbb{N}^{n};\\

(Proof of Lemma

3.2
The commutative case

Corollary 3.6
.

Assume that
R
is commutative. Let
n\in\mathbb{P}
and
p\in\mathbb{Z}
. Let
h_{k,\ i}
be an element of
R
for all

k\in\mathbb{Z}
and
i\in\left[n\right]
. Assume that

h_{k,\ n}=0\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \text{for all }k<0.

For any
\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
, we define

t_{\alpha}:=\det\left(\left(h_{\alpha_{i}+j,\ i}\right)_{i,j\in\left[n\right]}\right)\in R.

Let
\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
be such that
\alpha_{n}\leq-n
. Then,

\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}\beta\in\mathbb{N}^{n};\\
\left|\beta\right|=p\end{subarray}}t_{\alpha+\beta}=\det\left(\left(h_{\alpha_{i}+j,\ i}\right)_{i,j\in\left[n-1\right]}\right)\cdot h_{\alpha_{n}+n+p,\ n}.

Proof 3.7

(Proof of Corollary
3.6
.)
.

We have assumed that the ring

R
is commutative. Thus, every square matrix over
R
has a well-defined
determinant. Furthermore, the row-determinant of any square matrix over
R
is
the same as the determinant of this matrix (since our above definition of the
row-determinant clearly generalizes the standard definition of a determinant).
In other words, if
A
is any square matrix over
R
, then

\operatorname*{rowdet}A=\det A.

(35)

Hence, we can apply Corollary
3.1
, replacing
“
\operatorname*{rowdet}
” by
“
\det
” throughout the statement. As a
result, we obtain precisely the claim of Corollary
3.6
.

3.3
A Schur-like reindexing

Here are some more consequences of the first pre-Pieri rule:

Corollary 3.8
.

Let
n\in\mathbb{P}
and
p\in\mathbb{Z}
. Let

h_{k,\ i}
be an element of
R
for all
k\in\mathbb{Z}
and
i\in\left[n\right]
. Assume that

h_{k,\ n}=0\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \text{for all }k<0.

For any
m\in\left\{0,1,\ldots,n\right\}
and any
\lambda\in\mathbb{Z}^{m}
, we define

s_{\lambda}:=\operatorname*{rowdet}\left(\left(h_{\lambda_{i}+j-i,\ i}\right)_{i,j\in\left[m\right]}\right)\in R.

Fix an
n
-tuple
\mu\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
with
\mu_{n}=0
. Let
\overline{\mu}=\left(\mu_{1},\mu_{2},\ldots,\mu_{n-1}\right)
. Then,

s_{\overline{\mu}}\cdot h_{p,\ n}=\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}\beta\in\mathbb{N}^{n};\\
\left|\beta\right|=p\end{subarray}}s_{\mu+\beta}.

Proof 3.9

(Proof of Corollary
3.8
.)
.

Define
t_{\alpha}\in R
for each

\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
as in Corollary
3.1
.

Define an
n
-tuple
\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
by

\alpha=\left(\mu_{1}-1,\ \mu_{2}-2,\ \ldots,\ \mu_{n}-n\right).

Thus,

\alpha_{i}=\mu_{i}-i\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \text{for each }i\in\left[n\right].

(36)

Applying this to
i=n
, we obtain
\alpha_{n}=\underbrace{\mu_{n}}_{=0}-n=-n\leq-n
. Hence, Corollary
3.1
yields

\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}\beta\in\mathbb{N}^{n};\\
\left|\beta\right|=p\end{subarray}}t_{\alpha+\beta}=\operatorname*{rowdet}\left(\left(h_{\alpha_{i}+j,\ i}\right)_{i,j\in\left[n-1\right]}\right)\cdot h_{\alpha_{n}+n+p,\ n}.

(37)

However, using the definitions of
s_{\overline{\mu}}
and
\alpha
, it is
easy to see that

\operatorname*{rowdet}\left(\left(h_{\alpha_{i}+j,\ i}\right)_{i,j\in\left[n-1\right]}\right)=s_{\overline{\mu}}.

Furthermore, again using the definition of
\alpha
, we can easily check that

t_{\alpha+\beta}=s_{\mu+\beta}\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \text{for each }\beta\in\mathbb{N}^{n}

(indeed, both
t_{\alpha+\beta}
and
s_{\mu+\beta}
are defined as
row-determinants of certain matrices, and a simple calculation of indices
shows that these two matrices are identical). Finally, we have

\alpha_{n}+n+p=p\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \left(\text{since }\alpha_{n}=-n\right).

Using these three equalities, we can rewrite (
37
) as

\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}\beta\in\mathbb{N}^{n};\\
\left|\beta\right|=p\end{subarray}}s_{\mu+\beta}=s_{\overline{\mu}}\cdot h_{p,\ n}.

This proves Corollary
3.8
.

Corollary 3.10
.

Assume that
R
is commutative. Let

and

. Let
h_{k,\ i}
be an element of

for all

4
The second pre-Pieri rule

The “second pre-Pieri rule” structurally
resembles the first, but involves a sum over (a subset of)
\left\{0,1\right\}^{n}
instead of
\mathbb{N}^{n}
. This is, of course, analogous
to the relationship between the elementary symmetric functions and the
complete homogeneous symmetric functions, or the relationship between sets and
multisets, or various other “combinatorial
reciprocities”.

4.1

Before we state the second pre-Pieri rule, we observe that
\left\{0,1\right\}^{n}\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{n}

Theorem 4.1

Let
\xi
be the
n
-tuple

\displaystyle\left(1,2,\ldots,n\right)+\left(\underbrace{0,0,\ldots,0}_{n-p\text{

\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}\beta\in\left\{0,1\right\}^{n};\\

Again, we will use Definition

Lemma 4.3

Proof 4.4

(Proof of Lemma

Set

\left\{\nu_{1},\nu_{2},\ldots,\nu_{n}\right\}\subseteq\left\{\xi_{1},\xi_{2},\ldots,\xi_{n}\right\}\cup\left\{q\right\}

The

\left|\left\{\nu_{1},\nu_{2},\ldots,\nu_{n}\right\}\right|=n

5
A pre-LR rule?

We have now proved two fairly general determinantal identities – Theorem

2.1
and Theorem
4.1
-- and seen some of their
consequences. Anyone familiar with symmetric functions will likely view these
two identities as two “antipodal” statements, in the sense in which the complete homogeneous symmetric
functions are “antipodal” to the elementary
symmetric functions.
14
14

14

The analogy strikes the eye from many
directions: The summation signs
\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}\beta\in\mathbb{N}^{n};\\
\left|\beta\right|=p\end{subarray}}
and
\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}\beta\in\left\{0,1\right\}^{n};\\
\left|\beta\right|=p\end{subarray}}
in Theorem

and Theorem
4.1
are precisely the ones
that appear in the definitions of the respective symmetric functions; the
determinant

in Corollary
4.19
is a rather
straightforward generalization of the Jacobi–Trudi determinant for the
p
-th
elementary symmetric function; and so on.
The latter “antipodality” can be understood particularly well by viewing
both families of symmetric functions as corner cases of

(see, e.g.,
[

, Chapter 7]
or
[
Macdon95
, §I.3]
). It is
thus natural to ask whether our determinantal identities can be viewed as
corner cases of something more general, too:

Question 5.1

Is there a common generalization of Theorem

2.1
and Theorem
4.1
?

Such a generalization might resemble (perhaps even generalize) the
“immaculate Littlewood–Richardson rule” of
Berg, Bergeron, Saliola, Serrano and Zabrocki (
[

, Theorem 7.3]
).
Indeed, as we have seen above (in our proof of Proposition
3.14

the “right-Pieri rule”
