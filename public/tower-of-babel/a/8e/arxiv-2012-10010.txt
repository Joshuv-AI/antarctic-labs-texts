Orientation Averaging of Optical Chirality Near Nanoparticles and Aggregates
Atefeh Fazel-Najafabadi, Sebastian Schuster, Baptiste Auguié

Abstract
Artificial nanostructures enable fine control of electromagnetic fields at the nanoscale, a possibility that has recently been extended to the interaction between polarised light and chiral matter. The theoretical description of such interactions, and its application to the design of optimised structures for chiroptical spectroscopies, brings new challenges to the common set of tools used in nano-optics. In particular, chiroptical effects often depend crucially on the relative orientation of the scatterer and the incident light, but many experiments are performed with randomly-oriented scatterers, dispersed in a solution. We derive new expressions for the orientation-averaged local degree of optical chirality of the electromagnetic field in the presence of a nanoparticle aggregate. This is achieved using the superposition T -matrix framework, ideally suited for the derivation of efficient orientation-averaging formulas in light scattering problems. Our results are applied to a few model examples, and illustrate several non-intuitive aspects in the distribution of orientation-averaged degree of chirality around nanostructures. The results will be of significant interest for the study of nanoparticle assemblies designed to enhance chiroptical spectroscopies, and where the numerically-efficient computation of the averaged degree of optical chirality enables a more comprehensive exploration of the many possible nanostructures.



I
Introduction

Chiral materials naturally respond with a slight asymmetry to left or
right circularly-polarised light
Barron 2009
;
Nina Berova 2000
, and the minute difference in spectroscopic signals – in absorption, fluorescence, Raman scattering etc. – can be exploited to trace back precious 3D structural information at the nanoscale, such as the relative orientation of molecular groups (handedness). This is particularly important in the realm of biochemistry
Greenfield 2006
;
Nguyen et al. 2006
, where mirror-image molecules (enantiomers) can differ dramatically in their interaction with other chiral molecules. The ability to characterise the handedness of chiral molecules with optical spectroscopy, in the form of circular dichroism or related techniques, remains a challenging pursuit with important practical applications
Berova 2012
. The main limitation is that the chiroptical activity of most molecules is very weak; most naturally-occurring chiroptical effects have thus been restricted in their study and application to bulk samples such as highly-concentrated chemical solutions.

Artificial chiral nanostructures have opened remarkable new perspectives in this area
Amabilino 2009
, with demonstrations of enhanced chiroptical signals
Hentschel et al. 2017
;
Mun et al. 2020
. The rising interest in nano-optics in the past few decades has highlighted the potential of nano-materials and nano-structures engineered to bridge the gap between the wavelength of free propagating light and localised optical near-fields exciting molecules. Acting as nano-antennas, metallic or dielectric nanoparticles supporting resonant electromagnetic modes can funnel light into subwavelength regions associated with greatly amplified electromagnetic fields
Novotny and Hecht 2006
. This effect has been a cornerstone in the development of surface-enhanced Raman (SERS) or fluorescence spectroscopies
Le Ru and Etchegoin 2008
.
Such nanostructures can similarly enhance the chiroptical response of a chiral molecule
Schaaff and Whetten 2000
;
Lieberman et al. 2008
;
Govorov et al. 2010
;
Govorov 2011
;
Zhang and Govorov 2013
;
Zhao and Reinhard 2019
;
Najafabadi and Pakizeh 2017

Cohen and coworkers
Yang et al. 2009
;
Tang and Cohen 2010
recognised that engineered nanostructures may enhance the interaction between light and chiral matter not just through a locally-enhanced magnitude of electric (
\mathbf{E}
) and magnetic (
\mathbf{B}
) fields, but also conjointly by shaping the local degree of optical chirality
\mathscr{C}\propto\Im(\mathbf{E}^{*}\cdot\mathbf{B})
, where
\Im
stands for
‘imaginary part’
— a quantity requiring full consideration of the vectorial nature of both fields and their complex phase
Schäferling et al. 2012
.

Specifically, let us consider the time-averaged rate of absorption
A
of a chiral molecule excited by local electric
\mathbf{E}
and magnetic fields
\mathbf{B}
at a frequency
\omega
, and characterised by its electric dipole moment
\mathbf{p}=\alpha\mathbf{E}-i\gamma\mathbf{B}
, and magnetic dipole moment
\mathbf{m}=\beta\mathbf{B}+i\gamma\mathbf{E}

Hentschel 2013
,

A\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\frac{\omega}{2}\Im\left({\mathbf{E}}^{*}\cdot{\mathbf{p}}+{\mathbf{B}}^{*}\cdot{\mathbf{m}}\right),

(1)

where
\alpha
is the electric dipole polarisability,
\beta
the magnetic dipole polarisability, and
\gamma
characterises the mixed electric-magnetic dipole polarisability and is responsible for optical activity. Under the common simplifying assumption of monochromatic fields and a homogeneous embedding medium, this expression simplifies to
Hentschel 2013
:

A=\frac{\omega}{2}\left[\Im\left(\alpha\right)|{\mathbf{E}}|^{2}+\Im\left(\beta\right)|{\mathbf{B}}|^{2}+\Im\left(\gamma\right)\mathscr{C}\right],

(2)

where

\mathscr{C}=\frac{-\omega\epsilon_{0}}{2}\Im(\mathbf{E}^{*}\cdot\mathbf{B})

(3)

is referred to as the local degree of optical chirality (LDOC), first introduced by Lipkin
Lipkin 1964
.

While
\alpha,\beta,\gamma
are intrinsic properties of the scattering particle or molecule,
\mathscr{C}
encapsulates the chirality of the electromagnetic field at a given position in space
Lipkin 1964
. The differential absorbance of a molecule in response to a plane wave with left and right circular polarisations will be proportional to
\Im\left(\gamma\right)\mathscr{C}
, where
\mathscr{C}=\pm\frac{k\varepsilon_{0}E_{0}}{2}
, with
k
the wavenumber,
\varepsilon_{0}
the permittivity of free space, and
E_{0}
the amplitude of the incident electric field. By placing the molecule in the vicinity of a nanostructure, the local electromagnetic field, and therefore
\mathscr{C}
, can be amplified beyond this value
Tang and Cohen 2010
;
Schäferling et al. 2012
. This offers the possibility to engineer nanostructures that optimise the chiroptical response of analytes. Such designs can be modelled by solving Maxwell’s equations for a given nanostructure, calculating
\mathbf{E}
and
\mathbf{B}
which readily provide
\mathscr{C}
; this procedure has been used to estimate the enhancement of chiroptical signals for molecules in a variety of configurations
Hendry et al. 2010
;
Hentschel 2013
.

Although the design of useful structures benefits from extensive research and advances in surface-enhanced spectroscopies and nano-optics in the past decades, the consideration of chirality at the nano-scale, and its interaction with polarised light, brings a host of new challenges to our intuition, and our modelling capabilities. Where surface-enhanced spectroscopies such as SERS rely primarily on the amplification of the electric field intensity in the immediate vicinity of nanostructures, in pursuing enhanced optical activity one must also consider the polarisation state of near-field regions, which remains generally less intuitive, and for which we have fewer helpful rule-of-thumbs to guide us in the design of efficient chiroptical platforms
Poulikakos et al. 2019
;
Lasa-Alonso et al. 2020
.

An additional challenge presented by chiral nanostructures is the particular importance of the relative orientation between incident light and the scatterer. For example, a 2D structure fabricated by lithography may present a differential response to circularly polarised light known as
‘extrinsic’
chirality, though the same structure would display no circular dichroism if illuminated from all directions of incidence. This distinction is particularly relevant as many 3D chiral nanostructures are characterised in solution, where they are randomly-oriented with respect to incident light. To describe the optical response of such samples it is essential to be able to reliably compute the orientation-averaged response of the structure to incident light of a given polarisation (Fig.
1
). This can present a time-consuming hurdle for theoretical calculations. In the context of optical activity, a comparison with experiments may involve computing far-field orientation-averaged circular dichroism
Suryadharma and Rockstuhl 2018a
, and for experiments testing the effect of superchiral fields on molecular species, the orientation-averaged local degree of optical chirality
\langle\mathscr{C}\rangle
; the two quantities are not necessarily directly related to each other
Garcia-Guirado et al. 2019
.

Figure 1:
Schematic illustration of the light scattering problem under consideration. A rigid cluster is formed by
N
particles arranged in a fixed spatial configuration; each particle may be non-spherical and oriented arbitrarily with respect to the common
‘cluster’
reference frame
(O,x,y,z)
. The orientation and position
\mathbf{r_{i}}
of each particle (
i=1\dots N
) is arbitrary but fixed in this reference frame; in practice such particles would be held in place by a template
Guerrero-Martínez et al. 2011a
;
Kuzyk et al. 2018
;
Lan et al. 2018
, our calculations assume that the template has no impact on the optical properties of the structure. Incident light takes the form of a plane wave with wavevector
\mathbf{k}
from an arbitrary direction
(\varphi,\theta)
in the cluster reference frame, and we consider both possible states of circular polarisation (left,
L
, or right
R
). For each direction of incidence and either polarisation we can calculate the local degree of chirality
\mathscr{C}
in the vicinity of the particles, and map its spatial distribution (schematically represented by purple and green gradients). The quantity
\langle\mathscr{C}\rangle
derived in this work represents the average value for the chosen polarisation when the cluster as a whole is randomly oriented with respect to incident light, or equivalently, when
\mathbf{k}(\varphi,\theta)
spans the full solid angle in the cluster reference frame.

Many common tools used in nano-optics, such as the Finite-Element Method (FEM)
Volakis et al. 1998
, Finite-Difference Time Domain (FDTD)
Taflove and Hagness 2005
, Discrete-Dipole Approximation (DDA)
Draine and Flatau 1994
, Volume or Surface Integral Equation methods (VIE/SIE)
Kern and Martin 2009
;
Botha 2006
;
Reid and Johnson 2015
, solve the Maxwell equations for a given configuration of particles and a given incident field. These computations can be demanding even for just a few particles, and improving the methods is still an active area of research. This computational burden limits the ability to explore the vast landscape of possible structures and seek those that approach optimum bounds
Kramer et al. 2017
;
Fernandez-Corbaton et al. 2016
; it also often leads to shortcuts where accuracy is traded for practical computational time. For example, orientation-averaged properties may be approximated by considering three orthogonal directions of incidence (
x
,
y
,
z
axes), though the validity of this approximation is not clear and has received little attention to date. A rigorous result for the orientation-averaging of observable quantities such as far-field cross-sections, near-field intensity, or in our case here the LDOC
\langle\mathscr{C}\rangle
considers the integral over Euler angles
\varphi,\theta
describing the direction of the wavevector incident on the scatterer (or collection of scatterers) (Fig.
1
),

\langle\mathscr{C}(x,y,z)\rangle=\frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{0}^{\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\mathscr{C}(x,y,z;\varphi,\theta)\sin\theta\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\varphi\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\theta.

(4)

This averaging over the full solid angle of possible illumination directions is often complemented by a further averaging over the direction of polarisation for linearly-polarised light
Mishchenko 1989
;
Mishchenko 1990
;
Khlebtsov 1992
;
Borghese et al. 2007
. In the case of circular polarisation, however, the rotations of the wavevector do not affect the helicity of the light and it is therefore useful to consider the angular averaging over
(\varphi,\theta)
for left (
L
) or right (
R
) polarisations separately
Suryadharma and Rockstuhl 2018b
. The fully averaged optical response to unpolarised light will be the average of both.

In contrast to the methods previously mentioned, the
T
-matrix framework affords a relatively efficient solution to the Maxwell equations in a basis of vector spherical waves
Mishchenko et al. 2002a
; intrinsic to the method is therefore the description of a scatterer or collection of scatterers, to waves incident from any direction. This is a particular strength of the method which has led to analytical formulas for orientation-averaged quantities such as far-field cross-sections
Khlebtsov 1992
;
Mackowski 1994
. Such results were recently extended by Rockstuhl and coworkers for optical activity
Suryadharma and Rockstuhl 2018a
, and by Stout and co-workers for near-field quantities in clusters of spherical particles
Stout et al. 2002
;
Auger and Stout 2008
.
We extend here these previous results to the calculation of orientation-averaged near-field optical chirality. The rest of this manuscript is divided as follows. First, we summarise the essential formulas and our notations for the superposition
T
-matrix method, followed by our newly-derived formulas. In a second part, we illustrate these results by applying the formulas to model clusters of nanoparticles, and discuss the distribution and enhancement of near-field optical chirality around various nanostructures.
These results illustrate the power of analytical results enabled by the
T
-matrix method, which can provide rapid, accurate, and physically-insightful simulations of a vast range of nanostructures. We hope to encourage more researchers to adopt this method in their comparisons to experiments, but also to further improve the method beyond its current limitations
Schebarchov et al. 2019
;
Theobald et al. 2017
.

II
T
-matrix formalism and main formulas

We follow standard notations
Mishchenko et al. 2002b
;
Le Ru and Etchegoin 2008
for vector spherical waves and describe the geometry in the usual spherical coordinates
(r,\varphi,\theta)
(Fig.
1
); for the extension to
N
scatterers we follow closely the scatterer-centred superposition
T
-matrix approach presented by Auger and Stout
Stout et al. 2008
;
Auger and Stout 2008
. This section provides a brief summary of our choice of definitions for completeness.

II.1
A single scatterer

Waterman’s
T
-matrix method
Waterman 1965
;
Waterman 1969
offers a rigorous solution of Helmholtz’ wave equation by describing the light scattering process in terms of an incident field and a scattered field, both expanded into bases of regular and irregular Vector Spherical Wave Functions (VSWFs), respectively
Stout et al. 2002
,

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}_{\text{inc}}(k\mathbf{r})

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\sum_{m=-n}^{n}[

\displaystyle\widetilde{a}_{1,nm}\widetilde{\mathbf{M}}_{nm}\!(k\mathbf{r})

\displaystyle+\widetilde{a}_{2,nm}\widetilde{\mathbf{N}}_{nm}\!(k\mathbf{r})]

(5)

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}_{\text{sca}}(k\mathbf{r})

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\sum_{m=-n}^{n}[

\displaystyle{f}_{1,nm}{\mathbf{M}}_{nm}(k\mathbf{r})

\displaystyle+f_{2,nm}{\mathbf{N}}_{nm}(k\mathbf{r})]

(6)

where
\widetilde{\mathbf{M}}_{nm}
and
\widetilde{\mathbf{N}}_{nm}
are normalised vector spherical wave functions composed of spherical harmonics (
Y_{nm}(\varphi,\theta)
) and spherical Bessel (
j_{n}(k\mathbf{r})
functions for the ingoing wave, while
{\mathbf{M}}_{nm}
and
{\mathbf{N}}_{nm}
are composed of spherical harmonics and spherical Hankel functions (
h_{n}(k\mathbf{r})
) of the first kind for the scattered wave (See Appendix (
A
) for their explicit definition, following Stout and coworkers
Stout et al. 2002
;
Stout et al. 2008
;
Auger and Stout 2008
).
\widetilde{a}_{1,nm}
,
\widetilde{a}_{2,nm}
,
f_{1,nm}
, and
f_{2,nm}
are the associated expansion coefficients.

In a more compact form, we write the incident and scattered electric fields as:

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}_{\text{inc}}(k\mathbf{r})=\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}(k\mathbf{r})\widetilde{\mathbf{a}}

(7)

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}_{\text{sca}}(k\mathbf{r})=\boldsymbol{\Psi}(k\mathbf{r}){\mathbf{f}}

(8)

where
{\widetilde{\mathbf{a}},\mathbf{f}}
are (infinite-dimensional) column vectors of incident and scattered field coefficients.
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}=[\widetilde{\mathbf{M}}\quad\widetilde{\mathbf{N}}],\boldsymbol{\Psi}=[\mathbf{M}\quad\mathbf{N}]
are the corresponding, infinite-dimensional row vectors for 3D-vector-valued basis functions. We note here that all the fields expressed in the spherical basis can be easily converted to another coordinate system, such as the cartesian reference frame
(O,x,y,z)
in Fig.
1
, simply with a suitable conversion matrix (the conversion to cartesian coordinates is given in Appendix (
A
) for reference); this transformation will be left implicit in some formulas below to avoid cluttering already-lengthy formulas.

Given the linearity of the Helmholtz equation governing the scattering problem, the expansion coefficients of the incident field,
\widetilde{\mathbf{a}}
, and of the scattered field,
{\mathbf{f}}
, obey a linear relationship:

\mathbf{f}=\mathbf{T}\widetilde{\mathbf{a}},

(9)

where
\mathbf{T}
is the so-called
‘transition’
or
‘transfer’
matrix (
T
-matrix for short). This quantity is an inherent property of the scatterer at a given frequency, and is independent of the incident field. It is this characteristic which leads to analytical formulas for orientation-averaged properties
Auger and Stout 2008
.

The elements of the
T
-matrix can be determined through a variety of methods
Loke et al. 2009
;
Fruhnert et al. 2017
;
Reid
; for simple shapes the Extended Boundary Condition Method, obtained by enforcing the boundary conditions on the scatterer’s surface, is arguably the most accurate and efficient

. In particular, for a homogeneous, spherical scatterer-centred at the origin,
\mathbf{T}
is a diagonal matrix whose elements coincide with the Mie theory. For a system composed of several scatterers, the collective
T
-matrix can be built up from the individual
‘one-body
T
-matrices’
of a single scatterer
Stout et al. 2002
;
Mishchenko et al. 2002b
. Following the notations of Ref.
Stout et al. 2002
and Ref.
Mishchenko et al. 2002b
we briefly recapitulate this
‘multi-scatterer
T
-matrix’
method.

II.2
The
T
-matrix method for
N
scatterers

We now consider the situation depicted in Fig.
1
, where
N
particles are present. With
\mathbf{r}
we denote the position vector in the global
‘cluster’
reference frame. The position of the centre of the
j

, induces the position vector in this particle’s frame,
\mathbf{r}_{j}=\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}_{0}^{(j)}
.

The one-body
T
-matrix of the

particle is denoted

. To keep the formulas relatively concise we assume in the following that each scatterer’s

-matrix is provided in the orientation of the common reference frame; in practice, this means that the

-matrix of nonspherical particles, typically calculated in a high-symmetry orientation (

In a system composed of
N

e_{N}^{(j)}
the coefficients of the electric field exciting particle
j
, and
f_{N}^{(j)}
the scattered field coefficients from particle
j
,

\displaystyle f_{N}^{(j)}=T_{1}^{(j)}e_{N}^{(j)},

(10)

\displaystyle e_{N}^{(j)}=J^{(j,0)}\widetilde{a}+\sum_{\mathclap{l=1,l\neq j}}^{N}H^{(j,l)}f_{N}^{(l)}.

(11)

where the regular translation matrix
J^{(j,0)}
transforms the vector of incident field coefficients from the global origin (
\mathbf{r}_{0}
) to the position
\mathbf{r}_{j}
. The irregular translation matrices

convert from an irregular VSWF expansion at
\mathbf{r}_{l}
to a regular VSWF expansion at
\mathbf{r}_{j}
.

We can also formally consider the expansion coefficients
f_{N}^{(j)}
of the field scattered by the
j
th
particle in response to the incident field
\widetilde{a}
, now in the presence of all other particles. Specifically, we define the
N
-body

-matrix

of the

th

with
\widetilde{a}
through

\displaystyle f_{N}^{(j)}

\displaystyle=\qquad\quad T_{N}^{(j)}J^{(j,0)}\widetilde{a},

(12)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\text{inc. plane waves}}}}{{=}}\qquad\quad T_{N}^{(j)}\left(e^{i\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{r}_{0}^{(j)}}\widetilde{a}\right).

(13)

These

-body

-matrices can be obtained by replacing Eqs. (
12
) and (
11
) in Eq. (
10
) and solving the following coupled system of equations

T_{N}^{(j)}=T_{1}^{(j)}\left(I+\sum_{\mathclap{l=1,l\neq j}}^{N}H^{(j,l)}T_{N}^{(l)}J^{(l,j)}\right).

(14)

where
I
is the identity matrix.
Equation (
14
) can be used to implicitly define scatterer centred
N
-body
T
-matrices
T_{N}^{(j,l)}

;
Stout et al. 2002
by setting

T_{N}^{(j)}=\sum_{l=1}^{N}T_{N}^{(j,l)}J^{(l,j)}.

(15)

In this way,

described the full
N
-body contribution of particle

to the
T
-matrix of particle

.

After calculation of the one-body
T
-matrices, by replacing Eqs. (
15
) and (

8

N

\mathbf{E}_{\text{sca}}(k\mathbf{r})=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\sum_{l=1}^{N}\boldsymbol{\Psi}(k\mathbf{r})T_{N}^{(j,l)}J^{(l,0)}\widetilde{a}.

(16)

A great advantage of this particle-centred formalism is that the fields may be evaluated even inside the particle cluster, which is not possible when the whole cluster is described by a single global

-matrix

.

We can now proceed to evaluate
\mathscr{C}\propto\Im(\mathbf{E}^{*}\cdot\mathbf{B})
from the knowledge of the local fields. First, according to the Maxwell equation
\mathbf{B}=-i\omega^{-1}\nabla\times\mathbf{E}
, and using Eq. (
5
) and the relation between
\widetilde{\mathbf{M}}
and
\widetilde{\mathbf{N}}

, we have
\mathbf{B}_{\text{inc}}=-ik\omega^{-1}\sum[\widetilde{a}_{1,nm}\widetilde{\mathbf{N}}_{nm}+\widetilde{a}_{2,nm}\widetilde{\mathbf{M}}_{nm}]
. We re-write
\mathbf{B}
in the compact form (
\mathbf{B}_{\text{inc}}(k\mathbf{r})=\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}(k\mathbf{r})\widetilde{\mathbf{a}}
) analogous to Eq. (
7
) for the electric field by defining
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}=[\widetilde{\mathbf{N}}\quad\widetilde{\mathbf{M}}]
for the incident field and similarly
{\mathbf{\Phi}}=[{\mathbf{N}}\quad{\mathbf{M}}]
for the scattered magnetic field. Expanding Eq. (
3
) into incident and scattered fields as
\mathscr{C}=\frac{-\omega\varepsilon_{0}}{2}\Im\left(\textbf{E}_{\text{inc}}^{*}\textbf{B}_{\text{inc}}+\textbf{E}_{\text{inc}}^{*}\textbf{B}_{\text{sca}}+\textbf{E}_{\text{sca}}^{*}\textbf{B}_{\text{inc}}+\textbf{E}_{\text{sca}}^{*}\textbf{B}_{\text{sca}}\right)
and inserting the corresponding expressions yields

\begin{split}\mathscr{C}=&\frac{k\varepsilon_{0}E_{0}^{2}}{2}\Re\Bigg[\widetilde{a}^{\dagger}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}^{\dagger}(k\mathbf{r}){\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}}(k\mathbf{r})\widetilde{a}+\\
{}&\sum_{j=1}^{N}\sum_{l=1}^{N}\widetilde{a}^{\dagger}{\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}}^{\dagger}(k\mathbf{r})\boldsymbol{\Phi}(k\mathbf{r}_{j}){T}_{N}^{(j,l)}{J}^{(l,0)}\widetilde{a}+\\
{}&\sum_{j=1}^{N}\sum_{l=1}^{N}\widetilde{a}^{\dagger}J^{(0,l)}{T^{\dagger}}_{N}^{(j,l)}\boldsymbol{\Psi}^{\dagger}(k\mathbf{r}_{j})\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}(k\mathbf{r})\widetilde{a}+\\

where

. Note that each of the four terms is of the form

\begin{split}\mathscr{C}=&\frac{k\varepsilon_{0}E_{0}^{2}}{2}\Re\Bigg[\widetilde{a}^{\dagger}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}^{\dagger}(k\mathbf{r})\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}(k\mathbf{r})\widetilde{a}+\\

(19)

where the addition rule of the translation matrices
I=J^{(l,0)}J^{(0,l)}
is used.

This expression for
\mathscr{C}
makes no assumption on the polarisation state of the incident field. We now turn to the description of optical chirality under illumination with circularly-polarised plane waves.

II.3
Helicity versus parity bases for VSWFs

In the standard basis of transverse-electric and transverse-magnetic VSWFs, the
T
-matrix of Eq. (
9
) is usually written in the
2\times 2
block-matrix form

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}T_{11}&T_{12}\\
T_{21}&T_{22}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\widetilde{a}_{1}\\
\widetilde{a}_{2}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}f_{1}\\
f_{2}\end{bmatrix}

(20)

where subscripts 1 and 2 refer to electric and magnetic multipolar contributions, respectively. The sub-matrices
T_{11}
and
T_{22}
describe the coupling of electric-electric and magnetic-magnetic multipolar components, while
T_{12}
and
T_{21}
describe cross-coupling of electric and magnetic multipolar components.

For circularly-polarised light, it is more useful to consider a different basis of VSWFs
Suryadharma and Rockstuhl 2018a

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}T_{\scriptscriptstyle LL}&T_{\scriptscriptstyle LR}\\
T_{\scriptscriptstyle RL}&T_{\scriptscriptstyle RR}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\widetilde{a}_{\scriptscriptstyle L}\\
\widetilde{a}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}f_{\scriptscriptstyle L}\\
f_{\scriptscriptstyle R}\end{bmatrix}

(21)

where the subscripts (
R
) and (
L
) refer to right and left circularly polarised light. This matrix describes the scattering of circularly-polarised incident fields in the helicity basis. The transformation from the parity basis can be obtained via the helicity operator (
\Lambda=\frac{\nabla\times}{k}
) and leads to,

\displaystyle\mathbf{Z}_{R,nm}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\mathbf{M}_{nm}-\mathbf{N}_{nm}),\quad\Lambda\mathbf{Z}_{R,nm}=-\mathbf{Z}_{R,nm}

(22a)

\displaystyle\mathbf{Z}_{L,nm}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\mathbf{M}_{nm}+\mathbf{N}_{nm}),\quad\Lambda\mathbf{Z}_{L,nm}=\mathbf{Z}_{L,nm}

(22b)

.

These definitions lead to the following relation between the
T
-matrix components in parity and helicity bases

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}T_{\scriptscriptstyle LL}&T_{\scriptscriptstyle LR}\\
T_{\scriptscriptstyle RL}&T_{\scriptscriptstyle RR}\end{bmatrix}=\frac{1}{2}\begin{bmatrix}I&I\\
I&-I\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}T_{11}&T_{12}\\
T_{21}&T_{22}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}I&I\\
I&-I\end{bmatrix},

(23)

where
I
is the identity matrix with the same size as the 4 matrix blocks (
T_{11}
, etc.).
Consequently, electric and magnetic fields in the helicity form become

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}_{\text{tot}}=E_{0}\left(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}(k\mathbf{r})\widetilde{a}+\sum_{j=1}^{N}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}(k\mathbf{r}_{j})T_{N}^{(j)}J^{(j,0)}\widetilde{a}\right),

(24)

\displaystyle\mathbf{B}_{\text{tot}}=E_{0}\frac{-ik}{\omega}\left(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\Delta}}(k\mathbf{r})\widetilde{a}+\sum_{j=1}^{N}\boldsymbol{\Delta}(k\mathbf{r}_{j})T_{N}^{(j)}J^{(j,0)}\widetilde{a}\right),

(25)

where we introduce
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}=[\widetilde{\mathbf{Z}}_{L,nm}\quad\widetilde{\mathbf{Z}}_{R,nm}]
,
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\Delta}}=[\widetilde{\mathbf{Z}}_{L,mn}\quad-\widetilde{\mathbf{Z}}_{R,mn}]
, and the same convention for
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}
and
\boldsymbol{\Delta}
(composed of irregular VSWFs). Note that the
T_{N}^{(j)}
as well as
J^{(j,0)}
in Eq. (
24
) need to be also in the helicity basis, following the same transformation as Eq. (
23
).

With the general expression for
\mathscr{C}
and the transformations between parity and helicity bases, we can now proceed to calculate the orientation-averaged
\mathscr{C}
for circularly polarised incident light.

II.4
Orientation-averaging of
\mathscr{C}

A distinctive advantage of the
T
-matrix framework is that any variation of the incident field is captured in the
\widetilde{a}
expansion coefficients, while the
T
-matrix itself is unchanged. From this property and the orthonormality of VSWFs over the 2-sphere, the orientation average of any optical property that can be written as a bilinear product of the form
\widetilde{a}^{\dagger}\mathbf{M}\widetilde{a}
reduces to a simple trace formula
Auger and Stout 2008
;
Suryadharma and Rockstuhl 2018b
,

\langle\widetilde{a}^{\dagger}\mathbf{M}\widetilde{a}\rangle=4\pi\operatorname{Tr}(\mathbf{M})

(26)

where
\operatorname{Tr}
stands for
‘trace of’
and
\mathbf{M}
is any bilinear operator whose matrix coefficients are independent of the incident field. This formula can be directly applied to evaluate the orientation-average of Eq. (
19
). We consider an incident field described in the helicity basis, and restrict the coefficients to a single helicity (corresponding to either left or right polarisation). This corresponds to
\widetilde{a}=[0\quad a_{\scriptscriptstyle R}]^{t}
for
R
polarisation, and
\widetilde{a}=[a_{\scriptscriptstyle L}\quad 0]^{t}
for
L
polarisation. The result in both cases contains four different terms (See Appendix (
B
) for more details),

\langle\mathscr{C}\rangle=2\pi k\varepsilon_{0}E_{0}^{2}\,\Re\left(A_{0}+B_{0}+C_{0}+D_{0}\right)

(27)

with,

For
R
polarisation:

\begin{split}A^{\text{(R)}}_{0}=&{-1}/{4\pi}\\
B^{\text{(R)}}_{0}=&\operatorname{Tr}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{N}\sum_{l=1}^{N}\widetilde{\mathbf{Z}}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}^{\dagger}(k\mathbf{r}_{l})\left[\mathbf{Z}_{\scriptscriptstyle L}(k\mathbf{r}_{j})T_{\scriptscriptstyle LR}^{(j,l)}-\mathbf{Z}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}(k\mathbf{r}_{j})T_{\scriptscriptstyle RR}^{(j,l)}\right]\right)\\
C^{\text{(R)}}_{0}=&\operatorname{Tr}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{N}\sum_{l=1}^{N}\left[-T_{\scriptscriptstyle LR}^{\dagger(j,l)}\mathbf{Z}_{\scriptscriptstyle L}^{\dagger}(k\mathbf{r}_{j})-T_{\scriptscriptstyle RR}^{\dagger(j,l)}\mathbf{Z}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}^{\dagger}(k\mathbf{r}_{j})\right]\widetilde{\mathbf{Z}}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}(k\mathbf{r}_{l})\right)\\
D^{\text{(R)}}_{0}=&\operatorname{Tr}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{N}\sum_{l=1}^{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}J_{\scriptscriptstyle RR}^{(k,l)}\left(T^{\dagger(j,l)}_{\scriptscriptstyle LR}\mathbf{Z}_{\scriptscriptstyle L}^{\dagger}(k\mathbf{r}_{j})+T^{\dagger(j,l)}_{\scriptscriptstyle RR}\mathbf{Z}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}^{\dagger}(k\mathbf{r}_{j})\right)\left(\mathbf{Z}_{\scriptscriptstyle L}(k\mathbf{r}_{i})T_{\scriptscriptstyle LR}^{(i,k)}-\mathbf{Z}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}(k\mathbf{r}_{i})T_{\scriptscriptstyle RR}^{(i,k)}\right)\right).\end{split}

(28)

Notice that the sum of
B^{\text{(R)}}_{0}
and
C^{\text{(R)}}_{0}
simplifies to,

\Re(B^{\text{(R)}}_{0}+C^{\text{(R)}}_{0})=-2\operatorname{Tr}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{N}\sum_{l=1}^{N}\widetilde{\mathbf{Z}}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}^{\dagger}(k\mathbf{r}_{l})\mathbf{Z}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}(k\mathbf{r}_{j})T_{\scriptscriptstyle RR}^{(j,l)}\right).

(29)

The corresponding formulas for
L
polarisation read,

\begin{split}A^{\text{(L)}}_{0}=&{+1}/{4\pi}\\
B^{\text{(L)}}_{0}=&\operatorname{Tr}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{N}\sum_{l=1}^{N}\widetilde{\mathbf{Z}}_{\scriptscriptstyle L}^{\dagger}(k\mathbf{r}_{l})\left[\mathbf{Z}_{\scriptscriptstyle L}(k\mathbf{r}_{j})T_{\scriptscriptstyle LL}^{(j,l)}-\mathbf{Z}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}(k\mathbf{r}_{j})T_{\scriptscriptstyle RL}^{(j,l)}\right]\right)\\
C^{\text{(L)}}_{0}=&\operatorname{Tr}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{N}\sum_{l=1}^{N}\left[T_{\scriptscriptstyle LL}^{\dagger(j,l)}\mathbf{Z}_{\scriptscriptstyle L}^{\dagger}(k\mathbf{r}_{j})+T_{\scriptscriptstyle RL}^{\dagger(j,l)}\mathbf{Z}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}^{\dagger}(k\mathbf{r}_{j})\right]\widetilde{\mathbf{Z}}_{\scriptscriptstyle L}(k\mathbf{r}_{l})\right)\\
D^{\text{(L)}}_{0}=&\operatorname{Tr}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{N}\sum_{l=1}^{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}J_{\scriptscriptstyle LL}^{(k,l)}\left(T^{\dagger(j,l)}_{\scriptscriptstyle LL}\mathbf{Z}_{\scriptscriptstyle L}^{\dagger}(k\mathbf{r}_{j})+T^{\dagger(j,l)}_{\scriptscriptstyle RL}\mathbf{Z}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}^{\dagger}(k\mathbf{r}_{j})\right)\left(\mathbf{Z}_{\scriptscriptstyle L}(k\mathbf{r}_{i})T_{\scriptscriptstyle LL}^{(i,k)}-\mathbf{Z}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}(k\mathbf{r}_{i})T_{\scriptscriptstyle RL}^{(i,k)}\right)\right),\end{split}

(30)

where the sum of
B^{\text{(L)}}_{0}
and
C^{\text{(L)}}_{0}
simplifies to,

\Re(B^{\text{(L)}}_{0}+C^{\text{(L)}}_{0})=2\operatorname{Tr}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{N}\sum_{l=1}^{N}\widetilde{\mathbf{Z}}_{\scriptscriptstyle L}^{\dagger}(k\mathbf{r}_{l})\mathbf{Z}_{\scriptscriptstyle L}(k\mathbf{r}_{j})T_{\scriptscriptstyle LL}^{(j,l)}\right).

(31)

In some circumstances we may also be interested in averaging over both possible states of circular polarisation, with
\tfrac{\langle\mathscr{C}\rangle^{\text{(R)}}+\langle\mathscr{C}\rangle^{\text{(L)}}}{2}
, which should vanish everywhere for achiral cluster geometries.

The terms
A_{0}^{(R)}
and
A_{0}^{(L)}
of Eqs. (
28
) and (
31
), respectively, are equal and opposite and correspond to the results for circularly-polarised plane waves. This highlights the fact that in the absence of a scatterer, and for linearly polarised light,
\langle\mathscr{C}\rangle
vanishes identically:
a_{\text{R}}
and
a_{\text{L}}
are of the same magnitude, only
A_{0}^{(R)}
and
A_{0}^{(L)}
contribute to
\langle\mathscr{C}\rangle
, and cancel each other. This is also clear from Eq. (
19
) where the first term contains
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}^{\dagger}(k\mathbf{r})\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}(k\mathbf{r})
. An integration over the 2-sphere for linearly-polarised incoming plane waves will necessarily make this term vanish by orthogonality of different VSWFs.

The value of
\mathscr{C}
is often normalised with respect to the value of circularly-polarised plane waves with unit incident field,
\mathscr{C}=\pm k\varepsilon_{0}/2
, and we introduce

\langle\overline{\mathscr{C}}\rangle=\frac{2}{k\varepsilon_{0}E_{0}^{2}}\langle\mathscr{C}\rangle

(32)

for easier comparisons in the examples below.

III
Application to model nanostructures

We have applied the newly-derived formula Eqs. (
28
)–(
31
) to a range of cluster geometries, with both spherical and nonspherical scatterers, metallic and dielectric. Our custom-built computer program for the scatterer-centred superposition
T
-matrix method is based on the algorithms described in Refs.
Mackowski and Mishchenko 1996
;
Stout et al. 2002
and has been tested against FEM simulations
Schebarchov et al. 2019
. The validity of Eqs. (
28
) and (
31
) was confirmed by comparing the results to orientation-averaged values of

computed by numerical integration of Eq. (
4
) over the full solid angle. The

. Numerical implementations of the
T
-matrix method require us to truncate the series of VSWFs to a maximum multipolar order

; in our calculations a value of

was used, which was sufficient to ensure convergence of the results.

The following examples were chosen to illustrate the results in simple geometries but the method is equally applicable to more complex structures, for which an in-depth study is beyond the scope of this manuscript.

IV
Conclusion

We have derived analytical formulas for the orientation-averaged degree of optical chirality
\langle\mathscr{C}\rangle
around clusters of nanoparticles illuminated with circularly-polarised light, using the rigorous superposition-
T
-matrix framework. The formulas, although lengthy, are readily implemented in software, and provide an efficient method to calculate rigorously-averaged results, in contrast to numerical estimates obtained from several directions of incidence, as is commonly done with other numerical methods. The local degree of optical chirality informs us on the subtle interplay between complex electric and magnetic fields, and how their combination may enhance the chiroptical response of molecules in the vicinity of a cluster of particles. We illustrated the behaviour of
\langle\mathscr{C}\rangle
by mapping its distribution around single spheres and dimers of Ag and Si, with less intuitive results compared to the better-known electric field intensity. The importance of the analytical formula for
\langle\mathscr{C}\rangle
was illustrated for a helix of elongated particles, where we show that a simple averaging of the results from three orthogonal directions provides a poor estimate of the true value.

These few examples illustrate the broad range of studies that become tractable with an efficient and accurate formulas for
\langle\mathscr{C}\rangle
, notably the search for optimal structures that maximise the chiroptical response of adsorbed species, either at a specific location in space, or as averages over the surface of the particles.

The
T
-matrix framework used to arrive at these results is ideally-suited for many studies requiring orientation-averaging, and we hope this paper serves to demonstrate its particular usefulness in the context of near-field chiroptical properties where it has not received much attention to date, with most of the literature relying on purely numerical solutions such as FDTD, or coupled-dipole approximations with limited range of validity. Although the superposition
T
-matrix method provides a rigorous solution to the light scattering problem, it also suffers some limitations, in particular where the circumscribed spheres of neighbouring particles overlap
Schebarchov et al. 2019
. We hope this study will also trigger further work in improving the method and overcoming its current limitations
Theobald et al. 2017
.

Appendix B
Derivation of Eq. (
28
)

We provide here a step-by-step derivation to arrive at equations (
28
) and (
31
). The key ingredient is to carefully keep track of the block matrix structure of the involved quantities in the helicity basis for VSWFs. For the sake of brevity, we will explicitly perform this derivation for right circularly-polarised light, Eq. (
28
). The modifications needed to derive Eq. (
31
) are minimal.

As discussed in the main text, right circularly-polarised light is described with an expansion coefficient
\widetilde{a}^{\text{R}}
that has the block structure
\widetilde{a}^{\text{R}}_{\scriptscriptstyle LR}=[0\quad a_{\scriptscriptstyle R}]^{t}
in the helicity basis (more generally, we use the
LR
subscript to refer to helicity basis in the following). Then, starting from Eq. (
19
), we convert each term to its helical basis representation and insert the expansion coefficient for
\widetilde{a}
. The resulting equation is the sum of four terms
\mathscr{C}=\frac{k\varepsilon_{0}E_{0}^{2}}{2}\,\Re\left(A+B+C+D\right)
, where we named the terms in a way indicative of their contributions to the final results, and we derive them sequentially below.

First, notice that the basis transformation changing
\widetilde{a}
to
\widetilde{a}_{\scriptscriptstyle LR}
is simply given by

\widetilde{a}_{\scriptscriptstyle LR}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{bmatrix}I&I\\
I&-I\end{bmatrix}\widetilde{a},

(41)

which holds independent of whether we look at regular or irregular VSWFs. This transformation is self-adjoint, real, and involutive.

As a result, it is straightforward to get the precursor of
A^{\text{(R)}}_{0}
from Eq. (
19
):

\displaystyle A^{\text{(R)}}

\displaystyle=(\widetilde{a}^{\text{R}})^{\dagger}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}^{\dagger}(k\mathbf{r})\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}(k\mathbf{r})\widetilde{a}^{\text{R}},

(42)

\displaystyle=(\widetilde{a}^{\text{R}}_{\scriptscriptstyle LR})^{\dagger}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}^{\dagger}(k\mathbf{r})\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\Delta}}(k\mathbf{r})\widetilde{a}^{\text{R}}_{\scriptscriptstyle LR}.

(43)

At this point, we insert the definition of
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}
and
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\Delta}}
. When doing this, it is useful to remember that to transpose a block matrix, one first treats the blocks as entries of a matrix, transposes this matrix as usual, and then additionally transposes each of the component blocks. This gives:

A^{\text{(R)}}=\widetilde{a}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}^{\dagger}\widetilde{\mathbf{Z}}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}^{\dagger}\left(-\widetilde{\mathbf{Z}}_{\scriptscriptstyle L}\right)\widetilde{a}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}.

(44)

Orientation-averaging of this expression relies on the orthogonality relations between VSWFs on the sphere and must lead to a constant. The main difficulty is to keep track of the normalisation factors, but here we can also realise that the average value of
\mathscr{C}
for the incident field must equal the value of a single circularly-polarised plane wave, which is independent of its direction of propagation. This gives
A^{\text{(R)}}_{0}=-\tfrac{1}{4\pi}
.

This combination of matrix algebra before performing the orientation average repeats in the other terms of Eq. (
28
). In the following we therefore focus on the matrix structure. For
B^{\text{(R)}}
, we write

B^{\text{(R)}}=\\
{}\quad\sum_{j,l=1}^{N}[0,\widetilde{a}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}^{\dagger}]\begin{bmatrix}\widetilde{\mathbf{Z}}_{\scriptscriptstyle L}^{l,\dagger}\\
\widetilde{\mathbf{Z}}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}^{l,\dagger}\end{bmatrix}[\widetilde{\mathbf{Z}}_{\scriptscriptstyle L}^{j},-\widetilde{\mathbf{Z}}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}^{j}]\begin{bmatrix}(T^{(l,j)}_{N})_{\scriptscriptstyle LL}&(T^{(l,j)}_{N})_{\scriptscriptstyle LR}\\
(T^{(l,j)}_{N})_{\scriptscriptstyle RL}&(T^{(l,j)}_{N})_{\scriptscriptstyle RR}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}0\\
\widetilde{a}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}\end{bmatrix}\\
{}\quad=\sum_{j,l=1}^{N}\widetilde{a}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}^{\dagger}\widetilde{\mathbf{Z}}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}^{l,\dagger}\left(\widetilde{\mathbf{Z}}_{\scriptscriptstyle L}^{j}(T^{(l,j)}_{N})_{\scriptscriptstyle LR}-\widetilde{\mathbf{Z}}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}^{j}(T^{(l,j)}_{N})_{\scriptscriptstyle RR}\right)\widetilde{a}_{\scriptscriptstyle R},

(45)

which, upon integration, leads to
B^{\text{(R)}}_{0}
.

In the expressions for
C^{\text{(R)}}
we need to account for the transposition of block matrices:

C^{\text{(R)}}=\\
{}\quad\sum_{j,l=1}^{N}[0,\widetilde{a}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}^{\dagger}]\begin{bmatrix}(T^{(l,j)}_{N})_{\scriptscriptstyle LL}^{\dagger}&(T^{(l,j)}_{N})_{\scriptscriptstyle RL}^{\dagger}\\
(T^{(l,j)}_{N})_{\scriptscriptstyle LR}^{\dagger}&(T^{(l,j)}_{N})_{\scriptscriptstyle RR}^{\dagger}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\widetilde{\mathbf{Z}}_{\scriptscriptstyle L}^{l,\dagger}\\
\widetilde{\mathbf{Z}}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}^{l,\dagger}\end{bmatrix}[\widetilde{\mathbf{Z}}_{\scriptscriptstyle L}^{j},-\widetilde{\mathbf{Z}}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}^{j}]\begin{bmatrix}0\\
\widetilde{a}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}\end{bmatrix}\\
{}\quad=-\sum_{j,l=1}^{N}\widetilde{a}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}^{\dagger}\left((T^{(l,j)}_{N})_{\scriptscriptstyle LR}^{\dagger}\widetilde{\mathbf{Z}}_{\scriptscriptstyle L}^{l,\dagger}+(T^{(l,j)}_{N})_{\scriptscriptstyle RR}^{\dagger}\widetilde{\mathbf{Z}}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}^{l,\dagger}\right)\widetilde{\mathbf{Z}}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}^{j}\widetilde{a}_{\scriptscriptstyle R},

(46)

which, after orientation-averaging, yields
C^{\text{(R)}}_{0}
. Finally, the term
D^{\text{(R)}}
is derived as follows:

\displaystyle D^{\text{(R)}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i,j,k,l=1\\
\mathclap{\text{pairwise distinct}}\end{subarray}}^{N}[0,\widetilde{a}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}^{\dagger}]\begin{bmatrix}J^{(k,l)}_{\scriptscriptstyle LL}&{0}\\
{0}&J_{\scriptscriptstyle RR}^{(k,l)}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}(T^{(j,l)}_{N})_{\scriptscriptstyle LL}^{\dagger}&(T^{(j,l)}_{N})_{\scriptscriptstyle RL}^{\dagger}\\
(T^{(j,l)}_{N})_{\scriptscriptstyle LR}^{\dagger}&(T^{(j,l)}_{N})_{\scriptscriptstyle RR}^{\dagger}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\widetilde{\mathbf{Z}}_{\scriptscriptstyle L}^{j,\dagger}\\
\widetilde{\mathbf{Z}}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}^{j,\dagger}\end{bmatrix}[\widetilde{\mathbf{Z}}_{\scriptscriptstyle L}^{i},-\widetilde{\mathbf{Z}}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}^{i}]\begin{bmatrix}(T^{(i,k)}_{N})_{\scriptscriptstyle LL}&(T^{(i,k)}_{N})_{\scriptscriptstyle LR}\\
(T^{(i,k)}_{N})_{\scriptscriptstyle RL}&(T^{(i,k)}_{N})_{\scriptscriptstyle RR}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}0\\
\widetilde{a}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}\end{bmatrix},

(47)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i,j,k,l=1\\
\mathclap{\text{pairwise distinct}}\end{subarray}}^{N}\widetilde{a}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}^{\dagger}J_{\scriptscriptstyle RR}^{(k,l)}\left((T^{(j,l)}_{N})_{\scriptscriptstyle LR}^{\dagger}\widetilde{\mathbf{Z}}_{\scriptscriptstyle L}^{j,\dagger}+(T^{(j,l)}_{N})_{\scriptscriptstyle RR}^{\dagger}\widetilde{\mathbf{Z}}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}^{j,\dagger}\right)\left(\widetilde{\mathbf{Z}}_{\scriptscriptstyle L}^{i}(T^{(i,k)}_{N})_{\scriptscriptstyle LR}-\widetilde{\mathbf{Z}}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}^{i}(T^{(i,k)}_{N})_{\scriptscriptstyle RR}\right)\widetilde{a}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}.

(48)

As above, the orientation average of this expression will result in
D^{\text{(R)}}_{0}
. The proof for left-handed incident fields works the same way with appropriate index changes.
