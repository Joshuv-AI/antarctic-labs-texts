Deforming the ODE/IM correspondence with T ​ T ¯ \mathrm{T}\overline{\mathrm{T}}
Fabrizio Aramini, Nicolò Brizio, Stefano Negro, Roberto Tateo[0.3cm]

Abstract
The ODE/IM correspondence is an exact link between classical and quantum integrable models. The primary purpose of this work is to show that it remains valid after T ​ T ¯ \mathrm{T}\overline{\mathrm{T}} perturbation on both sides of the correspondence. In particular, we prove that the deformed Lax pair of the sinh-Gordon model, obtained from the unperturbed one through a dynamical change of coordinates, leads to the same Burgers-type equation governing the quantum spectral flow induced by T ​ T ¯ \mathrm{T}\overline{\mathrm{T}} . Our main conclusions have general validity, as the analysis may be easily adapted to all the known ODE/IM examples involving integrable quantum field theories.

Though initially referring only to the CFT vacuum states, the ODE/IM correspondence was later generalised to encompass excited states

In this perspective, we are dealing with two, a priori unrelated systems: a classical integrable equation – the modified sinh-Gordon one – and a quantum integrable model – such as the sine- and sinh-Gordon models. The correspondence can then be viewed as an equality between the conserved quantities of these two systems.

,

It is related to the Nambu-Goto string

\mathrm{T}\overline{\mathrm{T}}

\mathrm{T}\overline{\mathrm{T}}

\mathrm{T}\overline{\mathrm{T}}
deformation. We will proceed in steps, first recalling in section

In this work we choose to focus on the specific instance of the ODE/IM correspondence analysed in

\mathrm{T}\overline{\mathrm{T}}
-deformed classical field theory. To our knowledge, the only previous appearance of the Burgers’ equation in a classical theory concerns a very specific class of zero-momentum solutions to the sine-Gordon model
[
59
]

Finally, our results should be considered as a first step toward the study of irrelevant deformations using the ODE/IM correspondence as a powerful quantisation tool.

The quantum sine-Gordon model at finite volume

Integrable models and their properties have been
extensively studied during past decades, and powerful methods developed to determine the finite-size spectrum of an integrable quantum field theory.
One of these is the non-linear integral equation (NLIE) approach
[
62
,
63
,
64
,
65
,
66
,
5
,
67
,
68
]
, which we briefly review in this section before discussing the effects of the
\mathrm{T}\overline{\mathrm{T}}

2.1
The non-linear integral equation

Consider the sine-Gordon quantum field theory defined on a cylinder of radius
R/2\pi
. The complete information on its spectrum can be extracted from the
counting function

f_{\nu}(\theta)
, solution to the following NLIE

[
63
,
64
,
65
,
67
,
68
]

\displaystyle f_{\nu}(\theta)

\displaystyle=\nu(R,k\,|\,\theta)-\int_{\mathcal{C}_{1}}d\theta^{\prime}\,\mathcal{K}(\theta-\theta^{\prime})\,\log\left(1+e^{-f_{\nu}(\theta^{\prime})}\right)

\displaystyle+\int_{\mathcal{C}_{2}}d\theta^{\prime}\,\mathcal{K}(\theta-\theta^{\prime})\,\log\left(1+e^{f_{\nu}(\theta^{\prime})}\right)\,,

(2.1)

where

\nu(R,k\,|\,\theta)=2\pi\imath\,k-\imath\,mR\sinh(\theta)\;,

(2.2)

is the so-called driving term. In equations (
2.1
) and (
2.2
),
m
denotes the sine-Gordon soliton mass and
k\in[-1/2,1/2]
is the quasi-momentum, also known as the
twist
, whose role is to select the vacuum
[
69
,
14
]
. In (
2.1
), the convolution kernel
\mathcal{K}(\theta)
is

\mathcal{K}(\theta)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{dp}{2\pi}\cos(p\,\theta)\,\frac{\sinh\left(\pi p\frac{1-\alpha}{2\alpha}\right)}{2\cosh\left(\pi\frac{p}{2}\right)\,\sinh\left(\pi\frac{p}{2\alpha}\right)}\,,

(2.3)

where
\alpha=\beta^{-2}-1
and
\beta^{2}<1
is the sine-Gordon coupling.
For technical reasons, as in
[
14
]
, in the following we shall limit our attention to the range
\beta^{2}<1/2
, which corresponds to the region
\alpha>1
.

The information on the specific energy eigenstate state under consideration is encoded in the choice of the integration contours
\mathcal{C}_{1}
and
\mathcal{C}_{2}
. For the ground state
\mathcal{C}_{1}=\mathbb{R}+\imath 0^{+}=\mathcal{C}_{2}^{*}
, while for the excited states the contours
\mathcal{C}_{1}
and
\mathcal{C}_{2}
encircle a number of singularities
\{\theta_{i}\}
of
\log\left(1+e^{f_{\nu}(\theta_{i})}\right)
. See
[
70
,
71
,
67
,
68
]
for more details.

Energy and momentum can be obtained from the counting function from the following expression

\displaystyle E

\displaystyle=m\left(\int_{\mathcal{C}_{1}}\frac{dy}{2\pi\imath}\,\sinh(y)\,\log\left(1+e^{-f_{\nu}(y)}\right)-\int_{\mathcal{C}_{2}}\frac{dy}{2\pi\imath}\,\sinh(y)\,\log\left(1+e^{f_{\nu}(y)}\right)\right)\,,

(2.4)

\displaystyle P

\displaystyle=m\left(\int_{\mathcal{C}_{1}}\frac{dy}{2\pi\imath}\,\cosh(y)\,\log\left(1+e^{-f_{\nu}(y)}\right)-\int_{\mathcal{C}_{2}}\frac{dy}{2\pi\imath}\,\cosh(y)\,\log\left(1+e^{f_{\nu}(y)}\right)\right)\,.

In particular, the momentum can be computed exactly via the so-called
dilogarithm trick
. Using this, one easily checks that
P(R)=2\pi p/R
with
p\in\mathbb{Z}
(see for example the Lemma in section 7 of
[
72
]
).

Finally, in the following we shall adopt the following specific parametrization
[
14
]

mR=2\sqrt{\pi}\mathbf{s}^{\alpha+1}\frac{\Gamma\left(1+\frac{1}{2\alpha}\right)}{\Gamma\left(\frac{3}{2}+\frac{1}{2\alpha}\right)}\,,

(2.5)

where
\mathbf{s}
is a dimensionless scaling constant.

2.2
The
\mathrm{T}\overline{\mathrm{T}}
deformation

At the level of the non-linear integral equation (
2.1
), the
\mathrm{T}\overline{\mathrm{T}}
perturbation is introduced by implementing the following modification of the convolution kernel
[
24
]

\mathcal{K}(\theta)\xmapsto{\mbox{$\mathrm{T}\overline{\mathrm{T}}$}}\mathcal{K}(\theta)-\tau\frac{m^{2}}{2\pi}\cosh(\theta)\,.

(2.6)

Inserting (
2.6
) into equation (
2.1
), after simple manipulations, one finds that
f_{\nu}^{(\tau)}(\theta)
, the counting function of the deformed theory, still satisfies (
2.1
), up to a redefinition of the driving term

\nu^{(\tau)}=\nu(R_{0},k\,|\,\theta-\theta_{0})\;,

(2.7)

with

\displaystyle R_{0}^{2}=\left(R+\tau E(R,\tau)\right)^{2}-\tau^{2}P^{2}(R,\tau)\,,

(2.8)

\displaystyle\tanh{\theta_{0}}=\tau\frac{P(R)}{R+\tau E(R,\tau)}\,.

(2.9)

The redefinition (
2.7
) implies the famous inviscid Burgers’ flow equation for the finite-volume quantum spectrum of
\mathrm{T}\overline{\mathrm{T}}
-deformed field theories
[
24
,
23
]
:

\frac{\partial}{\partial\tau}E(R,\tau)=E(R,\tau)\frac{\partial}{\partial R}E(R,\tau)+\frac{P(R)^{2}}{R}\,.

(2.10)

3
The modified classical sinh-Gordon model

In this section, we will follow very closely
[
14
]
in the definition of the quantities of interest.
Let us consider the modified sinh-Gordon (mShG) model, with equation of motion (EoM)

\partial_{z}\partial_{\bar{z}}\eta-e^{2\eta}+p(z,s)p(\bar{z},s)e^{-2\eta}=0\,,

(3.1)

where the complex coordinates
\mathbf{z}=(z,\bar{z})
are dimensionless.
The function

p(z,s)=z^{2\alpha}-s^{2\alpha}\,,

(3.2)

is characterized by the pair of parameters
\alpha
and
s\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
. Introducing polar coordinates,

z=\rho\,e^{\imath\varphi}\,,\quad\bar{z}=\rho\,e^{-\imath\varphi}\,,

(3.3)

we can describe the field configurations
\eta(\rho,\varphi)
relevant to the ODE/IM correspondence as those that satisfy the following requirements

–

e^{-\eta(\rho,\varphi)}
is a single-valued, non-zero complex function on the cone
C_{\pi/\alpha}
with apex angle
\pi/\alpha
and
L+\bar{L}
punctures;

–

the
\rho\rightarrow\infty
asymptotic behaviour is
e^{-\eta(\rho,\varphi)}\sim\rho^{-\alpha}
;

–

the
\rho\rightarrow 0
asymptotic behaviour is
e^{-\eta(\rho,\varphi)}\sim\rho^{-l}
, with
l\in\left[-1/2,1/2\right]
.

Through the ODE/IM correspondence, the conserved quantities of the
classical
mShG model, evaluated on the above field configurations, are identified with the
quantum
ones of the sine-Gordon model, such as its energy and momentum (
2.4
). The parameter
l
is then related to the quasi-momentum in (
2.2
) as
l=2|k|-1/2
. The number and positions of the
L+\bar{L}
punctures determine the specific energy eigenstate on the quantum side of the correspondence and are constrained by a system of algebraic equations, the
monodromy-free conditions
of
[
12
,
73
]
, also described in
[
22
]
.
In this section we will describe more in detail the construction of the classical conserved charges of the mShG model. These charges can be understood geometrically as integrals along specific contours. We will leverage this perspective in the following section to apply the
\mathrm{T}\overline{\mathrm{T}}
deformation on the classical side of the ODE/IM correspondence.

3.1
Second order linear differential equations

As it is well known, the mShG equation (
3.1
) can be interpreted as the compatibility condition of the linear problem

\partial_{z}\Psi=L_{1}\Psi\,,\quad\partial_{\bar{z}}\Psi=L_{2}\Psi\,,

(3.4)

which involves the Lax pair

\begin{aligned} &L_{1}=-\frac{1}{2}\partial_{z}\eta\,\sigma^{3}+e^{\vartheta}\left(\sigma^{+}e^{\eta}-\sigma^{-}p(z,s)e^{-\eta}\right)\,,\\
&L_{2}=\frac{1}{2}\partial_{\bar{z}}\eta\,\sigma^{3}+e^{-\vartheta}\left(\sigma^{-}e^{\eta}-\sigma^{+}p(\bar{z},s)e^{-\eta}\right)\,.\end{aligned}\,

(3.5)

Here
\sigma^{3}
and
\sigma^{\pm}
are the Pauli matrices and
\vartheta
is the spectral parameter while
\Psi
in (
3.4
) is a two-dimensional vector.

One can write the general solution of the linear problem (
3.4
) as

\Psi=\begin{pmatrix}e^{\frac{\vartheta}{2}}e^{\frac{\eta}{2}}\psi\\
e^{-\frac{\vartheta}{2}}e^{-\frac{\eta}{2}}(\partial_{z}+\partial_{z}\eta)\psi\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}e^{\frac{\vartheta}{2}}e^{-\frac{\eta}{2}}(\partial_{\bar{z}}+\partial_{\bar{z}}\eta)\bar{\psi}\\
e^{-\frac{\vartheta}{2}}e^{\frac{\eta}{2}}\bar{\psi}\end{pmatrix}\,,

(3.6)

where the auxiliary fields
\psi
and
\bar{\psi}
solve the Schrödinger-type equations

\displaystyle\left(\partial_{z}^{2}-u(\mathbf{z})-e^{2\vartheta}p(z,s)\right)\psi(\mathbf{z})=0\,,

(3.7)

\displaystyle\left(\partial_{\bar{z}}^{2}-\bar{u}(\mathbf{z})-e^{-2\vartheta}p(\bar{z},s)\right)\bar{\psi}(\mathbf{z})=0\,.

The function
u(\mathbf{z})
depends on the field
\eta
appearing in (
3.1
) as:

u({\bf z})=\left(\partial_{z}\eta({\bf z})\right)^{2}-\partial_{z}^{2}\eta({\bf z})\,,\quad\bar{u}({\bf z})=\left(\partial_{\bar{z}}\eta({\bf z})\right)^{2}-\partial_{\bar{z}}^{2}\eta({\bf z})\,.

(3.8)

3.2
Integrals of motion

The modified sinh-Gordon equation (
3.1
) is related to the unmodified one by a simple change of variables.
In terms of the new coordinates
\mathbf{w}=(w,\bar{w})
,

\displaystyle\left(\begin{array}[c]{cc}dw&\\
d\bar{w}&\end{array}\!\!\!\!\!\!\right)=\left(\begin{array}[c]{cc}\sqrt{p(z,s)}dz&\\
\sqrt{p(\bar{z},s)}d\bar{z}&\end{array}\!\!\!\!\!\!\right),

the field

\displaystyle\hat{\eta}({\bf w})=\eta({\bf z}({\bf w}))-\frac{1}{4}\ln{p(z(w),s)p(\bar{z}(\bar{w}),s)}\,,

(3.13)

satisfies the sinh-Gordon equation

\partial_{w}\partial_{\bar{w}}\hat{\eta}-e^{2\hat{\eta}}+e^{-2\hat{\eta}}=0\,.

(3.14)

From a WKB-type analysis of (
3.7
)
[
74
,
75
]
, one can find the expressions for the integrals of motion (
IM
s)
\mathcal{J}_{2n-1}
and
\mathcal{\bar{J}}_{2n-1}
with
n\in\mathbb{N}_{>0}
. Explicitly:

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \mathcal{J}_{2n-1}&=\frac{1}{2(2n-1)\sin\left({\frac{\pi(2n-1)}{2\alpha}}\right)}\int\limits_{\Gamma_{1}}\left(dw\hat{P}_{2n}+d\bar{w}\hat{R}_{2n-2}\right)\,,\\
\bar{\mathcal{J}}_{2n-1}&=\frac{1}{2(2n-1)\sin\left({\frac{\pi(2n-1)}{2\alpha}}\right)}\int\limits_{\bar{\Gamma}_{1}}\left(d\bar{w}\hat{\bar{P}}_{2n}+dw\hat{\bar{R}}_{2n-2}\right)\,.\end{aligned}

(3.15)

The specification of the integration contours
\Gamma_{1}
and
\bar{\Gamma}_{1}
is of primary importance and will be discussed in a moment.
The integrands appearing in (
3.15
) are closed 1-forms, meaning that they satisfy a continuity equation

\partial_{\bar{w}}{\hat{P}_{2n}}=\partial_{w}{\hat{R}_{2n-2}}\,,\quad\partial_{w}{\hat{\bar{P}}_{2n}}=\partial_{\bar{w}}{\hat{\bar{R}}_{2n-2}}\,.

(3.16)

The expressions for
\hat{P}_{2n}
and
\hat{R}_{2n-2}
, in terms of
\hat{\eta}
, are known for generic
n
and can be found in
[
14
]
, however the following analysis will only involve
\hat{P}_{2}
and
\hat{R}_{0}
:

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\hat{P}_{2}=\frac{1}{2}\hat{u}\,,\quad\hat{R}_{0}=e^{-2\hat{\eta}}-1\,,\\
&\hat{\bar{P}}_{2}=\frac{1}{2}\hat{\bar{u}}\,,\quad\hat{\bar{R}}_{0}=e^{-2\hat{\eta}}-1\,.\end{aligned}

(3.17)

The potentials
\hat{u}
and
\hat{\bar{u}}
are defined as in (
3.8
) with
\eta
substituted for
\hat{\eta}
.

As a consequence of the continuity equation (
3.16
), these densities may be written in terms of the stress-energy tensor components
T_{2}
,
\bar{T}_{2}
and
\Theta_{0}
of the sinh-Gordon model in dimensionless coordinates:

\begin{split}&T_{2}(\mathbf{w})=\frac{1}{2}\hat{u}(\mathbf{w})=\hat{P}_{2}\,,\quad\bar{T}_{2}(\mathbf{w})=\frac{1}{2}\hat{\bar{u}}(\mathbf{w})=\hat{\bar{P}}_{2}\,,\\
&\Theta_{0}(\mathbf{w})=\hat{R}_{0}=\hat{\bar{R}}_{0}=e^{-2\hat{\eta}}-1\,.\end{split}

(3.18)

Finally, the total energy
E
and the total momentum

can be expressed as

E=\mathcal{E}+\bar{\mathcal{E}}\,,\quad P=\mathcal{E}-\bar{\mathcal{E}}\,,

(3.19)

where

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}=\frac{\mathcal{M}}{4}\int\limits_{\Gamma_{1}}\left(dw\,T_{2}(\mathbf{w})+d\bar{w}\,\Theta_{0}(\mathbf{w})\right)\,,

(3.20)

\displaystyle\bar{\mathcal{E}}=\frac{\mathcal{M}}{4}\int\limits_{\bar{\Gamma}_{1}}\left(dw\,\Theta_{0}(\mathbf{w})+d\bar{w}\,\bar{T}_{2}(\mathbf{w})\right)\,.

The dimensionful constant

3.4
The circumference of the cylinder

We shall now introduce the classical equivalent
\mathcal{M}\mathcal{R}
of the dimensionless quantity
mR
appearing in (

). The former is written in terms of simple integrals over
\Gamma_{2}
and
\bar{\Gamma}_{2}

\mathcal{M}\mathcal{R}=\mathcal{M}\mathcal{R}(s)=\frac{2}{\tan\left(\frac{\pi}{2\alpha}\right)}\int_{\bar{\Gamma}_{2}}dz\,\sqrt{p(z,s)}=\frac{2}{\tan\left(\frac{\pi}{2\alpha}\right)}\int_{\Gamma_{2}}d\bar{z}\,\sqrt{p(\bar{z},s)}\,,

(3.22)

where
\Gamma_{2}=c_{2}^{-}\cup\gamma_{2}\cup c_{2}^{+}
is represented in Figure
1
.
This object can be explicitly computed. First, let us consider the integral appearing in
[
14
]

\displaystyle\int_{\bar{\gamma}_{2}}dz\,\sqrt{p(z,s)}

\displaystyle=2\lim_{\rho\rightarrow+\infty}\int_{\rho}^{s}dt\,\sqrt{p(t,s)}

\displaystyle=2\lim_{\rho\rightarrow+\infty}\left(\int_{\rho}^{s}dt\,\left(\sqrt{p(t,s)}-t^{\alpha}\right)+\frac{s^{\alpha+1}}{\alpha+1}-\frac{\rho^{\alpha+1}}{\alpha+1}\right)

\displaystyle=\tan{\left(\frac{\pi}{2\alpha}\right)}\sqrt{\pi}s^{\alpha+1}\frac{\Gamma\left(1+\frac{1}{2\alpha}\right)}{\Gamma\left(\frac{3}{2}+\frac{1}{2\alpha}\right)}-2\lim_{\rho\rightarrow+\infty}\frac{\rho^{\alpha+1}}{\alpha+1},

(3.23)

where the factor two is due to the discontinuity on the square root type branch cut. This integral is obviously divergent. We choose to regularize it by altering the integration contour. In fact, we calculate the limiting behaviour

\lim_{\rho\rightarrow+\infty}\int_{\bar{c}^{+}_{2}(\rho)\,\cup\,\bar{c}^{-}_{2}(\rho)}dz\,\sqrt{p(z,s)}=\big(2-2\cos\left(\Omega(\alpha+1)\right)\big)\lim_{\rho\rightarrow+\infty}\frac{\rho^{\alpha+1}}{\alpha+1}.

(3.24)

Now, if we set
\Omega=\pi/(2\alpha+2)
, the above quantity exactly cancels the divergent term in (
3.23
) and we obtain the following finite expressions

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}\mathcal{R}(s)=2\sqrt{\pi}s^{\alpha+1}\frac{\Gamma\left(1+\frac{1}{2\alpha}\right)}{\Gamma\left(\frac{3}{2}+\frac{1}{2\alpha}\right)}\,,

(3.25)

establishing a match with the quantity defined in (
2.5
) on the quantum IM-side of the correspondence:
\mathcal{M}\mathcal{R}=mR
or, equivalently,
s=\mathbf{s}
.

This same result could be obtained by more standard regularization schemes, for example by subtracting the
\rho\rightarrow+\infty
divergent part appearing on the right-hand side of (
3.23
), or by replacing

\sqrt{p(z,s)}\rightarrow\left(p(z,s)\right)^{a}\,,

(3.26)

with
\operatorname{Re}(a)<-1/(2\alpha)
and analytically continuing the outcome to
a=1/2
. However, our contour type regularization is more natural, especially in view of the introduction of the
\mathrm{T}\overline{\mathrm{T}}
perturbation as a field dependent change of coordinates.

The same computation in the
\mathbf{w}
complex plane yields the following simple expression

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}\mathcal{R}\tan\left(\frac{\pi}{2\alpha}\right)=2\left(\bar{w}(A)-\bar{w}(A^{\prime})\right)=2\left(w(A^{\prime})-w(A)\right)\,.

(3.27)

According to the previous definitions (
3.20
) and to the above expression, the total energy and momentum associated to a given field configuration should depend on both
\mathcal{R}
and
\mathcal{M}
. However, an explicit calculation shows that the momentum is independent of
\mathcal{M}
:
P=P(\mathcal{R})
(see also the discussion in appendix
A
).

4
Adding the
\mathrm{T}\overline{\mathrm{T}}

At the classical level, the simplest way to introduce the
\mathrm{T}\overline{\mathrm{T}}
perturbation is through a dynamical change of coordinates that involves the components of the stress-energy tensor
[
60
]
.
Since we will repeatedly switch from one set of coordinates to the other, we take a moment to fix the notations. The set of coordinates
\bf{w}
will always be that in which the EoMs (
3.14
) and, consequently the current densities, look like the unperturbed ones.
In these coordinates, the effect of the
\mathrm{T}\overline{\mathrm{T}}
perturbation is implicitly hidden in the integration contour
\Gamma_{1}^{(t)}
, which is (equivalent to) the image of
\Gamma_{1}
, under a dynamical change of coordinates
[
60
]
.
On the other hand, in the set of coordinates
{\bf x}
, the effect of the
\mathrm{T}\overline{\mathrm{T}}
perturbation will be visible as a modification of both the EoMs and the current densities.
One of the main results of
[
60
]
is that these two alternative descriptions of a
\mathrm{T}\overline{\mathrm{T}}
-perturbed theory are in fact equivalent. This will allow us to prove that the energy of the
\mathrm{T}\overline{\mathrm{T}}
-deformed, classical mShG model satisfies the Burgers’ equation (
2.10
), thus proving the validity of the ODE/IM correspondence for
\mathrm{T}\overline{\mathrm{T}}
-deformed integrable quantum field theories.

4.1
The dynamical change of coordinates

The dynamical change of space-time coordinates
[
60
]
is defined as the following differential map:

\left(\begin{array}[c]{cc}dw&\\
d\bar{w}&\end{array}\!\!\!\!\!\!\right)=J^{T}\left(\begin{array}[c]{cc}dx&\\
d\bar{x}&\end{array}\!\!\!\!\!\!\right)\,.

(4.1)

The Jacobian and its inverse are

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &J^{T}(\mathbf{x})=\frac{1}{\Delta(\mathbf{w}(\mathbf{x}),0)}\left(\begin{array}[c]{cc}1+2t\Theta_{0}(\mathbf{w}(\mathbf{x}),0)&-2t\bar{T}_{2}(\mathbf{w}(\mathbf{x}),0)\\
-2tT_{2}(\mathbf{w}(\mathbf{x}),0)&1+2t\Theta_{0}(\mathbf{w}(\mathbf{x}),0)\end{array}\right)\,,\\
&{\left(J^{T}\right)}^{-1}(\mathbf{w},0)=\left(\begin{array}[c]{cc}1+2t\Theta_{0}(\mathbf{w},0)&2t\bar{T}_{2}(\mathbf{w},0)\\
2tT_{2}(\mathbf{w},0)&1+2t\Theta_{0}(\mathbf{w},0)\end{array}\right)\,,\end{aligned}

(4.2)

where
t
is a dimensionless coupling trivially related to
\tau
in (
2.6
), as we will see shortly, while

\Delta(\mathbf{w},0)=(1+2t\Theta_{0}(\mathbf{w}),0)^{2}-4t^{2}T_{2}(\mathbf{w},0)\bar{T}_{2}(\mathbf{w},0)\,.

(4.3)

The explicit form of (
4.1
) is

\displaystyle dw=\frac{1}{\Delta(\mathbf{w}(\mathbf{x}),0)}\left(dx+2t\Theta_{0}(\mathbf{w}(\mathbf{x}),0)dx-2t\bar{T}_{2}(\mathbf{w}(\mathbf{x}),0)d\bar{x}\right)\,,

(4.4)

\displaystyle d\bar{w}=\frac{1}{\Delta(\mathbf{w}(\mathbf{x}),0)}\left(d\bar{x}+2t\Theta_{0}(\mathbf{w}(\mathbf{x}),0)d\bar{x}-2tT_{2}(\mathbf{w}(\mathbf{x}),0)dx\right)\,.

Now, after a
\mathrm{T}\overline{\mathrm{T}}
deformation, all the quantities introduced in the previous sections will depend on the two independent parameters
\mathcal{R}
and
t
.
So for a generic value of
t
, and in agreement with the previous discussions, we can write:

\displaystyle

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(\mathcal{R},t)=\frac{\mathcal{M}}{4}\int\limits_{\Gamma_{1}^{(t)}}\left(dw\,T_{2}(\mathbf{w},0)+d\bar{w}\,\Theta_{0}(\mathbf{w},0)\right)\,,

(4.5)

\displaystyle\bar{\mathcal{E}}(\mathcal{R},t)=\frac{\mathcal{M}}{4}\int\limits_{\bar{\Gamma}_{1}^{(t)}}\left(dw\,\Theta_{0}(\mathbf{w},0)+d\bar{w}\,\bar{T}_{2}(\mathbf{w},0)\right)\,,

where, according to (
3.19
):

E(\mathcal{R},t)=\mathcal{E}(\mathcal{R},t)+\bar{\mathcal{E}}(\mathcal{R},t)\,,\quad P(\mathcal{R},t)=\mathcal{E}(\mathcal{R},t)-\bar{\mathcal{E}}(\mathcal{R},t)\,.

(4.6)

Let us stress again that in equations (
4.5
) it is the deformation of the integration contours which drives the evolution in
t
of the conserved charges.
Notice also how the map (
4.1
) implies that the contours
\bar{\Gamma}_{1}^{(t)}
and
\Gamma_{1}^{(t)*}
are not necessarily equivalent, since in general

(T_{2}(\mathbf{w}),0)^{*}-\bar{T}_{2}(\mathbf{w},0)\neq 0\,.

(4.7)

We shall now shift to the alternative point of view, from which the current densities evolve in
t
while the contours remain untouched. Using the invariance of
1
-forms under coordinate transformations, we can write

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &T_{2}(\mathbf{w},0)\,dw+\Theta_{0}(\mathbf{w},0)\,d\bar{w}=T_{2}(\mathbf{x},t)\,dx+\Theta_{0}(\mathbf{x},t)\,d\bar{x}\,,\\
&\Theta_{0}(\mathbf{w},0)\,dw+\bar{T}_{2}(\mathbf{w},0)\,d\bar{w}=\Theta_{0}(\mathbf{x},t)\,dx+\bar{T}_{2}(\mathbf{x},t)\,d\bar{x}\,,\end{aligned}

(4.8)

so that

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(\mathcal{R},t)=\frac{\mathcal{M}}{4}\int\limits_{\Gamma_{1}}\left(dx\,T_{2}(\mathbf{x},t)+d\bar{x}\,\Theta_{0}(\mathbf{x},t)\right)\,,

(4.9)

\displaystyle\bar{\mathcal{E}}(\mathcal{R},t)=\frac{\mathcal{M}}{4}\int\limits_{\bar{\Gamma}_{1}}\left(dx\,\Theta_{0}(\mathbf{x},t)+d\bar{x}\,\bar{T}_{2}(\mathbf{x},t)\right)\,.

Using the explicit form of the map (
4.4
) we obtain

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &T_{2}(\mathbf{x},t)=\frac{T_{2}(\mathbf{w}(\mathbf{x}),0)}{\Delta(\mathbf{w}(\mathbf{x}),0)}\,,\quad\bar{T}_{2}(\mathbf{x},t)=\frac{\bar{T}_{2}(\mathbf{w}(\mathbf{x}),0)}{\Delta(\mathbf{w}(\mathbf{x}),0)}\,,\\
&\Theta_{0}(\mathbf{x},t)=\frac{\Theta_{0}(\mathbf{w}(\mathbf{x}),0)+2t(\Theta_{0}(\mathbf{w}(\mathbf{x}),0)^{2}-T_{2}(\mathbf{w}(\mathbf{x}),0)\bar{T}_{2}(\mathbf{w}(\mathbf{x}),0))}{{\Delta(\mathbf{w}(\mathbf{x}),0})}\,,\end{aligned}

(4.10)

from which we conclude that

\displaystyle dw=dx-2t\left(\Theta_{0}(\mathbf{x},t)\,dx+\bar{T}_{2}(\mathbf{x},t)\,d\bar{x}\right)\,,

(4.11)

\displaystyle d\bar{w}=d\bar{x}-2t\left(\Theta_{0}(\mathbf{x},t)\,d\bar{x}+T_{2}(\mathbf{x},t)\,dx\right)\,.

The inverse map is

\displaystyle dx=dw+2t\left(\Theta_{0}(\mathbf{w},0)\,dw+\bar{T}_{2}(\mathbf{w},0)\,d\bar{w}\right)\,,

(4.12)

\displaystyle d\bar{x}=d\bar{w}+2t\left(\Theta_{0}(\mathbf{w},0)\,d\bar{w}+T_{2}(\mathbf{w},0)\,dw\right)\,.

Next, we are going to consider the effect of the

\mathcal{M}\mathcal{R}_{0}\,e^{-\uptheta_{0}}=\frac{2}{\tan{\left(\frac{\pi}{2\alpha}\right)}}\,\int\limits_{\bar{\Gamma}_{1}^{(t)}}dw\,,\quad\mathcal{M}\mathcal{R}_{0}\,e^{+\uptheta_{0}}=\frac{2}{\tan{\left(\frac{\pi}{2\alpha}\right)}}\,\int\limits_{\Gamma_{1}^{(t)}}d\bar{w}\,,

(4.13)

where
\uptheta_{0}(t=0)=0
and
\mathcal{R}_{0}(t=0)=\mathcal{R}

\mathrm{T}\overline{\mathrm{T}}

Using the map (

\displaystyle\int\limits_{\bar{\Gamma}_{1}^{(t)}}dw

where relations (

\int\limits_{\Gamma_{1}^{(t)}}d\bar{w}=\frac{1}{2}\tan{\left(\frac{\pi}{2\alpha}\right)}\mathcal{M}\mathcal{R}-\frac{8}{\mathcal{M}}\,t\mathcal{E}(\mathcal{R},t)\,.

(4.15)

Setting

\uptau=-\frac{8}{\mathcal{M}^{2}\tan{(\frac{\pi}{2\alpha})}}t\,,

(4.16)

and using relations (
4.6

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\mathcal{R}_{0}\,e^{-\uptheta_{0}}=\mathcal{R}+\uptau\left(E(\mathcal{R},\tau)-P(\mathcal{R},\tau)\right)\\

(4.17)

The previous analysis and the resulting equations (

plus a Minkowski rotation

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}\rightarrow\mathcal{R}_{0}\,e^{+\uptheta_{0}},

with rapidity

\displaystyle\uptheta_{0}=\frac{1}{2}\log\left(\frac{\mathcal{R}+\uptau\left(E(\mathcal{R},\tau)+P(\mathcal{R},\tau)\right)}{\mathcal{R}+\uptau\left(E(\mathcal{R},\tau)-P(\mathcal{R},\tau)\right)}\right)\,,

(4.19)

and

\mathcal{R}_{0}^{2}=\left(\mathcal{R}+\uptau E(\mathcal{R},\uptau)\right)^{2}-\uptau^{2}P^{2}(\mathcal{R},\uptau)\,,

(4.20)

as pictorially represented in Figure

Therefore, the net effect of the

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}(s)\rightarrow\left(\mathcal{R}^{+},\mathcal{R}^{-}\right)=\left(\mathcal{R}(s^{-}),\mathcal{R}(s^{+})\right)=\left(\mathcal{R}_{0}\,e^{-\uptheta_{0}},\mathcal{R}_{0}\,e^{+\uptheta_{0}}\right)\,.

4.2
The Burgers’ equation

As a final step in our analysis, we shall derive the Burgers’ equation involving only classical physical quantities.

From equations (
4.17
) and (
4.25
), we have

\sinh{\uptheta_{0}}=\tau\frac{P(\mathcal{R},\uptau)}{\mathcal{R}_{0}}=\tau\frac{P(\mathcal{R}_{0},0)}{\mathcal{R}}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\mathcal{R}P(\mathcal{R},\uptau)=\mathcal{R}_{0}P(\mathcal{R}_{0},0)\,,

(4.26)

implying that
P(\mathcal{R},\uptau)=P(\mathcal{R})\propto\mathcal{R}^{-1}
. Alternatively, the exactly-quantised expression

P(\mathcal{R})=\frac{2\pi p}{\mathcal{R}}\,,\quad p\in\mathbb{Z}\,,

(4.27)

can be obtained through the WKB type analysis reported in appendix
A
.
Putting (
4.17
) and (
4.25
) together, one get

\displaystyle\begin{cases}e^{+\uptheta_{0}}\,\left(E(\mathcal{R},\uptau)-P(\mathcal{R},\tau)\right)=E(\mathcal{R}_{0},0)-P(\mathcal{R}_{0},0)\\
e^{-\uptheta_{0}}\,\left(E(\mathcal{R},\tau)+P(\mathcal{R},\tau)\right)=E(\mathcal{R}_{0},0)+P(\mathcal{R}_{0},0)\\
\end{cases}\,,

(4.28)

that is

E^{2}(\mathcal{R},\tau)-P^{2}(\mathcal{R},\tau)=E^{2}(\mathcal{R}_{0},0)-P^{2}(\mathcal{R}_{0},0)\,.

(4.29)

Moreover, (
4.17
) and (
4.25
) imply the additional constraint
[
60
]

\frac{\partial}{\partial\uptau}\mathcal{R}=-E(\mathcal{R},\uptau)\,,

(4.30)

at fixed
\mathcal{R}_{0}
.
This result together with (
4.29
) is the solution, in implicit form, of the Burgers’ equation

[
58
]
:

\frac{\partial}{\partial\uptau}E(\mathcal{R},\uptau)=E(\mathcal{R},\uptau)\frac{\partial}{\partial\mathcal{R}}E(\mathcal{R},\uptau)+\frac{P(\mathcal{R})^{2}}{\mathcal{R}}\,.

(4.31)

In conclusion, assuming the validity of the ODE/IM at
\tau=0
, i.e.

E(R,0)_{\textit{quantum}}=E(R,0)_{\textit{classical}}\,,\quad\quad P(R)_{\textit{quantum}}=P(R)_{\textit{classical}}\,,

(4.32)

the fact that the quantum and the classical
\mathrm{T}\overline{\mathrm{T}}
-evolution equations coincide, implies

\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}\boxed{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\color[rgb]{1,1,1}\boxed{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}E(R,\tau)_{\textit{quantum}}=E(R,\tau)_{\textit{classical}}}}\,.

(4.33)

This equality constitutes the key result of the present work.

5
Conclusions

The main result of this paper is the extension to
\mathrm{T}\overline{\mathrm{T}}
perturbed models of the classical/quantum duality associated with the off-critical variant of the ODE/IM correspondence. Our work links properties of the
\mathrm{T}\overline{\mathrm{T}}
perturbation on quantum and classical levels that previously stood on separate grounds.
The analysis highlights a deep connection between the classical coordinate transformation, its invertibility, and the factorization property of the operator
\mathrm{T}\overline{\mathrm{T}}
, which plays an essential role at the quantum level.
In this work, attention was restricted to the modified sinh-Gordon case of
[
14
]
, however, our result has a much wider validity. First, we notice that the explicit form of the mShG potential (
3.2
) does not appear anywhere in section
4
– except for a few constants. Consequently, the validity of the result (
4.33
) can be immediately extended to ODE/IM correspondence based on more general potentials,
e.g.
the polynomial ones used in the context of AdS/CFT correspondence for the computation of polygonal Wilson loops
[
76
,
77
]
. Similarly, it is not difficult to see how the arguments of section
4
remain valid for
\mathrm{T}\overline{\mathrm{T}}
deformations of other known instances of the ODE/IM correspondence
[
19
,
78
,
18
,
21
,
22
,
79
]
and are also easily adaptable to the study of the Lorentz breaking deformations introduced in
[
60
]
.

We wish to stress that the Burgers’ equation (
4.31
) is actually a general property of
\mathrm{T}\overline{\mathrm{T}}
-deformed classical field theories. In fact, the argument used in section
4
teaches us that everything we need to prove (
4.31
) are the differential maps (
4.11
,
4.12
) and an appropriate choice of Cauchy cycles for both the
\mathbf{w}
and the
\mathbf{x}
coordinates,
over which we integrate the closed
1
-forms (
4.8

4.11
,
4.12
) over these cycles
we arrive at the equivalent of (
4.17
,
4.25
), from which the Burgers’ equation (
4.31
) immediately follows.

Another significant point emerging from our analysis is the link between the dynamical change of coordinates and the factorization property of
\mathrm{T}\overline{\mathrm{T}}

[
32
]
. This fact suggests the existence of coordinate transformations also for deformations driven by operators built using higher spin currents. However, preliminary inspections indicate that spacetime maps exist, besides
\mathrm{T}\overline{\mathrm{T}}
, only for
\mathrm{J}\overline{\mathrm{T}}

[
80
,

and the

The additional dimensions would correspond to the higher times associated to the commuting Hamiltonian flows generated by the higher conserved charges. A further, natural follow-up would be the extension of the ODE/IM to the recently discovered
\sqrt{\mbox{$\mathrm{T}\overline{\mathrm{T}}$}}
deformations
[

,
82
,

,

Finally, it would be nice to use the current framework to understand better the geometric meaning of the Hagedorn singularity appearing in the deformed quantum spectrum and explore at a deeper level the consequences of the

Appendix A
The conformal field theory limit

At the classical level, the ultraviolet limit yielding the conformal field theory behaviour corresponds to the following (left-moving) scaling limit:

z=y\,e^{-\frac{\vartheta}{1+\alpha}}\,,\quad\vartheta\rightarrow+\infty\,,\quad|z|\sim s\rightarrow 0^{+}\,,

(A.1)

with

y=e^{\frac{\vartheta}{1+\alpha}}z\,\quad{\bf E}=e^{2\alpha\vartheta/(1+\alpha)}s^{2\alpha}\,,

(A.2)

kept finite. In this limit, the first equation in (
3.7
) reduced to the Schrödinger equation of
[
1
]
, with the addition of a finite number of regular singularities
\left\{y_{a}\right\}

[
11
,
12
]

\displaystyle\left(\frac{d^{2}}{dy^{2}}-V\left(y,\{y_{a}\}_{a=1}^{L}\right)-y^{2\alpha}+{\bf E}\right)\psi_{\text{CFT}}(y)=0\,.

(A.3)

Here
V
is the so-called ‘monster potential’
[
11
]
,

V\left(y,\{y_{a}\}_{a=1}^{L}\right)=\frac{l(l+1)}{y}+2\frac{d^{2}}{dy^{2}}\sum_{a=1}^{L}\log\left(y^{2\alpha+2}-y_{a}\right)\,,

(A.4)

and the additional singularities are defined on the cone
y_{a}\in C_{\pi/\alpha}\setminus[{\bf E}^{1/(2\alpha)},+\infty)
, away from the apex:
y_{a}\neq 0
.

In the right-moving sector, the opposite scaling limit

\bar{z}=y\,e^{-\frac{\vartheta}{1+\alpha}}\,,\quad\vartheta\rightarrow-\infty\,,\quad|\bar{z}|\sim s\rightarrow 0^{+},

(A.5)

should be implemented. In this sector, a different number of singularities,
\bar{L}
, will mark the monster potential.

A.1
WKB analysis and the monster potential

A naive, straightforward WKB analysis of (
A.3
) runs into problems, due to the presence of two competing scales
E
and
\alpha
. A more refined analysis involves the following change of variables
[
90
]
:

y={\bf E}^{\frac{1}{2\alpha}}\hat{y}^{\frac{1}{2l+1}}\,,\quad\psi_{\text{CFT}}(y)={\bf E}^{-\frac{l}{2\alpha}}\hat{y}^{-\frac{l}{2l+1}}\hat{\psi}_{\text{CFT}}(\hat{y})\,.

(A.6)

In this new set of coordinates, equation (
A.3
) reads

\left(-\epsilon^{2}\frac{d^{2}}{d\hat{y}^{2}}+q(\hat{y})+\epsilon^{2}\xi_{L}(\hat{y},\epsilon)\right)\hat{\psi}_{\text{CFT}}(\hat{y})=0\,,

(A.7)

with

\displaystyle q(\hat{y})=\frac{1}{4\lambda^{2}}\hat{y}^{\frac{1}{\lambda}}\left(\hat{y}^{\frac{\alpha}{\lambda}}-1\right)\,,

(A.8)

The last term in the parenthesis, in equation (
A.7
), is

\displaystyle\xi_{L}(\hat{y},\epsilon)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{\lambda}\frac{d^{2}}{d\hat{y}^{2}}\sum_{a=1}^{L}\log\left(\hat{y}^{\frac{\alpha+1}{\lambda}}-\epsilon^{2}y_{a}\right)

\displaystyle+\frac{2\lambda-1}{\lambda^{3}}(\alpha+1)^{2}\hat{y}^{\frac{\alpha+1}{\lambda}-2}\sum_{a=1}^{L}\frac{\epsilon^{2}y_{a}}{\left(\hat{y}^{\frac{\alpha+1}{\lambda}}-\epsilon^{2}y_{a}\right)^{2}}\,,

(A.9)

with

\lambda=l+\frac{1}{2}\,,\quad\epsilon={\bf E}^{-\frac{\alpha+1}{2\alpha}}\,.

(A.10)

Now the WKB expansion of
\hat{\psi}_{\text{CFT}}(\hat{y})
is naturally organized by the single parameter
\epsilon
, which combines the two scales
E
and
\alpha

\hat{\psi}_{\text{CFT}}(\hat{y})=\text{exp}\left(\frac{1}{\epsilon}\sum_{b\geq 0}\epsilon^{b}S_{b}(\hat{y})\right)\,,

(A.11)

while

\xi_{L}(\hat{y},\epsilon)=\xi_{L}(\hat{y},0)+\sum_{c\geq 1}\epsilon^{c}\xi_{c}^{(L)}(\hat{x})\,.

(A.12)

The first few terms of the solution are easily computed by recursion,

\displaystyle S_{0}^{\prime}(\hat{y})=-\sqrt{q(\hat{y})}\,,\quad S_{1}^{\prime}(\hat{y})=-\frac{q^{\prime}(\hat{y})}{4q(\hat{y})}\,,

(A.13)

\displaystyle{S_{2}^{(L)}}^{\prime}(\hat{y})=-\frac{1}{48}\left(\frac{q^{\prime\prime}(\hat{y})}{q(\hat{y})^{\frac{3}{2}}}+5\frac{d}{d\hat{y}}\left(\frac{q^{\prime\prime}(\hat{y})}{q(\hat{y})^{\frac{3}{2}}}\right)\right)-\frac{\xi_{L}(\hat{y},0)}{2\sqrt{q(\hat{y})}}\,.

(A.14)

From
S_{2}^{(L)}(\hat{y})
we can extract the first
IM

[
88
]
as

\mathcal{E}=\frac{M}{4}\int_{\gamma_{\text{WKB}}}d\hat{y}\,{S_{2}^{(L)}}^{\prime}(\hat{y})\,.

(A.15)

Here the WKB integration contour
\gamma_{\text{WKB}}
starts from
+\infty
just above the real axis, encircles the turning point
\hat{y}=1
and goes back to
+\infty
, just below the real axis. The mass
M
sets the energy scale. Similarly, for the right-moving sector

\bar{\mathcal{E}}=\frac{M}{4}\int_{\gamma_{\text{WKB}}}d\hat{y}\,{S_{2}^{(\bar{L})}}^{\prime}(\hat{y})\,.

(A.16)

Now, we can find the exact expression for the total momentum:

\displaystyle P

\displaystyle=\mathcal{E}-\bar{\mathcal{E}}=-\frac{M}{4}\int_{\gamma_{\text{WKB}}}d\hat{y}\,\frac{\xi_{L}(\hat{y},0)}{2\sqrt{q(\hat{y})}}+\frac{M}{4}\int_{\gamma_{\text{WKB}}}d\hat{y}\,\frac{\xi_{\bar{L}}(\hat{y},0)}{2\sqrt{q(\hat{y})}}

\displaystyle=\frac{M}{\epsilon}L(\alpha+1)\,\int_{\mathbf{E}^{\frac{1}{2\alpha}}}^{+\infty}\frac{dy}{y^{2}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{y^{2\alpha}-\mathbf{E}}}-\frac{M}{\epsilon}\bar{L}(\alpha+1)\,\int_{\mathbf{E}^{\frac{1}{2\alpha}}}^{+\infty}\frac{dy}{y^{2}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{y^{2\alpha}-\mathbf{E}}}

\displaystyle=\frac{2\pi(L-\bar{L})}{\mathcal{R}}\,,

(A.17)

where, in the last equality in (
A.17
), we used the explicit form of
\mathcal{R}

\mathcal{R}=\frac{2\sqrt{\pi}}{M}\frac{\Gamma\left(1+\frac{1}{2\alpha}\right)}{\Gamma\left(\frac{3}{2}+\frac{1}{2\alpha}\right)}\,.

(A.18)

Therefore, we arrived at the quantization rule for the total momentum

P=\frac{2\pi\,p}{\mathcal{R}}\,,\quad p\in\mathbb{Z}\,,

(A.19)

Similarly we can compute the total energy, arriving at the following expression

E=\mathcal{E}+\mathcal{\bar{E}}=-\frac{\pi}{6\mathcal{R}}c_{\text{eff}}\,,

(A.20)

where
c_{\text{eff}}
is the effective central charge:

c_{\text{eff}}=1-\frac{6(l+\textstyle{\frac{1}{2}})^{2}}{\alpha+1}-12(L+\bar{L})\,.

(A.21)
