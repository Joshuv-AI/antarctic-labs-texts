Abstract
. We formulate the Asymptotic Length-Saturation Conjecture on the length sets of closed geodesics on hyperbolic manifolds whose fundamental groups are subarithmetic, that is, contained in an arithmetic group. We prove the first instance of the conjecture for punctured, Zariski dense covers of the modular surface.



1.
Introduction

By the length spectrum of a hyperbolic manifold
M
, we mean the set of lengths of closed geodesics on
M
, with multiplicity. As is well-known, closed geodesics on
M
correspond to hyperbolic conjugacy classes of its fundamental group

\Gamma=\pi_{1}(M)<\operatorname{Isom}(\mathbb{H}^{n})\cong PO(n,1),

and lengths of the former are a simple function of traces
of the latter. It is also classical to study the length set, that is, the set of lengths of closed geodesics, now counted
without
multiplicity; again, this is intimately related to the set
\mathcal{T}(\Gamma)
of traces (without multiplicity) of hyperbolic conjugacy classes of
\Gamma
. In this paper, we initiate a detailed study of the latter for (sub)arithmetic groups, from the viewpoint of local-global theory.

To motivate our main results, we begin with a few illustrative examples.

Example 1:
Consider the Hecke
(2,3,\infty)
triangle group, or rather, its orientation preserving double cover, the modular group
\Gamma=\operatorname{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
. The trace set
\mathcal{T}(\Gamma)
of the latter is elementarily seen to be all of
\mathbb{Z}
, as for any desired integer
t
, one simply expresses
t
as
t=a+d
and factors
bc=ad-1
to make a matrix
\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)\in\Gamma
having trace
t
. This is because
\Gamma
is an arithmetic (or better yet, congruence) group, and hence
any
solution to
ad-bc=1
over
\mathbb{Z}
gives an element.
We can compare these facts with the count for the number of points in
\Gamma
in an archimedean ball
B_{N}
of radius
N
: as with any lattice in
\operatorname{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
, we crudely have

\#\Gamma\cap B_{N}\ \sim\ cN^{2},

(1.1)

for some constant
c>0
, which means that the average number of times that a particular integer
t\asymp N
arises as a trace of a matrix in
B_{N}
is of order
N^{2}/N=N
.
But this does not take into account the fact that trace is a conjugacy class invariant.
For
t>2
, let
H(t)
denote the number of conjugacy classes of elements in
\Gamma
with trace
t
. As is well-known (see Chowla-Cowles-Cowles
[
CCC80
]
, though this was surely known to Selberg and likely before),
H(t)
is equal to
h(t^{2}-4)
, where
h(D)
is the classical class number, that is, the number of equivalence classes of binary quadratic forms of discriminant
D
(not necessarily primitive).
By the Prime Geodesic Theorem we have (compare to (
1.1
)):

\sum_{t<N}H(t)\ \sim\ {N^{2}\over 2\log N},

(1.2)

and so a “typical” value of
H(t)
(for
t\asymp N
) is more like
N/\log N
, rather than
N
. (Note that the fundamental unit
\epsilon_{D}
for discriminant
D=t^{2}-4
is about as small as possible,
\epsilon_{D}=(t+\sqrt{D})/2
, and hence this class number is as large as possible, of size about
\sqrt{D}
). The discrepancy in counting matrices versus counting conjugacy classes makes sense, as the archimedean size of elements under conjugation grow exponentially (the stabilizer group of a conjugacy class is a discrete subgroup of some
\operatorname{SO}(1,1)
), so
(\log N)
-many matrices of size
N
are grouped together. This is a minor issue here, but will play a major role in the next example.

Example 2:
Now consider the Hecke
(2,5,\infty)
triangle group, or rather its double cover, the group
\Gamma
generated by

\Gamma=\left\langle\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}0&1\\
-1&0\end{array}\right),\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}1&\phi\\
0&1\end{array}\right)\right\rangle,

where
\phi=(1+\sqrt{5})/2
is the golden mean. (Recall that
\operatorname{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
has similar generators, except with
\phi
replaced by
1
.) The group
\Gamma
is nonarithmetic, but it is
subarithmetic
, being a subgroup of the arithmetic group
\widetilde{\Gamma}:=\operatorname{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z}[\phi])
. The latter does not act discretely on
\mathbb{H}
, but
is
a lattice in
\operatorname{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})\times\operatorname{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
, where it acts by the Galois conjugate in the second factor. (The fact that
\Gamma
is a lattice in the first factor, while the second factor is non-compact, is one way to see its nonarithmeticity.) In fact
\Gamma
is a
thin
group (see, e.g.
[
Kon14
]
), as the set of
\mathbb{Z}
-points of its Zariski closure is exactly
\widetilde{\Gamma}
, and it has infinite index in the latter.
The set of traces of
\widetilde{\Gamma}
is again elementary to determine; it is the full order
\mathcal{O}=\mathbb{Z}[\phi]
, for the same reason as in Example 1. But now we may ask, which
t\in\mathcal{O}
are also traces of
\Gamma
?

The asymptotic count (
1.1
) of
matrices
in a ball is still of order
N^{2}
. But
now, since
\mathcal{O}\cong\mathbb{Z}\oplus\phi\mathbb{Z}
is a quadratic ring, the number of
t\in\mathcal{O}
with norm at most
N
is also roughly
N^{2}
. Therefore the average number of matrices in
B_{N}
having a given trace
t\in\mathcal{O}
is a positive constant.

But what happens when we group by conjugacy classes? (In Example 1, this caused the average count to drop by a factor of
\log N
, but here we don’t have this factor to spare!)
Let
H_{\Gamma}(t)
denote the number of conjugacy classes of
\Gamma
having trace
t\in\mathcal{O}
. As
\Gamma
is a lattice in
\operatorname{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
, we still have the Prime Geodesic Theorem (see (
1.2
)), that

\sum_{\mathbb{N}(t)<N}H_{\Gamma}(t)\ \sim\ {N^{2}\over 2\log N},

(1.3)

where
\mathbb{N}:\mathcal{O}\to\mathbb{Z}
is the algebraic norm. Therefore there can’t be more than
O(N^{2}/\log N)
elements
t\in\mathcal{O}
which actually arise as traces in
\Gamma\cap B_{N}
, and thus the density of those that do arise is zero!
While we can’t say much about the class number
H_{\Gamma}(t)
, in every conjugacy class that does arise, there should be about
\log N
matrices of size
N
, as before. So when counting matrices, even though the “average” multiplicity is bounded, what’s really going on is that
100\%
of the time, the multiplicity is exactly zero, and very rarely there are somewhat large (of size at least
\log N
) multiplicities. See also
recent work of McMullen
[
McM20
]
in this direction. We remark that the number of elements of
\widetilde{\Gamma}
in a ball
B_{N}
is roughly
N^{4}
, and
H_{\widetilde{\Gamma}}(t)
is roughly of order
t^{2}
.

Example 3:
For our last example, consider the Hecke
(2,7,\infty)
triangle group, or rather its double cover, the group
\Gamma
generated by

\Gamma=\left\langle\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}0&1\\
-1&0\end{array}\right),\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}1&\eta\\
0&1\end{array}\right)\right\rangle,

where
\eta=\cos(2\pi/7)
. The ring
\mathcal{O}=\mathbb{Z}[\eta]
is cubic, and so the number of matrices of size
N
in
\Gamma
is of order
N^{2}
, while the number of possible values of the trace up to
N
is
N^{3}
, and hence it is clear that very few numbers in
\mathcal{O}
can occur as traces.

Returning to the general setting, in light of these examples, to be able to say anything about the length set of a general manifold
M
, we need some conditions. First we should assume that its fundamental group
\Gamma
it is subarithmetic (or else the traces could be completely random numbers), and let
\mathcal{O}
be the ring of integers of the trace field (that is, the field generated by the traces) of
\Gamma
.

Obstruction 1:

Let
\alpha>0
be the “growth exponent” of
\Gamma
, in the sense that

\#\Gamma\cap B_{N}=N^{\alpha+o(1)}.

(When
\Gamma
is a geometrically finite group and
\delta
is the Hausdorff dimension of its limit set, then
\alpha=2\delta
.)
As in Example 3 (and Example 2), to be able to study the length set, we require that
\alpha
exceeds the rank of
\mathcal{O}
.
One can think of this as an “archimedean local obstruction.”

Obstruction 2:

There are also potentially other local obstructions. Already in the case of a classical congruence group
\Gamma(q):=\ker(\operatorname{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})\to\operatorname{SL}_{2}(q))
, only the numbers that are
2(\operatorname{mod}q)
can arise as traces in
\Gamma(q)
.
We say that
t\in\mathcal{O}
is
admissible
if, for every ideal
\mathcal{I}\subset\mathcal{O}
,
t\in\mathcal{T}(\Gamma)\operatorname{mod}\mathcal{I}
.
(We remark that the set of admissibles is “easy” to determine in practice, as follows from Strong Approximation for Zariski dense groups.)

Obstruction 3:

There is one final archimedean local obstruction. Given any manifold, we can take a cover that destroys the systole; that is, what was the shortest closed geodesic need not remain closed under a cover, making the shortest length (that is, smallest trace) of such a moving target. So we should allow for finitely many “small” values of
\mathcal{O}
to not arise as traces.

We may now formulate our main conjectures.

Definition 1.4
.

With notation as above, we say that a subarithmetic hyperbolic group
\Gamma

length-saturates
if: every admissible
t\in\mathcal{O}
with sufficiently large norm arises in the trace set of
\Gamma
.

Definition 1.5
.

We say that
\Gamma

asymptotically length-saturates
if

{\#\mathcal{T}(\Gamma)\cap B_{N}\over\#\{t\in\mathcal{O}\cap B_{N}\ :\ t\text{ is admissible\lx@text@rbrace}}\ \to\ 1,

(1.6)

as
N\to\infty
.

Thus the modular group length-saturates, as in Example 1 (see also work of Marklof
[
Mar96
]
studying distinct length sets of arithmetic 3-folds), while the Hecke
(2,5,\infty)
group does not even asymptotically length-saturate (it fails Obstruction 1).

Conjecture 1.7

(Asymptotic Length-Saturation)
.

Let
\Gamma
be a subarithmetic hyperbolic group, with growth exponent
\alpha
exceeding the rank of
\mathcal{O}
. Then
\Gamma
asymptotically length-saturates.

The stronger statement that with the same assumptions,
\Gamma
length-saturates is false. Indeed, already for certain cocompact arithmetic 2-folds corresponding to the norm-one elements of a quaternionic division algebra, the trace equation can cut out a ternary indefinite inhomogeneous quadric, which can exhibit infinitely many Brauer-Manin-type obstructions.

In this paper, we make the first progress towards
Conjecture
1.7
, by proving asymptotic length-saturation for punctured, Zariski-dense, submodular groups.

Theorem 1.8
.

Any punctured, Zariski dense cover of the modular surface is asymptotically length-saturating. In fact, it is effectively so, in that the right hand side of (
1.6
) is
1+O(N^{-\varepsilon})
for some
\varepsilon>0
.

Note that we do not require geometric finiteness of
\Gamma
, as we can pass, if needed, to a finitely generated but still Zariski dense and punctured subgroup of
\Gamma
.

Remark 1.9
.

The question of length-saturation is closely related to the Local-Global Conjecture for Apollonian packings, Zaremba’s Conjecture, and McMullen’s Classical Arithmetic Chaos Conjecture (see, e.g.
[
Kon13
,
Kon16
,
McM12
]
for discussions of these). Each of these problems amounts to understand the image of a linear form (which in the setting of this paper is the trace) of a Zariski dense subgroup (or sub-semigroup). In each of the previous cases, the expected multiplicity in a ball of size
N
was some fixed positive power of
N
.

Note that in our setting here, there is no restriction on the growth exponent
\alpha
of
\Gamma
; indeed, the Hausdorff dimension
\delta
of the limit set of
\Gamma
, which can be as small as
\delta>1/2
(due to the puncture, see
[
Bea65
]
).
Counting with multiplicity, we have that:

\#\{\gamma\in\Gamma\cap B_{N}\ :\ \operatorname{tr}(\gamma)<N\}\ =\ N^{2\delta+o(1)}.\qquad(N\to\infty)

So the multiplicity of a typical trace
t\asymp N
in the trace set
t\in\mathcal{T}(\Gamma)
may be extremely small,

\#\{\gamma\in\Gamma\cap B_{N}\ :\ \operatorname{tr}(\gamma)=t\}\ \overset{?}{=}\ N^{2\delta-1+o(1)},

(1.10)

where
2\delta-1
can be any quantity just above
0
, and yet our methods produce a density-one set of traces in this setting.

Also note that the methods introduced in
[
BK10
]
and applied to both the Zaremba
[
BK14a
]
and Apollonian
[
BK14b
]
settings required the linear form to have a bilinear structure. That is, the linear form was of type:

\gamma\mapsto\left\langle v,\gamma w\right\rangle

(1.11)

for some fixed vectors
v,w
. The trace is
not
of this form, and so the best one can currently say towards McMullen’s conjecture is a strong level of distribution
[
BK18
]
. It is not even currently known that a positive proportion of numbers arises in the set of traces of a Zaremba-type semigroup (see
[
Kon16
, §3]
).
For related work, see also the recent PhD thesis of Brooke Ogrodnik
[
Ogr21
]
.

Remark 1.12
.

Returning to the setting of this paper, here are some further remarks:

(1)

It is sometimes possible to completely determine the trace set of
\Gamma
, even if the latter is thin. For example, take the “Lubotzky 1-2-3” group,
\Gamma=\left\langle\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}1&3\\
0&1\end{smallmatrix}\bigr),\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}1&0\\
3&1\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)\right\rangle
. It is easy to see that every trace is
\equiv 2(\operatorname{mod}9)
, and indeed the element
\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}1&0\\
3&1\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}1&3\\
0&1\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)^{n}
has trace
2+9n
, so all admissible traces are represented by this one arithmetic progression.

(2)

Since we have assumed that
\Gamma
does contain a parabolic element, it is immediate that its trace set
\mathcal{T}(\Gamma)
comprises a positive proportion of integers, since, as above,
\mathcal{T}(\Gamma)
contains entire arithmetic progressions.
Without assuming that
M
is punctured, current technology cannot not even produce a positive proportion of traces!

(3)

On the other hand,
an argument based on Furstenberg’s topology on the integers shows that,
if
there is even a single local-global failure (that is, an admissible
t
not in
\mathcal{T}(\Gamma)
), then
finitely many such arithmetic progressions cannot possibly cover even a density-1 subset of
\mathcal{T}(\Gamma)
.

Note that it is easy to construct explicit examples of groups with arbitrarily many local-global failures. For just one family of such, fix
m
large, and consider the group
\Gamma_{0}<\operatorname{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
generated by
S_{1}:=\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}0&-1\\
1&0\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)
,

S_{2}:=\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}1&m\\
0&1\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}0&-1\\
1&0\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}1&-m\\
0&1\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)
,
and

S_{3}:=\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}1&-m\\
0&1\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}0&-1\\
1&0\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}1&m\\
0&1\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)
;
a fundamental domain for the action of
\Gamma_{0}
is shown in
Figure

.
It is easy to see that the systole of these groups grows with
m
.
By strong approximation, there is some
q_{0}
such that the reduction of
\Gamma_{0}
mod any prime
p\nmid q_{0}
is onto. Let
P=P(m)
be a very large prime coprime to
q_{0}
and let
\Gamma
be the group generated by
\Gamma_{0}
and the translation
\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}1&P\\
0&1\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)
. Then the reduction of
\Gamma
mod any

is clearly all of

1.1.
Methods

We use the (orbital) circle method to access the trace set
\mathcal{T}(\Gamma)
. In fact, our methods apply not just to the trace function
\operatorname{tr}:\operatorname{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})\to\mathbb{Z}
but to any linear form,
\mathscr{L}
, say, on
\operatorname{SL}_{2}
(and hence we do not group traces by conjugacy class).
It turns out (see (
2.1
)) that the trace function is the “generic” linear form.
The main theorem, from which
Theorem
1.8
follows immediately, is the following.

Theorem 1.13
.

Let
\Gamma
be a geometrically finite, punctured, Zariski dense subgroup of
\operatorname{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
, and let
\mathscr{L}:\Gamma\to\mathbb{Z}
be any linear form. Let
\mathcal{A}
denote the admissible values of
\mathscr{L}
; that is,
n\in\mathcal{A}
if and only if
n\in\Gamma(\operatorname{mod}q)
, for all
q
. Then there is some
\Theta>0
, so that:

{\#\{n\in\mathscr{L}(\Gamma)\cap[1,N]\}\over\#\{n\in\mathcal{A}\cap[1,N]\}}=1+O(N^{-\Theta}),

as
N\to\infty
.
The implied constant is effective.

In the case that the linear form is bilinear (as in (
1.11
)) and the critical exponent of
\Gamma
is sufficiently close to
1
, the above theorem is proved by the second-named author in
[
Zha20
]
.
One of the key innovations in the present paper is to use the parabolic element in
two
ways to produce not only arithmetic progressions, but values of binary quadratic polynomials in the set of values of the linear form
\mathscr{L}
.
By this we mean the following: given a fixed element
\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)\in\Gamma
and a parabolic, say,
\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}1&P\\
0&1\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)\in\Gamma
, we can compute that

\operatorname{tr}(\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}1&P\\
0&1\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)^{x})=a+d+cxP,

which is a linear form in
x
, whereas, say,

\operatorname{tr}(\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}1&P\\
0&1\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)^{x}\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}1&P\\
0&1\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)^{y}\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{smallmatrix}\bigr))=a^{2}+2bc+d^{2}+(a+d)cP(x+y)+c^{2}P^{2}xy

is quadratic as a function of the pair
(x,y)
.
(Using three or more copies of the parabolic produces cubic or higher forms; the added cost of increasingly larger coefficient sizes seems not to be advantageous for this problem.)

Then varying
x
and
y
, and letting
\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)
run over certain regions of
\Gamma
in a ball of size
N
, we study the “representation number”
\mathcal{R}_{N}(n)
of the number of times that
n\asymp N
occurs as a trace of such. In fact our construction of
\mathcal{R}_{N}
is more complicated, as we need to create multilinear forms at several scales for later estimates, see
§
2.1
.

Following the (orbital) circle method, we decompose
\mathcal{R}_{N}(n)
into a “main” term
\mathcal{M}_{N}(n)
and an “error”,
\mathcal{E}_{N}(n)
, where we integrate over the “major” and “minor” arcs, resp.
We note again that the main term is expected to be of size a singular series times
N^{2\delta-1}
(see (
1.10
)), which may be an arbitrarily small (but fixed) positive power of
N
. So we do not have much room to get an error off of the main term!

As in some other applications of the orbital circle method, we are only able to control the error terms in
L^{2}
, and hence produce only Asymptotic Length-Saturation, and not full Length-Saturation (which perhaps could be expected in this setting). Even this involves several novel techniques; there is a more standard analysis of cancellation in certain exponential sums and averages thereof, and there is also an appeal at some point to Hilbert’s Nullstellensatz in effective form (see
§
3.2
).

In the major arcs, we use the work of Bourgain-Varju
[
BV12
]
and Bourgain-Gamburd-Sarnak
[
BGS11
]
for an archimedean spectral gap, together with infinite volume counting methods of
[
BKS10
]
, to obtain an estimate for the main term
\mathcal{M}_{N}(n)
. But there are several surprises here as well!
It turns out that the singular series is a very short sum (of length
N^{\varepsilon}
) which is trying to approximate a quadratic Dirichlet
L
-function at
1
, see
§
3.6
. On GRH, this
L
-value can indeed be approximated by such a short sum, but our statement is unconditional! Since our error term estimate is anyway only as an “average” over
n
, we also average on the main term; that is, we show (see
Theorem
3.39
) that, for all but very few
n
’s, the approximation is valid. But then the have another problem: we need Siegel’s bound to know that the singular series, which is now one such
L
-value, is not too small. Again, because we are stating only an average result, we show (see
Theorem
3.42
) that we can bound these
L
-values from below for all but an exceptional set of values of
n
, with
effective
constants, leading to the effective constants in
Theorem
1.13
.

Outline

We begin in
§
2
with the setup of the circle method, introducing the main representation function, and its decomposition into a main term and error, corresponding to major and minor arcs.
The next two sections provide preparatory lemmata for the main arguments. We record in
§
3
various infinite volume counting theorems in congruence towers, the savings off of such counts in progressions for
arbitrarily large
modulus (this is where Nullstellensatz is used), as well as the analysis of the singular series, which involves Weil and Burgess bounds. In
§
4
, we prepare various exponential sum estimates used in the minor arcs analysis, using more standard analytic techniques such as estimates for Kloosterman-type sums. These allow us to complete the major arcs analysis in
§
5
and then the minor arc analysis in
§
6
.

2.
Preliminaries

We henceforth take
\Gamma<\operatorname{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
to be a given Zariski dense group.
We consider a linear form

\mathscr{L}:\operatorname{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})\to\mathbb{Z}
which is not everywhere vanishing; explicitly, this means that

\mathscr{L}\ :\ \left(\begin{array}[]{cc}a&b\\
c&d\end{array}\right)\ \mapsto\ Aa+Bb+Cc+Dd\ =\ \operatorname{tr}\left[\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}a&b\\
c&d\end{array}\right)\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}A&C\\
B&D\end{array}\right)\right],

(2.1)

and we assume that at least one coefficient
A,B,C,D
is non-zero.
Note that
\mathscr{L}
is of bilinear type (see (
1.11
)) exactly when its “discriminant”,

\Delta\ =\ \Delta_{\mathscr{L}}\ :=\ AD-BC

vanishes.
After conjugation (possibly renaming
A,B,C,D
above), we may assume that
\Gamma
contains the fixed parabolic element

\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}1&P\\
0&1\end{array}\right)\in\Gamma.

We make a few further simplifying assumptions.

•

We may assume that
\gcd(A,B,C,D)=1
, since otherwise we can pull out a common factor.

•

By applying fixed elements of
\Gamma
inside
\mathscr{L}
, we may assume that the coefficients
A,\ B,\ C
and
D
are all non-zero. Indeed, one has that:

\mathscr{L}\left(\gamma_{1}\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}a&b\\
c&d\end{array}\right)\gamma_{0}\right)=\operatorname{tr}\left[\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}a&b\\
c&d\end{array}\right)\gamma_{0}\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}A&C\\
B&D\end{array}\right)\gamma_{1}\right],

and by the Zariski density of
\Gamma
, there exist elements
\gamma_{0},\gamma_{1}\in\Gamma
so that

\gamma_{0}\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}A&C\\
B&D\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)\gamma_{1}

has every entry non-zero.

•

By Strong Approximation and passing to a finite index subgroup of
\Gamma
if necessary, we may assume that for all
(q_{1},q_{2})=1
, we have:

\Gamma/\Gamma(q_{1}q_{2})\cong\Gamma/\Gamma(q_{1})\times\Gamma/\Gamma(q_{2}),

(2.2)

where

\Gamma(q)\ :=\ \{\gamma\in\Gamma:\gamma\equiv I(\operatorname{mod}q)\}

(2.3)

is the “principal congruence” subgroup of (the possibly thin group)
\Gamma
.
Moreover, for all “good” primes
p
, we have that for
q=p^{\ell}
, the mod
q
reduction is onto

\Gamma(q)\backslash\Gamma=\operatorname{SL}_{2}(q).

•

For a finite list of “bad” primes
p
(including
p=2
), we have an exponent (“saturation level”)
k=k_{p}
so that

\Gamma(p^{k})\backslash\Gamma=\{I\},

(2.4)

and for
\ell>k
,

\Gamma(p^{\ell})\backslash\Gamma
is the full lift of the identity from
\operatorname{SL}_{2}(p^{k})
to
\operatorname{SL}_{2}(p^{\ell})
. In particular, the parabolic element
\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}1&P\\
0&1\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)\in\Gamma
satisfies

P\equiv 0(p^{k})

(2.5)

for all bad primes
p
.

•

We may assume, by increasing the saturation densities
k_{p}
if necessary, that

k_{p}>L_{p,B},

(2.6)

where
L=L_{p,B}
is determined by
p^{L}\|B
. (Here, as below,
B
is the coefficient of
b
in
\mathscr{L}
, as defined in (
2.1
).)

2.1.
Setup of the Circle Method

For
\gamma\in\Gamma
, we construct the shifted binary quadratic form:

\mathfrak{f}_{\gamma}(x,y)\ :=\ \mathscr{L}\bigg(\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}1&Px\\
0&1\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)\gamma\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}1&Py\\
0&1\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)\bigg),

(2.7)

so that, if
\gamma=\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}a_{\gamma}&b_{\gamma}\\
c_{\gamma}&d_{\gamma}\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)
, then

\mathfrak{f}_{\gamma}(x,y)=(Aa_{\gamma}+Bb_{\gamma}+Cc_{\gamma}+Dd_{\gamma})+(Ac_{\gamma}+Bd_{\gamma})Px+(Ba_{\gamma}+Dc_{\gamma})Py+Bc_{\gamma}P^{2}xy.

(2.8)

Note that, for any integers
x,y\in\mathbb{Z}
and any
\gamma\in\Gamma
, the value of
\mathfrak{f}_{\gamma}(x,y)
arises in
\mathscr{L}(\Gamma)
.
Let
N
be the main growing parameter, and
T,X
be parameters determined by:

T=N^{1/100},\ X=N^{99/200},\ \text{ so that }\ TX^{2}=N.

(2.9)

Decompose
T
further at

T=T_{1}T_{2},\ \text{ with }\ T_{2}=T_{1}^{\mathcal{C}},

(2.10)

with
\mathcal{C}
a very large constant depending only on the spectral gap for
\Gamma
, see (
3.7
).

We now define the main ensemble
\mathscr{F}_{T}
as follows

\mathscr{F}_{T}:=\left\{\gamma_{1}\cdot\gamma_{2}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}a&b\\
c&d\end{array}\right):{\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}\in\Gamma\atop{\frac{1}{2}T_{1}\leq\|\gamma_{1}\|<T_{1}\atop{\frac{1}{2}T_{2}\leq\|\gamma_{2}\|<T_{2}\atop\frac{1}{100}T<a,b,c,d}}}\right\}.

(2.11)

We show in
Lemma
3.3
that
\mathscr{F}_{T}
has cardinality
\asymp T^{2\delta}
.
This is a sub-multi-set of
\Gamma
, as the product
\gamma_{1}\gamma_{2}
may have multiplicity; that is, for a fixed
\xi\in\mathscr{F}_{T}
,

\sum_{\gamma\in\mathscr{F}_{T}}{\bf 1}_{\{\gamma=\xi\}}\ll T_{1}^{2\delta}.

(2.12)

Fixing a smooth nonnegative bump function
\Upsilon
with
\operatorname{supp}\Upsilon\subset[\frac{1}{2},1]
, define the main “representation number”

\mathcal{R}_{N}(n)\ :=\ \sum_{\gamma\in\mathscr{F}_{T}}\sum_{x\in\mathbb{Z}}\sum_{y\in\mathbb{Z}}\Upsilon\left({x\over X}\right)\Upsilon\left({y\over X}\right){\bf 1}_{\{\mathfrak{f}_{\gamma}(x,y)=n\}}.

We decompose
\mathcal{R}_{N}
into a “main” term and an error according to a (smoothed) major/minor arcs decomposition of the circle. To this end, let

\psi(t)\ :=\ \max(1-|t|,0)

be the “tent” function whose Fourier transform is the Fejér-type kernel:

\widehat{\psi}(\xi)={\sin^{2}(\pi\xi)\over(\pi\xi)^{2}}.

We fix parameters
Q_{0},K_{0}
to be determined as follows. We set

Q_{0}=N^{\alpha_{0}},\qquad K_{0}=N^{\kappa_{0}},

(2.13)

where the exponents satisfy

\kappa_{0}=3\alpha_{0}

(2.14)

and

5\alpha_{0}+\kappa_{0}<\Theta.

(2.15)

Here
\Theta
is the minimum of the two values in
Lemma
3.4
and
Lemma
3.5
.
Setting

\alpha_{0}=\Theta/10,\qquad\kappa_{0}=3\Theta/10

will satisfy all the criteria.

With these choices, let

\Psi_{N,K_{0}}(\beta):=\sum_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}\psi((\beta+m)\tfrac{N}{K_{0}}),

and define the “major arcs” weight function as:

\mathfrak{M}(\theta)\ :=\ \sum_{q<Q_{0}}\sum_{r(q)}^{\prime}\Psi_{N,K_{0}}(\theta-\tfrac{r}{q}).

(2.16)

Then the “main” term is given by:

\mathcal{M}_{N}(n)\ :=\ \int_{0}^{1}\mathfrak{M}(\theta)\widehat{\mathcal{R}_{N}}(\theta)e(-n\theta)d\theta,

(2.17)

and of course the error is

\mathcal{E}_{N}(n)\ :=\ \int_{0}^{1}(1-\mathfrak{M}(\theta))\widehat{\mathcal{R}_{N}}(\theta)e(-n\theta)d\theta,

so that

\mathcal{R}_{N}(n)=\mathcal{M}_{N}(n)+\mathcal{E}_{N}(n).

3.
Major Arc Technical Estimates

We record here a number of technical estimates needed in the analysis of the main term.

3.1.
Spectral Analysis and Counting

Let
\delta=\delta_{\Gamma}
be the Hausdorff dimension of the limit set of
\Gamma
, and recall that
\delta>1/2
. By Patterson-Sullivan theory
[
Pat76
,
Sul84
]
,
\delta
is related to the bottom eigenvalue
\lambda_{0}=\delta(1-\delta)
of the hyperbolic Laplacian acting on
L^{2}(\Gamma\backslash\mathbb{H})
. Work of Lax-Phillips
[
LP82
]
shows that the spectrum of the latter below
1/4
consists of finitely many eigenvalues. By Bourgain-Varju and Bourgain-Gamburd-Sarnak there is a uniform spectral gap, in the following sense.

Theorem 3.1

(
[
BV12
,
BGS11
]
)
.

There exists a “spectral gap,”

\Theta_{0}=\Theta_{0}(\Gamma)>0

(3.2)

so that, for all
q\geq 1
,
the eigenvalue
\lambda_{0}
is the bottom of the spectrum of
L^{2}(\Gamma(q)\backslash\mathbb{H})
, and all other eigenvalues are at least
\lambda_{\Theta}:=s_{\Theta}(1-s_{\Theta})
, where
s_{\Theta}:=\delta-\Theta_{0}
.
Here
\Gamma(q)
is as defined in (
2.3
).

Recalling the construction of
\mathscr{F}_{T}
from (
2.11
), we record the following counting results, which follow from now-standard techniques.

Lemma 3.3
.

As
T\to\infty
,

\#\mathscr{F}_{T}\ \asymp\ T^{2\delta}.

Proof.

This follows from infinite volume counting methods in Zariski dense groups with
\delta>1/2
; see, e.g.,
[
BKS10
]
.
∎

Lemma 3.4
.

There exists
\Theta>0
so that, for any
q\geq 1
,
\gamma_{0}\in\Gamma(q)\backslash\Gamma
,
|\beta|<1/X^{2}
, and
x,y\asymp X
, we have that

\sum_{\gamma\in\mathscr{F}_{T}\atop\gamma\equiv\gamma_{0}(\operatorname{mod}q)}e(\beta\mathfrak{f}_{\gamma}(x,y))=\frac{1}{[\Gamma:\Gamma(q)]}\sum_{\gamma\in\mathscr{F}_{T}}e(\beta\mathfrak{f}_{\gamma}(x,y))+O(|\mathscr{F}_{T}|N^{-\Theta}),

as
T\to\infty
.

Proof.

The proof is the same as that of
[
BKS10
, Theorem 1.14]
.
∎

Lemma 3.5
.

There exists
\Theta>0
so that, for
x,y\asymp X
and
n\asymp N
, we have:

\sum_{\gamma\in\mathscr{F}_{T}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\psi(\beta\tfrac{N}{K_{0}})e(\beta(\mathfrak{f}_{\gamma}(x,y)-n))d\beta\gg{|\mathscr{F}_{T}|\over K_{0}}+O(|\mathscr{F}_{T}|N^{-\Theta}),

as
T\to\infty
.

Proof.

We first note that

\int_{\mathbb{R}}\psi(\beta\tfrac{N}{K_{0}})e(\beta(\mathfrak{f}_{\gamma}(x,y)-n))d\beta={K_{0}\over N}\widehat{\psi}((\mathfrak{f}_{\gamma}(x,y)-n){K_{0}\over N})\geq 0,

and if
|\mathfrak{f}_{\gamma}(x,y)-n){K_{0}\over N}|<\frac{1}{2}
, then
\widehat{\psi}(\cdot)>\frac{2}{5}
. So we need to show the count:

\sum_{\gamma\in\mathscr{F}_{T}}{\bf 1}_{\{|\mathfrak{f}_{\gamma}(x,y)-n|<\frac{N}{2K_{0}}\}}\gg{|\mathscr{F}_{T}|\over K_{0}}+O(|\mathscr{F}_{T}|N^{-\Theta}).

The latter follows from the same techniques as the proof of
[
BKS10
, Theorem 1.15]
.
∎

3.2.
Nullstellensatz

Theorem 3.6
.

Let
\Theta_{0}
be the spectral gap in (
3.2
). Define
\mathcal{C}
by

\mathcal{C}=3\times 10^{8}/\Theta_{0}.

(3.7)

There exists an
\eta_{0}>0
depending only on the spectral gap for
\Gamma
, so that, for all
1\leq q<N
, and all
r(\operatorname{mod}q)
,

\sum_{\gamma\in\mathscr{F}_{T}}{\bf 1}_{\{c_{\gamma}\equiv r(\operatorname{mod}q)\}}\ \ll\ \frac{1}{q^{\eta_{0}}}|\mathscr{F}_{T}|.

(3.8)

Proof.

We first drop the condition
\frac{1}{100}T<a,b,c,d
from
\mathscr{F}_{T}
in (
2.11
), so that we need to count the number of
\gamma_{1}\asymp T_{1}
,
\gamma_{2}\asymp T_{2}
so that the “
c
” entry of
\gamma_{1}\gamma_{2}
,

\left\langle e_{2},\gamma_{1}\gamma_{2}e_{1}\right\rangle\equiv r(\operatorname{mod}q),

where
e_{j}
are standard basis vectors.
This decomposes into two cases according to the size of
q
.

Case
q<T_{2}^{\Theta_{0}/3}
:

In this case, we simply apply spectral theory in
\gamma_{2}
while leaving
\gamma_{1}
fixed, as follows. Break
\gamma_{2}
into progressions mod
q
:

\displaystyle\sum_{\gamma_{1}\asymp T_{1}}\sum_{\gamma_{2}\asymp T_{2}}{\bf 1}_{\{\left\langle e_{2},\gamma_{1}\gamma_{2}e_{1}\right\rangle\equiv r(\operatorname{mod}q)\}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\gamma_{1}\asymp T_{1}}\sum_{\gamma_{0}\in\Gamma(q)\backslash\Gamma}\left[{\bf 1}_{\{\left\langle e_{2},\gamma_{1}\gamma_{0}e_{1}\right\rangle\equiv r(\operatorname{mod}q)\}}\sum_{\gamma_{2}\asymp T_{2}\atop\gamma_{2}\equiv\gamma_{0}(\operatorname{mod}q)}1\right].

The bracketed term may be estimated using the uniform spectral gap (see
[
BKS10
]
) to give

\displaystyle\ll

\displaystyle\sum_{\gamma_{1}\asymp T_{1}}\sum_{\gamma_{0}\in\Gamma(q)\backslash\Gamma}{\bf 1}_{\{\left\langle e_{2},\gamma_{1}\gamma_{0}e_{1}\right\rangle\equiv r(\operatorname{mod}q)\}}\left[\frac{1}{q^{3}}T_{2}^{2\delta}+O(T_{2}^{2\delta-\Theta_{0}})\right]

\displaystyle\ll

\displaystyle\frac{1}{q}T_{2}^{2\delta}T_{1}^{2\delta}+q^{2}T_{1}^{2\delta}T_{2}^{2\delta-\Theta_{0}}.

Here we used that
[\Gamma:\Gamma(q)]\asymp q^{3}
.
This saves
1/q
(more than claimed) as long as
q<T_{2}^{\Theta_{0}/3}
.

Case
q\geq T_{2}^{\Theta_{0}/3}=T_{1}^{10^{8}}
:
The overview of the argument is as follows. For any fixed
\gamma_{2}
, we consider the set of
\gamma_{1}\asymp T_{1}
for which
\left\langle e_{2},\gamma_{1}\gamma_{2}e_{1}\right\rangle\equiv r(\operatorname{mod}q)
. Since different integers having the same residue class mod
q
differ by
q
, and
q
is huge compared to
T_{1}
, we will show by Nullstellensatz that in fact the modular restriction can be lifted to an absolute restriction
\left\langle e_{2},\gamma_{1}\gamma_{2}e_{1}\right\rangle=r_{*}
, for some integer
r_{*}
(depending on
\gamma_{2}
, which is fixed). Then we will relax the absolute restriction back down to a modular one, but with a much smaller modulus,
\left\langle e_{2},\gamma_{1}\gamma_{2}e_{1}\right\rangle\equiv r_{*}(\operatorname{mod}q_{*})
, where
q_{*}\asymp T_{1}^{\Theta_{0}/3}
, and apply the previous argument to save a power of
q_{*}
, which itself is a tiny power of
q
.

An issue arises in the use of Nullstellensatz that was overlooked in related arguments in
[
BK14b
,
BK15
]
. Write
\gamma_{2}e_{1}=(u,v)
and
\gamma_{1}e_{1}=(c,d)
, so that
\left\langle e_{2},\gamma_{1}\gamma_{2}e_{1}\right\rangle=uc+vd
, with
|u|,|v|\leq T_{2}
being “large” and fixed, and
|c|,|d|\leq T_{1}
being “small” variables. It was claimed that, since
(u,v)=1
, we may assume that, say,
(u,q)=1
, and rewrite the modular condition as
c+v\bar{u}d\equiv r\bar{u}(\operatorname{mod}q)
. Unfortunately the obvious linear transformation that allows this rewrite requires changing the coefficients
c,d
to ones of size bounded by
T_{1}T_{2}=T
, and this ruins the heights of the polynomials to be used in effective Nullstellensatz. So we need a more delicate argument to control the size of coefficients, as follows.

Suppose
q<N
has a divisor
\tilde{q}\mid q
of size
T_{1}<\tilde{q}<T_{2}^{\Theta_{0}/3}
, say. Then we relax
\left\langle e_{2},\gamma_{1}\gamma_{2}e_{1}\right\rangle\equiv r(\operatorname{mod}q)
to the same congruence mod
\tilde{q}
, and count as in the previous case. This saves
1/\tilde{q}>1/T_{1}
, which is a (very small) power of
N>q
, and completes the argument in this case.

Next we suppose that
q
has no divisor in this range. Let
\tilde{q}
be the largest divisor of
q
not exceeding
T_{1}
, and write
q_{0}:=q/\tilde{q}
. We again relax the congruence restriction to
\left\langle e_{2},\gamma_{1}\gamma_{2}e_{1}\right\rangle\equiv r(\operatorname{mod}q_{0})
; if we can save a small power of
q_{0}
, this also saves a small power of
q
.
Then any prime divisor
p
of
q_{0}
must exceed
T_{2}^{\Theta_{0}/3}
, for otherwise either
p
or
p\tilde{q}
is a divisor of
q
which is does not exceed
T_{2}^{\Theta_{0}/3}
.
Therefore
q_{0}
is “almost-prime”, that is, there are primes
p_{j}\geq T_{2}^{\Theta_{0}/3}=N^{\eta}
, say, so that

q_{0}=p_{1}p_{2}\cdots p_{\ell},

with
\ell<\lceil 1/\eta\rceil
.

Next we consider the values of
u+\alpha v
, for
\alpha=1,2,\dots,\ell+1
, and claim that at least one such value is coprime to
q_{0}
. (The point here is that
\ell
depends only on
q
, and is bounded only in terms of
\Theta_{0}
, which only depends on
\Gamma
.)
Consider first the primes
p_{j}
which divide either
u
or
v
(recall that
u
and
v
are coprime); then since
p_{j}>T_{2}^{\Theta_{0}/3}>\ell+1
(for
N
, and hence
T_{2}
, large enough), none of the
p_{j}
can divide
any
value of
u+\alpha v
.
Now consider the
p_{j}
which are coprime to
u
and
v
. Then since
u+\alpha v
is an arithmetic progression of length
\ell+1<p_{j}
, at most one value
\alpha_{j}\in\{1,2,\dots,\ell+1\}
can satisfy
u+\alpha_{j}\equiv 0(\operatorname{mod}p_{j})
.
Since the number of
\alpha
’s exceeds the number of
p_{j}
’s, there is some
\alpha
so that
u+\alpha v
is coprime to all the
p_{j}
, and hence coprime to
q_{0}
.

Again, this
\alpha
is bounded absolutely, and depends only on
q
,
u
, and
v
, and not on
c
and
d
(which depend on
\gamma_{1}
). Now we proceed with the Nullstellensatz argument.
Using the modulus
q_{0}
, we fix
\gamma_{2}
, let
(u,v)=\gamma_{2}e_{1}
, and consider the set

S=S_{\gamma_{2}}:=\{\gamma_{1}\in\Gamma,\ \|\gamma_{1}\|\leq T_{1}\ \text{ with }\ uc+vd\equiv r(\operatorname{mod}q_{0})\},

where we have set
(c,d):=\gamma_{1}e_{2}
. Using
\alpha
from the previous argument with
u+\alpha v
coprime to
q_{0}
, we write
uc+vd=(u+\alpha v)c+v(d-\alpha c)
, so that the congruence condition becomes

c+v\overline{(u+\alpha v)}(d-\alpha c)\equiv r\overline{(u+\alpha v)}(\operatorname{mod}q_{0}).

Now consider the (linear) polynomials
P_{\gamma_{1}}\in\mathbb{Z}[U,V]
given by

P_{\gamma_{1}}(U,V):=c+U(d-\alpha c)-V,

and consider the affine variety

\mathcal{V}:=\bigcap_{\gamma_{1}\in S}\{P_{\gamma_{1}}=0\}.

We claim that
\mathcal{V}(\mathbb{C})
is nonempty.
Note that the coefficients of
P_{\gamma_{1}}
are bounded by
(\ell+2)T_{1}
.
Then if
\mathcal{V}(\mathbb{C})
is empty, Hilbert’s Nullstellensatz, in effective form (see, e.g.,
[
MW83
, Theorem IV]
)
gives the existence of polynomials
Q_{\gamma_{1}}\in\mathbb{Z}[U,V]
and an integer
\mathfrak{d}\geq 1
so that

\sum_{\gamma_{1}\in S}P_{\gamma_{1}}(U,V)Q_{\gamma_{1}}(U,V)\ =\ \mathfrak{d},

(3.9)

and with
\mathfrak{d}
bounded (for
N
, and hence
T_{1}
, large enough) by

\mathfrak{d}\leq\exp(8^{7}(\log T_{1}+\log(\ell+2)+8\log 8)\leq T_{1}^{10^{7}}.

(“Large enough” is in terms of an implied constant depending only on
\Gamma
, since
\ell
depends only on
\Theta_{0}
).
But if we reduce (
3.9
) mod
q_{0}
and set
(U,V)\equiv(v\overline{(u+\alpha v)},r\overline{(u+\alpha v)})
, we get
\mathfrak{d}\equiv 0(\operatorname{mod}q_{0})
, which is impossible since
q_{0}=q/\tilde{q}>T_{1}^{10^{8}-1}
.

Therefore
\mathcal{V}(\mathbb{C})
is nonempty, and hence
\mathcal{V}(\mathbb{Q})
is nonempty, and so clearing denominators, there exist coprime integers
u_{*},v_{*},r_{*}
, so that for all
\gamma_{1}\in S
,

u_{*}c+v_{*}d=r_{*}.

We have turned our congruence condition into an archimedean condition. Now we take some
q_{*}\asymp T_{1}^{\Theta_{0}/3}
coprime to
u_{*},v_{*},r_{*}
, relax the archimedean condition back to a modular one,

u_{*}c+v_{*}d\equiv r_{*}(\operatorname{mod}q_{*}),

and count the number of
\gamma_{1}\asymp T_{1}
satisfying this. As before, the spectral argument saves
1/q_{*}
, which is some small power of
q
.
∎

3.3.
Singular Series Preliminaries

Recall that
\gcd(A,B,C,D)=1
and
\Delta=AD-BC
. Let
c_{q}
denote the Ramanujan sum. (There should be no confusion between
c_{q}
and bottom left element
c=\gamma_{c}
of a matrix
\gamma=\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)
.) We study here sums arising in the singular series analysis, of the form

\mathfrak{S}_{q}(n):=\frac{1}{|\Gamma(q)\backslash\Gamma|}\sum_{\gamma\in\Gamma(q)\backslash\Gamma}c_{q}(\mathfrak{f}_{\gamma}(x,y)-n),

for fixed
x,y\in\mathbb{Z}
.
Note immediately that the sum, being over all
\gamma\in\Gamma(q)\backslash\Gamma
, is independent of
x,y
, which we may assume are both
0
; thus
\mathfrak{f}_{\gamma}=Aa+Bb+Cc+Dd
.
By the structure of
\Gamma(q)\backslash\Gamma
in (
2.2
), the sum is multiplicative, so we may assume that
q=p^{\ell}
is a prime power. For “good” primes, we have that
\Gamma(q)\backslash\Gamma=\operatorname{SL}_{2}(q)
, and for “bad” primes,
\Gamma(\operatorname{mod}p^{k})=\{I\}
for some “saturation exponent”
k
, while
\Gamma(\operatorname{mod}p^{\ell})
for
\ell>k
is the full lift to
\operatorname{SL}_{2}(p^{\ell})
of the identity in
\operatorname{SL}_{2}(p^{k})
.

3.4.
Good Primes

Lemma 3.10
.

Assume that
q
is a power of a good prime. Then we have that

\mathfrak{S}_{q}(n)=\frac{1}{|\Gamma(q)\backslash\Gamma|}\sum_{\gamma\in\Gamma(q)\backslash\Gamma}c_{q}(a_{\gamma}+\Delta d_{\gamma}-n).

Proof.

Recalling that

\mathfrak{f}_{\gamma}(0,0)=\operatorname{tr}\left[\gamma\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}A&C\\
B&D\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)\right]=\operatorname{tr}\left[\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}\alpha&\beta\\
\kappa&\delta\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)\gamma\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}A&C\\
B&D\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}\delta&-\beta\\
-\kappa&\alpha\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)\right],

for any
\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}\alpha&\beta\\
\kappa&\delta\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)\in\operatorname{SL}_{2}
, and the sum being over all
\gamma\in\operatorname{SL}_{2}(q)
, we may simplify the expression in the following way. Assume WLOG that
(A,q)=1
; then we can rescale
\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}A&C\\
B&D\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)
to
\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}1&C\bar{A}\\
BA&DA\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)
, and continuing by elementary operations, we may replace
\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}A&C\\
B&D\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)
by
\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}1&0\\
0&\Delta\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)
, as claimed.
∎

3.4.1.
Case
\Delta\equiv 0(\operatorname{mod}p)

Lemma 3.11
.

Assume
\Delta\equiv 0(p)
.
For
q=p
a good prime, we have that:

\mathfrak{S}_{q}(n)=\begin{cases}-1\over p+1&\text{if $n\equiv 0(p)$,}\\
1\over p^{2}-1&\text{if $n\not\equiv 0(p)$.}\end{cases}

Proof.

Recall that

c_{p}(x)=\begin{cases}p-1&\text{if $x=0$,}\\
-1&\text{else.}\end{cases}

Write
\gamma=\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)
. Assume
p
is a good prime. From
Lemma
3.10
, we need to count the number of
\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)
with
a=n
or
a\neq n
.

Consider the case
n\equiv 0(\operatorname{mod}p)
. Then either
a\equiv n\equiv 0(\operatorname{mod}p)
or not. In the first case,
bc\equiv 1(\operatorname{mod}p)
and
d
is free (
p(p-1)
matrices) and
c_{p}=p-1
for a total contribution of
p(p-1)^{2}
. In the second case
a\neq n
, there are
p-1
choices for
a
, then
p^{2}
choices for
b,c

Lemma 3.12
.

Assume
\Delta\equiv 0(\operatorname{mod}p)
.
For

a power of a good prime (

), we have that:

\mathfrak{S}_{q}(n)=0.

Proof.

For prime powers, we have that:

c_{p^{\ell}}(x)=\begin{cases}0&\text{if $x\not\neq 0(p^{\ell-1})$,}\\
-p^{\ell-1}&\text{if $x\not\equiv 0(p^{\ell})$ but $x\equiv 0(p^{\ell-1})$,}\\

So there is no contribution unless

a_{1}d_{0}+d_{1}a_{0}-c_{1}b_{0}-b_{1}c_{0}\equiv 0(\operatorname{mod}p).

(3.13)

(This is just the Jacobian of the determinant.)
The above defines a 3-dimensional subspace restricting the values of

Assume WLOG that

∎

Lemma 3.14

Assume

Lemma 3.15
.

Assume
\Delta\not\equiv 0(\operatorname{mod}p)
.
Let

.
For

a power of a good prime (

\mathfrak{S}_{q}(n)=\begin{cases}0&\text{if $L\leq\ell-2$, or if $\ell$ is odd and $L\geq\ell$,}\\
{p^{-(\ell-3)/2}}(p^{2}-1)^{-1}\left({(n^{2}-4\Delta)/p^{L}\over p}\right)&\text{if $\ell$ is odd and $L=\ell-1$,}\\

\end{cases}

(3.16)

In any case,

\mathfrak{S}_{p^{\ell}}(n)\ll p^{-\ell/2}.

(3.17)

Proof.

We decompose
\operatorname{SL}_{2}

\operatorname{SL}_{2}(p^{\ell})\ =\ \bigsqcup_{c\in\mathbb{Z}/p^{\ell}}\mathscr{C}_{c},

Notice that
\Gamma_{\infty}=\{n_{x}\ :\ x\in\mathbb{Z}/p^{\ell}\}

\Gamma_{\infty}

\mathfrak{f}_{n_{x}\gamma}=a+\Delta d+cx

If

We are left to study the distribution of the values

from

ranging in
\mathscr{C}_{0}

\equiv n(\operatorname{mod}p^{\ell})

The equation

a^{2}-na+\Delta\equiv 0(\operatorname{mod}p^{\ell}),

which on completing the square gives the equation:

(a-\bar{2}n)^{2}\equiv\bar{4}(n^{2}-4\Delta)(\operatorname{mod}p^{\ell}).

(3.18)

We want to consider the number of solutions to (
3.18
) as compared to the solutions to the same equation but mod
p^{\ell-1}
:

(a-\bar{2}n)^{2}\equiv\bar{4}(n^{2}-4\Delta)(\operatorname{mod}p^{\ell-1}).

(3.19)

Consider first solutions to (
3.19
).
If

, then (
3.19
) has no solutions, and

.
Assume henceforth that

Let

.

Case:

) has no solutions. So we assume that

Assume WLOG that

But since

Thus are then

For each such value of

exactly cancel.

Case

and
\ell

In this case, (

) asks for
(a-\bar{2}n)^{2}\equiv 0(\operatorname{mod}p^{\ell-1})

The solutions to this are

(a-\bar{2}n)^{2}\equiv k^{2}p^{\ell}\overset{?}{\equiv}\bar{4}(n^{2}-4\Delta)(\operatorname{mod}p^{\ell}).

If

\mathfrak{S}_{q}=-{p^{(2-\ell)/2}\over p^{2}-1}.

Lastly, if

We summarize this subsection as follows.

Corollary 3.20
.

Let
p
be a good prime for
\Gamma
, and let

\mathfrak{S}^{(p)}(n):=1+\mathfrak{S}_{p}(n)+\mathfrak{S}_{p^{2}}(n)+\cdots

be the “local factor” at
p
. Then for all
p
,

\mathfrak{S}^{(p)}(n)=1+\mathfrak{S}_{p}(n)+\mathfrak{S}_{p^{2}}(n)+O(p^{-3/2}).

Moreover,

•

If
p\mid\Delta
, then

\mathfrak{S}^{(p)}(n)=1+O(p^{-1}).

•

If
p\nmid\Delta
and
p\nmid n^{2}-4\Delta
, then

\mathfrak{S}^{(p)}(n)=1+\frac{1}{p}\left({n^{2}-4\Delta\over p}\right)+O(p^{-2}).

•

If
p\nmid\Delta
and
p\|n^{2}-4\Delta
, then

\mathfrak{S}^{(p)}(n)=1+O(p^{-3/2}).

•

If
p\nmid\Delta
and
p^{2}\|n^{2}-4\Delta
, then

\mathfrak{S}^{(p)}(n)=1+O(p^{-1}).

•

If
p\nmid\Delta
and
p^{L}\|n^{2}-4\Delta
with

, then

\mathfrak{S}^{(p)}(n)=1+O(p^{-3/2}).

3.5.
Bad Primes

For bad primes, our strategy is as follows. Rather than evaluating
\mathfrak{S}_{q}(n)
explicitly, we show the following “density formula.”

Lemma 3.21
.

For any
\ell\geq 0
and any prime
p
(good or bad), we have that

1+\mathfrak{S}_{p}(n)+\cdots+\mathfrak{S}_{p^{\ell}}(n)=p^{\ell}{\#\{\gamma\in\Gamma(p^{\ell})\backslash\Gamma:\mathfrak{f}_{\gamma}\equiv n(\operatorname{mod}p^{\ell})\}\over[\Gamma:\Gamma(p^{\ell})]}.

(3.22)

This will tautologically capture the condition that
n
is admissible.
And then, for
\ell
large enough, we claim that
\mathfrak{S}_{p^{\ell}}(n)=0
, so these probabilities stabilize.

Proof of
Lemma
3.21
.

This follows immediately from

\mathfrak{S}_{p^{m}}(n)=\frac{1}{|\Gamma(p^{\ell})\backslash\Gamma|}\sum_{\gamma\in\Gamma(p^{\ell})\backslash\Gamma}c_{p^{m}}(\mathfrak{f}_{\gamma}-n),

for any
0\leq m\leq\ell
, together with the fact that

1+c_{p}(x)+\dots+c_{p^{\ell}}(x)={\bf 1}_{\{x\equiv 0(\operatorname{mod}p^{\ell})\}}p^{\ell}.

∎

Finally, we show that the densities stabilize.

Lemma 3.23
.

Let
p
be a bad prime, and let
k=k_{p}
be the “saturation level” of
p
, as in (
2.4
). Let
p^{L}\|B
(and recall that
B\neq 0
, and that
k>L
by (
2.6
)). If
\ell>2k
, then
\mathfrak{S}_{p^{\ell}}(n)=0
.

Proof.

Decompose
\Gamma(p^{\ell})\backslash\Gamma
into disjoint
\Gamma_{\infty}=\{n_{x}=\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}1&x\\
0&1\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)\}
cosets; here

x
ranges in
\mathbb{Z}/p^{\ell}
but is restricted (by saturation) to
x\equiv 0(p^{k})
.
We claim that the Ramanujan values on each coset exactly cancel. Note that
\mathfrak{f}_{\gamma}=Aa+Bb+Cc+Dd
changes when
\gamma\mapsto n_{x}\gamma
to

\mathfrak{f}_{n_{x}\gamma}=\mathfrak{f}_{\gamma}+(Ac+Bd)x.

Since
c\equiv 0(p^{k})
and
d\equiv 1(\operatorname{mod}p^{k})
, and
p^{L}\|B
with
k>L
, we have that

p^{L}\|Ac+Bd.

Now as
x
ranges over
p^{\ell}
subject to
x\equiv 0(\operatorname{mod}p^{k})
, since
\ell>2k>k+L
, the values of
\mathfrak{f}_{n_{x}\gamma}
range in some non-constant arithmetic progression.
The resulting Ramanujan values cancel exactly, as claimed.
∎

So the high powers of bad primes have vanishing
\mathfrak{S}_{q}
. For the lower powers, we give the following trivial estimate on
\mathfrak{S}_{q}
.

Lemma 3.24
.

For any prime
p
(good or bad) and any
\ell\geq 1
, we have:

|\mathfrak{S}_{p^{\ell}}(n)|\leq p^{\ell}.

(3.25)

Proof.

The density formula (
3.22
) gives upper and lower bounds for its left-hand side of:
p^{\ell}
and
0
, respectively. Replace
\ell
by
\ell-1
and subtract to get the claim.
∎

3.6.
Short Sum of
\mathfrak{S}_{q}

Define

\mathfrak{S}(n)=\sum_{q\geq 1}\mathfrak{S}_{q}(n).

Lemma 3.26
.

Assume that
\Delta=0
. Then the series defining
\mathfrak{S}(n)
is absolutely convergent,

\sum_{q<Q_{0}}\mathfrak{S}_{q}(n)=\mathfrak{S}(n)+O_{\varepsilon}(Q_{0}^{-1}n^{\varepsilon}),

as
Q_{0}\to\infty
,
and satisfies, for
n
admissible,

\frac{1}{\log\log n}\ll\mathfrak{S}(n)\ll 1.

Proof.

By
Lemma
3.11
and
Lemma
3.12
,
we have that

\sum_{q\geq Q_{0}}|\mathfrak{S}_{q}(n)|\ll_{\varepsilon}n^{\varepsilon}Q_{0}^{-1}.

For
n
admissible,

\mathfrak{S}(n)\asymp\prod_{p\mid n}\left(1-\frac{1}{p}\right),

where we also used
Lemma
3.21
and
Lemma
3.23
. The claim follows immediately.
∎

To prepare for the case
\Delta\neq 0
, we need some preliminaries.

Lemma 3.27
.

Let
\chi
be a Dirichlet character of conductor
M
and fix
\Pi\in\mathbb{Z}
. Then

\left|\sum_{q\asymp H\atop{\text{squarefree}\atop(q,\Pi)=1}}\chi(q)\right|\ll_{\varepsilon}H^{1/2}M^{3/16}(HM\Pi)^{\varepsilon},

as
H\to\infty
.

Proof.

To capture both the squarefree and coprime conditions, we use Möbius inversion.
Using
\zeta(s)/\zeta(2s)=\sum_{n\text{ squarefree}}1/n^{s}
, we have that
\mu(q)^{2}=\sum_{m^{2}\mid q}\mu(m)
.
Similarly,
\sum_{d\mid x}\mu(d)=1
if
x=1
and
0
otherwise. Therefore

\displaystyle\sum_{q\asymp H\atop{\text{squarefree}\atop(q,\Pi)=1}}\chi(q)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{q\asymp H\atop(q,\Pi)=1}\chi(q)\sum_{m^{2}\mid q}\mu(m)=\sum_{m\ll H^{1/2}\atop(m,\Pi)=1}\mu(m)\chi(m)^{2}\sum_{q\asymp H/m^{2}\atop(q,\Pi)=1}\chi(q)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{m\ll H^{1/2}\atop(m,\Pi)=1}\mu(m)\chi(m)^{2}\sum_{q\asymp H/m^{2}}\chi(q)\sum_{d\mid q\atop d\mid\Pi}\mu(d)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{m\ll H^{1/2}\atop(m,\Pi)=1}\mu(m)\chi(m)^{2}\sum_{d\ll H/m^{2}\atop d\mid\Pi}\mu(d)\chi(d)\sum_{q\asymp H/(m^{2}d)}\chi(q).

Applying Burgess
[
Bur62
]
to the last sum, we have that

\displaystyle\left|\sum_{q\asymp H\atop{\text{squarefree}\atop(q,\Pi)=1}}\chi(q)\right|

\displaystyle\ll_{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle\sum_{m\ll H^{1/2}}\sum_{d\ll H/m^{2}\atop d\mid\Pi}{H^{1/2}\over md^{1/2}}M^{3/16+\varepsilon}\ll_{\varepsilon}\Pi^{\varepsilon}{H^{1/2+\varepsilon}}M^{3/16+\varepsilon},

as claimed. (Slightly better estimates are available today but not needed here.)
∎

Going forward, we let

\mathfrak{B}_{1}
be the (finitely many) primes which are “bad” (for
\Gamma
),

\mathfrak{B}_{2}
be the primes not in
\mathfrak{B}_{1}
which divide
\Delta
, and

\mathfrak{B}_{3}=\mathfrak{B}_{3}(n)
be the primes not in
\mathfrak{B}_{1}
or
\mathfrak{B}_{2}
which divide
n^{2}-4\Delta
.
Let

\mathfrak{B}=\mathfrak{B}(n)=\sqcup_{j}\mathfrak{B}_{j}
, and set

\Pi=\Pi(\mathfrak{B}):=\prod_{p\in\mathfrak{B}}p.

For all the other primes,
Lemma
3.14
gives that

\mathfrak{S}_{p}(n)=\frac{1}{p}\left({n^{2}-4\Delta\over p}\right)+E_{p},

where

E_{p}=E_{p}(n):=\frac{p+\left({n^{2}-4\Delta\over p}\right)}{p(p^{2}-1)}\ll\frac{1}{p^{2}}.

We extend
E_{p}
to a multiplicative function

supported on square-free
q
.

Note that we now do not have absolute convergence, and must be much more careful.

We break the tail
\sum_{q\geq Q_{0}}
of
\mathfrak{S}(n)
into dyadic regions
\sum_{q\asymp H}
, with
Q_{0}\leq H\to\infty
.

Lemma 3.28

Theorem 3.42

There is an

Consider the characters
\chi_{n}=\left({n^{2}-4\Delta\over\cdot}\right)
appearing in (

\left({q_{n}\over\cdot}\right)

for some integer

Lemma 6.5

Suppose that

Proof.

The phase of

can be written as

Inputting (

First consider the case that

Recalling that

Now suppose that the pair
