Transitive Nonlocal Games
Prem Nigam Kar, Jitendra Prakash, David Roberson

Abstract
We study a class of nonlocal games, called transitive games, for which the set of perfect strategies forms a semigroup. We establish several interesting correspondences of bisynchronous transitive games with the theory of compact quantum groups. In particular, we associate a quantum permutation group with each bisynchronous transitive game and vice versa. We prove that the existence of a C ∗ C^{*} -strategy, the existence of a quantum commuting strategy, and the existence of a classical strategy are all equivalent for bisynchronous transitive games. We then use some of these correspondences to establish necessary and sufficient conditions for some classes of correlations, that arise as perfect strategies of transitive games, to be nonlocal.



I
Introduction

Nonlocal games have been studied under several different names such as Bell scenarios and quantum pseudo-telepathy games across various disciplines. Their structure and formulation is simple, yet their applications are profound. In
[
1
]
, the authors introduced and studied the graph isomorphism game. The
(G,G)
-isomorphism game is also known as the
G
-automorphism game. The authors of
[
1
]
also proved that the set of all perfect quantum strategies for the graph automorphism game is closed under composition. Hence, these quantum strategies imitate the composability of graph automorphisms, which are precisely the perfect deterministic strategies for the
(G,G)
-isomorphism game. In
[
13
,
22
]
, the authors also established several connections of nonlocal games with the theory of compact quantum groups and semigroups.

In
[
24
]
, Wang introduced quantum symmetry groups of finite spaces. However, this formulation was in terms of compact quantum groups. Hence, these quantum symmetry groups are not actual sets of transformations or functions like classical groups. In
[
14
]
, the authors introduced a notion of quantum bijections of finite classical and quantum sets. Quantum bijections of finite classical sets correspond to the perfect quantum strategies of the
K_{n}
-automorphism game as discussed in
Section

III
. Since all perfect quantum strategies for bisynchronous games can be shown to be a subset of the set of perfect quantum strategies of this game, it makes sense to study nonlocal games with composable strategies, as they can be viewed as quantum analogues of classical permutation groups.

Hence, we study
transitive games
, which are nonlocal games with composable perfect strategies, and explore connections of bisynchronous transitive games with quantum permutation groups. These games were first studied in
[
22
]
, and some connections of these games with compact quantum semigroups were established. However, this paper did not venture out to explore the perfect strategies for these games, which we do extensively in this paper.

We prove that if a bisynchronous transitive game
\mathscr{G}
has a perfect
C^{*}
-algebra strategy, then
C^{*}(\mathscr{G})
is a quantum permutation group. Moreover, given any quantum permutation group
\mathbb{G}
acting on
\{1,2,...,n\}
we prove that there is a bisynchronous transitive game
\mathscr{G}
such that
\mathbb{G}
induces the same orbitals as
C^{*}(\mathscr{G})
. Moreover,
C^{*}(\mathscr{G})
is the largest quantum permutation group inducing the same orbitals as
\mathbb{G}
and every other quantum permutation group inducing the same orbitals as
\mathbb{G}
can be obtained as a quotient of
C^{*}(\mathscr{G})
. Hence, these transitive games can be treated as tools to define quantum permutation groups. Using the existence of the Haar state on a compact quantum group, we shall prove that the existence of a perfect
C^{*}
-strategy implies the existence of a quantum commuting strategy for bisynchronous transitive games, thus working towards
[
17
, Problem 5.4]
.
We shall then move on to studying coherent and transitive correlations, which are correlations that arise as perfect strategies of transitive games. We shall give necessary and sufficient conditions for these correlations to be nonlocal.

Outline

II
Preliminaries

For an
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we let
[n]:=\{1,\dots,n\}
. For a nonempty finite set
W
, let
S_{W}
be the
symmetric group
on
W
. The identity element of the group
S_{W}
is the identity function
\mathrm{id}_{W}:W\to W
.

The Hilbert spaces in this paper are over the complex field, and their inner products are linear in the first component and conjugate-linear in the second component. The algebra of all bounded linear operators from a Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
to itself is denoted by
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
. A finite sequence
(P_{i})_{i=1}^{n}
of operators in
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
is called a
projection-valued measure
(abbreviated
PVM
) if each
P_{i}
is a projection, that is,
P_{i}=P_{i}^{*}=P_{i}^{2}
, and their sum is the identity operator
I_{\mathcal{H}}
. A
quantum state
is simply a unit vector in a Hilbert space.

All graphs considered in this paper are simple and without loops, unless stated otherwise. Let
G=(V(G),E(G))
be a graph, where
V(G)
and
E(G)
denote the set of vertices and edges of
G
, respectively. For
x,y\in V(G)
, we write
x\sim y
if
(x,y)\in E(G)
;
x\nsim y
if
(x,y)\notin E(G)
; and
x\nsimeq y
if
x
and
y
are distinct and nonadjacent.

II.1
Nonlocal games and their strategies

A
nonlocal game

\mathscr{G}
is a 6-tuple
\mathscr{G}=(X,Y,A,B,\pi,V)
, where
X,Y,A,B
are nonempty finite sets,
\pi:X\times Y\to[0,1]
is a probability distribution, and
V:A\times B\times X\times Y\to\{0,1\}
is a function called the
predicate
. Such a game
\mathscr{G}
involves two players, Alice and Bob, and a referee. The sets
X
and
Y
are question sets for Alice and Bob, respectively, and the sets
A
and
B
are their corresponding answer sets. The probability vector
\pi
determines the probability with which the referee sends each pair of questions
(x,y)\in X\times Y
to the players. The function
V
determines whether a pair of answers
(a,b)
wins the game for Alice and Bob when given the questions
(x,y)
: they win if and only if
V(a,b,x,y)\equiv V(a,b|x,y)=1
. The key property of nonlocal games is that the players cannot communicate once a round of the game begins.

Alice and Bob may employ different kinds of strategies based on the resources they have. The simplest kind of strategies are deterministic. A
deterministic strategy
for Alice and Bob for the game
\mathscr{G}
is a pair of functions
(f_{A},f_{B})
where
f_{A}:X\to A
and
f_{B}:Y\to B
. Upon receiving the questions
x
and
y
, Alice and Bob return
a=f_{A}(x)
and
b=f_{B}(y)
, respectively, to the referee. We say that the deterministic strategy
(f_{A},f_{B})
is
perfect
(or
winning
) if
V(f_{A}(x),f_{B}(y)|x,y)=1
for all
(x,y)\in X\times Y
. Players may also use
probabilistic
strategies utilizing
local randomness
or
shared randomness
. Such strategies are essentially convex combinations of deterministic strategies and, therefore, cannot yield better success rates. Deterministic strategies and their convex combinations are grouped together as
local strategies
or
classical strategies
. However, there may be better probabilistic strategies if the players utilise quantum resources. Let us first precisely define what we mean by a probabilistic strategy.

A
probabilistic strategy
or a
correlation
for the game
\mathscr{G}
can be thought of as a collection of probability distributions
\{p(a,b|x,y):(a,b)\in A\times B\}
for each
(x,y)\in X\times Y
. Here,
p(a,b|x,y)
is the probability that Alice and Bob return answers
a\in A
and
b\in B
, given that they received questions
x\in X
and
y\in Y
, respectively. Such a correlation
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p(a,b|x,y)}}\right)_{a\in A,b\in B,x\in X,y\in Y}
has some obvious properties:
p(a,b|x,y)\geq 0
for all
a,b,x,y
, and
\sum_{a,b}p(a,b|x,y)=1
for all
x,y
. Moreover, a correlation must satisfy the following
nonsignalling conditions
, stemming from the fact that the players cannot communicate during the game:

\displaystyle p_{A}(a|x)

\displaystyle:=\sum_{b}p(a,b|x,y)=\sum_{b}p(a,b|x,y^{\prime}),\quad\text{ for all }a,x,y,y^{\prime},\text{ and, }

\displaystyle p_{B}(b|y)

\displaystyle:=\sum_{a}p(a,b|x,y)=\sum_{a}p(a,b|x^{\prime},y),\quad\text{ for all }b,y,x,x^{\prime}.

This general class of correlations, denoted by
C_{ns}(|X|,|Y|,|A|,|B|)
is called the set of
nonsignalling
correlations. (Here
|X|
means the size of the set
X
.) For brevity’s sake, we shall simply denote it by
C_{ns}
, and the same convention goes for other subsets of
C_{ns}
that we will introduce next.

Correlations arising via local strategies are called
local
correlations, and the set of local correlations is denoted by
C_{loc}
.

In a
quantum strategy
for the game
\mathscr{G}
, Alice and Bob share a quantum state
\psi\in\mathcal{H}_{A}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{B}
, where
\mathcal{H}_{A}
and
\mathcal{H}_{B}
are finite-dimensional Hilbert spaces and perform
quantum measurements
on the shared quantum state. Such quantum measurements are given by PVMs:
(P_{x,a})_{a\in A}\subseteq\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}_{A})
corresponding to each question
x\in X
for Alice, and
(Q_{y,b})_{b\in B}\subseteq\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}_{B})
corresponding to each question
y\in Y
for Bob. Then, the quantum strategy
(\psi\in\mathcal{H}_{A}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{B},\{P_{x,a}:x\in X,a\in A\},\{Q_{y,b}:y\in Y,b\in B\})
defines the following correlation:

p(a,b|x,y)=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(P_{x,a}\otimes Q_{y,b}}}\right)\psi},{\psi}}}\right\rangle.

(II.1)

Correlations arising through quantum strategies are termed
quantum
correlations, and the set of quantum correlations is denoted by
C_{q}
.

There is also a more general "quantum" strategy called the
quantum commuting strategy
described as follows. Alice and Bob share a quantum state
\psi\in\mathcal{H}
, where
\mathcal{H}
is a (possibly, infinite-dimensional) Hilbert space, quantum measurements for Alice given by PVMs
(P_{x,a})_{a\in A}\subseteq\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
for each
x\in X
, quantum measurements for Bob given by PVMs
(Q_{y,b})_{b\in B}\subseteq\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
for each
y\in Y
, with the condition that
P_{x,a}Q_{y,b}=Q_{y,b}P_{x,a}
for all
a,b,x,y
. The corresponding correlation is:

p(a,b|x,y)=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle{P_{x,a}Q_{y,b}\psi},{\psi}}}\right\rangle.

(II.2)

Correlations arising through quantum commuting strategies are termed
quantum commuting
correlations, and the set of quantum commuting correlations is denoted by
C_{qc}
. In general, quantum commuting strategies yield a bigger class of correlations than the quantum strategies
[
20
,
21
,
9
]
. In fact, there exist quantum commuting strategies which
cannot
even be approximated to an arbitrary degree by quantum strategies, that is,
\overline{C_{q}}\subsetneq C_{qc}

[
11
]
.

A correlation
(p(a,b|x,y))
is called
perfect
(or
winning
) for the game
\mathscr{G}
if
V(a,b|x,y)=0
implies
p(a,b|x,y)=0
. In this paper we are only concerned with perfect correlations of a game
\mathscr{G}
, and therefore we will omit the probability distribution
\pi
from the description of the game.

A game
\mathscr{G}=(X,Y,A,B,V)
is called
synchronous
if
X=Y=:I
,
A=B=:O
, and
V(a,b|x,x)=0
for all
x\in I
and
a\neq b
. Additionally,
\mathscr{G}
is called
bisynchronous
if we have
V(a,a|x,y)=0
for all
a\in O
and
x\neq y
. For brevity, we shall describe
\mathscr{G}
simply as
\mathscr{G}=(I,O,V)
.

Graph-theoretic games make up most of the well-known examples of synchronous games. Consider the following two examples of synchronous games for graphs
G=(V(G),E(G))
and
H=(V(H),E(H))
.

The
(G,H)
-homomorphism game.
Here, the question set is
I=V(G)
and the answer set is
O=V(H)
. Alice and Bob need to convince the referee that they have a graph homomorphism from
G
to
H
. The predicate
V
is

V(a,b|x,y)=\begin{cases}0&\text{ if }a\neq b\text{ and }x=y,\\
0&\text{ if }a\nsim b\text{ and }x\sim y,\\
1&\text{ otherwise.}\end{cases}

(II.3)

This game is synchronous, and it can be verified that it admits a perfect local strategy if and only if there is a graph homomorphism
\phi:G\to H
. A perfect quantum strategy (resp., quantum commuting strategy) for the
(G,H)
-homomorphism game is called a
quantum homomorphism (resp., quantum commuting homomorphism) from
G
to
H
.

The
(G,H)
-isomorphism game.
Alice and Bob need to convince the referee that the graphs
G
and
H
are isomorphic. Thus, the predicate
V
is
V(a,b|x,y)=1
if and only if
\mathrm{rel}(a,b)=\mathrm{rel}(x,y)
, where
\mathrm{rel}(x,y)\in\{=,\sim,\nsimeq\}
. This game is bisynchronous, and it can be verified that it admits a perfect local strategy if and only if
G
is isomorphic to
H
. We say that
G
is
quantum isomorphic
(resp.
quantum commuting isomorphic
) to
H
if there exists a perfect quantum strategy (resp., quantum commuting strategy) for the
(G,H)
-isomorphism game. The
(G,G)
-isomorphism game will be called the
G
-automorphism game. The
G
-automorphism game always has a perfect deterministic strategy, e.g., the identity function.

Introduced in
[
10
]
, the
*
-algebra

\mathcal{A}(\mathscr{G})
of a synchronous game
\mathscr{G}=(I,O,V)
is defined to be the unital
*
-algebra generated by
\{u_{x,a}:x\in I,a\in O\}
with the following relations:

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} u_{x,a}=u_{x,a}^{*}=u_{x,a}^{2},&\quad\text{ for all }x\in I,a\in O,\\
\sum_{a\in O}u_{x,a}=1,&\quad\text{ for all }x\in I,\\
u_{x,a}u_{y,b}=0&\quad\text{ if }V(a,b|x,y)=0.\end{aligned}

(II.4)

In general,
\mathcal{A}(\mathscr{G})
could be trivial. If
\mathcal{A}(\mathscr{G})
is nontrivial, the game
\mathscr{G}
is said to have a
perfect algebraic strategy
. Even if
\mathcal{A}(\mathscr{G})
is nontrivial, it may not be realizable as a
C^{*}
-algebra. (For a very brief review of
C^{*}
-algebras we refer the reader to
Section

II.2
.) The game
\mathscr{G}
has a
perfect
C^{*}
-strategy
if there is a universal
C^{*}
-algebra with generators
\{u_{x,a}:x\in I,a\in O\}
satisfying Relations
II.4
. We shall denote this universal
C^{*}
-algebra as
C^{*}(\mathscr{G})
. The following theorem shows the relevance of the
*
-algebra
\mathcal{A}(\mathscr{G})
of a game
\mathscr{G}
.

Theorem II.1

(
[
10
, Theorem 3.2]
)
.

Let
\mathscr{G}=(I,O,V)
be a synchronous game. Then
\mathscr{G}
has a perfect

(a)

local strategy if and only if there exists a representation
\pi:\mathcal{A}(\mathscr{G})\to\mathbb{C}
;

(b)

quantum strategy if and only if there exists a representation
\pi:\mathcal{A}(\mathscr{G})\to\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
for some finite-dimensional Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}\neq 0
, in which case, the correlation
p
induced by the perfect quantum strategy is given by
p(a,b|x,y)=\frac{1}{\dim{\mathcal{H}}}\tr(\pi(u_{x,a}u_{y,b}))
;

(c)

quantum commuting strategy if and only if there exists a unital
*
-homomorphism
\pi:\mathcal{A}(\mathscr{G})\to\mathcal{B}
for some
C^{*}
-algebra
\mathcal{B}
equipped with a faithful tracial state
\tau\in\mathcal{B}^{*}
, in which case, the correlation
p
induced by the perfect quantum commuting strategy is given by
p(a,b|x,y)=\tau(\pi(u_{x,a}u_{y,b}))
, for all
a,b\in O,x,y\in I
.

We turn our focus to correlations associated with question set
I
and answer set
O
. Let
t\in\{loc,q,qc,ns\}
. A correlation
(p(a,b|x,y))\in C_{t}
is called
synchronous
if it satisfies
p(a,b|x,x)=0
for all
x\in I
and
a\neq b.
A
bisynchronous correlation
is a synchronous correlation that satisfies
p(a,a|x,y)=0
for all
x\neq y
and
a\in O
. We denote the subset of all synchronous (resp., bisynchronous) correlations in
C_{t}
by
C^{s}_{t}
(resp.,
C^{bs}_{t}
). It is easy to see that perfect correlations of synchronous (resp., bisynchronous) games are synchronous (resp., bisynchronous).

II.2
Compact quantum semigroups and groups

We collect some definitions and results about compact quantum groups, mostly from
[
15
, Chapter 1]
. We begin with a brief overview of
C^{*}
-algebras. A
C^{*}
-algebra

\mathcal{A}
is a Banach
*
-algebra whose norm satisfies
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|a^{*}a}}\right\|=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|a}}\right\|^{2}
for all
a\in\mathcal{A}
. In this paper, all algebras considered are unital, and the unit is denoted by
1
. A linear functional
\varphi\in\mathcal{A}^{*}
is called a
state
if
\varphi(1)=1
and
\varphi(a^{*}a)\geq 0
for all
a\in\mathcal{A}
. Additionally,
\varphi
is
faithful
if
\varphi(a^{*}a)=0
implies
a=0
for all
a\in\mathcal{A}
; and
\varphi
is
tracial
if
\varphi(ab)=\varphi(ba)
for all
a,b\in\mathcal{A}
. A
representation
of
\mathcal{A}
is a unital
*
-homomorphism
\pi:\mathcal{A}\to\mathcal{B(H)}
for some Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
. The
Gelfand-Naimark theorem
states that every
C^{*}
-algebra
\mathcal{A}
is
*
-isomorphic to a norm-closed
*
-subalgebra of
\mathcal{B(H)}
for some Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}

[
7
, Corollary II.6.4.10]
.

Let
\mathcal{G}=\{g_{i}\}_{i\in\Omega}
be a nonempty finite set of generators and
\mathcal{R}
be a nonempty finite set of relations on
\mathcal{G}
. A
representation
of
(\mathcal{G}|\mathcal{R})
is a set
\{T_{i}\}_{i\in\Omega}\subseteq\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
(for some Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
) satisfying the relations in
\mathcal{R}
, which also defines a representation of the free
*
-algebra
\langle\mathcal{G}\rangle
generated by
\mathcal{G}
. For
a\in\langle\mathcal{G}\rangle
, let
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|a}}\right\|:=\sup\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\pi(a)}}\right\|:\pi\text{ is a representation of }(\mathcal{G}|\mathcal{R})}}\right\}.
If
\max_{i\in\Omega}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|g_{i}}}\right\|<\infty
, then
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|.}}\right\|
defines a seminorm on
\langle\mathcal{G}\rangle
. Quotienting out seminorm-zero elements and then taking completion, yields a
C^{*}
-algebra denoted by C
{}^{*}(\mathcal{G}|\mathcal{R})
and called the
universal
C^{*}
-algebra generated by
(\mathcal{G}|\mathcal{R})
. A suitable reference on universal
C^{*}
-algebras is
[
7
, Section II.8.3]
.

In this paper, a tensor product of two
C^{*}
-algebras is always meant to be their
minimal
tensor product; see
[
7
, II.9.1.3]
for a definition. The minimal tensor product is associative:
(\mathcal{A}\otimes\mathcal{B})\otimes\mathcal{C}\simeq\mathcal{A}\otimes(\mathcal{B}\otimes\mathcal{C})
for
C^{*}
-algebras
\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B}
and
\mathcal{C}

[
7
, II.9.2.6]
. Let
M_{n}(\mathcal{A})
be the set of all
n\times n
matrices with entries from
\mathcal{A}
. We shall freely use the identification
\mathbb{M}_{n}\otimes\mathcal{A}\simeq M_{n}(\mathcal{A})
, via,
\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}E_{i,j}\otimes a_{i,j}\leftrightarrow[a_{i,j}]_{i,j=1}^{n}
, where
\{E_{i,j}:i,j\in[n]\}
is the set of canonical matrix units of
\mathbb{M}_{n}
, the space of all
n\times n
complex matrices.

Definition II.2
.

A pair,
\mathbb{G}=(\mathcal{A},\Delta)
, where
\mathcal{A}
is a
C^{*}
-algebra and
\Delta:\mathcal{A}\to\mathcal{A}\otimes\mathcal{A}
is a unital
*
-homomorphism (called
comultiplication
), is called a
compact quantum semigroup
if the following holds:

(a)

(
Coassociativity
)
(\Delta\otimes\mathrm{id})\Delta=(\mathrm{id}\otimes\Delta)\Delta
.

If the following
cancellation property
also holds, we refer to
\mathbb{G}
as a
compact quantum group
.

(b)

the spaces
(1\otimes\mathcal{A})\Delta(\mathcal{A}):=\mathrm{span}\{(1\otimes a)\Delta(b):a,b\in\mathcal{A}\}
and
(\mathcal{A}\otimes 1)\Delta(\mathcal{A})
are dense in
\mathcal{A}\otimes\mathcal{A}
.

For a compact quantum group
\mathbb{G}=(\mathcal{A},\Delta)
we shall write
C(\mathbb{G}):=\mathcal{A}
.

Let
G
be a compact topological group and
C(G)
be the commutative
C^{*}
-algebra of continuous complex-valued functions on
G
. Since
C(G)\otimes C(G)\simeq C(G\times G)
, we may define
\Delta:C(G)\to C(G)\otimes C(G)
by
\Delta(f)(g,h)=f(gh)
. Then,
(C(G),\Delta)
is a compact quantum group. Moreover, all compact quantum groups
\mathbb{G}
where the
C^{*}
-algebra
C(\mathbb{G})
is commutative, are of this type. Similarly, if
G
is a compact topological semigroup,
C(G)
is a compact quantum semigroup in a canonical manner, and all compact quantum semigroups
\mathbb{G}=(C(\mathbb{G}),\Delta)
where

is commutative, are obtained in this way.

The
convolution
of functionals
\omega_{1},\omega_{2}\in C(\mathbb{G})^{*}
is
\omega_{1}*\omega_{2}=(\omega_{1}\otimes\omega_{2})\Delta
. The following theorem states that every compact quantum group
\mathbb{G}
admits a unique Haar state.

Theorem II.3

(
[
15
, Theorem 1.2.1]
)
.

For a compact quantum group
\mathbb{G}
, there exists a unique state
h\in C(\mathbb{G})^{*}
(the
Haar state
) such that for all
\omega\in C(\mathbb{G})^{*}
, we have
\omega*h=h*\omega=\omega(1)h
.

The following proposition gives a nontrivial example of a compact quantum group.

Proposition II.4

(
[
15
, Proposition 1.1.4]
)
.

Let
\mathcal{A}
be the universal
C^{*}
-algebra generated by
\{u_{i,j}:i,j\in[n]\}
with relations defined by the matrices
[u_{i,j}]_{i,j=1}^{n}
and
[u_{i,j}^{*}]_{i,j=1}^{n}
being invertible. Let
\Delta:\mathcal{A}\to\mathcal{A}\otimes\mathcal{A}
be the unital
*
-homomorphism satisfying

\Delta(u_{i,j})=\sum_{k=1}^{n}u_{i,k}\otimes u_{k,j},\qquad i,j\in[n].

(II.5)

Then,
(\mathcal{A},\Delta)
is a compact quantum group.

As an application of
II.4
, for a nonempty finite set
W
, we define the
quantum permutation group

S_{W}^{+}=(\mathcal{A},\Delta)
as follows. Let
\mathcal{A}
be the universal
C^{*}
-algebra generated by
\{u_{i,j}:i,j\in W\}
and relations defined by the matrix
U=[u_{i,j}]_{i,j\in W}
being a
magic unitary
, that is,
u_{i,j}=u_{i,j}^{*}=u_{i,j}^{2}
for each
i,j\in W
, and the matrix
U
is a unitary
U^{*}U=UU^{*}=\mathrm{diag}(1,\dots,1)
. The comultiplication

III
Transitive games

We now introduce the class of nonlocal games of our interest. Any set
W
appearing in this section is always assumed to be finite and nonempty.

Definition III.1
.

A game
\mathscr{G}=(X,Y,A,B,V)
is called
transitive
if
X=Y=A=B=:W
, and for each
(a,b,x,y)\in W^{4}

such that

.
Such a transitive game
\mathscr{G}
will be written
\mathscr{G}=(W,V)
.

The following lemma is straightforward and another justification for the adjective “transitive”.

Lemma III.2
.

Let
W
be a set and let
\sim
be a transitive relation over
W\times W
. Define a game
\mathscr{G}=(W,V)
by
V(a,b|x,y)=1
if and only if
(a,b)\sim(x,y)
, for all

\mathscr{G}
is transitive.

Conversely, let
\mathscr{G}=(W,V)
be a transitive game. Then the relation
\sim
over
W\times W
defined by
(a,b)\sim(x,y)
if and only if
V(a,b|x,y)=1
, for all
a,b,x,y\in W
, is a transitive relation.

We first show that the set of perfect strategies of a transitive game, if nonempty, has a semigroup structure. Recall that a

.:S\times S\to S
which satisfies the associative property: for all

*

\Delta:C^{*}(\mathscr{G})\to C^{*}(\mathscr{G})\otimes C^{*}(\mathscr{G})
satisfying
\Delta(u_{x,a})=v_{x,a}=\sum_{r\in W}u_{x,r}\otimes u_{r,a}
, as required. Moreover, it is easy to check that
\Delta
satisfies
(\Delta\otimes\mathrm{id})\Delta=(\mathrm{id}\otimes\Delta)\Delta
.

Next, let
U=[u_{x,a}]_{x,a\in W}
. Then, since all the entries of

are self-adjoint projections,
[
17
, Remark 2.1]
tells us that
\sum_{x}u_{x,a}=1
for all

, so that

is a magic unitary, that is,
UU^{*}=\mathrm{diag}(1,\dots,1)=U^{*}U
.

Hence, by
II.4
,
(C^{*}(\mathscr{G}),\Delta)
is indeed a compact quantum group, and since
U=[u_{x,a}]
is a magic unitary,
C^{*}(\mathscr{G})
is a quantum permutation group acting on
W
.
∎

Conversely, we establish that every quantum permutation group acting on a set gives rise to a transitive bisynchronous game.

Theorem IV.3
.

Let
\mathbb{G}
be a quantum permutation group acting on a finite nonempty set
W
. Then, there exists a transitive bisynchronous game
\mathscr{G}=(W,V)
with a perfect
C^{*}
-strategy such that the quantum orbitals of
(C^{*}(\mathscr{G}),\Delta)
are the same as the quantum orbitals of
\mathbb{G}
.

Proof.

Let
U=[u_{x,a}]_{x,a\in W}
be the magic unitary generating
C(\mathbb{G})
. We define a game
\mathscr{G}=(W,V)
with predicate given by
V(a,b|x,y)=0
if and only if
u_{x,a}u_{y,b}=0
. We verify that
\mathscr{G}=(W,V)
is a transitive bisynchronous game. Bisynchronicity is easy to see; we establish transitivity using a trick from
[
13
]
. Let us assume that
V(a,b|c,d)=1
and
V(c,d|x,y)=1
. That is,
u_{c,a}u_{d,b}\neq 0
and
u_{x,c}u_{y,d}\neq 0
. Then,

\Delta(u_{x,a}u_{y,b})=\Delta(u_{x,a})\Delta(u_{y,b})=\sum_{k,l\in W}u_{x,k}u_{y,l}\otimes u_{k,a}u_{l,b}.

Multiplying by
u_{x,c}\otimes u_{c,a}
from the left and
u_{y,d}\otimes u_{d,b}
from the right,

\displaystyle(u_{x,c}\otimes u_{c,a})\Delta(u_{x,a}u_{y,b})(u_{y,d}\otimes u_{d,b})=\sum_{k,l\in W}u_{x,c}u_{x,k}u_{y,l}u_{y,d}\otimes u_{c,a}u_{k,a}u_{l,b}u_{d,b}=u_{x,c}u_{y,d}\otimes u_{c,a}u_{d,b}\neq 0,

where we used
u_{x,c}u_{x,k}=\delta_{k,c}u_{x,c}
(from synchronicity) and similar identities in the last equality. Hence,
V(a,b|x,y)=1
, as needed for transitivity.

Finally, it is evident that the quantum orbitals of
C^{*}(\mathscr{G})
are the same as that of
\mathbb{G}
.∎

Owing to
Theorem

, given a quantum permutation group
\mathbb{G}
acting on a finite nonempty set
W
and generated by
\{u_{x,a}:x,a\in W\}
, we define the
transitive bisynchronous game
\mathscr{G}=(W,V)
associated with

by
V(a,b|x,y)=0
if and only if
u_{x,a}u_{y,b}=0
. We should note that this correspondence is not one-to-one. Indeed, the game corresponding to a quantum permutation group only depends on its quantum orbitals. Hence, quantum permutation groups inducing the same orbitals have the same game corresponding to them. For example,

and

induce the same quantum orbitals, and the same corresponding game.

Remark IV.4
.

We note that

Let

IV.1
Equivalence of existence of local, quantum commuting, and
C^{*}
-algebraic strategies

We now proceed to show that if a transitive bisynchronous game has a perfect
C^{*}
-strategy, then it has a perfect quantum commuting strategy.

We recall the following results from
[
6
, Section 2]
. Suppose
\mathbb{G}=(\mathcal{A},\Delta)
is a compact quantum group with Haar state

\mathcal{O}(\mathbb{G})
be the Hopf
*
-algebra of
\mathbb{G}
equipped with counit
\epsilon
and antipode

but not on the
C^{*}
-algebra

be the left kernel of

for all

on
\mathcal{A}_{r}

of

and

from
\mathcal{A}

if the counit
\epsilon_{r}
of

is norm-bounded (and hence can be extended to a
*
-homomorphism on
\mathcal{A}_{r}

Let
\mathscr{G}=(W,V)
be a transitive bisynchronous game with a perfect
C^{*}
-algebraic strategy, and let

be the associated quantum permutation group acting on

on a compact quantum group is neither faithful nor tracial on

; see
[

,
25
]
for examples of such compact quantum groups. It is known that even for quantum permutation groups, the Haar state may not be faithful, see
[

]
or

, Section 2.7]
.

Theorem IV.7

(
[
6
, Theorem 2.2]
)
.

A compact quantum group

is co-amenable if, and only if, its Haar state is faithful and its counit is norm-bounded.

Theorem IV.8

(
[
15
, Proposition 1.7.9]
)
.

Let
\mathbb{G}
be a compact quantum group and

be its Haar state. Let
\mathcal{O}(\mathbb{G})
be the Hopf

-algebra of
\mathbb{G}
, and
\epsilon
and

be the counit and antipode of

is tracial if, and only if,

Proposition IV.9
.

The Haar state
h_{r}
on a reduced quantum permutation group is both faithful and tracial.

Proof.

Let
\mathbb{G}
be a quantum permutation group. Then, it is of Kac type (see
[

, Example 1.7.10]
) which implies that its Haar state is tracial. Hence, the Haar state

on the reduced quantum group satisfies: for all

Hence,

is tracial. Faithfulness of

is by the construction described in the previous paragraph.
∎

Now we can prove our result about the existence of a perfect qc strategy.

Theorem IV.10
.

Let
\mathscr{G}=(W,V)
be a transitive bisynchronous game. If
\mathscr{G}
has a perfect

Proof.

Let
\mathbb{G}=(C^{*}(\mathscr{G}),\Delta)

IV.2
Quantum permutation groups arising from transitive nonlocal games

We end this section by showing that transitive nonlocal games satisfying

for all

directly arise from graph automorphism games.

Proposition IV.12
.

Let
V
be the predicate of a transitive synchronous game. Then,

is the predicate of the graph automorphism game of an complete edge labelled directed graph with loops if and only if satisfies the following conditions:

(a)

for all

(b)

V(a,b|x,y)=V(x,y|a,b)
for all

.

Since the the predicate of the game is transitive, and it satisfies (a) and (b), it is easy to see that the relation

defined in

becomes an equivalence relation. Hence, we may divide

into equivalence classes

. Let us colour the complete directed graph (including loops) with vertex set

if and only if it is in the equivalence class

. Let us call this graph

. It is easy to see that

, which happens if and only
(a,b)

have the same colour. Hence,

is indeed the predicate of the automorphism game of the edge coloured complete directed graph

. The other direction is quite easy to see.
∎

.

Let
\mathscr{G}
be a bisynchronous transitive game with a perfect quantum strategy. Then,

is the quantum automorphism group of a complete edge and vertex labelled directed graph,

Let

V
Coherent and transitive correlations

All correlations considered in this section have the same questions and answers set, viz.,
W
. A correlation
p=(p(a,b|x,y))
is called
transitive
if for all
a,b,x,y\in W
, if we have
p(a,b|x,y)=0
, then
p(a,b|r,s)p(r,s|x,y)=0
for all
r,s\in W
.

A correlation
p=(p(a,b|x,y))
induces a (nonempty) relation
\mathcal{R}
on
W\times W
by: for
(a,b),(x,y)\in W\times W
we say
(a,b)\sim(x,y)
if
p(a,b|x,y)\neq 0
. It is again straightforward to verify that if
p
is transitive, then its induced relation
\mathcal{R}
is transitive.

A correlation
p
is said to be
coherent
if
p
is bisynchronous and its induced relation
\mathcal{R}
on
W\times W
is an equivalence relation. (In fact, we only need the relation to be symmetric and transitive: for each
(x,y)\in W\times W
, we have
\sum_{a,b}p(a,b|x,y)=1
which implies that there exists some
(a,b)\in W\times W
such that
(a,b)\sim(x,y)
. By symmetry,
(x,y)\sim(a,b)
, and hence by transitivity,
(x,y)\sim(x,y)
, as needed for reflexivity.) This nomenclature is partly justified by the following lemma.

Lemma V.1
.

Let

be a coherent correlation and let
\mathcal{R}
be the associated relation on

. Then the partition of

is a coherent configuration.

We verify the three conditions (a)–(c) in

II.8
. Let the equivalence classes arising from the equivalence relation
\mathcal{R}
be given by

.

To verify property (a), let

. Since
\mathcal{R}
is a partition of

, there exists some

such that
(x,x)\in R_{i}
. It then suffices to show that
R_{i}
does not contain “off-diagonal” elements. Let
(a,b)\sim(x,x)
. Then
p(a,b|x,x)\neq 0
, and since

is synchronous, we must have

.

To show (b), fix a class

, and let

. By

, so that

. Thus the converse of

To show (c), we borrow an argument used in the proof of

\mathcal{A}

We want to show that

\displaystyle=u_{x,x^{\prime}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sum_{y\in S}1}}\right)u_{z,z^{\prime}}=u_{x,x^{\prime}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sum_{y\in S}\sum_{y^{\prime}\in W}u_{y,y^{\prime}}}}\right)u_{z,z^{\prime}}

Correlation generated by the fundamental representation and Haar state

We begin with a simple family of coherent correlations, and give necessary and sufficient conditions for them to be nonlocal — they are nonlocal if and only if the coherent configuration associated with them is non-Schurian.

be a quantum permutation group acting on a set
W
with fundamental representation
[u_{i,j}]_{i,j\in W}
, and Haar state
h
. In
[
13
, Theorem 4.2]
, it was shown that if
R_{1},\dots,R_{m}\subseteq W\times W
are the quantum orbitals of
\mathbb{G}
, then

\displaystyle h(u_{x,a}u_{y,b})=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{|R_{i}|}&\text{ \ if \ }(x,y),\ (a,b)\in R_{i},\\
0&\text{ \ if \ }(x,y)\nsim_{2}\ (a,b).\end{cases}

Since the Haar state is tracial and
[u_{i,j}]_{i,j\in W}
is a magic unitary, we see that defining
p_{\mathbb{G}}(a,b|x,y):=h(u_{x,a}u_{y,b})
(for all
a,b,x,y\in W
) yields a quantum commuting correlation. We note that the correlation
p_{\mathbb{G}}
only depends on the quantum orbitals of
\mathbb{G}
. From
[
13
, Theorem 3.10]
, we know that the quantum orbitals of a quantum permutation group form a coherent configuration. Hence, the correlation
p_{\mathbb{G}}
is a coherent correlation.

Proposition V.2
.

Let
\mathbb{G}
be a quantum permutation group acting on a set
W
. Then,
p_{\mathbb{G}}
is local if and only if the coherent configuration of quantum orbitals of
\mathbb{G}
is Schurian.

Proof.

First, let us assume that the quantum orbitals of
\mathbb{G}
form a Schurian coherent configuration. Then, there is a finite group
G\subseteq S_{W}
, whose orbitals are the same as the quantum orbitals of
\mathbb{G}
. It is then easy to see that
C(G)
, its Haar state and its fundamental representation induce the same correlation
p_{\mathbb{G}}
as the Haar state and the fundamental representation of
\mathbb{G}
. Since
C(G)
is a commutative
C^{*}
-algebra, it follows that
p_{\mathbb{G}}
is indeed local.

Now, let us assume that
p_{\mathbb{G}}
is local. Then, it follows from
Theorem

II.1
that there is a commutative
C^{*}
-algebra
\mathcal{A}
, with projections
\{p_{i,j}:i,j\in W\}
, satisfying
\sum_{i\in W}p_{i,j}=\sum_{j\in W}p_{i,j}=1
and a faithful tracial state
s
, such that
p_{\mathbb{G}}(a,b|x,y)=s(p_{x,a}p_{y,b})
. In particular, the universal
C^{*}
-algebra generated by
\{u_{i,j}:i,j\in W\}
satisfying:

(a)

u_{i,j}^{2}=u_{i,j}^{*}=u_{i,j}
,

(b)

\sum_{i\in W}u_{i,j}=\sum_{j\in W}u_{i,j}=1
,

(c)

p(a,b|x,y)=0\Rightarrow u_{x,a}u_{y,b}=0
,

(d)

u_{x,a}u_{y,b}=u_{y,b}u_{x,a}
,

for all
i,j,x,y,a,b
is non-trivial. Since
[u_{i,j}]
is a magic unitary, and since this universal
C^{*}
-algebra is commutative, it is of the form
C(G)
, where
G\subseteq S_{W}
is a permutation group. It is easy to see that the orbitals of
G
are the same as that of
\mathbb{G}
. Hence, the coherent configuration of the quantum orbitals of
\mathbb{G}
is indeed Schurian by construction.
∎

To give an explicit example of a quantum permutation group whose quantum orbitals form a non-Schurian coherent configuration, we first need the following result.

Theorem V.3

(
[
13
, Theorem 4.5]
)
.

Let
X
and
Y
be connected graphs. Then,
X\cong_{qc}Y
if and only if there exists
x\in V(X)
and
y\in V(Y)
that are in the same orbit of
\mathrm{Qut}(X\sqcup Y)
.

Proposition V.4
.

Let
X
and
Y
be two connected non-isomorphic graphs such that
X\cong_{qc}Y
. Then, the quantum orbitals of
\mathrm{Qut}(X\sqcup Y)
form a non-Schurian coherent configuration. Hence,
p_{{\mathrm{Qut}{(X\sqcup Y)}}}
is nonlocal.

Proof.

Let
[u_{ij}]
be the fundamental representation of
\mathrm{Qut}(X\sqcup Y)
and consider the quantum orbitals of
\mathrm{Qut}(X\sqcup Y)
. From
Theorem

V.3
, it follows that there exists
x\in V(X)
and
y\in V(Y)
such that
u_{x,y}\neq 0
. Now, let us assume that there exists some permutation group
G\subseteq S_{V(X)\sqcup V(Y)}
which induces the same orbitals as
\mathrm{Qut}(X\sqcup Y)
. Let
[v_{ij}]
be the fundamental representation of
C(G)
. We see that if
\mathrm{rel}(i,k)\neq\mathrm{rel}(j,l)
, then
v_{i,j}v_{k,l}=0
. Moreover, the
v_{i,j}
’s commute. Hence,
G\subseteq\mathrm{Aut}(X\sqcup Y)
. Since
v_{x,y}\neq 0
, we see that there exists a graph automorphism
g
of
X\sqcup Y
mapping
x
to
y
. This, however, is impossible since
X
and
Y
are not isomorphic. Hence, the coherent configuration induced by the quantum orbitals of
\mathrm{Qut}(X\sqcup Y)
is non-Schurian. ∎

V.2
General coherent correlations

Now that we have established a strong connection between the nonlocality of a coherent correlation and the coherent configuration associated with it, we try to generalise similar results for all coherent correlations.

Let
\Omega=\{R_{i}\}_{i\in I}
be a coherent configuration. Then, it is easy to verify that the set of all coherent correlations
p
, for which
\sim_{p}
induces the same coherent configuration as
\Omega
is a semigroup. We shall denote this semigroup by
P_{\Omega}
. By
Lemma

III.5
, the hardest game
\mathscr{G}_{\Omega}
that can be won using these strategies
P_{\Omega}
is a bisynchronous transitive game. Let us denote the equivalence relation by
\sim_{\Omega}
. The predicate
V_{\Omega}
of this game
\mathscr{G}_{\Omega}
is given by

\displaystyle V_{\Omega}(a,b|x,y)=\begin{cases}0&\text{if \ }(x,y)\nsim_{\Omega}(a,b),\\
1&\text{if \ }(x,y)\sim_{\Omega}(a,b).\end{cases}

Since
\mathscr{G}_{\Omega}
is a transitive game, the set of all perfect qc-strategies for
\mathscr{G}_{\Omega}
is also a semigroup. If
P_{\Omega}
is non-empty,
\mathscr{G}_{\Omega}
has a non-trivial
C^{*}
-algebra
C^{*}(\mathscr{G}_{\Omega})
, which is a quantum permutation group. In particular, since all the correlations in
P_{\Omega}
are perfect qc-strategies for
\mathscr{G}_{\Omega}
, the following results hold.

Proposition V.5
.

Let
p
be a coherent qc correlation. Then, there exists

i.

a quantum permutation group
\mathbb{G}
with fundamental representation
[u_{i,j}]
acting on a set
W
, whose orbitals are same as the coherent configuration induced by
\sim_{p}
,

ii.

a
C^{*}
-algebra
\mathcal{A}
equipped with a faithful tracial state
s
, projections
e_{i,j}\in\mathcal{A}
for
i,j\in W
, such that
\sum_{j}e_{i,j}=\sum_{i}e_{i,j}=1
and
V(a,b|x,y)=0\Rightarrow e_{x,a}e_{y,b}=0
,

iii.

and a
*
-homomorphism from
C(\mathbb{G})\to\mathcal{A}
taking
u_{i,j}\to e_{i,j}

such that
p(a,b|x,y)=s(e_{x,a}e_{y,b})

The following corollary is merely a restatement of the previous result.

Corollary V.6
.

Let
p
be a coherent qc correlation. Then, there exists a quantum permutation group
\mathbb{G}\subseteq S_{W}^{+}
, with fundamental representation
[u_{i,j}]
acting on
W
, whose orbitals are the same as the partitions induced by
\sim_{p}
and a tracial state
s
on
C^{*}(G)
, such that
p(a,b|x,y)=s(u_{x,a}u_{y,b})
.

Proof.

Let
\pi
be the
*
-homomorphism from
C^{*}(G)
to
A
defined by
u_{i,j}\mapsto e_{i,j}
. It is now easy to verify that
s\circ\pi
is a tracial state on
C^{*}(\mathscr{G}_{\Omega_{p}})
satisfying the required conditions.
∎

Theorem V.7
.

Let
p
be a coherent local correlation. Then, there exists a group
G\subseteq S_{n}
acting on
W
, whose orbitals are the same as the partitions induced by
\sim_{p}
, and a probability distribution
\pi
on
G
such that

\displaystyle p(a,b|x,y)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\{g\in G\colon g(x)=a\\
\&\ g(y)=b\}\end{subarray}}\pi(g).

Proof.

Since
p
is a coherent correlation, we see that there exists a state
s
on
C^{*}(\mathscr{G}_{\Omega_{p}})
such that
p(a,b|x,y)=s(u_{x,a}u_{y,b})
. Let
\widetilde{C^{*}}(G_{\Omega_{p}})
be the quotient of
C^{*}(G)
by the relations
u_{x,a}u_{y,b}=u_{y,b}u_{x,a}
for all
a,b,x,y\in W
. Since
p
is a local correlation,
\widetilde{C^{*}}(\mathscr{G}_{\Omega_{p}})
is nontrivial. Moreover,
\widetilde{C^{*}}(\mathscr{G}_{\Omega_{p}})
is a commutative
C^{*}
-algebra, and also a quantum permutation group. Hence, there exists a group
G\subseteq S_{W}
, such that
\widetilde{C^{*}}(\mathscr{G}_{\Omega_{p}})=C(G)
. It is easy to see that the orbitals of
G
are the same as the partitions induced by
\sim_{p}
.

Since
p
is a local correlation, there exists a commutative
C^{*}
-algebra
A
equipped a faithful tracial state
s
, projections
e_{i,j}\in A
for
i,j\in W
, such that
\sum_{j}e_{i,j}=\sum_{i}e_{i,j}=1
, and a
*
-homomorphism
\phi
from
C^{*}(G)\to A
taking
u_{i,j}\to e_{i,j}
, where
[u_{i,j}]_{i,j}
is the fundamental representation of
\mathbb{G}
, such that
p(a,b|x,y)=s(e_{x,a}e_{y,b})
. Hence,
u_{i,j}\to e_{i,j}
, defines a
*
-homomorphism
\widetilde{\phi}
, which implies that
s\circ\widetilde{\phi}
is a state on
C(G)
. We know that states on
C(G)
correspond to regular complete probability measures on
G
. Since
G
is finite, the state
s\circ\widetilde{\phi}
corresponds to a probability distribution
\pi
on
G
.

Moreover, we have that

\displaystyle p(a,b|x,y)=s(u_{x,a}u_{y,b})=\int_{G}u_{x,a}u_{y,b}d\pi=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}g\in G\colon g(x)=a\\
\&\ g(y)=b\end{subarray}}\pi(g),

as needed.
∎

As a corollary we get:

Corollary V.8
.

Let
p
be a coherent quantum commuting correlation. If the coherent configuration of partitions induced by
\sim_{p}
is non-Schurian,
p
is nonlocal.

Proof.

Let us assume that
p
is a local coherent correlation such that the partitions induced by
\sim_{p}
form a non-Schurian coherent configuration. From
Theorem

V.7
it follows that there exists a group
G\subseteq S_{W}
, whose orbitals are the same as the partitions induced by
\sim_{p}
. This proves the contrapositive of the statement that was to be proved.
∎

We should note that when the coherent configuration of partitions induced by
\sim_{p}
is Schurian, the correlation can either be local or nonlocal.

Example V.9
.

Let us consider a classical group
G\subseteq S_{n}
. Then, the correlation induced by the Haar state of
G
, and its fundamental representation
p_{G}
is local. It is easy to see that
\sim_{p_{G}}
is an equivalence relation, and partitions induced by it are precisely the orbitals of
G
, which is a coherent configuration.

Example V.10
.

Let us take any nonlocal correlation
q
and consider
\lambda q+(1-\lambda)p_{S_{n}}
for some
0<\lambda<1
, where
p_{S_{n}}
, is the correlation induced by the Haar state and the standard representation of
S_{n}
. Then, clearly
p(a,b|x,y)=0
if and only if
\delta_{x,y}\neq\delta_{a,b}
. Hence,
p
is nonlocal and
\sim_{p}
partitions
[n]\times[n]
into the same partitions as the orbitals of
S_{n}
.

V.3
Transitive correlations

Now, we want to establish a similar set of results for transitive correlations. Proceeding in the same fashion as the case of coherent correlations, we can prove the following proposition.

Proposition V.11
.

Let
\sim
be a transitive relation on
W\times W
such that

i.

(a,b)\not\sim(x,x)
if
a\neq b

ii.

(a,b)\sim(x,y)
if and only if
(b,a)\sim(y,x)

Then, the set
P_{\sim}
of all transitive correlations
p
such that
\sim\ =\ \sim_{p}
, if it is non-empty, forms a semigroup.

Given such a transitive relation, it is easy to see that the hardest nonlocal game that can be won using these strategies is a synchronous transitive game, say
\mathscr{G}_{\sim}
. Since
\mathscr{G}_{\sim}
is a synchronous transitive game, the
C^{*}
-algebra of the game
C^{*}(\mathscr{G}_{\sim})
is a compact quantum semigroup. Hence, proceeding in a similar manner as we did in the case of coherent correlations, the following results follow.

Proposition V.12
.

Let
p
be a transitive qc correlation. Then, there exists a compact quantum semigroup
\mathbb{G}
acting on
W
, generated by projections
u_{i,j}
such that
\sum_{j}u_{i,j}=1
and a tracial state
s
on
C(\mathbb{G})
such that
such that
p(a,b|x,y)=s(u_{x,a}u_{y,b})
.

Proof.

Follows from the fact that
C^{*}(\mathscr{G}_{\sim_{p}})
is a compact quantum semigroup.
∎

Theorem V.13
.

Let
p
be a local transitive correlation. Then, there exists a semigroup
G
of functions from
W
to
W
, and a probability distribution
\pi
on
G
such that

p(a,b|x,y)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}f\in G:f(x)=a\\
\&\ f(y)=b\end{subarray}}\pi(f).

Proof.

Let us take
\widetilde{C^{*}}(G_{\sim_{p}})
to be the universal
C^{*}
-algebra generated by projections
u_{i,j}
such that

(a)

\sum_{j}u_{i,j}=1

(b)

u_{x,a}u_{y,b}=0
if
p(a,b|x,y)=0

(c)

u_{x,a}u_{y,b}=u_{y,b}u_{x,a}

Then,
\widetilde{C^{*}}(\mathscr{G}_{\sim_{p}})
is a compact quantum semigroup acting on
W
. Since
p
is a local transitive correlation,
\widetilde{C^{*}}(\mathscr{G}_{\sim_{p}})
is non-trivial. Hence, there exists a semigroup
G
such that
\widetilde{C^{*}}(\mathscr{G}_{\sim_{p}})=C(G)
. It is easy to see that
G
is a semigroup of functions from
W
to
W
, since the action of
G
on
W
can be constructed from the action of
\widetilde{C^{*}}(\mathscr{G}_{\sim_{p}})
on
\mathbb{C}^{W}
. Since
p
is a perfect local strategy of
\mathscr{G}_{\sim_{p}}
, it corresponds to a state
s
on
C(G)
, i.e., a probability distribution
\pi
on
G
such that

\displaystyle p(a,b|x,y)=s(u_{x,a}u_{yb})=\int_{G}u_{x,a}u_{y,b}d\pi\quad=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}f\in G:f(x)=a\\
\&\ f(y)=b\end{subarray}}\pi(f),

as needed.
∎
